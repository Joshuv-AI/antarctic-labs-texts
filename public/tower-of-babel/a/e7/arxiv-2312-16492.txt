Abstract
. For a class of reducible Hamiltonian partial differential equations (PDEs) with arbitrary spatial dimension, quantified by a quadratic polynomial with time-dependent coefficients, we present a comprehensive classification of long-term solution behaviors within Sobolev space. This classification is achieved through the utilization of Metaplectic and Schrödinger representations. Each pattern of Sobolev norm behavior corresponds to a specific n − n- dimensional symplectic normal form, as detailed in Theorems 1.1 and 1.2 . When applied to periodically or quasi-periodically forced n − n- dimensional quantum harmonic oscillators, we identify novel growth rates for the ℋ s − \mathcal{H}^{s}- norm as t t tends to infinity, such as t ( n − 1 ) ​ s ​ e λ ​ s ​ t t^{(n-1)s}e^{\lambda st} (with λ > 0 \lambda>0 ) and t ( 2 ​ n − 1 ) ​ s + ι ​ t 2 ​ n ​ s t^{(2n-1)s}+\iota t^{2ns} (with ι ≥ 0 \iota\geq 0 ). Notably, we demonstrate that stability in Sobolev space, defined as the boundedness of the Sobolev norm, is essentially a unique characteristic of one-dimensional scenarios, as outlined in Theorem 1.3 . As a byproduct, we discover that the growth rate of the Sobolev norm for the quantum Hamiltonian can be directly described by that of the solution to the classical Hamiltonian which exhibits the optimal growth, as articulated in Theorem 1.4 . Keywords: Quadratic Hamiltonian; Reducibility; Symplectic normal form; Metaplectic representation; Schrödinger representation; Classification of growth of Sobolev norm MSC 2020: 35Q40, 37C60, 35Q41, 47G30

Introduction of the Main Results

This paper focuses on the Sobolev norms of solutions to Hamiltonian partial differential equations (PDEs), particularly aiming to introduce new behavioral patterns and to provide a thorough classification of growth rates for certain quantum Hamiltonians. This task is approached through a reducibility argument and a complete classification of quantum normal forms.

1.2.
Classification of growth rates (GRs) of Sobolev norms

Proposed by Bourgain
[
12
]

[
14

25

31

33
]

Particularly, the existence of unbounded trajectories in Sobolev space is intrinsically linked to weak turbulent effects and energy cascades. In recent years, considerable advancements have been made in this area for a variety of quantum Hamiltonian systems.

As discussed in the previous subsection, examining the growth of Sobolev norms of solutions is intimately connected to the reducibility of the original quantum Hamiltonian, especially in the case of linear Hamiltonians. Following
[
27
]
, Bambusi-Grébert-Maspero-Robert
[
5
]
analyzed a
1-

t^{s}

{{\mathcal{H}}}^{s}-
norms through reducibility to a transport equation. For a
1-
D QHO with time quasi-periodic perturbations, which are quadratic polynomials of
(D,X)
, various growth rates of
{{\mathcal{H}}}^{s}-
norms have been observed depending on the normal forms of reducibility
[
43
,
46
]

t^{s}
polynomial growth for parabolic normal form, exponential growth for hyperbolic normal form, and
t^{2s}
polynomial growth when reducible to the Stark Hamiltonian.

It is important to note that almost reducibility (i.e., conjugation to a time-independent system through a sequence of transformations without demonstrating the convergence of this sequence) is also insightful for understanding the long-term behaviors of solutions. For a
1-
D QHO with a time quasi-periodic quadratic perturbation, it was shown in
[
44
]
that the growth of
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}
-norms can oscillate with an optimal
o(t^{s})
upper bound if the original equation is not reducible. Bambusi and his collaborators employed a similar concept for time-dependent Schrödinger equations
[
6
,
8
,
9
]

t^{\epsilon}

\epsilon>0

Another method often used to achieve unbounded trajectories involves constructing specific perturbations that induce infinite growth. For a
1-
D QHO, Delort
[
16
]
(also see Maspero
[
48
]
) designed a time-periodic order-zero pseudo-differential operator as the perturbation, leading to

{{\mathcal{H}}}^{s}-
norms. In an abstract setting, Maspero
[
49
,
50
]
further explored time-periodic perturbations and provided conditions under which certain solutions exhibit such polynomial growth. For a
2-

[
23
]
embedded Arnold diffusion into an infinite-dimensional quantum system, constructing a time-decaying potential that resulted in logarithmic growth of Sobolev norms over time. Similarly, Thomann
[
54
]
, based on studies of linear Lowest Landau Level equations with a time-dependent potential
[
53
]
, designed a perturbation that projects onto Bargmann-Fock space, resulting in polynomial growth of Sobolev norms over time for some traveling wave solutions. Bourgain
[
13
]
and Haus-Maspero
[
34

Several equivalent forms of the

Inspired by previous studies, the primary goal of this paper is to provide a comprehensive classification of the growth rates of Sobolev norms for solutions to Eq. (

Theorem 1.2
.

(Classification of GRs of Sobolev norms)

Consider Eq. (

1.3.
Strategy of proof

The classical-quantum correspondence facilitated by Weyl quantization is well-established. More specifically, for any given symbol
h=h(\xi,x)
, where
\xi,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
n\geq 1
, the Weyl operator
h^{W}
associated with
h
is defined as follows:

(1.15)

\left(h^{W}u\right)(x)=\int_{\xi,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{2\pi{\rm i}\langle\xi,x-y\rangle}\,h\left(\xi,\frac{x+y}{2}\right)u(y)\,dy\,d\xi,\qquad\forall\ u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}).

Notably, if
h
is a quadratic polynomial in
(\xi,x)
, then the corresponding Weyl operator
h^{W}
becomes a quadratic polynomial in
(D,X)
after symmetrization. Therefore, to comprehensively understand Eq. (
1.2
), it is essential to examine the associated finite-dimensional Hamiltonian system. This approach was utilized in
[
5
]
to achieve reducibility and the almost preservation of Sobolev norms for the time quasi-periodic perturbed QHO, by implementing a classical KAM scheme for “most” frequency vectors.
Similarly, in the
1-
D context, the (almost) reducibility and diverse behaviors of Sobolev norms have been explored in
[
43
,
44
,
46
]
. This was achieved by adapting Eliasson’s KAM scheme for time quasi-periodic
{\rm sl}(2,{\mathbb{R}})-
linear systems
[
19
]
, where the reducibility transformation is not necessarily close to the identity, to the quantum Hamiltonian.

The reducibility assumption of the affine system

(1.16)

z^{\prime}={\mathcal{A}}(t)z+\ell(t),\qquad{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot)\in C_{b}^{0}({\mathbb{R}},{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})),\qquad\ell(\cdot)\in C_{b}^{0}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}),

means that there exist
U(\cdot)\in C_{b}^{1}({\mathbb{R}},{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}}))
and
v(\cdot)\in C_{b}^{1}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
such that, via the transformation
z=U(t)w+v(t)
, the system (
1.16
) is conjugated to the affine system with constant coefficients
w^{\prime}={\mathcal{B}}w+l
. Similar to the strategy of reducibility in
[
46
]
, the reducibility of the quantum Hamiltonian (
1.2
) is achieved in two steps.

1)

Through the transformation
z=U(t)y
, the system (
1.16
) is conjugated to the affine system

(1.17)

y^{\prime}={\mathcal{B}}y+\tilde{\ell}(t),\qquad{\mathcal{B}}\in{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}}),\qquad\tilde{\ell}(\cdot)\in C_{b}^{0}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}),\ \ {\rm with}\ \ \tilde{\ell}(t)=U(t)^{-1}\ell(t).

Simultaneously, Eq. (
1.2
) is conjugated to the quantum Hamiltonian

(1.18)

\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\partial_{t}\phi=({\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{B}}}+{\mathcal{L}}_{\tilde{\ell}(t)})\phi,

via the
L^{2}-
unitary transformation
\psi={\mathcal{M}}(U(t))\phi
, where
{\mathcal{M}}(U(\cdot))
denotes the Metaplectic representation of
U(\cdot)
(refer to Section
2.3
for details).

2)

By applying the translation
y=w+U(t)^{-1}v(t)
, the system (
1.17
) is transformed into

w^{\prime}={\mathcal{B}}w+l,\qquad{\mathcal{B}}\in{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}}),\qquad l\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n},

where all coefficients are time-independent. In parallel, the Hamiltonian PDE (
1.18
) is conjugated to
\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\partial_{t}\varphi=({\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{B}}}+{\mathcal{L}}_{l})\varphi
(up to a scalar term, typically omitted, see Remark
11
), through the
L^{2}-
unitary transformation
\phi=\rho(U(t)^{-1}v(t))\varphi
. Here,
\rho(U(\cdot)^{-1}v(\cdot))
represents the Schrödinger representation of
U(\cdot)^{-1}v(\cdot)
(for details, see Section
2.2
).

The strategy for proving reducibility is summarized in the following diagram.

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}&{\rm Affine\ system}&&{\rm Hamiltonian\ PDE}&\\
&&&&\\
&z^{\prime}={\mathcal{A}}(t)z+\ell(t)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm Quantized}}{{\longrightarrow}}&\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\partial_{t}\psi=({\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{A}}(t)}+{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell(t)})\psi&\\
&&&&\\
z=U(t)y&\big\downarrow&&\big\downarrow&\psi={\mathcal{M}}(U(t))\phi\\
&&&&\\
&y^{\prime}={\mathcal{B}}y+\tilde{\ell}(t)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm Quantized}}{{\longrightarrow}}&\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\partial_{t}\phi=({\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{B}}}+{\mathcal{L}}_{\tilde{\ell}(t)})\phi&\\
&&&&\\
y=w+U(t)^{-1}v(t)&\big\downarrow&&\big\downarrow&\phi=\rho(U(t)^{-1}v(t))\varphi\\
&&&&\\
&w^{\prime}={\mathcal{B}}w+l&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm Quantized}}{{\longrightarrow}}&\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\partial_{t}\varphi=({\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{B}}}+{\mathcal{L}}_{l})\varphi&\end{array}

The classical-quantum correspondence in this context is achieved through the Metaplectic and Schrödinger representations. Section
3
, the cornerstone of this article, presents detailed estimates of the Sobolev norms for both representations (see Proposition
3.2
and
3.7
). The provided upper and lower bounds demonstrate the equivalence of the Sobolev norm under these representations, which allows us to concentrate on the constant QNFs established in Theorem
1.1
and to undertake concrete computations once the quantum Hamiltonian flow is explicitly defined. Moreover, we establish a link between the growth patterns of homogeneous classical and quantum Hamiltonian flows (refer to Corollary
3.3
- (ii)).
Consequently, for QNF1 to QNF3 in Theorem
1.1
, which are all homogeneous, only the Metaplectic representation is required and the behavior of solutions in Sobolev space can be directly inferred from the corresponding classical linear system.
For the remaining cases, by employing an inductive approach based on the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula, the quantum Hamiltonian flow is still explicitly derivable (as discussed in Section
6
).

s\geq 0
) is defined as

{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}):=\left\{f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}):{\mathcal{T}}^{\frac{s}{2}}f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\right\},\quad s\geq 0,

equipped with the graph norm, known as the
Sobolev norm
or
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}
-norm
:

\|f\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}:=\left(\|{\mathcal{T}}^{\frac{s}{2}}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}+\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and for
s<0
,
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})=({\mathcal{H}}^{-s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))^{\prime}
by duality. For convenience, we sometimes denote
L^{2}
and
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}
instead of
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
respectively,
when the dimension of the base space is explicitly stated, and we abbreviate
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{s}}
as
\|\cdot\|_{s}
.

Define the
Fourier transform

{{\mathcal{F}}}
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and its inverse
{{\mathcal{F}}}^{-1}
as follows

\displaystyle({{\mathcal{F}}}f)(\xi)\,=\,\hat{f}(\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\langle x,\xi\rangle}f(x)\,dx,\qquad f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),

\displaystyle({{\mathcal{F}}}^{-1}\hat{f})(x)\,=\,f(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{2\pi{\rm i}\langle x,\xi\rangle}\hat{f}(\xi)\,d\xi,\qquad\hat{f}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}).

Moreover, the
partial Fourier transform

{{\mathcal{F}}}_{k}
,
k=1,\cdots,n
, on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, and its inverse are defined as

\displaystyle({{\mathcal{F}}}_{k}f)(x_{1},\cdots,x_{k-1},\xi_{k},x_{k+1},\cdots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}x_{k}\xi_{k}}f(x)\,dx_{k},\qquad f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),

\displaystyle({{\mathcal{F}}}_{k}^{-1}\hat{f})(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{k-1},x_{k},\xi_{k+1},\cdots,\xi_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{2\pi{\rm i}x_{k}\xi_{k}}\hat{f}(\xi)\,d\xi_{k},\qquad\hat{f}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}).

For given
s\geq 0
, it is well known that
{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{F}}_{k}:{\mathcal{H}}^{s}\to{\mathcal{H}}^{s}
, and for
u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}
,

(2.2)

\|u\|_{s}=\|{\mathcal{F}}u\|_{s}=\|{\mathcal{F}}_{k}u\|_{s},\quad\|X_{k}^{s}u\|_{L^{2}}=\|D_{k}^{s}{\mathcal{F}}_{k}u\|_{L^{2}},\quad\|X_{j}^{s}u\|_{L^{2}}=\|X_{j}^{s}{\mathcal{F}}_{k}u\|_{L^{2}},\quad j\neq k.

According to
[
23
]
, for
u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}
,
s\geq 0
, the equivalent forms of the
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}-
norm can be expressed as follows:

\|u\|_{s}\simeq\|\langle D\rangle^{s}u\|_{L^{2}}+\|\langle X\rangle^{s}u\|_{L^{2}}=\|\langle X\rangle^{s}\hat{u}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\langle X\rangle^{s}u\|_{L^{2}}\simeq\|u\|_{L^{2}}+\||X|^{s}\hat{u}\|_{L^{2}}+\||X|^{s}u\|_{L^{2}},

and, in accordance with
[
30
]
, if
s\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
, then

\displaystyle\|u\|_{s}\simeq\sup_{\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}\atop{|\alpha|+|\beta|\leq s}}\|X^{\alpha}D^{\beta}u\|_{L^{2}}.

Furthermore, for
s>0
,
u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}\setminus\{0\}
is equivalent to one of the following arguments:

(i)

\|u\|_{s}>0
,

(ii)

\|\langle X\rangle^{s}u\|_{L^{2}}>0
and
\|\langle X\rangle^{s}{\mathcal{F}}u\|_{L^{2}}>0
,

(iii)

\|X_{j}^{s}u\|_{L^{2}}>0
and
\|X_{j}^{s}{\mathcal{F}}u\|_{L^{2}}>0
for
j=1,\cdots,n
.

2.2.
Schrödinger representation

Let
{\bf H_{n}}
represent the real
(2n+1)-
dimensional
Heisenberg group
, which is
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1}
equipped with the group law

(p,q,t)(p^{\prime},q^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\left(p+p^{\prime},q+q^{\prime},t+t^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}(\langle p,q^{\prime}\rangle-\langle q,p^{\prime}\rangle)\right),\quad p,q,p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\quad t,t^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}.

The map
\rho
, from
{\bf H_{n}}
to the group of unitary operators on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, is defined as

\rho(p,q,t)=e^{2\pi{\rm i}(\langle p,D\rangle+\langle q,X\rangle+t{\rm Id})}=e^{2\pi{\rm i}t}e^{2\pi{\rm i}(\langle p,D\rangle+\langle q,X\rangle)},\quad(p,q,t)\in{\bf H_{n}},

meaning, for
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\left(\rho(p,q,t)f\right)(x)=e^{2\pi{\rm i}t+2\pi{\rm i}\langle q,x\rangle+\pi{\rm i}\langle p,q\rangle}f(x+p),

\rho
then becomes an irreducible unitary representation of
{\bf H_{n}}
on the Hilbert space
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, known as the
Schrödinger representation
. Since the variable
t
always acts in a straightforward manner, it is often convenient to omit it entirely. Consequently, we redefine
\rho
from
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
to
B({\mathcal{H}}^{s})
,
s\geq 0
, as:

\rho\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}=\rho(p,q,0)=e^{2\pi{\rm i}(\langle p,D\rangle+\langle q,X\rangle)},\qquad\forall\ \begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n},

and (
2.3
) is reformulated as

(2.4)

\left(\rho\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}f\right)(x)=e^{2\pi{\rm i}\langle q,x\rangle+\pi{\rm i}\langle p,q\rangle}f(x+p),\qquad f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}).

A straightforward discussion shows that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\rho\begin{pmatrix}p_{1}\\
q_{1}\end{pmatrix}\rho\begin{pmatrix}p_{2}\\
q_{2}\end{pmatrix}=e^{\pi{\rm i}(\langle p_{1},q_{2}\rangle-\langle q_{1},p_{2}\rangle)}\rho\begin{pmatrix}p_{1}+p_{2}\\
q_{1}+q_{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad p_{1},q_{1},p_{2},q_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

and it can be easily verified that

(2.6)

\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\frac{d}{dt}\rho(t\ell)=\left\langle\ell,Z\right\rangle\rho(t\ell),\qquad\forall\ \ell\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}.

Proposition 2.1
.

For
{\mathcal{B}}\in{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
and
w(\cdot)\in C_{b}^{0}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
,

(2.7)

\left.\partial_{\tau}\rho\left(e^{\tau{\mathcal{B}}}w(t)\right)\right|_{\tau=0}=\left.\partial_{\tau}\rho\left(w(t)+\tau{\mathcal{B}}w(t)\right)\right|_{\tau=0},\qquad\forall\ t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Proof.

For simplicity, let us express
e^{\tau{\mathcal{B}}}w(t)=w(t)+\tau{\mathcal{B}}w(t)+g_{\mathcal{B}}(\tau)w(t)
, where
|g_{\mathcal{B}}(\tau)|=O(\tau^{2})
as
\tau\to 0
. Based on (
2.5
), we obtain

\rho\left(e^{\tau{\mathcal{B}}}w(t)\right)=e^{-\pi{\rm i}\langle w(t)+\tau{\mathcal{B}}w(t),{\mathbb{J}}_{n}g_{\mathcal{B}}(\tau)w(t)\rangle}\rho\left(w(t)+\tau{\mathcal{B}}w(t)\right)\rho(g_{\mathcal{B}}(\tau)w(t)).

Consequently,

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{\tau}\rho\left(e^{\tau{\mathcal{B}}}w(t)\right)\right|_{\tau=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{\tau}\left(e^{-\pi{\rm i}\langle w(t)+s{\mathcal{B}}w(t),{\mathbb{J}}_{n}g_{\mathcal{B}}(\tau)w(t)\rangle}\right)\right|_{\tau=0}\rho(w(t))

\displaystyle+\,\left.e^{-\pi{\rm i}\langle w(t)+\tau{\mathcal{B}}w(t),{\mathbb{J}}_{n}g_{\mathcal{B}}(\tau)w(t)\rangle}\right|_{\tau=0}\left.\partial_{\tau}\rho\left(w(t)+\tau{\mathcal{B}}w(t)\right)\right|_{\tau=0}

\displaystyle+\,\left.e^{-\pi{\rm i}\langle w(t)+\tau{\mathcal{B}}w(t),{\mathbb{J}}_{n}g_{\mathcal{B}}(\tau)w(t)\rangle}\right|_{\tau=0}\rho(w(t))\left.\partial_{\tau}\rho\left(g_{\mathcal{B}}(\tau)w(t)\right)\right|_{\tau=0}

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle P_{1}+P_{2}+P_{3},

where
g_{\mathcal{B}}(0)=g_{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}(0)=0
.
As a result, (
2.7
) is established since
P_{1}=P_{3}=0
,
P_{2}=\left.\partial_{\tau}\rho\left(w(t)+\tau{\mathcal{B}}w(t)\right)\right|_{\tau=0}
.∎

The following proposition can be established through a similar proof.

Proposition 2.2
.

For
w(\cdot)\in C_{b}^{1}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
,

\left.\partial_{\tau}\rho(w(t+\tau))\right|_{\tau=0}=\left.\partial_{\tau}\rho\left(w(t)+\tau w^{\prime}(t)\right)\right|_{\tau=0}
,
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Consider
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. The operator
\rho(w)
is well-defined on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, and consequently, on
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
s\geq 0
, as delineated in (
2.4
). For
s>0
, we extend the definition of
\rho(w)
to
{\mathcal{H}}^{-s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
by employing duality:

(2.8)

\langle\rho(w)u,v\rangle=\langle u,\rho(-w)v\rangle,\qquad u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{-s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),\quad v\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}).

Proposition 2.3
.

For
w(\cdot)\in C_{b}^{1}(\mathbb{R},{\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, it follows that
\partial_{t}\rho(w(t))\in B({\mathcal{H}}^{s},{\mathcal{H}}^{s-1})
for any
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

The proof of this proposition is provided in Appendix
B
.

2.3.
Metaplectic representation

The
symplectic group
, denoted by
{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, comprises
2n\times 2n
real matrices that preserve the symplectic form. Specifically, a matrix
{\mathbb{A}}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&F\end{pmatrix}
belongs to
{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, with
A
,
B
,
C
, and
F
being its four
n\times n
blocks, if and only if the conditions
A^{*}C=C^{*}A
,
B^{*}F=F^{*}B
and
A^{*}F-C^{*}B=I_{n}
are satisfied. Such a matrix
{\mathbb{A}}\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
is referred to as a
symplectic matrix
.

The
symplectic Lie algebra
,
{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, consists of all matrices
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\rm gl}(2n,{\mathbb{R}})
such that
e^{t{\mathcal{A}}}\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
for every
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. In other words,
{\mathcal{A}}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&F\end{pmatrix}
is in
{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, with
A
,
B
,
C
, and
F
as the four
n\times n
blocks of
{\mathcal{A}}
, if and only if the following hold:
F=-A^{*}
,
B=B^{*}
, and
C=C^{*}
. A matrix
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
is known as a
Hamiltonian matrix
.

Let
{\bf Q}
represent the space of real homogeneous quadratic polynomials on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
, equipped with the
Poisson bracket
. Specifically, for smooth functions
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
, the Poisson bracket is defined as

\left\{Q_{1}(\xi,x),Q_{2}(\xi,x)\right\}:=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial Q_{1}}{\partial\xi_{j}}\frac{\partial Q_{2}}{\partial x_{j}}-\frac{\partial Q_{1}}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial Q_{2}}{\partial\xi_{j}}\right).

Recall the definition of the homogeneous quadratic polynomial
{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\xi,x)
given in (
1.22
).

Proposition 2.4
.

(Proposition 4.42 in
[
24
]
) The mapping
{\mathcal{A}}\mapsto{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\xi,x)
constitutes a Lie algebra isomorphism from
{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
to
{\bf Q}
.

The Metaplectic representation
{\mathcal{M}}
of
{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
is defined as follows. Given
\mathbb{A}\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, it induces an automorphism
T_{\mathbb{A}}
of the Heisenberg group
{\bf H_{n}}
by

T_{\mathbb{A}}(p,q,t)=\left(\mathbb{A}\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix},t\right),\qquad p,q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\qquad t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Since the Schrödinger representation
\rho
, defined in (
2.3
), is an irreducible unitary representation of
{\bf H_{n}}
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, the composite
\rho\circ T_{\mathbb{A}}
is also an irreducible unitary representation of
{\bf H_{n}}
, satisfying

(\rho\circ T_{\mathbb{A}})(0,0,t)=e^{2\pi{\rm i}t},\qquad t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

By the Stone-von Neumann theorem, the two irreducible unitary representations
\rho
and
\rho\circ T_{\mathbb{A}}
are equivalent, meaning there exists a unitary operator
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{A})
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
such that

(2.9)

\rho\circ T_{\mathbb{A}}({\mathcal{X}})={\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{A})\rho({\mathcal{X}}){\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{A})^{-1},\qquad{\mathcal{X}}\in{\bf H_{n}}.

As demonstrated in
[
24
]
,
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{A})
can be uniquely chosen, up to factors
\pm 1
, so that
{\mathcal{M}}
becomes a double-valued unitary representation of
{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
. Hence,

(2.10)

{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{A}\mathbb{B})=\pm\,{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{A}){\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{B}),\qquad\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}}),

and, specifically,
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{A}){\mathcal{M}}\left(\mathbb{A}^{-1}\right)=\pm\,{\rm Id}
.
Therefore,
{\mathcal{M}}
maps
{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
into the group of unitary operators on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
modulo
{\pm\,{\rm Id}}
and is referred to as the
Metaplectic representation
of
{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
. In explicit formulations, the ambiguity of
\pm 1
typically manifests as the ambiguity in the sign of a square root. For simplicity, we often omit the sign
\pm 1
.

Given
\mathbb{A}\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, the operator
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{A})
is well-defined on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, and consequently, on
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
for
s\geq 0
, as specified in (
2.9
). For
s>0
, we extend the definition of
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{A})
to
{\mathcal{H}}^{-s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
using duality:

(2.11)

\langle{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{A})u,v\rangle=\langle u,{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{A}^{-1})v\rangle,\qquad u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{-s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),\quad v\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}).

Define the infinitesimal version
d{\mathcal{M}}
of the Metaplectic representation as

(2.12)

d{\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{A}}):=\left.\frac{d}{dt}{\mathcal{M}}\left(e^{t{\mathcal{A}}}\right)\right|_{t=0},\qquad{\mathcal{A}}\in{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}}).

In this context and subsequently, the sign of
{\mathcal{M}}(e^{t{\mathcal{A}}})
is chosen to ensure continuity in
t
and equality to
\rm Id
at
t=0
. Under this framework, the following theorem is established:

Theorem 2.5
.

(Theorem 4.45 in
[
24
]
)
For any
u
in the Schwartz space
{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
,

d{\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{A}})u=2\pi{\rm i}{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(Z)u.

Remark 6
.

The proof of the above theorem shows that for
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
d{\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{A}})=2\pi{\rm i}{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(Z)\in B({\mathcal{H}}^{s},{\mathcal{H}}^{s-2})
.

Consider
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
and
u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}
. Let us denote
\psi(t,x)=({\mathcal{M}}(e^{t{\mathcal{A}}})u)(x)
. Given that
{\mathcal{M}}(e^{(t+\tau){\mathcal{A}}})={\mathcal{M}}(e^{\tau{\mathcal{A}}}){\mathcal{M}}(e^{t{\mathcal{A}}})
for any
t,\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
, a straightforward computation, in conjunction with Theorem
2.5
, leads us to the following observation:

\partial_{t}\psi(t,x)=\lim\limits_{\tau\to 0}\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\psi(t+\tau,x)-\psi(t,x)\right)=\lim\limits_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\tau}\left({\mathcal{M}}(e^{\tau{\mathcal{A}}})-{\rm Id}\right)\psi(t,x)=2\pi{\rm i}{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(Z)\psi(t,x),

which lies in
{\mathcal{H}}^{s-2}
. The aforementioned equality implies that for
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
,

(2.13)

e^{2\pi{\rm i}\tau{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(Z)}={\mathcal{M}}(e^{\tau{\mathcal{A}}}),\qquad\forall\ \tau\in{\mathbb{R}},

thereby establishing a connection between the linear system

(2.14)

z^{\prime}(t)={\mathcal{A}}z(t),\qquad z(0)=z_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n},

and its corresponding Hamiltonian PDE

(2.15)

\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\partial_{t}\psi={\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(Z)\psi(t,x),\qquad\psi(0)=u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}).

It is evident that
z(t)=e^{t{\mathcal{A}}}z_{0}
solves the system (
2.14
), while
\psi(t)={\mathcal{M}}(e^{t{\mathcal{A}}})u
addresses Eq. (
2.15
). Moreover, in light of Theorem 1.2 in
[
47
]
,
\psi(t)
is globally well-posed in
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and exhibits an upper bound for the
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}
-norm that grows exponentially with
t
.

For specific matrices
\mathbb{A}\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, explicit formulas of the Metaplectic representation can be provided:

Theorem 2.6
.

(Theorem 4.51 & 4.53 in
[
24
]
)
Let
\mathbb{A}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&F\end{pmatrix}\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
and
u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}
,
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

(i)

If
\mathrm{det}\ A\neq 0
, then
\displaystyle({\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{A})u)(x)=(\mathrm{det}\ A)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{2\pi{\rm i}S(\xi,x)}\hat{u}(\xi)\,d\xi
,
where

S(\xi,x):=-\frac{1}{2}\langle x,CA^{-1}x\rangle+\langle\xi,A^{-1}x\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\xi,A^{-1}B\xi\rangle.

(ii)

If
\mathrm{det}\ B\neq 0
, then

\displaystyle({\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{A})u)(x)={\rm i}^{\frac{n}{2}}(\mathrm{det}\ B)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{2\pi{\rm i}S^{\prime}(x,y)}u(y)\,dy
,
where

S^{\prime}(x,y)=-\frac{1}{2}\langle x,FB^{-1}x\rangle+\langle y,B^{-1}x\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle y,B^{-1}Ay\rangle.

Remark 7
.

According to
[
24
]
, the aforementioned formulas are valid when
u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Consequently, they also apply to
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
for
s\geq 0
. Furthermore, by utilizing the definition by duality as presented in (
2.11
), it can be demonstrated that these formulas remain applicable for
u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{-s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, with
s\geq 0
.

The oscillatory integrals presented in Theorem
2.6
do not provide an explicit description for arbitrary
\mathbb{A}\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
. This is due to the prevalence of
2n\times 2n
symplectic matrices whose
n\times n
blocks are all singular. This issue will be further explored in Section
3
.

3.
Bounds of Sobolev norms under the representations

It is established that Eq. (
2.15
) is globally well-posed in
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
for
s\geq 0
. To achieve a more quantitative description of the
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}
-norm, which is the primary objective of this paper, we provide both upper and lower bounds of Sobolev norms. These bounds are determined under the application of the Metaplectic representation for any
\mathbb{A}\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
and the Schrödinger representation for any
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
.

3.1.
Bounds of Sobolev norms under Metaplectic representation

Recall from (
2.13
) that for
{\mathcal{B}}(\cdot)\in C_{b}^{0}({\mathbb{R}},{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}}))
and
|\tau|\leq 1
,
e^{\tau{\mathcal{B}}(\cdot)}\in C_{b}^{0}({\mathbb{R}},{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}}))
and satisfies
{\mathcal{M}}(e^{\tau{\mathcal{B}}(t)})=e^{2\pi{\rm i}\tau{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{B}}(t)}}
for every
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. According to Lemma 2.8 in
[
5
]
, there exist constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}>0
, dependent on
s\geq 0
and the uniform bounds of
{\mathcal{B}}(\cdot)
, ensuring that

(3.1)

c_{1}\|u\|_{s}\leq\|e^{2\pi{\rm i}\tau{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{B}}(t)}}u\|_{s}\leq c_{2}\|u\|_{s},\qquad u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s},\qquad t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

This estimate is particularly effective when the
L^{2}-
unitary transformation is of the exponential form, especially for near-identity transformations. However, as noted by Djoković
[
17
]
, not all elements in
C_{b}^{0}({\mathbb{R}},{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}}))
are encompassed in (
3.1
). Specifically, for a connected Lie group
G
, its Lie algebra
g
, and the exponential map
\exp:g\rightarrow G
of
G
, the
index
of an element
a\in G
, denoted by
{\rm ind}(a)
, is the smallest positive integer
m
such that
a^{m}
lies in the image of
\exp
.

Lemma 3.1
.

(Djoković
[
17
]
) The image of
{\rm ind}
from
{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
to
{\mathbb{Z}}
is
\{1,2\}
. In other words, for any
{\mathbb{B}}\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, there exists
\mathcal{B}\in{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
such that
{\mathbb{B}}^{2}=e^{\mathcal{B}}
.

Considering Lemma
3.1
, for
\mathbb{A}(\cdot)\in C_{b}^{0}({\mathbb{R}},{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}}))
, we cannot assure the existence of
{\mathcal{B}}(\cdot)\in C_{b}^{0}({\mathbb{R}},{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}}))
such that
\mathbb{A}(\cdot)=e^{{\mathcal{B}}(\cdot)}
.
To extend (
3.1
), we propose the following proposition, which reveals that only uniform boundedness is required, and even continuity is not a necessity.

Proposition 3.2
.

For
{\mathbb{A}}\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, for any
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

(3.2)

\|{\mathbb{A}}\|^{-|s|}\|u\|_{s}\lesssim\|{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{s}\lesssim\|{\mathbb{A}}\|^{|s|}\|u\|_{s},\qquad\forall\ u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}.

Furthermore, for
s>0
and
u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}\setminus\{0\}
, it holds that

(3.3)

\|{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{s}\gtrsim_{u}\|{\mathbb{A}}\|^{s}.

The estimate (
3.2
) is crucial for the
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}-
estimates of the
L^{2}-
unitary transformations in the reducibility argument, demonstrating the
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}-
equivalence between the original time-dependent equation and the reduced equation with a constant homogeneous part. Conversely, the lower bound (
3.3
) is applied to the time propagator of the homogeneous quadratic Hamiltonian PDE via (
2.13
), essentially contributing to the growth of the Sobolev norm for the solution to the reduced Hamiltonian PDE. More specifically, we arrive at the following corollary.

Corollary 3.3
.

Given
U\in C_{b}^{0}({\mathbb{R}},{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}}))
,
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, we have, for
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

(i)

for
u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}
,
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\|U\|_{C_{b}^{0}}^{-|s|}\|u\|_{s}\lesssim\|{\mathcal{M}}(U(t))u\|_{s},\ \|{\mathcal{M}}(U(t)^{-1})u\|_{s}\lesssim\|U\|_{C_{b}^{0}}^{|s|}\|u\|_{s}
,

(ii)

for
u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}\setminus\{0\}
,
s>0
,
\|e^{t{\mathcal{A}}}\|^{s}\lesssim_{u}\|{\mathcal{M}}(e^{t{\mathcal{A}}})u\|_{s}\lesssim\|e^{t{\mathcal{A}}}\|^{s}\|u\|_{s}
.

The remainder of this subsection is dedicated to the proof of Proposition
3.2
.

3.1.1.
Bounds for symplectic matrix with diagonal block form

Lemma 3.4
.

For
{\mathbb{A}}=\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&A^{*-1}\end{pmatrix}\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, we have, for any
s>0
,

(3.4)

\|{\mathbb{A}}\|^{-s}\|u\|_{s}\lesssim\|{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{A})u\|_{s}\lesssim\|{\mathbb{A}}\|^{s}\|u\|_{s},\quad\forall\ u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}.

Moreover, if
A={\rm diag}\{\zeta_{0},\cdots,\zeta_{n-1}\}
, then, for
u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}\setminus\{0\}
,

(3.5)

\|{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{A})u\|_{s}\gtrsim\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(|\zeta_{j-1}|^{s}\|X_{j}^{s}u\|_{L^{2}}+|\zeta_{j-1}|^{-s}\|X_{j}^{s}\hat{u}\|_{L^{2}}\right).

Proof.

Since
{\mathbb{A}}=\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&A^{*-1}\end{pmatrix}\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
implies that
A
is non-singular, by Theorem
2.6
-(i), we have

\left({\mathcal{M}}\left({\mathbb{A}}\right)u\right)(x)=(\mathrm{det}\ A)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{2\pi{\rm i}\langle\xi,A^{-1}x\rangle}\hat{u}(\xi)d\xi=(\mathrm{det}\ A)^{-\frac{1}{2}}u\left(A^{-1}x\right),\qquad u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s},\quad s>0.

A straightforward computation on the expression of solution implies that

(3.6)

\displaystyle\||X|^{s}{\mathcal{M}}\left({\mathbb{A}}\right)u\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\mathrm{det}\ A|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|x|^{2s}\left|u\left(A^{-1}x\right)\right|^{2}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\mathrm{det}\ A|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|Ay|^{2s}|u(y)|^{2}\,d\left(Ay\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\|A\|^{s}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|y|^{2s}|u(y)|^{2}dy\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and, if
A={\rm diag}\{\zeta_{0},\cdots,\zeta_{n-1}\}
, then

(3.7)

\||X|^{s}{\mathcal{M}}\left({\mathbb{A}}\right)u\|_{L^{2}}=\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\zeta^{2}_{j-1}y^{2}_{j}\right)^{s}|u(y)|^{2}\,dy\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\gtrsim\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\zeta_{j-1}|^{s}\|X_{j}^{s}u\|_{L^{2}}.

On the other hand, since

\left({\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{M}}\left({\mathbb{A}}\right)u\right)(\xi)=(\mathrm{det}\ A)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\left\langle A^{*}\xi,A^{-1}x\right\rangle}u\left(A^{-1}x\right)dx=|\mathrm{det}\ A|^{\frac{1}{2}}\hat{u}\left(A^{*}\xi\right),

it follows that

(3.8)

\left\||X|^{s}{\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{M}}\left({\mathbb{A}}\right)u\right\|_{L^{2}}=\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left|A^{*-1}\eta\right|^{2s}|\hat{u}(\eta)|^{2}d\eta\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\|A^{-1}\|^{s}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left|\eta\right|^{2s}|\hat{u}(\eta)|^{2}d\eta\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and, if
A={\rm diag}\{\zeta_{0},\cdots,\zeta_{n-1}\}
, then

(3.9)

\left\||X|^{s}{\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{M}}\left({\mathbb{A}}\right)u\right\|_{L^{2}}=\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\zeta^{-2}_{j-1}\eta^{2}_{j}\right)^{s}|\hat{u}(\eta)|^{2}\,d\eta\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\gtrsim\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\zeta_{j-1}|^{-s}\|X_{j}^{s}\hat{u}\|_{L^{2}}.

Combining (
3.6
) and (
3.8
), we obtain

\left\|{\mathcal{M}}\left({\mathbb{A}}\right)u\right\|_{s}\lesssim\left(\|A\|^{s}+\|A^{-1}\|^{s}\right)\|u\|_{s}\lesssim\|{\mathbb{A}}\|^{s}\|u\|_{s}.

The property (
2.10
) implies that
{\mathcal{M}}\left({\mathbb{A}}^{-1}\right){\mathcal{M}}\left({\mathbb{A}}\right)={\rm Id}
. Then, noting that
\|{\mathbb{A}}^{-1}\|=\|{\mathbb{A}}\|
, we have

(3.10)

\|u\|_{s}=\left\|{\mathcal{M}}\left({\mathbb{A}}^{-1}\right){\mathcal{M}}\left({\mathbb{A}}\right)u\right\|_{s}\lesssim\|{\mathbb{A}}\|^{s}\left\|{\mathcal{M}}\left({\mathbb{A}}\right)u\right\|_{s},

which implies that
\left\|{\mathcal{M}}\left({\mathbb{A}}\right)u\right\|_{s}\gtrsim\|{\mathbb{A}}\|^{-s}\|u\|_{s}
. Moreover, if
A={\rm diag}\{\zeta_{0},\cdots,\zeta_{n-1}\}
, then, combining (
3.7
) and (
3.9
), the lower bound (
3.5
) is established.∎

3.1.2.
Elementary symplectic (ES) matrices

To extend the application of Lemma
3.4
to any symplectic matrix, we introduce the concept of elementary symplectic matrices within
{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
.

A matrix
{\mathbb{A}}\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
is termed
elementary symplectic
, abbreviated as
ES
for brevity, if it falls into one of six specific categories. These categories are collectively denoted by
{\mathbb{A}}\in{\bf ES}:=\bigcup_{{\bf k}={\bf 1}}^{\bf 6}{\bf ES(k)}
.

ES(1).

{\mathbb{A}}={\mathcal{J}}_{k}
or
{\mathcal{J}}_{k}^{-1}
,
k=1,\cdots,n
, where

{\mathcal{J}}_{k}:=({\bf e}_{1},\cdots,{\bf e}_{k-1},\underset{\underset{k^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{-{\bf e}_{n+k}},{\bf e}_{k+1},\cdots,{\bf e}_{n},{\bf e}_{n+1},\cdots,{\bf e}_{n+k-1},\underset{\underset{(n+k)^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{{\bf e}_{k}},{\bf e}_{n+k+1},\cdots,{\bf e}_{2n}).

Note that, for
S\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
expressed by column vectors, i.e.,
S=(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n},\eta_{1},\cdots,\eta_{n})
,

S{\mathcal{J}}_{k}=(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{k-1},\underset{\underset{k^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{-\eta_{k}},\xi_{k+1},\cdots,\xi_{n},\eta_{1},\cdots,\eta_{k-1},\underset{\underset{(n+k)^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{\xi_{k}},\eta_{k+1},\cdots,\eta_{n}).

while for
S\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
expressed by row vectors, i.e.,
S=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{n})^{*}
,

{\mathcal{J}}_{k}S=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{k-1},\underset{\underset{k^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{\beta_{k}},\alpha_{k+1},\cdots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{k-1},\underset{\underset{(n+k)^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{-\alpha_{k}},\beta_{k+1},\cdots,\beta_{n})^{*}.

ES(2).

For
n\geq 2
,
{\mathbb{A}}={\mathbb{B}}_{jk}
for
1\leq j<k\leq n
, where

{\mathbb{B}}_{jk}:=({\bf e}_{1},\cdots,\underset{\underset{j^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{{\bf e}_{k}},\cdots,\underset{\underset{k^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{{\bf e}_{j}},\cdots,\underset{\underset{(n+j)^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{{\bf e}_{n+k}},\cdots,\underset{\underset{(n+k)^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{{\bf e}_{n+j}}\cdots,{\bf e}_{2n}).

Note that, for
S\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
,
S{\mathbb{B}}_{jk}
means the exchanges between
j^{\rm th}
,
k^{\rm th}
columns and between
(n+j)^{\rm th}
,
(n+k)^{\rm th}
columns, i.e., for
S=(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n},\eta_{1},\cdots,\eta_{n})
,

S{\mathbb{B}}_{jk}=(\xi_{1},\cdots,\underset{\underset{j^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{\xi_{k}},\cdots,\underset{\underset{k^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{\xi_{j}},\cdots,\xi_{n},\eta_{1},\cdots,\underset{\underset{(n+j)^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{\eta_{k}},\cdots,\underset{\underset{(n+k)^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{\eta_{j}},\cdots,\eta_{n}),

while
{\mathbb{B}}_{jk}S
means the exchanges between
j^{\rm th}
,
k^{\rm th}
rows and between
(n+j)^{\rm th}-
row and
(n+k)^{\rm th}-
rows, i.e., for
S=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{n})^{*}
,

{\mathbb{B}}_{jk}S=:(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\underset{\underset{j^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{\alpha_{k}},\cdots,\underset{\underset{k^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{\alpha_{j}},\cdots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\cdots,\underset{\underset{(n+j)^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{\beta_{k}},\cdots,\underset{\underset{(n+k)^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{\beta_{j}},\cdots,\beta_{n})^{*}.

ES(3).

For
n\geq 2
,
{\mathbb{A}}=\mathbb{E}_{q,j}
for
2\leq j\leq n
and
|q|\leq 1
, where

\mathbb{E}_{q,j}:=({\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{2},\cdots,\underset{\underset{j^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{{\bf e}_{j}+q{\bf e}_{1}},\cdots,{\bf e}_{n},\underset{\underset{(n+1)^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{{\bf e}_{n+1}-q{\bf e}_{n+j}},\cdots,{\bf e}_{2n}).

Note that, for
S=(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n},\eta_{1},\cdots,\eta_{n})\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, we have

S\mathbb{E}_{q,j}=(\xi_{1},\cdots,\underset{\underset{j^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{\xi_{j}+q\xi_{1}},\cdots,\xi_{n},\underset{\underset{(n+1)^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{\eta_{1}-q\eta_{j}},\cdots,\eta_{j},\cdots,\eta_{n}).

ES(4).

For
n\geq 2
,
{\mathbb{A}}=\mathbb{F}_{q,j}
for
2\leq j\leq n
and
|q|\leq 1
, where

\mathbb{F}_{q,j}:=({\bf e}^{*}_{1},\cdots,\underset{\underset{j^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{{\bf e}^{*}_{j}+q{\bf e}^{*}_{1}},\cdots,{\bf e}^{*}_{n},\underset{\underset{(n+1)^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{{\bf e}^{*}_{n+1}-q{\bf e}^{*}_{n+j}},\cdots,{\bf e}^{*}_{n+j},\cdots,{\bf e}^{*}_{2n})^{*}.

Note that, for
S=(\alpha^{*}_{1},\cdots,\alpha^{*}_{n},\beta^{*}_{1},\cdots,\beta^{*}_{n})^{*}
, we have

\mathbb{F}_{q,j}S=(\alpha^{*}_{1},\cdots,\underset{\underset{j^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{\alpha^{*}_{j}+q\alpha^{*}_{1}},\cdots,\alpha^{*}_{n},\underset{\underset{(n+1)^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{\beta^{*}_{1}-q\beta^{*}_{j}},\cdots,\beta^{*}_{j},\dots,\beta^{*}_{n})^{*}.

ES(5).

For
{\mathbb{A}}=\mathbb{T}_{\ell}
for
1\leq j\leq n
and
\ell\in[-n,n]\times[-1,1]^{n-1}
(or
[-1,1]
for
n=1
), where

\mathbb{T}_{\ell}:=({\bf e}_{1},\dots,{\bf e}_{n},{\bf e}_{n+1}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\ell_{j}{\bf e}_{j},{\bf e}_{n+2}+\ell_{2}{\bf e}_{1},\cdots,{\bf e}_{2n}+\ell_{n}{\bf e}_{1}).

Note that, for
S=(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n},\eta_{1},\cdots,\eta_{n})\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, we have

S\mathbb{T}_{\ell}=(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n},\eta_{1}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\ell_{j}\xi_{j},\eta_{2}+\ell_{2}\xi_{1},\cdots,\eta_{n}+\ell_{n}\xi_{1}).

ES(6).

{\mathbb{A}}=\mathbb{S}_{\ell}
for
\ell\in[-n,n]\times[-1,1]^{n-1}
, where

\mathbb{S}_{\ell}:=({\bf e}^{*}_{1},\cdots,{\bf e}^{*}_{n},{\bf e}^{*}_{n+1}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\ell_{j}{\bf e}^{*}_{j},{\bf e}^{*}_{n+2}+\ell_{2}{\bf e}^{*}_{1},\cdots,{\bf e}^{*}_{2n}+\ell_{n}{\bf e}^{*}_{1})^{*}.

Note that, for
S=(\alpha^{*}_{1},\cdots,\alpha^{*}_{n},\beta^{*}_{1},\cdots,\beta^{*}_{n})^{*}
, we have

\mathbb{S}_{\ell}S=(\alpha^{*}_{1},\cdots,\alpha^{*}_{n},\beta^{*}_{1}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\ell_{j}\alpha^{*}_{j},\beta^{*}_{2}+\ell_{2}\alpha^{*}_{1},\cdots,\beta^{*}_{n}+\ell_{n}\alpha^{*}_{1})^{*}.

For
{\bf k}={\bf 1},\cdots,{\bf 6}
, it is easy to verify that
{\mathbb{A}}^{-1}\in{\bf ES(k)}
if
{\mathbb{A}}\in{\bf ES(k)}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For
{\mathbb{A}}\in{\bf ES(k)}
,
{\bf k}={\bf 1},\cdots,{\bf 6}
, for
u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}\setminus\{0\}
,
s>0
, we have

(3.11)

\left\|{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\right\|_{s}\simeq\left\|u\right\|_{s},\qquad\|X^{s}_{j}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{L^{2}},\ \|X^{s}_{j}{\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{L^{2}}\gtrsim_{u}1,\quad j=1,\cdots,n.

Proof.

Let us show (
3.11
) for every type of
ES
matrix.

For
{\mathbb{A}}={\mathcal{J}}_{k}
or
{\mathcal{J}}_{k}^{-1}\in{\bf ES(1)}
, in view of (
2.2
), it is sufficient to show
{\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{J}}_{k})={\rm i}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{F}_{k}^{-1}
. Without loss of generality, let us suppose
k=1
.
For
J_{1}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc:cccc}&&&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&1&&&\\
&&&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&&0&&\\
&&&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&&&\ddots&\\
&&&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&&&&0\\
\hline\cr-1&&&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&&&&\\
&0&&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&&&&\\
&&\ddots&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&&&&\\
&&&0\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&&&&\end{array}\right)\in{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, noting that

e^{tJ_{1}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc:cccc}\cos(t)&&&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&\sin(t)&&&\\
&1&&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&&0&&\\
&&\ddots&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&&&\ddots&\\
&&&1\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&&&&0\\
\hline\cr-\sin(t)&&&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&\cos(t)&&&\\
&0&&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&&1&&\\
&&\ddots&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&&&\ddots&\\
&&&0\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&&&&1\end{array}\right)\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}}),

and especially,
e^{\frac{\pi}{2}J_{1}}={\mathcal{J}}_{1}
, we have, by Theorem
2.5
, that

d{\mathcal{M}}(J_{1})u=2\pi{\rm i}{\mathcal{Q}}_{J_{1}}(Z)u=\pi{\rm i}(D_{1}^{2}+X_{1}^{2})u,\qquad u\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}).

Then, for
h_{\alpha}(x)=h_{\alpha_{1}}(x_{1})\cdots h_{\alpha_{n}}(x_{n})
, with
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{n})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
and
h_{k}
the
k-
th Hermite function,

d{\mathcal{M}}(J_{1})h_{\alpha}=\pi{\rm i}(D_{1}^{2}+X_{1}^{2})h_{\alpha_{1}}(x_{1})...h_{\alpha_{n}}(x_{n})=\frac{\rm i}{2}(2\alpha_{1}+1)h_{\alpha}.

Since
v_{\alpha}(t,\cdot):=\left({\mathcal{M}}(e^{tJ_{1}}\right)h_{\alpha})(\cdot)
solves the equation
\partial_{t}v(t,\cdot)=d{\mathcal{M}}(J_{1})v(t,\cdot)
with
v(0,\cdot)=h_{\alpha}(\cdot)
,
we have that
{\mathcal{M}}(e^{tJ_{1}})h_{\alpha}=e^{td{\mathcal{M}}(J_{1})}h_{\alpha}=e^{\frac{{\rm i}t}{2}(2\alpha_{1}+1)}h_{\alpha}
, and hence, for
t=\frac{\pi}{2}
, for
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
,
{\mathcal{M}}\left({\mathcal{J}}_{1}\right)h_{\alpha}={\rm i}^{\alpha_{1}+\frac{1}{2}}h_{\alpha}
.
On the other hand, with
\mathcal{F}h_{0}=h_{0}
and by the recurrence formula (
2.1
),

\mathcal{F}h_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi^{k}}{k!}}\mathcal{F}(X-{\rm i}D)^{k}h_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi^{k}}{k!}}(-D-{\rm i}X)^{k}\mathcal{F}h_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi^{k}}{k!}}(-{\rm i})^{k}(-{\rm i}D+X)^{k}h_{0}=(-{\rm i})^{k}h_{k}.

Thus, for every
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
,
\mathcal{F}_{1}^{-1}h_{\alpha}={\rm i}^{\alpha_{1}}h_{\alpha}={\rm i}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{J}}_{1})h_{\alpha}
. Since
\{h_{\alpha}\}
is a complete orthonormal set, this establishes
{\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{J}}_{1})={\rm i}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{-1}
.

For
{\mathbb{A}}={\mathbb{B}}_{jk}\in{\bf ES(2)}
,
1\leq j<k\leq n
, we have that

({\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u)(x)=u(x_{1},\cdots,\underset{\underset{j^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{x_{k}},\cdots,\underset{\underset{k^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{x_{j}},\cdots,x_{n}),\quad({\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u)(\xi)=\hat{u}(\xi_{1},\cdots,\underset{\underset{j^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{\xi_{k}},\cdots,\underset{\underset{k^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{\xi_{j}},\cdots,\xi_{n}).

Hence,
\|X^{s}_{l}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{L^{2}}=\|X^{s}_{l}u\|_{L^{2}}
,
\|X^{s}_{l}{\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{L^{2}}=\|X^{s}_{l}{\mathcal{F}}u\|_{L^{2}}
for
l\neq k,j
, and

\|X^{s}_{j}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{L^{2}}=\|X^{s}_{k}u\|_{L^{2}},\quad\|X^{s}_{k}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{L^{2}}=\|X^{s}_{j}u\|_{L^{2}},

\|X^{s}_{j}{\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{L^{2}}=\|X^{s}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}u\|_{L^{2}},\quad\|X^{s}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{L^{2}}=\|X^{s}_{j}{\mathcal{F}}u\|_{L^{2}},

which implies (
3.11
).

For
{\mathbb{A}}=\mathbb{E}_{q,j}\in{\bf ES(3)}
, we see that
\|{\mathbb{A}}\|\simeq 1
since
-1\leq q\leq 1
. Noting that
{\mathbb{A}}
is of diagonal block form, in view of Lemma
3.4
, we have
\left\|{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\right\|_{s}\simeq\left\|u\right\|_{s}
for
u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}
.
A direct computation shows that

({\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u)(x)=u(x_{1}-qx_{j},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n}),\quad({\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u)(\xi)=\hat{u}(\xi_{1},\cdots,\underset{\underset{j^{\rm th}}{\uparrow}}{\xi_{j}-q\xi_{1}},\cdots,\xi_{n}),\quad u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}.

Hence,
\|X^{s}_{l}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{L^{2}}=\|X^{s}_{l}u\|_{L^{2}}
for
l\neq 1
,
\|X^{s}_{l}{\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{L^{2}}=\|X^{s}_{l}{\mathcal{F}}u\|_{L^{2}}
for
l\neq j
. Moreover,

\|X^{s}_{1}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{L^{2}}=\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|x_{1}|^{2s}|u(x_{1}-qx_{j},\cdots,x_{n})|^{2}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|y_{1}+qy_{j}|^{2s}|u(y)|^{2}dy\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=:F_{u}(q).

Since the function
F_{u}
is non-vanishing and continuous on
[-1,1]
provided that
u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}\setminus\{0\}
, we see that
F_{u}(q)\gtrsim_{u}1
. Similarly,
\|X^{s}_{j}{\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{L^{2}}\gtrsim_{u}1
, which completes the proof of (
3.11
) for
{\mathbb{A}}\in{\bf ES(3)}
. The proof is similar for
{\mathbb{A}}\in{\bf ES(4)}
.

For
{\mathbb{A}}=\mathbb{T}_{\ell}\in{\bf ES(5)}
, according to Theorem
2.6
-(i), we have

({\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u)(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{2\pi{\rm i}(\langle\xi,x\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\xi,B_{\ell}\xi\rangle)}\hat{u}(\xi)\,d\xi,\quad B_{\ell}:=\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\cdots&\ell_{n}\\
\ell_{2}&0&&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\
\ell_{n}&&&0\end{pmatrix}.

Then
({\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u)(\xi)=e^{\pi{\rm i}\langle\xi,B_{\ell}\xi\rangle}\hat{u}(\xi)
. For
j=1,\cdots,n
, we have
\|X_{j}^{s}{\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{L^{2}}=\|X_{j}^{s}{\mathcal{F}}u\|_{L^{2}}
,

\|X_{j}^{s}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{L^{2}}=\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|x_{j}|^{2s}\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{2\pi{\rm i}(\langle\xi,x\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\xi,B_{\ell}\xi\rangle)}\hat{u}(\xi)\,d\xi\right|^{2}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=:G_{j,u}(\ell).

Since the function
G_{j,u}
is non-vanishing and continuous on
[-n,n]\times[-1,1]^{n-1}
provided that
u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{s}\setminus\{0\}
, we see that
G_{j,u}(\ell)\gtrsim_{u}1
. Moreover, for
s\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
, we have

\|X_{j}^{s}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{L^{2}}=\|D_{j}^{s}{\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{A}})u\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\sum_{k=0}^{s}\sup_{|\beta|=s-k}\|\langle X\rangle^{k}D^{\beta}\hat{u}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|u\|_{s}.

By interpolation and (
3.10
), we obtain (
3.11
) for
{\mathbb{A}}\in{\bf ES(5)}
. The proof is similar for
{\bf ES(6)}
.∎

3.1.3.
Proof of (
3.2
) for
s>0
.

The subsequent proposition demonstrates that Lemma
3.4
can always be applied, up to a finite number of
ES
transformations. By combining this with Lemma
3.5
, we can establish the estimates (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) presented in Proposition
3.2
for the case where
s>0
.

Proposition 3.6
.

(
ES-digonalization of symplectic matrix)
For given
{\mathbb{A}}\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, there exist
\zeta_{0},\zeta_{1},\cdots,\zeta_{n-1}\in{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying

(3.12)

|\zeta_{0}|\simeq\|{\mathbb{A}}\|,\qquad 1\leq|\zeta_{j}|\leq 4^{j}\left(\frac{n!}{(n-j)!}\right)^{2}\|{\mathbb{A}}\|,\quad j=0,\cdots,n-1,

such that, through at most
(2n^{2}+6n)

ES
transformations,
L_{1},\cdots L_{k_{L}},R_{1},\cdots R_{k_{R}}\in{\bf ES}
,

(3.13)

Appendix C
Heuristic discussion from Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula

We will provide a heuristic proof for the explicit expressions of the solution in (
6.14
) and (
6.20
). For
{\mathcal{R}}^{(n)}_{k}={\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{B}}^{(n)}_{k}}+{\mathcal{L}}_{l_{k}^{(n)}}
,
k=4,5
, the challenge in deriving the expression for the propagator
e^{2\pi{\rm i}t{\mathcal{R}}^{(n)}_{k}}
comes from the non-commutativity between the homogeneous part
{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{B}}^{(n)}_{k}}
and the linear part
{\mathcal{L}}_{l_{k}^{(n)}}
. This non-commutativity prevents the direct application of the Metaplectic and Schrödinger representations separately. The primary approach is to express the propagator as
\exp\{2\pi{\rm i}t{\mathcal{R}}^{(n)}_{k}\}=\exp\{2\pi{\rm i}t{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{B}}^{(n)}_{k}}\}\exp\{2\pi{\rm i}t{\mathcal{L}}_{\tilde{l}_{k}}\}
with an appropriately chosen
\tilde{l}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. This
\tilde{l}_{k}
is determined using the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff (B-C-H) formula.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a Lie group with its associated Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. Consider the exponential map
\exp:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow{\mathcal{G}}
. For elements
Q,L\in\mathfrak{g}
, we denote
G=\ln(\exp\{Q\}\exp\{L\})
, where the logarithmic function is defined such that

\exp\{Q\}\exp\{L\}=\exp\{G\}
. This can also be expressed using the formal series
\ln(1+z):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}z^{k}
.

Proposition C.1
.

(B-C-H formula
[
26
]
) For
Q,L\in\mathfrak{g}
, the formal series
G=\ln(\exp\{Q\}\exp\{L\})
is a Lie series generated by
\{Q,L\}
. This means that
G
can be expressed as a series
Q+L+\cdots
, where all terms of order
\geq 2
can be represented as linear combinations of iterated commutators
[\cdot,\cdot]
, known as Lie elements. More precisely,
G
has the explicit series representation:

(C.1)

G=\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{p_{i}+q_{i}>0}\frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{m}\frac{[\overbrace{Q,\cdots,Q}^{p_{1}\ {\rm times}},\overbrace{L,\cdots,L}^{q_{1}\ {\rm times}},\cdots,\overbrace{Q,\cdots,Q}^{p_{m}\ {\rm times}},\overbrace{L,\cdots,L}^{q_{m}\ {\rm times}}]}{p_{1}!q_{1}!\cdots p_{m}!q_{m}!\sum_{i}(p_{i}+q_{i})},

where
[X,Y]={\rm ad}_{X}Y:=XY-YX
denotes the commutator of
X
and
Y
, and
[X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{n}]:=[X_{1},[X_{2},\cdots,[X_{n-1},X_{n}]\cdots]]
following the right-normed convention.

For the two non-commuting operators
Q=2\pi{\rm i}t{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(Z)
and
L=2\pi{\rm i}{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell_{0}(t)}(Z)
, where
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
is nilpotent and
\ell_{0}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
is to be determined, we observe that
\exp\{Q\}={\mathcal{M}}(e^{t{\mathcal{A}}})
and
\exp\{L\}=\rho(\ell_{0}(t))

based on the identities in (
2.13
) and (
2.6
).
We aim to demonstrate that the formal series (
C.1
) with
Q
and
L
specified above actually terminates after a finite number of terms. The finiteness of this series has also been established in Lemma 7 of
[
11
]
.

It is evident that all Lie elements resulting from
Q
and
L
are pseudo-differential operators of order less than
2
, given that
Q
is quadratic and
L
is linear with respect to the operators
(D,X)
.

Lemma C.2
.

For any
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
and
\ell_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
, we have
{\rm ad}^{m}_{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathcal{A}}(Z)}({\mathcal{L}}_{\ell_{0}}(Z))=(2\pi{\rm i})^{-m}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{m}\ell_{0}}(Z)
.

Proof.

By a straightforward computation, we have, for
1\leq j,k,l\leq n
,

\displaystyle[D_{j}D_{k},X_{l}]=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}(\delta_{kl}D_{j}+\delta_{jl}D_{k}),\qquad[X_{j}D_{k},X_{l}]=[D_{k}X_{j},X_{l}]=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\delta_{kl}X_{j},

\displaystyle[X_{j}D_{k},D_{l}]=[D_{k}X_{j},D_{l}]=-\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\delta_{jl}D_{k},\qquad[X_{j}X_{k},D_{l}]=-\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}(\delta_{kl}X_{j}+\delta_{jl}X_{k}),

which follows that, for
\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&F\end{pmatrix}\in{\rm gl}(2n,{\mathbb{R}})
and
\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
,

\left[\left\langle Z,\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&F\end{pmatrix}Z\right\rangle,\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\end{pmatrix},Z\right\rangle\right]=\frac{1}{\pi{\rm i}}\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&F\end{pmatrix}{\mathbb{J}}_{n}\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\end{pmatrix},Z\right\rangle.

Hence, for
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
and
\ell_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}

{\rm ad}_{{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(Z)}({\mathcal{L}}_{\ell_{0}}(Z))=-\frac{1}{2}[\langle Z,{\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{J}}_{n}Z\rangle,\langle\ell_{0},Z\rangle]=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{A}}\ell_{0}}(Z).

More generally, for
m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
, we have

{\rm ad}^{m}_{{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(Z)}({\mathcal{L}}_{\ell_{0}}(Z))=\frac{1}{(2\pi{\rm i})^{m}}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{m}\ell_{0}}(Z).\qed

Given that
Q=2\pi{\rm i}t{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(Z)
is a pseudo-differential operator of order
2
and

of order

•

(No

The logarithm of matrix

For any complex matrix
M\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
, finding the solution
X\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
to the matrix equation
M=e^{X}
, known as a logarithm of
M
, is an intriguing problem. Notably, the logarithm of a matrix is not uniquely determined, a phenomenon that persists even within the realm of real matrices. However, a particular type of logarithm, termed the principal logarithm, is defined under certain conditions.

Lemma D.1
.

[Principal logarithm, Theorem 1.31 and 11.1 of
[
35
]
]
For
M\in{\rm GL}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
, if
M
has no real negative eigenvalues, then there is a unique logarithm, denoted by
\ln(M)
and called the principle logarithm of
M
, all of whose eigenvalues lie in the strip
\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:-\pi<\Im z<\pi\}
. If
M
is a real matrix, so is
\ln(M)
. Besides,

\displaystyle\ln(M)=(M-I_{n})\int_{0}^{1}(s(M-I_{n})+I_{n})^{-1}ds
.

Furthermore, if
M\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, then we have

Lemma D.2
.

[Logarithm of symplectic matrices, Lemma 5.2.2 of
[
52
]
]
For
M\in{\rm Sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, if
M
has no real negative eigenvalues, then
M
has a real logarithm
X\in{\rm sp}(n,{\mathbb{R}})
.

Remark 21
.

In the detailed proof of Lemma
D.2
, it is demonstrated that the eigenvalues of the logarithm
X
are situated within the strip
\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:-\pi<\Im(z)<\pi\}
. By virtue of the uniqueness property of the principal logarithm, this logarithm
X
is identified as the principal logarithm of the matrix
M
.
