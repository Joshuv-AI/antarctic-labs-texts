Characterizing low-frequency qubit noise
Filip Wudarski, Yaxing Zhang, Alexander Korotkov, A. G. Petukhov, M. I. Dykman

Abstract
Fluctuations of the qubit frequencies are one of the major problems to overcome on the way to scalable quantum computers. Of particular importance are fluctuations with the correlation time that exceeds the decoherence time due to decay and dephasing by fast processes. The statistics of the fluctuations can be characterized by measuring the correlators of the outcomes of periodically repeated Ramsey measurements. This work suggests a method that allows describing qubit dynamics during repeated measurements in the presence of evolving noise. It made it possible, in particular, to evaluate the two-time correlator for the noise from two-level systems and obtain two- and three-time correlators for a Gaussian noise. The explicit expressions for the correlators are compared with simulations. A significant difference of the three-time correlators for the noise from two-level systems and for a Gaussian noise is demonstrated. Strong broadening of the distribution of the outcomes of Ramsey measurements, with a possible fine structure, is found for the data acquisition time comparable to the noise correlation time.



I
Introduction

Decoherence, and in particular fluctuations of qubit frequencies, are one of the major obstacles faced by quantum computing. Understanding the mechanisms of these fluctuations has been attracting much attention. To this end, much work has focused on the analysis of the fluctuation spectra, cf.
Álvarez and Suter 2011
;
Bylander et al. 2011
;
Sank et al. 2012
;
Yan et al. 2012
;
Paz-Silva and Viola 2014
;
Yoshihara et al. 2014
;
Kim et al. 2015
;
Brownnutt et al. 2015
;
O’Malley et al. 2015
;
Szańkowski et al. 2016
;
Yan et al. 2016
;
Myers et al. 2017
;
Quintana et al. 2017
;
Ferrie et al. 2018
;
Noel et al. 2019
;
von Lüpke et al. 2020
;
Wolfowicz et al. 2021
;
Wang and Clerk 2021

and references therein. For condensed-matter based qubits, the analysis is frequently based on the assumption that the fluctuations are Gaussian noise that comes from many independent sources each of which is weakly coupled to a qubit. However, this assumption does not necessarily apply, particularly for low-frequency noise,
Ristè et al. 2013
;
Serniak et al. 2018
;
Christensen et al. 2019
;
Schlör et al. 2019
. Such noise is often thought to come from random hops between the states of two-level systems (TLSs) coupled to a qubit. However, qubit noise from the TLSs is generally non-Gaussian
Paladino et al. 2002
;
Galperin et al. 2004
;
Galperin et al. 2006
;
Paladino et al. 2014
;
Müller et al. 2019
, reminiscent of the problem of spin decoherence in nuclear magnetic resonance associated with the spectral diffusion, see
Herzog and Hahn 1956
;
Klauder and Anderson 1962
.

To characterize noise that causes qubit dephasing it is important to know not just the noise spectrum, but also its statistics. The problem has attracted considerable interest
Li et al. 2013
;
Norris et al. 2016
;
Szańkowski et al. 2017
;
Sung et al. 2019
. A natural approach is to characterize higher-order spectral moments of noise of the qubit frequency. For zero-mean Gaussian noise, all moments are expressed in terms of the second moment. A deviation from the corresponding interrelation between the moments is a signature of the noise being non-Gaussian. For frequency noise of a mesoscopic vibrational system higher-order moments were studied in
Maizelis et al. 2011
;
Sun et al. 2015
. The possibility to measure the third spectral moment of qubit noise (the bispectrum) was demonstrated in
Sung et al. 2019
by building up on the approach
Norris et al. 2016
. This approach is based, ultimately, on using distinct sequences of refocusing pulses during Ramsey measurements. Therefore it is limited to the noise frequencies that are higher than the reciprocal lifetime of a qubit.

In the present paper we develop means for studying the statistics of a qubit noise with frequencies smaller than the reciprocal qubit lifetime. Such noise plays a critical role in the operation of a quantum computer as it limits the time over which repeated gate operations can be performed on a qubit without recalibrating it. However, we are not studying the range of extremely low frequencies, where the qubit frequency can be effectively measured in real time
Sank et al. 2012
. The stability of qubits over very long times is affected by several factors, such as cosmic rays or high-energy photons for superconducting qubits
Wilen et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2022
. Our approach can be extended to this time range, but here we concentrate on shorter times.

Our goal is to develop an analytical theory of the effects of noise statistics. The theory should be sufficiently general to account for different types of noise, including both Gaussian and non-Gaussian noise, and for the qubit dynamics involved in the measurements. We also aim at performing numerical simulations, which can be compared with the theory and in some cases can go beyond the range where analytical results can be obtained or become too cumbersome.

We study the first three moments of the qubit frequency noise. Such a study can be conveniently done by periodically repeating Ramsey measurements, cf. Ref.
Yan et al. 2012
, but going beyond noise spectroscopy. For the low-frequency noise it is advantageous to analyze the results primarily in the time rather than the frequency domain, particularly where we are not limited to the effects of the lowest order in the noise intensity.

The interplay of a large correlation time and the noise statistics should lead to a number of consequences, and we identify some of them. An example is a potentially large change of the variance of the qubit measurement outcomes beyond the binomial (Bernoulli) limit. Of significant interest is the occurrence of “anomalous” measurement outcomes, that is, of having an unlikely outcome with the probability much higher than what is expected from the Gaussian distribution of the outcome probabilities. The effect is particularly pronounced for TLSs, but it is different from the familiar mechanism associated with a strong coupling to a group of TLSs
Galperin et al. 2006
;
Paladino et al. 2014
. It emerges where the number of measurements is large, but not too large so that the overall duration of the data acquisition is not too long.

Identifying the mechanism of classical non-Gaussian qubit noise based on its moments is a hard problem, generally: such noise is often a result of “processing” of a Gaussian noise by nonlinear systems coupled to a qubit. A familiar example is the telegraph noise that comes from TLSs. Even in a simple model of two-level states in glasses
Anderson et al. 1972
;
Phillips 1972
;
Phillips 1987
this noise results ultimately from the interstate switching of a strongly-nonlinear (two-state) system due to its coupling to a bosonic reservoir. Noise identification is further exacerbated by the quantum uncertainty of the measurement outcomes.

Therefore it is helpful to have a “map” of the outcomes of the measurements depending on the noise correlation and statistics for different types of noise sources. We aim to develop such a map for TLSs, analytically and via simulations. We also study correlation functions for three important types of a Gaussian noise with a large correlation time, the exponentially correlated noise, the noise with a definite “color”, i.e., with a comparatively narrow peak in the power spectrum, and
1/f
noise.

The analytical calculations are fairly cumbersome. Therefore we separate the paper into three parts. One part present the results of the analytical calculations and describes the simulations and the comparison of the theory and simulations. The second part describes the general theoretical approach. The details of the theoretical calculations and some auxiliary results of the simulations are presented in the Appendices.

II
The correlation function of the measurement outcomes

We associate the operators acting on the qubit states with the Pauli operators
\sigma_{x,y,z}
and the unit operator
\hat{I}_{q}
. The ground and excited states of the qubit are the eigenstates of
\sigma_{z}
. In the Bloch sphere representation they are
\ket{0}\equiv\ket{\uparrow}
and
\ket{1}\equiv\ket{\downarrow}
, respectively. We consider a periodic sequence of Ramsey measurements sketched in Fig.
1

Sank et al. 2012
;
Yan et al. 2012
. In the first Ramsey measurement the qubit, initially in the state
\ket{0}
, is rotated at time
t=0
by
\pi/2
around the
y
-axis into the state
(\ket{0}+\ket{1})/\sqrt{2}
. At
t_{R}
it is rotated by
\pi/2
around the
y
-axis again and the occupation of the state
\ket{1}
is measured. After the measurement the qubit is reset to the ground state. The Ramsey measurements are then repeated with period
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
, which we call the cycle period. For simplicity we disregard the duration of the gate operations and the measurement as well as the gate and measurement errors.

Figure 1:
Schematics of the Ramsey measurements. The measurements of duration
t_{R}
are repeated with period
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
. After each measurement the qubit is reset to the ground state. Before each
\pi/2
pulse that precedes the measurement the phase of the qubit is incremented by
\phi_{R}
(not shown). The phase
\phi_{R}
mimics the phase accumulation due to the detuning of the qubit transition frequency from the frequency of the reference signal.

In a Ramsey measurement, the phase accumulated by the qubit over time
t_{R}
is compared with the phase accumulated over this time by a reference resonant signal. The accumulation of the phase difference
\theta{}
is thus determined by fluctuations
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)
of the qubit frequency. These fluctuations are described by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{fl}}=-\frac{1}{2}\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)\sigma_{z}\qquad(\hbar=1).

(1)

The random phase
\theta{}_{k}
accumulated over a
k
th cycle, i.e., over the time interval
(kt_{\mathrm{cyc}},kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R})
, is

\displaystyle\theta_{k}=\int_{kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}}^{kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}}\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)dt\ .

(2)

As seen from this equation, fluctuations
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)
with a typical correlation time much shorter than
t_{R}
are largely averaged out. Periodic repetition of the Ramsey measurements allows revealing fluctuations with correlation times not only on the scale of
t_{R}
, but also on the scale determined by the cycle period
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
.

We assume that the system, including noise, is stationary. Then measurement outcomes depend only on the time interval between the measurements. We consider the expectation values of the outcomes
r_{1},r_{2}(k)
, and
r_{3}(k,l)
of obtaining “1” in a single measurement, obtaining “1” and “1” in two measurements separated by
k
cycles, and obtaining three “1”s in three measurements in which two measurements are separated from the first one by
k
and
l
cycles, respectively; for concreteness, we assume
l>k\geq 1
. These expectation values are time correlators and are given by the correlation functions of the projection operator
\hat{\pi}=(\hat{I}_{q}-\sigma_{z})/2
,

\displaystyle r_{1}=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\pi}(t_{R}^{+})\rho(0^{-})],

\displaystyle r_{2}(k)=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\pi}(kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})\hat{\pi}(t_{R}^{+})\rho(0^{-})],

(3)

and

\displaystyle r_{3}(k,l)=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\pi}(lt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})\hat{\pi}(kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})

\displaystyle\times\hat{\pi}(t_{R}^{+})\rho(0^{-})],

(4)

where
\rho
is the density matrix of the system;
\hat{I}_{q}
is the unit operator in the qubit space. The superscripts “+” and “-” of the time arguments indicate that the operator is evaluated, respectively, right after or right before the corresponding instant of time, i.e., right after or right before the gate operation performed at this time,
t^{\pm}\equiv t\pm\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon\to+0
;
in particular,
0^{-}
is the time right before the rotation around the
y
-axis at
t=0
, whereas
t_{R}^{+}
is the time right after the rotation at
t=t_{R}
. Clearly,
r_{1}
is just the probability of obtaining “1” in a Ramsey measurement.

The traces in Eqs. (
II
) and (
II
) are calculated over the states of the qubit and the thermal reservoir coupled to the qubit, including the states of the TLSs if the TLSs play a role. The traces also imply averaging over the realizations of noise in the case where noise is a classical random force that modulates the qubit frequency.

The outcome of an
n
th Ramsey measurement
x_{n}
takes on values 1 or 0. The correlators
r_{1,2,3}
are determined by the expectation values of these outcomes and their products,

\displaystyle r_{1}=\mathbb{E}[x_{n}],\quad r_{2}(k)=\mathbb{E}[x_{n+k}x_{n}],

\displaystyle r_{3}(k,l)=\mathbb{E}[x_{n+l}x_{n+k}x_{n}]

(5)

For classical Gaussian noise the correlators
r_{3}(k,l)
are fully determined by
r_{1}
and
r_{2}(k)
. We find the corresponding relations. If they do not hold, this indicates that noise is non-Gaussian.

Along with
r_{2,3}
we will also consider centered correlators

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{2}(k)=\mathbb{E}[(x_{n+k}-r_{1})(x_{n}-r_{1})]\ ,

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{3}(k,l)=\mathbb{E}[(x_{n+l}-r_{1})(x_{n+k}-r_{1})(x_{n}-r_{1})]\ .

(6)

In what follows we analytically calculate the correlators
r_{1,2,3}
and compare them with the results of simulating the sequences
x_{n}
for several types of fluctuations. We typically simulate
N=10^{5}
cycles and average the results over 300 repetitions. This limits noise correlation time we can reliably simulate to
\lesssim 10^{4}t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
. We also do simulations where the number of cycles
N
is much smaller,
\lesssim 10^{2}
, so as to reveal the tail of the distribution of the outcomes that emerges in this case.

A note is due on the difference of the effects of TLSs on the correlators of different order. The Ramsey measurement probability
r_{1}
depends on the phase
\theta{}
accumulated between the Ramsey pulses due to noise. For a given
\theta
, the probability of obtaining “1” in a measurement is
Nielsen and Chuang 2011

\displaystyle p(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\left[1+e^{-t_{R}/T_{2}}\cos(\phi_{R}+\theta)\right]\ .

(7)

For a random
\theta
, the probability
r_{1}
is given by the mean value of
p(\theta)
.
In Eq. (
7
),
T_{2}^{-1}
is the qubit decoherence rate due to fast decay and dephasing processes. The phase
\phi_{R}
mimics the phase accumulated due to the difference between the qubit frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{q}}
and the frequency of the reference signal
\omega_{\mathrm{ref}}
,

\displaystyle\phi_{R}=(\omega_{\mathrm{q}}-\omega_{\mathrm{ref}})t_{R}.

(8)

This phase can be (and frequently is) also added in a controlled way by a gate operation, see Eq. (
53
) below.

In the model of noninteracting TLSs that we consider, the TLSs contribute to the phase
\theta{}
independently. The overall phase is a sum of these contributions. From Eq. (
7
), the value of
r_{1}
is determined by
\langle\exp(i\theta{})\rangle
and can be calculated by multiplying the contributions of individual TLSs
Paladino et al. 2002
;
Galperin et al. 2004
;
Galperin et al. 2006
. In contrast, the correlators
r_{2}
and higher-order correlators should contain terms that decay as individual TLSs, their pairs, triples, etc. Therefore these correlators may not be calculated as products of the contributions of individual TLSs.

III
Analytical results on dispersive coupling to two-level systems

We study two major mechanisms of qubit decoherence, classical fluctuations of the qubit frequency and the effect of coupling to two-level systems. The level spacing of the TLSs is assumed to be much smaller than the qubit level spacing. In this case the major effect of the TLSs is to modulate the qubit frequency as the TLSs switch between their states. We also discuss the effect of modulating the qubit frequency by classical Gaussian noise. The analysis is somewhat involved. Therefore we first provide the results, whereas their derivation is postponed till Secs.

X

III.1
Explicit general expressions

Qubit decoherence due to the dispersive qubit-to-TLSs coupling is described by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{q-TLS}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{z}\sum_{n}V^{(n)}{}\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)}

(9)

Here

n

is the coupling parameter; the states of an
n

\ket{0}^{(n)}
and
\ket{1}^{(n)}

and
\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)}\ket{i}^{(n)}=(-1)^{i}\ket{i}^{(n)}

\displaystyle r_{1}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}e^{-t_{R}/T_{2}}\mathrm{Re}\,\Bigl[e^{i\tilde{\phi}_{R}}\,\prod_{n}\Xi^{(n)}(t_{R})\Bigr]

(10)

\displaystyle\Xi^{(n)}(t_{R})=\Bigl[\left(\frac{W^{(n)}{}}{2\gamma^{(n)}}+iV^{(n)}{}\frac{\Delta W^{(n)}{}}{\gamma^{(n)}W^{(n)}{}}\right)\sinh\gamma^{(n)}t_{R}

\displaystyle+\cosh\gamma^{(n)}t_{R}\Bigr]\exp(-W^{(n)}{}t_{R}/2)

(11)

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{R}=\bigl(\omega_{\mathrm{q}}-\omega_{\mathrm{ref}}-\sum_{n}V^{(n)}{}\langle\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)}\rangle\bigr)t_{R}.

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{2}(k)=\frac{1}{4}e^{-2t_{R}/T_{2}}\sum_{s=1}^{N_{\mathrm{TLS}}}\sum_{\{m\}_{s}}\left\{\mathrm{Re}\left(e^{i\tilde{\phi}_{R}}\Bigl[\prod_{j=1}^{s}\xi_{k}^{(m_{j})}(t_{R})\Bigr]\Bigl[\prod_{n\notin\{m\}_{s}}\Xi^{(n)}(t_{R})\Bigr]\right)\right\}^{2}

III.2
Weak coupling

The case of weak coupling, where

Formally, for

\displaystyle\overline{\delta\omega}_{\mathrm{TLS}}=\sum_{n}\overline{\delta\omega}{}^{(n)},\quad\overline{\delta\omega}{}^{(n)}=V^{(n)}{}\Delta W^{(n)}/W^{(n)}.

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)}(t)\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)}(0)\rangle-\langle\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)}\rangle^{2}=w^{(n)}{}^{2}\exp(-W^{(n)}t)\ ,

(20)

where

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\theta}_{0}^{(n)}{}\hat{\theta}_{k}^{(n)}{}\rangle=(2V^{(n)}w^{(n)}/W^{(n)})^{2}\exp(-kW^{(n)}t_{\mathrm{cyc}})

\displaystyle\times\sinh^{2}(W^{(n)}t_{R}/2),\qquad k\geq 1.

(21)

Equation (
III.2
) is written in the form that relates it to the expression for the phase correlator
\langle\hat{\theta}_{0}^{(n)}{}\hat{\theta}_{k}^{(n)}{}\rangle
that follows from Eqs. (
18
) and (
19
).

An important feature of the correlator
\tilde{r}_{2}(k)
is that it does not exponentially decay with
k
. The decay is described by a superposition of the exponential factors that come from the decay of correlations of individual TLSs, with the decay rates
W^{(n)}
. We have also kept in Eq. (
III.2
) the factor
\exp(-\sum_{m\neq n}\overline{\theta^{(m)}{}^{2}})
that describes the collective effect of the system of the TLSs on the contribution of an individual TLS to the decay. Even though
\overline{\theta^{(n)}{}^{2}}\ll 1
for each TLS, the sum does not have to be small, if the number of the TLSs is large.

III.3
Short Ramsey measurement time

The expressions for the probability
r_{1}
and the centered pair correlator
r_{2}(k)
are simplified also in the case where the duration of the Ramsey measurement is small, so that
V^{(n)}t_{R},W^{(n)}t_{R}\ll 1
. The general expressions (
10
) and (
III.1
) give in this case,

\displaystyle r_{1}\approx\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\exp\left[-(t_{R}/T_{2})-\sum_{n}(w^{(n)}V^{(n)}t_{R})^{2}/2\right]

\displaystyle\times\cos\phi_{R}

(22)

and

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{2}(k)\approx\frac{1}{4}e^{-2t_{R}/T_{2}}t_{R}^{2}\sum_{n}V^{(n)}{}^{2}e^{-kW^{(n)}t_{\mathrm{cyc}}}

\displaystyle\times\exp\Bigl[-\sum_{m\neq n}(w^{(m)}V^{(m)}t_{R})^{2}\Bigr]\sin^{2}(\phi_{R}-\overline{\delta\omega}^{(n)}t_{R})\ .

(23)

Equations (
III.3
) and (
III.3
) hold for arbitrary ratios
V^{(n)}/W^{(n)}
. The considered limit is important. Indeed, TLSs with
W^{(n)}t_{R}\gg 1
do not contribute to the centered correlators
\tilde{r}_{2}(k)
and higher-order centered correlators: fast switching averages out their effect. The correlators
\tilde{r}_{2}(k)
are formed by the TLSs with the switching rates smaller than
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{-1}<t_{R}^{-1}
, i.e.,
W^{(n)}t_{R}<1
. The condition
V^{(n)}t_{R}\ll 1
is the weak-coupling condition, where the fluctuations weakly affect the quantum correlations responsible for the probability of observing “1” in a Ramsey measurement being
r_{1}>1/2
.

III.4
Strong coupling

In the limit of strong coupling to the TLSs,
V^{(n)}{}\gg W^{(n)}
, to the leading order

\displaystyle r_{1}\approx\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}e^{-t_{R}/T_{2}}\mathrm{Re}\Bigl\{e^{i\tilde{\phi}_{R}}\prod_{n}e^{-W^{(n)}t_{R}/2}

\displaystyle\times[\cos V^{(n)}{}t_{R}+i(\Delta W^{(n)}/W^{(n)})\sin V^{(n)}{}t_{R}]\Bigr\}\ .

(24)

The dependence of
r_{1}
on the duration of the Ramsey measurement
t_{R}
is determined by the product of the oscillating factors. For
V^{(n)}{}t_{R}\ll 1
we have
r_{1}-1/2\propto\exp(-\sum_{n}V^{(n)}{}^{2}t_{R}^{2}/2)
. As the characteristic
V^{(n)}{}t_{R}
increases,
r_{1}-1/2
quickly falls off for a large number of TLSs with different
V^{(n)}
.

The effect of the oscillating factor in Eq. (
III.4
) is particularly clear if the values of
V^{(n)}
for different TLSs are close. Here, for symmetric TLSs (
\Delta W^{(n)}=0
), we have
r_{1}-1/2\propto(\cos V^{(n)}t_{R})^{N_{\mathrm{TLS}}}
, which is a sharp periodic function of
V^{(n)}t_{R}
for a large number of TLSs.

Strong coupling to the qubit is pronounced provided the condition
V^{(n)}t_{R}\gtrsim 1
holds for
t_{R}\ll T_{2}
. It is plausible therefore that only one TLS will display a sufficiently strong coupling. In this case
r_{1}
oscillates with
t_{R}
with period
2\pi/V^{(n)}
. The correlator
\tilde{r}_{2}(k)
is oscillating with half of this period. For a symmetric TLS,

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{2}(k)\approx\frac{1}{4}e^{-2t_{R}/T_{2}}\sin^{2}\phi_{R}\sin^{2}(V^{(n)}t_{R})

\displaystyle\times\exp(-kW^{(n)}t_{\mathrm{cyc}})\ .

(25)

It is seen from a comparison of this expression with Eq. (
IV.1
) below that the dependence of
\tilde{r}_{2}(k)
on the angle
\phi_{R}
is different for a TLS and for Gaussian frequency noise, if the coupling is not weak.

Oscillations with the varying
t_{R}
is a characteristic feature of a strong coupling of TLSs to the qubit. However, for slowly decaying TLSs it might be easier to observe oscillations of the probability distribution of the measurement outcomes for a comparatively short acquisition time discussed in Sec.
VI.1
. The oscillations discussed in that section have the same physical origin but can be pronounced already for comparatively small
V^{(n)}t_{R}
, and observing them does not require varying the duration of the Ramsey measurement.

IV
Analytical results on Gaussian fluctuations of the qubit frequency

An important cause of the qubit frequency fluctuations is external classical noise with frequencies much lower than the qubit transition frequency. The effect of such noise is described by the term
-\frac{1}{2}\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)\sigma_{z}
in the qubit Hamiltonian, cf. Eq. (
1
). We consider zero-mean stationary Gaussian frequency fluctuations
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)
. Such fluctuations are fully characterized by their power spectrum

\displaystyle S_{q}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dte^{i\omega t}\langle\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(0)\rangle.

(26)

For classical fluctuations
S_{q}(\omega)=S_{q}(-\omega)
. The technique we develop can be extended to quantum noise as well.

IV.1
Explicit general expressions

Of relevance for the qubit is the accumulation of its phase due to the frequency fluctuations. For a classical noise of the qubit frequency, the phase accumulated over the time interval
(kt_{\mathrm{cyc}},kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R})
, is

\theta_{k}=\int_{kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}}^{kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}}\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)dt
,
cf. Eq. (
2
). This expression is the classical analog of the operator
\sum_{n}\hat{\theta}_{k}^{(n)}
, see Eq. (
18
). For a zero-mean Gaussian noise

\langle\theta_{k}\rangle=0.

The phases accumulated during subsequent measurements are related via noise power spectrum,

\displaystyle f_{k}\equiv\langle\theta_{n}\theta_{n+k}\rangle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int d\omega S_{q}(\omega)\exp(i\omega kt_{\mathrm{cyc}})\ ,

\displaystyle\times(1-\cos\omega t_{R})/\omega^{2}\ .

(27)

We use here that, because of the stationarity of noise
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)
, the correlator
\langle\theta_{n}\theta_{m}\rangle
depends only on
|n-m|
. The probability distribution of the phases
\theta_{n}
is Gaussian for the Gaussian distribution of
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)
. It is thus fully determined by the parameters
f_{k}
. As seen from Eq. (
IV.1
)
f_{0}>0
and
f_{0}>|f_{k}|
for
k\neq 0
. While
f_{k}=f_{-k}
, the correlators
f_{k}
can be negative, in general.

The phase correlators should be directly related to the probability
r_{1}
and the correlators
r_{2}(k),r_{3}(k,l)
of the Ramsey measurements. Intuitively, one can express these parameters in terms of the probability
p(\theta)
, Eq. (
7
),
of having “1” as an outcome of the Ramsey measurement for a given
\theta
,

\displaystyle r_{1}=\langle p(\theta_{n})\rangle,\quad r_{2}(k)=\langle p(\theta_{n})p(\theta_{n+k})\rangle

\displaystyle r_{3}(k,l)=\langle p(\theta_{n})p(\theta_{n+k})p(\theta_{n+l})\rangle

(28)

The averaging here is done over the distribution of the phases
\{\theta\}
. For the stationary distribution the result is independent of
n
.

Equation (
IV.1
) applies independent of the noise statistics. It is substantiated by the analysis of Sec.
X
, which is based on solving the master equation for a qubit in the presence of noise.

For Gaussian noise,
r_{1},r_{2}(k)
, and
r_{3}(k,l)
can be explicitly expressed in terms of the correlators
f_{k}
, and thus in terms of the noise power spectrum,

\displaystyle r_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\left[1+e^{-t_{R}/T_{2}}\exp(-f_{0}/2)\cos\phi_{R}\right],

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{2}(k)=\frac{1}{8}e^{-2t_{R}/T_{2}}\exp(-f_{0})\left[e^{f_{k}}-1\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\cos(2\phi_{R})(1-e^{-f_{k}})\right]\ ,

(29)

and

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{3}(k,l)=\frac{1}{32}\exp[-3(f_{0}/2)-3(t_{R}/T_{2})]\left\{\cos 3\phi_{R}\left[\exp\Bigl(-\sum_{i}f_{i}\Bigr)+2-\sum_{i}\exp(-f_{i})\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\cos\phi_{R}\left[\sum_{i}\exp\Bigl(-f_{i}+\sum_{j}{\vphantom{\sum}}^{\!\!\prime}f_{j}\Bigr)+6-\sum_{i}\Bigl(2e^{f_{i}}+e^{-f_{i}}\Bigr)\right]\right\}.

(30)

In Eq. (
IV.1
) the summation goes over
i,j=k,l,l-k
and the prime over the sum means that
j\neq i
.

It is seen from Eq. (
IV.1
) that the correlators
f_{k}
can be found for all
k
by measuring
r_{1}
and
\tilde{r}_{2}(k)
. They define
\tilde{r}_{3}(k,l)
, and therefore by measuring
\tilde{r}_{3}(k,l)
one can tell whether frequency noise is Gaussian.

It follows from Eq. (
IV.1
) that the centered correlator
\tilde{r}_{3}
goes to zero as
\phi_{R}
approaches
\pi/2
. On the other hand, for weak Gaussian noise, where
f_{k}\ll 1
, we have from Eq. (
IV.1
)
\tilde{r}_{2}(k)\propto f_{k}\sin^{2}\phi_{R}
, which means that in this limit
\tilde{r}_{2}\to 0
for
\phi_{R}\to 0
. Therefore one may be interested in measuring the pair and triple correlators for different values of
\phi_{R}
and comparing the results.

For weak noise,
f_{k}\ll 1
, the centered pair and triple correlators are

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{2}(k)\approx\frac{1}{4}e^{-2t_{R}/T_{2}}\exp(-f_{0})f_{k}\sin^{2}\phi_{R}

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{3}(k,l)\approx-\frac{1}{8}e^{-3t_{R}/T_{2}}\exp(-3f_{0}/2)

\displaystyle\times(f_{k}f_{l}+f_{k}f_{l-k}+f_{l}f_{l-k})\cos\phi_{R}\sin^{2}\phi_{R}

(31)

We keep the term
\propto f_{0}
in the exponent to account for the case where the parameters
f_{n>0}
are small because
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}\gg\tau_{\mathrm{corr}}
. Respectively, we do not simplify the expression for
r_{1}
. The correlator
\tilde{r}_{2}(k)
is of the first order in
f_{k}
whereas the correlator
\tilde{r}_{3}(k,l)
is bilinear in
f_{k},f_{l}
. In many cases of interest
f_{n}>0
for all
n
; then
\tilde{r}_{2}(k)>0
whereas
\tilde{r}_{3}(k,l)<0
for
\cos\phi_{R}>0
. Then, if in the experiment it is found that, for a weak noise, both
\tilde{r}_{2}>0
and
\tilde{r}_{3}>0
, this indicates that the noise is non-Gaussian.

IV.2
Exponentially correlated frequency noise

We now provide explicit expressions for the correlators
f_{k}=\langle\theta_{n}\theta_{n+k}\rangle
of the phases accumulated during Ramsey measurement for two explicit types of Gaussian frequency noise. These expressions are used in Sec.
V.2
to compare the analytical expressions with the results of simulations. The effect of noise correlations comes into play if the characteristic correlation time is comparable to
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
. Otherwise one can think of frequency noise as white (
\delta
-correlated). For
\langle\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t^{\prime})\rangle=D_{\mathrm{w}}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
we have

f_{k}=D_{\mathrm{w}}t_{R}\delta_{k,0},

where
D_{\mathrm{w}}
is white noise intensity. For such noise the centered measurement correlators
\tilde{r}_{2}(k),\tilde{r}_{3}(k,l)
should vanish, as indeed seen from Eqs. (
IV.1
) and (
IV.1
).

An important type of correlated Gaussian noise is exponentially correlated noise,

\displaystyle\langle\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\frac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{corr}}\tau_{\mathrm{corr}}^{-1}e^{-|t-t^{\prime}|/\tau_{\mathrm{corr}}}.

(32)

The power spectrum of such noise is Lorentzian,

\displaystyle S_{q}(\omega)\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dte^{i\omega t}\langle\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{corr}}/(1+\omega^{2}\tau_{\mathrm{cor}}^{2}).

(33)

The exponentially correlated noise may come from a filtered white Gaussian noise, a simple example being broad-band random voltage filtered by an
RC
-circuit. Another example is noise from dispersive coupling to thermal photons in a multi-mode cavity with the modes decay times being close to each other, so that these times can be approximated by
\tau_{\mathrm{corr}}
. In Eq. (
32
)
D_{\mathrm{corr}}
characterizes noise intensity, whereas
\tau_{\mathrm{corr}}
is noise correlation time.

Using Eq. (
IV.1
), which expresses the phase correlators
f_{k}
in terms of the power spectrum
S_{q}(\omega)
, one obtains

\displaystyle f_{0}=D_{\mathrm{corr}}\left[t_{R}-\tau_{\mathrm{corr}}\left(1-e^{-t_{R}/\tau_{\mathrm{corr}}}\right)\right],

\displaystyle f_{k}=D_{\mathrm{corr}}\tau_{\mathrm{corr}}\exp(-|k|t_{\mathrm{cyc}}/\tau_{\mathrm{corr}})

\displaystyle\times[\cosh(t_{R}/\tau_{\mathrm{corr}})-1]\quad(|k|>0)

(34)

As seen from Eq. (
IV.2
), the correlators
f_{k}
fall off exponentially with the increasing
|k|
for exponentially correlated noise. The rate of the decay is determined by the relation between the duration of the cycle
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
and noise correlation time
\tau_{\mathrm{corr}}
.

From Eq. (
IV.1
), for weak noise,
f_{n}\ll 1
, the correlator
\tilde{r}_{2}(k)
falls off exponentially with the increasing
k
. The correlator
\tilde{r}_{3}(k,l)
, on the other hand, which is quadratic in
f_{n}
, shows non-exponential decay even where the decay of
\tilde{r}_{2}(k)
is close to exponential. This is in agreement with the simulations discussed in Sec.
V.2
.

For stronger noise the decay of the correlators
\tilde{r}_{2}(k),\tilde{r}_{3}(k,l)
becomes nonexponential, even though
f_{k}
exponentially falls off with the increasing
k
.

IV.3
Noise with “color”

To illustrate the possibility of a nonmonotonic behavior of the correlators
f_{k}
as functions of
k
, we briefly describe the effect of noise with “color”, that is noise with a spectrum
S_{q}(\omega)
that has a peak at a nonzero frequency. A simple example is Johnson-Nyquist noise filtered by an RCL circuit. The power spectrum of such noise is

\displaystyle S_{q}(\omega)=D_{\mathrm{clr}}[(\omega^{2}-\omega_{\mathrm{clr}}^{2})^{2}+4\Gamma_{\mathrm{clr}}^{2}\omega^{2}]^{-1}

(35)

This spectrum has a peak at
(\omega_{\mathrm{clr}}^{2}+2\Gamma_{\mathrm{clr}}^{2})^{1/2}
. For
\omega_{\mathrm{clr}}\gg\Gamma_{\mathrm{clr}}
this peak is Lorentzian with halfwidth
\Gamma_{\mathrm{clr}}
.

It is straightforward to see that, for
\sqrt{2}\Gamma_{\mathrm{clr}}t_{R}<\omega_{\mathrm{clr}}t_{R}\ll 1
we have

\displaystyle f_{0}=D_{\mathrm{clr}}t_{R}^{2}/4\omega_{\mathrm{clr}}^{3}\sin\phi_{\mathrm{clr}},

\displaystyle f_{k}=\frac{D_{\mathrm{clr}}t_{R}^{2}}{2\omega_{\mathrm{clr}}^{3}\sin(2\phi_{\mathrm{clr}})}\exp(-kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}\omega_{\mathrm{clr}}\sin\phi_{\mathrm{clr}})

\displaystyle\times\cos(kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}\omega_{\mathrm{clr}}\cos\phi_{\mathrm{clr}}-\phi_{\mathrm{clr}})\quad(k>0),

(36)

where

\phi_{\mathrm{clr}}=\frac{1}{2}\arctan\frac{2\Gamma_{\mathrm{clr}}\sqrt{\omega_{\mathrm{clr}}^{2}-\Gamma_{\mathrm{clr}}^{2}}}{\omega_{\mathrm{clr}}^{2}-2\Gamma_{\mathrm{clr}}^{2}}.

From Eq. (
IV.3
), the correlators
f_{k}
display exponentially decaying oscillations as functions of
k
. For weak noise, such oscillations will be immediately seen in the correlators
r_{2}(k)
and will be also seen as broadened peaks in the power spectrum of the measurement outcomes discussed in Sec.
VII
.

IV.4
1/f
noise

Very often qubit decoherence is caused by
1/f
frequency noise, i.e., by noise with the power spectrum
S_{q}(\omega)\propto 1/\omega\equiv 1/2\pi f
, cf.
Yan et al. 2012
;
Paladino et al. 2014
;
Quintana et al. 2017
;
You et al. 2021
and references therein. If such noise comes from a large number of fluctuators, it becomes Gaussian, and the assumption that
1/f
noise is Gaussian is often made. Since the integral intensity of
1/f
noise diverges, the spectrum has to be cut both at low and high frequencies. A high-frequency cutoff is irrelevant for the problem of frequency noise that we consider, since the integration over the interval
t_{R}
between the Ramsey pulses filters out high-frequency noise components.

The low-frequency cutoff is model-dependent. We will present results for a simple physically motivated model in which the spectrum is smooth. This model is also related to the model of the noise from TLSs used in the simulations. In contrast to the simulated TLS models, it corresponds to coupling to a very large number of TLSs with the coupling constant being the same for all TLSs (cf. Refs.
Ithier et al. 2005
;
Yoshihara et al. 2014
) and with log-normal distribution of the switching rates
W^{(n)}
; the rates are assumed to be limited from below by
\omega_{\min}
. From Eq. (
19
), the power spectrum of such noise has the form

\displaystyle S_{q}(\omega)=\frac{2}{\pi}D_{\mathrm{fl}}\int_{\omega_{\min}}^{\infty}\frac{dW}{W^{2}+\omega^{2}}

\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{fl}}\omega^{-1}[1-(2/\pi)\tan^{-1}(\omega_{\min}/\omega)]

(37)

In the range
\omega\gg\omega_{\min}
we have
S_{q}(\omega)\approx D_{\mathrm{fl}}/\omega
, whereas
S_{q}(\omega)\approx 2D_{\mathrm{fl}}/\pi\omega_{\min}
for
\omega\ll\omega_{\min}
.

Calculating the integral in Eq. (
IV.1
) by closing the contour in the
\omega
-plane and using Eq. (
IV.4
), one can write the correlators of the qubit phase accumulated during remote measurements as

\displaystyle f_{k}=\frac{2}{\pi}D_{\mathrm{fl}}\int_{\omega_{\min}}^{\infty}\frac{dW}{W^{3}}[\cosh(Wt_{R})-1]

\displaystyle\times\exp(-kWt_{\mathrm{cyc}}),\qquad k>0,

(38)

whereas the mean square phase is

\displaystyle f_{0}=\frac{2}{\pi}D_{\mathrm{fl}}\left[\frac{t_{R}}{\omega_{\min}}+\int_{\omega_{\min}}^{\infty}\frac{dW}{W^{3}}\Bigl(e^{-Wt_{R}}-1\Bigr)\right]

(39)

For small
\omega_{\min}t_{R}\ll 1
the leading-order terms in
f_{k}
and
f_{0}
are logarithmic in
\omega_{\min}t_{R}
. The decay of the correlators
f_{k}
with the increasing
k
is nonexponential, in contrast to the case of exponentially correlated Gaussian noise. However, it becomes close to exponential in the limit of large
k\omega_{\min}t_{\mathrm{cyc}}\gg 1
,

f_{k}\approx D_{\mathrm{fl}}(t_{R}^{2}/\pi kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}\omega_{\min})\exp(-k\omega_{\min}t_{\mathrm{cyc}}).

On the other hand, for
kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}\gg t_{R}
but
k\omega_{\min}t_{\mathrm{cyc}}\ll 1
a reasonable numerical approximation is

\displaystyle f_{k}\approx D_{\mathrm{fl}}(t_{R}^{2}/\pi)[-\gamma_{E}-\log(k\omega_{\min}t_{\mathrm{cyc}})]

(40)

where
\gamma_{E}\approx 0.58
is the Euler constant.

The integrals in Eqs. (
IV.4
) and (
39
) can be expressed in terms of exponential integral and hyperbolic integral functions. These expressions are given in Appendix
G
.

V
Comparison of the theory and simulations

In this section we present the results of simulations of periodically repeated Ramsey measurements and compare them with the theoretical predictions. The results are aimed at illustrating major qualitative aspects of the effect of low-frequency fluctuations of the qubit frequency on the measurements. We chose the ratio of the period
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
of the repeated operation “measurement+reset” to the accumulation time of the measurement itself
t_{R}
(see Fig.
1
) to be equal to
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}/t_{R}=3
. We present the results for
\phi_{R}=\pi/4
, where all centered correlators are “visible”. With other simple choices of
\phi_{R}
, as seen from Eqs. (
IV.1
) and (
IV.1
), we have
\tilde{r}_{2}=0
for
\phi_{R}=0
in the case of weak coupling, whereas
\tilde{r}_{3}=0
for
\phi_{R}=\pi/2
. Also we assume that
t_{R}
is much smaller than
T_{2}
and disregard corrections
\propto t_{R}/T_{2}
. In this section we describe the correlators
r_{2}(k)
and
r_{3}(k,l)
found in sufficiently long simulations to obtain good statistics. The simulation algorithms are outlined in Appendix
B
.

V.1
Effect of the coupling to TLSs

One of the goals of the paper is to compare the effects of qubit frequency fluctuations
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)
induced by coupling to the TLSs and by Gaussian noise. Important for such comparison is the power spectrum
S_{q}(\omega)
of the TLSs-induced fluctuations
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)
. From Eq. (
9
),
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)
is an operator in the space of the TLSs’ states,
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)\Rightarrow\delta\hat{\omega}_{\mathrm{q}}(t)=\sum_{n}V^{(n)}\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)}
, and then

S_{q}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dte^{i\omega t}\langle\delta\hat{\omega}_{\mathrm{q}}(t)[\delta\hat{\omega}_{\mathrm{q}}(0)-\langle\delta\hat{\omega}_{\mathrm{q}}\rangle)]\rangle\ .

Using the explicit expression (
19
) for the correlator of
\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)}
, we obtain

\displaystyle S_{q}(\omega)=2\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{TLS}}}(w^{(n)}V^{(n)})^{2}W^{(n)}

\displaystyle\times(W^{(n)}{}^{2}+\omega^{2})^{-1}\ .

(41)

We present results of the simulations in which the coupling parameters are the same for all TLSs,
V^{(n)}=V
. We also assumed that the switching rates
W_{ij}^{(n)}
depend on
n
exponentially. In particular, for symmetric TLSs, where
W_{01}^{(n)}=W_{10}^{(n)}=W^{(n)}/2
, we assumed that

\displaystyle V^{(n)}=V,\qquad W^{(n)}t_{R}=\exp[-\alpha(n+n_{0})].

This leads to the spectrum
S_{q}(\omega)
being of the
1/f
form in a broad frequency range already for a small number of TLSs, see Appendix
C
. For a given
N_{\mathrm{TLS}}
, this range depends on the values of
n_{0}
and
\alpha
.

VI
Probability distribution for an intermediate acquisition time

The above results on the measurement outcomes refer to the case where the data has been accumulated over
M\gg 1
Ramsey measurements. The duration of the data acquisition
Mt_{\mathrm{cyc}}
was assumed to be sufficiently long, so that noise correlations decay, in which case the measurements are “ergodic”: time-average coincides with the ensemble average. However, if noise has a slowly varying component, of interest is a distribution of the measurement outcomes for shorter times. It is obtained by
M\gg 1
measurements for
Mt_{\mathrm{cyc}}
smaller than the decay time of the noise correlations.

There is a similarity between the qubit measurements where noise remains constant in time and transport measurements in condensed-matter systems in the presence of static disorder where electrons are elastically scattered by the disorder, but on the time scale of the measurement their energy is not changed. In the qubit case, the outcome of
M
measurements is a snapshot of the static qubit frequency, which remains constant but varies from one series of
M
measurements to another.

VI.1
Slow two-level systems

In the case of noise from TLSs, the ergodic limit corresponds to such acquisition duration that all TLSs have a chance to switch multiple times,

Mt_{\mathrm{cyc}}\gg 1/W^{(n)}
for all
n
. However, in the presence of very slow TLSs the inequality
Mt_{\mathrm{cyc}}\gg 1/W^{(n)}
does not necessarily hold even for large, but not too large
M
. For slowly switching TLSs the data will present a snapshot of their initial distribution.

We will assume that there is a set
\{N_{\mathrm{sl}}\}
of slowly switching TLSs, i.e.,
MW^{(n)}t_{\mathrm{cyc}}\ll 1
for
n\in\{N_{\mathrm{sl}}\}
. During data accumulation they remain in the initially occupied states
\ket{0}^{(n)}
or
\ket{1}^{(n)}
. Since the shift of the qubit frequency is determined by the operator
\sum_{n}V^{(n)}{}\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)}
[cf. Eq. (
9
)], the qubit will accumulate the same phase
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}t_{R}
during each of the
M
Ramsey measurements (in the absence of other sources of noise). This phase is

\displaystyle\theta(\{j_{n}\})=\sum_{n\in\{N_{\mathrm{sl}}\}}V^{(n)}{}(-1)^{j_{n}}t_{R},

(42)

where the parameters
j_{n}
take on values 0 or 1; we choose
j_{n}=0
if the occupied state is
\ket{0}^{(n)}
and
j_{n}=1
if the occupied state is
\ket{1}^{(n)}
. The probability to have a given
\theta(\{j_{n}\})
is determined by the probabilities
w_{j_{n}}^{(n)}
of the occupation of the corresponding initial states of the TLSs, which are given in Eq. (
17
).

The value of
\theta\equiv\theta(\{j_{n}\})
determines the probability
r_{1}
of obtaining “1” in a Ramsey measurement. If there are no fluctuations of the qubit frequency
r_{1}=p(\theta)
, where
p(\theta)
is given by Eq. (
7
). Because
\theta\equiv\theta(\{j_{n}\})
is random,
p(\theta)
also becomes random. This means that the outcomes of
M\gg 1
measurements will have a distribution, which is determined by the distribution of the values of
\theta(\{j_{n}\})
. In the simplest case where the slow TLSs are symmetric,
w_{0}^{(n)}=w_{1}^{(n)}=1/2
, and all
V^{(n)}{}=V
are the same [cf.
Ithier et al. 2005
;
Yoshihara et al. 2014
],
\theta(\{j_{n}\})
takes on values
\theta_{\mathrm{sl}}(n)=Vt_{R}(2n-N_{\mathrm{sl}})
with probabilities

P[\theta_{\mathrm{sl}}(n)]=2^{-N_{\mathrm{sl}}}\binom{N_{\mathrm{sl}}}{n}

(
n
is the number of the TLSs in the state
\ket{0}
.
We note a “cumulative” effect of the slow TLSs. The values of
\theta_{\mathrm{sl}}(n)
are determined by the coupling constant
V
multiplied by the difference of the number of TLSs in the states
\ket{0}
and
\ket{1}
. Therefore they may be significantly larger than for a single TLS.

The resulting probability distribution
\rho(m|M)
to have “1”
m
times in
M
measurements is a supersposition of binomial distributions for different
\theta_{\mathrm{sl}}(n)
weighted with the probability of having a given
\theta_{\mathrm{sl}}(n)
,

\displaystyle\rho(m|M)=\binom{M}{m}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{\mathrm{sl}}}P[\theta_{\mathrm{sl}}(n)]p^{m}[\theta_{\mathrm{sl}}(n)]

\displaystyle\times\{1-p[\theta_{\mathrm{sl}}(n)]\}^{M-m}

(43)

For brevity, we call “static” the limit in which switching between the TLSs’ states is disregarded and Eq. (
VI.1
) applies.

The probability distribution

VII
Periodic modulation of the qubit frequency

A potentially important cause of the time dependence of the qubit frequency is a low-frequency periodic signal. It can come, for example, from an AC power supply or from other low-frequency sources. For a sinusoidal modulation at frequency
\omega_{p}
, so that
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)=a_{p}\cos(\omega_{p}t+\phi_{p})
, the phase accumulation during the
k
th Ramsey measurement is

\displaystyle\theta_{k}=\int_{kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}}^{kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}}\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)dt=A_{p}\cos(k\omega_{p}t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+\tilde{\phi}_{p})\ ,

\displaystyle A_{p}=\frac{2a_{p}}{\omega_{p}}\sin\frac{\omega_{p}t_{R}}{2},\quad\tilde{\phi}_{p}=\phi_{p}+\frac{\omega_{p}t_{R}}{2}\ ,

(47)

where
a_{p}
and
\phi_{p}
are the amplitude and phase of the qubit frequency modulation.

A periodic frequency modulation can be revealed by studying the power spectrum of the measurement outcomes
x_{n}
, with
x_{n}
taking the values
0
or
1
. The spectrum obtained in a series of
N
measurements is related to the discrete Fourier transform
X(m)
of
x_{n}
,

\displaystyle X(m)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x_{n}\exp(2\pi imn/N)\ .

(48)

To obtain the power spectrum, one has to calculate
|X(m)|^{2}
and average the result over a repeated series of measurements. The outcome sensitively depends on how the averaging is done. If the measurements are synchronized with the modulation
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)
, i.e., all of them refer to the same phase
\phi_{p}
, the result depends on this phase. However, generally the modulation is unknown. In fact, the measurements are done to reveal its presence. Then
|X(m)|^{2}
is calculated each time for a different phase and there occurs averaging over
\phi_{p}
. It is this case that we consider, and the phase averaging is implied when we use the notation
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
. The power spectrum
R(m)
is then defined as

\displaystyle R(m)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X(m)|^{2}]=\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=0}^{N-1}e^{2\pi im(n_{1}-n_{2})/N}

\displaystyle\times\langle p(\theta_{n_{1}})p(\theta_{n_{2}})\rangle_{\phi}\ ,

(49)

where
p(\theta_{n})
is the probability for
x_{n}
to be equal to 1, cf. Eq. (
7
), whereas
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\phi}
means averaging over
\phi_{p}
. The averaging may also include averaging over noise in
\theta
; in this section we disregard this noise, for simplicity.

Since
p(\theta
) contains
\cos\theta
, the product
p(\theta_{n_{1}})p(\theta_{n_{2}})
in Eq. (
VII
) contains terms
\propto\exp[\pm ik(n_{1}-n_{2})\omega_{p}t_{\mathrm{cyc}}]
with amplitudes
\propto J_{k}^{2}(A_{p})
, where
J_{k}
is the Bessel function. They lead to peaks in
R(m)
at the values of
m
that correspond to multiples of
N\omega_{p}t_{\mathrm{cyc}}/2\pi
.

We will consider weak and slow frequency modulation, which is of utmost interest for the experiment,

\omega_{p}t_{R}\ll 1,\qquad A_{p}\approx a_{p}t_{R}\ll 1.

In this case, to the leading order in
A_{p}
, we find that
R(m)
has two sharp mirror-symmetric peaks at
m>0
, one near
N\omega_{p}t_{\mathrm{cyc}}/2\pi
and the other near
N-(N\omega_{p}t_{\mathrm{cyc}}/2\pi)
. For
|2\pi m/N)-\omega_{p}t_{\mathrm{cyc}}|\ll 1
,

\displaystyle R(m)\approx\frac{1}{8}A_{p}^{2}\sin^{2}\phi_{R}e^{-2t_{R}/T_{2}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1-\cos(N\omega_{p}t_{\mathrm{cyc}})}{[(2\pi m/N)-\omega_{p}t_{\mathrm{cyc}}]^{2}}.

(50)

The height of the peak is
\propto N^{2}
for a generically noninteger
N\omega_{p}t_{\mathrm{cyc}}/2\pi
. It is well beyond the noise floor. Observing the peak should allow identifying the presence of a slow periodic modulation of the qubit frequency.

The spectral peak (
VII
) can be broadened and its height can be reduced by fluctuations of the modulation frequency
\omega_{p}
or by fluctuations of the time intervals between the measurements
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
. For an illustration, we consider the simplest model where
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
fluctuates from cycle to cycle with variance
\langle(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}-\langle t_{\mathrm{cyc}}\rangle)^{2}\rangle=\sigma_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{2}
and where these fluctuations are Gaussian and there is no correlation between different cycles. A straightforward calculation shows that, in this case, Eq. (
VII
) for the shape of the spectral peak near
m\approx N\omega_{p}t_{\mathrm{cyc}}/2\pi
is modified to

\displaystyle R(m)\approx\frac{1}{8}A_{p}^{2}\sin^{2}\phi_{R}e^{-2t_{R}/T_{2}}(\Delta_{m}^{2}+\Delta_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{2})^{-2}\left\{(\Delta_{m}^{2}-\Delta_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{2})\left[1-e^{-N\Delta_{\mathrm{cyc}}}\cos(N\omega_{p}t_{\mathrm{cyc}})\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.-2\Delta_{m}\Delta_{\mathrm{sec}}e^{-N\Delta_{\mathrm{cyc}}}\sin(N\omega_{p}t_{\mathrm{cyc}})+N\Delta_{\mathrm{cyc}}(\Delta_{m}^{2}+\Delta_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{2})\right\}

(51)

where

\Delta_{m}=(2\pi m/N)-\omega_{p}t_{\mathrm{cyc}},\quad\Delta_{\mathrm{cyc}}=\omega_{p}^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{2}/2

Even for a small variance of
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
, where
\Delta_{\mathrm{cyc}}\ll 1
, the spectrum (
VII
) can be dramatically different from the spectrum (
VII
). Not only is the peak broadened but, for
N\Delta_{\mathrm{cyc}}\gg 1
, the maximum of the peak scales with
N
as
N
rather than as
N^{2}
. This suggests a sensitive way of revealing a weak aperiodicity of the pulse sequence and thus characterizing the relevant gate operations.

VIII
The master equation

We now proceed with the derivation of the results for the correlators of the periodically repeated Ramsey measurements. This and the next two sections can be read prior to Secs.
III
and
IV
where there are presented the results of the theory described below.

VIII.1
The Hamiltonian

We consider a qubit, which is coupled to TLSs, that has a fluctuating frequency, and is subjected to the control pulses in Fig.
1
. Its Hamiltonian is
H=H_{\mathrm{q}}+H_{\mathrm{q-TLS}}
. Here
H_{\mathrm{q-TLS}}
is given by Eq. (
9
) and describes the dispersive coupling to the TLSs, whereas
H_{\mathrm{q}}
has the form

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{q}}=-\frac{1}{2}\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle+\frac{\pi}{4}\sigma_{y}\sum_{k}[\delta(t-kt_{\mathrm{cyc}})+\delta(t-kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}-t_{R})].

(52)

The term
\propto\sigma_{y}
in Eq. (
VIII.1
) describes the periodically repeated pairs of Ramsey pulses of rotation about the
y
-axis, whereas
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)
describes the qubit frequency fluctuations due to external classical noise. Here we somewhat conditionally distinguish such classical noise from noise stemming from the coupling to TLSs; see however Appendix
A
where the effect of the TLSs is described by modeling
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)
by a telegraph noise

It is convenient to assume that
\langle\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)\rangle=0
. If
\langle\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)\rangle
were nonzero, it could be interpreted as the detuning of the mean qubit frequency from the frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{ref}}
of the reference signal used in the Ramsey measurements. Such detuning leads to a phase accumulation
\phi_{R}
during a measurement given by Eq. (
8
). It can be controlled in the experiment and can be implemented by incorporating into
H_{\mathrm{q}}
the term

\displaystyle H_{R}=-\frac{1}{2}\phi_{R}\sigma_{z}\sum_{k}\delta(t-kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}-t_{R}^{-}).

(53)

The Hamiltonian
H_{R}
describes rotations of the qubit around the
z
-axis prior to the second Ramsey pulse within a cycle, i.e., the pulse applied at time
kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}
, see Eq. (
VIII.1
) and also Fig.
1
. It mimics the effect of the detuning of the qubit frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{q}}
from the reference frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{ref}}
in the Ramsey measurement, with
\phi_{R}=(\omega_{\mathrm{q}}-\omega_{\mathrm{ref}})t_{R}
, see Eq. (
8
).

We note that, if
\langle\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)\rangle\neq 0
, the qubit frequency measured in the experiment incorporates
\langle\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}\rangle
. It is incremented by
\langle\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}\rangle
compared to the value in the absence of the coupling to the noise source, in particular, in the absence of the coupling to the TLSs. In a way, the nonzero
\langle\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}\rangle
is an artifact of the model. To relate the Hamiltonian
H_{R}
to the frequency detuning in the Ramsey measurement, one has to subtract the increment
\langle\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}\rangle
from the experimental value of
\omega_{\mathrm{q}}
when calculating
\phi_{R}
. This means that the phase
\phi_{R}
in Eq. (
53
) has to be replaced by
\tilde{\phi}_{R}=\phi_{R}-\langle\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)\rangle t_{R}
.

We consider a classical noise where
\langle\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)\rangle=0
. However, the above argument applies also to the noise from the coupling to the TLS, in which case the qubit frequency shift is
\sum_{n}V^{(n)}\langle\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)}\rangle
, cf. Eq. (
12
). On the practical side, one can consider the qubit-TLS coupling of the form

\displaystyle H^{\prime}_{\mathrm{q-TLS}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{z}\sum_{n}V^{(n)}\left(\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)}-\langle\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)}\rangle\right),

(54)

which does not lead to a renormalization of the mean qubit frequency. In this sense it is more relevant from the viewpoint of the experiment. Then in Eq. (
53
) one should use
\phi_{R}
rather than
\tilde{\phi}_{R}
. In particular, in the experiment one should use
\phi_{R}
. The result of the calculation will not change.

To analyze the dephasing due to the dispersive coupling to the TLSs, Eq. (
9
), we write the states of an
n
th TLS as

\ket{0}^{(n)}\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
0\end{array}\right)^{(n)},\qquad\ket{1}^{(n)}\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
1\end{array}\right)^{(n)},

and we use the Pauli operators
\hat{\tau}_{\varkappa}^{(n)}
to describe the TLS dynamics. Here

\varkappa=0,x,y,z
, with
\hat{\tau}_{0}^{(n)}\equiv\hat{I}_{\tau}^{(n)}
being a unit operator.

VIII.2
Dynamics during the Ramsey measurement

We first consider the qubit dynamics during a Ramsey measurement, i.e., in the time interval
nt_{\mathrm{cyc}}<t<nt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}
, cf. Fig.
1
. We assume that, in slow time compared to
1/\omega_{\mathrm{ref}}
, relaxation of the qubit and the TLSs is Markovian. The kinetic equation for the density matrix then has the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=i[\rho,H_{\mathrm{q}}+H_{\mathrm{q-TLS}}]+2\Gamma\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{+}]\rho

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{\phi}\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{z}]\rho+\sum_{n}\mathcal{L}_{TLS}^{(n)}\rho\ ,

(55)

where the last term describes relaxation of the TLSs,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm TLS}^{(n)}\rho=

\displaystyle W_{10}^{(n)}{}\mathcal{D}[\hat{\tau}_{+}^{(n)}]\rho+W_{01}^{(n)}{}\mathcal{D}[\hat{\tau}_{-}^{(n)}]\rho

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{\phi\mathrm{TLS}}^{(n)}{}\mathcal{D}[\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)}]\rho\ .

(56)

We use the conventional notation
\mathcal{D}[F]\rho=F\rho F^{\dagger}-(F^{\dagger}F\rho+\rho F^{\dagger}F)/2
for the relaxation operator;
\sigma_{\pm}=(\sigma_{x}\pm i\sigma_{y})/2,\hat{\tau}_{\pm}^{(n)}=(\hat{\tau}_{x}^{(n)}\pm i\hat{\tau}_{y}^{(n)})/2
.

Parameters
\Gamma
and
\Gamma_{\phi}
describe the qubit decay rate and the rate of dephasing due to fast dephasing processes. They give the familiar parameters of the Bloch equation for the qubit,

T_{1}=1/2\Gamma,\quad T_{2}=1/(\Gamma+\Gamma_{\phi}).

Parameters
W_{ij}^{(n)}
describe the rates of transitions
\ket{i}^{(n)}\to\ket{j}^{(n)}
between the states of an
n
th TLS (
i,j
take on the values
0,1
), whereas
\Gamma_{\phi\mathrm{TLS}}^{(n)}
is the TLS dephasing rate.
To make the TLSs fully incoherent this rate has to be much larger than other relaxation rates. In this case the off-diagonal matrix elements of
\rho
with respect to the TLS states,
{}^{(n)}\!\!\bra{i}\rho\ket{j}^{(n)}
with
i\neq j
, will decay fast and can be disregarded. However, as seen from the analysis below, for the considered dispersive qubit-to-TLS coupling these matrix elements do not affect the outcomes of qubit measurements. Therefore they will not be discussed, i.e., we will consider only the matris elements
{}^{(n)}\!\!\bra{i}\rho\ket{j}^{(n)}
with
i=j
.

In deriving Eq. (
VIII.2
) we assumed that each TLS is coupled to its individual thermal reservoir, that is, not only there is no direct interaction between the TLSs, but there is also no interaction mediated by a common thermal reservoir. In the case of phononic thermal reservoirs, this model goes back to the original papers
Anderson et al. 1972
;
Phillips 1972
.

We assume that at
t=0^{-}
the qubit is in the ground state
\ket{0}
and the TLSs are in their stationary states. From Eq. (
VIII.2
) the stationary density matrix of an
n
th TLS is

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{st}}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\hat{I}^{(n)}+\frac{\Delta W^{(n)}}{W^{(n)}}\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)})\Bigr]\ ,

(57)

\displaystyle W^{(n)}=W_{01}^{(n)}+W_{10}^{(n)},\quad\Delta W^{(n)}=W_{10}^{(n)}-W_{01}^{(n)}\ .

The density matrix of the whole system, the qubit and the TLSs, before the rotation around the
y
-axis at
t=0
is

\rho(t=0^{-})=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{z})\prod_{n}\rho_{\mathrm{st}}^{(n)}.

At
t=0
the qubit is rotated around the
y
-axis, as described by the term
\propto\sigma_{y}
with
n=0
in the Hamiltonian (
VIII.1
). The TLSs are not affected by unitary transformations on the qubit. The density matrix after the transformation becomes

\displaystyle\rho(t=0^{+})=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{x})\prod_{n}\rho_{\mathrm{st}}^{(n)}.

(58)

This equation provides the initial condition for the evolution of the density matrix during the first Ramsey measurement, i.e., in the time interval
0<t<t_{R}
. The solution of Eq. (
VIII.2
) in this time interval can be sought in the form

\displaystyle\rho(t)=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{z})\rho_{I}+\frac{1}{2}e^{-t/T_{1}}\sigma_{z}\rho_{z}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}e^{-t/T_{2}}\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\exp\left[i\alpha\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t^{\prime})\right]\sigma_{\alpha}\rho_{\alpha}

(59)

with the operators
\rho_{\lambda}
defined as

\displaystyle\rho_{\lambda}=\prod_{n}\sum_{\varkappa=0,z}C_{\lambda\varkappa}^{(n)}{}\hat{\tau}_{\varkappa}^{(n)}.

(60)

Here
\lambda=I,z,\pm
enumerates the components of the qubit-dependent part of the density matrix, whereas
\varkappa=0,z
enumerates the TLS operators
\hat{\tau}_{0}^{(n)}\equiv\hat{I}^{(n)}
and
\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)}
. The operators
\rho_{\lambda}
depend only on the TLS variables. The coefficients
C_{\lambda\varkappa}^{(n)}\equiv C_{\lambda\varkappa}^{(n)}(t)
describe the evolution of the density matrix in time.

The components of the density matrix
\rho
that contain
\hat{\tau}_{\pm}^{(n)}
are uncoupled from other components of
\rho
. They do not get coupled by the coupling to the qubit and by the gate operations on the qubit. They decay with the rates
W^{(n)}+\Gamma^{(n)}_{\phi\mathrm{TLS}}
and are not discussed below. This is why
\varkappa
in Eq. (
60
) runs through
0
and
z
only.

The equations for
C_{\lambda\varkappa}^{(n)}
are obtained by substituting Eq. (
VIII.2
) into the full master equation (
VIII.2
), multiplying the left- and right-hand sides in turn by
\hat{I}_{q},\hat{I}_{q}-\sigma_{z},\sigma_{\pm}
, and taking trace over the qubit states. Because the TLSs decay is independent of each other, the resulting equations for
\rho_{\lambda}
separate into equations for individual TLSs (see Appendix
E
). Equations (
VIII.2
) and (
VIII.2
) then reduce to the equation

\displaystyle\sum_{\varkappa}\dot{C}_{\lambda\varkappa}^{(n)}\hat{\tau}_{\varkappa}^{(n)}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TLS}}^{(n)}\sum_{\varkappa}C_{\lambda\varkappa}^{(n)}\hat{\tau}_{\varkappa}^{(n)}

\displaystyle+i\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\delta_{\lambda\alpha}\alpha V^{(n)}{}\left(C_{\alpha 0}^{(n)}\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)}+C_{\alpha z}^{(n)}\hat{\tau}_{0}^{(n)}\right)

(61)

with
\varkappa=0,z
. Multiplying this equation in turn by the TLS operators
\hat{I}^{(n)}
and
\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)}
and taking trace over the TLS states, we obtain equations for each of the coefficients
C_{\lambda\varkappa}^{(n)}
.

The last term in Eq. (
VIII.2
) describes the effect of the coupling to the qubit on the TLS dynamics. This term comes from the components of the density matrix
\rho
, which are proportional to
\sigma_{\pm}
. It determines the accumulation of the phase of the qubit between the Ramsey pulses at
t=0
and
t=t_{R}
.

The initial conditions for
C_{\lambda\varkappa}^{(n)}
follow from Eq. (
58
) and are given by Eq. (
E
) in Appendix
E
. The coefficients
C_{\lambda\varkappa}^{(n)}(t)
in terms of
C_{\lambda\varkappa}^{(n)}(0)
are given in Eqs. (
E
) and (
E
). Using these expressions we find that, by the end of the interval between the Ramsey pulses, i.e., for
t\to t_{R}^{-}
, we have, in particular,

\displaystyle C_{I0}^{(n)}{}(t_{R})=\frac{1}{2},\quad C_{+0}^{(n)}{}(t_{R})=\frac{1}{2}\Xi^{(n)}(t_{R}),

(62)

where function
\Xi_{n}(t_{R})
is given in Eq. (
III.1
). This function describes the effect of the coupling to an
n
th TLS on the probability of the Ramsey measurement outcome.
It depends on the interrelation between the strength of the TLS-to-qubit coupling
V^{(n)}{}
and the TLS relaxation rate
W^{(n)}
, that is, for a TLS to be effectively strongly coupled to the qubit it suffices to have
|V^{(n)}{}|
large compared to the TLS relaxation rate
W^{(n)}
.

The explicit expressions for the coefficients
C_{\lambda\varkappa}^{(n)}(t_{R})
determine the operators
\rho_{\lambda}(t_{R})
, as seen from Eq. (
60
). They thus describe the change of the density matrix
\rho(t)
over the time after the qubit was prepared in the state
(\ket{0}+\ket{1})/\sqrt{2}
and before it is going to be measured.

VIII.3
The probability of a Ramsey measurement outcome

The above results allow us to find the probability
r_{1}
of obtaining “1” as an outcome of the Ramsey measurement. In the Bloch sphere representation, the involved steps include the rotation of the density matrix
\rho(t_{R})
about the
z
-axis by the angle
\tilde{\phi}_{R}
. The corresponding unitary transformation is determined by Eq. (
53
) in which we replace
\phi_{R}
with
\tilde{\phi}_{R}
to allow for the shift of the average qubit frequency due to the coupling to the TLSs.

The rotation about the
z
-axis is followed by the rotation about the
y
-axis by
\pi/2
, as prescribed by the term
(\pi/4)\sigma_{y}\delta(t-t_{R})
in Eq. (
VIII.1
). Finally, the transformed density matrix
\rho(t_{R}^{+})
has to be multiplied by the operator
\hat{\pi}=(\hat{I}_{q}-\sigma_{z})/2
and the trace over the states of the qubit and the TLSs has to be taken along with the averaging over classical noise of the qubit frequency
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}
.

The aforementioned unitary transformations refer to the operators
\sigma_{x,y,z}
in the density matrix
\rho(t)
in Eq. (
VIII.2
). The operators
\rho_{\lambda}(t_{R})
are operators in the space of the TLSs, they are not affected by the gate operations on the qubit at time
t_{R}
, i.e.,
\rho_{\lambda}(t_{R}^{-})=\rho_{\lambda}(t_{R}^{+})=\rho_{\lambda}(t_{R})
(we remind that
\lambda
takes on the values
I,z,\pm
) . In terms of these operators

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}\rho(t_{R}^{+})=\mathfrak{R}(t_{R}^{+})+\hat{m},\quad\mathfrak{R}(t)=\frac{1}{4}(\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{z})

\displaystyle\times\left[\rho_{I}(t)+\frac{1}{2}e^{-t/T_{2}}\sum_{\alpha}e^{i\alpha(\theta+\tilde{\phi}_{R})}\rho_{\alpha}(t)\right]\ .

(63)

Here,
\hat{m}
is a sum of the terms proportional to
\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y}
, and
\sigma_{z}
; therefore
\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat{m}=0
. The term
\theta=\int_{0}^{t_{R}}\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)dt

is the phase accumulated because of slow classical qubit frequency noise. It
does not
include the contribution from the TLSs.

From Eqs. (
62
) and (
VIII.3
) we find that the probability of obtaining “1” in a Ramsey measurement is given by Eq. (
10
). To allow for a classical qubit frequency noise, one has to replace the factor
\exp(i\tilde{\phi}_{R})
in equation Eq. (
10
),

\displaystyle\exp(i\tilde{\phi}_{R})\to\exp(i\tilde{\phi}_{R})\langle e^{i\theta}\rangle\ ,

(64)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
implies averaging over the classical frequency noise. This noise does not affect the dynamics of the TLSs and therefore its effect is described just by an extra factor.

IX
The pair correlation function for the coupling to two-level systems

In this section we discuss the effect of the TLSs on the pair correlation function of the qubit measurements
r_{2}(k)
. To simplify the notations we will set
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}=0
. We start with the correlator for neighboring cycles, i.e.,
r_{2}(1)
, and as we move on we will extend the analysis to
r_{2}(k)
for an arbitrary
k
.

It is clear from the definition (
II
) that finding
r_{2}(1)
involves the following steps. After we have found
\hat{\pi}\rho(t_{R}^{+})
, we have to find how this operator evolves in the time interval from
t_{R}^{+}
to
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
during which the qubit is reset to the ground state
\ket{0}
. At
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
the qubit is rotated to
(\ket{0}+\ket{1})/\sqrt{2}
. We then have to consider the dynamics in the interval from
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{+}
to
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{+}+t_{R}
. At
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}
the qubit is again rotated and the evolved operator
\hat{\pi}\rho(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})
is multiplied by
\hat{\pi}
. The value of
r_{2}(1)
is given by the trace of the result. We will discuss each of these steps separately.

IX.1
Evolution during the reset,
t_{R}<t<t_{\mathrm{cyc}}

The dynamics of the system during the reset of the qubit can be formally described by the master equation (
VIII.2
) written for
\hat{\pi}\rho(t)
. In this equation one can assume that the qubit decay rate
\Gamma
is large,
\Gamma(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}-t_{R})\gg 1
. In this limit the part of
\hat{\pi}\rho(t)
described by the operator
\hat{m}
in Eq. (
VIII.3
) and thus proportional to
\sigma_{x,y,z}
will decay to zero. Therefore of interest is only the evolution of the operator
\mathfrak{R}(t)
in Eq. (
VIII.3
).

In the operator
\mathfrak{R}(t)
the qubit-dependent factor
\hat{I}+\sigma_{z}
does not change. However, the TLSs are not in their stationary states at
t_{R}
, and they keep evolving for
t>t_{R}
, each with its own rate. To describe this evolution it is convenient to first separate out the part
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}
of
\mathfrak{R}(t)
that would describe the system if the TLSs were in the stationary states, i.e., they were described by the density matrices
\rho_{\mathrm{st}}^{(n)}
. Using the explicit expressions (
62
), (
E
), and (
E
) for the operators
\rho_{I},\rho_{\pm}
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}(t)=r_{1}\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}(t)+\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}(t),\quad t_{R}^{+}\leq t<t_{\mathrm{cyc}}\ ,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}(t_{R})=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{z})\prod_{n}\rho_{\mathrm{st}}^{(n)}\ ,

(65)

and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}(t_{R})=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{z})\sum_{s\geq 1}^{N_{\mathrm{TLS}}}\sum_{\{m\}_{s}}\mathbb{K}_{\{m\}_{s}}

\displaystyle\times\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(m_{1})}...\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(m_{s})}\prod_{n\notin\{m\}_{s}}\rho_{\mathrm{st}}^{(n)}\ .

(66)

Here we have introduced sets
\{m\}_{s}
. Their components
m_{1},m_{2},...,m_{s}
enumerate different TLSs. The values of
m_{i}
(
i=1,...,s
) within each set run from 1 to
N_{\mathrm{TLS}}
. The sum

\sum_{\{m\}_{s}}
is taken over all
m_{i}
, for example, one can think of it as

\sum_{\{m\}_{s}}\equiv\sum_{N_{\mathrm{TLS}}\geq m_{1}>m_{2}...>m_{s}\geq 1}.

The parameters
\mathbb{K}_{{m}_{s}}
are given in Appendix
F
.

The form of
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}
can be understood by noting that, to describe the evolution of
\mathfrak{R}(t)
, we have to take into account the decay of all possible combinations of the TLSs. The parameter
s
in Eq. (
IX.1
) gives the number of the TLSs included into a combination,
1\leq s\leq N_{\mathrm{TLS}}
. By construction, the trace of any term in the sum over
s
is equal to zero.

The operator
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}(t)
describes the qubit in its ground state and the TLSs in their stationary states. It is not changed during reset, i.e.

\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{-})=\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}(t_{R}^{+})\ .

In contrast, the TLS operators
\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(m_{i})}
in
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}
exponentially decay with the rates
W^{(m_{i})}
because of the transitions between the states of the TLSs, as seen from Eq. (
VIII.2
). Since the TLSs are independent, over the duration of the reset
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}-t_{R}
each
\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(m_{i})}
in Eq. (
IX.1
) acquires a factor
\exp[-W^{(m_{i})}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}-t_{R})]
, so that at the end of the reset period, i.e., at the end of the cycle the expression for
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{-})
is given by Eq. (
IX.1
) in which one replaces

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(m_{i})}\to\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(m_{i})}\exp[-W^{(m_{i})}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}-t_{R})]\ ,

(67)

for all
m_{i}\in\{m\}_{s}
. Note that the terms
\rho_{\mathrm{st}}^{(n)}
with
n\notin\{m\}_{s}
do not change.

IX.2
The second Ramsey measurement

To find the dynamics of the operator
\mathfrak{R}(t)
in the time interval
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}<t\leq t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+}
we should take into account that at time
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
the qubit undergoes a unitary transformation of rotation around the
y
-axis, as seen from the Hamiltonian (
VIII.1
). Respectively, in the expression for
\mathfrak{R}(t)
the operator
\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{z}
is transformed into
\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{x}
.

IX.2.1
The contribution of the term
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}

The evolution of the operator
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}(t)
after the qubit rotation at
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
is described in the same exact way as it was done in Sec.
VIII.2
for
\rho(t)
. Indeed,
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{+})
has the same form as the density matrix
\rho(0^{+})
, Eq. (
58
), except that
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}
has an extra factor
r_{1}
. Thus the evolution of
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}(t)
in the time interval
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}<t<t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}
is given by Eqs. (
VIII.2
) and (
60
) with the coefficients
C_{\lambda\varkappa}^{(n)}
multiplied by
r_{1}
.

It follows from the above argument that if, after the next Ramsey rotation at
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}
, the transformed
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}
[i.e.,
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})
] is multiplied by
\hat{\pi}
and the trace is taken over the qubit and the TLSs, the result will be
r_{1}^{2}
. This is the contribution of
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}
to
r_{2}(1)
.

Further, to find the contribution of
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}
to
r_{2}(k)
with
k>1
we note that, by applying the decomposition of the density matrix (
VIII.2
) to
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}
, one can write
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})
as

\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})=\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}(t_{R}^{+})+\hat{m}^{\prime}\ ,

where
\hat{m}^{\prime}
is a sum of the terms proportional to
\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y}
, and
\sigma_{z}
.
Evaluating
r_{2}(k)
involves resetting the qubit after each
nt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+}
with
n<k
. After the reset,
\hat{m}^{\prime}
will go to zero. Therefore by the end of the second cycle,
t\to 2t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
, we will have
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}(2t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{-})=\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{-})=\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}(t_{R}^{+})
. The operator
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}
will evolve in the same way during the following cycles. The cycling does not change
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}
, that is
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}(nt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})=\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}(mt_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{-})
for any
m
and
n
. Therefore the contribution of
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{st}}
to the correlator
r_{2}(k)
with
k>1
is the same as for
k=1
. It is equal to
r_{1}^{2}
independent of
k
.

IX.2.2
The contribution of the term
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}

The term
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}
describes the effect of correlations in the TLS dynamics on the outcome of the qubit measurements. To analyze this effect we notice first that the outcome of the qubit rotation at
t=t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{z}\to\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{z}+\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\sigma_{\alpha}-\sigma_{z}\ .

(68)

We saw above that, after the rotation at
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}
, the last term,
\sigma_{z}
, is transformed into the terms that decay on reset; these terms also do not contribute to the trace over the qubit states if multiplied by
\hat{\pi}
. Therefore we will not consider the contribution from the term
\propto\sigma_{z}
in
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{+})
.

As seen from the master equation (
VIII.2
), the terms
\propto(\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{z})\hat{\tau}^{m_{1}}_{z}...\hat{\tau}^{m_{s}}_{z}
in
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{+})
commute with the Hamiltonian and therefore do not lead to mixing of the qubit and TLS states. We denote this part of
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{+})
as
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\prime}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{+})
. Over time
t_{R}
the operator
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\prime}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{+})
will decay as
\exp(-\sum_{i}W^{(m_{i})}t_{R})
. With the account taken of Eq. (
67
), after the Ramsey pulse at
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}
,
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\prime}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})
will have the form of Eq. (
IX.1
) in which
\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{z}
is replaced by
\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{x}
and also there is made a replacement

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(m_{i})}\to\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(m_{i})}\exp(-W^{(m_{i})}t_{\mathrm{cyc}}),\quad m_{i}\in\{m\}_{s}\ .

The trace of
\hat{\pi}\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\prime}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})
over the TLSs is zero, therefore this term will not contribute to
r_{2}(1)
.

However, this term determines the values of
r_{2}(k)
with
k>1
.
To see this we first note that, after the qubit reset at
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+}
, by the time
2t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
the operator
\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{x}
in
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\prime}
will transform into
\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{z}
and there will emerge an extra factor
\exp\Bigl[-\sum_{m_{i}}W^{(m_{i})}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}-t_{R})\Bigr]
in the sum over
\{m\}_{s}
. Thus
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\prime}(2t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{-})
will have the same structure as
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{-})
. It is seen from Eq. (
68
) that this structure will be reproduced from cycle to cycle, so that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\prime}(kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{-})=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{z})\sum_{s\geq 1}\sum_{\{m\}_{s}}\mathbb{K}_{\{m\}_{s}}\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(m_{1})}...\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(m_{s})}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\sum_{m_{i}\in\{m\}_{s}}W^{(m_{i})}(kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}-t_{R})\right]\prod_{n\notin\{m\}_{s}}\rho_{\mathrm{st}}^{(n)}\ ,

(69)

Moreover, it is easy to see that
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}(kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{-})=\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\prime}(kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{-})
provided no measurements are done at
nt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+}
with
n<k
. This is because the terms
\propto\sigma_{\pm}
in
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}
vanish on reset. Indeed, the rotation around the
y
-axis at
nt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}
transforms
\sigma_{\pm}
into
\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z}
with different coefficients, which all decay on reset.

The accumulation of the decay of different TLSs described by Eq. (
IX.2.2
) ultimately determines the correlator
r_{2}(k)
.
However, the very values of
r_{2}(k)
are determined by the terms in
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}(kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{+})
, which are
\propto\sigma^{\pm}
and emerge after the transformation Eq. (
68
). They have to be studied separately for each product of the TLS operators
\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(m_{1})}...\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(m_{s})}
in
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{corr}}
. The analysis is similar to that in Sec.
VIII.2
and is described in Appendix
F
. The result is Eq. (
III.1
) for the centered correlator
\tilde{r}_{2}(k)
.

The described method allows one to calculate higher-order correlators as well. However, the expressions are combersome and will not be provided here. In Appendix
A
we describe an alternative approach to calculating the probability
r_{1}
and the correlator
r_{2}(k)
, which is based on the properties of telegraph noise that drives a qubit and mimics the coupling to the TLSs.

X
Correlators of measurement outcomes for a Gaussian frequency noise

We now consider the effect of Gaussian fluctuations of the qubit frequency
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)
on the probability
r_{1}
of the Ramsey measurement outcomes and their 2- and 3-time correlation functions
r_{2}(k)
,
r_{3}(k,l)
. We will express these probabilities in terms of the correlation functions
f_{k}
of the phases
\theta_{n}
accumulated by the qubit between the Ramsey pulses applied at times
nt_{\mathrm{cyc}}
and
nt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}
with
n=0,1,...
,

\theta_{n}=\int_{nt_{\mathrm{cyc}}}^{nt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}}\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)dt,\quad f_{k}\equiv\langle\theta_{n}\theta_{n+k}\rangle\ ,

cf. Eqs. (
2
). Equation (
IV.1
) relates the correlators
f_{k}
to the power spectrum
S_{q}(\omega)
of noise
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)
. The probability distribution of the phases has the form

\displaystyle P(\{\theta\})=Z^{-1}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m,n}(\hat{f}{}^{-1})_{n-m}\theta_{n}\theta_{m}\right],

\displaystyle\sum_{m}(\hat{f}{}^{-1})_{n-m}f_{m-k}=\delta_{m,k}\ ,

(70)

where
\{\theta\}
is the set of
\theta_{n}
with different
n
, while

is the normalization factor.

The effect of classical noise can be easily described using the master equation approach. One does not have to care about the evolution of the TLS-dependent part of the density matrix, which significantly simplifies the calculation.

We begin with the first cycle that starts at
t=0
. Before there is applied the first Ramsey pulse (the rotation around the
y
-axis by
\pi/2
), the qubit is in the ground state. Its density matrix is

\rho(0^{-})=(\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{z})/2\ .

After the first Ramsey pulse at
t=0
we have
\rho(0^{+})=(\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{x})/2
. The evolution of the system at
0<t<t_{R}
is described by Eq. (
VIII.2
) in which one replaces the TLS operators by the numbers determined by the form of
\rho(0^{+})
, i.e.,
\rho_{I}\to 1,\rho_{z}\to-1,\rho_{\pm}\to 1
.

After the Ramsey pulse at
t=t_{R}
, we have, as seen from Eq. (
VIII.3
),

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}\rho(t_{R}^{+})=\mathfrak{R}_{0}(t_{R}^{+})+\hat{m}_{0},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{0}(t_{R}^{+})=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{z})p(\theta_{0}),

(71)

where the probability
p(\theta)
is given by the standard expression (
7
) and
\hat{m}_{0}
is a sum of terms proportional to the operators
\sigma_{x,y,z}
. Taking a trace over the qubit states and averaging the result over the phases
\theta
using Eq. (
X
) gives Eq. (
IV.1
) for

r_{1}=\langle p(\theta_{0})\rangle\equiv\int p(\theta_{0})\prod_{n}d\theta_{n}P(\{\theta\})

(the integral goes over all
\theta_{n}
, for the correlated phases
\theta_{n}
).

If the trace and the averaging are not done and instead the qubit is reset, the term
\hat{m}
in Eq. (
X
) will decay whereas the operator
\hat{I}_{q}+\sigma_{z}
, and thus
\mathfrak{R}_{0}(t_{r}^{+})

t\to t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
, the operator
\hat{\pi}\rho(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{-})
will become
\hat{\pi}\rho(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{-})=\mathfrak{R}_{0}(t_{R}^{+})=\mathfrak{R}_{0}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{-})=p(\theta_{0})\rho(0^{-})
.

To describe the dynamics during the next cycle we use again Eq. (
VIII.2
). The analysis is identical to that for the previous cycle, except that
\rho(0^{-})
is replaced with
p(\theta_{0})\rho(0^{-})
. After the pair of the Ramsey pulses applied during the cycle we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{0}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})=\mathfrak{R}_{1}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})+\hat{m}_{1},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{1}(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})=\frac{1}{2}p(\theta_{0})\Bigl[\hat{I}_{q}

\displaystyle-\sigma_{z}e^{-t_{R}/T_{2}}\cos(\phi_{R}+\theta_{1})\Bigr],

(72)

where
\hat{m}_{1}
has terms proportional only to
\sigma_{x}
and
\sigma_{y}
. Note that the random phase
\theta_{1}
has been accumulated over the time interval
(t_{\mathrm{cyc}},t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R})
, which is different from the time interval
(0,t_{R})
over which
\theta_{0}
was accumulated.

As a result of the reset during the time interval
(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+},2t_{\mathrm{cyc}})

\mathfrak{R}_{0}(2t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{-})=p(\theta_{0})\rho(0^{-})

k

X

\theta_{1}
will be replaced by
\theta_{k}
.

To find the pair correlator
r_{2}(k)

\mathfrak{R}_{0}(kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})

\hat{\pi}

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}\mathfrak{R}_{0}(kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})=\mathfrak{R}_{2}(kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})+\hat{m}_{2},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{2}(kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})=\frac{1}{2}\hat{I}_{q}p(\theta_{0})p(\theta_{k})\ .

Here again

, and
\sigma_{z}

Taking a trace over the qubit variables and averaging the result over the correlated phases

IV.1

\mathfrak{R}_{2}(kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}^{+})

\mathfrak{R}_{2}[(k+1)t_{\mathrm{cyc}}^{-}]=p(\theta_{0})p(\theta_{k})\rho(0^{-})\ ,

XI
Conclusions

This paper describes several features of slow qubit frequency fluctuations that allow one to characterize the mechanism of the fluctuations. Of primary interest are fluctuations with the correlation time that exceeds the decoherence time of the qubit due to its decay and dephasing by fast processes. The approach is based on periodically repeated Ramsey measurements. In such measurements one can vary the duration of the single measurement
t_{R}
and the period of the measurements
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
. One can find the probability
r_{1}
of observing “one” as a measurement outcome, and the two- and three-time correlation functions
r_{2}(k)
and
r_{3}(k,l)
of repeatedly observing “one” over time
kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}
, and
kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}
and
lt_{\mathrm{cyc}}
.

We have developed a fairly general analytical approach which allowed us to describe the qubit dynamics in the presence of an evolving noise with the account taken of the gate operations involved in the repeated measurements. This approach enabled calculating the probabilities
r_{1}
and
r_{2}(k)
for the dispersive coupling to the TLSs in the explicit form, and also finding
r_{1,2,3}
for a Gaussian noise.
The results cover a broad range of the noise characteristics. For the TLSs, those are the strength of the coupling to the qubit (in the units of frequency) compared to
t_{R}^{-1}
and to the TLSs switching rates, the difference of the switching rates between the TLSs’ states, as well as the actual number of the TLSs that contribute to the noise. For Gaussian noise these are the noise intensity and the noise power spectra.

A distinguishing feature of a Gaussian noise is the relation between the correlators. As we show, once
r_{1}
and
r_{2}(k)
have been measured, they determine the form of
r_{3}(k,l)
. We find the corresponding relation. If it does not hold, the noise is non-Gaussian. We also find analytically and confirm by simulations the form of
r_{2}(k)
for several important types of noise, in particular for the noise with the power spectrum of the form of a Lorentzian peak at zero frequency and for a
1/f
noise with a soft low-frequency cutoff. The cutoff we used is characteristic of the noise that results from the dispersive coupling to a large number of symmetric TLSs with the log-normal distribution of the switching rates.

The analytical results and the results of the simulations show explicitly the sensitivity of the correlators to various noise parameters, which we illustrate in the figures throughout the paper. We also show that the power spectrum of the measurement outcomes allows one to reveal a slow periodic modulation of the qubit frequency. The emerging narrow spectral peak is very sensitive to the periodicity of the measurements.

An advantageous feature of the proposed method is that it accounts for the evolution of the qubit step by step during and between the gate operations. Therefore it can be immediately extended to allow for gate errors and for measurement errors.

Along with long measurement sequences, one can also study the distribution of the instances of observing “one” in a relatively small number of measurements
M
(still
M\gg 1
), provided the total duration of the data acquisition
Mt_{\mathrm{cyc}}
is comparable to the noise correlation time. As we show analytically in the limiting cases and by simulations in the general case, this distribution can be significantly different from the conventional binomial distribution. In the case of coupling to slow TLSs, the distribution can have a fine structure that corresponds to the TLSs mostly staying in their initially occupied states during the data acquisition. On the other hand, even where
Mt_{\mathrm{cyc}}
largely exceeds the noise correlation time, the distribution of the instances of observing “one” is significantly broadened by the noise correlations compared to the binomial distribution. This is a simple test of the presence of noise correlations.

The noise from TLSs is often assumed to be the cause of slow fluctuations of the qubit frequency. The presented analysis provides a tool for testing this assumptions in various types of qubits. We believe that the developed methods can be extended to other types of noise of potential interest. In particular, this refers to a shot noise. In a way, this paper is the first step toward creating a “map” of the effects of the mechanisms of different types of slow qubit frequency fluctuations.

Appendix A
Effect of a telegraph noise on periodically repeated Ramsey measurements

In this section, we discuss an alternative method of deriving our major result in Eqs. (
10
) and (
III.1
) for the effect of the coupling to the TLSs on the probability of a Ramsey measurement outcome and the correlator of the outcomes. We describe this effect as resulting from classical telegraph noise
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)
that is added to the qubit frequency. Noise comes from random uncorrelated switching of the TLSs between their two states
\ket{0}^{(n)}
and
\ket{1}^{(n)}
.

The method is based on the relation (
IV.1
) between the sought parameters
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
and the random phases

\theta_{k}=\int_{kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}}^{kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}}\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)dt

accumulated during the
k
th Ramsey measurement. The idea is to relate
r_{1},r_{2}
to the characteristic function of the phases
\{\theta_{k}
}. We note that for the telegraph noise, generally,
\langle\theta_{k}\rangle\neq 0
.

The characteristic function is defined as the average over random phases
\theta_{k}
,

\displaystyle\Phi(\vec{q})=\langle e^{i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{\theta}}\rangle,

(75)

where we consider the values
\theta_{0},\theta_{1},...
and numbers
q_{1},q_{2},...
as components of vectors
\vec{\theta}=(\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...)
and
\vec{q}=(q_{0},q_{1},q_{2}...)
.

From Eq. (
7
),
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
can be written in terms of the characteristic function as

\displaystyle r_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}e^{-t_{R}/T_{2}}\mathrm{Re}[e^{i\tilde{\phi}_{R}}\Phi(q_{0}=1,q_{k\neq 0}=0)],

(76)

\displaystyle r_{2}(k)

\displaystyle=r_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{8}e^{-2t_{R}/T_{2}}\left\{\Phi(q_{0}=-1,q_{k}=1,q_{k^{\prime}\neq 0,k}=0)-|\Phi(q_{0}=1,q_{k\neq 0}=0)|^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\mathrm{Re}\left[e^{2i\tilde{\phi}_{R}}\Phi(q_{0}=1,q_{k}=1,q_{k^{\prime}\neq 0,k}=0)-e^{2i\tilde{\phi}_{R}}\Phi^{2}(q_{0}=1,q_{k\neq 0}=0)\right]\right\}.

(77)

In what follows we derive explicit expressions for the characteristic function when the qubit frequency is subject to telegraph noise.

A.1
Characteristic function for one TLS

We first consider the case where the qubit is coupled to one classical TLS, i.e.,
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)=V^{(n)}\tau_{z}^{(n)}(t)
. Here
\tau_{z}^{(n)}(t)
is a classical random variable, telegraph noise that takes values
\pm 1
depending on whether the considered
n
th TLS is in the state
\ket{0}^{(n)}
or
\ket{1}^{(n)}
, i.e.,
\tau_{z}^{(n)}
is the eigenvalue of
\hat{\tau}_{z}^{(n)}
on the corresponding states. It follows from the definition of
\theta_{n}

that the characteristic function in Eq. (
75
) can be written in the form

\displaystyle\Phi(\vec{q})=\langle\exp\left[i\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha(t)\tau_{z}^{(n)}(t)dt\right]\rangle,

(78)

where
\alpha(t)
is a piecewise-constant function of time and is only non-zero in between the two Ramsey pulses within each cycle,

\displaystyle\alpha(t)=q_{k}V^{(n)},\quad kt_{\rm seq}\leq t\leq kt_{\rm seq}+t_{R}.

(79)

whereas
\alpha(t)=0
for
kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}\leq t\leq(k+1)t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
.

A.1.1
Auxiliary functions

Telegraph noise
\tau_{z}^{(n)}(t)
is a Markov process. The Markovian property and the feature that noise takes the values
\pm 1
allow one to derive an important relation
Gurvitz 2019
, which extends to asymmetric TLSs that was previously obtained in
Shapiro and Loginov 1978
for symmetric TLSs,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle\tau_{z}^{(n)}(t)F[\tau_{z}^{(n)}]\rangle=-W^{(n)}\langle\tau_{z}^{(n)}(t)F[\tau_{z}^{(n)}]\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\tau_{z}^{(n)}(t)\frac{d}{dt}F[\tau_{z}^{(n)}]\rangle+\Delta W^{(n)}\langle F[\tau_{z}^{(n)}]\rangle,

(80)

where
F[\tau_{z}^{(n)}]
is an arbitrary function of
t
and an arbitrary functional of
\tau_{z}^{(n)}(t^{\prime})
for
0\leq t^{\prime}\leq t
, with
t=0
being the moment of imposing initial conditions. The last term in Eq. (
A.1.1
) can be understood by noting that
\langle\tau_{z}^{(n)}\rangle=\Delta W^{(n)}/W^{(n)}
.

We will use the relation (
A.1.1
) to reduce the calculation of the characteristic function
\Phi(\vec{q})
to a set of ordinary differential equations. To this end
we introduce the following functions:

\displaystyle\chi^{(n)}(t)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\exp\left[i\int_{0}^{t}\alpha(t^{\prime})\tau_{z}^{(n)}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\right]\right\rangle,

(81)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{(n)}(t)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\tau_{z}^{(n)}(t)\exp\left[i\int^{t}_{0}\alpha(t^{\prime})\tau_{z}^{(n)}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\right]\right\rangle.

(82)

From Eq. (
A.1.1
), functions
\chi^{(n)}(t)
and
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}(t)
satisfy a system of coupled differential equations,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\chi^{(n)}(t)

\displaystyle=i\alpha(t)\mathcal{X}^{(n)}(t),

(83)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{X}^{(n)}(t)

\displaystyle=-W^{(n)}\mathcal{X}^{(n)}(t)+i\alpha(t)\chi^{(n)}(t)

(84)

\displaystyle+\Delta W^{(n)}\chi^{(n)}(t).

where we used that
\tau_{z}^{(n)}{}^{2}=1
.

A.1.2
One-time characteristic function

With Eqs. (
83
) and (
84
) in hand, we are ready to derive the expression for
\Phi(q_{0}=1,q_{k\neq 0}=0)
in Eq. (
76
), which we refer to as the one-time characteristic function.

It follows from Eqs. (
78
) and (
81
) that, to compute
\Phi(q_{0}=1,q_{k\neq 0}=0)
, we simply need to compute
\chi^{(n)}(t)
assuming that
\alpha(t)=V^{(n)}
for
0\leq t\leq t_{R}
and
\alpha(t)=0
for
t>t_{R}
. For such
\alpha(t)
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi(q_{0}=1,q_{k\neq 0}=0)=\chi^{(n)}(t_{R}).

(85)

Solving Eqs. (
83
) and (
84
) in the interval
0\leq t\leq t_{R}
with
\alpha(t)=V^{(n)}
, we find

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\chi^{(n)}(t_{R})\\
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}(t_{R})\end{pmatrix}=\hat{T}(V^{(n)},t_{R})\begin{pmatrix}\chi^{(n)}(0)\\
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}(0)\end{pmatrix},\qquad\begin{pmatrix}\chi^{(n)}(0)\\
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}(0)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\Delta W^{(n)}/W^{(n)}\end{pmatrix},

(86)

\displaystyle\hat{T}(V^{(n)},t_{R})=\frac{1}{\gamma^{(n)}}\exp[-W^{(n)}t_{R}/2]

\displaystyle\times\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}W^{(n)}\sinh(\gamma^{(n)}t_{R})+\gamma^{(n)}\cosh(\gamma^{(n)}t_{R})&iV^{(n)}\sinh(\gamma^{(n)}t_{R})\\
(iV^{(n)}+\Delta W^{(n)})\sinh(\gamma^{(n)}t_{R})&\gamma^{(n)}\cosh(\gamma^{(n)}t_{R})-\frac{1}{2}W^{(n)}\sinh(\gamma^{(n)}t_{R})\end{pmatrix}.

(87)

Here we have introduced the transfer matrix
\hat{T}(V^{(n)},t_{R})
which will be useful below. Carrying out the matrix multiplication in Eq. (
86
), we obtain

\displaystyle\chi^{(n)}(t_{R})=\Xi^{(n)}(t_{R})

(88)

where
\Xi^{(n)}(t_{R})
is given by Eq. (
III.1
). Equations (
76
), (
85
), and (
88
) give the expression for
r_{1}
, which coincides with Eq. (
10
) for the case of one TLS.

A.1.3
Two-time characteristic function

We now evaluate
\Phi(q_{0}=\pm 1,q_{k}=1,q_{k^{\prime}\neq k,0})
in Eq. (
77
), which we refer to as the two-time characteristic functions. Again, we start with the case of the coupling to one TLS.
To this end, we need to solve Eqs. (
83
) and (
84
) for
\alpha(t)
of the form:

\displaystyle\alpha(t)

\displaystyle=\pm V^{(n)},\quad 0\leq t\leq t_{R},

\displaystyle\alpha(t)

\displaystyle=0,\quad t_{R}<t<kt_{\rm seq},

\displaystyle\alpha(t)

\displaystyle=V^{(n)},\quad kt_{\rm seq}\leq t\leq kt_{\rm seq}+t_{R}.

(89)

The relevant two-time characteristic functions in Eq. (
77
) are then expressed in terms of
\chi^{(n)}(t)
as

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}(q_{0}=\pm 1,q_{k}=1,q_{k^{\prime}\neq k,0})=\chi^{(n)}_{\pm}(kt_{\rm seq}+t_{R}),

(90)

where the subscript
\pm
corresponds to
\chi^{(n)}(t)
calculated for
\alpha(t)=\pm V^{(n)}
in the time interval
0\leq t\leq t_{R}
, respectively.

The function
\chi^{(n)}_{\pm}(kt_{\rm seq}+t_{R})
can be found using the transfer matrix in Eq. (
86
) to connect the solutions of
\chi^{(n)}(t)
in different regions where
\alpha(t)
is a constant. The solution is reduced just to matrix multiplication,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\chi^{(n)}_{\pm}(kt_{\rm seq}+t_{R})\\
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}(kt_{\rm seq}+t_{R}))\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\hat{T}(V^{(n)},t_{R})\hat{T}(0,kt_{\rm seq})\hat{T}(\pm V^{(n)},t_{R})\begin{pmatrix}\chi^{(n)}(0)\\
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}(0)\end{pmatrix}.

(91)

This gives

\displaystyle\chi^{(n)}_{+}(kt_{\rm seq}+t_{R})=[\Xi^{(n)}(t_{R})]^{2}+[\xi_{k}^{(n)}(t_{R})]^{2},

\displaystyle\chi^{(n)}_{-}(kt_{\rm seq}+t_{R})=|\Xi^{(n)}(t_{R})|^{2}+|\xi_{k}^{(n)}(t_{R})|^{2},

(92)

where
\xi_{k}^{(n)}(t_{R})
is given in Eq. (
III.1
). Substituting Eqs. (
90
) and (
A.1.3
) into Eq. (
77
) we obtain for the pair correlator
r_{2}(k)
the same expression as Eq. (
III.1
) written for the case of coupling to one TLS.

A.2
Characteristic function in the presence of multiple TLSs

Having found the characteristic function in the presence of one TLS, let us consider multiple independent TLSs. Qubit frequency noise is now a sum over the TLSs,
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}=\sum_{n}V^{(n)}\tau_{z}^{(n)}(t)
.

A key advantage of using the characteristic function is that, in the presence of many independent TLSs coupled to the qubit, it factors into a product of the characteristic functions for individual TLSs. Specifically, the one-time characteristic function now becomes

\displaystyle\Phi(q_{0}=1,q_{k\neq 0}=0)=\prod_{n}\chi^{(n)}(t_{R}),

(93)

with
\chi^{(n)}(t_{R})
given in Eq. (
88
). Similarly, the expression for the two-time characteristic function reads

\displaystyle\Phi(q_{0}=\pm 1,q_{k}=1,q_{k^{\prime}\neq k,0})=\prod_{n}\chi_{\pm}^{(n)}(kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}),

(94)

with
\chi_{\pm}^{(n)}(kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R})
given in Eq. (
A.1.3
).

Substituting Eqs. (
93
) and (
94
) into Eqs. (
76
) and (
77
), we immediately obtain the same expressions for
r_{1}
and
\tilde{r}_{2}(k)
as Eqs. (
10
) and (
III.1
).

Appendix B
Simulations

In this section we described the algorithms used in the simulations of qubit frequency noise induced by two-level systems and of Gaussian qubit frequency noise.

B.1
Simulating noise from the TLSs

Noise from the TLSs was simulated as a sum of telegraph noises produced by each TLS independently. An
n
th TLS has two states,
\ket{0}^{(n)}
and
\ket{1}^{(n)}
, in which its contributions to noise are
1
and
-1
, respectively. These contributions are multiplied by the parameter
V^{(n)}
of the coupling to the qubit to obtain the qubit frequency shift. The time was discretized with the same step
\delta t
for all TLSs. In the simulations we used
\delta t/t_{R}=0.1
, where
t_{R}
is the duration of the Ramsey measurement. As everywhere else, we used the relative length of the cycle
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}/t_{R}=3

At each time step a TLS can switch between its states
\ket{0}^{(n)}
and
\ket{1}^{(n)}
. The switching probabilities are

\displaystyle p_{01}^{(n)}=W_{01}^{(n)}\delta t,\qquad p_{10}^{(n)}=W_{10}^{(n)}\delta t\ .

(95)

where
W_{01}^{(n)}
and
W_{10}^{(n)}
are the switching rates. We determined numerically whether the TLS switches or not in a standard way by comparing
W_{ij}^{(n)}\delta t
with a random number from the uniform distribution
U(0,1)
.

The generated states of the TLS produce an array
d^{(n)}(m)
of random numbers that take values
\pm 1
. Here
m
enumerates the time steps. We collect
N=10^{5}
outcomes of the simulated Ramsey measurements , which means that we use
\tilde{N}=(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}/\delta t)\times 10^{5}
samples, that is,
1\leq m\leq\tilde{N}
. A
k
th Ramsey measurement, which is done in the time interval
kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}\leq t\leq kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}
, corresponds to the range of steps
k(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}/\delta t)<m\leq k(t_{\mathrm{cyc}}/\delta t)+(t_{R}/\delta t)
.
For our discretized time sequence, the random phase accumulated by the qubit in this time interval is

\displaystyle\theta{}_{k}=\sum_{m=1}^{\lceil t_{R}/\delta t\rceil}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{TLS}}}V^{(n)}d^{(n)}(\lfloor kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}/\delta t\rfloor+m).

(96)

We used the probability of obtaining “1” in a
k
th measurement
p(\theta_{k})
as given by Eq. (
7
). We compared
p(\theta_{k})
with a random numbers
o_{k}
from
U(0,1)
. If
p(\theta_{k})>o_{k}
, we set the outcome of the
k
th Ramsey measurement to
x_{k}=1
, otherwise we set
x_{k}=0
.
The whole procedure was independently repeated 300 times for statistical averaging. This allowed us to numerically analyze and compare the parameters
r_{1},r_{2}(k),r_{3}(k,l)
with the theory, as well as to investigate other parameters of interest, as discussed in the main text.

Of primary interest to us was the analysis of the TLSs that produce noise of the
1/f
type in a reasonably broad frequency range. There are many ways to obtain such noise. The results presented in the main text refer to the noise in which the coupling of the TLSs to the qubit was the same for all TLSs,
V^{(n)}=V
, but the distribution of the switching rates
W^{(n)}=W_{01}^{(n)}+W_{10}^{(n)}
was log-normal. We also simulated other types of the TLSs. They lead to qualitatively similar results.

B.2
Simulating Gaussian noise

The effect of a Gaussian noise on the outcomes of Ramsey measurements is fully characterized by the correlation function
f_{k}=\langle\theta_{n}\theta_{n+k}\rangle
of the phases
\theta{}_{k}
acquired by the qubit in the measurements. Since the measurements are periodically repeated, one has to sample
\theta{}_{k}
for successive
k
. The probability to have a given phase
\theta_{k}
depends on the entire history of the previously “observed” phases
\theta_{k^{\prime}}
with
k^{\prime}<k
. This means that the quantity of interest is the conditional probability

\displaystyle P(\theta{}_{k}|\theta{}_{0},...,\theta{}_{k-1})=P(\theta{}_{0},...,\theta{}_{k})

\displaystyle\times[P(\theta{}_{0},...,\theta{}_{k-1})]^{-1}

(97)

where
\theta_{0}
is the outcome of the first measurement.
The probability (
B.2
) has to be evaluated recursively starting with the probability of
\theta_{0}
. We will use that the distribution of the phases is stationary,

P(\{\theta\})=Z^{-1}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}(\hat{f}{}^{-1})_{k-k^{\prime}}\theta_{k}\theta_{k^{\prime}}\right],

where
Z
is the normalization constant and
\hat{f}{}^{-1}
is the matrix reciprocal to the matrix
f_{kk^{\prime}}
; we note that the matrix elements of the latter matrix are
f_{kk^{\prime}}=f_{|k-k^{\prime}|}
, cf. Eq. (
X
).

In evaluating the conditional probability given by Eq. (
B.2
) one should keep in mind that the values of
\theta{}_{k}
are correlated at a finite distance
k_{\mathrm{corr}}
, which is determined by the relation between the correlation time of the underlying noise and the period of the sequence
t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
. In other words, it means that, to a good approximation (which needs to be checked)
|f_{k}|
can be set equal to zero for
k>k_{\mathrm{corr}}
. Then for
k>k_{\mathrm{corr}}
one can approximate the conditional probability
P(\theta{}_{k}|\theta{}_{0},...,\theta{}_{k-1})
with
P(\theta{}_{k}|\theta{}_{k-k_{\mathrm{corr}}},\theta{}_{k-k_{\mathrm{corr}}+1},...,\theta{}_{k-1})
, i.e., instead of the probability (
B.2
) we have to calculate

\displaystyle P(\theta{}_{k}|\theta{}_{k-k_{\mathrm{corr}}},...,\theta{}_{k-1})=P(\theta{}_{k-k_{\mathrm{corr}}},...,\theta{}_{k})

\displaystyle\times[P(\theta{}_{k-k_{\mathrm{corr}}},...,\theta{}_{k-1})]^{-1}.

(98)

It is important that all conditional probabilities in Eq. (
B.2
) have a Gaussian form, albeit they are not zero-mean, because of the correlations. As we now show, we can sample each
\theta{}_{k}
from
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k},\sigma_{k})
, where
\mu_{k}
and
\sigma_{k}
are respectively the mean value and the standard deviation for
\theta_{k}
. They depend on the values of
\theta_{k^{\prime}}
with
k^{\prime}<k
.

The first phase to be sampled,
\theta{}_{0}
, is sampled with
\mu_{0}=0
and
\sigma_{0}=1/\sqrt{f_{0}}
. To find the distribution of the phases
\theta{}_{k}
with
k>0
we note that, when calculating
P(\theta{}_{k-k_{\mathrm{corr}}},...,\theta{}_{k})
, rather than using the full reciprocal matrix
(\hat{f}{}^{-1})_{|k-k^{\prime}|}
we should use a
(k_{\mathrm{corr}}+1)\times(k_{\mathrm{corr}}+1)
matrix reciprocal to the
(k_{\mathrm{corr}}+1)\times(k_{\mathrm{corr}}+1)
part of the matrix
f_{kk^{\prime}}
. This matrix
\Psi_{kk^{\prime}}
is defined by the equation

\sum_{m=k-k_{\mathrm{corr}}}^{k}\Psi_{km}f_{mk^{\prime}}=\delta_{kk^{\prime}},

Along with
\Psi_{kk^{\prime}}
we need the matrix
\psi_{kk^{\prime}}
, which is the reciprocal of the
k_{\mathrm{corr}}\times k_{\mathrm{corr}}
part of
f_{kk^{\prime}}
,

\sum_{m=k-k_{\mathrm{corr}}}^{k-1}\psi_{km}f_{mk^{\prime}}=\delta_{kk^{\prime}}.

The matrices
\Psi_{kk^{\prime}}
and
\psi_{kk^{\prime}}
are symmetric. However, even though
f_{kk}=f_{0}
is independent of
k
, the diagonal matrix elements of the matrices
\Psi_{kk}
and
\psi_{kk}
depend on
k
.

There is an important relation between the matrices
\hat{\psi}
and
\hat{\Psi}
,

\displaystyle\psi_{kk^{\prime}}=\Psi_{kk^{\prime}}-(\Psi_{mk}\Psi_{mk^{\prime}}/\Psi_{mm})

\displaystyle k,k^{\prime}=m-k_{\mathrm{corr}},\ldots,m-1;

(99)

This relation can be checked by multiplying from the left by
f_{k_{1}k}
and summing over
k=m-k_{\mathrm{corr}},\ldots,m-1
.

Taking the relation (
B.2
) into account, we can write the exponential in
P(\theta{}_{k-k_{\mathrm{corr}}},...,\theta{}_{k})
as

\displaystyle\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\Psi_{kk}(\theta{}_{k}-\mu_{k})^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=k-k_{\mathrm{corr}}}^{k-1}\psi_{mm^{\prime}}\theta{}_{m}\theta{}_{m^{\prime}}\right],

where

\displaystyle\mu_{k}=-\Psi_{kk}^{-1}\sum_{m=k-k_{\mathrm{corr}}}^{k-1}\Psi_{km}\theta{}_{m}

(100)

is the phase accumulated over
k_{\mathrm{corr}}
steps that preceded the
k
th step.

Ultimately, we have for the conditional probability of
\theta{}_{k}
the distribution

\displaystyle P(\theta{}_{k}|\theta{}_{k-k_{\mathrm{corr}}},\theta{}_{k-k_{\mathrm{corr}}+1},...,\theta{}_{k-1})

\displaystyle=(\Psi_{kk}/2\pi)^{1/2}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\Psi_{kk}(\theta{}_{k}-\mu_{k})^{2}\right]

(101)

We have also used here the Cramer rule that relates the matrix element
\Psi_{kk}
to the ratio of the determinants of the matrices
\hat{\Psi}
and
\hat{\psi}
.

The above prescription allowed us to sample a sequence of random phases
\theta{}_{k}
. Each obtained
\theta{}_{k}
was used to determine whether the outcome of the simulated Ramsey measurement gives “0” or “1” based on the probability (
7
). From the observed outcomes, we could calculate
r_{1},r_{2}(k)
, and
r_{3}(k,l)
as well as other statistical characteristics of the simulated sequence of periodic Ramsey measurements.

The value of
k_{\mathrm{corr}}
depends on a particular type of noise. We chose it in such a way that the results became virtually independent of the choice. For exponentially correlated frequency noise with the correlation time
\tau_{\mathrm{corr}}
one can choose
k_{\mathrm{corr}}=a_{\mathrm{corr}}\tau_{\mathrm{corr}}/t_{\mathrm{cyc}}
with a sufficiently large
a_{\mathrm{corr}}
. For the
1/f
-type noise we studied, with the characteristic minimal frequency
\omega_{\min}
, one can choose
k_{\mathrm{corr}}=a_{1/f}(\omega_{\min}t_{\mathrm{cyc}})^{-1}
with a sufficiently large
a_{1/f}
. However, for the parameters used in the simulations it was checked that the results become independent of
k_{\mathrm{corr}}
once one sets
k_{\mathrm{corr}}\approx 10
for the exponentially correlated noise and
k_{\mathrm{corr}}=400
for the
1/f
noise.

Appendix C
Power spectrum of the TLS-induced frequency fluctuations

The power spectrum of the qubit frequency fluctuations due to the coupling to TLSs becomes particularly simple if the TLSs are symmetric, the coupling is the same for all TLSs, and the distribution of the switching rates is log-normal, i.e., the probability density of having a given switching rate
W
is
\propto 1/W
. Such a distribution is natural if one thinks of a uniform distribution of the barriers which the TLSs have to overcome in switching. For a finite set of the TLSs the log-normal distribution is mimicked by the switching probabilities of the form

W^{{}^{(n)}}t_{R}=\exp\left(-\alpha(n+n_{0})\right),

(102)

where
\alpha
and
n_{0}
are control parameters. In the simulations we used
\alpha=0.75
and considered
n_{0}=0,5
or
7
.

The selected switching rates correspond to the power spectrum
S_{q}(\omega)
, which is a superposition of Lorentzian peaks given by Eq. (
V.1
). This superposition provides a
1/f
-type behavior. It is clearly seen in Fig.
7
already for 10 TLSs, in which case the
1/f
spectrum spreads over two decades in frequency. The range of
1/f
-type behavior can be extended by altering
\alpha
and
n_{0}
or by including more TLSs. The inset in Fig.
7
shows that, for 20 TLSs,
1/f
-behavior spreads over five decades.

x_{n}

m
ones in
M
measurements is

\langle m/M\rangle\equiv M^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{M}x_{n}\right]=r_{1},

(105)

\displaystyle\sigma_{M}^{2}=\langle(m/M)^{2}\rangle-r_{1}^{2}\equiv M^{-2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{n=1}^{M}x_{n}\right)^{2}\right]-r_{1}^{2}

(106)

Mt_{\mathrm{cyc}}
is much larger than the correlation time of the noise one should replace in the second line
M-k
with
M
.

r_{1}(1-r_{1})/M
in Eq. (
D
) is the standard variance of the binomial distribution. It falls off as
M^{-1}

Appendix E
TLS dynamics during the Ramsey measurement

We consider here the dynamics of the TLS-dependent components of the density matrix during the Ramsey measurement, i.e., in the time intervals
nt_{\mathrm{cyc}}<t<nt_{\mathrm{cyc}}+t_{R}
. These components,
\rho_{\lambda}
with
\lambda=I,z,\pm
, are defined in Eqs. (
VIII.2
) and (
VIII.2
). As indicated in the main text, the equations for
\rho_{\lambda}
with different
\lambda
are obtained by substituting Eq. (
VIII.2
) into the full master equation (
VIII.2
), multiplying the left- and right-hand sides by
\hat{I},\hat{I}_{q}-\sigma_{z},\sigma_{\pm}
, and taking trace over the qubit states. The left-hand side of the resulting equation for
\rho_{\lambda}
is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{\lambda}=\sum_{n}\sum_{\varkappa=0,z}\dot{C}_{\lambda\varkappa}^{(n)}{}\hat{\tau}_{\varkappa}^{(n)}\prod_{m\neq n}\sum_{\varkappa^{\prime}=0,z}C_{\lambda\varkappa^{\prime}}^{(m)}\hat{\tau}_{\varkappa^{\prime}}^{(m)}.

(107)

As seen from Eq. (
VIII.2
, the term
\sum_{n}\mathcal{L}^{(n)}\rho_{\lambda}
in the right-hand side of the equation for
\partial_{t}\rho_{\lambda}
has the same structure as Eq. (
107
): a sum over
n
multiplied by the product over
m\neq n
of
\sum_{\varkappa^{\prime}=0,z}C_{\lambda\varkappa^{\prime}}^{(m)}\hat{\tau}_{\varkappa^{\prime}}^{(m)}
. The commutator
[\rho,H_{\mathrm{q-TLS}}]
also has the same structure. One can divide the both sides of the equation for
\partial_{t}\rho_{\lambda}
by
\rho_{\lambda}
, reminiscent of the standard trick of separation of variables in a differential equation. This gives equations (
VIII.2
).

The equations for
C_{\lambda\varkappa}^{(n)}
are split into sets of pairs of coupled equations. For
\lambda=0,z
we have

\displaystyle\dot{C}_{\lambda 0}^{(n)}=0,\quad\dot{C}_{\lambda z}^{(n)}=\Delta W^{(n)}{}C_{\lambda 0}^{(n)}{}-W^{(n)}{}C_{\lambda z}^{(n)}{}\ ,

\displaystyle\Delta W^{(n)}{}=W_{10}^{(n)}{}-W_{01}^{(n)}{}\ .

(108)

The parameter

\Delta W^{(n)}{}
characterizes the asymmetry of the TLS, whereas
W^{(n)}{}=W_{10}^{(n)}{}+W_{01}^{(n)}{}
is the TLS relaxation rate.

If
\lambda=+
, Eq. (
VIII.2
) for
C_{\lambda\varkappa}^{(n)}
reads

\displaystyle\dot{C}_{+0}^{(n)}{}=iV^{(n)}{}C_{+z}^{(n)}{},

\displaystyle\dot{C}_{+z}^{(n)}{}=(\Delta W^{(n)}{}+iV^{(n)}{})C_{+0}^{(n)}{}-W^{(n)}{}C_{+z}^{(n)}{}\ ,

(109)

and by construction,
C_{-\varkappa}^{(n)}=C_{+\varkappa}^{(n)}{}^{*}
.

Solving these equations, we obtain for
\lambda=I,z

\displaystyle C_{\lambda z}^{(n)}{}(t)=\left[C_{\lambda z}^{(n)}{}(0^{+})-\frac{\Delta W^{(n)}{}}{W^{(n)}{}}C_{\lambda 0}^{(n)}{}(0^{+})\right]e^{-W^{(n)}{}t}

\displaystyle+\frac{\Delta W^{(n)}{}}{W^{(n)}{}}C_{\lambda 0}^{(n)}{}(0^{+}),\quad C_{\lambda 0}^{(n)}{}(t)=C_{\lambda 0}^{(n)}{}(0^{+})\ ,

(110)

whereas

\displaystyle C_{+\varkappa}^{(n)}{}(t)=\sum_{k=1,2}B_{\varkappa}^{(k;n)}\exp(\nu_{k}^{(n)}{}t),

\displaystyle\nu_{1,2}^{(n)}{}=-\frac{1}{2}W^{(n)}{}\pm\gamma^{(n)},

\displaystyle\gamma^{(n)}=\frac{1}{2}\left[(W^{(n)}{})^{2}+4iV^{(n)}{}(\Delta W^{(n)}{}+iV^{(n)}{})\right]^{1/2}\ ,

(111)

here

\displaystyle B_{0}^{(k;n)}=[\nu_{3-k}^{(n)}C_{+0}^{(n)}{}(0^{+})-iV^{(n)}{}C_{+z}^{(n)}{}(0^{+})]

\displaystyle\times(\nu_{3-k}^{(n)}{}-\nu_{k}^{(n)}{})^{-1},

\displaystyle B_{z}^{(k;n)}=-i(\nu_{k}^{(n)}{}/V^{(n)}{})B_{0}^{(k;n)}\quad(k=1,2).

(112)

These expressions are used in the main text to obtain
C_{\lambda\varkappa}^{(n)}(t)
in the explicit form.

To find the initial conditions for the equations for
C_{\lambda\varkappa}^{(n)}
in the above expressions we take into account that at the instant

the qubit is in the state

\displaystyle C_{Iz}^{(n)}{}(0^{+})=C_{\pm z}(0^{+})=-C_{zz}^{(n)}{}(0^{+})=\Delta W^{(n)}/2W^{(n)}\ ,

\displaystyle C_{I0}^{(n)}{}(0^{+})=C_{\pm 0}(0^{+})=-C_{z0}^{(n)}{}(0^{+})=\frac{1}{2},

(113)

With these initial conditions, we have in particular

\displaystyle C_{Iz}^{(n)}{}(t_{R})=-C_{zz}^{(n)}{}(t_{R})=\Delta W^{(n)}{}/2W^{(n)}{}\ ,

\displaystyle C_{I0}^{(n)}{}(t_{R})=-C_{z0}^{(n)}{}(t_{R})=\frac{1}{2}\ ,

(114)

and

\displaystyle C_{+z}^{(n)}{}(t_{R})=\frac{1}{2}e^{-W^{(n)}{}t_{R}/2}\left[\frac{\Delta W^{(n)}{}}{W^{(n)}{}}\cosh(\gamma^{(n)}t_{R})\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\left(i\frac{V^{(n)}{}}{\gamma^{(n)}}+\frac{\Delta W^{(n)}{}}{2\gamma^{(n)}}\right)\sinh(\gamma^{(n)}t_{R})\right];

(115)

the expression for
C_{+0}^{(n)}(t_{R})
is given in the main text, Eq. (
III.1
).

Appendix G
Explicit expressions for the phase correlators for
1/f
noise

For completeness, here we provide the explicit expressions for the correlators
f_{k}=\langle\theta_{0}\theta_{k}\rangle
of the phases accumulated by the qubit during Ramsey measurements separated by
k
cycles, i.e., separated by the time
kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}
. The expressions are related to Gaussian noise
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)
produced by a large number of TLSs with the same coupling to the qubit and with the log-normal distribution of the switching rates. The power spectrum
S_{q}(\omega)
of
\delta\omega_{\mathrm{q}}(t)
in this case is given by Eq. (
IV.4
).

The integral over
\omega
, Eq. (
IV.1
), that relates
f_{k}
to
S_{q}(\omega)
, can be expressed in terms of the exponential integral and hyperbolic sine and cosine integral functions
\mathrm{Ei}(z),\mathrm{shi}(z)
, and
\mathrm{chi}(z)
as follows

\displaystyle f_{k}=\frac{D_{\rm fl}t_{R}^{2}}{2\pi}\{2e^{-a_{k}b_{\min}}[(1-a_{k}b_{\min})[\cosh b_{\min}-1]+b_{\min}\sinh b_{\min}]/b_{\min}^{2}

\displaystyle+2a_{k}^{2}\mathrm{Ei}(-a_{k}b_{\min})-(a_{k}+1)^{2}\mathrm{Ei}(-a_{k}b_{\min}-b_{\min})-(a_{k}-1)^{2}\mathrm{Ei}(b_{\min}-a_{k}b_{\min})\},\quad k>0\ ,

(124)

and

\displaystyle f_{0}=\frac{D_{\rm fl}t_{R}^{2}}{\pi}[-\frac{1}{b_{\min}^{2}}+\frac{2}{b_{\min}}-(b_{\min}-1)\exp(-b_{\min})/b_{\min}^{2}-\mathrm{chi}(b_{\min})+\mathrm{shi}(b_{\min})]\ .

(125)

These expressions depend on two dimensionless parameters

a_{k}=\frac{kt_{\mathrm{cyc}}}{t_{R}}\ ,\qquad b_{\min}=\omega_{\min}t_{R}\ ,

that are convenient for a numerical evaluation of the correlators
f_{k}
.
