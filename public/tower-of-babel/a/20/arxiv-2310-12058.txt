HIFuzz: Human Interaction Fuzzing for small Unmanned Aerial Vehicles CCS: Computer systems organization External interfaces for robotics CCS: Human-centered computing Interaction devices CCS: Software and its engineering
Theodore Chambers, Michael Vierhauser, Ankit Agrawal, Michael Murphy, Jason Matthew Brauer, Salil Purandare, Myra B. Cohen, Jane Cleland-Huang

Abstract
. Small Unmanned Aerial Systems (sUAS) must meet rigorous safety standards when deployed in high-stress emergency response scenarios. However, tests that execute perfectly in simulation can fail dramatically in real-world environments. Fuzz testing can be used to increase system robustness by providing malformed input data aimed at triggering failure cases. In this paper, we apply fuzzing to support human interaction testing. Initial tests are run in simulation to provide broad coverage of the input space in a safe environment; however, they lack the fidelity of real-world tests. Field tests provide higher fidelity but can result in costly or dangerous crashes. We, therefore, propose and demonstrate HiFuzz, which executes large numbers of fuzz tests in simulation and then down-selects tests for deployment in human-in-the-loop simulations and safety-aware physical field tests. We apply HIFuzz to a multi-sUAS system and show that each test level serves a unique purpose in identifying known and unknown failures associated with human interactions.



1.
Introduction and Motivation

Small Unmanned Aerial Systems (sUAS) need to meet rigorous safety requirements when deployed in high-stress emergency response scenarios
(
Khan and Neustaedter 2019
;
Herdel et al. 2022
)
. This requires careful design, and systematic testing of the hardware, software, communication networks, and human interaction points in a system, as failures in any of these components can lead to erroneous behavior and even accidents
(
Hoang et al. 2023
)
. In this paper, we focus on the challenge of human interaction testing, where incorrect and unexpected human actions and inputs can result in potentially hazardous system behavior

(
Herdel et al. 2022
)
. This problem is particularly gnarly in complex, Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS), such as sUAS, where human inputs impact multiple subsystems, and previously unexplored feature interactions can trigger erroneous behavior
(
Luckeneder et al. 2017
;
DeVries and Cheng 2018
)
.
In over eight years of experience flying real-world, autonomous, multi-sUAS systems, we observed that humans have played a contributing role in many of the incidents that occurred. For example, during a test flight in the Spring of 2023 (cf. Figure
1
), one of our autonomous sUAS breached a geofence, flew off its designated flight path, and ascended to an altitude of 734 feet above ground level (AGL) – far above the legal limit of 400 feet AGL. A post-mortem analysis revealed a series of factors, including human-related missteps, that contributed to the incident. The remote pilot in charge (RPIC), who plays only a supervisory role under normal conditions, failed to set appropriate geofence-breach actions prior to the mission, placed the throttle in an incorrect position, lost situational awareness of the sUAS’ trajectory following the geofence breach, and failed to take timely action when the sUAS started to fly off-course. These observations align closely with prior studies reporting that human “errors” contributed to 65% to 85% of reported accidents across several different CPS domains
(
Nagel 1998
;
Kohn et al. 1999
;
Fan et al. 2018
)
. However, blaming the operator for these accidents, without investigating and addressing potential weaknesses in the design, is very shortsighted.

sUAS systems are complex in nature, and their design falls into the category of “wicked” design problems
(
Rittel and Webber 1973
;
Coyne 2005
)
for which there are no previously well-formulated solutions, no clear and definitive test of validity, and little opportunity to learn by trial and error prior to deployment
(
Rittel and Webber 1973
)
. Human-Centered Design (HCD) focuses on creating and validating intuitive interfaces that are tailored to human cognitive capabilities
(
Norman and Draper 1986
;
Hao et al. 2022
)
. However, in today’s agile development environment, sUAS tend to be built and released iteratively, thereby, increasing the likelihood that user-interaction design flaws will emerge long after deployment. Without a systematic and rigorous approach to human-interaction testing, designs that fail to anticipate and address normal human “mistakes”
(
Endsley 2011
;
Endsley 2017
)
will eventually lead to unwanted behavior, causing potentially dangerous incidents at critical moments of a flight. The problem is further exacerbated as CPS testing tends to emphasize technical challenges, such as those related to flight trajectories, collision avoidance, and computer vision, with limited focus on human-interaction testing. A more systematic approach for detecting and mitigating human-interaction design flaws in sUAS systems is therefore urgently needed to provide a far deeper understanding of how the system performs when diverse users interact with it in both expected and unexpected ways.

We address this need by presenting and validating the
H

Fuzz
testing framework named “
HIFuzz
”, where “HI” represents both human interactions and the fact that sUAS fly at height.
Fuzz
is analogous to traditional fuzz testing, where inputs are iteratively mutated and tested against the system with a goal of covering a large part of the behavior (and/or the code-base) of an application
(
Zhu et al. 2022
)
.
HIFuzz
supports both random and scenario-driven fuzzing, in which invalid, malformed, or unexpected human inputs are injected into a system to reveal software defects and vulnerabilities
(
Zhu et al. 2022
)
.
It includes three distinct levels (L1, L2, L3) progressing from a low-cost, limited-fidelity, large-scale, no-hazard environment, with fully simulated Proxy Human Agents (L1), via an intermediate level, where proxy humans are replaced with real humans (L2), to a high-stakes, high-cost, real-world environment (L3). The levels are separated by two dedicated gateways. G1 resides between L1 and L2 and is responsible for analyzing test results from L1, and down-selecting an appropriate set of tests to be executed in L2; while G2 represents a significant safety gateway in which standard safety assurance processes are followed, and hard decisions are made about executing
HIFuzz
tests in the real world. Our tests are supported by tools for generating and executing the Fuzz Tests. For example, in the case of levels L2 and L3, where real humans participate in the tests, we have developed a mobile app to interactively guide users through the actions they need to perform during test execution. The aim of
HIFuzz
is to emerge and address all human-interaction failures in advance of emergency deployments, where failures could jeopardize mission success and potentially lead to loss of life.

Our
HIFuzz
framework makes four key contributions. First, it presents a
novel and systematic approach for human-interaction testing
, aimed at detecting, analyzing, and mitigating previously unknown hazards associated with human-sUAS interactions. Second, it presents a
mobile application for guiding humans
through the interactive testing process. The app is designed for scenarios in which humans are prompted to perform actions and to provide interactive feedback during the test. Third, while
Fuzz Testing
has been commonly used for software and systems tests, to the best of our knowledge it has not previously been used
for human-interaction testing
. Our
HIFuzz
process, balances the need for executing thousands of tests that are explicitly designed to find vulnerabilities and weaknesses, with safety assurances needed for real-world sUAS flights, and is, therefore, a novel contribution at the intersection of Human-Computer Interaction and Software Testing. Finally, we
report on an extensive study
, in which we applied
HIFuzz
to our own multi-sUAS system and explored several critical research questions. Results clearly show that
HIFuzz
can play a key role in revealing weaknesses associated with human interactions, potentially leading to their mitigation and improved design solution.

2.
Defining an Individual
HIFuzz
Test

Fuzzing a CPS is more complex, and potentially more challenging, than fuzzing a software-only application for several reasons. One reason is that CPS often have a large state space due to the combination of both digital and physical states, and actions taken by a human are likely to differ according to the current state. Furthermore, CPS frequently exhibit real-time requirements, making the timing and sequencing of events crucial - including the exact timing of the human interaction
(
Fu et al. 2017
;
Yaacoub et al. 2020
)
. A fuzz test, therefore, needs to explore actions within the context of different states and at different times.

A
HIFuzz
test is executed in the context of either a single or multi-sUAS mission, and involves one or more humans performing a sequence of well-defined tasks, each task executed at a point in the mission when an sUAS is in an appropriate state. The characteristics of a mission will differ according to the System-under-Test (SuT), but could include a simple flight along a series of waypoints, or a more complex task such as search-and-rescue utilizing computer vision. Examples of human tasks include issuing a command via a hardware or user interface. A test passes if the mission runs to completion without incurring an sUAS crash, deviating significantly from the planned route, or raising a significant flight-time exception.

In this section, we define the properties that constitute the
HIFuzz
fuzz space. They can be used by engineers to specify the test space for their own SuT, thereby allowing the
HIFuzz
fuzzing engine to automatically generate and run the initial set of level
L1
tests. We divide the specification into two parts – first describing the actors, interaction devices, and drones, including their properties (props), and the environment. We utilize the term “Drone” here, rather than sUAS, to emphasize the actual vehicle and its onboard flight controller, as opposed to the term “sUAS” used throughout the rest of the paper to describe the complete hardware and software system. Secondly, we describe the test scripts composed of the mission flown by the drone, and the sequences of human interaction tasks that form the core component of each test.

2.1.
HIFuzz
Test Setup: Actors, Props, and Environment

Roles:

Each human enacted action is assigned to a specific role such as a
Remote Pilot in Command
(RPIC),
Observer
(OBS),
Mission Commander
(MC), or
Safety Officer
(SO). We define
\mathbf{R}
as the set of roles represented by
\mathbf{R}=\{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3},\ldots,r_{i}\}
. Prior to executing the test, depending on the current test level, either a human, or proxy-human assumes the assigned role.

Interaction Devices:

Humans perform a test action by using an interface device such as the radio control transmitter (RC), a GUI supported by a keyboard, mouse, and/or joystick, or another type of haptic device
(
Neto et al. 2010
;
Yu et al. 2014
;
Mallan et al. 2017
;
Chen et al. 2021
)
. We define
\mathbf{UI}
as the set of all available user interfaces, represented as
\mathbf{UI}=\{ui_{1},ui_{2},ui_{3},\ldots,ui_{j}\}
.

Drones and their Configurations:

Tests can specify a specific drone. This can be particularly useful in
L3
-level tests, for finding discrepancies caused by differences in operating environments, (e.g., different flight controller versions), exploring differing capabilities (e.g., different hardware), or investigating multi-sUAS human interactions in which more than one drone is involved. Inconsistencies across drones can cause accidents when their behavior fails to meet the human’s current mental model
(
Endsley 2017
)
. We therefore define
\mathbb{D}
as the set of drones, represented as
\mathbf{D}=\{d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},\ldots,d_{l}\}
. Further, each drone in
\mathbf{D}
can be configured by the user prior to flight – for example, by setting a geofence around the drone or assigning it a unique RTL (return to launch) flight altitude. We define
\mathbf{P}
as a set of configurable parameters for an sUAS given by
\mathbf{P}=\{p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},\ldots,p_{m}\}
; however, low-level parameter configuration, that normally occurs when tuning the flight-controller
(
Han et al. 2022
)
is out of scope of this paper, and we assume that each drone has been adequately tuned and is flight-worthy. Parameters of interest are therefore limited to those exposed to the operator through interfaces (e.g., GUI screens) that they are expected to access during pre-flight setup.

Simulation Environment:

Finally, for Level L1 and L2 tests, depending upon the simulation environment used, we can directly configure elements such as wind. We define
\mathbf{E}
as a set of configurable environmental parameters given by
\mathbf{E}=\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},\ldots,e_{n}\}
.

2.2.
HIFuzz
Scripts

Humans (serving in a specific role) enact a human-interaction task (HIT) in the context of an sUAS mission. Further, they execute the HIT when the sUAS and/or mission is in a specific state. For example, the RPIC might be asked to perform the action of switching to
position
mode when the drone is
flying
in
offboard
mode. This leads to the following specifications.

Missions:

A mission determines the flight plans and other tasks that one or more sUAS will execute to provide context for the test. The way that the mission is defined is specific to the SuT. We provide an example later in this paper as part of our case study. Nonetheless, we define
\mathbb{MSN}
as the set of available missions, represented as
\mathbf{MSN}=\{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3},\ldots,m_{q}\}
.

Human Interaction Task (HIT):

There are two types of HIT that a human will perform during a test. First, the human could provide input to an individual sUAS through a hardware device such as the RC – for example, by increasing the throttle, holding down the kill switch, or switching between modes. Second, the human could send a command to one or more sUAS via a GUI – for example, issuing a global RTL command.
We define
\mathbb{HIT}
as the ordered set of interaction tasks performed by a user, represented as
\mathbf{HIT}=\{hit_{1},hit_{2},hit_{3},\ldots,hit_{r}\}
.

However CPS behavior is impacted by the current state of the system. Therefore, each HIT has an associated set of preconditions that also need to be defined. These preconditions are based on
modes
,
flight life-cycle states
, and
configurations
. Modes are used by almost every flight controller to support common flight tasks such as
take-off
and
loiter
, and to provide various degrees of flight stability (e.g.,
stabilized
and
position-hold
)
(
Ardupilot 2023
;
PX4 2023a
)
. We define
\mathbf{M}
as the set of flight modes, given by
\mathbf{M}
=
\{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3},\ldots,m_{s}\}
, where each mode
m_{i}
in
\mathbf{M}
is reachable in the SuT. We also define
\mathbf{S}
as a set of flight life-cycle states such as taking-off, flying, and landing, given by
\mathbf{S}=\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},\ldots,s_{t}\}
. A drone can only be in one mode and one state at any time. Finally, we define configurations as the value assigned to any underlying parameter defined earlier as
\mathbf{P}
. Each HIT must define a mode and life-cycle precondition, and can optionally define a set of configuration parameters that serve as preconditions. Further, the precondition state must be reachable in at least one of the defined missions in order for any subsequent
HIFuzz
test to be valid.

2.3.
Defining the
HIFuzz
Actionable Test

Based on these definitions, we can now specify an individual
HIFuzz
test in a way that is sufficiently formal for automating test execution, but also readable to humans who serve as participants in the testing process. We utilize JSON to represent each test as shown in Listing
1
. The test definition includes the mission, environmental factors, roles, the locally sequenced HITS and preconditions performed by each role using a specific interaction device and drone. The JSON specification is used by the
HIFuzz

The
HIFuzz

2.4.
Test Outcome

Each fuzz test is ultimately executed within the
HIFuzz
platform, and its outcome is evaluated across two different dimensions – first to determine if the test was
valid
or
invalid
, and second to determine if valid tests
passed
or
failed
. An invalid test is one that fails to execute the full sequence of HITS, typically because preconditions for one or more of the HITS are never met. The outcome of valid tests is assessed as
passed
of
failed
based on
Mission Completion
criteria. These criteria are adjusted for different systems and domains, and also for different test levels, but will typically involve factors such as mission completion and mission adherence.

3.
HIFuzz
Process: Test Levels and Gateways

The
HIFuzz
process involves three testing stages (L1-L3) separated by two gateways (G1, G2), each of which serves a unique purpose. Individual tests are executed at each stage, however, the way they are executed, the role of human stakeholders, and the safety analysis that is performed prior to test execution differ greatly across stages. In this section, we therefore describe the role and functionality of each stage and gateway.

3.1.
Level L1: Large scale, simulated, fuzzing

The goal of L1 is to execute a large number of tests, as quickly as possible, without any of the risks involved in real-world sUAS flights. Therefore, L1 tests are run in the simulator using proxy human agents instead of humans. In the physical world, humans interact with sUAS via hardware devices, such as RC transmitters, and their inputs are encoded into radio signals transmitted to the flight controller and transformed into flight commands (e.g., throttle, yaw, pitch, and roll adjustments, or mode changes). These inputs can be simulated through software-based, low-level function calls to the flight controller. Humans also interact with sUAS via GUIs, and these interactions can be simulated if the SuT exposes its API function calls. Utilizing these techniques, L1 is able to simulate human interactions (i.e., HITS) entirely in software, enabling thousands of fuzz tests to be run in a low-cost, low-effort, non-hazardous environment.

The L1 process starts with a planning task in which the
HIFuzztester
specifies the test features that constitute the fuzz space. As described in
2
, these include roles, interaction devices, drones, environmental factors, missions, and HITs. The
HIFuzz
fuzzer then uses this specification to automatically generate combinations of the defined concepts and input values constrained by specific scenarios of interest.
The set of generated fuzz tests are then executed in simulation, and results are evaluated to determine if the test
passed
,
failed
, or basically
untested
if the sUAS completed its mission without the preconditions ever being met. All passed and failed outcomes are passed to Gateway G1.

3.2.
Gateway G1: Downselecting for Human-in-the-Loop Tests

G1 serves as a gateway between levels L1 and L2, and is responsible for selecting tests to be passed to L2. Its inputs are therefore the test result data from L1, which it uses to construct a profile constituting the complete set of L1 passed and failed tests and results. The tests are clustered to identify groupings of similar tests and outcomes, and the clusters used to guide the L2 test selection process. We describe the concrete clustering approach that we took, based on experimentation with our data, in Section
5
.

By default, we pass at least one representative test from each cluster to L2. However, the initial number of clusters can be predefined according to budgeted time or can follow a standard approach such as the “elbow-approach” which looks for the sweet-spot in terms of coupling and cohesion
(
Syakur et al. 2018
)
.

3.3.
Level L2: Humans in Simulated Environment

L2 executes a small subset of L1 tests, selected in the G1 gateway. The tests are again executed in a simulation environment, however, this time humans replace the proxy agents, and interact with the sUAS through the same hardware devices (e.g., RC transmitters) and GUIs used in physical deployments. Level L2 is designed to provide higher degrees of fidelity than L1, while operating within a completely safe testing environment; however, it introduces higher testing costs with respect to human time and effort.
By integrating humans into the testing environment, L2 allows us to issue commands directly from the RC transmitter used in the field, providing increased fidelity of user inputs, and allowing direct observation of the sUAS behavior by human operators.

3.4.
Gateway G2: Safety Assessment and Mitigation

While Test levels L1 and L2 seek to safely explore mission-breaking human-interaction faults that potentially cause erratic sUAS behavior, such as crashes and flight deviations, level L3’s real-world deployment means that failures are potentially hazardous and costly. Therefore, Gateway G2 serves as a
safety gateway
that ensures that each failed test from L2 is carefully assessed to determine if mitigations are needed, and that all tests deployed on the field with physical sUAS have undergone a rigorous hazard analysis and that any identified hazards have been sufficiently mitigated. The aim is to (1) assess human-interaction vulnerabilities and flaws identified in levels L1 and L2, (2) mitigate them, (3) repeat level L2 tests to demonstrate that they have been successfully mitigated, and only then (4) proceed to level L3 tests.
HIFuzz
does not dictate how the safety assessment should be performed as long as the process assesses hazards associated with each test case, e.g., using Fault-Tree Analysis (FTA) or Failure Mode Effect Criticality Analysis (FMEA/FMECA)
(
Reifer 1979
;
Sullivan et al. 1999
;
Leveson and Harvey 1983
)
, evaluates mitigations to determine whether the risk has been satisfactorily addressed, and when needed, provides a semi-formal safety case, e.g., a Safety Assurance Case (SAC) that includes guidelines targeted at the human participants describing how the test can be safely conducted in the field.

3.5.
Level L3: Field Testing with humans-in-the-loop

The goal at level L3 is simple. Execute all tests that (a) have previously produced a failed L2 outcome, (b) have been demonstrably mitigated, and (c) have a constructed safety case providing sufficient evidence that the problem has been resolved. Real-world flights introduce the very real potential for actual incidents, including costly loss of equipment that could occur when humans intervene in ways that the system is unable to handle; however, L3 tests are critical for demonstrating that solutions tested successfully in simulation are also effective in the physical world. Further, L3 testing allows tests to be conducted when operators are fully aware of potential problems, rather than during a live deployment when attention is on the mission rather than the tests, and where unwanted behavior could impact the successful outcome of a life-or-death mission. We provide examples of L3 tests in the evaluation section of this paper.

4.
The
HIFuzz
Framework

In this section, we describe the major components of the
HIFuzz
framework that support the functionality required by test levels (L1-L3) and gateways (G1, G2). Level L1 accepts a
specification
of the fuzzing space, and then uses a
fuzzer
to generate thousands of tests, and a
test runner
to coordinate their execution in a high-fidelity
simulation environment
. The test runner and simulation environment are also used by L2. In L1, the human is replaced by a
proxy human agent
supported by a
simulated RC interface
; while in levels L2 and L3 the humans need to be prompted to perform each task at the right stage of the mission. The
HIFuzz
platform supports this through a novel
mobile prompter
, controlled by the test runner. The platform also provides a
test verifier
which evaluates whether each executed test has passed or failed. Finally, the framework supports gateway G1 through a
gateway filter
which clusters test results and supports the tester in selecting tests to pass to level G2. In our current implementation of
HIFuzz
, Gateway G2 utilizes only a spreadsheet-based template, but could be augmented by external safety analysis and assurance tools.

4.1.
Test Fuzzer

The fuzzer takes as input a specification describing concrete instances of roles, drones, HITs, etc., as defined in Section
2
. There are many different ways to generate fuzz tests. Fuzzing is a random test generation approach (see
(
Zhu et al. 2022
)
for a survey) originally developed to find security flaws in programs by running the program with a large number of random malformed inputs
(
Miller et al. 1990
)
. It has evolved to include feedback (coverage-guided)
(
Padhye et al. 2019
;
Böhme et al. 2016
;
Fioraldi et al. 2023
)
and smart search heuristics (search-based fuzzing)
(
Böhme et al. 2017
;
Olsthoorn et al. 2021
)
. Some fuzzing works at the specification level (often using an input grammar)
(
Bendrissou et al. 2023
;
Godefroid et al. 2008
;
Srivastava and Payer 2021
)
while the majority of fuzzing techniques are greybox (using code-guided metrics to diversify coverage of program paths in the code)
(
Padhye et al. 2019
;
Böhme et al. 2016
;
Fioraldi et al. 2023
;
Böhme et al. 2017
;
Olsthoorn et al. 2021
)
. Finally, scenario-based approaches represent an alternative approach for specification-based fuzzing
(
Sun et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2020
;
Han and Zhou 2020
)
. We adopt this approach because it is well-matched to the scenario-based incidents that we observe over time.

Randomly generated fuzz tests select elements from each of the specified sets (e.g., roles, modes, states, actions) guided by a simple set of heuristics that are designed to avoid infeasible combinations. For example, a random approach might select a role, a drone and its configuration, a mission, a sequence of HITs and their preconditions, and an associated interaction device.
While random fuzzing can provide coverage of a large problem space, it can be inefficient at targeting specific problem scenarios

(
Liang et al. 2014
;
Hu et al. 2021
;
Kim et al. 2022
)
.
Scenario-driven fuzz testing addresses this challenge by directing resources to explore known problem areas in greater detail. A scenario-based approach defines one or more missions that execute the problem scenario and constrains the test configuration space to focus only on the scenario. For example, the scenario described in the introduction of this paper focused on how the human interacts with the system in preparing for, and responding to, a geofence breach – especially one for which no geofence is set. To fuzz around this scenario, we would select a mission that includes a geofence breach, and test various combinations of geofence settings and human responses (such as switching modes after the breach or setting the throttle at different levels before and after).

4.2.
HIFuzz
Test Runner

The
test runner
iterates through the generated tests, performing slightly different functions depending on whether a real-human or proxy-human is involved. At Level L1, the test runner invokes the mission in the simulation environment, monitors the runtime state of each drone, checks for precondition states, and delegates HITs to the Proxy Human Agent (PHA) when precondition states have been reached. At levels L2 and L3, humans are responsible for invoking each mission; however, prior to the start of the mission various participants need to know (1) how each drone should be configured (e.g., geofence actions), (2) which mission to invoke, and (3) when to perform a specific HIT during the mission. The test runner tracks the state of each mission and sends timely prompts to the
mobile prompter
which guide each human participant to perform their HIT at the right time. Our test environment assumes that the SuT provides an API that allows the test runner to register for, and receive, status updates conveying information about the current state and mode of each drone.

4.3.
Mobile Prompter

Determining the right time to perform a HIT can be challenging for humans because many of the HIT’s precondition states are internal to the drone and not readily visible to human observers.
HIFuzz
, therefore, provides a mobile app responsible for generating timely prompts. Furthermore, humans engaged in Level L3 tests, are not just test participants, but are front-line pilots with all the responsibilities and pressures brought about by that task. The mobile app is designed to minimize unnecessary mental overload of processing and responding to prompts, and is designed with a simple GUI which gives the user planning time as well as clear instructions on what actions to perform. We designed and implemented the mobile app following principles of human-centered design, and our two test participants reported that it was intuitive and gave them clear and timely directions. However, a full assessment of the mobile app is outside the scope of this paper, and we therefore present it as a supporting tool rather than a primary contribution of this work.

We provide example screens, including a possible workflow, in Figure
5
. Screen 1 is designed as a summary of the upcoming flight including any potential safety concerns. This is essential so that each participant is fully aware of what the drone will do during the mission, and can intervene if needed. It also provides each participant with a list of tasks they will need to perform, giving them time to mentally run through the actions they will need to take. The test runner waits for all participants to check the “proceed” button before starting the test. Screens 2a-g represent a potential sequence of prompts sent to an RPIC during a single test. In 2a the user enters their role (i.e., RPIC in this case) and acknowledges that their first responsibility is to safety rather than the test. All HITs planned for the test are supported by a sequenced pair of
plan
and
go
screens. The
plan
screen gives the RPIC time to mentally prepare for the upcoming task, while the
go
screen tells them to perform the task “now”. We only engage trained personnel in these tests, with the expectation (as required by regulators) that all participants are fully trained in their roles and know which switches and knobs to manipulate in order to execute the intended task. We notify the RPIC (Screen 2F) when there are no more test actions, and then conclude in 2g by asking awareness questions. Screen 3 is targeted at the Mission Commander who is responsible for launching missions from the Ground-based Control station, and Screen 4 is an example screen for the Safety Officer who has no direct control over any drone and is responsible only for observing actions and checking that protocols are followed.

4.4.
Replacing Humans with Proxies in L1

Proxy Human Agents replace humans in level L1 tests. In particular, the PHA serves two distinct functions for mimicking human inputs on hardware devices such as the RC,and mimicking human inputs via a GUI. One of the most critical components of the PHA is the simulated RC which sends commands directly to the flight controller. For example, on a physical RC, when a human moves a lever to change modes from
stabilized
to
land
, a radio signal is transmitted by the RC (transmitter) to the drone’s flight controller (the receiver). However, in level L1, we replace the radio signals with mavros
manual_control
messages
(
Ermakov [n. d.]
)
which, among other things, define the targeted system, and simulate RC movements for various switches and button presses including mode changes, throttle position, and the kill-switch. Mavros converts ROS messages to the MAVLink protocol
(
DroneCode [n. d.]
)
, which is the standard publish-subscribe messaging protocol for sUAS applications.

L1 tests are executed fully automated, currently composed of scenario-driven combinations of geofence breaches, mode switches, throttle positions, and the status of the drone during a mission. The
HIFuzz
test runner runs a single test per mission, and monitors the status of the mission. L1 tests are run in a high-fidelity simulation with a physics environment and continue exhaustively until all combinations have been checked for the isolated scenario. At the end of each mission, a reset is performed where the simulated sUAS is set to a default configuration to ensure pristine conditions for each simulation run. This enables the tests to run without interference for long periods of time, yet still replicate a human agent.

4.5.
sUAS Simulation Environment

Both, levels L1 and L2 require a simulation environment that accepts and executes a mission request – potentially involving multiple drones, reports the progress of each drone throughout the mission, reports error messages, and produces a readable flight log at the end of each flight. Furthermore, the interface of the simulation environment must match that of physical drones to support a seamless transition from L2 to L3. Common examples of simulation environments that can be used to meet these requirements are Gazebo
(
Open Robotics 2023
)
, jMAVSim
(
PX4 2022
)
, and AirSim
(
Shah et al. 2018
)
. Our system is deployed using Docker
(
Merkel 2014
)
in conjunction with Docker Compose, facilitating simulation scalability, with multiple containers being deployed, and achieving consistency across simulation runs. Docker Compose ensures a set of linked containers within a dedicated software-defined network, which includes our sUAS onboard software, MavROS and PX4 to interface with a drone, the Gazebo simulation environment to provide a physics engine and simulated sensor data, and lastly MQTT for communication and message exchange. Additionally, for level L2 we use QGroundControl
(
QGroundControl 2021
)
to connect a physical RC controller via the MAVLink protocol to allow a human to control the (simulated) drone.

4.6.
HIFuzz
Test Assessment

HIFuzz
utilizes an ensemble of test oracles and techniques to determine whether each flight has been executed correctly. These include runtime alerts, flight log analysis, and human feedback via the mobile app. For runtime alerts, we capture all the alerts generated by both the flight controller and our own software system. For our flight log analysis, we extract time series data from the mission logs related to the position and flight behavior of the sUAS. First, we establish a “blueprint” flight log, which represents an ideal mission execution, and then use it as a point of comparison for all tests executing the same mission. For each log in the dataset, we measure its deviation from the blueprint flight path by checking, for each position timestamp in the blueprint, the distance to the nearest sUAS position in the current log across the x, y, and z axes. Then we record the largest such distance as the maximum observed deviation of the current log from the blueprint. We also extract other features from each log in the set, such as the maximum altitude, the duration of the flight, the occurrence of freefalls, the final landing state, and the reported mission status from the drone.

5.
Experimentation:
HIFuzz
Applied to
D4A
System

We conducted our primary evaluation of
HIFuzz
using a multi-sUAS system that we have developed over several years and deployed in the real world as the
System-under-Test
. For double-blind review purposes, we refer to this as ‘Drones for All’ (
D4A
). Given the scope of this work, our evaluation focuses upon the effectiveness of the levels (L1-L3) and gateways (G1, G2), rather than on the tools we have developed (i.e., the Mobile App), or the safety-assessment (i.e., based on standard FMECA).
We therefore focus on addressing two specific research questions.

RQ1:

What kinds of system failures, associated with human interactions, could be identified during the HIFuzz process?

This first research question aims to establish a general understanding of the types of failures that were detected using
HIFuzz
.

RQ2:

Did each of the three test levels, and two gateways play a unique role in the human-interaction testing process, or did they all reveal the same set of problems?

The second research question aims to ascertain how, and to what extent, detected problems differ on the three levels, with and without human involvement.

The experiments described in this section were all executed in our
HIFuzz
platform as described in Section
4
.

5.1.
System under Test:
D4A

The two research questions were evaluated by applying
HIFuzz
to the
D4A
system.
D4A
is a distributed multi-user, multi-sUAS system, designed to support search-and-rescue
(
ANONYMOUS 2023
)
and aerial data collection and surveillance activities
(
ANONYMOUS 2023
)
. Each sUAS is equipped with an
Onboard Autonomous Pilot
(OAP) organized around a state machine which is dynamically configured for each mission. States represent specific tasks such as
takeoff
,
search
, or
fly-to-waypoint
and vary greatly in complexity. For example, in the
takeoff state
the sUAS ascends to a predefined altitude, uses its onboard sensors to detect when it reaches that altitude, and then transitions to a subsequent state such as
fly-to-waypoint
; while a
search
state utilizes extended onboard computer vision capabilities to detect objects, analyze the results, and make subsequent decisions, such as to
track
a person.

A Ground Control Station (GCS) provides coordination and support services between humans and sUAS. It is built around a set of microservices loosely connected via a message broker
(
Muccini and Moghaddam 2018
)
. Each microservice supports a specific capability such as airspace leasing, multi-sUAS coordination, or a task sequencing validity checker/Microservices interact via MQTT
(
MQTT 2023
)
with human operators and sUAS. Status data (e.g., GPS location, battery, health) and task progress updates (e.g., current task, potential adaptations), are sent to the GCS by both humans and sUAS to support monitoring, analysis, and planning.
Under normal operating conditions, human-machine interactions are primarily GUI-based (e.g., providing support for mission planning and facilitating situational awareness) via a dedicated
web application
; however, in case of an emergency, or to temporarily assume control for tasks that the machine has not yet been trained to perform, humans can directly issue commands to sUAS via handheld radio controllers. Communication is primarily asynchronous with data sent over a mesh radio with various QoS (Quality of Service) guarantees about message delivery.

5.2.
Scenario Based Fuzz Tests

Given the large feature space including all possible states, modes, roles, drones, actions, environmental conditions, and reasonable values for PX4 settings which humans are likely to configure prior to flight (e.g., geofence, battery failsafe), produces a vast test space of approximately 8,989,344 test configurations. If we add additional fuzzing to the specific times at which each task is performed, then this number can grow significantly higher. We, therefore, adopted a scenario-based approach. To select appropriate scenarios, we browsed through all of the 272 issues (dated from 07/24/21 to 08/31/23) reported in our
D4A
GitHub repository with the aim of identifying incidents reporting failures associated with a human at the field, such as the example depicted in Figure
6
. We selected two incidents as shown in Figure
2
, and for each of them, we provided a general description of the problem and our observations from inspecting their flight log.

5.3.
Modeling the test space

For each incident, we then defined all relevant roles, simulation environment configuration (wind, geofence), drones, flight modes, states, and human tasks needed to recreate conditions for the selected scenarios. For example, to replicate Scenario 1, and to fuzz-test its variants, we created a flight route that intersected a geofence, defined geofence status (on, off), and the complete set of available geofence actions for PX4. We included all reachable modes and states, three different drones, and three roles. Each of our drones is represented by a unique color;

blue
,
orange
, and
purple
. Furthermore, all of our drones have identical RC transmitter setups and capabilities, therefore, we did not need heuristics constraining which HITs should be applied to which drones. As our drones are not all configured identically due to gradual drift from the original configuration, we included three different drones in the tests, for use at level L3, where slightly different configurations may give different results. Figure
7a
shows the layout of the
D4A
radio controller on the left, with some of the possible configuration settings on the right. As some switches are inert (i.e., do not trigger a change in state) we focused on mode changes that are supported. The complete set of available modes is also shown in Table
3
. We added two representative wind properties, and several properties associated with geofence settings. These included specifying whether the geofence was activated or not for each test, and if activated, whether the distance to the geofence was predicted or not, and providing several geofence actions that could occur upon breach. For all internal sUAS parameters we accepted values as configured during prior PID tuning (proportional–integral–derivative controller). Finally, we included three human actions (HITs). Two of them were directly related to our scenario and included mode changes and throttle adjustments. The third, action was the emergency kill-switch (which immediately shuts down all motor functions of the drone), as this is important for any failed takeoff, hard landing, or potential fly-away (albeit at great cost and safety concern, if used in the air). This produced a smaller test space of approximately 160,524 test configurations.

We applied a systematic approach to generate combinations of configurations and actions (as explained in Section
4.1
) and fuzzed the exact timing at which each action was executed once its preconditions were found to be true. Finally, we created a simple flight test involving one drone taking off, flying to two waypoints, and returning home.

5.4.2.
G1 Gateway:

All tests in the profile were clustered
using Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS). WCSS calculates the sum of squared distances of all points within a cluster to the centroid of that cluster as
\text{WCSS}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{x\in C_{i}}||x-\mu_{i}||^{2}
. The optimal number of clusters for larger test samples were selected heuristically using the elbow method, whereas smaller test samples were placed in groups according to their test type. This resulted in nine clusters. For each cluster, relative z-scores were computed to represent the distance to the centroid. Tests closer to the centroid are considered more representative of the tests in the cluster than those further away. We therefore selected tests from above the 85th percentile and below the 15th percentile. We apportioned an approximate down-select size as 25-30 tests and first selected one or two abnormal tests closest to each centroid, and then remaining tests from those closest to the edges which were otherwise not well represented. We used the clusters merely as a guide to look for interesting and abnormal test results for further investigation to achieve as diverse a set of interesting test results to explore as possible.

5.4.3.
L2 Tests:

We executed all of the selected tests in the L2 simulation environment using a FrSky XD9 Plus Taranis Radio Handheld Controller
(
FrySky 2023
)
. L2 tests were run by two researchers from our team. The
tester
was responsible for the test setup, including the test runner and the supporting mobile app. For testing purposes, the tester also invoked each of the missions. The
RPIC
was responsible for supervising sUAS flights and following instructions generated by the test runner to apply the human interaction task at the correct stage of the mission. For each executed test, we preserved the flight logs, uploaded them into the PX4 evaluation platform
(
PX4 2023b
)
, and inspected the replayed flight log, logged messages, and graphs extracted from flight log data to further evaluate the flight outcomes. For each test, we assessed it as “acceptable” or “problematic”, where an acceptable test completed the mission as expected. This meant that it either flew the mission to completion, acted correctly upon the prescribed geofence action if any were assigned, or killed motors when commanded in a safe manner. Of the 31 tests passed to L2, 16 were marked as problematic following execution and inspection, 13 were marked as acceptable, and 2 were marked as untestable due to limitations in the L2 handling of the killswitch. It should be noted, however, that some differences in timing and the exact placement of switches occurred between L1 and L2 tests, as was expected when a human was added into the loop. 5.4.4.
G2 Gateway:

To perform the G2 safety analysis, we leveraged our existing development and testing process in which any observed flight problems are examined carefully to identify safety hazards, build a safety case, and where necessary design and implement mitigations. Once mitigations were implemented, we updated the safety case, and when and if, deemed safe for flight, we placed the test into the field-test backlog. The formality of the safety case was determined by the severity of the risk, meaning that some safety cases were supported only by discussions documented on GitHub. In contrast, more severe risks were modeled as formal safety cases using the Goal Structuring Notation (GSN)
(
Kelly and Weaver 2004
)
. In cases where we were uncertain that a problem observed in the L2 simulation would also occur in the real world, we determined if it was safe to execute a physical test in L3 supported by relevant safeguards. While providing a full description of the safety assurance process is outside the scope of this paper; we include one example in Figure
10
.
One author of this paper performed a preliminary inspection of the problematic test results using a bottom-up inductive approach to tag and classify the observed problems. They identified 8 initial categories, merged two pairs together, and removed one category that was related to the experimental fidelity of level L2 rather than to the core design of
D4A
. The five remaining categories were presented, discussed, modified, and ultimately agreed upon, at a design meeting attended by two
D4A
architects (also authors of this paper) and three researchers (two of whom were authors of this paper). We opened several issues in Github to serve as placeholders of future work, discussed and documented initial design solutions, and in one case over a period of several weeks, designed and implemented a solution as discussed in Section
5.4.6
.

5.4.5.
Challenges and Candidate Design Solutions

We now describe the five identified design challenges [DC1-DC5], including preliminary, designed, and/or implemented solutions.

Figure 9
.
Before the mitigation, RPICs needed to focus on fixing the position of the throttle during the takeoff, drawing attention away from the sUAS as the mission was launched. The change implemented as a result of
HIFuzz
fuzz-tests automates this process, allowing RPICs to focus on their supervisory roles.

[DC1] Incorrect Stick Positions:
We had previously observed that incorrect throttle positions were a contributing factors in several crashes and deviant flights that occurred when a human ceded control from the AOP. The problem was exacerbated by the default PX4 behavior requiring drones to arm with the throttle down, thereby requiring the RPIC to reposition the throttle to neutral during takeoff in preparation for any later emergency. This is illustrated for a multi-sUAS mission launch in Figure
9
. When a single RPIC supervises the flights of multiple sUAS, the added workload of adjusting throttle positions during take-off created the opportunity for error. Our tests replicated these problems and showed that similar problems can occur if the Direction-Stick (Forward/Back/Left/Right) is placed in a non-neutral position; however, this is unlikely in the physical RC due to the stick automatically returning to NEUTRAL when not held in a different position. Incorrect stick positions were factors in four of the L2 tests (#5,#84, #126, #PX1), some of which are included in Table
4
.

Mitigations:
We identified two contributing solutions. First, we reprogrammed the takeoff routine to allow take-offs with the throttle in the neutral position thereby eliminating an error-prone human task, and second we designed new alerts to warn the RPIC if the throttle was placed or left in a non-neutral position following takeoff.

[DC2] Insufficient Failsafes:
By design our
HIFuzz
study reported in this paper focused on geofence failsafes, and deliberately did not set other failsafes related to low-battery, or loss of RC or data signal etc.. However, the tests results showed two cases of fly-aways that occurred when the geofence was not active, in one case the sUAS flew so far away that it also lost signal. First, this highlights the need for multiple failsafe mechanisms to work in tandem, providing various layers of safety protection; however, it also introduces the challenge of consistently maintain failsafe configurations across divers sUAS. Human operators, such as RPICS, need to understand what sUAS are doing, and are likely to do under various circumstance, and therefore it is important for them to build a consistent and and reliable mental models of the sUAS behavior
(
Endsley 2017
)
. Further, with multiple sUAS at play simultaneously, it is easy for their failsafes configurations to become inconsistent with respect to activation and actions to be taken. We observed problems associated with insufficient failsafe mechanisms in four L2 test cases (#122, #187, #200, #220).

Mitigations:
Instead of holding the RPIC responsible for manually checking that all failsafes have been set appropriately at the start of each mission, automate the process as part of the prearming checks, push changes where needed, and raise an alert if failsafes are not able to be set correctly.

[DC3] The OAP tracks only a subset of failsafe activations:
The fuzz tests revealed a previously unexplored problem that The OAP did not handle failsafe actions taken directly by the PX4 flight controller. In some cases it continued to run in competition with the failsafe (#KT3) churning out confusing mode-switch messages to the RPIC. In other cases it ceded control but did not clear up its message queue, meaning that later reactivation of The OAP resulted in the execution of stale trajectory actions and jerky flight, which are difficult for the RPIC to fully understand or act upon. This was observed in five additional tests (#84, #115, #139, #352, #392).

Mitigations:
We logged this as an issue with an initial design solution to improve The OAP’s failsafe monitoring system and its ability to reset its state during failsafe context switches.

[DC4] Unsafe RC Transmitter Mappings:
Our analysis of the test results highlighted problems with the RC Transmitter switches and their mappings to modes and behaviors. They were not designed in an ideal way to support the
D4A
system. For example, the RC transmitter provided a setting that allowed the RPIC to manually switch to
offboard
mode, even though this is mode is solely the responsibility of the OAP. Further,
stabilized
and
posctrl
were accessed by the same switch, one simple move away from each other. A simple mistake which pushed the switch one click further than intended could cause the sUAS to enter the wrong mode with rather severe consequences. For example the RPIC may be unaware that they are flying in
stabilized
mode rather than
posctrl
, requiring far more advanced skills and creating an incredibly challenging task if they take control when the sUAS is at distance and altitude. These problems were observed in two tests (#63, #200).

Mitigations:
Build a formal model of the RC transmitter to check for task flow and edge cases. Redesign the mappings between switches and tasks according to the
D4A
uses cases. For example, remove the ability for a user to transition to
offboard
mode as this is
always
performed by The OAP automatically.

[DC5] Mode changes without sufficient alerts:
Finally, there were several cases in which mode switches delivered surprising results due to feature interactions. For example switching to
posctrl
with throttle down, or
stabilized
when the sUAS had momentum, all caused deviant flights. This was observed in four tests (#87, #97, #187, #220).

Mitigations:
Improve
D4A
’s monitoring and alert system to increase user situational awareness by providing warnings when known feature interactions were detected that were likely to cause deviant flight patterns.

5.4.6.
L3 Tests:

Only one of the identified mitigations has been implemented so far, with the others logged as issues with candidate design solutions. The implemented mitigation addresses DC1. We first constructed a basic safety case (cf. Figure
10
) depicting two goals G2 and G3, contextualized by their associated problematic fuzz tests. For Goal G2 “Drone establishes stable flight upon human takeover”, we considered four design solutions; however, solutions S1 and S2 were considered inadequate because they limited the autonomy of RPICs to make necessary flight decisions; while, solutions S3 and S4 were selected. S4 modified the arming and takeoff routine to allow RPICs to place the throttle in the neutral position prior to mission deployment. The new feature was implemented and then moved rapidly to field-tests as no significant new hazards were identified related to its test. The test passed, and the feature is now part of our stable code branch. We have deferred re-execution of the associated Fuzz Tests (i.e., #5, #126, #84, #PX1, #97, #187, #220) until both goals G2 and G3 have been addressed.

6.
Analysis of Results

We now discuss the results from our experiment with respect to each of the two research questions.

6.1.
RQ1: What kinds of system failures, associated with human interactions were identified during the
HIFuzz
process?

Our experiments were focused around two specific scenarios related to mode-switches surrounding geofence breaches and human-takeover of the sUAS. Despite the narrow focus of these initial fuzz tests, we identified five types of failures that created challenges for human operators. Our experiments focused on the RPIC who holds primary responsibility for supervising sUAS flights. The five problems covered areas associated with situational awareness, transparency, and coordination – all of which are particularly important in any human-machine teaming environment
(
Konaev and Chahal 2021
)
, such as the
D4A
ecosystem. DC5 identified problems of lost situational awareness when flight modes changed due to human actions or automated failsafes. This included cases in which a human mistakenly flips the wrong switch on the RC Transmitter without realizing what they’ve done. The tests showed that this could lead to significant flight deviations and other failures without proper alerts to the RPIC. A second, particularly egregious problem related to incorrect positioning of throttle and directional controls. Given the importance of this problem, and its role in previous field-accidents, DC1 focused on allowing the throttle to be placed in the neutral position during launch, thereby reducing the intense workload that an RPIC previously faced in moving throttle switches to neutral during a multi-sUAS launch. The implemented mitigation effectively reduces a non-trivial stress point, making it far easier for the RPIC to position the throttle correctly prior to flight, and warning them when an oversight or mistake occurs.

DC2 addresses the need for consistent and complete failsafes to be set, whilst acknowledging that it is difficult for humans to consistently manage this in a multi-sUAS fleet. The proposed solution automates the task of checking and setting failsafes during a pre-arming state. DC3 addresses a related problem in which the OAP fails to properly track and handle the full spectrum of automated and human-initiated failsafe actions as they occur during the flight. This results in potentially confusing flight behavior, such as thrashing between states as observed in one of our killswitch tests where the OAP continually fought against the kill motors action. This mitigation will improve the scope and consistency in how failsafes are managed.

Finally, DC5 identified flaws in the RC Transmitter mappings which gave humans access to modes that were intended only for automated control by the OAP. It also identified the need to reorganize switches in a way that would make it much harder for humans to accidentally transition to incorrect modes.This problem is being mitigated by remapping the Taranis RC transmitter to better meet the needs of humans interacting with
D4A
.

To answer RQ1 we have identified five core problems, all of which created potential human-interaction hazards in the
HIFuzz
tests, and which could be successfully reduced in severity through the proposed mitigation strategies.

6.2.
RQ2: Did each of the three test levels and the two gateways play a unique role in the human interaction testing process?

We address this RQ by summarizing the contributions from each test level and gateway. Test level L1 enabled us to run thousands of tests in a low-risk environment. Gateway G1 gave us a manageable and guided approach for identifying where human-interaction problems lurked, and carefully selecting tests for the human-in-the-loop simulations. Adding the human-in-the-loop at levels L2 and L3 provided several interesting results. For example, we found that the timings of events, even with the support of the test runner, were not quite identical, meaning that some outcomes looked different in terms of flight paths, but were ultimately examples of the same feature interactions at play. We also observed the human making occasional mistakes, which provided additional and unexpected results human actions. The primary example was when the RPIC running L2 tests accidentally changed the position of the direction stick even though that was not a planned activity. Originally we planned to discard the results, but the ‘mistake’ unearthed new deviant flight behaviors that contributed to our overall findings. In the spirit of Fuzz Testing, we retained these results. G3 allowed us to take a deep dive into analyzing the safety concerns associated with executing tests in the field and then determining appropriate mitigations.The G2 gateway in conjunction with L3 not only guided the process of design improvement, but provided a safety net that helped us ensure that tests could be executed safely, and that design decisions would produce a safer product in which there is greater tolerance to “normal” human errors. The interplay of each level against a subset of test cases is depicted in the left hand columns of Table
4
.

To answer RQ2, we found that moving across the levels from L1 to L2 and ultimately to L3, provided far more value than simply filtering down the number of tests. New and slightly different issues appeared as the fidelity of the simulation environment increased. While no type of simulation can match the fidelity of the real world and actual field tests, the simulation levels provided significant insights across a broad range of tests, while allowing us to explore potential human interaction errors in a safe environment, while L3 empowered us to carefully assess safety concerns and deploy real-world tests in a systematic and safe way.

Related Work

As our work and the presented
HIFuzz
framework bridges the gap between testing and human-CPS-interaction, we discuss relevant related work on
CPS and sUAS testing
in general
(
Abbaspour Asadollah et al. 2015
)
, and
fuzz testing

(
Zhu et al. 2022
)
in particular,
test generation
, as well as
Human-sUAS interaction
and
safety assurance
. We have already provided references to many of these topics; we present the most closely related work here in more detail.

\bullet

CPS Testing:
Several approaches in the past have used fuzzing in the context of CPS and sUAS applications. For example,
Kim
et al.

(
Kim et al. 2019
)
have proposed RVFuzzer, a testing solution for detecting input validation bugs in robotic vehicle control programs. Similar to our work, they focus on PX4 and ArduPilot sUAS applications, however, their main aim is to detect low-level controller malfunctions by monitoring vehicle control states.
Similarly, Kim
et al.

(
Kim et al. 2021
)
have created PGFUZZ, a policy-based fuzzing framework for robotic vehicles. Again focusing on sUAS applications and three flight controllers (Ardupilot, PX4, and Paparazzi). PGFUZZ focuses on safety and functional policies wrt. to user inputs, configuration parameters, and physical sUAS states.
While their work explicitly includes user input and user commands, with
HIFuzz
, we have created a comprehensive multi-level testing framework that utilizes fuzzing, combined with a thorough safety analysis process.

Han
et al.

(
Han et al. 2022
)
proposed a grey-box-based fuzzing
framework for detecting incorrect configurations in sUAS flight controllers. Their framework, LGDFuzzer, combined fuzzing with a genetic algorithm to detect potentially incorrect configurations, and to subsequently test them
through simulation. While their approach provided a fine-grained analysis of flight states and parameters, they did not take into account human-related actions or real-world physical testing.
Sun et al.
(
Sun et al. 2023
)
used fuzzing to find violations of traffic law in autonomous vehicles. They built a specification language to represent traffic laws, and then used fuzzing to find violations. Their focus is on breaking the specification using the vehicle, rather than on human actions.

\bullet

Human-Drone Interaction

Herdel
et al.

(
Herdel et al. 2022
)
conducted a comprehensive study focusing on applications with a human-drone interaction aspect. As part of their work, they identified over 100 applications in 16 different domains such as emergency response and surveillance, and derived related challenges and research opportunities.
One of the research challenges is directly related to our work, addressing different types of how people interact with sUAS and perform complex tasks.
With our
HIFuzz
platform, we strive to alleviate complex and potentially dangerous situations that need to be tested, by systematically defining and testing different scenarios with complex tasks, and user interactions, both in a safe environment and with real human input.

Rakotonarivo
et al.

(
Rakotonarivo et al. 2023
)
conducted a series of interviews with drone operators, safety consultants, and regulators to identify operational risks and challenges when operating sUAS.
With
HIFuzz
we provide support for one of the key recommendations: “Support exploration of operational parameters and estimate their impact on mission safety”, so that we can support “operators to explore
options that could simplify their procedures”.
Our 3-level testing process is specifically built to identify and mitigate potential safety issues before they arise in field testing, or worst-case, during live mission execution. Moreover, our model allows us to systematically capture and test diverse mission parameters and tasks and generate respective reports and documentation for passed and/or failed tests, that can further serve as input for thorough safety analysis and building, for example, Safety Assurance Cases.

\bullet

Safety Analysis:

A previous study by Vierhauser
et al.

(
Vierhauser et al. 2021
)
reviewed over 100 publications in order to identify hazardous human interaction errors. They ascribed the errors to three main categories: (1)
human-initiated
(i.e., for example, an RPIC behaving in a reckless manner), (2)
loss of situational awareness
(e.g., the system not providing sufficient information through the user interface to rectify/prevent an accident from occurring), and (3) a
lack of empowerment
(e.g., interfaces limiting the actions of the human, preventing them to intervene when needed).
Similarly, Belcastro
et al.

(
Belcastro et al. 2017
)
identified sUAS hazards based on analyzing 100 mishaps ranging from minor incidents up to fatal accidents. Their analysis details mission types, sUAS types, and their respective configuration.
Roseman and Argrow
(
Roseman and Argrow 2020
)
put a particular focus on hazards related to adverse weather conditions. As part of their work, they created a framework for analyzing weather forecast data to provide sUAS operators with risk assessment information and discuss potential effects and outcomes of weather hazards.

Several of the documented hazards could potentially have been detected by
HIFuzz
using our multi-step simulation and field testing approach.

8.
Limitations and Future Work

The research described in this paper is empirical in nature and is subject to three primary threats to validity.

First and foremost, we developed the
HIFuzz
framework for fuzz testing human interactions in multi-sUAS systems; however, our experiments focused upon our own
D4A
as the single SuT. We have designed and implemented
HIFuzz
as a generic framework for use with diverse sUAS systems and flight controllers; however, parts of the
proxy human agent
are specific to the PX4-based controller as they bypass the SuT and send commands directly to the controller. Versions for other common flight controllers (e.g., Ardupilot
(
ArduPilot 2019
)
) could also be integrated. Nevertheless, we performed conceptual walkthroughs of how
HIFuzz
could be applied to a broader set of robotics systems, and also how individual tests could be constructed on multiple collaborating sUAS. Starting with tests on individual sUAS allowed us to fine-tune the overall
HIFuzz
process; In future work, we plan on broadening the scope of
HIFuzz
and applying it to a more diverse set of systems including other CPS and larger sets of sUAS.

Second, one of the challenges of human interaction fuzz testing is supporting users in performing the right task at the right time during a test. The cognitive load of piloting or supervising an sUAS is quite high, and the testing task increases that load even further. We addressed this challenge by building the mobile app teleprompter system, which delivered clear and timely prompts to the users; however, they found the increased workload rather challenging. In future work, we will conduct a user study specifically dedicated to exploring alternative options, and potential improvements, such as adding an additional testing role (i.e., a COACH) responsible for supporting the RPIC during the testing process. As our tests were limited to the RPIC, which is potentially the most challenging role, we will also extend the study to include other roles such as the MC (Mission Commander) and SO (Safety Officer), assign a more extensive set of human-interaction tasks, and study the perception of our stakeholders to identify further points of perceived weaknesses. In addition, we plan to allow humans to interact more freely with the L2 simulation environment, and deal with a far broader set of emergency tasks including deviant flight behaviors. Their success at intervening could serve as an indicator of the robustness of the design with respect to human interactions.

Finally, as previously mentioned, the L2 level, while fully functional, had less fidelity to the field than we had intended, primarily because libraries used to interface the radio signals with software-based PX4 simulations had some limitations. In future work, we plan to augment, or ultimately entirely replace the L2 layer with a Hardware-In-The-Loop layer in which a physical flight controller is integrated closely into the simulated environment. This would further increase test fidelity and allow the RC transmitter to communicate over radio signals directly with the PX4 controller. This would also address one other limitation; while the
HIFuzz
framework was implemented as a generic framework for use with diverse sUAS systems and flight controllers, parts of the
proxy human agent
are specific to the PX4-based controller. Replacing the simulated PX4 engine with a hardware component would remove the need for this custom component. Overall, increasing fidelity would allow more robust human-interaction testing, and improve the overall fidelity of our
HIFuzz
pipeline.

9.
Conclusions

In this paper, we have presented the
HIFuzz
framework for supporting human-interaction tests progressing across three test levels and two gateways. This facilitates transitioning from large-scale, low-fidelity tests in simulation, to high-fidelity, high-stakes, real-world tests. Our focus has been upon the systematic application of each part of the
HIFuzz
process, to identify human-interaction hazards that guide us in the design, implementation, deployment, and testing of solutions intended to increase the fault-tolerance of the system for human errors. Our work is motivated by our own experiences in operating multi-sUAS flights and our observations that many flight problems could have been averted with additional support and improved design. In conclusion,
HIFuzz
effectively identified hazardous human interactions, helped us to identify actionable solutions, provided a safe pathway for deploying those solutions, and where full mitigations were not feasible it provided us with far deeper understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of our system.
