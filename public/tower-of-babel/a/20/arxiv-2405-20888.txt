Upper bounds on large deviations of Dirichlet L L -functions in the q q -aspect
Louis-Pierre Arguin, Nathan Creighton

Abstract
. We prove a result on the large deviations of the central values of even primitive Dirichlet L L -functions with a given modulus. For V ∼ α ​ log ⁡ log ​ q V\sim\alpha\log\log q with 0 < α < 1 0<\alpha<1 , we show that 1 φ ⁡ ( q ) ​ # ​ { χ ​ even, primitive mod ​ q : log ⁡ | L ⁡ ( χ , 1 2 ) | > V } ≪ e − V 2 log ⁡ log ⁡ q log ⁡ log ⁡ q . \frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\#\left\{\chi\text{ even, primitive mod }q:\log\left|L\left(\chi,\frac{1}{2}\right)\right|>V\right\}\ll\frac{e^{-\frac{V^{2}}{\log\log q}}}{\sqrt{\log\log q}}. This yields the sharp upper bound for the fractional moments of central values of Dirichlet L L -functions proved by Gao, upon noting that the number of even, primitive characters with modulus q q is φ ⁡ ( q ) 2 + O ⁡ ( 1 ) . \frac{\varphi(q)}{2}+O(1). The proof is an adaptation to the q q -aspect of the recursive scheme developed by Arguin, Bourgade and Radziwiłł for the local maxima of the Riemann zeta function, and applied by Arguin and Bailey to the large deviations in the t t -aspect. 1.
Introduction

This paper is on the central value of Dirichlet
L
-functions with fixed modulus
q
. The most famous result in the study of the distribution of the values of Dirichlet
L
-functions is Selberg’s Central Limit Theorem. In
[
Sel46
]
, it is shown that the normalised values of the logarithm of the Riemann zeta function in a dyadic interval high up the critical line have a Gaussian limiting distribution. More precisely, for any measurable set
E\subset\mathbb{C},

(1.1)

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}{\rm meas}\left\{t\in[T,2T]:\frac{\log\left(\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right)}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\log\log T}}\in E\right\}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{E}e^{-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{2}}dxdy.

A remarkable feature of Selberg’s Central Limit Theorem is that it gives information about the distribution of the imaginary part of the logarithm of the Riemann zeta function on the critical line, the fluctuation of which is connected to zeroes of the Riemann zeta function, without assuming the Riemann Hypothesis.

Much work has been done to generalise Equation (
1.1
), both in extending the range of the Gaussian behaviour, and proving central limit theorems for other problems related to Dirichlet
L
-functions. For example, in
[
HW20
]
, it is shown that the real parts of the logarithm of distinct Dirichlet
L
-functions in a dyadic interval high up the critical line behave like independent Gaussian variables. More precisely, for any integer
N
, if
(\chi_{j})^{N}_{j=1}
is a sequence of distinct Dirichlet characters, then Theorem 1.3 of
[
HW20
]
states that for
t
uniformly distributed in the interval
[T,2T]
with
T
large, the random vector

\left(\log\left|L\left(\frac{1}{2}+it,\chi_{1}\right)\right|,\dots\log\left|L\left(\frac{1}{2}+it,\chi_{N}\right)\right|\right)

approximately has the distribution of a real Gaussian random variable with mean
\mathbf{0}
and variance
\frac{1}{2}\left(\log\log T\right)\mathbf{I}_{N}.

The most famous conjecture about the non-vanishing of Dirichlet
L
-functions is the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis, which predicts that all non-trivial zeroes of primitive Dirichlet
L
-functions lie on the critical line with real part
\frac{1}{2}.
However, even on the critical line, one can consider whether it is possible for any Dirichlet
L
-function to vanish at a certain point. It is conjectured that no primitive Dirichlet
L
-function vanishes at the central point. In
[
Pra19
]
, it is shown that for large values of
q
, at least
50.073\%
of the central values of
L
-functions associated to primitive characters of modulus
q
are non-vanishing. In this article we are able to show the large deviations of order
\log\log q
are bounded above by a Gaussian tail. If one were able to extend this to show that the large deviations beyond the order
\sqrt{\log q\log\log q}
were also bounded by the same Gaussian tail, one would be able to show the non-vanishing of all central values, however the authors were unable to extend their results to this range.

There are many similarities between the distribution in the
q
-aspect of central values of Dirichlet
L
-functions with a large modulus, and the distribution in the
t
-aspect of the values of a given Dirichlet
L
-function, such as the Riemann zeta function, high up the critical line. The analysis of the distribution of values of
L
-functions via their moments is a widely investigated area, and parallels can be drawn here between the
q
-aspect and the
t
-aspect. The Keating-Snaith Conjecture from
[
KS00
]

predicts asymptotics for the moments of the Riemann zeta function. It conjectures that

(1.2)

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T(\log T)^{\lambda^{2}}}\int^{T}_{0}\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2\lambda}dt=C_{\lambda},

where
C_{\lambda}
is an explicit constant.
For the
q
-aspect, an analogous result is conjectured in
[
RS05
]
, for all
\lambda>0:

(1.3)

\lim_{q\to\infty}\frac{1}{\varphi(q)(\log q)^{\lambda^{2}}}\sum_{\chi\text{ primitive mod }q}\left|L\left(\chi,\frac{1}{2}\right)\right|^{2\lambda}=C_{1,\lambda},

where
C_{1,\lambda}
is an explicit constant.

In
[
Gao21
]
, it is shown that

(1.4)

\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}{\sum}_{\chi\text{ primitive mod $q$}}\left|L\left(\chi,\frac{1}{2}\right)\right|^{2\beta}\asymp(\log q)^{\beta^{2}},

for all
\beta\in(0,1)
. This means that, up to a constant, these moments of
\left|L\left(\chi,\frac{1}{2}\right)\right|
, for
\chi
ranging over the primitive characters with modulus
q
, match the moment generating function of a Gaussian random variable with variance
\frac{1}{2}\log\log q
.
Moreover, we have a lower bound for all the integer moments of the conjectured correct order from
[
RS05
]
.
Here, it is shown that, for
k
any fixed natural number and
q
a large prime,

(1.5)

\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\chi\text{ primitive mod $q$}}\left|L\left(\chi,\frac{1}{2}\right)\right|^{2k}\gg_{k}(\log q)^{k^{2}}.

Equation (
1.5
) is proven in
[
RS05
]
for the case where
q
is a large prime, but the method of proof easily generalises to the general case for large values of
q
not congruent to
2
modulo
4.
This means the integer moments of the central values are bounded below by the moment generating function of a Gaussian random variable. We prove further that the large deviations of the real part of the logarithm of the central value are bounded above by a Gaussian tail.

In
[
AB23
]
, a large deviations result is proven for the Riemann zeta function; this paper shows an analogous result holds for the central values of Dirichlet
L
-functions with a given modulus. It was necessary to prove certain results used in the proof of the large deviations result in
[
AB23
]
for the context of central values of Dirichlet
L
-functions; most notably, the twisted mollifier formula for second moments of the Riemann zeta function needed to be adapted to the
q
-aspect, cf. Theorem
1.5
. As in
[
IS99
]
, we restrict our attention to look at just the even primitive characters; the case of odd primitive characters yields the same results.

1.1.
Main results

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
q
be a large natural number, with
q\neq 2\text{ mod }4.
Suppose that

V\sim\alpha\log\log q,
with
0<\alpha<1
.
Then

(1.6)

\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\#\left\{\chi\text{ even, primitive mod }q:\log\left|L\left(\chi,\frac{1}{2}\right)\right|>V\right\}\ll\frac{e^{-\frac{V^{2}}{\log\log q}}}{\sqrt{\log\log q}}.

In Section
5
, we deduce an upper bound on the fractional moments of the central values of primitive Dirichlet
L
-functions, in line with Gao’s result, Equation (
1.4
).

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
0<\beta<1
and
q
be a large natural number, with
q\neq 2
mod
4
. Then we have

(1.7)

\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}{\sum}_{\chi\text{ even, primitive mod }q}\left|L\left(\chi,\frac{1}{2}\right)\right|^{2\beta}\ll(\log q)^{\beta^{2}},

where the implicit constant is uniform for
\beta
lying in any interval
(0,B)
, for any
B<1
.

Note that the range for
\beta
in Corollary
1.2
is controlled by the range of
\alpha
for which Theorem
1.1
is valid. Indeed, if we were able to extend the range of Theorem
1.1
to be valid for
0<\alpha<A
for some
A>1
, then we would be able to extend our bound on the moments in Corollary
1.2
to the range
0<\beta<A.

In
[
AB23
]
, they re-prove a result of Heap, Radziwiłł and Soundararajan
[
HRS19
]
which is the analogous result to Corollary
1.2
in the
t
-aspect.

Corollary 1.3

(Corollary 1.2 in
[
AB23
]
)
.

Let
0<\beta<2
. Then there exists a constant
C_{\beta}
such that

(1.8)

\frac{1}{T}\int^{2T}_{T}\left|\zeta(1/2+it)\right|^{2\beta}\leq C_{\beta}(\log T)^{\beta^{2}},

for all
T
sufficiently large.

The bounds in
[
AB23
]
have
C_{\beta}\to\infty
as
\beta\to 0
. However, by tightening the bounds when we adapt their method, we are able to avoid our constant in the
q
-aspect becoming unbounded near
\beta=0
.
Because we are able to tighten our bounds, we are able to attain (weaker) bounds which include the case when
V=o(\log\log q).

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
q
be a large natural number, with
q\neq 2
mod
4
and
V>0
with
V=o(\log\log q)
. Let
\mathscr{L}
be as in Equation (
1.12
). Then

(1.9)

\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\left\{\chi\text{ even, primitive mod }q:\log\left|L\left(\chi,\frac{1}{2}\right)\right|>V\right\}\ll\frac{\mathscr{L}e^{-\frac{V^{2}}{\log\log q}}}{\sqrt{\log\log q}}.

Note that
\mathscr{L}
grows very slowly in
q
. Indeed, for a given natural number
n
, the maximum value of
q
for which
\mathscr{L}=n
grows like the Ackermann function
F(4,n).

1.2.
Notation and structure of the paper

We will be taking expectations and sums over different sets of characters with modulus
q
.

•

The set of primitive even characters with modulus
q
, denoted
+
.

•

The set of all even characters with modulus
q
, denoted
\oplus
.

•

The set of all primitive characters with modulus
q
, denoted
\ast
.

Where we take probabilities,
\mathbb{P}
will always denote the probability where a character
\mathfrak{X}
is drawn uniformly at random from the space of primitive even characters with modulus

.

1.3.
Structure of the proof

Theorems
1.1
and
1.4
are proved in Section
4
. The proof of Corollary
1.2
follows easily and the details are given in Section
5
.
Theorem
1.1
is derived from the recursive scheme first developed for the
t
-aspect in
[
ABR20
]
. The idea is to approximate
\log|L(1/2,\chi)|
by Dirichlet polynomials of increasing length and whose values are restricted.
To this end, we split up the primes contributing most to the Dirichlet
L
-functions into intervals. We iteratively define steps by setting
q_{0}=1.5,
and setting

(1.10)

q_{l}=\exp\left(\frac{\log q}{\log_{l+1}(q)^{\mathbf{s}}}\right)

for
l\geq 1,
where
\log_{l}
means the natural logarithm iterated
l
times. Here,
\mathbf{s}
is a large constant, and taking
\mathbf{s}>10^{5}
as in (
4.10
).
We then set

(1.11)

n_{l}=\log\log q_{l},\quad l\geq 1,

which turns out to be the suitable scale for considering the variance of Dirichlet polynomials supported on primes up to
q_{l}.

We halt our steps at
\mathscr{L}
, the largest value of
l
such that

(1.12)

\exp(10^{6}(\log\log q-n_{l})^{10^{5}}e^{n_{l+1}})=q^{\frac{10^{6}}{(\log_{l+2}q)^{10^{5}-\mathbf{s}}}}\leq q^{\frac{1}{100}}.

For
k>0
, we define a truncated sum of the formal logarithm of the Dirichlet
L
-series at
\frac{1}{2}
:

(1.13)

\tilde{S}_{k}={\sum}_{p\leq e^{e^{k}}}\frac{\chi(p)}{p^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\frac{\chi(p)^{2}}{2p}.

The absolute value of the
L
-function at
\frac{1}{2}
may be controlled by the real part of the above series.
We further define

(1.14)

S_{k}={\sum}_{p\leq e^{e^{k}}}\Re\left(\frac{\chi(p)}{p^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\frac{\chi(p^{2})}{2p}\right).

We expect for
k
large,
S_{k}
to be close to
\Re(\log L\left(\chi,\frac{1}{2}\right))
.
Indeed, we only consider the values of
S_{k}
for
k
close to
\log\log q
, at the time steps
k=n_{l}
, for
1\leq l\leq\mathscr{L}
.

We also need to control the difference
\log\left|L(\chi,\frac{1}{2})\right|-S_{k}
.
To do so, we define a mollifier of the contribution of the primes in the interval
(q_{l-1},q_{l}]
.
Given
1\leq l\leq\mathscr{L}
, set

(1.15)

M_{l}(\chi,s)={\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|n\implies p\in(q_{l-1},q_{l}]\\
\Omega_{l}(n)\leq 10(n_{l}-n_{l-1})^{10^{5}}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(n)\chi(n)}{n^{s}},

where
\Omega_{l}(n)
denotes the number of prime factors with multiplicity of
n
in the interval
(q_{l-1},q_{l}]
. The idea is that the mollifiers are long enough that
M_{1}...M_{l}\left(\chi,\frac{1}{2}\right)
should mollify all primes up to
q_{l}
. We want to show
\left|M_{1}...M_{l}\right|
to be a good approximation of
\exp(-S_{n_{l}})
. Then, we will have
\left|M_{1}...M_{l}L(\chi,\frac{1}{2})\right|
not too small or large, so we will have mollified the
L
-function at
\frac{1}{2}
successfully.

The mollifier described above is part of a larger family of Dirichlet polynomials which vary corresponding to the even primitive characters mod
q
by a scaling of their coefficients. Namely, if
\chi
is an even primitive character mod
q
, then its associated Dirichlet
L
-function is

(1.16)

L(\chi,s)={\sum}_{n}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^{s}}.

For a sequence of real coefficients
(a_{m})
, which is uniform for all even primitive Dirichlet characters with modulus
q
and supported on the integers
1\leq m\leq M
, we consider the associated Dirichlet polynomial

(1.17)

Q(\chi,s)={\sum}_{n}\frac{a_{n}\chi(n)}{n^{s}}.

We say
Q=Q(\chi)
is a
degree
e^{n_{l}}
well-factorable polynomial
if we can write

(1.18)

Q=\prod^{l}_{\lambda=1}Q_{\lambda},

where
Q_{\lambda}
is a Dirichlet polynomial with the support of the coefficients restricted by their prime factors, and with the coefficients having the same dependence on
\chi
as in (
1.17
),
say

(1.19)

Q_{\lambda}(\chi,s)={\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|m\implies p\in(q_{\lambda-1},q_{\lambda}]\\
\Omega_{\lambda}(m)\leq 10(n_{\lambda}-n_{\lambda-1})^{10^{4}}\end{subarray}}\frac{\gamma_{m}\chi(m)}{m^{s}},

i.e., each factor
Q_{\lambda}(\chi,s)
has coefficients supported on integers with all prime factors in the interval
(q_{\lambda-1},q_{\lambda}]
, and is not too long.
We are only interested in the central values of the Dirichlet polynomials; for a Dirichlet polynomial
Q
with such a property we will write
Q
for
Q\left(\chi,\frac{1}{2}\right).

We show that if
Q
is a degree
e^{n_{l}}
well-factorable Dirichlet polynomial, then
M_{1}...M_{l}
mollifies
L
successfully, after a twist by
Q
. We consider the Euler product of
L
, which doesn’t converge at
\frac{1}{2}
, but can still give good intuition.
Since
M_{1}...M_{l}
is a good mollifier of
L
, we would expect only the primes greater than
q_{l}
to affect the value of
M_{1}...M_{l}L
. Hence, if
Q
is a Dirichlet polynomial which only depends on the primes up to
q_{l}
, then we would expect
LM_{1}...M_{l}
and
Q
to be weakly dependent.
This is a crucial step of the recursive scheme.
We show this in Section
3
, where we prove the following theorem:

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
Q
be a degree
e^{n_{l}}
well-factorable polynomial and
L
and the mollifiers
M_{1},...,M_{l}
be as defined above. Then the mollified twisted second moment satisfies

(1.20)

\mathbb{E}_{+}[\left|LM_{1}...M_{l}Q\right|^{2}]\ll\frac{\log q}{\log q_{l}}\mathbb{E}_{\oplus}[\left|Q\right|^{2}].

The expectation over
+
is the expectation where
\chi
is drawn uniformly at random from the primitive even characters mod
q
, whilst the expectation over
\oplus
is where
\chi
is drawn uniformly from all even characters mod
q
(so includes the imprimitive even characters), and has simpler orthogonality relations. These relations are discussed in Section
2
.
We see that the ratio of the expectations in Theorem
1.5
is bounded by a factor independent of the twist
Q
, which suggests
LM_{1}...M_{l}
and
Q
are weakly dependent.
We need a slightly stronger mollified twisted moment than in Theorem
1.5
, to handle twists by polynomial functions of the real parts of Dirichlet polynomials, such as
S_{k}-S_{k-1}.

We require a similar version to being well-factorable in the real case.
We say a function
F=F(\chi)
is
l
-sufficient
if we may write:

(1.21)

F=\prod^{l}_{j=1}F_{j},

where for each
1\leq j\leq l
, we have

(1.22)

F_{j}=K_{j}{\Big(}\Re{\Big(}\sum_{m}\chi(m)b^{j}_{(m)}{\Big)}{\Big)},

for some sequence of complex numbers
b^{j}_{(m)}
and polynomial
K_{j}
of degree
d_{j}
with real coefficients.
The coefficient
b^{j}_{(m)}
are assumed to be
0
unless
p|m\implies p\in(q_{j-1},q_{j}]
and
\Omega_{j}(m)\leq\nu_{j}
for
\nu_{j}=\frac{10(n_{j}-n_{j-1})^{10^{4}}}{d_{j}}
.
The bound on the number of prime factors ensures that the length of
F_{j}
is less than
10(n_{j}-n_{j-1})^{10^{4}}
.
In Section
3
we will prove:

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
1\leq l\leq\mathscr{L}
and let
F
be
l
-sufficient. Then the twisted second moment mollified by
F
satisfies

(1.23)

\mathbb{E}_{+}[\left|LM_{1}...M_{l}F\right|^{2}]\ll\frac{\log q}{\log q_{l}}\mathbb{E}_{\oplus}[\left|F\right|^{2}].

By applying Theorem
1.6
for suitable choices of
F,
we will deduce Theorem
1.5
.
In order to prove Theorem
1.6
, we require bounds on the twisted moments of Dirichlet
L
-functions by real parts of Dirichlet polynomials.
We will follow the formulae for twists by the whole Dirichlet polynomial (not just the real part), i.e.,

\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[\left|LM\right|^{2}\right]
,
where
M
is a short Dirichlet polynomial. In
[
BPRZ20
]
, the authors give a bound where
M
has length up to
q^{\frac{51}{101}}
. However, for our purposes it suffices to follow the proof of the simpler formula in
[
IS99
]
, for Dirichlet polynomials of length smaller than
q^{\frac{1}{2}}.

2.
Moments in the
q
-aspect

In this section we collate some simple bounds on the moments of even Dirichlet polynomials with a fixed modulus, which will be needed in the proof of Theorems
1.1
,
1.5
and
1.6
.

2.1.
Moments over all even characters with a fixed modulus

The following identity is exact, as opposed to the
t
-aspect, where the diagonal terms only give a leading order approximation.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
q
be a modulus,
N<\frac{q}{2}
a positive integer, and
(a_{n})^{N}_{n=1}
be a sequence of complex numbers. Then

(2.1)

\mathbb{E}_{\oplus}\left[\left|{\sum}^{N}_{n=1}a_{n}\chi(n)\right|^{2}\right]={\sum}^{N}_{n=1}\left|a(n)\right|^{2},

where the expectation is taken over even characters mod
q
.

Proof.

Given integers
1\leq m,n\leq N,
we have

(2.2)

{\sum}\limits_{\chi\text{ mod $q$ even}}\chi(n)\overline{\chi(m)}=\\
{\sum}\limits_{\chi\text{ mod $q$}}\left(\frac{\chi(n)+\chi(-n)}{2}\right)\overline{\chi(m)}.

This equals
\frac{\phi(q)}{2}
if
n=m
and
0
otherwise, since we cannot have
n\equiv-m\text{ mod $q$}
.
Upon substitution into the right-hand side of (
2.1
), we get:

(2.3)

\mathbb{E}_{\oplus}\left[\left|{\sum}^{N}_{n=1}a_{n}\chi(n)\right|^{2}\right]=\frac{2}{\varphi(q)}{\sum}\limits_{1\leq n,m\leq N}a_{n}\overline{a_{m}}{\sum}\limits_{\chi\text{ mod $q$ even}}\chi(n)\overline{\chi(m)}.

Only the diagonal terms survive, and we conclude. ∎

We deduce the following splitting theorem:

Lemma 2.2
.

If
A,B
are two Dirichlet polynomials varying with
\chi
, each of length shorter than
\sqrt{\frac{q}{2}}
with support on integers with prime factors in disjoint sets, then

(2.4)

\mathbb{E}_{\oplus}\left[\left|AB(1/2,\chi)\right|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\oplus}[|A(1/2,\chi)|^{2}]E_{\oplus}\left[\left|B(1/2,\chi)\right|^{2}\right].

Note again that the expectation splits exactly due to the exact orthogonality relations, which is a stronger result than in the
t
-aspect (cf
[
AB23
]
, Lemma 13).
We also require results on the moments of real parts of Dirichlet polynomials in the
q
-aspect. It is convenient to introduce the random variables
(X(p),p\text{ primes})
that are independent and uniformly distributed on the unit circle.
The random variable
X(n)
is then defined multiplicatively by

(2.5)

X(n)=X(p_{1})^{a_{1}}\dots X(p_{r})^{a_{r}},

for an integer
n
with prime factorisation
n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}...p_{r}^{a_{r}}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
q
be large,
K
be a polynomial of degree d with real coefficients,
N<\frac{q^{\frac{1}{2d}}}{2}
be a positive integer,
B=(b_{n})^{N}_{n=1}
be a sequence of real variables, with
b_{n}=0
unless
n
is a prime or a square of a prime.
Then
\mathbb{E}_{\oplus}\left[K\left(\Re\left(\sum^{N}_{n=1}b_{n}\chi(n)\right)\right)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[K\left(\Re\left(\sum^{N}_{n=1}b_{n}X(n)\right)\right)^{2}\right]
.

Proof.

Since
K
is a polynomial with real coefficients, we may write

(2.6)

K\left(\Re\left(\sum^{N}_{n=1}b_{n}\chi(n)\right)\right)={\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k\leq N^{d}\\
p|jk\implies p\leq N\end{subarray}}c_{j,k,B,K}\chi(j)\overline{\chi(k)}

for certain coefficients
c_{j,k,B,K}
depending on the polynomial
K
and the coefficients
B=(b_{n})^{N}_{n=1},
satisfying

(2.7)

c_{j,k,B,K}=\overline{c_{k,j,B,K}}.

We may cancel any common factors of
k
and
j
using the relation:
\chi(n)\overline{\chi(n)}=1
for any character with modulus
q
and any integer
n
coprime to
q
.
For coprime integers
m
and
n
with at most
N^{d}
with prime factors all at most
N
, let

(2.8)

C_{m,r,B,K}=\sqrt{mr}{\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{j}{(j,k)}=m\\
\frac{k}{(j,k)}=r\end{subarray}}c_{j,k,B,K}.

Note that the sum is zero unless
m
and
r
are coprime, with all their prime factors at most
N
so we may drop these conditions. By (
2.7
), we see

(2.9)

C_{m,r,B,K}=\overline{C_{r,m,B,K}}.

Here, the factor
\sqrt{mr}
in (
2.8
) was introduced to give the same scaling convention for coefficients at the point
s=\frac{1}{2}
used in other sections of the article, such as for the Dirichlet polynomial (
1.17
).
From (
2.6
) we see,

(2.10)

K\left(\Re\left(\sum^{N}_{n=1}b_{n}\chi(n)\right)\right)={\sum}\limits_{m,r\leq N^{d}\\
}\frac{C_{m,r,B,K}}{\sqrt{mr}}\chi(m)\overline{\chi(r)}.

Hence

(2.11)

K\left(\Re\left(\sum^{N}_{n=1}b_{n}\chi(n)\right)\right)^{2}={\sum}\limits_{m_{1},m_{2},r_{1},r_{2}\leq N^{d}\\
}\chi(m_{1}m_{2})\overline{\chi(r_{1}r_{2})}\frac{C_{m_{1},r_{1},B,K}C_{m_{2},r_{2},B,K}}{\sqrt{m_{1}r_{1}m_{2}r_{2}}}.

The restriction
N<\frac{q^{\frac{1}{2d}}}{2}
ensures that we cannot have
m_{1}m_{2}\equiv-r_{1}r_{2}\text{ mod $q$, }
and that

(2.12)

m_{1}m_{2}\equiv r_{1}r_{2}\text{ mod $q$ }\iff m_{1}m_{2}=r_{1}r_{2}.

Moreover, the restrictions on the support of the coefficients
(m_{1},r_{1})=(m_{2},r_{2})=1
ensure that (
2.12
) is only achieved in the support if

(2.13)

m_{1}=r_{2},m_{2}=r_{1}.

Using the relation (
2.1
) for the Dirichlet polynomial in (
2.11
), we see:

(2.14)

\mathbb{E}_{\oplus}\left[K\left(\Re\left(\sum^{N}_{n=1}b_{n}\chi(n)\right)\right)^{2}\right]={\sum}\limits_{m,r\leq N^{d}}\frac{C_{m,r,B,K}C_{r,m,B,K}}{mr}.

Finally, applying (
2.9
), we see

(2.15)

\mathbb{E}_{\oplus}\left[K\left(\Re\left(\sum^{N}_{n=1}b_{n}\chi(n)\right)\right)^{2}\right]={\sum}\limits_{m,r\leq N^{d}}\frac{\left|C_{m,r,B,K}\right|^{2}}{mr}.

The corresponding identity with
\chi(n)
replaced by
X(n)
is proved similarly

(2.16)

\mathbb{E}\left[K\left(\Re\left(\sum^{N}_{n=1}b_{n}X(n)\right)\right)^{2}\right]={\sum}\limits_{m,r\leq N^{d}}\frac{\left|C_{m,r,B,K}\right|^{2}}{mr}.

since the orthogonality relation (
2.1
) holds by the definition of
X(n)
.

∎

Using Lemma
2.3
, we deduce a splitting relation for the real part of Dirichlet polynomials.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
1\leq l\leq\mathscr{L}
and
F
be
l
-sufficient as defined in (
1.21
).
Then

(2.17)

\mathbb{E}_{\oplus}\left[F^{2}\right]=\prod^{l}_{j=1}\mathbb{E}_{\oplus}\left[K_{j}\left(\Re\left(\sum_{n}b^{(j)}_{n}\chi(n)\right)\right)^{2}\right].

Proof.

Using the notation from the proof of Lemma
2.3
, we see that for each
1\leq j\leq l
, the coefficients
C_{m,r,B^{(j)},K_{j}}
are supported on coprime integers with prime factors all lying in
(q_{j-1},q_{j}]
, and we similarly drop this condition from the sum. Then

(2.18)

K_{j}\left(\Re\left(\sum_{n}b^{(j)}_{n}\chi(n)\right)\right)^{2}=\sum_{m_{1},m_{2},r_{1},r_{2}}\chi(m_{1}m_{2})\overline{\chi(r_{1}r_{2})}\frac{C_{m_{1},r_{1},B^{(j)},K_{j}}C_{m_{2},r_{2},B^{(j)},K_{j}}}{\sqrt{m_{1}m_{2}r_{1}r_{2}}}.

Moreover, for all values of
r
and
m,
we have

(2.19)

C_{m,r,B^{(j)},K_{j}}=\overline{C_{r,m,B^{(j)},K_{j}}},

and since
b_{n}(j)
is zero unless
\Omega_{j}(n)\leq\nu_{j}
, we see
C_{r,m,B^{(j)},K_{j}}
is zero unless
\Omega_{j}(r),\Omega_{j}(m)\leq 10(n_{j}-n_{j-1})^{10^{4}}
. By Equation (
2.1
), we obtain:

(2.20)

\mathbb{E}_{\oplus}\left[\Re\left(K_{j}\left(\sum_{n}b^{(j)}_{n}\chi(n)\right)\right)^{2}\right]=\sum_{m,r}\frac{\left|C_{m,r,B^{(j)},K_{j}}\right|^{2}}{mr}.

Given coprime integers
u
and
v
, each having all their prime factors at most
q_{l}
, we may write

(2.21)

u=\prod^{l}_{j=1}m_{j},v=\prod^{l}_{j=1}r_{j},

where
m_{j}
and
r_{j}
are coprime integers with all their prime factors lying in the interval
(q_{j-1},q_{j}].

Then set

(2.22)

\tilde{C}_{u,v}=\prod^{l}_{j=1}C_{m_{j},r_{j},B^{(j)},K_{j}},

where we suppress the dependence of
\tilde{C}_{u,v}
on the sequences
B^{(j)}
and the polynomials
K_{j}
for ease of notation.
The relation (
2.19
) means that

(2.23)

\tilde{C}_{u,v}=\overline{\tilde{C}_{v,u}}.

We note that
\tilde{C}_{u,v}
is supported on coprime integers
u
and
v
with all their prime factors at most
q_{l}
, so we drop this from the summation criteria to show that for any even primitive character modulo
q
,

(2.24)

\prod^{l}_{j=1}K_{j}\left(\Re\left(\sum_{n}b^{(j)}_{n}\chi(n)\right)\right)={\sum}\limits_{u,v}\frac{\tilde{C}_{u,v}}{\sqrt{uv}}\chi(u)\overline{\chi(v)}.

The restriction on the support of the coefficients
\tilde{C}_{u,v}
means
\tilde{C}_{u,v}=0
unless
u,v\leq\frac{q^{{\frac{1}{2}}}}{2},
so we may apply (
2.1
) to yield

(2.25)

\mathbb{E}_{\oplus}\left[\prod^{l}_{j=1}K_{j}\left(\Re\left(\sum_{n}b^{(j)}_{n}\chi(n)\right)\right)^{2}\right]={\sum}\limits_{u,v}\frac{\left|\tilde{C}_{u,v}\right|^{2}}{uv}.

Splitting into the components from prime factors in each interval, and using the support of the coefficients
\tilde{C}_{u,v},
we may write the right-hand side of (
2.25
) as

(2.26)

{\sum}\limits_{\forall 1\leq j\leq l,p|m_{j}n_{j}\implies p\in(q_{j-1},q_{j}]}\frac{\left|\tilde{C}_{\prod^{l}_{j=1}m_{j},\prod^{l}_{j=1}n_{j}}\right|^{2}}{\prod^{l}_{j=1}m_{j}n_{j}}.

Using (
2.22
), we may write this as

(2.27)

\prod^{l}_{j=1}{\sum}\limits_{p|m_{j}n_{j}\implies p\in(q_{j-1},q_{j}]}\frac{\left|{C}_{m_{j},n_{j},B^{(j)},K_{j}}\right|^{2}}{m_{j}n_{j}}.

Finally, using (
2.20
), we may write the above expression as

(2.28)

\prod^{l}_{j=1}\mathbb{E}_{\oplus}\left[K_{j}\left(\Re\left(\sum_{n}b^{(j)}_{n}\chi(n)\right)\right)^{2}\right].

This completes the proof of Lemma
2.4
.
∎

2.2.
Moments over even primitive characters with a given modulus

In most of the proofs, we take the expectation of Dirichlet polynomials over all even characters. However, we require a bound on the expectation of certain Dirichlet polynomials where we restrict to just the even primitive characters.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
(a_{n})^{N}_{n=1}

be a sequence of length
N\leq q^{\frac{5}{6}}
.
Then we have the following bound on the expectation over even primitive characters with modulus
q
:

(2.29)

\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[\left|\sum^{N}_{n=1}a_{n}\chi(n)\right|^{2}\right]\ll\sum^{N}_{n=1}\left|a_{n}\right|^{2}

Note that (
2.29
) is an upper bound for the case where there is no restriction to primitive characters in (
2.1
), for the restricted length
N\ll q^{\frac{5}{6}}.

Proof.

Expanding the square out in (
2.29
) shows that the left-hand side is equal to

(2.30)

\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum^{N}_{j,k=1}a_{j}\overline{a_{k}}\left(\sum^{+}_{\chi\text{ even, primitive mod $q$ }}\chi(j)\overline{\chi(k)}\right)

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, this may be bounded as

(2.31)

\ll\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}{\sum}\limits_{j\leq N}\left|a_{j}\right|^{2}\left|{\sum}\limits_{k\leq N}\sum^{+}_{\chi\text{ even, primitive mod $q$ }}\chi(j)\overline{\chi(k)}\right|.

So it suffices to show that

(2.32)

\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}{\sum}\limits_{k\leq N}\left|\sum^{+}_{\chi\text{ even, primitive mod $q$ }}\chi(j)\overline{\chi(k)}\right|\ll 1,

uniformly for each
j
coprime to
q
.

Equation 3.2 in
[
IS99
]
gives the sum over primitive characters with modulus
q
(even and odd), at an individual value. Using Mobius inversion and the orthogonality of characters, they show, for any
m
coprime to
q
:

(2.33)

\sum^{\ast}_{\chi\text{ primitive, even or odd mod $q$}}\chi(m)={\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}vw=q\\
m\equiv 1\text{ mod }q\end{subarray}}\mu(v)\varphi(w).

We have

(2.34)

\sum^{*}_{\chi\text{ even, primitive mod $q$}}\chi(m)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum^{\dagger}_{\chi\text{ even or odd mod $q$}}\chi(m)+\chi(-m)\right).

When substituting the bounds from (
2.34
) and (
2.33
), we see that (
2.32
) is bounded as:

(2.35)

\ll\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\left({\sum}\limits_{k\leq N}\left|{\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}vw=q\\
j\equiv k\text{ mod $w$}\end{subarray}}\mu(v)\varphi(w)\right|+{\sum}\limits_{k\leq N}\left|{\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}vw=q\\
j\equiv-k\text{ mod }w\end{subarray}}\mu(v)\varphi(w)\right|\right).

If we write
t=(j-k,q)
and
u=(j+k,q)
,
then we transform
2.35
into:

(2.36)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}{\sum}\limits_{t,u|q}\left(\#\{k\leq N:(j-k,q)=t\}\left|{\sum}\limits_{w|t}\mu(\frac{q}{w})\varphi(w)\right|\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\#\{k\leq N:(j+k,q)=u\}{\sum}\limits_{w|u}\left|\mu(\frac{q}{w})\varphi(w)\right|\right).

But given any factors
t
and
u
of
q
, we have

(2.37)

\#\{k\leq N:(j-k,q)=t\}\ll\frac{N}{t}+1,\quad\#\{k\leq N:(j+k,q)=u\}\ll\frac{N}{u}+1,

so that (
2.36
) may be bounded as

(2.38)

\ll\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}{\sum}\limits_{u|q}\left(\frac{N}{u}+1\right)\left|{\sum}\limits_{{w|u}}\mu(\frac{q}{w})\varphi(w)\right|.

We may write the prime factorisations of
q,u
and
w
as:

(2.39)

q=p_{1}^{a_{1}}...p_{n}^{a_{n}},\quad u=p_{1}^{b_{1}}...p_{n}^{b_{n}},\quad w=p_{1}^{c_{1}}...p_{n}^{c_{n}},

respectively, where the
p_{i}
are distinct,
a_{i}\geq 1
for all
i
, and
0\leq c_{i}\leq b_{i}\leq a_{i}
. Examining the term
\mu(\frac{q}{w}),
we see that the contribution to (
2.38
) is zero, unless
b_{i}\in\{a_{i}-1,a_{i}\}
for all
i
.
Then the choices for
c_{i}
giving non-zero contribution to (
2.38
) are:

(2.40)

\begin{cases}c_{i}=a_{i}-1&b_{i}=a_{i}-1\\
c_{i}\in\{a_{i}-1,a_{i}\}&b_{i}=a_{i}\end{cases}.

If we define
S=\{1\leq i\leq n:b_{i}=a_{i}-1\}
, to represent the prime factorisation of
u
, then the expression in (
2.38
) becomes:

(2.41)

\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}{\sum}\limits_{S\subset\{1,...,n\}}\left(\frac{N}{q}\prod_{i\in S}p_{i}+1\right)\left|\prod_{i\in S}\mu(p_{i})\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}-1})\prod_{i\notin S}\left(\mu(1)\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}})+\mu(p_{i})\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}-1})\right)\right|.

We split the sum up by the factors in the first bracket.
We have to bound:

(2.42)

\mathscr{B}_{1}=\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}{\sum}\limits_{S\subset\{1,...,n\}}\left|\prod_{i\in S}\mu(p_{i})\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}-1})\prod_{i\notin S}\left(\mu(1)\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}})+\mu(p_{i})\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}-1})\right)\right|,

and

(2.43)

\mathscr{B}_{2}=\frac{N}{q\varphi(q)}{\sum}\limits_{S\subset\{1,...,n\}}\prod_{i\in S}p_{i}\left|\prod_{i\in S}\mu(p_{i})\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}-1})\prod_{i\notin S}\left(\mu(1)\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}})+\mu(p_{i})\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}-1})\right)\right|.

We will show that both
\mathscr{B}_{1}
and
\mathscr{B}_{2}
are of order
1
, completing the proof of Lemma
2.5
.

Bounding
\mathscr{B}_{1}

The expression for
\mathscr{B}_{1}
splits into a product over the prime factors of
q
.
If the factorisation is as in (
2.39
), then we see:

(2.44)

\mathscr{B}_{1}=\prod^{r}_{i=1}\frac{1}{\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}})}\left(\left|\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}-1})\right|+\left|\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}})-\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}-1})\right|\right).

Since
\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}-1})\leq\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}})
, we see that the inner term is identically
1
, and so
\mathscr{B}_{1}=1
.

Bounding
\mathscr{B}_{2}

The term for
\mathscr{B}_{2}
also splits as a product over prime factors of
q.

If the factorisation is as in (
2.39
), then we see:

(2.45)

\mathscr{B}_{2}\leq\frac{N}{q^{\frac{5}{6}}}\prod^{n}_{i=1}\frac{1}{p_{i}^{\frac{a_{i}}{6}}\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}})}\left(\left|p_{i}\varphi(p_{i})^{a_{i}-1}\right|+\left|\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}})-\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}-1})\right|\right).

For
p_{i}>2
, we bound the term in the product using that

(2.46)

\frac{1}{p_{i}}\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}})\leq\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}-1})\leq\frac{\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}})}{p_{i}-2}.

For
p_{i}=2
we simply use that

(2.47)

\left|\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}-1})\right|\ll\left|\varphi(p_{i}^{a_{i}})\right|,

to show that the term associated to
p_{i}=2
is bounded.
Under these bounds we see that the term in (
2.45
) yields:

(2.48)

\mathscr{B}_{2}\ll\frac{N}{q^{\frac{5}{6}}}\prod_{p_{i}>2}\frac{1}{p_{i}^{\frac{a_{i}}{6}}}\left(\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}-2}+\frac{p_{i}-1}{p_{i}}\right).

The term in the product:

(2.49)

\frac{1}{p_{i}^{\frac{a_{i}}{6}}}\left(\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}-2}+\frac{p_{i}-1}{p_{i}}\right)

can only be greater than
1
for finitely many choices of
p_{i}
and
a_{i}
, and so we see that

\mathscr{B}_{2}\ll\frac{N}{q^{5/6}}

By the bounds on
N
, this yields
\mathscr{B}_{2}\ll 1
. ∎

Remark
.

From the proof, one can show that

(2.50)

\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[\left|\sum^{N}_{n=1}a_{n}\chi(n)^{2}\right|\right]\ll\left(1+O\left(\frac{N}{q^{\frac{5}{6}}}\right)\right)\sum^{N}_{n=1}\left|a_{n}\right|^{2},

and hence deduce a version of Lemma
2.2
for splitting expectations over primitive characters. One could then perform all the expectations in this paper over the even primitive characters with modulus
q
, without adding in all the non-primitive characters. However, we take the view that, for simplicity, it is easier to just work with all the even characters, to avoid the cumulative error term in (
2.50
).

Following this proof, we can also get the expectation of polynomials of real parts of Dirichlet characters over primitive even characters, as opposed to over all even characters in Lemma
2.4
. We observe that the definition of an
l
-sufficient function means that the

is
O(q^{\frac{1}{100}}).

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
1\leq l\leq\mathscr{L}
and
F
be
l
-sufficient as defined in (

).
Then the expectation of F over primitive even characters satisfies

(2.51)

\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[F^{2}\right]\ll\prod^{l}_{j=1}\mathbb{E}_{\oplus}\left[K_{j}\left(\Re\left(\sum_{n}b^{(j)}_{n}\chi(n)\right)\right)^{2}\right].

Lemma 2.7
.

Given k an integer, let
\tilde{S}_{k}
be as defined in Equation (
1.13
). If
n<m
, and
2n\leq\frac{\log q}{e^{m}}
, then

(2.52)

\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[\left|\tilde{S}_{n}-\tilde{S}_{m}\right|\right]^{2k}\ll k!(m-n+1)^{k}.

Note this is an analogue of Lemma 3 in
[
Sou09
]
, but we give more details.
We first explain some intuition behind the result. We split the sums
\tilde{S}_{m}
and
\tilde{S}_{n}
into the contributions from the primes and the contribution from the square of the primes. The expectation of the contribution of the primes give the
m-n
term. For fixed
k
, the contribution of the square of the primes is negligible, and in
[
Sou09
]
is neglected.
But to take
k
increasing, we must consider the contribution of the squares of primes in (
2.52
). This gives an upper bound of
(m-n+1)^{k},
rather than
(m-n)^{k}
, which is what we would expect from just the primes.
We first consider the contribution of the primes, without the squares. We require a lemma on the expected value of Dirichlet polynomials supported on primes.

Lemma 2.8
.

If
a(p)
is a sequence of complex numbers, and
x
is a natural number such that
x^{k}\leq q,
then

(2.53)

{\sum}_{\chi}^{*}\left({\sum}\limits_{e^{e^{n}}\leq p\leq x}\left|\frac{a(p)\chi(p)}{p^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right|^{2k}\right)\ll qk!\left({\sum}\limits_{e^{e^{n}}\leq p\leq x}\frac{|a(p)^{2}|}{p}\right)^{k}.

Proof.

This follows from the proof of Lemma 3 in
[
Sou09
]
, using the orthogonality relations for Dirichlet characters from Lemma
2.5
.∎

Proof of Lemma
2.7
.

We use the same notation for expanding the
k^{th}
moment of a Dirichlet series as in Lemma 3 in
[
Sou09
]
, so that if

N=\prod^{R}_{i=1}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
,
then we set

(2.54)

a_{k,x}(N)={k\choose\alpha_{1}\ ...\ \alpha_{R}}\prod^{R}_{i=1}a(p_{i})^{\alpha_{i}}.

The contribution of the primes to the sum
\tilde{S}_{n}-\tilde{S}_{m}
is:

(2.55)

U_{1}:={\sum}\limits_{e^{e^{n}}\leq p\leq e^{e^{m}}}\frac{\chi(p)}{p^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

Hence applying Lemma
2.8
we see

(2.56)

\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[\left|U_{1}\right|^{2k}\right]\ll k!\left({\sum}\limits_{e^{e^{n}}\leq p\leq e^{e^{m}}}\frac{1}{p}\right)^{k}\ll k!(m-n)^{k},

where the last inequality follows by Mertens’s estimate.

We need to evaluate the contribution of the terms from the squares of primes.
The contribution of the squares of primes to the sum
\tilde{S}_{m}-\tilde{S}_{n}
is:

(2.57)

U_{2}:={\sum}\limits_{p\leq x}\frac{\chi(p^{2})}{2p}.

But we see on expansion that

(2.58)

U_{2}^{k}={\sum}\limits_{r}\frac{a_{k,x}(r)\chi(r^{2})}{r}.

and applying the same arguments as in the proof of Lemma 3 in
[
Sou09
]
, with the orthogonality relations for Dirichlet characters shows that

(2.59)

\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[\left|U_{2}\right|^{2k}\right]\ll k!\left({\sum}\limits_{{e^{e^{m}}}\leq p\leq e^{e^{n}}}\frac{1}{2p^{2}}\right)^{k}\leq k!(1)^{k}.

Hence

(2.60)

\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[\left|\tilde{S}_{m}-\tilde{S}_{n}\right|^{2k}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[\left|U_{1}+U_{2}\right|^{2k}\right]\ll k!(m-n+1)^{k}

by Hölder’s Inequality.

∎

By setting
k=\lceil\frac{V^{2}}{m-n+1}\rceil
in (
2.52
), we get by Stirling’s formula and Markov’s inequality:

Lemma 2.9
.

Let q be a large integer, with
q\neq 2
mod
4,

V>0

. Then

(2.61)

\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\#\{\chi\text{ even primitive mod }q:\left|\tilde{S}_{m}-\tilde{S}_{n}\right|>V\ll\frac{V+1}{(m-n+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{V^{2}}{m}\right).

We require a stronger result for the moments of the real parts of the truncated sums.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
n<m<\mathscr{L}
and
2k\leq\frac{\log q}{3\log q_{m}}
. Then

(2.62)

\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[\left|S_{q_{m}}-S_{q_{n}}\right|^{2k}\right]\ll\frac{(2k)!}{2^{2k}k!}(\log\log q_{m}-\log\log q_{n})^{k}.

Note that (
2.62
) is stronger than the analogous Lemma A.2 in
[
AB23
]
.

Proof.

Let

(2.63)

a_{r,m,n}=\begin{cases}1&p\text{ a prime in }(q_{n},q_{m}]\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Then set
U_{m,n}=\sum\limits_{r}\frac{a_{r,m,n}}{r^{1/2}}\chi(r)
, and
T_{1}=\Re U_{m,n}

to be the real part of the sum
U_{m,n}.
Then
T_{1}
is the contribution of the primes to the difference
S_{m}-S_{n}
.
Then if we set
T_{2}=(S_{m}-S_{n})-T_{1}
, then
T_{2}
is the contribution of the square of primes to the difference.
Clearly, we have

(2.64)

\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[T_{1}^{2k}\right]=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum^{k}_{j=0}{2k\choose j}\mathbb{E}_{+}[U_{m,n}^{j}\overline{U_{m,n}}^{2k-j}].

Using the same orthogonality relations as in the proof of (
2.52
), and observing that the choice of
k

has length

q_{m}^{2k}\leq(q_{m})^{\frac{\log q}{3\log q_{m}}}=q^{\frac{1}{3}}
we obtain

(2.65)

\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[T_{1}^{2k}\right]\ll\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum^{k}_{j=0}{2k\choose j}\mathbb{E}[\hat{U}_{m,n}^{j}\overline{\hat{U}_{m,n}}^{2k-j}],

where

(2.66)

\hat{U}_{m,n}=\sum\limits_{q_{n}\leq p\leq q_{m}}\frac{a_{p}}{p^{\frac{1}{2}}}X(p),

and
X(r)
is defined in (
2.5
).
Under this definition, (
2.65
) becomes:

(2.67)

\mathbb{E}(T_{1})^{2k}\ll\mathbb{E}[\Re(\hat{U}_{m,n})^{2k}].

We need the following proposition, which is a straightforward computation.

Proposition 2.11
.

For any complex numbers
a_{p}
, if
X(p)
are as defined in (
2.5
), then we have:

(2.68)

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{x\leq p\leq y}(\Re\ a_{p}X(p))^{2k}\right]\leq\frac{(2k!)}{2^{k}k!}(s^{2})^{k},

where

(2.69)

s^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{x\leq p\leq y}\left|a_{p}\right|^{2}.

Note that the right-hand side of (
2.68
) is the moment of a Gaussian random variable with mean
0
and variance
s^{2}
.
Substituting the values of
a_{p}
from (
2.63
), and setting

\tilde{H}(\beta,u_{1},k_{1},u_{2},k_{2})={\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|f_{1}f_{2}\implies p\in(q_{m-1},q_{m}]\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(f_{1})\mu(f_{2})}{[u_{1}k_{2}f_{1},u_{2}k_{1}f_{2}]}\left(\frac{f_{1}f_{2}(u_{1}k_{2},u_{2}k_{1})^{2}}{(u_{1}k_{2}f_{1},u_{2}k_{1}f_{2})^{2}}\right)^{i\beta}.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
P_{4}(\beta)
be defined as in (

), and
\varUpsilon(\beta)
be as defined in (

), for

In order to prove Lemma

in Equation (

(q_{j-1},q_{j}]
, for

to be lifted in bounding

Given positive integers
c_{1},c_{2},f_{1}
and
f_{2}
with all their prime factors at most

let

(3.91)

U(c_{1},c_{2},f_{1},f_{2})=\frac{f_{1}f_{2}(c_{1},c_{2})^{2}}{(c_{1}f_{1},c_{2}f_{2})^{2}}.

Further define

(3.92)

\Theta_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{2})={\sum}_{p|f_{1},f_{2}\implies p\leq q_{l}}\frac{\mu(f_{1})\mu(f_{2}))}{[c_{1}f_{1},c_{2}f_{2}]}\frac{U(c_{1},c_{2},f_{1},f_{2})^{i\beta}-U(c_{1},c_{2},f_{1},f_{2})^{-i\beta}}{2i\beta}.

Then we see that Equation (

(3.93)

\varUpsilon(\beta)={\sum}_{u_{1},k_{1},u_{2},k_{2}}\left|\tilde{C}_{u_{1},k_{1}}\tilde{C}_{u_{2},k_{2}}\right|\left|\Theta_{\beta}(u_{1}k_{2},u_{2}k_{1})\right|.

).

.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
be integers with all their prime factors at most
q_{l}
.

•

If
c_{1}=c_{2}
, then

(3.94)

\Theta_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{1})=\frac{1}{c_{1}}\prod_{p\leq q_{l}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right){\sum}_{p^{\prime}\leq q_{l}}\left(\frac{2\log p^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}-1}\right)+o_{\beta\to 0^{+},F}(1).

•

If there exists a unique prime

such that
v_{p}(c_{1})\neq v_{p}(c_{2})\iff p=p^{\ast},
then

(3.95)

\Theta_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{2})=\frac{(p^{\ast}-1)\log p^{\ast}}{(p^{\ast})^{\max\{v_{p^{\ast}}(c_{1}),v_{p^{\ast}}(c_{2})\}+1}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq q_{l}\\
p\neq p^{\ast}\end{subarray}}\frac{p-1}{p^{\max\{v_{p}(c_{1}),v_{p}(c_{2})\}+1}}+o_{\beta\to 0^{+},F}(1).

•

If the prime factorisations of
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
differ in more than one prime, then

(3.96)

\lim_{\beta\to 0^{+}}\Theta_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{2})=0.

Proof.

Define

(3.97)

\Phi_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{2})={\sum}_{p|f_{1},f_{2}\implies p\leq q_{l}}\frac{\mu(f_{1})\mu(f_{2}))}{[c_{1}f_{1},c_{2}f_{2}]}U(c_{1},c_{2},f_{1},f_{2})^{i\beta}.

Then

(3.98)

\Theta_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{2})=\frac{\Phi_{\beta}-\Phi_{-\beta}}{2i\beta},

and we can calculate
\Phi_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{2})
as the sum of functions doubly multiplicative in
f_{1}
and
f_{2}.

We first calculate the diagonal terms
\Theta(c_{1},c_{1})
.
We have

(3.99)

\Phi_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{1})=\frac{1}{c_{1}}{\sum}_{p|f_{1},f_{2}\implies p\leq q_{l}}\frac{\mu(f_{1})\mu(f_{2})}{[f_{1},f_{2}]}\left(\frac{f_{1}f_{2}}{(f_{1},f_{2})^{2}}\right)^{i\beta}.

On the diagonal terms,
\Phi_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{1})
factors as an Euler product.
If we write
d_{p,i}=v_{p}(f_{i})
, then we obtain

(3.100)

\Phi_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{1})=\frac{1}{c_{1}}\prod_{p\leq q_{l}}{\sum}_{d_{p,1},d_{p,2}\in\{0,1\}}\frac{(-1)^{d_{p,1}+d_{p,2}}}{p^{\max\{d_{p,1},d_{p,2}\}}}(p^{k_{1}+k_{2}-2\min\{k_{1},k_{2}\}})^{i\beta}.

We can write this as a product over primes in the interval,

(3.101)

\prod_{p\leq q_{l}}A_{p,\beta},\qquad A_{p,\beta}=1+\frac{1}{p}-\frac{2p^{i\beta}}{p}.

Hence,

(3.102)

\Theta_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{1})=\frac{1}{2i\beta}(\Phi_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{1})-\Phi_{-\beta}(c_{1},c_{1}))=\frac{\Phi_{-\beta}(c_{1},c_{1})}{2i\beta}\left(\frac{\Phi_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{1})}{\Phi_{-\beta}(c_{1},c_{1})}-1\right).

Each term in the Euler product for
\frac{\Phi_{\beta}}{\Phi_{-\beta}}
is
1+O(\beta)
, hence we may express (
3.102
) as

(3.103)

\Theta_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{1})=(\Phi_{0}(c_{1},c_{1})+O(\beta)){\sum}_{p\leq q_{l}}\frac{1}{2i\beta}\left(\frac{A_{p,\beta}}{A_{p,-\beta}}-1\right).

But setting
\beta=0
in (
3.101
) shows that:

(3.104)

\Phi_{0}(c_{1},c_{1})=\frac{1}{c_{1}}\prod_{p\leq q_{l}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right).

For each prime
p\leq q_{l},

(3.105)

A_{p,\beta}=\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\left(1-\frac{2i\beta\log p}{p-1}\right)+O(\beta^{2}),

hence

(3.106)

\frac{A_{p,\beta}}{A_{p,-\beta}}=1-\frac{4i\beta\log p}{p-1}+O(\beta^{2}).

Hence as
\beta\to 0^{+}
in (
3.103
), and we take the sum over
the contribution of all the primes in
(q_{j-1},q_{j}]
to the sum on the right-hand side, we obtain:

(3.107)

\Theta_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{1})=\frac{1}{c_{1}}\prod_{p\leq q_{l}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right){\sum}_{p^{\prime}\leq q_{l}}\left(\frac{2\log p^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}-1}\right)+o_{\beta\to 0^{+},F}(1).

This proves Equation (
3.94
).

It remains to calculate the off-diagonal terms for
\Theta_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{2}).

The Euler product, instead of the diagonal term (
3.100
), becomes the more complicated formula:

(3.108)

\Phi_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{2})=\prod_{p\in(q_{j-1},q_{j}]}\xi_{\beta}(p,c_{1},c_{2}),

where

(3.109)

\xi_{\beta}(p,c_{1},c_{2})={\sum}_{d_{p,1},d_{p,2}\in\{0,1\}}\frac{(-1)^{d_{p,1}+d_{p,2}}}{p^{\max\{v_{p}(u_{1}k_{2})+d_{p,1},v_{p}(u_{2}k_{1})+d_{p,2}\}}}\left(\frac{p^{d_{p,1}+d_{p,2}+2\min\{v_{p}(u_{1}k_{2}),v_{p}(u_{2}k_{1})\}}}{p^{2\min\{d_{p,1}+v_{p}(u_{1}k_{2}),d_{p,2}+v_{p}(u_{2}k_{1})\}}}\right)^{i\beta}.

This is different to most of the Euler products, since some of the terms may be
O(\beta)
, rather than
1+O(\beta)
.
We remark that this doesn’t occur when splitting
\Phi_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{1})
into the
A_{p,\beta}
in Equation (
3.103
), because the leading term for
A_{p,\beta}
is
A_{p,0}=1-\frac{1}{p}
which is non-zero, and hence
\frac{A_{p,\beta}}{A_{p,-\beta}}=1+O(\beta)
.

We now evaluate
\xi_{\beta}(p,c_{1},c_{2});
the limiting value will depend on whether or not
v_{p}(c_{1})=v_{p}(c_{2}).

If
v_{p}(c_{1})=v_{p}(c_{2})
, then

(3.110)

\xi_{\beta}(p,c_{1},c_{2})=\frac{1-2p^{i\beta-1}+p^{-1}}{p^{\max\{v_{p}(c_{1}),v_{p}(c_{2}k)\}}}.

We expand this to first order terms in
\beta
to show:

(3.111)

\xi_{\beta}(p,c_{1},c_{2})=\frac{p-1}{p^{\max\{v_{p}(c_{1}),v_{p}(c_{2})\}+1}}+O(\beta).

If
v_{p}(c_{1})\neq v_{p}(c_{2})
, then

(3.112)

\xi_{\beta}(p,c_{1},c_{2})=\frac{1-p^{i\beta-1}-p^{-i\beta}+p^{-1}}{p^{\max\{v_{p}(c_{1}),v_{p}(c_{2})\}}}.

We expand to terms of order
\beta^{2}
to show:

(3.113)

\xi_{\beta}(p,c_{1},c_{2})=\frac{i(p-1)\beta\log p}{p^{\max\{v_{p}(c_{1}),v_{p}(c_{2})\}+1}}\times(1+O(\beta)).

From (
3.113
), we see that if
v_{p}(c_{1})\neq v_{p}(c_{2})
for more than one prime
p\leq q_{l}
, then substituting into (
3.108
), we will have
\Phi_{\pm\beta}=O(\beta^{2})
,
since more than one term in the product will be
O(\beta)
.
Substituting the values for
\Phi_{\pm\beta}
into (
3.98
), we see that
\Theta_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{2})=O(\beta)
,
meaning that the coefficient vanishes in the limit as
\beta
approaches zero. This proves Equation (
3.96
).

It remains to consider the case where the prime factorisations differ in exactly one prime.
If we have exactly one prime
p^{\ast}\leq q_{l}
such that
v_{p^{\ast}}(c_{1})\neq v_{p^{\ast}}(c_{2})
,
then (
3.113
) gives expressions for
\xi_{\beta}(p^{\ast},c_{1},c_{2})
and
\xi_{-\beta}(p^{\ast},c_{1},c_{2})
, while (
3.111
) gives expressions for
\xi_{\beta}(p,c_{1},c_{2})
and
\xi_{-\beta}(p,c_{1},c_{2})
for all other primes
p\leq q_{l}
. Substituting these expressions into (
3.98
), we see:

(3.114)

\Theta_{\beta}(c_{1},c_{2})=\frac{2i(p^{\ast}-1)\beta\log p^{\ast}}{(p^{\ast})^{\max\{v_{p^{\ast}}(c_{1}),v_{p^{\ast}}(c_{2})\}+1}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq q_{l}\\
p\neq p^{\ast}\end{subarray}}\frac{p-1}{p^{\max\{v_{p}(c_{1}),v_{p}(c_{2})\}+1}}\times(1+O(\beta))\times\frac{1}{2i\beta}.

This completes the proof of Equation (
3.95
).
∎

4.1.
Method of proof

The orthogonality relations in the previous section are very similar to the orthogonality relations used in the
t
-aspect in

]

1.5
provides the analogue for the twisted second moment of zeta. Using these points of reference, the method of proof for the large deviations in the

[

]
.

We consider the event

(4.1)

H=\{\log(L(1/2,\mathfrak{X})>V)\},

where
\mathfrak{X}
is drawn uniformly at random from the even primitive characters with modulus
q
.
Since the number of even, primitive characters is asymptotic to
\frac{\varphi(q)}{2}
, we have

(4.2)

\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\#\{\chi\text{ even, primitive mod }q:\left|L(1/2,\chi)\right|>V\}\asymp\mathbb{P}(H).

We partition the event
H
on
good events
pertaining to the partial sums
S_{n_{l}}
defined in (
1.14
) (and (
1.13
) for the complex ones).
More precisely, we consider the restrictions
\{S_{n_{l}}\in[L_{l},U_{l}]\}
where

(4.3)

L_{l}=\kappa n_{l}-\mathbf{s}(\log_{l+2}q)\qquad U_{l}=\kappa n_{l}+\mathbf{s}(\log_{l+2}q),

where
\kappa
is the
slope

(4.4)

\kappa=\frac{V}{\log\log q}.

We recall that
V\sim\alpha\log\log q
for some
0<\alpha<1,
so that
\kappa\to\alpha.

The idea is that if that the “full sum”
\log\left(L(1/2,\mathfrak{X})\right)
is close to
\kappa\log\log q
, then the most likely values for
S_{n_{l}}
should be
\kappa n_{l}
.
The terms
\pm\mathbf{s}(\log_{l+2}q)
handles the fluctuations around the linear interpolation.
To properly mollify
\log\left(L(1/2,\mathfrak{X})\right)
, more restrictions are needed.
We recursively define the following events, where the dependence on
\frac{1}{2}
and
\mathfrak{X}
is suppressed for simplicity:

(4.5)

\displaystyle G_{l}

\displaystyle=A_{l}\cap B_{l}\cap C_{l}\cap D_{l}

\displaystyle A_{l}

\displaystyle=A_{l-1}\cap\left\{\left|\tilde{S}_{n_{l}}-\tilde{S}_{n_{l-1}}\right|\leq\mathscr{A}(n_{l-1}-n_{l})\right\}

\displaystyle B_{l}

\displaystyle=B_{l-1}\cap\left\{S_{n_{l}}\leq U_{l}\right\}

\displaystyle C_{l}

\displaystyle=C_{l-1}\cap\left\{S_{n_{l}}\geq L_{l}\right\}

\displaystyle D_{l}

\displaystyle=D_{l-1}\cap\left\{\left|Le^{-S_{n_{l}}}\right|^{2}\leq c_{l}\left|LM_{1}...M_{l}\right|^{2}+e^{-\mathscr{D}(\log\log q-n_{l-1})}\right\}.

where
c_{l}=\prod^{l}_{j=1}(1+e^{-n_{j-1}})
, and
A_{0},B_{0},C_{0},D_{0}=\{\text{primitive even characters mod }q\}
is the full sample space.

The following proposition gathers the necessary estimate to bound the probability of

on the good events.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let

\ll\frac{e^{-\kappa^{2}n_{l+1}}}{\sqrt{\log\log q}}e^{2(\log\log q-n_{l+1})}\sqrt{n_{l+1}-n_{l}}e^{-(2-2\kappa)\mathbf{s}\log_{l+2}q}\\
\ll\frac{e^{-\kappa^{2}\log\log q}}{\sqrt{\log\log q}}(\log_{l+1}q)^{\frac{1}{2}+\kappa^{2}-(2-2\kappa)\mathbf{s}}.

By construction of
\mathbf{s}
, the exponent of
\log_{l+1}q
is negative. We remark that
\log_{l+1}q\gg\log_{l+2}q,
so that this bound is even sharper than the total bound for Proposition
4.1
(ii).

It remains to bound
\mathbb{P}(D_{l+1}^{c}\cap A_{l+1}\cap G_{l}).
The event
D_{l+1}^{c}\cap A_{l+1}\cap G_{l}
is contained in

(4.51)

\{\left|LM_{1}...M_{l}\right|>e^{\mathscr{A}(\log\log q-n_{l})}\}\cap G_{l}.

This is because
\{\left|LM_{1}...M_{l}\right|\leq e^{\mathscr{A}(\log\log q-n_{l})}\}\cap D_{l}\}
is in
A_{l+1}\cap D_{l+1}
by using Lemma
4.2
.
By Markov’s inequality, we have

(4.52)

\mathbb{P}(\{\left|LM_{1}...M_{l}\right|>e^{\mathscr{A}(\log\log q-n_{l})}\}\cap G_{l})\ll e^{-2\mathscr{A}(\log\log q-n_{l})}\mathbb{E}_{\oplus}\left[\left|LM_{1}...M_{l}\right|^{2}1(G_{l})\right].

Using Lemma
4.4
, we can bound the right-hand side as

(4.53)

\displaystyle\ll e^{-2(\mathscr{A}-1)(\log\log q-n_{l})}\times\frac{e^{-\frac{L_{l}^{2}}{n_{l}}}}{\sqrt{n_{l}}}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{e^{-\kappa^{2}\log\log q}}{\sqrt{\log\log q}}(\log_{l+1}q)^{2\mathbf{s}\left(\kappa+1-\mathscr{A}\right)}.

The choice of
\mathscr{A}
ensures the exponent is negative.

4.5.
Proof of Proposition
4.1
(iii)

We have the obvious bound
\mathbf{P}(H\cap G_{\mathcal{L}})\leq\mathbf{P}(G_{\mathcal{L}})
.
It then suffices to apply Lemma
4.4
. Note that the length of the interval
[L_{\mathcal{L}},U_{\mathcal{L}}]
is of order one.
Therefore,

(4.54)

\mathbf{P}(G_{\mathcal{L}})\ll\sup_{v\in[L_{\mathcal{L}},U_{\mathcal{L}}]}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{L}}}}e^{-v^{2}/{n_{\mathcal{L}}}}\ll\frac{e^{-\kappa^{2}\log\log q}}{\sqrt{\log\log q}}.

5.
Proof of Corollary
1.2

Corollary
1.2
follows from Theorem
1.1
in a similar way to the proof of Corollary 1.2 in
[
AB23
]
.

Proof.

Consider the CDF of the random variable
\log L(1/2,\chi)
with
\chi
drawn uniformly from the even primitive characters mod
q
, i.e.,
F(V)=\mathbb{P}(\log\left|L(1/2,\chi)\right|\leq V)
.
Then if we set

(5.1)

\mathscr{M}_{\beta}=\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[\left|L(1/2,\chi)\right|^{2\beta}\right],

for
0<\beta<1
, the moments can be written as:

(5.2)

\mathscr{M}_{\beta}=\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}e^{2\beta V}dF(V)=\left[e^{2\beta V}S(V)\right]^{+\infty}_{-\infty}+\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}2\beta e^{2\beta V}S(V)dV.

Since
S(V)\leq 1
, the boundary term at
-\infty
is zero.
Since there are only finitely many characters, the boundary term at
+\infty
is also zero. The contribution of negative values of
V
in (
5.2
) is negligible, since

(5.3)

\int^{0}_{-\infty}2\beta e^{2\beta V}S(V)dV\leq\int^{0}_{-\infty}2\beta e^{2\beta V}dV=1.

In order to estimate
\int^{\infty}_{0}2\beta e^{2\beta V}S(V)
, consider
\beta_{-}
and
\beta_{+}
with

(5.4)

0<\beta_{-}<\beta<\beta_{+}<1.

We want to show the dominant contribution of to the integral in (
5.2
) comes from the interval
\left[\beta_{-}\log\log q,\beta_{+}\log\log q\right]
.
We take

\beta_{-}=\frac{\beta}{4},\quad\beta_{+}=\frac{3+\beta}{4}.

Using Theorem
1.1
to bound
S(V),
we have

(5.5)

\displaystyle\int^{\beta_{+}\log\log q}_{\beta_{-}\log\log q}\beta e^{2\beta V}S(V)dV

\displaystyle\ll\int^{\beta_{+}\log\log q}_{\beta_{-}\log\log q}e^{2\beta V}\frac{e^{-\frac{V^{2}}{\log\log q}}}{\sqrt{\log\log q}}dV

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\beta^{2}\log\log q}}{{\sqrt{\log\log q}}}\int^{\beta_{+}\log\log q}_{\beta_{-}\log\log q}e^{-(\beta\log\log q-V)^{2}/\log\log q}dV

\displaystyle\ll e^{\beta^{2}\log\log q}.

The contribution of the interval
[0,\beta_{-}\log\log q]
to the integral in (
5.2
) is of a lower order since it is bounded by:

(5.6)

\int^{\beta_{-}\log\log q}_{0}2\beta e^{2\beta V}dV\leq e^{\frac{\beta^{2}\log\log q}{2}}.

For the interval
\left[\beta_{+}\log\log q,\infty\right),
we require a bound on
S(V)
in this range. But we have

(5.7)

\mathbb{E}_{+}[\left|L(1/2,\chi)\right|^{2}]\ll\log q,

and so Markov’s inequality yields
S(V)\ll e^{-2V}\log q
.
Hence the contribution in the range
[\beta_{+}\log\log q,\infty)
to the integral in (
5.2
) is

(5.8)

\ll{\log q}\int^{\infty}_{\beta_{+}\log\log q}2\beta e^{2(\beta-1)V}dV=\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}e^{(2(\beta-1)\beta_{+}+1)\log\log q}.

This is
o(e^{\beta^{2}\log\log q})
by the choice of
\beta_{+}.

∎

Since we chose the parameters in Section
4.2
such that the inequalities held uniformly for
0<\kappa<1
, we see that the implicit constant in Equation
1.2
is bounded as
\beta\to 0
. As previously stated, this contrasts with the proof in
[
AB23
]
, where in Equation
1.8
, their bound had
C_{\beta}\to\infty
as
\beta\to 0
.
