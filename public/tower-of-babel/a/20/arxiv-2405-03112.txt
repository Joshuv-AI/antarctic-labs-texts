Inducibility of rainbow graphs
Emily Cairncross, Clayton Mizgerd, Dhruv Mubayi

Abstract
Fix k ≥ 11 k\geq 11 and a rainbow k k -clique R R . We prove that the inducibility of R R is k ! / ( k k − k ) k!/(k^{k}-k) . An extremal construction is a balanced recursive blow-up of R R . This answers a question posed by Huang, that is a generalization of an old problem of Erdős and Sós. It remains open to determine the minimum k k for which our result is true. More generally, we prove that there is an absolute constant C > 0 C>0 such that every k k -vertex connected rainbow graph with minimum degree at least C ​ log ⁡ k C\log k has inducibility k ! / ( k k − k ) k!/(k^{k}-k) .



1
Introduction

Fix a graph
F
on
k
vertices and another graph
G
on
n>k
vertices.
Write
I(F,G)
for the number of
k
-subsets
S\subset V(G)

such that
G[S]\cong F
and let

\varrho(F,G):=\frac{I(F,G)}{\binom{n}{k}}.

Many foundational questions in extremal graph theory deal with estimating
\varrho(F,G)
for various choices of
F
and
G
. One central question is to determine the minimum value when
F
is a clique and
G
has a specified edge density
[
15
,
13
,
16
]
, but there are also many fundamental questions about the maximum value regardless of edge density. This is the direction we take here.

Let
I(F,n)
be the maximum of

I(F,G)
over all
n
vertex graphs
G
. A standard averaging argument implies that

\operatorname{ind}(F,n):=\frac{I(F,n)}{{\binom{n}{k}}}\leq\frac{I(F,n-1)}{{\binom{n-1}{k}}}=\operatorname{ind}(F,n-1).

Thus,
\operatorname{ind}(F,n)
is a decreasing sequence bounded below by zero, so it has a limit. Define the
inducibility
of
F
to be

\operatorname{ind}(F):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{ind}(F,n).

The
iterated balanced blow-up of a graph
F
is a family
\mathcal{G}_{F}(n)
of graphs on
n
vertices defined inductively as follows. Label
V(F)
with
[k]:=\{1,\ldots,k\}
. For
n<k
, the family
\mathcal{G}_{F}(n)
contains only the empty graph on
n
vertices. For
n\geq k
, for any
G\in\mathcal{G}_{F}(n)
, we have a partition
V(G)=V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{k}
with the following properties:

1.

For all
i,j\in[k]
,
\big||V_{i}|-|V_{j}|\big|\leq 1
.

2.

For all
i\in[k]
, the induced subgraph
G[V_{i}]\in\mathcal{G}_{F}(|V_{i}|)
.

3.

For all
v\in V_{i},w\in V_{j}
with
i\neq j
, we have
vw\in E(G)
if and only if
ij\in E(F)
.

In many interesting cases, the construction above achieves the inducibility of
F
and we now define this formally (our definition is slightly different than that in
[
11
]
).

Definition 1.1
.

A graph
F
is a
fractalizer
if

\operatorname{ind}(F)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\max_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{F}(n)}\varrho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F,G}}\right).

In other words, the iterated balanced blow-up of
F
achieves the inducibility.

The subgraph induced by every
k
-set comprising exactly one vertex in each
V_{i}
is isomorphic to
F
. Consequently, for every
G\in\mathcal{G}_{F}(n)
,

I(F,G)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}I(F,G[V_{i}])+\prod_{i=1}^{k}|V_{i}|.

Together with a standard computation (see, e.g.
[
12
]
), this yields

\operatorname{ind}(F)\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}\max_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{F}(n)}\varrho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F,G}}\right)\geq\frac{k!}{k^{k}-k}.

(1)

Hence, if
F
is a fractalizer, then
\operatorname{ind}(F)\geq k!/(k^{k}-k)
.
In most cases we consider, the fact that
F
is a fractalizer will imply further that
\operatorname{ind}(F)=k!/(k^{k}-k)
.

The fundamental conjecture in this area, due to Pippenger and Golumbic
[
14
]
, states that for
k\geq 5
, the cycle
C_{k}
is a fractalizer and satisfies
\operatorname{ind}(C_{k})=k!/(k^{k}-k)
.
This conjecture has been resolved for
k=5
by Balogh, Hu, Lidický, and Pfender
[
1
]
(see also
[
11
]
), but remains open for all
k\geq 6
. Král, Norin, and Volec
[
10
]
showed that
I(C_{k},n)\leq 2n^{k}/k^{k}
.
More generally, Fox, Huang, and Lee
[
6
]
and Yuster
[
17
]
independently proved that random graphs are fractalizers asymptotically almost surely. Fox, Sauermann, and Wei
[
7
]
further proved that random Cayley graphs of abelian groups with small number of vertices removed are almost surely fractalizers.

We now consider these notions on colored and directed structures. A
tournament
is an orientation of a complete graph. An edge-coloring of a graph or tournament
G
is a function
\chi:E(G)\to T
where
T
is a set of colors; we say that
G
is
T
-colored. A colored graph or tournament
G
is
rainbow
if
\chi
is injective. Two colored graphs (or tournaments)
G
and
H
are
isomorphic
, written
G\cong H
, if there exists a bijection
\varphi:V(G)\to V(H)
such that the colors (and orientations) of all edges are preserved under
\varphi
. If
F
is a colored tournament or colored complete graph, then
\operatorname{ind}(F)
is defined identically as in the graph case, but with these altered definitions of graph isomorphism; naturally, the underlying graph
G
should have the colors or orientations corresponding to
F
. If
F
is an arbitrary colored graph, then we can color all missing edges with a single new color and view
F
as a colored complete graph. Consequently, we can define
fractalizer
for all these structures.

There are very few results on the inducibility of colored, oriented, or directed structures. The first exact result which involved an iterated construction was due to Huang
[
8
]
who determined the inducibility of the directed star. Later, in order to solve an old conjecture of Erdős and Hajnal
[
5
]
in hypergraph Ramsey theory, the third author and Razborov
[
12
]
proved the following result for
k\geq 4
(the case that
k=3
was proven earlier by Conlon, Fox, and Sudakov
[
3
]
).

Theorem 1.2

(
[
12
]
)
.

All rainbow tournaments
R
on
k\geq 4
vertices are fractalizers. In particular,
\mathrm{ind}(R)=k!/(k^{k}-k)
.

In this paper, we consider the question addressed by Theorem
1.2
in the undirected setting. The first conjecture in this setting is due to Erdős and Sós from the 1970s (see
[
5
, Equation (20)]
), and implies, in particular, that a rainbow triangle is not a fractalizer. Their conjecture was proved by Balogh et. al.
[
2
]
, who showed that a blow-up of a properly
3
-edge-colored
K_{4}
(instead of a rainbow
K_{3}
) achieves the inducibility of the rainbow triangle. See also
[
4
]
for similar computations, but in terms of the number of edges of each color instead of the number of vertices.

Huang
[
9
]
asked whether Theorem
1.2
can be extended to the undirected setting for cliques of size larger than three. This, in particular, would imply that the phenomenon conjectured by Erdős and Sós and proved in
[
2
]
(that
K_{k}
is not a fractalizer for
k=3
) fails to hold for larger
k
. Our first result addresses Huang’s question and proves that rainbow
K_{k}
are fractalizers for
k\geq 11
.

Theorem 1.3
.

All rainbow cliques
R
on
k\geq 11
vertices are fractalizers. In particular,

\mathrm{ind}(R)=\frac{k!}{k^{k}-k}.

We make the following observations regarding Theorem
1.3
.

•

Theorem
1.3
implies Theorem
1.2
for
k\geq 11
, since any construction of a tournament inducing
\ell
rainbow copies of
R
yields a corresponding construction of a complete graph that induces at least
\ell
rainbow (undirected) copies of
R
by ignoring orientations.

•

Similarly, if a graph
G
on
k
vertices is known to have inducibility
k!/(k^{k}-k)
, then the rainbow
k
-clique is a fractalizer as well by the following argument. Let
R
be a rainbow
k
-clique and let
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{m}\in E(R)
such that
(V(R),\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\})
is a rainbow copy of
G
. Let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}
be the colors assigned to
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
, respectively. Then any construction of an edge-colored graph inducing
\ell
rainbow copies of
R
induces at least
\ell
copies of
G
by deleting all edges except those colored by
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}
and then ignoring the edge colors. It follows that
\operatorname{ind}(R)\leq\operatorname{ind}(G)=k!/(k^{k}-k)
, so
R
is a fractalizer. Thus, the result of
[
1
]
that
C_{5}
is a fractalizer with
\operatorname{ind}(C_{5})=5!/(5^{5}-5)
implies that the rainbow
5
-clique is a fractalizer, and the result of
[
6
]
that random graphs are almost surely fractalizers implies that the rainbow
k
-clique is a fractalizer for large
k
.

•

We believe our proof of Theorem
1.3
has been optimized and requires
k\geq 11
. As
[
1
]
showed that rainbow
5
-cliques are fractalizers, and
[
2
]
showed that rainbow
3
-cliques are not fractalizers, it remains open to determine whether rainbow
k_{0}
-cliques are fractalizers only for
k_{0}\in\{4,6,7,8,9,10\}
.

Our proof of Theorem
1.3
follows the broad framework of the proof of Theorem
1.2
but there are several nontrivial technical difficulties that need to be addressed in the undirected setting. The difficulties arise due to the following reason: the role that each endpoint of an edge plays in a rainbow copy of a tournament is determined by the color and orientation of the edge, but this is no longer true in the undirected setting.
We overcome these obstacles by adding some new ideas, at the expense of requiring a slightly higher value of
k
. For example, our proof of Theorem
1.3
requires a bound on the color degree of a vertex and this was not needed in
[
12
]
.

For large values of
k
, we prove the following more general result which shows that the analog of Theorem
1.3
holds for much sparser graphs. The proof requires several major new ideas.

Theorem 1.4
.

There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that all connected rainbow graphs
R
with
k
vertices and minimum degree at least
C\log k
are fractalizers and satisfy

\mathrm{ind}(R)=\frac{k!}{k^{k}-k}.

We make the following observations regarding Theorem
1.4
.

•

Theorem
1.4
implies Theorem
1.3
for large
k
, since
R
may be viewed as a rainbow
k
-clique with edges deleted. Let
c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{m}
be the colors assigned to the deleted edges. Any construction of a colored complete graph inducing
\ell
rainbow copies of the rainbow
k
-clique yields at least
\ell
rainbow copies of
R
by deleting edges colored
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}
.

•

The requirement that
R
is connected in the statement of Theorem
1.4
is necessary, as disconnected rainbow graphs without isolated vertices are not fractalizers (see Section
4
).

•

We are not able to show that our requirement on minimum degree is tight, and this remains open.

Theorem
1.3
is proven in Section
2
and Theorem
1.4
is proven in Section
3
. In Section
4
, we justify the second observation above.

Proof of Theorem
1.3

We give the proof of Theorem
1.3
in the following subsections.

2.1
Setup

Fix
k\geq 11
and
T=\binom{[k]}{2}
. Let
R
be a
T
-colored rainbow
k
-clique with coloring function
\chi_{R}
and for concreteness, put
V(R):=[k]
and
\chi_{R}(ij)=\{i,j\}
for all
i,j\in[k]
.

Set

a:=\frac{k!}{k^{k}-k}.

Our goal is to prove that
\operatorname{ind}(R)\leq a
. To this end, fix
\gamma>0
and assume for contradiction
\operatorname{ind}(R)=a+\gamma
. Next choose
0<{\varepsilon}<\min\{\gamma,\operatorname{ind}(R)\}/100
. Let
c_{0}
be chosen so that
\operatorname{ind}(R,n)\leq\operatorname{ind}(R)+\varepsilon
for all
n>c_{0}
. Choose
n_{0}\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lceil 2k!c_{0}/{\varepsilon}}}\right\rceil
such that

\frac{n^{k}}{(n)_{k}}<1+{\varepsilon}

(2)

and

a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}-\binom{n-1}{k-1}}}\right)<\gamma\binom{n-1}{k-1}-{\binom{n-2}{k-2}}

(3)

for all
n>n_{0}
. This is possible since
\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{k}/(n)_{k}=1
and
n^{k-1}/(k-1)!-\binom{n-1}{k-1}=O(n^{k-2})
, while
\gamma\binom{n-1}{k-1}-{\binom{n-2}{k-2}}=\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{k-1}}}\right)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
Suppose that
n>n_{0}
is given and
H
is a
T
-colored
n
-vertex graph with coloring function
\chi_{H}
achieving
I(R,n)
. This implies

I(R,H)=I(R,n)=\operatorname{ind}(R,n){\binom{n}{k}}

where
a+\gamma=\operatorname{ind}(R)\leq\operatorname{ind}(R,n)\leq\operatorname{ind}(R)+\varepsilon=a+\gamma+\varepsilon
.

Definition 2.1
.

For
q\geq 0
and
t>0
, let
p(q,t)
be the maximum of
\prod_{i}q_{i}
where
q_{1}+\cdots+q_{t}=q
and each
q_{i}\geq 0
an integer.

The AMGM inequality yields
p(q,t)\leq(q/t)^{t}
and it is easy to see that

p(q,t)p(q^{\prime},t^{\prime})\leq p(q+q^{\prime},t+t^{\prime})

(4)

for all
q,q^{\prime}\geq 0
and
t,t^{\prime}>0
(see Appendix).

For a vertex
x
in
V(H)
and
i\in[k]
, write
d_{i}(x)
for the number of copies of
R
containing
x
where
x
plays the role of vertex
i
in
R
.
More formally,
d_{i}(x)
is the number of isomorphic embeddings
\phi:R\to H
such that
\phi(i)=x
.
Let
d(x)=\sum_{i}d_{i}(x)
be the number of copies of
R
containing
x
. We will refer to this as the
degree of
x
in
H
. Similarly, let
d(x,y)
be the number of copies of
R
containing both
x
and
y
. For
i\in[k]
, let
N_{i}(x)
be the set of
y\in V(H)\setminus\{x\}
for which there is a copy of
R
in
H
containing both
x
and
y
in which
x
plays the role of vertex
i
in
R
. Note that we do not have
N_{j}(x)\cap N_{j^{\prime}}(x)=\emptyset
for
j\neq j^{\prime}
, but all edges between
N_{j}(x)\cap N_{j^{\prime}}(x)
and
x
have the same color. However,
N_{i}(x)
has a (unique) partition
\cup_{j\neq i}N_{i}^{j}(x)
where
N_{i}^{j}(x)
comprises those
y
such that
x,y
lie in a copy of
R
with
x
playing the role of
i
and
y
playing the role of
j
. Indeed, the partition is obtained based on the color of a vertex to
x
. This gives

d(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}(x)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{j\neq i}|N_{i}^{j}(x)|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}p(|N_{i}(x)|,k-1).

(5)

We partition
V(H)
into
V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{k}
, where

V_{i}=\{x\in V(H):|N_{i}(x)|\geq|N_{j}(x)|\hbox{ for all }j\neq i\}.

If there is a tie, we break it arbitrarily. Set
n_{i}=|V_{i}|
for all
i\in[k]
.

2.2
Minimum degree

Here we show that a standard technique in extremal graph theory can be used to prove that
each vertex of
H
lies in at least the average number of copies of
R
(apart from a small error term).

Lemma 2.2
.

d(x)\geq an^{k-1}/(k-1)!
for all
x\in V(H)
.

Proof.

We write
d=b\pm c
for the inequalities
b-c\leq d\leq b+c
. Denote the average degree of
H
by

d(H):=\frac{k\cdot I(R,H)}{n}=\operatorname{ind}(R,n){\binom{n-1}{k-1}}.

We claim that for every
x\in V(H)

d(x)=d(H)\pm{\binom{n-2}{k-2}}.

(6)

This follows from a standard application of Zykov symmetrization. Indeed, if the degrees of two vertices
x
and
y
differ by more than
{\binom{n-2}{k-2}}
, say
d(x)>d(y)+{\binom{n-2}{k-2}}
, then we can delete
y
and duplicate
x
, meaning we add a new vertex
x^{\prime}
with
\chi_{H}(x^{\prime}z)=\chi_{H}(xz)
for all other vertices
z
, and
\chi_{H}(xx^{\prime})
can be arbitrary. This transformation increases the number of copies of
R
by at least

d(x)-d(y)-d(x,y)\geq d(x)-d(y)-{\binom{n-2}{k-2}}>0,

contradicting the maximality
I(R,H)=I(R,n)
. Hence all degrees lie in an interval of length at most
{\binom{n-2}{k-2}}
and (
6
) follows, since this interval must contain
d(H)
.
In particular, the minimum degree is at least

d(H)-{\binom{n-2}{k-2}}=\operatorname{ind}(R,n){\binom{n-1}{k-1}}-{\binom{n-2}{k-2}}\geq(a+\gamma){\binom{n-1}{k-1}}-{\binom{n-2}{k-2}}>a\frac{n^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}

for
n>n_{0}
. The last inequality follows from (
3
).
∎

2.3
Maximum color degree

Let

\alpha:=\frac{\max_{x,i,j}d_{\{i,j\}}(x)}{n}

where the maximum is taken over all vertices
x\in V(H)
and all colors
\{i,j\}\in T
and
d_{\{i,j\}}(x)
is the number of edges in
H
incident with
x
in color
\{i,j\}
. We upper bound this value.

Lemma 2.3
.

\alpha\leq 0.4
.

Proof.

Let
x,i,j
achieve this maximum, so that
d_{\{i,j\}}(x)=\alpha n
.
Then
|N_{i}^{j}(x)|\leq\alpha n
and
N_{i}(x)
has a partition
N_{i}^{j}(x)\bigcup\cup_{\ell\neq j}N_{i}^{\ell}(x)
where every copy of
R
containing
x
with
x
playing the role of
i
has exactly one vertex in each
N_{i}^{\ell}(x)
for all
\ell\in[k]\setminus\{i\}
. Further,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\bigcup_{\ell\neq j}N_{i}^{\ell}(x)}}\right|\leq n-d_{\{i,j\}}(x)

since a vertex incident to an edge colored
\{i,j\}
cannot play the role of
\ell\neq i,j
.
Consequently,

d_{i}(x)\leq|N_{i}^{j}(x)|\cdot p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-d_{\{i,j\}}(x),k-2}}\right)\leq\alpha\,n\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)n}{k-2}}}\right)^{k-2}.

The same upper bound holds for
d_{j}(x)
. For
\ell\not\in\{i,j\}
, we have
N_{\ell}(x)\leq n-d_{\{i,j\}}(x)
since
x
is playing the role of
\ell
, so we cannot include an edge incident to
x
of color
\{i,j\}
since the color must include
\ell
. Hence

d_{\ell}(x)\leq p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-d_{\{i,j\}}(x),k-1}}\right)\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)n}{k-1}}}\right)^{k-1}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)n}{k-2}}}\right)^{k-1}.

Altogether this yields

d(x)\leq 2\,\alpha\,n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)n}{k-2}}}\right)^{k-2}+(k-2)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)n}{k-2}}}\right)^{k-1}=(1+\alpha)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{k-2}}}\right)^{k-2}n^{k-1}.

Suppose for contradiction that
\alpha>0.4
. Since
k\geq 3
,
(1+\alpha)(1-\alpha)^{k-2}
is a decreasing function of
\alpha
for
\alpha\in(0.4,1]
,
and
d(x)\geq an^{k-1}/(k-1)!
by Lemma
2.2
. Therefore

\frac{1}{k^{k-1}-1}=\frac{a}{(k-1)!}\leq\frac{d(x)}{n^{k-1}}\leq 1.4\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{0.6}{k-2}}}\right)^{k-2}.

(7)

However, this fails to hold for
k\geq 11
(see Appendix), and we conclude that
\alpha\leq 0.4
as desired.
∎

2.4
The second largest neighborhood

For a vertex
x\in V(H)
, let
Z(x)
be the second largest set
in
\{N_{1}(x),\ldots,N_{k}(x)\}
and define

z:=z_{k,n}=\max_{x\in V(H)}\frac{|Z(x)|}{n}.

Lemma 2.4
.

z\leq 0.5
.

Proof.

Let
x
be such that
z=|Z(x)|/n
. Let
a_{i}=|N_{i}(x)|/n
and assume by relabeling that
a_{1}\geq a_{2}=z\geq a_{3}\geq\cdots\geq a_{k}
. Since
N_{j}(x)\cap N_{j^{\prime}}(x)\cap N_{j^{\prime\prime}}(x)=\emptyset
for any three distinct
j,j^{\prime},j^{\prime\prime}

we have
\sum a_{i}\leq 2
. Let
a_{3}+\cdots+a_{k}=s\leq 2-(a_{1}+z)
. Write
s=qz+r
where
q\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}
and
0\leq r<z
. If
x\leq y
, then
x^{k-1}+y^{k-1}<(x-\rho)^{k-1}+(y+\rho)^{k-1}
for
0<\rho<x
by convexity of
x^{k-1}
so successively increasing the largest
a_{i}
to
z
and decreasing the smallest
a_{j}
to 0 or
r
, we obtain

\sum_{i=3}^{k}a_{i}^{k-1}\leq qz^{k-1}+r^{k-1}\leq qz^{k-1}+\frac{r}{z}z^{k-1}=\frac{s}{z}z^{k-1}\leq\frac{2-(a_{1}+z)}{z}z^{k-1}.

Consequently,

\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{k-1}=a_{1}^{k-1}+z^{k-1}+\sum_{i=3}^{k}a_{i}^{k-1}\leq a_{1}^{k-1}+z^{k-1}+\frac{2-(a_{1}+z)}{z}z^{k-1}.

Since
a_{1}\geq z
, taking the derivative shows that for any
z
, this expression is increasing with
a_{1}
.
Using Lemma
2.3
, we note that
a_{1}+z\leq 1+\alpha<1.4
since

|N_{1}(x)|+|Z(x)|=|N_{1}(x)\cup Z(x)|+|N_{1}(x)\cap Z(x)|\leq n+d_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,2}}\right\}}(x)\leq n+\alpha n<1.4\cdot n.

Thus
a_{1}<1.4-z
and
a_{1}\leq 1
, so

\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{k-1}\leq a_{1}^{k-1}+z^{k-1}+\frac{2-(a_{1}+z)}{z}z^{k-1}\leq(\min\{1.4-z,1\})^{k-1}+z^{k-1}+\frac{0.6}{z}z^{k-1}.

Using (
5
) and Lemma
2.2
yields

\frac{1}{k^{k-1}-1}\leq\frac{d(x)}{n^{k-1}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{a_{i}}{k-1}}}\right)^{k-1}\leq\frac{1}{(k-1)^{k-1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\min\{1.4-z,1\})^{k-1}+z^{k-1}+\frac{0.6}{z}z^{k-1}}}\right).

Multiplying by
(k-1)^{k-1}
and using the fact that

2.6
Counting the copies of

in

Here we describe the broad framework we will use to count copies of

Call a copy

of

in

if it includes exactly one vertex in

for all

. We partition the copies of

in

comprises those copies that lie entirely inside some

We will bound each of these three terms separately. First, note that

(10)

Next we turn to

Let

Note that the color of some pair in every member of

This with along with (

h_{g}\leq\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}n_{\ell}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\delta{\binom{n}{2}}}{\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}n_{i}n_{j}}}}\right)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}n_{\ell}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\delta{\binom{n}{2}}}{{\binom{n}{2}}-\sum_{i}{\binom{n_{i}}{2}}}}}\right).

(12)

Our next task is to upper bound

. For a vertex

Let us enumerate the set

pairs in

If

and

Let

Observe that each

Let

in

(13)

On the other hand, we can bound

Finally, (

Using (

Since

\delta\in I\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1-\sum_{i}{\binom{n_{i}}{2}}/{\binom{n}{2}}}}\right]

2.7
The extremal case

Claim 2.6
.

If
\delta=0
, then
\operatorname{ind}(R)\leq a
.

Proof.

If
\delta=0
, then (

Let

We begin by bounding the summation in (

Suppose

Now, we consider the other endpoint of

(a)

\displaystyle d_{i}(x)\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{|B(x)|}{k_{1}(i)}}}\right)^{k_{1}(i)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n-|B(x)|}{k-k_{1}(i)-1}}}\right)^{k-k_{1}(i)-1}\hskip 199.16928pt

(b)

\displaystyle d_{i}(x)\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{|N_{i}(x)|}{k-1}}}\right)^{k-1}

(c)

\displaystyle d_{i}(x)\leq d_{\{i,j\}}(x)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n-d_{\{i,j\}}(x)}{k-2}}}\right)^{k-2}.

Further, the number of copies of
R
in
H
containing vertices
x,y\in V(H)
such that
x\in V(H)
plays the role of vertex
i\in[k]
in
R
is at most

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{|N_{i}(x)|}{k-2}}}\right)^{k-2}.

Proof.

We start by proving the three upper bounds on
d_{i}(x)
. To count the number of copies of
R
in
H
where
x
plays the role of
i
, we will recursively partition
|N_{i}(x)|
. First, we pick
k_{1}(i)
vertices from
B(x)\cap N_{i}(x)\subset V(H)
to play the role of the vertices adjacent to
i
in
R
. Notice that we may partition
B(x)\cap N_{i}(x)
into
k_{1}(i)
parts based on the color of each vertex to
x
as it uniquely determines its possible role in a copy of
R
. Set
B_{1}:=B(x)\cap N_{i}(x)
. We now recursively define
B_{r}
for all
r\in[\epsilon(i)]
. Let
2\leq r\leq\epsilon(i)
, let
m:=k_{1}(i)+\cdots+k_{r-1}(i)
, and suppose that we have chosen
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{m}\in V(H)
to play the roles of all vertices at distance
r-1
or less from
i
in
R
. Then

\displaystyle B_{r}:=

\displaystyle\,B_{r}(x,B_{1},B_{2},\ldots,B_{r-1},y_{1},\ldots,y_{k_{r-1}(i)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,N_{i}(x)\cap\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B(y_{1})\cup\cdots\cup B(y_{k_{r-1}(i)})}}\right)\setminus\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x\cup B_{1}\cup\cdots\cup B_{r-1}}}\right).

Here,
B_{r}
is the set of vertices in
H
that can play the role of vertices at distance
r
from
i
in
R
, given that we have already selected all vertices at distance at most
r-1
from
i
.

Note that by definition,
B_{r}\cap B_{\ell}=\emptyset
for all
r,\ell\in[\epsilon(i)]
and
\bigcup B_{r}\subseteq N_{i}(x)
. For the remainder of the proof, we write
k_{r}:=k_{r}(i)
for all
r\in[\epsilon(i)]
for convenience.

Each vertex
v\in B_{r}
has an edge to at least one of
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k_{r-1}}
. The color of this edge uniquely determines the role that
v
may play in a copy of
R
, so this allows us to uniquely partition
B_{r}
into
k_{r}
parts. We note that it may be the case that
v
cannot legally play any role, but that only decreases the number of possible copies of
R
, so we may assume that this does not occur. Let
P_{r}:=P(B_{r},k_{r})
be the set of tuples
\vec{y}\in B_{r}^{k_{r}}
with one vertex from each part of
B_{r}
,
so

|P_{r}|\leq p(|B_{r}|,k_{r}).

This gives

d_{i}(x)\leq\sum_{\vec{y}_{1}\in P_{1}}\cdots\sum_{\vec{y}_{\epsilon(i)-1}\in P_{\epsilon(i)-1}}p(|B_{\epsilon(i)}|,k_{\epsilon(i)})\leq\sum_{\vec{y}_{1}\in P_{1}}\cdots\sum_{\vec{y}_{\epsilon(i)-1}\in P_{\epsilon(i)-1}}p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|N_{i}(x)|-\sum_{r=1}^{\epsilon(i)-1}|B_{r}|,k_{\epsilon(i)}}}\right).

Using (
4
) from Section
2.1
we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{y}_{\epsilon(i)-1}\in P_{\epsilon(i)-1}}~p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|N_{i}(x)|-\sum_{r=1}^{\epsilon(i)-1}|B_{r}|,k_{\epsilon(i)}}}\right)

\displaystyle=p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|B_{\epsilon(i)-1}|,k_{\epsilon(i)-1}}}\right)\cdot p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|N_{i}(x)|-\sum_{r=1}^{\epsilon(i)-1}|B_{r}|,k_{\epsilon(i)}}}\right)

\displaystyle=p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|N_{i}(x)|-\sum_{r=1}^{\epsilon(i)-2}|B_{r}|,k_{\epsilon(i)-1}(i)+k_{\epsilon(i)}}}\right).

Using
\sum_{r=1}^{\epsilon(i)}k_{r}=k-1
, we obtain

\displaystyle d_{i}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\vec{y}_{1}\in P_{1}}\cdots\sum_{\vec{y}_{\epsilon(i)-2}\in P_{\epsilon(i)-2}}~p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|N_{i}(x)|-\sum_{r=1}^{\epsilon(i)-2}|B_{r}|,k_{\epsilon(i)-1}+k_{\epsilon(i)}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{y}_{1}\in P_{1}}\cdots\sum_{\vec{y}_{\epsilon(i)-2}\in P_{\epsilon(i)-2}}~p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|N_{i}(x)|-\sum_{r=1}^{\epsilon(i)-2}|B_{r}|,(k-1)-\sum_{r=1}^{\epsilon(i)-2}k_{r}}}\right).

Continuing this process, we obtain, for each
1\leq\ell\leq\epsilon(i)-1
,

d_{i}(x)\leq\sum_{\vec{y}_{1}\in P_{1}}\cdots\sum_{\vec{y}_{\ell}\in P_{\ell}}p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|N_{i}(x)|-\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}|B_{r}|,(k-1)-\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}k_{r}}}\right).

When
\ell=1
this becomes

\displaystyle d_{i}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\vec{y}_{1}\in P_{1}}p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|N_{i}(x)|-|B_{1}|,k-1-k_{1}}}\right)

(25)

\displaystyle=p(|B(x)|,k_{1})\cdot p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|N_{i}(x)|-|B(x)|,k-k_{1}-1}}\right).

As
|N_{i}(x)|\leq n-1<n
for all
i\in[k]
,

p(|B(x)|,k_{1})\cdot p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|N_{i}(x)|-|B(x)|,k-k_{1}-1}}\right)\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{|B(x)|}{k_{1}}}}\right)^{k_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n-|B(x)|}{k-k_{1}-1}}}\right)^{k-k_{1}-1},

so (a) holds. Alternatively, (
4
) also yields

p(|B(x)|,k_{1})\cdot p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|N_{i}(x)|-|B(x)|,k-k_{1}-1}}\right)\leq p(|N_{i}(x)|,k-1)\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{|N_{i}(x)|}{k-1}}}\right)^{k-1},

so (b) holds.

For (c), let
j\in[k]
such that
ij\in E
. We bound
d_{i}(x)
as before, but we choose the vertex
y
that plays role
j
separately. We see that

|P_{1}|\leq d_{\{i,j\}}(x)\cdot p(|B_{1}|-d_{\{i,j\}}(x),k_{1}-1).

This combined with (
25
) and (
4
) gives

\displaystyle d_{i}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\vec{y}_{1}\in P_{1}}p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|N_{i}(x)|-|B_{1}|,k-k_{1}-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq d_{\{i,j\}}(x)\cdot p(|B_{1}|-d_{\{i,j\}}(x),k_{1}-1)\cdot p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|N_{i}(x)|-|B_{1}|,k-k_{1}-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq d_{\{i,j\}}(x)\cdot p(n-d_{\{i,j\}}(x),k-2)

\displaystyle\leq d_{\{i,j\}}(x)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n-d_{\{i,j\}}(x)}{k-2}}}\right)^{k-2}.

It remains to prove the last sentence of the lemma. We proceed as before except that, for
\ell\in[\epsilon(i)]
such that
y\in B_{\ell}
, we require that
y
is chosen. This means that instead of choosing
k_{\ell}(i)
vertices from
B_{\ell}
, we only need to choose
k_{\ell}(i)-1
vertices from
B_{\ell}
as we have already chosen
y
. Following the same procedure as before, we see that

\displaystyle d_{i}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\vec{y}_{1}\in P_{1}}\cdots\sum_{\vec{y}_{\ell}\in P_{\ell}}p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|B_{\ell}|,k_{\ell}-1}}\right)\cdot p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|N_{i}(x)|-\sum_{r=1}^{\ell+1}|B_{r}|,(k-1)-\sum_{r=1}^{\ell+1}k_{r}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\vec{y}_{1}\in P_{1}}\cdots\sum_{\vec{y}_{\ell}\in P_{\ell}}p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|N_{i}(x)|-\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}|B_{r}|,(k-2)-\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}k_{r}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\vec{y}_{1}\in P_{1}}p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|N_{i}(x)|-|B_{1}|,k-2-k_{1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=p(|B(x)|,k_{1}\cdot p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|N_{i}(x)|-|B(x)|,k-2-k_{1}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq p(|N_{i}(x)|,k-2)

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{|N_{i}(x)|}{k-2}}}\right)^{k-2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

3.4
Maximum color and non-edge degrees

The following two claims are used in the proof of Lemma
3.5
to bound the size of the second largest neighborhood.

Let

\alpha:=\frac{\max_{x,i,j}d_{\{i,j\}}(x)}{n}

where the maximum is taken over all vertices
x\in V(H)
and all colors
\{i,j\}\in T
. We upper bound this value.

Claim 3.3
.

\alpha<\eta/4
.

Proof.

Let
x
achieve this maximum, so that
d_{\{i,j\}}(x)=\alpha n
for some
\{i,j\}\in T
.
By Lemma
3.1
(c) and
\alpha\leq 1
, we get

\displaystyle\max\{d_{i}(x),d_{j}(x)\}\leq\alpha n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)n}{k-2}}}\right)^{k-2}\leq n^{k-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{k-2}}}\right)^{k-2}.

For any other vertex
\ell\neq i,j
, we have
|N_{\ell}(x)|\leq(1-\alpha)n
, since
\ell
is adjacent to no edges of color
\{i,j\}
in
R
. Thus by Lemma
3.1
(b), we get

d_{\ell}(x)\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{|N_{\ell}(x)|}{k-1}}}\right)^{k-1}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)n}{k-1}}}\right)^{k-1}\leq n^{k-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{k-2}}}\right)^{k-2}.

The last inequality comes as decreasing the denominator increases the fraction, and the base is less than 1, so decreasing the exponent increases the result. Summing over all indices in
[k]
and using Lemma
3.2
, we get

\frac{1}{k^{k-1}-1}=\frac{a}{(k-1)!}\leq\frac{d(x)}{n^{k-1}}\leq k\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{k-2}}}\right)^{k-2}.

Rearranging yields

\frac{1}{k}\cdot\frac{(k-2)^{k-2}}{k^{k-1}-1}\leq(1-\alpha)^{k-2}.

We see that

\frac{(k-2)^{k-2}}{k^{k-1}-1}\geq\frac{(k-2)^{k-2}}{k^{k-1}}=\frac{1}{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{2}{k}}}\right)^{k-2}>\frac{1}{e^{2}k},

so

\frac{1}{e^{2}k^{2}}<(1-\alpha)^{k-2}\leq\exp(-(k-2)\alpha).

Assume for contradiction that
\alpha\geq\eta/4>C\log k/(4(k-1))
. Then

\frac{1}{e^{2}k^{2}}<k^{-C(1-1/(k-1))/4}<k^{-0.9C/4},

since
k\geq 11
. For
C>10
, this gives a contradiction for sufficiently large
k
.
∎

Let

\beta:=\frac{\max_{x}d_{\emptyset}(x)}{n}

where the maximum is taken over all vertices
x\in V(H)
and
d_{\emptyset}(x)
is the number of edges in
H
incident with
x
in color
\emptyset
(non-edges). Note that we may assume that
R
has at least one non-edge, since otherwise the proof from Section
2
suffices. Thus we may also assume that
H
has at least one non-edge, so
\beta>0
. We upper bound
\beta
.

Claim 3.4
.

\beta<1-\eta/2
.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that
\beta\geq 1-\eta/2
. Let
x
achieve this maximum so that
d_{\emptyset}(x)=\beta n
. This implies that
B(x)=(1-\beta)n
. For any
i\in V(R)
, Lemma
3.1
(a) gives

\displaystyle d_{i}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{B(x)}{k_{1}(i)}}}\right)^{k_{1}(i)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n-B(x)}{k-k_{1}(i)-1}}}\right)^{k-k_{1}(i)-1}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{(1-\beta)n}{k_{1}(i)}}}\right)^{k_{1}(i)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta n}{k-k_{1}(i)-1}}}\right)^{k-k_{1}(i)-1}

\displaystyle=n^{k-1}\frac{1}{k_{1}(i)^{k_{1}(i)}}(1-\beta)^{k_{1}(i)}\beta^{k-k_{1}(i)-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{k-1-k_{1}(i)}}}\right)^{k-1-k_{1}(i)}.

For this section, we take the convention
0^{0}=1
to handle the case that
k_{1}(i)=k-1
.

Let
q=k_{1}(i)/(k-1)\in(0,1]
. Then

d_{i}(x)\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{k-1}}}\right)^{k-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{(1-\beta)^{q}\beta^{1-q}}{q^{q}(1-q)^{1-q}}}}\right)^{k-1}.

(26)

We will first bound the term

\frac{(1-\beta)^{q}\beta^{1-q}}{q^{q}(1-q)^{1-q}}.

(27)

Regarding (
27
) as a function of
\beta
, we see that the derivative

\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{(1-\beta)^{q}\beta^{1-q}}{q^{q}(1-q)^{1-q}}}}\right)=\frac{(1-\beta)^{q-1}\beta^{-q}}{q^{q}(1-q)^{1-q}}((1-q)-\beta)

is negative for
\beta>1-q
since the fraction is nonnegative. Recall that
k_{1}(i)=\deg_{R}(i)\geq\eta(k-1)
by assumption, so
q>\eta
. We have also assumed for contradiction that
\beta\geq 1-\eta/2>1-\eta>1-q
. Thus decreasing
\beta
to
1-\eta/2
will only increase (
27
), i.e.

\frac{(1-\beta)^{q}\beta^{1-q}}{q^{q}(1-q)^{1-q}}\leq\frac{(\eta/2)^{q}(1-\eta/2)^{1-q}}{q^{q}(1-q)^{1-q}}.

(28)

We now have a function purely of
q
. Taking the derivative, we get

\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{(\eta/2)^{q}(1-\eta/2)^{1-q}}{q^{q}(1-q)^{1-q}}}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}(1-q)^{q-1}q^{-q}(2-\eta)^{1-q}\eta^{q}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\eta(1-q)}{q(2-\eta)}}}\right)

where all terms are positive except the logarithm, which is negative for
q>\eta/2
. Thus (
28
) is decreasing with
q
for
q>\eta/2
, so we may take the further upper bound

\frac{(1-\beta)^{q}\beta^{1-q}}{q^{q}(1-q)^{1-q}}\leq\frac{(\eta/2)^{\eta}(1-\eta/2)^{1-\eta}}{\eta^{\eta}(1-\eta)^{1-\eta}}=2^{-\eta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{\eta}{2(1-\eta)}}}\right)^{1-\eta}\leq\exp(-(\log 2-1/2)\eta).

(29)

We now have an appropriate upper bound. Substituting into (
26
) and recalling that
\eta>C\log k/k
, we see that

d_{i}(x)\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{k-1}}}\right)^{k-1}\exp(-(\log 2-1/2)(k-1)\eta)<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{k-1}}}\right)^{k-1}k^{-C(\log 2-1/2)}.

Using Lemma
3.2
, we get

\frac{1}{k^{k-1}-1}=\frac{a}{(k-1)!}\leq\frac{d(x)}{n^{k-1}}\leq k\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{k-1}}}\right)^{k-1}k^{-C(\log 2-1/2)}.

Rearranging terms and using the standard inequality (
8
) yields

\frac{1}{ek}\leq k^{-C(\log 2-1/2)}.

For
C>1/(\log 2-1/2)\approx 5.18
, this yields a contradiction for sufficiently large
k
.
∎

3.5
The second largest neighborhood

For a vertex
x\in V(H)
, let
Z(x)
be the second largest set
in
\{N_{1}(x),\ldots,N_{k}(x)\}
and define

z:=z_{k,n}=\max_{x\in V(H)}\frac{|Z(x)|}{n}.

Lemma 3.5
.

z<1-\eta/8
.

Proof.

Let
x\in V(H)
such that
z=|Z(x)|/n
. Suppose
x\in V_{i}
and
Z(x)=N_{j}(x)
for distinct

. Then we want to bound
|N_{i}(x)\cap Z(x)|
.

. If

, then

3.7
Counting the copies of
R
in
H

The way we count copies of
R
in
H
is very similar to the previous section and to
[
12
]
. While we do not have as much information in this case, without a focus on optimizing for small
k
, we allow ourselves to be less strict with the counting arguments.

Call a copy
f
of
R
in
H

transversal
if it includes exactly one vertex in
V_{i}
for all
i\in[k]
. We partition the copies of
R
in
H
as
H_{m}\cup H_{g}\cup H_{b}
where
H_{m}
comprises those copies that lie entirely inside some
V_{i}
,
H_{g}
comprises those copies that intersect every
V_{i}
whose edge coloring coincides with the natural one given by the vertex partition (meaning the map from
R
to
H
takes vertex
i
to a vertex in
V_{i}
), and
H_{b}
comprises all other copies of
R
(these include transversal copies, but some vertex in any such copy will be in an inappropriate
V_{i}
). Thus a transversal copy
f
is in
H_{b}
if and only if the unique map
\phi:[k]\to f
with
\phi(i)\in V_{i}
for all
i
is not a graph isomorphism from
R\to H[f]
. Let
h_{m}=|H_{m}|,h_{g}=|H_{g}|
and
h_{b}=|H_{b}|
so that

I(R,H)=h_{m}+h_{g}+h_{b}.

We will bound each of these three terms separately. As in Section
2.6
, let
D
be the set of all pairs
\{v,w\}
such that
v\in V_{i},w\in V_{j},
and
\chi_{H}(vw)\neq\chi_{R}(ij)
where
i\neq j
. Let
\delta:=|D|/\binom{n}{2}
. The identical reasoning as in Section
2.6
gives the first two bounds

h_{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}I(R,H[V_{j}])\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}I(R,n_{j})

(30)

and

h_{g}\leq\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}n_{\ell}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\delta{\binom{n}{2}}}{\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}n_{i}n_{j}}}}\right)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}n_{\ell}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\delta{\binom{n}{2}}}{{\binom{n}{2}}-\sum_{i}{\binom{n_{i}}{2}}}}}\right).

(31)

Our next task is to upper bound
h_{b}
. This argument must be carried out differently. For a vertex
x\in V(H)
and
j\in[k]
, recall that
N_{j}(x)\subset V(H)
is the set of
y
such that
x,y
lie in a copy of
R
with
x
playing the role of vertex
j
in
R
.
Let us enumerate the set
J
of ordered pairs
(e,f)
where
e\in D,f\in H_{b}
, and
e\subset f
.

We must show that each
f\in H_{b}
contains an edge in
D
. If
f
is transversal, then as we have noted, the natural map is not a graph isomorphism. Thus there is some incorrectly colored edge which is in
D
. If
f
is not transversal, there is some
i\in[k]
such that
|f\cap V_{i}|\geq 2
. Note that
f\notin H_{m}
, so
|f\cap V_{i}|<k
. As
R
is connected, there exist
v\in V_{i},~u\in V_{j}
for some
j\neq i
such that
vu
is an edge in
f
. Since
|f\cap V_{i}|\geq 2
, choose also
w\in f\cap V_{i}
with
w\neq v
.
If
\chi_{R}(ij)=\emptyset
then
vu\in D
. If
\chi_{R}(ij)=\{i,j\},
then as
\chi_{H}(vu)=\chi_{H}(wu)=\{i,j\}
would contradict that
f
is a copy of
R
in
H
, we must have that
uv
or
uw
in
D
.
This gives us that

h_{b}\leq|J|.

To bound
|J|
from above, we start by choosing some bad edge
vw\in D
. Let
f\subset V(H)
such that
(vw,f)\in J
. Either
v\in V_{i}
does not play the role of
i
or
w\in V_{j}

for some

or

for some
\ell\neq j
. We have

by the definition of

and the partition

. By the final statement of Lemma

,

|J|\leq\sum_{vw\in D}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{\ell\neq i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{|N_{\ell}(v)|}{k-2}}}\right)^{k-2}+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{|N_{\ell}(w)|}{k-2}}}\right)^{k-2}}}\right)\leq 2|D|(k-1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{zn}{k-2}}}\right)^{k-2}.

, we see that

3.8
The extremal case

Claim 3.7

.

Proof.

If

This is the same equation as (

3.9
The absurd case

Now, we consider the other endpoint of

Claim 3.8
.

If
\delta=1-\sum_{i}{\binom{n_{i}}{2}}/{\binom{n}{2}}
, then
\operatorname{ind}(R)\leq a
.

Proof.

If
\delta=1-\sum_{i}{\binom{n_{i}}{2}}/{\binom{n}{2}}

This is similar to (

\operatorname{ind}(R,n)\leq 0.74\operatorname{ind}(R)+0.25\,a<0.99\operatorname{ind}(R).

This contradiction completes the proof of the claim and the theorem.
∎

4
Disconnected rainbow graphs

In this section, we show that rainbow graphs with multiple connected components are not fractalizers.

Let
R=(V,E)
be a rainbow graph with
k
vertices and
\ell>1
connected components. Let
R=R_{1}\cup\cdots\cup R_{\ell}
be the connected components of size
c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell}
respectively. Assume also
c_{i}\geq 2
for all
i
(no isolated vertices). We will show that
R
is not a fractalizer.

We begin by upper bounding the number of copies
I(R,G_{n})
for
G_{n}\in\mathcal{G}_{R}(n)
an iterated balanced blowup. Then for any
i\in[\ell]
, by the same argument as for computing the inducibility of the iterated balanced blowup (see e.g.
[
14
]
),

I(R_{i},G_{n})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{k}}}\right)^{c_{i}}+k\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{k^{2}}}}\right)^{c_{i}}+k^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{k^{3}}}}\right)^{c_{i}}+\cdots=(1+o(1))\frac{n^{c_{i}}}{k^{c_{i}}-k}.

Any
S\subset V(G_{n})
with
G_{n}[S]\cong R
has a unique partition
S=S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{\ell}
where
G_{n}[S_{i}]\cong R_{i}
. Thus we can upper bound

I(R,G_{n})\leq\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}I(R_{i},G_{n})=(1+o(1))n^{k}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{k^{c_{i}}-k}.

(36)

However, consider instead the family of graphs
\mathcal{H}(n)
consisting of separate iterated balanced blowups of each part. Formally,
H\in\mathcal{H}(n)
if
|V(H)|=n
and we have a partition
V(H)=V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{\ell}
with the following properties:

1.

For all
i\in[\ell]
,
\big||V_{i}|-\frac{c_{i}}{k}n\big|\leq 1
.

2.

For all
i\in[\ell]
, the induced subgraph
G[V_{i}]\in\mathcal{G}_{R_{i}}(|V_{i}|)
.

3.

For all
v\in V_{i},w\in V_{j}
with
i\neq j
, we have
vw\notin E(H)
.

In
\mathcal{H}(n)
, there are no edges between any copy of
R_{i}
and any copy of
R_{j}
for distinct
i,j
. Since
R
is rainbow, copies of each component
R_{i}
exist only in
V_{i}
. Then for
H_{n}\in\mathcal{H}(n)
, we have

I(R,H_{n})=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}I(R_{i},H_{n}[V_{i}])=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}(1+o(1))\frac{c_{i}!}{c_{i}^{c_{i}}-c_{i}}\binom{\frac{c_{i}}{k}n}{c_{i}}=(1+o(1))n^{k}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{k^{c_{i}}-k(\frac{k}{c_{i}})^{c_{i}-1}}.

(37)

Comparing (
36
) with (
37
), we subtract larger numbers in the denominator of (
37
), so the family of graphs
\mathcal{H}(n)
induces asymptotically more copies than the family
\mathcal{G}_{R}(n)
. Thus
R
is not a fractalizer. Since
R
was generic, disconnected rainbow graphs without isolated vertices are not fractalizers.
