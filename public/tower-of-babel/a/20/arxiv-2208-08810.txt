Thomson decompositions of measures in the disk
Bartosz Malman

Abstract
We study the classical problem of identifying the structure of 𝒫 2 ​ ( μ ) \mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu) , the closure of analytic polynomials in the Lebesgue space L 2 ​ ( μ ) L^{2}(\mu) of a compactly supported Borel measure μ \mu living in the complex plane. In his influential work, Thomson showed that the space decomposes into a full L 2 L^{2} -space and other pieces which are essentially spaces of analytic functions on domains in the plane. For a family of measures μ \mu supported on the closed unit disk 𝔻 ¯ \overline{\mathbb{D}} which have a part on the open disk 𝔻 \mathbb{D} which is similar to the Lebesgue area measure, and a part on the unit circle 𝕋 \mathbb{T} which is the restriction of the Lebesgue linear measure to a general measurable subset E E of 𝕋 \mathbb{T} , we extend the ideas of Khrushchev and calculate the exact form of the Thomson decomposition of the space 𝒫 2 ​ ( μ ) \mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu) . It turns out that the space splits according to a certain decomposition of measurable subsets of 𝕋 \mathbb{T} which we introduce. We highlight applications to the theory of the Cauchy integral operator and de Branges-Rovnyak spaces.



1
Introduction and main results

1.1
Background

Let
\mu
be finite positive Borel measure in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
which is compactly supported, and consider the usual Lebesgue space
L^{2}(\mu)
of equivalence classes of Borel measurable functions which are square-integrable with respect to
\mu
. The set of
analytic polynomials

\mathcal{P}
consists of functions
p
of the form
p(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}p_{n}z^{n}
, where the coefficients
p_{n}
are complex numbers, and
z=x+iy
is the complex variable. The norm closure of
\mathcal{P}
in
L^{2}(\mu)
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
. The goal of this paper is to determine the structure of
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
for a family of relatively simple, but important in the applications, measures
\mu
living in the closed unit disk
\overline{\mathbb{D}}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq 1\}
.

We recall two basic examples of such polynomial closures that appear in analysis which turn out to very accurately illustrate what occurs in general.

(i)

Let
\mu
be the restriction to the interval
[0,1]
of the linear Lebesgue measure
dx
on the real line
\mathbb{R}
. Then a consequence of the classical Weierstrass polynomial approximation theorem is that the polynomials
p(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}p_{n}x^{n}
are dense in space of square-integrable function on
[0,1]
. Thus
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)=L^{2}(\mu)
in this case.

(ii)

Instead now, let
\mu=\textit{m}
be the linear Lebesgue measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
. The corresponding closure
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
is well-known to not coincide with the full space
L^{2}(\mu)=L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
, but instead with the Hardy space
H^{2}
, consisting of functions on
\mathbb{T}
with vanishing negative portion of their Fourier series. The space contains no non-trivial characteristic functions (the trivial ones correspond to the constant functions
0
and
1
). Each function
f\in H^{2}=\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
determines an analytic function in the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}
, and appropriately defined non-tangential boundary values of this extension coincide with
f
on
\mathbb{T}
. The magnitude of the values in
\mathbb{D}
of the extended function is controlled by the size of
f
on the carrier set of the defining measure, this carrier set being in this case
\mathbb{T}
. More precisely, for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, there exists a constant
C_{\lambda}>0
such that

|f(\lambda)|\leq C_{\lambda}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}.

(1)

It is well-known that, in fact, we have
C_{\lambda}=(1-|\lambda|^{2})^{-1/2}
.

(iii)

Let us now restrict the Lebesgue measure
m
on
\mathbb{T}
to proper subset
A
which is an arc of the circle:
d\mu=1_{A}\,d\textit{m}
, where
1_{A}
is the characteristic function of
A
. In this case,
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)=L^{2}(\mu)
, similarly to
(i)
above.

If (
1
) holds for some point
\lambda
, then the operation taking
f\in\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
to
f(\lambda)
is called a
bounded point evaluation
. The operation is called an
analytic bounded point evaluation
if an entire open neighbourhood of
\lambda
admits a uniform such bound. A long line of research on existence of bounded point evaluations culminated in the work of Thomson in
[
15
]
, who proved that the condition
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)=L^{2}(\mu)
is the only obstruction to existence of analytic bounded point evaluations for
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
. He proves that for any measure
\mu
, there exists a partition
\mu=\mu_{0}+\sum_{n\geq 1}\mu_{i}
, where each
\mu_{i}
is the restriction of
\mu
to a subset of its support, and a decomposition

\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)=L^{2}(\mu_{0})\oplus\Big(\oplus_{i\geq 1}\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu_{i})\Big)

(2)

such that for
i\geq 1
,
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu_{i})
contains no non-trivial characteristic functions, and there exists an open simply connected set
U_{i}
consisting of analytic bounded point evaluations for
\mu_{i}
. For
i\geq 1
, the pieces
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu_{i})
are thus essentially spaces of analytic functions on the domain
U_{i}
. The deep work of Aleman, Sundberg and Richter in
[
1
]
complements Thomson’s results, and provides important information about the growth, zero sets and boundary behaviour of functions in such
”analytic”

\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
-spaces on the unit disk, i.e., when the corresponding open set of analytic point evaluations is the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
.

For clarity, we mention that Thomson and Aleman, Richter and Sundberg, prove their main results also in the non-Hilbertian context, with exponents
t\neq 2
and Lebesgue spaces
L^{t}(\mu)
. In this paper, we shall focus on the case
t=2
, to avoid a few rather inessential technical details. We deal with a family of measures of the form

d\mu=(1-|z|)^{\alpha}dA+1_{E}\,d\textit{m},

(3)

where
dA
is the Lebesgue area measure of the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}
,
d\textit{m}
is the Lebesgue linear measure of the circle
\mathbb{T}
, and
1_{E}
is the characteristic (or indicator) function of a general measurable subset
E
of
\mathbb{T}
. In order for the measure to be finite, we need the restriction
\alpha>-1
. The parameter
\alpha
will not play an important role, unlike the parameter
E
which will be at the center of our attention. For this class of measures, we seek the exact form of the decomposition appearing in (
2
). An
”analytic”
piece
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu_{i})
will appear in the decomposition (
2
) of our measure, since the part
\mu_{\mathbb{D}}:=(1-|z|)^{\alpha}dA
of the measure ensures that
U=\mathbb{D}
will consist of analytic bounded point evaluations for
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
. The point in question is if a full
L^{2}
-piece appears in the decomposition, and if so, what is its exact structure.

1.2
Work of Khrushchev and our main result

To motivate and explain our main theorem, we recall the work of Khrushchev in
[
9
]
which implies some partial results of the type that we are interested in. We assume that
\mu
has the form (
3
), and we will explain the structure of
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
for different special choices of the set
E
.

(iv)

Assume that
E
in (
3
) is an arc. In this case, the space
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
will contain no
L^{2}
-summand. To see this, consider the usual Carleson curvilinear rectangle
S:=\{z\in\mathbb{D}:1-|z|>|E|,z/|z|\in E\},
and
\phi:\mathbb{D}\to S
a conformal mapping. A simple computation involving the harmonic measure of
S
will show that for any sequence of polynomials bounded in the norm of
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
, the sequence
p\circ\phi
must be bounded in the Nevanlinna class
\mathcal{N}
of the unit disk. Then the classical Khinchin–Ostrowski theorem (see
[
6
]
for precise definitions), which connects the values in
\mathbb{D}
to values in
\mathbb{T}
for converging sequences of functions in
\mathcal{N}
, ensures that any convergent sequence of polynomials in
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
cannot have a non-trivial characteristic function as its limit.

(v)

Khrushchev in
[
9
]
and Kegejan in
[
8
]
extend the idea in the previous point to sets more general than intervals. If
E
is a closed subset of
\mathbb{T}
, and
\mathbb{T}\setminus E=\cup_{n}\ell_{n}
is the decomposition of its complement into disjoint open arcs
\ell_{n}
, then the set
E
is called a
Beurling-Carleson set
if

\sum_{n}|\ell_{n}|\log(1/|\ell_{n}|)<\infty.

(4)

For such a set, Khrushchev’s approach in
[
9
]
is to carefully estimate the harmonic measure of a certain domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{D}
constructed from the set
E
and conclude, as before, that any sequence of analytic polynomials bounded in the
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
-norm cannot simultaneously converge pointwise to zero on
\mathbb{D}
and a non-zero quantity on
\mathbb{T}
(or vice versa). Thus, if
E
in (
3
) is a Beurling-Carleson set, then again
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
contains no non-trivial characteristic functions (we note that Khrushchev in
[
9
]
does not work with
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
-spaces, but with certain Banach spaces, but his results are directly applicable to our situation).

(vi)

Conversely, assume that
E
in (
3
) is a measurable subset of
\mathbb{T}
which
contains no Beurling-Carleson subsets of positive Lebesgue measure
. Under this assumption, Khrushchev constructs a sequence of analytic polynomials that simultaneously converges pointwise to
1
on
\mathbb{D}
, to
0
on
E
, and is bounded in the norm of
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
, with
\mu
given by (
3
). It follows that
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
contains
L^{2}(E)
as a summand.

Consideration of the above examples leads us to the following definition. Let
E
be a measurable subset of
\mathbb{T}
, and consider the quantity

C:=\sup_{G}\Big\{|G|:|G\setminus E|=0,G\text{ is a Beurling-Carleson set }\Big\}.

(5)

That is, we compute the supremum of the Lebesgue measures of Beurling-Carleson sets
G
which are
almost contained
in
E
, in the sense that the set difference
|G\setminus E|
has Lebesgue measure zero. Let
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
be a sequence of such Beurling-Carleson sets for which the supermum in (
5
) is attained:
\lim_{n\to\infty}|G_{n}|=C
. We define

\text{core}(E):=\cup_{n}G_{n},

(6)

and call this set for the
core
of
E
. The set
\text{core}(E)
is the biggest subset of
E
which can be synthesized from Beurling-Carleson sets. We define also

\text{res}(E):=E\setminus\text{core}(E),

(7)

which we call for the
residual
of
E
. Then
E
equals the union of its core and its residual, up to a set
N
of Lebesgue measure zero. This partition of
E
will be called for the
core-residual decomposition
of
E
. It is not hard to see that the decomposition is unique, up to sets of Lebesgue measure zero.

The main result of this paper is that the spaces
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
under consideration split up according to the core-residual decomposition.

Theorem A
.

Let
d\mu=(1-|z|)^{\alpha}dA+1_{E}\,d\textit{m}
, where
\alpha>-1
and
E
is any measurable subset of
\mathbb{T}
. Let

d\mu_{1}:=d\mu-1_{\text{res}(E)}d\textit{m}=(1-|z|)^{\alpha}dA+1_{\text{core}(E)}\,d\textit{m}.

(8)

Then

\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)=L^{2}(\text{res}(E))\oplus\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu_{1}),

(9)

where
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu_{1})
contains no non-trivial characteristic functions.

By
L^{2}(\text{res}(E))
above we mean the Lebesgue space of square-integrable functions on the set
\text{res}(E)
. It should be interpreted as the zero space if
E
is residual-free:
|\text{res}(E)|=0
. A consequence of the fact that
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu_{1})
contains no non-trivial characteristic functions is that every function
f\in\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu_{1})
has an analytic restriction to
\mathbb{D}
, and this restriction is a one-to-one mapping from
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu_{1})
to analytic functions in
\mathbb{D}
.

Our result here is a generalization of Khrushchev’s (when specialized to the Hilbertian setting). Indeed, the two examples
(v)
and
(vi)
above correspond, more or less, to the cases
|\text{core}(E)|=|E|
and
|\text{res}(E)|=|E|
, respectively. In the proofs, we do use Khrushchev’s general strategy, so our approach to his results is not new. Rather we extend his ideas to the general situation in which the residual and the core of the set
E
are simultaneously of positive measure.

We should make a comment on the nature of the set
\text{res}(E)
to better explain the difficulty that arises in our setting. Indeed, this set has the property found in example
(vi)
above: it contains no Beurling-Carleson subsets of positive Lebesgue measure. This property of a set
F
alone allows Khrushchev to construct a sequence of polynomials which simultaneously converges to 1 on
\mathbb{D}
, to 0 on
F
and is bounded in the
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
-norm if
F=E
in (
3
). But the case
F
being a proper subset of
E
is different, and the mentioned property of
F
alone (i.e., containing no Beurling-Carleson subsets of positive measure) cannot in general single-handedly allow for the existence of a non-trivial
L^{2}(F)
-summand in the space
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
. Indeed, consider a rather banal example
E=\mathbb{T}=F\cup(\mathbb{T}\setminus F)
. In this case,
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
certainly contains no
L^{2}
-summands, and
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)=H^{2}
as sets, with equality of norms. This example highlights the perhaps obvious fact that
F
being a residual set is
not
an intrinsic property of
F
, but is tightly related to the surrounding set
E
that the residual is produced with respect to. The
”splitting off”
of the piece
L^{2}(\text{res}(E))
from
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
is in fact related to the geometric way in which
\text{res}(E)
is contained in
E
, see ?THM?
below and the remark following it.

The theorem presented here is a specialization to the simplest and most important case of our most general result, which is ?THM?
below. For most of the paper, we adopt a convention similar to Khrushchev’s in
[
9
]
and work in the more general case of sets which we call
h
-Carleson
(where (
25
) replaces the definition (
4
) presented above), generalized core-residual decompositions, and measures
\mu
which are compatible with the new definition. The generalization is not deeper than replacing the function
t\log(1/t)
, which is doing the work in (
4
), by any function
h(t)
with similar properties. This change adds no technical troubles, and we would say that it rather helps to emphasize the essential parts in the proofs.

1.3
Application I: functions with sparse support and strong unilateral decay of Fourier coefficients

One of Khrushchev’s main applications of his results, which we described in the examples
(v)
and
(vi)
above, was in the study of the existence question for the following type of functions. Consider
f(\zeta)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{n}\zeta^{n}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
which vanishes on the measurable subset
\mathbb{T}\setminus E
;

f(\zeta)=0,\text{ for almost every }\zeta\in\mathbb{T}\setminus E,

(10)

and yet satisfies a unilateral rapid decay condition on its Fourier coefficients:

\lim_{n\to+\infty}|f_{n}|n^{K}=0,\quad\text{ for all }K\geq 0.

(11)

In other words, we look at of those square-integrable functions
f
which live only on
E
, and their
Cauchy transforms

C_{f}(z):=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{f(\zeta)}{1-z\overline{\zeta}}d\textit{m}(\zeta)=\sum_{n\geq 0}f_{n}z^{n},\quad z\in\mathbb{D}

(12)

are members of the algebra
\mathcal{A}^{\infty}
of analytic functions in
\mathbb{D}
which extend continuously to
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, and so do their derivatives of any order. Let us define

S_{+}(E):=\Big\{f\in L^{2}(E):\eqref{coefficientdecay}\text{ holds }\Big\}.

(13)

It is not hard to see that a bilateral version of (
11
) cannot hold for all possible sets
E
. A Fourier series with such bilateral rapid decay defines a function of the class
C^{\infty}
, and a closed set
E
which is nowhere dense in
\mathbb{T}
cannot support even a continuous function on
\mathbb{T}
. Khrushchev found, by non-constructive methods, that
S_{+}(E)
admits a non-zero function essentially if and only if
E
contains a Beurling-Carleson subset of positive Lebesgue measure. He showed also that if
E
contains no Beurling-Carleson subsets of positive Lebesgue measure, then
S_{+}(E)=\{0\}
.

In
[
12
]
, a construction of a non-zero function
f\in S_{+}(E)
has been presented, whenever
E
is a Beurling-Carleson set. From that construction it follows easily that
S_{+}(E)
is not only non-trivial, but even norm-dense in
L^{2}(E)
, whenever
E
is a Beurling-Carleson set. A natural follow-up is to try to characterize the measurable subsets
E
of
\mathbb{T}
for which the set
S_{+}(E)
is norm-dense in
L^{2}(E)
. The investigation carried out in the present paper leads us to the following conclusion.

Theorem B
.

Let
E
be a measurable subset of
\mathbb{T}
of positive Lebesgue measure. Then the norm-closure of the set
S_{+}(E)
coincides with
L^{2}(\text{core}(E))
. Hence the set
S_{+}(E)
is dense in
L^{2}(E)
if and only if
|\text{res}(E)|=0
.

In fact, the condition (
11
) can be very significantly weakened, and theorem as stated above still holds true even for this weaker condition. For instance, if
|\text{res}(E)|>0
, then even functions
f
living on
E
for which the Dirichlet integral of
C_{f}
is finite, will not be dense in
L^{2}(E)
. As usual, if
g
is analytic in
\mathbb{D}
, then its Dirichlet integral is the quantity

\int_{\mathbb{D}}|g^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\,dA(z).

(14)

We will establish this stronger versions in the proofs below.

Our proof of this stronger version, which is presented in Section
8
, has the following curious consequence. Assume that we have a function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
, and we have some information regarding where
f
lives:

f(\zeta)=0,\text{ for almost every }\zeta\in\mathbb{T}\setminus E,

(15)

Assume further that we can cut the Fourier series of the function
f
into two pieces with semi-bounded spectrum

f(\zeta)=\sum_{n<N}f_{n}\zeta^{n}+\sum_{n\geq N}f_{n}\zeta^{n}:=f_{-}(\zeta)+f_{+}(\zeta)

and measure that one of the pieces, say
f_{+}
, has
finite logarithmic energy
(see
[
6
]
). Namely,

\sum_{n\geq N}n|f_{n}|^{2}<\infty.

(16)

In that case, we can improve our information on the localization of
f
to

f(\zeta)=0,\text{ for almost every }\zeta\in\mathbb{T}\setminus\text{core}(E).

(17)

1.4
Application II: approximations in
\mathcal{H}(b)
-spaces

Theorem B has a different interpretation in the context of the de Branges-Rovnyak spaces
\mathcal{H}(b)
. The space
\mathcal{H}(b)
can be defined as the unique Hilbert space of analytic functions on
\mathbb{D}
with a reproducing kernel of the form

k_{b}(z,\lambda)=\frac{1-\overline{b(\lambda)}b(z)}{1-\overline{\lambda}z},\quad z,\lambda\in\mathbb{D}.

(18)

Here
b:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
is an analytic self-map of the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
. This class of spaces is important for its connections to the model theory of general linear operators on Hilbert space, and also for its various connections to complex function theory. One can consult the monographs
[
14
]
,
[
3
]
and
[
4
]
for more background.

It is in general hard to give explicit formulas for any inhabitants of a given space
\mathcal{H}(b)
, except for the kernel functions in (
18
). Thus, of special importance are results which explain what various functions enjoying some additional regularity properties can be found in a given space
\mathcal{H}(b)
, and to what extend these functions can be used to approximate a general
\mathcal{H}(b)
-function. With this in mind, we present a version of Theorem B which is specialized to the context of de Branges-Rovnyak spaces. The set
E
in the statement below is defined, up to a subset of Lebesgue measure zero, by the usual non-tangential boundary values of the analytic function
b
.

Theorem C
.

Let
b:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
be analytic, and consider the measurable set

E:=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{T}:|b(\zeta)|<1\}.

A necessary condition for the functions with finite Dirichlet integral in (
14
) to be norm-dense in
\mathcal{H}(b)
is that
|\text{res}(E)|=0
.

The papers
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
deal with positive approximation results. It is found, for instance, that if in addition to the condition
|\text{res}(E)|=0
, the function
1-|b(\zeta)|
is not too small on
E
, and the inner factor of
b
satisfies certain properties, then the set
\mathcal{A}^{\infty}\cap\mathcal{H}(b)
will be dense in
\mathcal{H}(b)
. In total thus, three conditions are presented in
[
12
]
which imply this density statement. By consideration of examples in
[
11
]
, these three conditions are presumed to be close to optimal. Theorem C confirms that one of these conditions, the vanishing of the residual set above, is in fact necessary.

1.5
A comment on the weighted circle case

A more general weighted variant of our problem, in which the characteristic function
1_{E}
in (
3
) is replaced by a non-negative measurable function
w
living on some carrier set
E
, is more difficult. In this setting, it is both the structure of the carrier set
E
and the local smallness of the weight
w
that cause
L^{2}
-summands to appear in the Thomson decomposition of the measure.

Significant efforts have been made to understand the conditions under which the space
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
contains
L^{2}(w\,d\textit{m})
, which of course is the biggest possible non-analytic summand. The curious reader can consult
[
10
]
, in which a smallness criterion on the weight
w
is shown to imply this
complete splitting
of the measure into an analytic piece on
\mathbb{D}
and a full
L^{2}(w\,d\textit{m})
-piece on
\mathbb{T}
. Interesting methods are employed in
[
10
]
which are different from ours, and rely on estimates of certain composition operators. Our present work has a different focus, and it is the identification of
any

L^{2}
-summands inside of
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
that is our principal interest. In the weighted context, we deduce from our results that
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
contains an
L^{2}
-summand if the carrier of the weight
w
has a non-trivial residual.

Corollary D
.

Let
\mu
have the form

d\mu=(1-|z|)^{\alpha}+w\,d\textit{m},

where
\alpha>-1
and
w
is an integrable measurable non-negative weight on
\mathbb{T}
. Consider the measurable set

E:=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{T}:w(\zeta)>0\}

and let
1_{\text{res}(E)}\cdot w
be the restriction of the weight
w
to the residual set of
E
. Then
L^{2}(1_{\text{res}(E)}\cdot w\,d\textit{m})
is contained in
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
.

The corollary is a direct consequence of ?THM?
below, from which Theorem A will also be deduced.

1.7
Conventions and notation

We identify the circle
\mathbb{T}
and the interval
[0,2\pi)
by the usual mapping. An arc
A
of the circle is sometimes called for an interval, and the notation
A=(a,b)
means that
A
is the shorter of the two circular arcs with endpoints
a,b\in\mathbb{T}
. For subsets
A
of
\mathbb{T}
, we use the notation
|A|:=m(A)
and a normalization so that
m(\mathbb{T})=1
. The symbol
L^{2}(A)
denotes the usual Lebesgue space of measurable functions living only on
A
and square-integrable with respect to the restriction of the Lebesgue linear measure to
A
. That is,
L^{2}(A)=L^{2}(1_{A}\cdot m)
. For a measure
\mu
, the notations
\mu_{\mathbb{D}}
and
\mu_{\mathbb{T}}
denote the restrictions of the measure to
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{T}
, respectively. We shall sometimes take complements of a closed set
A
with respect to an interval
I
. This complement is
I\setminus A
. Strictly speaking this might not be open (considered as a subset of
\mathbb{T}
) if
I
is closed, since two of the complementary arcs might be only half-open. In this case, being
open
means
relatively open
with respect to
I
.

2
Hausdorff-type measures

We begin our discussion by reviewing the notion of Hausdorff measures (or Hausdorff contents) and their basic properties. We will introduce also some useful variations of the classical definition.

2.1
Measure functions

Our Hausdorff-type measures will be defined using a measuring function
h
. The functions which we consider in our discussions satisfy the following basic assumptions:

(i)

h
is defined for real
t\in[0,\delta)
, where
\delta
is some positive number, and it is increasing and continuous, with
h(0)=0
,

(ii)

h(t)/t
is decreasing, with
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}h(t)/t=+\infty
.

Any function satisfying the above requirements will be called a
measure function
. We shall not make a fuss about the size of
\delta>0
in
(i)
above, so we assume that it is big enough so that all our computations are valid. Any function
h
satisfying
(i)
and
(ii)
can be modified or extended to a function on
(0,\infty)
with the same properties, in such a way that the its values near
0
remain unchanged. Such a modified function induces equivalent Hausdorff-type measures.

An immediate consequence of
(ii)
above is subadditivity:

\displaystyle h(t+s)=(t+s)\frac{h(t+s)}{t+s}=t\frac{h(t+s)}{t+s}+s\frac{h(t+s)}{t+s}

\displaystyle\leq t\frac{h(t)}{t}+s\frac{h(s)}{s}=h(s)+h(t).

We will develop our most comprehensive results for a general measure function
h
, but the main example we have in mind is

h(t)=t\log(e/t),\quad t\in(0,1],

which we extend by continuity to satisfy
h(0)=0
. The constant
e
appearing in the expression ensures that the function is increasing on
[0,1]
.

2.2
Hausdorff measures

For a measure function
h
satisfying the above properties, we recall the definition of the Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{M}_{h}
of a subset
E
of the unit circle, given by the expression

\mathcal{M}_{h}(E):=\inf_{L}\Big\{\sum_{\ell\in L}h(|\ell|):E\subset\cup_{\ell\in L},\,\ell\text{ open interval}\Big\}.

(19)

That is,
\mathcal{M}_{h}(E)
is the infimum of the numbers
\sum_{\ell\in L}h(|\ell|)
, taken over covers
L
of
E
. By continuity of
h
, in fact it matters not if we take the intervals
\ell
to be open, closed, or half-open, and sometimes it is convenient to assume that the intervals are one, or the other.

We define also a measure where the covering families
L
above are restricted to consist of dyadic intervals. Let

d_{n,j}=\{e^{it}:t\in[2\pi 2^{-n}j,2\pi 2^{-n}(j+1)]\},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\,0\leq j<2^{n}-1,

(20)

\mathcal{D}_{n}:=\{d_{n,j}\}_{j=1}^{2^{n-1}}

(21)

and

\mathcal{D}=\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{D}_{n}.

The set
\mathcal{D}_{n}
consists of the
dyadic intervals of
n
th generation
, and
\mathcal{D}
is the family of dyadic intervals covering
\mathbb{T}
. We define

\mathcal{M}_{h,d}(E):=\inf_{L}\Big\{\sum_{d\in L}h(|d|):E\subset\cup_{d\in L},\,d\in\mathcal{D}\Big\}.

(22)

The two quantities
\mathcal{M}_{h}(E)
and
\mathcal{M}_{h,d}(E)
are comparable.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any set
E\subset\mathbb{T}
we have

\mathcal{M}_{h}(E)\leq\mathcal{M}_{h,d}(E)\leq 2\mathcal{M}_{h}(E).

Proof.

The family of admissible covers
L
is more restrictive in the definition of
\mathcal{M}_{h,d}
, thus
\mathcal{M}_{h}(E)\leq\mathcal{M}_{h,d}(E).
On the other hand, if
L
is any cover of
E
consisting of any kind of intervals, then each
\ell\in L
can be covered by two dyadic intervals
d_{1},d_{2}
of equal length
|d_{1}|=|d_{2}|
which satisfy
|d_{1}|<|\ell|\leq 2|d_{1}|
. This implies, by the postulated properties of
h
, that

h(|\ell|)\leq h(|d_{1}|)+h(|d_{2}|)\leq 2h(|\ell|).

Summing over all
\ell\in L
and passing to the infimum over all covers
L
, we obtain
\mathcal{M}_{h,d}(E)\leq 2\mathcal{M}_{h}(E).

∎

The measure function
\mathcal{M}_{h,d}
has the following crucial continuity property. For a proof, we refer the reader to
[
6
, page 301]
or
[
2
, page 9]

Proposition 2.2
.

If
A_{n}
is an increasing sequence of subsets of
\mathbb{T}
for which
A=\cup_{n}A_{n}
holds, then

\mathcal{M}_{h,d}(A)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{M}_{h,d}(A_{n}).

2.3
Small Hausdorff measures

We will also find it convenient to work with the following versions of
\mathcal{M}_{h}
and
\mathcal{M}_{h,d}
which disregard sets of Lebesgue measure zero. We define the quantity
\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h}(E)
analogously to (
19
), but the family
\{\ell\}_{\ell\in L}
is only required to cover
E
up to a subset of Lebesgue measure zero. Hence

\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h}(E):=\inf_{L}\Big\{\sum_{\ell\in L}h(|\ell|):|E\setminus(\cup_{\ell\in L}\ell)|=0,\,\ell\text{ open interval}\Big\}.

(23)

and

\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h,d}(E):=\inf_{L}\Big\{\sum_{d\in L}h(|\ell|):|E\setminus(\cup_{d\in L}d)|=0,\,d\in\mathcal{D}\Big\}.

(24)

A version of ?THM?
obviously also holds for the measures
\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h}
and
\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h,d}
. Other simple relations between our Hausdorff-type measures are the following.

Proposition 2.3
.

For any measurable subset
E
of
\mathbb{T}
it holds that

\mathcal{M}_{h}(E)\geq\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h}(E)\geq h(|E|)

and

\mathcal{M}_{h,d}(E)\geq\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h,d}(E)\geq h(|E|).

Proof.

The ”small” versions
\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h}
and
\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h,d}
allow for more general covers
L
, so the first inequalities in both lines are clear. Moreover, if
|E\setminus(\cup_{\ell\in L}\ell)|=0
then certainly
|E|\leq\sum_{\ell\in L}|\ell|
, and by subadditivity of
h
we have

h(|E|)\leq\sum_{\ell\in L}h(|\ell|).

By taking infimum over admissable families
L
we obtain the right-most inequalities above.
∎

3
Cores, residuals, and their properties

In this section we introduce our principal tool, which we call the
core-residual decomposition
. We fix a measure function
h
satisfying the properties stated in Section
2
.

3.1
h
-Carleson sets

The following definition is classical.

Definition 3.1
.

A closed subset
A
of
\mathbb{T}
is called an
h
-Carleson
set if the system
L=\{\ell_{n}\}_{n}
of disjoint open arcs which constitute the complement
\mathbb{T}\setminus A
satisfies

\sum_{n}h(|\ell_{n}|)<\infty.

(25)

For completeness, we spend a moment to recall the basic properties of the
h
-Carleson sets which are needed in the following discussion. A simple first observation which one can make is that if we start with a family of not necessarily disjoint open arcs which satisfy (
25
), then the complement of their union is an
h
-Carleson set.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
L=\{\ell\}_{\ell\in L}
be a family of open intervals of
\mathbb{T}
for which we have

\sum_{\ell\in L}h(|\ell|)<\infty.

Then
A=\mathbb{T}\setminus(\cup_{\ell\in L}\ell)
is an
h
-Carleson set.

Proof.

The set
A
is obviously closed. Its complement is a union

\mathbb{T}\setminus A=\cup_{n}\alpha_{n}

of disjoint open arcs
\alpha_{n}
, and each
\alpha_{n}
is the union of those arcs
\ell\in L
which intersect it non-trivially. Thus
|\alpha_{n}|\leq\sum_{|\ell\cap\alpha_{n}|>0}|\ell|,
which by subadditivity of
h
implies

h(|\alpha_{n}|)\leq\sum_{|\ell\cap\alpha_{n}|>0}|h(|\ell|).

Whenever
\ell
intersects
\alpha_{n}
non-trivially, then
\ell
is contained in
\alpha_{n}
. Thus each
\ell\in L
appears in the sum above only for one distinct interval
\alpha_{n}
. Summing the above inequality over all arcs
\alpha_{n}
proves the claim.
∎

The class of
h
-Carleson sets is closed under taking finite unions and intersections.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
A
and
B
be
h
-Carleson sets. Then their union
A\cup B
and their intersection
A\cap B
are also
h
-Carleson sets.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{T}\setminus A=\cup_{n}\alpha_{n}
, where
\alpha_{n}
are pairwise disjoint open arcs, and
\mathbb{T}\setminus B=\cup_{m}\beta_{m}
be the corresponding decomposition of
\mathbb{T}\setminus B
. The complement
T\setminus(A\cap B)
is a disjoint union of open arcs which are themselves unions of arcs
\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{n}
. So the fact that
A\cap B
is
h
-Carleson follows from ?THM?
.

For the case of the union, we have

\mathbb{T}\setminus(A\cup B)=\cup_{n,m}\alpha_{n}\cap\beta_{m},

and in order to verify that
A\cup B
is an
h
-Carleson set, we need to show that

\sum_{n,m}h(|\alpha_{n}\cap\beta_{n}|)<\infty.

Let us forget about the indices
(n,m)
such that
\alpha_{n}\cap\beta_{m}
is void, they contribute nothing to the sum above. We partition the rest of the indices
(n,m)
into three families. The family
F_{1}
consists of those
(n,m)
such that
\alpha_{n}\subset\beta_{m}
, the family
F_{2}
consists of those
(n,m)
such that
\beta_{m}\subset\alpha_{n}
, and family
F_{3}
of those
(n,m)
such that
\alpha_{n}\cap\beta_{m}
is non-empty, but neither
\alpha_{n}
or
\beta_{m}
is contained in the other. Our task is then to show that

\sum_{(n,m)\in F_{i}}h(|\alpha_{n}\cap\beta_{m}|)<\infty

for
i=1,2,3
.

We start with
F_{1}
. If
(n,m)\in F_{1}
, then there is no other index
m^{\prime}
such that
(n,m^{\prime})\in F_{1}
, for that would imply
\beta_{m}\cap\beta_{m^{\prime}}
being non-trivial. It follows that each integer
n
appears at most once as a first coordinate of a pair in
F_{1}
. Moreover, clearly
|\alpha_{n}\cap\beta_{m}|=|\alpha_{n}|
, and so

\sum_{(n,m)\in F_{1}}h(|\alpha_{n}\cap\beta_{m}|)=\sum_{(n,m)\in F_{1}}h(|\alpha_{n}|)\leq\sum_{n}h(|\alpha_{n}|)<\infty,

by the assumption that
A
is
h
-Carleson. The family
F_{2}
is handled symmetrically.

When it comes to family
F_{3}
, note that any integer
n
appears as a first coordinate in at most two pairs in
F_{3}
. Indeed, if
(n,m)\in F_{3}
, then it follows that either the left or the right endpoint of
\alpha_{n}
is contained in
\beta_{m}
. Since
\alpha_{n}
obviously has only two endpoints, there can be at most two pairs in
F_{3}
with first coordinate
n
. Thus

\sum_{(n,m)\in F_{3}}h(|\alpha_{n}\cap\beta_{m}|)\leq\sum_{(n,m)\in F_{3}}h(|\alpha_{n}|)\leq 2\sum_{n}h(|\alpha_{n}|)<\infty.

By summing over all the three families we see that
A\cup B
is an
h
-Carleson set. ∎

3.2
Core-residual decomposition

With the definition and basic properties of
h
-Carleson sets in place, we can introduce our decomposition of measurable sets. The decomposition is very simple, but it has a crucial relation to the small Hausdorff-type measures. The relation is stated in ?THM?
.

Here and throughout the paper, if
|A\setminus B|=0
holds between two measurable subsets
A
and
B
, we say that
A
is
almost contained
in
B
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
E
be any measurable subset of
\mathbb{T}
and
h
a measure function. There exists a decomposition

E=(F\cup\tilde{E})\setminus N,

where
N
is a set of Lebesgue measure zero, and the disjoint measurable subsets
F
and
\tilde{E}
satisfy the following two properties:

(i)

if
A
is an
h
-Carleson set which is almost contained in
E
, then it is almost contained in
\tilde{E}
,

(ii)

\tilde{E}
is, up to a set of Lebesgue measure zero, a countable union of
h
-Carleson sets almost contained in
E
.

The decomposition is unique, up to a difference of sets of Lebesgue measure zero.

Proof.

We let the non-negative constant
C
be the supremum of Lebesgue measures of
h
-Carleson sets which are almost contained in
E
. If
C=0
then we are done by taking
E=F
. In the case
C>0
, let
\{E_{n}\}_{n}
be a sequence of
h
-Carleson sets almost contained in
E
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}|E_{n}|=C
. By ?THM?
, we can assume that
E_{n}\subset E_{n+1}
. Take
\tilde{E}:=\cup_{n}E_{n}
, and
F=E\setminus\tilde{E}
. Clearly we have
|\tilde{E}|=C
, and property
(ii)
holds.

Assume that
A
is an
h
-Carleson set almost contained in
E
. Then
A\cup E_{n}
is also an
h
-Carleson almost contained in
E
, by ?THM?
. We have

C=\lim_{n\to\infty}|E_{n}|\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}|A\cup E_{n}|=|A\cup\tilde{E}|.

However, by definition of
C
we have that
\lim_{n\to\infty}|A\cup E_{n}|\leq C
, and so we infer that

|A\cup\tilde{E}|=|\tilde{E}|.

Thus
A
is almost contained in
\tilde{E}
.

We establish the uniqueness of the decomposition. Let
E=F_{1}\cup\tilde{E}_{1}
be some other partition of
E
where the above stated properties hold and
\tilde{E}_{1}
is (up to Lebesgue measure zero) a union
\cup_{n}G_{n}
of some increasing sequence of
h
-Carleson sets
G_{n}
almost contained in
E
. It follows readily that the
h
-Carleson sets
E_{n}\cup G_{n}
are almost contained in the intersection
\tilde{E}\cap\tilde{E}_{1}.
But then

|\tilde{E}_{1}|=\lim_{n}|G_{n}|\leq\lim_{n}|G_{n}\cup E_{n}|\leq|\tilde{E}_{1}\cap\tilde{E}|,

so we must have
\tilde{E}_{1}\subset\tilde{E}
up to a set of Lebesgue measure zero. By reversing the argument we see that
\tilde{E}=\tilde{E}_{1}
, up to a set of Lebesgue measure zero, and so the uniqueness of the decomposition is established.
∎

Definition 3.2
.

Let
E
be any measurable subset of
\mathbb{T}
, and consider any decomposition of the form
E=(F\cup\tilde{E})\setminus N
as in ?THM?
. We will say that
F
and
\tilde{E}
form the
core-residual decomposition
of
E
with respect to
h
. We will use the notation

F=\text{res}_{h}(E)

and

\tilde{E}=\text{core}_{h}(E),

and call the two pieces for the
residual
and the
core
, respectively, of the measurable subset
E
.

Note that
\text{res}_{h}(E)
and
\text{core}_{h}(E)
are defined, up to a set of Lebesgue measure zero, for any measurable subset
E
of
\mathbb{T}
. The set
N
with
|N|=0
will play no role in our development, so we might without loss of generality assume that
N
is empty, by altering the set
E
on a set of Lebesgue measure zero.

The core-residual decomposition does not change if we pass to a portion of
E
contained in an interval, which is a fact that we will often implicitly use.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
E
be a measurable subset of
\mathbb{T}
and
I
be an interval of
\mathbb{T}
. Then

\text{res}_{h}(I\cap E)=I\cap\text{res}_{h}(E)

and

\text{core}_{h}(I\cap E)=I\cap\text{core}_{h}(E).

The above claim follows readily from the definition of the decomposition and the intersection statement in ?THM?
. We skip the straight-forward proof.

The next proposition is also quite simple, but it is our main one in this section. It showcases a connection between the core-residual decompositions and the Hausdorff-type measures introduced in Section
2
. A prototype version appears in
[
6
, page 303]

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
E
be any measurable subset of
\mathbb{T}
, and
I
an interval. Assume that
C
is a measurable subset of
I
which is almost contained in
E
. Then

\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h}\Big(I\setminus C\Big)=\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h}\Big(I\setminus(C\cap\text{core}_{h}(E))\Big).

Proof.

We have the set inclusion
I\setminus C\subseteq I\setminus(C\cap\text{core}_{h}(E))
, so the inequality

\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h}\Big(I\setminus C\Big)\leq\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h}\Big(I\setminus(C\cap\text{core}_{h}(E))\Big)

is plain. To see the converse inequality, assume that
L
is a family of open intervals for which we have

|(I\setminus C)\setminus(\cup_{\ell\in L}\ell)|=0

and

\sum_{\ell\in L}h(|\ell|)\leq\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h}(I\setminus C)+\epsilon,

(26)

for some
\epsilon>0
. Let
A
be the complement of
\cup_{\ell\in L}\ell
with respect to
I
. Then
A
is closed, and by ?THM?
and (
26
), it is an
h
-Carleson set. Moreover, clearly
A
is almost contained in
C
, which is almost contained in
E
by hypothesis. By the definition of the core-residual decomposition, it follows that
A
is almost contained in
C\cap\text{core}_{h}(E)
, and taking complements again, it follows that
I\setminus(C\cap\text{core}_{h}(E))
is almost contained in
\cup_{\ell\in L}\ell
. Thus, the family
L
is an admissible ”cover” of
I\setminus(C\cap\text{core}_{h}(E))
for the measure
\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h}
. Consequently, we have

\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h}(I\setminus(C\cap\text{core}_{h}(E))\leq\sum_{\ell\in L}h(|\ell|)\leq\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h}(I\setminus C)+\epsilon.

The proof of the proposition is complete by letting
\epsilon
tend to zero.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

To make some sense of the above result, take
C=E
in the statement above. Then the conclusion is that the
\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h}
-size of what remains from the interval
I
after removing
E
, is in fact the same as the
\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h}
-size of what remains after removing only
\text{core}_{h}(E)
. Thus, in a sense, the set
\text{res}_{h}(E)
is invisible for the Hausdorff-type measure
\mathcal{M}^{0}_{h}
.

4
A measure construction of Frostman

In the next result, we extend slightly the classical construction of the Frostman measures (refer to
[
6
, page 302]
or
[
5
, Appendix D]
for details). The properties
(ii)
and
(iii)
in the proposition below appear classically as features of the Frostman measures, and we obtain a useful additional feature
(i)
through a simple averaging process.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
U=\cup_{n}\alpha_{n}
be an open subset of
\mathbb{T}
, where
\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n}
is a disjoint family of open intervals. There exists a positive absolutely continuous Borel measure
\nu
which satisfies the following three properties:

(i)

\nu=\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}1_{\alpha_{n}}
, where
N
is some finite integer, and
c_{n}
are positive numbers,

(ii)

\nu(\Delta)\leq h(|\Delta|)
for each interval
\Delta
of
\mathbb{T}
,

(iii)

\nu(\mathbb{T})\geq\mathcal{M}_{h,d}(U)/24
.

Proof.

By ?THM?
there exist a positive integer
N
and closed intervals
\{d^{\prime}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
such that
d^{\prime}_{n}\subset\alpha_{n}
, and if
D=\cup_{n=1}^{N}d^{\prime}_{n}
, then
\mathcal{M}_{h,d}(D)\geq\mathcal{M}_{h,d}(U)/2
. The usual Frostman measure construction (see, for instance,
[
6
, page 302]
) will produce a measure
\tilde{\nu}
which is of the form
\tilde{\nu}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\tilde{\nu}_{n}
, with
\tilde{\nu}_{n}
supported on a closed interval
d_{n}
living inbetween
d^{\prime}_{n}
and
\alpha_{n}
, which satisfies
\tilde{\nu}(\Delta)\leq 4h(|\Delta|)
for any interval
\Delta\subset\mathbb{T}
, and
\tilde{\nu}(D)\geq\mathcal{M}_{h,d}(D)
. Since
D\subset U
, we also have
\tilde{\nu}(U)=\tilde{\nu}(D)\geq\mathcal{M}_{h,d}(D)\geq\mathcal{M}_{h,d}(U)/2
. Now we replace the measure
\tilde{\nu}
by its average over every
\alpha_{n}
. That is, we take
c_{n}=\tilde{\nu}_{n}(\alpha_{n})|\alpha_{n}|^{-1}
, and we define

\nu:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}1_{\alpha_{n}}.

(27)

It is obvious that
\nu(U)=\tilde{\nu}(U)
, and even that
\nu(\alpha_{n})=\tilde{\nu}(\alpha_{n})
. Moreover, we have not destroyed the smoothness of the measure either, as we will show that
\nu(\Delta)\leq 12h(|\Delta|)
for any interval
\Delta
. To this end, fix
n\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
and let
\delta
be any positive number which is smaller than the length of the interval
\alpha_{n}
. Let
a
and
b
be the endpoints of
\alpha_{n}
. By Frostman’s construction, we have that
\tilde{\nu}_{n}([t,t+\delta])\leq 4h(\delta)
for any
t\in[a,b-\delta]
. It follows then that

\int_{a}^{b-\delta}\tilde{\nu}_{n}([t,t+\delta])dt\leq 4(b-\delta-a)h(\delta)\leq 4|\alpha_{n}|h(\delta).

The left-hand side of this equation can be re-written using Fubini’s theorem:

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b-\delta}\tilde{\nu}_{n}([t,t+\delta])dt=\int_{a}^{b-\delta}\int_{\alpha_{n}}1_{t\leq s\leq t+\delta}\,d\tilde{\nu}_{n}(s)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\alpha_{n}}\int_{a}^{b-\delta}1_{t\leq s\leq t+\delta}\,dt\,d\tilde{\nu}_{n}(s)=\delta\tilde{\nu}_{n}(\alpha_{n})=\delta c_{n}|\alpha_{n}|.

Let
\Delta
be any subinterval of
\alpha_{n}
of length
|\Delta|=\delta
. Then, from the above computations and (
27
), we get

\nu(\Delta)=\delta c_{n}\leq 4h(|\Delta|).

Finally, let
\Delta
be an arbitrary interval. Then
\Delta
can be split up into at most three contiguous intervals
\Delta_{1}\cup\Delta_{2}\cup\Delta_{3}
, such that
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{3}
are partially contained in some intervals
\alpha_{n_{1}}
and
\alpha_{n_{2}}
, and
\Delta_{2}
totally covers the intervals
\alpha_{n}
which lie inbetween
\alpha_{n_{1}}
and
\alpha_{n_{2}}
. For the interval
\Delta_{2}
we have
\nu(\Delta_{2})=\tilde{\nu}(\Delta_{2})\leq 4h(|\Delta_{2}|)\leq 4h(|\Delta|)
, and we have above verified that
\nu(\Delta_{1})\leq 4h(|\Delta_{1}|)
and
\nu(\Delta_{3})\leq 4h(|\Delta_{3}|)
. Thus
\nu(\Delta)\leq 12h(|\Delta|)
. Upon division of the measure
\nu
by 12, we finally obtain a measure satisfying
\nu(\Delta)\leq h(|\Delta|)
for all intervals
\Delta\subset\mathbb{T}
, and
\nu(U)\geq\mathcal{M}_{h,d}(U)/24
.

∎

5
A real-variables construction

In this section we follow more or less the same path as Khrushchev does in
[
9

Lemma 5.1

Let

Consider the set

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{|J(x,r)\cap F|}{r}=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{|J(x,r)\cap F|}{|J(x,r)|}=0.

The set

has full measure in

with

6
A sequences which splits
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)

We will now show that for a large family of measures
\mu
, the condition that
|\text{res}_{h}(E)|>0
for the carrier set of
E
of
\mu_{\mathbb{T}}
implies that
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
contains an
L^{2}
-summand. We start by recalling some well-known results which will be used in the proof.

For a real-valued measure
\nu
living on
\mathbb{T}
we define its
Herglotz integral
by the formula

H_{\nu}(z):=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\zeta+z}{\zeta-z}d\nu(\zeta),\quad z\in\mathbb{D}.

The function
H_{\nu}
is analytic in
\mathbb{D}
, and its real part
\operatorname{Re}(H_{\nu})
is the usual Poisson integral
P_{\nu}
of
\nu
. It is well-known that if
d\nu=fd\textit{m}
is absolutely continuous, then
|H_{\nu}(z)|=\exp(|f(z)|)
for almost every
z\in\mathbb{T}
, in the sense of the usual non-tangential boundary values.

The following estimate for the growth of
P_{\nu}
in terms of smoothness of the measure
\nu
(or more precisely, smoothness of its positive part) is also well-known (see, for instance,
[
6
, page 297]
).

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
h
be a measure function satisfying the properties stated in Section
2
. There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that if
\nu(\Delta)\leq h(|\Delta|)
for any interval
\Delta
in
\mathbb{T}
, then

P_{\nu}(z)=\operatorname{Re}(H_{\nu}(z))\leq C\frac{h(1-|z|)}{1-|z|},\quad z\in\mathbb{D}.

(34)

We will also need the following approximation result which is a direct consequence of the classical Beurling-Wiener theorem. For a proof, see
[
7
]
, for instance.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
E
be a measurable subset of
\mathbb{T}
which satisfies
|E|<|\mathbb{T}|
. If a function
g\in L^{2}(E)
is non-zero almost everywhere on a set
F\subset E
, then the norm-closure of the analytic polynomial multiples of
g
in
L^{2}(E)
contains the space
L^{2}(F)
.

We now state the main result of this section, and the most general result of the paper.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
\mu=\mu_{\mathbb{D}}+\mu_{\mathbb{T}}
be a positive finite Borel measure on the closed disk

, where

is absolutely continuous, and

is such that for all bounded analytic functions

\int_{\mathbb{D}}|p(z)|^{2}d\mu_{\mathbb{D}}(z)\leq c_{1}\sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}|p(z)|^{2}\exp\Bigg(-c_{2}\frac{h(1-|z|)}{1-|z|}\Bigg)

h
and some positive constants

. Let

be a carrier set of

\mathbb{T}
such that

almost everywhere, and

\mu_{\mathbb{T}}
to the set

Before going into the proof, we make two remarks.

Remark 6.4

Firstly, note that in the case

, and the estimate holds for

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{D}}|p(z)|^{2}(1-|z|)^{\alpha}dA(z)\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}|p(z)|^{2}(1-|z|)^{c_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}}(1-|z|)^{\alpha-c_{2}}dA(z)

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\,\sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}|p(z)|^{2}(1-|z|)^{c_{2}},

where

Remark 6.5

Secondly, in fact

is the biggest

-summand contained in

in the case when

is the characteristic function of

Proof of ?THM?
.

We start by noting that if

Let

Proof of Theorem B

Theorem B stated in the introduction will follow easily from previous results found in

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
E
be a measurable subset of
\mathbb{T}
, and

be such that the Cauchy transform
C_{f}
has a finite Dirichlet integral:

\int_{\mathbb{D}}|C^{\prime}_{f}|^{2}dA<\infty.

Then

vanishes almost everywhere on
\text{res}(E)
.

Proof.

A simple computation shows that if
g
is an analytic function in
\mathbb{D}
, and
\{g_{n}\}_{n}
is the sequence of its Taylor coefficients, then we have

\int_{\mathbb{D}}|g|^{2}dA=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{|g_{n}|^{2}}{n+1}.

(43)

Thus, if
f(\zeta)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{n}\zeta^{n}
is the Fourier expansion of
f
, then the finiteness of the Dirichlet integral of
C_{f}
is equivalent to

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+1)|f_{n}|^{2}<\infty.

(44)

Let
p(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}p_{n}z^{n}
be any analytic polynomial. We can use Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to estimate

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{n=0}^{N}f_{n}\overline{p_{n}}\Big|\leq\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+1)|f_{n}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{|p_{n}|^{2}}{n+1}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\int_{\mathbb{D}}|C^{\prime}_{f}|^{2}dA\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{D}}|p|^{2}dA\Big)^{1/2}

(45)

Now consider
h\in L^{2}(\text{res}(E))
. By Parseval’s identity, and the fact that
f
lives only on the set
E
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{E}f\overline{h}\,d\textit{m}=\int_{E}f\overline{(h-p)}\,d\textit{m}+\int_{E}f\overline{p}\,d\textit{m}

\displaystyle=\int_{E}f\overline{(h-p)}+\sum_{n=0}^{N}f_{n}\overline{p_{n}}.

(46)

By ?THM?
for the case
\alpha=0
, for any
\epsilon>0
we can find an analytic polynomial
p
which satisfies

\int_{\mathbb{D}}|p|^{2}dA<\epsilon

(47)

and

\int_{E}|p_{n}-h|^{2}d\textit{m}<\epsilon.

(48)

Consequently, a combination of (
45
), (
46
), (
47
) and (
48
) shows that

\int_{E}f\overline{h}\,d\textit{m}=0,

for any
h\in L^{2}(\text{res}(E))
. Thus
f
must vanish almost everywhere on
\text{res}(E)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem B.

A set of functions in
S_{+}(E)
which live only on
\text{core}(E)
and are norm-dense in

has been constructed in
[

. Conversely, ?THM?
shows that if the Cauchy transform

of any function

has a finite Dirichlet integral, then

lives on

Let
D
be the space of analytic functions in
\mathbb{D}
with a finite Dirichlet integral in (
14
). We shall give two proofs of Theorem C stated in the introduction.

Firstly, the validity of the claim in Theorem C follows immediately from ?THM?
and the main result in
[
11
]
which characterizes the norm-density of
D\cap\mathcal{H}(b)
in
\mathcal{H}(b)
in terms of certain operator-theoretic properties of the multiplication operator
M_{z}:\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)\to\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
for the measure

d\mu=dA+(1-|b|^{2})d\textit{m},

(49)

the operator
M_{z}
being one of multiplication by the coordinate function z:

M_{z}f(z)=zf(z).

A necessary condition for density of
D\cap\mathcal{H}(b)
is, according to
[
11
]
, that
M_{z}:\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)\to\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
admits non non-trivial invariant subspace on which it acts as an isometry. Since the set

E:=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{T}:|b(\zeta)|<1\}

(50)

is the carrier set on
\mathbb{T}
of the weight
1-|b|^{2}
, ?THM?
implies that if
|\text{res}(E)|>0
, then
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mu)
admits such a subspace. So in that case
D\cap\mathcal{H}(b)
cannot be dense in
\mathcal{H}(b)
.

We can however give a proof of Theorem C which utilizes Theorem B and only the classical theory of
\mathcal{H}(b)
-spaces, passing around the abstract formulation in
[
11
]
. Since we have already seen one proof, we give only a sketch of the next argument. For references to the facts about
\mathcal{H}(b)
s-spaces used below, one can consult
[
3
]
and
[
4
]
.

Sketch of an alternative proof of Theorem C.

Let
\Delta(\zeta):=\sqrt{1-|b(\zeta)|^{2}},\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
.
It is well-known that for each
f\in L^{2}(E)
, with
E
given by (
50
), the Cauchy transform
C_{f\Delta}
of the function
f\Delta
(see (
12
)) is contained in
\mathcal{H}(b)
. The linear manifold of all such functions is usually denoted by
\mathcal{H}(\overline{b})
in the literature. If
b
is a so-called extreme point of the unit ball of
H^{\infty}
(which is the only case of interest for us), then it is known that the transform
f\mapsto C_{f\Delta}
is one-to-one on
L^{2}(E)
, and we have

\|C_{f\Delta}\|_{\mathcal{H}(b)}\leq\|C_{f\Delta}\|_{\mathcal{H}(\overline{b})}:=\|f\|_{L^{2}(E)}.

(51)

If
T_{\overline{b}}
is the usual Toeplitz operator with the co-analytic symbol
\overline{b}
, then
T_{\overline{b}}:\mathcal{H}(b)\to\mathcal{H}(\overline{b})
is a contraction, in particular it is a bounded operator. It is also known that the space
D
is invariant for any Toeplitz operator with a bounded co-analytic symbol.

Assume that
D\cap\mathcal{H}(b)
is dense in
\mathcal{H}(b)
. Our task is then to show that the set
E
given by (
50
) is residual-free. Let
\{g_{n}\}_{n}
be a sequence in
D\cap\mathcal{H}(b)
which tends to the function
C_{1_{E}\Delta}\in\mathcal{H}(b)
in the norm of
\mathcal{H}(b)
. By the invariance of
D
for the operator
T_{\overline{b}}
, it follows that the sequence of
D
-functions
T_{\overline{b}}g_{n}
tends to
T_{\overline{b}}C_{\Delta}=C_{\overline{b}\Delta}
in the norm of
\mathcal{H}(\overline{b})
. Since the functions
T_{\overline{b}}g_{n}
are in
\mathcal{H}(\overline{b})
, they are Cauchy transforms of the form
T_{\overline{b}}g_{n}=C_{f_{n}\Delta}
, for some
f_{n}\in L^{2}(E)
. By the norm-equality in (
51
) we have that
f_{n}\to\overline{b}
in
L^{2}(E)
, and so we can assume that
f_{n}\to\overline{b}
pointwise almost everywhere on
E
by passing to a subsequence. By Theorem B and the fact that
C_{f_{n}\Delta}=T_{\overline{b}}g_{n}\in D
we must have
f_{n}\equiv 0
on
\text{res}(E)
. But
\overline{b}\neq 0
almost everywhere on
E
(since
b
is analytic), so we see that
\text{res}(E)
must have Lebesgue measure zero, as claimed in the statement of Theorem C.
