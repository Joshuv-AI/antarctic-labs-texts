S ​ T ST -distributive and S ​ T ST -modular Lattices
M. R. Emamy-K. [0000-0002-5407-0392], Gustavo A. Meléndez Ríos ✉ [0000-0001-5980-3666]

Abstract
For two subsets S S and T T of a given lattice L , L, we define a relative distributive (modular) property over L , L, that underlies a large family including the usual class of distributive (modular) lattices. Our proposed class will be called S ​ T ST -distributive ( S ​ T ST -modular) lattices. In this paper, we find examples of maximal S S and T T to form S ​ T ST -distributive lattices for several non-distributive finite lattices of small order. In particular, we characterize the maximal pairs of subsets ( S , T ) (S,T) , subject to certain constraints, that induce S ​ T ST -distributivity in the lattice family 𝐌 n , n \mathbf{M}_{n,n} . Afterwards, we present an application of S ​ T ST -modular to convex sets and polytopes. This application is the original motivation for our new definitions. The aforementioned definitions are closely related to distributive elements and Stanley’s SS-lattices.

Introduction

We propose two new classes of lattices:
ST
-distributive lattices and
ST
-modular lattices. The idea behind them is to define relative distributive and modular properties that are fulfilled by certain pairs of subsets of a lattice that is not necessarily distributive nor modular. Naturally, we desire that these new classes include distributive and modular lattices respectively (i.e. are a generalization of them). In a sense, this is analogous to relative topologies in topological spaces.

This paper introduces one of the most basic questions regarding these new classes: Given a lattice, which pairs of subsets satisfy these relative distributive/modular properties? Of course, this question is non-trivial only if the given lattice is non-distributive/non-modular. The main goal coming out of this question is to identify families of lattices with particular characterizations of the subsets that induce these relative properties.

Here, we define these new classes precisely and present some initial results on
ST
-distributivity for a family of lattices called
\mathbf{M}_{n,n}
. We also show connections between
ST
-distributive lattices and two established concepts in lattice theory: distributive elements
G2011
and Stanley’s SS-lattices
S
. In addition, we present a potential applications of
ST
-modular lattices to convex sets.

Basic Properties

With the definitions established, this subsection introduces some basic properties of
ST
-distributive lattices. Two of them, the efficiency criteria and the closure of

The efficiency criteria stated in Property

Their advantage is that for some triples, we can determine distribution from simply comparing the elements rather than computing meets and joins.

Property 1

(Efficiency Criteria).
Let

or

and

3.

\displaystyle a\land\left(b\lor c\right)=\left(a\land b\right)\lor\left(a\land c\right)\;\>

(8)

\displaystyle a\lor\left(b\land c\right)=\left(a\lor b\right)\land\left(a\lor c\right)\;.

(9)

Proof

We show only that each condition implies Equation (
8

Duality gives Equation (

Condition 1

a\land\left(b\lor c\right)=a\land c=\left(a\land b\right)\lor\left(a\land c\right)\;.

a\land\left(b\lor c\right)=b\lor c\;.

On the other hand,

We make a

From now on, when we refer to a maximal

2.5.2
Preliminary Theoretical Results.

To develop our main theoretical results, we require some preliminary results: the modular comparability criteria for distributivity (MCCD) and Proposition
3
. The MCCD gives a simple condition that guarantees distribution for any triple of elements in a non-distributive but modular lattice.

Proposition 2

(Modular Comparability Criteria for Distributivity — MCCD).
Let
L
be a modular lattice with elements
a,b,c\in L
. If there is any comparability between any 2 of the 3 elements, then

\displaystyle a\land\left(b\lor c\right)

\displaystyle=\left(a\land b\right)\lor\left(a\land c\right)

(18)

\displaystyle a\lor\left(b\land c\right)

\displaystyle=\left(a\lor b\right)\land\left(a\lor c\right).

(19)

Proof

By duality, we need only to show Eq. (
18
). There are three cases. First, If
b\leq c
or
b\geq c
, we are done by Property
1
. Second, if
a\leq b
or
a\leq c
, then
a\leq b\lor c
. Thus, the Connecting Lemma
Bi
;
DP
and the absorption property of lattices give

a\land\left(b\lor c\right)=a=\left(a\land b\right)\lor\left(a\land c\right)\;.

(20)

Finally, we consider
a\geq b
or
a\geq c
. Suppose that
a\geq c
, then by modularity and the Connecting Lemma
Bi
;
DP
,

a\land\left(b\lor c\right)=\left(a\land b\right)\lor c=\left(a\land b\right)\lor\left(a\land c\right)\;.

(21)

The sub-case
a\geq b
then follows from commutativity of
\lor
. Therefore, Equation (
18
) is satisfied whenever
\left\{a,b,c\right\}
is not an antichain.
∎

The importance of the MCCD is that it implies that we need only to worry about triples that are antichains when checking for relative distributivity. Note that in general, the converse of MCCD is not true: a triple that is an antichain may distribute (e.g. a triple of singleton sets in a power set lattice). However, in
\mathbf{M}_{n,n}
, antichains never distribute.

Proposition 3

(Antichains Not Distributive in
\mathbf{M}_{n,n}
).
Suppose
n\geq 3
and
x,y,z\in\mathbf{M}_{n,n}
are distinct elements. If
\left\{x,y,z\right\}
is an antichain, then

\displaystyle x\land\left(y\lor z\right)

\displaystyle\neq\left(x\land y\right)\lor\left(x\land z\right)

(22)

\displaystyle y\land\left(x\lor z\right)

\displaystyle\neq\left(y\land x\right)\lor\left(y\land z\right)

(23)

\displaystyle z\land\left(x\lor y\right)

\displaystyle\neq\left(z\land x\right)\lor\left(z\land y\right).

(24)

Proof

There are two ways of choosing
x,y,z\in\mathbf{M}_{n,n}
such that
\left\{x,y,z\right\}
is an antichain.

Case 1
:
x,y,z
are all elements of the same level of
\mathbf{M}_{n,n}
. Without loss of generality, suppose that
\left\{x,y,z\right\}=\left\{a_{i},a_{j},a_{k}\right\}
. Then we are done by the fact that
a_{i},a_{j},a_{k}
are three elements in the
\bf{\bar{n}}
of an isomorphic copy of
\mathbf{M}_{n}
in
\mathbf{M}_{n,n}
.

Case 2
:
x,y,z
consist of three non-link elements split among the two levels of
\mathbf{M}_{n,n}
. Without loss of generality, suppose that
\left\{x,y,z\right\}=\left\{a_{i},a_{j},b_{k}\right\}
with
i,j\neq n
and
k\neq 1
. Then

\displaystyle a_{i}\land\left(a_{j}\lor b_{k}\right)=a_{i}\land 1=a_{i}\neq 0=0\lor 0=\left(a_{i}\land a_{j}\right)\lor\left(a_{i}\land b_{k}\right)

(25)

\displaystyle a_{j}\land\left(a_{i}\lor b_{k}\right)=a_{j}\land 1=a_{j}\neq 0=0\lor 0=\left(a_{j}\land a_{i}\right)\lor\left(a_{j}\land b_{k}\right)

(26)

\displaystyle b_{k}\land\left(a_{i}\lor a_{j}\right)=b_{k}\land b_{1}=a_{n}\neq 0=0\lor 0=\left(b_{k}\land a_{i}\right)\lor\left(b_{k}\land a_{j}\right).

(27)

∎

The implication of Proposition
3
is that for a pair of subsets
S
and
T
of a lattice
L
to be a maximal
ST
-pair, there can be no antichain of three distinct elements in
L
that has one element in
S
and two in
T
. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 6

(
ST
-breaking Antichain).
Given
S,T\subseteq\mathbf{M}_{n,n}^{*}
, an
ST
-breaking antichain is an ordered triple
(x,y,z)
of elements of
\mathbf{M}_{n,n}^{*}
that form an antichain
\left\{x,y,z\right\}\subseteq\mathbf{M}_{n,n}
with
x\in S
and
y,z\in T
.

Observe that by commutativity of lattice operations,
(x,y,z)
is an
ST
-breaking antichain if and only if
(x,z,y)
is.

2.6
Connection to Distributive Elements

Now that we are done with our main results regarding
ST
-distributive lattices, we relate said lattices with distributive elements of lattices. These have a history of changing in meaning. In Garret Birkhoff’s

2.7

Before moving on to

-distributive lattices are more general. First, we recall the definition of an SS-lattice.

Definition 10

(SS-lattice
S
).
Consider a lattice
L

\Delta

\left(L,\Delta\right)

\left[\Delta,K\right]
denotes the sublattice of

From this definition, we can observe how we can obtain examples of
ST
-distributive lattices from it: if
\left(L,\Delta\right)
is an SS-lattice, then
L
is
\Delta K
-distributive and

-distributive for all its chains

-distributive lattices are a bigger class because the conditions specified on Definition

are weaker than those required for an SS-lattice. First, we let

and

S
distributes into
T
rather than demanding that the generated sublattice
\left[S,T\right]
be distributive. This concludes our treatment of
ST
-distributive lattices.

3.2
Application to Convex Sets

Having defined what is an
ST
-modular lattice, we will take a different route from that taken with
ST
-distributive lattices. Rather than searching for
ST
-modular lattices, we will propose an application of this relative modular property to convex sets. This application is the original motivation for this concept. For it we will need two ingredients: the lattice of convex sets and a property of convex hulls in Proposition
9
. In the following,
\mathrm{conv}\left(A,B\right)
denotes the convex hull of
A\cup B
for two sets
A
and
B
.

Definition 12

(Lattice of Convex Sets
B
).
The lattice of convex sets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the lattice
\langle L,\lor,\land\rangle
where
L
is the set of all convex sets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with lattice operations

A\land B=A\cap B\qquad A\lor B=\mathrm{conv}\left(A,B\right)\;.

(53)

We mention two facts of this lattice: that it is ordered by inclusion (
\subseteq
) and that it can be shown to be non-modular. The latter will make our proposed relative modularity non-trivial. Now, we present our second ingredient: a statement regarding the intersection of an affine set with the convex hull of two convex sets.

Proposition 9

(Interesection of Affine Set and Convex Hull
E
).
Suppose we have
A,B,C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
A
affine,
B
and
C
convex, and
A\supseteq C
. Then:

A\cap\mathrm{conv}\left(B,C\right)=\mathrm{conv}\left(A\cap B,C\right)\;.

(54)

Proof

The inclusion
\supseteq
is trivial because
A\cap\mathrm{conv}\left(B,C\right)
is convex and contains both
A\cap B
and
C=A\cap C
. For the other inclusion (
\subseteq
), let
x\in A\cap\mathrm{conv}\left(B,C\right)
. Then
x\in\mathrm{conv}\left(B,C\right)
and since
\mathrm{conv}\left(B,C\right)
is the union of all line segments connecting a point in
B
with a point in
C
, there exist
b\in B
,
c\in C
and
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
such that

x=\left(1-\alpha\right)b+\alpha c\;.

(55)

If
\alpha=0
, then
x=b\in B
and given that
x\in A
,
x\in A\cap B
and we are done. Similarly, if
\alpha=1
, then
x=c\in C
and we are done. Otherwise, solve Eq. (
55
) for
b
to get

b=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}x-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}c\;.

(56)

It follows that
b\in A
. This due to the fact that
x,c\in A
(recall
C\subseteq A
), the sum of their coefficients is 1, and
A
is affine. This means that the convex combination presentation in Eq. (
55
) is a convex combination of a point in
A\cap B
and a point in
C
. Therefore,
x\in\mathrm{conv}\left(A\cap B,C\right)
.∎

We are ready to present our application of
ST
-modularity to convex sets. The main idea is that we can use our relative modularity to describe certain behaviors of convex sets. For instance, we can translate Proposition
9
into the language of the lattice of convex sets to get
ST
-meet modularity in said lattice.

Corollary 2

(
ST
-meet Modularity in the Lattice of Convex Sets).
Let
L
be the lattice of convex sets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If
S
is the set of all affine sets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
T=L
, then Proposition
9
simply says that
L
is
ST
-meet modular. This is because for
A\in S
,
B,C\in T
with
A\geq C
, it implies

A\land\left(B\lor C\right)=\left(A\land B\right)\lor C\;.

(57)

The relevance of this connection is that this
ST
-meet modularity can be used to prove classical theorems on convex polytopes. We will provide two examples. Before doing so, we clarify some definitions and notations that will be used in the proofs. We define a
convex polytope
as the convex hull of a finite set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a
face
of a convex polytope as the intersection of the polytope with a supporting hyperplane (following
E
). A
vertex
is a 0-dimensional face. Given a hyperplane
H
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
H^{+}
denotes one of the closed halfspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that it defines and
int\left(H^{+}\right)
its corresponding open halfspace. Finally, for a finite set of points
\left\{x_{1},...,x_{n}\right\}
, we write
\mathrm{conv}\left(x_{1},...,x_{n}\right)
as short hand for
\mathrm{conv}\left(\left\{x_{1},...,x_{n}\right\}\right)
.

Theorem 3.1

(Faces of Convex Polytope
E
).

1.

A convex polytope has a finite number of faces.

2.

Each face is a convex polytope.

Proof

We prove Statement 2 first and then derive Statement 1 from it.

Statement 2
: Let
P=\mathrm{conv}\left(x_{1},...,x_{n}\right)
be a convex polytope and let
F
be a face of
P
. Then there exists a supporting hyperplane
H
of
P
such that
F=P\cap H
with
x_{1},...,x_{j}\in F
and
x_{j+1},...,x_{n}\in int\left(H^{+}\right)
for some
j\in\left\{1,...,n\right\}
. Then

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=H\cap P

(60)

\displaystyle=H\cap\mathrm{conv}\left(x_{1},...,x_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=H\cap\mathrm{conv}\left(\mathrm{conv}\left(x_{j+1},...,x_{n}\right),\mathrm{conv}\left(x_{1},...,x_{j}\right)\right)\;.

Since
H
is affine,
\mathrm{conv}\left(x_{1},...,x_{j}\right)
and
\mathrm{conv}\left(x_{j+1},...x_{n}\right)
are both convex, and
H\supseteq\mathrm{conv}\left(x_{1},...,x_{j}\right)
, we can rewrite the right-side expression using Proposition
9
:

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\mathrm{conv}\left(H\cap\mathrm{conv}\left(x_{j+1},...,x_{n}\right),\mathrm{conv}\left(x_{1},...,x_{j}\right)\right)

(63)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{conv}\left(\emptyset,\mathrm{conv}\left(x_{1},...,x_{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{conv}\left(x_{1},...,x_{j}\right)\;.

Therefore,
F
is a convex polytope.

Statement 1
: Now, observe that we just showed that each face of
P
is the convex hull of a subset of
\left\{x_{1},...,x_{n}\right\}
, which is a finite set. Thus, the number of faces of
P
must be finite.
∎

Theorem 3.2

(Vertices of Convex Polytope
E
).
A convex polytope is the convex hull of its vertices.

Proof

Let
P=\mathrm{conv}\left(x_{1},...,x_{n}\right)
be a convex polytope. Suppose that this convex hull presentation of
P
is minimal in the sense that

P\neq\mathrm{conv}\left(x_{1},...,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},...,x_{n}\right)=S_{i}

(64)

for all
i
in
1,...,n
. We show that this implies that each
x_{i}
is a vertex and hence,
P
is the convex hull of its vertices.

First, note that by supposition,
x_{i}\not\in S_{i}
. Second, we have that
S_{i}
is compact because it is the convex hull of a compact set:
\left\{x_{1},...,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},...,x_{n}\right\}
Putting these together we get that
S_{i}
is a convex and compact set not containing
x_{i}
. This implies the existence of a hyperplane
H
passing through
x_{i}
such that
S_{i}\subseteq int\left(H^{+}\right)
. We claim that
H
is an appropriate hyperplane to establish that
x_{i}
is a vertex.

We must show that (a)
\left\{x_{i}\right\}=H\cap P
and (b)
P\subseteq H^{+}
. For the former, observe that

H\cap P=H\cap\mathrm{conv}\left(S_{i},\left\{x_{i}\right\}\right)\;.

(65)

Since
H
is affine,
\left\{x_{i}\right\}
and
S_{i}
are both convex, and
H\supseteq\left\{x_{i}\right\}
, we can rewrite the right-hand side with Proposition
9
to obtain:

\displaystyle H\cap P

\displaystyle=\mathrm{conv}\left(H\cap S_{i},\left\{x_{i}\right\}\right)

(68)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{conv}\left(\emptyset,\left\{x_{i}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{x_{i}\right\}\;.

It remains to show that
P
is contained in
H^{+}
. Note that
\left\{x_{i}\right\}
and
S_{i}
are both subsets of
H^{+}
and that
P=\mathrm{conv}\left(\left\{x_{i}\right\},S_{i}\right)
. Then
P\subseteq H^{+}
because halfspaces are convex. Therefore,
x_{i}
is a vertex of
P
.
∎

The main takeaway from all this is that
ST
-modularity may have applications to the study of convex sets and polytopes.

4
Conclusion

This paper has introduced two new classes of lattices:
ST
-distributive lattices and
ST
-modular lattices. They extend distributive and modular lattices with corresponding relative properties satisfied by certain pairs of subsets of a lattice.

Our main result is the full characterization of the maximal
ST
-pairs (of disjoint and non-empty subsets that exclude the identity elements) of the lattice family
\mathbf{M}_{n,n}
for
n\geq 3
. To this end, we developed an algorithm that finds all such pairs and applied it
\mathbf{M}_{3,3}
and
\mathbf{M}_{4,4}
. We found 27 and 42 maximal
ST
-pairs respectively. We classified them into 5 types of pairs based on how they are constructed. We then showed that these constructions generalize to all
\mathbf{M}_{n,n}
for
n\geq 3
(one of them even for all lattices) and that these are the only constructions possible in this family. Finally, we counted how many pairs of each type
\mathbf{M}_{n,n}
has as a function of
n
.

We also established some connection between our proposed classes of lattices and existing concepts in mathematics. We mentioned how to get
ST
-distributivity from distributive elements (as defined in
Bi
) and renamed two subclasses of these meet and join distributive elements, respectively, to fit our new definitions. We also discussed how our
ST
-distributive lattices include and extend Stanley’s
SS
-lattices. Furthermore, we illustrated how
ST
-modularity can be used to describe properties of convex sets which can then be used to prove results on convex polytopes.

Needless to say, there is plenty to be done in this new and exciting area of research. Naturally, we can continue our search for
ST
-distributive lattices with other families of lattices and also search for
ST
-modular ones. Of particular interest are cubical lattices (face lattices of hypercubes) and lattices of cut-complexes of hypercubes. We can also consider restricting our search to pairs of sublattices. All this can eventually lead to the development of a general theory of
ST
-distributive and
ST
-modular lattices with potential connections to other areas of mathematics (e.g. convex sets). The possibilities are endless!
