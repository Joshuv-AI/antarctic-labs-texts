The Maclaurin inequality through the probabilistic lens
Lorenz Frühwirth, Michael Juhos, Joscha Prochno

Abstract
In this paper we take a probabilistic look at Maclaurin’s inequality, which is a refinement of the classical AM–GM mean inequality. In a natural randomized setting, we obtain limit theorems and show that a reverse inequality holds with high probability. The form of Maclaurin’s inequality naturally relates it to U-statistics. Then as a consequence of the classical Maclaurin inequalities, we know that S k 1 ( n ) ≥ S k 2 ( n ) S_{k_{1}}^{(n)}\geq S_{k_{2}}^{(n)} for k 1 < k 2 k_{1}<k_{2} . In the present article we consider the ratio ℛ k 1 , k 2 , p ( n ) := S k 2 , p ( n ) S k 1 , p ( n ) , \mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}:=\frac{S_{k_{2},p}^{(n)}}{S_{k_{1},p}^{(n)}}, evaluated at a random vector ( X 1 , … , X n ) (X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n}) sampled either from the normalized surface measure on the ℓ p n \ell_{p}^{n} -sphere or from a distribution generalizing both the uniform distribution on the ℓ p n \ell_{p}^{n} -ball and the cone measure on the ℓ p n \ell_{p}^{n} -sphere; by the Maclaurin inequality, we always have ℛ k 1 , k 2 , p ( n ) ≤ 1 \mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}\leq 1 . Introduction and main results

1.1
Introduction

There are a number of classical inequalities frequently used throughout mathematics. A natural question is then to characterize the equality cases or to determine to what degree a reverse inequality may hold. The latter shall be the main focus here, and we begin by motivating and illustrating the approach in the case of the classical inequality of arithmetic and geometric means (AM
–
GM inequality), which has attracted quite some attention in the past decade. Let us recall that the AM
–
GM inequality states that for any finite number of non-negative real values, the geometric mean is less than or equal to the arithmetic mean. More precisely, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n}\geq 0
it holds that

\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\Bigr)^{1/n}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}

and equality holds if and only if
x_{1}=\dotsb=x_{n}
. Setting
y_{i}:=\sqrt{x_{i}}
for
i=1,\dotsc,n
, we obtain

\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\Bigr)^{1/n}\leq\Bigl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

For a point
y
in the Euclidean unit sphere
\mathbb{S}_{2}^{n-1}:=\{x=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\mathord{:}\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert x_{i}\rvert^{2}=1\}
, this leads to the estimate

\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lvert y_{i}\rvert\Bigr)^{1/n}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}.

It is natural to ask whether this inequality can be reversed for a “typical” point in
\mathbb{S}_{2}^{n-1}
, and Gluskin and Milman
[
12
, Proposition 1]
showed that, for any
t>0
,

\Surf^{(n)}_{2}\Bigl(\Bigl\{x\in\mathbb{S}_{2}^{n-1}\;\mathord{:}\;\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lvert x_{i}\rvert\Bigr)^{1/n}\geq t\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigr\}\Bigr)\geq 1-(1.6\sqrt{t})^{n},

where
\Surf^{(n)}_{2}
denotes the unique rotationally invariant probability measure on
\mathbb{S}_{2}^{n-1}
.
For large dimensions
n\in\mathbb{N}
this means that with high probability, we can reverse the AM
–
GM inequality up to a constant. The problem was revisited later by Aldaz
[
1
, Theorem 2.8]
who showed that for all
\varepsilon>0
and
k>0
there exists an
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
n\geq N
,

\Surf^{(n)}_{2}\Biggl(\Biggl\{x\in\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1}\;\mathord{:}\;\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}(\gamma+\log 2)}}{\sqrt{n}}<\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lvert x_{i}\rvert\Bigr)^{1/n}<\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}(\gamma+\log 2)}}{\sqrt{n}}\Biggr\}\Biggr)\geq 1-\frac{1}{n^{k}},

where
\gamma=0.5772\ldots
is Euler’s constant. The previous works then motivated Kabluchko, Prochno, and Vysotsky
[
15
]
to study the asymptotic behavior of the
p
-generalized AM
–
GM inequality, which states that for any
p\in(0,\infty)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lvert x_{i}\rvert\Bigr)^{1/n}\leq\Bigl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert x_{i}\rvert^{p}\Bigr)^{1/p}.

The authors analyzed the quantity

\mathscr{R}^{(n)}:=\frac{\bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lvert x_{i}\rvert\bigr)^{1/n}}{\lVert x\rVert_{p}},

where
\lVert x\rVert_{p}:=\bigl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert x_{i}\rvert^{p}\bigr)^{1/p}
for
x=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Similar to the case of the classical AM
–
GM inequality above it is natural to consider points
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
that are in some sense uniformly distributed on the
\ell_{p}^{n}
unit sphere
\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1}
or the
\ell_{p}^{n}
unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{p}^{n}
respectively, where

\mathbb{B}_{p}^{n}:=\bigl\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\mathord{:}\;\lVert x\rVert_{p}\leq 1\bigr\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1}:=\bigl\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\mathord{:}\;\lVert x\rVert_{p}=1\bigr\}.

In
[
15
, Theorem 1.1]
it is shown that, for a constant
m_{p}\in(0,\infty)
depending only on
p
,

\sqrt{n}\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{-m_{p}}\mathscr{R}^{(n)}-1\bigr),\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

converges to a centered normal distribution with known variance, and in
[
15
, Theorem 1.3]
a large deviations principle (LDP) for the sequence
(\mathscr{R}^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is proved (see Section
2.2
for the definition of an LDP).

The work
[
15
]
of Kabluchko, Prochno, and Vysotsky was then complemented by Thäle
[
24
]
, who obtained a Berry
–
Esseen type bound and a moderate deviations principle for a wider class of distributions on the
\ell_{p}^{n}
balls (first defined in
[
3
]
). In a subsequent paper, Kaufmann and Thäle
[
18
, Theorem 1.1]
were able to identify the sharp asymptotics of
(\mathscr{R}^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Quite recently, Frühwirth and Prochno
[
10
]
approached another fundamental inequality in analysis, namely Hölder’s inequality, by probabilistic means. More precisely, they studied the asymptotic behavior of the classical Hölder ratio
\mathscr{R}_{p,q}^{(n)}
defined by

\mathscr{R}_{p,q}^{(n)}:=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert x_{i}y_{i}\rvert}{\lVert x\rVert_{p}\lVert y\rVert_{q}},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

where
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
p,q\in(1,\infty)
with
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
. The points
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
were drawn at random according to the uniform distribution on the
\ell_{p}^{n}
and
\ell_{q}^{n}
unit ball, respectively. In
[
10
]
, a central limit theorem and large and moderate deviations principles are proved for the sequence
(\mathscr{R}_{p,q}^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

The aim of this article is to study a refined version of the AM
–
GM inequality, known as Maclaurin’s inequality. It is another classical inequality in analysis and states that if for a point
x=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
k\in\{1,2,\dotsc,n\}
, and
p\in(0,\infty)
one defines the
p
-
generalized
k
th
symmetric mean

S_{k,p}^{(n)}:=S_{k,p}^{(n)}(x):=\left(\binom{n}{k}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dotsb<i_{k}\leq n}\lvert x_{i_{1}}\rvert^{p}\dotsm\lvert x_{i_{k}}\rvert^{p}\right)^{1/(kp)},

then one has that

S_{k,p}^{(n)}\geq S_{k+1,p}^{(n)},

(1)

where
1\leq k\leq n-1
. By iterating Equation (
1
) over all
1\leq k\leq n-1
, one recovers the
p
-generalized AM
–
GM inequality, i.e., we have

\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lvert x_{i}\rvert\right)^{1/n}=S_{n,p}^{(n)}\leq S_{n-1,p}^{(n)}\leq\dotsb\leq S_{1,p}^{(n)}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert x_{i}\rvert^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

This motivates the study of the Maclaurin ratio

\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}:=\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}(X_{p}^{(n)}):=\frac{S_{k_{2},p}^{(n)}}{S_{k_{1},p}^{(n)}},

(2)

with
1\leq k_{1}<k_{2}\leq n
and
X_{p}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
a random point; note
\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}\in[0,1]
because of (
1
). As already explained before, it is natural to consider
X_{p}^{(n)}\sim\U(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{n})
or
X_{p}^{(n)}\sim\U(\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1})
. The uniform distribution on
\mathbb{B}_{p}^{n}
is given by the normalized Lebesgue measure, whereas there are two meaningful uniform distributions on
\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1}
, namely the surface probability measure and the cone probability measure. In the present article, we are going to treat
\U(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{n})
and the cone measure on
\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1}
in a unified way by introducing a family of distributions denoted by
P_{p,R^{(n)}}^{(n)}
: this is defined to be the distribution of the random vector
R^{(n)}Y
, where
R^{(n)}
and
Y
are independent,
R^{(n)}
almost surely takes values in
(0,\infty)
, and
Y
is distributed according to the cone measure on
\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1}
; clearly
R^{(n)}
is the radial part of the distribution, while
Y
is its directional part.

1.2
Main results
—
limit theorems for the Maclaurin ratio

Let us now present our main results. For the sake of brevity, we first introduce the following general assumption on our random quantity.

Assumption A
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
and
X_{p}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a random vector so that either
X_{p}^{(n)}\sim P_{p,R^{(n)}}^{(n)}
or
X_{p}^{(n)}\sim\Surf_{p}^{(n)}
, where
\Surf_{p}^{(n)}
is the surface probability measure on
\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1}
.

In the statements of the main results, the following quantities will appear. For a real
-
valued random variable
X
distributed according to the
p
-
Gaussian distribution (the definition of which is given in Section
2.3
), we set

\begin{split}m_{p}&:=\Erw[\log\lvert X\rvert]\in\Bigl[-1,-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr],\\
s_{p}^{2}&:=\Var\Bigl[\log\lvert X\rvert-\frac{1}{p}\lvert X\rvert^{p}\Bigr]\in(0,\infty),\\
\rho_{p}^{2}&:=\frac{1}{4}\Var\bigl[\bigl(\lvert X\rvert^{p}-1-p\bigr)^{2}\bigr]\in(0,\infty).\end{split}

(3)

1.2.1
Central limit theorems and Berry
–
Esseen bounds for
\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}

Theorem A
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed, let
p\in[1,\infty)
, and for each
n\geq k
, we let
X_{p}^{(n)}
be a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that Assumption
A
holds. Then, the Maclaurin ratio
\mathscr{R}_{k,n,p}^{(n)}
satisfies

\sqrt{n}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-m_{p}}\mathscr{R}_{k,n,p}^{(n)}-1\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{d}}Z,

where
Z\sim\Normal(0,s_{p}^{2})
.

We are also able to provide a quantitative version of Theorem
A
, i.e., a Berry
–
Esseen type result. For real-valued random variables
X
and
Y
on a common probability space, their Kolmogorov distance is defined by

d_{\text{Kol}}(X,Y):=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl\lvert\prb[X\leq t]-\prb[Y\leq t]\bigr\rvert.

(4)

Theorem B
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed, let
p\in[1,\infty)
, and for each
n\geq k
, let
X_{p}^{(n)}
be a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that Assumption
A
holds. Then there exists a constant
c_{k,p}\in(0,\infty)
such that, for all
n\geq k
,

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(\sqrt{n}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-m_{p}}\mathscr{R}_{k,n,p}^{(n)}-1\right),Z\right)\leq\frac{c_{k,p}\sqrt{\log(n)}}{\sqrt{n}},

where
Z\sim\Normal(0,s_{p}^{2})
.

The proof of Theorem
B
uses Taylor expansion in order to write
\mathscr{R}_{k,n,p}^{(n)}
as a sum of a certain U
-
statistic, for which a Berry
–
Esseen result is available already, and the remainder term which is treated as a perturbation whose behaviour is controlled with the help of Theorem
D
given below.

Remark 1.1
.

Theorem
B
gives a similar asymptotic bound of the distance to a normal distribution as the classical theorem of Berry
–
Esseen. The “
\sqrt{\log(n)}
” on the right
-
hand side seems to be an artifact of our method of proof and we conjecture that this factor is not necessary.

An almost immediate consequence of Theorem
A
is the following answer to the question stated in the introduction in the context of the AM
–
GM
-
inequality, in how much the Maclaurin inequality can be reversed.

Corollary 1.2
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
A
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\prb\bigl[S_{n,p}^{(n)}\geq\mathrm{e}^{m_{p}}S_{k,p}^{(n)}\bigr]=\frac{1}{2},

that is, with probability approaching
\frac{1}{2}
, we can reverse Maclaurin’s inequality up to the factor
\mathrm{e}^{m_{p}}
.

Whereas Theorem
A
compares the
k
th
symmetric mean against the geometric mean (for fixed
k
), the next theorem compares the
k_{1}
th
against the
k_{2}
th
symmetric mean (for fixed
k_{1}\neq k_{2}
) and provides a CLT.

Theorem C
.

Let
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed with
k_{1}<k_{2}
, let
p\in[1,\infty)
, and for each
n\geq k_{2}
let
X_{p}^{(n)}\sim\Keg_{p}^{(n)}
be a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, the Maclaurin ratio
\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}
satisfies

\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{np}{k_{2}-k_{1}}\left(\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}-1\right)+\frac{p}{2}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{d}}Z,

where
Z\sim\Normal(0,\rho_{p}^{2})
.

Theorem
C
basically is a consequence of a variation of the delta
-
method and the CLT for U
-
statistics. (Theorem
A
too can be proved directly in this manner instead of via Theorem
B
.) Concerning reversal of Maclaurin’s inequality we can make the following statement.

Corollary 1.3
.

Under the premises of Theorem
C
, for any
c\in(0,1)
, we know

\lim_{n\to\infty}\prb\bigl[S_{k_{2},p}^{(n)}\geq cS_{k_{1},p}^{(n)}\bigr]=1.

This means, with probability approaching
1
, Maclaurin’s inequality can be reversed up to a constant factor
c
arbitrarily close to
1
.

1.2.2
Moderate deviations principles for
\mathscr{R}_{k,n,p}^{(n)}

Theorem D

(Moderate deviations)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed,
p\in[1,\infty)
, and for each
n\geq k
let
X_{p}^{(n)}
be a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that Assumption
A
holds. Further, let
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of positive real numbers such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{b_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}=0
, and either
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}b_{n}=\infty
if
X_{p}^{(n)}\sim\Keg_{p}^{(n)}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, or
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{b_{n}}{\sqrt{\log(n)}}=\infty
if
X_{p}^{(n)}\sim\Surf_{p}^{(n)}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{b_{n}}\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{-m_{p}}\mathscr{R}_{k,n,p}^{(n)}-1\bigr)\Bigr)_{n\geq k}
satisfies a moderate deviations principle at speed
(b_{n}^{2})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with good rate function
\I\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
given by
\I(t):=\frac{t^{2}}{2s_{p}^{2}}
.

The main point in the proof of Theorem
D
is to establish exponential equivalence of the expressions
\binom{n}{k}^{-1}\sum_{i_{1}<\dotsb<i_{k}}X_{i_{1}}\dotsm X_{i_{k}}
and
\bigl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}\bigr)_{k}
, thereby reducing a problem with dependent summands to one with independent summands, where moderate deviations are well
-
understood.

Remark 1.4
.

We emphasize the fact that Theorems
A
,
B
, and
D
are “oblivious” to the parameter
k
, i.e., the limiting objects to not depend on (fixed!)
k
and take the same forms as for the known case
k=1
, i.e., the AM
–
GM setting; even more, shift and scaling of the quantity under consideration,
\mathrm{e}^{-m_{p}}\mathscr{R}_{k,n,p}^{(n)}-1
, make no use of
k
. Compare this to Theorem
C
, where the limiting distribution indeed does not depend on
k_{1}
or
k_{2}
, but at least the difference
k_{2}-k_{1}
enters the proper scaling.

Remark 1.5
.

The question of large deviations principles for
\mathscr{R}_{k,n,p}^{(n)}
remains open still, as does that of further limit theorems for
\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}
. The reason is a lack of suitable tools; there do exist general results concerning LDPs for U
-
statistics, but their strict conditions are not met by our setting. For more details we refer the reader to Remark
2.14
.

, and the Euclidean norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2}
. The induced Borel
\sigma
-

\Borel(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and measurability by default refers thereto.

; the expectation, variance, and covariance w.r.t.

\Erw
,

, and

2.2
Basics from probability and large deviations theory

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-
valued random variables and let
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of real numbers tending to infinity. A function
\I\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
is called a good rate function (GRF) iff
\I
is not constant
\infty
and the sublevel set
\I^{-1}([0,a])
is compact for any
a\in[0,\infty)
. We say that
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies a large deviations principle (LDP) in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
at speed
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with GRF
\I
if and only if

-\inf_{x\in A^{\circ}}\I(x)\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{s_{n}}\log\prb[\xi_{n}\in A]\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{s_{n}}\log\prb[\xi_{n}\in A]\leq-\inf_{x\in\overline{A}}\I(x)

(5)

for all
A\in\Borel(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
A^{\circ}
and
\overline{A}
denote the interior and the closure of
A
, respectively.

There are different ways to show that a certain sequence of random variables satisfies an LDP, one of the most commonly used is the so called contraction principle (see, e.g., Theorem 4.2.1 in
[
5
]
).

Proposition 2.1

(Contraction principle)
.

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a continuous function, and let
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-
valued random variables that satisfies an LDP at speed
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with GRF
\I\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
. Then, the sequence
(f(\xi_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
at speed
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with the GRF
\mathbb{J}\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
given by

\mathbb{J}(y):=\inf_{x\in f^{-1}(\{y\})}\I(x).

We shall also use Cramér’s large deviations theorem for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-
valued random variables (see, e.g.,
[

Proposition 2.2

(Cramér’s theorem)
.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of i.i.d.
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-
valued random variables, write
\Lambda(t):=\log\Erw[\mathrm{e}^{\langle t,X_{1}\rangle}]
for

, and suppose that
0\in\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda}^{\circ}
, where

\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda}:=\bigl\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\;\mathord{:}\;\Lambda(t)<\infty\bigr\}.

Then, the sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
defined by

\xi_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

satisfies an LDP in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
at speed
n
with GRF
\Lambda^{*}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
, where

\Lambda^{*}(x):=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl[\langle x,t\rangle-\Lambda(t)\bigr]

is the

–

of

.

Two sequences of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-
valued random variables
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\eta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are said to be exponentially equivalent at speed
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
if and only if we have, for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{s_{n}}\log\prb\bigl[\bigl\lVert\xi_{n}-\eta_{n}\bigr\rVert_{2}>\varepsilon\bigr]=-\infty.

(6)

The following result can be found, e.g., in
[
5
, Theorem 4.2.13]
; it states that if a sequence of random vectors satisfies an LDP and is exponentially equivalent to another sequence of random vectors, then both satisfy the same LDP.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\eta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be sequences of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-
valued random variables. Assume that
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP at speed
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with GRF
\I\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
and that
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\eta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are exponentially equivalent at speed
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Then,
(\eta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP at speed
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with the same GRF
\I\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
.

We say that a sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-
valued random variables satisfies a moderate deviations principle (MDP) if and only if
\bigl(\frac{\xi_{n}}{\sqrt{n}b_{n}}\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP at speed
(b_{n}^{2})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with some GRF
\I\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
for some positive sequence
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}b_{n}=\infty
and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{b_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}=0
.
The scaling
\sqrt{n}b_{n}
typically grows faster than that in a central limit theorem but slower than that in a law of large numbers (and hence in an “ordinary” LDP). This property of an MDP is nicely illustrated by the following Cramér
-
type theorem (see, e.g.,
[
5
, Theorem 3.7.1]
).

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of i.i.d.
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-
valued random variables such that
0\in\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda}^{\circ}
, as in Proposition
2.2
, and suppose
\Erw[X_{1}]=0
and that
\Sigma
, the covariance matrix of
X_{1}
, is invertible. Let
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of real numbers with

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}b_{n}=\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{b_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}=0.

Then, the sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\xi_{n}:=\frac{1}{b_{n}\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
satisfies an LDP in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
at speed
(b_{n}^{2})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with GRF
\I\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
given by

\I(x):=\frac{1}{2}\langle x,\Sigma^{-1}x\rangle,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The following statement, going back to Nagaev
[
20
]
and generalized by Gantert, Ramanan, and Rembart
[
11
]
, gives an asymptotic bound for sums of i.i.d. random variables with
stretched exponential
tail probabilities; its formulation uses the following notion: a function
\ell\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is called slowly varying (at infinity) if we have, for all
a\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ell(ax)}{\ell(x)}=1.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of i.i.d. positive random variables and assume that there exist slowly varying functions
c,b\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
and constants
r\in(0,1)
and
t^{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that we have, for all
t\in[t^{*},\infty)
,

\prb\left[X_{1}\geq t\right]\leq c(t)\exp\left(-b(t)t^{r}\right).

Then, for
m:=\Erw[X_{1}]
and any
x\in(m,\infty)
, we get

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{b(n)n^{r}}\log\left(\prb\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\geq x\right]\right)\leq-(x-m)^{r}.

The analogous statement holds true for the lower tail
\prb[X_{1}\leq t]
and
\prb\bigl[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\leq x\bigr]
with
x<m
.

The following result is taken from
[
2
, Lemma 4.1]
; it allows to estimate the Kolmogorov distance of a perturbed random variable to a Gaussian random variable.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
Y_{1}
,
Y_{2}
be random variables, let
Z\sim\Normal(0,\sigma^{2})
with
\sigma^{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and let
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then,

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl\lvert\prb[Y_{1}+Y_{2}\geq t]-\prb[Z\geq t]\bigr\rvert\leq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl\lvert\prb[Y_{1}\geq t]-\prb[Z\geq t]\bigr\rvert+\prb[\lvert Y_{2}\rvert>\varepsilon]+\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}.

2.3
Measures on finite
-
dimensional
\ell_{p}
spaces

For
p\in[1,\infty)
(the case
p\in(0,1)\cup\{\infty\}
is not relevant for the present article) and
n\in\mathbb{N}
the space
\ell_{p}^{n}
is
\mathbb{R}^{n}
endowed with the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{p}
defined by

\lVert(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n})\rVert_{p}:=\Biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert x_{i}\rvert^{p}\Biggr)^{1/p};

the corresponding unit ball and unit sphere are denoted by

\mathbb{B}_{p}^{n}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\mathord{:}\;\lVert x\rVert_{p}\leq 1\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\mathord{:}\;\lVert x\rVert_{p}=1\}.

The standard measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the
n
-
dimensional Lebesgue measure
\Leb^{(n)}
, defined on
\Borel(\mathbb{R}^{n})
; it induces the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{B}_{p}^{n}
.

We equip
\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1}
with the trace
\sigma
-
algebra induced by
\Borel(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, denoted
\Borel(\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1})
. There are two meaningful “uniform” distributions on
\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1}
, namely the cone probability measure
\Keg^{(n)}_{p}
and the surface probability measure
\Surf^{(n)}_{p}
. In the following, we will briefly discuss their theoretical foundation as well as the relation between these two distributions. The cone probability measure
\Keg^{(n)}_{p}
of
A\in\Borel(\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1})
is defined as

\Keg^{(n)}_{p}(A):=\frac{\Leb^{(n)}(\{ra\;\mathord{:}\;r\in[0,1],a\in A\})}{\Leb^{(n)}(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{n})}.

From a result of Schechtman and Zinn
[
23
]
, and Rachev and Rüschendorf
[
22
]
, we know that
X_{p}^{(n)}\sim\Keg^{(n)}_{p}
if and only if

X_{p}^{(n)}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{d}}}{{=}}}\frac{Y^{(n)}}{\lVert Y^{(n)}\rVert_{p}},

(7)

where
Y^{(n)}=(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
is a vector of i.i.d. random variables whose common distribution is
\gamma_{p}
, the
p
-
generalized Gaussian distribution, or
p
-
Gaussian for short; it is defined by its Lebesgue-density,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{p}}{\mathrm{d}x}(x)=\frac{1}{2p^{1/p}\Gamma(1+\frac{1}{p})}\mathrm{e}^{-\lvert x\rvert^{p}/p},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(8)

The surface probability measure
\Surf^{(n)}_{p}
is defined to be the
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff probability measure or, equivalently, the
(n-1)
-dimensional normalized Riemannian volume measure on
\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1}
. We have the following relation between
\Keg^{(n)}_{p}
and
\Surf^{(n)}_{p}
(see
[
21
, Lemma 2]
and
[
15
, Lemma 2.2]
).

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in[1,\infty)
. Then, for all
x=(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n})\in\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1}
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\!\Surf^{(n)}_{p}}{\mathrm{d}\!\Keg^{(n)}_{p}}(x)=C_{n,p}\Biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert x_{i}\rvert^{2p-2}\Biggr)^{1/2},

where

C_{n,p}:=\Biggl(\int_{\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1}}\Biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert\xi_{i}\rvert^{2p-2}\Biggr)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}\!\Keg^{(n)}_{p}(\xi)\Biggr)^{-1}.

It then follows, for all
x\in\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1}
,

n^{-\lvert 1/p-1/2\rvert}\leq\frac{\mathrm{d}\!\Surf_{p}^{(n)}}{\mathrm{d}\!\Keg_{p}^{(n)}}(x)\leq n^{\lvert 1/p-1/2\rvert}.

If
p\in\{1,2\}
, there easily follows
\Surf^{(n)}_{p}=\Keg^{(n)}_{p}
. We remark that also in case
p=\infty
we know
\Surf^{(n)}_{\infty}=\Keg^{(n)}_{\infty}
(see
[
21
]
). In contrast, for all
p\in(1,\infty)
with
p\neq 2
, we have
\Surf^{(n)}_{p}\neq\Keg^{(n)}_{p}
. Nevertheless, for large
n\in\mathbb{N}
, one can prove that
\Surf^{(n)}_{p}
and
\Keg^{(n)}_{p}
are close in the total variation distance (see
[
21
, Theorem 2]
).

Proposition 2.8
.

For all
p\in[1,\infty)
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\lVert\Keg^{(n)}_{p}-\Surf^{(n)}_{p}\rVert_{\text{TV}}:=\sup_{A\in\Borel(\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1})}\lvert\Keg^{(n)}_{p}(A)-\Surf^{(n)}_{p}(A)\rvert\leq\frac{c_{p}}{\sqrt{n}},

where
c_{p}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
depends only on
p
.

Barthe, Guédon, Mendelson, and Naor
[
3
]
have introduced the following unifying generalization of both
\U(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{n})
and
\Keg_{p}^{(n)}
: take a sequence
(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of i.i.d.
p
-
Gaussian random variables and a random variable
W
taking values in
[0,\infty)
, independent of
(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
; let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the random variable

X:=\frac{(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}}{\bigl(\lVert(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\rVert_{p}^{p}+W\bigr)^{1/p}}

(9)

takes values in
\mathbb{B}_{p}^{n}
. If
W=0
almost surely, then
X\sim\Keg_{p}^{(n)}
as is immediately seen by comparison with Equation (
7
). If
W
follows an exponential distribution with rate
\frac{1}{p}
, then
X\sim\U(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{n})
. A complete characterization of all distributions on
\mathbb{B}_{p}^{n}
, which have the probabilistic representation (
9
), is hitherto unknown. Since it had been devised, authors have been using this generalized
p
-
radial distribution in several works, for instance Kabluchko, Prochno, and Thäle
[
14
]
or Kaufmann, Sambale, and Thäle
[
16
]
.

The ansatz (
9
) has inspired further generalization by Kaufmann and Thäle
[
17
]
who have introduced a homogeneous factor to the joint density of
(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
in order to also take into account eigenvalues or singular values of random matrices in Schatten classes. A different route has been taken by Heiny, Johnston and Prochno
[
13
]
, who have considered random vectors of the form
X=R\Theta
, where
R
and
\Theta
are independent,
R\in[0,\infty)
almost surely, and
\Theta\sim\Surf_{2}^{(n)}
; note that the distribution of
X
is invariant under orthogonal transformations.

In the present article we take up the latter thread for further generalization: Let
(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be i.i.d.
p
-
Gaussian as before, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
R^{(n)}
be an a.s. nonnegative real
-
valued random variable, independent of
(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. Then we denote by
P_{p,R^{(n)}}^{(n)}
the distribution of the random vector

X_{p}^{(n)}:=R^{(n)}\,\frac{(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}}{\lVert(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\rVert_{p}},

(10)

or, since
\Theta_{p}^{(n)}:=\frac{(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}}{\lVert(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\rVert_{p}}\sim\Keg_{p}^{(n)}
by Schechtman and Zinn, equivalently

X_{p}^{(n)}=R^{(n)}\Theta_{p}^{(n)}.

Obviously
\lVert X_{p}^{(n)}\rVert_{p}=R^{(n)}
a.s.; if
R^{(n)}=1
a.s., then
X_{p}^{(n)}\sim\Keg_{p}^{(n)}
, and if
R^{(n)}\leq 1
a.s., then
P_{p,R^{(n)}}^{(n)}
is a probability distribution on
\mathbb{B}_{p}^{n}
. The following proposition collects some basic facts about
P_{p,R^{(n)}}^{(n)}
; the proof is given in Section
3.1
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in[1,\infty)
.

1.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\mu=P_{p,R^{(n)}}^{(n)}
for some a.s. nonnegative random variable
R^{(n)}
if and only if there exists a probability measure
\rho
on
[0,\infty)
such that for any nonnegative measurable map
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
the following polar integration formula is valid,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{[0,\infty)}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{p}^{n-1}}f(r\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\!\Keg_{p}^{(n)}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(r),

and then
\rho
is the distribution of
R^{(n)}
.

In particular,
\mu=\U(\mathbb{B}_{p}^{n})
if and only if
(R^{(n)})^{n}\sim\U([0,1])
.

2.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\Leb^{(n)}
, then
\mu=P_{p,R^{(n)}}^{(n)}
for some a.s. nonnegative random variable
R^{(n)}
if and only if
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\!\Leb^{(n)}}=g\circ\lVert\cdot\rVert_{p}

\Leb^{(n)}
-
a.e. for some nonegative measurable function
g\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
.

3.

Let
X\sim P_{p,R^{(n)}}^{(n)}
for some a.s. nonnegative random variable
R^{(n)}
, then
\lVert X\rVert_{p}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{d}}}{{=}}}R^{(n)}
; and if
\prb[X=0]=0
, then
\lVert X\rVert_{p}
and
\frac{X}{\lVert X\rVert_{p}}
are independent, and
\frac{X}{\lVert X\rVert_{p}}\sim\Keg_{p}^{(n)}
.

4.

Let
Y=(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with
Y_{1}\sim\gamma_{p}
and let
W
be an a.s. nonnegative random variable; define
R^{(n)}:=\frac{\lVert Y\rVert_{p}}{(\lVert Y\rVert_{p}^{p}+W)^{1/p}}
, then
R^{(n)}\geq 0
a.s., and
R^{(n)}
and
\frac{Y}{\lVert Y\rVert_{p}}
are independent. In particular
R^{(n)}\frac{Y}{\lVert Y\rVert_{p}}=\frac{Y}{(\lVert Y\rVert_{p}^{p}+W)^{1/p}}\sim P_{p,R^{(n)}}^{(n)}
.

Remark 2.10
.

1.

Proposition
2.9
, 2., tells us that, basically,
P_{p,R^{(n)}}^{(n)}
is constant on
p
-
spheres and thus indeed captures the idea of a measure which is symmetric w.r.t.
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{p}
.

2.

Proposition
2.9
, 4., shows that
P_{p,R^{(n)}}^{(n)}
is a true generalization of the
p
-
radial measures introduced by Barthe et al. Note that the latter mainly arise from projecting the cone measure onto the first few coordinates.

Finally we remark that if
X_{p}^{(n)}\sim P_{p,R^{(n)}}^{(n)}
and
\prb[R^{(n)}=0]>0
, then also
\prb[S_{k,p}^{(n)}(X_{p}^{(n)})=0]>0
, and therefore the ratio
\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}(X_{p}^{(n)})
is not defined on a set of positive measure. For that reason we restrict ourselves to the case
\prb[R^{(n)}=0]=0
in the sequel.

2.4
Basics from the theory of U
-
statistics

Since the Maclaurin ratio
\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}
can be expressed via suitable U
-
statistics, we need a few results from the theory of U
-
statistics. For
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\leq n
define the set
J_{k}^{(n)}:=\bigl\{(i_{1},\dotsc,i_{k})\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}^{k}\;\mathord{:}\;i_{1}<\dotsb<i_{k}\bigr\}
.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\leq n
, and let
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of real
-
valued random variables.

1.

A kernel of order
k
is a symmetric measurable function
\varphi^{(k)}\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
.

2.

The U
-
statistic of order
k
with the kernel
\varphi^{(k)}
of order
k
is the random variable

U_{n}(\varphi^{(k)}):=U_{n}(\varphi^{(k)})(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n}):=\binom{n}{k}^{-1}\sum_{i\in J_{k}^{(n)}}\varphi^{(k)}(X_{i_{1}},\dotsc,X_{i_{k}}).

The following proposition collects a few well-known results (see, e.g.,
[
19
,
9
]
).

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of i.i.d. real-valued random variables, let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\varphi^{(k)}\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a kernel of order
k
and, for
n\geq k
, let
U_{n}(\varphi^{(k)})
be the associated U
-
statistic.

1.

(Hoeffding’s decomposition.) If
\Erw\left[\lvert\varphi^{(k)}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{k})\rvert\right]<\infty
, then
\Erw[U_{n}(\varphi^{(k)})]=\Erw[\varphi^{(k)}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{k})]=:\theta
, and we can write

U_{n}(\varphi^{(k)})=\theta+\sum_{h=1}^{k}\binom{k}{h}U_{n}(\varphi_{h}),

(11)

where the kernels
\varphi_{h}\colon\mathbb{R}^{h}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are defined recursively by

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}(x_{1})

\displaystyle:=\Erw\bigl[\varphi^{(k)}(x_{1},X_{2},\dotsc,X_{k})\bigr]-\theta,\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\varphi_{h}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{h})

\displaystyle:=\Erw\bigl[\varphi^{(k)}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{h},X_{h+1},\dotsc,X_{k})\bigr]-\theta-\sum_{c=1}^{h-1}\sum_{i\in J_{c}^{(h)}}\varphi_{c}(x_{i_{1}},\dotsc,x_{i_{c}})\quad\text{for $h\in\{2,\dotsc,k\}$.}

They satisfy
\Erw[U_{n}(\varphi_{h})]=0
for all
h\in\{1,\dotsc,k\}
, and in the case
\Erw[\varphi^{(k)}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{k})^{2}]<\infty
the U
-
statistics
U_{n}(\varphi_{1}),\dotsc,U_{n}(\varphi_{h})
are pairwise uncorrelated, and their variances are given by

\Var\left[U_{n}(\varphi_{h})\right]=\binom{n}{h}^{-1}\Erw\left[\varphi_{h}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{h})^{2}\right].

2.

(Central limit theorem.) Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and, for
k_{1},\dotsc,k_{m}\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\varphi^{(1)},\dotsc,\varphi^{(m)}
be kernels of orders
k_{1},\dotsc,k_{m}
resp. such that
\Erw[\varphi^{(i)}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{k_{i}})^{2}]<\infty
for all
i\in\{1,\dotsc,m\}
. Let
\theta_{i}:=\Erw[U_{n}(\varphi^{(i)})]
, then the following multivariate CLT holds,

\left(\sqrt{n}\left(U_{n}(\varphi^{(i)})-\theta_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{m}\right)_{n\geq\max\{k_{1},\dotsc,k_{m}\}}\xrightarrow{\text{d}}\Normal(0,\Sigma).

The covariance matrix
\Sigma=(\sigma_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is given by
\sigma_{i,j}=k_{i}k_{j}\Erw[\varphi_{1}^{(i)}(X_{1})\varphi_{1}^{(j)}(X_{1})]
, where
\varphi_{1}^{(l)}
is defined relative to
\varphi^{(l)}
as in
1
, for
l\in\{i,j\}
.

3.

(Berry
–
Esseen.) Assume
\Erw\bigl[\lvert\varphi^{(k)}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{k})\rvert^{3}\bigr]<\infty
. Then, there exists a constant
C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

d_{\text{Kol}}\bigl(\sqrt{n}\bigl(U_{n}(\varphi^{(k)})-\theta\bigr),Z\bigr)\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}},

where
\theta=\Erw[\varphi^{(k)}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{k})]
and
Z\sim\Normal(0,\sigma^{2})
with variance
\sigma^{2}:=k^{2}\Var[\varphi_{1}(X_{1})]
, and
\varphi_{1}
is defined as in
1
.

The following propositions establish an MDP and an LDP, respectively, for U
-
statistics with product kernels under more general assumptions than in the classical theory. The proofs are given in Section
3.2
.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be an sequence of i.i.d. positive random variables such that
\Erw[X_{1}]>0
and there exists
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
\Erw\bigl[\exp(t_{0}X_{1})\bigr]<\infty
. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and define the kernel
\varphi^{(k)}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by
(y_{1},\dotsc,y_{k})\mapsto\prod_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}
. Let
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of positive real numbers such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}b_{n}=\infty
and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{b_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}=0
. Then the sequences
\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{b_{n}}\bigl(U_{n}(\varphi^{(k)})-m^{k}\bigr)\Bigr)_{n\geq k}
and
\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{b_{n}}km^{k-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-m)\Bigr)_{n\geq k}
are exponentially equivalent at speed
b_{n}^{2}
.

In particular,
\bigl(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{b_{n}}\bigl(U_{n}(\varphi^{(k)})-m^{k}\bigr)\bigr)_{n\geq k}
satisfies an MDP in
\mathbb{R}
at speed
b_{n}^{2}
with GRF
\I\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
,
x\mapsto\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}
, where
m=\Erw[X_{1}]
and
\sigma^{2}=m^{2(k-1)}k^{2}\Var[X_{1}]
.

Remark 2.14
.

1.

Although stated quite generally, we shall need Proposition
2.13
mainly for the case
X_{i}=\lvert Y_{i}\rvert^{p}
with
Y_{i}\sim\gamma_{p}
i.i.d. To the authors’ best knowledge, the current general results concerning MDPs and LDPs for U
-
statistics (e.g. Eichelsbacher and Schmock
[
8
]
for MDP, Eichelsbacher and Löwe
[
7
]
for LDP) require the much stricter Cramér’s condition
\Erw[\exp(t_{0}\lvert\varphi(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{k})\rvert)]<\infty
for a kernel
\varphi
of order
k
, with some
t_{0}>0
. But as one may verify, for
Y_{i}
as given above there always holds true

We need to show that, for any

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

with

Call

\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\Biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\Biggr)^{k-2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\\
\lvert\alpha\rvert=k-2\end{subarray}}\binom{k-2}{\alpha}x_{i}^{2}x^{\alpha}.

(12)

The main task is now to count how often a given monomial
x^{\alpha}
, for
\alpha\in C_{n,k}
, appears on the right
-
hand side of (
12

\alpha\in C_{n,k}
, then
m:=\lvert\{i\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}\;\mathord{:}\;\alpha_{i}\geq 2\}\rvert\geq 1

where

, that is,

. Employing said lemma we get

The claimed exponential equivalence means that we need to show, for any
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{b_{n}^{2}}\log\left(\prb\left[\left\lvert\frac{\sqrt{n}}{b_{n}}\,\frac{k!}{n^{k}}\sum_{i\in J_{k}^{(n)}}X_{i_{1}}\dotsm X_{i_{k}}-\frac{\sqrt{n}}{b_{n}}\Biggl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\Biggr)^{k}\right\rvert>\varepsilon\right]\right)=-\infty.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. By the previous calculation it suffices to show

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{b_{n}^{2}}\log\left(\prb\left[\frac{1}{b_{n}\sqrt{n}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)^{k-2}>\varepsilon\right]\right)=-\infty.

We take a closer look at the probability in the logarithm, where we get, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
M\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\displaystyle\prb\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}b_{n}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)^{k-2}>\varepsilon\right]

\displaystyle=\prb\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}b_{n}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)^{k-2}>\varepsilon,\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)^{k-2}>M\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\prb\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}b_{n}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)^{k-2}>\varepsilon,\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)^{k-2}\leq M\right]

Now it suffices to prove
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{b_{n}^{2}}\log(\alpha_{n})=-\infty
and
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{b_{n}^{2}}\log(\beta_{n})=-\infty

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{b_{n}^{2}}\log(\alpha_{n}+\beta_{n})=\sup\Biggl\{\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{b_{n}^{2}}\log(\alpha_{n}),\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{b_{n}^{2}}\log(\beta_{n})\Biggr\}=-\infty.

(13)

Since there exists a

with
\Erw[\exp(t_{0}X_{1})]<\infty
by assumption, we know from Theorem
2.2
that
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP in

at speed

Now we turn to
(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

. Thus we have
\prb[X_{1}^{2}\geq t]\leq c\exp(-bt^{1/2})
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. First we assume
\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{b_{n}^{2}}>0
. Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{n}b_{n}=\infty

n_{0}\geq k
such that we have
\frac{\sqrt{n}b_{n}\varepsilon}{M}\geq A

where again we have used Proposition

Step 2.
We now show that

\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

\displaystyle\prb\Biggl[\frac{\sqrt{n}}{b_{n}}\Biggl\lvert U_{n}(\varphi^{(k)})-\frac{k!}{n^{k}}\sum_{i\in J_{k}^{(n)}}X_{i_{1}}\dotsm X_{i_{k}}\Biggr\rvert>\varepsilon\Biggr]

Lemma 3.3
.

Let

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{s_{n}}\log(\Keg_{p}^{(n)}(D_{n})),

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{s_{n}}\log(\Surf_{p}^{(n)}(D_{n}))

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{s_{n}}\log(\Keg_{p}^{(n)}(D_{n})),

where we use the convention
\log(0):=-\infty

Proof.

Let

2.7
there exists a constant

, which leads to

\frac{-c\log(n)}{s_{n}}+\frac{1}{s_{n}}\log(\Keg_{p}^{(n)}(D_{n}))\leq\frac{1}{s_{n}}\log(\Surf_{p}^{(n)}(D_{n}))\leq\frac{c\log(n)}{s_{n}}+\frac{1}{s_{n}}\log(\Keg_{p}^{(n)}(D_{n})).

Since by assumption
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log(n)}{s_{n}}=0
, we can take the limits superior and inferior as
n\rightarrow\infty
to get the claimed result.
∎

with GRF

We also provide an alternate, direct proof here, where we assume slightly different, partly more general premises. It is going to use the following auxiliary statement, which in general appears to be well
-
known and widely used; nevertheless for the convenience of the reader we provide a version with its proof that suits precisely our needs.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
d,l\in\mathbb{N}
, let
R\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function for which there exist
M,\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\lvert R(x)\rvert\leq M\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{l}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\lVert x\rVert_{2}\leq\delta
, and let
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-
valued random variables such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1/2-1/(2l)}Z_{n}=0
in probability. Then
\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{(l-1)/2}R(Z_{n})=0
in probability.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, then for all
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\prb\bigl[n^{(l-1)/2}\lvert R(Z_{n})\rvert\geq\varepsilon\bigr]

\displaystyle=\prb\bigl[n^{(l-1)/2}\lvert R(Z_{n})\rvert\geq\varepsilon\wedge\lVert Z_{n}\rVert_{2}\leq\delta\bigr]+\prb\bigl[n^{(l-1)/2}\lvert R(Z_{n})\rvert\geq\varepsilon\wedge\lVert Z_{n}\rVert_{2}>\delta\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\prb\bigl[n^{(l-1)/2}M\lVert Z_{n}\rVert_{2}^{l}\geq\varepsilon\bigr]+\prb\bigl[\lVert Z_{n}\rVert_{2}\geq\delta\bigr]

\displaystyle=\prb\Biggl[\bigl\lVert n^{1/2-1/(2l)}Z_{n}\bigr\rVert_{2}\geq\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon}{M}\biggr)^{1/l}\Biggr]+\prb\bigl[\bigl\lVert n^{1/2-1/(2l)}Z_{n}\bigr\rVert_{2}\geq n^{1/2-1/(2l)}\delta\bigr].

Because of
\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1/2-1/(2l)}Z_{n}=0
in probability, we get immediately

\lim_{n\to\infty}\prb\Biggl[\bigl\lVert n^{1/2-1/(2l)}Z_{n}\bigr\rVert_{2}\geq\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon}{M}\biggr)^{1/l}\Biggr]=0.

Also we have
n^{1/2-1/(2l)}\geq 1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and this implies

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\prb\bigl[\bigl\lVert n^{1/2-1/(2l)}Z_{n}\bigr\rVert_{2}\geq n^{1/2-1/(2l)}\delta\bigr]\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\prb\bigl[\bigl\lVert n^{1/2-1/(2l)}Z_{n}\bigr\rVert_{2}\geq\delta\bigr]=0,

and the claim follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem

\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n})

\Erw[Y_{1}]=0

\begin{split}U_{n}(\varphi_{1}^{(k_{1})})&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i},\\
U_{n}(\varphi_{2}^{(k_{1})})&=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\Biggl(\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\biggr)^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}^{2}\Biggr),\\
U_{n}(\varphi_{3}^{(k_{1})})&=\frac{1}{n(n-1)(n-2)}\Biggl(\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\biggr)^{3}-3\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}^{3}\Biggr).\end{split}

(16)

The multivariate CLT yields

\left(\begin{pmatrix}Z_{n}^{(1)}\\
Z_{n}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}:=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}Y_{i}\\
Y_{i}^{2}-p\end{pmatrix}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\xrightarrow{\text{d}}\begin{pmatrix}N_{1}\\
N_{2}\end{pmatrix}\sim\Normal\left(\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}p&c_{1,2}\\
c_{1,2}&v_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right),

(17)

where we additionally have introduced
c_{1,2}:=\cov[Y_{1},Y_{1}^{2}]
and
v_{2}:=\Var[Y_{1}^{2}]
. Plugging this into the representations (
16
) of the
U_{n}(\varphi_{h}^{(k_{1})})
leads to

\begin{split}V_{n}^{(1)}&:=\sqrt{n}\,U_{n}(\varphi_{1}^{(k_{1})})=Z_{n}^{(1)},\\
V_{n}^{(2)}&:=nU_{n}(\varphi_{2}^{(k_{1})})=\frac{n}{n-1}\Bigl((Z_{n}^{(1)})^{2}-p-\frac{Z_{n}^{(2)}}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigr),\\
V_{n}^{(3)}&:=n^{3/2}\,U_{n}(\varphi_{3}^{(k_{1})})=\frac{n^{2}}{(n-1)(n-2)}\biggl((Z_{n}^{(1)})^{3}-3Z_{n}^{(1)}\Bigl(p+\frac{Z_{n}^{(2)}}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigr)+\frac{2}{n^{3/2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}^{3}\biggr),\\
V_{n}^{(4,i)}&:=n^{2}\sum_{h=4}^{k_{i}}\binom{k_{i}}{h}U_{n}(\varphi_{h}^{(k_{i})}).\end{split}

(18)

With Slutsky’s theorem, we get
(n^{-1/2}\,Z_{n}^{(2)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\to 0
in probability, according to the strong law of large numbers
\bigl(n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}^{3}\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\to\Erw[Y_{1}^{3}]
a.s. and hence
\bigl(n^{-3/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}^{3}\bigr)_{n\geq 1}\to 0
a.s., and therefrom and from (
17
) we conclude

\left(\begin{pmatrix}V_{n}^{(1)}\\
V_{n}^{(2)}\\
V_{n}^{(3)}\\
V_{n}^{(4,i)}\end{pmatrix}\right)_{n\geq 4}\xrightarrow{\text{d}}\begin{pmatrix}N_{1}\\
N_{1}^{2}-p\\
N_{1}^{3}-3p\,N_{1}\\
H(N_{1})\end{pmatrix},

(19)

where the convergence of
(V_{n}^{(4,i)})_{n\geq k_{2}}
is argued with Lee
[
19
, Section 3.2.3]
; see there for further details, especially regarding the function
H
.
N_{2}
is going to be needed later.

Returning to Hoeffding’s decomposition we now have

(S_{k_{1},p}^{(n)})^{k_{1}p}=1+k_{1}\,\frac{V_{n}^{(1)}}{\sqrt{n}}+\binom{k_{1}}{2}\frac{V_{n}^{(2)}}{n}+\binom{k_{1}}{3}\frac{V_{n}^{(3)}}{n^{3/2}}+\frac{V_{n}^{(4,i)}}{n^{2}}.

Consequently, everything after the constant term converges to zero in probability, and we are going to apply Taylor series expansion, keeping explicit track only of terms
\xi_{n}
for which
\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{3/2}\xi_{n}=0
in probability does
not
hold true.

Recall that for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(1+x)^{\alpha}=1+\alpha x+\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}{2}\,x^{2}+\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)}{6}\,x^{3}+\rho(x),

(20)

where there exist
M,\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that the remainder term satisfies
\lvert\rho(x)\rvert\leq Mx^{4}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
with
\lvert x\rvert\leq\delta
. In our case,
x_{n}:=k_{1}\,\frac{V_{n}^{(1)}}{\sqrt{n}}+\binom{k_{1}}{2}\frac{V_{n}^{(2)}}{n}+\binom{k_{1}}{3}\frac{V_{n}^{(3)}}{n^{3/2}}+\frac{V_{n}^{(4,i)}}{n^{2}}
, so
\Var[x_{n}]\leq\frac{C}{n}
for all
n\geq k_{2}
with some constant
C\in(0,\infty)
. Therefore,
\Var[n^{3/8}x_{n}]\leq Cn^{-1/4}
and so by Chebyshev’s inequality
(n^{3/8}x_{n})_{n\geq k_{2}}
converges to zero in probability, and hence via Lemma
3.5
(with
l=4
)
(n^{3/2}\rho(x_{n}))_{n\geq k_{2}}
converges to zero in probability. Also, after plugging
x_{n}
into (
20
) and expanding the powers, we get terms of the shape
\frac{(V_{n}^{(i)})^{a}(V_{n}^{(j)})^{b}}{n^{c}}
, and since
n^{3/2}\frac{(V_{n}^{(i)})^{a}(V_{n}^{(j)})^{b}}{n^{c}}=n^{3/2-c}(V_{n}^{(i)})^{a}(V_{n}^{(j)})^{b}
converges to zero in probability if
c\geq 2
, we collect the latter together with the remainder term in a single variable, that is,

\displaystyle(S_{k_{1},p}^{(n)})^{\pm 1}

\displaystyle=\Biggl(1+k_{1}\,\frac{V_{n}^{(1)}}{\sqrt{n}}+\binom{k_{1}}{2}\frac{V_{n}^{(2)}}{n}+\binom{k_{1}}{3}\frac{V_{n}^{(3)}}{n^{3/2}}+\frac{V_{n}^{(4,i)}}{n^{2}}\Biggr)^{\pm 1/(k_{1}p)}

\displaystyle=1\pm\frac{1}{k_{1}p}\Biggl(k_{1}\,\frac{V_{n}^{(1)}}{\sqrt{n}}+\binom{k_{1}}{2}\frac{V_{n}^{(2)}}{n}+\binom{k_{1}}{3}\frac{V_{n}^{(3)}}{n^{3/2}}\Biggr)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1\mp k_{1}p}{2k_{1}^{2}p^{2}}\Biggl(\frac{k_{1}^{2}(V_{n}^{(1)})^{2}}{n}+k_{1}^{2}(k_{1}-1)\frac{V_{n}^{(1)}V_{n}^{(2)}}{n^{3/2}}\Biggr)

\displaystyle\quad\pm\frac{(1\mp k_{1}p)(1\mp 2k_{1}p)}{6k_{1}^{3}p^{3}}\,\frac{k_{1}^{3}(V_{n}^{(1)})^{3}}{n^{3/2}}+R_{1}

\displaystyle=1\pm\frac{V_{n}^{(1)}}{p\sqrt{n}}\pm\frac{(k_{1}-1)V_{n}^{(2)}}{2pn}\pm\frac{(k_{1}-1)(k_{1}-2)V_{n}^{(3)}}{6pn^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(1\mp k_{1}p)(V_{n}^{(1)})^{2}}{2p^{2}n}+\frac{(1\mp k_{1}p)(k_{1}-1)V_{n}^{(1)}V_{n}^{(2)}}{2p^{2}n^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\quad\pm\frac{(1\mp k_{1}p)(1\mp 2k_{1}p)(V_{n}^{(1)})^{3}}{6p^{3}n^{3/2}}+R_{1},

where
(n^{3/2}R_{1})_{n\geq k_{2}}\to 0
in probability; we also have an analogous expansion w.r.t.
k_{2}
.

With all this at our disposal, we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\Biggl(1+\frac{V_{n}^{(1)}}{p\sqrt{n}}+\frac{(k_{2}-1)V_{n}^{(2)}}{2pn}+\frac{(k_{2}-1)(k_{2}-2)V_{n}^{(3)}}{6pn^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{(1-k_{2}p)(V_{n}^{(1)})^{2}}{2p^{2}n}+\frac{(1-k_{2}p)(k_{2}-1)V_{n}^{(1)}V_{n}^{(2)}}{2p^{2}n^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{(1-k_{2}p)(1-2k_{2}p)(V_{n}^{(1)})^{3}}{6p^{3}n^{3/2}}+R_{2}\Biggr)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\Biggl(1-\frac{V_{n}^{(1)}}{p\sqrt{n}}-\frac{(k_{1}-1)V_{n}^{(2)}}{2pn}-\frac{(k_{1}-1)(k_{1}-2)V_{n}^{(3)}}{6pn^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{(1+k_{1}p)(V_{n}^{(1)})^{2}}{2p^{2}n}+\frac{(1+k_{1}p)(k_{1}-1)V_{n}^{(1)}V_{n}^{(2)}}{2p^{2}n^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{(1+k_{1}p)(1+2k_{1}p)(V_{n}^{(1)})^{3}}{6p^{3}n^{3/2}}+R_{1}\Biggr)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{(k_{2}-k_{1})(V_{n}^{(1)})^{2}}{2pn}+\frac{(k_{2}-k_{1})V_{n}^{(2)}}{2pn}+\frac{(k_{2}-k_{1})(k_{1}+k_{2})(V_{n}^{(1)})^{3}}{3pn^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{(k_{2}-k_{1})(k_{1}+k_{2}-1)V_{n}^{(1)}V_{n}^{(2)}}{2pn^{3/2}}+\frac{(k_{2}-k_{1})(k_{1}+k_{2}-3)V_{n}^{(3)}}{6pn^{3/2}}+R_{3},

where
R_{3}
collects all terms
\xi_{n}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{3/2}\xi_{n}=0
in probability, thus also
\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{3/2}R_{3}=0
in probability. We rearrange further,

\displaystyle\frac{np}{k_{2}-k_{1}}(\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{n}^{(2)}-(V_{n}^{(1)})^{2}}{2}+\frac{(k_{1}+k_{2})(V_{n}^{(1)})^{3}}{3\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{(k_{1}+k_{2}-1)V_{n}^{(1)}V_{n}^{(2)}}{2\sqrt{n}}+\frac{(k_{1}+k_{2}-3)V_{n}^{(3)}}{6\sqrt{n}}+R_{4},

where now
\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1/2}R_{4}=0
in probability. At this point we see
\bigl(V_{n}^{(2)}-(V_{n}^{(1)})^{2}\bigr)_{n\geq k_{2}}\xrightarrow{\text{d}}-p
from (
19
), and that all the other terms, when multiplied with
\sqrt{n}
, converge to some random variable in distribution. Therefore we have to ensure that
\bigl(\sqrt{n}\bigl(V_{n}^{(2)}-(V_{n}^{(1)})^{2}+p\bigr)\bigr)_{n\geq k_{2}}
converges in distribution, and we have to determine its limit. Now by both (
18
) and (
17
)

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\Bigl(V_{n}^{(2)}-(V_{n}^{(1)})^{2}+p\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\Bigl(\frac{n}{n-1}\Bigl((Z_{n}^{(1)})^{2}-p-\frac{Z_{n}^{(2)}}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigr)-(Z_{n}^{(1)})^{2}+p\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{n}}{n-1}\Bigl((Z_{n}^{(1)})^{2}-p\Bigr)-\frac{nZ_{n}^{(2)}}{n-1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{d}}-N_{2}.

Thus, together with (
19
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\Bigl(\frac{np}{k_{2}-k_{1}}\bigl(\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}-1\bigr)+\frac{p}{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}\Bigl(V_{n}^{(2)}-(V_{n}^{(1)})^{2}+p\Bigr)+\frac{(k_{1}+k_{2})(V_{n}^{(1)})^{3}}{3}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{(k_{1}+k_{2}-1)V_{n}^{(1)}V_{n}^{(2)}}{2}+\frac{(k_{1}+k_{2}-3)V_{n}^{(3)}}{6}+\sqrt{n}R_{4}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{d}}-\frac{N_{2}}{2}+\frac{(k_{1}+k_{2})N_{1}^{3}}{3}-\frac{(k_{1}+k_{2}-1)N_{1}(N_{1}^{2}-p)}{2}

\displaystyle\quad\qquad+\frac{(k_{1}+k_{2}-3)(N_{1}^{3}-3pN_{1})}{6}

\displaystyle=pN_{1}-\frac{N_{2}}{2}.

A routine calculation confirms that the limit has the claimed distribution. ∎

Remark 3.6
.

A closer inspection of the proof reveals that the statement can be generalized to such sequences
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of i.i.d. random variables that
\Erw[\lvert X_{1}\rvert^{4p}]<\infty
; then, calling
m:=\Erw[\lvert X_{1}\rvert^{p}]
and
v:=\Var[\lvert X_{1}\rvert^{p}]
, one can show

\sqrt{n}\Biggl(\frac{np}{k_{2}-k_{1}}\bigl(\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}-1\bigr)+\frac{v}{2m^{2}}\Biggr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{d}}\Normal(0,\rho^{2}),

where one has

\rho^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\Var\Biggl[\Biggl(\frac{\lvert X_{1}\rvert^{p}-m}{m}-\frac{v}{m^{2}}\Biggr)^{2}\Biggr].

The reader is invited to check that
\rho
is zero iff
\lvert X_{1}\rvert
almost surely takes at most two values
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
; in the case
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
under the condition
\prb[\lvert X_{1}\rvert=x_{1}]=\frac{x_{2}^{p}}{x_{1}^{p}+x_{2}^{p}}
.

3.5
Proofs of the corollaries

Proof of Corollary
1.2
.

Adopting the notation of Theorem
A
we have

\sqrt{n}\Bigl(\mathrm{e}^{-m_{p}}\mathscr{R}_{k,n,p}^{(n)}-1\Bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{d}}Z,

and because the normal CDF
\Phi
is continuous everywhere, this implies, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\prb\Bigl[\sqrt{n}\Bigl(\mathrm{e}^{-m_{p}}\mathscr{R}_{k,n,p}^{(n)}-1\Bigr)\geq t\Bigr]=\prb[Z\geq t]=1-\Phi\Bigl(\frac{t}{s_{p}}\Bigr).

In particular for
t=0
this becomes, after some rearrangement,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\prb\bigl[\mathscr{R}_{k,n,p}^{(n)}\geq\mathrm{e}^{m_{p}}\bigr]=1-\Phi(0)=\frac{1}{2}.

Writing out
\mathscr{R}_{k,n,p}^{(n)}
(see Equation (
2
)) yields the claimed formula.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

From Theorem
C
we have

\sqrt{n}\biggl(\frac{np}{k_{2}-k_{1}}\Bigl(\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}-1\Bigr)+\frac{p}{2}\biggr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{d}}Z,

and because the CDF of
Z
is continuous everywhere we even have uniform convergence of the CDFs, or also of the tail probabilities, that is,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Biggl\lvert\prb\biggl[\sqrt{n}\biggl(\frac{np}{k_{2}-k_{1}}\Bigl(\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}-1\Bigr)+\frac{p}{2}\biggr)\geq t\biggr]-\Bigl(1-\Phi\Bigl(\frac{t}{\rho_{p}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\Biggr\rvert=0.

This implies that, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
and any sequence
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}t_{n}=t
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\prb\biggl[\sqrt{n}\biggl(\frac{np}{k_{2}-k_{1}}\Bigl(\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}-1\Bigr)+\frac{p}{2}\biggr)\geq t_{n}\biggr]=1-\Phi\Bigl(\frac{t}{\rho_{p}}\Bigr),

where we interpret
\Phi(-\infty):=0
and
\Phi(\infty):=1
. Now choose
t_{n}:=-n
, then we see

\lim_{n\to\infty}\prb\biggl[\sqrt{n}\biggl(\frac{np}{k_{2}-k_{1}}\Bigl(\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}-1\Bigr)+\frac{p}{2}\biggr)\geq-n\biggr]=1-\Phi\Bigl(-\frac{\infty}{\rho_{p}}\Bigr)=1.

On the left
-
hand side notice

\prb\biggl[\sqrt{n}\biggl(\frac{np}{k_{2}-k_{1}}\Bigl(\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}-1\Bigr)+\frac{p}{2}\biggr)\geq-n\biggr]=\prb\biggl[\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}\geq 1-\frac{k_{2}-k_{1}}{p\sqrt{n}}-\frac{k_{2}-k_{1}}{2n}\biggr];

now let
c\in(0,1)
, then
1-\frac{k_{2}-k_{1}}{p\sqrt{n}}-\frac{k_{2}-k_{1}}{2n}\geq c
for eventually all
n
, and hence eventually

\prb\biggl[\sqrt{n}\biggl(\frac{np}{k_{2}-k_{1}}\Bigl(\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}-1\Bigr)+\frac{p}{2}\biggr)\geq-n\biggr]\leq\prb\bigl[\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}\geq c\bigr]\leq 1.

Letting
n\to\infty
and writing out
\mathscr{R}_{k_{1},k_{2},p}^{(n)}
concludes the proof.
∎
