Unambiguous and Co-Nondeterministic Computations of Finite Automata and Pushdown Automata Families and the Effects of Multiple Counters 1 1 1 A preliminary report [ 28 ] appeared in the Proceedings of the 18th Annual Conference on Theory and Applications of Models of Computation (TAMC 2024), Hong Kong, China, May 13-15, 2024, Lecture Notes in Computer Science, vol. 14637, pp. 14–25, Springer, 2024.


Abstract
Nonuniform families of polynomial-size finite automata and pushdown automata respectively have strong connections to nonuniform-NL and nonuniform-LOGCFL. We examine the behaviors of unambiguous and co-nondeterministic computations produced by such families of automata operating multiple counters, where a counter is a stack using only a single non-bottom symbol. As immediate consequences, we obtain various collapses of the complexity classes of families of promise problems solvable by finite and pushdown automata families when all valid instances are limited to either polynomially long strings or unary strings. A key technical ingredient of our proofs is an inductive counting of reachable vertices of each computation graph of finite and pushdown automata that operate multiple counters simultaneously.

1.1
Two Important Open Questions on Nonuniform (Stack-)State Complexity Classes

Nondeterministic computation
has played an important role in computational complexity theory as well as automata theory.
Associated with such computation, there are two central and crucial questions to resolve. (i) Can any co-nondeterministic computation be simulated on an appropriate nondeterministic machine?
(ii) Can any nondeterministic machine be made unambiguous?
In the polynomial-time setting, these questions correspond to the famous
\mathrm{NP}=?\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}\mathrm{NP}

\mathrm{NP}=?\mathrm{UP}

In this work, we attempt to resolve these questions in the setting of nonuniform polynomial state complexity classes.

of polynomial-size 2dfa’s

with

, where

is upper-bounded by

Choose a sufficiently large number

in

.
Let

. We call by a

The total number of distinct strings

is exactly

Since

, there exist two distinct strings

and

enters the same inner state, say,

in the first blocks of

and

and

is also accepted by

∎

3.2
Reducing the Number of Counters in Use

It is possible to reduce the number of counters in use on multi-counter automata and multi-counter pushdown automata.
Minsky
[
14
]
earlier demonstrated how to simulate a Turing machine on a 2-counter automaton with exponential overhead. Since we cannot use the same simulation technique due to its large overhead, we need to take another, more direct approach toward
2\mathrm{NCT}_{k}
and
2\mathrm{NPDCT}_{k}
.
Even without the requirement of polynomial ceiling, it is possible in general to reduce the number of counters in use down to “4” for 2ncta’s and “3” for 2npdcta’s as shown below.

Proposition 3.2

For any constants
k,k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq 4
and
k^{\prime}\geq 3
,
2\mathrm{NCT}_{k}=2\mathrm{NCT}_{4}
and
2\mathrm{NPDCT}_{k^{\prime}}=2\mathrm{NPDCT}_{3}
.
The same holds for the deterministic case.

A core of the proof of Proposition
3.2
is the following lemma on the simulation of every pair of counters by a single counter with the heavy use of an appropriately defined “pairing” function. Let
{\cal M}=\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denote any polynomial-size family of 2ncta’s or of 2npdcta’s running in time, in particular,
(n|x|)^{t}
for a fixed constant
t\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. This
{\cal M}
satisfies the following lemma. See also
[
27
]
for the reduction of the number of counters in the uniform setting.

Lemma 3.3

There exists a fixed deterministic procedure by which any single move of push/pop operations of two counters of
M_{n}
can be simulated by a series of operations with one counter with the help of 3 extra counters. These extra 3 counters are emptied after each simulation and thus they are reusable for any other purposes. If we freely use a stack during this simulation procedure, then we need only two extra counters instead of three. The state complexity of the procedure is
n^{O(1)}
.

For readability, we postpone the proof of Lemma
3.3
until Appendix and continue the proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

We first look into the case of
2\mathrm{NCT}_{k}
for every index
k\geq 4
and wish to prove that
2\mathrm{NCT}_{k}\subseteq 2\mathrm{NCT}_{4}
.

Let
{\cal M}=\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denote any nonuniform family of polynomial-size
k
-counter 2ncta’s running in polynomial time, where
M_{n}
has the form
(Q_{n},\Sigma,k,\{1,\bot\},\delta_{n},q_{0,n},Q_{acc,n},Q_{rej,n})
. Take two polynomials
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
such that
|Q_{n}|\leq p_{1}(n)
and
M_{n}
halts within
p_{2}(n,|x|)
steps for any number
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any string
x\in\Sigma^{*}
.

In what follows, we fix
n
and
x
arbitrarily. We abbreviate
p_{2}(n,p_{1}(n))+1
as
p
and consider the pairing function
\langle i_{1},i_{2}\rangle_{p}
defined as
\langle i_{1},i_{2}\rangle_{p}=i_{1}\cdot p+i_{2}
for any
i_{1},i_{2}\in[0,p-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
.

We first group together
2\cdot\lfloor k/2\rfloor
counters into pairs and apply Lemma
3.3
to simulate each pair of counters by a single counter with the help of three extra reusable counters, say, CT1–CT3. This process successfully eliminates
\lfloor k/2\rfloor

counters except for CT1–CT3.
We repeat this process until there remains one counter other than CT1–CT3. Since there are the total of four counters left unremoved, this shows that
2\mathrm{NCT}_{k}\subseteq 2\mathrm{NCT}_{4}
.

Next, we intend to show that
2\mathrm{NPDCT}_{k^{\prime}}\subseteq 2\mathrm{NPDCT}_{3}
for any
k^{\prime}\geq 3
. In this case, we follow the same argument as described above
by removing all counters except for one counter and CT1–CT3. Finally, four counters are left unremoved. As shown in Lemma
3.3
, we can further reduce the number of counters to three. This is because we can utilize a stack, which is originally provided to an underlying pushdown automaton.

\Box

It is not clear that “4” and “3” are the smallest numbers supporting Proposition
3.2
for 2ncta’s and 2npdcta’s, respectively. We may conjecture that
2\mathrm{NCT}_{i}\neq 2\mathrm{NCT}_{i+1}
and
2\mathrm{NPDCT}_{j}\neq 2\mathrm{NPDCT}_{j+1}
for all
i\in[3]
and
j\in[2]
.

3.3

Let us consider the question raised in Section

on the closure property under complementation,
namely, the

Proof of Theorem

.

\Box

]

5
Effects of Polynomial Ceiling Bounds

Let us recall the notion of polynomial ceiling, which refers to the input restriction to only polynomially long strings. We will see significant effects given by the polynomial ceilings.

5.1
Elimination of Counters by Polynomial Ceiling Bounds

In Section
3.2
, we have discussed how to reduce the number of counters down to three or four.

In the presence of polynomial ceilings, it is possible to eliminate “all” counters from multi-counter automata and multi-counter pushdown automata.

Theorem 5.1

Let

Proof.

Here, we prove only (2) since the proof of (1) is in essence similar. Since

for all

, we obtain
2\mathrm{NPD}/\mathrm{poly}\subseteq 2\mathrm{NPDCT}_{k}/\mathrm{poly}

We next show that

.
Consider any family
{\cal L}=\{(L_{n}^{(+)},L_{n}^{(-)})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

]
. However, we do not know, for instance, whether
2\mathrm{N}=2\mathrm{U}
or even
2\mathrm{NCT}_{k}=2\mathrm{UCT}_{k}
for any
k\geq 4
even though (1) holds.
The latter equality is compared to Theorem
3.4
, which states that
2\mathrm{NCT}_{4}=\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{NCT}_{4}
.
Hereafter, we focus on the statement (2) of Theorem
5.5
and provide its proof.
The basis of the proof relies on the result of Reinhardt and Allender
[
15
]
, which asserts that
\mathrm{LOGCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
coincides with
\mathrm{UAuxPDA,\!\!TISP}(n^{O(1)},\log{n})/\mathrm{poly}
(which is currently expressed as
\mathrm{LOGUCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
).

Proof of Theorem
5.5
(2).

Since unambiguity is a special case of nondeterminism,
2\mathrm{UPD}
is obviously contained in
2\mathrm{NPD}
. Thus,
\mathrm{ptime}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{UPD}/\mathrm{poly}\subseteq\mathrm{ptime}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{NPD}/\mathrm{poly}
immediately follows.
In what follows, we intend to prove the opposite inclusion under the polynomial runtime restriction, namely,
\mathrm{ptime}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{NPD}/\mathrm{poly}\subseteq\mathrm{ptime}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{UPD}/\mathrm{poly}
.

The proof constitutes the verification of the following claim concerning
\mathrm{LOGUCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
and
\mathrm{ptime}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{UPD}
. A similar claim was proven first in
[
23
]
, and later proven in
[
24
]
for
\mathrm{UL}/\mathrm{poly}
and in
[
22
]
for
\mathrm{LOGCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
. Refer to these references for their individual arguments.

Claim 5.6

The following two statements hold.

(i)

\mathrm{LOGCFL}\subseteq\mathrm{LOGUCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
implies
\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{LOGCFL}/\mathrm{poly}\cap\mathrm{PHSP}\subseteq\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{LOGUCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
.

(ii)

\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{LOGCFL}/\mathrm{poly}\cap\mathrm{PHSP}\subseteq\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{LOGUCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
implies
\mathrm{ptime}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{NPD}/\mathrm{poly}\subseteq\mathrm{ptime}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{UPD}
.

It is known that
\mathrm{LOGCFL}\subseteq\mathrm{LOGUCFL}/\mathrm{poly}

[
15
]
. From this fact, Claim
5.6
(i) makes
\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{LOGCFL}/\mathrm{poly}\cap\mathrm{PHSP}
collapse to
\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{LOGUCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
, and Claim
5.6
(ii) further yields
\mathrm{ptime}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{NPD}/\mathrm{poly}\subseteq\mathrm{ptime}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{UPD}
, as requested.

Hereafter, we wish to give the proof of Claim
5.6
.

(i) Let us begin with the proof of Claim
5.6
(i).
Assume that
\mathrm{LOGCFL}\subseteq\mathrm{LOGUCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
and let
(L,m)
denote any parameterized decision problem in
\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{LOGCFL}/\mathrm{poly}\cap\mathrm{PHSP}
. By definition,
m
is log-space computable.
Since
m
is also polynomially honest, there is a polynomial
q
satisfying
|x|\leq q(m(x))
for all
x
. We intend to turn
(L,m)
into its corresponding language, say,
P
as follows.
Since
(L,m)\in\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{LOGCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
, there are a polynomially-bounded advice function
h
and an advised aux-2npda
M_{0}
such that
M_{0}
recognizes the language
\{(x,h(m(x)))\mid x\in L\}
in time
m(x)^{O(1)}
and space
O(\log{m(x)})
.
We define
L_{n}^{(+)}=L\cap\Sigma_{(n)}
and
L_{n}^{(-)}=\overline{L}\cap\Sigma_{(n)}
for all indices
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\overline{L}=\Sigma^{*}-L
and
\Sigma_{(n)}=\{x\in\Sigma^{*}\mid m(x)=n\}
.
The desired set
P
is then defined to be
\{(x,1^{t})\mid x\in L,t\in\mathbb{N},m(x)\leq t\}
.

To show that
P\in\mathrm{LOGCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
, we consider the following algorithm. Let
\hat{h}(t)
express the string
h(0)\#h(1)\#\cdots\#h(t)
.

On input
(x,1^{t})
with advice string
\hat{h}(t)
, first check whether
m(x)>t
using log space. If so, reject the input immediately. This is possible because
m
is log-space computable. Otherwise, run
M_{0}
on
x
with the help of advice string
h(m(x))
, which is retrieved from
\hat{h}(t)
.

This algorithm can be carried out on an appropriate advised aux-2npda running in time
(t|x|)^{O(1)}
and space
O(\log{t|x|})
. Hence, we obtain
P\in\mathrm{LOGCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
.

Our assumption then guarantees that
P
belongs to
\mathrm{LOGUCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
. Take an advice function
g
and an advised aux-2upda
M_{1}
for
P
.
From these
M_{1}
and
g
, it is possible to define another advised aux-2upda
N
that works as follows. On input
x
, compute
m(x)
using log space and run
M_{1}
on the input
(x,1^{m(x)})
with advice string
g(m(x))
. This implies that
L
is recognized by
N
in time
m(x)^{O(1)}
using space
O(\log{m(x)})
with the help of
g
. Hence, we can conclude that
(L,m)
falls in
\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{LOGUCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
.

(ii) We then prove Claim
5.6
(ii). Assume that
\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{LOGCFL}/\mathrm{poly}\cap\mathrm{PHSP}\subseteq\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{LOGUCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
.
Take any family
{\cal L}=\{(L_{n}^{(+)},L_{n}^{(-)})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of promise problems in
\mathrm{ptime}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{NPD}/\mathrm{poly}
over alphabet
\Sigma
and
take the parameterized decision problem
(K,m)
induced from
{\cal L}
via a size-incorporated family
{\cal K}=\{(K_{n}^{(+)},K_{n}^{(-)})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
{\cal L}
. Notice that
K=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}K_{n}^{(+)}
and
\overline{K}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}K_{n}^{(-)}
.
Since
2\mathrm{NPD}
is
\mathrm{L}
-good as remarked earlier,
the statements (b)&(c) of Definition
4.3
guarantee that
(K,m)\in\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{LOGCFL}/\mathrm{poly}\cap\mathrm{PHSP}
iff
{\cal L}\in\mathrm{ptime}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{NPD}/\mathrm{poly}
. As a consequence, we obtain
(K,m)\in\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{LOGCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
.
Since
\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{LOGCFL}/\mathrm{poly}\cap\mathrm{PHSP}\subseteq\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{LOGUCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
by our assumption,
(K,m)
belongs to
\mathrm{para}\mbox{-}\mathrm{LOGUCFL}/\mathrm{poly}
. By using Definition
4.3
(b), we can conclude that
{\cal L}
falls in
\mathrm{ptime}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{UPD}/\mathrm{poly}
.
Therefore, Claim
5.6
(ii) is true.

This completes the entire proof of the theorem.

\Box

6
Case of Unary Alphabets

Through Section
5
, we have discussed collapses of nonuniform state complexity classes when promised inputs are all limited to polynomially long ones.
Here, we briefly mention the simplest case of unary instances in comparison with polynomial ceilings.

In the simple case of unary inputs, let us first recall the result of Geffert, Mereghetti, and Pighizzini
[
5
]
, who demonstrated a simulation of a “complementary” 2nfa (i.e., a two-way finite automaton making co-nondeterministic moves) on unary inputs by another 2nfa with a polynomial increase of the state complexity. From this fact, we can instantly draw a conclusion that
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{N}/\mathrm{unary}
coincides with
2\mathrm{N}/\mathrm{unary}
as stated in Fig.
1
. This collapse was already mentioned in
[
9
]
.

Proposition 6.1

2\mathrm{N}/\mathrm{unary}=\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{N}/\mathrm{unary}
.

Next, we recall the result of Geffert and Pighizzini
[
6
]
, in which any 2nfa can be simulated by an appropriate 2ufa with a polynomial increase of the state complexity of the 2nfa. From this follows the collapse of
2\mathrm{N}/\mathrm{unary}
to
2\mathrm{U}/\mathrm{unary}
as stated in Fig.
1
.

Proposition 6.2

2\mathrm{N}/\mathrm{unary}=2\mathrm{U}/\mathrm{unary}
.

The above two propositions should be compared to Theorems
5.5
(1) and
5.4
(1) regarding polynomial-ceiling restrictions.

7
Future Directions and Open Questions

We have continued a study on the features of nonuniform (stack-)state complexity classes, initiated in 1978 by Sakoda and Sipser
[
17
]
.

To obtain two major contributions of Section
5
(Theorems
5.5
and
5.4
), we have employed a generic proof argument, introduced in
[
23
]
, of exploiting a close connection between nonuniform state complexity classes and parameterized complexity classes as described in Section
4
. This connection is proven in Section
5
to be quite useful even if there is no (known) complete problems.

Another important contribution is to supplement multiple counters to underlying finite and pushdown automata. With the use of such counters, we have significantly expanded the scope of nonuniform (stack-)state complexity research in Section
3
.

Here, we provide a short list of open questions for the avid reader.

1.

In Proposition
3.2
, we have reduced the number of counters used by each finite automaton down to
4
and by each pushdown automaton to
3
. Are those numbers the minimum? If so, can we prove that
2\mathrm{NCT}_{2}\neq 2\mathrm{NCT}_{3}\neq 2\mathrm{NCT}_{4}
?

2.

In Theorems
3.4
and
3.6
, we have shown that
2\mathrm{NCT}_{4}=\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{NCT}_{4}
and
2\mathrm{NPDCT}_{3}=\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{NPDCT}_{3}
. Can we remove any multiple counters and show that
2\mathrm{N}=\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{N}
and
2\mathrm{NPD}=\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}2\mathrm{NPD}
?

3.

Without any input-size bounds, is it true that
2\mathrm{N}=2\mathrm{U}
and
2\mathrm{NPD}=2\mathrm{UPD}
? Moreover, do their counter versions
2\mathrm{NCT}_{4}=2\mathrm{UCT}_{4}
and
2\mathrm{NPDCT}_{3}=2\mathrm{UPDCT}_{3}
hold?

4.

We have seen the usefulness of polynomial ceilings. Kapoutsis
[
11
]
discussed the cases of non-polynomial ceilings, including
2^{O(n^{k})}
ceilings and
2^{O(\log^{k}{n})}
ceilings. Can we obtain results similar to ones in this work when all families in a given complexity class have those ceilings?

5.

In Section
6
, we have had a short discussion on the case of unary instances in nonuniform families of machines. It is of importance to explore the unary input case in a wider range of nonuniform state complexity classes.

6.

In comparison to Theorem
5.4
(2), Theorem
5.5
(2) requires the “polynomial runtime” bound of underlying machines because of the use of Claim
5.6
.
Can we remove this “polynomial runtime” restriction from Theorem
5.5
(2)?

Appendix: Proof of Lemma
3.3

In this Appendix, we provide the omitted proof of Lemma
3.3
from the main text for the readability.

Given a family
{\cal M}=\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of polynomial-size machines, we assume that
M_{n}
runs in time
(n|x|)^{k}
for a fixed constant
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.
We start with fixing a number
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\Sigma^{*}
arbitrarily in the following explanation. Let
p
denote
(n|x|)^{k}
for convenience.
Now, we explain how to reduce 2 counters to one counter (with the help of 3 extra reusable counters).

For each index
j\in[2]
,
i_{j}
denotes the current content of the
j
th counter of
M_{n}
. Let us consider the pair
(i_{1},i_{2})
. We encode this pair into a number defined as
i_{1}\cdot p+i_{2}
, which is succinctly expressed as
\langle i_{1},i_{2}\rangle_{p}
. We wish to simulate the behavior of the counters of the machine using four counters CT1, CT2, CT3, and CT4. The number
\langle i_{1},i_{2}\rangle_{p}
will be stored in CT3, and CT2 is used to remember the value
p
during the simulation.

(a) Here, we describe how to produce
p
in CT2 using CT1, CT4, and a tape head.
Firstly, we store the current tape head position into CT4 by moving the tape head to the start cell
so that we will recover this tape head position after the production of
p
in CT2. Reset CT1 and CT2 to be empty.
(i) Produce
n|x|
in CT1 by sweeping the input tape
n
times between the two endmarkers by the tape head. Note that
n
is a constant for
M_{n}
and it should be provided by
M_{n}
’s inner states.
(ii) Increment CT2 by
n|x|
by moving the tape head. Decrement CT1 by 1.
(iii) Repeat (ii) until CT1 becomes empty.
(iv) Finally, move the tape head to the start cell and return it back to the original position by emptying CT4. Reset CT1 and CT4 to be empty.

(b) Now, we describe how to check whether
i_{j}=0
or not for each
j\in[2]
for the current value
\langle i_{1},i_{2}\rangle_{p}
stored in CT3. Initially, we set CT4 to be empty. Firstly, we check if CT3 is empty. If so, then we know
i_{1}=i_{2}=0
and thus we terminate the procedure (b).
Assume that CT3 is not empty. Reset CT1 to be empty. Note that CT2 contains
p
by the procedure (a). In the following process, we maintain the condition that the sum of the contents of CT3 and CT4 equals
\langle i_{1},i_{2}\rangle_{p}
. Similarly, we keep the condition that the sum of the contents of CT1 and CT2 equals
p
.
(i) Decrement CT2 and CT3 and increment CT1 and CT4 simultaneously by one until either CT2 or CT3 becomes empty.
(iii) If CT3 is empty, then we know that
i_{1}=0
. In this case,
i_{2}
must be positive.
Move the content of CT4 to CT3 by decrementing CT4 and incrementing CT3 simultaneously until CT4 is empty. Reset CT1 and CT2 to be empty.
(iv) If CT2 is empty, then we know that
i_{1}\neq 0
. Move the content of CT1 to CT2. Repeat (ii)-(iv) until (ii) stops with CT3 being empty. Note that
i_{1}
equals the total number of repetitions.
(v) Finally, recover
\langle i_{1},i_{2}\rangle_{p}
in CT3 by moving the content of CT4 to CT3.

(c) Let us describe how to simulate a push/pop operation of the counters made by
M_{n}
. Assume that
(i_{1},i_{2})
is changed to
(j_{1},j_{2})
by
M_{n}
in a single step. After the procedure (b), we already know whether
i_{1}
as well as
i_{2}
is either zero or positive. (i) If
j_{1}=i_{1}+1
, then keep incrementing CT3 and CT1 as we decrement CT2 down to
0
. Move the content of CT1 to CT2 by emptying CT1. (ii) If
j_{2}=i_{2}+1
, then we push “
1
” into CT3.
(iii) If
i_{1}>0
and
j_{1}=i_{1}-1
, then we decrement CT1, CT2, and CT3 until CT2 becomes empty. Add the remaining content of CT1 to CT2.
(iv) If
i_{2}>0
and
j_{2}=i_{2}-1
, then we pop “
1
” from CT3.

(d) If a stack is usable during the processes (a)–(c), then we can use the stack as a counter by first writing a separator, say,
\#
used as a new bottom marker and starting either pushing “
1
” or popping it. This makes it possible to reduce the number of counters further down to three.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
