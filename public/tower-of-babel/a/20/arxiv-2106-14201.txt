The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/Takhtajan.html
Igor KRICHEVER acd and Nikita NEKRASOV bce

Abstract
We show that Novikov–Veselov hierarchy provides a complete family of commuting symmetries of two-dimensional O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) sigma model. In the first part of the paper we use these symmetries to prove that the Fermi spectral curve for the double-periodic sigma model is algebraic. Thus, our previous construction of the complexified harmonic maps in the case of irreducible Fermi curves is complete. In the second part of the paper we generalize our construction to the case of reducible Fermi curves and show that it gives the conformal harmonic maps to even -dimensional spheres. Remarkably, the solutions are parameterized by spectral curves of turning points of the elliptic Calogero–Moser system.

Introduction

Harmonic maps of two-dimensional Riemann surface
\Sigma
to a Riemann manifold
M

are of interest both in physics and mathematics. The harmonic maps are the critical points of the Dirichlet functional, the sigma model action

\displaystyle S(X)=\int_{\Sigma}\sqrt{h}h^{ab}g_{ij}(X)\partial_{a}X^{i}\partial_{b}X^{j}\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y=\int_{\Sigma}g_{ij}(X){{\partial}}X^{i}{\bar{\partial}}X^{j}.

(1.1)

Here the map
{\Phi}\colon\Sigma\to M
is represented, locally, by the pullbacks
X^{i}(x,y)={\Phi}^{*}x^{i}
,
i=1,\dots,\dim M
, of the coordinate functions
(x^{i})
on
M
to
\Sigma
, with
x
,
y
local real coordinates on
\Sigma
. In (
1.1
)
(x,y)
denote the real coordinates on
\Sigma
, while
{{\partial}}={{\partial}}_{z}
,
{{\bar{\partial}}}={\partial}_{\bar{z}}
denote the
z
and
\bar{z}
-derivatives in the complex structure
determined by the conformal class of the metric
h_{ab}
via
h_{ab}{\rm d}y^{a}{\rm d}y^{b}\propto{\rm d}z{\rm d}{\bar{z}}
. Finally,
g_{ij}(X){\rm d}X^{i}{\rm d}X^{j}
is a Riemann metric on the target manifold
M
.

Remark 1.1
.

Assume
M
is a real slice of a complex manifold
M_{\mathbb{C}}
whose metric
g_{ij}(X)\,{\rm d}X^{i}{\rm d}X^{j}
admits an analytic continuation to
M_{\mathbb{C}}
as a holomorphic symmetric
(2,0)
-tensor field
g_{\mathbb{C}}
with positive definite real part. Likewise, we can allow for the complex metric
h_{\mathbb{C}}=h_{ab}\,{\rm d}y^{a}{\rm d}y^{b}
on the worldsheet
\Sigma
, which defines two conformal structures: one by
h_{\mathbb{C}}({{\partial}}_{z},{{\partial}}_{z})=0
, another by
h_{\mathbb{C}}({{\partial}}_{\bar{z}},{{\partial}}_{\bar{z}})=0
.
For real
h
these structures are complex conjugate to each other (as points in
{\mathcal{M}}_{g}
). In what follows we shall make these abstract constructions quite explicit in the case of a two dimensional torus
\Sigma=S^{1}\times S^{1}
.

In case
M
admits a group
G
of isometries, extending, possibly infinitesimally, to the action of
\operatorname{Lie}(G_{\mathbb{C}})
on
M_{\mathbb{C}}
preserving
g_{\mathbb{C}}
, the action (
1.1
) can be deformed to

\displaystyle S_{h_{\mathbb{C}},A_{\mathbb{C}}}(X)=\int_{\Sigma}\sqrt{h_{\mathbb{C}}}h_{\mathbb{C}}^{ab}(g_{\mathbb{C}})_{ij}(X){\nabla}_{a}X^{i}{\nabla}_{b}X^{j}\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y,

(1.2)

where
{\nabla}_{a}X^{i}={{\partial}}_{a}X^{i}+(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{a}^{\rm A}V_{\rm A}^{i}(X)
is the covariant derivative in the background gauge field
A_{\mathbb{C}}
on
\Sigma
, a connection on a principal
G_{\mathbb{C}}
-bundle
\mathcal{P}
over
\Sigma
. The action (
1.2
) then describes the harmonic sections of the bundle

M_{\mathbb{C}}\times_{G_{\mathbb{C}}}{\mathcal{P}}

over
\Sigma
. We call this problem a
twisted sigma model
. We only consider the case of flat
G_{\mathbb{C}}
-connections,

{{\partial}}_{a}(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{b}-{{\partial}}_{b}(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{a}+[(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{a},(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{b}]=0

on a topologically trivial bundle
{\mathcal{P}}={\Sigma}\times G_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Remark 1.2
.

This paper is a continuation of
[
22
]
, where the most important case is the complexified twisted one. In particular, it motivates the Fermi-curve approach we also employ presently. Nevertheless, in this paper, mostly, we study the real untwisted case.

The zero-curvature representation of the equation of motion for the two-dimensional
O(N)
sigma-model, whose target manifold is the sphere
M=S^{N-1}
with the metric induced by a standard imbedding into
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, was discovered by Pohlmeyer in
[
29
]
where an infinite series of conservation laws was constructed.
In
[
32
]
these results were extended to a wider class of models including the principal chiral field model – the sigma model with the target space
M=G
, a Lie group with the bi-invariant metric
\operatorname{tr}\big(X^{-1}{\rm d}X\big)^{2}
.

In
[
16
]
it was shown that
locally
the principal chiral field equations are integrable in the following strong sense: their solutions admit a non-linear analog of the d’Alembert representation as a superposition of functions depending only on one variable. The problem of constructing the
global

solutions, i.e., solutions defined on a compact Riemann surface is much harder.

Remark 1.3
.

The models studied in
[
16
,
29
,
32
]
were the sigma models with Minkowski source. Accordingly, the equations of motion of these models are hyperbolic. The equations of motion of the sigma models studied in the present paper are elliptic. The equations of motion and their zero-curvature representation
are easily transformed to the elliptic case by the simple change of the light-cone variables

\xi_{\pm}=x\pm t
to the holomorphic-antiholomorphic variables
z
,
\bar{z}
.
However, the nature of solutions in the two cases is quite different, the compactness of the source
\Sigma
playing a key rôle.

1.1
Worldsheet is a two-torus

Our main interest is in the double-periodic two-dimensional sigma model, i.e., we take
\Sigma
to be a two-dimensional torus
T^{2}={S}^{1}\times{S}^{1}
. Its conformal structure is parameterized by the complex number
\tau
, with
\operatorname{Im}\tau>0
, which usually is introduced by representing
{\Sigma}\approx{\mathbb{C}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\oplus{\tau}{\mathbb{Z}}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Sigma={\mathbb{R}}^{2}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}=\{(z,\bar{z})\sim(z+\omega_{\alpha},\bar{z}+\bar{\omega}_{\alpha})\,|\,{\alpha}=x,y\},\\
&\omega_{x}={\bar{\omega}}_{x}=1,\qquad\omega_{y}=\tau,\qquad{\bar{\omega}}_{y}={\bar{\tau}}.\end{split}

(1.3)

In the complexified approach we have the complex metric

\displaystyle h_{\mathbb{C}}=\frac{({\rm d}x+{\tau}{\rm d}y)({\rm d}x+{\bar{\tau}}{\rm d}y)}{{\tau}_{2}},

(1.4)

whose conformal class is parametrized by two complex numbers
{\tau}_{1}
,
{\tau}_{2}
, with
\operatorname{Re}({\tau}_{2})>0
, or, equivalently, by

{\tau}={\tau}_{1}+{\mathrm{i}}{\tau}_{2},\qquad{\bar{\tau}}={\tau}_{1}-{\mathrm{i}}{\tau}_{2}.

We shall call the real two-torus
T^{2}
endowed with the complex metric (
1.4
) with
\operatorname{Re}({\tau}_{2})>0
a
Euclidean torus
T^{2}
in what follows.

The holomorphic vector field
{{\partial}}_{z}
and the antiholomorphic vector field
{{\partial}}_{\bar{z}}
are given by

\displaystyle{{\partial}}_{z}=\frac{{{\partial}}_{y}-{\bar{\tau}}{{\partial}}_{x}}{2{\mathrm{i}}{\tau}_{2}},\qquad{\partial}_{\bar{z}}=\frac{{{\partial}}_{y}-{\tau}{{\partial}}_{x}}{-2{\mathrm{i}}{\tau}_{2}}

and the (
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear) Hodge star acts on
1
-forms on
\Sigma
via

\displaystyle\star({\rm d}x+{\tau}{\rm d}y)={\mathrm{i}}({\rm d}x+{\tau}{\rm d}y),\qquad

\displaystyle\star({\rm d}x+{\bar{\tau}}{\rm d}y)=-{\mathrm{i}}({\rm d}x+{\bar{\tau}}{\rm d}y),

\displaystyle\star{\rm d}x=-\frac{{\tau}_{1}{\rm d}x+{\tau}{\bar{\tau}}{\rm d}y}{{\tau}_{2}},\qquad

\displaystyle\star{\rm d}y=\frac{{\rm d}x+{\tau}_{1}{\rm d}y}{{\tau}_{2}}.

In what follows we shall use the notations
T_{x}
,
T_{y}
for the translations

\displaystyle T_{x}\colon\ (x,y)\mapsto(x+1,y),\qquad T_{y}\colon\ (x,y)\mapsto(x,y+1)

(1.5)

of the universal cover
{\tilde{\Sigma}}={\mathbb{R}}^{2}
of
{\Sigma}={\tilde{\Sigma}}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, also known as the deck transformations.

Below, in using the derivatives along
\Sigma
, we either use specifically
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
-derivatives
{{\partial}}_{z}
and
{{\partial}}_{\bar{z}}
, or use the exterior derivative

{\rm d}_{\Sigma}={\rm d}x{{\partial}}_{x}+{\rm d}y{{\partial}}_{y}={\rm d}z{{\partial}}_{z}+{\rm d}{\bar{z}}{{\partial}}_{\bar{z}}.

The differentials
{\rm d}w_{\alpha}
,
{\rm d}k
,
{\rm d}{\Omega}
etc. denote the
(1,0)
-differentials along the Fermi-curve.

1.2
Spectral curves from zero-curvature equations

The harmonic maps of the two-torus
T^{2}
to
S^{3}
were constructed in
[
13
]
via the zero-curvature representation for the principal chiral
{\rm SU}(2)
model. The latter is the compatibility condition for the system of two linear equations

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{z}-\frac{U(z,\bar{z})}{\lambda+1}\right)\Psi(z,\bar{z},\lambda)=0,\qquad\left(\partial_{\bar{z}}+\frac{V(z,\bar{z})}{\lambda-1}\right)\Psi(z,\bar{z},\lambda)=0

(1.6)

with
U=X^{-1}\partial_{z}X
and
V=X^{-1}\partial_{\bar{z}}X
. Since the fundamental group of a torus is abelian the operators

\displaystyle B_{\alpha}(\lambda):=(T_{\alpha}^{*}\Psi(\lambda))\Psi^{-1}(\lambda),\qquad\alpha=x,y

of monodromy of flat connection (
1.6
) commute,
[B_{x}({\lambda}),B_{y}({\lambda})]=0
. Therefore, the branch points of the two-sheeted covers (for the
{\rm SU}(2)
case the matrices
B_{\alpha}
are of rank 2) of the complex
\lambda
-plane defined by the characteristic equations

\displaystyle R_{\alpha}(\mu_{\alpha},\lambda):=\det\left(\mu_{\alpha}\cdot\mathbb{I}-B_{\alpha}(\lambda)\right)=0

(1.7)

coincide. The key step in
[
13
]
was the proof that the number of these branch points is

finite
. The hyperelliptic curve defined by these branch points is a normalization of the analytic spectral curves (
1.7
).

In general, with this construction, given the hyperelliptic curve and a point on its Jacobian, we get a quasi-periodic harmonic map of the universal cover of
\Sigma
. Solving the
periodicity
constraint is a pain. Indeed, the double-periodic maps are singled out by certain conditions on periods of certain abelian differentials of the second kind on the hyperelliptic curve.
These equations are transcendental and so hard to control that the genus of the hyperelliptic curves in the examples found in
[
13
]
is
at most
three.

For further comparison with the results of that work, we recall one more observation made in
[
13
]
:
there is only one class of harmonic maps which can not be reconstructed by the algebraic-geometrical data, and those are the ones for which the Floquet multipliers
\mu_{\alpha}
are constants
.
These maps are the conformal maps to a
totally geodesic

2
-sphere in
S^{3}
which up to isometry of
S^{3}
is the locus

\big\{g^{2}=-1\,|\,g\in{\rm SU}(2)\big\}\subset{\rm SU}(2)\approx S^{3},

i.e., the
big equator two-sphere
. Although for such maps the spectral curve does not exist,
they can be found explicitly
[
1
]
.

Remark 1.4
.

Conformal
harmonic maps are the maps
X
for which the pull-back
X^{*}(g)
of the target space metric
g
is conformally equivalent to the worldsheet metric
h
on
\Sigma
. They are of special interest in geometry since their images are immersed
minimal
surfaces. A slight generalization of conformal harmonic maps are
branched conformal
harmonic maps, where
X^{*}(g)
becomes degenerate at a finite number of points.

.

We thank N. Hitchin for bringing to our attention the reference
[
6
]
where non-conformal harmonic maps
T^{2}\longrightarrow S^{n}
for arbitrary
n
were constructed from finite-dimensional orbits of symmetries of zero-curvature equations, while in
[
5
]
, using the ideas from twistor theory and the theory of solitons it was shown that
all
harmonic maps, except for
one special class
, can be found in terms of some finite-dimensional orbits of commuting Hamiltonian flows. The
special class
harmonic maps, the ones outside the reach of the zero-curvature representation framework, were called
pseudoholomorphic
in
[
7
]
,
super-minimal
in
[
2
]
or
isotropic
in
[
11
]
. We treat these maps in the second part of the paper.

1.3
Spheres from Euclidean spaces, and complexification

Let
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
be equipped with a non-degenerate quadratic form
{\bf q}\mapsto({\bf q},{\bf q})
.
The group
G_{\mathbb{C}}
of linear transformations
{\bf q}\mapsto g\cdot{\bf q}
preserving
({\bf q},{\bf q})

is isomorphic to
O(N,{\mathbb{C}})
. The hypersurface
M_{\mathbb{C}}
defined by
({\bf q},{\bf q})=1

has the complex metric
g_{\mathbb{C}}
induced from the complexified Euclidean metric
({\rm d}{\bf q},{\rm d}{\bf q})
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
. In the basis
{\bf e}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,N
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
in which
({\bf q},{\bf q})
looks like

({\bf q},{\bf q})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(q^{i}\big)^{2}

the real slice
M=S^{N-1}
corresponds to
q^{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. There are other real slices, of de Sitter, anti-de Sitter, or Lobachevsky geometry, where some of the
q^{i}
’s are purely imaginary.

Turning on the twists breaks the
O(N,{\mathbb{C}})
symmetry down to its maximal torus. Given a flat
O(N,{\mathbb{C}})
-connection
A_{\mathbb{C}}
on
\Sigma=S^{1}\times S^{1}
one can find a basis
{\bf e}_{i}
such that
the holonomies

\displaystyle g_{x}=P\exp\left(\oint{\rm d}x(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{x}\right),\qquad g_{y}=P\exp\left(\oint{\rm d}y(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{y}\right)

(the flatness guarantees the conjugacy classes of the path ordered exponentials do not depend on
y
and
x
, respectively)
are diagonal: for
N=2n
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&g_{x}({\bf e}_{2a-1}\pm{\mathrm{i}}{\bf e}_{2a})=w_{x,a}^{\pm 1}({\bf e}_{2a-1}\pm{\mathrm{i}}{\bf e}_{2a}),\\
&g_{y}({\bf e}_{2a-1}\pm{\mathrm{i}}{\bf e}_{2a})=w_{y,a}^{\pm 1}({\bf e}_{2a-1}\pm{\mathrm{i}}{\bf e}_{2a}),\qquad a=1,\dots,n,\end{split}

(1.8)

with some complex numbers
w_{x,a},w_{y,a}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
,
a=1,\dots,n
, while for
N=2n+1
(
1.8
) are accompanied by additional four choices:

g_{x}{\bf e}_{N}=\pm{\bf e}_{N},\qquad g_{y}{\bf e}_{N}=\pm{\bf e}_{N}.

Remark 1.6
.

Another deformation of the sigma-model breaking
O(N,{\mathbb{C}})
invariance is to disentangle the quadratic forms

({\rm d}{\bf q},{\rm d}{\bf q})
and
({\bf q},{\bf q})
. In this way on gets an ellipsoid (in modern parlance, a squashed sphere)
sigma model. Its point particle limit, i.e., the study of geodesics on the ellipsoid, is connected
[
26
]
to the so-called winding string
[
22
]
case of the twisted sigma model on a sphere. The connection in the general case is not known.

In the spherical
O(N)
-symmetric case, the embedding into the vector space allows to reformulate the sigma model as a constrained linear sigma model:

\displaystyle S\sim\int_{\Sigma}({{\partial}}_{z}{\bf q},{{\partial}}_{\bar{z}}{\bf q})+u(({\bf q},{\bf q})-1),

(1.9)

where
u=u(x,y)
is a double-periodic function on
\Sigma
, the Lagrange multiplier.

1.4
Fermi-curves

Our approach to solving the
O(N)
sigma model equations does not use their zero-curvature representation. It is a further development of the scheme proposed in
[
18
,
20
]
and extended in
[
22
]
. Roughly, this approach can be described as the construction of
integrable linear operators with self-consistent potentials
. The construction consists of two steps. The first step is to parametrize a periodic linear operator by
a spectral curve and a line bundle
(
divisor
)
on it
. The spectral curve parametrizes Bloch solutions of the linear equation. The second step of the construction is the characterization of the spectral curves for which there exists a set of points on the curve such that the corresponding Bloch solutions satisfy quadratic relation.

Unlike the zero-curvature representation,
the linear equations

\displaystyle(-{{\partial}}_{z}{{\partial}}_{\bar{z}}+u)q^{i}(z,\bar{z})=0,\qquad i=1,\dots,N,

(1.10)

are the equations of motion following from (
1.9
) and not some auxiliary linear equation. The potential
u
is the Lagrange multiplier enforcing the constraint that the vector
{\bf q}=(q^{i})_{i=1}^{N}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
with the coordinates
q^{i}
lies on the complexified unit sphere, i.e.,

\displaystyle({\bf q},{\bf q})=1.

(1.11)

From the equations (
1.10
) and (
1.11
) it is easy to express
u
in terms of the solutions to the linear equation:

\displaystyle u=-({{\partial}}_{z}{\bf q},{\partial}_{\bar{z}}{\bf q}).

(1.12)

1.5
Novikov–Veselov hierarchy

In the next section we show that the Novikov–Veselov (NV) hierarchy is a symmetry of the
O(N)
model.
Recall, that NV hierarchy
[
30
,
31
]
is the system of compatible linear equations

\displaystyle H\psi:=(-\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}+u)\psi=0,

(1.13)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t_{n}}-L_{n})\psi=0,

(1.14)

with

\displaystyle L_{n}={\rm L}_{n}(z,{\bar{z}},{{\partial}}_{z})={\partial}_{z}^{2n+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}w_{i,n}(z,\bar{z})\partial_{z}^{i},

differential operator in the variable
z
with
(z,{\bar{z}})
-dependent coefficients.
The compatibility condition of the equations (
1.13
), (
1.14
) is the so-called Manakov’s triple equation

\displaystyle{{\partial}}_{t_{n}}H=[L_{n},H]+B_{n}H,

(1.15)

where
B_{n}={\rm B}_{n}(z,{\bar{z}},{\partial}_{z})
also a differential operator in the variable
z
with
(z,{\bar{z}})
-dependent coefficients.

Remark 1.7
.

The complete set of times in the NV hierarchy contains twice as many variables:

(t,\bar{t}):=(t_{1},\bar{t}_{1},t_{2},\bar{t}_{2},\dots)
. The equation defining the evolution under the times
\bar{t}_{n}
have the Manakov triple form with
(L_{n},B_{n})
replaced by the differential operators

{\bar{L}}_{n}={\bar{\rm L}}_{n}(z,{\bar{z}},{{\partial}}_{\bar{z}}),\qquad{\bar{B}}_{n}={\bar{\rm B}}_{n}(z,{\bar{z}},{{\partial}}_{\bar{z}})

in the variable
\bar{z}
.

To prove that the
NV hierarchy is the hierarchy of symmetries of the
O(N)
sigma model
means to prove the constraint (
1.11
) remains invariant along the
t_{n}
and
{\bar{t}}_{m}
evolution,
given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t_{n}}q^{i}=L_{n}q^{i},\qquad\partial_{{\bar{t}}_{m}}q^{i}={\bar{L}}_{m}q^{i}.

As we shall see soon, this
is highly nontrivial, yet purely local statement, i.e., not relying on any assumptions on the global behaviour of solutions.

The Manakov triple-type equations assume the Lax form upon a restriction to the space of zero modes of the operator
H
, i.e., onto the space of solutions of equations (
1.13
). Roughly speaking, they preserve the
spectral data
associated with
zero-eigenlevel
of the operator
H
. For operators
H
with doubly periodic potential
u
, the latter means that the flows defined by the equation (
1.15
) preserve the Bloch–Floquet set
C_{u}
of the operator
H
: the set of pairs
p:=(w_{x},w_{y})\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
for which there exists a double Bloch solution

{\psi}_{p}(z,{\bar{z}})={\psi}(z,{\bar{z}};p)
of the equation (
1.13
):

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}^{*}{\psi}_{p}=w_{\alpha}(p){\psi}_{p},\qquad{\alpha}=x,y.

(1.16)

In
[
19
]
it was shown that for a generic smooth periodic potential the locus
C_{u}
is a smooth Riemann surface of infinite genus. Moreover, it was shown that the
algebraically-integrable potentials
are dense in the space of all smooth periodic potentials. The latter are the potentials for which the normalization
{{\mathcal{C}}}_{u}
of
C_{u}
called the
Fermi-curve
is of finite genus. For such potentials
{{\mathcal{C}}}_{u}
is compactified by two smooth infinity points
P_{\pm}
.

Another characterization of algebraically integrable potentials similar to the one that defines the famous finite-gap potentials of the one-dimensional Schrödinger operator is as follows: they are such potentials, that among the flows given by the equations (
1.15
), only a
finite number
are linearly independent.

The
t_{n}
derivative of (
1.10
) with
u
given by (
1.12
) gives that the vector
{\bf v}_{n}=L_{n}{\bf q}
satisfies the linearized equation:

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{D}}{\bf v}_{n}=0,

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{D}}=\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}+\big({\bf q}\otimes(\partial_{\bar{z}}{\bf q})^{t}\big)\partial_{z}+\big({\bf q}\otimes(\partial_{z}{\bf q})^{t}\big)\partial_{\bar{z}}+({{\partial}}_{z}{\bf q},{{\partial}}_{\bar{z}}{\bf q}).

(1.17)

The elliptic operator (
1.17
) is defined on
T^{2}
, i.e., its coefficients are double periodic. Therefore
\operatorname{ker}({\mathfrak{D}})
is finite dimensional. Hence, for any solution of the elliptic double periodic
O(N)
sigma model the corresponding potential
u
(
1.12
) is algebraically integrable, i.e., the associated Fermi curve
{\mathcal{C}}_{u}
is of finite genus.

Remark 1.8
.

That result is an analog of the above mentioned key result in
[
13
]
.

Since it was proved in
[
22
]
that a smooth
irreducible
Fermi curve of finite genus gives a solution to the periodic
O(N)
sigma model if and only if there is a certain meromorphic function on it, it is tempting to say that a periodic
O(N)
sigma model is
algebraically integrable
.
Unfortunately, this is only partially true. The periodicity constraints that such a curve should satisfy are
given in terms of periods of meromorphic differentials on the curve. They are transcendental and hard to control.

In Section
5
we extend our construction to the case of
reducible
Fermi curves. It gives
branched conformal maps of two-torus to the real
even
dimensional spheres
M=S^{2n}
, i.e., the periodic solutions of the two-dimensional
O(2n+1)
sigma model. It turns out that for the reducible Fermi curve the periodicity constraints admit an explicit solution: the irreducible components are the spectral curves of the elliptic Calogero–Moser system corresponding to its
turning points
. We recall these notions in what follows.

Remark 1.9
.

It should be emphasized that the conformal maps to
S^{2}
are given by our construction with
nontrivial
reducible Fermi curves. As it was mentioned above for such maps the spectral curves arising in the framework of the zero-curvature representation are trivial. Therefore, in general these two types of the spectral curves are different. The question: are they interconnected in some special cases seems very interesting but at the moment is open.

2
Novikov–Veselov hierarchy

The NV hierarchy in its original form (
1.15
) is a system of equations on the potential
u
of the Schrödinger operator
H
and coefficients of operators
L_{n}
. These are well-defined equations in the sense that the number of equations is equal to the number of unknowns. Recently, their Sato-type representation was proposed in
[
8
]
. Below, following
the work of
[
21
]
, we present the hierarchy
in yet another form, the most suitable for our purposes. Namely, as a system of commuting flows on the space of special wave solutions of the Schrödinger equation.

Remark 2.1
.

Throughout this section
z
and
\bar{z}
are independent variables.

2.1
The phase space

First recall that a wave (a.k.a. formal Baker–Akhiezer) solution of the Schrödinger equation (
1.13
) is a formal solution to (
1.13
) of the form

\displaystyle{\psi}(z,{\bar{z}};k)={\rm e}^{kz}\left(1+\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}{\xi}_{s}(z,\bar{z})k^{-s}\right).

(2.1)

Substituting (
2.1
) into (
1.13
) gives a system of equations

\displaystyle{\partial}_{\bar{z}}\xi_{s+1}=H{\xi}_{s}\equiv-{{\partial}}_{z}{{\partial}}_{\bar{z}}\xi_{s}+u\xi_{s},\qquad s=0,1,\dots,

(2.2)

which recurrently determine all the
\xi_{s}
uniquely once the initial conditions
\chi_{s}(z)=\xi_{s}(z,0)
are given.

Thus the space
{\bf P}
of pairs
(H,{\psi})
, where
H=-{{\partial}}_{z}{{\partial}}_{\bar{z}}+u
and
{\psi}
is a formal wave solution is identified with the space:

\displaystyle{\bf P}\simeq\{(u(z,{\bar{z}}),{\chi}_{1}(z),{\chi}_{2}(z),\dots,{\chi}_{n}(z),\dots)\},

(2.3)

i.e., with the space of
a function of two variables, and a sequence of functions of one variable
.

For any formal series
\psi
of the form (
2.1
) define the
dual
formal series

\displaystyle\psi^{*}(z,\bar{z},k)={\rm e}^{-kz}\left(1+\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\xi_{s}^{*}(z,\bar{z})k^{-s}\right)

(2.4)

via the equations

\displaystyle\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(\psi^{*}\partial_{z}^{s}\psi)\frac{{\rm d}k}{k}=-\delta_{s,0},\qquad s=0,1,\dots.

(2.5)

Substitution of (
2.1
) and (
2.4
) into the equation (
2.5
) gives the equations
recurrently defining
\xi_{s}^{*}
in terms of the coefficients of
\psi
:

\displaystyle\xi_{1}^{*}+\xi_{1}=0,\qquad\xi_{2}^{*}+\xi_{2}+\xi_{1}\xi_{1}^{*}+2{{\partial}}_{z}\xi_{1}=0,\qquad\dots.

(2.6)

Lemma 2.2
.

If
\psi
is a formal BA solution of the Schrödinger equation (
1.13
) then the dual BA function solves the same equation

\displaystyle H{\psi}^{*}=0.

Proof.

Taking the
\bar{z}
derivative of the equation (
2.5
) and using the equation (
1.13
) we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{res}_{\infty}\big(({\partial}_{\bar{z}}{\psi}^{*}){{\partial}}_{z}^{s}\psi\big)\frac{{\rm d}k}{k}={{\partial}}_{z}^{s-1}u,\qquad s>0.

(2.7)

Then taking the
z
derivative of the equation (
2.7
) we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{res}_{\infty}\big(({{\partial}}_{z}{\partial}_{\bar{z}}{\psi}^{*}){{\partial}}_{z}^{s}\psi\big)\frac{{\rm d}k}{k}=0,\qquad s>0.

(2.8)

The homogeneous equations (
2.8
) imply that
{{\partial}}_{z}{\partial}_{\bar{z}}\psi^{*}
is equal to
\psi^{*}
up to a
k
-independent factor, i.e.,
{{\partial}}_{z}{\partial}_{\bar{z}}\psi^{*}={\tilde{u}}(z,\bar{z})\psi^{*}
. Comparing the leading terms and using the first equations in (
2.2
) and (
2.5
) we get
{\tilde{u}}=-{\partial}_{\bar{z}}{\xi}_{1}^{*}={\partial}_{\bar{z}}\xi_{1}=u
. The lemma is proved.
∎

Definition 2.3
.

A
reflected
formal BA solution of equation (
1.13
)
{\psi}^{\sigma}
is defined by

\displaystyle\psi^{\sigma}(z,\bar{z};k):=\psi(z,\bar{z};-k).

Lemma
2.2
can be thus formulated as follows: the map

\displaystyle s\colon\ {\psi}\to\left({\psi}^{*}\right)^{\sigma}

is an involution of
\bf P
,
s^{2}={\rm id}
.

Definition 2.4
.

A
self-dual
formal BA solution of equation (
1.13
) is fixed point of
s
, i.e., a wave solution that satisfies the constraint

\displaystyle\psi^{\sigma}(z,\bar{z};k):=\psi(z,\bar{z};-k)=\psi^{*}(z,\bar{z};k),

(2.9)

equivalently

\displaystyle\xi_{s}^{*}=(-1)^{s}\xi_{s}

for all
s\geq 1
.

Our next goal is to show that
the space
{\mathcal{P}}
of pairs
\{(H,\psi)\}
, where
H
is a two-dimensional Schrödinger operator and
\psi
its self-dual wave solution, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}:={\bf P}^{s}=\big\{H,\psi\,|\,H\psi=0,\,\psi^{*}=\psi^{\sigma}\big\}

can be identified with the space

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}\simeq\{(u(z,{\bar{z}}),{\chi}_{1}(z),{\chi}_{3}(z),\dots,{\chi}_{2n-1}(z),\dots)\},

where
\chi_{2n+1}(z)
are the part of the data in (
2.3
) with odd indices.

Observe that the first equation in (
2.6
) is satisfied with
\xi_{1}^{*}=-\xi_{1}
, the second equation determines
\xi_{2}=\xi_{2}^{*}
in terms of
\xi_{1}
, then the third equation is automatically satisfied by
\xi_{3}^{*}=-\xi_{3}
. It turns out that the same remains true for all odd
s
. Namely,

Lemma 2.5
.

Assume the equations

\displaystyle\operatorname{res}_{\infty}\big(\psi^{\sigma}\partial_{z}^{s}\psi\big)\frac{{\rm d}k}{k}=-\delta_{s,0},\qquad s=0,1,\dots,2n,

(2.10)

are satisfied. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{res}_{\infty}\big(\psi^{\sigma}\partial_{z}^{2n+1}\psi\big)\frac{{\rm d}k}{k}=0

(2.11)

holds.

Proof.

Using the identity

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\big((\partial_{z}^{i}\psi^{\sigma})\partial_{z}^{j}\psi\big)=\big((\partial_{z}^{i+1}\psi^{\sigma})\partial_{z}^{j}\psi\big)+\big((\partial_{z}^{i}\psi^{\sigma})\partial_{z}^{j+1}\psi\big)

(2.12)

it is easy to show by induction in
i
that the equations (
2.10
) imply

\displaystyle\operatorname{res}_{\infty}\big(\big(\partial_{z}^{i}\psi^{\sigma}\big)\partial_{z}^{j}\psi\big)\frac{{\rm d}k}{k}=0,\qquad 0<i+j\leq 2n.

(2.13)

From (
2.13
) with
i=j=n
and the identity
\operatorname{res}_{\infty}f(k){\rm d}k=-\operatorname{res}_{\infty}f(-k){\rm d}k
, we get

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{z}\left(\operatorname{res}_{\infty}\big(\big(\partial_{z}^{n}\psi^{\sigma}\big)\partial_{z}^{n}\psi\big)\frac{{\rm d}k}{k}\right)=2\operatorname{res}_{\infty}\big(\big(\partial_{z}^{n}\psi^{\sigma}\big)\partial_{z}^{n+1}\psi\big)\frac{{\rm d}k}{k}.

(2.14)

Then, by induction in
i
using (
2.12
) we get that (
2.14
) implies (
2.11
).
The lemma is proved.
∎

Now we are going to present the constraint (
2.9
) defining the self-dual wave solution in another, equivalent, form. For that first recall, that the formal adjoint to
w\cdot{{\partial}}_{z}^{i}
is the operator

\big(w\cdot{{\partial}}_{z}^{i}\big)^{*}=(-{{\partial}}_{z})^{i}\cdot w,

where
w
stands for the operator of multiplication by the function
w(z,{\bar{z}})
. Below we will often use the notion of the left action of an operator which is identical to
the formal adjoint action, i.e., the identity

\displaystyle(f{\mathcal{D}}):={\mathcal{D}}^{*}f.

(2.15)

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\Phi
denote the so-called
wave operator

\displaystyle\Phi=1+\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\xi_{s}(z,\bar{z})\partial_{z}^{-s}

corresponding to the formal BA solution (
2.1
) of the equation (
1.13
). Then the constraint (
2.9
) is equivalent to the operator equation

\displaystyle\Phi^{*}=\partial_{z}\cdot\Phi^{-1}\cdot\partial_{z}^{-1},

(2.16)

where
\Phi^{*}
is the formal adjoint to
\Phi
,

{\Phi}^{*}=1+\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\big({-}{{\partial}}_{z})^{-s}\cdot{\xi}_{s}(z,{\bar{z}}\big).

Proof.

By definition of
\Phi
we have

\displaystyle\psi=\Phi{\rm e}^{kz},\qquad\psi^{\sigma}=\Phi{\rm e}^{-kz}.

Therefore, for the proof of (
2.16
) it is enough to show that for the function

\displaystyle\psi^{*}=\big({\rm e}^{-kz}\partial_{z}\cdot\Phi^{-1}\cdot{\partial}^{-1}_{z}\big)

the equations (
2.5
) hold. The latter is an easy corollary of the identity

\displaystyle\operatorname{res}_{\infty}\big({\rm e}^{-kz}{\mathcal{D}}_{1}\big)\big({\mathcal{D}}_{2}{\rm e}^{kz}\big){\rm d}k=-\operatorname{res}_{\partial}({\mathcal{D}}_{2}{\mathcal{D}}_{1})

(2.17)

valid for any pseudo-differential operators
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\operatorname{res}_{\infty}\big({\rm e}^{-kz}\partial_{z}\cdot\Phi^{-1}\cdot\partial_{z}^{-1}\big)\big(\partial_{z}^{s}\cdot\Phi{\rm e}^{kz}\big)\frac{{\rm d}k}{k}=-\operatorname{res}_{\partial}\big(\partial_{z}^{s}\cdot\Phi\cdot\Phi^{-1}\cdot\partial_{z}^{-1}\big)=-\operatorname{res}_{\partial}\partial_{z}^{s-1}.

The lemma is proved.
∎

Corrolary 2.7
.

Let
\psi
be a self-dual formal BA function then the pseudo-differential operator

\displaystyle{{\mathcal{L}}}={{\partial}}_{z}+\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}v_{s}(z,\bar{z}){{\partial}}_{z}^{-s}

such that the equation

\displaystyle{{\mathcal{L}}}{\psi}=k\psi

(2.18)

holds satisfy the equation

\displaystyle{{\mathcal{L}}}^{*}=-{{\partial}}_{z}{{\mathcal{L}}}{{\partial}}_{z}^{-1}.

(2.19)

To prove (
2.19
) note, that
{{\mathcal{L}}}={\Phi}\cdot{{\partial}}_{z}\cdot\Phi^{-1}
, then use (
2.16
).

Remark 2.8
.

The corollary
2.7
identifies the space of operators
{\mathcal{L}}
corresponding to the
self-dual formal BA solution of the Schrödinger equation (at fixed
\bar{z}
) with the phase space of the so-called BKP hierarchy.

2.2
The flows

Let us denote the
strictly
positive differential part of the pseudo-differential operator
{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}
by

{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}_{+}
, i.e., if

{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}=\sum_{i=-n}^{\infty}F_{n}^{(i)}\partial_{z}^{-i},

then

\displaystyle{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}_{+}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{n}^{(-i)}\partial_{z}^{i},\qquad{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}_{-}={{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}-{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}_{+}=F_{n}^{(0)}+F_{n}^{(1)}{{\partial}}_{z}^{-1}+O\big({{\partial}}_{z}^{-2}\big).

Remark 2.9
.

Note that this definition differs from the one used in the KP theory. There
the “
+
” subscript denotes the non-negative part of a pseudo-differential operator.

By definition of the residue of a pseudo-differential operator, the first leading coefficients
of
{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}_{-}
are

\displaystyle F_{n}^{(0)}={\operatorname{res}}_{{\partial}}\big({{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}{{\partial}}_{z}^{-1}\big),\qquad F_{n}^{(1)}={\operatorname{res}}_{\partial}\ {{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}.

(2.20)

From the equation (
2.19
) and the relation

{\operatorname{res}}_{\partial}D=-{\operatorname{res}}_{\partial}D^{*}
it follows that

\displaystyle F_{2n+1}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{res}_{\partial}\big({{\mathcal{L}}}^{2n+1}{{\partial}}_{z}^{-1}\big)^{*}=\operatorname{res}_{\partial}\big(\partial_{z}^{-1}\cdot\big({{\mathcal{L}}}^{2n+1}\big)^{*}\big)

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{res}_{{\partial}}\big({{\mathcal{L}}}^{2n+1}{{\partial}}_{z}^{-1}\big)=-F_{2n+1}^{(0)}=0.

(2.21)

The latter implies

\displaystyle\big({{\mathcal{L}}}^{2n+1}_{+}\big)^{*}={{\partial}}_{z}\cdot{{\mathcal{L}}}^{2n+1}_{+}\cdot{{\partial}}_{z}^{-1}.

(2.22)

In what follows we need the following statement (see
[
21
, Lemma 4.3]
).

Lemma 2.10
.

The equation

\displaystyle H{{\mathcal{L}}}^{2n+1}_{+}=-F_{2n+1,\bar{z}}^{(1)}+B_{2n+1}H,

(2.23)

where
B_{2n+1}
is a pseudo-differential operator in the variable
z
, holds.

Proof.

Each operator
{\mathcal{D}}
of the form
{\mathcal{D}}=\sum_{i=N}^{\infty}(a+b\partial_{\bar{z}})\partial_{z}^{-i}

can be uniquely represented in the form
{\mathcal{D}}=D_{1}+D_{2}H
,
where
D_{1,2}
are pseudo-differential operators in the variable
z
.
Consider such a representation for the operator
H{{\mathcal{L}}}^{m}=D_{1}+D_{2}H
. From the definition
of
{\mathcal{L}}
it follows that
H{{\mathcal{L}}}^{m}\psi=0
. That implies
D_{1}=0
or the equation

\displaystyle H{{\mathcal{L}}}^{m}=D_{2}H.

(2.24)

We have the identity

\displaystyle\big[\partial_{\bar{z}}\partial_{z}-u,{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}_{+}\big]={{\mathcal{L}}}_{+,z,\bar{z}}^{n}+{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}_{+,\bar{z}}\partial_{z}-\big[u,{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}_{+}\big]+{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}_{+,z}\partial_{z}^{-1}\cdot u-{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}_{+,z}\cdot\partial_{z}^{-1}\cdot H.

The first three terms are differential operators in the
z
variable. By definition of

{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}_{+}
the fourth term is also a differential operator.
Therefore, the pseudo-differential operator
D_{n,1}
in the decomposition

H{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}_{+}=D_{n,1}+B_{n}H
is in fact a
differential
operator.

In the same way we get the equation

\displaystyle H{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}_{-}={\tilde{D}}_{n,1}+{\tilde{B}}_{2n}H,

where

\displaystyle\tilde{D}_{n,1}={{\mathcal{L}}}_{-,z,\bar{z}}^{n}+{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}_{-,\bar{z}}\partial_{z}-\big[u,{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}_{-}\big]+{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}_{-,z}\partial_{z}^{-1}\cdot u.

(2.25)

From the equation (
2.21
) it follows that the operator
\tilde{D}_{2n+1,1}
is a pseudo-differential
operator of order not greater than
0
.
The equation (
2.24
) implies
H{{\mathcal{L}}}_{+}^{n}=-H{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}_{-}+D_{2}H
. Hence,

D_{2n+1,1}=-\tilde{D}_{2n+1,1}
is a differential operator of the order
0
, i.e., it is an operator of multiplication by a function. This function is easily found from
the leading coefficient of the right-hand side of the equation (
2.25
).
Direct computations give the equation (
2.23
). The lemma is proved.
∎

Now we are ready to define NV hierarchy explicitly.

Theorem 2.11
.

The equations

\displaystyle{{\partial}}_{t_{n}}u={{\partial}}_{\bar{z}}F_{2n+1}^{(1)},

(2.26)

\displaystyle{{\partial}}_{t_{n}}\psi=\big({{{\mathcal{L}}}}^{2n+1}_{+}-k^{2n+1}\big)\psi

(2.27)

define a family of commuting flows on the space
{\mathcal{P}}
of self-dual formal BA solutions of the
Schrödinger operators.

Proof.

From (
2.18
) and definition of the operator
{{\mathcal{L}}}^{2n+1}_{+}
it follows that

\displaystyle\big({{\mathcal{L}}}^{2n+1}_{+}-k^{2n+1}\big)\psi=-{{\mathcal{L}}}^{2n+1}_{-}\psi.

The equation (
2.21
) implies that the operator
{{\mathcal{L}}}^{2n+1}_{-}
is of order at most
-1
. Hence,
the equation (
2.27
) is equivalent to a well-defined system of equations on the coefficients
\xi_{s}
of the formal BA function
\psi
.

In turn, the equation (
2.23
) implies that the equation (
2.26
) is equivalent to the operator equation (
1.15
) with
L_{n}={{\mathcal{L}}}_{+}^{2n+1}
, which is a compatibility condition for the Schrödinger equation and the equation (
2.27
). Hence, the vector field defined by the right-hand sides of (
2.26
) and (
2.27
) is tangent to the space of formal Baker–Akhiezer solutions of the Schrödinger equation. Let us show that it is tangent to the space
{\mathcal{P}}
of selfdual solutions, i.e., preserve constraint (
2.9
).

From the equation (
2.27
) and the same equation with
k
replaced by
-k
it follows that

\displaystyle\partial_{t_{n}}\big({\psi}^{\sigma}\big({{\partial}}_{z}^{s}{\psi}\big)\big)={\psi}^{\sigma}\big({{\partial}}_{z}^{s}\cdot{{\mathcal{L}}}_{+}^{2n+1}{\psi}\big)+\big(\big({{\mathcal{L}}}_{+}^{2n+1}{\psi}^{\sigma}\big){{\partial}}_{z}^{s}{\psi}\big).

(2.28)

It is easy to show that in conjunction with the self-duality
\psi^{*}=\psi^{\sigma}
the equation (
2.5
) implies

\displaystyle{\operatorname{res}}_{\infty}\left(\big({{\partial}}_{z}^{i}{\psi}^{\sigma}\big)\big({{\partial}}_{z}^{j}{\psi}\big)\frac{{\rm d}k}{k}\right)=0,\qquad i+j>0.

The coefficients of the operator
{{\mathcal{L}}}_{+}^{2n+1}
are
k
-independent. Therefore, from the equations (
2.28
) and (
2.21
) we deduce

\displaystyle\partial_{t_{n}}{\operatorname{res}}_{\infty}\left(\psi^{\sigma}\big({{\partial}}_{z}^{s}{\psi}\big)\frac{{\rm d}k}{k}\right)=0,\qquad s\geq 0,

i.e., that
\partial_{t_{n}}
is tangent to
{\mathcal{P}}
.

The commutativity of NV flows is easy to demonstrate. Indeed, (
2.27
) implies

\displaystyle\big[{{\partial}}_{t_{n}}-{{\mathcal{L}}}^{2n+1}_{+},{{\partial}}_{t_{m}}-{{\mathcal{L}}}^{2m+1}_{+}\big]\psi=0.

(2.29)

The operator in the left-hand side of the equation (
2.29
) is a differential operator in the variable
z
. From the very form of
\psi
it follows that if such an operator annihilates it, then it itself must be zero. The theorem is proved.
∎

2.3
Integrals

A direct corollary of the Schrödinger equation for a self-dual BA function is the equation

\displaystyle{{\partial}}_{\bar{z}}\big({\psi}^{\sigma}{\psi}_{z}-{\psi}^{\sigma}_{z}{\psi}\big)+{{\partial}}_{z}\big({\psi}^{\sigma}{\psi}_{\bar{z}}-{\psi}^{\sigma}_{\bar{z}}{\psi}\big)=0,

which is nothing but the conservation

\displaystyle{\rm d}_{\Sigma}*j=0

(2.30)

of the current

\displaystyle j={\psi}^{\sigma}{\rm d}_{\Sigma}{\psi}-{\psi}{\rm d}_{\Sigma}{\psi}^{\sigma}.

(2.31)

In other words the
1
-form
j_{z}{\rm d}z-j_{\bar{z}}{\rm d}\bar{z}
, where

\displaystyle j_{z}:={\psi}^{\sigma}{{\partial}}_{z}\psi-({{\partial}}_{z}\psi^{\sigma})\psi=2\left(k+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}J_{n}k^{-2n-1}\right),

(2.32)

\displaystyle j_{\bar{z}}:={\psi}^{\sigma}{{\partial}}_{\bar{z}}\psi-({{\partial}}_{\bar{z}}{\psi}^{\sigma})\psi=2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\bar{J}}_{n}k^{-2n-1}

(2.33)

is closed.
The current (
2.31
) is the Noether current corresponding to the maximal commutative
subalgebra of an infinite orthogonal group, acting on the formal BA functions by linear transformations,
preserving the
\operatorname{res}_{\infty}{\rm d}k/k\psi^{\sigma}{\psi}
pairing, namely,

J(k_{1},k_{2})={\psi}^{\sigma}(k_{1}){\rm d}_{\Sigma}{\psi}(k_{2})-{\rm d}_{\Sigma}{\psi}^{\sigma}(k_{1}){\psi}(k_{2})=-J(-k_{2},-k_{1})

obeys

{\rm d}_{\Sigma}\star J(k_{1},k_{2})=0,\qquad{\rm d}_{\Sigma}J(k_{1},k_{2})+\operatorname{res}_{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}k}{k}J(k_{1},k)\wedge J(k,k_{2})=0.

By definition (
2.15
) of the left action of a differential operator
{\mathcal{D}}
we have the identity

\displaystyle(h{\mathcal{D}})f=h({\mathcal{D}}f)+\sum_{i=1}^{d-i}\partial_{z}^{i}\big(h\big({\mathcal{D}}^{(i)}f\big)\big),

where
d
is the order of
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{(i)}
is differential operator of degree
d-i
whose coefficients are explicit differential polynomials in the coefficients of the operator
{\mathcal{D}}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle j_{z}=\big({\rm e}^{-kz}\partial_{z}\Phi^{-1}\partial_{z}^{-1}\big)\big(\partial_{z}\Phi{\rm e}^{kz}\big)+\big({\rm e}^{-kz}\partial_{z}\Phi^{-1}\big)\big(\Phi{\rm e}^{kz}\big)=2k+\partial_{z}Q^{(1)},

(2.34)

where the coefficients of the series

\displaystyle Q^{(1)}=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}Q^{(1)}_{n}k^{-2s-1}

are universal polynomials in the coefficients of the self-dual BA function.

For further use, let us show that the coefficient
J_{n}
of the series (
2.32
) is equal to the function
F_{2n+1}^{(1)}
in the right-hand side of equation (
2.26
). Indeed, from (
2.16
), (
2.18
), (
2.17
) and (
2.21
) it follows that

\displaystyle 2J_{n}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{res}_{k}\big(\big(\psi^{\sigma}{{\mathcal{L}}}^{2n+1}\big)\psi_{z}-\psi_{z}^{\sigma}\big({{\mathcal{L}}}^{2n+1}\psi\big)\big)k^{-1}{\rm d}k

\displaystyle=\operatorname{res}_{\partial}\big({{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}+\partial_{z}{{\mathcal{L}}}^{n}\partial_{z}^{-1}\big)=2F_{2n+1}^{(1)}.

(2.35)

We conclude this section by proving that
J_{n}
are densities of the NV hierarchy integrals.

Lemma 2.12
.

The equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t_{n}}J_{n}=\partial_{z}Q_{n},

where
Q_{n}
are explicit differential polynomials in the coefficients
\xi_{s}
of the self-dual BA solution of the Schrödinger equation, hold.

Proof.

The equation (
2.27
) and the same equation for
\psi^{\sigma}
with
k
replaced by
-k
imply

\displaystyle\partial_{t_{n}}J=\psi^{\sigma}\big(\partial_{z}\big({{\mathcal{L}}}_{+}^{2n+1}\psi\big)\big)+\big(\psi^{\sigma}\big({{\mathcal{L}}}_{+}^{2n+1}\big)^{*}\big)(\partial_{z}\psi)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\partial_{t_{n}} J =}{}+\big(\psi^{\sigma}\big({{\mathcal{L}}}_{+}^{2n+1}\big)^{*}\partial_{z}\big)\psi+\big(\psi^{\sigma}\partial_{z}\big)\big({{\mathcal{L}}}^{2n+1}\psi\big).

Therefore, from (
2.35
) and (
2.22
) it follows that
\partial_{t_{n}}J=\partial_{z}Q_{n}
where the coefficients of
Q_{n}
are explicit polynomials in the coefficients
\xi_{s}
of the self-dual BA solution
\psi
. The lemma is proved.
∎

3
The NV flows as symmetries of
\boldsymbol{O(N)}
model

Our next goal is to show that the NV hierarchy defines a set of commuting symmetries of the
O(N)
sigma model.

First observe that if the equations

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{z}^{j}{\bf q},\partial_{z}^{j}{\bf q}\big)=0,\qquad 0<j<m,

(3.1)

hold then (
1.10
) implies
{\partial}_{\bar{z}}\big(\partial_{z}^{m}{\bf q},\partial_{z}^{m}{\bf q}\big)=0
.

We will call the solutions of the
O(N)
model satisfying (
3.1
)
w_{m}
-harmonic
. Notice, that for
m>1
they are conformal. In this way the condition of being
w_{m}
-harmonic defines a filtration on the space of conformal maps.

The equations of motion (
1.10
), (
1.12
) of the model are invariant under a conformal change of variables
z\mapsto f(z),{\bar{z}}\mapsto{\bar{f}}({\bar{z}})
. Hence if for a
w_{m}
-harmonic map
\big(\partial_{z}^{m}{\bf q}
,
\partial_{z}^{m}{\bf q}\big)\neq 0
, then without loss of generality we may assume that the equation

\displaystyle\big({\partial}_{z}^{m}{\bf q},{\partial}_{z}^{m}{\bf q}\big)=(-1)^{m+1}

(3.2)

holds.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
{\bf q}(z,\bar{z})
be a complex solution of the
O(N)
sigma model obeying (
3.1
), (
3.2
). Then there exists a unique up to multiplication by
(z,\bar{z})
-independent factor
\rho(k)
, such that

\displaystyle\psi\longmapsto\psi\rho(k),\qquad\rho(k)=\exp\left(\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\rho_{s}k^{-2s+1}\right)

(3.3)

is a self-dual formal BA solution
\psi
of the Schrödinger equation with the potential
u
given by (
1.12
) such that the constraint (
1.11
) is invariant under the commuting flows

\displaystyle\partial_{t_{n}}{\bf q}={{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{+}^{2n+1}{\bf q}

(3.4)

with
{\mathcal{L}}
defined by (
2.18
).

Remark 3.2
.

Note, that the operator
{\mathcal{L}}
defined by (
2.18
) is invariant under the transformation (
3.3
). Hence, the right-hand side is uniquely defined by
\bf q
.

Proof.

Consider the
moments
defined by the formula

\displaystyle T_{i}(z,{\bar{z}}):=\big({\bf q},{{\partial}}_{z}^{i}{\bf q}\big),\qquad i=0,1,\dots.

The original constraint (
1.11
) translates to
T_{0}=1
. By assumptions (
3.1
) (
3.2
), the next moments equal
T_{i}=0
,
1<i<2m

4.3

The reconstruction of an algebraically integrable potential

and an admissible divisor

It follows from the non-degeneracy of the imaginary part of the matrix of

{\Gamma}
with a fixed first jet

\Gamma

\Gamma

G

equations (

4g

Let

of curves

Note that in

5.2.1

It turns out that in the case of reducible Fermi curves the constraints (

Theorem 5.6

(
[
12
]
)
.

The equations (

is the normalization of the spectral curve

Recall that the

\displaystyle{{\mathcal{X}}}_{N}=T^{*}\big(E^{N}\backslash{\rm diag}\big)\big\{({\rho}_{i},z_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\,|\,{\rho}_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}},\,z_{i}\in E,\,z_{i}\neq z_{j},\,i\neq j\big\},

\displaystyle H_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\rho}_{i}^{2}+{\nu}^{2}\sum_{i<j}{\wp}(z_{i}-z_{j}).

This is an algebraic integrable system with the Lax operator
[
17

\displaystyle L({\alpha})=\left\|{\rho}_{i}{\delta}_{ij}+{\nu}\frac{{\sigma}({\alpha}+z_{i}-z_{j})}{{\sigma}(z_{i}-z_{j}){\sigma}({\alpha})}(1-{\delta}_{ij})\right\|_{i,j=1}^{N}.

The flows of eCM linearize on the Jacobian of the spectral curve

\displaystyle C_{N}\subset M=\overline{{\mathbb{C}}\times(E\backslash\{{\alpha}=0\})},

\displaystyle C_{N}\colon\ \operatorname{Det}(k-L({\alpha}))=k^{N}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}k^{N-j}c_{j}({\alpha})=0.

The coefficients

of the characteristic polynomial (

with poles at

of order

,

, etc.

In what follows the value of
\nu
is immaterial, so we set it to be equal to

.
As shown in
[
17
]
, near

the polynomial

admits a factorization of the form

\displaystyle R(k,{\alpha})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\big(k+a_{i}\alpha^{-1}+h_{i}+O(\alpha)\big),

with

and

for

. This implies that the closure of the affine curve defined by (

({\infty},0)
, at which

contained in the remaining

N

on the second blowup.

Due to the degenerate nature of the residue of

at

\displaystyle R(k,\alpha):=f(k+\zeta(\alpha),\alpha),

\displaystyle f(p,\alpha)=\frac{1}{\sigma(\alpha)}\ \sigma\left(\alpha+\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\right)H(p)=\frac{1}{\sigma(\alpha)}\ \sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{1}{n!}\partial_{\alpha}^{n}\sigma(\alpha)\frac{\partial^{n}H}{\partial p^{n}},

\displaystyle H(p)=p^{N}+\sum_{l=1}^{N}I_{l}p^{N-l},

whose coefficients

For generic

By this definition we always have
