1.
Introduction & main result

In this paper, we address the so-called fractional Calderón problem (see
[
GSU20
]
) through fractional Schrödinger equation and study the global identifiability of the potential based on the boundary response. So far the fractional Calderón problem remains studied (see
[
GSU20
,
GRSU20
,
GLX17
]
) based on the data measured in the exterior of the domain. In
[
GSU20
]
, it has been shown that one can recover the potential
q
from the exterior measurements of the non-local Cauchy data
\left(v|_{W},(-\Delta)^{a}v|_{\widetilde{W}}\right)
, where
W,\widetilde{W}
be the non-empty open subsets of
\Omega_{e}:=\Omega_{e}
, and
v
solves

\begin{cases}\left((-\Delta)^{a}+q\right)v=0\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
supp\,v\subseteq\overline{\Omega}\cup\overline{W}\\
\overline{W}\cap\overline{\Omega}=\emptyset.\end{cases}

Here we would like to address the inverse problem through introducing the exterior to boundary response map, and based on that we look for a new global uniqueness result of recovering the potential.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

(n\geq 2
) be a smooth bounded domain, and
W\subset\Omega_{e}
be another smooth domain such that
\overline{W}\cap\overline{\Omega}=\emptyset
. Let us take
q\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
, and
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(W)
which is extended by
0
outside of
W
. Let
0<a<1
, and consider the fractional Schrödinger equation. Let
u\in H^{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the solution of

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\left((-\Delta)^{a}+q\right)u=0\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
u=f\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega_{e}\end{cases}

Let us define

\mathcal{E}_{a}(\overline{\Omega})=e^{+}d^{a}(x)C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})

where
e^{+}
denotes extension by zero from
\Omega
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
d
is a
C^{\infty}

function in
\overline{\Omega}
, positive in
\Omega
and satisfying
d(x)=dist(x,\partial\Omega)
near
\partial\Omega
.

It follows from
[
GSU20
, Section 6]
and
[
Gru15
]
, that
u\in\mathcal{E}_{a}(\overline{\Omega})
. That
\frac{u(x)}{d^{a}(x)}\Big|_{x\in\partial\Omega}
exists as a function in
C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
, see Subsection
2.3
.

Let us define the exterior to boundary response map:

(1.2)

\mathcal{A}_{q}:C^{\infty}_{c}(W)\to C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)\mbox{ defined by }\mathcal{A}_{q}(f)=\frac{u(x)}{d^{a}(x)}\Big|_{\partial\Omega}

where
u\in\mathcal{E}_{a}(\overline{\Omega})
solves (
1.1
).
It is a well-defined map. Later we explore its various properties of
\mathcal{A}_{q}
, see Subsection
2.3
.

In this article, we would like to see whether the map

q\mapsto\mathcal{A}_{q}\quad\mbox{ is injective or not.}

This is the inverse problem we study here.

Let us also introduce the partial boundary data problem.
Let
\Sigma\subset\partial\Omega
be a non-empty open set. We define the partial boundary response map

(1.3)

\mathcal{A}^{\Sigma}_{q}:C^{\infty}_{c}(W)\to C^{\infty}(\Sigma)\mbox{ defined by }\mathcal{A}^{\Sigma}_{q}(f)=\frac{u(x)}{d^{a}(x)}\Big|_{\Sigma}.

Here is our main results.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\partial\Omega
be a non-empty open subset. Suppose for two different
q^{1},q^{2}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
:

(1.4)

\mathcal{A}^{\Sigma}_{q^{1}}(f)=\mathcal{A}^{\Sigma}_{q^{2}}(f),\quad\forall f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(W)

Then it implies
q^{1}=q^{2}
in
\Omega
.

The Theorem
1.1
is a global uniqueness result in
the inverse problem for the fractional Schrödinger equation
with both partial exterior (
W\subset\Omega_{e}
) and boundary data (
\Sigma\subset\partial\Omega
).

As an application, we will now solve a Robin boundary valued local inverse problem. Let
\Omega
,
W
,
q\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
,
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(W)
are same as in Theorem
1.1
. Let
0<a<1
, and we consider the following Robin boundary value problem for the degenerate elliptic equation in the half space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}=\{(y,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
:

(1.5)

\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot\left(y^{1-2a}\nabla U(y,x)\right)=0\quad\mbox{in }(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\\[2.84526pt]
\partial_{y}U(0,x)+q(x)U(0,x)=0\quad\mbox{on }\Omega\\[2.84526pt]
U(0,x)=\begin{cases}f\quad\mbox{on }W\\[2.84526pt]
0\quad\mbox{on }\Omega_{e}\setminus\overline{W}\end{cases}\end{cases}

The above local problem is the Caffarelli-Silvestre extension
[

]
in the half space

of the nonlocal problem (

) on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It has a non-zero solution
U\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+},y^{1-2a})
. The trace

solves (
1.1
) where

be non-empty subset. We know from

]

Theorem 1.2
.

Let

are as in Theorem

The sole point is here we are measuring on

(W)

\times

2.
Preliminaries: Direct problem

We begin with a survey of preliminaries results.

2.1.
Fractional Laplacian and fractional Sobolev space

Let
0<a<1
and we recall the fractional Laplacian operator
(-\Delta)^{a}
defined over the space of Schwartz class functions
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
:

(2.1)

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad(-\Delta)^{a}u(x)=\mathscr{F}^{-1}\{\lvert{\xi}\rvert^{2a}\widehat{u}(\xi)\},\quad u\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

where
\widehat{\cdot}
and
\mathscr{F}^{-1}
denote the Fourier transform and its inverse respectively.

There are many equivalent definitions of the fractional Laplacian (see
[
Kwa17
]
). For instance, it is given by the principal value integral as (
0<a<1
)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{a}u(x)

\displaystyle=C_{n,a}\ \mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{\lvert{x-y}\rvert^{n+2a}}\,dy.

where
C_{n,a}=\frac{4^{a}\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}+a)}{\pi^{n/2}\Gamma(-a)}
.
Through out the paper,
\Gamma
stands for the usual Gamma function.

The fractional Laplacian extends as a bounded map:

(-\Delta)^{a}:\ H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\mapsto H^{s-2a}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

as
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
a\in(0,1)
. Here we recall that

H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\{u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\,|\,\langle\xi\rangle^{s}\widehat{u}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}

where
\langle\xi\rangle=(1+\lvert\xi\rvert^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denotes the space of tempered distributions in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let
\Omega
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that is either bounded with smooth boundary or equal to
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
.
r^{+}
stands for restriction from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\Omega
,
e^{+}

stands for extension by zero from

\Omega
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
r^{-}
and
e^{-}
are similar for
\Omega_{e}:=\Omega_{e}
. We define the following Sobolev spaces.

\displaystyle\overline{H}^{s}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=r^{+}H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

\displaystyle\dot{H}^{s}(\overline{\Omega})

\displaystyle:=\{u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\,|\,supp\,u\subset\overline{\Omega}\}.

The above Sobolev spaces equipped with the norm equipped with the norm

\|u\|_{\overline{H}^{s}(\Omega)}=\underset{v\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\,v|_{\Omega}=u}{inf}\,\|v\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

We recall

\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+},y^{1-2a})

\displaystyle=\{U:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\mbox{ measureable, }\,y^{\frac{1-2a}{2}}U\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+})\},

\displaystyle H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+},y^{1-2a})

\displaystyle=\{U\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+},y^{1-2a}),\,\,\nabla U\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+},y^{1-2a})\}

and the trace space of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+},y^{1-2a})
at
y=0
is
H^{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

2.2.
Exterior value problem

Probabilistically, the fractional Laplacian operator
(-\Delta)^{a}
represents the infinitesimal generator of a symmetric
2a
-stable Lévy process in the entire space.
Here we are interested in the restriction of
(-\Delta)^{a}
to a bounded domain
\Omega
.
For example, one can think of the homogeneous Dirichlet exterior value problem for the fractional Laplacian operator
\left(e.g.\,(-\Delta)^{a}v=g\right.

\left.\mbox{in }\Omega\mbox{ and }v=0\mbox{ in }\Omega_{e}\right)
which represents the infinitesimal generator of a symmetric
2a
-stable Lévy process for which particles are killed upon leaving the domain
\Omega
. See
[
App09
]
.

Existence, uniqueness & stability

Let
q\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, then for a given
h\in\widetilde{H}^{a}(\Omega)^{*}
and
f\in H^{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
there exist a solution (weak)
u\in H^{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
solving (see
[
GSU20
]
)

(2.2)

\begin{cases}((-\Delta)^{a}+q)u=h&\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
u=f&\mbox{ in }\Omega_{e}.\end{cases}

In addition, we have the
H^{a}
stability estimate as follows:

(2.3)

\|u\|_{H^{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C_{n,s}\left(\|h\|_{\widetilde{H}^{a}(\Omega)^{*}}+\|f\|_{H^{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right),

for some constant
C_{n,a}>0
independent of
h
and
f
.

Eigenvalue condition

Let
q\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
is such that,

((-\Delta)^{a}+q)w=0\mbox{ in }\Omega,\quad w=0\mbox{ in }\Omega_{e}\quad\mbox{ has only $w=0$ solution}.

Then the above problem (
2.2
) has a unique solution.

Regularity

We denote
\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(\overline{\Omega})
equals to
e_{\Omega}d^{\mu}C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
for
\mathscr{R}(\mu)>-1
, where
d(x)
is a smooth positive extension into
\Omega
of
dist(x,\partial\Omega)
near
\partial\Omega
. In general, for
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
with
\mathscr{R}(\mu)>-1
one has
\mathcal{E}_{\mu-k}(\overline{\Omega})=span\,D^{(k)}\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(\overline{\Omega})
, where
D^{(k)}
denotes the smooth differential operators of order
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let
q\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
and
u\in\dot{H}^{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the homogeneous Dirichlet problem

(2.4)

\begin{cases}\left((-\Delta)^{a}+q\right)u=h\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
u=0\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega.\end{cases}

Then due to the results in
[
ROS14
]
and
[
Gru15
]
respectively, we have the following:

•

h\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\Longrightarrow u\in d^{a}\,C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})

for some small
\alpha\in(0,1)
, with satisfying

(2.5)

\lvert|\frac{u(x)}{d^{a}(x)}\big|_{\partial\Omega}\rvert|_{C^{\alpha}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C_{n,a}\,\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

•

h\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})\Longleftrightarrow u\in\mathcal{E}_{a}(\overline{\Omega}).

2.3.
Well-definedness of the map
\mathcal{A}_{q}

Let us recall that

\mathcal{A}_{q}:C^{\infty}_{c}(W)\to C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega),\qquad\mathcal{A}_{q}(f)=\frac{u(x)}{d^{a}(x)}\Big|_{\partial\Omega}

where
u\in H^{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
solves

(2.6)

\begin{cases}\left((-\Delta)^{a}+q\right)u=0\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
u=f\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega_{e}\end{cases}

where
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(W)
and extended by
0
outside, and
W
be some smooth open set in
\Omega_{e}
, with satisfying
\overline{W}\cap\overline{\Omega}=\emptyset
.

We find
w=(u-f)\in\dot{H}^{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the solution of

\begin{cases}\left((-\Delta)^{a}+q\right)w=(-\Delta)^{a}f\mbox{ in }\Omega\\[2.84526pt]
w=0\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega.\end{cases}

Let us note that
h:=(-\Delta)^{a}f\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
, it simply follows since

h(x)=C_{n,a}\int_{W}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2a}}dy,\quad x\in\overline{\Omega}

has no singularity as
\overline{W}\cap\overline{\Omega}=\emptyset
. Therefore, by the above regularity result, we have
w\in\mathcal{E}_{a}(\overline{\Omega})
, that
\frac{w(x)}{d^{a}(x)}\big|_{\partial\Omega}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
.

Since
\frac{w(x)}{d^{a}(x)}\big|_{\overline{\Omega}}=\frac{u(x)}{d^{a}(x)}\big|_{\overline{\Omega}}
, hence
u\in\mathcal{E}_{a}(\overline{\Omega})
and in particular
\frac{u(x)}{d^{a}(x)}\big|_{\partial\Omega}\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
, where
u
solves (
2.6
).

This shows the map
f\mapsto\mathcal{A}_{q}(f)
is well-defined from
C^{\infty}_{c}(W)
to
C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
.

Boundedness of
\mathcal{A}_{q}

The estimate (
2.5
) asserts that we can think of
\mathcal{A}_{q}
being a bounded map with respect to
L^{\infty}
-norm, i.e.

\|\mathcal{A}_{q}(f)\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C_{n,a}(dist(\overline{\Omega},\overline{W}))^{-a}\,\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(W)}

where we have used the fact that since
\overline{\Omega}\cap\overline{W}=\emptyset
and

(dist(x,\overline{W}))^{-a}\sim\int_{W}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n+2a}}dy,\quad x\in\overline{\Omega}.

Range characterization of
\mathcal{A}_{q}

Let us consider the range of this map
\mathcal{A}_{q}
to define the space

\mathcal{R}_{\partial\Omega}=\Big\{\frac{u(x)}{d^{a}(x)}\Big|_{\partial\Omega}\,:\,u\mbox{ solves }\eqref{nra2},\,f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(W)\Big\}.

Proposition 2.1
.

\mathcal{R}_{\partial\Omega}
is dense in
C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
.

Proof.

The proof is given there in
[
GSU20
, Theorem 1.3]
.
∎

Since
\mathcal{A}_{q}
is an open (not necessarily injective) linear map, and its range (
\mathcal{R}_{\partial\Omega}
) is dense, thus we can assert that its set of pre-images is also dense in the domain of that map, i.e:

Z=\{0\}\cup\{f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(W),\,\mathcal{A}_{q}(f)\neq 0\}\quad\mbox{ is dense in }C^{\infty}_{c}(W).

2.4.
Boundary value problem

In this subsection, we will introduce the local boundary value problem for the fractional
Schrödinger operator. This class of solutions are the key to solve our inverse problem.

Example of large
a
-harmonic function

The following functions are the example of a large
a
-harmonic function on the unit ball
B(0;1)
: (see
[
Aba15
]
)

u_{\sigma}(x)=\begin{cases}c_{n,a}\frac{1}{(1-|x|^{2})^{\sigma}}\mbox{ in }B(0;1)\\
c_{n,a+\sigma}\frac{1}{(|x|^{2}-1)^{\sigma}}\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{B}(0;1)\end{cases},\quad\sigma\in(0,1-a)

solves

(-\Delta)^{a}u_{\sigma}=0\mbox{ in }B(0;1).

Clearly, over the boundary
\partial B(0;1)
,
u_{\sigma}
blows up to infinity.

The function

(2.7)

u_{1-a}(x)=\begin{cases}c_{n,a}\frac{1}{(1-|x|^{2})^{1-a}}\mbox{ in }B(0;1)\\[2.84526pt]
0\quad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{B}(0;1)\end{cases}

solves

(-\Delta)^{a}u_{1-a}=0\mbox{ in }B(0;1).

Over the boundary
\partial B(0;1)
, we define the following limit

\frac{u_{1-a}}{d(x)^{a-1}}|_{\partial B(0;1)}=\underset{|x|\to 1}{lim}\,\frac{u_{1-a}}{(1-|x|)^{a-1}}=\frac{c_{n,a}}{2^{1-a}}\neq 0

some non-zero constant.

2.5.
Fractional Schrödinger equation:

Motivated by the above example in general
we would like to study the following problem:

(2.8)

\begin{cases}\left((-\Delta)^{a}+q\right)u=0\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
supp\,u\subseteq\overline{\Omega}.\end{cases}

We will be more specific about the boundary condition soon. Before that in order to continue in that direction, let us introduce order-reducing operators of plus/minus type, see
[
Gru14
,
Gru15
,
Gru16
]
. We define

\Xi^{t}_{\pm}:=Op(\chi^{t}_{\pm})\mbox{ on }\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\chi^{t}_{\pm}=\left(\langle\xi^{\prime}\rangle\pm i\xi_{n}\right)^{t}.

These symbols extend analytically in
\xi_{n}
to
Im\,\xi_{n}\lessgtr 0
. Hence, by the Payley-Wiener theorem
\Xi^{t}_{\pm}
preserve support in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\pm}
. Then for all
s\in\mathbb{R}

\Xi^{t}_{+}:\dot{H}^{s}(\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{+})\to\dot{H}^{s-t}(\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{+}),\quad r^{+}\Xi^{t}_{-}e^{+}:\overline{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})\to\overline{H}^{s-t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}).

In fact,
\Xi^{t}_{+}
and
r^{+}\Xi^{t}_{-}e^{+}
are disjoints. The inverses are
\Xi^{-t}_{+}
and
r^{+}\Xi^{-t}_{-}e^{+}
.

The operators
\Xi^{t}_{+}
maps
\mathcal{E}_{t}(\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{+})\cap\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
to
e^{+}C^{\infty}(\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{+})
with the property that

\begin{cases}\gamma_{0}\left(\Xi_{+}^{t}u\right)=\Gamma(t+1)\gamma_{0}(u/x_{n}^{t}),\qquad\quad\mbox{(Dirichlet)}\\
\gamma_{1}\left(\Xi_{+}^{t}u\right)=\Gamma(t+2)\gamma_{0}\left(\partial_{x_{n}}(u/x_{n}^{t})\right),\quad\mbox{(Neumann)}\end{cases}u\in\mathcal{E}_{t}(\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{+})\cap\mathcal{E}^{\prime}

where
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}
are the Dirichlet and Neumann boundary trace operator respectively. And
\Gamma
stands as the well-known Gamma function.

Following that we define the
a(s)
-transmission spaces, which can be thought of as a generalization of
\mathcal{E}_{a}
spaces. We define, as in

]

Here

•

•

with continuous inclusions, i.e. multiplication by any

(2.9)

Neumann boundary value problem

One can also study the Neumann problems as follows:

(2.10)

\begin{cases}\left((-\Delta)^{a}+q\right)u=h\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\\

3.
Inverse problem: Uniqueness result

Here we now complete the proof of the Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of the Theorem
1.1
.

Let
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(W)
and we extend it by
0
elsewhere. Let
v^{k}_{f}=v^{k}\in H^{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\mathcal{E}_{a}(\overline{\Omega})
solves

\begin{cases}\left((-\Delta)^{a}+q^{k}\right)v^{k}=0\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
v^{k}=f\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega_{e}.\end{cases}\quad k=1,2.

Let
\Sigma\subset\partial\Omega
be a non-empty open subset. By our hypothesis we have

(3.1)

\frac{v^{1}_{f}}{d^{a}}\Big|_{\Sigma}=\frac{v^{2}_{f}}{d^{a}}\Big|_{\Sigma},\quad\forall f\in H^{a}_{0}(W)

and we would like to show that, it implies
q^{1}=q^{2}
in
\Omega
.

Let
g\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
be some non-zero function such that
supp\,g\subseteq\overline{\Sigma}
, and

u^{k}\in\mathcal{E}_{a-1}(\overline{\Omega})
be the non-zero solutions of the inhomogeneous Dirichlet problem:

(3.2)

\begin{cases}\left((-\Delta)^{a}+q^{k}\right)u^{k}=0\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
\frac{u^{k}(x)}{d(x)^{a-1}}=g\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\\
u^{k}=0\quad\mbox{in }\Omega_{e}.\end{cases}\quad k=1,2.

Next we use the integration by-parts formula (
2.12
) for
u^{k}\in\mathcal{E}_{a-1}(\overline{\Omega})
and
w^{k}=(v^{k}-f)\in\mathcal{E}_{a}(\overline{\Omega})
to have

(3.3)

\int_{\Omega}w^{k}\,(-\Delta)^{a}u^{k}-\int_{\Omega}u^{k}\,(-\Delta)^{a}w^{k}=-\Gamma(a)\Gamma(a+1)\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{u^{k}}{d^{a-1}}\,\frac{w^{k}}{d^{a}},\qquad k=1,2.

Since

\begin{cases}\left((-\Delta)^{a}+q^{k}\right)w^{k}=(-\Delta)^{a}f\mbox{ in }\Omega\\[2.84526pt]
\frac{w^{k}}{d^{a}}|_{\partial\Omega}=\frac{v^{k}}{d^{a}}|_{\partial\Omega},\end{cases}\quad k=1,2

so together with the equation (
3.2
), we re-write the above identity (
3.3
) as

(3.4)

\int_{\Omega}u^{k}\,(-\Delta)^{a}f=\Gamma(a)\Gamma(a+1)\int_{\Sigma}g\,\frac{v^{k}}{d^{a}},\quad k=1,2

since
supp\,g\subseteq\Sigma\subseteq\partial\Omega
.

Then by using our hypothesis (
3.1
), we conclude

\int_{\Omega}(u^{1}-u^{2})\,(-\Delta)^{a}f=0,\quad\forall f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(W).

Since as the difference
(u_{1}-u_{2})\in H^{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, so it follows that

\int_{W}f\,(-\Delta)^{a}(u^{1}-u^{2})=0,\quad\forall f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(W)

and hence,

(-\Delta)^{a}(u^{1}-u^{2})=0\quad\mbox{in }W.

Since
(u^{1}-u^{2})=0
in
W
as well (cf. (
3.2
)), therefore by the unique continuation of the fractional Laplacian
[
GSU20
, Theorem 1.2]
, we actually have

u^{1}=u^{2}\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}.

This leads to the following identity follows from the equations in (
3.2
):

(q^{1}-q^{2})u^{1}=0\quad\mbox{in }\Omega.

Since
u^{1}
is non-zero in every open set in
\Omega
, thanks to the unique continuation of the fractional Laplacian
[
GSU20
, Theorem 1.2]
, and it is smooth in the set containing the support of
q^{1},q^{2}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
, therefore we conclude

q^{1}\equiv q^{2}.

This completes our discussion of the proof.
∎

As we see our proof relies on the class of solutions of the non-local boundary valued problem, here we would like to offer some more discussion and new results about them.

4.1.
Local characterization of the large
a
-harmonic functions in ball & application

It is possible to give a local characterization of the large
a
-harmonic functions in ball as the solution of local boundary value problem. Let
u\in\mathcal{E}_{a-1}(\overline{\Omega})
be the solution of

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{a}u=0\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
\frac{u(x)}{d(x)^{a-1}}=f(x)\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\\
u=0\quad\mbox{in }\Omega_{e}.\end{cases}

We will provide a local characterization of the related Poisson operator of the above boundary value problem, and then show some application including unique continuation result and density result.

Dirichlet Green’s kernel

Let us recall the Green’s kernel associated with the fractional Laplacian operator in a bounded domain. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a Lipschitz domain, then we define the Green’s kernel
G^{a}_{\Omega}(\cdot,\cdot)
as

G^{a}_{\Omega}(x,z)=c_{n,-a}\frac{1}{|x-z|^{n-2a}}-H^{a}_{\Omega}(x,z),\quad x,z\in\Omega,\quad x\neq z,

where
H^{a}_{\Omega}\in H^{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
solves

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{a}H^{a}_{\Omega}(x,\cdot)=0\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
H^{a}_{\Omega}(x,z)=c_{n,-a}\frac{1}{|x-z|^{n-2a}}\mbox{ in }\Omega_{e}.\end{cases}

Then
G^{a}_{\Omega}(x,\cdot)
is known as the Dirichlet Green’s kernel for the bounded domain
\Omega
, solving

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{a}_{x}G^{a}_{\Omega}(x,\cdot)=\delta_{z}(x)\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
G^{a}_{\Omega}(x,z)=0\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega.\end{cases}

Here we mention that,
G^{a}_{\Omega}(x,z)=G^{a}_{\Omega}(z,x)
for all
z,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and as shown in
[
ROS14
]
, the following limit exists and we call

\forall\,\omega\in\partial\Omega,\,x\in\Omega,\quad D^{a}G_{\Omega}(x,\omega):=\underset{\Omega\ni z\to\,\omega}{lim}\,\,\,\frac{G^{a}_{\Omega}(x,z)}{d^{a}(z)}.

Poisson formula

Let
n\geq 1
and
u\in\mathcal{E}_{a-1}(\overline{\Omega})
be the solution of the following problem

(4.1)

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{a}u=0\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
\frac{u(x)}{(d(x))^{a-1}}|_{\partial\Omega}=f\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)\\
u=0\quad\mbox{in }\Omega_{e}.\end{cases}

By using he integration by parts formula (
2.12
), the solution of (
4.1
) can be expressed as

(4.2)

u(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega}D^{a}G_{\Omega}(x,\omega)f(\omega)\,dS(\omega).

Case:
\Omega
is a ball

In particular for
\Omega=B(\theta;r)
the ball of radius
r>0
and centered at
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have for
n\geq 2

(4.3)

G^{a}_{B(\theta;r)}(x,z)=\begin{cases}\widetilde{c}_{n,a}\,\frac{1}{|z-x|^{n-2a}}\left(\int_{0}^{R_{0}(x,z)}\frac{t^{a-1}}{(1+t)^{n/2}}\,dt\right),\quad x,z\in B(\theta;r),\quad x\neq z,\\
0\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(\theta;r)\end{cases}

where

(4.4)

R_{0}(x,z)=\frac{(r^{2}-|x-\theta|^{2})(r^{2}-|z-\theta|^{2})}{r^{2}|x-z|^{2}}

and
\widetilde{c}_{n,a}
is some constant depending only on
n
and
a
.

Lemma 4.1
.

(4.5)

\forall\,\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1},\,\,x\in B(0;1),\quad\underset{B(0;1)\ni z\to\,\omega}{lim}\,\,\,\frac{G^{a}_{B(0;1)}(x,z)}{(1-|z|^{2})^{a}}=k_{n}\,\frac{(1-|x|^{2})^{a}}{|x-\omega|^{n}}

where
\kappa_{n}=\frac{1}{n\alpha(n)}
,
\alpha(n)
is the volume of the unit ball.

The proof of this Lemma also appears in the work of
[
Bog99
,
Hmi94
]
. To be self-contained we present it here.

Proof.

Let us recall the expression of
G^{a}_{B(0;1)}
given in (
4.3
).

G^{a}_{B(0;1)}(x,z)=\begin{cases}\widetilde{c}_{n,a}\,\frac{1}{|z-x|^{n-2a}}\left(\int_{0}^{R_{0}(x,z)}\frac{t^{a-1}}{(1+t)^{n/2}}\,dt\right),\quad x,z\in B(0;1),\quad x\neq z,\\
0\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(0;1)\end{cases}

where

R_{0}(x,z)=\frac{(1-|x|^{2})(1-|z|^{2})}{|x-z|^{2}}

and
\widetilde{c}_{n,a}
is some constant depending only on
n
and
a
.

Note that, for
t\in[0,R_{0}]
, one simply has

\frac{t^{a-1}}{(1+R_{0})^{\frac{n}{2}}}\leq\frac{t^{a-1}}{(1+t)^{\frac{n}{2}}}\leq t^{a-1}.

Hence, it follows that

\frac{1}{(1+R_{0})^{\frac{n}{2}}}\frac{R_{0}^{a}}{a}\leq\int_{0}^{R_{0}(x,z)}\frac{t^{a-1}}{(1+t)^{n/2}}\,dt\leq\frac{R_{0}^{a}}{a}.

Then by using the expression of
R_{0}(x;z)
, as a simple consequence of the above estimate we obtain the following limit as

\forall\,\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1},\,x\in B(0;1),\quad\underset{B(0;1)\ni z\to\,\omega}{lim}\,\,\,\frac{G^{a}_{B(0;1)}(x,z)}{(1-|z|^{2})^{a}}=\frac{\widetilde{c}_{n,a}}{a}\,\frac{(1-|x|^{2})^{a}}{|x-\omega|^{n}}

and
\frac{\widetilde{c}_{n,a}}{a}=\kappa_{n}=\frac{1}{n\alpha(n)}
, where
\alpha(n)
is the volume of the unit ball.
This proves the lemma.
∎

Following that, here we present some local characterization result for a function satisfying non-local equation in ball only, and vice versa.

Proposition 4.2

(Local characterization)
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
u\in\mathcal{E}_{a-1}(\overline{B}(0;1))
be the solution of the following problem

(4.6)

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{a}u=0\quad\mbox{ in }B(0,1)\\
\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}|_{\partial B(0;1)}=f\in C^{\infty}(\partial B(0;1))\\
u=0\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{B}(0;1).\end{cases}

Then
\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}\in C^{\infty}(B(0;1))
solves

(4.7)

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)\,\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}=0\quad\mbox{ in }B(0,1)\\
\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}|_{\partial B(0;1)}=f\quad\mbox{on }\partial B(0;1)),\end{cases}

and vice-versa.

Proof.

We recall (
4.5
) for the expression of
D^{a}G_{B(0;1)}(x,\omega)
to write the Poisson formula (
4.2
) as

(4.8)

u(x)=\frac{1}{n\alpha(n)}\,(1-|x|^{2})^{a}\int_{\partial B(0;1)}\frac{f(\omega)}{|x-\omega|^{n}}\,dS(\omega),\quad x\in B(0;1)

or,

\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}=\frac{1}{n\alpha(n)}\,(1-|x|^{2})\int_{\partial B(0;1)}\frac{f(\omega)}{|x-\omega|^{n}}\,dS(\omega),\quad x\in B(0;1).

The above expression is nothing but the Poisson integral formula of the harmonic functions, that it solves (
4.7

(-\Delta)\,\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}=0\mbox{ in }B(0;1),\quad\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}=f\mbox{ on }\partial B(0;1).

Conversely, let us define
v\in C^{\infty}(\overline{B}(0;1))
by the Poisson integral as

v(x)=\frac{1}{n\alpha(n)}\,(1-|x|^{2})\int_{\partial B(0;1)}\frac{f(\omega)}{|x-\omega|^{n}}\,dS(\omega),\quad x\in B(0;1)

solving

(-\Delta)v=0\quad\mbox{in }B(0;1),\quad v=f\in C^{\infty}(\partial B(0;1))

Then we define

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}v(x),\quad x\in B(0;1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n\alpha(n)}\,(1-|x|^{2})^{a}\int_{\partial B(0;1)}\frac{f(\omega)}{|x-\omega|^{n}}\,dS(\omega)

which solves (
4.6
) thanks to (
4.8
).
This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

4.2.
Application

The above result enable us to prove the following boundary unique continuation principle in ball.

Proposition 4.4

(Boundary UCP)
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{E}_{a-1}(\overline{B}(0;1))
be a solution of

(4.11)

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{a}u=0\quad\mbox{ in }B(0,1)\\
supp\,u\subseteq\overline{B}(0;1).\end{cases}

Let
\Gamma\subset\partial B(0;1)
be some non-empty connected open subset such that

\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}|_{\Gamma}=\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a}}|_{\Gamma}=0.

Then
u\equiv 0
.

Proof.

Since
\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}|_{\Gamma}=0
, then
\partial_{\nu}\left(\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}\right)|_{\Gamma}=\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a}}|_{\Gamma}
. The rest follows from the boundary unique continuation principle for the harmonic function. As we find
(-\Delta)\,\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}=0
in
B(0;1)
with
\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}|_{\Gamma}=\partial_{\nu}\left(\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}\right)|_{\Gamma}=0
. This implies
\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}=0
in
B(0;1)
; or,
u\equiv 0
in
B(0;1)
. This completes the proof.
∎

We will be proving the set consisting of the product of
a
-harmonic functions

in ball forms a dense set in

Let
u_{1},u_{2}
solving

(4.12)

u=0\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{B}(0;1)\end{cases}

where
\Gamma\subset\partial B(0;1)
be some non-empty open set.

Proposition 4.5

(Density result)
.

Let
n\geq 2
. The set
\{u_{1}u_{2}\}
where

Proof.

By writing

Lack of injectivity

As a final application, here we would like to roll out the following result, which says: There are non-zero
g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
with
supp\,g\Subset\Omega
and non-zero
v\in H^{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
which solves

(4.14)

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{a}v=g\quad\mbox{in }\Omega\\
v=0\quad\mbox{in }\Omega_{e}\\
supp\,g\Subset\Omega\end{cases}

with satisfying

(4.15)

\frac{v(x)}{d(x)^{a}}=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.

Note that, for
a=1
, a function
v
satisfying both (
4.14
) and (
4.15
) must be satisfying
v=0
in
\Omega\setminus\overline{\omega}
, where
\omega=supp\,g
. This follows from the standard unique continuation result for the harmonic function, see
[
Lax56
]
. Here for
0<a<1
, we can not expect such result that
v=0
near
\partial\Omega
, in fact that would imply
v=0
everywhere, since it means
v=(-\Delta)^{a}v=0
in
\Omega\setminus\overline{\omega}
.

Let us multiply the above equation (
4.14
) by
u\in\mathcal{E}_{a-1}(\overline{\Omega})
solving

(4.16)

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{a}u=0\quad\mbox{in }\Omega\\
\frac{u(x)}{d(x)^{a-1}}=f\quad\mbox{on }\partial\Omega\\
u=0\quad\mbox{in }\Omega_{e}\end{cases}

and then by using the integration by parts formula (
2.12
), we find

(4.17)

\int_{\omega}gu=0,\quad\mbox{for all }u\mbox{ solving }\eqref{nra15}.

Let us take
\Omega=B(0;1)
. Since by Lemma
4.2
,
u\in\mathcal{E}_{a-1}(\overline{B}(0;1))
solving (
4.16
) means

\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{B}(0;1))
is harmonic, therefore by re-writing (
4.17
) as

(4.18)

\int_{\omega}g(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}\,\,\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}\,dx=0,\quad\mbox{for all }\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}\mbox{ harmonic in }B(0;1)

it implies
g(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}\in H^{\perp}(\omega)
, where
H(\omega)
is set of all harmonic functions in
\omega
.

Now we can choose some
0\neq h\in H^{\perp}(\omega)
and consider
g=h(1-|x|^{2})^{1-a}\neq 0
in (
4.14
). Then we claim as the corresponding solution of (
4.14
)
v\neq 0
will be satisfying (
4.15
) i.e
\frac{v(x)}{d^{a}(x)}=0
on
\partial B(0;1)
.

Let us establish our claim here: In order to show (
4.15
) for our choice of
g
, let us multiply (
4.14
) by
u
solving (
4.16
) where
f\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
is arbitrary. Next by doing integration by parts (cf. (
2.12
)), we obtain

\int_{\partial B(0;1)}\frac{v(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a}}\,f\,d\sigma=\int_{\omega}g\,u\,dx

Since by our choice of
g
,
\int_{\omega}gu=0
, (as
g(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}\in H^{\perp}(\omega)
and
\frac{u(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a-1}}\in H(\omega)
), therefore
\int_{\partial B(0;1)}\frac{v(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a}}f=0
for all
f\in C^{\infty}(\partial B(0;1))
, forcing
\frac{v(x)}{(1-|x|^{2})^{a}}=0
on
\partial B(0;1)
, i.e. (
4.15
).
