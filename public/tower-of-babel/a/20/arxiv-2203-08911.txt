Complex Analytic Dependence on the Dielectric Permittivity in ENZ Materials: The Photonic Doping Example
Robert V. Kohn and Raghavendra Venkatraman

Abstract
. Motivated by the physics literature on “photonic doping” of scatterers made from “epsilon-near-zero” (ENZ) materials, we consider how the scattering of time-harmonic TM electromagnetic waves by a cylindrical ENZ region Ω × ℝ \Omega\times\mathbb{R} is affected by the presence of a “dopant” D ⊂ Ω D\subset\Omega in which the dielectric permittivity is not near zero. Mathematically, this reduces to analysis of a 2D Helmholtz equation div ( a ( x ) ∇ u ) + k 2 u = f \mathrm{div}\,(a(x)\nabla u)+k^{2}u=f with a piecewise-constant, complex valued coefficient a a that is nearly infinite (say a = 1 δ a=\frac{1}{\delta} with δ ≈ 0 \delta\approx 0 ) in Ω ∖ D ¯ . \Omega\setminus\overline{D}. We show (under suitable hypotheses) that the solution u u depends analytically on δ \delta near 0 0 , and we give a simple PDE characterization of the terms in its Taylor expansion. For the application to photonic doping, it is the leading-order corrections in δ \delta that are most interesting: they explain why photonic doping is only mildly affected by the presence of losses, and why it is seen even at frequencies where the dielectric permittivity is merely small. Equally important: our results include a PDE characterization of the leading-order electric field in the ENZ region as δ → 0 \delta\to 0 , whereas the existing literature on photonic doping provides only the leading-order magnetic field.



1.
Introduction

A body of literature has developed concerning the design of electromagnetic devices using epsilon-near-zero (ENZ) materials
[
10
,
13
,
18
]
. A significant part of it focuses on the transverse magnetic setting, where the magnetic field has the form
\vec{H}(x)=(0,0,H(x_{1},x_{2}))
and the time-harmonic Maxwell’s equations reduce to the two-dimensional equation

(1.1)

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\nabla H\right)-\omega^{2}\mu H=f.

Here
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(x_{1},x_{2})
is the dielectric permittivity and
\mu=\mu(x_{1},x_{2})
is the magnetic permeability, both of which are in general complex-valued and frequency-dependent (i.e.,
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\omega)
and
\mu=\mu(\omega)
, where
\omega
denotes frequency). Within this time-harmonic setting, the electric field is described by the gradient of the scalar function
H
through
\vec{E}=\frac{1}{i\omega\varepsilon}(-\partial_{x_{2}}H,\partial_{x_{1}}H,0).
The function
f
models a source that can be quite general.

It is easy to see from (
1.1
) what is special about ENZ materials: since it is natural to expect that
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\nabla H
is bounded, we expect
H
to be nearly constant in a region where
\varepsilon
is nearly
0
. In the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, we are not solving a PDE in the ENZ region but rather selecting the constant value of
H
. This creates curious effects; for example, parts of the ENZ region that are distant from one another are nevertheless tightly coupled, since they share the same (constant) value of
H
; moreover the shape of the ENZ region is less important than it would be for a PDE, since all that is happening there is the selection of a constant
H
. This effect has for example been used (i) to design entirely new types of waveguides
[
1
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
24
]
, and (ii) to show how “dopants” can give an ENZ region an “effective magnetic permeability”
\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}
that is entirely different from its true magnetic permeability
\mu

[
15
,
20
,
24
]
.

The preceding discussion assumes that the PDE (
1.1
) has a meaningful limit as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
in the region occupied by the ENZ material. We shall prove this, but we are mainly interested in the corrections that appear when
\varepsilon
is
nonzero
. Making a choice, we shall focus on the second application mentioned above– photonic doping– though the strategy we follow should also be applicable to waveguides.

To state the main results of this paper, we begin by describing the photonic doping problem that we study. The scatterer has cross-section
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with finitely many cylindrical dopant rods. We let
D\subset\Omega
denote the union of the dopants (see Fig.
1
), and consider the Helmholtz equation (
1.1
) with piecewise constant
\varepsilon
and
\mu
, with
\varepsilon
small in magnitude in the ENZ region
\Omega\setminus\overline{D}
. Our analysis is sufficiently general to permit
\varepsilon
and
\mu
taking distinct constant complex values in the exterior
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega},
the ENZ region
\Omega\setminus\overline{D},
and each of the dopant rods, respectively. So long as the
|\varepsilon|\ll 1
in the ENZ region, the mathematical analysis of the problem is not complicated by the value of these other constants, as well as the (finite) number of connected components of the dopant
D
.

Figure 1.
The photonic doping problem

However, for simplicity, and in order to focus on the mathematical essence, we make some reductions. We assume that there is a single dopant rod
D
(see Fig.
2
) immersed in the ENZ region; we take
\varepsilon=1
in the exterior; and we take
\varepsilon=\delta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\},|\delta|\ll 1
in the ENZ region. Also, to simplify the presentation, we assume that
\varepsilon=1
in the dopant. Thus, we shall study the case

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\delta}(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega},\\
\delta&x\in\Omega\setminus\overline{D},\\
1&x\in D.\end{array}\right.

We shall assume that the dopant is not resonant (see Section
2.2
for a precise statement, and
Section
5
for some discussion about what happens if the dopant is resonant). Our analysis requires no other condition on
\delta;
in particular, it applies equally to the case
\mathrm{Im}(\delta)>0
(corresponding to a lossy material) and the case
\mathrm{Im}(\delta)<0
(corresponding to a material with gain) – provided, of course, that
|\delta|
is sufficiently small. As for the value of
\mu
, we take it to be the same in all regions (though the case where it takes different values in the three regions would not be fundamentally different). For the exact assumptions on the regularity of
\Omega
and
D
, as well as our assumptions on the source, we refer the reader to Section
2
.

Our main result, stated in Theorem
2.2
below, asserts that the solution to
(
1.1
) depends analytically on the complex variable
\delta
in a neighborhood of
0
.
Let us emphasize that the behavior of the magnetic field
\vec{H}
in the limit
\delta=0
is correctly
found (though not proved) in the physics literature
[
15
,
20
]
.
As previously mentioned, this amounts to selecting the correct constant value of the scalar function
H
.
However, the physics literature offers no analytical understanding about:

(1)

the character and magnitude of corrections due to
\delta\neq 0,
and

(2)

the character and magnitude of the electric field
\vec{E}
, which, as we show below, has a nonzero limit as
|\delta|\to 0.

As corollaries to our analysis, we obtain simple and reasonably explicit information on both points. Such
information is physically relevant. To explain why, we remind the reader that for real materials, the permittivity

\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{\prime}+i\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}
is a complex-analytic function of the frequency
\omega
; moreover its imaginary part
represents losses (which are always present in real materials), so
\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(\omega)>0
when

\omega
is real and positive (see e.g.
[
12
]
sections 77 and 80). Thus, while the notion
of an epsilon-near-zero material is a useful idealization, the physically relevant value of our
parameter
\delta
has positive imaginary part, and the real part of
\delta
is nonzero except at isolated frequencies.
For the effect of “photonic doping” to be robust with respect to material losses and frequency
variation, the corrections due to nonzero
\delta
must be small. Our analyticity results show that
these corrections are of order
O(|\delta|)
.

Concerning (2), we recall that the Poynting vector
S
is defined by

S:=\frac{1}{2}\vec{E}\times\overline{\vec{H}}
. (Here, as usual, for any complex number

z,

\overline{z}
denotes its complex conjugate.) The real part of
S
is the time-averaged power flow,
while the imaginary part is the reactive power flow. It is of interest to understand the
spatial structure of these flows. Since the Poynting vector involves both the magnetic field
\vec{H}

and the electric field
\vec{E},
this requires understanding both fields in the limit
\delta\rightarrow 0
.
For ENZ waveguides, this was the focus of
[
14
]
; we are not aware of a similar study in the photonic doping setting.

Some related literature

We close this introduction with a brief discussion of some related literature.

(1)

The paper
[
3
]
by O. Bruno considers the PDE
\mathrm{div}(a(x)\nabla u)=0
for coefficient fields
a
that take two values: the conductivity of the inclusions is
\delta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\},
and that of the background matrix is
1.
It studies the effective conductivity as a function of
\delta
for both periodic and random coefficient fields
a,
and provides conditions on the geometry of the inclusions that ensure analyticity of the effective conductivity near
\delta\approx 0
and
\delta\approx\infty.
Our approach is not fundamentally different, but our geometry is more specific; moreover, our focus is the Helmholtz equation.

(2)

The papers
[
2
]
and
[
23
]
study how a periodic array of inclusions can lead to a frequency-dependent effective
\mu
, in a suitable homogenized limit. In photonic doping, an effective permeability
\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}
emerges due to the presence of the dopant (see Remark
2.3
below). However, our setting is different from
[
2
,
23
]
since we do not consider an array of inclusions, and in particular, no homogenization procedure is being carried out. And yet our setting shares some features with that of
[
2
,
23
]
, since here and also there, the effective permeability is directly related to the solution of a Helmholtz equation in the dopant with boundary condition
1.

(3)

In the present work, we assume that the ENZ region is a uniform electromagnetic medium,
characterized by two constants, its complex permittivity and permeability. There are real materials
and experimental systems for which this is the case
(see for example
[
8
,
9
,
18
]
).
A different but related body of literature is about metamaterials that achieve an effective
\varepsilon

that is close to zero at certain frequencies, as a consequence of their specific
microstructure (see
[
18
]
, or for a recent example
[
16
]
). In such systems, the
robustness of the effect to the variation of the frequency
\omega
or the presence of loss
depends not only on the effects studied here, but also on how well their behavior is captured
by the effective
\varepsilon
.

(4)

In our study of photonic doping the parameter
\delta
tends to zero while the geometry
is fixed. It is of course interesting to ask whether other exotic
effects can be achieved by letting the microstructure vary as a physical parameter
approaches
0
or
\infty
. The answer is yes; indeed, many papers on
electromagnetic metamaterials have this character.
For divergence-form equations of the form
\mathrm{div}\,(a(x)\nabla u)=0
with
u
scalar, the full set of limits
obtainable this way has been characterized
[
4
]
. The analogous question for
linear elasticity has also been addressed, and the set of all possible limits is
surprisingly rich
[
5
]
. We are not aware of analogous results for
Maxwell’s equations.

Organization of the paper

Setting up the problem

1

To be precise, we suppose

\displaystyle f\in L^{2}(K)

2.2.
The nonresonance condition and three auxiliary functions

Our analysis requires that the dopant be non-resonant, in other words that
k^{2}
not be a Dirichlet eigenvalue of the
Laplacian in
D
. Equivalently:

(2.3)

We assume that the Helmholtz operator
-\Delta-k^{2}

has trivial kernel in
H^{1}_{0}(D)
.

By elliptic theory, this condition holds for all but a countable collection of
k^{2}\in(0,\infty).
(For some
discussion about what happens when this condition fails, see Section
5
.)

The solutions of the following three auxiliary problems will play key roles in our analysis:

Problem 1:
Let
s:\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the unique solution to

(2.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta s&=k^{2}s+f\quad&\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega},\\
s&=0\quad&\mbox{ along }\partial\Omega,\\
s&\mbox{ satisfies the radiation condition (\ref{e.bcinfty}) at infinity.}\end{aligned}\right.

Problem 2:
Let
\psi_{e}
denote the unique solution to

(2.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\psi_{e}&=k^{2}\psi_{e}\quad&\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega},\\
\psi_{e}&=1\quad&\mbox{ along }\partial\Omega,\\
\psi_{e}&\mbox{ satisfies the radiation condition (\ref{e.bcinfty}) at infinity. }\end{aligned}\right.

Problem 3:
Let
\psi_{d}
denote the unique solution to

(2.6)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\psi_{d}&=k^{2}\psi_{d}\quad&\mbox{ in }{D},\\
\psi_{d}&=1\quad&\mbox{ along }\partial D.\end{aligned}\right.

The existence and uniqueness of solutions to Problems 1 and 2 is well-known, see for example
[
17
, Theorem 9.11]
.
The existence and uniqueness of
\psi_{d}\in H^{1}(D)
follows by Fredholm theory from our nonresonance hypothesis (
2.3
).

Our analysis will require dividing by the complex number

(2.7)

\beta:=k^{2}|\Omega\setminus\overline{D}|+\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial\psi_{e}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}-\int_{\partial D}\frac{\partial\psi_{d}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

(Our sign conventions for the normal derivatives are shown in Fig.
2
:
\nu_{\Omega}
denotes
the outward pointing unit normal to
\partial\Omega,
and
\nu_{D}
denotes the outward pointing unit normal to
\partial D.
) It is therefore important to know that this never vanishes.

Lemma 2.1
.

The complex number
\beta
defined by (
2.7
) is always nonzero.

Proof.

A result sometimes known as Rellich’s lemma says that if
u
solves
\Delta u+k^{2}u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}

and satisfies the radiation condition (
2.2
) at infinity, then

-2\,\mbox{Im}\left(k\int_{\partial\Omega}u\frac{\partial\overline{u}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\right)=\\
2\,\mbox{Im}(k)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}}(|k|^{2}|u|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2})\,dx+\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\int_{|x|=R}(|\partial u/\partial r|^{2}+k^{2}|u|^{2})\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}

(see e.g. equation (3.10) of
[
6
]
); moreover the second term on the right
can be written in terms of the
far-field pattern of
u
, and it never vanishes (unless
u=0
). Specializing to
u=\psi_{e}
, we have

(2.8)

-2\,\mbox{Im}\left(k\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial\overline{\psi}_{e}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\right)=\\
2\,\mbox{Im}(k)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}}(|k|^{2}|\psi_{e}|^{2}+|\nabla\psi_{e}|^{2})\,dx+\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\int_{|x|=R}(|\partial\psi_{e}/\partial r|^{2}+k^{2}|\psi_{e}|^{2})\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

The function
\psi_{d}
satisfies a similar relation: multiplying its PDE by
\overline{\psi}_{d}
and integrating by parts gives

\int_{\partial D}\frac{\partial\psi_{d}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}=\int_{D}(|\nabla\psi_{d}|^{2}-k^{2}|\psi_{d}|^{2})\,dx.

Taking the complex conjugate then multiplying by
k
and
taking the imaginary part gives

(2.9)

\mbox{Im}\left(k\int_{\partial D}\frac{\partial\overline{\psi}_{d}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\right)=\mbox{Im}(k)\int_{D}(|\nabla\psi_{d}|^{2}+|k|^{2}|\psi_{d}|^{2})\,dx.

We turn now to the assertion of the lemma. If
k
is real then
\psi_{d}
is real, and it follows from (
2.8
)
that the imaginary part of
\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial\psi_{e}/\partial\nu_{\Omega}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}
is nonzero. Since the other
terms in (
2.7
) are real,
\beta\neq 0
. If
k
is not real then the imaginary part of
k
is positive (since

0\leqslant\mathrm{arg}\,k<\pi
). From (
2.7
) we have

k\overline{\beta}=\overline{k}\,|k|^{2}|\Omega\setminus\overline{D}|+k\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial\overline{\psi}_{e}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}-k\int_{\partial D}\frac{\partial\overline{\psi}_{d}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

Equations (
2.8
) and (
2.9
) show that the imaginary part of the right hand side is
strictly negative, from which it follows that
\beta\neq 0
.
∎

2.3.
Notation, conventions, and some function spaces

Unless otherwise specified, all spaces of functions in the sequel consist of complex-valued functions.
All PDEs are to be interpreted in the weak sense, by testing with an
H^{1}
function.

We will frequently need to consider the restriction to
\partial\Omega
of a function in
H^{1}(\Omega)
, so let us
recall the facts we’ll be using.
As the domain
\Omega
is bounded with Lipschitz boundary, each function
f\in H^{1}(\Omega)
admits a trace
\mathrm{tr}(f)\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega).
This trace map is a bounded linear functional on
H^{1}(\Omega)
, whose kernel is the space
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),
and range is the fractional Sobolev space
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
(see
[
17
, Theorem 3.38]
). The space
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
is a Hilbert space with respect to the norm defined variationally via: for any
g\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega),

(2.10)

\displaystyle\|g\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}:=\inf\left\{\|\widetilde{G}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}:G\in H^{1}(\Omega)\mbox{ and }\mathrm{tr}\,G=g\right\}.

For a proof of the assertion that this definition is equivalent to other definitions (say via integral kernels or by locally flattening the boundary, using a partition of unity, and appealing to a Fourier based definition within each flattened patch) of the fractional Sobolev space
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
within the setting of Lipschitz domains, we refer the reader to
[
11
, Section 5.1]
.
Finally, we define
H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
to be the dual space of
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega).
It is equivalently characterized as the space of traces of normal components
V\cdot\nu_{\Omega}
of measurable vector fields
V
on
\Omega
such that
V\in(L^{2}(\Omega))^{2},
and the divergence of
V
is square integrable, i.e.,
\mathrm{div}\,V\in L^{2}(\Omega)
(see
[
22
, Chapter 20]
for a proof).

An application of this latter equivalence that we will use repeatedly, is an estimate of the normal derivative of
s
defined via (
2.4
). We let
\Psi\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega})
denote a cut off function that satisfies
\Psi\equiv 1
in a neighborhood of
\Omega.
Considering the vector field
V:=\Psi\nabla s,
from (
2.4
) it is clear that
V\in(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}))^{2},
with
\mathrm{div}\,V\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}),
has compact support, and finally, satisfies
V\cdot\nu=\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu}.
Therefore, by the above characterization of
H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega),
it follows that

(2.11)

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}\right\|_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\|V\cdot\nu_{\Omega}\|_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C(\|V\|_{L^{2}}+\|\mathrm{div}\,V\|_{L^{2}})\leqslant C\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega})},

using the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{spt}\,\Psi}|s|^{2}+|\nabla s|^{2}\,dx\leqslant C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}}|f|^{2}\,dx,

which is proved in showing the existence and uniqueness of
s
(see for example
[
17
]
).

2.4.
A summary of our main results

Our main theorem is

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose the dopant is non-resonant, in other words that (
2.3
) holds. Then
there exists
\Lambda>0
, depending only on
k
and the sets
\Omega
and
D

(and
independent
of the source
f
and the value of
\delta
) such that
if
|\delta|<\frac{1}{\Lambda},
then the map
\delta\mapsto u_{\delta}\in H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{C})

is complex analytic. To be precise, there exist
\{v_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}\subset H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

independent of
\delta
, such that

(2.12)

\displaystyle u_{\delta}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\delta^{j}v_{j}\quad\quad\mbox{ in }H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

with
v_{j}
satisfying: for each open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},
there exists a constant
K_{U}>0
(independent of
\delta
), such that

\displaystyle\|v_{j}\|_{H^{1}(U)}\leqslant K_{U}\Lambda^{j}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}}.

As will be clear from the proof, we obtain a complete characterization of all the “correctors”
v_{j}
that
appear in the statement of Theorem
2.2
. But it is the leading-order correctors that are the most important, and the
easiest to describe. Therefore we devote the remainder of this section to a self-contained description of the leading-order
correctors, and statements of some corollaries that they permit us to draw.

The zeroth order term in (
2.12
) is of course the easiest to describe: it is

\displaystyle v_{0}(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}c^{*}\psi_{e}+s&x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\\
c^{*}&x\in\Omega\setminus\overline{D}\\
c^{*}\psi_{d}&x\in D,\end{array}\right.

where

(2.13)

c^{*}:=-\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1},

which is finite since
\beta\neq 0
by Lemma
2.1
.

Remark 2.3
.

As noted in the introduction,
v_{0}
is constant in the ENZ region. In the physics literature, the term “effective permeability”
\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}
is used for the choice of
\mu
that would have given the same value to

in the ENZ region without any dopant. In our setting, the value of this choice is

(2.14)

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}:=\frac{|\Omega\setminus\overline{D}|+\int_{D}\psi_{d}\,dx}{|\Omega|}\mu.

Indeed, the preceding description of
\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}
reduces to the value such that

(2.15)

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}|\Omega|+\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial\psi_{e}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}=\omega^{2}\mu|\Omega\setminus\overline{D}|+\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial\psi_{e}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}-\int_{\partial D}\frac{\partial\psi_{d}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

A bit of manipulation using Green’s theorem, the PDE solved by
\psi_{d}
, our convention that
k^{2}=\omega^{2}\mu
, and

-\int_{\partial D}\frac{\partial\psi_{d}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}=k^{2}\int_{D}\psi_{d}\,dx,

reveals that (
2.14
) and (
2.15
) are equivalent.

To identify the leading order electric field
\frac{1}{i\omega\varepsilon(x)}(-\partial_{x_{2}}u_{\delta},\partial_{x_{1}}u_{\delta},0)
we must discuss the
j=1
term in (
2.12
). To do so, we introduce new correctors.
We let
\phi_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})
denote the unique weak solution to the PDE

(2.16)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\phi_{0}&=k^{2}c^{*}\quad\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\setminus\overline{D}\\
\frac{\partial\phi_{0}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}&=c^{*}\frac{\partial\psi_{e}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}+\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}\quad\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\\
\frac{\partial\phi_{0}}{\partial\nu_{D}}&=c^{*}\frac{\partial\psi_{d}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\quad\quad\mbox{ on }\partial D,\\
\strokedint_{\Omega\setminus\overline{D}}&\phi_{0}\,dx=0;\end{aligned}\right.

and we define the exterior corrector
\lambda_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}),

(2.17)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\lambda_{0}&=k^{2}\lambda_{0}\quad\quad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega},\\
\lambda_{0}&=\phi_{0}\quad\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\\
\lambda_{0}&\mbox{ satisfies the radiation condition (\ref{e.bcinfty}) at infinity},\end{aligned}\right.

and the dopant corrector
\chi_{0}\in H^{1}(D),
given by

(2.18)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\chi_{0}&=k^{2}\chi_{0}\quad\quad\mbox{ in }D,\\
\chi_{0}&=\phi_{0}\quad\quad\mbox{ on }\partial D.\end{aligned}\right.

We emphasize that the corrector
\phi_{0}
in the ENZ region
\Omega\setminus\overline{D}
solves a
Poisson equation with constant right hand side
, not a Helmholtz equation. The choice of
c^{*}
by (
2.13
) exactly ensures that the problem (
2.16
) is well-posed; the problems defining
(\lambda_{0},\chi_{0})
are also well-posed, since we have assumed that the dopant isn’t resonant. Define

(2.19)

e_{0}:=-\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial\lambda_{0}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}-\int_{\partial D}\frac{\partial\chi_{0}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\right),

and set
c_{\delta}:=c^{*}+\delta e_{0}.
The following result captures the essential information available from the first two terms in (
2.12
).

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
|\delta|<\frac{1}{\Lambda},
where
\Lambda
is as in Theorem
2.2
, and let
v_{\delta}\in H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be defined via

(2.20)

v_{\delta}(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}&c_{\delta}\psi_{e}(x)+s(x)+\delta\lambda_{0}(x)&x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\\
&c_{\delta}+\delta\phi_{0}&x\in\Omega\setminus\overline{D}\\
&c_{\delta}\psi_{d}+\delta\chi_{0}&x\in D.\end{array}\right.

Then, for any open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with compact closure, there exists a constant
C_{U}>0
independent of
\delta
such that

(2.21)

\displaystyle\|u_{\delta}-v_{\delta}\|_{H^{1}(U)}\leqslant C_{U}\delta^{2}.

Let us note that we obtain local in
H^{1}
estimates only because of the unboundedness of the domain. In particular, within the ENZ region
\Omega\setminus\overline{D},
the differences above are global, in the following sense: the estimate (
2.21
) says that
u_{\delta}
is close to being a constant, where closeness is measured in terms of
H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})
.

Remark 2.5
.

A consequence of Corollary
2.4
is that at leading order, the electric field distribution in the ENZ region is given by

\displaystyle\vec{E}:=\frac{1}{i\omega}(-\partial_{x_{2}}\phi_{0},\partial_{x_{1}}\phi_{0},0).

Indeed, this follows, since within the TM setting of our paper, the magnetic field is
\vec{H}=(0,0,u_{\delta}),
and the electric field is
\vec{E}=\frac{1}{i\omega\varepsilon_{\delta}(x)}(-\partial_{x_{2}}u_{\delta},\partial_{x_{1}}u_{\delta},0).
Similarly, from Corollary
2.4
, it is also clear that one can read off information about the electric field distribution in the other regions.

Our next corollary generalizes the so-called “Ideal Fluid Analogy” introduced in
[
14
]
. In that paper, the authors consider the setting in which both the dielectric permittivity
\varepsilon_{\delta}
and the magnetic permeability (
k^{2}=\omega^{2}\mu
in the notation of the introduction), both vanish in the ENZ region. As explained in the introduction, our next corollary clarifies and complements the result in two ways: first, of course, it describes corrections to the leading order theory recalled in the introduction due to nonzero
\delta
(e.g., via the presence of losses), and second, it is not restricted to the case of vanishing magnetic permeability. In order to state this corollary, we recall the notion of the Poynting vector, as the authors in
[
14
]
formulate their ideal fluid analogy in terms of the Poynting vector. It is defined as the function
S_{\delta}:=\frac{1}{2}\vec{E}_{\delta}\times\overline{\vec{H}_{\delta}},
with
\overline{z}
denoting the complex conjugate of a complex number
z\in\mathbb{C}
. We have the following characterization of the limit of
S_{\delta}
as
\delta\to 0:

Corollary 2.6
.

In the ENZ set
\Omega\setminus\overline{D},
the Poynting vector field
S_{\delta}
converges in
L^{2}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})
strongly to
{S}=\frac{\overline{c^{*}}}{2i\omega}\nabla\phi_{0}
, which satisfies the PDE system

(2.22)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \nabla\cdot{S}&=-\frac{1}{2i\omega}k^{2}|c^{*}|^{2}=\frac{i\omega\mu}{2}|c^{*}|^{2},\quad\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\setminus\overline{D},\\
\nabla\times{S}&=0,\quad\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\setminus\overline{D},\\
\nu_{\Omega}\cdot{S}&=\frac{1}{2i\omega}\overline{c^{*}}\left(c^{*}\frac{\partial\psi_{e}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}+\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}\right),\quad\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\\
\nu_{D}\cdot{S}&=\frac{1}{2i\omega}|c^{*}|^{2}\frac{\partial\psi_{d}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\quad\quad\mbox{ on }\partial D.\end{aligned}\right.

The “ideal fluid analogy” explored in
[
14
]
rests on the observation that if
\mu=0
in the ENZ region then
\nabla\cdot S=0
and
\nabla\times S=0
there (so the real and imaginary parts of
S
are the gradients of harmonic functions). Corollary
2.6
provides a simple extension to our setting, which applies for any value of
\mu.
In particular, the real and imaginary parts of
S
in the ENZ region are the gradients of functions satisfying Poisson-type equations of the form
\Delta W=\mbox{constant},
where the right-hand-side is the real or imaginary part of
\tfrac{1}{2}i\omega\mu|c^{*}|^{2}.

2.5.
Main ingredients of the proof

The main idea in the proof of Theorem
2.2
and its corollaries is to pursue a perturbative ansatz of the form

(2.23)

\displaystyle u_{\delta}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\delta^{j}v_{j},

and plug it into the PDE (
2.1
). When
|\delta|\ll 1
, we expect that
u_{\delta}
is essentially constant in the ENZ region. To this end, by linearity of the PDE (
2.1
), one seeks

\displaystyle v_{0}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}c\psi_{e}+s&\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\\
c&\mbox{ in }\Omega\setminus\overline{D}\\
c\psi_{d}&\mbox{ in }D,\end{array}\right.

for an unknown constant
c,
where the functions
s,\psi_{e},
and
\psi_{d}
are as defined via (
2.4
), (
2.5
) and (
2.6
). Observing that continuity of
v_{0}
across
\partial\Omega
and
\partial D
is enforced by construction, it remains to determine the unknown constant
c.
The value of this constant is determined at the next order. Indeed, plugging in (
2.23
) into (
2.1
), and matching the terms at
O(\delta)
and enforcing continuity of
\varepsilon_{\delta}^{-1}\frac{\partial u_{\delta}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}
(respectively
\varepsilon_{\delta}^{-1}\frac{\partial u_{\delta}}{\partial\nu_{D}})
at the interface
\partial\Omega
(respectively
\partial D
), one arrives at the system (
2.16
) for
\phi_{0}:=v_{1}|_{\Omega\setminus\overline{D}}
in the ENZ region, and the systems (
2.17
) and (
2.18
) respectively for the restrictions
\lambda_{0}
and
\chi_{0}
of the corrector
v_{1}
in the regions
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
and
D
respectively.
We note
that the PDE (
2.16
) is a Poisson equation, and the value
c=c^{*}
is exactly the unique choice of the constant
c
that makes this problem consistent. The choice of the boundary conditions for
\phi_{0}
ensure continuity of
\varepsilon_{\delta}^{-1}\frac{\partial v_{0}}{\partial\nu}
across both the interfaces, and those for
\lambda_{0}
and
\chi_{0}
yield continuity of
v_{1}
across the interfaces. The subsequent order correctors are then defined inductively, where at each stage, the constant
c^{*}
is corrected by suitable constants
\delta^{j}e_{j},
that render the Poisson problems in the ENZ region consistent, providing the Dirichlet data for the exterior and dopant Helmholtz problems.

The bulk of the proof lies in setting up the induction procedure above, and arguing that the system for the infinite system of correctors does indeed close, with bounds sufficient to ensure analyticity. The key point, as one might naturally expect in the context of divergence form PDEs with piecewise constant coefficients, is that the regions
\Omega\setminus\overline{D}
,
D
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
interact with each other through their Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps.

3.
An Operator Theoretic Set-up

We introduce various operators that will be used in the proof of our main result.

3.1.
Dirichlet-to-Neumann operators outside the ENZ region

Let
\mathcal{D}^{e}_{k}
and
\mathcal{D}^{d}_{k}
denote the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operators associated with the Helmholtz operator
-\Delta-k^{2}
in the exterior and in the dopant regions respectively. To be precise, given
g_{e}\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
(resp.
g_{d}\in H^{1/2}(D)
), define these operators via

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u_{e}&=k^{2}u_{e}\quad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega},\\
u_{e}&=g_{e}\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\\
u_{e}&\mbox{ satisfies the radiation condition (\ref{e.bcinfty}) at infinity }\\
\mathcal{D}^{e}_{k}(g_{e})&:=\frac{\partial u_{e}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}\in H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega),\end{aligned}\right.

and

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u_{d}&=k^{2}u_{d}\quad\mbox{ in }D,\\
u_{d}&=g_{d}\quad\mbox{ on }\partial D,\\
\mathcal{D}^{d}_{k}(g_{d})&:=\frac{\partial u_{d}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\in H^{-1/2}(\partial D),\end{aligned}\right.

respectively. A basic fact that we will use from
[
6
,
7
,
17
]
is

Lemma 3.1
.

Under the assumptions in Section
2.2
, the operators
\mathcal{D}^{e}_{k}
and
\mathcal{D}^{d}_{k}
define bounded linear operators: to be precise, there exist constants
C_{k}^{e},C_{k}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{D}^{e}_{k}(g_{e})\|_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\leqslant C_{k}^{e}\|g_{e}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)},

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{D}^{d}_{k}(g_{d})\|_{H^{-1/2}(\partial D)}\leqslant C_{k}^{d}\|g_{d}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial D)},

With this definition at hand, by a convenient abuse of notation, we will write

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathcal{D}^{e}_{k}(g)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}:=\langle\mathcal{D}^{e}_{k}(g),\mathbf{1}\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega),H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)},

where
\mathbf{1}(x)\equiv 1
for all
x\in\partial\Omega

and, we use a similar convention at
\partial D.
By duality,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathcal{D}^{e}_{k}(g_{e})\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\right|\leqslant C_{k}^{e}\|g_{e}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\|\mathbf{1}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)},

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\partial D}\mathcal{D}^{d}_{k}(g_{d})\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\right|\leqslant C_{k}^{d}\|g_{d}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial D)}\|\mathbf{1}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial D)}.

A tensorized version of
\mathcal{D}_{k}^{e},\mathcal{D}_{k}^{d}
is easily defined via: given
(g_{e},g_{d})\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial D),
set

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{k}(g_{e},g_{d}):=(\mathcal{D}_{k}^{e}(g_{e}),\mathcal{D}_{k}^{d}(g_{d})).

3.2.
An averaging operator

For any
(h_{e},h_{d})\in H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{-1/2}(\partial D),
we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(h_{e},h_{d}):=-\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}h_{e}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}-\int_{\partial D}h_{d}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\right)\in\mathbb{C},

recalling that
\beta\neq 0
by Lemma
2.1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The functional
\mathcal{A}:H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial D)\to\mathbb{C}
is a linear map that is bounded in the sense that there exists a constant
C_{\mathcal{A}}>0
with

(3.2)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}(h_{e},h_{d})|\leqslant C_{\mathcal{A}}(\|h_{e}\|_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)}+\|h_{d}\|_{H^{-1/2}(\partial D)}).

Proof.

The desired estimate follows easily from (
3.1
) and the triangle inequality.
∎

3.3.
The solution operators

We introduce solution operators in the various regions. In order to define this operator, we let
\underline{L}^{2}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})
denote the set of square integrable functions in
\Omega\setminus\overline{D},
whose average is zero: precisely,

\underline{L}^{2}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D}):=\left\{f\in L^{2}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D}):\int_{\Omega\setminus\overline{D}}f=0\right\},

and

\displaystyle\underline{H}^{1}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D}):=\left\{f\in H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D}):\int_{\Omega\setminus\overline{D}}f=0\right\}\,.

We define the solution operator in the ENZ region, denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{k}:\underline{L}^{2}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})\times H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{-1/2}(\partial D)\to\underline{H}^{1}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{k}(g,h_{e},h_{d}):=\hat{\phi},

where
\hat{\phi}=\hat{\phi}(g,h_{e},h_{d})
is the unique
H^{1}
weak solution to the boundary value problem

(3.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\hat{\phi}&=k^{2}\mathcal{A}(h_{e},h_{d})+k^{2}g,\quad\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\setminus\overline{D},\\
\frac{\partial\hat{\phi}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}&=\mathcal{A}(h_{e},h_{d})\frac{\partial\psi_{e}}{\partial\nu}+h_{e}\quad\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial\hat{\phi}}{\partial\nu_{D}}&=\mathcal{A}(h_{e},h_{d})\frac{\partial\psi_{d}}{\partial\nu_{D}}+h_{d},\quad\quad\mbox{ on }\partial D,\\
\strokedint_{\Omega\setminus\overline{D}}&\hat{\phi}=0.\end{aligned}\right.

The choice of the constant
\mathcal{A}(h_{e},h_{d})
is exactly such that this system is consistent.

In order to define correctors in the exterior and in the dopant region, we define the Helmholtz solution operators
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{e}:H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega})
, and respectively,
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{d}:H^{1/2}(\partial D)\to H^{1}(D),
via

(3.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta v&=k^{2}v\quad\quad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\\
v&=g_{e}\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega),\\
v&\mbox{ satisfies the radiation condition (\ref{e.bcinfty}) at infinity}\\
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{e}&(g_{e}):=v,\end{aligned}\right.

and respectively,

(3.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta v&=k^{2}v\quad\quad\mbox{ in }D\\
v&=g_{d}\in H^{1/2}(\partial D),\\
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{d}&(g_{d}):=v,\end{aligned}\right.

That
\mathcal{H}^{d}_{k}
is well-defined is guaranteed by our assumption (
2.3
) that the dopant isn’t resonant.
As usual, we tensorize this to define
\mathcal{H}_{k}:H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial D)\to H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega})\times H^{1}(D)
via

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}(g_{e},g_{d})=(\mathcal{H}_{k}^{e}(g_{e}),\mathcal{H}_{k}^{d}(g_{d})).

Lemma 3.3
.

The operator
\mathcal{P}_{k}:\underline{L}^{2}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})\times H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{-1/2}(\partial D)\to H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D}),
and
\mathcal{H}_{k}:H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(D)\to H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega})\times H^{1}(D)
are both bounded linear operators between the respective Hilbert spaces.

Proof.

See
[
17
, Theorem 8.19]
for the assertion regarding
\mathcal{P}_{k}
, and
[
17
, Theorem 9.11 and page 286]
for the statement regarding
\mathcal{H}_{k}.

∎

Denoting the boundary trace operator for the ENZ region,
\Omega\setminus\overline{D}
by
\mathcal{T},
it then follows that the composition
\mathcal{T}\mathcal{P}_{k}:\underline{L}^{2}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})\times H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{-1/2}(\partial D)\to H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial D)
is a bounded linear operator. The main object of our proof is the iteration map
\mathcal{I}_{k}:\underline{H}^{1}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})\times H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{-1/2}(\partial D)\to\underline{H}^{1}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})\times H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{-1/2}(\partial D)
defined via

(3.7)

\displaystyle(g,h_{e},h_{d})\in\underline{H}^{1}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})\times H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{-1/2}(\partial D)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\mapsto\mathcal{I}_{k}(g,h_{e},h_{d}):=(\mathcal{P}_{k}(g,h_{e},h_{d}),\mathcal{D}_{k}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{P}_{k}(g,h_{e},h_{d})).

\mathcal{I}_{k}

Recall the number
c^{*}

(3.9)

and that

\displaystyle(\lambda_{0},\chi_{0})=\mathcal{H}_{k}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{P}_{k}\left(0,\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}},0\right).

Finally, we note

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\lambda_{0}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}},\frac{\partial\chi_{0}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\right)=\mathcal{D}_{k}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{P}_{k}\left(0,\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}},0\right),

\delta,
the operator
(I-\delta\mathcal{I}_{k})
is invertible, and its inverse defines a bounded linear operator on the space
\underline{H}^{1}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})\times H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{-1/2}(\partial D).
It is a standard fact that the associated Neumann series converges absolutely on compact subsets of
\{|\delta|\leqslant\frac{1}{\Lambda}\}
. Using the linearity of the operators
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{k},\mathcal{T},\mathcal{P}_{k}
and the definition of the Neumann series, and (
3.8
), we find

(4.9)

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{\delta}:=c^{*}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\delta^{j+1}e_{j}=c^{*}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\delta^{j+1}\mathcal{A}\left(\mathcal{D}_{k}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{P}_{k}\mathcal{I}_{k}^{j}\left(0,\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}},0\right)\right)

\displaystyle=c^{*}+\delta\mathcal{A}\left(\mathcal{D}_{k}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{P}_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\delta^{j}\mathcal{I}_{k}^{j}\left(0,\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}},0\right)\right)

\displaystyle=c^{*}+\delta\mathcal{A}\mathcal{D}_{k}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{P}_{k}(I-\delta\mathcal{I}_{k})^{-1}\left(0,\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}},0\right),

satisfies
|\hat{c}_{\delta}|<\infty
for all
|\delta|<\frac{1}{\Lambda}
. Next, we define, and compute using (
3.9
) and (
4.6
) that

(4.10)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{\delta}:=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\delta^{j}\phi_{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\delta^{j}\mathcal{P}_{k}\mathcal{I}_{k}^{j}\left(0,\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}},0\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{k}\left((I-\delta\mathcal{I}_{k})^{-1}\left(0,\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}},0\right)\right)

where, the convergence is understood in the sense of the Hilbert space
H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})
.
Finally, we define, and compute using (
3.10
) and (
4.6
) that

(4.11)

\displaystyle(\hat{\lambda}_{\delta},\hat{\chi}_{\delta}):=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\delta^{j}(\lambda_{j},\chi_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\delta^{j}\mathcal{H}_{k}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{P}_{k}\mathcal{I}_{k}^{j}\left(0,\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}},0\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{k}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{P}_{k}\left((I-\delta\mathcal{I}_{k})^{-1}\left(0,\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}},0\right)\right),

where, once again, the convergence of the infinite sum is understood in the sense of the Hilbert space
H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{D})\times H^{1}(D).

3.
In this step we define a candidate for the solution
u_{\delta}
of (
2.1
). Indeed, define

(4.12)

\hat{u}_{\delta}(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\hat{c}_{\delta}\psi_{e}+s+\delta\hat{\lambda}_{\delta}&x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\\
\hat{c}_{\delta}+\delta\hat{\phi}_{\delta}&x\in\Omega\setminus\overline{D},\\
\hat{c}_{\delta}\psi_{d}+\delta\hat{\chi}_{\delta}&x\in D.\end{array}\right.

By construction,
\hat{u}_{\delta}
and
\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{\delta}}\frac{\partial u_{\delta}}{\partial\nu}
are continuous along
\partial\Omega
and
\partial D,
and in particular,
\hat{u}_{\delta}\in H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
by Lemma
3.3
. Using partial sums, it is easily seen that by construction,
\hat{u}_{\delta}
is a weak solution to (
2.1
). As, by assumption, (
2.1
) is non-resonant,
\hat{u}_{\delta}
coincides with its unique solution.

4.
We are ready to complete the proof of the theorem. Defining
v_{j}:=\lambda_{j}
in the exterior,
:=e_{j}+\phi_{j}
in the ENZ region, and
:=\chi_{j}
in the dopant, the conclusion of the theorem follows using the estimates (
4.7
) and (
4.8
) from Step 2. The bound on
v_{j}
follows by combining the estimates in Step 2 with (
2.11
).
∎

Having completed the proof of our main result, we turn to the proofs of the corollaries stated in Section
2.4
.

Proof of Corollary
2.4
.

From the proof of Theorem
2.2
, we know that
u_{\delta}=\hat{u}_{\delta},
defined in (
4.12
). It follows, therefore, that

u_{\delta}(x)-v_{\delta}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\left(\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\delta^{j}e_{j}\right)\psi_{e}+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\delta^{j}\lambda_{j},&x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\\
\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\delta^{j}(e_{j}+\phi_{j})&x\in\Omega\setminus\overline{D}\\
\left(\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\delta^{j}e_{j}\right)\psi_{d}+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\delta^{j}\chi_{j},&x\in D.\end{array}\right.

In the above, each of the summations is interpreted as a convergent series in
H^{1}
of the appropriate region (and
H^{1}_{loc}
in the exterior). The proof of the corollary is then immediate from (
4.6
),
|\delta|<\frac{1}{\Lambda},
and estimates (
4.7
) and (
4.8
).
∎

Proof of Corollary
2.6
.

Since the Poynting vector is defined by
\vec{S}_{\delta}=\frac{1}{2}\vec{E}_{\delta}\times\overline{\vec{H}_{\delta}},
the proof of this corollary is a short computation that combines
\vec{H}_{\delta}=(0,0,u_{\delta}),
and
\vec{E}_{\delta}=\frac{1}{i\omega}(-\partial_{x_{2}}\phi_{0},\partial_{x_{1}}\phi_{0},0)+O(\delta)
(see Remark
2.5
), along with (
2.21
) to pass to the limit
\delta\to 0
limit. The boundary conditions for
S
come from the Neumann boundary conditions for
\phi_{0}
in (
2.16
).
∎

5.
What if the Dopant is Resonant?

For any choice of the dopant geometry, there will be some choices of
k
for which the dopant is resonant, and it is
natural to ask what happens then. The answer one finds in
[
15
]
is that:

(5.1)

if the dopant resonance is excited then the magnetic field

\displaystyle\mbox{vanishes in the ENZ region
and }\mu_{\rm eff}=\infty\,.

This section starts by briefly discussing what goes wrong when the dopant is resonant; then we shall recover (
5.1
) using our framework, and we’ll go beyond it by identifying the leading-order electric field.

The dopant is resonant when
k^{2}
is an eigenvalue
of the Laplace operator on
D
with zero Dirichlet boundary conditions, i.e., there exist finitely many (say
m\in\mathbb{N}
) eigenfunctions
\{U_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}\subset H^{1}_{0}(D)
that are orthonormal in
L^{2}(D),
and satisfy, for every
j=1,\ldots,m,

\displaystyle-\Delta U_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k^{2}U_{j}\quad\mbox{in $D$}

\displaystyle U_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\quad\quad\mbox{at $\partial D$}.

The finiteness of
m
follows from standard theory. For such
k
, our framework breaks down in one of two
mutually exclusive ways:

(1)

Suppose first that the eigenfunctions
U_{j},j=1,\ldots,m
each satisfy
\int_{D}U_{j}=0,
i.e.,
they each have mean zero in
D.
In this case the auxiliary function
\psi_{d}
exists but it isn’t unique. Indeed,
setting
\Psi_{d}:=\psi_{d}-1,
the existence of
\psi_{d}
is equivalent to the solvability of the PDE

\displaystyle-\Delta\Psi_{d}=k^{2}(\Psi_{d}+1)\quad\quad\mbox{ in }D,

for
\Psi_{d}\in H^{1}_{0}(D).
By the Fredholm alternative,
\Psi_{d}
exists, as the constant function
1
is
orthogonal in
L^{2}(D)
to
U_{j},
i.e.,
\int_{D}U_{j}=0
for each
j=1,\ldots,m.
However it is nonunique, since for any
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}\subset\mathbb{C},
the choices

(5.2)

\displaystyle\psi_{d}=\Psi_{d}+1=1+k^{2}\left[\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(-\Delta)\setminus\{k^{2}\}}\frac{\int_{D}\phi_{\lambda}\,dx}{\lambda-k^{2}}\phi_{\lambda}\right]+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}U_{j}

solve (
2.6
). (Here
\sigma(-\Delta)
denotes the set of Laplacian eigenvalues with zero
Dirichlet boundary data, and
\phi_{\lambda}
is the eigenfunction associated with eigenvalue
\lambda,

normalized so that
\|\phi_{\lambda}\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1.
)

Heuristically, we can say in this case that “the dopant resonance isn’t excited,” since there is apparently nothing
preventing us from choosing
\alpha_{j}=0
for
j=1,\ldots,m
. Moreover, since
\int U_{j}=0,
the value of

c^{*}
(which feels
\psi_{d}
only via
\int_{D}\psi_{d}
) is oblivious to the choice of
\{\alpha_{j}\}
. However,
the correction
\chi_{0}
(see (
2.18
)) depends on the choice of
\{\alpha_{j}\}
.

Let us record that, from (
5.2
),

(5.3)

\displaystyle\int_{D}\psi_{d}=|D|+k^{2}\left[\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(-\Delta)\setminus\{k^{2}\}}\frac{\bigl(\int_{D}\phi_{\lambda}\,dx\bigr)^{2}}{\lambda-k^{2}}\right].

(This sum is convergent: by Weyl’s law, the
j
th eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}\sim j^{2/d}=j,
so the factors
\frac{1}{\lambda-k^{2}}
share the decay of only the harmonic series [which logarithmically blows up!]. However, the convergence of the series defining
\int\psi_{d}
is assisted by the fact that the sequence of
L^{2}
-normalized eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda}
converge weakly in
L^{2}
to zero, so
\int_{D}\phi_{\lambda}\,dx\to 0.
)

(2)

Suppose instead that one of the eigenfunctions
U_{j}
is such that
\int_{D}U_{j}\neq 0.
(This happens, for instance,
if
k^{2}
is the smallest Dirichlet eigenvalue of
-\Delta
in
D
since the eigenspace is then simple and the eigenfunction –
taken to be real-valued – doesn’t change sign.) In this case, rather than nonuniqueness the difficulty is nonexistence:
the boundary value problem (
2.6
) describing
\psi_{d}
doesn’t have a solution.

For reasons that will become clear presently, we view this as the case when “the dopant resonance is excited.”

To achieve a better understanding, it is natural to consider a limit in which the resonant situation is approached via
nonresonant ones. There are (at least) two natural approaches:

(i)

We can ask what happens as
k^{2}
approaches an eigenvalue along the real axis; this means choosing an eigenvalue

\lambda_{*}
of
-\Delta
in
D
with the Dirichlet boundary condition, and considering
k
such that

\lambda_{*}-k^{2}=\gamma,
where
\gamma
is real with
0<|\gamma|\ll 1.

(ii)

We can consider what happens when resonance is prevented by material losses. This can be achieved, for
example, by taking the magnetic permeability
\mu
to have a positive imaginary part. This amounts in our framework
to taking
k^{2}=\lambda_{*}+i\gamma
, where
\lambda_{*}
is an eigenvalue (as above) and
0<\gamma\ll 1
.

Either way, our analysis applies to the limit
\delta\rightarrow 0
when
\gamma
is held fixed. We are therefore able to consider the limiting behavior when
\delta\to 0

first, then

\gamma\to 0.

Let us pursue this idea in case (2) (when
\psi_{d}
doesn’t exist). Our analysis encompasses both alternatives (i) and (ii) suggested above, and is in fact more general. Fixing
\lambda_{*}\in\sigma(-\Delta),
we consider
any

k\in\mathbb{C}
with
0\leqslant\arg\,k<\pi
such that
k^{2}
is near
\lambda_{*}
, say

\lambda_{*}-k^{2}=\gamma\quad\mbox{ with }|\gamma|\mbox{ small but nonzero}.

We shall identify the limiting behavior as
|\gamma|\to 0
with

0<|\delta|\ll|\gamma|\ll 1.

To begin, we observe (by an obvious adjustment to (
5.3
)) that

(5.4)

\displaystyle\int_{D}\psi_{d}=|D|+k^{2}\left[\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(-\Delta)}\frac{\left(\int_{D}\phi_{\lambda}\,dx\right)^{2}}{\lambda-k^{2}}\right].

We are assuming that at least one of the eigenfunctions associated with
\lambda=\lambda_{*}
has nonzero mean. It follows that
the value of (
5.4
) blows up as
|\gamma|\to 0
, though it is finite (and large, of order

1/|\gamma|
) for fixed
\gamma\neq 0
. Therefore

|\beta|\sim\left|\int_{\partial D}\frac{\partial\psi_{d}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\right|\sim\frac{1}{|\gamma|},

and it follows that
c^{*}
is small, and
|\mu_{\rm eff}|
is large:

(5.5)

|c^{*}|=O(|\gamma|)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mu_{\rm eff}=O(|\gamma|^{-1})

For the rest of this section it is convenient to track the dependence of various quantities on
\gamma,
and we do this by a subscript: for instance, we specify the dependence of
c^{*}
on
\gamma
through the notation
c^{*}_{\gamma}.

Theorem
2.2
applies, and asserts that the leading order corrections in the ENZ region, in the
\delta\to 0
limit (for fixed
\gamma
) satisfy (
2.16
):

(5.6)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta\phi^{\gamma}_{0}=k_{\gamma}^{2}c_{\gamma}^{*}\quad\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\setminus\overline{D}\\
&\frac{\partial\phi_{0}^{\gamma}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}=c_{\gamma}^{*}\frac{\partial\psi_{e}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}+\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}\quad\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\\
&\frac{\partial\phi^{\gamma}_{0}}{\partial\nu_{D}}=c_{\gamma}^{*}\frac{\partial\psi^{\gamma}_{d}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\quad\quad\mbox{ on }\partial D,\\
&\strokedint_{\Omega\setminus\overline{D}}\phi^{\gamma}_{0}\,dx=0.\end{aligned}\right.

It is convenient to pass to the
\gamma\to 0^{+}
limit by writing (
5.6
) in weak form. Toward this end, testing (
2.16
) with
h\in C^{\infty}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D}),
we obtain

(5.7)

\int_{\Omega\setminus\overline{D}}\nabla\phi_{0}^{\gamma}\cdot\nabla h\,dx-\int_{\partial\Omega}h\left(c_{\gamma}^{*}\frac{\partial\psi_{e}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}+\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}\right)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}-\int_{\partial D}h\left(c_{\gamma}^{*}\frac{\partial\psi^{\gamma}_{d}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\right)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}=\\
k_{\gamma}^{2}c_{\gamma}^{*}\int_{\Omega\setminus\overline{D}}h\,dx.

Arguing formally, if
\phi_{0}^{\gamma}
converges weakly to
\widehat{\phi_{0}}
in
H^{1}(D)
, then since
k_{\gamma}^{2}\to\lambda_{*}
,
\psi_{e},s
are independent of
\gamma,
and
c^{*}_{\gamma}\to 0
as
\gamma\to 0,
it is clear how to pass to the limit in the first two terms on the left, as well as the term on the right hand side of (
5.7
); it remains to pass to the limit in the term
\int_{\partial D}hc_{\gamma}^{*}\frac{\partial\psi_{d}^{\gamma}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.
The evaluation of this limit is hindered by the fact that
c_{\gamma}^{*}\to 0
while, formally,
\psi_{d}^{\gamma}
blows up. This can however be easily analyzed by integration by parts:

(5.8)

\displaystyle c_{\gamma}^{*}\int_{\partial D}h\frac{\partial\psi_{d}^{\gamma}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}

\displaystyle=c_{\gamma}^{*}\int_{D}h\Delta\psi_{d}^{\gamma}\,dx+c_{\gamma}^{*}\int_{D}\nabla h\cdot\nabla\psi_{d}^{\gamma}\,dx

\displaystyle=-c_{\gamma}^{*}k_{\gamma}^{2}\int_{D}h\psi_{d}^{\gamma}\,dx+c_{\gamma}^{*}\int_{D}\nabla h\cdot\nabla\psi_{d}^{\gamma}\,dx.

Let
\{U_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
denote the eigenfunctions of
-\Delta
in
D
with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions associated with an eigenvalue
\lambda_{*}
; here,
m
denotes the multiplicity of
\lambda_{*},
which is finite by Fredholm theory. Then, using (
5.2
) with
\lambda_{*}-k^{2}=\gamma\not\in\sigma(-\Delta),
one easily finds that

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\gamma\beta_{\gamma}=\lambda_{*}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\int_{D}U_{j}\,dx\right)^{2},

and so, the limit

(5.9)

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\frac{c^{*}_{\gamma}}{\gamma}=-\frac{\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}}{\gamma\beta_{\gamma}}\to-\frac{\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}}{\lambda_{*}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\int_{D}U_{j}\,dx\right)^{2}}=:\overline{C}.

exists and is finite. Since
k_{\gamma}^{2}\to\lambda_{*}
as
\gamma\to 0,
using (
5.9
), it then follows that the right hand side of (
5.8
) converges to

\displaystyle\overline{C}\sum_{j=1}^{m}-\lambda_{*}^{2}\left(\int_{D}U_{j}\,dx\right)\left(\int_{D}hU_{j}\,dx\right)+\lambda_{*}\left(\int_{D}U_{j}\,dx\right)\left(\int_{D}\nabla h\cdot\nabla U_{j}\,dx\right),

which, by another integration by parts, rewrites as

(5.10)

\displaystyle\overline{C}\lambda_{*}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\int_{D}U_{j}\,dx\right)\left(\int_{\partial D}h\frac{\partial U_{j}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\right).

Therefore, sending
\gamma\to 0
in (
5.7
), and using (
5.10
), we find that
\widehat{\phi_{0}}
is the unique solution to the problem

(5.11)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta\widehat{\phi_{0}}=0\quad\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\setminus\overline{D}\\
&\frac{\partial\widehat{\phi_{0}}}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}=\frac{\partial s}{\partial\nu_{\Omega}}\quad\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\\
&\frac{\partial\widehat{\phi_{0}}}{\partial\nu_{D}}=\overline{C}\lambda_{*}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\int_{D}U_{j}\,dx\right)\frac{\partial U_{j}}{\partial\nu_{D}}\quad\quad\mbox{ on }\partial D,\\
&\strokedint_{\Omega\setminus\overline{D}}\widehat{\phi_{0}}\,dx=0.\end{aligned}\right.

In particular, the electric field distribution in the ENZ region, to leading order, is still identified by this procedure. (We note that (
5.11
) does have a solution, since by (
5.9
) the consistency condition for Laplace’s equation with Neumann boundary conditions is satisfied.)

The preceding discussion applies only in the double limit when
\delta\to 0
first then
\gamma\to 0
.
It would be interesting to have a more complete understanding, for example one that applies for
\delta
in a neighborhood of

0
that doesn’t depend on
\gamma
. However this seems to require a method different from that of the present paper.
