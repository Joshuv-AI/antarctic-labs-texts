Decoupling multivariate functions using a non-parametric Filtered CPD approach footnoteinfo This work was supported by the Flemish fund for scientific research FWO under license number G0068.18N.
Jan Decuyper, Koen Tiels, Siep Weiland, Johan Schoukens

Abstract
Black-box model structures are dominated by large multivariate functions. Usually a generic basis function expansion is used, e.g. a polynomial basis, and the parameters of the function are tuned given the data. This is a pragmatic and often necessary step considering the black-box nature of the problem. However, having identified a suitable function, there is no need to stick to the original basis. So-called decoupling techniques aim at translating multivariate functions into an alternative basis, thereby both reducing the number of parameters and retrieving underlying structure. In this work a filtered canonical polyadic decomposition (CPD) is introduced. It is a non-parametric method which is able to retrieve decoupled functions even when facing non-unique decompositions. Tackling this obstacle paves the way for a large number of modelling applications.



keywords

decoupling multivariate functions, CPD, model reduction, nonlinear system identification

Introduction

An essential observation on black-box model structures is that they often consist of a large number of (nonlinear) multivariate functions.
The black-box nature of the problem dictates that the underlying complexity is unknown. It is therefore a very pragmatic and useful idea to construct a basis function expansion and tune the paramaters accordingly. Notice that this is a general practice both for static functions, e.g. multivariate polynomials, as well as for dynamic models, e.g. a
Nonlinear AutoRegressive with eXogenous inputs (NARX)
model where a nonlinear multivariate function maps delayed input and output samples onto the present output
(
Billings 2013
)
. It is in both cases necessary to include ‘sufficient’ terms in order to be able to count on the approximation properties of the model structure. This may lead to an excessive number of parameters. For purposes of analysis there is no need to stick to the particular parametrisation.
By changing the model parametrisation, one may achieve decoupling properties, simplify the parametrisation, and/or enable further analysis.

These insights have led to the development of so-called decoupling techniques. Given a generic multivariate nonlinear function

\bm{q}=\bm{f}(\bm{p})

(1)

with
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\bm{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the prime objective of a decoupling technique is to introduce an appropriate linear transformation of
\bm{p}
, denoted
\bm{V}
, such that the nonlinear relationship may be described by univariate functions in this alternative basis. The decoupled function is then of the following form

\bm{f}(\bm{p})=\bm{W}\bm{g}(\bm{V}^{\text{T}}\bm{p})

(2)

where in the fully decoupled case, the
i
th function is
g_{i}(z_{i})
with
z_{i}=\bm{v}_{i}^{\text{T}}\bm{p}
, emphasising that all functions are strictly univariate. The number of allowed univariate functions, denoted
r
, is crucial since it will determine whether the implied equivalence of (
2

\bm{W}
, maps the function back onto the outputs. The linear transformations then have the following dimensions:
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
and
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
.

It follows from (
2

Dreesen et al. 2015
when proposing a decoupling algorithm. The idea relies on the tri-linear form which arises when computing the Jacobian of the right hand side. Denoting the left and right hand side Jacobian by
\bm{J}
and
\bm{J}^{\prime}
, respectively, we have that

\bm{J}^{\prime}=\bm{W}\operatorname{diag}\left(\begin{bmatrix}h_{1}(z_{1})\quad\cdots\quad h_{r}(z_{r})\end{bmatrix}\right)\bm{V}^{\text{T}}

(3)

in which case
h_{i}(z_{i})\coloneqq\frac{dg_{i}(z_{i})}{dz_{i}}
represents the derivative of the univariate function
g_{i}(z_{i})
with respect to its argument. Evaluating this Jacobian in
N
operating points, i.e. for
\{\bm{p}[1],\cdots,\bm{p}[N]\}
, and collecting the evaluations in a third dimension, expands the data object into in a three-way array
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m\times N}
. The diagonal plane is denoted by
\bm{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times r}
. This is illustrated graphically in Fig.
1

j^{\prime}_{jk\ell}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{ji}~v_{ki}~h_{\ell i},

(4)

is the

(
Kolda and Bader 2009
)
.
The latter is illustrated on the right in Fig.
1
. The decomposed tensor may be written in shorthand notation

\mathcal{J}^{\prime}=\llbracket\bm{W},\bm{V},\bm{H}\rrbracket

(5)

1.

Collect the Jacobian matrices of the known coupled function,

Compute a diagonal tensor decomposition of

{\mathcal{J}\approx\mathcal{J}^{\prime}}

Often a canonical polyadic decomposition or CPD is used. In that case

According to (
3

3.

A final step therefore parametrises the columns

The method of

The equality in (

2.

The uniqueness of the tensor decomposition into its factors,

In this work, a new method is presented, leveraging the powerful idea of decomposing the Jacobian tensor while tackling the challenges of approximate decoupling and non-unique decompositions.

The objective is to have a procedure which returns

,

, and

\underset{\bm{W},\bm{V},\bm{g}}{\operatorname{arg~min}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lVert\bm{f}(\bm{p}[k])-\bm{W}\bm{g}(\bm{V}^{\text{T}}\bm{p}[k])\rVert_{2}^{2},

(6)

thereby loosening the equality of (

Similar to

\underset{\bm{W},\bm{V},\bm{H}}{\operatorname{arg~min}}~\lVert\mathcal{J}-\llbracket\bm{W},\bm{V},\bm{H}\rrbracket\rVert_{F}^{2},

(7)

with the additional requirements that, both for

•

non-exact decompositions, i.e. for values of

Alternative methods that aim at ensuring unique decompositions rely either on introducing information of the Hessian, or on introducing explicit polynomial constraints on the unknown

. In this work a first-order approach is applied, avoiding the prohibitively expensive computation of the Hessian, while resorting to non-parametric methods to ensure smoothness, hence keeping the decomposition independent of any choice of basis expansion.

3
Non-parametric Filtered-CPD

The idea revolves around introducing non-parametric finite difference filters into the decomposition routine of the Jacobian tensor. A finite difference filter is an operation which approximates the derivative operator. For each
i
, consider
N
evaluation points and evaluations
\{z_{i}{[k]},g_{i}{[k]}\}_{k=1}^{N}
, such that
\bm{g}_{i}\coloneqq(g_{i}[k]\mid k=1,\ldots,N),
and
\bm{z}_{i}\coloneqq(z_{i}[k]\mid k=1,\ldots,N)
. Given that
{h_{i}(z_{i})\coloneqq\frac{dg_{i}(z_{i})}{dz_{i}}}
, we can compute a finite difference approximation,
{\bm{h}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}
, by applying a finite difference filter to the vector of evaluations
\bm{g}_{i}
,

\bm{h}_{i}=\bm{S}_{i}^{-1}\bm{D}_{i}\bm{S}_{i}\bm{g}_{i}

(8)

where
\bm{S}_{i}
is a sorting matrix, arranging the elements of
\bm{g}_{i}
in ascending order of
z_{i}
, and
\bm{D}_{i}
embodies the finite difference operator. Should
\bm{z}_{i}
be equidistantly spaced, with a spacing of
\delta_{z_{i}}
,
\bm{D}_{i}
could for instance be defined by the Toeplitz matrix

\bm{D}_{i}=\frac{1}{\delta_{z_{i}}}\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1&-1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&-1&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\dots&0&1&-1\\
0&\cdots&0&0&1\end{array}\right]

(9)

where this particular example is known as a 2-point forward differencing scheme (or a right-derivative approximation). In this context, the term
finite difference filter
, is used to refer to the sequence of operations

\bm{F}\coloneqq\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{D}\bm{S}

(10)

In practice, the elements of
\bm{z}_{i}
, will not be equidistant. A generalisation of
\bm{D}
is provided in Appendix
A
. Introducing finite difference filters will prove beneficial in two ways:

•

It will allow a decomposition of
\mathcal{J}
into
\{\bm{W},\bm{V},\bm{G}\}
, where
\bm{G}
stores evaluations of the functions
g_{i}
, hence removing the need for an integration step (Section
3.1
).

•

It will enable to include a smoothness objective in the decomposition (Sections
3.2
)
(
Decuyper et al. 2019
)
.

3.1
A decomposition into
\bm{W}
,
\bm{V}
, and
\bm{G}

Exploiting the relationship of (
8
) we may propose an alternative factorisation of the Jacobian tensor where the third factor contains
\bm{G}
, i.e a matrix holding evaluations of the
(to be determined)
univariate functions
g_{i}(z_{i})
along its columns. This is in contrast to the factor
\bm{H}
which stores derivative information. The sought after decomposition is then of the form

\mathcal{J}\approx\llbracket\bm{W},\bm{V},\mathcal{F}(\bm{V})\circ\bm{G}\rrbracket,~\text{with}

(11)

\bm{H}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\coloneqq\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{F}(\bm{V})\circ\bm{G}

(12)

The operation ‘
\circ
’ symbolises the columnwise filtering of (
8
),
i.e. computing the finite difference approximation of each branch and storing the results in the columns of
\bm{H}
. For convenience, the finite difference filters, corresponding to each column, are stored in a three-way array
\mathcal{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N\times r}
such that

\mathcal{F}_{[:,:,i]}(\bm{V})\coloneqq\bm{F}_{i}=\bm{S}_{i}^{-1}\bm{D}_{i}\bm{S}_{i}

(13)

Notice the explicit dependence on the factor
\bm{V}
. The factor
\bm{V}
defines the axes on the basis of which the finite difference is computed. Both the sorting matrix
\bm{S}_{i}
, and the finite difference operator
\bm{D}_{i}
, therefore depend on
\bm{V}
(see Appendix
A
). (
12
) may alternatively be written in vectorised form

\operatorname{vec}\left(\bm{H}\right)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\bm{F}_{1}&0&\dots&0\\
0&\bm{F}_{2}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\cdots&0&\bm{F}_{r}\end{array}\right]\operatorname{vec}\left(\bm{G}\right)

(14)

where the vec operator transforms a matrix into a vector by concatenating its columns.
Given that the original decomposition
\{\bm{W},\bm{V},\bm{H}\}
has been translated to
\{\bm{W},\bm{V},\bm{G}\}
, also the smoothness requirement, which was formulated in Section
2
, is transferred onto
\bm{G}
.

3.2
Adding a smoothness objective

Smoothness and finite differences are intimately linked, e.g. abrupt local changes will be emphasised by a finite difference filter. Appendix
A

We will introduce an alternating-least-squares (ALS) algorithm in order to solve the decomposition

\mathcal{J}\approx\llbracket\bm{W},\bm{V},\mathcal{F}_{C}(\bm{V})\circ\bm{G}\rrbracket

(15)

The matricisations of the third order tensor along its rows, columns and tubes will be denoted by
\bm{J}_{(1)},\bm{J}_{(2)},\bm{J}_{(3)}
, respectively, and ‘
\odot
’ will denote the Khatri-Rao product. We may then write
(
Kolda and Bader 2009
)

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{(1)}

\displaystyle\approx\bm{W}((\mathcal{F}_{C}(\bm{V})\circ\bm{G})\odot\bm{V})^{\text{T}}

(16a)

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{(2)}

\displaystyle\approx\bm{V}((\mathcal{F}_{C}(\bm{V})\circ\bm{G})\odot\bm{W})^{\text{T}}

(16b)

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{(3)}

\displaystyle\approx(\mathcal{F}_{C}(\bm{V})\circ\bm{G})(\bm{V}\odot\bm{W})^{\text{T}}

(16c)

In the first step of the procedure the factors
\{\bm{W},\bm{V},\bm{G}\}
are initialised with random numbers. Following the ALS approach, each factor is then updated while treating both others as constants. This leads to the following objective functions and update formulas:

1.

Update
\bm{W}\rightarrow\bm{W}^{+}

From Eq.(
16a
) we infer the following objective

\underset{\bm{W}}{\operatorname{arg~min}}~\|\bm{J}_{(1)}-\bm{W}((\mathcal{F}_{C}(\bm{V})\circ\bm{G})\odot\bm{V})^{\text{T}}\|_{F}^{2}

(17)

For convenience we will denote
{\bm{H}_{C}\coloneqq\mathcal{F}_{C}(\bm{V})\circ\bm{G}}
. Given that
\bm{W}
appears linearly in the objective, an analytical update formula is obtained,

\bm{W}^{+}=\bm{J}_{(1)}\left(\left(\bm{H}_{C}\odot\bm{V}\right)^{\text{T}}\right)^{\dagger}

(18)

Using the property

(\bm{A}\odot\bm{B})^{\text{T}}(\bm{A}\odot\bm{B})=\left(\bm{A}^{\text{T}}\bm{A}\right)*\left(\bm{B}^{\text{T}}\bm{B}\right)

(19)

with ‘
*
’ denoting the Hadamard product, leads to the more efficient update formula, requiring only the inversion of an
r\times r
matrix

\bm{W}^{+}=\bm{J}_{(1)}\left(\bm{H}_{C}\odot\bm{V}\right)\left(\left(\bm{H}_{C}^{\text{T}}\bm{H}_{C}\right)*\left(\bm{V}^{\text{T}}\bm{V}\right)\right)^{\dagger}

(20)

2.

Update
\bm{V}\rightarrow\bm{V}^{+}

Apart from the tensor approximation objective, which is implied by (
16b
), the update of
\bm{V}
must also take into account the smoothness of
\bm{G}
. The smoothness is indeed affected by
\bm{V}
since
\bm{V}
defines the
z

\bm{z}=\bm{V}^{\text{T}}\bm{p}
(see Fig.
2
). The smoothness objective is therefore explicitly added using regularisation,

\begin{split}\underset{\bm{V}}{\operatorname{arg~min}}&~\|\bm{J}_{(2)}-\bm{V}((\mathcal{F}_{C}(\bm{V})\circ\bm{G})\odot\bm{W})^{\text{T}}\|_{F}^{2}\\
&+\lambda\|\left(\mathcal{F}_{L}(\bm{V})\circ\bm{G}\right)-\left(\mathcal{F}_{R}(\bm{V})\circ\bm{G}\right)\|_{F}^{2}\end{split}

(21)

where
\lambda
is a hyperparameter which balances both objectives. The additional term penalises divergent results from a left and a right finite difference filtering operation, ultimately steering the optimisation towards smooth solutions
2
2

2

In order to promote smoothness equally on all the columns of
\bm{G}
, a normalisation is required. Denoting
{\bm{H}_{L}\coloneqq\mathcal{F}_{L}\circ\bm{G}}
, and
{\bm{H}_{R}\coloneqq\mathcal{F}_{R}\circ\bm{G}}
, all columns are normalised by their rms value, i.e.
\bm{h}_{L_{i}}/\text{rms}(\bm{h}_{L_{i}})
, and
\bm{h}_{R_{i}}/\text{rms}(\bm{h}_{R_{i}})
.
. Given that
\bm{V}
appears nonlinearly in (
21
), nonlinear optimisation is required to obtain
\bm{V}^{+}
.

3.

Update
\bm{G}\rightarrow\bm{G}^{+}

In analogy with (
21
), the update formula of
\bm{G}
is also found from a joint objective function.

\begin{split}\underset{\bm{G}}{\operatorname{arg~min}}&~\|\bm{J}_{(3)}-(\mathcal{F}_{C}(\bm{V})\circ\bm{G})(\bm{V}\odot\bm{W})^{\text{T}}\|_{F}^{2}\\
&+\lambda\|\left(\mathcal{F}_{L}(\bm{V})\circ\bm{G}\right)-\left(\mathcal{F}_{R}(\bm{V})\circ\bm{G}\right)\|_{F}^{2}\end{split}

(22)

It can be shown that
\bm{G}
appears linearly in (
22
). This allows for an analytical update formula (to be consulted in Appendix
B
).

Steps (1) to (3) are iterated until convergence is reached or a stopping criteria is met. In each step the updated factors are passed on to the next. The appropriate value of
\lambda
is to be determined from a line search.
The univariate functions
g_{i}(z_{i})
are ultimately obtained from parameterising the columns
\bm{g}_{i}
using a basis expansion which can be freely chosen by the user.

5
Conclusion

Decoupling multivariate functions is a useful model reduction technique. It reveals underlying structure while potentially reducing the number of parameters. An established procedure is to compute a collection of Jacobian matrices, store them in a three-way array, and resort to a diagonal tensor decomposition to retrieve the decoupled function. In practice, however, the tensor decomposition tends to be non-unique, preventing the retrieval of an appropriate solution. In this work, non-parametric finite difference filters are used to promote smooth solutions. Smoothness ensures that the decomposition points to a decoupled function. A toy problem illustrates that accurate decoupled forms can be retrieved.

Appendix A
Non-equidistant finite differences

A finite difference expression for non-equidistantly spaced points can be obtained through Lagrange interpolation
(
Singh and Bhadauri 2009
)
. Consider
\bm{z}_{w}
to be a window of
k
neighbouring elements of
z
and let
\bm{g}_{w}
be the corresponding window of function evaluations. The Lagrange polynomial is the polynomial of lowest degree which interpolates all
k
points within the window. It is a linear combination of basis functions

L(z)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\bm{g}_{w}[j]l_{j}(z)

(26)

where
l_{j}
are known as Lagrange basis polynomials

l_{j}(z)\coloneqq\prod_{\begin{matrix}1\leq i\leq k\\
i\neq j\end{matrix}}\frac{z-\bm{z}_{w}[i]}{\bm{z}_{w}[j]-\bm{z}_{w}[i]}

(27)

At the grid points
\bm{z}_{w}
, the basis polynomials have the following two properties:

1.

For all
s\neq j
the product will be zero since the numerator
\bm{z}_{w}[s]-\bm{z}_{w}[i]
attains zero at
s=i
. Hence
\forall(s\neq j):

l_{j}(\bm{z}_{w}[s])=\prod_{\begin{matrix}1\leq i\leq k\\
i\neq j\end{matrix}}\frac{\bm{z}_{w}[s]-\bm{z}_{w}[i]}{\bm{z}_{w}[j]-\bm{z}_{w}[i]}=0

(28)

2.

On the other hand

l_{j}(\bm{z}_{w}[j])=\prod_{\begin{matrix}1\leq i\leq k\\
i\neq j\end{matrix}}\frac{\bm{z}_{w}[j]-\bm{z}_{w}[i]}{\bm{z}_{w}[j]-\bm{z}_{w}[i]}=1

(29)

Given that all basis polynomials are zero when
{s\neq j}
, and since they equal to one for
s=j
, it follows that
L(\bm{z}_{w}[j])=\bm{g}_{w}[j]
, meaning that
L(z)
passes exactly through all
k
points in the window. Having found a function which relates neighbouring points, also a derivative relationship between neighbouring points (i.e. a finite difference) may be obtained. A first order derivative expression is given by

L^{(1)}(z)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\bm{g}_{w}[j]l^{(1)}_{j}(z)

(30)

which is a weighted sum with the weights following from
l^{(1)}_{j}(z)\coloneqq\frac{dl_{j}(z)}{dz}

l^{(1)}_{j}(z)\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{matrix}s=1\\
s\neq j\end{matrix}}^{k}\left[\frac{1}{\bm{z}_{w}[j]-\bm{z}_{w}[s]}\prod_{\begin{matrix}i=1\\
i\neq(s,j)\end{matrix}}^{k}\frac{z-\bm{z}_{w}[i]}{\bm{z}_{w}[j]-\bm{z}_{w}[i]}\right]

(31)

Notice that the linear combination, expressed by (
30
), corresponds to the operations encoded in the rows of the matrix
\bm{D}
(
9
). When computing a 3-points finite difference,
k=3
, the weights
l^{(1)}_{j}(z)
with
j=1,\ldots,k
form the elements of
\bm{D}
. Contrary to (
9
), all weights may differ from one another since they are computed on the basis of the non-equidistant spacing of the
k
points in
\bm{z}_{w}
. Going from one row to the next may be seen as sliding the window of the finite difference operator over the
z
-axis. The positioning of the window allows switching between forward, central, and backward differencing, additionally also the length may be changed. This flexibility enables to compute a number of alternative finite difference filters, which are all approximations of the first order derivative, e.g. a
3-points central finite difference filter
. The
i
th row of a 3-points (
k=3
) central differencing filter of size
N
, denoted
\bm{D}_{C}
, is based on the window
\bm{z}_{w_{i}}=\left[\bm{z}_{s}[i-1]\quad\bm{z}_{s}[i]\quad\bm{z}_{s}[i+1]\right]^{\text{T}}
, with
\bm{z}_{s}
the sorted version of
\bm{z}
. The weights are accordingly found by evaluating the basis functions
l_{j}^{(1)}(z)
in the central point of the window, i.e.
l_{j}^{(1)}(\bm{z}_{w_{i}}[2])
with
j=1,\ldots,k
. The following filter is obtained:

\bm{D}_{C}\coloneqq\left[\begin{array}[]{cccccccc}l_{1}^{(1)}(\bm{z}_{w_{1}}[1])&l_{2}^{(1)}(\bm{z}_{w_{1}}[1])&l_{3}^{(1)}(\bm{z}_{w_{1}}[1])&0&\dots&0\\
l_{1}^{(1)}(\bm{z}_{w_{2}}[2])&l_{2}^{(1)}(\bm{z}_{w_{2}}[2])&l_{3}^{(1)}(\bm{z}_{w_{2}}[2])&0&\dots&0\\
0&l_{1}^{(1)}(\bm{z}_{w_{3}}[2])&l_{2}^{(1)}(\bm{z}_{w_{3}}[2])&l_{3}^{(1)}(\bm{z}_{w_{3}}[2])&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\dots&0&l_{1}^{(1)}(\bm{z}_{w_{N-1}}[2])&l_{2}^{(1)}(\bm{z}_{w_{N-1}}[2])&l_{3}^{(1)}(\bm{z}_{w_{N-1}}[2])\\
0&\dots&0&l_{1}^{(1)}(\bm{z}_{w_{N}}[3])&l_{2}^{(1)}(\bm{z}_{w_{N}}[3])&l_{3}^{(1)}(\bm{z}_{w_{N}}[3])\\
\end{array}\right]

(32)

The first and the last row face a boundary and are treated as 3-points right (backward) and 3-points left (forward) filters, correspondingly. In an analogue way also a left and a right derivative approximation may be constructed by shifting the window left or right of the diagonal.

Appendix B
Update formula of
\bm{G}

Lemma 1.
Let (
22
) be the objective function from which
\bm{G}
is to be updated. The claim is that
\bm{G}
appears linearly in this joint objective such that an analytical update formula may be derived.

\begin{split}\underset{\bm{G}}{\operatorname{arg~min}}&~\left\|\bm{J}_{(3)}-(\mathcal{F}_{C}(\bm{V})\circ\bm{G})(\bm{V}\odot\bm{W})^{\text{T}}\right\|_{F}^{2}\\
&+\lambda\|\left(\mathcal{F}_{L}(\bm{V})\circ\bm{G}\right)-\left(\mathcal{F}_{R}(\bm{V})\circ\bm{G}\right)\|_{F}^{2}\end{split}

(
22
)

Proof:
Consider the vectorised form of the objective function. For clarity the dependence on
\bm{V}
is dropped, i.e.
\mathcal{F}_{C}(\bm{V})=\mathcal{F}_{C}
.

\begin{split}&\underset{\bm{G}}{\operatorname{arg~min}}~\left\|\text{vec}(\bm{J}_{(3)})-\operatorname{vec}\left(\left(\mathcal{F}_{C}\circ\bm{G}\right)\left(\bm{V}\odot\bm{W}\right)^{\text{T}}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}\\
&+\lambda\|\text{vec}\left(\mathcal{F}_{L}\circ\bm{G}\right)-\text{vec}\left(\mathcal{F}_{R}\circ\bm{G}\right)\|_{F}^{2}\end{split}

(33)

Denoting the Knonecker product by ‘
\otimes
’ we may use the property,
\text{vec}\left(\bm{A}\bm{X}\bm{B}\right)=(\bm{B}^{\text{T}}\otimes\bm{A})\text{vec}(\bm{X})
. With
\bm{A}=\bm{I}_{N}
,
\bm{B}=\left(\bm{V}\odot\bm{W}\right)^{\text{T}}
and
\bm{X}=\left(\mathcal{F}_{C}\circ\bm{G}\right)
we have that

\begin{split}\operatorname{vec}&\left(\left(\mathcal{F}_{C}\circ\bm{G}\right)\left(\bm{V}\odot\bm{W}\right)^{\text{T}}\right)=\\
&\quad\quad\quad\left(\left(\bm{V}\odot\bm{W}\right)\otimes\bm{I}_{N}\right)\text{vec}\left(\mathcal{F}_{C}\circ\bm{G}\right)\end{split}

(34)

Substituting (
34
) and applying the property of (
14
), the objective is rewritten into

\begin{split}&\underset{\bm{G}}{\operatorname{arg~min}}~\left\|\text{vec}(\bm{J}_{(3)})\right.\\
&-\left.\left(\left(\bm{V}\odot\bm{W}\right)\otimes\bm{I}_{N}\right)\text{blkdiag}\left(\mathcal{F}_{C}\right)\text{vec}\left(\bm{G}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}\\
&+\lambda\|\left(\text{blkdiag}\left(\mathcal{F}_{L}\right)-\text{blkdiag}\left(\mathcal{F}_{R}\right)\right)\text{vec}(\bm{G})\|_{F}^{2}\end{split}

(35)

Reordering both terms leads to the expression

\begin{split}&\underset{\bm{G}}{\operatorname{arg~min}}~\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\text{vec}(\bm{J}_{(3)})\\
\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\right.\\
&-\left.\begin{bmatrix}\left(\left(\bm{V}\odot\bm{W}\right)\otimes\bm{I}_{N}\right)\text{blkdiag}\left(\mathcal{F}_{C}\right)\\
\lambda\left(\text{blkdiag}\left(\mathcal{F}_{L}\right)-\text{blkdiag}\left(\mathcal{F}_{R}\right)\right)\end{bmatrix}\text{vec}(\bm{G})\right\|_{F}^{2}\end{split}

(36)

where
\bm{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{rN}
is a vector of zeros and from which it is clear that
\text{vec}(\bm{G})
enters linearly in the objective. ∎

An analytical update formula is then obtained by defining

\bm{K}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\left(\left(\bm{V}\odot\bm{W}\right)\otimes\bm{I}_{N}\right)\text{blkdiag}\left(\mathcal{F}_{C}\right)\\
\lambda\left(\text{blkdiag}\left(\mathcal{F}_{L}\right)-\text{blkdiag}\left(\mathcal{F}_{R}\right)\right)\end{bmatrix},

(37)

leading to the least-squares solution

\text{vec}(\bm{G}^{+})=\bm{K}^{\dagger}\begin{bmatrix}\text{vec}(\bm{J}_{(3)})\\
\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}

(38)

To ensure that smoothness is promoted equally on all columns of
\bm{G}
, a normalisation is required (similar to the update of
\bm{V}
). The block diagonal filters are therefore scaled by the root-mean-squared value of their corresponding columns.

\text{blkdiag}(\mathcal{F}_{L})=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\frac{\bm{F}_{L_{1}}}{\text{rms}(\bm{h}_{L_{1}})}&0&\dots&0\\
0&\frac{\bm{F}_{L_{2}}}{\text{rms}(\bm{h}_{L_{2}})}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\cdots&0&\frac{\bm{F}_{L_{r}}}{\text{rms}(\bm{h}_{L_{r}})}\end{array}\right]

(39)

In an analogue way
\text{blkdiag}(\mathcal{F}_{R})
is normalised.
