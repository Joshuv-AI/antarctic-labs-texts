The directed landscape
Duncan Dauvergne, Janosch Ortmann, Bálint Virág

Abstract
The conjectured limit of last passage percolation is a scale-invariant, independent, stationary increment process with respect to metric composition. We prove this for Brownian last passage percolation. We construct the Airy sheet and characterize it in terms of the Airy line ensemble. We also show that last passage geodesics converge to random functions with Hölder- 2 / 3 − 2/3^{-} continuous paths. This work completes the construction of the central object in the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang universality class, the directed landscape.

Introduction

Figure 1
:
An example of last passage across three functions. The purple path is the last passage path from
(x,3)
to
(y,1)
. It can be viewed as either maximizing the sum of increments along the path, or minimizing the sum of gaps. We will always think of our sequences of functions as being labelled so that
f_{i}
sits above
f_{i+1}
. Our notions of ‘right’ and ‘left’ in the paper are with respect to this picture.

For a sequence of differentiable real-valued functions
f=(\dots,f_{-1},f_{0},f_{1},\dots)
with domain
\mathbb{R}
, and coordinates
x\leq y
and
n\leq m
, define the last passage value as

f[(x,m)\rightarrow(y,n)]=\sup_{\pi}\int_{x}^{y}f^{\prime}_{\pi(t)}(t)\,dt.

(1)

Here the supremum is over nonincreasing functions
\pi:[x,y]\to\mathbb{Z}
with
\pi(x)=m
and
\pi(y)=n
. The integral is just a sum of increments of
f
(see Figure
1
), so the same can be defined for continuous
f
, in particular for a sequence
B
of independent two-sided Brownian motions. This model, Brownian last passage percolation, is a representative of a class of models that have been the focus of intense research in recent years.
By continuity, optimizing paths exist in (
1
); let
\pi_{n}
denote one for
B[(0,n)\rightarrow(1,1)]
. As one of the highlights of this paper, we show that
\pi_{n}
has a scaling limit.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists a random continuous function
\Pi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
and a new coupling of all the paths
\pi_{n}
such that

\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\left|\frac{\pi_{n}(s)-n(1-s)}{2n^{2/3}}-\Pi(s)\right|\to 0\qquad\text{ almost surely. }

The limit
\Pi
is the directed geodesic, a random Hölder-
2/3^{-}
continuous function defined in terms of a new limiting object, the directed landscape. The directed landscape is the full four-parameter scaling limit of Brownian last passage percolation, see Definition
1.4
. To describe it completely, we must first discuss the parabolic Airy line ensemble.

The parabolic Airy line ensemble is a random sequence of ordered functions
\mathcal{A}_{1}>\mathcal{A}_{2}>\dots
. The shifted process
\{\mathcal{A}_{i}(t)+t^{2}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
, constructed by
Prähofer and Spohn 2002
via a determinantal formula, is stationary. The process
\mathcal{A}_{1}(t)+t^{2}
is known as the Airy process (sometimes Airy
2
) and describes the large-
n
scaling limit of the function
y\mapsto B[(0,n)\rightarrow(y,1)]
. The remaining lines have interpretations in terms of last passage percolation with multiple disjoint paths. The process
\mathcal{A}
satisfies an important Brownian Gibbs property which allows for a probabilistic understanding of the object. This was shown by

Corwin and Hammond 2014
. They used this property to rigorously show that Airy lines are nonintersecting and locally absolutely continuous with respect to Brownian motion with variance
2
.
For brevity, in the remainder of the paper we often omit the word parabolic and simply refer to
\mathcal{A}
as the Airy line ensemble.

The Airy line ensemble doubles as the limiting eigenvalue process of Brownian motion on Hermitian matrices. Construction of the Airy sheet, the scaling limit of the two-parameter function
(x,y)\mapsto B[(x,n)\rightarrow(y,1)]

(conjectured in
Corwin et al. 2015
)
does not follow from the integrable methods that give convergence to the Airy line ensemble. This is partly because the Airy sheet seems to be fundamentally different from random matrix limits. As the first step in our construction of the directed landscape, we show that the Airy sheet law can be described in terms of last passage percolation across the Airy line ensemble.

Definition 1.2
.

The
Airy sheet
is a random continuous function
\mathcal{S}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
so that

(i)

\mathcal{S}
has the same law as
\mathcal{S}(\cdot+t,\cdot+t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

(ii)

\mathcal{S}
can be coupled with a parabolic Airy line ensemble so that
\mathcal{S}(0,\cdot)=\mathcal{A}_{1}(\cdot)
and for all
(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}\times\mathbb{Q}^{2}
there exists a random integer
K_{x,y,z}
such that for all
k\geq K_{x,y,z}
almost surely

\mathcal{A}[(-\sqrt{k/(2x)},k)\rightarrow(z,1)]-\mathcal{A}[(-\sqrt{k/(2x)},k)\rightarrow(y,1)]=\mathcal{S}(x,z)-\mathcal{S}(x,y).

(2)

Theorem 1.3
.

The Airy sheet exists and is unique in law. Moreover, for every
n
, there exists a coupling so that

B[(2x/n^{1/3},n)\rightarrow(1+2y/n^{1/3},1)]=2\sqrt{n}+2(y-x)n^{1/6}+n^{-1/6}(\mathcal{S}+o_{n})(x,y),

where
o_{n}
are random functions asymptotically small in the sense that on every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
there exists
a>1
with

\mathbb{E}a^{\sup_{K}|o_{n}|^{3/2}}\to 1
.

Remark 1.1
.

1.

We prove in Proposition
8.2
that Definition
1.2
uniquely determines a probability measure on random continuous functions. In other words, the Airy sheet, as defined here, exists and is unique in law. The Airy sheet exists because it is the limit of Brownian last passage percolation, see Theorem
8.3
. Theorem
1.3
is the combination of these two results.

2.

Equation (
2
) can be loosely interpreted as saying that the Airy sheet value
\mathcal{S}(x,y)
is the renormalized limit, as
k\to\infty
, of the last passage value in
\mathcal{A}
from
(-\sqrt{k/(2x)},k)
to
(y,1)
. While we were not able to prove that such a limit exists, we believe that such a description is possible, see Conjecture
14.2
. Instead, we make sense of this picture by analyzing differences of last passage values. See Remark
8.2
for more discussion about why it is easier to look at differences.

3.

We show in the preprint
Dauvergne and Virág 2021b
that the Airy sheet is also the limit of classical integrable models of last passage percolation: geometric, exponential, and Poisson models. We expect it to be a universal limit object in the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) universality class, see
Corwin 2016
for an informal description. We prove convergence of Brownian last passage percolation to the Airy sheet in this paper. However, one consequence of the work
Dauvergne and Virág 2021b
is that any limiting formula established in one of these models will apply to all others as well.

4.

Definition
1.2
is not in terms of explicit formulas for distributions, which has traditionally been the main approach to KPZ limits. A celebrated example of such a definition in a different context is that of the Schramm-Loewner evolution. As is the case there, what may be more desirable than exact formulas is a set of tools to work directly with this limiting object. In this paper we concentrate on establishing the Airy sheet and the directed landscape as a limit; in upcoming work we use the present description of the Airy sheet and the directed landscape to understand the limiting geometry.

5.

Tightness for the Airy sheet limit for certain models is known, e.g. see
Pimentel 2018
; a short proof for Brownian last passage percolation is provided in Lemma
8.4
. Uniqueness of the limit is much harder, and is one of the main results of this paper. Theorem
1.3
says that Brownian last passage percolation looks the same on all scales in a precise sense.

6.

The process
\mathcal{S}(x,y)+(x-y)^{2}
is stationary in both variables, see Lemma
9.1
, and
\mathcal{S}(0,0)
has GUE Tracy-Widom distribution. The GUE Tracy-Widom limit for
\mathcal{S}(0,0)
is well known; for Brownian last passage percolation it was independently shown by
Baryshnikov 2001
and
Gravner et al. 2001
.

7.

By monotonicity, (
2
) is equivalent to the following Busemann function definition. For every triple
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the left hand side of (
2
) converges to the right hand side as
k\to\infty
, see Remark
8.1
. Busemann functions have been used previously in last passage percolation to study problems around infinite geodesics, e.g. see
Cator and Pimentel 2012
,
Georgiou et al. 2017
.

A direct consequence of Theorem
1.3
is the celebrated 1-2-3 (or KPZ) scaling for Airy sheets. Define the
Airy sheet of scale
s
by

\mathcal{S}_{s}(x,y)=s\,\mathcal{S}(x/s^{2},y/s^{2}).

Let
\mathcal{S}_{s},\,\mathcal{S}_{t}
be independent Airy sheets of scale
s
and
t
. Then the
metric composition
is an Airy sheet of
scale
r
:

\mathcal{S}_{r}(x,z)=\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{S}_{s}(x,y)+\mathcal{S}_{t}(y,z),\qquad\text{ with }r^{3}=s^{3}+t^{3}.

The metric composition law is a semigroup property for the max-plus algebra. The Airy sheet is an analogue of the Gaussian distribution in this semigroup, inspiring a definition of the analogue of Brownian motion there. It is natural to have a parameter space, directed
\mathbb{R}^{4}
,

\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}=\{(x,s;y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:s<t\}

representing increments from time
s
to time
t
. We will think of
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
as representing ordered pairs of points in spacetime with a one-dimensional space. The coordinates
x
and
y
are spatial and the coordinates
s
and
t
are temporal.

Definition 1.4
.

The
directed landscape
is a random continuous function
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the metric composition law

\mathcal{L}(x,r;y,t)=\max_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{L}(x,r;z,s)+\mathcal{L}(z,s;y,t)\qquad\text{ for all }\;(x,r;y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow},\;s\in(r,t),

(3)

and with the property that
\mathcal{L}(\cdot,t_{i};\cdot,t_{i}+s_{i}^{3})

are independent Airy sheets of scale
s_{i}
for any set of disjoint time intervals
(t_{i},t_{i}+s_{i}^{3})
.

Figure 2
:
An illustration of the metric composition law for the directed landscape. The geodesic from
(x,r)
to
(y,t)
passes through a point
(z^{*},s)
for some
z^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
, giving that the right hand side of (
3
) is greater than or equal the left. See (
5
) and surrounding discussion for a precise definition of a geodesic in
\mathcal{L}
. Also, for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
, a concatenation of the geodesic from
(x,r)
to
(z,s)
with the geodesic from
(z,s)
to
(y,t)
gives a candidate for a maximizing path from
(x,r)
to
(y,t)
, yielding the opposite inequality. The original Brownian lines that give rise to
\mathcal{L}(\cdot,r;\cdot,s)
and
\mathcal{L}(\cdot,s;\cdot,t)
are independent. This allows us to build up
\mathcal{L}
at any finite set of times using metric composition with independent increments, and hence construct the directed landscape from independent Airy sheets analogously to the construction of Brownian motion.

Remark 1.2
.

1.

We will prove in Section
10
that Definition
1.4
uniquely determines a probability measure on random continuous functions from
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
to
\mathbb{R}
. See Figure
2
for an illustration of the construction.

2.

We show in the preprint
Dauvergne and Virág 2021b
that the directed landscape is also the limit of classical integrable models of last passage percolation: geometric, exponential, and Poisson models. We expect it to be a universal limit object in the KPZ universality class, capturing all important limiting information.
We prove the Brownian last passage case in this paper. One consequence of the work
Dauvergne and Virág 2021b
is that any limiting formula established in one of these models will apply to all others as well.

3.

Independent increment processes on semigroups are more complicated than those on groups; for example, for Brownian motion,
B(s)-B(0)
and
B(t)-B(0)
clearly determine the increment
B(s)-B(t)
. In semigroups, the increments cannot be computed in this way and have to be specified for all pairs of times
s<t
. This what the directed landscape does for the metric composition semigroup.

4.

The two-time formula of
Johansson 2017
gives the joint distribution of any pair of directed landscape values of the form
\big(\mathcal{L}(x,r;y_{1},s_{1}),\mathcal{L}(x,r;y_{2},s_{2})\big)
.
Johansson and Rahman 2019
and
Liu 2019
independently extended this to multiple endpoints
(y_{i},s_{i})
.

5.

The KPZ fixed point of
Matetski et al. 2016
is a Markov process in
t
which can be written in terms of the directed landscape and its initial condition
h_{0}
as

h_{t}(y)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}h_{0}(x)+\mathcal{L}(x,0;y,t).

(4)

Matetski et al. 2016
first established the KPZ fixed point
h
as the limit of TASEP i.e. essentially exponential last passage percolation. They showed that the finite dimensional distributions of
h
can be expressed in terms of a tractable Fredholm determinant formula involving Brownian hitting probabilities. Recently,
Nica et al. 2020
showed that Brownian last passage percolation also converges to the KPZ fixed point, thus rigorously showing (
4
).

6.

The directed landscape contains more information than the KPZ fixed point, namely the joint distribution of the coupled evolution for all initial conditions. This allows for a full description of the law of limiting geodesics as in Theorem
1.1
. As will be shown in upcoming work, this also allows for a description of the scaling limit of TASEP second class particle trajectories.

7.

Since convergence to the directed landscape is uniform on compact sets and the directed landscape is continuous, this immediately implies that last passage values along any space-like curve converge uniformly to an Airy process. Previous approaches to such results include finding explicit determinantal formulas for space-like curves, see
Borodin and Olshanski 2006
and
Borodin and Ferrari 2008
, and geometric analysis of slow decorrelations, see
Ferrari 2008
and
Corwin et al. 2012
.

8.

The negative of the directed landscape can be thought of as a “signed directed metric” on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
; it satisfies the triangle inequality for points in the right time order. Signed directed metrics occur naturally in fields such as geometry, e.g. Perelman’s
\mathcal{L}
-distance, see
Perelman 2002
.

Letting
(x,s)_{n}=(s+2x/n^{1/3},-\lfloor sn\rfloor)

Theorem 1.5

(Full scaling limit of Brownian last passage percolation)
.

There exists a coupling of Brownian last passage percolation and the directed landscape

B_{n}[(x,s)_{n}\rightarrow(y,t)_{n}]\;\;=\;\;2(t-s)\sqrt{n}+2(y-x)n^{1/6}+n^{-1/6}(\mathcal{L}+o_{n})(x,s;y,t).

Here each

is a sequence of independent Brownian motions. Each

is a random function asymptotically small in the sense that on every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
there exists
a>1

\mathbb{E}a^{\sup_{K}|o_{n}|^{3/4}}\to 1
.

Theorem
1.5
is restated and proven in the body as Theorem

We have strong control over the modulus of continuity of the directed landscape. In this next proposition and throughout the paper, by a
random constant
we simply mean an almost surely finite random variable.

Proposition 1.6
.

Let
\mathcal{R}(x,t;y,t+s)=\mathcal{L}(x,t;y,t+s)+(x-y)^{2}/s

|\mathcal{R}(u)-\mathcal{R}(v)|\leq C\left(\tau^{1/3}\log^{2/3}(\tau^{-1}+1)+\xi^{1/2}\log^{1/2}(\xi^{-1}+1)\right).

for all
\xi,\tau>0
and points
u,v\in K
with spatial and temporal coordinates of distance at most
\xi,\tau
respectively. Here

C
is a random constant depending on
K
with
\mathbb{E}a^{C^{3/2}}<\infty
for some
a>1
.

See Proposition
10.5
for a version of Proposition
1.6
that keeps track of the dependence on the compact set.
The spatial fluctuations of
\mathcal{L}
have been known to be locally Brownian since
Corwin and Hammond 2014
. The temporal modulus of continuity has also been previously obtained in the context of Poisson last passage percolation, see
Hammond and Sarkar 2020
, building on related work from
Basu et al. 2014
and
Basu et al. 2019

The directed landscape is a rich object containing all asymptotic information about last passage percolation in this scaling. In particular, as advertised above, we can take limits of last passage paths.
For a continuous path
h:[t,s]\to\mathbb{R}
, define the
length
of
h
by

\int d\mathcal{L}\circ h=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\;\;\inf_{t=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{k}=s}\;\;\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{L}(h(t_{i-1}),t_{i-1};h(t_{i}),t_{i}).

(5)

This is the analogue of defining the length of a curve in Euclidean space by piecewise linear approximation. We call
h
a
directed geodesic
if equality holds for all subdivisions before taking any infima. We show that with probability one, directed geodesics exist between every pair of endpoint, see Lemma
13.2
.
Moreover, the directed geodesic between any fixed pair of endpoints is almost surely unique and Hölder-
2/3^{-}
continuous. Note that uniqueness may fail for some exceptional pairs. In particular, the directed geodesic is more regular than a Brownian path!

Theorem 1.7

(Continuity of directed geodesics)
.

Fix
u=(x,t;y,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
. Then almost surely, there is a unique directed geodesic
\Pi_{u}
from
(x,t)
to
(y,s)
. Its distribution
only depends on
u
through scaling: as random continuous functions from
[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\Pi_{(x,t;y,s)}(s+(t-s)r)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\Pi_{(0,0;0,1)}(r)+x+(y-x)r.

Moreover, for
u=(0,0;0,1)
we have

|\Pi_{u}(t+s)-\Pi_{u}(t)|\leq Cs^{2/3}\log^{1/3}(2/s)

for all
s>0
with
t,t+s\in[0,1]
. The random constant satisfies
\mathbb{E}a^{C^{3}}<\infty
for some
a>1
.

is proven in Section
12
.
For
u=(x,s;y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}

Our approach to the proofs is probabilistic. It is based on understanding the geometry of last passage percolation using a continuous version of the Robinson-Schensted-Knuth (RSK) correspondence. Our reliance on formulas is minimal – we only
use estimates about the Airy line ensemble from
Dauvergne and Virág 2021a
, which rely solely on the determinantal nature of the Airy point process and the Brownian Gibbs property of the Airy line ensemble. On the other hand, our results imply convergence of formulas. For example, the super-exponential control of the error term in Theorem
1.3
guarantees uniform convergence of moment generating functions.

The starting point for our proofs is a combinatorial fact about the continuous RSK correspondence. This correspondence and its application to Brownian paths was developed in
O’Connell and Yor 2002
and
O’Connell 2003
. The continuous RSK correspondence maps an
n
-tuple of continuous functions
f:[0,1]\times\{1,\ldots,n\}\to\mathbb{R},(x,i)\mapsto f_{i}(x)
to an
n
-tuple
Wf
in the same space, which we call the melon of
f
. The functions in the melon are ordered decreasingly:
Wf_{1}\geq Wf_{2}\geq\dots\geq Wf_{n}
. The main property used in the last passage literature is that the melon satisfies

(Wf)_{1}(t)=f[(0,n)\rightarrow(t,1)].

(6)

We use the stronger fact that for any
s<t
, remarkably

Wf[(s,n)\rightarrow(t,1)]=f[(s,n)\rightarrow(t,1)].

(7)

The identity (
6
) is equivalent to (
7
) when
s=0
by the ordering of
Wf
. A generalization of (
7
) to
k
disjoint paths with arbitrary start and end points
(s_{i},n),(t_{i},1)
for
i=1,\dots,k
is proven as Proposition
4.1
. This proposition is the crucial deterministic input in our construction of the Airy sheet, and we use it throughout the first part of the paper.

Long after completing the first version of the paper, we learned that Proposition
4.1
was proven in
Biane et al. 2005
, Lemma 4.8, for the case of disjoint paths with all equal start and end points.
Noumi et al. 2004
also obtained close relatives of this result in a discrete setting, see Theorem 1.7.
A version of this formula in the special case when
s_{i}=0
for all
i
was previously obtained and studied in the planar positive temperature setting by
O’Connell and Warren 2016
, see the discussion directly below equation (20) and Theorem 3.4 from that paper.

With
B
restricted to the first
n
lines,
WB
has the law of Brownian motions conditioned not to intersect; these converge to the Airy line ensemble in the right scaling. So one might hope that the properly rescaled last passage problem in
WB
converges to a last passage problem in the Airy line ensemble. This seems incredible at first because a large part of the last passage percolation is taking place in parts of the melon that disappear in the limit. Indeed, the limit only sees the top few lines and a time window of order
n^{-1/3}
.

The technical part of the proof is to show that the last passage path from
(2xn^{-1/3},n)
to
(1,1)
in
WB
follows a parabola of the form

k\mapsto(1-2\sqrt{k/(2x)}n^{-1/3},k)

(8)

These asymptotics follow from a multi-step path transformation lemma (Lemma
5.2
), a detailed analysis of a last passage problem on the Airy line ensemble (Section
6
), and an analysis of an optimization problem (Lemma
7.1
). Analyzing the last passage problem across the Airy line ensemble is the most technical part of the paper. It requires delicate structural results about the Airy line ensemble from
Dauvergne and Virág 2021a
. In this paper, we take these structural results as black box inputs for the proof.

For nearby starting points
x
and
x^{\prime}
the parabolas in (
8
) diverge as
k\to\infty
, while the last passage values should be close. This suggests that there is not much information contained in these paths away from the top corner. We turn this intuition into a proof of Theorem
1.3
in Sections
7
and
8
. In order to facilitate the proof, one key idea is too look at differences of last passage values. It is easier to directly show that these differences only depend on the top corner, and then extract the result for the last passage values themselves by averaging, see Remark
8.2
.

The directed landscape can be patched together from Airy sheets. For the convergence of last passage percolation to the directed landscape, there is a technical tightness issue that we handle in Section
11
.

The focus of this paper is to construct the limiting objects, and we do not explore their properties in detail here. However, our description makes several natural questions about the directed landscape accessible. We will analyze the geometry of this object in future work.

Our results complete the construction of the central object in the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang universality class. derived a formula for the distribution of
g\mathcal{S}
, the metric composition of a fixed function
g
and the Airy sheet
\mathcal{S}
.
Baik and Liu 2019
found formulas for the joint distribution of
\{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(a,b_{i}):i\in\{1,\dots,k\}\}
for any fixed
a,b_{1},\dots,b_{k}
for a related limiting object
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
that in our language would be the directed landscape on the cylinder. We note the conjectured limit
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
can be described by wrapping the directed landscape
\mathcal{L}
around the cylinder and redefining path lengths locally. Recently,
Johansson and Rahman 2019
and
Liu 2019
proved formulas analogous to those of Baik and Liu with
\mathcal{L}
in place of
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
.

Probabilistic and geometric methods have been used previously to prove qualitative statements about last passage percolation. As an early example,
Johansson 2000b
studied transversal fluctuations. More recently,
Pimentel 2018
showed tightness of the Airy sheet in a different model, and proved that the Airy sheet locally looks like a sum of independent Brownian motions.
Ferrari and Occelli 2019

analyzed the covariance of last passage values at two different times. For other probabilistic and geometric approaches, see
Basu et al. 2014
,
Basu et al. 2019
, and
Hammond and Sarkar 2020
discussed above.

Hammond 2016
used a probabilistic approach to prove Radon-Nikodym derivative and other regularity bounds for the Airy line ensemble with respect to Brownian bridges. Subsequent papers (see
Hammond 2020
,
Hammond 2019a
,
Hammond 2019b
) combined this work with geometric reasoning to understand problems about the geometry of last passage paths in Brownian last passage percolation and the roughness of limiting growth profiles in that model.

Preliminaries

2.1
Last passage across general functions

For an interval
I\subset\mathbb{Z}
, let
C^{I}
be the space of all continuous functions

f:\mathbb{R}\times I\to\mathbb{R},\qquad(x,i)\mapsto f_{i}(x).

We will often think of
f
as a sequence of functions
\{f_{i}:i\in I\}
.
When
I=\{1,\dots,n\}
, we will simply write
C^{n}
. We call a nonincreasing function
\pi:[x,y]\to I
which is cadlag on
[x,y]
and satisfies
\pi(x)\geq\ell
and
\pi(y)=m
a
path
from
(x,\ell)
to
(y,m)
. Unfortunately the left endpoint
(x,\ell)
is not specified by the function
\pi
and has to be given separately. We will define the left limit of
\pi
at
x
to be
\ell
, and the right limit at
y
to be
m
.
Our paths are nonincreasing instead of nondecreasing to accommodate the natural indexing of the Airy line ensemble.

We define the
length
of
\pi
with respect to a coordinatewise differentiable function
f\in C^{I}
by

\int df\circ\pi:=\int_{x}^{y}f^{\prime}_{\pi(t)}(t)dt.

For each
\pi
, this is just a sum of increments of
f
, so this definition extends to all continuous
f
. Note that for many of the cases we are interested in, the functions
f_{i}
are ordered so that
f_{i}\geq f_{i+1}
. Hence when visualizing nondecreasing path length with respect to a set of such functions, it is natural to draw nondecreasing paths as rising physically, see Figure
1
.

For
x\leq y\in\mathbb{R}
and
m\leq\ell\in I
define the
last passage value
of
f
from
(x,\ell)
to
(y,m)
by

f[(x,\ell)\to(y,m)]=\sup_{\pi}\int df\circ\pi,

where the supremum is taken over all paths
\pi
from
(x,\ell)
to
(y,m)
.
See Figure
1
for an illustration of this definition. We say that a point
(x,t)

lies along
a path
\pi:[s,r]\to\mathbb{Z}
if
t\in[s,r]
and if

\lim_{q\to t^{-}}\pi(q)\geq x\geq\lim_{q\to t^{+}}\pi(q).

In other words, if the graph of
\pi
is connected at its jumps by vertical lines, then
(x,t)
will lie on this connected version of the graph.

For
f\in C^{I}
, define the
gap process

g=g(f)
by
g_{i}=f_{i}-f_{i+1}
. We can alternately define path length in terms of the gap process. For a nonincreasing path
\pi
from
(x,\ell)
to
(y,m)
, we have

\int df\circ\pi=f_{m}(y)-f_{\ell}(x)-\sum_{i=m}^{\ell-1}g_{i}(t_{i}),

(9)

where

t_{i}\in[x,y]\quad\text{ is the unique time so that }\quad\pi(s)\begin{cases}\geq i+1&\text{ for }s<t_{i},\\
\leq i&\text{ for }s\geq t_{i}.\end{cases}

(10)

We call
t_{m+1},\ldots,t_{\ell}
the
jump times
of
\pi
.
Thus the last passage value can be thought of as a difference of endpoints minus a minimal sum of gaps. This definition will be useful when we deal with nonintersecting sets of lines
f\in C^{I}
whose gap processes are nonnegative. By (
9
) we have

f[(x,\ell)\to(y,m)]=f_{m}(y)-f_{\ell}(x)-\inf_{x\leq t_{\ell-1}\leq\dots\leq t_{m}\leq y}\sum_{i=m}^{\ell-1}g_{i}(t_{i}),

(11)

which implies that the last passage value is continuous in
(x,y)
.

We now extend the definition of last passage to disjoint collections of paths. Let

U=\{(x_{i},\ell_{i}):i\in\{1,\dots k\}\}\qquad\;\text{and}\;\qquad V=\{(y_{i},m_{i}):i\in\{1,\dots k\}\}

be two sequences of ordered pairs in
\mathbb{R}\times I
with
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
and
m_{i}\leq\ell_{i}
for all
i
. The points
(x_{i},\ell_{i})
and
(y_{i},m_{i})
will be endpoints of disjoint paths
\pi_{i}
. Define
\mathcal{Q}(U,V)
to be the
set of disjoint paths

\mathbf{\pi}=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k})
from
U
to
V
. More precisely,
\pi\in\mathcal{Q}(U,V)
if

(i)

For all
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, the function
\pi_{i}
is a path from
(x_{i},\ell_{i})
to
(y_{i},m_{i})
.

(ii)

For all
i\in\{1,\dots,k-1\}
, we have that
\pi_{i}(t)<\pi_{i+1}(t)
for all
t\in(x_{i},y_{i})\cap(x_{i+1},y_{i+1})
. In order to ensure existence of disjoint paths with repeated endpoints, we do not enforce a disjointness condition at the endpoints.

For a path
\pi\in\mathcal{Q}(U,V)
, we define the
length
of
\pi
with respect to
f
by

\int df\circ\pi=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\;\int df\circ\pi_{i}.

With the above definition of
\mathcal{Q}(U,V)
, we say that
(U,V)
is an
endpoint pair
if the following conditions hold. For this definition
U=\{(x_{i},\ell_{i})\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,k\}}
and
V=\{(y_{i},m_{i})\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,k\}}
.

(i)

For all
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, we have that
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
and
\ell_{i}\geq m_{i}
.

(ii)

For all
i\in\{1,\dots k-1\}
, we have that
x_{i}\leq x_{i+1}
and
y_{i}\leq y_{i+1}
.

(iii)

The set of paths
\mathcal{Q}(U,V)
is nonempty.

For an endpoint pair
(U,V)
and a function
f\in C^{I}
, we define the
last passage value
of
f
across
(U,V)
by

f[U\rightarrow V]=\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{Q}(U,V)}\;\int df\circ\pi.

In the case when
k=1
, we recover the previous definition of the last passage path.

We also define the set of
last passage paths
between
U=\{(x_{i},\ell_{i})\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,k\}}
and
V=\{(y_{i},m_{i})\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,k\}}
by

P_{f}[U,V]=\left\{\pi\in\mathcal{Q}(U,V):\int df\circ\pi=\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{Q}(U,V)}\;\int df\circ\sigma\right\}.

(12)

We will omit the subscript
f
above when the function
f
is clear from context or does not change throughout a proof.

2.2
Melons

Let
C^{n}_{+}
be the space of continuous functions

f:[0,\infty)\times\{1,\dots,n\}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad(x,i)\mapsto f_{i}(x).

For
f\in C^{n}_{+}
, we can define a function
Wf\in C^{n}_{+}
by the formula

\sum_{i=1}^{k}(Wf)_{i}(t)=f[(0,n)^{k}\rightarrow(t,1)^{k}],\qquad\qquad\text{ for all }k\in\{1,\dots,n\},t\in[0,\infty).

Here
(s,i)^{k}
is the sequence with
k
copies of the point
(s,i)
. The function
Wf
can be thought of as the recording tableau of a continuous version of the Robinson-Schensted-Knuth bijection, see Section 6 in
O’Connell 2003
. We call
Wf
the
melon
of
f
. We will explore the process of constructing melons more in Section
4
. Paths in the melon
Wf
are ordered so that
(Wf)_{1}\geq(Wf)_{2}\geq\dots\geq(Wf)_{n}
(see the discussion at the beginning of Section
4
). This is where the term melon comes from: since paths in
Wf
avoid each other and all start from
0
, they look like stripes on a watermelon. Note that in physics literature, the term watermelon is often used for ensembles of nonintersecting random walks or Brownian motions which fit into this context.

2.3
Brownian melons and the Airy line ensemble

We now introduce the main object of study in this paper, Brownian last passage percolation. See
Weiss et al. 2017
for background on the integrable aspects of this model. Let
B\in C^{\mathbb{Z}}
be a sequence of independent two-sided Brownian motions. Let
B^{n}
be
B
restricted to
\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\{1,\ldots\,n\}
. We are concerned with finding the scaling limit of last passage values across the sequence
B
. By a result in Section
4
, we will be able to relate these last passage values to last passage values across the
Brownian
n
-melon

WB^{n}
.

There are many remarkable descriptions of the Brownian
n
-melon
WB^{n}
. The description that will be most useful to us here is that
WB^{n}
can be described as the distributional limit as
\epsilon\to 0
of a sequence of
n
independent Brownian motions
B^{i}_{\epsilon}:[0,1/\epsilon]\to\mathbb{R}
with
B^{i}_{\epsilon}(0)=i\epsilon
conditioned so that

B^{1}_{\epsilon}(t)>B^{2}_{\epsilon}(t)>\dots>B^{n}_{\epsilon}(t).

This was first proven as Theorem 7 in
O’Connell and Yor 2002
, see also
Biane et al. 2005
. The top lines of the Brownian
n
-melon have a scaling limit known as the Airy line ensemble.
This next theorem was proven in many parts, see
Prähofer and Spohn 2002
,
Johansson 2003
,
Adler and Van Moerbeke 2005
,
Corwin and Hammond 2014
. We note that the final version of this theorem in
Corwin and Hammond 2014
proves it for nonintersecting Brownian bridges, rather than nonintersecting Brownian motions. The two convergence statements are equivalent via the scaling relationship

P(s)=\frac{t-s}{\sqrt{t}}B\left(\frac{s}{t-s}\right)

relating a system of nonintersecting Brownian bridges
P
on
[0,t]
to a system of nonintersecting Brownian motions
B
on
[0,\infty]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
WB^{n}
be a Brownian
n
-melon. Define the rescaled melon

A^{n}=(A^{n}_{1},\dots,A^{n}_{n})

by

A^{n}_{i}(y)=n^{1/6}\left((WB^{n})_{i}(1+2yn^{-1/3})-2\sqrt{n}-2yn^{1/6}\right).

Then
A^{n}
converges to a random sequence of functions
\mathcal{A}=(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2},\dots)\in C^{\mathbb{N}}
in law with respect to product of uniform-on-compact topology on
C^{\mathbb{N}}
. For every
y\in\mathbb{R}
and
i<j
, we have that
\mathcal{A}_{i}(y)>\mathcal{A}_{j}(y)
. The function
\mathcal{A}
is called the
(parabolic) Airy line ensemble.

Figure 5
:
A sketch of a ‘Brownian melon’ from time
0
to just after time
1
. If we zoom in around the location
(1,2\sqrt{n})
on a
O(n^{-1/3})\times O(n^{-1/6})
parallelogram with slope
\sqrt{n}
, then we get the Airy line ensemble.

The shifted line ensemble
\mathcal{A}(y)+y^{2}
is stationary in time. We will refer to this object as the
stationary Airy line ensemble
. However, for our purposes, the parabolic Airy line ensemble is the object of interest.

The function
\mathcal{A}_{1}(y)+y^{2}
is known as the
Airy process
(sometimes Airy
2
). We now collect a few key facts about the Airy line ensemble and the Airy process. For this proposition and throughout the paper, we say that a Brownian motion (or bridge, or melon) has
variance
v
if its quadratic variation in an interval
[s,t]
is proportional to
v(t-s)
.

Proposition 2.2

(
Corwin and Hammond 2014
, Proposition 4.1)
.

Fix an interval
[a,a+b]\subset\mathbb{R}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and define
B_{i}(t)=\mathcal{A}_{i}(a+t)-\mathcal{A}_{i}(a)
for
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
. Then on the interval
[0,b]
the sequence
(B_{1},\dots,B_{k})
is absolutely continuous with respect to the law of
k
independent Brownian motions with variance
2
.

The one-point distributions of the Airy process follow a GUE Tracy-Widom distribution. This well-known result goes back to
Baik et al. 1999
. As this will be used throughout the paper, we state it here as a theorem. The tail bounds on GUE Tracy-Widom random variables that we use go back to
Tracy and Widom 1994
, see also
Ramirez et al. 2011
for short proofs.

Theorem 2.3

(
Tracy and Widom 1994
,
Baik et al. 1999
)
.

For every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the random variable
\mathcal{A}_{1}(t)+t^{2}
has GUE Tracy-Widom distribution. In particular, it satisfies the tail bounds

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{1}(t)+t^{2}>m)\leq ce^{-dm^{3/2}}\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{1}(t)+t^{2}<-m)\leq ce^{-dm^{3}}

for universal constants
c
and
d
.

We will also use the following bound on two-point distributions of the Airy process.

Lemma 2.4

(
Dauvergne and Virág 2021a
, Lemma 6.1)
.

There are constants
c,d>0
such that for every
t\in\mathbb{R},s,a>0,
we have

\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{A}_{1}(t)+t^{2}-\mathcal{A}_{1}(t+s)-(t+s)^{2}|>a\sqrt{s})\leq ce^{-da^{2}}.

Finally, we will also need the finite versions of Theorem
2.3
and Lemma
2.4
, as well as a proposition bounding the entire Brownian
n
-melon below a particular function.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
W^{n}_{1}
be the top line of a Brownian
n
-melon. There exist constants
c
and
d
such that for all
m>0
and all
n\geq 1

\mathbb{P}(|W^{n}_{1}(1)-2\sqrt{n}|\geq mn^{-1/6})\leq ce^{-dm^{3/2}}.

Theorem
2.5
is proven in
Ledoux and Rider 2010
for

. For greater values of

Ledoux 2007

Proposition 2.6
.

Fix

such that for every

Proposition 2.7

(

There exist positive constants

and

3
The geometry of last passage paths

Last passage paths can be thought of as geodesics in a metric space. This is a guiding principle for many of the proofs in the paper. With this intuition in mind, we devote this section to stating and proving some basic facts about the geometry of last passage paths. Many of these facts are well-known and well-used in the context of last passage percolation.

For the rest of this section, let
f\in C^{\mathbb{Z}}
. The first lemma states that last passage paths have the geodesic property that they maximize length between any two points on the path. Its proof is straightforward and hence omitted.

Lemma 3.1

(Geodesic property)
.

Let
((x,\ell),(y,m))
be an endpoint pair of single points. Then for any
\pi
in the set of last passage paths
P_{f}[(x,\ell),(y,m)]
, and any times
s<t\in[x,y]
, we have that

\int_{s}^{t}df\circ\pi=\max\left\{\int_{s}^{t}df\circ\tau:\;\;\tau\text{ is a path from }(s,\pi(s))\text{ to }(t,\pi(t))\right\}.

The next fact is a straightforward consequence of Lemma
3.1
. Its proof is again omitted.

Lemma 3.2

(Metric composition law)
.

Let
((x,\ell),(y,m))
be an endpoint pair of single points. Then for any
k\in\{m,\dots,\ell\}
, we have that

\displaystyle f[(x,\ell)\rightarrow(y,m)]

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\in[x,y]}f[(x,\ell)\rightarrow(z,k)]+f[(z,k)\rightarrow(y,m)]

and for any
k\in\{m+1,\dots,\ell\}
, we have

\displaystyle f[(x,\ell)\rightarrow(y,m)]

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\in[x,y]}f[(x,\ell)\rightarrow(z,k)]+f[(z,k-1)\rightarrow(y,m)].

Lemma
3.2
implies a
triangle inequality
for last passage values. For any
x\leq z\leq y
and
m\leq k\leq\ell
we have

f[(x,\ell)\rightarrow(y,m)]\geq f[(x,\ell)\rightarrow(z,k)]+f[(z,k)\rightarrow(y,m)].

(15)

Note that in this equation, the inequality is reversed compared to the triangle inequality for metric spaces. It will also be useful to understand the right hand side above as a function of
z
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
((x,\ell),(y,m))
be an endpoint pair and fix
k\in\{m,\dots,\ell\}
. For
z\in[x,y]
, define

h_{1}(z)=f[(x,\ell)\rightarrow(z,k)]-f_{k}(z)\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad h_{2}(z)=f[(z,k)\rightarrow(y,m)]+f_{k}(z).

Then

f[(x,\ell)\rightarrow(y,m)]=\sup_{z\in[x,y]}h_{1}(z)+h_{2}(z),

the function
h_{1}
is nondecreasing and the function
h_{2}
is nonincreasing.

Proof.

The representation of
f[(x,\ell)\rightarrow(y,m)]
as a supremum over
h_{1}+h_{2}
follows immediately from Lemma
3.2
. By the sum of gaps representation (
9
),

h_{1}(z)=-f_{\ell}(x)-\inf\sum_{i=k+1}^{\ell}f_{i-1}(t_{i})-f_{i}(t_{i}),

where the infimum is over all sequences
t_{\ell}\leq\dots\leq t_{k+1}\in[x,z]
. As we increase
z
, this infimum can only get smaller, so
h_{1}
is nondecreasing. The proof that
h_{2}
is nonincreasing is similar.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

For any endpoint pair
((x,\ell),(y,m))
, the set
P[(x,\ell),(y,m)]
is closed with respect to the topology of convergence of jump times (
10
).

Lemma
3.4
follows from the continuity of
f
. The next lemma shows that we can pick out rightmost and leftmost paths in the set
P[(x,\ell),(y,m)]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
((x,\ell),(y,m))
be an endpoint pair. There exist paths
\pi^{-},\pi^{+}\in P[(x,\ell),(y,m)]
such that for any
\pi\in P[(x,\ell),(y,m)]
and any
z\in[x,y]
we have

\pi^{-}(z)\leq\pi(z)\leq\pi^{+}(z).

We refer to
\pi^{-}
as the
leftmost last passage path
and
\pi^{+}
as the
rightmost last passage path
.

Proof.

The set of last passage paths is closed by Lemma
3.4
. Thus it suffices to show that for any paths
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in P[(x,\ell),(y,m)]
, that there exist paths
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in P[(x,\ell),(y,m)]
such that

\tau_{1}(z)\leq\pi_{i}(z)\leq\tau_{2}(z)\quad\text{ for }i=1,2.

Define paths
\tau_{1}=\pi_{1}\wedge\pi_{2}
and
\tau_{2}=\pi_{1}\vee\pi_{2}
from
(x,\ell)
to
(y,m)
. Then,

\int df\circ\tau_{1}+\int df\circ\tau_{2}=\int df\circ\pi_{1}+\int df\circ\pi_{2},

since the paths
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
cover the same parts of lines in
f
as
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
. Since the paths
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
maximize length, each of the paths
\tau_{i}
must maximize length as well.
∎

Lemma 3.6

(Monotonicity and continuity of last passage paths)
.

For
x\leq y
, let
\pi^{+}[x,y]
denote the rightmost last passage path in
P[(x,n),(y,1)]
. Then
\pi^{+}[x,y]
is a nondecreasing, right continuous functions of both
x
and
y
in the topology of convergence of jump times. Similarly the leftmost path
\pi^{-}[x,y]
is a nondecreasing, left continuous function of both
x
and
y
.

Proof.

We just prove the statements for rightmost paths.
Let
x_{1}\leq x_{2}
,
y_{1}\leq y_{2}
, and
x_{2}\leq y_{1}
.
On the interval
[x_{2},y_{1}]
, define

\tau_{1}=\pi^{+}[x_{1},y_{1}]\wedge\pi^{+}[x_{2},y_{2}],\quad\tau_{2}=\pi^{+}[x_{1},y_{1}]\vee\pi^{+}[x_{2},y_{2}].

We can extend
\tau_{1}
to the interval
[x_{1},x_{2}]
by defining it to be equal to
\pi^{+}[x_{1},y_{1}]
there and similarly extend
\tau_{2}
to
[y_{1},y_{2}]
by setting it to be equal to
\pi^{+}[x_{2},y_{2}]
. For
i=1,2
,
\tau_{i}
is a path from
(x_{i},n)
to
(y_{i},1)
.
We have

\int df\circ\tau_{1}+\int df\circ\tau_{2}=\int df\circ\pi^{+}[x_{1},y_{1}]+\int df\circ\pi^{+}[x_{2},y_{2}].

Since
\pi^{+}[x_{1},y_{1}]
and
\pi^{+}[x_{2},y_{2}]
maximize length, we have that
\tau_{i}\in P[(x_{i},n),(y_{i},1)]
for
i=1,2
. Moreover,
\tau_{2}\geq\pi^{+}[x_{2},y_{2}]
by construction. Since
\pi^{+}[x_{2},y_{2}]
is a rightmost last passage path, this is in fact equality. Hence
\tau_{1}=\pi^{+}[x_{1},y_{1}]
as well, showing monotonicity.

Now we prove right continuity in
x
. The proof of right continuity in
y
is similar. Fix
y\in\mathbb{R}
and let
x_{m}\downarrow x\in\mathbb{R}
. By monotonicity, the sequence of paths
\pi^{+}[x_{m},y]
has a limit
\pi
in the topology of convergence of jump times. Since path length is a continuous function in this topology, we have

\int df\circ\pi=\lim_{m\to\infty}\int df\circ\pi^{+}[x_{m},y]=\lim_{m\to\infty}f[(x_{m},n)\to(y,1)]=f[(x,n)\to(y,1)].

The final equality follows from the continuity of last passage values in
x
, see (
11
).
Therefore
\pi
is a last passage path from
(x,n)
to
(y,1)
. Moreover, by monotonicity
\pi^{+}[x,y]\leq\pi^{+}[x_{m},y]
on the interval
[x_{m},y]
for all
m
. Therefore
\pi^{+}[x,y]\leq\pi
as well. Since
\pi^{+}[x,y]
is the rightmost last passage path,
\pi=\pi^{+}[x,y]
as desired.
∎

We now show that paths exhibit a tree structure.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
x_{1}\leq x_{2}<y_{1}\leq y_{2}
be points in
\mathbb{R}
. Let
\pi^{+}[x_{i},y_{i}]
denote the rightmost last passage path in
P[(x_{i},n),(y_{i},1)]
. Then there is a (possibly empty) interval
[a,b]\subset[x_{2},y_{1}]
such that the following holds.

(i)

\pi^{+}[x_{1},y_{1}](s)=\pi^{+}[x_{2},y_{2}](s)\text{ for all }s\in(a,b).

(ii)

\pi^{+}[x_{1},y_{1}](s)<\pi^{+}[x_{2},y_{2}](s)\text{ for all }s\in[x_{2},y_{1}]\setminus[a,b].

In particular, if
x_{1}=x_{2}
, then the last passage paths to
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
follow the same path up to time
b
, and are entirely disjoint afterwards (so they form two branches in a tree).
The same tree structure holds for
\pi^{-}
in place of
\pi^{+}
.

The discrepancy at the endpoints
a,b
is due to the fact that the paths are not continuous.

Proof.

Let

I=\{t\in[x_{2},y_{1}]:\pi^{+}[x_{1},y_{1}](t)=\pi^{+}[x_{2},y_{2}](t)\}.

For any two points
t_{1}<t_{2}\in I
, by the geodesic property of last passage paths, Lemma
3.1
, the paths
\pi^{+}[x_{i},y_{i}]|_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}
are both last passage paths between the points

(t_{1},\pi^{+}[x_{1},y_{1}](t_{1}))\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad(t_{2},\pi^{+}[x_{1},y_{1}](t_{2})).

Moreover, both of these paths are rightmost last passage paths on this interval, so they must be equal. Hence
[t_{1},t_{2}]\subset I
. Since
t_{1},t_{2}
are arbitrary points in
I
, it follows that
I
is an interval, as desired.

Part (ii) of the lemma follows from monotonicity of last passage paths, Lemma
3.6
.
∎

We end this section with a monotonicity result for sums of last passage values.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
x=(x_{1},n),y=(y_{1},1)
and define
x^{\prime}
,
y^{\prime}
similarly.
Assume
x_{1}\leq x_{1}^{\prime}<y_{1}\leq y_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

f[x\rightarrow y]+f[x^{\prime}\rightarrow y^{\prime}]\geq f[x\rightarrow y^{\prime}]+f[x^{\prime}\rightarrow y].

Proof.

By the ordering of the points, there must exist a point
z=(t,k)
that lies along the rightmost last passage paths both from
x
to
y^{\prime}
and
x^{\prime}
to
y
. Because of the geodesic property

\displaystyle f[x\rightarrow y^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=f[x\rightarrow z]+f[z\rightarrow y^{\prime}]\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad f[x^{\prime}\rightarrow y]=f[x^{\prime}\rightarrow z]+f[z\rightarrow y].

The result is then the sum of the triangle inequalities

f[x\rightarrow y]\geq f[x\rightarrow z]+f[z\rightarrow y]\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad f[x^{\prime}\rightarrow y^{\prime}]\geq f[x^{\prime}\rightarrow z]+f[z\rightarrow y^{\prime}].\qed

4
Melons

Recall that
C^{n}_{+}
is the space of
n
-tuples of continuous functions from
[0,\infty)
to
\mathbb{R}
. Recall also the melon map
f\mapsto Wf
, introduced in Section
2.2
, defined so that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}(Wf)_{i}(t)=f[(0,n)^{k}\rightarrow(t,1)^{k}]

(16)

for all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
t\in[0,\infty)
.
In this section, we show that certain last passage values are preserved by the map
f\mapsto Wf
.

To do this, we first approach the construction of
Wf
by successively sorting pairs of functions. This approach is taken in
O’Connell and Yor 2002
,
Biane et al. 2005
. Let
f_{1},f_{2}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be two continuous functions. For
x<y\in[0,\infty)
, define the minimal gap size

G(f_{1},f_{2})(x,y)=\min_{s\in[x,y]}[f_{1}(s)-f_{2}(s)].

Then the last passage values satisfy

\begin{split}W(f_{1},f_{2})_{1}(t)&=f_{1}(t)-f_{2}(0)-G(f_{1},f_{2})(0,t),\\
W(f_{1},f_{2})_{2}(t)&=f_{2}(t)-f_{1}(0)+G(f_{1},f_{2})(0,t).\end{split}

(17)

To see the above formula for
W(f_{1},f_{2})_{2}
given the formula for
W(f_{1},f_{2})_{1}
, simply note that

f[(0,n)^{n}\rightarrow(t,1)^{n}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(t)-f_{i}(0)

for any
f\in C^{n}_{+},t\geq 0
. The formula for
W(f_{1},f_{2})_{2}
uses the case
n=2
.
Now, for
f\in C^{n}_{+}
and
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, define

\sigma_{i}(f)=(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{i-1},W(f_{i},f_{i+1})_{1},W(f_{i},f_{i+1})_{2},f_{i+2}\dots,f_{n}).

Now let
(i_{1},\dots,i_{n\choose 2})
be any sequence of numbers in
\{1,\dots,n-1\}
such that
\tau_{i_{1}}\cdots\tau_{i_{n\choose 2}}
is the reverse permutation
n(n-1)\cdots 1
, where
\tau_{i}=(i,i+1)
is an adjacent transposition. Then we can alternately define the melon of
f
by

\mathcal{W}f:=\sigma_{i_{1}}\dots\sigma_{i_{n\choose 2}}(f).

(18)

By the discussion immediately preceding Proposition 2.8 in
Biane et al. 2005
, the above function is independent of the choice of reduced decomposition of
n\cdots 1
. Moreover, by Corollary 2.9 there,

\sigma_{i}\mathcal{W}f=\mathcal{W}f

for any
\sigma_{i}
.
This implies that
(\mathcal{W}f)_{1}\geq(\mathcal{W}f)_{2}\geq\dots\geq(\mathcal{W}f)_{n}
. Combining this with the fact that
(\mathcal{W}f)_{i}(0)=0
for all
i
, we get that for any
t>0
, there exists a last passage path from
(0,n)^{k}
to
(t,1)^{k}
that only uses the top
k
paths. This implies that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\mathcal{W}f)_{i}(t)=\mathcal{W}f[(0,n)^{k}\to(t,1)^{k}].

(19)

The fact that
Wf=\mathcal{W}f
then follows from our Proposition
4.1
.

Biane et al. 2005
in Section 4.5 note that the transformation (
18
) yields a nonintersecting walk representation of the recording tableaux given by the RSK bijection. From this the equivalent formula (
16
) follows from Greene’s theorem, see
Sagan 2013
. A proof of this connection with RSK can be found in
O’Connell 2003
.
After posting a previous version of this paper, we learned from Neil O’Connell that Proposition
4.1
for
k
identical starting points and
k
identical endpoints follows from Lemma 4.8. of
Biane et al. 2005
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
U=\{(x_{i},n)\}_{i\in\{1,\dots k\}}
and
V=\{(y_{i},1)\}_{i\in\{1,\dots k\}}
be an endpoint pair with
x_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
. Then for any
f\in C^{n}_{+}
, we have that

f[U\rightarrow V]=\mathcal{W}f[U\rightarrow V].

We will prove this proposition in three steps. We first deal with the case
n=2
and when
U
and
V
each have one element.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
f=(f_{1},f_{2})
. For every
0\leq x\leq y
, we have that

f\big[(x,2)\rightarrow(y,1)\big]=Wf\big[(x,2)\rightarrow(y,1)\big].

Proof.

Last passage values are left unchanged by shifting functions up or down by a constant. Hence we may assume that
f_{1}(0)=f_{2}(0)=0
. To avoid carrying bulky notation, we also set

s(x,y)=\max_{s\in[x,y]}[f_{2}(s)-f_{1}(s)].

We have that

f\big[(x,2)\rightarrow(y,1)\big]=f_{1}(y)-f_{2}(x)+s(x,y)

and by (
17
), that

Wf\big[(x,2)\rightarrow(y,1)\big]=f_{1}(y)+s(0,y)-f_{2}(x)+s(0,x)+\sup_{t\in[x,y]}[f_{2}(t)-f_{1}(t)-2s(0,t)].

Therefore to prove the lemma, it is enough to show that

s(x,y)-s(0,y)-s(0,x)=\sup_{t\in[x,y]}[f_{2}(t)-f_{1}(t)-2s(0,t)].

(20)

To prove (
20
), we divide into cases. First suppose that
s(0,x)=s(0,y)
. In this case, since
s(0,\cdot)
is an nondecreasing function, we have that
s(0,t)=s(0,x)=s(0,y)
for all
t\in[x,y]
. Therefore

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[x,y]}[f_{2}(t)-f_{1}(t)-2s(0,t)]

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in[x,y]}[f_{2}(t)-f_{1}(t)]-s(0,y)-s(0,x)

\displaystyle=s(x,y)-s(0,y)-s(0,x).

For the case when
s(0,x)<s(0,y)
, observe that

\sup_{t\in[x,y]}[f_{2}(t)-f_{1}(t)-2s(0,t)]\leq-\inf_{t\in[x,y]}s(0,t)=-s(0,x).

(21)

Moreover, since
s(0,\cdot)
is nonconstant on the interval
[x,y]
, continuity of the functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
implies that there exist times
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
in
[x,y]
such that

s(0,t_{1})=f_{2}(t_{1})-f_{1}(t_{1})=s(0,x)\qquad\;\text{and}\;\qquad f_{2}(t_{2})-f_{1}(t_{2})=s(0,y).

The first equation above implies that the inequality in (
21
) is in fact equality, and the second equation implies that
s(x,y)=s(0,y)
. Combining these facts proves (
20
).
∎

Next, we extend the
n=2
case to deal with an arbitrary number of paths.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
f=(f_{1},f_{2}).
For every endpoint pair of the form
U=\{(x_{i},2)\}_{i\in\{1,\dots k\}}
and
V=\{(y_{i},1)\}_{i\in\{1,\dots k\}}
, we have that

f\big[U\rightarrow V\big]=Wf[U\rightarrow V].

Proof.

Since there are only two lines, there can only be disjoint paths from
U
to
V
if
y_{i}\leq x_{i+2}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,k-2\}
.
For the same reason, whenever
x_{i+1}<y_{i}
, if
\pi
is a
k
-tuple of disjoint paths from
U
to
V
then

\pi_{i}(t)=1\;\text{and}\;\pi_{i+1}(t)=2\qquad\text{ for all }t\in[x_{i+1},y_{i}).

(22)

Therefore we can write, recalling that
\mathcal{Q}(U,V)
denotes the set of all
k
-tuples of disjoint paths from
U
to
V

\displaystyle\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{Q}(U,V)}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int df\circ\pi_{i}=

\displaystyle\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{Q}(U,V)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{x_{i}\vee y_{i-1}}^{y_{i}\wedge x_{i+1}}df\circ\pi_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathbf{1}(x_{i+1}<y_{i})[f_{1}(y_{i})+f_{2}(y_{i})-f_{1}(x_{i+1})-f_{2}(x_{i+1})]\right).

In the above formula, we treat
y_{0}
as
0
and
x_{k+1}
as
\infty
.
Now, since
(Wf)_{1}+(Wf)_{2}=f_{1}+f_{2}
, the second term under the right hand supremum above is preserved by mapping
f\mapsto Wf
. We need to check that the same is true of the first term. Since the intervals
(x_{i}\vee y_{i-1},y_{i}\wedge x_{i+1})
are all disjoint from each other, the only condition that forces interactions between the coordinates of a path
\pi\in\mathcal{Q}(U,V)
is condition (
22
). Therefore

\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{Q}(U,V)}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{x_{i}\vee y_{i-1}}^{y_{i}\wedge x_{i+1}}df\circ\pi_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\;\;\sup_{\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{Q}((x_{i}\vee y_{i-1},2),(y_{i}\wedge x_{i+1},1))}\;\;\int df\circ\pi_{i}.

By Lemma
4.2
, each supremum term in the sum on the right hand side is preserved by the map
f\mapsto Wf
.
∎

Before proving Proposition
4.1
, we record an extension of the metric composition law in Lemma
3.2
. First, for a set of
k
-tuples
Z=\{(w_{i},m)\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,k\}}
, define

Z^{-}=\{(w_{i},m-1)\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,k\}}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
f\in C^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Let
(U,V)
be an endpoint pair of the form
U=\{(x_{i},n)\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,k\}}
,
V=\{(y_{i},1)\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,k\}}
, and let
m\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. Then

f[U\rightarrow V]=\sup_{Z}f[U\rightarrow Z]+f[Z^{-}\rightarrow V],

where the supremum is taken over all
k
-tuples
Z=\{(z_{i},m)\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,k\}},
such that both
(U,Z)
and
(Z^{-},V)
are endpoint pairs.

The proof of Lemma
4.4
is straightforward and hence we omit it.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

It is enough to show that for any sequence of continuous paths
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
with
f_{i}(0)=0
for all
i
and any
m\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
,

\sigma_{m}f[U\rightarrow V]=f[U\rightarrow V].

For any
f
, by Lemma
4.4
applied twice, we can write

f[U\rightarrow V]=\sup_{T,Z}\Big(f[U\rightarrow T]+f[T^{-}\rightarrow Z]+f[Z^{-}\rightarrow V]\Big).

(23)

Here the supremum is over all
T=\{(t_{i},m+2)\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,k\}},

Z=\{(z_{i},m)\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,k\}}
such that
(U,T),

(T^{-},Z)
, and
(Z^{-},V)
are all endpoint pairs. For a fixed
T,Z
, when we apply
\sigma_{m}
to
f
, the first and third terms under the supremum in (

) do not change since the relevant components

do not change. The middle term is preserved under the transformation

by Lemma

. Hence the right hand side of (
23
) is also preserved under

.
∎

We finish this section with a straightforward consequence of Proposition
4.1
.
As in Section
2.1
define two paths
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
to be disjoint if either
\pi_{1}>\pi_{2}
or
\pi_{1}<\pi_{2}
on the entire interior of both functions’ domains. As before, we define
\pi_{f}^{+}[x,y]
as the rightmost last passage path in the set
P_{f}[(x,n),(y,1)]
. We define the leftmost path
\pi_{f}^{-}[x,y]
similarly.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
U=((x_{1},n),(x_{2},n))
and
V=((y_{1},1),(y_{2},1))
be an endpoint pair with
0\leq x_{1}
. The paths
\pi_{f}^{-}[x_{1},y_{1}]
and
\pi_{f}^{+}[x_{2},y_{2}]
are disjoint if and only if the paths
\pi_{Wf}^{-}[x_{1},y_{1}]
and
\pi_{Wf}^{+}[x_{2},y_{2}]
are disjoint.

Proof.

We first show that the paths
\pi_{f}^{-}[x_{1},y_{1}]
and
\pi_{f}^{+}[x_{2},y_{2}]
are disjoint precisely when

f[(x_{1},n)\to(y_{1},1)]+f[(x_{2},n)\to(y_{2},1)]=f[U\to V].

(24)

To see this, note that
if the paths
\pi_{f}^{-}[x_{1},y_{1}]
and
\pi_{f}^{+}[x_{2},y_{2}]
are disjoint, then
(\pi_{f}^{-}[x_{1},y_{1}],\pi_{f}^{+}[x_{2},y_{2}])
forms a last passage path from
U
to

. Moreover, if equality holds in (

), then there must exist
\pi\in P_{f}[(x_{1},n),(y_{1},1)]
and
\tau\in P_{f}[(x_{2},n),(y_{2},n)]

Now, Proposition

implies that (

) holds if and only if it holds with

. By the same reasoning as above, this is true if and only if the paths

∎

5
Properties of melon paths

In this section, we collect some key deterministic facts about last passage paths in melons.
The first is about the location of last passage paths. We state the following lemma for functions
f\in C^{n}_{+}
that start at
0
and stay ordered, as any melon
Wf
has this property.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
f\in C^{n}_{+}
be such that
f_{i}(0)=0
and
f_{i}\geq f_{i+1}
for all
i
. Fix
j\leq k\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
U=\{(x_{i},n)\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,k\}},V=\{(y_{i},1)\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,k\}}
be an endpoint pair with
x_{i}=0
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,j\}
. Then there exists a last passage path
\pi

from
U
to
V
, see (
12
),
such that
\pi_{i}(t)=i
for all
t\in[0,y_{1}),i\in\{1,\dots,j\}
.

For the proof, it may help the reader to recall the ordering constraints required on
U,V
and any set of disjoint paths from
U
to
V
, see Section
2.1
.

Proof.

Let
g
be the gap process of
f
defined by
g_{i}=f_{i}-f_{i+1}
. By the identity (
9
), we can write

\begin{split}f[U\rightarrow V]&=\sup_{\tau}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(f_{1}(y_{i})-f_{n}(x_{i})-\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}g_{r}(t_{i,r})\right)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(f_{1}(y_{i})-f_{n}(x_{i})-\inf_{\tau}\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}g_{r}(t_{i,r})\right).\end{split}

(25)

Here the supremum is over all disjoint
k
-tuples of paths from
U
to
V
, and
t_{i,r}
is the jump time of the path
\tau_{i}
to line
r
, see (
10
). Hence, any last passage path
\pi
across
f
minimizes the sum of the gaps. In particular, this implies that if
\pi
is a last passage path, then for
i\in[1,j]
when
x_{i}=0
, we can replace each function
\pi_{i}
with a function that immediately jumps up to line
i
to take advantage of the zero-sized gaps there: since
g_{i}(t)\geq 0
for all
i,t
, this process cannot decrease the length of the path. By the ordering constraints on the functions
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,k\}}
, we must also have have that
\pi_{i}(t)\geq i
for all
t<y_{1}
.
∎

Melons opened up at other times

We will introduce melons starting at points other than
0
. Let
f\in C^{n}
.
We define the
melon at

6
The Airy line ensemble last passage problem

In this section, we study a last passage problem in the Airy line ensemble that arises naturally when we try to understand the limit of the right side of the equality in Proposition
4.1
. To motivate the study of this problem, we first see how it arises in the study of Brownian last passage percolation via the melon identity in Lemma
5.3
.

Recall from Section
2.5
the scaling operations
\bar{x}=2xn^{-1/3}
and
\hat{y}=1+2yn^{-1/3}
, and the bracket notation
[]
,
\{\}
, and
\langle\rangle
for last passage across Brownian motions, a Brownian melon, and the Airy line ensemble. By Proposition
4.1
, the Brownian last passage value from
\bar{x}
to
\hat{y}
equals a last passage value across Brownian melon. By Lemma
3.2
, this analysis can be broken down into an analysis of a last passage problem across the top right corner of the melon and a last passage problem up to that corner. By continuity, the last passage problem across the top right corner will translate to a last passage problem in the Airy line ensemble.

The harder part is the last passage up to the top right corner; these are values of the form
\{\bar{x}\to(\hat{z},k)\}_{n}
. To tackle this problem, we approximate a variant,

\{\bar{x}\to(\hat{z},k)\}_{n}-W_{k}^{n}(\hat{z}),

which has the added advantage of monotonicity in
z
(see Lemma
3.3
). Here and throughout we use the notation
W^{n}_{k}:=(WB^{n})_{k}
for the
k
th line in a Brownian
n
-melon.

In the sequel, for a random array
\{R_{n,k}:n,k\in\mathbb{N}\}
we will write

R_{n,k}={\mathfrak{o}}(r_{k})\qquad\text{ if for all }\epsilon>0\qquad\;\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(|R_{n,k}/r_{k}|>\epsilon)<\infty.

(32)

The idea behind this notation is that if we can pass to a limit in
n
to get a sequence
R_{k}
, then by the Borel-Cantelli lemma,
R_{k}/r_{k}\to 0
almost surely.

Proposition 6.1
.

For each
n
, let
W^{n}=WB^{n}
be a Brownian
n
-melon. Let
x>0
and let
z_{k}
be an arbitrary sequence of real numbers. Let

F^{n}_{k}(z)=n^{1/6}\left[\{\bar{x}\to(\hat{z},k)\}_{n}-W_{k}^{n}(\hat{z})+2xn^{1/6}\right].

Then

F^{n}_{k}(z_{k})=2\sqrt{2kx}+2z_{k}x+{\mathfrak{o}}(\sqrt{k}).

The analysis in Proposition
6.1
will boil down to understanding a last passage problem across the Airy line ensemble. As mentioned at the beginning of the section, this Airy line ensemble last passage problem arises out of an application of Lemma
5.3
. This application is dealt with by the following lemma.

Lemma 6.2
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then in the setup of Proposition
6.1
, we have

F^{n}_{k}(z_{k})-2z_{k}x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\langle(0,k)\rightarrow(x,1)\rangle\qquad\;\text{as}\;n\to\infty.

(33)

Proof.

By Lemma
5.3
, we have

F^{n}_{k}(z_{k})=2xn^{1/3}-n^{1/6}W^{*}_{\hat{z}_{k}}B^{n}[(\hat{z}_{k}-\bar{x},1)\to_{\mathfrak{f}}(\hat{z}_{k},k)]

(34)

where the notation
\mathfrak{f}
refers to the backwards first passage value, see (
27
), and
W^{*}_{\hat{z}_{k}}
is the reverse melon opened up at
\hat{z}_{k}
, see (
26
). By time-reversal symmetry of Brownian motion,
W^{*}_{\hat{z}_{k}}B_{n}
is equal to
WB_{n}
in distribution. Therefore by Theorem
2.1
, the top corner of
W^{*}_{\hat{z}_{k}}B_{n}
converges in distribution after proper rescaling to the Airy line ensemble.

Now, for
f,\hat{g}=(g,\dots,g)\in C^{n},
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, backwards first passage values across
f
and
\alpha f+\hat{g}
are simply related by scaling by

and translation by an increment of

. Using this, we can rewrite the right side of (

) as a backwards first passage value across a sequence of functions that converge uniformly on compact sets to the Airy line ensemble. Since backwards first passage values are continuous with respect to uniform convergence on compact sets, this implies that the right hand side of (

) converges to

-\mathcal{A}[(z_{k}-x,1)\rightarrow_{\mathfrak{f}}(z_{k},k)].

By flip symmetry of Airy line ensemble,

This is equal in distribution to the right hand side of (

since

is stationary.
∎

We can now state the key theorem about last passage values across the Airy line ensemble. Together with Lemma
6.2
this implies Proposition
6.1
.

Theorem 6.3
.

Fix
x>0
, and recall that
\langle(0,k)\rightarrow x\rangle
is the last passage value across the Airy line ensemble
\mathcal{A}
from line
k
at time
0
to line
1
at time
x
. Then there exists a constant
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
\epsilon>0
, we have

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\langle(0,k)\rightarrow x\rangle-{2\sqrt{2kx}}}{k^{3/7}\log^{d}k}>\epsilon\right)<\infty.

The intuition behind Theorem
6.3
is as follows. Since the Airy line ensemble arises as the scaling limit at the edge of Dyson’s Brownian motion, we can loosely interpret it as an infinite sequence of Brownian motions conditioned never to intersect. This intuition is made rigorous by the Brownian Gibbs property for the Airy line ensemble. This property states that conditioned on the values of
\mathcal{A}
on the boundary of a region, inside that region
\mathcal{A}
consists of independent Brownian bridges, conditioned so that the whole process remains nonintersecting and continuous. The typical spacing between the
k
th and
(k+1)
st Airy lines is
O(k^{-1/3})
, so this picture, along with Brownian scaling, suggests that on
o(k^{-2/3})
time scales,
\mathcal{A}_{k}
behaves like an independent Brownian motion.

It is reasonable to expect that the last passage path across
\mathcal{A}
from
(0,k)
to
(x,1)
spends roughly the same amount of time –
O(k^{-1})
– on each Airy line. By the above heuristic, Airy lines behave like Brownian motions on this scale, suggesting that
\langle(0,k)\rightarrow x\rangle
should be close to the corresponding Brownian last passage value of
2\sqrt{2kx}
. Theorem
6.3
proves this.

We believe that
\langle(0,k)\rightarrow x\rangle
still behaves like a Brownian last passage value at a finer precision. In particular, we expect that the true fluctuation of
\langle(0,k)\rightarrow x\rangle
around
2\sqrt{2kx}
should be
O(k^{-1/6})
as in Brownian last passage percolation.
While the
o(k^{3/7}\log^{d}k)
error we get could be improved by a more careful application of our methods, even our most optimistic heuristic proofs of the above theorem did not yield this error. It would be of interest to improve the above result to get a bound that is
o(1)
as
k\to\infty
, as this would yield a slightly nicer representation of the Airy sheet in the limit; see Problem
14.2
.

To prove Theorem
6.3
we require structural results about the Airy line ensemble from
Dauvergne and Virág 2021a
. The Brownian Gibbs property suggests that if we sample the points in
\mathcal{A}
on a fine grid, then what lies in between is simply independent Brownian motions, conditioned not to intersect only when the grid points are close together.
Dauvergne and Virág 2021a
make this picture rigorous. To state the results of that paper, we need two definitions. These definitions are illustrated in Figure
7
.

Definition 6.4
.

Fix

. For a fixed

s_{r}=rt/\ell

r\in\{0,1,\dots,\ell\}

and
\mathcal{A}_{i+1}
are close at one of the two endpoints

|\mathcal{A}_{i}(s_{r-1})-\mathcal{A}_{i+1}(s_{r-1})|\leq\delta\quad\;\text{or}\;\quad|\mathcal{A}_{i}(s_{r})-\mathcal{A}_{i+1}(s_{r})|\leq\delta.

The

(b)

Proof of Theorem
6.3

We set
x=1

\mathcal{B}^{k}=\mathcal{B}^{k}(1,{\left\lceil k^{2/3+\gamma}\right\rceil},k^{-1/3-\gamma/4})

Step 1: Splitting up the paths.

\mathcal{B}_{k,i}=H_{k,i}+R_{k,i}+X_{k,i}

for
r\in\{0,\dots,{\left\lceil k^{2/3+\gamma}\right\rceil}\}
, and the error term
X_{k,i}
is equal to zero except for on intervals
[s_{r-1},s_{r}]
where the vertex
(i,r)
is in a component of size greater than one in the graph
G_{2k}
. On such intervals,
X_{k,i}
is the difference between a Brownian bridge from
0
to
0
and a Brownian bridge conditioned to avoid
U_{i,r}-1
other Brownian bridges with certain start and endpoints. Here
U_{i,r}
is the size of the component of
(i,r)
in
G_{2k}
and the two Brownian bridges used in the definition of
X_{k,i}
are independent.

By Lemma
6.9
applied twice, we have that

L(H_{k})+F(R_{k})+F(X_{k})\leq L(\mathcal{B}^{k})\leq L(H_{k})+L(R_{k})+L(X_{k}).

(40)

By Theorem
2.5
, the main term

L(H_{k})=2\sqrt{2k}+Y_{k}k^{-1/6},

(41)

where
\{Y_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of random variables satisfying a tail bound

\mathbb{P}(|Y_{k}|>m)\leq ce^{-dm^{3/2}}

for
c,d
not depending on
m
and
k
. To translate Theorem
2.5
to a bound on last passage values, we have used the identity (
16
).

Step 2: Bounding the piecewise linear term.
First, we have the bound

|L(R_{k})|,|F(R_{k})|\leq M_{k},

where
M_{k}
is the maximum absolute slope of any of the piecewise linear segments in
R_{k}
. The slopes in
R_{k}
come from increments in the Airy line ensemble minus the increments of the Brownian motions
H_{k}
on the grid points. Recalling that
S_{k}(\ell)=\{1,\dots,k\}\times\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, we have the following upper bound for
M_{k}
:

{\left\lceil k^{2/3+\gamma}\right\rceil}\left[\max_{(i,r)\in S_{k}({\left\lceil k^{2/3+\gamma}\right\rceil})}|H_{k,i}(s_{r})-H_{k,i}(s_{r-1})|+\max_{(i,r)\in S_{k}({\left\lceil k^{2/3+\gamma}\right\rceil})}|\mathcal{A}_{i}(s_{r})-\mathcal{A}_{i}(s_{r-1})|\right].

By a standard Gaussian bound on the first term and Theorem
6.8
for the second term, for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
we have that

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(M_{k}\geq k^{1/3+\gamma/2}\log^{d}k\right)<\infty.

(42)

Step 3: Bounding the large component error.

To bound
L(X_{k})
and
F(X_{k})
, we divide
\{1,\dots,k\}
into
n={\left\lceil k^{2/3+\gamma}\right\rceil}
intervals

I_{k,i}=\left\{{\left\lfloor\frac{(i-1)k}{n}\right\rfloor}+1,\dots,{\left\lfloor\frac{ik}{n}\right\rfloor}\right\},\qquad i\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

This, and the division of time into the intervals
[s_{r-1},s_{r}]
for
r\in\{1,\dots,n\}
breaks the line ensemble
X_{k}
into
n^{2}
boxes. Each last passage path can meet at most
2n-1
of these boxes. So we have that

L(X_{k})\leq(2n-1)Z_{k},

(43)

where
Z_{k}
is the maximal last passage value among all values that start and end in the same box (including the boundary). Specifically,

Z_{k}=\max_{(i,r)\in[1,n]^{2}}\max\left\{X_{k}[(\ell_{1},t_{1})\rightarrow(\ell_{2},t_{2})]:\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in I_{k,i},\;t_{1},t_{2}\in[s_{r-1},s_{r}]\right\}.

We have that
Z_{k}\leq N_{k}D_{k}
, where

\displaystyle N_{k}

\displaystyle=\max_{(i,r)\in[1,n]^{2}}\left|\left\{\ell\in I_{k,i}:X_{k,\ell}|_{[s_{r-1},s_{r}]}\neq 0\right\}\right|\quad\;\text{and}\;

\displaystyle D_{k}

\displaystyle=\max\bigg\{|X_{k,\ell}(t)-X_{k,\ell}(m)|:\ell\in[1,k],t,m\in[s_{r-1},s_{r}]\text{ for some }r\in\{1,\dots,n\}\bigg\}.

That is,
N_{k}
is the maximum number of nonzero line segments in any box, and
D_{k}
is the maximum increment over any line segment in a box. Since
X_{k,\ell}=\mathcal{B}_{k,\ell}-H_{k,\ell}-R_{k,\ell}
, we can bound
D_{k}
in terms of the deviations of the other paths. To bound the deviation of
R_{k,\ell}
, we use the bound on
M_{k}
above. The deviation of
H_{k,\ell}
can be bounded with standard bounds on Gaussian random variables. On the event where
\mathcal{B}^{k}|_{\{1,\dots,k\}\times[0,1]}=\mathcal{A}|_{\{1,\dots,k\}\times[0,1]}
, we can bound the deviation of
\mathcal{B}_{k,\ell}
using Theorem
6.8
. Therefore for some constant
d\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(D_{k}>k^{-1/3-\gamma/2}\log^{d}k,\;\mathcal{B}^{k}=\mathcal{A}|_{\{1,\dots,k\}\times[0,1]}\right)<\infty.

(44)

Combining equations (
44
) and (
39
) gives

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(D_{k}>k^{-1/3-\gamma/2}\log^{d}k\right)<\infty.

(45)

The quantity
N_{k}
is equal to the maximum number of edges in the graph
G_{k}
in a region of the form
I_{k,i}\times\{r\}
for some
r\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. This can be bounded by using Proposition
6.7
and a union bound, which yields

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(N_{k}>k^{1/3-\gamma}k^{-3\gamma/4}\log^{2}k\right)<\infty.

Combining this with the bound in (
43
) and (
45
) implies that for some constant
d>0
, that

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(L(X_{k})>k^{2/3-5\gamma/4}\log^{d}k\right)<\infty.

(46)

We can symmetrically bound
F(X_{k})
.

Step 4: Putting it all together.

By combining the inequalities (
40
), (
41
), (
42
) and (
46
), we get that for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
,

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(|L(\mathcal{B}^{k})-2\sqrt{2k}|>k^{2/3-5\gamma/4}\log^{d}k+k^{1/3+\gamma/2}\log^{d}k\right)<\infty.

Taking
\gamma=4/21
and increasing the power of
\log k
from
d
to
d+1
completes the proof.
∎

7
Melon paths are parabolas

In this section we use the results of Section
6
to establish bounds on the location of melon last passage paths. We will also establish that last passage paths that start or end close together meet with high probability. These facts will allow us to construct the Airy sheet in Section
8
. Throughout the section we write
W=WB^{n}
for the melon, and use the last passage and scaling notation
\bar{x}=2xn^{-1/3}
and
\hat{y}=1+2yn^{-1/3}
introduced in Section
2.5
.

Let
\hat{Z}^{n}_{k}(x,y)=1+n^{-1/3}Z^{n}_{k}(x,y)
denote the jump time from line
k+1
to
k
on the rightmost last passage path from
\bar{x}
to
\hat{y}
in the melon, see (
10
). Observe that
Z^{n}_{k}(x,y)
is nonincreasing in
k
, and
Z^{n}_{k}(x,y)
is a nondecreasing function in both
x
and
y
by monotonicity of last passage paths, Lemma
3.6
. The next lemma gives asymptotics for
Z^{n}_{k}
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
K
be a compact subset of
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
. Then

\sup_{(x,y)\in K}\left|Z^{n}_{k}(x,y)+\sqrt{\frac{k}{2x}}\right|={\mathfrak{o}}(\sqrt{k})

(47)

and
Z^{n}_{k}(x,y)
is tight as a function of
n
for each fixed
k\in\mathbb{N},(x,y)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

We first fix
x,y\in K
, rescale by
n^{1/6}
and center so that the triangle inequality

\{\bar{x}\rightarrow(\hat{z},k)\}+\{(\hat{z},k)\rightarrow\hat{y}\}\leq\{\bar{x}\rightarrow\hat{y}\}

reads

F^{n}_{k}(x,z)+G^{n}_{k}(z,y)\leq H_{n}(x,y)

(48)

with

\displaystyle H_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{1/6}\{\bar{x}\rightarrow\hat{y}\}-2n^{2/3}-2(y-x)n^{1/3},

\displaystyle F^{n}_{k}(x,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{1/6}(\{\bar{x}\rightarrow(\hat{z},k)\}-W^{n}_{k}(\hat{z}))+2xn^{1/3},

\displaystyle G^{n}_{k}(z,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{1/6}(W^{n}_{k}(\hat{z})+\{(\hat{z},k)\rightarrow\hat{y}\})-2yn^{1/3}-2n^{2/3}.

The basic proof strategy for bounding
Z^{n}_{k}(x,y)
is as follows. On the one hand,

F^{n}_{k}(x,Z^{n}_{k}(x,y))+G^{n}_{k}(Z^{n}_{k}(x,y),y)=H_{n}(x,y)

(49)

On the other hand, we can show that
F^{n}_{k}(x,z)+G^{n}_{k}(z,y)<H_{n}(x,y)
whenever
z
is sufficiently far away from
-\sqrt{k/(2x)}
.
To show this inequality, we use the bound on melon last passage values given in Proposition
6.1
to control
F^{n}_{k}
, and Theorem
2.5
which implies that
H_{n}(x,y)
is tight in
n
for
x,y
fixed.

We will show that for every
\epsilon\in(0,1)
we have

\sup_{z:\;|z+\sqrt{k/(2x)}|>\epsilon\sqrt{k}}F^{n}_{k}(x,z)+G^{n}_{k}(z,y)\leq-\epsilon^{2}\sqrt{kx}/2+{\mathfrak{o}}(\sqrt{k}).

(50)

By Lemma
3.3
,

F^{n}_{k}(x,\cdot)
is monotonically increasing and
G^{n}_{k}(\cdot,y)
is monotonically decreasing. We can use this monotonicity to bound the left hand side of (
50
) by a supremum over a finite set.
Let

A=(12\epsilon^{2})^{-1}\mathbb{Z}\cap[1/4,2],

and for
z\in[-n^{1/3}+x,y]
, define

\begin{split}{\left\lfloor z\right\rfloor}_{n,k}&=\max\{w\in-\sqrt{k/x}A\cup\{x-n^{1/3}\}:w<z\}\qquad\;\text{and}\;\\
{\left\lceil z\right\rceil}_{n,k}&=\min\{w\in-\sqrt{k/x}A\cup\{y\}:w>z\}.\end{split}

(51)

We also set
{\left\lfloor x-n^{1/3}\right\rfloor}_{n,k}=x-n^{1/3}
and
{\left\lceil y\right\rceil}_{n,k}=y
. The monotonicity of
F^{n}_{k}(x,\cdot)
and
G^{n}_{k}(\cdot,y)
implies that the left hand side of (
50
) is bounded above by

\sup_{z:\;|z+\sqrt{k/(2x)}|>\epsilon\sqrt{k}}F^{n}_{k}(x,{\left\lceil z\right\rceil}_{n,k})+G^{n}_{k}({\left\lfloor z\right\rfloor}_{n,k},y).

(52)

Therefore to show (
50
), we just need to show that (
52
) is bounded above by
-\epsilon^{2}\sqrt{kx}/2+{\mathfrak{o}}(\sqrt{k})
. The quantity in (
52
) is easier to work with since we are taking a supremum over only finitely many distinct terms. Moreover, the number of terms is uniformly bounded in
n
and
k
, so it is enough to control the terms individually. Note that the bound on
F^{n}_{k}(x,z)+G^{n}_{k}(z,y)
in (
52
) is much cruder when
z\notin-\sqrt{k/x}A
.
This crude estimate will suffice for our purposes since the quantity inside the supremum in (
50
) is not close to the maximum for such
z
.

In particular, setting
z_{k,a}=-a\sqrt{k/x}
, it is enough to show that

F^{n}_{k}(x,{\left\lceil z_{k,a}\right\rceil}_{n,k})+G^{n}_{k}({\left\lfloor z_{k,a}\right\rfloor}_{n,k},y)\leq-\epsilon^{2}\sqrt{kx}/2+{\mathfrak{o}}(\sqrt{k})

(53)

for every fixed
a\in[1/4,1/\sqrt{2}-\epsilon]\cup[1/\sqrt{2}+\epsilon,2]
.

To prove (
53
), we establish pointwise bounds on
F^{n}_{k}
and
G^{n}_{k}
. Proposition
6.1
gives that for a fixed
a>0
we have

F^{n}_{k}(x,z_{k,a})=2\sqrt{kx}(\sqrt{2}-a)+{\mathfrak{o}}(\sqrt{k}).

(54)

Proposition
6.1
also yields the bound

F^{n}_{k}(x,y)=2\sqrt{2kx}+{\mathfrak{o}}(\sqrt{k}).

(55)

The triangle inequality (
48
) with
x^{\prime}=x/(2a^{2})
gives

G^{n}_{k}(z_{k,a},y)\leq H_{n}(x^{\prime},y)-F^{n}_{k}(x^{\prime},z_{k,a}).

(56)

Now,
H_{n}(x^{\prime},y)
is equal to a rescaled and shifted Brownian last passage value by Proposition
4.1
. Therefore by Theorem
2.5
and (
16
), which together give bounds on single Brownian last passage values, it is tight in
n
and hence
H_{n}(x^{\prime},y)={\mathfrak{o}}(\sqrt{k})
. Moreover, Proposition
6.1
gives that

F^{n}_{k}(x^{\prime},z_{k,a})=2\sqrt{2kx^{\prime}}+2z_{k,a}x^{\prime}+{\mathfrak{o}}(\sqrt{k})=\frac{\sqrt{kx}}{a}+{\mathfrak{o}}(\sqrt{k})

and so

G^{n}_{k}(z_{k,a},y)\leq-\frac{\sqrt{kx}}{a}+{\mathfrak{o}}(\sqrt{k}).

(57)

We also have the bound

G^{n}_{k}(x-n^{1/3},y)\leq H_{n}(0,y)-F^{n}_{k}(0,x-n^{1/3})=H_{n}(0,y)={\mathfrak{o}}(\sqrt{k}).

(58)

The first equality here follows from the fact that
F^{n}_{k}(0,\cdot)=0
, and the second equality again follows from Theorem
2.5
.

Finally, the bound in (
53
) follows from (
54
) and (
58
) when
a\leq\inf A
, from (
54
) and (
57
) when
a\in(\inf A,\sup A)
, and from (
55
) and (
57
) when
a\geq\sup A
.

Now by Theorem
2.5
,
H^{n}(x,y)
is tight in
n
. With (
50
) and (
49
) this implies that
Z^{n}_{k}(x,y)=\sqrt{k/(2x)}+{\mathfrak{o}}(\sqrt{k})
for
x,y
fixed. Since
Z^{n}_{k}(x,y)
is monotone in
x
and
y
, the claim (
47
) follows.

For every
k,x,y
, the sequence
Z^{n}_{k}(x,y)
is tight in
n
since
Z^{n}_{k}(x,y)+\sqrt{k/(2x)}={\mathfrak{o}}(\sqrt{k})
and we have the monotonicity
y=Z^{n}_{1}(x,y)\geq Z^{n}_{2}(x,y)\geq\dots
.
∎

Lemma
7.1
has an important consequence for disjointness of last passage paths.
Recall that two paths
\pi
and
\tau
are
disjoint
if either
\pi>\tau
or
\tau>\pi
on the intersection of the interiors of both their domains. Recall that
\pi\{x,y\}
is the rightmost last passage path in the melon from
(x,n)
to
(y,1)
.

Lemma 7.2
.

Fix
x>0
and
y_{1}<y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big(\pi\{\bar{x}-\bar{\epsilon},\hat{y}_{1}\}\;\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\;\pi\{\bar{x}+\bar{\epsilon},\hat{y}_{2}\}\text{ are disjoint}\big)=0.

(59)

Proof.

We will prove a stronger statement, with the leftmost last passage path
\pi^{-}\{\bar{x}-\bar{\epsilon},\hat{y}_{1}\}
replacing one of the rightmost paths
\pi\{\bar{x}-\bar{\epsilon},\hat{y}_{1}\}
. Disjointness of
\pi\{\bar{x}-\bar{\epsilon},\hat{y}_{1}\}
and
\pi\{\bar{x}+\bar{\epsilon},\hat{y}_{2}\}
implies disjointness of
\pi^{-}\{\bar{x}-\bar{\epsilon},\hat{y}_{1}\}
and
\pi\{\bar{x}+\bar{\epsilon},\hat{y}_{2}\}
by monotonicity.

By Lemma
4.5
, disjointness of the paths
\pi^{-}\{\bar{x}-\bar{\epsilon},\hat{y}_{1}\}
and
\pi\{\bar{x}+\bar{\epsilon},\hat{y}_{2}\}
is equivalent to disjointness of the original Brownian last passage paths
\pi^{-}[\bar{x}-\bar{\epsilon},\hat{y}_{1}]
and
\pi[\bar{x}+\bar{\epsilon},\hat{y}_{2}]
. Here
\pi^{-}[\bar{x}-\bar{\epsilon},\hat{y}_{1}]
is the leftmost last passage path in
B^{n}
from
\bar{x}-\bar{\epsilon}
to
\hat{y}_{1}
. Hence the probability in (
59
) is bounded above by

\mathbb{P}\big(\pi^{-}[\bar{x}-\bar{\epsilon},\hat{y}_{1}]\;\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\;\pi[\bar{x}+\bar{\epsilon},\hat{y}_{2}]\text{ are disjoint}\big).

(60)

By time-reversal symmetry of the increments of Brownian motion under the map
t\mapsto 1-t

the probability in (
60
) equals

\mathbb{P}\big(\pi^{-}[-\bar{y}_{2},1-\bar{x}-\bar{\epsilon}]\;\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\;\pi[-\bar{y}_{1},1-\bar{x}+\bar{\epsilon}]\text{ are disjoint}\big).

(61)

By translation invariance and Brownian scaling, the probability (
61
) remains unchanged if the points
-\bar{y}_{2},1-\bar{x}-\bar{\epsilon},-\bar{y}_{1},1-\bar{x}+\bar{\epsilon}
are replaced by their images under any linear function
L(t)=at+b
for some
a>0
. In particular, for each
n
we may choose the linear function
L=L_{n,\epsilon}
sending
-\bar{y}_{1}\mapsto\bar{y}:=2(\bar{y}_{2}-\bar{y}_{1})
and
1-\bar{x}+\bar{\epsilon}\mapsto 1
. For
t\in[-1,2]
, we have

L_{n,\epsilon}(t)=(1-2\bar{y}_{2}+\bar{y}_{1}+\bar{x}-\bar{\epsilon})t+2\bar{y}_{2}-\bar{y}_{1}+O(n^{-2/3}).

Therefore for all large enough
n
, we have

L_{n,\epsilon}(-\bar{y}_{2})=\bar{y}_{2}-\bar{y}_{1}+O(n^{-2/3})\geq 0,\qquad L_{n,\epsilon}(1-\bar{x}-\bar{\epsilon})=1-2\bar{\epsilon}+O(n^{-2/3})\geq 1-3\bar{\epsilon}.

(62)

For such
n
, after translating back to melon paths we get that the probability in (
61
) is equal to

\mathbb{P}\big(\pi^{-}\{L_{n,\epsilon}(-\bar{y}_{2}),L_{n,\epsilon}(1-\bar{x}-\bar{\epsilon})\}\;\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\;\pi\{\bar{y},\hat{0}\}\text{ are disjoint}\big).

By monotonicity of last passage paths, Lemma
3.6
, and (
62
), this is bounded above by

\mathbb{P}\big(\pi^{-}\{0,1-3\bar{\epsilon}\}\;\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\;\pi\{\bar{y},\hat{0}\}\text{ are disjoint}\big).

(63)

Now, the path
\pi^{-}\{0,1-3\bar{\epsilon}\}
starts at zero and therefore simply follows the top line in the melon, so the paths
\pi^{-}\{0,1-3\bar{\epsilon}\}
and
\pi\{\bar{y},\hat{0}\}
are disjoint if and only if
\pi\{\bar{y},\hat{0}\}
jumps up to line
1
after time
1-3\bar{\epsilon}
. This jump time is
\hat{Z}^{n}_{1}(y,0)
, so (
63
) is equal to

\mathbb{P}\big(Z^{n}_{1}(y,0)\geq-3\epsilon\big).

Hence to prove (
59
) we just need to show that

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big(Z^{n}_{1}(y,0)\geq-3\epsilon\big)=0.

(64)

To prove (
64
), we just need to show that any subsequential limit
Z_{1}(x,0)
of the sequence of random variables
\{Z^{n}_{1}(x,0):n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is strictly negative almost surely. Note that this sequence is tight by Lemma
7.1
.

Let
A^{n}
denote the rescaling of the melon
WB^{n}
in Theorem
2.1
. By that theorem and Lemma
7.1
, the collection of random variables
(A^{n},\{Z^{n}_{k}(x,0):k\in\mathbb{N}\})
is tight. Let
(\mathcal{A},\{Z_{k}(x,0):k\in\mathbb{N}\})
denote a joint subsequential limit of these random variables. The asymptotics in Lemma
7.1
guarantee that

\lim_{k\to\infty}Z_{k}(x,0)=-\infty.

(65)

almost surely. Moreover, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
the points
\{(Z_{i}(x,0),i):i\leq k\}
are jump times along a last passage path from
(Z_{k}(x,0),k)
to
(1,0)
in
\mathcal{A}
since the prelimiting points satisfied this property.

By (
65
), there exists a random
K\in\mathbb{N}
such that
Z_{1}(x,0)
is a jump time on a last passage path in
\mathcal{A}
from
(-1,K)
to
(0,1)
. Now, by Proposition
2.2
, the top
k
lines of
\mathcal{A}
restricted to the interval
[-1,0]
are absolutely continuous with respect to
k
independent Brownian motions. Therefore for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, all jump times on any last passage path in
\mathcal{A}
from
(-1,k)
to
(0,1)
are contained in the open interval
(-1,0)
. Hence all jump times on a last passage path in
\mathcal{A}
from
(-1,K)
to
(0,1)
are contained in the open interval
(-1,0)
. In particular,
Z_{1}(x,0)<0
almost surely, as desired.
∎

8
Constructing the Airy sheet

In this section, we construct the joint limit of last passage values at two times, known as the Airy sheet. We start by recalling the definition given in the introduction. Recall the notation
\langle\rangle
for last passage values across
\mathcal{A}
from Section
2.5
.

Definition 8.1
.

The
Airy sheet
is a random continuous function
\mathcal{S}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
so that

(i)

\mathcal{S}
has the same law as
\mathcal{S}(\cdot+t,\cdot+t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

(ii)

\mathcal{S}
can be coupled with an Airy line ensemble
\mathcal{A}
so that
\mathcal{S}(0,\cdot)=\mathcal{A}_{1}(\cdot)
and for all
(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}\times\mathbb{Q}^{2}
there exists a random variable
K_{x,y,z}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
k\geq K_{x,y,z}
, almost surely

\big\langle(-\sqrt{k/(2x)},k)\rightarrow z\big\rangle-\big\langle(-\sqrt{k/(2x)},k)\rightarrow y\big\rangle=\mathcal{S}(x,z)-\mathcal{S}(x,y).

We leave the existence of the Airy sheet to Theorem
8.3
, and first show that it is unique.

Proposition 8.2
.

The Airy sheet is unique in law.

Proof.

By equation (5.15) in
Prähofer and Spohn 2002

\mathcal{A}_{1}(z)+z^{2}
is stationary and ergodic. By Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem for any fixed
y
we have that almost surely,

\mathcal{S}(0,y)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{2m}\int_{-m}^{m}\mathcal{S}(0,y)-(\mathcal{S}(0,z)+z^{2})dz+\mathbb{E}\mathcal{A}_{1}(0).

We can then use property (i) to translate the above formula (applied to
\mathcal{S}(0,y-x)
) to get an almost sure formula for any
\mathcal{S}(x,y)
:

\mathcal{S}(x,y)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{2m}\int_{-m}^{m}\mathcal{S}(x,y)-(\mathcal{S}(x,z+x)+z^{2})dz+\mathbb{E}\mathcal{A}_{1}(0).

When
(x,y)\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}\times\mathbb{Q}
, the integrand on right hand side above is determined by condition (ii) for rational values of
z
, and hence is determined by that condition for all values of
z
by continuity. Therefore
\{\mathcal{S}(x,y):(x,y)\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}\times\mathbb{Q}\}
is determined by the definition of
\mathcal{S}
. By stationarity and continuity, this implies that the distribution of
\mathcal{S}
is uniquely determined by its definition.
∎

Remark 8.1
.

We can exchange condition (ii) in Definition
8.1
for the following Busemann function definition. Almost surely, for all
x>0
and
y,z\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that

Remark 8.2
.

One of the crucial ideas in Definition

is to look at
differences
of last passage values, rather than just the last passage values themselves. Hopefully, the reader can now appreciate the importance of this. We showed that differences of last passage values are contained in

via coalescence arguments, which only required that last passage paths starting at distinct points

diverge away from the Airy line ensemble corner. Ultimately, this just required that the error estimate on the

Lemma 9.1

10
The directed landscape

The goal of this section is to construct the directed landscape, a scale-invariant, stationary, independent increment process with respect to metric composition. The following definition is based on analogies from last passage percolation. Recall that

\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}=\{(x,t;y,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:t<s\}.

Let
C(\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow},\mathbb{R})
be the space of continuous functions from
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
to
\mathbb{R}
with the uniform-on-compact topology.

Definition 10.1
.

A
directed landscape
is a random function
\mathcal{L}
taking values in the space
C(\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow},\mathbb{R})
that satisfies the following properties.

I.

(Airy sheet marginals) For any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
s>0
the increment over time interval
[t,t+s^{3})
,

(x,y)\mapsto\mathcal{\mathcal{L}}(x,t;y,t+s^{3})

is an Airy sheet of scale
s
.

II.

(Independent increments) For any disjoint time intervals
\{(t_{i},s_{i}):i\in\{1,\dots k\}\}
, the random functions

\mathcal{L}(\cdot,t_{i};\cdot,s_{i}),\quad i\in\{1,\dots,k\}

are independent.

III.

(Metric composition law) Almost surely, for any
r<s<t
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
we have that

\mathcal{L}(x,r;y,t)=\max_{z\in\mathbb{R}}[\mathcal{L}(x,r;z,s)+\mathcal{L}(z,s;y,t)]\quad\text{ for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}.

We note here that the metric composition law implies a reverse
triangle inequality
for the directed landscape. For any
r<s<t
and
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}
we have that

\mathcal{L}(x,r;y,t)\geq\mathcal{L}(x,r;z,s)+\mathcal{L}(z,s;y,t).

(100)

Condition III could be weakened so that the metric composition law only holds at every fixed quintuple
(r,s,t,x,y)
almost surely, rather than at all quintuples simultaneously.

We need to show that an object satisfying the above properties exists and is unique. Before constructing the directed landscape, we note that if such an object
\mathcal{L}
exists and is unique, then it must have the following symmetries.

Lemma 10.2
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the directed landscape. Assuming that
\mathcal{L}
exists and is unique, we have the following equalities in distribution as functions in
C(\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow},\mathbb{R})
. Here
r,c\in\mathbb{R}
, and
q>0
.

1.

(Time stationarity)

\mathcal{L}(x,t;y,t+s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}(x,t+r;y,t+s+r).

2.

(Spatial stationarity)

\mathcal{L}(x,t;y,t+s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}(x+c,t;y+c,t+s).

3.

(Flip symmetry)

\mathcal{L}(x,t;y,t+s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}(-y,-s-t;-x,-t).

4.

(Skew stationarity)

\mathcal{L}(x,t;y,t+s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}(x+ct,t;y+ct+sc,t+s)+s^{-1}[(x-y-sc)^{2}-(x-y)^{2}].

5.

(Rescaling)

\mathcal{L}(x,t;y,t+s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}q\mathcal{L}(q^{-2}x,q^{-3}t;q^{-2}y,q^{-3}(t+s)).

Proof.

All of these statements can be checked by straightforward computation. The only tools needed are symmetries and rescaling properties of the Airy sheet, along with the definition of the directed landscape.
∎

We will also work with the process

11
Convergence to the directed landscape

In this section, we show that the directed landscape is the distributional limit of last passage percolation. For each

, let

(112)

at those points. It is defined on any compact subset of

Let

a>1
, the equivalent of (

8.4
.

Now let
\mathcal{J}
be a subsequential limit of

. By the definition of
\mathcal{J}_{n}
and the Airy sheet convergence in Theorem

, the marginals

are all rescaled Airy sheets. Also,

Moreover,
\mathcal{J}

disjoint intervals since when

12
Directed geodesics

In this section, we construct geodesics in the directed landscape
\mathcal{L}
. First, for a continuous path
\pi:[t,s]\to\mathbb{R}
, we can define the
length
of
\pi
by

\int d\mathcal{L}\circ\pi=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\inf_{\{t=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{k}=s\}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{L}(\pi(t_{i-1}),t_{i-1};\pi(t_{i}),t_{i}).

(122)

In other words, the length of
\pi
is the infimum over all partitions of the sum of increments in
\mathcal{L}
along
\pi
. This definition is the analogy of defining curve length in Euclidean space by piecewise linear approximation. By the triangle inequality (
100
) for
\mathcal{L}
, we always have

\int d\mathcal{L}\circ\pi\leq\mathcal{L}(\pi(t),t;\pi(s),s).

We say that
\pi
is a
directed geodesic
from
(\pi(t),t)
to
(\pi(s),s)
if equality holds. This is equivalent to saying that equality holds in (
122
) for all subdivisions before taking any infima. A simple induction argument shows that if for any
t_{0}=t<t_{1}<t_{2}<t_{3}=s
we have

\int d\mathcal{L}\circ\pi=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathcal{L}(\pi(t_{i-1}),t_{i-1};\pi(t_{i}),t_{i}),

(123)

then
\pi
is a directed geodesic.

Theorem 12.1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the directed landscape, and fix
u=(x,t;y,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
. Then almost surely, there exists a unique directed geodesic
\Pi_{u}
from
(x,t)
to
(y,s)
.

By the properties of the directed landscape in Lemma
10.2
, the distribution of
\Pi_{u}
is independent of the point
u\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
up to symmetry. Let
u=(0,0;0,1)
. For
r>0
the path
\Pi_{r^{3}u}(\cdot)
has the same distribution as
r^{2}\Pi_{u}(\cdot/r^{3})
. The distribution of
\Pi_{v}
for any
v
can be obtained as a translated shear of this family. So it suffices to prove Theorem
12.1
for
u=(0,0;0,1)
.

To obtain tightness on the path locations we will use Lemma
9.5
. This lemma can be used in conjunction with Proposition
9.2
to give a bound over multiple maximum locations at once. For
(x,t;y,t+r^{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
we define
Z^{+}_{t,r}(x,y)
as the rightmost maximizer of the function

f(z)=\mathcal{L}(x,t;z,t+r^{3}/2)+\mathcal{L}(z,t+r^{3}/2;y,t+r^{3}),

and similarly define
Z^{-}_{t,r}(x,y)
as the leftmost maximizer.

Lemma 12.2
.

Fix
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
r>0
, and let
K=[a,a+b]\times[c,c+b]\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\exists(x,y)\in K\text{ such that }|Z^{+}_{t,r}(x,y)-(x+y)/2|

\displaystyle>mr^{2}\big)\leq\left(\frac{b}{mr^{2}}\right)^{2}c_{1}e^{-c_{2}m^{3}}

for constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. The same bound holds for
Z^{-}
.

Proof.

The functions
Z^{+}_{t,r}
and
Z^{-}_{t,r}
are nondecreasing functions of
x
and
y
by Proposition
9.2
. Hence to prove the theorem, it suffices to take a union bound over
(x,y)
in the grid

mr^{2}\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:d(x,K)\leq\sqrt{2}mr^{2}\}.

We have enlarged the compact set
K
to guarantee that we have an outer grid boundary containing
K
. The bound then follows if we can show that for every fixed
x,y
,

\mathbb{P}\big(|Z^{+}_{t,r}(x,y)-(x+y)/2|>mr^{2}\big)\leq c_{1}e^{-c_{2}m^{3}},

and similarly for
Z^{-}
. Now, the probability on the left side above is independent of
x,y
by the spatial and skew stationarity of
\mathcal{L}
, Lemma
10.2
. Moreover, for
x=y=0
, the function
f
is then a sum of independent Airy processes of scale

. Applying Lemma
9.5
then yields the desired bound.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem

. As in Lévy’s construction of Brownian motion, our proof gives an essentially optimal modulus of continuity bound on
\Pi_{u}
.

Proposition 12.3
.

There exists a random constant
C
such that the path
\Pi_{u}
in Theorem

with

satisfies

|\Pi_{u}(t_{1})-\Pi_{u}(t_{2})|\leq C|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{2/3}\log^{1/3}\left(\frac{2}{|t_{1}-t_{2}|}\right)

for all

\mathbb{P}(C>b)\leq c_{1}e^{-c_{2}b^{3}}

for some constants

.

Proof of Theorem
12.1
and Proposition
12.3
.

We first approximate the path
\Pi=\Pi_{u}
on dyadic rationals.
Let
\Pi_{1}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be chosen so that
\Pi_{1}(0)=\Pi_{1}(1)=0
and

\mathcal{L}(0,0;\Pi_{1}(1/2),1/2)+\mathcal{L}(\Pi_{1}(1/2),1/2;0,1)=\mathcal{L}(u),

and so that

is linear at times in between. Now for

and

. We then recursively define

\Pi_{n}(t_{n-1,i})=\Pi_{n-1}(t_{n-1,i})
for all

, and so that for odd

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\Pi_{n}(t_{n,i-1}),t_{n,i-1};\Pi_{n}(t_{n,i}),t_{n,i})

\displaystyle+\mathcal{L}(\Pi_{n}(t_{n,i}),t_{n,i};\Pi_{n}(t_{n,i+1}),t_{n,i+1})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(\Pi_{n}(t_{n,i-1}),t_{n,i-1};\Pi_{n}(t_{n,i+1}),t_{n,i+1}).

Define
\Pi_{n}
to be linear at times in between. Such a sequence of paths

exists by property III of
\mathcal{L}
.
We now show that
\Pi_{n}
has a uniform limit satisfying the desired modulus of continuity.

Fix
b>0
, and for

, define
h_{k}=b2^{-2k/3}k^{1/3}
. Also define
\ell_{0}=0
and for

\ell_{k}=h_{1}+\ldots+h_{k}
. We recall the notation

and
Z^{-}_{t,r}
from Lemma

.
Define

\displaystyle A_{k}

\displaystyle=\Bigg\{\text{There exists }(x,y)\in[-\ell_{k},\ell_{k}]^{2},i\in\{1,\dots,2^{k}\},\text{ such that either }

\displaystyle\left|Z^{+}_{t_{k,i-1},2^{-k/3}}(x,y)-(x+y)/2\right|\geq h_{k+1}\;\text{ or }\;\left|Z^{-}_{t_{k,i-1},2^{-k/3}}(x,y)-(x+y)/2\right|\geq h_{k+1}\Bigg\}.

By Lemma
12.2
and a union bound,

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{k\geq 0}A_{k}\right)\leq c_{1}e^{-c_{2}b^{3}}

(124)

for universal constants

. On the event

||\Pi_{1}||_{\infty}\leq\ell_{1}
. Also, if

13
Joint limits of last passage paths

In this section, we show that last passage paths in
\mathcal{L}_{n}
, defined in (
112
), converge jointly to directed geodesics in
\mathcal{L}
. The proof is essentially topological, once we establish a basic fact about the directed landscape. As a byproduct of the proof, we show that almost surely in
\mathcal{L}
, there exists a directed geodesic between every pair of points: Lemma
13.2
. This result is not implied by Theorem
12.1
, which only concerns geodesics between single points. Lemma
13.2
can also be used to give an alternate proof of existence and almost sure uniqueness of directed geodesics, but does not yield the strong modulus of continuity bound in Theorem
12.1
.

The proof of geodesic convergence is topological and follows from a deterministic convergence statement about particular functions
f:\mathbb{R}_{\uparrow}^{4}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
. We say that a function
f:\mathbb{R}_{\uparrow}^{4}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
landscape
if for every pair
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
and
s<r<t\in\mathbb{R}
we have that

f(x,s;y,t)=\max_{z\in\mathbb{R}}f(x,s;z,r)+f(z,r;y,t)

(126)

We say that a landscape
f
is
proper
if the following four conditions hold.

(i)

The landscape
f
is continuous.

(ii)

For every bounded set
[-b,b]^{4}\cap\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, there exists a constant
c_{b}
such that

\left|f(x,t;y,t+s)-\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{s}\right|\leq c_{b}

for all
(x,t;y,t+s)\in[-b,b]^{4}
.

(iii)

For every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, there exists a constant
b_{K}>0
such that the set where the function on the right hand side of (
126
) attains its maximum is contained in
[-b_{K},b_{K}]
for all
(x,s;y,t)\in K
and
r\in(s,t)
.

(iv)

For all
r<t
,
x\leq x^{\prime}
, and
y\leq y^{\prime}
, we have that

f(x,r;y,t)+f(x^{\prime},r;y^{\prime},t)\geq f(x,r;y^{\prime},t)+f(x,r;y^{\prime},t)

(127)

For any landscape
f
, we can define length
\int df\circ\pi
as in (
122
) with
f
in place of
\mathcal{L}
. Again, we say that a continuous function
\pi:[t,s]\to\mathbb{R}
is a geodesic from
(\pi(t),t)
to
(\pi(s),s)
if

\int df\circ\pi=f(\pi(t),t;\pi(s),s).

The directed landscape
\mathcal{L}
is almost surely a proper landscape. This follows by Lemma
10.8
(for (i) and (iii)), Proposition
10.6
(for (ii)), and Lemma
9.1
and continuity (for (iv)). Also, the last passage percolations
\mathcal{L}_{n}
are landscapes. Our goal is to prove a deterministic statement about convergence of geodesics. We need a notion of convergence that accommodates geodesics with different domains.

For this, let
\mathcal{G}
be the set of all continuous functions
\pi:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. For
\pi:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\in\mathcal{G}
, let

\mathfrak{g}\pi=\{(\pi(r),r):r\in[a,b]\}.

The set
\mathfrak{g}\pi
is the graph of
\pi
with the coordinates switched. This is the natural order of coordinates in landscape notation. A sequence of functions
\pi_{n}\to\pi
in
\mathcal{G}
if
\mathfrak{g}\pi_{n}\to\mathfrak{g}\pi
in the Hausdorff topology. When
\pi_{n},\pi
have the same domain, then this is equivalent to uniform convergence of functions.

Theorem 13.1
.

Let
f_{n}
be a sequence of landscapes converging to a proper landscape
f
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
. Suppose that for a fixed
(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, there is a unique geodesic
\pi
from
u
to
v
in
f
. Suppose that
(u_{n},v_{n})\to(u,v)
, and
that for all large enough
n
, there exists a geodesic
\pi_{n}
from
u_{n}
to
v_{n}
in
f_{n}
. Then
\pi_{n}\to\pi
in
\mathcal{G}
as
n\to\infty
.

We first show that geodesics exist in any proper landscape.
Recall that
\pi
is a
rightmost geodesic
from
(x,t)
to
(y,s)
if
\pi\geq\tau
for any other geodesic
\tau
from
(x,t)
to
(y,s)
. We can similarly define leftmost geodesics.

Lemma 13.2
.

Let
f
be a proper landscape. Then for any
(x,t;y,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, there exist rightmost and leftmost geodesics from
(x,t)
to
(y,s)
.

Proof.

We will show the existence of a rightmost geodesic
\pi
. The leftmost geodesic exists by a symmetric argument.
We define
\pi(t)=x
and
\pi(s)=y
, and for every
r\in(s,t)
, set
\pi
to be the rightmost maximizer of the function on the right hand side of (
126
). The function
\pi
is bounded by condition (iii) in the definition of proper landscape. We need to check that
\pi
is continuous and that
\pi
is a geodesic.

To check continuity, consider a sequence
r_{n}\to r\in[s,t]
.
We will use the equality

f(x,s;y,t)=f(x,s;\pi(r_{n}),r_{n})+f(\pi(r_{n}),r_{n};y,t).

(128)

First consider the case when
r_{n}\to s
. By continuity of
f
and the boundedness of
\pi
, the second term on the right hand side of (
128
) remains bounded as
n\to\infty
. Therefore the first term
f(x,s;\pi(r_{n}),r_{n})
also remains bounded, and so by condition (ii) in the definition of proper landscape we must have
\pi(r_{n})\to\pi(r)
since otherwise

would get arbitrarily large and negative as

. The case when

follows a symmetric argument.

Now suppose

and let

be a limit point of
\pi(r_{n})
. By passing to a subsequence, we can assume that
\pi_{n}(r_{n})\to w
, and that either

for all

or
r_{n}>r
for all
n
. These two cases have symmetric arguments, so we will assume
r_{n}<r
for all
n
. By continuity of

, along this subsequence the right hand side of (
128

f(x,s;w,r)+f(w,r;y,t).

In particular, (
128

implies that
w
maximizes the function

z\mapsto f(x,s;z,r)+f(z,r;y,t).

Since
\pi(r)
is the rightmost maximizer of this function,

. We now prove the opposite inequality.

Metric composition implies that there exists a point
z_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

f(x,s;y,t)=f(x,s;z_{n},r_{n})+f(z_{n},r_{n};\pi(r),r)+f(\pi(r),r;y,t).

(129)

Equation (
129

f(x,s;y,t)=f(x,s;z_{n},r_{n})+f(z_{n},r_{n};y,t).

Therefore both

\pi(r_{n})
maximize the function in the right hand side (

is the rightmost maximizer. The sequence

Moreover,

as

. Since

and

as desired.

To check that
\pi
is a geodesic, just as in (

By the metric composition law, we can find a point

f(x,s;\pi(r_{2}),r_{2})=f(x,s;z_{1},r_{1})+f(z_{1},r_{1};\pi(r_{2}),r_{2}).

By the construction of

f(x,s;y,t)=f(x,s;z_{1},r_{1})+f(z_{1},r_{1};\pi(r_{2}),r_{2})+f(\pi(r_{2}),r_{2};y,t).

(132)

By the triangle inequality for

(z_{1},r_{1}),(\pi(r_{2}),r_{2})
, and
(y,t)
, (

f(x,s;y,t)\leq f(x,s;z_{1},r_{1})+f(z_{1},r_{1};y,t).

In particular,

is the rightmost maximizer of this function.
By a similar argument, we can find a point

such that

f(x,s;y,t)=f(x,s;\pi(r_{1}),r_{1})+f(\pi(r_{1}),r_{1};z_{2},r_{2})+f(z_{2},r_{2};y,t).

(133)

Now, we can sum the equalities (

)
and apply property (iv) of proper landscapes to the points

and

\displaystyle 2f(x,s;y,t)\leq

\displaystyle f(x,s;z_{1},r_{1})+f(z_{1},r_{1};z_{2},r_{2})+f(z_{2},r_{2};y,t)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle f(x,s;\pi(r_{1}),r_{1})+f(\pi(r_{1}),r_{1};\pi(r_{2}),r_{2})+f(\pi(r_{2}),r_{2};y,t)

We will also need the following lemma about landscape convergence.

Lemma 13.3
.

Let

. Using condition (ii) in the definition of proper landscape for

Proof of Theorem
13.1
.

Let
(u,v)=(x,t;y,s)

.

. Since each

(136)

v
. Since
w\notin\mathfrak{g}\pi
, this contradicts the uniqueness of
\pi
.

Therefore either
r_{n}\to t
or
r_{n}\to s
. We will only deal with the case
r_{n}\to t
, as the case where
r_{n}\to s
is similar. In this case, all the points
(u_{n},v_{n}),(w_{n},v_{n})
are contained in a common compact set in
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, so

f_{n}(u_{n},v_{n})-f_{n}(w_{n},v_{n})\to f(u,v)-f(w,v).

(137)

On the other hand, all the points
(u_{n},w_{n})
lie in a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, and they converge to a point
(u,w)=(x,t,x^{\prime},t)
with
x\neq x^{\prime}
. Therefore by Lemma
13.3
, we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(u_{n},w_{n})=-\infty.

Combining this with (
137
) contradicts (
136
) for large enough
n
.
∎

Next, we apply Theorem
13.1
to prove convergence of last passage paths in the prelimiting last passage percolations
\mathcal{L}_{n}
, Theorem
1.8
. This is not immediate from Theorem
13.1
since the rescaled last passage paths in Theorem
1.8
are not geodesics in
\mathcal{L}_{n}
. However, we can show that these rescaled paths will always be close to geodesics by tweaking the definitions.

Recall that
\mathcal{L}_{n}
is the rescaled version of

B[(x,t)_{n}\rightarrow(y,s)_{n}]=B[(t+2xn^{-1/3},-{\left\lfloor tn\right\rfloor})\rightarrow(s+2yn^{-1/3},-{\left\lfloor sn\right\rfloor})].

Fix
(x,s;y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
and let

\pi_{n}=\pi_{n}[(x,t)_{n},(y,s)_{n}]

be any last passage path in
B
from
(x,t)_{n}
to
(y,s)_{n}
. Let

h_{n}
be the increasing affine function mapping
[t,s]
onto
[t+2xn^{-1/3},s+2yn^{-1/3}]
, and define the rescaled last passage path

\tau_{n}(r)=\frac{\pi_{n}\circ h_{n}(r)+nh_{n}(r)}{2n^{2/3}}

for
r\in[t,s]
. We would like to use Theorem
13.1
to show that
\tau_{n}
converges to a directed geodesic in
\mathcal{L}
, but as written the path
\tau_{n}
is not a geodesic in
\mathcal{L}_{n}
. In particular, it is not continuous. We work around this by constructing geodesics
\sigma_{n}
which are close to
\tau_{n}
.
First define a function
\tilde{\pi}_{n}:[t+2xn^{-1/3},s+2yn^{-1/3}]\to\mathbb{R}
whose inverse will be continuous and will help us define
\sigma_{n}
. The function
\tilde{\pi}_{n}
has the following specifications.

•

Let
z_{1}<\dots<z_{k}
be the set of points in
[t+2xn^{-1/3},s+2yn^{-1/3}]
when

\lim_{z\to z_{i}^{+}}\pi_{n}(z)\neq\lim_{z\to z_{i}^{-}}\pi_{n}(z).

Here recall the convention that

\lim_{z\to(t+2xn^{-1/3})^{-}}\pi_{n}(z)=-{\left\lfloor tn\right\rfloor}\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad\lim_{z\to(s+2yn^{-1/3})^{+}}\pi_{n}(z)=-{\left\lfloor sn\right\rfloor}.

Set
z_{0}=t+2xn^{-1/3}
and
z_{k+1}=s+2yn^{-1/3}
.
On each of the intervals
[z_{i},z_{i+1})
, let
\tilde{\pi}_{n}
be equal to the linear function satisfying
\tilde{\pi}_{n}(z_{i})=\pi_{n}(z_{i})
and
\tilde{\pi}_{n}(z_{i+1})=\pi_{n}(z_{i})-1/2
. Informally, we are giving the flat parts of
\pi_{n}
some slope so that there is a continuous inverse. The constant
1/2
yields better behavior at the right endpoint than the more natural
1
.

•

Let
\tilde{\pi}_{n}(s+2yn^{-1/3})=-{\left\lfloor sn\right\rfloor}-1/2
.

The function
\tilde{\pi}_{n}
is a strictly decreasing function from
[t+2xn^{-1/3},s+2yn^{-1/3}]
to
[-{\left\lfloor sn\right\rfloor}-1/2,-{\left\lfloor tn\right\rfloor}]
. There is a unique surjective nonincreasing function

\tilde{\pi}_{n}^{-1}:[-{\left\lfloor sn\right\rfloor}-1/2,-{\left\lfloor tn\right\rfloor}]\to[t+2xn^{-1/3},s+2yn^{-1/3}]

such that

\tilde{\pi}_{n}^{-1}\circ\tilde{\pi}_{n}

is the identity map on
[t+2xn^{-1/3},s+2yn^{-1/3}]
. The construction of
\tilde{\pi}_{n}^{-1}
guarantees that any point
(\tilde{\pi}_{n}^{-1}(r),{\left\lceil r\right\rceil})
lies along the path
\pi
, see Section
2.1
for the definition of a point lying along a path. Therefore the geodesic property of
\pi
guarantees that for any partition
r_{0}<\dots<r_{k}
of
[-{\left\lfloor sn\right\rfloor}-1/2,-{\left\lfloor tn\right\rfloor}]
we have

B[(x,t)_{n}\to(y,s)_{n}]=\sum_{i=1}^{k}B[(\tilde{\pi}_{n}^{-1}(r_{i}),{\left\lceil r_{i}\right\rceil})\to(\tilde{\pi}_{n}^{-1}(r_{i-1}),{\left\lceil r_{i-1}\right\rceil})].

(138)

Now define the function
\sigma_{n}:[{\left\lfloor tn\right\rfloor}/n,({\left\lfloor sn\right\rfloor}+1/2)/n]\to\mathbb{R}
by

\sigma_{n}(r)=\frac{n(\tilde{\pi}_{n}^{-1}(-rn)-r)}{2n^{2/3}}.

By (
138
), the function
\sigma_{n}
is a geodesic in
\mathcal{L}_{n}
from
(x+O(n^{-2/3}),t+O(n^{-1}))
to
(y+O(n^{-2/3}),s+O(n^{-1}))
.
Therefore by Theorem
13.1
, in a coupling where
\mathcal{L}_{n}\to\mathcal{L}
almost surely and
\mathcal{L}
has a unique directed geodesic
\Pi
from
(x,t)
to
(y,s)
, the functions
\sigma_{n}
will converge to
\Pi
in
\mathcal{G}
. That is, the graphs of
\sigma_{n}
will converge to the graph of
\Pi
in the Hausdorff topology. We want to show that in this case, the functions
\tau_{n}
converge to
\Pi
uniformly. This is accomplished by the following lemma.

Lemma 13.4
.

With all functions defined as above, suppose that
\sigma_{n}
converges almost surely in
\mathcal{G}
to a function
\sigma:[t,s]\to\mathbb{R}
. Then
\tau_{n}\to\sigma
uniformly almost surely.

Proof.

Fix a point
\omega
in the probability space such that
\sigma_{n}(\omega)\to\sigma(\omega)
in
\mathcal{G}
. We show that
\tau_{n}(\omega)\to\sigma(\omega)
uniformly.
We will work with the set

\Gamma_{n}=\{(\tau_{n}(r),h_{n}(r)-2\tau_{n}(r)n^{-1/3}):r\in[t,s]\}.

Let
\alpha_{n}
be the linear map from
\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
given by
\alpha_{n}(x,t)=(t+2xn^{-1/3},-tn)
. We have

\alpha_{n}\Gamma_{n}=\{(h_{n}(r),\pi_{n}\circ h_{n}(r)):r\in[t,s]\}.

The set
\alpha_{n}\Gamma_{n}
is the graph of
\pi_{n}
. Recall that

\mathfrak{g}\sigma_{n}=\{(\sigma_{n}(r),r):r\in[t,s]\}.

Letting
\beta(x,s)=(x,{\left\lceil s\right\rceil})
, we also have that

\beta\alpha_{n}\mathfrak{g}\sigma_{n}=\{(\tilde{\pi}_{n}^{-1}(r),{\left\lceil r\right\rceil}):r\in[-{\left\lfloor sn\right\rfloor}-1/2,-{\left\lfloor tn\right\rfloor}]\}.

The set
\beta\alpha_{n}\mathfrak{g}\sigma_{n}
consists of all points that lie along
\pi_{n}
.
In particular,
\alpha_{n}\Gamma_{n}\subset\beta\alpha_{n}\mathfrak{g}\sigma_{n}
, and so

\Gamma_{n}\subset\alpha_{n}^{-1}\beta\alpha_{n}\mathfrak{g}\sigma_{n}.

(139)

A straightforward computation yields that

||\alpha_{n}^{-1}\beta\alpha_{n}(u)-u||\leq 2n^{-1/3}

(140)

for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Thus all points in
\Gamma_{n}
are in the
2n^{-1/3}
-neighborhood of
\mathfrak{g}\sigma_{n}
. The Hausdorff convergence of
\mathfrak{g}\sigma_{n}
to
\mathfrak{g}\sigma
then implies that the sequence of closures
\bar{\Gamma}_{n}
has subsequential limits and they are all subsets of
\mathfrak{g}\sigma
.

Since
\tau_{n}(r)
is a coordinate of
\Gamma_{n}
for all
r,n
this implies that there exists a constant
C(\omega)
such that
|\tau_{n}(r)|\leq C
for all
n\in\mathbb{N},r\in[t,s]
. Also, since
h_{n}
is the affine map from
[t+2xn^{-1/3},s+2yn^{-1/3}]\to[t,s]
, we have that
h_{n}(r)=r+O(n^{-1/3})
. Therefore

(\tau_{n}(r),h_{n}(r)-\tau_{n}(r)n^{-1/3})=(\tau_{n}(r),r+O(n^{-1/3})).

(141)

Equation (
141
) implies that any subsequential limit of
\bar{\Gamma}_{n}
must intersect each of the lines
\mathbb{R}\times\{r\}
for every
r\in[t,s]
. Since
\mathfrak{g}\sigma
intersects each of these lines exactly once and all subsequential limits of
\bar{\Gamma}_{n}
are subsets of
\mathfrak{g}\sigma
, we get that
\bar{\Gamma}_{n}\to\mathfrak{g}\sigma
.

Equation (
141
) also implies that the Hausdorff distance between
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma_{n}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathfrak{g}\tau_{n}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
is
O(n^{-1/3})
, and so
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathfrak{g}\tau_{n}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\to\mathfrak{g}\sigma
as well. By the equivalence of Hausdorff convergence of graphs and uniform convergence to continuous functions, this completes the proof.
∎

Theorem
13.1
combined with Lemma
13.4
immediately allows us to conclude joint convergence of last passage paths to directed geodesics. This next theorem is a restatement of Theorem
1.8
.

Theorem 13.5
.

Let the Brownian last passage percolations
\mathcal{L}_{n}
and
\mathcal{L}
be coupled so that
\mathcal{L}_{n}\to\mathcal{L}
uniformly on compact sets almost surely. Then there exists an event
A
of probability
1
with the following property. For
u=(x,t;y,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, let
C_{u}
be the set where the directed geodesic
\Pi_{u}
is unique in
\mathcal{L}
, and let
h_{u,n}
be the increasing affine function mapping
[t,s]
onto
[t+2xn^{-1/3},s+2yn^{-1/3}]
.

For any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, and any sequence of last passage paths
\pi_{u,n}
from
(x,t)_{n}
to
(y,s)_{n}
in
\mathcal{L}_{n}
, we have that

\frac{\pi_{u,n}\circ h_{u,n}+nh_{u,n}}{2n^{2/3}}\to\Pi_{u}\quad\text{uniformly on the almost sure event }A\cap C_{u}.

In Theorem
13.5
,
A
is the event where
\mathcal{L}
is a proper landscape. This guarantees that we can apply Theorem
13.1
on
A\cap C_{u}
. Note that the set
C_{u}
is not obviously a Borel measurable event in itself. However, when
\mathcal{L}
is a proper landscape, uniqueness of the directed geodesic from
(x,t)
to
(y,s)
is equivalent to the statement that each of the continuous functions

f_{r}(z)=\mathcal{L}(x,t;z,r)+\mathcal{L}(z,r;y,s)

have a unique maximum, for
r\in(t,s)\cap\mathbb{Q}
. Therefore
C_{u}\cap A
is Borel measurable since on the event
A
, uniqueness of geodesics can be checked with only countably many conditions.

14
Open questions

There are several natural open questions related to the directed landscape. We collect a few of these here. Some of these are geometric in nature, while others are about desired explicit formulas.

Definition
8.1
constructs the Airy sheet on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
as a deterministic function of the Airy line ensemble
\mathcal{A}
. We believe that the whole Airy sheet should be a deterministic function of
\mathcal{A}
in the following way. Define
\mathcal{H}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
so that
\mathcal{H}|_{(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}}
is defined by (
66
) and
\mathcal{H}(0,y)=\mathcal{A}_{1}(y)
. Now define
\mathcal{H}(-x,y)
for
x>0
by applying the same formulas to the reflected version of
\mathcal{A}
:
\mathcal{A}_{\cdot}(-\,\cdot)
. This defines
\mathcal{H}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as a deterministic functional of
\mathcal{A}
.

Conjecture 14.1
.

The function
\mathcal{H}
is an Airy sheet.

Since the first version of this paper appeared, we proved Conjecture
14.1
, see Theorem 1.21 in
Dauvergne and Virág 2021b
.

Another way to clarify the Airy sheet definition would be to give the sheet values directly as a limit. The following would suffice.

Conjecture 14.2
.

There exists a deterministic function
a:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
so that for every
x>0
, almost surely as
k\to\infty
we have

\mathcal{A}[(-\sqrt{k/(2x)},k)\rightarrow(0,1)]-a(x,k)\to\mathcal{S}(x,0).

Conjecture
14.2
would follow from an improved bound on the last passage problem across the Airy line ensemble that was analyzed in Section
6
. In that section, we show that

\mathcal{A}[(0,k)\to(x,1)]=2\sqrt{2kx}+o(\sqrt{k}).

We believe that the correct error term here is
O(k^{-1/6})
, as in Brownian last passage percolation. This would give that
a(x,k)=\mathbb{E}\mathcal{A}_{1}(0)-\mathbb{E}\mathcal{A}_{k}(0)-\sqrt{2kx}.
The first term is the expectation of a GUE Tracy-Widom random variable and the second term is equal to
-(3\pi k/2)^{2/3}+o(1)
as
k\to\infty
.

Consider the directed geodesics
\Pi_{x,y}
from time
(x,0)
to time
(y,1)
. By invariance, the following intersection question has only two parameters.

Problem 14.3
.

Find a formula for the probability that
\Pi_{0,0}
and
\Pi_{x,y}
intersect.

Even the most basic distributions related to the directed geodesic
\Pi=\Pi_{0,0}
are not known. The first question depends only on two Airy processes, so it should be doable using continuum statistics.

Problem 14.4
.

(a) Find the distribution of
\Pi(s)
for all
s
.

(b) Find the distribution of
\max_{s}\Pi(s)
.

Proposition
12.3
gives tail bounds on these quantities of the form
e^{-x^{3}}
.

We also believe that the beginning of geodesics are special.

Conjecture 14.5
.

Consider the process
\eta_{t}:[0,1/2]\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\eta_{t}(s)=\Pi(t+s)-\Pi(t).

Let
0\leq t<u<1/2
. The law of
\eta_{t}
and
\eta_{u}
are mutually absolutely continuous if and only if
t>0
.

Finally, there should be a stochastic calculus for the 1-2-3 scaling. A natural precise question for the development of this theory is the following.

Question 14.6
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function with compact support. Define the
h
-shift
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
of the directed landscape
\mathcal{L}
by the length formula:

\int d\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\circ\pi=\int d\mathcal{L}\circ\pi+\int h(\pi(t),t)\,dt

Is the distribution of
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
absolutely continuous with respect to the directed landscape? If so, what is the density?
