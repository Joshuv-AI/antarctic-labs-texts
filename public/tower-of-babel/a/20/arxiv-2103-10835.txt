Let

Preliminaries

2.1.
Topological transformation groups

A
topological dynamical system
(t.d.s. for short) is a triple

\mathcal{X}=(X,\Gamma,\Pi)
, where
X
is a compact metric space with metric
\rho
,
\Gamma
is a
Hausdorff topological group with the unit
e_{\Gamma}
and
\Pi:\Gamma\times X\rightarrow X
is a
continuous map such that
\Pi(e_{\Gamma},x)=x
and

\Pi(s,\Pi(t,x))=\Pi(st,x)
. We shall fix
\Gamma
and suppress the
action symbol. Then for any
t\in\Gamma
,
t:X\rightarrow X
is a homeomorphism, and
e_{\Gamma}={\rm Id}_{X}
is the identity map.

Let
(X,\Gamma)
be a t.d.s. and
x\in X
, then
\mathcal{O}(x,\Gamma)=\{tx:t\in\Gamma\}
denotes the

orbit
of
x
. A subset

A\subseteq X
is called
invariant
if
ta\subseteq A
for all

a\in A
and
t\in\Gamma
. When
Y\subseteq X
is a closed and

\Gamma
-invariant subset of the system
(X,\Gamma)
, we say that the system

(Y,\Gamma)
is a
subsystem
of
(X,\Gamma)
. If
(X,\Gamma)
and
(Y,\Gamma)
are two dynamical systems, their
product system
is the
system
(X\times Y,\Gamma)
, where
t(x,y)=(tx,ty)
.

A system
(X,\Gamma)
is called
minimal
if
X
contains no proper
non-empty closed invariant subsets.
(X,\Gamma)
is called
transitive
if
for all non-empty open sets
U,V
, there is some
t\in\Gamma
such that
U\cap t^{-1}V\neq\emptyset
.
It is easy to verify that a system is
minimal iff every orbit is dense. A point
x\in X
is called a
minimal
point if
(\overline{\mathcal{O}(x,\Gamma)},\Gamma)
is a minimal subsystem.
A system
(X,\Gamma)
is
weakly mixing
if the product system
(X\times X,\Gamma)
is transitive.

When
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}
,
(X,\Gamma)
is determined by a homeomorphism
T
, i.e.
T
is the transformation corresponding to
1
of
\mathbb{Z}
. In this case, we usually denote
(X,\mathbb{Z})
by
(X,T)
.

2.2.
Furstenberg families

2.2.1.
Furstenberg families

A collection
\mathcal{G}
of subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
is
a (Furstenberg) family
if it is hereditary upward, i.e.
B_{1}\subset B_{2}
and

B_{1}\in\mathcal{G}
imply
B_{2}\in\mathcal{G}
. A family
\mathcal{G}
is called
proper
if it is neither empty nor the entire power set of
\mathbb{Z}
, or,
equivalently if
\mathbb{Z}\in\mathcal{G}
and
\emptyset\not\in\mathcal{G}
. Any nonempty
collection
\mathcal{A}
of subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
generates a family

\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{A}):=\{B\subset\mathbb{Z}:B\supset A
for some
A\in\mathcal{A}\}
.

For a family
\mathcal{G}
, its
dual
is the family
\mathcal{G}^{\ast}:=\{B\subset\mathbb{Z}:B\cap A\neq\emptyset\ \text{for all}\ A\in\mathcal{G}\}
. It is not hard to see that
\mathcal{G}^{*}=\{B\subset\mathbb{Z}:\mathbb{Z}\setminus B\not\in\mathcal{G}\}
, from which we have that if
\mathcal{G}
is a
family, then
(\mathcal{G}^{*})^{*}=\mathcal{G}.

2.2.2.
Filter and Ramsey property

If a family
\mathcal{G}
is closed under finite intersections and is proper,
then it is called a
filter
. A family
\mathcal{G}
has the

if
A=A_{1}\cup A_{2}\in\mathcal{G}

implies that
A_{1}\in\mathcal{G}
or
A_{2}\in\mathcal{G}
. It is well known that

Lemma 2.1
.

[
8
, Lemma 9.4, Lemma 9.5]

A proper family has the Ramsey property if and only if its dual
\mathcal{G}^{*}
is a filter. Let

be a family having Ramsey property, then for all

and
B\in\mathcal{G}^{*}
, we have

.

{{\bf P}\Gamma}_{0}^{*}={{\bf P}\Gamma}_{0}\setminus\{e_{\Gamma}\}

5.3.
The weight of
\Gamma
-polynomials

Let
\mathbb{Z}_{+}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
.
The
weight
,
w(g)
, of a
\Gamma
-polynomial
g(n)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}T_{j}^{p_{j}(n)}
is the
pair
(l,k)
,
l\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
for which
p_{j}=0
for any
j>l
and, if

l\neq 0
, then
p_{l}\neq 0
and
{\rm deg}(p_{l})=k
. A weight
(l,k)
is greater than a weight

(l^{\prime},k^{\prime})
, denoted by
(l,k)>(l^{\prime},k^{\prime})
, if
l>l^{\prime}
or
l=l^{\prime}
,
k>k^{\prime}
.

For example,
T_{1}^{n},T_{2}^{n},T_{1}^{n}T_{2}^{n^{3}}
have weights
(1,1),(2,1),(2,3)
respectively, and
(2,3)>(2,1)>(1,1)
.

Let us now define an equivalence relation on
{{\bf P}\Gamma}
:
g(n)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}T_{j}^{p_{j}(n)}
is equivalent to
h(n)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}T_{j}^{q_{j}(n)}
, if
w(g)=w(h)
and, if it is
(l,k)
,
the leading coefficients of the polynomials
p_{l}
and
q_{l}
coincide; we write then

g\sim h
.
For example,

T_{3}^{n^{2}}\sim T_{1}^{n^{6}}T_{3}^{n^{2}+3n}\sim T_{1}^{n}T_{2}^{n^{3}+3n}T_{3}^{n^{2}+5n}.

The
weight
of an equivalence class is the weight of any of its elements.

5.4.
System and its weight vector

A
system

\mathcal{S}
is a finite subset of
{{\bf P}\Gamma}
. For a system
\mathcal{S}
, if we write
\mathcal{S}=\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{v}

then we require that
f_{i}\neq f_{j}
for
1\leq i\neq j\leq v
. For every system
\mathcal{S}
we define its
weight vector

\phi(A)
as follows. Let
w_{1}<w_{2}<\ldots<w_{q}
be the set of the distinct weights of all equivalence classes
appeared in
\mathcal{S}
. For
i=1,2,\ldots,q
, let
\phi(w_{i})
be the number of the equivalence classes of elements of
\mathcal{S}
with the weight
w_{i}
.
Let the weight vector
\phi(\mathcal{S})
be

\phi(\mathcal{S})=(\phi(w_{1})w_{1},\phi(w_{2})w_{2},\ldots,\phi(w_{q})w_{q}).

For example, let
\mathcal{S}=\{T_{1}^{n}
,
T_{1}^{2n}
,
T_{1}^{3n}
,

T_{1}^{n^{2}}
,
T_{1}^{n^{2}+n}
,
T_{2}^{3n^{2}+2n}
,
T_{1}^{n^{6}}T_{2}^{3n^{2}+n}
,
T_{1}^{n^{4}+n^{3}+n}T_{2}^{3n^{2}+2n}
,
T_{1}^{n}T_{3}^{n^{3}}
,
T_{2}^{n^{5}}T_{3}^{2n^{3}+n^{2}}
,
T_{1}^{n^{2}}T_{2}^{n}T_{3}^{3n^{3}+2n^{2}}\}
. Then

\phi(\mathcal{S})=\big(3(1,1),1(1,2),1(2,2),3(3,3)\big)
.

Let
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
be two systems. We say that
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

precedes
a system
\mathcal{S}
if there exists a weight
w
such
that
\phi(\mathcal{S})(w)>\phi(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})(w)
and
\phi(\mathcal{S})(u)=\phi(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})(u)
for all weight
u>w
. We denote it by

\phi(\mathcal{S})\succ\phi(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
or
\phi(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})\prec\phi(\mathcal{S})
.

For example, let
w_{1}<w_{2}<\ldots<w_{q}
be a sequence of weights, then

(a_{1}w_{1},\ldots,a_{q}w_{q})\succ(b_{1}w_{1},\ldots,b_{q}w_{q})

if and only if
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q})>(b_{1},\ldots,b_{q})
.

5.5.
The PET-induction

5.5.1.

In order to prove that a result holds for all systems
\mathcal{S}
, we start with the system whose weight
vector is
\{1(1,1)\}
. That is,
\mathcal{S}=\{T_{1}^{a_{1}n}\}
,
where
a_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Then let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq{{\bf P}\Gamma}_{0}
be a system whose weight vector is
greater than
\{1(1,1)\}
, and assume that for all systems
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
preceding
\mathcal{S}
, we have that the
result holds for
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. Once we show that the result still holds for
\mathcal{S}
, we complete the whole proof.
This procedure is called the
PET-induction
.

5.5.2.

For example, we outline how to use the language of PET-induction to formulate the proof of Theorem
3.3
.
Now
\Gamma=\langle T\rangle=\{T^{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, and
{{\bf P}\Gamma}=\{T^{p(n)}:p\in\mathcal{P}\}
. For each
T^{p(n)}\in{{\bf P}\Gamma}
,
its weight
w(T^{p(n)})=(1,k)
, where
k
is the degree of
p(n)
. A system
\mathcal{S}
has the form of
\{T^{p_{1}(n)},T^{p_{2}(n)},\ldots,T^{p_{d}(n)}\}
, where
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\in\mathcal{P}
are distinct polynomials. Its weight vector
\phi(\mathcal{S})
has the form of

\big(a_{1}(1,1),a_{2}(1,2),\ldots,a_{k}(1,k)\big).

Under the order of weight vectors, one has

\begin{split}&\big(1(1,1)\big)\prec\big(2(1,1)\big)\prec\ldots\prec\big(m(1,1)\big)\prec\ldots\prec\big(1(1,2)\big)\prec\big(1(1,1),1(1,2)\big)\prec\ldots\prec\\
&\big(m(1,1),1(1,2)\big)\prec\ldots\prec\big(2(1,2)\big)\prec\big(1(1,1),2(1,2)\big)\prec\ldots\prec\big(m(1,1),2(1,2)\big)\prec\ldots\prec\\
&\big(m(1,1),k(1,2)\big)\prec\ldots\prec\big(1(1,3)\big)\prec\big(1(1,1),1(1,3)\big)\prec\ldots\prec\big(m(1,1),k(1,2),1(1,3)\big)\\
&\prec\ldots\prec\big(2(1,3)\big)\prec\ldots\prec\big(a_{1}(1,1),a_{2}(1,2),\ldots,a_{k}(1,k)\big)\prec\ldots.\end{split}

To prove Theorem
3.3
, we will use induction on the weight vectors.
We start from the systems with the weight vector
(1(1,1))
, i.e.
\mathcal{S}=\{T^{a_{1}n}\},a_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.
After that, we assume that the result holds for all systems whose weight vectors are

\prec

\big(a_{1}(1,1)
,
a_{2}(1,2)
,
\ldots
,
a_{k}(1,k)

\big)
. Then we show
that the result also holds for the system with weight vector
\big(

a_{1}(1,1),

a_{2}(1,2),\ldots,a_{k}(1,k)

\big)
, and hence the proof is completed.

To illustrate the basic ideas, we show the result for the system
\{T^{an^{2}+bn}\}
, and the system
\mathcal{S}=\{T^{n^{2}},T^{2n^{2}}\}
, whose weight vectors are
\big(1(1,2)\big)
and
\big(2(1,2)\big)
respectively.
