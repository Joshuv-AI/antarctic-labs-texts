Nonexistence of minimizers for the second conformal eigenvalue near the round sphere in low dimensions Thanks: The second author was supported by the NSERC Discovery Grant RGPIN-2022-04213.
Bruno Premoselli, Jérôme Vétois

Abstract
. We consider the problem of minimizing the second conformal eigenvalue of the conformal Laplacian in a conformal class of metrics with renormalized volume. We prove, in dimensions n ∈ { 3 , … , 10 } n\in\left\{3,\dotsc,10\right\} , that a minimizer for this problem does not exist for metrics sufficiently close to the round metric on the sphere. This is in striking contrast with the situation in dimensions n ≥ 11 n\geq 11 , where Ammann and Humbert [ AH ] obtained the existence of minimizers for the second conformal eigenvalue on any smooth closed non-locally conformally flat manifold. As a byproduct of our techniques, we also obtain a lower bound on the energy of sign-changing solutions of the Yamabe equation in dimensions 3, 4 and 5, which extends a result obtained by Weth [ W ] in the case of the round sphere.



1.
Introduction and main result

We let
\left(M,g\right)
be a smooth closed Riemannian manifold of dimension
n\geq 3
. We let
\left[g\right]
be the conformal class of the metric
g
. For each metric
\hat{g}\in\left[g\right]
, we denote by
L_{\hat{g}}
the conformal Laplacian of
\left(M,\hat{g}\right)
, i.e.

L_{\hat{g}}:=\Delta_{\hat{g}}+c_{n}\Scal_{\hat{g}},

where
\Delta_{\hat{g}}:=-\divergence_{\hat{g}}\left(\nabla\cdot\right)
is the Laplace–Beltrami operator of
\left(M,\hat{g}\right)
,
\Scal_{\hat{g}}
is the scalar curvature of
\left(M,\hat{g}\right)
and
c_{n}:=\frac{n-2}{4\left(n-1\right)}
. Since
M
is closed, for each
\hat{g}\in\left[g\right]
the eigenvalues of
L_{\hat{g}}
form a nondecreasing sequence
\left(\lambda_{k}\left(L_{\hat{g}}\right)\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\lambda_{1}\left(L_{\hat{g}}\right)<\lambda_{2}\left(L_{\hat{g}}\right)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{k}\left(L_{\hat{g}}\right)\leq\cdots\to\infty.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the
k
-th conformal eigenvalue of
\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)
is defined as

\Lambda_{k}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right):=\inf_{\hat{g}\in\left[g\right]}\left(\lambda_{k}\left(L_{\hat{g}}\right)\Vol\left(M,\hat{g}\right)^{\frac{2}{n}}\right),

where
\lambda_{k}\left(L_{\hat{g}}\right)
is the
k
-th eigenvalue of
L_{\hat{g}}
and
\Vol\left(M,\hat{g}\right)
is the volume of
\left(M,\hat{g}\right)
. This invariant was first introduced and studied by Ammann and Humbert
[
AH
]
and further studied by El Sayed
[
ElS
]
. It is not difficult to see that if
\Lambda_{1}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)\geq 0
, then
\Lambda_{1}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)
coincides with the classical Yamabe invariant, i.e.

\Lambda_{1}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)=\inf_{\hat{g}\in\left[g\right]}\left(\Vol\left(M,\hat{g}\right)^{\frac{2-n}{n}}\int_{M}\Scal_{\hat{g}}\dv_{\hat{g}}\right),

where
\dv_{\hat{g}}
is the volume element of
\left(M,\hat{g}\right)
. In this paper, we consider the case of metrics with positive Yamabe invariant. In this case, it follows from the work of Trudinger
[
Tr
]
, Aubin
[
A
]
and Schoen
[
Sc1
]
that
\Lambda_{1}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)
is always attained by some smooth positive function, and, moreover,

\Lambda_{1}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)\leq\Lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{0}\right]\right)

with equality if and only if
\left(M,g\right)
is conformally equivalent to the round
n
-sphere
\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},g_{0}\right)
.

In this paper, we focus on the case where
k=2
and
\Lambda_{1}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)>0
. In this case, Ammann and Humbert
[
AH
]
obtained

2\Lambda_{1}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}\leq\Lambda_{2}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}\leq\Lambda_{1}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}+\Lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{0}\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}.

(1.1)

They also obtained that
\Lambda_{2}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)
is attained provided the second inequality in (
1.1
) is strict, i.e.

\Lambda_{2}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}<\Lambda_{1}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}+\Lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{0}\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}

(1.2)

and that if (
1.2
) is satisfied, then
\Lambda_{2}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)
is attained at a “generalized” metric
\hat{g}:=\left|u\right|^{2^{*}-2}g
for some
u\in C^{3,\vartheta}\left(M\right)
for some
\vartheta<2^{*}-2
(see Section
2
for more details). Ammann and Humbert also obtained in
[
AH
]
that, by test-functions computations, if
n\geq 11
and
\left(M,g\right)
has non-vanishing Weyl tensor somewhere, then (
1.2
) is satisfied and that, for each
n\geq 3
,
\Lambda_{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{0}\right]\right)=2^{\frac{2}{n}}\Lambda_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{0}\right])
is never attained.

Except for the trivial case of the round sphere
\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},g_{0}\right)
, the existence of minimizers for
\Lambda_{2}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)
in dimensions 3 to 10 is an open problem. Our main result provides a partial negative answer to this question:

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
3\leq n\leq 10
. Then there exist
\delta\in\left(0,\infty\right)
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for every smooth metric
g
on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, if
\left\|g-g_{0}\right\|_{C^{m}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n}\right)}<\delta
, then

\Lambda_{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}=\Lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}+\Lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{0}\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}

and
\Lambda_{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g\right]\right)
is not attained by any generalized metric.

We recall that every smooth metric on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
which is not conformally equivalent to
g_{0}
is also not locally conformally flat. Theorem
1.1
establishes a striking dichotomy between the case where
3\leq n\leq 10
and the case where
n\geq 11
. First, when
3\leq n\leq 10
, Theorem
1.1
has to be understood as a perturbative nonexistence result of extremals for
\Lambda_{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g\right]\right)
for
g
close to the round metric
g_{0}
, the analogue of which fails when
n\geq 11
by the results of
[
AH
]
. Second, Theorem
1.1
establishes that, when
3\leq n\leq 10
, (
1.2
) cannot be guaranteed by solely enforcing local geometric assumptions on
g
. This again strongly contrasts with the results of
[
AH
]
in dimensions
n\geq 11
, where a minimizer for
\Lambda_{2}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)
is proven to exist for any
g
which is not locally conformally flat. Determining whether (
1.2
) holds true and whether there exist extremals for
\Lambda_{2}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)
when
3\leq n\leq 10
therefore requires new ideas. Theorem 1.1 can be understood as a step forward in this direction : when
\left(M,g\right)=\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},g\right)
it reveals that a necessary condition for (1.2) to hold is that
g
is sufficiently far from the round metric
g_{0}
in a strong sense. Not being conformally diffeomorphic to
g_{0}
, in particular, is not enough, which is very surprising in view of the definition of
\Lambda_{2}
. How Theorem 1.1 may adapt on a general manifold is still unclear, but it seems to hint that global infomation on
\left(M,g\right)
is needed to obtain (1.2).

Eigenvalue optimization problems in conformal classes have attracted a lot of attention in recent years. When
n\geq 3
and in the case of the conformal Laplacian
L_{g}
which we consider here, the invariants
\Lambda_{k}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)
that we define are the only meaningful ones when
\Lambda_{1}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)>0
. Indeed, it is for instance proven by Ammann and Jammes
[
AJ
]
that, if
\Lambda_{k}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)>0
, then

\sup_{\hat{g}\in\left[g\right]}\left(\lambda_{k}\left(L_{\hat{g}}\right)\Vol\left(M,\hat{g}\right)^{\frac{2}{n}}\right)=\infty.

On the other hand, in the case where
\Lambda_{k}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)<0
, it is natural to replace the infimum in the definition of
\Lambda_{k}
by a supremum since otherwise
\Lambda_{k}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)=-\infty
(see Proposition 8.1 in
[
AH
]
). This therefore leads to a maximization problem, which is very different in nature. We refer to the work of Gursky and Pérez-Ayala
[
GP
]
where this problem is studied in the case where
k=2
. Most of previous work on eigenvalue optimization problems in conformal classes of closed manifolds of dimension larger than or equal to
2
concern the Laplace–Beltrami operator
\Delta_{g}
, for which again only the maximization problem is interesting. In dimensions
n\geq 3
, the maximization of conformal eigenvalues of
\Delta_{g}
was recently investigated by Pétrides
[
Pe3
]
. In dimension
2
, this problem was investigated by many authors. In this case, we refer for instance to the work of Nadirashvili and Sire
[
NS
]
, Petrides
[
Pe1
,
Pe2
]
, Matthiesen and Siffert
[
MS
]
and Karpukhin and Stern
[
KS1
,
KS2
]
. To the best of our knowledge, another remarkable feature of Theorem
1.1
is that it is the first nonexistence result of extremals for conformal eigenvalues of any kind (for any dimension
n\geq 2
and any of the operators
\Delta_{g}
and
L_{g}
) for metrics that are not conformal to the round metric on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we discuss the connection between the second conformal eigenvalue and sign-changing solutions of the Yamabe equation of lowest energy. We also state a stronger result than Theorem
1.1
, in dimensions 3, 4 and 5, namely Theorem
2.1
. Section
3
is devoted to a sharp bubbling analysis of sign-changing solutions of the Yamabe equation whose energies converge to
\Lambda_{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{0}\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}
. We prove bubble-tree convergence results as well as sharp pointwise asymptotics. We then prove Theorems
1.1
and
2.1
in Section
4
.

2.
The second conformal eigenvalue and sign-changing solutions of the Yamabe equation

The second conformal eigenvalue of the conformal Laplacian has a strong connection with sign-changing solutions of the Yamabe equation

L_{g}u=\left|u\right|^{2^{*}-2}u\quad\text{in }M,

(2.1)

where
2^{*}:=\frac{2n}{n-2}
is the critical Sobolev exponent. Indeed, Ammann and Humbert
[
AH
]
proved that if
\Lambda_{1}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)\geq 0
and
\Lambda_{2}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)
is attained, then there exists a sign-changing function
u\in L^{2^{*}}\left(M\right)
such that the “generalized” metric
\hat{g}:=\left|u\right|^{2^{*}-2}g
satisfies

\lambda_{2}\left(L_{\hat{g}}\right)=\Lambda_{2}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\Vol\left(M,\hat{g}\right)=1,\text{ i.e.}\int_{M}\left|u\right|^{2^{*}}\dv_{g}=1

(see
[
AH
]
*Section 3.2 for the rigorous definition of
\lambda_{2}\left(L_{\hat{g}}\right)
when
u\in L^{2^{*}}\left(M\right)\backslash\left\{0\right\}
). It is also shown in
[
AH
]
that
u
is a second “generalized” eigenvector associated to
\lambda_{2}\left(L_{\hat{g}}\right)
, which implies that
u\in C^{3,\vartheta}\left(M\right)
for some
\vartheta<2^{*}-2
and, up to a renormalization factor,
u
can be made into a sign-changing solution of (
2.1
) with energy

\int_{M}\left|u\right|^{2^{*}}\dv_{g}=\Lambda_{2}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}.

Furthermore, in this case
u
is a sign-changing solution of (
2.1
) of least energy among all sign-changing solutions, and it has exactly two nodal domains. We again refer to
[
AH
]
*Section 3 for more details. As mentioned in the introduction, (
1.2
) is sufficient to ensure that
\Lambda_{2}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)
is attained. Equation (
2.1
) has to be understood as the Euler-Lagrange equation for minimizers of
\Lambda_{2}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)
. Its hidden meaning is that, for a generalized metric
\hat{g}:=\left|u\right|^{2^{*}-2}g
attaining
\Lambda_{2}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)
,
\lambda_{2}\left(L_{\hat{g}}\right)
is simple. This unusual feature for an eigenvalue optimization problem is a direct consequence of the definition of
\Lambda_{2}\left(M,\left[g\right]\right)
as an infimum (see for instance
[
GP
]
*Remark 6.1 for a detailed explanation).

A consequence of Theorem
1.1
is that, for every smooth metric
g
on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
sufficiently close to
g_{0}
in
C^{m}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n}\right)
for some sufficiently large
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there does not exist any sign-changing solution
u
of (
2.1
) such that

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}\left|u\right|^{2^{*}}\dv_{g}\leq\Lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}+\Lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{0}\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}.

Theorem
1.1
thus gives a lower bound on the energy of sign-changing solutions of the Yamabe equation (
2.1
) on the sphere of dimension lower than or equal to 10 when equipped with metrics sufficiently close to the round metric. In fact, in dimensions 3, 4 and 5, we obtain a stronger result:

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume that
n\in\left\{3,4,5\right\}
. There exist
\delta,\varepsilon\in\left(0,\infty\right)
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for every smooth metric
g
on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, if
\left\|g-g_{0}\right\|_{C^{m}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n}\right)}<\delta
, then the energy of every sign-changing solution of the Yamabe equation

L_{g}u=\left|u\right|^{2^{*}-2}u\quad\text{in }\mathbb{S}^{n}

is greater than
2\Lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{0}\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}+\varepsilon
.

Theorem
2.1
extends a result obtained by Weth
[
W
]
in the exact case of the round sphere. Notice, however, that the result of Weth
[
W
]
holds for all dimensions
n\geq 3
. This is specific to the exact case
g=g_{0}
. Indeed, as shown by the results of Ammann and Humbert
[
AH
]
, at least in the case where
n\geq 11
, Theorem
2.1
is false when
g
is not conformal to
g_{0}
.

We prove Theorems
1.1
and
2.1
in the next two sections. By using (
2.1
) together with a contradiction argument, which is explained in details at the beginning of Section
3
, the proof of Theorems
1.1
and
2.1
amounts to ruling out the existence of sequences
\left(u_{k}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of sign-changing solutions of (
2.1
) with
g=g_{k}
, where
g_{k}
converges to
g_{0}
in
C^{m}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n}\right)
as
k\to\infty
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that the energies of
\left(u_{k}\right)_{k}
converge to
2\Lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{0}\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}
. In dimensions 6 to 10, we assume in addition that, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Lambda_{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{k}\right]\right)
is attained by the generalized metric
\left|u_{k}\right|^{2^{*}-2}g_{k}
. The proof of Weth
[
W
]
in the case where
g_{k}=g_{0}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
relies on the symmetries of
\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},g_{0}\right)
and uses the action of the conformal group of the sphere on the sign-changing solutions of the Yamabe equation. In our setting, however, the metrics
\left(g_{k}\right)_{k}
do not have any symmetries in general, and we need to perform a much finer asymptotic analysis of
\left(u_{k}\right)_{k}
. As a first result, in Lemma
3.1
, we prove that
\left(u_{k}\right)_{k}
behaves like the difference between two positive solutions of the Yamabe equation on the sphere (see (
3.12
)). This amounts to say that
\Lambda_{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{k}\right]\right)
is asymptotically attained by the disjoint union of two round spheres. The rest of Section
3
is devoted to obtaining sharp pointwise estimates for the blow-up of
\left(u_{k}\right)_{k}
, which, in particular, captures the local geometry of
\left(g_{k}\right)_{k}
. This refined blow-up analysis is based on iterated estimates and makes crucial use of arguments previously developed in the context of positive solutions (see the work of Chen and Lin
[
CL
]
, Schoen
[
Sc2
,
Sc3
]
, Li and Zhu
[
LZhu
]
, Druet
[
Dr
]
, Marques
[
Mar
]
, Li and Zhang
[
LZha1
,
LZha2
]
and Khuri, Marques and Schoen
[
KMS
]
; see also the counterexamples in high dimensions of Brendle
[
B
]
and Brendle and Marques
[
BM1
]
and the survey article by Brendle and Marques
[
BM2
]
) and more recently in the context of sign-changing solutions (see Premoselli
[
Pr
]
and Premoselli and Vétois
[
PV1
,
PV2
,
PV3
]
). We finally prove Theorems
1.1
and
2.1
in Section
4
by using the analysis developed in Section
3
.

Although similar at first glance, the settings of Theorems
1.1
and
2.1
are quite different from that of the celebrated compactness result of Khuri, Marques and Schoen
[
KMS
]
. We point out two crucial differences: first, since the functions
\left(u_{k}\right)_{k}
change sign, the concentration points are neither isolated nor simple; second, since
g_{k}\to g_{0}
as
k\to\infty
in
C^{m}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n}\right)
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the Riemannian masses of the metrics
\left(g_{k}\right)_{k}
converge to
0
at any point of
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, so that no local sign restriction argument is available to rule out blow-up. Therefore, and unlike in
[
KMS
]
, our contradiction does not originate from a local sign restriction due to the Positive Mass Theorem. In dimensions 3, 4 and 5, instead, we obtain a contradiction by means of a Pohozaev-type identity in a region where we observe that a large virtual mass is created solely by the interaction between the two bubbles. In dimensions 6 to 10, the Pohozaev-type identity is not sufficient to conclude since additional lower-order terms appear which involve more of the geometry of the metrics
\left(g_{k}\right)_{k}
at the concentration points. In this case, we still manage to obtain a sharp asymptotic estimate on
\Lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{k}\right]\right)
(see (
4.2
)). We then obtain a contradiction with this estimate by doing another estimation of
\Lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{k}\right]\right)
based on a better family of test-functions, which construction again relies on the analysis of Section
3
. The contradiction when
6\leq n\leq 10
thus really comes from the minimality of
\Lambda_{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{k}\right]\right)
.

3.
Asymptotic analysis and symmetry estimates

The section and the next are devoted to the proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
2.1
. We begin with recalling some well-known facts about constant-sign solutions of the Yamabe equation in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. By splitting the solutions into positive and negative parts, it is easy to see that there does not exist any sign-changing solution of the Yamabe equation

L_{g_{0}}u=\left|u\right|^{2^{*}-2}u\quad\text{in }\mathbb{S}^{n}.

(3.1)

with energy smaller than or equal to
2\Lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{0}\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}
. Moreover, according to the classification result of Obata
[
O
]
, every nonzero nonnegative solution of (
3.1
) has energy equal to
\Lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{0}\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}
and is either constant or of the form

u=\left(\frac{2\sqrt{n\left(n-2\right)}\mu}{2\mu^{2}+\big(4-\mu^{2}\big)\big(1-\cos\left(\dist_{g_{0}}\left(\cdot,x\right)\right)\big)}\right)^{\frac{n-2}{2}}

for some
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
\mu\in\left(0,2\right)
. We also recall that, letting
\xi
be the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\Delta_{\xi}:=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{y_{i}}^{2}
, the stereographic projection gives a bijection between the solutions of (
3.1
) and the solutions
u
of the equation

\Delta_{\xi}u=\left|u\right|^{2^{*}-2}u\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n},

(3.2)

which belong to the energy space
D^{1,2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)
defined as the closure of
C^{\infty}_{c}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)
with respect to the norm
\left\|\nabla\cdot\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)}^{2}
. By using this bijection, we obtain that every nonzero nonnegative solution of (
3.2
) has energy equal to
\Lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{0}\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}
and is of the form

u=\left(\frac{\sqrt{n\left(n-2\right)}\widetilde{\mu}}{\widetilde{\mu}^{2}+\left|\cdot-y\right|^{2}}\right)^{\frac{n-2}{2}}

for some
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\widetilde{\mu}\in\left(0,\infty\right)
, and that there does not exist any sign-changing solution
u\in D^{1,2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)
of (
3.2
) with energy smaller than or equal to
2\Lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{0}\right]\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}
. It is well-known that the positive solutions of (
3.2
) are the extremals for the Sobolev inequality in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.

\Lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n},\left[g_{0}\right]\right)=\inf_{v\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\nabla v\right|^{2}\dy}{\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|v\right|^{2^{*}}\dy\right)^{\frac{n-2}{n}}},

where
\dy
is the volume element of

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
d\in\left\{2,\dotsc,n-4\right\}
, we define

H_{k,x,d}\left(y\right):=\sum_{\left|\alpha\right|=d}h_{k,x,\alpha}y^{\alpha}\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(3.36)

It is easy to see that
H_{k,x,d}
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
and

H_{k,x}=\sum_{d=2}^{n-4}H_{k,x,d}.

As a consequence of Lemma
3.3
, we obtain

\sum_{b=1}^{n}\left(H_{k,x,d}\left(y\right)\right)_{bb}=0\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{b=1}^{n}\left(H_{k,x,d}\left(y\right)\right)_{ab}y_{b}=0\quad\forall a\in\left\{1,\dotsc,n\right\},\,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(3.37)

For each
d\in\left\{0,\dots,n-4\right\}
, we define

w_{k,x,d}\left(y\right):=\sum_{\left|\alpha\right|=d}\sum_{a,b=1}^{n}\left(h_{k,x,\alpha}\right)_{ab}\partial_{y_{a}}\partial_{y_{b}}\left(y^{\alpha}\right)\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(3.38)

It is easy to see that
w_{k,x,d}
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d-2\in\left\{0,\dots,n-6\right\}
, which by (
3.35
) captures the main term in the Taylor expansion of
\Scal_{\hat{g}_{k,x}}\left(y\right)
at 0. We claim that, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
,

w_{k,x,d}\left(y\right)=0\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,d\in\left\{0,1,2,3\right\}.

(3.39)

This is obvious in the case where
d\in\left\{0,1\right\}
. When
d\in\left\{2,3\right\}
,
w_{k,x,d}
is a homogeneous polynomial of order
0
or
1
, respectively. By using (
3.35
) and remarking that

\Scal_{\hat{g}_{k,x}}\left(y\right)=\bigO\big(\left|y\right|^{2}\big)

uniformly with respect to
y\in\B_{g_{0}}\left(x,r_{0}\right)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, which follows from properties of the conformal normal coordinates (see
[
LP
]
), we obtain (
3.39
). As a consequence of (
3.39
), we obtain that if
3\leq n\leq 7
, then

w_{k,x,d}\left(y\right)=0\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,d\in\left\{0,\dotsc,n-4\right\},

(3.40)

hence (
3.38
) is trivial in this case. In dimensions
n\geq 8
, another result we need from Khuri, Marques and Schoen
[
KMS
]
(see also Li and Zhang
[
LZha1
,
LZha2
]
) is the following:

Lemma 3.4
.

Assume that
n\geq 8
. Let
\left(h_{k,x,\alpha}\right)_{k,x,\alpha}
be as in Lemma
3.3
. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
d\in\left\{4,\dotsc,n-4\right\}
, let
w_{k,x,d}
be defined by (
3.38
). Then there exists a unique family of real numbers
\left(\gamma_{k,x,d,l,m}\right)_{0\leq m\leq\left[\left(d-2\right)/2\right],0\leq l\leq m+2}
such that the function
v_{k,x,d}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

v_{k,x,d}\left(y\right):=\big(1+\left|y\right|^{2}\big)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\left[\left(d-2\right)/2\right]}\sum_{l=0}^{m+2}\gamma_{k,x,d,l,m}\left|y\right|^{2l}\Delta_{\xi}^{m}w_{k,x,d}\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

solves the equation

\Delta_{\xi}v_{k,x,d}=\left(2^{*}-1\right)U_{0}^{2^{*}-2}v_{k,x,d}+U_{0}w_{k,x,d}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n},

(3.41)

where

U_{0}\left(y\right):=\left(\frac{\sqrt{n\left(n-2\right)}}{1+\left|y\right|^{2}}\right)^{\frac{n-2}{2}}\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Moreover,

v_{k,x,d}\left(0\right)=\left|\nabla v_{k,x,d}\left(0\right)\right|=0

(3.42)

and, for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
,

\left|\nabla^{j}v_{k,x,d}\left(y\right)\right|=\bigO\left(\sum_{\left|\alpha\right|=d}\frac{\left|h_{k,x,\alpha}\right|}{\left(1+\left|y\right|\right)^{n-d-2+j}}\right)

(3.43)

uniformly with respect to
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

It is easy to see that

w_{k,x,d}\left(y\right)=\sum_{a,b=1}^{n}\partial_{y_{a}}\partial_{y_{b}}\left(H_{k,x,d}\right)_{ab}\left(y\right)=\divergence_{\xi}\divergence_{\xi}H_{k,x,d}\left(y\right)\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(3.44)

so that, in particular,
w_{k,x,d}
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d-2\in\left\{2,\dots,n-6\right\}
. We recall that two homogeneous polynomials
p
and
q
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are said to be orthogonal if

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}pqd\sigma=0,

where
\dsigma
is the volume element of the round metric on the
\left(n-1\right)
-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Since
w_{k,x,d}
is homogeneous of degree
d-2
, we obtain

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}w_{k,x,d}\dsigma=\left(n+d-2\right)\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}w_{k,x,d}\dy,

(3.45)

where
\mathbb{B}^{n}:=\B_{\xi}\left(0,1\right)
. On the other hand, by using (
3.37
) and (
3.44
) together with an integration by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}w_{k,x,d}\dy

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\partial_{y_{b}}\left(H_{k,x,d}\right)_{ab}\left(y\right)y_{a}\dsigma\left(y\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(\partial_{y_{b}}\left(\sum_{a=1}^{n}\left(H_{k,x,d}\right)_{ab}\left(y\right)y_{a}\right)-\left(H_{k,x,d}\right)_{bb}\left(y\right)\right)\dsigma\left(y\right)

\displaystyle=0.

(3.46)

It follows from (
3.45
) and (
3
) that
w_{k,x,d}
is orthogonal to 1. In a similar way, for each
i\in\left\{1,\dotsc,n\right\}
, we obtain

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}w_{k,x,d}\left(y\right)y_{i}\dsigma\left(y\right)=\left(n+d-1\right)\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}w_{k,x,d}\left(y\right)y_{i}\dy

(3.47)

and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}w_{k,x,d}\left(y\right)y_{i}\dy

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}\partial_{y_{a}}\left(\sum_{b=1}^{n}\partial_{y_{b}}\left(H_{k,x,d}\right)_{ab}\left(y\right)y_{i}-\left(H_{k,x,d}\right)_{ai}\left(y\right)\right)\dy

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(\sum_{b=1}^{n}\partial_{y_{b}}\left(H_{k,x,d}\right)_{ab}\left(y\right)y_{i}-\left(H_{k,x,d}\right)_{ai}\left(y\right)\right)y_{a}\dsigma\left(y\right)

\displaystyle=0.

(3.48)

It follows from (
3.47
) and (
3
) that
w_{k,x,d}
is orthogonal to
y_{i}
. We are now in position to apply
[
KMS
]
*Proposition 4.1 (see also the remark below), from which Lemma
3.4
then follows.
