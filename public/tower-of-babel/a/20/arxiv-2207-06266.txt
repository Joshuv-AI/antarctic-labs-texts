Recognizing and Realizing Inductively Pierced Codes
Ryan Curry, R. Amzi Jeffs, Nora Youngs, Ziyu Zhao

Abstract
We prove algebraic and combinatorial characterizations of the class of inductively pierced codes, resolving a conjecture of Gross, Obatake, and Youngs. Starting from an algebraic invariant of a code called its canonical form, we explain how to compute a piercing order in polynomial time, if one exists. Given a piercing order of a code, we explain how to construct a realization of the code using a well-formed collection of open balls, and classify the minimal dimension in which such a realization exists.

Introduction

Given a collection
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\}
of convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one may combinatorially record how these sets intersect and cover one another by computing the
code
of the collection
\mathcal{U}
, defined as

\displaystyle\code(\mathcal{U})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\Big\{\sigma\subseteq[n]\,\Big|\,\bigcap_{i\in\sigma}U_{i}\setminus\bigcup_{j\in[n]\setminus\sigma}U_{j}\neq\emptyset\Big\}

\displaystyle=\,\{\sigma\subseteq[n]\,|\,\text{there exists $p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ with $p\in U_{i}$ if and only if $i\in\sigma$}\}.

Above,
[n]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
Observe that
\code(\mathcal{U})
is a subset of the Boolean lattice
2^{[n]}
.
The collection
\mathcal{U}
is called a
realization
of
\code(\mathcal{U})
, and the region
\bigcap_{i\in\sigma}U_{i}\setminus\bigcup_{j\in[n]\setminus\sigma}U_{j}
is called the
atom
of
\sigma
in the realization
\mathcal{U}
.
Elements of a code are called
codewords
.
With this terminology, the codewords of
\code(\mathcal{U})
are exactly the subsets of
[n]
whose atoms are nonempty in the realization
\mathcal{U}
.

In 2013 Curto, Itskov, Veliz-Cuba, and Youngs
[
6
]
initiated the study of
open convex codes
, which are the codes that have realizations consisting of convex open sets.
Their work was motivated by the neuroscientific study of place cells, and for this reason it is typical to refer to the indices in
[n]
as
neurons
, and think of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as a stimulus space in which the various
U_{i}
are the place fields where each neuron is active.
Codes are a granular tool for capturing the intersection and covering patterns of a collection of sets, and so the study of codes that have realizations consisting of convex sets is of purely mathematical interest as well.
Indeed, work in this direction has led to novel theorems in discrete geometry, interesting constructive results, and rich families of extremal examples
[
4
,
5
,
14
,
3
,
11
,
12
,
2
,
7
,
10
]
.

.

Consider the code
\mathcal{C}=\{123,45,12,1,2,4,5,\emptyset\}
on five neurons.
Here we are eliminating brackets and commas in codewords to make our notation more concise—for example
123
represents
\{1,2,3\}
.
We will adopt this convention elsewhere as well.
Figure

shows a realization of
\mathcal{C}
using open balls in the plane.

Our work focuses exclusively on realizations consisting of convex open sets, and for this reason we use the more concise term
convex code
in place of “open convex code.”
An efficient characterization of all convex codes is unfortunately out of the question; recent work by Lienkaemper, Kunin, and Rosen
[
13
]
indicates that recognizing whether or not a code is convex is NP-hard, even in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
One way around this problem is to restrict to a simpler class of codes.
For example Cruz, Giusti, Itskov, and Kronholm
[
4
]
gave a construction showing that every intersection complete code is convex.

In this vein, our work treats the class of
k
-inductively pierced
codes, which can be built up iteratively from certain intervals of codewords (see Definition
2
below).
We give an algebraic and geometric characterization of
k
-inductively pierced codes (Theorem
5
), proving a conjecture of Gross, Obatake, and Youngs
[
9
]
. Furthermore, we exactly characterize the minimum dimension in which an inductively pierced code has a well-formed realization by open balls (Theorem
12
).
Importantly, all of our characterizations are efficient in the following sense: given the canonical form of a code, we can compute a piercing order in polynomial time if one exists, and a piercing order allows us to quickly compute the smallest dimension in which a code can be realized by a well-formed collection of open balls.
This is discussed in Section
4
.
To present our results formally, we first require some additional background.

Inductively Pierced Codes.

For any
\sigma\subseteq\tau\subseteq[n]
, the
interval
between
\sigma
and
\tau
is the set

[\sigma,\tau]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{\gamma\subseteq[n]\mid\sigma\subseteq\gamma\subseteq\tau\}.

The
rank
of an interval
[\sigma,\tau]
is equal to
|\tau\setminus\sigma|
.
Given a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
and a neuron
i\in[n]
, we will often consider the code
\mathcal{C}\setminus i
, called the
deletion
of
i
,
which is obtained by deleting
i
from every codeword in
\mathcal{C}
where it appears.
For any
\sigma\subseteq[n]
, we let
\mathcal{C}\setminus\sigma
denote the code obtained by successively deleting the neurons from f
\sigma
.
Geometrically, if
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\}
is a realization of
\mathcal{C}
then deleting
U_{i}
from this realization for all
i\in\sigma
yields a realization of
\mathcal{C}\setminus\sigma
.

Informally, a
k
-inductively pierced code is one which can be built up as a union of intervals with rank at most
k
that fit together appropriately.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be a code and let
i\in[n]
be a neuron.
We say that
i
is an
(abstract)
k
-piercing
of
\mathcal{C}
if there exist
\sigma\subseteq\tau\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}
so that

(i)

[\sigma,\tau]
has rank
k
,

(ii)

[\sigma,\tau]
is contained in
\mathcal{C}\setminus i
, and

(iii)

\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{C}\setminus i)\cup[\sigma\cup\{i\},\tau\cup\{i\}]
.

We say that
[\sigma,\tau]
is the interval
associated to
the piercing.
A code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
is
k
-inductively pierced
if
\mathcal{C}=\{\emptyset\}
, or there exists a neuron
i\in[n]
which is a
k^{\prime}
-piercing of
\mathcal{C}
for some
k^{\prime}\leq k
, and
\mathcal{C}\setminus i
is
k
-inductively pierced.
A code is called simply
inductively pierced
if it is
k
-inductively pierced for some value of
k
.

From the definition, any
k
-inductively pierced code admits an ordering
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{n}
of the neurons in
[n]
so that for each
j
, the neuron
i_{j}
is a
k^{\prime}
-piercing of
\mathcal{C}\setminus\{i_{j+1},\ldots,i_{n}\}
for some
k^{\prime}\leq k
.
We call such an order a
k
-piercing order
; note that there is no requirement that such an ordering be unique.

Inductively pierced codes were previously studied by Gross, Obatake, and Youngs
[
9
]
.
Motivated by the study of Euler diagrams, their work focused on
0
-,
1
-, and
2
-inductively pierced codes and their possible realizations in the plane.
We note that our definition of inductively pierced codes differs from theirs in notation, but is equivalent.
Our notation will allow us to more easily characterize inductively pierced codes, and describe their realizations in higher dimensions.

The Neural Ideal.

Curto, Itskov, Veliz-Cuba, and Youngs
[
6
]
introduced an algebraic approach to understanding codes and their realizations.
Their approach used
pseudo-monomials
in the polynomial ring
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, which are polynomials of the form
\prod_{i\in\sigma}x_{i}\prod_{j\in\tau}(1-x_{j})
where
\sigma
and
\tau
are disjoint subsets of
[n]
.
It will be useful to partially order pseudo-monomials by divisibility, so that in particular we can pick out
minimal
pseudo-monomials from a given set.
Each
\sigma\subseteq[n]
can be associated to an
indicator pseudo-monomial

\rho_{\sigma}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\prod_{i\in\sigma}x_{i}\prod_{j\in[n]\setminus\sigma}(1-x_{j}).

These indicators allow us to uniquely associate each code to an ideal in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.

Definition 3
.

Given a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
, we define the
neural ideal

J_{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
as follows:

J_{\mathcal{C}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\langle\rho_{\sigma}\,|\,\sigma\notin\mathcal{C}\rangle.

The
canonical form
of
J_{\mathcal{C}}
, denoted
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
, is the set of minimal pseudo-monomials in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Although the definition of
J_{\mathcal{C}}
uses indicator pseudo-monomials, which have degree equal to
n
, the elements of the canonical form can in general have much lower degree.
The pseudo-monomials in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
have the following important geometric interpretation:
\prod_{i\in\sigma}x_{i}\prod_{j\in\tau}(1-x_{j})
is an element of
J_{\mathcal{C}}
if and only if
\bigcap_{i\in\sigma}U_{i}\subseteq\bigcup_{j\in\tau}U_{j}
in every (possibly not convex or open) realization
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\}
of
\mathcal{C}
.
Thus
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
can be regarded as a minimal description of the intersection and covering information in any realization of
\mathcal{C}
.

Additionally,
J_{\mathcal{C}}
provides a generalization of the well-studied Stanley-Reisner ideal
[
15
,
17
]
of a simplicial complex: if
\mathcal{C}
is a simplicial complex then
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
consists of monomials corresponding to minimal non-faces of
\mathcal{C}
, and
J_{\mathcal{C}}
is the Stanely-Reisner ideal of
\mathcal{C}
(see
[
6
, Section 4.4]
). The ideal
J_{\mathcal{C}}
and the canonical form are both unique to a particular code, and can be used to characterize codewords. Using a slight abuse of notation, we can evaluate polynomials in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
on elements of
2^{[n]}
, where
f(\sigma)
is computed by setting
x_{i}=1
if
i\in\sigma
, and
x_{i}=0
otherwise. We make use of the following useful facts: given
\sigma\subseteq[n]
, we have
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}
if and only if
f(\sigma)=0
for all
f\in J_{\mathcal{C}}
and if and only if
f(\sigma)=0
for all
f\in\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
.

Conventions.

We are interested in using the canonical form to study inductively pierced codes and their realizations.
To streamline our work, we will adopt several conventions, which we explain below.

(1)

Every code contains
\emptyset
as a codeword.

This means that sets in a realization do not cover all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and in particular we may restrict our attention to realizations consisting of bounded sets.
Algebraically, this means that
\prod_{i\in[n]}(1-x_{i})
is not in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
, and hence
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
does not contain any pseudo-monomial that is entirely a product of
(1-x_{i})
terms.

(2)

Every neuron appears in some codeword.

This means that no set in a realization is empty.
Algebraically, this means that
x_{i}
is not in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
for any
i\in[n]
.

(3)

No two neurons appear in exactly the same codewords.

This means that no two sets in a realization are equal.
Note that if two neurons did have identical behavior, deleting one does not change whether or not a code is realizable, and so this assumption does not affect our analysis.
Algebraically, this means that for any
i\neq j
we do not have both
x_{i}(1-x_{j})
and
x_{j}(1-x_{i})
in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Note that every code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
can be associated to a code
\mathcal{D}
satisfying (2) and (3) above, so that
\mathcal{D}
is convex if and only if
\mathcal{C}
is convex.
Indeed, up to permutation, any neurons that do not appear can be forgotten, and if two neurons have identical behavior one of them can be discarded.

Characterizing Inductively Pierced Codes.

Note that our conventions (1) and (2) imply that every pseudo-monomial in
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
has degree at least two.
We say that a code
\mathcal{C}
is
degree two
if every pseudo-monomial in
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
has degree exactly two.
Gross, Obatake, and Youngs
[
9
]
showed that under the conventions (1)–(3), every
2
-inductively pierced code is degree two.
Their techniques imply the more general result that any inductively pierced code is degree two.
We will postpone the proof of this and our other results until Section
2
.

Proposition 4
.

If
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
is inductively pierced, then
\mathcal{C}
is degree two—that is, all elements of
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
have degree two.

Following
[
9
]
, we associate each degree two code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
to a graph
G(\mathcal{C})
called the
general relationship graph
of
\mathcal{C}
, which has vertex set
[n]
and an edge
ij
whenever
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
does not contain any pseudo-monomial whose two variables are
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
.
Geometrically,
ij
is an edge in
G(\mathcal{C})
if and only if
U_{i}
and
U_{j}
intersect and neither contains the other in every realization of
\mathcal{C}
.
It turns out that the general relationship graph determines whether or not
\mathcal{C}
is inductively pierced.

Below, recall that a graph is
chordal
if it can be built up by successively adding
simplicial vertices
, whose neighborhood among previously added vertices is a clique. The reverse of the order in which these vertices are added is called a
perfect elimination order
.

Theorem 5
.

A code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
is inductively pierced if and only if
\mathcal{C}
is degree two and
G(\mathcal{C})
is chordal.
If
k
is the smallest integer so that
\mathcal{C}
is
k
-inductively pierced, then the largest clique in
G(\mathcal{C})
has size
k+1
.

Computing Piercing Orders.

Theorem
5
gives a positive resolution to a conjecture of Gross, Obatake, and Youngs
[
9
]
.
However, we would like to not only characterize inductively pierced codes, but also find a way to recognize them efficiently.
To this end, we introduce a second auxiliary object associated to a degree two code.
Given a degree two code
\mathcal{C}
, let
P(\mathcal{C})
be the partially ordered set whose elements are
[n]
and which has comparability relation defined by

\text{$i<j$ in $P(\mathcal{C})$ if and only if $x_{i}(1-x_{j})\in\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})$}.

Recall that
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
cannot contain both
x_{i}(1-x_{j})
and
x_{j}(1-x_{i})
by convention (3), so this relation is antisymmetric.
Moreover, if we have
i<j<k
in
P(\mathcal{C})
, then
x_{i}(1-x_{j})
and
x_{j}(1-x_{k})
both lie in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
, and so
x_{i}(1-x_{k})=\big(x_{i}(1-x_{j})\big)(1-x_{k})+x_{i}\big(x_{j}(1-x_{k})\big)
lies in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
.
In fact,
x_{i}(1-x_{k})
lies in
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
since
\mathcal{C}
is degree two, hence this relation is transitive, and defines a partial order.
As previously noted in the discussion on the neural ideal, we have
x_{i}(1-x_{j})
in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
if and only if
U_{i}\subseteq U_{j}
in every realization of
\mathcal{C}
, so
P(\mathcal{C})
can be regarded as capturing pairwise containments of sets in any realization of
\mathcal{C}
.

We will use
P(\mathcal{C})
and
G(\mathcal{C})
to identify piercings of
\mathcal{C}
, and thus find piercing orders.
It turns out that piercings of
\mathcal{C}
correspond to elimination neurons, defined below.

Definition 6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be a degree two code.
We say that a neuron
i\in[n]
is an
elimination neuron
if
i
is a simplicial vertex in
G(\mathcal{C})
and a minimal element in
P(\mathcal{C})
.

Proposition 7
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be a degree two code.
Then a neuron
i\in[n]
is a
k
-piercing of
\mathcal{C}
if and only if
i
is an elimination neuron with
k
neighbors in
G(\mathcal{C})
.

Note that Proposition
7
already provides a novel characterization of piercing orders: they are the linear orders on
[n]
whose reverse is simultaneously a perfect elimination order of
G(\mathcal{C})
and a linear extension of
P(\mathcal{C})
.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be a code, and suppose that a neuron
i\in[n]
is a piercing of
\mathcal{C}
. Then
\mathcal{C}
is inductively pierced if and only if
\mathcal{C}\setminus i
is inductively pierced.

Propositions
7
and
8
will let us argue that not only can we find piercings efficiently, but also that we will also never “get stuck” when trying to build a piercing order.
In other words, a piercing order may be computed greedily.
For a full discussion of this algorithm, see Section
4
.
For now, we summarize our realization results.

Realizing Inductively Pierced Codes.

Since inductively pierced codes have a significant amount of combinatorial structure, it is reasonable to hope that they admit structured realizations.
Rather than seek realizations by arbitrary convex open sets, we seek realizations by open balls.
This can be achieved from any piercing order, and we can easily characterize the dimension in which such realizations exist.
Below we make the additional requirement that our realizations are well-formed, in the sense that the boundaries of sets intersect generically.

Definition 9
.

A collection
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{n}\}
of
(d-1)
-dimensional spheres in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called
well-formed
if for every
k\leq d
the intersection of
k
-many spheres from
\mathcal{S}
is either empty, or a
(d-k)
-dimensional sphere, and the intersection of
(d+1)
-many spheres from
\mathcal{S}
is empty. A collection of open balls is called well-formed if their boundaries comprise a well-formed collection of spheres.

The above definition of well-formed spheres mirrors the definition of well-formed curves used by Gross, Obatake, and Youngs
[
9
]
and the literature on Euler diagrams.
Indeed, a collection of well-formed circles in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a special case of a collection of well-formed curves.
To characterize the exact dimensions in which an inductively pierced code admits a well-formed realization by open balls, we require two combinatorial definitions.

Definition 10
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be a degree two code, and suppose that the largest clique in
G(\mathcal{C})
has size
k+1
.
For every
\sigma\subseteq[n]
of size
k
on which
G(\mathcal{C})
is a clique, the
attaching set
of
\sigma
consists of all
i\in[n]\setminus\sigma
whose neighborhood contains
\sigma
.

In other words, the attaching set of
\sigma
consists of the vertices which can be added to
\sigma
to form a clique of size
k+1
.
Note that the attaching set of
\sigma
is always an independent set, as an edge between two of its elements would yield a clique of size
k+2
in
G(\mathcal{C})
.

Definition 11
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be a degree two code, and suppose that the largest clique in
G(\mathcal{C})
has size
k+1
.
We say that
\mathcal{C}
is
splittable
if the following holds for every
\sigma\subseteq[n]
of size
k
on which
G(\mathcal{C})
is a clique: the attaching set of
\sigma
can be partitioned as
A\sqcup B
so that
P(\mathcal{C})
restricted to the attaching set consists of two disjoint total orders on
A
and
B
respectively.

Theorem 12
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be an inductively pierced code, and let
k\geq 1
. If the largest clique in
G(\mathcal{C})
has size
k+1
, then

(I)

\mathcal{C}
has a well-formed realization by open balls in
\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
,

(II)

\mathcal{C}
has such a realization in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
if and only if
\mathcal{C}
is splittable, and

(III)

\mathcal{C}
does not have such a realization in
\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
.

Note that Theorem
12
disregards 0-inductively pierced codes.
These codes are simple to realize since their realizations consist of sets that are either pairwise disjoint or nested, which can always be managed in
\mathbb{R}^{1}
.

.

.

Consider the following code:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=

This code is 2-inductively pierced, and the natural linear order on

is a piercing order.
It further turns out that

is splittable.
Note, for example, that the attaching set of

is
\{5,7,9\}
, and the partition

has

a total order on each part.

shows

and

Example 14
.

Consider the following code:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=

\displaystyle\{123,124,125,

\displaystyle\,\,\,12,13,14,15,23,24,25,1,2,3,4,5,\emptyset\}.

This code is 2-inductively pierced; indeed, the natural linear order is a piercing order, and the largest clique in
G(\mathcal{C})
has size three. However, this code is not splittable. The attaching set of
\sigma=\{1,2\}
is
\{3,4,5\}
and this set cannot be partitioned into two linearly ordered subsets in
P(\mathcal{C})
, since
P(\mathcal{C})
is an antichain on this set. Thus, Theorem
12
implies that this code cannot be realized with open balls in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, but such a realization is possible in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
One such realization in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
consists of two intersecting unit balls
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
, with
U_{3}
,
U_{4}
, and
U_{5}
equal to small disjoint balls centered at points on the circle where the boundaries of
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
meet.

2
Characterizing Inductively Pierced Codes

To begin, we justify several important statements about the canonical form, which may be of general interest.
First, we describe the effect of eliminating a neuron in any (not necessarily inductively pierced) code.
We then characterize how a piercing impacts the canonical form, again without assuming that the code in question is inductively pierced.
This yields a short proof of Proposition
4
, establishing that every inductively pierced code is degree two.

Lemma 15
.

For any code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
, the canonical form of
\mathcal{C}\setminus n\subseteq 2^{[n-1]}
consists of exactly the pseudo-monomials in
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
which do not depend on the variable
x_{n}
; that is,

\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}\setminus n})=\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})\setminus\{f\in\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})\mid x_{n}\text{ or }(1-x_{n})\text{ divides }f\}.

Proof.

First we argue that the pseudo-monomials in
J_{\mathcal{C}\setminus n}
are exactly those in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
that do not depend on
x_{n}
.
Let
f
be any pseudo-monomial that does not depend on
x_{n}
.
Observe that
f(\sigma)=0
if and only if
f(\sigma\setminus\{n\})=0
for every
\sigma\subseteq[n]
.
Thus
f
vanishes on every codeword of
\mathcal{C}
if and only if
f
vanishes on every codeword of
\mathcal{C}\setminus n
.
Since the pseudo-monomials in the neural ideal of a code are exactly those which vanish on every codeword, we conclude that
f\in J_{\mathcal{C}\setminus n}
if and only if

3
Realizing Inductively Pierced Codes

Below, we will make use of existing results regarding
set-theoretically independent
collections of open balls in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
—in our language, a collection
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{n}\}
of balls is set-theoretically independent whenever
\code(\mathcal{U})
is the full power set
2^{[n]}
.
For concision, we will use the simpler term
independent
.
We will need the following facts regarding collections of independent balls:

(i)

The largest size of a collection of independent balls in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
d+1
. This was first established by Rényi, Rényi, and Surányi
[
16
]
.

(ii)

Let
\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{k}\}
be a collection of
k\leq d
independent balls in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then the intersection of the boundaries of the
U_{i}
is a
(d-k)
-dimensional sphere
S
.
In particular, the
U_{i}
are a well-formed collection of balls.
This result appears as Theorem 1 in a paper by Anusiak
[
1
]
.

(iii)

In the situation of item (ii),
S
is the set of common limit points of all atoms in the realization
\mathcal{U}
(Theorem 3 of
[
1
]
).

(iv)

In the situation of item (ii), suppose additionally that
k=d
, and let
U_{d+1}
be any ball.
Then
S=\{p,q\}
is a 0-dimensional sphere, and the collection of balls
\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{d},U_{d+1}\}
is independent if and only if the closure of
U_{d+1}
contains exactly one of
p
or
q
(Theorem 2 of
[
1
]
).

To build well-formed realizations of inductively pierced codes, we will use the following geometric analog of an abstract piercing.

Definition 18
.

Let
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\}
be a realization of
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
An interval
[\sigma,\tau]\subseteq\mathcal{C}
is called
pierceable
at a point
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
if the codewords which arise in any small neighborhood of
p
are exactly those in
[\sigma,\tau]
.
Equivalently,
[\sigma,\tau]
is pierceable at
p
if and only if
p
is a limit point of the atom of every codeword in
[\sigma,\tau]
and no others.

Given a collection
\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\}
of well-formed (but not necessarily independent) balls in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a point
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it will be convenient to assign
p
to a vector
\rho(p)
of length
n
on the symbols
\{+,0,-\}
, as follows.
The
i
-th coordinate of
\rho(p)
will record whether
p
lies in the interior, boundary, or complement of the closure of
U_{i}
, with
+
,
0
, and
-
denoting these respective possibilities.
With this notation, an interval
[\sigma,\tau]
is pierceable at
p
if and only if
\rho(p)
has
+
’s in the coordinates of
\sigma
,
0
’s in the coordinates of
\tau\setminus\sigma
, and
-
’s everywhere else (note that to establish this we need to use item (iii) above on the collection of balls indexed by
\tau\setminus\sigma
).
With this observation, we can prove the following technical lemma.

Lemma 19
.

Let
1\leq k\leq d
, and let
\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{k}\}
be a collection of independent balls in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then every interval
[\sigma,\tau]\subseteq 2^{[k]}
is pierceable at some point.

Proof.

Let
p
be a point in the intersection of the boundaries of all
U_{i}
, noting that such a point exists because
k\leq d
.
Observe that
\rho(p)
is the all zeroes vector.
We wish to find a point whose vector has
+
for each index in
\sigma
,
0
for each index in
\tau\setminus\sigma
, and
-
for all other indices.
To achieve this, for every

, we will replace

by a new point, so that our new point has the correct symbols in the first
i

0
’s in the remaining indices.

Let

, and assume that
\rho(p)
has the correct symbols in indices less than

, and

’s for indices equal to

lies in the intersection of the boundaries of all

with

with

equal to zero in the

Let

Observe that

Thus

By moving

, proving the result.
∎

The following lemma allows us to perform a (geometric)
k
-piercing in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that all previously pierceable intervals remain pierceable, and all new intervals are also pierceable, provided that
k<d
.
By applying this lemma repeatedly, we will prove Lemma
21
, establishing a slightly stronger version of part (I) of Theorem
12
.

Lemma 20
.

Let
1\leq k<d
.
Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be a code, suppose that the neuron
n
is a
k
-piercing of
\mathcal{C}
with associated interval
[\sigma,\tau]
, and let
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{C}\setminus n
.
If
\mathcal{D}
has a well-formed realization
\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{n-1}\}
by open balls in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
p
is a point where
[\sigma,\tau]
is pierceable in this realization, then for any sufficiently small open ball
U_{n}
centered at
p
the following hold:

(i)

\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\}
is a well-formed realization of
\mathcal{C}
by open balls,

(ii)

every interval in
\mathcal{D}
that was pierceable in the original realization is also pierceable in this new realization, and

(iii)

every interval in
\mathcal{C}
whose top element contains
n
is pierceable in this new realization.

Proof.

Item (i) is immediate since
[\sigma,\tau]
is pierceable at
p
.
The point
p
must lie on the common intersection of the boundaries of all
U_{i}
with
i\in\tau\setminus\sigma
.
This common intersection is a sphere
S
of dimension at least one since
k<d
.
The point
p
lies in the interior of all
U_{j}
with
j\in\sigma
, and in the complement of the closure of all
U_{j}
with
j\in[n]\setminus\tau
.
The same is true for all points in a small neighborhood of
p
, and hence
S
contains an infinite set of points at which
[\sigma,\tau]
is pierceable.
For every interval
[\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}]
in
\mathcal{D}
that was pierceable in our original realization (including the interval
[\sigma,\tau]
itself), choose a point
p^{\prime}\neq p
at which this interval was pierceable.
Choosing
U_{n}
small enough that its closure avoids all such
p^{\prime}
guarantees item (ii).

For item (iii), define
\gamma=(\tau\cup\{n\})\setminus\sigma
and observe that the
U_{i}
with
i
in
\gamma
comprise a collection of
k+1\leq d
independent balls.
Each interval
[\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}]
in
\mathcal{C}
with
n\in\tau^{\prime}
must be a subset of
[\sigma,\tau\cup\{n\}]
.
Up to removing the indices in
\sigma
, such an interval corresponds to an interval in
2^{\gamma}
, which is pierceable by Lemma
19
.
Since
n\in\tau
, the point where this interval is pierceable lies in the closure of
U_{n}
.
In particular, this point lies in the interior of all
U_{i}
with
i\in\sigma
, and in the complement of the closure of all
U_{j}
with
j\in[n-1]\setminus\tau
.
Hence the interval
[\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}]
is pierceable at this point in our new realization, proving the result.
∎

Lemma 21
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be a
k
-inductively pierced code.
Then
\mathcal{C}
has a well-formed realization by open balls in
\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
, with the following property: every interval of rank at most
k
in
\mathcal{C}
is pierceable at some point.

Proof.

We may assume that the natural linear order on
n
is a piercing order, and proceed by induction on
n
, applying Lemma
20
at each step.
The base case
n=1
is immediate by choosing any open ball
U_{1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
.
For the inductive step, one must only note that the intervals described in items (ii) and (iii) account for all intervals of rank at most
k
in
\mathcal{C}
.
Indeed, when performing a
k
-piercing, the only new intervals of rank at most
k
that are introduced to the code are those of the form
[\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}\cup\{n\}]
, which are covered by case (iii), while case (ii) covers all previously introduced intervals of rank at most
k
.
∎

We now turn our attention to the task of performing geometric
k
-piercings in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
Since the intersection of
k
-many independent spheres in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a 0-dimensional sphere consisting of only two points, not every
k
-piercing can be realized geometrically in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
However, when a code is splittable (recall Definition
11
), certain
k
-piercings can be achieved.
We say that an interval
[\sigma,\tau]
in a splittable code
\mathcal{C}
is
accessible
if it has rank less than
k
, or if it has rank exactly
k
and the following holds: the intersection of
\sigma
with the attaching set
A\sqcup B
of
\tau\setminus\sigma
is equal to
A
or
B
.

Lemma 22
.

Let
k\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be an inductively
k
-pierced code, and suppose that
\mathcal{C}
is splittable.
Then
\mathcal{C}
has a well-formed realization by open balls in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, with the following property: every accessible interval in
\mathcal{C}
is pierceable at some point.

Proof.

By relabeling, we can reduce to the case where the natural linear order on
[n]
is a piercing order.
Similarly to the proof of Lemma
21
, we will proceed by induction on
n
.
For the base case
n=1
, any open ball
U_{1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
has every interval pierceable, and the result follows. For
n\geq 2
, define
\mathcal{D}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{C}\setminus n
, let
[\sigma,\tau]
be the interval associated to the
k
-piercing of
\mathcal{C}
by
n
, and let
\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{n-1}\}
be a realization of
\mathcal{D}
satisfying the conclusion of the lemma.
If
[\sigma,\tau]
has rank less than
k
(i.e.
n
is a
k^{\prime}
-piercing of
\mathcal{C}
with
k^{\prime}<k
) then Lemma
20
yields a realization of
\mathcal{C}
with the desired properties.

This leaves the case that
[\sigma,\tau]
has rank exactly
k
.
In this case, let
A\sqcup B
be the attaching set of
\tau\setminus\sigma
in
\mathcal{C}
, and assume without loss of generality that
n\in A
.
Since
\mathcal{C}
is splittable and
n
is minimal in
P(\mathcal{C})
,
n
is less than every
a\in A
and is not comparable to any
b\in B
.
It follows that
\sigma
contains
A
and no elements of
B
.
Noting that the attaching set of
\tau\setminus\sigma
in
\mathcal{D}
is
A\setminus\{n\}\sqcup B
, we see that the interval
[\sigma,\tau]
is accessible in
\mathcal{D}
, and by inductive hypothesis there exists a point
p
where this interval is pierceable in the realization
\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{n-1}\}
.

Consider a small open ball
U_{n}
centered at
p
.
By making
U_{n}
small enough, we can guarantee that
\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\}
is a well-formed realization of
\mathcal{C}
by open balls, and that every interval in
\mathcal{D}
that was pierceable remains pierceable, except possibly the interval
[\sigma,\tau]
itself.
Moreover, Lemma
19
guarantees that any new intervals
[\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}]
of rank
k
or less with
n\in\tau^{\prime}
are pierceable in our new realization as well.
The only remaining interval in
\mathcal{C}
which could be accessible is
[\sigma,\tau]
.
Observe that this interval is accessible in
\mathcal{C}
if and only if
B=\emptyset
.
In this case, the 0-dimensional sphere which is the intersection of the boundaries of all

indexed by

will consist of
p
and a point
q
, and the interval

will be pierceable at both these points in the realization of
\mathcal{D}
.
The set
U_{n}
must avoid the point
q
, lest the spheres indexed by

not form an independent collection, and so
[\sigma,\tau]
remains pierceable at
q
in our constructed realization of
\mathcal{C}
.
This proves the result.
∎

The above lemmas give the required constructive results for Theorem

, and it remains to establish the theorem’s restrictions on well-formed realizations of inductively pierced codes—namely that if

has a well-formed realization by open balls in

must be splittable, and that realizations in

Lemma 23
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be an inductively pierced code, suppose that the largest clique in
G(\mathcal{C})
has size
k+1
, and suppose that
\mathcal{C}
has a well-formed realization by open balls in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
Then
\mathcal{C}
is splittable.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be the set of vertices of a clique of size

in
G(\mathcal{C})

In any well-formed realization of
\mathcal{C}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
by balls, the intersection of the boundaries of the balls indexed by

will be a
0

p
and
q
.
The balls indexed by the attaching set of
\sigma
must each contain exactly one of
p
or
q
in their closure.
Let
A
be the set of neurons whose associated open balls contain

, and let
B
be the neurons whose associated balls contain
q
.
Clearly
S=A\sqcup B

It remains to argue that

is a total order on

and

respectively, and that no element of

is comparable to any element of

.
To see that

is a total order on

is an independent set in

contains one of the pseudo-monomials

Since

and

share the point

does not occur in

and

are comparable in

.
Since any pair of elements in

are comparable,

is a total order on
A
.
A symmetric argument establishes that

is a total order on

Finally, note that if

and

and

are not comparable in

because

contains

does not, and symmetrically

∎

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}

4
Computing Piercing Orders and Concluding Remarks

Motivated by the work of Gross, Obatake, and Youngs
[
9
]
, we provide a complete characterization of inductively pierced codes. Additionally, we show that inductively pierced codes can be realized with open balls, and we exactly characterize the smallest dimension in which such realizations exist.
A consequence of our characterization is that an inductively pierced code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
can be realized in dimension
n-1
or less.
This strongly contrasts the general situation for convex codes: Jeffs
[
11
]
showed that there are codes
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
which can only be realized in dimensions that are
exponential
in terms of
n
.

Within our results and their proofs, we have developed an implicit algorithm for a) recognizing if a code is inductively pierced, b) computing a piercing order, c) determining the minimal dimension in which the code may be realized with open balls, and d) producing such a realization.
We summarize this algorithm below.
Recall that the codes we work with satisfy the mild requirements (1)–(3) outlined in the introduction.

Algorithm for computing a piercing order.

1.

Compute
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
.

Note:
Current algorithms for this are not necessarily efficient—see for example the discussion by Garcia, Puente, Kruse, Liu, Miyata, Petersen, Phillipson, and Shiu
[
8
, Page 1]
.
We conjecture below that
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
can be computed efficiently when
\mathcal{C}
is inductively pierced, even if we do not know this a priori.

2.

Verify that
\mathcal{C}
is degree two. If not, then
\mathcal{C}
is not inductively pierced.

3.

Form
G(\mathcal{C})
and
P(\mathcal{C})
from
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
.

4.

Search for an elimination neuron
i\in[n]
(recall Definition
6
).
This can be done in
O(n^{2})
time by computing the neighborhood of each neuron in
G(\mathcal{C})
and checking whether it is simplicial, and then comparing all simplicial vertices according to
P(\mathcal{C})
to find a minimal one.
If no elimination neuron exists, then
\mathcal{C}
is not inductively pierced.

5.

Perform a permutation of
[n]
so that
n
is our elimination neuron.
Record this permutation and the clique consisting of
n
and its neighbors.

6.

Delete
n
from
G(\mathcal{C})
and
P(\mathcal{C})
to obtain
G(\mathcal{C}\setminus n)
and
P(\mathcal{C}\setminus n)
respectively.
Repeat from Step 4 until no neurons remain, tracking the permutations from each time that we perform in Step 5.

7.

Compose the recorded permutations to obtain a piercing order for
\mathcal{C}
.
The smallest value of
k
for which
\mathcal{C}
is
k
-inductively pierced is size of the largest clique recorded in an instance of Step 5, minus one.

8.

Determine if
\mathcal{C}
is splittable.
Each clique of size
k
will be contained in one of the
(k+1)
-cliques recorded in Step 5, so there are at most
n(k+1)
such cliques to check.
For each, we can compute its attaching set and check whether it is possible to partition it as in Definition
11
.

9.

If
\mathcal{C}
is splittable, we form a realization in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Otherwise, we form a realization in
\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
.
To realize
\mathcal{C}
by open balls, sequentially add open balls according to the piercing order.
Lemmas
20
,
21
, and
22
determine the points on the boundaries of these balls where subsequent balls should be added to form the appropriate code.

Conjecture 25
.

Fix
d\geq 2
.
Given a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
, there is a polynomial-time algorithm to determine whether or not every pseudo-monomial in
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
has degree at most
d
.
Here, the input to the algorithm is a list of the codewords in
\mathcal{C}
, with each codeword given as a binary vector of length
n
.

Note that when
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
consists of pseudo-monomials with degree bounded by a fixed constant
d
, there is a polynomial-time algorithm for determining
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
.
Indeed, the total number of pseudo-monomials in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
of degree at most
d
is
2^{d}\binom{n}{d}+2^{d-1}\binom{n}{d-1}+\cdots+2d+1=O(n^{d})
, and so we could simply evaluate each of these pseudo-monomials on all codewords to determine which ones lie in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
, and the compare them pairwise to find the minimal ones, which will comprise
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
.
Hence if this conjecture is true, then our results imply that there is a polynomial-time algorithm for determining whether or not a code is inductively pierced, even if we are not initially given
\CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
.

Concluding Remarks.

Our work proves that inductively pierced codes must be realizable using open balls, but there are codes that are not inductively pierced but nevertheless admit a realization by open balls. Indeed, there are such codes that are not degree two, and there are also such codes that are degree two but have
G(\mathcal{C})
non-chordal.
The code
\mathcal{C}=\{12,13,23,1,2,3,\emptyset\}
is a simple example of the first case, as its canonical form is
\{x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\}
, but it can be realized in two dimensions using open balls, as in Figure
4
.
For an example of the second case, consider
\mathcal{D}=\{12,14,23,34,1,2,3,4,\emptyset\}
, which has canonical form
\{x_{1}x_{3},x_{2}x_{4}\}
and hence its associated graph is a 4-cycle, which is not chordal. However,
\mathcal{D}
has a realization by open balls in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as shown in Figure
4
.

Figure 4:
Two codes that are not inductively pierced, but can be realized with well-formed collections of open balls in the plane. (Left) The code
\mathcal{C}
is not degree two. (Right) The graph
G(\mathcal{D})
is not chordal.

It seems plausible to seek larger classes of codes that admit realizations that are constructed iteratively from open balls.
However, codes such as
\mathcal{D}
above, whose realizations cover non-contractible subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, will pose a challenge to such an approach. Had we arranged
U_{1},U_{2}
, and
U_{3}
in a vertical line, then there would have been no way to add an appropriate open ball (or any convex set)
U_{4}
at the final step.
This highlights the special structure of inductively pierced codes, whose realizations do not create loops or other topologically interesting features.

Figure 5:
An inductively pierced code realized by ellipsoidal regions in the plane. This code cannot be realized by open balls in the plane.

Finally, note that when realizations are not restricted to open balls, our characterization of realization dimension does not apply, even when a code is inductively pierced.
For example, consider the complete code on four neurons,
\mathcal{C}=2^{[4]}
. This code is inductively pierced—its canonical form is empty and thus trivially meets the degree two condition, and its graph
G(\mathcal{C})
is the complete graph
K_{4}
and thus is chordal.
Theorem
12
implies that
\mathcal{C}
is not realizable in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
using open balls, but it does have realization in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
using ellipsoidal regions, as shown in Figure
5
.
The principal difference between this realization and any realization using open balls is that the intersection of two ellipses need not be a lower-dimensional ellipse. In the figure, the sets
U_{2}
and
U_{3}
“pass through” one another, and their boundaries share four common points.
Accounting for such possibilities renders it more difficult to formulate simple inductive constructions for realizations by ellipses. A characterization of minimal dimension for realizations of inductively pierced codes using ellipses (or other types of convex regions) remains an open question.
