Assume-guarantee contract algebras are bounded Sugihara monoids
José Luis Castiglioni, Rodolfo C. Ertola-Biraben

Abstract
In [Incer Romeo, I. X., The Algebra of Contracts . Ph.D. Thesis, UC Berkeley (2022)] an algebraic perspective on assume-guarantee contracts is proposed. This proposal relies heavily on a construction involving Boolean algebras. However, the structures thus proposed lack a clearly prescribed set of basic operations, necessary if we want to see them as a class of algebras (in the sense of Universal Algebra). In this article, by prescribing a suitable set of basic operations on contracts, we manage to describe these algebras as (a generating set of members of) well-known varieties.



1
Introduction

The aim of this short note is to observe that the class of assume-guarantee contract algebras, as presented in
[
3
]
, can be seen as a particular class of bounded odd Sugihara monoids (as presented in
[
2
]
).
More concretely, we show that choosing an adequate signature for assume-guarantee contract algebras, they may be regarded as odd Sugihara monoids.
Furthermore, any bounded three-valued odd Sugihara monoid (see Section
3.1
for its definition) is isomorphic (as a bounded Sugihara monoid) to an assume-guarantee contract algebra (as defined in Section
2
).
Hence, from an algebraic point of view, we can abstractly identify assume-guarantee contract algebras and bounded three-valued Sugihara monoids.

Now, we follow
[
3
]
for introducing the basic ideas regarding contracts.
We refer the reader to that work for further details.

The notion of contract derives from the theory of abstract data types and was first suggested by Meyer in the context of the programming language Eiffel,
following the original ideas introduced by Floyd and Hoare to assign logical meaning to sequential imperative programs in the form of triples of assertions.

Assume-Guarantee contracts (for short, AGC) provide a formal framework for contract-based design.
AGCs assume that all the functional and non-functional behaviours of the system have been modelled in advance as elements of an underlying set of behaviours.
Two concepts to implement compositional design with contracts are required:

•

a notion of order on contracts and

•

an operation to obtain system specifications from the specifications of subsystems.

Following this intuition, an abstract algebraic description of AGCs is proposed.
An AGC-algebra would be some very particular type of lattice ordered monoid, where the lattice structure renders account of the notion of order on contracts and the monoid operation is that allowing the obtention of system specifications from the specifications of subsystems.
That is to say, we think AGC-algebras as algebras
(A,\wedge,\vee,\|,\sim,\bot,e,\top)
of type (2,2,2,1,0,0,0) such that
(A,\vee,\wedge,\bot,\top)
is a bounded distributive lattice,
(A,\|,e)
is a commutative monoid and
\sim:A\to A
is the interchange between assumptions and guarantees, that is, an involution on the lattice reduct of
A
, making it a De Morgan algebra.

Odd Sugihara monoids are well-known involutive commutative residuated lattices, of interest in the study of certain relevance logics
[
7
]
.
Their lattice reducts are De Morgan algebras.
The definition and properties of the classes of odd Sugihara monoids of interest for this work are presented in Section
3.1
.
As was shown in
[
2
]
, the variety of bounded odd Sugihara monoids is categoricaly equivalent to that of Gödel algebras.
In Section
4
, we show that this equivalence restricts to one between tha category of Boolean algebras (
\mathsf{BA}
) and that of bounded three-valued odd Sugihara monoids (
\mathsf{B3SM}
).
This equivalence allows us to show that every bounded three-valued Sugihara monoid is isomorphic to an AGC-algebra.
In particular, the category of AGC-algebras (
\mathbb{AGCA}
), which is a full subcategory of
\mathsf{B3SM}
, is also equivalent to
\mathsf{BA}
.

In Section
5
, we show that the variety
\mathsf{B3SM}
is term equivalent to other well-studied varieties and, in consequence, AGC-algebras may also be seen (up to term equivalence) as algebras in these other varieties.

Finally, in Section
6
we compare our work with the adjunction in
[
4
]
.

3
Odd Sugihara monoids

Choosing the type of concrete AGC-algebras in the previous section was not innocent.
In this section we shall introduce well-known algebras of the same type that will help to abstractly characterize them.
The following definitions are based in
[
7
]
.
Note that the same classes of algebras appear in
[
2
]

Definition 3.1
.

An
odd Sugihara monoid
is an algebra
(A;\wedge,\vee,\cdot,\sim,e)
of type (2,2,2,1,0) such that

A1

(A;\vee,\wedge)
is a distributive lattice,

A2

(A;\cdot,e)
is a commutative monoid such that
x\leq x\cdot x
,

A3

\sim\sim x=x
,

A4

if
z\cdot x\leq y
, then
z\ \cdot\sim y\leq\ \sim x
,

A5

\sim e=e
.

Definition 3.2
.

A
bounded odd Sugihara monoid
is an algebra
(A;\wedge,\vee,\cdot,\sim,0,e,1)
of type (2,2,2,1,0,0,0) such that

(A;\vee,\wedge,0,1)
is a bounded lattice,

(A;\wedge,\vee,\cdot,\sim,e)
is an odd Sugihara monoid.

It is well known that the class of bounded odd Sugihara monoids forms a variety, which we denote by
\mathsf{BOSM}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

For any
a,b
in a bounded odd Sugihara monoid,

(i)

a\wedge b\leq a\cdot b
,

(ii)

a\ \cdot\sim a\leq e
,

(iii)

a\ \wedge\sim(a\cdot 1)=0
,

(iv)

(a\ \cdot\sim b)\wedge(b\ \cdot\sim a)\leq e
.

Proof.

Due to
A2
, we have that
a\wedge b\leq(a\wedge b)\cdot(a\wedge b)
.
Since
a\wedge b\leq a,b
, by monotonicity of
\cdot
it follows that
(a\wedge b)\cdot(a\wedge b)\leq a\cdot b
.
Hence, it follows that
a\wedge b\leq a\cdot b
.

In order to prove (ii), use that
e
is the identity of the monoidal structure of
\mathbf{A}
, that is,
a\cdot e=a
.
By
A4
, it follows that
a\ \cdot\sim a\leq\ \sim e
.
By
A5
,
a\ \cdot\sim a\leq e
.

For (iii), from
a\cdot 1\leq a\cdot 1
by
A4
it follows that
a\ \cdot\sim(a\cdot 1)\leq 0
and then we get our goal by part (i). Finally, using (i) and (ii), and the fact that the monoid reduct of
\mathbf{A}
is commutative, we have that,
(a\ \cdot\sim b)\wedge(b\ \cdot\sim a)\leq(a\ \cdot\sim b)\cdot(b\ \cdot\sim a)=(a\ \cdot\sim a)\cdot(b\ \cdot\sim b)\leq e\cdot e=e
. ∎

Since odd Sugihara monoids are commutative residuated lattices, it follows, by the distributivity of the lattice reducts of the Sugihara monoids and part (iv) of the previous proposition, that the variety of (bounded) odd Sugihara monoids is generated by its totally ordered members (see Section 3 in
[
6
]
for details).

4
A categorical equivalence

This section surveys results from
[
2
]
. There, a categorical equivalence between the categories of bounded odd Sugihara monoids and Gödel algebras is presented.
Restricting this equivalence to one between Boolean algebras and bounded three-valued Sugihara monoids, we elicit the desired abstract characterization of concrete AGC-algebras as bounded three-valued Sugihara monoids.
Furthermore (see Theorem 6.4. in
[
2
]
), we have that any bounded three-valued Sugihara monoid is isomorphic (as bounded Sugihara monoid) to a concrete AGC-algebra, and hence,
\mathsf{B3SM}
is the variety generated by the class of concrete AGC-algebras.
As a moral, we get that abstract AGC-algebras are exactly bounded three-valued Sugihara monoids.

Recall that a
Gödel algebra
is a prelinear Heyting algebra, that is to say, a Heyting algebra satisfying the equation
(x\to y)\vee(y\to x)=1
.
On the other hand, a Boolean algebra may be characterized as a Gödel algebra satisfying
x\vee\neg x=1
.
Boolean algebras form a subvariety of the variety
\mathsf{GA}
of Gödel algebras.
Hence, it is natural to ask for the restriction of a functor from the category of Gödel algebras to the (full) subcategory of Boolean algebras.
Let us now build such a functor, following Section 6 in
[
2
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
{\bf{A}}=(A;\wedge,\vee,\to,0,1)
be a Gödel algebra.
Consider the set
S(\emph{{\bf\emph{{A}}}}):=\{(a,b)\in A\times A:a\vee b=1\}
endowed with the following operations.
For any
(a,b),(c,d)\in S(\emph{{\bf\emph{{A}}}})
, we define:

\sim(a,b):=(b,a)
,

(a,b)\wedge(c,d):=(a\wedge c,b\vee d)
,

(a,b)\vee(c,d):=(a\vee c,b\wedge d)
,

(a,b)\cdot(c,d):=(((a\to d)\wedge(c\to b))\to(a\wedge c),(a\to d)\wedge(c\to b))
.

The algebra
(S(\emph{{\bf\emph{{A}}}});\wedge,\vee,\cdot,\sim,(0,1),(1,1),(1,0))
of type (2,2,2,1,0,0,0) defined above is denoted by
\mathbf{S(A)}
.

The following is a known result.

Proposition 4.2

(Theorem 6.1. of
[
2
]
)
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is a Gödel algebra, then
\mathbf{S(A)}
is a bounded odd Sugihara monoid.

It is known that, in fact,
S
defines a functor from the category of Gödel algebras to the category of bounded odd Sugihara monoids (see
[
2

for details).

On the other hand, let us consider the following map.

.

Let
{\bf{A}}=(A;\wedge,\vee,\cdot,\sim,0,e,1)

Consider the set

{\bf{A^{-}}}
.
Clearly,
\bf{A^{-}}
is a bounded sublattice of

The following is a known result, which is not difficult to check.

Proposition 4.4

(Section 5 in
[

)
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is a bounded odd Sugihara monoid, then
{\bf{A^{-}}}

for details).
Furthermore, there is a categorical equivalence between these categories.

Theorem 4.5

(Theorem 6.6. of
[
2
]
)
.

(\ )^{-}:\mathsf{BOSM}\rightleftarrows\mathsf{GA}:S

Let us now check that the categorical equivalence given in Theorem

restricts to one between
\mathsf{BA}
and
\mathsf{B3SM}

On the one hand, note that, when
\mathbf{A}
is a Boolean algebra, we have that

Hence, in this case, we can simply write

(a,b)\cdot(c,d)=([(a^{\prime}\vee d)\wedge(c^{\prime}\vee b)]^{\prime}\vee(a\wedge c),(a^{\prime}\vee d)\wedge(c^{\prime}\vee b))=(a\wedge c,(b\wedge d)\vee(a\wedge c)^{\prime}).

Hence, we have got the following result.

Proposition 4.6
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is a Boolean algebra, then
\mathbf{S(A)}
is a bounded three-valued Sugihara monoid.

Note that, in fact,
\mathbf{S(A)}
is a concrete AGC-algebra.

On the other hand, let
(A,\wedge,\vee,\cdot,\sim,0,e,1)\in\mathsf{B3SM}

From Definition

and Proposition

\bf{A^{-}}
is a Gödel algebra whose implication is given by

5
Some varieties term-equivalent to
\mathsf{B3SM}

In this section different well-known varieties of algebras of relevance for this work are presented.
We recall some basic properties of the members of these varieties.
Furthermore, since these varieties are shown to be term-equivalent to
\mathsf{B3SM}
, they turn out to be alternative abstract descriptions for AGCs.

Definition 5.1
.

A distributive
double p-algebra
(dp-algebra for short) is an algebra
(A;\wedge,\vee,\neg,D,0,1)
of type (2, 2, 1, 1, 0, 0) such that

(A;\wedge,\vee,0,1)
is a bounded distributive lattice,

for every
a\in A
,
\neg a
is the greatest element
b
of
A
such that
a\wedge b=0
,

for every
a\in A
,
Da
is the smallest element
b
of
A
such that
a\vee b=1
.

It is well known that dp-algebras form a variety, which we denote by
\mathsf{DP}
.

An
(A;\wedge,\vee,\neg,D,0,1)\in\mathsf{DP}
is said to be
regular
if for every
a,b\in A
,
a\wedge Da\leq b\vee\neg b
, where
\leq
stands for the usual lattice order.
Clearly, regular dp-algebras form a subvariety of
\mathsf{DP}
.
As a brief historical remark, let us note that regular dp-algebras were studied by Varlet in
[
10
]
.

Let
(A;\wedge,\vee,\neg,D,0,1)
be a dp-algebra.
An element
a\in A
is said to be
central
if
\neg a=0
and
Da=1
.
A dp-algebra with its universe having a central element is said to be a
centred dp-algebra
(cdp-algebra for short).
It is easily seen that regularity and centrality are independent concepts.
However, in a regular dp-algebra there is at most one central element.

A
three-valued dp-algebra
(3dp-algebra for short) is a regular dp-algebra that satisfies the equations

(S)

\neg x\vee\neg\neg x=1
(Stonean equation),

(cS)

Dx\wedge DDx=0
(coStonean equation).

3dp-algebras form a subvariety of regular dp-algebras, which we denote by
\mathsf{3DP}
.
The variety of c3dp-algebras, which is denoted by
\mathsf{C3DP}
, is an expansion of
\mathsf{3DP}
with the
0
-ary operation

satisfying the equations
\neg c=0
and
Dc=1
.
It is shown in
[
1
]
that
\mathsf{3DP}
is the variety generated by the totally ordered dp-algebra with three elements (
\bot<m<\top
).
Similar arguments show that
\mathsf{C3DP}
is the variety generated by the totally ordered centred dp-algebra with three elements.
Furthermore, c3dp-algebras are functionally complete (straightforwardly from results in

1
]
).

In 1920, Łukasiewicz introduced in
[
5
]
the notion of three-valued logic as the logic given by the logical matrix

is the three-element Wajsberg hoop.
It is well known that bounded Wajsberg hoops are term equivalent to MV-algebras.
Hence,
\textrm{\L}_{3}
may alternatively be regarded as the three-element MV-algebra.

In 1940, Moisil introduced the notion of three-valued Łukasiewicz algebra as an attempt to give an algebraic approach to the three-valued propositional calculus considered by Łukasiewicz in a different signature.
Following Monteiro
[
9

(A;\wedge,\vee,\sim,\nabla,0,1)

An element

Any

The class of all centred

We now state the result announced at the beginning of this section.

Theorem 5.4
.

The varieties
\mathsf{B3SM}
,
\mathsf{C3DP}
, and
\mathsf{C3\L}
are term equivalent.

Proof.

We will make explicit the term equivalences between the different varieties and leave to the reader the straightforward but tedious labour of completing the details of the proof.

Let
(A;\vee,\wedge,\cdot,\sim,0,e,1)
be a bounded three-valued Sugihara monoid.
For any
x\in A
, define unary operations
\neg x:=\ \sim(x\cdot 1)
and
Dx:=\ \sim x\cdot 1
, and the
0
-operation
c:=e
.
Then,
(A;\wedge,\vee,\neg,D,0,c,1)
is a c3dp-algebra.

Conversely, if
(A;\wedge,\vee,\neg,D,0,c,1)
is a c3dp-algebra and we define a binary operation
\cdot
by

x\cdot y:=(x\wedge\neg\neg y)\vee(\neg\neg x\wedge y),

a unary operation
\sim
by

\sim x:=Dx\wedge(x\vee\neg x),

and the
0
-ary operation
e:=c
,
then
(A,\vee,\wedge,\cdot,\sim,0,e,1)
is a bounded three-valued Sugihara monoid.

On the other hand, the term equivalence between
\mathsf{C3DP}
and
\mathsf{C3\L}
is given, in one direction, by
\sim x:=Dx\wedge(x\vee\neg x)
,
\nabla x:=\neg\neg x
, and
z:=c
.
In the other direction, it is given by
\neg x:=\ \sim\nabla x
,
Dx:=\nabla\sim x
, and
c:=z
.
∎

Since term equivalent varieties are equivalent categories, the following corollary is an immediate consequence of Theorem
5.4
and Corollary
4.8
.

Corollary 5.5
.

The varieties
\mathsf{B3SM}
,
\mathsf{C3DP}
,
\mathsf{C3\L}
, and
\mathsf{BA}
are all equivalent as categories.

Remark 5.6
.

In view of Remark
4.9
and Theorem
5.4
, abstract AGC-algebras may be alternatively characterized, in suitable signatures, either as elements of the varieties
\mathsf{B3SM}
,
\mathsf{C3DP}
or
\mathsf{C3\L}
.
Hence, also as centred three-valued bounded Wajsberg hoops or as three-valued MV-algebras.

Furthermore, since any algebra in
\mathsf{C3DP}
is functionally complete, it follows that any AGC-algebra (in any good signature) is also functionally complete.
In particular, the varieties
\mathsf{B3SM}
and
\mathsf{C3\L}
are also functionally complete.

A comparison with recent work

In
[

]
, the set
{C}(B)
of all contracts on a Boolean algebra
\bf{B}
is regarded as a Heyting algebra satisfying Stone equation, that is, equation
\neg x\vee\neg\neg x=1
, and having a minimum dense element
e=(1,1)
.
If we consider the variety
\mathsf{ASA}
of Stone algebras having a minimum dense element (named
augmented Stone algebras
in
[
4
]
),
the assignment
\bf{B}\mapsto\bf{{C}(B})
defines a functor
\bf{C}:\mathsf{BA}\to\mathsf{ASA}
.
In
[
4
]
it is shown that
\bf{C}
is part of an adjoint pair
\bf{C}\dashv\mathrm{\bf{Clos}}
, where
\mathrm{\bf{Clos}}(\bf{A})
is the Stonean subalgebra of an augmented Stone algebra
\bf{A}
formed by all its complemented elements.

It is not hard to see that in any pseudo-complemented bounded distributive lattice
(A;\wedge,\vee,\neg,0,1)
having a minimum dense element
e

[e)=\{a\in A:a\leq e\}

N

Na:=\neg a\wedge e

\mathrm{\bf{Clos}}(A)
.
Furthermore, in
[
4
]

(\ )^{+}:\mathsf{ASA}\to\mathsf{BA}

A\mapsto[e)
instead of
\mathrm{\bf{Clos}}

\bf{C}\dashv(\ )^{+}

\bf{C}(B)
