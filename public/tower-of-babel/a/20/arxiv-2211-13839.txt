Bivariate log-symmetric models: distributional properties, parameter estimation and an application to fatigue data analysis
Roberto Vila, Narayanaswamy Balakrishnan, Helton Saulo, Ana Protazio

Abstract
The bivariate Gaussian distribution has been a key model for many developments in statistics. However, many real-world phenomena generate data that follow asymmetric distributions, and consequently bivariate normal model is inappropriate in such situations. Bidimensional log-symmetric models have attractive properties and can be considered as good alternatives in these cases. In this paper, we discuss bivariate log-symmetric distributions and their characterizations. We establish several distributional properties and obtain the maximum likelihood estimators of the model parameters. A Monte Carlo simulation study is performed for examining the performance of the developed parameter estimation method. A real data set is finally analyzed to illustrate the proposed model and the associated inferential method.

1
Introduction

A distribution is said to be log-symmetric when the corresponding random variable and its reciprocal have the same distribution
(
Jones, 2008
, see)
. A characterization of distributions of this type can be constructed by taking the exponential function of a symmetric random variable. Hence, log-symmetric distributions may be used to describe strictly positive data. The class of log-symmetric distributions is quite broad and includes a large portion of bimodal distributions and those with lighter or heavier tails than the log-normal distribution; see
Vanegas and Paula, 2016
. Some examples of log-symmetric distributions are log-normal, log-Student-
t
, log-logistic, log-Laplace, log-power-exponential, log-slash, etc; see
Crow and Shimizu, 1988
,
Jones, 2008
, and
Vanegas and Paula, 2016
, for pertinent details.

Another important feature of the log-symmetric class is that they are closed under scale change and under reciprocity, according to
Puig, 2008
, which are also desirable properties for distributions that are used to describe strictly positive data. Further, log-symmetric models allow one to model the median or the asymmetry (relative dispersion).

In addition, the log-symmetric class has many other desirable statistical properties. For example, the two parameters of the log-symmetric distribution are orthogonal and they can be interpreted directly as median and skewness (or relative dispersion, taking into account two parameters that are interpreted as measures of position and scale, as stated by
Vanegas and Paula, 2016
, which are, in the context of asymmetric distributions, the ones that mean the most, being complete measures of location and shape, respectively.

The main objective of this work is to extend in a natural way the definition of univariate log-symmetric distributions to the bivariate case, to
study their main statistical properties, to develop maximum likelihood (ML) method for the estimation of model parameters, and finally to show its applicability to the analysis of fatigue data.

The rest of this work is organized as follows. In Section
2
, the bivariate log-symmetric (BLS) model is proposed. In Section
3
, some mathematical properties such as stochastic representation, quantile function, conditional distribution, Mahalanobis distance, independence, moments, correlation function, among others, are discussed.
In Section
4
, we describe the ML method for the estimation of the BLS model parameters.
In Section
5
, we perform a Monte Carlo simulation study for evaluating the performance of the ML estimators.
In Section
6
, we apply the BLS models to a data set on material fatigue to demonstrate the practical utility of the BLS models. Finally, in Section
7
, some concluding remarks are made.

Bivariate log-symmetric model

A continuous random vector
\bm{T}=(T_{1},T_{2})
is said to follow a bivariate log-symmetric (BLS) distribution if its joint probability density function (PDF) is given by

\displaystyle f_{T_{1},T_{2}}(t_{1},t_{2};\bm{\theta})={1\over t_{1}t_{2}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{g_{c}}}\,g_{c}\Biggl({\widetilde{t_{1}}^{2}-2\rho\widetilde{t_{1}}\widetilde{t_{2}}+\widetilde{t_{2}}^{2}\over 1-\rho^{2}}\Biggr),\quad t_{1},t_{2}>0,

(2.1)

where

\widetilde{t_{i}}=\log\biggl[\Bigl({t_{i}\over\eta_{i}}\Bigr)^{1/\sigma_{i}}\biggr],\ \eta_{i}=\exp(\mu_{i}),\ i=1,2,

with
\bm{\theta}=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\rho)
being the parameter vector,
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma_{i}>0
,
i=1,2
;
\rho\in(-1,1)
;
Z_{g_{c}}>0
is the partition function given by

\displaystyle Z_{g_{c}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}{1\over t_{1}t_{2}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\,g_{c}\Biggl({\widetilde{t_{1}}^{2}-2\rho\widetilde{t_{1}}\widetilde{t_{2}}+\widetilde{t_{2}}^{2}\over 1-\rho^{2}}\Biggr)\,{\rm d}t_{1}{\rm d}t_{2},

(2.2)

and
g_{c}
is a scalar function, referred to as the density generator; see
Fan et al., 1990
.
We use, in this case, the notation
\bm{T}\sim{\rm BLS}(\bm{\theta},g_{c})
.
In this paper, we prove that, when it exists, the variance-covariance matrix of a random vector

, denoted by

, is a matrix function of the following dispersion matrix
(see Subsections

3.8

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}^{2}&\rho\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\\
\rho\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}&\sigma_{2}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

From (

), it is clear that the random vector
\bm{X}=(X_{1},X_{2})

,

\bm{X}
is

\displaystyle f_{X_{1},X_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2};\bm{\theta}_{*})={1\over\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{g_{c}}}\,g_{c}\Biggl({\widetilde{x_{1}}^{2}-2\rho\widetilde{x_{1}}\widetilde{x_{2}}+\widetilde{x_{2}}^{2}\over 1-\rho^{2}}\Biggr),\quad-\infty<x_{1},x_{2}<\infty,

\bm{\theta}_{*}=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\rho)

is the partition function given in (
2.2
). In this case, we shall use the notation
\bm{X}\sim{\rm BES}(\bm{\theta}_{*},g_{c})
.

A simple calculation shows that the joint cumulative distribution function (CDF) of
\bm{T}\sim{\rm BLS}(\bm{\theta},g_{c})
, denoted by
F_{T_{1},T_{2}}(t_{1},t_{2};\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle F_{T_{1},T_{2}}(t_{1},t_{2};\bm{\theta})=F_{X_{1},X_{2}}\big(\log(t_{1}),\log(t_{2});\bm{\theta}_{*}\big),

\bm{X}\sim{\rm BES}(\bm{\theta}_{*},g_{c})

\bm{X}

3
Some basic properties

In this section, some distributional properties of the proposed bivariate log-symmetric distributions are discussed.

3.1
Characterization of the partition function
Z_{g_{c}}

Proposition 3.1
.

The partition function
Z_{g_{c}}
given in (
2.2
) is independent of the parameter vector
\bm{\theta}
. To be specific, we have

\displaystyle Z_{g_{c}}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_{c}\big({z_{1}}^{2}+{z_{2}}^{2}\big)\,{\rm d}{z_{1}}{\rm d}{z_{2}}=\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{c}(u)\,{\rm d}{u}.

Proof.

The proof of the first identity follows by considering in (
2.2
) the following change of variables through the Jacobian method:

\displaystyle z_{1}=\widetilde{t}_{1},\quad z_{2}={\widetilde{t}_{2}-\rho\widetilde{t}_{1}\over\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}},

where
\widetilde{t}_{i}
,
i=1,2
, are as defined in (
2.1
).

The proof of the second identity follows by using
integration in polar coordinates:
z_{1}=r\cos(\theta)
,
z_{2}=r\sin(\theta)
, with
r\geqslant 0
and
0\leqslant\theta\leqslant 2\pi
, and then the change of variables
u=r^{2}
,
{\rm d}u=2r{\rm d}r
. As this is easy to establish, we omit its proof here.
∎

3.2
Stochastic representation

Proposition 3.2
.

The random vector
\bm{T}=(T_{1},T_{2})
has a BLS distribution if

\displaystyle T_{1}=\eta_{1}\exp(\sigma_{1}Z_{1}),

\displaystyle T_{2}=\eta_{2}\exp\big(\sigma_{2}{\rho}Z_{1}+\sigma_{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2}\big),

where
Z_{1}=RDU_{1}
and
Z_{2}=R\sqrt{1-D^{2}}U_{2}
;

U_{1}
,
U_{2}
,
R
, and
D
are mutually independent random variables,
\rho\in(-1,1)
,
\eta_{i}=\exp(\mu_{i})
, and
\mathbb{P}(U_{i}=-1)=\mathbb{P}(U_{i}=1)=1/2
,
i=1,2
. The random variable
D
is positive and has its PDF as

\displaystyle f_{D}(d)={2\over\pi\sqrt{1-d^{2}}},\quad d\in(0,1).

Moreover, the positive random variable
R
is called the generator of the elliptical random vector
\bm{X}=(X_{1},X_{2})
.
In other words,
R
has is PDF as

\displaystyle f_{R}(r)={2rg_{c}(r^{2})\over\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{c}(u)\,{\rm d}{u}},\quad r>0.

Proof.

It is well-known that
(
Abdous et al, 2005
, see)
the vector
\bm{X}
has a BES distribution if

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{llll}&X_{1}=\mu_{1}+\sigma_{1}Z_{1},\\[5.69046pt]
&X_{2}=\mu_{2}+\sigma_{2}{\rho}Z_{1}+\sigma_{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2}.\end{array}

As
X_{i}=\log(T_{i})
,
i=1,2
, the proof readily follows.
∎

3.3
Marginal quantiles

Let
\bm{T}=(T_{1},T_{2})\sim{\rm BLS}(\bm{\theta},g_{c})
and
p\in(0,1)
.
By using the stochastic representation of Proposition
3.2
, we readily obtain

\displaystyle p=\mathbb{P}(T_{1}\leqslant Q_{T_{1}})=\mathbb{P}\big(\eta_{1}\exp(\sigma_{1}Z_{1})\leqslant Q_{T_{1}}\big)=\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{1}\leqslant\log\biggl[\Bigl({Q_{T_{1}}\over\eta_{1}}\Bigl)^{1/\sigma_{1}}\biggl]\right)

and

\displaystyle p=\mathbb{P}(T_{2}\leqslant Q_{T_{2}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(\eta_{2}\exp(\sigma_{2}{\rho}Z_{1}+\sigma_{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2})\leqslant Q_{T_{2}}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left({\rho}Z_{1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2}\leqslant\log\biggl[\Bigl({Q_{T_{2}}\over\eta_{2}}\Bigl)^{1/\sigma_{2}}\biggl]\right).

Hence, the
p
-th quantile of
T_{1}
and the
p
-th quantile of
T_{2}
are given by

\displaystyle\log\biggl[\Bigl({Q_{T_{1}}\over\eta_{1}}\Bigl)^{1/\sigma_{1}}\biggl]=Q_{Z_{1}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Q_{T_{1}}=\eta_{1}\exp(\sigma_{1}Q_{Z_{1}})

and

\displaystyle\log\biggl[\Bigl({Q_{T_{2}}\over\eta_{2}}\Bigl)^{1/\sigma_{2}}\biggl]=Q_{{\rho}Z_{1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Q_{T_{2}}=\eta_{2}\exp(\sigma_{2}Q_{{\rho}Z_{1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2}}),

respectively.

3.4
Conditional distribution

Proposition 3.3
.

The joint PDF of
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
, given in Proposition
3.2
, is

\displaystyle f_{Z_{1},Z_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})={g_{c}(z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2})\over\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{c}(u)\,{\rm d}{u}},\quad-\infty<z_{1},z_{2}<\infty.

Moreover, the marginal PDFs of
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
, denoted by
f_{Z{{}_{1}}}
and
f_{Z{{}_{2}}}
, respectively, are

\displaystyle f_{Z{{}_{1}}}(z_{1})={{\displaystyle\int_{|z_{1}|}^{\infty}}{2g_{c}(w^{2})\over\sqrt{1-{z_{1}^{2}\over w^{2}}}}\,{\rm d}{w}\over\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{c}(u)\,{\rm d}{u}}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{Z{{}_{2}}}(z_{2})={{\displaystyle\int_{|z_{2}|}^{\infty}}{2g_{c}(w^{2})\over\sqrt{1-{z_{2}^{2}\over w^{2}}}}\,{\rm d}{w}\over\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{c}(u)\,{\rm d}{u}},\quad-\infty<z_{1},z_{2}<\infty.

Then,
f_{Z_{i}}(z_{i}\,|\,Z_{j}=z_{j})
(
i\neq j
) and
f_{Z_{i}}(z_{i})
,
i,j=1,2
, are both even functions.

Proof.

For more details on the proof, see Propositions 3.1 and 3.2 of reference
Saulo et al., 2022b
.
∎

Let
X
and
Y
be two continuous random variables with joint PDF
f_{X,Y}
, and marginal PDFs
f_{X}
and
f_{Y}
, respectively.

•

Let
B
be a Borel subset of
\mathbb{R}
.
The conditional CDF of
X
, given
\{Y\in B\}
, denoted by
{F}_{X}(x\,|\,Y\in B)
, is defined as (for every
x
)

\displaystyle{F}_{X}(x\,|\,Y\in B)=\mathbb{P}(X\leqslant x\,|\,Y\in B)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}{f}_{X}(u\,|\,Y\in B)\,{\rm d}u\quad\text{if}\ \mathbb{P}(Y\in B)>0,

(3.3)

where
{f}_{X}(u\,|\,Y\in B)
is the corresponding conditional PDF given by

\displaystyle{f}_{X}(u\,|\,Y\in B)=\frac{\int_{B}{f}_{X,Y}(u,v)\,{\rm d}v}{\mathbb{P}(Y\in B)}.

We use
X\,|\,Y\in B
to denote the random variable
X
having the conditional CDF in (
3.3
), given
\{Y\in B\}
;

•

Let
\varepsilon>0
, and further
\mathbb{P}(y-\varepsilon<Y\leqslant y+\varepsilon)>0
. Then, we define the conditional CDF of
X
, given
Y=y
, denoted by
{F}_{X}(x\,|\,Y=y)
, as (for every
x
)

\displaystyle{F}_{X}(x|Y=y)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathbb{P}(X\leqslant x\,|\,y-\varepsilon<Y\leqslant y+\varepsilon),

provided that the limit exists.
If the limit exists, there is a nonnegative function
{f}_{X}(u|Y=y)
(called the conditional PDF) so that (for every
x
)

\displaystyle{F}_{X}(x\,|\,Y=y)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}{f}_{X}(u\,|\,Y=y)\,{\rm d}u.

At every point
(x,y)
at which
f_{X,Y}
is continuous and
f_{Y}(y)>0
is continuous, the PDF
{f}_{X}(u\,|\,Y=y)
exists and is given by (see Theorem 6, p. 109, of
Rohatgi et al., 2015
)

\displaystyle{f}_{X}(u\,|\,Y=y)={f_{X,Y}(u,y)\over f_{Y}(y)}.

For simplicity, we shall denote it by
X\,|\,Y=y
.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
\bm{T}=(T_{1},T_{2})\sim{\rm BLS}(\bm{\theta},g_{c})
, then
the PDF of
T_{2}|T_{1}=t_{1}
is given by

\displaystyle f_{T_{2}}(t_{2}\,|\,T_{1}=t_{1})={1\over t_{2}\sigma_{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\,{f_{Z_{2}}\biggl({1\over\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\,\widetilde{t}_{2}-{\rho\over\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\,\widetilde{t}_{1}\,\bigg|\,Z_{1}=\widetilde{t}_{1}\biggr)},

(3.4)

where
\widetilde{t}_{i}
,
i=1,2
, are as defined in (
2.1
), and
{Z_{1}}
and
{Z_{2}}
are as in Proposition
3.2
.

Proof.

If
T_{1}=t_{1}
, then
Z_{1}=\log\big[(t_{1}/\eta_{1})^{1/\sigma_{1}}\big]=\widetilde{t}_{1}
. Thus, the conditional distribution of
T_{2}
, given

, is the same as the distribution of

\displaystyle\eta_{2}\exp\big(\sigma_{2}{\rho}\widetilde{t}_{1}+\sigma_{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2}\big)\,\big|\,T_{1}=t_{1}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle F_{T_{2}}(t_{2}\,|\,T_{1}=t_{1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(\eta_{2}\exp(\sigma_{2}{\rho}\widetilde{t}_{1}+\sigma_{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2})\leqslant t_{2}\,\big|\,T_{1}=t_{1}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\biggl(Z_{2}\leqslant{1\over\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\,\widetilde{t}_{2}-{{\rho}\over\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\,\widetilde{t}_{1}\,\bigg|\,Z_{1}=\widetilde{t}_{1}\biggr).

Then, the conditional PDF of
T_{2}
, given

, follows readily.

Theorem 3.5
.

For a Borel subset
B
of
(0,\infty)
, we define the following Borel set:

\displaystyle{B}_{\rho}={1\over\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\,\log\biggl[\Bigl({B\over\eta_{2}}\Bigr)^{1/\sigma_{2}}\biggr]-{\rho\over\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\,\widetilde{t_{1}},

(3.5)

where
\widetilde{t}_{1}
is as in (
2.1
).
If
\bm{T}=(T_{1},T_{2})\sim{\rm BLS}(\bm{\theta},g_{c})
, then the PDF of
T_{1}\,|\,T_{2}\in B
is given by

\displaystyle f_{T_{1}}(t_{1}|T_{2}\in B)={1\over t_{1}\sigma_{1}}\,f_{Z_{1}}(\widetilde{t}_{1})\,{\int_{{B}_{\rho}}f_{Z_{2}}(w\,|\,Z_{1}=\widetilde{t}_{1})\,{\rm d}w\over\mathbb{P}(\rho Z_{1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2}\in{B}_{0})},

with
{Z_{1}}
and
{Z_{2}}
as in Proposition
3.2
.

Proof.

Let
B
be a Borel subset of
(0,\infty)
. Note that

\displaystyle f_{T_{1}}(t_{1}\,|\,T_{2}\in B)=f_{T_{1}}(t_{1})\,{\int_{B}f_{T_{2}}(t_{2}\,|\,T_{1}=t_{1})\,{\rm d}t_{2}\over\mathbb{P}(T_{2}\in B)}.

As
f_{T_{1}}(t_{1})=f_{Z_{1}}(\widetilde{t}_{1})/(\sigma_{1}t_{1})
and
\mathbb{P}(T_{2}\in B)=\mathbb{P}(\rho Z_{1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2}\in{B}_{0})
, where
{B}_{0}
is as given in (
3.5
) with
\rho=0
, the term on the right-hand side of the above identity equals

\displaystyle{1\over\sigma_{1}t_{1}}\,f_{Z_{1}}(\widetilde{t}_{1})\,{\int_{B}f_{T_{2}}(t_{2}\,|\,T_{1}=t_{1})\,{\rm d}t_{2}\over\mathbb{P}(\rho Z_{1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2}\in{B}_{0})}.

By using the expression for

provided in Lemma

, the above expression equals

\displaystyle{1\over t_{1}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\,f_{Z_{1}}(\widetilde{t}_{1})\,{\int_{B}{1\over t_{2}}\,f_{Z_{2}}\Big({1\over\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\,\widetilde{t}_{2}-{\rho\over\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\,\widetilde{t}_{1}\,\Big|\,Z_{1}=\widetilde{t}_{1}\Big)\,{\rm d}t_{2}\over\mathbb{P}(\rho Z_{1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2}\in{B}_{0})},

where

,

, are as in (

).
Finally, by applying the change of variable

\displaystyle{1\over t_{1}\sigma_{1}}\,f_{Z_{1}}(\widetilde{t}_{1})\,{\int_{{B}_{\rho}}f_{Z_{2}}(w\,|\,Z_{1}=\widetilde{t}_{1})\,{\rm d}w\over\mathbb{P}(\rho Z_{1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2}\in{B}_{0})},

in which
{B}_{\rho}
is as in (
3.5
).
This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Corollary 3.6

(Gaussian generator)
.

Let
\bm{T}=(T_{1},T_{2})\sim{\rm BLS}(\bm{\theta},g_{c})
and
g_{c}(x)=\exp(-x/2)
be the generator of the bivariate log-normal distribution. Then, for each Borel subset
B
of
(0,\infty)
, the PDF of
T_{1}\,|\,T_{2}\in B
is given by (for
t_{1}>0
)

\displaystyle f_{T_{1}}(t_{1}|T_{2}\in B)={1\over t_{1}\sigma_{1}}\,\phi\Bigl(\log\Bigl[\Big({t_{1}\over\eta_{1}}\Big)^{1/\sigma_{1}}\Bigr]\Bigr)\,\dfrac{\Phi\Bigl({1\over\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\Big\{\log\Bigl[\big({B\over\eta_{2}}\big)^{1/\sigma_{2}}\Bigr]-\rho\log\Big[\big({t_{1}\over\eta_{1}}\big)^{1/\sigma_{1}}\Big]\Big\}\Bigr)}{\Phi\Big(\log\Big[\big(\frac{B}{\eta_{2}}\big)^{1/\sigma_{2}}\Big]\Big)},

where we use the notation
\Phi(C)=\int_{C}\phi(x){\rm d}x
, with
\phi(x)=g_{c}(x^{2})/\sqrt{2\pi}
.

Proof.

It is well-known that the bivariate normal distribution admits a stochastic representation as in (
3.2
), where
Z_{1}\sim N(0,1)
and
Z_{2}\sim N(0,1)
are independent. Consequently,

and

.
Further, a simple algebraic manipulation shows that

\displaystyle\int_{{B}_{\rho}}f_{Z_{2}}(w\,|\,Z_{1}=\widetilde{t}_{1})\,{\rm d}w=\Phi(B_{\rho}),

where
B_{\rho}
is the Borel set defined in (
3.5
).
Then, by using Theorem
3.5
, the required result follows.
∎

Corollary 3.7

(Student-

.

Let
\bm{T}=(T_{1},T_{2})\sim{\rm BLS}(\bm{\theta},g_{c})
and
g_{c}(x)=(1+(x/\nu))^{-(\nu+2)/2}
,
\nu>0
, be the generator of the bivariate log-Student-
t
distribution with
\nu
degrees of freedom. Then, for each Borel subset
B
of
(0,\infty)
, the PDF of
T_{1}|T_{2}\in B
is given by (for
t_{1}>0
)

f_{T_{1}}(t_{1}|T_{2}\in B)={1\over t_{1}\sigma_{1}}\,f_{\nu}\Bigl(\log\Bigl[\big({t_{1}\over\eta_{1}}\big)^{1/\sigma_{1}}\Bigr]\Bigr)\,\dfrac{F_{\nu+1}\Big(\sqrt{\nu+1\over\nu+\widetilde{t_{1}}^{2}}\,{1\over\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\Big\{\log\Bigl[\big({B\over\eta_{2}}\big)^{1/\sigma_{2}}\Bigr]-\rho\log\Big[\big({t_{1}\over\eta_{1}}\big)^{1/\sigma_{1}}\Big]\Big\}\Big)}{F_{\nu}\Big(\log\Big[\big(\frac{B}{\eta_{2}}\big)^{1/\sigma_{2}}\Big]\Big)},

where
F_{\nu}(C)=\int_{C}f_{\nu}(x){\rm d}x
, with
f_{\nu}(x)
being the standard Student-
t
PDF.

Proof.

It is well-known that the bivariate Student-
t
distribution has a stochastic representation as in (
3.2
), where
Z_{1}=Z_{1}^{*}\sqrt{\nu/Q}\sim t_{\nu}
and
Z_{2}=Z_{2}^{*}\sqrt{\nu/Q}\sim t_{\nu}
,
Q\sim\chi^{2}_{\nu}
(chi-square with
\nu
degrees of freedom) is independent of
Z_{1}^{*}
and
{\rho}Z_{1}^{*}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2}^{*}
; here,
Z_{1}^{*}
and
Z_{2}^{*}
are independent and identically distributed standard normal random variables.

As
\rho Z_{1}^{*}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2}^{*}\sim N(0,1)
, we have

\rho Z_{1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2}=(\rho Z_{1}^{*}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2}^{*})\sqrt{\nu/Q}\sim t_{\nu}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\rho Z_{1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2}\in{B}_{0}\big)=F_{\nu}({B}_{0}).

On the other hand, if
\bm{X}=(X_{1},X_{2})\sim{\rm BES}(\bm{\theta}_{*},g_{c})
, by Remark 3.7 of
Saulo et al., 2022b
, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{{\nu+1\over(\nu+r^{2})(1-\rho^{2})}}\biggl({X_{2}-\mu_{2}\over\sigma_{2}}-\rho r\biggr)\,\Bigg|\,{X_{1}-\mu_{1}\over\sigma_{1}}=r\sim t_{\nu+1}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{\nu+1}\leqslant x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{{\nu+1\over(\nu+r^{2})(1-\rho^{2})}}\biggl({X_{2}-\mu_{2}\over\sigma_{2}}-\rho r\biggr)\leqslant x\,\Bigg|\,{X_{1}-\mu_{1}\over\sigma_{1}}=r\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{2}\leqslant\sqrt{{\nu+r^{2}\over\nu+1}}\,x\,\Bigg|\,Z_{1}=r\right),\quad T_{\nu+1}\sim t_{\nu+1}.

By taking
x=\sqrt{(\nu+1)/(\nu+r^{2})}\,w
with
r=\widetilde{t}_{1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Biggl(T_{\nu+1}\leqslant\sqrt{\nu+1\over\nu+\widetilde{t_{1}}^{2}}\,w\Biggr)=\mathbb{P}(Z_{2}\leqslant w\,|\,Z_{1}=\widetilde{t}_{1}).

By differentiating the above identity with respect to
w
, we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{\nu+1\over\nu+\widetilde{t_{1}}^{2}}\,f_{\nu+1}\Biggl(\sqrt{\nu+1\over\nu+\widetilde{t_{1}}^{2}}\,w\Biggr)=f_{Z_{2}}(w\,|\,Z_{1}=\widetilde{t}_{1}).

Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{{B}_{\rho}}f_{Z_{2}}(w\,|\,Z_{1}=\widetilde{t}_{1})\,{\rm d}w=\sqrt{\nu+1\over\nu+\widetilde{t_{1}}^{2}}\int_{{B}_{\rho}}f_{\nu+1}\Biggl(\sqrt{\nu+1\over\nu+\widetilde{t_{1}}^{2}}\,w\Biggr)\,{\rm d}w=F_{\nu+1}\left(\sqrt{\nu+1\over\nu+\widetilde{t_{1}}^{2}}\,{B}_{\rho}\right).

Now, by using Theorem
3.5
, the required result follows.
∎

3.5
Mahalanobis Distance

The squared Mahalanobis distance of a random vector
\bm{T}=(T_{1},T_{2})
and the vector
\log(\bm{\eta})=(\log(\eta_{1}),\log(\eta_{2}))
of a bivariate log-symmetric distribution is defined as

\displaystyle d^{2}(\bm{T},\log(\bm{\eta}))={\widetilde{T_{1}}^{2}-2\rho\widetilde{T_{1}}\widetilde{T_{2}}+\widetilde{T_{2}}^{2}\over 1-\rho^{2}},

where

\widetilde{T_{i}}=\log\biggl[\biggl({T_{i}\over\eta_{i}}\biggr)^{1/\sigma_{i}}\biggr],\ \eta_{i}=\exp(\mu_{i}),\ i=1,2.

We now derive formulas for the CDF and PDF of the above random variable
d^{2}(\bm{T},\log(\bm{\eta}))
.

Proposition 3.8
.

If
\bm{T}\sim{\rm BLS}(\bm{\theta},g_{c})
, then the CDF of
d^{2}(\bm{T},\log(\bm{\eta}))
, denoted by
F_{d^{2}(\bm{T},\log(\bm{\eta}))}
, is given by

\displaystyle F_{d^{2}(\bm{T},\log(\bm{\eta}))}(x)

\displaystyle=4\int_{0}^{\sqrt{x}}\biggl[F_{Z_{2}}\Big(\sqrt{x-z_{1}^{2}}\,\Big|\,Z_{1}=z_{1}\Big)-{1\over 2}\biggr]f_{Z_{1}}(z_{1})\,{\rm d}z_{1}\,\bm{\cdot}\mathds{1}_{(0,\infty)}(x)

(3.6)

\displaystyle={4\over Z_{g_{c}}}\,\int_{0}^{\sqrt{x}}\left[\int_{0}^{\sqrt{x-z_{1}^{2}}}g_{c}(z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2})\,{\rm d}z_{2}\right]{\rm d}z_{1}\,\bm{\cdot}\mathds{1}_{(0,\infty)}(x),

(3.7)

where
Z_{g_{c}}
is as in Proposition
3.1
.

Proof.

As
\bm{T}=(T_{1},T_{2})
admits the stochastic representation in Proposition
3.2
, there exist
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
such that
\widetilde{T_{1}}=Z_{1}
and
\widetilde{T_{2}}={\rho}Z_{1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{2}
. Then, a simple algebraic manipulation shows that

\displaystyle d^{2}(\bm{T},\log(\bm{\eta}))=Z_{1}^{2}+Z_{2}^{2}.

(3.8)

Upon using the law of total expectation, we get (for
x>0
)

\displaystyle F_{d^{2}(\bm{T},\log(\bm{\eta}))}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}(\mathds{1}_{\{Z_{1}^{2}+Z_{2}^{2}\leqslant x\}}\,|\,Z_{1})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\Big(\mathds{1}_{\{|Z_{2}|\leqslant\sqrt{x-Z_{1}^{2}}\}}\,\Big|\,Z_{1}\mathds{1}_{\{|Z_{1}|\leqslant\sqrt{x}\}}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\sqrt{x}}^{\sqrt{x}}\left[\int_{-\sqrt{x-z_{1}^{2}}}^{\sqrt{x-z_{1}^{2}}}f_{Z_{2}}(z_{2}\,|\,Z_{1}=z_{1})\,{\rm d}z_{2}\right]f_{Z_{1}}(z_{1})\,{\rm d}z_{1}.

(3.9)

As
f_{Z_{2}}(z_{2}\,|\,Z_{1}=z_{1})
and
f_{Z_{1}}(z_{1})
are both even functions (see Proposition
3.3
), the proof of the first equality in (
3.6
) follows from (
3.9
). The second equality in (
3.7
) follows by using the joint PDF
f_{Z_{1},Z_{2}}
given in Proposition
3.3
in (
3.9
).
∎

Proposition 3.9
.

If
\bm{T}\sim{\rm BLS}(\bm{\theta},g_{c})
, then the PDF of
d^{2}(\bm{T},\log(\bm{\eta}))
, denoted by
f_{d^{2}(\bm{T},\log(\bm{\eta}))}
, is given by

\displaystyle f_{d^{2}(\bm{T},\log(\bm{\eta}))}(x)={\pi\over Z_{g_{c}}}\,g_{c}(x),\quad x>0,

where
Z_{g_{c}}
is as in Proposition
3.1
.

Proof.

The proof follows immediately by differentiating (
3.7
) with respect to
x
and then by using the following well-known Leibniz integral rule:

\displaystyle{{\rm d}\over{\rm d}x}\int_{a(x)}^{b(x)}h(x,y)\,{\rm d}y=h(x,b(x))b^{\prime}(x)-h(x,a(x))a^{\prime}(x)+\int_{a(x)}^{b(x)}{\partial h(x,y)\over\partial x}\,{\rm d}y.

∎

3.7
Moments

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
\bm{X}=(X_{1},X_{2})\sim{\rm BES}(\bm{\theta}_{*},g_{c})
and
\bm{T}=(T_{1},T_{2})\sim{\rm BLS}(\bm{\theta},g_{c})
.
If the moment-generating function (MGF) of
X_{i}
, denoted by
M_{X_{i}}(s_{i})
,
i=1,2
, exists, then the moments of
T_{i}
are

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(T_{i}^{r})=\eta_{i}^{r}\vartheta(\sigma_{i}^{2}r^{2}),\quad\text{with}\ \eta_{i}=\exp(\mu_{i}),\ i=1,2,\ r\in\mathbb{R},

for some scalar function
\vartheta
, which is called the characteristic generator
(
Fan et al., 1990
, see)
.

For example, when
g_{c}(x)=\exp(-x/2)
(Gaussian generator),
\vartheta(x)=\exp(x/2)
, and when
g_{c}(x)=(1+(x/\nu))^{-(\nu+2)/2}
,
\nu>0
(Student-
t
generator),
\vartheta
does not exist.

Proof.

We only show the case for
i=1
, because the other one follows in a similar way.

As the random variable
X_{1}
has a MGF
M_{X_{1}}(s_{1})
, the domain of the characteristic function
\varphi_{X_{1}}(t)
can be extended to the complex plane, and

\displaystyle M_{X_{1}}(s_{1})=\varphi_{X_{1}}(-is_{1}).

As
X_{1}=\log(T_{1})
, by using the above identity, we immediately get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(T_{1}^{r})=\mathbb{E}[\exp(rX_{1})]=M_{X_{1}}(r)

\displaystyle=\exp(r\mu_{1})M_{S_{1}}(\sigma_{1}r)

\displaystyle=\exp(r\mu_{1})\varphi_{S_{1}}(-i\sigma_{1}r)=\exp(r\mu_{1})\varphi_{S_{1},S_{2}}(-i\sigma_{1}r,0),

(3.11)

with
(S_{1},S_{2})\sim{\rm BES}(\bm{\theta}_{*_{0}},g_{c})
,
\bm{\theta}_{*_{0}}=(0,0,1,1,\rho)
, and
\varphi_{S_{1},S_{2}}(s_{1},0)

is the marginal characteristic function. On the other hand, the characteristic function of the BES distribution is given by
(
Balakrishnan and Lai, 2009

\displaystyle\varphi_{S_{1},S_{2}}(s_{1},s_{2})=\vartheta(s_{1}^{2}+2\rho s_{1}s_{2}+s_{2}^{2}),

where

Finally, by using (

3.9
Some other properties

If
\bm{T}=(T_{1},T_{2})\sim{\rm BLS}(\bm{\theta},g_{c})
, then the following properties follow immediately as a consequence of the definition of the BLS distribution, analogous to the properties stated by
Vanegas and Paula, 2016
:

(P1)

The CDF of
\bm{T}
is given by

F_{T_{1},T_{2}}(t_{1},t_{2};\bm{\theta})=F_{S_{1},S_{2}}(\widetilde{t}_{1},\widetilde{t}_{2};\bm{\theta}_{*_{0}})
, with
(S_{1},S_{2})\sim{\rm BES}(\bm{\theta}_{*_{0}},g_{c})
and
\bm{\theta}_{*_{0}}=(0,0,1,1,\rho)
;

(P2)

The random vector
(T_{1}^{*},T_{2}^{*})=([T_{1}/\eta_{1}]^{1/\sigma_{1}},[T_{2}/\eta_{2}]^{1/\sigma_{2}})
follows standard BLS distribution. In other words,
(T_{1}^{*},T_{2}^{*})\sim{\rm BLS}(\bm{\theta}_{0},g_{c})
with
\bm{\theta}_{0}=(1,1,1,1,\rho)
;

(P3)

(c_{1}T_{1},c_{2}T_{2})\sim{\rm BLS}(c_{1}\eta_{1},c_{2}\eta_{2},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},g_{c})
for all constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
;

(P4)

(T_{1}^{c_{1}},T_{2}^{c_{2}})\sim{\rm BLS}(\eta_{1}^{c_{1}},\eta_{2}^{c_{2}},c_{1}^{2}\sigma_{1},c_{2}^{2}\sigma_{2},g_{c})
for all constants
c_{1}\neq 0
and
c_{2}\neq 0
.

Proposition 3.14
.

If
\bm{T}=(T_{1},T_{2})\sim{\rm BLS}(\bm{\theta},g_{c})
, then
the random vectors
({\eta_{1}/T_{1}},{\eta_{2}/T_{2}})
and
({T_{1}/\eta_{1}},{T_{2}/\eta_{2}})
are identically distributed. Furthermore,

({\eta_{1}/T_{1}},{\eta_{2}/T_{2}})\sim{\rm BLS}(\bm{\theta}_{\bullet},g_{c})
and
({T_{1}/\eta_{1}},{T_{2}/\eta_{2}})\sim{\rm BLS}(\bm{\theta}_{\bullet},g_{c})
, with
\bm{\theta}_{\bullet}=(1,1,\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\rho)
.

Proof.

By using the well-known identity that
(
James, 2004
, see e.g. p. 59 of)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(a_{1}<X\leqslant b_{1},a_{2}<Y\leqslant b_{2})=F_{X,Y}(b_{1},b_{2})-F_{X,Y}(b_{1},a_{2})-F_{X,Y}(a_{1},b_{2})+F_{X,Y}(a_{1},a_{2})

for any two random variables
X
and
Y
and for all
a_{1}<b_{1}
and
a_{2}<b_{2}
, with
a_{1}=\eta_{1}/w_{1}
,
b_{1}=\infty
,
a_{2}=\eta_{2}/w_{2}
and
b_{2}=\infty
, for all
(w_{1},w_{2})\in(0,\infty)^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big({\eta_{1}\over T_{1}}\leqslant w_{1},{\eta_{2}\over T_{2}}\leqslant w_{2}\Big)=1-F_{T_{2}}\Big({\eta_{2}\over w_{2}}\Big)-F_{T_{1}}\Big({\eta_{1}\over w_{1}}\Big)+F_{T_{1},T_{2}}\Big({\eta_{1}\over w_{1}},{\eta_{2}\over w_{2}}\Big).

(3.13)

Because

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\biggl({T_{1}\over\eta_{1}}\leqslant w_{1},{T_{2}\over\eta_{2}}\leqslant w_{2}\biggr)=F_{T_{1},T_{2}}({\eta_{1}w_{1}},{\eta_{2}w_{2}}),

(3.14)

by taking partial derivatives with respect to
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
in (
3.13
) and (
3.14
), we have the joint PDF of
{\eta_{1}/T_{1}}
and
{\eta_{2}/T_{2}}
, and the joint PDF of
{T_{1}/\eta_{1}}
and
{T_{2}/\eta_{2}}
, to be related as follows:

\displaystyle f_{{\eta_{1}\over T_{1}},{\eta_{2}\over T_{2}}}(w_{1},w_{2})=f_{{T_{1}\over\eta_{1}},{T_{2}\over\eta_{2}}}(w_{1},w_{2})={1\over w_{1}w_{2}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{g_{c}}}\,g_{c}\Biggl({\widetilde{w}_{1}^{2}-2\rho\widetilde{w}_{1}\widetilde{w}_{2}+\widetilde{w}_{2}^{2}\over 1-\rho^{2}}\Biggr),\quad w_{1},w_{2}>0,

with
\widetilde{w_{i}}=\log({w_{i}}^{1/\sigma_{i}})
,
i=1,2
. This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

4
Maximum likelihood estimation

Let
\{(T_{1i},T_{2i}):i=1,\ldots,n\}
be a bivariate random sample of size
n
from the
{\rm BLS}(\bm{\theta},g_{c})
distribution with PDF as given in (
2.1
), and let
(t_{1i},t_{2i})
be the corresponding sample of
(T_{1i},T_{2i})
. Then, the log-likelihood function for
\bm{\theta}=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\rho)
,
without the additive constant, is given by

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{\theta})=-n\log(\sigma_{1})-n\log(\sigma_{2})-{n\over 2}\,\log\big({1-\rho^{2}}\big)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log g_{c}\Biggl({\widetilde{t_{1i}}^{2}-2\rho\widetilde{t}_{1i}\widetilde{t}_{2i}+\widetilde{t_{2i}}^{2}\over 1-\rho^{2}}\Biggr),\quad t_{1i},t_{2i}>0,

where

\widetilde{t}_{ki}=\log\biggl[\Bigl({t_{ki}\over\eta_{k}}\Bigr)^{1/\sigma_{k}}\biggr],\ \eta_{k}=\exp(\mu_{k}),\ k=1,2;\ i=1,\ldots,n.

In the case when a supremum
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}=(\widehat{\eta_{1}},\widehat{\eta_{2}},\widehat{\sigma_{1}},\widehat{\sigma_{2}},\widehat{\rho})
exists, it must satisfy the likelihood equations

\displaystyle{\partial\ell({\bm{\theta}})\over\partial\eta_{1}}\bigg|_{{\bm{\theta}}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}=0,\quad{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\eta_{2}}=0,\quad{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\sigma_{1}}\bigg|_{{\bm{\theta}}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}=0,\quad{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\sigma_{2}}\bigg|_{{\bm{\theta}}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}=0,\quad{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\rho}\bigg|_{{\bm{\theta}}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}=0,

(4.1)

where

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\eta_{1}}=\frac{2}{\sigma_{1}\eta_{1}(1-\rho^{2})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\rho\widetilde{t}_{2i}-\widetilde{t}_{1i}\big)G(\widetilde{t}_{1i},\widetilde{t}_{2i}),

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\eta_{2}}=\frac{2}{\sigma_{2}\eta_{2}(1-\rho^{2})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\rho\widetilde{t}_{1i}-\widetilde{t}_{2i}\big)G(\widetilde{t}_{1i},\widetilde{t}_{2i}),

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\sigma_{1}}=-\frac{n}{\sigma_{1}}+{2\over\sigma_{1}(1-\rho^{2})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{t}_{1i}\big(\rho\widetilde{t}_{2i}-\widetilde{t}_{1i}\big)G(\widetilde{t}_{1i},\widetilde{t}_{2i}),

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\sigma_{2}}=-\frac{n}{\sigma_{2}}+{2\over\sigma_{2}(1-\rho^{2})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{t}_{2i}\big(\rho\widetilde{t}_{1i}-\widetilde{t}_{2i}\big)G(\widetilde{t}_{1i},\widetilde{t}_{2i}),

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\rho}={n\rho\over 1-\rho^{2}}-{2\over(1-\rho^{2})^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\rho\widetilde{t}_{1i}-\widetilde{t}_{2i}\big)\big(\rho\widetilde{t}_{2i}-\widetilde{t}_{1i}\big)G(\widetilde{t}_{1i},\widetilde{t}_{2i}),

(4.2)

with the notation

\displaystyle G(\widetilde{t}_{1i},\widetilde{t}_{2i})=g_{c}^{\prime}\Biggl({\widetilde{t_{1i}}^{2}-2\rho\widetilde{t}_{1i}\widetilde{t}_{2i}+\widetilde{t_{2i}}^{2}\over 1-\rho^{2}}\Biggr){\Bigg/}g_{c}\Biggl({\widetilde{t_{1i}}^{2}-2\rho\widetilde{t}_{1i}\widetilde{t}_{2i}+\widetilde{t_{2i}}^{2}\over 1-\rho^{2}}\Biggr),\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

A simple observation shows that the likelihood equations in (
4.1
) can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{t}_{1i}\,G(\widetilde{t}_{1i},\widetilde{t}_{2i})\bigg|_{{\bm{\theta}}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}=0,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\widetilde{t}_{1i}^{2}-\widetilde{t}_{2i}^{2}\big)\,G(\widetilde{t}_{1i},\widetilde{t}_{2i})\bigg|_{{\bm{\theta}}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}=0,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{t_{2i}}\left[2\rho\widetilde{t_{2i}}-(1+\rho^{2})\widetilde{t}_{1i}\right]G(\widetilde{t}_{1i},\widetilde{t}_{2i})\bigg|_{{\bm{\theta}}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}=-{n\widehat{\rho}(1-\widehat{\rho}^{2})\over 2}\,.

Any nontrivial root
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}
of the above likelihood equations is known as
an ML estimator in the loose sense. When the parameter value provides the absolute maximum of the log-likelihood function, it is called an ML estimator in the strict sense.

In the following proposition, we discuss the existence of the ML estimator
\widehat{\rho}
when the other parameters are all known.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
g_{c}
be a density generator satisfying the following condition:

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{c}(x)=r(x)g_{c}(x),\quad-\infty<x<\infty,

(4.3)

for some real-valued function
r(x)
such that
\lim_{\rho\to\pm 1}r(x_{\rho,i})=c\in(-\infty,0)
, where
x_{\rho,i}=(\widetilde{t_{1i}}^{2}-2\rho\widetilde{t}_{1i}\widetilde{t}_{2i}+\widetilde{t_{2i}}^{2})/(1-\rho^{2})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
.
If the parameters
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are all known, then (
4.2
) has at least one root in the interval
(-1,1)
.

Proof.

As
g^{\prime}_{c}(x_{\rho,i})=r(x_{\rho,i})g_{c}(x_{\rho,i})
, we have

G(\widetilde{t}_{1i},\widetilde{t}_{2i})=r(x_{\rho,i})
. Then, by using the condition that
\lim_{\rho\to\pm 1}r(x_{\rho,i})=c<0
, from (
4.2
), we see that

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\to 1^{-}}{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\rho}=-\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\rho\to-1^{+}}{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\rho}=+\infty.

Hence, by Intermediate value theorem, there
exists at least one solution in the interval
(-1,1)
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Observe that in Table
1
, the density generators
of the Bivariate Log-normal (or Bivariate Log-power-exponential with
\xi=0
) and the Bivariate Log-Kotz type (with
\delta=1
)
satisfy the hypotheses of Proposition
4.1
with
r(x_{\rho,i})=-1/2
and
r(x_{\rho,i})=(-\lambda x_{\rho,i}+\xi-1)/x_{\rho,i}{\longrightarrow}-\lambda
as
\rho\to\pm 1
,
\forall i=1,\ldots,n
, respectively. Then, Proposition
4.1
can be applied to guarantee the existence of an ML estimator
\widehat{\rho}
of
\rho
in the loose sense.

On the other hand, the density generators of the Bivariate Log-Kotz type (with
\delta<1
),
the Bivariate Log-Student-
t
,
the Bivariate Log-Pearson Type VII and
the Bivariate Log-power-exponential (with
\xi\neq 0
)
satisfy the condition (
4.3
) with

r(x)=(-\lambda\delta x^{\delta}+\xi-1)/x
,

r(x)=-(\nu+2)/2(1+{x\over\nu})
,

r(x)=-\xi/(\theta+x)

and

r(x)=-x^{-\xi/(\xi+1)}/2(\xi+1)
,
respectively, but in all these cases
r(x_{\rho,i})\longrightarrow 0
as
\rho\to\pm 1
,
\forall i=1,\ldots,n
.

For the BLS model, no closed-form solution to the maximization problem is available, and an MLE can only be found via numerical optimization. Under mild regularity conditions
(
Cox and Hinkley, 1974
;
Davison, 2008
)
, the asymptotic distribution of ML estimator
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}
of
\bm{\theta}
is easily determined by the convergence in law:
(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathscr{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(\bm{0},I^{-1}(\bm{\theta}))
,
where

\bm{0}
is the zero mean vector and
I^{-1}(\bm{\theta})
is the inverse expected
Fisher information matrix.
The main use of the last convergence is to construct confidence regions and to perform
hypothesis testing for
\bm{\theta}

(
Davison, 2008
)
.

7
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we have introduced a class of bivariate log-symmetric models, which is the result of an exponential transformation on a variable that follows a bivariate symmetric distribution. We have studied some of its statistical properties and also discussed the maximum likelihood estimation of the model parameters. A Monte Carlo simulation study has been carried out to evaluate the performance of the maximum likelihood estimators. The simulation results have shown that the estimators perform very well, with empirical bias values being close to zero. We have applied the proposed models to a real fatigue data set, and the results are seen to be favorable to the log-Laplace model among all the models considered. As part of future research, it will be of interest to study bivariate log-symmetric regression models. Furthermore, some hypothesis and misspecification tests (via Monte Carlo simulation) could all be studied. Work on these problems is currently in progress and we hope to report these findings in future.
