Nematic, chiral and topological superconductivity in transition metal dichalcogenides
Constantin Schrade, Liang Fu

Abstract
We introduce and study a realistic model for superconductivity in twisted bilayer WSe 2 , where electron pairing arises from spin-valley fluctuations in the weak-coupling regime. Our model comprises both the full continuum model moiré bandstructure and a short-ranged repulsive interaction. By calculating the spin-valley susceptibility, we identify a Fermi surface nesting feature near half-filling of the top-most moiré band, which induces significantly enhanced spin-valley fluctuations. We then analyze the dominant Kohn-Luttinger pairing instabilities due to these spin-valley fluctuations and show that the leading instability corresponds to a two-component order parameter, which can give rise to nematic, chiral and topological superconductivity. As our findings are asymptotically exact for small interaction strengths, they provide a viable starting point for future studies of superconductivity in twisted transition metal dichalcogenide bilayers.

Here, we address these questions by introducing a model for superconductivity in tWSe
2
, where electron pairing arises from spin-valley fluctuations. Our model comprises both the moiré bandstructure of tWSe
2
and a short-ranged repulsive interaction.
Near half-filling of the top-most moiré band, we demonstrate that the Fermi surfaces of the
\pm K
valleys exhibit a strong nesting feature. This nesting maps the occupied states of one valley onto the other valley’s unoccupied states, leading to strong spin-valley fluctuations especially near the insulating state
bib:Bi2021
. We show that, interestingly, electron pairing mediated by such enhanced spin-valley fluctuations can give rise to nematic, chiral and topological superconductivity. Our findings are asymptotically exact in the weak coupling limit
bib:Raghu2010
and provide a starting point for studies of superconductivity in TMD systems.

II
Model

II.1
Continuum model Hamiltonian

We consider two layers of WSe
2
that are initially aligned in the AA-stacking configuration and, subsequently, rotated by a small twist angle
\theta
, see Fig.
1
(a). In this situation, the
\pm K
valleys of the layers have comparable energy so that inter-layer tunneling becomes effective, which leads to a layer-hybridized moiré bandstructure. For the discussion of this moiré bandstructure, we focus separately on the spin-up (spin-down) valence bands of the
+K
(
-K
) valleys. This approach is justified by the substantial valley-dependent spin splitting in the valence bands and the effective decoupling of the valleys due to their large momentum space separation.
The Hamiltonian for the continuum model moiré bands of spin-up electrons at the
+K
valleys then reads
bib:Wu2019
,

H_{\uparrow}=\int\mathrm{d}{{\boldsymbol{r}}}\,\Psi_{\uparrow}^{{\dagger}}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\begin{pmatrix}h^{t}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})&\Delta_{T}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\\
\Delta^{{\dagger}}_{T}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})&h^{b}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})\end{pmatrix}\Psi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{r}}}),

(1)

where
\Psi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})=(c^{t}_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{r}}}),c^{b}_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{r}}}))^{T}
is an electron spinor with components corresponding to the top/bottom
(t/b)
layer.
The Hamiltonian for spin-down electrons at the
-K
valleys is obtained from
H_{\uparrow}
via a time-reversal operation.

First, we discuss the diagonal components of
H_{\uparrow}
, which represent the individual layers. They are given by,

h^{t/b}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})=-\frac{\hbar^{2}({{\boldsymbol{k}}}-{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{\pm})^{2}}{2m^{*}}\pm\frac{V_{z}}{2}+\sum_{j=1,3,5}2V\cos({\boldsymbol{b}}_{j}\cdot{{\boldsymbol{r}}}\pm\psi).

(2)

Here, the first term describes the kinetic energy with effective mass
m^{*}=0.43m_{0}
(
m_{0}
is the bare electron mass) and momentum shifts
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{\pm}=\left[4\pi|\theta|/(3a_{0})\right](-\sqrt{3}/2,\mp 1/2)
(
a_{0}=3.317\,\text{\AA}
is the monolayer lattice constant), that account for for the layer rotation in momentum space. The second term corresponds to a layer potential difference
V_{z}
due to an out-of-plane displacement field. The third term models the moiré potential with amplitude
V
, phase offset
\psi
, and reciprocal lattice vectors
{\boldsymbol{b}}_{j}=C^{j-1}_{3}(4\pi|\theta|/\sqrt{3}a_{0},0)
(
C_{3}
is a
2\pi/3
rotation).

Besides the individual layer terms,
H_{\uparrow}
also includes off-diagonal terms for inter-layer coupling. They read,

\Delta_{\text{T}}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})=w(1+e^{-i{\boldsymbol{b}}_{2}\cdot{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}+e^{-i{\boldsymbol{b}}_{3}\cdot{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}),

(3)

where
w
corresponds to the inter-layer coupling strength.

Having defined the continuum model Hamiltonian, we now proceed by analyzing its spatial symmetries. We find that the continuum model exhibits a three-fold rotation symmetry given by
U^{{\dagger}}_{C_{3}}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})H_{C_{3}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\uparrow}(C_{3}({{\boldsymbol{r}}}))U_{C_{3}}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})=H_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})
with the Hamiltonian density
H_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})
,
U_{C_{3}}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})=e^{i(1+\tau_{z}/2){\boldsymbol{b}}_{2}\cdot{{\boldsymbol{r}}}_{2}}e^{i{\boldsymbol{b}}_{3}\cdot{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}
, and a layer-space Pauli matrix
\tau_{z}
. Besides the rotation symmetry, which is also a microscopic symmetry of the moiré lattice, the continuum model exhibits an emergent mirror symmetry along
y=0
given by
H_{M_{x}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\uparrow}(M_{x}({{\boldsymbol{r}}}))=H_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\downarrow}({{\boldsymbol{r}}})
. Interestingly, this mirror symmetry flips the two valleys.

II.2
Moiré bandstructure

As a final step, we plot the moiré bands along high-symmetry lines of the Brillouin zone for
(\theta,V,\psi,w)=(5.1^{\circ},9\,\text{meV},128^{\circ},18\,\text{meV})

bib:Devakul2021
with
V_{z}=0\,\text{meV}
and
V_{z}=42\,\text{meV}
, see Fig.
1
(b) and (c). We find that finite displacement field induces an approximate saddle point in the top-most moiré bands
\xi_{\uparrow/\downarrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})
at
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{\pm}
. At these points, the Fermi velocity is greatly reduced, which leads to an enhancement of the density of states (DOS) for a filling of
n\approx 0.9
electrons per moiré unit cell, see Fig.
1
(d). The emergence of an enhanced DOS near
n\approx 1
is in accordance with experiments
bib:Wang2020
and signals that correlation effects due to spin-valley fluctuations may be amplified. In our further analysis, we will fix above choice for
(\theta,V,\psi,w)
that we obtained from density functional calculations
bib:Devakul2021
and the field value
V_{z}=42\,\text{meV}
.

III
Spin-valley fluctuations

We now discuss the ordering instabilities that arise from the spin-valley fluctuations. To identify the spin-valley fluctuations with dominant modulation vectors, we will analyze the maxima of the spin-valley susceptibility,

\chi_{\alpha\beta}({\boldsymbol{q}},\omega)=-\frac{1}{\beta N}\sum_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}\text{Tr}\left[\sigma_{\alpha}G({{\boldsymbol{k}}},i\omega)\sigma_{\beta}G({{\boldsymbol{k}}}+{\boldsymbol{q}},i\omega)\right].

(4)

In this definition,
\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}\in\{\sigma_{z},\sigma_{\pm}=(\sigma_{x}\pm i\sigma_{y})/2\}
are spin/valley-space Pauli matrices, ‘Tr’ denotes the trace over the spin/valley indices,
\beta
is the inverse temperature, and
N
is the number of superlattice unit cells. Furthermore, the non-interacting Green’s function is given by,

G_{s_{1}s_{2}}({{\boldsymbol{k}}},i\omega)=\delta_{s_{1}s_{2}}[i\omega-\xi_{s_{1}}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})+\mu]^{-1}.

(5)

Here,
\xi_{s}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})
are the dispersions for the top-most moiré band of the
\pm K
valleys with
\xi_{\downarrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})=\xi_{\uparrow}(-{{\boldsymbol{k}}})
due to time-reversal symmetry,
\mu
is the chemical potential, and
\omega
corresponds to a fermionic Matsubara frequency.

Having defined the spin-valley susceptibility, we will now investigate its static longitudinal and transversal components,
\chi_{zz}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\chi_{zz}({\boldsymbol{q}},0)
and
\chi_{+-}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\chi_{+-}({\boldsymbol{q}},0)
. In our model, these two components of the spin-valley susceptibility will show different dependencies on the modulation vector
{\boldsymbol{q}}
as the external displacement
fields lifts the
SU(2)
valley rotation symmetry
bib:Pan2020
.

First, we focus on the filling of
n\approx 1
electron per moiré unit cell because it is the experimentally most relevant case. In this situation, the Fermi surfaces have the shape of triangles centered around the
\gamma
point of the Brillouin zone as shown in Fig.
2
(a).
As shown in Fig.
2
(b), we numerically find that
\chi_{+-}({\boldsymbol{q}})
is enhanced near
{\boldsymbol{q}}\approx{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}_{j}
with
{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}_{j}=C^{j-1}_{3}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{+}
, indicating an instability towards an inter-valley excitonic insulator that pairs electrons and holes of opposite valleys
bib:Bi2021
.
However, due to the broken
SU(2)
valley rotation symmetry, the same features are
not
present in the longitudinal valley susceptibility. Instead, as shown in Fig.
2
(c),
\chi_{zz}({\boldsymbol{q}})
is enhanced near
{\boldsymbol{q}}\approx{\boldsymbol{0}}
, suggesting that the excitonic instability competes with a spin/valley-polarized state
bib:Devakul2021
.

We now compare these results with the situation away from
n\approx 1
. For fillings
n\lesssim 0.9
, the Fermi surfaces shrink to smaller triangles and the dominant modulation vectors in the spin-valley susceptibility are modified. For example, at
n\approx 0.5
,
\chi_{+-}({\boldsymbol{q}})
is enhanced near
{\boldsymbol{q}}\approx{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}^{\prime}_{j}
with
{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}^{\prime}_{j}=C^{j-1}_{3}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{-}
, while
\chi_{zz}({\boldsymbol{q}})
shows maxima near the Brillouin zone boundaries. For
n>1
, the Fermi surfaces are center at the Brillouin zone corners. At
n\approx 1.5
,
\chi_{+-}({\boldsymbol{q}})
is enhanced near
{\boldsymbol{q}}\approx{\boldsymbol{0}}
, while
\chi_{zz}({\boldsymbol{q}})
is again enhanced near the Brillouin zone edges.

IV
Superconductivity

IV.1
Kohn-Luttinger mechanism

We will now demonstrate that spin-valley fluctuations can mediate superconductivity by a Kohn-Luttinger mechanism that realizes Cooper pairing from a nominally repulsive interaction term in the weak-coupling regime
bib:Kohn1965
;
bib:Raghu2010
;
bib:Chubukov1993
;
bib:Maiti2013
.

Before discussing the details of our approach, we highlight that the Kohn-Luttinger mechanism has also been valuable for understanding unconventional superconductivity in the intermediate-coupling regime and has already been applied to graphene systems
bib:Lin2018
;
bib:Gonzalez2019
;
bib:Ghazaryan2021
;
bib:You2022
;
bib:Guinea2022
. For
t
WSe
2
, bandstructure effects remain critical for understanding correlated states in these samples. In particular, by tuning the van-Hove singularity in the moiré bandstructure close to half-filling, a significant increase in the longitudinal resistance peak was noted, which indicates the emergence of the correlated insulator state
bib:Wang2020
. Therefore, we expect that the Kohn-Luttinger mechanism can offer qualitative insights into the realized superconducting pairing symmetry.

To start, we introduce the Hubbard Hamiltonian,

H=\sum_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}s}\xi_{s}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})c^{{\dagger}}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}s}c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}s}+\frac{U}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{{\boldsymbol{k}}},{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{q}}\\
s,s^{\prime}\end{subarray}}c^{{\dagger}}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}s}c^{{\dagger}}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}+{{\boldsymbol{k}}}-{\boldsymbol{q}}s^{\prime}}c_{{\boldsymbol{p}}s^{\prime}}c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}s},

(6)

where the quadratic part comprises the dispersion
\xi_{s}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})
of the top-most moiré band of the continuum model and
c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}s}
is the electron annihilation operator with momentum
{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
and spin
s
. In particular, as we omit multi-band effects, there is an emergent mirror symmetry
M_{y}
along
x=0
with
\xi_{s}(M_{y}{{\boldsymbol{k}}})=\xi_{s}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})
. The interaction term in the Hamiltonian is taken as a local (compared to moiré scale) repulsive interaction with a strength
U>0
.

Next, we compute the Cooper channel interaction vertex, which describes scattering of electrons with spin polarization
s
and
s^{\prime}
from the Fermi surface momenta
(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime},-\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})

to
(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}},-\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}})
. In general, the interaction vertex comprises a contribution from opposite-spin and equal-spin scattering. However, the equal-spin scattering leads to pairing with a ‘
{{\boldsymbol{d}}}
-vector’
pointed orthogonal to the spin polarization
bib:Sigrist2005
. Such an equal-spin pairing exhibits a suppressed critical temperature
bib:Frigeri2004
;
bib:Frigeri2004_2
and, hence, our focus will be on opposite-spin scattering. To second order in
U
, we find that the interaction vertex
bib:supplemental
,

\Gamma(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})=U+U^{2}\chi_{+-}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}+\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}).

(7)

Notably, this result is different for TMD monolayers, where a parabolic dispersion induces a momentum dependence only at order
U^{3}

bib:Hsu2014
.

Next, we perform a mean-field decoupling of the effective pairing interaction and compute
the superconducting order parameter from the self-consistency equation
bib:supplemental
,

\int_{\text{FS}_{\uparrow}}\frac{d\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}}{v_{\uparrow}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})}\Gamma(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})\Delta(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})=\lambda\Delta(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}),

(8)

Here,
v_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})=||\partial\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})/\partial{{\boldsymbol{k}}}||
is the Fermi velocity and
\Delta(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}})
is the superconducting
order parameter along the
+K
valley Fermi surface,
\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})=\mu 0
. The order parameters at the
-K
Fermi surface,
\xi_{\downarrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})=\mu
, is
\Delta^{\prime}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}})=-\Delta(-\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}})
. Three remarks about Eq. (
8
) are in order:

(1) The negative eigenvalues
\lambda<0
correspond to sectors with an attractive interaction in the Cooper channel and grow in magnitude under renormalization. In particular, the most negative eigenvalue
\lambda^{(0)}
is most relevant in the renormalization group sense, triggering the leading superconducting instability at
T_{c}\propto\exp(-1/|\lambda^{(0)}|)

bib:Raghu2010
.

(2) The eigenvectors
\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})
correspond to form factors of the superconducting order parameters and transform as irreducible representations
\{A_{1},A_{2},E\}
of the point group
C_{3v}
that is generated by
C_{3}
and
M_{y}
. In particular, since
C_{3v}
has no inversion center, singlet and triplet pairings mix in tWSe
2

bib:Hsu2014
;
bib:Yuan2014
.

(3) In the weak coupling limit, the term
\propto U
in
\Gamma(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})
is parametrically larger than the term
\propto U^{2}
. For an attractive interaction in the Cooper channel, the eigenvectors
\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})
thus need to annihilate the first-order term
\propto U
exactly in the expression for interaction vertex of Eq. (
7
)
bib:Raghu2010
;
bib:Cho2013
. Or, explained differently, the eigenvectors
\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})
need to be in the null-space of the matrix representation of the first-order term
\propto U
in Eq. (
7
).
Such an annihilation implies that
s
-wave pairing,
\Delta^{(s)}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\propto 1
, is suppressed. In particular, the
s
-wave pairing with
\Delta(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}})\propto 1
cannot annihilate this first-order contribution and thus cannot be realized. Furthermore, the absence of inversion symmetry in our system results in a mixture of singlet and triplet pairing channels. Consequently, suppression of
s
-wave pairing extends to other channels within the
A_{1}
representation
bib:Hsu2014
. Hence, the pairing needs to arise from the remaining representations, which are the
1d

A_{2}
representation or the
2d

E
-representation.

IV.2
Superconducting pairings

To determine the leading eigenvalue
\lambda^{(0)}
and the representation of the associated superconducting order parameter, we solve Eq. (
8
) numerically for a broad range of fillings
n\in[0.4,1.6]
. As shown in Fig.
3
and
4
, we find for
1.4\lesssim n\lesssim 1.45
that
\lambda^{(0)}
is in the
A_{2}
representation with a single-component order parameter
\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\propto\Delta_{1}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})
. In contrast, for all other
n
, we find that
\lambda^{(0)}
is in the
E
representation with a two-component order parameter,
\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})=\eta_{1}\Delta_{1}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})+\eta_{2}\Delta_{2}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})
. Notably, we also observe that the magnitude of
\lambda^{(0)}
, which we associate with the pairing strength, is greatly enhanced for
n\approx 0.9
. This enhanced pairing strength
leads to a significant enhancement in the critical temperature
T_{c}
and suggest a pronounced tendency towards superconductivity at
n=0.9
.

To understand the origin of the
E
representation superconductivity, we note that the DOS of
\xi_{+}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})
is maximal near the saddle points
{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}^{\prime}_{j}=C^{j-1}_{3}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{-}
for
n\approx 1
. The amplitude of the scattering processes between these points is
\propto\chi\equiv\chi_{+-}({{\boldsymbol{Q}}}^{\prime}_{i}+{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}^{\prime}_{j})
, which are exactly the points where
\chi_{+-}({\boldsymbol{q}})
is maximal. We thus construct a three-patch model for the order parameters
\boldsymbol{\Delta}=[\Delta({{\boldsymbol{Q}}}^{\prime}_{1}),\Delta({{\boldsymbol{Q}}}^{\prime}_{2}),\Delta({{\boldsymbol{Q}}}^{\prime}_{3})]^{T}
that (analogous to Eq. (
8
)) is given by the eigenvalue equation
\chi(\boldsymbol{P}-\boldsymbol{1})\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\lambda\boldsymbol{\Delta}
with
(\boldsymbol{P})_{ij}=1
and
(\boldsymbol{1})_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
. The most negative eigenvalue
\lambda^{(0)}=-\chi
is indeed found to be doubly degenerate with eigenvectors
(-1,1,0)^{T}
and
(1,1,-2)^{T}
in the
E
representation. In contrast, the positive eigenvalue
\lambda^{(1)}=2\chi
is in the
A
representation with eigenvector
(1,1,1)^{T}
.

IV.3
Ginzburg-Landau analysis

Lastly, we want to identify the superconducting state
\boldsymbol{\eta}=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
realized in the
E
representation of our model.
For that purpose, we write down the (
C_{3v}
-symmetric) Ginzburg-Landau free energy for our system,

F_{\text{GL}}=a(T-T_{c})\,\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}+b_{1}(\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*})^{2}+b_{2}|\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}|^{2}+...,

(9)

where
a>0
induces the superconducting phase if
T<T_{c}
and
b_{1}>0
ensures thermodynamic stability. The sign of
b_{2}
fixes the form of the superconducting state: If
b_{2}<0
,
F_{\text{GL}}
is minimized by the
nematic state
with
\boldsymbol{\eta}=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)
and
\varphi\in[0,2\pi)
. The latter spontaneously breaks the three-fold rotation symmetry of the lattice but preserves time-reversal symmetry. If
b_{2}>0
,
F_{\text{GL}}
is minimized by the
chiral state
with
\boldsymbol{\eta}=(1,\pm i)
. The chiral state breaks time-reversal symmetry spontaneously and is characterized by chiral Majorana edge states at its boundaries.

Considering the possibility of both a chiral state or a nematic state in tWSe
2
, an interesting question is how these possible pairing states could be detected in a possible future experiments. For example, an experimental signature of the Majorana edge modes in the chiral state is a quantized thermal Hall conductivity. Another possibility is to use the Polar-Kerr effect to detect the time-reversal symmetry breaking of the superconducting order parameter in the chiral state. The nematic state, on the other hand, could be distinguished by anisotropies in the upper critical field and optical responses

bib:Venderbos2016
;
bib:Cao2021_2
;
bib:Jin2021
.

V
Discussion and conclusion

Having identified the pairing symmetry for a superconducting state in tWSe
2
, it is now instructive to compare our theoretical considerations
to the experimental results of Wang
et al
bib:Wang2020
.
As already discussed in introduction, this experiment reported the emergence of a putative superconducting state in tWSe
2
upon doping way from a correlated insulator state at half-filling. In the experiment, two features pointed towards the possible emergence of superconductivity:
(1) A significant reduction in resistance below approximately
3\,K
at a twist angle of
5.1^{\circ}
, near half-filling, and under the influence of a displacement field, and (2) a flattening of the current-voltage curve below about
2\,K
under the same conditions. It should be noted that the zero-resistance state observed in Ref.
bib:Wang2020
“was unstable to repeated thermal cycling”. Further experimental study is needed to establish reproducible superconductivity in tWSe
2
.

In our theory work, we introduced a realistic model for superconductivity in tWSe
2
based on spin-valley fluctuations. We have shown that the enhancement of spin-valley fluctuations around half-filling can provide a mechanism for Cooper pairing and predicted an unconventional, two-component order parameter for superconductivity in tWSe
2
, realizing either a
chiral topological superconductor or a time-reversal invariant nematic superconductor. In future studies, it will be interesting to explore experimental signatures of the predicted two-component order parameter.

VI
Effective pairing interaction

In this first section of the Supplemental Material (SM), we provide details on the derivation of the effective pairing interaction from a Kohn-Luttinger renormalization of the bare repulsive interaction.

-
As a first step in this derivation, we write the partition function of the Hamiltonian
H
(as given in Eq. (6) of the main text) in terms of a
coherent state Feynman path integral over Grassman variables,

\begin{split}Z&=\int\mathcal{D}[c^{*},c]\,e^{-S_{0}-S_{I}},\\
S_{0}&=\sum_{s}\sum_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\omega_{n}}c^{*}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},s}(i\omega_{n})\left[-i\omega_{n}+\xi_{s}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\right]c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},s}(i\omega_{n}),\\
S_{I}&=\frac{U}{\beta N}\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{{\boldsymbol{k}}},{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{q}}\\
\omega_{n},\omega^{\prime}_{n},\omega_{\nu}\end{subarray}}c^{*}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}+{\boldsymbol{q}},s}(i\omega_{n}+i\omega_{\nu})c^{*}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{q}},s^{\prime}}(i\omega^{\prime}_{n}-i\omega_{\nu})c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime},s^{\prime}}(i\omega^{\prime}_{n})c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},s}(i\omega_{n}).\end{split}

(10)

Next, we introduce a low-energy effective action for modes in the vicinity of the Fermi surfaces
bib:Shankar1994
;
bib:Vafek2014
,

S^{\text{eff}}_{I}=\langle S_{I}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle S^{2}_{I}\rangle-\langle S_{I}\rangle^{2}\right),

(11)

where
\langle...\rangle
denotes the averaging over ‘high’ energy modes with action
S_{0}
. We note that the first term in the expression for
S^{\text{eff}}_{I}
corresponds to the bare repulsive interaction and is
\propto U
. In contrast, the second term renormalizes the bare repulsive interaction and is
\propto U^{2}
.

To evaluate the renormalization of the bare repulsive interaction, we write it in the more compact form,

S^{\text{eff}}_{I}-\langle S_{I}\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\left[\sum_{1,2,3,4}\sum_{1^{\prime},2^{\prime},3^{\prime},4^{\prime}}\langle c^{*}_{1}c^{*}_{2}c_{3}c_{4}c^{*}_{1^{\prime}}c^{*}_{2^{\prime}}c_{3^{\prime}}c_{4^{\prime}}\rangle-\langle S_{I}\rangle^{2}\right]=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{1,2,3,4}\sum_{1^{\prime},2^{\prime},3^{\prime},4^{\prime}}\langle c^{*}_{1}c^{*}_{2}c_{3}c_{4}c^{*}_{1^{\prime}}c^{*}_{2^{\prime}}c_{3^{\prime}}c_{4^{\prime}}\rangle_{\text{con}}.

(12)

Here, we have introduced the short-hand notation
\sum_{1,2,3,4}\equiv(U/\beta N)\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\sum_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{q}}}\sum_{\omega_{n},\omega^{\prime}_{n},\omega_{\nu}}
.
Moreover, in the second equality, we have noted that the Wick contractions which correspond to disconnected Feynman diagrams in
\langle S^{2}_{I}\rangle
are cancelled by
\langle S_{I}\rangle^{2}
. In particular, the average
\langle...\rangle_{\text{con}}
only involves Wick contractions that correspond to connected Feynman diagrams.

VII
Mean-field theory and self-consistency equation

In this second section of the SM, we provide details on the derivation of the pairing Hamiltonian within mean-field theory and determine the self-consistency equation for the superconducting order parameter.

In a first step, we consider the effective pairing interaction in the Hamiltonian form,

H_{I}^{\text{eff}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}}\Gamma({{\boldsymbol{k}}},{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})\,c^{{\dagger}}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\uparrow}c^{{\dagger}}_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\downarrow}c_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime},\downarrow}c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime},\uparrow}\quad\text{with}\quad\Gamma({{\boldsymbol{k}}},{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})=U+U^{2}\chi_{+-}({{\boldsymbol{k}}}+{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}),

(18)

and perform a mean-field decoupling,

\begin{split}H_{I}^{\text{eff}}\rightarrow H_{\Delta}&=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}}\Gamma({{\boldsymbol{k}}},{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})\,\left[\langle c^{{\dagger}}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\uparrow}c^{{\dagger}}_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\downarrow}\rangle c_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime},\downarrow}c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime},\uparrow}+c^{{\dagger}}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\uparrow}c^{{\dagger}}_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\downarrow}\langle c_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime},\downarrow}c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime},\uparrow}\rangle\right]\\
&=-\sum_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\,c_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\downarrow}c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\uparrow}-\sum_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})^{*}\,c^{{\dagger}}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\uparrow}c^{{\dagger}}_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\downarrow},\end{split}

(19)

where we have defined the superconducting order parameter as,

\begin{split}\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})&=-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}}\Gamma({{\boldsymbol{k}}},{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})\,\langle c^{{\dagger}}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime},\uparrow}c^{{\dagger}}_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime},\downarrow}\rangle.\end{split}

(20)

In a second step, we write the pairing Hamiltonian
H_{\Delta}
and the normal state Hamiltonian
H_{0}
as a Bogoliubov-de Gennes Hamiltonian matrix,

\begin{split}H&=H_{0}+H_{\Delta}\\
&=\sum_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\,c^{{\dagger}}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\uparrow}c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\uparrow}+\xi_{\downarrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\,c^{{\dagger}}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\downarrow}c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\downarrow}-\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\,c_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\downarrow}c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\uparrow}-\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})^{*}\,c^{{\dagger}}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\uparrow}c^{{\dagger}}_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\downarrow}\\
&=\sum_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\left[c^{{\dagger}}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\uparrow}c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\uparrow}+c^{{\dagger}}_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\downarrow}c_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\downarrow}\right]-\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\,c_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\downarrow}c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\uparrow}-\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})^{*}\,c^{{\dagger}}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\uparrow}c^{{\dagger}}_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\downarrow}\\
&=\sum_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}\begin{pmatrix}c^{{\dagger}}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\uparrow}&c_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})&-\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})^{*}\\
-\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})&-\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\uparrow}\\
c^{{\dagger}}_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}+E_{0}\end{split}

(21)

where we have used that
\xi_{\downarrow}(-{{\boldsymbol{k}}})=\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})
and defined the energy offset
E_{0}
. We now introduce the Bogoliubov transformations,

\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\uparrow}\\
c^{{\dagger}}_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}&=\begin{pmatrix}u^{*}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}&v_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}\\
-v_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}&u_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},1}\\
\gamma^{{\dagger}}_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},2}\end{pmatrix}\ \ \text{with}\ \ u_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}=\frac{\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})}{|\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})|}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})}{E({{\boldsymbol{k}}})}\right)},\quad v_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})}{E({{\boldsymbol{k}}})}\right)},\end{split}

(22)

and apply it to the Bogoliubov-de Gennes Hamiltonian matrix. This yields the diagonalized Hamiltonian,

\begin{split}H&=\sum_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma^{{\dagger}}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},1}&\gamma_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}E({{\boldsymbol{k}}})&0\\
0&-E({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},1}\\
\gamma^{{\dagger}}_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},2},\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}E({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\left[\gamma^{{\dagger},1}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}\gamma_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},1}+\gamma^{{\dagger}}_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},2}\gamma_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},2}\right]+E^{\prime}_{0},\end{split}

(23)

where we have defined the quasiparticle energy
E({{\boldsymbol{k}}})=\sqrt{\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})^{2}+|\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})|^{2}}

and
another energy offset
E^{\prime}_{0}
.

In a third step, we evaluate the expectation value,

\begin{split}\langle c^{{\dagger}}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\uparrow}c^{{\dagger}}_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\downarrow}\rangle&=\frac{1}{2}\left[-u_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}v_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}\langle\gamma^{{\dagger}}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},1}\gamma_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},1}\rangle+v_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}u_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}\langle\gamma_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},2}\gamma^{{\dagger}}_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},2}\rangle\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}u_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}v_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}\left[1-\langle\gamma^{{\dagger}}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},1}\gamma_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},1}\rangle-\langle\gamma^{{\dagger}}_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},2}\gamma_{-{{\boldsymbol{k}}},2}\rangle\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}u_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}v_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}\left[1-2f(E({{\boldsymbol{k}}}))\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}u_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}v_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}\tanh\left(\frac{E({{\boldsymbol{k}}})}{2k_{B}T}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{4}\frac{\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})}{E({{\boldsymbol{k}}})}\tanh\left(\frac{E({{\boldsymbol{k}}})}{2k_{B}T}\right).\end{split}

(24)

Here,
f
denotes the Fermi-Dirac distribution. We now reinsert the expression of the expectation value in the definition of the superconducting order parameter. Under the assumption that the superconducting order parameter is sufficiently small, we find the self-consistency equation,

\begin{split}\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})&=-\frac{1}{8N}\sum_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}}\Gamma({{\boldsymbol{k}}},{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})\,\frac{\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})}{\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})}\tanh\left(\frac{\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})}{2k_{B}T}\right).\end{split}

(25)

In a final step, we rewrite this self-consistency equation as an integral eigenvalue equation,

\int_{\text{FS}_{\uparrow}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}}{v_{\uparrow}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})}\Gamma(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})\Delta(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})=\lambda\Delta(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}),

(26)

which determines the superconducting order parameter on the
+K
Fermi surface,
\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})-\mu=0
, with
v_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})=||\partial\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})/\partial{{\boldsymbol{k}}}||
denoting the Fermi velocity. We note that the superconducting order parameters at the
+K
and
-K
Fermi surfaces are given by
\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})
and
\Delta^{\prime}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})=-\Delta(-{{\boldsymbol{k}}})
, respectively. This concludes our derivation of the self-consistency equation of the superconducting order parameter.

VIII
Numerical evaluation of the self-consistency equation

In this third section of the SM, we give details on the numerical evaluation of the self-consistency equation.

To start, we write the line integral in the self-consistency equation as an area integral over a small neighborhood
of the
+K
Fermi surface given by the level set
\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})-\mu=\pm\Omega
,

\int_{\text{FS}_{\uparrow}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}}{v_{\uparrow}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})}\Gamma(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})\Delta(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})\approx\frac{1}{2\Omega}\int_{|\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})-\mu|<\Omega}\mathrm{d}{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}\,\Gamma(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})\Delta(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})\approx\frac{1}{2\Omega}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}\\
|\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})-\mu|<\Omega\end{subarray}}\delta{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}\,\Gamma(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})\Delta(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}).

(27)

Here, we have noted that the width of the area defined by the level set is roughly
2\Omega|\nabla_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})|^{-1}=2\Omega\,v_{\uparrow}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})^{-1}
and divided the integrand by this factor. Subsequently, we have written the area integral in terms of a Riemann sum.

There are now two alternative approaches for numerically solving the eigenvalue equation:

A first approach is to fix a set of vectors
\{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}_{i}\}
in momentum space within the window
|\xi_{+}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})-\mu|<\Omega
and write the Riemann sum
as a matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
with entries
[\boldsymbol{A}]_{ij}=\Gamma({{\boldsymbol{k}}}_{i},{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}_{j})\,\delta{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}_{j}/2\Omega
. Diagonalization of this matrix yields the desired eigenvalues
\lambda

and eigenvectors
\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})
. However, it is important to note that within this approach the resulting eigenvectors are not automatically classified in terms of the irreducible representations of C
3v
.

A second approach is to fix an irreducible representation
D
of C
3v
and expand the superconducting order parameter in terms of basis functions
that are orthogonal with respect to the Fermi surface inner product,

\Delta^{D}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})=\sum_{i}c^{D}_{i}\psi^{D}_{i}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\quad\text{with}\quad\left(\frac{1}{2\Omega}\right)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{{\boldsymbol{k}}},{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}\\
|\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})-\mu|<\Omega\\
|\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})-\mu|<\Omega\end{subarray}}\psi_{i}^{D}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})^{*}\psi_{j}^{D}({{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})\,\delta{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\,\delta{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}=\delta_{ij}.

(28)

This expansion allows us to write the self-consistency equation in the matrix form,

\boldsymbol{A}^{D}\boldsymbol{c}^{D}=\lambda^{D}\boldsymbol{c}^{D}\quad\text{with}\quad[\boldsymbol{A}^{D}]_{ij}=\left(\frac{1}{2\Omega}\right)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{{\boldsymbol{k}}},{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime}\\
|\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})-\mu|<\Omega\\
|\xi_{\uparrow}({{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})-\mu|<\Omega\end{subarray}}\psi_{i}^{D}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})^{*}\Gamma(\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})\psi_{j}^{D}({{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime})\,\delta{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\,\delta{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\prime},

(29)

so that we can solve for
\boldsymbol{c}^{D}=(c_{1},c_{2},...)^{T}
to obtain the eigenvalues
\lambda^{D}
and the eigenvectors
\Delta^{D}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})
.

Finally, we outline a two-step protocol
bib:Platt2013
to generate the orthogonal basis functions for a
d
-dimensional representation
D
:
As a first step, we fix a set of linear-independent vectors
\{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{n,i}\}_{i=1,..,d}
that connect a site on the superlattice with an
n
-th nearest neighbor site. This set of vectors allows us to define the lattice harmonics,

\tilde{\psi}^{D}_{n,i}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})=\sum_{G}\text{char}_{D}([G])\,e^{i{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\cdot G{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{n,i}},

(30)

where the summation is over all the group elements
G
of
C_{3v}
and
\text{char}_{D}([G])
denotes the character of the conjugacy class
[G]

in the representation
D
. In a second step, we use a Gram-Schmidt procedure to orthogonalize the lattice harmonics, leading to a desired set of orthogonal basis functions
\psi_{i}^{D}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})
. This concludes our discussion on the numerical evaluation of the self-consistency equation.

IX
Ginzburg-Landau free energy

In this fourth section of the Supplemental Material, we present details on the analysis of the phenomenological Ginzburg-Landau free energy, which allows to us to identify the candidate pairing states that is realized in the
E
-representation of our model for superconductivity in tWSe
2
.

In a first step, we introduce a general linear combination of the superconducting order parameter,

\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})=\eta_{1}\Delta_{1}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})+\eta_{2}\Delta_{2}({{\boldsymbol{k}}}),

(31)

where
\{\Delta_{1}({{\boldsymbol{k}}}),\Delta_{2}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\}
are two linear-independent basis functions of the
E
-representation and
\{\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\}
are complex expansion coefficients.

In a second step, we expand the Ginzburg-Landau free energy in terms of
\boldsymbol{\eta}=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
,

F_{\text{GL}}=a\,\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}+b_{1}(\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*})^{2}+b_{2}|\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}|^{2}.

(32)

Here, we have required that the Ginzburg-Landau free energy is (1) a real function, (2) invariant under all symmetry operations of
C_{3v}
, (3) an even function in
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
. Moreover, we have denoted the Ginzburg-Landau expansion coefficients by
\{a,b_{1},b_{2}\}
. We point out that
a>0
induces the superconducting phase if
T<T_{c}
. Also, we note that
|\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}\times\boldsymbol{\eta}|
is not an independent quartic term because
|\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}|^{2}=(\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*})^{2}-|\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}\times\boldsymbol{\eta}|
.

In a third step, we perform a variation of the Ginzburg-Landau free energy with respect to
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}
. This yields the requirement,

\begin{split}\frac{\delta F_{\text{GL}}}{\delta\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}}=a\,\boldsymbol{\eta}+2b_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}\right)\boldsymbol{\eta}+2b_{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}\right)\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}0.\end{split}

(33)

To ensure thermodynamic stability, we further require that
b_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{>}}0
.

In a fourth step, we note that if
b_{2}>0
, the superconducting order parameter needs to satisfy
\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}=0
for minimizing the Ginzburg-Landau free energy. From this constraint, we see that the superconducting order parameter needs to take on the chiral form,

\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\propto\Delta_{1}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\pm i\Delta_{2}({{\boldsymbol{k}}}).

(34)

In a final step, we note that if
b_{2}<0
, the superconducting order parameter needs to maximize
\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}
for minimizing the Ginzburg-Landau free energy. This condition implies that the superconducting order parameter needs to take on the nematic form,

\Delta({{\boldsymbol{k}}})\propto\cos\varphi\,\Delta_{1}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})+\sin\varphi\,\Delta_{2}({{\boldsymbol{k}}}),

(35)

where
\varphi
is an arbitrary phase. This concludes our derivation of the form of the superconducting order parameter.

In this seventh section of the Supplemental Material, we introduce and discuss an effective model that captures some of the features of the full continuum model of tWSe

X.1
Hamiltonian

As a starting point, we introduce the Hamiltonian of the effective model,

\tilde{H}=\sum_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},s}[\tilde{\xi}_{s}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})-\mu]c^{{\dagger}}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},s}c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},s}+\frac{U}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{{\boldsymbol{k}}},{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{q}}\\
s,s^{\prime}\end{subarray}}c^{{\dagger}}_{{\boldsymbol{q}},s}c^{{\dagger}}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}+{{\boldsymbol{k}}}-{\boldsymbol{q}},s^{\prime}}c_{{\boldsymbol{p}},s^{\prime}}c_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},s}

(36)

In this expression, we have defined the dispersion relations, which describe (as shown in Fig. 6(a)) electron hopping on a triangular lattice,

\tilde{\xi}_{s}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})=-2\tilde{t}\sum_{i=1,2,3}\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right)\cos({{\boldsymbol{k}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{i})+s\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right)\sin({{\boldsymbol{k}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{i}),

(37)

Here, the lattice vectors are given by
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{1}=a_{0}/|\theta|(0,1)^{T}
,
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{2}=a_{0}/|\theta|(\sqrt{3}/2,-1/2)^{T}
, and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{3}=a_{0}/|\theta|(-\sqrt{3}/2,-1/2)^{T}
. Moreover, the tunneling amplitude is denoted by
\tilde{t}
. As shown in Fig. 6(b), the dispersion relation
\tilde{\xi}_{s}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})
is qualitatively similar to the dispersion relation
\xi_{s}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})
obtained from the full continuum model. In particular, the dispersion relation shows a saddle points at
\kappa_{\pm}
that lead to a vHs at half-filling, as depicted in Fig. 6(c).

X.2
Comparing the effective model to the continuum model

Next, want to compare the effective model to the continuum model of . For that purpose

we have computed the valley susceptibility from the expression,

\tilde{\chi}_{\alpha\beta}({\boldsymbol{q}})=-\frac{1}{\beta N}\sum_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},i\omega}\text{Tr}\left[\sigma_{\alpha}\tilde{G}({{\boldsymbol{k}}},i\omega)\sigma_{\beta}\tilde{G}({{\boldsymbol{k}}}+{\boldsymbol{q}},i\omega)\right]\quad\text{with}\quad\tilde{G}_{s_{1}s_{2}}({{\boldsymbol{k}}},i\nu)=\delta_{s_{1}s_{2}}[i\nu-\tilde{\xi}_{s_{1}}({{\boldsymbol{k}}})+\mu]^{-1}.

The results of the computation are shown in Fig. 7. Most notably, we have found that the transversal susceptibility

Finally, we compare the possible superconducting orders arising from inter-valley fluctuations in the ‘
\pi/6
