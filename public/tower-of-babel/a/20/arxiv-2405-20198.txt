Well-posedness of Hibler’s parabolic-hyperbolic sea ice model
Felix Brandt

Abstract
. This paper proves the local-in-time strong well-posedness of a parabolic-hyperbolic regularized version of Hibler’s sea ice model. Hibler’s model is the most frequently used sea ice model in climate science. Lagrangian coordinates are employed to handle the hyperbolic terms in the balance laws. The resulting problem is regarded as a quasilinear non-autonomous evolution equation. Maximal L p \mathrm{L}^{p} -regularity of the underlying linearized problem is obtained on an anisotropic ground space in order to deal with the lack of regularization in the balance laws.

1.
Introduction

Recent years have witnessed an increase in research on sea ice dynamics both, from the theoretical and the applied side.
One reason for this might be the role of sea ice in climate science, acting as an insulator in between the atmosphere and the ocean.
For more details on the importance of sea ice in climate studies, we refer e. g. to the survey article of Hunke, Lipscomb and Turner
[
15
]
.
Golden et al.
[
12
]
provide an overview of sea ice modeling.
To date, almost all climate models capturing sea ice dynamics rely on Hibler’s viscous-plastic model introduced in
[
13
]
in 1979.
It is a 2D large-scale model that describes dynamic as well as thermodynamic aspects of sea ice.
In particular, the complex mechanical behavior of sea ice is taken into account in the viscous-plastic rheology.

First well-posedness results to Hibler’s model were obtained by Brandt, Disser, Haller-Dintelmann and Hieber
[
6
]
as well as Liu, Thomas and Titi
[
23
]
.
In
[
6
]
, a fully parabolic variant of Hibler’s model is investigated, and local-in-time strong well-posedness and global-in-time well-posedness close to equilibrium solutions are shown.
The parabolic-hyperbolic nature of the model is stressed in
[
23
]
, but the authors strongly modify the stress tensor and establish local strong well-posedness of the resulting problem.

It is the aim of the present paper to prove the local strong well-posedness of the
parabolic-hyperbolic
variant of Hibler’s model, where the
most commonly used regularization for the stress tensor
is employed.
Unlike the models considered in
[
6
]
or
[
23
]
, this is the model typically studied in numerical simulations.

In contrast to the rigorous mathematical analysis, which only started quite recently, there is vast literature on numerical analysis and simulations of Hibler’s model, see for example the work of Zhang and Hibler
[
37
]
.
In order to ease computational costs, Hunke and Dukowicz
[
14
]
suggested an elastic-viscous-plastic rheology.
This model was later on modified in order to reduce the discrepancy with observations, see e. g. the article of Kimmrich, Danilov and Losch
[
16
]
.
Lemieux and Tremblay
[
20
]
implemented an inexact Newton method for Hibler’s model.
In
[
26
]
, Mehlmann and Richter developed a modified Newton solver.
For further works in this direction, we also refer to the articles of Seinen and Khouider
[
33
]
, Shih, Mehlmann, Losch and Stadler
[
34
]
or Yaremchuk and Panteleev
[
36
]
.
Mehlmann and Korn
[
25
]
provided numerical studies of sea ice on triangular grids for an integration into the ocean general circulation model ICON-O.

As indicated above, we invoke a regularized stress tensor here.
In fact, although the original stress tensor describes an idealized viscous-plastic material, it gives rise to a degenerate problem, see also
Section 2
.
As a result, already Hibler
[
13
]
suggested to cut off the viscosities.
In the present paper, we follow the most common regularization of the stress tensor as e. g. suggested by Kreyscher et al.
[
18
]
.
Recently, different possibilities to modify the rheology were suggested by Chatta, Khouider and Kesri
[
8
]
and Ringeisen, Losch and Tremblay
[
32
]
.

For the analysis, we employ the Lagrangian change of coordinates in order to deal with the hyperbolic effects in the balance laws.
The advantage is that the hyperbolic terms in the balance laws are annihilated by the introduction of Lagrangian coordinates.
More precisely, the time derivative with respect to Lagrangian coordinates corresponds precisely to the material derivative in the Eulerian formulation.
For the Lagrangian approach in the context of the compressible Navier-Stokes equations, we refer for instance to the article of Danchin
[
9
]
.
Let us stress, however, that the present problem differs significantly from compressible Navier-Stokes equations.
First, the regularized stress tensor
\sigma_{\delta}
shown in (
2.2
) depends on the deformation tensor
\varepsilon=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}(\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}v_{\mathrm{ice}}+(\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}v_{\mathrm{ice}})^{\top})
in a nonlinear way.
In addition, the pressure is replaced by a so-called ice strength
P=P(h,a)
as displayed in (
2.1
) which is in turn a function of the ice thickness
h
and the ice concentration
a
for which balance laws are prescribed.

For the principal variable
u=(v_{\mathrm{ice}},h,a)
capturing the horizontal ice velocity
v_{\mathrm{ice}}
, the ice thickness
h
and the ice concentration
a
, a suitable operator
A(t,u)
and right-hand side
F(t,u)
, we rewrite the resulting
non-autonomous
and
quasilinear
system in Lagrangian coordinates as a Cauchy problem of the form

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}u+A(t,u)u=F(t,u),\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0ptt>0,\kern 5.0ptu(0)=u_{0},

on a Banach space
\mathrm{X}_{0}
.
For the linearized problem, we employ the so-called
maximal regularity
method.
Roughly speaking, this means that for
A(u_{0})\coloneqq A(0,u_{0})\colon\mathrm{X}_{1}\to\mathrm{X}_{0}
and the time trace
\tr
, the operator

\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}+A(u_{0}),\tr\Bigr)\colon\mathbb{E}_{1}\to\mathbb{E}_{0}\times\mathrm{X}_{\gamma}

is an isomorphism between suitable Banach spaces
\mathbb{E}_{1}
and
\mathbb{E}_{0}\times\mathrm{X}_{\gamma}
.
In this paper, we choose the
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-framework, so we consider solutions in
\mathbb{E}_{1}\coloneqq\mathrm{W}^{1,p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{0})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{1})
, meaning that every term in the differential equation lies in
\mathbb{E}_{0}\coloneqq\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{0})
.
The space
\mathrm{X}_{\gamma}
then is the real interpolation space
(\mathrm{X}_{0},\mathrm{X}_{1})_{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}},p}
.
For further details on maximal regularity in general and maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity in particular, we refer for instance to the monographs of Amann
[
2
]
, Lunardi
[
24
]
or Prüss and Simonett
[
30
]
, the memoir of Denk, Hieber and Prüss
[
11
]
or the book chapter of Kunstmann and Weis
[
19
]
.
It is a key step in our analysis to introduce an anisotropic ground space of the form
\mathrm{X}_{0}=\mathrm{L}^{q}\times\mathrm{W}^{1,q}\times\mathrm{W}^{1,q}
for the ice velocity, ice thickness and ice concentration to obtain the maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity of the linearized problem.
In fact, we even show that the associated operator matrix admits a bounded
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus by means of theory for diagonally dominant block operator matrices and deduce the maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity from there.
We then use the maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity to set up a fixed point argument in the Lagrangian formulation.
For this, a precise tracking of the change of coordinates is required to derive suitable nonlinear estimates.

Recently, sea ice has also been studied in different contexts.
In
[
7
]
, sea ice is investigated in the time periodic setting, see also
[
5
, Section 7.2]
.
Moreover, local strong well-posedness of the interaction problem of sea ice with a rigid body is established in
[
3
]
.
The paper
[
4
]
introduces sea ice as a thin layer in between the atmosphere and the ocean and establishes local strong well-posedness as well as global strong well-posedness close to equilibria of the coupled model.
The motivation is the coupled atmosphere-ocean model introduced by Lions, Temam and Wang
[
21
,
22
]
.
In the papers
[
7
,
3
,
4
]
, the fully parabolic regularized variant of Hibler’s model as analyzed in
[
6
]
is taken into account.
Let us also mention the recent work of Piersanti and Temam
[
29
]
on the modeling and analysis of the dynamics of shallow ice sheets.

For convenience of the reader, we briefly elaborate on the function spaces used in this paper.
Consider a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with boundary of class
\mathrm{C}^{2}
, and suppose that
G
either represents
\Omega
, or an interval of the form
(0,T)
, for
T>0
.
With
p\in[1,\infty]
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\mathrm{L}^{p}(G)
and
\mathrm{W}^{k,p}(G)
the Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces on
G
, respectively.
The space
\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)
,
q\in(1,\infty)
, contains the functions in
\mathrm{W}^{1,q}(\Omega)
whose trace vanishes on
\partial\Omega
.
Moreover, for
l\in\{0,1\}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the space
\mathrm{C}^{l,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
consists of all functions
f\in\mathrm{C}^{l}(\overline{\Omega})
such that all partial derivatives
\partial^{\beta}f
of orders
|\beta|\leq l
are
\alpha
-Hölder continuous on
\overline{\Omega}
.
By
\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{1}([0,\infty))
, we denote the functions in
\mathrm{C}^{1}([0,\infty))
that are bounded on
[0,\infty)
, and whose derivative is additionally bounded on
[0,\infty)
.
The space
\mathrm{BUC}([0,T])
represents the bounded and uniformly continuous functions on
[0,T]
.
For
s>0
and
p
,
q\in(1,\infty)
, we use
\mathrm{B}_{qp}^{s}(\Omega)
for the Besov spaces on
\Omega
.
Note that they can be obtained from the Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces by real interpolation, see e. g.
[
35
, Sec. 2.4 and 4.3.1]
.
More precisely, for
s
,
p
,
q
as above, we have
\mathrm{B}_{qp}^{s}(\Omega)=(\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega),\mathrm{W}^{2,q}(\Omega))_{\frac{s}{2},p}
.
Finally, for a Banach space
\mathrm{X}
, we denote by
\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{X})
and
\mathrm{W}^{1,p}(0,T;\mathrm{X})
the respective Bochner spaces.

This article is organized as follows.
In
Section 2
, we introduce Hibler’s model and state the main result,
Theorem 2.2
, on the local-in-time strong well-posedness.

Section 3
is then dedicated to the transformation to Lagrangian coordinates and to the reformulation of the main result, see
Theorem 3.1
.
In addition, we discuss the continuous dependence on the initial data as well as a blow-up criterion in the Lagrangian formulation.
The focal point of
Section 4
is the proof of the bounded
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus and the maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity of the linearized operator matrix with the anisotropic ground space.
In
Section 5
, we prove the main result.
For this, we start with the reformulation as a fixed point problem.
Thereafter, we derive the estimates of the nonlinear terms.
We then deduce
Theorem 2.2
from
Theorem 3.1
by the invertibility of the change of coordinates for small time.

Section 6
presents some concluding remarks.

2.
Hibler’s sea ice model and main result

In this section, we first recall Hibler’s viscous-plastic sea ice model from
[
13
]
and then state the main result on the local strong well-posedness.
In the sequel, we consider a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with boundary
\partial\Omega
of class
\mathrm{C}^{2}
and a time interval
(0,T)
, where
0<T<\infty
.
The model variables are the horizontal sea ice velocity
v_{\mathrm{ice}}\colon(0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the mean ice thickness
h\colon(0,T)\times\Omega\to[\kappa,\infty)
, with
\kappa>0
denoting a small parameter, and the ice concentration
a\colon(0,T)\times\Omega\to(0,1)
.
The latter variable describes the ratio of area covered by thick ice, i. e., sea ice with a thickness larger than
h_{\bullet}
, where
h_{\bullet}>0
is a certain thickness level.
The lower bound of the mean ice thickness means that in each control area, there is at least some sea ice.
In the sequel, we denote by
u
the principle variable, i. e.,
u=(v_{\mathrm{ice}},h,a)
.

The characteristics of sea ice as a viscous-plastic material are encoded in the stress tensor introduced below.
In the following,
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(v_{\mathrm{ice}})=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}(\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}v_{\mathrm{ice}}+(\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}v_{\mathrm{ice}})^{\top})
represents the deformation tensor.
We remark that
\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}
is the gradient in two variables.
The same is valid for
\mathrm{div}_{\mathrm{H}}\,
, denoting the horizontal divergence.
Moreover, for given constants
p^{*}>0
and
c_{\bullet}>0
, the ice strength
P
takes the explicit shape

(2.1)

P=P(h,a)=p^{*}h\mathrm{e}^{-c_{\bullet}(1-a)}.

It can be found in
[
13
, p. 822, eq. (17)]
.
For the ratio
e>1
of the major to minor axes of the elliptical yield curve of the principle components of the stress, we introduce

\triangle^{2}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\left(\varepsilon_{11}^{2}+\varepsilon_{22}^{2}\right)\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{e^{2}}\Bigr)+\frac{4}{e^{2}}\varepsilon_{12}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{11}\varepsilon_{22}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{e^{2}}\Bigr).

The bulk and shear viscosities are as made precise in
[
13
, p. 819, eq. (7) and (8)]
, and they take the shape

\zeta(\varepsilon,P)=\frac{P(h,a)}{2\triangle(\varepsilon)}\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt\eta(\varepsilon,P)=e^{-2}\zeta(\varepsilon,P).

The stress tensor
\sigma
accounting for the behavior of sea ice as a viscous-plastic material then reads as

\sigma=2\eta(\varepsilon,P)\varepsilon+[\zeta(\varepsilon,P)-\eta(\varepsilon,P)]\tr(\varepsilon)\Id_{2}-\frac{P}{2}\Id_{2},

with
\Id_{2}
denoting the identity matrix.
In spite of the fact that the above law describes an idealized viscous-plastic behavior, the bulk and shear viscosities
\zeta
and
\eta
become singular for
\varepsilon\to 0
.
For this reason, we invoke the same regularization as in
[
25
]
and
[
6
]
, see also
[
18
]
, by defining

\triangle_{\delta}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\sqrt{\delta+\triangle^{2}(\varepsilon)}

for
\delta>0
.
Accordingly, we introduce the regularized viscosities

\zeta_{\delta}(\varepsilon,P)\coloneqq\frac{P(h,a)}{2\triangle_{\delta}(\varepsilon)}\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt\eta_{\delta}(\varepsilon,P)\coloneqq e^{-2}\zeta_{\delta}(\varepsilon,P),

resulting in the regularized stress tensor
\sigma_{\delta}
studied in the remainder of this article and given by

(2.2)

\sigma_{\delta}\coloneqq 2\eta_{\delta}(\varepsilon,P)\varepsilon+[\zeta_{\delta}(\varepsilon,P)-\eta_{\delta}(\varepsilon,P)]\tr(\varepsilon)\Id_{2}-\frac{P}{2}\Id_{2}.

Hibler’s model consists of a momentum equation for the sea ice velocity
v_{\mathrm{ice}}
coupled to balance laws for
h
and
a
.
The momentum balance includes the internal ice stress expressed by
\mathrm{div}_{\mathrm{H}}\,\sigma_{\delta}
as well as external forcing terms and takes the shape

m_{\mathrm{ice}}\left(\partial_{t}v_{\mathrm{ice}}+(v_{\mathrm{ice}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}})v_{\mathrm{ice}}\right)=\mathrm{div}_{\mathrm{H}}\,\sigma_{\delta}-m_{\mathrm{ice}}c_{\mathrm{cor}}v_{\mathrm{ice}}^{\perp}-m_{\mathrm{ice}}g\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}H+\tau_{\mathrm{atm}}+\tau_{\mathrm{ocn}}(v_{\mathrm{ice}}).

In the above
m_{\mathrm{ice}}=\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}h
represents the ice mass for a constant ice density
\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}>0
,
c_{\mathrm{cor}}>0
is the Coriolis parameter,
g
denotes the gravity,
H\colon(0,T)\times\Omega\to[0,\infty)
is the sea surface dynamic height and
\tau_{\mathrm{atm}}
as well as
\tau_{\mathrm{ocn}}(v_{\mathrm{ice}})
correspond to the atmospheric wind and oceanic forces.
The latter two read as

\tau_{\mathrm{atm}}=\rho_{\mathrm{atm}}C_{\mathrm{atm}}|V_{\mathrm{atm}}|R_{\mathrm{atm}}V_{\mathrm{atm}}\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt\tau_{\mathrm{ocn}}(v_{\mathrm{ice}})=\rho_{\mathrm{ocn}}C_{\mathrm{ocn}}|V_{\mathrm{ocn}}-v_{\mathrm{ice}}|R_{\mathrm{ocn}}(V_{\mathrm{ocn}}-v_{\mathrm{ice}})

for constant densities
\rho_{\mathrm{atm}}>0
and
\rho_{\mathrm{ocn}}>0
, constant drag coefficients
C_{\mathrm{atm}}>0
and
C_{\mathrm{ocn}}>0
, velocities
V_{\mathrm{atm}}
and
V_{\mathrm{ocn}}
of the atmosphere and the ocean and rotation matrices
R_{\mathrm{atm}}
and
R_{\mathrm{ocn}}
.

The balance laws of the mean ice thickness
h
and the ice concentration
a
are given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}h+\mathrm{div}_{\mathrm{H}}\,(v_{\mathrm{ice}}h)&=S_{\mathrm{h}}(h,a),\\
\partial_{t}a+\mathrm{div}_{\mathrm{H}}\,(v_{\mathrm{ice}}a)&=S_{\mathrm{a}}(h,a),\end{aligned}\right.

where
S_{\mathrm{h}}
and
S_{\mathrm{a}}
represent thermodynamic source terms.
For a function
f_{\mathrm{gr}}\in\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{1}([0,\infty))
modeling the ice growth rate, see for instance the one suggested by Hibler
[
13
]
, these terms are

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{h}}(h,a)

\displaystyle=f_{\mathrm{gr}}\left(\frac{h}{a}\right)a+(1-a)f_{\mathrm{gr}}(0)\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{a}}(h,a)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{f_{\mathrm{gr}}(0)}{\kappa}(1-a),&if $f_{\mathrm{gr}}(0)>0$,\\
0,&if $f_{\mathrm{gr}}(0)<0$,\end{cases}\kern 5.0pt+\kern 5.0pt\begin{cases}0,&if $S_{\mathrm{h}}>0$,\\
\frac{a}{2h}S_{\mathrm{h}},&if $S_{\mathrm{h}}<0$.\end{cases}

In the present setting, the model is completed by Dirichlet boundary conditions for the sea ice velocity, so
v_{\mathrm{ice}}=0
on
(0,T)\times\partial\Omega
, while no boundary conditions for
h
and
a
are assumed.
Moreover, the initial conditions read as
v_{\mathrm{ice}}(0)=v_{\mathrm{ice},0}
,
h(0)=h_{0}
and
a(0)=a_{0}
.

The previous considerations are summarized in the complete system of equations

(2.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}v_{\mathrm{ice}}+(v_{\mathrm{ice}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}})v_{\mathrm{ice}}&=\frac{1}{m_{\mathrm{ice}}}\mathrm{div}_{\mathrm{H}}\,\sigma_{\delta}-c_{\mathrm{cor}}v_{\mathrm{ice}}^{\perp}-g\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}H\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{m_{\mathrm{ice}}}(\tau_{\mathrm{atm}}+\tau_{\mathrm{ocn}}(v_{\mathrm{ice}})),&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0pt(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
\partial_{t}h+\mathrm{div}_{\mathrm{H}}\,(v_{\mathrm{ice}}h)&=S_{\mathrm{h}}(h,a),&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0pt(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
\partial_{t}a+\mathrm{div}_{\mathrm{H}}\,(v_{\mathrm{ice}}a)&=S_{\mathrm{a}}(h,a),&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0pt(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
v_{\mathrm{ice}}&=0,&&\kern 5.0pt\text{on}\kern 5.0pt(0,T)\times\partial\Omega,\\
v_{\mathrm{ice}}(0)=v_{\mathrm{ice},0},\kern 5.0pth(0)&=h_{0},\kern 5.0pta(0)=a_{0},&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0pt\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Before stating the main result, we require some further preparation with regard to notation which we address below.
For brevity, we define the spaces
\mathrm{X}_{0}
and
\mathrm{X}_{1}
by

(2.4)

\mathrm{X}_{0}\coloneqq\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)^{2}\times\mathrm{W}^{1,q}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{W}^{1,q}(\Omega)\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt\mathrm{X}_{1}\coloneqq\mathrm{W}^{2,q}(\Omega)^{2}\cap\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)^{2}\times\mathrm{W}^{1,q}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{W}^{1,q}(\Omega),

where
q\in(1,\infty)
.
In the sequel, we will assume that
p
,
q\in(1,\infty)
satisfy

(2.5)

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}<\frac{1}{2}.

With regard to the initial data, for
p
,
q\in(1,\infty)
fulfilling (
2.5
), we then introduce the time trace space
\mathrm{X}_{\gamma}=(\mathrm{X}_{0},\mathrm{X}_{1})_{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}},p}
taking the shape

(2.6)

\mathrm{X}_{\gamma}=\mathrm{B}_{qp,\mathrm{D}}^{2-\nicefrac{{2}}{{p}}}(\Omega)^{2}\times\mathrm{W}^{1,q}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{W}^{1,q}(\Omega).

In the above, the subscript
D
indicates Dirichlet boundary conditions.
For
p
,
q\in(1,\infty)
such that (
2.5
) holds true, it follows from Sobolev embeddings, see for example
[
35
, Theorem 4.6.1]
, that

(2.7)

\mathrm{X}_{\gamma}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{B}_{qp}^{2-\nicefrac{{2}}{{p}}}(\Omega)^{2}\times\mathrm{W}^{1,q}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{W}^{1,q}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{C}^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})^{2}\times\mathrm{C}^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\times\mathrm{C}^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}),

for some
\alpha>0
.
In order to guarantee that the initial values lie in the physically relevant ranges of the respective variables, we introduce an open set
V\subset\mathrm{X}_{\gamma}
with

(2.8)

V\coloneqq\{u=(v_{\mathrm{ice}},h,a)\in\mathrm{X}_{\gamma}:h>\kappa\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pta\in(0,1)\}.

In view of (
2.7
), it is legitimate to make pointwise assumptions on
h
and
a
for
u\in\mathrm{X}_{\gamma}
if (
2.5
) is valid.

For the statement of the main result, we explicitly state the assumptions on the external forcing terms.

Assumption 2.1
.

Let
q\in(1,\infty)
.
We make the following assumptions on the data.

(1)

The wind and ocean velocities
V_{\mathrm{atm}}
and
V_{\mathrm{ocn}}
satisfy
V_{\mathrm{atm}}
,
V_{\mathrm{ocn}}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,t;\mathrm{L}^{2q}(\Omega)^{2})
for all
t>0
.

(2)

For the sea surface dynamic height
H
, it is valid that
\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}H\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,t;\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)^{2})
for all
t>0
.

(3)

It holds that
f_{\mathrm{gr}}\in\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{1}([0,\infty))
for the ice growth rate
f_{\mathrm{gr}}
.

The above assumptions on the integrability and regularity of the data are relatively mild, and it seems that they are physically reasonable.
For examples of ice growth rate functions
f_{\mathrm{gr}}
, which generally depend on the season, we refer for instance to the ones considered by Hibler
[
13
, p. 821]
.

We are now in the position to formulate the main result of this article.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
p,q\in(1,\infty)
be such that (
2.5
) is valid, let
u_{0}\in V
, where
V
was introduced in (
2.8
), and recall the spaces
\mathrm{X}_{0}
and
\mathrm{X}_{1}
from (
2.4
).
Moreover, suppose that the external terms
V_{\mathrm{atm}}
,
V_{\mathrm{ocn}}
,
H
and
f_{\mathrm{gr}}
fulfill
Assumption 2.1
.
Then there is
T>0
such that (
2.3
) has a unique solution
u=(v_{\mathrm{ice}},h,a)
with

u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{0})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{1})\cap\mathrm{C}([0,T];V).

3.
Transformation to Lagrangian coordinates

This section is dedicated to the transformation to Lagrangian coordinates which is a major part of the strategy to show the local strong well-posedness.
The coordinate transform from Eulerian to Lagrangian coordinates involves the characteristics associated to the ice velocity
v_{\mathrm{ice}}
.
In other words,
X
solves

(3.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}X(t,y_{\mathrm{H}})&=v_{\mathrm{ice}}(t,X(t,y_{\mathrm{H}})),&&\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0ptt>0,\\
X(0,y_{\mathrm{H}})&=y_{\mathrm{H}},&&\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0pty_{\mathrm{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.\end{aligned}\right.

Given
t\in(0,T)
, we use
Y(t,\cdot)=[X(t,\cdot)]^{-1}
to denote the inverse of
X(t,\cdot)
, and we will elaborate on the invertibility in more details later on.
The variables in Lagrangian coordinates are then defined by

(3.2)

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice}}(t,y_{\mathrm{H}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq v_{\mathrm{ice}}(t,X(t,y_{\mathrm{H}})),\kern 5.0pt\tilde{h}(t,y_{\mathrm{H}})\coloneqq h(t,X(t,y_{\mathrm{H}}))\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt\tilde{a}(t,y_{\mathrm{H}})\coloneqq a(t,X(t,y_{\mathrm{H}})).

Integrating in time in (

), and inserting the change of variables from (

X(t,y_{\mathrm{H}})=y_{\mathrm{H}}+\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice}}(s,y_{\mathrm{H}})\,\mathrm{d}s.

The main reason for the transform of the Lagrangian coordinates is that this allows us to circumvent the hyperbolic effects in the balance laws.
In fact, it readily follows that the time derivative in Lagrangian coordinates coincides with the material derivative in Eulerian coordinates.
Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice}}(t,y_{\mathrm{H}})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}v_{\mathrm{ice}}(t,X(t,y_{\mathrm{H}}))+(v_{\mathrm{ice}}(t,X(t,y_{\mathrm{H}}))\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}})v_{\mathrm{ice}}(t,X(t,y_{\mathrm{H}})),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{h}(t,y_{\mathrm{H}})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}h(t,X(t,y_{\mathrm{H}}))+v_{\mathrm{ice}}(t,X(t,y_{\mathrm{H}}))\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}h(t,X(t,y_{\mathrm{H}}))\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{a}(t,y_{\mathrm{H}})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}a(t,X(t,y_{\mathrm{H}}))+v_{\mathrm{ice}}(t,X(t,y_{\mathrm{H}}))\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}a(t,X(t,y_{\mathrm{H}})).

We next compute the other transformed terms from (

).
For the internal ice stress, we first recall the so-called
Hibler operator
related to
\mathrm{div}_{\mathrm{H}}\,\sigma_{\delta}
, and we define the matrix
\mathbb{S}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
such that

\mathbb{S}\varepsilon=\begin{pmatrix}\left(1+\frac{1}{e^{2}}\right)\varepsilon_{11}+\left(1-\frac{1}{e^{2}}\right)\varepsilon_{22}&\frac{1}{e^{2}}\left(\varepsilon_{12}+\varepsilon_{21}\right)\\
\frac{1}{e^{2}}\left(\varepsilon_{12}+\varepsilon_{21}\right)&\left(1-\frac{1}{e^{2}}\right)\varepsilon_{11}+\left(1+\frac{1}{e^{2}}\right)\varepsilon_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

An identification of
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
with the vector
(\varepsilon_{11},\varepsilon_{12},\varepsilon_{21},\varepsilon_{22})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
then allows us to interpret the action of
\mathbb{S}
to
\varepsilon
as the multiplication by the matrix

\mathbb{S}=\left(\mathbb{S}_{ij}^{kl}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}1+\frac{1}{e^{2}}&0&0&1-\frac{1}{e^{2}}\\
0&\frac{1}{e^{2}}&\frac{1}{e^{2}}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{e^{2}}&\frac{1}{e^{2}}&0\\
1-\frac{1}{e^{2}}&0&0&1+\frac{1}{e^{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

This also leads to
\triangle^{2}(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon^{\top}\mathbb{S}\varepsilon

Defining
S_{\delta}\coloneqq S_{\delta}(\varepsilon,P)\coloneqq\frac{P}{2}\frac{\mathbb{S}\varepsilon}{\triangle_{\delta}(\varepsilon)}
, we find
\sigma_{\delta}(\varepsilon,P)=S_{\delta}(\varepsilon,P)-\frac{P}{2}\Id_{2}
.
Hibler’s operator is then defined by

\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{H}}v_{\mathrm{ice}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}h}\mathrm{div}_{\mathrm{H}}\,S_{\delta}=\frac{1}{\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}h}\mathrm{div}_{\mathrm{H}}\,\left(\frac{P}{2}\frac{\mathbb{S}\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\delta+\varepsilon^{\top}\mathbb{S}\varepsilon}}\right).

From
[
6
, Section 3]
, we recall its representation

(3.3)

\displaystyle(\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{H}}v_{\mathrm{ice}})_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,l=1}^{2}\frac{P}{2\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}h}\frac{1}{\triangle_{\delta}(\varepsilon)}\Bigl(\mathbb{S}_{ij}^{kl}-\frac{1}{\triangle_{\delta}^{2}(\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{S}\varepsilon)_{ik}(\mathbb{S}\varepsilon)_{lj}\Bigr)\partial_{k}\varepsilon_{jl}+\frac{1}{2\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}h\triangle_{\delta}(\varepsilon)}\sum_{j=1}^{2}(\partial_{j}P)(\mathbb{S}\varepsilon)_{ij},

where
i=1,2

For the principal part, it is advantageous to define coefficients by

(3.4)

a_{ij}^{kl}(\varepsilon,P)\coloneqq-\frac{P}{2\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}h}\frac{1}{\triangle_{\delta}(\varepsilon)}\left(\mathbb{S}_{ij}^{kl}-\frac{1}{\triangle_{\delta}^{2}(\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{S}\varepsilon)_{ik}(\mathbb{S}\varepsilon)_{jl}\right).

At this stage, it is also worthwhile invoking the linearized Hibler operator.
For
u_{0}\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(\overline{\Omega})^{2}\times\mathrm{C}(\overline{\Omega})\times\mathrm{C}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
h_{0}>\kappa

\displaystyle{}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,l=1}^{2}a_{ij}^{kl}(\varepsilon(v_{\mathrm{ice},0}),P(h_{0},a_{0}))\mathrm{D}_{k}\mathrm{D}_{l}v_{\mathrm{ice},j}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}h_{0}\triangle_{\delta}(\varepsilon(v_{\mathrm{ice},0}))}\sum_{j=1}^{2}(\partial_{j}P(h_{0},a_{0}))(\mathbb{S}\varepsilon(v_{\mathrm{ice}}))_{ij}.

The above conditions on

are especially satisfied provided

Concerning the transformed terms, we start with the symmetric part of the gradient, transforming to

For the coefficients as in (

a_{ij}^{klm}(\tilde{\varepsilon}(\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice}}),P(\tilde{h},\tilde{a}))\coloneqq(\partial_{k}Y_{m})a_{ij}^{kl}(\tilde{\varepsilon}(\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice}}),P(\tilde{h},\tilde{a})).

Let us observe that the coefficients

For simplicity of notation, we do not write this dependence explicitly.
Besides, we compute

\partial_{m}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{jl}(v_{\mathrm{ice}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{2}\bigl((\partial_{m}\partial_{j}Y_{n})\partial_{n}\tilde{v}_{ice,l}+(\partial_{j}Y_{n})\partial_{m}\partial_{n}\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice},l}+(\partial_{m}\partial_{l}Y_{n})\partial_{n}\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice},j}+(\partial_{l}Y_{n})\partial_{m}\partial_{n}\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice},j}\bigr).

In view of (

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{H}}(\tilde{u})\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,l,m=1}^{2}a_{ij}^{klm}(\tilde{\varepsilon}(\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice}}),P(\tilde{h},\tilde{a}))\partial_{m}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{jl}(\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice}})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}\tilde{h}\triangle_{\delta}(\tilde{\varepsilon}(\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice}}))}\sum_{j,k=1}^{2}(\partial_{j}Y_{k})(\partial_{k}\tilde{h}+c_{\bullet}\partial_{k}\tilde{a})(\mathbb{S}\tilde{\varepsilon}(\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice}}))_{ij}.

The transformed terms associated to the horizontal divergence of the ice strength

(3.8)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice}}&=\tilde{\mathbb{A}}^{\mathrm{H}}(\tilde{u})\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice}}-\tilde{B}(\tilde{u})\binom{\tilde{h}}{\tilde{a}}-c_{\mathrm{cor}}\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice}}^{\perp}-g\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}H+\frac{1}{\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}\tilde{h}}\tau_{\mathrm{ice}}(\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice}}),&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0pt(0,T)\times\Omega,\\

4.
Linear theory

In this section, we address the linear theory.
More precisely, we establish the boundedness of the
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus of the operator matrix associated to the linearization of the parabolic-hyperbolic sea ice model.
This result is of independent interest due to its interesting functional analytic consequences for the operator matrix such as a characterization of its fractional power domains, or stochastic maximal regularity.
We refer here also to the discussion in
Section 6
.
As a corollary, we especially obtain the maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity.
Note that this (weaker) property is sufficient for the fixed point argument.

First, we recall the
\mathrm{L}^{q}
-realization of the linearized Hibler operator as well as the bounded
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus that it admits.
For
u_{0}\in\mathrm{C}^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})^{2}\times\mathrm{C}^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\times\mathrm{C}^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, with
\alpha>0
and
h_{0}\geq\kappa
, we recall the linearized Hibler operator
\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{H}}(u_{0})
from (
3.5
).
The
\mathrm{L}^{q}
-realization of the linearized Hibler operator subject to Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
is then given by

(4.1)

\left[A_{\mathrm{D}}^{\mathrm{H}}(u_{0})\right]v_{\mathrm{ice}}\coloneqq\left[\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{H}}(u_{0})\right]v_{\mathrm{ice}},\kern 5.0pt\text{with}\kern 5.0pt\mathrm{D}(A_{\mathrm{D}}^{\mathrm{H}}(u_{0}))\coloneqq\mathrm{W}^{2,q}(\Omega)^{2}\cap\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)^{2}.

The following result can be obtained analogously as in
[
6
, Section 4]
.
Let us observe that the reduced regularity in the
h

a
-component does not affect the arguments as the coefficients remain Hölder-continuous, because they depend on

and

in a smooth way.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

as well as

for some
\alpha>0
and with

, and consider

Then there exists

such that

with

-angle

for all

.

Note that the latter lemma especially implies the maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity of

[
11

In fact, mimicking the procedure in

Next, let

and
f_{2}
,
f_{3}\colon(0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
.
Besides, we consider suitable initial data
u_{0}=(v_{\mathrm{ice},0},h_{0},a_{0})
as well as

, for

, and with

and

For
\omega\geq 0
, the linear problem under investigation is given by

(4.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice}}-(\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{H}}(u_{1})-\omega)\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{ice}}+B_{1}(u_{1})\binom{\tilde{h}}{\tilde{a}}&=f_{1},&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0pt(0,T)\times\Omega,\\

As a preparation, we briefly recall some theory on the

To this end, we first invoke the notion of a diagonally dominant block operator matrix.

Definition 4.2
.

Consider Banach spaces
\mathrm{X}
and
\mathrm{Y}
as well as linear operators
A\colon\mathrm{D}(A)\subset\mathrm{X}\to\mathrm{X}
,
B\colon\mathrm{D}(B)\subset\mathrm{Y}\to\mathrm{X}
,
C\colon\mathrm{D}(C)\subset\mathrm{X}\to\mathrm{Y}
and
D\colon\mathrm{D}(D)\subset\mathrm{Y}\to\mathrm{Y}
.
Moreover, set
\mathrm{Z}\coloneqq\mathrm{X}\times\mathrm{Y}
.
If

(1)

the operators
A
and
D
are closed, linear and densely defined, and

(2)

it holds that
C
is relatively
A
-bounded and
B
is relatively
D
-bounded, i. e.,
\mathrm{D}(D)\subset\mathrm{D}(B)
,
\mathrm{D}(A)\subset\mathrm{D}(C)
, and there are constants
c_{A}
,
c_{D}
,
C\geq 0
with

\displaystyle\|Cx\|_{\mathrm{X}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{A}\cdot\|Ax\|_{\mathrm{X}}+L\cdot\|x\|_{\mathrm{X}}\kern 5.0pt\text{for all}\kern 5.0ptx\in\mathrm{D}(A),\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\|By\|_{\mathrm{X}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{D}\cdot\|Dy\|_{\mathrm{Y}}+L\cdot\|y\|_{\mathrm{Y}}\kern 5.0pt\text{for all}\kern 5.0pty\in\mathrm{D}(D),

then the operator matrix
\mathcal{A}\colon\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A})\coloneqq\mathrm{D}(A)\times\mathrm{D}(D)\subset\mathrm{Z}\to\mathrm{Z}
defined by

(4.4)

\mathcal{A}\binom{x}{y}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\binom{x}{y},\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0pt\binom{x}{y}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}),

is called
diagonally dominant
.

The following lemma tailored to our setting is a direct consequence of
[
1

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
as defined in (

\mathrm{X}

\phi_{A}^{\infty}\in[0,\pi)

\phi\in(\phi_{A}^{\infty},\pi)
, there is
\omega\geq 0
such that
\mathcal{A}+\omega\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathrm{Z})
and
\phi_{\mathcal{A}+\omega}^{\infty}\leq\phi
.

Let us note that the diagonal dominance of

together with the assumption

already requires the boundedness of the operator

Theorem 4.4

Proof.

The idea of the proof is to make use of

At this stage, the choice of the anisotropic ground space

For this purpose, we recall from

-A_{\mathrm{D}}^{\mathrm{H}}(u_{1})+\omega\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)^{2})

\phi_{-A_{\mathrm{D}}^{\mathrm{H}}(u_{1})+\omega}^{\infty}<\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}
upon observing that

On the other hand, for such

It thus remains to handle the off-diagonal terms.
With regard to the remark following

The shape of

\left\|\frac{\partial_{h}P(h_{1},a_{1})}{2\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}h_{1}}\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}h\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)}\leq C\cdot\|h\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,q}(\Omega)},\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0pth\in\mathrm{W}^{1,q}(\Omega),

and likewise for the corresponding

Next, let

Employing the Banach algebra structure of
\mathrm{W}^{1,q}(\Omega)
by

6.
Concluding remarks

In this final section, we discuss possible extensions of the results presented in the previous sections, sketch possible difficulties and mention remaining problems.

In order to fully exploit the parabolic regularization, it would be possible to introduce time weights of the shape
t^{1-\mu}
for
\mu\in(\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}},1]
and to consider
\mathrm{L}_{\mu}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{0})
instead of
\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{0})
, where
f\in\mathrm{L}_{\mu}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{0})
if and only if
t^{1-\mu}f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{0})
.
The spaces
\mathrm{W}_{\mu}^{1,p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{0})
and
\mathrm{L}_{\mu}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{1})
are defined analogously.
The underlying data space then is
\mathbb{E}_{0,\mu}\coloneqq\mathrm{L}_{\mu}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{0})
, while the associated time trace space becomes
\mathrm{X}_{\gamma,\mu}\coloneqq(\mathrm{X}_{0},\mathrm{X}_{1})_{\mu-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}},p}
, and the corresponding maximal regularity space reads as

\mathbb{E}_{1,\mu}\coloneqq\mathrm{W}_{\mu}^{1,p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{0})\cap\mathrm{L}_{\mu}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{X}_{1}).

An implementation of time weights would require an adjustment of the nonlinear estimates.
For the theory of critical spaces in the context of evolution equations and weighted spaces, we also refer to the work of Prüss, Simonett and Wilke
[
31
]
.

The bounded
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus of the operator matrix
A(u_{1})
from (
4.3
) as proved in
Theorem 4.4
paves the way for interesting applications such as the theory of Banach scales due to Amann
[
2
, Chapter 5]
or the consideration of the problem in the stochastic setting via stochastic maximal regularity, see also
[
27
]
for more details on this property.

The investigation of global-in-time results as e. g. obtained in
[
6
, Theorem 2.3]
, see also
[
5
, Theorem 3.6.6]
, is left to future study for the present problem.
In fact, there are several difficulties here.
First, the linearized operator matrix
A(u_{*})
at a constant equilibrium solution of the shape
u_{*}=(0,h_{*},a_{*})\in V
seems to be “more singular” than in the fully parabolic situation.
Indeed, zero is no longer a semi-simple eigenvalue, and the lack of invertibility cannot be overcome as for instance in
[
5
, Section 7.2]
by restricting to functions with spatial average zero.
Another difficulty arises in the transfer of global-in-time results from the Lagrangian to the Eulerian setting.
For this, it is necessary to have a global-in-time Lagrangian change of coordinates at hand.
In
[
10
]
, Danchin, Hieber, Mucha and Tolksdorf address this aspect in the context of free boundary value problems in the half space by using Da Prato-Grisvard theory, allowing for maximal
\mathrm{L}^{1}
-regularity in the critical (homogeneous) Besov space.
For a different approach to such problems, we also refer to the work of Ogawa and Shimizu
[
28
]
.

One can also ask whether the results established in
[
7
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
remain valid in the present situation of the parabolic-hyperbolic problem.
We also leave this to future study.
