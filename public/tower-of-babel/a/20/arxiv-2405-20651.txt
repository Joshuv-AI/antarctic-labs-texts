Wetting by active fluids
Yongfeng Zhao (赵永峰) 1,2,†, Ruben Zakine 3, Adrian Daerr 4, Yariv Kafri 5, Julien Tailleur 6, Frédéric van Wijland 4

Abstract
The Young-Dupré equation is a cornerstone of the theory of capillary and wetting phenomena that governs the shape of droplets adsorbed on surfaces in equilibrium. But many living and synthetic materials, such as swarming bacteria and active colloids, are composed of self-propelled particles that are inherently out of thermal equilibrium. The description of the wetting of surfaces by such active fluids thus requires a new framework. Here, we develop an analogue to the Young-Dupré equation for systems made of self-propelled particles. A key step is to define the liquid-gas surface tension of active fluids as the force exerted along the interface, which we show from first principles to be negative, even when active materials separate into stable liquid and gas phases. The active Young-Dupré equation explains why partial wetting appears in simulations where the surface tensions do not balance and reveals the underlying feedback mechanism: the interface is stable only because of steady flows, which are themselves generated by the parity-symmetry-breaking interface. Unlike in passive fluids, where the droplets are scale-free, this feedback loop selects the sizes and shapes of adsorbed droplets in active materials. Our results outline a framework for understanding how active matter wets surfaces.

The Young-Dupré equation describes the equilibrium wetting of a
macroscopic liquid droplet on a solid surface as illustrated
in Fig.
1

\varphi
between the
liquid-gas interface and the solid surface through

\gamma_{\rm GS}-\gamma_{\rm LS}=\gamma_{\rm LG}\cos\varphi\;,

(1)

where
\gamma_{XY}
is the surface tension between phases
X
and
Y

This equation is a cornerstone of the theory of capillary
phenomena
[
1

[
2

[
3

A remarkable property of the Young-Dupré equation is that it endows interfaces with mechanical properties:
Eq. (
1

This macroscopic description emerges despite the fact that interfaces are smeared fluctuating objects at the particle scale where the forces are
actually exerted, see Fig.

In equilibrium, an important challenge has thus
been to bridge these macroscopic ‘forces’ to their microscopic origin,
which results from the interplay between particle interactions and
thermal fluctuations
[
4
]
. Meeting this challenge has
led to a comprehensive understanding of interface physics in
equilibrium
[
1
]
.

1
Active surface (ex)tension: when interfaces push on confining walls.

An inspiring and fruitful starting point consists in directly
exporting equilibrium results to active materials. However, an
important difficulty arises from the continuous injection of energy
and momentum in the steady states of active systems, which has made the very definition of surface tension a challenging task. While a mechanical definition of fluid-solid surface tension has been proposed recently
[
26
]
, the liquid-gas surface tension has
been the topic of much
controversy
[
10
,
27
,
15
,
28
,
23
,
25
]
.
Progress has been made on this question by considering the workhorse
models of self-propelled particles interacting via pairwise
forces
[
29
]
. These systems undergo a Motility-Induced Phase Separation (MIPS) between
a dilute and a dense phase in the presence of repulsive forces at
large enough density and propulsion
speed
[
29
,
30
]
.
Similarly to the passive case, partial wetting is then observed in the presence of
a confining solid surface, as
illustrated in Fig.
1
c.
(All our simulations are detailed in Methods.)

For
such models, an interesting inroad into surface tension was recently
proposed
[
10
]
by assuming that the equilibrium expression
of a liquid-gas surface
tension
[
31
,
32
]
also holds for
active interfaces:

\gamma_{\rm LG}=\int_{{\bf r}_{\rm G}}^{{\bf r}_{\rm L}}dr_{\perp}\,[p_{\perp}-p_{\parallel}(r_{\perp})]\;,

(2)

where
{\bf r}_{\rm G}
and
{\bf r}_{\rm L}
are positions in the bulk gas
and liquid phases, respectively, and
r_{\perp}
is a coordinate normal
to the interface. In Eq. (
2
),
p_{\perp}
is the
pressure normal to the interface while
p_{\parallel}
is the
tangential pressure. However,
Eq. (
2
) has been challenged for a number of
reasons
[
27
,
15
,
28
,
23
]
, starting from the resulting
negative value of
\gamma_{\rm LG}
that, in passive systems, signals an unstable
interface. Furthermore, a
direct
measurement of a force
resulting from the surface tension defined in
Eq. (
2
) is lacking. This has left room for
alternative definitions of
\gamma_{\rm LG}
that result in a positive
value
[
27
,
15
,
23
,
25
]
. We note that active field theories, which have proven useful to account for the self-organization of active-matter systems
[
33
,
34
]
, are here of little help: since active fluids do not admit a free energy, there is no ambiguity-free way to couple external potentials and density fields directly at the coarse-grained scale. This prohibits a top-down approach to active mechanics, which would start from an active field theory and predict the force exerted by the corresponding active system on its environment.

Instead, starting from first principles, we show that Eq. (
2
)
can be derived from microscopics, leading to a surface tension
\gamma_{\rm LG}
that is a measurable force exerted along interfaces, and that this force is indeed negative. To do so, we consider self-propelled particles
interacting via pairwise forces, such that the dynamics of particle

i
at position
\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}
and orientation
\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{i}
reads:

\dot{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}_{i}=v_{0}\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{i}-\mu\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla_{\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}}U(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{j})-\mu\nabla_{\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}}U_{w}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i})\;,

(3)

where
v_{0}
is the particle propulsion speed,
\mu
its
mobility and
U
a repulsive pair potential. When the system is in presence of a confining
solid boundary, we model the latter as a repulsive potential

U_{w}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
, following the seminal work of Navascués and
Berry
[
32
]
. For the sake of concreteness, we consider
overdamped active Brownian particles (ABPs) in two
dimensions
[
29
]
so that

\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{i}=(\cos\theta_{i},\sin\theta_{i})
and the orientations evolve as

\dot{\theta}_{i}=\sqrt{2/\tau_{p}}\,\xi_{i}
, with
\tau_{p}
the persistence
time and
\xi_{i}
a unit-variance centered Gaussian white noise. We
note, however, that our results should straightforwardly extend to
d=3
dimensions and to
the broader class of systems whose particle-propulsion forces have an
autonomous dynamics, which warrants the existence of an equation of
state for the pressure
[
35
,
36
]
. As an example, we generalize our results to active
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck particles
[
5
,
37
]
in SM Section
S12
.

For the class of models we consider, the sole hydrodynamic field is the conserved density

\rho(\bm{\mathrm{r}},t)=\langle\sum_{i}\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}(t))\rangle
, whose dynamics reads
[
38
,
39
]
:

\dot{\rho}=-\nabla\cdot\bm{\mathrm{J}};\quad\text{with}\quad\mu^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{J}}=\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}-\rho\nabla_{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}U_{w}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\;,

(4)

where
\bm{\mathrm{J}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=\langle\sum_{i}\dot{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}_{i}\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}(t))\rangle
is
the particle current (See SM Section
S2
). In Eq. (
4
),

\bm{\sigma}=\bm{\sigma}^{\rm IK}+\bm{\sigma}^{\rm a}
acquires the
physical meaning of a stress tensor in the steady state, so that the pressure in a bulk phase is
p=-{\rm Tr}\bm{\sigma}/2

[
40
]
. The stress tensor comprises
two
contributions
[
41
,
42
,
40
,
39
]
:

\bm{\sigma}^{\rm IK}
is the standard Irving-Kirkwood contribution
stemming from the pairwise interactions between the particles and
\bm{\sigma}^{\rm a}
is a contribution due to the active
forces.
Equation (
4
) is well established
[
40
,
39
,
26
]
: it relates the drag density exerted on the
particles,
{\bf f}_{d}\equiv-\mu^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{J}}
, and the stress tensor

\bm{\sigma}
to any external force applied to the system; it is the starting point of our theory.

To derive the liquid-gas surface tension from first principles, we consider a phase-separated system in a rectangular box, with confining walls along the
\hat{y}
direction at the top and bottom of the system and periodic boundary conditions along the horizontal
\hat{x}
direction, as shown in Fig.
2
a.
To endow the
definition (
2
) with a proper mechanical
interpretation, we compute the
total force exerted on the upper wall by the particles,
F_{w,\rm tot}=\int_{0}^{L_{x}}dx\int_{y_{b}}^{\infty}dy\rho(x,y)\partial_{y}U_{w}(y)
(See SM Section
S3
for further details). At the macroscopic scale,
F_{w,\rm tot}
stems both from the contributions of the bulk gas and liquid pressures,
p_{\rm G}
and
p_{\rm L}
, and from interface contributions that we use to identify the liquid-gas surface tension.
Using Eq. (
4
), we integrate
J_{y}\equiv\bm{\mathrm{J}}\cdot\hat{y}
over the
upper half of the system, for
y>y_{b}
and
0\leq x\leq L_{x}
,
which yields

F_{w,\rm tot}=-\int_{0}^{L_{x}}\!\!\!\!\!dx^{\prime}\sigma_{yy}(x^{\prime},y_{b})-\int_{y\geq y_{b}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!d^{2}{\bf r}\frac{J_{y}}{\mu}\;.

(5)

In the steady-state,
\nabla\cdot{\bf J}=0
implies that the integral of
J_{y}
vanishes.
Furthermore, we can add and subtract
\int_{0}^{L_{x}}\!dx^{\prime}\sigma_{xx}(x^{\prime},y_{b})=-\ell_{\rm G}p_{\rm G}-\ell_{\rm L}p_{\rm L}
to Eq. (
5
), where
\ell_{{\rm G}/{\rm L}}
are the total lengths of the gas and liquid
phases, respectively. This leads to

F_{w,\rm tot}=-n_{{\rm LG}}\gamma_{\rm LG}+\ell_{\rm G}p_{\rm G}+\ell_{\rm L}p_{\rm L}\;,

(6)

where
n_{\rm LG}
is the number of liquid-gas
interfaces, allowing us to identify the liquid-gas surface tension

\gamma_{\rm LG}
as

\gamma_{\rm LG}=\int_{x_{\rm L}}^{x_{\rm G}}dx\,[\sigma_{yy}(x,y_{b})-\sigma_{xx}(x,y_{b})]\;.

(7)

Equation (
6
) is the first central result of our work: it unambiguously defines the liquid-gas surface tension
\gamma_{\rm LG}
as the contribution to the force exerted on the upper wall due to the presence of interfaces. It matches our equilibrium intuition and Eq. (
7
) is indeed equivalent to the one proposed in
Eq. (
2
), thanks to the identification

p_{\perp}=-\sigma_{xx}(x,y)
and
p_{\parallel}(x,y)=-\sigma_{yy}(x,y)

[
39
,
43
,
15
,
44
]
. Since Eq. (
7
) provides a way to measure
\gamma_{\rm LG}
in a phase-separated system without confining walls, the force exerted by the active interface on the upper wall in Eq. (
6
) is independent of the wall properties: the mechanical surface tension thus obeys an equation of state.
As shown in Fig.
2
b,
Eqs. (
6
) and (
7
) agree quantitatively in
our simulations over a wide range of persistence lengths,
independently of the choice of the wall potential.
The

negative
value of the surface tension is also confirmed: the
interface exerts a repulsive, outward force on the upper and bottom
walls, in contrast with the equilibrium case.
Our results thus settle the long-standing debate on the liquid-gas surface tension in active fluids: the mechanical surface tension can be unambiguously defined as Eq. (
7
) and measured as Eq. (
6
). All other definitions proposed in the literature
[
23
,
25
,
27
,
45
]
—which may play an important role in coarsening
[
46
]
, interface dynamics
[
17
]
, or nucleation theory
[
47
]
—are not related to mechanics, and should probably not be referred to as
tensions
.

2
The Wilhelmy plate experiment

The negative value of the liquid-gas surface tension
can then be demonstrated using a setup inspired by the Wilhelmy plate
experiment
[
48
]
. In the latter, a plate—or a
ring in a variant introduced by Pockels and du
Noüy—is inserted into a liquid-gas interface and
slowly pulled out of the liquid. In equilibrium, the positive surface
tension
\gamma_{\rm LG}
then exerts a restoring force on the plate,
pulling it back into the liquid and resisting the outward motion. The
measurement of the force on the plate can then be used to extract

\gamma_{\rm LG}
.

Figure
2
c shows a similar experiment carried out with a disk slowly
pulled out of the liquid phase. As in
the Wilhelmy-plate experiment, a capillary bridge forms, leading to a
deformation of the interface. In our active bath, we then expect
surface tension to induce a net force on the plate, and the negative
value of
\gamma_{\rm LG}
to translate into an
outward

force. To test this prediction, we then release our plate at the
configuration shown in Fig.
2
c, and let its position
{\bf r}_{\rm p}(t)
evolve
according to:

\displaystyle M\ddot{\bf r}_{p}=-\sum_{i}\nabla_{{\bf r}_{\rm p}}U_{w}({\bf r}_{\rm p}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i})\;,

(8)

where
M
is the mass of the plate and
U_{w}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
now describes the
interaction potential between the plate and the active particles. Four
representative trajectories are displayed in Fig.
2
d, which all
show the
expulsion
of the plate out of the liquid phase, in
stark contrast with the passive case. (See also SM Movie 1.) The expulsion of the plate by
the fluid is an unambiguous demonstration that the surface tension of
active fluids, understood in its traditional mechanical sense, is
indeed negative. As shown in SM Section
S4
, the outward average force exerted on
the plate can be related quantitatively to the negative liquid-gas
surface tension starting from the mechanical balance
(
4
) between the drag, the divergence of the stress
tensor, and the external force.

3
The active Young-Dupré equation

Our derivation of Eq. (
7
) as a
bona
fide
liquid-gas surface tension comes with a challenge: In
the stable partial wetting situation shown in Fig.
2
a, all surface
tensions can now be measured, but they lead to violations of the
Young-Dupré equation (
1
). Indeed, our measurements, detailed in Methods, give
\gamma_{\rm LS}=-631\pm 6
,
\gamma_{\rm GS}=-907\pm 5
, and
\gamma_{\rm LG}=-192\pm 3
in our simulation units
so:

|\gamma_{\rm GS}-\gamma_{\rm LS}|>|\gamma_{\rm LG}|\;.

(9)

According to the passive Young-Dupré Eq. (
1
), this mechanical imbalance implies a complete dewetting.

To account for mechanical equilibrium at the triple
point, we integrate the projection of Eq. (
4
) along

\hat{x}
over the region
\mathcal{S}
shown in Fig.
3
a,
which encompasses the liquid-gas interface up to a height
y_{I}
. As detailed in SM Section
S5
, the generalization of the derivation of Eq. (
6
) detailed above leads to

0=\gamma_{\rm LS}-\gamma_{\rm GS}-y_{I}\Delta p+\gamma_{\rm LG}\cos\phi(y_{I})+F_{D}(y_{I})\;,

(10)

where the fluid-solid surface tensions are defined
as
[
26
]

\gamma_{\rm GS,LS}=y_{I}p_{\rm G,L}+\int_{-\infty}^{y_{I}}dy\,\sigma_{xx}(x_{\rm G/L},y)
,

\phi(y_{I})
is the angle between the liquid-gas interface and the
horizontal axis at the interface coordinate
(x_{I},y_{I})
, and
F_{D}=\iint_{\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{f}_{d}\cdot\hat{x}\,d^{2}{\bf r}
is
the total drag exerted on the particles along
\hat{x}
in the region

\mathcal{S}
. We note that, contrary to the case depicted in
Fig.
1
for which the Young-Dupré law is typically derived, the
liquid-gas interface is curved in our simulations, leading—as in passive systems—to
\Delta p\equiv p_{\rm L}-p_{\rm G}\neq 0
. As we show below, the drag, which is absent in the equilibrium Young-Dupré equation, is the key ingredient that allows for partial wetting by resolving the apparent contradiction found in Eq. (
9
).

We now use the simulations shown in Fig.
3
to test the
prediction for the contact angle
\varphi
. To do so, we need to
evaluate each term entering Eq. (
10
). The
surface tensions can be evaluated in the bulk of the system, where
they are independent of
y_{I}
. Then, a natural definition of the
contact angle
\varphi
amounts to taking the limit of
\phi(y_{I})
as
y_{I}\to 0
. In this limit, the contribution of the pressure difference
vanishes and we recover the active Young-Dupré equation:

\gamma_{\rm GS}-\gamma_{\rm LS}=\gamma_{\rm LG}\cos\varphi+F_{D}(0)\;,

(11)

where
F_{D}(0)
is the drag experienced by particles in contact with
the wall. Figure
3
a compares the prediction of the active Young-Dupré
equation with the liquid-gas interface measured in numerical
simulations, leading to a very good agreement between measured and
predicted contact angles. As expected, the macroscopic contact angle
describes the behaviour of the smoothly varying interface at
y\geq\ell_{p}
as it approaches the bottom wall, and it does not resolve the
microscopic structure on the scale
y<\ell_{p}
. In SM Fig.
S7
, we show that our theory holds for other persistence times and wall
stiffnesses. Figure
S8
also shows how all observables entering the active Young-Dupré equation vary with the particle stiffness, leading to a wetting transition in the limit of soft particles.

Note that our active Young-Dupré equation can be used to predict the full shape of the interface by
writing that

\cot\phi(y_{I})=x_{I}^{\prime}(y_{I})
. Equation (
10
) can
then be turned into a differential equation for
x_{I}(y_{I})
, whose
solution reads

x_{I}(y_{I})-x_{I}(L_{y}/2)=\int_{L_{y}/2}^{y_{I}}dy\,\frac{\gamma_{\rm LS}-\gamma_{\rm GS}+F_{D}(y)-y\Delta p}{\sqrt{\gamma_{\rm LG}^{2}-[\gamma_{\rm LS}-\gamma_{\rm GS}+F_{D}(y)-y\Delta p]^{2}}}\;.

(12)

The predictions of Eq. (
12
) are shown as red curves in
Fig.
3
c, and compare well with the interfaces measured in the
simulations. The Active Young-Dupré equation can thus be used to
account for the full interface shapes.

Finally, we stress that partial wetting, leading to a finite contact angle, is only possible thanks to the strong vortex flow created at the triple point (Fig.
3
b). Indeed, solving Eq. (
12
) for
F_{D}=0
predicts a circular arc of radius
\gamma_{\rm LG}/\Delta p
, which is shown in green in Fig.
3
c. In all our simulations, this current-free solution systematically fails to describe the interface. Partial wetting is thus only possible in this system thanks to the presence of steady-state currents.

We note that, contrary to standard active ratchets
[
49
]
, steady currents are not here a simple consequence of parity symmetry breaking by an external potential since, unlike in
[
26
]
, the confining walls are invariant by translation along
\hat{x}
. Instead the currents emerge from a subtle feedback mechanism: They result from the spontaneous symmetry breaking induced by the liquid-gas interface, which is itself stable only thanks to the currents.

Let us now show that, in addition to their role in stabilizing partial wetting, the steady currents also lead to a physics of wetting very different from that of equilibrium systems.

4
Droplet instability and intermittent dynamics

In equilibrium, the solution of the passive Young-Dupré
equation—including its correction due to Laplace pressure—is a
circular segment with a fixed contact angle such that

\cos\varphi_{\rm eq}=\frac{\gamma_{\rm GS}-\gamma_{\rm LS}}{\gamma_{\rm LG}}
(Fig.
4
a). Increasing the average
density then leads to droplets of increasing radii at fixed contact
angles
\varphi_{\rm eq}
, that can all be collapsed onto a master
curve by dividing the interface coordinate
(x_{I},y_{I})
by the droplet
height
h
: The equilibrium system admits scale-free
solutions illustrated in Fig.
4
b.

In the active case, the active Young-Dupré equation also includes the
drag force, and the Laplace pressure is dressed by currents (See
SM Section
S6
). Since the current field is a complex, non-local functional of the full system configuration, droplets of increasing sizes lead to different values of
F_{D}
, so that the droplet interface cannot be scale free. In particular, a macroscopic droplet
requires a diverging radius of curvature, leading to a locally
straight interface, as depicted in Fig.
1
a. This requires
an angle
\phi(y_{b})
independent of
y_{b}
, which is in direct
contradiction with the fact that
F_{D}(y_{b})
varies with
y_{b}
. As
such, we expect the active Young-Dupré equation to forbid macroscopic
active droplets.

To test this prediction, we simulated droplets of increasing sizes and
report their average density profiles and interfaces on
Figs.
4
d-f and Fig.
4
c, respectively. As the
density increases, not only do the average profiles vary, but large
droplets break into smaller ones (Fig.
4
f). First, the drag
force selects admissible droplet sizes, hence breaking the scale-free
nature of the passive system. Then, like any object embedded in an
active fluid (see
[
50
]
for a review), the droplets have complex interactions mediated by the particle currents. The result is a rich intermittent dynamics with many merging and splitting
events illustrated in Fig.
4
g-i and in SM Movie 2.
Note that, for the same parameters, Fig.
2
a shows that phase-separated profiles are stable over time in the absence of confining walls. (See also SM Section
S9
and SM Movies 3 and 4.) The droplet instability reported here thus has nothing to do with well established bulk instabilities leading to microphase separation
[
46
,
20
]
.

For larger persistence lengths, we observe a second scenario leading to droplet size selection, distinct from the growing-and-splitting mechanism reported in Fig.
4
. Indeed, SM Fig.
S9
and Movie 5 show a reversed Ostwald-ripening phenomenon leading to the growth of small distant droplets at the expense of the large initial droplet. Again, this is a surface effect since systems of comparable sizes show a standard Ostwald ripening when the confining walls are replaced by periodic boundary conditions, leading to complete phase separation.

All in all, our results thus show that the physics of adsorbed active droplets is very rich. The active Young-Dupré equation that we have derived here captures their mechanical equilibria. Together with recent progress in predicting the emergent currents in active fluids
[
50
]
, our results thus pave the way to a complete theory for the dynamics and instability mechanisms of adsorbed droplets.

5
Conclusion.

In this article, we have laid out the foundations for a theory of capillary phenomena in active matter, from the determination of forces exerted along interfaces to the physics of active wetting and adsorbed droplets.

First, we have provided an unambiguous first-principles derivation of the liquid-gas surface tension
\gamma_{\rm LG}
of an active fluid undergoing motility-induced phase separation. Our results show that the liquid-gas interfaces push on the confining wall, in contrast to equilibrium fluids: the mechanical liquid-gas surface tension is indeed negative. An important prediction that can be tested experimentally is the expulsion of partially-immersed objects from the liquid phase, which, again, is at odds with equilibrium phenomenology.

Second, deriving an Active Young-Dupré equation, we have identified the key ingredient that allows partial wetting in active fluids undergoing motility-induced phase separation.
The breaking of parity symmetry in the vicinity of the triple point leads to the emergence of large vortices of steady-state particle currents. The resulting steady drag stabilizes the imbalance between the mechanical surface tensions at play, leading to a finite contact angle at the triple point. These steady currents lead to new physics for adsorbed droplets: their large-scale structure and their non-local dependence on the system configuration prevent the existence of scale-free droplet solutions, leading instead to a rich dynamics with intermittent merging and splitting events.

Our results should first be put to work in the context of synthetic active colloids
[
51
,
52
]
, for which quantitative contacts between microscopic active models and experiments have already been achieved
[
53
,
54
,
55
]
. Recent results on the partial wetting of solid boundaries by active fluids appear as an ideal test ground for our theory
[
56
]
. Partial wetting is also observed in many biological active systems
[
57
,
58
,
59
,
19
,
21
,
60
]
.
There, in addition to the steady drag, our Active Young-Dupré equation will also need to comprise terms to account for possible momentum sources that would violate the equations of state for pressure and surface tension
[
38
,
61
]
, or for the coupling to momentum-conserving fluids. Already, the physics that we uncovered will have to be considered when studying the wetting of surfaces by cellular tissues
[
60
]
.

Methods

9.1
Simulations of active and passive systems.

Here we describe the models used for active and passive simulations, the modeling of the particle-wall interactions, and the numerical implementations of all models.

Active dynamics.

Our complete active dynamics read

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}_{i}

\displaystyle=v_{0}\bm{\mathrm{u}}(\theta_{i})-\mu\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla_{\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}}U(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{j})-\mu\nabla_{\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}}U_{w}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i})\;,

(13)

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2/\tau_{p}}\,\xi_{i}\;.

(14)

The particle interaction potential is modelled using a stiff harmonic potential:

U(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=\epsilon\left(1-\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)^{2}\Theta(r_{0}-r)\;,

(15)

with
\epsilon=50
and
r_{0}=1
.
\Theta
is the Heaviside step function. This choice of interaction potential allows for large time-steps and fast simulations but we checked that our results are robust to the detailed form of the repulsive potentials by also using a WCA potential:

U_{\rm WCA}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=4\epsilon\left(\frac{\sigma^{12}}{r^{12}}-\frac{\sigma^{6}}{r^{6}}\right)\Theta(r_{0}-r)\;,

(16)

where
r_{0}=2^{1/6}\sigma
.

In all our simulations, the self-propulsion speed was set to
v_{0}=5
. The persistence length
\ell_{p}=v_{0}\tau_{p}
was varied by changing
\tau_{p}
from
20/3
to
100
.

Passive dynamics.

For the passive dynamics, we consider
N
coupled overdamped Langevin equations in
d=2
space dimensions, given by

\dot{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}_{i}=-\mu\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla_{\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}}U(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{j})-\mu\nabla_{\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}}U_{w}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i})+\sqrt{2D}\bm{\eta}_{i}\;,

(17)

where
i,j
are the particle labels,
D=\mu k_{B}T
is the particle diffusivity, and
\bm{\eta}_{i}
is a zero-mean Gaussian white noise satisfying
\langle\eta_{i,\alpha}(t)\eta_{j,\beta}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
. As in the active case, we always take
\mu=1
.

The particle interaction potential is modelled using a piece-wise polynomial function for
U(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
, with a short-range repulsion and an attractive tail, which allows us to tune independently the repulsion and attraction strengths:

U(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=\epsilon_{r}\left(1-\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)^{2}\Theta(r_{0}-r)-\epsilon_{a}\frac{r}{r_{0}}\left(3-\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)^{2}\Theta(3r_{0}-r)\;.

(18)

We again use
r_{0}=1
. For all passive simulations, we used
D=7
,
\epsilon_{r}=100
,
\epsilon_{a}=1
, which leads to phase separation.

Wall-particle interactions.

Since we use overdamped Langevin dynamics, we have to resolve explicitly the interactions between particles and walls. To do so, we model them using an explicit potential
U_{w}
.
Most of our active simulations rely on a harmonic repulsive potential. For instance, the top confining wall is modelled as

U_{w}(x,y;\epsilon_{w})=\epsilon_{w}(y-L_{y})^{2}\Theta(y-L_{y})\;.

(19)

For passive particles, repulsive walls do not lead to wetting and we thus also include attractive tails in the wall-particle interactions using the same functional form as Eq. (
18
). This time, the—say—top confining wall is modelled as

U_{w}(x,y;\epsilon_{wa})=\epsilon_{wr}\left(y-L_{y}-2r_{w0}\right)^{2}\Theta(y-L_{y}-2r_{w0})-\epsilon_{wa}(y-L_{y})^{2}(3r_{w0}-y+L_{y})\Theta(y-L_{y})\;.

In all the passive simulations, we use
r_{w0}=1
and
\epsilon_{wr}=100
, and
\epsilon_{wa}=5
, which leads to partial wetting.

When needed, the bottom and side walls are built using the same confining potentials.

Numerical implementations.

To simulate the active dynamics, we use Euler time discretization with time step
\delta t=10^{-3}
to integrate Eqs. (
13
)-(
14
). To simulate the passive dynamics, we use Heun’s method
[
1
]
with time step
\delta t=10^{-4}
to integrate Eq. (
17
).

In both cases, the forces between the particles are calculated using cell lists: The system is binned into cells of linear size equal to the interaction range (
r_{0}
for active particles,
3r_{0}
for passive particles). The forces on the particles in a cell are then calculated by considering possible interactions with the particles in the same cell and in the
8
neighbouring cells.

Due to the heavy numerical requirements of the project, it was necessary to use a C++ code parallelized using message passing interface (MPI) to allow for large-scale long-time simulations. To enable parallel computing, the simulation box is thus divided into blocks governed by different threads. The positions of the particles in the cells at the block boundaries are communicated to the neighbouring threads after every time step. To give a feeling of the required numerical effort, the simulations appearing in Fig. 2c-d and Fig. 3 comprise roughly
6\times 10^{5}
particles. These were run using 32 cores of AMD EPYC Zen 3 CPUs with maximal frequency of 3.5GHz and each simulation took around 60 days. The total CPU (wall) time spent in Fig. 2 and Fig. 3 is estimated around
10^{2}
CPU years and the overall numerical effort to produce the figures appearing in the main text and the SI is estimated to 10 centuries of CPU time.

Finally, we note that active matter has been doomed with strong finite-size effects since the birth of the field 30 years ago. Typically, boundary layers and other microscopic structures survive up to scales comparable to the persistence length, which both leads to rich physics
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
as well as make, e.g., the order of phase transitions very hard to assess
[
5
,
6
]
. Wetting is no exception to this rule, which explains the numerical effort required to produce the figures included in this article. The reason is that the values of the state variables like pressure and surface tension have to be measured in bulk phases, at macroscopic distances from the boundaries (See SM Section
S11
for a discussion of the finite-size effects).

9.2
Measurement of the stress-tensor field (Figures 2 & 3).

Most measurements that appear in this article are based on the spatial integration of Eq. (
4
). As a result, the data that we need to record are also spatial integrals. In practice, we used the cell list to compute these integrals, as well as to produce the heat maps that are shown in the figures.

We store the integral of the stress tensor over the
(i,j)
-th cell, centered on
(x_{i},y_{j})=((i+1/2)r_{0},(j+1/2)r_{0})
, which is computed as

\sigma(x_{i},y_{j})=\frac{1}{r_{0}^{2}}\int_{ir_{0}}^{(i+1)r_{0}}dx\int_{jr_{0}}^{(j+1)r_{0}}dy\,\sigma(x,y)\;.

(20)

In turn, the various contributions entering the stress tensor are computed as:

\displaystyle\rho(x_{i},y_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{0}^{2}}\left\langle\sum_{k\sim(i,j)}1\right\rangle\;,

(21)

\displaystyle I^{(1)}_{\alpha\beta}(x_{i},y_{j})+I^{\rm ext}_{\alpha\beta}(x_{i},y_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{0}^{2}}\left\langle\sum_{k\sim(i,j)}f_{k,\beta}u_{k,\alpha}\right\rangle\;,

(22)

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha\beta}(x_{i},y_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{0}^{2}}\left\langle\sum_{k\sim(i,j)}\left(u_{k,\alpha}u_{k,\beta}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\right)\right\rangle\;,

(23)

where
f_{k,\alpha}=-\sum_{l}\partial_{\alpha}U(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{k}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{l})-\partial_{\alpha}U_{w}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{k})
is the total force experienced by particle
k
and
k\sim(i,j)
denotes a summation over particles in the
(i,j)
-th cell. The integration of the IK tensor over cell
(i,j)
requires a summation over pairs of interacting particles. This includes both particles located in cell
(i,j)
as well as particles located at
\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{k}
and
\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\ell}
such that
\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{k\ell}\equiv\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{k}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\ell}
has a finite intersection with cell
(i,j)
. The summation is then carried out as
[
7
]
:

\sigma^{\rm IK}_{\alpha\beta}(x_{i},y_{j})=\frac{1}{2r_{0}^{2}}\sum_{k\vee\ell}\langle\tilde{r}_{k\ell,\alpha}\partial_{\beta}U(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{k}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\ell})\rangle\;,

(24)

where
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}_{k\ell}
is the fraction of
\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{k\ell}
that lies inside the
(i,j)
-th cell and
k\vee\ell
refers to pairs such that the line segment going from
\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{k}
to
\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\ell}
has a non-zero intersection with the cell
(i,j)
(see Fig.
5
).

Figure 5:
The definition of
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}_{k\ell}
in different scenarios. The squares represent cell
(i,j)
.

The time average of the observables defined in Eqs. (
20
)-(
24
) is then calculated by averaging over repeated measurements carried out every two time units. Note that our cell list includes extra space at
x,y\leq 0
and
x,y\geq L_{x},L_{y}
to properly measure the full stress-tensor field.

9.7
Estimation of statistical errors.

Throughout the article, error bars represent statistical uncertainties, estimated as three times the standard error of the mean of the measured observables. To estimate the standard error of the mean, we use a block-average strategy
[
8
]
. First, we sample our observables, denoted
X(t)
for clarity, at high frequency, every
\Delta t=2
time units. We then calculate a block average
\bar{X}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}X(t+k\Delta t)
using a block size
N=50
. Since
N\Delta t\geq\tau_{p}
, it is reasonable to assume that our successive measurements of
\bar{X}(t)
are uncorrelated and we indeed checked for all our observables that this was the case. Then denoting
\bar{X}_{i}=\bar{X}(iN\Delta t)
, we record the accumulated sum of
\bar{X}_{i}
and
\bar{X}_{i}^{2}
respectively, and estimate the standard error as

(28)

where
M
is the number of blocks and
\sigma_{\bar{X}}
denotes standard deviation
\sigma^{2}_{\bar{X}}=M^{-1}\sum_{i}\bar{X}_{i}^{2}-M^{-2}(\sum_{i}\bar{X}_{i})^{2}
. Provided
N
is large enough, this estimate is unaffected by the correlations in
X(t)

[
8
]
.

For spatial averages, we assume (and verify numerically) that the fluctuations of the density and stress fields are spatially uncorrelated in homogeneous bulk phases on our coarse-graining scale.

S2
Time-evolution of the density field and generalized stress tensor

To make this manuscript self-contained and to use consistent notations, we follow
[
39
]
and construct explicitly the dynamics of the density field of active particles interacting via pairwise forces that are given in Eq. (
3
). We first introduce the density, orientation and nematic-order fluctuating fields, which are defined as:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},t)=\sum_{i}\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}(t))\;,\ \hat{\bm{\mathrm{m}}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},t)=\sum_{i}\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{i}\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}(t))\;,

(S1)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\mathrm{Q}}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},t)=\sum_{i}\left[\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(t)\otimes\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(t)-\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{I}\right]\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}(t))\;,

(S2)

where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity matrix, and
d=2
is the space dimension. Their statistical averages are denoted by
\rho
,
\bm{\mathrm{m}}
, and
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
, respectively.

We start from the evolution equation for the single-particle density function
\Psi(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\theta,t)=\langle\sum_{i}\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}(t))\delta(\theta-\theta_{i}(t))\rangle
, which measures the average density of particles at position
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
with orientation
\theta
at time
t
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}\cdot\left(-v_{0}\bm{\mathrm{u}}(\theta)\Psi+\mu\int d\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}d\theta^{\prime}\,\Psi^{(2)}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\theta,\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime},\theta^{\prime},t)\nabla_{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}U(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime})+\mu\Psi\nabla_{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}U_{w}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\right)

(S3)

\displaystyle+D\Delta_{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}\Psi+\tau_{p}^{-1}\partial_{\theta\theta}\Psi\;.

We include translational diffusion
D=\mu k_{B}T
for generality. The equation contains the two-particle density function
\Psi^{(2)}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\theta,\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime},\theta^{\prime},t)
:

\Psi^{(2)}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\theta,\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime},\theta^{\prime},t)=\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k\neq i}\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}(t))\delta(\theta-\theta_{i}(t))\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{k}(t))\delta(\theta^{\prime}-\theta_{k}(t))\right\rangle

(S4)

and it is thus not closed. The dynamics of
\Psi^{(2)}
can be calculated in a similar fashion, and it involves the three-particle density function
\Psi^{(3)}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\theta,\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime},\theta^{\prime},\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime},t)
. This procedure can be carried out sequentially to obtain an infinite series of equations, similar to the BBGKY hierarchy for equilibrium systems.

Next we use Eq. (
S3
) to calculate the dynamics of the average density field
\rho(\bm{\mathrm{r}},t)
. For the sake of clarity, we use
\partial_{\alpha}\equiv\partial/\partial r_{\alpha}
and always sum over repeated indices. The evolution equation of
\rho
can be calculated by integrating Eq. (
S3
) over
\theta
to get

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=-\partial_{\alpha}J_{\alpha}\;.

(S5)

The dynamics Eq. (
S5
) has the form of a continuity equation
where the current
{\bf J}
is defined as

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle v_{0}m_{\alpha}-\mu\int d\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}\,\rho^{(2)}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime},t)\partial_{\alpha}U(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime})-\mu\rho\partial_{\alpha}U_{w}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})-D\partial_{\alpha}\rho

(S6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{0}m_{\alpha}+\mu\partial_{\beta}\sigma^{\rm IK}_{\alpha\beta}-\mu\rho\partial_{\alpha}U_{w}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})-D\partial_{\alpha}\rho\;.

(S7)

In Eq. (
S6
), we have defined the two-particle density

\rho^{(2)}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime},t)=\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k\neq i}\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}(t))\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{k}(t))\right\rangle\;,

(S8)

and
\sigma^{\rm IK}
in Eq. (
S7
) is the Irving-Kirkwood stress tensor
[
39
,
1
]

\sigma^{\mathrm{IK}}_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{2}\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}\,\frac{r^{\prime}_{\alpha}r^{\prime}_{\beta}}{r^{\prime}}\frac{dU(r^{\prime})}{dr^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{1}d\lambda\,\rho^{(2)}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}+(1-\lambda)\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\lambda\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime},t)\;.

(S9)

Furthermore, from Eq. (
S3
) we determine the dynamics of
m_{\alpha}
as,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial m_{\alpha}}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle\partial_{\beta}\left(-v_{0}Q_{\alpha\beta}+\mu\int d\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}\,\partial_{\beta}U(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime})\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k\neq i}u_{\alpha}(\theta_{i})\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}(t))\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{k}(t))\right\rangle\right.

\displaystyle+\mu m_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}U_{w}+D\partial_{\beta}m_{\alpha}\Biggr)-\partial_{\alpha}\left(\frac{v_{0}\rho}{d}\right)-\frac{m_{\alpha}}{\tau_{p}}\;.

(S10)

We note that Eq. (
S10
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial m_{\alpha}}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle\partial_{\beta}\left(-v_{0}Q_{\alpha\beta}-\mu I^{(1)}_{\alpha\beta}-\mu I^{\rm ext}_{\alpha\beta}+D\partial_{\beta}m_{\alpha}-\frac{v_{0}\rho}{d}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\right)-\frac{m_{\alpha}}{\tau_{p}}\;,

(S11)

where
\delta_{\alpha\beta}
is the Kronecker delta, and we define for clarity:

\displaystyle I^{(1)}_{\alpha\beta}=

\displaystyle-\int d\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}\,\partial_{\beta}U(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime})\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k\neq i}u_{\alpha}(\theta_{i})\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{i}(t))\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{k}(t))\right\rangle\;,

(S12)

\displaystyle I^{\rm ext}_{\alpha\beta}=

\displaystyle-m_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}U_{w}\;.

(S13)

In the steady state, we have that
\partial_{t}\rho=\partial_{t}m_{\alpha}=0
, leading to
\partial_{\alpha}J_{\alpha}=0
so that
J_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},t)
is divergence-free, and Eq. (
S11
) leads to

m_{\alpha}=\tau_{p}\partial_{\beta}\left(-v_{0}Q_{\alpha\beta}-\mu I^{(1)}_{\alpha\beta}-\mu I^{\rm ext}_{\alpha\beta}+D\partial_{\beta}m_{\alpha}-\frac{v_{0}\rho}{d}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\right)\;.

(S14)

Substituting into Eq. (
S7
), we find that, in the steady-state:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu}J_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle\partial_{\beta}\left[-\frac{\left(D_{\rm eff}+D\right)}{\mu}\rho\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\frac{dD_{\rm eff}}{\mu}Q_{\alpha\beta}-\ell_{p}I^{(1)}_{\alpha\beta}-\ell_{p}I^{\rm ext}_{\alpha\beta}+\frac{D}{\mu}\ell_{p}\partial_{\beta}m_{\alpha}+\sigma^{\rm IK}_{\alpha\beta}\right]-\rho\partial_{\alpha}U_{w}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{\beta}\sigma_{\alpha\beta}-\rho\partial_{\alpha}U_{w}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\;,

(S15)

where
D_{\rm eff}=v_{0}^{2}\tau_{p}/d
is the effective diffusivity of free active particles, and
\ell_{p}=v_{0}\tau_{p}
is the persistence length.

Equation (
S15
) can be read as a force balance equation, which endows
\sigma
with the mechanical interpretation of a stress tensor, with

\sigma_{\alpha\beta}=-\mu^{-1}\left(D_{\rm eff}+D\right)\rho\delta_{\alpha\beta}-d\mu^{-1}D_{\rm eff}Q_{\alpha\beta}-\ell_{p}I^{(1)}_{\alpha\beta}-\ell_{p}I^{\rm ext}_{\alpha\beta}+\mu^{-1}D\ell_{p}\partial_{\beta}m_{\alpha}+\sigma^{\rm IK}_{\alpha\beta}\;.

(S16)

We note that the stress tensor for passive systems is obtained by setting
\ell_{p}=0
and
D_{\rm eff}=0
, which gives back the standard expression

\sigma^{\rm passive}_{\alpha\beta}=-\frac{D}{\mu}\rho\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\sigma^{\rm IK}_{\alpha\beta}\;.

(S17)

The full stress tensor Eq. (
S16
) comprises two contributions
\bm{\sigma}=\bm{\sigma}^{a}+\bm{\sigma}^{\rm passive}
. Note that, for simplicity, we take
D=0
when studying active systems (but
D=\mu k_{B}T\neq 0
in passive simulations). We note that Eq. (
S15
) is exact in the steady state. A physical discussion of the various terms entering
\bm{\sigma}^{a}
can be found in
[
39
]
. Throughout the article, we use Eq. (
S16
) to carry out measurements of the stress tensor from particle simulations.

S10
Adsorbed droplets undergoing reverse Ostwald ripening

In the main text, we show in Fig.
4
that a large enough active droplet adsorbed on a wall is unstable and undergoes a growing-and-splitting dynamics. The splitting dynamics, shown in SM Movie 2, starts with a bubble nucleation inside the droplet, next to the wall. The bubble then grows in size and the droplet finally splits. Notably, the two resulting droplets then undergo a standard Ostwald ripening: the smaller droplet evaporates, leading to the growth of the larger one. The larger droplet then splits again at a later time.

For a larger persistence length (
\ell_{p}=100
), a second size-selection mechanism is observed. As shown in Fig.
S9
a-c and e-g, small droplets nucleate next to the wall, away from the large initial droplet. Then, the larger droplet evaporates while a reversed Ostwald ripening drives the growth of the distant smaller adsorbed droplets, leading to a final state with multiple droplets of similar sizes.

We note that, for the same simulation parameters, bulk phase separated systems are stable (SM Movie 6) and no sign of reversed Ostwald ripening is observed in the bulk. As for Fig.
4
, the size-selection mechanism is thus specific to adsorbed droplets and requires the presence of a confining wall.

To best contrast the two size-selection mechanisms, we adjust particle stiffness and persistence length such that, in our large simulation box, we observe either one or two droplets. In Fig.
S9
d, the reversed Ostwald ripening makes a small distant droplet grow until it absorbs half the particles from the larger initial droplet. In Fig.
S9
h, the opposite is observed, corresponding to a standard Ostwald ripening mechanism.

We present two phase diagrams in Fig.
S9
i,j to show the regions in the parameter space corresponding to the two regimes. The phase diagrams are obtained by manually observing the dynamics of a single droplet of varying size for each point in the parameter space. We construct the diagram using a system size of
L_{x}=1024
and
L_{y}=432
, while the stability of larger droplets is tested using a larger system with
L_{x}=1536
and
L_{y}=688
. We find that single droplets in the reverse Ostwald ripening regime are unstable at any size, and that small distant droplets always nucleate and grow. This is used to distinguish the reverse and normal ripening regimes. The phase diagrams reveal that the transition to reverse ripening is triggered by increasing persistence, increasing wall stiffness, or decreasing particle stiffness. A fully wetting regime appears in the limit of large persistence, stiff walls, and soft particles. The reverse ripening regime appears to be an intermediate regime along the transition from partial to full wetting.
