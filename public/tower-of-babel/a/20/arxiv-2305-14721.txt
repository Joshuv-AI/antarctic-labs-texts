1.
Introduction

A great deal of research is being conducted to mathematically model and analyze nonlinear flows and transport processes through a porous medium. Many models of porous media are based on Darcy’s law and Darcy-Forchheimer’s law (cf.
[
17
]
). Darcy’s law is an equation that describes the flow of a fluid through a porous medium, derived for slow linearly viscous flows where momentum transfer dissipation is negligible. Darcy’s empirical flow model suggests that there is a linear relationship between flow rate and the pressure drop in a porous media, that is,
\nabla p=-\frac{\nu}{\kappa}\boldsymbol{u}_{f},
where
\boldsymbol{u}_{f}
is the Darcy velocity,
\kappa
is the permeability of the porous medium,
\nu
is the dynamic viscosity of the fluid, and
p
is the pressure. In certain cases, nature does not abide by Darcy’s law; instead, it follows Forchheimer law, which states that the relationship between the flow rate and pressure gradient is nonlinear at high velocities, and this nonlinearity increases with the increasing flow rate (cf.
[
17
]
). The Darcy-Forchheimer law states that
\nabla p=-\frac{\nu}{\kappa}\boldsymbol{v}_{f}-\gamma\rho_{f}|\boldsymbol{v}_{f}|^{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{f}
, where
\gamma>0
is the Forchheimer coefficient,
\boldsymbol{v}_{f}
stands for the Forchheimer velocity and
\rho_{f}
is the density. Therefore, Forchheimer’s law suggests that Darcy’s law is still applicable, but with an additional nonlinear term to account for the increased pressure drop. In this work, we consider an approximation for the stochastic counterpart of convective Brinkman-Forchheimer extended Darcy (CBFeD) model, which is based on a Darcy-Forchheimer law.

1.2.
Aims, novelties, difficulties and approaches

The major goal of this article is to study the approximations of stochastic CBFeD equations in (
1.4
) by stochastic CBFeD equations forced by Poisson random measures. One of the key motivations for studying this problem is to way a path for the numerical simulations of stochastic CBFeD equations driven by pure jump noise. The authors of
[
7
]
initiated work in the direction of approximating solutions of stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) including stochastic Burgers equation driven by Brownian motion by SPDEs forced by pure jump noise/random kicks. The works
[
28
,
15
,
23
]
, etc., extended this results to stochastic 2D Navier-Stokes equations, stochastic 3D Navier-Stokes equations with damping and stochastic 3D tamed Navier-Stokes equations, respectively.

We point out here that the approximation results obtained in the work
[
23
]
for stochastic 3D tamed Navier-Stokes equations (a special case of 3D critical CBF equations with
\mu=\beta=1
) is in the state space
\mathrm{D}([0,T];\mathbb{V})
with an additional
\mathbb{H}^{2}
regularity assumption on the noise coefficient (
[
23
, Hypothesis H5]
). It should also be noted that the approximation results derived in
[
15
]
for stochastic 3D Navier-Stokes equations with damping hold true for the system (
1.4
) with
\alpha=0
,
3<r<5
and
4\beta\mu\geq 1
for
r=3
in the state space
\mathrm{D}([0,T];\mathbb{H})
and
\mathrm{D}([0,T];\mathbb{V})
with an additional
\mathbb{H}^{2}
regularity assumption on the noise coefficient (
[
15
, Assumption 2.2]
). In this work, using less regular assumptions (see Hypothesis
3.4
below) on the noise coefficient compared to
[
15
,
23
]
(cf.
[
28
, Hypothesis H.5]
for 2D stochastic NSE), we prove the approximation results for stochastic 2D and 3D CBFeD equations in
\mathrm{D}([0,T];\mathbb{H})
for
d=2
with
r\in[1,\infty)
and
d=3
with
r\in[3,\infty)
(
2\beta\mu>1
for
d=r=3
).

We follow the works
[
7
,
28
,
15
,
23
]
, etc. to obtain the main result of this paper. In order to prove approximations of stochastic CBFeD equations by pure jump type stochastic CBFeD equations in
\mathrm{D}([0,T];\mathbb{H})
, we first establish the usual energy estimates (Lemmas
3.5
and
3.6
) under mild assumptions on the initial data (
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{H}
) and noise coefficient (Hypothesis
2.1
and
3.1
). The difficulty lies in establishing the tightness of the approximating equations in the state space
\mathrm{D}([0,T];\mathbb{H})
. Assuming that the initial data has higher regularity (
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{V}
), and that the coefficients of the jump noise take values in a more regular space (Hypothesis
3.4
), we overcome this difficulty by deriving a uniform estimate of the stronger norm of the approximating solutions (Lemma
3.7
). It should be noted that Hypothesis
3.4
is weaker than the regularity assumptions on the noise coefficients made in
[
23
, Hypothesis H5]
and
[
15
, Assumption 2.2]
. By using Aldou’s criterion, we can prove the tightness of the approximating equations in
\mathrm{D}([0,T];\mathbb{H})
with these uniform estimates (Proposition

). We demonstrate, through a martingale characterization and

, that the limit of the solutions of approximating equations is the solution of the 2D and 3D stochastic CBFeD equations driven by Brownian motion (
1.4
) for
d=2,r\in[1,\infty)
and
d=3,r\in[3,\infty)
with
2\beta\mu\geq 1
for

(Theorem

\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}})
,
\alpha\geq 0
(cf.
[
24
]
). For
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{H}
and
\alpha>0,
one can define

\mathrm{A}^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{u}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\boldsymbol{e}_{k},\ \boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A}^{\alpha}),
where
\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A}^{\alpha})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{H}:\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2\alpha}|\boldsymbol{u}_{k}|^{2}<+\infty}}\right\}.

Here
\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A}^{\alpha})
is equipped with the norm

\|\mathrm{A}^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\mathbb{H}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2\alpha}|\boldsymbol{u}_{k}|^{2}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

It can be easily seen that
\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A}^{0})=\mathbb{H}
,
\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A}^{\frac{1}{2}})=\mathbb{V}
and
\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A}^{-\frac{1}{2}})=\mathbb{V}^{\prime}
. We set
\mathbb{V}_{\alpha}=\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}})
with
\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\mathbb{V}_{\alpha}}=\|\mathrm{A}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\mathbb{H}}.

2.3.
Bilinear operator

Let us define the
trilinear form

b(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{R}
by

b(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\boldsymbol{u}(x)\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}(x)\cdot\boldsymbol{w}(x)\/\mathrm{d}\/x=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}(x)\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}_{j}(x)}{\partial x_{i}}\boldsymbol{w}_{j}(x)\/\mathrm{d}\/x.

If
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}
are such that the linear map
b(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\cdot)
is continuous on
\mathbb{V}
, the corresponding element of
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}
is denoted by
\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})
. We also denote
\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{u})=\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})=\mathcal{P}[(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}]
.
An integration by parts yields

(2.3)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} b(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})&=0,\ \text{ for all }\ \boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{V},\\
b(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})&=-b(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v}),\ \text{ for all }\ \boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{V}.\end{aligned}}}\right.

For
r\in[1,3]
, using Hölder’s inequality, we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\langle\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}),\boldsymbol{v}\rangle}}\right|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|b(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u})}}\right|\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{4}}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbb{V}},
for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{V}
so that

\|\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{u})\|_{\mathbb{V}^{\prime}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{4}}^{2},\ \text{ for all }\ \boldsymbol{u}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{4},

and we conclude that
\mathrm{B}(\cdot):\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{4}\to\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{4}{3}}
. Furthermore, we have

(2.4)

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{u})-\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{v})\|_{\mathbb{V}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{4}}+\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{4}}}}\right)\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{4}},

hence
\mathrm{B}(\cdot):\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{4}\to\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{4}{3}}
is a locally Lipschitz operator.
An application of Hölder’s inequality yields

\displaystyle|b(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})|=|b(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v})|\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{2(r+1)}{r-1}}}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathbb{V}},

for all
\boldsymbol{u}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}
,
\boldsymbol{v}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{2(r+1)}{r-1}}
and
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{V}
, so that we obtain

(2.5)

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\|_{\mathbb{V}^{\prime}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{2(r+1)}{r-1}}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}^{\frac{2}{r-1}}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{r-3}{r-1}},

using the interpolation inequality. Therefore, we deduce

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\langle\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}),\boldsymbol{v}\rangle}}\right|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|b(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u})}}\right|\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{2(r+1)}{r-1}}}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbb{V}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}^{\frac{r+1}{r-1}}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{r-3}{r-1}}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbb{V}},

for
r>3
and all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{V}
, which results to

(2.7)

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{u})\|_{\mathbb{V}^{\prime}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}^{\frac{r+1}{r-1}}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{r-3}{r-1}}.

Using (
2.5
), for
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}
, we also obtain

(2.8)

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{u})-\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{v})\|_{\mathbb{V}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{r-3}{r-1}}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}^{\frac{2}{r-1}}+\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{r-3}{r-1}}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}^{\frac{2}{r-1}}}}\right)\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}},

for
r>3
, by using the interpolation inequality. Therefore, the map
\mathrm{B}(\cdot):\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}\to\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{r+1}{r}}
is locally Lipschitz.

Nonlinear operator

Let us now consider the nonlinear operator
