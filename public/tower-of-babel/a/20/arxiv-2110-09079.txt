On vortex stretching for anti-parallel axisymmetric flows
Kyudong Choi, In-Jee Jeong

Abstract
We consider axisymmetric incompressible inviscid flows without swirl in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} , under the assumption that the axial vorticity is non-positive in the upper half space and odd in the last coordinate, which corresponds to the flow setup for head-on collision of anti-parallel vortex rings. For any such data, we establish monotonicity and infinite growth of the vorticity impulse on the upper half-space. As an application, we achieve infinite growth of Sobolev norms for certain classical/smooth and compactly supported vorticity solutions in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} .

Introduction

We are concerned with the Cauchy problem of the three-dimensional Euler equations in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which governs the dynamics of incompressible and inviscid fluids. In terms of the vorticity, the equations read

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\omega+u\cdot\nabla\omega=\omega\cdot\nabla u,\\
&u=\nabla\times(-\Delta)^{-1}\omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

with
u(t,\cdot),\omega(t,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
denoting the velocity and vorticity of the fluid, respectively. The right-hand side of the first equation in (
1.1
) is commonly referred to as the vortex stretching term, as it can increase the size of
|\omega(t,\cdot)|
along the flow trajectories. For Euler flows in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, it is a very interesting problem to understand what types of flow configurations can lead to large growth of the vorticity maximum, especially in view of the Beale–Kato–Majda criterion which states that smooth solutions can blow up in finite time if and only if the quantity
\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}
does. In the current work, we consider the specific scenario of head-on collision of anti-parallel vortex rings, which has been extensively studied using experimental and numerical methods. For such flow configurations, we obtain maximum principles and infinite growth for physically natural averaged quantities involving the vorticity. As a byproduct, we are able to deduce infinite growth of the vorticity
L^{\infty}
norm for certain globally defined
classical
solutions to (
1.1
) (see the estimate (
1.8
)), among others. For some
C^{\infty}
–
smooth
solutions, we obtain infinite growth of the vorticity
C^{\alpha}
Hölder norm (see Theorem
1.5
).

This flow setup exhibits several fascinating features including strong vortex stretching, bulging instability, rebound effects, breakdown of axisymmetry and vortex reconnection, and has been studied extensively using experimental, theoretical, and computational methods. The authors in
[
13

demonstrate that the evolution of the vortex rings goes through three stages, before their breakdown: (i) free-traveling, (ii) vortex stretching, and (iii) viscous dissipation. This is intuitively clear: initially, the rings are separated and they travel towards each other as the interaction is weak. As they become sufficiently close, the rings start to stretch out to radial direction due to incompressibility. Numerical simulations and experiments clearly show that as the Reynolds number becomes large, the rings get stretched further away from the symmetry axis before viscous dissipation becomes dominant. In the current work, we employ the inviscid equation and focus on the vortex stretching phenomenon for large times. The basic question we try to answer is: what is the rate of vorticity growth as the time goes to infinity? To answer this question, one can hope to gain some insights from the simpler case of two-dimensional flows.

Vortex dynamics in the two-dimensional case
. Under the odd symmetry assumption in

Dyson model for vortex rings
. Returning to axisymmetric flows and the problem of finding the rate of vortex stretching, it is natural to first consider the dynamics of circular vortex filaments (thin-cored vortex rings), which are the axisymmetric versions of point vortices in 2D. The well-known difficulty in the filament case is that for a fixed circulation
\Gamma

To handle this difficulty, the usual assumption is that the core of each vortex ring remains circular with a

1.3
Outline of the proof

Let us outline the proof of Theorem
1.1
, which is inspired by a related work of Iftimie–Sideris–Gamblin
[
36
]
. Among others, the authors prove that for compactly supported, non-negative, and non-trivial initial vorticity on the positive quadrant
(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}
(extended to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by odd-odd symmetry as described above), the 2D Euler solution satisfies

\begin{split}\iint_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}x_{1}\omega(t,\mathbb{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{x}\geq ct,\end{split}

(1.10)

with
c=c(\omega_{0})>0
. Their proof consists of two main steps: the first is to establish the monotonicity property

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\iint_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}x_{1}\omega(t,\mathbb{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{x}\geq 0,\qquad\frac{d}{dt}\iint_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}x_{2}\omega(t,\mathbb{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{x}\leq 0.\end{split}

Then, the second step is to prove a lower bound on the kinetic energy
E=\iint_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}|u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{x}
of the form

\begin{split}E\leq C\left(\iint_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}x_{2}\omega(t,\mathbb{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{x}\right)^{p}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\iint_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}x_{1}\omega(t,\mathbb{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{x}\right)^{q}\end{split}

with some
p,q>0
and
C>0
depending on the initial data. Then, using conservation of
E
and monotonicity of
\iint_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}x_{2}\omega(t,\mathbb{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{x}
, we conclude that

\begin{split}\left(\iint_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}x_{2}\omega_{0}(\mathbb{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{x}\right)^{-p}\frac{E}{C}\leq\left(\frac{d}{dt}\iint_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}x_{1}\omega(t,\mathbb{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{x}\right)^{q},\end{split}

from which (
1.10
) follows.

While we follow the above steps, there are some difficulties arising in our case due to the form of the axisymmetric Biot–Savart law and the presence of vortex stretching.

Monotonicity lemmas
: It turns out that in the axisymmetric case, the monotone quantities are

\begin{split}P(t)=\iint_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}-r^{2}\omega^{\theta}(t,r,z)\,\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z,\qquad Z(t)=\iint_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}-z\omega^{\theta}(t,r,z)\,\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z;\end{split}

the former increases monotonically in time while the later decreases. While this tendency of vortex dynamics is clear from numerous simulations and computations, we were not able to find a rigorous proof in the literature. In the meanwhile, this confirms that the “rebound effect” of vortex rings is a purely viscous phenomenon as argued in
[
13
]
; the convection term only brings the antiparallel rings towards each other. Unlike the two-dimensional case, the proof of

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\iint_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}-z\omega^{\theta}(t,r,z)\,\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z\leq 0\end{split}

is highly nontrivial and we could establish it only by using the formulation of the Biot–Savart law in terms of certain elliptic integrals (see Lemma
3.3
).

•

Kinetic energy bound
: Following the ideas of Iftimie–Sideris–Gamblin, we try to bound
E
using
\dot{P}=\frac{d}{dt}P
and other conserved quantities. However, inspecting the expressions for
E
and
\dot{P}
given in Lemma
3.2
, it seems impossible to obtain a bound of the form
E\lesssim(\dot{P})^{a}
for some
a>0
, unlike the 2D case. Indeed,
E
grows faster than
\dot{P}
if one places the initial vorticity further away from the axis, keeping the
L^{p}
norms of
r^{-1}\omega_{0}
under control. To handle this issue, we use
P
itself, which controls the mass of vorticity far from the axis: our key estimate is

\begin{split}E\lesssim\dot{P}^{1-\frac{1}{q}}P^{\frac{1}{q}},\end{split}

for any
q<\frac{15}{13}
. Integrating this in time gives the bound
P(t)\gtrsim 1+t^{\frac{2}{15}-}
.

•

Relation between
P
and vorticity maximum
: Growth of
P
implies that, in some averaged sense, the center of vorticity in the radial direction increases. Recalling the Cauchy formula for axisymmetric flows without swirl, this implies growth of the vorticity maximum. Unfortunately, to deduce
\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\to\infty
from
P(t)\to\infty
, we need an additional assumption on the distribution function of the initial vorticity, which is expressed in (
1.5
): heuristically, the measure of the set on which the vorticity takes very small values cannot be too large. Under this assumption
(
1.5
) with
\delta=0
, which can be easily achieved by patch-type vorticities, we obtain

\begin{split}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\|\omega\|_{L^{\infty}}.\end{split}

This implies
\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\gtrsim t^{\frac{1}{15}-}
.
We note that the condition (
1.5
) with
\delta=0
(and
p=\infty
)
can be satisfied for some
C^{\gamma}
–data
\omega_{0}
whenever
\gamma<1
while it fails
for typical
C^{1}
–data
\omega_{0}
. Fortunately, there is a little room for
\delta>0
in the analysis of Lemma
3.1
providing the existence of Lipschitz data
\omega_{0}
with infinite growth.

•

Upgrading growth rate by contradiction
: We perform an additional estimate which enhances the growth rate of
\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}
. The idea is to
assume
a hypothetical bound of the form
\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim t^{\beta}
. For instance, when
\delta=0
, we get
\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim P(t)^{\frac{15\beta}{2}+}
using the lower bound
P(t)\gtrsim 1+t^{\frac{2}{15}-}
. Then, we can use this new estimate to obtain an improved bound on the kinetic energy:

\begin{split}E\lesssim\dot{P}^{1-\frac{1}{q}}P^{(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{45}{56}\beta{-})\frac{1}{q}},\end{split}

for any
q<\frac{17}{14}
. This time, we obtain

\begin{split}\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\gtrsim P^{\frac{1}{2}}\gtrsim 1+t^{\frac{1}{\frac{5}{3}+\frac{15}{8}\beta}-}.\end{split}

This is a contradiction to
\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim t^{\beta}
when
\beta<\beta_{0}=\frac{1}{45}(\sqrt{670}-20)\simeq 0.13
.

•

Infinite gradient growth
: The construction in Theorem
1.5
of compactly supported
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
–initial data

\omega_{0}(\mathbb{x})
exhibiting growth of the
C^{\alpha}
–norm of the vorticity requires combining several ideas from earlier works with some new ingredients which are specific to the three-dimensional axisymmetric Euler equations. The basic idea is to place a smoothed out version of the Bahouri–Chemin solution (
[
2
]
) in the
(r,z)
–coordinate plane. This induces a strong hyperbolic flow near the origin, which stretches the vorticity gradient (see
[
31
,
24
,
4
,
15
,
16
,
68
,
45
,
64
,
39
,
44
,
38
,
18
,
37
]
for a few references utilizing this observation).
However, since our domain is
unbounded
, most of the mass could escape to infinity which would slow down the rate of stretching at the origin.
In the current setup, we show that this is not allowed unless
\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}
diverges to infinity–this is due to the presence of the vortex stretching term. Here, it is important that we can take the support of
\omega^{\theta}_{0}(r,z)
(as a function on the right half-plane
\{(r,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,r\geq 0\}
) to touch the axis
\{r=0\}
. Furthermore, under the contradiction hypothesis that the
C^{\alpha}
–norm of the vorticity is uniformly bounded, we obtain a uniform-in-time control of the velocity gradient near the origin. This is then sufficient to achieve infinite vorticity gradient growth, proceeding as in Zlatoš
[
68
]
.

1.4
Notation and conventions

From now on, for a simpler notation, we denote the scalar axial vorticity
\omega^{\theta}(r,z)
simply by the symbol
\omega
unless there might be confusion. We shall write
\Pi=\{(r,z):r\geq 0,z\in\mathbb{R}\}
and
\Pi_{+}=\{(r,z):r,z\geq 0\}
. Similarly,
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:x_{3}\geq 0\}
. As usual,
C
will denote various positive absolute constants whose value could vary from a line to another. When
C
depends on a few parameters, we will sometimes express the dependence using subscripts. Unless otherwise specified, the
L^{p}
norms are always taken with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Elliptic integrals

It will be convenient to introduce some elliptic integral functions in order to simplify the axisymmetric Biot–Savart law. We begin with

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}(s)&:=\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\cos(\theta)}{(2(1-\cos(\theta))+s)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\\
&=\frac{1}{(4+s)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left((2+s)\mathcal{E}_{K}\left(\frac{4}{4+s}\right)-(4+s)\mathcal{E}_{E}\left(\frac{4}{4+s}\right)\right).\end{split}

Then,

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(s)&=-\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\cos(\theta)}{2(2(1-\cos(\theta))+s)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\\
&=\frac{1}{2s(4+s)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left(s\mathcal{E}_{K}\left(\frac{4}{4+s}\right)-(2+s)\mathcal{E}_{E}\left(\frac{4}{4+s}\right)\right)\end{split}

(2.1)

and

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}(s)&=\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{3\cos(\theta)}{4(2(1-\cos(\theta))+s)^{\frac{5}{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\\
&=\frac{1}{4s^{2}(4+s)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\left(-s(2+s)\mathcal{E}_{K}\left(\frac{4}{4+s}\right)+(16+4s+s^{2})\mathcal{E}_{E}\left(\frac{4}{4+s}\right)\right).\end{split}

(2.2)

Here, the elliptic integrals are defined by

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{K}(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{1}(1-t^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1-\alpha t^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{E}(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{1}(1-t^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1-\alpha t^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t\end{split}

for
0\leq\alpha<1
. The integrals
\mathcal{E}_{K}
and
\mathcal{E}_{E}
are usually referred to as the
complete elliptic integrals
of the first and second kind, respectively. We note the relations

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{E}^{\prime}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\left(\mathcal{E}_{E}(\alpha)-\mathcal{E}_{K}(\alpha)\right),\qquad\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\prime}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\alpha(1-\alpha)}\left(\mathcal{E}_{E}(\alpha)-(1-\alpha)\mathcal{E}_{K}(\alpha)\right),\end{split}

which can be used to verify (
2.1
) and (
2.2
).

2.2
Stream function and Biot–Savart law

The stream function for axisymmetric no-swirl flows has the form

\begin{split}\psi(r,z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{\Pi}\left[\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{r\bar{r}\cos(\theta)}{\sqrt{r^{2}-2r\bar{r}\cos(\theta)+\bar{r}^{2}+(z-\bar{z})^{2}}}\mathrm{d}\theta\right]\omega(\bar{r},\bar{z})\,\mathrm{d}\bar{r}\mathrm{d}\bar{z}.\end{split}

(2.3)

Following the notation of Feng–Šverák
[
27
]
, we define

\begin{split}\zeta(r,\bar{r},z,\bar{z}):=\left(\frac{(r-\bar{r})^{2}+(z-\bar{z})^{2}}{r\bar{r}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

Then, one may perform the
\theta
-integration in (
2.3
) and obtain a simple expression for
\psi
:

\begin{split}\psi(r,z)=\iint_{\Pi}G(r,\bar{r},z,\bar{z})\omega(\bar{r},\bar{z})\,\mathrm{d}\bar{r}\mathrm{d}\bar{z},\end{split}

with

\begin{split}G(r,\bar{r},z,\bar{z}):=\frac{(r\bar{r})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{2\pi}\mathcal{F}(\zeta^{2}(r,\bar{r},z,\bar{z})).\end{split}

With this stream function, the velocity is given by

\begin{split}u^{r}(r,z)=-\frac{1}{r}\partial_{z}\psi(r,z),\qquad u^{z}(r,z)=\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}\psi(r,z).\end{split}

(2.4)

One may check directly with (
2.4
) that

\begin{split}\partial_{r}(ru^{r})+\partial_{z}(ru^{z})=0,\qquad\omega={-\partial_{r}u^{z}+\partial_{z}u^{r}}.\end{split}

That is,
u=(u^{r},u^{z})
indeed defines an incompressible vector field in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with associated curl
\omega
. Differentiating
G
, we obtain convenient integral expressions

\begin{split}u^{r}(r,z)=\iint_{\Pi}\frac{\bar{z}-z}{\pi r^{\frac{3}{2}}\bar{r}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\zeta^{2})\omega(\bar{r},\bar{z})\,\mathrm{d}\bar{r}\mathrm{d}\bar{z},\end{split}

\begin{split}u^{z}(r,z)=\iint_{\Pi}\left(\frac{r-\bar{r}}{\pi r^{\frac{3}{2}}\bar{r}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\zeta^{2})+\frac{\bar{r}^{\frac{1}{2}}}{4\pi r^{\frac{3}{2}}}\left[\mathcal{F}(\zeta^{2})-2\zeta^{2}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\zeta^{2})\right]\right)\omega(\bar{r},\bar{z})\,\mathrm{d}\bar{r}\mathrm{d}\bar{z}.\end{split}

We note that the
energy of the flow is given simply by

\begin{split}E[\omega]={\pi}\iint_{\Pi}\psi(r,z)\omega(r,z)\,\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z={\frac{1}{2}}\iiiint_{\Pi^{2}}{(r\bar{r})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\mathcal{F}(\zeta^{2})\,\omega(r,z)\omega(\bar{r},\bar{z})\,\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}\bar{r}\mathrm{d}\bar{z}.\end{split}

We remark that
0\leq E<\infty
for

r^{-1}\omega\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with

r\omega\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. In this case, the quantity
E[\omega]
represents the kinetic energy of the flow (after integration by parts):

E=\frac{1}{2}\iiint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|u|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{x}

(
e.g.
see Lemmas 2.3, 2.4 of
[
11
]
).

2.3
Axisymmetric flows without swirl

Let us briefly review the well-posedness theory for axisymmetric flows without swirl. A standard reference is
[
50
]
.

Global existence and uniqueness with
L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}
. As we have mentioned earlier, there is a unique global-in-time solution to (
1.2
) under the assumption
\omega_{0},r^{-1}\omega_{0}\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. This is a natural extension of Yudovich theory for two-dimensional Euler. The analogy is apparent if one introduces the
relative
vorticity function

\xi(t,r,z)=r^{-1}\omega(t,r,z):

(
1.2
) is simply

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\xi+u\cdot\nabla\xi=0.\end{split}

Since the velocity is incompressible, formally we have
\|\xi(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{p}}=\|\xi_{0}\|_{L^{p}}
for all
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Then, using the estimate (
e.g.
see Lemma 2 of
[
14
]
)

\begin{split}\|r^{-1}{u^{r}}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|\xi\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}}=C\|\xi_{0}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}},\end{split}

we obtain a priori estimates for the
L^{p}
–norms of the vorticity:

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\|\omega\|_{L^{p}}\leq C_{p}\|\xi_{0}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}}\|\omega\|_{L^{p}},\end{split}

\begin{split}\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{p}}\leq\|\omega_{0}\|_{L^{p}}\exp\left(Ct\|\xi_{0}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}}\right).\end{split}

In particular, we see that under the assumption
\xi_{0}\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}
, higher regularity of the vorticity propagates in time, using the Beale–Kato–Majda criterion (
[
3
]
). For global existence and uniqueness, the assumption
\omega_{0},\xi_{0}\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
can be weakened; see
[
14
]
for instance.

Flow map
. Given a solution in the class
\omega,\xi\in L^{\infty}_{t,loc}(L^{1}\cap L^{\infty})(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we shall denote
\Phi(t,\cdot)
to be the associated flow map, which is defined by the unique solution to the ODE

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\Phi(t,\mathbb{x})=u(t,\Phi(t,\mathbb{x})),\qquad\Phi(0,\mathbb{x})=\mathbb{x}\end{split}

(2.5)

(for solvability,
e.g.
see
Section 2.4 of
[
12
]
).
Then, along the flow, we have the important Cauchy formula

\begin{split}\frac{\omega(t,\Phi(t,\mathbb{x}))}{\Phi^{r}(t,\mathbb{x})}=\frac{\omega_{0}(\mathbb{x})}{r}\end{split}

(2.6)

which follows from the conservation of
\xi
along
\Phi
. Here,
\Phi=(\Phi^{r},\Phi^{z})
in cylindrical coordinates.

A priori estimate
. For later use, we recall a simple estimate.

Lemma 2.1
.

Define

\begin{split}X(r,\bar{r},z,\bar{z},\theta):=r^{2}+\bar{r}^{2}-2r\bar{r}\cos(\theta)+(z-\bar{z})^{2}.\end{split}

For any
q\in[0,3)
,
we have

\begin{split}\sup_{(\bar{r},\bar{z})\in\Pi}\iint_{\Pi}{\int_{0}^{2\pi}X^{-\frac{q}{2}}\mathrm{d}\theta}|\omega(r,z)|\,\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z\leq C_{q}\|r^{-1}\omega\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1-\frac{q}{3}}\|r^{-1}\omega\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{\frac{q}{3}}\end{split}

with a constant
C_{q}>0
depending only on
q
.

Proof.

We note that for
\mathbb{x}=(r,\theta,z)
and
\bar{\mathbb{x}}=(\bar{r},0,\bar{z})
, we have

\begin{split}\iint_{\Pi}{\int_{0}^{2\pi}X^{-\frac{q}{2}}\mathrm{d}\theta}|\omega(r,z)|\,\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z=\iiint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{|\bar{\mathbb{x}}-\mathbb{x}|^{q}}\frac{|\omega(\mathbb{x})|}{r}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{x}.\end{split}

The statement is clear when
q=0
. For
0<q<3
, we consider the regions
\{|\mathbb{x}-\bar{\mathbb{x}}|\leq L\}
and
\{|\mathbb{x}-\bar{\mathbb{x}}|>L\}
, for some
L>0
to be determined. In the latter region, we have that

\begin{split}\iiint_{|\mathbb{x}-\bar{\mathbb{x}}|>L}\frac{1}{|\bar{\mathbb{x}}-\mathbb{x}|^{q}}\frac{|\omega(\mathbb{x})|}{r}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{x}\leq CL^{-q}\|r^{-1}\omega\|_{L^{1}}\end{split}

since
q>0
and in the former, we obtain

\begin{split}\iiint_{|\mathbb{x}-\bar{\mathbb{x}}|\leq L}\frac{1}{|\bar{\mathbb{x}}-\mathbb{x}|^{q}}\frac{|\omega(\mathbb{x})|}{r}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{x}\leq\|r^{-1}\omega\|_{L^{\infty}}\iiint_{|\mathbb{x}|\leq L}|\mathbb{x}|^{-q}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{x}\leq C_{q}L^{3-q}\|r^{-1}\omega\|_{L^{\infty}}\end{split}

since
q<3
. The choice

\begin{split}L=\left(\frac{\|r^{-1}\omega\|_{L^{1}}}{\|r^{-1}\omega\|_{L^{\infty}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\end{split}

gives the desired estimate.
∎

The
t^{2}
bound on the vorticity maximum
. We provide a proof that the vorticity maximum for general axisymmetric flows without swirl cannot grow faster than
t^{2}
in time, assuming that the initial vorticity
satisfies

\sup\{r:(r,z)\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega_{0}(\cdot))\}<\infty

together with (
1.3
).

We need the following lemma from Feng–Šverák
[
27
]
.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
27
, Propositions 2.11, 2.13]
)
.

The velocity
u=K[\omega]
satisfies

\begin{split}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|r\omega\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{4}}\|r^{-1}\omega\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{4}}\|r^{-1}\omega\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

Recall our convention that the
L^{p}
norms are taken always with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Now to prove the claim, we define
R(t)=\sup\{r:(r,z)\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega(t,\cdot))\}
. We may assume that
R(t)
is increasing in time by redefining it to be
\sup_{t^{\prime}\leq t}R(t^{\prime})
. Then

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}R(t)&\leq\|u^{r}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|r\omega\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{4}}\|r^{-1}\omega\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{4}}\|r^{-1}\omega\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\leq CR(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|r^{-1}\omega\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|r^{-1}\omega\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&=CR(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|r^{-1}\omega_{0}\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|r^{-1}\omega_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

Integrating in time gives

\begin{split}R(t)\leq C(1+t)^{2}.\end{split}

(2.7)

Recalling the Cauchy formula (
2.6
), the
t^{2}
–bound on the support can be translated to a bound on
\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}
:

\begin{split}\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq R(t)\|r^{-1}\omega_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C(1+t)^{2},\end{split}

where
C>0
depends on the initial data
\omega_{0}
.

3
Key Inequalities

From now on, we shall impose the assumption (
1.4
) on
\omega
, so that the dynamics reduces to
\Pi_{+}=\{(r,z):r,z\geq 0\}
.

3.1
Relation between
P
and vorticity
L^{p}
norms

We define

\begin{split}P(t):=\iint_{\Pi_{+}}-r^{2}\omega(t,r,z)\,\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z=\iiint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}}-r^{2}\xi(t,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}x\geq 0.\end{split}

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
0\leq\delta<1
. Assume that
\|\xi_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{0}<0\}}\|_{L^{\frac{1-\delta}{1-((2-\delta)/p)}}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})}<\infty
for some
p\in[2-\delta,\infty]

and

R_{0}=\sup\{r:(r,z)\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega_{0}(\cdot))\}<\infty
. Then, we have

\begin{split}P(t)\leq R(t)^{\delta}\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})}^{2-\delta}\|\xi_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{0}<0\}}\|^{1-\delta}_{L^{\frac{1-\delta}{1-((2-\delta)/p)}}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})},\end{split}

where
R(t)=\sup\{r:(r,z)\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega(t,\cdot))\}
.

Proof.

We estimate using Hölder’s inequality

\begin{split}\iiint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}}-r^{2}\xi\,\mathrm{d}x&=\iiint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}}-r^{\delta}\frac{r^{1-\delta}}{\omega^{1-\delta}}\omega^{2-\delta}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq R(t)^{\delta}\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})}^{2-\delta}\left(\iiint_{\{\xi(t,\cdot)<0\}}\xi(t,\cdot)^{-\frac{1-\delta}{1-((2-\delta)/p)}}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1-\frac{(2-\delta)}{p}}\\
&=R(t)^{\delta}\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})}^{2-\delta}\|\xi_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{0}<0\}}\|^{1-\delta}_{L^{\frac{1-\delta}{1-((2-\delta)/p)}}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})}.\end{split}

In the last equality, we have used that the distribution function of
\xi(t,\cdot)
is invariant in time.
∎

It is worth considering the special case in which
\xi_{0}=-\lambda\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{0}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}
for some
\lambda>0
and a bounded open set
\Omega_{0}\subset\Pi_{+}\cap\{r\geq\frac{1}{2}\}
. The associated solution takes the form

\begin{split}\xi(t,\cdot)=-\lambda\mathbf{1}_{\Omega(t)}.\end{split}

Denoting
R(t):=\sup\{r:\mbox{there exists }(r,z)\in\Omega(t)\},
we immediately obtain from
|\Omega(t)|=|\Omega_{0}|
(
|\cdot|
denotes the three-dimensional Lebesgue measure) that

\begin{split}P(t)\leq\lambda|\Omega_{0}|R^{2}(t),\end{split}

and combining this with

\begin{split}\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\geq{\frac{1}{2}\lambda R(t),}\end{split}

we arrive at the following precise result:

\begin{split}\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(|\Omega_{0}|^{-1}\|\xi_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}P(t)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

3.2
Estimates on kinetic energy and derivative of
P

In addition to

\begin{split}X(r,\bar{r},z,\bar{z},\theta)=r^{2}+\bar{r}^{2}-2r\bar{r}\cos(\theta)+(z-\bar{z})^{2}\end{split}

which has been defined in the above, we set

\begin{split}\bar{X}(r,\bar{r},z,\bar{z},\theta)&=r^{2}+\bar{r}^{2}+2r\bar{r}\cos(\theta)+(z-\bar{z})^{2},\\
Y(r,\bar{r},z,\bar{z},\theta)&=r^{2}+\bar{r}^{2}-2r\bar{r}\cos(\theta)+(z+\bar{z})^{2},\\
\bar{Y}(r,\bar{r},z,\bar{z},\theta)&=r^{2}+\bar{r}^{2}+2r\bar{r}\cos(\theta)+(z+\bar{z})^{2}.\end{split}

For ease of notation, let us write
\omega=\omega(r,z)\,\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z
and
\bar{\omega}=\omega(\bar{r},\bar{z})\,\mathrm{d}\bar{r}\mathrm{d}\bar{z}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The kinetic energy
E\geq 0
satisfies

\begin{split}\frac{E}{C}\leq\iiiint_{(\Pi_{+})^{2}}\left[\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}{\frac{(r\bar{r})^{2}(z\bar{z})(\cos(\theta))^{2}}{X^{\frac{1}{2}}\bar{X}Y}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right]\,\omega\bar{\omega}\,\leq CE.\end{split}

(3.1)

Moreover,
\dot{P}:=\frac{d}{dt}P\geq 0
satisfies

\begin{split}\frac{\dot{P}}{C}\leq\iiiint_{(\Pi_{+})^{2}}\left[\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{(r\bar{r})^{2}(z+\bar{z})(\cos(\theta))^{2}}{Y^{\frac{3}{2}}\bar{Y}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right]\,\omega\bar{\omega}\,\leq C\dot{P}.\end{split}

(3.2)

Here,
C>0
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

We begin with the expression

We now proceed to the estimate of
J_{2}
: using (
4.4
) and Hölder’s inequality,

\begin{split}J_{2}&=\iiiint_{(\Pi_{+})^{2}}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{(r\bar{r})^{2}\bar{z}Y^{q-1}(\cos(\theta))^{2}}{(X\bar{X}^{2})^{\frac{q}{2}}Y^{\frac{3}{2}-q}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\omega\bar{\omega}\\
&\lesssim\iiiint_{(\Pi_{+})^{2}}\frac{(r\bar{r})^{3-q}\bar{z}^{4(q-1)}}{d^{q-1}}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{(\cos(\theta))^{2}}{X^{\frac{1}{2}}\bar{X}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\omega\bar{\omega}\\
&\lesssim\iiiint_{(\Pi_{+})^{2}}\frac{(r\bar{r})^{\frac{3}{2}-q+\tau}\bar{z}^{4(q-1)}}{d^{q-1+2\tau}}\,\omega\bar{\omega}\\
&\lesssim\left(\iiiint_{(\Pi_{+})^{2}}(r\bar{r})^{2}\,\omega\bar{\omega}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{3}{2}-q+\tau)}\left(\iint_{\Pi_{+}}\bar{z}^{\frac{16(q-1)}{1+2q-2\tau}}\bar{\omega}\,\iint_{\Pi_{+}}\frac{\omega}{d^{\frac{4(q-1+2\tau)}{1+2q-2\tau}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}+q-\tau)}.\end{split}

As long as
q<\frac{17}{14}
, we can take
\tau>0
sufficiently small that

\begin{split}\iint_{\Pi_{+}}\bar{z}^{\frac{16(q-1)}{1+2q-2\tau}}|\bar{\omega}|\lesssim 1,\qquad\sup_{(\bar{r},\bar{z})\in\Pi_{+}}\iint_{\Pi_{+}}\frac{|\omega|}{d^{\frac{4(q-1+2\tau)}{1+2q-2\tau}}}\lesssim t^{\frac{2(q-1+2\tau)}{1+2q-2\tau}\beta}.\end{split}

This gives

\begin{split}J_{2}\lesssim P^{\frac{3}{2}-q+\tau}t^{\frac{q-1+2\tau}{2}\beta}.\end{split}

This finishes the proof of
Claim
.

In the following,
\epsilon>0
will denote a sufficiently small constant whose value could change from a line to another. Given
Claim
, we now observe,
for any
1<q<17/14
,

\begin{split}J\lesssim J_{1}+J_{2}\lesssim P^{\frac{1}{2}+{\varepsilon}}(1+t)^{\frac{1}{2}(q-1+2\varepsilon)\beta}\end{split}

since
P
is increasing
(recall (
4.2
) and the above for
J
).
Recall from Proposition
4.1
that

\begin{split}P\gtrsim(1+t)^{\frac{2}{15}-\epsilon}.\end{split}

Hence, combining the previous two inequalities, we deduce

\begin{split}J\lesssim P^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{15}{4}(q-1)\beta+\epsilon}.\end{split}

Returning to the energy estimate (
4.1
):

\begin{split}E\lesssim\dot{P}^{1-\frac{1}{q}}J^{\frac{1}{q}}\end{split}

and applying the above upper bound for
J
, we obtain that

\begin{split}\dot{P}P^{\frac{(1/2)}{q-1}+\frac{15}{4}\beta+\epsilon}\gtrsim 1,\end{split}

which gives upon integration
after taking
q
close to
17/14
that

\begin{split}P\gtrsim t^{\frac{1}{\frac{10}{3}+\frac{15}{4}\beta}-\epsilon}.\end{split}

(4.5)

On the other hand, from the assumption on the vorticity maximum, the estimate (
2.7
) with Lemma
3.1
implies that

\begin{split}P(t)\lesssim R(t)^{\delta}\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})}^{2-\delta}\lesssim(1+t)^{2\delta}t^{(2-\delta)\beta}\lesssim(1+t)^{2\delta+(2-\delta)\beta},\end{split}

(4.6)

where
R(t)=\sup\{r:(r,z)\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega(t,\cdot))\}
. Therefore, we derive a contradiction to (
4.5
) once

\begin{split}2\delta+(2-\delta)\beta<\frac{1}{\frac{10}{3}+\frac{15}{4}\beta}.\end{split}

(4.7)

Note that, for each
\delta\in[0,3/20)
, we denote the unique positive root of

(2\delta+(2-\delta)\beta)\cdot(\frac{10}{3}+\frac{15}{4}\beta)=1

by
\beta_{0}=\beta_{0}(\delta)
. Observing that the previous inequality (
4.7
) holds for any
\beta\in(0,\beta_{0})
, we are done.
∎

.

from Corollary

.

5
Infinite gradient growth

In this section, we distinguish between
the axial vorticity vector
\omega(\mathbb{x})
and its scalar quantity
\omega^{\theta}(r,z)=r\xi(r,z)
, and we prove Theorem
1.5
.

Step 1
.
Choice of initial data and contradiction hypothesis
: We consider

\xi_{0}(r,z)\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}),\quad\overline{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}:=\{r\geq 0,z\in\mathbb{R}\},

which is
an odd function in
z
,
satisfying the following properties on the first quadrant
\overline{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}:=\{r\geq 0,z\geq 0\}
:

•

0\leq(-\xi_{0})\leq 1
on
(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}
,

•

{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\xi_{0})\cap\overline{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}\subset[0,1]^{2}
,

•

\xi_{0}=-1
on a set
\Omega_{0}\subset[0,1]^{2}
satisfying
\int_{\Omega_{0}}\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z\geq\frac{9}{10}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z
,

•

\xi_{0}=-z
on
[0,1/2]\times[0,1/10]
.

It is not difficult to see that there exists such
\xi_{0}(r,z)
such that the corresponding axial vorticity

\omega_{0}(\mathbb{x})=\omega^{\theta}_{0}e_{\theta}(\theta)=r\xi_{0}(r,z)e_{\theta}(\theta)

belongs to the class
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. For instance, the troublesome near the axis
\{r=0\}
can be removable once we assume

\xi_{0}(r,z)=a(z),\quad 0\leq r\leq r_{0},\quad z\in\mathbb{R},

for some
r_{0}>0
and for some function

a\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
since

re_{\theta}(\theta)=(-r\sin\theta,r\cos\theta,0)=(-x_{2},x_{1},0)

is smooth in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Towards a contradiction, assume that the corresponding solution
\omega
satisfies

\begin{split}\sup_{t\geq 0}\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq M\end{split}

for some
M>0
and
\alpha>0
. Our convention of the Hölder norm is simply

\begin{split}\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}&:=\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{C^{\alpha}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\\
&:=\sup_{\mathbb{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\omega(t,\mathbb{x})|+\sup_{\mathbb{x}\neq\mathbb{x}^{\prime}}\frac{|\omega(t,\mathbb{x})-\omega(t,\mathbb{x}^{\prime})|}{|\mathbb{x}-\mathbb{x}^{\prime}|^{\alpha}}.\end{split}

Step 2
.
Bounds on the solution
: To begin with, we observe that

\begin{split}\sup_{(r,z)\in\Omega_{0}}\Phi^{r}(t,(r,z))\leq M,\quad t\geq 0,\end{split}

(5.1)

where
\Phi
is the trajectory map (
2.5
).
This simply follows from

\begin{split}M\geq-\omega(t,\Phi(t,(r,z)))=-\Phi^{r}(t,(r,z))\xi_{0}(r,z)=\Phi^{r}(t,(r,z))\end{split}

for
(r,z)\in\Omega_{0}
, from the definition of
\Omega_{0}
. Next,
we recall the Calderon–Zygmund estimate (cf.
[
50
]
)

\begin{split}\|\nabla u\|_{C^{\alpha}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq C_{\alpha}\|\omega\|_{C^{\alpha}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq C_{\alpha}M.\end{split}

(5.2)

Step 3
.
Non-trivial mass on a bounded region for all time
:
We claim that there exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that

\begin{split}\int_{\{0\leq z\leq 2,\,\,0\leq r\leq M\}}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega(t)}\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\{0\leq z\leq 2,\,\,r>0\}}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega(t)}\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z\geq c>0,\quad t\geq 0.\end{split}

(5.3)

where
\Omega(t):=\Phi(t,\Omega_{0})
.
Indeed, the equality above simply follows from (
5.1
). For the inequality, we first observe

\begin{split}{\int_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}z(-\xi(t,r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}&\leq{\int_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}{z}(-\xi_{0}(r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}\\
&\leq{\int_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}(-\xi_{0}(r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}={\int_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}(-\xi(t,r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z},\end{split}

where we used monotonicity of
the vorticity
z
–impulse (Lemma
3.3
) and the fact that
(-\xi_{0})
as a function on
\overline{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}
is non-negative and supported in
[0,1]^{2}
.
Then we estimate

\begin{split}{\int_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}(-\xi(t,r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}&\geq{\int_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}z(-\xi(t,r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}\geq{\int_{\{z>2,\,\,r>0\}}z(-\xi(t,r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}\\
&\geq 2{\int_{\{z>2,\,\,r>0\}}(-\xi(t,r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}\\
&=2{\int_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}(-\xi(t,r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}-2{\int_{\{0\leq z\leq 2,\,\,r>0\}}(-\xi(t,r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z},\end{split}

which implies

\begin{split}{\int_{\{0\leq z\leq 2,\,\,r>0\}}(-\xi(t,r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}\geq\frac{1}{2}{\int_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}(-\xi(t,r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}.\end{split}

(5.4)

The right-hand term of (
5.4
) has a lower bound:

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}{\int_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}(-\xi(t,r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}&=\frac{1}{2}{\int_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}(-\xi_{0}(r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2}{\int_{\Omega_{0}}\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{9}{10}{\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}\end{split}

while the left-hand term of (
5.4
) has an upper bound:

\begin{split}{\int_{\{0\leq z\leq 2,\,\,r>0\}}(-\xi(t,r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}&={\int_{\{0\leq z\leq 2,\,\,r>0\}\cap\Omega(t)}\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}+{\int_{\{0\leq z\leq 2,\,\,r>0\}\setminus\Omega(t)}(-\xi(t,r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}.\end{split}

For the last term above, we estimate

\begin{split}{\int_{\{0\leq z\leq 2,\,\,r>0\}\setminus\Omega(t)}(-\xi(t,r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}&\leq{\int_{\Omega(t)^{c}}(-\xi(t,r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}={\int_{\Omega_{0}^{c}}(-\xi_{0}(r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}\\
=&{\int_{[0,1]^{2}\setminus\Omega_{0}}(-\xi_{0}(r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}\leq{\int_{[0,1]^{2}\setminus\Omega_{0}}\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}\leq\frac{1}{10}{\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}.\end{split}

Combining the above estimates, (
5.4
) shows

\begin{split}\left(\frac{9}{20}-\frac{1}{10}\right)\cdot{\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z}\leq{\int_{\{0\leq z\leq 2,\,\,r>0\}\cap\Omega(t)}\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z},\end{split}

which gives the claim (
5.3
)

Step 4
.
Velocity gradient near the origin
:

First, we recall the Biot-Savart law in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(
e.g.
see
[
50
]
):

u(t,\mathbb{x})=[\nabla\times(-\Delta)^{-1}\omega(t,\cdot)](\mathbb{x})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{(\mathbb{x}-\mathbb{y})\times\omega(t,\mathbb{y})}{|\mathbb{x}-\mathbb{y}|^{3}}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{y}.

We observe

u^{x_{3}}(t,\mathbb{x})|_{x_{1}=x_{2}=0}=C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\frac{r^{2}\xi(t,r,z)}{(r^{2}+(x_{3}-z)^{2})^{3/2}}r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z

and

[\partial_{x_{3}}u^{x_{3}}](t,\mathbb{x})|_{x_{1}=x_{2}=0}=-C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\frac{r^{2}(x_{3}-z)\xi(t,r,z)}{(r^{2}+(x_{3}-z)^{2})^{5/2}}r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z.

Since
\xi
is odd in
z
, we have, at the origin,

\begin{split}-\partial_{z}u^{z}(t,\mathbf{0})=C\int_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}\frac{r^{2}z}{(r^{2}+z^{2})^{\frac{5}{2}}}(-\xi(t,r,z))\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z\end{split}

for some absolute constant
C>0
. Now we estimate
-\partial_{z}u^{z}(t,\mathbf{0})
from above and below.
To begin with, we observe the uniform bound

\begin{split}0\leq-\partial_{z}u^{z}(t,\mathbf{0})\leq C(\|\xi\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\|\xi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})})=C(\|\xi_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\|\xi_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})})\leq C.\end{split}

This follows from considering separately the regions
\{r^{2}+z^{2}\leq 1\}
and
\{r^{2}+z^{2}>1\}
.
We now proceed to obtain a lower bound. Since the integrand is non-negative, we may estimate

\begin{split}-\partial_{z}u^{z}(t,\mathbf{0})&\geq C\int_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}\frac{r^{2}z}{(r^{2}+z^{2})^{\frac{5}{2}}}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega(t)}\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z\\
&\geq\frac{C}{(M^{2}+2^{2})^{\frac{5}{2}}}\int_{\{0\leq z\leq 2,\,\,0\leq r\leq M\}}r^{2}z\mathbf{1}_{\Omega(t)}\,r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}z\geq C_{M},\end{split}

(5.5)

where the last inequality follows from (
5.3
), and
C_{M}>0
is a constant depending only on
M
.
Next, recall that from the divergence-free condition
\partial_{r}(ru^{r})+\partial_{z}(ru^{z})=0
, we have

\begin{split}2\partial_{r}u^{r}(t,\mathbf{0})=-\partial_{z}u^{z}(t,\mathbf{0})>0.\end{split}

Therefore, using (
5.5
) with (
5.2
), we can take sufficiently small
L=L(M,\alpha)>0
such that for
(r,z)\in[0,L]^{2}
,

\begin{split}-\frac{5}{4}\partial_{z}u^{z}(t,\mathbf{0})>-\partial_{z}u^{z}(t,r,z)>-\frac{3}{4}\partial_{z}u^{z}(t,\mathbf{0})\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\frac{5}{4}\partial_{r}u^{r}(t,\mathbf{0})>\partial_{r}u^{r}(t,r,z)>\frac{3}{4}\partial_{r}u^{r}(t,\mathbf{0})\end{split}

uniformly for all
t\geq 0
. In particular, on
[0,L]^{2}
, it is guaranteed that the velocity field is pointing southeast for all times since
u^{r}|_{r=0}\equiv 0
and
u^{z}|_{z=0}\equiv 0
.

Step 5
.
Growth of the Hölder norm
: From the equation for
\xi
, we obtain that

\begin{split}{\partial_{t}\frac{\xi}{z^{\alpha}}+u\cdot\nabla\frac{\xi}{z^{\alpha}}=-\frac{u^{z}}{\alpha z}\frac{\xi}{z^{\alpha}}.}\end{split}

We take the point
x^{\epsilon}_{0}:=(\epsilon,L)
with
0<\epsilon<L
. As long as
x^{\epsilon}(\tau):=\Phi(\tau,x^{\epsilon}_{0})\in[0,L]^{2}
for all
\tau\in[0,t]
, we have from integrating the above that

\begin{split}{-\frac{\xi}{{z^{\alpha}}}(t,x^{\epsilon}(t))=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{u^{z}}{{\alpha z}}(\tau,x^{\epsilon}(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\cdot\left(-\frac{\xi_{0}}{{z^{\alpha}}}(x^{\epsilon}_{0})\right)\geq L^{1-\alpha}\exp\left(ct\right),\quad{c=c(M,\alpha)>0.}}\end{split}

Here, we could have assumed that
L>0
is sufficiently small, so that
\xi_{0}=-z
on
[0,L]^{2}
. Let
0<T_{\epsilon}<\infty
be the first time when
\Phi^{r}(t,x^{\epsilon}_{0})=L
.
The definition of
T_{\epsilon}
makes sense since the trajectory is moving to the southeast and cannot touch the axis
\{z=0\}
due to the unique solvability of the O.D.E. (
2.5
).
From the uniform bound on
\partial_{r}u^{r}
on
[0,L]^{2}
and the fact that
u^{r}(t,0,z)=0
, we have that
T_{\epsilon}\to\infty
as
\epsilon\to 0
. This shows that

\begin{split}{-\frac{\xi}{z^{\alpha}}(T_{\epsilon},x^{\epsilon}(T_{\epsilon}))\geq L^{1-\alpha}\exp\left(cT_{\epsilon}\right)}\end{split}

can be as large as we want. But then from

\begin{split}{\|\omega\|_{C^{\alpha}}\geq-\frac{\omega^{\theta}}{z^{\alpha}}(T_{\epsilon},x^{\epsilon}(T_{\epsilon}))=-L\frac{\xi}{z^{\alpha}}(T_{\epsilon},x^{\epsilon}(T_{\epsilon})),}\end{split}

we obtain a contradiction by taking
\epsilon\to 0
.

6
Enstrophy growth for Navier–Stokes solutions

In this section, we demonstrate that infinite enstrophy growth for Euler can be translated to
enstrophy inflation
for Navier–Stokes with small viscosity. While this is strictly speaking not necessary, we restrict ourselves to the axisymmetric systems without swirl: in this case, the 3D Navier–Stokes equations simplify to

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\left(\frac{\omega}{r}\right)+u\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{\omega}{r}\right)=\nu\left(\Delta+\frac{2}{r}\partial_{r}\right)\left(\frac{\omega}{r}\right),\qquad u=K[\omega].\end{split}

(6.1)

In the statement below, we assume that
\omega_{0},\omega_{0}^{\nu}
are axisymmetric axial vorticities.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\omega_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
be compactly supported with finite kinetic energy. Furthermore, assume that the corresponding Euler solution (
1.2
) exists globally in time with
u\in L^{\infty}_{t,loc}W^{1,\infty}_{x}
and

\begin{split}\limsup_{t\to\infty}\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=+\infty.\end{split}

Then, for any sequence of initial data
\omega_{0}^{\nu}\in L^{\infty}\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with finite kinetic energy which converges to
\omega_{0}
strongly in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
as
\nu\rightarrow 0^{+}
, we have that

\begin{split}\liminf_{\nu\to 0^{+}}\sup_{t\in[0,\infty]}\|\omega^{\nu}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=+\infty,\end{split}

where
\omega^{\nu}
is the solution to (
6.1
) with initial data
\omega^{\nu}_{0}
and viscosity
\nu
.

Observe that
Corollary
1.2

(
e.g.
see (
1.9
) of Remark
1.3
) provides a class of Euler initial data for which the hypothesis of the above result is satisfied. Global in time existence and uniqueness of strong solutions with initial data
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
to the axi-symmetric Navier–Stokes equations without swirl (
6.1
) is well-known (
[
63
]
).

Proof.

Towards a contradiction, assume that there exists a sequence
\nu_{n}\to 0^{+}
such that

\begin{split}\sup_{n\geq 1}\sup_{t\in[0,\infty]}\|\omega^{\nu_{n}}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq M\end{split}

for some
M>0
. We take some
T>0
, and since the sequence
\{\omega^{\nu_{n}}\}_{n\geq 1}
is precompact in the weak star topology of
L^{\infty}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
, by passing to a subsequence if necessary, we derive

\begin{split}\omega^{\nu_{n}}\longrightarrow\hat{\omega}\end{split}

weakly in star in
L^{\infty}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
. It is not difficult to see that
\hat{\omega}
is a weak solution to (
1.2
). Furthermore, using that
\omega^{\nu}(t,\cdot)
is weakly continuous in time with values in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, it can be checked that
\hat{\omega}(t,\cdot)\rightarrow\omega_{0}
as
t\to 0^{+}
weakly in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. From weak-strong uniqueness of Euler in the class
u\in L^{\infty}_{t}(W^{1,\infty}\cap L^{2})_{x}
(see
[
14
,
5
]
), we deduce that
\hat{\omega}=\omega
, and this is a contradiction since weak convergence implies

\begin{split}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}\leq M\end{split}

for all
T>0
. ∎
