Rich groups, weak second order logic, and applications
Olga Kharlampovich, Alexei Myasnikov, Mahmood Sohrabi

Abstract
In this paper we initiate a study of first-order rich groups, i.e., groups where the first-order logic has the same power as the weak second order logic. Surprisingly, there are quite a lot of finitely generated rich groups, they are somewhere in between hyperbolic and nilpotent groups (these ones are not rich). We provide some methods to prove that groups (and other structures) are rich and describe some of their properties. As corollaries we look at Malcev’s problems in various groups.



1
Introduction

The study of efficiency of the first-order logic goes back to Hilbert and Godel, and later, where algebra concerns, to Tarski and Malcev. For a group (or a ring, or any structure)
A
the first-order theory
Th(A)
is the set of all sentences in group (ring, etc.) language that are true in
A
, so
Th(A)
is all the possible information about
A
expressible in the first-order logic of group theory. Two groups (rings, etc.)
A
and
B
are called elementarily equivalent (
A\equiv B
) if
Th(A)=Th(B)
, i.e., they are indistinguishable in the first-order logic. Tarski and Malcev pushed forward a problem of describing groups and rings (in some natural classes) that are elementarily equivalent. If
A
and
B
are isomorphic then, obviously,
A\equiv B
. The most interesting question here is if
A\equiv B
then how close to being isomorphic
A
and
B
could be, i.e., how good the description by
Th(A)
of
A
is? It was soon understood (Löwenheim-Skolem theorem) that for any infinite structure
A
there is a structure
B
such that
A\equiv B
and they have different cardinalities, so in the question above one may want to assume that
A
and
B
have the same cardinality. This leads to the notion of categoricity, and stability, and some other important developments in modern model theory. But categoricity is a rather rare phenomenon. The following seems to be the general picture. Given a finitely generated group (or a ring or a structure)
A
there is in general little hope to characterize all arbitrary countable groups
B
with
A\equiv B
. But, rather often there is a decent chance to describe such
B
if it is assumed to be finitely generated. Algebraically, it makes sense to consider finitely generated objects, rather then countable ones. In the opposite direction (towards uncountable objects) one can start with a group or
a ring
A
and “complete” it by adding to
A
solutions to various classes of equations until it is algebraically complete (with respect to some theory).
The typical picture here is arithmetic, i.e., the ring of integers
\mathbb{Z}
: for every finitely generated ring
B
if
\mathbb{Z}\equiv B
then
\mathbb{Z}\cong B
; there are countable non-isomorphic such
B
(“non-standard models” of arithmetic); towards uncountable models one can go from
\mathbb{Z}
to
\mathbb{Q}
and then to completions
{\mathbb{C}}
, or
\mathbb{R}
, or
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, which are tame from the view-point of the first-order logic. Since Tarski and Malcev there has been many interesting results about elementary equivalence of finitely generated groups and rings. A question dominating research in this area has been if and when elementary equivalence between finitely generated groups (rings) implies isomorphism. Recently, Avni et. al.
[
3
]
coined the term
first-order rigidity
: a finitely generated group (ring)
A
is first-order rigid if any other finitely generated group (ring) elementarily equivalent to
A
is isomorphic to
A
.

In another direction Malcev proposed to test the expressive power of the first-order logic by studying definable subgroups of a given group. A subgroup
K
of a group
A
is definable in
A
if there is a first-order formula
\phi(x)
without parameters such that
K
consists precisely of those elements in
A
that make
\phi(x)
true. The main general question here is how rich are definable subsets in a group or algebra.
In particular, in 1965 Malcev asked which subgroups of free groups
F
are definable in the first-order logic. This problem was solved in
[
25
]
and
[
43
]
. It turned out that among proper subgroups of
F
only cyclic ones are definable. The same holds in arbitrary non-elementary torsion-free hyperbolic groups. It follows that the first-order theory is not quite adequate in describing subgroups in these groups. On the other hand there are groups which have interesting definable subgroups (nilpotent, metabelian, etc.). However, till now there were no known infinite groups where all subgroups were
uniformly definable
, i.e., groups
G
where for each natural
n
there is a formula
\Phi_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y)
which holds in
G
on elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},b
if and only if
b
belongs to the subgroup generated by
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
. This is a very strong definability property which is not amenable to any known algebraic techniques.

We intend to address the first-order rigidity and Malcev’s question on definability of subgroups in a wide class of groups and rings.
This approach allows one to show that the language of the first-order logic in various classical groups and algebras has the same expressive power as the language of the weak second-order logic. Such groups include

GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}),SL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}),T_{n}(\mathbb{Z}),n\geq 3

[
37
]
, various finitely generated metabelian groups (for example, the free nonabelian ones), many polycyclic groups, free associative algebras, free group algebras over infinite fields, and many others.
To prove this we show that the group (ring)
G
in question is bi-interpretable with
HF(G)
— the superstructure of the hereditary finite sets over
G
. In this case the weak second-order logic over
G
is interpretable in
G
. We term such a group (or any such structure)
G

rich
. In fact, to show that
G
is rich it suffices to bi-interpret it with any rich structure. Notice, that the arithmetic
\mathbb{Z}
is rich, so to prove that
G
is rich it suffices to show that it is bi-interpretable with the ring
\mathbb{Z}
.

Many objects associated to a rich group (or ring)
G
, such as finitely generated subgroups (subrings, ideals), the geometry of its Cayley graph, as well as many others are uniformly definable in it. We observe, that rich groups are first-order rigid. Furthermore, finitely generated rich structures are completely characterized by a single axiom. Interestingly, as we mentioned above free and torsion-free hyperbolic groups are not rich. Actually they are very far from being rich, however, their group algebras over infinite fields are rich
[
22
]
. This shows how much more expressive is the first order ring language of a group algebra of a free group compared with the first order language of the group.

There are many examples of first-order rigid groups and algebras. For instance, Avni, Lubotzky and Meiri
[
3
]
showed that non-uniform higher dimensional lattices are first-order rigid, as well as finitely generated profinite groups
[
30
]
. Lasserre showed that under some natural conditions polycyclic groups
[
28
]
are also first-order rigid, etc. Some of these groups are rich and some not. It turns out that studying richness or lack there of is also very useful in studying arbitrary groups (rings) elementarily equivalent to a given one. For example, when a finitely generated group
G
is bi-interpretable with
{\mathbb{Z}}
, in many cases arbitrary groups that are elementarily equivalent to
G
seem to have a very particular structure, they are kind of “completions” or “closures” of
G
with respect to a ring
R
elementarily equivalent to
{\mathbb{Z}}
. When dealing with classical groups or algebras such notions of completion or closure coincide with the classical ones, where completions have the same “algebraic scheme”, but the points are over the ring
R
as above. The typical example is the group
G=\textrm{SL}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
, it is rich (See (1) in Theorem
26
below), so it is first-order rigid, moreover, any other group
H
with
G\equiv H
is isomorphic to
\textrm{SL}_{n}(R)
with
R\equiv\mathbb{Z}
. On the other hand the “extent” to which a group
G
is not rich also often seems to affect the structure of arbitrary groups elementarily equivalent to
G
. Again it seems such groups are “deformations” of “exact completions” or “exact closures” of
G
over a ring
R
as above. It only seems proper that these deformations can usually be captured by cohomological data. For example, the group
\textrm{T}_{n}(\mathcal{O})
is not rich, if the ring
\mathcal{O}
of integers of a number field has an infinite group of units (though
\textrm{T}_{n}(\mathcal{O})
and
\mathcal{O}
are mutually interpretable in each other). In this case the lack of richness is modulo the infinite center, therefore any group
H
with
H\equiv\textrm{T}_{n}(\mathcal{O})
is an abelian deformation of a group
\textrm{T}_{n}(R)
where
R\equiv\mathcal{O}
(See (3) in Theorem
26
below). It is very interesting to study groups which are rich but there is no any obvious “algebraic scheme” lying around, for example, finitely generated free metabelian groups.
In this case the completions still exist, but they are not described by any algebraic schemes, the schemes here are more general.

2
Interpretability and bi-iterpretagbility

2.1
Interpretability

The model-theoretic technique of interpretation or definability is crucial in our considerations. Because of that we remind here some precise definitions and several known facts that may not be very familiar to algebraists.

A
language
(or
a signature
)
L
is a triple
(Fun,Pr,C)
, where
Fun=\{f,\ldots\}
is a set of functional (or operational) symbols
f
coming together with their arities
n_{f}\in\mathbb{N}
,
Pr
is a set of relation (or predicate) symbols
Pr=\{P,\ldots\}
coming together with their arities
n_{P}\in\mathbb{N}
, and a set of constant symbols
C=\{c,\ldots\}
. Sometimes we write
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
or
P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
to show that
n_{f}=n
or
n_{P}=n
. Usually we denote variables by small letters
x,y,z,a,b,u,v,\ldots
, while the same symbols with bars
\bar{x},\bar{y},\ldots
denote tuples of the corresponding variables, say
\bar{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. In this paper we always assume, if not said otherwise, that the languages we consider are finite. The following languages appear frequently throughout the text: the language of semigroups
\{\cdot\}
, where
\cdot
is the binary multiplication symbol; the language of monoids
\{\cdot,1\}
, where 1 is the constant symbol for the identity element; the language of groups
\{\cdot,^{-1},1\}
, where
-1
is the symbol of inversion; and the language of rings
\{+,\cdot,0\}
with the standard symbols for addition, multiplication, and the additive identity
0
. Sometimes we add the constant
1
to form the language of unitary rings (a priori, our rings are not unitary).

An interpretation of a constant symbol
c
in a set
A
is an element
c^{A}\in A
. For a functional symbol
f
an interpretation in
A
is a function
f^{A}\colon A^{n_{f}}\to A
, and for a predicate
P
it is a set
P^{A}\subseteq A^{n_{P}}
.

A structure in the language
L
(an
L
-structure) with the base set
A
, sometimes denoted by
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;L\rangle
or simply by

{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;f,\ldots,P,\ldots,c,\ldots\rangle
, is the set
A
together with interpretations
f^{A},\ldots,P^{A},\ldots,c^{A},\ldots
Sometimes we will denote this structure also by
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;f^{A},\ldots,P^{A},\ldots,c^{A},\ldots\rangle
. For a given structure
{\mathbb{A}}
by
L({\mathbb{A}})
we denote the language of
{\mathbb{A}}
. When the language
L
is clear from the context, we follow the standard algebraic practice and denote the structure
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;L\rangle
simply by
A
. For example, we refer to a field
\mathbb{F}=\langle F;+,\cdot,0,1\rangle
simply as
F
, or to a group
\mathbb{G}=\langle G;\cdot,^{-1},1\rangle
as
G
, etc. Sometimes we refer to a first-order formula in a language
L
as to an
L
-formula and denote by
{\mathcal{F}}_{L}
the set of all
L
-formulas.

The graph of a constant
c
in
A
is the set
\{c^{A}\}
, the graph of a function
f
in
A
is
\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n_{f}},b)\in A^{n_{f}+1}\mid f^{A}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n_{f}})=b,b_{1},\ldots,b_{n_{f}}\in A\}
, and the graph of a predicate
P
in
A
is
P^{A}
or
\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n_{P}})\in A^{n_{P}}\mid{\mathbb{A}}\models P^{A}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n_{P}})\}
.

Let
{\mathbb{B}}=\langle B;L({\mathbb{B}})\rangle
be a structure. A subset
A\subseteq B^{n}
is called
definable
(
0
-definable
, or
absolutely definable
, or
definable without parameters
) in
{\mathbb{B}}
if there is a formula
\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
(without parameters) in
L({\mathbb{B}})
such that
A=\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in B^{n}\mid{\mathbb{B}}\models\phi(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\}
. In this case we denote
A
by
\phi(B^{n})
or
\phi({\mathbb{B}})
and say that
\phi
defines
A
in
{\mathbb{B}}
. If
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
is a formula in
L({\mathbb{B}})
and
\bar{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})
is a tuple of elements from
B
, then the set
\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in B^{n}\mid{\mathbb{B}}\models\psi(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n},p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})\}
is called
definable
in
{\mathbb{B}}

with parameters

\bar{p}
and denoted by
\psi(B^{n},\bar{p})
or
\psi({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
.

Let
c
,
f
and
P
be a constant, an operator and a predicate from some language
L
(may be
L\neq L({\mathbb{B}})
) which have interpretations
c^{A}
,
f^{A}
,
P^{A}
on the set
A\subseteq B^{n}
. The interpretations
c^{A}
,
f^{A}
and
P^{A}
are
definable
in
{\mathbb{B}}
if their graphs are definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
. Sometimes in this case we say that the constant
c
, the operator
f
and the predicate
P
are definable on
A
in
{\mathbb{B}}
.

In the same vein an algebraic structure
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;f,\ldots,P,\ldots,c,\ldots\rangle
is
definable
(or
0
-
definable
, or
absolutely definable
) in
{\mathbb{B}}
if there is a definable subset
A^{\ast}\subseteq B^{n}
and interpretations
f^{A},P^{A},c^{A}
on
A^{\ast}
of the symbols
f,P,c
all definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
, such that the structure
{\mathbb{A}}^{\ast}=\langle A^{\ast};f^{A},\ldots,P^{A},\ldots,c^{A},\ldots\rangle
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{A}}
. (Notice, that constants
c,\ldots
belong to the language of
{\mathbb{A}}
, they are not parameters.)
For example, if
Z(G)
is the center of a group

then it is definable as a group in
G
.

One can do a bit more in terms of definability. In the notation above if
\sim
is a definable equivalence relation on a definable subset
A^{\ast}\subseteq B^{n}
then we say that the quotient set
A^{\ast}/\sim
is
interpretable
in
{\mathbb{B}}
. Furthermore, interpretations
f^{A},P^{A},c^{A}
of symbols

on the quotient set
A^{\ast}/\sim
are
interpretable
in
{\mathbb{B}}
if the full preimages of their graphs in
A^{\ast}
are definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
.

Definition 1
.

An algebraic structure
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;f,\ldots,P,\ldots,c,\ldots\rangle
is absolutely interpretable (or
0
-interpretable) in a structure
{\mathbb{B}}=\langle B;L({\mathbb{B}})\rangle
if there is a subset
A^{\ast}\subseteq B^{n}
definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
, there is an equivalence relation
\sim
on
A^{\ast}
definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
, and there are interpretations
f^{A}
,
P^{A}
,
c^{A}
of the symbols

on the quotient set
A^{\ast}/{\sim}
all interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
, such that the structure
{\mathbb{A}}^{\ast}=\langle A^{\ast}/{\sim};f^{A},\ldots,P^{A},\ldots,c^{A},\ldots\rangle
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{A}}
.

For example, if
N
is a normal definable subgroup of a group
G
, then the equivalence relation
x\sim y
on
G
given by
xN=yN
is definable in
G
, so the quotient set
G/N
of all right cosets of
N
is interpretable in
G
. It is easy to see that the multiplication induced
from
G
on
G/N
is also interpretable in
G
. This show that the quotient group
G/N
is interpretable in
G
.

Now we introduce some useful notation. An interpretation of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
is described by the following set of formulas in the language
L({\mathbb{B}})

\Gamma=\{U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}),E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2}),Q_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{t_{Q}})\mid Q\in L({\mathbb{A}})\}

(here
\bar{x}
and
\bar{x}_{i}
are
n
-tuples of variables) which interpret
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
(as in the Definition
1
above). Namely,
U_{\Gamma}
defines in
{\mathbb{B}}
a subset
A_{\Gamma}=U_{\Gamma}(B^{n})\subseteq B^{n}
,
E_{\Gamma}
defines an equivalence relation
\sim_{\Gamma}
on
A_{\Gamma}
, and the formulas
Q_{\Gamma}
define preimages of graphs for constants, functions, predicates

on the quotient set
A_{\Gamma}/\sim_{\Gamma}
in such a way that the structure
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}})=\langle A_{\Gamma}/\sim_{\Gamma};L({\mathbb{A}})\rangle
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{A}}
. Note, that we interpret a constant

in the structure
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}})
by the
\sim_{\Gamma}
-equivalence class of some tuple
\bar{b}_{c}\in A_{\Gamma}
defined in
{\mathbb{B}}
by the formula

. We refer to
\Gamma
as an
interpretation code
or just
code
. The number

is called the
dimension
of
\Gamma
, denoted

. And we write
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}})
if the code
\Gamma
interprets
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
as described above. By
\mu_{\Gamma}
we denote a surjective map
A_{\Gamma}\to A
(here
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;L({\mathbb{A}})\rangle
) that gives rise to an isomorphism
\bar{\mu}_{\Gamma}\colon\Gamma({\mathbb{B}})\to{\mathbb{A}}
. We refer to this map
\mu_{\Gamma}
as the
coordinate map
of the interpretation
\Gamma
. Sometimes we call the relation
\sim_{\Gamma}
the
kernel
of the coordinate map
\mu_{\Gamma}
and denote it by
\ker(\mu_{\Gamma})
. Finally, notation
\mu\colon{\mathbb{B}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{A}}
means that
{\mathbb{A}}
is interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
with the coordinate map
\mu

We use this notation throughout the paper.

More generally, the formulas that interpret
{\mathbb{A}}
in

may contain elements from

that are not in the language

, i.e., some parameters, say

\Gamma=\{U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y}),E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y}),Q_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{t_{Q}},\bar{y})\mid Q\in L({\mathbb{A}})\}

(1)

so that after the assignment

the code
\Gamma
interprets
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
. In this event we write
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
(here
\bar{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})
), and say that

is interpretable in

by the code
\Gamma

. We refer to

. In the case when

one gets again the absolute interpretability. The coordinate map

\mu_{\Gamma,\bar{p}}

with the code

and coordinate map
\mu_{\Gamma,\bar{p}}
. Sometimes we will also write

. More generally, a subset

\Gamma

(\psi_{1}\vee\psi_{2})^{\prime}=\psi^{\prime}_{1}\vee\psi^{\prime}_{2},\ \ (\psi_{1}\wedge\psi_{2})^{\prime}=\psi^{\prime}_{1}\wedge\psi^{\prime}_{2},

(\psi_{1}\to\psi_{2})^{\prime}=\psi^{\prime}_{1}\to\psi^{\prime}_{2},\ \ (\neg\psi_{1})^{\prime}=\neg\psi^{\prime}_{1}.

Now for quantifiers we define (below
\forall\bar{x}
denotes
\forall x_{1}\ldots\forall x_{n}
and
\exists\bar{x}
denotes
\exists x_{1}\ldots\exists x_{n}
, provided
\bar{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
)

\exists x\>\psi_{0}\;\;\to\;\;\exists\bar{x}\>(U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\wedge\psi^{\prime}_{0}),\ \ \forall x\>\psi_{0}\;\;\to\;\;\forall\bar{x}\>(U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\to\psi^{\prime}_{0}).

And finally, we define the
\Gamma
-translation by

\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\;\;\to\;\;\psi_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m},\bar{y})=\psi^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m},\bar{y})\wedge\bigwedge\limits_{i=1}^{m}U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{i},\bar{y}).

We use the following denotation below. Assume that
{\mathbb{A}}
is interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
with parameters
\bar{p}
and code
\Gamma
, and
\mu_{\Gamma}\colon U_{\Gamma}(B^{n},\bar{p})\to A
is the coordinate map of the interpretation
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
, where

. For any map
\psi\to\psi^{+}
translating formulas

in

into formulas

in

(it could be
\Gamma

{\mathbb{A}}\models\psi\Longleftrightarrow_{\mu_{\Gamma}}{\mathbb{B}}\models\psi^{+}

to express the following statement: for any elements

and
\bar{b}_{i}\in\mu_{\Gamma}^{-1}(a_{i})
if
{\mathbb{A}}\models\psi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
then
{\mathbb{B}}\models\psi^{+}(\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{m},\bar{p})
,
and inversely for any
\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{m}\in B^{n}
if
{\mathbb{B}}\models\psi^{+}(\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{m},\bar{p})
then there are
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in A
, such that
\bar{b}_{i}\in\mu_{\Gamma}^{-1}(a_{i})
and

.

The following is a principal result on interpretability (in various forms it appears in the literature, see, for example
[
20
]
or
[
34
]
).

Lemma 1
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}
be interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
with parameters
\bar{p}
, so
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
for some code
\Gamma
,
n=\dim\Gamma
, and
\mu_{\Gamma}
be the corresponding coordinate map. Then in the notations above one has:

1)

For any formula
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
of
L({\mathbb{A}})
the translation
\psi_{\Gamma}
is effectively constructed, and

{\mathbb{A}}\models\psi\Longleftrightarrow_{\mu_{\Gamma}}{\mathbb{B}}\models\psi_{\Gamma}.

In particular, if
{\mathbb{A}}
is
0
-interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
, then the formula
\psi_{\Gamma}
does not contain parameters from
{\mathbb{B}}
.

2)

If
S\subseteq A^{m}
is a definable with parameters subset then
\mu_{\Gamma}^{-1}(S)\subseteq B^{m\cdot n}
is definable with parameters. Namely, if
S\subseteq A^{m}
defines by a formula
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},a_{1},\ldots,a_{q})
, where
a_{i}\in A
, then
\mu_{\Gamma}^{-1}(S)
defines by a formula
\psi_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m},\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{q},\bar{p})
with
\bar{b}_{i}\in\mu_{\Gamma}^{-1}(a_{i})
. In particular, if
{\mathbb{A}}
is
0
-interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
and
S\subseteq A^{m}
is definable (without parameters), then
\mu_{\Gamma}^{-1}(S)
is definable (without parameters).

3)

{\mathbb{A}}\models\psi\Longleftrightarrow_{\mu_{\Gamma}}{\mathbb{B}}\models\psi^{+},

where
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
is any
\forall\exists
-formula in
L({\mathbb{A}})
of the type

\forall\>t_{1}\ldots\forall\>t_{s}\;(\bigwedge\limits_{i=1}^{r}\varphi^{i}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s},x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\>\longrightarrow\\
\longrightarrow\>\exists z_{1}\ldots\exists\>z_{q}\;\bigwedge\limits_{j=1}^{d}\psi^{j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{q},t_{1},\ldots,t_{s},x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\,)

and
\psi^{+}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m},\bar{y})
is an
L({\mathbb{B}})
-formula defined by

(\forall\>\bar{t}_{1}\ldots\forall\>\bar{t}_{s}\;(\bigwedge\limits_{i=1}^{r}\varphi^{i}_{\Gamma}(\bar{t}_{1},\ldots,\bar{t}_{s},\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m},\bar{y})\>\longrightarrow\\
\longrightarrow\>\exists\bar{z}_{1}\ldots\exists\>\bar{z}_{q}\;\bigwedge\limits_{j=1}^{d}{\psi^{j}_{\Gamma}(\bar{z}_{1},\ldots,\bar{z}_{q},\bar{t}_{1},\ldots,\bar{t}_{s},\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m},\bar{y})}\,)\,)\;\wedge\\
\;\wedge\bigwedge\limits_{i=1}^{m}U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{i},\bar{y}).

Proof.

The statement 1) follows by induction on construction of
\psi
and the definition of the interpretation, while 2), 3) follow from 1).
∎

The result above shows that every set definable in
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
is definable with parameters in
{\mathbb{B}}
. However, the converse is not true in general, i.e., a subset of
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
may not be definable in
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
. Following
[
34
]
we say that interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
is
pure
if every subset of
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
is definable in
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
. We will say more on pure interpretations in Section
2.3
.

Recall that a
sentence
in

Lemma 2
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}
be regularly interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
, so
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\phi)
for some code
\Gamma
and formula

\psi

of

Proof.

Let
\psi
be a sentence in the language
L({\mathbb{A}})
. To construct

take the
\Gamma

and put

\psi_{\Gamma,\phi}=\forall\bar{y}(\phi(\bar{y})\rightarrow\psi_{\Gamma}(\bar{y})).

Also we may take
\psi_{\Gamma,\phi}=\exists\bar{y}(\phi(\bar{y})\rightarrow\psi_{\Gamma}(\bar{y}))
.
∎

Again, we refer to the map

or
reduction
with respect to the code
\Gamma
and formula
\phi
.

Admissibility conditions for an interpretation
\Gamma
:
Let
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
, where
\Gamma
is the code (
1
). Then there is a set
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}
of formulas in the language
L({\mathbb{B}})
in variables
\bar{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})

{\mathbb{B}}^{\prime}
and any tuple

of elements of
{\mathbb{B}}^{\prime}

the code

\bar{q}

\Gamma

say that:

1.

U_{\Gamma}({\mathbb{B}}^{\prime},\bar{q})\neq\emptyset
:

\exists\,\bar{x}\;U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y});

2.

E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{q})
defines an equivalence relation
\sim
on

\forall\,\bar{x}\;(U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\,\longrightarrow\,E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{x},\bar{y})),

\exists\,\bar{x}\;(U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\wedge c_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y})),

4.

Every function

\forall\,\bar{x}_{1}\ldots\forall\,\bar{x}_{n_{f}}\;(\bigwedge\limits_{i=1}^{n_{f}}U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{i},\bar{y})\,\longrightarrow\,\exists\,\bar{z}\;(U_{\Gamma}(\bar{z},\bar{y})\wedge f_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{n_{f}},\bar{z},\bar{y}))),

\wedge\,f_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{n_{f}},\bar{z},\bar{y})\,\wedge\,f_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,\bar{x}_{n_{f}}^{\ast},\bar{z}^{\ast},\bar{y})\,\longrightarrow\,E_{\Gamma}(\bar{z},\bar{z}^{\ast},\bar{y}));

5.

Every predicate
P\in L({\mathbb{A}})
has a correct interpretation on

:

\forall\,\bar{x}_{1}\ldots\forall\,\bar{x}_{n_{P}}\forall\,\bar{x}_{1}^{\ast}\ldots\forall\,\bar{x}_{n_{P}}^{\ast}\\
(\bigwedge\limits_{i=1}^{n_{P}}(U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{i},\bar{y})\,\wedge\,U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{i}^{\ast},\bar{y})\,\wedge\,E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{i},\bar{x}_{i}^{\ast},\bar{y}))\,\longrightarrow\,\\
\,\longrightarrow\,(P_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{n_{P}},\bar{y})\longleftrightarrow P_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,\bar{x}_{n_{P}}^{\ast},\bar{y}))).

It is clear that if the language
L({\mathbb{A}})
contains at least one constant

then the item 1 above can be omitted. In this way

if and only if
L({\mathbb{A}})
-structure

is well-defined and it is interpreted in
{\mathbb{B}}
. (However, we cannot assert that
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}}^{\prime},\bar{q})\simeq{\mathbb{A}}
!)

Note since the language
L({\mathbb{A}})
is finite then the set
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}
is also finite. In this case one can take a single formula
\Theta_{\Gamma}(\bar{y})=\bigwedge\{\theta(\bar{y})\mid\theta(\bar{y})\in\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}\}

-structure
{\mathbb{B}}^{\prime}

{\mathbb{B}}^{\prime}\models\Theta_{\Gamma}(\bar{q})
if and only if
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}}^{\prime},\bar{q})
is well-defined.

Now, let
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\phi)
be a regular interpretation of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
, i.e.,
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
for any tuple
\bar{p}\in\phi({\mathbb{B}})
. Consider the set
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}=\{\theta_{i}(\bar{y})\mid i\in I\}
of admissible conditions.
Then for every

the sentence

\theta_{i,\phi}=\forall\,\bar{y}\>(\phi(\bar{y})\,\to\,\theta_{i}(\bar{y}))

holds in
{\mathbb{B}}
. Let
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma,\phi}=\{\theta_{i,\phi}\mid i\in I\}\cup\{\exists\bar{y}\,\phi(\bar{y})\}
.
Then for every
L({\mathbb{B}})
-structure
{\mathbb{B}}^{\prime}
if
{\mathbb{B}}^{\prime}\models\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma,\phi}
then the set
\phi({\mathbb{B}}^{\prime})
is not empty, and for every tuple

the

-structure
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}}^{\prime},\bar{q})

Composition of interpretations:
Let
{\mathbb{A}},{\mathbb{B}}
and
{\mathbb{C}}
be algebraic structures. To find out how the composition of interpretations
\mu_{\Gamma}\colon{\mathbb{B}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{A}}
and
\mu_{\Delta}\colon{\mathbb{C}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
works, we define previously the composition of codes. Suppose that
\Gamma
and
\Delta
are codes, such that
\Gamma
is a set of
L({\mathbb{B}})
-formulas as in (
1
) and
\Delta
is a set of
L({\mathbb{C}})
-formulas:

\Delta=\{U_{\Delta}(\bar{x},\bar{z}),E_{\Delta}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{z}),Q_{\Delta}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{t_{Q}},\bar{z})\mid Q\in L({\mathbb{B}})\},

where
|\bar{x}|=|\bar{x}_{i}|=\dim\Delta
,
|\bar{z}|=\dim_{par}\Delta
. Then the
composition
of codes
\Gamma
and
\Delta
is the code

\Gamma\circ\Delta=\{U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta},E_{\Gamma\circ\Delta},Q_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}\mid Q\in L({\mathbb{A}})\}=\{(U_{\Gamma})_{\Delta},(E_{\Gamma})_{\Delta},(Q_{\Gamma})_{\Delta}\mid Q\in L({\mathbb{A}})\}.

Note that

\displaystyle\dim\Gamma\circ\Delta=\dim\Gamma\cdot\dim\Delta,

\displaystyle\dim_{par}\Gamma\circ\Delta=\dim_{par}\Gamma\cdot\dim\Delta+\dim_{par}\Delta.

The following are two important technical results about the transitivity of interpretations, that we use throughout the paper.

Lemma 3
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}},{\mathbb{B}}
and
{\mathbb{C}}
be algebraic structures. Then the following hold:

1)

If
{\mathbb{A}}
is interpretable with parameters (absolutely interpretable) in
{\mathbb{B}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
is interpretable with parameters (absolutely interpretable) in
{\mathbb{C}}
, then
{\mathbb{A}}
is interpretable with parameters (absolutely interpretable) in
{\mathbb{C}}
. In more detail, if
\mu_{\Gamma,\bar{p}}\colon{\mathbb{B}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{A}}
and
\mu_{\Delta,\bar{q}}\colon{\mathbb{C}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
then
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{C}},\bar{p}^{\ast})
, where
\bar{p}^{\ast}=(\bar{p}_{0},\bar{q})
with
\bar{p}_{0}\in\mu_{\Delta,\bar{q}}^{-1}(\bar{p})
(and the
L({\mathbb{A}})
-structure
\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{C}},\bar{p}^{\ast})
does not depend on the choice of
\bar{p}_{0}\in\mu_{\Delta,\bar{q}}^{-1}(\bar{p})
).

2)

If
{\mathbb{A}}
is regularly interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
is regularly interpretable in
{\mathbb{C}}
, then
{\mathbb{A}}
is regularly interpretable in
{\mathbb{C}}
. In more detail, if
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\phi)
and
{\mathbb{B}}\simeq\Delta({\mathbb{C}},\psi)
then
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{C}},\phi^{\ast})
, where
\phi^{\ast}=\phi_{\Delta}\wedge\psi
. Moreover,
\bar{p}^{\ast}\in\phi^{\ast}({\mathbb{C}})
if and only if there exist
\bar{p}\in\phi({\mathbb{B}})
,
\bar{q}\in\psi({\mathbb{C}})
(and consequently
there exists an interpretation
{\mathbb{B}}\simeq_{\mu_{\Delta,\bar{q}}}\Delta({\mathbb{C}},\bar{q})
) and
\bar{p}_{0}\in\mu^{-1}_{\Delta,\bar{q}}(\bar{p})
such that
\bar{p}^{\ast}=(\bar{p}_{0},\bar{q})
.

Proof.

1) By Lemma
1
for any tuples
\bar{b},\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{i},\ldots,
of length
n=\dim\Gamma
of elements of
{\mathbb{B}}
, any their preimages
\bar{c},\bar{c}_{1},\ldots,\bar{c}_{i},\ldots,
under
\mu_{\Delta,\bar{q}}
(that are tuples of length
m=\dim\Gamma\cdot\dim\Delta
from
{\mathbb{C}}
) and any
\bar{p}_{0}\in\mu_{\Delta,\bar{q}}^{-1}(\bar{p})
of length

of elements of
{\mathbb{C}}
one has

{\mathbb{B}}\models U_{\Gamma}(\bar{b},\bar{p})\iff{\mathbb{C}}\models U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}(\bar{c},\bar{p}_{0},\bar{q});

(2)

{\mathbb{B}}\models E_{\Gamma}(\bar{b}_{1},\bar{b}_{2},\bar{p})\iff{\mathbb{C}}\models E_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}(\bar{c}_{1},\bar{c}_{2},\bar{p}_{0},\bar{q});

(3)

{\mathbb{B}}\models Q_{\Gamma}(\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{t_{Q}},\bar{p})\iff{\mathbb{C}}\models Q_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}(\bar{c}_{1},\ldots,\bar{c}_{t_{Q}},\bar{p}_{0},\bar{q}).

(4)

Here if
\bar{c}_{i}\in C^{m}
and the right parts of (
2
), (
3
), (
4
) are true, then
\bar{c}_{i}\in\mu_{\Delta,\bar{q}}^{-1}(B^{n})
and the left parts are true as well.

Furthermore, all formulas from the admissibility conditions
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}
are equivalent to
\forall\exists
-formulas of the type that in Lemma
1
. Since
{\mathbb{B}}\models\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}(\bar{p})
, so Lemma
1
proves that the algebraic structure
{\mathbb{C}}
satisfies the admissibility conditions

on parameters

. Thus

is well-defined and it does not depend on the choice of

.

Consider the map
\mu_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}\colon U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}({\mathbb{C}},\bar{p}^{\ast})\to A

\mu_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}=\mu_{\Gamma,\bar{p}}\circ\mu_{\Delta,\bar{q}}
. As

is surjective and (

is an

-isomorphism and due to (

-isomorphism as well. Thereby,

is interpretable in
{\mathbb{C}}
with the code

and parameters

. It is clear that if

2) Suppose now that
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\phi)
and

. Take any tuples
\bar{p}\in\phi({\mathbb{B}})

. Then

and there exists an interpretation

. And since

, then by Lemma

there exists
\bar{p}\in\phi({\mathbb{B}})
with

. Thus for any

there exists an interpretation

, i.e.,
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{C}},\phi^{\ast})
.
∎

2.2
Properties preserved under interpretations

Interpretations provide a useful tool to show that algebraic structures satisfy some particular properties, that are inherited under interpretations. We say that a property
P
is
preserved (or inherited) under interpretations
if for any structures
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
such that
{\mathbb{A}}
is interpreted in
{\mathbb{B}}
if
P
holds in
{\mathbb{B}}
then
P
holds in
{\mathbb{A}}
. Some of such properties
P
are preserved only under absolute or regular interpretations, while others are preserved under interpretations with parameters. In this section we mention some of these properties that we use in the sequel.

Lemma 4
.

The following holds:

1)

Let
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\phi)
for some code
\Gamma
and a formula
\phi
, i.e.,
{\mathbb{A}}
is regularly interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
. Then if the first-order theory
Th({\mathbb{B}})
is decidable then
Th({\mathbb{A}})
is also decidable.

2)

Let
{\mathbb{A}}_{1},{\mathbb{A}}_{2}
be
L({\mathbb{A}})
-structures and
{\mathbb{B}}_{1},{\mathbb{B}}_{2}
be
L({\mathbb{B}})
-structures. If
{\mathbb{A}}_{i}=\Gamma({\mathbb{B}}_{i},\phi)
,
i=1,2
, for some code
\Gamma
and a formula
\phi
, then
{\mathbb{B}}_{1}\equiv{\mathbb{B}}_{2}
implies
{\mathbb{A}}_{1}\equiv{\mathbb{A}}_{2}
.

3)

If
{\mathbb{A}}=\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
and
{\mathbb{B}}\preceq{\mathbb{B}}_{1}
is an elementary extension of
{\mathbb{B}}
then
{\mathbb{B}}_{1}
satisfies the admissibility conditions
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}
on the tuple
\bar{p}
,
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
is a substructure of
{\mathbb{A}}_{1}=\Gamma({\mathbb{B}}_{1},\bar{p})
and
{\mathbb{A}}\preceq{\mathbb{A}}_{1}
is an elementary extension of
{\mathbb{A}}
.

Proof.

If
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\phi)
for some code
\Gamma
and a formula
\phi
, then by Lemma
2
there is an algorithm that for any sentence
\psi
in the language
L({\mathbb{A}})
constructs a sentence
\psi_{\Gamma,\phi}
in the language
L({\mathbb{B}})
such that

{\mathbb{A}}\models\psi\Longleftrightarrow{\mathbb{B}}\models\psi_{\Gamma,\phi}.

Therefore, if
Th({\mathbb{B}})
is decidable then
Th({\mathbb{A}})
is decidable. This proves 1).

Almost the same argument works for 2), here one only need to notice that the sentence
\psi_{\Gamma,\phi}
depends only on
\psi,\Gamma
, and
\phi
.

3) Since
{\mathbb{B}}_{1}
is an elementary extension of
{\mathbb{B}}
the admissibility condition
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}
holds in
{\mathbb{B}}_{1}
on the tuple
\bar{p}
. Hence
(\Gamma,\bar{p})
interprets in
{\mathbb{B}}_{1}
an
L({\mathbb{A}})
-structure
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}}_{1},\bar{p})
, which has
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
as a substructure. Let
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
be a formula in
L({\mathbb{A}})
and
\psi_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m},\bar{y})
its
\Gamma
-translation. Now take a tuple of elements
\bar{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
from the structure
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
. We intend to show that
\psi(\bar{a})
holds in
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
if and only if it holds in
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}}_{1},\bar{p})
. Consider a tuple
\bar{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})
, where
b_{i}
is a tuple from
A_{\Gamma}=U_{\Gamma}(B^{n})
such that
a_{i}
is the
\sim_{\Gamma}
-equivalence class of
b_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
. Then by Lemma
1

\psi(\bar{a})
holds in
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
if and only if
\psi_{\Gamma}(\bar{b},\bar{p})
holds in
{\mathbb{B}}
. Since
{\mathbb{B}}\preceq{\mathbb{B}}_{1}
the formula
\psi_{\Gamma}(\bar{b},\bar{p})
holds in
{\mathbb{B}}
if and only if it holds in
{\mathbb{B}}_{1}
. Then, again by Lemma
1

\psi_{\Gamma}(\bar{b},\bar{p})
holds in
{\mathbb{B}}_{1}
if and only if
\psi(\bar{a})
holds in
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}}_{1},\bar{p})
. Hence

\Gamma({\mathbb{B}}_{1},\bar{p})
is an elementary extension of
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
, as claimed. This proves the lemma.
∎

We also mention the following basic result. Let
{\mathbb{N}}
be the set of natural numbers. Then
{\mathbb{N}}=\langle{\mathbb{N}};+,\cdot,0,1\rangle
is called the
arithmetic
or the
standard arithmetic
. The Gödel first incompleteness theorem (more precisely, the Gödel’s argument there) implies that the first-order theory
Th({\mathbb{N}})
of
{\mathbb{N}}
is undecidable. We state this in the form of the following result.

Lemma 5
.

[
54
,
16
]

If the arithmetic
{\mathbb{N}}
is interpretable (with parameters) in a structure
{\mathbb{B}}
then the first-order theory
Th({\mathbb{B}})
is undecidable.

Note that
{\mathbb{N}}
is
0
-interpretable in the ring of integers
{\mathbb{Z}}=\langle{\mathbb{Z}};+,\cdot,0,1\rangle
. Indeed, by Lagrange’s Four Squares Theorem every integer is a sum of four squares, so the formula

\exists x_{1}\exists x_{2}\exists x_{3}\exists x_{4}(y=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2})

defines
{\mathbb{N}}
in
{\mathbb{Z}}
. Hence, if
{\mathbb{Z}}
is interpretable (with parameters) in
{\mathbb{A}}
then
Th({\mathbb{A}})
is undecidable. The following theorem, due to J. Robinson, shows this result can be extended to arbitrary rings of algebraic integers or algebraic number fields.

Theorem 1
.

[
45
]

The following holds:

1)

The ring
{\mathbb{Z}}
is
0
-definable in any ring of algebraic integers
\mathcal{O}
.

2)

The ring of algebraic integers
\mathcal{O}
is
0
-definable in its field of fractions.

3)

It follows from 1) and 2) that the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}
is
0
-definable in any algebraic number field
F
.

Scanlon extended the result above as follows.

Theorem 2
.

[
51
]

The ring
{\mathbb{Z}}
is interpretable in any finitely generated infinite field
F
.

Corollary 1
.

If any of the rings
\mathcal{O}
or
F
from Theorems
1
and
2
are interpretable in a structure
{\mathbb{A}}
then the first-order theory
Th({\mathbb{A}})
is undecidable.

At present, there are known many rings, groups, and monoids
R
where the arithmetic
{\mathbb{N}}
is interpretable (see, for instance, the Sections 6-14 below), so for all of them Corollary
1
holds when
\mathcal{O}
or
F
is replaced by
R
. However, the rings
\mathcal{O}
and
F
above most often appear as interpretable in other structures, especially, the rings of algebraic integers
\mathcal{O}
typically occur via interpretations in finitely-generated groups (see, for example,
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
, where the rings
\mathcal{O}
are interpreted in groups and rings by means of equations).
In this direction we would like to mention the following general result.

Theorem 3

(Noskov
[
40
]
)
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated soluble group. Then if
G
is non-virtually abelian then
{\mathbb{Z}}
is interpretable in
G
.

There are many properties that are preserved under interpretations:
\lambda
-stability, being
\lambda
-saturation,
\omega
-categoricity, independence property, and finite Morley rank, to mention a few (for the proof about Morley rank we refer to
[

]
, the others are rather easy to prove using the translation
\phi\to\phi_{\Gamma}
above).

2.3
Bi-interpretability

In this section we discuss a very strong version of mutual interpretability of two structures, so-called
bi-interpretability
.

Definition 2
.

Two algebraic structures
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are called bi-interpretable (with parameters) in each other if the following conditions hold:

1)

{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are interpretable (with parameters) in each other, so
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
and
{\mathbb{B}}\simeq\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{q})
for some codes
\Gamma
and
\Delta
and tuples of parameters
\bar{p},\bar{q}
. By transitivity
{\mathbb{A}}
, as well as
{\mathbb{B}}
, is interpretable (with parameters) in itself, so
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{p}^{\ast})
and
{\mathbb{B}}\simeq\Delta\circ\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{q}^{\ast})
, where
\circ
denotes composition of interpretations and
\bar{p}^{\ast}
,
\bar{q}^{\ast}
the corresponding parameters.

2)

There is a formula
\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{s})
in the language
L({\mathbb{A}})
such that
\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{p}^{\ast})
defines in
{\mathbb{A}}
an isomorphism
\bar{\mu}_{\mathbb{A}}\colon\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{p}^{\ast})\to{\mathbb{A}}
(more precisely, it defines the coordinate map
\mu_{\mathbb{A}}\colon A_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}\to A
). Similarly, there is a formula
\theta_{\mathbb{B}}(\bar{v},x,\bar{t})
in the language
L({\mathbb{B}})
such that
\theta_{\mathbb{B}}(\bar{v},x,\bar{q}^{\ast})
defines in
{\mathbb{B}}
an isomorphism
\bar{\mu}_{\mathbb{B}}\colon\Delta\circ\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{q}^{\ast})\to{\mathbb{B}}
(more precisely, it defines the coordinate map
\mu_{\mathbb{B}}\colon B_{\Delta\circ\Gamma}\to B
).

Here
|\bar{p}^{\ast}|=\dim_{par}\Gamma\circ\Delta=|\bar{p}|\cdot\dim\Delta+|\bar{q}|
,
|\bar{q}^{\ast}|=\dim_{par}\Delta\circ\Gamma=|\bar{q}|\cdot\dim\Gamma+|\bar{p}|
and
|\bar{u}|=|\bar{v}|=\dim\Delta\circ\Gamma=\dim\Gamma\circ\Delta=\dim\Gamma\cdot\dim\Delta
.

Remark 1
.

On the definition of bi-interpretability:

a)

In the Definition
2
the parameters
\bar{p}^{\ast}
and
\bar{q}^{\ast}
can be explicitly expressed from
\bar{p}
and
\bar{q}
using the coordinate maps
\mu_{\Gamma}\colon A_{\Gamma}\to A
and
\mu_{\Delta}\colon B_{\Delta}\to B
of the interpretations
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
and
{\mathbb{B}}\simeq\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{q})
. Indeed, following the construction of the compositions
\Gamma\circ\Delta
and
\Delta\circ\Gamma
one can see that
\bar{p}^{\ast}=(\bar{p}_{0},\bar{q})
and
\bar{q}^{\ast}=(\bar{q}_{0},\bar{p})
, where
\bar{p}_{0}\in\mu^{-1}_{\Delta}(\bar{p})
and
\bar{q}_{0}\in\mu^{-1}_{\Gamma}(\bar{q})
(see Lemma
3
).

b)

Note that the tuples
\bar{p}_{0},\bar{q}_{0}
(and consequently
\bar{p}^{\ast},\bar{q}^{\ast}
) from above are not unique, they are defined up to the equivalence relations
\sim_{\Delta}
and
\sim_{\Gamma}
. Nevertheless, all these tuples define the same interpretations
\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{p}^{\ast})
and
\Delta\circ\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{q}^{\ast})
(see Lemma
3
).

c)

The requirement in 2) that the formula
\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{p}^{\ast})
defines in
{\mathbb{A}}
a coordinate map
\mu_{\mathbb{A}}\colon A_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}\to A
can be strengthened to the requirement that it defines the composition
\mu_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}=\mu_{\Gamma}\circ\mu_{\Delta}
. Indeed, we can redefine the isomorphism
\bar{\mu}_{\Gamma}\colon\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})\to{\mathbb{A}}
by taking
\alpha\circ\bar{\mu}_{\Gamma}
, where
\alpha=\bar{\mu}_{\mathbb{A}}\circ\bar{\mu}^{-1}_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}
is the automorphism of
{\mathbb{A}}
. So we can always assume, adjusting
\bar{\mu}_{\Gamma}
if necessary, that
\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{p}^{\ast})
defines in
{\mathbb{A}}
the isomorphism
\bar{\mu}_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}
.

In the case when the formula
\theta_{\mathbb{A}}
above defines the coordinate map
\mu_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}\colon A_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}\to A
, we will refer to the isomorphism
\bar{\mu}_{\Gamma}\colon\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})\to{\mathbb{A}}
and its coordinate map
\mu_{\Gamma}\colon A_{\Gamma}\to A
as
compatible with the formula

\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{p}^{\ast})
. Item c) in Remark
1
tells that such isomorphism always exists.

Usually in examples of bi-interpretable algebraic structures one has the coordinate map
\mu_{\Gamma}
compatible with the formula
\theta_{\mathbb{A}}
and simultaneously the coordinate map
\mu_{\Delta}
compatible with the formula
\theta_{\mathbb{B}}
. In this instance the formula
\theta_{\mathbb{A}}
defines the composition
\mu_{\Gamma}\circ\mu_{\Delta}
and
\theta_{\mathbb{B}}
defines
\mu_{\Delta}\circ\mu_{\Gamma}
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
be bi-interpretable (with parameters) in each other with respect to interpretations
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
and
{\mathbb{B}}\simeq\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{q})
for some codes
\Gamma
and
\Delta
and tuples of parameters
\bar{p},\bar{q}
. Then the following holds:

1)

There is a formula
\sigma_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{y},\bar{z})
in the language
L({\mathbb{A}})
with
|\bar{y}|=|\bar{p}|\cdot\dim\Delta
,
|\bar{z}|=|\bar{q}|
, which defines in
{\mathbb{A}}
a non-empty set of tuples
D_{\sigma_{\mathbb{A}}}
such that in the notation in Definition
2
and Remark
1

\bar{p}^{\ast}=(\bar{p}_{0},\bar{q})\in D_{\sigma_{\mathbb{A}}}
and for any tuple
\bar{a}=(\bar{a}_{0},\bar{c})
over
{\mathbb{A}}
with
|\bar{a}_{0}|=|\bar{y}|,|\bar{c}|=|\bar{z}|
if
\bar{a}\in D_{\sigma_{\mathbb{A}}}
then:

–

the
L({\mathbb{A}})
-structure
\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{c})
is well-defined and
\bar{a}_{0}\in\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{c})
(more precisely,
\bar{a}_{0}\in U_{\Delta}({\mathbb{A}},\bar{c})
);

–

the
L({\mathbb{A}})
-structure
\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{a})
is well-defined;

–

the formula
\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{a})
defines in
{\mathbb{A}}
an isomorphism
\bar{\mu}\colon\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{a})\to{\mathbb{A}}
(more precisely, a coordinate map
\mu\colon U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}({\mathbb{A}},\bar{a})\to A
).

2)

Similarly, there is a formula
\sigma_{\mathbb{B}}(\bar{s},\bar{t})
in the language
L({\mathbb{B}})

with
|\bar{s}|=|\bar{q}|\cdot\dim\Gamma
,
|\bar{t}|=|\bar{p}|
, which defines in
{\mathbb{B}}
a non-empty set of tuples
D_{\sigma_{\mathbb{B}}}
such that
\bar{q}^{\ast}=(\bar{q}_{0},\bar{p})
is in
D_{\sigma_{\mathbb{B}}}
and for any tuple
\bar{b}=(\bar{b}_{0},\bar{d})\in D_{\sigma_{\mathbb{B}}}
,
|\bar{b}_{0}|=|\bar{s}|,|\bar{d}|=|\bar{t}|
, the
L({\mathbb{B}})
-structures
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{d})
and
\Delta\circ\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{b})
are well-defined,
\bar{b}_{0}\in\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{d})
, and the formula
\theta_{\mathbb{B}}(\bar{v},x,\bar{b})
defines an isomorphism
\Delta\circ\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{b})\to{\mathbb{B}}
.

Proof.

We use notation from Definition
2
and Remark
1
.

It suffices to prove 1). To construct the required formula
\sigma_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{y},\bar{z})
we will use the formulas
\Theta_{\Delta}(\bar{z})
(that says that
\bar{c}
satisfies in
{\mathbb{A}}
the admissibility conditions
\mathcal{AC}_{\Delta}(\bar{z})
) and
\Theta_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}(\bar{y},\bar{z})
(that says that
\bar{a}
satisfies in
{\mathbb{A}}
the admissibility conditions
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}(\bar{y},\bar{z})
). Thus
{\mathbb{A}}\models\Theta_{\Delta}(\bar{c})\wedge\Theta_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}(\bar{a})
if and only if the
L({\mathbb{A}})
-structures
\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{c})
and
\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{a})
are well-defined, in particularly, when
\bar{a}=\bar{p}^{\ast}
. Note that if
\bar{a}\in\Theta_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}({\mathbb{A}})
then there exists
\bar{x}
such that
{\mathcal{A}}\models U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}(\bar{x},\bar{a})
, and since
U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}
is the
\Delta
-translation of the formula
U_{\Gamma}
, so
\bar{a}_{0}\in U_{\Delta}({\mathbb{A}},\bar{c})
(see the algorithm of
\Delta
-translation for details).

Assume now that the
L({\mathbb{A}})
-structure
\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{a})
is well-defined. The formula
\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{a})
defines in
{\mathbb{A}}
an isomorphism
\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{a})\to{\mathbb{A}}
if
\bar{a}=\bar{p}^{\ast}
. We are going to impose restrictions on
\bar{a}
such that
\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{a})
defines an isomorphism for all
\bar{a}\in D_{\sigma_{\mathbb{A}}}
. Let us write the following formulas to say that

(i)

a morphism
\mu\colon U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}({\mathbb{A}},\bar{a})\to A
exists:

\theta_{1}(\bar{y},\bar{z})\>=\>\forall\,\bar{u}\;(U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}(\bar{u},\bar{y},\bar{z})\>\longleftrightarrow\>\exists\,x\;\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{y},\bar{z}));

(ii)

the morphism
\mu
is well-defined and the morphism
\bar{\mu}
is injective:

\theta_{2}(\bar{y},\bar{z})\>=\>\forall\,\bar{u}_{1}\>\forall\,\bar{u}_{2}\>\forall\,x_{1}\>\forall\,x_{2}\;(U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}(\bar{u}_{1},\bar{y},\bar{z})\,\wedge\,U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}(\bar{u}_{2},\bar{y},\bar{z})\,\wedge\\
\wedge\,\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u}_{1},x_{1},\bar{y},\bar{z})\,\wedge\,\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u}_{2},x_{2},\bar{y},\bar{z})\>\longrightarrow\\
\longrightarrow\>(E_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}(\bar{u}_{1},\bar{u}_{2},\bar{y},\bar{z})\,\longleftrightarrow\,(x_{1}=x_{2})\,)\,);

(iii)

the morphism
\mu
is surjective:

\theta_{3}(\bar{y},\bar{z})\>=\>\forall\,x\>\exists\,\bar{u}\;\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{y},\bar{z});

(iv)

the morphism
\mu
preserves constants form
L({\mathbb{A}})
: for each
c\in L({\mathbb{A}})

\theta_{c}(\bar{y},\bar{z})\>=\>\forall\,\bar{u}\;(c_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}(\bar{u},\bar{y},\bar{z})\,\longrightarrow\,\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u},c,\bar{y},\bar{z}));

(v)

the morphism
\mu
preserves functions form
L({\mathbb{A}})
: for each
f\in L({\mathbb{A}})

\theta_{f}(\bar{y},\bar{z})\>=\>\forall\,\bar{u}_{0}\>\forall\,\bar{u}_{1}\ldots\forall\,\bar{u}_{n_{f}}\>\forall\,x_{0}\>\forall\,x_{1}\ldots\forall\,x_{n_{f}}\;(\bigwedge\limits_{i=0}^{n_{f}}\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u}_{i},x_{i},\bar{y},\bar{z})\,\wedge\\
\wedge\,f_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}(\bar{u}_{1},\ldots,\bar{u}_{n_{f}},\bar{u}_{0},\bar{y},\bar{z})\,\longrightarrow\,(f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{f}})=x_{0})\,);

(vi)

the morphism
\mu
preserves predicates form
L({\mathbb{A}})
: for each
P\in L({\mathbb{A}})

\theta_{P}(\bar{y},\bar{z})\>=\>\forall\,\bar{u}_{1}\ldots\forall\,\bar{u}_{n_{P}}\>\forall\,x_{1}\ldots\forall\,x_{n_{P}}\;(\bigwedge\limits_{i=1}^{n_{P}}\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u}_{i},x_{i},\bar{y},\bar{z})\,\longrightarrow\\
\longrightarrow\,(P_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}(\bar{u}_{1},\ldots,\bar{u}_{n_{P}},\bar{y},\bar{z})\,\longleftrightarrow\,P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{P}})\,)\,).

Now, to write down the formula
\sigma_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{y},\bar{z})
from 1) it suffices to take conjunction of the formulas
\Theta_{\Delta}(\bar{z})
,
\Theta_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}(\bar{y},\bar{z})
,
\theta_{1}(\bar{y},\bar{z})
,
\theta_{2}(\bar{y},\bar{z})
,
\theta_{3}(\bar{y},\bar{z})
,
\theta_{c}(\bar{y},\bar{z})
,
\theta_{f}(\bar{y},\bar{z})
,
\theta_{P}(\bar{y},\bar{z})
for all
c,f,P\in L({\mathbb{A}})
. This proves 1) and the lemma.
∎

Corollary 2
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
be bi-interpretable (with parameters) in each other with respect to interpretations
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
and
{\mathbb{B}}\simeq\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{q})
for some codes
\Gamma
and
\Delta
and tuples of parameters
\bar{p},\bar{q}
. Then in the notation of Lemma
6
the interpretation
\Gamma\circ\Delta
together with the formula
\sigma_{\mathbb{A}}
regularly interprets
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{A}}
, i.e.,
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\sigma_{\mathbb{A}})
. Furthermore, for any tuples
\bar{a}_{1}
and
\bar{a}_{2}
which satisfy
\sigma_{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{A}}
the formula

\Theta_{\bar{a}_{1},\bar{a}_{2}}(\bar{u}_{1},\bar{u}_{2})=\exists\,x\>(\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u}_{1},x,\bar{a}_{1})\wedge\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u}_{2},x,\bar{a}_{2}))
defines an isomorphism
\lambda_{\bar{a}_{1},\bar{a}_{2}}\colon\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{a}_{1})\to\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{a}_{2})
such that
{\lambda_{\bar{a}_{1},\bar{a}_{2}}(\bar{u}_{1}/\sim_{(\Gamma\circ\Delta,\bar{a}_{1})})}=\bar{u}_{2}/\sim_{(\Gamma\circ\Delta,\bar{a}_{2})}
. A similar statement holds for the interpretation
\Delta\circ\Gamma
.

Algebraic structures
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are called
0
-bi-interpretable
or
absolulutely bi-interpretable
in each other if in the definition above the tuples of parameters
\bar{p}
and
\bar{q}
are empty.

Unfortunately,
0
-bi-interpretability is rather rare. Indeed, the following result puts quite strong restrictions on such structures.

Lemma 7
.

[
20
, Section 5.4, Ex. 8 (b)]

If
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are
0
-bi-interpretable in each other then their groups of automorphisms are isomorphic.

Fortunately, there is a notion of regular bi-interpretability, which is less restrictive, occurs more often, and which enjoys many properties of
0
-bi-interpretability.

Definition 3
.

Two algebraic structures
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are called regularly bi-interpretable in each other if the following conditions hold:

1)

{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are regularly interpretable in each other, so
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\phi)
and
{\mathbb{B}}\simeq\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\psi)
for some codes
\Gamma
and
\Delta
and the corresponding formulas
\phi,\psi
(without parameters). By transitivity
{\mathbb{A}}
, as well as
{\mathbb{B}}
, is regularly interpretable in itself, so
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\phi^{\ast})
and
{\mathbb{B}}\simeq\Delta\circ\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\psi^{\ast})
, where
\circ
denotes composition of interpretations and
\phi^{\ast}=\phi_{\Delta}\,\wedge\,\psi
,
\psi^{\ast}=\psi_{\Gamma}\,\wedge\,\phi
(see Lemma
3
).

2)

There is a formula
\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{s})
in the language of
{\mathbb{A}}
such that for every tuple
\bar{p}^{\ast}
satisfying
\phi^{\ast}(\bar{s})
in
{\mathbb{A}}
the formula
\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{p}^{\ast})
defines in
{\mathbb{A}}
an isomorphism
\bar{\mu}_{{\mathbb{A}},\bar{p}^{\ast}}\colon\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{p}^{\ast})\to{\mathbb{A}}
and there is a formula
\theta_{\mathbb{B}}(\bar{v},x,\bar{t})
in the language of
{\mathbb{B}}
such that for every tuple
\bar{q}^{\ast}
satisfying
\psi^{\ast}(\bar{t})
in
{\mathbb{B}}
the formula
\theta_{\mathbb{B}}(\bar{v},x,\bar{q}^{\ast})
defines in
{\mathbb{B}}
an isomorphism
\bar{\mu}_{{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q}^{\ast}}\colon\Delta\circ\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{q}^{\ast})\to{\mathbb{B}}
.

Here
|\bar{s}|=\dim_{par}\Gamma\circ\Delta=\dim_{par}\Gamma\cdot\dim\Delta+\dim_{par}\Delta

3
Weak second order logic

In this section we describe the weak second order logic (WSOL) over an arbitrary structure
{\mathbb{A}}
. Informally, this logic allows one to use three different types of variables, one is for elements in
{\mathbb{A}}
, another — for finite subsets of
{\mathbb{A}}
, and the third one — for natural numbers. Also, one is allowed to use all the functions and predicates from the language of
{\mathbb{A}}
, the “membership” predicate
\in
for finite subsets of
{\mathbb{A}}
, and addition and multiplication for natural numbers. Otherwise, the formulas are built like in the usual first-order languages. This logic is much more powerful than the standard first-order logic in
{\mathbb{A}}
, indeed, everything which is describable in finitary set theory over
{\mathbb{A}}
can be described in the weak second order logic over
{\mathbb{A}}
.

Below, we describe WSOL over
{\mathbb{A}}
in three different but equivalent ways, each one will be useful for different purposes. Firstly, we introduce two larger structures, containing
{\mathbb{A}}
, the superstructure
HF({\mathbb{A}})
of hereditary finite sets over
{\mathbb{A}}
, and the list superstructures
S({\mathbb{N}},{\mathbb{A}})
. We show that they are bi-interpretable in each other, so they have essentially the same expressive power. The crucial result here is that the expressive power of the first-order theory of
HF({\mathbb{A}})
(or
S({\mathbb{N}},{\mathbb{A}})
) is the same as the power of WSOL over
{\mathbb{A}}
. Secondly, we describe a particular fragment
L_{WSOL}
of the logic
L_{\omega_{1},\omega}
that is equivalent over
{\mathbb{A}}
to the weak second order logic. More precisely, in this fragment formulas with free variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
express the same things in
{\mathbb{A}}
as formulas in the language of
HF({\mathbb{A}})
or
S({\mathbb{N}},{\mathbb{A}})
which have only free variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
that run over
{\mathbb{A}}
. This description will be used to show that some properties of
{\mathbb{A}}
are definable in the WSOL.

3.1

3.2
The weak second order logic as a fragment of

For a language

the logic
L_{\omega_{1},\omega}
admits a new (in comparison with the first-order logic) rule of forming formulas: if
\Phi
is a countable set of formulas in
L_{\omega_{1},\omega}
then
\bigwedge\Phi
(conjunction of
\Phi
) and
\bigvee\Phi
(disjunction of
\Phi
) are also formulas in
L_{\omega_{1},\omega}
. Below, following
[
2
]

which is equivalent to WSOL over
{\mathbb{A}}
(were

).

We fixed an arbitrary effective enumeration of the set
{\mathcal{F}}_{L}
of first-order formulas in
L
by natural numbers, so there is an injection
\nu\colon{\mathcal{F}}_{L}\to{\mathbb{N}}
such that for every formula
\phi\in{\mathcal{F}}_{L}
one can compute the number
\nu(\phi)
and given the number
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
one can decide if

for some
\phi
and if so find the formula
\phi
. We assume that
\nu({\mathcal{F}}_{L})
has infinite complement so we can extend the enumeration to the fragment
L_{WSOL}
. Similarly, we have an effective enumeration
\mu\colon{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
of the first-order formulas of the arithmetic. We define formulas in
L_{WSOL}
by induction and, simultaneously, do two other things: we extend the enumeration
\nu
to the the fragment
L_{WSOL}
and define if a given formula
\phi\in L_{WSOL}
has complexity at most

.

Recall that a formula
\phi=Q_{1}x_{1}\ldots Q_{m}x_{m}\psi
, where

are quantifiers

and
\psi
is quantifier-free, is in

(

),

(

) and the

has at most

alternations of quantifiers.

We say that a set of formulas in

Let

Definition 4

Definition 5

•

Lemma 17

Let

Proof.

Let

6
Properties definable in rich groups

In this section we describe various properties that are definable in in first-order logic in rich groups.

The general strategy is to describe a property by a single formula in WSOL and then transform this formula into a first-order one using richness of the group. We focus mostly on groups though many results hold for general structures.

6.1
Malcev’s problem

The generalized
Mal’cev’s problem
for a given group
G
asks to describe subgroups which are first-order definable in
G
.

In some groups subgroups are mostly undefinable, for example, in a free non-abelian group
F
a proper subgroup is definable (with parameters) if and only if it is cyclic
[
25
]
,
[
43
]
.

On the other hand, in rich groups not only all finitely generated subgroups are definable, but they are definable in a very uniform way. Below for elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
in a group
G
by
\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle
we denote the subgroup generated by
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
in
G
.

Definition 7
.

We say that finitely generated subgroups of
G
are uniformly definable if for any natural number
n
there exists a first-order formula

\phi_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y)
such that for any
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},g\in G
the formula

\phi_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},y)
defines the subgroup
\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle
in
G
, i.e., for any
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},g\in G

G\models\phi_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},g)\Longleftrightarrow g\in\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle.

Theorem 11
.

In a rich group
G
finitely generated subgroups are uniformly definable.

Proof.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
X_{n}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
and
X_{n}^{-1}=\{x_{1}^{-1},\ldots,x_{n}^{-1}\}
. Fix an arbitrary computable enumeration

w_{0},w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{m},\ldots

of all words in the alphabet
X^{\pm 1}.=X\cup X^{-1}
, so the function
n\to w_{m}
is computable (see the proof of Lemma
19
for an example of such an enumeration).
Observe, that

b\in\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle\Longleftrightarrow\bigvee_{i\in\mathbb{N}}(b=w_{i}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})).

The set of formulas
\{(y=w_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))\mid i\in\mathbb{N}\}
is computably enumerable, so the formula

\psi_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y)=\bigvee_{i\in\mathbb{N}}(y=w_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))

is in WSOL. Since the group
G
is rich there is a first-order fiormula
\phi_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y)
which is equivalent to
\psi_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y)
on
G
. This proves the theorem.
∎

Corollary 7
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated rich group. Then for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
the set of
n
-generating tuples of
G
is
0
-definable in
G
.

6.2
Properties of subgroups

We say that a subgroup
H\leq G
is
malnormal
if
gHg^{-1}\cap H
is trivial for any
g\in G-H.

Theorem 12
.

If
G
is a rich group, then for any
n
there exists a formula

\phi_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
such that for any
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in G
the formula

G\models\phi_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})

if the subgroup generated by
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
is
1) normal,
2) malnormal,
3)free,
4) finitely presented,
5) residually finite,
6) amenable,
7) has property T,
8) isomorphic to a given finitely generated recursively presented group,
9) simple,
10) solvable.

Proof.

In all the cases we will find a WSOL formula that express the corresponding property in
G
. Since
G
is rich, this implies that there exists a first order formula that express the same property.

1) Fix an arbitrary computable enumeration

w_{0},w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{N},\ldots

of all words in the alphabet
X\cup X^{-1}
(so the function
n\to w_{n}
is computable). The formula

\forall g\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}(\bigvee_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}a_{i}^{g}=w_{j}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}))

is in WSOL and states that the subfgroup
\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle
is normal. Since the group is rich the same property can be defined by a first order formula.

2) The following WSOL formula defines malnormality

\forall g\forall x(\bigvee_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}x=w_{i}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\implies\neg\bigvee_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}x=w_{i}(a_{1}^{g},\ldots,a_{n}^{g})).

3) The formula
\bigvee_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}w_{j}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\neq 1
express that the subgroup
\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle
is free.

4) Let
\bar{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. Consider the following predicate:

P_{n}(y,y_{1},\bar{x})=\exists s\in\langle\bar{x}\rangle(y=y_{1}^{s}\vee y=(y_{1}^{-1})^{s})

We claim that this predicate is definable in
G
in WSOL. Indeed, in the notation above,
P_{n}(y,y_{1},\bar{x})
is equivalent in
G
to the WSOL formula

\bigvee_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}(y=y_{1}^{w_{i}(\bar{x})}\vee y=(y_{1}^{-1})^{w_{i}(\bar{x})}).

For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
take an arbitrary tuple
\tau=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\in\{1,\ldots,m\}^{k}
. Let
\bar{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})
.
Consider the following formula

P_{n,k,\tau}(y,\bar{x},\bar{z})=\exists y_{1}\ldots\exists y_{k}(y=y_{1}\ldots y_{k}\bigwedge_{j=1}^{k}P_{n}(y_{j},z_{t_{j}},\bar{x}))

Now the subgroup
\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle
has relations
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}
if and only if for every trivial element
y
in
\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle
we have

P_{n,k,\tau}(y,\bar{x},r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})
for some
k,\tau.
The subgroup is finitely presented if it has relations
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}
for some finite set of elements
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}
. This can be expressed by the infinite disjunction.

5) A finite index normal subgroup of
\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle
is finitely generated as a normal subgroup (by the multiplication table of the finite quotient). Let
\{r_{i1},\ldots,r_{im_{i}}|i\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
be the set of all generating sets of finite index normal subgroups
(generators of them as normal subgroups). Now we can write a WSOL formula that
says that for every non-trivial element
y
there exist
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}
such that we have the negation
\neg P_{n,k,\tau}(y,\bar{x},r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})
for any
k,\tau.

6) One has to use the Følner criterion about the ratio of the cardinality of the boundary to a finite set. Følner criterium can be written as a formula in
L_{WSOL}.

7) One can use the result of Shalom
[
52
]
that every property (T) group is
a quotient of a finitely presented property (T) group.
The set of quotients of a f.p. group is definable in WSOL. Then we have to take a countable disjunction over all f.p. groups with property T. To show that this disjunction is over an arithmetic set of formulas we show that the class of finite presentations with property (T) is recursively enumerable.
By
[
21
, Theorem3]
, property (T) is equivalent to the existence of a solution for the equation (1) in
[
21
, Theorem3]
. Since equations in
{\mathbb{R}}
are decidable and we can enumerate the word problem in the finite presentation, if the solution exists, we eventually find it. So we can enumerate finite presentations and for each of them start the procedure that looks for a solution. If the procedure stops in some group we add it to the list of groups with property (T). A recursively enumerable set of numbers is arithmetic.

8) Recall the following result
[
53
]
,
[
42
, Theorem 3.5]
: For every finitely generated group
G
, there exists an
\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{1},\omega}
-sentence
\sigma
with the property that for any group
H
we have
H\models\sigma
if and only if
H\cong G
.

The proof goes as follows. Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
be a generating set for
G
. Let
u_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\ldots,u_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\ldots
(respectively
v_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\ldots,v_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\ldots
) be the enumeration of all group words in the alphabet
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
such that
u_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=1
(respectively

v_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\not=1
) in
G
for all
i
. Then the formula

\exists x_{1}\ldots\exists x_{n}\left(\forall g\bigvee(g=u_{i}\vee g=v_{j})\wedge\left(\bigwedge_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}u_{i}=1\wedge v_{i}\neq 1\right)\right)

has the required property.
If
G
is recursively presented then the set of words that are equal to 1 is Diophantine, therefore its complement is arithmetic and the formula belongs to
L_{WSOL}.

The proof of 9), 10) is straightforward.

Rich existentially closed structures

In this section, following Belyaev and Taitslin
[
2
]
, we describe some natural classes of groups, rings and semigroups, where the existentially closed structures are rich.

Recall that a substructure
{\mathbb{A}}
of a structure
{\mathbb{B}}
is said to be
existentially closed
in
{\mathbb{B}}
if for every quantifier-free formula
\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots y_{m})
and all elements

such that

{\mathbb{A}}
.
A model
{\mathbb{A}}
of a theory
T
is called

in
T
if it is existentially closed in every superstructure
{\mathbb{B}}
that is itself a model of
T
.

Definition 8
.

Let
T
be a consistent first-order theory in a finite signature

. Denote by

the class of all existentially closed models in

.
We say that

satisfies a condition (BT) if the following holds:

1)

Every model in

is infinite.

2)

There is a formula
\theta(x,y,\bar{z})
in the language
L
such that:

a)

for any
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;L\rangle\in E(T)
and any finite subset

there is a tuple
\bar{c}
over
A
such that

H_{\bar{c}}=\{(a,b)\mid{\mathbb{A}}\models\theta(a,b,\bar{c})\}

b)

for any tuple
\bar{c}
over
A
with
|\bar{c}|=|\bar{z}|
the formula
\theta(x,y,\bar{c})
defines in
{\mathbb{A}}
a finite subset of

.

The following is a crucial result.

Theorem 13
.

[

]

•

•

•

Let

Corollary 8
.

All existentially closed models of the theories mentioned in Theorem

We will mention some corollaries for groups. Note first that every non-trivial algebraically closed group is also existentially closed. Here a group
G
is algebraically closed if it is closed under solutions of finite systems of equation with coefficients in
G
in any overgroup.

Properties of algebraically closed groups:

1)

Every countable group can be embedded in a countable algebraically closed group.

2)

Every algebraically closed group is simple.

3)

No algebraically closed group is finitely generated.

4)

No algebraically closed group is recursively presented.

5)

Any finitely generated group with decidable word problem embeds into any algebraically closed non-trivial group.

For us the following corollary is of interest.

Corollary 9
.

Every countable group embeds into an absolutely rich countable group which satisfies the conditions 2)-5) above.

g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\in G^{\prime}
, or , equivalently,
in the form

u=g_{1}^{x_{1}}g_{1}^{-\alpha_{1}}\ldots g_{m}^{x_{m}}g_{m}^{-\alpha_{m}}

(10)

where
g_{i}^{x_{i}}
is a conjugation of
g_{i}
by
x_{i}
, and
g_{i}^{-\alpha_{i}}
is the standard exponentiation of
g_{i}
by the integer
-\alpha_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. It was shown in Section 14.3 that there exists a formula
\exp_{2}(u,v,\bar{z})
such that for any
g,h\in G^{\prime}
and
\alpha=\bar{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\ast}
the formula

\exp_{2}(g,h,\bar{m})
holds in
G
if and only if
g=h^{\alpha}
. Using formula
\exp_{2}(u,v,\bar{z})
and definability of the commutant
G^{\prime}
in
G
(see 3.1) one can write down the condition (
10
) by a group theory formula uniformly in
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})
, as claimed.

14.5.3
Interpretation of the action of
{\mathbb{Z}}[a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}]
on
G^{\prime}
.

Lemma 24
.

Let
g,h\in G^{\prime}
and
P\in{\mathbb{Z}}[a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}]
,
m\leq n
. Then
g^{P}=h
if and only if the following condition holds:

\forall\alpha_{1},\ldots\alpha_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}(g^{P(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})}=h\ mod\ (G^{\prime})^{I_{\bar{\alpha}}}).

(11)

Proof.

If
g^{p}=h
then the condition (
11
) holds since

P(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})=P(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})\ mod\ I_{\bar{\alpha}}.

Conversely, suppose for
h\in G^{\prime}
the condition (
11
) holds. We need to show that
g^{P}=h
.

We will prove this by induction on
m,2\leq m\leq n
.
Suppose first that
P=P(a_{1},a_{2})
. By Proposition
4
,
G^{\prime}
is a free module over
\mathbb{Z}[a_{1},a_{1}^{-1},a_{2},a_{2}^{-1}]
with the basis
\{[x_{i},x_{j}]^{a_{3}^{\delta_{3}}\ldots a_{j}^{\delta_{j}}}\}
for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
,
\delta_{3},\ldots,\delta_{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{j-2}.

Let

g=\Pi[x_{i},x_{j}]^{a_{3}^{\delta_{3}}\ldots a_{j}^{\delta_{j}}\gamma_{ij\bar{\delta}}(a_{1},a_{2})},

h=\Pi[x_{i},x_{j}]^{a_{3}^{\delta_{3}}\ldots a_{j}^{\delta_{j}}\beta_{ij\bar{\delta}}(a_{1},a_{2})}.

Then the condition (
11
) becomes

[x_{i},x_{j}]^{a_{3}^{\delta_{3}}\ldots a_{j}^{\delta_{j}}\gamma_{ij\bar{\delta}}(a_{1},a_{2})P(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}=

[x_{i},x_{j}]^{a_{3}^{\delta_{3}}\ldots a_{j}^{\delta_{j}}\beta_{ij\bar{\delta}}(a_{1},a_{2})}+[x_{i},x_{j}]^{a_{3}^{\delta_{3}}\ldots a_{j}^{\delta_{j}}(a_{1}-\alpha_{1})f_{1}+(a_{2}-\alpha_{2})f_{2}},

for some
f_{1},f_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[a_{1},a_{2}]
. Note that both the left-hand and the right-hand sides of these equalities are in the normal form, so
the following polynomials are equal:

{\gamma_{ij\ldots k_{s}}(a_{1},a_{2})P(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})={\beta_{ij\ldots k_{s}}(a_{1},a_{2})}+{(a_{1}-\alpha_{1})f_{1}+(a_{2}-\alpha_{2})f_{2}}}

Evaluating these polynomials at
a_{1}=\alpha_{1},a_{2}=\alpha_{2}
one gets

\gamma_{ij\ldots k_{s}}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})P(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})={\beta_{ij\ldots k_{s}}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}

for any
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Hence (see Section 14.5.1)

\gamma_{ij\ldots k_{s}}(a_{1},a_{2})_{P}(a_{1},a_{2})=\beta_{ij\ldots k_{s}}(a_{1},a_{2}),

so
g^{P}=h
, as claimed.

Now assume that the statement of Lemma 2 is true for
m-1
and we will prove it for
m
. Condition (
11
) for
m
implies that

g^{P(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m-1},\beta)}=hh_{\beta}^{(a_{m}-\beta)}\ mod\ (G^{\prime})^{I_{\bar{\alpha}}},

where
h_{\beta}\in(G^{\prime})^{I_{\beta}}.

Note that for a given
\alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}
one has

g^{P(a_{1},...,a_{m})}=g^{P(a_{1},...,a_{m-1},\alpha_{m})}g^{(a_{m}-\alpha_{m})f}

for some
f\in{\mathbb{Z}}[a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}]
. By induction
g^{P(a_{1},...,a_{m-1},\beta)}=hh_{1}
, so

g^{P(a_{1},...,a_{m})}h^{-1}=(g^{f}h_{\beta})^{(a_{m}-\beta)}.

Suppose we consider the equality above for various pair-wise distinct values of
\alpha_{m}
, say
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots
. Then

g^{P(a_{1},...,a_{m})}h^{-1}=(g^{f_{1}}h_{\beta_{1}})^{(a_{m}-\beta_{1})}=(g^{f_{2}}h_{\beta_{2}})^{(a_{m}-\beta_{2})}=\ldots

It follows from Proposition
4
that
G^{\prime}
can be considered as a free module over
{\mathbb{Z}}[a_{m},a_{m}^{-1}]
with an infinite basis. Let
b
be an aribitrary basis element. Suppose
g^{P(a_{1},...,a_{m})}h^{-1}
contains
b^{r(a_{m},a_{m}^{-1})}
and each
(g^{f_{i}}h_{\beta_{i}})
contains
b^{r_{i}(a_{m},a_{m}^{-1})}.
Then

r(a_{m},a_{m}^{-1})=r_{i}(a_{m},a_{m}^{-1})(a_{m}-\beta_{i})

for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}.
Therefore
r(a_{m},a_{m}^{-1})=0.

This shows that
g^{P(a_{1},...,a_{m})}=h
, as claimed.

Every element
Q\in{\mathbb{Z}}[a_{1},a_{1}^{-1},\ldots,a_{n},a_{n}^{-1}]
can be written as

Q=P(a_{1},...,a_{m})(a_{1}^{k_{1}}\ldots a_{m}^{k_{m}})^{-1}

for some
k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}\geq 0
.
Therefore
g^{Q}=h
if and only if
g^{P(a_{1},...,a_{m})}=h^{(a_{1}^{k_{1}}\ldots a_{m}^{k_{m}})}.

This gives the interpretation of the action of
{\mathbb{Z}}[a_{1},a_{1}^{-1},\ldots,a_{n},a_{n}^{-1}]
on
G^{\prime}
.

∎

14.6
Proof of Theorem
30

Using the Magnus embedding

x_{i}\rightarrow\begin{pmatrix}a_{i}&0\\
t_{i}&1\end{pmatrix},

where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
are the generators of the free abelian group and
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\}
the base of the free
\mathbb{Z}\bar{G}
module
T
. Then
G^{\prime}
has
the structure of the the submodule of
T
generated by
t_{i}(x_{j}-1)-t_{j}(x_{i}-1),i\neq j.
Denote this submodule by
G^{\prime}_{{\mathbb{Z}}\bar{G}}
.

The module
G^{\prime}_{{\mathbb{Z}}\bar{G}}
is a two sorted structure
({\mathbb{Z}}\bar{G},G^{\prime},\delta)
where
\delta
is the predicate describing the action of
{\mathbb{Z}}\bar{G}
on
G^{\prime}
. We will show that
G^{\prime}_{{\mathbb{Z}}\bar{G}}
is bi-interpretable with
\mathbb{Z}
.

By Theorem
14
every infinite f.g. integral domain is bi-interpretable with
{\mathbb{Z}}.
Therefore
\mathbb{Z}\bar{G}
is by-interpretable with
\mathbb{Z}
. Every f.g. free
\mathbb{Z}\bar{G}
-module is bi-interpretable with
{\mathbb{Z}}\bar{G}
(with some
{\mathbb{Z}}\bar{G}
-basis as constants), and, therefore, it is bi-interpretable with
\mathbb{Z}
. Since
{\mathbb{Z}}
is bi-interpretable with
T
and with the submodule
B
generated by
[u_{1},u_{2}]
, we can define a map

T\rightarrow_{\phi}{\mathbb{Z}}\rightarrow_{\psi}B\leq T
. The graph of the map
\psi\circ\phi
is definable in
T
. The restriction of the map
\phi
to
G^{\prime}_{{\mathbb{Z}}\bar{G}}
,
\phi|_{G^{\prime}_{{\mathbb{Z}}\bar{G}}}
is the interpretation of
G^{\prime}_{{\mathbb{Z}}\bar{G}}
in
{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\psi
is the interpretation of
{\mathbb{Z}}
in
G^{\prime}_{{\mathbb{Z}}\bar{G}}
. Te graph of
\psi\circ\phi|_{G^{\prime}_{{\mathbb{Z}}\bar{G}}}
is definable and therefore
G^{\prime}_{{\mathbb{Z}}\bar{G}}
is bi-interpretable with
{\mathbb{Z}}
. Denote by
\alpha
the bijection from
G^{\prime}_{{\mathbb{Z}}\bar{G}}
to
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

We have the interpretation of
G
on the set

and, therefore, in
{\mathbb{Z}}
defined by

\tau(u_{1}^{m_{1}}\ldots u_{n}^{m_{n}}h)=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n},\alpha(h)).

We have already shown that the exponentiation and the module structure of

.

Now, given an
n+1

of integers we reconstruct the element of the group as

This proves the theorem.

14.7
Applications and open problems

Let
M_{n}
be a free metabelian group of finite rank
n\geq 2
. Since
M_{n}
is bi-interpretable with
{\mathbb{N}}
it is rich and it satisfies all the properties of rich groups mentioned in Section
5
. Here we list only those ones that answer to some open questions.

Theorem 31
.

For any finite
n\geq 2
the free metabelian group
M_{n}
is prime, atomic, homogeneous, and QFA.

Proof.

By Theorem
30

M_{n}
is bi-interpretable with

, hence by Lemma
16

5
).
The word problem is decidable in

, hence it is arithmetical. Now by Theorem
9
the group

is QFA.
