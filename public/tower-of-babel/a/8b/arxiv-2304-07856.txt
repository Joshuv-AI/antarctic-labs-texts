Abstract




Abstract

Model mis-specification in multivariate econometric models can strongly influence estimates of quantities of interest such as structural parameters, forecast distributions or responses to structural shocks, even more so if higher-order forecasts or responses are considered, due to parameter convolution. We propose a simple method for addressing these specification issues in the context of Bayesian VARs. Our method, called coarsened Bayesian VARs (cBVARs), replaces the exact likelihood with a coarsened likelihood that takes into account that the model might be mis-specified along important but unknown dimensions. Coupled with a conjugate prior, this results in a computationally simple model. As opposed to more flexible specifications, our approach avoids overfitting, is simple to implement and estimation is fast. The resulting cBVARs perform well in simulations for several types of mis-specification. Applied to US data, cBVARs improve point and density forecasts compared to standard BVARs, and lead to milder but more persistent negative effects of uncertainty shocks on output.

Introduction

Inference in multivariate econometric models is often plagued by issues such as measurement errors, structural breaks, outliers, or non-Gaussian features, such as asymmetric and fat tailed errors. Especially in recessionary episodes or when large shocks hit the economy (e.g., the global financial crisis or the Covid-19 pandemic), violations of the standard model assumptions become prevalent. Moreover, selecting appropriate variables is necessary to strike a balance between a large model, which might include many irrelevant predictors, and a small tractable model, which could omit important information. These issues can be partly addressed by making the model larger, more flexible, and non-Gaussian, but this typically leads to substantial increases in computational complexity and risks overfitting the data. For example,
Cogley and Sargent 2005
;
Primiceri 2005
;
Kalli and Griffin 2018
;
Huber, et al. 2020
;
Clark, et al. in-press
;
Goulet Coulombe, et al. 2022
;
Korobilis, et al. 2021
;
Karlsson, Mazur, and Nguyen 2023
propose models that allow for time-variation and non-linearities in the conditional mean, the conditional variance or both. These methods often outperform their linear counterparts in terms of estimation reliability and predictive accuracy. However, they are difficult to tune and the computational burden increases markedly with the size of the dataset. Similarly, to address the omitted variable problem it is common to rely on factor models
(
Stock and Watson 2002
;
Bernanke, Boivin, and Eliasz 2005
;
Kaufmann and Schumacher 2019
, e.g.,)
or large Bayesian VARs
(
Bańbura, Giannone, and Reichlin 2010
;
Koop 2013
;
Chan 2022
;
Gefang, Koop, and Poon 2023
, see, e.g.,)
. Factor models, however, require the selection of the number of factors and call for filtering techniques to estimate the latent factors. Large VARs, on the other hand, are often specified to be linear and homoskedastic. Both assumptions are necessary to retain conjugacy, and thus enable fast estimation, but could be unrealistic. And one could introduce a separate stochastic process to capture measurement errors
(
Cogley and Sargent 2015
, see, e.g.,)
, but doing so requires knowledge about the nature of the measurement error and is thus not easy to apply in general.

In summary, while there exist methods to handle specific types of mis-specification of standard, simple, econometric models, these methods are not commonly used in practice, due to their complexity and computational costs, in addition to the fact that the type of mis-specification is often unknown. Small, linear, Gaussian econometric models, such as vector autoregressions (VARs), remain the workhorse of applied macroeconomists. Hence, our objective in this paper is to modify standard (Bayesian) VARs
(
Kadiyala and Karlsson 1997
;
Sims and Zha 1998
;
Carriero, Clark, and Marcellino 2015
;
Giannone, Lenza, and Primiceri 2015b
, see, e.g.,)
to make them more robust to general and unknown forms of mis-specification, without changing the simple model specification and retaining computational simplicity and efficiency. We build on a recent paper,
Miller and Dunson 2018
, and propose a robust version of a conjugate VAR. We call this model coarsened Bayesian VAR (cBVAR). The cBVAR replaces the exact likelihood with a coarsened likelihood that takes general and unknown forms of model mis-specification into account. The key idea is that instead of conditioning on the observed data
\bm{Y}
, one conditions on the event that the difference in the sampling distribution of the observed data
P_{\bm{Y}}
and of the idealized data
P_{\bm{Y}^{*}}
(with
\bm{Y^{*}}
denoting the idealized data) is smaller than a constant number
c
, where the idealized data are such that all the model assumptions are valid. The conditioning event effectively requires a distance metric and we use relative entropy.
Miller and Dunson 2018
provide a simple approximation of the coarsened likelihood under relative entropy distance that reduces to raising the standard likelihood to a fraction. This fraction can be interpreted as a learning rate and controls how much the model learns from the data. The smaller the extent of the mis-specification, the closer the fraction is to one. Viceversa, for large mis-specification the fraction is close to zero, making the posteriors similar to the priors, as the observed data are considered as ”unreliable” (at least for learning via a standard BVAR).

Under a standard natural conjugate prior, we derive closed form posterior distributions for the cBVAR, and provide a simple and scalable algorithm to estimate the model. The resulting cBVAR relies on a single parameter (the learning rate), which can be selected either by cross-validation or using an information criterion, whereas the other hyperparameters are set using the coarsened marginal likelihood. This constitutes our main technical contribution.

To assess the empirical performance of the cBVAR, we first use simulated data, generated from a variety of data generating processes (DGPs), characterized by different types of mis-specification. Specifically, we compare the impulse responses functions (IRFs) obtained from the cBVAR and from a standard BVAR (both conditional on the true identification strategy) to the true IRFs generated by DGPs featuring alternative combinations of non-Gaussianity, stochastic volatility, random coefficients, and larger information sets than those considered in the BVAR. It turns out that the standard conjugate BVAR produces IRFs which are far off from the true ones, much more so than those from the cBVAR (except for the case where the DGP coincides with the BVAR). The precision of the cBVAR IRFs depends on the learning rate, and selecting the latter with our proposed information criterion generally improves upon the BVAR.

Next, we use a forecasting application to show that coarsening can often lessen the effects of mis-specification for actual US data. Specifically, we focus on point and density forecasting monthly US unemployment, inflation and short-term interest rate, using three model sizes (small, medium, and large), Our findings indicate that gains from coarsening the likelihood are largest if models are small (i.e. the risk of omitted variable bias is particularly pronounced), with relative gains decreasing if the model becomes larger, but still present and systematic, in particular for longer forecast horizons. As macroeconomists tend to prefer small models for empirical analysis, this finding can be of substantial practical use. Moreover, the gains from coarsening are generally larger when the Covid period is included in the evaluation sample, in line with the increased mis-specification of the standard BVAR during uncommon times. As with the simulated data, the predictive accuracy strongly depends on the learning rate, and the information criterion provides good guidance on how to set it.

Finally, as an illustration of the effects of coarsening the likelihood for structural analysis, we compute IRFs to uncertainty shocks from a standard BVAR and the associated cBVAR. It turns out that with the latter the uncertainty shocks produce milder but more persistent negative responses of output, the more so the smaller the learning rate.

The remainder of the paper is structured in the following way. The next section provides an intuitive introduction to the coarsening idea of
Miller and Dunson 2018
. This section mainly serves to set the stage for our model developments that follow in Section 3 in which we derive the coarsened likelihood for the BVAR and then back out the corresponding posterior distributions. Section 4 provides synthetic data evidence while Section 5 includes our real-data forecasting exercise. Section 6 deals with the effects of uncertainty shocks on real activity and Section 7 briefly discusses possible model extensions. The final section offers a brief summary and conclusions.

2
Coarsened posterior distributions

This section follows the discussion in
Miller and Dunson 2018
and motivates our use of the coarsened likelihood function to back out a coarsened posterior distribution. To set the stage, the data we observe is defined as
\bm{Y}=(\bm{y}_{1},\dots,\bm{y}_{T})^{\prime}
which is a
T\times M
matrix with empirical sampling distribution
P_{\bm{Y}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\delta_{y_{t}}
. However, the unobserved idealized dataset is given by
\bm{Y^{*}}
with associated sampling distribution
P_{\bm{Y}^{*}}
defined analogously to
P_{\bm{Y}}
. Suppose that
\bm{Y}
arises from
\bm{Y}^{*}
through a stochastic process which is, unfortunately, unknown and let
d(P_{\bm{Y}},P_{\bm{Y}^{*}})
denote a distance function with
d(\cdot,\cdot)\geq 0
. We assume that
d(P_{\bm{Y}},P_{\bm{Y}^{*}})<c
for some threshold parameter
c\geq 0
. Particular examples of such stochastic processes are simple measurement error models that assume that
\bm{y}_{t}=\bm{y}_{t}^{*}+\bm{\varpi}
with
\bm{\varpi}
denoting a random measurement error with a particular distribution such as a Gaussian or multivariate student t or nonlinear models that assume that
\bm{y}_{t}=g(\bm{y}_{t}^{*})
for some nonlinear function
g
.

Standard Bayesian practice would specify a likelihood
p(\bm{Y}|\bm{\vartheta})
, with
\bm{\vartheta}
denoting a vector of parameters, and a prior on
\bm{\vartheta}\sim p(\bm{\vartheta})
, both of which are then used to back out the posterior distribution:

p(\bm{\vartheta}|\bm{Y})\propto p(\bm{Y}|\bm{\vartheta})~p(\bm{\vartheta}).

This procedure, however, neglects the fact that
\bm{Y}
is a corrupted version of
\bm{Y}^{*}
. An alternative would be to set up an auxiliary model
p(\bm{Y}|\bm{Y}^{*})
. However, this is not feasible since, in applications with actual data, the process giving rise to
\bm{Y}
from
\bm{Y}^{*}
is not known (or might render the resulting model computationally involved).
Miller and Dunson 2018
propose a simple alternative. Instead of conditioning on
\bm{Y}
when forming the posterior, one could condition on
d(P_{\bm{Y}},P_{\bm{Y}^{*}})<c
instead. Since
c
is typically unknown to the researcher one could specify a prior on it, i.e.
C\sim\pi
. Doing so leads to the coarsened posterior:

p(\bm{\vartheta}|\hat{d}(P_{\bm{Y}},P_{\bm{Y}^{*}})<C)\propto\mathbb{P}(\hat{d}(P_{\bm{Y}},P_{\bm{Y}^{*}})<C|\bm{\vartheta})~p(\bm{\vartheta}),

where the probability
\mathbb{P}(\hat{d}(P_{\bm{Y}},P_{\bm{Y}^{*}})<C|\bm{\vartheta})
can be interpreted as a likelihood function. Notice that this coarsened likelihood is generally not a probability distribution of
\bm{Y}
given
\bm{\vartheta}
.

Different alternatives for the distance function
d
can be used. The choice of the discrepancy function can be based on the expected (or more feared) type of mis-specification. For example, as noted by
Miller and Dunson 2018
, robustness to outliers requires a discrepancy function that is little sensitive to movements of small amounts of probability mass to the outlying region (e.g., the first Wasserstein distance). Among different distance functions
d
, one particularly appealing variant stands out, which considers differences in the entire likelihood function: the relative entropy. Moreover, using the relative entropy between
P_{\bm{Y}}
and
P_{\bm{Y}^{*}}
and an exponential prior on
C\sim Exp(\alpha)
leads to a particularly simple and accurate approximation of the coarsened posterior. In this case, it can be approximated as follows:

p(\bm{\vartheta}|\hat{d}(P_{\bm{Y}},P_{\bm{Y}^{*}})<C)\mathrel{\raisebox{3.01385pt}{\mbox{$\underset{\vbox{\hbox{$\sim$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\propto$}}}$}}}p(\bm{\vartheta})~\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(\bm{y}_{t}|\bm{\vartheta},\bm{y}_{1},\dots,\bm{y}_{t-1})^{\zeta_{T}},

(1)

with
\mathrel{\raisebox{3.01385pt}{\mbox{$\underset{\vbox{\hbox{$\sim$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\propto$}}}$}}}
denoting approximately proportional to. Equation (
1
) implies that the coarsened posterior is simply equal to the prior times a tempered likelihood.

This particular form of the coarsened posterior resembles the intermediate approximating distribution of sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) methods. The main difference is that the learning rate in SMC grows from
0
(i.e. the likelihood plays no role) to
1
(i.e. one obtains the uncoarsened posterior). If one would fix the learning rate in SMC to
\zeta_{T}
, the result would be a sequential coarsened posterior.

The standard likelihood is raised by the learning rate
\zeta_{T}
which is given by:

\zeta_{T}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+T}.

To understand the role of the coarsening parameter
\alpha
, it is useful to note that the resulting posterior corresponds to shrinking the sample size from
T
to
\zeta_{T}T
. Hence, when
\alpha
is small and coarsening is relevant, the sample size is substantially reduced and the posterior is much less concentrated. This is sensible, as substantial coarsening should be associated to a larger extent of mis-specification, and hence uncertainty should indeed be larger. Yet, if
\alpha
is set to a small number when the model is instead approximately correct, posterior credible sets will be too large and model complexity likely under-estimated. Therefore, the choice of the parameter
\alpha
is very important for coarsening to be helpful.

It is worth stressing that this form of the coarsened posterior does not require the computation of the relative entropy term since it is absorbed in the constant of proportionality (and thus independent of
\bm{\vartheta}
). In macroeconomic time series it might make sense that
\alpha
is a function of
T
as well. This is because longer time series have the potential to be corrupted by extraordinary events that imply appreciable departures from a linear and homoskedastic model. In this regard,
Miller and Dunson 2018
suggest that as a simple starting point one could set
\alpha=T
, implying that you need to observe at least
T
additional observations to be robust to a presumed perturbation. Alternatively,
\alpha
could be set as a decreasing function in
T
, effectively capturing the notion that longer series tend to feature more outliers, structural breaks and other non-linear features.

An interesting question is whether the choice of a specific prior, in particular a robust one, could make coarsening less relevant. As discussed by
Miller and Dunson 2018
, this is not the case since a robust prior makes the results less sensitive to the choice of the prior but the importance of mis-specification of the likelihood remains the same, and the likelihood dominates the prior when the sample size grows (even though the coarsened posterior does not concentrate as
T
diverges just because of coarsening). A related issue is whether the choice of the prior matters more in the coarsened than in the standard case, since the ”weight” of the likelihood decreases, particularly so when
\alpha
is low. Intuitively, this makes sense. Conditional on the prior, if we believe that the information contained in the likelihood is severely corrupted (either through wrongly specifying the likelihood or measurement errors, both of which fall into our definition of mis-specification) a good choice would be to downweight this piece of information.

It is also worth discussing the relationship of coarsening with robust control theory and with entropic tilting, to clarify they they are quite different. In robust control theory (see e.g.
Hansen, et al. 2006
,
Hansen and Sargent 2008
) the decision maker has one reference model but she evaluates a decision rule under a set of alternative models that are perturbed versions of the reference model.
Hansen, et al. 2006
measure the difference between the reference and perturbed models using relative entropy, taking the maximum value of the difference as a parameter that measures the set of perturbations against which the decision maker seeks robustness (and restricts the extent of model misspeciﬁcation). They also provide conditions that permit to consider the perturbed models as the multiple priors that appear in the max–min expected utility theory of
Gilboa and Schmeidler 1989
. Instead, in our context, the prior is unique, we condition on the fact that the reference model can be at a certain (entropic) distance from the true model, and we contaminate the likelihood to take that into account when forming the posterior distribution of the model parameters.

Entropic tilting (see e.g.
Robertson, Tallman, and Whiteman 2005
instead minimizes the Kullback-Leibler distance between the predictive density under restrictions and the unconditional one. Coarsening does not aim to reduce the distance between the model in use and the true one, rather it takes it as given and assumes that it is smaller than a certain value, and tries to robustify estimation and inference for the model in use.

To sum up the discussion so far, the coarsened posterior under a relative entropy distance metric can be approximated through a simple power posterior with learning rate
\zeta_{T}
. In principle, the value of
\alpha
controls the amount of mis-specification the researcher expects from a given dataset. Hence, choosing
\alpha
wisely is key for robust statistical inference and in the next sections we offer some guidance on how to select it. But before doing so, we discuss a simple yet practically relevant example.

\bm{a}
are given by, respectively:

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{V}}=(\zeta_{T}\bm{X}^{\prime}\bm{X}+\underline{\bm{V}}^{-1})^{-1},\hskip 10.00002pt\overline{\bm{A}}=\overline{\bm{V}}(\zeta_{T}\bm{X}^{\prime}\bm{Y}+\underline{\bm{V}}^{-1}\underline{\bm{a}}).

Notice that we end up with the original posterior in the case that
\alpha\to\infty
and thus
\zeta_{T}=1
.

The shrinkage parameter
\lambda
governing the tightness of the prior is set as follows. The coarsened marginal likelihood (ML) is given by:

\tilde{p}(\bm{Y}|\lambda)=\int\int\tilde{p}(\bm{Y}|\bm{A},\bm{\Sigma},\lambda)p(\bm{A},\bm{\Sigma})d\bm{A}d\bm{\Sigma}\propto\left(\frac{|\overline{\bm{V}}|}{|\underline{\bm{V}}|}\right)^{M/2}|\overline{\bm{S}}|^{-\frac{\zeta_{T}T+\underline{v}}{2}}.

We then maximize the coarsened ML with respect to
\lambda
:

\lambda^{*}=\argmax_{\lambda}\tilde{p}(\bm{Y}|\lambda).

This choice neglects the fact that
\lambda
itself might be a random variable. However, notice that using this plug-in estimate implies that we do not have to recompute possibly high dimensional quantities such as the posterior covariance matrix and its Cholesky factor and/or inverse. The fact that the shrinkage parameter is selected based on the coarsened likelihood immediately implies that the amount of coarsening impacts the prior tightness as well.

The coarsened posterior can be interpreted as a posterior that arises from adjusting the sample size from
T
to
\zeta_{T}T

(
Miller and Dunson 2018
)
. Hence, if the data is corrupted, coarsening the likelihood by
\zeta_{T}
(that depends on
\alpha
) makes the model tolerant for values of
\bm{A}
and
\bm{\Sigma}
that might have been learned from a sample of size
\alpha
from the idealized sampling density
P_{\bm{Y}^{*}}
but effectively comes from
P_{\bm{Y}}
(with sample size
T
). Put differently, if the amount of mis-specification is large (implying that
\alpha
should be set small) less weight is put on the likelihood function and hence more weight is attributed to the prior. For a given tightness of the prior, this immediately implies more uncertainty surrounding parameter estimates and functions thereof. This makes sense since any inferences made from the mis-specified model should be considered with some caution.

3.3
Setting the learning rate

Apart from the hyperparameters that control the tightness of the prior, we need to decide on the learning rate
\alpha
.
Miller and Dunson 2018
propose several ways of choosing
\alpha
. The first one would be to use prior information on the amount of mis-specification. However, for possibly large-dimensional macroeconomic datasets this turns out to be unfeasible. A second approach relies on cross-validation to decide on
\alpha
. This essentially implies to recompute quantities of interest (such as forecasts or impulse responses) for different values of
\alpha
. Third, a principled approach to deciding on
\alpha
is to introduce a measure of model fit and a loss function that takes model complexity into account.

We briefly discuss the third approach here. A natural measure of model fit would be the likelihood evaluated at the posterior mean of
\bm{A}
and
\bm{\Sigma}
. This is given by
\text{MF}(\alpha)=p(\bm{Y}|\overline{\bm{A}},\overline{\bm{\Sigma}})
with
\overline{\bm{\Sigma}}=\overline{\bm{S}}/(\underline{v}+\zeta_{T}T-M-1)
. Measuring model complexity is slightly more complicated. Since we use a continuous shrinkage prior the number of non-zero elements in
\overline{\bm{A}}
is always
k
. However, under the coarsened posterior the corresponding prior implies stronger shrinkage (keeping
\lambda
fixed). Hence, we simply measure complexity by counting the number of effective coefficients as follows:

\text{MC}(\alpha)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{I}(|\overline{a}_{j}|<\tau),

(6)

with
\mathbb{I}(\bullet)
denoting the indicator function that equals one if its argument is true and
\overline{a}_{j}
is the
j^{th}
element of
\overline{\bm{a}}=\text{vec}(\overline{a})
. As a standard choice for the macro datasets we consider in this paper we use
\tau=0.01
.

We compute
\text{MF}(\alpha)
and
\text{MC}(\alpha)
over a grid
\alpha\in\{25,50,75,100,125,250,350,500,1000,\infty\}
. This yields a curve in the
(\text{MF}(\alpha),\text{MC}(\alpha))
-space. The optimal value of
\alpha,\alpha^{*}
, is then selected by searching the
\alpha
that yields the most favorable fit/penalty combination. This is achieved by computing a straight line from the first point
(\text{MF}(25),\text{MC}(25))
to the last point
(\text{MF}(\infty),\text{MC}(\infty))
. The value
\alpha^{*}
is then the one that has the greatest distance to the line. In the following, we will refer to this method of selecting
\alpha^{*}
as BIC, due to the similarity to the BIC criterion used in time series analysis for model specification.

We now investigate whether this carries over to a standard US macroeconomic dataset and consider the forecasting performance of the different coarsened VARs vis-á-vis the standard (uncoarsened) BVAR.

6
Structural analysis with Coarsened BVARs: The effects of macroeconomic uncertainty

In this section we apply the cBVAR to a topical structural application and show that coarsening has empirically relevant effects on estimated impulse responses for real data as well. We focus on how uncertainty shocks impact the economy and pay particular attention on how real activity reacts to unexpected variations in uncertainty. The study of the effects of uncertainty shocks has attracted considerable attention in the literature, in particular after the seminal paper by
Bloom 2009
, see also the review in
Bloom 2014
. One common finding in the uncertainty literature is that real activity, after declining initially, tends to overshoot its initial level after some time. However, most of the papers that find this real activity overshoot phenomenon use observed proxies of economic uncertainty. Since uncertainty is a latent quantity, using proxies ignores possible measurement errors and this could impact IRF estimates
(
Carriero, Clark, and Marcellino 2018
)
so that the real activity overshoot vanishes.
However, models that estimate uncertainty alongside the remaining model parameters and allow for heteroskedasticity and time-varying parameters are more difficult to estimate and tune. Hence, a simpler model that is robust with respect to measurement errors in uncertainty would be preferable and we use the cBVAR to investigate whether coarsening helps in reducing the real activity overshoot in response to increases in economic uncertainty.

While there is no consensus on the definition of uncertainty, the forecast-based measure suggested by
Jurado, Ludvigson, and Ng 2015
is quite popular. We now add this JLN uncertainty measure to our large-scale VAR, ordering it first, and compute IRFs to the resulting uncertainty shock. Our main focus is on understanding whether and what changes in the IRFs are implied by different values of
\alpha
. Since the forecasting results pointed towards important effects of the pandemic, we recompute the IRFs for the full sample (i.e., including the pandemic observations) and for a shorter sample that ends in 2019M12.

\alpha

\alpha
is crossed, the IRFs of the cBVAR approach the ones of the BVAR and also produce a real activity overshoot.

\alpha

These results suggest that coarsening is relevant also for a proper assessment of the effects of economic shocks. Interestingly, values of
\alpha
close to the threshold that implies no real activity overshoot (both in terms of industrial production and the unemployment rate) are also those that produce favorable forecasts (in particular for the one-year-ahead forecast horizon that roughly matches the timing of the output overshoot).

Finally, in the discussion so far we have focused on the IRFs conditional on specific values of
\alpha
. Using our BIC-inspired approach to selecting
\alpha
leads to an optimal value of
\alpha=100
for both samples, and hence to impulse responses which do not display an overshoot in real activity (both in terms of industrial production and unemployment rates).

7
Extensions

In this paper we apply the general idea of coarsening to multivariate time series models with a particular, conjugate, prior structure. This conjugate structure implies that the likelihood and prior have the same Kronecker structure, which simplifies computations but can be restrictive in some cases. For example, it could be (and this is to a certain degree evidenced by our forecasting results) that some variables are more subject to model mis-specification than others. For instance, during the first few months of the pandemic the unemployment rate exhibited substantial fluctuations, outside the range of past movements. By contrast, inflation remained rather stable. Hence, using separate
\alpha
’s for unemployment and inflation might be preferable over using a single
\alpha
(and thus a common learning rate
\zeta_{T}
).

If the researcher is willing to trade computational simplicity for flexibility, she could use any equation-by-equation estimation algorithm for VARs (such as the one proposed in
Carriero, Clark, and Marcellino 2019
), and then use equation-specific
\alpha
’s. These can be set using the BIC-inspired criterion outlined above. Another alternative, which would remain conjugate (and thus permit order-invariant inference) and allow for equation-specific
\alpha
’s, is to use the large asymmetric conjugate VAR of
Chan 2022
. But both options are much more involved to implement than the cBVAR, and thus face the risk of being less used in practice.

Another potential shortcoming of our approach is that the parameter
\alpha
is time-invariant. For example, it could be that time series models feature more mis-specification in turbulent times (such as recessions). To capture this freature, one needs to take a stance about which regimes require different values of
\alpha
and how to group observations into these regimes. This is feasible, but it would substantially complicate inference and require a law of motion for
\alpha
, so that we leave it aside.

Finally, our approach could be applied to other popular models in macroeconomics. For instance, dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models such as the ones developed by
Smets and Wouters 2007
rely on Bayesian updating to obtain estimates of the structural parameters. However, they are likely to be mis-specified and
Canova and Matthes 2021
discuss this issue and provide a solution based on model combination. Another straightforward way of handling mis-specification in DSGE models is to apply coarsening by obtaining an estimate of the likelihood through filtering and then simply modifying this likelihood estimate.

8
Conclusions

Common practice in empirical macro is to estimate simple and interpretable models to derive stylized facts and inform decision making. However, these simple models often suffer from mis-specification of various, and unknown, types, which has a deleterious effect on estimation, inference, and predictive performance. In this paper, we offer a simple solution to get more robust results. Our proposal is to modify standard Bayesian multivariate econometric models. Instead of conditioning on the observed data we propose to condition on the event that there is a distance between the sampling distribution of the observed data and a hypothetical idealized sampling distribution. Using relative entropy as loss metric gives rise to a simple approximation that amounts to tempering the likelihood. We use this result and apply it to the conjugate VAR case, deriving analytically coarsened posterior distributions that can be used for robust forecasting and structural analysis.

In simulations, we show that standard BVARs in the presence of mis-specification of different forms produce estimates of impulse responses that are substantially biased. This bias can be reduced through coarsening, the gains in terms of MAE can be up to almost 50 percent.

Using actual US monthly data, we show that the cBVAR produces more accurate point and density forecasts than the standard BVAR, with larger gains at longer horizons and when using small models, and it does not suffer much from the substantial outliers observed during the pandemic. This suggests that our approach can be also used to obtain more robust inference in the presence of large outliers.

Finally, we show that a coarsened model can help also for more structural applications. When we consider the effects of uncertainty shocks, we find that the cBVAR produces no evidence of a real activity overshoot, contrary to the literature that relies on observed proxies of uncertainty, with negative real activity responses that are milder but more persistent than with a standard BVAR.
