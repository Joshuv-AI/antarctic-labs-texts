Complex networks widely appear in nature and are governed by diverse and different dynamics and mechanisms
18
,
19
. While many exotic phenomena such as chaotic behavior appear in many systems
20

21
, the most basic property of a complex system of any type is its stable states
22

basin of attraction
, a regime in the phase space that drags the system to the stable state once entering it. The boundaries of the basin of attraction describe how stable the state is, but finding them in the case of an
N

Network stability and perturbations

Consider a complex network with
N
nodes characterized by the adjacency matrix
A_{ij}
and a degree distribution
P(k)
.
A_{ij}=1
imply that nodes
i
and
j
are connected and
0
otherwise. The activity of node
i
,
x_{i}(t)
, accounts for the level of activity of the node in the network dynamics and can vary over time due to interactions with other nodes in the network or external interventions. The edge connecting nodes
i
and
j
indicate that the two nodes interact with each other such as two proteins in a PPI network or two species in an ecological network. The strength (weight) of the interaction,
\omega_{ij}
, follows a distribution
P(\omega)
and indicates how significant the two nodes affect each other. Often the average weight
\omega=\sum_{\omega^{\prime}=-\infty}^{\infty}P(\omega^{\prime})\omega^{\prime}
is used for simplicity. The time evolution of the activity of node
i
follow

\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}x_{i}}{\dif{t}}}=M_{0}(x_{i})+\omega\sum_{j=0}^{N}A_{ij}M_{1}(x_{i})M_{2}(x_{j}).

(1)

Here
M_{0}(x_{i})
account for the self-dynamics, i.e. how the node will behave if it was isolated, while the product
\omega M_{1}(x_{i})M_{2}(x_{j})
describe the interaction between the node and each of his neighbours.

The fixed points of the system
\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}=(\mathbf{x}_{\alpha,1},.....,\mathbf{x}_{\alpha,N-1})
are obtained from Eq. (
1
) by setting the derivatives to zero. Each of the fixed points is surrounded by its basin of attraction

\mathfrak{B}_{\alpha}=\Big\{\mathbf{x}(t=0)\,\,\Big|\,\,\mathbf{x}(t\rightarrow\infty)=\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}\Big\},

(2)

a regime in the phase space that directs the system, if initiate within it, to
\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}
.

If the system has more than a single stable state (often bi-stable) one of them (usually the one with high activity) is preferred. In the case of bi-stable systems we denote the lower stable state as
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and the higher as
\mathbf{x}_{1}
with the basin of attractions
\mathfrak{B}_{0}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
respectively. If a system is initiate in
\mathfrak{B}_{0}
it will move toward
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and if it is initiate in
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
it will move toward
\mathbf{x}_{1}
. Thus, the larger the basin of attraction is, the more stable is the state.

As a benchmark for studying network stability we consider three different dynamics characterizing three classes of dynamics. The first is Cellular dynamics described using Michaelis–Menten kinetics
23
which is a representative of all dynamics with
\mathbf{x}_{1}>0
and
\mathbf{x}_{0}=0
(Fig.
1
a
)

\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}x_{i}}{\dif{t}}}=Bx_{i}^{a}+\omega\sum_{j=0}^{N}A_{ij}\frac{x_{j}^{h}}{1+x_{j}^{h}}.

(3)

The second is Neuronal dynamics using the Wilson–Cowan model
24
,
25
,
26
representing classes with
\mathbf{x}_{1}>0
and
\mathbf{x}_{0}>0
(Fig.
1
b
)

\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}x_{i}}{\dif{t}}}=-x_{i}+\omega\sum_{j=0}^{N}A_{ij}\frac{1}{1+e^{\mu-\delta x_{i}}}.

(4)

and finally Social dynamics
15
which represents classes with
\mathbf{x}_{1}>0
and
\mathbf{x}_{0}<0
(Fig.
1
c
)

\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}x_{i}}{\dif{t}}}=B-\gamma x_{i}+\omega\sum_{j=0}^{N}A_{ij}\tanh{[\alpha(x_{j}-\mu)]}.

(5)

Since
\mathbf{x}_{1}
is the preferred state we are interested in how stable it is i.e. if the system is perturbed to
\mathbf{x}_{1}+\vec{\Delta}
will it stay in
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
? This question is not simple since the system is
N
-dimensional and it is very hard to find the boundaries of
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
especially for real-world dynamics which usually doesn’t characterize by a Lyapunov function
27
. One approach to counter this problem is to reduce the system to 1
D
by considering only the average activity
\mathbf{x}
. In such cases Eq. (
1
) take the form
28

\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}\mathbf{x}}{\dif{t}}}=f(\mathbf{x})=M_{0}(\mathbf{x})+\beta M_{1}(\mathbf{x})M_{2}(\mathbf{x}).

(6)

where
\beta
is the average weighed degree of the network.

As can be seen in Fig.
1
a-c
, the number of steady states depend on
\beta
. Here we will focus on the regime where the network has two stable states. The stable states
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and
\mathbf{x}_{1}
are obtaining by setting
f(\mathbf{x})=0
and demanding
\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}f}{\dif{\mathbf{x}}}}<0
(Fig.
1
d-f
). The stability of states can be described using the dynamical potential
29

U(\mathbf{x})=-\int_{0}^{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})d\mathbf{x}^{\prime},

(7)

where the stable states corresponds to local minima. The potential gap
\Delta U
between a stable state and the near maxima describe how stable the state is. As shown in Fig.
1
g-i
,
\mathbf{x}_{1}
gets more stable for denser networks.

Clique dynamics

Dense network motifs exist in many real-world networks (Fig.
2
a-h
). Their distinct structure is usually denser compared to the network itself and with higher activity compared to the average (Fig.
2
i-m
). Thus, their dynamics is different then the dynamics of the average (Eq. (
6
)) with different effects on the network stability. To describe the dynamics of motifs we will use a
cliques
as a representative structure but our results are valid for all dense motifs.

A clique
\mathcal{Q}
is a subset of nodes of size
q=|\mathcal{Q}|
where every pair of nodes in
\mathcal{Q}
are connected (Fig.
2
a-c
). We study effect of clique on the network stability by describing the dynamics of the clique activity
\mathbf{x}_{q}
which follow (see SI)

\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}\mathbf{x}_{q}}{\dif{t}}}=f_{q}(\mathbf{x}_{q})=M_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{q})+M_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{q})\big[\beta_{q,in}M_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{q})+\beta_{q,out}M_{2}(\mathbf{x})\big],

(8)

where
\beta_{q,in}=\omega(q-1)
and
\beta_{q,out}=\omega(\kappa_{q}-q+1)
are the average weight within and outside the clique of a single node and
\kappa_{q}
is the average degree of nodes in the clique (see SI).

The fixed points of the clique are obtained by setting the derivative in Eq. (
8
) to zero. The clique’s high stable state
\mathbf{x}_{q,1}
is obtained by setting
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{1}
and the low stable state
\mathbf{x}_{q,0}
by setting
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{0}
(Fig.
3
a
). The high stable state
\mathbf{x}_{q,1}
increases with the clique size (Fig.
3
b
) and the stability of the clique can be describe using the potential (Fig.
3
c
)

U_{q}(\mathbf{x}_{q})=-\int_{0}^{\mathbf{x}_{q}}f_{q}(\mathbf{x}_{q}^{\prime})d\mathbf{x}_{q}^{\prime},

(9)

where we set the activity of the network outside the clique to
\mathbf{x}_{0}
to isolate the dynamics of the clique from the support of the network.

Interestingly, as the clique size increases, the potential gap
\Delta U_{q}
increases as well (Fig.
3
d
) making
\mathbf{x}_{q,1}
more stable. The direct implication of
U_{q}
is that as long as the clique is within the potential well, even if the entire system is perturbed to
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, the clique will rise to
\mathbf{x}_{q,1}
and drag the network back to
\mathbf{x}_{1}
(Fig.
3
e-f
). Thus, the effect of the clique on the network stability can be describe by
\Delta_{c}
, the largest clique perturbation
\mathbf{x}_{q}=\mathbf{x}_{q,1}-\Delta_{c}
that will still bring the network back to
\mathbf{x}_{1}
.

To find
\Delta_{c}
we need to find the corresponding clique activity
\mathbf{x}_{q}
fitting the maxima of
U_{q}
. This can be found from the condition

\begin{cases}R(\mathbf{x}_{q,1}-\Delta_{c})=\beta_{q,out}M_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{0}),\\[10.0pt]
\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}f(\mathbf{x}_{q})}{\dif{\mathbf{x}_{q}}}}\bigg|_{\mathbf{x}_{q}=\mathbf{x}_{q,1}-\Delta_{c}}>0,\end{cases}

(10)

where

R(x)=-\beta_{q,in}M_{2}(x)-\frac{M_{0}(x)}{M_{1}(x)}.

(11)

Example I: Cellular dynamics.–
To illustrates this phenomena let us consider the cellular dynamics in Eq. (
3
). In this case the clique dynamics described in Eq. (
8
) takes the form

\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}\mathbf{x}_{q}}{\dif{t}}}=-B\mathbf{x}_{q}^{a}+\beta_{q,in}\frac{\mathbf{x}_{q}^{h}}{1+\mathbf{x}_{q}^{h}}+\beta_{q,out}\frac{\mathbf{x}^{h}}{1+\mathbf{x}^{h}}.

(12)

Thus,
\Delta_{c}
can be obtained from Eq. (
10
) as

-\beta_{q,in}\frac{(\mathbf{x}_{q,1}-\Delta_{c})^{h}}{1+(\mathbf{x}_{q,1}-\Delta_{c})^{h}}+B(\mathbf{x}_{q,1}-\Delta_{c})^{a}=0.

(13)

where Eq. (
11
) takes the form

R(x)=-\beta_{q,in}\frac{x^{h}}{1+x^{h}}+Bx^{a}.

(14)

In the limit of large dense cliques
\beta_{q,in}\gg 1
, both
\Delta_{c}
and
\mathbf{x}_{q,1}
follow asymptotic behaviour

\mathbf{x}_{q,1},\Delta_{c}\sim\beta_{q,in}^{\frac{1}{a-h}}.

(15)

as shown in Fig.
3
b,g
.

Interestingly, larger clique size does not necessarily guarantee higher
\Delta_{c}
and therefore higher stability as shown in Fig.
3
g
for Human and Yeast PPI networks. The reason is that being close to many cliques increases the clique stability even if the clique itself is not so large.

Since a node can be part of many cliques we need a measurement to estimate its contribution to the network stability. To do so we will define the stability as the sum of maximal perturbation of all cliques that a node is part of

S_{i}=\sum_{\mathcal{Q},i\in\mathcal{Q}}\Delta_{c}(\mathcal{Q}).

(16)

Fig.
3
h-k
shows the stability of nodes in Human and Yeast PPI networks. As can be seen the distribution of nodes stability
P(S)
follows a power-law behaviour indicating that a few nodes are the most important nodes that contribute to the networks stability.

Global stability

In cases where
\mathbf{x}_{0}>0
, a critical clique size
q_{c}
exists which above it the low stable state
\mathbf{x}_{q,0}
disappear and
\mathbf{x}_{q,1}
becomes globally stable (Fig.
4
). In such cases the clique spontaneously rises to
\mathbf{x}_{q,1}
for any initial condition and drag the network to
\mathbf{x}_{1}
, making
\mathbf{x}_{1}
globally stable as well. Thus, while
\Delta_{c}
describe the
local
stability of
\mathbf{x}_{1}
,
q_{c}
describe its
global
stability. The clique critical size can be found by demanding
\mathbf{x}_{q}
to be half stable

\begin{cases}R(\mathbf{x}_{q_{c},1}-\Delta_{c})=\beta_{q,out}M_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{0}),\\[10.0pt]
\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}f(\mathbf{x}_{q})}{\dif{\mathbf{x}_{q}}}}\bigg|_{\mathbf{x}_{q}=\mathbf{x}_{q_{c},1}-\Delta_{c}}=0,\end{cases}

(17)

where
\mathbf{x}_{q_{c},1}
is the clique fixed point for
q_{c}
.

This phenomena can happen only when
\mathbf{x}_{0}>0
due to the supportive nature of the clique. If
\mathbf{x}_{0}=0
the nodes in the clique will not support each other without activation and the system will stay at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
as shown for Cellular dynamics (Fig.
3
).

-\beta_{q,in}\delta e^{\mu-\delta(\mathbf{x}_{q_{c},1}-\Delta_{c})}=(1+e^{\mu-\delta(\mathbf{x}_{q_{c},1}-\Delta_{c})})^{2}.\end{cases}

(19)

where

R(x)=-\frac{\beta_{q,in}}{1+e^{\mu-\delta x}}+x.

To demonstrate how forming a clique can spontaneously raise the system from

we initiate a system in

In cases where

which described by

\Delta_{c}

\mathbf{x}_{1}

Polarization

In cases where
\mathbf{x}_{1}>0
and
\mathbf{x}_{0}<0
such as in social dynamics (Eq. (
5
)), an interesting polarization phenomenon may happen where the activity in the network is spread among the stable states
15
,
16
. Here we show how cliques significantly affect this phenomenon and even make it disappear.

Example III: Social dynamics.–
To demonstrate this phenomena let us consider the social dynamics. Cliques are widely common in social networks (Fig.
2
c
) and function as echo chambers where clique member exchange opinion intensively
17
. The clique dynamics of Eq. (
5
) can be obtained from Eq. (
8
) as

\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}\mathbf{x}_{q}}{\dif{t}}}=B-\gamma\mathbf{x}_{q}+\beta_{q,in}\tanh[\alpha(\mathbf{x}_{q}-\mu)]+\beta_{q,out}\tanh[\alpha(\mathbf{x}-\mu)].

(21)

The fixed points of the clique are obtained by setting Eq. (
21
) to zero. Interestingly, the clique stable states are more extreme than the stable state of the networks itself i.e.
|\mathbf{x}_{q,1}|>|\mathbf{x}_{1}|
and
|\mathbf{x}_{q,0}|>|\mathbf{x}_{0}|
as shown in Fig.
5
a-d
. The stability of the
\mathbf{x}_{1}
can be studied using the potential
U_{q}(\mathbf{x}_{q})
(Fig.
5
e-f
) obtained from Eq. (
9
) where the potential gap
\Delta U_{q}
increases with the clique size. To find
\Delta_{c}
we can use Eq. (
10
) as

-\beta_{q,in}\tanh[\alpha(\mathbf{x}_{q,1}-\Delta_{c}-\mu)]-B+\gamma(\mathbf{x}_{q,1}-\Delta_{c})=\beta_{q,out}\tanh[\alpha(\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mu)],

(22)

where Eq. (
11
) takes the form

R(x)=-\beta_{q,in}\tanh[\alpha(x-\mu)]-B+\gamma x.

(23)

Here it is important to distinguish between biased (
B\neq 0
) and unbiased (
B=0
) social dynamics. In the unbiased case where the activity of both states is equally strong
|\mathbf{x}_{0}|=|\mathbf{x}_{1}|
the clique function as echo chamber and above
\mathbf{x}_{q,1}-\Delta_{c}
will rise to
\mathbf{x}_{q,1}
, however, the network itself will stay at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
(Fig.
5

). The reason for this is that the neighbours of the cliques are being pulled toward
\mathbf{x}_{0}
by their neighbours stronger than they are being pulled by the clique. This create a polarization state between the clique and the rest of the network and the stability of
\mathbf{x}_{1}
is no enhanced by the clique. This can explain how radical groups can sustain a radical opinion whilst the rest of the network is in the complete opposite opinion

However, in the biased case
|\mathbf{x}_{0}|<|\mathbf{x}_{1}|
one opinion is significantly more influencing then the other. Thus, the clique rise to
|\mathbf{x}_{1}|
and pull its neighbours up since it over comes its neighbours due to its high influence (Fig.

\mathbf{x}_{1}
and show that an echo chamber can alter the entire opinion of a social network if one of the state is significantly more influential then the other. This might explain how new trends and fashions can rapidly take over a population.

Summary

Cliques are common, dense structures that appear in many real-world systems. Their distinct structure makes their activity different compared to the entire network. This difference results in novel phenomena that depend on the network dynamics. On the basic level, cliques enhance the dynamical stability of high activity states, as shown for cellular dynamics, and can be used to efficiently revive failed networks
31
. In other cases, when cliques of sufficient size are formed, local stable states can become globally stable and spontaneously attract the system, as shown for neuronal dynamics. Also, cliques can form polarization phenomena due to the echo chamber effect and have a different stable state compared to the entire network. Furthermore, if these cliques are extremely influential, it can also result in a global opinion change, attracting the entire system to their opinion. These properties make cliques important for external intervention, such as drag targets, where nodes that are part of many cliques are excellent candidates for these purposes.
