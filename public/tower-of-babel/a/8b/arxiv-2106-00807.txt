Finite distributive nearlattices
Luciano J. González, Ismael Calomino

Abstract
. Our main goal is to develop a representation for finite distributive nearlattices through certain ordered structures. This representation generalizes the well-known representation given by Birkhoff for finite distributive lattices through finite posets. We also study finite distributive nearlattices through the concepts of dual atoms, boolean elements, complemented elements and irreducible elements. We prove that the sets of boolean elements and complemented elements form semi-boolean algebras. We show that the set of boolean elements of a finite distributive lattice is a boolean lattice.

2.
Dual atoms and boolean elements

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a distributive nearlattice and
a\in A
. We will say that
a
is a
dual atom
if
a\neq 1
and for each
x\in A
,
a\leq x\leq 1
implies
x=a
or
x=1
. We say that
a
is a
boolean element
if the principal filter
[a)
is a boolean lattice.

Let us denote by
\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
and
\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
the collections of all dual atoms of
\mathbf{A}
and of all boolean elements of
\mathbf{A}
, respectively. It follows that
\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})\subseteq\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
. If
a\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
and
x\in[a)
, we shall denote the complement of
x
in
[a)
by
\neg_{a}x
. The next properties will be used throughout the paper without mention.

Remark 2.2
.

Let
a\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
. Then, for all
x\in A
, we have that
x\leq a
or
x\vee a=1
. Moreover, if
\mathbf{A}
is a finite distributive nearlattice, then for every
x<1
, there exists
a\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
such that
x\leq a
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a distributive nearlattice. Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
be such that
a_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{n}
exists in
A
. Then
a_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{n}\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
.

Proof.

Let
a^{*}=a_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{n}
. Since
\langle[a^{*}),\wedge_{a^{*}},\vee,a^{*},1\rangle
is a bounded distributive lattice, it only remains to verify that each element of
[a^{*})
has a complement. For each
a_{i}
, let us denote the complement of an element
x\in[a_{i})
by
\neg_{i}x
. That is, for every
x\in[a_{i})
,
\neg_{i}x\in[a_{i})
such that
x\wedge_{a_{i}}\neg_{i}x=a_{i}
and
x\vee\neg_{i}x=1
.

Let
x\in[a^{*})
. Thus
x=(a_{1}\vee x)\wedge\dots\wedge(a_{n}\vee x)
. We define the element
\neg x:=\neg_{1}(a_{1}\vee x)\wedge\dots\wedge\neg_{n}(a_{n}\vee x)
(notice that the last meet exists because
a^{*}\leq a_{i}\leq\neg_{i}(a_{i}\vee x)
, for all
i
). It is clear that
\neg x\in[a^{*})
. Now we show that
\neg x
is the complement of
x
in
[a^{*})
. On the one hand, we have

\displaystyle\neg x\wedge x

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{1\leq i\leq n}\neg_{i}(a_{i}\vee x)\wedge\bigwedge_{1\leq i\leq n}(a_{i}\vee x)=\bigwedge_{1\leq i\leq n}[\neg_{i}(a_{i}\vee x)\wedge(a_{i}\vee x)]

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{i}=a^{*}.

On the other hand, we have

\neg x\vee x=\left(\bigwedge_{1\leq i\leq n}\neg_{i}(a_{i}\vee x)\right)\vee x=\bigwedge_{1\leq i\leq n}\left(\neg_{i}(a_{i}\vee x)\vee x\right).

Since every
a_{i}
is a dual atom, it follows that

\neg_{i}(a_{i}\vee x)=\begin{cases}1\ &\text{ if }\ x\leq a_{i}\\
a_{i}\ &\text{ if }\ x\nleq a_{i}\end{cases}

Hence, in any case, we obtain that
\neg_{i}(a_{i}\vee x)\vee x=1
. Then
\neg x\vee x=1
. We have proved that
\neg x
is the complement of
x
in
[a^{*})
. Therefore,
\langle[a^{*}),\wedge_{a^{*}},\vee,a^{*},1\rangle
is a boolean lattice, and thus
a^{*}=a_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{n}\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
.
∎

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a distributive nearlattice and
a\in A
. We define the following set:

X_{a}:=\{x\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A}):a\leq x\}.

Thus,
X_{a}=\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})\cap[a)=\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}([a))
. If
\mathbf{A}
is finite and
a\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
, then since
[a)
is a finite boolean lattice it follows that

a=\bigwedge X_{a}.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a distributive nearlattice and
a,b\in A
. Then
X_{a\vee b}=X_{a}\cap X_{b}
. If
a\wedge b
exists, then
X_{a\wedge b}=X_{a}\cup X_{b}
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to show directly that
X_{a\vee b}=X_{a}\cap X_{b}
.

It is clear that
X_{a}\cup X_{b}\subseteq X_{a\wedge b}
. Let now
x\in X_{a\wedge b}
. Since
a\wedge b\leq x
, we have that
x=(a\vee x)\wedge(b\vee x)
. Given that
x\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
, it follows that
(a\leq x\text{ or }a\vee x=1)
and
(b\leq x\text{ or }b\vee x=1)
. If
a\vee x=1
and
b\vee x=1
, then
x=1
, which is a contradiction. Hence,
a\leq x
or
b\leq x
. That is,
x\in X_{a}\cup X_{b}
. Therefore
X_{a\wedge b}\subseteq X_{a}\cup X_{b}
.
∎

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=\langle A,\vee,1\rangle
be a finite distributive nearlattice. Then
\langle\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A}),\vee,1\rangle
is a nearlattice subalgebra of
\mathbf{A}
.

Proof.

We need to prove that
\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
is closed under
\vee
, and if
a,b\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
are such that
a\wedge b
exists in
A
, then
a\wedge b\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
.

Let
a,b\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
. Notice that
a\vee b\leq\bigwedge X_{a\vee b}
, and since
a,b\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
, it follows that
a=\bigwedge X_{a}
and
b=\bigwedge X_{b}
. By Proposition
2.3
, we have
\bigwedge X_{a\vee b}\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
. Let us show that
a\vee b=\bigwedge X_{a\vee b}
. Suppose, towards a contradiction, that
a\vee b\neq\bigwedge X_{a\vee b}
. So
\bigwedge X_{a\vee b}\nleq a\vee b
. Then, there is a prime ideal
P
such that
a\vee b\in P
and
\bigwedge X_{a\vee b}\notin P
. Thus,
a,b\in P
and
P\cap X_{a\vee b}=\emptyset
. Since
\bigwedge X_{a}\in P
,
\bigwedge X_{b}\in P
and
P
is prime, it follows that there is
x_{a}\in X_{a}
such that
x_{a}\in P
and there is
x_{b}\in X_{b}
such that
x_{b}\in P
. Since
x_{a}
and
x_{b}
are dual atoms, we have

x_{a}\vee x_{b}=\begin{cases}1\ &\text{ if }\ x_{a}\neq x_{b}\\
x_{a}=x_{b}\ &\text{ if }\ x_{a}=x_{b}\end{cases}

Since
P
is proper and
x_{a}\vee x_{b}\in P
, we have that
x_{a}=x_{b}
. Let
x:=x_{a}=x_{b}
. Thus
a\vee b\leq x
. Then
x\in X_{a\vee b}\cap P
, which is a contradiction. Hence
a\vee b=\bigwedge X_{a\vee b}\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
. Let now
a,b\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
be such that
a\wedge b
exists in
A
. By Lemma
2.4
, we have

\bigwedge X_{a\wedge b}=\bigwedge(X_{a}\cup X_{b})=\bigwedge X_{a}\wedge\bigwedge X_{b}=a\wedge b.

Then, it follows by Proposition
2.3
that
a\wedge b=\bigwedge X_{a\wedge b}\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
. Therefore,
\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
is a nearlattice subalgebra of
\mathbf{A}
.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite distributive nearlattice. Let
a\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
and
b\in A
. If
a\leq b
, then
b\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
. That is,
[a)_{\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})}=[a)_{A}
.

Proof.

Let
a\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
and
b\in A
be such that
a\leq b
. Since
a
is a boolean element, we have
a=\bigwedge X_{a}
. We also have that
b\leq\bigwedge X_{b}
. Suppose that
\bigwedge X_{b}\nleq b
. Then, there is a prime ideal
P
such that
b\in P
and
\bigwedge X_{b}\notin P
. Thus,
a\in P
and
X_{b}\cap P=\emptyset
. Since
\bigwedge X_{a}\in P
and
P
is prime, we obtain that there is
x\in X_{a}\cap P
. Then,
x\notin X_{b}
. Since
x\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
and
b\nleq x
, it follows that
b\vee x=1
. This is a contradiction because
b,x\in P
and
P
is a proper ideal. Hence
b=\bigwedge X_{b}\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
.
∎

A
semi-boolean algebra
(
[
1
]
) is a join-semilattice with a top element such that every principal upset is a Boolean lattice. In
[
1
]
Abbot show that the semi-boolean algebras are in a one-to-one correspondence with the implication algebras (also known as Tarski algebras).

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=\langle A,\vee,1\rangle
be a finite distributive nearlattice. Then, the nearlattice subalgebra
\langle\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A}),\vee,1\rangle
is a semi-boolean algebra.

Proof.

Let
a\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
. By Proposition
2.6
, we have
[a)_{\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})}=[a)_{A}
, and since
\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
is a nearlattice subalgebra of
\mathbf{A}
, it follows that
[a)_{\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})}
is a boolean lattice. Hence
\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
is a semi-boolean algebra.
∎

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
A
be a finite distributive nearlattice. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\bigwedge\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
exists in
A
;

(2)

There is
a\in A
such that
\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})=[a)
;

(3)

\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
is a boolean lattice.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
Let
a:=\bigwedge\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
. It follows by Proposition
2.3
that
a\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
. Let
b\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
. Then,
a=\bigwedge\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})\leq\bigwedge X_{b}=b
. Thus
b\in[a)
. Hence
\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})\subseteq[a)
. Now, by Proposition
2.6
, we have
[a)=[a)_{\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})}\subseteq\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
. Therefore,
\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})=[a)
.

(2)\Rightarrow(3)
It follows straightforward by Theorem
2.7
.

(3)\Rightarrow(1)
Let
a\in A
be the least element of
\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
. Thus
[a)=[a)_{\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})}=\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
. Since
\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})\subseteq\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})=[a)
, we obtain that
\bigwedge\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
exists in
A
.
∎

Corollary 2.9
.

If
\mathbf{L}
is a finite distributive lattice, then
\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{L})
is a boolean lattice. Moreover, for each
a\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{L})
and
x\in[a)
, we have
\neg_{a}x=\neg x\vee a
, where
\neg x
denotes the complement of
x
in
\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{L})
.

Proof.

From the previous proposition, it is clear that
\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{L})
is a boolean lattice. Let
a\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{L})
and
x\in[a)
. Since
[a)
is a boolean lattice, there is unique
\neg_{a}x\in[a)
such that
x\vee\neg_{a}x=1
and
x\wedge\neg_{a}x=a
. As
a\leq x
, by Proposition
2.6
we have
x\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{L})
and, there is
\neg x\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{L})
such that
x\vee\neg x=1
and
x\wedge\neg x=c
, where
c\in A
is the least element of
\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{L})
. Consider the element
\neg x\vee a\in[a)
. So,
(\neg x\vee a)\vee x=(\neg x\vee x)\vee a=1\vee a=1
and
(\neg x\vee a)\wedge x=(\neg x\wedge x)\vee(a\wedge x)=c\vee a=a
. Hence, we have
\neg_{a}x=\neg x\vee a
.
∎

3.
Annihilators

Let
\mathbf{A}=\langle A,\vee,1\rangle
be a distributive nearlattice. For every
a\in A
, let
a^{\top}=\{x\in A:a\vee x=1\}
(see
[
7
,
14
]
). It follows that
a^{\top}\in\mathrm{Fi}(\mathbf{A})
, for all
a\in A
. We will say that an element
a\in A
is
dense
if
a^{\top}=\{1\}
. Denote by
\mathrm{D}(A)
the set of all dense elements of
A
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite distributive nearlattice. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\bigwedge\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
exists in
A
;

(2)

There is
a\in A
such that
\mathrm{D}(A)=(a]
;

(3)

\mathrm{D}(A)
is not empty.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
Let
a:=\bigwedge\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
. Let
x\in a^{\top}
. So
x\vee a=1
. Suppose that
x<1
. Then, there is
b\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
such that
x\leq b
. Thus
b=b\vee a=1
, which is a contradiction. Hence
x=1
. It follows that
a^{\top}=\{1\}
, and thus
a\in\mathrm{D}(A)
. We obtain that
(a]\subseteq\mathrm{D}(A)
. Let now
b\in\mathrm{D}(A)
. So
b^{\top}=\{1\}
. Let
x\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
. Since
x\notin b^{\top}
, we have
b\vee x\neq 1
. Thus
b\leq x
. Then
b\leq\bigwedge\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})=a
. Hence
b\in(a]
. Therefore,
\mathrm{D}(A)\subseteq(a]
.

(2)\Rightarrow(3)
It is immediate.

(3)\Rightarrow(1)
Let us show that
\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
has a lower bound. As
\mathrm{D}(A)\neq\emptyset
, there is
a\in A
such that
a^{\top}=\{1\}
. Let
x\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
and suppose that
a\nleq x
. Thus,
x\vee a=1
. Then
x\in a^{\top}=\{1\}
, which is a contradiction because
x\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
. Hence
a\leq x
, for all
x\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
. Therefore,
\bigwedge\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
exists in
A
.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite distributive nearlattice. The following properties are satisfied:

(1)

Let
a,b\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
. Then,
a\leq b
if and only if
a^{\top}\subseteq b^{\top}
.

(2)

For each
a\in A
, we have
a^{\top}=\left(\bigwedge X_{a}\right)^{\top}
.

Proof.

(1)
It is easy to show that
a\leq b
implies
a^{\top}\subseteq b^{\top}
. Conversely, assume that
a^{\top}\subseteq b^{\top}
. Let
x\in X_{b}
. Then
x\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
and
b\vee x=x\neq 1
, i.e.,
x\notin b^{\top}
. So,
x\notin a^{\top}
. Since
a\vee x\neq 1
and
x
is dual atom, we have
a\leq x
. Thus
x\in X_{a}
. Thus
X_{b}\subseteq X_{a}
. Therefore,
a=\bigwedge X_{a}\leq\bigwedge X_{b}=b
.

(2)
Let
a\in A
and
b:=\bigwedge X_{a}
. By Proposition
2.3
,
b\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
. Since
a\leq b
we have
a^{\top}\subseteq b^{\top}
. Let
x\in b^{\top}
. Thus,

1=b\vee x=\left(\bigwedge X_{a}\right)\vee x=\bigwedge\{y\vee x\colon y\in X_{a}\}.

Then
y\vee x=1
, for all
y\in X_{a}
. Suppose that
a\vee x<1
. So, there exists
y\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
such that
a\vee x\leq y<1
. Thus
a\leq y
and
x\leq y
. It follows that
y\in X_{a}
. Then
y=y\vee x=1
, which is a contradiction. Hence,
a\vee x=1
. Thus
x\in a^{\top}
. Then, we obtain that
b^{\top}\subseteq a^{\top}
. Therefore,
a^{\top}=b^{\top}=\left(\bigwedge X_{a}\right)^{\top}
.
∎

Given a finite distributive nearlattice
\mathbf{A}
we define the map
\pi_{A}\colon A\to\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
as follows: for every
a\in A
,

\pi_{A}(a)=\bigwedge X_{a}.

From Proposition
2.3
, it follows that
\pi_{A}
is well defined.

Remark 3.3
.

Notice that
a\leq\pi_{A}(a)
, for all
a\in A
. Moreover, for each
a\in A
,
\pi_{A}(a)
is the least boolean element
b
such that
a\leq b
. Thus, we also have that
\pi_{A}(a)=a
, for all
a\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite distributive nearlattice. Then, the map
\pi_{A}\colon A\to\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
is an onto N-homomorphism.

Proof.

Let
a,b\in A
. By definition and Lemma
2.4
, we have
\pi_{A}(a\vee b)=\bigwedge X_{a\vee b}=\bigwedge\left(X_{a}\cap X_{b}\right)
. Since
X_{a}\cap X_{b}\subseteq X_{a},X_{b}
, it follows that
\pi_{A}(a)\vee\pi_{A}(b)\leq\pi_{A}(a\vee b)
. By Remark
3.3
, we have
a\leq\pi_{A}(a)
and
b\leq\pi_{A}(b)
. Thus
a\vee b\leq\pi_{A}(a)\vee\pi_{A}(b)
. Then, since
\pi_{A}(a)\vee\pi_{A}(b)
is a boolean element, it follows by Remark
3.3
that
\pi_{A}(a\vee b)\leq\pi_{A}(a)\vee\pi_{A}(b)
. Therefore,
\pi_{A}(a\vee b)=\pi_{A}(a)\vee\pi_{A}(b)
.

Now assume that
a\wedge b
exists in
\mathbf{A}
. Then,

\pi_{A}(a\wedge b)=\bigwedge X_{a\wedge b}=\bigwedge\left(X_{a}\cup X_{b}\right)=\bigwedge X_{a}\wedge\bigwedge X_{b}=\pi_{A}(a)\wedge\pi_{A}(b).

Finally,
\pi_{\mathbf{A}}(1)=\bigwedge X_{1}=\bigwedge\emptyset=1
.
∎

It is well known that if
\mathbf{L}
is a bounded distributive lattice, then the subset
\mathrm{Comp}(\mathbf{L})
of all complemented elements of
L
form a boolean algebra. Given that in a distributive nearlattice may not exists the least element, we generalize the concept of complemented element as follows. Recall that
\veebar
denotes the supremum in
\mathrm{Fi}(\mathbf{A})
and
a^{\top}\in\mathrm{Fi}(\mathbf{A})
, for all
a\in A
.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a distributive nearlattice. An element
a\in A
is said to be
complemented
if
[a)\veebar a^{\top}=A
.

We denote by
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
the set of all complemented elements of
\mathbf{A}
.

Proposition 3.6
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is a bounded distributive lattice, then
\mathrm{Comp}(\mathbf{A})=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
.

Proof.

Let
a\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
. So
[a)\veebar a^{\top}=A
. Let
0
be the least element of
A
. Since
0\in[a)\veebar a^{\top}
, there is
b\in a^{\top}
such that
a\wedge b=0
. Thus, we have
a\vee b=1
and
a\wedge b=0
. Then,
b
is the complement of
a
in
A
. Hence
a\in\mathrm{Comp}(A)
.

Let now
a\in\mathrm{Comp}(A)
. Let
a^{*}\in A
be the complement of
a
. Thus
a\wedge a^{*}=0
and
a\vee a^{*}=1
. Let
x\in A
. Then
(a\vee x)\wedge(a^{*}\vee x)=(a\wedge a^{*})\vee x=x
. Since
x\vee a\in[a)
and
a^{*}\vee x\in a^{\top}
, it follows that
x\in[a)\veebar a^{\top}
. Hence
[a)\veebar a^{\top}=A
. Therefore,
a\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
.
∎

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=\langle A,\vee,1\rangle
be a distributive nearlattice. Then
\langle\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A}),\vee,1\rangle
is a nearlattice subalgebra of
\mathbf{A}
.

Proof.

Let
a,b\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
. Thus
[a)\veebar a^{\top}=[b)\veebar b^{\top}=A
. Then,

[a\vee b)\veebar(a\vee b)^{\top}=\left([a)\cap[b)\right)\veebar(a\vee b)^{\top}=\left([a)\veebar(a\vee b)^{\top}\right)\cap\left([b)\veebar(a\vee b)^{\top}\right).

Since
a\leq a\vee b
, we have
a^{\top}\subseteq(a\vee b)^{\top}
. Thus
A=[a)\veebar a^{\top}\subseteq[a)\veebar(a\vee b)^{\top}
. Then
A=[a)\veebar(a\vee b)^{\top}
. Analogously, we have
A=[b)\veebar(a\vee b)^{\top}
. It follows
[a\vee b)\veebar(a\vee b)^{\top}=A
. Hence,
a\vee b\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
.

Assume now that
a\wedge b
exists in
A
. Then,

\displaystyle[a\wedge b)\veebar(a\wedge b)^{\top}

\displaystyle=([a)\veebar[b))\veebar\left(a^{\top}\cap b^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\left([a)\veebar[b)\veebar a^{\top}\right)\cap\left([a)\veebar[b)\veebar b^{\top}\right)=A.

Hence
a\wedge b\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
. Therefore,
\langle\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A}),\vee,1\rangle
is a nearlattice subalgebra of
\mathbf{A}
.
∎

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=\langle A,\vee,1\rangle
be a distributive nearlattice. Then
\langle\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A}),\vee,1\rangle
is a semi-boolean algebra.

Proof.

By the previous proposition we know that
\langle\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A}),\vee,1\rangle
is a distributive nearlattice. Thus,
[a)_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})}
is a bounded distributive lattice, for all
a\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
. Let
a\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
. Let us show that each element of the lattice
[a)_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})}
has a complement. Let
b\in[a)_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})}
. So
a\leq b
and
b\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
. Then
[b)\veebar b^{\top}=A
. Given that
a\in[b)\veebar b^{\top}
, there exist
x\in[b)
and
y\in b^{\top}
such that
a=x\wedge y
. Thus
a\leq y
and
y\vee b=1
. Notice that
a\leq b,y
. Then
a\leq b\wedge y
. Since
b\leq x
, it follows that
b\wedge y\leq x\wedge y=a
. Hence
a=b\wedge y
. Now we show that
y\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
. Since
a=b\wedge y
, we obtain that
[a)=[b)\veebar[y)
and
a^{\top}=b^{\top}\cap y^{\top}
. Moreover, since
b\in y^{\top}
, it follows that
[b)\subseteq y^{\top}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=[a)\veebar a^{\top}=\left([b)\veebar[y)\right)\veebar\left(b^{\top}\cap y^{\top}\right)=\left([b)\veebar[y)\veebar b^{\top}\right)\cap\left([b)\veebar[y)\veebar y^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=[y)\veebar y^{\top}.

Hence, we obtain that
y\in[a)_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})}
,
b\vee y=1
and
b\wedge y=a
. That is,
y
is the complement of
b
in
[a)_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})}
. Then
[a)_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})}
is a boolean algebra. Therefore,
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
is a semi-boolean algebra.
∎

Proposition 3.9

(
[
6
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a distributive nearlattice. Then,
\mathbf{A}
is a semi-boolean algebra if and only if
A=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite distributive nearlattice. Then,
\mathbf{A}
is a semi-boolean algebra if and only if
\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathbf{A}
is a semi-boolean algebra. Then,
A=\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
and
A=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
. Hence
\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
. Conversely, assume that
\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
. Let
a\in A
. Then, by Proposition
3.2
,
a^{\top}=b^{\top}
, where
b=\bigwedge X_{a}
. Thus,
b\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
and
a\leq b
. Then
A=[b)\veebar b^{\top}\subseteq[a)\veebar b^{\top}=[a)\veebar a^{\top}
. Thus

. Hence

. Therefore,
\mathbf{A}
is a semi-boolean algebra.
∎

4.
Connection with the free distributive lattice extension

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a distributive nearlattice. A pair
\langle L(\mathbf{A}),e\rangle
, where
L(\mathbf{A})
is a bounded distributive lattice and
e\colon A\to L(\mathbf{A})
is an N-embedding, is said to be a
free distributive lattice extension
of
\mathbf{A}
if
e[A]
is finitely meet-dense in
L(\mathbf{A})
and the following universal property holds: for every bounded distributive lattice
\mathbf{M}
and every N-homomorphism
h\colon A\to M
, there exists a unique lattice homomorphism
\widehat{h}\colon L(\mathbf{A})\to M
such that
h=\widehat{h}\circ e
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a distributive nearlattice and let
\langle L(\mathbf{A}),e\rangle
be the free distributive lattice extension of
\mathbf{A}
. The following properties are satisfied:

(1)

e\left[[a)\right]=\left[e(a)\right)
.

(2)

\mathrm{Fig}_{L(\mathbf{A})}\left(e\left[a^{\top}\right]\right)=e(a)^{\top}
.

Proof.

(1)
It is clear that
e\left[[a)\right]\subseteq[e(a))
. Conversely, if
u\in\left[e(a)\right)
, then
e(a)\leq u
. Since
e[A]
is finitely meet-dense in
L(\mathbf{A})
, there exist
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in A
such that
u=e(x_{1})\wedge\ldots\wedge e(x_{n})
. Then
e(a)\leq e(x_{i})
, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Since
e
is injective, we have
a\leq x_{i}
,
\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Then, there exists
y:=x_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge x_{n}\in[a)
such that
e(y)=u
. Hence
u\in e\left[[a)\right]
. Therefore,
e\left[[a)\right]=\left[e(a)\right)
.

(2)
We know that
e(a)^{\top}
is a filter of
L(\mathbf{A})
, and it is clear that
e\left[a^{\top}\right]\subseteq e(a)^{\top}
. Then, we have
\mathrm{Fig}_{L(\mathbf{A})}\left(e\left[a^{\top}\right]\right)\subseteq e(a)^{\top}
. Let now
u\in e(a)^{\top}
. So
u\vee e(a)=1_{L(\mathbf{A})}
. Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in A
be such that
u=e(a_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge e(a_{n})
. Thus
1_{L(\mathbf{A})}=u\vee e(a)=(e(a_{1})\vee e(a))\wedge\dots\wedge(e(a_{n})\vee e(a))
. Then
1_{L(\mathbf{A})}=e(a_{i}\vee a)
for all
i
. Hence
a_{i}\vee a=1
for all
i
. That is,
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in a^{\top}
. Thus,
e(a_{1}),\dots,e(a_{n})\in e\left[a^{\top}\right]
. Then
u=e(a_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge e(a_{n})\in\mathrm{Fig}_{L(\mathbf{A})}\left(e\left[a^{\top}\right]\right)
. Hence,
\mathrm{Fig}_{L(\mathbf{A})}\left(e\left[a^{\top}\right]\right)=e(a)^{\top}
.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite distributive nearlattice and
\langle L(\mathbf{A}),e\rangle
its free distributive nearlattice extension. Then,

(1)

e\left[\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})\right]=\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(L(\mathbf{A}))
;

(2)

e\left[\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})\right]\subseteq\mathrm{Bo}(L(\mathbf{A}))
;

(3)

e\left[\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\right]\subseteq\mathrm{Comp}(L(\mathbf{A}))
.

Proof.

(1) Let
a\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
. Let
u\in L(\mathbf{A})
be such that
e(a)\leq u\leq 1_{L(\mathbf{A})}
(recall that
1_{L(\mathbf{A})}=e(1)
). Thus, there are
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in A
such that
u=e(a_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge e(a_{n})
. Since
e(a)\leq u\leq e(a_{i})
for all
i
, we obtain that
a\leq a_{i}\leq 1
for all
i
. Then, for every
i
,
a=a_{i}
or
a_{i}=1
. If
a_{i}=1
for all
i
, then
u=1_{L(\mathbf{A})}
. Otherwise, there is
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
a_{i}=a
. Thus,
e(a)\leq u\leq e(a_{i})=e(a)
. Then
u=e(a)
. Hence
e(a)\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(L(\mathbf{A}))
. Let now
u\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(L(\mathbf{A}))
. There are
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in A
such that
u=e(a_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge e(a_{n})
. Since
u\neq 1_{L(\mathbf{A})}
, it follows that there is
i
, such that
a_{i}\neq 1
. Then, since
u\leq e(a_{i})<1_{L(\mathbf{A})}
, we have
u=e(a_{i})
. Moreover, it is straightforward to show directly that
a_{i}\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})
. Hence
u\in e\left[\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})\right]
.

(2) Let
a\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
. It is clear that
\langle[e(a)),\wedge,\vee,e(a),1_{L(\mathbf{A})}\rangle
is a bounded distributive sublattice of
L(\mathbf{A})
. We need only to prove that every element of
[e(a))
is complemented. Recall that
[e(a))=e\left[[a)\right]
. Thus, let
e(x)\in[e(a))
with
x\in[a)
. Since
[a)
is a boolean lattice, there is
\neg_{a}x\in[a)
such that
x\vee\neg_{a}x=1
and
x\wedge_{a}\neg_{a}x=a
. Then
1_{L(\mathbf{A})}=e\left(x\vee\neg_{a}x\right)=e(x)\vee e\left(\neg_{a}x\right)
and
e(a)=e\left(x\wedge_{a}\neg_{a}x\right)=e(x)\wedge e\left(\neg_{a}x\right)
. Thus,
e\left(\neg_{a}x\right)
is the complement of
e(x)
in
\left[e(a)\right)
, i.e.,
e\left(\neg_{a}x\right)=\neg_{e(a)}e(x)
. Therefore,
e(a)\in\mathrm{Bo}(L(\mathbf{A}))
.

(3) Let
a\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})
. So
[a)\veebar a^{\top}=A
. We need to prove that
e(a)\in\mathrm{Comp}(L(\mathbf{A}))
. From Proposition
3.6
, it is equivalent to show that
[e(a))\veebar e(a)^{\top}=L(\mathbf{A})
(here
\veebar
is the join of
\mathrm{Fi}(L(\mathbf{A}))
). Since
[a)\veebar a^{\top}=A
, it follows that

\mathrm{Fig}_{L(\mathbf{A})}\left(e\left[[a)\veebar a^{\top}\right]\right)=\mathrm{Fig}_{L(\mathbf{A})}\left(e[A]\right)=L(\mathbf{A}).

By
[
11
, Thm. 3.3]
, we know that the map
\Phi\colon\mathrm{Fi}(\mathbf{A})\to\mathrm{Fi}(L(\mathbf{A}))
defined by
\Phi(F)=\mathrm{Fig}_{L(\mathbf{A})}\left(e[F]\right)
for every
F\in\mathrm{Fi}(\mathbf{A})
, is a lattice isomorphism. Then,

\begin{split}\mathrm{Fig}_{L(\mathbf{A})}\left(e\left[[a)\veebar a^{\top}\right]\right)&=\Phi\left([a)\veebar a^{\top}\right)=\Phi\left([a)\right)\veebar\Phi\left(a^{\top}\right)\\
&=\mathrm{Fig}_{L(\mathbf{A})}\left(e\left[[a)\right]\right)\veebar\mathrm{Fig}_{L(\mathbf{A})}\left(e\left[a^{\top}\right]\right)\\
&=[e(a))\veebar\mathrm{Fig}_{L(\mathbf{A})}\left(e\left[a^{\top}\right]\right).\end{split}

Hence, by Lemma
4.2
, we have

L(\mathbf{A})=\mathrm{Fig}_{L(\mathbf{A})}\left(e\left[[a)\veebar a^{\top}\right]\right)=[e(a))\veebar e(a)^{\top}.

Therefore, we have shown that
e(a)\in\mathcal{C}(L(\mathbf{A}))
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is a finite distributive nearlattice, then
L(\mathbf{A})
is a finite distributive lattice. Hence, by Corolary
2.9
, it follows that
\mathrm{Bo}\left(L(\mathbf{A})\right)
is a boolean lattice and for all
a\in\mathrm{Bo}(\mathbf{A})
,
e\left(\neg_{a}x\right)=\neg e(x)\vee e(a)
for all
x\in[a)
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite distributive nearlattice and
\langle L(\mathbf{A}),e\rangle
its free distributive lattice extension. Then
\pi_{L(\mathbf{A})}\circ e=e\circ\pi_{A}
, i.e., the following diagram commutes:

\textstyle{A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{A}}
\scriptstyle{e}
\textstyle{\mathrm{Bo}({\bf{A}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{e}
\textstyle{L({\bf{A}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{L({\bf{A}})}}
\textstyle{\mathrm{Bo}\left(L({\bf{A}})\right)}

Proof.

Let
a\in A
. By Proposition
4.3
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\pi_{L(\mathbf{A})}\left(e(a)\right)

\displaystyle=\bigwedge\{z\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(L(\mathbf{A})):e(a)\leq z\}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge\{e(x):x\in\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A}),\,e(a)\leq e(x)\}

5.
A discrete representation

In this section we develop a representation for the class of finite distributive nearlattices through certain ordered structures. This representation is a nice generalization of that given by Birkhoff for finite distributive lattice through finite posets (
[
4
]
).

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a distributive nearlattice. An element
a\in A
is said to be
meet-irreducible
(or simply
irreducible
) if for all
x,y\in A
such that
x\wedge y
exists in
A
,
a=x\wedge y
implies
a=x
or
a=y
.

Let us denote by
\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A})
the set of all irreducible elements of
\mathbf{A}
. Notice that
\mathrm{At}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{A})\subseteq\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A})
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a distributive nearlattice and
a\in A
. Then,
a
is irreducible if and only if for all
x,y\in A
such that
x\wedge y
exists,
x\wedge y\leq a
implies
x\leq a
or
y\leq a
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite distributive nearlattice. Then, for every
a\in A
,

a=\bigwedge\{x\in\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A}):a\leq x\}.

Proof.

Let
S=\{a\in A:a\neq\bigwedge\{x\in\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A}):a\leq x\}\}
. We suppose by contradiction that
S\neq\emptyset
. Then, since
A
is finite,
S
has a maximal element
m
. Thus
m\notin\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A})
. Then, there exist
a,b\in A
such that
m=a\wedge b
,
m<a
and
m<b
. Since
m
is a maximal element of
S
, it follows that
a,b\notin S
. Thus
a=\bigwedge\{x\in\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A}):a\leq x\}
and
b=\bigwedge\{\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A}):b\leq x\}
. Hence, by Proposition
5.2
, we have

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=\left(\bigwedge\{x\in\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A}):a\leq x\}\right)\wedge\left(\bigwedge\{x\in\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A}):b\leq x\}\right)

\displaystyle=\bigwedge\{x\in\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A}):m\leq x\}.

Thus, we obtain that
m\notin S
, which is a contradiction. Therefore,
S=\emptyset
.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite distributive nearlattice and
\langle L(\mathbf{A}),e\rangle
its free distributive lattice extension. Then,
e\left[\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A})\right]=\mathrm{Irr}(L(\mathbf{A}))
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A})
. Let
u,v\in L(\mathbf{A})
be such that
e(x)=u\wedge v
. There are
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\in A
such that
u=e(a_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge e(a_{n})
and
v=e(b_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge e(b_{m})
. Thus, we have that
e(x)\leq e(a_{i}),e(b_{j})
, for all
i=1,\dots,n
and
j=1,\dots,m
. Then
x\leq a_{i},b_{j}
, for all
i,j
, which implies that there exists
a_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{n}\wedge b_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge b_{m}
. Then
e(x)=e(a_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{n}\wedge b_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge b_{m})
. So
x=a_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{n}\wedge b_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge b_{m}
. Since
x
is irreducible, it follows that
x=a_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{n}
or
x=b_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge b_{m}
. Then
e(x)=u
or
e(x)=v
. Hence
e(x)\in\mathrm{Irr}(L(\mathbf{A}))
. Therefore
e\left[\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A})\right]\subseteq\mathrm{Irr}(L(\mathbf{A}))
. Now it is straightforward to show the inclusion
\mathrm{Irr}(L(\mathbf{A}))\subseteq e\left[\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A})\right]
.
∎

From now on, given a poset
\langle X,\leq\rangle
,
\mathcal{D}(X)
will denote the collection of all downsets of
X
and let us consider the bounded distributive lattice
\langle\mathcal{D}(X),\cap,\cup,\emptyset,X\rangle
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite distributive nearlattice and
\langle L(\mathbf{A}),e\rangle
its free distributive lattice extension. Let us consider
\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A})
(
\mathrm{Irr}(L(\mathbf{A}))
) as a sub-poset of
A
(
L(\mathbf{A})
), that is,
x\leq y
if and only if
x\vee y=y
, for all
x,y\in\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A})
. Since
L(\mathbf{A})
is a finite distributive lattice, it follows that the map
\alpha\colon L(\mathbf{A})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{Irr}(L(\mathbf{A})))
defined by
\alpha(u)=\{z\in\mathrm{Irr}(L(\mathbf{A})):u\nleq z\}
is an isomorphism. Hence, by Proposition
5.4
, we have that the map
\widehat{\alpha}\colon L(\mathbf{A})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A}))
given by
\widehat{\alpha}(u)=\{x\in\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A}):u\nleq e(x)\}
is an isomorphism. Therefore, we have the following.

Proposition 5.5
.

For every finite distributive nearlattice
\mathbf{A}
, we have that
\langle\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A})),\widehat{e}\rangle
is the free distributive lattice extension of
\mathbf{A}
, where
\widehat{e}\colon A\to\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A}))
is given by
\widehat{e}(a)=(\widehat{\alpha}\circ e)(a)=\{x\in\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A}):a\nleq x\}
.

Definition 5.6
.

A
DN-structure
is a pair
\langle X,\gamma\rangle
such that
X
is a poset and
\gamma\colon\mathcal{D}(X)\to\{0,1\}
is a map satisfying the following:

(S1)

\gamma(X)=1
;

(S2)

\gamma([x)^{c})=1
, for all
x\in X
;

(S3)

for all
U,V\in\mathcal{D}(X)
,
U\subseteq V
implies
\gamma(U)\leq\gamma(V)
.

We say that a DN-structure
\langle X,\gamma\rangle
is
finite
if the poset
X
is finite. Let
\langle X,\gamma\rangle
be a DN-structure. We define

\mathbf{N}(X):=\{U\in\mathcal{D}(X):\gamma(U)=1\}.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
\langle X,\gamma\rangle
be a finite DN-structure. Then
\langle\mathbf{N}(X),\cup,X\rangle
is a distributive nearlattice and
\langle X,\leq\rangle\cong\langle\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{N}(X)),\subseteq\rangle
.

Proof.

First let us show that
\mathbf{N}(X)
is closed under
\cup
. Let
U_{1},U_{2}\in\mathbf{N}(X)
. Thus
\gamma(U_{1})=\gamma(U_{2})=1
. Then, since
U_{1}\subseteq U_{1}\cup U_{2}
, it follows by (S3) that
\gamma(U_{1}\cup U_{2})=1
. Hence
U_{1}\sqcup U_{2}\in\mathbf{N}(X)

Now let
U_{1},U_{2},V\in\mathbf{N}(X)
be such that
V\subseteq U_{1},U_{2}
. Thus
V\subseteq U_{1}\cap U_{2}
. By (S3), we have
U_{1}\cap U_{2}\in\mathbf{N}(X)
. Then,
U_{1}\cap U_{2}
is the meet of
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
in
[V)_{\mathbf{N}(X)}
. Hence
\langle[V)_{\mathbf{N}(X)},\cap,\cup,U,X\rangle
is a bounded distributive lattice. Therefore,
\langle\mathbf{N}(X),\cup,X\rangle
is a distributive nearlattice.

It is well-known that
\mathrm{Irr}(\mathcal{D}(X))=\{[x)^{c}:x\in X\}
, and thus
\langle\mathrm{Irr}(\mathcal{D}(X)),\subseteq\rangle\cong\langle X,\leq\rangle
. Let us prove that
\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{N}(X))=\mathrm{Irr}(\mathcal{D}(X))
. By (S2), we have
\mathrm{Irr}(\mathcal{D}(X))\subseteq\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{N}(X))
. Let now
U\in\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{N}(X))
. Since
U^{c}
is a finite upset of
X
, we have that
U^{c}=[x_{1})\cup\dots\cup[x_{n})
, for some
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in U^{c}
. Thus
U=[x_{1})^{c}\cap\dots\cap[x_{n})^{c}
. Then, since
U\in\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{N}(X)
, we obtain that
U=[x_{i})^{c}
, for some
i=1,\dots,n
. Hence
\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{N}(X))\subseteq\mathrm{Irr}(\mathcal{D}(X))
. Therefore, we have that
\langle\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{N}(X)),\subseteq\rangle\cong\langle X,\leq\rangle
.
∎

Let
\langle A,\vee,1\rangle
be a finite distributive nearlattice. Let
\mathbf{S}(A)=\langle\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A}),\leq\rangle
.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
\langle A,\vee,1\rangle
be a finite distributive nearlattice. Then, the pair
\langle\mathbf{S}(A),\gamma_{A}\rangle
is a DN-structure, where
\gamma_{A}\colon\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{S}(A))\to\{0,1\}
is the map defined by
\gamma_{A}(U)=1
if and only if
\bigwedge(\mathbf{S}(A)\setminus U)
exists in
A
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to show directly that the map
\gamma_{A}
satisfies conditions (S1)–(S3).
∎

Given a finite distributive nearlattice
\mathbf{A}
, we have that
\langle\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{S}(A)),\cup,\mathbf{S}(A)\rangle
is a finite distributive nearlattice, where
\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{S}(A))=\{U\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{S}(A)):\gamma_{A}(U)=1\}
.

Theorem 5.9

(Discrete representation)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=\langle A,\vee,1\rangle
be a finite distributive nearlattice. Then,
\mathbf{A}\cong\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{S}(A))
.

Proof.

From Proposition
5.5
, we have that the map
\widehat{e}\colon A\to\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{S}(A))
is an N-embedding, where
\widehat{e}(a)=\{x\in\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A}):a\nleq x\}
, for every
a\in A
. Thus,
\mathbf{A}\cong\widehat{e}[A]
. Let us show that
\widehat{e}[A]=\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{S}(A))
. Then,

\displaystyle U\in\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{S}(A))

\displaystyle\iff U\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{S}(A))\text{ and }\gamma_{A}(U)=1

\displaystyle\iff U\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{S}(A))\text{ and }\bigwedge\mathbf{S}(A)\setminus U\text{ exists in }A

\displaystyle\iff\text{there exists }a\in A\text{ such that }U=\{x\in\mathrm{Irr}(\mathbf{A}):a\nleq x\}

\displaystyle\iff\text{there exists }a\in A\text{ such that }U=\widehat{e}(a)

\displaystyle\iff U\in\widehat{e}[A].

Hence,
\mathbf{A}\cong\widehat{e}[A]=\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{S}(A))
.
∎

Remark 5.10
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite distributive nearlattice. If
\mathbf{A}
is in fact a lattice, then it follows that
\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}))=\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}))
. Thus
\mathbf{A}\cong\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}))
. Moreover, it is clear that the finite posets are in one-to-one correspondence with the DN-structures
\langle X,\gamma\rangle
such that
\gamma(U)=1
, for all
U\in\mathcal{D}(X)
. Therefore, from the representation above established we can obtain the representation given by Birkhoff for finite distributive lattices.
