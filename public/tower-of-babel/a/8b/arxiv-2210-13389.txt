A Regularized Conditional GAN for Posterior Sampling in Image Recovery Problems
Matthew C. Bendel, Rizwan Ahmad, Philip Schniter

Abstract
In image recovery problems, one seeks to infer an image from distorted, incomplete, and/or noise-corrupted measurements. Such problems arise in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography, deblurring, super-resolution, inpainting, phase retrieval, image-to-image translation, and other applications. Given a training set of signal/measurement pairs, we seek to do more than just produce one good image estimate. Rather, we aim to rapidly and accurately sample from the posterior distribution. To do this, we propose a regularized conditional Wasserstein GAN that generates dozens of high-quality posterior samples per second. Our regularization comprises an ℓ 1 \ell_{1} penalty and an adaptively weighted standard-deviation reward. Using quantitative evaluation metrics like conditional Fréchet inception distance, we demonstrate that our method produces state-of-the-art posterior samples in both multicoil MRI and large-scale inpainting applications. The code for our model can be found here: https://github.com/matt-bendel/rcGAN .

Introduction

We consider image recovery, where one observes measurements
\boldsymbol{y}=\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{x})
of the true image
\boldsymbol{x}
that may be masked, distorted, and/or corrupted
\boldsymbol{x}
with noise, and the goal is to infer
\boldsymbol{x}
from
\boldsymbol{y}
.
This includes linear inverse problems arising in, e.g., deblurring, super-resolution, inpainting, colorization, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), where
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{Ax}+\boldsymbol{w}
with known linear operator
\boldsymbol{A}
and noise
\boldsymbol{w}
.
But it also includes non-linear inverse problems like those arising in phase-retrieval and dequantization, as well as image-to-image translation problems.
In all cases, it is impossible to perfectly infer
\boldsymbol{x}
from
\boldsymbol{y}
.

Image recovery is often posed as finding the single “best” recovery
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
, which is known as a
point estimate
of
\boldsymbol{x}

[
1
]
.
But point estimation is problematic due to the
perception-distortion tradeoff

[
2
]
, which establishes a fundamental tradeoff between distortion (defined as some distance between
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\boldsymbol{x}
) and perceptual quality (defined as some distance between
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
and the set of clean images).
For example, the minimum mean-squared error (MMSE) recovery
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathsf{mmse}}
is optimal in terms of
\ell_{2}
distortion, but can be unrealistically smooth.
Although one could instead compute an approximation of the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate
[
3
]
or minimize some combination of perceptual and distortion losses,
it’s unclear which combination would be most appropriate.

Another major limitation with point estimation is that it’s unclear how certain one can be about the recovered
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
For example, with deep-learning-based recovery, it’s possible to hallucinate a nice-looking
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
, but is it correct?
Quantifying the uncertainty in
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
is especially important in medical applications such as MRI, where a diagnosis must be made based on the measurements
\boldsymbol{y}
.
Rather than simply reporting our best guess of whether a pathology is present or absent based on
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
, we might want to report the probability that the pathology is present (given all available data).

Yet another problem with point estimation is that the estimated
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
could pose issues with fairness
[
4
]
.
For example, say we are inpainting a face within an image.
With a racially heterogeneous prior distribution,
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathsf{mmse}}
(being the posterior mean) will be biased towards the most predominant race.
The same will be true of many other point estimates
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

To address the aforementioned limitations of point estimation, we focus on
generating samples from the posterior distribution

p_{\mathsf{x}|\mathsf{y}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{y})
, which represents the complete state-of-knowledge about
\boldsymbol{x}
given the measurements
\boldsymbol{y}
.
The posterior correctly fuses prior knowledge with measurement knowledge, thereby alleviating any concerns about fairness (assuming the data used to represent the prior is fairly chosen
[
4
]
).
Furthermore, the posterior directly facilitates uncertainty quantification via, e.g., pixel-wise standard deviations or pathology detection probabilities (see Appendix
A
).
Also, if it was important to report a single “good” recovery, then posterior sampling leads to an easy navigation of the perception-distortion tradeoff.
For example, averaging
P\geq 1
posterior samples gives a close approximation to the less-distorted-but-oversmooth
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathsf{mmse}}
with large
P
and sharp-but-more-distorted
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
with small
P
.
Additionally, posterior sampling unlocks other important capabilities such as adaptive acquisition
[
5
]
and counterfactual diagnosis
[
6
]
.

Concretely, given a training dataset of image/measurement pairs
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
,
our goal is to learn a generating function
G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
that, for a new
\boldsymbol{y}
, maps random code vectors
\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})
to posterior samples
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}=G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y})\sim p_{\mathsf{x}|\mathsf{y}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{y})
.
There exist several well-known approaches to this task, with recent literature focusing on
conditional generative adversarial networks (cGANs)

[

,
conditional variational autoencoders (cVAEs)

,

[
14

15

16
]
,
and score/diffusion/Langevin-based generative models

[
17
,
18
,

,
20
,

.
Despite it being a longstanding problem, posterior image sampling remains challenging.
Although score/diffusion/Langevin approaches have dominated the recent literature due to advances in accuracy and diversity, their sample-generation speeds remain orders-of-magnitude behind those of cGANs, cVAEs, and cNFs.

We choose to focus on cGANs, which are typically regarded as generating samples of high quality but low diversity.
Our proposed cGAN tackles the lack-of-diversity issue using a novel regularization that consists of supervised-
\ell_{1}

This is not a heuristic choice; we prove that our regularization enforces consistency with the true posterior mean and covariance under certain conditions.

Experimentally, we demonstrate our regularized cGAN on accelerated MRI and large-scale face completion/inpainting.
We consider these applications for three main reasons.
First, uncertainty quantification in MRI, and fairness in face-generation, are both of paramount importance.
Second, posterior-sampling has been well studied for both applications, and fine-tuned cGANs

2
Problem formulation and background

We build on the Wasserstein cGAN framework from
[
8
]
.
The goal is to design a generator network
G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
such that, for fixed
\boldsymbol{y}
, the random variable
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}=G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y})
induced by
\boldsymbol{z}\sim p_{\mathsf{z}}
has a distribution that best matches the posterior
p_{\mathsf{x}|\mathsf{y}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{y})
in Wasserstein-1 distance.
Here,
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{Y}
, and
\mathcal{Z}
denote the sets of
\boldsymbol{x}
,
\boldsymbol{y}
, and
\boldsymbol{z}
, respectively, and
\boldsymbol{z}
is drawn independently of
\boldsymbol{y}
.

The Wasserstein-1 distance can be expressed as

\displaystyle W_{1}(p_{\mathsf{x}|\mathsf{y}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y}),p_{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}|\mathsf{y}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y}))=\sup_{D\in L_{1}}\E_{\mathsf{x}|\mathsf{y}}\{D(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\}-\E_{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}|\mathsf{y}}\{D(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})\},

(1)

where
L_{1}
denotes functions that are 1-Lipschitz with respect to their first argument and
D:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a “critic” or “discriminator” that tries to distinguish between true
\boldsymbol{x}
and generated
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
given
\boldsymbol{y}
.
Since we want the method to work for typical values of
\boldsymbol{y}
, we define a loss by taking an expectation of (
1
) over
\boldsymbol{y}\sim p_{\mathsf{y}}
.
Since the expectation commutes with the supremum in (
1
),
we have
[
8
]

\displaystyle\E_{\mathsf{y}}\{W_{1}(p_{\mathsf{x}|\mathsf{y}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y}),p_{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}|\mathsf{y}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y}))\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{D\in L_{1}}\E_{\mathsf{x,y}}\{D(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\}-\E_{\widehat{\mathsf{x}},y}\{D(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})\}

(2)

\displaystyle=\sup_{D\in L_{1}}\E_{\mathsf{x,z,y}}\{D(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})-D(G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y}),\boldsymbol{y})\}.

(3)

In practice,
D
is implemented by a neural network
D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
with parameters
\boldsymbol{\phi}
, and
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})
are trained by alternately minimizing

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{adv}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})\triangleq\E_{\mathsf{x,z,y}}\{D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})-D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y}),\boldsymbol{y})\}

(4)

with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and minimizing
-\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{adv}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})+\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{gp}}(\boldsymbol{\phi})
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\phi}
, where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{gp}}(\boldsymbol{\phi})
is a gradient penalty that is used to encourage
D_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\in L_{1}

[
24
]
.
Furthemore, the expectation over
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
in (
4
) is replaced in practice by a sample average over the training examples
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
.

One of the main challenges with the cGAN framework in image recovery problems is that, for each measurement example
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}
, there is only a single image example
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
.
Thus, with the previously described training methodology, there is no incentive for the generator to produce diverse samples
G(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y})|_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim p_{\mathsf{z}}}
for a fixed
\boldsymbol{y}
.
This can lead to the generator learning to ignore the code vector
\boldsymbol{z}
, which causes a form of “mode collapse.”

Although issues with stability and mode collapse are also present in
unconditional
GANs (uGANs) or discretely conditioned cGANs
[
25
]
,
the causes are fundamentally different than in continuously conditioned cGANs like ours.
With continuously conditioned cGANs, there is only
one
example of a valid
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
for each given
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}
, whereas with uGANs there are many
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
and with discretely conditioned cGANs there are many
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
for each conditioning class.
As a result, most strategies that are used to combat mode-collapse in uGANs
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
are not well suited to cGANs.
For example, mini-batch discrimination strategies like MBSD
[
29
]
, where the discriminator aims to distinguish a mini-batch of true samples
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\}
from a mini-batch of generated samples
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\}
,
don’t work with cGANs because the posterior statistics are very different than the prior statistics.

To combat mode collapse in cGANs, Adler & Öktem
[
8
]
proposed to use a three-input discriminator
D^{\mathsf{adler}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
and replace
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{adv}}
from (
4
) with the loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{adler}}_{\mathsf{adv}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle\triangleq\E_{\mathsf{x,z_{1},z_{2},y}}\big\{\tfrac{1}{2}D^{\mathsf{adler}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x},G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}),\boldsymbol{y})+\tfrac{1}{2}D^{\mathsf{adler}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{2},\boldsymbol{y}),\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle\quad-D^{\mathsf{adler}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}),G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{2},\boldsymbol{y}),\boldsymbol{y})\big\},

(5)

which rewards variation between the first and second inputs to
D^{\mathsf{adler}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
.
They then proved that minimizing
\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{adler}}_{\mathsf{adv}}
in place of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{adv}}
does not compromise the Wasserstein cGAN objective, i.e.,
\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{adler}}_{\mathsf{adv}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{adv}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})
.
As we show in Section
4
, this approach does prevent complete mode collapse, but it leaves much room for improvement.

3
Proposed method

3.1
Proposed regularization: supervised-
\ell_{1}
plus SD reward

We now propose a novel cGAN regularization framework.
To train the generator, we propose to solve

\displaystyle\textstyle\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\{\beta_{\mathsf{adv}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{adv}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})+\mathcal{L}_{1,\mathsf{SD},P_{\mathsf{train}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\beta_{\mathsf{SD}})\}

(6)

with appropriately chosen
\beta_{\mathsf{adv}},\beta_{\mathsf{SD}}>0
and
P_{\mathsf{train}}\geq 2
, where the regularizer

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathcal{L}_{1,\mathsf{SD},P_{\mathsf{train}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\beta_{\mathsf{SD}})\triangleq\mathcal{L}_{1,P_{\mathsf{train}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\beta_{\mathsf{SD}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{SD},P_{\mathsf{train}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

(7)

is constructed from the
P_{\mathsf{train}}
-sample supervised-
\ell_{1}
loss and standard-deviation (SD) reward terms

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1,P_{\mathsf{train}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\triangleq\textstyle\E_{\mathsf{x,z_{1},...,z_{P},y}}\big\{\|\boldsymbol{x}-\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\scriptscriptstyle(P_{\mathsf{train}})}\|_{1}\big\}

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{SD},P_{\mathsf{train}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\triangleq\textstyle\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2P_{\mathsf{train}}(P_{\mathsf{train}}-1)}}\sum_{i=1}^{P_{\mathsf{train}}}\E_{\mathsf{z_{1},...,z_{P},y}}\big\{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}-\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\scriptscriptstyle(P_{\mathsf{train}})}\|_{1}\big\},

(9)

and where
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}
denote the generated samples and
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\scriptscriptstyle(P)}
their
P
-sample average:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\triangleq G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}),\qquad\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\scriptscriptstyle(P)}\triangleq\textstyle\frac{1}{P}\sum_{i=1}^{P}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}.

(10)

The use of supervised-
\ell_{1}
loss and SD reward in (
7
) is not heuristic.
As shown in Proposition
3.1
, it encourages the samples
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}
to match the true posterior in both mean and covariance.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose
P_{\mathsf{train}}\geq 2
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
has complete control over the
\boldsymbol{y}
-conditional mean and covariance of
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
.
Then the parameters

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{1,\mathsf{SD},P_{\mathsf{train}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\beta_{\mathsf{SD}}^{\mathcal{N}})
with

\displaystyle\textstyle\beta_{\mathsf{SD}}^{\mathcal{N}}\triangleq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi P_{\mathsf{train}}(P_{\mathsf{train}}+1)}}

(11)

yield generated statistics

\displaystyle\E_{\mathsf{z_{i}}|\mathsf{y}}\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})|\boldsymbol{y}\}

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathsf{x}|\mathsf{y}}\{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{y}\}=\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathsf{mmse}}

(12a)

\displaystyle\Cov_{\mathsf{z_{i}}|\mathsf{y}}\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})|\boldsymbol{y}\}

\displaystyle=\Cov_{\mathsf{x}|\mathsf{y}}\{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{y}\}

(12b)

when the elements of
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{x}
are independent Gaussian conditioned on
\boldsymbol{y}
.
Thus, minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{1,\mathsf{SD},P_{\mathsf{train}}}
encourages the
\boldsymbol{y}
-conditional mean and covariance of
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
to match those of the true
\boldsymbol{x}
.

See Appendix
B
for a proof.
In imaging applications,
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{x}
may not be independent Gaussian conditioned on
\boldsymbol{y}
, and so the value of
\beta_{\mathsf{SD}}
in (
11
) may not be appropriate.
Thus we propose a method to automatically tune
\beta_{\mathsf{SD}}
in Section
3.2
.

\boldsymbol{\theta}=[\mu,\sigma]^{\top}

G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(z,y)=\mu+\sigma z

for any

, we recover the true

.

We note that regularizing a cGAN with supervised-
\ell_{1}
loss alone is not new; see, e.g.,
[
7
]
.
In fact, the use of supervised-
\ell_{1}
loss is often preferred over
\ell_{2}
in image recovery because it results in sharper, more visually pleasing results
[
30
]
.
But regularizing a cGAN using supervised-
\ell_{1}
loss
alone
can push the generator towards mode collapse, for reasons described below.
For example, in
[
7
]
,
\ell_{1}
-induced mode collapse led the authors to use dropout to induce generator variation, instead of random
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
.

Why not supervised-
\ell_{2}
regularization?

One may wonder: Why regularize using supervised-
\ell_{1}
loss plus an SD reward in (
7
) and not a more conventional choice like supervised-
\ell_{2}
loss plus a variance reward, or even supervised-
\ell_{2}
loss alone?
We start by discussing the latter.

The use of supervised-
\ell_{2}
regularization in a cGAN can be found in
[
7
]
.
In this case, to train the generator, one would aim to solve
\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\{\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{adv}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\}
with some
\lambda>0
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\triangleq\E_{\mathsf{x,y}}\big\{\|\boldsymbol{x}-\E_{\mathsf{z}}\{G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y})\}\|^{2}_{2}\big\}.

(13)

Ohayon et al.
[
23
]
revived this idea for the explicit purpose of fighting mode collapse.
In practice, however, the
\E_{\mathsf{z}}
term in (
13
) must be implemented by a finite-sample average, giving

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2,P_{\mathsf{train}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\triangleq\textstyle\E_{\mathsf{x,z_{1},...,z_{P},y}}\big\{\big\|\boldsymbol{x}-\frac{1}{P_{\mathsf{train}}}\sum_{i=1}^{P_{\mathsf{train}}}G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i},\boldsymbol{y})\big\|^{2}_{2}\big\}

(14)

for some
P_{\mathsf{train}}\geq 2
.
For example, Ohayon’s implementation
[
31
]
used
P_{\mathsf{train}}=8
.
As we show in Proposition
3.2
,
\mathcal{L}_{2,P_{\mathsf{train}}}

induces
mode collapse rather than prevents it.

Proposition 3.2
.

Say
P_{\mathsf{train}}
is finite and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
has complete control over the
\boldsymbol{y}
-conditional mean and covariance of
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
.
Then the parameters

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{2,P_{\mathsf{train}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

yield generated statistics

\displaystyle\E_{\mathsf{z_{i}}|\mathsf{y}}\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})|\boldsymbol{y}\}

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathsf{x}|\mathsf{y}}\{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{y}\}=\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathsf{mmse}}

(15a)

\displaystyle\Cov_{\mathsf{z_{i}}|\mathsf{y}}\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})|\boldsymbol{y}\}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}.

(15b)

Thus, minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{2,P_{\mathsf{train}}}
encourages mode collapse.

The proof (see Appendix
C
) follows from the bias-variance decomposition of (
14
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2,P_{\mathsf{train}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\textstyle\E_{\mathsf{y}}\big\{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathsf{mmse}}-\E_{\mathsf{z_{i}}|\mathsf{y}}\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\boldsymbol{y}\}\|_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{P_{\mathsf{train}}}\tr[\Cov_{\mathsf{z_{i}}|\mathsf{y}}\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\boldsymbol{y}\}]+\E_{\mathsf{x}|\mathsf{y}}\{\|\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathsf{mmse}}\|_{2}^{2}|\boldsymbol{y}\}\big\},

(16)

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathsf{mmse}}\triangleq\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathsf{mmse}}
is the MMSE error.
Figure
1
(c) shows that
\mathcal{L}_{2,P_{\mathsf{train}}}
regularization causes mode collapse in the toy example, and Section
4.2
shows that it causes mode collapse in MRI.

Why not supervised
\ell_{2}
plus a variance reward?

To mitigate
\mathcal{L}_{2,P_{\mathsf{train}}}
’s incentive for mode-collapse, the second term in (
16
) could be canceled using a variance reward, giving

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2,\mathsf{var},P_{\mathsf{train}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\triangleq\textstyle\mathcal{L}_{2,P_{\mathsf{train}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{1}{P_{\mathsf{train}}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{var},P_{\mathsf{train}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

(17)

\displaystyle\text{with~}\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{var},P_{\mathsf{train}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\triangleq\textstyle\frac{1}{P_{\mathsf{train}}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{P_{\mathsf{train}}}\E_{\mathsf{z_{1},...,z_{P},y}}\{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\scriptscriptstyle(P)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{2}^{2}\}.

(18)

since Appendix
D
shows that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{var},P_{\mathsf{train}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is an unbiased estimator of the posterior trace-covariance:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{var},P_{\mathsf{train}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\textstyle\E_{\mathsf{y}}\{\tr[\Cov_{\mathsf{z_{i}}|\mathsf{y}}\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\boldsymbol{y}\}]\}\text{~~for any~}P_{\mathsf{train}}\geq 2.

(19)

However, the resulting
\mathcal{L}_{2,\mathsf{var},P_{\mathsf{train}}}
regularizer in (
17
) does not encourage the generated covariance to match the

posterior covariance, unlike the proposed
\mathcal{L}_{1,\mathsf{SD},P_{\mathsf{train}}}
regularizer in (

) (recall Proposition

).
For the toy example, this behavior is visible in Fig.

, the number of averaged outputs, for several training
\beta_{\mathsf{SD}}
and MRI recovery at
R=4
. Also shown is the theoretical behavior for true-posterior samples.

We now propose a method to auto-tune
\beta_{\mathsf{SD}}
in (
7
) for a given training dataset.
Our approach is based on the observation that larger values of
\beta_{\mathsf{SD}}
tend to yield samples
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
with more variation.
But more variation is not necessarily better; we want samples with the correct amount of variation.
To assess the correct amount of variation, we compare the expected
\ell_{2}
error of the
P
-sample average
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\scriptscriptstyle(P)}
to that of
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}
.
When
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}
are true-posterior samples, these errors follow a particular relationship, as established by Proposition
3.3
below (see Appendix
E
for a proof).

Proposition 3.3
.

Say
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\sim p_{\mathsf{x}|\mathsf{y}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{y})
are independent samples of the true posterior and, for any
P\geq 1
, their
P
-sample average is
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\scriptscriptstyle(P)}\triangleq\frac{1}{P}\sum_{i=1}^{P}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{P}

\displaystyle\triangleq\E\{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\scriptscriptstyle(P)}-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}^{2}|\boldsymbol{y}\}=\tfrac{P+1}{P}\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{mmse}}\text{,\hskip 10.22217ptwhich implies\hskip 10.22217pt}\tfrac{\mathcal{E}_{1}}{\mathcal{E}_{P}}=\tfrac{2P}{P+1}.

(20)

Experimentally we find that
\mathcal{E}_{1}/\mathcal{E}_{P}
grows with the SD reward weight
\beta_{\mathsf{SD}}
.
(See Fig.
2
.)
Thus, we propose to adjust
\beta_{\mathsf{SD}}
so that the observed SNR-gain ratio
\mathcal{E}_{1}/\mathcal{E}_{P_{\mathsf{val}}}
matches the correct ratio
(2P_{\mathsf{val}})/(P_{\mathsf{val}}+1)
from (
20
), for some
P_{\mathsf{val}}\geq 2
that does not need to match
P_{\mathsf{train}}
.
(We use
P_{\mathsf{val}}=8
in Section
4
.)
In particular, at each training epoch
\tau
, we approximate
\mathcal{E}_{P_{\mathsf{val}}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{1}

as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{P_{\mathsf{val}},\tau}

\displaystyle\triangleq\textstyle\frac{1}{V}\sum_{v=1}^{V}\|\frac{1}{P_{\mathsf{val}}}\sum_{i=1}^{P_{\mathsf{val}}}G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\tau}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i,v},\boldsymbol{y}_{v})-\boldsymbol{x}_{v}\|_{2}^{2}

(21)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{1,\tau}

\displaystyle\triangleq\textstyle\frac{1}{V}\sum_{v=1}^{V}\|G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\tau}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1,v},\boldsymbol{y}_{v})-\boldsymbol{x}_{v}\|_{2}^{2},

(22)

with validation set
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{v},\boldsymbol{y}_{v})\}_{v=1}^{V}
and
i.i.d. codes
\{\boldsymbol{z}_{i,v}\}_{i=1}^{P_{\mathsf{val}}}
.
We update
\beta_{\mathsf{SD}}
using gradient descent:

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathsf{SD},\tau+1}

\displaystyle=\textstyle\beta_{\mathsf{SD},\tau}-\mu_{\mathsf{SD}}\cdot\big([\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{1,\tau}/\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{P_{\mathsf{val}},\tau}]_{\text{dB}}-[2P_{\mathsf{val}}/(P_{\mathsf{val}}+1)]_{\text{dB}}\big)\beta_{\mathsf{SD}}^{\mathcal{N}}\text{~~for~~}\tau=0,1,2,\dots

(23)

with

\beta_{\mathsf{SD},0}=\beta_{\mathsf{SD}}^{\mathcal{N}}
,
some
\mu_{\mathsf{SD}}>0
,
and
[x]_{\text{dB}}\triangleq 10\log_{10}(x)
.

4
Numerical experiments

4.1
Conditional Fréchet inception distance

As previously stated, our goal is to train a generator
G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
so that, for typical fixed values of
\boldsymbol{y}
, the generated distribution
p_{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}|\mathsf{y}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{y})
matches the true posterior
p_{\mathsf{x}|\mathsf{y}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{y})
.
It is essential to have a quantitative metric for evaluating performance with respect to this goal.
For example, it is not enough that the generated samples are “accurate” in the sense of being close to the ground truth, nor is it enough that they are “diverse” according to some heuristically chosen metric.

We quantify posterior-approximation quality using the conditional Fréchet inception distance (CFID)
[
22
]
, a computationally efficient approximation to the conditional Wasserstein distance

\displaystyle\CWD\triangleq\E_{\mathsf{y}}\{W_{2}(p_{\mathsf{x}|\mathsf{y}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y}),p_{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}|\mathsf{y}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y}))\}.

(24)

In (
24
),
W_{2}(p_{\mathsf{a}},p_{\mathsf{b}})
denotes the Wasserstein-2 distance between distributions
p_{\mathsf{a}}
and
p_{\mathsf{b}}
, defined as

\displaystyle W_{2}(p_{\mathsf{a}},p_{\mathsf{b}})\triangleq\min_{p_{\mathsf{a,b}}\in\Pi(p_{\mathsf{a}},p_{\mathsf{b}})}\E_{\mathsf{a,b}}\{\|\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}\|^{2}_{2}\},

(25)

where
\Pi(p_{\mathsf{a}},p_{\mathsf{b}})\triangleq\textstyle\big\{p_{\mathsf{a,b}}:p_{\mathsf{a}}=\int p_{\mathsf{a,b}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{b}\text{~and~}p_{\mathsf{b}}=\int p_{\mathsf{a,b}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{a}\big\}
denotes the set of joint distributions
p_{\mathsf{a,b}}
with prescribed marginals
p_{\mathsf{a}}
and
p_{\mathsf{b}}
.
Similar to how FID
[
32
]
—a popular uGAN metric—is computed, CFID approximates CWD (
24
) as follows:
i) the random vectors
\boldsymbol{x}
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
, and
\boldsymbol{y}
are replaced by low-dimensional embeddings
\underline{\boldsymbol{x}}
,
\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{x}}}
, and
\underline{\boldsymbol{y}}
, generated by the convolutional layers of a deep network, and
ii) the embedding distributions
p_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}
and
p_{\widehat{\underline{\mathsf{x}}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}
are approximated by multivariate Gaussians.
More details on CFID are given in Appendix
F
.

4.2
MRI experiments

We consider parallel MRI recovery, where the goal is to recover a complex-valued multicoil image
\boldsymbol{x}
from zero-filled measurements

Data.

[

at acceleration

Architecture.

[

Architecture and training details are given in Appendix

.

Competitors.

Conclusion

We propose a novel regularization framework for image-recovery cGANs that consists of supervised-
\ell_{1}
loss plus an appropriately weighted standard-deviation reward.
For the case of an independent Gaussian posterior, we proved that our regularization yields generated samples that agree with the true-posterior samples in both mean and covariance.
We also established limitations for alternatives based on supervised-
\ell_{2}
regularization with or without a variance reward.
For practical datasets, we proposed a method to auto-tune our standard-deviation reward weight.

Experiments on parallel MRI and large-scale face inpainting showed our proposed method outperforming all cGAN and score-based competitors in CFID, which measures posterior-approximation quality, as well as other metrics like FID, PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, and DISTS.
Furthermore, it generates samples thousands of times faster than Langevin/score-based approaches.

In ongoing work, we are extending our approach so that it can be trained to handle a wide range of recovery tasks, such as MRI with a generic acceleration and sampling mask
[
46
]
, or inpainting with a generic mask.
We are also extending our approach to other applications, such as super-resolution, deblurring, compressive sensing, denoising, and phase retrieval.

Limitations.

We acknowledge several limitations of our work.
First, while our current work focuses on how to build a fast and accurate posterior sampler, it’s not yet clear how to best exploit the resulting posterior samples in each given application.
For example, in MRI, where the posterior distribution has the potential to help assess uncertainty in image recovery, it’s still not quite clear how to best convey uncertainty information to radiologists (e.g., they may not gain much from pixel-wise SD images).
More work is needed on this front.
Second, we acknowledge that, because radiologists are risk-averse, more studies are needed before they will feel comfortable incorporating generative deep-learning-based methods into the clinical workflow.
Third, we acknowledge that the visual quality of our
R=8
MRI reconstructions falls below clinical standards.
Fourth, some caution is needed when interpreting our CFID, FID, and DISTS perceptual metrics because the VGG-16 backbone used to compute them was trained on ImageNet data.
Although there is some evidence that the resulting DISTS metric correlates well with radiologists’ perceptions
[
41
]
, there is also evidence that ImageNet-trained features may discard information that is diagnostically relevant in medical imaging
[
47
]
.
Thus our results need to be validated with a pathology-centric radiologist study before they can be considered relevant to clinical practice.

Appendix A
Posterior samples facilitate calibrated/fair detection

Say that
s\in\{1,0\}
denotes the presence or absence of a particular pathology (e.g., brain tumor), and say that we train a soft classifier
c(\cdot)
on ground-truth images
\boldsymbol{x}
and calibrate it such that

\displaystyle c(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\Pr\{s=1|\boldsymbol{x}\}.

(A.1)

Now say that we observe a distorted/corrupted/incomplete measurement
\boldsymbol{y}=\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{x})
.
We would like to infer the probability that the pathology is present given
\boldsymbol{y}
, i.e., compute
\Pr\{s=1|\boldsymbol{y}\}
.
Note that

\displaystyle\Pr\{s=1|\boldsymbol{y}\}

\displaystyle=\int\Pr\{s=1,\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{y}\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\int\Pr\{s=1|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\}p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}

(A.2)

\displaystyle=\int\Pr\{s=1|\boldsymbol{x}\}p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\int c(\boldsymbol{x})p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\E\{c(\boldsymbol{x})|\boldsymbol{y}\}

(A.3)

\displaystyle=\lim_{P\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{P}\sum_{i=1}^{P}c(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\text{~~for~~}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\sim\text{i.i.d.~}p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{y}),

(A.4)

where the last equality follows from the law of large numbers.
So, given access to many independent posterior samples
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}
, equation (
A.4
) says that we can simply plug them into our calibrated classifier
c(\cdot)
and average the result to compute
\Pr\{s=1|\boldsymbol{y}\}
.
Conversely, if we have access to only the posterior mean
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathsf{mmse}}=\E\{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{y}\}
, then because

\displaystyle\Pr\{s=1|\boldsymbol{y}\}=\E\{c(\boldsymbol{x})|\boldsymbol{y}\}\neq c(\E\{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{y}\})=c(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathsf{mmse}})

(A.5)

for any non-linear
c(\cdot)
, the plug-in probability estimate will be incorrect.
In fact, there exists no point estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
that gives the correct
\Pr\{s=1|\boldsymbol{y}\}
for general
c(\cdot)
.

Although above we defined
c(\cdot)
as a (soft) binary pathology classifier, the same results hold if we define
c(\cdot)
as a K-ary classifier of any protected attribute, such as race, gender, etc.
This implies that, if we have a machine-learning system that has been calibrated to classify fairly on clean ground-truth data
\boldsymbol{x}
, then the use of posterior samples
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}
enables it to classify fairly on distorted/corrupted/incomplete measurements
\boldsymbol{y}=\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{x})
, whereas the use of generic point-estimates
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
does not.

(D.8)

(D.9)

where (

CFID implementation details

With the Gaussian approximation described in Section
4.1
, where
p_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}
and
p_{\widehat{\underline{\mathsf{x}}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}
are approximated by
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}})
and
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}})
, respectively, the CWD in (
24
) reduces to

\displaystyle\CFID

\displaystyle\triangleq\E_{\mathsf{y}}\big\{\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}\|_{2}^{2}+\tr\big[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}-2\big(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\big]\big\}.

(F.1)

The values in (
F.1
) are computed using

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{y}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}}^{-1}(\underline{\boldsymbol{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\mathsf{y}}})

(F.2)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{x}}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{y}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}}^{\top}

(F.3)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{y}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}}^{-1}(\underline{\boldsymbol{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\mathsf{y}}})

(F.4)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{y}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}}^{\top}.

(F.5)

Plugging (
F.2
)-(
F.5
) into (
F.1
), the CFID can be written as
[
22
, Lemma 2]
:

\displaystyle\CFID

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}}\|_{2}^{2}+\tr\Big[(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{y}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}})^{\top}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad+\tr\Big[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}-2\big(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Big],

(F.6)

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{y}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}}^{-1}
is typically implemented using a pseudo-inverse.

We now detail how the means and covariances in (
F.6
) are computed.
We start with a dataset
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t})\}_{t=1}^{n}
of truth/measurement pairs.
For each
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}
, we generate a set of
P
posterior samples
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{ti}\}_{i=1}^{P}
.
We merge these samples with
P
repetitions of
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}
to obtain
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{ti},\boldsymbol{y}_{ti},\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{ti})\}_{i=1}^{P}
for
t=1\dots n
.
These terms are processed by a feature-generating network to yield the feature embeddings
\{(\underline{\boldsymbol{x}}_{ti},\underline{\boldsymbol{y}}_{ti},\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{ti})\}_{i=1}^{P}
, which are then packed into matrices
\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}
,
\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}
, and
\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}}
with
Pn
rows.
We used the VGG-16 feature-generating network
[
49
]
for our MRI experiments, since
[
41
]
found that it gave results that correlated much better with radiologists’ perceptions, while we used the standard Inception-v3 network
[
50
]
for our inpainting experiments.
The embeddings are then used to compute the sample-mean values

\displaystyle\textstyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}}\triangleq\frac{1}{Pn}\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\underline{\boldsymbol{X}},\quad\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\mathsf{y}}}\triangleq\frac{1}{Pn}\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}},\quad\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}}\triangleq\frac{1}{Pn}\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}}.

(F.7)

We then subtract the sample mean from each row of
\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}
,
\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}
, and
\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}}
to give the zero-mean embedding matrices
\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\mathsf{zm}}\triangleq\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}-\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}}^{\top}
,
\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{\mathsf{zm}}\triangleq\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}-\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\mathsf{y}}}^{\top}
, and
\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{\mathsf{zm}}\triangleq\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}}-\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}}^{\top}
, which are then used to compute the sample covariance matrices

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{x}}}

\displaystyle\triangleq\textstyle\frac{1}{Pn}\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\mathsf{zm}}^{\top}\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\mathsf{zm}},\quad\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{y}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}}\triangleq\frac{1}{Pn}\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{\mathsf{zm}}^{\top}\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{\mathsf{zm}},\quad\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}}\triangleq\frac{1}{Pn}\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{\mathsf{zm}}^{\top}\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{\mathsf{zm}}

(F.8a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}}

\displaystyle\triangleq\textstyle\frac{1}{Pn}\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\mathsf{zm}}^{\top}\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{\mathsf{zm}},\quad\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\mathsf{y}}}\triangleq\frac{1}{Pn}\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{\mathsf{zm}}^{\top}\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{\mathsf{zm}}.

(F.8b)

We plug the sample statistics from (
F.7
)-(
F.8
) into (
F.2
)-(
F.5
), which yields the statistics needed to compute the CFID in (
F.6
).
In
[
22
]
, the authors use
P=1
in all of their experiments.
To be consistent with how we evaluated the other metrics, we use

unless otherwise noted.

Appendix G
MR imaging details

We now give details on magnetic resonance (MR) image recovery.
Suppose that the goal is to recover the
N
-pixel MR image
\boldsymbol{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
from the multicoil measurements
\{\boldsymbol{k}_{c}\}_{c=1}^{C}
, where
[
39
]

\boldsymbol{k}_{c}=\boldsymbol{MFS}_{c}\boldsymbol{i}+\boldsymbol{n}_{c}.

(G.1)

In (
G.1
),
C
refers to the number of coils,
\boldsymbol{k}_{c}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{M}
are the measurements from the
c
th coil,
\boldsymbol{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times N}
is a sub-sampling operator containing rows from
\boldsymbol{I}_{N}
—the
N\times N
identity matrix,
\boldsymbol{F}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N\times N}
is the unitary 2D discrete Fourier transform,
\boldsymbol{S}_{c}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N\times N}
is a diagonal matrix containing the sensitivity map of the
c
th coil, and
\boldsymbol{n}_{c}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{M}
is noise.
From (
G.1
), it can be seen that the MR measurements are collected in the spatial Fourier domain, otherwise known as the “k-space.”
The sensitivity maps
\{\boldsymbol{S}_{c}\}
are estimated from
\{\boldsymbol{k}_{c}\}
using ESPIRiT
[
40
]
(in our case via SigPy
[
51
]
), which yields maps with the property
\sum_{c=1}^{C}\boldsymbol{S}_{c}^{\textsf{H}}\boldsymbol{S}_{c}=\boldsymbol{I}_{N}
.
The ratio
R\triangleq\frac{N}{M}
is known as the acceleration rate.

There are different ways that one could apply the generative posterior sampling framework to multicoil MR image recovery.
One is to configure the generator to produce posterior samples
\hat{\boldsymbol{i}}
of the complex image
\boldsymbol{i}
. Another is to configure the generator to produce posterior samples
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
of the stack
\boldsymbol{x}\triangleq[\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{C}^{\top}]^{\top}
of “coil images”
\boldsymbol{x}_{c}\triangleq\boldsymbol{S}_{c}\boldsymbol{i}
and later coil-combining them to yield a complex image estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{i}}\triangleq[\boldsymbol{S}^{\textsf{H}}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{S}^{\textsf{H}}_{C}]\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
We take the latter approach.
Furthermore, rather than feeding our generator with k-space measurements
\boldsymbol{k}_{c}
, we choose to feed it with aliased coil images
\boldsymbol{y}_{c}\triangleq\boldsymbol{F}^{\textsf{H}}\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}\boldsymbol{k}_{c}
.
Writing (
G.1
) in terms of the coil images, we obtain

\boldsymbol{y}_{c}=\boldsymbol{F}^{\textsf{H}}\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}\boldsymbol{MFx}_{c}+\boldsymbol{w}_{c},

(G.2)

where
\boldsymbol{w}_{c}\triangleq\boldsymbol{F}^{\textsf{H}}\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}\boldsymbol{n}_{c}
.
Then we can stack
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{c}\}
and
\{\boldsymbol{w}_{c}\}
column-wise into vectors
\boldsymbol{y}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
, and set
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{I}_{C}\otimes\boldsymbol{F}^{\textsf{H}}\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}\boldsymbol{MF}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{NC\times NC}
, to obtain the formulation
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{Ax}+\boldsymbol{w}
described in Section
1
.

To train our generator, we assume to have access to paired training examples
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t})\}
, where
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
is a stack of coil images and
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}
is the corresponding stack of k-space coil measurements.
The fastMRI multicoil dataset
[
33
]
provides
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{k}_{t})\}
, from which we can easily obtain
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t})\}
.

Appendix H
Data-consistency

In this section, we describe a data-consistency procedure that can be optionally used when our cGAN is used to solve a
linear
inverse problem, i.e., to recover
\boldsymbol{x}
from
\boldsymbol{y}
under a model of the form

\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{Ax}+\boldsymbol{w},

(H.1)

where
\boldsymbol{A}
is a known linear operator and
\boldsymbol{w}
is unknown noise.
The motivation is that, in some applications, such as medical imaging or inpainting, the end user may feel comfortable knowing that the generated samples
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}
are consistent with the measurements
\boldsymbol{y}
in that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{A}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}.

(H.2)

When (
H.2
) holds,
\boldsymbol{A}^{+}\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{A}^{+}\boldsymbol{A}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
must also hold, where
(\cdot)^{+}
denotes the pseudo-inverse.
The quantity
\boldsymbol{A}^{+}\boldsymbol{A}
can be recognized as the orthogonal projection matrix associated with the row space of
\boldsymbol{A}
.
So, (
H.2
) requires the component of
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
in the row space of
\boldsymbol{A}
to equal
\boldsymbol{A}^{+}\boldsymbol{y}
, while placing no constraints on the component of
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
in the nullspace of
\boldsymbol{A}
.
This suggests the following data-consistency procedure:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{A}^{+}\boldsymbol{A})\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{\mathsf{raw}}+\boldsymbol{A}^{+}\boldsymbol{y}.

(H.3)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{\mathsf{raw}}
is the raw generator output.
We note that a version of this idea for point estimation was proposed in
[
3
]
.

The data-consistency procedure (
H.3
) ensures that any generative method will generate only the component of
\boldsymbol{x}
that lies in the nullspace of
\boldsymbol{A}
.
Consequently, (
H.3
) is admissible only when
\boldsymbol{A}
has a non-trivial nullspace.
Also, because no attempt is made to remove the noise
\boldsymbol{w}
in
\boldsymbol{y}
, this approach is recommended only for low-noise applications.
For high-noise applications, an extension based on the dual-decomposition approach
[
52
]
would be more appropriate, but we leave this to future work.

When applying (
H.3
) to the MRI formulation in Appendix
G
, we note that
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{I}_{C}\otimes\boldsymbol{F}^{\textsf{H}}\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}\boldsymbol{MF}
is an orthogonal projection matrix, and so
\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{A}^{+}\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{I}\otimes\boldsymbol{F}^{\textsf{H}}(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M})\boldsymbol{F}
.

For our MRI experiments, we take inspiration from the UNet architecture from
[
36
]
, using it as the basis for the cGAN generators.
The primary input
\boldsymbol{y}
is concatenated with the code vector
\boldsymbol{z}
and fed through the UNet.
The network consists of 4 pooling layers with 128 initial channels.
However, instead of pooling, we opt to use convolutions with kernels of size
3\times 3
, “same” padding, and a stride of 2 when downsampling.
Likewise, we upsample using transpose convolutions, again with kernels of size
3\times 3
, “same” padding, and a stride of 2.
All other convolutions utilize kernels of size
3\times 3
, “same” padding, and a stride of 1.

Within each encoder and decoder layer we include a residual block, the architecture of which can be found in
[
8
]
.
We use instance-norm for all normalization layers and parametric ReLUs as our activation functions, in which the network learns the optimal “negative slope.”
Finally, we include 5 residual blocks at the base of the UNet, in between the encoder and decoder. This is done in an effort to artificially increase the depth of the network and is inspired by
[
54
]
.
Our generator has
86\,734\,334
trainable parameters.

I.1.3
cGAN discriminator architecture

Our discriminator is a standard CNN with 6 layers and 1 fully-connected layer.
In the first 3 layers, we use convolutions with kernels of size
3\times 3
, “same” padding. We reduce spatial resolution with average pooling, using
2\times 2
kernels with a stride of 2.
We use batch-norm as our normalization layer and leaky ReLUs with a “negative-slope” of 0.2 as our activation functions. The network outputs an estimated Wasserstein score for the whole image.

I.1.4
E2E-VarNet

For the Sriram et al.’s E2E-VarNet
[
37
]
, we use the same training procedure and hyperparameters outlined in
[
19
]
other than replacing the sampling pattern with the GRO undersampling mask.
As in
[
19
]
, we use the SENSE-based coil-combined image as the ground truth instead of the RSS image.

I.1.5
Langevin approach

For Jalal et al.’s MRI approach
[
19
]
, we do not modify the original implementation from
[
38
]
other than replacing the default sampling pattern with the GRO undersampling mask.
We generated 32 samples for 72 different test images using a batch-size of 4, which took roughly 6 days.
These samples were generated on a server with 4 NVIDIA V100 GPUs, each with 32 GB of memory.
We used 4 samples per batch (and recorded the time to generate 4 samples in Table
1
) because the code from
[
38
]
is written to generate one sample per GPU.

I.2
Inpainting

I.2.1
Our cGAN

For our generator and discriminator, we use the CoModGAN networks from
[
9
]
.
Unlike CoModGAN, however, we train our cGAN with
\mathcal{L}_{1,\mathsf{SD},P_{\mathsf{train}}}

We use the same general training and testing procedure described in Section
4.2
, but with
\beta_{\mathsf{adv}}=
5e-3,
n_{\mathsf{batch}}=100

Running PyTorch on a server with 4 Tesla A100 GPUs, each with 82 GB of memory, the training takes approximately 2 days.

We use the PyTorch implementation of CoModGAN from
[

]
and train the model
to inpaint a
128\times 128
centered square on
256\times 256
CelebA-HQ images.
The total training time on a server with 4 NVIDIA A100 GPUs, each with 82 GB of memory, is roughly 2 days.

]

]
.

1\,000

The total generation time on a server with 4 NVIDIA A100 GPUs, each with 82 GB of memory, is roughly 9 days.

Appendix J
Additional experimental results

J.1
CFID decomposition into mean and covariance components

In this section, we investigate the small-sample bias effects of CFID, which have been previously noted in
[
22
]
.
To do this, we write the CFID from (
F.1
) as a sum of two terms: a term that quantifies the conditional-mean error and a term that quantifies the conditional-covariance error:

\displaystyle\CFID

\displaystyle=\CFID_{\mathsf{mean}}+\CFID_{\mathsf{cov}}

(J.1)

\displaystyle\CFID_{\mathsf{mean}}

\displaystyle\triangleq\E_{\mathsf{y}}\{\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}\|_{2}^{2}\}

(J.2)

\displaystyle\CFID_{\mathsf{cov}}

\displaystyle\triangleq\tr\big[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}-2\big(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\big].

(J.3)

To verify that (
J.3
) quantifies the error in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}
, notice that (
J.3
) equals zero when
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}\underline{\widehat{\mathsf{x}}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\underline{\mathsf{x}}\underline{\mathsf{x}}|\underline{\mathsf{y}}}
and is otherwise positive (by Cauchy Schwarz).

[

,
20
]
, we did not have the computational resources to evaluate them on all datasets, and that’s why certain table entries are blank.

For both MRI experiments, Table
J.1
shows our method outperforming the competing methods in both the mean and covariance components of CFID (and thus the total CFID) for all sample sizes.

J.1
shows our method outperforming CoModGAN in both the mean and covariance components (and thus the total CFID) for all sample sizes.

J.1
shows our method outperforming the score-based approach in total CFID on the 3000- and 30 000-sample test sets but not on the 1000-sample test set.
However, we now argue that the 1000-sample inpainting experiment is heavily affected by small-sample bias, and therefore untrustworthy.
Looking at the mean component of CFID (i.e.,
\CFID_{\mathsf{mean}}^{1}
,
\CFID_{\mathsf{mean}}^{2}
, and
\CFID_{\mathsf{mean}}^{3}
) across the inpainting experiments, we see that the values are relatively small and stable with sample size.
But looking at the covariance component of CFID (i.e.,
\CFID_{\mathsf{cov}}^{1}
,
\CFID_{\mathsf{cov}}^{2}
, and
\CFID_{\mathsf{cov}}^{3}
) across the inpainting experiments, we see that the values are large and heavily dependent on sample size.
For the 1000-sample inpainting experiment, the total CFID is dominated by the covariance component and thus strongly affected by small-sample bias.
Consequently, for the 1000-sample inpainting experiment, the total CFID is not trustworthy.
