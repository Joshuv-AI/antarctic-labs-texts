Large Deviations of Piecewise-Deterministic-Markov-Processes with Application to Stochastic Calcium Waves
Gaetan Barbet, James MacLaurin, Moshe Silverstein

Abstract
We prove a Large Deviation Principle for Piecewise Deterministic Markov Processes (PDMPs). This is an asymptotic estimate for the probability of a trajectory in the large size limit. Explicit Euler-Lagrange equations are determined for computing optimal first-hitting-time trajectories. The results are applied to a model of stochastic calcium dynamics. It is widely conjectured that the mechanism of calcium puff generation is a multiscale process: with microscopic stochastic fluctuations in the opening and closing of individual channels generating cell-wide waves via the diffusion of calcium and other signaling molecules. We model this system as a PDMP, with N ≫ 1 N\gg 1 stochastic calcium channels that are coupled via the ambient calcium concentration. We employ the Large Deviations theory to estimate the probability of cell-wide calcium waves being produced through microscopic stochasticity.

Overview of Stochastic Models of Calcium Signaling

Classical models of calcium signalling are almost entirely deterministic
[
30
]
. These models typically assumed that the calcium concentration (and signalling molecules such as IP3) within the cell can be well-approximated as homogeneous, and therefore the dynamics can be accurately described by ordinary differential equations for the evolution of their concentrations in time
[
19
]
. However recent experimental evidence / mathematical analysis has called this into question
[
32
,
30
,
50
,
29
,
41
,
48
,
44
,
45
]
. It has been demonstrated that (i) the interspike interval can show significant variability, and (ii) intracellular calcium concentrations show steep gradients, and therefore it is widely postulated that much of the emergent phenomena are stochastic in nature
[
44
,
45
]
.

One of our fundamental aims is to develop an accurate microscopic model of calcium signalling (which is thoroughly stochastic) and then use statistical mechanical techniques to determine effective macroscopic equations. Calcium signaling is organized in a hierarchical manner
[
15
,
19
,
23
]
. At the lowest (most microscopic) level, stochastic release of
Ca^{2+}
upon binding of IP3 receptor results in a small localized increase in cytoplasmic
Ca^{2+}
concentration, which is often termed a ‘blip’. The IP3 receptors are typically tightly clustered, which means that a
Ca^{2+}
blip can stimulate the release of additional
Ca^{2+}
through neighboring receptors, so that the entire cluster emits a localized ‘puff’. At the highest level of organization, if enough puffs are generated, they can form a propagating wave of increased
Ca^{2+}
across an entire cell. There is considerable evidence that calcium puffs and waves are nonlinear stochastic phenomena: it has been observed for instance that IP3 channels can be highly active even when the average open probability is less than half its maximum
[
49
]
. The very presence of abortive calcium waves is indicative of the stochastic origins of the phenomena. Indeed clusters have a diameter of
60-100
nanometers whereas the distance between clusters is much larger:
3-7
micrometers
[
19
]
.

There is a well-established literature that examines how spatially-distributed calcium waves and patterns can arise out of microscopic stochastic models. Some of the earliest work is in the Fire-Diffuse-Fire model of Keizer and Smith
[
33
,
32
]
. In this model, Ryanodine Receptors open (and release calcium) once the ambient calcium exceeds a threshold; before closing and entering a refractory state before being ready to open again. Keizer and Smith were able to demonstrate that this model exhibits spatially-distributed waves. Coombes, Hinch and Timofeeva
[
17
,
16
]
developed a similar model. They estimated the release probability for a cluster of channels and determined that it is approximately a sigmoidal function of the local calcium concentration. Keener
[
30
]
further extended these works by taking the opening and closing of individual channels to be stochastic. An advantage of all of the previous works is that the models are all spatially extended, unlike this paper. However the model in this paper fully couples the calcium concentration to the opening / closing of the channel. It thus allows a more detailed estimation of how the feedback between the opening / closing of channels and the local calcium concentration leads to calcium waves.

There have already been some efforts towards a detailed derivation of effective macroscopic equations from a microscopic model.
Hinch and Chapman
[
28
]
used exponential asymptotic methods were employed to determine the relative frequency of calcium sparks. Falcke
et al
employed approximations to determine estimates for the approximate probability of calcium puffs (given the frequency of blips), and also estimates for the probability of a wave throughout the cell
[
41
,
19
]
.

This paper is structured as follows. In Section I, we introduce the model and outline our main results. In Section II, we determine the Euler-Lagrange equations that follow from the Large Deviation Principle. These can be used to estimate optimal trajectories for first-hitting-time problems. In Section III we apply the theory to estimate the probability of the spark-to-wave transition in calcium dynamics. Finally in Section IV, we outline the proofs.

3
Application to Calcium Signalling

We apply this theory to determine estimates for the typical time it takes for cells with stochastically opening and closing calcium channels to change from most of the channels being closed to most being open
[
19
]
. There are thus two components to the model: the opening and closing channels, and the calcium concentration (that evolves deterministically between the opening and closing of the channels). The model is simplified to be such that the calcium concentration throughout the cell is spatially homogeneous.

To begin with, we must outline a simple microscopic model of stochastic calcium effects. Following for instance
[
17
,
30
,
44
,
45
]
, we employ a hybrid model: with the calcium diffusion modeled deterministically, the IP3 concentration constant throughout the cell, and the channel opening and closing modeled stochastically.

Our basic aim is to determine the probability of calcium puffs and waves by studying a similar stochastic model to
[
17
,
30
,
44
,
45
]
. These papers performed detailed numerical simulations, and analytically estimated the probabilities. To calculate the probability of a calcium puff in a cluster, the calcium and IP3 concentration throughout the cluster is assumed to be homogeneous. However there is a feedback effect of the opening / closing of the channels: when these bind or unbind the calcium / IP3, they alter the local concentration. Thus the opening and closing of channels within a cluster is not independent because the channels communicate with each other via the calcium / IP3 concentrations.

. Write

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{\alpha}(x(s),u(s))ds

(118)

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{\alpha}(\hat{x}(s),\hat{u}(s))ds

(119)

\displaystyle w_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=z_{\alpha}\big(\Lambda^{-1}_{\alpha}(t)\big)

(120)

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{z}_{\alpha}\big(\hat{\Lambda}^{-1}_{\alpha}(t)\big).

(121)

It is noted in Lemma that there is a constant
C_{t}>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}}\Lambda_{\alpha}(t)\leq C_{t}

(122)

We are going to demonstrate that for any
t\geq 0
, there exists a constant
c_{\epsilon,t}
such that for all
s\leq t
,

\displaystyle\tilde{d}^{\circ}_{s}(z_{\alpha},\hat{z}_{\alpha})\leq\tilde{d}^{\circ}_{C_{s}}(w_{\alpha},\hat{w}_{\alpha})+c_{\epsilon,t}\tilde{d}^{\circ}_{s}(z_{\alpha},\hat{z}_{\alpha}).

(123)

An application of Gronwall’s Inequality to (
123
) implies that if
w_{\alpha}=\hat{w}_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in\mathscr{R}
, then necessarily
z_{\alpha}=\hat{z}_{\alpha}
. This implies the Lemma.

With an aim of proving (
123
), it follows from Calculus that

\displaystyle\Lambda^{-1}_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda^{-1}_{\alpha}(x(s),u(s))ds

(124)

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}^{-1}_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda^{-1}_{\alpha}(\hat{x}(s),\hat{u}(s))ds.

(125)

Our assumptions on the set
\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}
imply that

\displaystyle\Lambda^{-1}_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle\leq t\epsilon^{-1}

(126)

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}^{-1}_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle\leq t\epsilon^{-1}.

(127)

Define

\displaystyle\tilde{z}_{\alpha}(t)=w_{\alpha}\big(\hat{\Lambda}(t)\big).

(128)

The triangle inequality implies that

\displaystyle\tilde{d}^{\circ}_{t}(z_{\alpha},\hat{z}_{\alpha})\leq\tilde{d}^{\circ}_{t}(z_{\alpha},\tilde{z}_{\alpha})+\tilde{d}^{\circ}_{t}(\tilde{z}_{\alpha},\hat{z}_{\alpha}).

(129)

The definition of the Skorohod metric implies that

\displaystyle\tilde{d}^{\circ}_{t}(z_{\alpha},\tilde{z}_{\alpha})\leq\sup_{s\leq t}\bigg|\int_{0}^{s}\lambda_{\alpha}(x(r),u(r))/\lambda_{\alpha}(\hat{x}(r),\hat{u}(r))dr-s\bigg|.

(130)

Our assumption that the reaction rates and functions are Lipschitz, together with Lemma
1.2
implies that there is a constant
\bar{C}_{\epsilon,t}>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\sup_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}}\big|\lambda_{\alpha}(x(s),u(s))-\lambda_{\alpha}(\hat{x}(s),\hat{u}(s))\big|\leq

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{\epsilon,t}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\sup_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}}\big|z_{\alpha}(s)-\hat{z}_{\alpha}(s)\big|

(131)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{\epsilon,t}\sup_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}}\tilde{d}^{\circ}_{t}(z_{\alpha},\hat{z}_{\alpha}).

(132)

Since the reaction rates are (by assumption in the statement of the Lemma), bounded from below, we thus obtain that there is a constant
\grave{C}_{\epsilon,t}
such that for all
s\leq\bar{t}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}}\tilde{d}^{\circ}_{s}(z_{\alpha},\tilde{z}_{\alpha})\leq\grave{C}_{\epsilon,\bar{t}}\sup_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}}\tilde{d}^{\circ}_{s}(z_{\alpha},\hat{z}_{\alpha}).

(133)

The definition of the Skorohod Metric, together with the bounds in (
126
) also implies that

\displaystyle\tilde{d}^{\circ}_{t}(\tilde{z}_{\alpha},\hat{z}_{\alpha})\leq\tilde{d}^{\circ}_{C_{t}}(w_{\alpha},\hat{w}_{\alpha}).

(134)

We have thus proved (
123
).
∎

The following exponential tightness result is a standard requirement for Large Deviation Principles.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any
L>0
, there exists a compact set
\mathcal{K}_{L}\subset\Upsilon
such that

\displaystyle\underset{N\to\infty}{\overline{\lim}}N^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}\big((z,x,u)\notin\mathcal{K}_{L}\big)\leq-L.

(135)

Proof.

We can equivalently formulate the system in terms of an empirical measure, and the exponential tightness is an immediate consequence. In more detail, write

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}^{N}=N^{-1}\sum_{j\in I_{N}}\delta_{w^{j}}\in\mathcal{P}\big(\mathcal{D}\big([0,T],\mathbb{Z}^{+}\big)^{|\mathscr{R}|}\big).

(136)

Here
w^{j}:=\big(w^{j}_{\alpha}\big)_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}}\subset\mathcal{D}\big([0,T],\mathbb{Z}^{+}\big)^{|\mathscr{R}|}
are inhomogeneous counting processes, i.e. they are such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(w^{j}_{\alpha}(t+\Delta)=w^{j}_{\alpha}(t)+1\;|\;\mathcal{F}_{t}\big)

\displaystyle\simeq\Delta\lambda_{\alpha}(x(t),u(t))+O(\Delta^{2})

(137)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(w^{j}_{\alpha}(t+\Delta)=w^{j}_{\alpha}(t)\;|\;\mathcal{F}_{t}\big)

\displaystyle\simeq 1-\Delta\lambda_{\alpha}(x(t),u(t))+O(\Delta^{2}).

(138)

Here

\displaystyle x(t)=

\displaystyle x(0)+N^{-1}\sum_{j\in I_{N}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}}\xi_{\alpha}w^{j}_{\alpha}(t)

(139)

\displaystyle\frac{du}{dt}=

\displaystyle A(u(t),x(t))

(140)

We then find that, substituting
z_{\alpha}(t)=N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}w^{j}_{\alpha}(t)
, the above system has the same probability law as the original system. Write
\mathcal{D}_{*}\big([0,T],\mathbb{Z}^{+}\big)^{|\mathscr{R}|}\big)\subseteq\mathcal{D}\big([0,T],\mathbb{Z}^{+}\big)^{|\mathscr{R}|}
to consist of all processes that are (i) equal to
0
at time
0
, (ii) non-decreasing.

More precisely, we see that there exists a continuous mapping
\Psi:\mathcal{P}\big(\mathcal{D}_{*}\big([0,T],\mathbb{Z}^{+}\big)^{|\mathscr{R}|}\big)
such that
(z,x,u)=\Psi\big(\hat{\mu}^{N}\big)
, with unit probability. For a positive number
L>0
, we are going to define a compact set
\mathcal{K}_{L}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\big(\mathcal{D}_{*}\big([0,T],\mathbb{Z}^{+}\big)^{|\mathscr{R}|}\big)
such that

\displaystyle\underset{N\to\infty}{\overline{\lim}}N^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{\mu}^{N}\notin\mathcal{K}_{L}\big)\leq-L.

(141)

This suffices for the lemma because the continuity of
\Psi
then implies that
\Psi(\mathcal{K}_{L})
is compact.

For a positive integer
p\geq 1
, write
\mathcal{U}_{p,T}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{*}\big([0,T],\mathbb{Z}^{+}\big)^{|\mathscr{R}|}
to consist of all paths that are less than or equal to
p
at time
T
. Its easy to check that
\mathcal{U}_{p}
is compact with respect to the Skorohod Topology (there are at most
p
‘spike times’, and these spike times must be in the compact time interval
[0,T]
). Write
Y^{p,j}_{\alpha}
to be independent counting processes of unit intensity. Thus the time-rescaled representation of Poisson Processes
[
3
]
means that we can write

\displaystyle w^{j}_{\alpha}(t)=Y^{j}_{\alpha}\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{\alpha}(x(s),u(s))ds\bigg)

(142)

For another number
b_{p}>0
, we find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{\mu}^{N}(\mathbf{w})(\mathcal{U}_{p,T})<1-b_{p}\big)\leq\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{\mu}^{N}(\mathbf{Y})\big(\mathcal{U}_{p,KT}\big)<1-b_{p}\big).

(143)

since
\lambda\leq K
uniformly. Thanks to Chernoff’s Inequality, for a constant
c>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{\mu}^{N}(\mathbf{Y})\big(\mathcal{U}_{p,KT}\big)<1-b_{p}\big)\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}}N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\chi\big\{w^{j}_{\alpha}(KT)>p\big\}\geq b_{p}\bigg)

(144)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big|\mathscr{R}\big|\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(c\sum_{j=1}^{N}\chi\big\{w^{j}_{\alpha}(KT)>p\big\}-Ncb_{p}\bigg)\bigg]

(145)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big|\mathscr{R}\big|\bigg\{1+\mathbb{P}\big(w^{j}_{\alpha}(KT)>p\big)\big(\exp(c)-1\big)\bigg\}^{N}\exp\big(-Ncb_{p}\big)

(146)

Through taking
p
large enough, and
1\ll c\ll-\log\mathbb{P}\big(w^{j}_{\alpha}(KT)>p\big)
, we find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{\mu}^{N}(\mathbf{w})(\mathcal{U}_{p,T})<1-b_{p}\big)\leq\exp\big(-pN\big).

(147)

Now define
\mathcal{K}_{L}
to consist of all measures
\mu
such that for all
p\geq p_{L}
(for an integer
p_{L}
to be specified below),

\displaystyle\mu(\mathcal{U}_{p,KT})\geq b_{p}.

(148)

We thus find through a union of events bound that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\hat{\mu}^{N}\notin\mathcal{K}_{L}\bigg)\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{p=p_{L}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigg(\hat{\mu}^{N}(\mathbf{Y})\big(\mathcal{U}_{p,KT}\big)<1-b_{p}\bigg)

(149)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{p=p_{L}}^{\infty}\exp\big(-pN\big).

(150)

Thus for large enough
p_{L}
, it must be that for all
N\geq 1
,

\displaystyle N^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}\bigg(\hat{\mu}^{N}\notin\mathcal{K}_{L}\bigg)\leq-L.

(151)

∎

We finally note the proof of Lemma
1.2
.

Proof.

It is clear from the definition of
x
that there is a universal constant such that

\left\|x(s)-\tilde{x}(s)\right\|\leq C\sup_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}}|z_{\alpha}(s)-\tilde{z}_{\alpha}(s)|.

(152)

It is demonstrated in Billingsley
[
6
]
that convergence in the Skorohod metric implies convergence in the supremum norm, i.e as
d^{\circ}_{\infty}(z,\tilde{z})\to 0
, it must be that

\sup_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\big|z_{\alpha}(s)-\tilde{z}_{\alpha}(s)\big|\to 0.

(153)

The Lemma now follows from a standard application of Gronwall’s Inequality.
∎
