Abstract
. New symmetries, norm computations and spectral information are obtained for the Leray transform on a class of unbounded hypersurfaces in ℂ 2 {\mathbb{C}}^{2} . Emphasis is placed on certain distinguished measures, with results on operator norm monotonicity established by proving new polygamma inequalities. Classical techniques of Bernstein-Widder and Euler-Maclaurin play crucial roles in our analysis. Underpinning this work is a projective geometric theory of duality, which manifests here in the form of Hölder invariance.



1.
Introduction

The Leray (or Cauchy-Leray) transform
\bm{L}
is a higher dimensional analogue of the familiar Cauchy transform on planar domains.
Let
\Omega
be a convex domain in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
with
C^{2}
boundary
b\Omega:={\mathcal{S}}
and defining function
\rho
.
Given a function
f
defined on
{\mathcal{S}}
, its Leray transform
\bm{L}f
is the holomorphic function defined for
z\in\Omega
by the following formula:

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\bm{L}f(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\zeta\in{\mathcal{S}}}f(\zeta){\mathscr{L}}(z,\zeta),

(1.1b)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}(z,\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{n}}\frac{\partial\rho(\zeta)\wedge(\overline{\partial}\partial\rho(\zeta))^{n-1}}{\langle\partial\rho(\zeta),\zeta-z\rangle^{n}}.

The
(n,n-1)
-form
{\mathscr{L}}
is called the
Leray kernel
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the ordinary
bilinear
pairing of
(1,0)
-forms and vectors.
The operator
\bm{L}
reproduces holomorphic functions from their boundary values and generates holomorphic functions from more general boundary data.
It is straightforward to check that formula (
1.1a
) is independent of the choice of defining function
\rho
used in (
1.1b
); see
[
23
, Section IV.3.2]
.
Additional background information on this operator is given in Section
2
.

A body of recent work
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
has investigated the mapping and invariance properties of
\bm{L}
in a variety of settings, with the construction of interesting holomorphic function spaces seen as an important application.
For instance, Barrett and Edholm
[
7
]
studied
\bm{L}
on the family of unbounded hypersurfaces

(1.2)

M_{\gamma}=\{(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}:\text{Im}{(\zeta_{2})}=|\zeta_{1}|^{\gamma}\},\qquad\gamma>1,

and used their findings to construct dual pairs of projectively invariant Hardy spaces.
In the present work, further analysis of the Leray transform on
M_{\gamma}
is carried out and detailed analytic information is obtained.

Throughout the paper, we always assume
\gamma>1
.

1.1.
Distinguished measures

Let us parameterize
M_{\gamma}
by
(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})=(re^{i\theta},s+ir^{\gamma})
, where
r\geq 0,\,\,s\in{\mathbb{R}},\,\,\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, and consider measures of the form

\mu_{d}=r^{d}\,dr\wedge d\theta\wedge ds,\qquad d\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Two measures are of particular importance to higher-dimensional Cauchy-Leray theory:
the
pairing measure

\sigma
and the
preferred measure

\nu
; see
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
.
On
M_{\gamma}
, they take the form

(1.3)

\displaystyle\sigma=r^{\gamma-1}\,dr\wedge d\theta\wedge ds,\qquad(d=\gamma-1),

(1.4)

\displaystyle\nu=r^{\frac{\gamma+1}{3}}\,dr\wedge d\theta\wedge ds,\qquad\big(d=\tfrac{\gamma+1}{3}\big).

The pairing measure
\sigma
arises as (a constant multiple of) the Leray-Levi measure corresponding to the natural defining function
\rho(\zeta)=|\zeta_{1}|^{\gamma}-\text{Im}(\zeta_{2})
; see (
2.1b
).
This measure also appears in an alternative description of the Leray transform, facilitating a natural bilinear pairing between projectively invariant dual Hardy spaces; see
[
7
, Proposition 6.18]
.
The preferred measure
\nu
admits a projective transformation law; see (
2.2
).
This leads to a corresponding Leray transformation law in (
2.4
), and to the definition of the aforementioned Hardy spaces in (
2.5
).
These notions were introduced by Barrett
[
5
, Sections 7 and 8]
and developed further in
[
6
,
7
]
.
See
[
7
, Section 2]
for a detailed discussion in the
M_{\gamma}
setting.

Remark 1.5
.

Note that in the Heisenberg case (
\gamma=2
), the pairing and preferred measures are equal and coincide with ordinary Lebesgue measure
\mu_{1}
on the parameter space
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.
In fact, the Leray transform and the (orthogonal) Szegő projection coincide on
L^{2}(M_{2},\mu_{1})
.

\lozenge

In the present article, we show that the behavior of
\bm{L}
on
M_{\gamma}
can be understood rather explicitly.
In particular, its exact norm is computed in a number of settings:

Theorem 1.6
.

The norm of
\bm{L}
in
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
is determined for many
\gamma
and
d
, including:

(a)

The norm of the Leray transform on
L^{2}(M_{2},\mu_{d})
is given by

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{2},\mu_{d})}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}(1-d)\sec{\big(\frac{d\pi}{2}\big)}},&d\in(-1,1)\cup(1,3)\\
1,&d=1.\end{cases}

(b)

The norm of the Leray transform on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{1})
is given by

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{1})}=\begin{cases}(\gamma-1)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}-1}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}(\gamma-2)\gamma\csc\big(\frac{2\pi}{\gamma}\big)},&\gamma\in(1,2)\cup(2,\infty)\\
1,&\gamma=2.\end{cases}

(c)

The norm of the Leray transform on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
is given by

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)}=\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}}.

(d)

The norm of the Leray transform on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)
is given by

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}}}.

While the proofs of the parts of Theorem
1.6
employ different techniques that appear throughout the paper, they all depend critically on analysis of the Leray symbol function
J(d,\gamma,k)
defined in (
1.7
) below.
The proof of part
(a)
is found in Corollary
3.16
.
Parts
(b)
and
(c)
are found in Section
4
, where the Bernstein-Widder theorem and the notion of complete montonicity play important roles; see Corollaries
4.25
and
4.27
, which themselves are “endpoint cases” of the more general Theorem
4.23
.
The preferred measure result, part
(d)
, is the most difficult to prove.
Starting in Section
5
, we use the Euler-Maclaurin summation formula to carry out lengthy analysis, eventually transforming the problem into concrete questions about the positivity of certain explicit polynomials.
The calculation of the norm of
\bm{L}
with respect to the preferred measure
\nu
concretely answers an open question on a natural pairing of projective dual Hardy spaces on
M_{\gamma}
; see Proposition
2.6
.

To contextualize our results and methods, we provide a short summary of previously known facts about the Leray transform acting on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
.

1.2.
The Leray symbol function

In
[
7
]
, the rotational symmetry in
\zeta_{1}
yields an orthogonal decomposition of
\bm{L}
on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
into
sub-Leray operators

\bm{L}_{k}
; see Section
2.3
:

\bm{L}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}\bm{L}_{k}.

We recall for non-negative
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, the
Leray symbol function
of
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
:

(1.7)

J(d,\gamma,k)=\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2k+1+d}{\gamma}\big)\Gamma\big(2k+2-\frac{2k+1+d}{\gamma}\big)}{\Gamma(k+1)^{2}}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)^{2k+2}(\gamma-1)^{-\left(2k+2-\frac{2k+1+d}{\gamma}\right)}.

Proposition 1.8

(Barrett-Edholm
[
7
]
)
.

\bm{L}_{k}
is bounded on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
if and only if

(1.9)

d\in(-2k-1\,,\,(2k+2)(\gamma-1)+1):={\mathcal{I}}_{k}(\gamma).

When
d
is taken in this interval,

(1.10)

\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}=\sqrt{J(d,\gamma,k)}.

The above intervals are nested, i.e.,
{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(\gamma)\subset{\mathcal{I}}_{k+1}(\gamma)
, and they exhaust the real line as
k\to\infty
.
Thus, for any given
d
, at most a finite number of
\bm{L}_{k}
are unbounded on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
.
Remarkably, the symbol function (and therefore the norm of the
\bm{L}_{k}
) stabilizes to the same value as
k\to\infty
, independent of the choice of
d
:

Proposition 1.11

(Barrett-Edholm
[
7
]
)
.

For any
d\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the following limit holds

\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}}}.

In Figure
1
below, the stabilization of the symbol function can be seen for several different
d
values in the case
\gamma=5
.
There is an intriguing relationship between the limit in Proposition
1.11
and a projective geometric invariant on
M_{\gamma}
.
This is related to the Leray essential norm conjecture; see
[
7
, Section 2.3]
.

The range of
d
for which the full operator
\bm{L}
is bounded on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
can now be deduced from the previous two propositions:

Corollary 1.12

(Barrett-Edholm
[
7
]
)
.

The Leray transform
\bm{L}
is a bounded operator from
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
to itself if and only if
d\in(-1,2\gamma-1)={\mathcal{I}}_{0}(\gamma)
.

Remark 1.13
.

Due to the prominence of measures
\sigma
and
\nu
, we use alternate notation when the symbol function corresponds to
d=\gamma-1
and
d=\tfrac{\gamma+1}{3}
, as indicated by (
1.3
) and (
1.4
):

(1.14a)

\displaystyle C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)

\displaystyle:=J(\gamma-1,\gamma,k),

(1.14b)

\displaystyle C_{\nu}(\gamma,k)

\displaystyle:=J\big(\tfrac{\gamma+1}{3},\gamma,k\big).

We call
C_{\sigma}
and
C_{\nu}
the
pairing symbol
and
preferred symbol
functions, respectively.

1.3.
Symbol function monotonicity and symmetries

The results in Section
1.2
show that
\bm{L}
is bounded on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
if and only if the sub-Leray operator
\bm{L}_{0}
is bounded there.
But the norm of the full Leray transform is determined by maximizing the function

k\mapsto\sqrt{J(d,\gamma,k)}=\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}

over the positive integers, which is often very difficult.
One way to approach this maximization problem is to investigate when the symbol function is monotone.

In
[
7
]
it is shown that for
\gamma\neq 2
, the pairing symbol function
k\mapsto C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)
(corresponding to
d=\gamma-1
) is strictly decreasing.
But this is not necessarily true for other values of
d
, as is seen in Figure
1
, which illustrates how the behavior of the symbol function can change as
d
varies within the boundedness interval
{\mathcal{I}}_{0}(\gamma)=(-1,2\gamma-1)
.

Figure 1.
Behavior of
k\mapsto J(d,5,k)
for certain
1\leq d\leq 4
.

While symbol function monotonicity fails to hold in general, for many choices of
d
and
\gamma
(including the cases that we are most interested in), it does hold:

Theorem 1.15
.

The sub-Leray operators
\bm{L}_{k}
exhibit the following monotone behavior.

(a)

If
\gamma=2
and
d\in(-1,1)\,\cup\,(1,3)
, the function
k\mapsto J(d,\gamma,k)
is strictly decreasing on the non-negative integers.
Thus

\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{2},\mu_{d})}>\left\|\bm{L}_{k+1}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{2},\mu_{d})}.

(b)

If
\gamma>2
and
d\in(-1,1]\cup[\gamma-1,2\gamma-1)
, the function
k\mapsto J(d,\gamma,k)
is strictly decreasing on the non-negative integers.
Thus

\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}>\left\|\bm{L}_{k+1}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}.

(c)

If
\gamma<2
and
d\in(-1,\gamma-1]\cup[1,2\gamma-1)
, the function
k\mapsto J(d,\gamma,k)
is strictly decreasing on the non-negative integers.
Thus

\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}>\left\|\bm{L}_{k+1}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}.

(d)

Let
\gamma\neq 2
.
The preferred symbol function
k\mapsto C_{\nu}(\gamma,k)=J\big(\frac{\gamma+1}{3},\gamma,k\big)
is strictly increasing on the non-negative integers.
Thus,

\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)}<\left\|\bm{L}_{k+1}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)}.

The monotonicity result in part
(a)
is shown in Theorem
3.13
, parts
(b)
and
(c)
are shown in Theorem
4.23
, and part
(d)
in Theorem
5.22
.
The proofs here depend on careful analysis of certain combinations of polygamma functions.
This is previewed in Section
1.4
below.

Remark 1.16
.

The norm and monotonicity results in Theorems
1.6
and
1.15
have immediate implications for the spectra of the related self-adjoint operators
\bm{L}^{*}\bm{L}
,
\bm{L}\bm{L}^{*}
and the anti self-adjoint
\bm{L}^{*}-\bm{L}
; see
[
7
, Section 5.3]
.

1.3.1.
Hölder symmetry of the symbol

In what follows,
\gamma^{*}=\tfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}
denotes the Hölder conjugate of
\gamma
.
In
[
7
]
the pairing symbol function is shown to be Hölder symmetric, i.e.,

C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)=C_{\sigma}(\gamma^{*},k).

This observation meshes well with our understanding of Cauchy-Leray theory from a projective dual point of view, as
M_{\gamma^{*}}
is known to be the projective dual hypersurface of
M_{\gamma}
; see
[
7
, Section 6.1]
.
But it was never clarified in the previous work whether a more general version of this correspondence holds when other measures are considered, and in particular, if the preferred symbol function
C_{\nu}(\gamma,k)
is Hölder invariant.

We prove here that a form of Hölder invariance holds for every choice of
\gamma\neq 2
,
d\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Indeed, given a pair
(\gamma,d)
, we show that there is a unique
d^{\prime}
such that the behavior of
\bm{L}_{k}
on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
parallels its behavior in
L^{2}(M_{\gamma^{*}},\mu_{d^{\prime}})
.
To understand this symmetry and the value of
d^{\prime}
more explicitly, we re-parameterize the exponent of
\mu_{d}
as follows.
Given
d\in{\mathbb{R}}
(since
\gamma\neq 2
), choose the unique
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying

(1.17)

d=a(\gamma-2)+1:=\delta_{a}(\gamma).

Theorem 1.18
.

The symbol function
J(\delta_{a}(\gamma),\gamma,k)
is Hölder symmetric, in that

J(\delta_{a}(\gamma),\gamma,k)=J(\delta_{a}(\gamma^{*}),\gamma^{*},k).

This theorem is proved in Section
3.1
.
Taking
a=\tfrac{1}{3}
corresponds to
d=\tfrac{\gamma+1}{3}
, which implies the Hölder symmetry of the preferred symbol function:

C_{\nu}(\gamma,k)=C_{\nu}(\gamma^{*},k).

Taking
a=0
shows that
J(1,\gamma,k)=J(1,\gamma^{*},k)
, verifying the analogous invariance property holds for the symbol functions associated to the Lebesgue measure
\mu_{1}
.
The Hölder symmetry of the symbol function plays an important role in the proofs of both Theorems
1.6
and
1.15
, and arises from the projective dual nature of the Leray transform; see Section
2.1
.

1.4.
Polygamma inequalities and complete monotonicity

Recall the
digamma function

\psi
, which is the logarithmic derivative of the familiar
\Gamma
-function:

(1.19)

\psi(r)=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(r)}{\Gamma(r)}.

Further derivatives of
\psi
are called
polygamma functions
.
They satisfy interesting functional identities and arise naturally in both analytic and number theoretic settings; see
[
1
]
.
The polygamma functions admit several practical representations; we make use of both the series form given in (
3.17
) and the integral form given in (
4.5
).

The following combination of polygamma functions plays a central role in this paper.

\Phi(r,q):=r^{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(r+1-q)+2r\psi^{\prime}(r+1-q).

Figure
2
shows the function
r\mapsto\Phi(r,q)
plotted for certain fixed
q
.
In Lemma
3.21
, we show the behavior of the Leray symbol function is closely tied to the behavior of
\Phi(r,q)
:

•

if
\Phi(r,q)<1
for
r>q
, then
k\mapsto J(\delta_{1-q}(\gamma),\gamma,k)
is strictly decreasing.

•

if
\Phi(r,q)>1
for
r>q
, then
k\mapsto J(\delta_{1-q}(\gamma),\gamma,k)
is strictly increasing.

(Here
\delta_{1-q}(\gamma)
is given by (
1.17
) with
a=1-q
.)

Figure 2.
Behavior of
r\mapsto\Phi(r,q)
for certain
0\leq q\leq 1

Theorem 1.20
.

Sharp polygamma inequalities related to the symbol function are obtained.

(a)

If
q\in(-\infty,0]\cup[1,\infty)
, the following holds for
r>q

\Phi(r,q)=r^{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(r+1-q)+2r\psi^{\prime}(r+1-q)<1.

(b)

When
q=\frac{2}{3}
, the following holds for
r>\tfrac{2}{3}

\Phi\big(r,\tfrac{2}{3}\big)=r^{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(r+\tfrac{1}{3})+2r\psi^{\prime}(r+\tfrac{1}{3})>1.

The proof of Theorem
1.20
part
(a)
is found in Corollary
4.21
, where the inequality follows by proving the
complete monotonicity
of a closely related function in Theorem
4.15
.
The
q=\tfrac{2}{3}
result in part
(b)
corresponds directly to the preferred symbol function.
We prove this inequality in Theorem
5.5
using a series representation for
\Phi(r,\tfrac{2}{3})
.

There is a large body of literature on inequalities involving combinations of polygamma functions; see, e.g.,
[
2
,
3
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
22
]
and the references therein.
The inequalities in Theorem
1.20
appear to be new and we suspect that mathematicians working in special function theory will take interest in results found in Sections
4
,
5
, and the Appendix.

The paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
provides background material on the Leray transform.
In Section
3
we establish new symmetries for the symbol function, which is then related to
\Phi(r,q)
.
In Section
4
we introduce the Bernstein-Widder theorem and prove several polygamma and symbol function inequalities.
In Section
5
the Euler-Maclaurin formula is used to study the preferred symbol function and the corresponding
\Phi(r,\tfrac{2}{3})
.
The proof of one especially difficult estimate used in Section
5
is postponed until the Appendix.

2.
The Leray transform

The hypersurfaces
M_{\gamma}
serve as the boundary of (unbounded) convex domains in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.
But the Leray transform
\bm{L}
and Leray kernel

{\mathscr{L}}(z,\zeta)=\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{n}}\frac{\partial\rho(\zeta)\wedge(\overline{\partial}\partial\rho(\zeta))^{n-1}}{\langle\partial\rho(\zeta),\zeta-z\rangle^{n}}

can in fact be defined on the more general class of
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex domains.
A domain
\Omega
is
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex if every (non-empty) intersection with a complex line is both connected and simply connected.
This condition ensures the non-vanishing of the denominator of the Leray kernel for any
z\in\Omega
.
Both the Leray transform and the notion of
{\mathbb{C}}
-convexity can be profitably studied in projective space
{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{P}^{n}
.
This is briefly discussed in Sections
2.1
and
2.2
below; see
[
4
]
for a detailed treatment of this topic.

It is often useful to decompose the Leray kernel
{\mathscr{L}}
into two pieces:

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\ell(z,\zeta)

\displaystyle=\langle\partial\rho(\zeta),\zeta-z\rangle^{-n},

(2.1b)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rho}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{n}}\partial\rho(\zeta)\wedge(\overline{\partial}\partial\rho(\zeta))^{n-1}.

We refer to
\lambda_{\rho}
as the
Leray-Levi measure
.
Readers are invited to draw the connection between
\lambda_{\rho}
and the volume form
\theta\wedge d\theta^{n-1}
associated to the pseudo-hermitian structure arising from the contact form
\theta=i\partial\rho
; see
[
21
]
.
By itself,
\lambda_{\rho}
clearly depends on the choice of defining function, though natural choices of
\rho
often correspond to important measures.
See
[
17
]
for a survey on
\bm{L}
and related operators within the Cauchy-Fantappiè framework.

Barrett and Lanzani
[
8
]
study the
L^{2}
-theory of
\bm{L}
on smoothly bounded, strongly convex Reinhardt domains in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.
They obtain detailed spectral information on
\bm{L}^{*}\bm{L}
,
\bm{L}\bm{L}^{*}
and
\bm{L}^{*}-\bm{L}
, and relate the essential norm of
\bm{L}
to a geometric invariant of the domain.
Several articles by Lanzani and Stein have investigated other aspects the Leray transform.
In
[
18
]
they prove that
\bm{L}
preserves
L^{p}
spaces (
1<p<\infty
) whenever the hypersurface
{\mathcal{S}}
is bounded, strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-linearly convex and
C^{1,1}
smooth.
In
[
19
,
20
]
they show that counter-examples to
L^{p}
-boundedness exist when the smoothness or convexity hypotheses are relaxed.

2.1.
Projective invariance

In
[
5
, Section 5]
, Barrett defines a projective geometric invariant associated to any smoothly bounded, strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex hypersurface
{\mathcal{S}}
.
He uses it
[
5
, Section 8]
to construct a projectively invariant measure
\nu_{\mathcal{S}}
:
If
\Phi
is an automorphism of
{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{P}^{n}
, the measure transforms as

(2.2)

\Phi^{*}\big(\nu_{\Phi({\mathcal{S}})}\big)=|\det\Phi^{\prime}|^{\frac{2n}{n+1}}\,\nu_{\mathcal{S}}.

We refer to
\nu_{\mathcal{S}}
as the
preferred measure
.
Equation (
2.2
) shows that the operator

(2.3)

f\mapsto(\det\Phi^{\prime})^{\frac{n}{n+1}}\cdot(f\circ\Phi)

maps
L^{2}\big(\Phi({\mathcal{S}}),\nu_{\Phi({\mathcal{S}})}\big)
isometrically to
L^{2}({\mathcal{S}},\nu_{\mathcal{S}})
.
If we use the Radon-Nikodym Theorem to re-express the Leray kernel (
1.1b
) in terms of the preferred measure, then the Leray transform admits the transformation law

(2.4)

\bm{L}_{\mathcal{S}}\left((\det\Phi^{\prime})^{\frac{n}{n+1}}(f\circ\Phi)\right)=(\det\Phi^{\prime})^{\frac{n}{n+1}}\left(\bm{L}_{\Phi({\mathcal{S}})}(f)\circ\Phi\right);

see
[
5
, Section 9]
and
[
9
, Section 5]
.
When the Leray transform is bounded on
L^{2}({\mathcal{S}},\nu_{\mathcal{S}})
(e.g., when Lanzani-Stein conditions of
[
18
]
hold), it can be used to define projectively invariant Hardy spaces consisting of the boundary values of holomorphic functions:

(2.5)

H^{2}({\mathcal{S}},\nu_{\mathcal{S}})=\bm{L}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(L^{2}({\mathcal{S}},\nu_{\mathcal{S}})\right).

2.2.
Projective duality

Given a smooth strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex hypersurface
{\mathcal{S}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{P}^{n}
, there is a unique complex tangent hyperplane at each
\zeta\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
This determines a unique point in a dual copy of
{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{P}^{n}
; the set of all such points form the
dual hypersurface

{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
.

In
[
5
, Section 6]
, Barrett shows that
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
is smoothly bounded and strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex whenever
{\mathcal{S}}
is, and further, that
{\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
are diffeomorphic.
If
w:{\mathcal{S}}\to{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
is a such a diffeomorphism, it can be used to pull back the space
H^{2}({\mathcal{S}}^{*},\nu_{{\mathcal{S}}^{*}})
by setting

H_{\sf dual}^{2}({\mathcal{S}},\nu_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{*})=w^{*}\left(H^{2}({\mathcal{S}}^{*},\nu_{{\mathcal{S}}^{*}})\right),\qquad\mathrm{where}\qquad\nu_{\mathcal{S}}^{*}=w^{*}(\nu_{{\mathcal{S}}^{*}}).

The
pairing measure

\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}
is defined as the geometric mean of
\nu_{\mathcal{S}}
and
\nu_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{*}
.

Given functions
f\in H^{2}({\mathcal{S}},\nu_{\mathcal{S}})
and
g\in H_{\sf{dual}}^{2}({\mathcal{S}},\nu_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{*})
, define their
bilinear pairing
as

\langle\!\langle f,g\rangle\!\rangle=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}}f(\zeta)g(\zeta)\,\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}(\zeta).

The map
\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}:g\mapsto\langle\!\langle\cdot,g\rangle\!\rangle
gives a quasi-isometric identification of
H_{\sf{dual}}^{2}({\mathcal{S}},\nu_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{*})
with function theoretic dual space
H^{2}({\mathcal{S}},\nu_{\mathcal{S}})^{\prime}
.
In the
M_{\gamma}
setting, we have the following result:

Proposition 2.6

(Barrett-Edholm
[
7
]
)
.

The operator
\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}:H_{\sf{dual}}^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)\mapsto H^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)^{\prime}
is an invertible map with

\left\|\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}^{-1}\right\|_{H^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)^{\prime}}=\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)}.

In light of Theorem
1.6
part
(d)
we can now express this as a concrete number:

Theorem 2.7
.

The operator
\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}:H_{\sf{dual}}^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)\mapsto H^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)^{\prime}
is an invertible map with

(2.8)

\left\|\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}^{-1}\right\|_{H^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)^{\prime}}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}}}.

2.3.
Leray theory on
M_{\gamma}

The following material is found in
[
7
, Sections 3, 5]
.
As in the introduction, parameterize
M_{\gamma}
by
(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})=(re^{i\theta},s+ir^{\gamma})
and consider the measures

\mu_{d}=r^{d}\,dr\wedge d\theta\wedge ds,\qquad d\in{\mathbb{R}}.

The pairing measure
\sigma
(
d=\gamma-1
) arises as (a constant multiple of) the Leray-Levi measure corresponding to the defining function
\rho(\zeta)=|\zeta_{1}|^{\gamma}-\text{Im}{(\zeta_{2})}
.

Rotational symmetry in
\zeta_{1}
yields a decomposition of
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
into an orthogonal sum of subspaces
L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
, each consisting of functions of the form
f_{k}(r,s)e^{ik\theta}
.
Likewise, the Leray transform decomposes into orthogonal
sub-Leray
operators
\bm{L}_{k}
, each mapping
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
to
L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
whenever it is bounded:
\bm{L}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}\bm{L}_{k}
.
Given
f\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
, the following inequality holds; see
[
7
, Theorem 5.13]
:

(2.9)

\left\|\bm{L}_{k}f\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}\leq\sqrt{J(d,\gamma,k)}\cdot\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})},

where
J(d,\gamma,k)
is the symbol function defined in (
1.7
).
It turns out there always exist functions
f_{k}(r,s)e^{ik\theta}\in L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
achieving equality in the relation (
2.9
) above.

If
f(r,s,\theta)=\sum_{k}f_{k}(r,s)e^{ik\theta}
, orthogonality implies the following; see
[
7
, Corollary 5.28]
:

\left\|\bm{L}f\right\|^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|\bm{L}_{k}f\right\|^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|\bm{L}_{k}f_{k}\,e^{ik\theta}\right\|^{2}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}J(d,\gamma,k)\left\|f_{k}\right\|^{2}\leq M\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|f_{k}\right\|^{2}=M\left\|f\right\|^{2},

where
M
denotes the supremum of
J(d,\gamma,k)
as
k
ranges over the non-negative integers.
But since the norm of each
\bm{L}_{k}
is achieved by a function in
L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
, it follows that

(2.10)

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}=\sup\left\{\sqrt{J(d,\gamma,k)}:k=0,1,2,\dots\right\}.

3.
Symmetries and Monotonicity

3.1.
Hölder symmetry,
\gamma\neq 2

Proposition
1.8
says
\bm{L}_{k}
is bounded on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
if and only if
d\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(\gamma)
.
Here, it is shown that for each
d
there is a unique
d^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that the behavior of
\bm{L}_{k}
in
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
closely parallels its behavior in
L^{2}(M_{\gamma^{*}},\mu_{d^{\prime}})
, where
\gamma^{*}
is the Hölder conjugate of
\gamma
.
For
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
, define

(3.1)

\delta_{a}(\gamma)=a(\gamma-2)+1.

Theorem 3.2
.

The symbol function
J(\delta_{a}(\gamma),\gamma,k)
is Hölder symmetric, in that

J(\delta_{a}(\gamma),\gamma,k)=J(\delta_{a}(\gamma^{*}),\gamma^{*},k).

Proof.

From (
1.7
) we have

J(\delta_{a}(\gamma),\gamma,k)=\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2k+2+(\gamma-2)a}{\gamma}\big)\Gamma\big(2k+2-\frac{2k+2+(\gamma-2)a}{\gamma}\big)}{\Gamma(k+1)^{2}}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)^{2k+2}(\gamma-1)^{\big(\frac{2k+2+(\gamma-2)a}{\gamma}-2k-2\big)}.

But notice that

\displaystyle\frac{2k+2+(\gamma^{*}-2)a}{\gamma^{*}}=\frac{2k+2-2a}{\gamma^{*}}+a

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)(2k+2-2a)+a

\displaystyle=2k+2-a-\frac{2k+2-2a}{\gamma}

(3.3)

\displaystyle=2k+2-\frac{2k+2+(\gamma-2)a}{\gamma}.

Thus, we have both

(3.4a)

\displaystyle\Gamma\bigg(\frac{2k+2+(\gamma^{*}-2)a}{\gamma^{*}}\bigg)=\Gamma\bigg(2k+2-\frac{2k+2+(\gamma-2)a}{\gamma}\bigg),

(3.4b)

\displaystyle\Gamma\bigg(\frac{2k+2+(\gamma-2)a}{\gamma}\bigg)=\Gamma\bigg(2k+2-\frac{2k+2+(\gamma^{*}-2)a}{\gamma^{*}}\bigg).

Using that

\Big(\frac{\gamma^{*}}{2}\Big)^{2k+2}=\Big(\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big)^{2k+2}(\gamma-1)^{-2k-2},

we deduce from (
3.3
) that

\displaystyle(\gamma^{*}-1)^{\big(\frac{2k+2+(\gamma^{*}-2)a}{\gamma^{*}}-2k-2\big)}

\displaystyle=(\gamma-1)^{\big(2k+2-\frac{2k+2+(\gamma^{*}-2)a}{\gamma^{*}}\big)}=(\gamma-1)^{\big(\frac{2k+2+(\gamma-2)a}{\gamma}\big)}.

Combining the previous two equations we have

(3.5)

\Big(\frac{\gamma^{*}}{2}\Big)^{2k+2}(\gamma^{*}-1)^{\big(\frac{2k+2+(\gamma^{*}-2)a}{\gamma^{*}}-2k-2\big)}=\Big(\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big)^{2k+2}(\gamma-1)^{\big(\frac{2k+2+(\gamma-2)a}{\gamma}-2k-2\big)}.

Putting together the equations in (
3.4
) with (
3.5
) gives the result.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

The computations in the theorem above yield a Hölder symmetric reformulation of the symbol function whenever
\gamma\neq 2
:

J(\delta_{a}(\gamma),\gamma,k)=\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2k+2+(\gamma-2)a}{\gamma}\big)\Gamma\big(\frac{2k+2+(\gamma^{*}-2)a}{\gamma^{*}}\big)}{\Gamma(k+1)^{2}}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)^{\big(\frac{2k+2+(\gamma-2)a}{\gamma}\big)}\Big(\frac{\gamma^{*}}{2}\Big)^{\big(\frac{2k+2+(\gamma^{*}-2)a}{\gamma^{*}}\big)}.

\lozenge

Corollary 3.7
.

The pairing symbol function and the preferred symbol function are both Hölder symmetric, i.e.,
C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)=C_{\sigma}(\gamma^{*},k)
and
C_{\nu}(\gamma,k)=C_{\nu}(\gamma^{*},k)
.

Proof.

The pairing measure
\sigma=\mu_{d}
with
d=\gamma-1
.
This
d
value coincides with
\delta_{a}(\gamma)
with
a=1
, so Theorem
3.2
applies.
The preferred measure
\nu=\mu_{d}
with
d=\frac{\gamma+1}{3}
.
This
d
value coincides with
\delta_{a}(\gamma)
with
a=\frac{1}{3}
, so Theorem
3.2
again applies.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

In addition to
\sigma
and
\nu
, Barrett and Edholm
[
7
, Section 6.2.2]
also introduce the
dual preferred measure

\nu^{*}
on a
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex hypersurface
{\mathcal{S}}

{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
.
(Recall the discussion in Section
2.1

.)

When
{\mathcal{S}}=M_{\gamma}

It follows from (

) that

.

Remark 3.9
.

When

.

3.2.
Heisenberg monotonicity

From (
1.7
), observe that the symbol function greatly simplifies when
\gamma=2
:

(3.10)

J(d,2,k)=\frac{\Gamma\big(k+\frac{1+d}{2}\big)\Gamma\big(k+\frac{3-d}{2}\big)}{\Gamma(k+1)^{2}}.

A fraction involving
\Gamma
-functions in this form is sometimes called Gurland’s ratio; see
[
22
]
.

Proposition 3.11
.

For each non-negative integer
k
,

\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{2},\mu_{d})}=\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{2},\mu_{2-d})}.

Proof.

From (
3.10
) it is immediate that
J(d,2,k)=J(2-d,2,k)
.
Now use (
1.10
).
∎

Remark 3.12
.

When
\gamma=2
and
d=1
, we see that
\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{2},\mu_{1})}=1
for every
k
.
This of course is unsurprising, since the Leray transform is the Szegő projection on
L^{2}(M_{2},\mu_{1})
.

\lozenge

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
d
be any real number.
The function
k\mapsto J(d,2,k)
is decreasing whenever it is finite.
Consequently,

\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{2},\mu_{d})}\geq\left\|\bm{L}_{k+1}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{2},\mu_{d})}.

The inequality is strict for
d\neq 1
.

Proof.

When
d=1
, Remark
3.12
says that the norm of
\bm{L}_{k}
is equal to 1 for all
k
.

When
d\neq 1
, we see from (
1.9
) that
J(d,2,k)
is finite if and only if

d\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(2)=(-2k-1,2k+3).

Since
{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(2)\subset{\mathcal{I}}_{k+1}(2)
and these intervals exhaust the real line, it is clear that
J(d,2,\gamma)
can be infinite for
at most
a finite number of integers
k
.

Observe that
d\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(2)
if and only if
2-d\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(2)
.
Proposition
3.11
says it is sufficient to consider the case when
d>1
, which we now assume for the rest of the proof.

Taking logarithms, (
3.10
) becomes

\log{J(d,2,k)}=\log{\Gamma\big(k+\tfrac{1+d}{2}\big)}+\log{\Gamma\big(k+\tfrac{3-d}{2}\big)}-2\log{\Gamma(k+1)}.

Now treat
k
as a continuous variable and differentiate:

(3.14)

\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\log{J(d,2,k)}=\psi(k+\tfrac{1+d}{2})+\psi(k+\tfrac{3-d}{2})-2\psi(k+1),

where
\psi
is the digamma function defined in (
1.19
).
Now consider the right-hand side of (
3.14
) in two separate pieces.
By the mean value theorem,

(3.15a)

\psi\big(k+\tfrac{1+d}{2}\big)-\psi(k+1)=\psi^{\prime}(\xi_{1})\big(\tfrac{d-1}{2}\big),

for some
\xi_{1}\in\big(k+1,k+\tfrac{1+d}{2}\big)
.
Similarly,

(3.15b)

\psi\big(k+\tfrac{3-d}{2}\big)-\psi(k+1)=-\psi^{\prime}(\xi_{2})\big(\tfrac{d-1}{2}\big),

for some
\xi_{2}\in\big(k+\tfrac{3-d}{2},k+1\big)
.

Combining (
3.15a
), (
3.15b
) and (
3.14
) we use the mean value theorem again,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\log{J(d,2,k)}=\big(\tfrac{d-1}{2}\big)(\psi^{\prime}(\xi_{1})-\psi^{\prime}(\xi_{2}))=\big(\tfrac{d-1}{2}\big)\psi^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{3})(\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}),

for some
\xi_{3}\in(\xi_{2},\xi_{1})
.
It is well-known that
\psi^{\prime\prime}<0
on the positive reals (see, e.g., formula (
3.17
) below).
This means that
k\mapsto\log{J(d,2,k)}
is strictly decreasing for
k\in[0,\infty)
, which implies that
k\mapsto J(d,2,k)
is strictly decreasing for
k\in[0,\infty)
.
Now restrict
k
to the non-negative integers and recall (
1.10
) to complete the proof.
∎

By Corollary
1.12
, the Leray transform is bounded on
L^{2}(M_{2},\mu_{d})
if and only if
d\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0}(2)=(-1,3)
.
When this holds, the norm of the full Leray transform can be computed:

Corollary 3.16
.

Let
d\in(-1,3)
.
The norm of the Leray transform on
L^{2}(M_{2},\mu_{d})
is

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{2},\mu_{d})}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}(1-d)\sec{\big(\frac{d\pi}{2}\big)}},&d\in(-1,1)\cup(1,3)\\
1,&d=1.\end{cases}

This formula is continuous at
d=1
.

Proof.

The
d=1
case can be seen directly from (
3.10
) (also see Remark
3.12
) and L’Hôpital’s rule shows continuity at
d=1
.
When
d\neq 1
, Theorem
3.13
says
k\mapsto J(d,\gamma,k)
is strictly decreasing in
k
.
Thus by (
2.10
),

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{2},\mu_{d})}=\sqrt{J(d,2,0)}.

Now compute:

J(d,2,0)=\big(\tfrac{1-d}{2}\big)\Gamma\big(1-\tfrac{1-d}{2}\big)\Gamma\big(\tfrac{1-d}{2}\big)=\big(\tfrac{1-d}{2}\big)\pi\csc\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\tfrac{d\pi}{2}\big)=\tfrac{\pi}{2}(1-d)\sec\big(\tfrac{d\pi}{2}\big).

In this computation we have used the factorial property of the
\Gamma
-function along with Euler’s reflection formula:
\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\pi\csc(\pi z)
.
∎

3.3.
Polygamma functions and ratios of successive symbols

In this section, once again consider
\gamma\neq 2
and set
\delta_{a}(\gamma)=a(\gamma-2)+1
.

The polygamma functions are the successive derivatives of the digamma function
\psi
, defined in (
1.19
).
For integers
m\geq 1
, these functions admit the following series representations; see
[
1
, Equation 6.4.10]
:

(3.17)

\psi^{(m)}(r)=(-1)^{m+1}m!\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(r+j-1)^{m+1}}.

The following polygamma combination is closely tied to Leray symbol function and is prominently featured throughout the paper (recall Figure
2
in Section
1
):

(3.18a)

\Phi(r,q)=2r\psi^{\prime}(r+1-q)+r^{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(r+1-q).

In light of (
3.17
), it is often useful to write this function as a series

(3.18b)

\Phi(r,q)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{2r(j-q)}{(r+j-q)^{3}}.

The establishment of new inequalities related to the polygamma functions has been an active area of research for decades; see
[
2
,
3
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
22
]
for results with similar flavor to those we prove below.
A typical way in which new polygamma inequalities are obtained is to show that some auxiliary function is
completely monotone
(see Section
4.2
), a checkable condition thanks to the celebrated Bernstein-Widder theorem (see Theorem
4.7
).

In Sections
4
and
5
, we prove inequalities involving
\Phi(r,q)
and related functions, both by use of Bernstein-Widder and in situations where it fails to apply.
Our inequalities appear to be new and the methods are likely adaptable to more general settings.

For later convenience, we re-frame the finiteness of the symbol function in terms of
\delta_{1-q}
.
(Setting
a=1-q
in (
3.1
) yields a symmetric formulation of the following result):

Lemma 3.19
.

Let
\gamma\neq 2
. The symbol function
J(\delta_{1-q}(\gamma),\gamma,k)
is finite for all non-negative integers
k
if and only if

|q|<\frac{\gamma}{|\gamma-2|}.

Proof.

By Proposition
1.8
,
J(\delta_{1-q}(\gamma),\gamma,k)
is finite for all
k
if and only if

\delta_{1-q}(\gamma)\in\bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(\gamma)={\mathcal{I}}_{0}(\gamma)=(-1,2\gamma-1).

For
\gamma>2
this means

\displaystyle-1<(1-q)(\gamma-2)+1<2\gamma-1\qquad

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\qquad-\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-2}<q<\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-2},

confirming the inequality. When
\gamma<2
,

\displaystyle-1<(1-q)(\gamma-2)+1<2\gamma-1\qquad

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\qquad-\frac{\gamma}{2-\gamma}<q<\frac{\gamma}{2-\gamma},

confirming the inequality.
∎

Remark 3.20
.

Observe that the estimate appearing in Lemma
3.19
is Hölder symmetric, i.e., invariant under the change of variable
\gamma\mapsto\gamma^{*}
.

\lozenge

Lemma 3.21
.

Let
\gamma\neq 2
and
|q|<\dfrac{\gamma}{|\gamma-2|}
, so that
J(\delta_{1-q}(\gamma),\gamma,k)
is finite for
k\geq 0
.

(1)

If
\Phi(r,q)<1
for
r>q
, then
k\mapsto J(\delta_{1-q}(\gamma),\gamma,k)
is strictly decreasing on the non-negative integers.

(2)

If
\Phi(r,q)>1
for
r>q
, then
k\mapsto J(\delta_{1-q}(\gamma),\gamma,k)
is strictly increasing on the non-negative integers.

Proof.

Strict increasing (resp. decreasing) behavior of the function
k\mapsto J(\delta_{1-q}(\gamma),\gamma,k)
would follow by showing the below ratio is strictly greater than 1 (resp. strictly less than 1) for non-negative integers
k
.
Throughout the proof, set
a=1-q
:

\displaystyle\frac{J(\delta_{a}(\gamma),\gamma,k+1)}{J(\delta_{a}(\gamma),\gamma,k)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{2k+4+(\gamma-2)a}{\gamma}\right)\Gamma\left(2k+4-\frac{2k+4+(\gamma-2)a}{\gamma}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{2k+2+(\gamma-2)a}{\gamma}\right)\Gamma\left(2k+2-\frac{2k+2+(\gamma-2)a}{\gamma}\right)}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2k+2}\right)^{2}(\gamma-1)^{\frac{2}{\gamma}-2}

By the Hölder symmetry of the symbol function (Theorem
3.2
), it is sufficient to restrict analysis to
\gamma\in(1,2)
; upon making the substitution
x=\frac{2}{\gamma}
, this is equivalent to analyzing the behavior of the following function for
x\in(1,2)
:

\frac{\Gamma\left((k+2-a)x+a\right)\Gamma\left((k+2-a)(2-x)+a\right)}{\Gamma\left((k+1-a)x+a\right)\Gamma\left((k+1-a)(2-x)+a\right)}\cdot\frac{x^{-x}(2-x)^{x-2}}{(k+1)^{2}}:=e^{A(k,x)}

Upon taking a logarithm, observe that
A(k,1)\equiv 0
, and further that
A(k,x)
can be written

\displaystyle A(k,x)=B(k,x)+B(k,2-x),

where

(3.22)

B(k,x):=\log\left[\frac{\Gamma\left((k+2-a)x+a\right)}{\Gamma\left((k+1-a)x+a\right)}\right]-x\log x-\log(k+1).

For the remainder of the proof we focus on case (2), noting that the same argument with trivial modifications in appropriate places will prove case (1).

We have just seen that the statement

(3.23)

\frac{J(\delta_{a}(\gamma),\gamma,k+1)}{J(\delta_{a}(\gamma),\gamma,k)}>1

for all non-negative integers
k
and
\gamma\in(1,2)
is equivalent to the statement that
A(k,x)>0
for all non-negative integers
k
and
x\in(1,2)
.

Since
A(k,1)\equiv 0
for all non-negative integers
k
, the desired positivity of
A(k,x)
will follow by establishing a stronger condition, namely, that for
x\in(1,2)

(3.24)

\frac{\partial A}{\partial x}(k,x)=\frac{\partial B}{\partial x}(k,x)-\frac{\partial B}{\partial x}(k,2-x)>0.

Estimate (
3.24
) will in turn follow if it can be established that
\frac{\partial B}{\partial x}(k,x)
is strictly increasing for
x\in(1,2)
, since
0<2-x<x
for such
x
.
It would thus suffice to show

(3.25)

\frac{\partial^{2}B}{\partial x^{2}}(k,x)>0.

Recalling the representation (
3.17
) of
\psi^{\prime}
by a series, calculation now shows

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}B}{\partial x^{2}}(k,x)

\displaystyle=(k+2-a)^{2}\psi^{\prime}((k+2-a)x+a)-(k+1-a)^{2}\psi^{\prime}((k+1-a)x+a)-\frac{1}{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(k+2-a)^{2}}{(j+(k+2-a)x+(a-1))^{2}}-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(k+1-a)^{2}}{(j+(k+1-a)x+(a-1))^{2}}-\frac{1}{x}

(3.26)

\displaystyle=D(k+1,x)-D(k,x),

where

(3.27)

D(k,x):=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(k+1-a)^{2}}{(j+(k+1-a)x+(a-1))^{2}}-\frac{k}{x}.

Temporarily regard
k
as a continuous variable.
By (
3.26
), it would now be sufficient to show that
D(k,x)
increases as a function of
k\in[0,\infty)
in order to conclude that estimate (
3.25
) holds.
We claim that for
x\in(1,2)
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial D}{\partial k}(k,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\left[\frac{(k+1-a)^{2}}{(j+(k+1-a)x+(a-1))^{2}}\right]-\frac{1}{x}

(3.28)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{2(k+1-a)(j+a-1)}{(j+(k+1-a)x+(a-1))^{3}}-\frac{1}{x}\quad>0.

The inequality in (
3.28
) is equivalent to saying that, for
k\in[0,\infty)
and
x\in(1,2)
,

(3.29)

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{2x(k+1-a)(j+a-1)}{(j+(k+1-a)x+(a-1))^{3}}>1.

Now substituting
q=1-a
and
r=x(k+1-a)=x(k+q)
.
The inequality (
3.29
) would follow by showing that, for
r>q
,

(3.30)

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{2r(j-q)}{(j+r-q)^{3}}=\Phi(r,q)>1.

This is the condition listed in case (2) of the theorem.
Retracing our steps, we see that inequality (
3.30
) implies inequality (
3.28
).
Now, by (
3.26
), this implies inequality (
3.25
).
This implies (
3.24
), which in turn shows (
3.23
), meaning that
k\mapsto J(\delta_{1-q}(\gamma),\gamma,k)
is an increasing function on the non-negative integers.

To prove case (1), return to (
3.23
) and retrace the same steps, changing “
>
” to “
<
” and “increases” to “decreases” in all necessary places.
∎

4.
Polygamma inequalities and complete monotonicity

4.1.
An initial estimate

We now examine the properties of

\Phi(r,q)=2r\psi^{\prime}(r+1-q)+r^{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(r+1-q).

We start from well-known upper and lower estimates on the polygamma functions; see
[
16
, Theorem 3]
.
If
x>0
and
m
is a positive integer, then

(4.1)

\frac{(m-1)!}{x^{m}}+\frac{m!}{2x^{m+1}}<(-1)^{m+1}\psi^{(m)}(x)<\frac{(m-1)!}{x^{m}}+\frac{m!}{x^{m+1}}.

We use this to describe the behavior of
\Phi(r,q)
as
r\to\infty
. (Compare with Figure
2
.)

Proposition 4.2
.

Let q be a fixed real number. Then

\lim_{r\to\infty}\Phi(r,q)=1.

Proof.

Directly from (
4.1
), we have for
r>\max\{q-1,0\}
,

\frac{2r^{2}-2rq+3r}{(r+1-q)^{2}}<2r\psi^{\prime}(r+1-q)<\frac{2r^{2}-2rq+4r}{(r+1-q)^{2}},

and

-\frac{(r-q+3)r^{2}}{(r+1-q)^{3}}<r^{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(r+1-q)<-\frac{(r-q+2)r^{2}}{(r+1-q)^{3}}.

Now combine these:

(4.3)

\frac{r^{3}+(2-3q)r^{2}+(3-5q+2q^{2})r}{(r+1-q)^{3}}<\Phi(r,q)<\frac{r^{3}+(4-3q)r^{2}+(4-6q+2q^{2})r}{(r+1-q)^{3}}.

Taking the limit as
r\to\infty
gives the result.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Given
q
, we must show either
\Phi(r,q)>1
for
r>q
, or
\Phi(r,q)<1
for
r>q
in order to apply Lemma
3.21
.
This requires better estimates than those obtained in (
4.3
).
This is easily seen when
q=0
, in which case

\frac{r^{3}+2r^{2}+3r}{r^{3}+3r^{2}+3r+1}<\Phi(r,0)<\frac{r^{3}+4r^{2}+4r}{r^{3}+3r^{2}+3r+1}.

This lower bound is always less than 1, while the upper bound is eventually greater than 1.
Similarly, neither the upper nor lower bound in (
4.3
) is strong enough to warrant application of Lemma
3.21
for any
q
.
Our goal is now to improve the estimates on
\Phi(r,q)
.

\lozenge

4.2.
The Bernstein-Widder theorem

Let us recall another well-known formula for the polygamma functions
\psi^{(m)}
; see
[
1
, Equation 6.4.1]
:

(4.5)

\psi^{(m)}(r)=(-1)^{m+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{m}e^{-rt}}{1-e^{-t}}\,dt.

Let
f
be a real-valued function defined on
(c,\infty)
, for some
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
We say that
f
is
strictly completely monotone
on
(c,\infty)
if it is of class
C^{\infty}
and

(4.6)

(-1)^{m}\frac{d^{m}}{dr^{m}}f(r)>0

for all non-negative integers
m
and
r>c
.

Strictly completely monotone functions are characterized by the following theorem, which can be found in
[
25
, page 161]
.

Theorem 4.7

(Bernstein-Widder)
.

A function
f
is strictly completely monotone on
(c,\infty)
if and only if
f(r-c)
is the Laplace transform of a finite positive Borel measure
\mu
on
(c,\infty)
.
In other words,

f(r-c)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-rt}\,d\mu(t).

Our goal is to use Bernstein-Widder to analyze
\Phi(r,q)
and closely related functions.

Let us define

(4.8a)

\Theta(r,q)=r^{2}\psi^{\prime}(r+1-q)

and observe that

(4.8b)

\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial r}(r,q)=\Phi(r,q).

Lemma 4.9
.

For
x>0
we have the following

(4.10)

\Theta(x+q,q)=x+2q-\frac{1}{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{M(t,q)}{(e^{t}-1)^{3}}e^{-xt}\,dt,

where

(4.11)

M(t,q)=g_{0}(t)-g_{1}(t)q+g_{2}(t)q^{2},

and
g_{0},g_{1},g_{2}
are positive functions on
(0,\infty)
.
Explicitly,

(4.12a)

\displaystyle g_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{t}(2-2e^{t}+t+te^{t}),

(4.12b)

\displaystyle g_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=2(e^{t}-1)(1-e^{t}+te^{t}),

(4.12c)

\displaystyle g_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=t(e^{t}-1)^{2}.

Proof.

From definition (
4.8a
) and (
4.5
) we have

\displaystyle\Theta(x+q,q)

\displaystyle=(x+q)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{te^{-(x+1)t}}{1-e^{-t}}\,dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{te^{qt}}{e^{t}-1}(x+q)^{2}e^{-(x+q)t}\,dt.

Integrate by parts twice, setting

u_{1}=\frac{te^{qt}}{e^{t}-1},\qquad dv_{1}=(x+q)^{2}e^{-(x+q)t}\,dt,

for the first application, and
u_{2}=\frac{du_{1}}{dt}
and
dv_{2}=v_{1}\,dt
for the second.
This means that

u_{2}=\frac{e^{t}-1-te^{t}-(t-te^{t})q}{(e^{t}-1)^{2}}\,e^{qt},\qquad v_{1}=-(x+q)e^{-(x+q)t},

and consequently,

du_{2}=\frac{g_{0}(t)-g_{1}(t)q+g_{2}(t)q^{2}}{(e^{t}-1)^{3}}\,e^{qt}\,dt,\qquad v_{2}=e^{-(x+q)t},

where
g_{0},g_{1},g_{2}
are given in the statement of the theorem.
Now,

\displaystyle\Theta(x+q,q)=\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{1}\,dv_{1}

\displaystyle=u_{1}v_{1}\Big|_{0}^{\infty}-\int_{0}^{\infty}v_{1}\,du_{1}

\displaystyle=u_{1}v_{1}\Big|_{0}^{\infty}-u_{2}v_{2}\Big|_{0}^{\infty}+\int_{0}^{\infty}v_{2}\,du_{2}

(4.13)

\displaystyle=(x+q)-\big(\tfrac{1}{2}-q\big)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{M(t,q)}{(e^{t}-1)^{3}}\,e^{-xt}\,dt.

Using the definitions of
M(t,q)
and the
g_{j}(t)
given above, we see that for
t
near 0,

M(t,q)=\Big(q^{2}-q+\frac{1}{6}\Big)t^{3}+\Big(q^{2}-\frac{7q}{6}+\frac{1}{4}\Big)t^{4}+O\big(t^{5}\big)

On the other hand, if
t
is large enough (say,
t>1
), there is a constant
C_{q}
such that

|M(t,q)|\leq C_{q}\,te^{2t}.

From these estimates on
M(t,q)
we see that the integral appearing in (
4.13
) converges for
x>0
.
It now only remains to show the positivity of the
g_{j}(t)
for
t>0
.

Let us first set
h_{0}(t)=2-2e^{t}+t+te^{t}
, so that
g_{0}(t)=e^{t}h_{0}(t)
.
Calculating derivatives,

h_{0}^{\prime}(t)=1+(t-1)e^{t},\qquad h_{0}^{\prime\prime}(t)=te^{t},

and so
0=h_{0}(0)=h^{\prime}_{0}(0)=h^{\prime\prime}(0)
.
Also, clearly
h_{0}^{\prime\prime}(t)>0
, for
t>0
which now implies that
h_{0}^{\prime}(t)>0
, which in turn implies
h_{0}(t)>0
.
Thus
g_{0}(t)>0
for
t>0
.

Now let
h_{1}(t)=1-e^{t}+te^{t}
, so that
g_{1}(t)=2(e^{t}-1)h_{1}(t)
.
We see that

h_{1}^{\prime}(t)=te^{t},

and so
0=h_{1}(0)=h_{1}^{\prime}(0)
.
Clearly
h_{1}^{\prime}(t)>0
for
t>0
, which implies
h_{1}(t)>0
and therefore
g_{1}(t)>0
for
t>0
.

Finally, it is immediate from the formula that
g_{2}(t)>0
for
t>0
.
∎

4.3.
Completely monotone functions

For real
q
define the following function

(4.14)

F_{q}(x)=\Theta(x+q,q)-x-2q+\frac{1}{2}.

Theorem 4.15
.

Let
q\in(-\infty,0]\cup[1,\infty)
.
The function
x\mapsto F_{q}(x)
is strictly completely monotone on
x>0
.

Proof.

From Lemma
4.9
, we have

(4.16)

F_{q}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{M(t,q)}{(e^{t}-1)^{3}}\,e^{-xt}\,dt,

where
M(t,q)=g_{0}(t)-g_{1}(t)q+g_{2}(t)q^{2}
, and the
g_{j}
are given in (
4.12
).

Regarding
M(t,q)
as a quadratic in the
q
variable, the discriminant is

\displaystyle\Delta(t)

\displaystyle=g_{1}(t)^{2}-4g_{0}(t)g_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=4-16e^{t}+24e^{2t}-16e^{3t}+4e^{4t}+(-4e^{t}+8e^{2t}-4e^{3t})t^{2}

(4.17)

\displaystyle=4(e^{t}-1)^{2}(1-2e^{t}+e^{2t}-e^{t}t^{2}).

The roots to
M(t,q)=0
are therefore given by taking

q=\frac{g_{1}(t)\pm\sqrt{\Delta(t)}}{2g_{2}(t)};

we now label these roots as

(4.18a)

\displaystyle s_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{te^{t}+1-e^{t}+\sqrt{1-2e^{t}+e^{2t}-t^{2}e^{t}}}{t(e^{t}-1)},

(4.18b)

\displaystyle s_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{te^{t}+1-e^{t}-\sqrt{1-2e^{t}+e^{2t}-t^{2}e^{t}}}{t(e^{t}-1)}.

Observe that the term under the square root is positive for
t>0
:

\displaystyle 1-2e^{t}+e^{2t}-t^{2}e^{t}>0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\cosh{t}>1+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2j}}{(2j)!}>0.

We now claim for
t>0
that

(4.19)

0<s_{2}(t)<s_{1}(t)<1.

The fact that
s_{2}(t)<s_{1}(t)
is immediate.
The inequality
s_{2}(t)>0
follows from the fact that
M(t,q)>0
for all
q\leq 0
and
t>0
(since each
g_{j}(t)>0
for
t>0
).

For the remaining inequality we show that it is both true and sharp.
Indeed,

\displaystyle s_{1}(t)<1\qquad

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\qquad te^{t}-t>te^{t}+1-e^{t}+\sqrt{1-2e^{t}+e^{2t}-t^{2}e^{t}}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\qquad e^{t}-1-t>\sqrt{1-2e^{t}+e^{2t}-t^{2}e^{t}}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\qquad(e^{t}-1)^{2}-2t(e^{t}-1)+t^{2}>(e^{t}-1)^{2}-t^{2}e^{t}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\qquad(t-2)e^{t}+t+2>0.

Now set
E(t):=(t-2)e^{t}+t+2
.
Then

E^{\prime}(t)=(t-1)e^{t}+1,\qquad E^{\prime\prime}(t)=te^{t},

and so
0=E(0)=E^{\prime}(0)=E^{\prime\prime}(0)
.
Since
E^{\prime\prime}(t)>0
for
t>0
, we have that
E^{\prime}(t)>0
which in turn implies that
E(t)>0
, thus confirming that
s_{1}(t)<1
.
(Notice that the function
E
, along with the same line of reasoning given here, appeared in Lemma
4.9
, where the function was called
h_{0}
.)

For the sharpness of this inequality, observe that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}s_{1}(t)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{te^{t}}{te^{t}}\cdot\frac{1+t^{-1}(e^{-t}-1)+\sqrt{t^{-2}(1-e^{-t})^{2}-e^{-t}})}{1-e^{-t}}=1.

Now since
0<s_{2}(t)<s_{1}(t)<1
for all
t>0
, we conclude that
M(t,q)>0
for
t>0
and
q\in(-\infty,0]\cup[1,\infty)
.
The Bernstein-Widder theorem now shows that
F_{q}(x)
is strictly completely monotone for
q
in this range.
∎

Remark 4.20
.

The proof of Theorem
4.15
shows the right endpoint
q=1
of the interval
0<s_{2}(t)<s_{1}(t)<1
is sharp.
No claim of sharpness is made for the left endpoint, however.
Numerical evidence suggests that
s_{2}(t)
is a strictly increasing function on
t>0
, which would imply that the left endpoint can be slightly improved from
q=0
to

q=\lim_{t\searrow 0}s_{2}(t)=\frac{3-\sqrt{3}}{6}\approx 0.211325.

In any case, we can say that
F_{q}(x)
fails to be completely monotone for
q\in\big(\tfrac{3-\sqrt{3}}{6},1\big)
.

\lozenge

Corollary 4.21
.

Choose
q\in(-\infty,0]\cup[1,\infty)
.
Then
\Phi(r,q)<1
for
r>q
.

Proof.

Fix
q\in(-\infty,0]\cup[1,\infty)
and set
x=r-q
.
Theorem
4.15
now says the function

r\mapsto F_{q}(r-q)=\Theta(r,q)-r-q+\frac{1}{2}

is strictly completely monotone for
r>q
.
Now differentiate in
r
to see that

\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial r}(r,q)-1<0,

for all
r>q
.
Since
\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial r}(r,q)=\Phi(r,q)
by (
4.8b
), we obtain the result.
∎

Remark 4.22
.

Notice that
q=\frac{2}{3}
(the
q
-value corresponding to the preferred symbol function) lies in the interval for which complete monotonicity of
F_{q}(x)
is known to fail; see Remark
4.20
.
This means the Berstein-Widder approach is not applicable, and the preferred symbol function must be handled using other means (see Section
5
).

\lozenge

4.4.
Consequences for the Leray transform

The results in Sections
4.2
and
4.3
are now combined with Lemma
3.21
.
Recall (Corollary
1.12
) that the Leray transform is bounded on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})
if and only if
d\in(-1,2\gamma-1)={\mathcal{I}}_{0}(\gamma)
.
We now see that the norms of the sub-Leray operators
\bm{L}_{k}
are
strictly decreasing
in
k
for a range of
d
values with a combined length of more than half the length of
{\mathcal{I}}_{0}(\gamma)
.

Theorem 4.23
.

Let
\bm{L}_{k}
denote the sub-Leray operator for each non-negative integer
k
.

(1)

If
\gamma>2
and
d\in(-1,1]\cup[\gamma-1,2\gamma-1)
, then the function
k\mapsto J(d,\gamma,k)
is strictly decreasing on the non-negative integers.
Thus

\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}>\left\|\bm{L}_{k+1}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}.

(2)

If
\gamma<2
and
d\in(-1,\gamma-1]\cup[1,2\gamma-1)
, then the function
k\mapsto J(d,\gamma,k)
is strictly decreasing on the non-negative integers.
Thus

\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}>\left\|\bm{L}_{k+1}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}.

Consequently, in both settings, the norm of the full Leray transform is

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}=\left\|\bm{L}_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}.

Proof.

By Corollary
4.21
,
\Phi(r,q)<1
for
q\in(-\infty,0]\cup[1,\infty)
and
r>q
.
On the other hand, Lemma
3.19
says that the symbol function
J(\delta_{1-q}(\gamma),\gamma,k)
is finite for all non-negative integers
k
if and only if
|q|<\frac{\gamma}{|\gamma-2|}
.

Upon intersecting these two intervals, Lemma
3.21
says that for
\gamma\neq 2
, the function
k\mapsto J(\delta_{1-q}(\gamma),\gamma,k)
is strictly decreasing on the non-negative integers for

q\in\Big(\tfrac{-\gamma}{|\gamma-2|},0\Big]\cup\Big[1,\tfrac{\gamma}{|\gamma-2|}\Big).

Let us write

(4.24)

d=\delta_{1-q}(\gamma)=(1-q)(\gamma-2)+1

and recall that
\sqrt{J(d,\gamma,k)}=\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}
by equation (
1.10
).

Now consider four separate cases:

When
\gamma>2
and
q\in\big[1,\tfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-2}\big)
, equation (
4.24
) implies that
k\mapsto\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}
is strictly decreasing for
d\in(-1,1]
.

When
\gamma>2
and
q\in\big(\tfrac{-\gamma}{\gamma-2},0\big]
, equation (
4.24
) implies that
k\mapsto\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}
is strictly decreasing for
d\in[\gamma-1,2\gamma-1)
.

When
\gamma<2
and
q\in\big[1,\tfrac{\gamma}{2-\gamma}\big)
, equation (
4.24
) implies that
k\mapsto\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}
is strictly decreasing for
d\in[1,2\gamma-1)
.

When
\gamma<2
and
q\in\big(\tfrac{-\gamma}{2-\gamma},0\big]
, equation (
4.24
) implies that
k\mapsto\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{d})}
is strictly decreasing for
d\in(-1,\gamma-1]
.

This establishes both (1) and (2).
In all settings encompassed by these two cases, the
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},d)
-norm of
\bm{L}_{k}
decreases with
k
, implying that
\bm{L}_{0}
is the sub-Leray operator with the biggest norm.
Equation (
2.10
) now says that
\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},d)}=\left\|\bm{L}_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},d)}
.
∎

Both the pairing measure
\sigma=r^{\gamma-1}\,dr\wedge d\theta\wedge ds
(
d=\gamma-1
) and Lebesgue measure
\mu_{1}=r\,dr\wedge d\theta\wedge ds
(
d=1
) fall within the range of applicability of Theorem
4.23
.
We now record the norms of the Leray transform in both settings:

Corollary 4.25
.

Let
\sigma=r^{\gamma-1}\,dr\wedge d\theta\wedge ds
.
The Leray transform is bounded on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
with norm

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)}=\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}}.

Proof.

Since
\sigma
corresponds to
d=\gamma-1
, Theorem
4.23
applies. Thus by (
1.7
),

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)}=\left\|\bm{L}_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)}=\sqrt{C_{\sigma}(\gamma,0)}=\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}}.

Recall that the pairing symbol function
C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)=J(\gamma-1,\gamma,k)
.
∎

Remark 4.26
.

Barrett and Edholm calculated
\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)}
in
[

, Proposition 4.18]
using a different approach.
The argument given there is tailored to the pairing measure (
q=0
) and seems not to easily generalize to other measure settings.

\lozenge

Corollary 4.27
.

Let
\mu_{1}=r\,dr\wedge d\theta\wedge ds
.
The Leray transform
\bm{L}
is bounded on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{1})
with norm

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{1})}=\begin{cases}(\gamma-1)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}-1}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}(\gamma-2)\gamma\csc\big(\frac{2\pi}{\gamma}\big)},&\gamma\in(1,2)\cup(2,\infty)\\

\gamma=2
.

Proof.

Since
\mu_{1}
corresponds to

, Theorem
4.23
applies once again for

:

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{1})}=\left\|\bm{L}_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{1})}=\sqrt{J(1,\gamma,0)}

\displaystyle=(\gamma-1)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}-1}\sqrt{\big(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\big)^{2}\,\Gamma\big(\tfrac{2}{\gamma}\big)\Gamma\big(2-\tfrac{2}{\gamma}\big)}

\displaystyle=(\gamma-1)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}-1}\sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{4}(\gamma-2)\gamma\csc\big(\tfrac{2\pi}{\gamma}\big)},

where we’ve used the factorial property of the

-function as well as Euler’s reflection formula.

The
\gamma=2

3.10
) and
L’Hôpital’s rule confirms continuity.

5.
The preferred symbol function

In this section we prove that the preferred symbol function
k\mapsto C_{\nu}(\gamma,k)
,
\gamma\neq 2
, is strictly
increasing
, in stark contrast with the strictly decreasing symbol function behavior seen in the earlier parts of the paper.
Our goal is to show

\Phi(r,\tfrac{2}{3})>1,\qquad\mathrm{for}\qquad r>\tfrac{2}{3}.

Unlike the work in Section
4
, we are unable to invoke complete monotonicity and the Berstein-Widder theorem to prove this inequality, since it is known that the related function
F_{q}
is not completely monotone for
q=\frac{2}{3}
; see Remark
4.20
.

In place of the integral representations used in the previous section, we use here the infinite series description obtained from (
3.18b
):

(5.1)

\Phi\big(r,\tfrac{2}{3}\big)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{r}(j),\qquad\mathrm{where}\qquad f_{r}(j)=\frac{18r(3j-2)}{(3r+3j-2)^{3}}.

5.1.
Two tools

Two classical results are crucial to the subsequent analysis.

The first is
Descartes’ Rule of Signs
(see
[
24
]
for a simple proof):

Proposition 5.2

(Descartes)
.

Let
p
be a single variable polynomial with real coefficients, with monomial terms arranged so that exponents appear in ascending order.
The number of positive roots of
p
(counted with multiplicities) is either (i) equal to the number of sign changes between consecutive (non-zero) coefficients, or (ii) less than that by an even number.

The second is the
Euler-Maclaurin formula
relating sums and integrals; see
[
11
, Section 9.5]
.
For our purposes it is sufficient to use the following first-order version:

Proposition 5.3

(Euler-Maclaurin)
.

Let
m<n
be integers and
f\in C^{1}[m,n]
.
Then

(5.4)

\sum_{j=m+1}^{n}f(j)=\int_{m}^{n}f(x)\,dx+\frac{f(n)-f(m)}{2}+\int_{m}^{n}f^{\prime}(x)P_{1}(x)\,dx,

where
P_{1}(x)=B_{1}(x-\lfloor x\rfloor)
,
B_{1}(x)=x-\tfrac{1}{2}
is the first Bernoulli polynomial and
\lfloor x\rfloor
the greatest integer less than or equal to
x
.

We refer to the rightmost integral in (
5.4
) as the
Bernoulli integral
.

5.2.
Applying Euler-Maclaurin

We use the Euler-Maclaurin formula to re-express the sum in (
5.1
), then use carefully chosen bounds to estimate the Bernoulli integral.
After a considerable amount of analysis, the behavior of
r\mapsto\Phi\big(r,\frac{2}{3}\big)
is reduced to a problem about the positive real roots of an explicit polynomial of degree 16.
This in turn is completely understood using Descartes’ Rule of Signs and direct evaluation.

The computations in Section
5
and the Appendix involve lengthy manipulations of polynomials and rational functions. While the reader could (in principle) work these out by hand, we highly recommend the use of software when verifying these computations.
We have made a detailed and heavily notated Mathematica notebook available at the Github page of the first author.
Please follow the link given in
[
10
]
.

Theorem 5.5
.

If
r>\tfrac{2}{3}
, then
\Phi\big(r,\tfrac{2}{3}\big)=2r\psi^{\prime}\big(r+\tfrac{1}{3}\big)+r^{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}\big(r+\tfrac{1}{3}\big)>1
.

Proof.

Start with the function
f_{r}
from (
5.1
) and use the first order Euler-Maclaurin formula (
5.4
) with
m=0
and
n\to\infty
.
Computation yields the first two terms on the right side of (
5.4
):

(5.6)

\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{r}(x)\,dx=1-\frac{4}{(3r-2)^{2}},\qquad\frac{f_{r}(\infty)-f_{r}(0)}{2}=\frac{18r}{(3r-2)^{3}},

where
f_{r}(\infty)=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{r}(n)
.
Computation also shows that

f^{\prime}_{r}(x)=\frac{54r(4+3r-6x)}{(3x+3r-2)^{4}},

from which the following expression for the Bernoulli integral in (
5.4
) is calculated:

\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{\prime}_{r}(x)P_{1}(x)\,dx=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}f_{r}^{\prime}(x+N)B_{1}(x)\,dx=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{81r(9N^{2}-3N-9r^{2}-2)}{(3r+3N+1)^{3}(3r+3N-2)^{3}}.

Denote the rational function appearing in the sum above by

(5.7)

S(r,N)=\frac{81r(9N^{2}-3N-9r^{2}-2)}{(3r+3N+1)^{3}(3r+3N-2)^{3}}.

Now inserting (
5.6
) and (
5.7
) into (
5.4
), we see by (
5.1
) that

\displaystyle\Phi\big(r,\tfrac{2}{3}\big)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{r}(j)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{4}{(3r-2)^{2}}+\frac{18r}{(3r-2)^{3}}+\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}S(r,N)

(5.8)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{6r-1}{(3r+1)^{3}}+\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}S(r,N).

(Note that we have peeled off the
N=0
term from the summation.)

It thus remains to show that

(5.9)

\frac{6r-1}{(3r+1)^{3}}+\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}S(r,N)

is strictly positive for
r>\frac{2}{3}
.

5.2.1.
The function
S(r,x)

We now show that
x\mapsto S(r,x)
(
x
is for now regarded as a non-negative real variable) has a single local extrema (a maximum) on
[0,\infty)
.
Indeed,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S}{\partial x}(r,x)=\frac{243r[(-4+39r-36r^{2}+162r^{3})+(18+18r+216r^{2})x+(54-54r)x^{2}-108x^{3}]}{(3r+3x+1)^{4}(3r+3x-2)^{4}}

Write the cubic polynomial in
x
appearing in the brackets above by

(5.10)

p_{r}(x):=(-4+39r-36r^{2}+162r^{3})+(18+18r+216r^{2})x+(54-54r)x^{2}-108x^{3}.

It is easily checked that for
r>\frac{2}{3}
the coefficients of
1
and
x
are positive, while the coefficient of
x^{2}
changes sign at
r=1
, and the coefficient of
x^{3}
is a negative constant.
Thus for all
r
in this range, there is exactly one sign change in consecutive coefficients when the monomial terms of
p_{r}(x)
are considered in ascending order.
Descartes’ Rule of Signs thus guarantees a
unique
positive real root of the function
x\mapsto p_{r}(x)
, where the values of
p_{r}(x)
change from positive to negative.
Denote this root by
Q_{r}
.

We claim that
Q_{r}\in\big(\frac{3r}{2}+\frac{1}{6},\frac{3r}{2}+\frac{1}{3}\big)
: Indeed, for
r>\frac{2}{3}
,

\displaystyle p_{r}\big(\tfrac{3r}{2}+\tfrac{1}{6}\big)=81r>0,

\displaystyle p_{r}\big(\tfrac{3r}{2}+\tfrac{1}{3}\big)=4+66r-\tfrac{225}{2}r^{2}<0.

(A more precise estimate on
Q_{r}
is obtained in Lemma
A.3
.)

We see from (
5.7
) that
x\mapsto S(r,x)
starts negative, then becomes (and remains) positive for
x
large enough, since the function tends to zero as
x\to\infty
and its derivative changes signs exactly once.
In particular, the global maximum value of
S(r,Q_{r})
is positive.

We now trivially re-write the quantity in (
5.9
) as

(5.11)

\frac{6r-1}{(3r+1)^{3}}+S(r,1)+S(r,2)+\sum_{N=3}^{\infty}S(r,N).

(Separating out the
N=1
and
N=2
terms from the rest of the summation simplifies the following estimate.)
We claim that

(5.12)

\sum_{N=3}^{\infty}S(r,N)>\int_{2}^{\infty}S(r,x)\,dx-S(r,Q_{r}).

To see this, set
K:=\lfloor Q_{r}\rfloor
, the greatest integer less than or equal to
Q_{r}
.
From the discussion above,
S(r,\cdot)
increases on
(1,K)
and decreases on
(K+1,\infty)
.
We now consider two cases:
K\leq 2
and
K\geq 3
.

When
K\leq 2
, we have that
S(r,\cdot)
decreases on
(3,\infty)
, so

(5.13a)

\sum_{N=3}^{\infty}S(r,N)>\int_{3}^{\infty}S(r,x)\,dx.

But also note that

(5.13b)

0>\int_{2}^{3}S(r,x)\,dx-S(r,Q_{r}),

since any integral over an interval of unit length is overestimated by the maximum value of its integrand.
Combining (
5.13a
) with (
5.13b
) now yields (
5.12
) for
K\leq 2
.

When
K\geq 3
, we are able to write

(5.14a)

\displaystyle\sum_{N=3}^{K}S(r,N)

\displaystyle>\int_{2}^{K}S(r,x)\,dx,

(5.14b)

\displaystyle\sum_{N=K+1}^{\infty}S(r,N)

\displaystyle>\int_{K+1}^{\infty}S(r,x)\,dx.

Using reasoning identical to what justified (
5.13b
), we see that

(5.14c)

0>\int_{K}^{K+1}S(r,x)\,dx-S(r,Q_{r}).

Combining (
5.14a
), (
5.14b
) and (
5.14c
) yields (
5.12
) for
K\geq 3
.

From (
5.12
), the following quantity is a lower bound on (
5.9
) = (
5.11
):

(5.15)

\frac{6r-1}{(3r+1)^{3}}+S(r,1)+S(r,2)+\int_{2}^{\infty}S(r,x)\,dx-S(r,Q_{r}).

We now show (
5.15
) is positive by computing the above integral and estimating
S(r,Q_{r})
.

A partial fraction decomposition of
S(r,x)
helps to yield the following:

(5.16)

\int_{2}^{\infty}S(r,x)\,dx=\frac{3r(108r^{3}+594r^{2}+1035r+616)}{2(3r+4)^{2}(3r+7)^{2}}-2r\log\bigg(\frac{3r+7}{3r+4}\bigg).

We also have the following estimate on
S(r,Q_{r})
: for
r>\frac{2}{3}
,

(5.17)

S(r,Q_{r})<\frac{16}{3125r^{3}}.

The proof this estimate is given in Lemma
A.4
of the Appendix.
(The right hand side of (
5.17
) is the leading term in the Taylor expansion of
S(r,Q_{r})
at
\infty
; see Remark
A.9
.)

We now use (
5.16
) and (
5.17
) to define a new function:

\displaystyle H(r)

\displaystyle:=\frac{6r-1}{(3r+1)^{3}}+S(r,1)+S(r,2)+\int_{2}^{\infty}S(r,x)\,dx-\frac{16}{3125r^{3}}

(5.18)

\displaystyle=\frac{6r-1}{(3r+1)^{3}}+\frac{81r(4-9r^{2})}{(3r+1)^{3}(3r+4)^{3}}+\frac{81r(28-9r^{2})}{(3r+4)^{3}(3r+7)^{3}}+

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{3r(108r^{3}+594r^{2}+1035r+616)}{2(3r+4)^{2}(3r+7)^{2}}-2r\log\bigg(\frac{3r+7}{3r+4}\bigg)-\frac{16}{3125r^{3}}

Upon combining (
5.16
) with the bound (
5.17
), we see that
H(r)
is a lower bound for (
5.15
).
Our goal is now to show
H(r)
is positive for
r>\frac{2}{3}
.
Note that while
H
is itself not a rational function, its second derivative is rational.
This is crucial to the coming argument.

5.2.2.
The function
H(r)

We now present

in a more digestible fashion.
Combining all of the rational functions in (

(5.19)

H(r)=\frac{F_{1}(r)}{6250r^{3}(3r+1)^{3}(3r+4)^{3}(3r+7)^{3}}-2r\log\bigg(\frac{3r+7}{3r+4}\bigg),

where

is a polynomial of degree 12 with integer coefficients, the full formula of which is included in Table

The leading coefficient of

(written

in the table) is

Note that the denominator of the rational function in (

.
Thus,

\lim_{r\to\infty}H(r)=2-2=0.

Now differentiate

to obtain

(5.20)

H^{\prime}(r)=\frac{F_{2}(r)}{3125r^{4}(3r+1)^{4}(3r+4)^{4}(3r+7)^{4}}-2\log\bigg(\frac{3r+7}{3r+4}\bigg),

where

is a polynomial of degree 15 with integer coefficients (the full formula is also included in Table
1
). Since the denominator polynomial has degree 16, it follows that

\lim_{r\to\infty}H^{\prime}(r)=0.

Finally, differentiate

to obtain

(5.21)

where

is a polynomial of degree 16 with integer coefficients, the exact expression of which is included in Table
1
.
Since the denominator polynomial has degree 20, it follows that

\lim_{r\to\infty}H^{\prime\prime}(r)=0.

The following statements are easily verified from Table
1
below:

(1)

The coefficients of the

terms in
F_{3}
are negative.

(2)

The coefficients of the
r^{8},r^{9},\cdots,r^{16}
terms in

are positive.

(3)

Descartes’ Rule of Signs thus guarantees that

has a unique positive real root.
Since the denominator of
H^{\prime\prime}(r)
is positive for
r>0
, we see that
H^{\prime\prime}(r)
changes sign exactly once for
r>0
.

(4)

Evaluation of the rational function

shows this sign change occurs inside the interval
\big(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}\big)
:

H^{\prime\prime}\left(\tfrac{1}{3}\right)=-\frac{437\,616\,243}{25\,600\,000},\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad H^{\prime\prime}\left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right)=\frac{49\,618}{2\,278\,125}.

(5)

We conclude that
H^{\prime\prime}>0
on the interval
\big(\frac{2}{3},\infty\big)
.

(6)

Since

on
\big(\frac{2}{3},\infty\big)
,

strictly increases on this interval.
Since
\lim\limits_{r\to\infty}H^{\prime}(r)=0
, we conclude that
H^{\prime}<0
on
\big(\frac{2}{3},\infty\big)
.

(7)

Since

on

,

strictly decreases on this interval. Since
\lim\limits_{r\to\infty}H(r)=0
, we conclude that

on
\big(\frac{2}{3},\infty\big)
.

Since

is positive for

, the expression in (

.
This in turn shows that (

Thus by (

\displaystyle\Phi\big(r,\tfrac{2}{3}\big)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{r}(j)>1+H(r)>1

for

, completing the proof.
∎

5.3.
Consequences for Leray transform

We now prove that for
\gamma\neq 2
, the
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)
norm of
\bm{L}_{k}
is strictly increasing in
k
.

Theorem 5.22
.

Let
\gamma\neq 2
.
Then
k\mapsto C_{\nu}(\gamma,k)
is a strictly increasing function on the non-negative integers.
Thus,

\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)}<\left\|\bm{L}_{k+1}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)}.

Proof.

We have just seen in Theorem
5.5
that
\Phi\big(r,\tfrac{2}{3}\big)>1
for
r>\frac{2}{3}
.
Lemma
3.21
now applies, saying the preferred symbol function
k\mapsto C_{\nu}(\gamma,k)
is strictly increasing on the non-negative integers.
Thus, recalling (
1.14b
) and (
1.10
), we have

(5.23)

\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)}=\sqrt{C_{\nu}(\gamma,k)}<\sqrt{C_{\nu}(\gamma,k+1)}=\left\|\bm{L}_{k+1}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)},

completing the proof.
∎

We now easily deduce the
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)
norm of the full Leray transform.

Theorem 5.24
.

The norm of the Leray transform on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)
is given by

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}}}.

Proof of Theorem
5.24
.

The strictly increasing behavior seen in (
5.23
) combines with (
2.10
) and Proposition
1.11
to give

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu)}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}}},

completing the proof.
∎

shows that the coefficients of

P^{(14)}

(0,\tfrac{2}{3})
. Indeed,

\displaystyle P^{(14)}(0)=-\frac{2\,159\,106\,379\,702\,272}{5^{7}}\approx-2.76366\cdot 10^{10},

\displaystyle P^{(14)}\Big(\frac{2}{3}\Big)=\frac{18\,441\,535\,745\,869\,667\,168\,145\,408}{5^{7}}\approx 2.36052\cdot 10^{20}.

P^{(14)}(r)>0,\qquad r>\frac{2}{3}.

Remark A.9
.

Applying the cubic formula to the polynomial
p_{r}(x)
given in (
A.1
), we have

\displaystyle Q_{r}=\frac{1}{6\alpha_{r}}\left(3+25r^{2}+(1-r)\alpha_{r}+\alpha_{r}^{2}\right),\qquad\mathrm{where}

\displaystyle\alpha_{r}=\left(125r^{3}+36r-3\sqrt{375r^{4}+69r^{2}-3}\right)^{1/3}.

The bounds
M(r)
and
m(r)
in Lemma
A.3
come from the Taylor expansion of
Q_{r}
at
\infty
:

Q_{r}=\frac{3r}{2}+\frac{1}{6}+\frac{3}{25r}-\frac{21}{3125r^{3}}+O\Big(\frac{1}{r^{4}}\Big).

It should be emphasized that the negative degree terms in
M(r)
and
m(r)
are crucial to the proof of Lemma
A.4
; simply using affine functions to bound
Q_{r}
is not sufficient to prove the estimate on
S(r,Q_{r})
.

Similarly, the bound on
S(r,Q_{r})
also comes from its the Taylor expansion at
\infty
:

S(r,Q_{r})=\frac{16}{3125r^{3}}+O\Big(\frac{1}{r^{5}}\Big)

This method of generating candidates for sufficiently sharp bounds is likely to have application for many other polygamma inequalities, and more generally, in other problems in which the Euler-Maclaurin formula is utilized.
