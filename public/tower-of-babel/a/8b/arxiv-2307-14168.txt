Liron Cohen ORCID:0000-0002-6608-3000, Vincent Rahli ORCID:0000-0002-5914-8224

Abstract
. TT 𝒞 □ \text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}} is a generic family of effectful, extensional type theories with a forcing interpretation parameterized by modalities. This paper identifies a subclass of TT 𝒞 □ \text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}} theories that internally realizes continuity principles through stateful computations, such as reference cells. The principle of continuity is a seminal property that holds for a number of intuitionistic theories such as System T. Roughly speaking, it states that functions on real numbers only need approximations of these numbers to compute. Generally, continuity principles have been justified using semantical arguments, but it is known that the modulus of continuity of functions can be computed using effectful computations such as exceptions or reference cells. In this paper, the modulus of continuity of the functionals on the Baire space is directly computed using the stateful computations enabled internally in the theory.

Introduction

The framework
\text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}

[
Cohen+Rahli:fscd:2022
]
is a generic family of
effectful, extensional type theories with a forcing interpretation
parameterized by modalities. More concretely,
\text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
uses a general
possible-worlds forcing interpretation parameterized by an abstract
modality
\Box
, which, in turn, can be instantiated with
simple covering relations, leading to a general sheaf model. In
addition,
\text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
is parameterized by a type of time-progressing choice
operators
C
, enabling support for internal effectful
computations.

\text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
is particularly suitable for studying effectful theories, and
indeed,
\text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
was called an “unprejudiced” type theory since these
parameters can be instantiated to obtain theories that are either
“agnostic”, i.e., compatible with classical reasoning (in the sense
that classical axioms, such as the Law of Excluded Middle, can be
validated), or that are “intuitionistic”, i.e., incompatible with
classical reasoning (in the sense that classical axioms can be proven
false).

This paper uses the
\text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
framework to reason about the continuity
principle which is a seminal property in intuitionistic theories which
contradicts classical mathematics but is generally accepted by
constructivists.
Roughly speaking, the principle states that functions on real numbers
only need approximations of these numbers to compute.
Brouwer, in particular, assumed his so-called
continuity
principle for numbers
to derive that all real-valued functions on the
unit interval are uniformly
continuous
[
Kleene+Vesley:1965
,
Dummett:1977
,
Beeson:1985
,
Bridges+Richman:1987
,
Troelstra+VanDalen:1988
]
.
The continuity principle for numbers, sometimes referred to as the
weak continuity principle, states that all functions on the Baire
space (i.e.,
{\mathfrak{B}}:\mkern-2.0mu\equiv\mathsf{Nat}\rightarrow\mathsf{Nat}
, the set of
infinite sequences of numbers) have a modulus of continuity. More
concretely, given a function
F
of type

{\mathfrak{B}}\rightarrow\mathsf{Nat}
and a function
\alpha
of
type
{\mathfrak{B}}
, the principle states that
F(\alpha)

can only depend on an initial segment of
\alpha
, and the length of
the smallest such segment is the modulus of continuity of
F
at

\alpha
. This is standardly formalized as follows, where

{\mathfrak{B}}_{n}:\mkern-2.0mu\equiv\{x:\mathsf{Nat}\mid x<n\}\rightarrow\mathsf{Nat}
is
the set of finite sequences of numbers of length
n
:

\texttt{WCP}=\boldsymbol{\Pi}F\mathtt{:}{\mathfrak{B}}\rightarrow\mathsf{Nat}.\boldsymbol{\Pi}\alpha\mathtt{:}{\mathfrak{B}}.\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}n\mathtt{:}\mathsf{Nat}.\boldsymbol{\Pi}\beta\mathtt{:}{\mathfrak{B}}.(\alpha\mathtt{=}\beta\mathtt{\in}{\mathfrak{B}}_{n})\rightarrow(F(\alpha)\mathtt{=}F(\beta)\mathtt{\in}\mathsf{Nat})\|

A number of theories have been shown to satisfy Brouwer’s continuity
principle, or uniform variants, such as N-HA
ω
by
Troelstra
[
Troelstra:1973
, p.158]
, MLTT by Coquand and
Jaber
[
Coquand+Jaber:2010
,
Coquand+Jaber:2012
]
, System T by
Escardó
[
Escardo:2013
]
, CTT by Rahli and
Bickford
[
Rahli+Bickford:cpp:2016
]
, BTT by Baillon, Mahboubi and
Pedrot
[
Baillon+Mahboubi+Pedrot:csl:2022
]
, to cite only a few
(see
for further details).
These proofs often rely on a semantical forcing-based
approach
[
Coquand+Jaber:2010
,
Coquand+Jaber:2012
]
, where the
forcing conditions capture the amount of information needed when
applying a function to a sequence in the Baire space, or through
suitable models that internalize (C-Spaces in
[
Xu+Escardo:2013
]
)
or exhibit continuous behavior (e.g., dialogue trees
in
[
Escardo:2013
,
Baillon+Mahboubi+Pedrot:csl:2022
]
).

Not only can functions on the Baire space be proved to be continuous,
but using effectful computations, as for example described
in
[
Longley:1999
]
, one can
compute
the modulus of continuity of
such a function.
However, as shown for example by Kreisel
[
Kreisel:1962
, p.154]
,
Troelstra
[
Troelstra:1977b
, Thm.IIA]
, and Escardó and
Xu
[
Escardo+Xu:2015
,
Xu:phd:2015
]
, continuity is not an
extensional property in the sense that two equal functions might have
different moduli of continuity.
Therefore, to realize continuity, the existence of a modulus of continuity has to be
truncated as explained, e.g.,
in
[
Escardo+Xu:2015
,
Xu:phd:2015
,
Rahli+Bickford:cpp:2016
,
Rahli+Bickford:mscs:2017
]
,
which is what the
\|\_\|
operator achieves in
WCP
.
Following the effectful approach, continuity was shown to be realizable
in
[
Rahli+Bickford:cpp:2016
,
Rahli+Bickford:mscs:2017
]
using
exceptions.

Instead of using exceptions, a more straightforward way to compute
the modulus of continuity of a function on the Baire space is to use
reference cells. This was explained, e.g., in
[
Longley:1999
]
,
where the use of references can be seen as the programming
counterparts of the more logical forcing conditions.
The computation using references is more efficient than when
using exceptions as it allows computing the modulus of continuity of
a function
F
at a point
\alpha
simply by executing
F
on

\alpha
, while recording the highest argument that
\alpha
is
applied to, while using exceptions requires
repeatedly searching for the modulus of continuity.

Following this line of work, in this paper we show how to use stateful
computations to realize a continuity principle. This allows deriving
constructive type theories that include continuity axioms where the
modulus of continuity is internalized in the sense that it is computed
by an expression of the underlying programming language.
Concretely, we do so for
\text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
, which is
presented in more details in
Section

2
.
More precisely, we prove in this paper that all
\text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
functions are
continuous for some instances of
\Box
and
C
: namely
for “non-empty” equality modalities, and reference-like stateful
choice operators.
Our proof is for a variant of the weak continuity principle (see

3
), which we show to be inhabited by a
program that relies on a choice operator to keep track of the
modulus of continuity of a given function, following Longley’s
method
[
Longley:1999
]
.
This variant is restricted to “pure” (i.e., without side effects)
functions
F
,
\alpha
, and
\beta
, and
Section

4.1
discusses
issues arising with impure functions.

Roadmap.

After presenting in
Section

2
the main aspects of
\text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
that
are relevant to the results presented in this paper,

One key contribution of this paper, discussed in
Section

,

\text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}

Some consequences of this fact are further discussed in

.
Another key contribution, discussed in
Section

, is

\text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}

Finally,

\text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}

This section presents

The choice operators are time-progressing elements that we will in

.

The version presented here extends the one introduced
in
[

]
in particular with the following

•

An operator that allows making a choice

t_{1}\mathrel{\coloneqq}t_{2}
). Computations are performed against worlds

2.2
), and

]
already
provided computations to “read” choices from a world. However,
even though
[

]

2.3
, it did not include

\text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
’s semantics accordingly, in effect

mutability
requirement.

•

An operator to generate a “fresh” choice name

). While the version of
\text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
presented
in
[
Cohen+Rahli:fscd:2022
]
provided metatheoretical
computations to generate new choice names in the form of the

requirement presented in
Section

2.3
, it
did not provide corresponding object computations. We remedy this

\text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}

internalizes the
extendability
requirement.

•

A type that states the “purity” of an expression, i.e., that
the expression has no side effects. This will allow us to formalize

Section

4.1
provides further details.

Moreover, the version presented here differs from the one presented
in
[
Cohen+Rahli:csl:2023
]
as follows:

•

Section

2
contains further details regarding

,

2.2.
Worlds

To capture the time progression notion which underlines choice operators,
\text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
is parameterized by a
Kripke frame
[
Kripke:1963a
,
Kripke:1965
]
defined as follows:

{defi}
[

Kripke Frame]

A Kripke frame consists of a set of
worlds

W
equipped
with a reflexive and transitive binary relation
⊑
.

Let
\mathit{w}
range over
W
.
We sometimes write
\mathit{w}^{\prime}\mathrel{\hyperref@@ii[def:worlds]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\sqsupseteq}}}}\mathit{w}
for
\mathit{w}\mathrel{\hyperref@@ii[def:worlds]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\sqsubseteq}}}}\mathit{w}^{\prime}
.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathit{w}}
be the collection of predicates on world extensions, i.e., functions in

\forall\mathit{w}^{\prime}\mathrel{\hyperref@@ii[def:worlds]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\sqsupseteq}}}}\mathit{w}.{\mathbb{P}}
.
Note that due to
⊑
’s transitivity, if

P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathit{w}}
then for every
\mathit{w}^{\prime}\mathrel{\hyperref@@ii[def:worlds]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\sqsupseteq}}}}\mathit{w}
it naturally
extends to a predicate in
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathit{w}^{\prime}}
.
We further define the following notations for quantifiers.

\forall^{\sqsubseteq}_{\mathit{w}}(P)
states that
P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathit{w}}
is true for all
extensions of
\mathit{w}
, i.e.,
P\ \mathit{w}^{\prime}
holds in all worlds

\mathit{w}^{\prime}\mathrel{\hyperref@@ii[def:worlds]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\sqsupseteq}}}}\mathit{w}
.

\exists^{\sqsubseteq}_{\mathit{w}}(P)
states that
P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathit{w}}
is true at an extension
of
\mathit{w}
, i.e.,
P\ \mathit{w}^{\prime}
holds for some world

\mathit{w}^{\prime}\mathrel{\hyperref@@ii[def:worlds]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\sqsupseteq}}}}\mathit{w}
.
For readability, we sometime write
\forall^{\sqsubseteq}_{\mathit{w}}(\mathit{w}^{\prime}.P)
instead of

\forall^{\sqsubseteq}_{\mathit{w}}(\lambda\mathit{w}^{\prime}.P)
and
\exists^{\sqsubseteq}_{\mathit{w}}(\mathit{w}^{\prime}.P)
instead of

\exists^{\sqsubseteq}_{\mathit{w}}(\lambda\mathit{w}^{\prime}.P)
.

Remark
\thethm

The fresh operator used in
[

2.5.
Different Levels of Effects

As mentioned above,
\text{TT}^{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
provides types that allow capturing different
levels and kinds of effects, which we summarize here using natural
numbers as an example:

•

Read & write
: The type
\mathsf{Nat}
according to

Figure

2
is the type of potentially “fully” effectful
numbers that can both read and write, as they can modify the world
they compute against (e.g.,

(\delta\mathrel{\coloneqq}\underline{1})\mathtt{;}\delta
), and in addition can
compute to different values in successive worlds, and therefore
return different values depending on the values they read (e.g.,

!\delta
).

•

Write-only
: The type
|\mathsf{Nat}|_{{\mathtt{r}}}
according to

Figure

2
is the type of potentially effectful numbers that
can write but effectively cannot read (or in a limited way) because
they are constrained to compute to the same number in all extensions
of the current world, which therefore limits their use of reading,
as for example, reading a reference cells is likely to influence the
outcome of the computation (e.g.,

(\delta\mathrel{\coloneqq}\underline{1})\mathtt{;}\underline{0}
, which writes but
does not read).
Note that here and below, the read and write constraints are only
indicative as for example
!\delta\mathtt{;}\underline{0}
reads
but still belongs to
|\mathsf{Nat}|_{{\mathtt{r}}}
, and is considered
write-only on the basis that it is observationally equivalent to

\underline{0}
.

•

Read-only
:
|\mathsf{Nat}|_{{\mathtt{w}}}
is the type of potentially
effectful numbers that can read but effectively cannot write as

\mathsf{Nat}
is the type of numbers that can both read and write
(e.g.,
!\delta
, which reads but does not write).

•

No read or write
: The type
|\mathsf{Nat}|_{\mathtt{r}\mathtt{w}}
is the
type of potentially effectful numbers that can only use effects in
a limited way and effectively cannot read or write because the

|\_|_{{\mathtt{w}}}
operator requires computations to end in the same
worlds they started with, effectively preventing writes, while

|\_|_{{\mathtt{r}}}
requires computations to compute to the same value
in successive worlds, effectively preventing reads (e.g.,

\underline{0}
, but also
!\delta\mathtt{;}\underline{0}
, which
reads in a limited way that does not affect the computation, or

(\delta\mathrel{\coloneqq}!\delta)\mathtt{;}\underline{0}
, which writes in
a limited way that does not affect the computation).

•

Pure

|\mathsf{Nat}|_{{\mathtt{c}}}
is the type of pure natural
numbers, that do not contain choices, which is a syntactic
restriction (e.g.,
\underline{0}
but not

!\delta\mathtt{;}\underline{0}
as it contains a choice name

\delta
even though this name does not affect the computation).

We therefore obtain the following inclusions, where we write

\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{upd}}}}(\delta,\alpha)&:\mkern-2.0mu\equiv&\lambda x.(\mathtt{let}\ y\,\,{=}\,\,x\ \mathtt{in}\ ((\mathtt{if}\ !\delta\ <\ y\ \mathtt{then}\ \delta\mathrel{\coloneqq}y\ \mathtt{else}\ \mathtt{\star})\mathtt{;}\alpha(y)))\end{array}

More precisely, the following is true for any world
\mathit{w}
:

\mathit{w}\vDash\lambda F.\lambda\alpha.\langle\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha),\lambda\beta.\lambda e.\mathtt{\star}\rangle{\boldsymbol{\mathtt{\in}}}\mathsf{CONT_{p}}

The rest of this section describes the proof of this theorem.
First, we intuitively explain how

\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)
computes the modulus of continuity of a function

F
at a point
\alpha
. This is done using the following steps:

(1)

selecting, using
{\boldsymbol{\mathtt{\nu}}}
, a fresh choice name
\delta
(the
variable
x
gets replaced with the freshly generated name
\delta

when computing
mod
), with the appropriate restriction (here
a restriction that requires choices to be numbers as mentioned in

Section

2.3
);

(2)

setting
\delta
to
0
using
x\mathrel{\coloneqq}\underline{0}
(where

x
is
\delta
when this expression computes);

(3)

applying
F
to a modified version of
\alpha
,
namely
\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{upd}}}}(\delta,\alpha)
, which computes as
\alpha
, except
that in addition it increases
\delta
’s value every time
\alpha
is
applied to a number larger than the last chosen one;

(4)

returning the last chosen number using

!x
(again
x
is
\delta
when this
expression computes), increased by one in order to return a number
higher than any number
F
applies
\alpha
to.

Let us illustrate how
mod
computes through the following
example:

Example 4.1
.

Given the following expressions:

\begin{array}[]{l}F:\mkern-2.0mu\equiv\lambda\alpha.\alpha(\alpha(\underline{2}))\\
\alpha:\mkern-2.0mu\equiv\lambda n.\mathsf{succ}(n)\end{array}

the expression
\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)
computes as follows.
First, it generates a fresh name
\delta
, which is used to record the
values to which
\alpha
is applied to.
Then,
\delta
is initialized with
\underline{0}
.
It then computes
F(\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{upd}}}}(\delta,\alpha))
, i.e.,

t_{1}:\mkern-2.0mu\equiv\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{upd}}}}(\delta,\alpha)(\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{upd}}}}(\delta,\alpha)(\underline{2}))
.
Due to
upd
’s definition, the argument

t_{2}:\mkern-2.0mu\equiv\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{upd}}}}(\delta,\alpha)(\underline{2})
is evaluated,
which in turn results in the evaluation of
\underline{2}
.
Since
\underline{2}
is a value,
\delta
’s value, which is
\underline{0}
,
is compared to
\underline{2}
, and since
2>0
,
\delta
is updated with

\underline{2}
. The computation of
t_{2}
proceeds by applying
\alpha
to

\underline{2}
, which results in
\underline{3}
.
Going back to the computation of
t_{1}
, once the argument
t_{2}
has
been evaluated to
\underline{3}
,
\delta
’s value, which is now

\underline{2}
is updated with
\underline{3}
since
3>2
. The
computation of
t_{1}
then proceeds by applying
\alpha
to

\underline{3}
, which is the result returned by

F(\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{upd}}}}(\delta,\alpha))
.
Finally,
mod
reads
\delta
’s value, which is now

\underline{3}
and returns
\underline{4}
, which is higher
than the two values
\alpha
is applied to, namely
\underline{2}
and

\underline{3}
.

We divide the proof of the validity of the continuity principle, i.e.,
that it is inhabited by the expression presented in

Equation

2
, into the following three components, where

F\in{\mathfrak{B}}\rightarrow\mathsf{N}
and
\alpha\in{\mathfrak{B}}
:

•

Proving that the modulus is a number, i.e.,
\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)\in\mathsf{N}
;

•

Proving that
\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)
returns the highest number that
\alpha

is applied to in the computation it performs;

•

Given
\beta\in{\mathfrak{B}}
, proving that
F(\alpha)
and

F(\beta)
return the same number if
\alpha
and
\beta

agree up to
\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)
.

4.1.
Purity

The proof presented below relies, among other things, on the fact that

\alpha\mathtt{=}\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{upd}}}}(\delta,\alpha)\mathtt{\in}{\mathfrak{B}}
given a fresh
choice name
\delta
, where
\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{upd}}}}(\delta,\alpha)
is used to “probe”
F

in
\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)
.
This equality is however not true for

\alpha\in\mathsf{Nat}\rightarrow\mathsf{Nat}
. Consider for example the
function

\alpha:\mkern-2.0mu\equiv\lambda n.\mathtt{if}\ !\gamma\ <\ \underline{1}\ \mathtt{then}\ \underline{0}\ \mathtt{else}\ \underline{1}
,
where
\gamma
is a choice name. To prove

\alpha\mathtt{=}\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{upd}}}}(\delta,\alpha)\mathtt{\in}\mathsf{Nat}\rightarrow\mathsf{Nat}

we have to prove that

\alpha(n)\mathtt{=}\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{upd}}}}(\delta,\alpha)(n)\mathtt{\in}\mathsf{Nat}

for all
n\in\mathsf{Nat}
.
Given
n:\mkern-2.0mu\equiv(\gamma\mathrel{\coloneqq}\underline{1})\mathtt{;}\underline{1}
, the term

\alpha(n)
computes to
\underline{0}
in a world where

\gamma
is
\underline{0}
because
n
is not evaluated, while

\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{upd}}}}(\delta,\alpha)(n)
first evaluates
n
, setting

\gamma
to
\underline{1}
, and therefore computes to
\underline{1}
in
any world.
To avoid this, instead of using
\mathsf{Nat}
, we use
\mathsf{N}
,
thereby limiting the effects expressions can have.

According to
\mathsf{N}
’s semantics, which is as follows:

\mathit{w}\vDash t{\boldsymbol{\mathtt{\scriptstyle\equiv}}}t^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{\mathtt{\in}}}\mathsf{N}\iff\Box_{\mathit{w}}(\mathit{w}^{\prime}.\exists(n:{\rm Nature}).t\Rrightarrow_{\mathit{w}^{\prime}}\underline{n}\wedge t^{\prime}\Rrightarrow_{\mathit{w}^{\prime}}\underline{n})

to prove that
\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)\in\mathsf{N}
w.r.t. a world
\mathit{w}
, we
have to prove that it computes to the same number in all extensions of

\mathit{w}
. However, this will not be the case if
F
or
\alpha
have side
effects. For example, if
F
is

\lambda f.f(!{\delta_{0}})\mathtt{;}\underline{0}
, for
some choice name
\delta_{0}
, then it could happen that
f
gets applied
to
\underline{0}
in some world
\mathit{w}_{1}
if
!{\delta_{0}}

returns
\underline{0}
, and to
\underline{1}
in some world

\mathit{w}_{2}\mathrel{\hyperref@@ii[def:worlds]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\sqsupseteq}}}}\mathit{w}_{1}
if
!{\delta_{0}}
returns
\underline{1}
. As

\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)
returns the highest number that
F
applies its
argument to, then
\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)
would in this instance return
different numbers in different extensions, and would therefore not
inhabit
\mathsf{N}
.

Therefore, to validate a version of continuity which requires the
modulus of continuity to be “time-invariant” as in

Equation

1
, one can require that both
F
and

\alpha
are pure (i.e., name-free) terms.
Thanks to
\boldsymbol{\Pi_{p}}
, we get to assume that both
F
and

\alpha
are in
\mathtt{Pure}
and therefore are name-free.
Note that it would not be enough to use the following pattern:

\boldsymbol{\Pi}F\mathtt{:}{\mathfrak{B}}\rightarrow\mathsf{N}.(F\mathtt{=}F\mathtt{\in}\mathtt{Pure})\rightarrow\dots
,
because then for the continuity principle to even be a type, we would
have to prove that
F
is name-free to prove that

F\mathtt{=}F\mathtt{\in}\mathtt{Pure}
is a type, only knowing that

F\in{\mathfrak{B}}\rightarrow\mathsf{N}
, due to the semantics of equality
types, which is not true in general, and which is why we instead use

\_\cap\mathtt{Pure}
.

Let us now discuss a potential solution to avoid such a purity
requirement, as well as some difficulties it involves, which we leave
investigating to future work.
One could try to validate instead the following version of the
continuity axiom, which mixes the uses of
\mathsf{N}
and

\mathsf{Nat}
, where

{\mathfrak{B}}^{r}_{n}=\{x:\mathsf{N}\mid x<^{r}n\}\rightarrow\mathsf{N}
,
assuming the
existence of some type
x<^{r}n
that can relate an

x\in\mathsf{N}
with an
n\in\mathsf{Nat}
:

\boldsymbol{\Pi}F\mathtt{:}{\mathfrak{B}}\rightarrow\mathsf{N}.\boldsymbol{\Pi}\alpha\mathtt{:}{\mathfrak{B}}.\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}n\mathtt{:}\mathsf{Nat}.\boldsymbol{\Pi}\beta\mathtt{:}{\mathfrak{B}}.(\alpha\mathtt{=}\beta\mathtt{\in}{\mathfrak{B}}^{r}_{n})\rightarrow(F(\alpha)\mathtt{=}F(\beta)\mathtt{\in}\mathsf{N})\|

A first difficulty with this is the type
x<^{r}n
, which to prove
that it holds in some world
\mathit{w}
would require proving that
x
is
equal to all possible values that
n
can take in extensions of
\mathit{w}
.
Another related difficulty is that it is at present unclear whether
this principle can be validated constructively. More precisely, proving its
validity would require:

(1)

Proving that
\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)\in\mathsf{Nat}
, which is now straightforward.

(2)

Next, we have to prove that

\boldsymbol{\Pi}\beta\mathtt{:}{\mathfrak{B}}.(\alpha\mathtt{=}\beta\mathtt{\in}{\mathfrak{B}}^{r}_{\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)})\rightarrow(F(\alpha)\mathtt{=}F(\beta)\mathtt{\in}\mathsf{N})
,
i.e., given
\beta\in{\mathfrak{B}}
such that

\alpha\mathtt{=}\beta\mathtt{\in}{\mathfrak{B}}^{r}_{\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)}
, we have to prove

F(\alpha)\mathtt{=}F(\beta)\mathtt{\in}\mathsf{N}
.
The assumption
\alpha\mathtt{=}\beta\mathtt{\in}{\mathfrak{B}}^{r}_{\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)}
tells us
that given
k\in\mathsf{N}
such that
k<^{r}\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)
,
then
\alpha(k)\mathtt{=}\beta(k)\mathtt{\in}\mathsf{N}
.
As mentioned above, for
k<^{r}\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)
to be true, it must
be that
k
is less than
\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)
in all extensions of the
current world. However, without the purity constraint,

\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)
can compute to different numbers in different
extensions.

Going back to our goal

F(\alpha)\mathtt{=}F(\beta)\mathtt{\in}\mathsf{N}
,
given the semantics of
\mathsf{N}
presented above, to prove this it
is enough to assume that
F(\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{upd}}}}(\delta,\alpha))

computes to some number
\underline{m}
in some world
\mathit{w}
, and to
prove that
F(\beta)
also computes to
\underline{m}
in

\mathit{w}
.
We can then inspect the computation

F(\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{upd}}}}(\delta,\alpha))\Mapsto^{\mathit{w}}_{\mathit{w}_{1}}\underline{k}
,
where
\delta
is the name generated by
\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)
,
and show that it can be converted into a computation

F(\beta)\Mapsto^{\mathit{w}}_{\mathit{w}_{2}}\underline{k}
, by
replacing
\alpha(\underline{i})
with

\beta(\underline{i})
, whenever we encounter such an
expression.
To do this, we need to know that
\alpha(\underline{i})

and
\beta(\underline{i})
compute to the same number
using
\alpha\mathtt{=}\beta\mathtt{\in}{\mathfrak{B}}^{r}_{\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)}
. However, we only
know that
\underline{i}
is less than
\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)
in
\mathit{w}
, which is
not enough to use this assumption, as
\underline{i}
might be greater
than
\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)
in some other world
\mathit{w}^{\prime}
.
We can address this issue using classical logic to prove that there
exists a
\mathit{w}^{\prime}\mathrel{\hyperref@@ii[def:worlds]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\sqsupseteq}}}}\mathit{w}
such that for all
\mathit{w}^{\prime\prime}\mathrel{\hyperref@@ii[def:worlds]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\sqsupseteq}}}}\mathit{w}
,
the smallest number that
\alpha
is applied to in the computation of

\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)
w.r.t.
\mathit{w}^{\prime}
is less than the number that

\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)
computes to w.r.t.
\mathit{w}^{\prime\prime}
.
In the argument sketched above we can then use
\mathit{w}^{\prime}
instead of
\mathit{w}
.

Assumptions

4.3.
The Modulus is a Number

In this section we prove that
\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)\in\mathsf{N}

precisely, we prove the following:

(

.

If
\mathtt{namefree}(F)
,
\mathtt{namefree}(\alpha)
,

\mathit{w}\vDash F{\boldsymbol{\mathtt{\in}}}\mathsf{N}^{{\mathfrak{B}}}
, and

\mathit{w}\vDash\alpha{\boldsymbol{\mathtt{\in}}}{\mathfrak{B}}

, then

\Box_{\mathit{w}}(\mathit{w}^{\prime}.\exists(n:{\rm Nature}).\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)\Rrightarrow_{\mathit{w}^{\prime}}\underline{n})

(3)

To prove the above,

\Box_{\mathit{w}}(\mathit{w}^{\prime}.\exists(n:{\rm Nature}).F(\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{upd}}}}(\delta,\alpha))\Rrightarrow_{\mathit{w}^{\prime}}\underline{n}).

But for this we first need to start

4

Going back to the proof of

, we use

\exists(n:{\rm Nature}).\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)\Rrightarrow_{\mathit{w}_{1}}\underline{n}

for some

first have to find a number

\hyperref@@ii[def:continuity+realizer]{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}{\mathtt{mod}}}}(F,\alpha)

\underline{n}
