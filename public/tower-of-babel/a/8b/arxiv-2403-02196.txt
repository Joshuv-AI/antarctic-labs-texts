1.
Introduction and summary

In solving differential or difference equations via series solutions one typically encounters Gevrey 1 divergent series
of the form
\hat{w}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{n}a_{n}\varepsilon^{n}
in which
a_{n}\sim K\Gamma(n+\alpha)/F^{n+\alpha}
as
n\to\infty
. Hence, the growth of the coefficients is of the form ‘factorial over power’.
To make
sense of these formal series one introduces the Borel transform via
B(t)=\sum_{n}\frac{a_{n}}{\Gamma(n+\alpha)}t^{n}
. This
infinite series has a finite radius of convergence and the singularities in the finite Borel plane (the complex
t
-plane)
will cause the Stokes-phenomenon, that is, the birth of exponentially-small terms when Stokes curves are crossed.
Each of these singularities is linked to its own exponentially-small scale.
To return to the
\varepsilon
-plane one
uses the Laplace transform
\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-t/\varepsilon}B(t)\,{\rm d}t
.
For more details see, for example,
[
7
]
.

In solving
q
-difference equations, but also in the definition of the basic hypergeometric functions, we will encounter
a very different type of divergence. We will have an extra parameter
q
and in this article we will assume that
|q|<1
.
In §
2
we will introduce all the special notation.
The typical growth that we will encounter is
a_{n}\sim Kq^{-\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}/F^{n}
, as
n\to\infty
. Hence, the divergence
is much stronger, and as a consequence we have to use different Borel and Laplace transforms.
They are introduced in §
3
.
The
q
-Borel transform
is, obviously,
B_{q}(t)=\sum_{n}a_{n}q^{\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}t^{n}
. In the interesting cases it will have a finite radius of
convergence, and the singularities in the Borel plane will still cause the Stokes phenomenon, but now all the finite
singularities are linked to the same exponentially-small scale! For the corresponding
q
-Laplace transform we have
to introduce an alternative for
{\rm e}^{-t/\varepsilon}
.
The growth of this alternative exponential function
{\rm E}_{q}\left(t\right)

is, in fact, in between algebraic and exponential, and this is the reason that all the Borel singularities
are linked to the same exponentially-small scale.

In Gevrey asymptotics an optimal truncated asymptotic expansion can be re-expanded
in terms of hyperterminants. These level 1 hyperasymptotic approximations determine
uniquely the solutions not only numerically, but also asymptotically. The first
hyperterminant is the simplest function with a Stokes phenomenon in its
asymptotic tail, and as a consequence, the level 1 hyperasymptotic expansion
incorporates the Stokes phenomenon of our original problem. See
[
10
]
.

In §
4
we introduce a
q
version of the
traditional hyperterminant function.
It will be the simplest function incorporating the
q
-Stokes phenomenon
in its asymptotic tail. We will
derive many of its properties, because this
q
-hyperterminant is the main
building block in the re-expansions of the optimal truncated asymptotic expansions.
One of our main results is a complete uniform asymptotic approximation
of the
q
-hyperterminant in terms of the error functions
\operatorname{erfc}
.
In this way we demonstrate that the
q
-Stokes phenomenon also has
‘Berry-smoothing’. See
[
3
]
.

Basic hypergeometric functions
{{}_{r}\phi_{s}}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{b}};q;z\right)
, with

r>s+1
, are generally divergent with the
q
-growth mentioned above.
As a first application we will study in §
5
the formal series
{{}_{2}\phi_{0}}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a,b}{-};q;z\right)

and show that the singularities in its
q
-Borel plane are just
simple poles. We will use this information to obtain exponentially improved
asymptotic approximations.

Like the classical first Painlevé equation, which appears widely as a model in physics, every solution of its
q
-difference version

(1.1)

q{\rm P}_{\rm I}\ :\quad w(qz)w^{2}(z)w(z/q)=w(z)-z,\qquad 0<|q|<1,

is a transcendental function
[
8
]
. This
q
-difference equation appears in
Sakai’s list of discrete Painlevé equations
[
11
]
, with an initial-value
space of type
A_{7}^{(1)}
and symmetry group
A_{1}^{(1)}
, and is conjectured to
arise as a model in topological string/spectral theory
[
4
]
. Yet, hardly
anything is known about the asymptotic behaviours of its solutions near the
singularities of the equation, with one exception
[
6
]
.
In §§
6
–
10
we will study, as a second application,
the solutions of

q{\rm P}_{\rm I}
with the property
w(z)\sim z
as
|z|\to 0
. Its divergent asymptotic
expansion has again the same
q
-growth. We locate the singularities in the

q
-Borel plane (again simple poles), and we compute the corresponding
Stokes multipliers. It is surprising that the dominant Stokes multiplier
seems to be
K_{0}(q)=\left(q;q\right)_{\infty}^{-2}
. The other Stokes multipliers can be
obtained via a transseries analysis. All of them can be expressed in terms
of
K_{0}(q)
. These Stokes multipliers are the
coefficients in the
q
-Stokes phenomenon and in the level 1 re-expansions in terms of
our
q
-hyperterminant.

Two open problems are the following. (1) For the
q
-hyperterminant we give in
§
4
many of its
properties and even a full uniform asymptotic expansion across the Stokes curve, but
we still need an efficient method to numerically compute this function in the full
complex plane.

(2) We still need a rigorous proof for the observation that
K_{0}(q)=\left(q;q\right)_{\infty}^{-2}
.
In appendix
B
we do show that a similar identity holds for
the Stokes multiplier of a similar but slightly simpler
q
-difference equation.
We include initial steps that might help us for the Stokes multiplier of
q{\rm P}_{\rm I}
.

2.
q
-special functions

As mentioned in the introduction we will assume that
|q|<1
, and we use the notation

\left(a;q\right)_{\infty}=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(1-aq^{n}\right)
, and

\left(a;q\right)_{\nu}=\left(a;q\right)_{\infty}/\left(aq^{\nu};q\right)_{\infty}
,
a,\nu\in\mathbb{C}
.
Thus for
n
a positive integer we have
\left(a;q\right)_{n}=(1-a)(1-aq)\cdots(1-aq^{n-1})
.

There are two
q
-exponential functions

(2.1)

e_{q}(z)=\frac{1}{\left(z;q\right)_{\infty}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\left(q;q\right)_{n}}z^{n},\qquad|z|<1,

and

(2.2)

E_{q}(z)=\left(-z;q\right)_{\infty}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}}{\left(q;q\right)_{n}}z^{n},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Formula
[
9
, (3.11)]
gives us for
z>1
and
0<q<1

(2.3)

\left(-z;q\right)_{\infty}=\frac{q^{-\frac{1}{12}}\sqrt{z}}{\left(-q/z;q\right)_{\infty}}\exp\left(\frac{-1}{\ln q}\left(\textstyle\frac{{1}}{{2}}\displaystyle\ln^{2}z+\textstyle\frac{{1}}{{6}}\displaystyle\pi^{2}\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos\left(2\pi n\frac{\ln z}{\ln q}\right)}{n\sinh\left(2\pi^{2}n/\ln q\right)}\right).

This can be seen as an asymptotic approximation as
z\to+\infty
, with the observation
that the infinite series in (
2.3
) represents a
q
-periodic function.
Hence, the growth of
\left(-z;q\right)_{\infty}
lies in between algebraic and exponential growth.

The expansions in (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) are special cases of the
q
-binomial
theorem (see
[
5
,
17.2.37
]
) which reads

(2.4)

\frac{\left(az;q\right)_{\infty}}{\left(z;q\right)_{\infty}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(a;q\right)_{n}}{\left(q;q\right)_{n}}z^{n},\qquad|z|<1.

We will encounter divergent series when we introduce the basic hypergeometric
functions

(2.5)

{{}_{r}\phi_{s}}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a_1,\ldots,a_r}{b_1,\ldots,b_s};q;z\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(a_{1};q\right)_{n}\cdots\left(a_{r};q\right)_{n}}{\left(b_{1};q\right)_{n}\cdots\left(b_{s};q\right)_{n}\left(q;q\right)_{n}}\left(\left(-1\right)^{n}q^{\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}\right)^{1+s-r}z^{n}.

Note that in this representation the most important factor for convergence
is the
q^{\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}
. The infinite series converges for all
z
when

r<s+1
, and for
|z|<1
when
r=s+1
. In the case when
r>s+1
the
series diverges and we have to make sense of this series via the

q
-Borel-Laplace transform introduced in the next section.

3.
The
q
-Borel-Laplace transform

Poincaré divergent series are typical of the form
\sum n!z^{n}
. We will see in this article
that in the
q
-world we encounter divergent series of the form

\sum q^{-\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}z^{n}
. Hence, the divergence is much stronger. Let

\hat{w}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
be a formal series with

|a_{n}|\leq\left|L^{n}q^{-\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}\right|
,
for large
n
. Then the obvious
q
-Borel transform is

B_{q}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}q^{\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}t^{n}
. This infinite series will typically
converge for
|t|<\widetilde{L}
, and ideally we are able to obtain information about
all the singularities of the analytical continuation of
B_{q}(t)
in the complex

t
-plane. Via a
q
-Laplace transform we will go back to the
z
-plane and obtain
a
q
-Borel-Laplace transform of formal series
\hat{w}(z)
.

In the literature there are many versions of the
q
-Laplace transform.
See for example
[
12
]
.
We will consider only the ones that are in terms of normal integrals.
Two of them are defined in
[
13
]
and
[
14
]
. We will have
to introduce the functions

(3.1)

{\rm E}_{q}\left(\tau\right)=\exp\left(\frac{\left(\ln(\tau/\sqrt{q})\right)^{2}}{2\ln q}\right)=q^{\frac{1}{8}}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(\frac{\ln^{2}\tau}{2\ln q}\right),

and the theta function with base
q
is

(3.2)

\theta_{q}\left(\tau\right)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}q^{\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}\tau^{n}=(q,-\tau,-q/\tau;q)_{\infty}.

Both
F(\tau)={\rm E}_{q}\left(\tau\right)
and
F(\tau)=1/\theta_{q}\left(\tau\right)
have
the properties

(3.3)

F\left(\tau^{-1}\right)=\tau F\left(\tau\right),\qquad F\left(q^{n}\tau\right)=\tau^{n}q^{\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}F\left(\tau\right).

In the case of
F(\tau)={\rm E}_{q}\left(\tau\right)
the second property holds for all
n\in\mathbb{C}
,
and in the case of
F(\tau)=1/\theta_{q}\left(\tau\right)
it holds
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. We obtain

(3.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\theta_{q}\left(\tau\right)}\frac{\,{\rm d}\tau}{\tau}&=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{q^{n+1}}^{q^{n}}\frac{1}{\theta_{q}\left(\tau\right)}\frac{\,{\rm d}\tau}{\tau}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{q}^{1}\frac{1}{\theta_{q}\left(q^{n}t\right)}\frac{\,{\rm d}t}{t}\\
&=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{q}^{1}\frac{t^{n}q^{\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}}{\theta_{q}\left(t\right)}\frac{\,{\rm d}t}{t}=\int_{q}^{1}\frac{\,{\rm d}t}{t}=-\ln q,\end{split}

and more importantly

(3.5)

C_{q}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{n-1}{\rm E}_{q}\left(t/z\right)\,\,{\rm d}t=\frac{-1}{\ln q}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n-1}}{\theta_{q}\left(t/z\right)}\,{\rm d}t=q^{-\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}z^{n},

in which

(3.6)

C_{q}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-2\pi\ln q}}.

From (
3.3
) we obtain that the function
\tau\mapsto\theta_{q}(\tau){\rm E}_{q}\left(\tau\right)

is
q
-periodic, which also follows from (
2.3
) because we have

(3.7)

\theta_{q}\left(\tau\right){\rm E}_{q}\left(\tau\right)=q^{\frac{1}{24}}\left(q;q\right)_{\infty}\exp\left(\frac{-\pi^{2}}{6\ln q}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos\left(2\pi n\frac{\ln\tau}{\ln q}\right)}{n\sinh\left(2\pi^{2}n/\ln q\right)}\right).

From the definition of
{\rm E}_{q}\left(\tau\right)
it follows that its growth lies
in between algebraic and exponential as
|\tau|\to\infty

but also as
\tau\to 0
. The right-hand side of (
3.7
) is
q
-periodic. Hence,

1/\theta_{q}\left(\tau\right)
has the same growth property.

Now we have all the tools to introduce the
q
-Laplace transforms for a function
B_{q}(t)
:

(3.8)

w(z)=C_{q}\int_{0}^{\infty}B_{q}(t){\rm E}_{q}\left(t/z\right)\frac{\,{\rm d}t}{t},

(3.9)

\widetilde{w}(z)=\frac{-1}{\ln q}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{q}(t)}{\theta_{q}\left(t/z\right)}\frac{\,{\rm d}t}{t}.

It follows from (
3.5
) that both of these functions have the asymptotic
expansion
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
as
z\to 0
.

We will mainly use Laplace transform (
3.8
), because it will give us a bit more freedom.
One reason is that the second property of (
3.3
) holds for all
n\in\mathbb{C}
.
However, most of the results below also hold if we use (
3.9
).

4.
The
q
-hyperterminant function

The hyperterminant function that we introduce in this section is a
q
version of the ones that we
use in hyperasymptotics. See for example
[
10
]
.

Let

(4.1)

F_{q}\left(z;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{\sigma}\right)=\sigma^{1-N}C_{q}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{N-1}{\rm E}_{q}\left(t\right)}{t+\sigma z}\,{\rm d}t,\qquad\qquad|\arg(\sigma z)|<\pi.

Then we have the following identities:

(4.2)

F_{q}\left(z;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{\sigma}\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\frac{\left(-1\right)^{m}}{\sigma^{N+m}z^{m+1}}q^{-\frac{1}{2}(N+m)(N+m-1)}+\left(-z\right)^{-M}F_{q}\left(z;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N+M}{\sigma}\right),

that is,
z^{-1}\sigma^{-N}q^{-\frac{1}{2}N(N-1)}{{}_{2}\phi_{0}}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0,q}{-};q;\frac{1}{\sigma zq^{N}}\right)

and
F_{q}\left(z;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{\sigma}\right)
have the same formal expansion.

(4.3)

F_{q}\left(z;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{\sigma q^{-j}}\right)=\frac{q^{\frac{1}{2}j(j+1)}}{\sigma^{j}}F_{q}\left(z;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N-j}{\sigma}\right),

and hence we can make the
\sigma
-entry always
1

(4.4)

F_{q}\left(z;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{\sigma}\right)=\sigma^{\frac{\ln\sigma}{2\ln q}-\frac{1}{2}}F_{q}\left(z;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N+\frac{\ln\sigma}{\ln q}}{1}\right),

or make the
N
-entry 1 via

(4.5)

F_{q}\left(z;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{\sigma}\right)=\sigma^{1-N}q^{-\frac{1}{2}(N-1)(N-2)}F_{q}\left(z;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{\sigma q^{N-1}}\right).

Recurrence relations:
w(z)=F_{q}\left(z;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{\sigma}\right)
does satisfy

(4.6)

q^{1-N}w(zq)+z\sigma w(z)=\sigma^{1-N}q^{-\frac{1}{2}N(N-1)},

and hence

(4.7)

q^{1-N}w(zq)+\left(\sigma z-q^{1-N}\right)w(z)-\frac{\sigma z}{q}w(z/q)=0.

Note that
w(z)=z^{N-1}{\rm E}_{q}\left(-\sigma z\right)
is an ‘elementary’ solution of (
4.7
).
Compare the right-hand side of (
4.11
).
(This observation can be used to derive (
4.6
) from (
4.7
).)
It follows from (
4.2
) and
(
3.1
) that, unfortunately,
w(z)=F_{q}\left(z;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{\sigma}\right)

is a dominant solution of (
4.7
) for both
z\to 0
and
z\to\infty
.
Corresponding normalising conditions are

(4.8)

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\sigma zq^{N-1}\right)^{-j}}{(q;q)_{j}}F_{q}\left(zq^{j};\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{\sigma}\right)=\frac{\sigma^{-N}q^{-\frac{1}{2}N(N-1)}}{z\left(\frac{q^{1-N}}{\sigma z};q\right)_{\infty}},\qquad\qquad\left|\sigma zq^{N-1}\right|>1,

(4.9)

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\sigma zq^{N-2}\right)^{j}}{(q;q)_{j}}F_{q}\left(zq^{-j};\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{\sigma}\right)=\frac{\sigma^{1-N}q^{-\frac{1}{2}(N-1)(N-2)}}{\left(\sigma zq^{N-1};q\right)_{\infty}},\qquad\qquad\left|\sigma zq^{N-1}\right|<1.

Reflection formula

(4.10)

z\sigma F_{q}\left(z;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{\sigma}\right)=F_{q}\left(\frac{1}{z};\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{2-N}{\frac 1{\sigma}}\right).

Combining this reflection formula with (
4.2
) we identify

F_{q}\left(1;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\frac 12}{1}\right)=F_{q}\left(1;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\frac 32}{1}\right)=\frac{1}{2}q^{\frac{1}{8}}
.

Stokes phenomenon:

(4.11)

F_{q}\left(z{\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}};\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{\sigma}\right)-F_{q}\left(z;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{\sigma}\right)=2\pi{\rm i}C_{q}\left(-z\right)^{N-1}{\rm E}_{q}\left(-\sigma z\right).

Stokes smoothing: Taking
|z|
large and
N=\left|\frac{\ln(z\sigma)}{\ln q}\right|+{\mathcal{O}}(1)
, then

(4.12)

\displaystyle\begin{split}F_{q}\left(z;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{\sigma}\right)&\sim-\pi{\rm i}C_{q}\left(-z\right)^{N-1}{\rm E}_{q}\left(-\sigma z\right)\operatorname{erfc}\left({\rm i}\tau_{p}N/\sqrt{2}\right)\\
&\qquad+\frac{q^{-\frac{1}{2}N(N-1)}}{\sigma^{N}z}\left(\frac{1}{1+\frac{q^{\frac{1}{2}-N}}{\sigma z}}-\frac{1}{\ln\left(-\sigma zq^{N-\frac{1}{2}}\right)}\right),\end{split}

as
z\to\infty
, with
\tau_{p}=\frac{\ln\left(-\sigma zq^{N-\frac{1}{2}}\right)}{N\sqrt{-\ln q}}
. Proof:

Below we use the substitution

t=q^{\frac{1}{2}-N}{\rm e}^{\tau N\sqrt{-\ln q}}
and obtain

(4.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}F_{q}\left(z;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{\sigma}\right)&=\frac{q^{\frac{1}{8}}C_{q}}{\sigma^{N-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{N-\frac{3}{2}}{\rm e}^{\frac{\ln^{2}t}{2\ln q}}}{t+\sigma z}\,{\rm d}t\\
&=\frac{N\sqrt{-\ln q}C_{q}}{\sigma^{N}zq^{\frac{1}{2}N(N-1)}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{2}N^{2}\tau^{2}}f(\tau)}{\tau_{p}-\tau}\,{\rm d}\tau\\
&=\frac{N\sqrt{-\ln q}C_{q}}{\sigma^{N}zq^{\frac{1}{2}N(N-1)}}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{2}N^{2}\tau^{2}}f(\tau_{p})}{\tau_{p}-\tau}\,{\rm d}\tau+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{2}N^{2}\tau^{2}}g(\tau)\,{\rm d}\tau\right)\\
&\sim-\pi{\rm i}C_{q}\left(-z\right)^{N-1}{\rm E}_{q}\left(-\sigma z\right)\operatorname{erfc}\left({\rm i}\tau_{p}N/\sqrt{2}\right)+\frac{g(0)}{\sigma^{N}zq^{\frac{1}{2}N(N-1)}},\end{split}

in which we have used

(4.14)

f(\tau)=\frac{\tau_{p}-\tau}{1-\exp((\tau-\tau_{p})N\sqrt{-\ln q})},\qquad g(\tau)=\frac{f(\tau)-f(\tau_{p})}{\tau_{p}-\tau},\qquad f(\tau_{p})=\frac{1}{N\sqrt{-\ln q}},

and the identity

(4.15)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{2}N^{2}\tau^{2}}}{\tau_{p}-\tau}\,{\rm d}\tau=\pi{\rm i}{\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{2}N^{2}\tau_{p}^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}\left({\rm i}\tau_{p}N/\sqrt{2}\right).

In fact it is easy to obtain more terms in the uniform asymptotic expansion
via the Taylor-series of
g(\tau)
about
\tau=0
, because

g(\tau)=g_{1}(\tau)-g_{2}(\tau)
with
g_{1}(\tau)=\frac{1}{1-\exp((\tau-\tau_{p})N\sqrt{-\ln q})}

and
g_{2}(\tau)=\frac{f(\tau_{p})}{\tau_{p}-\tau}
. The Taylor series for
g_{2}(\tau)
is just the
geometric progression, and for
g_{1}(\tau)
we can use the nonlinear differential equation

g_{1}^{\prime}(\tau)=N\sqrt{-\ln q}\left(g_{1}^{2}(\tau)-g_{1}(\tau)\right)
.
We write
\displaystyle g_{1}(\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}\tau^{n}
, and obtain

(4.16)

c_{0}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{q^{\frac{1}{2}-N}}{\sigma z}},\qquad(n+1)c_{n+1}=N\sqrt{-\ln q}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{n}c_{m}c_{n-m}-c_{n}\right),

n=0,1,2,\ldots
. This results in the uniform asymptotic expansion

(4.17)

\displaystyle\begin{split}F_{q}\left(z;\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{\sigma}\right)&\sim-\pi{\rm i}C_{q}\left(-z\right)^{N-1}{\rm E}_{q}\left(-\sigma z\right)\operatorname{erfc}\left({\rm i}\tau_{p}N/\sqrt{2}\right)\\
&\qquad+\frac{q^{-\frac{1}{2}N(N-1)}}{\sigma^{N}z}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2^{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)_{n}}{N^{2n}}\left(c_{2n}-\frac{\tau_{p}^{-2n-1}}{N\sqrt{-\ln q}}\right).\end{split}

5.
Exponentially-improved asymptotics for
{}_{2}\phi_{0}

We did mention just below (

) that for the definition of
{}_{r}\phi_{s}
,
when

, the

-Borel-Laplace transform is needed to make sense of these formal series.
In

In this section we give details for the case

. We do give the Stokes phenomenon
for this function, and via exponentially-improved asymptotics we do define this

We start with the formal series

(5.1)

\hat{w}(z)={{}_{2}\phi_{0}}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a,b}{-};q;z\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(a;q\right)_{n}\left(b;q\right)_{n}}{\left(q;q\right)_{n}}q^{-\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}\left(-z\right)^{n},

which does satisfy the recurrence relation

(5.2)

\left(1-abqz\right)\hat{w}(zq^{2})-\left(1-(a+b)zq\right)\hat{w}(zq)-zq\hat{w}(z)=0.

Its
q
-Borel transform is

(5.3)

B_{q}(t)={{}_{2}\phi_{1}}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a,b}{0};q;-t\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(a;q\right)_{n}\left(b;q\right)_{n}}{\left(q;q\right)_{n}}\left(-t\right)^{n},\qquad\qquad|t|<1,

with recurrence relation

(5.4)

abt\,B_{q}(tq^{2})-\left(1+(a+b)t\right)B_{q}(tq)+(1+t)B_{q}(t)=0.

To obtain information about the singularities in the complex
t
-plane we use
Heine’s second transformation

[
5
,
17.6.7
]
which can be presented
as

(5.5)

B_{q}(t)={{}_{2}\phi_{1}}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a,b}{0};q;-t\right)=\frac{\left(-bt;q\right)_{\infty}}{\left(-t;q\right)_{\infty}}{{}_{1}\phi_{1}}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{b}{-bt};q;-at\right).

Hence, we obtain that
B_{q}(t)
has simple poles at
t=-q^{-j}
,

j=0,1,2,\ldots
, with local behaviour

(5.6)

B_{q}(t)\approx\frac{d_{j}(a,b)}{1+tq^{j}},\qquad\qquad j=0,1,2,\ldots,

with
d_{-1}(a,b)=0
,
d_{0}(a,b)=\frac{\left(a;q\right)_{\infty}\left(b;q\right)_{\infty}}{\left(q;q\right)_{\infty}}

and from (
5.4
) we obtain the recurrence relation

(5.7)

\left(1-q^{-j}\right)d_{j}=\left(1-(a+b)q^{-j}\right)d_{j-1}+abq^{-j}d_{j-2},\qquad\qquad j=1,2,3,\ldots.

This information can be used in the Cauchy integral representation for the
n
th
coefficient in (
5.3
) and this gives us the large
n
asymptotic expansion
for the late coefficients:

(5.8)

\frac{\left(a;q\right)_{n}\left(b;q\right)_{n}}{\left(q;q\right)_{n}}\sim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}d_{j}(a,b)q^{jn},\qquad\qquad{\rm as}~n\to\infty.

More importantly, we do obtain
q
-Borel-Laplace transforms of formal series

{{}_{2}\phi_{0}}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a,b}{-};q;z\right)
:

(5.9)

w(z)=C_{q}\int_{0}^{\infty}{{}_{2}\phi_{1}}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a,b}{0};q;-t\right){\rm E}_{q}\left(t/z\right)\frac{\,{\rm d}t}{t}.

Let us for the moment assume that
0<q<1
.
This integral representation is well defined for all
\arg z
, that is, for
z

on the Riemann surface of the complex logarithm. When we push the
contour of integration across the poles at, say
t={\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}}q^{-j}
we obtain

(5.10)

{w}\left({\rm e}^{-2\pi{\rm i}}z\right)-{w}(z)\sim 2\pi{\rm i}C_{q}{\rm E}_{q}\left(-1/z\right)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{n}(a,b)q^{\frac{1}{2}n(n+1)}\left(-z\right)^{n},

as
z\to 0
. This is the Stokes phenomenon.

To obtain the exponentially improved expansion we start with the Cauchy integral
representation of a truncated version of (
5.3
)

(5.11)

B_{q}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\left(a;q\right)_{n}\left(b;q\right)_{n}}{\left(q;q\right)_{n}}\left(-t\right)^{n}+\frac{t^{N}}{2\pi{\rm i}}\oint_{\{0,t\}}\frac{\tau^{-N}B_{q}(\tau)}{\tau-t}\,{\rm d}\tau,

in which the closed contour of integration encloses
0
and
t
, but not the poles
at
\tau=-q^{-j}
. We ‘blow-up’ the contour of integration to include the contributions
of the simple poles as well and obtain

(5.12)

B_{q}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\left(a;q\right)_{n}\left(b;q\right)_{n}}{\left(q;q\right)_{n}}\left(-t\right)^{n}+\left(-t\right)^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{d_{j}(a,b)q^{(N-1)j}}{t+q^{-j}}.

We use this result in (
5.9
) and obtain for the
q
-Borel-Laplace
transform the re-expansion

(5.13)

w(z)\sim\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\left(a;q\right)_{n}\left(b;q\right)_{n}}{\left(q;q\right)_{n}}q^{-\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}\left(-z\right)^{n}-\left(-z\right)^{N-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}d_{j}(a,b)F_{q}\left(\frac{1}{z};\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{q^{-j}}\right),

as
z\to 0
. In the case that we take
|z|
small and take the optimal number of terms

N=\left|\frac{\ln z}{\ln q}\right|+{\mathcal{O}}(1)
, then (
5.13
) is an
exponentially improved expansion, that is, we do incorporate exponentially small
terms that do capture the Stokes phenomenon (
5.10
).

8.
The Stokes phenomenon and exponentially improved asymptotics

From the details above it should be obvious that both
v_{\rm I}(z)
and

v_{\rm I}\left({\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}}z\right)
have the same asymptotic
expansion (
6.2
). Hence, the difference has to be exponentially small.
Starting with
\arg z\in(0,2\pi)
we can combine the
q
-Borel-Laplace
integral representations of both functions into the same integral, but with a contour
of integration that encircles the simple poles of
B_{\rm I}(t)
. In this way we obtain
the Stokes phenomenon

(8.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}v_{\rm I}(z)-v_{\rm I}\left({\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}}z\right)&\sim 2\pi{\rm i}C_{q}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q^{j}K_{j}(q){\rm E}_{q}\left(q^{-j}z^{-1}\right)\\
&=2\pi{\rm i}C_{q}{\rm E}_{q}\left(z^{-1}\right)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q^{\frac{1}{2}j(j+3)}K_{j}(q)z^{j}.\end{split}

One important observation is the following. If the singularities
t=-q^{-j}
would
have been singularities in a normal Borel plane, then they would contribute at
different exponential levels, however, here it should be obvious from the right-hand
side of (
8.1
) that they contribute at the same exponential
level. It seems from §
7
that
K_{j}(q)=q^{-j^{2}}{\mathcal{O}}(1)
, as
j\to\infty
.
If that is the case then the sums in (
8.1
) will be divergent.

With an analysis that is very similar to the one at the end of §
5
we
obtain for the
q
-Borel-Laplace transform the re-expansion

(8.2)

v_{\rm I}(z)\sim\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}c_{n}z^{n}-z^{N-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q^{j}K_{j}(q)F_{q}\left(\frac{-1}{z};\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{q^{-j}}\right),

as
z\to 0
. In the case that we take
|z|
small and take the optimal number of terms

N=\left|\frac{\ln z}{\ln q}\right|+{\mathcal{O}}(1)
, then (
8.2
) is an
exponentially improved expansion, that is, we do incorporate exponentially small
terms that do capture the Stokes phenomenon (
8.1
).

9.
Transseries analysis

We will have a look at exponentially small terms by using a transseries
v(z)=v_{0}(z)+v_{1}(z)+v_{2}(z)+\cdots

in (
6.1
), in which
v_{0}(z)\sim\widehat{v_{\rm I}}(z)
, see (

),

is exponentially small,
v_{2}(z)
is double exponentially small, and so on.
For

we obtain the linear

-difference equation

v_{0}(qz)v_{0}^{2}(z)v_{1}(z/q)+2v_{0}(qz)v_{0}(z)v_{0}(z/q)v_{1}(z)+v_{0}(z/q)v_{0}^{2}(z)v_{1}(qz)=z^{-1}v_{1}(z).

Using the fact that
v_{0}(z)\sim 1
we have approximately

(9.2)

v_{1}(z/q)+2v_{1}(z)+v_{1}(qz)\approx z^{-1}v_{1}(z).

If (
8.1
) is correct then we should have

(9.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}v_{1}(z)\approx C{\rm E}_{q}\left(z^{-1}\right)\Longrightarrow v_{1}(z/q)&\approx\frac{C}{z}{\rm E}_{q}\left(z^{-1}\right),~~v_{1}(qz)\approx Cqz{\rm E}_{q}\left(z^{-1}\right),\end{split}

with
C=2\pi{\rm i}C_{q}K_{0}\left(q\right)
.
We observe that we have
v_{1}(z/q)\approx z^{-1}v_{1}(z)
,
and these two terms are the dominant terms in (
9.2
), as
z\to 0
.

We can also use this analysis to confirm that in the
q
-Borel plane we will have simple poles at
t=q^{-j}
: We are looking
for exponentially-small solutions of (
9.2
). The principle of dominant balance does show us that
the only acceptable balance is
v_{1}(z/q)=z^{-1}v_{1}(z)
, and this has the general solution

v_{1}(z)=P(z)/\theta_{q}\left(z^{-1}\right)
,
with
P(z)
being
q
-periodic, that is,
P(qz)=P(z)
. Recall (
3.7
).
Once we have this dominant behaviour we obtain a formal solution of (
9.1
) of the form
of the right-hand side of (
8.1
) with undetermined, and potentially

q
-periodic, coefficients
K_{j}(q)
.
Hence, the
B_{\rm I}(t)
in integral representation (
6.5
) should have simple poles at
t=q^{-j}
.

When we take for
v_{1}(z)
the right-hand side of (
8.1
), use it in (
9.1
),
and compare the
z
-Taylor coefficients we obtain the equations

(9.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(q+4\right)K_{0}+q\left(1-q\right)K_{1}&=0,\\
\left(q^{4}+2q^{3}+8q^{2}+7q+4\right)K_{0}+q^{2}\left(2+3q\right)K_{1}+q^{4}\left(1-q^{2}\right)K_{2}&=0,\\

+q^{3}\left(2+7q+7q^{2}+4q^{3}+2q^{4}\right)K_{1}+q^{6}\left(2+q+2q^{2}\right)K_{2}+q^{9}\left(1-q^{3}\right)K_{3}&=0,\end{split}

confirming (

), (

) and (

).
Hence, all the

, can be computed via this method.

To go a bit deeper in the exponential asymptotics, and to determine the singularities
that appear on the boundary of the sector of validity of the dominant asymptotic
expansion we present the transseries as

(9.5)

v(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C^{n}(z)v_{n}(z),

where the free
q
-periodic function

keeps track of the exponential scale.
We substitute this into the original equation (

) and at level

we obtain

Lemma A.1

For the sequence

Thus
\left|q^{p(n,m,k,\ell)}\right|\leq 1
.

where the sequence

is a solution of

[

&=\frac{{\rm e}}{\pi\sqrt{3}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}(3n+1)}\left(\frac{256}{27}\right)^{n}\leq\left(\frac{256}{27}\right)^{n}\end{split}

using

n^{n+1/2}{\rm e}^{-n}\sqrt{2\pi}\leq n!\leq n^{n+1/2}{\rm e}^{-n}{\rm e}.

d_{n}(q)
is a polynomial of degree
n(n-1)
with positive integer coefficients, and the first
n

coefficients are the same for
d_{n},d_{n+1},d_{n+2},\ldots
. Hence,
d_{n}(0)=1
. We also have the symmetry

d_{n}(q)=q^{n(n-1)}d_{n}(1/q)
.

Proof.

The statement about the degree and the positive integer coefficients follows, via induction, from recurrence relation
(
A.1
). Let
n\geq 2
. We do observe from (
A.2
) that
p(n,m,k,\ell)=0
only when
m=k=\ell=0
,
and the smallest non-zero value for
p(n,m,k,\ell)
is
n
, and this happens when
(m,k,\ell)=(1,0,0)
,

(m,k,\ell)=(0,0,1)
,
(m,k,\ell)=(n,0,0)
, and
(m,k,\ell)=(0,0,n)
. Hence, (
A.1
) can be presented as

(A.8)

d_{n+1}-d_{n}=q^{n}\left(2d_{n-1}+2d_{n}\right)+\cdots,\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad d_{n+1}-d_{n}=4q^{n}+h.o.t.

∎

Lemma A.3
.

For coefficients defined in (
A.1
) we use the notation

(A.9)

d_{n}(q)=\sum_{m=0}^{n(n-1)}d_{n,m}q^{m}.

Then for
|q|\leq\frac{27}{256}
the Stokes multiplier
K_{0}(q)
has the expansion

(A.10)

K_{0}(q)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d_{n+1,n}q^{n},

and we have

(A.11)

c_{n}(q)\sim q^{-\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}K_{0}(q),

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

In Lemma
A.1
we showed that
\left|d_{n}(q)\right|\leq M^{n}
with
M=\frac{256}{27}
, and in Lemma
A.2
we showed that

d_{n+1}(q)-d_{n}(q)=4q^{n}+{\mathcal{O}}(q^{n+1})

for
n\geq 2
. Thus by Cauchy’s formula

(A.12)

d_{n+1}(q)-d_{n}(q)=\frac{q^{n}}{2\pi{\rm i}}\oint_{|t|=1}\frac{d_{n+1}(t)-d_{n}(t)}{(t-q)t^{n}}\,{\rm d}t

for
|q|<1
and
n\geq 2
. Then

(A.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}|d_{n+1}(q)-d_{n}(q)|&\leq\frac{\left|q\right|^{n}}{2\pi}\oint_{|t|=1}\frac{|d_{n+1}(t)-d_{n}(t)|}{|t-q|\left|t\right|^{n}}|\,{\rm d}t|\\
&\leq 2M^{n+1}\frac{\left|q\right|^{n}}{2\pi}\oint_{|t|=1}\frac{|\,{\rm d}t|}{|t-q|}=2M\frac{\left(M|q|\right)^{n}}{1-|q|}\end{split}

for
|q|<1
and
n\geq 2
. Fix
\rho\in(0,1)
and assume that
|q|\leq\frac{\rho}{M}<\frac{1}{M}
and
m>n\geq 2
. Then

(A.14)

\displaystyle\begin{split}|d_{m}(q)-d_{n}(q)|&\leq\sum_{k=n}^{m-1}|d_{k+1}(q)-d_{k}(q)|\leq\frac{2M}{1-|q|}\sum_{k=n}^{m-1}\left(M|q|\right)^{k}\\
&=\frac{2M}{1-|q|}\frac{\left(M|q|\right)^{n}-\left(M|q|\right)^{m}}{1-M|q|}\leq\frac{2M^{2}}{M-1}\frac{\rho^{n}}{1-\rho}.\end{split}

Thus
\{d_{n}(q)\}
is uniformly Cauchy on compact subsets of
|q|<\frac{1}{M}
. Therefore, it has a limit that is analytic on
|q|<\frac{1}{M}
. We can call it
K_{0}(q)
. Taking
m\to\infty
in the above, we have

(A.15)

|K_{0}(q)-d_{n}(q)|\leq\frac{2M^{2}}{M-1}\frac{\left(M|q|\right)^{n}}{1-\rho}

for
|q|\leq\frac{\rho}{M}<\frac{1}{M}
and
n\geq 2
. We can use this and induction to show that both (
A.10
)
and (
A.11
) hold.
It would be nice to show that
K_{0}(q)
is actually analytic in
|q|<1
.
∎

Appendix B
A simple nonlinear equation

The nonlinear equation

(B.1)

w(z)w(z/q)=w(z)-z,

is a much simpler version of (
1.1
), and a formal divergent solution
is of the form
\displaystyle\widehat{w}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\widetilde{c_{n}}z^{n}
.
We use the ‘Riccati’-ansatz
\displaystyle w(z)=z\frac{h(z)}{h(qz)}

and obtain for
h(z)
the linear equation

h(z)-h(qz)=\frac{z}{q}h(z/q)
. In this way we obtain the solution

(B.2)

w(z)=z\frac{{{}_{2}\phi_{0}}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0,0}{-};q;-z/q\right)}{{{}_{2}\phi_{0}}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0,0}{-};q;-z\right)},

which can be used to prove that

(B.3)

\widetilde{c_{n}}\sim\frac{q^{-\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}}{\left(q;q\right)_{\infty}},

as
n\to\infty
.

We try a similar ‘Riccati’-ansatz for our main equation (
6.1
)
via
\displaystyle v(z)=\frac{h(z)}{h(qz)}
and obtain the equation

(B.4)

zh(z)h(z/q)=h(z)h(zq^{2})-h(zq)h(zq^{2}).

(Is it possible to make one more step and obtain a linear equation?)
We are interested in the formal solution

\displaystyle\widehat{h}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e_{n}z^{n}
.
The coefficients have the recurrence relation

(B.5)

\left(1-q^{n}\right)e_{n}=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}e_{m}e_{n-m-1}q^{-m}-\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}e_{m}e_{n-m}\left(q^{2m}-q^{n+m}\right),

with
e_{0}
arbitrary. We take
e_{0}=1
. These
e_{n}
have the same behaviour as
our main
c_{n}
, but the recurrence relation (
B.5
) seems
much simpler than (
6.3
). We take
e_{n}=q^{-\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}f_{n}
.
These
f_{n}
are very similar to
d_{n}
: They do satisfy

(B.6)

\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}f_{m}f_{n-m-1}q^{(m+1)(n-m-1)}=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}f_{m}f_{n-m}q^{m(n-m+2)}\left(1-q^{n-m}\right),

and again
f_{5}=1+2q+5q^{2}+10q^{3}+20q^{4}+34q^{5}+\cdots
. Ideally we show now that

(B.7)

f_{n}\sim\frac{1}{\left(q;q\right)_{\infty}^{2}},\qquad\qquad{\rm or}\qquad\qquad f_{n}=\frac{1}{\left(q;q\right)_{n}^{2}}+{\mathcal{O}}\left(q^{n}\right),

as
n\to\infty
. Taylor series expansions, and numerics does show that (
B.7
)
is correct.
