Abstract
. Motivated by the work of Pandey, Ofek, and Shalit on the one hand and deformation theory on the other, we study the Grassmannian of n n -dimensional multiplier-coinvariant subspaces of the Drury-Arveson space. We show that this space admits a natural map to the symmetrized polyball that induces an isomorphism between the configuration space of n n points in the ball and the subspace of projection onto spaces spanned by n n distinct kernels. We discuss the tautological bundle on our Grassmannian and the corresponding operator algebra bundle. We construct examples of bundles of complete Pick spaces from homogeneous hypersurfaces in 𝔹 d {\mathbb{B}}_{d} . Along with these bundles, we construct examples of Cowen-Doulas tuples of operators from the compressed Arveson d d -shift.



1.
Introduction

Let
{\mathbb{D}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
be the open unit disc. The classical Pick interpolation problem asks to find an analytic function
f\colon{\mathbb{D}}\to\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
that satisfies
f(z_{i})=w_{i}
, for some
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in{\mathbb{D}}
and
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}
that were initially prescribed. This problem admits an elegant solution due to Pick and Nevanlinna. Namely, such a function exists if and only if the Pick matrix
\left(\frac{1-w_{i}\overline{w_{j}}}{1-z_{i}\overline{z_{j}}}\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}
is positive. Moreover, the problem for matrix-valued functions admits essentially the same solution. This fact can be encoded in the statement that the reproducing kernel Hilbert space
H^{2}({\mathbb{D}})
of all analytic functions on
{\mathbb{D}}
with square-summable Taylor coefficients at the origin is a complete Pick space. In this paper, we assume that the reader has some familiarity with reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS for short) and the complete Pick property. We give a brief description of the complete Pick property in Subsection
2.3
. One of the standard references on these topics is the excellent book
[
3
]
.

The complete Pick property is a powerful geometric property. The strength of the complete Pick property manifests itself in several ways. Suppose
{\mathcal{H}}
is an RKHS on a set
X
with the complete Pick property. In that case, a celebrated theorem of Agler and McCarthy
[
2
]
guarantees that there exists a
d\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
and an embedding
b\colon X\to{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
, where
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
is the unit ball of
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
(we use the convention that
{\mathbb{C}}^{\infty}
is
\ell^{2}
). Moreover,
{\mathcal{H}}
is isomorphic as an RKHS to a space of analytic functions on a variety in
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
. The family of universal complete Pick spaces of analytic functions on
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
is the family
H^{2}_{d}
where
H^{2}_{d}
is the Drury-Arveson space on
d
variables. The isomorphism of
{\mathcal{H}}
with a subspace of the Drury-Arveson space induces a completely isometric isomorphism of the corresponding multiplier algebras. This observation led Davidson, Ramsey, and Shalit
[
25
,
26
]
to study isomorphisms of multiplier algebras of subvarieties of
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
cut out by WOT closed ideals of multiplier on
H^{2}_{d}
. One of their main results is that two such algebras are completely isometrically isomorphic if and only if there exists an automorphism of
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
that maps one variety onto the other. A similar result for finite-dimensional Pick spaces was obtained by Rochberg
[
56
]
.

The importance of the complete Pick property is not restricted to the classical function spaces. Recently, the complete Pick property was extended to the noncommutative setting
[
4
,
10
,
12
,
13
,
23
]
. In particular, the full Fock space is a complete Pick nc-RKHS. In many ways, the full Fock space is a better analog of
H^{2}({\mathbb{D}})
than
H^{2}_{d}
. Moreover,
H^{2}_{d}
can be identified with the space of symmetric tensors in the full Fock space. This fact is utilized in this paper, and we discuss the full Fock space in Subsection
2.5
.

The inspiration for this paper came from the work of Ofek, Pandey, and Shalit
[
48
]
. In
[
48
]
, the authors construct the space of
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}
isomorphism classes of complete Pick spaces on
n
points, where
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
is fixed. They equip the space with a Banach-Mazur type distance and other distances that induce the same topology on the space. Our approach to this, however, is influenced by algebraic geometry. By the aforementioned theorem of Agler and McCarthy, we know that if
{\mathcal{H}}
is a complete Pick space on
X
, a set of cardinality
n
, then there exists
d\leq n-1
, such that
X
embeds into
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
, where
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
is the unit ball of
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
, and
{\mathcal{H}}
is isomorphic to a multiplier-coinvariant subspace of the Drury-Arveson space
H^{2}_{d}
. Therefore, we consider the Grassmannian
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
of
n
-dimensional multiplier-coinvariant subspaces of
H^{2}_{d}
. On
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
, the strong and norm topologies coincide, so we consider this space equipped with the metric induced by the norm. There is a natural action of the group of holomorphic automorphisms of
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
on
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
. The first main result of the paper is:

Theorem A

(Theorems
3.6
and
4.4
)
.

There is a natural continuous map
\Psi_{d,n}\colon\mathbb{P}_{d,n}\to{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
. Here
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
is the symmetrized polyball equipped with the symmetric distance (see Subsection
2.1
for details). Moreover,
\Psi_{d,n}
restricts to a homeomorphism from
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}^{0}
, the subspace of projections in
B(H^{2}_{d})
onto subspaces spanned by
n
distinct kernels to the configuration space of
n
unordered points in
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
that we denote by
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
.

As seen in Section
3
, the space
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
behaves like a blowup of
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
(see for example
[
37
]
). At this point, it is natural to ask what is the advantage of this point of view compared to the space of isomorphism classes of complete Pick spaces on
n
points. One key difference is that on
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
, we have the tautological bundle over this Grassmannian, and the bundle is equivariant with respect to the action of
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
. We discuss bundles of complete Pick spaces in Section
5
. In particular, we raise Question
5.3
. Roughly speaking, the question is whether the Grassmannian of complete Pick spaces
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
possesses the universal property of the Grassmannian to some extent. Classically, given a rank
n
vector bundle
{\mathcal{E}}
on a compact topological space
Z
, there exists a
d
and a map
\varphi\colon Z\to\mathrm{Gr}(n,d)
, such that
{\mathcal{E}}
is the pullback via
\varphi
of the tautological bundle on the Grassmannian. We ask whether this is true for bundles of complete Pick spaces.

We use a well-known construction of finite morphisms from algebraic geometry to provide examples of bundles of complete Pick spaces. Let
p
be a homogenous polynomial that does not vanish on the
d
-th coordinate axis. Let
V(p)\subset{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
be the hypersurface cut out by
p
. We project
V(p)
onto the first
d-1
coordinates and obtain a neighborhood of the origin
U\subset{\mathbb{B}}_{d-1}
and a vector bundle
{\mathcal{E}}_{p}
on
U
. The fiber
{\mathcal{E}}_{p,\lambda}
for
\lambda\in U
can be described as
\bigcap_{j=1}^{d-1}\ker(S_{j}^{*}-\overline{\lambda_{j}})
, where
S_{1},\ldots,S_{d-1}
are the compressions of the first
d-1
coordinates of the Arveson
d
-shift to the complement of the ideal generated by
p
. This result is obtained from the following theorem.

Theorem B
.

Let
p\in{\mathbb{C}}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]
be a homogeneous polynomial, such that
p
does not vanish on the
d
-th coordinate axis. Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{p}=H^{2}_{d}\ominus M_{p}H^{2}_{d}
and
S_{j}=P_{{\mathcal{H}}_{p}}M_{z_{j}}|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{P}}
be the compressions of the Arveson
d
-shift. Then, there exists a neighborhood of the origin
U\subset{\mathbb{B}}_{d-1}
, such that the
d-1
-tuple
S_{1}^{*},\ldots,S_{d-1}^{*}
is in the Cowen-Douglas class on
U
.

The Cowen-Douglas class of operators was introduced in the seminal work
[
19
]
as a vast generalization of the backward shift on
H^{2}({\mathbb{D}})
. Tuples of commuting operators in the Cowen-Douglas class were studied by Curto and Salinas
[
21
]
and many others. In particular, Lu, Yang, and Zu
[
45
]
have studied operators in the Cowen-Douglas class arising from compressions of the shifts on the Hardy space of the bidisc. Their results are akin to ours but in a different setting.

In Section
7
, we discuss a stratification of
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
and
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}
. In particular, we show the following theorem.

Theorem C

(Theorem
7.1
)
.

There is an open dense subset
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}^{reg}\subset\mathrm{Pick}_{n}
that has a structure of a smooth manifold. Moreover, the restriction of the tautological bundle on
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
to the set of regular points is a vector bundle on
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}^{reg}
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Geometry of the unit ball and its symmetrization

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We will denote the open unit ball in
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
by

{\mathbb{B}}_{d}=\left\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}\mid\sum_{j=1}^{d}|z_{j}|^{2}<1\right\}.

In particular, if
d=1
,
{\mathbb{B}}_{1}={\mathbb{D}}
, the open unit disc. This section collects some necessary information from
[
58
]
. The set
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
is a homogenous space. For every point
0\neq\lambda\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
, there is a unique involution that exchanges
\lambda
and
0
given by

\varphi_{\lambda}(z)=\dfrac{\lambda-P_{\lambda}z-\sqrt{1-\|\lambda\|^{2}}(I-P_{\lambda})z}{1-\langle z,\lambda\rangle}.

Here,
P_{\lambda}z=\frac{1}{\|\lambda\|^{2}}\langle z,\lambda\rangle\lambda
. The automorphism group of the ball is generated by the involutions described above and unitaries. We will denote the automorphism group by
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
. In fact,
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
is a quotient of
\mathrm{SU}(1,d)
by a finite subgroup.

The pseudohyperbolic distance on
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
is defined by
d_{ph}(z,w)=\|\varphi_{z}(w)\|
. The analog of the Schwarz-Pick lemma for the ball tells us that every analytic self-map of
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
is a contraction with respect to
d_{ph}
. Hence, automorphisms are isometries with respect to
d_{ph}
.

In this paper, we will deal with finite subsets of
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
, allowing for multiplicities. To this end we consider the polyball
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n}={\mathbb{B}}_{d}\times\cdots\times{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
(
n
times). The symmetric group
S_{n}
acts on this product by permuting the components. We will denote by
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
the quotient by this action. We call
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
the symmetrized polyball (also known as the
n
-th symmetric power of
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
). If we consider the polyball as a subset of
{\mathbb{C}}^{dn}
, then
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
can be viewed as an image in
{\mathbb{C}}^{dn}
under a symmetrization map. In particular, if
d=1
, our
{\mathbb{X}}_{1,n}
is biholomorphic to the symmetrized polydisc (see for example the works of Agler, Young, and Lykova in the case of the bidisc
[
5
,
6
,
1
]
). It is sometimes convenient to consider points
[X]\in{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
as formal sums. We consider the free abelian group
D({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
generated by the points of
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
. Namely, the elements of
D({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
are finite formal sums of points in
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
with integer coefficients, for example
2[(0,0)]+[(1/2,0)]-[(1/2i,-1/2i)]
. The positive elements
D_{+}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})\subset D({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
are all such combinations with non-negative coefficients. The degree map is defined as
\deg\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}[\lambda_{j}]\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}
. We note that we have the following identification

{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}=\left\{D\in D_{+}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})\mid\deg D=n\right\}.

It will be sometimes convenient to view elements of
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
as multisets and sometimes as described above.

The space
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
inherits a metric from the polyball. First, we observe that

d(X,Y)=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}d_{ph}(x_{j},y_{j})

defines a metric on
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n}
. Here, we write
X=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),Y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n}
. In
[
48
]
, the symmetric distance between the images
[X]
and
[Y]
of
X
and
Y
, respectively, in
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
is given by

d_{s}([X],[Y])=\min_{\sigma\in S_{n}}d(X,\sigma(Y)).

Another distance that appears in various contexts that is related to the above is the optimal matching distance on
{\mathbb{C}}^{dn}
defined by

d_{o}([X],[Y])=\min_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\|x_{j}-y_{\sigma(j)}\|.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
0<r<1
, then for every two subsets of
n
points
X,Y\subset r\overline{{\mathbb{B}}_{d}}
,

d_{s}([X],[Y])\leq\frac{1}{1-r^{2}}d_{o}([X],[Y]).

Proof.

For
z,w\in{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n}
, it follows from
[
58
, Theorem 2.2.2]
that

\|\varphi_{w}(z)\|^{2}=1-\frac{(1-\|w\|^{2})(1-\|z\|^{2})}{|1-\langle z,w\rangle|^{2}}=\frac{|1-\langle z,w\rangle|^{2}-(1-\|w\|^{2})(1-\|z\|^{2})}{|1-\langle z,w\rangle|^{2}}.

Consequently, from the definition of the pseudohyperbolic distance on
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n}
, we have that

\displaystyle d_{ph}(z,w)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|1-\langle z,w\rangle|^{2}-(1-\|w\|^{2})(1-\|z\|^{2})}{|1-\langle z,w\rangle|^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|w\|^{2}+\|z\|^{2}-\langle z,w\rangle-\langle w,z\rangle+|\langle z,w\rangle|^{2}-\|z\|^{2}\|w\|^{2}}{|1-\langle z,w\rangle|^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|z-w\|^{2}+|\langle z,w\rangle|^{2}-\|z\|^{2}\|w\|^{2}}{|1-\langle z,w\rangle|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-r^{2})^{2}}\|z-w\|^{2}

verifying the desired identity.
∎

Note that since
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
is complete with respect to the pseudohyperbolic metric, so is
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n}
with respect to
d
.

Proposition 2.2
.

The metric space
({\mathbb{X}}_{d,n},d_{s})
is complete

Proof.

Let
\{X_{k}\}\in{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n}
be a sequence, such that the corresponding sequence
\{[X_{k}]\}\subset{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
is Cauchy with respect to
d_{s}
. For each
\sigma\in S_{n}
, we set

A_{\sigma}=\left\{k\in{\mathbb{N}}\mid d_{s}([X_{k}],[X_{k+1}])=d(X_{k},\sigma(X_{k+1})\right\}

Since
{\mathbb{N}}=\bigcup_{\sigma\in S_{n}}A_{\sigma}
at least one of the
A_{\sigma}
is infinite. Passing to a subsequence, we may assume that there is always one
\sigma
. Now set
Y_{k}=\sigma^{k-1}(X_{k})
and note that

d(Y_{k},Y_{k+1})=d(\sigma^{k-1}(X_{k}),\sigma^{k}(X_{k+1}))=d(X_{k},\sigma(X_{k+1}))=d_{s}([X_{k}],[X_{k+1}]).

Hence,
\{Y_{k}\}
is a Cauchy sequence that converges to some
Y
. By continuity of the quotient map
\{[X_{k}]\}
converges to
[Y]
.
∎

2.2.
Joint spectrum of commuting tuples

Let
A=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{d})\in B({\mathcal{H}})^{d}
be a commuting
d
-tuple of operators. For a point
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
, we will write
A-\lambda I
for the
d
-tuple
(A_{1}-\lambda_{1}I,\ldots,A_{d}-\lambda_{d}I)
. For a multi-index
\alpha\in\left({\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}\right)^{d}
we will write
A^{\alpha}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}A_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}
. The row and column norms of a
d
-tuple are defined by

\|A\|_{row}=\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{d}A_{j}A_{j}^{*}\right\|^{1/2}\text{ and }\|A\|_{col}=\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{d}A_{j}^{*}A_{j}\right\|^{1/2}.

In particular, we say that
A
is a row contraction, if
\|A\|_{row}\leq 1
. If the inequality is strict,
A
is called a strict row contraction. We say that
A
is a commuting (strict) row contraction if
A
is both a commuting tuple and a (strict) row contraction. There is an optimal inequality between these norms for every
d
-tuple of matrices
A
:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\|A\|_{col}\leq\|A\|_{row}\leq\sqrt{d}\|A\|_{col},

The notion of a joint spectrum of a commuting tuple of matrices is well-established. In general, for a
d
-tuple of commuting operators, there are many notions of a joint spectrum that are not equivalent (see
[
20
]
for an excellent overview). Fortunately for us, all of these definitions coincide in the case of matrices and agree with the following one.

Definition 2.3
.

We define the joint spectrum of a commuting tuple
A\in M_{n}^{d}
to be

\sigma(A)=\left\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}\mid\bigcap_{j=1}^{d}\ker(A_{j}-\lambda_{j}I)\neq\{0\}\right\}.

We note that it is easy to see using the simultaneous upper-triangular form that for a commuting row contraction, we have that
\sigma(A)\subset\overline{{\mathbb{B}}_{d}}
. It also follows from the work of Popescu
[
55
]
(see also
[
60
]
for a different proof) that
\sigma(A)\subset{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
if and only if
A
is jointly similar to a strict row contraction. In other words, there exists
S\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}
, such that the
d
-tuple
S^{-1}AS=(S^{-1}A_{1}S,\ldots,S^{-1}A_{d}S)
is a strict row contraction.

In this paper, we will need more information from the spectrum. To this end, we adjust the definition slightly. First, we need the notion of a root subspace of a tuple
A
.

R_{\lambda}(A)=\bigcap_{|\alpha|=n}\ker(A-\lambda I)^{\alpha}.

We note that in the case
d=1
,
R_{\lambda}
is the classical root subspace of
A
. Namely, the space spanned by the eigenvectors corresponding to
\lambda
and the associated vectors, if any. It follows from
[
38
, Corollary 9.1.3]
that
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}R_{\lambda}(A)
. This sum is a non-orthogonal direct sum decomposition. In particular,
\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}\dim R_{\lambda}(A)=n
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
A\in M_{n}^{d}
be similar to a strict commuting row contraction. We set

\sigma_{J}(A)=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}\dim R_{\lambda}(A)[\lambda]\in{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}.

It will be important for us in the later stages of the paper to understand the continuity properties of the joint spectrum. In this presentation, we follow Elsner
[
33
]
. We fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
A,B\in M_{n}^{d}
two commuting
d
-tuples. We define
S_{n}(\Delta,r)
as the spectral radius of the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}0&\Delta&&\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&0&\Delta\\
r&\cdots&r&r\end{pmatrix}.

The number
\tilde{\Delta}(B)
measures the non-normality of
B
. More precisely, if
D_{n}
stands for the algebra of diagonal matrices and
N_{n}
for the algebra of strictly upper-triangular ones, then one defines

\tilde{\Delta}(B)=\min\{\|N\|_{col}\mid U^{*}BU=D+N,\,U\in U_{n},\,D\in D_{n},\text{ and }N\in N_{n}\}

By
[
33
, Theorem 7.1]
, we have that

d_{H}(\sigma(A),\sigma(B))\leq S_{n}(\tilde{\Delta}(B),\|A-B\|_{col}).

Here
d_{H}
stands for the Hausdorff distance. Now as observed in
[
32
]
and used in
[
16
]
to the joint spectrum case,

d_{H}(\sigma(A),\sigma(B))\leq n^{1/n}(2M)^{1-1/n}\|A-B\|_{col}^{1/n}.

Here
M=\max\{\|A\|_{col},\|B\|_{col}\}
. If we replace the column norm with the row norm, we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
A,B\in M_{n}^{d}
be two commuting row contractions, then there exists a constant
c(n,d)>0
that depends only on
n
and
d
, such that

d_{H}(\sigma(A),\sigma(B))\leq c(n,d)\|A-B\|_{row}^{1/n}.

However, the Hausdorff distance is unsatisfactory for our purposes since it ignores multiplicities. Therefore, we need to refine this result. To this end, we need some lemmas. First, Theorem 3.4 in
[
44
]
together with the definition of a stable subspace of a commuting
d
-tuple
A
yield the lemma below.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
A\in M_{n}^{d}
be a commuting tuple of matrices. Then for every
\lambda\in\sigma(A)
and for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
, such that if
B\in M_{n}^{d}
is a commuting tuple, such that
\|A-B\|<\delta
, then there exists a
B
-invaraint subspace
{\mathcal{N}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, such that
\|P_{{\mathcal{N}}}-P_{{\mathcal{R}}_{\lambda}(A)}\|<\varepsilon
.

The next lemma is a consequence of
[
15
, Theorem VI.1.8]
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a Hilbert space and
{\mathcal{K}}_{1},{\mathcal{K}}_{2}\subset{\mathcal{H}}
be
n
-dimensional subspaces. Let
P_{1},P_{2}\in B({\mathcal{H}})
be the projections onto
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{K}}_{2}
, respectively. Given, an isometry
V_{1}\colon{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathcal{H}}
, such that
\mathrm{ran}V_{1}={\mathcal{K}}_{1}
, we can find an isometry
V_{2}\colon{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathcal{H}}
, such that
\mathrm{ran}V_{2}={\mathcal{K}}_{2}
and, moreover,

\|P_{1}-P_{2}\|\leq\|V_{1}-V_{2}\|\leq\sqrt{2}\|P_{1}-P_{2}\|.

Now, we are ready to prove the proposition that we will require. Recall that Definition
2.4
describes
\sigma_{J}(A)\in{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
as the joint spectrum of
A
with keeping track of multiplicities.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
A_{m}\in M_{n}^{d}
be a sequence of commuting row contractions that converges to a commuting row contraction
A
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{o}(\sigma_{J}(A_{n}),\sigma_{J}(A))=0.

Proof.

Let us fix
0<\varepsilon<1
. We obtain
\delta>0
from Lemma
2.6
and let
m_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
be such that for
m\geq m_{0}
, we have that
\|A_{m}-A\|<\min\{\delta,\varepsilon\}
. Now fix
\lambda\in\sigma(A)
, then for each
m\geq m_{0}
, we can find
{\mathcal{N}}_{m}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, that is
A_{m}
-invariant and
\|P_{{\mathcal{N}}_{m}}-P_{{\mathcal{R}}_{\lambda}(A)}\|<\varepsilon
. In particular,
\dim{\mathcal{N}}_{m}=\dim{\mathcal{R}}_{\lambda}(A)=k
. Let
V,W\colon{\mathcal{K}}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be isometries with
\mathrm{ran}V={\mathcal{N}}_{m}
and
\mathrm{ran}W={\mathcal{R}}_{\lambda}(A)
, and
\|V-W\|<\sqrt{2}\varepsilon
. Then,

\|V^{*}A_{m}V-W^{*}AW\|\leq\|A_{m}V-AW\|+\|V-W\|<(1+2\sqrt{2})\varepsilon.

Note that since these spaces are invariant,
\sigma(W^{*}AW)=\{\lambda\}
and
\sigma(V^{*}A_{m}V)\subset\sigma(A)
. In particular, we have that
\sigma(V^{*}A_{m}V)
is contained in a disc of radius
c(n,d)(1+2\sqrt{2})^{1/n}\varepsilon^{1/n}
centered at
\lambda
. Now, let us take
\varepsilon
small enough so that the discs with the above radii centered at different points of the spectrum are disjoint. We get that each such disc contains precisely
\dim{\mathcal{R}}_{\lambda}(A)
joint eigenvalues of
A_{m}
counting multiplicities. Therefore, we can choose a permutation that shows that

d_{o}(\sigma_{J}(A_{m}),\sigma_{J}(A))\leq c(n,d)(1+2\sqrt{2})^{1/n}\varepsilon^{1/n}.

This completes the proof.
∎

2.3.
Drury-Arveson space

The Drury-Arveson space is a Hilbert space of functions first considered by Drury
[
29
]
. However, Arveson
[
11
]
was the first to identify its many properties analogous to the classical Hardy space
H^{2}({\mathbb{D}})
. We will provide only necessary information on the Drury-Arveson space in this section. The interested reader should consult the outstanding surveys
[
39
]
and
[
61
]
. We will assume basic familiarity with reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS for short). We refer the reader to
[
3
]
for the necessary background.

The Drury-Arveson space on
d
variables
H^{2}_{d}
(also known as the symmetric Fock space) is the completion of the polynomial ring
{\mathbb{C}}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]
with respect to the following inner product

\langle z^{\alpha},z^{\beta}\rangle=\delta_{\alpha,\beta}\frac{\alpha!}{|\alpha|!}.

Here,
\alpha,\beta\in\left({\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}\right)^{d}
are multi-indices. We write
z^{\alpha}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}z_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}
,
\alpha!=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}!
, and
|\alpha|=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}
. This inner product is also known as the Bombieri inner product
[
14
]
. We observe, that if
d=1
, that
H^{2}_{1}=H^{2}({\mathbb{D}})
. It was observed by Arveson
[
11
]
that
H^{2}_{d}
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with the kernel

k(z,w)=\frac{1}{1-\langle z,w\rangle};

In particular,
H^{2}_{d}
is a space of holomorphic functions on
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
. For
\lambda\in{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
, we will denote by
k_{\lambda}\in H^{2}_{d}
the kernel function
k_{\lambda}(z)=k(z,\lambda)
. More generally, for a multi-index
\alpha\in\left({\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}\right)^{d}
, we will denote by
k_{\lambda}^{(\alpha)}\in H^{2}_{d}
the vector such that for each
f\in H^{2}_{d}
,

\langle f,k^{(\alpha)}_{\lambda}\rangle=\dfrac{\partial^{\alpha}f}{\partial z^{\alpha}}(\lambda).

Now, by the properties of the inner product on
H^{2}_{d}
and the Taylor series expansion at the origin, we have that

\langle f,z^{\alpha}\rangle=\dfrac{1}{\alpha!}\dfrac{\partial^{\alpha}f}{\partial z^{\alpha}}(0)\|z^{\alpha}\|^{2}=\dfrac{1}{|\alpha|!}\dfrac{\partial^{\alpha}f}{\partial z^{\alpha}}(0).

Hence,
k_{0}^{(\alpha)}=|\alpha|!z^{\alpha}
. More generally, we have the following lemma:

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\lambda\in{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
and let
\alpha\in({\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\})^{n}
be a multi-index. Then,

k_{\lambda}^{(\alpha)}(z)=\dfrac{\partial^{\alpha}k}{\partial\bar{w}^{\alpha}}(z,\lambda)=\dfrac{|\alpha|!z^{\alpha}}{(1-\langle z,\lambda\rangle)^{n+1}}

Proof.

We prove the claim by induction on
|\alpha|=n
. The claim for
n=0
is the definition of the kernel of
H^{2}_{d}
. Hence, we may assume that
n>0
. Let us assume without loss of generality that
\alpha_{1}>0
. Then
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}+e_{1}
. Let us write now

\dfrac{\partial^{\alpha}f}{\partial z^{\alpha}}(\lambda)=\lim_{z\to 0}\dfrac{1}{z}\left[\dfrac{\partial^{\alpha^{\prime}}f}{\partial z^{\alpha^{\prime}}}(\lambda+ze_{1})-\dfrac{\partial^{\alpha^{\prime}}f}{\partial z^{\alpha^{\prime}}}(\lambda)\right]=\lim_{z\to 0}\left\langle f,\frac{k_{\lambda+ze_{1}}^{(\alpha^{\prime})}-k_{\lambda}^{(\alpha^{\prime})}}{\bar{z}}\right\rangle.

In other words,
\frac{1}{\bar{z}}\left(k_{\lambda+ze_{1}}^{(\alpha^{\prime})}-k_{\lambda}^{(\alpha^{\prime})}\right)
converges weakly to
k_{\lambda}^{(\alpha)}
. Now fix any
\mu\in{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
and we have by induction

k_{\lambda}^{\alpha}(\mu)=\lim_{z\to 0}\dfrac{1}{\bar{z}}\left[\dfrac{\partial^{\alpha^{\prime}}k}{\partial\bar{w}^{\alpha^{\prime}}}(\mu,\lambda+ze_{1})-\dfrac{\partial^{\alpha^{\prime}}k}{\partial\bar{w}^{\alpha^{\prime}}}(\mu,\lambda)\right].

The proof is complete.
∎

It is not hard to check that the multiplication by coordinates defines contractive operators
M_{z_{j}}f=z_{j}f
, for
1\leq j\leq d
on
H^{2}_{d}
. Moreover, the row
M_{z}=(M_{z_{1}},\ldots,M_{z_{d}})
is a contraction. A row contraction
T=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{d})\in B({\mathcal{H}})^{d}
is called pure, if for every
\xi\in{\mathcal{H}}
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{|\alpha|=n}\|T^{\alpha*}\xi\|^{2}=0
. Arveson proved that
M_{z}
is the universal pure row contraction. This property is one important universality property of the Drury-Arveson space. We remark in passing that a finite-dimensional pure row contraction is similar to a strict contraction see
[
55
]
and
[
60
]
. Therefore, the joint spectrum of a finite-dimensional pure row contraction is contained in
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
.

The multiplier algebra of
H^{2}_{d}
will be denoted by
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}
. This is an operator algebra equipped with the multiplier norm. It is well known that
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}
is the WOT closure of the algebra generated by the
M_{z_{j}}
. By standard facts about reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, the algebra
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}
consists of bounded analytic functions on
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
. However, the inclusion
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}\hookrightarrow H^{\infty}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
is contractive and strict, unless
d=1
. The second important universal property of the Drury-Arveson space was proved by Agler and McCarthy
[
2
]
based on earlier works of McCullough and of Quiggin. In order to describe this universal property, we need first to recall the complete Pick property.

Recall that the classical Pick interpolation problem is to find an analytic function
f\colon{\mathbb{D}}\to\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
satisfying
f(\lambda_{i})=w_{i}
, for
1\leq i\leq n
, where
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in{\mathbb{D}}
and
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
. The solution of Pick and of Nevanlinna was that such a function exists if and only if the matrix
\left(\frac{1-w_{i}\overline{w_{j}}}{1-\lambda_{i}\overline{\lambda_{j}}}\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}
is positive – this matrix is known as the Pick matrix. Moreover, it is true that if we want to find an analytic matrix-valued function
f
of sup-norm at most
1
that satisfies
f(\lambda_{i})=W_{i}
, then the condition is essentially unchanged. Namely, we only need the positivity of the block matrix
\left(\frac{I-W_{i}W_{j}^{*}}{1-\lambda_{i}\overline{\lambda_{j}}}\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}
. Note that the function
k(z,w)=\frac{1}{1-z\bar{w}}
is the Szegö kernel, which is the reproducing kernel for
H^{2}({\mathbb{D}})
. This leads to a generalization of this question to arbitrary reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces. To be more precise, we say that a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}
of functions on a set
X
has the scalar Pick property, if for every set of points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in X
and values
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
, there exists a contractive multiplier
f
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}
, such that
f(x_{i})=w_{i}
if and only if the Pick matrix
\left((1-w_{i}\overline{w_{j}})k(x_{i},x_{j})\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}
is positive. It is rather straightforward to prove that the Pick matrix is positive if such a contractive multiplier exists. The converse usually fails. We will say that the space has the complete Pick property if the claim holds for matrix-valued multipliers of all sizes.

We add the relatively common hypothesis that our complete Pick spaces are irreducible. Namely, no two kernels are linearly dependent, and no two are orthogonal (see
[
3
]
). The theorem of Agler and McCarthy
[
2
, Theorem 4.2]
states that if
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}
is a complete Pick space, then there exists
d\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
and a map
b\colon X\to{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
, such that the kernel
k
is equivalent to the pullback of the kernel of the Drury-Arveson space via
b
. Therefore, in particular, the space is unitarily equivalent (as a reproducing kernel Hilbert space) to a multiplier-coinvariant subspace of
H^{2}_{d}
.

This development led Davidson, Ramsey, and Shalit
[
25
,
26
]
to study subvarieties of the
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
cut out by multipliers and the corresponding reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces and multiplier algebras. They have shown that given two subvarieties
V,W\subset{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
with ideals
I_{V},I_{W}\subset{\mathcal{M}}_{d}
of multipliers that vanish on
V
and
W
, respectively, the quotients
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}/I_{V}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}/I_{W}
are completely isometrically isomorphic if and only if there exists an automorphism
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
, such that
\varphi(V)=W
. It follows from
[
25
, Remark 9.3]
that every
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
gives rise to a unitary
U_{\varphi}\in B(H^{2}_{d})
whose adjoint is a linear extension of

U_{\varphi}^{*}k_{\lambda}=\sqrt{1-\|\varphi^{-1}(0)\|^{2}}k_{\lambda}(\varphi^{-1}(0))k_{\varphi(\lambda)}.

In particular, if
\varphi=\varphi_{\mu}
for some
\mu\in{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
, then
U_{\varphi}^{*}1=\sqrt{1-\|\mu\|^{2}}k_{\mu}
which is the normalized kernel at
\mu
. Moreover, for every
f\in{\mathcal{M}}_{d}
, if
M_{f}
is the multiplication operator by
f
, then
U_{\varphi}M_{f}U_{\varphi}^{*}=M_{f\circ\varphi}
. As mentioned in the introduction, this result was also obtained by Rochberg
[
56
]
in the case of finite-dimensional spaces.

2.4.
Finite-dimensional complete Pick spaces

Following Ofek, Pandey, and Shalit
[
48
]
, we will be interested in finite-dimensional complete Pick spaces. Recall that if
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}
is a complete Pick space on
X
with
|X|=n
, then by the Agler-McCarthy theorem, there exists an embedding
b\colon X\to{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
(including the possibility that
d=\infty
) of
X
into
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
. We may also choose an equivalent kernel and assume that
b(x_{1})=0
. The points of
b(X)
then span at most an
n-1
-dimensional subspace, implying that we can choose
d\leq n-1
(see also
[
57
, Theorem 3.2]
for a more precise and detailed result). Therefore, we will work only with
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
from now on.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be sets and
{\mathcal{H}}_{k_{1}}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{k_{2}}
be RKHSs on them. Let
\varphi\colon X_{1}\to X_{2}
be a map. Following
[
48
]
, we define a morphism of RKHSs to be a bounded linear operator of the form
Tk_{1,x}=f(x)k_{2,\varphi(x)}
, where
f\colon X_{1}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a bounded function. An isomorphism of RKHSs is one that arises from an invertible
\varphi
and non-vanishing
f(x)
. In particular, an isomorphism is isometric if and only if
k_{2}
is a rescaling of
k_{1}
in the sense of
[
3
, Section 2.6]
. Based on this definition, Ofek, Pandey, and Shalit have defined a version of the Banach-Mazur distance for a complete Pick space of dimension
n
:

\rho_{RK}\left({\mathcal{H}}_{k_{1}},{\mathcal{H}}_{k_{2}}\right)=\log\left(\inf\left\{\|T\|\|T^{-1}\|\mid T\colon{\mathcal{H}}_{k_{1}}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{k_{2}}\text{ is an RKHS isomorphism}\right\}\right).

Let us denote the space of all complete Pick RKHS of dimension
n
by
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}
. Let
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n,0}
be the subset of the product consisting of distinct points. Namely,

{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n,0}=\left\{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n}\mid\forall\,i\neq j,\,z_{i}\neq z_{j}\right\}.

This set is the complement of the ramification locus of the symmetrization map. We denote by
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
the image of
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n,0}
in
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
. Since every complete Pick RKHS on
n
points embeds in
{\mathbb{B}}_{n-1}
, we can define a surjective map
\Phi_{n}\colon{\mathbb{X}}_{n-1,n}^{0}\to\mathrm{Pick}_{n}
that sends a set of
n
distinct points to the corresponding complete Pick space. To be more precise, if
[X]=\sum_{j=1}^{n}[\lambda_{j}]\in{\mathbb{X}}_{n-1,n}^{0}
, then
\Phi_{n}([X])=[\mathrm{Span}\{k_{\lambda_{1}},\ldots,k_{\lambda_{n}}\}]
. Here, the brackets on the right-hand side indicate the equivalence class. Note that the fiber of
\Phi_{n}
over a point
{\mathcal{H}}
consists of all subspaces of
H^{2}_{d}
spanned by
n
kernel functions that are isomorphic as RKHSs to
{\mathcal{H}}
. In particular, the fiber is a homogeneous space under
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{n-1})
by the results of
[
26
]
and
[
56
]
. We note that
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
acts on
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n}
diagonally, and this action commutes with the actions of
S_{n}
. Hence, we get an action of
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
on
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
. Moreover, the set
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
is
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
-invariant. The map
\Phi_{n}
described above is a quotient map (not in the topological sense, a priori). In
[
48
]
, the authors define the invariant symmetric distance by

\rho_{s}([X],[Y])=\inf_{\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})}d_{s}([X],\varphi([Y])).

The action of
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
on
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
is proper by
[
43
, Example in Section 1.2]
. Consequently, by
[
43
, Remark 1.4.1]
the diagonal action of
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
on
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n}
is proper if and only if, for every
z\in{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n}
, there exists a neighborhood
U
of
z
, such that the set
G(U|U)=\{\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})\mid\varphi(U)\cap U\neq\emptyset\}
is relatively compact. Since the action of
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
on
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
is proper, for
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\in{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n}
, we can choose neighborhoods
U_{i}\subset{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
of
\lambda_{i}
, such that
G(U_{i}|U_{i})
is relatively compact. Thus setting
U=\prod_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}
, we get that
G(U|U)=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}G(U_{i}|U_{i})
which is relatively compact. It is now easy to see that the action of
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
on
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
and
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
is proper
[
43
, Remark 1.1.1]
. By
[
43
, Section 2]
, every orbit of
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
in
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
and
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
is closed. Hence, the symmetric invariant metric induces a metric on the quotient
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}/\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
. In fact, the metric induces the quotient topology. By
[
48
, Theorem 5.4]
, the topology induced by
\rho_{RK}
on
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}
is the same as the quotient topology on
{\mathbb{X}}_{n-1,n}^{0}/\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{n-1})
. However, the metrics are not equivalent by
[
48
, Example 6.3]
.

2.5.
Multivariable Blaschke multipliers

In
[
47
]
, McCullough and Trent show that every multiplier invariant subspace of the Drury-Arveson space is an image of a partially isometric multiplier
\Theta\colon H^{2}_{d}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}\to H^{2}_{d}
, where
{\mathcal{K}}
is an auxiliary Hilbert space. We will need to use such multipliers. However, we will require a precise formula. Therefore, we use the idea of Alpay and Kaptanoğlu. In
[
8
]
, Alpay and Kaptanoğlu solve an interpolation problem on the unit ball of
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
and provide a concrete formula for the interpolating function. We will use their construction to show that the corresponding function is a partially isometric multiplier of McCullough and Trent.

To prove this, we will require some noncommutative techniques. The full Fock space
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
is the completion of the free algebra
{\mathbb{C}}\langle z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}\rangle
with respect to the inner product that makes the monomials an orthonormal basis. The full Fock space is the noncommutative analog of the Hardy space (see, for example
[
9
,
24
,
23
,
22
,
52
,
54
,
40
]
). In particular, the full Fock space is a noncommutative reproducing kernel Hilbert space in the sense of Ball, Marx, and Vinnikov
[
12
]
(see also
[
59
]
). The operators of left multiplication by the variables are isometries on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
that we will denote by
L_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq d
. The left shift

L=\begin{pmatrix}L_{1}&\ldots&L_{d}\end{pmatrix}\colon{\mathbb{C}}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}

is a row isometry. In fact, by the results of Frazho
[
36
]
, Bunce
[
17
]
, and Popescu
[
51
]
, it is the universal pure row isometry. Similarly, the operators
R_{j}
, the operators of multiplication by the coordinates on the right, form a row isometry
R
, which we call the right shift. The WOT-closed algebra
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}
generated by the
L_{j}
is the algebra of left multipliers on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
. One can view both
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
and
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}
as noncommutative functions on the row ball (quantization of the
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
) (see
[
41
]
for details on the theory of noncommutative functions and
[
52
,
54
,
59
]
for function theory on the row ball). In particular, Popescu in
[
50
]
and independently Davidson and Pitts
[
24
]
have proved a noncommutative version of the Beurling theorem. Namely, if
{\mathcal{H}}\subset\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
is a right shift invariant subspace, then there exists a row isometry
\Theta\colon\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{k}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
, where
k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
, such that the coordinates of
\Theta
belong to
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}
and
{\mathcal{H}}=\Theta\left(\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{k}\right)
.

As was already observed by Arveson
[
11
]
, the Drury-Arveson space embeds isometrically into the full Fock space as the symmetric tensors. We will identify
H^{2}_{d}
with its image in
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
. It is quite easy to see that
H^{2}_{d}\subset\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
is both left and right coinvariant and that for all
1\leq j\leq d
,
L_{j}^{*}|_{H^{2}_{d}}=M_{z_{j}}^{*}=R_{j}^{*}|_{H^{2}_{d}}
. Hence, if
X\subset{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{X}\subset H^{2}_{d}
is the subspace spanned by
\{k_{\lambda}\mid\lambda\in X\}
, then
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\ominus{\mathcal{H}}_{X}
is both left and right invariant (see
[
22
,
23
]
for more details). Using the ideas of
[
8
]
, we will construct the isometric left multiplier with image
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\ominus{\mathcal{H}}_{X}
. To do this, we require the fact proved by Voiculescu in
[
63
]
(see also
[
22
,
46
,
53
,
59
]
for different points of view on this), that every automorphism
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
gives rise to a unitary
U_{\varphi}\in B(\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d})
that extends the unitary on the Drury-Arveson space defined above. We refer the reader to
[
22
, Section 4]
for all the necessary information on these unitaries. In particular, for every
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
, we set
\varphi(L)=U_{\varphi}L(I_{d}\otimes U_{\varphi}^{*})
, Clearly,
\varphi(L)
is a row isometry.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\lambda\in{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
and let
\varphi_{\lambda}
be the involution that exchanges
\lambda
and
0
. Then the range of
\varphi_{\lambda}(L)
is
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\ominus{\mathbb{C}}k_{\lambda}
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}=\varphi_{\lambda}(L)({\mathbb{C}}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d})
. If
f\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\perp}
, then for
G\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
,

0=\langle\varphi_{\lambda}(L)G,f\rangle=\langle L(U_{\varphi_{\lambda}}\otimes I_{d})^{*}G,U_{\varphi_{\lambda}}^{*}f\rangle.

Since the orthogonal complement of the range of
L
is spanned by the vacuum vector
1
, it follows that
U_{\varphi_{\lambda}}^{*}f=\alpha
, for some
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
implying that
f=\frac{\alpha}{\|k_{\lambda}\|}k_{\lambda}
as
\alpha U_{\varphi_{\lambda}}1=\frac{\alpha}{\|k_{\lambda}\|}k_{\lambda}
.
∎

Given
X=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
, we now construct a row isometry inductively. We set
F_{1}(L)=\varphi_{\lambda_{1}}(L)
, and assume for
2\leq m\leq n
, that
F_{m-1}
has been defined. Then, we define

F_{m}(L)=F_{m-1}(L)(U_{m}\otimes I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}})A_{m}(L),\text{ where }A_{m}(L)=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{\lambda_{m}}&0\\
0&I_{(m-1)(d-1)}\end{pmatrix}.

Here,
U_{m}
is a scalar unitary of size
(m-1)(d-1)+1
satisfying
U_{m}e_{1}=\frac{1}{\|F_{m-1}(\lambda_{m})\|}F_{m-1}(\lambda_{m})^{*}
. Observe that
A_{m}(L)\in M_{(m-1)(d-1)+1,(m-1)(d-1)+d}(\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d})
and
F_{m}(L)
is a row of size
(m-1)(d-1)+d
. Finally, we set
F_{X}(L)=F_{n}(L)
.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
X=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
are distinct, then
F_{X}(L)
is a row isometry and the range of
F_{X}(L)
is
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\ominus{\mathcal{H}}_{X}
.

Proof.

It is rather immediate that the
F_{X}
is a row isometry by induction on
n
. Since
F_{1}
is a row isometry, we take products of isometries.

We prove the second part of the claim by induction on
n
. The case
n=1
is the content of the preceding lemma. To prove for
n
, we note that for a product of two isometries
V_{1}V_{2}
, we have
\ker(V_{2}^{*}V_{1}^{*})=\ker V_{1}^{*}\oplus V_{1}(\ker V_{2}^{*})
. Thus
\ker F_{m}(L)^{*}=\ker F_{m-1}(L)^{*}\oplus F_{m-1}(L)(\ker A_{m}(L)^{*}(U_{m}^{*}\otimes I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}))
. By induction,
\ker F_{m-1}(L)^{*}=\mathrm{Span}\{k_{\lambda_{1}},\ldots,k_{\lambda_{m-1}}\}
. Moreover, we note that by Lemma
2.10
, the kernel of
\ker A_{m}(L)^{*}={\mathbb{C}}k_{\lambda_{m}}e_{1}
. We conclude that
\dim\ker F_{m}(L)^{*}=m
. Now we compute

F_{m}(L)^{*}k_{\lambda_{m}}=A_{m}(L)^{*}(U_{m}^{*}\otimes I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}})F_{m-1}(L)^{*}k_{\lambda_{m}}=A_{m}(L)^{*}(U_{m}^{*}\otimes I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}})(F_{m-1}(\lambda_{m})^{*}\otimes k_{\lambda_{m}})=0.

This completes the proof.
∎

Let us write
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}=(\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\ominus H^{2}_{d})\oplus(H^{2}_{d}\ominus{\mathcal{H}}_{X})\oplus{\mathcal{H}}_{X}
. Then, the range projection of
F_{X}(L)
decomposes as
F_{X}(L)F_{X}(L)^{*}=2I-P_{H^{2}_{d}}-P_{{\mathcal{H}}_{X}}
. Let
M_{b_{X}}=P_{H^{2}_{d}}F_{X}(L)|_{{\mathbb{C}}^{(m-1)(d-1)+d}\otimes H^{2}_{d}}
. Then, since
H^{2}_{d}
is left multiplier coinvariant and
P_{{\mathcal{H}}_{X}}\leq P_{H^{2}_{d}}
, we get that

M_{b_{X}}M_{b_{X}}^{*}=P_{H^{2}_{d}}F_{X}(L)F_{X}(L)^{*}|_{H^{2}_{d}}=I-P_{{\mathcal{H}}_{X}}.

In particular,
M_{b_{X}}
is a partially isometric multiplier with range
H^{2}_{d}\ominus{\mathcal{H}}_{X}
. We caution the reader that the notation
b_{X}
is somewhat misleading. In fact, the row multiplier
b_{X}
depends on the choice of order on
X
and the choice of unitaries. However, when we use this construction, we will make explicit choices.

3.
The Grassmannian of coinvariant spaces

In this section, we define the space that we will study along with its connection to
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
and explore its basic properties.

Definition 3.1
.

We define
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}\subset B(H^{2}_{d})
as the space of all projections onto multiplier coinvariant
n
-dimensional subspaces. Namely,

\mathbb{P}_{d,n}=\left\{P\in B(H^{2}_{d})\mid P=P^{2}=P^{*},\,\forall f\in{\mathcal{M}}_{d}:\,PM_{f}^{*}P=M_{f}^{*}P,\,\dim(PH^{2}_{d})=n\right\}.

The topology on
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
is the norm topology.

Now, we study the general form of an
n
-dimensional multiplier coinvariant subspace of
H^{2}_{d}
. We are convinced that these results are well-known to experts. However, since we have not found a reference, we include them here for completeness.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
M_{z}=(M_{z_{1},\ldots,M_{z_{d}}})
be the
d
-tuple of multiplication operators by coordinate functions defined on
H_{d}^{2}
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
. Then

\bigcap_{|\alpha|=n}\ker(M_{z}^{*}-\bar{\lambda}I)^{\alpha}=\mathrm{Span}\left\{k^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}\mid|\beta|\leq n-1\right\}.

Proof.

It is immediate to check that for every multi-index
\beta
with
|\beta|<|\alpha|
and every
f\in H^{2}_{d}
,

\langle f,(M_{z}^{*}-\bar{\lambda}I)^{\alpha}k^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}\rangle=\dfrac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial z^{\beta}}\left((z-\lambda)^{\alpha}f\right)(\lambda)=0.

Hence,
\mathrm{Span}\left\{k^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}\mid|\beta|\leq n-1\right\}\subset\bigcap_{|\alpha|=n}\ker(M_{z}^{*}-\bar{\lambda}I)^{\alpha}
. Now, by considering appropriate polynomials, we can easily see that the set
\left\{k^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}\mid|\beta|\leq n-1\right\}
is linearly independent. Now let
f\in\bigcap_{|\alpha|=n}\ker(M_{z}^{*}-\bar{\lambda}I)^{\alpha}
. For every polynomial
p\in{\mathbb{C}}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]
, we can write
p=q+\sum_{|\alpha|=n}(z-\lambda)^{\alpha}q_{\alpha}
, where
\deg q\leq n-1
. Hence,
\langle f,p\rangle=\langle f,q\rangle
. This implies that every functional on the joint kernel is a linear combination of inner products against
\{z^{\beta}\}_{|\beta|\leq n-1}
. Consequently,
\dim\bigcap_{|\alpha|=n}\ker(M_{z}^{*}-\bar{\lambda}I)^{\alpha}\leq\dim\mathrm{Span}\left\{k^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}\mid|\beta|\leq n-1\right\}
and we are done.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}\subset H^{2}_{d}
be a multiplier coinvariant subspace with
\dim{\mathcal{H}}=n
. Then there exist points
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}\in{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
, such that
{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
( a non-orthogonal direct sum), where for
1\leq j\leq m
,
k_{\overline{\lambda_{j}}}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{j}\subseteq\bigcap_{|\alpha|=n}\ker(M_{z}^{*}-\lambda_{j}I)^{\alpha}

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}\subset H^{2}_{d}
be a finite-dimensional multiplier coinvariant subspace. Let us consider the tuple
A=M_{z}^{*}|_{{\mathcal{H}}}
. This tuple is a commuting pure column contraction, and its spectrum is contained in
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
. Let
{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}R_{\lambda_{j}}(A)
be the decomposition of
{\mathcal{H}}
into root subspaces of
A
. Then, by the previous lemma
k_{\overline{\lambda_{j}}}\in R_{\lambda_{j}}(A)
. Moreover,
R_{\lambda_{j}}(A)\subseteq\bigcap_{|\alpha|=\dim{\mathcal{H}}}\ker(M_{z}^{*}-\lambda_{j})^{\alpha}
. Therefore,
R_{\lambda_{j}}(A)
is spanned by a kernel function and some directional derivatives of it at
\overline{\lambda_{j}}
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The root subspaces of a single matrix are spanned by Jordan chains and eigenvectors. In our case, every joint eigenvalue has a one-dimensional eigenspace. Hence, the directional derivatives that span
R_{\lambda_{j}}(A)
are a form of generalized Jordan chain.

Now note that the above discussion leads us to define a map from
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
to
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
.

Definition 3.5
.

We define a map
\Psi_{d,n}\colon\mathbb{P}_{d,n}\to{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
given by
\Psi_{d,n}(P)=\sigma_{J}(PM_{z}|_{PH^{2}_{d}})
.

Theorem 3.6
.

The map
\Psi_{d,n}
is surjective and continuous.

Proof.

Let us prove continuity first. Let
\{P_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
be a sequence that converges to some
P\in\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
. Let us fix an isometry
W\colon{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to H^{2}_{d}
with
\mathrm{ran}W=PH^{2}_{d}
. Applying Lemma
2.7
, we obtain a sequence of isometries
V_{k}\colon{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to H^{2}_{d}
with
\mathrm{ran}V_{k}=P_{k}H^{2}_{d}
and, moreover,

\|P_{k}-P\|\leq\|V_{k}-W\|\leq\sqrt{2}\|P_{k}-P\|.

Set
A_{k}=V_{k}^{*}M_{z}(I_{d}\otimes V_{k})
and
A=W^{*}M_{z}(I_{d}\otimes W)
. We note that
\sigma_{J}(A_{k})=\Psi_{d,n}(P_{k})
and
\sigma_{J}(A)=\Psi_{d,n}(P)
. It is immediate that
\|A_{k}-A\|_{row}\leq 2\sqrt{2}\|P_{k}-P\|
. By Proposition
2.8
, we get that
\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{o}(\sigma_{J}(A_{k}),\sigma_{J}(A))=0
. Since
A
is a strict row contraction, its joint spectrum is contained in some
r\overline{{\mathbb{B}}_{d}}
for
0<r<1
. Changing
r
slightly, we see that for
k
big enough, the joint spectrum of the
A_{k}
is contained in
r\overline{{\mathbb{B}}_{d}}
. We conclude by applying Lemma
2.1
that
\Psi_{d,n}(P_{k})\to\Psi_{d,n}(P)
.

To prove surjectivity, we fix an arbitrary
\lambda\in{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and consider the subspace
{\mathcal{H}}_{n,\lambda}^{(1)}=\mathrm{Span}\{k_{\lambda},k_{\lambda}^{(e_{1})},\ldots,k_{\lambda}^{(ne_{1})}\}
, where
e_{1}
is the first vector of the standard basis. We argue that
{\mathcal{H}}_{n,\lambda}^{(1)}
is multiplier coinvariant. If this is true, then
\Psi_{d,n}\left(P_{{\mathcal{H}}^{(1)}_{n,\lambda}}\right)=n[\lambda]
. Now, sums of such spaces can give us any point with multiplicity. It is immediate that points with no multiplicity are in the image of
\Psi_{d,n}
. Finally, for
f\in{\mathcal{M}}_{d}
and
g\in H^{2}_{d}
, we have that

\langle g,M_{f}^{*}k_{\lambda}^{(ne_{1})}\rangle=\dfrac{\partial^{n}(fg)}{\partial z_{1}^{n}}(\lambda)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\dfrac{\partial^{n-j}f}{\partial z_{1}^{n-j}}(\lambda)\dfrac{\partial^{j}g}{\partial z_{1}^{j}}(\lambda)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\dfrac{\partial^{n-j}f}{\partial z_{1}^{n-j}}(\lambda)\langle g,k_{\lambda}^{(je_{1})}\rangle.

verifying that
M_{f}^{*}k_{\lambda}^{(ne_{1})}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n,\lambda}^{(1)}
.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

It is clear that the choice of
e_{1}
in the proof above is arbitrary.

Example 3.8
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}=\mathrm{Span}\{1,k_{1/n}\}\subset H^{2}({\mathbb{D}})
and
{\mathcal{H}}=\mathrm{Span}\{1,z\}\subset H^{2}({\mathbb{D}})
. Let
P_{n}=P_{{\mathcal{H}}_{n}}
and
P=P_{{\mathcal{H}}}
. It is clear that
\Psi_{1,2}(P_{n})=[0]+[1/n]
and
\Psi_{1,2}(P)=2[0]
. To see what happens on the side of the projections, we set

f_{n}(z)=\dfrac{k_{1/n}-1}{\|k_{1/n}-1\|}=\sqrt{n^{2}-1}(k_{1/n}-1)=\dfrac{\sqrt{n^{2}-1}}{n}\dfrac{z}{1-z/n}.

It is clear now that
\{1,f_{n}\}
is an orthonormal basis for
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
. Now, we note that

\|f_{n}-z\|^{2}=\left\|\dfrac{\sqrt{n^{2}-1}}{n}\dfrac{1}{1-z/n}-1\right\|^{2}=2\left(1-\dfrac{\sqrt{n^{2}-1}}{n}\right).

In particular,
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}=z
. Now for a unit vector
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{D}})
,

\|P_{n}f-Pf\|=\left\|\langle f,f_{n}\rangle f_{n}-\langle f,z\rangle z\right\|\leq|\langle f,f_{n}\rangle|\|f_{n}-z\|+|\langle f,f_{n}-z\rangle|\rightarrow 0.

This shows, in particular, that the distance induced by the norm on finite-dimensional complete Pick RKHSs is different from the Banach-Mazur distance of Ofek, Pandey, and Shalit by
[
48
, Example 6.2]

The following example shows that for
d\geq 2
, the map
\Psi_{d,n}
is not invertible.

Example 3.9
.

Consider the following subspaces of
H^{2}_{2}
:
{\mathcal{H}}_{1,n}=\mathrm{Span}\{1,k_{1/ne_{1}}\}
,
{\mathcal{H}}_{2,n}=\mathrm{Span}\{1,k_{1/ne_{2}}\}
,
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}=\mathrm{Span}\{1,z_{1}\}
, and
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}=\mathrm{Span}\{1,z_{2}\}
. Denote by
P_{1,n}
,
P_{2,n}
,
P_{1}
, and
P_{2}
the respective projection. As in the previous example, it is not hard to show that
P_{1,n}\rightarrow P_{1}
and
P_{2,n}\rightarrow P_{2}
. Clearly,
\Psi_{2,2}(P_{1})=\Psi_{2,2}(P_{2})=2[0]
,
\Psi_{2,2}(P_{1,n})=[0]+[1/ne_{1}]
, and
\Psi_{2,2}(P_{2,n})=[0]+[1/ne_{2}]
. The later sequences converge to
2[0]
.

Remark 3.10
.

Let
d\geq 2
. The fiber
\Psi_{d,2}^{-1}(2[0])
can easily be described using Proposition
3.3
as the collection of projection onto spaces of the form
\mathrm{Span}\{1,f\}
, where
f
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
1
. It is easy to see that the map
\Psi_{d,2}^{-1}(2[0])\to\mathbb{P}^{d-1}({\mathbb{C}})
, that sends a projection to the point corresponding to the coefficients of
f
is a homeomorphism. In this sense, the map
\Psi_{d,2}
and, more generally,
\Psi_{d,n}
are akin to blowdown maps from algebraic geometry.

4.
The subspace
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}^{0}

As we saw, a naive attempt to construct an inverse map will immediately run into an obstacle for
d\geq 2
. The intuitive reason is that though the space
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
keeps track of multiplicities of points in
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
, the space
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
also keeps track of “directions” at each point with multiplicity. Since multiple points are problematic, we must restrict our attention to
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
.

Definition 4.1
.

We set
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}^{0}=\Psi_{d,n}^{-1}\left({\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}\right)
. In particular, This is the space of projections onto multiplier coinvariant
n
-dimensional subspaces of
H^{2}_{d}
spanned by
n
distinct kernel functions.

Given
[X]\in{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
, let us fix
b_{X}\colon{\mathbb{B}}_{d}\to{\mathbb{B}}_{(n-1)(d-1)+d}
a multiplier, such that
M_{b_{X}}
is a partial isometry with range
H^{2}_{d}\ominus{\mathcal{H}}_{X}
obtained in Subsection
2.5
. Recall that at each step of the construction of
b_{X}
, there was a choice of a unitary. We will show that this choice does not affect the corresponding projection.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a Hilbert space and fix a unit vector
w\in{\mathcal{H}}
. For every
\varepsilon>0
, if
v_{1},v_{2}\in{\mathcal{H}}
are unit vectors with
\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|<\varepsilon
, then for every unitary
U_{1}\in B({\mathcal{H}})
, such that
U_{1}w=v_{1}
, there exists a unitary
U_{2}\in B({\mathcal{H}})
, such that
U_{2}w=v_{2}
and
\|U_{1}-U_{2}\|<\sqrt{2}\varepsilon
.

Proof.

If
v_{2}=e^{i\theta}v_{1}
, then we set
U_{2}=e^{-i\theta}U_{1}
. Hence,
\|U_{1}-U_{2}\|=|1-e^{-i\theta}|=\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|
. Therefore, we will assume from now on that
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are linearly independent.

Since we can consider
U_{1}^{*}
and
U_{2}^{*}
, we will assume that
U_{1}v_{1}=w
. Now let
{\mathcal{K}}=\{v_{1},v_{2}\}^{\perp}
. We set
U_{2}|_{{\mathcal{K}}}=U_{1}|_{{\mathcal{K}}}
. Let
v_{1}^{\prime}=\frac{v_{2}-\langle v_{2},v_{1}\rangle v_{1}}{\|v_{2}-\langle v_{2},v_{1}\rangle v_{1}\|}
and
v_{2}^{\prime}=-\frac{v_{1}-\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle v_{2}}{\|v_{1}-\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle v_{2}\|}
. Let
U_{1}v_{1}^{\prime}=w^{\prime}
and set
U_{2}v_{2}=w
and
U_{2}v_{2}^{\prime}=w^{\prime}
. Note that

\|U_{1}v_{2}-U_{2}v_{2}\|=\|U_{1}v_{2}-U_{1}v_{1}+U_{1}v_{1}-w\|\leq\|v_{2}-v_{1}\|.

Similarly,
\|U_{1}v_{2}^{\prime}-U_{2}v_{2}^{\prime}\|\leq\|v_{1}^{\prime}-v_{2}^{\prime}\|=\|v_{2}-v_{1}\|
.
Therefore, writing every unit vector
x=\alpha v_{2}+\beta v_{2}^{\prime}+v
with
v\in{\mathcal{K}}
, we have that

\|U_{1}x-U_{2}x\|\leq|\alpha|\|U_{1}v_{2}-U_{2}v_{2}\|+|\beta|\|U_{1}v_{2}^{\prime}-U_{2}v_{2}^{\prime}\|\leq\sqrt{2}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|.

∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
[X_{m}]\in{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
be a sequence that converges to
[X]\in{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
. Fix
b_{X}
, as above. Then, there exists a choice of
b_{X_{m}}
, such that
b_{X_{m}}\to b_{X}
uniformly on compacta in
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
.

Proof.

Let us fix a preimage
X=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
of
[X]
. By
[
48
, Proposition 3.3]
, if we set
\delta=\min\{d_{ph}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})\mid 1\leq i,j\leq n\}
, then for
d_{s}([X],[X_{m}])<\delta/2
, the symmetric distance coincides with the Hausdorff distance. In particular, in every pseudohyperbolic ball of radius less than
\delta/2
around
\lambda_{i}
, there exists precisely one point of
[X_{m}]
for
m
big enough. In other words, up to ignoring a prefix of the sequence, we may choose a preimage
X_{m}=\{\lambda_{1,m},\ldots,\lambda_{n,m}\}
, such that for all
1\leq j\leq n
,
\lim_{m\to\infty}\lambda_{j,m}=\lambda_{j}
.

Now we observe that it is not hard to show that if a sequence of points
\mu_{m}\in{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
converges to
\mu
, the corresponding involutions
\varphi_{\mu_{m}}
converge uniformly on compacta to
\varphi_{\mu}
.

Now we prove the claim by induction on
n
. The base case is handled by the above paragraph. Let us write
X^{\prime}=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1}\}
and, similarly,
X_{m}=\{\lambda_{1,m},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1,m}\}
. Let us assume that we have constructed
b_{X_{m}^{\prime}}
, such that
b_{X_{m}^{\prime}}
converges uniformly on compacta in
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
to
b_{X^{\prime}}
. Now take
0<r<1
and fix an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
. Since
\lim_{m\to\infty}\lambda_{n,m}=\lambda_{n}
, for big enough
m
, we have

\|b_{X_{m}^{\prime}}(\lambda_{n,m})-b_{X^{\prime}}(\lambda_{n})\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{3\sqrt{2}}.

In particular, the vectors
v_{m}=\frac{1}{\|b_{X_{m}^{\prime}}(\lambda_{n,m})\|}b_{X_{m}^{\prime}}(\lambda_{n,m})
and
v=\frac{1}{\|b_{X^{\prime}}(\lambda_{n})\|}b_{X^{\prime}}(\lambda_{n})
are close. By construction,

b_{X}(z)=b_{X^{\prime}}(z)U\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{\lambda_{n}}(z)&0\\
0&I_{(n-1)(d-1)}\end{pmatrix}.

Here,
U
is a unitary satisfying
Ue_{1}=v
. Let
U_{m}
be a unitary obtained from Lemma
4.2
such that
U_{m}e_{1}=v_{m}
and
\|U-U_{m}\|<\varepsilon/3
. Set

b_{X_{m}}(z)=b_{X_{m}^{\prime}}(z)U_{m}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{\lambda_{n,m}(z)}&0\\
0&I_{(n-1)(d-1)}\end{pmatrix}.

Then,

\displaystyle\|b_{X}(z)-b_{X_{m}}(z)\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|b_{X^{\prime}}(z)U\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{\lambda_{n}}(z)&0\\
0&I_{(n-1)(d-1)}\end{pmatrix}-b_{X_{m}^{\prime}}(z)U_{m}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{\lambda_{n,m}(z)}&0\\
0&I_{(n-1)(d-1)}\end{pmatrix}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|U\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{\lambda_{n}}(z)&0\\
0&I_{(n-1)(d-1)}\end{pmatrix}-U_{m}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{\lambda_{n,m}(z)}&0\\
0&I_{(n-1)(d-1)}\end{pmatrix}\right\|

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\|b_{X^{\prime}}(z)-b_{X_{m}^{\prime}}(z)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{\lambda_{n}}(z)-\varphi_{\lambda_{n,m}(z)}\|+\|b_{X^{\prime}}(z)-b_{X_{m}^{\prime}}(z)\|+\dfrac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Now, by induction, we have uniform convergence for
|z|\leq r
of
b_{X_{m}^{\prime}}
to
b_{X^{\prime}}
. Hence, for
m
big enough, the above expression is less than
\varepsilon
for all
|z|\leq r
.
∎

We note that though
b_{X}
is not uniquely defined by
X
, the operator
I-M_{b_{X}}M_{b_{X}}^{*}
is the projection onto the space spanned by the kernels at the points of
X
. Therefore, the map in the following theorem is well-defined.

Theorem 4.4
.

Define a map
\Phi_{d,n}\colon{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}\to\mathbb{P}_{d,n}^{0}
by
[X]\mapsto I-M_{b_{X}}M_{b_{X}}^{*}
. Then
\Phi_{d,n}
is continuous,
\Psi_{d,n}\circ\Phi_{d,n}=\mathrm{Id}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}}
, and
\Phi_{d,n}\circ\Psi_{d,n}|_{\mathbb{P}_{d,n}^{0}}=\mathrm{Id}_{\mathbb{P}_{d,n}^{0}}
.

Proof.

Let
\{[X_{m}]\}\in{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
be a sequence that converges to
[X]\in{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
. By
[
28
, Lemma 5]
, the norm and SOT topologies restricted to
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
coincide. Hence, it suffices to show that
M_{b_{X_{m}}}M_{b_{X_{m}}}^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle SOT}}{{\rightarrow}}M_{b_{X}}M_{b_{X}}^{*}
. Fix some preimage
X\in{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n}
of
[X]
and construct
b_{X}
. By the preceding proposition, we can choose preimages
X_{m}
and construct
b_{X_{m}}
, such that
b_{X_{m}}
converges to
b_{X}
uniformly on compacta. Since the sequence is bounded, we have that
M_{b_{X_{m}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle WOT}}{{\longrightarrow}}M_{b_{X}}
. It is then a well known fact on multipliers that
M_{b_{X_{m}}}^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle SOT}}{{\longrightarrow}}M_{b_{X}}^{*}
. Again since the sequences are bounded, we have that
M_{b_{X_{m}}}M_{b_{X_{m}}}^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle SOT}}{{\longrightarrow}}M_{b_{X}}M_{b_{X}}^{*}
as desired.
∎

The case of the Hardy space
H^{2}({\mathbb{D}})
is different. The intuitive reason is that there are no (complex) directions to obstruct the inverse map in the case of the disc. Given
X\in{\mathbb{X}}_{1,n}
, we can define the associate Blaschke product counting multiplicities.

Proposition 4.5
.

The map
\Psi_{1,n}\colon\mathbb{P}_{1,n}\to{\mathbb{X}}_{1,n}
is a homeomorphism.

Proof.

The proof follows the same lines as the proof of the theorem. However, it is much less complicated, for let
[X]=\sum_{j=1}^{m}n_{j}[\lambda_{j}]\in{\mathbb{X}}_{1,n}
, then we can associate to
[X]
the Blaschke product

b_{X}(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\dfrac{z-\lambda_{j}}{1-\overline{\lambda_{j}}z}\right)^{n_{j}}.

This allows us to extend the argument in the theorem to the points with multiplicity, as well.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

The group
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
acts on
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
by
\varphi\cdot P=U_{\varphi}^{*}PU_{\varphi}
. To see this, we first note that
\varphi\cdot P
is a projection with
n
-dimensional range. Moreover, for every
f\in{\mathcal{M}}_{d}
,

(\varphi\cdot P)M_{f}^{*}(\varphi\cdot P)=U_{\varphi}^{*}PU_{\varphi}M_{f}^{*}U_{\varphi}^{*}PU_{\varphi}=U_{\varphi}^{*}PM_{f\circ\varphi}^{*}PU_{\varphi}=U_{\varphi}^{*}M_{f\circ\varphi}^{*}PU_{\varphi}=M_{f}^{*}(\varphi\cdot P).

Now for
P\in\mathbb{P}_{d,n}^{0}
, with
\Psi_{d,n}(P)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}[\lambda_{j}]
and
\varphi=\varphi_{\mu}
, we have

\displaystyle(\varphi_{\mu}\cdot P)k_{\varphi_{\mu}(\lambda_{j})}=(U_{\varphi_{\mu}}PU_{\varphi_{\mu}})k_{\varphi_{\mu}(\lambda_{j})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\|\mu\|^{2}}(U_{\varphi_{\mu}}P)\overline{k_{\mu}(\varphi_{\mu}(\lambda_{j}))}k_{\lambda_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\sqrt{1-\|\mu\|^{2}}}{1-\langle\mu,\varphi_{\mu}(\lambda_{j})\rangle}U_{\varphi_{\mu}}k_{\lambda_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1-\|\mu\|^{2}}{(1-\langle\mu,\varphi_{\mu}(\lambda_{j})\rangle)(1-\langle\mu,\lambda_{j}\rangle)}k_{\varphi_{\mu}(\lambda_{j})}.

Note that by
[
58
, Theorem 2.2.2(iii)]
,

1-\langle\mu,\varphi_{\mu}(\lambda_{j})\rangle=1-\langle\varphi_{\mu}(0),\varphi_{\mu}(\lambda_{j})\rangle=\dfrac{1-\|\mu\|^{2}}{1-\langle\mu,\lambda_{j}\rangle}.

Hence,
(\varphi_{\mu}\cdot P)k_{\varphi_{\mu}(\lambda_{j})}=k_{\varphi_{\mu}(\lambda_{j})}
. Therefore,
\Psi_{d,n}(\varphi_{\mu}\cdot P)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}[\varphi_{\mu}(\lambda_{j})]
. The same claim holds if
\varphi
is a unitary. Hence, we get that
\Psi_{d,n}
is
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
-equivariant. From
[
43
, Remark 1.1.1]
we conclude that the action of
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
on
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
is proper. Moreover, the results above combine to show that
\mathbb{P}_{n-1,n}^{0}/\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
is homeomorphic to
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}
.

5.
Bundles of complete Pick spaces and their multiplier algebras

In this section, we will use classical results on Hilbert and Banach space bundles to discuss deformations of complete Pick spaces and the corresponding multiplier algebras. Some old but good references on bundles are
[
28
]
and
[
27
]
.

The tautological bundle on
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
is
P\mapsto PH^{2}_{d}
. This is a vector bundle by
[
31
, Proposition 2.3]
. We will denote the tautological bundle by
{\mathcal{E}}_{d,n}
. Note that
{\mathcal{E}}_{d,n}
is the restriction of the tautological bundle on the strong Grassmannian of
H^{2}_{d}
in the sense of Dixmier and Douady. Namely, if
\mathrm{Gr}_{f}
is the collection of all projections in
B(H^{2}_{d})
equipped with the strong topology, then
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
embeds into
\mathrm{Gr}_{f}
since the strong and norm topologies on
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
coincide. Moreover, we can consider
{\mathfrak{M}}_{d,n}=\bigsqcup_{P\in\mathbb{P}_{d,n}}P{\mathcal{M}}_{d}|_{PH^{2}_{d}}
. Note that each
f\in{\mathcal{M}}_{d}
gives rise to a section of
{\mathfrak{M}}_{d,n}
given by
\gamma_{f}(P)=PM_{f}|_{PH^{2}_{d}}
. We denote by
\Lambda
the collection of all such sections. By
[
27
, Proposition 10.2.3]
, it suffices to show that the map
P\mapsto\|\gamma_{f}(P)\|
is continuous to deduce that
\Lambda
generates a Banach algebra bundle structure on
{\mathfrak{M}}_{d,n}
. The continuity of the norm is, however, immediate since the topology on
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
is of norm convergence. By
[
35
, Theorem II.13.18]
, there is a unique topology on
{\mathfrak{M}}_{d,n}
, such that the Banach bundle structure
\Gamma
generated by
\Lambda
consists of continuous sections. We denote by
\Gamma_{b}
the collection of all bounded continuous sections and note that
\Lambda\subset\Gamma_{b}
. We equip
\Gamma_{b}
with the norm
\|x\|=\sup_{P\in\mathbb{P}_{d,n}}\|x(P)\|
. It is then clear that
\Gamma_{b}
is a Banach algebra.

For every
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we can consider the construction above for
M_{m}({\mathcal{M}}_{d})
. Namely, we consider the bundle
P\mapsto(I_{m}\otimes P)M_{m}({\mathcal{M}}_{d})|_{{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes PH^{2}_{d}}
. As mentioned above, the collection
M_{m}(\Lambda)
generates a Banach algebra structure on this bundle. The collection of continuous sections can be identified with
M_{m}(\Gamma)
. Therefore, we can identify this bundle with
M_{m}({\mathfrak{M}}_{d,n})\cong M_{m}\otimes{\mathfrak{M}}_{d,n}
. We summarize the discussion in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.1
.

We have a Hilbert space bundle
{\mathcal{E}}_{d,n}
and a Banach algebra bundle
{\mathfrak{M}}_{d,n}
on
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
. Moreover, for every
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have a Banach algebra bundle
M_{m}({\mathfrak{M}}_{d,n})
. If
\Gamma_{b}
is the collection of all bounded continuous sections of
{\mathfrak{M}}_{d,n}
equipped with the sup norm, then
\Gamma_{b}
is an operator algebra.

Proof.

We need only to prove that
\Gamma_{b}
is an operator algebra. To see that
\Gamma_{b}
is an operator space, we note that
M_{m}(\Gamma_{b})
is precisely the collection of bounded continuous sections of
M_{m}({\mathfrak{M}}_{d,n})
as obtained in the construction above. Consequently, every fiber turns out to be an operator algebra. Therefore, by Ruan’s axioms and the condition of the Blecher-Ruan-Sinclair theorem
[
49
]
, it follows that
\Gamma_{b}
is an operator algebra.
∎

At this point, we would like to point the reader’s attention to a natural question. We note that if
Z
is a paracompact space and
\varphi\colon Z\to\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
is a continuous map, then we obtain a vector bundle on
Z
via
{\mathcal{F}}=\varphi^{*}{\mathcal{E}}_{d,n}
. If we, further, assume that
\varphi(Z)\subset\mathbb{P}_{d,n}^{0}
, the bundle
\varphi^{*}{\mathcal{E}}_{d,n}
is a bundle of complete Pick spaces on
Z
. If we identify
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}^{0}
with
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
via the maps from Theorem
4.4
, then we have a map
\varphi\colon Z\to{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
. Recall that
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n,0}
is the collection of all
n
-tuples of distinct points in
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
. If we write
\pi_{d,n}
for the symmetrization map, then
\pi_{d,n}\colon{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n,0}\to{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
is a principal
S_{n}
bundle. Therefore, we can obtain a principal
S_{n}
-bundle on
Z
via
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n,0}\times_{{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}}Z
and the projection map onto the second coordinate, where the fiber product is constructed, of course, using
\varphi
. Let
z_{0}\in Z
and
\varphi(z_{0})\in V\subset{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
be a neighborhood, such that the principal bundle is trivialized over it. We can construct a frame of
{\mathcal{F}}
on
\phi^{-1}(V)
by taking the sections
z\mapsto k_{\lambda_{j}(z)}
, where
\lambda_{1}(z),\ldots,\lambda_{n}(z)
is the trivialization of the
S_{n}
-bundle on

Z=\bigcup_{\alpha}U_{\alpha}
for the

-bundle, we can see that the structure group of
{\mathcal{F}}
can be reduced to
S_{n}
. Moreover, for every
\alpha

k_{\alpha}(z)=\left(\langle k_{\lambda_{j}(z)},k_{\lambda_{i}(z)}\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}
. For every
\alpha
and
\beta
in our index set, there exists a permutation matrix
h_{\alpha,\beta}
, such that
h_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}k_{\alpha}h_{\alpha,\beta}=k_{\beta}
. In fact,
h_{\alpha,\beta}
is the transition map for the frames above. Rewriting this we see that
k_{\alpha}h_{\alpha,\beta}=h_{\alpha,\beta}k_{\beta}
. Hence, we can define a map by
f_{\alpha}\colon U_{\alpha}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to U_{\alpha}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
by
f_{\alpha}(z,\xi)=(z,k_{\alpha}(z)\xi)
. Then, for every

and every

(h_{\alpha,\beta}f_{\alpha})(z,\xi)=(z,h_{\alpha,\beta}k_{\alpha}(z)\xi)=(z,k_{\beta}(z)h_{\alpha,\beta}\xi)=(f_{\beta}h_{\alpha,\beta})(z,\xi).

Therefore,
f
defines an automorphism of
{\mathcal{F}}
. This leads us to the following definition.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
Z
be a paracompact space and
{\mathcal{F}}
a Hermitian vector bundle of dimension

with structure bundle

. A complete Pick bundle structure on

is given by a covering

of

of
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathcal{F}})
, such that on every element of
{\mathfrak{U}}
, the bundle

is trivial and

gives us a continuous family of complete Pick kernels on
n
points. We call the triple

a complete Pick bundle.

6.
Cowen-Douglas class operators from hypersurfaces

Assume that
p\in{\mathbb{C}}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]
is a reduced homogeneous polynomial of degree
n
. Let
Z(p)
be the variety cut out by
p
and
V(p)={\mathbb{B}}_{d}\cap Z(p)
. We may assume (up to a unitary) that
p(0,\ldots,0,1)\neq 0
.

Lemma 6.1
.

There exists
c<1
, such that for all
\lambda\in V(p)
,
|\lambda_{d}|\leq c
.

Proof.

Let
e_{d}=(0,\ldots,0,1)
and
\overline{V(p)}=\overline{{\mathbb{B}}_{d}}\cap Z(p)
. Note that
\lambda_{d}=\langle\lambda,e_{d}\rangle
. This function is continuous on
\overline{V(p)}
and bounded by
1
by Cauchy-Schwartz. However, by our assumption,
p(e_{d})\neq 0
. Thus, by the equality close of Cauchy-Schwartz and the fact that
p
is homogenous, we get that
\lambda\mapsto|\lambda_{d}|
never attains the value
1
on
\overline{V(p)}
. By compactness, there exists
c<1
, such that
\max_{\lambda\in\overline{V(p)}}|\lambda_{d}|=c
.
∎

We will write
z^{\prime}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d-1})
. We aim to produce a vector bundle of complete Pick spaces from projecting the hypersurface cut out by
p
onto the coordinate plane of
z^{\prime}
. The following lemma is necessary since we are dealing with the part of the hyperplane inside
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

There exists a neighborhood
U
of the origin in the coordinate plane of
z^{\prime}
, such that for every
\lambda\in U
,
p(\lambda,z_{d})
has precisely
n
roots counting multiplicities in
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
.

Proof.

This follows from the continuity of roots of
p(\lambda,z)
in
\lambda
and the fact that
p(0,z)=az^{n}
, for some
a\neq 0
.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

By shrinking
U
, if necessary, we may assume that
U
is invariant under conjugation (say, a polydisc or a ball). From now on, we will assume that it is the case when we speak of
U
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{p}=(pH^{2}_{d})^{\perp}
. Note that
{\mathcal{H}}_{p}=\oplus_{m=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{H}}_{p,m}
, where
{\mathcal{H}}_{p,m}
is the
m
-th graded component of
{\mathcal{H}}_{p}
. We will denote by
P_{m}
the projection onto
{\mathcal{H}}_{p,m}
, for
m\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
. Let
S_{j}=P_{{\mathcal{H}}_{p}}M_{z_{j}}|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{p}}
for
1\leq j\leq d
. Let
S=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{d})
be the corresponding row contraction. Recall that
SS^{*}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}S_{j}S_{j}^{*}=I-P_{0}
, where
P_{0}
is the projection onto the graded component of degree
0
in
{\mathcal{H}}_{p}
.

Now let
U
be as above and
\lambda\in U
. Set
{\mathcal{H}}_{p,\lambda}={\mathcal{H}}_{p}\cap\bigcap_{j=1}^{d-1}\ker\left(M_{z_{j}}-\lambda_{j}I\right)^{*}
and observe that

{\mathcal{H}}_{p,\lambda}^{\perp}=\overline{pH^{2}_{d}}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(z_{j}-\lambda_{j})H^{2}_{d}.

Let
J_{p,\lambda}\subset{\mathbb{C}}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]
be the ideal generated by
p
and
z_{j}-\lambda_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,z_{d-1}
. Therefore, by what we have above,
{\mathcal{H}}_{p,\lambda}=J_{p,\lambda}^{\perp}
.

Lemma 6.4
.

We have that
\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{p,\lambda}=n
and if
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r}
are the roots of
p(\lambda,z)
of multiplicities
m_{1},\ldots,m_{r}
, respectively, then
{\mathcal{H}}_{p,\lambda}
is spanned by
\{k_{(\lambda,\mu_{j})}^{(\ell e_{d})}\}_{j=1,\ell=0}^{r,m_{j}-1}
, where
e_{d}
is the last vector of the standard basis of
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
.

Proof.

Fix
1\leq j\leq k
and
0\leq\ell\leq m_{j}-1
. For every
f\in H^{2}_{d}
and
1\leq i\leq d-1
, we have that

\left\langle f,(M_{z_{i}}-\lambda_{i})^{*}k_{(\lambda,\mu_{j})}^{(\ell e_{d})}\right\rangle=\left\langle(z_{i}-\lambda_{i})f,k_{(\lambda,\mu_{j})}^{(\ell e_{d})}\right\rangle=\dfrac{\partial^{\ell}}{\partial z_{d}^{\ell}}\left((z_{i}-\lambda_{i})f\right)(\lambda,\mu_{j})=0.

Hence,
k_{(\lambda,\mu_{j})}^{(\ell e_{d})}\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{d-1}\ker(M_{z_{i}}-\lambda_{i}I)^{*}
. Now for
f\in H^{2}_{d}
, we have that

\left\langle pf,k_{(\lambda,\mu_{j})}^{(\ell e_{d})}\right\rangle=\sum_{b=0}^{\ell}\dfrac{\partial^{b}p}{\partial z_{d}^{b}}(\lambda,\mu)\dfrac{\partial^{\ell-b}f}{\partial z_{d}^{\ell-b}}(\lambda,\mu)=0.

The last equality follows from the fact that
\dfrac{\partial^{b}p}{\partial z_{d}^{b}}(\lambda,\mu)=0
for
0\leq b\leq m_{j}-1
. Hence,
k_{(\lambda,\mu_{j})}^{(\ell e_{d})}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{p,\lambda}
. Moreover, the vectors
k_{(\lambda,\mu_{j})}^{(\ell e_{d})}
are linearly independent. Thus,
\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{p,\lambda}\geq n
.

On the other hand, the quotient
{\mathbb{C}}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]/J_{p,\lambda}
is finite dimensional, since it is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{C}}[z]/(p(\lambda,z))
. The dimension of the latter space is
n
. Now we can choose a basis
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}
for a complement of
J_{p,\lambda}
. Hence, we can write every
q\in{\mathbb{C}}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]
as
q=q_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}q_{j}
, where
q_{0}\in J_{p,\lambda}
. Hence, for every
f\in{\mathcal{H}}_{p,\lambda}
,

\langle q,f\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}\langle q_{j},f\rangle.

In other words,
f
is determined completely, by
\langle q_{j},f\rangle
for
j=1,\ldots,n
. We conclude that
\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{p,\lambda}=n
.
∎

Therefore, we obtain a map
\varphi\colon U\to\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
given by
\varphi(\lambda)=P_{{\mathcal{H}}_{p,\lambda}}
. To show that this map is continuous, we will show that the tuple of operators
S^{\prime*}=(S_{1}^{*},\ldots,S_{d-1}^{*})
is in the Cowen-Douglas class for
U
. To do this, we first observe that it is clear that for
\lambda\in U
,
\ker(S^{\prime*}-\bar{\lambda}I)={\mathcal{H}}_{p,\lambda}
. Moreover, if
f\in{\mathcal{H}}_{p}
is orthogonal to
\bigvee_{\lambda\in U}{\mathcal{H}}_{p,\lambda}
, then
f=0
. This condition, in particular, implies that
f
is identically
0
on a neighborhood of
0
in
V(p)
. Hence, by analyticity, it is identically
0
on
V(p)
. Therefore, we only need to show that the range of the operator
S^{\prime*}-\overline{\lambda}I\colon{\mathcal{H}}_{p}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{p}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{d-1}
is closed, for each
\lambda\in U
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\lambda\in U
,
f\in\mathcal{H}_{p}
and
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r}\in{\mathbb{C}}
be the roots of the one variable polynomial
p(\lambda,\cdot)
with multiplicities
m_{1},\ldots,m_{r}
. Then,
f
takes the form

f=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(S_{j}-\lambda_{j})h_{j}

for some
h_{1},\ldots,h_{d-1}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}
if and only if every point
(\lambda,\mu_{j})
is a root of
f
of order
m_{j}
, for
1\leq j\leq r
.

Proof.

If
f=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(S_{j}-\lambda_{j})h_{j}
for some
h_{1},\ldots,h_{d-1}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}
, then, clearly,
f\perp{\mathcal{H}}_{p,\lambda}
, as desired. To prove the converse, we begin by showing that any
f\in H^{2}_{d}
, such that every point
(\lambda,\mu_{j})
is a root of
f
of order
m_{j}
, for
1\leq j\leq r
. satisfies the following identity

(6.1)

f(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(z_{j}-\lambda_{j})g_{j}+\prod_{i=1}^{r}(z_{d}-\mu_{i})^{m_{i}}g_{d}

for some
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}\in H^{2}_{d}
. We prove this with the help of mathematical induction on
n=\deg p=\sum_{i=1}^{r}m_{i}
. To this end, we write our roots as
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}
, repeating them with appropriate multiplicity. Since
f(\lambda,\mu_{1})=0
, it follows from the solution to the Gleason problem in
H^{2}_{d}

[
7
, Section 3]
, that there exists
f_{1}^{1},\ldots,f_{d}^{1}\in H^{2}_{d}
such that

f(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}(z_{i}-\lambda_{i})f_{i}^{1}+(z_{d}-\mu_{1})f_{d}^{1}

verifying the desired identity for
n=1
. Now assume that the Equation (
6.1
) holds for
k<n
observe that we have following two cases – either
\mu_{k+1}=\mu_{k}
or
\mu_{k+1}\neq\mu_{k}
.

Case I:

Let
\mu_{k+1}\neq\mu_{k}
. Since
f(\lambda,\mu_{k+1})=0
and
\mu_{i}\neq\mu_{k+1}
for
1\leq i\leq k
, it follows from induction hypothesis that
g_{d}(\lambda,\mu_{k+1})=0
. Consequently, Gleason property of
H^{2}_{d}
yields that there exist
g_{j}^{1},\ldots,g_{d}^{1}
such that

g_{d}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(z_{j}-\lambda_{j})g_{j}^{1}+(z_{d}-\mu_{k+1})g_{d}^{1}.

Now substituting
g_{d}
in the Equation (
6.1
) with
k
in place of
n
, we have that

f(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(z_{j}-\lambda_{j})\left(g_{j}+\prod_{i=1}^{k}(z_{d}-\mu_{i})g_{j}^{1}\right)+\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}(z_{d}-\mu_{i})g^{1}_{d}

verifying the induction step.

Case II:

Let
\mu_{k+1}=\mu_{k}
and
t
be the cardinality of the set
\{i:\mu_{k+1}=\mu_{i},i=1,\ldots,k\}
. Note that
\frac{\partial^{t}f}{\partial z_{d}^{t}}(\lambda,\mu_{k+1})=0
. Consequently, differentiating the equation

f(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(z_{j}-\lambda_{j})g_{j}+\prod_{i=1}^{k}(z_{d}-\mu_{i})g_{d}

with respect to
z_{d}
of order
t
and evaluating at
(\lambda,\mu_{k+1})
we have that
g_{d}(\lambda,\mu_{k+1})=0
. Then, the induction step, in this case, follows from a similar argument as in Case I.

Now observe that

\displaystyle f(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(z_{j}-\lambda_{j})g_{j}+\prod_{i=1}^{n}(z_{d}-\mu_{i})g_{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(z_{j}-\lambda_{j})g_{j}+\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}(z_{d}-\mu_{i})-p\right)g_{d}+pg_{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(z_{j}-\lambda_{j})\widetilde{g}_{j}+pg_{d}.

Where the last equality holds since
p
is a monic polynomial of degree
n
and
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r}
are the roots of
p(\lambda,\cdot)
. Thus,
\prod_{i=1}^{n}(z_{d}-\mu_{i})-p
belongs to the ideal in the polynomial ring generated by
z_{1}-\lambda_{1},\ldots,z_{d-1}-\lambda_{d-1}
. Furthermore, since
{\mathcal{H}}_{p}
is multiplier coinvariant, we have that
P_{\mathcal{H}_{p}}M_{z_{j}}P_{\mathcal{H}_{p}}=P_{\mathcal{H}_{p}}M_{z_{j}}
, for all
1\leq j\leq d
. Therefore,

f=P_{\mathcal{H}_{p}}(f)=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(S_{j}-\lambda_{j})P_{\mathcal{H}_{p}}(g_{j})

completing the proof.
∎

Theorem 6.6
.

The tuple
S^{\prime*}
is in the Cowen-Douglas class.

Proof.

The result follows from the Lemma above and
[
18
, Theorem 2, pp 756]
.
∎

(6.2)

\mathcal{K}_{\bigwedge^{2}E_{S_{2}}}(0)=\partial\overline{\partial}\log(h(z))|_{z=0}=3+|\lambda+\mu|^{2}.

In

Lemma 6.8

Suppose that the adjoint of the multiplication operators

on a reproducing kernel Hilbert space

for some open connected domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}

\Phi_{X}(z)K(z,w)~=~K(z,w)\Phi_{X}(w)^{*}

for some holomorphic function
\Phi_{X}:\Omega\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}
with
\Phi_{X}^{2}=\Phi_{X}
.

In our set-up for
p(z_{1},z_{2})=(z_{1}-\lambda z_{2})(z_{1}-\mu z_{2})

on

is in
\mathrm{B}_{2}(U)

[

that

can be modelled as the multiplication operator by coordinate functions on a reproducing kernel Hilbert space of holomorphic functions on

can be defined as

(6.3)

K(z,w):=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{(1-az\overline{w})}&\frac{\lambda(\overline{\lambda-\mu})\overline{w}}{(1-az\overline{w})(1-cz\overline{w})}\\
\frac{\overline{\lambda}(\lambda-\mu)z}{(1-az\overline{w})(1-\overline{c}z\overline{w})}&\frac{|\lambda-\mu|^{2}(1-(1-|\lambda||\mu|)z\overline{w})(1-(1+|\lambda||\mu|)z\overline{w})}{(1-az\overline{w})(1-bz\overline{w})(1-cz)(1-\overline{c}\overline{w})}\end{pmatrix}

Thus,
S_{2}^{*}
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{p}
is irreducible if and only if there is no non-trivial projection
X_{0}
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
satisfying the following equation.

X_{0}K_{0}(z,0)^{-1}K_{0}(z,w)K_{0}(0,w)^{-1}~=~K_{0}(z,0)^{-1}K_{0}(z,w)K_{0}(0,w)^{-1}X_{0},~~~z,w\in U.

Here,
K_{0}(z,w)=K(0,0)^{-\frac{1}{2}}K(z,w)K(0,0)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
and
K
is the reproducing kernel described in the equation (
6.3
). Let
\hat{K}_{0}(z,w)
– called the normalized kernel – denote the kernel
K_{0}(z,0)^{-1}K_{0}(z,w)K_{0}(0,w)^{-1}
on
U
.

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
\lambda,\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
p
be the polynomial
p(z_{1},z_{2})=(z_{1}-\lambda z_{2})(z_{1}-\mu z_{2})
. Then the operator
S_{2}^{*}
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{p}
is irreducible.

Proof.

Since
\hat{K}_{0}
is equivalent to the reproducing kernel
K
defined in the equation (
6.3
), it is enough to show that the multiplication operator on the reproducing kernel Hilbert space corresponding to the kernel
\hat{K}_{0}
by the coordinate function is irreducible. In view of Lemma
6.8
above, it amounts to show that
I_{2\times 2}
is the only self-adjoint projection matrix commuting with
\hat{K}_{0}(z,w)
for all
z,w\in U
where
I_{2\times 2}
is the identity matrix of size
2
. So let us begin with a
2\times 2
self-adjoint projection matrix
X
satisfying

X\hat{K}_{0}(z,w)=\hat{K}_{0}(z,w)X,~~~\text{for all}~~z,w\in U.

In particular,
X
commutes with the matrix
\partial\overline{\partial}\hat{K}_{0}(0,0)
which turns out to be a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries
1
and
2+|\lambda+\mu|^{2}
. Consequently,
X
has to be a diagonal matrix. Moreover, since the
12
-th entry of
\hat{K}_{0}
turns out to be

\hat{K}_{0}(z,w)_{12}=\frac{\lambda|\lambda-\mu|(1+\lambda\overline{\mu})z\overline{w}^{2}}{(\lambda-\mu)(1-(1+|\lambda|^{2})z\overline{w})(1-(1+\lambda\overline{\mu})z\overline{w})},

there exists
z_{0}\in U
such that the
12
-th entry of
\hat{K}_{0}(z_{0},z_{0})
is not zero. However, this implies that the diagonal entries of
X
have to be equal as
X
also commutes with
\hat{K}_{0}(z_{0},z_{0})
. Finally, since
X
is a projection,
X
has to be
I_{2\times 2}
.
∎

7.
Stratification of
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}

Let
G=\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}}_{d})
. Recall that
G
acts on
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}
by conjugation. Moreover, the bundle
{\mathcal{E}}_{d,n}
is equivariant with respect to the natural action on the total space of the bundle. We will identify
\mathbb{P}_{d,n}^{0}
with
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
and consider
{\mathcal{E}}_{d,n}
as bundle on
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
. There is a natural stratification on
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
given by

{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}(m)=\left\{[X]\in{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}\mid\mathrm{aff.span}(X)\leq m\right\},\text{ for }1\leq m\leq\min\{d,n-1\}.

For every
[X]\in{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
and every representative
X=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
, we can consider the matrix with columns
C_{X}=\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1}-\lambda_{n}
. We note that
[X]\in{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}(m)
if and only if the minors of size
m+1
of
C_{X}
all vanish. Therefore,
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}={\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}(\min\{d,n-1\})
and for
m<\min\{d,n-1\}
, the sets
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}(m)
are closed. By
[
58
, Proposition 2.4.1]
, the automorphisms of
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
preserve affine subsets of
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
. Hence, the sets
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}(m)
are
G
-invariant. In particular, if we identify
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}
with
{\mathbb{X}}_{n-1,n}^{0}/G
, we obtain a stratification of
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}
by the sets
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}(m)
, which are the images of
{\mathbb{X}}_{n-1,n}^{0}(m)
under the quotient map.

There is another way to describe
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}(m)
. Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a complete Pick space on the set
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. We may assume that the kernel
k
of
{\mathcal{H}}
is normalized at
x_{1}
. This implies that the function
g(x_{i},x_{j})=1-1/k(x_{i},x_{j})
is a positive kernel. We will call the dimension of the corresponding reproducing kernel Hilbert space the embedding dimension of
{\mathcal{H}}
and denote it by
\mathrm{emb.dim}({\mathcal{H}})
. This is due to the fact that a Kolmogorov decomposition of
g
gives an embedding of
X
into
{\mathbb{B}}_{\mathrm{emb.dim}({\mathcal{H}})}
. It is clear that the embedding dimension is an invariant of the complete Pick space. We get that

\mathrm{Pick}_{n}(m)=\left\{{\mathcal{H}}\in\mathrm{Pick}_{n}\mid\mathrm{emb.dim}({\mathcal{H}})\leq m\right\}.

Indeed, one inclusion is clear since if we have
[X]\in{\mathbb{X}}_{n-1,n}^{0}(m)
for
m<n-1
, we move
[X]
by an automorphism so that one of the points is the origin. We then note that
[X]
is contained in a subspace, and the intersection of this subspace with
{\mathbb{B}}_{n-1}
is a unit ball of dimension at most
m
. Hence, the image of
[X]
in
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}
lies in
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}(m)
. Conversely, for
{\mathcal{H}}
as above with
\mathrm{emb.dim}({\mathcal{H}})\leq m
, we take the map
b\colon X\to{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
, for
d\leq m
and compose it with the embedding of
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
in
{\mathbb{B}}_{n-1}
as the intersection with a coordinate plane. This shows that equivalence class of
{\mathcal{H}}
in
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}
lies in the image of
{\mathbb{X}}_{n-1,n}^{0}(m)
.

Also, the argument above shows that for points
[X]\in{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}(m)
, we have a non-trivial stabilizer. In fact, the stabilizer is a compact infinite subgroup of
G
. First, consider the open dense submanifold
{\mathbb{X}}_{n-1,n}^{0}\setminus{\mathbb{X}}_{n-2,n-1}^{0}
of
{\mathbb{X}}_{n-1,n}^{0}
. Observe that there are points in this submanifold with a non-trivial stabilizer. To see this, fix
0<r<1
and consider the point
[X]=[0]+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}r[e_{j}]
. Clearly, the standard representation of
S_{n-1}
stabilizes this point. However, if
[X]
is such that the pseudohyperbolic distances between every two points in
[X]
are different, then since
G
acts by isometries on
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}^{n,0}
, the stabilizer of
[X]
is trivial. It is immediate from the definition of the topology on
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}
that the set of all such points is open and dense in
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
. Moreover, all those points are regular points in the sense of
[
30
, Definition 2.8.3]
or
[
43
, Definition on page 12]
(see also
[
43
, Theorem 2]
). Let us denote by
{\mathbb{X}}_{n-1,n}^{0,reg}
the collection of all regular points and by
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}^{reg}
its image in
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}
. By
[
43
, Theorem 4]
,
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}^{reg}
is connected and every point in
{\mathbb{X}}_{n-1,n}^{0,reg}
has trivial stabilizer. We summarize this discussion and its consequences in the following theorem.

Theorem 7.1
.

The space
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}^{reg}
has a structure of a smooth manifold and
{\mathbb{X}}_{n-1,n}^{0,reg}
is the total space of a principal
G
-bundle over
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}^{reg}
. Moreover, the vector bundle
{\mathcal{E}}_{n-1,n}|_{{\mathbb{X}}_{n-1,n}^{0,reg}}
gives rise to a vector bundle
{\mathcal{E}}_{n}^{reg}
on
\mathrm{Pick}_{n}^{reg}
.

Proof.

The first part follows from
[
62
, Proposition 5.2]
. The second follows from
[
62
, Proposition 9.4]

∎

We note that the stratification above is part of the orbit type stratification on
{\mathbb{X}}_{d,n}^{0}
.
