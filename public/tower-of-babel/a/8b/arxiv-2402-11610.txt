Communication Cost in Simulating Unknown Entangled States
Kelvin Onggadinata, Paweł Kurzyński, Dagomir Kaszlikowski

Abstract
We demonstrate how to universally simulate ensemble statistics of projective local measurements on any n n -qubit state shared among n n observers with classical communication and shared randomness. Our technique originates from protocols designed to simulate quantum non-locality [in Horizons of the Mind , Springer, Cham (2014)] and classical simulation of quantum circuits [Phys. Rev. Lett. 115, 070501 (2015)]. The protocol preserves three crucial aspects of the simulated quantum scenario in contrast to other approaches: no involvement of additional parties, none of the observers knows the global state of the system, and local measurement settings remain undisclosed.



I
Introduction

Classical simulation of local measurements on entangled quantum states is a fundamental problem that is particularly interesting in quantum information theory, since it relates quantum resources to classical ones. It was shown that such measurements can be classically simulated using a finite amount of communication if the prior information shared by the parties is infinite
Massar et al. 2001
. Toner and Bacon demonstrated that if Alice and Bob share two real-valued random variables
\hat{\lambda}_{1},\hat{\lambda}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, represented as unit vectors, they can simulate arbitrary local projective measurements on maximally entangled two-qubit state with a single bit of communication
Toner and Bacon 2003
. This result was later generalised to POVM
Degorre et al. 2005
. A single bit of communication is also enough to simulate local projective measurements on weakly entangled two-qubit states
Renner and Quintino 2023
and there is a strong numerical evidence that one bit of communication is also enough to simulate measurements on partially entangled pure two-qubit states
Sidajaya et al. 2023
. However, simulation of general measurements on an arbitrary two-qubit state requires two bits of communication
Renner et al. 2023
. Simulations of local measurements on two higher-dimensional systems
Brassard et al. 1999
;
Degorre et al. 2007
;
Vértesi and Bene 2009
and multi-qubit states
Branciard and Gisin 2011
;
Branciard et al. 2012
;
Brassard et al. 2016
;
Brassard et al. 2019
were also considered.

In our paper we handle this problem in a different way, focusing on classical simulation of quantum theory in phase space where quasi-probability distributions are a necessity. As a main tool we use the protocol to simulate quasi-probabilities with positive probability distributions first proposed by Abramsky and Brandenburger
Abramsky and Brandenburger 2014
and later by Pashayan
et al.

Pashayan et al. 2015
(these two protocols are essentially the same). For more details see also our complementary work
Onggadinata et al. 2024
. While our protocol requires an average communication cost that scales exponentially with the number of qubits, it preserves crucial features of the simulated quantum scenario. First, in the quantum scenario
n
parties share an
n
-qubit state
\rho
produced by some source. Without a prior knowledge of the source, the parties know nothing about the state of the system. In addition, each party locally chooses a measurement setting and does not reveal it to the others. Finally, no one else is involved in the scenario. We speculate that the exponential cost of communication is the price one has to pay to retain these features.

The structure of the paper is as follows. We first introduce a quasi-probabilistic representation of
n
-qubit states. In this representation states correspond to classical probability distributions, but measurements correspond to quasi-stochastic processes. Next we show how these quasi-stochastic processes can be classically simulated with standard stochastic processes. We discuss this simulation in the setting in which observers are spatially separated and highlight the necessity of communication. This leads to our protocol for a universal classical simulation of local projective measurements on
n
-qubits.

II
Quasi-stochastic representation of qubits

We demonstrate how to map
n
-qubit states to positive probability distributions and local projective measurements as quasi-stochastic processes (we can also simulate POVMs but this is beyond the scope of this paper). Such representations have been known since Wigner’s original paper
Wigner 1932
and subsequently formalised via so-called
frames

Ferrie and Emerson 2008
;
Ferrie and Emerson 2009
. However, here we chose a specific implementation of frames, most suitable to our approach.

To begin, we observe that any positive probability distribution over
M
bits, i.e., bit strings
a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{M}
,
a_{i}=\pm 1
, can be always written as follows

\displaystyle p(a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{M})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{M}}(1+\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}\langle A_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
i<j\end{subarray}}^{M}a_{i}a_{j}\langle A_{i}A_{j}\rangle+\dots

(1)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{M}\langle A_{1}A_{2}\dots A_{M}\rangle),

where the modulus of each correlation function
\langle A_{i}\rangle,\langle A_{i}A_{j}\rangle,\dots,\langle A_{1}A_{2}\dots A_{M}\rangle
is not greater than one. Of course, this does not guarantee positivity, which requires rather complicated constraints on the correlation functions. We can now represent
n
-qubit quantum states using such
M
-bit probability distributions, where
M=2n
.

As a starting point, let us first represent a single qubit with a Bloch-vector
{\bf{s}}=(s_{x},s_{y},s_{z})
as

p(aa^{\prime})=\frac{1}{4}(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(s_{x}a+s_{y}a^{\prime}+s_{z}aa^{\prime})).

(2)

following the representation in
Onggadinata et al. 2023a
. To complete the picture we now need to represent projective measurements along some arbitrary direction
{\bf{m}}
on the Bloch sphere. Since the state is described by a positive probability distribution, the measurement is a quasi-stochastic process converting a bit string
aa^{\prime}
to a single bit
\alpha=\pm 1
:

\eta_{{\bf{m}}}(\alpha|aa^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{3}\alpha(m_{x}a+m_{y}a^{\prime}+m_{z}aa^{\prime})).

(3)

It is indeed quasi-stochastic because some transition probabilities are negative. These measurement probabilities follow the Born rule:

q(\alpha|{\bf{m}})=\sum_{a,a^{\prime}}\eta_{{\bf{m}}}(\alpha|aa^{\prime})p(aa^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}(1+\alpha{\bf{s}}\cdot{\bf{m}}).

(4)

It is then evident that this formalism, i.e., a positive probability distribution
p(aa^{\prime})
and the quasi-stochastic process
\eta_{{\bf{m}}}(\alpha|aa^{\prime})
are equivalent to quantum mechanics of a single qubit in the Heisenberg picture.

We can simplify the above formulas by introducing a set of vectors
{\bf{n}}_{aa^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(a,a^{\prime},aa^{\prime})
. It is straightforward to check that they form a three-dimensional tetrahedron and

\displaystyle p(aa^{\prime})=\frac{1}{4}(1+{\bf{s}}\cdot{\bf{n}}_{aa^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\eta_{{\bf{m}}}(\alpha|aa^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}(1+3\alpha{\bf{m}}\cdot{\bf{n}}_{aa^{\prime}}).

(5)

Before we generalize this formalism to
n
-qubits let us consider another special case: two qubits. A two-qubit state
\rho
is described by two local Bloch vectors,
{\bf{s}}
and
{\bf{r}}
, and a
3\times 3
correlation matrix
{\bf{T}}

\rho=\frac{1}{4}(1+{\bf{s}}\cdot{\bm{\sigma}}\otimes I+I\otimes{\bf{r}}\cdot{\bm{\sigma}}+\sum_{k,l}T_{kl}\sigma_{k}\otimes\sigma_{l}),

(6)

where
{\bm{\sigma}}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})
and
\sigma_{i}
(
i=x,y,z
) are Pauli matrices. This state is mapped to a four-bit positive probability distribution

\displaystyle p(aa^{\prime},bb^{\prime})=\frac{1}{16}(1+{\bf{s}}\cdot{\bf{n}}_{aa^{\prime}}+{\bf{r}}\cdot{\bf{n}}_{bb^{\prime}}+{\bf{n}}_{aa^{\prime}}\cdot{\bf{T}}{\bf{n}}_{bb^{\prime}}).

(7)

Local measurements are now represented by the same quasi-stochastic processes as in the single qubit case, i.e.,

q(\alpha,\beta|{\bf{m}}_{A},{\bf{m}}_{B})=\sum_{a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime}}\eta_{{\bf{m}}_{A}}(\alpha|aa^{\prime})\eta_{{\bf{m}}_{B}}(\beta|bb^{\prime})p(aa^{\prime},bb^{\prime}),

(8)

where the probability distribution
q
is what quantum theory predicts.
If one wishes to perform a global two-qubit measurement, they have to use an appropriate quasi-stochastic process

\eta(\alpha\beta|aa^{\prime},bb^{\prime})=c_{aa^{\prime}bb^{\prime}\alpha\beta}+{\bf{s}}_{\alpha\beta}\cdot{\bf{n}}_{aa^{\prime}}\otimes{\bf{n}}_{bb^{\prime}},

(9)

where
\sum_{\alpha\beta}c_{aa^{\prime}bb^{\prime}\alpha\beta}=1
and
{\bf{s}}_{\alpha\beta}
is a nine-dimensional real vector. This, however, is beyond the scope of this paper (and it can be easily found).

Continuing the trend, an arbitrary
n
-qubit probability distribution reads

\displaystyle p(a_{1}a^{\prime}_{1},a_{2}a^{\prime}_{2},\dots,a_{n}a^{\prime}_{n})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{n}}\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\chi_{k}(a_{1}a^{\prime}_{1},a_{2}a^{\prime}_{2},\dots,a_{n}a^{\prime}_{n})\right),

(10)

where

\displaystyle\chi_{k}(a_{1}a^{\prime}_{1},a_{2}a^{\prime}_{2},\dots,a_{n}a^{\prime}_{n})=

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{\kappa_{k}\in K_{k}}\left(\sum_{j_{1}\ldots j_{k}=x,y,z}T^{(\kappa_{k})}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}({\mathbf{n}}_{a_{i}a^{\prime}_{i}})_{j_{i}}\right),

(11)

and
K_{k}
is the family of all
k
-qubit subsets
\kappa_{k}
. The element
T^{(\kappa_{k})}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{k}}
is the element of the correlation tensor for the
k
-qubit subset
\kappa_{k}
. In particular, for single-qubit subsets
\kappa_{1}
, the element
T^{(\kappa_{1})}_{j_{1}}
is the Bloch’s vector coordinate. Finally, note that the number of subsets in
K_{k}
is
\binom{n}{k}
. The non-negativity is guaranteed by the non-negativity of a represented quantum state
\rho
via the formula

\displaystyle p(a_{1}a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}a_{2}^{\prime},\dots,a_{n}a_{n}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=\text{Tr}\left[\rho\Pi_{a_{1}a_{1}^{\prime}}\otimes\Pi_{a_{2}a_{2}^{\prime}}\otimes\dots\otimes\Pi_{a_{n}a_{n}^{\prime}}\right],

(12)

where
\Pi_{a_{i}a_{i}^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{4}(1+{\bf{n}}_{a_{i}a_{i}^{\prime}}\cdot\bm{\sigma})
is an element of the symmetric, informationally complete (SIC)-POVM
Renes et al. 2004
. Finally, projective measurements on such
n
-qubit distributions are as discussed before.

For instance, the probability distribution for the three-qubit GHZ state
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|000\rangle+|111\rangle)
reads

\displaystyle p_{GHZ}(aa^{\prime},bb^{\prime},cc^{\prime})=\frac{1}{64}\times

\displaystyle\Big[1+({\bf n}_{aa^{\prime}})_{z}({\bf n}_{bb^{\prime}})_{z}+({\bf n}_{aa^{\prime}})_{z}({\bf n}_{cc^{\prime}})_{z}+({\bf n}_{bb^{\prime}})_{z}({\bf n}_{cc^{\prime}})_{z}+

\displaystyle({\bf n}_{aa^{\prime}})_{x}({\bf n}_{bb^{\prime}})_{x}({\bf n}_{cc^{\prime}})_{x}-({\bf n}_{aa^{\prime}})_{x}({\bf n}_{bb^{\prime}})_{y}({\bf n}_{cc^{\prime}})_{y}-

\displaystyle({\bf n}_{aa^{\prime}})_{y}({\bf n}_{bb^{\prime}})_{x}({\bf n}_{cc^{\prime}})_{y}-({\bf n}_{aa^{\prime}})_{y}({\bf n}_{bb^{\prime}})_{y}({\bf n}_{cc^{\prime}})_{x}\Big],

(13)

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle p_{GHZ}(aa^{\prime},bb^{\prime},cc^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{16}\Big[1+\frac{1}{3}(aa^{\prime}bb^{\prime}+aa^{\prime}cc^{\prime}+bb^{\prime}cc^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{3\sqrt{3}}(abc-ab^{\prime}c^{\prime}-a^{\prime}bc^{\prime}-a^{\prime}b^{\prime}c)\Big].

(14)

The presented picture offers a consistent representation of quantum theory where quantum states are equivalent to non-negative probability distributions and measurements are quasi-stochastic processes. Thus, quantum behaviour in our picture, is solely the property of measurements because we do not have quasi-probabilities in classical theory.

III
Classical simulation of quasi-stochastic processes

Any quasi-stochastic process is described by a quasi-stochastic matrix, i.e., a matrix with entries that can be negative, but its columns sum up to one. In our case a quasi-stochastic process
\eta_{{\bf{m}}}(\alpha|aa^{\prime})
, describing a quantum measurement, is a
2\times 4
matrix. Such a matrix can always be written as a linear combination of two non-negative stochastic matrices
S^{\pm}
weighted by a negative binary distribution (so-called
nebit
, see
Kaszlikowski and Kurzyński 2021
):

S=(1+\delta)S^{+}-\delta S^{-}

(15)

with
\delta\geq 0
. We showed in
Onggadinata et al. 2023b
how to find a nebit decomposition of any quasi-stochastic matrix using the following components:

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\{0,-2\min_{k,l}S_{kl}\}

(16)

\displaystyle S^{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}

(17)

\displaystyle S^{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\delta}(S+\delta S^{-})

(18)

where
\mathbf{1}
is a
2\times 4
matrix with all entries equal to
1
. Note that
1\geq\delta\geq(\sqrt{3}-1)/2\approx 0.366
, where the lower bound corresponds to measurements along direction
{\bf{m}}={\bf{x}},{\bf{y}},{\bf{z}}
and the upper bound corresponds to measurements along tetrahedral directions
{\bf{m}}={\bf{n}}_{aa^{\prime}}
.

We now show how to simulate an action of a quasi-stochastic process
S
, representing a projective measurement given by
\eta_{{\bf{m}}}(\alpha|aa^{\prime})
, on a non-negative single-qubit distribution
p(aa^{\prime})
. This is a classical simulation that uses a standard probability calculus with non-negative probabilities only. It was first discovered, although in a different but related scenario, by Abramsky and Brandenburger
Abramsky and Brandenburger 2014
and later by Pashayan
et. al.

Pashayan et al. 2015
. Additional details can be found in our complementary work
Onggadinata et al. 2024
.

To simulate
S
, we use a binary random bit
r=\pm 1
, distributed with probability
\{t,1-t\}

t=\frac{1+\delta}{1+2\delta}\,.

(19)

Note that
t>1-t
since
1+\delta>\delta
. We then perform a controlled operation conditional on the outcome: apply
S^{+}
if
r=+1
, else apply
S^{-}
. We get

\displaystyle q^{\pm}(\alpha)=\sum_{aa^{\prime}}S^{\pm}(\alpha|aa^{\prime})p(aa^{\prime})

(20)

Therefore, in a single round a string
aa^{\prime}
transforms into a single bit
\alpha
. However, we keep the track of
r
, hence we have a string
r\alpha
.

N
rounds we hold a table of such pairs. The next step is to modify it in the following way. For each event
\{r=-1,\alpha\}\equiv\{-1,\alpha\}
we find an event

and remove both of them from the table. The schematic representation of the removal procedure is shown below.

(24)

hence

(25)

V
Conclusions

Using techniques developed in
Abramsky and Brandenburger 2014
and
Pashayan et al. 2015
we showed how to simulate ensemble statistics of
n
local projective measurements on any unknown
n
-qubit state. By unknown we mean that the global state is never revealed to the observers. They only have access to information about their local parts. Moreover, in our protocol local measurement settings stay private. This sets our protocol apart from previous ones in the literature on the topic. If the protocol cannot be further improved, it may explain why our communication scales exponentially with the number of observers. Whether this scaling is a consequence of not knowing the state remains an open question at this moment.

Although we presented our simulation at the ensemble level, it also works as a
single-shot
simulation, where a finite number of source samples is used to generate a single measurement outcome. This type of simulation yields noisy measurements, the extent of which diminishes with an increasing number of source samples. We have opted not to present the single-shot version to maintain the clarity of the paper.

A straightforward modification of the protocol leads to simulations of any
n
-partite
d
-dimensional quantum systems, which is fairly obvious as such systems can be simulated with quantum computer operating on many qubits.
