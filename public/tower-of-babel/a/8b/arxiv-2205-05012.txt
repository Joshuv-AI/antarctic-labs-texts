Abstract
. We introduce the notion of a pseudomultiplier of a Hilbert space ℋ \mathcal{H} of functions on a set Ω \Omega . Roughly, a pseudomultiplier of ℋ \mathcal{H} is a function which multiplies a finite-codimensional subspace of ℋ \mathcal{H} into ℋ \mathcal{H} , where we allow the possibility that a pseudomultiplier is not defined on all of Ω \Omega . A pseudomultiplier of ℋ \mathcal{H} has singularities , which comprise a subspace of ℋ \mathcal{H} , and generalize the concept of singularities of an analytic function, even though the elements of ℋ \mathcal{H} need not enjoy any sort of analyticity. We analyse the natures of these singularities, and obtain a broad classification of them in function-theoretic terms.

1.
Introduction

Many different mathematical structures have been brought
to bear on the study of functions. In the last half-century
notable theories which have yielded insights into
function-theoretic problems include those of locally convex
spaces, ordered vector spaces, commutative Banach
algebras and the geometry of Banach spaces. Perhaps the
richest structure of all is that of Hilbert spaces; when a
function-theoretic problem can be formulated in terms of
Hilbert space geometry one can have high hopes of making
progress on it
[
3
]
. Accordingly, there has been a substantial
development of the theory of Hilbert function spaces (also
called reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces and functional
Hilbert spaces)
[
8
]
,
[
19
]
. In this paper we approach singularities of functions from
a Hilbert space perspective.

By a
Hilbert function space on a set
\Omega
we mean a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
whose elements are
complex-valued functions on
\Omega
such that, for each
\lambda\in\Omega
, the linear functional
f\mapsto f(\lambda)
is continuous on
\mathcal{H}
. It follows that, for each
\lambda\in\Omega
there exists an element of
\mathcal{H}
, which we shall denote by
k_{\lambda}
, such that
f(\lambda)=\left\langle f,k_{\lambda}\right\rangle
for every
f\in\mathcal{H}
. We call
k_{\lambda}
the
kernel
corresponding to
\lambda
, and we call the function
k:\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
given by

k(\mu,\lambda)=k_{\lambda}(\mu)\quad\mbox{ for all }\lambda,\mu\in\Omega

the
reproducing kernel
of
\mathcal{H}
.

Even when we study problems which are not framed in Hilbert space terms we may still be able to use Hilbert space methods. This seminal idea is
the basis of a substantial body of work stemming from
papers by Sarason
[
18
]
and Adamyan, Arov and Krein

[
1
]
which has had a strong influence not only in
analysis but also in control theory
[
13
]
. In these papers
bounded analytic functions are analysed and constructed in
terms of their action as multipliers on the Hardy space

H^{2}
.
See the beginning of Section 2 for a definition of
H^{2}
.

Both the elements of
H^{2}
and the multipliers of
H^{2}

are functions possessing a high degree of
regularity, and one might expect that the same
assertion will be true of most Hilbert function spaces one is
likely to encounter, and that therefore operator-theoretic
methods should be well adapted to problems involving
highly regular functions, but of less use for the study of
singularities of functions. However, the cited paper of
Adamyan, Arov and Krein and its forerunner by Akhiezer

[
6
]
shows that this is not entirely true. One can
construct meromorphic functions in the disc with a
prescribed number of poles by the spectral analysis of
operators on
H^{2}
. In this approach a function with
singularities determines a multiplier not on the whole of

H^{2}
but on a closed subspace of finite codimension; such an object is called a
pseudomultiplier
of the Hilbert space in question. The
power of operator theory in analysing functions with
singularities is thus far from vacuous, and we are led to
wonder about its potential and its limitations.

In
[
4
]
the domain of definition
of a pseudomultiplier, its
associated “Pick kernel”, and the particular case of Hilbert
spaces of analytic functions of one variable were studied.
The paper
[
5
]
is
devoted to a key result of Adamyan, Arov and Krein and
the extent to which it can have analogues for other function
spaces. Pseudomultipliers also
appear naturally in connection with nearly invariant subspaces for backward shift operators on vector-valued Hardy spaces (see
[
9
]
).

In this paper we will develop the theory of pseudomultipliers and local subspaces.
The main notions and results are presented in the overview of the paper and illustrated by examples.

We are very grateful to a helpful referee who made numerous discerning comments and suggestions.

2.
Overview of the paper

Before giving a formal definition of a pseudomultiplier, let us consider some archetypal examples.
The Hardy space
H^{2}
is the space of analytic functions
f
on the open unit disc
\mathbb{D}
such that

\sup_{0<r<1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(re^{i\theta})|^{2}\ d\theta<\infty.

When endowed with pointwise addition and scalar multiplication and the inner product

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle=\lim_{r\to 1-}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(re^{i\theta})\overline{g(re^{i\theta})}\ d\theta

H^{2}
is a Hilbert function space, whose reproducing kernel is the
Szegő kernel
k
, defined by

k(\lambda,\mu)=\frac{1}{1-\bar{\mu}\lambda}\quad\mbox{ for all }\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{D}.

An elementary introduction to
H^{2}
can be found in
[
20
, Chapter 13]
. More thorough and far-reaching treatments are in
[
14
,
16
]
.

2.1. Pseudomultipliers

Definition 2.1
.

A multiplier
of a Hilbert function space
\mathcal{H}
on a set
\Omega
is defined to be a function
\varphi:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
such that
\varphi f\in\mathcal{H}
for every
f\in\mathcal{H}
. Here
\varphi f
denotes the pointwise product of
\varphi
and
f
.

One can show directly from the definitions that every function in the space
H^{\infty}
of bounded analytic functions on
\mathbb{D}
is a multiplier of
H^{2}
.
On the other hand, if
\varphi
is a multiplier of
H^{2}
, then since the constant function
\mathbf{1}\in H^{2}
, we have
\varphi=\varphi\mathbf{1}\in H^{2}
, and so
\varphi
is analytic on
\mathbb{D}
. Moreover, the operator
X_{\varphi}
on
H^{2}
defined, for all
f\in H^{2}
, by

(X_{\varphi}f)(\lambda)=\varphi(\lambda)f(\lambda),\quad\mbox{ for all }\lambda\in\Omega

(2.1)

is linear and is easily seen to have a closed graph, and hence
X_{\varphi}
is a bounded linear operator on
H^{2}
. The calculation

\displaystyle\left\langle X_{\varphi}^{*}k_{\lambda},f\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle k_{\lambda},X_{\varphi}f\right\rangle=\left\langle k_{\lambda},\varphi f\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{(\varphi f)(\lambda)}=\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}\left\langle k_{\lambda},f\right\rangle\mbox{ for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{D}\;\text{ and all }\;f\in H^{2}

shows that
X_{\varphi}^{*}k_{\lambda}=\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}k_{\lambda}
, so that
\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}
is an eigenvalue of
X_{\varphi}^{*}
, and therefore
|\varphi(\lambda)|\leq\|X_{\varphi}\|
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, which is to say that
\varphi
is bounded on
\mathbb{D}
. Thus the multipliers of
H^{2}
are precisely the elements of
H^{\infty}
.

Example 2.2
.

A pseudomultiplier of
H^{2}
.
Consider the function
\varphi(z)=1/z
, defined on the set
\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
. This function is clearly not a multiplier of
H^{2}
, since it is unbounded on
\mathbb{D}
, nor even defined on the whole of
\mathbb{D}
. On the other hand it is close to being a multiplier in the sense that there is a finite-codimensional closed subspace of
H^{2}
, namely
zH^{2}
, which is a closed subspace of codimension
1
in
H^{2}
with the property that, for every
f\in zH^{2}
,
\varphi f
is the restriction to
\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
of a function in
H^{2}
.

Example 2.3
.

Another pseudomultiplier of
H^{2}
.
Choose distinct complex numbers
a,b
and define

\psi(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}a&\mbox{ if }z\neq 0\\
&\mbox{ }\\
b&\mbox{ if }z=0.\end{array}\right.

For any
f\in H^{2}
, it is easy to see that
\psi f\in H^{2}
if and only if
f(0)=0
, that is, if and only if
f
is in the closed
1
-codimensional subspace
zH^{2}
of
H^{2}
. Hence
\psi
is a pseudomultiplier of
H^{2}
.

In the light of these two examples we introduce the following notion.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert function space on a set

\Omega
. We say that a function

\varphi:D_{\varphi}\subset\Omega\to\mathbb{C}

is a
pseudomultiplier
of
\mathcal{H}
if

(1)

D_{\varphi}
is a set of uniqueness for
\mathcal{H}
;

(2)

the subspace
E_{\varphi}
of
\mathcal{H}
, defined to be

\left\{h\in\mathcal{H}:{\rm there\ exists}\ g\in\mathcal{H}\ {\rm such\ that}\ g(\lambda)=\varphi(\lambda)h(\lambda)\ {\rm for\ all}\ \lambda\in D_{\varphi}\right\}

is closed in
\mathcal{H}
, and

(3)

E_{\varphi}
has finite codimension in
\mathcal{H}
.

If
\mathcal{H}
is a Hilbert function space on a set
\Omega
then we say that a subset
D
of
\Omega
is a
set of uniqueness for
\mathcal{H}
if, for any functions
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
, if
f(\lambda)=g(\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in D
then
f=g
.

When conditions (1) to (3) hold we define the operator

X_{\varphi}:E_{\varphi}\to\mathcal{H}
by
X_{\varphi}h=g
where

g
is the element of
\mathcal{H}
(necessarily unique, by condition (1))
such that
g(\lambda)=\varphi(\lambda)h(\lambda)
for all

\lambda\in D_{\varphi}
.
The space
E_{\varphi}
will be called the
regular space of
\varphi
,
while the space
E_{\varphi}^{\perp}
will be called the
singular space
of
\varphi
and will be denoted by
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}
.

For the pseudomultiplier
\varphi(z)=1/z
on
H^{2}
of Example
2.2
,
D_{\varphi}=\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
and
E_{\varphi}
is the closed subspace
zH^{2}
of
H^{2}
, which has codimension
1
in
H^{2}
. Thus
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}=H^{2}\ominus zH^{2}
, the subspace of constant functions in
H^{2}
. The operator
X_{\varphi}:zH^{2}\to H^{2}
is given by

X_{\varphi}zf=f
for all
f\in H^{2}
.
For the pseudomultiplier
\psi
of
H^{2}
of Example
2.3
,
D_{\psi}=\mathbb{D}
,
E_{\psi}=zH^{2}
,
\mathcal{S}_{\psi}
is the subspace of constant functions in
H^{2}
and
X_{\psi}
is the scalar operator
X_{\psi}f=af
on
zH^{2}
.

The Hilbert function spaces on which pseudomultipliers act need not be spaces of analytic functions.

Example 2.5
.

A pseudomultiplier on a Sobolev space.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the space
W^{1,2}[0,1]
of absolutely continuous
functions on
[0,1]
whose weak derivatives are in
L^{2}
and
with inner product given by

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle=\int^{1}_{0}f(t)\overline{g(t)}dt+\int^{1}_{0}f^{\prime}(t)\overline{g^{\prime}(t)}dt.

It is well known that
W^{1,2}[0,1]
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, with reproducing kernel
k
given by

\displaystyle k(\lambda,\mu)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm cosech}1\cosh(1-\mu)\cosh\lambda&\mbox{if $0\leq\lambda\leq\mu\leq 1,$}\\
{\rm cosech}1\cosh(1-\lambda)\cosh\mu&\mbox{if $0\leq\mu\leq\lambda\leq 1$.}\end{array}\right.

See, for example,
[
20
, Problem 6.10]
, where verification of the reproducing formula is left as an exercise, or
[
2
, Section 3]
for a full calculation.

Let us check that
\chi(t)=\sqrt{t}
is a pseudomultiplier of

\mathcal{H}
with
E_{\chi}=k^{\perp}_{0}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\chi}=\mathbb{C}k_{0}
.
Consider a function
f\in k_{0}^{\perp}
, so that
f\in W^{1,2}[0,1]
and
f(0)=0
.
Let us prove that the function
\chi f
is absolutely continuous and
(\chi f)^{\prime}
is in
L^{2}(0,1)
.
Note that

(\chi f)^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}f(t)+\sqrt{t}f^{\prime}(t).

To prove that
(\chi f)^{\prime}
is in
L^{2}(0,1)
, it is enough to show that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}f(t)
is in

L^{2}(0,1)
. Since
f(0)=0
, for
x\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle x^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}f(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{x}f^{\prime}(t)\,dt

(2.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{x}\left(x^{-1}\int_{0}^{x}f^{\prime}(t)\,dt\right).

By assumption
f^{\prime}
is in
L^{2}(0,1)
, and so, by Hardy’s inequality,

\left(x^{-1}\int_{0}^{x}f^{\prime}(t)\,dt\right)
is in
L^{2}(0,1)
. Thus

\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}f(t)
is in

L^{2}(0,1)
. Hence
(\chi f)^{\prime}
is in
L^{2}(0,1)
.
Therefore
(\chi f)^{\prime}\in L^{1}(0,1)
, and so

(\chi f)(x)=\int_{0}^{x}(\chi f)^{\prime}(t)\ dt

is absolutely continuous on
[0,1]
.
Therefore, for
f\in k_{0}^{\perp}
,
\chi f\in W^{1,2}
.
Conversely, if
f(0)\neq 0
then
(\chi f)^{\prime}\notin L^{2}[0,1]
and so
\chi f\notin W^{1,2}[0,1]
. Thus,
\chi
is a pseudomultiplier of
W^{1,2}
,
E_{\chi}=k_{0}^{\perp}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\chi}=\mathbb{C}k_{0}
.
\square

Nor need it be true that the kernel vectors
\{k_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Omega\}
are linearly independent in
\mathcal{H}
.

Example 2.6
.

Linearly dependent kernels.
Let
a,b
be distinct points in
\mathbb{D}
and let
\mathcal{H}
comprise the functions
\{f\in H^{2}:f(a)=f(b)\}
, with the inner product inherited from
H^{2}
. Thus, if
k
is the reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{H}
, we have
k_{a}=k_{b}
. Consider an element
\upsilon\in H^{\infty}
such that
\upsilon(a)\neq\upsilon(b)
. It is clear that, for any
f\in\mathcal{H}
,
\upsilon f\in\mathcal{H}
if and only if
f(a)=f(b)=0
. Thus
E_{\upsilon}=k_{a}^{\perp}=k_{b}^{\perp}
, a closed subspace of
\mathcal{H}
of codimension
1
, and
\upsilon
is a pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}
.

2.2. The singular space

Informally speaking, the singular space
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}
is what stops the pseudomultiplier
\varphi
from being a multiplier.
Pseudomultipliers that are not multipliers always have
singularities
in the sense that
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}
is non-zero.
The reader will have noticed that in the three examples
2.2
,
2.3
and
2.5
the pseudomultipliers
\varphi,\psi
, and
\chi
all fail to be multipliers of
\mathcal{H}
, in each case on account of a lack of regularity at
0
;
\varphi
is unbounded in a neighborhood of
0
,
\psi
is discontinuous at
0
and

\chi
is not locally in
L^{2}
at
0
. These examples motivated us to call
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}
the
singular space
of
\varphi
.

In this paper we develop a structure theory for the singular spaces of pseudomultipliers.
The principal result is that
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}
can be orthogonally decomposed into the direct sum of
the
polar space
of
\varphi
, which we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
, and the set of
ambiguous vectors of
\varphi
, which we denote by
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
.
The definitions of
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
, which we shall give shortly, depend on the notion of
\varphi
seeing a vector
.

2.3 Seeing a vector

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert function space on
\Omega
and fix
v\in\mathcal{H}
.
We shall formalise the idea of enlarging the set
\Omega
by adjoining an extra point
p\notin\Omega

in such a way that the vector
v
is the reproducing kernel associated with
p
.
Let
\tilde{\Omega}=\Omega\cup\{p\}
, and for
f\in\mathcal{H}

define
f_{v}:\tilde{\Omega}\to\mathbb{C}
by the formula

f_{v}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f(\lambda)&{\rm if}\qquad\lambda\in\Omega\\
\\
\left\langle f,v\right\rangle&{\rm if}\ \lambda=p\ .\end{array}\right.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{v}=\{f_{v}:f\in\mathcal{H}\}
. Since
f=0
in

\mathcal{H}
implies that
f_{v}=0
in
\mathcal{H}_{v}
, we see that
the formula

\left\langle f_{v},g_{v}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{v}}\ =\ \left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

defines an inner product on
\mathcal{H}_{v}
. Furthermore, with
this inner product,
\mathcal{H}_{v}
is a Hilbert function space on

\tilde{\Omega}
, and the restriction map

\mathcal{H}_{v}\ni f_{v}\mapsto f_{v}|\Omega=f\in\mathcal{H}

is a Hilbert space isomorphism.

Now let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert function space, fix
v\in\mathcal{H}
, and let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}
.
Since
D_{\varphi}
is a set of uniqueness for
\mathcal{H}
,

D_{\varphi}
is also a set of uniqueness for
\mathcal{H}_{v}
and
thus
\varphi
is also a pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}_{v}
.
Its domain when so regarded is still just
D_{\varphi}
and its
regular space is unchanged. One can wonder, however, whether

\varphi
can be extended to a pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}_{v}
with domain containing
D_{\varphi}\cup\{p\}
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert function space on
\Omega
,
let
v\in\mathcal{H}
, let
c\in\mathbb{C}
, and let
\varphi
be a
pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}
. We say that
\varphi

sees
v

with value
c
if there exists a pseudomultiplier
\psi
of

\mathcal{H}_{v}
such that
\psi
extends
\varphi
(that is,

D_{\varphi}\subseteq D_{\psi}
and
\varphi=\psi|D_{\varphi}
),
p\in D_{\psi},E_{\varphi}=E_{\psi}|\Omega
, and
\psi(p)=c
. We say
\varphi

sees

v

if there exists
c\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\varphi
sees
v

with value
c
; in this case we also say that
v
is
visible

to
\varphi
.

Example 2.8
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert function space on
\Omega
and let
\varphi
be a
pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}
. For any
c\in\mathbb{C},\varphi
sees the zero vector in
\mathcal{H}
with value
c
. To prove this fact,
in Definition
2.7
choose
v=0
and
\psi
to be given by
\psi(\lambda)=\varphi(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in\Omega
and
\psi(p)=c
. One finds that
f_{v}\in E_{\psi}
if and only if
f\in E_{\varphi}
and that
\psi
is a pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}_{v}
. Thus
\varphi
sees
0
with value
c
.

Example 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert function space on
\Omega

and let

\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}
. If
\lambda\in D_{\varphi}
, then
\varphi
sees
k_{\lambda}
with value

\varphi(\lambda)
.

To prove this statement, let
\tilde{\Omega}=\Omega\cup\{p\}
, where
p\notin\Omega
, and let
D_{\psi}=D_{\varphi}\cup\{p\}
. Define a function

\psi:D_{\psi}\to\mathbb{C}
by
\psi(z)=\varphi(z)
if
z\in D_{\varphi}
and
\psi(p)=\varphi(\lambda)
. Let
v=k_{\lambda}
, so that
\mathcal{H}_{v}
is a Hilbert space of functions on
\tilde{\Omega}
. We claim that
\psi
is a pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}_{v}
and that
E_{\varphi}=E_{\psi}|\Omega
. Indeed, consider any function
f\in\mathcal{H}
. We have

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274ptf_{v}\in E_{\psi}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\psi f_{v}\in\mathcal{H}_{v}|_{D_{\psi}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\text{there exists }\;g\in\mathcal{H}\;\;\text{such that}\;(\psi f)_{v}=g_{v}|_{D_{\psi}}

Now, for any
g\in\mathcal{H}
,
(\psi f)_{v}=g_{v}|_{D_{\psi}}
if and only if
\varphi(z)f(z)=g(z)
for
z\in D_{\varphi}
and
\psi(p)f_{v}(p)=\left\langle g,v\right\rangle
, which is to say that
\varphi f=g
on
D_{\varphi}
and
\varphi(\lambda)\left\langle f,v\right\rangle=\left\langle g,v\right\rangle
.
Thus
f_{v}\in E_{\psi}
if and only if
\varphi f
extends to an element
g\in\mathcal{H}
and
\varphi(\lambda)f(\lambda)=g(\lambda)
.
Thus
f_{v}\in E_{\psi}
if and only if
f\in E_{\varphi}
. Hence, by Definition
2.7
,
\varphi
sees
k_{\lambda}
with value
\varphi(\lambda)
, as claimed.
\square

Example 2.10
.

What vectors does
1/z
see in
H^{2}
?
Consider the pseudomultiplier
\varphi
of Example
2.2
:
\varphi(z)=1/z
on
(\mathcal{H},\Omega)=(H^{2},\mathbb{D})
. Here,
D_{\varphi}=\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
and
E_{\varphi}=zH^{2}
.
We claim that the vectors in
\mathcal{H}=H^{2}
visible to
\varphi
are precisely the scalar multiples of the kernels
k_{\lambda}
, for
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}.

To prove this claim, we first note that, by Example
2.9
,
\varphi
does see
k_{\lambda}
with value
\varphi(\lambda)=1/\lambda
for every
\lambda\in D_{\varphi}
. Conversely, suppose
\varphi
sees a vector
v\neq 0
with value
c
. By definition, there exists a pseudomultiplier
\psi
of
\mathcal{H}_{v}
such that
\psi
extends
\varphi
,
p\in D_{\psi},E_{\varphi}=E_{\psi}|\mathbb{D}
, and
\psi(p)=c
. Consider any function
h\in H^{2}
. The function
f
defined by
f(z)=zh(z)
is in
E_{\varphi}=zH^{2}
. Hence there exists
\tilde{f}\in E_{\psi}
such that
\tilde{f}|\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}=f
. We have
\psi\tilde{f}\in\mathcal{H}_{v}
, and therefore

(\psi\tilde{f})(p)=\left\langle(\psi\tilde{f})|\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\},v\right\rangle=\left\langle\tfrac{1}{z}f,v\right\rangle=\left\langle\tfrac{1}{z}zh,v\right\rangle=\left\langle h,v\right\rangle.

Now
(\psi\tilde{f})(p)=\psi(p)\tilde{f}(p)=c\left\langle f,v\right\rangle=c\left\langle zh,v\right\rangle
.
Therefore, for every
h\in H^{2}
,
c\left\langle zh,v\right\rangle=\left\langle h,v\right\rangle
, and so, if
S
denotes the forward shift operator on
H^{2}
, given by
(Sh)(z)=zh(z)
for all
h\in H^{2}
, then

\left\langle h,v\right\rangle=\left\langle czh,v\right\rangle=\left\langle cSh,v\right\rangle=\left\langle h,\bar{c}S^{*}v\right\rangle.

Hence
\bar{c}S^{*}v=v
. Since, by assumption,
v\neq 0
,
v
is an eigenvector of
S^{*}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
1/\bar{c}
. It is a simple calculation to show (see for example
[
3
, Lemma 1.67]
) that the eigenvectors of
S^{*}
are the non-zero scalar multiples of the Szegő kernels
k_{\lambda}
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, that is,
S^{*}k_{\lambda}=\bar{\lambda}k_{\lambda}
. Hence
\bar{c}\bar{\lambda}k_{\lambda}=k_{\lambda}
, and so
c\lambda=1
, and we conclude that
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
, and that
v
is a scalar multiple of the kernel
k_{\lambda}
, for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
, as claimed.
\square

2.4. Ambiguous points and the ambiguous space

In order to define the ambiguous space
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
of a pseudomultiplier
\varphi
we first consider the points in
\Omega
at which the value of
\varphi
is not determined by the operator
X_{\varphi}
. We call such points
ambiguous points
of
\varphi
.
For
c\in\mathbb{C}
and
\alpha\in D_{\varphi}
, define the function

\varphi_{c,\alpha}
on
D_{\varphi}
by

\varphi_{c,\alpha}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\varphi(\lambda)&\ \ {\rm if}\quad\lambda\in D_{\varphi}\setminus\{\alpha\}\\
\\
c&\ \ {\rm if}\quad\lambda=\alpha.\end{array}\right.

(2.6)

Typically, modification of
\varphi
in
this manner will affect the operator
X_{\varphi}:E_{\varphi}\to\mathcal{H}
. This modification could happen in two possible ways:
E_{\varphi}

could be changed, or
E_{\varphi}
could remain unaltered but
the action of
X_{\varphi}
could be changed.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier and let
\alpha\in D_{\varphi}
. We say
\alpha
is an
ambiguous point for

\varphi
, or an
ambiguity of
\varphi
, if there exists
c\in\mathbb{C}
with the properties
that
c\not=\varphi(\alpha),\;E_{\varphi}=E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}
, and

X_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}=X_{\varphi}
. We say
\alpha
is an
unambiguous point for

\varphi
if
\alpha
is not an
ambiguous point for
\varphi
.

The pseudomultiplier
\psi
of
H^{2}
given in Example
2.3
has a discontinuity at
0
which is an ambiguous point for
\psi
. Indeed, for any choice of
c\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{a,b\}
, the one-point modification
\psi_{c,0}
of
\psi
is still an instance of Example
2.3
, so that
E_{\psi_{c,0}}=k_{0}^{\perp}=E_{\psi}
. Moreover, for any
f\in k_{0}^{\perp}
,

X_{\psi}f=af=X_{\psi_{c,0}}f
. Another example of an ambiguous point is furnished by the pseudomultiplier
\chi
in Example
2.5
. It has an ambiguous point at
0
, as we show in Example
4.2
below.

In Section
4
, Theorem
4.14
, we shall prove the following connection between visibility of vectors and ambiguous points.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier of a Hilbert function space
\mathcal{H}
on a set
\Omega
and let
\alpha\in\Omega
. Then
\alpha
is an ambiguous point for

\varphi
if and only if
\varphi
sees
k_{\alpha}
with
arbitrary value.

Theorem
2.12
leads naturally to a more general notion, an

ambiguous vector
for a pseudomultiplier.

Definition 2.13
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}
and
let

v\in\mathcal{H}
. We say that
v
is an ambiguous vector
for

\varphi
if
\varphi
sees
v
with arbitrary value, and
we define
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
to be the set of ambiguous vectors for
\varphi
.

It follows from Theorem
2.12
that if
\alpha
is a ambiguous point for

then

is an ambiguous vector for
\varphi
.
Observe also that Example
2.8
immediately implies that, for any Hilbert function space
\mathcal{H}
, the zero vector of
\mathcal{H}
is an ambiguous vector of every pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}
.

Example 2.14
.

\varphi(z)=1/z
on
H^{2}
has no non-zero ambiguities.
By Example

, the pseudomultiplier

of

for some

. Moreover, for such

,
\varphi
sees
k_{\lambda}
only with value
\varphi(\lambda)
. Thus
\varphi
does not see any non-zero vector with arbitrary value, which is to say that
\varphi
has no ambiguous vectors other than

.

Example 2.15
.

Consider the pseudomultiplier
\chi(t)=\sqrt{t}
on

of Example

.
We will show in Example

.

2.5. Definable vectors and the polar space

In addition to singularities that arise from ambiguous vectors, pseudomultipliers can possess another type of singular vector, which we call a
polar vector
.
We say that a vector

d\in\mathcal{H}
is
definable for

\varphi
if
d\not=0,\varphi
sees
d
, and
d\perp\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
. We denote by

\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
the set of vectors that are
definable for
\varphi
.
For any definable vector
v
for a pseudomultiplier
\varphi
, there is a unique
c\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\varphi
sees
v
with value
c
. We denote this number
c
by
\varphi(v)
. Thus we can regard
\varphi
as a function on
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
.

Example 2.16
.

The definable vectors for
1/z
on
H^{2}
.
By Example
2.14
, for the pseudomultiplier
\varphi(z)=1/z
of
H^{2}
,
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}=\{0\}
, and so the definable vectors for
\varphi
coincide with the non-zero vectors visible to
\varphi
. Hence, by Example
2.10
,

\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
comprises the non-zero scalar multiples of the
kernels
k_{\lambda}
, for
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
.

Definition 2.17
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier and let
p\in\mathcal{H}
. We say
p
is a
polar vector for

\varphi
if
p\not=0
and there exists a sequence of definable vectors
\{d_{n}\}\subseteq\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
such that
d_{n}\to p
(with respect to

\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
) and
\varphi(d_{n})\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.
We define
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
, the

polar space of

\varphi
, to be the set of vectors
v\in\mathcal{H}
such that
either
v
is a polar vector of
\varphi
or
v=0
.

Remark 2.18
.

In
[
4
, Section 1]
we introduced a notion more directly modelled on the familiar notion of a pole from Complex Analysis. If
\varphi
is a pseudomultiplier of a Hilbert function space
\mathcal{H}
on a set
\Omega
we said in
[
4
]
that a point
\alpha\in\Omega
is a
pseudopole
of
\varphi
if there exist functions
g\in\mathcal{H}
and
h\in E_{\varphi}
such that
h(\alpha)=0
,
g
extends
\varphi h
and
g(\alpha)\neq 0
.

From the perspective of this paper, pseudopoles are superseded by the more general notion of polar vectors. Indeed, we shall prove in Proposition
6.8
that if
\alpha
is a pseudopole of a pseudomultiplier
\varphi
and
\alpha
is in the closure of
D_{\varphi}
in
\Omega
then
P_{\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}^{\perp}}k_{\alpha}
is a polar vector of
\varphi
. Here, by the closure of
D_{\varphi}
we mean the closure in
\Omega
with respect to the natural metric on
\Omega
, induced by the norm on
\mathcal{H}
and the identification of a point of
\Omega
with its reproducing kernel in
\mathcal{H}
.
\square

Example 2.19
.

The polar space of
1/z
.
In Example
2.16
we proved that the set of definable vectors

for

It is enough to show that

is in
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
.
Choose
\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{D}
,

, such that
\lambda_{n}\to 0
. By Example

,
\varphi
sees

with value
\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}
. Since

in

if and only if

in the usual topology of

,

,

2.6. Decomposition of the singular space

In Theorem
7.2
we shall prove that every singular vector of a pseudomultiplier can be represented in a unique way as the sum of a polar vector and an ambiguous vector.

Theorem 2.22
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert function space on a set

\Omega
.
Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}
.
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
is a closed
subspace of
\mathcal{H}
and

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\oplus\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}.

This theorem contains the very interesting fact that the polar space
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
, which is the set of polar vectors of a pseudomultiplier
\varphi
, together with the zero vector, constitutes a linear subspace of
\mathcal{H}
.

.

The pseudomultiplier

, and the polar space of

7.3
for proofs.

.

2.7. Locality

In Section
8
we shall introduce the notion of a visible subspace for a pseudomultiplier.
We generalise the notion of
\varphi
seeing a vector to
\varphi
seeing a closed subspace of
\mathcal{H}
.
We say that
\varphi
sees a closed subspace
\mathcal{V}
if there is a bounded linear operator
C:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
such that, for all
f\in E_{\varphi}
,

P_{\mathcal{V}}X_{\varphi}f=CP_{\mathcal{V}}f

(2.7)

where
P_{\mathcal{V}}
is the orthogonal projection of
\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathcal{V}
.

Example 2.25
.

Let
\varphi\in H^{\infty}
, so that
\varphi
is a
multiplier of
H^{2}
. The only vectors in
H^{2}
seen by

\varphi
are the eigenvectors of
X^{*}_{\varphi}
. In
particular, if
\varphi
is such that
\varphi^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
, then
\varphi
does not see the vector
v(\lambda)=\lambda,\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
. However,
\varphi
does see the space

\mathcal{V}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\operatorname{span}\{1,v\}
: for
f\in H^{2},P_{\mathcal{V}}X_{\varphi}f
depends only the first two
Taylor coefficients of
f
, that is, on
P_{\mathcal{V}}f
.
Thus
\varphi
sees
\mathcal{V}
, and there is a
unique

C

such that equation (
8.1
) holds for all
f\in E_{\varphi}
.
\square

We observe that in the case when
\varphi
is a
multiplier of
\mathcal{H}
,
\varphi
sees a closed subspace
\mathcal{V}
of
\mathcal{H}

if and only if
\mathcal{V}
is an invariant subspace for
X^{*}_{\varphi}
.
In Proposition
8.8
we show that a closed subspace
\mathcal{V}
is visible to a psudomultiplier
\varphi
if and only if
X_{\varphi}^{*}\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}+E_{\varphi}^{\perp}
.

In Section
9

we introduce the idea of a
local subspace
of a
Hilbert function space over a subset
D
of
\Omega
.
In Section
10
we shall clarify the sense in which a subspace
which is local over a set
D
can genuinely be attached to a
set of points in
D
. The following key example shows the naturalness of the notion of a local subspace.

Example 2.26
.

Consider the subspace

\mathcal{M}=\operatorname{span}\{1,z\}
of
H^{2}
. The elements of
\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
are the functions which vanish together with their
first derivatives at
0
, and we accordingly regard
\mathcal{M}

as being local at
0
. Notice that

\mathcal{M}
is not spanned by kernel functions – rather, it is a
limit of two-dimensional spaces spanned by kernels. For

f\in H^{2}
we have

f^{\prime}(0)=\lim\limits_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{f(\alpha)-f(0)}{\alpha}=\lim\limits_{\alpha\to 0}\left\langle f,\frac{k_{\alpha}-k_{0}}{\overline{\alpha}}\right\rangle.

In a sense to be made
precise,

\mathcal{M}
is the limit as
\alpha\to 0
of the space

\operatorname{span}\{1,\overline{\alpha}^{-1}(k_{\alpha}-1)\}

(which equals
\operatorname{span}\{1,k_{\alpha}\}
). Likewise
\operatorname{span}\{1,z,z^{2}\}
is
the limit as

\alpha,\beta\to 0
of

\operatorname{span}\{1,k_{\alpha},k_{\beta}\}
.

This example provides the model for the notion of a
local subspace of a general Hilbert function space.

Let
D\subset\Omega
. A closed subspace
\mathcal{M}
of

\mathcal{H}
is
local over

D
if
\mathcal{M}
is a cluster point of a collection of spans of uniformly boundedly many kernels of points in
D
(see Definition
9.11
).
We say that
\alpha\in\Omega
is a
support point
of
\mathcal{M}
if there exist a positive integer
n
, a sequence
(\alpha_{j})
in
\Omega
, a sequence
(c_{j})
in

\mathbb{C}
and a sequence
(E_{j})
of subsets of
\Omega

such that
\#E_{j}=n,\alpha_{j}\in E_{j},\operatorname{span}\{k_{\lambda}:\lambda\in E_{j}\}\to\mathcal{M}
and
c_{j}k_{\alpha_{j}}\to k_{\alpha}
. The
support
of

\mathcal{M}
is the set of support points of
\mathcal{M}
. It will
be denoted by
\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{M}
. We say that
\mathcal{M}
is
punctual
if
\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{M}
is a singleton set. Example
2.26
shows that the local subspace

\mathcal{M}=\operatorname{span}\{1,z\}
of
H^{2}
is punctual with
\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{M}=\{0\}
.

The main result of Section
9
is Theorem
9.14
, which asserts that all local spaces are seen by all pseudomultipliers. This fact confirms the importance of the notion of local subspaces.

Theorem 2.27
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}
. If
\mathcal{M}
is a
subspace of
\mathcal{H}
which is local over
D_{\varphi}
,
then
\varphi
sees
\mathcal{M}
.

In light of Theorem
2.27
it appears that the structure of local subspaces is an important component of the theory of pseudomultipliers. Therefore, in the final section of the paper we study such local subspaces. In Theorem
10.13
, we establish the structure
of local subspaces
\mathcal{M}
of projectively complete Hilbert function spaces
\mathcal{H}
, as follows.

Theorem 2.28
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a projectively complete Hilbert function
space on
\Omega
, let
D\subset\Omega
and let
\mathcal{M}
be a local space over
D
. There exist subspaces
\mathcal{M}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{N}
of
\mathcal{H}
such that

(i)

\mathcal{M}_{i}
is punctual for
i=1,\dots,N
;

(ii)

\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{M}_{i}=\{\alpha_{i}\}\subset{\rm pc}(D),1\leq i\leq N
;

(iii)

\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{1}+\mathcal{M}_{2}+\dots+\mathcal{M}_{N}
;

(iv)

\sum\limits^{N}_{i=1}\dim\mathcal{M}_{i}=\dim\mathcal{M}
, and

(v)

\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{M}=\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N}\}
.

In this theorem,
\mathrm{pc}(D)
denotes the projective closure of a subset
D
of
\Omega
, which is defined immediately after Definition
10.10
.

3.
Examples of pseudomultipliers

In Definition
2.4
we introduced pseudomultipliers
\varphi
and their associated multiplication operators
X_{\varphi}
, as a generalization of the notion of a multiplier (as in Definition
2.1
) of a Hilbert function space.
Firstly, we
allowed the possibility that
\varphi
only be defined on a
proper subset of
\Omega
and secondly we allowed

X_{\varphi}
only to be defined on a proper subspace of
\mathcal{H}
of finite codimension.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert function space on a set

\Omega
.
We will start with examples of functions

\varphi:D_{\varphi}\subset\Omega\to\mathbb{C}

which are not pseudomultipliers.
Firstly, we assume that
D_{\varphi}
is a set of uniqueness for
\mathcal{H}
.
Let the subset
E_{\varphi}
of
\mathcal{H}
be defined by

\left\{h\in\mathcal{H}:{\rm there\ exists}\ g\in\mathcal{H}\ {\rm such\ that}\ g(\lambda)=\varphi(\lambda)h(\lambda)\ {\rm for\ all}\ \lambda\in D_{\varphi}\right\}

There are two ways that a function
\varphi

defined on a set of uniqueness can fail to be a
pseudomultiplier:
E_{\varphi}
might not be closed or

E_{\varphi}
might be closed but not of finite codimension.

Let
X_{\varphi}
be the operator

X_{\varphi}:E_{\varphi}\to\mathcal{H}
given by
X_{\varphi}h=g
where

g
is the element of
\mathcal{H}

such that
g(\lambda)=\varphi(\lambda)h(\lambda)
for all

\lambda\in D_{\varphi}
.

Remark 3.1
.

In the case that
E_{\varphi}
is closed
in
\mathcal{H}
, it is an easy consequence of the closed graph
theorem that
X_{\varphi}
is bounded. Conversely, if

X_{\varphi}
is bounded then
E_{\varphi}
is closed. For
suppose
f_{n}\in E_{\varphi}
and
f_{n}\to g
in
\mathcal{H}
. Then
the sequence
(X_{\varphi}f_{n})
is bounded in
\mathcal{H}
and, by
passing to a subsequence if necessary, we can suppose that

X_{\varphi}f_{n}
tends weakly to some
h\in\mathcal{H}
. For any

\lambda\in D_{\varphi}
we have

(X_{\varphi}f_{n})(\lambda)=\varphi(\lambda)f_{n}(\lambda)\to\varphi(\lambda)g(\lambda)

and

(X_{\varphi}f_{n})(\lambda)\to h(\lambda).

Thus
h\in\mathcal{H}
extends
\varphi(g|D_{\varphi})
, and so

g\in E_{\varphi}
. Thus
E_{\varphi}
is closed. In particular, if

E_{\varphi}
has finite codimension, then
\varphi
is a
pseudomultiplier if and only if
X_{\varphi}
is a bounded
operator.

Example 3.2
.

Let
\Omega=\mathbb{D}
and let
\mathcal{H}=H^{2}
, the
classical Hardy space. Let
D_{\varphi}=\mathbb{D}
and
define
\varphi
by
\varphi(z)=(1-z)^{-1}
. Here

E_{\varphi}=(1-z)H^{2}
which is a nonclosed subspace of

H^{2}
. Thus
\varphi
is not a pseudomultiplier.
\square

Example 3.3
.

Let
\Omega=\mathbb{D}
and let
\mathcal{H}=H^{2}
. Let
\psi
be an inner function on
\mathbb{D}
which is not a finite Blascke product and let
\varphi=1/\psi
.
Let

D_{\varphi}=\mathbb{D}\setminus\psi^{-1}\{0\}
.
Here

E_{\varphi}=\psi H^{2}
is a closed
subspace of

\mathcal{H}
. Yet
\varphi
is not a pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}

since
E_{\varphi}
has infinite codimension in
\mathcal{H}
.
\square

Example 3.4
.

Let
\Omega=\mathbb{D}^{2}
and let
\mathcal{H}=H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
, the Hardy space on the bidisc. Let

D_{\varphi}=\{z\in\mathbb{D}^{2}:z_{1}\not=0\}
and define
\varphi

by

\varphi(z)=z^{-1}_{1}
. Here

E_{\varphi}=z_{1}H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
is a closed

\mathcal{H}
. Yet
\varphi
is not a pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}

since

has infinite codimension.
\square

There
are also two ways that a pseudomultiplier
\varphi

can fail to be a multiplier:
D_{\varphi}=\Omega
but

or
E_{\varphi}=\mathcal{H}
but

D_{\varphi}\not=\Omega
.

Example 3.5
.

Let
\Omega
be a nonempty open proper subset of

\mathbb{D}
and let

\mathcal{H}
be the space of analytic functions
f
on
\Omega

that have an extension
f^{\sim}
to
\mathbb{D}
such that

f^{\sim}\in H^{2}
. Let
\mathcal{H}
have the inner product defined
by

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\ =\ \left\langle f^{\sim},g^{\sim}\right\rangle_{H^{2}}.

(3.1)

Fix
\alpha\in\mathbb{D}\backslash\Omega
and define

on

by

.
Then

and, since equation (

) implies that
we may identify

with

, we have

a closed subspace of

but is

4.
Ambiguous points

In this section we shall repeatedly use a notation introduced in the overview of the paper.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert function space on
\Omega
.
For a pseudomultiplier
\varphi
of
\mathcal{H}
,
c\in\mathbb{C}
and
\alpha\in D_{\varphi}
, define the pseudomultiplier

\varphi_{c,\alpha}
on
D_{\varphi}
by

\varphi_{c,\alpha}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\varphi(\lambda)&\ \ {\rm if}\quad\lambda\in D_{\varphi}\setminus\{\alpha\}\\
\\
c&\ \ {\rm if}\quad\lambda=\alpha.\end{array}\right.

(4.1)

Recall Definition
2.11
: a point
\alpha\in D_{\varphi}
is an
ambiguous point for
a pseudomultiplier

\varphi
of
\mathcal{H}
if there exists
c\in\mathbb{C}
with the properties
that
c\not=\varphi(\alpha),E_{\varphi}=E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}
, and

X_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}=X_{\varphi}
.
An ambiguous point is a point at which the value of a
pseudomultiplier
\varphi
is not determined by the
associated operator
X_{\varphi}
.

In Definition
4.11
below we shall extend the notion of an ambiguous point of
\varphi
to points
\alpha\in\Omega
not assumed to be in
D_{\varphi}
. We then prove in Theorem
4.14
that

\alpha\in\Omega
is ambiguous for
\varphi
if and
only if, for every
c\in\mathbb{C},\ \varphi
sees

k_{\alpha}
with value
c
.
This linkage between ambiguous points and the notion of seeing a vector is useful for the
organization of the wide variety of examples of
pseudomultipliers and for the analysis of their singularities.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\psi
be a multiplier of a Hilbert function space
\mathcal{H}
on a set
\Omega
. Suppose that
\alpha\in\Omega
has the property
that there exists a sequence
\{\lambda_{n}\}\subseteq\Omega
with
\lambda_{n}\not=\alpha
and
k_{\lambda_{n}}\to k_{\alpha}
. Let

\varphi=\psi_{z,\alpha}
where
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{\psi(\alpha)\}
. Then
\varphi
is a pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}
and
\alpha
is an
ambiguous point for
\varphi
.

Proof.

This follows immediately
from Definition
2.11
and the following simple observation:

{\rm if}\ c\not=\psi(\alpha),\ {\rm then}\ E_{\psi_{c,\alpha}}=k^{\perp}_{\alpha}\ {\rm and}\ X_{\psi_{c,\alpha}}=X_{\psi}|k^{\perp}_{\alpha}.

(4.2)

To prove equation (
4.2
), fix
c\not=\psi(\alpha)
and first assume
that
f\in k^{\perp}_{\alpha}
. Evidently,

\psi_{c,\alpha}(\lambda)f(\lambda)=\psi(\lambda)f(\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in\Omega\backslash\{\alpha\}
. But since
f\in k^{\perp}_{\alpha},f(\alpha)=0
, and
so
\psi_{c,\alpha}(\alpha)f(\alpha)=\psi(\alpha)f(\alpha)
.
Thus,
f\in k^{\perp}_{\alpha}
implies that
\psi_{c,\alpha}f=\psi f\in\mathcal{H}
and we have shown that
k^{\perp}_{\alpha}\subseteq E_{\psi}
and
X_{\psi_{c,\alpha}}|k^{\perp}_{\alpha}=X_{\psi}|k^{\perp}_{\alpha}
. There remains to show that

E_{\psi_{c,\alpha}}\subseteq k^{\perp}_{\alpha}
. Accordingly,
fix
f\in E_{\psi_{c,\alpha}}
. Thus,
\psi_{c,\alpha}f\in\mathcal{H}
. But
\psi
is a multiplier and so we see that

g=\psi_{c,\alpha}f-\psi f\in\mathcal{H}
. Furthermore,

g(\lambda)=0
if
\lambda\not=\alpha
. Recalling our
technical hypothesis we see that

g(\alpha)=\left\langle g,k_{\alpha}\right\rangle=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left\langle g,k_{\lambda_{n}}\right\rangle=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}g(\lambda_{n})=0.

Hence
0=g(\alpha)=(c-\psi(\alpha))f(\alpha).

But

c\not=\psi(\alpha)
, so
f(\alpha)=0
i.e.
f\in k^{\perp}_{\alpha}
. This concludes the proof of equation (
4.2
).

\square

h_{1}(\alpha)=c_{1}
and
h_{2}(\alpha)=c_{2}
. Now define
f\in\mathcal{H}
by the formula

f=(c_{2}-\varphi(\alpha))f_{1}-(c_{1}-\varphi(\alpha))f_{2},

and define
h\in\mathcal{H}
by the formula

h=(c_{2}-\varphi(\alpha))h_{1}-(c_{1}-\varphi(\alpha))h_{2}.

We claim that
\varphi f=h
on
D_{\varphi}
. To see this,
first fix
\lambda\in D_{\varphi}\backslash\{\alpha\}
and
note that

\displaystyle\varphi(\lambda)f(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(c_{2}-\varphi(\alpha))\varphi(\lambda)f_{1}(\lambda)-(c_{1}-\varphi(\alpha))\varphi(\lambda)f_{2}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(c_{2}-\varphi(\alpha))\varphi_{c_{1},\alpha}(\lambda)f_{1}(\lambda)-(c_{1}-\varphi(\alpha))\varphi_{c_{2},\alpha}((\lambda)f_{2}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(c_{2}-\varphi(\alpha))h_{1}(\lambda)-(c_{1}-\varphi(\alpha))h_{2}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h(\lambda).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\varphi(\alpha)f(\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(\alpha)\Big((c_{2}-\varphi(\alpha)f_{1}(\alpha)-(c_{1}-\varphi(\alpha))f_{2}(\alpha)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(\alpha)\Big((c_{2}-\varphi(\alpha))-(c_{1}-\varphi(\alpha))\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(\alpha)(c_{2}-c_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(c_{2}-\varphi(\alpha)\Big)c_{1}-\Big(c_{1}-\varphi(\alpha)\Big)c_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(c_{2}-\varphi(\alpha)\Big)h_{1}(\alpha)-\Big(c_{1}-\varphi(\alpha)\Big)h_{2}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h(\alpha).

Thus, in fact,
\varphi f=h
on
D_{\varphi}
. But this means
that
f\in E_{\varphi}
. Since, by assumption,
E_{\varphi}\subseteq k^{\perp}_{\alpha},f\in k^{\perp}_{\alpha}
, i.e.

f(\alpha)=0
. Thus

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(c_{2}-\varphi(\alpha)\Big)f_{1}(\alpha)-\Big(c_{1}-\varphi(\alpha)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(c_{2}-\varphi(\alpha)\Big)-\Big(c_{1}-\varphi(\alpha)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{2}-\ c_{1},

so that
c_{1}=c_{2}
as was to be shown.

\square

Lemmas
4.4
and
4.5
combine to give a
simple criterion for a point
\alpha
to be an ambiguous
point for a pseudomultiplier.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier and let
\alpha\in D_{\varphi}
. Then
\alpha
is an ambiguous point for

\varphi
if and only if
E_{\varphi}\subseteq k^{\perp}_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

First assume that
E_{\varphi}\subseteq k^{\perp}_{\alpha}
. By Lemma
4.5
, there exists
c\not=\varphi(\alpha)
such that
E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}\subseteq k^{\perp}_{\alpha}
. But then Lemma
4.4
implies that

E_{\varphi}=E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}
and
X_{\varphi}=X_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}
. Thus
\alpha
is an ambiguous
point for
\varphi
.

Conversely, assume that
\alpha
is an ambiguous point for

\varphi
. Thus there exists
c\not=\varphi(\alpha)
such
that
E_{\varphi}=E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}
and
X_{\varphi}=X_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}
. We need to show that
E_{\varphi}\subseteq k^{\perp}_{\alpha}
. Accordingly, fix
f\in E_{\varphi}
. To see that
f\in k^{\perp}_{\alpha}
we argue by
contradiction. Assume that
f(\alpha)\not=0
. Now
since
f\in E_{\varphi}=E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}
there exist

h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\varphi f=h_{1}
and

\varphi_{c,\alpha}f=h_{2}
on
D_{\varphi}
. Hence, if we set

h=h_{1}-h_{2}
we have that
h\in\mathcal{H}
and

\displaystyle h(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{1}(\lambda)-h_{2}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(\lambda)f(\lambda)-\varphi_{c,\alpha}(\lambda)f(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}0&{\rm if}\quad\lambda\in D_{\varphi}\backslash\{\alpha\}\\
\\
\Big(\varphi(\alpha)-c\Big)f(\alpha)&{\rm if}\quad\lambda=\alpha.\end{array}\right.

Note that since
\varphi(\alpha)\not=c
and
f(\alpha)\not=0
, this formula implies that
h\not=0
. Also, the
formula implies that
\varphi h=\varphi(\alpha)h
on

D_{\varphi}
so that
h\in E_{\varphi}
and
X_{\varphi}h=\varphi(\alpha)h
. But the formula similarly implies
that
X_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}h=ch
. Thus, since

\varphi(\alpha)\not=c
and
h\not=0,X_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}h\not=X_{\varphi}h
, contradicting
the fact that
X_{\varphi}=X_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}
. This
completes the proof of Proposition
4.6
.
\square

Thus
\alpha
is an ambiguous point for
\varphi
if and
only if
E_{\varphi}\subseteq k^{\perp}_{\alpha}
. A
consequence of Lemmas
4.4
and
4.5
is that, for
ambiguous points
\alpha
, there is a rather rigid
dichotomy for the behavior of the spaces

E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}
as

varies.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier and let
\alpha\in D_{\varphi}
. If
\alpha
is an
ambiguous point for
\varphi
then exactly one of the following two
statements holds:

(i)

E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}\subseteq k^{\perp}_{\alpha}\text{ for all }c\in\mathbb{C};

(4.5)

(ii)

There exists
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
such that

E_{\varphi_{\gamma,\alpha}}\not\subset k^{\perp}_{\alpha}\text{ but }E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}\subset k^{\perp}_{\alpha}

(4.6)

for all
c\in\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\{\gamma\}
.

Furthermore,

if equation (
4.5
) holds then

E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}=E_{\varphi}
and

X_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}=X_{\varphi}
for all

c\in\mathbb{C}
;

if equation (
4.6
) holds then

E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}=E_{\varphi}
and

X_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}=X_{\varphi}
for all

c\in\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\{\gamma\}
while

E_{\varphi}
is a subspace of
E_{\varphi_{\gamma,\alpha}}

of codimension 1 and
X_{\varphi}=X_{\varphi_{\gamma,\alpha}}|E_{\varphi}
.

Proof.

That either of equations (
4.5
) or (
4.6
) occurs is
simply a restatement of Lemma
4.5
. If (
4.5
) occurs,
then Lemma
4.4
implies that
E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}=E_{\varphi}
and
X_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}=X_{\varphi}
for all

c\in\mathbb{C}
. If (
4.6
) occurs then again Lemma

4.4
implies that
E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}=E_{\varphi}

and
X_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}=X_{\varphi}
for all
c\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{\gamma\}
. Finally, suppose that
(
4.6
) occurs, so that
E_{\varphi_{\gamma,\alpha}}\not\subseteq k^{\perp}_{\alpha}
. Since by Lemma
4.4
,

E_{\varphi}=E_{\varphi_{\gamma,\alpha}}\cap k^{\perp}_{\alpha}
,
necessarily
E_{\varphi}\subseteq E_{\varphi_{\gamma,\alpha}}
and
E_{\varphi}
has
codimension 1 in
E_{\varphi_{\gamma,\alpha}}
. Also, in

4.4
implies that
X_{\varphi}=X_{\varphi_{\gamma,\alpha}}|E_{\varphi}
.

\square

Lemma

has a number of corollaries about the

4.7
, the inclusion (
4.5
) occurs when

is essential, and the relation
(
4.6
) occurs when
\alpha
is removable.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier and assume that

\alpha\in D_{\varphi}
is an ambiguous point for
\varphi
. Then
\alpha

is an essential ambiguous point for
\varphi
if
and only if
E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}\subset k^{\perp}_{\alpha}

for all
c\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

By definition,
\alpha
is essential
if and only if
\alpha
is ambiguous for
\varphi_{c,\alpha}

for all
c\in\mathbb{C}
. Hence the proposition follows
immediately from Proposition
4.6
.
\square

Notice that in Definition
2.11

\alpha
was said to be
ambiguous if
\varphi
could be redefined at
\alpha

without the underlying operator
X_{\varphi}
changing. In
fact, if
\alpha
is ambiguous,
\varphi
can be
arbitrarily
redefined at
\alpha
if
\alpha
is
essential and arbitrarily redefined at
\alpha
with
one exception if
\alpha
is removable.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier and let

\alpha\in D_{\varphi}
be an ambiguous point for
\varphi
.

(i)

If
\alpha
is essential, then
E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}=E_{\varphi}
and
X_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}=X_{\varphi}
for all

c\in\mathbb{C}
.

(ii)

If
\alpha
is removable then there exists
a unique
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
such that
E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}=E_{\varphi}

if and only if
c\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{\gamma\}
. Moreover, this number
\gamma
has the property that
X_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}=X_{\varphi}
if and only if
c\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{\gamma\}
.

Proof.

(i) Suppose that
\alpha
is an essential ambiguous point for
\varphi
and
c\in\mathbb{C}
. By Proposition
4.6

E_{\varphi}\subseteq k_{\alpha}^{\perp}
. Thus, for any function

,
f(\alpha)=0
and so
\varphi_{c,\alpha}f=\varphi f
on

, from which it follows that
E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}=E_{\varphi}
and

.

(ii) Suppose that

is a removable ambiguous point for
\varphi
. Then

is not an essential ambiguous point for

, and so, by Proposition

, there exists

it is alternative (ii) that holds, and so

for all

, and by the “Furthermore” of Lemma

,
E_{\varphi_{c,\alpha}}=E_{\varphi}
and

for all

.
It follows that

is unique.

\square

We conclude our remarks concerning ambiguous points

\alpha\in D_{\varphi}
by noting the following fact.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier on
\mathcal{H}
and let

\alpha\in D_{\varphi}
. If
\alpha
is a removable ambiguous point for

(i)

there exists a unique

is not an ambiguous point for

(ii)

.

.

Suppose that
\alpha

\varphi
.

, there exists

such that
\alpha
is not an ambiguous point for

, so that, to prove statement (i), we must only show that this

(ii), this value (denoted by

(ii) Since

(4.7)

.

.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier and let
\alpha\in\Omega
. Then
\alpha
is ambiguous for
\varphi
if and
only if, for every
c\in\mathbb{C},\ \varphi
sees

k_{\alpha}
with value
c
.

Proof.

First suppose that
\varphi
sees

k_{\alpha}
with arbitrary value. Thus, for each
c
there
exists a pseudomultiplier
\omega_{c}
of
\mathcal{H}_{k_{\alpha}}
such that
\omega_{c}
extends
\varphi,E_{\varphi}=E_{\omega_{c}}|\Omega,p\in D_{\omega_{c}}
, and
\omega_{c}(p)=c
. In
particular if
f\in E_{\varphi}
then
f_{k_{\alpha}}\in E_{\omega_{c}}
and there exists
h\in\mathcal{H}
such that

\omega_{c}f_{k_{\alpha}}=h_{k_{\alpha}}
. Noting that
\omega_{c}

extends
\varphi
and
\omega_{c}(p)=c
, we
deduce that the following holds.

If
f\in E_{\varphi}
, then there exists
h\in\mathcal{H}
such
that

\displaystyle\varphi(\lambda)f(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h(\lambda)\qquad{\rm if}\ \lambda\in D_{\varphi}\quad{\rm and}

(4.8)

\displaystyle c\ f(\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h(\alpha)

\alpha\in D_{\varphi}
or
\alpha\in\Omega\smallsetminus D_{\varphi}
. If
\alpha\in D_{\varphi}
, then noting that in equations
(

), while

depends on

and

do not,
we deduce that
\varphi(\alpha)f(\alpha)=cf(\alpha)
for

. Hence,

implies that
\alpha
is an ambiguous
point for
\varphi
.

Now assume that
\alpha
is an ambiguous point for

\varphi
. Fix

and define
\omega_{c}
on

by the

\\

with value

is an ambiguous point for

.

sees

with value

.

\square

5.
Ambiguous vectors and the ambiguous space

We have shown that a point
\alpha\in\Omega
is an ambiguous point for a pseudomultiplier

\varphi
if and only if
\varphi
sees
k_{\alpha}
with
arbitrary value. We have also abstracted this property to say that
a vector

v\in\mathcal{H}
is an ambiguous vector
for a pseudomultiplier

\varphi
of
\mathcal{H}
if
\varphi
sees
v
with arbitrary value (see Definition
2.13
).
Thus, if
\varphi
is a pseudomultiplier and
\alpha
is a point,

\alpha
is an ambiguous point for
\varphi
if and only if

k_{\alpha}
is an ambiguous vector for
\varphi
. An
ambiguous vector need not be a kernel. In this section we shall study general ambiguous vectors for
\varphi
.

The following lemma gives an
operator-theoretic expression of the concepts of
seeing a vector and ambiguity. Recall that if
\varphi
is a
pseudomultiplier then
X_{\varphi}
is a bounded linear
transformation from
E_{\varphi}
into
\mathcal{H}
. Thus

X^{*}_{\varphi}
is a bounded linear
transformation from
\mathcal{H}
into
E_{\varphi}
.
Recall also a basic fact in the theory of multipliers that if

\varphi
is a multiplier of
\mathcal{H}
and
k_{\lambda}
is the
reproducing kernel for
\lambda\in\Omega
, then

X_{\varphi}^{*}k_{\lambda}=\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}k_{\lambda}
. We generalize this fact to
pseudomultipliers in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}
and let
v\in\mathcal{H}
.
Then

(i)

\varphi
sees
v
with value
c
if and only if there
exists
u\in E^{\perp}_{\varphi}
such that

X^{*}_{\varphi}v=\overline{c}v+u.

(5.1)

(ii)

\varphi
sees
v
with value
c
if and only if

X^{*}_{\varphi}v=\overline{c}P_{E_{\varphi}}v.

(5.2)

(iii)
If
\varphi
sees
v
with two distinct values then
v
is an ambiguous vector for
\varphi
.

Proof.

(i). Fix a pseudomultiplier
\varphi,c\in\mathbb{C}
, and
v\in\mathcal{H}
. Define
\psi
on
D_{\varphi}\cup\{p\}\subseteq\Omega\cup\{p\}
by the formula

\psi(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\varphi(\lambda)&\qquad\mbox{ for all }\lambda\in D_{\varphi}\\
\\
c&\qquad\text{ if }\lambda=p,\end{array}\right.

so that
\varphi
sees
v
with value
c
if and only if

(E_{\psi})|\Omega=E_{\varphi}.

(5.3)

By Lemma
3.13
, equation (
5.3
) is equivalent to the inclusion

(E_{\psi})|\Omega\supset E_{\varphi}.

(5.4)

Assume that
\varphi
sees
v
with value
c
, so that equation (
5.4
) holds. It means that, for any
f\in E_{\varphi}
there exists
g\in\mathcal{H}
such that
g_{v}=\psi f_{v}
on
D_{\psi}
.
It follows that
g(z)=\varphi(z)f(z)=(X_{\varphi}f)(z)
for all
z\in D_{\varphi}
, and (since
g_{v}(p)=(\psi f)(p)
)

c\left\langle f,v\right\rangle=\left\langle g,v\right\rangle.

(5.5)

Thus

c\left\langle f,v\right\rangle=\left\langle X_{\varphi}f,v\right\rangle\qquad{\rm for\ all}\quad f\in E_{\varphi},

and so
X_{\varphi}^{*}v-\bar{c}v\in E_{\varphi}^{\perp}
, and equation (
5.1
) holds.

Conversely, suppose there exists
u\in E^{\perp}_{\varphi}
such
that
X^{*}_{\varphi}v=\overline{c}v+u
. Thus

\left\langle f,X^{*}_{\varphi}v\right\rangle=\left\langle f,\overline{c}v\right\rangle\qquad{\rm for\ all}\quad f\in E_{\varphi},

from which it follows that

\left\langle X_{\varphi}f,v\right\rangle=c\left\langle f,v\right\rangle\qquad{\rm for\ all}\quad f\in E_{\varphi},

and hence

\left(X_{\varphi}f\right)_{v}(p)=cf_{v}(p)\qquad{\rm for\ all}\quad f\in E_{\varphi}\ .

(5.6)

We claim that equation (
5.3
) holds. To see this, consider any
f\in E_{\varphi}
. To show that
f\in E_{\psi}|\Omega
it suffices to prove that

f_{v}\in E_{\psi}
, since
f_{v}|\Omega=f
. Now
f_{v}\in E_{\psi}
if and only if there exists
g\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\psi f_{v}=g_{v}
, which is to say that
\varphi f=g
on
D_{\varphi}
, and, corresponding to evaluation at
p
,
c\left\langle f,v\right\rangle=\left\langle g,v\right\rangle
. Equation (
5.6
) shows that we may obtain such an element
g
of
\mathcal{H}
by choosing
g=X_{\varphi}f
.
We have shown that
\varphi
sees
v
with value
c
.
This establishes Lemma
5.1
(i).

(ii) is an immediate consequence of Part (i) and the
fact that
{\rm ran}\ X^{*}_{\varphi}\subseteq E_{\varphi}
.

(iii)
Suppose
\varphi
sees
v
with values
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, where
c_{1}\neq c_{2}
.
By part (ii) the following equations hold:

X^{*}_{\varphi}v=\overline{c}_{1}P_{E_{\varphi}}v\;\text{and}\;X^{*}_{\varphi}v=\overline{c}_{2}P_{E_{\varphi}}v.

Hence

(\overline{c}_{1}-\overline{c}_{2})P_{E_{\varphi}}v=0
, and therefore
P_{E_{\varphi}}v=0
and

X^{*}_{\varphi}v=0
.
Thus
X^{*}_{\varphi}v=\overline{c}P_{E_{\varphi}}v
for any
c\in\mathbb{C}
, that is,

is an ambiguous vector for
\varphi
.

\square

Corollary 5.2
.

Scaling of visible vectors.
If
\varphi
sees a vector
v
with value
c
then, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
\varphi
sees
\lambda v
with value
\bar{\lambda}c
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\varphi
sees a vector
v\in\mathcal{H}
with value
c

and consider any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. By Lemma
5.1
there exists
u\in E^{\perp}_{\varphi}
such
that
X^{*}_{\varphi}v=\overline{c}v+u
. Multiply this equation through by
\lambda

Since

, it follows from Lemma

that
\varphi
sees
\lambda v
with value
\bar{\lambda}c
.

\square

The set of ambiguous vectors of a pseudomultiplier
\varphi

plays an important role: recall that in Definition
2.13
we denoted it by
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
.
As a further corollary of Lemma

we obtain an operator-theoretic
characterization of ambiguous vectors and the ambiguous space.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier and let

.
Then

(i)

v
is an ambiguous vector for
\varphi
if and only if
v\in E^{\perp}_{\varphi}\cap\ker X^{*}_{\varphi}
.

(ii)

\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}=E^{\perp}_{\varphi}\cap\ker X^{*}_{\varphi}

(5.7)

is a finite-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

(i). Suppose

is ambiguous for
\varphi
.
By Lemma
5.1
,

X^{*}_{\varphi}v=\overline{c}P_{E_{\varphi}}v

for every

. Taking

gives

and taking

gives
v\in E^{\perp}_{\varphi}
. Conversely, if

then both sides of equation (

) are

is

.

(ii) follows from Part (i).

\square

Example 5.4
.

Example 5.5

Example 5.6
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the Hilbert space of functions

If
\chi(t)=\sqrt{t}
, then we claim that

Also

is a 2 dimensional subspace consisting of ambiguous vectors.

Proof.

To prove that
E_{\chi}=\{f:f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=0\}
, consider any function
f\in\mathcal{H}
such that
f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=0
. We have to show that
\chi f
and
(\chi f)^{\prime}
are absolutely continuous on
[0,1]
and

is in

.
It follows from Example

that the function

is absolutely continuous on
[0,1]
.

Now let us show that
(\chi f)^{\prime\prime}\in L^{2}(0,1)
. We have, for

(\chi f)^{\prime\prime}(t)=-\tfrac{1}{4}t^{-\tfrac{3}{2}}f(t)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}f^{\prime}(t)+\sqrt{t}f^{\prime\prime}(t).

(5.8)

The third term in the sum on the right hand side of equation (

,
because

is in
L^{2}(0,1)

) is
\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}f^{\prime}(t)

Since

, for

\displaystyle t^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}f^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime\prime}(u)\,du

(5.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{t}\left(t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime\prime}(u)\,du\right).

By assumption

is in

\left(t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime\prime}(u)\,du\right)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}f^{\prime}(t)

.

We claim that the first term on the right hand side of equation (

\displaystyle x^{-\tfrac{3}{2}}f(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{-\tfrac{3}{2}}\int_{0}^{x}f^{\prime}(t)\,dt\qquad\qquad(\text{let }u=t/x)

(5.10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{-\tfrac{3}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(xu)x\,du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(xu)\,du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{xu}f^{\prime\prime}(t)\,dt\,du.

By Hölder’s inequality,

\left|\int_{0}^{xu}f^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\ d\xi\right|\leq|xu|^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\left\{\int_{0}^{xu}|f^{\prime\prime}(\xi)|^{2}\ d\xi\right\}^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\leq|xu|^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}}.

(5.11)

Therefore

\displaystyle|x^{-\tfrac{3}{2}}f(x)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{xu}f^{\prime\prime}(t)\,dt\,du\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle x^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}|xu|^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\,du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}u^{{\tfrac{1}{2}}}\,du=\tfrac{2}{3}\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}},

so that

is bounded on
[0,1]
. Hence the weak derivative
(\chi f)^{\prime\prime}\in L^{2}(0,1)
, and consequently
(\chi f)^{\prime\prime}\in L^{1}(0,1)

(\chi f)^{\prime}(x)-(\chi f)^{\prime}(0)=\int_{0}^{x}(\chi f)^{\prime\prime}(t)\ dt.

Note that, by equation (
2.5
) and the fact that
f(0)=0
,

(\chi f)^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}f(t)+\sqrt{t}f^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{t}\left(t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(u)\,du\right)+\sqrt{t}f^{\prime}(t).

Since
f^{\prime}
is in
L^{2}(0,1)
, by Hardy’s inequality,
\left(t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(u)\,du\right)
is in
L^{2}(0,1)
, and so, because
f^{\prime}(0)=0
,
(\chi f)^{\prime}(0)=0
. Hence
(\chi f)^{\prime}
is absolutely continuous on
[0,1]
.

Therefore, for
f\in\mathcal{H}
such that
f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=0
,
\chi f\in\mathcal{H}
.
Conversely, if
f\in\mathcal{H}
is such that the condition

is not satisfied, that is

or
f^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
then
(\chi f)^{\prime\prime}\notin L^{2}[0,1]

and so

. Thus,
\chi
is a pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}
and

E_{\chi}=\{f\in\mathcal{H}:f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=0\}
.
For

, we have
|f(0)|\leq\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}
and
|f^{\prime}(0)|\leq\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}
, and so there exist

such that, for all

\left\langle f,k_{0}\right\rangle=f(0)
and
\left\langle f,s_{0}\right\rangle=f^{\prime}(0)
.
Thus
E_{\chi}^{\perp}=\operatorname{span}\{k_{0},s_{0}\}
,

Let us show that
\mathcal{A}_{\chi}=\mathbb{C}k_{0}+\mathbb{C}s_{0}
. By Proposition
5.3
, the ambiguous vectors for
\chi
are the members of
E_{\chi}^{\perp}\cap\ker X_{\chi}^{*}
. We need to show that every vector

belongs to

.
Note that, for every

\displaystyle\left\langle X_{\chi}^{*}(c_{1}k_{0}+c_{2}s_{0}),f\right\rangle=\left\langle c_{1}k_{0}+c_{2}s_{0},X_{\chi}f\right\rangle=c_{1}\overline{(\chi f)(0)}+c_{2}\overline{(\chi f)^{\prime}(0)}=0.

Therefore the ambiguous space

\square

6.
Definable vectors and polar vectors

In this section we shall study definable vectors, polar vectors, and, more generally, the polar space of a pseudomultiplier
\varphi
.
By way of a preliminary observation relevant to the notion of a definable vector, we note that, if
\varphi
sees
v
then
\varphi
is constant on the coset
v+\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier, let
v\in\mathcal{H}
, and
let
a\in\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
. If
\varphi
sees
v
with value

c
, then
\varphi
sees
v+a
with value
c
. In particular,
if
\varphi
sees
v
, then
\varphi
sees
v+a
.

Proof.

Suppose
\varphi
sees
v
with value

c
. Thus, by Lemma
5.1
, there exists
u\in E^{\perp}_{\varphi}
such that

X^{*}_{\varphi}v=\overline{c}v+u.

Noting that
X^{*}_{\varphi}a=0
, we see that

\displaystyle X^{*}_{\varphi}(v+a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{c}v+u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{c}(v+a)+u-\overline{c}a.

Since
\overline{c}a\in E^{\perp}_{\varphi}
, so also

u-\overline{c}a\in E^{\perp}_{\varphi}
. Hence by Lemma

5.1
,
\varphi
sees
v+a
with value
c
.
\square

If
\varphi
is a pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}
then
\varphi
naturally extends to
have values on its visible vectors. These values are
arbitrary on the ambiguous vectors but unique on the
unambiguous visible vectors. We now know
from Lemma
6.1
, that
\varphi
is constant on each
coset
v+\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
. Since, for any visible vector
v
, the
coset
v+\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
contains a unique visible
vector that is orthogonal to
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
, to wit
P_{{\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}^{\perp}}}v
,
the following definition is reasonable.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier. We say a vector

d\in\mathcal{H}
is
definable for

\varphi
if
d\not=0,\varphi
sees
d
, and
d\perp\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
. We let

\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
denote the set of vectors that are
definable for

\varphi
.

By Lemma
5.1
(iii),
if
\varphi
sees
v
with two distinct values then
v
is an ambiguous vector for
\varphi
.
Therefore, if
d
is definable for
\varphi
then there
exists a
unique

c\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\varphi
sees

d
with value
c
.
We refer to this
c
as the
value of

\varphi
at
d
and denote it by
\varphi(d)
.

We remark that the exclusion of the vector
0
from being
definable has the following happy consequences. No
vector is both definable and ambiguous, and, by Lemma
6.1
, every vector
in
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}+\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
is an unambiguously
seen vector.

Remark 6.3
.

We proved in Example
2.16

that, for the pseudomultiplier
\varphi(z)=1/z
on
H^{2}
, the set of definable vectors

\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
comprises the non-zero scalar multiples of the
kernels
k_{\lambda}
, for
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
. The vector
k_{0}
is a limit of the definable vectors
k_{(1/n)}
for
\varphi

k_{0}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}k_{(1/n)}.

However,
k_{0}
is not itself definable because, for all
n\geq 1
,
\varphi(k_{1/n})=n
, and so

\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi(k_{1/n})=\infty.

Thus
k_{0}
is a polar vector for
\varphi
.

\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
is both a
subspace and a topologically closed set in
\mathcal{H}
. Not so
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
.
Quite apart from the fact that
0
is not in

\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
, in
the example above
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
is very far from being a
subspace but not far from being a closed set in
\mathcal{H}
.
For example, in Example
2.16
one need only adjoin
\mathbb{C}k_{0}
to
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
to obtain a closed set.

\square

We shall repeatedly have a use for the following minor technical observation.

Lemma 6.4
.

If
d
is a definable vector of a pseudomultiplier
\varphi
then
P_{E_{\varphi}}d\neq 0
.

Proof.

Suppose
P_{E_{\varphi}}d=0
, and so

d\in E_{\varphi}^{\perp}
. By Lemma
5.1
, since
d
is visible for
\varphi
with value
\varphi(d)
,

X_{\varphi}^{*}d=\overline{\varphi(d)}P_{E_{\varphi}}d=0,

and thus

. Hence, by Proposition

,

. However, by the definition of definable vectors,

and

, which is a contradiction. Hence

.

\square

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier. Then
\varphi
is a
continuous function on
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
with respect to the metric of
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
, with the topology
inherited from
\mathcal{H}
, is a metric space, it suffices to
prove sequential continuity. Let
d_{n}\to d
in
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
. By Lemma

,

\displaystyle X^{*}_{\varphi}d_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\varphi(d_{n})}\ P_{E_{\varphi}}d_{n},

\displaystyle X^{*}_{\varphi}d

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\varphi(d)}\ P_{E_{\varphi}}d.

By Lemma

,

. On letting

.
\square

Recall that we introduced the

of a pseudomultiplier in Definition

:
for a pseudomultiplier
\varphi
of

and for

, we say that

and
there exists a sequence

such that
d_{n}\to p
(with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
) and

as

.

.

Example 6.6
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=H^{2}
, the classical Hardy space on

\mathbb{D}
, let
n\geq 2
, and define
\varphi
on
\mathbb{D}
by

\varphi(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{\lambda^{n}}&\qquad\text{ if }\lambda\not=0\\
\\
1&\qquad\text{ if }\lambda=0.\end{array}\right.

Here

E_{\varphi}=\{f\in H^{2}:f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=\dots=f^{(n)}(0)=0\}=z^{n+1}H^{2}.

Let us describe the space of ambiguous vectors
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
for
\varphi
.
By Proposition
5.3
,

\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}=E_{\varphi}^{\perp}\cap\ker X_{\varphi}^{*}.

The operator
X_{\varphi}:z^{n+1}H^{2}\to H^{2}
is given by
X_{\varphi}=(S^{*})^{n}P_{z^{n+1}H^{2}}^{*}

where
S
denotes the forward shift operator on
H^{2}
and
P_{z^{n+1}H^{2}}:H^{2}\to z^{n+1}H^{2}
is the orthogonal projection operator (so that
P_{z^{n+1}H^{2}}^{*}
is the injection operator
z^{n+1}H^{2}\to H^{2}
). Thus
X_{\varphi}^{*}=P_{z^{n+1}H^{2}}S^{n}
and so, for
f\in H^{2}
,

\displaystyle X_{\varphi}^{*}f=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle S^{n}f\in H^{2}\ominus z^{n+1}H^{2}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle z^{n}f\in

span
{1,z, …, z^n}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle f\text{ is constant.}

Hence, the space of ambiguous vectors for
\varphi
is

\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}=E_{\varphi}^{\perp}\cap\ker X_{\varphi}^{*}=(H^{2}\ominus z^{n+1}H^{2})\cap\mathbb{C}\mathbf{1}=\mathbb{C}\mathbf{1}.

Let us describe the set of definable vectors
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
of
\varphi
. By Lemma
5.1
, for

f\in H^{2}
,
\varphi
sees
f
with value
c
if and only if there
exists
u\in E^{\perp}_{\varphi}
such that

X^{*}_{\varphi}f=\overline{c}f+u,

(6.2)

equivalently, if and only if there exist
a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

X_{\varphi}^{*}f=\bar{c}f+a_{0}+a_{1}z+\dots+a_{n}z^{n},

that is, if and only if there exist
a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

P_{z^{n+1}H^{2}}z^{n}f-\bar{c}f=a_{0}+a_{1}z+\dots+a_{n}z^{n}.

(6.3)

Now
P_{z^{n+1}H^{2}}z^{n}f=z^{n}(f-f(0))
, and so equation (
6.3
) is equivalent to

z^{n}(f-f(0))-\bar{c}f=a_{0}+a_{1}z+\dots+a_{n}z^{n}.

(6.4)

Substitution of
z=0
shows that equation (
6.4
) implies that
a_{0}=-\bar{c}f(0)
,
and so equation (
6.4
) can be written

(z^{n}-\bar{c})(f-f(0))=a_{1}z+\dots+a_{n}z^{n}.

Thus
\varphi
sees
f
with value
c
if and only if there exist
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

f=f(0)+\frac{a_{1}z+\dots+a_{n}z^{n}}{z^{n}-\bar{c}}\in H^{2}.

The rational function on the right hand side is in
H^{2}
if and only if
|c|>1
, and so the visible vectors for
\varphi
consist of the rational functions of the form

f(z)=a_{0}+\frac{a_{1}z+\dots+a_{n}z^{n}}{z^{n}-\bar{c}}\text{ where }|c|>1\text{ and }a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{C},

or alternatively, if we define

for
j=1,2,\dots,n
, of the functions

f(z)=a_{0}+\frac{b_{1}z+b_{2}z^{2}+\dots+b_{n}z^{n}}{1-\bar{c}^{-1}z^{n}}\text{ where }|c|>1\text{ and }a_{0},b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n}\in\mathbb{C}.

(6.5)

We showed above that
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
consists of the constant functions, and so the visible function
f
in equation (
6.5
) is definable if and only if
f\neq 0
but
f(0)=0
.
Thus

\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}=\left\{f(z)=\frac{b_{1}z+b_{2}z^{2}+\dots+b_{n}z^{n}}{1-\bar{c}^{-1}z^{n}}:|c|>1\text{ and }b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n}\in\mathbb{C}\text{ are not all zero}\right\}.

(6.6)

Now we can describe the polar space
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
for
\varphi
. For every non-zero polar vector

p
there exists a convergent sequence of definable vectors

\left\{d_{k}=\frac{b_{1k}z+b_{2k}z^{2}+\dots+b_{nk}z^{n}}{1-\bar{c_{k}}^{-1}z^{n}}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\text{ in }\mathbf{D}_{\varphi},

where
b_{1k},b_{2k},\dots,b_{nk}\in\mathbb{C}
, not all zero, are such that
d_{k}\to p
and

\varphi(d_{k})=c_{k}\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
.
The non-zero limits of such convergent sequences
\{d_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
H^{2}
coincide with

\operatorname{span}\{z,z^{2},\dots,z^{n}\}\setminus\{0\}
, so that
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}=\operatorname{span}\{z,z^{2},\dots,z^{n}\}
.
We may observe that

\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}\oplus\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}=\mathbb{C}\mathbf{1}\oplus\operatorname{span}\{z,z^{2},\dots,z^{n}\}=H^{2}\ominus z^{n+1}H^{2}=E_{\varphi}^{\perp}.

\square

Remark 6.7
.

The combination of Examples

The following proposition shows how the notion of a pseudopole of a pseudomultiplier, as introduced in
[
4
]
(see Remark
2.18
above), relates to polar vectors.
In this paper by the closure of
D_{\varphi}
we mean the closure in
\Omega
with respect to the natural metric on
\Omega
, induced by the norm on
\mathcal{H}
and the identification of a point of
\Omega
with its reproducing kernel in
\mathcal{H}
.

Proposition 6.8
.

If
\alpha
is a pseudopole of a pseudomultiplier
\varphi
and
\alpha
is in the closure of
D_{\varphi}
in
\Omega
then
P_{\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}^{\perp}}k_{\alpha}
is a polar vector of
\varphi
.

Proof.

By assumption,
\alpha
is in the closure of
D_{\varphi}
in
\Omega
, and so we can choose a sequence
(\alpha_{n})
in
D_{\varphi}
such that
\alpha_{n}\to\alpha
as
n\to\infty
.
Since
\alpha
is a pseudopole of
\varphi
there exists
h\in E_{\varphi}
and
g\in\mathcal{H}
such that
g=X_{\varphi}h
,
h(\alpha)=0
and
g(\alpha)\neq 0
. For
n\geq 1
let

d_{n}=P_{\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}^{\perp}}k_{\alpha_{n}}=k_{\alpha_{n}}-P_{\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}}k_{\alpha_{n}}.

Since
\varphi
sees
k_{\alpha_{n}}
with value
\varphi(\alpha_{n})
, it follows from Lemma
6.1
that
\varphi
sees
d_{n}
with value
\varphi(\alpha_{n})
. Since
d_{n}\perp\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
,
d_{n}
is a definable vector for
\varphi
and
\varphi(d_{n})=\varphi(\alpha_{n})
. Moreover

0\neq g(\alpha)=\left\langle g,k_{\alpha}\right\rangle=\lim_{n}\left\langle g,k_{\alpha_{n}}\right\rangle,

while, for all
\lambda\in D_{\varphi}
,

\left\langle g,k_{\lambda}\right\rangle=g(\lambda)=\varphi(\lambda)h(\lambda).

Hence

\lim_{n}\left\langle g,k_{\alpha_{n}}\right\rangle=\lim_{n}\varphi(\alpha_{n})h(\alpha_{n})=g(\alpha)\neq 0.

Since also

and therefore

as

.

The nature of polar vectors is much more rigid than one might at
first expect. This is brought out in the following
proposition, which gives a clean relationship between
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
and the set
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
of polar vectors of
\varphi
.

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier and let
p\in\mathcal{H}

with
p\not=0
. The following are equivalent.

(i)

p
is a polar vector for
\varphi
.

(ii)

p\in\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}^{-}
and for every
neighborhood
U
of
p,\varphi
is unbounded on
U\cap\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
.

(iii)

p\in\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}^{-}\backslash\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
.

(iv)

p\in\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}^{-}
and

\lim\limits_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textstyle{d\to p}}}{{d\in\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}}}}\ \varphi(d)=\infty
.

Proof.

It is clear that (iv)
\Rightarrow
(i)

\Rightarrow
(ii). Suppose that (ii) holds but (iii) is false,
so that
p\in\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
.
By Lemma
6.4
,
P_{E_{\varphi}}d\neq 0
. We may therefore assume

\|P_{E_{\varphi}}p\|=1
. Let
U
be a neighborhood of
p
in

\mathcal{H}
such that, for all
v\in U
,

\|(X^{*}_{\varphi}-\overline{\varphi(p)}\ P_{E_{\varphi}})v\|<1

(6.7)

and

\|P_{E_{\varphi}}v\|>\frac{1}{2}.

Consider
v\in\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}\cap U
. Since
v
is
definable,

X^{*}_{\varphi}v=\overline{\varphi(v)}\ P_{E_{\varphi}}v.

Substituting in (
6.7
) we have

\|(\overline{\varphi(v)}-\overline{\varphi(p)})\ P_{E_{\varphi}}v\|<1

and so

|\varphi(v)-\varphi(p)|<2.

Thus
\varphi
is bounded on
U\cap\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
,
contradicting (ii). Thus (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii).

Now suppose (iii) holds but (iv) is false:
p\in\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}^{-}\smallsetminus\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
but

\varphi(d)\not\rightarrow\infty
as
d\to p
in
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
. Then there exists a sequence
(d_{n})
in
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
and a positive constant

such that

and
|\varphi(d_{n})|\leq C
for all

. By Lemma
5.1
,
for all

,

X^{*}_{\varphi}d_{n}=\varphi\overline{(d_{n})}\ P_{E_{\varphi}}d_{n}.

Passing to a subsequence if necessary, we can assume

\varphi(d_{n})\to z\in\mathbb{C}
. Taking limits in the
foregoing equation we find

X^{*}_{\varphi}p=\overline{z}P_{E_{\varphi}}p.

Thus
\varphi
sees
p
with value

. Since each

, we have also

. By assumption

, and so

\Rightarrow
(iv).

\square

The definable vectors provide an alternative viewpoint on the polar space.

Theorem 6.11
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier of a Hilbert function space

on a set

. Suppose

. Then

(i)

\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
is non-empty.

(ii)

\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}=\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}^{-}\smallsetminus\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
.

Proof.

(i). Let us show that
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}=\emptyset
implies that
\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional.

For any point
\alpha\in D_{\varphi}
, let
d_{\alpha}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}P_{\mathcal{A}^{\perp}_{\varphi}}k_{\alpha}
.
Let us show that
d_{\alpha}
is definable if and only if
\alpha
is not ambiguous. Suppose that
\alpha
is ambiguous. Then by Theorem
4.14
,
k_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
, and so
d_{\alpha}=0
,
and therefore
d_{\alpha}
is not definable.
Suppose now that
\alpha
is not ambiguous.
Then
\varphi
sees
k_{\alpha}
with the unique value
\varphi(\alpha)
. By definition

.
By Lemma
6.1
,
\varphi
sees
d_{\alpha}
with the unique value

. Thus

is definable.

By assumption,

, and so, for every
\alpha\in D_{\varphi}
,

is not definable, that is, every

contains

.

(ii) Since

7.
The singular space

Pseudomultipliers fail to act as operators on all of
\mathcal{H}
for one of two reasons. Either they have an
ambiguous point (such as
\sqrt{t}
acting on Sobolev
space) or they have a polar vector (such as
\frac{1}{z}
acting on

H^{2}
). In this section we prove a theorem to the effect that that every singularity
of a pseudomultiplier can be written in a unique way as the sum of a polar vector
and an ambiguous vector. As a corollary of our basic theorem one
obtains the interesting fact that if one adjoins the zero
vector to the set of polar vectors of a pseudomultiplier then one
obtains a finite-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}
.
Furthermore, this subspace is precisely the set of
vectors that can be approximated by definable vectors
but that are not themselves definable.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
\varphi
be a
pseudomultiplier. We define the
order of
\varphi

to be
\dim E^{\perp}_{\varphi}
. If the order of
\varphi
is

m
, then we say that
\varphi
is an
m
-pseudomultiplier
.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier.
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
is a closed
subspace of
\mathcal{H}
and

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\oplus\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}.

Proof.

First recall that, by Proposition
5.3
,

\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}=E^{\perp}_{\varphi}\cap\ker X^{*}_{\varphi}
is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}=E^{\perp}_{\varphi}
. Hence we need to show that

\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}\ominus\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}.

(7.1)

We first show that
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}\ominus\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
. Consider
p\in\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
. We can suppose
p\not=0
, so that
p
is a
polar vector for

\varphi
. Choose
\{d_{n}\}\subseteq\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
with

d_{n}\to p
and
\varphi(d_{n})\to\infty
.
We claim that
p\in\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}
. By Lemma
5.1
,

X^{*}_{\varphi}d_{n}=\overline{\varphi(d_{n})}\big(d_{n}-P_{\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}}d_{n}\big).

(7.2)

Since
X^{*}_{\varphi}d_{n}\to X^{*}_{\varphi}p
and

\varphi(d_{n})\to\infty
we conclude from equation (
7.2
) that

d_{n}-P_{\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}}d_{n}\to 0
. Hence
p-P_{\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}}p=0
, that is,
p\in\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}
. Next, since

d_{n}\perp\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
, so also
p=\lim d_{n}\perp\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
. Thus
p\in\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}^{\perp}=\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}\ominus\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
, and we have proved that
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}\ominus\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
.

We now show that
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}\ominus\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
. Consider
v\in\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}\ominus\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
. If
v=0
, then obviously
v\in\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
. Assume that
v\not=0
. We claim that
v
is a
polar vector for
\varphi
. Choose a sequence
\{c_{n}\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
with

c_{n}\to\infty
and
\overline{c}_{n}\not\in\sigma(X^{*}_{\varphi})
for all
n
. Define
v_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
by
the formula

v_{n}=\overline{c}_{n}\left(\overline{c}_{n}I-X^{*}_{\varphi}\right)^{-1}v.

(7.3)

We claim that
\varphi
sees
v_{n}
with value
c_{n}
. We have

\displaystyle X^{*}_{\varphi}v_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X^{*}_{\varphi}\overline{c}_{n}\left(\overline{c}_{n}I-X^{*}_{\varphi}\right)^{-1}v

(7.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{c}_{n}\left(\left(X^{*}_{\varphi}-\overline{c}_{n}I\right)\left(\overline{c}_{n}I-X^{*}_{\varphi}\right)^{-1}v+\overline{c}_{n}\left(\overline{c}_{n}I-X^{*}_{\varphi}\right)^{-1}v\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{c}_{n}v_{n}+\overline{c}_{n}v

and
v\in\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}
. Hence by Lemma
5.1
,

\varphi
sees
v_{n}
with value
c_{n}
.
Now define
w_{n}
by

w_{n}=P_{\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}^{\perp}}v_{n}=v_{n}-P_{\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}}v_{n}.

Since
\varphi
sees
v_{n}
with value
c_{n}
, it follows from Lemma
6.1
that
\varphi
sees
w_{n}
with value
c_{n}
, and so
w_{n}
is a definable vector for
\varphi
. Note that

w_{n}\perp\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
by definition of
w_{n}
.
Furthermore, from equation (
7.3
) we see that
v_{n}\to v
as
n\to\infty
.
By assumption
v\in\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}\ominus\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
, and so
v\perp\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
. Thus

w_{n}\to P_{\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}^{\perp}}v=v
, and for sufficiently large
n,w_{n}\not=0
.
Consequently, for sufficiently large
n,w_{n}\in\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}
and

\varphi(w_{n})=c_{n}
. Since
w_{n}\to v
and
c_{n}\to\infty

we conclude that
v
is a polar vector. Thus
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}\ominus\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
.
Therefore
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
is a closed
subspace of
\mathcal{H}
and

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\oplus\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}.

\square

Theorem
7.2
gives a concrete expression of the polar
space in terms of the singular and ambiguous spaces.

Example 7.3
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=H^{2}
, the classical Hardy space on

. Consider the function

\varphi(z)=\frac{1}{z(z-{\tfrac{1}{2}})}

2

The operator

Let us describe the space of ambiguous vectors
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
for
\varphi

By Proposition
5.3
,

\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}=E_{\varphi}^{\perp}\cap\ker X_{\varphi}^{*}.

Consider any

X_{\varphi}^{*}f=0

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle X_{\varphi}^{*}(c_{1}k_{0}+c_{2}k_{{\tfrac{1}{2}}}),z(z-{\tfrac{1}{2}})g\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle c_{1}k_{0},X_{\varphi}z(z-{\tfrac{1}{2}})g\right\rangle+\left\langle c_{2}k_{{\tfrac{1}{2}}},X_{\varphi}z(z-{\tfrac{1}{2}})g\right\rangle

For

.

of

. By Lemma

, for

Since
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}=\{0\}

,

.

of

for all

for

.

.

8.
Visible subspaces of a pseudomultiplier

The notion of a pseudomultiplier seeing a vector has proved
fruitful. We shall now take a slightly different approach to
that notion which will lead to a generalization, the idea
of a pseudomultiplier
seeing a subspace
.

An obvious way to generalize Definition
2.7
, of
\varphi
seeing a vector
v
with value
c
, would be
to formalize the situation where knowledge of
n

moments of
f,\{\left\langle f,v_{1}\right\rangle,\ \left\langle f,v_{2}\right\rangle,\dots,\left\langle f,v_{n}\right\rangle\}
,
implies that the corresponding moments of
X_{\varphi}f,\{\left\langle X_{\varphi}f,v_{1}\right\rangle,\dots,\left\langle X_{\varphi}f,v_{n}\right\rangle\}
, are
determined. Knowing the moments
\{\left\langle f,v_{1}\right\rangle,\dots,\left\langle f,v_{n}\right\rangle\}
is equivalent to knowing
P_{\mathcal{V}}f
where

P_{\mathcal{V}}
denotes orthogonal projection on the space
\mathcal{V}=\operatorname{span}\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
. Further, since

X_{\varphi}
is linear, the only way the moments of

X_{\varphi}f
can be determined by the moments of
f
is
linearly. The following definition is therefore a natural
generalization of Definition
2.7
.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier and let
\mathcal{V}

be a closed subspace of
\mathcal{H}
. We say that
\varphi

sees

\mathcal{V}
if there is a bounded linear operator

C:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
such that, for all
f\in E_{\varphi}
,

P_{\mathcal{V}}X_{\varphi}f=CP_{\mathcal{V}}f,

(8.1)

where
P_{\mathcal{V}}
is the orthogonal projection of
\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathcal{V}
.
We say that
\mathcal{V}
is a
regular space for

\varphi

if
\varphi
sees
\mathcal{V}
and there is a
unique

C

such that equation (
8.1
) holds for all
f\in E_{\varphi}
; when
this is so we denote
C
by
\varphi(\mathcal{V})
and call it
the
value of

\varphi
at
\mathcal{V}
. Alternatively we
say that
\varphi

sees

\mathcal{V}

with value

C
.

Proposition 8.2
.

Let
\varphi
be a
multiplier of
\mathcal{H}
and let
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
be a closed
subspace. The following two statements are equivalent.

(1)

\varphi
sees
\mathcal{V}

(2)

X^{*}_{\varphi}\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, that is,

\mathcal{V}
is a closed invariant subspace for
X^{*}_{\varphi}
.

Moreover, every closed invariant subspace for
X^{*}_{\varphi}
is regular for
\varphi

with value
C=P_{\mathcal{V}}X_{\varphi}|_{\mathcal{V}}
.

Proof.

Since
\varphi
is a multiplier of
\mathcal{H}
,
E_{\varphi}=\mathcal{H}
.

(1)
\Longrightarrow
(2).
Let
\varphi
see
\mathcal{V}
with value
C:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
. By definition
C
is bounded and

P_{\mathcal{V}}X_{\varphi}=CP_{\mathcal{V}}
on
\mathcal{H}
. Thus
X_{\varphi}^{*}P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*}=P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*}C^{*}
.
Hence
X_{\varphi}^{*}\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, as claimed.

(2)
\Longrightarrow
(1).
Suppose that
X_{\varphi}^{*}\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
. We must find a bounded linear operator
C:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
such that
P_{\mathcal{V}}X_{\varphi}=CP_{\mathcal{V}}
on
\mathcal{H}
.
Let
C=P_{\mathcal{V}}X_{\varphi}|_{\mathcal{V}}
. We need to check that

P_{\mathcal{V}}X_{\varphi}f=P_{\mathcal{V}}X_{\varphi}P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*}P_{\mathcal{V}}f

for all
f\in\mathcal{H}
, that is, that, for all
f\in\mathcal{H}
,

X_{\varphi}(1-P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*}P_{\mathcal{V}})f\in\mathcal{V}^{\perp}.

(8.2)

The last equation is equivalent to saying that, for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
and
f\in\mathcal{H}
,

\left\langle(1-P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*}P_{\mathcal{V}})f,X_{\varphi}^{*}v\right\rangle=0.

(8.3)

Since, by assumption
X_{\varphi}^{*}v\in\mathcal{V}
and since
1-P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*}P_{\mathcal{V}}
is the orthogonal projection on
\mathcal{V}^{\perp}
, equation (
8.3
) is true.
Hence
P_{\mathcal{V}}X_{\varphi}=CP_{\mathcal{V}}
on
\mathcal{H}
, as required.

\square

Remark 8.3
.

By Proposition
3.9
, for any multiplier
\varphi
of a Hilbert function space
\mathcal{H}
, the vectors which are visible to
\varphi
are precisely the eigenvectors of
X_{\varphi}^{*}
. Proposition
8.2
shows that a closed subspace
\mathcal{V}
of
\mathcal{H}
is visible to
\varphi
if and only if
\mathcal{V}
is an invariant subspace for
X_{\varphi}^{*}
. From these facts it is apparent that even if
\varphi
sees a closed subspace
\mathcal{V}
of
\mathcal{H}
, it does not follow that
\varphi
sees the individual vectors in
\mathcal{V}
.

For example, for
\varphi\in H^{\infty}
,
\varphi
is a multiplier of
H^{2}
. The only vectors in
H^{2}
seen by

\varphi
are the eigenvectors of
X^{*}_{\varphi}
. In
particular, if
\varphi(z)=a_{0}+a_{1}z
for some
a_{0},a_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
,
a_{1}\neq 0
, then
X_{\varphi}^{*}
is the Toeplitz operator
T_{\bar{\varphi}}
, and if

v(z)=z,\text{ for }z\in\mathbb{T}
then, for
z\in\mathbb{T}
,

X_{\varphi}^{*}v(z)=P_{+}\bar{\varphi}(z)z=P_{+}((\overline{a_{0}+a_{1}z})z)=P_{+}(\overline{a_{0}}z+\overline{a_{1}}|z|^{2})=\overline{a_{0}}z+\overline{a_{1}}=\overline{a_{0}}v+\overline{a_{1}},

which is not a scalar multiple of
v
. Thus
v
is not an eigenvector of
X_{\varphi}^{*}
,
so that

\varphi
does not see the vector
v
. However,
\mathcal{V}=\operatorname{span}\{1,v\}
is an invariant subspace for
X_{\varphi}^{*}
, since
X_{\varphi}^{*}1=\overline{a_{0}},
and so

X_{\varphi}^{*}\operatorname{span}\{1,v\}=\operatorname{span}\{1,v\}
. Therefore
\varphi
does see the space
\mathcal{V}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\operatorname{span}\{1,v\}
.
\square

Example 8.4
.

Consider the multiplier
\varphi(z)=z
on
H^{2}
. By Proposition

,
the closed subspaces
\mathcal{V}
of
H^{2}
visible to
\varphi
are precisely the closed invariant subspaces for

. Here

is the unilateral shift

on

. By Beurling’s Theorem, the closed invariant subspaces for

are the spaces of the form

, where

is an inner function.

Example 8.5
.

Let
\varphi\in H^{\infty}
, so that
\varphi
is a
multiplier of
H^{2}
. Let

\alpha\in\mathbb{D}
and let

denote the

, that is,

\left\langle f,k^{(r)}_{\alpha}\right\rangle\ =\ f^{(r)}(\alpha)

for all

. Then
\varphi
sees
\mathcal{V}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\operatorname{span}\{k_{\alpha},k^{(1)}_{\alpha},\dots,k^{(n)}_{\alpha}\}
for any

. This is because

, and Leibniz’ rule shows that

.
By Proposition

, there is a

,

.

Lemma 8.6
.

Let
\varphi
be a
pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}
and let
\mathcal{V}
be a closed subspace of
\mathcal{H}
.
If
\varphi
sees
\mathcal{V}
then

X^{*}_{\varphi}\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}+E^{\perp}_{\varphi}
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi
see
\mathcal{V}
with value
C:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
. By definition
C
is bounded and

P_{\mathcal{V}}X_{\varphi}=CP_{\mathcal{V}}
on
E_{\varphi}
. For all
v\in\mathcal{V}
and
f\in E_{\varphi}
we have

\left\langle X_{\varphi}f,v\right\rangle=\left\langle P_{\mathcal{V}}X_{\varphi}f,v\right\rangle=\left\langle CP_{\mathcal{V}}f,v\right\rangle=\left\langle f,C^{*}v\right\rangle,

and so

\left\langle f,(X_{\varphi}^{*}-C^{*})v\right\rangle=0\text{ for all }f\in E_{\varphi}.

That is,

(X_{\varphi}^{*}-C^{*})v\in E_{\varphi}^{\perp}\text{ for all }v\in\mathcal{V}.

Hence
X_{\varphi}^{*}\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}+E_{\varphi}^{\perp}
, as claimed.

\square

Lemma 8.7
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a closed subspace of a Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}
and let
E
be a closed finite-codimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}
. The orthogonal projection of
E
onto
\mathcal{V}
is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Let
P_{\mathcal{V}}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{V}
be the operation of orthogonal projection, so that
P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{H}
is the inclusion operator and
P
, defined to be
P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*}P_{\mathcal{V}}
, is the Hermitian operator in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
with range
\mathcal{V}
.
Let
M=E^{\perp}+PE^{\perp}
.
M
is a finite-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}
and is invariant under
P
. Therefore, since
P
is Hermitian,
M
is a reducing subspace for
P
, and the restrictions
P|M
and
P|M^{\perp}
are also Hermitian projections.

Since
E^{\perp}\subseteq M,M^{\perp}\subseteq E
. Let
M_{0}=E\cap M
. We have
E=M^{\perp}+\tilde{M}
, where
\tilde{M}
is the orthogonal complement of
M^{\perp}
in
E
, so that
\tilde{M}=\{x\in E:x\perp M^{\perp}\}=E\cap M=M_{0}
. Hence
E=M_{0}+M^{\perp}
, and therefore
PE=PM_{0}+PM^{\perp}
. Now
PM^{\perp}
is the range of
P|M^{\perp}
, which is a Hermitian projection, and therefore
PM^{\perp}
is closed in
\mathcal{H}
.
PM_{0}
, on the other hand, has finite dimension, and so, by
[
10
, Proposition III.4.3]
,
PE=PM_{0}+PM^{\perp}
is a closed set in
\mathcal{H}

as the sum of the closed subspace
PM^{\perp}
of
\mathcal{H}
and the finite-dimensional subspace
PM_{0}
of
\mathcal{H}
.

\square

The referee pointed out that Lemma
8.7
is a special case of the general result that the range of a bounded linear
operator is closed if it is finite co-dimensional, a fact well known to experts in the theory of Fredholm operators. Indeed, apply this fact to the operator
P_{\mathcal{V}}:E\to\mathcal{V}
.

The following proposition generalizes Proposition
8.2
to a pseudomultiplier.

Proposition 8.8
.

Let
\varphi
be a
pseudomultiplier of
\mathcal{H}
and let
\mathcal{V}
be a closed subspace of
\mathcal{H}
.

(i)

\varphi
sees
\mathcal{V}
if and only if

X^{*}_{\varphi}\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}+E^{\perp}_{\varphi}
.

(ii)

\mathcal{V}
is regular if and only if
X_{\varphi}^{*}\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}+E_{\varphi}^{\perp}
and
\mathcal{V}\cap E^{\perp}_{\varphi}=\{0\}
.

Proof.

(i) By Lemma
8.6
,
\varphi
sees
\mathcal{V}
implies that

X^{*}_{\varphi}\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}+E^{\perp}_{\varphi}
.

Conversely, suppose that
X_{\varphi}^{*}\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}+E_{\varphi}^{\perp}
.
By Lemma
8.7
, the projection
P_{\mathcal{V}}E_{\varphi}
of
E_{\varphi}
on
\mathcal{V}
is closed in
\mathcal{V}
.
We must find a bounded linear operator
C:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
such that
P_{\mathcal{V}}X_{\varphi}=CP_{\mathcal{V}}
on
E_{\varphi}
, equivalently, we have to show that there exists
a bounded linear operator
C^{*}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
such that

P_{E_{\varphi}}X_{\varphi}^{*}P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*}=P_{E_{\varphi}}P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*}C^{*}.

Since
X^{*}_{\varphi}\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}+E^{\perp}_{\varphi}
, we obtain

P_{E_{\varphi}}X_{\varphi}^{*}P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*}(\mathcal{V})\subseteq P_{E_{\varphi}}(\mathcal{V}+E^{\perp}_{\varphi})=P_{E_{\varphi}}P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*}(\mathcal{V}).

Hence, for the bounded linear operators

P_{E_{\varphi}}X_{\varphi}^{*}P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*}:\mathcal{V}\to E_{\varphi}\;\text{and}\;P_{E_{\varphi}}P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*}*:\mathcal{V}\to E_{\varphi},

the inclusion
\text{ran}(P_{E_{\varphi}}X_{\varphi}^{*}P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*})\subseteq\text{ran}(P_{E_{\varphi}}P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*})
holds.
By the generalized Douglas factorization lemma
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
(
[
12
]
), there exists a bounded linear operator
C^{*}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
such that
P_{E_{\varphi}}X_{\varphi}^{*}P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*}=P_{E_{\varphi}}P_{\mathcal{V}}^{*}C^{*}.

Therefore there exists a bounded linear operator
C:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
such that

CP_{\mathcal{V}}f=P_{\mathcal{V}}X_{\varphi}f
for all
f\in E_{\varphi}
, and so
\varphi
sees
\mathcal{V}
, as claimed.

(ii) Recall that
\mathcal{V}
is regular for
\varphi
if
\varphi
sees
\mathcal{V}
with the
unique
value

C:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
, which is a bounded linear operator such that

CP_{\mathcal{V}}f=P_{\mathcal{V}}X_{\varphi}f
for all
f\in E_{\varphi}
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{V}
is regular for
\varphi
. By Part (i)
X_{\varphi}^{*}\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}+E_{\varphi}^{\perp}
. We need to show that
\mathcal{V}\cap E^{\perp}_{\varphi}=\{0\}
.

By Lemma
8.7
, the projection
P_{\mathcal{V}}E_{\varphi}
of
E_{\varphi}
on
\mathcal{V}
is closed in
\mathcal{V}
. Consider the unique
bounded linear operator
C:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
such that

CP_{\mathcal{V}}f=P_{\mathcal{V}}X_{\varphi}f
for all
f\in E_{\varphi}
. Let
C_{0}:P_{\mathcal{V}}E_{\varphi}\to\mathcal{V}
be the restriction of
C
to the closed subspace
P_{\mathcal{V}}E_{\varphi}
of
\mathcal{V}
. Therefore
C_{0}
is a bounded operator on
P_{\mathcal{V}}E_{\varphi}
.

For any bounded linear operator
C
on
\mathcal{V}
,
\varphi
sees
\mathcal{V}
with value
C
if and only if
C
is an extension of the operator
C_{0}:P_{\mathcal{V}}E_{\varphi}\to\mathcal{V}
. Since
C_{0}
is a bounded operator on the closed subspace
P_{\mathcal{V}}E_{\varphi}
of
\mathcal{V}
,
C_{0}
has a unique extension to
\mathcal{V}
if and only if
P_{\mathcal{V}}E_{\varphi}=\mathcal{V}
. We assert that
P_{\mathcal{V}}E_{\varphi}=\mathcal{V}
if and only if
\mathcal{V}\cap E_{\varphi}^{\perp}=\{0\}
.
Indeed, suppose that
P_{\mathcal{V}}E_{\varphi}=\mathcal{V}
but
\mathcal{V}\cap E_{\varphi}^{\perp}\neq\{0\}
.
Then there exists
v\in\mathcal{V}\cap E_{\varphi}^{\perp}
such that
v\neq 0
. By assumption there exists
f\in E_{\varphi}
such that
P_{\mathcal{V}}f=v
. Then

\|v\|^{2}=\left\langle v,v\right\rangle=\left\langle v,P_{\mathcal{V}}f\right\rangle=\left\langle v,f\right\rangle=0,

and so
v=0
, a contradiction. Thus
\mathcal{V}\cap E_{\varphi}^{\perp}=\{0\}
.

Conversely, suppose
\mathcal{V}\cap E_{\varphi}^{\perp}=\{0\}
but
\mathcal{V}\ominus P_{\mathcal{V}}E_{\varphi}\neq\{0\}
. Pick any non-zero
v\in\mathcal{V}\ominus P_{\mathcal{V}}E_{\varphi}
.
For every
f\in E_{\varphi}
we have
0=\left\langle v,P_{\mathcal{V}}f\right\rangle=\left\langle v,f\right\rangle
, and so
v\in\mathcal{V}\cap E_{\varphi}^{\perp}
, which contradicts the hypothesis
\mathcal{V}\cap E_{\varphi}^{\perp}=\{0\}
.
Thus
P_{\mathcal{V}}E_{\varphi}=\mathcal{V}
if and only if
\mathcal{V}\cap E_{\varphi}^{\perp}=\{0\}
, as asserted, and so
\mathcal{V}
is regular for
\varphi
if and only if
X_{\varphi}^{*}\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}+E_{\varphi}^{\perp}
and
\mathcal{V}\cap E_{\varphi}^{\perp}=\{0\}
, which is statement (ii).

\square

9.
Local subspaces over a subset of
\Omega

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert function space on a set
\Omega
.
For
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega
define
d(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
by

d(\alpha,\beta)=\|k_{\alpha}-k_{\beta}\|.

(9.1)

Then
d
is a pseudometric on
\Omega
, that is, it
satisfies the axioms of a metric except for the strict
positivity of
d
when
\alpha\not=\beta
. Clearly
d

is a metric on

\Omega
if and only if
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega
and
\alpha\not=\beta
imply
k_{\alpha}\not=k_{\beta}
, alternatively,
if and only if the elements of
\mathcal{H}
separate the points of

\Omega
.

Definition 9.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert function space on a set

\Omega
. We shall say that
\Omega
is
complete

(for
\mathcal{H}
) if

\Omega
is complete with respect to the pseudometric
d

defined by (
9.1
) (that is, if every sequence in

\Omega
which is Cauchy with respect to
d
has a limit
point in

\Omega
with respect to
d
).

Thus,
\Omega
is complete for
\mathcal{H}
if and only if the set
of kernels
\{k_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Omega\}
is a closed
subset of
\mathcal{H}
. It is reasonable to restrict attention to
pseudomultipliers of Hilbert function spaces on complete
sets. The “natural” Hilbert function spaces do have complete

\Omega
’s, and in any case there is an obvious process of
completion – regard
\mathcal{H}
as a function space on the
closure of
\{k_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Omega\}
in
\mathcal{H}
:
pseudomultipliers extend in a natural way.

For any finite set
F\subset\Omega
we shall denote by

\#F
the number of elements of
F
and by
\mathcal{V}_{F}
the
span of
\{k_{\alpha}:\alpha\in F\}
in
\mathcal{H}
. Notice that,
for any finite
F\subset\Omega
, there exists a subset

F_{0}
of
F
such that
\mathcal{V}_{F_{0}}=\mathcal{V}_{F}
and

\#F_{0}=\dim\mathcal{V}_{F}
.

Definition 9.2
.

Let
F
be a finite subset of

\Omega
. A subspace
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathcal{H}

lies over

F

if
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{V}_{F}
. We say that a subspace
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathcal{H}

lies over

F

tautly
if in addition
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{V}_{F_{0}}
for
some subset
F_{0}
of
F
.

Thus, in
H^{2}
, if
\alpha\in\mathbb{D}\smallsetminus\{0\},\operatorname{span}\{k_{0}+k_{\alpha}\}
lies over
\{0,\alpha\}
, but does
not lie tautly over
\{0,\alpha\}
. Note that
\mathcal{M}
lies
over
F
tautly if and only if there exists
F_{0}\subset F

with
\#F_{0}=\dim\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{V}_{F_{0}}
.
The property of tautness connects with the visibility of
subspaces, as the following proposition shows.

Proposition 9.3
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier and let
F
be a finite
subset of
D_{\varphi}
. If
\mathcal{M}
is a subspace of

\mathcal{H}
which lies over

tautly then
\varphi
sees

\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

This is immediate from the facts that

\varphi
sees
k_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in D_{\varphi}
,
and hence sees
\mathcal{V}_{F_{0}}
for
F_{0}\subset D_{\varphi}
, while
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{V}_{F_{0}}
for some

F_{0}\subset F
.
\square

There is no converse: if one knows that a pseudomultiplier

\varphi
sees a space
\mathcal{M}
which lies over

, one cannot conclude that
\mathcal{M}
is taut over

F
. Indeed, if
\varphi
happens to be a constant function,
then
\varphi
sees
every
subspace. However, the
above example,
\mathcal{M}=\operatorname{span}\{k_{0}+k_{\alpha}\}\subset H^{2}
is likely to be typical. If
\varphi\in H^{\infty}
and

\varphi(0)\not=\varphi(\alpha)
, and if
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{C}u

then
\varphi
sees
\mathcal{M}
if and only if
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{C}k_{0}
or
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{C}k_{\alpha}
, that is, if and only if

\mathcal{M}
is taut over
\{0,\alpha\}
.

For any closed subspace
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathcal{H}
we shall
denote by

the orthogonal projection operator
from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{M}
.

Definition 9.4
.

Let
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2},\dots

\mathcal{H}
. We say that
\mathcal{M}_{j}

The notion of a subspace lying over a finite subset, which we

\Omega
.

Further we will use the well-known result that if

and

Definition 9.5
.

Let

. A closed subspace

of

\mathcal{H}

.

Let

Proposition 9.7
.

Let

\mathcal{M}
be a

. The following are

(i)

\mathcal{M}
lies over

;

(ii)

\mathcal{M}
has a basis of vectors that lie over

;

(iii)

\mathcal{M}
lies over

.

Not every vector in
\mathcal{H}
lies over a set

. Below we give some examples in

on
\mathbb{D}
. To this end we introduce a notion of

.

Definition 9.8
.

A holomorphic function
f
on
\mathbb{D}
is said to be
n

, the equation
f(z)=w
has at most
n

\mathbb{D}
, counting multiplicities.

It is easy to see that an equivalent condition is: a holomorphic function
f
on
\mathbb{D}
is
n
-valent if, for every positive

and for all
w\in\mathbb{C}
, the equation
f(z)=w
has at most
n
solutions in the disc
r\mathbb{D}
, counting multiplicities.

Proposition 9.9
.

If
(f_{j})
is a convergent sequence in
H^{2}
with limit
f
and, for some positive integer

,
f_{j}
is
n
-valent for every

, then

is
n
-valent.

Proof.

For all

and

, by the Argument Principle,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{r\mathbb{T}}\frac{f_{j}^{\prime}(z)}{f_{j}(z)-w}\,dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{the number of solutions in $r\mathbb{D}$ of }f_{j}(z)=w

(9.2)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n.

Since

in
H^{2}
, we have

and
f^{\prime}_{j}\to f^{\prime}

uniformly
on

as

.
It follows that

\frac{f_{j}^{\prime}(z)}{f_{j}(z)-w}\to\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)-w}

uniformly on

as

. Hence, if we let

in the inequality (

), we obtain

\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{r\mathbb{T}}\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)-w}\,dz\leq n.

Thus, again by the Argument Principle, the number of solutions of the equation
f(z)=w
in
r\mathbb{D}
is at most
n
, that is,

is
n
-valent.

\square

Corollary 9.10
.

If
f\in H^{2}
lies over
\mathbb{D}
then there exists a positive integer
n
such that
f
is
n
-valent.

Proof.

Let
f\in H^{2}
lie over
\mathbb{D}
. Then there exists a positive integer
n
and a sequence
(F_{j})
of subsets of
\mathbb{D}
such that
\#F_{j}\leq n
and a function
f_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{F_{j}}
for each

such that

f_{j}\to f
. Since
f_{j}
is the span of at most
n
Szegő kernels,

is a rational function of degree at most

-valent. Hence, by Proposition

is
n
-valent.

\square

Corollary
9.10
provides an easy way to see that not all functions in
H^{2}
lie over
\mathbb{D}
. For example, it is immediate that any infinite Blaschke product does not lie over
\mathbb{D}
.
However, as we showed in Example
2.26
, the vector

does lie over
\mathbb{D}
.

What is the appropriate notion of lying tautly over

D
? By analogy with Proposition
9.3
, it
should guarantee visibility to pseudomultipliers
defined on
D
. The right notion is the following,
but we vary the nomenclature: “local” seems more
fitting than “taut” here.

Definition 9.11
.

Let
D\subset\Omega
. A closed subspace
\mathcal{M}
of

\mathcal{H}
is
local over

D
if there exists an
integer
n
, a sequence of subsets
(F_{j})
of
D

and a sequence of subspaces
(\mathcal{M}_{j})
of
\mathcal{H}

such that
\#F_{j}\leq n
for all
j
and

(i)

\mathcal{M}_{j}
lies tautly over
F_{j}
for each

j
, and

(ii)

\mathcal{M}_{j}\to\mathcal{M}
.

We have emphasized the similarity to Definition

9.5
, but we could also have described local
subspaces slightly more economically, as the
following proposition shows.

Proposition 9.12
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert function space on a set
\Omega
,
let
D\subset\Omega
and let
\mathcal{M}
be a closed
subspace of
\mathcal{H}
. The following are equivalent.

(i)

\mathcal{M}
is local over
D
;

(ii)

there exist an integer
n
and a sequence

(E_{j})
of subsets of
D
such that
\#E_{j}\leq n
and

\mathcal{V}_{E_{j}}\to\mathcal{M}
;

(iii)

\mathcal{M}
is finite-dimensional and there
exists a sequence
(E_{j})
of subsets of
D
such that

\mathcal{V}_{E_{j}}\to\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

Condition (ii) is little more than a
rephrasing of the definition of a local space (see
Definition
9.2
and
9.11
). Suppose (ii) holds.
Then
\dim\mathcal{V}_{E_{j}}\leq n
for all
j
, and since

\mathcal{V}_{E_{j}}\to\mathcal{M}
it follows that
\dim\mathcal{M}\leq n
, and so (iii) holds. Conversely, suppose (iii)
holds. It is not hard to see that
\mathcal{V}_{E_{j}}
must
have the same dimension as
\mathcal{M}
for all
sufficiently large
j
. For such
j
we may pick
F_{j}\subset E_{j}\subset D
such that
\mathcal{V}_{F_{j}}=\mathcal{V}_{E_{j}}
and
\#F_{j}=\dim\mathcal{M}
. Then
\mathcal{V}_{F_{j}}\to\mathcal{M}
and so (ii) holds.
\square

Thus the local subspaces of
\mathcal{H}
over
D
are
precisely the finite-dimensional limits of the spaces
of the form
\mathcal{V}_{F},F\subset D
.

Example 9.13
.

In
H^{2}
, a subspace which lies over
\mathbb{D}
but
is not local over
\mathbb{D}
is
\mathbb{C}v
, where

. We have already seen that

lies over

\mathbb{D}
. Suppose there is a sequence
(E_{j})
of

\mathbb{D}
such that
\mathcal{V}_{E_{j}}\to\mathbb{C}v
. We must have
\#E_{j}=1
for

, hence
\mathcal{V}_{E_{j}}=\mathbb{C}k_{\alpha_{j}}
for some
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{D}
. If

, we must have

in

. On considering the

and

. Thus

is not local over
\mathbb{D}
.

Theorem 9.14
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudomultiplier. If
\mathcal{M}
is a
subspace of
\mathcal{H}
which is local over

then

sees
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

By Proposition

, there is a sequence
(F_{j})
of subsets

of

.

.
Hence, by Proposition

10.
Projectively complete Hilbert function spaces and local subspaces

Our next task is to clarify the sense in which a subspace
which is local over a set
D
can genuinely be attached to a
set of points in
D
. A further assumption on
D
will be
required, for consider the example of a proper
dense subset
D
of
\mathbb{D}
. If
\alpha\in\mathbb{D}\smallsetminus D
then it is clear that the subspace

\mathbb{C}k_{\alpha}
of
H^{2}
is local over
D
, yet

\mathbb{C}k_{\alpha}
as a subspace of
H^{2}
(as a function
space on
D
) is not associated with a point of the domain
of the function space. This example suggests we confine
attention to domains
\Omega
which are complete in
some sense. In fact the appropriate notion of
completeness turns out to be a little stronger than the
natural one given in Definition
9.1
. Subject to this
completeness assumption, it transpires that the structure
of local subspaces is remarkably transparent (Theorem

10.13
below).

Lemma 10.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2},\dots
be closed subspaces of

\mathcal{H}
such that

\dim\mathcal{M}<\infty
and
\mathcal{M}_{j}\to\mathcal{M}
. If

(u_{j})
is a bounded sequence with
u_{j}\in\mathcal{M}_{j}
for
each
j
then there exists

u\in\mathcal{M}
and a sub-sequence
(u_{j_{k}})
of
(u_{j})
such
that
u_{j_{k}}\to u
as
k\to\infty
.

Proof.

Pick a sub-sequence
(u_{j_{k}})
of

(u_{j})
such that
u_{j_{k}}
converges weakly to an element

u\in\mathcal{H}
. We have

u-u_{j}=u-P_{\mathcal{M}}u+P_{\mathcal{M}}(u-u_{j})+(P_{\mathcal{M}}-P_{\mathcal{M}_{j}})u_{j}.

Let
j\to\infty
along the sequence
(j_{k})
. Since

u-u_{j}\to 0
weakly and
\mathcal{M}
is finite-dimensional,

P_{\mathcal{M}}(u-u_{j})\to 0
in norm. Since
(u_{j})
is bounded
and
P_{\mathcal{M}_{j}}\to P_{\mathcal{M}}
, the final term on the
right hand side also tends to
0
in norm. Thus

(u-u_{j_{k}})
converges in norm, and its limit must be its
weak limit, which is
0
. Thus
u=P_{\mathcal{M}}u
, that is,
u\in\mathcal{M}
and
u_{j_{k}}\to u
.
\square

To obtain our structure theorem for local subspaces we
need to introduce
projective Hilbert space

P\mathcal{H}
. This is the set of one-dimensional subspaces of the
Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, with a natural metric
p
defined
as follows. For each one-dimensional subspace
\mathbb{C}x
of
\mathcal{H}
, where
x\in\mathcal{H}
and
\|x\|=1
, we may
consider its intersection
\mathbb{T}x=\{\lambda x:|\lambda|=1\}
with the unit sphere
\mathcal{H}_{1}
of
\mathcal{H}
.
This gives a one-one correspondence between the
elements of
P\mathcal{H}
and a subset of the set
\mathcal{C}
of
all closed subsets of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. We may give
\mathcal{C}
the
Hausdorff metric
\rho
induced by the standard metric of

\mathcal{H}
restricted to
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. This induces a metric

p
on
P\mathcal{H}
. Note that, for
x,y\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
,

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbb{T}x,\mathbb{T}y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\lambda\in\mathbb{T}}\|x-\lambda y\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\lambda\in\mathbb{T}}\{\|x\|^{2}+\|\lambda y\|^{2}-2{\rm Re}\overline{\lambda}\left\langle x,y\right\rangle\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{1-|\left\langle x,y\right\rangle|^{2}\}^{1/2}.

Hence the metric
p
on
P\mathcal{H}
is given explicitly by

p(\mathbb{C}x,\mathbb{C}y)=\{1-|\left\langle x,y\right\rangle|^{2}\}^{1/2},\quad x,y\in\mathcal{H}_{1}.

(10.1)

We note that there are other natural ways of defining a metric on
P\mathcal{H}
.
For example, we could identify the one-dimensional subspace
\mathbb{C}x
, for
x\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
, with the orthogonal projection operator on
\mathbb{C}x
. The
operator norm would then induce a metric on
P\mathcal{H}
. A little calculation
then gives the distance between
\mathbb{C}x
and
\mathbb{C}y
as
\sqrt{1-|\left\langle x,y\right\rangle|^{2}}
.
This is identical to
p
.

P\mathcal{H}
is complete with respect to

p
(if
(\mathbb{C}x_{j})
is a Cauchy sequence we can
construct a sequence
(y_{j})
in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
with
y_{j}\in\mathbb{C}x_{n_{j}}
and
\|y_{j+1}-y_{j}\|<2^{-j}
; then

(y_{j})
is Cauchy in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, and if
y
is its limit,

\mathbb{C}x_{j}\to\mathbb{C}y)
. The canonical mapping

\mathcal{H}\smallsetminus\{0\}\to P\mathcal{H}:x\mapsto\mathbb{C}x
is easily shown to be continuous, so if
(x_{j}),(c_{j})
are sequences in
\mathcal{H}\smallsetminus\{0\},\mathbb{C}
respectively and
c_{j}x_{j}\to x
in
\mathcal{H}
then

\mathbb{C}x_{j}\to\mathbb{C}x
. Conversely, if
\mathbb{C}x_{j}\to\mathbb{C}x
in
P\mathcal{H}
, then the calculation
above shows that

\left\|\lambda_{j}\frac{x_{j}}{\|x_{j}\|}-\frac{x}{\|x\|}\right\|\to 0

where
\lambda_{j}
in
\mathbb{T}
is chosen so that

\lambda_{j}\left\langle x_{j},x\right\rangle>0
. We infer that
\mathbb{C}x_{j}\to\mathbb{C}x
if and only if there is a sequence
(c_{j})
in

\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\{0\}
such that
c_{j}x_{j}\to x
.

Definition 10.2
.

A Hilbert function space

(\mathcal{H},\Omega)
is
projectively complete

for
\mathcal{H}
if the set
\{{\mathbb{C}}{k_{\lambda}}:\lambda\in\Omega,k_{\lambda}\neq 0\}
is closed in
P\mathcal{H}
.
In this case we shall also say that
\Omega
is projectively complete for
\mathcal{H}
.

Since
P\mathcal{H}
is a complete metric space, so too is

\{{\mathbb{C}}{k_{\lambda}}:\lambda\in\Omega,k_{\lambda}\neq 0\}

with respect to the restriction of

p
whenever
(\mathcal{H},\Omega)
is projectively complete.

Proposition 10.3
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert function space on
\Omega
. Then

\Omega
is projectively complete for
\mathcal{H}
if and only if
whenever
(c_{j}),(\alpha_{j})
are sequences in
\mathbb{C},\Omega
respectively and

c_{j}k_{\alpha_{j}}\to u\in\mathcal{H}\setminus\{0\},

there exist
c\in\mathbb{C}
and
\alpha\in\Omega
such
that

u=ck_{\alpha}.

Proof.

Suppose
\Omega
is projectively complete for
\mathcal{H}
and
c_{j}k_{\alpha_{j}}\to u\neq 0
.
Then almost every
k_{\alpha_{j}}\neq 0
and, by
continuity of the canonical mapping

\mathcal{H}\setminus\{0\}\to P\mathcal{H}
, we have
\mathbb{C}k_{\alpha_{j}}\to\mathbb{C}u
.
Thus
\mathbb{C}u
lies in the closure of
\{\mathbb{C}k_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Omega,k_{\lambda}\neq 0\}

and so, by the definition of projective completeness,
\mathbb{C}u\in\{\mathbb{C}k_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Omega,k_{\lambda}\neq 0\}
.
Hence
u=ck_{\alpha}
for some scalar
c
and some
\alpha\in\Omega.

Conversely, suppose
(\mathcal{H},\Omega)
is such that
c_{j}k_{\alpha_{j}}\to u\neq 0
implies that
u=ck_{\alpha}
for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
and
\alpha\in\Omega
.
Consider any element
\mathbb{C}x,x\in\mathcal{H}\setminus\{0\},
of the closure of

\{\mathbb{C}k_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Omega,k_{\lambda}\neq 0\}
in
P\mathcal{H}
.
Pick a sequence
(\alpha_{j})
in
\Omega
such that
\mathbb{C}k_{\alpha_{j}}\to\mathbb{C}x
. As noted above, there
is a sequence
(c_{j})
of non-zero scalars such that
c_{j}k_{\alpha_{j}}\to x
. By hypothesis,
x=ck_{\alpha}
for some
c\neq 0
and
\alpha\in\Omega
.
Hence
\mathbb{C}x\in\{\mathbb{C}k_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Omega,k_{\lambda}\neq 0\}
.
Thus
\Omega
is projectively complete for
\mathcal{H}
.

\square

If the mapping
\alpha\mapsto\mathbb{C}k_{\alpha}
maps
\Omega
injectively into

\mathcal{H}\setminus\{0\}

then
p
induces a metric
d
on
\Omega
, given by

d(\lambda,\mu)=p(\mathbb{C}k_{\lambda},\mathbb{C}k_{\mu})=\left\{1-\frac{|\left\langle k_{\lambda},k_{\mu}\right\rangle|}{\|k_{\lambda}\|\ \|k_{\mu}\|}\right\}^{1/2}.

Example 10.4
.

\mathbb{D}
is projectively complete for
H^{2}
. By
(
9.1
), for
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{D}
we have

\displaystyle p(\mathbb{C}k_{\lambda},\mathbb{C}k_{\mu})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{1-\frac{|\left\langle k_{\lambda},k_{\mu}\right\rangle|}{\|k_{\lambda}\|\ \|k_{\mu}\|}\right\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{1-\frac{(1-|\lambda|^{2})^{1/2}(1-|\mu|^{2})^{1/2}}{|1-\overline{\lambda}\mu|}\right\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{1-\frac{(1-|\lambda|^{2})(1-|\mu|^{2})}{|1-\overline{\lambda}\mu|^{2}}\right\}^{1/2}h(\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\lambda-\mu}{1-\overline{\lambda}\mu}\right|h(\lambda,\mu)

where

h(\lambda,\mu)=\left\{1+\frac{(1-|\lambda|^{2})^{1/2}(1-|\mu|^{2})^{1/2}}{|1-\overline{\lambda}\mu|}\right\}^{-1/2}.

We have
1/\sqrt{2}\leq|h|\leq 1
, and so the metric induced
on
\mathbb{D}
by
p
is uniformly equivalent to the standard
pseudo-hyperbolic distance
[
15
, Sec. 1.4.4]
, for which
\mathbb{D}
is
complete.
\square

Example 10.5
.

\mathbb{D}
is not projectively complete for

, the subspace of

consisting of functions which vanish at

. The reproducing kernel here is

is uniformly equivalent to

with

Alternatively, one may apply Proposition
10.3
with
c_{j}=j,\alpha_{j}=1/j,u(\lambda)=\lambda
.

Example 10.6
.

The closed unit interval is projectively complete for the Sobolev space

of Example

.
To see this fact note that the reproducing kernel of

is given by equation (

),

.

Proposition 10.7
.

Let
\Omega
be a compact metric space and let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space
of continuous functions on
\Omega
. Suppose that the reproducing kernel of

\mathcal{H}
has no zero on
\Omega\times\Omega
. Then
\Omega
is projectively complete
for
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Apply Proposition
10.3
. Suppose that
c_{j}k_{\alpha_{j}}\to u\neq 0
in
\mathcal{H}
for some scalars
c_{j}
and points
\alpha_{j}\in\Omega
.
Since
\Omega
is compact metric we may assume (passing to a
subsequence if necessary) that
(\alpha_{j})
converges to some point
\beta\in\Omega
. Consider any point
\lambda\in\Omega
. We have

\displaystyle u(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle u,k_{\lambda}\right\rangle=\lim_{j}\left\langle c_{j}k_{\alpha_{j}},k_{\lambda}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{j}c_{j}k(\lambda,\alpha_{j}).

Since
u\neq 0
there is some
\lambda
for which
u(\lambda)\neq 0
.
In view of the fact that
k(\lambda,\alpha_{j})\to k(\lambda,\beta)\neq 0
,
it must be that the
sequence
(c_{j})
converges to a non-zero limit
c
in
\mathbb{C}
. Hence we have,
for any
\lambda\in\Omega
,

u(\lambda)=ck(\lambda,\beta)=ck_{\beta}(\lambda).

Thus
u=ck_{\beta}
. By Proposition
10.3
,

\Omega
is projectively complete for
\mathcal{H}
.

\square

Definition 10.8
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert function space on
\Omega
and
let
\mathcal{M}
be a local subspace of

over

\Omega

in

and

. The

of

\mathcal{M}
is the set of support points of
\mathcal{M}
. It will
be denoted by
\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{M}
.

Note that if

then

.
For if

are as in the definition then, by Lemma

, the sequence
(c_{j}k_{\alpha_{j}})
has a subsequence which

.

Example 10.9
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\operatorname{span}\{1,v\}

, where

. We have already seen in Example

.

Definition 10.10
.

A local subspace
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathcal{H}
is
punctual
if

consists of a single point.

Definition 10.11
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert function space over a set
\Omega
.
For a subset

of
\Omega
we define the

closure
of

in
\Omega
to be the set

pc(D)=\{\lambda\in\Omega:\mathbb{C}k_{\lambda}\;\text{is in the closure of}\;\{\mathbb{C}k_{\alpha}:\alpha\in D\}\;\text{
in}\;P\mathcal{H}\}.

Lemma 10.12
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and let
\mathcal{M}
be a subspace of
\mathcal{H}
of finite dimension
m
.
Let
(\mathcal{V}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be a sequence of subspaces of
\mathcal{H}
which converges to
\mathcal{M}
as
i\to\infty
.
Let
k
be a positive integer no greater than
m
.
For every positive integer
i
let
\mathcal{W}_{i}
be a
k
-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{V}_{i}
. Then there exists a sequence
(i_{q})_{q\geq 1}
of positive integers and a
k
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{W}
of
\mathcal{M}
such that
P_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}\to P_{\mathcal{W}}
in norm as
i\to\infty
along the sequence
(i_{q})_{q\geq 1}
.

Proof.

For each
i
let
e_{i}^{1},\dots,e_{i}^{k}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{W}_{i}
. Since
\mathcal{M}
is finite-dimensional and each
e_{i}^{1}
is a unit vector, there is a sequence
(i_{q})_{q\geq 1}
of positive integers and a vector
u^{1}\in\mathcal{M}
such that
P_{\mathcal{M}}e_{i_{q}}^{1}\to u^{1}
as
q\to\infty
.
Passing to a sub-sequence of
(i_{q})_{q\geq 1}
, we may assume that
P_{\mathcal{M}}e_{i_{q}}^{2}\to u^{2}
as

for some vector

of norm at most

. Continuing in this way, we arrive at vectors
u^{1},u^{2},\dots,u^{k}\in\mathcal{M}
such that
P_{\mathcal{M}}e_{i_{q}}^{\ell}\to u^{\ell}
as
q\to\infty
for
\ell=1,2,\dots,k
.
Since
e_{i}^{\ell}\in\mathcal{W}_{i}
and
\mathcal{W}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{i}
