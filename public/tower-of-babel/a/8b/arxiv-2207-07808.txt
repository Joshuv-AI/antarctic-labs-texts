Abstract
In this note we present some generalized versions of the Krein-Rutman theorem for sectorial operators. They are formulated in a fashion that can be easily applied to elliptic operators. Another feature of these generalized versions is that they contain some information on the generalized eigenspaces associated with non-principal eigenvalues, which are helpful in the study of the dynamics of evolution equations in ordered Banach spaces.

1
Introduction

It is well known that the Krein-Rutman theorem (see e.g.
[
14
,
19
]
) for bounded operators (particularly for compact operators) plays a crucial role in the discussion of the principal eigenvalue problem of elliptic operators via their resolvents. But generally only part of the information concerning the principal eigenvalue and eigenvectors can be obtained. One reason is that there may be no one-one correspondence between the boundary spectrum of an elliptic operator
A
and the peripheral spectrum of its resolvent
R_{\lambda}(A)
. As a remedy, one has to do some tricky PDE argument when refined information on the principal eigenvalue and eigenvectors is needed; see e.g. Du
[
4
, Theorem 1.4]
, Evans
[
5
, Sec. 6.5, Theorem 3]
and Ni
[
17
]
etc.
It is therefore of particular interest to give variants of the classical Krein-Rutman theorem for unbounded operators.

In Greiner et al.
[
8
]
it was proved that the spectral bound
s(A)
of the generator
A
of a positive
C_{0}
-semigroup on a Banach space with a normal reproducing cone is contained in
\sigma(A)
. The monograph
[
2
]
contains a far reaching theory concerning the above question in the framework of generators of
C_{0}
-semigroups on the functional space
C_{0}(X)
consisting of continuous functions vanishing at infinity, where
X
is a locally compact space; see
[
2
, Chap. B-III]
. These extensions make the Krein-Rutman theorem to be more efficient in studying the spectral properties of unbounded operators.

When dealing with an elliptic differential operator
L
on a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
as in (
4.1
), the frequently used spaces in which
L
may generate a
C_{0}
-semigroup are the Sobolev ones. However, in many cases the cone of nonnegative functions can neither have interior nor be normal in a Sobolev space. (The above two properties were required in most abstract results in the literature.) Of course,
L
can be considered as an operator in the space
C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, in which the cone
{\mathcal{C}}
of nonnegative functions have nonempty interior. But as it was pointed in Kielhofer
[
12
]
,

L
can fail to generate a
C_{0}
-semigroup in this space. To cover this situation, Nussbaum
[
18
]
made an effort to extend a major part of the Krein-Rutman theorem to operators which may not generate a
C_{0}
-semigroup.

In a recent paper
[
16
]
, the authors reformulated the classical Perron-Frobenius theory and Krein-Rutman theorem by using an elementary dynamical approach. Inspired by this work, in this note we present another generalization of the Krein-Rutman theorem for sectorial operators in a formalism that seems to be more natural and suitable for elliptic operators. It is different in some ways from those mentioned above and can be easily applied to elliptic operators, which enables us to reduce significantly the technical PDE argument involved in the investigation of elliptic eigenvalue problems. Another feature of these generalized versions is that they contain some information on the generalized eigenspaces associated with non-principal eigenvalues, which are helpful in the study of the dynamics of evolution equations in ordered Banach spaces (see e.g.
[
10
]
). As an illustrating example, the principal eigenvalue problem of a general elliptic operator associated with degenerate mixed boundary condition is discussed.

The remaining part of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we do some preliminary work, and in Section
3
we prove generalized Krein-Rutman type theorems for sectorial operators. Section
4
consists of an example illustrating the theoretical results mentioned above.

Preliminaries

Let
X
be a real Banach space with norm
\|\cdot\|
.
Given
M\subset X
, the
interior
and
closure
of
M
are denoted respectively by
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{M}}
and
\overline{M}
. When we need to emphasize in which space the interior and closure are taken, we also use the notations
\mbox{int}_{X}M
and
\mbox{Cl}_{X}M
in place of
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{M}}
and
\overline{M}
, respectively.
For
x\in X
, set

d(x,M)=\inf_{y\in M}\|x-y\|.

2.1
Some basic knowledge in the spectral theory of operators

Let
A
be a closed densely defined operator in
X
. Denote by
\sigma(A)
and
\rho(A)
the
spectrum
and
resolvent set
of
A
, respectively. For
\lambda\in\rho(A)
, let
R_{\lambda}(A):=(\lambda-A)^{-1}
be the resolvent of
A
.

Let
\mathbb{X}=X+{\mathbf{i}}X
be the complexification of
X
(see e.g.
[
16
, Sect. 7]
for details), and define the complexification
\mathbb{A}
of
A
as

\mathbb{A}u=Ax+{\mathbf{i}}Ay,\hskip 28.45274pt\forall\,u=x+{\mathbf{i}}y\in\mathbb{X}.

Given
\mu\in\sigma(A)=\sigma(\mathbb{A})
, let

\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})=\{\xi\in\mathbb{X}:\,\,(\mathbb{A}-\mu)^{j}\xi=0\mbox{ for some }j\geq 1\}.

(2.1)

Then
\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})
is an invariant subspace of
\mathbb{A}
. For each
\xi\in\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})\setminus\{0\}
, it is clear that there is an integer
k\geq 1
such that

(\mathbb{A}-\mu)^{j}\xi\neq 0\,\,\,(0\leq j\leq k-1),\hskip 14.22636pt(\mathbb{A}-\mu)^{k}\xi=0.

(2.2)

For convenience in statement, we call the number
k
in (
2.2
) the
rank
of
\xi
, denoted by
\mbox{rank}(\xi)
.

For
\mu\in\sigma(A)
, it follows by the invariance of
\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})
that

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(A):=\{\mbox{Re}\,\xi:\,\,\xi\in\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})\}\,\,\left(=\{\mbox{Im}\,\xi:\,\,\xi\in\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})\}\right)\end{array}

(2.3)

is an invariant subspace of
A
in
X
, which will be referred to as the
generalized eigenspace
of
A
pertaining to
\mu
. (The second equality in (
2.3
) is due to the fact that if
\xi\in\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})
, then
\pm\mathbf{i}\xi\in\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})
.)

Lemma 2.1

Let
\mu\in\sigma(A)
, and
\lambda\in\rho(A)
. Then

R_{\lambda}(A)\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(A)=\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(A)=\mbox{GE}_{(\lambda-\mu)^{-1}}(R_{\lambda}(A)).

Proof.
It suffices to check that

R_{\lambda}(\mathbb{A})\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})=\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})=\mbox{GE}_{\lambda_{\mu}}(R_{\lambda}(\mathbb{A})),

(2.4)

where
\lambda_{\mu}:=(\lambda-\mu)^{-1}
.

Let
\xi\in\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})
. Then
(\mathbb{A}-\mu)^{k}\xi=0
for some
k\geq 1
. Hence

(\mathbb{A}-\mu)^{k}\left(R_{\lambda}(\mathbb{A})\xi\right)=R_{\lambda}(\mathbb{A})(\mathbb{A}-\mu)^{k}\xi=R_{\lambda}(\mathbb{A})0=0.

It follows that
R_{\lambda}(\mathbb{A})\xi\in\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})
. Simple calculations also yield

(\lambda-\mathbb{A})^{k}\left(R_{\lambda}(\mathbb{A})-\lambda_{\mu}\right)^{k}\xi=\lambda_{\mu}^{k}(\mathbb{A}-\mu)^{k}\xi=0.

(2.5)

Since
\lambda\in\rho(\mathbb{A})
, (
2.5
) implies that
\left(R_{\lambda}(\mathbb{A})-\lambda_{\mu}\right)^{k}\xi=0
. Therefore
\xi\in\mbox{GE}_{\lambda_{\mu}}(R_{\lambda}(\mathbb{A}))
.
In conclusion, we have

R_{\lambda}(\mathbb{A})\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})\subset\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})\subset\mbox{GE}_{\lambda_{\mu}}(R_{\lambda}(\mathbb{A})).

The verification of the inverse inclusions is similar. We omit it.

\blacksquare

Denote by
\sigma_{e}(A)
the
essential spectrum
of
A
in the terminology of Browder
[
3
, pp. 107-108, Def. 11]
. Then each
\mu\in\sigma(A)\setminus\sigma_{e}(A)
is isolated in
\sigma(A)
with
\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(A)
being a finite-dimensional subspace of
X
; see
[
3
, pp. 108]
.

The
spectral bound

\mbox{spb}(A)
and
essential spectral bound

\mbox{spb}_{e}(A)
of
A
are defined as

\mbox{spb}(A)=\sup\{\mbox{Re}\,\mu:\,\,\mu\in\sigma(A)\},\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{spb}_{e}(A)=\sup\{\mbox{Re}\,\mu:\,\,\mu\in\sigma_{e}(A)\}.

(We assign
\mbox{spb}_{e}(A)=-\infty
if
\sigma_{e}(A)=\emptyset
.) Set

\sigma_{b}(A)=\sigma(A)\cap\{\mbox{Re}\,z=\mbox{spb}(A)\}.

\sigma_{b}(A)
is called the
boundary spectrum
of
A
.

Let
{\mathscr{L}}(X)
be the space of bounded linear operators on
X
.
If
A\in{\mathscr{L}}(X)
, we define the
spectral radius

r(A)
and
essential spectral radius

r_{e}(A)
as

r(A)=\sup\{|\mu|:\,\,\mu\in\sigma(A)\},\hskip 14.22636ptr_{e}(A)=\sup\{|\mu|:\,\,\mu\in\sigma_{e}(A)\}.

It is a basic knowledge that
r(A)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|A^{k}\|^{1/k}
.

2.2
Cones and positive operators

Let
X
be a Banach space. A
wedge
in
X
is a closed subset
K\subset X
with
K\neq\{0\}
such that
tK\subset K
for all
t\geq 0
.
A convex wedge
K
with
K\cap(-K)=\{0\}
is called a
cone
.

A cone
K
is said to be
total
(resp.,
solid
), if
\overline{K-K}=X
(resp.,
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{K}}\neq\emptyset
).

From now on we always assume that there has been given a cone
K
in
X
.

An operator
A\in{\mathscr{L}}(A)
is called
positive
(resp.
strongly positive
) if
AK\subset K
(resp.
A(K\setminus\{0\})\subset\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{K}}
).

Definition 2.2

[
16
]
In case
K
is a solid cone, a positive operator
A
is called weakly irreducible, if the boundary
\partial K
of
K
contains no eigenvectors of
A
pertaining to nonnegative real eigenvalues.

Remark 2.3

It is almost obvious that strongly positive operators and primitive operators are weakly irreducible.
(Recall that
A
is said to be primitive, if there is an integer
m\geq 1
such that
A^{m}(K\setminus\{0\})\subset\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{K}}
; see
[
15
, pp. 285]
.)
We also infer from the argument following Definition 7.5 in
[
16
]
that irreducibility (in the terminology of
[
15
]
) implies weak irreducibility defined as above.

We finally recall some generalized versions of the Krein-Rutman Theorem for bounded operators given in
[
16
]
to conclude this section.

3
Krein-Rutman Type Theorems for Sectorial Operators

Let
X,Y
be two real Banach spaces with
Y\hookrightarrow X
; moreover,
Y
is
dense
in
X
. Denote by
\|\cdot\|
and
\|\cdot\|_{1}
the norms of
X
and
Y
, respectively.

Let
K
be a cone in
Y
, and
A
a closed densely defined operator in
X
with
-A
being sectorial (see
[
9
, Chap. 1]
for definition).
We always assume that the following standing assumptions are fulfilled:

(A1)

R_{\lambda}(A)K\subset K
for
\lambda>0
sufficiently large.

(A2)

s_{e}:=\mbox{spb}_{e}(A)<\mbox{spb}(A):=s
.

(A3)

\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(A)\subset Y
for every
\mu\in\sigma(A)
with
\mbox{Re}\,\mu>s_{e}
.

As usual, if
s\in\sigma(A)
, then we call
s
the
principal eigenvalue
of
A
. Consequently eigenvectors pertaining to
s
are referred to as
principal eigenvectors
.

One of our main results is the following general Krein-Rutman type theorem.

Theorem 3.1

Assume
K
is total in
Y
. Then the following assertions hold:

(1)

s
is an eigenvalue of
A
admitting a principal eigenvector
u\in K
.

(2)

If
\mbox{int}_{Y}K\neq\emptyset
and contains a principal eigenvector of
A
, then
s
shares the same algebraic and geometric multiplicities.

(3)

All eigenvectors of
A
corresponding to other eigenvalues
\mu\neq s
with
\mbox{Re}\,\mu>s_{e}
are contained in
Y\setminus\mbox{int}_{Y}K
.

(4)

If
\mu\in\sigma(A)
,
\mbox{Re}\,\mu>s_{e}
and
\mbox{Im}\,\mu\neq 0
, then

\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(A)\cap K=\{0\}.

(3.1)

Proof.
For
t\in\mathbb{R},\,\,t>s_{e}
, set

\Sigma_{1}(t):=\{\mu\in\sigma(A):\,\,\mbox{Re}\,\mu\geq t\}.

(3.2)

Since
-A
is sectorial,
\Sigma_{1}(t)

is a compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
for every
t>s_{e}
(by the definition of sectorial operators). As every
\mu\in\sigma(A)\setminus\sigma_{e}(A)
is isolated in
\sigma(A)
, one concludes that
\Sigma_{1}(t)
consists of a finite number of elements.

Let
\eta\in(s_{e},s]
, and
\Sigma_{0}(\eta)=\sigma(A)\setminus\Sigma_{1}(\eta)
.
By the finiteness of
\Sigma_{1}(t)
(
t>s_{e}
) one trivially checks that for some
\delta=\delta(\eta)>0
,

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{Re}\,\mu\leq\eta-\delta,\hskip 28.45274pt\forall\,\mu\in\Sigma_{0}(\eta)\end{array}

(3.3)

Hence
\Sigma_{0}(\eta)
and
\Sigma_{1}(\eta)
form a spectral decomposition of
\sigma(A)
.
Denote by

X=X_{0}(\eta)\oplus X_{1}(\eta)

(3.4)

the corresponding decomposition of
X
. Then
X_{1}(\eta)=\oplus_{\mu\in\Sigma_{1}(\eta)}\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(A)
is a finite-dimensional subspace of
X
. By (A3) we have
X_{1}(\eta)\subset Y
.

For notational simplicity, we rewrite
X_{i}(\eta):=X_{i}
(
i=0,1
).
Let us split the argument below into several steps.

Step 1.
We show that

X_{1}\cap K\neq\{0\}.

(3.5)

Let
P=\mbox{Cl}_{X}K
, the closure of
K
in
X
. Obviously
P
is a cone in
X
. Recalling that
X_{1}\subset Y
, to prove (
3.5
) it suffices to check that

X_{1}\cap P\neq\{0\}.

For this purpose, put
\tilde{A}=A-\eta+\delta
, where
\delta
is the positive number in (
3.3
).
\sigma(\tilde{A})
has a corresponding spectral decomposition
\sigma(\tilde{A})=\tilde{\Sigma}_{0}(\eta)\cup\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}(\eta)
with

\tilde{\Sigma}_{i}(\eta)=\Sigma_{i}(\eta)-\eta+\delta,\hskip 28.45274pti=0,1.

We observe that

\sup\{\mbox{Re}\,\mu:\,\,\mu\in\tilde{\Sigma}_{0}(\eta)\}\leq-\delta,\hskip 14.22636pt\inf\{\mbox{Re}\,\mu:\,\,\mu\in\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}(\eta)\}\geq\delta.

(3.6)

The direct sum decomposition of
X
corresponding to the above spectral decomposition of
\sigma(\tilde{A})
remains the same as in (
3.4
).

We claim that
P\not\subset X_{0}
. Indeed, suppose on the contrary that
P\subset X_{0}
. Then since
K
is total in
Y
, one would have

Y=\mbox{Cl}_{Y}{(K-K)}\subset\mbox{Cl}_{X}{(K-K)}\subset\mbox{Cl}_{X}{(P-P)}\subset X_{0}.

(We emphasize that the closures
\mbox{Cl}_{Y}
and
\mbox{Cl}_{X}
are taken with respect to the topologies of
Y
and
X
, respectively.)
Because
Y
is dense in
X
, we therefore have
X=\mbox{Cl}_{X}Y\subset\mbox{Cl}_{X}X_{0}=X_{0}
, a contradiction.

Take a
u_{0}\in P\setminus X_{0}
. Write
u_{0}=x_{0}+x_{1}
, where
x_{i}\in X_{i}
. Clearly
x_{1}\neq 0
. Let
u(t)=e^{t\tilde{A}}u_{0}
(
t\geq 0
), where
e^{t\tilde{A}}
is the
C_{0}
-semigroup generated by
\tilde{A}
.
Then

u(t)=e^{t\tilde{A}}x_{0}+e^{t\tilde{A}}x_{1}:=x_{0}(t)+x_{1}(t).

We infer from (
3.6
) that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|x_{0}(t)\|=0,\hskip 14.22636pt\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|x_{1}(t)\|=\infty.

(3.7)

By (A1) we have
R_{\lambda}(A)K\subset K\subset P
for
\lambda>0
sufficiently large. Therefore

R_{\lambda}(A)P=R_{\lambda}(A)\,\overline{K}=\overline{R_{\lambda}(A)K}\subset\overline{K}\subset P,

(3.8)

where the closures are taken in
X
. (The second equality in (
3.8
) is due to the fact that
R_{\lambda}(A)\in{\mathscr{L}}(X)
.)
This guarantees that
A
is
semigroup positive
, i.e.,
e^{tA}P\subset P
for
t\geq 0
(see e.g. Kato
[
11
, Lemma 5.1]
). Hence

e^{t\tilde{A}}P=e^{t(\delta-\eta)}e^{tA}P\subset P,\hskip 28.45274ptt\geq 0.

(3.9)

In particular, we have
u(t)=e^{t\tilde{A}}u_{0}\in P
for all
t\geq 0
.

Now we show that
X_{1}\cap P\neq\{0\}
and complete the proof of (
3.5
). First, by the first equality in (
3.7
) we see that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}d\left(u(t),X_{1}\right)=0
.
Now suppose on the contrary that
X_{1}\cap P=\{0\}
. Then by
[
16
, Lemma 2.4]
one deduces that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|u(t)\|=0
. This contradicts (
3.7
).

Step 2.
The verification of assertions (1) and (2).

Take
\eta=s
. Then

\Sigma_{1}(\eta)=\sigma_{b}(A).
Let
K_{1}=X_{1}\cap K
, where
X_{i}=X_{i}(\eta)=X_{i}(s)
(
i=0,1
) are given as in (
3.4
). Since
X_{1}
is a finite-dimensional subspace of
Y
, (
3.5
) implies that
K_{1}
is a cone in
X_{1}
.
As
K
is total in
Y
, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{Cl}_{X_{1}}(K_{1}-K_{1})&=\mbox{Cl}_{Y}(K_{1}-K_{1})=\mbox{Cl}_{Y}(X_{1}\cap K-X_{1}\cap K)\\[4.30554pt]
&\subset\mbox{Cl}_{Y}\left(X_{1}\cap(K-K)\right)=X_{1}\cap\mbox{Cl}_{Y}(K-K)\\[4.30554pt]
&=X_{1}\cap Y=X_{1}.\end{array}

That is,
K_{1}
is total in
X_{1}
.
Let
A_{1}=A|_{X_{1}}
. For
\lambda>0
sufficiently large, we infer from (
2.6
) that
R_{\lambda}(A_{1})X_{1}=X_{1}
. Thus by (A1) one easily verifies that

R_{\lambda}(A_{1})K_{1}\subset K_{1}.

(3.10)

Note that
\sigma(A_{1})=\Sigma_{1}(\eta)=\sigma_{b}(A)
. Let

\sigma(A_{1})=\{\mu_{i}=s+{\mathbf{i}}\beta_{i}:\,\,0\leq i\leq n\}.

We may assume that
|\beta_{0}|=\min_{0\leq i\leq n}|\beta_{i}|
.
Fix a number
\lambda>s
such that (
3.10
) holds. Then
|\lambda-\mu_{0}|=\min_{0\leq i\leq n}|\lambda-\mu_{i}|
, and hence

r(R_{\lambda}(A_{1}))=\sup\{1/|\lambda-\mu_{i}|:\,\,0\leq i\leq n\}=1/|\lambda-\mu_{0}|:=r.

By Theorem
2.4
one concludes that
r
is an eigenvalue of
R_{\lambda}(A_{1})
with an eigenvector
w\in K_{1}
. On the other hand, since
1/|\lambda-\mu_{i}|<r
for
\mu_{i}\in\sigma(A_{1})
with
\mu_{i}\neq\mu_{0},\overline{\mu}_{0}
, we see that the circle
{\mathbb{S}}_{r}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,\,|z|=r\}
in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
contains at most two eigenvalues of
R_{\lambda}(A_{1})
, i.e.,
1/(\lambda-\mu_{0})
and
1/(\lambda-\overline{\mu}_{0})
. Thus one necessarily has
1/(\lambda-\mu_{0})=1/(\lambda-\overline{\mu}_{0})=r
, which implies that
\beta_{0}=0
. It follows that
\mu_{0}=s
is an eigenvalue of
A
; furthermore,
w
is an eigenvector of
A
corresponding to
s
. This completes the proof of (1).

If
\mbox{int}_{Y}K\neq\emptyset
and contains a principal eigenvector
v
, one easily verifies that

\mbox{int}_{X_{1}}K_{1}
is nonvoid and
v\in\mbox{int}_{X_{1}}K_{1}
.
Thus by Theorem
2.4
we deduce that the eigenvalue
1/(\lambda-s)
of
R_{\lambda}(A_{1})
has the same algebraic and geometric multiplicities. Consequently by (
2.6
) the algebraic and geometric multiplicities of the principal eigenvalue
s
of
A
coincide. Hence assertion (2) holds true.

Step 3.
The verification of assertions (3) and (4).

Let
\mu\in\sigma(A)\setminus\{s\}
,
\mbox{Re}\,\mu>s_{e}
.
Take a real number
\eta
with
s_{e}<\eta<s
such that

\mu\in\Sigma_{1}(\eta)
.
Let
X_{1}=X_{1}(\eta)
,
K_{1}=X_{1}\cap K
, and
A_{1}=A|_{X_{1}}
. Then as in Step 2 it can be shown that
K_{1}
is a total cone in
X_{1}
. Furthermore, (
3.10
) remains valid for
\lambda>0
sufficiently large. Take a
\lambda>s
such that (
3.10
) holds and consider the resolvent operator
R_{\lambda}(A_{1})
of
A_{1}
on
X_{1}
.
Then by Theorem
2.4
(4), we deduce that
\mbox{int}_{X_{1}}K_{1}
does not contain eigenvectors of
R_{\lambda}(A_{1})
pertaining to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{\mu}:=1/(\lambda-\mu)
. Now if
A
has an eigenvector
v\in\mbox{int}_{Y}K
corresponding to
\mu
. Then one easily verify that
v\in\mbox{int}_{X_{1}}K_{1}
and is an eigenvector of
R_{\lambda}(A_{1})
corresponding to
\lambda_{\mu}
. This leads to a contradiction and verifies assertion (3).

If
\mbox{Im}\,\mu\neq 0
, Theorem
2.4
(3) asserts that
\mbox{GE}_{\lambda_{\mu}}(R_{\lambda}(A_{1}))\cap K_{1}=\{0\}
. We also infer from (
2.6
) that

\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(A_{1})=\mbox{GE}_{\lambda_{\mu}}(R_{\lambda}(A_{1}))
. Therefore
\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(A)\cap K=\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(A_{1})\cap K_{1}=\{0\}
. This completes the proof of assertion (4).
\blacksquare

Remark 3.4

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
3.3
, one may expect that the boundary spectrum
\sigma_{b}(A)
consists of exactly one eigenvalue of
A
. Unfortunately the easy counterexample below indicates that this may fail to be true.

Example 3.1.
Let
X=Y=\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For computational convenience, here we make a convention that
\mathbb{R}^{3}
consists of column vectors. Denote
v^{\prime}
the transpose of a row vector
v=(x,y,z)
.
Define a cone
K
in
X
as

K=\{(x,y,z)^{\prime}\in X:\,\,z\geq\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\}.

Then
\mbox{int}_{Y}K=\{(x,y,z)^{\prime}\in X:\,\,z>\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\}
. Let

A=\left(\begin{matrix}0&-1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{matrix}\right).

For
\lambda>0
, simple computations yield

R_{\lambda}(A):=(\lambda-A)^{-1}=\left(\begin{matrix}B&O\\[4.30554pt]
O&{\lambda^{-1}}\end{matrix}\right),\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{where }\,B=\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{2}}\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda&-1\\
1&\lambda\end{matrix}\right).

Therefore

R_{\lambda}(A)u=\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{2}}\left(\lambda x-y,\,x+\lambda y,\,\frac{1+\lambda^{2}}{\lambda}z\right)^{\prime}:=\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{2}}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{z})^{\prime}.

Observe that

\tilde{x}^{2}+\tilde{y}^{2}=(x^{2}+y^{2})+\lambda^{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})=(1+\lambda^{2})(x^{2}+y^{2}).

(3.12)

Now let
u=(x,y,z)^{\prime}\in K
. Then
x^{2}+y^{2}\leq z^{2}
. Since
(1+\lambda^{2})/\lambda^{2}>1
, by (
3.12
) we deduce that

\tilde{x}^{2}+\tilde{y}^{2}\leq(1+\lambda^{2})z^{2}\leq\left(\frac{1+\lambda^{2}}{\lambda}\right)^{2}z^{2}=\tilde{z}^{2}.

This implies
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{z})^{\prime}\in K
. Thus we see that
R_{\lambda}(A)K\subset K
.

If
u\in\partial K
,
u\neq 0
, then
x^{2}+y^{2}=z^{2}\neq 0
. By (
3.12
) we find that

\tilde{x}^{2}+\tilde{y}^{2}=(1+\lambda^{2})z^{2}<\left(\frac{1+\lambda^{2}}{\lambda}\right)^{2}z^{2}=\tilde{z}^{2}.

Hence
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{z})^{\prime}\in\mbox{int}_{Y}K
. Therefore
R_{\lambda}(A)u\in\mbox{int}_{Y}K
. This indicates that the operator given by
A
satisfies all the requirements in Theorem
3.3
. However, all the eigenvalues of
A
has the same real part
s=0
.

To guarantee the uniqueness of elements in
\sigma_{b}(A)
, Nussbaum
[
18
]
used the notion of “
u_{0}
-positivity ” due to Krasnosel’skii
[
13
]
; see
[
18
, Theorem 1.3]
. Here we remark that if the semigroup
e^{tA}
has some strong positivity property, then one can still ensure the uniqueness of elements in
\sigma_{b}(A)
.

Theorem 3.5

In addition to
(A1)-(A4)
, assume that

(A5)

for any
t>0
and
\mu\in\sigma(A)
with
\mbox{Re}\,\mu>s_{e}
,

e^{tA}(K_{\mu}\setminus\{0\})\in\mbox{int}_{Y}K,\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{where }K_{\mu}=\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(A)\cap K.

Then
\sigma_{b}(A)=\{s\}
.

Proof.
Let
Y^{\prime}=\oplus_{\mu\in\sigma_{b}(A)}\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(A)
,
A^{\prime}=A|_{Y^{\prime}}
. Denote
K^{\prime}=Y^{\prime}\cap K
. Then by Theorem
3.3
we see that
K^{\prime}\neq\{0\}
. Hence
K^{\prime}
is a cone in
Y^{\prime}
. Let
v\in K^{\prime}
,
v\neq 0
. By (A5) we have
e^{tA}v\in\mbox{int}_{Y}K
for
t>0.
Since
e^{tA}v\in Y^{\prime}
, one trivially verifies that
e^{tA}v\in\mbox{int}_{Y^{\prime}}{K^{\prime}}
. Therefore
K^{\prime}
is a solid cone in
Y^{\prime}
and

e^{tA^{\prime}}(K^{\prime}\setminus\{0\})\subset\mbox{int}_{Y^{\prime}}{K^{\prime}},\hskip 28.45274ptt>0.

(3.13)

Now let
\mu:=s+{\mathbf{i}}\beta\in\sigma_{b}(A)
. Then
\lambda:=e^{\mu t}=e^{st}e^{{\mathbf{i}}\beta t}
is an eigenvalue of
e^{tA^{\prime}}
with
|\lambda|=e^{st}:=r(t)
. But (

) implies that
r(t)
is the unique eigenvalue of
e^{tA^{\prime}}
on the circle
{\mathbb{S}}_{r(t)}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}|\,\,|z|=r(t)\}
for

. Hence we necessarily have
e^{{\mathbf{i}}\beta t}=1
, and therefore
\beta t\in\{2k\pi:\,\,k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
for all

. But this is impossible unless

. This proves what we desired.
\blacksquare

4
Principal Eigenvalue Problem of Elliptic Operators Associated with Degenerate Mixed Boundary Conditions

As an illustrating example, we consider the principal eigenvalue problem of the elliptic operator
L
on a smooth bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}(n\geq 1)
:

Lu=-\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}a_{ij}(x)\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}+\sum^{n}_{i=1}b_{i}(x)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}+c(x)u,

(4.1)

which is associated with the mixed boundary condition:

Bu:=\alpha(x)u+\beta(x)\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=0

(4.2)

on the boundary
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
of
\Omega
, where
\nu
stands for the unit outward normal vector field on
\Gamma
. The coefficients of
L
and
B
are assumed to be
C^{\infty}
functions satisfying the hypotheses below:

(H1)

a_{ij}=a_{ji}
(
1\leq i,j\leq n
), and there is
\theta>0
such that

\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}a_{ij}(x)\varsigma_{i}\varsigma_{j}\geq\theta|\varsigma|^{2},\hskip 28.45274pt\forall\,\varsigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\,x\in\bar{\Omega};

(H2)

c,\alpha,\beta
are nonnegative functions satisfying that

\alpha(x)+\beta(x)>0,\hskip 28.45274pt\forall\,x\in\Gamma.

(4.3)

In the case of the Dirichet boundary condition or the Robin boundary condition (regular case), this problem has already been well understood; see e.g.
[
1
, Theorem 12.1]
,
[
4
, Theorem 1.4]
and also
[
7
,
18
]
. Here we are interested in a degenerate case where
\beta
may vanish on a proper subset of
\Gamma
. In such a situation, if
L
has a divergence form (hence
L
enjoys some symmetric properties), one can find some nice results concerning the principal eigenvalue problem of
L
in Taira
[
21
, Theorem 1.2]
. As an application of the abstract results given in Section
3
, we deal with the general case and present a less involved argument on the problem.

\bullet
Some fundamental results.
First, making use of the classical Hopf’s lemma, one can easily verify the comparison result below:

Lemma 4.1

Let
u\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})\cap C^{2}(\Omega)
,
u\not\equiv 0
. Assume that

Lu+\lambda u\geq 0\,\,(\mbox{in }\Omega),\hskip 14.22636ptBu\geq 0\,\,(\mbox{on }\Gamma),

where
\lambda\geq 0
. Then
u(x)>0
for
x\in\Omega
.

Denote by
W^{s,p}(\Omega)
(
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
1\leq p<\infty
) the Sobolev spaces equipped with the standard norms. We infer from
Taira
[
21
, pp.5, Theorem 1]
that the following existence and uniqueness result holds true.

Lemma 4.2

Let
1<p<\infty
,
s>1+1/p
, and let
\lambda\geq 0
. Then for any
f\in W^{s-2,p}(\Omega)
, the homogeneous boundary value problem

Lu+\lambda u=f\mbox{ $($in\,\,$\Omega$$)$},\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{ $Bu=0$ $($on\,\,$\Gamma$$)$}

(4.4)

has a unique solution
u\in W^{s,p}(\Omega)
. Here the boundary condition is understood in the sense that
B
can be viewed as a linear operator from
W^{s,p}(\Omega)
to Besov space
B_{*}^{s-1-1/p,\,p}(\Gamma)
(see
[
21
]
pp. 3 for details).

Note that Lemma
4.2
implies that if
f\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
, then the solution
u
of (
4.4
) belongs to
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
, and hence it solves (
4.4
) in the classical sense. Indeed, if
f\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
then
f\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
for any
1<p<\infty
. Lemma
4.2
then asserts that
u\in W^{3,p}(\Omega)
. Taking a number
p>1
sufficiently large so that
W^{3,p}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
, one immediately concludes that
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
.

By virtue of
[
21
, pp.4, Theorem 1]
we also deduce that

\|u\|_{C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\|u\|_{W^{3,p}(\Omega)}\leq C\|f\|_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}\leq C\|f\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})}

(4.5)

for all
f\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
, where
C
denotes a general constant independent of
f
.

\bullet
Resolvent strong positivity of
A=-L
.
Let
X=L^{2}(\Omega)
, and set

Y=\{u\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}):\,\,\mbox{$u$ satisfies \eqref{e:bc}}\}.

Y
is equipped with the usual norm of
C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
. Clearly
Y\hookrightarrow X
.

Let
K
be the positive cone in
Y
consisting of nonnegative functions.

Denote by
A
the operator
-L
with domain

{\mathcal{D}}(A)=\{u\in H^{2}(\Omega):\,\,Bu=0\},

where the boundary condition
Bu=0
is understood in the same sense as in Lemma
4.2
. Invoking
[
21
, pp.5, Theorem 2]
we deduce that
-A
is a sectorial operator in
X
with compact resolvent. Hence by
[
Kry
, Ex. 2.4, (i)]
it is easy to see that

\mbox{spb}_{e}(A)=-\infty<\mbox{spb}(A)<\infty.

Thus
A
fulfills (A2) in Section
3
.

We infer from Lemma
4.2
and (
4.5
) that
R_{\lambda}(A)Y\subset Y
for
\lambda\geq 0
; furthermore,
R_{\lambda}(A)|_{Y}
is compact as an operator on the space
Y
.

The following result indicates that
A
fulfills hypotheses (A1) and (A4) in Section
3
. The proof of such a result is somewhat standard. We include the details in the Appendix part for the readers’ convenience.

Lemma 4.3

R_{\lambda}(A)(K\setminus\{0\})\subset\mbox{int}_{Y}K
for each
\lambda\geq 0
.

\bullet
Regularity of the generalized eigenfunctions.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be the complexification of
A
with
{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{A})={\mathcal{D}}(A)+{\mathbf{i}}{\mathcal{D}}(A)
.
We start with the eigenfunctions of
\mathbb{A}
. Let
\mu=a+{\mathbf{i}}b\in\sigma(A)
, and let
w=u+{\mathbf{i}}v
be a corresponding eigenfunction of
\mathbb{A}
, where
u,v\in D(A)
. Then

\mathbb{A}w=\mu w
amounts to say that

Au=au-bv,\hskip 14.22636ptAv=av+bu.

(4.6)

Since
u,v\in H^{2}(\Omega)
, by Lemma
4.2
and (
4.6
) one finds that
u,v\in H^{4}(\Omega)
. Further by a simple bootstrap argument we finally conclude that
u,v\in H^{s}(\Omega)
for all
s\geq 0
. It follows by the Sobolev embeddings that
u,v\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
.

Now let
g\in\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})
and
\mbox{rank}\,(g)\geq 2
. Set
k=\mbox{rank}\,(g)-1
. Then
(\mathbb{A}-\mu)^{k}g:=w
is an eigenfunction of
\mathbb{A}
. Hence
w
a
C^{\infty}
function on
\overline{\Omega}
.

Note that it is readily implied in
(\mathbb{A}-\mu)^{k}g=w
that
(\mathbb{A}-\mu)^{j}g\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{A})
for all
j\leq k
. In particular,

(\mathbb{A}-\mu)^{k-1}g:=f_{1}\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{A})\subset{\mathbb{H}}^{2}(\Omega):=H^{s}(\Omega)+{\mathbf{i}}H^{s}(\Omega).

Therefore by
(\mathbb{A}-\mu)f_{1}=w
we find that

\mathbb{A}f_{1}=w+\mu f_{1}:=\tilde{f}_{1}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{2}(\Omega).

(4.7)

It follows by Lemma
4.2
that
f_{1}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{4}(\Omega)
. This in turn implies that
\tilde{f}_{1}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{4}(\Omega)
. (
4.7
) and Lemma
4.2
then asserts that
f_{1}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{6}(\Omega)
. Once again by a bootstrap argument we see that
f_{1}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{s}(\Omega)
for all
s\geq 0
.

Repeating the above argument with
w
and
f_{1}
therein replaced by
f_{1}
and
(\mathbb{A}-\mu)^{k-2}g:=f_{2}
, respectively, one deduces that
f_{2}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{s}(\Omega)
for
s\geq 0
. Continuing this procedure we finally obtain that
g=f_{k}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{s}(\Omega)
for all
s\geq 0
. The Sobolev embeddings then immediately imply that
g
is a
C^{\infty}
function.

It follows from the above results that
\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(A)\subset Y
for any
\mu\in\sigma(A)
. Hence
A
fulfills hypothesis (A3).

By far we have seen that the operator
A
satisfies hypotheses (A1)-(A4).

\bullet
The verification of hypothesis (A5).

Let
\mu\in\sigma(A)
. Denote by
\mathbb{A}_{\mu}
the restriction of
\mathbb{A}
on
\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})
. Given
g\in\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})
, let
u=u(t)
be the solution of equation
\dot{u}=\mathbb{A}u
with
u(0)=g
. Then
u(t)=e^{t\mathbb{A}}g=e^{t\mathbb{A}_{\mu}}g
. Since
\mathbb{A}_{\mu}
is a bounded operator on
\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})
, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}u(t)&=e^{t\mathbb{A}_{\mu}}g=e^{\mu t}e^{t(\mathbb{A}_{\mu}-\mu)}g=e^{\mu t}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{j}}{j!}(\mathbb{A}_{\mu}-\mu)^{j}g\\[4.30554pt]
&=e^{\mu t}\left(I+\frac{t}{1!}(\mathbb{A}_{\mu}-\mu)+\cdots+\frac{t^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}(\mathbb{A}_{\mu}-\mu)^{(k-1)}\right)g,\end{array}

(4.8)

where
k=\mbox{rank}\,(g)
. Noticing that
(\mathbb{A}_{\mu}-\mu)^{j}g\in\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(\mathbb{A})
for any integer
j\geq 0
, by what we have proved above it is clear that
(\mathbb{A}_{\mu}-\mu)^{j}g
is a
C^{\infty}
function. Consequently for each
t\geq 0
fixed,
u(t)
is a
C^{\infty}
function in the space variable on
\overline{\Omega}
.
We write
u(t,x)=u(t)(x)
for
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\overline{\Omega}
. Then it can be easily seen that
u=u(t,x)
is a complex
C^{\infty}
function on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\overline{\Omega}
.

Now we come back to the real situation. The above result implies that for each
v_{0}\in\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(A)
, the function
v(t,x):=v(t)(x)
, where
v(t)=e^{tA}v_{0}
, is a
C^{\infty}
function on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\overline{\Omega}
.
Therefore
v
is a classical solution of the parabolic equation:

\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}+Lv=0,\hskip 28.45274ptx\in\Omega,\,\,t>0

(4.9)

associated with the boundary condition
Bv=0
on
\Gamma
. Thanks to the Hopf’s lemma for parabolic equations (see e.g. Friedman
[
6
, Chap. 2, Theorem 14]
or Smith
[
20
, Chap. 7, Theorem 2.2]
), using almost the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
4.3
(see the Appendix), it can be shown that
v(t,\cdot)\in\mbox{int}_{Y}K
if
v_{0}\in K
,
v_{0}\neq 0
. This is precisely what we desired.

Now that
A
satisfies hypotheses
(A1)-(A5)
, as a straightforward application of Theorems
3.3
and
3.5
, one immediately obtains the following result.

Theorem 4.4

The following assertions hold true:

(1)

The spectral bound
s
of
A
is an algebraically simple eigenvalue with a corresponding eigenvector
w\in\mbox{int}_{Y}K
.

(2)

\mbox{GE}_{\mu}(A)\cap K=\{0\}
for any
\mu\in\sigma(A)\setminus\{s\}
.

(3)

\sigma_{b}(A)=\{s\}

Remark 4.5
