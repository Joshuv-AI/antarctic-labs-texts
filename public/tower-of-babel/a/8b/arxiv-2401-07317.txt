Remarks on some Limit Geometric Properties related to an Idempotent and Non-Associative Algebraic Structure
Walter Briec

Abstract
This article analyzes the geometric properties of an idempotent, non-associative algebraic structure that extends the Max-Times semiring. This algebraic structure is useful for studying systems of Max-Times and Max-Plus equations, employing an appropriate notion of a non-associative determinant. We consider a connected ultrametric distance and demonstrate that it implies, among other properties, an analogue of the Pythagorean relation. To this end, we introduce a suitable notion of a right angle between two vectors and investigate a trigonometric concept associated with the Chebyshev unit ball. Following this approach, we explore the potential implications of these properties in the complex plane. We provide an algebraic definition of a line passing through two points, which corresponds to the Painlevé-Peano-Kuratowski limit of a sequence of generalized lines. We establish that this definition leads to distinctive geometric properties; in particular, two distinct parallel lines may share an infinite number of points.

Introduction

It is well known that algebras associated with idempotent semirings are related to particular geometric representations. This article investigates some of these properties through the non-associative symmetrization of Max-Times algebras. The concept of
\mathbb{B}
-convexity was introduced in
[
12
]
as a Painlevé-Kuratowski limit of generalized convexities.

The letter
\mathbb{B}

It was chosen due to the origins of the names of several members of the mathematics department at the University of Perpignan at the time of the publication of the first paper
[

Hahn-Banach-type separation properties

-convexity was previously limited to the Max-Times semi-module

8

Note that there exist other approaches to extending idempotent algebraic structures. For example, Izhakian and Rowen

Relaxing associativity preserves both symmetry and idempotency. It has been shown that this notion of convexity can be equivalently characterized by the Kuratowski-Painlevé limit of the generalized convex hull of two points as defined in

More recently, these structures have been examined from an algebraic perspective to compute the limit solutions of systems of Max-Times equations, particularly by introducing a special determinant formulation. These results also have geometric implications for determining the equation of a limit hyperplane passing through
n
points, with possible extensions to Max-Plus algebra.

This paper continues prior research on this topic from a geometric perspective, introducing appropriate notions of distance. Throughout the paper, an idempotent analogue of subtraction plays a key role.

First, we define a space notion suited to the algebraic structure under consideration and generalize the definition of
\mathbb{B}
-space introduced in
[
13
]
. Second, we construct a sequence of generalized metrics via an appropriate homeomorphic transformation of the vector space structure. We demonstrate that their limit properties yield an ultrametric distance intimately linked to the algebraic structure considered. To this end, we derive a notion of orthogonality from the limit inner product defined in
[
8
]
. As a result, we establish geometric properties of a special class of right-angle triples, specifically proving a Pythagorean-type relation within this framework. Using the limiting properties of the generalized inner product, we construct a pseudo-cosine limit function, where the pseudo-cosine is derived from the limiting inner product, and the pseudo-sine stems from the two-dimensional determinant formula established in
[
11
]
. Following this approach, we explore potential implications of these properties in the complex plane.

Finally, the paper investigates a notion of line corresponding to the non-associative algebraic structure under study. We show that the Painlevé-Kuratowski limit of a sequence of generalized lines admits an algebraic description extending a previous result in
[
8
]
for a line segment. We establish a condition ensuring that two boundary lines are parallel, analogous (in a certain sense) to the criterion for parallel lines in a vector space. The paper concludes with an analysis of the two-dimensional case, in which a special idempotent geometry is examined. Building on
[
11
]
, we demonstrate how to compute the equation of a two-dimensional line. The final result describes a plane geometry where a unique line passes through two distinct points, and where a line segment can be extended to a half-line. However, two distinct parallel lines may share an infinite number of points.

The paper is structured as follows. Section 1 introduces the framework of the non-associative idempotent algebraic structure defined in
[
8
]
, along with some associated convexity notions. Section 2 defines field and space concepts related to this algebraic structure, providing illustrative examples. Section 3 derives an ultrametric distance as the limit of a sequence of generalized metrics. In this context, a triangular equality is established for a special class of right-angle triples. Section 4 explores inner product properties established in
[
9
]
and introduces a related notion of pseudo-cosine. Section 5 presents an algebraic description of a line. The paper concludes with an analysis of the geometric implications of the considered algebraic structure.

Definition 2.1.1

A triple
(K,\boxplus_{K},\cdot_{K})
is an idempotent pseudo-field
if:

(a)
There exits a binary operation
\boxplus_{K}:K\times K\longrightarrow K
that satisfies the following properties:
(i)
for all
\lambda\in K
we have
\lambda\boxplus_{K}\lambda=\lambda
(idempotence);
(ii)
for all
(\lambda,\mu)\in K\times K
we have
\lambda\boxplus_{K}\mu=\mu\boxplus_{K}\lambda
(commutativity);
(iii)
there exists a neutral element
0_{K}\in K
such that
\lambda\boxplus_{K}0_{K}=0_{K}\boxplus_{K}\lambda=\lambda
for all
\lambda\in K
;
(iv)
every
\lambda\in K
has a symmetric element
-\lambda
such that
(-\lambda)\boxplus_{K}\lambda=\lambda\boxplus_{K}(-\lambda)=0_{X}
(symmetry);
(v)
for any mutually non-symmetric triple of elements
(\lambda,\mu,\eta)\in K^{3}
,
(\lambda\boxplus_{K}\mu)\boxplus_{K}\eta=\lambda\boxplus_{K}(\mu\boxplus_{K}\eta)
(weakened form of associativity).

(b)
There exists a scalar multiplication
\cdot_{K}:K\times K\longrightarrow K
that satisfies the following properties:
(i)
for all
\lambda\in K
and all
(\lambda,\mu)\in K\times K
, we
have
\lambda\cdot_{K}\mu=\mu\cdot_{K}\lambda
(commutativity);
(ii)
for all
\lambda,\mu,\eta\in K\times K\times K
we have
(\lambda\cdot_{K}\mu)\cdot_{K}\eta=\lambda\cdot_{K}(\mu\cdot_{K}\eta)
(associativity);
(iii)
there is an element
1_{K}\in K
such that for all
\lambda\in K
we have
1_{K}\cdot_{K}\lambda=\lambda
;
(iv)
for all
\lambda\in K\backslash\{0_{K}\}
there exists an element
\lambda^{-1}
such that
\lambda\cdot_{K}\lambda^{-1}=1_{K}
.

(c)
The multiplication
\cdot_{K}
is distributive over the operation
\boxplus_{K}
: for all
\lambda\in K
and all
(\mu,\nu)\in K\times K
, we
have

\lambda\cdot_{K}(\mu\boxplus_{K}\nu)=(\lambda\cdot_{K}\mu)\boxplus_{K}(\lambda\cdot_{K}\nu).

d_{\boxplus}\big(x^{(1)},x^{(m)}\big)=\max\big\{d_{\boxplus}\big(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\big),d_{\boxplus}\big(x^{(2)},x^{(m)})\big)\big\}.

From the decomposition property established in
[
10
]
, we have
Co^{\infty}\big(x^{(k)},x^{(m)})=Co^{\infty}\big(x^{(k)},x^{(k+1)})\cup Co^{\infty}\big(x^{(k+1)},x^{(m)})
. It follows that for any
k\in[m-1]
,
x^{(k+1)}\in Co^{\infty}\big(x^{(k)},x^{(m)}\big)
. Hence:

d_{\boxplus}\big(x^{(k)},x^{(m)}\big)=\max\Big\{d_{\boxplus}\big(x^{(k)},x^{(k+1)}\big),d_{\boxplus}\big(x^{(k+1)},x^{(m)}\big)\Big\}.

By recurrence the proof is then immediate.
\Box

Proposition 2.3.6

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
x=-y
, then for all
z\in Co^{\infty}(x,y)\backslash\{x,y\}
,
d_{\boxplus}(x,y)=d_{\boxplus}(x,z)=d_{\boxplus}(z,y).

Proof:
Suppose that
z\in Co^{\infty}\big(x,y)
, in such a case there are some
r,s,t,w\in[0,1]
with
\max\{r,s,t,w\}=1
such that

z=rx\boxplus sx\boxplus ty\boxplus wy.
Since
z\notin\{x,y\}
we have
\max\{r,s\}=\max\{t,w\}=1
, and
\min\{r,s\}=\max\{t,w\}=1
, otherwise either
z=x
or
z=y
. If
r=s=t=w=1
, then
z=0
and the result is immediate. Consequently
\max\{r,s\}=\max\{t,w\}=1
,
\min\{r,s\}<1
and

\min\{t,w\}<1
. Dropping the symmetric terms, it follows that

z=rx\boxplus sx\boxplus ty\boxplus wy=\min\{r,s\}x\boxplus\min\{t,w\}y.

Since
\min\{r,s\}<1
and
\min\{t,w\}<1
, this implies that
d_{\boxplus}(x,y)=d_{\boxplus}(x,z)=d_{\boxplus}(z,y).
\Box

3
Some Trigonometric Properties in Limit

In this section, we show that certain properties related to the notion of orthogonality can be derived within the framework of the idempotent and symmetric algebraic structure we consider.

3.1
Orthogonality and Pythagorean Properties in Limit

In the following, for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
, we say that
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are
\varphi_{p}
-orthogonal
if:

\langle x,y\rangle_{p}=0.

(49)

In addition, we say that
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are
\digamma
-orthogonal
if:

\langle x,y\rangle_{\infty}=0.

(50)

In the sequel, the earlier results are extended to the general case of a triple
[x,y,z]
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Notice that we have for all natural numbers
p

\big\langle x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z,y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z\big\rangle_{p}=\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varphi_{p}}}{{\sum_{i\in[n]}}}x_{i}y_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varphi_{p}}}{{\sum_{i\in[n]}}}(-x_{i}z_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varphi_{p}}}{{\sum_{i\in[n]}}}(-y_{i}z_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varphi_{p}}}{{\sum_{i\in[n]}}}{z_{i}}^{2}.

(51)

Therefore:

\displaystyle\lim

{}_{p\longrightarrow\infty}\big\langle x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z,y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z\big\rangle_{p}=\digamma_{[4n]}\big(x_{1}y_{1},...,x_{n}y_{n},-x_{1}z_{1},...,-x_{n}z_{n},-y_{1}z_{1},...,-y_{n}z_{n},{z_{1}}^{2},...,{z_{n}}^{2}\big).

(52)

In the following, we introduce the operation
\langle\!\langle\cdot,\cdot,\cdot\rangle\!\rangle_{\boxplus}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as:

\langle\!\langle x,y,z\rangle\!\rangle_{\infty}=\lim_{p\longrightarrow\infty}\big\langle x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z,y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z\big\rangle_{p}.

(53)

In the following, for all
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we say that
[x,y,z]
is
\digamma
-
right-angled in
z
if

\langle\!\langle x,y,z\rangle\!\rangle_{\infty}=0.

(54)

In general, a right-angled triangle is not a
\digamma
-right-angled triple. This is shown in the next example.

Example 3.1.1

Let

and

x-z=(-2,-4,2)
and

. Therefore

is a right-angled triangle.However

\digamma_{{[12]}}(2,-6,2,3,2,-3,-6,-6,-6,9,4,9)=9\not=0
. Hence

is not a
\digamma
-right-angled triple.

The next result was established in
[
10
]
. It plays an important role in the remainder. It means that if the limit sum is zero then the

-generalized sum is zero for any

Suppose that there is some

. Then for all

,
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varphi_{p}}}{{\sum_{i\in[n]}}}x_{i}=0

Proposition 3.1.3

Let

, and let

be a triple.
The triple

is
\digamma
-right-angled in

is

\big\langle x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z,y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z\big\rangle_{p}=0
.

Proof:
If
[x,y,z]
is a
\digamma
-right-angled triple in

then:

\langle\!\langle x,y,z\rangle\!\rangle_{\infty}=0.

It follows that

\big\langle x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z,y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z\big\rangle_{p}=0

for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
and the first implication follows.

Conversely if for all
p
,
[x,y,z]
is a
\varphi_{p}
-right-angled triple in

, then for all

\big\langle x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z,y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z\big\rangle_{p}=0.

Hence

\lim_{p\longrightarrow\infty}\big\langle x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z,y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z\big\rangle_{p}=\langle\!\langle x,y,z\rangle\!\rangle_{\infty}=0,

Proposition 3.1.5

Let

\digamma
-right-angled triple in

with

,
\Card\,(J)\in\{0,1\}
and such that for all

\Big[x_{[I_{k}]},y_{[I_{k}]},0_{[I_{k}]}\Big]

of
\mathbb{R}^{I_{k}}

and

Corollary 3.1.6

Suppose that

. If

is a right-angled triangle in

, then

.

The following result is an idempotent and non-associative analogue of the Pythagorean theorem.

Proposition 3.1.7

Let
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
[x,y,z]
is a
\digamma
-right-angled triple in
z
. Then for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
:

d^{2}_{{\varphi_{p}}}(x,y)=d^{2}_{{\varphi_{p}}}(x,z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}d^{2}_{{\varphi_{p}}}(y,z),

and

d_{\boxplus}(x,y)=\max\{d_{\boxplus}(x,z),d_{\boxplus}(y,z)\}.

Proof:
If
[x,y,z]
is a
\digamma
-right-angled triple in
z
then

\langle\!\langle x,y,z\rangle\!\rangle_{\infty}=0.

Thus, for all
p
,
\langle x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z,y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z\rangle_{p}=0
. It follows that

\langle\phi_{p}(x)-\phi_{p}(z),\phi_{p}(y)-\phi_{p}(z)\rangle=0.

Hence

\|\phi_{p}(x)-\phi_{p}(y)\|^{2}=\|\phi_{p}(x)-\phi_{p}(z)\|^{2}+\|\phi_{p}(y)-\phi_{p}(z)\|^{2}.

We deduce that

\displaystyle\Big\|\phi_{p}\Big(\phi_{p}^{-1}\big(\phi_{p}(x)-\phi_{p}(y)\big)\Big)\Big\|^{2}=\varphi_{p}\Big(\varphi_{p}^{-1}(\|\phi_{p}\Big(\phi_{p}^{-1}\big(\phi_{p}(x)-\phi_{p}(z)\big)\Big)\|^{2})\Big)+\varphi_{p}\Big(\varphi_{p}^{-1}(\|\phi_{p}\Big(\phi_{p}^{-1}\big(\phi_{p}(y)-\phi_{p}(z)\big)\Big)\|^{2}\big)\Big).

Thus:

d^{2}_{{\varphi_{p}}}(x,y)=\|x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}y\|_{{\varphi_{p}}}^{2}=\|x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z\|_{{\varphi_{p}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}\|y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}z\|_{{\varphi_{p}}}=d^{2}_{{\varphi_{p}}}(x,z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}d^{2}_{{\varphi_{p}}}(y,z).

Taking the limit, yields:

d^{2}_{\boxplus}(x,y)=\lim_{p\longrightarrow\infty}d^{2}_{{\varphi_{p}}}(x,y)=\lim_{p\longrightarrow\infty}d^{2}_{{\varphi_{p}}}(x,z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}d^{2}_{{\varphi_{p}}}(y,z)=\max\{d^{2}_{\boxplus}(x,z),d^{2}_{\boxplus}(y,z)\}.

The final statement immediately follows.
\Box

3.2
Cosine, Sine in Limit and some Parametrization of the Unit Square

The purpose of this section is to demonstrate that we can use the limiting properties of standard trigonometric concepts to derive analogues of well-known geometric properties within the framework of the non-associative algebra we are studying.
Paralleling the usual definition of a cosine, let us define the
\varphi_{p}
-pseudo-cosine of the angle between two vectors as:

\cos_{p}(x,y)=\frac{\langle x,y\rangle_{p}}{\|x\|_{{\varphi_{p}}}\|y\|_{{\varphi_{p}}}}.

(55)

Taking the limit, we define the pseudo
\digamma
-cosine as:

\cos_{\infty}(x,y)=\frac{\langle x,y\rangle_{\infty}}{\|x\|_{\infty}\|y\|_{\infty}}.

(56)

It is now natural to define a sine function in limit. In
[
11
]
a
\varphi_{p}
exterior product is defined for all
v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

as:

\big(v_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\wedge}}v_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\wedge}}\cdots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\wedge}}v_{n}\big)=|v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{n}|_{p}\big(e_{1}{\wedge}e_{2}{\wedge}\cdots{\wedge}e_{n}\big){,}

(57)

where:

\displaystyle\big(v_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\wedge}}v_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\wedge}}\cdots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\wedge}}v_{n}\big)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{p}^{-1}\big(\phi_{p}(v_{1}){\wedge}\phi_{p}(v_{2}){\wedge}\cdots{\wedge}\phi_{p}(v_{n})\ \big)

(58)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{p}^{-1}\Big(|\phi_{p}(v_{1}),\phi_{p}(v_{2}),...,\phi_{p}(v_{n})|\Big)\big(e_{1}{\wedge}e_{2}{\wedge}\cdots{\wedge}e_{n}\big).

(59)

Taking the limit yields:

\big(v_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\infty}}{{\wedge}}v_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\infty}}{{\wedge}}\cdots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\infty}}{{\wedge}}v_{n}\big)=|v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{n}|_{\infty}\big(e_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\infty}}{{\wedge}}e_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\infty}}{{\wedge}}\cdots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\infty}}{{\wedge}}e_{n}\big).

(60)

In the case of two vectors the exterior product can be identified to the exterior product and the oriented area of the cross product can be interpreted as the positive area of the parallelogram having
x
and
y
as sides:

{x\wedge y=\left|x,y\right|=\left\|x\right\|\left\|y\right\|\sin(x,y).}

(61)

The
\varphi_{p}
-sine function for two vectors
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is defined as

\sin_{p}(x,y)=\frac{|x,y|_{p}}{{\|x\|_{{\varphi_{p}}}\|y\|_{{\varphi_{p}}}}}=\frac{\varphi_{p}^{-1}\big(\varphi_{p}(x_{1})\varphi_{p}(y_{2})-\varphi_{p}(x_{2})\varphi_{p}(y_{1})\big)}{{\|x\|_{{\varphi_{p}}}\|y\|_{{\varphi_{p}}}}}.

(62)

Along this line, let us define

\sin_{\infty}(x,y)=\frac{|x,y|_{\infty}}{\|x\|_{\infty}\|y\|_{\infty}}=\lim_{p\longrightarrow\infty}\sin_{p}(x,y)=\frac{x_{1}y_{2}\boxminus x_{2}y_{1}}{\max\{|x_{1},x_{2}|\}\max\{|y_{1},y_{2}|\}}.

(63)

Lemma 3.2.1

For all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have the following properties.

(a)

\cos_{p}(x,y)=\varphi_{p}^{-1}\big(\cos\big(\phi_{p}(x),\phi_{p}(y))\big)
and
\sin_{p}(x,y)=\varphi_{p}^{-1}\big(\sin(\phi_{p}(x),\phi_{p}(y))\big).

(b)

{\cos_{p}(x,y)}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}{\sin_{p}(x,y)}^{2}=1
.

(c)

\max\{|\cos_{\infty}(x,y)|,|\sin_{\infty}(x,y)|\}=1
.

Proof:

(a)
The first statement is immediate. To prove the second part note that:

\sin_{p}(x,y)=\frac{|x,y|_{p}}{{\|x\|_{{\varphi_{p}}}\|y\|_{{\varphi_{p}}}}}=\frac{\varphi_{p}^{-1}\big(|\phi_{p}(x),\phi_{p}(y)|)}{{\varphi_{p}^{-1}(\|\phi_{p}(x)\|)\varphi_{p}^{-1}(\|\phi_{p}(y)\|)}}.

Therefore

\sin_{p}(x,y)=\varphi_{p}^{-1}\Big(\frac{|\phi_{p}(x),\phi_{p}(y)|}{{\|\phi_{p}(x)\|\|\phi_{p}(y)\|}}\Big)=\varphi_{p}^{-1}\big(\sin(\phi_{p}(x),\phi_{p}(y))\big).

(b)
We have

\displaystyle{\cos_{p}(x,y)}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}{\sin_{p}(x,y)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\varphi_{p}\Big(\big[\varphi_{p}^{-1}\big(\cos\big(\phi_{p}(x),\phi_{p}(y))\big)\big]^{2}\Big)+\varphi_{p}\Big(\big[\varphi_{p}^{-1}\big(\sin\big(\phi_{p}(x),\phi_{p}(y))\big)\big]^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{p}\Big(\varphi_{p}^{-1}\big[\big(\cos\big(\phi_{p}(x),\phi_{p}(y))\big]^{2}\big)\Big)+\varphi_{p}\Big(\varphi_{p}^{-1}\big(\big[\sin\big(\phi_{p}(x),\phi_{p}(y))\big]^{2}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\big(\cos\big(\phi_{p}(x),\phi_{p}(y)))\big)^{2}+\big(\sin\big(\phi_{p}(x),\phi_{p}(y))\big)^{2}=1.

(c)
From
(a)
and
(b)
, we have

\max\{|\cos_{\infty}(x,y)|^{2},|\sin_{\infty}(x,y)|^{2}\}=\lim_{p\longrightarrow\infty}{\cos_{p}(x,y)}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}{\sin_{p}(x,y)}^{2}=1.

Therefore,

\max\{|\cos_{\infty}(x,y)|,|\sin_{\infty}(x,y)|\}=1.\quad\Box

Let
C_{\infty}(0,1]=\big\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\max\{|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|\}=1\big\}
be the circle defined with respect to the
\|\,\|_{\infty}

\mathbb{R}^{2}

(64)

\alpha:C_{\infty}(0,1]\longrightarrow[0,8]

\alpha(x_{1},x_{2})=\left\{\begin{matrix}x_{2}&\text{ if }&x\in[a,b]\\

3.3
Some Formalism on the Complex Scalar Field

We can continue the previous analogies, this time using complex numbers and studying the relative properties of the unit square.
Let us denote
C_{{\varphi_{p}}}(0,1]=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|x\|_{{\varphi_{p}}}=1\}
. We have the relations
\phi_{p}\Big(C_{{\varphi_{p}}}(0,1]\Big)=C(0,1]
.
Now, let us consider the subsets of
\mathbb{C}
defined as
C^{\natural}_{{\varphi_{p}}}(0,1]=\{z=a+\mathrm{i}b\in\mathbb{C}:\|(a,b)\|_{{\varphi_{p}}}=1\}
and

C^{\natural}_{\infty}(0,1]=\{z=a+\mathrm{i}b\in\mathbb{C}:\|(a,b)\|_{\infty}=1\}
. Let us consider the bijective map
\varphi^{\natural}:\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined as:

\varphi_{p}^{\natural}(a+\mathrm{i}b)=\varphi_{p}(a)+\mathrm{i}\varphi_{p}(b).

(71)

Its reciprocal is the map
{\varphi^{\natural}}^{-1}:\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined as

{\varphi_{p}^{\natural}}^{-1}(a+\mathrm{i}b)=\varphi_{p}^{-1}(a)+\mathrm{i}\varphi_{p}^{-1}(b).
The map
\varphi_{p}^{\natural}
is homogeneous with respect to the real scalar field, i.e. for all
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
,
\varphi_{p}^{\natural}\big(\rho(a+\mathrm{i}b)\big)=\rho\big(\varphi_{p}(a)+\mathrm{i}\varphi_{p}(b)\big)=\rho\varphi_{p}^{\sharp}\big(a+\mathrm{i}b\big).
Therefore, for all
z=\rho e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\in\mathbb{C}
, with
\rho=|z|
, we have

{\varphi_{p}^{\natural}}^{-1}(z)=\rho\big(\varphi_{p}^{-1}(\cos\theta)+\mathrm{i}\varphi_{p}^{-1}(\sin\theta)\big).

(72)

For all
z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
, let us define:

z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\cdot}}w={\varphi_{p}^{\natural}}^{-1}\Big(\varphi_{p}^{\natural}(z)\varphi_{p}^{\natural}(w)\Big)

(73)

It follows that if
z=a+\mathrm{i}\,b
and
w=c+\mathrm{i}d
, then:

z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\cdot}}w=\varphi_{p}^{-1}\Big(\varphi_{p}(a)\varphi_{p}(b)-\varphi_{p}(b)\varphi_{p}(d)\Big)+\mathrm{i}\,\varphi_{p}^{-1}\Big(\varphi_{p}(a)\varphi_{p}(c)+\varphi_{p}(b)\varphi_{p}(c)\Big).

(74)

Equivalently

z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\cdot}}w=(ab\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{-}}bd)+\mathrm{i}\,(ad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}bc).

(75)

Along this line, taking the limit with respect to the product topology of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we define the product:

z\boxtimes w=\lim_{p\longrightarrow\infty}z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\cdot}}w=(ab\boxminus bd)+\mathrm{i}\,(ad\boxplus bc).

(76)

Note that this product is equivalent to the matrix product defined in
[
11
]
for matrices representing complex numbers. We have for all complex numbers of the form
z=a+\mathrm{i}\,b
and
w=c+\mathrm{i}\,d
:

\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\
b&a\end{pmatrix}\boxtimes\begin{pmatrix}c&-d\\
d&c\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}ac\boxminus bd&-(ad\boxplus bc)\\
ad\boxplus bc&ac\boxminus bd\end{pmatrix}.

(77)

Moreover, for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
, and all
z=a+\mathrm{i}b\in\mathbb{C}
we define the
\varphi_{p}
-module of
z
as
|z|_{p}=\|(a,b)\|_{{\varphi_{p}}}
. Similarly,

|z|_{\infty}=\|(a,b)\|_{\infty}=\max\{|a|,|b|\}
. In the following, we also extend the operation
\boxplus
to the complex numbers. For all
z=a+\mathrm{i}b
and
w=c+\mathrm{i}d
, we have :

z\boxplus w=(a\boxplus c)+\mathrm{i}(b\boxplus d).

(78)

We can then establish the following relations.

Lemma 3.3.1

We have the following properties:

(a)
For all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, there exists some
\theta\in[0,8]
such that

z=\|z\|_{\infty}(\mathrm{pcos}(\theta)+\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{psin}(\theta))

(b)
If
z=a+\mathrm{i}\,b
then
\sqrt{z\boxtimes\bar{z}}=|z|_{\infty}=\max\{|a|,|b|\}
.

(c)
If
z\not=0
, then
z\cdot\frac{1}{|z|_{\infty}^{2}}\bar{z}=1.

(d)
Suppose that
z,w\in C^{\natural}_{\infty}(0,1]
, then
z\boxtimes w\in C^{\natural}_{\infty}(0,1]
.

(e)
Suppose that
z,w\in\mathbb{C}
, then
|z\boxtimes w|_{\infty}=|z|_{\infty}|w|_{\infty}
.

Proof:

(a)
Suppose that
z=a+\mathrm{i}\,b
. If
z=0
, the result is trivial. If
z\not=0
then

z=\max\{|a|,|b|\}\frac{a+\mathrm{i}\,b}{\max\{|a|,|b|\}}=|z|_{\infty}\Big(\frac{a}{\max\{|a|,|b|\}}+\mathrm{i}\frac{b}{\max\{|a|,|b|\}}\Big).

However
\Big(\frac{a}{\max\{|a|,|b|\}}+\mathrm{i}\frac{b}{\max\{|a|,|b|\}}\Big)\in C_{\infty}^{\natural}(0,1].
Hence there is some
\theta\in[0,8]
such that

\Big(\frac{a}{\max\{|a|,|b|\}}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\,b}{\max\{|a|,|b|\}}\Big)=\mathrm{pcos}\theta+\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{psin}\theta.

Hence

z=\|z\|_{\infty}\big(\mathrm{pcos}(\theta)+\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{psin}(\theta)\big).

(b)
We have
z\boxtimes\bar{z}=(a+\mathrm{i}\,b)\boxtimes(a-\mathrm{i}\,b)=(a^{2}\boxplus b^{2})+\mathrm{i}\,(ab\boxminus ab)=a^{2}\boxplus b^{2}=\max\{|a|,|b|\}^{2}
which yields the result.
(c)
is immediate from
(b)
.

(d)
Suppose that

z=a+\mathrm{i}\,b
and
w=c+\mathrm{i}\,d
. We first assume that
z,w\in C^{\natural}_{\infty}(0,1].
This implies that
\max\{|a|,|b|\}=\max\{|c|,|d|\}=1
. By definition we have:

z\boxtimes w=\big(ac\boxminus bd\big)+\mathrm{i}\,\big(ad\boxplus bc\big).

We need to prove that
\max\big\{|ac\boxminus bd|,|ad\boxplus bc|\big\}=1.
We consider the following cases:

(i)
if
|a|<1
and
|c|<1
then
|bd|=1
and consequently
|ac\boxminus bd|=1
;
(ii)
if
|a|<1
and
|d|<1
then
|bc|=1
, therefore
|ad\boxplus bc|=1
;
(iii)
if
|c|<1
and
|b|<1
then
|ad|=1
and we deduce that
|ad\boxplus bc|=1
;
(iv)
if
|b|<1
and
|d|<1
then
|ac|=1
, hence we have
|ac\boxminus bd|=1
.

Suppose now that
|a|=|b|=|c|=|d|=1
. All we need to show is that we cannot have
ac\boxminus bd=ad\boxplus bc=0
. In such a case we have

ac=bd
and
ad=-bc
. However, since
|c|=|d|=1
, this implies that
{d^{2}}=-c^{2}
that is a contradiction. Consequently, we deduce that that if
z,w\in C^{\natural}_{\infty}(0,1]
then
z\boxtimes w\in C^{\natural}_{\infty}(0,1]
.

(e)
Suppose that
z=|z|_{\infty}\big(\mathrm{pcos}(\theta)+\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{psin}(\theta)\big)
and
w=|w|_{\infty}\big(\mathrm{pcos}(\alpha)+\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{psin}(\alpha)\big)
.
We have:

z\boxtimes w=|z|_{\infty}|w|_{\infty}\big(\mathrm{pcos}(\theta)\mathrm{pcos}(\alpha)\boxminus\mathrm{psin}(\theta)\mathrm{psin}(\alpha)+\mathrm{i}\,(\mathrm{pcos}(\theta)\mathrm{psin}(\alpha)\boxplus\mathrm{psin}(\theta)\mathrm{pcos}(\alpha))\big).

Hence, since

\left|\mathrm{pcos}(\theta)\mathrm{pcos}(\alpha)\boxminus\mathrm{psin}(\theta)\mathrm{psin}(\alpha)+\mathrm{i}\,(\mathrm{pcos}(\theta)\mathrm{psin}(\alpha)\boxplus\mathrm{psin}(\theta)\mathrm{pcos}(\alpha))\right|_{\infty}=1

we deduce the result.
\Box

4
Algebraic Formulation of the Kuratowski-Peano Limit of a Line

4.1
Algebraic Formulation of a Line

In this section, we demonstrate that the non-associative algebraic structure we consider allows for establishing an algebraic formulation of any limiting line passing through two points. In the standard convex case it is easy to give an algebraic formulation of a line passing trough two different points. In such a case one can define the line passing trough
x
and
y
(
x\not=y
) as:

\mathcal{D}(x,y)=\{tx+sy:s+t=1,s,t\in\mathbb{R}\}.

(79)

Note that there is no restriction imposing the non-negativity of
s
and
t
which limits a line to a line-segment. Paralleling this definition one can define a
\varphi_{p}
-line as:

\mathcal{D}^{(p)}(x,y)=\big\{tx\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}sy:s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}t=1,s,t\in\mathbb{R}\big\}.

(80)

It was pointed out in
[
8
]
that the subset
\{tx\boxplus sy:\max\{s,t\}=1,s,t\geq 0\}
is not path-connected and does not describe, over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the convex hull in the limit of
\{x,y\}
.
To that end it was shown in
[
8
]
that
Co^{\infty}(x,y)=\Big\{tx\boxplus rx\boxplus sy\boxplus wy:\max\{t,r,s,w\}=1,t,r,s,w\geq 0\Big\}
. In what follows, we take a parallel approach to give the algebraic description of a line. Let us consider the following subset:

\mathcal{D}^{\infty}(x,y):=\Big\{tx\boxplus rx\boxplus sy\boxplus wy:t\boxplus r\boxplus s\boxplus w=1,t,r,s,w\in\mathbb{R}\Big\}.

(81)

Lemma 4.1.1

For all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
x\not=y
,

\mathcal{D}^{\infty}(x,y)\subset\Linf_{p\longrightarrow\infty}\mathcal{D}^{(p)}(x,y).

Proof:
Suppose that
z\in\Big\{tx\boxplus rx\boxplus sy\boxplus wy:t\boxplus r\boxplus s\boxplus w=1,t,r,s,w\in\mathbb{R}\Big\}
. By definition,
there exist
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
with

\alpha_{1}\boxplus\alpha_{2}\boxplus\beta_{1}\boxplus\beta_{2}=1
and such that

z=\alpha_{1}x\boxplus\alpha_{2}x\boxplus\beta_{1}y\boxplus\beta_{2}y.

Since
\alpha_{1}\boxplus\alpha_{2}\boxplus\beta_{1}\boxplus\beta_{2}=1
, there exists some
p_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
p\geq p_{0}
,
\alpha_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}\alpha_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}\beta_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}\beta_{2}\not=0
. For all
p\geq p_{0}
, define

z^{(p)}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}\alpha_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}\beta_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}\beta_{2}}\big(\alpha_{1}x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}\alpha_{2}x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}\beta_{1}y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{+}}\beta_{2}y\big).

By construction, for all
p\geq p_{0}
,

z^{(p)}\in\mathcal{D}^{(p)}(x,y)
. Taking the limit on both sides yields:

\displaystyle\lim_{p\longrightarrow\infty}z^{(p)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}\boxplus\alpha_{2}\boxplus\beta_{1}\boxplus\beta_{2}}\big(\alpha_{1}x\boxplus\alpha_{2}x\boxplus\beta_{1}y\boxplus\beta_{2}y\big)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}x\boxplus\alpha_{2}x\boxplus\beta_{1}y\boxplus\beta_{2}y=z.

Consequently,
z\in\Linf_{p\longrightarrow+\infty}\mathcal{D}^{(p)}(x,y)
.

\Box

Lemma 4.1.2

Let
\big\{(s^{(p)},t^{(p)})\big\}_{p\in\mathbb{N}}
be sequence of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Suppose there is an increasing subsequence
\{p_{q}\}_{q\in\mathbb{N}}
of natural numbers such that

s^{(p_{q})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{q}}}{{+}}t^{(p_{q})}=1
and
\lim\limits_{q\longrightarrow\infty}(s^{(p_{q})},t^{(p_{q})})=(s,t)
. We have the following properties:

(a)
If
\max\{\,|s|,|t|\,\}>1
then
s=-t
.

(b)
If
s=-t
then
|s|=|t|\geq 1
.

(c)
If
\min\{\,|s|,|t|\,\}\leq 1
then
\max\{s,t\}=1
.

(d)
If
s\not=-t
then
\max\{s,t\}=1
.

Proof:

(a)
In such a case if
|s|>1
then
t=\lim\limits_{q\longrightarrow\infty}{t_{p_{q}}}=\lim\limits_{q\longrightarrow\infty}1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{q}}}{{-}}{s_{p_{q}}}=-s
. The proof is symmetrical if

|t|>1
.
(b)
Let us prove that in such a case we have
|s|\geq 1
and
|t|\geq 1
. If
|s|<1
then
t=\lim\limits_{q\longrightarrow\infty}{t_{p_{q}}}=\lim\limits_{q\longrightarrow\infty}1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{q}}}{{-}}{s_{p_{q}}}=1
, which contradicts
s=-t
. Similarly, if
|t|<1
we have a contradiction. Therefore
|s|\geq 1
and
|t|\geq 1
.
(c)
If
s=1
, then the conclusion is obvious. If
|s|<1
, then
t=\lim\limits_{q\longrightarrow\infty}{t_{p_{q}}}=\lim\limits_{q\longrightarrow\infty}1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{q}}}{{-}}{s_{p_{q}}}=1
and
\max\{s,t\}=1
. To conclude if
s=-1
, then

t=\lim\limits_{q\longrightarrow\infty}{t_{p_{q}}}=\lim\limits_{q\longrightarrow\infty}1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{q}}}{{+}}{1}=1
, which ends the proof of
(c)
.
(d)
From
(a)
,
s\not=-t
implies that
\max\{\,|s|,|t|\,\}\leq 1
. Hence
\min\{\,|s|,|t|\,\}\leq 1
and the result is then immediate from
(c)
.
\Box

The next lemma is a preliminary result to show the upper limit of the sequence
\{\mathcal{D}^{{(p)}}\}_{p\in\mathbb{N}}
is identical to
\mathcal{D}^{\infty}
.

Lemma 4.1.3

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
x\not=y
and suppose that
z\in\Ls_{p\longrightarrow\infty}\mathcal{D}^{{(p)}}(x,y)
. Then there exists a sequence
\big\{(s^{(p)},t^{(p)})\big\}_{p\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and an increasing subsequence
\{p_{{q}}\}_{q\in\mathbb{N}}
of natural numbers such that

s^{(p_{{q}})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{{q}}}}{{+}}t^{(p_{q})}=1
,
\lim\limits_{q\longrightarrow\infty}(s^{(p_{q})},t^{(p_{q})})=(s,t)
and

z_{i}=sx_{i}\boxplus ty_{i},

for all
i\in[n]
such that
sx_{i}\not=-ty_{i}
.

Proof:
Suppose that
z\in\Ls_{p\longrightarrow\infty}\mathcal{D}^{(p)}(x,y)
. In such a case there exists an increasing sequence of natural numbers
\{p_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and sequence
\{z^{(p_{k})}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with
z^{(p_{k})}\in\mathcal{D}^{(p_{k})}(x,y)
for all
k
and
\lim_{q\longrightarrow\infty}z^{(p_{k})}=z
. This implies that there exists a sequence
\{s^{(p_{k})},t^{(p_{k})}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
such that

z^{(p_{k})}=t^{(p_{k})}x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{k}}}{{+}}s^{(p_{k})}y,

(82)

with
s^{(p_{k})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{k}}}{{+}}t^{(p_{k})}=1
for all
k
. Let us prove that
\big\{(s^{(p_{k})},t^{(p_{k})})\big\}_{q\in\mathbb{N}}
is a bounded sequence. Suppose that this is not the case and let us show a contradiction. Suppose for example that
\{s^{(p_{k})}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is not bounded. An elementary calculus shows that since
t^{(p_{k})}=1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{k}}}{{-}}s^{(p_{k})}
. Thus:

z^{(p_{k})}=x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{k}}}{{+}}s^{(p_{k})}(y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{k}}}{{-}}x).

(83)

However
\lim_{k\longrightarrow\infty}y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{k}}}{{-}}x=y\boxminus x
. Since
x\not=y
,
y\boxminus x\not=0
and this implies that
\lim_{k\longrightarrow\infty}\|z^{(p_{q})}\|=\infty
that is a contradiction. Consequently
\{s^{(p_{k})}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded. Since
s^{(p_{k})}=1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{k}}}{{-}}t^{(p_{k})}
this also implies that
\{t^{(p_{k})}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

\{(s^{(p_{k})},t^{(p_{k})})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

which yields the result.
\Box

Proposition 4.1.4

For all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
x\not=y
,

\Lim_{p\longrightarrow\infty}\mathcal{D}^{(p)}(x,y)=\mathcal{D}^{\infty}(x,y).

Proof:
To prove this result, we need to establish that
\Ls_{p\longrightarrow\infty}\mathcal{D}^{(p)}(x,y)\subset\Big\{tx\boxplus rx\boxplus sy\boxplus wy:t\boxplus r\boxplus s\boxplus w=1,t,r,s,w\in\mathbb{R}\Big\}
.
Suppose that
z\in\Ls_{p\longrightarrow\infty}\mathcal{D}^{(p)}(x,y)
. By hypothesis, there exists an increasing sequence of natural numbers
\{p_{q}\}_{q\in\mathbb{N}}
and sequence
\{z^{(p_{q})}\}_{q\in\mathbb{N}}
with
z^{(p_{q})}=t^{(p_{q})}x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{q}}}{{+}}s^{(p_{q})}y\in\mathcal{D}^{(p_{q})}(x,y)
for all
q
such that
\lim_{q\longrightarrow\infty}z^{(p_{q})}=z
. Moreover,
\lim_{p\longrightarrow\infty}(s^{(p_{q})},t^{(p_{q})})=(s,p)
and

\lim_{p\longrightarrow\infty}s^{(p_{q})}x_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{q}}}{{+}}t^{(p_{q})}y_{i}=sx_{i}\boxplus ty_{i},\quad(1)

for all
i
such that
sx_{i}\not=-ty_{i}
. Let
I=\{i\in[n]:sx_{i}\boxplus ty_{i}=0\}
.
We consider three cases.

(a)

I=\emptyset
. If

.(a),

.
Let us consider the vector

. Moreover, since

, for all

we have
sx_{i}\boxplus ty_{i}=sx_{i}\boxplus x_{i}\boxplus(1-\epsilon)y_{i}\boxplus ty_{i}
. From equation

z=sx\boxplus ty=sx\boxplus x\boxplus(1-\epsilon)y\boxplus ty.

Therefore

.
If
\min\{|s|,|t|\}\leq 1
then, from Lemma
4.1.2
,
\max\{s,t\}=1
. Suppose
that
t=1
. Then,
sx\boxplus ty=sx\boxplus 0x\boxplus y\boxplus y
and

s\boxplus 0\boxplus 1\boxplus 1=1
. One can proceeds symmetrically if
s=1
.

(b)

I\not=\emptyset
and for all

,
x_{i}=y_{i}
. In such a case for all

,

.(b) we have
|s|\geq 1
and

. Let us consider the vector

where
\epsilon>0
is sufficiently small. Clearly, since

, we have

.

If

then, since

and

If

then, since
sx_{i}\not=-tx_{i}
,

and

sx_{i}\boxplus x_{i}\boxplus(1-\epsilon)y_{i}\boxplus ty_{i}=sx_{i}\boxplus ty_{i}=z_{i}.

We conclude that

.

(c)

I\not=\emptyset
and there is some
i_{0}\in I
, such that
x_{i_{0}}\not=y_{i_{0}}

. Suppose that this is not the case. We have
sx_{i_{0}}\boxplus ty_{i_{0}}=sx_{i_{0}}\boxplus(-s)y_{i_{0}}=0
. However this implies

that is a contradiction. Moreover, from Lemma
4.1.2
.(c) we have
\max\{|s|,|t|\}=1
and

. Assume for example that

.

Suppose that
i\in I
. We have
ty_{i}=y_{i}=-sx_{i}

{s}\boxplus 0\boxplus 1\boxplus 1=1

. If

\Ls_{p\longrightarrow\infty}\mathcal{D}^{(p)}(x,y)\subset\Big\{tx\boxplus rx\boxplus sy\boxplus wy:t\boxplus r\boxplus s\boxplus w=1,t,r,s,w\in\mathbb{R}\Big\}.\quad\Box

In the following it is shown that

Proposition 4.1.6

Let

\{(u^{(p)},v^{(p)})\}_{p\in\mathbb{N}}

(u^{(p)},v^{(p)})\in\mathcal{D}^{(p)}(x,y)\times\mathcal{D}^{(p)}(x,y)
and
\lim_{p\longrightarrow\infty}(u^{(p)},v^{(p)})=(u,v)
. Let
\mathcal{I}(u,v)=\{i\in[n]:u_{i}v_{i}<0\}
. Since
\lim_{p\longrightarrow\infty}(u^{(p)},v^{(p)})=(u,v)
, there is some
p_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all

,
\mathcal{I}(u,v)=\mathcal{I}(u^{(p)},v^{(p)})
. For all

and all

, there is a unique point

such that

where, for all

.
For all

\Ls_{p\longrightarrow\infty}\big(H_{i}\cap\mathcal{D}^{(p)}(x,y)\big)\subset H_{i}\cap\mathcal{D}^{\infty}(x,y)

. Let
\gamma_{i}\in\Ls_{p\longrightarrow\infty}\big(H_{i}\cap\mathcal{D}^{(p)}(x,y)\big)
. This implies that there is an increasing sequence

such that

\lim_{q\longrightarrow\infty}\gamma_{i}^{(p_{q})}=\gamma_{i}\in H_{i}\cap\mathcal{D}^{\infty}(x,y).

Let us denote

. For all

one can find a sequence
\{i_{k}\}_{k\in[n(u,v)-1]}
such that

\gamma_{i_{k}}^{(p_{q})}\cdot\gamma_{i_{k+1}}^{(p_{q})}\geq 0.

It follows that

\Lim_{q\longrightarrow\infty}Co^{p_{q}}\Big(\gamma_{i_{k}}^{(p_{q})},\gamma_{i_{k+1}}^{(p_{q})}\Big)=Co^{\infty}\Big(\gamma_{i_{k}},\gamma_{i_{k+1}}\Big).

However,

\displaystyle\Ls_{p\longrightarrow\infty}Co^{p}(u^{(p)},v^{(p)})

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{k\in[{n(u,v)-1}]}\Ls_{q\longrightarrow\infty}Co^{p_{q}}\Big(\gamma_{i_{k}}^{(p_{q})},\gamma_{i_{k+1}}^{(p_{q})}\Big)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{k\in[{n(u,v)-1}]}\Lim_{q\longrightarrow\infty}Co^{p_{q}}\Big(\gamma_{i_{k}}^{(p_{q})},\gamma_{i_{k+1}}^{(p_{q})}\Big)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{k\in[{n(u,v)-1}]}Co^{\infty}\Big(\gamma_{i_{k}},\gamma_{i_{k+1}}\Big).

Now, since any set
Co^{\infty}\Big(\gamma_{i_{k}},\gamma_{i_{k+1}}\Big)
is path-connected, it follows that
there exists a continuous map
\psi:[0,1]\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}^{\infty}(x,y)
such that
\psi(0)=u
and
\psi(1)=v
.

In the next statement, it is shown that, for all

Proposition 4.2.1

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and suppose that
x\not=y
. Let
I=\{i\in[n]:x_{i}\not=y_{i}\}
and
J=[n]\backslash I
. We have the following inclusions:

(a)
If
J=\emptyset
then

\{t(y\boxminus x):t\in[1,\infty[\,\}\subset\mathcal{D}^{\infty}(x,y)\quad\text{ and }\quad\{t(x\boxminus y):t\in[1,\infty[\,\}\subset\mathcal{D}^{\infty}(x,y).

(b)
If
J\not=\emptyset
then

x_{[J]}+\Big\{t(y\boxminus x):t\in[1,\infty[\,\Big\}\subset\mathcal{D}^{\infty}(x,y)\quad\text{ and }\quad x_{[J]}+\Big\{t(x\boxminus y):t\in[1,\infty[\,\Big\}\subset\mathcal{D}^{\infty}(x,y),

where
x_{[J]}=\sum\limits_{k\in J}x_{k}e_{k}=\sum\limits_{k\in J}y_{k}e_{k}=y_{[J]}.

Proof:

(a)
We first note that since
J=\emptyset
and
I=[n]

x\boxminus y=x\boxplus x\boxplus 0y\boxplus(-y)\in\Big\{tx\boxplus rx\boxplus sy\boxplus wy:t\boxplus r\boxplus s\boxplus w=1,t,r,s,w\in\mathbb{R}\Big\}.

For all
t\geq 1
, let us consider the vector

z=tx\boxplus(-t)y.

Since
t\geq 1
, we have

z=tx\boxplus x\boxplus 0y\boxplus(-t)y.

Moreover
t\boxplus 1\boxplus 0\boxplus(-t)=1
. Clearly
z\in\Big\{tx\boxplus rx\boxplus sy\boxplus wy:t\boxplus r\boxplus s\boxplus w=1,t,r,s,w\in\mathbb{R}\Big\}
, which proves the first part of
(a)
. The proof of the second part is symmetrical swapping
x
and
y
.
(b)
Let us denote
\mathbb{R}_{[I]}=\{\sum_{k\in I}x_{k}e_{k}:x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
. By hypothesis
x,y\in x_{[J]}+\mathbb{R}_{[I]}
. Hence

\mathcal{D}^{\infty}(x,y)\subset x_{[J]}+\mathbb{R}_{[I]}.
Let
n_{I}=\mathrm{Card}(I)
and let
\mathcal{D}_{[I]}^{\infty}(x,y)
be the canonical decomposition of
\mathcal{D}^{\infty}(x,y)
over
\mathbb{R}_{[I]}
. By definition for all
i\in I
we have
x_{i}\not=y_{i}
. Since
\mathbb{R}_{[I]}
is isomorphic to its restriction projection onto
\mathbb{R}^{n_{I}}
we deduce from
(a)
that

\big\{t(x_{[I]}\boxminus y_{[I]}):t\in[1,\infty[\,\big\}\subset\mathcal{D}_{[I]}^{\infty}(x,y).

However
\mathcal{D}^{\infty}(x,y)=x_{[J]}+\mathcal{D}_{[I]}^{\infty}(x,y)
. Moreover
x_{[J]}\boxminus y_{[J]}=0.
Hence

\displaystyle x_{[J]}+\big\{t(x\boxminus y):t\in[1,\infty[\,\big\}

\displaystyle=x_{[J]}+\big\{t(x_{[I]}\boxminus y_{[I]}):t\in[1,\infty[\,\big\}

\displaystyle\subset x_{[J]}+\mathcal{D}_{[I]}^{\infty}(x,y)

\displaystyle\subset\mathcal{D}^{\infty}(x,y).

The proof of the second part of the statement is symmetrical.
\Box

Traditional axioms of planar Euclidean geometry postulate that:
(a_{1})
a straight line may be drawn between any two points;
(a_{2})
any terminated straight line may be extended indefinitely;
(a_{3})
a circle may be drawn with any given point as center and any given radius,
(a_{4})
all right angles are equal;
(v)
for any given point not on a given line, there is exactly one line through the point that does not meet the given line.

It is well known that the fifth axiom does not follow from the first four. Clearly, the geometric implications of the algebraic structure proposed in this paper do not conflict with the first 4 axioms. Not surprisingly, this is not the case for the fifth. In particular:

(i)
two distinct parallel lines may have an infinity of common points (Figure 12);

(ii)
there may exist an infinity of
\digamma
-lines passing by two distinct points
x
and
y
(Figure 12);

(iii)
two secant lines may have an infinity of common points (Figure 13);

(iv)
a
\digamma
-line may not be spanned by two distinct points it contains (Figure 13).

Lemma 4.3.2

Let
\mathcal{D}^{\infty}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\infty}
be two parallel
\digamma

\mathbb{R}^{2}

\mathcal{D}^{\infty}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\langle a,z\rangle_{\infty}^{-}\leq c\leq\langle a,z\rangle_{\infty}^{+}\leq c\}

and

\mathcal{E}^{\infty}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\langle a,z\rangle_{\infty}^{-}\leq d\leq\langle a,z\rangle_{\infty}^{+}\leq c\}.

Proof:
Suppose now that
\mathcal{D}^{\infty}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\infty}
are two
\digamma
-line respectively spanned from
x,y
and
u,v
, where
x,y,u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. By definition we have:

\mathcal{E}^{\infty}=\Big\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:(u_{2}\boxminus v_{2})z_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle-}}{{\smile}}(v_{1}\boxminus u_{1})z_{2}\leq|u,v|_{\infty}\leq(u_{2}\boxminus v_{2})z_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle+}}{{\smile}}(v_{1}\boxminus u_{1})z_{2}\Big\}.

If
\mathcal{D}^{\infty}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\infty}
are parallel then
(u\boxminus v)\propto(x\boxminus y)
. Therefore, there is a real number
\alpha\not=0
such that

\mathcal{E}^{\infty}=\Big\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\alpha(x_{2}\boxminus y_{2})z_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle-}}{{\smile}}\alpha(y_{1}\boxminus x_{1})z_{2}\leq|u,v|_{\infty}\leq\alpha(x_{2}\boxminus y_{2})z_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle+}}{{\smile}}\alpha(y_{1}\boxminus x_{1})z_{2}\Big\}.

Suppose that
\alpha>0
, then:

\mathcal{E}^{\infty}=\Big\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:(x_{2}\boxminus y_{2})z_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle-}}{{\smile}}(y_{1}\boxminus x_{1})z_{2}\leq\alpha^{-1}|u,v|_{\infty}\leq(x_{2}\boxminus y_{2})z_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle+}}{{\smile}}(y_{1}\boxminus x_{1})z_{2}\Big\}.

\mathcal{E}^{\infty}=\Big\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:(x_{2}\boxminus y_{2})z_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle-}}{{\smile}}(y_{1}\boxminus x_{1})z_{2}\geq\alpha^{-1}|u,v|_{\infty}\geq(x_{2}\boxminus y_{2})z_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle+}}{{\smile}}(y_{1}\boxminus x_{1})z_{2}\Big\}.

Conclusion

In this article, we have explored certain geometric properties emerging from the deformation of a vector space structure defined over a scalar field. In particular, we have focused on notions of lines that can be seen as special limits of linear varieties. For future research, it would be valuable to investigate the connections between these notions and those typically derived from Max-Plus algebras, which are traditionally used in the formalism of tropical mathematics, as initiated in

]
and

]
