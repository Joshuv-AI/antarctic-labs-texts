Abstract
Stochastic games combine controllable and adversarial non-determinism with stochastic behavior and are a common tool in control, verification and synthesis of reactive systems facing uncertainty. Multi-objective stochastic games are natural in situations where several—possibly conflicting—performance criteria like time and energy consumption are relevant. Such conjunctive combinations are the most studied multi-objective setting in the literature. In this paper, we consider the dual disjunctive problem. More concretely, we study turn-based stochastic two-player games on graphs where the winning condition is to guarantee at least one reachability or safety objective from a given set of alternatives. We present a fine-grained overview of strategy and computational complexity of such disjunctive queries (DQs) and provide new lower and upper bounds for several variants of the problem, significantly extending previous works. We also propose a novel value iteration-style algorithm for approximating the set of Pareto optimal thresholds for a given DQ.

Introduction

Stochastic games (SG), e.g.
[
23
,
38
]
, combine controllable and adversarial non-determinism with stochastic behavior.
In their turn-based two-player version, SGs are played on graphs where the vertices are called states, and every state either belongs to one of the two players Eve and Adam, or is controlled by a probabilistic environment.
In each round, the player in control of the state chooses an action—an edge of the graph—and the game transitions to the successor state.
In probabilistic states, the successor is sampled according to a fixed observable distribution over the outgoing edges.

Simple
SGs
[
23
]
have just a single
reachability
objective.
A key question is whether Eve can control her states such that a target is reached with at least a certain probability, no matter the behavior of the opponent Adam.
Dually, Adam has a
safety objective
in this setting:
He should maximize the chance of staying in the—from his point of view—safe region, i.e., avoiding Eve’s target states.

Multi-objective stochastic games

[
21
]
extend this by allowing Boolean combinations of several different probability or expectation thresholds on various objectives.
Such games have been used to synthesize optimal controllers in application scenarios where the system at hand is exposed to an environment with both stochastic and non-deterministic aspects
[
7
,
22
,
28
]
.
A natural subclass of multi-objectives are
disjunctive queries
(DQ)
[
21
,
29
]
where the player has to satisfy at least one alternative from a given set of options.

In this paper, we study DQs with both reachability and safety options.
More specifically, given a game and a finite set of reachability and safety objectives, each equipped with a desired threshold probability, we ask whether Eve can satisfy at least one option with probability at least the respective threshold.
Our motivation for studying DQs is twofold.
(1) DQs are interesting in their own right as they allow for a fine-grained specification of alternatives over desirable outcomes of a controlled stochastic system.
(2) DQs are equivalent to the more widely used
conjunctive
queries (CQs) under an alternative semantics, namely if the opponent Adam has to
reveal
his strategy to Eve before the game starts.
This amounts to changing the quantification order over strategies from
\exists\forall
—which is the standard order—to
\forall\exists
.
In general, this makes a difference since multi-objective SGs are not
determined

[
21
]
.
This optimistic “
asserted-exposure
” (
\forall\exists
) semantics is interesting in situations where the non-deterministic system state can actually be observed at any given point in time.
For instance, in the smart heating case study from
[
36
]
, the position of the doors in a house is non-deterministically controlled by Adam and not directly observable.
However, if door sensors were to be installed, the
\forall\exists
-semantics would be more adequate since the door positions are now observable (but still uncontrollable).

Suppose that Eve’s objective is the DQ “reach

with probability at least

\mathbb{P}(\lozenge T_{1})\geq x\vee\mathbb{P}(\lozenge T_{2})\geq y

The coordinate systems next to the states show their

(x,y)

For
s_{1}
, the Pareto set is the whole box
[0,1]^{2}

For
s_{2}

(0,1)
and
(1,0)
and all point-wise smaller vectors, forming a triangle.
This is because Adam has to distribute the whole probability mass
somewhere

(x,y)
,
x+y\leq 1

However, if
x+y>1
, Adam can prevent Eve from winning in state
s_{2}

For example, if
(x,y)=(0.6,0.6)
, Adam can randomize equally between both actions, and no target is reached with at least
0.6

This also shows that solving a DQ is
not
equivalent to solving each objective separately:
From
s_{2}

T_{1}

T_{2}

(0.5,0.5)

Finally, Eve can achieve each threshold vector
(x,y)
with
x\leq 0.5
or
y\leq 0.5
from
s_{0}
because
s_{1}
is reached with probability
0.5
where she can put all probability mass on one of the targets.
On the other hand, Eve cannot ensure any vector
(x,y)
with
x>0.5\wedge y>0.5
because once she has fixed a strategy, Adam can mirror it so that both targets are reached with
exactly

0.5
.

The example in Figure

Firstly, Pareto frontiers are not necessarily convex, as in state
s_{0}

This is in contrast to

where the set of achievable probability thresholds is always convex
[
21

26
]
.
Secondly, as mentioned above, SGs with multiple objectives are in general not
determined

[
21
]
, i.e., it is relevant which player fixes their strategy first, thereby
revealing
it to the other player before the game starts.
In fact, in the example above, switching the quantification order allows Eve to take advantage by reacting to the strategy of Adam.
For instance, she could then ensure that at least one of the targets is reached with probability at least
0.75
.

•

Related work

SGs were introduced by Shapley
[
38
]
in 1953.
Simple SGs—the turn-based variant with a reachability objective—are one of the intriguing problems in
\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}
but not known to be in
\mathsf{P}

[
24
]
.
See
[
23
,
32
]
for an overview of solution algorithms.
Turn-based SGs were studied with a variety of other objectives, e.g.
[
14
,
17
,
18
]
.
Other types of SGs include concurrent games
[
12
,
33
]
, limited information games
[
3
,
15
]
, and bidding games
[
4
]
.

Stochastic systems with multiple objectives have been extensively studied for more than a decade.
Markov decision processes, SGs with a single player, were investigated with multiple reachability or LTL objectives
[
26
]
as well as multiple discounted sum
[
16
]
, total reward
[
31
]
or mean payoff objectives
[
20
]
.
Further, the question of percentile queries was addressed in
[
30
,
37
]
, and combinations of probabilistic and non-probabilistic objectives in
[
8
]
.
Non-standard multi-objective queries were developed together with domain experts in
[
6
]
.

For SGs with multiple objectives, many decidability questions are still open.
For conjunctive reachability, it is only known that the Pareto set can be approximated
[
2
]
with guaranteed precision, even in non-stopping games.
For total reward, the problem is proven decidable only for stopping games with two-dimensional queries
[
10
]
but it can be approximated in higher dimensional stopping SGs
[
21
]
. If only deterministic strategies are allowed, the exact problem is undecidable
[
21
]
,
and so are generalized mean-payoff objectives in SGs
[
39
]
.
However, keeping mean-payoff above a certain threshold with some probability is
\mathsf{coNP}
-complete
[
13
]
.
Further, lexicographic preferences over multiple reachability or safety objectives can be reduced to single objectives
[
19
]
.
The tool PRISM-games
[
34
]
implements compositional approaches to verification and strategy synthesis of several multi-objective problems
[
7
,
35
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, disjunctive queries were so far only considered as a special case of more general Boolean combinations for expected rewards
[
21
]
, mean-payoff SGs
[
39
]
, and in deterministic generalized reachability games on graphs
[
29
]
.

Preliminaries

General definitions.

For sets
A
and
B
, the set of functions
A\to B
is written
B^{A}
.
The set of finite words over a non-empty set
A
is written
A^{*}
.
For countable sets
A
we let
\mathsf{Dist}(A)\vcentcolon=\{P\in[0,1]^{A}\mid\sum_{a\in A}P(a)=1\}
be the set of all
probability distributions
on
A
.
The
support
of
d\in\mathsf{Dist}(A)
is defined as
\mathsf{supp}(d)=\{a\in A\mid d(a)>0\}
.
The
i
-th component of a vector
\vec{x}\in[0,1]^{n}
is denoted
x_{i}
.
We compare vectors
\vec{x},\vec{y}\in[0,1]^{n}
component-wise, i.e.,
\vec{x}\leq\vec{y}
iff
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
.
A set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
convex
if for all
\vec{x},\vec{y}\in X
and
p\in[0,1]
it holds that
p\vec{x}+(1{-}p)\vec{y}\in X
.
The
convex hull

\mathsf{conv}(X)
of
X
is the smallest convex superset of
X
.
Given sets
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a real number
p\in[0,1]
, we define the
p
-convex combination

pX+(1{-}p)Y\vcentcolon=\{p\vec{x}+(1{-}p)\vec{y}\mid\vec{x}\in X,\vec{y}\in Y\}
.
The
downward-closure
of
X\subseteq[0,1]^{n}
is defined as
\mathsf{dwc}(X)\vcentcolon=\{\vec{y}\in[0,1]^{n}\mid\exists\vec{x}\in X\colon\vec{y}\leq\vec{x}\}
.

X
is called
downward-closed
if
X=\mathsf{dwc}(X)
.
A
closed half-space
is a set
\{\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\vec{n}\cdot\vec{x}\geq d\}
where
\vec{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{\vec{0}\}
and
d\in\mathbb{R}
.
A
polyhedron
is the intersection of finitely many closed half-spaces.
Polyhedra are convex.

Stochastic games and strategies.

Intuitively, the games considered in this paper are played by moving a pebble along the edges (called
transitions
from now on) of a finite directed graph.
The vertices (subsequently called
states
) of this graph are partitioned into three classes which determine the states controlled by Eve, Adam, and the probabilistic environment, respectively:

Definition 1

(SG)
.

A
stochastic game (SG)
is a tuple
\mathcal{G}=(S_{\mathsf{E}},S_{\mathsf{A}},S_{\mathsf{P}},{s_{0}},P,\mathsf{Act})
, where
S_{\mathsf{E}}\uplus S_{\mathsf{A}}\uplus S_{\mathsf{P}}=\vcentcolon S
are finite disjoint sets of
states
controlled either by Eve (
S_{\mathsf{E}}
), Adam (
S_{\mathsf{A}}
), or the probabilistic environment (
S_{\mathsf{P}}
).
The game starts in the
initial state

{s_{0}}\in S
.
Each
s\in S_{\mathsf{E}}\cup S_{\mathsf{A}}
has a non-empty set
\mathsf{Act}(s)\subseteq S
of
actions
available to Eve (Adam, resp.). For all
s\in S_{\mathsf{P}}
,
P\colon S_{\mathsf{P}}\to\mathsf{Dist}(S)
is a probability distribution over the successors of
s
.

For
s\in S_{\mathsf{P}}
and
t\in S
we write
P(s,t)
rather than
P(s)(t)
.
A state
s\in S
is called
sink
if either
s\in S_{\mathsf{P}}
and
P(s,s)=1
or
s\in S\setminus S_{\mathsf{P}}
and
\mathsf{Act}(s)=\{s\}
.

P
and
\mathsf{Act}
together induce a directed graph on
S
.
We often sketch this
game graph
in our figures (e.g. Figure
1
), drawing Eve’s, Adam’s and the probabilistic states with boxes, diamonds and circles, respectively; and we omit the self-loops on sinks to ease the presentation.
For
k\geq 0
, we let
\mathcal{G}^{\leq k}
be the restriction of
\mathcal{G}
to
k
-steps, that is,
\mathcal{G}^{\leq k}
is obtained from
\mathcal{G}
by counting the transitions from
{s_{0}}
taken so far and entering an error sink once their number exceeds
k
.
A
Markov decision process
(MDP) is the 1-player version of an SG, i.e., either
S_{\mathsf{E}}=\emptyset
or
S_{\mathsf{A}}=\emptyset
. A
Markov chain
(MC) is a 0-player SG, i.e.,
S=S_{\mathsf{P}}
. For technical reasons, we allow MDPs and MCs with countably infinite state spaces.
SGs, on the other hand, are always
finite
in this paper.

Strategies
define the semantics of SGs.
A (general) strategy for Eve is a function
\sigma\colon S^{*}S_{\mathsf{E}}\to\mathsf{Dist}(S)
such that
\mathsf{supp}(\sigma(\pi s))\subseteq\mathsf{Act}(s)
for all
\pi s\in S^{*}S_{\mathsf{E}}
.
A strategy
\sigma
is
deterministic
if
\sigma(\pi s)
is a point-distribution for all
\pi s\in S^{*}S_{\mathsf{E}}
, i.e., if it is not randomized.

To describe strategies by finite means (if possible), we use
strategy automata
.
Formally, a strategy automaton for Eve is a structure
\mathcal{M}=(M,\mu,\nu,m_{0})

with
M
a countable set of memory elements,

\mu\colon S\times M\to M
a
memory update
function,

\nu\colon S_{\mathsf{E}}\times M\to\mathsf{Dist}(S)
a
next move
function,
and
m_{0}\in M
an initial memory state.
Given a strategy automaton
\mathcal{M}
, the
induced MDP
is the game

\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{M}}~\vcentcolon=~(\,\emptyset,\,S_{\mathsf{A}}\times M,\,(S_{\mathsf{P}}\cup S_{\mathsf{E}})\times M,\,(s_{0},m_{0}),\,P^{\mathcal{M}},\,\mathsf{Act}^{\mathcal{M}}\,)~,

where the transition probability function
P^{\mathcal{M}}
is defined as follows:
Let
m,m^{\prime}\in M
be arbitrary and let
s\in S
.
Then, if
s\in S_{\mathsf{P}}
, we let
P^{\mathcal{M}}((s,m),(s^{\prime},m^{\prime}))\vcentcolon=P(s,s^{\prime})
if
\mu(s^{\prime},m)=m^{\prime}
;
if
s\in S_{\mathsf{E}}
, then
P^{\mathcal{M}}((s,m),(s^{\prime},m^{\prime}))\vcentcolon=\nu((s,m))(s^{\prime})
if
\mu(s^{\prime},m)=m^{\prime}
;
and
P^{\mathcal{M}}((s,m),(s^{\prime},m^{\prime}))=0
in all other cases.
Moreover,
\mathsf{Act}^{\mathcal{M}}((s,m))=\{(s^{\prime},m^{\prime})\mid s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Act}(s)\wedge\mu(s^{\prime},m)=m^{\prime}\}
for all
(s,m)\in S_{\mathsf{A}}\times M
.

\mathcal{M}
is called a
stochastic-update
strategy if the memory update
\mu
may additionally randomize over
M
(see
[
9
]
for details).
From the definition it is clear that
\mathcal{M}

realizes
a strategy in the general form
\sigma\colon S^{*}S_{\mathsf{E}}\to\mathsf{Dist}(S)
.
We define the
memory size
of
\sigma
as the smallest
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
such that there exists a strategy automaton
\mathcal{M}=(M,\mu,\nu,m_{0})
with
|M|=k
that realizes
\sigma
.
If
k=\infty
, then
\sigma
is
infinite-memory
, and otherwise
finite-memory
.
If
k=1
, then
\sigma
is called
memoryless
.
An
MD
strategy is both memoryless and deterministic.
The above definitions are analogous for the other player Adam, interchanging
S_{\mathsf{E}}
and
S_{\mathsf{A}}
.

Throughout the paper, we consistently denote Eve’s strategies with
\sigma
and Adam’s strategies with
\tau
.
We usually identify strategies with their realizing automata.
Given a strategy
\sigma
, a
counter-strategy

\tau
is a strategy in the induced MDP
\mathcal{G}^{\sigma}
and may depend on
\sigma
.

Reachability-safety queries and determinacy.

Given an MC
(S,s_{0},P)
and a set
T\subseteq S
, the
reachability probability
of
T
is
\mathbb{P}(\lozenge T)\vcentcolon=\sum_{\pi\in Paths(T)}Pr(\pi)
where
Paths(T)
is the set of finite paths
\pi
of the form
\pi={s_{0}}s_{1}\ldots s_{k}
with
k\geq 0
,
s_{k}\in T
and
s_{i}\notin T
for all
i=0,\ldots,k-1
;
and
Pr(\pi)\vcentcolon=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}P(s_{i},s_{i+1})
.
Dually, we define
\mathbb{P}(\square T)\vcentcolon=1-\mathbb{P}(\lozenge\overline{T})
, where
\overline{T}\vcentcolon=S\setminus T
.
Intuitively,
\mathbb{P}(\lozenge T)
is the probability to eventually reach a
target
state in
T
and
\mathbb{P}(\square T)
is the probability to stay forever within
T
, i.e., to avoid the
unsafe set

\overline{T}
.
We write
\mathbb{P}^{\sigma,\tau}
to emphasize that we consider the probability measure in the Markov chain
\mathcal{G}^{\sigma,\tau}
induced by some strategies
\sigma,\tau
.

Definition 2

(Disjunctive Queries
[
21
]
)
.

Given an SG
\mathcal{G}
with state space
S
, an
n
-dimensional
disjunctive query (DQ)
for
\mathcal{G}
is an expression of the form

\varphi=\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(\star_{i}T_{i})\geq x_{i}

with
\star_{i}\in\{\lozenge,\square\}
,
T_{i}\subseteq S
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, and
\vec{x}\in(0,1]^{n}
a threshold vector.

Note that we only allow (non-strict)
lower
bounds in DQs. This is w.l.o.g. as upper bounds on reachability or safety can be recast as lower bounds on the dual objective.
The standard semantics of a DQ is defined as follows
[
21
]
: Eve can
achieve
1
1

1

We use the term “achieve” rather than “win” for consistency with previous works, e.g.
[
26
,
21
,
10
]
.

\varphi
, or equivalently,
\varphi
is
achievable
in
\mathcal{G}
if

\exists\sigma\forall\tau\colon\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{\sigma,\tau}(\star_{i}T_{i})\geq x_{i}

where
\sigma
ranges over all strategies of Eve,
\tau
over those of Adam and
\mathbb{P}^{\sigma,\tau}
is the probability measure of the induced Markov chain
\mathcal{G}^{\sigma,\tau}
.
A strategy of Eve witnessing achievability of
\varphi
is called an
achieving strategy
.
Note that the quantification order is such that Eve has to reveal her strategy to Adam
before
the game actually starts.
Since the games are not determined, this may be a disadvantage (see
[
21
]
or our example in the introduction).
Therefore, we also consider the alternative semantics obtained by swapping the quantification order.
We call this semantics
asserted-exposure
(
\forall\exists
for short).
In the
\forall\exists
-semantics, Adam’s strategy is
exposed
to Eve
before
the game begins, i.e.,
\varphi
is
\forall\exists
-achievable if

\forall\tau\exists\sigma\colon\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{\sigma,\tau}(\star_{i}T_{i})\geq x_{i}

By definition,
\mathcal{G}
is
determined
for
\varphi
iff the standard and the alternative

-semantics coincide.

We will consider the following subclasses of DQs:
If
\star_{i}=\lozenge
(
\star_{i}=\square
) for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, then we call
\varphi
a
reachability
(
safety
) DQ.
We say that
\varphi
is
mixed
to emphasize that it may contain both
\lozenge
and
\square
.
If
\vec{x}=(1,\ldots,1)
then
\varphi
is called
qualitative
DQ, and otherwise
quantitative
DQ.
If each state contained in a target/unsafe set is a sink then
\varphi
is called a
sink

All of the above notions are defined analogously for
conjunctive queries
(CQs).

Pareto sets.

If the threshold vector
\vec{x}
in a query
\varphi
has not been fixed, we can think of
\varphi
as a
query template
.
We define the set
Pareto set
for query template
\varphi
in a given SG
\mathcal{G}
as

\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{G},\varphi)~\vcentcolon=~\{\,\vec{x}\in[0,1]^{n}\,\mid\,\exists\sigma\,\forall\tau\colon\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{\sigma,\tau}(\star_{i}T_{i})\geq x_{i}\,\}

and similarly for CQs.
Further, for
k\geq 0
, we call
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{G}^{\leq k},\varphi)
the
horizon-
k

k

We define
\mathcal{A}^{\forall\exists}(\mathcal{G},\varphi)
as the set of vectors achievable in the alternative
\forall\exists
-semantics.
Note that in general,
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{G},\varphi)\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\forall\exists}(\mathcal{G},\varphi)
, and equality holds iff
\mathcal{G}
is determined for all
\varphi(\vec{x})
,
\vec{x}\in[0,1]^{n}
.
The Pareto sets
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{G},\varphi)
and
\mathcal{A}^{\forall\exists}(\mathcal{G},\varphi)
generalize the notion of
lower
and
upper value
in single-dimensional games.
Indeed, they coincide for
n=1
as single-dimensional SGs are determined
[
24
]
.
Furthermore,
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{G},\varphi)
is convex for CQs
[
21
]
.

Goal-unfolding.

The following construction is folklore (e.g.
[
29
,
21
]
).
Given an SG
\mathcal{G}
with states
S
and an
n
-dimensional query
\varphi
, we define the
goal-unfolding

\mathsf{Unf}(\mathcal{G},\varphi)
of
\mathcal{G}
with respect to
\varphi
as a game with state space
S\times\{0,1\}^{n}
.
Let
(s,\vec{v})
be a state of the unfolding.
Intuitively,
\mathsf{Unf}(\mathcal{G},\varphi)
remembers which targets/unsafe sets have already been visited during a specific play.
This is encoded in the
n
-bit vector
\vec{v}
.
That is, if
\mathcal{G}
transitions from
s
to
t
, then in the unfolding
(s,\vec{v})
moves to
(t,\vec{u})
where
\vec{u}
is obtained from
\vec{v}

Conclusion and Future Work

We have presented a detailed picture of computational and strategy complexity of SGs with DQ winning conditions.
The results were obtained in part by providing reductions from CQs to DQs and applying results from the literature.
Future work on the complexity side includes closing the gaps in Tables
1
and
2
; however, we conjecture that this requires significant new insights.
For example, a major obstacle towards proving
\mathsf{PSPACE}
membership of the quantitative general-strategies DQs problem is that one has to reason about
exact
reachability probabilities in the exponentially large goal-unfolding.
It is not at all obvious that the number of bits needed for the rational representations of these quantities remains polynomially bounded.

We have also argued that DQs are equivalent to CQs in the optimistic “asserted-exposure” (
\forall\exists
) semantics obtained by changing the quantification order over strategies—unlike in simple SGs, this makes a difference since our games are not always determined.
Moreover, we have formulated the first VI-style algorithm for DQs in the standard and CQs in the
\forall\exists
-semantics.
It should be straightforward to extend our algorithm to expected rewards as well.
Another interesting application of the algorithm is to certify determinacy (for a finite step bound).
Regarding future work, it would be appealing to implement the algorithm in a tool such as PRISM-games and to experiment with more realistic case studies.
Yet another direction is to investigate (counter-)strategy synthesis for Eve in the
\forall\exists
-semantics, e.g., by constructing strategy templates where some choices depend on Adam’s observable strategy.

The game is as follows (Figure
8
):
In Stage A, the environment chooses one of the
2^{n}-1
combinations
\mathcal{T}_{1}\subsetneq\mathcal{T}
uniformly at random and visits all targets in the chosen
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, thereby spoiling the objectives
\square\overline{T}
for all
T\in\mathcal{T}_{1}
.
In Stage B, Eve then freely selects another such combination
\mathcal{T}_{2}\subsetneq\mathcal{T}
.
Depending on
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, a coin with bias
p(\mathcal{T}_{2})
is tossed:
With a certain
penalty

p(\mathcal{T}_{2})
, all targets
\mathcal{T}
are visited immediately, and with the remaining probability the game enters Stage C where Adam chooses any probability distribution over combinations of at most
n-|\mathcal{T}_{2}|-1

The penalty
p(\mathcal{T}_{2})
is strictly monotonically increasing in
|\mathcal{T}_{2}|
, that is, selecting a larger set
\mathcal{T}_{2}
involves more risk from Eve’s perspective.

Intuitively, the idea is that one can find a threshold

in
\varphi
, such that Eve must select
exactly

\mathcal{T}_{2}=\mathcal{T}_{1}
in Stage B.
This clearly requires
2^{n}-1

need to be remembered.
This behavior is enforced as follows.
There are two cases:
(a) If

and so Adam can visit all the remaining targets

(b) Otherwise

\mathcal{T}_{2}\supseteq\mathcal{T}_{1}
.
In this case, if the game reaches Stage C, at least one target will be avoided with positive probability.

\mathcal{T}_{2}\supseteq\mathcal{T}_{1}

\mathcal{T}_{2}
.
Then the

(the one that maximizes the highest achievable threshold probability

) of Eve consists in choosing exactly
\mathcal{T}_{2}=\mathcal{T}_{1}
.

More formally, suppose that Eve fixes the strategy
\sigma^{*}
described above.
For any counter-strategy of
\tau

be the random variable describing the number of different visited targets.
We can consider a counter-strategy
\tau
that maximizes the expected value

(such a
\tau

is just a finite MDP).
Then since the game and the strategy

such that Adam can achieve

.
Indeed, if he could achieve
\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(\lozenge T_{i})\geq z

, which is a contradiction.
Consequently,
\sigma^{*}
achieves the DQ

.

On the other hand, if Eve plays any strategy
\sigma\neq\sigma^{*}
we can consider each possible combination
\mathcal{T}_{1}\subsetneq\mathcal{T}
chosen in Stage A separately:
If
|\mathcal{T}_{2}|<|\mathcal{T}_{1}\cup\mathcal{T}_{2}|
(case (a) from above) then Adam can enforce visiting
all
targets with probability 1 as explained earlier.
If
|\mathcal{T}_{2}|\geq|\mathcal{T}_{1}\cup\mathcal{T}_{2}|
and hence
\mathcal{T}_{2}\supseteq\mathcal{T}_{1}
(case (b) from above) then nothing changes compared to playing
\sigma^{*}
if
\sigma
actually selects
\mathcal{T}_{2}=\mathcal{T}_{1}
, i.e., behaves exactly like
\sigma^{*}
given
\mathcal{T}_{1}
(which is possible).
However, if
\sigma

\mathcal{T}_{2}\supsetneq\mathcal{T}_{1}
then for the penalties we have

which means that also in this case, the probability to reach each individual target is strictly larger than if
\mathcal{T}_{2}=\mathcal{T}_{1}
had been selected by
\sigma

Overall, Adam can enforce
\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(\lozenge T_{i})\geq z

selected in Stage A,
\sigma

with positive probability.
∎
