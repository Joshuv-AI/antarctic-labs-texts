Minimax Optimal rates of convergence in the shuffled regression, unlinked regression, and deconvolution under vanishing noise
Cecile Durot, Debarghya Mukherjee

Abstract
Shuffled regression and unlinked regression represent intriguing challenges that have garnered considerable attention in many fields, including but not limited to ecological regression, multi-target tracking problems, image denoising, etc. However, a notable gap exists in the existing literature, particularly in vanishing noise, i.e., how the rate of estimation of the underlying signal scales with the error variance. This paper aims to bridge this gap by delving into the monotone function estimation problem under vanishing noise variance, i.e., we allow the error variance to go to 0 0 as the number of observations increases. Our investigation reveals that, asymptotically, the shuffled regression problem exhibits a comparatively simpler nature than the unlinked regression; if the error variance is smaller than a threshold, then the minimax risk of the shuffled regression is smaller than that of the unlinked regression. On the other hand, the minimax estimation error is of the same order in the two problems if the noise level is larger than that threshold. Our analysis is quite general in that we do not assume any smoothness of the underlying monotone link function. Because these problems are related to deconvolution, we also provide bounds for deconvolution in a similar context. Through this exploration, we contribute to understanding the intricate relationships between these statistical problems and shed light on their behaviors when subjected to the nuanced constraint of vanishing noise.

Introduction

In a standard supervised learning problem, we observe finitely many (say
n
many) realizations of a paired random variable
(X,Y)
(where
Y
is the response variable and
X
is the covariate) and construct a predictor of
Y
from
X
based on those realizations.
This setup encompasses widely used statistical models, such as standard regression and classification.
A fundamental assumption for this class of problems is the availability of
paired
observations
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
, and, in particular, the knowledge of which
X
corresponds to which
Y
.
However, the information about this pairing is unavailable in many real-world applications.
To illustrate, consider a concrete example originated in
[
18
]
and elaborated in
[
58
]
: suppose we have a collection of photographs of
n
actors at young ages (denoted as
\mathcal{X}_{n}
) and a collection of photos of the same group of actors at older ages (denoted as
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
). The challenge is to match the young-age photographs to the corresponding old-age pictures of the same actors. In this scenario, if we lack labels such as the actors’ names in the images, we face the challenge that each
X_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
corresponds to a certain
Y_{j}\in\mathcal{Y}_{n}
, yet the specific pairings are unknown. This type of data is called
shuffled data
, and the corresponding problem is termed as
shuffled regression/classification
problem.
Another common application of shuffled regression, frequently encountered in ecological regression, involves the analysis of voting patterns among groups of individuals. For instance, consider a scenario where two competing political parties, party A and party B, exist within a region of
n
voters. Each voter is represented by a pair of variables
(X_{i},Y_{i})
, where
X_{i}
is their background information, and
Y_{i}
is a binary indicator for their vote (with
Y_{i}=1
denoting a vote for party A and
Y_{i}=0
indicating a vote for party B). We do not observe
\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\}
, i.e., the specific votes of the individuals, but we have information about the total votes garnered by parties A and B in the region. Consequently, we possess a permuted version of
{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}}
to ensure privacy and anonymity. There is a vast body of literature on a variety of applications and analyses of ecological regression; interested readers may consult
[
30
,
38
,
41
,
66
,
4
,
13
]
and references therein.
In addition to ecological regression, permuted or shuffled data are frequently encountered in various other fields of research.
For example, the multi-target tracking problem (
[
53
,
24
]
), aims to match measurements obtained from different environments.
In computer vision, shuffled regression is used to match two different sets of images of a group of objects, where one set of images may be a noisier/rotated/distorted version of the other (e.g., see
[
33
,
62
]
for more details).
In
[
44
]
, the authors studied a permuted monotone matrix model motivated by bacterial growth dynamics based on genome assemblies.
Another application of the shuffled regression lies in the seriation problem is statistics, as analyzed in
[
28
]
. The seriation problem has found its application in various fields, including but not limited to sociology (
[
29
]
), biology (
[
59
]
), archaeology (
[
52
]
), and anthropology (
[
15
]
).

So far, we have presented examples where we have access to
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
and
\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\}
, but we do not know which
X_{i}
corresponds to which
Y_{i}
.
However, in many other applications,
\mathcal{X}_{n}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
may be generated from different units.
Consider the example of finding the relation between wages and housing transactions as presented in
[
7
]
.
In general, we expect a monotonically increasing relationship between these two variables, i.e., individuals with higher incomes are likely to pay more for housing.
However, it is almost impossible to find a set of data in which we have information about the income and housing transactions of the same group of people, as different agencies typically collect information regarding wage and housing prices.
Therefore, we have some realization of
X
variable (wage), say
\mathcal{X}_{m}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{m}\}
and some other realizations of
Y
(housing price), say
\mathcal{Y}_{n}=\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\}
.
Furthermore,
\mathcal{X}_{m}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
can be independent as they can be collected from different groups of people or may contain some overlap, i.e., some individuals are present in both groups.
This type of data is called
unlinked data
, and the corresponding statistical problem to comprehend the relation between
X
and
Y
is called
unlinked regression/classification
.
Estimation of monotone link function (i.e., when
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]=m_{0}(X)
for some monotone function of
X
) using unlinked data is even less explored in the literature, with notable exceptions are
[
7
]
and
[
2
]
. In
[
2
]
, the authors first rigorously established that the monotone link function is identifiable and proposed an estimator of
m_{0}
under various smoothness assumptions on the tail of the characteristic function of the error distribution.

A common thread between shuffled and unlinked regression lies in the absence of links between the pair
(X,Y)
, i.e., we do not know which
X
corresponds to which
Y
.
This poses a natural question regarding the informativeness of these unpaired
X
’s in estimating the signal/mean function.
The key distinction between shuffled and unlinked regression is that in shuffled regression, we possess the set of
X_{i}
’s corresponding to the set of
Y_{i}
’s. In contrast, in unlinked regression,
X_{i}
’s can be entirely independent of
Y_{i}
’s, i.e., they can be obtained from two separate datasets.
Consider the following additive noise model with a monotone link function studied in this paper:

Y_{i}=m_{0}(X_{i})+\sigma_{n}\delta_{i}\,,

(1.1)

where
\sigma_{n}
represents the scale of the error that may change with
n
.
At first blush, it appears that shuffled regression provides
more
information than unlinked regression, which is indeed true for the noiseless case where
Y=m_{0}(X)
; in shuffled regression, we can recover the precise link between
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
as
Y_{(i)}=m_{0}(X_{(i)})
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, where
X_{(j)}
(resp.
Y_{(j)}
) denotes the
j^{th}
order statistic of
X_{i}
’s (resp.
Y_{i}
’s). Conversely, no such recovery is possible for unlinked regression, even in the absence of noise. However, at the other extreme (i.e., when the noise variance is of the order
1
), whether the shuffled regression model holds an advantage over the unlinked regression model for estimating the underlying mean function is still being determined.
Hence, it is natural to anticipate that a threshold exists regarding the magnitude of noise: if the standard deviation of the noise falls below that threshold, shuffled data is more informative than unlinked data, resulting in a fast estimation of the signal.
This observation is the key motivation of our paper: to quantify the precise dependence of the noise variance on the estimation rate of
m_{0}
and determine a threshold below which the shuffled regression model exhibits a faster rate for estimating
m_{0}
compared to the unlinked regression.

Estimation of the mean function under the shuffled and the unlinked model shares a resemblance with the classical deconvolution problem.
The relationship between the models we are interested in and the deconvolution model has also led us to study the deconvolution model from a minimax point of view under vanishing noise.
In deconvolution, the parameter of interest is the distribution (or its functional) of some random variable
Z
(say, the signal of some experiment).
However, we do not observe any realization of
Z
; rather, we observe
n
independent observations
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
generated as
Y_{i}=Z_{i}+{\epsilon}_{i}
where
{\epsilon}_{i}
is independent from
Z_{i}
.
In other words, we have access to the realizations of a random variable
Y
, whose distribution is a convolution between the distribution of
Z
(parameter of interest) and the distribution of error
{\epsilon}
(to relate it to (
1.1
), we may think
Z_{i}=m_{0}(X_{i})
and
{\epsilon}=\sigma_{n}\delta
).
Therefore, one needs to
deconvolute
the
distribution of
{\epsilon}
from the distribution of
Y
to get back to the distribution of
Z
.
Analysis of such deconvolution problems boasts a rich historical background and has found extensive applications across various domains. Examples include its use in medical imaging (
[
46
,
40
]
), astronomy (
[
60
,
37
,
43
]
), signal processing (
[
35
,
45
,
36
]
), image deblurring (
[
56
,
68
,
32
]
), and volatility estimation (
[
11
,
65
,
47
]
).
From a statistical perspective, the deconvolution problem was initially studied in a series of papers, including
[
26
,
25
,
27
,
61
,
69
,
9
]
, where the authors establish the minimax optimal rate of the deconvolution problem depending on the tail of the characteristic function of
{\epsilon}
and construct estimators of the distribution/density of
Z
that are minimax rate optimal, primarily via a kernel smoothing technique under certain smoothness assumptions on the distribution of
Z
.
The loss function typically used is the
L_{p}
distance between the estimated density or distribution of
Z
and the true one.
This line of research established that the rate of convergence is particularly slow if the tail of the characteristic function of the error is very thin (e.g., Gaussian distribution), where the rate decays polynomially in
\log{n}
(
n
is the number of observations), e.g., Theorem 1 and 4 of
[
26
]
.
Interested readers may consult
[
14
]
for further references.
More recently, deconvolution with respect to the Wasserstein metric has gained significant attention in the literature.
One key reason is that Wasserstein distance is a flexible metric for the space of all probability distributions.
It provides a non-trivial notion of distance between two measures even when they have non-overlapping/disjoint support. Furthermore, the Wasserstein deconvolution problem is related to geometric inference, which aims to recover various geometric properties (e.g., Betti number) of a set upon observing some points from that set (e.g., see
[
10
,
6
]
). The minimax optimal rate of Wasserstein deconvolution in the presence of supersmooth error (error whose characteristic function has a thin tail, precisely defined later) in any dimension has been established in
[
17
]
and for ordinary smooth error (errors whose characteristic function has a thick tail) in one dimension is investigated in
[
16
]
. As before, errors with thin-tailed characteristic functions lead to a very slow rate of convergence for the Wasserstein deconvolution problem.

Although theoretically, the rate of deconvolution can be very slow,
general deconvolution estimators seem to work well in practice, even in the presence of noise with thin-tailed characteristic functions. One way to explain this phenomenon, as illustrated in
[
19
]
, is that the scale of the error is small. In other words, it is intuitive to expect that if the standard deviation of the error is small, then estimation of the signal density should be easier. The rate of convergence of the deconvolution problem involving the error variance was initially investigated in
[
27
]
. Later,
[
19
]
established the rate of convergence of a deconvolution kernel density estimator when the noise variance
\sigma_{n}^{2}
goes to
0
as
n\uparrow\infty
, and the distribution of the signal is smooth. However, the minimax optimal rate of estimation with respect to the variance of the error is not known. In this paper, we bridge this gap. As in
[
19
]
, we assume that observations take the form
Y_{i}=Z_{i}+\sigma_{n}\delta_{i}
where we assume
\sigma_{n}\downarrow 0
as
n\uparrow\infty
(similar to (
1.1
) with
m_{0}(X_{i})
replaced by
Z_{i}
), but we do not make any smoothness assumption on the signal distribution. Here, we consider the Wasserstein deconvolution problem, i.e., we aim to estimate the distribution of
Z
and measure the estimation error with respect to the Wasserstein metric.
We summarize our contributions below:

Our contributions:
Our primary contribution in this paper is to present an asymptotic comparison of the minimax risks of the unlinked regression and the shuffled regression problems under a vanishing noise condition. In particular, we study the model (
1.1
) under the assumption that the noise standard deviation
\sigma_{n}
goes to
0
as
n\uparrow\infty
, without any smoothness assumption on the link function
m_{0}
, and quantify the minimax risk in terms of
(n,\sigma_{n})
. We also construct an estimator that attains the minimax risk. Our analysis reveals a phase-transition phenomenon: there exists a threshold of order (close to)
n^{-1/2}
, such that if
\sigma_{n}
is below that threshold, then the minimax risk of estimation of
m_{0}
in shuffled regression is smaller than that of the unlinked regression. On the other hand, the minimax risks are of the same order in the two problems when
\sigma_{n}
is larger than the threshold.
Furthermore, we also establish the minimax risk (in terms of
(n,\sigma_{n})
) for estimating the signal distribution in the Wasserstein-deconvolution problem when the error standard deviation
\sigma_{n}\downarrow 0
as
n\uparrow\infty
and the characteristic function of
\delta
has a thin tail. Our theory also indicates that the minimax risk of the unlinked regression and deconvolution has the same order irrespective of the value of
\sigma_{n}
, i.e., both problems showcase a similar level of difficulty.

Organization of the paper:
We organize our paper as follows: in Section
2
, we analyze the deconvolution problem under vanishing noise conditions and establish both minimax upper and lower bounds on the estimation of the distribution of the signal. In Sections
3
and
4
, we present our results for the unlinked and shuffled regression models, respectively. In Section
5
, we present some simulation results in the context of a conjecture (Conjecture
4.4
). Finally, we end this paper with our conclusion and some future research direction in Section
6
. The proofs of the main results are postponed to Appendix
A
whereas the proofs of intermediate results are given in Appendix
B
.

2
Deconvolution under vanishing noise

This section presents our main results for the deconvolution problem with vanishing noise. In this setting, we observe
\mathcal{Y}_{n}=\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\}
where
Y_{i}=Z_{i}+\sigma_{n}\delta_{i}
for some unobserved
\mathcal{Z}_{n}=\{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mu_{Z}
and
\{\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n}\}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mu_{\delta}
independent of
\mathcal{Z}_{n}
. Here, we assume
E[\delta_{i}]=0
,
{\sf var}(\delta_{i})=1
, and
\sigma_{n}
is a bounded sequence that is allowed to approach zero as
n\uparrow\infty
. Moreover, as is customary in deconvolution problems for identifiability purposes,
\sigma_{n}
, and the distribution
\mu_{\delta}
are assumed to be known.
We address the problem of Wasserstein deconvolution, i.e., we aim to estimate
\mu_{Z}
(the distribution of the signal) based on observed
Y_{i}
’s and evaluate the performance of an estimator via
W_{1}
-Wasserstein distance between that estimator and
\mu_{Z}
. Recall that, given any two distributions
(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
on
\mathbb{R}
, the
W_{1}
distance between them is defined as:

W_{1}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\int\int|x-y|\pi(dx,dy),

where
\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
is the collection of all possible coupling
\pi
with the marginals
(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
.

Before presenting the main results, we first lay the setup.
Our first assumption is on the structure of the distribution of
Z
, namely
\mu_{Z}
, in terms of the finiteness of its first few moments as elaborated below:

Assumption 2.1

(Moments of
\mu_{Z}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}(M,a)
be the set of all distributions
\mu
whose
(a+2)^{th}
moment is bounded by
M
. Assume that
\mu_{Z}\in\mathcal{D}(M,a)
for some
a,M>0
.

The above assumption is quite general in that it only requires a few finite moments for the signal distribution. Unlike several other research papers, we do not need the distribution of
Z
to be smooth or even have a density function.
For any distribution
\mu
, we denotes its characteristic function by
\mu^{*}(t)
, defined as:

\mu^{*}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}[{\sf exp}{(itX)}]=\int e^{itx}\ d\mu(x)\,.

The rate of estimation of
\mu
in this deconvolution problem typically depends on the tail of the characteristic function of the error distribution
\mu_{\delta}
(e.g., see
[
26
]
).
Roughly speaking, if the tail of
\mu_{\delta}^{*}(t)
is
thin
, then the deconvolution problem is harder than when the tail of
\mu_{\delta}^{*}(t)
is
thicker
. Based on the thickness of the tail of the characteristic function, the error distribution in the deconvolution problem can be broadly classified into two categories: i) supersmooth error and ii) ordinary smooth error (e.g., see
[
26
,
17
]
). In this paper, we work with the supersmooth error, which is defined below:

Definition 2.2

(Supersmooth distribution)
.

The distribution
\mu
is said to be supersmooth if its characteristic function
\mu^{*}
satisfies the following bound for some positive
\gamma_{1},c_{1},\beta
, and
\tilde{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}
:

|\mu^{*}(t)|(1+|t|)^{-\tilde{\beta}}{\sf exp}(|t|^{\beta}/\gamma_{1})\leq c_{1}\mbox{ for all }t\in\mathbb{R}.

In other words, a distribution is supersmooth if the modulus of its characteristic function decays exponentially as
t\uparrow\infty
. Henceforth, we assume that the distribution of
\delta
is supersmooth:

Assumption 2.3
.

The distribution of
\delta
is supersmooth with some parameter
(c_{1},\tilde{\beta},\beta,\gamma_{1})
as mentioned in Definition
2.2
.

In the sequel, we denote by
\mu_{\epsilon}
the distribution of
\epsilon_{i}:=\sigma_{n}\delta_{i}
so that the distribution of the sample
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
is
(\mu_{Z}\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n}
where
\star
stands for the convolution operation, and
\otimes n
denotes the
n
-fold product measure. Under the above assumption on the distribution of
\delta
, we have the following minimax lower bound on the Wasserstein deconvolution problem:

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
(\sigma_{n})
be a bounded sequence of positive numbers and the distribution of
Z
satisfies Assumption
2.1
for some
a>0
and
M>0
.
If the error distribution
\mu_{\delta}
satisfies Assumption
2.3
and
E|\delta|^{2+a}<\infty
then for sufficiently large
M
, there exists
C>0
such that for all
n
,

\inf_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}(M,a)}E_{(\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\mu,\hat{\mu}_{n})\geq C\left(\sigma_{n}\left(\log n\right)^{-1/\beta}+n^{-1/2}\right)

(2.1)

where the infimum is over all estimators
\hat{\mu}_{n}
of the distribution of
Z
based on the observed responses
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
.

Theorem
2.4
quantifies a lower bound on the minimax risk of the Wasserstein deconvolution problem in terms of
(n,\sigma_{n})
.
It is evident from this theorem that the lower bound is of the order of
\sigma_{n}\left(\log n\right)^{-1/\beta}
if
\sigma_{n}\geq n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{1/\beta}
, and of the order of
n^{-1/2}
otherwise.
It is instructive to compare our lower bound with Theorem 2 of
[
17
]
. In that theorem, the authors obtained the lower bound
(\log{n})^{-p/\beta}
for
W_{p}^{p}
metric. Setting
p=1
, their results yield the lower bound
(\log{n})^{-1/\beta}
, which coincides with ours when
\sigma_{n}=1
. The key difference between our result and that of
[
17
]
is the quantification of the precise effect of
\sigma_{n}
in the lower bound. Our lower bound shows that the smaller the value of
\sigma_{n}
, the faster one can estimate
\mu_{Z}
based on
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
.
However, as the theorem shows, the fastest achievable rate is
n^{-1/2}
. This is intuitive because
n^{-1/2}
is the price one must pay even when
\sigma_{n}=0
, i.e.
Y_{i}=Z_{i}
, as
n^{-1/2}
is the fastest possible rate at which one can estimate
\mu_{Z}
upon observing
n
i.i.d. observations
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
.

Now that we have a lower bound for the minimax risk of the Wasserstein deconvolution problem, we turn to establish an upper bound. For the upper bound, we need an additional assumption on
\mu^{*}_{\delta}
, the characteristic function of
\delta
. Towards that end, define
r_{\delta}=1/\mu_{\delta}^{*}
, the reciprocal of the characteristic function, and for
\ell=0,1,2
, we denote by
r_{\delta}^{(\ell)}
the
\ell
-th derivative of
r_{\delta}
(hence,
r_{\delta}^{(0)}=r_{\delta}
). We make the following assumption on the function
r_{\delta}
and its derivatives:

Assumption 2.5
.

The distribution of
\delta
has a symmetric density around zero with a positive characteristic function, and there exist positive
\gamma_{2}
,
c_{2}
,
\beta
,
\tilde{\beta}
, such that for every
\ell\in\{0,1,2\}
and every
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

|r_{\delta}^{(\ell)}(t)|\leq c_{2}(1+|t|^{\tilde{\beta}}){\sf exp}(|t|^{\beta}/\gamma_{2}).

The above assumption is primarily motivated by the assumption of Theorem 4 of
[
17
]
. One example is the Gaussian distribution, which satisfies the above constraint. Our following theorem presents an upper bound on the minimax risk of the Wasserstein deconvolution problem.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
(\sigma_{n})
be a bounded sequence of positive numbers,

a,M,\eta
be positive numbers where
\eta
is arbitrarily small. Assume that the distribution of
Z
satisfies Assumption
2.1
,
E|\delta|^{a+2}\leq M
, and
r_{\delta}
is twice continuously differentiable and satisfies Assumption
2.5
. Then, there exists a constant
L>0
such that for any small
\eta>0
:

\inf_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}(M,a)}E_{(\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\mu,\hat{\mu}_{n})\leq Lv_{n}

where

\displaystyle v_{n}=\begin{cases}\sigma_{n}\left(\log\left(n\sigma_{n}^{2}\log n\right)\right)^{-1/\beta}&\mbox{ if }\sigma_{n}\geq n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{\eta}\\
n^{-1/2}(\log\log n)^{-1/\beta}(\log n)^{\eta}&\mbox{ if }\sigma_{n}\in(n^{-1/2},n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{\eta})\\
n^{-1/2}&\mbox{ if }\sigma_{n}\leq n^{-1/2}\end{cases}

(2.2)

In the above theorem, one may take
\eta>0
to be as close to
0
as possible.
Combining Theorems
2.4
and
2.6
gives the minimax rates in the deconvolution model with supersmooth noise for an extensive range of noise levels that are allowed to tend to zero as
n\to\infty
, provided that the noise distribution satisfies simultaneously Assumptions
2.3
and
2.5
.
As mentioned previously, centered Gaussian distribution satisfies both assumptions with
\beta=2
. Let us now compare the upper and lower bounds of the deconvolution problem as obtained in Theorem
2.4
and Theorem
2.6
to elaborate on when they match exactly and when there is a gap:

1.

If
\sigma_{n}\geq n^{-1/2+\eta}
or
\sigma_{n}\leq n^{-1/2}
then the upper and lower bound on the minimax risk match exactly, which is equal to
\sigma_{n}\left(\log n\right)^{-1/\beta}
in the former case and
n^{-1/2}
in the latter case.

2.

For the intermediate values of
n^{-1/2}\leq\sigma_{n}\leq n^{-1/2+\eta}
, then although the lower and upper bounds do not match completely, they match up to a log factor (that is negligible as compared to the rate close to
n^{-1/2}
) as described below:

(a)

If
\sigma_{n}\in(n^{-1/2},n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{\eta}],
then our upper bound for the minimax risk is
n^{-1/2}(\log\log n)^{-1/\beta}(\log n)^{\eta}
, whereas the lower bound is
n^{-1/2}
(assuming that
\eta<1/\beta
). Therefore the bounds match up to the factor
(\log n)^{\eta}/(\log\log n)^{1/\beta}
.

(b)

If
\sigma_{n}\in(n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{\eta},n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{1/\beta}],
then the upper bound is of the order of
\sigma_{n}(\log\log n)^{-1/\beta}
and the lower bound is
n^{-1/2}
(assuming again that
\eta<1/\beta
), which implies that the rates match up to the maximal factor
(\log n/\log\log n)^{1/\beta}
.

(c)

If
\sigma_{n}\in(n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{1/\beta},n^{-1/2+\eta}],
then the upper bound is at most
\sigma_{n}(\log\log n)^{-1/\beta}
and the lower bound is
\sigma_{n}(\log n)^{-1/\beta}
, i.e., the upper and lower bound match up to a factor of
(\log n/\log\log n)^{1/\beta}
.

3
Unlinked regression under vanishing noise

This section presents a theoretical analysis for estimating the monotone link function in the presence of unlinked data. Recall that, under the setup of the unlinked regression, the observations are assumed to be generated from the model
Y=m_{0}(X)+\sigma_{n}\delta
where
m_{0}
is a left-continuous non-decreasing function, the error
\delta\sim\mu_{\delta}
for some known distribution function
\mu_{\delta}
,
\sigma_{n}
is a known constant that is typically assumed to be less than or equal to
1
(may approaches to
0
as
n\uparrow\infty
or remains bounded/constant), and
X\sim\mu_{X}
for some unknown distribution
\mu_{X}
.
As the error scale is absorbed in
\sigma_{n}
,
\delta
is assumed to be centered, with variance 1, and independent of
X\in[0,1]
.
We observe
\mathcal{X}_{n}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
, a set of i.i.d. copies of
X
and
\mathcal{Y}_{n}=\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\}
, a set of i.i.d. copies of
Y
.
Here, the data
\mathcal{X}_{n}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
can potentially be collected from different samples (i.e., they can be independent), from the same sample with an unknown link or anything in between (i.e., there is a non-trivial intersection). The two samples could be of different sizes. Still, since we aim to compare the unlinked regression model with the shuffled regression model, in which both samples are necessarily of the same size, we restrict ourselves to the case of samples of the same size.

Our goal here is to estimate
m_{0}
based on our observations
\mathcal{X}_{n}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
and our knowledge about
(\mu_{\delta},\sigma_{n})
.
Although we assume that
X
is supported on
[0,1]
, our proofs generally apply to any compactly supported
X
. For all positive
M
and
a
, define
\mathcal{M}(M,a)
as the set of non-decreasing functions
m
on
[0,1]
that are left-continuous with right-hand limits at every point (LCRR) and satisfy:

\int|m(x)|^{a+2}d\mu_{X}(x)\leq M\,.

(3.1)

Henceforth we assume
m_{0}\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)
for some
(M,a)
. Therefore, our parameter space is general, incorporating non-smooth and unbounded non-decreasing functions. One can also identify
\mathcal{M}(M,a)
with the set of all quantile functions on
{\mathbb{R}}
with certain moment conditions. The minimax risk of estimating
m_{0}
is defined as:

\mathcal{R}_{U}(M,a,n,\mu):=\inf_{\widehat{m}\in\mathcal{M}}\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}\sup_{\pi\in\Pi(m,n)}E_{\pi}\left[\int|\widehat{m}-m|d\mu\right]

(3.2)

where
\Pi(m,n)
is the set of distributions of observations
\mathcal{X}_{n}\cup\mathcal{Y}_{n}
with
m_{0}=m
, and
\mu
can be either the population distribution
\mu_{X}
of
X
(in which case the minimax risk is called
the population minimax risk
) or the empirical distribution
\hat{\mu}_{X}
of the observations
\mathcal{X}_{n}
(in which case the minimax risk is called
the empirical minimax risk
).

Here the infimum is taken over all monotone estimators of
m
, i.e.
\widehat{m}\in\mathcal{M}
where
\mathcal{M}
is the set of all LCRR functions on
[0,1]
(more specifically, set of all quantile functions).
Similar to the preceding section, our primary focus is on quantifying the specific impact of
\sigma_{n}
on the minimax risk of estimation, particularly when
\sigma_{n}
approaches zero as
n
increases.
We start with a lower bound on the minimax risk (as defined in (
3.2
)) on the unlinked regression model in terms of
(\sigma_{n},n)
, presented in the theorem below:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(\sigma_{n})
be a bounded sequence of positive numbers,
a>0
and
M>0
.
Assume that the characteristic function of the error
\delta
satisfies Assumption
2.3
,
that
E|2\delta|^{2+a}\leq M
, and that
X
has a continuous distribution. Then we have:

\mathcal{R}_{U}(M,a,n,\mu)\geq C\left(\sigma_{n}\left(\log{n}\right)^{-1/\beta}+n^{-1/2}\right)\,,

for all
\sigma_{n}>0
, both for
\mu=\mu_{X}
or
\mu=\hat{\mu}_{X}
.

It is instructive to compare the rate obtained in the above theorem with that of Theorem
2.4
; in particular, we obtain the same lower bound as in the deconvolution problem. This indicates that the unlinked regression problem is, at most, as hard as the deconvolution problem. If this lower bound is sharp, then the minimax risk of unlinked regression and deconvolution share the same scaling law concerning
(n,\sigma_{n})
. That these two problems are related has previously been alluded to in
[
7
]
and
[
2
]
, however, without rigorous justification. The critical difference between these two problems is the observed
\mathcal{X}_{n}
, but as it seems, it does not contribute to the rate of estimation of the signal distribution, i.e., the distribution of
Z:=m_{0}(X)
. Nevertheless, in the unlinked regression model, we are interested in estimating
m_{0}
, and the loss function is the
L_{1}
-distance w.r.t. distribution of
X
, and without
\mathcal{X}_{n}
we cannot identify
m_{0}
.

In the definition of the minimax risk
\mathcal{R}_{U}(M,a,n,\mu)
(equation (
3.2
)), we restricted ourselves to monotone estimators of
m_{0}
, as that is our parameter space.
In the following corollary, we show that the infimum could be relaxed to contain all possible estimators (including non-monotone estimators of
m_{0}
) both for
\mu=\mu_{X}
(population distribution of
X
) or
\hat{\mu}_{X}
(empirical distribution of
\mathcal{X}_{n}
) in (
3.2
), provided that
X
has a density that is bounded from above and away from zero:

Corollary 3.2
.

Under the same setting and assumptions pertaining to Theorem
3.1
, the same minimax lower bound holds
even if the infimum is extended to all possible estimators (not necessarily monotone) as long as
X
has a density bounded from above and bounded away from zero on its support.

Now that we have a lower bound for the minimax risk, we turn to an upper bound. For this task, we assume that
X
has a density bounded from above and away from zero on its support.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
(\sigma_{n})
be a sequence of bounded positive numbers. Assume that the error
\delta
satisfies Assumption
2.5
, that
E|\delta|^{a+2}\leq M
, and that
X
has a density that is bounded between
c_{X}
and
C_{X}
on its support where
c_{X}>0
is known. Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{U}(M,a,n,\mu)\leq Lv_{n}

for
\mu\in\{\mu_{X},\hat{\mu}_{X}\}
, where

v_{n}
is same as defined in Theorem
2.6
.

It is evident from the above theorem that
we obtain the same upper bound as in the deconvolution problem (Theorem
2.6
).
Since the lower bounds are the same in the two problems, the minimax risk of unlinked regression and deconvolution share the same scaling law concerning
(n,\sigma_{n})
. Hence, the minimax rates given in Table
1
for the deconvolution problem also apply to the unlinked regression problem. Consequently, both problems share the same inherent difficulty regarding the rate of estimation of the underlying parameter of interest.

4
Shuffled regression under vanishing noise

In this Section, we present our theoretical analysis for the shuffled regression model.
Recall that, in shuffled regression, the i.i.d. pairs
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
are generated from the model
Y_{i}=m_{0}(X_{i})+\sigma_{n}\delta_{i}
where
\delta_{i}
is centered, has variance 1, and is independent of
X_{i}\in[0,1]
. The distribution of
\delta
and the standard-error
\sigma_{n},
are assumed to be known.
We only observe the samples
\mathcal{X}_{n}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{n}=\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\}
, where each
Y_{i}
is generated by some
X_{j}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
, but we do not now which
X_{j}
, as if the
X
values got shuffled. Another equivalent assumption is that we observe
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
and
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{ord}}=\{X_{(1)}\leq X_{(2)}\leq\dots\leq X_{(n)}\}
, i.e. the
the order statistics of
X_{i}
’s, and this is what we assume in this section.
In general, the set of covariables
\mathcal{X}_{n}
could be either random or deterministic, but since we aim to compare the shuffled regression model with the unlinked regression model, in which the covariates have to be random, we restrict ourselves here to the case of random covariates.

The monotone link function
m_{0}
is our parameter of interest.
The retrieval of an unknown monotone link function in shuffled regression has recently been explored in
[
55
]
and
[
58
]
. However, the previous papers investigated the
fixed design
model, i.e.,
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are assumed to be fixed, whereas as mentioned above, we focus on the
random design
model. Moreover, contrary to earlier literature, we allow for unbounded regression function
m_{0}
, and we study the effect of a diminishing variance
\sigma_{n}
explicitly in the rate of estimation. Estimation of
m_{0}
in the presence of random
X
s, all the more so under vanishing error variance, remains largely unknown. We aim to bridge this gap in this subsection.

Akin to the unlinked regression model, here also the true link function
m_{0}
is assumed to be in
\mathcal{M}(M,a)
(see equation (
3.1
)) for some positive
a
and
M
. The minimax risk of estimation of
m_{0}
is defined as follows:

\mathcal{R}_{S}(M,a,n,\mu):=\inf_{\widehat{m}\in\cal M}\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}E_{m}\left[\int|\widehat{m}-m|d\mu_{X}\right]

where
E_{m}
denotes the expectation with respect to the joint distribution of
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
and
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{ord}}
when
m_{0}=m
,
\mu
can be either the population distribution
\mu_{X}
of
X
(in which case the minimax risk is called
the population minimax risk
) or the empirical distribution
\hat{\mu}_{X}
of the observations
\mathcal{X}_{n}
(in which case the minimax risk is called
the empirical minimax risk
),
and infimum is taken over the set
\mathcal{M}
of all non-decreasing functions
m
on
[0,1]
that are left-continuous with right-hand limits at every point.

We first compute an upper bound on the minimax risk. We obtain a general bound that is valid under arbitrary noise distribution, which is of the order of
\sigma_{n}
for the empirical risk, and possibly a slightly larger order for the population risk. This bound is of particular interest when
\sigma_{n}
is small. For larger
\sigma_{n}
we obtain a more precise bound under supersmooth noise. The bounds are given in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(\sigma_{n})
be a sequence of bounded positive numbers. Suppose that
X
has a density bounded below by a known
c_{X}>0
on its support and
m_{0}
satisfies (
3.1
) for some
(a,M)>0
.
Then we have the following upper bound on the empirical minimax risk given
E\delta^{2}<\infty
:

\mathcal{R}_{S}(M,a,n,\hat{\mu}_{X})\leq K\sigma_{n}

where
K>0
does not depend on
n
, and for the population minimax risk we have:

\mathcal{R}_{S}(M,a,n,\mu_{X})\leq K\left(\sigma_{n}+n^{-\frac{a+1}{a+2}}\log{n}\right)\,.

Finally, assume that the error
\delta
satisfies Assumption
2.5
, that
E|\delta|^{a+2}\leq M
, and that
X
has a density that is bounded between
c_{X}
and
C_{X}
on its support where
c_{X}>0
is known. Then, if
\sigma_{n}\geq n^{-1/2}
and
\eta>0
is arbitrarily small, we have

\mathcal{R}_{S}(M,a,n,\mu)\leq K\min\left\{\sigma_{n},v_{n}\right\}.

where
v_{n}
is as defined in (
2.2
).

It is instructive to compare the upper bounds of Theorem
4.1
with the bounds for the unlinked monotone regression obtained in Section
3
. To do that, first note that the last inequality in Theorem
4.1
concludes that if
\sigma_{n}\in(n^{-1/2},n^{-1/2}(\log\log n)^{-1/\beta}(\log n)^{\eta}]
, then

\mathcal{R}_{S}(M,a,n,\mu)\leq K\sigma_{n},,

and iif
\sigma_{n}\geq n^{-1/2}(\log\log n)^{-1/\beta}(\log n)^{\eta}
, then

\mathcal{R}_{S}(M,a,n,\mu)\leq Kv_{n}

Hence, the upper bounds of the unlinked and the shuffled regression matches for large
\sigma_{n}
. As mentioned in the previous section, it is evident from Theorem
3.1
, that one can never estimate
m_{0}
faster than
n^{-1/2}
in the unlinked regression setup, even if
\sigma_{n}=0
. However, for the shuffled regression, the empirical and population minimax risks decay at a rate faster than
n^{-1/2}
as soon as
\sigma_{n}\ll n^{-1/2}
. This reveals that estimation of
m_{0}
using shuffled data is easier than unlinked data in the presence of a small noise.

Now we present our results corresponding to the lower bound on the minimax risk of monotone shuffled regression. In Theorem
4.1
, the upper bound for the population risk consists of two terms:
\sigma_{n}
and
n^{-(a+1)/(a+2)}\log{n}
. We first focus on the second term, which is free of
\sigma_{n}
which indicates that the upper bound cannot be faster than
n^{-(a+1)/(a+2)}\log{n}
even if
\sigma_{n}=0
. The case
\sigma_{n}=0
deserves special attention in the shuffled regression model.
In the absence of noise, we can recover the unobserved link between
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{ord}}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
precisely as
Y_{(i)}=m_{0}(X_{(i)})
due to the monotone non-decreasing nature of
m_{0}
.
Consequently, we know the precise value of
m_{0}
at the design points.
This is the reason why the bound that we have for the empirical minimax risk is zero when
\sigma_{n}=0
. However, we cannot recover
m_{0}
exactly over the whole interval
[0,1]
even if
\sigma_{n}=0
and the price we pay in the population minimax risk is precisely the second term, i.e.,
n^{-(a+1)/(a+2)}\log{n}
for the population minimax risk. The following theorem establishes that this price is minimax optimal up to a log factor:

Theorem 4.2
.

Consider the noiseless scenario, i.e.
Y_{i}=m_{0}(X_{i})
for some
m_{0}\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)
. Then we have:

\mathcal{R}_{S}(M,a,n,\mu_{X})\geq Cn^{-\frac{a+1}{a+2}}(\log{n})^{-\frac{1+{\epsilon}}{a+2}}\,,

for any small
{\epsilon}>0
and some constant
C>0
.

Theorem
4.2
underscores the importance of the extra factor
n^{-(a+1)/(a+2)}
of the upper bound in Theorem
4.1
.
However, the lower bound does not capture the optimal dependence on
\sigma_{n}
; furthermore, it is not apparent from Theorem
4.1
how the smoothness of the noise (in terms of the decay of its characteristic function) affects the rate of convergence. Obtaining a lower bound for the estimation of the monotone link function in a shuffled regression model is a challenging problem. To the best of our knowledge, it is yet to be addressed in full generality. The only known lower bound in this area is Theorem 3 of
[
55
]
, which is i) for the fixed design model and ii) for bounded regression function
m_{0}
(with a known bound for the sup-norm of
m_{0}
) and iii) under a fixed noise variance.
Moreover, extending the proof from
[
55
]
to accommodate the random design setup within their framework is not straightforward.
We take a step towards bridging this gap by obtaining a lower bound on the random design model when the errors are generated from a Gaussian distribution with mean
0
and variance
\sigma_{n}
, that is allowed to tend to zero as
n\to\infty
:

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
(\sigma_{n})
be a sequence of bounded positive numbers.
Consider the above setup where
X
has a continuous distribution and
Y=m_{0}(X)+\sigma_{n}\delta
, where we observe
\mathcal{Y}_{n},\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{ord}}
. Assume
\delta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Then, there exist positive constants
c,C
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{S}(M,a,n,\mu_{X})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle c\sigma_{n}\left(\frac{\log{n}}{\sqrt{\log{\log{n}}}}\right)^{-1}

provided that
\sigma_{n}\geq Cn^{-1/2}\log{n}(\log{\log{n}})^{-1/2}.

A few remarks are in order; the lower bound above reveals that for Gaussian noise with sufficiently large
\sigma_{n}
, the rate of estimation can never be faster than
\sigma_{n}\sqrt{\log{\log{n}}}/\log{n}
. Since the standard Gaussian distribution satisfies Assumption
2.3
with
\beta=2
, this rate is faster than the upper bound obtained for the minimax rate (see Theorem
4.1
), which is of the order
\sigma_{n}(\log{n})^{-1/2}
.
Therefore, we have a gap between the upper and lower bounds on the minimax risk of the shuffled regression problem, and it is not immediate whether the upper bound or the lower bound on the minimax risk is improvable.
Although we do not have a definite answer, we strongly believe that with a finer analysis, it is possible to improve the lower bound. In fact, we can refine Theorem
4.2
, so that the minimax lower bound matches with the minimax upper bound (which is also the minimax lower bound of the deconvolution problem, see Table
1
) if the following conjecture is true:

Conjecture 4.4
.

Let
N=(n_{1},\dots,n_{n})
be a multinomial vector with paramter
n
and probabilities
(n^{-1},\dots,n^{-1})
. Then for arbitrary
C>0
one can find
c>0
such that

\lim\inf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j\ s.t.\ n_{j}>0}\left(1-Ce^{-c\frac{\log n}{n_{j}}}\right)^{2}\right]>0

(4.1)

Here, both

are constants independent of
n

Theorem 4.5
.

Consider the above setup where

6
Conclusion and future work

This manuscript investigates the relationship between the monotone shuffled regression model and the monotone unlinked regression model, explicitly considering the vanishing variance condition where the noise variance approaches
0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
Our findings reveal a phase transition; if
\sigma_{n}
falls below a certain threshold close to
n^{-1/2}
, then the minmax risk of the shuffled regression model (in terms of both
n
and
\sigma_{n}
) decays faster than that of the unlinked regression model.
However, when
\sigma_{n}
is larger than the threshold, both problems exhibit similar minimax risk, which aligns with the minimax risk of the Wasserstein deconvolution problem. The main findings for Gaussian noise are summarized in Table
2
.

Nevertheless, several unresolved questions remain, serving as avenues for future investigations:

1.

Our analysis relies on supersmooth error, as defined in Definition
2.2
. It would be intriguing to explore whether similar phase transitions also occur with ordinary smooth errors – i.e., error distributions characterized by heavier-tailed characteristic functions.

2.

As the primary objective of this paper is to compare the minimax risk between unlinked and shuffled regression, we adopt a random design model, where the unobserved signal values are treated as random – a prerequisite for the unlinked regression model. In contrast, shuffled regression does not necessitate random signals. Notably, recent research by
[
55
,
58
]
explored monotone shuffled regression within a fixed design framework, assuming signals to be fixed unobserved points, albeit with
\sigma_{n}\asymp 1
.
Therefore, it is intriguing to investigate the minimax risk of shuffled regression in a fixed design scenario with a diminishing noise variance and possibly unbounded regression function.

3.

In this paper, we demonstrate that the minimax risk associated with shuffled regression, unlinked regression, and the deconvolution problem is of the same order when
\sigma_{n}
is not too small (of the order much larger than
n^{-1/2}
). This implies a comparable difficulty in estimating the signal distribution for all three problems. However, achieving a more
accurate
result would involve establishing asymptotic equivalence among these three experiments in the sense of Le-Cam (
[
42
]
). This endeavor calls for an entirely new set of tools and is deferred as an intriguing avenue for future exploration.

4.

Finally, our findings are contingent on the univariate signal. Extending these results for the multivariate signals would be a compelling direction for future exploration.

A.1
Notations and basic facts

Let
X
be a random variable supported on
[0,1]
with a continuous distribution.
For all
m\in\cal M
, we denote by
m^{-1}
the generalized inverse defined for
x\in\mathbb{R}
by

m^{-1}(x)=\sup\{t\in[0,1]\mbox{ such that }m(t)\leq x\}

(A.1)

with the convention that the supremum of an empty set is 0. By left-continuity of
m
we then have
m\circ m^{-1}(x)\leq x
for all
x\geq m(0)
, from which we derive that for all
t\neq 0
and all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have the equivalence

m(t)\leq x\Longleftrightarrow t\leq m^{-1}(x).

Hence, if we
define
\mu_{m}
as the distribution of the random variable
m(X)
, for all
m\in{\mathcal{M}}
, its distribution function is
F_{X}\circ m^{-1}
where
F_{X}
is the distribution function of
X
. (We used here that
X\neq 0
almost surely.)
Moreover,

\displaystyle\int|x|^{a+2}d\mu_{m}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int|m(x)|^{a+2}d\mu_{X}(x)

(A.2)

so
m
belongs to
\mathcal{M}(M,a)
defined in Section
3
if and only if the corresponding
\mu_{m}
belongs to
\mathcal{D}(M,a)
defined in Section
2
.

We denote by
\hat{\mu}_{X}
the empirical distribution of the
X
-sample
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
and by
\widehat{F}_{X}
and
\widehat{F}_{X}^{-1}
the corresponding distribution and quantile functions.

For all
m\in\cal M
,
\hat{\mu}_{m}
denotes the distribution that puts mass
n^{-1}
at each
m(X_{i}
),
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Using the above properties of the generalized inverse, one obtains that the corresponding distribution function is
\widehat{F}_{X}\circ m^{-1}
and the quantile function is
m\circ\widehat{F}_{X}^{-1}
.

We recall that from
[
3
, Theorem 2.10 and Theorem 2.9]
, for all probability measures
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
on
\mathbb{R}
with finite first moment and corresponding distribution functions
F
and
G
, and quantile functions
F^{-1}
and
G^{-1}
one has

W_{1}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{1}|F^{-1}(x)-G^{-1}(x)|dx

(A.3)

and

W_{1}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|F(x)-G(x)|dx.

(A.4)

Now, for arbitrary
m_{0},m_{1}\in\cal M
and
U
a uniform variable on
[0,1]
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|m_{0}(x)-m_{1}(x)|d\mu_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E|m_{0}(X)-m_{1}(X)|

(A.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E|m_{0}\circ F_{X}^{-1}(U)-m_{1}\circ F_{X}^{-1}(U)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu_{m_{0}},\mu_{m_{1}})

provided that
\mu_{m_{0}}
and
\mu_{m_{1}}
have a finite first moment, where we used (
A.3
) for the last equality. Similarly,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|m_{0}(x)-m_{1}(x)|d\hat{\mu}_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{m_{0}},\hat{\mu}_{m_{1}}).

(A.6)

Note that
\hat{\mu}_{m}
has finite first moment for any
m\in\cal M
as a discrete probability measure on a finite support.

A.2
Entropy results

We recall that the
\delta
-entropy with bracketing of a class of functions
\mathcal{M}
with respect to a norm

with

and the bracket

.

.

Suppose that

has a continuous distribution.
Then, for all positive

and

such that for all

\log N_{[]}(\delta,\mathcal{M}(M,a),\|\,.\,\|_{2})\leq L\delta^{-1}

.

Let

be a continuous distribution on

A.4
Proof of Theorem
2.4

By Lemma
A.3
,
\mu_{\theta}\in\mathcal{D}(M,a)
for all
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{b_{n}}
. Hence, Lemma
A.3
also proves that for all estimators
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
we have

\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}(M,a)}E_{(\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}_{n})\geq\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\theta}_{n}}E_{(\mu_{\tilde{\theta}_{n}}\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\tilde{\mu}_{n},\mu_{\tilde{\theta}_{n}})\geq C\sigma_{n}(\log n)^{-1/\beta}.

We shall prove below that for arbitrary estimator
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
we have

\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}(M,a)}E_{(\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}_{n})\geq Cn^{-1/2}.

(A.13)

Theorem
2.4
immediately follows from the previous two displays.

We turn to the proof of (
A.13
).
From the definition of Wasserstein distance
W_{1}
, for arbitrary distributions
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
we have

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu_{1}\star\mu_{\epsilon},\mu_{2}\star\mu_{\epsilon})=\inf_{Z_{1},Z_{2},\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}}E|(Z_{1}+\epsilon_{1})-(Z_{2}+\epsilon_{2})|

where the infimum is taken over all
\{Z_{1},Z_{2},\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\}
such that
Z_{i}
has distribution
\mu_{i}
and is independent of
\epsilon_{i}
that has distribution
\mu_{\epsilon}
, for
i=1
and
i=2
. Restricting the above infimum to cases where
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{2}
yields

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu_{1}\star\mu_{\epsilon},\mu_{2}\star\mu_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\inf_{Z_{1},Z_{2},\epsilon}E|(Z_{1}+\epsilon)-(Z_{2}+\epsilon)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{Z_{1},Z_{2}}E|Z_{1}-Z_{2}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\geq W_{1}(\mu_{1}\star\mu_{\epsilon},\mu_{2}\star\mu_{\epsilon}).

Hence,

\displaystyle\inf_{\tilde{\mu}_{n}}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}(M,a)}E_{(\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}_{n})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\inf_{\tilde{\mu}_{n}}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}(M,a)}E_{(\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon},\tilde{\mu}_{n}\star\mu_{\epsilon}).

By convexity of the function
x\mapsto x^{b}
where
b=a+2
, for
Y=Z+\epsilon
we have

\displaystyle E|Z|^{b}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{b-1}\left(E|Y|^{b}+E|\epsilon|^{b}\right)\leq M\,.

provided that
E|2Y|^{b}\leq M
and
E|2\epsilon|^{b}\leq M
. Hence,
\mu
belongs to
\mathcal{D}(M,a)
whenever
\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon}
belongs to
\mathcal{D}(2^{-b}M,a)
and therefore,

\displaystyle\inf_{\tilde{\mu}_{n}}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}(M,a)}E_{(\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}_{n})\geq\inf_{\tilde{\mu}_{n}}\sup_{\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{D}(2^{-b}M,a)}E_{(\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon},\tilde{\mu}_{n}\star\mu_{\epsilon}).

This implies that

\displaystyle\inf_{\tilde{\mu}_{n}}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}(M,a)}E_{(\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}_{n})\geq\inf_{\tilde{\mu}_{n}}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}(2^{-b}M,a)}E_{\mu^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}_{n})

where the infimum on the right hand side is now taken over all estimators
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
of the common distribution of the observations
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
.
Denote by
\mu_{0}
the standard Gaussian distribution and by
\mu_{1}
the Gaussian distribution with variance 1 and mean
n^{-1/2}
. For simplicity, we assume
M
large enough so that both
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
belong to
\mathcal{D}(2^{-b}M,a)
. (For smaller
M
, it suffices to normalize the above Gaussian laws to ensure that they both belong to
\mathcal{D}(2^{-b}M,a)
). We then have

\displaystyle\inf_{\tilde{\mu}_{n}}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}(M,a)}E_{(\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}_{n})\geq\inf_{\tilde{\mu}_{n}}\sup_{\mu\in\{\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\}}E_{\mu^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}_{n}).

We will show below that for the right hand side we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\tilde{\mu}_{n}}\sup_{\mu\in\{\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\}}E_{\mu^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}_{n})\geq Cn^{-1/2}

(A.14)

for some
C>0
. Equation (
A.13
) then follows from the previous two displays.

For an arbitrary estimator
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
, we denote by
\hat{\mu}_{n}
a distribution in
\{\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\}
that is closest to
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
in the
W_{1}
-distance (it does not matter how we handle ties).
For any
\mu\in\{\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\}
we then have

W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{n},\mu)\leq W_{1}(\tilde{\mu}_{n},\mu)+W_{1}(\tilde{\mu}_{n},\hat{\mu}_{n})\leq 2W_{1}(\tilde{\mu}_{n},\mu).

Hence,

\displaystyle E_{\mu^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\tilde{\mu}_{n},\mu)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}E_{\mu^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{n},\mu)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}P_{\mu^{\otimes n}}(\hat{\mu}_{n}\neq\mu)W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}).

Now, with
F_{0}
and
F_{1}
the respective distribution functions of
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
we have

W_{1}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F_{0}(t)-F_{1}(t)|dt=n^{-1/2}

where the first equality follows from
[
3
, Theorem 2.9]
whereas the second one follows from simple algebra combined to the definitions of
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
. Therefore,

\inf_{\tilde{\mu}_{n}}\sup_{\mu\in\{\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\}}E_{\mu^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}_{n})\geq\frac{n^{-1/2}}{2}\inf_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}\left(1-\min_{\mu\in\{\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\}}P_{\mu^{\otimes n}}(\hat{\mu}_{n}=\mu)\right).

Hence, it follows from Birgé’s inequality
[
31
, Theorem B.10]
that with
K
the Kullback divergence we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\tilde{\mu}_{n}}\sup_{\mu\in\{\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\}}E_{(\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}_{n})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{n^{-1/2}}{2}\left(1-\frac{2e}{2e+1}\vee\frac{nK(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}{\log 2}\right).

We have
K(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=n^{-1}/2
so the right-hand side of the above display is larger than
n^{-1/2}/13
. This completes the proof of (
A.14
), whence the proof ov (
A.13
) and the proof of Theorem
2.4
.
\Box

Proof of Theorem
3.1

A.6.1
Lower bound for
\mu_{X}

It follows from the definition of minimax risk for the unlinked regression model that

\mathcal{R}_{U}(M,a,n,\mu_{X})\geq\inf_{\widehat{m}}\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}E_{m}\left[\int_{0}^{1}|\widehat{m}-m|d\mu_{X}\right]

(A.17)

where for fixed
m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)
,
E_{m}
denotes the expectation under the distribution of observations
\mathcal{X}_{n}\cup\mathcal{Y}_{n}
, where
\mathcal{X}_{n}
is independent of
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
. Note that we can restrict the infimum on the right-hand side to those
\widehat{m}
for which
E_{m}\int|\widehat{m}|d\mu_{X}
is finite for all
m
since otherwise the supremum is infinite.
Let
\widehat{m}
be an arbitrary such estimator.
If the
X
-sample is independent of the
Y
-sample, then the conditional expectation
\widehat{m}^{Y}:=E_{m}(\widehat{m}|Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
does not depend on
m
and can be viewed as an estimator of
m_{0}
based solely on the
Y
-sample. By Jensen’s inequality for conditional expectations,

\displaystyle E_{m}|\widehat{m}-m|\geq E_{m}|\widehat{m}^{Y}-m|

and therefore, (
A.17
) still holds with the infimum restricted to estimators
\widehat{m}
that are solely based on observations
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
.

It is immediate from (
A.2
), that the measure
\mu_{m}
of
m(X)
has finite first moment for all
m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)
. This implies that in the definition (
3.2
) of the minimax risk with
\mu=\mu_{X}
, we can restrict the infimum to estimators
\widehat{m}
such that the corresponding measure
\mu_{\widehat{m}}
has finite first moment since otherwise, the supremum is infinite.
Hence, it follows from (
A.5
) that

\mathcal{R}_{U}(M,a,n,\mu_{X})\geq\inf_{\widehat{m}}\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}E_{m}W_{1}(\mu_{\widehat{m}},\mu_{m}).

Since for all
m
,
\mu_{m}
is the distribution of
m(X)
(with distribution function
F_{X}\circ m^{-1}
),
\mu_{\widehat{m}}
is a pseudo-estimator of the distribution of
m_{0}(X)
. Here, we call pseudo-estimator an estimator that is allowed to depend on
F_{X}
. Hence,

\mathcal{R}_{U}(M,a,n,\mu_{X})\geq\inf_{\hat{\mu}}\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}E_{m}W_{1}(\hat{\mu},\mu_{m})

where the infimum is extended to all pseudo-estimators of the distribution of
m_{0}(X)
that relies only on the
Y
-sample. As the
Y
-sample has distribution
(\mu_{m}\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n_{y}}
under
E_{m}
(for any
m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)
), we conclude:

\mathcal{R}_{U}(M,a,n,\mu_{X})\geq\inf_{\hat{\mu}}\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}E_{(\mu_{m}\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n_{y}}}W_{1}(\hat{\mu},\mu_{m}).

Recall that
m
belongs to
\mathcal{M}(M,a)
if and only if the corresponding
\mu_{m}
belongs to
\mathcal{D}(M,a)
(see (
A.2
)). Moreover, any
\mu\in\mathcal{D}(M,a)
can be written in the form
\mu=\mu_{m}
with
m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)
such that
m^{-1}=F_{X}^{-1}\circ L
with
L
the distribution function of
\mu
. Note that we use here the fact that
F_{X}\circ F_{X}^{-1}
is the identity function by continuity of the distribution of
X
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{U}(M,a,n,\mu_{X})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\mu}}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}(M,a)}E_{(\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n_{y}}}W_{1}(\hat{\mu},\mu)

where the infimum is taken over all pseudo-estimators that are based on the
Y
-sample. The conclusion of Theorem
3.1
now follows from Theorem
2.4
.

A.6.2
Lower bound for
\hat{\mu}_{X}

For arbitrary estimator
\widehat{m}
, let
\widetilde{m}
be an estimator in
\mathcal{M}(M,a)
such that

\int|\widehat{m}-\widetilde{m}|d\hat{\mu}_{X}\leq\inf_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}\int|\widehat{m}-m|d\hat{\mu}_{X}+\eta

where
\eta>0
is arbitrarily small.
It then follows from the triangle inequality that for all
m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)

\displaystyle\int|\widetilde{m}-m|d\hat{\mu}_{X}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int|\widehat{m}-m|d\hat{\mu}_{X}+\int|\widehat{m}-\widetilde{m}|d\hat{\mu}_{X}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\int|\widehat{m}-m|d\hat{\mu}_{X}+\eta

and therefore,

\mathcal{R}_{U}(M,a,n,\hat{\mu}_{X})\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\inf_{\widetilde{m}\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}\sup_{\pi}E_{\pi}\int|\widetilde{m}-m|d\hat{\mu}_{X}-\eta\right).

Combining with Lemma
A.2
yields that there exists
C>0
such that

\mathcal{R}_{U}(M,a,n,\hat{\mu}_{X})\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\inf_{\widetilde{m}\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}\sup_{\pi}E_{\pi}\int|\widetilde{m}-m|d\mu_{X}-Cn^{-1/2}-\eta\right).

We choose
\eta=Cn^{-1/2}
. If
\sigma_{n}\geq C^{\prime}n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{1/\beta}\vee n^{-1/2}
for some sufficiently large
C^{\prime}>0
, then it follows from the lower bound already obtained for
\mu_{X}
that there exists
c>0
such that the infimum in the right-hand side above is larger than

c\sigma_{n}\left(\log n\right)^{-1/\beta}

which is larger than
3Cn^{-1/2}
. Therefore,

\mathcal{R}_{U}(M,a,n,\hat{\mu}_{X})\geq\frac{c\sigma_{n}}{6}\left(\log n\right)^{-1/\beta}\,.

Now we consider the case when
\sigma_{n}\leq C^{\prime}n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{1/\beta}\vee n^{-1/2}
. As before, consider only the estimators of the form
\hat{m}
that solely relies on
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
.
For such estimators,

\displaystyle E_{m}\left[\int|\widehat{m}-m|d\hat{\mu}_{X}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{m}|\widehat{m}(X_{i})-m(X_{i})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{m}\int|\widehat{m}-m|d\mu_{X}.

Hence, similar as above it follows from (
A.5
) that

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{m}}\sup_{\pi}E_{\pi}\left[\int|\widehat{m}-m|d\hat{\mu}_{X}\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\mu}}\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}E_{(\mu_{m}\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\hat{\mu},\mu_{m})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\mu}}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}(M,a)}E_{(\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\hat{\mu},\mu)

where the infimum in the right-hand side is extended to all pseudo-estimators of the distribution of
m_{0}(X)
based on the
Y
-sample. It follows from Theorem
2.4
that there exists
C>0
such that the right-hand side is larger than
Cn^{-1/2}
, which completes the proof of
Theorem
3.1
.
\Box

A.7
Proof of Corollary
3.2

Assume that
X
has a density that is bounded from above and bounded away from zero.
Consider an arbitrary estimator
\widehat{m}
and let
\widehat{m}_{0}\in\cal M
be an estimator such that

\int_{0}^{1}|\widehat{m}(x)-\widehat{m}_{0}(x)|dx\leq\inf_{m\in\cal M}\int_{0}^{1}|\widehat{m}(x)-m(x)|dx+\eta

where
\eta>0
is arbitrarily small. We consider here integrals with respect to the Lebesgue measure because by assumption, it is known and equivalent to the unknown distribution of
X
. It follows from the triangle inequality and the definition of
\widehat{m}_{0}
that for all
m\in\cal M

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|\widehat{m}_{0}(x)-m(x)|dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|\widehat{m}_{0}(x)-\widehat{m}(x)|dx+\int_{0}^{1}|\widehat{m}(x)-m(x)|dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1}|\widehat{m}(x)-m(x)|dx+\eta.

Denoting by
c_{X}
and
C_{X}
two positive numbers such that
c_{X}\leq f_{X}\leq C_{X}

one obtains

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|\widehat{m}_{0}(x)-m(x)|d\mu_{X}(x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{X}\int_{0}^{1}|\widehat{m}_{0}(x)-m(x)|dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2C_{X}}{c_{X}}\int_{0}^{1}|\widehat{m}(x)-m(x)|d\mu_{X}(x)+C_{X}\eta.

Hence,

\inf_{\widehat{m}}\sup_{\pi}E_{\pi}\left[\int|\widehat{m}-m|d\mu_{X}\right]\geq\frac{c_{X}}{2C_{X}}\left(\inf_{\widehat{m}_{0}\in\cal M}\sup_{\pi}E_{\pi}\left[\int|\widehat{m}_{0}-m|d\mu_{X}\right]-C_{X}\eta\right).

Choosing
\eta
small enough so that
C_{X}\eta
is smaller than half the infimum in the right-hand side then yields

\inf_{\widehat{m}}\sup_{\pi}E_{\pi}\left[\int|\widehat{m}-m|d\mu_{X}\right]\geq\frac{c_{X}}{4C_{X}}\inf_{\widehat{m}\in\cal M}\sup_{\pi}E_{\pi}\left[\int|\widehat{m}-m|d\mu_{X}\right]

so same the lower bound on
\mu_{X}
than in Theorem
3.1
, with now the infimum extended to all possible estimators, follows from Theorem
3.1
.

To show that the lower bound on
\hat{\mu}_{X}
in Theorem
3.1
still holds if the infimum is extended to all possible estimators, for arbitrary estimator
\widehat{m}
we defined
\widehat{m}_{0}\in\cal M
as an estimator such that

\int_{0}^{1}|\widehat{m}-\widehat{m}_{0}|d\hat{\mu}_{X}\leq\inf_{m\in\cal M}\int_{0}^{1}|\widehat{m}-m|d\hat{\mu}_{X}+\eta

where
\eta>0
is arbitrarily small. Similar as above we then have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|\widehat{m}_{0}-m|d\hat{\mu}_{X}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1}|\widehat{m}-m|d\hat{\mu}_{X}+\eta

for all
m\in\cal M
, so repeating similar arguments as above completes the proof of
Corollary
3.2

\Box

A.8
Proof of Theorem
3.3

We consider a slight modification of the set
\mathcal{M}(M,a)
, namely
\mathcal{M}^{0}(M,a)
, which is the set of all functions
m\in\mathcal{M}
with:

\int|m(x)|^{a+2}dx\leq M/c_{X}.

It is immediate that
\mathcal{M}(M,a)\subset\mathcal{M}^{0}(M,a)
.
Note that, the set
\mathcal{M}^{0}(M,a)
does not depend on
\mu_{X}
, except on
c_{X}
that is assumed to be known.

We will build an estimator which maximal risk is smaller than
v_{n}
in order. The estimator is built in two steps. First, note that from Theorem
2.6
, it follows that there exists an estimator
\hat{\mu}_{Z}
of the distribution of
Z:=m_{0}(X)
based on the
Y
-sample that satisfies

\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}(M,a)}E_{(\mu\star\mu_{\epsilon})^{\otimes n}}W_{1}(\mu,\hat{\mu}_{Z})\leq Lv_{n}.

Second, we define our estimator for
m_{0}
as the minimum constrast estimator with respect to
W_{1}
-distance: the estimator
\widehat{m}_{n}
belongs to
\mathcal{M}^{0}(M,a)
and satisfies

W_{1}\left(\hat{\mu}_{Z},\hat{\mu}_{\widehat{m}_{n}}\right)\leq\inf_{m\in\mathcal{M}^{0}(M,a)}W_{1}\left(\hat{\mu}_{Z},\hat{\mu}_{m}\right)+\eta

where
\eta>0
is arbitrarily small. This is indeed an estimator since it does not depend on unknown parameters. Here for each
m\in\mathcal{M}^{0}(M,a)
,
\hat{\mu}_{m}
is the empirical measure that puts mass
n^{-1}
at each
m(X_{i}
),
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Hence,
\hat{\mu}_{m}
estimates the distribution of
m(X)
and our estimator minimizes the
W_{1}
-distance between this estimator and an estimator of the distribution of
m_{0}(X)
.

Since
X
has a bounded density,
\mathcal{M}^{0}(M,a)
is included in
\mathcal{M}(M^{\prime},a)
where
M^{\prime}=C_{X}M/c_{X}
with
c_{X}>0
and
C_{X}<\infty
are lower bound and the upper bound on the density of
X
. Hence,
\widehat{m}_{n}
belongs to
\mathcal{M}(M^{\prime},a)
so
it follows from Lemma
A.2
(with
M
replaced by
M^{\prime}
) that there exists
L>0
such that for all probability measures
\pi
that belong to
\Pi(m,n)
for some
m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)
we have

\displaystyle E_{\pi}\int|\widehat{m}_{n}-m|d\mu_{X}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle E_{\pi}\int|\widehat{m}_{n}-m|d\hat{\mu}_{X}+Ln^{-1/2}.

For the first term on the right-hand side, it follows from (
A.6
) that

\displaystyle\int|\widehat{m}_{n}-m|d\hat{\mu}_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{\widehat{m}_{n}},\hat{\mu}_{m}).

(A.18)

Since
\mathcal{M}(M,a)\subset\mathcal{M}^{0}(M,a)
, it follows from the triangle inequality combined to the definition of
\widehat{m}_{n}
that for all
m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)
,

\displaystyle\int|\widehat{m}_{n}-m|d\hat{\mu}_{X}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{\widehat{m}_{n}},\hat{\mu}_{Z})+W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{Z},\hat{\mu}_{m})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{Z},\hat{\mu}_{m})+\eta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{Z},\mu_{m})+2W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{m},\mu_{m})+\eta

where
\eta>0
is arbitrarily small. In the sequel we choose
\eta=n^{-1/2}
.
Due to (
A.2
), we have
\mu_{m}\in\mathcal{D}(M,a)
if and only if
m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)
so we obtain that there exists
L>0
such that

\displaystyle E_{\pi}\int|\widehat{m}_{n}-m|d\mu_{X}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Lv_{n}+Ln^{-1/2}+2E_{\pi}W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{m},\mu_{m}).

(A.19)

Now,
\hat{\mu}_{m}
is the empirical distribution of the sample
m(X_{1}),\dots,m(X_{n})
whereas
\mu_{m}
is the common distribution of those variables so by

[
3
, Theorem 3.2]
, with
F_{m}
the distribution function of
\mu_{m}
we have

\displaystyle E_{\pi}W_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{m},\mu_{m})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sqrt{F_{m}(t)(1-F_{m}(t))}dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2n^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{P\left(|m(X)|>t\right)}dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2n^{-1/2}\left(1+\frac{2\sqrt{M}}{a}\right)

where the last inequality is obtained with similar arguments as for the proof of (
A.15
).
Combining the two last displays yields that there exists
L>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}\sup_{\pi\in\Pi(m,n)}E_{\pi}\int|\widehat{m}_{n}-m|d\mu_{X}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Lv_{n}+Ln^{-1/2}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{U}(M,a,n,\mu)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}\sup_{\pi\in\Pi(m,n)}E_{\pi}\int|\widehat{m}_{n}-m|d\mu_{X}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Lv_{n}+Ln^{-1/2}.

The inequality in Theorem
3.3
with
\mu=\mu_{X}
follows since
v_{n}\geq n^{-1/2}
.

Now, since (as proved above)
\widehat{m}_{n}
belongs to
\mathcal{M}(M^{\prime},a)
where
M^{\prime}=C_{X}M/c_{X}
, the inequality with
\mu=\hat{\mu}_{X}
follows from Lemma
A.2
together with the the inequality with
\mu=\mu_{X}
. This completes the proof of Theorem
3.3
.

A.9
Proof of Theorem
4.1

We use the same notation
\mathcal{M}^{0}(M,a)
as in Section
A.8
.
Let
\hat{\mu}_{Y}
denote the empirical distribution generated by
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
, i.e.
\hat{\mu}_{Y}
puts mass
n^{-1}
at each
Y_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. For any
m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)
, we further recall that
\hat{\mu}_{m}
denotes the distribution that puts mass
n^{-1}
at each
m(X_{i}
),
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. With these notations at our disposal, we define the minimum contrast estimator
\widetilde{m}_{n}
as an element of
\mathcal{M}^{0}(M,a)
that satisfies :

W_{2}\left(\hat{\mu}_{Y},\hat{\mu}_{\widetilde{m}_{n}}\right)\leq\inf_{m\in\mathcal{M}^{0}(M,a)}W_{2}\left(\hat{\mu}_{Y},\hat{\mu}_{m}\right)+\eta

(A.20)

where
\eta>0
is arbitrarily small (we will choose
\eta=\sigma_{n}^{2}
) and the
W_{2}
-Wasserstein distance beetween probability measures
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
on
\mathbb{R}
is defined by

W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}|x-y|^{2}\pi(dx,dy)\,,

(A.21)

where
\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
is the set of all probability measures on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
with marginals
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
. We will also consider a piece-wise constant version of the estimator
\widetilde{m}_{n}
: let
\widehat{m}_{n}
denote the estimator that coincides with
\widetilde{m}_{n}
at each order statistic
X_{(i)}
, for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and that is constant on the intervals
[0,X_{(1)}]
,
[X_{(n)},1]
and on all intervals
(X_{(i-1)},X_{(i)}]
for
i\in\{2,\dots,n\}
.
Let
m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)
, and
U
be a random variable independent of the
(X_{i},{\epsilon}_{i})
’s, with uniform distribution on the finite set
\{1,\dots,n\}
. Define

T_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m(X_{i})\mathds{1}_{U=i},\ \qquad\ T_{Y}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\mathds{1}_{U=i}\,.

Conditionally on the
X_{i}
’s and
\delta_{i}
’s,
T_{m}
has distribution
\hat{\mu}_{m}
and the distribution of
T_{Y}
is
\hat{\mu}_{Y}
. By definition (
A.21
) of the
W_{2}
-Wasserstein distance, with
\mathbb{E}_{U\mid(X,\delta)}
the conditional expectation given the
X_{i}
’s and
\delta_{i}
’s, and
m_{0}=m
, one concludes that

\displaystyle W^{2}_{2}\left(\hat{\mu}_{Y},\hat{\mu}_{m}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}_{U\mid(X,\delta)}\left|T_{m}-T_{Y}\right|^{2}=\sigma_{n}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{U\mid(X,\delta)}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\mathds{1}_{U=i}\right|^{2}=\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}^{2}.

Let
m_{n}
denote either
\widehat{m}_{n}
or
\widetilde{m}_{n}
.
By
[
3
, Theorem 2.10]
(that extends (
A.6
) for
W_{1}
to
W_{p}
for arbitrary
p\geq 1
),

\displaystyle\int|m_{n}-m|^{2}d\hat{\mu}_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\widetilde{m}_{n}(X_{(i)})-m(X_{(i)})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\hat{\mu}_{\widetilde{m}_{n}},\hat{\mu}_{m})

so it follows from the triangle inequality that

\displaystyle\int|m_{n}-m|^{2}d\hat{\mu}_{X}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\left(W_{2}^{2}(\hat{\mu}_{\widetilde{m}_{n}},\hat{\mu}_{Y})+W_{2}^{2}(\hat{\mu}_{Y},\hat{\mu}_{m})\right).

Since
\mathcal{M}(M,a)\subset\mathcal{M}^{0}(M,a)
, it follows from the previous inequality combined to the definition of
\widetilde{m}_{n}
that for all
m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)
, if
m_{0}=m
then we have

\displaystyle\int|m_{n}-m|^{2}d\hat{\mu}_{X}\leq 4W_{2}^{2}(\hat{\mu}_{Y},\hat{\mu}_{m})+2\eta.

Hence we arrive at

\displaystyle\int|m_{n}-m|^{2}d\hat{\mu}_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\widetilde{m}_{n}(X_{(i)})-m(X_{(i)})\right)^{2}

(A.22)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{4\sigma_{n}^{2}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}^{2}+2\eta\,.

Taking expectation on both sides and combining with Holder inequality proves that

\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}E_{m}\left[\int\left|m_{n}-m\right|\ d\hat{\mu}_{X}\right]\leq K\left(\sigma_{n}+\eta^{1/2}\right).

Choosing
\eta=\sigma_{n}^{2}
yields

\mathcal{R}_{S}(M,a,n,\hat{\mu}_{X})\leq K\sigma_{n}.

(A.23)

This proves the first claim of Theorem
4.1
.
Now, the shuffled regression model is a special case of the unlinked regression model in the sense that the distribution corresponding to
E_{m}
in the shuffled regression model belongs to
\Pi(m,n)
for all
m
, so using the notation of Section
3
, for all estimators
\widehat{m}_{n}
we have

\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}E_{m}\left[\int\left|\widehat{m}_{n}-m\right|\ d\hat{\mu}_{X}\right]\leq\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}\sup_{\pi\in\Pi(m,n)}E_{\pi}\left[\int|\widehat{m}_{n}-m|d\hat{\mu}_{X}\right]

(A.24)

and therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{S}(M,a,n,\hat{\mu}_{X})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(M,a,n,\hat{\mu}_{X})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Lv_{n}

provided that the assumptions of the Theorem
3.3
are satisfied. This completes the proof of Theorem
4.1
for the empirical minimax risk since for the upper bound, we can consider the smallest of the two bounds above.

Next, we turn to the population minimax risk. For this task, we calculate the distance between
\widehat{m}_{n}
and
m
with respect to
\mu_{X}
, where
m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)
. With
X_{(0)}:=0
we have

\displaystyle\int|\widehat{m}_{n}-m|\ d\mu_{X}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{X_{(j-1)}}^{X_{(j)}}|\widehat{m}_{n}(x)-m(x)|\ d\mu_{X}(x)+\int_{X_{(n)}}^{1}|\widehat{m}_{n}(x)-m(x)|\ d\mu_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{X_{(j-1)}}^{X_{(j)}}|\widetilde{m}_{n}(X_{(j)})-m(x)|\ d\mu_{X}(x)+\int_{X_{(n)}}^{1}|\widetilde{m}_{n}(X_{(n)})-m(x)|\ d\mu_{X}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=2}^{n}\max\left\{|\widetilde{m}_{n}(X_{(j)})-m(X_{(j-1)})|,|\widetilde{m}_{n}(X_{(j)})-m(X_{(j)})|\right\}(F_{X}(X_{(j)})-F_{X}(X_{(j-1)}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{0}^{X_{(1)}}|\widetilde{m}_{n}(X_{(1)})-m(x)|\ d\mu_{X}(x)+\int_{X_{(n)}}^{1}|\widetilde{m}_{n}(X_{(n)})-m(x)|\ d\mu_{X}(x)

where the sum in the last term is bounded above by

\displaystyle\sum_{j=2}^{n}|\widetilde{m}_{n}(X_{(j)})-m(X_{(j)})|(F_{X}(X_{(j)})-F_{X}(X_{(j-1)}))

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j=2}^{n}(m(X_{(j)})-m(X_{(j-1)}))(F_{X}(X_{(j)})-F_{X}(X_{(j-1)}))\,.

Hence,

\displaystyle\int|\widehat{m}_{n}-m|\ d\mu_{X}\leq T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}+T_{4}

where

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\widetilde{m}_{n}(X_{(j)})-m(X_{(j)})|(F_{X}(X_{(j)})-F_{X}(X_{(j-1)}))

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{n}(m(X_{(j)})-m(X_{(j-1)}))(F_{X}(X_{(j)})-F_{X}(X_{(j-1)}))

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle=|\widetilde{m}_{n}(X_{(n)})-m(X_{(n)})|(1-F_{X}(X_{(n)}))

\displaystyle T_{4}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{X_{(1)}}|m(X_{(1)})-m(x)|\ d\mu_{X}(x)+\int_{X_{(n)}}^{1}|m(X_{(n)})-m(x)|\ d\mu_{X}(x)\,.

We now analyze each
T_{i}
separately. The rest of the proof relies on some properties of order statistics, which we state below:

Lemma A.5
.

If
X
has a continuous distribution function
F
, then
F(X)\sim{\sf unif}([0,1])
. As a consequence, if
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}F
with the order statistics
X_{(1)},\dots,X_{(n)}
, then
F(X_{(1)}),\dots,F(X_{(n)})
have same distribution as
U_{(1)},\dots,U_{(n)}
, n order statistics from uniform distribution on
[0,1]
. Furthermore, if we define
\delta_{n,i}=U_{(i)}-U_{(i-1)}
for
1\leq i\leq n+1
(with the convention
U_{(0)}=0
and
U_{(n+1)}=1
), then:

\left(\delta_{n,1},\dots,\delta_{n,n+1}\right)\overset{d}{=}\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\alpha_{i}},\dots,\frac{\alpha_{n+1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\alpha_{i}}\right)

where
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n+1}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}{\sf Exp}(1)
.

For
T_{1}
we have

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\widetilde{m}_{n}(X_{(j)})-m(X_{(j)}))^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{n}(F_{X}(X_{(j)})-F_{X}(X_{(j-1)}))^{2}}\,.

Hence, (
A.22
) with
\eta=\sigma^{2}
yields:

\displaystyle E_{m}[T_{1}]

\displaystyle\leq E_{m}\left[\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\widetilde{m}_{n}(X_{(j)})-m(X_{(j)}))^{2}}\sqrt{n\sum_{j=1}^{n}(F_{X}(X_{(j)})-F_{X}(X_{(j-1)}))^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\sigma_{n}E_{m}\left[\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}^{2}}\right]E_{m}\left[\sqrt{n\sum_{j=1}^{n}(F_{X}(X_{(j)})-F_{X}(X_{(j-1)}))^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\sigma_{n}\|\delta\|_{2}

where
C>0
is a universal constant.
Here the last line follows from an application of Jensen’s inequality and using the fact that
F_{X}(X)\sim{\sf unif}([0,1])
, see Lemma
A.5
. The later fact implies that (omitting the subscript
m
since the expectation below does not depend on
m

E[(F_{X}(X_{(j)})-F_{X}(X_{(j-1)}))^{2}]=E[(U_{(j)}-U_{(j-1)})^{2}]\leq Kn^{-2}

for some constant
K
. Hence, fot
T_{1}
we have

E_{m}(T_{1})\leq C\sigma_{n}

for some universal constant
C>0
.

Now for
T_{2}
we use the following:

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left[\sup_{j}\left(F_{X}(X_{(j)})-F_{X}(X_{(j-1)})\right)\right]\left(m\left(X_{(n)}\right)-m\left(X_{(1)}\right)\right)

where by monotonicity of
m
,

\displaystyle m\left(X_{(n)}\right)-m\left(X_{(1)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{1-F_{X}(X_{(n)})}\int_{X_{(n)}}^{1}m\ d\mu_{X}-\frac{1}{F_{X}(X_{(1)})}\int_{0}^{X_{(1)}}m\ d\mu_{X}\right)

\displaystyle\leq M^{\frac{1}{a+2}}\left(\left(1-F_{X}(X_{(n)})\right)^{-\frac{1}{a+2}}+F_{X}(X_{(1)})^{-\frac{1}{a+2}}\right)\,.

Now it follows from Lemma
A.5

that (omitting again the unnecessary subscript
m
)

\displaystyle E^{1/2}\left[\sup_{j}\left(U_{(j)}-U_{(j-1)}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{\log{n}}{n}\right)\,,

\displaystyle E^{1/2}[U_{(1)}^{2}]

\displaystyle=O(n^{-1})\,,

\displaystyle E^{1/2}[(1-U_{(n)})^{2}]

\displaystyle=O(n^{-1})\,

where
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
are i.i.d. variables with a uniform distribution on
[0,1]
and
U_{(1)}\leq\dots\dots\leq U_{(n)}
are the corresponding order statistics.
Hence

\displaystyle E\left[\left[\sup_{j}\left(F_{X}(X_{(j)})-F_{X}(X_{(j-1)})\right)\right]\left(\left(1-F(X_{(n)})\right)^{-\frac{1}{a+2}}+F(X_{(1)})^{-\frac{1}{a+2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=E\left[\left[\sup_{j}\left(U_{(j)}-U_{(j-1)})\right)\right]\left(\left(1-U_{(n)}\right)^{-\frac{1}{a+2}}+U_{(1)}^{-\frac{1}{a+2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq Cn^{-1+\frac{1}{a+2}}\log{n}

where
C>0
is a universal constant.
Here in the last line we used the following fact:

E\left[U_{(1)}^{-\frac{2}{a+2}}\right]\leq Cn^{\frac{2}{a+2}},\ \ \ \ E\left[\left(1-U_{(n)}\right)^{-\frac{2}{a+2}}\right]\leq Cn^{\frac{2}{a+2}}\,.

We prove below the first inequality, as the proof the second inequality is similar. Note that the distribution function and the density function of
U_{(1)}
are respectively given by

F_{U_{(1)}}(x)=1-(1-x)^{n},\ \ \ f_{U_{(1)}}(x)=n(1-x)^{n-1}\,.

Therefore we have:

\displaystyle E[U_{(1)}^{-\frac{2}{a+2}}]

\displaystyle=n\int_{0}^{1}x^{-\frac{2}{a+2}}(1-x)^{n-1}\ dx

\displaystyle=n\int_{0}^{1}x^{\frac{a}{a+2}-1}(1-x)^{n-1}\ dx

\displaystyle=nB\left(\frac{a}{a+2},n\right)

\displaystyle=n\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{a}{a+2}\right)\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{a}{a+2}+n\right)}\,.

It follows from Wendall’s limit theorem (see 1.1.6 of
[
54
]
) for Gamma functions that

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha)}{\Gamma(n)n^{\alpha}}=1\,,\ \ \forall\ \ \alpha>0\,.

Therefore for all large

(with
\alpha=a/(a+2))
we have
\Gamma(n+a/(a+2))\geq(1/2)\Gamma(n)n^{a/(a+2)}
, whence

\displaystyle E[U_{(1)}^{-\frac{2}{a+2}}]\leq 2n\Gamma\left(\frac{a}{a+2}\right)n^{-\frac{a}{a+2}}=2\Gamma\left(\frac{a}{a+2}\right)n^{\frac{2}{a+2}}\,.

We have elaborate on the calculation for
U_{(1)}
and the calculation of
1-U_{(n)}
is similar.
Hence

E_{m}(T_{2})\leq Cn^{-1+\frac{1}{a+2}}\log{n}

where
C>0
depends only on
M
and
a
.

We next bound
T_{3}
. The bound on
T_{3}
follows from the similar line of argument as of

. Note that:

\displaystyle E_{m}[T_{3}]

\displaystyle\leq E_{m}\left[\left(\sum_{j}|\widetilde{m}_{n}(X_{(j)})-m(X_{(j)})|\right)(1-F_{X}(X_{(n)}))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{E_{m}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j}|\widetilde{m}_{n}(X_{(j)})-m(X_{(j)})|\right)^{2}\right]}\sqrt{E\left[\left(n(1-F_{X}(X_{(n)}))\right)^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{E_{m}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j}\left(\widetilde{m}_{n}(X_{(j)})-m(X_{(j)})\right)^{2}\right]}\sqrt{E\left[\left(n(1-F_{X}(X_{(n)}))\right)^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sigma_{n}(\|\delta\|_{2}+1)\sqrt{E\left[\left(n(1-U_{(n)})\right)^{2}\right]}\leq C\sigma_{n}

where
C>0
is a universal constant.

Finally for
T_{4}
, note that:

\displaystyle E_{m}[T_{4}]

\displaystyle=E_{m}\left[\int_{0}^{1}(m(X_{(1)})-m(x))\mathds{1}_{x\leq X_{(1)}}\ d\mu_{X}(x)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+E_{m}\left[\int_{0}^{1}(m(x)-m(X_{(n)}))\mathds{1}_{x\geq X_{(n)}}\ d\mu_{X}(x)\right]

\displaystyle\leq E_{m}\left[m(X_{(1)})F_{X}(X_{(1)})\right]+M^{\frac{1}{a+2}}E\left[F_{X}(X_{(1)})^{1-\frac{1}{a+2}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-E_{m}\left[m(X_{(n)})\left(1-F(X_{(n)})\right)\right]+M^{\frac{1}{a+2}}E\left[\left(1-F_{X}(X_{(n)})\right)^{1-\frac{1}{a+2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq E_{m}\left[m(X_{(1)})F_{X}(X_{(1)})\right]-E_{m}\left[m(X_{(n)})\left(1-F(X_{(n)})\right)\right]+Cn^{-1+\frac{1}{a+2}}

where
C>0
depends only on
M
and
a
.
For the first term, observe that:

\displaystyle E_{m}[m(X_{(1)})F_{X}(X_{(1)})]

\displaystyle\leq E_{m}\left[\frac{F_{X}(X_{(1)})}{F_{X}(X_{(2)})-F_{X}(X_{(1)})}\int_{X_{(1)}}^{X_{(2)}}m(x)\ d\mu_{X}(x)\right]

\displaystyle\leq M^{\frac{1}{a+2}}E\left[F_{X}(X_{(1)})\left(F_{X}(X_{(2)})-F_{X}(X_{(1)})\right)^{-\frac{1}{a+2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq M^{\frac{1}{a+2}}\sqrt{E\left[F_{X}^{2}(X_{(1)})\right]}\sqrt{E\left[\left(F_{X}(X_{(2)})-F_{X}(X_{(1)})\right)^{-\frac{2}{a+2}}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq Cn^{-1+\frac{1}{a+2}}

where
C>0
depends only on
M
and
a
.
. The calculation for the second term is similar and skipped for the brevity. Combining, we get

E_{m}(T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}+T_{4})\leq C\left(\sigma_{n}+n^{-1+\frac{1}{a+2}}\log{n}\right)

where

depends only on

and

. Hence,

\displaystyle\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}E_{m}\int|\widehat{m}_{n}-m|\ d\mu_{X}\leq C\left(\sigma_{n}+n^{-1+\frac{1}{a+2}}\log{n}\right)

and the second claim in Theorem

follows. To prove the last claim for the population risk, note that if

then the above upper bound simplifies to

\displaystyle\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}E_{m}\int|\widehat{m}_{n}-m|\ d\mu_{X}\leq 2C\sigma_{n}

since

Then, we again use (

A.10
Proof of Theorem
4.2

To prove the lower bound, we use Le-Cam’s two point method. Towards that end, set
X\sim U([0,1])
, define
m_{0}\equiv 0
and
\tilde{m}
as a function which is
0
on
(Cn^{-1},1]
(for some constant
C
to be chosen later) and
\left(x\log^{1+{\epsilon}}{(1/x)}\right)^{-\frac{1}{a+2}}
on
[0,Cn^{-1}]
. Note that the function
\left(x\log^{1+{\epsilon}}{(1/x)}\right)^{-\frac{1}{a+2}}
is decreasing in a neighborhood of
0
and consequently
\tilde{m}
is decreasing function. Furthermore we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{m}(X)|^{a+2}]\leq\int_{0}^{C/n}\left(x\log^{1+{\epsilon}}{(1/x)}\right)^{-1}\ dx\leq M\,.

Hence both
m_{0},\tilde{m}\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)
. Moreover we have the following lower bound on the
L_{1}
distance between
\tilde{m}
and
m_{0}
:

\displaystyle d(\tilde{m},m_{0})=\int\left|\tilde{m}-m_{0}\right|\ d\mu_{X}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{Cn^{-1}}\left(x\log^{1+{\epsilon}}{(1/x)}\right)^{-\frac{1}{a+2}}\ dx

\displaystyle\geq(n/C)^{\frac{1}{a+2}}(\log{(n/C)})^{-\frac{1+{\epsilon}}{a+2}}\int_{0}^{Cn^{-1}}\ dx

\displaystyle\geq(n/C)^{-1+\frac{1}{a+2}}(\log{(n/C)})^{-\frac{1+{\epsilon}}{a+2}}\triangleq\tau_{n}\,.

Recall that, for any two measure
P
and
Q
the TV (total variation) distance between them is defined as:

\|P-Q\|_{\sf TV}=\sup_{A}|P(A)-Q(A)|

for all measurable set
A
. Let
P_{m_{0}}
(resp.
P_{\tilde{m}}
) denote the distribution of
(X,m_{0}(X))
(resp.
(X,\tilde{m}(X))
). Using Le-Cam’s method we have:

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{m}}\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}E_{m}\left[d(\hat{m},m)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\tau_{n}}{2}\inf_{\hat{m}}\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}P^{\otimes n}_{m}(d(\hat{m},m)\geq\tau_{n}/2)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\tau_{n}}{2}\inf_{\hat{m}}\max_{m\in\{m_{0},\tilde{m}\}}P^{\otimes n}_{m}(d(\hat{m},m)\geq\tau_{n}/2)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\tau_{n}}{4}\inf_{\hat{m}}\left\{P^{\otimes n}_{m_{0}}(d(\hat{m},m_{0})\geq\tau_{n}/2)+P^{\otimes n}_{\tilde{m}}(d(\hat{m},\tilde{m})\geq\tau_{n}/2)\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\tau_{n}}{4}\inf_{\hat{m}}\left\{P^{\otimes n}_{m_{0}}(d(\hat{m},m_{0})\geq\tau_{n}/2)+P^{\otimes n}_{\tilde{m}}(d(\hat{m},m_{0})<\tau_{n}/2)\right\}\,,

using that
d(m_{0},\tilde{m})\geq\tau_{n}
for the last inequality. Hence,

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{m}}\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}E_{m}\left[d(\hat{m},m)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\tau_{n}}{4}\inf_{\hat{m}}\left\{1-\left(P^{\otimes n}_{\tilde{m}}(d(\hat{m},m_{0})\geq\tau_{n}/2)-P^{\otimes n}_{m_{0}}(d(\hat{m},m_{0})\geq\tau_{n}/2)\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\tau_{n}}{4}\left\{1-\left\|P^{\otimes n}_{m_{0}}-P^{\otimes n}_{\tilde{m}}\right\|_{\sf TV}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau_{n}}{4}\left\{1-n\left\|P_{m_{0}}-P_{\tilde{m}}\right\|_{\sf TV}\right\}\,.

Now we bound
\left\|P_{m_{0}}-P_{\tilde{m}}\right\|_{\sf TV}
for which we use the following fact:

Lemma A.6
.

For any two measure
(\mu,\nu)
we have

\|\mu-\nu\|_{\sf TV}\leq\mathbb{P}(X\neq Y)
for any
(X,Y)
with
X\sim\mu
and
Y\sim\nu
.

We now generate a coupling as follows: i) generate
X\sim U([0,1])
; ii) set
Z_{1}=(X,m_{0}(X))\equiv(X,0)
and iii) set
Z_{2}=(X,\tilde{m}(X))
. Then it is immediate
Z_{1}\sim P_{m_{0}}
and
Z_{2}\sim P_{\tilde{m}}
. Consequently:

\displaystyle\|P_{m_{0}}-P_{\tilde{m}}\|_{\sf TV}\leq\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}\neq Z_{2})=\mathbb{P}(X\leq Cn^{-1})=Cn^{-1}\,.

Therefore, choosing
C=1/2
, we have:

\inf_{\hat{m}}\sup_{m\in\mathcal{M}(M,a)}E_{m}\left[\int\left|\hat{m}-m\right|\ d\mu_{X}\right]\geq\frac{\tau_{n}}{8}=\frac{1}{8}(2n)^{-1+\frac{1}{a+2}}(\log{(2n)})^{-\frac{1+{\epsilon}}{a+2}}\,.

This completes the proof of Theorem
4.2
.
\Box

B.10
) yields

\displaystyle\sup_{m\in{\mathcal{M}}(M,a)}E_{m}\left[\int|\widehat{m}-m|d\mu_{X}\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int E_{\theta}W_{1}(\mu_{\widehat{m}^{NY}},\mu_{\theta})dP_{\tilde{\theta}_{n}}(\theta)-Cn^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\mu}}\int E_{\theta}W_{1}(\hat{\mu},\mu_{\theta})dP_{\tilde{\theta}_{n}}(\theta)-Cn^{-1/2}

where the infimum is extended to all psudo-estimators.
Taking the infimum on the left hand side of the inequality completes the proof of Lemma
A.7
.
\Box

B.7
Proof of Lemma
A.8

Recall the definition of
h_{0}
:

\displaystyle h_{0,n_{j}}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n_{j}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C_{r}}{(1+z^{2})^{r}}\times f_{\epsilon}(y_{1}-z)\times\dots\times f_{\epsilon}(y_{n_{j}}-z)dz

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C_{r}}{(1+z^{2})^{r}}\times(2\pi\sigma_{n}^{2})^{-n_{j}/2}{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}\sum_{j}y_{j}^{2}-\frac{n_{j}}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}z^{2}+\frac{z}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{j}}y_{i}\right)dz

\displaystyle=(2\pi\sigma_{n}^{2})^{-n_{j}/2}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}\sum_{j}(y_{j}-\bar{y})^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C_{r}}{(1+z^{2})^{r}}\times{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{n_{j}}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}(z-\bar{y})^{2}\right)dz

\displaystyle=(2\pi\sigma_{n}^{2})^{-n_{j}/2}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}\sum_{j}(y_{j}-\bar{y})^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C_{r}}{(1+(z+\bar{y})^{2})^{r}}\times{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{n_{j}}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}z^{2}\right)dz.

For the numeration of the chi-square divergence note that:

\displaystyle h_{\theta,n_{j},s,1}(\mathbf{y})-h_{\theta,n_{j},s,0}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\int H(b_{n}(z-t_{s,n}))f_{\epsilon}(y_{1}-z)\dots f_{\epsilon}(y_{n_{j}}-z)\ dz

\displaystyle=(2\pi\sigma_{n}^{2})^{-\frac{n_{j}}{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}\sum_{j}(y_{j}-\bar{y})^{2}}\int H(b_{n}(z-t_{s,n}))e^{-\frac{n_{j}}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}(z-\bar{y})^{2}}\ dz.

Therefore, we have:

\displaystyle\int\frac{\left(h_{\theta,n_{j},s,1}(\mathbf{y})-h_{\theta,n_{j},s,0}(\mathbf{y})\right)^{2}}{h_{0,n_{j}}(\mathbf{y})}\ d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\int(2\pi\sigma_{n}^{2})^{-n_{j}/2}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}\sum_{j}(y_{j}-\bar{y})^{2}}\frac{\left(\int H(b_{n}(z-t_{s,n}))e^{-\frac{n_{j}}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}(z-\bar{y})^{2}}\ dz\right)^{2}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C_{r}}{(1+(z+\bar{y})^{2})^{r}}\times{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{n_{j}}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}z^{2}\right)\ dz}\ d\mathbf{y}

Now we do the change of variable
\mathbf{s}=A\mathbf{y}
where
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{j}\times n_{j}}
is an orthonormal matrix. The first row of A is
(n_{j}^{-1/2},\dots,n_{j}^{-1/2})
and the rest of the rows are obtained by applying Gram-Schmidt algorithm. Therefore, we have
s_{1}=\sqrt{n_{j}}\bar{y}
. Furthermore,
\|\mathbf{s}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
and consequently,
\sum_{j}(y_{j}-\bar{y})^{2}=\sum_{j}y_{j}^{2}-n_{j}\bar{y}^{2}=\|\mathbf{s}\|^{2}-s_{1}^{2}=\sum_{j=2}^{n_{j}}s_{j}^{2}
. Using this change of variable we have:

\displaystyle\int\frac{\left(h_{\theta,n_{j},s,1}(\mathbf{y})-h_{\theta,n_{j},s,0}(\mathbf{y})\right)^{2}}{h_{0,n_{j}}(\mathbf{y})}\ d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\int(2\pi\sigma_{n}^{2})^{-n_{j}/2}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}\sum_{j=2}^{n_{j}}s_{j}^{2}}\frac{\left(\int H(b_{n}(z-t_{s,n}))e^{-\frac{n_{j}}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}(z-s_{1}/\sqrt{n_{j}})^{2}}\ dz\right)^{2}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C_{r}}{(1+(z+s_{1}/\sqrt{n_{j}})^{2})^{r}}\times{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{n_{j}}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}z^{2}\right)\ dz}\ d\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{n}^{2}}}\int\frac{\left(\int H(b_{n}(z-t_{s,n}))e^{-\frac{n_{j}}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}(z-s_{1}/\sqrt{n_{j}})^{2}}\ dz\right)^{2}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C_{r}}{(1+(z+s_{1}/\sqrt{n_{j}})^{2})^{r}}\times{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{n_{j}}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}z^{2}\right)\ dz}\ ds_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{n_{j}}{2\pi\sigma_{n}^{2}}}\int\frac{\left(\int H(b_{n}(z-t_{s,n}))e^{-\frac{n_{j}}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}(z-s_{1})^{2}}\ dz\right)^{2}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C_{r}}{(1+(z+s_{1})^{2})^{r}}\times{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{n_{j}}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}z^{2}\right)\ dz}\ ds_{1}.

For notational simplicity let
\phi_{n}
be the density of
\mathcal{N}(0,n_{j}^{-1}\sigma_{n}^{2})
, that is

\phi_{n}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{n_{j}}{2\pi\sigma_{n}^{2}}}e^{-\frac{n_{j}}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}t^{2}}.

We then have

\displaystyle\int\frac{\left(h_{\theta,n_{j},s,1}(\mathbf{y})-h_{\theta,n_{j},s,0}(\mathbf{y})\right)^{2}}{h_{0,n_{j}}(\mathbf{y})}\ d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\int\frac{\left(\int H(b_{n}(z-t_{s,n}))\phi_{n}(z-s_{1})\ dz\right)^{2}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C_{r}}{(1+(z+s_{1})^{2})^{r}}\phi_{n}(z)\ dz}\ ds_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\int\frac{\left(\int H(b_{n}(z+s_{1}-t_{s,n}))\phi_{n}(z)\ dz\right)^{2}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C_{r}}{(1+(z+s_{1})^{2})^{r}}\phi_{n}(z)\ dz}\ ds_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\int H(b_{n}(z+s_{1}-t_{s,n}))\phi_{n}(z)\ dz\right)^{2}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C_{r}}{(1+(z+s_{1})^{2})^{r}}\phi_{n}(z)\ dz}\ ds_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{\left(\int H(b_{n}(z+s_{1}-t_{s,n}))\phi_{n}(z)\ dz\right)^{2}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C_{r}}{(1+(z+s_{1})^{2})^{r}}\phi_{n}(z)\ dz}\ ds_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\triangleq T_{1}+T_{2}\,.

Here
T_{1}
denotes the integral over the positive real line and
T_{2}
denotes the integral over the negative real line. We start with
T_{1}
and the calculation for
T_{2}
is similar. Before delving deep into the analysis of
T_{1}
, we give a generic lower bound on the denominator. Considering the two cases on whether
0\leq s\leq 1
or
s>1
we obtain:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C_{r}}{(1+(z+s_{1})^{2})^{r}}\phi_{n}(z)\ dz

\displaystyle\geq\int_{|z+s_{1}|\leq s_{1}\vee 1}\frac{C_{r}}{(1+(z+s_{1})^{2})^{r}}\phi_{n}(z)\ dz

\displaystyle\geq(s_{1}\vee 1)^{-2r}\frac{C_{r}}{2^{r}}\int_{-s_{1}\vee 1-s_{1}}^{s_{1}\vee 1-s_{1}}\phi_{n}(z)\ dz

\displaystyle\geq(s_{1}\vee 1)^{-2r}\frac{C_{r}}{2^{r}}\int_{-1}^{0}\phi_{n}(z)\ dz

\displaystyle=(s_{1}\vee 1)^{-2r}\frac{C_{r}}{2^{r}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}-\Phi(-\sigma_{n}^{-1}\sqrt{n_{j}})\right\}

\displaystyle\geq(s_{1}\vee 1)^{-2r}\frac{C_{r}}{2^{r}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}-\Phi(-1)\right\}

(B.11)

where
\Phi
denotes the distribution function of the standard Gaussian distribution. For the last inequality we use that
\sigma_{n}^{-1}\sqrt{n_{j}}\geq 1
.

We now establish a bound on
T_{1}
. Let
\{M_{n}\}
be sequence
\geq 1
and diverges to
\infty
as
n\uparrow\infty
. We further divide it into two parts:

T_{1}=\int_{0}^{M_{n}}+\int_{M_{n}}^{\infty}\triangleq T_{11}+T_{12}\,.

For
T_{11}
observe that:

\displaystyle T_{11}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{M_{n}}\frac{\left(\int H(b_{n}(z+s_{1}-t_{s,n}))\phi_{n}(z)\ dz\right)^{2}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C_{r}}{(1+(z+s_{1})^{2})^{r}}\phi_{n}(z)\ dz}\ ds_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int H(b_{n}(z+s_{1}-t_{s,n}))\phi_{n}(z)\ dz\right)^{2}\ ds_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad+C\int_{1}^{M_{n}}s_{1}^{2r}\left(\int H(b_{n}(z+s_{1}-t_{s,n}))\phi_{n}(z)\ dz\right)^{2}\ ds_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+M_{n}^{2r})\int\left(\int H(b_{n}(z+s_{1}-t_{s,n}))\phi_{n}(z)\ dz\right)^{2}\ ds_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2CM_{n}^{2r}\int\left(H_{n}\star\phi_{n}(s_{1})\right)^{2}\ ds_{1}

where
H_{n}(z)=H(b_{n}(z-t_{s,n}))
and
\star
stands for the convolution product. With
H_{n}^{*}
and
\phi_{n}^{*}
the fourier transforms of
H_{n}
and
\phi_{n}
respectively, the fourier transform of
H_{n}\star\phi_{n}
is the product
H_{n}^{*}\times\phi_{n}^{*}
so it follows from the Parceval theorem that

\displaystyle T_{11}

\displaystyle\leq 2CM_{n}^{2r}\int|H_{n}^{*}(t)|^{2}|\phi_{n}^{*}(t)|^{2}\ dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2CM_{n}^{2r}}{b^{2}_{n}}\int\left|H^{*}\left(\frac{t}{b_{n}}\right)\right|^{2}e^{-\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}t^{2}}{n_{j}}}\ dt

\displaystyle=\frac{2CM_{n}^{2r}}{b_{n}}\int\left|H^{*}(t)\right|^{2}e^{-\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}t^{2}b_{n}^{2}}{n_{j}}}\ dt

\displaystyle=\frac{2CM_{n}^{2r}}{b_{n}}\int_{1}^{2}\left|H^{*}(t)\right|^{2}e^{-\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}t^{2}b_{n}^{2}}{n_{j}}}\ dt

since
H
satisfies the conditions in Section
A.3
and in particular,
H^{*}(t)
is zero outside
[1,2]
. Hence,

\displaystyle T_{11}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{M_{n}^{2r}}{b_{n}}e^{-\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}b_{n}^{2}}{n_{j}}}\,.

Now for
T_{12}
we have

\displaystyle T_{12}

\displaystyle=\int_{M_{n}}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\int H(b_{n}(z+s_{1}-t_{s,n}))\phi_{n}(z)\ dz\right)^{2}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{C_{r}}{(1+(z+s_{1})^{2})^{r}}\phi_{n}(z)\ dz}\ ds_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{M_{n}}^{\infty}s_{1}^{2r}\left(\int H(b_{n}(z+s_{1}-t_{s,n}))\phi_{n}(z)\ dz\right)^{2}\ ds_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{M_{n}}^{\infty}s_{1}^{2r}\left(\int_{b_{n}|s_{1}+z-t_{s,n}|\geq s_{1}^{\alpha_{0}}}|H(b_{n}(s_{1}+z-t_{s,n}))|\phi_{n}(z)\ dz\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\int_{b_{n}|s_{1}+z-t_{s,n}|\leq s_{1}^{\alpha_{0}}}|H(b_{n}(s_{1}+z-t_{s,n}))|\phi_{n}(z)\ dz\right)^{2}\ ds_{1}

where
\alpha_{0}\in(1/2,1)
will be chosen later. Recall that by assumption,
|H(t)|\leq c(1+t^{2})^{-r}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence, for all
t>1
we have
|H(t)|\leq c2^{-r}t^{-2r}
so defining
H_{+}
to be the supremum of
|H(t)|
we get

\displaystyle T_{12}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{M_{n}}^{\infty}s_{1}^{2r}\left(c2^{-r}\int_{b_{n}|s_{1}+z-t_{s,n}|\geq s_{1}^{\alpha_{0}}}b_{n}^{-2r}|s_{1}+z-t_{s,n}|^{-2r}\phi_{n}(z)\ dz\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+H_{+}\int_{b_{n}|s_{1}+z-t_{s,n}|\leq s_{1}^{\alpha_{0}}}\phi_{n}(z)\ dz\right)^{2}\ ds_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{M_{n}}^{\infty}s_{1}^{2r}\left(c2^{-2r}s_{1}^{-2r\alpha_{0}}+H_{+}\int_{b_{n}|s_{1}+z-t_{s,n}|\leq s_{1}^{\alpha_{0}}}\phi_{n}(z)\ dz\right)^{2}\ ds_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2Cc^{2}2^{-4r}\int_{M_{n}}^{\infty}s_{1}^{-2r(2\alpha_{0}-1)}\ ds_{1}+2CH_{+}^{2}\int_{M_{n}}^{\infty}s_{1}^{2r}\left(\mathbb{P}_{s_{1}}\right)^{2}\ ds_{1}

where

\mathbb{P}_{s_{1}}=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\sigma_{n}Z}{\sqrt{n_{j}}}+s_{1}-t_{s,.n}\right|\leq\frac{s_{1}^{\alpha_{0}}}{b_{n}}\right).

Here,
Z
denotes a standard Gaussian random variable.
Now, we bound
\mathbb{P}_{s_{1}}
. Before going into the details, note that as long as
b_{n}>1
(which will be immediate from our choice later),
\alpha_{0}<1
and
s_{1}\geq M_{n}\geq 4
(for all large
n
as
M_{n}
diverges to infinity), we have
s_{1}>(4s_{1}^{\alpha_{0}})/b_{n}
and by definition
t_{s,n}\leq 1\leq s_{1}/4
. This implies:

s_{1}-\frac{s_{1}^{\alpha_{0}}}{b_{n}}-t_{s,n}\geq\frac{s_{1}}{2}\,.

Since
Z
has the same distribution as
-Z
this implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{s_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(-\frac{\sigma_{n}Z}{\sqrt{n_{j}}}+s_{1}-t_{s,.n}\leq\frac{s_{1}^{\alpha_{0}}}{b_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}Z}{\sqrt{n_{j}}}\geq\frac{s_{1}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(Z\geq\frac{s_{1}}{2}\right)

since
\sqrt{n_{j}}/\sigma_{n}\geq 1
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{s_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{8}}\leq C_{1}s_{1}^{-2r\alpha_{0}}.

Here the last line follows from the fact that exponetial decay is faster than polynomial decay and
C_{1}
is some constant. Therefore,

\displaystyle T_{12}

\displaystyle\leq 2C(c^{2}2^{-4r}+C_{1}^{2}H_{+}^{2})\int_{M_{n}}^{\infty}s_{1}^{-2r(2\alpha_{0}-1)}\ ds_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}M_{n}^{1-2r(2\alpha_{0}-1)}

provided that
1<2r(2\alpha_{0}-1)
. Since by assumption,
r>3/2
, a sufficient condition is that
2\alpha_{0}-1>1/3
, that is
\alpha_{0}>2/3
.
The calculation for
T_{2}
(i.e. when
s_{1}<0
) is similar, hence skipped for the brevity. Finally combining the bounds obtained above, we obtain:

\int\frac{\left(h_{\theta,n_{j},s,1}(\mathbf{y})-h_{\theta,n_{j},s,0}(\mathbf{y})\right)^{2}}{h_{0,n_{j}}(\mathbf{y})}\ d\mathbf{y}\leq C_{3}\left(\frac{M_{n}^{2r}}{b_{n}}e^{-\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}b_{n}^{2}}{n_{j}}}+M_{n}^{1-2r(2\alpha_{0}-1)}\right)\,.

If we choose
\alpha_{0}\in(2/3,1)
and
M_{n}=e^{c\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}b_{n}^{2}}{n_{j}}}
for some constant
c
such that
2cr<1
then we get that:

\int\frac{\left(h_{\theta,n_{j},s,1}(\mathbf{y})-h_{\theta,n_{j},s,0}(\mathbf{y})\right)^{2}}{h_{0,n_{j}}(\mathbf{y})}\ d\mathbf{y}\leq Ce^{-c_{1}\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}b_{n}^{2}}{n_{j}}}\,.

for some constants
C,c
. This completes the proof of Lemma
B.7
.
\Box

B.8
Proof of Lemma
A.9

The proof is based on Chernoff’s bound on Binomial random variable (e.g., see Lemma 1.3 of
[
48
]
), which states that if
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. Bernoulli random variable with
P(X=1)=p=1-P(X=0)
, then for any
t\geq np
, we have:

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\geq t\right)\leq\left(\frac{np}{t}\right)^{t}e^{t-np}\,.

We will now use the fact that
(n_{1},\dots,n_{n})\sim\mathrm{mult}(n:n^{-1},\dots,n^{-1})
. Hence, each coordinate
n_{j}
is marginally distributed as Binomial random variable with parameter
n
and probability
n^{-1}
. Therefore we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{j}n_{j}>t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(n_{j}>t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)^{t}e^{t-1}=\frac{1}{e}e^{\log{n}+t-t\log{t}}\hskip 7.22743pt

Now using
t=K\frac{\log{n}}{\log{\log{n}}}
we have:

\displaystyle\log{n}+t-t\log{t}

\displaystyle=\log{n}+K\frac{\log{n}}{\log{\log{n}}}-K\frac{\log{n}}{\log{\log{n}}}\log{\left(K\frac{\log{n}}{\log{\log{n}}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\log{n}+K\frac{\log{n}}{\log{\log{n}}}-K\log{n}-K\frac{\log{n}}{\log{\log{n}}}\log{\left(\frac{K}{{\log{\log{n}}}}\right)}

\displaystyle=-\log{n}\left[K-1+\frac{K}{{\log{\log{n}}}}\left(1-\log{K}+\log{\log{\log{n}}}\right)\right]

Therefore, it is immediate that taking
K=2
suffices as for large
n
, the term in square bracket of the above equation is larger than
1/2
.
This completes the proof of Lemma
A.9
.
\Box
