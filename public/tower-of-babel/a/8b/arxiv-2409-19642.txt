Solving Fredholm Integral Equations of the Second Kind via Wasserstein Gradient Flows
Francesca R. Crucinio, Adam M. Johansen

Abstract
Motivated by a recent method for approximate solution of Fredholm equations of the first kind, we develop a corresponding method for a class of Fredholm equations of the second kind . In particular, we consider the class of equations for which the solution is a probability measure. The approach centres around specifying a functional whose gradient flow admits a minimizer corresponding to a regularized version of the solution of the underlying equation and using a mean-field particle system to approximately simulate that flow. Theoretical support for the method is presented, along with some illustrative numerical results.



1
Introduction

Fredholm integral equations of the second kind are defined by

\pi(x)=\varphi(x)+\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{k}(x,y)\pi(y)\mathrm{d}y

(1)

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, with
\pi
an unknown function on

\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\varphi
an observed forcing on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

\mathrm{k}:\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
a positive integral kernel—where
\pi
and
\mathrm{k}
belong to suitably regular classes.
We focus on the case in which
\pi
is a positive function with known finite integral, i.e.
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\pi(x)\mathrm{d}x<\infty
, so that we can renormalize the left hand side of (
1
) and consider the normalized version of
\pi
and define the new forcing as
\varphi(x)/\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\pi(x)\mathrm{d}x
(which we denote by
\pi,\varphi
too with a slight abuse of notation).
Such equations find applications throughout statistics and data science in contexts as diverse as econometrics
[
22
,
12
]
, modelling light transport
[
56
,
55
]
and reinforcement learning
[
18
]
. In the homogeneous case (i.e. when
\varphi\equiv 0
), solving (
1
) corresponds to finding the eigenfunction
\pi
of
\mathrm{k}
for a given eigenvalue
\lambda
; the eigen–decomposition of positive kernels
\mathrm{k}
is normally referred to as Karhunen–Loève decomposition (see
[
20
]
for a recent review) and has applications to Gaussian process regression
[
61
]
, spatial statistics
[
15
,
52
]
and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) in infinite dimensional spaces
[
1
]
. When
\mathrm{k}
is the density of a Markov kernel (i.e.
\int\mathrm{k}(x,y)\mathrm{d}y=1
) and
\lambda=1
, solving (
1
) is equivalent to finding an invariant measure of
\mathrm{k}
. This is useful when dealing with approximate MCMC kernels
[
2
,
47
]
or tractable approximations to transition densities
[
7
]
.

Henceforth we will use the same symbol to denote measures and their Lebesgue densities whenever they possess them—particularly
\pi
and
\mathrm{k}
such that, e.g.,
\mathrm{d}\pi(y)=\pi(y)\mathrm{d}y
. To solve (
1
) we consider a minimization problem with target functional

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\pi)=\textup{KL}\left(\pi\middle|\varphi+\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{k}(\cdot,y)\pi(y)\mathrm{d}y\right)+\alpha\textup{KL}\left(\pi\middle|\pi_{0}\right),

(2)

where
\textup{KL}\left(\mu\middle|\nu\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log((\mathrm{d}\mu/\mathrm{d}\nu)(y))\mathrm{d}\mu(y)
denotes the Kullback–Leibler divergence between
\mu
and
\nu
, for a given regularization
parameter
\alpha>0
and reference measure
\pi_{0}
. Henceforth, we implicitly work under the assumption that the
\pi
of (
1
) is the Lebesgue density of a probability distribution over a Euclidean space.
Standard approaches normally rely upon discretization of
\pi
and
\mathrm{k}
through basis functions or using numerical integration (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
for a survey of classical methods). These methods are particularly effective in low-dimensional settings, but they become challenging when
d
is not small and in the case in which the solution
\pi
is defined over an unbounded set.
In this paper we focus on the latter issue, and develop a method to solve Fredholm integral equations of the second kind on unbounded domains.

Approaches based upon loss minimization have mostly focused on maximum entropy solutions
[
46
,
34
,
37
]
and
\mathbb{L}^{2}
loss
[
32
]
. However, the use of the Kullback–Leibler divergence is common in the literature on Fredholm integral equations of the first kind
[
31
,
51
,
13
,
35
]
and in that context a functional closely related to (
2
) has been recently shown to have properties similar to those of Tikhonov regularization
[
16
]
.

We extend the approach of
[
16
]
to equations of the second kind, obtaining a particle approximation of a nonlinear stochastic differential equation (SDE) of McKean–Vlasov type (an MKVSDE) associated with the Wasserstein gradient flow
minimizing (
2
). We also study the convergence of the minimizers of (
2
) when the regularization parameter
\alpha\to 0
.
This approach is particularly well-suited to integral equations on unbounded domains since is not based on a fixed discretization, but on an adaptive stochastic discretization with diffusive behaviour.

The main difficulty which occurs when adapting the approach of
[
16
]
to the context of Fredholm equations of the second kind is a consequence of the presence of the unknown distribution,
\pi
, on both sides of the first KL divergence in (
2
): this results in an MKVSDE whose drift depends upon nested expectations w.r.t.
\pi
. As a consequence, the algorithm developed herein has higher cost w.r.t. the number of particles in our particle system than that of
[
16
]
and the fundamental difference in the drift of the MKVSDE means that novel results are required to justify our approach. We derive explicit bounds on the error of the proposed numerical algorithm which can be used to set the number of particles in the system and the
time discretization step.

Finally, we demonstrate empirically that our method provides good solutions to integral equations of the form (
1
) on unbounded domains and can be applied more generally than other methods (e.g. those based on the von Neumann decomposition; see our first numerical experiment). We stress that the presence of the regularization term
\textup{KL}\left(\pi\middle|\pi_{0}\right)
in (
2
) is crucial for obtaining significant improvements in the case in which the integral operator induced by the kernel
\mathrm{k}
is not invertible or poorly conditioned—as one might expect, regularization is critical in order to obtain good solutions to this ill-posed inverse problem.

The remainder of the manuscript is organized as follows. In Section
2
we
study the minimization problem in (
2
) and give conditions under which it admits a unique minimizer. We derive the Wasserstein gradient flow and the corresponding McKean–Vlasov SDE in Section
3
. Section
4
introduces an interacting particle system approximating the MKVSDE and a numerical scheme which approximates the continuous time dynamics. We also provide explicit error bounds on the approximation.
Finally, in Section
5
, we test our method on several examples and compare with other methods commonly used in the literature.

2
The Minimization Problem

We first introduce the minimization problem for (
2
) and give conditions under which it is well-posed, before studying the convergence to the unregularized problem and highlighting connections with maximum entropy methods, a popular technique to solve low-dimensional Fredholm integral equations.

2.1
Stability of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}

We study the properties of the functional

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\pi)=\textup{KL}\left(\pi\middle|\varphi+\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{k}(\cdot,y)\pi(y)\mathrm{d}y\right)+\alpha\textup{KL}\left(\pi\middle|\pi_{0}\right),

for

\alpha\geq 0
, and establish that it admits a unique minimizer.

In addition, we consider the following assumption on the kernel

\mathrm{k}
which guarantees finiteness of the corresponding term in
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
.

Assumption 1
.

The density of the kernel
\mathrm{k}
and the forcing
\varphi
are such that
\mathrm{k}\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},[0,+\infty))
,
\varphi\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},[0,+\infty))
and there exists
\mathtt{M}\geq 0
such that for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have
\mathrm{k}(x,y)+\left\|\nabla\mathrm{k}(x,y)\right\|+\left\|\nabla^{2}\mathrm{k}(x,y)\right\|+\left\|(\partial^{3}_{ij\ell}\mathrm{k}(x,y))_{i,j,\ell}\right\|\leq\mathtt{M}
, where
\partial^{3}_{ij\ell}
denotes the third derivative of
\mathrm{k}
w.r.t. component
i,j,\ell\in\{1,2\}
, and
\varphi(x)+\left\|\nabla\varphi(x)\right\|+\left\|\nabla^{2}\varphi(x)\right\|\leq\mathtt{M}
.

Assumption
1
restricts the class of kernels
\mathrm{k}
to smooth kernels with bounded derivatives up to the third order.
This might seem restrictive, however, smooth kernels are particularly challenging while discountinuous or degenerate kernels normally allow for specific solution methodologies (see, e.g.,
[
41
, Ch. 11]
).
In this section, we will require only that
\mathrm{k}\in\mathrm{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},[0,+\infty))
. The control of higher order derivatives is used to establish convergence result for the interacting particle system (in both continuous and discrete time) .

To address stability issues we also
consider the following regularized functional:

\forall\alpha,\eta\geq 0,\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\qquad\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\eta}(\pi)=\mathcal{F}^{\eta}(\pi)+\alpha\textup{KL}\left(\pi\middle|\pi_{0}\right),

(4)

where
\eta\geq 0
is a hyperparameter and
\mathcal{F}^{\eta}(\pi):=\textup{KL}\left(\pi\middle|\varphi+\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{k}(\cdot,y)\pi(y)\mathrm{d}y+\eta\right)
.
In the case
\eta=0
,
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\eta}(\pi)
coincides with
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\pi)
.
However, establishing the uniqueness of the minimizer of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
is difficult. In what follows we study the function
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
for

\alpha,\eta>0
and show that
\mathcal{F}^{\eta}_{\alpha}
, restricted to
\mathcal{P}^{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, is coercive in this case.
Hence,
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
admits a unique minimizer
\pi_{\alpha,\eta}^{\star}
.

Proposition 1
.

Under Assumption
1
, we have the following

(a)

For any
\eta\geq 0
,
\mathcal{F}^{\eta}:\mathcal{P}^{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
is lower bounded and convex.

(b)

For any
\alpha,\eta>0
,
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\eta}:\mathcal{P}^{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
is proper, strictly convex, coercive and lower semi-continuous. In particular,
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
admits a unique minimizer
\pi_{\alpha,\eta}^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}^{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

Proof.

See Appendix
A

∎

The proof of Proposition

to show that

Under the assumption that

2.3
Maximum Entropy Methods

Minimizing the functional
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
is related to finding the maximum entropy solution to (
1
)
[
36
]
. In fact,
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
can be seen as the Lagrangian associated with the following primal problem

\arg\min\left\{\textup{KL}\left(\pi\middle|\pi_{0}\right):\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ \textup{KL}\left(\pi\middle|\varphi+\lambda\int\mathrm{k}(\cdot,y)\pi(y)\mathrm{d}y\right)=0\right\}.

(5)

To highlight the connection with maximum entropy methods, we observe that if we replace the
KL
penalty with an entropic penalty, we obtain a functional

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(\pi)=\textup{KL}\left(\pi\middle|\varphi+\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{k}(\cdot,y)\pi(y)\mathrm{d}y\right)-\alpha\mathrm{H}(\pi),

(6)

corresponding to the Lagrangian associated with the following primal problem

\arg\max\left\{\mathrm{H}(\pi):\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ \textup{KL}\left(\pi\middle|\varphi+\lambda\int\mathrm{k}(\cdot,y)\pi(y)\mathrm{d}y\right)=0\right\},

(7)

where
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the set of probability distributions with
finite entropy.
However,
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}
is not lower bounded and the corresponding minimization problem is not well-defined and therefore is not considered here.

Maximum entropy solutions to Fredholm integral equations were originally proposed in
[
46
]
under moment constraints obtained by selecting different families of basis functions
[
37
,
34
]
.
In the one dimensional case, such a solution can be written analytically (see, e.g.,
[
34
, Prop. 3.1]
), and these approaches are particularly effective.
However, in higher dimensions maximum entropy methods generally require discretization of the support of the solution
\pi
.

3
A Wasserstein Gradient Flow for
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
and an associated McKean–Vlasov SDE

Having established that the regularized functional (
4
) admits a unique minimizer, we exploit the connection between minimization of functionals in the space of probability
measures and partial differential equations (PDEs) identified in
[
38
,
49
]
to obtain an SDE whose invariant measure can be related to the minimizer of

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
.

We start by deriving the Wasserstein gradient flow of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
and, after observing that the corresponding PDE is a Fokker–Plank equation, use standard stochastic calculus to obtain a McKean–Vlasov SDE (MKVSDE) whose law satisfies the Fokker–Plank PDE.

3.1
Wasserstein Gradient Flow

To derive the Wasserstein gradient flow PDE for

[
4

10.1.1]

\pi_{0}

Assumption 3
.

The following hold:

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\eta}(\pi)

3.2
McKean–Vlasov SDE

Since (

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bm{X}_{t}=\left\{\int b^{\eta}(\bm{X}_{t},z,\pi_{t})\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}(z)-\alpha\nabla U(\bm{X}_{t})\right\}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2(1+\alpha)}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}

4
Particle System

As discussed in the previous section, the drift of the MKVSDE (
9
) cannot be computed analytically, since it depends on the law
\pi_{t}
of
\bm{X}_{t}
at each time step.
Thus, it is not possible to directly approximate (
9
) with a time-discretized process.

A classical approach to circumvent this issue is to consider an interacting particle system
(\bm{X}_{t}^{1:N})_{t\geq 0}=\{(\bm{X}_{t}^{i,N})_{t\geq 0}\}_{i=1}^{N}
which approximates (
9
) for any

N\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
and satisfies a classical SDE
[
45
,
10
]
.
We introduce the particle system
(\bm{X}_{t}^{1:N})_{t\geq 0}
which satisfies the
following SDE, for any
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
,
\bm{X}_{0}^{i,N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathrm{d}\bm{X}_{t}^{i,N}=\left\{\int b^{\eta}(\bm{X}_{t}^{i,N},z,\pi_{t}^{N})\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}^{N}\left(z\right)-\alpha\nabla U(\bm{X}_{t}^{i,N})\right\}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2(1+\alpha)}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}^{i},

(11)

where
\{(\bm{B}_{t}^{i})_{t\geq 0}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a family of independent
Brownian motions and
\pi_{t}^{N}
is the empirical measure
associated with
(\bm{X}_{t}^{1:N})_{t\geq 0}

\pi_{t}^{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\bm{X}_{t}^{i,N}}
for every
t\geq 0
.

In the following proposition we establish a propagation of chaos result,
showing that there exists a unique strong solution to (
11
) and that this
solution approximates (
9
) for any finite time horizon. The proof is classical, see, e.g.
[
16
, Prop. 7]

4.1
Euler–Maruyama Discretization

Having obtained a particle system (
11
) whose invariant distribution converges to the minimizer
\pi_{\alpha,\eta}^{\star}
of (
2
), we now consider the numerical approximation of (
11
) using an Euler–Maruyama time discretization scheme. For any

N\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider the following time discretization
given by
X_{0}^{1:N}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
and

\forall n\in\mathbb{N},k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}:\qquad X_{n+1}^{k,N}=X_{n}^{k,N}+\gamma b(X_{n}^{k,N},\pi^{N}_{n})+\sqrt{2\gamma(\alpha+1)}Z_{n+1}^{k},

(12)

where
\{Z_{n}^{k}\}_{k,n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a family of independent standard Gaussian random
variables,
\gamma>0
is a stepsize,
b
is

b(x,\pi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}b^{\eta}(x,z,\pi)\mathrm{d}\pi(z)-\alpha\nabla U(x),

(13)

and
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}

\pi^{N}_{n}=(1/N)\sum_{k=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{n}^{k,N}}
.

Since under our assumptions the drift
b
is Lipschitz continuous in both arguments (see Appendix
D.1
for a proof),
[
10
]

For simplicity, we use an Euler–Maruyama time discretization instead of the tamed one proposed in
[

4.2
Implementation Guidelines

Efficient Computation

Notice that the drift of (
9
) involves nested expectations w.r.t.
\pi_{t}
contrary to the drift of the MKVSDE obtained for equations of the first kind in
[
16
]
. In particular, we can decompose the drift into

\displaystyle b(x,\pi)

\displaystyle=\int b^{\eta}(x,z,\pi)\mathrm{d}\pi(z)-\alpha\nabla U(x)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\lambda\nabla_{2}\mathrm{k}(z,x)}{\lambda\pi\left[\mathrm{k}(z,\cdot)\right]+\varphi(z)+\eta}\mathrm{d}\pi(z)+\frac{\lambda\pi[\nabla_{1}\mathrm{k}(x,\cdot)]+\nabla\varphi(x)}{\lambda\pi\left[\mathrm{k}(x,\cdot)\right]+\varphi(x)+\eta}-\alpha\nabla U(x).

Naïve implementation of the evaluation of
b
can lead to
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
cost; precomputing
\pi^{N}_{n}\left[\mathrm{k}(X_{n}^{j,N},\cdot)\right]
and storing its value for each
j
allows computation at
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
cost.

Particle systems such as this also lend themselves to natural parallel implementation. On appropriate shared-memory architectures one could parallelise both the pre-computation of
\pi^{N}_{n}\left[\mathrm{k}(X_{n}^{j,N},\cdot)\right]
and then the propagation of the particles themselves leaving an
\mathcal{O}(N)
cost for direct parallel implementations with width
N
; further performance improvements could be obtained by using fork-and-join techniques to carry out the computations associated with individual particles.

Choice of
\pi_{0}

The reference measure
\pi_{0}
is the mechanism by which regularization is specified and can be chosen to impose particular properties (e.g. degree of smoothness, localized support) on
\pi
or to favour solutions with such properties.
While the presence of
\pi_{0}
might seem arbitrary, (
1
) is an inverse problem, and thus regularization is required to obtain stable results. This manifests itself in the fact that
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
admits a unique minimizer only when
\alpha>0
(Proposition
1
).

The choice of an improper reference measure
\pi_{0}\propto C
with
C>0
might seem a natural pragmatic choice. However, this results in a MKVSDE whose drift does not depend on
\pi_{0}

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bm{X}_{t}=\left\{\int b^{\eta}(\bm{X}_{t},z,\pi_{t})\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}(z)\right\}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2(1+\alpha)}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}.

(16)

This scheme corresponds to constructing the gradient flow for the functional (
6
), that, as previously discussed, does not necessarily lead to a unique minimizer. In addition, our proof of convergence of
\pi_{t}
to the unique minimizer (Proposition
4
) relies on the fact that
\alpha>0
with some
\pi_{0}
satisfying Assumption
3
.

The first experiment shows that the presence of
\pi_{0}
stabilizes the minimizer
\pi_{\alpha,\eta}^{\star}
.
See also
[
16
, App. D.1]
for a discussion of the impact of
\pi_{0}
in a similar context.

Tuning and algorithmic set up

The value of
\alpha
controls the amount of regularization introduced by the cross-entropy penalty, and, as shown by Proposition
1
, when
\alpha>0
the functional
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
is coercive and thus admits a unique minimizer.
The value of
\alpha
could be fixed a priori, or, to guarantee a good trade-off between accuracy and regularization, it could be selected by cross-validation as suggested in
[
16
,
59
,
3
]
for integral equations of the first kind.

The parameter
\eta
was introduced in (
4
) to deal with the possible instability of the functional
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
; we did not find performances to be significantly
influenced by this parameter as long as its value is sufficiently small. In practice, in the experiments in Section
5
we set
\eta\equiv 0
and observe that the resulting algorithm is stable. This was also empirically observed in related contexts by
[
16
]
where a tamed Euler discretization is employed, and by
[
43
]
where a standard Euler discretization is employed.

The values of the number of particles

N
and the time discretization step
\gamma
control the quality of the numerical approximation of (
9
).
Combining the
result in Proposition
5
with (
14
) we obtain the following global error estimate: for any
T\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{n\in\{0,\dots,n_{T}\}}\left\|\bm{X}_{n\gamma}^{\star}-X_{n}^{\ell,N}\right\|\right]\leq\frac{c_{1}(T)}{\sqrt{N}}+c_{2}(T)\sqrt{\gamma}.

(17)

This global error estimate differs from the one in
[
16
, Eq. (19)]
: for equations of the second kind,
\pi
appears on both sides of (
1
) and thus no observed data is available (leading to the lack of the
\gamma m^{-1/2}
term in
[
16
, Eq. (19)]
);
the different analysis of the time discretization scheme discussed below (
14
) leads to the different rate w.r.t.
\gamma
.

Choosing
N
amounts to the classical task of selecting an appropriate sample size for Monte Carlo approximations, while the choice of
\gamma
corresponds to the specification of a timescale on which to discretize a (stochastic) process; hence, one can exploit the vast literature on Monte Carlo methods and discrete time approximations of SDEs to select these values
[
40
]
.

Given a fixed computational budget
B
, as the cost of running Algorithm
1
is
\mathcal{O}(N^{2}/\gamma)
, we can minimize the r.h.s. of (
17
) subject to
B=N^{2}/\gamma
and obtain, via a simple Lagrange multiplier argument, that the optimal values of
N,\gamma
are
N=B^{1/3}[c_{1}(T)/(2c_{2}(T))]^{2/3}
and
\gamma=N^{2}/B=(c_{1}(T)/2c_{2}(T))^{4/3}B^{-1/3}
.
This suggests that, optimally,
N
and
1/\gamma
both scale with the cube root of the computational effort available—suggesting setting
\gamma=O(N^{-1})
, which results in an error decaying at the usual Monte Carlo rate of
O(N^{-1/2})
with an overall cost scaling as
O(N^{3})
.

It is straightforward to choose the number of time steps
n_{T}
adaptively
by approximating the value of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
through numerical
integration by replacing the r.h.s. of (
1
) with (
15
) and using standard kernel density estimation
[
53
]
for the l.h.s..
Once the value of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\eta}
stops decreasing, a
minimizer has apparently been reached and the iteration can be stopped.

5.2

The Karhunen–Loève expansion of a given stochastic process with covariance function
\mathrm{k}(x,y)
is given by the pairs of eigenvalues and eigenfunctions

of
\mathrm{k}
. For any eigenvalue

\varphi\equiv 0

We consider two examples commonly used in the Gaussian process literature

\mathrm{k}(x,y)=\exp(-(y-x)^{2})

\mathrm{k}(x,y)=\exp(-|y-x|)

The Karhunen–Loève expansion of the exponential kernel is known analytically

We compare the results obtained with the Nyström method, i.e. solving the eigenvalue problem associated with the matrix obtained by discretizing

We compare the results of the two methods for increasing precision, corresponding to the number of particles

6
Discussion

In this paper we extended the approach of
[
16
]
to Fredholm integral equations of the second kind.
Under similar assumptions to those in
[
16
]
on the kernel
\mathrm{k}
, we show that the regularized functional (
4
) admits a unique minimizer which is the limiting distribution of the McKean–Vlasov SDE (
9
). In addition, we study the limiting behaviour of the minimizer when the regularization parameter tends to 0 (Proposition
2
).

We employ an interacting particle system (
11
) to approximate this SDE and derive ergodicity and propagation of chaos results.
Combining the latter with strong convergence results of Euler–Maruyama schemes we obtain the bound (
17
) which provides a practical guideline for the selection of the time discretization step
\gamma
and the number of particles
N
.

Our method addresses a wide variety of equations of the second kind and is competitive with standard methods based on deterministic discretizations. As in the case of
[
16
]
, the presence of the reference measure
\pi_{0}
proves beneficial when the problem difficulty increases (Section
5.1
).
In addition, FE2kind-WGF is robust to small deviations from Assumption
1
, as shown in Section
5.2
where we consider a kernel
\mathrm{k}
which is not continuously differentiable.

While we focused on one dimensional examples to benchmark our method against other algorithms proposed in the literature, we believe that FE2kind-WGF has the potential to tackle higher dimensional problems and, as shown in
[
16
, Section 5.4]
for equations of the first kind, to significantly outperform methods based on deterministic discretizations with the same storage cost. This is also confirmed in the one-dimensional case by Figure
3
left panel. One area in which this could prove particularly beneficial is spatial statistics, in which multidimensional spatial Karhunen–Loève expansions are often employed
[
28
,
15
]
.

One advantage of the Nyström method against FE2kind-WGF is that the latter requires knowledge of the eigenvalue
\lambda
for which we seek to find the corresponding eigenfunction. One possible way to relax this requirement is to modify the functional (
6
) to
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\pi,\lambda)
, allowing the definition of a minimization problem over the product space
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
as explored in
[
42
]
in a different setting.

A.1
Auxiliary Result for the Proof of Proposition
1

The following auxiliary result is adapted from
[
27
, Appendix A.1]
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\nu,\mu
be
\sigma
-finite and finite measures, respectively, on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and denote by
\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the set of
\sigma
-finite measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Assume that
\mu\ll\nu
. Then, the Kullback–Leibler divergence
\textup{KL}\left(\mu\middle|\nu\right)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log((\mathrm{d}\mu/\mathrm{d}\nu)(y))\mathrm{d}\mu(y)
is convex in both arguments.

Proof.

The KL divergence above can be written as an
f
-divergence for
f(s)=s\log s
with the convention
0\log 0=0
,
\textup{KL}\left(\mu\middle|\nu\right)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathrm{d}\mu/\mathrm{d}\nu)\mathrm{d}\nu
.
The convex conjugate of
f
is, for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle f^{\star}(u):=\sup_{s>0}(su-f(s))=e^{u-1},

and satisfies
f(s)+f^{\star}(u)\geq su
by Fenchel’s inequality.

We will now show that the KL divergence can be expressed as the solution of a dual concave problem:

\displaystyle\textup{KL}\left(\mu\middle|\nu\right)=\sup_{h\in\mathrm{C}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left[\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{h(x)-1}\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\right],

(19)

where
\mathrm{C}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the set of real-valued bounded continuous functions over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since the supremum in (
19
) is taken over a convex function of
\nu,\mu
we will consequently obtain that
\textup{KL}\left(\mu\middle|\nu\right)
is convex in both arguments.

We now prove (
19
). If
\mu
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\nu
we have

\displaystyle\textup{KL}\left(\mu\middle|\nu\right)-\left[\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{h(x)-1}\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\right)\mathrm{d}\nu+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{\star}(h(x))\mathrm{d}\nu(x)-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\geq 0,

by Fenchel’s inequality. This gives an upper bound on the
\sup
in (
19
). We will now establish that it is also bounded below by the KL divergence.

Let us define
h^{\star}:=\log\mathrm{d}\mu/\mathrm{d}\nu+1
and observe that

\displaystyle\textup{KL}\left(\mu\middle|\nu\right)=\left[\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h^{\star}(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{h^{\star}(x)-1}\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\right].

Then, if
h_{n}=\log\mathrm{d}\mu/\mathrm{d}\nu\mathbbm{1}_{1/n\leq\log\mathrm{d}\mu/\mathrm{d}\nu\leq n}+1\in\mathrm{C}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the monotone and dominated convergence theorems
give

\displaystyle\left[\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h_{n}(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{h_{n}(x)-1}\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\right]\to\textup{KL}\left(\mu\middle|\nu\right)

as
n\to\infty
. Hence, we obtain a lower bound on the
\sup
in (
19
).

It follows that the optimal value in (
19
) is bounded above and below by
\textup{KL}\left(\mu\middle|\nu\right)
giving the result.
∎
