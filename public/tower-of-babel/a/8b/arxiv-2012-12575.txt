Abstract
. Given a graph and a representation of its fundamental group, there is a naturally associated twisted adjacency operator, uniquely defined up to conjugacy. The main result of this article is the fact that this operator behaves in a controlled way under graph covering maps. When such an operator can be used to enumerate objects, or compute a partition function, this has concrete implications on the corresponding enumeration problem, or statistical mechanics model. For example, we show that if Γ ~ \widetilde{\Gamma} is a finite covering graph of a connected graph Γ \Gamma endowed with edge-weights 𝗑 = { 𝗑 𝖾 } 𝖾 \mathsf{x}=\{\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}\}_{\mathsf{e}} , then the spanning tree partition function of Γ \Gamma divides the one of Γ ~ \widetilde{\Gamma} in the ring ℤ ⁡ [ 𝗑 ] \mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x}] . Several other consequences are obtained, some known, others new.

1.
Introduction

The aim of this article is to present a result of algebraic graph theory, probably known to the experts,
in a fairly self-contained and elementary manner.
This brings into what we believe to be the correct framework several well-known results in combinatorics,
statistical mechanics, and
L
-function theory, but also provides new ones.
In order to preserve its non-technical nature, we focus in the present article on relatively direct consequences,
leaving the more elaborate implications to subsequent papers, see in particular
[
12
]
.

We now explain our main result in an informal way,
referring to Section
2
for precise definitions
and background, to Theorem
3.6
for the complete formal statement,
and to Section
3.2
for its proof.

Given a locally finite weighted graph
\Gamma
and a representation
\rho
of its fundamental group,
one can define a
twisted adjacency operator

\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho}
, see Equation (
1
),
which is well-defined up to conjugacy.
Consider a covering map
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
of finite degree between two connected locally finite graphs.
Via this map, the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma})
embeds into
\pi_{1}(\Gamma)
. As a consequence,
any representation
\rho
of
\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma})
defines an

induced representation

\rho^{\#}
of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma)
.
Our main result is the fact that the operator
\mathsf{A}^{\rho}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}

is conjugate to
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho^{\#}}
.

Let us mention that the existence of a natural isomorphism between the vector spaces on which
the twisted adjacency operators act can be
understood as a chain-complex version of the so-called
Eckmann-Shapiro lemma
, originally stated in
group cohomology (see Remark
3.7
).
The interesting part of Theorem
3.6
, which we have not been able to find in the literature,
is the fact that the explicitized natural isomorphism conjugates the aptly defined twisted adjacency operators.

As an immediate consequence of this result, we see that the decomposition of
\rho^{\#}
into irreducible representations
leads to a direct sum decomposition of
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho^{\#}}
, and therefore of
\mathsf{A}^{\rho}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}
.
For example, if
\rho
is taken to be the trivial representation,
we readily obtain the fact that
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}
is a direct summand of
\mathsf{A}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}
, see Corollary
3.8
.
(Here, the absence of superscript means that these operators are not twisted,
or twisted by the trivial representation.)
Furthermore, if the covering is normal, then
\mathsf{A}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}
factors as
a direct sum of the operators
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho}
twisted by the irreducible representations of the Galois
group of the covering, see Corollary
3.9
.

Whenever
\mathsf{A}^{\rho}_{\Gamma}
can be used to enumerate combinatorial objects in
\Gamma
, or in
an associated graph
\mathsf{G}
, these statements have very concrete combinatorial implications.
More generally, if these operators can be used to compute some partition functions of the weighted graph
(\Gamma,\mathsf{x})
,
or of an associated weighted graph
(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})
, these results have often non-trivial
consequences on the corresponding
models. Several of these implications are well-known, but others are new.
We now state some of them, referring to Section
4
for details.

There is an obvious place to start, namely the
matrix-tree theorem
: the Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}
allows to
enumerate spanning trees (STs) and rooted spanning forests (RSFs) in
\mathsf{G}
.
More generally, if
\mathsf{G}=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{E})
is endowed
with edge-weights
\mathsf{x}=\{\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}\}_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}
, then a weighted version of
\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}
allows to compute the corresponding partition
functions
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})
and
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{RSF}}(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})
,
which can be thought of as elements of the polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x}]=\mathbb{Z}[\{\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}\}_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}]
.
Applying Corollary
3.8
to the Laplacian
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}=\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}
, we obtain the following result:
if
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}
is a finite covering graph of a finite connected graph
\mathsf{G}
endowed with
edge-weights
\mathsf{x}
, and if
\tilde{\mathsf{x}}
denotes these weights lifted to
the edges of
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}
, then
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})

divides
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\mathsf{x}})

and
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{RSF}}(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})
divides
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{RSF}}(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\mathsf{x}})
in the
ring
\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x}]
.
As an immediate consequence, the number of spanning trees in
\mathsf{G}
divides the number of spanning
trees in
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}
(a fact first proved by Berman
[
5
]
using a different method),
and similarly for rooted spanning forests (to the best of our knowledge, a new result).

Another interesting class of operators is given by the weighted skew-adjacency matrices defined by
Kasteleyn
[
25
,
26
]
in his study of the
dimer model
on surface graphs.
For this model, Corollary
3.8
can only be applied to cyclic coverings,
yielding a known result
[
19
,
30
]
.
Applying Corollary
3.9
to the case of a graph embedded in the torus yields an immediate proof
of the classical fact that the dimer characteristic polynomial behaves multiplicatively under so-called

enlargement of the fundamental domain

[
29
, Theorem 3.3]
.
However, applying our results to the study of the dimer model on graphs embedded in the Klein bottle
leads to new powerful results, that are harnessed in the parallel article
[
12
]
.

Let us finally mention that our main result can be interpreted as the fact that the operators
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho}

satisfy the so-called
Artin formalism
, a set of axioms originating from the study of
L
-series of
Galois field extensions
[
2
,
3
]
. As a consequence, we obtain several results on
the associated
L
-series
L(\Gamma,\mathsf{x},\rho)=\det(\operatorname{I}-\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho})^{-1}
, providing a wide generalization
of the results of Stark and Terras
[
38
,
39
]
, see Section
3.3
.

We conclude this introduction with one final remark.
There are two ways to consider graphs: either as combinatorial objects, or as topological ones (namely
1
-dimensional CW-complexes). Hence, there are two corresponding ways to define and study the associated fundamental
groups and covering maps. In our pursuit of simplicity, we have chosen the combinatorial one. As a result, we
provide the reader with a brief and dry but self-contained treatment of the required parts of algebraic
topology translated from the topological to the combinatorial category,
see Sections
2.1
–
2.3
.

This paper is organised as follows. Section
2
deals with the necessary background material and
claims no originality: we start from scratch with graphs, their fundamental groups and covering maps, before
moving on to connections on graphs, and basics of representation theory of groups.
Section
3
contains the definition of the twisted operators, our main result with its proof and
corollaries, together with the analogy with algebraic number theory via the Artin formalism.
Finally, Section
4
deals with the aforementioned combinatorial applications.

2.
Background on graphs and representations

In this section, we first recall the combinatorial definitions of the
fundamental group of a graph and of graph coverings, adapting
the standard references
[
37
]
and
[
18
]
to our purposes,
see also
[
31
]
.
We then proceed with connections on graph vector bundles following
[
27
]
,
and linear representations of groups following
[
36
]
.

2.1.
Graphs and directed graphs

This first paragraph deals with the elementary concepts of graph and directed graph.
Since there is no universal agreement on the relevant terminology and notation, we
record here these formal definitions following
[
37
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
directed graph
(or
digraph
)
\Gamma
consists of a set
\mathsf{V}
of
vertices
,
a set
\mathsf{D}
of
(directed) edges
, together with
maps
s,t\colon\mathsf{D}\to\mathsf{V}
assigning to each edge
e\in\mathsf{D}
its
source
vertex
s(e)\in\mathsf{V}
and its
target
vertex
t(e)\in\mathsf{V}
.

A
graph

\Gamma
consists of sets
\mathsf{V},\mathsf{D}
and
maps
s,t\colon\mathsf{D}\to\mathsf{V}
as above,
together with an involution of
\mathsf{D}

assigning to each
edge
e\in\mathsf{D}
its
inverse

\overline{e}\in\mathsf{D}
such that
\overline{e}\neq e
and
s(\overline{e})=t(e)
.
We let
\mathsf{E}=\mathsf{D}/(e\sim\overline{e})
denote the set of
unoriented edges
, and write
\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}
for the unoriented
edge corresponding to
e,\overline{e}\in\mathsf{D}
.

A (directed) graph is
locally finite
if for all
v\in\mathsf{V}
, the
sets
\mathsf{D}_{v}=\{e\in\mathsf{D}\,|\,s(e)=v\}
and
\mathsf{D}^{v}=\{e\in\mathsf{D}\,|\,t(e)=v\}
are finite.
It is called
finite
if both sets
\mathsf{V}
and
\mathsf{D}
are finite.

Note that these graphs are not simple in general: we allow multiple edges as well as
self-loops
, i.e. edges
e
with
s(e)=t(e)
.
Note also that in this formalism, graphs are special types of directed graphs. Moreover,
given a directed graph
\Gamma
, one can build an associated graph (still denoted by
\Gamma
) by
formally adding an inverse
\overline{e}
to each edge
e\in\mathsf{D}
.

Let us fix a directed graph
\Gamma
.
A
path of length
n\geq 1
is a sequence
\gamma=(e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n})
of edges such that
t(e_{i})=s(e_{i+1})
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
.
We shall write
s(\gamma)=s(e_{1})
and
t(\gamma)=t(e_{n})
for the source and target of
\gamma
, respectively.
A
path of length
0
, or
constant path

\gamma
, is given by a vertex, which is both
the source and target of
\gamma
.
A
loop
(based at
v
) is a path
\gamma
with
s(\gamma)=t(\gamma)=v
.

The directed graph
\Gamma
is said to be
connected
if for any
v,w\in\mathsf{V}
, there is a path
\gamma
with
s(\gamma)=~v
and
t(\gamma)=w
.

2.2.
The fundamental group of a graph

Let us now assume that
\Gamma
is a graph, and fix a vertex
v\in\mathsf{V}
.

Note that the set of loops based at
v
is a monoid
with respect to the concatenation of paths, with
neutral element
1
given by the constant path based at
v
.
Let us call two loops based at
v
(or more generally, two paths with same source and same target)

homotopic
if one can be obtained from the other
by removing or adding loops of the form
(e,\overline{e})
along the path.
Then, the set of homotopy classes of loops based at
v
forms a group,
with the inverse of
\gamma=(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
given by
\overline{\gamma}=(\overline{e}_{n},\dots,\overline{e}_{1})
.

Definition 2.2
.

This group is the
fundamental group of the graph
\Gamma
based at
v
, and is denoted
by
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v)
.

If
\Gamma
is connected, then the isomorphism class of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v)
is easily seen not to depend on
the base vertex
v
.

By a slight abuse of terminology, we define the
fundamental group of a directed graph
\Gamma

as the fundamental group of the associated graph obtained by adding an inverse to each edge of
\Gamma
.

We will make use of the alternative definition of the fundamental group, based on a spanning tree.
Recall that a
circuit
(of length
n\geq 1
) is a loop
\gamma=(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})

such that
e_{i+1}\neq\overline{e}_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
,
e_{1}\neq\overline{e}_{n}
, and such that the vertices
t(e_{1}),\dots,t(e_{n})
are all distinct. A
spanning tree
of
\Gamma
is a connected non-empty subgraph
T\subset\Gamma

without circuits, such that the vertices of
T
coincide with the vertices of
\Gamma
.
Note that the number of vertices and edges in a finite tree satisfy
|\mathsf{V}(T)|-|\mathsf{E}(T)|=1
.

The
fundamental group of the graph
\Gamma
based at
T
, denoted by
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,T)
, is defined as the quotient
of the free group over
\mathsf{D}
by the relations
\overline{e}=e^{-1}
for all edges of
\Gamma
, and
e=1
for all edges of
T
.
If
\Gamma
is connected, then it admits a spanning tree, and the groups
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v)
and
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,T)
are
easily seen to be isomorphic for all
v\in\mathsf{V}
and all spanning trees
T
of
\Gamma
.
As a consequence, if
\Gamma
is connected and finite, its fundamental group is free
of rank
|\mathsf{E}|-|\mathsf{V}|+1
.

2.3.
Covering graphs

A
morphism of digraphs

p
from
\widetilde{\Gamma}=(\widetilde{\mathsf{V}},\widetilde{\mathsf{D}},\tilde{s},\tilde{t})

to
\Gamma=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{D},s,t)
consists of two maps
p_{0}:\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}\to\mathsf{V}
and
p_{1}:\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}\to\mathsf{D}
such
that
s\circ p_{1}=p_{0}\circ\tilde{s}
and
t\circ p_{1}=p_{0}\circ\tilde{t}
.
A
morphism of graphs

p\colon\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
is a morphism of digraphs which also satisfies the
equality
p_{1}(\overline{e})=\overline{p_{1}(e)}
for all
e\in\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}
.

As one easily checks, a morphism of graphs
p\colon\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
induces in the obvious way a
homomorphism of groups
p_{*}\colon\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{v})\to\pi_{1}(\Gamma,p(\tilde{v}))
.

Definition 2.3
.

A
covering map
is a morphism of directed graphs
p\colon\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
with
p_{0}\colon\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}\to\mathsf{V}

surjective, such that for all
\tilde{v}\in\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}
, the restriction of
p_{1}
defines
bijections
\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}_{\tilde{v}}\to\mathsf{D}_{p(\tilde{v})}
and
\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}^{\tilde{v}}\to\mathsf{D}^{p(\tilde{v})}
.
In that case,
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is called a
covering digraph
of
\Gamma
.

If
\Gamma
is a connected digraph and
p
is a covering map, then the fibers
p_{0}^{-1}(v)
and
p_{1}^{-1}(e)

have the same cardinality for all
v\in\mathsf{V}
and
e\in\mathsf{D}
. This
is called the
degree
of the covering.
From now on, we will drop the subscripts in
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
and denote both maps by
p
.

Note that any morphism of digraphs
p\colon\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
extends to a unique morphism between the associated graphs
(obtained by adding an inverse to each edge).
Moreover, if the morphism of digraphs is a covering map, then so is the associated morphism of graphs.
In such a case, the graph
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is called a
covering graph
of
\Gamma
.

The following
path lifting property
is a direct consequence of the definitions,
but nevertheless a fundamental feature of a covering map
p\colon\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
.
Given any
path
\gamma
in
\Gamma
with
s(\gamma)=v_{0}
and any
\tilde{v}_{0}\in p^{-1}(v_{0})
,
there is a unique path
\tilde{\gamma}
in
\widetilde{\Gamma}
with
p(\tilde{\gamma})=\gamma

and
s(\tilde{\gamma})=\tilde{v}_{0}
.
Furthermore, for
\gamma
a loop,
the formula
[\gamma]\cdot\tilde{v}_{0}=t(\tilde{\gamma})
defines an action
of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})
on
p^{-1}(v_{0})
. If
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is connected, then
this action is easily seen to be transitive, with
isotropy group of
\tilde{v}_{0}
equal to
p_{*}(\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{v}_{0}))
.
As a consequence, the degree of the covering coincides with
the index of
p_{*}(\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{v}_{0}))
in
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})
.

The easy proof of the following lemma is left to the reader.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
p\colon\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
is a covering map, then:

(i)

the homomorphism
p_{*}\colon\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{v})\to\pi_{1}(\Gamma,p(\tilde{v}))
is injective;

(ii)

for any
e\in\mathsf{D}
, we have the equalities

\{\tilde{t}(\tilde{e})\in\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}\,|\,\tilde{e}\in p^{-1}(e)\}=p^{-1}(t(e))\quad\text{and}\quad\{\tilde{s}(\tilde{e})\in\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}\,|\,\tilde{e}\in p^{-1}(e)\}=p^{-1}(s(e))\,.

Let us finally recall that a covering map
p\colon\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
is said to be
normal
(or
regular
)
if
p_{*}(\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},v))
is a normal subgroup of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,p(v))
. In such a case,
we denote the quotient group by
G(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
. This is nothing but the group of covering transformations
of this covering map, usually referred to as the
Galois group
.

2.4.
Connections on graphs

Following
[
27
, Section 3.1]
, let us fix a
vector bundle
on a graph
\Gamma
, i.e.
a finite-dimensional complex vector space
W
and the choice of a vector space
W_{v}
isomorphic to
W
for each
v\in\mathsf{V}
.
Such a vector bundle can be identified with
W_{\Gamma}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{v\in\mathsf{V}}W_{v}\simeq W^{\mathsf{V}}
.

Definition 2.5
.

A
connection
on a vector bundle
W_{\Gamma}
is the choice
\Phi=(\varphi_{e})_{e\in\mathsf{D}}
of an
isomorphism
\varphi_{e}\colon W_{t(e)}\to W_{s(e)}
for each
e\in\mathsf{D}
, such
that
\varphi_{\overline{e}}=\varphi_{e}^{-1}
for all
e\in\mathsf{D}
.

Two connections
\Phi=(\varphi_{e})_{e\in\mathsf{D}}
and
\Phi^{\prime}=(\varphi^{\prime}_{e})_{e\in\mathsf{D}}
are said to be

gauge-equivalent
if there is a family of automorphisms
\{\psi_{v}\colon W_{v}\to W_{v}\}_{v\in\mathsf{V}}

such that
\psi_{s(e)}\circ\varphi_{e}=\varphi^{\prime}_{e}\circ\psi_{t(e)}
for all
e\in\mathsf{D}
.

Let us fix a base vertex
v_{0}\in\mathsf{V}
, a connection
\Phi
on a vector bundle
W_{\Gamma}
,
and let us denote
W_{v_{0}}
simply by
W
.
Any loop
\gamma=(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
based at
v_{0}
gives an
automorphism
\varphi_{e_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\varphi_{e_{n}}
of
W
called the
monodromy
of
\gamma
.
This construction defines a homomorphism

\rho^{\Phi}\colon\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}(W)\,,

i.e. a representation of the fundamental group of
\Gamma
in
W
.

Any representation
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})\to\operatorname{GL}(W)
is of the form
\rho^{\Phi}
for some connection
\Phi
: indeed, one can fix a spanning tree
T\subset\Gamma
(recall that
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})\simeq\pi_{1}(\Gamma,T)
),
set
\varphi_{e}=\operatorname{id}_{W}
for each edge of
T
and
\varphi_{e}=\rho_{e}
for each of the remaining edges of
\Gamma
.
Furthermore, given two connections
\Phi
and
\Phi^{\prime}
on
W_{\Gamma}
,
one easily checks that
\rho^{\Phi}
and
\rho^{\Phi^{\prime}}
are conjugate representations
if and only if
\Phi
and
\Phi^{\prime}
are gauge-equivalent connections.

In other words, the

\operatorname{GL}(W)
-character variety

of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})
, i.e. the set of conjugacy classes of homomorphisms
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})\to\operatorname{GL}(W)
,
is given by the set of connections on
W_{\Gamma}
up to gauge-equivalence.

Remark 2.6
.

The definition of a connection as a family of isomorphisms
\varphi_{e}\colon W_{s(e)}\to W_{t(e)}
seems more natural, but
leads to antihomomorphisms of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})
. On the other hand, our convention yields
homomorphisms, and is coherent with the definition of a local coefficient system for twisted homology,
see e.g.
[
41
, p. 255]
.

2.5.
Linear representations of groups

We now recall the necessary notation and terminology of linear representations of groups, following
[
36
]
.
Throughout this subsection,
G
denotes a group.

Let us first recall that the
degree
of a
representation
\rho\colon G\to\operatorname{GL}(W)
, denoted by
\deg(\rho)
, is defined as the dimension of
W
,
which we always assume to be finite.
The only representation of degree
0
is written
\rho=0
, while the degree
1
representation
sending all elements of
G
to
1\in\mathbb{C}^{*}=\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{C})
is denoted by
\rho=1
.

Let us now fix two linear representations
\rho\colon G\to\operatorname{GL}(W)

and
\rho^{\prime}\colon G\to\operatorname{GL}(W^{\prime})
. The
direct sum
of
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}

is the representation
\rho\oplus\rho^{\prime}\colon G\to\operatorname{GL}(W\oplus W^{\prime})

given by
(\rho\oplus\rho^{\prime})_{g}=\rho_{g}\oplus\rho_{g^{\prime}}
.
A representation of
G
is said to be
irreducible
if it is not the direct sum
of two representations that are both not
0
.

Now, fix a subgroup
H<G
of finite index, and a representation
\rho\colon H\to\operatorname{GL}(W)
.
There is a representation
\rho^{\#}\colon G\to\operatorname{GL}(Z)

which is uniquely determined up to isomorphism by the following two properties.
Let
R\subset G
denote a
set of representatives
of
G/H
, i.e. each
g\in G
can be written uniquely as
g=rh\in G
with
r\in R
and
h\in H
.

(i)

We have
Z=\bigoplus_{r\in R}\rho_{r}^{\#}(W)
; in particular, the space
W
is a subspace of
Z
.

(ii)

For any
h\in H
and
w\in W
, we have
\rho_{h}^{\#}(w)=\rho_{h}(w)
.

Indeed, the first property ensures that any element of
Z
can be written uniquely as
\sum_{r\in R}\rho_{r}^{\#}(w_{r})

with
w_{r}\in W
, while the second one implies that for any
g\in G,r\in R
and
w\in W
, we have
\rho_{g}^{\#}(\rho_{r}^{\#}(w))=\rho_{r^{\prime}}^{\#}(\rho_{h}(w))
where
gr=r^{\prime}h\in G
with
r^{\prime}\in R
and
h\in H
.

This representation
\rho^{\#}\colon G\to\operatorname{GL}(Z)
is said to be
induced
by
\rho\colon H\to\operatorname{GL}(W)
.

Example 2.7
.

Let us fix a finite index subgroup
H<G
and consider the trivial representation
\rho=1
of
H
.
By definition, the induced representation
\rho^{\#}\colon G\to\operatorname{GL}(Z)
is given by the action by left multiplication
of
G
on the vector space
Z
with basis
G/H
.
Since
G
acts by permutation on the set
G/H
, which is finite, the subspace
of
Z
generated by the sum of these basis elements is fixed by this action.
Therefore, the induced representation splits as
\rho^{\#}=1\oplus\rho^{\prime}
for some
representation
\rho^{\prime}
of
G
.

Let us assume further that
H
is a normal subgroup of
G
. In such a case,
the induced representation can be written as
\rho^{\#}=\rho_{\mathrm{reg}}\circ\mathrm{pr}
,
with
\mathrm{pr}\colon G\to G/H
the canonical projection and
\rho_{\mathrm{reg}}
the so-called
regular
representation
of
G/H
.
Since this group is finite, this representation splits as

\rho_{\mathrm{reg}}=\bigoplus_{\rho\text{ irred.}}\rho^{\oplus\deg(\rho)}\,,

the sum being over all irreducible representations of
G
(see
[
36
, Section 2.4]
).

3.
Twisted operators on graph coverings

This section contains the proof of our main result, Theorem
3.6
, which
relates twisted adjacency operators on directed graphs connected by a covering map.
We start in Section
3.1
by defining the relevant twisted operators,
while Section
3.2
deals with Theorem
3.6
, its proof, and a couple of
corollaries.
Finally, Section
3.3
shows how this result can be interpreted as
a combinatorial version of the Artin formalism for these operators, yielding consequences on associated
L
-series.

3.1.
Twisted weighted adjacency operators

Fix a locally finite directed graph
\Gamma=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{D},s,t)
. Let us assume that it is endowed with

edge-weights
, i.e. a collection
x=\{x_{e}\}_{e\in\mathsf{D}}
of complex numbers attached to the edges.
The associated
weighted adjacency operator

\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}
acts on
\mathbb{C}^{\mathsf{V}}
via

(\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}f)(v)=\sum_{e\in\mathsf{D}_{v}}x_{e}\,f(t(e))\quad\text{for all~$f\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathsf{V}}$ and~$v\in\mathsf{V}$}\,.

Adapting
[
27
, Section 3.2]
to our purposes, this operator can be twisted by a
representation
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})\to\operatorname{GL}(W)

in the following way. Fix a vector bundle
W_{\Gamma}\simeq W^{\mathsf{V}}
and a connection
\Phi=(\varphi_{e})_{e\in\mathsf{D}}

representing
\rho
.

Definition 3.1
.

The associated
twisted weighted adjacency operator

\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho}
is the operator on
W^{\mathsf{V}}
given by

(1)

(\mathsf{A}^{\rho}_{\Gamma}f)(v)=\sum_{e\in\mathsf{D}_{v}}x_{e}\,\varphi_{e}(f(t(e)))\quad\text{for all~$f\in W^{\mathsf{V}}$ and~$v\in\mathsf{V}$}\,.

Several remarks are in order.

Remark 3.2
.

(i)

We make a slight abuse of notation in the sense that the
operator
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho}
depends on the choice of a connection
\Phi
and is
therefore not entirely determined by
\rho
.
However, by
Section
2.4
, conjugate representations are given by gauge equivalent connections.
Furthermore, the corresponding twisted operators are conjugated by an element of
\operatorname{GL}(W)^{\mathsf{V}}\subset\operatorname{GL}(W^{\mathsf{V}})
.
Therefore, the conjugacy class of
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho}
only depends on the conjugacy class of
\rho
.

(ii)

If a representation
\rho
is given by the direct sum of
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
, then the
operator
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho}
is conjugate to
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho_{1}}\oplus\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho_{2}}
.

(iii)

The operator
\mathsf{A}^{1}_{\Gamma}
is nothing but the untwisted operator
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}
.

Obviously, any given directed graph
\Gamma
defines a single untwisted operator
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}
,
so it might seem at first sight that the applications of our results will be rather limited.
However, there are many natural assignments
\mathsf{G}\mapsto\Gamma
mapping a directed graph
\mathsf{G}
to
another directed graph
\Gamma
so that
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}
provides a new operator on
\mathsf{G}
.
Moreover, if there is a natural homomorphism
\alpha\colon\pi_{1}(\Gamma)\to\pi_{1}(\mathsf{G})
, then
a
\rho
-twisted version of this new operator can be understood as
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho\circ\alpha}
.
Finally, if the assignment
\mathsf{G}\mapsto\Gamma
preserves covering maps, then our results apply to these new
twisted operators as well.

We now give three explicit examples of such natural maps
\mathsf{G}\mapsto\Gamma
, claiming no exhaustivity.
It is easy indeed to find additional interesting ones, e.g. the
Fisher correspondance
used
in the study of the Ising model
[
14
]
.

Example 3.3
.

Let
\mathsf{G}=(\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{G}),\mathsf{E}(\mathsf{G}))
be a graph
endowed with
symmetric
edge-weights,
i.e. labels
\mathsf{x}=(\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}})_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}(\mathsf{G})}
associated to its unoriented edges.
Consider the associated graph
\Gamma=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{E})
defined by
\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{G})
and
\mathsf{E}=\mathsf{E}(\mathsf{G})\cup\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{G})
,
where each
v\in\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{G})
produces an unoriented edge
\{v,\overline{v}\}
and the
source, target, and involution maps of
\Gamma
are given by extending the ones of
\mathsf{G}

via
s(v)=t(v)=v
for all
v\in\mathsf{V}
.
(Concretely, the graph
\Gamma
is obtained from
\mathsf{G}
by adding a self-loop at each vertex.)
Also, extend the edge-weights on
\mathsf{G}
to symmetric edge-weights on
\Gamma

via
\mathsf{x}_{v}=-\sum_{e\in\mathsf{D}(\mathsf{G})_{v}}\mathsf{x}_{e}
. Then, the corresponding weighted adjacency operator
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}

is (the opposite of) the
Laplacian

\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}
on
\mathsf{G}
.
It can be used to count spanning trees of
\mathsf{G}
— this is the celebrated
matrix-tree theorem
—
but also rooted spanning forests, see Section
4.1
.

Note that there is a natural homomorphism
\alpha\colon\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})\to\pi_{1}(\mathsf{G},v_{0})

mapping all the newly introduced self-loops to the neutral element. Given any representation
\rho
of
\pi_{1}(\mathsf{G},v_{0})
,
the associated twisted operator
\mathsf{A}^{\rho\circ\alpha}_{\Gamma}

is the
vector bundle Laplacian

\Delta^{\rho}_{\mathsf{G}}
of
[
27
]
.
When the representation
\rho
takes values in
\mathbb{C}^{*}
or
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, then
\Delta^{\rho}_{\mathsf{G}}

can be used to study cycle-rooted spanning forests
[
16
,
27
]
,
while representations of higher degree yield more involved combinatorial objects.

Example 3.4
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph endowed with symmetric edge-weights
\mathsf{x}=(\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}})_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}
.
Fix an orientation of the edges of
\Gamma
and consider the same graph
\Gamma
endowed with the anti-symmetric
edge-weights
x=\{x_{e}\}_{e\in\mathsf{D}}
given by
x_{e}=\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}
if the orientation of
e\in\mathsf{D}
agrees with
the fixed orientation, and
x_{e}=-\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}
otherwise. Then, the operator
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}
is a weighted skew-adjacency
operator that was used by Kasteleyn
[
25
,
26
]
and many others in the study of the 2-dimensional
dimer and Ising models,
see Section
4.2
.
Such operators twisted by
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-representations
are also considered by Kenyon in his study of the double-dimer model
[
28
]
.

Example 3.5
.

Let us start with a graph
\mathsf{G}=(\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{G}),\mathsf{D}(\mathsf{G}),s_{\mathsf{G}},t_{\mathsf{G}},i)
endowed with symmetric edge-weights
\mathsf{x}=\{\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}\}_{\mathsf{e}}
, and consider the
associated
directed line graph

\Gamma=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{D},s,t)
defined by

\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{D}(\mathsf{G}),\quad\mathsf{D}=\{(e,e^{\prime})\in\mathsf{V}\times\mathsf{V}\,|\,t_{\mathsf{G}}(e)=s_{\mathsf{G}}(e^{\prime})\text{ but }e^{\prime}\neq\overline{e}\},\quad s(e,e^{\prime})=e,\quad t(e,e^{\prime})=e^{\prime},

and endowed with the edge-weights
x=\{x_{e,e^{\prime}}\}_{(e,e^{\prime})\in\mathsf{D}}
defined by
x_{e,e^{\prime}}=\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}
.
Then, the operator
\operatorname{I}-\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}

is considered by Stark and Terras
[
38
,
39
]
in their study of prime cycles (see Section
3.3
),
while a similar operator is defined by Kac and Ward
[
21
]
in their exploration of the planar Ising model
(see Section
4.2
).
Note also that there is a natural homomomorphism
\alpha\colon\pi_{1}(\Gamma,e_{0})\to\pi_{1}(\mathsf{G},s(e_{0}))
, so any
representation
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(\mathsf{G},v_{0})\to\operatorname{GL}(W)
defines a twisted
operator
\operatorname{I}-\mathsf{A}^{\rho\circ\alpha}_{\Gamma}
.

3.2.
The main result

We are finally ready to state and prove our main theorem.

Let
\Gamma=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{D},s,t)
be a locally finite connected directed graph
with weights
x=(x_{e})_{e\in\mathsf{D}}
,
and let
p\colon\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
be a covering map of finite degree
d
,
with
\widetilde{\Gamma}=(\widetilde{\mathsf{V}},\widetilde{\mathsf{D}},\tilde{s},\tilde{t})
connected.
The weights
x
on
\Gamma
lift to weights
\tilde{x}
on
\widetilde{\Gamma}
via
\tilde{x}_{\tilde{e}}\coloneqq x_{p(\tilde{e})}

for all
\tilde{e}\in\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}
, so
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is a
weighted directed graph, which is locally finite.

Fix base vertices
v_{0}\in\mathsf{V}
and
\tilde{v}_{0}\in p^{-1}(v_{0})
,
and recall from Lemma
2.4
that
p
induces an
injection
p_{*}\colon\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{v}_{0})\to\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})
between the fundamental groups of the associated
graphs, so
\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{v}_{0})
can
be considered as a subgroup of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})
of index
d
.
Therefore, as explained in
Section
2.5
,
any representation
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{v}_{0})\to\operatorname{GL}(W)
induces a
representation
\rho^{\#}\colon\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})\to\operatorname{GL}(Z)
.

Theorem 3.6
.

For any covering map of connected directed graphs
p\colon\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
as above and any
representation
\rho
of
\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{v}_{0})
,
the operators
\mathsf{A}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}^{\rho}
and
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho^{\#}}
are conjugate.

Remark 3.7
.

The existence of a natural isomorphism
W^{\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}}\simeq Z^{\mathsf{V}}
is a chain-complex version
of the
Eckmann-Shapiro Lemma
, traditionally stated in the context of group (co)homology
(see e.g.
[
8
, p. 73]
).
Moreover, the tensor-product definition of the induced representation (see
[
36
, Chapter 7]
) makes
the existence of this isomorphism a routine check.
The interesting part of Theorem
3.6
is the explicit form of this natural isomorphism
in our setting, which turns out to conjugate the relevant twisted adjacency operators.

Before giving the proof of Theorem
3.6
, we present a couple of consequences.

Corollary 3.8
.

If
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is a connected covering digraph of
\Gamma
of finite degree,
then
\mathsf{A}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}
is conjugate to
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}\oplus\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho^{\prime}}
for some
representation
\rho^{\prime}
of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})
.

Proof.

Applying Theorem
3.6
to the trivial representation
\rho=1
of
\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{v}_{0})
,
we get that
\mathsf{A}^{\rho}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}=\mathsf{A}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}
is conjugate to
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho^{\#}}
,
with
\rho^{\#}
the induced representation of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})
.
By the first part of Example
2.7
, it splits as
\rho^{\#}=1\oplus\rho^{\prime}
for some
representation
\rho^{\prime}
of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})
. The statement now follows from
the second and third points of Remark
3.2
.
∎

Corollary 3.9
.

If
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
is a normal covering map of finite degree with
\widetilde{\Gamma}
connected,
then
\mathsf{A}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}
is conjugate to

\bigoplus_{\rho\text{ irred.}}\left(\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho\circ\mathrm{pr}}\right)^{\oplus\deg(\rho)}\,,

where the direct sum is over all irreducible representations of
G(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
,
and
\mathrm{pr}
stands for the canonical projection of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})
onto
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})/p_{*}(\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{v}_{0}))=G(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of Theorem
3.6
applied to the trivial representation
\rho=1

of
\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{v}_{0})

together with Example
2.7
and the second point of Remark
3.2
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

Let
p\colon\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
be a covering map sending the base
vertex
\tilde{v}_{0}
of
\widetilde{\Gamma}

to the base vertex
v_{0}
of
\Gamma
,
with
\Gamma=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{D},s,t)
a locally finite and connected directed graph endowed with edge-weights
x=(x_{e})_{e\in\mathsf{D}}
,
and
\widetilde{\Gamma}=(\widetilde{\mathsf{V}},\widetilde{\mathsf{D}},\tilde{s},\tilde{t})
a (locally finite) connected directed graph
endowed with the lifted edge-weights
\tilde{x}=(\tilde{x}_{\tilde{e}})_{\tilde{e}\in\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}}

defined by
\tilde{x}_{\tilde{e}}=x_{p(\tilde{e})}
.
As always, we use the same notation
\widetilde{\Gamma},\Gamma
for the directed graphs and for the associated graphs.

Let
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{v}_{0})\to\operatorname{GL}(W)
be a representation,
and let
\widetilde{\Phi}=(\widetilde{\varphi}_{\tilde{e}})_{\tilde{e}\in\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}}
be an arbitrary connection on a vector bundle
W_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}=\bigoplus_{\tilde{v}\in\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}}W_{\tilde{v}}
such
that
\rho^{\widetilde{\Phi}}=\rho
(recall Section
2.4
).
Consider the vector bundle on
\Gamma
given by
Z_{\Gamma}=\bigoplus_{v\in\mathsf{V}}Z_{v}
, where

Z_{v}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{\tilde{v}\in p^{-1}(v)}W_{\tilde{v}}\,.

This definition leads to the equality
Z_{\Gamma}=\bigoplus_{v\in\mathsf{V}}\bigoplus_{\tilde{v}\in p^{-1}(v)}W_{\tilde{v}}=\bigoplus_{\tilde{v}\in\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}}W_{\tilde{v}}=W_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}
.

Next, consider the connection
\Phi=(\varphi_{e})_{e\in\mathsf{D}}

defined by

\varphi_{e}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{\tilde{e}\in p^{-1}(e)}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\tilde{e}}\colon\bigoplus_{\tilde{e}\in p^{-1}(e)}W_{\tilde{t}(\tilde{e})}\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{\tilde{e}\in p^{-1}(e)}W_{\tilde{s}(\tilde{e})}\,.

Note that the second point of Lemma
2.4
gives
\bigoplus_{\tilde{e}\in p^{-1}(e)}W_{\tilde{t}(\tilde{e})}=\bigoplus_{\tilde{v}\in p^{-1}(t(e))}W_{\tilde{v}}=Z_{t(e)}
and
similarly for
Z_{s(e)}
. Therefore, the formula displayed above defines a map
\varphi_{e}\colon Z_{t(e)}\to Z_{s(e)}
, and
\Phi
is a connection on the vector bundle
Z_{\Gamma}
.

Let us denote by
\mathsf{A}^{\Phi}_{\Gamma}
the operator
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}
twisted by the connection
\Phi
,
and similarly for
\mathsf{A}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}^{\widetilde{\Phi}}
.
For any
f\in Z_{\Gamma}=W_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}
and
e\in\mathsf{D}_{v}
, one can
write

f(t(e))=\bigoplus_{\tilde{v}\in p^{-1}(t(e))}f(\tilde{v})=\bigoplus_{\tilde{e}\in p^{-1}(e)}f(\tilde{t}(\tilde{e}))

by Lemma
2.4
. For any
v\in\mathsf{V}
, this leads to

(\mathsf{A}^{\Phi}_{\Gamma}f)(v)=\sum_{e\in\mathsf{D}_{v}}x_{e}\,\varphi_{e}(f(t(e)))=\sum_{e\in\mathsf{D}_{v}}x_{e}\bigoplus_{\tilde{e}\in p^{-1}(e)}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\tilde{e}}(f(\tilde{t}(\tilde{e})))=\sum_{e\in\mathsf{D}_{v}}\bigoplus_{\tilde{e}\in p^{-1}(e)}\tilde{x}_{\tilde{e}}\,\widetilde{\varphi}_{\tilde{e}}(f(\tilde{t}(\tilde{e})))\,.

Since
p
is a morphism of graphs, this sum is equal to

\bigoplus_{\tilde{v}\in p^{-1}(v)}\sum_{\tilde{e}\in\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}_{\tilde{v}}}\tilde{x}_{\tilde{e}}\,\widetilde{\varphi}_{\tilde{e}}(f(\tilde{t}(\tilde{e})))=\bigoplus_{\tilde{v}\in p^{-1}(v)}(\mathsf{A}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}^{\widetilde{\Phi}}f)(\tilde{v})=(\mathsf{A}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}^{\widetilde{\Phi}}f)(v)\in\bigoplus_{\tilde{v}\in p^{-1}(v)}W_{\tilde{v}}=Z_{v}\,.

In conclusion, the explicit operator
\mathsf{A}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}^{\widetilde{\Phi}}
representing
\mathsf{A}^{\rho}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}
coincides with
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\Phi}
. Therefore, we are left with the proof that the connection
\Phi
on
Z_{\Gamma}
is such that
\rho^{\Phi}=\rho^{\#}
.

To do so, let us check that
\rho^{\Phi}
satisfies the two defining properties of the representation
\rho^{\#}\colon\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})\to\operatorname{GL}(Z)
induced by
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{v}_{0})\to\operatorname{GL}(W)
, as stated in Section
2.5
.
Consider a loop
\gamma
in
\Gamma
based at
v_{0}
. For any
\tilde{v}\in p^{-1}(v_{0})
,
the automorphism
\rho_{[\gamma]}^{\Phi}
maps the elements
of
W_{\tilde{v}}\subset Z_{v_{0}}=Z
to the component of
Z

corresponding to the endpoint of the lift of
\gamma
starting at
\tilde{v}
. In other words, and with the notation of Section
2.3
, we have the equality
\rho_{[\gamma]}^{\Phi}(W_{\tilde{v}})=W_{[\gamma]\cdot\tilde{v}}
.
Since
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is connected, the action of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})
on
p^{-1}(v_{0})
is transitive,
with the isotropy group of
\tilde{v}_{0}
equal to
p_{*}(\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{v}_{0}))
.
Therefore, we see that the space
Z=\bigoplus_{\tilde{v}\in p^{-1}(v_{0})}W_{\tilde{v}}
is indeed the direct sum of the images of
W_{\tilde{v}_{0}}
by any set of representatives of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})/p_{*}(\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{v}_{0}))
.
Finally, for any loop
\widetilde{\gamma}
in
\widetilde{\Gamma}
based at
\tilde{v}_{0}
and any vector
w\in W_{\tilde{v}_{0}}
,
we have
\rho_{p_{*}([\widetilde{\gamma}])}^{\Phi}(w)=\rho_{[\widetilde{\gamma}]}^{\widetilde{\Phi}}(w)=\rho_{[\widetilde{\gamma}]}(w)
by definition, thus showing the second point. This concludes the proof.
∎

3.3.
The Artin formalism for graphs

In his foundational work in algebraic number theory
[
2
,
3
]
, Artin associates an
L
-series
to any Galois field extension endowed with a representation of its Galois group.
He shows that these
L
-series satisfy four axioms, the so-called
Artin formalism

(see
[
33
, Chapter XII.2]
for a modern account).
Since then, analogous axioms have been shown to hold for
L
-series in topology
[
32
]
, in
analysis
[
20
]
, and for some
L
-series associated to finite graphs
[
39
]
.

The aim of this subsection is to explain how Theorem
3.6
can be interpreted as (the non-trivial
part of) an Artin formalism for graphs. We also show that our approach allows for wide generalisations of the results of Stark and Terras
[
38
,
39
]
.

In what follows, for simplicity, we omit the basepoint when we write fundamental groups. Recall from Section
3.1
that to any weighted locally finite directed graph
\Gamma=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{D},s,t)

endowed with a representation
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(\Gamma)\to\operatorname{GL}(W)
, we associate
a twisted weighted adjacency operator
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho}
in
\operatorname{End}(W^{\mathsf{V}})
, well defined up to
conjugation.
Recall also that a covering
p\colon\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
is said to be normal if
\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma})

is a normal subgroup of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma)
. In this case, the quotient group
G=\pi_{1}(\Gamma)/\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma})

is called the Galois group of the covering.
Given a normal covering
p\colon\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
(also simply written as
\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma
) and a
representation
\rho\colon G\to\operatorname{GL}(W)
of its Galois group, one can form
the representation
\rho\circ\mathrm{pr}\colon\pi_{1}(\Gamma)\to G\to\operatorname{GL}(W)
of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma)
.
We denote by
\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma,\rho)=\left[\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho\circ\mathrm{pr}}\right]\in\operatorname{End}(W^{\mathsf{V}})/\operatorname{GL}(W)^{\mathsf{V}}
the conjugacy class of the associated twisted adjacency operator.

Proposition 3.10
.

The map which to a normal covering
\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma
and a representation
\rho
of its Galois group
associates the class of operators
\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma,\rho)
satisfies the following four axioms.

1.

\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma,1)=[\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}]
, the untwisted adjacency operator on
\Gamma
.

2.

Given any two representations
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
of
G
,
we have

\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma,\rho_{1}\oplus\rho_{2})=\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma,\rho_{1})\oplus\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma,\rho_{2})\,.

3.

If
H
is a normal subgroup of
G
and
\overline{\Gamma}=H\backslash\widetilde{\Gamma}
denotes the corresponding covering
of
\Gamma
, then for any representation
\rho
of
G/H
, we have

\mathcal{O}(\overline{\Gamma}/\Gamma,\rho)=\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma,\rho\circ\pi)\,,

where
\pi\colon G\to G/H
denotes the canonical projection.

4.

If
H
is a subgroup of
G
and
\overline{\Gamma}=H\backslash\widetilde{\Gamma}
, then for any
representation
\rho
of
H
, we have

\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\overline{\Gamma},\rho)=\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma,\rho^{\#})\,,

where
\rho^{\#}
is the representation of
G
induced by
\rho
.

Proof.

The first and second points are reformulations of the trivial Remarks
3.2
(ii) and (iii),
while the third point follows from the fact that the
composition
\pi\circ\mathrm{pr}\colon\pi_{1}(\Gamma)\to G/H
coincides with the
canonical projection of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma)
onto
\pi_{1}(\Gamma)/\pi_{1}(\overline{\Gamma})
.
As for the last point, let
\overline{p}\colon\overline{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
denote the relevant covering map,
and
\overline{\mathrm{pr}}
the canonical projection of
\pi_{1}(\overline{\Gamma})
onto
\pi_{1}(\overline{\Gamma})/\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma})=~H
. By naturality, the composition of
\overline{\mathrm{pr}}

with the inclusion of
H
in
G
coincides with
\mathrm{pr}\circ\overline{p}_{*}
. Therefore,
the representation induced by
\rho\circ\overline{\mathrm{pr}}
coincides with
\rho^{\#}\circ\mathrm{pr}
.
The fourth point is now a formal consequence of Theorem
3.6
:

\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\overline{\Gamma},\rho)=[\mathsf{A}_{\overline{\Gamma}}^{\rho\circ\overline{\mathrm{pr}}}]=[\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{(\rho\circ\overline{\mathrm{pr}})^{\#}}]=[\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho^{\#}\circ\mathrm{pr}}]=\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma,\rho^{\#})\,.\qed

With the
L
-series of
[
39
]
in mind, it is natural to
consider
\det(\operatorname{I}-\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho\circ\mathrm{pr}})^{-1}
as the object of study.
The fact that these
L
-series satisfy the Artin formalism follows from the proposition above.

Actually, our method easily yields results on more general
L
-series, as follows.
Let us fix a map associating to a weighted graph
(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})

a weighted directed graph
(\Gamma,x)
, as in Examples
3.3
–
3.5
. Formally, we want this assignment to preserve
the ingredients of Theorem
3.6
:
a covering map
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}\to\mathsf{G}
of locally finite connected graphs is sent to a covering
map
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
of locally finite connected digraphs, and there is a natural group
homomorphism
\alpha\colon\pi_{1}(\Gamma)\to\pi_{1}(\mathsf{G})
.
Given any representation
\rho
of
\pi_{1}(\mathsf{G})
, we can now consider the
L
-series

L(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x},\rho)=\det(\operatorname{I}-\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho\circ\alpha})^{-1}\in\mathbb{C}[\![\mathsf{x}]\!]\,.

By the Amitsur formula (see
[
1
,
35
]
), it can be written as

L(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x},\rho)=\prod_{[\gamma]}\det(1-x(\gamma)\rho_{\gamma})^{-1}\,,

where the product is over all loops
\gamma
in
\Gamma
that cannot be expressed
as
\delta^{\ell}
for some path
\delta
and integer
\ell>1
,
loops considered up to change of base vertex.
Also,
x(\gamma)
denotes the product of the weights of the edges of
\gamma
, while
\rho_{\gamma}
is
the monodromy of the loop
\gamma
. (Note that changing the base point yields a conjugate monodromy,
so
\det(1-x(\gamma)\rho_{\gamma})
is well defined.)
Of course, these loops in
\Gamma
correspond to some class of loops in
\mathsf{G}
, a class which depends on the way
\Gamma
is obtained from
\mathsf{G}
.
But for any such assignment, the results of Section
3.2
have straightforward implications on the corresponding
L
-series,
and on the corresponding class of loops in
\mathsf{G}
.

For concreteness, let us focus on the directed line graph assignment
\mathsf{G}\mapsto\Gamma
described in Example
3.5
.
The corresponding twisted weighted operator
\operatorname{I}-\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho\circ\alpha}
coincides with the operator
considered in
[
39
, Theorem 7]
, where the authors restrict themselves
to representations of a finite quotient of
\pi_{1}(\mathsf{G})
, i.e. representations of the Galois group of a finite cover of
\mathsf{G}
.
In the expression displayed above, the product is over so-called
prime cycles
in
\mathsf{G}
,
i.e. equivalence classes of cyclic loops in
\mathsf{G}
that do not contain a subpath of the form
(e,\overline{e})
and that cannot be expressed as
the power of a shorter loop.
In the special case when
\rho
factorises through a finite quotient of
\pi_{1}(\mathsf{G})
,
this is what Stark and
Terras define as the
multiedge Artin
L
-function
of
\mathsf{G}
, an object extending
several other functions introduced in
[
38
,
39
]
.

The theory of Section
3
applied to
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho\circ\alpha}
now allows us to easily extend their results
to this more general
L
-function.
For example, our Corollary
3.8
shows that if
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}

is a finite connected covering graph of a connected graph
\mathsf{G}
, then
L(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x},1)^{-1}

divides
L(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\mathsf{x}},1)^{-1}
, extending Corollary 1 of
[
38
, Theorem 3]
.
Also, our Corollary
3.9
recovers the corollary of
[
39
, Proposition 3]
,
while our Theorem
3.6
extends Theorem 8 of
[
39
]
.
Finally, expanding the equality
\log L(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\mathsf{x}},\rho)=\log L(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x},\rho^{\#})

yields an extension of the technical Lemma 7 of
[
39
]
to more general covers and representations.

We conclude this section by recalling that our approach immediately yields similar results for any
assignment
\mathsf{G}\mapsto\Gamma
preserving covering maps.

4.
Combinatorial applications

Each time the determinant of an operator counts combinatorial objects,
Theorem
3.6
and Corollaries
3.8
and
3.9
have combinatorial implications.
This is the case for the operators given in Examples
3.3
and
3.4
,
whose determinants count spanning trees and perfect matchings, respectively.
We explain these applications in Sections
4.1
and
4.2
.
We also briefly enumerate additional applications in Section
4.3
.

4.1.
Spanning trees and rooted spanning forests

Our first combinatorial application relies on a slightly generalised version of the
matrix-tree theorem, that we now recall.

Let
\mathsf{G}=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{E})
be a finite graph endowed with symmetric
weights
\mathsf{x}=\{\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}\}_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}
, that we consider as formal variables.
Let
\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}
be the associated Laplacian, acting on
\mathbb{C}^{\mathsf{V}}
via

\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}f(v)=\sum_{e\in\mathsf{D}_{v}}\mathsf{x}_{e}(f(v)-f(t(e)))

for
f\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathsf{V}}
and
v\in\mathsf{V}
.
Set
n\coloneqq|\mathsf{V}|
, and consider the characteristic polynomial in
|\mathsf{E}|+1
variables

P_{\mathsf{G}}(\lambda)\coloneqq\det(\lambda\operatorname{I}-\Delta_{\mathsf{G}})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}c_{i}\,\lambda^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x},\lambda]\,.

Then, the coefficient
c_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x}]
admits the combinatorial interpretation

(-1)^{n-i}\,c_{i}=\sum_{F\subset\mathsf{G},\;|\pi_{0}(F)|=i}\phi(F)\prod_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}(F)}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}\,,

where the sum is over all spanning forests
F
in
\mathsf{G}
with
i
connected components
(or equivalently, with
n-i
edges), and
\phi(F)\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
denotes
the number of possible
roots
of
F
: if
F=\bigsqcup_{j}T_{j}
denotes the decomposition
of
F
into connected components, then
\phi(F)=\prod_{j}|\mathsf{V}(T_{j})|
.

For example, there is a unique spanning forest
F
in
\mathsf{G}
with
n
connected components
(given by the vertices of
\mathsf{G}
), it admits a unique root, leading to
the expected value
c_{n}=1
. As additional reality checks, we have the
values
-c_{n-1}=2\sum_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}
and
c_{0}=\det(\Delta_{\mathsf{G}})=0
.
Finally, since connected spanning forests coincide with spanning trees,
and all spanning trees admit exactly
n
roots, we have

(-1)^{n-1}\,c_{1}=n\sum_{T\subset\mathsf{G}}\prod_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}(T)}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}\,,

the sum being over all spanning trees of
\mathsf{G}
. This
latter result is nothing but Kirchoff’s matrix-tree theorem.

Remark 4.1
.

This result can be derived from the (usual version of the) matrix-tree theorem
applied to the graph obtained from
\mathsf{G}
by adding one vertex connected to each vertex of
\mathsf{G}
by an edge
of weight
-\lambda
.

Let us also mention that this result was obtained by Chung and Langlands
in the context of graphs endowed with vertex-weights rather than edge-weights
[
10
]
.
Theorem
3.6
trivially extends to graphs endowed with
vertex-weights (in addition to edge-weights), and it is a routine task to adapt the results
of the present subsection to this more general case.

Definition 4.2
.

The
spanning tree partition function
of a weighted graph
(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})
is

\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})\coloneqq\sum_{T\subset\mathsf{G}}\prod_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}(T)}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}\,,

the sum being over all spanning trees in
\mathsf{G}
. Similarly,
the
rooted spanning forest partition function
of
(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})
is

\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{RSF}}(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})\coloneqq\sum_{F\subset\mathsf{G}}\phi(F)\prod_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}(F)}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}\,,

the sum being over all spanning forests in
\Gamma
.

Note that if one sets all the weights to
1
, then
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\mathsf{G},1)

is the number of spanning trees in
\mathsf{G}
,
while
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{RSF}}(\mathsf{G},1)
counts the number of rooted spanning forests in
\mathsf{G}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}
be a finite covering graph of a finite connected graph
\mathsf{G}
endowed with
edge-weights
\mathsf{x}=\{\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}\}_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}
, and let
\tilde{\mathsf{x}}
denote these weights lifted to
the edges of
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}
. Then
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})

divides
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\mathsf{x}})

and
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{RSF}}(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})
divides
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{RSF}}(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\mathsf{x}})
in the
ring
\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x}]
.

This immediately leads to the following corollary. The first point is known since the work of Berman
(see
[
5
, Theorem 5.7]
), while the second one appears to be new.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}
be a finite covering graph of a finite connected graph
\mathsf{G}
.

(i)

The number of spanning trees in
\mathsf{G}
divides the number of spanning trees in
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}
.

(ii)

The number of rooted spanning forests in
\mathsf{G}
divides the number of rooted spanning forests in
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}
.∎

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

First note that
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{RSF}}(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\mathsf{x}})
is multiplicative with respect to connected sums
while
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\mathsf{x}})
vanishes for
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}
not connected.
Therefore, it can be assumed that
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}
is connected.
Let
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}\to\mathsf{G}
be a covering map between two finite connected graphs,
with edge-weights
\mathsf{x}
on
\mathsf{G}
inducing lifted edge-weights
\tilde{\mathsf{x}}
on
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}
.
Let
\widetilde{\Gamma}
(resp.
\Gamma
) be the graph associated with
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}
(resp.
\mathsf{G}
) as in Example
3.3
.
Note that the graphs
\widetilde{\Gamma}
and
\Gamma
remain finite and connected, and the covering map
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}\to\mathsf{G}
trivially extends to a covering
map
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
. By Example
3.3
and Corollary
3.8
, we know
that
\Delta_{\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}}=\mathsf{A}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}

is conjugate to
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}\oplus\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho}=\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}\oplus\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}^{\rho}
for some
representation
\rho
of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v_{0})
. Therefore,
setting
P_{\mathsf{G}}^{\rho}(\lambda)\coloneqq\det(\lambda\operatorname{I}-\Delta^{\rho}_{\mathsf{G}})\in\mathbb{C}[\mathsf{x},\lambda]
,
we have the equality

P_{\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}}(\lambda)=P_{\mathsf{G}}(\lambda)\cdot P^{\rho}_{\mathsf{G}}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{C}[\mathsf{x},\lambda]\,.

Observe that
P_{\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}}(\lambda)
and
P_{\mathsf{G}}(\lambda)
belong to
\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x},\lambda]
, so
P^{\rho}_{\mathsf{G}}(\lambda)
belongs
to the intersection of
\mathbb{C}[\mathsf{x},\lambda]
with the field of fractions
Q(\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x},\lambda])={\mathbb{Q}}(\mathsf{x},\lambda)
, i.e. it belongs to the ring
{\mathbb{Q}}[\mathsf{x},\lambda]
.
Since the leading
\lambda
-coefficient of
P_{\mathsf{G}}(\lambda)
is equal to
1
, the greatest common divisor of its coefficients is
1
.
An application of Gauss’s lemma (see e.g.
[
34
, Chapter IV.2, Corollary 2.2]
) now implies that
P^{\rho}_{\mathsf{G}}(\lambda)
belongs to
\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x},\lambda]
.
In conclusion,
P_{\mathsf{G}}(\lambda)
divides
P_{\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}}(\lambda)
in
\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x},\lambda]
.

By the extended matrix-tree theorem stated above, we have
P_{\mathsf{G}}(-1)=\pm\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{RSF}}(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})

divides
P_{\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}}(-1)=\pm\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{RSF}}(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\mathsf{x}})
in
\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x}]
,
proving the second claim.

To show the first one, consider again the
equation
P_{\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}}(\lambda)=P_{\mathsf{G}}(\lambda)\cdot P^{\rho}_{\mathsf{G}}(\lambda)
in
\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x},\lambda]
,
and observe that
P_{\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}}(\lambda)
and
P_{\mathsf{G}}(\lambda)
are both multiples of
\lambda
.
Dividing both sides by
\lambda
and setting
\lambda=~0
, the matrix-tree theorem
(in the form stated above) implies

|\mathsf{V}(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}})|\cdot\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\mathsf{x}})=\pm|\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{G})|\cdot\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})\cdot P^{\rho}_{\mathsf{G}}(0)\,,

i.e.
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\mathsf{x}})=\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})\cdot g(\mathsf{x})
,
with
g(\mathsf{x})=\frac{\pm 1}{\deg(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}/\mathsf{G})}P^{\rho}_{\mathsf{G}}(0)\in{\mathbb{Q}}[\mathsf{x}]
.
Since both
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\mathsf{x}})
and
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})

belong to
\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x}]
and the greatest common divisor of the coefficients
of
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})
is
1
, one more application of Gauss’s lemma yields that
g(\mathsf{x})

lies in
\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x}]
, and concludes the proof.
∎

4.2.
Perfect matchings

In this subsection, we review some applications of Theorem
3.6
to perfect matchings,
and more generally to the dimer model.

Recall that a
perfect matching
(or
dimer configuration
) in a graph
\Gamma
is a
family of edges
M\subset\mathsf{E}
such that
each vertex of
\Gamma
is adjacent to a unique element of
M
.
If
\Gamma
is finite and endowed with symmetric edge-weights
\mathsf{x}=\{\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}\}_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}
, then
one defines the
dimer partition function
of
\Gamma
as

\mathsf{Z}_{\text{dimer}}(\Gamma,\mathsf{x})=\sum_{M}\prod_{\mathsf{e}\in M}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}\,,

the sum being over all perfect matchings in
\Gamma
.
Note that if all the weights are equal to
1
, then
\mathsf{Z}_{\text{dimer}}(\Gamma,1)
simply counts the
number of perfect matchings in
\Gamma
.

Now, assume that
\Gamma
is embedded in the plane, and endowed with an orientation of its edges so that
around each face of
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, there is an odd number of edges oriented clockwise.
Let
x=\{x_{e}\}_{e\in\mathsf{D}}
be the anti-symmetric edge-weights obtained as in Example
3.4
,
and let
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}
be the associated weighted skew-adjacency operator. By Kasteleyn’s celebrated theorem
[
25
,
26
]
,
the Pfaffian of
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}
is equal to
\pm\mathsf{Z}_{\text{dimer}}(\Gamma,\mathsf{x})
.

With this powerful method in hand, we can try to use Theorem
3.6
in studying the dimer
model on symmetric graphs. Quite unsurprisingly, the straightforward applications of our theory are
not new. Indeed, the only divisibility statement that we obtain via Corollary
3.8

is the following known result (see Theorem 3 of
[
19
]
for the bipartite case, and Section IV.C
of
[
30
]
for a general discussion).

Proposition 4.5
.

Fix a planar, finite, connected weighted graph
(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{\mathsf{x}})
invariant
under rotation around a point in the complement of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
,
of angle
\frac{2\pi}{d}
for some odd integer
d
.
Let
(\Gamma,\mathsf{x})
be the resulting quotient weighted graph.
Then, the partition function
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathrm{dimer}}(\Gamma,\mathsf{x})

divides
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathrm{dimer}}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{\mathsf{x}})
in the ring
\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x}]
.

Proof.

Let us fix an orientation of the edges of
\Gamma
satisfying the clockwise-odd condition.
It lifts to an orientation of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
which trivially satisfies the same condition
around all faces except possibly the face containing the center of rotation; for this latter face,
it does satisfy the condition since
d
is odd.
Hence, we have a
d
-fold cyclic covering of connected weighted
graphs
(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{x})\to(\Gamma,x)
, and Corollary
3.8
can be applied.
Together with Kasteleyn’s theorem, it yields the following equality in
\mathbb{C}[\mathsf{x}]
:

\mathsf{Z}_{\text{dimer}}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{\mathsf{x}})^{2}=\det(\mathsf{A}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}})=\det(\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma})\det(\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho})=\mathsf{Z}_{\text{dimer}}(\Gamma,\mathsf{x})^{2}\cdot\det(\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\rho})\,.

This ring being factorial, it
follows that
\mathsf{Z}_{\text{dimer}}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\tilde{\mathsf{x}})=\mathsf{Z}_{\text{dimer}}(\Gamma,\mathsf{x})\cdot g

for some
g\in\mathbb{C}[\mathsf{x}]
. The fact that
g
belongs to
\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x}]
follows from Gauss’s lemma
as in the proof of Theorem
4.3
.
∎

Our approach is limited by the fact that we consider graph coverings
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
which,
in the case of normal coverings, correspond to free actions of
G(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
on
\widetilde{\Gamma}
.
For this specific question of enumerating dimers on symmetric planar graphs, the discussion of Section IV
of
[
30
]
is more complete, as non-free actions are also considered.

However, our approach is quite powerful when applied to non-planar graphs.
Indeed, recall that Kasteleyn’s theorem can be extended to weighted graphs embedded in
a closed (possibly non-orientable) surface
\Sigma
, but the computation of the
dimer partition function requires the Pfaffians of
2^{2-\chi(\Sigma)}
different
(possibly complex-valued) skew-adjacency matrices
[
40
,
13
,
11
]
.
In particular, the partition function of any graph embedded in the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
is given by
4
Pfaffians.
For the Klein bottle
\mathcal{K}
, we also need
4
Pfaffians, which turn out to be two pairs of conjugate complex numbers, so
2
well-chosen Pfaffians are sufficient. We now illustrate the use of Theorem
3.6
in these two cases.

Let us first consider a toric graph
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{T}^{2}
, and let
\widetilde{\Gamma}=\Gamma_{mn}

denote the lift of
\Gamma
by the natural
m\times n
covering of the torus by itself.
This covering is normal with Galois group
G(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)\simeq\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
.
This group being abelian, all the irreducible representations are of degree
1
;
more precisely, they are given by
\{\rho(z,w)\}_{w^{m}=1,\,z^{n}=1}
, where
\rho(z,w)

maps a fixed generator of
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
(resp.
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
) to
w\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
(resp.
z\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
).
Writing
P_{mn}(z,w)=\det(\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma_{mn}}^{\rho(z,w)})
and
P_{1,1}=P
, Corollary
3.9
immediately yields
the equality

P_{mn}(1,1)=\prod_{z^{n}=1}\prod_{w^{m}=1}P(z,w)\,.

This is the well-known Theorem 3.3 of
[
29
]
, a result of fundamental importance in the study of the dimer
model on biperiodic graphs.

Figure 1.
A graph
\Gamma
embedded in the Klein bottle
\mathcal{K}
(pictured as a square with opposite sides identified
according to the arrows), and the lift
\Gamma_{mn}\subset\mathcal{K}
, here with
m=2
and
n=3
.

Let us now consider a weighted graph
\Gamma
embedded in the Klein bottle
\mathcal{K}
,
and let
\widetilde{\Gamma}=\Gamma_{mn}
denote the lift of
\Gamma
by the natural
m\times n

cover
\mathcal{K}_{mn}\to\mathcal{K}
of the Klein bottle by itself (with
n
odd), as illustrated in Figure
1
.
Now, we can interpret the two skew-adjacency matrices of
\widetilde{\Gamma}=\Gamma_{mn}

used in the computation of the corresponding dimer partition function as
weighted adjacency operators twisted by
1
-dimensional representations
\rho,\rho^{\prime}
of
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{K}_{mn})<\pi_{1}(\mathcal{K})
.
Using Theorem
3.6
, we see that these matrices are conjugate to
the skew-adjacency operators on
\Gamma\subset\mathcal{K}
twisted by the corresponding
induced representations
\rho^{\#},(\rho^{\prime})^{\#}
of
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{K})
.
Unlike that of the torus, the fundamental group of the Klein bottle is not abelian,
so the representations
\rho^{\#},(\rho^{\prime})^{\#}
need not split as products of
1
-dimensional
representations.
It turns out that they split as products of representations of degree
1
and
2
,
yielding a closed formula for
\mathsf{Z}_{\text{dimer}}(\Gamma_{mn},x)
in terms of determinants of
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}^{\tau}
,
with
\tau
of degree
1
and
2
.
This result is at the core of the study of the dimer model on Klein bottles of the first-named
author
[
12
]
.

As a final remark, let us note that all the considerations of this subsection
can be applied equally well to the Ising model,
either via the use of Kac-Ward matrices
[
21
]
, or
via skew-adjacency matrices on the associated Fisher graph
[
14
]
.

4.3.
Further combinatorial applications

We conclude this article with a very brief and informal description of additional applications of
our results.

As discovered by Forman
[
16
]
, the determinant of
\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}^{\rho}
with
\deg(\rho)=1
can be
expressed as a sum over
cycle-rooted spanning forests
(CRSFs) in
\mathsf{G}
, each forest being counted with a
complex weight depending on
\rho
.
If there is a finite connected covering
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}\to\mathsf{G}
and a degree
1
representation
of
\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{v}_{0})
such that the induced representation of
\pi_{1}(\mathsf{G},v_{0})

admits a degree
1
subrepresentation
\rho^{\prime}
, then the CRSF partition function on
\mathsf{G}
twisted
by
\rho^{\prime}
divides the partition function on
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}
twisted by
\rho
, in the ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathsf{x}]
.
Furthermore, in the case of a normal abelian covering of degree
d
, Corollary
3.9
gives a factorisation
of the CRSF partition function of
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}
in terms of
d
CRSF partition functions of
\mathsf{G}
.

Finally, let
X
be a finite CW-complex
of dimension
r
with weights
\mathsf{x}=(\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}})_{\mathsf{e}}
associated
to the cells of top dimension. Let
\mathsf{G}
be the weighted graph with vertex set given by the
(r-1)
-dimensional cells
of
X
, two such vertices being connected by an unoriented edge of
\mathsf{G}
each time they are in the boundary
of an
r
-dimensional cell. (Note that if
r=1
, then the
1
-dimensional cell complex
X
is nothing but the
geometric realisation of the graph
\mathsf{G}
.) Finally, let
\Gamma
denote the weighted graph obtained from
\mathsf{G}
as in
Example
3.3
. Then, the resulting operator
\mathsf{A}_{\Gamma}
is the
Laplacian

\Delta_{X}
acting on
r
-cells of
X
.
This operator can be used to count so-called
higher dimensional rooted forests

in
X
, see
[
22
,
6
]
and references therein.
Using Corollary
3.8
, it is now straightforward to prove that, given any finite cover
\widetilde{X}\to X
,
the corresponding rooted forest partition function of
X
divides the rooted forest partition function
of
\widetilde{X}
, extending Theorem
4.3
to higher dimensional objects.

Addendum

After our paper was in press, we became aware of the article
[
9
]
whose main result is very similar to Theorem
4.3
.
In a nusthell, Theorem 1.3 of
[
9
]
extends the spanning tree part of our Theorem
4.3

from graphs to digraphs, showing the divisibility of the partition functions enumerating the corresponding
combinatorial objects, so-called
rooted arborescences
.
Note that this result can easily be obtained by our methods using the digraph version of the matrix-tree theorem.

In Conjecture 1.7 of
[
9
]
, it is asked whether the (integer) coefficients of the quotient polynomial are always non-negative. Moreover, Conjecture 5.5 of
[
9
]
asserts that this polynomial can be expressed as a sum over tuples of vector fields on
\mathsf{G}
.
The authors prove their conjectures in the case of coverings of degree
2
.
The aim of this addendum is to provide an affirmative answer to these conjectures in the case of arbitrary degree coverings of (undirected) graphs.

To state the corresponding result, recall that a
(non-zero) vector field
on a directed graph
\mathsf{G}
is a directed subgraph of
\mathsf{G}
consisting of one outgoing edge for each vertex of
\mathsf{G}
.
We let
\mathcal{V}(\mathsf{G})
denote the set of vector fields on
\mathsf{G}
.
Note that vector fields appear in
[
16
]
and are also known as
oriented cycle rooted spanning forests
, see e.g.
[
27
]
.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}
be a covering graph of degree
(d+1)
of a finite connected graph
\mathsf{G}
endowed with
edge-weights
\mathsf{x}=\{\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}\}_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}
, and let
\tilde{\mathsf{x}}
denote these weights lifted to
the edges of
\widetilde{\mathsf{G}}
. Then
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})

divides
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\mathsf{x}})
in the ring
\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x}]
, and the quotient can be expressed as

(2)

\frac{\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\mathsf{x}})}{\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})}\;\;\;=\sum_{(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{d})\in\mathcal{V}(\mathsf{G})^{d}}f(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{d})\prod_{k=1}^{d}\prod_{e\in\gamma_{k}}\mathsf{x}_{e}

for some non-negative integer-valued map
f\colon\mathcal{V}(\mathsf{G})^{d}\to\{0,1,2,\dots\}
.

Let us stress that the right-hand side of (
2
) is not written as a linear combination of distinct monomials: in particular, upon shuffling elements of a
d
-tuple of distinct vector fields, one obtains the same monomial
\prod_{k=1}^{d}\prod_{e\in\gamma_{k}}\mathsf{x}_{e}
.

Proof.

Let us use the same notations as in the proof of Theorem
4.3
. We know from that theorem that the quotient
\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{\mathsf{x}})/\mathsf{Z}_{\mathit{ST}}(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{x})
is a homogeneous integer-coefficient polynomial equal to
\det\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}^{\rho}\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x}]
. We thus need to show the following two facts:

1.

the coefficients of this polynomial are non-negative;

2.

for each monomial
\prod_{\sf e\in\mathsf{E}}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}^{n_{\mathsf{e}}}
, there exists
(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{d})\in\mathcal{V}(\mathsf{G})^{d}
such that for each
\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}
, whose oriented versions we denote by
\{e,\overline{e}\}
, we have
n_{\mathsf{e}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{e\in\gamma_{k}\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\overline{e}\in\gamma_{k}\}}\right)
.

We start with the first assertion concerning the non-negativity of the coefficients.
Let us first recall how the representation
\rho
of
\pi_{1}(\mathsf{G},v_{0})
is obtained (see Example
2.7
), and investigate some of its properties.
Consider the representation
1^{\#}
of
\pi_{1}(\mathsf{G},v)
induced by the trivial
representation of
\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{v})
: it is given by the action by left multiplication of
\pi_{1}(\mathsf{G},v)

on the vector space
Z
with basis
\pi_{1}(\mathsf{G},v)/\pi_{1}(\widetilde{\mathsf{G}},\tilde{v})
.
Fix the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
Z
with respect to which this basis is orthonormal.
Since
1^{\#}
acts by permutation on the elements of this basis, this representation is unitary with respect to
the inner product defined above.
Moreover, since
Z
is finite-dimensional, we have an orthogonal decomposition
Z=\mathbb{C}\oplus W
,
where
\mathbb{C}
is generated by the sum of the basis vectors and
W
consists of vectors with vanishing sum of coordinates.
This leads to a decomposition
1^{\#}=1\oplus\rho
,
with
\rho
the representation appearing in the polynomial
\det\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}^{\rho}\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathsf{x}]
.
Since
1^{\#}
is unitary, we have now shown that
\rho
is a
unitary representation.
Hence, we are left with the proof that the coefficients of the polynomial
\det\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}^{\rho}
are non-negative whenever
\rho
is a unitary representation.

To do so, let us consider a unitary connection
\Phi=(\varphi_{e})_{e\in\mathsf{D}}
representing
the unitary representation
\rho\in\operatorname{GL}(W)
(recall subsection
2.4
),
which defines a twisted Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}^{\Phi}
whose gauge-equivalence class is
\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}^{\rho}
.
In order to factor
\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}^{\Phi}
, we introduce a refinement of the connection as in
[
27
]
:
for each directed edge
e\in\mathsf{D}
, consider unitary automorphisms
\varphi_{s(e),e},\varphi_{e,t(e)}\in\operatorname{GL}(W)
such that
\varphi_{\overline{e},t(\overline{e})}=\varphi_{s(e),e}^{-1}
and
\varphi_{e}=\varphi_{s(e),e}\circ\varphi_{e,t(e)}
.
Let us further define the spaces of
W
-valued forms on
\mathsf{G}
via

\Omega^{0}(\mathsf{G},W)=\{f:\mathsf{V}\to W\}=W^{\mathsf{V}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\Omega^{1}(\mathsf{G},W)=\{\alpha:\mathsf{D}\to W\;|\;\alpha_{e}=-\alpha_{\overline{e}}\}\simeq W^{\mathsf{E}}\,.

Note that the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
W
naturally extends to inner products on
\Omega^{0}(\mathsf{G},W)
and
\Omega^{1}(\mathsf{G},W)
via

(f,g)_{\Omega^{0}}=\sum_{v\in\mathsf{V}}\langle f(v),g(v)\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad(\alpha,\beta)_{\Omega^{1}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e\in\mathsf{D}}\langle\alpha(e),\beta(e)\rangle

for
f,g\in\Omega^{0}(\mathsf{G},W)
and
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega^{1}(\mathsf{G},W)
.
Next, we define twisted coboundary and boundary maps

\delta_{\Phi}\colon\Omega^{0}(\mathsf{G},W)\to\Omega^{1}(\mathsf{G},W)\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{\Phi}\colon\Omega^{1}(\mathsf{G},W)\to\Omega^{0}(\mathsf{G},W)

by

\delta_{\Phi}f(e)=\varphi_{e,t(e)}f\left(t(e)\right)-\varphi_{s(e),e}^{-1}f\left(s(e)\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{\Phi}\alpha(v)=-\sum_{e\in\mathsf{D}_{v}}\varphi_{s(e),e}\,\alpha(e)

for
f\in\Omega^{0}(\mathsf{G},W),\alpha\in\Omega^{1}(\mathsf{G},W),v\in\mathsf{V}
and
e\in\mathsf{D}
.
Since the automorphisms
\varphi_{s(e),e}
and
\varphi_{e,t(e)}
are unitary, one can now check that
the maps
\delta_{\Phi}
and
\partial_{\Phi}
are adjoint of one another. In other words,
for all
f\in\Omega^{0}(\mathsf{G},W)
and
\alpha\in\Omega^{1}(\mathsf{G},W)
, there is an equality between

(f,\partial_{\Phi}\alpha)_{\Omega^{0}}=\sum_{v\in\mathsf{V}}\langle f(v),\partial_{\Phi}\alpha(v)\rangle=-\sum_{v\in\mathsf{V}}\sum_{e\in\mathsf{D}_{v}}\langle f(v),\varphi_{s(e),e}\alpha(e)\rangle=-\sum_{e\in\mathsf{D}}\langle f(s(e)),\varphi_{s(e),e}\alpha(e)\rangle

and

\displaystyle(\delta_{\Phi}f,\alpha)_{\Omega^{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e\in\mathsf{D}}\langle\delta_{\Phi}f(e),\alpha(e)\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e\in\mathsf{D}}\left(\langle\varphi_{e,t(e)}f(t(e)),\alpha(e)\rangle-\langle\varphi_{s(e),e}^{-1}f(s(e)),\alpha(e)\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e\in\mathsf{D}}\left(-\langle f(s(\overline{e})),\varphi_{s(\overline{e}),\overline{e}}\alpha(\overline{e})\rangle-\langle f(s(e)),\varphi_{s(e),e}\alpha(e)\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{e\in\mathsf{D}}\langle f(s(e)),\varphi_{s(e),e}\alpha(e)\rangle\,.

Finally, we let
{\rm diag}_{\sf x}
denote the block-scalar map on
\Omega^{1}(\mathsf{G},W)
equal to
{\mathsf{x}}_{e}
on the block
W
indexed by the edge
e
. For each
f\in\Omega^{0}(\mathsf{G},W)
and
v\in\mathsf{V}
, we now have

\displaystyle(\partial_{\Phi}\circ{\rm diag}_{\mathsf{x}}\circ\delta_{\Phi})(f)(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathsf{D}_{v}}\mathsf{x}_{e}\varphi_{s(e),e}\left(\varphi_{s(e),e}^{-1}f\left(s(e)\right)-\varphi_{e,t(e)}f\left(t(e)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathsf{D}_{v}}{\mathsf{x}}_{e}\left[f\left(s(e)\right)-\varphi_{e}f\left(t(e)\right)\right]=\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}^{\Phi}(f)(v)\,.

In view of this factorisation
\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}^{\Phi}=\partial_{\Phi}\circ{\rm diag}_{\mathsf{x}}\circ\delta_{\Phi}
,
we can now compute
\det\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}^{\rho}=\det\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}^{\Phi}
by an application of the Cauchy–Binet formula. For this purpose, we identify linear maps with matrices in orthonormal bases of
\Omega^{0}(\mathsf{G},W)
and
\Omega^{1}(\mathsf{G},W)
induced by concatenating copies of a given orthonormal basis of
W
.

For a matrix
M
, we will denote by
M^{I}_{J}
the sub-matrix of
M
indexed by rows
I
and columns
J
, and we omit
I
or
J
in this notation when it is equal to the full set of indices.
With a slight abuse of notation, we denote by
\delta
the matrix of
\delta_{\Phi}
and by
\partial
the matrix of
\partial_{\Phi}
with respect to the above chosen orthonormal bases.
Setting
d=\dim W
,
the Cauchy–Binet formula yields

\det\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}^{\Phi}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subset\mathsf{E}\times\{1,\ldots,d\}\\
|J|=d|\mathsf{V}|\end{subarray}}\det\partial_{J}\,\det({\rm diag}_{\sf x})_{J}^{J}\,\det\delta^{J}\,.

Since
\det({\rm diag}_{\sf x})^{J}_{J}=\prod_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}^{n_{\mathsf{e}}(J)}
with
n_{\mathsf{e}}(J)=|J\cap\{\mathsf{e}\}\times\{1,\ldots,d\}|
, we find that the coefficient of an arbitrary monomial
\prod_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{e}}^{n_{\mathsf{e}}}

in the above expansion is equal to

(3)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subset\mathsf{E}\times\{1,\ldots,d\},\;|J|=d|\mathsf{V}|\\
n_{\mathsf{e}}(J)=n_{\mathsf{e}},\forall\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}\end{subarray}}\\
\det\partial_{J}\,\det\delta^{J}\;=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subset\mathsf{E}\times\{1,\ldots,d\},\;|J|=d|\mathsf{V}|\\
n_{\mathsf{e}}(J)=n_{\mathsf{e}},\forall\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}\end{subarray}}\left|\det\partial_{J}\right|^{2}\,,

using the fact that the maps
\delta_{\Phi}
and
\partial_{\Phi}
are adjoint.
This shows that this coefficient is non-negative, and concludes the proof of the first assertion.

Let us now prove the second one. Let
(n_{\mathsf{e}})_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}
be a tuple of non-negative integers which corresponds to one of the monomials of our polynomial
\det\Delta_{\mathsf{G}}^{\Phi}
. Since the coefficient of this monomial is given by the right-hand side of (
3
) and is non-zero, there exists
J\subset\mathsf{E}\times\{1,\ldots,d\}
satisfying
|J|=d|\mathsf{V}|
and
n_{\mathsf{e}}(J)=n_{\mathsf{e}},\forall\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}
, such that

(4)

\left|\det\partial_{J}\right|^{2}\neq 0\,.

Let us write
\partial=(\partial^{i}_{j})_{i\in\mathsf{V}\times\{1,\ldots,d\},j\in\mathsf{E}\times\{1,\ldots,d\}}
. By (
4
), and using the classical expansion of the determinant as a sum over permutations, there exists a bijection
\sigma:\mathsf{V}\times\{1,\ldots,d\}\to J
such that

(5)

\prod_{i\in\mathsf{V}\times\{1,\ldots,d\}}\partial^{i}_{\sigma(i)}\neq 0\,.

Fix
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. For each
v\in\mathsf{V}
, set
i=(v,k)
and let
\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}
and
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
be defined by
\sigma(i)=(\mathsf{e},\ell)
. Since
\partial^{i}_{\sigma(i)}\neq 0
by (
5
), we see that
v
must be one of the boundary-vertices of
\mathsf{e}
. Let
e\in\mathsf{D}_{v}
be the oriented version of
\mathsf{e}
for which
v=s(e)
and set
\gamma_{k}(v)=e
. (If
\mathsf{e}
is a self-loop, simply pick an arbitrary orientation.)
By definition, the collection
\left(\gamma_{k}(v)\right)_{v\in\mathsf{V}}\in\prod_{v\in\mathsf{V}}\mathsf{D}_{v}
defines an element
\gamma_{k}
of
\mathcal{V}(\mathsf{G})
. Furthermore, by construction and since
\sigma
is a bijection, for each edge
\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}
, whose oriented versions we denote by
\{e,\overline{e}\}
, we have

\displaystyle n_{\mathsf{e}}

\displaystyle=n_{\mathsf{e}}(J)=\Big|\sigma^{-1}\left(\{\mathsf{e}\}\times\{1,\ldots,d\}\right)\Big|

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{v\in\mathsf{V}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sigma(v,k)\in\{\mathsf{e}\}\times\{1,\ldots,d\}\}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{e\in\gamma_{k}\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\overline{e}\in\gamma_{k}\}}\right)\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

We note that the assertion proved in the second part of the proof of Theorem
A.1
can also be obtained as an easy corollary of
[
23
, Theorems 5.1]
. A slightly stronger version of that assertion appears in
[
24
]
as well.

As a final remark, note that our method of proof adapts partly to the setup of digraphs. However, it fails, without further input, to prove the full Conjecture 5.5 of
[
9
]
. Indeed, our argument to show the non-negativity of
f
relies on a symmetry property which does not hold for general digraphs.
