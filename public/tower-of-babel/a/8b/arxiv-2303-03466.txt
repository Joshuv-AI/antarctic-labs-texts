F-Polynomials of Donaldson-Thomas Transformations
Daping Weng

Abstract
. F F -polynomials are integer coefficient polynomials encoding the mutations of cluster variables inside a cluster algebra. In this article, we study the F F -polynomials associated with the action of Donaldson-Thomas transformations on cluster variables. For acyclic quivers, quivers of surface types, and quivers associated with triples of flags, we give explicit descriptions of their Donaldson-Thomas F F -polynomials in terms of generating functions for ideals inside a labeled poset. We also describe the combinatorial procedure needed to modify these labeled posets to obtain Donaldson-Thomas F F -polynomials for full subquivers and triangular extensions.



1.
Introduction

Cluster algebra, introduced by Fomin and Zelevinsky
[
FZ02
]
, is a special family of commutative algebras whose generators and relations are given by a certain recursive procedure called cluster mutations. The combinatorics of a cluster algebra is captured by a family of quivers, which are directed graphs without oriented
1
-cycles and
2
-cycles, related by quiver mutations. Based on the family of mutation equivalent quivers, cluster algebras can be classified into smaller families, such as
finite type
(admitting a Dynkin type quiver),
acyclic type
(admitting a quiver with no oriented cycles),
surface type
(admitting a quiver that comes from a triangulation of a decorated surface),
finite mutation type
(admitting finitely many mutation equivalent quivers up to isomorphisms), and
locally acyclic type
(admitting localizations to acyclic cluster algebras).

Kontsevich and Soibelman
[
KS08
]
associate a 3d Calabi-Yau category with stability conditions with a quiver (with potential) and construct a Donaldson-Thomas invariant of such categories. This Donaldson-Thomas invariant gives rise to a unique formal automorphism, called the
Donaldson-Thomas transformation
, on the corresponding cluster algebra. Keller
[
Kel17
]
characterizes the DT transformation combinatorially by a reddening mutation sequence on the quiver. Using combinatorial methods, reddening mutation sequences have been constructed for finite type and acyclic quviers
[
BDP14
]
, plabic graph quivers
[
FS18
]
, a subfamily of finite mutation type quivers
[
Mil17
]
, and a subfamily of locally acyclic quivers called Banff quivers
[
BM20
]
. On the other hand, based on Keller’s characterization, the DT transformations of many cluster algebras of geometric origins have been constructed geometrically on moduli spaces of
G
-local systems
[
GS18
,
GS19
]
, Grassmannians
[
Wen21
]
, double Bott-Samelson cells
[
Wen20
,
SW19
]
, and braid varieties
[
CW22
,
CGG+22
]
.

The special generators of a cluster algebra produced by cluster mutations are called
cluster variables
, and they are grouped into overlapping subsets of the same size called
clusters
. One important property of cluster algebras is the
Laurent phenomenon

[
FZ02
]
, which states that given a fixed cluster (also known as an
initial cluster
), one can write any other cluster variable as a Laurent polynomial in terms of the initial cluster variables. Moreover, according to the separation formula of Fomin and Zelevinsky
[
FZ07
]
, this Laurent polynomial can be further arranged as a product of a Laurent monomial, which is encoded by an integer tuple called the
G
-vector
, and another Laurent polynomial that can be obtained from a polynomial
F
via substitution of variables (Theorem
3.5
). The polynomial
F
is called an
F
-polynomial
. Lee and Schiffler
[
LS15
]
prove that
F
-polynomials have positive integer coefficients. Gross, Hacking, Keel, and Kontsevich
[
GHKK18
]
give a geometric interpretation of
F
-polynomials as counting certain tropical curves called
broken lines
, which implies not only the positivity result on coefficients but also the fact that
F
-polynomials always have a constant term
1
. However, due to the level of complexity in constructing broken lines,
F
-polynomials are generally poorly understood.

The DT transformation acts on a cluster algebra by permuting its clusters and mapping cluster variables to cluster variables. Thus, one can apply the separation formula to
\mathrm{DT}(A_{i})
for any initial cluster variable
A_{i}
and extract a
G
-vector
g_{i}
and an
F
-polynomial
F_{i}
(see Equation (
4
)). It follows from tropical cluster duality
[
NZ12
]
that the
G
-vector
g_{i}
is always a negative basis vector; thus, all the information of the action of
\mathrm{DT}
is contained in the
F
-polynomial
F_{i}
. In this article, we focus on some special families of quivers and cluster variables and relate their
F
-polynomials of DT transformations to certain generating functions, which we call
ideal functions
, of ideals in a labeled poset (Definitions
2.1

2.2
,
2.3
). Below is a summary of our main results.

2.
Ideal Functions from Labeled Posets

A
poset
(short for “partially ordered set”) is a finite set
P
with a partial order
\leq
. A poset is said to be
pointed
if there is a unique minimal element in
P
.

Definition 2.1
.

An
ideal
of a poset
P
is a subset
I\subset P
satisfying

(i\in I\text{ and }j\leq i)\implies(j\in I).

Note that we allow ideals to be empty. Given a collection of elements in
P
, the ideal
generated
by these elements is the intersection of all ideals containing these elements. We denote the set of ideals in a poset
P
by
\mathcal{I}(P)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a set of formal commuting variables, and let
\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{X})
be the field of rational functions with rational coefficients in the variables in
\mathbf{X}
.

Definition 2.2
.

An
\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{X})
-labeling
, or just
labeling
, of a poset
P
is a map
L:P\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{X})
. We do not assume this map to be injective. We call the pair
(P,L)
a
labeled poset
. If the image of the labeling
L
lies inside the subset
\mathbf{X}\subset\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{X})
, then we say
L
is
simple
. A simple poset labeled by a simple labeling is called a
simply-labeled poset
. Note that when the labeling
L
is simple, the ideal function
F_{(P,L)}
naturally lives inside
\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{X}]
with positive coefficients. To simplify the notation, we define
L_{i}:=L(i)
for any labeling
L
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
(P,L)
be a labeled poset. The
ideal function
associated with this labeled poset is defined to be the function

F_{(P,L)}:=\sum_{I\subset\mathcal{I}(P)}L_{I},\quad\text{where}\quad L_{I}:=\prod_{i\in I}L_{i}.

We view
F_{(P,L)}
as an element in
\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{X})
. In particular, the empty ideal always contributes a constant term
1
to the ideal function.

To better visualize ideal functions, we can make use of Hasse diagrams. The
Hasse diagram
of a poset
P
is a quiver whose vertex set is
P
, with an arrow from
i
to
j
whenever
i>j
and there is no
k
such that
i>k>j
. Note that Hasse diagrams are automatically acyclic quivers. Moreover, we can draw a Hasse diagram so that the arrows are always oriented downward. Now given an additional
\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{X})
-labeling
L:P\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{X})
, we can replace each vertex
i
in the Hasse diagram with its image
L_{i}
. To write the ideal function, we just need to read from bottom up along the Hasse diagram to find all the ideals in
P
, and each ideal is going to contribute a term in the ideal function. We adopt the convention that if we represent a labeled poset with a Hasse diagram, then its ideal function is denoted by adding a square bracket around the Hasse diagram.

Let us prove some basic lemmas about ideal functions.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
(P,L)
be a labeled poset and suppose
P
is pointed. Let
P^{\prime}
be obtained from
P
by removing the unique minimal element
i_{\min}
. Let
(P^{\prime},L^{\prime})
be the labeled poset obtained by restricting the partial order and the labeling to
P^{\prime}
. Then

F_{(P,L)}=1+L_{i_{\min}}F_{(P^{\prime},L^{\prime})}.

Proof.

It follows from the fact that any non-empty ideal in
P
contains the minimal element
i_{\min}
.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
(P,L)
be a labeled poset. Let
j<i
be a pair of vertices in
P
. Let
P^{\prime}:=P\sqcup\{k\}
and we extend the partial order to
P^{\prime}
by declaring
j<k<i
. We choose an invertible element
L_{0}\in\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{X})
such that
1+L_{0}
is also invertible, and define a new labeling
L^{\prime}
on
P^{\prime}
by

L^{\prime}_{s}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}L_{j}(1+L_{0})^{-1}&\text{if $s=j$},\\
L_{0}&\text{if $s=k$},\\
L_{i}(1+L_{0}^{-1})&\text{if $s=i$},\\
L_{s}&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Then

F_{(P^{\prime},L^{\prime})}=F_{(P,L)}.

Proof.

The ideals in
P
belong to three mutually exclusive families.

For those ideals
I
in
P
that do not contain
j
, they are also ideals in
P^{\prime}
. It follows from the construction of
P^{\prime}
that
L_{I}=L^{\prime}_{I}
for these ideals.

For those ideals
I
in
P
that contains
j
but not
i
, each of them corresponds to two ideals in
P^{\prime}
, namely
I
and
I\sqcup\{k\}
. By construction, we have

L^{\prime}_{I}+L^{\prime}_{I\sqcup\{k\}}=L_{I}(1+L_{0})^{-1}+L_{I}(1+L_{0})^{-1}L_{0}=L_{I}.

For any ideal
I
in
P
that contains both
i
and
j
,
I\sqcup\{k\}
is an ideal in
P^{\prime}
, and we have

L^{\prime}_{I\sqcup\{k\}}=L_{I}(1+L_{0})^{-1}L_{0}(1+L_{0}^{-1})=L_{I}.

Note that these three cases exhaust all ideals in
P^{\prime}
as well. This implies
F_{(P^{\prime},L^{\prime})}=F_{(P,L)}
.
∎

Note that Lemma
2.6
can be used in the reverse direction as well. If
i\rightarrow k\rightarrow j
are arrows in a Hasse diagram
H
of a labeled poset, with
k
not connecting to any other vertex, then we can remove
k
from the poset
P
and relabel the vertices
i
and
j
without changing the ideal function.

If the labeling map
L
labels some poset elements as
0
, then we can further simplify the labeled poset without changing the ideal function.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
P
be a poset. The
opposite poset

P^{\mathrm{op}}
is the poset with the same underlying set as
P
but with the opposite order. An ideal in
P^{\mathrm{op}}
is called an
anti-ideal
in
P
. Given a collection of elements in
P
, the anti-ideal
generated
by these elements is the same as the ideal generated by the same collection of elements in
P^{\mathrm{op}}
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Suppose
(P,L)
is a labeled poset and suppose
S\subset P
such that
L|_{S}
is the constant zero map. Let
P_{S}
be the complement to the anti-ideal generated by
S
and let
L_{S}:=L|_{P_{S}}
. Then

F_{(P,L)}=F_{(P_{S},L_{S})}.

Proof.

Let
A_{S}
be the anti-ideal generated by
S
. If an ideal
I
in
P
intersects
A_{S}
non-trivially, then
I
must contain some element
i\in S
. Since
L|_{S}=0
, this implies that
L_{I}=0
. Thus, the only non-zero terms in
F_{(P,L)}
correspond to ideals
I
that are disjoint from
A_{S}
. But they are the same as ideals in
P_{S}
. Therefore we can conclude that
F_{(P,L)}=F_{(P_{S},L_{S})}
.
∎

3.
Overview of Quivers and Cluster Algebras

Within this article, we always assume quivers do not have oriented
1
-cycles or oriented
2
-cycles. We denote the set of vertices of
Q
by
I
and record the arrows in
Q
by its
exchange matrix

\epsilon
, an
I\times I
skew-symmetric matrix whose entries are

\epsilon_{ij}:=\#(i\rightarrow j)-\#(j\rightarrow i).

Given a vertex
k
of
Q
, we can
mutate

Q
in the direction of
k
to get a new quiver
Q^{\prime}
with the same set of vertices, but with its exchange matrix changed to

\epsilon^{\prime}_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\epsilon_{ij}&\text{if $k\in\{i,j\}$},\\
\epsilon_{ij}+[\epsilon_{ik}]_{+}[\epsilon_{kj}]_{+}-[-\epsilon_{ik}]_{+}[-\epsilon_{kj}]_{+}&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Here
[a]_{+}:=\max\{a,0\}
. Quivers obtained from
Q
via a sequence of mutations are said to be
mutation equivalent
to
Q
. Mutation equivalence is an equivalence relation, and we denote it by
\sim
.

The quiver with
principal coefficient
associated with
Q
is a quiver
\tilde{Q}
constructed from
Q
by adding a single frozen vertex
i^{\prime}
for each vertex
i
in
Q
, together with a single arrow pointing from
i
to
i^{\prime}
. Note that we are not allowed to mutate at frozen vertices. The exchange matrix of
\tilde{Q}
is hence of the form
\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon&\mathrm{id}\\
-\mathrm{id}&0\end{pmatrix}
. Now for a given sequence of quiver vertices
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{l})
of
Q
, we can mutate the quiver
\tilde{Q}
along this sequence and obtain another quiver
\tilde{Q}^{\prime}=\mu_{\mathbf{k}}(\tilde{Q})=\mu_{k_{l}}\circ\mu_{k_{l-1}}\circ\cdots\circ\mu_{k_{1}}(\tilde{Q})
. The exchange matrix of
\tilde{Q}^{\prime}
is of the form
\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon^{\prime}&c\\
-c&\ast\end{pmatrix}
, where
\epsilon^{\prime}
is the exchange matrix of the quiver
\mu_{\mathbf{k}}(Q)
. The
I\times I
matrix
c
is called the
C
-matrix
associated with
Q
and the mutation sequence
\mathbf{k}
. The row vectors of the
C
-matrix are called
C
-vectors
. In particular, the matrix
c
satisfies an exceptional sign coherence property; this property was conjectured by Fomin and Zelevinsky in
[
FZ07
]
, proved in the skew-symmetric case by Derksen, Weyman, and Zelevinsky and in the full generality by Gross, Hacking, Keel, and Kontsevich.

Theorem 3.1

(Sign coherence
[
DWZ10
,
GHKK18
]
)
.

For each
C
-vector
c_{i}
, either all entries in that row vector are non-negative or all entries in that row vector are non-positive.

Using this exceptional property, Keller
[
Kel17
]
defines a coloring on vertices of any quiver mutation equivalent to an initial quiver
Q
; based on this coloring scheme, he further defines special mutation sequences called “reddening sequences” and “maximal green sequences”.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
Q
be a quiver and let
\mathbf{k}
be a mutation sequence. Let
c
be the
C
-matrix associated with the quiver
Q
and the mutation sequence
\mathbf{k}
. A vertex
i
in
\mu_{\mathbf{k}}(Q)
is
green
if all entries in
c_{i}
are non-negative, and is
red
if all entries in
c_{i}
are non-positive. Note that by Theorem
3.1
any vertex in
\mu_{\mathbf{k}}(Q)
is either green or red.

Definition 3.3
.

A mutation sequence
\mu_{\mathbf{k}}
on an initial quiver
Q
is said to be
reddening
if all vertices in
\mu_{\mathbf{k}}(Q)
are red. A reddening sequence is said to be
maximal green
if it only mutates at green vertices at each step.

To each family of mutation equivalent quivers, Fomin and Zelevinsky
[
FZ02
]
introduce a commutative algebra called the “cluster algebra”. To construct the cluster algebra with an initial quiver
Q
, we first associate a formal variable
A_{i}
with each vertex
i
of
Q
. Let
\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{A})
be the field of rational functions in the set of formal variables
\mathbf{A}=\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
. Now for any mutation sequence
\mu_{\mathbf{k}}
, we will follow a recursive procedure to produce new elements in
\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{A})
. Suppose the elements we obtain for
Q^{\prime}=\mu_{k_{l-1}}\circ\cdots\circ\mu_{k_{1}}(Q)
are
\{A^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, and the exchange matrix of
Q^{\prime}
is
\epsilon^{\prime}
; then for
Q^{\prime\prime}=\mu_{k_{l}}(Q^{\prime})
, we produce a new collection by the following
mutation formula
:

(1)

A^{\prime\prime}_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{1}{A^{\prime}_{k}}\left(\prod_{j}A^{\prime[\epsilon^{\prime}_{kj}]_{+}}_{j}+\prod_{j}A^{\prime[-\epsilon^{\prime}_{kj}]_{+}}_{j}\right)&\text{if $i=k$},\\
A^{\prime}_{i}&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Such a collection of elements is called a
cluster
and elements inside a cluster are called
cluster variables
.

Definition 3.4

(
[
FZ02
, Definition 2.3]
)
.

The cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}
associated with (the family of quivers mutation equivalent to) the quiver
Q
is the subalgebra inside
\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{A})
generated by all cluster variables.

Fock and Goncharov
[
FG09
]
introduce a dual version of cluster algebras called the
cluster Poisson algebra
. The construction of a cluster Poisson algebra is only slightly different from that of cluster algebra. We again start with a collection of formal variables
\mathbf{X}=\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, and we mutate them along mutation sequences to produce a new collection of elements in
\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{X})
for each quiver in the mutation equivalent family. In contrast to the mutation of cluster variables (
1
), the cluster Poisson variables mutate according to
[
FG09
, Equation (13)]
,
[
FZ07
, Proposition 3.9]
:

(2)

X^{\prime\prime}_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}X^{\prime-1}_{k}&\text{if $i=k$},\\
X^{\prime}_{i}\left(1+X^{\prime-\mathrm{sign}(\epsilon^{\prime}_{ik})}_{k}\right)^{-\epsilon^{\prime}_{ik}}&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

For each quiver
Q^{\prime}\sim Q
, we can take the Laurent polynomial ring
\mathbb{Q}[X^{\prime\pm 1}_{i}]_{i\in I}
. The
cluster Poisson algebra
is then defined to be the intersection

\mathcal{X}:=\bigcap_{Q^{\prime}\sim Q}\mathbb{Q}[X^{\prime\pm 1}_{i}]_{i\in I}.

Note that, unlike cluster algebras, cluster Poisson variables may not be elements inside the cluster Poisson algebra.

For any quiver
Q^{\prime}\sim Q
with an exchange matrix
\epsilon^{\prime}
, we have a map
p:\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{A})
defined by

(3)

p(X^{\prime}_{i})=\prod_{j\in I}A^{\prime\epsilon^{\prime}_{ij}}_{j},

where
A^{\prime}_{j}
’s and
X_{i}
’s are variables associated with the quiver
Q^{\prime}
. By comparing the mutation formulas (
1
) and (
2
), it is not hard to see that the
p
maps commute with mutations and hence there is a unique
p
map
p:\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{A})
regardless of which quiver we use.

Let
Q^{\prime}=\mu_{\mathbf{k}}(Q)
be a quiver mutation equivalent to
Q
. Let
A^{\prime}_{i}
be cluster variables and let
X^{\prime}_{i}
be cluster Poisson variables associated with
Q^{\prime}
. Then
A^{\prime}_{i}
and
X^{\prime}_{i}
can be written in terms of the initial cluster variables and the initial cluster Poisson variables (those associated with
Q
) as follows.

Theorem 3.5

(Separation Formula
[
FZ07
, Proposition 3.13, Corollary 6.3]
)
.

There exists a collection of integers
g_{ij}
and polynomials
F_{A^{\prime}_{i}}(\mathbf{X})
in the initial cluster Poisson variables
\mathbf{X}
with integer coefficients such that

A^{\prime}_{i}=\left(\prod_{j}A_{j}^{g_{ij}}\right)p(F_{A^{\prime}_{i}}(\mathbf{X}))\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad X^{\prime}_{i}=\prod_{j}\left(X_{j}^{c_{ij}}F_{A^{\prime}_{j}}(\mathbf{X})^{\epsilon^{\prime}_{ij}}\right).

The integers
c_{ij}
are entries of the corresponding
C
-matrix.

Definition 3.6
.

The integers
g_{ij}
form a matrix
g
called the
G
-matrix
associated with the mutation sequence
\mu_{\mathbf{k}}
on the initial quiver
Q
. The row vectors of the
G
-matrix are called
G
-vectors
. The polynomial
F_{A^{\prime}_{i}}
is called the
F
-polynomial
associated with the cluster variable
A^{\prime}_{i}
with respect to the initial quiver
Q
.

F
-polynomials have many interesting properties. Below are a couple of them.

Theorem 3.7

(Positivity
[
LS15
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

F
-polynomials have positive integer coefficients.

Theorem 3.8

(Constant Term
[
GHKK18
, Corollary 5.5]
)
.

The constant term of an
F
-polynomial is
1
.

On the other hand, the
G
-matrix is closely related to its corresponding
C
-matrix via the following theorem.

Theorem 3.9

(Tropical cluster duality
[
NZ12
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

g^{t}=c^{-1}
. As a corollary, the
G
-matrix
g
also enjoys a sign coherence along its column vectors.

Recall that two quivers
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
are said to be
isomorphic
if there is a bijection
\sigma:I\rightarrow I^{\prime}
such that
\epsilon^{\prime}_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}=\epsilon_{ij}
. If
\mu_{\mathbf{k}}
is a mutation sequence on an initial quiver
Q
such that
Q^{\prime}=\mu_{\mathbf{k}}(Q)
is isomorphic to
Q
via
\sigma
, then
\sigma
induces an automorphism on the cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}
and an automorphism on the cluster Poisson algebra
\mathcal{X}
. By abuse of notation, we also denote these automorphisms by
\sigma
, and it is defined on the cluster variables and cluster Poisson variables of
Q^{\prime}
and
Q
by

\sigma(A_{i})=A^{\prime}_{\sigma(i)}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\sigma(X_{i})=X^{\prime}_{\sigma(i)}.

Automorphisms defined by isomorphisms between mutation equivalent quivers are called
cluster transformations
.

Theorem 3.10

(
[
GS18
, Theorem 3.4]
)
.

If
\mu_{\mathbf{k}}
is a reddening sequence on an initial quiver
Q
, then
\mu_{\mathbf{k}}(Q)
is isomorphic to
Q
. In particular, there is a unique isomorphism
\sigma
such that
c_{i\sigma(j)}=-\delta_{ij}
.

Definition 3.11
.

The cluster transformation defined by a reddening sequence on
Q
(together with the unique isomorphism
\sigma
) is called a
cluster Donaldson-Thomas transformation
; we denote cluster Donaldson-Thomas transformation by
\mathrm{DT}
.

Theorem 3.12

(
[
GS18
, Corollary 3.7]
)
.

A cluster DT transformation is unique if it exists. In particular,
\mathrm{DT}
commutes with cluster mutations and does not depend on the choice of an initial quiver.

Since cluster DT transformations map cluster variables to cluster variables, we can apply the separation formula (Theorem
3.5
) to the cluster variable
\mathrm{DT}(A_{i})
and the cluster Poisson variable
\mathrm{DT}(X_{i})
. Moreover, because of the tropical cluster duality (Theorem
3.9
), we know that the integers
g_{ij}=-\delta_{ij}
; therefore we have

(4)

\mathrm{DT}(A_{i})=A_{i}^{-1}p(F_{i}(\mathbf{X}))\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\mathrm{DT}(X_{i})=X_{i}^{-1}\prod_{j}F_{j}(\mathbf{X})^{\epsilon_{ij}}.

Note that since
\mathrm{DT}
is unique, the
F
-polynomials
F_{i}
do not depend on the choice of reddening sequence. In conclusion, for any quiver
Q
with cluster DT transformations (i.e., reddening sequences), there is a unique collection of
F
-polynomials for the cluster DT transformation, and conversely, the information of the cluster DT transformation is completely determined by its
F
-polynomials with respect to an initial quiver
Q
.

4.
Properties of
F
-Polynomials for DT Transformations

Similar to cluster variables, the
F
-polynomials themselves also have a mutation formula. The
F
-polynomial associated with every initial cluster variable is the constant polynomial
1
. Now suppose we have obtained the collection of
F
polynomials
\{F^{\prime}_{i}\}
for a certain quiver
Q^{\prime}
with exchange matrix
\epsilon^{\prime}
and
C
-matrix
c^{\prime}
. Then mutating again in the direction
k
keeps all
F
-polynomial
F^{\prime}_{i}
with
i\neq k
unchanged, and changes
F^{\prime}_{k}
to
[
FZ07
, Proposition 5.1]
:

(5)

F^{\prime\prime}_{k}=\mu_{k}(F^{\prime}_{k})=\frac{1}{F^{\prime}_{k}}\left(\prod_{j}X_{j}^{[c^{\prime}_{kj}]_{+}}F^{\prime[\epsilon^{\prime}_{kj}]_{+}}_{j}+\prod_{j}X_{j}^{[-c^{\prime}_{kj}]_{+}}F^{\prime[-\epsilon^{\prime}_{kj}]_{+}}_{j}\right).

Note that by sign coherence (Theorem
3.1
), one and only one of
\prod_{j}X_{j}^{[c^{\prime}_{kj}]_{+}}
and
\prod_{j}X_{j}^{[-c^{\prime}_{kj}]_{+}}
is non-trivial.

Now let us focus on the
F
-polynomials for cluster DT transformations (
4
). Suppose
Q^{\prime}=\mu_{k}(Q)
. We may use
Q^{\prime}
instead of
Q
as an initial quiver, and there is a different collection of
F
-polynomials
\{F^{\prime}_{i}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime})\}_{i\in I}
for
\mathrm{DT}
with respect to
Q^{\prime}
as the initial quiver. Please be aware that the variables
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}=\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}
associated with
Q^{\prime}
are different from those associated with
Q
. By taking the inverse of the cluster Poisson mutation formula (
2
), we get the following relation:

(6)

X_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}X^{\prime-1}_{k}&\text{if $i=k$},\\
X^{\prime}_{i}\left(1+X^{\prime\mathrm{sign}(\epsilon_{ik})}_{k}\right)^{\epsilon_{ik}}&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Given an expression
F
(e.g. polynomial) in the
\mathbf{X}
variables, we can use Equation
6
to rewrite it in terms of the
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
variables. We denote the resulting expression by
\widehat{F}
.

Proposition 4.1

(Relating
F
-polynomials of DT for adjacent clusters)
.

Suppose
Q^{\prime}=\mu_{k}(Q)
. The
F
-polynomails
\{F^{\prime}_{i}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime})\}_{i\in I}
of
\mathrm{DT}
with respect to
Q
’ are related to the
F
-polynomials
\{F_{i}(\mathbf{X})\}_{i\in I}
of
\mathrm{DT}
with respect to
Q
by

F^{\prime}_{i}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(1+X^{\prime}_{k})\widehat{\mu_{k}(F_{k})}&\text{if $i=k$},\\
&\\
\widehat{F}_{i}&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

Suppose we first use
Q
as the initial quiver and let
\mu_{\mathbf{k}}
be a reddening sequence on
Q
. For simplicity, let us assume that the quiver isomorphism
\sigma
relating
Q
and
\mu_{\mathbf{k}}(Q)
is the identity map. Now after the reddening sequence
\mu_{\mathbf{k}}
, we can mutate one more time in the direction of
k
. The
F
-polynomials after this extra mutation are all identical to the
F
-polynomials of the
\mathrm{DT}
transformation except for
F_{k}
, which mutates according to (
5
).

Next, we would like to change the initial seed to
Q^{\prime}
. Note that for
i\neq k
, the initial cluster variable
A^{\prime}_{i}
is identical to the initial cluster variable
A_{i}
of
Q
. Therefore

\mathrm{DT}(A^{\prime}_{i})=\mathrm{DT}(A_{i})=A_{i}^{-1}p(F_{i})=A^{\prime-1}_{i}p(F_{i}),

and hence the
F
-polynomial
F^{\prime}_{i}
is just
F_{i}
after a substitution according to (
6
), i.e.,
\widehat{F}_{i}
.

Now consider the cluster variable
A^{\prime}_{k}
. According to (
1
), and (
4
), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{DT}(A^{\prime}_{k})=

\displaystyle\frac{A_{k}}{p(F_{k})}\left(\prod_{j}A_{j}^{-[\epsilon_{kj}]_{+}}p(F_{j})^{[\epsilon_{kj}]_{+}}+\prod_{j}A_{j}^{-[-\epsilon_{kj}]_{+}}p(F_{j})^{[-\epsilon_{kj}]_{+}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{k}\left(\prod_{j}A_{j}^{-[-\epsilon_{kj}]_{+}}\right)\frac{1}{p(F_{k})}\left(\prod_{j}A_{j}^{-\epsilon_{kj}}p(F_{j})^{[\epsilon_{kj}]_{+}}+\prod_{j}p(F_{j})^{[-\epsilon_{kj}]_{+}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{k}\left(\prod_{j}A_{j}^{-[-\epsilon_{kj}]_{+}}\right)\frac{p(X_{k})^{-1}}{p(F_{k})}\left(\prod_{j}p(F_{j})^{[\epsilon_{kj}]_{+}}+p(X_{k})\prod_{j}p(F_{j})^{[-\epsilon_{kj}]_{+}}\right)

Note that since the
C
-matrix after
\mathrm{DT}
is
-\mathrm{id}
. By comparing with the mutation formula for
F
-polynomials (
5
), the last expression is in turn equal to

(7)

\mathrm{DT}(A^{\prime}_{k})=A_{k}\left(\prod_{j}A_{j}^{-[-\epsilon_{kj}]_{+}}\right)p(X_{k})^{-1}p(\mu_{k}(F_{k})).

On the other hand, from (
1
) we can also deduce that

A_{k}\prod_{j}A_{j}^{-[-\epsilon_{kj}]_{+}}=A^{\prime-1}_{k}\left(1+\prod_{j}A_{j}^{\epsilon_{kj}}\right)=A^{\prime-1}_{k}\left(1+p(X_{k})\right).

By plugging this into (
7
), we get

\mathrm{DT}(A^{\prime}_{k})=A^{\prime-1}_{k}\left(1+p(X_{k})^{-1}\right)p(\mu_{k}(F_{k}))=A^{\prime-1}_{k}p\left((1+X^{\prime}_{k})\widehat{\mu_{k}(F_{k})}\right).

From this we can conclude that the
F
-polynomial of
\mathrm{DT}
with respect to the initial quiver
Q^{\prime}
is
F^{\prime}_{k}=(1+X^{\prime}_{k})\widehat{\mu_{k}(F_{k})}
.
∎

Next, let us consider
F
-polynomials of cluster DT transformations on subquivers. Let
Q
be a quiver with vertex set
I
and exchange matrix
\epsilon
. A
full subquiver

Q^{\prime}
of
Q
is defined by a non-empty subset
I^{\prime}
of
I
with an exchange matrix
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon|_{I^{\prime}\times I^{\prime}}
. Regarding cluster DT transformations on subquivers, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
Mul16
, Theorem 1.4.1]
)
.

If
Q
admits a cluster DT transformation, then so does any full subquiver
Q^{\prime}
of
Q
.

However, it is in general not true that if
\mu_{\mathbf{k}}
is a reddening sequence on
Q
, then the restriction of
\mu_{\mathbf{k}}
to
Q^{\prime}
will also be a reddening sequence. Nevertheless, we can still relate the
F
-polynomials of
\mathrm{DT}
on a full subquiver
Q^{\prime}
to those of
\mathrm{DT}
on the whole quiver
Q
.

Proposition 4.3

(Relating
F
-polynoimials of
\mathrm{DT}
for full subquivers)
.

Let
Q^{\prime}
be a subquiver of
Q
defined by a subset
I^{\prime}\subset I
. Suppose the cluster DT transformation exists on
Q
. Then the
F
-polynomials of the DT transformation on
Q^{\prime}
can be obtained from those for
Q
with indices in
I^{\prime}
by substituting
X_{j}=0
for all
j\notin I^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The proof of this proposition is based on the idea of degeneration of scattering diagrams
[
BFMMNC20
]
. By following the construction in
[
GHKK18
]
, one can construct a scattering diagram from any quiver
Q
with a choice of functions attached to the initial walls. To obtain a degeneration of the scattering diagram, we choose to attach the function
1+tX_{j}
instead of
1+X_{j}
to the
j
th initial wall for each
j\notin I^{\prime}
. We denote the resulting consistent scattering diagram by
\mathfrak{D}_{t}
. Note that
\mathfrak{D}_{1}
is the same as the ordinary cluster scattering diagram
\mathfrak{D}_{Q}
for the quiver
Q
, and
\mathfrak{D}_{0}
is the product of the ordinary cluster scattering diagram
\mathfrak{D}_{Q^{\prime}}
with a lineality space
\mathbb{R}^{|I\setminus I^{\prime}|}
.

The
\mathrm{DT}
transformation corresponds to a path
\gamma
that goes from the positive chamber to the negative chamber of the scattering diagram
\mathfrak{D}_{t}
, and for each cluster variable
A_{i}
,
\mathrm{DT}_{t}(A_{i})
is equal to the outcome of the wall-crossing transformation of
A_{i}
along the path
\gamma
. With the extra parameter
t
,
\mathrm{DT}_{t}(A_{i})
is now a polynomial in
t
(it is a polynomial because
t
only appears along with the variables
X_{j}
for
j\notin I^{\prime}
inside the
F
-polynomial factor). Setting
t=1
recovers
\mathrm{DT}_{t=1}(A_{i})=\mathrm{DT}_{Q}(A_{i})
for each
i\in I
, which admits a separation formula in the form of (
4
). On the other hand, setting
t=0
only extracts the constant term (with respect to
t
) from
\mathrm{DT}_{t}(A_{i})
. We observe that the consistent scattering diagram
\mathfrak{D}_{t}
is the same as the consistent scattering diagram
\mathfrak{D}_{Q}
with each
X_{j}
substituted by
tX_{j}
for all
j\notin I
. Therefore
\mathrm{DT}_{t}(A_{i})
is the same as
\mathrm{DT}_{Q}(A_{i})
with
X_{j}
substituted by
tX_{j}
for all
j\notin I
. Thus, setting
t=0
is the same as setting
X_{j}=0
in the separation formula for
\mathrm{DT}_{Q}(A_{i})
, and hence we can conclude that for
i\in I
,
\mathrm{DT}_{Q^{\prime}}(A_{i})=A_{i}^{-1}p\left(F_{i}\big|_{X_{j}=0\text{ for all $j\notin I^{\prime}$}}\right)
.
∎

By combining Proposition
4.3
and Lemma
2.8
, we obtain the following corollary, which is useful when computing

-polynomials of DT transformations for full subquivers.

Corollary 4.4

(Truncation for full subquivers)
.

Let
Q^{\prime}
be a full subquiver of a quiver
Q
that admits a reddening sequence. Suppose
i
is a vertex in
Q^{\prime}
whose
F
-polynomial of
\mathrm{DT}_{Q}
is equal to the ideal function of a simply-labeled poset
F_{(P,L)}
. Then the
F
-polynomial of
\mathrm{DT}_{Q^{\prime}}
at
i
is equal to
F_{(P^{\prime},L^{\prime})}
, where
(P^{\prime},L^{\prime})
is a simply-labeled poset obtained from
(P,L)
by deleting the anti-ideal in
P
generated by elements labeled by
X_{j}
with
j
not in
Q^{\prime}
.

Lastly, we would like to consider triangular extensions of quivers. Let
Q_{1}
be a quiver with vertex set
I_{1}
and exchange matrix
\epsilon_{1}
, and let
Q_{2}
be a quiver with vertex set
I_{2}
and exchange matrix
\epsilon_{2}
. A
triangular extension
of
Q_{1}
by
Q_{2}
is a quiver
Q
with vertex set
I=I_{1}\sqcup I_{2}
and exchange matrix
\epsilon=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{1}&\delta\\
-\delta&\epsilon_{2}\end{pmatrix}
where all entries of
\delta
are non-negative. The following theorem describes a way to construct reddening sequences on the triangular extension
Q
from reddening sequences of
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
.

Theorem 4.5

(
[
CL19
, Theorem 4.5, Remark 4.6]
)
.

If
\mathbf{k}_{1}
is a maximal green (resp. reddening) sequence of
Q_{1}
and
\mathbf{k}_{2}
is a maximal green (resp. reddening) sequence of
Q_{2}
, then the concatenation
(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})
is a maximal green (resp. reddening) sequence of the triangular extension of
Q_{1}
by
Q_{2}
.

Based on this theorem, we can view the cluster DT transformation
\mathrm{DT}_{Q}
as the composition of those of its full subquivers
\mathrm{DT}_{Q_{2}}\circ\mathrm{DT}_{Q_{1}}
. Moreover, we prove the following result for
F
-polynomials of cluster DT transformations on triangular extensions.

Proposition 4.6
.

Suppose
Q
is a triangular extension of
Q_{1}
by
Q_{2}
. Let
\delta
be the matrix encoding the arrows from vertices of
Q_{1}
to vertices of
Q_{2}
. Suppose both
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
admit DT transformations, and let
F^{\prime}_{i}
be the
F
-polynomial of
\mathrm{DT}_{Q_{1}}
or
\mathrm{DT}_{Q_{2}}
at the vertex
i
(depending on whether
i
is in
Q_{1}
or
Q_{2}
). Then the
F
-polynomials of the DT transformation on
Q
are given by

F_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}F^{\prime}_{i}&\text{if $i$ is in $Q_{1}$},\\
F^{\prime}_{i}\big|_{X_{j}\rightsquigarrow X_{j}\prod_{k}F^{\prime\delta_{kj}}_{k}}&\text{if $i$ is in $Q_{2}$}.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{k}_{1}
be a reddening sequence of
Q_{1}
and let
\mathbf{k}_{2}
be a reddening sequence of
Q_{2}
. By Theorem
4.5
, the mutation sequence
(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})
is a reddening sequence for the triangular extension
Q
. Since
(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})
starts with
\mathbf{k}_{1}
and never mutates anywhere on
Q_{1}
after finishing
\mathbf{k}_{1}
, we know from the
F
-polynomial mutation formula (
5
) that
F_{i}=F^{\prime}_{i}
for all vertices
i
in
Q_{1}
.

On the other hand, if we view vertices of
Q_{2}
as frozen during the mutation sequence
\mathbf{k}_{1}
, then the arrows between
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
must change in the same way as the framing arrows in the quiver
\tilde{Q}_{1}
with principal coefficients. By the sign coherence theorem
3.1
, there will never be arrows created between
I_{2}
and the framing vertices in
\tilde{Q}_{1}
, and hence the
C
-vector of any vertex
j\in I_{2}
remains a standard basis vector. After finishing the mutation sequence
\mathbf{k}_{1}
, the arrows between
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
must be reversed such that there are
\delta_{kj}
many arrows pointing from a vertex
j\in I_{2}
to a vertex
k\in I_{1}
. Thus, according to Theorem
3.5
, at the moment right after the mutation sequence
\mathbf{k}_{1}
, the cluster Poisson variable
X^{\prime}_{j}
of a vertex
j\in I_{2}
is expressed in terms of the initial cluster Poisson variables as

(8)

X^{\prime}_{j}=X_{j}\prod_{k\in I_{1}}F^{\prime\delta_{kj}}_{k}.

In particular, since
\delta_{kj}\geq 0
, the right-hand side of (
8
) is always a polynomial. Finally, the remaining mutation sequence
\mathbf{k}_{2}
will give rise to
F
-polynomials
F^{\prime}_{j}
for each vertex
j\in I_{2}
in terms of these intermediate cluster Poisson variables, and to re-express them in terms of the initial cluster Poisson variables, we just need to substitute (
8
) into
F^{\prime}_{j}
’s, and that is what is precisely the formula claimed in this proposition.
∎

By combining Proposition
4.6
with Lemma
2.5
, we obtain the following corollary, which is useful when computing
F
-polynomials of DT transformations for triangular extensions.

Corollary 4.7
.

In addition to the assumptions in Propsition
4.6
, let us suppose that all
F
-polynomials of
\mathrm{DT}_{Q_{1}}
and
\mathrm{DT}_{Q_{2}}
are equal to ideal functions of simply-labeled pointed posets
(P(i),L(i))
. Then for any vertex
j
in
Q_{2}
, its
F
-polynomial of
\mathrm{DT}_{Q}
is equal to the ideal function of a simply-labeled pointed poset obtained by attaching
\delta_{ij}
many copies of the
(P(i),L(i))
to any Hasse diagram vertex
X_{j}
with an arrow from the lowest point of each
P(i)
to that vertex labeled by
X_{j}
.

5.
F
-Polynomials of DT on Acyclic Quivers

Definition 5.1
.

A quiver is said to be
acyclic
if there are no oriented cycles in the quiver. A vertex
i
in an acyclic quiver
Q
is said to be a
source
if
\epsilon_{ij}\geq 0
for all
j\in I
.

Let
Q
be an acyclic quiver. We define a simply-labeled poset
(P(i),L(i))
for each vertex
i
of
Q
as follows. The elements in
P(i)
are homotopic families of oriented paths in
Q
that end at
i
. We are using homotopy here because we would like to count the composition of a path
\gamma
from
j
to
i
with the idle path at
i
the same as
\gamma
itself. The partial order on
P(i)
is given by inclusion as subpaths up to homotopy. In particular, the idle path at
i
is considered a path as well, and it is the unique minimal element in
P(i)
. We call
P(i)
the
ascendant tree
of the vertex
i
. We label the ascendant tree
P(i)
by labeling each path
\gamma
from
j
to
i
by
X_{j}
.

Theorem 5.2
.

For each vertex
i
in an acyclic quiver
Q
, the
F
-polynomial of the cluster DT transformation on
Q
for the vertex
i
is equal to the ideal function of the simply-labeled pointed poset
(P(i),L(i))
, i.e.,

F_{i}=F_{(P(i),L(i))}.

Proof.

We will do an induction on the number of vertices of
Q
. If there is only one vertex, then the cluster DT transformation is given by a single mutation at that vertex, which gives an
F
-polynomial
F=1+X=[X]
. Now inductively, let
i
be a source of
Q
. The full subquiver
Q^{\prime}
with the vertex
i
removed is still acyclic, and the whole quiver can be viewed as a triangular extension of a singleton quiver
\{i\}
by
Q^{\prime}
. Now by Proposition
4.6
, the
F
-polynomial for the source
i
is just
F=1+X_{i}=[X_{i}]
, whereas the
F
-polynomial for any other vertex
j
can be obtained by taking
F_{(P^{\prime}(j),L^{\prime}(j))}
(which is the
F
-polynomial for
\mathrm{DT}_{Q^{\prime}}
by the inductive hypothesis) and then substituting any
X_{k}
in it by
X_{k}(1+X_{i})^{\epsilon_{ik}}
. But this is precisely attaching
\epsilon_{ik}
many branches on top of every vertex labeled by
X_{k}
in the Hasse diagram, and we see that the resulting substituted polynomial coincides with the ideal function
F_{(P(j),L(j))}
as claimed.
∎

6.

Let

be an oriented surface with a set
M
of marked points. We call the marked points in the interior of
S

punctures
and call the marked points along
\partial S

boundary marked points
. In particular, if
\partial S\neq\emptyset
, we require that there is at least one marked point on each connected component of
\partial S
Fomin, Shapiro, and Thurston
[
FST08
]
introduce the concept of “tagged triangulations”, generalizing ideal triangulations of surfaces.

Definition 6.1
.

On a surface with marked points
(S,M)
, an
arc
is a homotopy equivalence family of unoriented curves
\gamma
on
S
, connecting one point in
M
to another point (possibly the same point) in
M
, such that
\gamma
is not null-homotopic, and there is no self-intersection.

Definition 6.2
.

There are two possible decorations we can put on either end of an arc:
plain
or
notched
. A
tagged arc
is an arc with (possibly different) decorations at its two ends, such that

•

the arc does not bound a once-punctured monogon;

•

if an arc ends at a boundary marked point, then that end must be plain;

•

if the arc is a loop, then both ends are tagged the same way.

We denote a notched end of an arc by
, and denote a plain end of an arc by not decorating it.

Definition 6.3
.

Two distinct tagged arcs
\alpha
and
\beta
are said to be
compatible
if the followings are satisfied.

•

The untagged versions of
\alpha
and
\beta
are either homotopic or do not intersect in their interior (up to homotopy).

•

If the untagged versions of
\alpha
and
\beta
are not homotopic and they share an end point at
p
, then they have be tagged in the same way near
p
.

•

If the untagged versions of
\alpha
and
\beta
are homotopic, then one of their ends are decorated in the same way and the other end is decorated differently.

A maximal set of pairwise compatible tagged arcs is called a
tagged triangulation
.

Note that it follows from the definition that notched ends can only be present near a puncture, and if both decorations are present near a puncture, then there can only be two tagged arcs with homotopic supports and opposite decorations at that puncture.

Theorem 7.7
.

For any bounded face

Since the exchange matrix
\epsilon

is not necessarily invertible, we cannot directly conclude that

Instead, we need to make use of a concrete maximal green sequence on

•

If

•

For
n>3
, the quiver
Q_{n-1}
embeds as a full subquiver inside
Q_{n}
at the lower left hand corner. For each

, let
\mathbf{k}_{a}
be the mutation sequence
((n-a-3,a,0),(n-a-3,a-1,1),(n-a-3,a-2,2),\dots,(n-a-3,0,a))
. Then the composition
(\mathbf{k}_{0},\mathbf{k}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{k}_{n-3})
mutates only at green vertices and turns the right most quiver vertex in each row red. We then postcompose this mutation sequence by the maximal green sequence of
Q_{n-1}
inductively.

After applying this maximal green sequence, the resulting quiver is actually the mirror image of
Q_{n}
after flipping over the vertical line of symmetry. Thus, the cluster DT transformation is the composition of this maximal green sequence together with the quiver isomorphism of flipping the quiver horizontally.
