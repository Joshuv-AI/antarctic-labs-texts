Flavio Ascari, Roberto Bruni, Roberta Gori, Francesco Logozzo

Abstract
. Sound over-approximation methods have been proved effective for guaranteeing the absence of errors, but inevitably they produce false alarms that can hamper the programmers. Conversely, under-approximation methods are aimed at bug finding and are free from false alarms. We introduce Sufficient Incorrectness Logic (SIL), a new under-approximating, triple-based program logic to reason about program errors. SIL is designed to set apart the initial states leading to errors. We prove that SIL is correct and complete for a minimal set of rules, and we study additional rules that can facilitate program analyses. We formally compare SIL to existing triple-based program logics. Incorrectness Logic and SIL both perform under-approximations, but while the former exposes only true errors, the latter locates the set of initial states that lead to such errors. Hoare Logic performs over-approximations and as such cannot capture the set of initial states leading to errors in nondeterministic programs – for deterministic and terminating programs, Hoare Logic and SIL coincide. Finally, we instantiate SIL with Separation Logic formulae (Separation SIL) to handle pointers and dynamic allocation and we prove its correctness and, for loop-free programs, also its completeness. We argue that in some cases Separation SIL can yield more succinct postconditions and provide stronger guarantees than Incorrectness Separation Logic and can support effective backward reasoning.



1.
Introduction

Formal methods aim to provide tools for reasoning and establishing program guarantees.
Historically, research in formal reasoning progressed from manual correctness proofs to effective, automatic methods that improve program reliability and security.
In the late 60s,
Floyd 1967
and
Hoare 1969
independently introduced formal systems to reason about programs.
In the 70s/early 80s, the focus was on mechanization, with the introduction of numerous techniques such as predicate transformers
(
Dijkstra 1975
)
, Abstract Interpretation
(
Cousot and Cousot 1977
)
, model checking
(
Clarke and Emerson 1981
)
, type inference
(
Damas and Milner 1982
)
and mechanized program proofs
(
Coquand and Huet 1985
)
.
Those seminal works, in conjunction with the development of automatic and semi-automatic theorem provers (e.g.,
(
de Moura 2007
)
) brought impressive wins in proving program correctness of real-world applications.
For instance, the Astrée abstract interpreter automatically proves the absence of runtime errors in millions of lines of safety-critical C
(
Blanchet et al. 2003
)
, the SLAM model checker was used to check Windows drivers
(
Ball and Rajamani 2001
)
, CompCert is a certified C compiler developed in Coq
(
Leroy 2009
)
, and VCC uses the calculus of weakest precondition to verify safety properties of annotated Concurrent C programs
(
Cohen et al. 2009
)
.

Despite the aforementioned successes, effective program correctness methods struggle to reach mainstream adoption.
As program correctness is undecidable, all those methods
over-approximate
programs behaviours.
Over-approximation guarantees soundness (if the program is proved to be correct, no error will arise) but it may introduce many false positives.
False positives are seen as a distraction by professional programmers, who are skeptical of the adoption of tools with a high false positive ratio.
Tools such as Microsoft Prefix
(
Hackett et al. 2006
)
or Microsoft CodeContracts
(
Fähndrich and Logozzo 2010
)
aim at reducing the number of false positives by using human-provided annotations.
Nevertheless, in some scenarios, the annotation effort may be over-killing so that even those methods struggle to enjoy universal adoption.

To address the aforementioned issues, researchers have recently invested more efforts in effective
under-approximations
.
The overall goal is to develop
principled
techniques to assist programmers with timely feedback on their code about the presence of true errors, with few or zero false alarms.
For instance, Microsoft Sage combines SMT solving with program executions (which are an under-approximation of the program semantics) to find security bugs
(
Godefroid et al. 2012
)
and the Bounded Model Checking technique unrolls recursion up to a certain depth to find property violations
(
Biere et al. 2003
)
.

Recently,
O’Hearn 2020
introduced Incorrectness Logic (IL).
Intuitively, IL uses under-approximations to reduce the problem complexity by dropping disjunctions during the state space exploration.
IL is the theoretical foundation of many successful bug-catching tools
(
Raad et al. 2020
;
Raad et al. 2022
;
Distefano et al. 2019
;
Le et al. 2022
;
Carbonneaux et al. 2022
)
and the first Workshop on “Formal Methods for Incorrectness” affiliated with POPL’24 witnesses the interest around this new line of research.
IL triples resemble Floyd-Hoare logic ones, but they have a different meaning: any error state that satisfies the postcondition can be produced by some input states that satisfy the precondition. Since in this paper we deal with different kinds of program logics, a compact legenda of the different notation used in the literature for the various triples is reported in Fig.
1
.
It is worth noting that in IL nothing is said about
which input states are responsible for a given error
.
This is possibly rooted in the
forward
flavour of the under-approximation, which follows the ordinary direction of code execution.

1.1.
Backward under-approximation and Lisbon Triples

At POPL’19 in Lisbon, according to the origins of IL first narrated by
O’Hearn 2020
and further discussed in
Zilberstein et al. 2023a
, Derek Dreyer and Ralf Jung suggested Peter O’Hearn to look at bug-finding in terms of a logic for proving the presence of faults. However, the proposed model of triples did not fit well with a key feature of Pulse, a bug-catching tool developed mainly by Jules Villard at Facebook, namely its ability to drop the analysis of some program paths, for which IL provides a sound logical foundation instead. The idea of such ‘Lisbon’ triples is that
for any initial state satisfying the precondition, there exists some trace of execution leading to a final state satisfying the postcondition
and it can be dated back to Hoare’s calculus of possible correctness
(
Hoare 1978
)
, even if no form of approximation was considered there. This condition aims at finding input states causing bugs: if the postcondition denotes an error, any initial state satisfying the precondition exhibits at least one unwanted behaviour. Lisbon triples were then briefly accounted for in
Möller et al. 2021
and
Le et al. 2022
, under the name
backwards under-approximate triples
. Later, the idea of finding sources of error was one of the motivations leading to the more general Outcome Logic (OL)
(
Zilberstein et al. 2023a
)
, which is able to reason about multiple executions at the same time via the outcome conjunction
\oplus
.
Zilberstein et al. 2023a
encode Lisbon triples as OL triples matching the specific pattern
\langle{P}\rangle\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \langle{Q\oplus\top}\rangle
. OL can also exploit triples à la HL for partial correctness. In fact, OL has been designed to provide a single unified theory that can be used for both correctness and incorrectness reasoning.
A sound and complete proof system for Lisbon triples has been independently proposed in the recent preprint
(
Raad et al. 2024
)
, where they are combined with IL triples for non-termination analysis of programs.

Ideally, given an incorrectness specification, the goal of backward under-approximation would be to report their
weakest preconditions
to the programmer, for discerning all dangerous input states that lead to bugs.
However, all the above proposals exploit logic rules that are designed according to the forward semantics of programs and thus are best suited to infer postconditions starting from some given precondition: in general, their backward analysis may require some ingenious guess of the right predicates to instantiate the consequence rule with.
Here, by exploiting backward program semantics, we design a minimal, sound and complete proof system for Lisbon triples that is aimed at inferring weakest preconditions.

In this paper, we introduce Sufficient Incorrectness Logic (SIL), a backward-oriented under-approximation proof system that focuses on
finding the sources of incorrectness rather than just highlighting the presence of bugs
.
SIL formalizes exactly Lisbon triples: a valid SIL triple

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}P\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

can be interpreted as “
all input states that satisfy
P
have at least one execution of the program
\mathsf{r}
leading to a state that satisfies
Q
".
In the absence of nondeterminism, SIL guarantees that a state satisfying the precondition
always
leads to an error.
In the presence of nondeterminism, SIL guarantees that there
exists
an execution that leads to an error.
Sufficient incorrectness preconditions are extremely valuable to programmers, because, by pointing out the sources of errors, they serve as starting point to scope down debugging, fuzzing, and testing in general, as was already observed in
Zilberstein et al. 2023a
.
Differently from OL, SIL goals include: (i) defining a default deduction mechanism that starts from some specification of erroneous outcomes and traces the computation back to some initial states that can produce such errors; (ii) exhibiting a minimal set of rules that are complete and correct for backward under-approximation; and (iii) spelling out, a posteriori, a formalization and generalization of the backward analysis step performed by industrial grade analysis tools for security developed at Meta, such as Zoncolan
(
Distefano et al. 2019
)
, Mariana Trench
(
Gabi 2021
)
, and Pysa
(
Bleaney and Cepel 2019
)
(see Section
4.6
).

1.3.
Why Sufficient Incorrectness Logic?

SIL characterizes the initial states leading to an error.
However, one may ask if we do need to develop a new logic and whether existing ones suffice.
This is a legitimate question, and we answer it by comparing SIL with existing program logics.

1.3.1.
SIL and Incorrectness Logic

Let us consider the program
\mathsf{r}42
in Figure
2(a)
, also used by
O’Hearn 2020
to show the essence of IL triples.
We assume that
Q_{42}\triangleq(z=42)
denotes the set of erroneous states.
Any SIL triple provides a sufficient condition for the input state that causes the error.
For instance, with the deduction system introduced in this paper (but not with IL
(
O’Hearn 2020
, §3.1)
) we can prove the triple

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathit{odd}(y)\wedge\mathit{even}(x)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}42\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q_{42}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

stating that any state where
x
is even and
y
is odd will lead to the error (i.e.,
z=42
).
In IL, but
not
in SIL, one can prove that some errors can be reached even when we start in a safe state.
For instance, the following triple holds in IL:

(1)

[z=11]\penalty\ \mathsf{r}42\penalty\ [Q_{42}\wedge\mathit{odd}(y)\wedge\mathit{even}(x)].

However, that triple is not valid in SIL, because it is not true that for any state where
z=11
the program will reach an error, e.g., when we start from the state
z\mapsto 11,x\mapsto 0,y\mapsto 0
.

1.3.2.
SIL and Hoare logic

If a program
\mathsf{r}
is deterministic and non-divergent, then SIL triples are equivalent to Hoare triples—we only interpret the postcondition in the latter as the error states.
For instance, in the example of Figure
2(a)
, the program
\mathsf{r}42
is trivially deterministic and non-divergent, so the Hoare triple

\{\mathit{odd}(y)\wedge\mathit{even}(x)\}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}42\penalty\ \{Q_{42}\}

is valid (where
Q_{42}\triangleq(z=42)
as before).
However, this is no longer true in the presence of divergence and nondeterminism.
To illustrate this, consider the program
\mathsf{r42nd}
in Figure
2(b)
, which introduces nondeterminism in the program
\mathsf{r}42
of Figure
2(a)
.
The SIL triple

(2)

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathit{odd}(y)\wedge\mathit{even}(x)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r42nd}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q_{42}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

is still valid: any state satisfying the precondition can lead to the error when an even value is assigned to
x
.
However, the Hoare triple

\{\mathit{odd}(y)\wedge\mathit{even}(x)\}\penalty\ \mathsf{r42nd}\penalty\ \{Q_{42}\}

is no longer valid, because starting from an initial state where
\mathtt{z}\neq 42
,
\mathtt{x}
may be assigned an odd value, hence never entering the conditional.
Otherwise stated, in Hoare triples, the postcondition must over-approximate all the possible outcomes, which in the presence of nondeterminism (or divergence) means that it cannot single out the error states, as under-approximating logics can do.

1.3.3.
SIL and Outcome logic

Outcome Logic (OL)
(
Zilberstein et al. 2023a
)
extends HL by allowing to express both correctness and incorrectness properties. Although OL has been designed as a general framework that is parametric in a monoid of outcomes, the instance we compare with SIL is based on the powerset monoid, called the nondeterministic instance of OL (OL
{}_{\text{nd}}
).
Even though Lisbon triples can be expressed in OL, we show a simple example of a Lisbon triple whose proof in SIL is straightforward but which we were not able to derive in OL
{}_{\text{nd}}
.
To understand the example, it is worth noting that in OL
{}_{\text{nd}}
there are three different forms of disjunctive predicates that coexist with different meanings. To see this, take the two atomic assertions
P_{x}\triangleq(x=0)
and
P_{y}\triangleq(y=0)
. A set of states
m
satisfies their union
P_{x}\cup P_{y}
if
\forall\sigma\in m.(\sigma(x)=0\vee\sigma(y)=0)
.
Instead,
m
satisfies the disjunction
P_{x}\vee P_{y}
if
(\forall\sigma\in m.\sigma(x)=0)\vee(\forall\sigma\in m.\sigma(y)=0)
. Lastly,
m
satisfies the outcome composition
P_{x}\oplus P_{y}
if
m
can be decomposed as the union of two non-empty sets
m_{x}
and
m_{y}
that satisfy
P_{x}
and
P_{y}
respectively. The difference between
P_{x}\cup P_{y}
and
P_{x}\vee P_{y}
should be clear. If we take
m\triangleq\{\sigma\mid\sigma(x)=0\wedge\sigma(y)=1\}
, then
m
satisfies both
P_{x}\cup P_{y}
and
P_{x}\vee P_{y}
, but not
P_{x}\oplus P_{y}
.

Now take the nondeterministic code

(3)

\mathsf{r}_{xy}\triangleq\texttt{((y=0)?; x := 0)}\;\boxplus\;\texttt{((x=0)?; y := 0)}

and let
Q\triangleq(x=0\cap y=0)
be an incorrectness specification. By straightforward application of SIL rules we can derive the Lisbon triple:

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}P_{x}\cup P_{y}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}_{xy}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

The corresponding OL
{}_{\text{nd}}
triple is

\langle{P_{x}\cup P_{y}}\rangle\penalty\ \mathsf{r}_{xy}\penalty\ \langle{Q\oplus\top}\rangle

but, by using the rules available in
Zilberstein et al. 2023a
, to our best efforts, we were only able to prove the OL
{}_{\text{nd}}
triple:

\langle{P_{x}\oplus P_{y}}\rangle\penalty\ \mathsf{r}_{xy}\penalty\ \langle{Q\oplus\top}\rangle.

Notably, the simpler Lisbon triples
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}P_{x}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}_{xy}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
and
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}P_{y}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}_{xy}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
can be derived in both SIL and OL
{}_{\text{nd}}
proof systems, but the single triple
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}P_{x}\cup P_{y}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}_{xy}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
exposes the weakest SIL precondition for the specification
Q
, which is useful to determine the least general assumptions that may lead to an error. Thus the example suggests that weakest preconditions are not necessarily derivable in OL
{}_{\text{nd}}
.
Note that a revised and improved version of OL is currently under development
(
Zilberstein 2024
)
, where completeness is achieved by introducing, among others, a dedicated rule for disjunction and exploiting the equivalence
P_{x}\cup P_{y}\equiv P_{x}\vee P_{y}\vee(P_{x}\oplus P_{y})
.

1.3.4.
SIL and Necessary Preconditions

Cousot et al. 2011
;
Cousot et al. 2013
introduced Necessary Conditions (NC) as a principled foundation for automatic precondition inference.
Intuitively a necessary precondition is such that it removes entry states that inevitably will lead to errors
without
removing any good execution.
Therefore, whereas sufficient conditions (e.g., weakest liberal preconditions) require the caller of a function to supply parameters that will never cause an error, necessary conditions only prevent the invocation of the function with arguments that will definitely lead to some error.
Whilst Necessary Conditions and SIL have overlaps (e.g., they focus on providing conditions for errors) they express two different concepts.

Let us illustrate it with the example of Figure
2(b)
with again error states
Q_{42}\triangleq(z=42)
.
The correctness specification is thus
\neg Q_{42}
, that is
z\neq 42
.
A necessary precondition for correctness is also
z\neq 42
because, no matter which nondeterministic value gets assigned to
\mathtt{x}
, if
z=42
on entry, the program will reach an error state.
We denote it with the NC triple

(4)

(z\neq 42)\penalty\ \mathsf{r42nd}\penalty\ (\neg Q_{42}).

Please note that the precondition of the SIL triple (
2
) has a non-empty intersection with the necessary condition in (
4
), i.e., the formula
z\neq 42\wedge\mathit{odd}(y)\wedge\mathit{even}(x)
is satisfiable.
This should not come as a surprise: due to nondeterminism, a state satisfying that formula admits both correct and erroneous executions.

Our initial motivation for the work in this paper emerged from the attempt to characterize the validity conditions for existing triple-based program logics as under- or over-approximations of forward or backward semantics.
This led to the taxonomy of HL, IL and NC depicted in Figure
3
.
In this formalization process, we realized there was a missing combination, the one that originated SIL.

The taxonomy is obtained as follows.
Given a (possibly nondeterministic) program
\mathsf{r}
let us denote by
\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket
(resp.
\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket
) its forward (resp. backward) collecting semantics.
The forward semantics
\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket P
denotes the set of all possible output states of
\mathsf{r}
when execution starts from a state in
P
(and
\mathsf{r}
terminates).
Vice versa, the backward semantics
\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q
denotes the largest set of input states that can lead to a state in
Q
.

We define the validity of the various logics in terms of forward and backward semantics.

Definition 1.1 (Triples validity).

For any program
\mathsf{r}
and sets of states
P,Q
we let the following:

HL triples:

\{P\}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \{Q\}

is valid if

\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket P\subseteq Q
;

IL triples:

[P]\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ [Q]

is valid if

\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket P\supseteq Q
;

NC triples:

(P)\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ (Q)

is valid if

\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q\subseteq P
;

SIL triples:

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}P\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

is valid if

\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q\supseteq P
.

By using the validity conditions in Definition
1.1
, we can characterize HL, IL, NC, and SIL according to (i) whether the condition is expressed in terms of forward or backward semantics, and (ii) whether it is an over- or an under-approximation (Figure
3
).
Indeed, in HL and NC it is safe to enlarge the respective target set (i.e.,
Q
and
P
) so we do categorize them as over-approximations.
Similarly, in IL and SIL it is safe to shrink their target sets, so we do classify them as under-approximations.
Furthermore, from the validity conditions, we can immediately derive the consequence rules for each logic: for HL and SIL we are allowed to weaken
Q
and strengthen
P
, while for IL and NC we can do the opposite, i.e. the direction of the consequence rules are determined by the diagonals in Figure
3
.
Notwithstanding the fact that NC and IL share the same consequence rule, we will show that NC and IL are not related, whereas NC is tightly connected to HL.
We obtain that an NC triple
(P)\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ (Q)
is valid
if and only if
the HL triple
\{\lnot P\}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \{\lnot Q\}
is valid.
By duality, one might expect a similar connection to hold between IL and SIL, but we show this is not the case.

Interestingly, IL and SIL share the possibility to drop disjuncts, which was in fact one of the leading motivations for the introduction of IL to increase scalability and make the methods effective: the difference is that IL drops disjuncts in the postconditions, while SIL in the preconditions, a feature that is illustrated in Section
6.5
.

Using classical atomic commands for heap manipulation and a suitable language of assertions, we instantiate SIL to the case of Separation Logic, to derive a novel sound and (relatively) complete logic that can identify the causes of memory errors.
We call that new logic Separation SIL.
Intuitively, Separation SIL borrows the ability to deal with pointers from Separation Logic and combines it with the backward under-approximation principles of SIL.

We exemplify Separation SIL using the motivating example of
Raad et al. 2020
for Incorrectness Separation Logic (ISL).
Consider the program in Figure

The authors derive the incorrectness triple

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}v\mapsto z*z\mapsto-*\text{true}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{rclient}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}x\not\mapsto{}*\text{true}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

The triple (
5
) proves the existence of a faulty execution starting from at least one state in the precondition.
However, the Separation SIL triple (
6
) has both a more succinct postcondition capturing the error and a stronger guarantee:
every
state in the precondition reaches the error, giving (many) actual witnesses for testing and debugging the code.
In Section

we show the derivation and in Section

comment on how SIL principles apply in it.

1.6.
Contributions

After comparing our work with well-established results in Section
2
and recalling some basic concepts and notation in Section
3
, we make the following three main contributions:

•

In Section
4
, we introduce SIL, a novel logic for sufficient incorrectness triples. SIL enhances program analysis frameworks with the ability to identify the source of incorrectness. We prove that SIL is correct and complete for a minimal set of rules, and we propose some additional rules for program analysis.

•

Section
5
gives some insights into the relations among SIL, HL, IL and NC by constructing a taxonomy of program logics. Following the classification, we prove that IL and SIL perform under-approximations in different ways, i.e., finding the existence vs. the causes of errors, that SIL and HL coincide for deterministic and terminating programs, and that NC is isomorphic to HL and is not related to IL.

•

In Section
6
, we instantiate SIL to Separation Logic formulae to handle memory errors. We prove that Separation SIL is correct and, for loop-free programs, is also complete. By means of a simple single example, we claim that Separation SIL triples can be more convenient than ISL ones for testing and debugging and that our backward-oriented deduction strategy is more natural than OL’s.

We conclude in Section
7
pointing out some future work. Appendix
A
contains proofs and minor technical results.

2.
Related work

The origins of triples for highlighting sufficient preconditions for incorrectness have already been discussed in Section
1.1
.
In the literature, backwards under-approximation triples were mentioned in
Möller et al. 2021
;
Le et al. 2022
, but neither of them fully developed a corresponding program logic. Instead, we develop such a backward-oriented,
sound and complete
proof system for the first time, study the properties of the deductive system, compare it with existing program logics, and instantiate it for Separation Logic.
Moreover,
Le et al. 2022
introduce the notion of
manifest errors
in the context of IL, but mention that backwards under-approximation is able to characterize them directly. Intuitively, an error is manifest if it happens regardless of the context. Manifest errors can be easily captured in SIL: thanks to the completeness result, a postcondition
Q
defines a manifest error if and only if the SIL triple
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\text{true}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
is provable.

The idea of tracking the sources of errors was one of the motivations that led to Outcome Logic
(
Zilberstein et al. 2023a
)
, which is able to express both SIL and HL triples. Manifest errors can also be characterized with OL triples of the form
\langle{\text{true}}\rangle\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \langle{Q\oplus\top}\rangle
. Some differences between OL and SIL have been already sketched in Section
1.3.3
and we refer to Section
4.5
for a more technical comparison. In particular, we argue that SIL proof rules are simpler to use for inferring the source of errors starting from an incorrectness specification.

Exact Separation Logic (ESL)
(
Maksimovic et al. 2023
)
combines Separation Logic and Incorrectness Separation Logic to recover the exact semantics of a program and produce function summaries that can be used for both correctness and incorrectness. In the schema in Figure
3
, ESL would be placed between HL and IL.
Following this idea, we could position the calculus of possible correctness
(
Hoare 1978
)
between NC and SIL; OL along the diagonal between HL and SIL; and the forward-oriented core set of rules in
Raad et al. 2024
between IL and SIL.

Using abstract interpretation techniques,
Cousot 2024
defines a general procedure to derive proof systems starting from the underlying triple validity conditions. Most of the above logics can be recovered in Cousot’s framework by combining a small set of abstractions in different ways, as summarised in
Cousot 2024
.

Zhang and Kaminski 2022
propose a calculus for quantitative weakest pre and strongest postconditions, that subsume the Boolean case.
Using these, they propose a classification reminiscent of Definition
1.1
. They devise notions of total/partial (in)correctness that correspond to the four logics, and draw the same correspondence between HL and NC. However, they neither develop proof systems, nor compare in detail SIL and IL with other logics.

Dynamic Logic (DL)
(
Harel et al. 2000
)
is an extension of modal logic which is able to describe program properties.
It is known that HL and IL can be encoded in DL using forward box and backward diamond operator, respectively
(
O’Hearn 2020
)
. Moreover, both SIL and NC can be encoded using forward diamond (the latter via Proposition
5.4
and the HL encoding).
Thus, all four conditions in Definition
1.1
can be encoded in DL, which can then be used to understand their connections and possibly get new insights.

3.5.
Necessary Conditions

For contract inference,
Cousot et al. 2013
;
Cousot et al. 2011
introduced the notion of Necessary Conditions (NC).
The goal was to relax the burden on programmers: while sufficient conditions require the caller of a function to supply parameters that will never cause an error, NC only prevents the invocation of the function with arguments that will inevitably lead to some error.
Intuitively, for
Q
the set of good final states, the NC triple

(P)\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ (Q)

means that any state
\sigma\in P
admits at least one non-erroneous execution of the program
\mathsf{r}
.
Recently, the same concept has been applied to the context of security
(
Mackay et al. 2022
)
.

Following the original formulation
(
Cousot et al. 2013
)
, we can partition the traces of a nondeterministic execution starting from a memory
\sigma
in three different sets:
\mathcal{T}(\sigma)
, those without errors,
\mathcal{E}(\sigma)
, those with an error, and
\mathcal{I}(\sigma)
, those which do not terminate.
A sufficient precondition
\overline{P}
is such that
(\sigma\models\overline{P})\implies(\mathcal{E}(\sigma)=\emptyset)
, that is
\overline{P}
excludes all error traces.
Instead, a necessary precondition
\underline{P}
is a formula such that
(\mathcal{T}(\sigma)\neq\emptyset\lor\mathcal{I}(\sigma)\neq\emptyset)\implies(\sigma\models\underline{P})
, which is equivalent to

(\sigma\not\models\underline{P})\implies(\mathcal{T}(\sigma)\cup\mathcal{I}(\sigma)=\emptyset).

In other words, a necessary precondition
rules out no good run
: when it is violated by the input state, the program has only erroneous executions.
Note that we consider infinite traces as good traces. We do this by analogy with sufficient preconditions, where bad traces are only those which end in a bad state.

4.
Sufficient Incorrectness Logic

In this section, we explain the rationale for SIL, give a minimal sound and complete set of rules and present some additional rules that can speed up program analysis.

4.1.
Rationale

As outlined in the Introduction, the main motivation for studying sufficient incorrectness conditions is to enhance program analysis frameworks with the ability to identify the source of incorrectness and provide programmers with evidence of the inputs that can cause the errors. This is different from IL, which can be used to determine the presence of bugs, but cannot precisely identify their sources. SIL is also different in spirit from HL and NC, that aim to prove the absence of bugs or prevent them.
Roughly, the SIL triple

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}P\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

requires that for all
\sigma\in P
, there exists at least one
\sigma^{\prime}\in Q
such that
\sigma\in\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket\sigma^{\prime}
or, equivalently,
\sigma^{\prime}\in\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket\sigma
.
In other words, any SIL triple
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}P\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
asserts that “
all states in
P
have at least one execution leading to a state in
Q
".
More concisely, this property amounts to the validity condition

\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q\supseteq P.

Please note that, in the presence of nondeterminism, states in
P
are required to have one execution leading to
Q
, not necessarily all of them.
We make this equivalence formal by means of the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1 (Characterization of SIL validity).

For any
\mathsf{r}\in\mathsf{RCmd}
,
P,Q\subseteq\Sigma
we have

\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q\supseteq P\iff\forall\sigma\in P\,.\,\exists\sigma^{\prime}\in Q\,.\,\sigma^{\prime}\in\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket\sigma

A convenient way to exploit SIL is to assume that the analysis takes as input the incorrectness specification
Q
, which represents the set of erroneous final states.
Then, any valid SIL triple
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}P\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
yields a precondition which surely captures erroneous executions. In this sense,
P
can be considered a sufficient condition for incorrectness. This is dual to the interpretation of IL, where, for a given precondition
P
, any IL triple
[P]\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ [Q]
yields a set
Q
of final states which are for sure reachable, so that any error state in
Q
is a true bug reachable from some input in
P
.

4.3.
Correctness and Completeness

SIL is both correct and complete.
Correctness can be proved by induction on the derivation tree of a triple. Intuitively, if the premises of a rule are valid, then its consequence is valid as well, as we briefly observed in the previous section.
To prove completeness, we rely on the fact that rules other than
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{cons}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
are exact, that is, if their premises satisfy the equality
\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q=P
, their conclusion does as well. Using this, we prove the triple
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
for any
\mathsf{r}
and
Q
. Then we conclude using
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{cons}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
to get a proof of
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}P\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
for any
P\subseteq\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q
.
This is formalized by:

Theorem 4.2 (SIL is sound and complete).

A SIL triple is provable if and only if it is valid.

4.5.
SIL and Outcome Logic

As discussed in the Introduction, OL already recognized the importance of locating the source of errors, and showed that any SIL triple
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}P\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
is valid if and only if the corresponding OL
{}_{\text{nd}}
(the nondeterministic instance of OL, see Section
1.3.3
) triple
\langle{P}\rangle\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \langle{Q\oplus\top}\rangle
is valid.
However, the two approaches differ in the proof systems: in fact, many SIL rules cannot be derived from OL
{}_{\text{nd}}
rules.

The first thing to observe is that OL
{}_{\text{nd}}
assertions use a higher-level language, which describes sets of subsets of
\Sigma
, while SIL assertions are just subsets of
\Sigma
. In the OL
{}_{\text{nd}}
assertion language, subsets of
\Sigma
(i.e., SIL assertions) are called
atomic assertions
, which are then combined with higher level operators to denote sets of sets of states.
To see this, take the two atomic assertions
P_{x}\triangleq(x=0)
and
P_{y}\triangleq(y=0)
. In SIL, their conjunction
P_{x}\cap P_{y}
is satisfied by any state
\sigma
such that
\sigma(x)=\sigma(y)=0
, while in OL
{}_{\text{nd}}
the compound expression
P_{x}\wedge P_{y}
is satisfied by any set of states
m
such that
\forall\sigma\in m.\sigma(x)=\sigma(y)=0
.
This means that the correspondence between SIL and OL
{}_{\text{nd}}
triples can only be drawn when the latter is in the specific form
\langle{P}\rangle\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \langle{Q\oplus\top}\rangle
with
P
,
Q
atomic assertions, while in general a proof in OL involves assertions not in that form. Thus, there is no simple way to translate a proof in OL
{}_{\text{nd}}
to a proof in SIL.
As discussed in Section
1.3.3
, disjunction further distinguishes the assertion languages: the union
P_{x}\cup P_{y}
is the only kind of disjunction available in SIL, while OL
{}_{\text{nd}}
has also
\lor
and
\oplus
.

Some SIL rules can be derived from OL ones. For instance, rule
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{seq}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
can be derived from
\langle\mathsf{Seq}\rangle
, and
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{atom}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
is a combination of
\langle\mathsf{One}\rangle
,
\langle\mathsf{Assign}\rangle
and
\langle\mathsf{Assume}\rangle
.
However, we could not derive three key rules of SIL, namely
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{choice}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
,
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{disj}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
and
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{iter}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
.
All these rules depend on the union of atomic assertions, which cannot be mentioned in OL because it may not exist in the outcome monoid.

Extending the syntax of outcome predicates and exploiting some rules that are much more similar to ours, a preprint
(
Zilberstein 2024
)
presents a revised version of OL and recovers completeness for Lisbon triples.
However, since it mixes forward and backward-oriented rules, mixes over- and under-approximations and addresses more general (weighted) settings,
we claim that the direct use of SIL rules is more straightforward and convenient.
This advantage is more evident when we instantiate SIL for Separation Logic: in the small axioms for atomic commands, SIL can exploit an assertion language that is expressive enough to guarantee relative completeness, whereas the corresponding instance of OL
(
Zilberstein et al. 2023b
)
is not complete.

4.6.
Industrial Applications

SIL represents
a posteriori
formalization and theoretical justification of the parallel, modular, and compositional static analysis implemented in industrial grade static analyzers for security developed and used at Meta, such as Zoncolan
(
Distefano et al. 2019
)
, Mariana Trench
(
Gabi 2021
)
, and Pysa
(
Bleaney and Cepel 2019
)
.
Those tools automatically find more than
50
% of the security bugs in the Meta family of apps and many SEVs
(
Distefano et al. 2019
, Fig. 5)
.

In order to scale up to hundreds of millions of lines of code, static analyses need to be parallelizable and henceforth modular and compositional.
Modularity implies that the analysis can infer
meaningful
information without full knowledge of the global program.
Compositionality means that the
results
of analyzing modules are good enough that one does not lose information in using the inferred triple instead of inlining the code.

The analysis implemented in the aformentioned tools is a modular backward analysis that determines which input states for a function will lead to a security error, likewise the SIL rules described in this paper.
In particular, the analysis infers sufficient incorrectness preconditions (modularity) for callees that can be used by the callers (compositionality) to generate their incorrectness preconditions.
When the propagation of the inferred sufficient precondition reaches an attacker-controlled input, the analyzers check if that input is included in the propagated error condition.
If it is the case, then it emits an error.
The function analyses are parallelized and a strategy similar to the iteration rule of Figure
7
is used to compute the fixpoint in presence of mutually recursive functions.

5.
Relations Among Logics

We follow the two-dimensional scheme in Figure
3
to carry out an exhaustive comparison among the four validity conditions. While the duality between HL and IL was crystal clear from the introduction of IL in
O’Hearn 2020
, to the best of our knowledge its relations with NC has not been explored yet. Pursuing such formal comparison leads to some surprising results:

•

Although NC and IL share some similarities, not only we show that they are not comparable, but we prove a bijective correspondence between NC and HL that exploits double negation.

•

While the classification in Figure
3
and the previous item suggests that the proof strategy used to define the bijective correspondence between NC and HL can be extended to relate IL and SIL, we show that this is not the case: IL and SIL are not comparable.

•

More in general, each one of the conditions (and the corresponding logics) focus on different aspects of programs: none of them is better than the other; instead, each one has its precise application.

Next, we discuss the classification scheme, we deepen the comparison between NC and other logics, we show how SIL and IL differ, and we extend the discussion along other axes of comparison.

5.1.
Taxonomy

The validity of HL and IL triples correspond to the constraints
\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket P\subseteq Q
and
\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket P\supseteq Q
, respectively.
Therefore, their validity is expressed using the forward semantics. The difference between the two inequalities resides in the direction of the inclusion: the postcondition is an over-approximation of the forward semantics in HL, while it is an under-approximation in IL.
Thus we can classify HL and IL along the first column of Figure
3
.
Dually, the validity condition for SIL triples, namely
\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q\supseteq P
, can be placed in the second column, since it is expressed in terms of backward semantics, and in the second row, because the precondition is an under-approximation of the backward semantics.

The fourth inequality
\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q\subseteq P
defines over-approximations of backward semantics, and is dual to both SIL and HL. It turns out that
\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q\subseteq P
is the validity condition for NC.
Indeed, a precondition
P
is necessary for
Q
if every state which can reach a state in
Q
is in
P
. If
Q
describes good final states, then
\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q
defines all states
\sigma
which can reach a good state, so every necessary precondition
\underline{P}
must contain at least all states in
\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q
.
More formally, given a final state
\sigma^{\prime}\in Q
, all traces starting in
\sigma
and ending in
\sigma^{\prime}
are in
\mathcal{T}(\sigma)
. This means that every state
\sigma\in\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket\sigma^{\prime}
has a trace in
\mathcal{T}(\sigma)
(the one ending in
\sigma^{\prime}
), so it must belong to a necessary precondition
\underline{P}
as well.
This is captured by the following:

Proposition 5.1 (NC as backward over-approximation).

Given a correctness postcondition
Q
for the program
\mathsf{r}
, any possible necessary precondition
\underline{P}
for
Q
satisfies:

\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q\subseteq\underline{P}.

An analogous characterization appeared in
Zhang and Kaminski 2022
in terms of weakest precondition.
We will use (NC) to understand how NC relates to IL (Section
5.2
) and HL (Section
5.3
).

5.2.
Necessary Preconditions and Incorrectness Logic

Sufficient preconditions are properties implying the weakest liberal precondition:
\overline{P}
is sufficient for a postcondition
Q
if and only if
\overline{P}\implies\textbf{wlp}[\mathsf{r}](Q)
, which in turn is equivalent to validity of the HL triple
\{\overline{P}\}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \{Q\}
. Necessary and sufficient preconditions are dual, and so are IL and HL. Moreover, NC and IL enjoy the same consequence rule: both can strengthen the postcondition and weaken the precondition. This suggest that there may be a relation between NC and IL. However, the following example shows this is not the case.

Example 5.2.

Let us consider once again the simple deterministic program
\mathsf{r}42
of Figure
2(a)
, with
Q_{42}\triangleq(z=42)
.
Let
Q^{\prime}_{42}\triangleq(Q_{42}\wedge\mathit{odd}(y)\wedge\mathit{even}(x))
and
P_{11}\triangleq(z=11)
. Then IL triple (
1
) is exactly
[P_{11}]\penalty\ \mathsf{r}42\penalty\ [Q^{\prime}_{42}]
, so we know this is valid.
However, we observe that the NC triple
(P_{11})\penalty\ \mathsf{r}42\penalty\ (Q^{\prime}_{42})
is not valid because the state
\sigma
such that
\sigma(x)=0
,
\sigma(y)=1
,
\sigma(z)=10
has an execution leading to
Q^{\prime}_{42}
but is not in
P_{11}
.
Moreover, take for instance
\underline{P}=(\mathit{odd}(y)\land\mathit{even}(x))
, which makes the NC triple
(\underline{P})\penalty\ \mathsf{r}42\penalty\ (Q^{\prime}_{42})
valid (any state
not
satisfying
\underline{P}
has either an even
y
or an odd
x
, and those variables are not changed by the program). Then it is clear that
P_{11}\not\implies\underline{P}
. This shows that not only IL triples do not yield NC triples, but also that in general there are NC preconditions which are not implied by IL preconditions.

Conversely, consider
\neg Q_{42}=(z\neq 42)
. The NC triple
(\text{true})\penalty\ \mathsf{r}42\penalty\ (\neg Q_{42})
is clearly valid, but the IL triple
[\text{true}]\penalty\ \mathsf{r}42\penalty\ [\neg Q_{42}]
is not: for instance, the final state
\sigma^{\prime}
such that
\sigma^{\prime}(x)=\sigma^{\prime}(y)=\sigma^{\prime}(z)=11
is not reachable from any initial state. It follows that the IL triple
[P]\penalty\ \mathsf{r}42\penalty\ [\neg Q_{42}]
is not valid for any
P
.
∎

Given a valid NC triple
(\underline{P})\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ (Q)
and a valid IL triple
[P]\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ [Q]
, we are guaranteed that
P\cap\underline{P}\neq\emptyset
, that is there are states which satisfy both
P
and
\underline{P}
. However, in general neither
P\subseteq\underline{P}
nor
\underline{P}\subseteq P
hold.
The difference between NC and IL is striking when we look at their quantified definitions:

(NC
∀
)

\displaystyle\forall\sigma^{\prime}\in Q\,.\,\forall\sigma\in\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket\sigma^{\prime}\,.\,\sigma\in P

(IL
∃
)

\displaystyle\forall\sigma^{\prime}\in Q\,.\,\exists\sigma\in\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket\sigma^{\prime}\,.\,\sigma\in P

While (NC
∀
) universally quantifies on initial states (
all
initial states with a good run must satisfy the precondition), (IL
∃
) existentially quantifies on them.
We observe that, whenever
\mathsf{r}
is reversible (i.e.,
\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket
is injective) any valid IL triple is also a valid NC triple.

5.3.
Necessary Preconditions and Hoare Logic

It turns out that NC is strongly connected to weakest liberal preconditions and thus to HL.
In fact, let
Q
be a postcondition: a finite trace is in
\mathcal{T}(\sigma)
if its final state satisfies
Q
and in
\mathcal{E}(\sigma)
otherwise.
In general, a necessary precondition has no relationship with
\textbf{wlp}[\mathsf{r}](Q)
. However, if we consider
\lnot Q
instead of
Q
, we observe that “erroneous" executions becomes those in
\mathcal{T}(\sigma)
and “correct" ones those in
\mathcal{E}(\sigma)
. This means that

(\mathcal{T}(\sigma)=\emptyset)\iff\sigma\in\textbf{wlp}[\mathsf{r}](\lnot Q),

from which we derive

\lnot\underline{P}\implies\textbf{wlp}[\mathsf{r}](\lnot Q)

or, equivalently,

\lnot\textbf{wlp}[\mathsf{r}](\lnot Q)\implies\underline{P}.

Example 5.3.

Building on Example
3.6
, the correctness specification for
\mathsf{r42nd}
is
\neg Q_{42}=(z\neq 42)
. We have that
\textbf{wlp}[\mathsf{r42nd}](\lnot\lnot Q_{42})=\textbf{wlp}[\mathsf{r42nd}](Q_{42})=Q_{42}
, because if initially
z\neq 42
then there is the possibility that
x
is assigned an odd value and
z
is not updated.
Therefore, a condition
P
is implied by
\lnot\textbf{wlp}[\mathsf{r}](\lnot\lnot Q_{42})=\lnot Q_{42}
if and only if it is necessary. For instance,
(z\neq 42\vee\mathit{odd}(y))
is necessary, while
(z>42)
is not.
∎

The next bijection establishes the connection between NC and HL:

Proposition 5.4 (Bijection between NC and HL).

For any
\mathsf{r}\in\mathsf{RCmd}
and
P,Q\subseteq\Sigma
we have:

\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket P\subseteq Q\iff\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket(\lnot Q)\subseteq\lnot P.

The proposition means that a necessary precondition is just the negation of a sufficient precondition for the negated post.
This was also observed using weakest (liberal) preconditions in
Zhang and Kaminski 2022
.

5.4.
Sufficient Incorrectness Logic and Hoare Logic

In general, HL and SIL are different logics, but they coincide whenever the program
\mathsf{r}
is deterministic and terminates for every input (cf. Section
1.3.2
). This is formally captured by:

Proposition 5.5 (SIL vs HL).

For any
\mathsf{r}\in\mathsf{RCmd}
and
P,Q\subseteq\Sigma
we have:

•

if
\mathsf{r}
is deterministic,
\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q\supseteq P\implies\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket P\subseteq Q

•

if
\mathsf{r}
is terminating,
\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket P\subseteq Q\implies\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q\supseteq P

5.5.
Sufficient Incorrectness Logic and Incorrectness Logic

In Figure
3
, we highlight the fact that (HL) and (NC) are isomorphic (Proposition
5.4
). It is natural to ask if there is a similar connection between (IL) and (SIL). The next example answers negatively.

Example 5.6.

Since IL and SIL enjoy different consequence rules, neither of the two can imply the other with the same
P
and
Q
. For negated
P
and
Q
, consider the simple program below

\mathsf{r1}\triangleq x:=1

are valid.
So neither (IL) implies negated (SIL) nor the other way around.
∎

To gain some insights on why (SIL) and (IL) are not equivalent,
given a regular command
\mathsf{r}
, we define the set of states that only diverges
D_{\mathsf{r}}
and the set of unreachable states
U_{\mathsf{r}}
:

D_{\mathsf{r}}\triangleq\{\sigma\,|\,\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket\sigma=\emptyset\}\qquad U_{\mathsf{r}}\triangleq\{\sigma^{\prime}\,|\,\sigma^{\prime}\not\in\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket\Sigma\}=\{\sigma^{\prime}\,|\,\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket\sigma^{\prime}=\emptyset\}.

In a sense,
U_{\mathsf{r}}
is the set of states which “diverge" going backward.

Lemma 5.7.

For any regular command
\mathsf{r}\in\mathsf{RCmd}
and sets of states
P,Q\subseteq\Sigma
it holds that:

(1)

\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket P\supseteq P\setminus D_{\mathsf{r}}
;

(2)

\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q\supseteq Q\setminus U_{\mathsf{r}}
.

Lemma
5.7

highlights the asymmetry between over and under-approximation: the composition of a function with its inverse is increasing (but for non-terminating states).
This explains why (HL) and (NC) are related while (IL) and (SIL) are not: on the over-approximating side,
P\setminus D_{\mathsf{r}}\subseteq\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket P
can be further exploited if we know
\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket P\subseteq Q
via (HL), but it cannot when
\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket P\supseteq Q
via (IL).

The preprint
(
Raad et al. 2024
)
introduces a forward-oriented proof system with a core set of rules that are sound for both IL and SIL. It also becomes complete for IL, resp. SIL, when augmented with the corresponding consequence rule.

5.7.
Weakest/Strongest Conditions

Depending on the way in which program analysis is conducted, one can be interested in deriving either the most general or most specific hypotheses under which the reasoning can take place.
For instance, given a correctness program specification
Q
one is typically interested in finding the weakest liberal preconditions that make
Q
satisfied, i.e., to impose the minimal constraint on the input that guarantee program correctness.
Conversely, to infer necessary conditions we can be interested in devising the strongest hypotheses under which some correct run is possible.

To investigate the existence of weakest/strongest pre and postconditions, it is convenient to take into account the consequence rules of the four kinds of triples.
The consequence rule of each logic explains how pre and postconditions can be weakened/strengthened.
The concrete semantics is trivially a strongest (HL and NC) or weakest (IL and SIL) condition for the target property (
P
computing backward and
Q
forward).
It turns out that having a strongest/weakest condition on the “source" property is a prerogative of over-approximation, i.e., that over and under-approximation are not fully dual theories.

Proposition 5.8 (Existence of weakest conditions).

For any regular command
\mathsf{r}\in\mathsf{RCmd}
:

•

given
Q
, there exists a weakest
P
such that
\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket P\subseteq Q
(HL);

•

given
P
, there exists a weakest
Q
such that
\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q\subseteq P
(NC).

Proposition 5.9 (Non-existence of strongest conditions).

For any regular command
\mathsf{r}\in\mathsf{RCmd}
:

•

for some
Q
, there is no strongest
P
such that
\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket P\supseteq Q
(IL);

•

for some
P
, there is no strongest
Q
such that
\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket Q\supseteq P
(SIL).

The reason why strongest conditions may not exist for IL and SIL is that the collecting semantics (both forward and backward) is additive but not co-additive. In other words, rule {
\mathsf{disj}
} is sound for all triples, while a dual rule for conjunction such as

\{P_{1}\cap P_{2}\}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \{Q_{1}\cap Q_{2}\}\lx@proof@logical@and\{P_{1}\}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \{Q_{1}\}\{P_{2}\}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \{Q_{2}\}

is valid for HL and NC but neither for IL nor SIL.
So, for instance, given
Q
and two HL preconditions
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
(

and
\{P_{2}\}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \{Q\}
) also their union is a precondition for

, ie.
\{P_{1}\cup P_{2}\}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \{Q\}
, which can be proved using {
\mathsf{disj}
}. However, given two IL triples

5.8.
Termination and Reachability

Termination and reachability are two sides of the same coin when switching from forward to backward reasoning, and over- and under-approximation behave differently with respect to this notion.

For HL, given the definition of collecting semantics, we can only distinguish a precondition which always causes divergence: if
Q
is empty, all states in the precondition must always diverge. However, if just one state in
P
has one terminating computation, its final state must be in
Q
, so we do not know any more whether states in
P
diverge or not. Moreover, because of the over-approximation, a non empty
Q
does not mean there truly are finite executions, as those may be introduced by the approximation.
Dually, NC cannot say much about reachability of
Q
unless
P
is empty, in which case
Q
is unreachable.

On the contrary, under-approximation has much stronger guarantees on divergence/reachability. Any IL triple
[P]\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ [Q]
ensures that all states in
Q
are reachable from states in
P
, which means in particular that every state in
Q
is reachable. Dually, a SIL triple
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}P\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
means that all states in
P
have a convergent computation (which ends in a state in
Q
). This observation motivates the choice of a forward (resp. backward) rule for iteration in IL (resp. SIL): a backward (resp. forward) rule would need to prove reachability of all points in the postcondition (resp. precondition). Instead, the forward rule of IL (resp. backward rule of SIL) ensures reachability (resp. termination) by construction, as it builds
Q
(resp.
P
) only with points which are known to be reachable (resp. terminating) by executing the loop.

6.
Separation Sufficient Incorrectness Logic

We instantiate SIL to handle pointers and dynamic memory allocation, introducing Separation SIL. The goal of Separation SIL is to identify the causes of memory errors: it takes the backward under-approximation principles of SIL and combines it with the ability to deal with pointers from Separation Logic (SL)
(
Reynolds 2002
;
O’Hearn et al. 2001
)
.

6.1.

We denote by
\mathsf{HRCmd}

7
) (in blue the new primitives):

\mathsf{HACmd}\ni\mathsf{c}::=\;\texttt{skip}\mid\texttt{x := a}\mid\texttt{b?}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\texttt{x := alloc()}\mid\texttt{free(x)}\mid\texttt{x := [y]}\mid\texttt{[x] := y}}

and Boolean expressions
\texttt{b}\in\mathsf{BExp}
cannot dereference a variable: to use a value from the heap, the value must be loaded in a variable beforehand.

\mathsf{r}

\displaystyle\text{mod}(\texttt{skip})=\emptyset\qquad

\displaystyle\text{mod}(\texttt{x := a})=\{\texttt{x}\}

\displaystyle\text{mod}(\texttt{x := alloc()})=\{\texttt{x}\}

\displaystyle\text{mod}(\texttt{free(x)})=\emptyset\qquad

\displaystyle\text{mod}(\texttt{x := [y]})=\{\texttt{x}\}

\displaystyle\text{mod}(\texttt{[x] := y})=\emptyset

\displaystyle\text{mod}(\mathsf{r}_{1};\mathsf{r}_{2})=\text{mod}(\mathsf{r}_{1})\cup\text{mod}(\mathsf{r}_{2})

\displaystyle\text{mod}(\mathsf{r}_{1}\boxplus\mathsf{r}_{2})=\text{mod}(\mathsf{r}_{1})\cup\text{mod}(\mathsf{r}_{2})

Please note that
free(x)
and
[x] := y
do not modify
x
: this is because they only modify the value
pointed by

x
, not the actual value of
x
(the memory address itself).

6.2.
Assertion Language

Our assertion language for pre and postconditions is derived from both SL and Incorrectness Separation Logic (ISL):

\text{Asl}\ni p,q,t::=\text{false}\mid\lnot p\mid p\land q\mid\exists x.p\mid a\asymp a\mid\textbf{emp}\mid x\mapsto\texttt{a}\mid x\not\mapsto{}\mid p*q

In the above productions,
\asymp\in\{=,\neq,\leq,<,\dots\}
encodes standard comparison operators,
x\in\text{Var}
is a generic variable and
\texttt{a}\in\mathsf{AExp}
is an arithmetic expression. The first five constructs describe standard first order logic. The others describe heaps and come from Separation Logic, with the exception of
x\not\mapsto{}
, which was introduced by
Raad et al. 2020
.

The constant
emp
denotes an empty heap.
The assertion
x\mapsto a
stands for an heap with a single memory cell pointed by
x
and whose content is
a
, while
x\not\mapsto{}
describes that
x
points to a memory cell that was previously deallocated.
The separating conjunction
p*q
describes an heap which can be divided in two disjoint sub-heaps, one satisfying
p
and the other
q
.
We let
x\mapsto-\triangleq\exists v.x\mapsto v
describe that
x
is allocated but we do not care about its exact value.
Given a formula
p\in\text{Asl}
, we call
\text{fv}(p)\subseteq\text{Var}
the set of its free variables.

To prove soundness of Separation SIL, we first define a denotational semantics for heap regular commands.
We consider a finite set of variables
Var
and an infinite set

of memory locations; we define the set of values
\text{Val}\triangleq\mathbb{Z}\uplus\text{Loc}
where
\uplus
is disjoint union.
Stores
s\in\text{Store}
are (total) functions from variables to values (so either integers or memory locations). A heap
h\in\text{Heap}
is a partial function
h:\text{Loc}\rightharpoonup\text{Val}\uplus\{\bot\}
. If
h(l)=v\in\text{Val}
, location
l
is allocated and holds value
v
, if
l\notin\text{dom}(h)
then it is not allocated. The special value
\bot
describes a deallocated memory location: if
h(l)=\bot
, that location was previously allocated and then deallocated.
As notation, we use

for function update. For heaps specifically,
[]
is the empty heap, and
[l\mapsto v]
is a shortcut for
[][l\mapsto v]
, that is the heap defined only on
l
and associating to it value
v
.

We consider as states
\sigma
pairs of a store and a heap, plus the special state
err
representing the occurrence of an error: letting
\Sigma=\text{Store}\times\text{Heap}
, states are taken from
\Sigma_{e}=\Sigma\uplus\{\textbf{err}\}
. The denotational semantics of atomic commands
\llparenthesis\cdot\rrparenthesis:\mathsf{HACmd}\rightarrow\wp(\Sigma_{e})\rightarrow\wp(\Sigma_{e})
is in Figure
13(a)
. To simplify the presentation, we define it as
\llparenthesis\cdot\rrparenthesis:\mathsf{HACmd}\rightarrow\Sigma\rightarrow\wp(\Sigma_{e})
, we let
\llparenthesis\mathsf{c}\rrparenthesis\textbf{err}=\{\textbf{err}\}
, and we lift it to set of states by union.
Please note that, since arithmetic expressions
a
and Boolean expressions
b
cannot contain any dereferencing, their evaluation only depends on the store and not on the heap.

We define the forward collecting semantics of heap commands,
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket:\mathsf{HRCmd}\rightarrow\wp(\Sigma_{e})\rightarrow\wp(\Sigma_{e})
, similarly to (
8
) using the different semantics of atomic commands for
\mathsf{c}\in\mathsf{HACmd}
.

The semantics
\{\hskip-2.0pt|\cdot|\hskip-2.0pt\}
of a formula
p\in\text{Asl}
is a set of states in
\Sigma
.
As notation, we write
h_{1}\perp h_{2}
when
\text{dom}(h_{1})\cap\text{dom}(h_{2})=\emptyset
, and we say the two heaps are disjoint. For two disjoint heaps
h_{1}\perp h_{2}
, we define the
\bullet
operation as the merge of the two:
h_{1}\bullet h_{2}
is defined on
\text{dom}(h_{1})\cup\text{dom}(h_{2})
, and its value on
l
is either
h_{1}(l)
or
h_{2}(l)
(the only one defined).
The full definition of
\{\hskip-2.0pt|\cdot|\hskip-2.0pt\}
is given in Figure
13(b)
.

Just like SIL, we define validity of a Separation SIL triple
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}p\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
by the condition
\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket\{\hskip-2.0pt|q|\hskip-2.0pt\}\supseteq\{\hskip-2.0pt|p|\hskip-2.0pt\}
.
To prove soundness of Separation SIL, we rely on a stronger lemma, whose proof is by induction on the derivation tree.

Lemma 6.1.

Let
p,q,t\in\text{Asl}
and
\mathsf{r}\in\mathsf{HRCmd}
. If
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}p\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
is provable and
\text{fv}(t)\cap\text{mod}(\mathsf{r})=\emptyset
then:

\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket\{\hskip-2.0pt|q*t|\hskip-2.0pt\}\supseteq\{\hskip-2.0pt|p*t|\hskip-2.0pt\}.

Then we can prove soundness of the proof system by taking
t=\textbf{emp}
and using
p*\textbf{emp}\equiv p
.

Corollary 6.2 (Separation SIL is sound).

If a Separation SIL triple is provable then it is valid.

The presented proof system is not complete. However, we will show how to recover relative completeness
(
Apt and Olderog 2019
, §4.3)
in Section
6.7
.

[v\mapsto z*z\mapsto-]\penalty\ \mathsf{rclient}\penalty\ [v\mapsto y*y\mapsto-*x\not\mapsto{}].

6.6.
Observations on SIL Principles

In Section
6.5
, we use
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{cons}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
to drop the disjunct
q
from
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}p\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \texttt{x := [v]}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}t\lor q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
. Similarly, we could have used
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{cons}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
to drop the disjunct
x\not\mapsto{}
in the precondition for
y:=\text{alloc}()
in
\mathsf{r}_{b}
. This is analogous to the IL ability to drop disjuncts in the post, but with respect to the backward direction.

Furthermore, we use the postcondition
x\not\mapsto{}*\text{true}
. The reader might be wondering why we had to include the
(*\ \text{true})
: is it not possible to just frame it in when we plug the proof in a larger program?
The issue is that in final reachable states
x
is not the only variable allocated (there are also
v
and
y
), so the final heap should talk about them as well. Adding
(*\ \text{true})
is just a convenient way to focus only on the part of the heap that describes the error, that is
x\not\mapsto{}
, and just leave everything else unspecified since we do not care about it.

6.7.
Relative completeness of Separation SIL

The proof system in Section
6.3
is not complete. To move towards completeness, we first limit the assertion language to the existential fragment of first-order logic:

\displaystyle\text{Asl}\ni p,q,t::=

\displaystyle\;\text{false}\mid\text{true}\mid p\land q\mid p\lor q\mid\exists x.p\mid a\asymp a

\displaystyle\mid\textbf{emp}\mid x\mapsto\texttt{a}\mid x\not\mapsto{}\mid p*q

We remove negation, so we don’t include universal quantifiers and heap assertions must be positive. However, we argue that this is sufficient to find bugs: for instance, in the example in Section
6.5
, we only used assertions from this subset.

With this limited assertion language, the proof system in Section
6.3
is complete for all atomic commands except
alloc
.
To deal with
alloc
, we need the ability to refer to the specific memory location that was allocated. However, the naive solution to add a constraint
x=\alpha
in the post of
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{alloc}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
makes the frame rule unsound: for instance, the following triple is not valid:

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\textbf{emp}*\alpha\mapsto-\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \texttt{x := alloc()}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}(x\mapsto-\land x=\alpha)*\alpha\mapsto-\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}.

To recover the frame rule, just like ISL needs the deallocated assertion in the post
(
Raad et al. 2020
, §3)
, we need a "will be allocated" assertion in the pre. To this end we use the
\not\mapsto{}
assertion, and change the semantic model to only allocate a memory location that is explicitly
\bot
instead of one not in the domain of the heap.
We formalize this by letting
\mathit{avail}(l)\triangleq h(l)=\bot
in Figure
13(a)
, and replacing the axiom
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{alloc}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
with

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\beta\not\mapsto{}{}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \texttt{x := alloc()}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}x=\beta\land x\mapsto v\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

Soundness still holds for this different semantics. Moreover, we can prove relative completeness
(
Apt and Olderog 2019
, §4.3)
for loop-free programs:

Theorem 6.3 (Relative completeness for loop-free programs).

Suppose to have an oracle to prove implications between formulas in
Asl
. Let
\mathsf{r}\in\mathsf{HRCmd}
be a regular command without
*
and
p,q\in\text{Asl}
such that
\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket\{\hskip-2.0pt|q|\hskip-2.0pt\}\supseteq\{\hskip-2.0pt|p|\hskip-2.0pt\}
. Then the triple
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}p\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
is provable.

The proof relies on the possibility to rewrite any
q
in an equivalent assertion of the form
\exists x_{1}.\cdots\exists x_{n}.\penalty\ \bigvee_{1\leq i\leq k}q_{i}
where all
q_{i}
are assertions involving atoms composed with
\wedge
and
*
only.
This way, completeness is proved for such
q_{i}
first and then extended to the entire
q
thanks to rules
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{disj}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
and
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{exists}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
.
Notably, we show that the weakest (possible) precondition
\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket\{\hskip-2.0pt|q|\hskip-2.0pt\}
of loop-free programs is always expressible as an assertion
t\in\text{Asl}
, namely
\{\hskip-2.0pt|t|\hskip-2.0pt\}=\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket\{\hskip-2.0pt|q|\hskip-2.0pt\}
, and prove that the triple
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}t\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
can be derived. Then, by
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{cons}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
, the theorem follows for any
p
that implies
t
.

7.
Conclusion and Future Work

We have introduced SIL as a correct and complete program logic aimed to locate the causes of errors.
Furthermore, we instantiated SIL to a sound and (relatively) complete proof system for handling memory errors and discussed its advantages over ISL and OL.

Unlike IL, which was designed to expose erroneous outputs, SIL provides sufficient conditions that explain why such errors can occur.
SIL can be characterized as a logic based on backward under-approximation, which helped us to compare it against HL, IL and NC.
This is captured in the taxonomy of Figure
3
and in the rule-by-rule comparison of Figure
11
, which we used to clarify the analogies and differences between the possible approaches.
We obtained some surprising connections: although NC and IL share the same consequence rules, they are not comparable; NC triples are isomorphic to HL ones, but such a correspondence cannot be extended to relate IL and SIL; and we pointed out the main reasons why duality arguments cannot apply in this case.
The following list addresses the overall connections between the different logics:

HL vs NC::

there is an isomorphism given by
\{P\}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \{Q\}
iff
(\neg P)\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ (\neg Q)
.

HL vs IL::

in general there is no relation between forward over- and under-approximation triples: the only triples common to HL and IL are the exact ones.

HL vs SIL::

for deterministic and terminating programs HL and SIL judgements do coincide. More precisely, we have that for terminating programs
\{P\}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \{Q\}
implies
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}P\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
and for deterministic programs
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}P\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
implies
\{P\}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \{Q\}
. OL
(
Zilberstein et al. 2023a
)
is correct for both HL and SIL and the revised OL in the preprint
(
Zilberstein 2024
)
is also complete.

NC vs IL::

although NC and IL share the same consequence rule, they are not comparable unless the program is reversible (in which case IL implies NC).

NC vs SIL::

in general there is no relation between backward over- and under-approximation triples: the only triples common to NC and SIL are the exact ones.

IL vs SIL::

there is no relation. The independently developed preprint
(
Raad et al. 2024
)
proposes a correct and complete proof system for IL and SIL.

We conclude that each logic focuses on different aspects: none of them is “better” than the others; instead, each one has its merits.

7.1.
Future Work

It seems interesting to explore a synergic use of different logics for practical issues. For instance, a forward, IL-based analysis determines, at every program point, a set of truly reachable states, which can then be used to narrow down a subsequent backward, SIL-based analysis to find input states which truly generate the pointed errors.
To this aim, it could be useful to extend the taxonomy of HL, NC, IL and SIL by incorporating some other approaches
(
Maksimovic et al. 2023
;
Zilberstein et al. 2023a
;
Raad et al. 2024
;
Hoare 1978
)
, as discussed in Section
2
.

Lastly, we plan to explore the expressiveness of other assertion languages in order to extend the relative completeness proof of Separation SIL to deal with loops.

A.5.
Proofs about Separation SIL

Given two stores
s,s^{\prime}\in\text{Store}
and a heap command
\mathsf{r}\in\mathsf{HRCmd}
, we use the notation
s\dot{\sim}_{\mathsf{r}}s^{\prime}
to indicate that they coincide on all variables not modified by
\mathsf{r}
:
\forall x\notin\text{mod}(\mathsf{r})\,.\,s(x)=s^{\prime}(x)
. Please note that
\dot{\sim}_{\mathsf{r}}
is an equivalence relation.

Lemma A.4.

Let
(s,h)\in\Sigma
,
\mathsf{r}\in\mathsf{HRCmd}
. If
(s^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\llbracket\mathsf{r}\rrbracket(s,h)
then
s\dot{\sim}_{\mathsf{r}}s^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on the syntax of
\mathsf{r}
. We prove here only some relevant cases.

Case

x := a

(s^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\llbracket\texttt{x := a}\rrbracket(s,h)
means that
s^{\prime}=s[x\mapsto\llparenthesis\texttt{a}\rrparenthesis s]
. Particularly, this means that for all variables
y\neq x
,
s^{\prime}(y)=s(y)
, which is the thesis because
\text{mod}(\texttt{x := a})=\{x\}
.

Case

free(x)

(s^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\llbracket\texttt{free(x)}\rrbracket(s,h)
means that
s^{\prime}=s
, which is the thesis because
\text{mod}(\texttt{free(x)})=\emptyset
.

Case

\mathsf{r}_{1};\mathsf{r}_{2}

(s^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\llbracket\mathsf{r}_{1};\mathsf{r}_{2}\rrbracket(s,h)
means that there exists
(s^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime\prime})\in\llbracket\mathsf{r}_{1}\rrbracket(s,h)
such that
(s^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\llbracket\mathsf{r}_{2}\rrbracket(s^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime\prime})
. By inductive hypothesis, since
\text{mod}(\mathsf{r}_{1})\subseteq\text{mod}(\mathsf{r}_{1};\mathsf{r}_{2})
, we have
s^{\prime\prime}\dot{\sim}_{\mathsf{r}_{1};\mathsf{r}_{2}}s
. Analogously,
\text{mod}(\mathsf{r}_{2})\subseteq\text{mod}(\mathsf{r}_{1};\mathsf{r}_{2})
implies
s^{\prime}\dot{\sim}_{\mathsf{r}_{1};\mathsf{r}_{2}}s^{\prime\prime}
. From these, we get
s^{\prime}\dot{\sim}_{\mathsf{r}_{1};\mathsf{r}_{2}}s
.
∎

The next technical proposition states some semantic properties of the assertion language to be exploited in the proof of Lemma
6.1
.

Proposition A.5.

Let
p\in\text{Asl}
,
s,s^{\prime}\in\text{Store}
,
h\in\text{Heap}
and
a\in\text{AExp}
.

(1)

If
\forall x\in\text{fv}(p)\,.\,s(x)=s^{\prime}(x)
and
(s,h)\in\{\hskip-2.0pt|p|\hskip-2.0pt\}
then
(s^{\prime},h)\in\{\hskip-2.0pt|p|\hskip-2.0pt\}

(2)

If
(s,h)\in\{\hskip-2.0pt|p[a/x]|\hskip-2.0pt\}
then
(s[x\mapsto\llparenthesis a\rrparenthesis s],h)\in\{\hskip-2.0pt|p|\hskip-2.0pt\}

By structural induction on the syntax of assertions.

Proof of Lemma

The proof is by induction on the derivation tree of the provable triple
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}p\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{assign}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

(s,h_{t})\in\{\hskip-2.0pt|t|\hskip-2.0pt\}

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{alloc}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{load}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

Case

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{exists}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

A.6.
Completeness of Separation SIL

Some of the following equivalences are standard, but we collect them all here for convenience.

Lemma A.6.

For all assertions
p_{1},p_{2},q
, variables
x,x^{\prime}\in\text{Var}
and arithmetic expressions
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathsf{AExp}
, the following equivalences hold:

(1)

(p_{1}\lor p_{2})\land q\equiv(p_{1}\land q)\lor(p_{2}\land q)

(2)

(p_{1}\lor p_{2})*q\equiv(p_{1}*q)\lor(p_{2}*q)

(3)

\exists x.(p\lor q)\equiv(\exists x.p)\lor(\exists x.q)

(4)

\exists x.(p\land q)\equiv(\exists x.p)\land q
if
x\notin\text{fv}(q)

(5)

\exists x.(p*q)\equiv(\exists x.p)*q
if
x\notin\text{fv}(q)

(6)

a_{1}\asymp a_{2}\land(p_{1}*p_{2})\equiv(a_{1}\asymp a_{2}\land p_{1})*p_{2}

(7)

(p_{1}*x\mapsto x^{\prime})\land(p_{2}*x\mapsto x^{\prime})\equiv(p_{1}\land p_{2})*x\mapsto x^{\prime}

(8)

(p_{1}*x\not\mapsto{})\land(p_{2}*x\not\mapsto{})\equiv(p_{1}\land p_{2})*x\not\mapsto{}

Proof.

Point (1), (3) and (4) are standard in first-order logic. Point (2), (5) and (6) are standard in separation logic
(
Reynolds 2002
)
.

For point (7) we observe

\displaystyle\{\hskip-2.0pt|(p_{1}*x\mapsto x^{\prime})\land(p_{2}*x\mapsto x^{\prime})|\hskip-2.0pt\}

\displaystyle=\{(s,h)\,|\,(s,h)\in\{\hskip-2.0pt|p_{1}*x\mapsto x^{\prime}|\hskip-2.0pt\},(s,h)\in\{\hskip-2.0pt|p_{2}*x\mapsto x^{\prime}|\hskip-2.0pt\}\}

\displaystyle=\{(s,h)\,|\,h=h_{1}\bullet[s(x)\mapsto s(x^{\prime})],(s,h_{1})\in\{\hskip-2.0pt|p_{1}|\hskip-2.0pt\}h=h_{2}\bullet[s(x)\mapsto s(x^{\prime})],(s,h_{2})\in\{\hskip-2.0pt|p_{2}|\hskip-2.0pt\}\}

\displaystyle=\{(s,h)\,|\,h=h^{\prime}\bullet[s(x)\mapsto s(x^{\prime})],(s,h^{\prime})\in\{\hskip-2.0pt|p_{1}|\hskip-2.0pt\},(s,h^{\prime})\in\{\hskip-2.0pt|p_{2}|\hskip-2.0pt\}\}

\displaystyle=\{\hskip-2.0pt|x\mapsto x^{\prime}*(p_{1}\land p_{2})|\hskip-2.0pt\}

Point (8) is analogous.
∎

Lemma A.7.

Let
q\in\text{Asl}
be a formula without
\lor
and
\exists
, and let
x^{\prime}
be a fresh variable. Then,

(1)

for some
p
,
q\land(x\mapsto x^{\prime}*\text{true})\equiv x\mapsto x^{\prime}*p

(2)

for some
p
,
q\land(x\not\mapsto{}*\text{true})\equiv x\not\mapsto{}*p

Proof.

The proof is by induction on the structure of
q
.

Case

q=\text{false}

Take
p=\text{false}
.

Case

q=\text{true}

Take
p=\text{true}
.

Case

q=q_{1}\land q_{2}

We consider point (1) first. By inductive hypothesis, there exists
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
such that

\displaystyle q_{1}\land(x\mapsto x^{\prime}*\text{true})

\displaystyle\equiv x\mapsto x^{\prime}*p_{1}

\displaystyle q_{2}\land(x\mapsto x^{\prime}*\text{true})

\displaystyle\equiv x\mapsto x^{\prime}*p_{2}

so that

\displaystyle q\land(x\mapsto x^{\prime}*\text{true})

\displaystyle\equiv q_{1}\land q_{2}\land(x\mapsto x^{\prime}*\text{true})

\displaystyle\equiv q_{1}\land(x\mapsto x^{\prime}*\text{true})\land q_{2}\land(x\mapsto x^{\prime}*\text{true})

\displaystyle\equiv(x\mapsto x^{\prime}*p_{1})\land(x\mapsto x^{\prime}*p_{2})

\displaystyle\equiv x\mapsto x^{\prime}*(p_{1}\land p_{2})

where we used Lemma
A.6
.7 for the last equivalence.
The case for point (2) is analogous using Lemma
A.6
.8 instead.

Case

Both points follow from Lemma
A.6

p_{1}=\text{true}
and
p_{2}=x\mapsto x^{\prime}
(resp.
p_{2}=x\not\mapsto{}

Case

\displaystyle\{\hskip-2.0pt|z\not\mapsto{}\land(x\not\mapsto{}*\text{true})|\hskip-2.0pt\}

\displaystyle=\{(s,h)\,|\,(s,h)\in\{\hskip-2.0pt|z\not\mapsto{}|\hskip-2.0pt\},(s,h)\in\{\hskip-2.0pt|x\not\mapsto{}*\text{true}|\hskip-2.0pt\}\}

\displaystyle=\{(s,h)\,|\,h=[s(x)\mapsto\bot],s(z)=s(x)\}

\displaystyle=\{\hskip-2.0pt|z=x\land x\not\mapsto{}|\hskip-2.0pt\}

Case

q=q_{1}*q_{2}

We consider point (1) first. By inductive hypothesis, there exists

and
p_{2}
such that

\displaystyle q_{1}\land(x\mapsto x^{\prime}*\text{true})

\displaystyle\equiv x\mapsto x^{\prime}*p_{1}

\displaystyle q_{2}\land(x\mapsto x^{\prime}*\text{true})

\displaystyle\equiv x\mapsto x^{\prime}*p_{2}

Take

. We have

\displaystyle\{\hskip-2.0pt|x\mapsto x^{\prime}*p|\hskip-2.0pt\}=\{\hskip-2.0pt|x\mapsto x^{\prime}*p_{1}*q_{2}|\hskip-2.0pt\}\cup\{\hskip-2.0pt|x\mapsto x^{\prime}*q_{1}*p_{2}|\hskip-2.0pt\}

\displaystyle\{\hskip-2.0pt|q\land(x\mapsto x^{\prime}*\text{true})|\hskip-2.0pt\}

\displaystyle=\{\hskip-2.0pt|(q_{1}*q_{2})\land(x\mapsto x^{\prime}*\text{true})|\hskip-2.0pt\}

\displaystyle=\{(s,h)\,|\,(s,h)\in\{\hskip-2.0pt|x\mapsto x^{\prime}*\text{true}|\hskip-2.0pt\},h=h_{1}\bullet h_{2},(s,h_{1})\in\{\hskip-2.0pt|q_{1}|\hskip-2.0pt\},(s,h_{2})\in\{\hskip-2.0pt|q_{2}|\hskip-2.0pt\}\}

Proof of Theorem

.

First we fix

\mathsf{r}
, that there exists
p\in\text{Asl}
such that
\{\hskip-2.0pt|p|\hskip-2.0pt\}=\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket\{\hskip-2.0pt|q|\hskip-2.0pt\}

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}p\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

in a normal form: we rename all quantified variables to fresh names, and use Lemma

does not contain
\lor
and

.

, so we conclude the inductive step.

\{\hskip-2.0pt|p|\hskip-2.0pt\}=\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}_{1}}\rrbracket\{\hskip-2.0pt|t|\hskip-2.0pt\}
and
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}p\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}_{1}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}t\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
is provable. Now
\{\hskip-2.0pt|p|\hskip-2.0pt\}=\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}_{1}}\rrbracket\{\hskip-2.0pt|t|\hskip-2.0pt\}=\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}_{1}}\rrbracket\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}_{2}}\rrbracket\{\hskip-2.0pt|q|\hskip-2.0pt\}=\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}_{1};\mathsf{r}_{2}}\rrbracket\{\hskip-2.0pt|q|\hskip-2.0pt\}
and we can prove
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}p\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}_{1};\mathsf{r}_{2}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
using
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{seq}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
and the two proofs given by the inductive hypothesis.

Case

\mathsf{r}=\mathsf{r}_{1}\boxplus\mathsf{r}_{2}

For
i=1,2
, by inductive hypothesis on
\mathsf{r}_{i}
we know that there exists an assertion
p_{i}\in\text{Asl}
such that
\{\hskip-2.0pt|p_{i}|\hskip-2.0pt\}=\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}_{i}}\rrbracket\{\hskip-2.0pt|q|\hskip-2.0pt\}
and
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}p_{i}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}_{i}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
is provable. Therefore
\{\hskip-2.0pt|p_{1}\lor p_{2}|\hskip-2.0pt\}=\{\hskip-2.0pt|p_{1}|\hskip-2.0pt\}\cup\{\hskip-2.0pt|p_{2}|\hskip-2.0pt\}=\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}_{1}}\rrbracket\{\hskip-2.0pt|q|\hskip-2.0pt\}\cup\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}_{2}}\rrbracket\{\hskip-2.0pt|q|\hskip-2.0pt\}=\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}_{1}\boxplus\mathsf{r}_{2}}\rrbracket\{\hskip-2.0pt|q|\hskip-2.0pt\}
and we can prove
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}p_{1}\lor p_{2}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}_{1}\boxplus\mathsf{r}_{2}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
using
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{choice}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
and the two proofs given by the inductive hypothesis.

Now take any
p,q\in\text{Asl}
such that
\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket\{\hskip-2.0pt|q|\hskip-2.0pt\}\supseteq\{\hskip-2.0pt|p|\hskip-2.0pt\}
. By the proof above we know that there exists
p^{\prime}
such that
\llbracket\overleftarrow{\mathsf{r}}\rrbracket\{\hskip-2.0pt|q|\hskip-2.0pt\}=\{\hskip-2.0pt|p^{\prime}|\hskip-2.0pt\}
and
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}p^{\prime}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

\{\hskip-2.0pt|p|\hskip-2.0pt\}\subseteq\{\hskip-2.0pt|p^{\prime}|\hskip-2.0pt\}
, the implication
p\implies p^{\prime}
holds. Using the oracle for this implication we can prove the triple
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}p\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
using
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathsf{cons}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
and the proof of
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}p^{\prime}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\penalty\ \mathsf{r}\penalty\ \mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}q\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.8889pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
