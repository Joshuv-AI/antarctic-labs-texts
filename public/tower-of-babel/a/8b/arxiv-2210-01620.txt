SAM as an Optimal Relaxation of Bayes
Thomas Möllenhoff & Mohammad Emtiyaz Khan

Abstract
Sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) and related adversarial deep-learning methods can drastically improve generalization, but their underlying mechanisms are not yet fully understood. Here, we establish SAM as a relaxation of the Bayes objective where the expected negative-loss is replaced by the optimal convex lower bound, obtained by using the so-called Fenchel biconjugate. The connection enables a new Adam-like extension of SAM to automatically obtain reasonable uncertainty estimates, while sometimes also improving its accuracy. By connecting adversarial and Bayesian methods, our work opens a new path to robustness.

Introduction

Sharpness-aware minimization (SAM)
(
Foret et al. 2021
)
and related adversarial methods
(
Zheng et al. 2021
;
Wu et al. 2020
;
Kim et al. 2022
)
have been shown to improve generalization, calibration, and robustness in various applications of deep learning
(
Chen et al. 2022
;
Bahri et al. 2022
)
, but the reasons behind their success are not fully understood.
The original proposal of SAM was geared towards biasing training trajectories towards flat minima, and the effectiveness of such minima has various Bayesian explanations, for example, those relying on the optimization of description lengths
(
Hinton & Van Camp 1993
;
Hochreiter & Schmidhuber 1997
)
, PAC-Bayes bounds
(
Dziugaite & Roy 2017
;
Dziugaite & Roy 2018
;
Jiang et al. 2020
;
Alquier 2021
)
, or marginal likelihoods
(
Smith & Le 2018
)
.
However, SAM is not known to directly optimize any such Bayesian criteria, even though some connections to PAC-Bayes do exist
(
Foret et al. 2021
)
.
The issue is that the ‘max-loss’ used in SAM fundamentally departs from a Bayesian-style ‘expected-loss’ under the posterior; see
Figure

1(a)
. The two methodologies are distinct, and little is known about their relationship.

Here, we establish a connection by using a relaxation of the Bayes objective where the expected negative-loss is replaced by the tightest convex lower bound. The bound is optimal, and obtained by Fenchel biconjugates which naturally yield the maximum loss used in SAM (
Figure

1(a)
).
From this, SAM can be seen as optimizing the relaxation of Bayes to find the mean of an isotropic Gaussian posterior while keeping the variance fixed.
Higher variances lead to smoother objectives, which biases the solution towards flatter regions (
Figure

1(b)
).
Essentially, the result connects SAM and Bayes through a Fenchel biconjugate that replaces the expected loss in Bayes by a maximum loss.

What do we gain by this connection? The generality of our result makes it possible to easily combine the complementary strengths of SAM and Bayes. For example, we show that the relaxed-Bayes objective can be used to learn the variance parameter, which yields an Adam-like extension of SAM (
Algorithm

1
). The variances are cheaply obtained from the vector that adapts the learning rate, and SAM’s hyperparameter is adjusted for each parameter dimension via the variance vector.
The extension improves the performance of SAM on standard benchmarks while giving comparable uncertainty estimates to the best Bayesian methods.

Our work complements similar extensions for SGD, RMSprop, and Adam from the Bayesian deep-learning community
(
Gal & Ghahramani 2016
;
Mandt et al. 2017
;
Khan et al. 2018
;
Osawa et al. 2019
)
.
So far there is no work on such connections between SAM and Bayes, except for a recent empirical study by
Kaddour et al. 2022
.

Husain & Knoblauch 2022
give an adversarial interpretation of Bayes, but it does not cover methods like SAM. Our work here is focused on connections to approximate Bayesian methods, which can have both theoretical and practical impact.
We discuss a new path to robustness by connecting the two fields of adversarial and Bayesian methodologies.

SAM as an Optimal Relaxation of Bayes

In SAM, the training loss
\ell(\bm{\theta})
is replaced by the maximum loss within a ball of radius
\rho>0
around the parameter
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{P}
, as shown below for an
\ell_{2}
-regularized problem,

\mathcal{E}_{\text{SAM}}(\bm{\theta};\rho,\delta)=\sup_{\|\bm{\epsilon}\|\leq\rho}\,\ell(\bm{\theta}+\bm{\epsilon})+\frac{\delta}{2}\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2},

(1)

with
\delta>0
as the regularization parameter.
The use of ‘maximum’ above differs from Bayesian strategies that use ‘expectation’, for example, the variational formulation by
Zellner 1988
,

\mathcal{L}(q)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}\sim q}\left[-\ell(\bm{\theta})\right]-\mathbb{D}_{\KLop}{}\left(q(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\,\|\,p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\right){},

(2)

where
q(\bm{\theta})
is the generalized posterior
(
Zhang 1999
;
Catoni 2007
)
,
p(\bm{\theta})
is the prior, and
\mathbb{D}_{\KLop}{}\left(\cdot\,\|\,\cdot\right){}
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence (KLD).
For an isotropic Gaussian posterior
q(\bm{\theta})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{m},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})
and prior
p(\bm{\theta})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\theta}\,|\,0,\mathbf{I}/\delta)
, the objective with respect to the mean
\mathbf{m}
becomes

\mathcal{E}_{\text{Bayes}}(\mathbf{m};\sigma,\delta)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\epsilon}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})}\left[\ell(\mathbf{m}+\bm{\epsilon}^{\prime})\right]+\frac{\delta}{2}\|\mathbf{m}\|^{2},

(3)

which closely resembles
Equation

1
, but with the maximum replaced by an expectation.
The above objective is obtained by simply plugging in the posterior and prior, then collecting the terms that depend on
\mathbf{m}
, and finally rewriting
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}+\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}^{\prime}
.

There are some resemblances between the two objectives which indicate that they might be connected. For example, both use local neighborhoods: the isotropic Gaussian can be seen as a ‘softer’ version of the ‘hard-constrained’ ball used in SAM. The size of the neighborhood is decided by their respective parameters (
\sigma
or
\rho
in
Figure

1(a)
).
But, apart from these resemblances, are there any deeper connections? Here, we answer this question.

We will show that
Equation

1
is equivalent to optimizing with respect to
\mathbf{m}
an
optimal
convex relaxation of
Equation

3
while keeping
\sigma
fixed. See
Section

2.2
where an equivalence is drawn between
\sigma
and
\rho
, revealing similarities between the mechanisms used in SAM and Bayes to favor flatter regions.
Figure

1(b)
shows an illustration where the relaxation is shown to upper bound the expected loss.

2.1
From expected-loss to max-loss via Fenchel biconjugates

We use results from convex duality to connect expected-loss to max-loss. Our main result relies on Fenchel conjugates
(
Hiriart-Urruty & Lemaréchal 2001
)
, also known as convex conjugates. Consider a function
f(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{u}$}$})
, defined over
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{u}$}$}\in\mathcal{U}
and taking values in the extended real line. Given a dual space
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}\in\mathcal{V}
, the Fenchel biconjugate
f^{**}
and conjugate
f^{*}
are defined as follows,

f^{**}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{u}$}$})=\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}}^{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{V}}\,\,\langle\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}^{\prime},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{u}$}$}\rangle-f^{*}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}^{\prime}),\quad\quad~~f^{*}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}^{\prime})=\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{u}$}$}}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}\in\,\mathcal{U}}\,\,\langle\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{u}$}$}^{\prime\prime},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}^{\prime}\rangle-{f(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{u}$}$}^{\prime\prime})},

(4)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product.
Note that
f
need not be convex, but both
f^{*}
and
f^{**}
are convex functions. Moreover, the biconjugate is the
optimal
convex lower bound:
f(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{u}$}$})\geq f^{**}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{u}$}$})
.

We will use the conjugate functions to connect the max-loss in SAM to the expected-loss involving regular, minimal exponential-family distributions
(
Wainwright et al. 2008

2.2
SAM as an optimal relaxation of the Bayes objective

We will now show that a minimizer of the SAM objective in
Equation

1
can be recovered by minimizing a relaxation of the Bayes objective where expected-loss
f
is replaced by its convex lower bound
f^{**}
. The new objective, which we call relaxed-Bayes, always lower bounds the Bayes objective of
Equation

2
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}\in\mathcal{M}}\,\mathcal{L}(q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}})\,\geq\,\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}\in\mathcal{M}}\,\,f^{**}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$})-\mathbb{D}_{\KLop}{}\left(q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\,\|\,p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\right){}

(11)

\displaystyle{=\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}\in\mathcal{M}}\left\{\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}},\sigma}\,\,-\left[\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}}}\,\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}+\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$})-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}\|^{2}\right]-\mathbb{D}_{\KLop}{}\left(q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}(\bm{\theta})\,\|\,\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}p(\bm{\theta})e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}-\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}\|^{2}}\right){}+\log\mathcal{Z}\right\}},

where in the second line we substitute
f^{**}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$})
from (
9
), and merge
\mathbb{E}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}\sim q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}}[\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}\|^{2}]
with the KLD term.
\mathcal{Z}
denotes the normalizing constant of the second distribution in the KLD.
The relaxed-Bayes objective has a specific difference-of-convex (DC) structure because both
f^{**}
and KLD are convex with respect to
\bm{\mu}
. Optimizing the relaxed-Bayes objective relates to double-loop algorithms where the inner-loop uses max-loss to optimize for
(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$},\sigma)
, while the outer loop solves for
\bm{\mu}
. We will use the structure to simplify and connect to SAM.

Due to the DC structure, it is possible to switch the order of maximization with respect to
\bm{\mu}
and
(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$},\sigma)
, and eliminate
\bm{\mu}
by a direct maximization
(
Toland 1978
)
. The maximum occurs when the KLD term is 0 and the two distributions in its arguments are equal.
A detailed derivation is in
Appendix

C
, which gives us a
minimization
problem over
(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$},\sigma)

\mathcal{E}_{\text{relaxed}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$},\sigma;\delta^{\prime})=\left[\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}}}\,\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}+\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$})-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}\|^{2}\right]+{\underbrace{\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{2}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}\|^{2}-\frac{P}{2}\log(\sigma^{2}\delta^{\prime})}_{=-\log\mathcal{Z}}},

(12)

where
\delta^{\prime}=1/(\sigma^{2}+1/\delta_{0})
and we use a Gaussian prior
p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\,|\,0,\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{I}$}$}/\delta_{0})
. This objective uses max-loss in the proximal form,
which is similar to the SAM loss in
Equation

1
with the difference that the hard-constraint is replaced by a softer penalty term.

The theorem below is our main result which shows that, for a given
\rho
, a minimizer of the SAM objective in
Equation

1
can be recovered by optimizing
Equation

12
with respect to
\mathbf{m}
while keeping
\sigma
fixed.

{theorem}
[]

For every
(\rho,\delta)
, there exist
(\sigma,\delta^{\prime})
such that

\arg\min_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}\in\Theta}\,\,\mathcal{E}_{\text{SAM}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$};\rho,\delta)=\arg\min_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}\in\Theta}\,\,\mathcal{E}_{\text{relaxed}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$},\sigma;\delta^{\prime}),

(13)

assuming that the SAM-perturbation satisfies
\|\bm{\epsilon}\|=\rho
at a stationary point.

A proof is given in
Appendix

D
and essentially follows from the equivalences between proximal and trust-region formulations.
The result establishes SAM as a special case of relaxed-Bayes, and shows that
\rho
and
\sigma
play the exactly same role.
This formalizes the intuitive resemblance discussed at the beginning of
Section

2
, and suggests that, in both Bayes and SAM, flatter regions are preferred for higher
\sigma
because such values give rise to smoother objectives (
Figure

1(b)
).

The result can be useful for combining complementary strengths of SAM and Bayes. For example, uncertainty estimation in SAM can now be performed by estimating
\sigma
through the relaxed-Bayes objective. Another way is to exploit the DC structure in
\bm{\mu}
-space to devise new optimization procedures to improve SAM. In
Section

3
, we will demonstrate both of these improvements of SAM.

It might also be possible to use SAM to improve Bayes, something we would like to explore in the future. For Bayes, it still is challenging to get a good performance in deep learning while optimizing for both mean and variance (despite some recent success
(
Osawa et al. 2019
)
). Often, non-Bayesian methods are used to set the variance parameter
(
Plappert et al. 2018
;
Fortunato et al. 2018
;
Bisla et al. 2022
)
. The reasons behind the bad performance remain unclear. It is possible that a simplistic posterior is to blame
(
Foong et al. 2020
;
Fortuin et al. 2022
;
Coker et al. 2022
)
, but
our result suggests to not disregard the opposite hypothesis
(
Farquhar et al. 2020
)
.
SAM works well, despite using a simple posterior, and it is possible that the poor performance of Bayes is due to poor algorithms, not simplistic posteriors. We believe that our result can be used to borrow algorithmic techniques from SAM to improve Bayes, and we hope to explore this in the future.

3
Bayesian-SAM to Obtain Uncertainty Estimates for SAM

In this section, we will show an application of our result to improve an aspect of SAM. We will derive a new algorithm that can automatically obtain reasonable uncertainty estimates for SAM. We will do so by optimizing the relaxed-Bayes objective to get
\sigma
. A straightforward way is to directly optimize
eq.

12
, but we will use the approach followed by
Khan et al. 2018
, based on the natural-gradient method of
Khan & Rue 2021
, called the Bayesian learning rule (BLR). This approach has
been shown to work well on large problems, and is more promising because it leads to Adam-like algorithms, enabling us to leverage existing deep-learning techniques to improve training
(
Osawa et al. 2019
)
, for example, by using momentum and data augmentation.

To learn variances on top of SAM, we will use a multivariate Gaussian posterior
q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\,|\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{V}$}$})
with mean
\mathbf{m}
and a diagonal covariance
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{V}$}$}=\diag(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$})^{-1}
, where
\mathbf{s}
is a vector of precision values (inverse of the variances). The posterior is more expressive than the isotropic Gaussian used in the previous section, and is expected to give better uncertainty estimates. As before, we will use a Gaussian prior
p(\bm{\theta})=\mathcal{N}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\,|\,0,\mathbf{I}/\delta_{0})
.

The BLR uses gradients with respect to
\bm{\mu}
to update
\bm{\lambda}
. The pair
(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$})
is defined similarly to
Equation

6
. We will use the update given in
Khan & Rue 2021
which when applied to
Equation

11
gives us,

\bm{\lambda}\leftarrow(1-\alpha)\bm{\lambda}+\alpha\left[\nabla f^{**}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$})+\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{0}\right],

(14)

where
\alpha>0
is the learning rate and
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{0}=\left(0,-\delta_{0}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{I}$}$}/2\right)
is the natural parameter of
p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
. This is obtained by using the fact that
\nabla_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}\mathbb{D}_{\KLop}{}\left(q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\,\|\,p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\right){}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{0}
; see
Khan & Rue 2021
.
For
\alpha=1
, the update is equivalent to the difference-of-convex (DC) algorithm
(
Tao & An 1997
)
. The BLR generalizes this procedure by using
\alpha<1
, and automatically exploits the DC structure in
\bm{\mu}
-space, unlike the naive direct minimization of (
12
) with gradient descent.

The update (
14
) requires
\nabla f^{**}
, which is obtained by maximizing a variant of
Equation

9
, that replaces the isotropic covariance by a diagonal one. For a scalable method, we solve them inexactly by using the ’local-linearization’ approximation used in SAM, along with an additional approximation to do single block-coordinate descent step to optimize for the variances. The rest of the derivations is similar to the previous applications of the BLR; see

Khan et al. 2018
;
Osawa et al. 2019
.
The details are in
Appendix

E
, and the resulting algorithm, which we call Bayesian-SAM is shown in
Algorithm

1
.

bSAM differs slightly from a direct application of Adam
(
Kingma & Ba 2015
)
on the SAM objective, but uses the same set of hyperparameters. The differences are highlighted in
Algorithm

1
, where bSAM is obtained by replacing the blue boxes with the red boxes next to them. For SAM with Adam, we use the method discussed in
Bahri et al. 2022
;
Kwon et al. 2021
for language models. The main change is in line 5, where the perturbation
\bm{\epsilon}
is adapted by using a
vector

\mathbf{s}
, as
opposed to SAM which uses a
scalar

\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{g}$}$}\|
. Modifications in line 3 and 8 are aimed to improve the variance estimates because with just Adam we may not get good variances; see the discussion in
Khan et al. 2018
. Finally, the modification in line 9 is due to the Newton-like updates arising from BLR
(
Khan & Rue 2021
)
where
\mathbf{s}
is used instead of
\sqrt{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}}
to scale the gradient as explained in
Appendix

E
.

Due to their overall structural similarity, the bSAM algorithm has a similar computational
complexity to Adam with SAM. The only additional overhead compared to
SAM-type methods is the computation of a random noise perturbation which is
negligible compared to gradient computations.

5
Discussion

In this paper, we show a connection between SAM and Bayes through a Fenchel biconjugate. When the expected negative loss in the Bayes objective is replaced by the biconjugate, we obtain a relaxed Bayesian objective, which involves a maximum loss, similar to SAM. We showed that SAM can be seen as optimizing the mean while keeping variance fixed. We then derived an extension where the variances are optimized using a Bayesian learning algorithm applied to the relaxation. The numerical
results show that the new variant improves both uncertainty estimates and generalization, as expected of a method that combines the strengths of Bayes and SAM.

We expect this result to be useful for researchers interested in designing new robust methods. The principles used in SAM are related to adversarial robustness which is different from Bayes. We show that the Fenchel conjugate is the bridge between the two, and we used it to obtain an extension of SAM. Since SAM gives an excellent performance on many deep-learning benchmark, the techniques used there will be useful in improving Bayesian methods. Finally, the use of convex duality
can be useful to improve theoretical understanding of SAM-like methods. It is possible to use the results from convex-optimization literature to study and improve adversarial as well as Bayesian methods. Our work may open such directions and provide a new path for research in improving robustness.
Expectations with respect to distributions arise naturally in many other problems, and often sampling is used to approximate them. Our paper gives a different way to approximate such expectations using convex optimization rather than sampling. Such problems may also benefit from our results.

For this case, our derivation proceeds in two steps, where we show that both the following two inequalities are true at the same time,

\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}\sim q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}^{\prime\prime}}}[J(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})]\leq J_{*},\quad\quad\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}\sim q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}^{\prime\prime}}}[J(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})]\geq J_{*}.

This is only possible when the two are equal, which will establish the result.

To prove the first inequality, we take a sequence
\{\bm{\theta}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}

for which
J(\bm{\theta}_{t})\to J_{*}
when sending
t\to\infty
. By the definition of supremum, there always exists such a
sequence. From the sequence
\{\bm{\theta}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
we will now construct a
sequence
\{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}_{t}^{\prime\prime}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
for which the expectation of
J(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
also converges to

J_{*}
. This will establish that the supremum
over
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}^{\prime\prime}
is larger or equal than the supremum over
\bm{\theta}
. It is only
larger or equal, since hypothetically there could be another sequence
of
\{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}_{t}^{\prime\prime}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
which achieves
higher value. We will see soon that this cannot be the case.

We use the sequence
\{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}_{t}^{\prime\prime}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
where
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}_{t}^{\prime\prime}=(\bm{\theta}_{t},\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}_{t}+\bm{\theta}_{t}\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\top})
with
\{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{S}_{+}^{P\times P}
is taken in the space of
symmetric positive-definite matrices with the sequence such that
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}_{t}\to 0
.
Now, we can see that the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}\sim q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}_{t}^{\prime\prime}}}[J(\bm{\theta})]
converges to the supremum as follows:

\begin{split}\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}\sim q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}_{t}^{\prime\prime}}}[J(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})]&=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}}\sim\text{\mbox{${\cal N}$}}(0,\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{I}$}$}})}\left[J\left(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}+\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}_{t}^{1/2}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}\right)\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}}\sim\text{\mbox{${\cal N}$}}(0,\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{I}$}$}})}\left[\lim_{t\to\infty}J\left(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}+\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}_{t}^{1/2}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}\right)\right]\\
&=\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}\in\text{\real}^{P}}J(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}).\end{split}

In the above steps, we used the fact that
J
is
Gaussian-integrable and
continuous. A sufficient condition for the integrability is that
\ell(\bm{\theta})
is lower-bounded and majorized by quadratics. With this, we have established that the supremum over
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}^{\prime\prime}
is at least as large as the one over
\bm{\theta}
.

To prove the second inequality, we will show that the supremum over

\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}^{\prime\prime}
is also less or equal
than the supremum over
\bm{\theta}
. To see that, we note that

J(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\leq J_{*}
for all
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\Theta
. This implies that for any distribution
q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}^{\prime\prime}}
, we have that

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\sim q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}^{\prime\prime}}}[J(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})]\leq J_{*}
.
Since the inequality holds for any distribution, we also have that

\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{M}}~\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\sim q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}^{\prime\prime}}}[J(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})]\leq J_{*}
. Having established both inequalities,
we now know that the suprema in (
10
) agrees whenever the supremum over
\bm{\theta}
is finite.

When
J_{*}
is infinite:
For such cases, we will show that
\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}\sim q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}^{\prime\prime}}}[J(\bm{\theta})]=\infty
, which will complete the proof. Again, by
definition of supremum, there exists a sequence
\{\bm{\theta}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
for which
J(\bm{\theta}_{t})\to\infty
when
t\to\infty
. This means that for any
M>0
there is a
parameter
\bm{\theta}_{t(M)}
in the sequence such that
J(\bm{\theta}_{t(M)})>M
. Now from the previous part of the proof, we know that we can construct a
sequence
q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}_{t}^{\prime\prime}}
of Gaussians whose expectation converges to

J(\bm{\theta}_{t(M)})
. Since the above holds for any
M>0
, the
supremum over the expectation is also
+\infty
.

Conditions for existence of
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}^{\prime}
for which
J_{*}
is finite:

The final step of our proof is to give the conditions when there exist at
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}^{\prime}
for which
J_{*}
is finite, and therefore the result is non-trivial.

Since
\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
is majorized by a quadratic function, there
exists a constant
c\in\real
, vector
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{b}$}$}\in\real^{P}
and matrix

\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{A}$}$}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{P\times P}
such that the following bound holds:

\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\leq c+\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{b}$}$}^{\top}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}+\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}^{\top}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{A}$}$}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}.

Then for the candidate
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}^{\prime}=(-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{b}$}$},-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{A}$}$})
we know that

J(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})+\langle\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}^{\prime},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\rangle\leq c

has a finite upper bound for any
\bm{\theta}
. Hence, the supremum is
finite for this choice of
\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}\in\Omega
.

Domain of
f^{*}(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})
:

For a Gaussian distribution, valid
(\bm{\lambda}_{1}^{\prime},\bm{\lambda}_{2}^{\prime})\in\Omega
satisfy the constraint
\bm{\lambda}_{2}^{\prime}\prec 0
.
We now show that if
\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}\notin\Omega
, then
f^{*}(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})=+\infty
. Since
\bm{\lambda}_{2}^{\prime}\nprec 0
, there exists a direction
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{d}$}$}\neq 0
such that
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{d}$}$}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}_{2}^{\prime}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{d}$}$}\geq 0
and
{\bm{\lambda}_{1}^{\prime}}^{\top}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{d}$}$}\geq 0
.
Taking the sequence
\{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
with
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}=t\cdot\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{d}$}$}
we see

J(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})=t{\bm{\lambda}_{1}^{\prime}}^{\top}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{d}$}$}+t^{2}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{d}$}$}^{\top}{\bm{\lambda}_{2}^{\prime}}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{d}$}$}+\ell(t\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{d}$}$})\geq\ell(t\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{d}$}$})\to\infty~\text{ as }t\to\infty,

since coercivity of
\ell(\bm{\theta})
implies that
\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t})\to\infty
for any sequence
\{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
with
\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{t}\|\to\infty
.
∎

Some additional comments on the assumptions used in the theorem are
in order.

1.

Any reasonable loss function that one would want to minimize is lower-bounded, so this assumption is always satisfied in practice.

2.

The existence of an upper-bounding quadratic holds for functions with Lipschitz
continuous gradient, which is a standard assumption in optimization
known as the descent lemma. In practice, our conjugates are also
applicable to cases where this assumption is not satisfied. In such a
case, one can consider the conjugate function only in a local
neighbourhood around the current solution by restricting the set of
admissible
\bm{\theta}
in the supremum. It may not matter that
the bounding quadratic will eventually intersect the loss far away
outside of our current region of interest.

3.

We assumed that
\ell(\bm{\theta})
is continuous
since virtually all loss functions used in deep learning are
continuous. Interestingly, if
\ell(\bm{\theta})
is not continuous, the
conjugate function
f^{*}
will involve its semicontinous relaxation.

4.

The coercivity assumption on
\ell(\bm{\theta})
is another standard assumption in optimization. For non-coercive losses, there could exist quadratic upper-bounds which are flat in some direction. This would lead to a dual variable
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}^{\prime}
at the boundary of
\Omega
.

The above
Section

2.1
also holds for other exponential family distributions such as scalar Bernoulli and Gamma distributions, but we do not give more details here because they are not relevant for connecting to SAM. There are also cases where the result does not hold. The simplest case
is a Gaussian with fixed covariance.
The above proof does not work there, because the
closure (see e.g.,
Csiszár & Matúš 2005
;
Malagò et al. 2011
) of the family of distributions does not contain all Dirac delta distributions. Intuitively, the requirement is that we should be able to approach any delta as a limit of a sequence of members of the family, which excludes the fixed-covariance case.

Appendix C
Derivation of the Equivalent Relaxed Objective (

)

Inserting the biconjugate (
9
) into the relaxed Bayes objective (
11
) yields

\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}\in\mathcal{M}}\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}},\sigma}\,\,-\left[\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}}}\,\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}+\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$})-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}\|^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}\sim q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}}\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}\|^{2}+\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})-\log q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\right],

where we expanded the KLD as
\mathbb{D}_{\KLop}{}\left(q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\,\|\,p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\right){}=\mathbb{E}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}\sim q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}}[\log q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})]-\mathbb{E}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}\sim q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}}[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})]
.

Interchanging the order of maximization and noticing that the first term does not depend on
\bm{\mu}
, we can find a closed-form expression for the maximization in
\bm{\mu}
as follows:

\displaystyle\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}\in\mathcal{M}}~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}\sim q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}}\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}\|^{2}+\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})-\log q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}\sim q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}}\left[\log\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}\|^{2}\right)p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})-\log q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\right]+\log\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle=\log\mathcal{Z}-\inf_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{D}_{\KLop}{}\left(q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\,\|\,\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}\|^{2}\right)p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\right){}

\displaystyle=\log\mathcal{Z}.

In the last step,
the KLD is attained at zero with
q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}\|^{2}\right)p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
.

Finally, we compute
\mathcal{Z}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle=\int\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}\|^{2}\right)p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\,\mathrm{d}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}

\displaystyle=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{P/2}\int(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-P/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}\|^{2}\right)p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})\,\mathrm{d}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}

\displaystyle=(\sigma^{2})^{P/2}(\sigma^{2}+1/\delta_{0})^{-P/2}\exp\left(-\frac{\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}\|^{2}}{2(\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta})}\right),

where in the last step we inserted the normalization constant for the product of the two Gaussians
\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\bm{\theta}\,|\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$},\sigma^{2}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{I}$}$})
and
\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\bm{\theta}\,|\,0,\frac{1}{\delta_{0}}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{I}$}$})
as for example given in
Rasmussen & Williams 2006
.

This gives the following expression

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle=\frac{P}{2}\log(\sigma^{2})-\frac{P}{2}\log(\sigma^{2}+1/\delta_{0})-\frac{1}{2(\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta_{0}})}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{P}{2}\log(\sigma^{2})+\frac{P}{2}\log(\delta^{\prime})-\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{2}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}\|^{2},

(18)

where
\delta^{\prime}=1/(\sigma^{2}+1/\delta_{0})
.

Switching to a minimization over
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$},\sigma^{2}
and inserting the result for the exact
\bm{\mu}
-solution (
18
) leads to the final objective

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{m},\sigma^{2}}~\left[\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}}}\,\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}+\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$})-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}\|^{2}\right]+\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{2}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}\|^{2}-\frac{P}{2}\log(\sigma^{2})-\frac{P}{2}\log(\delta^{\prime}),

which is the energy function in (
12
).

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem
2.2

See
2.2

Proof.

We will show that any stationary point of
\mathcal{E}_{\text{SAM}}
is also a stationary point of
\mathcal{E}_{\text{relaxed}}
when
\sigma
and
\delta^{\prime}
are chosen appropriately. The correspondence between the stationary points follows directly from the equivalences between proximal and trust-region formulations
(
Parikh & Boyd 2013
, Sec. 3.4)
.

To show this, we first write the two objectives in terms of perturbation
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}^{\prime}
and
\bm{\epsilon}
respectively,

\displaystyle\min_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}}\,\,\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}}^{\prime}}\,

\displaystyle\,\,\ell(\mathbf{m}+\bm{\epsilon}^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\bm{\epsilon}^{\prime}\|^{2}+\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{2}\|\mathbf{m}\|^{2},

\displaystyle\min_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}}\,\,\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}}}\,

\displaystyle\,\,\ell(\bm{\theta}+\bm{\epsilon})-\mathbf{i}\{\|\bm{\epsilon}\|\leq\rho\}+\frac{\delta}{2}\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2},

where
\mathbf{i}\{\|\cdot\|\leq\rho\}
is the indicator function
which is zero inside the
\ell_{2}
-ball of radius
\rho
and
+\infty

otherwise.

Since these are min-max problems, we need an appropriate notion of “stationary point”, which we consider here to be a local Nash equilibirum
(
Jin et al. 2020
, Prop. 3)
. At such points, the first derivatives in
\bm{\theta}
and
\bm{\epsilon}^{\prime}
(or
\bm{\epsilon}
) vanish. Taking the derivatives and setting them to 0, we get the following conditions for the two problems,

\displaystyle\delta^{\prime}\mathbf{m}_{*}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\ell(\mathbf{m}_{*}+\bm{\epsilon}^{\prime}_{*}),

\displaystyle\bm{\epsilon}^{\prime}_{*}=\sigma^{2}\,\nabla\ell(\mathbf{m}_{*}+\bm{\epsilon}^{\prime}_{*}),

\displaystyle\delta\bm{\theta}_{*}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\ell(\bm{\theta}_{*}+\bm{\epsilon}_{*}),

\displaystyle\bm{\epsilon}_{*}=\frac{\rho}{\mu}\nabla\ell(\bm{\theta}_{*}+\bm{\epsilon}_{*}),

for some constant multiplier
\mu>0
. Here, we used the assumption that the constraint
\|\bm{\epsilon}_{*}\|\leq\rho
is active at our optimal point. Since the constraint is active,
the negative gradient is an element of the (non-trivial) normal cone,
which gives us the multiplier
\mu
in the second line above.

The two equations are structurally equivalent. Any pair
(\mathbf{m}_{*},\bm{\epsilon}_{*}^{\prime})

satisfying the first line also satisfies the second line for
\sigma^{2}=\rho/\mu
and
\delta^{\prime}=\delta
. The opposite is also true when using the pair
(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}_{*},\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}_{*})
in the first line, which proves the theorem.
We assume that the constraint in the perturbation is active, that is,
\|\bm{\epsilon}_{*}\|=\rho
. This assumption would be violated if there exists a local maximum within a
\rho
-ball around the parameter
\bm{\theta}_{*}
. However, such a local maximum is unlikely to exist since the parameter
\bm{\theta}_{*}
is determined by minimization and tends to lie inside a flat minimum.
∎

Appendix E
Derivation of the bSAM Algorithm

In this section of the appendix, we show how the natural gradient descent update (
14
) leads us to the proposed bSAM algorithm shown in
Algorithm

1
.
As mentioned in the main text, for bSAM we consider a generalized setting where our Gaussian distribution
q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=\mathcal{N}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\,|\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{V}$}$})
has diagonal covariance. This corresponds to the following setting:

\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})=(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}^{\top}),\quad\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}=\left(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}^{2}+\text{diag}(\mathbf{s})^{-1}\right),\quad\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}=\left(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}\cdot\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$},-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\text{diag}(\mathbf{s})\right).

(19)

Here,
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
is the mean and
\text{diag}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$})=\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{V}$}$}^{-1}
denotes the entries of the inverse diagonal covariance matrix. All operations (squaring, multiplication) are performed entrywise.

E.1
The gradient of the biconjugate

First, we discuss how to compute that gradient of the Fenchel biconjugate, as the BLR update (
14
) requires it in every iteration.
The diagonal Gaussian setup leads to a slightly generalized expression for the Fenchel biconjugate function:

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle f^{**}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$})=\min_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P},\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{b}$}$}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{P}}\left[~\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}}~\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}+\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$})-\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}\|^{2}~\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}}\sim q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$})\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\min_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P},\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{b}$}$}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{P}}\left[~\sup_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}}~\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}+\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$})-\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}\|^{2}~\right]+\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$})\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\tr(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}^{-1}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{V}$}$}),

(20)

where
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}=\text{diag}(1/\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{b}$}$})
denotes the diagonal covariance matrix with
\mathbf{1}/\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{b}$}$}
on its diagonal.

It follows from results in convex analysis, for instance from
Rockafellar & Wets 1998
, that
\nabla f^{**}(\bm{\mu})=(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}_{*}^{-1}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{*},-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{b}$}$}_{*})
, that is, the gradient of the biconjugate can be constructed from the optimal solution
(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{*},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{b}$}$}_{*})
of the optimization problem (
20
).

In (
20
) there are three optimization steps: over
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}
, and
\mathbf{b}
. For the first two, we will make very similar approximation to what is used for SAM
(
Foret et al. 2021
)
, and simply add an additional optimization over
\mathbf{b}
.
To simplify computation, we will make an additional approximation. Instead of an iterative joint minimization in
\mathbf{m}
and
\mathbf{b}
, we will perform a single block-coordinate descent step where we first minimize in
\mathbf{m}
for fixed
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{b}$}$}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}
and then afterwards in
\mathbf{b}
for fixed
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}
. This will give rise to a simple algorithm that works well in practice.

Optimization with respect to
\bm{\epsilon}
.

To simplify the supremum in
\bm{\epsilon}
we will use SAM’s technique of local linearization
(
Foret et al. 2021
)
with one difference. Instead of using the current solution to linearize, we will sample a random linearization point
\bm{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\theta}\,|\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$},\diag(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$})^{-1})
. This is preferable from a Bayesian perspective and is also used in the other Bayesian variants
(
Khan et al. 2018
)
. Linearization at the sampled
\bm{\theta}
then gives us,

\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}+\bm{\epsilon})\approx\ell(\bm{\theta})+\langle\nabla\ell(\bm{\theta}),\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}+\bm{\epsilon}-\bm{\theta}\rangle.

Using this approximation in (
20
), we get a closed form
solution for
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{*}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}\nabla\ell(\bm{\theta})
.

Optimization with respect to
\mathbf{m}
.

To get a closed-form solution for the optimization in
\mathbf{m}
, we again consider a linearized loss:
\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}+\bm{\epsilon})\approx\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}+\bm{\epsilon})+\langle\nabla\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}+\bm{\epsilon}),\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}\rangle
. Moreover, we set
\bm{\epsilon}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{V}$}$}\nabla\ell(\bm{\theta})
, corresponding to a fixed
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{b}$}$}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}
. This reduces the optimization of (
20
) to

\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{*}=\argmin_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}}~\langle\nabla\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}+\bm{\epsilon}),\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}_{*}^{-1/2}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$})\|^{2},

(21)

which has closed form solution
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{*}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}_{*}\nabla\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}+\bm{\epsilon})
which we here leave to depend
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}_{*}
.

Optimization with respect to
\bm{\Sigma}
via
\mathbf{b}
.

Inserting
\bm{\epsilon}_{*}
into (
20
) with the linearized loss, the minimization over
\mathbf{b}
while fixing
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}
also has a simple closed-form solution as shown below,

\mathbf{b}_{*}=\argmin_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{P}}~\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}^{1/2}\nabla\ell(\bm{\theta})\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{P}\frac{\mathbf{b}_{i}}{\mathbf{s}_{i}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{s}\cdot\mathbf{g}^{2}}=|\mathbf{g}|\cdot\sqrt{\mathbf{s}},

(22)

where
\bm{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{m},\diag(\mathbf{s})^{-1})
and
\mathbf{g}=\nabla\ell(\bm{\theta})
.

E.2
Simplifying the natural-gradient updates

Having a way to approximate the biconjugate-gradient, we now rewrite and simplify the BLR update (
14
).
The derivations are largely similar to the ones used to derive the
variational online Newton approaches, see
Khan et al. 2017
;
Khan et al. 2018
;
Khan & Rue 2021
.

Inserting the parametrization (
19
) and our expression
\nabla f^{**}(\bm{\mu})=(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}_{*}^{-1}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{*},-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{b}$}$}_{*})
into the BLR update (
14
), we get
the following updates

\displaystyle\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}\cdot\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}

\displaystyle\leftarrow(1-\alpha)\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}\cdot\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}+\alpha\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}_{*}^{-1}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{*},

\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle\leftarrow(1-\alpha)\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}+\alpha(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{b}$}$}_{*}+\delta_{0}),

where we also used the prior
\bm{\lambda}_{0}=(0,-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\delta_{0}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{I}$}$})
. By changing the order of the updates, we can write
them in an equivalent form that resembles an adaptive gradient method:

\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle\leftarrow(1-\alpha)\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}+\alpha(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{b}$}$}_{*}+\delta_{0}),

(23)

\bm{\omega}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}-\alpha\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{V}$}$}\left[\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}_{*}^{-1}\left(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{*}\right)+\delta\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}\right].

(24)

Now, inserting the solutions
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\Sigma}$}$}_{*}^{-1}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{*})=\nabla\ell(\bm{\theta}+\bm{\epsilon})
and
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{b}$}$}_{*}=|\nabla\ell(\bm{\theta})|\cdot\sqrt{\mathbf{s}}
from
Equations

21
and
22
into these updates, we arrive at

\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle\leftarrow(1-\alpha)\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}+\alpha\left(|\nabla\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})|\cdot\sqrt{\mathbf{s}}+\delta_{0}\right),

(25)

\bm{\omega}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}-\alpha\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{V}$}$}\left(\nabla\ell(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}+\bm{\epsilon})+\delta_{0}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}\right),

(26)

where
\bm{\epsilon}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{V}$}$}\nabla\ell(\bm{\theta})
and
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\,|\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{V}$}$})
.

E.3
Further modifications for large-scale deep learning

Now we will consider
further modifications to the updates (
25
) – (
26
) to arrive at our bSAM method shown in
Algorithm

1
. These are similar to the ones used for VOGN
(
Osawa et al. 2019
)
.

First, we consider a stochastic approximation to deal with larger
problems. To that end, we now briefly describe the general learning setup. The loss is then written as a sum of
individual losses,
\ell(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{i}(\bm{\theta})
where
N
is
the number of datapoints. The
N
datapoints are disjointly partitioned into
K
minibatches

\{B_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
of
B
examples each.

We then apply a stochastic variant to the
bound
N\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}f_{i}^{**}(\bm{\mu})\leq f^{**}(\bm{\mu})
, where the individual functions are
f_{i}(\bm{\mu})=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}\sim q_{\bm{\mu}}}[-\ell_{j}(\bm{\theta})]
.
Essentially, the variant computes the gradient on an incrementally sampled
{f_{i}}^{**}
rather than considering the full dataset.

With
\mathbf{s}=N\hat{\mathbf{s}}
,
\delta_{0}=N\delta
this turns (
25
) – (
26
) into the following updates:

\displaystyle N\hat{\mathbf{s}}

\displaystyle\leftarrow(1-\alpha)N\hat{\mathbf{s}}+\alpha\left[N\delta+N\sqrt{N\hat{\mathbf{s}}}\cdot|\mathbf{g}|\right],

(27)

\bm{\omega}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}-\alpha\left[N\delta\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}+N\mathbf{g}_{\epsilon}\right]/(N\hat{\mathbf{s}}),~

(28)

where
\mathbf{g}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\nabla\ell_{j}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$})
,

\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\theta}$}$}\,|\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$},\text{diag}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\sigma}$}$}^{2}))
,
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\sigma}$}$}^{2}=(N\hat{\mathbf{s}})^{-1}
and
\mathcal{M}
denotes a randomly drawn minibatch.
Moreover, we have
\mathbf{g}_{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\nabla\ell_{j}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}+\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$})
with
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\epsilon}$}$}=\mathbf{g}/(N\hat{\mathbf{s}})=\rho\,\mathbf{g}/\hat{\mathbf{s}}
where we introduce the step-size
\rho>0
in the
adversarial step to absorb the
1/N
factor and to gain additional control over the perturbation strength.

Following the practical improvements of
Osawa et al. 2019
, we introduce
a damping parameter
\gamma>0
and seperate step-size
\beta_{2}>0
on the precision estimate, as well as an exponential moving average for the gradient
\beta_{1}>0
.
Under these changes, and dividing out the
N
-factors in (
27
) – (
28
) we arrive at the following method:

\displaystyle\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{g}$}$}_{m}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\beta_{1}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{g}$}$}_{m}+(1-\beta_{1})\left(\delta\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}+\mathbf{g}_{\epsilon}\right),

(29)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{s}}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\beta_{2}\hat{\mathbf{s}}+(1-\beta_{2})\left[\delta+\gamma+\sqrt{N\hat{\mathbf{s}}}\cdot|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{g}$}$}|\right],

(30)

\bm{\omega}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\omega}$}$}-\alpha\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{g}$}$}_{m}/\hat{\mathbf{s}},

(31)

with
\mathbf{g}
and
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{g}$}$}_{\epsilon}
defined as above. In practice, the method performed better without the

\sqrt{N}
-factor in the
\hat{\mathbf{s}}
-update. Renaming
\hat{\mathbf{s}}
as
\mathbf{s}
, the updates (
29
) – (
31
) are equivalent to bSAM (
Algorithm

1
).

F.3
Effect of using
f^{**}
instead of
f

In this section, we empirically check the effect of using
f^{**}
in the Bayesian objective instead of
f
. To that end, we compute an exact solution to the relaxed problem as well as the original problem (
2
) for a full Gaussian variational family.

For small problems, gradients of the biconjugate function can be easily approximated using convex optimization. To see this, notice that:

\displaystyle\nabla f^{**}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$})

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\text{$\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}^{\prime}\in\Omega$}}~f^{*}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}^{\prime})-\langle\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}^{\prime},\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}\rangle

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\text{$\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}^{\prime}\in\Omega$},c\in\mathbb{R}}~c-\langle\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}^{\prime},\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}\rangle~~\text{ s.t. }~~c\geq f^{*}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}^{\prime}).

(32)

This is a optimization problem with linear cost function and convex constraints. To implement the constraints, we approximate the loss by a maximum over linear functions, which is always possible for convex losses. The linear functions are first-order approximations of the loss at
L
points
\bm{\theta}_{i}\sim q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}}}
drawn from the current variational distribution.
Under this approximation, the constraint in (
32
) can be written as
L
seperate constraints for
i\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
as follows:

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}~\bm{\theta}^{\top}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{1}^{\prime}+\bm{\theta}^{\top}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{2}^{\prime}\bm{\theta}+\ell(\bm{\theta}_{i})+\langle\nabla\ell(\bm{\theta}_{i}),\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq c

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow-\frac{1}{4}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{1}^{\prime}+\nabla\ell(\bm{\theta}_{i}))^{\top}{\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{2}^{\prime}}^{-1}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{1}^{\prime}+\nabla\ell(\bm{\theta}_{i}))-c

\displaystyle\leq\langle\nabla\ell(\bm{\theta}_{i}),\bm{\theta}_{i}\rangle-\ell(\bm{\theta}_{i}).

(33)

The convex program (
32
) with convex constraints (
33
) is then solved using the software package CVXPY
(
Diamond & Boyd 2016
)

to obtain the gradient of the biconjugate function.

Having the gradients of the biconjugate available, our posterior is obtained by the Bayesian learning rule of
Khan & Rue 2021
,

\displaystyle\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}\leftarrow(1-\alpha)\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}+\alpha\begin{cases}\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{0}+\nabla f(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}),&\text{ for Bayes~\eqref{eq:elbo},}\\
\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\lambda}$}$}_{0}+\nabla f^{**}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\bm{\mu}$}$}),&\text{ for relaxed-Bayes~\eqref{eq:relaxedBayes}.}\end{cases}

(34)

Figure

7
compares both solutions obtained by iterating (
34
) on the logistic regression problem described in
Section

F.1
. We can see that
the relaxation induces a lower-bound as expected, but the relaxed solution is still a reasonable approximation to the true posterior.
