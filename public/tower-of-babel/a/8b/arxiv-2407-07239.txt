RotRNN \xspace : Modelling Long Sequences with Rotations
Rares Dolga, Kai Biegun, Jake Cunningham, David Barber

Abstract
Linear recurrent models, such as State Space Models (SSMs) and Linear Recurrent Units (LRUs), have recently shown state-of-the-art performance on long sequence modelling benchmarks. Despite their success, they come with a number of drawbacks, most notably their complex initialisation and normalisation schemes. In this work, we address some of these issues by proposing RotRNN \xspace – a linear recurrent model which utilises the convenient properties of rotation matrices. We show that RotRNN \xspace provides a simple model with fewer theoretical assumptions than prior works, with a practical implementation that remains faithful to its theoretical derivation, achieving comparable scores to the LRU and SSMs on several long sequence modelling datasets.

Introduction

State Space Models (SSMs)
Gu et al. 2021
;
Smith et al. 2023
;
Gupta et al. 2022
have shown state-of-the-art performance on long sequence modelling tasks. SSMs utilise a linear recurrent layer, avoiding the quadratic scaling problem of Transformers
Vaswani et al. 2017
and the vanishing and exploding gradients of classical Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)
(
Rumelhart et al. 1987
;
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997
;
Cho et al. 2014
;
Koutnik et al. 2014
)
. They can be viewed both as a linear RNN for fast inference and as a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) for efficient training.
Either implementation, however, involves computing series of matrix powers, leading to numerically unstable state values without careful initialisation and normalisation.
The Linear Recurrent Unit (LRU)
Orvieto et al. 2023
, on the other hand, introduces a diagonal matrix parameterisation motivated by the eigendecomposition of the recurrent state matrix.
While this makes initialisation simpler than previous works, normalising the LRU layer relies on restrictive assumptions, and practical implementations of the algorithm deviate from the theoretical derivation.
In this work, we propose an algorithm that tackles some of these issues of the LRU.
By parameterising the state matrix as a rotation, we are able to use less restrictive assumptions during normalisation, and allow for an implementation that faithfully reflects the theoretical motivation.
We provide a more in-depth comparison between our work and prior algorithms in App.
C
.

Background

State Space Models

SSMs
Gu et al. 2021
;
Smith et al. 2023
;
Gupta et al. 2022
are derived from continuous-time linear ODEs given by
\dot{x}(t)=A^{\prime}x(t)+B^{\prime}u(t)
and
y(t)=Cx(t)
, where
B^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}
is the input matrix,
A^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the state matrix,
C\in\mathbb{R}^{O\times N}
is the output matrix and
u(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
is the continuous-time input.
Since this is a linear time-invariant system, it can be easily discretised and expressed as the discrete recursion

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} x_{t}&=Ax_{t-1}+Bu_{t};\qquad y_{t}=Cx_{t}\end{aligned}

(1)

where
u_{t}
,
A
and
B
are discretised versions
u(t)
,
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
, respectively.
Eq.
1
is the foundation of the SSM layer in S4
Gu et al. 2021
and its variants
(
Gupta et al. 2022
;
Smith et al. 2023
;
Gu and Dao 2023
)
, allowing for powerful sequence modelling capabilities provided
A
is carefully initialised to ensure numerical stability.

Linear Recurrent Units

Instead of discretising a latent continuous-time ODE, the LRU
Orvieto et al. 2023
achieves similar performance by carefully initialising and normalising linear RNNs.
To derive the LRU parameterisation scheme, the first step is to perform eigenvalue decomposition on the matrix
A
. Since
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is a real, square matrix, it can be written (up to arbitrarily small perturbation of the entries
Axler 2024
) as

A=P\Lambda P^{-1}

(2)

where
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues and
P\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
is a complex-valued invertible matrix of eigenvectors.
Unrolling the recurrence in Eq.
1
as
x_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}A^{t-k}Bu_{k}
, premultiplying both sides by
P^{-1}
, and plugging in Eq.
2
, we obtain

\tilde{x}_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}\Lambda^{t-k}\tilde{B}u_{k};\qquad y_{t}=\tilde{C}\tilde{x}_{t}.

(3)

where
\tilde{x}_{t}=P^{-1}x_{t}
,
\tilde{B}=P^{-1}B
,
\tilde{C}=CP
. The LRU aims to directly learn the matrices
\tilde{B}
and
\tilde{C}
, along with the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}=\gamma_{j}e^{i\theta_{j}}
, for learnable parameters
\gamma_{j},\theta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
.

In practice, however, the LRU deviates from this theory. Firstly, it does not enforce the eigenvalues of the real matrix
A
to come in conjugate pairs
Axler 2024
.
Moreover, there is no constraint ensuring that
P
and
P^{-1}
are consistent, these matrices simply being absorbed into the learnable
\tilde{C}
and
\tilde{B}
.
Finally, LRU state normalisation (see App.
C
) only holds in the limit of infinite sequence length.

3
RotRNN
\xspace

In our method, which we call the
Rot
ational
R
ecurrent
N
eural
N
etwork (RotRNN
\xspace
), we aim to overcome the drawbacks of the LRU by parameterising the state matrix
A
as a rotation matrix.
The reason for this choice is three-fold: i) The magnitude of the eigenvalues of a rotation matrix are always 1 – this makes it easy to ensure that the recurrent state does not explode or vanish; ii) Rotation matrices can be generated smoothly from any real-valued matrix (see §
3.2
) and are robust to initialisation; iii) The orthogonality of rotation matrices allows us to derive a simple normalisation scheme without infinite sequence length assumptions.

3.1
Normalisation

We define the RotRNN
\xspace
recurrence relation as

x_{t}=\alpha(\gamma Ax_{t-1}+Bu_{t})

(4)

where
\alpha
is a normalisation constant and
\gamma\in[0,1]
is a learnable scalar decay factor.
Writing the convolutional form of Eq.
4

we obtain

x_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}\alpha^{t+1-k}\gamma^{t-k}A^{t-k}Bu_{k}

(5)

Theorem 1
.

Following the white-noise input assumption of the LRU
Orvieto et al. 2023
,
u_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
i.i.d., then if
||x_{1}||=1
and
\alpha=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}+\mathrm{Tr}\left[{B^{\top}B}\right]}}
we have that
\mathbb{E}\left[{||x_{t}||^{2}}\right]=1
for all timesteps
t
.

We provide proofs of all theorems and lemmas stated in the main text in App.
A
. Note that the initial condition of
||x_{1}||=1
is easily enforced by scaling the first state of the SSM appropriately.
Looking closer at Eq.
5
, the normalisation constant
\alpha
is raised to the power
t+1-k
. Hence, if
\alpha<1
, we observe that the weighting of early inputs in the sequence goes to zero, and the model only attends to the very recent past. Since we desire that the recurrent decay is controlled only by
\gamma
, we instead enforce
\alpha=1
, shifting all normalisation into the matrix
B
. This requires that
Tr[B^{\top}B]=1-\gamma^{2}
, which can be achieved by simply re-scaling
B
with the coefficient
\xi:=\sqrt{\frac{1-\gamma^{2}}{Tr[B^{\top}B]}}
.
Our final expression thus becomes

x_{t}=\gamma Ax_{t-1}+\xi Bu_{t}\;\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;\;x_{t}=\xi\sum_{k=1}^{t}\gamma^{t-k}A^{t-k}Bu_{k}

(6)

Multi-Head Decay

We find that having a single scalar decay
\gamma\in[0,1]
does not generalise well to problems which require retaining information from horizons at different scales.
We address this by inputting the
K
-dimensional vector
u_{t}
into
H
independent RotRNN
\xspace
heads, each with input matrix
B_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{N/H\times K}
.
The final projection
y_{t}=Cx_{t}
, where
x_{t}
is the concatenation of the hidden states from each head, can be viewed as an information mixing linear layer.

3.2
Rotation Parametrisation

The group comprising all rotation matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is known as the special orthogonal group,
SO(N)
.
To smoothly map any matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
onto
SO(N)
, one can use the following lemma:

Lemma 2
.

Let
S=M-M^{\top}
, then

A=\exp{(S)}\in SO(N)
, where
\exp
is the matrix exponential defined by
\exp(S):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}S^{k}
.

However, computing Eq.
5
involves taking matrix powers of
A
, which are slow for high-dimensional dense matrices.
To make this computation more efficient, we make use of the following lemma to decompose a dense rotation matrix into block-diagonal form.

Lemma 3
.

Let
P\in O(N)
be an orthogonal matrix and let
D\in SO(N)
be a block-diagonal rotation matrix, then
A=PDP^{\top}\in SO(N)
.

The matrix
D
has
N/2
blocks of the form

\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta_{i}&-\sin\theta_{i}\\
\sin\theta_{i}&\cos\theta_{i}\end{pmatrix}

along the diagonal if
N
is even, and
\frac{N-1}{2}
blocks if
N
is odd, with the remaining value on the diagonal being 1.
When computing matrix powers, the dense orthogonal matrices cancel out leaving
A^{k}=PD^{k}P^{\top}
, and the blocks of
D^{k}
are of the form

\begin{pmatrix}\cos k\theta_{i}&-\sin k\theta_{i}\\
\sin k\theta_{i}&\cos k\theta_{i}\end{pmatrix}
.
Therefore, by learning
P
and
\theta=\{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{m}\}
directly, we can easily generate rotation matrices that are amenable to computing fast matrix powers.
It is important to note that, although it is hard to parameterise
P
such that the space of
P
spans the entire group of orthogonal matrices, we can use Lemma
2
to obtain
P\in SO(N)\subset O(N)
. While this means that
A=PDP^{\top}
is not a surjective map onto
SO(N)
, we find it is sufficiently general to achieve good results in practice (see §
4
).

5
Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper we propose RotRNN
\xspace
, a novel linear recurrent model that utilises the convenient properties of rotation matrices.
We show that, in lieu of extensive hyperparameter searches, RotRNN
\xspace
provides comparable results to the state-of-the-art on the LRA benchmark. Future work should focus on exploring the limits of RotRNN
\xspace
performance, as well as performing hyperparameter sweeps and ablation studies to better understand the benefits and drawbacks of our model.

Appendix C
Comparison to Prior Work

C.1
Linear Recurrent Units

The RotRNN
\xspace
algorithm proposed in this paper is inspired in part by the LRU
Orvieto et al. 2023
.
As well as sharing superficial similarities in the structure of the recurrent layer, more formal comparisons can be drawn between the two architectures.
Recall that the eigenvalue decomposition of the state matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
in the LRU can be written as
A=P\Lambda P^{-1}
, where
P\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
is a matrix of eigenvectors and
\Lambda
is the diagonal matrix of complex eigenvalues.
The authors choose to directly learn the entries of
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}\}
as
\lambda_{j}=\gamma_{j}e^{i\theta_{j}}
, with learnable parameters
\gamma_{j}
and
\theta_{j}
.
In the special case that
\gamma_{j}=1\;\forall j=1,\dots,N
, and the eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs – i.e.
\theta_{j+1}=-\theta_{j}
for
j=1,3,5,\dots,N-1
– or are equal to 1, then the eigenvalues of
A
match the eigenvalues of a rotation matrix.

In particular, if the matrix
P
is unitary, then

\displaystyle AA^{*}

\displaystyle=P\Lambda P^{-1}(P\Lambda P^{-1})^{*}

(15)

\displaystyle=P\Lambda P^{*}P\Lambda^{*}P^{*}

(16)

\displaystyle=I

(17)

where
∗
denotes the complex-conjugate transpose, and we have used the fact that
|\lambda_{j}|=1\;\forall j=1,\dots,N
. Since
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, we then have that
AA^{\top}=AA^{*}=I
and hence
A
is orthogonal
1
1

1

A similar argument can be used to show
A^{\top}A=I
.
We also have

\displaystyle\det(A)

\displaystyle=\det(P)\det(D)\det(P^{*})

(18)

\displaystyle=\det(PP^{*})\det(D)

(19)

\displaystyle=1

(20)

since
PP^{*}=I
and
D
is a diagonal matrix comprising 1s and complex-conjugate pairs of magnitude 1. Therefore, the resulting
A
is a rotation matrix.

This special case of the LRU closely mirrors the RotRNN
\xspace
with decay
\gamma=1
, albeit with different normalisation schemes.
Unfortunately, this is almost impossible to achieve with the LRU, as
P
and
P^{-1}
are absorbed into the learnable
\tilde{B}
and
\tilde{C}
matrices of Eq.
3
, and consistency between the inverses is not enforced.
Moreover, there are no constraints on the eigenvalues to come in complex-conjugate pairs, and the likelihood of learning such a structure naturally is negligible.

To compare the normalisation schemes between the two works, we give a brief overview of the derivation for the normalisation constant found in the LRU. Assuming white-noise input, one can calculate the expected norm of the LRU hidden state:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{||x_{t}||^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t}\Lambda^{i}\tilde{B}u_{t-i}\right)^{*}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{t}\Lambda^{j}\tilde{B}u_{t-j}\right)}\right]

(21)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[{u_{t-i}^{*}\tilde{B}^{*}{\Lambda^{i}}^{*}\Lambda^{j}\tilde{B}u_{t-j}}\right]\right]

(22)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{B}\mathbb{E}\left[{u_{t-j}u_{t-i}^{*}}\right]\tilde{B}^{*}{\Lambda^{i}}^{*}\Lambda^{j}]

(23)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{B}\tilde{B}^{*}{\Lambda^{i}}^{*}\Lambda^{i}]

(24)

Since
\Lambda
is diagonal (
24
) can be re-written into a summation of terms
b_{k}|\lambda_{k}|^{2}
, where
b_{k}
is the norm of each row of
\tilde{B}
, and
\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
is the
k
’th diagonal entry of
\Lambda
.
Hence the expected norm becomes:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{||x_{t}||^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{N}b_{k}|\lambda_{k}|^{2i}

(25)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}b_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{t}|\lambda_{k}|^{2i}

(26)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{t=\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{N}b_{k}\frac{1}{1-|\lambda_{k}|^{2}}

(27)

To ensure expected norm at the limit of
t\rightarrow\infty
is constant, one can normalise the rows of
B
element-wise by
\sqrt{1-|\lambda_{k}|^{2}}
. To contrast this to our normalisation method outlined in §
3.1
, we highlight that our derivation does not require the limit of infinite sequence length, and ensures that the expected norm of the hidden state at every timestep is always 1. However, we do use the same white-noise input assumption as the LRU, which we hope to be able to overcome in future work.

C.2
State Space Models

The relationship between RotRNN
\xspace
and SSMs, namely S4
Gu et al. 2021
and S5
Smith et al. 2023
, is less formally equivalent, but we can still draw comparisons between the structures of the recurrent layers. The S4 recurrent layer can be viewed as a stack of single-input-single-output (SISO) SSMs, whereby independent recurrent layers operate on each channel of the vector-valued input. The outputs of these independent SSMs are concatenated and passed through a “mixing layer” to combine information.
S5, on the other hand, uses a single multi-input-multi-output (MIMO) SSM as its recurrent layer, and as such does not require a separate mixing layer to share information across dimensions.
The input-output structure of RotRNN
\xspace
sits somewhere in-between S4 and S5. The use of multiple independent RotRNN
\xspace
heads outlined in §
3
can be viewed as a stack of independent MIMO recurrent layers, in which the user may specify both the number heads and the dimension of each head separately. The multiplication with the output matrix
C
can be viewed as a linearly mixing the hidden states of each independent head.
