On the existence of eigenvalues of a one-dimensional Dirac operator
Daniel Sánchez-Mendoza, Monika Winklmeier

Abstract
The aim of this paper is to study the existence of eigenvalues in the gap of the essential spectrum of the one-dimensional Dirac operator in the presence of a bounded potential. We employ a generalized variational principle to prove existence of such eigenvalues, estimate how many eigenvalues there are, and give upper and lower bounds for them.



1
Introduction

In this paper we study the one-dimensional Dirac operator given by the differential expression

\tau=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\\
-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}&0\end{pmatrix}+V(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\\
-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}(x)&W(x)\\
W(x)&-M_{2}(x)\end{pmatrix}

(1)

for
x\in\mathbb{R}
or
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Such operators are used in quantum mechanics to describe fermionic particles.
Our main assumptions are that
M_{1},\,M_{2}
and
W
are real valued bounded measurable functions whose limits for
|x|\to\infty
exist,
and that
\inf\{M_{1}\}>\sup\{-M_{2}\}
.
It is well known that the minimal operator associated with
\tau
on
\mathbb{R}
is essentially selfadjoint.
If we consider
\tau
on the halfline
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, then it is symmetric and has defect indices
(1,1)
, so we need a boundary condition at
0
in order to obtain a selfadjoint operator, see Proposition
2.1
. Let us denote by
T
the selfadjoint realisation of
\tau
on
\mathbb{R}
. Then the eigenvalues of the matrix
\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}V(x)
determine the essential spectrum of the Dirac operator which turns out to be of the form
\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)=(-\infty,\lambda_{e-}]\cup[\lambda_{e+},\infty)
, while the gap
(-m_{2},m_{1})
between the ranges of
M_{1}
and
-M_{2}
belongs to its resolvent set
\rho(T)
, see Proposition
2.2
and Proposition
4.1
.
Therefore, discrete eigenvalues of
T
may appear only in
(\lambda_{e-},-m_{2}]\cup[m_{1},\lambda_{e+})
.
Also for selfadjoint realisations of
\tau
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with boundary condition parameter
\alpha
, denoted
T_{\alpha+}
, the essential spectrum is determined by the limit of
V
at infinity.
Moreover,
(-m_{2},m_{1})\subseteq\rho(T_{\alpha+})

provided that
\alpha\in\{0,\pi/2\}
. For a different choice of
\alpha
this is not necessarily true,
see for instance Remark
5.3
.

Operators given by (
1
) appear, for example, as the radial part of the three-dimensional Dirac operator with a radial symmetric potential after a separation ansatz.
More precisely, the so called partial wave operators are given by, see
[
Tha92
, Thm. 4.14]
,

h_{m_{j},\kappa_{j}}=\begin{pmatrix}mc^{2}+\Phi_{sc}(x)+\Phi_{el}(x)&c\{-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}+\frac{\kappa_{j}}{x}\}+\Phi_{am}(x)\\[4.30554pt]
c\{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}+\frac{\kappa_{j}}{x}\}+\Phi_{am}(x)&-mc^{2}-\Phi_{sc}(x)+\Phi_{el}(x)\end{pmatrix}

for
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
The parameters
j\in\{\frac{1}{2},\,\frac{3}{2},\,\dots\}
,
m_{j}\in\{-j,\,-j+1,\,\dots,j\}
,
\kappa_{j}=\pm(j+\frac{1}{2})
stem from the separation ansatz and describe the angular moment.
The constants
m
and
c
are the mass of the particle and the velocity of light.
The functions
\Phi_{sc}
,
\Phi_{el}
,
\Phi_{am}
are interpreted as scalar potential, electric potential and the anomalous magnetic moment.
If we set
c=1
and apply the unitary transformation

U=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}

gives
Uh_{m_{j},\kappa_{j}}U^{*}=\tau

with

M_{1}(x)=m+\Phi_{sc}(x)+\Phi_{el}(x)
,

M_{2}(x)=m+\Phi_{sc}(x)-\Phi_{el}(x)
,

W(x)=-\frac{\kappa_{j}}{x}-\Phi_{am}(x)
.
Since in this work we always assume that
M_{j}
and
W
are bounded, we can deal with this operator only in the unphysical situation when
\kappa=0
. We will remove the boundness hypothesis of
M_{j}
and
W
in a future work so we can handle the physically relevant cases.

Operators of the form (
1
) arise also in the description of Dirac fermions in graphene, see for instance
[
JK14
]
and references therein.
Jakubský and Krejčiřík studied their spectrum under the assumption that
M_{1}(x)=M_{2}(x)=M\geq 0
.
In this case,
M
and
\pm\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}+W
commute and the square of the Dirac operator is a diagonal operator.
If
W
is differentiable, then the entries on the diagonal are the Schrödinger operators

H_{\pm}=M^{2}+(\pm\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}+W)(\mp\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}+W)=M^{2}-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+W^{2}\pm W^{\prime}
.
With the help of the min-max principle for semibounded operators, they could show existence of eigenvalues of
H_{\pm}
and then conclude the existence of eigenvalues of the Dirac operator in the gap of its essential spectrum.
See also Remark
3.7
.

Recently, there has been interest in one-dimensional Dirac operators which are not derived from the Dirac operator in higher dimensions, see
[
DKTZ23
]
.
The authors consider a one-dimensional Dirac fermion on the real line with a screened Coulomb potential and study its eigenvalues in the gap of its essential spectrum.
In their case,
W(x)=0
,
M_{1}(x)=m-e\Phi(x)
,
M_{2}(x)=m+e\Phi(x)
where

\Phi(x)=\frac{Q}{2}\mu e^{-|x|/\mu}
.
They transform the Dirac eigenvalue equation expression into an autonomous dynamical system and show that for suitable choices of the physical constants, eigenvalues appear in the gap of the essential spectrum.

In the work
[
KK24
]
the authors study the Dirac operator
T_{\alpha+}
on the halfline
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with a possibly unbounded and complex potential
V(x)=\begin{pmatrix}M&0\\
0&-M\end{pmatrix}+Q(x)
for some
M>0
, and ask when it does not have eigenvalues.
They find sufficient conditions on
Q
and
\alpha
which guarantee that
T_{\alpha,+}
does not have eigenvalues.
While in our work we need the boundary value parameter

\alpha
to be equal to
0
or
\frac{\pi}{2}
so that the variational principle works,
the possible values of
\alpha
that are allowed in
[
KK24
]
are
\alpha\in(0,\pi/2)
.
The reason for this is that their method relies on an
explicit formula for the resolvent of the free Dirac operator
(T_{\alpha,+})|_{Q=0}
which does not hold otherwise.
In fact, the free Dirac operator has a discrete eigenvalue for
\alpha\in(\frac{\pi}{2},\pi)
.
See Proposition
2.1
and Remark
5.3
.

In order to obtain information on the discrete spectrum of a linear semibounded operator, very often a min-max or max-min principle is used. We now state these classical variational principles, for the proof see
[
Sch12
, Thm. 12.1]
or
[
RS78
, Thm. XIII.2]
.
Let
A
be a lower semibounded selfadjoint linear operator on a Hilbert space
H
with associated sesquilinear form
\mathfrak{a}
and set
\lambda_{e}\coloneqq\inf\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(A)
. Then
\sigma(A)\cap(-\infty,\lambda_{e})
consists of at most countably many eigenvalues of finite multiplicities which may accumulate only at
\lambda_{e}
. Let
(\lambda_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be the increasing sequence of these eigenvalues counted with multiplicity, extended by
\lambda_{e}
if there are only finitely many. Then

\lambda_{n}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}L\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{a})\\
\dim L=n\end{subarray}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in L\\
x\neq 0\end{subarray}}p(x)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}L\subseteq H\\
\dim L=n-1\end{subarray}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{a})\cap L^{\perp}\\
x\neq 0\end{subarray}}p(x),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},

(2)

where
p(x)
is the Rayleigh quotient

p(x)\coloneqq\frac{\mathfrak{a}[x,x]}{\|x\|^{2}},\qquad x\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{a})\setminus\{0\}.

Remark 1.1
.

For every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
we can define the quadratic form

\mathfrak{a}(\lambda)[x]\coloneqq\mathfrak{a}[x,x]-\lambda\|x\|^{2},\qquad x\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{a})\setminus\{0\}.

(3)

Clearly, for fixed
x\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{a})\setminus\{0\}
, the function
\lambda\mapsto a(\lambda)[x]

is decreasing and has a unique zero equal to
p(x)
.
Hence the classical variational principles in (
2
) can be written as min-max or max-min over the zeros of the function in (
3
).

However, the classical variational principles do not work for the discrete spectrum in a gap of the essential spectrum of a selfadjoint operator. This is because the min-max or max-min expression in (
2
) will always be less than or equal to the infimum of the essentials spectrum. In particular, for our Dirac operator these expression will always give
-\infty
.

We employ a generalized variational principle in order to obtain information on the eigenvalues of the Dirac operator that are within the essential spectral gap, see (
9
) and Theorem
3.4
. For the generalized variational principle to be applicable, the operator must be a block operator matrix whose domain is the direct sum of subspaces. This condition is fulfilled by the operator
T
on
\mathbb{R}
, as well as
T_{\alpha+}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
if
\alpha\in\{0,\pi/2\}
.
Formally the generalized variational principle looks like the classical one but instead of the zeros of (
3
) the zeros of a more complicated functional appear in (
2
).
Such principles have been investigated in
[
BEL00
]
,
[
EL04
]
.
Applications to Dirac operators can be found e.g. in
[
KLT04
]
,
[
Win08
]
.
See also the book
[
Tre08
]
.

In Section
2
we will define the one-dimensional Dirac operator on
\mathbb{R}
and on the halflines
\mathbb{R}_{\pm}
, set our assumptions on the potential, and fix notation.
In Section
3
we state the variational principle in an abstract setting and obtain a first sufficient condition for the existence of at least one eigenvalue in the essential spectral gap (Corollary
3.5
).
Moreover, the special case of constant
M_{1}=M_{2}
is discussed in Proposition
3.6
.
In Sections
4
and
5
we derive from the abstract variational principle estimates on the number and location of eigenvalues in the essential spectral gap of the Dirac operator on
\mathbb{R}
and on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
The main theorems are Theorem
4.6
where the eigenvalues of the Dirac operator are compared to those of an associated Sturm-Liouville operator,
and Theorem
4.8
which can be considered a localized version of the previous one.
In particular, it shows that deep enough valleys in
W
produce eigenvalues of the Dirac operators.
We test our results on a toy example that can be solved analytically (Example
4.14
) and on the one-dimensional hydrogenic Dirac operator studied in
[
DKTZ23
]
.
We find that our numerical results agree very well with theirs.

Notation.

Let
A
be a closed linear operator defined on a Hilbert space
H
.
We denote its domain, resolvent and spectrum by
\mathcal{D}(A)
,
\rho(A)
and
\sigma(A)
. We use
\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(A)
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}(A)
for its point and discrete spectrum; and for its essential spectrum we use the definition from
[
GGK90
, Sec. XVII.2]

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(A)\coloneqq\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:A-\lambda\text{ is not Fredholm}\}.

If
A
is selfadjoint, the spectral subspace associated to an interval
I
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}_{I}A
.
For a pencil of closed linear operators
(S(\lambda))_{\lambda\in\Omega}
, where
\Omega
an open subset of
\mathbb{C}
, we define its resolvent set and its spectrum by

\displaystyle\rho(S)

\displaystyle=\{\lambda\in\Omega:0\in\rho(S(\lambda))\},

\displaystyle\sigma(S)

\displaystyle=\{\lambda\in\Omega:0\in\sigma(S(\lambda))\}.

The sets
\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(S)
,
\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}(S)
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(S)
are defined similarly.

2
The one-dimensional Dirac operator

In this section we define the one-dimensional Dirac operator given by

\tau=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\\
-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}&0\end{pmatrix}+V(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\\
-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}(x)&W(x)\\
W(x)&-M_{2}(x)\end{pmatrix}

on
\mathbb{R}
and on
\mathbb{R}_{\pm}
, as well as some relevant quantities for its spectrum.
We will always assume the following conditions for the Dirac operator defined on
\mathbb{R}
, with the obvious modifications for
\mathbb{R}_{\pm}
:

(A1)

M_{1},M_{2},W
are bounded measurable real valued functions.

(A2)

-m_{2}<m_{1}
where

m_{i}\coloneqq\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}M_{i}(x)
for
i=1,2
.

(A3)

The following limits exist:

W_{\pm}\coloneqq\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}W(x),\quad M_{j\pm}\coloneqq\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}M_{j}(x).

We set

V_{\pm}\coloneqq\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}V(x)=\begin{pmatrix}M_{1\pm}&W_{\pm}\\
W_{\pm}&-M_{2\pm}\end{pmatrix}.

Clearly, the eigenvalues of
V_{+}
are

\lambda_{+}^{\pm}=\frac{M_{1+}-M_{2+}}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{M_{1+}+M_{2+}}{2}\right)^{2}+W_{+}^{2}}\ ,

and those of
V_{-}
are

\lambda_{-}^{\pm}=\frac{M_{1-}-M_{2-}}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{M_{1-}+M_{2-}}{2}\right)^{2}+W_{-}^{2}}\ .

From these eigenvalues we define

\lambda_{e-}\coloneqq\max\{\lambda_{+}^{-},\ \lambda_{-}^{-}\},\qquad\lambda_{e+}\coloneqq\min\{\lambda_{+}^{+},\ \lambda_{-}^{+}\},

(4)

and note that
(A2)
and (
4
) imply that

\lambda_{\pm}^{-}\leq\lambda_{e-}\leq-m_{2}<m_{1}\leq\lambda_{e+}\leq\lambda_{\pm}^{+}.

(5)

In the special case
M_{1}=M_{2}
, the above simplifies to

\lambda_{+}^{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{M_{1+}^{2}+W_{+}^{2}},\qquad\lambda_{-}^{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{M_{1-}^{2}+W_{-}^{2}}

and

\lambda_{e+}=-\lambda_{e-}=\min\left\{\sqrt{M_{1-}^{2}+W_{-}^{2}}\,,\,\sqrt{M_{1+}^{2}+W_{+}^{2}}\right\}
.

Now we define
\tau
as an operator on the Hilbert spaces
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{2}
and
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\pm})^{2}

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that

(A1)
,
(A2)
,
(A3)
hold.

(i)

The operator

T=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\\
-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}&0\end{pmatrix}+V=\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}+W\\
-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}+W&-M_{2}\end{pmatrix}

with domain

\mathcal{D}(T)=\left\{\Psi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{2}:\Psi\text{ is absolutely continuous, }\tau\Psi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{2}\right\}=H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{2}

is selfadjoint and all its eigenvalues are simple.

(ii)

For every
\alpha\in[0,\pi)
the operator

T_{\alpha\pm}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\\
-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}&0\end{pmatrix}+V=\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}+W\\
-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}+W&-M_{2}\end{pmatrix}

with domain

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(T_{\alpha\pm})

\displaystyle=\left\{\Psi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\pm})^{2}:\begin{aligned} &\Psi\text{ is absolutely continuous, }\tau\Psi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\pm})^{2},\\
&\Psi(0)=c\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha\\
\sin\alpha\end{pmatrix}\text{ for some }c\in\mathbb{C}\end{aligned}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\Psi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{\pm})^{2}:\Psi(0)=c\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha\\
\sin\alpha\end{pmatrix}\text{ for some }c\in\mathbb{C}\right\}

is selfadjoint and all its eigenvalues are simple.
All selfadjoint realizations of
\tau
on
\mathbb{R}_{\pm}
are of this form.

Proof.

By
[
Wei87
, Thm. 6.8]

\tau
is in the limit point case at
\pm\infty
, which implies that any selfadjoint realization on
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{R}_{\pm}
has simple eigenvalues.

Note that the operator
T
defined above is the maximal symmetric operator associated to
\tau
on
\mathbb{R}
. By
[
Wei87
, Thm. 5.7]

\tau
admits only one selfadjoint realization, which is
T
by its maximality.

If we consider
\tau
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, then due to
V
being bounded,
\tau
is in the limit circle case at
0
. Therefore all selfadjoint realizations are given by fixing an appropriate boundary condition at
0
. In fact, they are given by the family
(T_{\alpha+})_{\alpha\in[0,\pi)}
as defined above, see
[
Wei87
, Thm. 5.8]

or
[
Wei03
, Beispiel 15.26]
.
The case of
\mathbb{R}_{-}
can be treated similarly.
∎

Next we recall that the limit behaviour of
V
for
x\to\pm\infty
determines the essential spectrum of
T
and
T_{\alpha\pm}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
T
and
T_{\alpha\pm}
be as in Proposition
2.1
.

(i)

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T_{\alpha\pm})=(-\infty,\,\lambda_{\pm}^{-}]\cup[\lambda_{\pm}^{+},\,\infty)
.

(ii)

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)=(-\infty,\,\lambda_{e-}]\cup[\lambda_{e+},\,\infty)
.

Proof.

From
[
Wei87
, Thm. 16.5 and 16.6]
we have that

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T_{\alpha\pm})=(-\infty,\lambda_{\pm}^{-}]\cup[\lambda_{\pm}^{+},\infty)
.
Note that
T
and
T_{0-}\oplus T_{0+}
are two-dimensional selfadjoint extensions of the symmetric operator
T|_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}
with
\mathcal{D}_{0}=\{\Psi\in\mathcal{D}(T):\Psi(0)=0\}
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T_{0-}\oplus T_{0+})=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T_{0-})\cup\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T_{0+})=(-\infty,\,\lambda_{e-}]\cup[\lambda_{e+},\,\infty).

Remark 2.3
.

If
V
can be written as
V=V_{1}+V_{2}
with
V_{1}\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and

V_{2}
of bounded variation and
converging to a diagonal matrix as
x\to\infty
, then
(-\infty,\,\lambda_{+}^{-})\cup(\lambda_{+}^{+},\,\infty)\subseteq\sigma_{\mathrm{ac}}(T_{\alpha+})
by
[
Wei87
, Thm. 16.7]
.
This is the case if for instance
M_{1},M_{2}
are of bounded variation and
W(x)\to 0
as
x\to\infty
fast enough.

Similar assertions hold for
T_{\alpha-}
and
T
with
\lambda_{+}^{\pm}
replaced by
\lambda_{-}^{\pm}
and
\lambda_{e\pm}
respectively.

3
Variational principle for eigenvalues of a block operator matrix in a gap of the essential spectrum

The aim of this section is to study eigenvalues of an abstract off-diagonal dominant block operator matrix.
In Sections
4
and
5
the results will be applied to the Dirac operator on the real line
\mathbb{R}
and on the halfline
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and define the block matrix

\mathcal{T}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B^{*}&D\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{T})=\mathcal{D}(B^{*})\oplus\mathcal{D}(B)\subseteq\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}

where
B
is a densely defined closed linear operator and
A
and
D
are bounded selfadjoint operators on
\mathcal{H}
.
Then
\mathcal{T}
is a selfadjoint linear operator.

We set
d\coloneqq\max\sigma(D)
,
a\coloneqq\min\sigma(A)
and assume that
d<a
.
Let us define the sesquilinear form

\mathfrak{s}(\lambda)
by

\mathfrak{s}(\lambda)[x,y]\coloneqq\langle(A-\lambda)x,y\rangle-\langle(D-\lambda)^{-1}B^{*}x,B^{*}y\rangle,\quad\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{s}(\lambda))=\mathcal{D}(B^{*}),\quad\re\lambda>d.

(6)

We write

\mathfrak{s}(\lambda)[x]\coloneqq\mathfrak{s}(\lambda)[x,x]

for the associated quadratic form.

Note that for
\re\lambda>d
and
x,y\in\mathcal{D}(B^{*})

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}(\lambda)[x,y]=\langle(A-\lambda)x,y\rangle-\langle(D-\overline{\lambda})(D-\lambda)^{-1}B^{*}x,(D-\lambda)^{-1}B^{*}y\rangle,

hence for
\re\lambda>d
and
x\in\mathcal{D}(B^{*})

\displaystyle\re(\mathfrak{s}(\lambda)[x])

\displaystyle\geq(a-\re\lambda)\|x\|^{2}+(\re\lambda-d)\|(D-\lambda)^{-1}B^{*}x\|^{2}\geq(a-\re\lambda)\|x\|^{2}.

(7)

Therefore
\mathfrak{s}(\lambda)
is a sectorial form and it is not hard to see that it is closed.
Hence it defines a closed
m
-sectorial operator
S(\lambda)
according to
[
Kat66
, Ch.VI, §2]
which has the same lower bound as the form.
Moreover,
\mathfrak{s}(\lambda)
is symmetric if
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and the associated operator is selfadjoint in this case.
The operator
S(\lambda)
is called the
Schur complement
of
\mathcal{T}
.

The next proposition is proved in
[
KLT04
, Prop. 2.2]
.
Our situation corresponds to Case III there.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
\re\lambda>d
the operator
\mathcal{T}-\lambda
admits the factorization

\mathcal{T}-\lambda=\begin{pmatrix}\bbold{1}&B(D-\lambda)^{-1}\\
0&\bbold{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}S(\lambda)&0\\
0&D-\lambda\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bbold{1}&0\\
(D-\lambda)^{-1}B^{*}&\bbold{1}\end{pmatrix}.

(8)

The
m
-sectorial operator
S(\lambda)
associated to the form in (
6
) is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} S(\lambda)&=A-\lambda-B(D-\lambda)^{-1}B^{*},\\
\mathcal{D}(S(\lambda))&=\{x\in\mathcal{D}(B^{*}):(D-\lambda)^{-1}B^{*}x\in\mathcal{D}(B)\},\end{aligned}\qquad

\displaystyle\re\lambda>d.

The pencil
S(\cdot)
is holomorphic of type (B) (see
[
Kat66
, Ch.VII, §4]
),

\displaystyle\rho(\mathcal{T})\cap\{\re\lambda>d\}

\displaystyle=\rho(S)\cap\{\re\lambda>d\},

\displaystyle\sigma(\mathcal{T})\cap\{\re\lambda>d\}

\displaystyle=\sigma(S)\cap\{\re\lambda>d\},

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T})\cap\{\re\lambda>d\}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(S)\cap\{\re\lambda>d\},

and
\dim\ker(\mathcal{T}-\lambda)=\dim\ker(S(\lambda))
for
\re\lambda>d
.

Corollary 3.2
.

(d,a)\subseteq\rho(\mathcal{T})
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in(d,a)
.
Then the Schur complement
S(\lambda)
is defined and
for
x\in\mathcal{D}(S(\lambda))\subseteq\mathcal{D}(B^{*})
we have

\langle S(\lambda)x,x\rangle=\mathfrak{s}(\lambda)[x]\geq(a-\lambda)\|x\|^{2}

by (
7
).
Therefore
0\in\rho(S(\lambda))
and consequently
\lambda\in\rho(\mathcal{T})
.
∎

The next proposition gathers some properties of the family of forms

(\mathfrak{s}(\lambda))_{\re\lambda>d}
and the operator pencil
(S(\lambda))_{\re\lambda>d}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

(i)

The domain of the form
\mathfrak{s}(\lambda)
is
\mathcal{D}(B^{*})
, in particular it is independent of
\lambda
for
\re\lambda>d
.

(ii)

For every
x\in\mathcal{D}(B^{*})
, the function
\mathfrak{s}(\cdot)[x]
is continuous. The pencil
S(\cdot)
is continuous in the norm resolvent sense.

(iii)

For every
x\in\mathcal{D}(B^{*})\setminus\{0\}
, the function
(d,\infty)\ni\lambda\mapsto\mathfrak{s}(\lambda)[x]
is strictly decreasing.

(iv)

For every
\lambda\in(d,a)
, the operator
S(\lambda)
is strictly positive, in particular its negative spectral subspace is

\mathcal{L}_{(-\infty,0)}S(\lambda)=\{0\}
.

(v)
