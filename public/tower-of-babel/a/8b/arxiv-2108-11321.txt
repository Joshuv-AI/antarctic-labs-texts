Adaptive Extended Kalman Filter (ROSE-Filter) for Positioning System
Reiner Marchthaler (orcid: 0000-0001-9108-3072)

Abstract
This paper illustrates the way for estimating position and orientation of a vehicle with an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF). For this purpose a non-linear model is designed and an adaptive calculation of measurement noise covariance matrix is used, a so called ROSE-Filter ( R apid O ngoing S tochastic covariance E stimation-Filter). Input of the system is the measured position from a two dimensional position system. Estimated is the pose (position and orientation) and the velocity of the vehicle.



I
Introduction

The exact pose (position and orientation) of objects is required for a lot of applications.
This pose is base of controlling vehicles and creating a model of the environment.

Sensors, like GPS just frequently measured the position not accurately.
Therefore it is necessary to filter the position and estimate the orientation of the object.

The Kalman filter is a popular approach to solve this topic.
This filter requires to first define a model to estimate the pose.
There are different applications which are based on linear models and non-linear models.
Each approach has its own advantages and disadvantages. Linear models have problems estimating the orientation of an object.
However non-linear models can estimate the pose but are less stable as linear models. Hence a stable non-linear model is required.

This paper focuses on vehicles, like cars or robots, based on a planar vehicle model.
The planar vehicle model uses the steering for changing the direction of the vehicle to describe the x- and y- position of the center of gravity and the orientation of the vehicle.
Such kind of modeling is described in more detail in

III
Kalman Filter

III-A

Kalman Equations

In general a physical system can be written as state-space model

\displaystyle\underline{x}\scalebox{0.7}{(i+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underline{f}\bigl(\underline{x}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)},\underline{u}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\bigr)+\underline{z}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}

(29)

\displaystyle\underline{y}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underline{h}\bigl(\underline{x}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)},\underline{u}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\bigr)+\underline{\omega}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}

(30)

where
\underline{f}\bigl(\underline{x}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)},\underline{u}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\bigr)
and
\underline{h}\bigl(\underline{x}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)},\underline{u}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\bigr)
are two (nonlinear) equation vectors.

\underline{x}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}
is the state vector,
\underline{u}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}
is the control vector and
\underline{y}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}
is the measurements (or observations) at time step k.
Model uncertainties are illustrated with the random vector
\underline{z}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}
and errors of measurements are described by the random vector
\underline{\omega}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}
.

A Kalman Filter for such a state-space representation is given by the following equations

\displaystyle\underline{K}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underline{\hat{P}}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\!\cdot\!\underline{C}_{\,J}^{T}\!\cdot\!\bigl(\underline{C}_{\,J}\!\cdot\!\underline{\hat{P}}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\!\cdot\!\underline{C}^{T}_{\,J}+\underline{R}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\bigr)^{-1}

(31)

\displaystyle\underline{\tilde{x}}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underline{\hat{x}}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}+\underline{K}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\!\cdot\!\Bigl(\underline{y}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}-\underline{h}\bigl(\underline{\hat{x}}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)},\underline{u}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\bigr)\Bigr)

(32)

\displaystyle\underline{\tilde{P}}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl(\underline{I}-\underline{K}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\!\cdot\!\underline{C}_{\,J}\bigr)\!\cdot\!\underline{\hat{P}}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\!\cdot\!\bigl(\underline{I}-\underline{K}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\!\cdot\!\underline{C}_{\,J}\bigr)^{T}

(33)

\displaystyle+\underline{K}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\!\cdot\!\underline{R}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\!\cdot\!\underline{K}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}^{T}\vskip 2.84526pt

\displaystyle\underline{\hat{x}}\scalebox{0.7}{(i+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underline{f}\bigl(\underline{\tilde{x}}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)},\underline{u}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\bigr)

(34)

\displaystyle\underline{\hat{P}}\scalebox{0.7}{(i+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underline{A}_{\,J}\!\cdot\!\bigl(\underline{\tilde{P}}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}+\underline{Q}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\bigr)\!\cdot\!\underline{A}^{T}_{\,J}

(35)

with
\underline{Q}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}=\!\text{Var}\bigl(\underline{z}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\bigr)
,
\underline{R}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}=\!\text{Var}\bigl(\underline{\omega}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\bigr)
and the Jacobian matrices
\underline{A}_{\,J}
and
\underline{C}_{\,J}

\displaystyle\underline{A}_{\,J}\!=\!\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial\underline{f}}{\partial\tilde{x}_{1}}&\!\!\cdots\!\!&\dfrac{\partial\underline{f}}{\partial\tilde{x}_{n}}\end{bmatrix},\hskip 2.84526pt\underline{C}_{\,J}\!=\!\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial\underline{h}}{\partial\tilde{x}_{1}}&\!\!\cdots\!\!&\dfrac{\partial\underline{h}}{\partial\tilde{x}_{n}}\end{bmatrix}

III-C

Covariance of Measurement Noise

The Covariance of measurement noise can be calculated by

\displaystyle\underline{R}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Var}\bigl(\underline{\omega}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\bigr)=\text{Var}\bigl(\underline{y}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\bigr)

(41)

\displaystyle\underline{R}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{E}\Bigl(\bigl(\underline{y}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}-\text{E}\bigl(\underline{y}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\bigr)\bigr)\!\cdot\!\bigl(\underline{y}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}-\text{E}\bigl(\underline{y}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\bigr)\bigr)^{T}\Bigr)

(42)

The linear model described in section
II-B
can be used to estimate the expected value
\text{E}\bigl(\underline{y}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\bigr)

Choosing the state vector
\underline{x}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}
as

\displaystyle\underline{x}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}=\begin{bmatrix}x\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}&v_{x}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{or}\quad\underline{x}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}=\begin{bmatrix}y\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}&v_{y}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\end{bmatrix}

(43)

the matrices
\underline{A}_{\,J}
and
\underline{C}_{\,J}
is written as

\displaystyle\underline{A}_{J}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}t\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}^{T}\quad\underline{C}_{J}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\end{bmatrix}

(44)

Assuming a time invariant variance of random vector
\underline{z}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}
and
\underline{\omega}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}
the update of the state vector for this linear model can be simplified as (see

\displaystyle\text{E}\bigl(\underline{y}\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\bigr)=\underline{\tilde{x}}(k)=\underline{y}(k)+\bigl(\underline{I}-\underline{K}\!\cdot\!\underline{C}_{J}\bigr)\!\cdot\!\underline{A}_{J}\!\cdot\!\underline{\tilde{x}}(k-1)

\displaystyle K\!=\!\frac{0.125}{\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}t\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}}\!\cdot\!\begin{bmatrix}\scalebox{0.7}{$\Delta$}t\scalebox{0.7}{(i)}\!\cdot\!(\scalebox{0.95}{$-\lambda^{2}-8\!\cdot\!\!\lambda+(\lambda+4)\!\cdot\!\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+8\!\cdot\!\!\lambda)}$}\;\\

V
Conclusion

This paper illustrates the way for estimating position, orientation and velocity of a vehicle by measuring its x- and y-position.
For this, a Extended Kalman-Filter (EKF) uses a model which calculates the trajectory based on circular path. With such a model the given position can be smoothed and the orientation of the vehicle, curvature of the driven course and the velocity can be estimated.

Changing in the covariance of measurement noise can be determined continuously by using a ROSE-Filter. Thus for every measured x- and y-position the correct covariance of measurement noise is available .
