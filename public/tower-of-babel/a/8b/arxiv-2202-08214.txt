Introduction

One of the central research programs in proof complexity, initiated by Cook and Reckhow
[
8
]
,
is to obtain the separation
\mathsf{NP}\neq\mathsf{coNP}
via proving superpolynomial lower bounds for
sizes of proofs in all propositional proof systems. Among specific propositional proof systems that have been extensively studied are systems that operate with De Morgan formulas.
The most natural examples of such systems are
Frege systems
, the standard “textbook” proof systems for propositional logic (for example, Hilbert-style or sequent calculus).
Obtaining superpolynomial lower bounds for general Frege systems turns out to be very hard, such bounds are probably out of reach in the nearest future. Nevertheless a significant progress
has been achieved: strong
lower bounds have been proven for AC
0
-Frege systems, which are Frege systems restricted to operate only with bounded depth, unbounded fan-in De Morgan formulas
[
1
,
17
,
15
,
5
,
11
,
12
]
.

Unfortunately all intricate lower bound techniques currently developed for proof systems operating with De Morgan formulas fail once De Morgan language is extended with
counting connectives. One of the long standing open problems at the frontier of proof complexity is to prove a superpolynomial lower bound for AC
{}^{0}[p]
-Frege systems, which
are defined similarly to AC
0
-Frege systems with the difference that formulas can contain also counting modulo
p
connective. This problem manifests itself already at the
level of resolution, the system operating with disjunctions of literals, that is with De Morgan formulas of depth
1
.

Resolution over linear equations

\mathsf{Res(lin_{\mathcal{R}})}
is a propositional proof system extending resolution by allowing linear equations over a ring
\mathcal{R}
in place of literals.
Such extensions for various
\mathcal{R}
equip resolution with the ability to perform basic counting arguments
efficiently
1
1

1

Typically for
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{Z}
or for
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
for modular counting.
. Although
\mathsf{Res(lin_{\mathcal{R}})}
is a very simple extension of resolution,
which is the most well-understood propositional proof system with a plenty of exponential lower bounds proven for it, no superpolynomial lower bounds have been proven for unrestricted

\mathsf{Res(lin_{\mathcal{R}})}
to date. Thus
\mathsf{Res(lin_{\mathcal{R}})}
is a good starting point for devising novel techniques that work for proof systems combining De Morgan language with counting connectives since
\mathsf{Res(lin_{\mathcal{R}})}
is one of the simplest such systems and since

\mathsf{Res(lin_{\mathcal{R}})}
is very close to resolution, which is the most well-studied proof system.

The strength of
\mathsf{Res(lin_{\mathcal{R}})}
needs to be understood primarily for

\mathsf{coNP}
-complete language of unsatisfiable CNFs, the central
\mathsf{coNP}
-complete language
used to compare propositional proofs systems. However, in case
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{Z}

is also naturally a proof system for
\mathsf{coNP}
-complete language

Another interesting and related task is to identify natural hardness criterions for

Related work

1.1.1
Resolution over linear equations

Proof system
\mathsf{Res(lin_{\mathcal{R}})}
was first introduced in the regime
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{Z}
by Raz and Tzameret in
[
18
]
, where they showed that
\mathsf{Res(lin_{\mathbb{Z}})}

has short proofs for many instances that are frequently used as hard instances in proof complexity. Subsequently Itsykson and Sokolov introduced in
[
13
]
proof system

\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{2}})}
,
proved several upper and lower bounds for
tree-like

\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{2}})}
and proved that
\mathsf{Res(lin_{\mathbb{Z}})}
p-simulates
\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{2}})}
.
In
[
14
]
Khaniki proved almost
quadratic lower bounds for resolution over polynomial equations over finite fields and thus, in particular, for
\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}
. In
[
10
]
the computational model of
linear branching programs
(LBPs) was
introduced, which is related to
\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{2}})}
in the same way as ordinary
boolean branching programs are related to resolution. More specifically,
[
10
]

introduced the notions of
strongly
and
weakly read-once
LBPs
and showed that weakly read once LBPs correspond to what can naturally be called
the
(weakly) regular

\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{2}})}
whereas for strongly read-once LBPs

[
10
]
proved exponential lower bounds. Subsequently
[
9
]
proved a
superpolynomial lower bound for refutations of the Bit Pigeonhole Principle in strongly regular
\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{2}})}
.

1.1.2
Binary value principle

A variant of the Subset Sum Principle subsequently
called in
[
4
]
the Binary Value Principle (BVP) is represented by the single equation
x_{1}+2x_{2}+\dots+2^{n-1}x_{n}=-1
, which is unsatisfiable over the Boolean
assignments or, in other words, 0-1 unsatisfiable
3
3

3

Strictly speaking BVP is the negation of
x_{1}+2x_{2}+\dots+2^{n-1}x_{n}=-1
, it says that the natural number represented by the bit string
x_{1}\ldots x_{n}
is never
-1
.

\mathsf{Res(lin_{\mathcal{R}})}
is like resolution a refutation system: it refutes
\neg\phi
for a tautology
\phi
.
.
As recent research in proof complexity showed, quite surprisingly, this simple principle turns out to be hard even for strong algebraic proof systems.
In
[
4
]
it was proved that BVP does not have short proofs even in the ideal proof system assuming Shub-Smale hypothesis. In
[
3
]
Alekseev proved unconditional lower
bound for BVP in a pretty strong extension of polynomial calculus, where introduction of new variables and taking radicals are allowed.

1.2
Our contributions

Let
q
be a prime power which can depend on
n
, the number of variables. We base hardness criterions for instances of the form
A\cdot x=b
on the notions of an error correcting code and what we call
(s,r)
-robustness
, a combinatorial, algebraic
property of linear systems
A\cdot x=b
, which we introduce. As a step towards general

\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}
lower bounds for such instances we prove superpolynomial lower bounds
for a nontrivial dag-like fragment of
\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}
and also for two tree-like
fragments, which capture features of
\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}
that are in a sense complementary to those captured by our dag-like fragment.

1.

We consider a natural proof system
\mathsf{LinDags}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
for
\mathsf{LinSys}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
where
refutations are dags where nodes represent splittings on possible values of a linear form.
This is a fragment of
\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}
. Using dynamic programming argument one can show
that the fragment
\mathsf{BinDags}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
of
\mathsf{LinDags}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
, which uses only binary splittings on variables, can simulate
\mathsf{LinDags}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
. We then consider a regular variant
\mathsf{BinRegDags}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
of

\mathsf{BinDags}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
where each variable appears at most
once on every path from the root to a leaf. This proof system can no longer simulate

\mathsf{LinDags}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
since it can do dynamic argument at most once. Still,
\mathsf{BinRegDags}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}

is nontrivial dag-like proof system which improves on dynamic programming and can efficiently refute systems like

\{x_{i}+2x_{n+i}=0\}_{i\in[n]}\cup\{x_{1}+\ldots+x_{2n}=2\}
(for this
system written as
A\cdot x=b
the size of the image
|A(\{0,1\}^{n})|
is exponential,
which makes dynamic programming alone insufficient). We outline definitions of proof systems we consider in this paper in Section
1.2.1
.

2.

We prove
2^{\Omega(r)}
lower bound for refutations in
\mathsf{BinRegDags}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
of
instances
A\cdot x=b
satisfying a property, which we call

(s,r)
-robustness
,
s\geq r
. In particular, for such instances the space

\mathcal{C}_{A}:=\{y\cdot A\,|\,y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{k}\}
is an error correcting code (ECC) with
the distance at least
s
. We show that random instances, obtained by randomly choosing

A
, are with high probability
(n/3,\Omega\left((n/(q+1)\ln q))^{1/3}\right))
-robust. As a consequence, we obtain that all explicit codes that meet the Gilbert-Varshamov bound for random codes, such as algebraic geometry codes, can be used to construct
explicit instances with these parameters (at the moment we can only choose
A
). We state this dag-like lower bound in
Section
1.2.2
.

3.

We prove
2^{\Omega(((q+1)\ln q)^{-1/3}d^{1/5})}
lower bound for tree-like
\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}

and
\mathsf{LinTrees}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
(tree-like version of
\mathsf{LinDags}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
) for instances

A\cdot x=b
, were
A
is ECC with minimal distance
d
.

1.2.3
Lower bounds for tree-like models with linear splittings

In order to extend our approach to lower bounds from
\mathsf{BinRegDags}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
to

\mathsf{Res(lin^{\neq}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}
we need to deal with splittings on arbitrary linear forms. Focusing
now on this side of
\mathsf{Res(lin^{\neq}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}
we prove a lower bound for
\mathsf{Res(lin^{\neq}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}

proofs with linear splittings, but restricting them to be tree-like.

We introduce the following proof system
\mathsf{LinTrees}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
for
\mathsf{LinSys}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
, which is equivalent to tree-like
\mathsf{Res(lin^{\neq}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}
:

Definition

(Definition
5
)
.

Let
T
be a tree, where internal nodes correspond to splittings on values of linear forms under boolean assignments:
every internal node
u
is marked
with a linear form
f_{u}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and for every
a\in f_{u}(\{0,1\}^{n})
there is precisely one edge marked with equation
f_{u}=a
leading to a child of
u
. If
u
is a node of
T
, denote

B_{u}\cdot x=c_{u}
the system of linear equations formed by marks on edges on the path from the root of
T
to
u
. Then
T
is a
\mathsf{LinTrees}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
refutation of
A\cdot x=b
if at any
leaf
u
of
T
the system
A\cdot x=b\wedge B_{u}\cdot x=c_{u}
has no solutions in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
. Note that the system
A\cdot x=b
being refuted can possibly have solutions
in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
, the only requirement is that it has no solutions in
\{0,1\}^{n}
.

We show that our instances
(A,b)\in\mathsf{LinSys}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
that are constructed using good ECCs are also hard for
\mathsf{LinTrees}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
. In case of
\mathsf{LinTrees}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
the hardness criterion
is simpler: the lower bound is purely in terms of
d_{A}
(the minimal distance of
the code generated by the rows of
A
) and
q
.

We obtain the lower bound
2^{\Omega({((q+1)\ln q)^{-1/3}}d_{A}^{1/5})}
via constructing
for every
\mathsf{LinTrees}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
proof
T
an embedding
of the full binary tree of depth
\Omega({((q+1)\ln q)^{-1/3}}d_{A}^{1/5})
into
T
. The underlying technique that we employ for the construction of such an embedding is standard in proof complexity
for tree-like lower bounds and is based on defining a strategy for Delayer in a Prover-Delayer game. Given the depth
h
of the full binary tree, Delayer’s strategy is a strategy for choosing

h
branching nodes in a proof tree while traversing it from the root to the leaves. One can say that proof trees are constituting strategies of the Prover in the following game. The game starts with

A\cdot x=b
, Prover at each step suggests a linear form
f
and Delayer in turn either chooses an equation
f=a
to add
or declares the current position a branching point, chooses two children
f=a
,
f=b
and allows one of them to be added by the Prover. The endgame condition is precisely the leaf condition.
It is easy to see that if Delayer’s strategy
guarantees that Delayer chooses at least
h
branching points then for any proof tree
T
this strategy defines an embedding of the full binary tree of depth
h
into
T
.

Our second main theorem is as follows:

Theorem

(Theorem
25
)
.

Let
(A,b)\in\mathsf{LinSys}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
. For
\mathsf{LinTrees}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
Prover-Delayer game on
A\cdot x=b
there exists a strategy of Delayer that guarantees selection of

\Omega({((q+1)\ln q)^{-1/3}}d_{A}^{1/5})
branching points.

As we argued above, the immediate corollary is the
\mathsf{LinTrees}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
lower bound.

Corollary

(Corollary
29
)
.

Let
(A,b)\in\mathsf{LinSys}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
. Every
\mathsf{LinTrees}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
refutation of
A\cdot x=b
is of size
2^{\Omega(((q+1)\ln q)^{-1/3}d_{A}^{1/5})}
. In particular, every
\mathsf{LinTrees}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
refutation
of random instances and Hermitian instances are of size
2^{\Omega(((q+1)\ln q)^{-1/3}n^{1/5})}
.

As a side result we also obtain tree-like
\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}
lower bounds for ECC instances. We do not know neither whether
\mathsf{LinTrees}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
, which is tree-like
\mathsf{Res(lin^{\neq}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}
,
p-simulates tree-like
\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}
nor whether the converse p-simulation takes place. However we can show that
the existence of Delayer’s strategies for
\mathsf{LinTrees}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}

\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}

This allows to transfer
\mathsf{LinTrees}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}

\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}

Theorem

(Theorem
16
)
.

Let
(A,b)\in\mathsf{LinSys}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
. If there exists Delayer’s strategy guaranteeing selection of
h
branching
points for
\mathsf{LinTrees}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}

then there exists a Delayer’s strategy guaranteeing selection of
h
branching points for tree-like
\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}

Corollary

(Corollary
30
)
.

Let
(A,b)\in\mathsf{LinSys}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
. Every tree-like
\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}
refutation of
A\cdot x=b
is of size
2^{\Omega(((q+1)\ln q)^{-1/3}d_{A}^{1/5})}
. In particular, every tree-like
\mathsf{Res(lin_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})}

of random instances and Hermitian instances are of size

.

5.1
Linear splitting tree refutations

We begin with a high-level overview of the Delayer’s strategy and sketch the argument why it guarantees
a good number of branching points. We postpone the details to the Section
5.1.1
.

Assume
A\cdot x=b

is the starting linear system. The core idea is to prolong the 0-1 satisfiability of the linear system
C_{u,\leq\tau}\cdot x=d_{u,\leq\tau}
which comprises all equations of small weight (having at most

\tau=\Theta(d_{A}^{4/5})
of nonzero coefficients) in the span of
A\cdot x=b\wedge B_{u}\cdot x=c_{u}
, where
B_{u}\cdot x=c_{u}
represents the equations introduced during the game. By ensuring 0-1 satisfiability of this system for as long as possible, the strategy prevents the Prover from quickly reaching a state where

C_{u,\leq\tau}\cdot x=d_{u,\leq\tau}
is 0-1 unsatisfiable and depends on only a small number of variables, which would clearly imply the existence of short
refutations for
C_{u,\leq\tau}\cdot x=d_{u,\leq\tau}
.

The first guess would be the following strategy of Delayer. Suppose Prover chooses a form
f_{u}
. Let
f_{u}^{\prime}
be a linear form of minimal weight such that
f_{u}^{\prime}\equiv\alpha f_{u}+h
,
where
h
lies in the span of linear forms
A\cdot x\cup B_{u}\cdot x
and
\alpha\neq 0\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. Note that the equation
f_{u}^{\prime}=c
lifts to
f_{u}=\tilde{c}
for some
\tilde{c}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. The Delayer’s strategy prescribes the following steps:

1.

Non-branching case.
If
C_{u,\leq\tau}\cdot x=d_{u,\leq\tau}\models_{0,1}f_{u}^{\prime}=c
for some
c\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
(where semantic implication is over 0-1 assignments) then simply add
f_{u}=\tilde{c}
and proceed to the next round.

2.

Branching case.
Otherwise, select
c_{1}\neq c_{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
such that both
C_{u,\leq\tau}\cdot x=d_{u,\leq\tau}\wedge f_{u}^{\prime}=c_{i}
remain 0-1 satisfiable for
i=1,2
. Declare the current position a branching point and let Prover choose one of the equations
f_{u}=\tilde{c}_{i}
to add. We call the equation chosen by Prover a
branching equation
.

The main problem is that after adding an equation
f_{u}=\tilde{c}
the linear system of equations with weight
\leq\tau
in the span of

A\cdot x=b\wedge B_{u}\cdot x=c_{u}\wedge f_{u}=\tilde{c}

can
include several more equations compared to the original system
C_{u,\leq\tau}\cdot x=d_{u,\leq\tau}
, potentially rendering the system 0-1 unsatisfiable. Consequently, in the branching case, it is possible that at least one of the branches
i\in\{1,2\}
result in a 0-1 unsatisfiable system of short (weight
\leq\tau
) equations within the
span of

A\cdot x=b\wedge B_{u}\cdot x=c_{u}\wedge f_{u}=\tilde{c}_{i}

.

Fortunately, we know by Theorem
17
.(
2
) that all ECC
linear systems with minimal distance
d
are always 0-1 satisfiable if the number
of equations is at most
(d/(q+1)\ln q)^{1/3}
. Assume that addition of
f_{u}=\tilde{c}

to
B_{u}\cdot x=c_{u}
introduces new equations
H\cdot x=a
to

C_{u,\leq\tau}\cdot x=d_{u,\leq\tau}
. Although the minimal distance
d

for the system
C_{u,\leq\tau}\cdot x=d_{u,\leq\tau}\wedge H\cdot x=a
can be low,
we can still use Theorem
17
.(
2
) to show 0-1
satisfiability of the system by proving the following:

Claim 23
.

Denote
\rho_{0}
a satisfying 0-1 assignment for

C_{u,\leq\tau}\cdot x=d_{u,\leq\tau}\wedge f^{\prime}_{u}=c
. For some

\delta<1
and
\tau_{0}:=\tau^{\delta}
there exists a partial assignment

\rho\subset\rho_{0}
such that:

1.

All equations of weight
\leq\tau_{0}
in the span of

C_{u,\leq\tau}\cdot x=d_{u,\leq\tau}\wedge H\cdot x=a
are satisfied by
\rho
.

2.

Remaining equations in

(C_{u,\leq\tau}\cdot x)\upharpoonright_{\rho}=d_{u,\leq\tau}\wedge(H\cdot x)\upharpoonright_{\rho}=a

are of weight
\geq\tau_{0}
.

3.

Dimension of the span of
(C_{u,\leq\tau}\cdot x)\upharpoonright_{\rho}=d_{u,\leq\tau}\wedge(H\cdot x)\upharpoonright_{\rho}=a
is at most the dimension of the span of

From Claim
23
and Theorem
17
.(
2
)
it follows that if

is at most

then

is 0-1 satisfiable. Consequently,

We can conclude that in every endgame position the dimension of the span of

is at least
(\tau_{0}/(q+1)\ln q)^{1/3}
. Unfortunately this does not give any bound on

We thus modify the strategy above by replacing

then the following holds:

1.

The system

. This is sufficient to guarantee that the current position is not the endgame position. Note that modified strategy by definition

2.

.(
2

extends

which are entailed by

By the same argument as in the proof of Claim

there exists a partial assignment
\rho

. We then can use Theorem

.(

For every system

with

and every equation

From Theorem

This finishes the argument showing
