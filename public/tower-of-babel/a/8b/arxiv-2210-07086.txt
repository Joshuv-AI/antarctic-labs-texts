1.
Introduction

This paper is concerned with the Fredholm determinants of operators that are introduced via linear systems and with their applications to the sinh-Gordon equations. The study has application in Tracy and Widom’s approach
[
41
]
to random matrix theory.

We begin by fixing some notation concerning linear systems.
Let
H
be a separable complex Hilbert space with orthonormal basis
(e_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
, and let
{\mathcal{L}}(H)
denote the algebra of bounded linear operators on
H
. We shall denote the adjoint of
B\in\mathcal{L}(H)
by
B^{\dagger}
. Let
H_{0}
be a separable complex Hilbert space which serves as the input and output space; let
B:H_{0}\rightarrow H
and
C:H\rightarrow H_{0}
be bounded linear operators.
On the state space
H
, let
(T_{t})_{t>0}
be a strongly continuous and bounded semigroup with infinitesimal generator
-A
, which is densely defined on domain
{\mathcal{D}}(A)
, where
{\mathcal{D}}(A)
is itself a Hilbert space for the graph norm

\|f\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}}(A)}=\|f\|^{2}+\|Af\|^{2}
.

The continuous time linear system
(-A,B,C)
is

\displaystyle{\frac{dX}{dt}}

\displaystyle=-AX+BU

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=CX,

(1.1)

\displaystyle X(0)

\displaystyle=0.

The scattering function of
(-A,B,C)
is
\phi(t)=Ce^{-tA}B
, which is a bounded and weakly continuous function
\phi:(0,\infty)\rightarrow{\mathcal{L}}(H_{0})
.

Suppose that
\phi\in L^{2}((0,\infty);{\mathcal{L}}(H_{0}))
. Then the Hankel integral operator with scattering function

\phi
is the operator

(1.2)

\Gamma_{\phi}f(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(x+y)f(y)\,dy\qquad(f\in L^{2}((0,\infty);H_{0})).

Such Hankel operators do not themselves form an algebra, although they have an algebraic structure which has been exploited in
[
35
]
and section 3.5 of
[
28
]
. An important fact is that every bounded self-adjoint Hankel integral operator on
L^{2}(0,\infty)
can be realised as the Hankel operator associated with a linear system
(-A,B,C)
in continuous time with state space
H
, and such that
\phi(t)=Ce^{-tA}B
where
(e^{-tA})
is a strongly continuous semigroup on
H
(see
[
34
]
and
[
32
]
).

Recall that
K\in\mathcal{L}(H)
is Hilbert–Schmidt if

\left\|K\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left\|Ke_{j}\right\|^{2}

is finite. We shall denote the set of all Hilbert–Schmidt operators on
H
by
{\mathcal{L}}^{2}={\mathcal{L}}^{2}(H)
. This space contains the ideal of trace class operators
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}=\{K_{1}K_{2}\,:\,K_{1},K_{2}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{2}\}
.
There are now several criteria for ensuring the boundedness of a Hankel operator; see, for example
[
34
]
and
[
36
]
. We shall repeatedly use the basic result that
if
t\|\phi(t)\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{L}}^{2}(H_{0},H_{0})}
is integrable, then
\Gamma_{\phi}
determines a Hilbert–Schmidt operator
L^{2}((0,\infty);H_{0})\rightarrow L^{2}((0,\infty);H_{0})
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
(-A,B,C)
be a linear system as in (
1
) with scattering function
\phi
, and suppose that
\Gamma_{\phi}
is trace class. Let
\phi_{(x)}(t)=\phi(t+2x)
.
Then the tau function of
(-A,B,C)
is defined to be
\tau(x)=\det(I+\Gamma_{\phi_{(x)}})
, for

.

This tau function is analogous to the tau function introduced by Jimbo, Miwa and Ueno
[
25
]
to describe the isomonodromy of rational differential equations, and generalizes the classical theta function. Mumford
[
33
]
constructed solutions of the cubic nonlinear Schrödinger,
KdV
,
mKdV
and sine-Gordon equations in terms of classical theta functions on abelian varieties. Ercolani and McKean
[
14
]
extended the analysis of
KdV
to infinite-dimensional abelian varieties in which case the scattering function
\phi
is of rapid decay.

In
[
6
]
, we reinterpreted the results of
[
35
]
and
[
28
]
in terms of an algebra of operators on
H
with a special associative product, and showed how nonlinear differential equations such as
KdV
emerge from algebraic identities in this product. In the present paper, we continue this analysis by addressing the sinh-Gordon equation in section
5
.
The simplest Darboux transformation of a linear systems is
(-A,B,C)\mapsto(-A,B,-C)
which takes
R\mapsto-R
. In section
5
, we introduce a
2\times 2
block matrix system which enables use to introduce a tau function for the sinh-Gordon equation in the form
\det(I+\Gamma_{\phi_{(x)}})(I-\Gamma_{\phi_{(x)}})^{-1}
.

In
[
13
]
, the authors consider the algebra of integrable operators of the form
\lambda I+K
where
K
is an integral operator on
L^{2}(L)
for a curve
L
in
{\mathbb{C}}
with kernel

where
\sum_{j=1}^{N}f_{j}(z)g_{j}(z)=0
. They achieve several results using Riemann–Hilbert theory and make applications to some integrable operators in random matrix theory. Integrable operators of this form include the Christoffel–Darboux kernel from orthogonal polynomials when

is a real subinterval. In
[
41
]
, the authors systematically consider these kernels and their scaling limits for classical orthogonal polynomials and obtain integrable operators that are fundamental to random matrix theory. In this application, the Fredholm determinant
\det(I+\lambda K)
is of primary interest. In particular, they show that the Airy and Bessel kernels can be expressed as products of Hankel integral operators on
L^{2}(0,\infty)
.

In section
3
we give sufficient conditions
T\in{\mathcal{A}}
to be expressed as a product of Hankel integral operators.

3.
Products of Hankel operators

In what follows we shall require some of the concepts of abstract differential calculus as developed in
[
11
]
.
Let
S
be an associative and unital complex algebra. There is a natural multiplication map
\mu:S\otimes S\to S
,
\mu\bigl(\sum a_{j}\otimes b_{j}\bigr)=\sum a_{j}b_{j}
. We shall denote the nullspace of this map as
\Omega^{1}S
and so we have an exact sequence

(3.1)

0\longrightarrow\Omega^{1}S\longrightarrow S\otimes S\longrightarrow S\longrightarrow 0;

In this section, we shall mainly be interested in the case where
S={\mathbb{C}}(x)
, the space of rational functions. In these cases, the tensor product
S\otimes S
may be regarded as consisting of functions of the form
\sum f_{j}(x)g_{j}(y)
acting on a product space. There are several natural derivation maps that act on these spaces.

Definition 3.1
.

For
S={\mathbb{C}}(x)
define

(1)

d:S\rightarrow\Omega^{1}S

:f\mapsto df
or
f(x)\mapsto f(x)-f(y)
, the noncommutative differential;

(2)

\delta:S\otimes S\rightarrow S\otimes S:

f(x,y)\mapsto(x-y)f(x,y)
, the inner derivation on
S\otimes S
as an
S

x\in S
;

(3)

D:\Omega^{1}S\to S\otimes S:

fdg\mapsto f(x){\frac{g(x)-g(y)}{x-y}}

x-y
; in particular,
D\circ d:s\rightarrow S\otimes S:

g(x)\mapsto{\frac{g(x)-g(y)}{x-y}}
is the divided difference;

(4)

\partial:S\rightarrow S:

f(x)\mapsto f^{\prime}(x)
, the usual derivative.

See (
6.13

{\mathcal{S}}

We allow poles at

\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
, there exists a matrix function
T(x,y)\in M_{N\times N}({\mathbb{C}})
for

that satisfies

(3.9)

{\frac{\partial^{2}T}{\partial x^{2}}}(x,y)-{\frac{\partial^{2}T}{\partial y^{2}}}(x,y)=-2\Bigl({\frac{d}{dx}}T(x,x)\Bigr)T(x,y)\qquad(0<x<y)

and

(3.10)

{\frac{d}{dx}}\log\det(I+\lambda K_{x})=\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}T(x,x)\qquad(x>0).

Proof.

Using Proposition
3.2
, write
K
in the form
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Gamma_{\psi_{j}}\Gamma_{\phi_{j}}
with
\psi_{j},\phi_{j}\in L^{2}((0,\infty);{\mathbb{R}})
, or in matrix form

\begin{bmatrix}K&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{\psi_{1}}&\dots&\Gamma_{\psi_{N}}\\
0&\dots&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{\phi_{1}}&0\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\Gamma_{\phi_{N}}&0\end{bmatrix}.

Consider
N\times N
matrix functions

\Phi_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{1}&\dots&\psi_{N}\\
{}&0&{}\end{bmatrix},\Phi_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{1}&{}\\
\vdots&0\\
\phi_{N}&{}\end{bmatrix}.

First we factorize
K
as a product of matrix Hankel operators.
By Proposition
3.2

K
is a complex-linear combination of Hankel products
\Gamma_{\psi}\Gamma_{\phi}
, where
\psi,\phi\in L^{2}((0,\infty);{\mathbb{R}})
.
Given measurable
\phi:(0,\infty)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\int_{0}^{\infty}t|\phi(t)|^{2}dt
is finite, the Hankel operator with scattering function
\phi
is self-adjoint and Hilbert–Schmidt, hence can be realised by a linear system
(-A,B,C)
in continuous time with input and output space
{\mathbb{C}}

We can apply this to the real and imaginary parts of the entries of
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
, and introduce for

(-A_{j},B_{j},C_{j})=\Biggl(\begin{bmatrix}-A_{j1}&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\dots&-A_{jN}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}B_{j1}&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\dots&B_{jN}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}C_{j1}&\dots&C_{jN}\\
\vdots&\dots&\vdots\\
0&\dots&0\end{bmatrix}\Biggr).

We can suppose that there are linear systems
(-A_{1},B_{1},C_{1})
and
(-A_{2},B_{2},C_{2})
with state space
H

{\mathbb{C}}^{N}

\Phi_{2}(t)=C_{2}e^{-tA_{2}}B_{2}

(3.12)

\det(I+\lambda K)=\det(I+\lambda\Gamma_{\Phi_{1}}\Gamma_{\Phi_{2}})=\det\begin{bmatrix}I&\lambda\Gamma_{\Phi_{1}}\\
-\Gamma_{\Phi_{2}}&I\end{bmatrix}.

Let
\bigl(-{\hat{A}},{\hat{B}},{\hat{C}}\bigr)

\bigl(-{\hat{A}},{\hat{B}},{\hat{C}}\bigr)=\Biggl(\begin{bmatrix}-A_{1}&0\\
0&-A_{2}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0&B_{1}\\
B_{2}&0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\lambda C_{1}&0\\
0&-C_{2}\end{bmatrix}\Biggr)

which has scattering function

(3.13)

\hat{\Phi}(t)={\hat{C}}e^{-t{\hat{A}}}{\hat{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\lambda C_{1}e^{-tA_{1}}B_{1}\\
-C_{2}e^{-tA_{2}}B_{2}&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\lambda\Phi_{1}(t)\\

The operator function

and we clearly have

(3.15)

Its determinant satisfies

\det(I+R_{0})=\det(I+\Gamma_{\hat{\Phi}})=\det(I+\lambda K).

Let

{\hat{T}}(x,y)=-{\hat{C}}e^{-x{\hat{A}}}(I+{\hat{R}}_{x})^{-1}e^{-y{\hat{A}}}{\hat{B}}

To find this determinant, we consider the Gelfand–Levitan equation

{\hat{\Phi}}(x+y)+{\hat{T}}(x,y)+\int_{x}^{\infty}{\hat{T}}(x,z){\hat{\Phi}}(z+y)\,dz=0\qquad(0<x<y)

which has solution

as one verifies by substituting the given formula into the equation. Also, the Lyapunov equation gives

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}\Bigl((I+{\hat{R}}_{x})^{-1}{\frac{d{\hat{R}}_{x}}{dx}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle={\frac{d}{dx}}\log\det(I+{\hat{R}}_{x}).

Also,

is a solution of

∎

4.
A linear system for Darboux addition

The pair of linear systems
(-A,B,C)
giving
I+R_{x}
and
(-A,B,-C)
giving
I-R_{x}
are related by a Darboux transformation, as discussed in Theorem 3.4 of
[
6
]
. As there, we introduce the matricial system

(4.1)

\bigl(-{\hat{A}},{\hat{B}},{\hat{C}}\bigr)=\Biggl(\begin{bmatrix}-A&0\\
0&-A\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0&B\\
B&0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}C&0\\
0&C\end{bmatrix}\Biggr)

which has scattering function

{\hat{\Phi}}(t)={\hat{C}}e^{-t{\hat{A}}}{\hat{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&Ce^{-tA}\\
Ce^{-tA}B&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\phi(t)\\
\phi(t)&0\end{bmatrix}.

Let

\displaystyle{\hat{R}}_{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{-t{\hat{A}}}{\hat{B}}{\hat{C}}e^{-t{\hat{A}}}\,dt

(4.2)

\displaystyle=\int_{x}^{\infty}\begin{bmatrix}0&e^{-tA}BCe^{-tA}\\
e^{-tA}BCe^{-tA}&0\end{bmatrix}\,dt=\begin{bmatrix}0&R_{x}\\
R_{x}&0\end{bmatrix}.

so that when
I\pm R_{x}
are invertible, we have

\hat{F}_{x}=(I+\hat{R}_{x})^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}(I-R_{x}^{2})^{-1}&-R(I-R_{x}^{2})^{-1}\\
-R(I-R_{x}^{2})^{-1}&(I-R_{x}^{2})^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

We can then define

(4.3)

\displaystyle{\hat{T}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-{\hat{C}}e^{-t{\hat{A}}}(I+{\hat{R}}_{x})^{-1}e^{-y{\hat{A}}}{\hat{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}W(x,y)&V(x,y)\\
V(x,y)&W(x,y)\end{bmatrix}

where

(4.4)

\displaystyle W(x,y)

\displaystyle=Ce^{-xA}(I-R^{2}_{x})^{-1}R_{x}e^{-yA}B

(4.5)

\displaystyle V(x,y)

\displaystyle=-Ce^{-xA}(I-R_{x}^{2})^{-1}e^{-yA}B.

Proposition 4.1
.

(i)

The functions
V(x,x)
and

S(x)=\log\det(I+R_{x})-\log\det(I-R_{x})

satisfy

(4.6)

{\frac{d}{dx}}S(x)=2V(x,x).

(ii)

The Fredholm determinant satisfies

(4.7)

\det(I-\Gamma_{\phi_{x}}^{2})=\exp\Bigl(-4\int_{x}^{\infty}(t-x)V(t,t)^{2}\,dt\Bigr),

(iii)

and with
q(x)=-4\lfloor A\rfloor
,
the function

(4.8)

U(x)=\exp\Bigl(2\int_{x}^{\infty}V(t,t)\,dt\Bigr)=\exp(-S(x))

(4.9)

{\frac{d^{2}U}{dx^{2}}}=q(x)U(x).

Proof.

(i) From Lyapunov’s equation, we have

\displaystyle{\frac{d}{dx}}S(x)

\displaystyle={\frac{d}{dx}}\Bigl(\log\det(I+R_{x})-\log\det(I-R_{x})\Bigr)

\displaystyle={\hbox{trace}}\Bigl((I+R_{x})^{-1}R^{\prime}_{x}+(I-R_{x})^{-1}R_{x}^{\prime}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=-2{\hbox{trace}}\Bigl((I-R_{x}^{2})^{-1}e^{-xA}BCe^{-xA}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=-2Ce^{-xA}(I-R_{x}^{2})^{-1}e^{-xA}B

(4.10)

\displaystyle=2V(x,x).

(ii) Then

(4.11)

-V(x,x)=Ce^{-xA}(I-R_{x}^{2})^{-1}e^{-xA}B=\bigl\lfloor(I-R_{x})^{-1}(I+R_{x})\bigr\rfloor

so by Lemma
2.5

\displaystyle-{\frac{d}{dx}}V(x,x)

\displaystyle={\frac{\partial}{\partial x}}\bigl\lfloor(I-R_{x})^{-1}(I+R_{x})\bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=\bigl\lfloor-2A-2(I-R_{x})^{-1}(AR_{x}+R_{x}A)(I-R_{x})^{-1}\bigr\rfloor

(4.12)

\displaystyle=-2\bigl\lfloor A\bigr\rfloor-2V(x,x)^{2}.

and likewise

(4.13)

2W(x,x)=Ce^{-xA}2R_{x}(I-R_{x}^{2})^{-1}e^{-xA}B=\bigl\lfloor 2R_{x}(I-R_{x})^{-1}(I+R_{x})\bigr\rfloor

so

\displaystyle 2{\frac{d}{dx}}W(x,x)

\displaystyle={\frac{\partial}{\partial x}}\bigl\lfloor 2R_{x}(I-R_{x})^{-1}(I+R_{x})\bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=\bigl\lfloor-4(I-R_{x})^{-1}(AR_{x}+R_{x}A)(I-R_{x})^{-1}\bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=-4Ce^{-xA}(I-R_{x}^{2})^{-1}e^{-xA}BCe^{-xA}(I-R_{x}^{2})^{-1}e^{-xA}B

(4.14)

\displaystyle=-4V(x,x)^{2}

We have

\displaystyle-4V(x,x)^{2}

\displaystyle=2{\frac{d}{dx}}W(x,x)

\displaystyle={\hbox{trace}}\,\hat{T}(x,z)

\displaystyle=-{\hbox{trace}}\,\hat{C}e^{-x\hat{A}}(I+\hat{R}_{x})^{-1}e^{-x\hat{A}}\hat{B}

\displaystyle=-{\frac{d}{dx}}{\hbox{trace}}(I+\hat{R}_{x})^{-1}e^{-x\hat{A}}\hat{B}\hat{C}e^{-x\hat{A}}

\displaystyle={\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}}{\hbox{trace}}\log(I+\hat{R}_{x})

\displaystyle={\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}}\log\det(I+\hat{R}_{x})

\displaystyle={\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}}\log\det(I-R_{x}^{2}).

(iii) Also

satisfies

, so as in Riccati’s equation

\displaystyle{\frac{U^{\prime\prime}(x)}{U(x)}}-\Bigl({\frac{U^{\prime}(x)}{U(x)}}\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=-2{\frac{d}{dx}}V(x,x)

\displaystyle=-4\lfloor A\rfloor-4V(x,x)^{2}

\displaystyle=-4\lfloor A\rfloor-\Bigl({\frac{U^{\prime}(x)}{U(x)}}\Bigr)^{2}

5.
Solutions of the sinh-Gordon equation

Howland
[
24
]
observed that Hankel matrices are analogous to one-dimensional Schrödinger operators with the role of the Laplacian played by Carleman’s operator

(5.1)

\Gamma f(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\frac{f(y)dy}{x+y}}\qquad(f\in L^{2}(0,\infty)).

Power
[
36
]
gives several unitarily equivalent forms of this operator. To obtain such operators in terms of linear systems, we recall Proposition 2.1 from
[
3
]
.
Let
H=L^{2}((0,\infty);{\mathbb{C}})
which has dense linear subspace

{\mathcal{D}}(A)=\{f\in L^{2}((0,\infty);{{\mathbb{C}}}):yf(y)\in L^{2}((0,\infty);{\mathbb{C}})
. Suppose that
h\in L^{\infty}((0,\infty);{\mathbb{R}})
and
h(y)/\sqrt{y}\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
. Then we let

\displaystyle A:{\mathcal{D}}(A)\rightarrow H:\quad f(y)\mapsto yf(y);

\displaystyle B_{t}:{\mathbb{C}}\rightarrow H:\quad\beta\mapsto h(y)\exp\Bigl(-{\frac{t}{y}}\Bigr)\beta

(5.2)

\displaystyle C_{t}:H\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}:\quad f\mapsto\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\Bigl(-{\frac{t}{y}}\Bigr)h(y)f(y)\,dy

where the input and output operators depend upon the real parameter
t>0
. Then we introduce the scattering function

(5.3)

\phi(x;t)=C_{t}e^{-xA}B_{t}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\Bigl(-xy-{\frac{2t}{y}}\Bigr)h(y)^{2}\,dy\qquad(t>0)

which satisfies the linear PDE

(5.4)

{\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial x\partial t}}=2\phi,

which may be interpreted as a linear counterpart of the sinh-Gordon equation. We also introduce

R_{(x;t)}=\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{-uA}B_{t}C_{t}e^{-uA}\,du.

In the following result, the pair
(x,t)
may be regarded as light-cone co-ordinates, rather than space and time.

Theorem 5.1
.

For the linear system (
5
), let

(5.5)

S(x;t)=\log\det(I+R_{(x;t)})-\log\det(I-R_{(x;t)}).

Then
S
gives a solution of the sinh-Gordon equation

(5.6)

{\frac{\partial^{2}S}{\partial x\,\partial t}}=2\sinh 2S.

Proof.

As an integral operator on
L^{2}(0,\infty)
,
R_{(x;t)}
has a kernel

(5.7)

{\frac{h(y)h(z)}{y+z}}\exp\Bigl(-x(y+z)-t\Bigl({\frac{1}{y}}+{\frac{1}{z}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\qquad(y,z>0)

which has the form of a Howland operator. This
R_{(x;t)}
evidently defines a Hilbert–Schmidt linear operator on
L^{2}(0,\infty)
.
Suppose for simplicity that
h
is real-valued. Then
R_{(x;t)}
is the Schur product of the Carleman operator
\Gamma
from (
5.1
) with kernel
1/(y+z)
and the tensor product of
h(y)e^{-xy-t/y}
with itself. Power
[
36
]
showed that
\Gamma
has spectrum
[0,\pi]
, hence
R_{(x;t)}
is self-adjoint and positive with trace

\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}\,R_{(x;t)}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\frac{h(y)^{2}}{2y}}e^{{}_{-}2xy-2t/y}\,dy\leq{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{h(y)^{2}}{y}\,dy.

So
R_{(x;t)}
is trace class and there exists
x_{0}>0
such that for all
x>x_{0}
, we have
\|R_{(x;t)}\|<1
; hence
I\pm R_{(x;t)}
are invertible. (Similar results hold for complex
h
by polarization.)

Note that due to the special form of the linear system

\displaystyle-{\frac{\partial R_{(x;t)}}{\partial t}}

\displaystyle=A^{-1}R_{(x;t)}+R_{(x;t)}A^{-1}

\displaystyle=A^{-1}(AR_{(x;t)}+R_{(x;t)}A)A^{-1}

\displaystyle=-A^{-1}{\frac{\partial R_{(x;t)}}{\partial x}}A^{-1}

(5.8)

\displaystyle=A^{-1}e^{-xA}B_{t}C_{t}e^{-xA}A^{-1}

gives an operator of rank one so

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}}

\displaystyle={\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}}\Bigl((I+R_{(x;t)})^{-1}{\frac{\partial R_{(x;t)}}{\partial t}}+(I-R_{(x;t)})^{-1}{\frac{\partial R_{(x;t)}}{\partial t}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=C_{t}e^{-xA}A^{-1}(I-R_{(x;t)}^{2})^{-1}A^{-1}e^{-xA}B_{t}.

We write
2S(x;t)=\int_{x}^{\infty}\psi(s;t)ds
,
where
\psi(x;t)=-4V(x,x)
as in (
4
). By (
4
), we have

(5.9)

2{\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}}=-16\lfloor A\rfloor-\psi^{2}

so by integrating with respect to
x
we deduce

-2\psi(x;t)=8{\frac{\partial}{\partial x}}\log\det(I+R_{(x;t)})-\int_{x}^{\infty}\psi(y;t)^{2}\,dy,

which implies when we differentiate with respect to
t
and multiply by
\psi
, that

(5.10)

-2\psi{\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}}=8\psi{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\partial t}}\log\det(I+R_{(x;t)})-\psi(x;t)\int_{x}^{\infty}{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\psi(y;t)^{2}\,dy.

We also have

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}}

\displaystyle=2\lfloor A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\rfloor\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}\rfloor

\displaystyle\qquad+2\lfloor(I-R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\rfloor\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}(I-R_{(x;t)})^{-1}\rfloor

(5.11)

\displaystyle\qquad-4\lfloor(I-R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\rfloor-4\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}(I-R_{(x;t)})^{-1}\rfloor,

and from (
5
),

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\lfloor A\rfloor}{\partial t}}

\displaystyle=\lfloor A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\rfloor\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A\rfloor

\displaystyle\qquad+\lfloor A(I+R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\rfloor\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}\rfloor

(5.12)

\displaystyle\qquad-\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A\rfloor-\lfloor A(I+R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\rfloor.

Also, from (
5.9
) we have

(5.13)

{\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial t\partial x}}=-8{\frac{\partial\lfloor A\rfloor}{\partial t}}-\psi{\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}}.

We need to combine the equations (
5.10
) and (
5.13
) and thereby obtain the sinh-Gordon equation.
The expression (
5
) involves factors
I-R
; whereas (
5
) involves only
I+R
; we reconcile these by multiplying by
\psi=4\lfloor(I+R)/(I-R)\rfloor
, which cancels out the factors of
I-R
.
From (
5
), we have

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\partial t}}\log\det(I+R_{(x;t)})

\displaystyle=-\lfloor A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\rfloor\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}\rfloor+\lfloor A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\rfloor

(5.14)

\displaystyle+\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}\rfloor;

hence from the
\ast
product (
2.6
) we obtain

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\partial x}}\log\det(I+R_{(x;t)})

\displaystyle=-4\bigl\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}\bigr\rfloor\bigl\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})(I-R_{(x;t)})^{-1}\bigr\rfloor\bigl\lfloor A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\rfloor

\displaystyle\qquad+4\bigl\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}\bigr\rfloor\bigl\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})(I-R_{(x;t)})^{-1}\bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+4\bigl\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})(I-R_{(x;t)})^{-1}\bigr\rfloor\bigl\lfloor A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}\bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=-4\bigl\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})(I-R_{(x;t)})^{-1}-(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A\bigr\rfloor\bigl\lfloor A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\bigr\rfloor

(5.15)

\displaystyle\qquad+\psi-4\bigl\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A\bigr\rfloor+\psi-4\bigl\lfloor A(I+R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\partial x}}\log\det(I+R_{(x;t)})

\displaystyle=4\bigl\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A\bigr\rfloor\bigl\lfloor A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle\quad-4\bigl\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})(I-R_{(x;t)})^{-1}\bigr\rfloor\bigl\lfloor A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle\qquad+\psi-4\bigl\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A\bigr\rfloor+\psi-4\bigl\lfloor A(I+R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=4\bigl\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A\bigr\rfloor\bigl\lfloor A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle\qquad+4\bigl\lfloor A(I+R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\bigr\rfloor-\psi

\displaystyle\qquad+\psi-4\bigl\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A\bigr\rfloor+\psi-4\bigl\lfloor A(I+R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=\psi+4\bigl\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A\bigr\rfloor\bigl\lfloor A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})\bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle\qquad-4\bigl\lfloor(I+R_{(x;t)})A^{-1}(I+R_{(x;t)})^{-1}A\bigr\rfloor

(5.16)

\displaystyle=\psi+2{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bigl\lfloor A\bigr\rfloor}.

Hence (
5.10
) gives

(5.17)

-2\psi{\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}}=16{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\bigl\lfloor A\bigr\rfloor+8\psi-\psi(x;t)\int_{x}^{\infty}{\frac{\partial\psi^{2}}{\partial t}}\,dy

so (
5.13
) becomes

2{\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial x\partial t}}=8\psi-2\psi(x;t)\int_{x}^{\infty}\psi(y;t){\frac{\partial\psi(y;t)}{\partial t}}\,dy,

so

(5.18)

-{\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}}+\int_{x}^{\infty}\psi(y;t)\int_{y}^{\infty}\psi(v;t){\frac{\partial\psi(v;t)}{\partial t}}\,dvdy=4\int_{x}^{\infty}\psi(y;t)\,dy,

so we need to compare the integral in (
5.18
) with the
\sinh 2S
term in (
5.6
).

We introduce the integration operator
F_{\psi}:L^{2}(0,\infty)\rightarrow L^{2}(0,\infty)
, which depends upon
t>0
by

F_{\psi}f(x;t)=\int_{x}^{\infty}\psi(s;t)f(s)\,ds.

Choosing
f=1
, we obtain by induction the formula

F_{\psi}^{n}\circ 1(x;t)={\frac{1}{n!}}\Bigl(\int_{x}^{\infty}\psi(s;t)ds\Bigr)^{n}={\frac{1}{n!}}(F_{\psi}\circ 1)^{n}(x;t).

Now we express the right-hand side of (
5.6
) as a composition of operators

\displaystyle\sinh 2S

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\frac{2^{2n+1}S^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\frac{(F_{\psi}\circ 1)^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}F_{\psi}^{2n+1}\circ 1(x;t)=(I-F_{\psi}^{2})^{-1}F_{\psi}\circ 1(x;t).

Hence the equation (
5.18
) gives

-{\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}}=4(I-F_{\psi}^{2})^{-1}F_{\psi}\circ 1(x;t)

which gives

2{\frac{\partial^{2}S}{\partial t\partial x}}=4\sinh 2S.

which is the sinh-Gordon equation.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
\phi_{(x;t)}(y)=\phi(y+2x;t)
, with
\phi
as in Theorem
5.1
for (i) and (ii).

(i)

Then

(5.19)

S(x;t)=\log\det(I+\Gamma_{\phi_{(x;t)}})-\log\det(I-\Gamma_{\phi_{(x;t)}})

satisfies the sinh-Gordon equation (
5.6
).

(ii)

The function
\varphi(z;t)=\log\det(I-\Gamma_{\phi_{(z;t)}}^{2})
is holomorphic on
\{z:\mathop{{\rm Re}}z>x_{0}\}
for some
x_{0}>0
and
\varphi(z;t)\rightarrow 0
as
\mathop{{\rm Re}}z\rightarrow\infty
.

(iii)

Now let

\phi(x;s/2)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-xy-s/y}dy\qquad(\mathop{{\rm Re}}x>0,s>0).

Then the conclusion of (ii) holds for the associated Hankel operator
\Gamma_{\phi_{(z;t)}}
.

Proof.

(i) We have

\det(I+\lambda\Gamma_{\phi_{(x;t)}})=\det(I+\lambda\Theta_{x}^{\dagger}\Xi_{x})=\det(I+\lambda\Xi_{x}\Theta_{x}^{\dagger})=\det(I+\lambda R_{(x;t)})\qquad(\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}),

so the formulas (
5.5
) and (
5.19
) for
S(x;t)
are equivalent, and the result holds by Theorem
5.1
. See also
[
42
]
.

(ii) By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

|\phi_{(z;t)}(x)|^{2}\leq\|h\|^{2}_{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-xy-4t/y}|h(y)|^{2}dy\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(x+4\mathop{{\rm Re}}z)y}dy;

hence the Hilbert–Schmidt norm satisfies

\displaystyle\|\Gamma_{\phi_{(z;t)}}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{L}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}x|\phi_{(z;t)}(x)|^{2}dx

(5.20)

\displaystyle\leq\|h\|^{2}_{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl\{\int_{0}^{\infty}{\frac{xy}{x+4\mathop{{\rm Re}}z}}e^{-xy}dx\Bigr\}{\frac{e^{-4t/y}}{y}}|h(y)|^{2}dy

where the inner integral is bounded and converges to zero as
\mathop{{\rm Re}}z\rightarrow\infty
, so
\|\Gamma_{\phi_{(z;t)}}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{L}}^{2}}\rightarrow 0
by the dominated convergence theorem, hence the result.

(iii) The conclusion of Corollary
5.2
(ii) holds, but the hypotheses are not quite satisfied, so we provide a special argument. Let

(5.21)

K_{1}(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-z\cosh u)\cosh u\,du\qquad(\mathop{{\rm Re}}z>0)

be the modified Bessel function of the third kind of order
1
, also known as MacDonald’s function of the second kind, which is holomorphic on
\{z\>\mathop{{\rm Re}}z>0\}
with
|K_{1}(z)|\leq K_{1}(\mathop{{\rm Re}}z)
. This also satisfies

{\frac{e^{-t}}{t}}\leq K_{1}(t)\leq\sqrt{{\frac{\pi}{2t}}}e^{-t}+{\frac{e^{-t}}{t}}\qquad(t>0)

by simple estimates following from
\cosh u=1+2\sinh^{2}(u/2)
.
Then for
\alpha=0
, we have

(5.22)

\displaystyle\phi(x;s/2)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{{\frac{4s}{x}}}K_{1}(2\sqrt{sx})

so
\int_{0}^{\infty}x|\phi(x+2x_{0};s/2)|^{2}dx
is finite for all
x_{0},s>0
and we can deduce
\|\Gamma_{\phi_{(x_{0})}}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{2}}\rightarrow 0
as
x_{0}\rightarrow\infty
, as in Corollary
5.2
(ii).

∎

6.
Hankel determinants

The Wishart ensemble is a standard model in random matrix theory
[
16
, page 91]
which produces the Laguerre ensemble of random eigenvalues on
(0,\infty)
. The Laguerre ensemble is in turn used as a model in the theory of wireless transmission, and in an integral model of quantum field theory at finite temperature
[
8
]
. The
KP
,
KdV
and sinh-Gordon differential equation can be interpreted as aspects of a single hierarchy.

One can produce particular solutions to such differential equations in terms of the Painlevé transcendental functions. Chen and Its
[
8
]
considered the singularly perturbed Laguerre weight
y^{\alpha}e^{-y-s/y}
for
y>0
and
\alpha>0
. Let
h(y)=y^{\alpha/2}
, and observe that

(6.1)

\phi(x;s/2)=\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{\alpha}e^{-xy-s/y}\,dy

is a moment generating function for moments of this weight, so we have

(-1)^{j+k}\Bigl({\frac{\partial^{j+k}}{\partial x^{j+k}}}\Bigr)_{x=1}\phi(x;s/2)=\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{\alpha+j+k}e^{-y-s/y}\,dy.

Then the corresponding Hankel determinants are given by

(6.2)

D_{n}(s)=\det\Bigl[\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{\alpha+j+k}e^{-y-s/y}\,dy\Bigr]_{j,k=0}^{n-1}\qquad(s>0;n=1,2,\dots),

which turns out to be the isomonodromic tau function for a particular sequence of solutions of Painlevé
\text{III}^{\prime}
differential equation. This terminology refers to the work of Okamoto.

Example 6.1
.

Let
s=0
and
\alpha>0
, let
G(z)
be Barnes
G
-function such that
G(0)=1
and
G(z+1)=\Gamma(z)G(z)
, where
\Gamma(z)
is Euler’s gamma function. Then by (1.19) of
[
8
]

D_{n}(0)={\frac{G(n+1)G(n+\alpha+1)}{G(\alpha+1)}}.

Let
\alpha=0
. By Andréief’s identity
[
15
]
, we have

(6.3)

\det\bigl[(-1)^{j+k-2}K_{1}^{(j+k-2)}(t)\bigr]_{j,k=1}^{n}\\
={\frac{1}{n!}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dots\int_{0}^{\infty}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(\cosh u_{j}-\cosh u_{k})^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}e^{-t\cosh u_{j}}\cosh u_{j}\,du_{j}

with
K_{1}
as in (
5.21
). With the change of variable
x_{j}=2/(1+\cosh u_{j})
, this may be written as

{\frac{1}{n!}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dots\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\Bigl(-t\Bigl({\frac{2}{x_{j}}}-1\Bigr)\Bigr)\prod_{j=1}^{n}{\frac{x_{j}^{-2n}(2-x_{j})}{\sqrt{1-x_{j}}}}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(x_{j}-x_{k})^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}dx_{j}.

This is associated with the generalized unitary ensemble for the scalar potential

(6.4)

u_{n}(x)=t\Bigl({\frac{2}{x}}-1\Bigr)+2n\log x+(1/2)\log(1-x)-\log(2-x),

with

(6.5)

u_{n}^{\prime}(x)={\frac{-2t}{x^{2}}}+{\frac{2n}{x}}-{\frac{1/2}{1-x}}+{\frac{1}{2-x}},

and

(6.6)

u_{n}^{\prime\prime}(x)={\frac{4t}{x^{3}}}+{\frac{-2n}{x^{2}}}-{\frac{1/2}{(1-x)^{2}}}+{\frac{1}{(2-x)^{2}}},

Dyson introduced a technique for funding the asymptotics of such determinants, which has been refined by Chen
[
10
, page 4603]
and others into the Coulomb fluid method. In this, the point distribution
n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{j}}
of the
x_{j}
is approximated in the weak topology on probability measures as
n\rightarrow\infty
by a continuous distribution
\rho
which is found by potential theory. We write

\mathop{\mathcal{H}}\rho(x)=\mathop{\mathrm{p.v.}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\frac{\rho(y)}{x-y}}{\frac{dy}{\pi}}

for the Hilbert transform of
\rho
.

Fix
\xi\in(0,1)
and write
t
for
2n\xi
.
The zeros of
u_{n}^{\prime}(x)
may be approximated by the zeros of
h(x)=(2n-1/2)(x-1)(x-2)(x-t/n)
by Rouché’s theorem, so we locate a real zero of
u_{n}^{\prime}(x)
near to
2\xi
. Note that

{\frac{u_{n}^{\prime\prime}(2\xi)}{n}}={\frac{1}{2\xi^{2}}}-{\frac{1}{2n(1-2\xi)^{2}}}+{\frac{1}{n(2-\xi)^{2}}}

is positive for all
0<\xi<1/2
and all sufficiently large
n
, so
u_{n}
is convex near to this minimizer.
For a continuous
V:[0,1]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, we introduce the energy functional

(6.7)

E_{V}(\rho)=\Bigl\{\int_{0}^{1}V(x)\rho(x)\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\log{\frac{1}{|x-y|}}\rho(x)\rho(y)\,dxdy\Bigr\},

where
V
indicates an electric field and the
\log|x-y|
term involves electrostatic interaction between points
x,y\in[0,1]
, where the charge distribution is
\rho
. The equilibrium distribution is defined to be the minimizer of this energy functional; see
[
38
]
.

We consider the approximate scaled potential that is given by the first two summands in (
6.4
), namely

(6.8)

u_{0}(x)=2\xi\Bigl({\frac{2}{x}}-1\Bigr)+2\log x,

with

u_{0}^{\prime}(x)={\frac{-2\xi}{x^{2}}}+{\frac{2}{x}};\quad u_{0}^{\prime\prime}(x)={\frac{8\xi}{x^{3}}}-{\frac{2}{x^{2}}}\qquad(0<x<1)

which has a local minimum at
x=2\xi
.

Proposition 6.2
.

(i)

The equilibrium distribution for
u_{0}
is
\sigma_{0}
, where

\sigma_{0}(x)={\frac{\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}{\pi\sqrt{ab}}}\Bigl({\frac{\xi(a+b)}{abx}}+{\frac{2\xi}{x^{2}}}-{\frac{1}{x}}\Bigr)\qquad(a<x<b),

is supported on
(a,b)
, where

a,b={\frac{4\pi\xi}{(2\pi-1)^{2}}}\bigl(2\pi\pm\sqrt{4\pi-1}\bigr).

(ii)

For
1/4<\xi<1/2
, the free logarithmic Sobolev inequality

(6.9)

E_{u_{0}}(p)-E_{u_{0}}(\sigma_{0})\leq{\frac{2}{8\xi-2}}\int_{a}^{b}(2\pi\mathop{\mathcal{H}}p(x)-u_{0}^{\prime}(x))^{2}p(x)\,dx.

holds for all probability density functions
p\in L^{3}(a,b)
.

(iii)

Let
\rho_{n}
be the minimizer of
E_{u_{n}/n}(\rho)
over all continuous
\rho
such that
\rho\geq 0
and
\int_{0}^{1}\rho(x)dx=1
.
Suppose that
\rho_{n}
has a continuous density which is supported in a single interval. Then
\rho_{n}
converges weakly as
n\rightarrow\infty
to
\sigma_{0}
.

Proof.

(i) By standard results
[
38
]
Theorem 1.3 and p. 215, there exists a unique continuous probability density function on
[0,1]
that attains the minimum of
E_{V}(\rho)
for continuous
V
.
We aim to solve the singular integral problem

u_{0}^{\prime}(x)=\mathop{\mathrm{p.v.}}\int_{a}^{b}{\frac{2\sigma_{0}(y)}{x-y}}\,dy=2\pi\mathop{\mathcal{H}}\sigma_{0}(x)

for the equilibrium density
\sigma_{0}
subject to the constraint
\int_{a}^{b}\sigma_{0}(y)\,dy=1
. Since
u_{0}
is convex, the solution is continuous and positive on a single interval

(a,b)
, where
0<a<b<1
, and we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}(x)

\displaystyle={\frac{\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}{2\pi^{2}}}\int_{a}^{b}{\frac{u_{0}^{\prime}(x)-u_{0}^{\prime}(y)}{x-y}}{\frac{dy}{\sqrt{(b-y)(y-a)}}}

\displaystyle={\frac{\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}{2\pi^{2}}}\int_{a}^{b}\Bigl({\frac{4\xi(x+y)}{x^{2}y^{2}}}-{\frac{2}{xy}}\Bigr){\frac{dy}{\sqrt{(b-y)(y-a)}}}

(6.10)

\displaystyle={\frac{\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}{\pi\sqrt{ab}}}\Bigl({\frac{\xi(a+b)}{abx}}+{\frac{2\xi}{x^{2}}}-{\frac{1}{x}}\Bigr)\qquad(a<x<b),

where the final step follows from the substitution
y=a+(b-a)\sin^{2}\phi
and some elementary integrals. The initial factor has the form of a semicircular distribution, which is modulated by a rational function with poles at
x=0
outside of the support interval
(a,b)
. The endpoints of this interval are subject to the constraints

0=\int_{a}^{b}{\frac{u_{0}^{\prime}(x)\,dx}{\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}}=-{\frac{2\pi\xi(a+b)}{ab\sqrt{ab}}}+{\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{ab}}},

and

1=\int_{a}^{b}{\frac{xu_{0}^{\prime}(x)\,dx}{\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}}=2\pi-{\frac{4\pi\xi}{\sqrt{ab}}},

so that

(6.11)

a,b={\frac{2\pi^{2}\xi}{(\pi-1/2)^{2}}}\mp{\frac{2\pi\xi}{\pi-1/2}}\sqrt{{\frac{\pi^{2}}{(\pi-1/2)^{2}}}-1}.

(ii) We have
u_{0}^{\prime\prime}(x)=(8x\xi-2x)/x^{3}>8\xi-2>0
. The result follows from Theorem 3.1 of
[
1
]
.

(iii) Returning to the original potential, we have
u_{n}=nu_{0}+f
where the correction term
f(x)=(1/2)\log(1-x)-\log(2-x)
. There is a corresponding correction to the equilibrium density,
\rho_{n}=\sigma_{0}+\tilde{\rho}/n
where

\displaystyle{\tilde{\rho}}(x)

\displaystyle={\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}}\mathop{\mathrm{p.v.}}\int_{a}^{b}{\frac{\sqrt{(b-y)(y-a)}}{y-x}}f^{\prime}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle={\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}}\ \mathop{\mathrm{p.v.}}\int_{a}^{b}{\frac{\sqrt{b-y)(y-a)}}{y-x}}\Bigl({\frac{-1/2}{1-y}}+{\frac{1}{2-y}}\Bigr)\,dy

\displaystyle={\frac{1}{4\pi\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}}\Bigl({\frac{\sqrt{(1-a)(1-b)}}{1-x}}+1+{\frac{2\sqrt{(2-a)(2-b)}}{x-2}}\Bigr)

where all the roots are positive square roots and we have used the evaluation (258) in the Appendix to
[
10
]
. This has the form of an arcsine distribution, modulated by a rational function with poles outside
(a,b)
.
∎

Biane
[
1
]
interprets the tangent space to
L^{2}(\sigma_{0})
as a space of functions with finite Dirichlet norm, which has a natural interpretation in the present context. Let
\psi:(a,b)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be a differentiable function such that

(6.12)

\sigma(\psi)^{2}={\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}}\int_{a}^{b}{\frac{\psi(x)}{\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}}\ \mathop{\mathrm{p.v.}}\int_{a}^{b}{\frac{\sqrt{(b-y)(y-x)}}{x-y}}\psi^{\prime}(y)\,dydx,

is finite. Then by results of
[
9
]
, the asymptotic distribution of a linear statistic
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi(x_{j})
is a Gaussian with mean
N\int_{a}^{b}\psi(x)\sigma_{0}(x)\,dx
and variance

\sigma(\psi)^{2}
where
\sigma(\psi)
depends upon
[a,b]
but not upon the equilibrium distribution
\sigma_{0}
itself. In terms of the Chebyshev polynomials, we introduce
\psi((a+b)/2(+(b-a)t/2)=\sum_{k}a_{k}T_{k}(t)
where

{\frac{a_{k}}{2}}=\int_{-1}^{1}\psi((a+b)/2+(b-a)t/2)T_{k}(t){\frac{dt}{\pi\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}}

so the variance is
\sigma(\psi)^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}ka_{k}^{2}/4.
Then we introduce
\phi(\theta)=\psi((a+b)/2+(b-a)\cos\theta/2)
has an expansion

\phi(\theta)=(1/2)a_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}\cos k\theta,

which gives a self-adjoint and Hilbert–Schmidt Hankel matrix
\Gamma_{\phi}=[a_{j+k}]_{j,k=0}^{\infty}
.

Remark 6.3
.

In their study of the Laguerre unitary ensemble, Forrester and Witte
[
17
]
obtain solutions of
\mathrm{PIII}^{\prime}
from similar formulas involving the modified Bessel function of the first kind
I_{\nu}
,
although their Corollary 4.5 has a Toeplitz rather than a Hankel determinant.

Example 6.4
.

For
s,x,\alpha>0
in (
6.1
), consider

v(z)=-\alpha\log z+\sqrt{sx}\Bigl(z+{\frac{1}{z}}\Bigr)\qquad(z>0).

By a simple scaling, this is the scalar potential that one needs to study the determinant (
6.2
) with weight

y^{\alpha}e^{-y-s/y}
on
(0,\infty)
. Then
v
is convex with

(6.13)

{\frac{v^{\prime}(z)-v^{\prime}(y)}{z-y}}={\frac{\alpha}{z}}+\sqrt{sx}\Bigl({\frac{z+y}{z^{2}y^{2}}}\Bigr)

a sum of products of rational functions of
z
and
y
, and there exist
b>a>0
, such that the integral equation

(6.14)

v(z)=2\int_{a}^{b}\log|z-y|\rho(y)dy+C\qquad(z\in(a,b))

for some
C\in{\mathbb{R}}
with the normalization
\rho(y)\geq 0
and

\int_{a}^{b}\rho(y)\,dy=1,

has solution

\rho(z)={\frac{\sqrt{(b-z)(z-a)}}{2\pi\sqrt{ab}}}\Bigl[{\frac{\alpha}{z}}+{\frac{\sqrt{sx}}{z^{2}}}+{\frac{\sqrt{sx}}{2z}}\Bigl({\frac{1}{a}}+{\frac{1}{b}}\Bigr)\Bigr]\qquad(a<z<b)

by a similar argument to (
6
). We need
a>0
to ensure that the resulting
\rho
is integrable. The relevant integrals arise from (248) in the appendix to
[
10
]
. Let
(P_{j,N})_{j=0}^{\infty}
be the monic polynomials that are orthogonal for the weight
e^{-Nv(z)}
, which gives rise to the integral equation (
6.14
) multiplied through by
N
, with the normalizations preserved. Then using the results of
[
9
]
, we observe that
N\int_{a}^{b}\rho(x)\,dx
gives the number of zeros of
P_{N,N}(z)
and have an asymptotic formula

\log P_{N,N}(z)\sim N\int_{a}^{b}\log(z-y)\rho(y)dy\qquad(N\rightarrow\infty)

for
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}.
This type of double scaling is standard in random matrix theory and addresses the singularity of the weight; see section 4 of
[
9
]
for more details.
