Simplifying the Type A A Argyres–Douglas Landscape
Christopher Beem,​, Mario Martone,​, Matteo Sacchi,​, Palash Singh,​, and Jake Stedman

Abstract
A well-established organisational principle for Argyres–Douglas-type 𝒩 = 2 \mathcal{N}=2 superconformal field theories in four dimensions is to characterise such theories by the data defining a(n irregular) Hitchin system on ℂ ​ ℙ 1 \mathbb{CP}^{1} . The dictionary between Hitchin system data and various features of the corresponding SCFT has been studied extensively, but the overall structure of the resulting space of SCFTs still appears quite complicated. In this work, we systematically delineate a variety of simplifications that arise within this class of constructions due to several large classes of isomorphisms between SCFTs associated with inequivalent Hitchin system data (and their exactly marginal gaugings). We restrict to the most studied class of theories, namely the type A A theories without outer automorphism twists.

Introduction

Four-dimensional superconformal field theories (SCFTs) with eight supercharges provide a rich playground for studying strongly-coupled physics and non-trivial dualities. Over the years, a large number of such theories have been constructed as non-Lagrangian SCFTs. A special class of such theories is class

Such marked points are normally referred to as regular punctures in theories of class

Irregular Hitchin systems and notation

The theories that we consider in this paper are realised by compactification of 6d

3
3

3

Setups with more than one irregular singularity typically give rise to theories consisting of a non-conformal gauging of non-Lagrangian conformal Argyres–Douglas theories. For the analysis of such theories arising from a Hitchin system with multiple irregular singularities in the
A_{1}

Bonelli:2011aa

Nanopoulos:2010zb

The regular singularity is characterised by the presence of a simple pole for the Higgs field with residue lying in a nilpotent co-adjoint orbit of
\mathfrak{sl}_{N}
. The nilpotent orbit can be identified with an integer partition
[Y]
of
N
. The general strategy of characterising these theories in terms of the corresponding Hitchin system, and in particular in terms of the choice of regular and irregular singularity, has been pursued extensively by Xie and collaborators starting with
Xie:2012hs

Following
witten2008gauge
;
Xie:2012hs
and many others in the mathematics literature, we adopt a local description of the Higgs field behaviour near the irregular singularity which involves potentially fractional powers of the local coordinate (
i.e.
, passing to a ramified cover), but in exchange the coefficient matrices of the singular terms lie in a Cartan subalgebra so can in fact be simultaneously diagonalised. This requires the presence of a branch cut across which there is a compensating gauge transformation. The characterisation of irregular singularities using only the local behaviour of the Higgs field omits some details of the structure of the underlying Higgs bundle. A more careful treatment can be given in the setting of
ramifiedly good filtered Higgs bundles
as in
Fredrickson:2017yka
;
Fredrickson:2017jcf
. Fortunately, it seems that this level of detail is not required for the purpose of characterising SCFTs; the restriction to the simpler data of the Higgs field singularity is in line with the approach taken in the pioneering series of works by Xie and collaborators.

For
y

\Phi(y)=\frac{u_{k}}{y^{2+\frac{k}{n}}}\mathrm{d}y+\sum_{l=-n}^{k-1}\frac{u_{l}}{y^{2+\frac{l}{n}}}\mathrm{d}y+\cdots~,

(6)

2.2
Coulomb branch spectrum

The Seiberg–Witten curve of a 4d
\mathcal{N}=2
SCFT captures many properties of the effective field theory on its Coulomb branch
Seiberg:1994rs
;
Seiberg:1994aj
. The relevant data that we discuss here is the spectrum of Coulomb branch operators (
i.e.
, the set of scaling dimensions/
\mathfrak{u}(1)_{r}
charges of Coulomb branch operators), the rank of the flavour symmetry, and the number of
\mathcal{N}=2
supersymmetric exactly marginal deformations (
i.e.
, the dimension of the
\mathcal{N}=2
conformal manifold). In the class
\mathcal{S}
setting, the Seiberg–Witten curve is identified with the spectral curve of the Hitchin system
Gaiotto:2009hg
. To match the notation most commonly used in the class
\mathcal{S}
setting, we change coordinates from
y
to
z=\frac{1}{y}
so the irregular singularity is at
z\rightarrow\infty
. The spectral curve of the Hitchin system is determined by the Higgs field according to

T^{\ast}\mathbb{CP}^{1}\supset\Sigma\;:\;\mathrm{det}(\lambda-\Phi(z))=0~,

(23)

where
\lambda=x\,\mathrm{d}z
is identified with the Seiberg–Witten differential and
x
parameterises the fibre direction.

For
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{N}
, the spectral curve takes the following general form,

x^{N}+\sum_{i=2}^{N}\phi_{i}(z)x^{N-i}=0~,

(24)

where the
\phi_{i}(z)
are Laurent polynomials in
z
and
\phi_{i}(z)(dz)^{i}\in H^{0}(\mathbb{CP}^{1},K_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}^{\otimes i})
. The integral of the Seiberg–Witten differential around closed cycles in
\Sigma
gives the mass of BPS particles, and thus one assigns scaling dimensions

\Delta[\lambda]=1~~\Longrightarrow~~\Delta[x]+\Delta[z]=1~,

(25)

For a monomial in the equation for the spectral curve of the form
v_{i,j}x^{i}z^{j}
, the dimension of the coefficient
v_{i,j}
can be computed by demanding that each term in the spectral curve has the same scaling dimension overall. Given the leading term in (
24
), this requires

\Delta[v_{i,j}]=(N-i)\Delta[x]-j\Delta[z]~.

(26)

These parameters are assigned different physical interpretations depending on their scaling dimensions:

•

\Delta[v_{i,j}]=0

\Longrightarrow

\mathcal{N}=2
exactly marginal deformations (EMD);

•

0<\Delta[v_{i,j}]<1

\Longrightarrow
relevant chiral couplings;

•

\Delta[v_{i,j}]=1

\Longrightarrow
mass deformations;

•

\Delta[v_{i,j}]>1

\Longrightarrow
Coulomb branch operator expectation values.

In any SCFT (without decoupled free vector multiplets), all parameters of dimension
0\leqslant\Delta_{1}<1
correspond to sources for Coulomb branch operators and are paired with vevs of those operators, which carry dimension
1<\Delta_{2}\leqslant 2
such that
\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}=2

. In particular, associated with EMDs there will be vevs of exactly marginal operators with
\Delta[v_{i,j}]=2
. We will be primarily interested in mass deformations, exactly marginal deformations, and the Coulomb branch spectrum of these theories.

For the
D_{p}^{b}\left(\mathfrak{sl}_{N},[Y]\right)
theories considered here, the Seiberg–Witten curve should in principle be computed as a spectral curve of the corresponding Hitchin system by identifying the global Higgs fields compatible with the prescribed regular and irregular singularities. However, absent a simple characterisation of these global solutions, a procedure for indirectly determining the Coulomb branch spectrum—the “Newton polygon method”—has been proposed in
Xie:2012hs
.

In this approach, the differentials in (

\phi_{i}(z)=v_{i,q_{i}}\,z^{q_{i}}+v_{i,q_{i}-1}\,z^{q_{i}-1}+\cdots+v_{i,-p_{i}}\,z^{-p_{i}}~.

(27)

Here

are the

associated to the partition

The most naïve assumption—that all

present in this expression are non-zero and can be varied independently—is incorrect as can be checked in various simple examples. However, it appears that
for all monomials whose coefficients scale as
\Delta\geqslant 1

Xie:2017vaf

.

Summarising, the Seiberg–Witten curve for
D_{p}^{b}\left(\mathfrak{sl}_{N},[Y]\right)
can be computed by writing down all monomials that appear in (

and

. This naïve expression contains some extra terms

corresponding to those

A necessary self-consistency condition for this procedure is that the number of (naïve) terms with scaling dimension
0\leqslant\Delta<1
must be greater than or equal to the number of terms with scaling dimension
2-\Delta

is in fact sufficient to guarantee a physically sensible interpretation of the corresponding Hitchin system as arising from a four-dimensional SCFT. Namely, if and only if there are more dimension-two terms than (naïve) dimension-zero terms then the construction is declared inconsistent. This condition, also referred to as the
conformality constraint
in
Xie:2017aqx
, leads to the following condition on the commutant (Levi) sub-algebras associated to the coefficients

\mathfrak{l}_{k}=\mathfrak{l}_{k-1}=\cdots=\mathfrak{l}_{-n+1}\supset\mathfrak{l}_{-n}~,

(28)

where

is the Levi sub-algebra corresponding to the Cartan element

in (

2.3
Flavour symmetries and central charges

The flavour symmetry algebras of the
D_{p}\left(\mathfrak{sl}_{N},[Y]\right)
theories generically receive contributions from both the regular and the irregular singularities,

\mathfrak{g}_{F}^{\text{irreg}}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{Y}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{F}~.

(29)

There can be accidental symmetry enhancements such that this is a proper inclusion. In practice, these symmetry patterns can be usually understood from the quiver description of the 3d mirror theory reviewed in the next subsection. Above,
\mathfrak{g}_{Y}
is the (standard) symmetry associated to a regular puncture labelled by the partition
[Y]=[N^{l_{N}},\dots,1^{l_{1}}]
, which is given by
Gaiotto:2009we

\mathfrak{g}_{Y}=\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{N}\mathfrak{u}(l_{i})\right)/\mathfrak{u}(1)~.

(30)

For the maximal puncture
[Y]=[1^{N}]
one has an associated symmetry algebra of
\mathfrak{g}_{[1^{N}]}=\mathfrak{su}(N)
, while for the empty puncture
[Y]=[N]
there is no contribution to the flavour symmetry—
\mathfrak{g}_{[N]}=\emptyset
.

The contribution from the irregular singularity has been argued to take the form
Xie:2017vaf
;
Xie:2017aqx
,

\mathfrak{g}_{F}^{\text{irreg}}=\mathfrak{u}(N-b)\oplus\mathfrak{u}(1)^{m-1}~,

(31)

where
m=\gcd(b,p)
. In particular, the rank is given by

\text{rk}\left(\mathfrak{g}_{F}^{\text{irreg}}\right)=N-b+\gcd(b,p)-1~.

(32)

This simplifies considerably for type I (
b=N
) and type II (
b=N-1)
theories
Giacomelli:2017ckh
.

The flavour central charge associated to some symmetry
\mathfrak{g}
is given by the following ’t Hooft anomaly
Tachikawa:2011dz
;
Chacaltana:2012zy
:

k_{\mathfrak{g}}=-2\text{Tr}R\,T^{a}\,T^{b}\eqqcolon-2\text{Tr}R\,\mathfrak{g}^{2}~,

(33)

where
T^{a}
are the generators of
\mathfrak{g}
. The
a
and
c
central charges are defined similarly using the conformal/Weyl anomaly. The problem of computing these central charges for
D_{p}^{b}\left(\mathfrak{sl}_{N},[Y]\right)
can be simplified to the problem of computing these for the cases with maximal regular puncture
D_{p}^{b}\left(\mathfrak{sl}_{N},[1^{N}]\right)
, which we shall refer to as the UV SCFT for the remainder of this section. This is because the former can be thought of as the IR limit of the RG flow triggered by giving a nilpotent vev of the form
[Y]
to the
\mathfrak{su}(N)
moment map of the UV SCFT. Thus the central charges for the IR theory can be computed in terms of the UV central charges by matching anomalies along the RG flow.

Furthermore, a linear combination of
a
and
c
can be determined in terms of the Coulomb branch spectrum via the Shapere–Tachikawa relation
Shapere:2008zf
6
6

6

It is possible to directly access the values of
a
and
c
from purely Coulomb branch data, but it requires more detailed information about the stratification of the singular locus
Martone:2020nsy
;
Argyres:2020wmq
which is not readily available in our case.

2a-c=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{r}(2\Delta_{i}-1)~,

(34)

where
r
is the rank of the 4d SCFT, defined as the complex dimension of the Coulomb branch, and
\Delta_{i}
are the scaling dimensions of the Coulomb branch operators.

Note that the flavour central charge of the
\mathfrak{su}(N-b)\subset\mathfrak{g}_{F}^{\text{irreg}}
symmetry does not change under the nilpotent Higgsing discussed above and has been conjectured to be
Xie:2017aqx
;
Xie:2017vaf

k_{\mathfrak{su}(N-b)}=2\left(N-b+\frac{n}{n+k}\right)~.

(35)

Meanwhile the flavour central charge of the
\mathfrak{su}(N)
symmetry associated to the maximal puncture of the UV SCFT is conjectured to be
Xie:2016evu

k_{\mathfrak{su}(N)}=2\left(N-\frac{n}{n+k}\right)~.

(36)

The
c
central charge of the UV SCFT is not known in complete generality. However for
b=N,N-1
, the
c
central charge is known from the geometric engineering construction to be
Shapere:2008zf
;
Xie:2015rpa
;
Giacomelli:2017ckh

c=\Big(2r+\text{rk}(\mathfrak{g}_{F})\Big)\frac{\Delta_{\text{max}}}{12}+\frac{r}{6}~,

(37)

where
\Delta_{\text{max}}
is the scaling dimension of the Coulomb branch operator with the largest scaling dimension. These expressions along with the Shapere–Tachikawa relation determine all the central charges of the UV SCFT.

2.3.1
IR central charges from anomaly matching

We detail here the computation of flavour central charges for theories with partially reduced punctures. The most involved aspect of the calculation is identifying the contribution of the various free hypermultiplets that arise under the corresponding nilpotent Higgsing. These are to be subtracted from the UV central charges in order to recover those of the irreducible IR SCFT.

The difference between
a
and
c
central charges is determined by the cubic
\mathfrak{u}(1)_{r}
anomaly, which is unbroken by Higgs branch RG flows so the anomaly can be matched by simply subtracting the contributions of free hypermultiplets
Giacomelli:2020ryy
,

48(a^{\rm UV}-c^{\rm UV})=48(a^{\rm IR}-c^{\rm IR})+2(\#\text{ of Nambu--Goldstone modes})~.

(38)

Here the number of free hypermultiplets is, alternatively, the quaternionic dimension of the Higgs branch stratum associated to the Higgsing in question. For a general nilpotent Higgsing associated to the partition
[Y]
, the number of Nambu–Goldstone modes associated with flat directions for the moment map operators is given by (
cf.

Tachikawa:2015bga
),

\#\text{ of Nambu--Goldstone modes}=\frac{1}{2}\left\{N^{2}-\sum_{i}s_{i}^{2}\right\}\,,\,\,\,\text{where}\,\,\,{}^{t}Y=[s_{1},s_{2},\dots]~.

(39)

Meanwhile, the flavour symmetry of the IR theory is given by
\mathfrak{g}_{Y}
as above, and the flavour central charge of any of the
\mathfrak{su}(l_{i})
subalgebras can be expressed as

k^{\rm IR}_{\mathfrak{su}(l_{i})}=I_{\mathfrak{su}(l_{i})\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{su}(N)}\,k^{\rm UV}_{\mathfrak{su}(N)}+k_{\mathfrak{su}(l_{i})}^{\rm NG}~,

(40)

where
k_{\mathfrak{su}(l_{i})}^{\rm NG}
is the contribution from the massless Nambu–Goldstone modes to the above ’t Hooft anomaly in the IR and
I_{\mathfrak{su}(l_{i})\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{su}(N)}
is the embedding index. To understand the former contribution, we take a closer look at the
\mathfrak{su}(N)
representation theory.

Let us consider the Higgsing to a specific regular puncture with partition

[Y]=[N^{l_{N}},\dots,\alpha^{l_{\alpha}},\dots,1^{l_{1}}]~.

(41)

The fundamental representation of
\mathfrak{su}(N)
then decomposes under the remaining global symmetry according to

\text{fund}_{\mathfrak{su}(N)}\longrightarrow\left(V_{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}},l_{\alpha}\right)\bigoplus_{\alpha\neq i=1}^{N}\,\left(V_{\frac{i-1}{2}},l_{i}\right)~,

(42)

where the first entry,
V_{j}
, is the spin-
j
representation of the embedded
\mathfrak{su}(2)
, while the second entry,
l_{i}
, denotes the fundamental representation of
\mathfrak{su}(l_{i})
and the trivial representation of the remaining
\mathfrak{su}(l_{j\neq i})\subset\mathfrak{g}_{Y}
subalgebras. We will follow this notation for the rest of this work.

With respect to the moment map operator, one should consider instead the decomposition of the adjoint representation. This is obtained by exploiting the identity
\text{fund}\otimes\overline{\text{fund}}=\text{adj}\oplus\text{sing}
, giving

\begin{split}\text{adj}_{\mathfrak{su}(N)}\oplus(V_{0},1)\longrightarrow\left(V_{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}\otimes V_{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}},l_{\alpha}\otimes\overline{l_{\alpha}}\right)&\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}^{N}\left(V_{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}\otimes V_{\frac{i-1}{2}},l_{\alpha}\otimes\overline{l_{i}}\oplus l_{i}\otimes\overline{l_{\alpha}}\right)\\
&\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
i,j\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}^{N}\left(V_{\frac{i-1}{2}}\otimes V_{\frac{j-1}{2}},l_{i}\otimes\overline{l_{j}}\oplus l_{j}\otimes\overline{l_{i}}\right)~.\end{split}

(43)

The tensor product of
\mathfrak{su}(2)
representations decomposes as follows:

V_{\frac{i-1}{2}}\otimes V_{\frac{j-1}{2}}=\bigoplus_{\frac{|i-j|}{2}}^{\frac{i+j-2}{2}}V_{k}~.

(44)

The highest weight component in each of the resulting
\mathfrak{su}(2)
representations, say
V_{\frac{M-1}{2}}
, becomes massive under the nilpotent vev and is integrated out leaving behind
M-1
components which rearrange into
\frac{M-1}{2}
Nambu–Goldstone modes in the IR whose contribution we want to count. Hence, we find that the total number of Nambu–Goldstone modes in the IR is

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=\frac{|i-j|}{2}}^{\frac{i+j-2}{2}}2k=\frac{(i+j-|i-j|)(i+j-2+|i-j|)}{8}\eqqcolon n_{i,j}~.

(45)

This result along with the explicit decomposition of the adjoint representation into representations of
\mathfrak{g}_{Y}
tells us the exact number of Nambu–Goldstone modes that transform in various
\mathfrak{su}(l_{\alpha})
subalgebra representations.

A Nambu–Goldstone mode transforming in a representation
\mathcal{R}
of
\mathfrak{su}(l_{i})
contributes
-4T_{\mathfrak{su}(l_{\alpha})}(\mathcal{R})
to
k^{\rm IR}_{\mathfrak{su}(l_{i})}
, where
T_{\mathfrak{su}(l_{\alpha})}(\mathcal{R})
is the Dynkin index of the representation. For
\mathfrak{su}(l)
, we have

T_{\mathfrak{su}(l)}(\text{sing})=0\;,\quad T_{\mathfrak{su}(l)}(\text{fund})=\frac{1}{2}\;,\quad T_{\mathfrak{su}(l)}(\text{adj})=T_{\mathfrak{su}(l)}(\text{fund}\otimes\overline{\text{fund}})=l~.

(46)

Moreover, the Dynkin index distributes over direct sum of representations

T(\mathcal{R}_{i}\oplus\mathcal{R}_{j})=T(\mathcal{R}_{i})+T(\mathcal{R}_{j})~.

(47)

Therefore we sum up all these contribution to explicitly write down
k_{i}^{\rm NG}
as follows:

\begin{split}k_{\mathfrak{su}(l_{i})}^{\rm NG}=-4n_{\alpha,\alpha}T_{\mathfrak{su}(l_{\alpha})}\left(l_{\alpha}\otimes\overline{l_{\alpha}}\right)&-4\sum_{\alpha\neq i=1}^{N}n_{\alpha,i}T_{\mathfrak{su}(l_{\alpha})}(l_{\alpha}\otimes\overline{l_{i}}\oplus l_{i}\otimes\overline{l_{\alpha}})\\
&-4\sum_{\alpha\neq i,j=1}^{N}n_{i,j}T_{\mathfrak{su}(l_{\alpha})}\left(l_{i}\otimes\overline{l_{j}}\oplus l_{j}\otimes\overline{l_{i}}\right)~.\end{split}

(48)

This can be simplified by using the above expressions for the Dynkin index

\begin{split}k_{\mathfrak{su}(l_{i})}^{\rm NG}=-4\left(n_{\alpha,\alpha}l_{\alpha}+\sum_{\alpha\neq i=1}^{N}n_{\alpha,i}\left(\frac{1}{2}\times l_{i}+l_{i}\times\frac{1}{2}\right)+0\right)~,\end{split}

(49)

where we have used the fact that
l_{\alpha}\otimes l_{i}
implies that there are a total of
l_{i}
Nambu–Goldstone modes in the fundamental representation of
\mathfrak{su}(l_{\alpha})
to calculate the Dynkin index, therefore
T_{\mathfrak{su}(l_{\alpha})}\left(l_{\alpha}\otimes l_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\times l_{i}
. This can be simplified to obtain the following simple expression

k_{\mathfrak{su}(l_{i})}^{\rm NG}=-4\sum_{i=1}^{N}n_{\alpha,i}\,l_{i}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{(\alpha+i-|\alpha-i|)(\alpha+i-2+|\alpha-i|)}{2}l_{i}~.

(50)

Combining with (
40
) this determines the flavour central charges of the IR theory.

2.4
3d quivers: mirrors and direct reductions

A key tool in the analysis of Argyres–Douglas SCFTs characterised by irregular Hitchin systems is the description of related three-dimensional theories as Lagrangian quiver gauge theories. In particular, the three-dimensional reductions of these theories admit Lagrangian quiver mirror descriptions, which can be taken as a physical counterpart to the statement that the corresponding moduli spaces of irregular Higgs bundles can be related to certain Nakajima quiver varieties
Nakajima:1994nid

boalch2008irregular
;
boalch2020diagrams

These are therefore (non-mirror) dual descriptions of the three-dimensional reductions of the theories in question. In this section we review the state of the art of assigning Lagrangian quiver descriptions to the 3d mirrors of Argyres–Douglas SCFTs of type
A
and, for type I, also to the 3d reductions .
