Quantum topology optimization of ground structures using noisy intermediate-scale quantum devices
Yuki Sato, Ruho Kondo, Satoshi Koide, Seiji Kajita

Abstract
To arrive at some viable product design, product development processes frequently use numerical simulations and mathematical programming techniques. Topology optimization, in particular, is one of the most promising techniques for generating insightful design choices. Topology optimization problems reduce to an NP-hard combinatorial optimization problem, where the combination of the existence or absence of the material at some positions is optimized. In this study, we examine the usage of quantum computers as a potential solution to topology optimization problems. The proposed method consists of two variational quantum algorithms (VQAs): the first solves the state equilibrium equation for all conceivable material configurations, while the second amplifies the likelihood of an optimal configuration in quantum superposition using the first VQA’s quantum state. Several experiments, including a real device experiment, show that the proposed method successfully obtained the optimal configurations. These findings suggest that quantum computers could be a potential tool for solving topology optimization problems and they open the window to the near-future product designs.



Introduction

Because of its potential to improve product development, topology optimization has been effectively employed in a variety of industries, including the automotive industry
[
1
]
.
The central notion of topology optimization is to replace structural optimization problems with a material distribution problem, which is a problem getting binary variable distributions with a value of
1
represent the “exist” and
0
the “absence” of the material.
Topology optimization is categorized into two types: the one is for continuum structures
[
1
]
and the other for discrete structures, such as truss structures
[
2
]
.
For discrete structures, truss topology optimization methods were widely studied.
Ground structure methods, in which several pre-placed nodes connected by as many edges as feasible are prepared and then the existence or absence of each edge is optimized using the mixed integer programming, are commonly built in these methods
[
3
,
4
]
.
It is well-known that determining minimum weight truss structures with discrete member sizes is an NP-hard problems
[
2
]
, and thus the heuristic-based approaches have been developed
[
5
,
6
]
.
Such difficulty arises from the nested nature of ground structure problems; the inner problem is for solving the state equilibrium and the outer one is for the structural optimization using the inner solution.
That is, searching all conceivable structures is almost impossible, and hence the optimized results greatly depends on what heuristic algorithms used.

In this study, we focus on the use of quantum computers as a possible approach to tackle this mathematically hard problem.
Quantum computers have widely gained a lot of attention in recent decades due to their promise to perform quicker computations with fewer memories than those of classical computers
[
7
]
.
To deal with topology optimization problems on quantum computers, two key techniques are required.
The first is the method of solving state equilibrium equations which explain the physical phenomena we are interested in, and the second is a way for solving material distribution problems, which are intrinsically combinatorial optimization problems.
Quantum algorithms for linear systems are necessary for the first one because issues for solving the state equilibrium equation commonly reduce to problems for solving linear systems.
Several quantum algorithms for linear systems have been proposed, and some of them theoretically promise an exponential speedup over classical algorithms
[
7
,
8
]
, whereas fault-torelant quantum computers may be required to implement them.
There also have been proposed classical-quantum hybrid algorithms for solving linear systems
[
9
,
10
]
, targeting the implementation of so-called noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices.
However, these algorithms embed the solution of linear systems into the amplitude of quantum states, which raises difficulty in efficiently extracting the solution to the classical computer.
As for the second one, on the other hand, there are several quantum algorithms for combinatorial optimization
[
11
,
12
]
, which are based on the well-known Grover’s algorithm
[
13
]
.
There are also classical-quantum hybrid algorithms for combinatorial optimization
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
, most of which rely on the quantum approximate optimization algorithms (QAOA)
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
.
Although bridging the difficulties of solving linear systems and combinatorial optimization is non-trivial, two crucial parts have already been identified in the literature.
We focus on the classical-quantum hybrid algorithms among these core techniques because they are predicted to execute some useful calculations using NISQ devices.
Our proposed approach is implemented using a genuine device in this study.

Variational quantum algorithms (VQAs)
[
20
]
are one of the most possible classical-quantum hybrid algorithms for NISQ devices.
VQAs commonly express a cost function as the expectation value of a set of observables, which is then assessed on a quantum computer utilizing a trial quantum state prepared by a parametrized quantum circuit.
To minimize the cost function, the cost function is iteratively evaluated by updating the classical parameters.
The most well-known VQAs are variational quantum eigensolvers
[
21
,
22
]
which were proposed to calculate the lowest eigenvalue of a system and have been extended for excited states
[
23
]
.
VQAs for linear systems and combinatorial optimization problems could also be found in the literature, as previously noted.
They are, insufficient for quantum computing-based topology optimization because the conventional quantum algorithms for combinatorial optimization struggle to handle the nested nature of topology optimization problems, in which the outer problem solves the structural optimization and the inner one solves the state equilibrium.
Therefore, we offer a quantum algorithm for topology optimization issues in which two VQAs are run sequentially, with the ground structure method as the aim.
The first solves the state equilibrium equation for all conceivable material configurations and prepares a quantum state in which the state variables for all possible material configurations are embedded.
Although the state variables are embedded in the amplitude of a quantum state, our focus is not to obtain the state variables themselves, but to obtain an optimal structure whose performance is calculated based on the state variables.
The second algorithm performs such optimization, that is, it prepares a parametrized quantum state describing the probability of each possible configuration and then amplifies the probability of an optimal configuration, using the quantum state obtained by the first VQA.

Results

Problem definition

This study focuses on the topology optimization problem for heat path designs
[
24
]
, based on the ground structure method
[
2
]
.
Let
V:=\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
E:=\{e_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
be the node and edge sets, respectively, where
N
is the number of nodes and
m
is the number of edges.
A ground structure can then be defined as the undirected graph
G=(V,E)
.
Here, we assume that each edge consists of one of two materials with different thermal conductivity values, either
\lambda
or
\lambda^{\prime}=\varepsilon\lambda
, where
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
Based on this assumption, we consider the problem of assigning either material to each edge.
Let
\boldsymbol{x}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
be the structure to be optimized.

x_{j}=1
indicates that the
j
-th edge has the thermal conductivity
\lambda
, and
x_{j}=0
means that
j
-th edge has the thermal conductivity
\lambda^{\prime}=\varepsilon\lambda
.
Then, an optimization problem to find an optimal configuration of two kinds of edges can be formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\{0,1\}^{m}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{U}(x))

(1)

subject to:

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}K_{j}\left((1-\varepsilon)x_{j}+\varepsilon\right)\right)\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{F},

(2)

where
\mathcal{L}
is the objective function to be minimized,
\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the temperature vector whose
i
-th component represents the temperature of the
i
-th node at steady-state for configuration
\boldsymbol{x}
,
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the heat source vector whose
i
-th component represents a heat source at the
i
-th node, and
K_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the element stiffness matrix of the
j
-th edge, which is determined by the material property
\lambda
, as defined in the supplementary material.
The constraint condition in Eq. (
2
) represents the governing equation of steady-state heat conduction, i.e., thermal equilibrium state.
The goal is to find an optimal structure
\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}
that minimizes
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{x}))
.

Specifically, in the present study, we look at how to give material attributes to each edge of ground structures so that the temperature at the steady-state on a predetermined target node
v_{\text{target}}
could be minimized under the existence of a heat source node
v_{\text{source}}
and a heat sink node
v_{\text{base}}
.
In this case, the
k
-th component of the heat source vector,
F_{k}
, is given as

\displaystyle F_{k}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }v_{k}=v_{\text{source}}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

(3)

The objective function
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{x}))
is formulated as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(U(\boldsymbol{x}))=U_{l}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{U}_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the
l
-th component of the temperature vector
\boldsymbol{U}
, with
l
being the index satisfying
v_{l}=v_{\text{target}}
.
We tackle the above problem by a two-step optimization procedure.

Formulation

The use of two quantum states to represent the temperature
\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{x})
and the structure
\boldsymbol{x}
, as well as executing separate optimization procedures to acquire these two quantum states, is a crucial feature of the present method.
In the first step, a parametrizred quantum state
\Ket{\psi(\theta)}
is trained such that its amplitude has the information about the
i
-th component of
\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{x})
, i.e.,
\braket{\boldsymbol{x},i|\psi(\theta)}\propto\boldsymbol{U}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})
where
\Bra{\boldsymbol{x},i}=\Bra{\boldsymbol{x}}\otimes\Bra{i}
.
Here, we summarize the result of our proposed formulation.
See Methods
Methods
section for detailed derivation of the optimization problems.

The problem for solving the governing equation in Eq. (
2
) can be formulated as the optimization problem for encoding the state
\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{x})
into the amplitude of
\Ket{\psi(\theta)}
, which is given as

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\quad F_{u}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\dfrac{\Braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\Ket{b}\Bra{b}\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}{\Braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})|A|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}},

(5)

where
A
and
\Ket{b}
are respectively defined as

A:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\dfrac{1}{2}\left((1+\varepsilon)I^{\otimes m}-(1-\varepsilon)Z_{j}\right)\otimes K_{j},

(6)

and

\displaystyle\Ket{b}:=\Ket{+}^{\otimes m}\otimes\Ket{f},

(7)

where
Z_{j}
represents the Pauli operator
Z
applied to the
j
-th qubit,
\Ket{f}
is the quantum state whose amplitude corresponds to the heat source vector
\boldsymbol{F}
, and
\Ket{+}:=\left(\Ket{0}+\Ket{1}\right)/\sqrt{2}
.
Owing to the linearity of the governing equation (
2
), we may assume that the norm of the heat source vector
\boldsymbol{F}
is equal to
1
, without losing generality, allowing us to express the vector
\boldsymbol{F}
as a quantum state vector
\Ket{f}
,
The heat source vector in (
3
) can be represented using a quantum state as

\Ket{f}:=\Ket{v_{\text{source}}},

(8)

where
\Ket{v_{\text{source}}}
is a vector whose component
v_{\text{source}}
is
1
corresponding to the node, while the other components are
0
.
The optimized parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\ast}
is now fixed in the following step.

In the second step, we present another parametrized quantum state
\Ket{\phi(\eta)}
whose bit string outcome is measured by the computational basis that matches with the structure
\boldsymbol{x}
.
Let
O
denote a Hermitian operator defined as

O:=\Ket{v_{\text{target}}}\Bra{v_{\text{target}}},

(9)

where
\Ket{v_{\text{target}}}
represents a vector in which the component corresponding to the node
v_{\text{target}}
is
1
and the other components are
0
.
Then we redefine the objective function in Eq. (
4
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{x}))=\boldsymbol{U}^{\dagger}O\boldsymbol{U},

(10)

where
\dagger
represents the Hermitian transpose.
Now we consider the weighted sum of the objective function values for all possible structures and optimize the weighting coefficients to minimize the weighted sum value, resulting in only the weighting coefficient corresponding to the optimal solution with the minimum objective function value being
1
and anything else
0
.
For all feasible structures, the weighted sum of the objective function values can be stated as

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{m}}P(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\dagger}O\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{x})\propto\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{m}}P(\boldsymbol{x})\Braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\ast})|\left(\Ket{x}\Bra{x}\otimes O\right)|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\ast})},

(11)

where
P(\boldsymbol{x})
represents the probability of each possible structure.
Hence, the objective function describes the expectation value of the target node temperature over all the ground structures.
It is obvious that the optimal
P(\boldsymbol{x})
corresponds to the optimal solution
\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}
of the original problem.
Parametrizing
P(\boldsymbol{x})
by parameters
\boldsymbol{\eta}
through a parametrized quantum state
\Ket{\phi(\eta)}
, i.e.,
P_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\left|\Braket{x|\phi(\boldsymbol{\eta})}\right|^{2}
, we can formulate the optimization problem for obtaining the optimal structure as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\quad F_{s}(\boldsymbol{\eta})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{m}}P_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{x})\Braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\ast})|\left(\Ket{x}\Bra{x}\otimes O\right)|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\ast})}.

(12)

The quantum state
\Ket{\phi(\eta^{\ast})}
is measured once the optimized parameters
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\ast}
are obtained.
When all the preceding steps are completed successfully, the likelihood of identifying optimum structures
\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}
will become
1
.

section for detailed derivation of Eq. (
12
).

Discussion

The proposed method requires
m+\lceil\log_{2}N\rceil
qubits to represent the temperature vector for all possible structures, while classical approaches require
\mathcal{O}(N)
bits to store the temperature vector for a certain structure.
When we assume that the ground structures are given as
k
-regular graph, the number of edges is given as
m=kN/2
.
Therefore, the number of qubits required in the proposed method is
kN/2+\lceil\log_{2}N\rceil
, which is roughly the same order as that in the classical approaches with respect to
N
when
N
is large.
Because the proposed method deals with all structures simultaneously, whereas the classical approaches usually deal with a certain structure at once, this scaling is an advantage of the proposed approach in terms of the resources required.
When we treat all structures simultaneously in the classical approaches, we require
\mathcal{O}(N)2^{m}
bits to store the temperature vector for all structures at once.
Actually, in the classical approaches, it is not required to store the temperature vector for all structures at once, while
2^{m}
calculations are required for the brute-force approach.

The results show that the proposed method is capable of obtaining the exact optimal structure for the three edges-design problem.
The key property of the proposed method is that no amplitude estimation with respect to
\Ket{\Psi(\theta)}
is required.
Although existing quantum linear solvers including the HHL algorithm
[
7
]
may efficiently solve linear systems, the solutions are encoded in the amplitude of quantum states.
To decode the solution to the classical computer, amplitude estimation
[
29
,
30
]
or quantum state tomography
[
31
,
32
]
is necessary.
The proposed method, in contrast, leverages the quantum state encoding the linear system solutions as the second step optimization module, and the solution we want to extract to the classical computer is the bit string representing the optimal structure.
Hence, amplitude estimation and quantum state tomography are not required.
This would be a noteworthy feature of the proposed method.
As a result of the experimental findings, it was discovered that the effect of the noise and the statistical error created severe oscillations, potentially raising the issue of scalability.
In the future, we will perform a detailed analysis of these factors’ impact on scalability.

We believe that our proposed method can also be applied to other research fields where mixed integer programming problems exist, given the mathematical structure of the topology optimization problems based on the ground structures.

Methods

Formulation for quantum computing

Here, we formulate two optimization problems to solve the original optimization problem in Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
) based on quantum computing.
The one is for solving the governing equation (
2
), and is introduced in the following section.
The other is for the original optimization problem in Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
), which is formulated below.

Solving the linear system
\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{F}
by variational quantum algorithm

First, we derive an optimization problem for using quantum computing to solve the governing equation (
2
).
We assume that
N=2^{n}
, which means that the governing equation with the dimension of
N
can be handled in a
n
-qubits quantum system.
In the supplemental information, the case where this assumption does not hold is examined.

Let
u_{i}^{(\boldsymbol{x})}
be defined as the
i
-th node temperature of the structure
\boldsymbol{x}
.
The vector
\Ket{\Psi}
is then defined as follows:

\displaystyle\Ket{\Psi}:=\dfrac{1}{2^{m/2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}^{(\boldsymbol{x})}\Ket{\boldsymbol{x}}\otimes\Ket{i},

(13)

which is the solution of the following linear system:

A\Ket{\Psi}=\Ket{b},

(14)

where
A
and
\Ket{b}
are respectively defined in Eqs (
6
) and (
7
).
We explain this relationship utilizing an example of
m=2
.
When
m=2
, the Hermitian
A
and the quantum state
\Ket{b}
can be written in the matrix form, as follows:

A=\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon K_{1}+\varepsilon K_{2}&O&O&O\\
O&\varepsilon K_{1}+K_{2}&O&O\\
O&O&K_{1}+\varepsilon K_{2}&O\\
O&O&O&K_{1}+K_{2}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(15)

\Ket{b}=\dfrac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{F}\\
\boldsymbol{F}\\
\boldsymbol{F}\\
\boldsymbol{F}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(16)

Therefore, solving Eq. (
14
) with respect to
\Ket{\Psi}
yields

\displaystyle\Ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=A^{-1}\Ket{b}=\dfrac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}(\varepsilon K_{1}+\varepsilon K_{2})^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}\\
(\varepsilon K_{1}+K_{2})^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}\\
(K_{1}+\varepsilon K_{2})^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}\\
(K_{1}+K_{2})^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\dfrac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{U}(0,0)\\
\boldsymbol{U}(0,1)\\
\boldsymbol{U}(1,0)\\
\boldsymbol{U}(1,1)\\
\end{bmatrix},

(17)

where
U(x_{1},x_{2})
represents the solution of Eq. (
2
).
That is, the solution of Eq. (
14
) includes the temperature vectors in all possible configurations.

To solve Eq. (
14
), we now introduce an objective function to be minimized, as follows:

\displaystyle E(\Ket{\Psi}):=\dfrac{1}{2}\Braket{\Psi|A|\Psi}-\sqrt{\Braket{\Psi\Ket{b}\Bra{b}\Psi}}.

(18)

Since the
A
is positive definite, owing to the positive definiteness of the stiffness matrix
K_{j}
, this objective function has a unique minimum, and the stationary condition, i.e.,
dE/d\ket{\Psi}
requiring that the gradient at the minimum equals to
0
yields

\displaystyle A\Ket{\Psi}=\dfrac{\Braket{b|\Psi}}{\|\Braket{b|\Psi}\|}\Ket{b},

(19)

which implies that Eq. (
14
) holds up to a global phase of
\Ket{\Psi}
at the minimum of Eq. (
18
).
Because the global phase in quantum states has no relevance, Eq. (
14
) may be solved by minimizing
E
in Eq. (
18
) with respect to
\Ket{\Psi}
.
It should noted here that the norm of the vector
\Ket{\Psi}
is not necessarily
1
because the operator
A
is not unitary.
This means that
\Ket{\Psi}
is not a quantum state.
Thus, to deal with the vector
\Ket{\Psi}
in quantum computers, we introduce a quantum state
\Ket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
and a parameter
r\in\mathbb{R}
and define
\Ket{\Psi}:=r\Ket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
.
Because the quantum state
\Ket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
is parametrized by parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with
p
being the number of parameters, we can replace the problem of minimizing
E
in Eq. (
18
) with respect to
\Ket{\Psi}
by a problem of finding parameters
(\boldsymbol{\theta},r)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}
that minimize
E^{\prime}
defined as

\displaystyle E^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\theta},r):=\dfrac{1}{2}r^{2}\Braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})|A|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}-r\sqrt{\Braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\Ket{b}\Bra{b}\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}.

(20)

The minimizer of
r
can calculated analytically due to the parabolic nature of
E^{\prime}
with respect to
r
, be obtained as

\displaystyle r^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\dfrac{\sqrt{\Braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\Ket{b}\Bra{b}\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}}{\Braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})|A|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}.

(21)

Then, the objective function to be minimized with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is derived, by substituting the minimizer of
r
in Eq. (
21
) into Eq. (
20
), as follows:

\displaystyle E^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\theta},r^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))=-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\Braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\Ket{b}\Bra{b}\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}{\Braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})|A|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}.

(22)

Omitting the constant
1/2
results in an objective function to be minimized for solving the governing equation (
2
) defined as

\displaystyle F_{u}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=-\dfrac{\Braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\Ket{b}\Bra{b}\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}{\Braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})|A|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}.

(23)

Consequently, the optimization problem in Eq.(

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\quad F_{u}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\dfrac{\Braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\Ket{b}\Bra{b}\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}{\Braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})|A|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}.

Solving structural optimization

Next, we formulate an another optimization problem for solving the original optimization problem in Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
) by quantum computing.
In particular, we consider the objective function expressed written in the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(U(\boldsymbol{x}))=\boldsymbol{U}^{\dagger}O\boldsymbol{U},

(25)

where
O
is a Hermitian matrix and
\dagger
represents the Hermitian transpose.
The purpose of structural optimization is to find the structure
\boldsymbol{x}
that minimize the objective function
\mathcal{L}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\ast}
denote the optimal parameters of the problem in Eq. (
24
).
Then, the weighted sum of the objective function values for all conceivable structures can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}}P(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\dagger}O\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=r^{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}}P(\boldsymbol{x})\Braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\ast})|\left(\Ket{\boldsymbol{x}}\Bra{\boldsymbol{x}}\otimes O\right)|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\ast})}

\displaystyle=r^{2}\Braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\ast})|\left(\rho\otimes O\right)|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\ast})},

(26)

where the weighting coefficient for each structure corresponds to its probability
P(\boldsymbol{x})
and

\displaystyle\rho:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}}P(\boldsymbol{x})\Ket{\boldsymbol{x}}\Bra{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(27)

When the likelihood of the structure with the least objective function value is highest, the weighed sum of the objective function achieves the minimum.
Consequently, minimizing Eq. (
26
) with respect to the probability
P(\boldsymbol{x})
yields the probability distribution of
P(\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast})=1
where
\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}
is the optimal structure.
Thus, we now parametrize the probability
P(\boldsymbol{x})
, by introducing a parametrized quantum state
\ket{\phi(\boldsymbol{\eta})}
on
m
qubits, as follows:

\displaystyle P_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\left|\Braket{\boldsymbol{x}|\phi(\boldsymbol{\eta})}\right|^{2},

(28)

which subsequently yields a parametrized mixed state
\rho(\boldsymbol{\phi})
as

\displaystyle\rho(\boldsymbol{\eta}):=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{m}}P_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{x})\Ket{\boldsymbol{x}}\Bra{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(29)

Omitting
r^{2}
, which is fixed after the first optimization step in Eq. (
5
) is completed and is constant with regard to
\boldsymbol{\eta}
, we may eventually state the structural optimization problem, as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\quad F_{s}(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\ast}),

(30)

where

\displaystyle F_{s}(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\Braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\left(\rho(\boldsymbol{\eta})\otimes O\right)|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}.

(31)

When the optimized parameters
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\ast}
is obtained, measuring the quantum state
\Ket{\phi(\eta^{\ast})}
produces the optimal structure
\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}
with a probability of
1
if the entire operation is completed successfully.
It is remarkable that the proposed method requires no amplitude estimation with respect to
\Ket{\psi(\theta)}
.
That is, the temperature obtained in the first stage is employed in the second step for structural optimization while it is embedded in the amplitude of a quantum state.
The bit string representing the optimal structure is the end result of the method, and this information is easily acquired from the quantum state.

Setting all parameters

For the initialization of parameters
\boldsymbol{\eta}

Denoting a parametrized quantum circuit by
U_{\eta}

\displaystyle\Ket{\phi(\boldsymbol{\eta})}=U_{\eta}\Ket{+}^{\otimes m}.

\boldsymbol{\eta}

When it is an odd number, the entangler, CZ gates in the ansatz, causes the phase difference among the structures in quantum superposition.

Algorithm

Based on the formulation in the previous sections, we now discuss the algorithm for quantum topology optimization.

1.

Initialize both parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}
to
\boldsymbol{0}
. The parameter initialization strategy is described in the supplementary information.

2.

Solve the optimization problem in Eq. (
5
) for obtaining state fields. Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
denote the obtained solution. The parameters which give the lowest objective function value in the optimization history are output as the solution
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.

3.

Solve the optimization problem in Eq. (
12
) for amplifying the probability of the optimum structures, using the solution
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
in Step 2 as
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\ast}
. Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
denote the obtained solutions. Again, the parameters which give the lowest objective function value in the optimization history are output as the solution
\bar{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
.

4.

Prepare the quantum state
\ket{\varphi(\bar{\boldsymbol{\eta}})}
and measure it in the computational basis. The structure corresponding to the bit string observed with the highest probability is output as the optimal structure.

The objective functions are assessed using quantum computing expectation evaluations during the optimization method.
The expectation evaluations are performed based on the extended Bell measurement
[
33
]
and the inversion test
[
34
]
.
The supplemental information provided a detailed explanation.
