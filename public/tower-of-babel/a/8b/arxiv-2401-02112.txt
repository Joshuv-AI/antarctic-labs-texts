Singularity-agnostic incomplete U-statistics for testing polynomial constraints in Gaussian covariance matrices
Dennis Leung, Nils Sturma

Abstract
. Testing the goodness-of-fit of a model with its defining functional constraints in the parameters could date back to Spearman 1927 , who analyzed the famous “tetrad” polynomial in the covariance matrix of the observed variables in a single factor model. Despite its long history, the Wald test typically employed to operationalize this approach could produce very inaccurate test sizes in many situations, even when the regular conditions for the classical normal asymptotics are met and a very large sample is available. Focusing on testing a polynomial constraint in a Gaussian covariance matrix, we obtained a new understanding of this baffling phenomenon: When the null hypothesis is true but “near-singular”, the standardized Wald test exhibits slow weak convergence, owing to the sophisticated dependency structure inherent to the underlying U-statistic that ultimately drives its limiting distribution; this can also be rigorously explained by a key ratio of moments encoded in the Berry-Esseen bound quantifying the normal approximation error involved. As an alternative, we advocate the use of an incomplete U-statistic to mildly tone down the dependence thereof and render the speed of convergence agnostic to the singularity status of the hypothesis. In parallel, we develop a Berry-Esseen bound that is mathematically descriptive of the singularity-agnostic nature of our standardized incomplete U-statistic, using some of the finest exponential-type inequalities in the literature.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

We consider testing a hypothesis defined by a polynomial equality constraint in the covariance matrix
\Theta=(\theta_{uv})_{1\leq u,v\leq p}
of a
p
-variate centered Gaussian random vector
{\bf X}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(0,\Theta)
. That is, for a non-constant polynomial
f:\mathbb{R}^{p+1\choose 2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
in the entries
\theta_{uv}
, we consider a hypothesis of the form

(1.1)

H:f(\Theta)=0;

the domain of
f(\cdot)
is
\mathbb{R}^{p+1\choose 2}
due to the symmetry of
\Theta
. To simplify our exposition, we will throughout assume that

(1.2)

\Theta\text{ is strictly positive definite}.

Testing a hypothesis like
H
dates back to the times of
Spearman 1927
and
Wishart 1928
, who considered constraints of the form

(1.3)

\theta_{uv}\theta_{wz}-\theta_{uz}\theta_{vw}=0,\quad\theta_{uz}\theta_{vw}-\theta_{uw}\theta_{vz}=0,\quad\theta_{uv}\theta_{wz}-\theta_{uw}\theta_{vz}=0\\
\text{ for all distinct indices }u,v,w,z

in a covariance matrix
\Theta
with
p\geq 4
.
These polynomials
in the preceding display are precisely the
2\times 2
minors of
\Theta
that only involve its off-diagonal entries, which are also known as
tetrads
in the factor-analysis literature. It can be easily shown that if
{\bf X}
comes from a one-factor model, which prescribes that the covariance matrix has the structure

(1.4)

\Theta=\Psi+LL^{T}

for a positive definite
p
-by-
p
diagonal
uniqueness
matrix
\Psi
and a
p
-by-
1

factor loading
matrix
L
with entries in
\mathbb{R}
,
the tetrads must all vanish as in (
1.3
)
(
de Loera et al. 1995
, Theorem 2.1)
. In fact, selected tetrads in
\Theta
also vanish if
{\bf X}
comes from a Gaussian latent tree
(
Shiers et al. 2016
,
Leung and Drton 2018
)
, which includes the one-factor model as a special case; hence, the null hypothesis in (
1.1
) with
f
being a tetrad forms a basis to test the fit of such models. For a factor model with
s\geq 2
factors, besides the
(s+1)\times(s+1)
“off-diagonal” minors of
\Theta
, there are other polynomials whose vanishing is also a necessary condition for the model to hold, such as the pentads for
s=2

(
Kelley 1935
)
and septads for
s=3

(
Drton et al. 2007
)
. More generally,
many instances of testing similar hypotheses concerning polynomial constraints in Gaussian covariance matrices also arise in graphical modeling and causal discovery; we refer the readers to
Shafer et al. 1993
,
Bollen and Ting 2000
,
Chen et al. 2014
,
Spirtes et al. 2000
,
Drton et al. 2008
,
Silva et al. 2006
,
Sullivant et al. 2010
,
Chen et al. 2017
for a non-exhaustive list of references, which also serve as the primary motivations for this work. In what follows, let
\nabla f:\mathbb{R}^{p+1\choose 2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p+1\choose 2}
be the gradient function of
f
; at the true covariance matrix
\Theta
, we call the hypothesis
H

(i)

regular
, if
\nabla f(\Theta)\neq 0
, or

(ii)

singular
, if
\nabla f(\Theta)=0
.

If the hypothesis
H
is regular, but the Euclidean norm of its gradient
|\nabla f(\Theta)|
is close to zero, we describe
H
as
near-singular
.
As common in the literature, we also refer to the true covariance parameter
\Theta
as a singular point or singularity (with respect to its ambient space of strictly positive definite matrices) if
\nabla f(\Theta)=0
.

Suppose we observe independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) copies

(1.5)

{\bf X}_{1},\dots,{\bf X}_{n}\sim_{i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}_{p}(0,\Theta)

that have the same distribution as
{\bf X}
. We can compute the
sample covariance matrix
\hat{\Theta}\equiv n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\Theta}_{i}
, where
\hat{\Theta}_{i}\equiv(\hat{\theta}_{uv}({\bf X}_{i}))_{1\leq u,v\leq p}
and for a pair
1\leq u,v\leq p
,

(1.6)

\hat{\theta}_{uv}({\bf x})=x_{u}x_{v}

is a function that multiplies the
u
-th and
v
-th coordinates of a
p
-vector
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})^{T}
.
We will use
V(\Theta)
to denote the
{p\choose 2}
-by-
{p\choose 2}
covariance matrix of the Wishart-distributed outer product
{\bf X}{\bf X}^{T}
treated as a
{p+1\choose 2}
-vector of its upper diagonal entries; in particular, it has the structure

(1.7)

V(\Theta)_{uv,wz}=\theta_{uw}\theta_{vz}+\theta_{uz}\theta_{vw}\text{ for any $1\leq u\leq v\leq p$ and $1\leq w\leq z\leq p$},

and is itself strictly positive definite as a consequence of
\Theta
being so under (
1.2
); see
Eaton 2007
. To test (
1.1
), it is a standard practice to deploy the Wald test, which is based on the Student-type statistic

\hat{T}_{f}=\frac{\sqrt{n}f(\hat{\Theta})}{\sqrt{(\nabla f(\hat{\Theta}))^{T}V(\hat{\Theta})\nabla f(\hat{\Theta})}}

that estimates the standardized statistic

(1.8)

T_{f}=\frac{\sqrt{n}f(\hat{\Theta})}{\sqrt{(\nabla f(\Theta))^{T}V(\Theta)\nabla f(\Theta)}}

normalized by the true limiting variance
(\nabla f(\Theta))^{T}V(\Theta)\nabla f(\Theta)
. In the regular case where
\nabla f(\Theta)\neq 0
, it is well established by the classical theory that
T_{f}
converges weakly to the standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
under
H
, which also implies
\hat{T}_{f}
has the same weak limit by Slutsky’s theorem. However, when
H
is singular,
\hat{T}_{f}
could take on non-standard limiting distributions; for instance, if all the off-diagonal entries of
\Theta
are zero, then
Drton and Xiao 2016
and
Pillai and Meng 2016
’s results imply that for any monomial
f(\Theta)=\theta_{u_{1}v_{1}}^{a_{1}}\dots\theta_{u_{m}v_{m}}^{a_{m}}
, where the
(u_{i},v_{i})
’s are distinct pairs of indices such that
u_{i}\leq v_{i}
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}
are positive exponents, the
Wald statistic

\hat{T}_{f}^{2}
has a null limiting distribution of
\frac{1}{(\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r})^{2}}\chi_{1}^{2}
. More generally, for constraints defined by smooth functions that are not necessarily polynomials,
Gaffke et al. 1999
,
Gaffke 2002
obtained the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{T}_{f}^{2}
at singular points under second-order regularity conditions, and a thorough literature review on more related studies
can be found in
Dufour et al. 2017
. The latter paper is also perhaps the most accomplished in relation to the context of our present work, as the authors have provided a full characterization of the limiting distributions of
\hat{T}_{f}
for a polynomial constraint in the model parameters under different so-called
singularity orders
of the hypothesis, as well as stochastic upper bounds for all possible limiting distributions.

1000
repeated experiments) of two types of statistics with critical values calibrated based on their asymptotic null distribution

), with a
4

having a one-factor structure as in (

). The entries in the loading matrix
L
are all taken to be
0.2
, with the uniqueness matrix
\Psi
picked so that the diagonal entries of
\Theta
are
1
.

T_{f}
and
\hat{T}_{f}
are the standardized and studentized Wald test statistics. With a computational budget of
N=2n
,
\sqrt{n}U_{n,N}^{\prime}/\sigma
and
\sqrt{n}U_{n,N}^{\prime}/\hat{\sigma}
are the incomplete U-statistics defined in (
2.6
), respectively normalized by the true limiting variance
\sigma^{2}
in (
2.8
) and a data-driven estimate
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
of it.

However, most existing works only focus on pinning down the limiting null distributions of
\hat{T}_{f}
at singularities, but have not paid much attention to the important scenarios where
H
is near-singular, i.e.
H
is regular but
|\nabla f(\Theta)|\approx 0
: While the classical theory guarantees that
T_{f}
, and hence
\hat{T}_{f}
, is normally distributed under the null
in the limit
, the near-singularity can render the weak convergence in question
too slow
for
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
to be accurate as a reference null distribution for any reasonable sample size
n
. For instance, Figure
1.1
shows that, under a near-singular one-factor model and a sample size of
100
(which is already moderately large in practice), for testing a tetrad, the sizes of
T_{f}
and
\hat{T}_{f}
calibrated based on their asymptotic null distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
could be very “off” from the target nominal levels. In fact, for this setting, the curve for
T_{f}
doesn’t align well with the
45^{\circ}
line until
n
is ramped up to around
400
, and that for
\hat{T}_{f}
is still far from aligning. Note that the one-factor model behind this figure translates into a near-singular
H
because the off-diagonal entries of
\Theta
are
0.04
, which make the gradient vector
\nabla f(\Theta)
close to zero. Such near-singular situations are arguably even more relevant than exactly singular situations, because unlike the set of singularities
\{\Theta:\nabla f(\Theta)=0\}
which is confined to be a lower-dimensional subset of measure zero within the ambient space, covariance matrices
\Theta
with the property
|\nabla f(\Theta)|\approx 0
are prevalent occurrences. In addition, while stochastic upper bounds on all the possible limiting null distributions of
\hat{T}_{f}^{2}
have been developed in
(
Dufour et al. 2017
, Theorem 4.1)
, in principle they can only be leveraged to offer asymptotically conservative tests in the limit and offer no indictions on how the test sizes behave in finite samples.
In light of all these considerations, it would be preferable to have an alternative test to Wald that is not only
singarity-agnostic
with respect to the
limiting distribution
, but also with respect to the
convergent speed
, in the sense that both aspects are immune to the degree of singularity of
H
, whether it be regular, nearly singular or exactly singular. In passing, we remark that
Mitchell et al. 2019
is the only work we are aware of which explicitly addresses near-singularity issues in testing, but their paper concerns the likelihood ratio test which is not our current subject matter.

This brings us to our recent work
Sturma et al. 2022
that proposed to use an
incomplete U-statistic
with a high-dimensional symmetric kernel to test a model defined by many functional constraints; for instance,
to test a one-factor model as in (
1.4
), each element of the kernel is constructed as an unbiased estimator of a given tetrad in (
1.3
). The proposal was immediately inspired by the works of
Chen and Kato 2019
,
Song et al. 2019
on normal approximation for high-dimensional incomplete U-statistics, which are in turn based on the seminal works of
Chernozhukov et al. 2013
,
Chernozhukov et al. 2017
on sums of high-dimensional independent vectors, but could also trace back to a one-dimensional result in the 80’s by
Janson 1984
. The latter result implies that, when suitably rescaled, an incomplete U-statistic with an appropriate
computational budget
always converges weakly to the standard normal distribution, regardless of the underlying
degeneracy
status of the kernel; see (
2.7
) below. Hence, the main thrust of
Sturma et al. 2022
was to offer a singularity-agnostic test
with respect to the limiting distribution
. However, while
Sturma et al. 2022
expanded upon the techniques of
Chen and Kato 2019
,
Song et al. 2019
to prove a
Berry-Esseen-type
bound for the accuracy of the high-dimensional normal approximation under a “mixed degeneracy” condition, the bound there was not qualitatively refined enough to describe another favorable attribute of the incomplete U-statistic construction – that it has shown every sign that it is also singularity agnostic
with respect to the convergent speed
, by demonstrating stable and tight test size control in simulation studies. As an aside, obtaining sharper high-dimensional Berry-Esseen bounds than those originating in the pioneering works of
Chernozhukov et al. 2013
,
Chernozhukov et al. 2017
is an active area of research; see
Fang and Koike 2021
,
Chernozhuokov et al. 2022
,
Lopes 2022
,
Chernozhukov et al. 2023
for the state-of-the-art.

1.2.
New contributions

In this paper, focusing on a single polynomial constraint hypothesis on a normal covariance matrix as in (
1.1
), we offer new insights into testing in the presence of (near-)singularities. Precisely, we will

(i)

unravel exactly how the limiting normal behavior of
T_{f}
is plagued by its slow convergent speed in the hairy near-singular scenarios, as measured by a Berry-Esseen bound for the underlying U-statistic (Section
2.2
), and

(ii)

develop a refined Berry-Esseen (B-E) bound (Theorem
3.1
) capable of describing the fact that our proposed incomplete U-statistic, when normalized by its theoretical limiting variance, is indeed singularity-agnostic
with respect to the convergent speed
when the computational budget is suitably chosen (Section
3
).

As it will become clear later, these two endeavors complement each other, and the word “convergent speed” above encompasses more than just how the distributional approximation accuracy scales with the sample size
n
. In Figure
1.1
, the two curves in light and dark green align nicely with the
45^{\circ}
line, and they respectively plot the test sizes of
\sqrt{n}U_{n,N}^{\prime}/\sigma
and
\sqrt{n}U_{n,N}^{\prime}/\hat{\sigma}
, which are versions of our proposed incomplete U-statistic normalized by its theoretical limiting variance
\sigma^{2}
and the practical data-driven Studentizer
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
suggested in
Sturma et al. 2022
, respectively. While our main Berry-Esseen bound in Theorem
3.1
is proven for the former, we believe it sheds light on the excellent test-size control of the latter that we have experienced in practice, and is an important first step towards theoretically justifying incomplete U-statistics as a strong singularity-agnostic choice for testing
H
.
Its proof builds upon the “iterative conditioning” arguments pioneered by
Chen and Kato 2019
,
Song et al. 2019
for establishing incomplete U-statistic Berry-Esseen (B-E) bounds, but contains a game-changing innovation that differentiates from these prior works: Unlike the aforementioned works where a
uniform
B-E bound for the
complete U-statistic
figures as a proof ingredient, in order to
elicit
a final B-E bound for our incomplete U-statistic that is truly descriptive of its singularity-agnostic nature, we have instead used a
nonuniform
version to crucially tighten the arguments via “completing the square” (Section
3.1
).
Moreover, to successfully pull off the said trick, the nonuniform bound itself amalgamates some of the finest exponential-type inequalities in quite an unexpected way, including the moment inequalities of
Giné et al. 2000
,
Adamczak 2006
for canonical U-statistics, the recent Bernstein inequalities for sums of independent sub-Weibull random variables
(
Kuchibhotla and Chakrabortty 2022
,
Zhang and Wei 2022
)
, as well as the exponential randomized concentration inequalities in the Stein’s method literature
(
Shao 2010
,
Leung and Shao 2023a
,
Leung and Shao 2023b
)
.

2.
Why incomplete U-statistics for
H
?

We shall first recap the construction of our proposed incomplete U-statistic in
Sturma et al. 2022
for testing
H
.

2.1.
Incomplete U-statistic for
H

Similarly to
Sturma et al. 2022
, a real polynomial
f
in
\Theta
of degree
m
is first written as

(2.1)

f(\Theta)=a_{0}+\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{1\leq u_{i}\leq v_{i}\leq p\text{ for }i\in[r]}a_{u_{1}v_{1},\dots,u_{r}v_{r}}\theta_{u_{1}v_{1}}\dots\theta_{u_{r}v_{r}},

where
a_{0}
and
a_{u_{1}v_{1},\dots,u_{r}v_{r}}
’s are the real coefficients; this notation for a multivariate polynomial is somewhat less efficient than the usual multi-index notation because a pair
u_{i}\leq v_{i}
may appear repeatedly in a given combination for
u_{1}v_{1},\dots,u_{r}v_{r}
, but is adopted for the ease of defining our kernel functions below.
Recall
\hat{\theta}_{uv}
in (
1.6
), and let
\breve{h}_{f}:(\mathbb{R}^{p})^{m}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

be the function in
m
vector arguments
{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
defined by

(2.2)

\breve{h}_{f}({\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{m})=a_{0}+\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{1\leq u_{i}\leq v_{i}\leq p\text{ for }i\in[r]}a_{u_{1}v_{1},\dots,u_{r}v_{r}}\hat{\theta}_{u_{1}v_{1}}({\bf x}_{1})\dots\hat{\theta}_{u_{r}v_{r}}({\bf x}_{r}),

which forms an
unbiased
estimator for
f(\Theta)
upon inserting the i.i.d. data from (
1.5
) as
\breve{h}_{f}({\bf X}_{1},\dots,{\bf X}_{m})
.
If
\mathcal{S}_{m}
is the set of all permutations of
(1,\dots,m)
, we can then symmetrize
\breve{h}_{f}
with respect to its arguments by defining the function

(2.3)

h({\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{m})=h_{f}({\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{m})=\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\breve{h}_{f}({\bf x}_{\pi_{1}},\dots,{\bf x}_{\pi_{m}}),

to serve as a
kernel
for the U-statistic

(2.4)

U_{n}={n\choose m}^{-1}\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})\in I_{n,m}}h({\bf X}_{i_{1}},\dots,{\bf X}_{i_{m}}),

which is also unbiased for
f(\Theta)
; for a classical reference on U-statistic theory, we refer the reader to
Korolyuk and Borovskich 2013
.
An important function related to
h(\cdot)
is the
projection kernel

g:\mathbb{R}^{p}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

(2.5)

g({\bf x})\equiv\mathbb{E}[h({\bf x},{\bf X}_{2},\dots,{\bf X}_{m})]=a_{0}+\\
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\;\ \sum_{j=1}^{r}\;\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq u_{i}\leq v_{i}\leq p\\
\text{ for }i\in[r]\end{subarray}}a_{u_{1}v_{1},\dots,u_{r}v_{r}}\theta_{u_{1}v_{1}}\dots\theta_{u_{j-1}v_{j-1}}\hat{\theta}_{u_{j}v_{j}}({\bf x})\theta_{u_{j+1}v_{j+1}}\dots\theta_{u_{r}v_{r}}

For an integer
N
such that
1\leq N\leq{n\choose m}
, let
\{Z_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})}\}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})\in I_{n,m}}
be
{n\choose m}
many independent and identically distributed Bernoulli random variables with success probability

p_{n,N}\equiv N{n\choose m}^{-1}

such that

P(Z_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})}=1)=1-P(Z_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})}=0)=p_{n,N};

moreover, these
Z_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})}
’s are also independent of the data
{\bf X}_{1},\dots,{\bf X}_{n}
from (
1.5
).
In
Sturma et al. 2022
, we have proposed using the

incomplete
U-statistic under
Bernoulli sampling

(2.6)

U^{\prime}_{n,N}=\frac{1}{\hat{N}}\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})\in I_{n,m}}Z_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})}h({\bf X}_{i_{1}},\dots,{\bf X}_{i_{m}}),

to test (
1.1
),
where

\hat{N}\equiv\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})\in I_{n,m}}Z_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})}
counts the actual number of
h({\bf X}_{i_{1}},\dots,{\bf X}_{i_{m}})
being summed over; we call
N
the
computational budget
that induces
\hat{N}
.
A motivation of our proposal could trace back to a weak convergence result in
Janson 1984
, which implies that, if
N,n
tend to
\infty
in such a way that
\alpha_{n}\equiv n/N
converges to a positive constant, then for

,

(2.7)

\frac{\sqrt{n}U_{n,N}^{\prime}}{\sigma}\longrightarrow_{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1)\text{ under }H,

where
\sigma_{g}^{2}\equiv\text{var}_{H}[g({\bf X})]=\mathbb{E}_{H}[g^{2}]
,
\sigma_{h}^{2}\equiv\text{var}_{H}[h({\bf X}_{1},\dots,{\bf X}_{m})]=\mathbb{E}_{H}[h^{2}]
and

(2.8)

\sigma^{2}\equiv m^{2}\sigma_{g}^{2}+\alpha_{n}\sigma_{h}^{2}.

With careful calculations, one can check (Lemma
B.1
(i)
) that

(2.9)

\sigma_{g}^{2}=\frac{(\nabla f(\Theta))^{T}V(\Theta)\nabla f(\Theta)}{m^{2}},

and hence

(2.10)

H
is singular if and only if
\sigma_{g}^{2}=0

given that

is strictly positive definite; on the contrary, unlike
\sigma_{g}
, the standardizing factor

for

is always strictly positive, whether

is singular or not, because it must be that
\sigma_{h}^{2}>0
under our assumptions (Lemma

(ii)
). That the weak limit in (

) is always

suggests that a test of
H
based on

is singularity-agnostic
with respect to the limiting distribution
. In practice, a data-driven variance estimate
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
formed with a “divide-and-conquer” strategy can be used in place of

to normalize
U_{n,N}^{\prime}
; we refer to
Sturma et al. 2022
for the details.

However, the asymptotic result in (
2.7
) says nothing about its convergent speed, most naturally gauged by Berry-Esseen-type bounds.
To fully appreciate our main result for
U_{n,N}^{\prime}
(Theorem
3.1
) in that respect, we shall first explore why the Wald statistic with normal quantiles as critical values cannot be expected to perform well when
\nabla f(\Theta)\neq 0
but “near singularities” hit, even the classical theory seemingly suggests otherwise by guaranteeing its limiting normality.

2.2.
The predicament of the Wald test

To illustrate our point, we consider the statistic
T_{f}
in (
1.8
)
normalized by the theoretical limiting variance
(\nabla f(\Theta))^{T}V(\Theta)\nabla f(\Theta)
, whose convergence to normality under a regular
H
is guaranteed by the classical theory
(
Lehmann and Romano 2022
, Chapter 14.4)
. With simple calculations, one can show that

(2.11)

T_{f}=\frac{\sqrt{n}U_{n}}{m\sigma_{g}}+o_{p}(1),

i.e.
T_{f}
is essentially the U-statistic in (
2.4
) up to a small term converging to zero in probability; see Appendix
B.2
. Hence, in light of (
2.10
), the normal weak limit of
T_{f}
under a regular
H
is essentially driven by the central limit theorem for complete U-statistics
(
Koroljuk and Borovskich 1994
, Theorem 4.2.1)
, which states that

(2.12)

\frac{\sqrt{n}U_{n}}{m\sigma_{g}}\longrightarrow_{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1)\text{ when }\sigma_{g}\neq 0.

The normal approximation accuracy of (
2.12
) is typically gauged by the Berry-Esseen bound for U-statistics
(
Chen et al. 2011
, Theorem 10.3)
as in

(2.13)

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\left|P_{H}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}U_{n}}{m\sigma_{g}}\leq z\right)-\Phi(z)\right|\leq\frac{(1+\sqrt{2})(m-1)\sigma_{h}}{\sqrt{m(n-m+1)}\sigma_{g}}+\frac{6.1\mathbb{E}_{H}[|g|^{3}]}{n^{1/2}\sigma_{g}^{3}}.

Our
key observation
is that the ratio of moments

(2.14)

\frac{\sigma_{h}}{\sigma_{g}}

figuring in the bound (
2.13
) could be extremely large for the kernel
h
in (
2.3
) when
H
approaches being singular, so much so that the factor
\sqrt{m(n-m+1)}
in the denominator could fail to counter-balance it even with a large
n
. When this happens, the vacuous B-E bound in (
2.13
) is actually informing that the distribution of
\frac{\sqrt{n}U_{n}}{m\sigma_{g}}
, and hence that of
T_{f}
via the first-order approximation in (
2.11
), could be very poorly approximated by
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
In what follows, we shall use Isserlis’ theorem
(
Isserlis 1918
,
Wick 1950
)
on mixed normal central moments to explain this phenomenon.

Theorem 2.1

(Isserlis’ theorem)
.

For
r\in\mathbb{N}
, let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{2r}
be jointly normal variables that are all centered. Then

(2.15)

\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}\cdots Y_{2r}]=\sum_{\mathcal{J}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}\mathbb{E}[Y_{u_{\ell}}Y_{v_{\ell}}],

where the summation is over all partitions
\mathcal{J}=\big\{\{u_{1},v_{1}\},\dots,\{u_{r},v_{r}\}\big\}
of
[2r]=\{1,\dots,2r\}
into disjoint pairs
\{u_{\ell},v_{\ell}\}\in[2r]^{2}
; in particular, there are
\ (2r)!/(2^{r}r!)
distinct pairings of
[2r]
.

This result can be found in
Janson 1997
, and we will call a summand in (
2.15
) an
Isserlis expansion term
. (Also note that the form in (
1.7
) can be derived from Theorem
2.1
.) It is the easiest to illustrate our claimed phenomenon about the ratio in (
2.14
) with a prototypical example: the tetrad. In what follows, suppose we are to test the tetrad equality constraint

(2.16)

f(\Theta)=\theta_{uz}\theta_{vw}-\theta_{uw}\theta_{vz}=0\text{ for }u\neq v\neq w\neq z,

which has
a corresponding degree-two kernel

(2.17)

h({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg[\hat{\theta}_{uz}({\bf x}_{1})\hat{\theta}_{vw}({\bf x}_{2})-\hat{\theta}_{uw}({\bf x}_{1})\hat{\theta}_{vz}({\bf x}_{2})+\hat{\theta}_{vw}({\bf x}_{1})\hat{\theta}_{uz}({\bf x}_{2})-\hat{\theta}_{vz}({\bf x}_{1})\hat{\theta}_{uw}({\bf x}_{2})\Bigg]

from (
2.3
).
Moreover, for simplicity, we further assume
the covariance matrix
\Theta
has a specific one-factor structure such that

(2.18)

\theta_{uv}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }u=v;\\
\rho&\text{if }u\neq v,\end{cases}

for a constant
\rho\in(0,1)
, which makes the gradient of (
2.16
) take the form

(2.19)

\nabla f(\Theta)=(\theta_{vw},\theta_{uz},-\theta_{vz},-\theta_{uw})^{T}=\rho(1,1,-1,-1)^{T};

compare (
1.4
). The following observations can be made for this example:

2.2.1.
First observation for the tetrad example (
2.16
):
\sigma_{g}^{2}\rightarrow 0
as
|\nabla f(\Theta)|\rightarrow 0

This is just a simple consequence of the expression for
\sigma_{g}^{2}
in (
2.9
), the form of the tetrad gradient (
2.19
) and the structure of
V(\Theta)
in (
1.7
), as
|\nabla f(\Theta)|\rightarrow 0
is equivalent to
\rho\rightarrow 0
.

2.2.2.
Second observation for the tetrad example (
2.16
):
\sigma_{h}^{2}\rightarrow 1
as
|\nabla f(\Theta)|\rightarrow 0

Upon expanding
h^{2}({\bf X}_{1},{\bf X}_{2})
, the variance quantity
\sigma_{h}^{2}
can be seen as

(2.20)

\sigma_{h}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{H}[h({\bf X}_{1},{\bf X}_{2})^{2}]=\frac{1}{4}\times\Bigg\{\mathbb{E}\big[\hat{\theta}_{uz}({\bf X}_{1})^{2}\hat{\theta}_{vw}({\bf X}_{2})^{2}\big]+\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_{uw}({\bf X}_{1})^{2}\hat{\theta}_{vz}({\bf X}_{2})^{2}]+\\
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_{vw}({\bf X}_{1})^{2}\hat{\theta}_{uz}({\bf X}_{2})^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_{vz}({\bf X}_{1})^{2}\hat{\theta}_{uw}({\bf X}_{2})^{2}]+\underbrace{\cdots\cdots\cdots}_{\text{ $2\cdot{4\choose 2}$ expected cross terms}}\Bigg\},

where each expectation explicitly shown in the braces of (
2.20
) is the square of one of the four terms

(2.21)

\hat{\theta}_{uz}({\bf X}_{1})\hat{\theta}_{vw}({\bf X}_{2}),\quad\hat{\theta}_{uw}({\bf X}_{1})\hat{\theta}_{vz}({\bf X}_{2}),\quad\hat{\theta}_{vw}({\bf X}_{1})\hat{\theta}_{uz}({\bf X}_{2})\text{ and }\hat{\theta}_{vz}({\bf X}_{1})\hat{\theta}_{uw}({\bf X}_{2})

that come from plugging the data
({\bf X}_{1},{\bf X}_{2})
into the four additive terms in the bracket of (
2.17
), and the terms hidden in “
\cdots\cdots\cdots
” are the expectations of the cross terms from multiplying any two terms in (
2.21
). In particular, with Theorem
2.1
, it is straight-forward to compute that

(2.22)

\text{ each expectation explicitly shown in \eqref{sigmah_sq_expand} must be evaluated as}\\
1+\underbrace{\Big\{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{ $\frac{8!}{2^{4}4!}-1$ finitely many additive terms, each equal to either $0$, $\rho^{2}$ or $\rho^{4}$}\end{subarray}\Big\}}_{\text{ summing to $4(\rho^{4}+\rho^{2})$}}.

For instance, the first expectation
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_{uz}({\bf X}_{1})^{2}\hat{\theta}_{vw}({\bf X}_{2})^{2}]
in (
2.20
)
can be computed with Isserlis’ theorem as

(2.23)

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_{uz}({\bf X}_{1})^{2}\hat{\theta}_{vw}({\bf X}_{2})^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{1u}^{2}X_{1z}^{2}X_{2v}^{2}X_{2w}^{2}]=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[X_{1u}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[X_{1z}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[X_{2v}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[X_{2w}^{2}]}_{=1\cdot 1\cdot 1\cdot 1=1}+\cdots

under (
2.18
), where
{\bf X}_{i}=(X_{i1},\dots,X_{ip})^{T}
for each
i\in[n]
;
we have only displayed the Isserlis expansion term that takes the value
1
and corresponds to the pairwise partition where each of the sample indices (i.e.
1
or
2
)
and
variable indices (i.e.
u
,
v
,
w
or
z
)
exactly pairs with itself
. The rest of the Isserlis expansion terms not displayed, for which such self-pairings are not simultaneously satisfied, must either be
0
,
\rho^{2}
or
\rho^{4}
under the structure of
\Theta
assumed in (
2.18
), as described in (
2.22
); examples of such terms are

\mathbb{E}[X_{1u}X_{1z}]\mathbb{E}[X_{1u}X_{1z}]\mathbb{E}[X_{2v}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[X_{2w}^{2}]=\rho\cdot\rho\cdot 1\cdot 1=\rho^{2},\\
\mathbb{E}[X_{1u}X_{1z}]\mathbb{E}[X_{1u}X_{1z}]\mathbb{E}[X_{2v}X_{2w}]\mathbb{E}[X_{2v}X_{2w}]=\rho\cdot\rho\cdot\rho\cdot\rho=\rho^{4}\text{ and }\\
\mathbb{E}[X_{1u}X_{2v}]\mathbb{E}[X_{1u}X_{2v}]\mathbb{E}[X_{1z}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[X_{2w}^{2}]=0\cdot 0\cdot 1\cdot 1=0,

which result from different configurations of the index pairings and can be shown as summing to
4(\rho^{4}+\rho^{2})
by simple counting. At the same time, one can also show that

(2.24)

\text{ each of the $2\cdot{4\choose 2}$ cross terms not explicitly displayed in \eqref{sigmah_sq_expand} must be}\\
\text{ a sum of
$\frac{8!}{2^{4}4!}$ many terms, each with magnitude no larger than $\rho$};

this is because simultaneous self-pairings of all sample and variable indices as in the first expansion term (with value
1
) in (
2.23
) cannot occur.
For instance, taking the expectation of the cross term from multiplying the first two terms in (
2.21
) and applying Theorem
2.1
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_{uz}({\bf X}_{1})\hat{\theta}_{vw}({\bf X}_{2})\hat{\theta}_{uw}({\bf X}_{1})\hat{\theta}_{vz}({\bf X}_{2})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{1u}^{2}(X_{1w}X_{1z})X_{2v}^{2}(X_{2w}X_{2z})]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[X_{1u}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[X_{1w}X_{1z}]\mathbb{E}[X_{2v}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[X_{2w}X_{2z}]}_{=1\cdot\rho\cdot 1\cdot\rho=\rho^{2}}+\cdots

for which all the Isserlis expansion terms cannot form exact self-pairings of all the indices to give the value
1
. In light of both facts in (
2.22
) and (
2.24
), one can then conclude from (
2.20
) that
\sigma_{h}^{2}\rightarrow 1
as
\rho\rightarrow 0
(which is equivalent to
|\nabla f(\Theta)|\rightarrow 0
).

The observations in Sections
2.2.1
and
2.2.2
have essentially shown the following: for the tetrad, the ratio
\sigma_{h}/\sigma_{g}
in (
2.14
) can be arbitrarily large as the hypothesis
H
approaches being singular. However, there is nothing special about this example which we have picked only for its simplicity. The above phenomenon can also be observed for other complicated polynomials, and can be similarly explained when applying Isserlis’ theorem to evaluate
\sigma_{h}^{2}
and
\sigma_{g}^{2}
; in short, “simultaneous self-pairing” of indices prevents
\sigma_{h}
from tending to zero even when the gradient
\nabla f(\Theta)
(and hence
\sigma_{g}
) approaches zero.

2.3.
Incomplete U-statistic should strike a fine balance

While Section
2.2
demonstrated how the normal distributional approximation accuracy of
T_{f}
can be plagued by the largeness of the ratio in (
2.14
) under near singularity, we now explore why
U_{n,N}^{\prime}
is a good candidate to circumvent this issue.

Interestingly, we shall first consider the following average of independent terms:

(2.25)

S_{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}=\frac{1}{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}h({\bf X}_{(i-1)m+1},\dots,{\bf X}_{im}),

where
h
is the same kernel in (
2.3
), and the summands are constructed with non-overlapping batches of samples and are hence independent. By the classical central limit theorem, it must be the case that, under
H
,

(2.26)

\frac{\sqrt{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}S_{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}}{\sigma_{h}}\longrightarrow_{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1)\text{ whether }\sigma_{g}\text{ equals }0\text{ or not};

recall
\sigma_{h}^{2}>0
. Moreover, the classical B-E bound
(
Berry 1941
,
Esseen 1942
)
says that for an absolute constant
C>0
,

(2.27)

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\left|P_{H}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}S_{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}}{\sigma_{h}}\leq z\right)-\Phi(z)\right|\leq\frac{C\mathbb{E}_{H}[|h|^{3}]}{\sqrt{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}\sigma_{h}^{3}}.

At this point, it is appropriate to introduce the following result about the
hypercontractivity
of a polynomial in jointly normal random variables, which refers to the fact that the lower norms of such a variable can control its higher norms up to an absolute constant.

Lemma 2.2

(Hypercontractivity of polynomials in jointly normal random variables)
.

Let
{\bf G}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{r})^{T}\sim\mathcal{N}_{r}(0,\Sigma)
be an
r
-variate centered normal random vector, and
Q({\bf G})=Q(g_{1},\dots,g_{r})
be a polynomial of degree
k
in the variables
g_{1},\dots,g_{r}
. Then for
1<q_{1}\leq q_{2}<\infty
and an absolute constant
C(k)>0
depending only on
k
,

\|Q({{\bf G}})\|_{q_{2}}\leq C(k)\bigg(\frac{q_{2}-1}{q_{1}-1}\bigg)^{k/2}\|Q({{\bf G}})\|_{q_{1}}.

Remark
: Lemma
2.2
is a direct consequence of the Khinchine-Kahane inequality for Rademacher chaos
(
De la Pena and Giné 1999
, Theorem 3.2.5, p.115)
and will be used repeatedly in the sequel; we also note that the original version stated in
De la Pena and Giné 1999
assumes
\Sigma
to be the identity matrix, but our version here is an immediate consequence because one can write
{\bf G}=\Sigma^{1/2}{\bf Z}
for a standard normal vector
{\bf Z}
, and it is easy to see that
Q({\bf G})=Q(\Sigma^{1/2}{\bf Z})
is also a degree-
k
polynomial in
{\bf Z}
.

We can now see why
S_{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}
is well approximated by a normal distribution even if
\sigma_{g}
is very close to zero: Since
h({\bf X}_{1},\dots,{\bf X}_{m})
is a polynomial of degree
2m
in jointly Gaussian random variables by the construction in (
2.2
)-(
2.3
), its hypercontractivity from Lemma
2.2
implies that, for an absolute constant
C(m)>0
,

(2.28)

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{H}[|h|^{3}]}{\sigma_{h}^{3}}\leq C(m).

Thus, we can further write the B-E bound in (
2.27
) as

(2.29)

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\left|P_{H}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}S_{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}}{\sigma_{h}}\leq z\right)-\Phi(z)\right|\leq\frac{C(m)}{\sqrt{n}};

unlike the
U_{n}
in (
2.4
), which ultimately drives the asymptotic distribution of
T_{f}
and whose normal approximation accuracy in (
2.13
) could be plagued by the large ratio
\sigma_{h}/\sigma_{g}
when
H
is nearly singular, the normal approximation of
S_{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}
is robust no matter what
\sigma_{g}
is. As a side note, since the projection kernel
g({{\bf X}})
is always a degree-2 polynomial in Gaussian random variables as per its structure in (
2.5
), Lemma
2.2
also suggests that the bound

(2.30)

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{H}[|g|^{3}]}{\sigma_{g}^{3}}\leq C

for an absolute constant
C>0
; unlike the ratio
\frac{\sigma_{h}}{\sigma_{g}}
,
\frac{E_{H}[|g|^{3}]}{\sigma_{g}^{3}}
is not the source of poor normal approximation for
U_{n}
despite appearing in the U-statistic B-E bound
(
2.13
). The moral here is that
S_{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}
benefits from being a sum of the
independent
hypercontractive terms
\{h({\bf X}_{(i-1)m+1},\dots,{\bf X}_{im})\}_{i=1,\dots,\lfloor n/m\rfloor}
and
has good normal approximation property as suggested in (
2.29
). On the contrary, the complete U-statistic
U_{n}
in (
2.4
) is a sum of similarly hypercontractive terms
\{h({\bf X}_{i_{1}},\dots,{\bf X}_{i_{m}})\}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})\in I_{n,m}}
, but the intricate dependence among them cripples the normal approximation accuracy by injecting the ratio
\frac{\sigma_{h}}{\sigma_{g}}
in the B-E bound (
2.13
); in fact, an examination of the proof behind (
2.13
)
(
Chen et al. 2011
, Section 10.2.1)
precisely suggests that
\frac{\sigma_{h}}{\sigma_{g}}
stems from such dependence.

However, despite being (pleasantly)
singularity-agnostic with respect to the convergent speed,

S_{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}
pays the price of essentially dividing down the sample size by
m
, while
U_{n}
, which drives the distribution of
T_{f}
, is aggressive in terms of power by summing over all the
{n\choose m}
sample indices in
I_{n,m}
; see
Serfling 1980
for an optimality result about complete U-statistics regarding the latter aspect. Intuitively, one should expect, by summing over only a random sample of indices in
I_{n,m}
as in (
2.6
) with an appropriately chosen computational budget
N
,
U_{n,N}^{\prime}
shall
strike a balanced middle ground
between
S_{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}
and
U_{n}
, i.e., summing over more terms than
S_{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}
on average to be more power efficient, but carrying less dependence between the summands than
U_{n}
to maintain the normal approximation accuracy under near-singular situations.

3.
A singularity-agnostic Berry-Esseen bound for
U_{n,N}^{\prime}

We now state our main result: a Berry-Esseen bound for the normal convergence in (
2.7
) that truly mathematizes our intuition at the end of Section
2.3
about
U_{n,N}^{\prime}
. We call the bound “singularity-agnostic” because it captures the fact that the convergent speed of (
2.7
) is not affected by the underlying singularity status of
H
, when the computational budget
N
is appropriately chosen; recall that singularity can also be characterized by the zeroness of
\sigma_{g}
as per (
2.10
).

Theorem 3.1

(“Singularity-agnostic” Berry-Esseen bound for
U^{\prime}_{n,N}
)
.

Let
U_{n,N}^{\prime}
be constructed as in (
2.6
) with the kernel
h
in (
2.3
) and data from (
1.5
); assume (
1.2
),
\log(n)\leq N
,
2\leq m
and
2m<n
hold. Then one has the generic bound

(3.1)

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\Bigg|P_{H}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{n}U_{n,N}^{\prime}}{\sigma}\leq z\Big)-\Phi(z)\Bigg|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{N}}\Bigg\{\frac{\sqrt{1-3p_{n,N}+3p_{n,N}^{2}}}{1-p_{n,N}}+\log n\Bigg\}+\frac{C(m)}{\sqrt{n}}+\mathfrak{R},

for an error term
\mathfrak{R}\geq 0
with the property that:

(i)

(Regular case) If
\sigma_{g}\neq 0
, then

\mathfrak{R}\leq C(m)\Bigg\{\sum_{r=2}^{m}\frac{\sqrt{N}(\log(2n^{r}+1))^{3r}}{\sqrt{(1-p_{n,N})n^{r}}}\Bigg\}+C\Bigg(\frac{p_{n,N}}{1-p_{n,N}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigg).

(ii)

(Singular case) If
\sigma_{g}=0
, then

\mathfrak{R}\leq\frac{C(m)\sqrt{N}(\log(2n^{m}+1))^{3m}}{n\sqrt{1-p_{n,N}}}+C\frac{p_{n,N}}{1-p_{n,N}}.

In particular, with the additional assumptions that
n\leq N\leq mn
and
2m+1<n
, we have the overall bound

(3.2)

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\Bigg|P_{H}\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{n}U_{n,N}^{\prime}}{\sigma}\leq z\Bigg)-\Phi(z)\Bigg|\leq\frac{C(m)\log(2n^{m}+1))^{3m}}{\sqrt{n}},

regardless of the value of
\sigma_{g}
.

The bound
(
3.2
) is arguably our most important statement,
as the
C(m)
in (
3.2
) is a constant that
only
depends on
m
. Just like the B-E bound for
S_{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}
in (
2.29
), it informs that the convergent speed of
U_{n,N}^{\prime}
to normality won’t be severely slowed down by the singularity status of
H
, as opposed to its complete counterpart
U_{n}
whose slow convergence is suggested by the largeness of the ratio in (
2.14
) under near-singular situations. At the same time, when
n\leq N\leq mn
,
U_{n,N}^{\prime}
is summing over more terms on average than
n/m
, achieving more efficient use of the data than
S_{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}
.
In particular,
N=2n
is a “balanced” choice for the computational budget advocated in
Sturma et al. 2022
, which is also adopted for the small simulation study behind Figure
1.1
. We also comment on three other noteworthy aspects:

(a)

As
N
approaches
{n\choose m}
, the “success probability”
p_{n,N}
of the Bernoulli samplers tends to
1
; since
1-p_{n,N}
figures in the denominators, the right hand side of the generic bound in (
3.1
) will blow up. This is natural: the incomplete
U_{n,N}^{\prime}
should behave very much like
U_{n}
when
N
is close to
{n\choose m}
, and that the bound becoming vacuous simply indicates that the normal approximation accuracy could be very low, much like the large ratio in (
2.14
) advises that the convergence in (
2.12
) could be slow under near-singular situations.

(b)

Theorem
3.1
(i)
is most comparable to a B-E bound for one-dimensional incomplete U-statistic proven in
Peng et al. 2022
. Precisely, under the same assumptions and
\sigma_{g}\neq 0
,
Peng et al. 2022
suggests that

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\left|P_{H}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}U_{n,N}^{\prime}}{\sigma}\leq z\right)-\Phi(z)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigg\{\frac{\mathbb{E}_{H}[|h|^{3}]}{\sqrt{N}\sigma_{h}^{3}}+\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{1/3}+\frac{\mathbb{E}_{H}[|g|^{3}]}{\sqrt{n}\sigma_{g}^{3}}+\left[\frac{m}{n}\left(\frac{\sigma_{h}^{2}}{\sigma_{g}^{2}m}-1\right)\right]^{1/2}\Bigg\}

(3.3)

\displaystyle\leq C(m)\Bigg\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{1}{n^{1/3}}+\left[\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{(\sigma_{h}/\sigma_{g})^{2}}{m}-1\right)\right]^{1/2}\Bigg\},

where the last inequality is an immediate consequence of the hypercontractivity in (
2.28
) and (
2.30
). However, due to the existence of the ratio
\sigma_{h}/\sigma_{g}
which can be extremely large as described in Section
2.2
for a near-singular
H
, (
3.3
) is not refined enough to capture the singularity-agnostic nature of
U_{n,N}^{\prime}
as in (
3.2
). Moreover, compared to the rate
n^{-1/3}
in (
3.3
), our bound in (
3.2
) has the rate
n^{-1/2}
up to some logarithm factors, which is near-optimal.

(c)

Our paper really concerns a non-trivial kernel
h
with degree
m\geq 2
, and Theorem
3.1
is also proven for this case. If
m=1
, then
\sigma_{h}=\sigma_{g}
; from a singularity perspective, one can simply use
S_{\lfloor n/1\rfloor}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h({\bf X}_{i})
, which has the singularity-agnostic B-E bound (
2.29
).

The proof of Theorem
3.1
involves analyzing the tail behavior of the
Hoeffding projections
of
U_{n}
, which is one of our main technical innovations. In what follows, if
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}
are
m
distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\Psi:(\mathbb{R}^{p})^{m}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a function with
m
arguments in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we use the notation
P_{1}\times\dots\times P_{m}\Psi=\int\Psi({\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{m})d(P_{1}\times\dots\times P_{m})
. Let
\delta_{\bf x}
denote the Dirac measure at
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. For each
r\in[m]
and a
symmetric
kernel
\Psi:(\mathbb{R}^{p})^{m}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
, let
\pi_{r}(\Psi):(\mathbb{R}^{p})^{r}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the Hoeffding projection kernel

(3.4)

\pi_{r}(\Psi)({\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{r})=(\delta_{{\bf x}_{1}}-P_{H})\times\cdots\times(\delta_{{\bf x}_{r}}-P_{H})\times P_{H}^{m-r}\Psi,

which is also symmetric in
{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{r}
and is said to be
canonical
(or
completely degenerate
), in the sense that, for any
i=1,\dots,r

(3.5)

\int\pi_{r}(\Psi)({\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{r})dP_{H}({\bf x}_{i})=0\text{ almost surely}.

In particular, for our interested kernel
h
defined in (
2.3
),
\pi_{1}(h)=g
; moreover,
if

d\equiv\max\Bigg\{1\leq r\leq m:\int h({\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{m})dP_{H}^{m-r+1}=0\text{ almost surely}\Bigg\},

i.e.
h
is
P_{H}
-degenerate of order
d-1
,
then
we have
the
Hoeffding decomposition

(3.6)

U_{n}=\sum_{r=d}^{m}{m\choose r}U_{n}^{(r)},

where

(3.7)

U_{n}^{(r)}\equiv{n\choose r}^{-1}\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})\in I_{n,r}}\pi_{r}(h)({\bf X}_{i_{1}},\dots,{\bf X}_{i_{r}})

is each a canonical U-statistic of degree
r
known as a
Hoeffding projection
;
we refer to
De la Pena and Giné 1999
for these standard results. The following inequalities, which stem from a Bernstein inequality proven for the Hoeffding projections in Section
4
, are crucial for establishing Theorem
3.1
.

Lemma 3.2

(“Consequences” of Bernstein inequalities for the
U_{n}^{(r)}
’s)
.

Let
U_{n}^{(r)}
be constructed as in (
3.7
) with the kernel
h
in (
2.3
) and data from (
1.5
). Assume (
1.2
) and
2m<n
. There exist absolute constants
C_{1}(m),C_{2}(m),C_{3}(m),c_{1}(m),c_{2}(m)>0
such that, for all
r=d,\dots,m
,

(i)

P_{H}\Big(\Big|\sqrt{n}U_{n}^{(r)}\Big|>C_{1}(m)\sigma_{h}n^{(1-r)/2}(\log(2n^{r}+1))^{3r}\Big)\leq\frac{C_{2}(m)}{n}
and

(ii)

P_{H}\Big(|\sqrt{n}U_{n}^{(r)}|>z\Big)\leq C_{3}(m)\exp\Big(-\frac{c_{1}(m)z^{2}n^{r-1}}{\sigma_{h}^{2}}\Big)
for
0\leq z\leq c_{2}(m)\sigma_{h}n^{\frac{1-r}{2}}

Lemma
3.2
(ii)
is particularly remarkable, as it captures the sub-Gaussian behavior of the tail of
U_{n}^{(r)}
for smaller values of
z
, just like the classical Bernstein’s inequality for a sum of independent sub-exponential variables
(
Giné and Nickl 2016
, Proposition 3.1.8)
.
Like existing works on B-E bounds for incomplete U-statistics
(
Chen and Kato 2019
,
Song et al. 2019
,
Peng et al. 2022
,
Sturma et al. 2022
)
, we will rewrite
U_{n,N}^{\prime}
as

(3.8)

U_{n,N}^{\prime}=\frac{N}{\hat{N}}W_{n},

where

(3.9)

W_{n}\equiv U_{n}+\sqrt{1-p_{n,N}}B_{n},

for

(3.10)

B_{n}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})\in I_{n,m}}\frac{Z_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})}-p_{n,N}}{\sqrt{1-p_{n,N}}}h({\bf X}_{i_{1}},\dots,{\bf X}_{i_{m}});

essentially, up to the factor
\sqrt{1-p_{n,N}}
,
W_{n}
is a sum of the complete U-statistic
U_{n}
and
B_{n}
, and it is a simple matter to check that (
3.8
)-(
3.10
) hold. With these preparations, we are now primed to prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

It is a direct consequence of (
3.8
) that

(3.11)

\frac{\sqrt{n}U_{n,N}^{\prime}}{\sigma}=\Bigg(\frac{N}{\hat{N}}-1\Bigg)\frac{\sqrt{n}W_{n}}{\sigma}+\frac{\sqrt{n}W_{n}}{\sigma},

which allows us to simplify the problem slightly:
By Bernstein’s inequality for bounded random variables
(
Vaart and Wellner 1996
, Lemma 2.2.9, p.102)
,

\displaystyle P\Bigg(\Bigg|\frac{\hat{N}}{N}-1\Bigg|>\frac{\sqrt{2\log n}}{\sqrt{N}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\Bigg(-\frac{\log(n)}{1-p_{n,N}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}\max(p_{n,V},1-p_{n,V})\sqrt{\frac{\log(n)}{N}}}\Bigg)

(3.12)

\displaystyle\leq 2n^{-1/2},

where the last inequality uses the assumption
\log(n)\leq N
.
On the other hand, since
\bar{\Phi}(z)\leq\min\big(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{z\sqrt{2\pi}}\big)e^{-z^{2}/2}
for any
z>0

(
Chen et al. 2011
, p.16)
,

(3.13)

P_{H}\Bigg(\Bigg|\frac{\sqrt{n}W_{n}}{\sigma}\Bigg|>\sqrt{2\log n}\Bigg)\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\Bigg|P_{H}\Bigg(\Bigg|\frac{\sqrt{n}W_{n}}{\sigma}\Bigg|>z\Bigg)-\bar{\Phi}(z)\Bigg|+\frac{1}{2n}.

Since

|\Phi(z)-\Phi(z+t)|\leq\phi(0)|t|\leq\frac{|t|}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
for any
z,t\in\mathbb{R}
, combining the two tail inequalities (
3.12
) and (
3.13
), from (
3.11
),
we further have

(3.14)

\Bigg|P_{H}\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{n}U_{n,N}^{\prime}}{\sigma}\leq z\Bigg)-\Phi(z)\Bigg|\leq C\Bigg\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{N}}+\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\Bigg|P_{H}\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{n}W_{n}}{\sigma}\leq z\Bigg)-\Phi(z)\Bigg|\Bigg\}.

So for proving Theorem
3.1
, it suffices to prove a B-E bound of the same form for

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\Bigg|P_{H}\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{n}W_{n}}{\sigma}\leq z\Bigg)-\Phi(z)\Bigg|

on the right hand side of (
3.14
), which will utilize the decomposition of
W_{n}
into a sum between
U_{n}
and
B_{n}
in (
3.9
). Note that, conditional on the observed data
\{{\bf X}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
,
B_{n}
is a sum of independent terms driven by the randomness of the Bernoulli flips
Z_{\bf i}
, so it is expected to follow some form of central limit theorem. In particular, the following B-E bound for
B_{n}
proven in Appendix
C.1
will be useful.

Lemma 3.3

(B-E bound for
B_{n}
)
.

Under the same assumptions as Theorem
3.1
, we have a B-E bound of the form

\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\Bigg|P_{H}\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{N}B_{n}}{\sigma_{h}}\leq z^{\prime}\Bigg)-\Phi(z^{\prime})\Bigg|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{N}}\frac{\sqrt{1-3p_{n,N}+3p_{n,N}^{2}}}{1-p_{n,N}}+\frac{C(m)}{\sqrt{n}}.

From this point on, we will separate the proof into the regular and singular cases corresponding to
\sigma_{g}\neq 0
and
\sigma_{g}=0
.
We will focus on proving Theorem
3.1
(i)
here, where another technical innovation lies due to the need to handle the delicate near-singular situations with a
nonuniform
Berry-Esseen bound for
U_{n}
. The proof of Theorem
3.1
(ii)
is deferred to Appendix
C.3
.

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
3.1
(i)

To bound
\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}|P_{H}(\frac{\sqrt{n}W_{n}}{\sigma}\leq z)-\Phi(z)|
in (
3.14
) when
\sigma_{g}>0
, it suffices to consider
z\geq 0
only, or else one can replace the kernel
h(\cdot)
with
-h(\cdot)
. Let
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
be two independent
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
random variables that are also independent of the data
\{{\bf X}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
, and use
p=p_{n,N}
and
\alpha=\alpha_{n}
to simplify notation. From (
3.9
), we write the chain of inequalities

\displaystyle P_{H}\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{n}W_{n}}{\sigma}\leq z\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{H}\Bigg[P\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}B_{n}}{\sigma_{h}}\leq\frac{z\sigma}{\sigma_{h}\sqrt{\alpha(1-p)}}-\sqrt{\frac{N}{1-p}}\frac{U_{n}}{\sigma_{h}}\Bigg|\{{\bf X}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq P_{H}\left(Z_{1}\leq\frac{z\sigma}{\sigma_{h}\sqrt{\alpha(1-p)}}-\sqrt{\frac{N}{1-p}}\frac{U_{n}}{\sigma_{h}}\right)+\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|P(\sqrt{N}B_{n}\sigma_{h}^{-1}\leq z^{\prime})-\Phi(z^{\prime})\Big|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[P_{H}\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{n}U_{n}}{m\sigma_{g}}\leq\frac{z\sigma}{m\sigma_{g}}-\frac{\sigma_{h}\sqrt{\alpha(1-p)}}{m\sigma_{g}}Z_{1}\Bigg|Z_{1}\Bigg)\Bigg]+\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}|P(\sqrt{N}B_{n}\sigma_{h}^{-1}\leq z^{\prime})-\Phi(z^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq P\Big(Z_{2}\leq\frac{z\sigma}{m\sigma_{g}}-\frac{\sigma_{h}\sqrt{\alpha(1-p)}}{m\sigma_{g}}Z_{1}\Big)+\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}|P(\sqrt{N}B_{n}\sigma_{h}^{-1}\leq z^{\prime})-\Phi(z^{\prime})|+\varepsilon_{1}

\displaystyle=\Phi\Bigg(\frac{\sigma z}{\sqrt{m^{2}\sigma_{g}^{2}+\alpha(1-p)\sigma_{h}^{2}}}\Bigg)+\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}|P(\sqrt{N}B_{n}\sigma_{h}^{-1}\leq z^{\prime})-\Phi(z^{\prime})|+\varepsilon_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi(z)+\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}|P(\sqrt{N}B_{n}\sigma_{h}^{-1}\leq z^{\prime})-\Phi(z^{\prime})|+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2},

where

(3.15)

\varepsilon_{1}\equiv\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\Big|P_{H}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{n}U_{n}}{m\sigma_{g}}\leq\frac{z\sigma}{m\sigma_{g}}-\frac{\sigma_{h}\sqrt{\alpha(1-p)}}{m\sigma_{g}}Z_{1}\Big|Z_{1}\Big)-\Phi\Big(\frac{z\sigma}{m\sigma_{g}}-\frac{\sigma_{h}\sqrt{\alpha(1-p)}}{m\sigma_{g}}Z_{1}\Big)\Big|\Bigg],

and

\varepsilon_{2}\equiv\Bigg|\Phi\Bigg(\frac{\sigma z}{\sqrt{m^{2}\sigma_{g}^{2}+\alpha(1-p)\sigma_{h}^{2}}}\Bigg)-\Phi(z)\Bigg|;

the last equality above uses that
m\sigma_{g}Z_{2}+\sigma_{h}\sqrt{\alpha(1-p)}Z_{1}
is distributed as a
\mathcal{N}(0,m^{2}\sigma_{g}^{2}+\alpha(1-p)\sigma_{h}^{2})
random variable. By an analogous and reverse argument,

P\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{n}W_{n}}{\sigma}\leq z\Bigg)\geq\Phi(z)-\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}|P(\sqrt{N}B_{n}\sigma_{h}^{-1}\leq z^{\prime})-\Phi(z^{\prime})|-\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2},

which together with the previous inequality implies

(3.16)

\Bigg|P\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{n}W_{n}}{\sigma}\leq z\Bigg)-\Phi(z)\Bigg|\leq\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}|P(\sqrt{N}B_{n}\sigma_{h}^{-1}\leq z^{\prime})-\Phi(z^{\prime})|+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}.

Combining (
3.14
), (
3.16
), and Lemma
3.3
, to finish proving Theorem
3.1
(i)
, it suffices to let
\mathfrak{R}=\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}
and form a bound for it by bounding
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
separately:

3.1.1.
Bound on
\varepsilon_{1}

A critical ingredient to develop our bound for
\varepsilon_{1}
is the following
nonuniform
B-E bound for
U_{n}
with terms that decay exponentially in the magnitude of the argument in the distribution function.

Lemma 3.4

(A nonuniform B-E bound for
U_{n}
)
.

Let
U_{n}
be constructed as in (
2.4
) with the kernel
h
in (
2.3
) and data from (
1.5
); assume
2m<n
. For any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

\Bigg|P_{H}\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{m\sigma_{g}}U_{n}\leq x\Bigg)-\Phi(x)\Bigg|\leq C_{1}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{H}[|g|^{3}]}{\sqrt{n}\sigma_{g}^{3}}+\\
P_{H}\left(\Bigg|\frac{\sqrt{n}}{m\sigma_{g}}\sum_{r=2}^{m}{m\choose r}U_{n}^{(r)}\Bigg|>\frac{|x|+1}{3}\right)+C_{2}e^{-|x|/6}\Bigg(\frac{(\sqrt{2}+2)(m-1)\sigma_{h}}{\sqrt{m(n-m+1)}\sigma_{g}}\Bigg).

The proof is in Appendix
C.2
. Note that the first term
C_{1}\mathbb{E}_{H}[|g|^{3}]/(\sqrt{n}\sigma_{g}^{3})
in the bound has no dependence on
x
so Lemma
3.4
is, strictly speaking, “partially nonuniform”. With more effort, we could have made this term dependent on
x
as well, which is not necessarily for our purpose; the crucial part is that the last term containing the ratio
\sigma_{h}/\sigma_{g}
from (
2.14
) decays in
|x|

exponentially
, which provides a pathway for eliminating this ratio in our final B-E bound for
U^{\prime}_{n,N}
.

Now, we define

\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}\equiv\frac{z\sigma}{m\sigma_{g}}-\frac{\sigma_{h}\sqrt{\alpha(1-p)}}{m\sigma_{g}}Z_{1}

, with the understanding that
\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}
depends on both
z
and
Z_{1}
. Moreover, it is easy to see that

(3.17)

\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}\sim\mathcal{N}\Big(\frac{z\sigma}{m\sigma_{g}},\tau^{2}\Big)

with randomness driven by
Z_{1}
, where
\tau\equiv\frac{\sqrt{\alpha(1-p)}\sigma_{h}}{m\sigma_{g}}
. By
treating
Z_{1}
as fixed and apply Lemma
3.4
to the absolute difference inside the expectation operator in the definition of
\varepsilon_{1}
in (
3.15
), we get

(3.18)

\varepsilon_{1}\leq\frac{C_{1}\mathbb{E}_{H}[|g|^{3}]}{\sqrt{n}\sigma_{g}^{3}}+\frac{C_{2}(\sqrt{2}+2)(m-1)\sigma_{h}}{\sqrt{m(n-m+1)}\sigma_{g}}\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}\Big[e^{-|\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}|/6}\Big]+\\
\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}\Big[P_{H}\Big(\Big|\frac{\sqrt{n}}{m\sigma_{g}}\sum_{r=2}^{m}{m\choose r}U_{n}^{(r)}\Big|>\frac{|\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}|+1}{3}\Big|Z_{1}\Big)\Big],

where the subscript in
\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}[\cdot]
emphasizes that the expectation is driven by the random variable
Z_{1}
. We now have to bound the two expectations of the type
\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}[\cdot]
on the right hand side of (
3.18
). For the first one, we note from (
3.17
) that

\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}\Big[e^{-|\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}|/6}\Big]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-|x|/6}\phi\Bigg(\frac{x-\frac{z\sigma}{m\sigma_{g}}}{\tau}\Bigg)dx;

since
e^{-|x|/6}
is symmetrically decreasing as
|x|\longrightarrow\infty
, intuitively,
\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}\Big[e^{-|\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}|/6}\Big]
should attain the largest value when the highest mass from the
\mathcal{N}(\frac{z\sigma}{m\sigma_{g}},\tau^{2})
density can be placed at
x=0
, which happens when
z=0
. Indeed, that
\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}[e^{-|\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}|/6}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}[e^{-|\mathfrak{z}_{0,Z_{1}}|/6}]=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}\tau}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x/6}\exp\Big(-\frac{x^{2}}{2\tau^{2}}\Big)dx
is implied by the
rearrangement inequality

(
Lieb and Loss 2001
, Theorem 3.4, p.82)
, so we can proceed using “completing the square” as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}\Bigg[e^{-|\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}|/6}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}\tau}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x/6}\exp\Big(-\frac{x^{2}}{2\tau^{2}}\Big)dx

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{\tau^{2}}{72}\right)\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}\tau}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-(-6^{-1}\tau^{2}))^{2}}{2\cdot\tau^{2}}\right)dx

\displaystyle=2\exp\left(\frac{\tau^{2}}{72}\right)\bar{\Phi}(\tau/6)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{\tau^{2}}{72}\right)\min\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{6}{\tau\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)\exp\left(\frac{-\tau^{2}}{72}\right)

(3.19)

\displaystyle=2\min\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{6}{\tau\sqrt{2\pi}}\right),

where the last inequality uses the well-known
\bar{\Phi}(x)\leq\min(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{x\sqrt{2\pi}})e^{-x^{2}/2}
for
x>0

(
Chen et al. 2011
, p.16)
.
For the second
\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}[\cdot]
expectation in (
3.18
), we first apply a union bound to the tail probability and obtain

(3.20)

P_{H}\Big(\Big|\frac{\sqrt{n}}{m\sigma_{g}}\sum_{r=2}^{m}{m\choose r}U_{n}^{(r)}\Big|>\frac{|\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}|+1}{3}\Big|Z_{1}\Big)\leq\\
\sum_{r=2}^{m}P_{H}\Big(|\sqrt{n}U_{n}^{(r)}|>\frac{m\sigma_{g}(|\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}|+1)}{3{m\choose r}(m-1)}\Big|Z_{1}\Big);

each summand on the right hand side can be bounded using Lemma
3.2
as

(3.21)

P_{H}\Big(|\sqrt{n}U_{n}^{(r)}|>\frac{m\sigma_{g}(|\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}|+1)}{3{m\choose r}(m-1)}\Big|Z_{1}\Big)\\
\leq\begin{cases}C(m)\exp\Big(-\frac{c(m)\sigma_{g}^{2}\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}^{2}n^{r-1}}{\sigma_{h}^{2}}\Big)&\text{if }|\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}|+1\leq\mathfrak{c}_{m}n^{\frac{1-r}{2}}\frac{\sigma_{h}}{\sigma_{g}};\\
\frac{C(m)}{n}&\text{if }|\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}|+1>\mathfrak{C}_{m}n^{\frac{1-r}{2}}(\log(2n^{r}+1))^{3r}\frac{\sigma_{h}}{\sigma_{g}},\end{cases}

for some special constants
\mathfrak{c}_{m},\mathfrak{C}_{m}>0
depending only on
m
, whose values will
not
change in their ensuing reoccurrences. Combining (
3.20
) and (
3.21
) and take the outer expectation
\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}[\cdot]
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}\Big[P_{H}\Big(\Big|\frac{\sqrt{n}}{m\sigma_{g}}\sum_{r=2}^{m}{m\choose r}U_{n}^{(r)}\Big|>\frac{|\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}|+1}{3}\Big|Z_{1}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{1}(m)}{n}+C_{2}(m)\sum_{r=2}^{m}\Bigg\{\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}\Big[\exp\Big(-\frac{c(m)\sigma_{g}^{2}\mathfrak{z}_{0,Z_{1}}^{2}n^{r-1}}{\sigma_{h}^{2}}\Big)\Big]

(3.22)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}\Big[I\Big(\mathfrak{c}_{m}n^{\frac{1-r}{2}}\frac{\sigma_{h}}{\sigma_{g}}-1<|\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}|\leq\mathfrak{C}_{m}n^{\frac{1-r}{2}}(\log(2n^{r}+1))^{3r}\frac{\sigma_{h}}{\sigma_{g}}-1\Big)\Big]\Bigg\}.

Now we bound the two
\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}[\cdot]
expectations in (
3.22
). First, from (
3.17
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}\Big[\exp\Big(-\frac{c(m)\sigma_{g}^{2}\mathfrak{z}_{0,Z_{1}}^{2}n^{r-1}}{\sigma_{h}^{2}}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{m\sigma_{g}}{\sqrt{2\pi\alpha(1-p)\sigma_{h}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\Bigg\{-\Bigg[\frac{\sigma_{g}^{2}}{\sigma_{h}^{2}}\Bigg(c(m)n^{r-1}+\frac{m^{2}}{2\alpha(1-p)}\Bigg)\Bigg]x^{2}\Bigg\}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{\sqrt{2\alpha(1-p)c(m)n^{r-1}+m^{2}}}

(3.23)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(m)\sqrt{N}}{\sqrt{\big(1-p\big)n^{r}}}.

Second, from the normal distribution of
\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}
in (
3.17
) again,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}\Big[I\Big(\mathfrak{c}_{m}n^{\frac{1-r}{2}}\frac{\sigma_{h}}{\sigma_{g}}-1<|\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}|\leq\mathfrak{C}_{m}n^{\frac{1-r}{2}}(\log(2n^{r}+1))^{3r}\frac{\sigma_{h}}{\sigma_{g}}-1\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n^{\frac{1-r}{2}}\sigma_{h}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\tau\sigma_{g}}(\mathfrak{C}_{m}(\log(2n^{r}+1))^{3r}-\mathfrak{c}_{m})

(3.24)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(m)\sqrt{N}[(\log(2n^{r}+1))^{3r}-1]}{\sqrt{(1-p)n^{r}}}

Now, gathering (
3.22
), (
3.23
) and (
3.24
), we obtain

(3.25)

\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}\Bigg[P_{H}\Bigg(\Big|\frac{\sqrt{n}}{m\sigma_{g}}\sum_{r=2}^{m}{m\choose r}U_{n}^{(r)}\Big|>\frac{|\mathfrak{z}_{z,Z_{1}}|+1}{3}\Bigg|Z_{1}\Bigg)\Bigg]\leq\\
C(m)\Bigg\{\frac{1}{n}+\sum_{r=2}^{m}\frac{\sqrt{N}(\log(2n^{r}+1))^{3r}}{\sqrt{(1-p)n^{r}}}\Bigg\}.

Finally, applying both the bounds (
3.19
) and (
3.25
) to (
3.18
), we get

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[|g|^{3}]}{\sqrt{n}\sigma_{g}^{3}}+C(m)\Bigg\{\sum_{r=2}^{m}\frac{\sqrt{N}(\log(2n^{r}+1))^{3r}}{\sqrt{(1-p)n^{r}}}\Bigg\}

(3.26)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}+C(m)\Bigg\{\sum_{r=2}^{m}\frac{\sqrt{N}(\log(2n^{r}+1))^{3r}}{\sqrt{(1-p)n^{r}}}\Bigg\},

where the last inequality uses the hypercontractivity of
g({\bf X})
from (
2.30
).

3.1.2.
Bound on
\varepsilon_{2}

To simplify notation we let
\tilde{\sigma}=\sqrt{m^{2}\sigma_{g}^{2}+\alpha(1-p)\sigma_{h}^{2}}
. By Taylor’s expansion around
z
, that
\sigma\geq\tilde{\sigma}
, and the assumption that
z\geq 0
, we get

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\phi(z)z\Bigg(\frac{\sigma}{\tilde{\sigma}}-1\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigg(\frac{\sigma-\tilde{\sigma}}{\tilde{\sigma}}\Bigg)\text{ by $\sup_{z\geq 0}z\phi(z)\leq C$}

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigg(\frac{n{n\choose m}^{-1}\sigma_{h}^{2}}{\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}\Bigg)\text{ by multiplying $\frac{\sigma+\tilde{\sigma}}{\tilde{\sigma}}$ to the previous expression}

(3.27)

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigg(\frac{n{n\choose m}^{-1}\sigma_{h}^{2}}{\alpha(1-p)\sigma_{h}^{2}}\Bigg)=\frac{Cp}{1-p}

Finally, collecting (
3.26
) and (
3.27
), we get

\mathfrak{R}=\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}\leq C(m)\Bigg\{\sum_{r=2}^{m}\frac{\sqrt{N}(\log(2n^{r}+1))^{3r}}{\sqrt{(1-p_{n,N})n^{r}}}\Bigg\}+C\Bigg(\frac{p_{n,N}}{1-p_{n,N}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigg),

as claimed in Theorem
3.1
(i)
.

3.2.
Proof for the overall bound in
(
3.2
)

It is a simple consequence of combining parts
(i)
and
(ii)
because

\displaystyle p_{n,N}=N{n\choose m}^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m!}{(n-1)\cdots(n-m+1)}\cdot m\quad\text{ (by $N\leq mn$)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m(m-1)\cdots 2}{(2m+1)\dots(m+3)}\cdot m\quad\text{ (by $2m+1<n$)}

\displaystyle<1.

∎

4.
Bernstein’s inequalities for the Hoeffding projections

In this section we will establish a Bernstein inequality for the Hoeffding projections
U_{n}^{(r)}
from (

5.
Discussion

Under the Gaussian setting of the present paper, via an investigation into the information encoded by a key ratio of moments appearing in the standard Berry-Esseen bound for complete U-statistics as in (
2.13
), we have obtained a solid understanding of why the classical asymptotics of the Wald test cannot be useful near singularities. With the same understanding, our main result in Section
3
also pinpoints why our proposed incomplete U-statistic construction is a natural alternative for circumventing the issue by trading off just enough statistical power.
We conclude by discussing several open issues that are expected to lead to much future research.

5.1.
Relaxation of Gaussian assumptions

The standard Gaussian setting as in (
1.5
) serves as a good starting point for looking into the behavior of the Wald test in face of (near-)singularities, and the proofs of our theorems have suggested that the hypercontractive estimate (or Khintchine-type inequality) in Lemma
2.2
plays a key role in explaining the singularity-agnostic behavior of
S_{\lfloor n/m\rfloor}
and
U_{n,N}^{\prime}
with respect to the weak convergent speed. However, we have only adopted the Gaussian assumptions more for its neatness rather than absolute necessity, as similar Khintchine-type inequalities for log-concave distributions can also be found in the literature; see
Carbery and Wright 2001
and the references therein.

5.2.
Berry-Esseen bound for one-dimensional Studentized incomplete U-statistics.

While our B-E bound in Theorem
3.1
captures that the version of
U_{n,N}^{\prime}
normalized by the theoretical limiting variance
\sigma^{2}
is singularity-agnostic with respect to both the limiting distribution and convergent speed, it would be ideal to be able to establish the same for the practical version normalized by the data-driven “divide-and-conquer”
Studentizer

\hat{\sigma}^{2}
recommended in
Sturma et al. 2022
and
Chen and Kato 2019
. Note that establishing B-E bounds for Studentized statistics is a highly non-trivial task that is the subject matter of self-normalized limit theory
(
de la Peña et al. 2009
)
, and it was not until recently that optimal-rate B-E bounds for Studentized
complete
U-statistics of any kernel degree appeared in works by one of the present authors
Leung and Shao 2023a
,
Leung and Shao 2023b
, under minimalistic moment assumptions. We note that the uniform B-E bound for Studentized U-statistics in
Leung and Shao 2023a
also very much parallels the bound for standardized U-statistics in (
2.13
), in that an informative moment ratio like
\sigma_{h}/\sigma_{g}
also shows up.

5.3.
Extension to multivariate or high-dimensional settings

As has been mentioned at the end of Section
1.1
, even more challenging is to develop similar singularity-agnostic B-E bounds in the spirit of Theorem
3.1
, in the multivariate or high-dimensional settings where multiple polynomial constraints are handled at once by an incomplete U-statistic with a vector-valued kernel; after all, a statistical model such as the factor analysis is described by a multitude of such constraints like the vanishing tetrads in (
1.3
). This endeavor is particularly well-motivated, as
Dufour et al. 2017
have shown that the Wald statistic for multiple polynomial constraints may diverge in distribution, and hence a singularity-agnostic alternative is even more sought after with this in mind.

Definition A.1

For a given

The above definition comes from

If

c(\alpha)\sup_{p\geq 1}p^{-1/\alpha}\|Y\|_{p}\leq\|Y\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}\leq C(\alpha)\sup_{p\geq 1}p^{-1/\alpha}\|Y\|_{p};

(\alpha)

Lemma D.1

(Sub-Weibull order for

)

For a given

Proof of Lemma

.

For

, in light of the definition of a projection kernel in (

the latter form implies

(ii)

(i)

Lemma D.2
