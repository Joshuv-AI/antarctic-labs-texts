A New Family of Weighted One-Parameter Flux Reconstruction Schemes
W. Trojak, F. D. Witherden

Abstract
The flux reconstruction (FR) approach offers a flexible framework for describing a range of high-order numerical schemes; including nodal discontinuous Galerkin and spectral difference schemes. This is accomplished through the use of so-called correction functions. In this study we employ a weighted Sobolev norm to define a new extended family of FR correction functions, the stability of which is affirmed through Fourier analysis. Several of the schemes within this family are found to exhibit reduced dissipation and dispersion overshoot. Moreover, many of the new schemes possess higher CFL limits whilst maintaining the expected rate of convergence. Numerical experiments with homogeneous linear convection and Burgers’ turbulence are undertaken, and the results observed to be in agreement with the theoretical findings.

[

2
Flux Reconstruction

Consider using the FR approach to solve the 1D conservative equation

\frac{\partial u}{\partial{t}}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial{x}}=0,\quad\mathrm{for}\quad x\in\mathbf{\Omega},\quad\mathrm{and}\quad t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.

(1)

The FR approach involves partitioning the domain,
\mathbf{\Omega}
, into
N
distinct elements, each denoted by
\mathbf{\Omega}_{n}=\{x|x_{n}<x<x_{n+1}\}
, such that

\mathbf{\Omega}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathbf{\Omega}_{n}\hskip 28.45274pt\mathrm{and}\hskip 28.45274pt\bigcap_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathbf{\Omega}_{n}=\emptyset.

(2)

The solution
u
in Eq. (
) is approximated in each
\mathbf{\Omega}_{n}
by
u^{\delta}_{n}=u^{\delta}_{n}(x,t)
, which is a polynomial of degree
p
within
\mathbf{\Omega}_{n}
, and the flux
f
is approximated by
f^{\delta}_{n}=f^{\delta}_{n}(x,t)
, a degree
p+1
polynomial within
\mathbf{\Omega}_{n}
.

To permit the use of standardised operators, the mapping of each element,
\mathbf{\Omega}_{n}
, to a reference element,
\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}=\{\zeta|-1\leq\zeta\leq 1\}
, is calculated

\zeta=\Theta_{n}(x)=2\left(\frac{x-x_{n}}{x_{n+1}-x_{n}}\right)-1,

(3)

which has the inverse

x=\Theta_{n}^{-1}(\zeta)=\left(\frac{1-\zeta}{2}\right)x_{n}+\left(\frac{1+\zeta}{2}\right)x_{n+1}.

(4)

The governing equation, Eq. (
), may then be cast into the reference domain as

\frac{\partial\hat{u}^{\delta}}{\partial{t}}+\frac{\partial\hat{f}^{\delta}}{\partial{\zeta}}=0,

(5)

where

\displaystyle\hat{u}^{\delta}=\hat{u}^{\delta}(\zeta,t)

\displaystyle=J_{n}u^{\delta}_{n}(\Theta_{n}^{-1}(\zeta),t),

(6a)

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{\delta}=\hat{f}^{\delta}(\zeta,t)

\displaystyle=f^{\delta}_{n}(\Theta_{n}^{-1}(\zeta),t).

(6b)

For linearly deformed 1D elements the Jacobian is simply
J_{n}=(x_{n+1}-x_{n})/2
.

If inside the reference element, there is a set of points where the solution is known,
\zeta_{i}
(
i=\{0\dots p\}
), then
\hat{u}^{\delta}
and
\hat{f}^{\delta D}
may be defined with Lagrange polynomials as

\hat{u}^{\delta}=\sum_{i=0}^{p}\hat{u}^{\delta}_{i}\;l_{i}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\hat{f}^{\delta D}=\sum_{i=0}^{p}\hat{f}^{\delta D}_{i}\;l_{i},

(7)

where
\hat{u}^{\delta}_{i}=\hat{u}^{\delta}(\zeta_{i})
and
\hat{f}^{\delta D}_{i}
is the flux calculated from
\hat{u}^{\delta}_{i}
. The Lagrange polynomials in the previous definition take the form

l_{i}=\prod_{j=0,j\neq i}^{p}\left(\frac{\zeta-\zeta_{j}}{\zeta_{i}-\zeta_{j}}\right).

(8)

The flux polynomial in Eq. (
) currently represents a transformed flux that is discontinuous between elements. This is made
C^{0}
continuous in 1D as

\hat{f}^{\delta}=\hat{f}^{\delta D}+\hat{f}^{\delta C}=\hat{f}^{\delta D}+(\hat{f}^{\delta I}_{L}-\hat{f}^{\delta D}_{L})h_{L}+(\hat{f}^{\delta I}_{R}-\hat{f}^{\delta D}_{R})h_{R}.

(9)

\hat{f}^{\delta I}_{L}
is a common flux at the left element interface,
\zeta=-1
. This value is common with a collocated point in the associated adjacent element.
\hat{f}^{\delta I}_{R}
is similarly defined. This common value is typically calculated using an approximate Riemann solver, but any E-flux is sufficient
[
20
,
21
]
. Here,
h_{L}
and
h_{R}
are degree
p+1
correction function polynomials with boundary conditions

\displaystyle h_{L}(-1)=1,\hskip 14.22636pth_{L}(1)=0,

(10a)

\displaystyle h_{R}(-1)=0,\hskip 14.22636pth_{R}(1)=1.

(10b)

These conditions are such that the corrected flux,
\hat{f}^{\delta C}
, takes the value of
\hat{f}^{\delta I}_{L}
and
\hat{f}^{\delta I}_{R}
are the respective interfaces. The spatial derivative of the corrected flux may then be taken

\frac{\partial\hat{f}^{\delta}}{\partial{\zeta}}(\zeta_{i})=\sum_{j=0}^{p}\hat{f}^{\delta D}_{j}\;\frac{\mathrm{d}l_{j}}{\mathrm{d}\zeta}(\zeta_{i})+(\hat{f}^{\delta I}_{L}-\hat{f}^{\delta D}_{L})\frac{\mathrm{d}h_{L}}{\mathrm{d}\zeta}(\zeta_{i})+(\hat{f}^{\delta I}_{R}-\hat{f}^{\delta D}_{R})\frac{\mathrm{d}h_{R}}{\mathrm{d}\zeta}(\zeta_{i}).

(11)

As the correction functions were degree
p+1
, the derivative of the corrected flux and the solution are now both in a degree
p
polynomial space. Hence they may be combined in the governing equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{u}^{\delta}_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{\partial\hat{f}^{\delta}}{\partial{\zeta}}(\zeta_{i}).

(12)

The solution can then be advanced with a suitable choice of time integration or ODE method.

The nature of a particular FR scheme depends on three factors: the location of the solution points, the interface flux formulation, and the form of the correction functions. In their 2007 paper introducing FR, Huynh
[
3
]
found that a collocation-based nodal DG scheme (NDG) is recovered if the left and right correction functions are taken to be the right and left Radau polynomials, respectively. They also showed how, at least for a linear flux function, it is possible for FR to recover any SD scheme. Moreover, Huynh also presented several new and novel schemes with various stability and accuracy properties.

Through a tensor-product construction the FR approach can be readily extended to handle quadrilateral and hexahedral elements. Further details can be found in
[
3
,
22
]
.

The defining feature of the FR methodology is the unification it brings to several schemes through the correction functions. The identification of such functions is the main concern of the present work, extending the OSFR set of schemes
[
12
]
. Specifically, the OSFR corrections functions are obtained by setting:

h_{L}=\frac{(-1)^{p}}{2}\bigg[L_{p}-\frac{\eta_{p}L_{p-1}+L_{p+1}}{1+\eta_{p}}\bigg],

and

h_{R}=\frac{1}{2}\bigg[L_{p}+\frac{\eta_{p}L_{p-1}+L_{p+1}}{1+\eta_{p}}\bigg],

where
L_{p}=L_{p}(\zeta)
is a Legendre polynomial of degree
p
and

\eta_{p}=\frac{c(2p+1)(a_{p}p!)^{2}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad a_{p}=\frac{(2p)!}{2^{p}(p!)^{2}},

with
c
being a free scalar parameter which must lie within the range

\frac{-2}{(2p+1)(a_{p}p!)^{2}}<c<\infty.

Such correction functions satisfy

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}h_{L}\frac{\partial\hat{u}^{\delta}}{\partial{\zeta}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta-c\frac{\partial^{p}\hat{u}^{\delta}}{\partial{\zeta}^{p}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{p+1}h_{L}}{\mathrm{d}{\zeta}^{p+1}}

\displaystyle=0,

(13a)

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}h_{R}\frac{\partial\hat{u}^{\delta}}{\partial{\zeta}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta-c\frac{\partial^{p}\hat{u}^{\delta}}{\partial{\zeta}^{p}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{p+1}h_{R}}{\mathrm{d}{\zeta}^{p+1}}

\displaystyle=0.

(13b)

This property ensures that the resulting scheme will be linearly stable in the broken-Sobolev type norm

\sum^{N}_{n=1}\|\hat{u}^{\delta}_{n}\|^{2}_{W^{c}_{2}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\int_{x_{n}}^{x_{n+1}}(u_{n}^{\delta})^{2}+\frac{c}{2}J_{n}^{2p}\bigg(\frac{\partial^{p}u_{n}^{\delta}}{\partial{x}^{p}}\bigg)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(14)

i.e. the energy is sufficiently constrained to give a bounded functional space of solutions.

Weighted Energy Stability

Following on from the definition of OSFR, we may define the Jacobi weighted Sobolev norm
[
23
]
in the reference domain as

\|\hat{u}^{\delta}\|^{2}_{W_{2}^{\iota,w}}=\int^{1}_{-1}{\Bigg(\big(\hat{u}^{\delta}\big)^{2}+\iota\bigg(\frac{\partial^{p}\hat{u}^{\delta}}{\partial{\zeta}^{p}}\bigg)^{2}\Bigg)(1-\zeta)^{\alpha}(1+\zeta)^{\beta}}\mathrm{d}{\zeta},

For brevity we will define the average as

\overline{w}_{\alpha,\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\int^{1}_{-1}{w_{\alpha,\beta}(\zeta)}\mathrm{d}{\zeta}.

(17)

\displaystyle=\tilde{h}_{L,p}\underbrace{\bigg(\frac{({\beta+1})_{p}}{p!}\bigg)\bigg[\bigg(\frac{p+\alpha}{p+\beta}\bigg)\frac{\iota p(p+1)+(p+\beta)(p+\beta+1)A_{p}}{\iota p(p+1)+(p+\alpha)(p+\alpha+1)A_{p}}+1\bigg]}_{\kappa_{p}^{(\alpha,\beta)}},

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{L,p+1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{A_{p}J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p}(1)\tilde{h}_{L,p}}{\iota J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p-1}(1)+A_{p}J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p+1}(1)}=-\frac{A_{p}(p+1)(p+\alpha)\tilde{h}_{L,p}}{\iota p(p+1)+(p+\alpha+1)(p+\alpha)A_{p}},

repeating for the right and combining these results we obtain

\displaystyle h_{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{p}}{\kappa_{p}^{(\alpha,\beta)}}\bigg(J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p}-\frac{\iota(p+1)(p+\alpha)J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p-1}+(p+1)(p+\alpha)A_{p}J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p+1}}{\iota p(p+1)+(p+\alpha+1)(p+\alpha)A_{p}}\bigg),

(28a)

\displaystyle h_{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa_{p}^{(\beta,\alpha)}}\bigg(J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p}+\frac{\iota(p+1)(p+\beta)J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p-1}+(p+1)(p+\beta)A_{p}J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p+1}}{\iota p(p+1)+(p+\beta+1)(p+\beta)A_{p}}\bigg).

(28b)

We now have definitions for the left and right correction functions which lead to the decay of the weighted Sobolev norm being defined by Eq. (

Instead to further understand the stability of this set of schemes we will turn to semi-discrete and fully-discrete Fourier analysis.

It has been shown

h_{L}(\zeta_{z})=h_{R}(\zeta_{z})=0
for

The simplest method of achieving an SD in FR is by prescribing the interior zeros to be a set of quadrature points. In the first proof of energy stability of SD schemes the canonical Gauss–Legendre quadrature
[
19
]
was utilised, but other quadratures have been considered. For example Lui et al.
[

p^{\mathrm{th}}
Gauss–Jacobi points are the roots of the
p^{\mathrm{th}}
order Jacobi polynomial
[
30
]
. These give rise to the correction functions

h_{L,\mathrm{SD}}=\frac{(1-\zeta)}{2}\frac{J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p}(\zeta)}{J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p}(-1)}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad h_{R,\mathrm{SD}}=\frac{(1+\zeta)}{2}\frac{J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p}(\zeta)}{J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p}(1)},

(29)

where the correction functions are normalised for the edge value as Jacobi polynomials do not guarantee unit magnitude at
\pm 1
. The recurrence relation for Jacobi polynomials
[
31
]
leads us to the following form

\displaystyle h_{L,\mathrm{SD}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{p}\gamma_{p}(\beta)\bigg(-a_{p}J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p-1}+\frac{1}{2}(1-b_{p})J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p}-c_{p}J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p+1}\bigg),

(30a)

\displaystyle h_{R,\mathrm{SD}}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{p}(\alpha)\bigg(a_{p}J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p-1}+\frac{1}{2}(1+b_{p})J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p}+c_{p}J^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p+1}\bigg).

(30b)

Where

\displaystyle a_{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{(p+\alpha)(p+\beta)}{(2p+\alpha+\beta+1)(2p+\alpha+\beta)},

(31a)

\displaystyle b_{p}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}{(2p+\alpha+\beta+2)(2p+\alpha+\beta)},

(31b)

\displaystyle c_{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{(p+1)(p+1+\alpha+\beta)}{(2p+\alpha+\beta+1)(2p+\alpha+\beta+2)},

(31c)

\displaystyle\gamma_{p}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(p+1)\Gamma(x+1)}{\Gamma(p+x+1)}.

(31d)

This is included for completeness, however the focus will be put on the Jacobi SD function of Eq.(

(32)

where

is a multi-index and

3.2
Limits on Stability

In order for the correction function to be stable, Eq. (
) must be satisfied. Moreover, the norm described by Eq. (
) must indeed be a norm;
i.e.
it must be positive, definite, homogeneous, and obey the triangle inequality. Of particular concern to us is the positivity condition; for the remaining conditions follow immediately due to the linear nature of differentiation. Hence, it is required that

0<\|\hat{u}^{\delta}\|^{2}_{W_{2}^{\iota,w}}=\int^{1}_{-1}{\Bigg(\big(\hat{u}^{\delta}\big)^{2}+\iota\bigg(\frac{\partial^{p}\hat{u}^{\delta}}{\partial{\zeta}^{p}}\bigg)^{2}\Bigg)(1-\zeta)^{\alpha}(1+\zeta)^{\beta}}\mathrm{d}{\zeta}<\infty,

(33)

for
\hat{u}^{\delta}\neq 0
.
Therefore, following the method of
[
18
]
we can substitute Eq. (
) into Eq. (
) as

0<\sum^{p}_{i=0}\tilde{u}^{2}_{i}q_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}+\iota\big(b_{p}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\big)^{2}q_{0}^{(\alpha,\beta)}<\infty.

(34)

Then grouping terms of the same order

0<\sum^{p-1}_{i=0}\tilde{u}_{i}^{2}q_{i}^{(\alpha,\beta)}+\bigg(q_{p}^{(\alpha,\beta)}+\iota\big(b_{p}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\big)^{2}q_{0}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\bigg)\tilde{u}_{p}^{2}<\infty.

(35)

As
\tilde{u}_{i}^{2}
is always positive, the limit on the value of
\iota
is

-\!\iota_{\mathrm{crit}}=-\frac{q_{p}^{(\alpha,\beta)}}{\big(b_{p}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\big)^{2}q_{0}^{(\alpha,\beta)}}\leqslant\iota<\infty.

(36)

The value of
\iota_{\mathrm{crit}}
can be evaluated in a closed form that shows it is always positive. Consider

\begin{split}\iota_{\mathrm{crit}}&=\frac{q_{p}^{(\alpha,\beta)}}{\big(b_{p}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\big)^{2}q_{0}^{(\alpha,\beta)}}\\
&=\bigg(\frac{\alpha+\beta+1}{2p+\alpha+\beta+1}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{({\alpha+1})_{p}({\beta+1})_{p}}{({\alpha+\beta+1})_{p}}\bigg)\times\\
\bigg(\frac{2^{p}}{({p+\alpha+\beta+1})_{p}}\bigg)^{2}.\end{split}

(37)

A consequence of this is that the norm is always valid for
\iota=0
, which is of particular interest due to the norms’ analogy to that of nodal DG.

To show this definition of
\iota_{\mathrm{crit}}
is consistent with previous work, let us consider
\alpha=\beta=0
. Remembering that
({1})_{p}=p!
, we get

\frac{q_{p}^{(0,0)}}{\big(b_{p}^{(0,0)}\big)^{2}q_{0}^{(0,0)}}=\frac{(p!)^{3}}{(2p+1)}\bigg(\frac{2^{p}}{(2p)!}\bigg)^{2},

which, noting that
c=2\iota
, is equivalent to the result presented in section
.

In section
the set of SD correction function was extended using Gauss–Jacobi quadratures. The advantage of this method of extending the set of SD correction functions is that we may use Eq. (
) in order to understand if SD provides a valid norm. We therefore assert:

Lemma 3.1
.

SD Energy Stability.
An SD correction function with
\alpha,\beta\in(-1,\infty)
will always give the
\|\hat{u}^{\delta}\|_{W_{2}^{\iota,w}}
to be positive and hence is a valid norm.

Proof.

Using Eq.(
) to produce an expression of
\iota
for SD schemes, which, after normalisation by
\iota_{\mathrm{crit}}
, gives

\frac{\iota_{\mathrm{SD}}}{\iota_{\mathrm{crit}}}=\frac{p}{p+1}.

(38)

As
\iota_{\mathrm{crit}}
is always positive, so must
\iota_{\mathrm{SD}}
always be positive.
∎

In section
, two conditions were imposed, Eq. (

4.1

The semi-discrete Fourier analysis of other FR schemes has been performed in several works

\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_{j}}{\partial{t}}=-\frac{2}{h}\Big(\mathbf{C}_{+}\mathbf{u}_{j+1}+\mathbf{C}_{0}\mathbf{u}_{j}+\mathbf{C}_{-}\mathbf{u}_{j-1}\Big).

(39)

Here we have assumed a uniform mesh with grid spacing
h
. The operator matrices are defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{+}

\displaystyle=(1-\kappa)\mathbf{g}_{R}\mathbf{l}_{L}^{T},

(40a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{D}-\kappa\mathbf{g}_{L}\mathbf{l}_{L}^{T}-(1-\kappa)\mathbf{g}_{R}\mathbf{l}_{R}^{T},

(40b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{-}

\displaystyle=\kappa\mathbf{g}_{L}\mathbf{l}_{R}^{T},

(40c)

where
\kappa
is the upwinding ratio (fully upwinded and central differenced for
\kappa=1
and
0.5
respectively) and
\mathbf{D}
is the differentiation matrix. The array
\mathbf{l_{L}}
interpolates from the solution points to the left interface and
\mathbf{l_{R}}
is similarly defined.
\mathbf{g_{L}}
and
\mathbf{g_{R}}
are values of the left and right correction function gradients at the solution points. If we now impose a solution of the form

\mathbf{u}_{j}=\exp{(i(k\mathbf{x}_{j}-\omega t))}=\exp{\bigg(ik\bigg[\frac{h}{2}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}+1)+x_{j}-ct\bigg]\bigg)},

(41)

applying this to Eq. (

\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_{j}}{\partial{t}}=-\underbrace{\frac{2}{h}\Big(\mathbf{C}_{+}\exp{(ikh)}+\mathbf{C}_{0}+\mathbf{C}_{-}\exp{(-ikh)}\Big)}_{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{u_{j}}.

(42)

Differentiating
\mathbf{u}_{j}
with respect to time we find

c\mathbf{u}_{j}=-\frac{2i}{hk}\Big(\mathbf{C}_{+}\exp{(ikh)}+\mathbf{C}_{0}+\mathbf{C}_{-}\exp{(-ikh)}\Big)\mathbf{u}_{j}.

(43)

This is clearly an eigenvalue problem, where
c
is the modified phase speed (
\omega/k
) of the scheme and for a given wavenumber,

Therefore, the necessary and sufficient condition on scheme stability can be written as
\Im{(c)}\leqslant 0\;\forall\;k\in[0,k_{\mathrm{nq}}]

6
Conclusions

A new set of FR correction functions, defined in terms of Jacobi polynomials with stability affirmed through Fourier analysis, was defined using a weighted norm. This family proves the feasibility of incorporating a weight function into the definition of an FR scheme. Using these functions it is possible to recover, at least for a linear flux function, a range of spectral difference schemes. Theoretical Fourier analysis studies were then used to show that these new schemes can be used to increase wave resolution, while convergence studies showed that these new schemes can maintain high order convergence with increased temporal stability. The norm that is used to define this set can also permit the definition of a quasi-DG set, defined in a weighted
\ell_{2}
norm, but ultimately found to not be the same as DG. Numerical experiments were then performed to validate the theoretical findings, which showed that the optimal correction functions for a heterogeneous linear PDE and Burgers’ turbulence lay approximately in the same region predicted by theoretical investigations. The numerical results are indicative that this correction function family may be of use when compared to existing schemes applied to ILES. A summary of how the present work is positioned relative to the literature is shown in Fig.
. Furthermore, there are some areas addressed by this paper which may be furthered by studying how to pick a suitable correction function, and is it feasible to locally or globally adapt the correction function based on the solution.
