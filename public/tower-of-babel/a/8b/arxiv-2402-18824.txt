Batch size invariant Adam
Xi Wang xwang3@cs.umass.edu, Laurence Aitchison laurence.aitchison@bristol.ac.uk

Abstract
We propose a batch size invariant version of Adam, for use in large-scale, distributed settings, in which the mini- batch is divided into micro- batches which are distributed among worker nodes. For the v v term, standard Adam first computes the average over micro- batch gradients, then squares, while in the batch size invariant Adam proposed here, we first square the micro- batch gradients, then average. Previous work (e.g. Malladi et al. 2022) used an alternative approach that involved a square-root scaling of the learning rate, but this approach requires strong assumptions to work; in particular that the gradient variance dominates the square of the expected gradient. In contrast, the approach proposed here gives batch size invariance without this assumption. We confirm that in practice our scheme gives batch size invariance in a much larger range of scenarios than the previous approach.

Introduction

Adam
(
Kingma & Ba 2014
)
and Adam
(
Loshchilov & Hutter 2017
)
are state-of-art optimizerss, commonly used for LLM pretraining runs
(
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Touvron et al. 2023
, e.g.)
.
Given the huge investments in these pretraining runs, it is critical to understand how to tune the hyperparameters of Adam

.
However, as with most optimization algorithms, setting the Adam hyperparameters is difficult.
As an example of this, we might want to tune the hyperparameters in smaller-scale settings and transfer those hyperparameters to a single, very large-scale training run.
However, to do that effectively, we need our optimization to behave in the same way for any
mini-

This property, which we call batch size invariance, can be achieved straightforwardly in SGD, by scaling the learning rate linearly with the batch size (i.e.
\eta\propto B
)
(
Krizhevsky 2014
;
Stephan et al. 2017
;
Goyal et al. 2017
;
Smith & Le 2017
;
Smith et al. 2017
;
Ma et al. 2018
;
Golmant et al. 2018
;
McCandlish et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Shallue et al. 2019
)
.
However, for modern adaptive optimizers such as Adam
(
Kingma & Ba 2014
;
Loshchilov & Hutter 2017
)
, it is more difficult to achieve batch size invariance.

To understand the key barrier to obtaining truly batch size invariant Adam, we need to look at the Adam update rule,

\displaystyle\Delta w_{t}

\displaystyle=-\eta\frac{\hat{m}_{t}}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_{t}}+\epsilon}.

(1)

The key term here is
\hat{v}_{t}
, which is a debiased, exponential moving average estimate of the raw second moment of the gradient,
\operatorname{E}\left[(g^{\prime})^{2}\right]
(here, the expectation is over randomness in the choice of datapoints incorporated in the
mini-
batch, and we use
g^{\prime}
for the
mini-
batch gradient as we will use
g
for the
micro-
batch gradient later in the manuscript).
Of course, the raw second moment is the sum of the mean-squared gradient,
\operatorname{E}\left[g^{\prime}\right]^{2}
, and the variance of the gradient,
\operatorname{Var}\left[g^{\prime}\right]
.
The key issue is that the variance of the
mini-
batch gradient depends on the
mini-
batch size,
\operatorname{Var}\left[g^{\prime}\right]\propto 1/B

(
Granziol et al. 2022
;
Malladi et al. 2022
;
Hilton et al. 2022
)
.
There is therefore an inevitable
mini-
batch size dependence in
\hat{v}_{t}
, which appears as a change in the effective learning rate,
\eta/(\sqrt{\hat{v}_{t}}+\epsilon)
.

Recent work suggested correcting for these effects by tweaking the learning rate,
\eta

(
Granziol et al. 2022
;
Malladi et al. 2022
;
Hilton et al. 2022
)
.
In particular, they proposed a square-root scaling (i.e.
\eta\propto\sqrt{B}
).
However, for this square-root scaling to be valid, strong assumptions need to hold, namely that
\operatorname{Var}\left[g^{\prime}\right]
“dominates” (i.e. is much bigger than)
\operatorname{E}\left[g^{\prime}\right]^{2}
.
Of course, this can hardly be guaranteed to hold.
In fact, there are two settings where this assumption might break down.
First, close to initialization, where
\operatorname{E}\left[g^{\prime}\right]

will be large.
Second, as the batch size gets very large (e.g. in large LLM pretraining runs), we would expect
\operatorname{Var}\left[g^{\prime}\right]\propto 1/B
to become small.

We take a different approach to batch size invariance in Adam, by modifying the Adam updates themselves to eliminate the batch size dependence at source.
In particular, we consider a large-scale setting in which a
mini-
batch is split into a number of
micro-
batches, which may be distributed among worker nodes.
Standard Adam computes the average gradient (across
micro-
batches), then squares.
In contrast, we consider an alternative scheme, which first squares the
micro-
batch gradients, then averages across
micro-
batches.
We prove that this alternative scheme is batch size invariant under much weaker assumptions.
In particular, we do not need the gradient variance to dominate the expectation.
We just need sufficiently small updates (e.g. learning rates), which is required by
Malladi et al. 2022
anyway, and is just the Adam analogue of the critical batch size in SGD
(
Ma et al. 2018
;
Golmant et al. 2018
;
McCandlish et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Shallue et al. 2019
)
.

Related work

The closest prior work is
Granziol et al. 2022
,
Malladi et al. 2022
and
Hilton et al. 2022
, as they propose an alternative approach to obtaining batch size invariant Adam by scaling the learning rate to try to post-hoc correct for changes in the effective learning rate,
\eta/(\sqrt{\hat{v}}+\epsilon)
, caused by changes in
\hat{v}
.
In contrast, we propose to eliminate changes in the effective learning rate at source, by proposing a modification to Adam updates.
There are three key differences between our approach and this prior work.
First, our approach gives a linear scaling of the learning rate,
\eta\propto B
, while the prior work proposes a square-root scaling,
\eta\propto\sqrt{B}
(this is not a contradiction: both are correct in their respective setups).
Second, our theoretical approaches are radically different.
In particular,
Malladi et al. 2022
use stochastic differential equations.
In particular, they take the choice of datapoints in the
mini-
batch to be random, and they consider the updates to be random variables.
In contrast, in our actual proof (Appendix
C
), we consider the choice of datapoints in the
mini-
batches as fixed, and thus the updates become deterministic.
Instead, we ask: if we give the same set of datapoints to two optimizers, in what settings are the resulting weight updates exactly equivalent?
Third, our approach gives batch size invariance under much weaker assumptions.
In particular, we do not need the gradient variance to dominate the expectation, we only need the updates to be sufficiently small (which is required by
Malladi et al. 2022
anyway).

It turns out that this requirement for the updates to be sufficiently small is also encountered in SGD, where it is understood as a critical batch size
(
Ma et al. 2018
)
.
Notably, this work assumes takes
\eta\propto B
so a critical batch size is intimately related to a critical learning rate.

Shallue et al. 2019
introduced the term “perfect” scaling for the region in which training is batch size invariant and studied the effect of e.g. architectures on the critical batch size.
The dependence of the critical batch size on dataset
(
Golmant et al. 2018
)
, gradient noise scale
(
McCandlish et al. 2018
)
and curvature
Zhang et al. 2019
.
We observe a similar threshold for batch size invariant Adam (Fig.
2
), though in our plots it is most straightforwardly interpreted as a critical learning rate.

The notion of computing the squared gradients for
micro-
batches was also proposed in
Zhang et al. 2023
, albeit in a very different context.
In particular, they were trying to do Adam on large
mini-
batches in memory constrained, single-node settings.
They therefore considered splitting the
mini-
batch into
micro-
batches, and aggregating gradients across the
micro-
batch.
They found that they were able to reduce memory consumption by directly aggregating squared
micro-
batch gradients, rather than by aggregating gradients and squaring at the end.
However, given common results that smaller batches tend to work better
(
Keskar et al. 2016
, e.g.)
, it is not clear why you would do aggregate across
micro-
batches on a single node: why not just apply Adam updates to the
micro-
batches?
This is confirmed by our results, which show that as updates get too large, and invariance breaks down and smaller batches converge faster (Fig.
2
).

Importantly,
Zhang et al. 2023
is entirely focused on reducing memory consumption: they do not consider batch size invariance, and such, their proposed algorithm is not batch size invariant.
In particular, they consider reducing memory consumption by taking a fixed-size
mini-
batch and splitting it into an increasingly large number of
micro-
batches.
Of course, as we have more
micro-
batches, each
micro-
batch gets smaller, so the variance of the
micro-
batch gradient estimate gets larger, so
\hat{v}
will increase and the effective learning rate will fall.
Our contribution is therefore entirely orthogonal: establishing that a particular variant of Adam with a particular choice of hyperparameter scaling is invariant to batch size for small enough updates.

That said, the key insight in
Zhang et al. 2023
is that you can avoid the memory cost of explicitly accumulating the average gradient by immediately incorporating the
micro-
batch gradients into
m_{t}
and the
micro-
batch square gradients into
v_{t}
(which is possible in Alg. (
3
) as the
m_{t}
and
v_{t}
updates are just sums over
micro-
batches).
While
Zhang et al. 2023
did not consider the multi-node setting, the key insight holds true in multi-node settings for the server that aggregates gradient updates.
In particular, this server again does not need to retain a gradient accumulator, but can just accumulate directly into
m_{t}
and
v_{t}
.
This raises the prospect of a potential memory saving of 25% at the server (in standard Adam, we need to accumulate four quantities for each parameter,
g^{\prime}_{t}
,
m_{t}
,
v_{t}
and
w_{t}
, while if you use squared
micro-
batch gradients, you only need
m_{t}
,
v_{t}
and
w_{t}
).
That raises the tantalising prospect that the batch size invariant Adam considered here is also more efficient in practice in large-scale distributed settings.

We frame our proposal in terms of
micro-
batches computed on separate workers, as that makes it easy to compute the squared gradient for the
micro-
batch on a worker.
However, our proposal can also be used in the single-worker setting.
For instance, it would be possible to treat each datapoint as a
micro-
batch, and compute the sum of squared gradients for individual datapoints using techniques from

Dangel et al. 2020
(which critically avoid needing to materialise the full tensor of individual datapoint gradients, which has an impractically large size of
B\times
number of parameters).

3
Methods

To set the context, we introduce the concept of
micro-
batch. In particular, we assume a
mini-
batch of
B
samples can be equally split into
\kappa

micro-
batches, and then we aggregate
\kappa
sets of gradient computed at
different
microbaches under the
same
parameter values for the actual parameter updates.
The idea of
micro-
batch is widely adopted in the training of deep networks, e.g.
micro-
batching facilitates larger
mini-
batch sizes under constrained memory by enabling gradient accumulation and supports data parallelism through distributing
micro-
batches across workers for parallel gradient computation.
However, our focus diverges from these applications; we leverage
micro-
batching to explore how optimization algorithms are influenced by
mini-
batch size, i.e. value of
\kappa
. Notice that, we assume a
fixed

micro-
batch size through the analysis and we will use batch size to denote the
micro-
batch
size in later paragraphs.

We begin by writing down standard Adam updates applied to individual
micro-
batches (denoted
t
) (Alg.
1
).
Here,
g_{t}(w)
is the gradient of the objective for the
t
th
micro-
batch.
Note that we have written Adam here in a slightly unusual parameterization, using,

\displaystyle\beta_{1}(\gamma_{1})

\displaystyle=1-\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle\beta_{2}(\gamma_{2})

\displaystyle=1-\gamma_{2}

(2)

as the scalings we derive later on are much more simply expressed in terms of
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
than in terms of
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
.
Note that we can recover Adam from AdamW by setting
\lambda=0
, and incorporating any AdamW weight decay into the objective.

Standard Adam updates (Alg.
2
) are performed on a
mini-
batch, formed by merging
\kappa

micro-
batches.
Importantly, to write down these merged, updates we continue to take
t
to be
micro-
batches
, rather than steps or
mini-
batches.
Of course, each update of standard Adam consumes a single
mini-
batch consisting of
\kappa

micro-
batches.
Thus, if we start at
w_{t-\kappa}
then a single step of standard Adam will consume
\kappa

micro-
batches, taking us to
w_{t}
.
Another step consumes another
\kappa

micro-
batches, taking us to
w_{t+\kappa}
.
Thus, while
t
indexes the number of
micro-
batches,
t/\kappa
indexes optimizer steps.
This unusual convention has the key benefit that the time indices in standard Adam remains aligned with the time indices in
Micro
Adam (Alg.
1
), which will be important as we will be assessing equivalence between
Micro
Adam and standard Adam or batch size invariant Adam.
However, this notation does have the disadvantage that it modifies slightly the form of the debiasing equations (see the powers of
\text{steps}=t/\kappa
in the expressions for
\hat{m}
and
\hat{v}
in Alg. (
2
) and Alg. (
3
)).

Batch size dependence in standard Adam.

A standard Adam update (Alg.
2
) applies to a
mini-
batch
formed by merging
\kappa

micro-
batches.
However, the arguments given in the introduction indicate that standard Adam is not batch size invariant.
By “batch size invariant”, we mean an algorithm for which, in some sensible limit, a single update on a
mini-
batch formed of
\kappa

micro-
batches is equivalent to
\kappa
update steps of
Micro
Adam on the underlying
micro-
batches (Alg.
1
).
To see that standard Adam (Alg.
2
) is not batch size invariant, we consider the
\hat{v}_{t}
terms in the various algorithms.
For
Micro
Adam (Alg.
1
),
\hat{v}_{t}
is an exponential moving average of the raw second moment of the
micro-
batch
gradients,

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}\left[g_{t}^{2}(w_{t-1})\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{E}\left[g\right]^{2}+\operatorname{Var}\left[g\right].

(3)

where
\operatorname{E}\left[g\right]
and
\operatorname{Var}\left[g\right]
is the mean and variance of
micro-
batch gradients.
In contrast, for standard Adam (Alg.
2
),
\hat{v}_{t}
is an exponential moving average of the raw second moment of the
minibatch
gradients

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}\left[(\tfrac{1}{\kappa}{\textstyle\sum}_{k=1}^{\kappa}g_{t-k+\kappa}(w_{t-\kappa}))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{E}\left[g\right]^{2}+\tfrac{1}{\kappa}\operatorname{Var}\left[g\right].

(4)

Critically, as we have more
micro-
batches,
\kappa
, in the
mini-
batch, we get a better estimate of the gradient and hence the variance term falls.
And as the effective learning rate,
\eta/(\sqrt{\hat{v}_{t}}+\epsilon)
depends on
\hat{v}_{t}
, we can see that the effective learning rate depends unavoidably on the batch size.

Batch size invariant Adam.

We therefore consider a batch size invariant version of Adam (Alg.
3
) in which the red term is the average over squared
micro-
batch gradients (as opposed to standard Adam, where the red term is the square of average
micro-
batch gradients).
Thus, in batch size invariant Adam (Alg.
3
), just like in
Micro
Adam (Alg.
1
),
\hat{v}
estimates the expected raw second moment of the fixed-size
micro-
batch gradients,

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}\left[\tfrac{1}{\kappa}{\textstyle\sum}_{k=1}^{\kappa}g_{t-k+\kappa}^{2}(w_{t-\kappa})\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{E}\left[g\right]^{2}+\operatorname{Var}\left[g\right]

(5)

This expectation thus does not depend on
\kappa
, avoiding a key source of batch size dependence.

Theorem statements

While we have clearly eliminated one source of batch size dependence, we have yet to prove that Alg. (
3

Conclusions

We developed a batch size invariant Adam for large-scale distributed settings, which computes the expectation of the squared
microbatch
gradients, rather than the squared
minibatch
gradients used by standard Adam.
We proved batch size invariance under mild conditions (just sufficiently small updates), without needing to go to stochastic differential equations.
Furthermore, insights from
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
suggest that this approach may even be more efficient than standard Adam in practical, large-scale settings.

\displaystyle=w_{t-\kappa},

(15a)

\displaystyle=m_{t-\kappa}

\displaystyle m^{\prime}_{t}(\delta=0)

Appendix B
Warmup by proving a similar result for SGD

To prove the equivalance of the merged and unmerged optimizers, it is easiest to first consider SGD.
A single SGD step can be written,

\displaystyle g_{t}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{grad}_{t}(w_{t-1})

(16a)

\displaystyle w_{t}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sgd}(w_{t-1},g_{t},\eta)

(16b)

where
\operatorname{grad}_{t}
is a function that computes the gradients for the
t
th microbatch, and
\operatorname{sgd}
is a function that applies a single SGD update,

\displaystyle\text{grad}_{t}(w)

\displaystyle=\nabla\mathcal{L}_{t}(w_{t-1}),

(17a)

\displaystyle\text{sgd}(w,g,\eta)

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda\eta)w-\eta g.

(17b)

We have written the updates in this slightly unusual form to make it possible to carefully distinguish partial and total derivatives, which turns out to be important in our setting to apply the chain rule correctly.
Specifically, the chain rule applied to
g_{t}
and
w_{t}
gives,

\displaystyle\frac{dg_{t}}{d\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial g_{t}}{\partial w_{t-1}}\frac{dw_{t-1}}{d\eta},

(18a)

\displaystyle\frac{dw_{t}}{d\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial w_{t}}{\partial w_{t-1}}\frac{dw_{t-1}}{d\eta}+\frac{\partial w_{t}}{\partial g_{t}}\frac{dg_{t}}{d\eta}+\frac{\partial w_{t}}{\partial\eta}.

(18b)

The partial derivatives here are formally defined as,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g_{t}}{\partial w_{t-1}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{\operatorname{grad}_{t}(w_{t-1}+h)-\operatorname{grad}_{t}(w_{t-1})}{h}

(19a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w_{t}}{\partial w_{t-1}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{\operatorname{sgd}(w_{t-1}+h,g_{t},\eta)-\operatorname{sgd}(w_{t-1},g_{t},\eta)}{h}

(19b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w_{t}}{\partial g_{t}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{\operatorname{sgd}(w_{t-1},g_{t}+h,\eta)-\operatorname{sgd}(w_{t-1},g_{t},\eta)}{h}

(19c)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w_{t}}{\partial\eta}

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{\operatorname{sgd}(w_{t-1},g_{t},\eta+h)-\operatorname{sgd}(w_{t-1},g_{t},\eta)}{h}

(19d)

i.e. the partial derivatives compute the change in the output of the
function
(either
\operatorname{grad}_{t}
or
\operatorname{sgd}
) as one of the arguments to those functions changes, while all the other arguments are held fixed.
In contrast, the total derivatives (
d/d\eta
) represent the total change through the whole “compute graph”, i.e. the total change in
w_{t}
and
g_{t}
if we start from a fixed
w_{t-\kappa}
and change
\eta
.

To compute the total derivative for
w_{t}
, we use the following partial derivatives,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w_{t}}{\partial w_{t-1}}

\displaystyle=1-\lambda\eta

(20a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w_{t}}{\partial g_{t}}

\displaystyle=-\eta

(20b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w_{t}}{\partial\eta}

\displaystyle=-g_{t}-\lambda w_{t-1}.

(20c)

Substituting these partial derivatives into the total derivative,

\displaystyle\frac{dw_{t}}{d\eta}

\displaystyle=(1-\eta\lambda)\frac{dw_{t-1}}{d\eta}-\eta\frac{dg_{t}}{d\eta}-g_{t}-\lambda w_{t-1}.

(21)

Evaluating at
\eta=0
,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dw_{t}}{d\eta}\right\rvert_{\eta=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{dw_{t-1}}{d\eta}\right\rvert_{\eta=0}-g_{t}-\lambda w_{t-1},

(22)

we get a simple recursive expression.

Then, if we fix
w_{t-\kappa}
, and consider computing
w_{t}
through
\kappa
steps of gradient descent, we get,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dw_{t}}{d\eta}\right\rvert_{\eta=0}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}\left(g_{t-\kappa+k}+\lambda w_{t-\kappa+k-1}\right),

(23)

We can simplify this expression by noting that at
\eta=0
, the weights at all timesteps are equal,
w_{t-1}=w_{t-2}=\dotsm=w_{t-k}
.
This has important implications for the gradient terms.
Specifically,
g_{t}
is the gradient for the
t
th datapoint/microbatch, evaluated using the model parameters at timestep
t-1
, i.e.
w_{t-1}
.
But if the weights do not change, the average of these gradients can be computed based on the initial set of weights (i.e. based on
w_{t-\kappa}
),

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\kappa}{\textstyle\sum}_{k=1}^{\kappa}g_{t-k+\kappa}\Bigr\rvert_{\eta=0}=\tfrac{1}{\kappa}{\textstyle\sum}_{k=1}^{\kappa}\operatorname{grad}_{t-k+\kappa}(w_{t-k+\kappa-1})\Bigr\rvert_{\eta=0}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\kappa}{\textstyle\sum}_{k=1}^{\kappa}\operatorname{grad}_{t-k+\kappa}(w_{t-\kappa})=g^{\prime}_{t}.

(24)

Here,
g^{\prime}_{t}
is this average gradient for data across all
\kappa
steps, but evaluated using the initial weights,
w_{t-\kappa}
.
Thus the gradient resulting from the
\kappa
updates is,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dw_{t}}{d\eta}\right\rvert_{\eta=0}

\displaystyle=-\kappa\lambda w_{t-\kappa}-\kappa g^{\prime}_{t}.

(25)

This is starting to resemble a single step of a merged optimizer which performs a single step using a minibatch consisting of the
\kappa
minibatches.

However, to be sure of the connection to a single step of a merged optimizer, we need to formally define this optimizer.

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{t}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{grad}^{\prime}_{t}(w_{t-\kappa})

(26a)

\displaystyle w^{\prime}_{t}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sgd}(w_{t-\kappa},g^{\prime}_{t},\eta^{\prime})

(26b)

where
\operatorname{grad}^{\prime}_{t}
is a function that computes the gradients for the
t
th
minibatch
,

\displaystyle\text{grad}^{\prime}_{t}(w)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\kappa}{\textstyle\sum}_{k=1}^{\kappa}\text{grad}_{t-\kappa+k}(w)

(27)

Now, we compute the gradient of
w^{\prime}_{t}
wrt
\eta
, taking
\eta^{\prime}=\kappa\eta
,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dw^{\prime}_{t}}{d\eta}\right\rvert_{\eta=0}

\displaystyle=-\kappa\lambda w_{t-\kappa}-\kappa g^{\prime}_{t}.

(28)

This is exactly equal to the gradient wrt
\eta
of the multi-step gradient in Eq. (
25
), which establishes our result.

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem
1

In Appendix
A
, we established that the limits in Theorem
1
can be understood as derivatives (Eq.
14
).

However, computing gradients through multiple Adam updates is not trivial.
To do so correctly, we need to be careful to distinguish partial and total derivatives.
To that end, we write
Micro
Adam updates (Alg.
1
) (i.e. standard Adam applied to microbatches) in an unusual form,

\displaystyle g_{t}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{grad}_{t}(w_{t-1})

(29a)

\displaystyle m_{t}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{ema}_{1}(m_{t-1},g_{t},\delta)

(29b)

\displaystyle v_{t}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{ema}_{2}(v_{t-1},g_{t},\delta)

(29c)

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{norm\_ema}_{t}(m_{t},v_{t})

(29d)

\displaystyle w_{t}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{update}(w_{t-1},u_{t},\delta)

(29e)

where,

\displaystyle\operatorname{ema}_{1}(m,g,\delta)

\displaystyle=(1-\delta\bar{\gamma}_{1})m+\delta\bar{\gamma}_{1}g

(30a)

\displaystyle\operatorname{ema}_{2}(v,g,\delta)

\displaystyle=(1-\delta\bar{\gamma}_{2})v+\delta\bar{\gamma}_{2}g^{2}

(30b)

\displaystyle\operatorname{update}(w,u,\delta)

\displaystyle=(1-\delta\bar{\eta}\lambda)w-\delta\bar{\eta}u.

(30c)

\displaystyle\operatorname{norm\_ema}_{t}(m,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{m}_{t}(m)}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_{t}(v)}+\epsilon}

(30d)

where,

\displaystyle\hat{m}_{t}(m)

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{1-\beta_{1}^{t}}=\frac{m}{1-(1-\delta\bar{\gamma}_{1})^{t}}

(30e)

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{t}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{v}{1-\beta_{2}^{t}}=\frac{v}{1-(1-\delta\bar{\gamma}_{2})^{t}}

(30f)

We are interested in the three state variables,
m_{t}
,
v_{t}
and
w_{t}
, that propagate across timesteps.
Using the chain rule, the gradients for these variables can be written,

\displaystyle\frac{dm_{t}}{d\delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial m_{t}}{\partial g_{t}}\frac{dg_{t}}{d\delta}+\frac{\partial m_{t}}{\partial m_{t-1}}\frac{dm_{t-1}}{d\delta}+\frac{\partial m_{t}}{\partial\delta}

(31a)

\displaystyle\frac{dv_{t}}{d\delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial v_{t}}{\partial g_{t}}\frac{dg_{t}}{d\delta}+\frac{\partial v_{t}}{\partial v_{t-1}}\frac{dv_{t-1}}{d\delta}+\frac{\partial v_{t}}{\partial\delta}

(31b)

\displaystyle\frac{dw_{t}}{d\delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial w_{t}}{\partial w_{t-1}}\frac{dw_{t-1}}{d\delta}+\frac{\partial w_{t}}{\partial u_{t}}\frac{du_{t}}{d\delta}+\frac{\partial w_{t}}{\partial\delta}

(31c)

We begin by considering the first state variable,
m_{t}
.
We substitute the partial derivatives,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial m_{t}}{\partial g_{t}}

\displaystyle=\delta\bar{\gamma}_{1}

(32a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial m_{t}}{\partial m_{t-1}}

\displaystyle=1-\delta\bar{\gamma}_{1}

(32b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial m_{t}}{\partial\delta}

\displaystyle=\bar{\gamma}_{1}(g_{t}-m_{t-1}),

(32c)

into Eq. (
31a
),

\displaystyle\frac{dm_{t}}{d\delta}

\displaystyle=\delta\bar{\gamma}_{1}\frac{dg_{t}}{d\delta}+(1-\delta\bar{\gamma}_{1})\frac{dm_{t-1}}{d\delta}+\bar{\gamma}_{1}(g_{t}-m_{t-1}).

(33)

Now, we evaluate at
\delta=0
,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dm_{t}}{d\delta}\right\rvert_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{dm_{t-1}}{d\delta}\right\rvert_{\delta=0}+\bar{\gamma}_{1}(g_{t}-m_{t-1}),

(34)

which gives a simple, recursive result.

We follow almost exactly the same procedure for
v_{t}
.
We substitute the partial derivatives,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v_{t}}{\partial g_{t}}

\displaystyle=2\delta\bar{\gamma}_{2}g_{t},

(35a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v_{t}}{\partial v_{t-1}}

\displaystyle=1-\delta\bar{\gamma}_{2},

(35b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v_{t}}{\partial\delta}

\displaystyle=\bar{\gamma}_{2}(g_{t}^{2}-v_{t-1}).

(35c)

into Eq. (
31b
),

\displaystyle\frac{dv_{t}}{d\delta}

\displaystyle=2\delta\bar{\gamma}_{2}g_{t}\frac{dg_{t}}{d\delta}+(1-\delta\bar{\gamma}_{2})\frac{dv_{t-1}}{d\delta}+\bar{\gamma}_{2}(g_{t}^{2}-v_{t-1}).

(36)

Now, we evaluate at
\delta=0
,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dv_{t}}{d\delta}\right\rvert_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{dv_{t-1}}{d\delta}\right\rvert_{\delta=0}+\bar{\gamma}_{2}(g_{t}^{2}-v_{t-1}),

(37)

which gives a simple, recursive result.

Finally, we follow a similar process for
w_{t}
. We substitute the partial derivatives,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w_{t}}{\partial u_{t}}

\displaystyle=\delta\bar{\eta}

(38a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w_{t}}{\partial w_{t-1}}

\displaystyle=1-\delta\bar{\eta}\lambda

(38b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w_{t}}{\partial\delta}

\displaystyle=-\bar{\eta}(u_{t}+\lambda w_{t-1}).

(38c)

into Eq. (
31c
),

\displaystyle\frac{dw_{t}}{d\delta}

\displaystyle=\delta\bar{\eta}\frac{du_{t}}{d\delta}+(1-\delta\bar{\eta}\lambda)\frac{dw_{t-1}}{d\delta}-\bar{\eta}(u_{t}+\lambda w_{t-1}).

(39)

Now, we evaluate at
\delta=0
,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dw_{t}}{d\delta}\right\rvert_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{dw_{t-1}}{d\delta}\right\rvert_{\delta=0}-\bar{\eta}(u_{t}+\lambda w_{t-1}).

(40)

which gives a simple, recursive result.

Now, we consider fixing
m_{t-\kappa}
,
v_{t-\kappa}
, and
w_{t-\kappa}
and performing
\kappa

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dm_{t}}{d\delta}\right\rvert_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=\bar{\gamma}_{1}\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}\left(g_{t-\kappa+k}-m_{t-\kappa+k-1}\right)

(41a)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dv_{t}}{d\delta}\right\rvert_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=\bar{\gamma}_{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}\left(g_{t-\kappa+k}^{2}-v_{t-\kappa+k-1}\right)

(41b)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dw_{t}}{d\delta}\right\rvert_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=-\bar{\eta}\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}\left(u_{t-\kappa+k}+\lambda w_{t-\kappa+k-1}\right).

(41c)

Remember that as
\delta\rightarrow 0
all the state variables do not change, and are equal to their initial values,

\displaystyle m_{t-\kappa}

\displaystyle=m_{t-\kappa+1}=\dotsm=m_{t-1}=m_{t}

(42a)

\displaystyle\phantom{m}\mathllap{v}_{t-\kappa}

\displaystyle=\phantom{m}\mathllap{v}_{t-\kappa+1}=\dotsm=\phantom{m}\mathllap{v}_{t-1}=\phantom{m}\mathllap{v}_{t}

(42b)

\displaystyle\phantom{m}\mathllap{w}_{t-\kappa}

\displaystyle=\phantom{m}\mathllap{w}_{t-\kappa+1}=\dotsm=\phantom{m}\mathllap{w}_{t-1}=\phantom{m}\mathllap{w}_{t}.

(42c)

Using these constant values, along with Eq. (
24
),

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dm_{t}}{d\delta}\right\rvert_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=-\kappa\bar{\gamma}_{1}m_{t-\kappa}+\bar{\gamma}_{1}{\textstyle\sum}_{k=1}^{\kappa}g_{t-\kappa+k}

(43a)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dv_{t}}{d\delta}\right\rvert_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=-\kappa\bar{\gamma}_{2}v_{t-\kappa}+\bar{\gamma}_{2}{\textstyle\sum}_{k=1}^{\kappa}g_{t-\kappa+k}^{2}

(43b)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dw_{t}}{d\delta}\right\rvert_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=-\bar{\eta}({\textstyle\sum}_{k=1}^{\kappa}u_{t-\kappa+k}+\kappa\lambda w_{t-\kappa}).

(43c)

terms, which requires us to consider the debiasing steps.
Debiasing actually stops doing anything for fixed
t
, in the limit as
\delta\rightarrow 0
, (i.e.
\hat{m}_{t}(m)=m
).
To avoid this trivial equivalence, we therefore consider a limit in which
t
increases as
\delta
falls,

\displaystyle t=\bar{t}/\delta,

(44)

where
\bar{t}
is fixed.
That makes sense, because
\delta
decreases, all the updates become smaller, so we may want to perform more iterations.
We are going to use the standard limit,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}(1+\tfrac{a}{x})^{bx}

\displaystyle=e^{ab}.

(45)

In particular, considering the
(1-\bar{\gamma}_{1}\delta)^{t}

), taking

, and identifying

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}(1-\bar{\gamma}_{1}\delta)^{t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}(1-\tfrac{\bar{\gamma}_{1}}{x})^{\bar{t}x}=e^{-\bar{\gamma}_{1}\bar{t}}.

(46)

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}(1-\bar{\gamma}_{2}\delta)^{t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}(1-\tfrac{\bar{\gamma}_{2}}{x})^{\bar{t}x}=e^{-\bar{\gamma}_{2}\bar{t}}.

(47)

(where the second equation is exactly the same thing, but for

In this limit, the debiasing steps can be written,

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\hat{m}_{t}(m)

(48)

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\hat{v}_{t}(v)

\displaystyle=e^{-\bar{\gamma}_{2}\bar{t}}v.

(49)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dm_{t}}{d\delta}\right\rvert_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=-\kappa\bar{\gamma}_{1}m_{t-\kappa}+\bar{\gamma}_{1}{\textstyle\sum}_{k=1}^{\kappa}g_{t-\kappa+k}

(50a)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dv_{t}}{d\delta}\right\rvert_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=-\kappa\bar{\gamma}_{2}v_{t-\kappa}+\bar{\gamma}_{2}{\textstyle\sum}_{k=1}^{\kappa}g_{t-\kappa+k}^{2}

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dw_{t}}{d\delta}\right\rvert_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=-\kappa\bar{\eta}\left(\frac{u_{t-\kappa}e^{-\bar{\gamma}_{1}\bar{t}}}{\sqrt{v_{t-\kappa}e^{-\bar{\gamma}_{2}\bar{t}}}+\epsilon}+\lambda w_{t-\kappa}\right).

(50c)

Overall, these updates resembles a single step of the merged, batch size invariant Adam.

But to be sure, we write batch size invariant Adam updates in Alg. (

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{t}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{grad}^{\prime}_{t}(w_{t-\kappa})

(51a)

\displaystyle s^{\prime}_{t}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{gradsq}^{\prime}_{t}(w_{t-\kappa})

(51b)

\displaystyle m_{t}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{ema}_{1}^{\prime}(m_{t-\kappa},g^{\prime}_{t},\delta)

(51c)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{ema}_{2}^{\prime}(v_{t-\kappa},s^{\prime}_{t},\delta)

(51d)

(51e)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{update}^{\prime}(w^{\prime}_{t-1},u^{\prime}_{t},\delta)

(51f)

where,

\displaystyle\operatorname{grad}^{\prime}_{t}(w_{t-\kappa})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\kappa}{\textstyle\sum}_{k=1}^{\kappa}\operatorname{grad}_{t-k+\kappa}(w_{t-\kappa}),

(52a)

(52b)

\displaystyle\operatorname{ema}_{1}^{\prime}(m,g,\delta)

\displaystyle=(1-\delta\kappa\bar{\gamma}_{1})m+\delta\kappa\bar{\gamma}_{1}g

(52c)

\displaystyle\operatorname{ema}_{2}^{\prime}(\phantom{m}\mathllap{v},s,\delta)

\displaystyle=(1-\delta\kappa\bar{\gamma}_{2})\phantom{m}\mathllap{v}+\delta\kappa\bar{\gamma}_{2}s

(52d)

\displaystyle\operatorname{update}^{\prime}(w,u,\delta)

\displaystyle=(1-\delta\kappa\bar{\eta}\lambda)w-\delta\kappa\bar{\eta}u.

(52e)

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{m}^{\prime}_{t/\kappa}(m)}{\sqrt{\hat{v}^{\prime}_{t/\kappa}(v)}+\epsilon}

(52f)

where,

\displaystyle\hat{m}^{\prime}_{t/\kappa}(m)

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{1-(1-\gamma_{1}^{\prime})^{t/\kappa}}

(52g)

\displaystyle\hat{v}^{\prime}_{t/\kappa}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{v}{1-(1-\gamma_{2}^{\prime})^{t/\kappa}}

(52h)

Now, the gradient of the state variables for the merged, batch size invariant Adam updates is,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dm_{t}^{\prime}}{d\delta}\right\rvert_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=\kappa\bar{\gamma}_{1}m_{t-\kappa}+\bar{\gamma}_{1}{\textstyle\sum}_{k=1}^{\kappa}g_{t-k+\kappa}

(53a)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dv_{t}^{\prime}}{d\delta}\right\rvert_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=\kappa\bar{\gamma}_{2}v_{t-\kappa}+\bar{\gamma}_{2}{\textstyle\sum}_{k=1}^{\kappa}g_{t-k+\kappa}^{2}

(53b)

\displaystyle=-\kappa\bar{\eta}(u_{t}+\lambda w_{t-\kappa}).

(53c)

41
), we are almost done.

\kappa u_{t}
, again in a limit in which
t
increases as
\delta
decreases,
t=\bar{t}/\delta

(1-\gamma_{1}^{\prime})^{t/\kappa}

Thus, the debiasing steps can be written,
