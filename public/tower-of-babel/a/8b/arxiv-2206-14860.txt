Feynman diagrams in terms of on-shell propagators
F. T. Brandt, J. Frenkel, D. G. C. McKeon

Abstract
It is shown that the usual expression for a Feynman diagram in terms of the Feynman propagator Δ F ​ ( x − y ) \Delta_{F}(x-y) can be replaced by an equivalent expression involving the positive-energy on-shell propagator Δ + ​ ( x − y ) \Delta^{+}(x-y) , supplemented by appropriate functions associated with time-ordering. When this alternate way of expressing a Feynman diagram is Fourier transformed into momentum space, the momentum associated with each function Δ + ​ ( x − y ) \Delta^{+}(x-y) is on-shell, and is only conserved at each vertex if an energy is attributed to the contributions of the time-ordering functions. The resulting expression is analogous to what Kadyshevsky had obtained by deriving an alternate expansion for the S S –matrix. A detailed explanation of how this alternate expansion is derived is given, and it is shown how it provides a straightforward way of determining the imaginary part of a Feynman diagram, which makes it useful when using unitarity methods for computing a Feynman diagram. By considering a number of specific Feynman diagrams in self-interacting scalar models and in QED, we show how this alternate approach can be related to the old perturbation theory and can simplify direct calculations of Feynman diagrams.

\omega_{k+q}=\sqrt{k^{2}+q^{2}+2kqx}
, where
x
is the cosine of the angle between
\vec{k}
and
\vec{q}
. Thus, we must evaluate the integral over this angle which involves

\int_{-1}^{1}dx\delta[k+q+\sqrt{k^{2}+q^{2}+2kqx}-p^{0}]={\theta(p^{0}-k-q)}\frac{p^{0}-k-q}{kq},

(36)

where we have used the relation (
25
), together with the fact that the argument of the
\delta
-function vanishes at the point

x_{0}=1+\frac{{p^{0}}^{2}-2p^{0}(k+q)}{2kq}.

(37)

Two-loop sunrise self-energy diagrams with a quartic interaction, corresponding
to the integral in Eq. (
33
).

It turns out that the requirement that
-1<x_{0}<1
, leads to the condition
k+q+|k-q|<p^{0}<2(k+q)
.
Using these relations and performing the remaining angular integrations in Eq. (
35
), we obtain

\Im\Pi(p)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{3!}\frac{\pi}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\theta(p^{0}-k-q-|k-q|)\theta(2(k+q)-p^{0}).

(38)

These integrals may be evaluated by using the change of variables
k+q=Q_{+}
and
k-q=Q_{-}
. Then, a straightforward integration over the variables
Q_{+}
and
Q_{-}
leads to the following result

\Im\Pi(p)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{3!}\frac{\pi}{(4\pi)^{4}}\frac{1}{2}p^{2},

(39)

where we have set
{(p^{0})}^{2}\rightarrow p^{2}
by Lorentz invariance.

The above result was obtained in four space-time dimensions. In
D
-dimensions, the corresponding result can be written in the form

\Im\Pi(p)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{3!}\frac{\pi}{(4\pi)^{D}}\frac{\Gamma^{3}(D/2-1)}{\Gamma(3D/2-3)}\frac{1}{\Gamma(D-2)}(p^{2})^{D-3},

(40)

which reduces to Eq. (
39
) for
D=4
.

With the help of this result for the imaginary part of the sunrise graph, one can write a dispersion relation for the self-energy
\Pi(p)

\Pi(p)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{4m^{2}}^{\infty}ds\frac{\Im\Pi(s)}{s-p^{2}-i\epsilon}.

(41)

Using the expression (
40
) and rescaling
s=p^{2}x
, we obtain in the massless case that

\Pi(p)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{3!}\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{D}}\frac{\Gamma^{3}(D/2-1)}{\Gamma(3D/2-3)\Gamma(D-2)}(-p^{2})^{D-3}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\frac{x^{D-3}}{x-1-i\epsilon}.

(42)

This integral can be performed using the formula 3.194 in
grads
, which leads to the following result

\Pi(p)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{3!}\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{D}}\frac{\Gamma(3-D)\Gamma^{3}(D/2-1)}{\Gamma(3D/2-3)}(-p^{2})^{D-3},

(43)

where
-p^{2}\rightarrow-(1+i\epsilon)p^{2}
. The above expression agrees with that obtained in the massless case by using conventional Feynman propagators, as expected (see for example Eq. (C4) of
Brandt:2021nseBrandt:2021nev
).
But the present approach is more convenient for clarifying the way that the unitarity of the
S
–matrix arises from the intermediate states. The corresponding result obtained for a non-zero mass is more involved and can be expressed in terms of elliptic integrals
Delbourgo:2003ziDavydychev:2003cw
.

V
Spinor fields

The use of on-shell propagators is also possible when dealing with spinor fields. The Feynman propagator, much like that for a scalar field in Eq. (
1
) is given by

\displaystyle S_{F}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta(x^{0})S^{+}(x)+\theta(-x^{0})S^{-}(x),

(44)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(i\gamma\cdot\partial+m\right)\Delta_{F}(x),

where

\displaystyle S^{\pm}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(i\gamma\cdot\partial+m\right)\Delta^{\pm}(x)

(45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}i}\frac{e^{-i(\pm\omega_{k}x^{0}-\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x})}}{2\omega_{k}}(\pm\omega_{k}\gamma^{0}-\vec{k}\cdot\vec{\gamma}+m).

(Only if
m=0
does
S^{\pm}(x)=-S^{\mp}(-x)
.)

The vacuum polarization is given by

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mu\nu}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{4}x_{1}d^{4}x_{2}D_{F\;\mu\lambda}(y_{1}-x_{1})D_{F\;\nu\sigma}(y_{2}-x_{2})

(46)

\displaystyle(-ie)^{2}\,{\rm Tr\,}\left[\gamma^{\lambda}S_{F}(x_{1}-x_{2})\gamma^{\sigma}S_{F}(x_{2}-x_{1})\right],

where

D_{F\;\lambda\sigma}(x)=-i\eta_{\lambda\sigma}\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{e^{-ip\cdot x}}{p^{2}+i\epsilon}.

(47)

Using Eqs. (
7
) we find that

\gamma^{\mu}S_{F}(x)\gamma^{\nu}S_{F}(-x)=\theta(x^{0})\,\gamma^{\mu}S^{+}(x)\gamma^{\nu}S^{-}(-x)+\theta(-x^{0})\,\gamma^{\mu}S^{-}(x)\gamma^{\nu}S^{+}(-x).

(48)

Following the same steps that lead from Eq. (
8
) to (
27
), we find that

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mu\nu}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-ie^{2}}{4}\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{e^{-ip\cdot(y_{1}-y_{2})}}{(p^{2}+i\epsilon)^{2}}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\omega_{p+k}\omega_{k}}

(49)

\displaystyle{\rm Tr\,}\left[\frac{\gamma_{\mu}(\omega_{k}\gamma^{0}+\vec{k}\cdot\vec{\gamma}+m)\gamma_{\nu}(-\omega_{p+k}\gamma^{0}+(\vec{p}+\vec{k})\cdot\vec{\gamma}+m)}{-p^{0}+\omega_{k}+\omega_{p+k}-i\epsilon}\right.

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;+\left.\frac{\gamma_{\mu}(-\omega_{k}\gamma^{0}+\vec{k}\cdot\vec{\gamma}+m)\gamma_{\nu}(\omega_{p+k}\gamma^{0}+(\vec{p}+\vec{k})\cdot\vec{\gamma}+m)}{p^{0}+\omega_{k}+\omega_{p+k}-i\epsilon}\right].

Using the well known trace formulae, it follows that the vacuum polarization may be written in momentum space as

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mu\nu}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}\omega_{p+k}}\left(\frac{1}{p^{0}+\omega_{k}+\omega_{p+k}-i\epsilon}+\frac{1}{-p^{0}+\omega_{k}+\omega_{p+k}-i\epsilon}\right)

(50)

\displaystyle\left[m^{2}\eta_{\mu\nu}-\omega_{k}\omega_{p+k}\left(2\eta_{\mu 0}\eta_{\nu 0}-\eta_{\mu\nu}\right)+k^{i}(p+k)^{j}\left(\eta_{\mu i}\eta_{\nu j}+\eta_{\mu j}\eta_{\nu i}-\eta_{\mu\nu}\eta_{ij}\right)\right].

This expression may be conveniently evaluated in the reference frame in which
p^{\mu}=(p^{0},\vec{0})
. In this frame, it is easy to verify that
\Pi_{\mu\nu}
satisfies the Ward identity
p^{\mu}\Pi_{\mu\nu}(p)=0
at the integrand level (in any dimensions). Since
p^{\mu}\Pi_{\mu\nu}(p)
is a four-vector, this must hold in any frame so that
\Pi_{\mu\nu}
should have the transverse form

\Pi_{\mu\nu}(p)=(\eta_{\mu\nu}p^{2}-p_{\mu}p_{\nu})\Pi(p^{2}).

(51)

It is interesting to note that this approach naturally preserves gauge invariance.
The imaginary part of the function
\Pi(p^{2})
may be easily evaluated from Eq. (
50
) to give, in the above reference frame where
p^{2}={(p^{0})}^{2}
,

\Im\Pi(p^{2})=\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi}\frac{1}{3}\left(1+\frac{2m^{2}}{p^{2}}\right)\sqrt{1-\frac{4m^{2}}{p^{2}}}\theta(p^{2}-4m^{2}).

(52)

Using this expression, one can write for
\Pi(p^{2})
a dispersion relation of the form given in Eq. (
41
). The integration can be performed and leads to the result

\Pi(p^{2})-\Pi(0)=-\frac{e^{2}}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}dx\,x(1-x)\ln\left[1-\frac{p^{2}}{m^{2}}x(1-x)\right],

(53)

which agrees with that obtained by using the covariant Feynman rules to compute the photon self-energy

PeskinSchroeder
;
weinberg2005quantum
;
Schwartz2013
.

VI
Pion decay to photons

This process takes place through an anomaly in the divergence of the axial-vector current
AdlerBellJackiw
. In QCD, it provides evidence that the number of quark colors should be equal to 3, in agreement with the experimental data
PeskinSchroeder
;
weinberg2005quantum
;
Schwartz2013
. Here, we will calculate the amplitude for the decay
\pi^{0}\rightarrow 2\gamma
in QED, by using the present approach. The one-loop graphs for this process are shown in Fig.
10
, where the coupling of the pion to the fermions is
g_{\pi}\gamma^{5}
.

Feynman diagrams contributing to the amplitude for the pion decay.

The sum of the diagrams gives

\Gamma^{\mu\nu}=-g_{\pi}e^{2}\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}{\rm Tr\,}\left[\gamma^{\nu}S_{F}(k)\gamma^{\mu}S_{F}(k+p)\gamma^{5}S_{F}(k-q)+(\mu\leftrightarrow\nu,p\leftrightarrow q)\right].

(54)

In order to evaluate these contributions, one can use for the spinor propagators the form given in Eqs.
(
44
) and (
45
). However, a simplification occurs if we first calculate the trace over the
\gamma
-matrices. To this end, we note that the trace of
\gamma^{5}
with any number, less than 4, of
\gamma
-matrices vanishes. The trace of
\gamma^{5}
with four
\gamma
-matrices, is

{\rm Tr\,}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{\alpha}\gamma^{\beta}\gamma^{5}=-4i\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}.

(55)

Thus, since the trace of
\gamma^{5}
with an odd number of
\gamma
-matrices vanishes, we get the relation

{\rm Tr\,}\gamma^{\nu}(\not{k}+m)\gamma^{\mu}(\not{k}+\not{p}+m)\gamma^{5}(\not{k}-\not{q}+m)=4im\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}p_{\alpha}q_{\beta}

(56)

which is independent of
k
.
This transverse result arises in consequence of gauge invariance.

In this way, one can write Eq. (
54
) in the alternative form

\Gamma^{\mu\nu}=-8g_{\pi}e^{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}p_{\alpha}q_{\beta}\,i\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{1}{k^{2}-m^{2}+i\epsilon}\frac{1}{(k+p)^{2}-m^{2}+i\epsilon}\frac{1}{(k-q)^{2}-m^{2}+i\epsilon}.

(57)

We remark that the above integral corresponds to a comtribution coming from a one-loop 3-point scalar function. This can be computed in our approach by using Eq. (
1
) for the scalar propagators, together with the procedure outlined in Sec.
II
. After s straightforward calculation, we obtain the result

\Gamma^{\mu\nu}=-8g_{\pi}e^{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}p_{\alpha}q_{\beta}\Gamma(p,q),

(58)

where the cubic scalar vertex can be written as

\displaystyle\Gamma(p,q)=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{2\omega_{k}}\frac{1}{2\omega_{k+p}}\frac{1}{2\omega_{k-q}}\left[\frac{1}{\omega_{k}+\omega_{k+p}-p^{0}-i\epsilon}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}+\omega_{k-q}-q^{0}-i\epsilon}+\right.

\displaystyle\left.\frac{1}{\omega_{k+p}+\omega_{k-q}-p^{0}-q^{0}-i\epsilon}\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{k}+\omega_{k+p}-p^{0}-i\epsilon}+\frac{1}{\omega_{k}+\omega_{k-q}-q^{0}-i\epsilon}\right)+\left({p^{0}\rightarrow-p^{0}}\atop{q^{0}\rightarrow-q^{0}}\right)\right].

(59)

One can see that this contribution has a similar form to that of the last term in Eq. (
30
).

We will now evaluate the imaginary part of this expression in the physical case when
p^{2}=0
,
q^{2}=0
with
p^{0}
and
q^{0}
positive. Then, one finds that

\Im\Gamma(p,q)=\pi\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{2\omega_{k}}\frac{1}{2\omega_{k+p}}\frac{1}{2\omega_{k-q}}\delta(\omega_{k+p}+\omega_{k-q}-p^{0}-q^{0})\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{k}+\omega_{k+p}-p^{0}}+\frac{1}{\omega_{k}+\omega_{k-q}-q^{0}}\right).

(60)

The
\delta
-function corresponds to the contribution coming from the intermediate state involving the cut lines shown in Fig.
10

25
), by employing a procedure similar to that used in Eqs. (
35
)–(
39
). We then get, after some calculation, the Lorentz invariant expression

\Im\Gamma(s)=\frac{1}{16\pi}\frac{1}{s}\ln\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{1-4m^{2}/s}}{1-\sqrt{1-4m^{2}/s}}\right)\theta(s-4m^{2}),

(61)

where
s=(p+q)^{2}
. Using this imaginary part in the dispersion relation

\Gamma((p+q)^{2})=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{4m^{2}}^{\infty}d\sigma\frac{\Im\Gamma(\sigma)}{\sigma-(p+q)^{2}-i\epsilon}.

(62)

one can compute the full cubic scalar vertex
\Gamma((p+q)^{2})

where we have now set

The above result is in agreement with the one found by using the covariant Feynman rules
PeskinSchroeder

. It has been shown that such a result implies that the classical chiral symmetry, which turns up for massless fermions, is violated by quantum effects.

VII
Discussion

The usual approach to evaluating Feynman diagrams through the use of the Feynman propagator
\Delta_{F}(x-y)
of Eq. (
1
) has the advantage of retaining manifest covariance, while providing the physical interpretation of having a particle propagating forward in time from
y
to
x
and an antiparticle propagating similarly from
x
to
y
.
Nevertheless, re-expressing Feynman diagrams in terms of the on-shell propagator
\Delta^{+}[\pm(x-y)]
of Eq. (
2
) may be of interest.

Establishing the connection between the perturbative method introduced in Ref.
Kadyshevsky:1967rs
and the usual Feynman approach is our first result. This method makes the determination of the imaginary part of a Feynman diagram quite straightforward, coming as it does entirely from the step function
\theta
. It is somewhat simpler than using the “largest time” approach of Ref.
tHooft:1973wagVeltman:1994wz
. Once the imaginary part of the scattering amplitude is determined, a dispersion relation can be used to compute the full amplitude, as was done in Eqs. (
41
), (
43
), (
53
) and (
63
).
This alternative to the direct evaluation of Feynman integrals is considered, for example, in Ref.
Elvang:2015rqa
.

As we have shown in scalar models and in QED, the integration over the
\tau
parameters leads to results that are equivalent to those arising when using the old perturbation theory (OPT).
Since the present method is based on the forms (
1
) and (
44
) which involve the Lorentz invariant Feynman propagator (
4
), it may be thought of as a covariant formulation of the OPT.
An interesting feature of this method is that it consistently preserves the gauge invariance in QED.
In this context, we have examined the vacuum polarization and the decay process of the neutral pion in two photons.

Such an approach is convenient in some circumstances when it provides physical insights on quantum field theory
weinberg2005quantum
;
Schwartz2013
;
Isgur:1972qs
. For example,
an appropriate version of OPT is given by Schwinger’s proper time formalism, which is the best way to carry out certain effective action calculations
Schwinger:1951nm
.
Moreover, the OPT is also convenient to give a general proof of the infrared finiteness of averaged transition probabilities
GSterman
.
Another helpful feature of OPT is that it clarifies the way that the singularities of the
S
–matrix arise from various physical intermediate states. This may be useful in connection with the unitarity methods applied to loop computations in gauge theories
bern:1994zxbern:1995cg
;
Britto:2004nc
;
Anastasiou:2006gt
;
Forde:2007mi
;
Bern:2007dw
, which rely on the fact that loop amplitudes are determined by their singularities.

Appendix A
Unitarity and cut diagrams

Let us illustrate the equivalence between the imaginary parts of Feynman graphs found in the present approach and the cutting rules of Cutkosky. To this end we consider, for definiteness, the two loop self-energy graph discussed in Sec.
II
. As we have shown, performing the
\tau
-integrations, we obtain the same result as that found in the old time-ordered perturbation theory. Thus, the contribution from this graph may be written in the compact form (compare with Eq. (
30
) where we set the interaction vertex equal to
g
)

G(p)=g^{4}\int\frac{d\gamma_{1}d\gamma_{2}d\gamma_{3}}{(p^{0}-E_{\gamma_{1}}+i\epsilon)(p^{0}-E_{\gamma_{2}}+i\epsilon)(p^{0}-E_{\gamma_{3}}+i\epsilon)},

(64)

where the integrals over
\gamma_{i}
denote integrations over the internal momentum with the corresponding energy factors
1/2\omega_{j}
, as well as a summation over all time-ordered configurations. Here
E_{\gamma_{i}}
is the energy of the intermediate state
\gamma_{i}
which contains a sum of mass-shell energies
\omega_{j}
of the lines in the state
\gamma_{i}
(see, for example, Eq. (
II
))

The unitarity condition may be represented as shown in Fig.
11
, where the sum is over all cuts
C
.

Figure 11:
Unitarity applied to the two-loop self-energy graph.

The above relation can be written in the form

\sum_{\mbox{all }C}G_{C}(p)=2i\Im G(p),

(65)

where the sum over all cuts of
G(p)
is connected with the decay rate of the particle with momentum
p
.

Let us denote by
G_{n}
the graph to the left of the cut involving the on-shell state
\gamma_{n}
which has an energy denominator replaced by
2\pi\delta(p^{0}-E_{\gamma_{n}})
. The corresponding graph to the right side of the cut, denoted by
G^{\star}_{n}
, is computed according to the complex conjugate rules.
Then, using expressions like the one given in Eq. (
64
) and suppressing for simplicity the integrals and overall factors, we obtain the relation

\sum_{n}G_{n}^{\star}G_{n}=\sum_{n=1}^{3}\left[\prod_{j=n+1}^{3}\frac{1}{p^{0}-E_{\gamma_{j}}-i\epsilon}2\pi\delta(p^{0}-E_{\gamma_{n}})\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{p^{0}-E_{\gamma_{i}}+i\epsilon}\right].

(66)

On the other hand (omitting the same factors), the imaginary part of
G
(times
2i
) is

2i\,\Im G=i\,\left[\prod_{j=1}^{3}\frac{1}{p^{0}-E_{\gamma_{j}}+i\epsilon}-\prod_{j=1}^{3}\frac{1}{p^{0}-E_{\gamma_{j}}-i\epsilon}\right].

(67)

The expressions (
66
) and (
67
) are equal, as may be verified using the identity (
22
) which implies that

i\left(\frac{1}{A+i\epsilon}-\frac{1}{A-i\epsilon}\right)=2\pi\delta(A).

(68)

The equality of the Eqs. (
66
) and (
67
) is equivalent to the unitarity relation (
65
). The above derivation may be generalized in a straightforward way to an arbitrary Feynman diagram.
(see also the section 9.6 of
GSterman
