Prescribed L p L_{p} curvature problem
Yingxiang Hu, Mohammad N. Ivaki

Abstract
. In this paper, we establish the existence of smooth, origin-symmetric, strictly convex solutions to the prescribed even L p L_{p} curvature problem.



1.
Introduction

The prescribed curvature problem, asks the following question:

Question
.

Given a positive, smooth function
f:\mathbb{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, is there a closed, smooth, strictly convex hypersurface whose
k
-th elementary symmetric function of the principal curvatures as a function of the unit normal vector is
f
?

The case
k=n
of this problem is the celebrated Minkowski problem, which is completely solved; see, e.g.
[
Lew38
,
Nir53
,
CY76
,
Pog78
,
Caf90
]
. Let
S_{k}
denote the
k
-th elementary symmetric function of principal curvatures of a hypersurface. For the case
k<n
, in
[
GG02
]
, Bo Guan and Pengfei Guan proved the following result:

Theorem
.

Let
\ell\geq 1
and
f:\mathbb{S}^{n}\to(0,\infty)
be a
C^{\ell,1}
-smooth, even function (i.e.
f(x)=f(-x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
). Then there exists an origin-symmetric,
C^{\ell+2,\alpha}
-smooth (for all
0<\alpha<1
), strictly convex hypersurface whose
S_{k}
is equal to
f
.

Note that while the restriction to even (or group invariant)
f
is unnecessary, in view of
[
GG02
, Thm. 1.4]
, dropping such a condition may lead to non-existence of strictly convex solutions. A variation of Guan-Guan’s result by dropping the evenness assumption at the cost of introducing an exponential weight factor was established by Sheng, Trudinger and Wang in
[
STW04
]
. See also
[
Che59
,
TW83
,
CNS85
,
Tru90
,
Ger96
,
Ger97
,
CNS98
,
GLM06
,
LS13
,
CLW18
,
CX22
]
.

Over the past hundred years, the Minkowski problem has massively evolved, and fundamental generalizations have been put forward by many researchers. One of the most notable generalizations is known as the
L_{p}
-Minkowski problem, which was introduced by Lutwak in his fundamental development of Brunn-Minkowski-Firey theory; see, for example,
[
Lut93
,
LO95
,
Lut96
,
LYZ00
,
LYZ02
,
LYZ04
,
CW06
]
and
[
LR10
,
BLYZ12
,
BLYZ13
,
HLW16
,
BCD17
,
BIS19
,
BBCY19
,
Li19
,
CHLL20
,
HXZ21
,
GLW22
,
KM22
,
Mil23
,
IM23
]
. Motivated by the vast and ever-growing literature on the
L_{p}
-Minkowski problem, we would like to ask the following question:

Question
.

Given a smooth function
f:\mathbb{S}^{n}\to(0,\infty)
, is there a closed, smooth, strictly convex hypersurface with the support function
s
such that for some constant
c
,

(1.1)

\displaystyle fs^{p-1}S_{k}=c\quad?

The case
k=n
is the
L_{p}
-Minkowski problem. In this paper, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
1<p<k+1
,
1\leq k<n
and
\ell\geq 2
. Let
f\in C^{\ell}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
be a positive, even function. Then there exists an origin-symmetric,
C^{\ell+1,\alpha}
-smooth (for all
0<\alpha<1
), strictly convex hypersurface with support function
s
such that

fS_{k}=s^{1-p}.

For the case
p>k+1
and without the evenness assumption on
f
, the existence of strictly convex solutions to (
1.1
) was established in
[
GRW15
]
by an elliptic method, and in
[
BIS21
, Thm. 3.2]
via a flow approach. Moreover, the case
p=k+1
was recently treated in
[
Lee23
]
through a compactness argument relying on the result of
[
GRW15
]
. Let us mention that, for the case
p=1
, the authors in
[
GG02
]
used the special structure of
S_{k}
to first obtain lower and upper bounds on the principal curvatures of the convex hypersurface which then, in combination with an ODE argument or a result of Schneider
[
Sch88
]
yields the
C^{0}
estimate. Moreover, for the case
p>k+1
, we have lower and upper barriers at our disposal, and the
C^{0}
estimate follows immediately. None of these approaches work for
1<p<k+1
. Here, instead, we employ a geometric lemma of Chou and Wang (see
2.1
) in combination with a gradient estimate in a novel way to obtain the lower and upper bounds on the support function
s
and the
C^{2}
estimate
simultaneously
.

Let us also mention that, a great deal of progress has been made on the significantly important counterpart problem of (
1.1
), where
S_{k}
is replaced by
\sigma_{k}^{-1}
(see the next section for the definition of
\sigma_{k}
), known as the
L_{p}
-Christoffel-Minkowski problem, see
[
Fir67
,
Fir70
,
GM03
,
HMS04
,
GMZ06
,
GX18
,
LWW19
]
and
[
Iva19
,
BIS23a
,
BIS23b
]
.
Our approach here for deducing the
C^{2}
estimate also works for the even
L_{p}
-Christoffel-Minkowski problem for
1<p<k+1
and provides a simpler argument than the one in
[
GX18
]
, which answers Question 2 in the aforementioned paper; see also
[
Gua23
]
.

2.
Preparation

Let
(\mathbb{S}^{n},g,\nabla)
denote the unit sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
equipped with its standard round metric and Levi-Civita connection. Let
\mathcal{M}
be a closed, smooth, strictly convex hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The Gauss map of
\mathcal{M}
, denoted by
\nu
, takes the point
y\in\mathcal{M}
to its unique unit outward normal
x=\nu(y)\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
. The support function of
\mathcal{M}
is defined by

(2.1)

\displaystyle s(x)=\langle\nu^{-1}(x),x\rangle,\quad x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}.

The eigenvalues of
\tau[s]:=\nabla^{2}s+gs
with respect to
g
are the principal radii of curvature
\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
; cf.
[
Sch14
]
. Note that if
\tau[s]>0
, the hypersurface
\mathcal{M}
can be recovered through the map
x\mapsto sx+\nabla s
.
Let
\sigma_{k}=\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{k}}
denote the
k
-th elementary symmetric function of principal radii of curvature. Then we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{S_{k}(y)}=\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n-k}}\right)\circ\nu(y)\quad\forall y\in\mathcal{M}.

In the sequel, we obtain the lower and upper bounds on the support function
s
and the
C^{2}
-estimate. Our main observation is that a suitable gradient estimate (see
2.2
) combined with the following geometric lemma due to Chou and Wang suffices to carry out this task all at once.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
CW00
]
Let
\mathcal{M}
be a smooth, origin-symmetric, strictly convex hypersurface. Let
R=\max s
and
r=\min s
.
We have either
\frac{R}{r}\leq\sqrt{n+1}
or

\frac{R^{2}}{r}\leq C_{n}\max_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}\lambda_{n},

where
C_{n}
is a constant depending only on
n
.

Proof.

For completeness, we give a proof of this lemma here.
Suppose
R>r\sqrt{n+1}.

Due to the convexity, we may find two perpendicular directions, say
e_{1},e_{2}
, such that
s(e_{2})=r
and

(2.3)

\displaystyle s(e_{1})>R/\sqrt{n+1}.

Now project
\mathcal{M}
to the
x_{1}x_{2}
-plane and denote the corresponding convex body by
P.
Since
P
is origin-symmetric,
(\pm R/\sqrt{n+1},0)
are in the interior of
P
and
(0,\pm r)\in\partial P.

For simplicity,
P_{r}:=\frac{1}{r}P
, and write
D
for the disk of radius
1
and centred at the origin. We have
D\subset P_{r}
. Let
\tilde{D}
denote the convex hull of
D
and
(\pm R/(r\sqrt{n+1}),0)
. Then
\partial\tilde{D}
is the union of four tangential segments to the circle
\partial D
and two closed arcs of
\partial D
. The four tangent lines are given by

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{R}{r\sqrt{n+1}}\right)^{2}-1}}\left(x\pm\frac{R}{r\sqrt{n+1}}\right).

Now it is easy to verify that the rectangle

-\frac{R}{2r\sqrt{n+1}}\leq x_{1}\leq\frac{R}{2r\sqrt{n+1}},\quad-\frac{1}{2}\leq x_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}

is contained in the interior of
\tilde{D}.
Since the ellipse

(2.4)

\displaystyle E_{0}=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2}):4r^{2}(n+1)\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{R^{2}}+4x_{2}^{2}\leq 1\right\}

lies in this rectangle,
rE_{0}
is contained in the interior of
P
. Therefore, for some
r/2\leq h\leq r
,

E_{1}:=\{(x_{1},x_{2}):4(n+1)\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{R^{2}}+\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{h^{2}}\leq 1\}\subset P

while touching
P
at

(2.5)

\displaystyle\vec{a}:=\left(\frac{R}{2\sqrt{n+1}}\cos\theta,h\sin\theta\right)

for some
-\frac{\pi}{2}\leq\theta\neq 0\leq\frac{\pi}{2}.
We may assume
-\frac{\pi}{2}\leq\theta<0
. Hence, by comparing the slope of the tangent line of
E_{1}
at
\vec{a}
with the one joining
\vec{a}
and
(\pm R/(\sqrt{n+1}),0)
, and in view of the convexity of
P
, we have

(2.6)

\displaystyle\frac{-h\sin\theta}{\frac{R}{\sqrt{n+1}}-\frac{R}{2\sqrt{n+1}}\cos\theta}\geq-\frac{4(n+1)h^{2}}{R^{2}}\frac{\frac{R}{2\sqrt{n+1}}\cos\theta}{h\sin\theta}.

This gives

\sin^{2}\theta\geq\frac{3}{4}.

Now the claim follows from estimating the radius of curvature of
E_{1}
at
\vec{a}
from below:

(2.7)

\displaystyle\frac{2\sqrt{n+1}\left(\frac{R^{2}}{4(n+1)}\sin^{2}\theta+h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{hR}\geq\frac{|\sin\theta|^{3}R^{2}}{4(n+1)h}\geq\frac{1}{C_{n}}\frac{R^{2}}{r},

where
C_{n}:=32(n+1)/\sqrt{27}
.
∎

2.1.
Apriori estimates

Suppose
\mathcal{M}
with the support function
s
is a smooth, strictly convex solution to (
1.1
). Then we have

(2.8)

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n-k}}(\tau[s])=s^{p-1}f.

In the remainder of this section, we will always assume
p\in(1,k+1)
and
1\leq k<n
and that
s
with
\tau[s]>0
is an
even
solution to (
2.8
).

From (
2.8
), we have the following basic estimates:

(2.9)

\displaystyle R^{p-k-1}\leq\frac{c_{n,k}}{\min f},\quad r^{p-k-1}\geq\frac{c_{n,k}}{\max f}.

Therefore, there is a lower bound on
R
and an upper bound on
r
. To obtain the
C^{0}
and
C^{2}
estimates, the following gradient estimate plays a crucial role in our argument. Unlike the one in
[
HL13
]
, our proof
does not
rely on any particular structure of the curvature function
S_{k}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
0<\gamma<2(p-1)/k
, there exists a constant
\beta\geq 1
, depending on
\gamma
,
\,k,\,p,\,\min f
and
\|f\|_{C^{1}}
such that

(2.10)

\displaystyle\frac{s^{2}+|\nabla s|^{2}}{s^{\gamma}}\leq\beta R^{2-\gamma}.

Proof.

Let
\rho^{2}=s^{2}+|\nabla s|^{2}
and
\zeta=\frac{\rho^{2}}{s^{\gamma}}
where
0<\gamma<2(p-1)/k
. For simplicity, we put
\tau=\tau[s]
. We may assume
\max\zeta>R^{2-\gamma}
(i.e.
\beta>1
). Therefore, at a point
x_{0}
where
\zeta
attains its maximum we have

(2.11)

\displaystyle(\nabla^{2}s+sg)\nabla s=\frac{\gamma}{2}\frac{\rho^{2}}{s}\nabla s.

That is, at
x_{0}
,
\nabla s\neq 0
is an eigenvector of
\tau^{\sharp_{g}}
. Hence, we may find an orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}\}
for
T_{x_{0}}\mathbb{S}^{n}
, such that
e_{1}=\frac{\nabla s}{|\nabla s|}
and
\tau|_{x_{0}}
is diagonal. In particular,
\tau_{1i}=0
for
i=2,\ldots,n
, while

(2.12)

\displaystyle\tau_{11}=\frac{\gamma}{2}\frac{\rho^{2}}{s}.

Moreover, at
x_{0}
we have

(2.13)

\displaystyle\zeta_{;ii}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{s^{\gamma}}(\tau_{\ell ii}s_{\ell}+\tau_{ii}^{2}-s\tau_{ii})-4\gamma\frac{\tau_{\ell i}s_{\ell}s_{i}}{s^{\gamma+1}}

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma\rho^{2}(\tau_{ii}-s\delta_{ii})}{s^{\gamma+1}}+\gamma(\gamma+1)\frac{\rho^{2}s_{i}^{2}}{s^{\gamma+2}},

and

(2.14)

\displaystyle\frac{\zeta_{;ii}}{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\rho^{2}}\left(\tau_{\ell ii}s_{\ell}+\tau_{ii}^{2}-s\tau_{ii}\right)-4\gamma\frac{\tau_{ii}s_{i}^{2}}{s\rho^{2}}

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma(\tau_{ii}-s\delta_{ii})}{s}+\gamma(\gamma+1)\frac{s_{i}^{2}}{s^{2}}.

Let
F=\sigma_{n}/\sigma_{n-k}
and
F^{ii}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial\lambda_{i}}
. Note that
\nabla\tau
is fully symmetric, and
F
is
k
-homogeneous. Using (
2.12
),
\zeta_{;ii}\leq 0
and
\zeta_{i}=0
for
i=1,\ldots,n
we have

(2.15)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{\rho^{2}}F^{ii}\left(\tau_{\ell ii}s_{\ell}+\tau_{ii}^{2}-s\tau_{ii}\right)-4\gamma F^{11}\frac{\tau_{11}s_{1}^{2}}{s\rho^{2}}

\displaystyle-\gamma F^{ii}\frac{\tau_{ii}}{s}+\gamma F^{ii}+\gamma(\gamma+1)F^{11}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{s^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\rho^{2}}\left((s^{p-1}f)_{1}s_{1}+F^{ii}\tau_{ii}^{2}-ks^{p}f\right)-2\gamma^{2}F^{11}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{s^{2}}

\displaystyle-\gamma ks^{p-2}f+\gamma F^{ii}+\gamma(\gamma+1)F^{11}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{s^{2}}.

Therefore,

(2.16)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq 2(p-1)\frac{s^{p-2}s_{1}^{2}f}{\rho^{2}}+2\frac{s^{p-1}s_{1}f_{1}}{\rho^{2}}+\frac{2}{\rho^{2}}\sum_{i>1}F^{ii}\tau_{ii}^{2}-2k\frac{s^{p}f}{\rho^{2}}

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}\rho^{2}}{2s_{1}^{2}}-\gamma(\gamma-1)\right)F^{11}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{s^{2}}-\gamma ks^{p-2}f+\gamma F^{ii}

\displaystyle\geq 2(p-1)\frac{s^{p-2}s_{1}^{2}f}{\rho^{2}}+2\frac{s^{p-1}s_{1}f_{1}}{\rho^{2}}-2k\frac{s^{p}f}{\rho^{2}}

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}\rho^{2}}{2s_{1}^{2}}-\gamma(\gamma-1)\right)F^{11}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{s^{2}}-\gamma ks^{p-2}f.

For
0<\gamma\leq 2
,

(2.17)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{2}\rho^{2}}{2s_{1}^{2}}-\gamma(\gamma-1)\geq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}-\gamma(\gamma-1)=\gamma\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)\geq 0.

Now if for some
\beta>2
we had

\frac{\rho^{2}}{s^{\gamma}}{}_{|_{x_{0}}}\geq\beta R^{2-\gamma},

then

(2.18)

\displaystyle s_{1}^{2}\geq\beta R^{2-\gamma}s^{\gamma}-s^{2}\geq\frac{\beta}{2}R^{2-\gamma}s^{\gamma},

and hence

(2.19)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2s^{p-2}f}{\rho^{2}}\left((p-1)s_{1}^{2}+ss_{1}(\log f)_{1}-ks^{2}-\frac{k\gamma}{2}(s^{2}+s_{1}^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2s^{p-2}f}{\rho^{2}}\left(\left(p-1-\frac{k\gamma}{2}\right)s_{1}^{2}+ss_{1}(\log f)_{1}-\left(k+\frac{k\gamma}{2}\right)s^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2s^{p-2}f}{\rho^{2}}\left(\left(p-1-\frac{k\gamma}{2}\right)\frac{\beta}{2}R^{2-\gamma}s^{\gamma}-c_{1}\beta^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{1-\frac{\gamma}{2}}s^{1+\frac{\gamma}{2}}-c_{2}s^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2s^{p+\gamma-2}f}{\rho^{2}}\left(\left(p-1-\frac{k\gamma}{2}\right)\frac{\beta}{2}R^{2-\gamma}-c_{1}\beta^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{1-\frac{\gamma}{2}}s^{1-\frac{\gamma}{2}}-c_{2}s^{2-\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2s^{p+\gamma-2}R^{2-\gamma}f}{\rho^{2}}\left(\left(p-1-\frac{k\gamma}{2}\right)\frac{\beta}{2}-c_{1}\beta^{\frac{1}{2}}-c_{2}\right).

Here, on the last three lines, we used
0<\gamma<2(p-1)/k
. Moreover, the constant
c_{1}
depends on
\min f
and
\|f\|_{C^{1}}
, and the constant
c_{2}
depends on
k
. However, we would obtain a contradiction for
\beta
large enough.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

We have

(2.20)

\displaystyle 1/C\leq s\leq C,\quad\nabla^{2}s+gs\geq 1/C,\quad\|s\|_{C^{2}}\leq C,

where
C>0
is a constant depending only on
n
,
\gamma
,
k
,
p
,
\min f
and
\|f\|_{C^{2}}
.

Proof.

Let
F:=(\sigma_{n}/\sigma_{n-k})^{\frac{1}{k}}.
Note that
F^{k}=fs^{p-1}
. In view of the identity
\nabla^{2}_{ii}\sigma_{1}=\Delta\tau[s]_{ii}-n\tau[s]_{ii}+\sigma_{1}
and concavity of
F
, there holds

(2.21)

\displaystyle 0\leq F^{ij}g_{ij}\sigma_{1}\leq F^{ij}\nabla^{2}_{ij}\sigma_{1}+n(s^{p-1}f)^{\frac{1}{k}}-\Delta(s^{p-1}f)^{\frac{1}{k}}.

We calculate

\displaystyle-k\Delta(s^{p-1}f)^{\frac{1}{k}}={}

\displaystyle(1-p)s^{\frac{p-1}{k}-1}f^{\frac{1}{k}}\sigma_{1}-n(1-p)(s^{p-1}f)^{\frac{1}{k}}

(2.22)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{k}(1-p)(p-k-1)s^{\frac{p-1}{k}-2}|\nabla s|^{2}f^{\frac{1}{k}}

\displaystyle+2(1-p)s^{\frac{p-1}{k}-1}g(\nabla s,\nabla f^{\frac{1}{k}})-ks^{\frac{p-1}{k}}\Delta f^{\frac{1}{k}}.

Thus, for
p>1
, at a point where
\sigma_{1}
attains its maximum we have

(2.23)

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}\leq c_{1}\left(\frac{|\nabla s|^{2}}{s}+R\right).

Due to
2.2
,

(2.24)

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}\leq c_{1}\left(\frac{|\nabla s|^{2}}{s}+R\right)\leq c_{2}(s^{\gamma-1}R^{2-\gamma}+R)\leq c_{3}R\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{1-\gamma}.

In view of
2.1
, for some constant
C
depending on
n,\,\gamma,\,k,\,p
,
\min f
and
\|f\|_{C^{2}}
we have

(2.25)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{\gamma}\leq C.

Now, the uniform lower and upper bounds on the support function and the uniform upper bound on the principal radii of curvature follow from (
2.9
). Moreover, the uniform lower bound on the principal radii of curvature follows from the Newton-Maclaurin inequality

(2.26)

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}=fs^{p-1}\sigma_{n-k}\geq c_{n,k}(\min f)r^{p-1}\sigma_{n}^{\frac{n-k}{n}}

and the upper bound on the principal radii of curvature.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

In the proof of
2.3
, we only used the
concavity
of the curvature function
(\sigma_{n}/\sigma_{n-k})^{\frac{1}{k}}
.

In view of the Evans-Krylov and the Schauder regularity theory, as well as the apriori estimates in
2.3
, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 2.5
.

For any
\ell\geq 2
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant
C>0
depending only on
n,\,k,\,p,\,\alpha
,
\min f
and
\|f\|_{C^{\ell}}
such that

(2.27)

\displaystyle\|s\|_{C^{\ell+1,\alpha}}\leq C.

3.
Existence of solutions

It is known that the only smooth, strictly convex solution to

(3.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n-k}}(\tau[s])=fs^{p-1},\quad 1\leq k<n,\quad 1<p<k+1

when
f\equiv 1
is an origin-centred sphere; see
[
Sim67
, Satz 6.4]
and
[
LW23
, Cor. 1.2]
(see also
[
GLM18
]
where the uniqueness is proved for
p>1-k
). Employing this uniqueness result and the apriori estimates
2.5
, we may now closely follow the approach in
[
GG02
]
or
[
GX18
, Prop. 6.1]
with minor changes to prove the existence of an origin-symmetric, smooth, strictly convex to (
3.1
) (alternatively, one may use a result of Li
[
Li89
]
; see also
[
Li90
,
Li97
]
). The details are as follows.

Let
v\in C^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
be a positive function and
q:=\frac{p-1}{k}+1\in(1,2)
. Consider the auxiliary equation

(3.2)

\displaystyle F(\nabla^{2}s+vg)=\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n-k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}(\nabla^{2}s+vg)=\frac{s^{q}f}{v}.

We may assume
F(1,\ldots,1)=1
for simplicity.
Moreover, define

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}[v]=\{s\in C^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n}):s>0,\,\nabla^{2}s+vg>0\}.

Using the following apriori estimates and a continuity argument, we will prove the existence of a solution to (
3.2
) in
\mathcal{A}[v]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
s\in C^{4}(\mathbb{S}^{n})\cap\mathcal{A}[v]
be a solution to (
3.2
).
There exists a constant
C_{1}>0
depending only on
\min v
,
\min f
,
\|v\|_{C^{2}}
and
\|f\|_{C^{2}}
such that

(3.4)

\displaystyle 1/C_{1}\leq s\leq C_{1},\quad|\nabla^{2}s|\leq C_{1}

Proof.

The lower and upper bounds on
s
follow easily by considering the points at which
\min s
and
\max s
are attained. Next, we deduce the
C^{2}
estimate. Note that
\nabla^{2}s+vg
is positive definite; hence, we only need to obtain an upper bound on
\Delta s
. Assume
\Delta s
attains its maximum at
x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
. Take a local orthonormal frame around
x_{0}
such that
s_{ij}(x_{0})
is diagonal (hence
\nabla^{2}s+gv
and
F^{ij}
are both diagonal). Now, at
x_{0}
, using
(\Delta s)_{;ij}\leq 0
and concavity of
F
, we calculate

(3.5)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq F^{ii}(\Delta s)_{;ii}

\displaystyle=F^{ii}(\Delta s_{;ii}+2\Delta s-2ns_{;ii})

\displaystyle=F^{ii}\Delta(s_{;ii}+v)-2nF^{ii}(s_{;ii}+v)+(2\Delta s+2nv-\Delta v)\sum F^{ii}

\displaystyle\geq\Delta F-2nF+(2\Delta s+2nv-\Delta v)\sum F^{ii}.

We may assume that
\Delta s|_{x_{0}}\geq(-nv+\frac{1}{2}\Delta v)|_{x_{0}}
. Due to (
3.2
) and
q>1
,

(3.6)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq\frac{f}{v}(qs^{q-1}\Delta s+q(q-1)s^{p-2}|\nabla s|^{2})

\displaystyle+2qs^{q-1}g(\nabla s,\nabla(fv^{-1}))+s^{q}\Delta(fv^{-1})-2ns^{q}\frac{f}{v}

\displaystyle\geq qs^{q-1}\frac{f}{v}\Delta s-2qs^{q-1}|\nabla s||\nabla(fv^{-1})|+s^{q}\Delta(fv^{-1})-2ns^{q}\frac{f}{v}.

It follows that

(3.7)

\displaystyle\Delta s\leq c_{1}s+c_{2}|\nabla s|,

where
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
are positive constants depending on
\min v
,
\min f
,
\|v\|_{C^{2}}
and
\|f\|_{C^{2}}
, as
q\in(1,2)
. Moreover, by
[
GG02
, Lem. 3.1]
, for any function
s\in C^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
, we have

(3.8)

\displaystyle\|s\|_{C^{1}}^{2}\leq 4\|s\|_{C^{0}}\|s\|_{C^{2}}.

Therefore,
\Delta s\leq C_{1}
, where
C_{1}
is a positive constant depending on
\min v
,
\min f
,
\|v\|_{C^{2}}
and
\|f\|_{C^{2}}
.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
s\in C^{4}(\mathbb{S}^{n})\cap\mathcal{A}[v]
be a solution to (
3.2
). Then there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
depending only on
\min v
,
\min f
,
\|v\|_{C^{2}}
and
\|f\|_{C^{2}}
such that

(3.9)

\displaystyle C_{2}^{-1}g\leq\nabla^{2}s+vg\leq C_{2}g.

Proof.

By the Newton-MacLaurin inequality, we have

(3.10)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n-k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}(\nabla^{2}s+vg)\leq c_{n,k}\sigma_{n}^{\frac{1}{n}}(\nabla^{2}s+vg).

In view of Proposition
3.1
, the left-hand side is bounded from below while we know the eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}s+vg
are bounded from above. Hence, the eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}s+vg
are bounded from below.
∎

Theorem 3.3
.

Assume
v,f\in C^{4}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
,
v>0
and
f>0
. Then there exists a unique solution
s\in C^{5,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{n})\cap\mathcal{A}[v]
to (
3.2
), where
0<\alpha<1
. Moreover, there exists
C>0
depending only on
n
and
\alpha
, such that

(3.11)

\displaystyle\|s\|_{C^{5,\alpha}}\leq C.

Proof.

Let
v^{t}:=tv+(1-t)
and
f^{t}=tf+(1-t)
.
We show that for each
t\in[0,1]
, the equation

(3.12)

\displaystyle F(\nabla^{2}s+v^{t}g)=\frac{s^{q}}{v^{t}}f^{t}

admits a unique solution
s^{t}
in
\mathcal{A}[v^{t}]
. Assuming the existence, the uniqueness follows from the comparison theorem; see, e.g.
[
GT83
, Thm. 17.1]
).

Let us define

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\left\{\text{$a\in[0,1]$: \eqref{s3:degree-eq} is solvable in $C^{5,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{n})\cap\mathcal{A}[v^{t}]$ for all $t\in[0,a]$}\right\}.

Note that
\mathcal{I}\neq\emptyset
,
since
s^{0}=1
is the solution of (
3.12
),
0\in\mathcal{I}
. Now, we show that
\mathcal{I}
is closed in
[0,1]
. Let
t\in\mathcal{I}
and
s^{t}\in C^{5,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{n})\cap\mathcal{A}[v^{t}]
be the solution to (
3.12
). In view of Corollary
3.2
, equation (
3.12
) is uniformly elliptic at
s^{t}
; hence, by Proposition
3.1
we have

(3.13)

\displaystyle\|s^{t}\|_{C^{2}}\leq C,

where
C
is independent of
t
. Moreover, the Evans-Krylov regularity theory, in combination with the Schauder estimate, implies that, for some
C
is independent of
t
:

(3.14)

\displaystyle\|s^{t}\|_{C^{5,\alpha}}\leq C.

Thus,
\mathcal{I}
is closed in
[0,1]
.

Next, we show that
\mathcal{I}
is open in
[0,1]
. Note that the linearized operator of the mapping

(3.15)

\displaystyle s\mapsto F(\nabla^{2}s+v^{t}g)-\frac{s^{q}}{v^{t}}f^{t}

at
s^{t}
is given by

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}\eta=F^{ij}(\nabla^{2}s^{t}+v^{t}g)\eta_{ij}-q(s^{t})^{q-1}\frac{f^{t}}{v^{t}}\eta,\quad\eta\in C^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n}).

It follows from the maximum principle that

(3.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}:C^{5,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{n})\rightarrow C^{3,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{n})

is one-to-one. Thus,
\mathcal{L}_{t}
is invertible if and only if its index is zero; i.e.
\operatorname{ind}(\mathcal{L}_{t})=0
. To show that
\operatorname{ind}(\mathcal{L}_{t})=0
, due to the homotopy invariance of the index, it suffices to show that
\operatorname{ind}(\mathcal{L}_{0})=0
. For some
c>0
, the linearized operator
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is given by

(3.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}\eta=c\Delta\eta-q\eta,\quad\eta\in C^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n}).

Hence, it is invertible from
C^{5,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
onto
C^{3,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
and
\operatorname{ind}(\mathcal{L}_{0})=0
. By the implicit function theorem,
\mathcal{I}
is open in
[0,1]
and thus
\mathcal{I}=[0,1]
.
∎

Proof of
1.1
.

Let us consider the Banach space

(3.19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}:=\{w\in C^{4}(\mathbb{S}^{n}):w(x)=w(-x)\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}\}.

Recall that
f\in\mathcal{B}
and
f>0
. For
w\in\mathcal{B}
and
0\leq t\leq 1
, we write
v=e^{w}
and let
s^{t}
denote the unique solution of (
3.2
) in
\mathcal{A}[v]
with
f^{t}=tf+(1-t)
. Due to the uniqueness,
s^{t}
is even; hence,
\log s^{t}\in\mathcal{B}
. Moreover, due to Theorem
3.3
, the map

(3.20)

\displaystyle\phi_{t}:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathcal{B},\quad w\mapsto\log s^{t}.

is compact. Let
I:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}
be the identity map and

(3.21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}:=\{w\in\mathcal{B}:\penalty\ \|w\|_{C^{5}}<R\}.

In view of
2.5
,
\phi_{t}
has no fixed points on the boundary of
\mathcal{O}
, provided
R
is sufficiently large. Therefore,
\deg(I-\phi_{t},\mathcal{O},0)
is well-defined, provided
R
is sufficiently large.

If
w
is a fixed point of
\phi_{0}
; that is, if
w
satisfies
\phi_{0}w=w
, then
s=e^{w}
is a solution to the equation

(3.22)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n-k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}(\tau[s])=s^{q-1},\quad q=\frac{p-1}{k}+1.

By the uniqueness result
[
Sim67
, Satz 6.4]
,
e^{w}=1
and
w=0
. Hence,

(3.23)

\displaystyle\deg(I-\phi_{0},\mathcal{O},0)=\deg(I-\phi_{0},B_{\delta}(0),0)\quad\text{for any small}\penalty\ \delta>0.

Set
\tilde{\phi}_{0}v=e^{\phi_{0}(\log v)}
. Then

(3.24)

\displaystyle\deg(I-\phi_{0},B_{\delta}(0),0)=\deg(I-\tilde{\phi}_{0},B_{\delta}(1),0).

Let
\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{0,v}
denote the linearized operator of
\tilde{\phi}_{0}
at
v
. Our next goal is to show that
I-\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{0,1}
is invertible. Note that

(3.25)

\displaystyle F(\nabla^{2}(\tilde{\phi}_{0}v)+vg)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\tilde{\phi}_{0}v)^{q}}{v},

\displaystyle F^{ij}(\nabla^{2}(\tilde{\phi}_{0}v)+vg)((\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{0,v}\eta)_{ij}+\eta\delta_{ij})

\displaystyle=\frac{q(\tilde{\phi}_{0}v)^{q-1}\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{0,v}\eta}{v}-\frac{(\tilde{\phi}_{0}v)^{q}\eta}{v^{2}}.

In particular, for
v=1
, we have
\tilde{\phi}_{0}v=e^{\phi_{0}(0)}=1
,
(\tilde{\phi}_{0}v)_{ij}+v\delta_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
and
F^{ij}=c\delta_{ij}
for some constant
c>0
. Therefore,

(3.26)

\displaystyle(q-c\Delta)(\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{0,1}\eta)=(cn+1)\eta.

From this identity and that
q>1
we obtain

\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{0,1}=(n+\frac{1}{c})(\frac{q}{c}-\Delta)^{-1}.

If
(I-\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{0,1})\eta=0
, then
\eta
satisfies
\Delta\eta=(-n+\frac{q-1}{c})\eta
, and due to
q>1
we must have
\eta=0
. Therefore,
I-\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{0,1}
is injective; hence, by the Fredholm alternative, it is invertible. Moreover, in view of
[
Nir01
, Thm. 2.8.1]
,

(3.27)

\displaystyle\deg(I-\tilde{\phi}_{0},B_{\delta}(1),0)=(-1)^{\beta},

where
\beta
is the number of eigenvalues of
\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{0,1}
greater than one. To compute
\beta
, let
\eta\in C^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
satisfy

\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{0,1}\eta=\lambda\eta,

with some
\lambda>1
. Then (
3.26
) gives

(3.28)

\displaystyle\Delta\eta=\left(\frac{q}{c}-\frac{n+\frac{1}{c}}{\lambda}\right)\eta.

Since
\frac{q}{c}-\frac{n+\frac{1}{c}}{\lambda}>\frac{q}{c}-n-\frac{1}{c}>-n
, and
0
is the only eigenvalue of
\Delta
greater than
-n
, we must have
\lambda=\frac{cn+1}{q}
. That is,
\beta=1
and

(3.29)

\displaystyle\deg(I-\phi_{t},\mathcal{O},0)=-1.

Hence, for each
0\leq t\leq 1
,
\phi_{t}
has a fixed point in
\mathcal{B}
. In particular, for
t=1
, we have a
C^{4}
-smooth, strictly convex solution
s
to (
1.1
), which settles the existence part of our main theorem. The regularity of
s
now follows from
2.5
.
∎
