Sparse and Integrative Principal Component Analysis for Multiview Data
Lin Xiao, Luo Xiao

Abstract
We consider dimension reduction of multiview data, which are emerging in scientific studies. Formulating multiview data as multivariate data with block structures corresponding to the different views, or views of data, we estimate top eigenvectors from multiview data that have two-fold sparsity, elementwise sparsity and blockwise sparsity. We propose a Fantope-based optimization criterion with multiple penalties to enforce the desired sparsity patterns and a denoising step is employed to handle potential presence of heteroskedastic noise across different data views. An alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) algorithm is used for optimization. We derive the ℓ 2 \ell_{2} convergence of the estimated top eigenvectors and establish their sparsity and support recovery properties. Numerical studies are used to illustrate the proposed method.



1
Introduction

Multiview data which refers to multiple sets of features generated on the same set of subjects has become increasingly prevalent in a variety of fields. In biomedical research, multiple types of genomics
data measured by different technologies on disparate platforms have been produced to gain insight into cancer genetics and molecular biology (
[
35
]
). A typical example is The Cancer
Genome Atlas (TCGA,
https://www.cancer.gov/about-nci/organization/ccg/research/structural-genomics/tcga
) which consists of measurements of RNA gene expression (GE), DNA methylation (ME), miRNA expression (miRNA), and reverse phase protein array (RPPA) on a
common set of 348 breast cancer tumor samples and each dataset is referred to as a data
view. In the area of multimedia analytics, multiview data can be generated by representing
images/texts/videos from disparate perspectives. For instance, the UCI multiple features
dataset (
https://archive.ics.uci.edu/ml/datasets/Multiple+Features
) contains 2000 images for handwritten numerals from ‘0’ to ‘9’, where each image is presented
in terms of six categories of visual features, including Fourier coefficients, profile correlations, Karhunen-Loeve coefficients, pixel values, Zernike moments and morphological features.

One major focus of multiview data analysis is to uncover the underlying associations across different data views. For example, the four data views in TCGA might represent different biological components but they are also considered biologically related since they are generated from the same set of subjects. Joint analysis of all data views makes it possible to identify this type of association. Thus, there has been growing interest and demands in
effectively combining the strength of each data viewthrough an integrative statistical learning paradigm, e.g., integrative clustering (
[
32
]
,
[
53
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
50
]
), integrative classification (
[
43
]
,
[
56
]
) and integrative regression (
[
34
]
).

One fundamental tool in the joint analysis of multiview data is dimension reduction, through which meaningful decomposition of variations across different data views is identified. Canonical correlation analysis (CCA,
[
24
]
) is a classic method to capture joint variation between two data views and has been extended to handle more than two data views
[
51
]
with a number of variants being proposed (
[
38
]
,
[
1
]
,
[
5
]
). CCA-based methods assume that all data views are generated from a common latent subspace and thus ignore individual variation specific to each data view. With more than two data views available, it might be also of interest to identify variation shared by only a subset of data views.
To remedy the limitations of CCA, the joint and individual variations explained (JIVE,
[
36
]
) method proposes a decompsoition model in which joint variation shared by all data views as well as individual variation specific to each data view are taken into account.
Several variants of JIVE (COBE
[
57
]
; AJIVE
[
18
]
) have also been proposed to provide identifiable JIVE-type decomposition for multiview data. Going further, methods that can incorporate partially shared structures have been developed as well, including deterministic matrix decompositions (SLIDE,
[
23
]
;
[
25
]
) and probabilistic approaches
[
30
]
. Specifically, deterministic models (SLIDE,
[
23
]
;
[
25
]
) allow different types of structures (joint/partially shared/individual) by inducing structured sparsity on the loading matrix while probabilistic models
[
30
]
achieve so by introducing a structural sparsity prior. Recently, two matrix decomposition based approaches, BIDIFAC (
[
39
]
) and BIDIFAC+ (
[
37
]
), have been developed for bidimensionally linked data that are matched both at the sample level and the feature level. The uni-dimensional version of BIDIFAC and BIDIFAC+ can be applied to extract different types of variations for multiview data as well. It is worth mentioning that a related but distinct area is joint analysis of linked data,
where different populations of subjects are measured on the same set of features; see, e.g.,
[
20
]
,
[
49
]
and
[
10
]
.

In this work, our goal is to develop a theoretically sound approach for the joint analysis of multiview data. Motivated by the fact that the integrative approaches based on deterministic matrix decomposition (JIVE, SLIDE, BIDIFAC, BIDIFAC+) mentioned above can be essentially regarded as generalizations of traditional principal component analysis with different sparsity patterns (
[
36
]
,
[
23
]
), we aim to develop a method directly using the framework of sparse PCA, for which there is abundant body of works that can be borrowed and statistical properties are much more tractable.

As an important tool for dimension reduction, sparse PCA has attracted lots of attention in the last two decades and we refer to
[
59
]
for an extensive literature review on sparse PCA methods. One dominant line of work formulates sparse PCA as a an optimization problem with a non-smooth objective function and non-convex constraints. Some important approaches in this field include variance-maximization based formulation (
[
27
]
,
[
28
]
), regression based formulation
[
58
]
, singular value decomposition based formulation (
[
42
]
,
[
51
]
). One computational challenge associated with the non-smooth and non-convex formulation is that some existing numerical algorithms are unable to provide convergence guarantee. Recently there have been a few works that introduce new algorithms that can solve non-convex problems with guarantee of convergence to a stationary point (
[
12
]
,
[
15
]
). But a good starting point is still crucial for these algorithms to work out (
[
12
]
). The other line of sparse PCA work is based on convex relaxation.
[
13
]
formulates sparse PCA as a semidefinite programming problem using convex relaxation.
[
47
]
develops Fantope projection and selection (FPS) with good computational and theoretical properties
[
33
]
. FPS focuses on the sparsity of the whole principal subspace and does not allow individual eigenvectors to have different support regions.

[
11
]
addresses this issue by proposing a deflated Fantope based method, which sequentially estimates the eigenvectors and allows different support regions across eigenvectors.

We propose a new sparse and integrative principal component analysis (SIPCA) for multiview data.
We concatenate multiview data vectors into a single multivariate data vector with variables within each data view grouped together as blocks and consider the popular spiked covariance model.
To accommodate hetereogenity in the data as the views might measure different characteristics, we allow the variance of errors to differ across views.
We formulate a convex optimization problem through Fantope (
[
47
]
) and impose a combination of penalties to induce two-fold sparsity on the eigenvectors: elementwise sparsity and blockwise sparsity. As shall be shown later, an eigenvector with blocks of zeros corresponding to variation shared by a subset of views and thus such sparsity patterns are desired as they reveal the associations among the views, similar to the matrix decomposition methods such as JIVE (
[
36
]
) and SLIDE (
[
23
]
). The Fantope based formulation is appealing for several reasons. First, it guarantees a convergent algorithm. Indeed, it allows us to develop an alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM,
[
7
]
) algorithm for estimation, which guarantees convergence under mild conditions. Second, it could naturally accommodate data heterogeneity and allows us to use a denoised sample covariance in the formulation; see Section
2
for details.
Third, it enables us to investigate the theoretical properties of SIPCA. We have derived the
\ell_{2}
rate of convergence of the estimated eigenvectors from SIPCA and also established their sparsity and support recovery properties. To our best knowledge, this is the first work with theoretical guarantee in the multiview data analysis literature.
Moreover, the theoretical work also contributes to the PCA literature when multiple penalties are presented.

As we are preparing this manuscript, we have become aware that
[
55
]
proposes a similar Fantope based method for multivariate functional data. While our focus is on multiview data, the methods are similar. One major methodological difference is that we denoise the sample covariance to accommodate data heterogeneity which is necessary for multiview data. However, such a step might not be needed for functional data as smoothing can be used to remove errors. Another important difference is that
[
55
]
does not have a theoretical study to validate their method, while theoretical validation is a major focus of our work. It is worth noting that
the theoretical properties established in our work can be applied to multivariate functional data, thus providing a theoretical support of
[
55
]
for multivariate functional data.

The rest of this manuscript is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we formulate the SIPCA and describe the estimation procedure and the optimization algorithm in detail. In Section
3
, we provide a theoretical analysis for the proposed estimate. Section
4
presents extensive simulations comparing SIPCA with existing methods. In section
5
, we apply SIPCA to the aforementioned TCGA data. We conclude with a discussion in Section
6
. Technical details and additional materials are provided in the Appendix.

2
Sparse spiked covariance model for multiview data

Consider multiview data with
I
views.
We concatenate the data vector from all data views for one sample and consider the popular spiked covariance model (
[
26
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
40
]
,
[
14
]
)
for the concatenated sample:

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{y}&=\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e},\\
\boldsymbol{x}&=(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\intercal},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{I}^{\intercal})^{\intercal},\,\,\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{\mu},\,\,\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{x})=\Sigma_{1},\\
\boldsymbol{e}&=(\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{\intercal},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{I}^{\intercal})^{\intercal},\,\,\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{e}=0,\,\,\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{e})=\Sigma_{e},\\
\boldsymbol{x},&\,\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{I}\text{ are mutually independent}.\end{split}

(1)

In the model,
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the signal vector,
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}}
is the sub vector corresponding to the
i
th data view, and
p=\sum_{i}p_{i}
. We shall discuss the signal covariance matrix
\Sigma_{1}
corresponding to the signal
\boldsymbol{x}
later.
The noise vector
\boldsymbol{e}
is similarly partitioned into
I
sub noise vectors.
For the noise covariance
\Sigma_{e}
, we allow the noise variances to vary across different data views. Specifically,

\Sigma_{e}=\text{blockdiag}(\sigma_{1}^{2}I_{p_{1}},\cdots,\sigma_{I}^{2}I_{p_{I}}),

(2)

where
\sigma_{i}^{2},1\leq i\leq I
, might be distinct. Such an assumption seems reasonable or even necessary to accommodate data heterogeneity as the different data views may be measured differently. Let
\Sigma
be the covariance of
\boldsymbol{y}
, then
\Sigma=\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{e}
.

We assume that the signal covariance matrix
\Sigma_{1}
is of rank
r
and has the eigendecomposition
\Sigma_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\gamma_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{\intercal}
,
where
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{r}>0
are eigenvalues
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are eigenvectors satisfying
\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{j},\boldsymbol{v}_{k}\rangle=\mathbb{I}(j=k)
. To ensure identifiability of the eigenvectors, we shall assume that the eigenvalues are distinct.
Similar to the blockwise partition of the signal vector
\boldsymbol{x}
, the eigenvector
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}
can be partitioned as
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}=((\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{1})^{\intercal},\ldots,(\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{I})^{\intercal})^{\intercal}
with

\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}}
corresponding to the
i
th data view.
The vector
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{i}
quantifies the contribution of the
i
th data view to the
j
th principal component. Indeed, if
\|\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{i}\|_{2}=0
, then the
i
th data view does not contribute to the variation in the signal along the direction of the
j
th principal component.
Likewise,
\Sigma_{1}
can be partitioned into blocks of submatrices
\left\{(\Sigma_{1})^{k\ell}\right\}_{1\leq k,\ell\leq I}
with the
(k,\ell)th
block
(\Sigma_{1})^{k\ell}
being defined as follows:

(\Sigma_{1})^{k\ell}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{k}(\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{\ell})^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{k}\times p_{\ell}}.

The matrix
(\Sigma_{1})^{k\ell}
quantifies the covariance in the signal vector between the
k
th and
\ell
th data views. Notice that for an arbitrary
p\times p
matrix, we can always partition it into blocks of submatrices.

In previous works (BIDIFAC+
[
37
]
, SLIDE
[
23
]
), the variation of the multiview data is decomposed into three types: (i) joint variation shared by all data views; (ii) partially-shared variation shared by only a subset of data views; and (iii) individual variation unique to one data view. To that end, we impose blockwise sparsity structures on the eigenvectors. Take
I=3
data views for instance. For an arbitrary eigenvector
\boldsymbol{v}=\left((\boldsymbol{v}^{1})^{\intercal},(\boldsymbol{v}^{2})^{\intercal},(\boldsymbol{v}^{3})^{\intercal}\right)^{\intercal}
which can be partitioned into three blocks, the potential blockwise sparsity structures it can possess include three types as listed below:

(1)

Type I (joint structure):
\left((\boldsymbol{v}^{1})^{\intercal},(\boldsymbol{v}^{2})^{\intercal},(\boldsymbol{v}^{3})^{\intercal}\right)^{\intercal}
.

(2)

Type II (partially shared structure):
\left(0^{\intercal},(\boldsymbol{v}^{2})^{\intercal},(\boldsymbol{v}^{3})^{\intercal}\right)^{\intercal}
,
\left((\boldsymbol{v}^{1})^{\intercal},0^{\intercal},(\boldsymbol{v}^{3})^{\intercal}\right)^{\intercal}
,
\left((\boldsymbol{v}^{1})^{\intercal},(\boldsymbol{v}^{2})^{\intercal},0^{\intercal}\right)^{\intercal}
.

(3)

Type III (individual structure):
\left((\boldsymbol{v}^{1})^{\intercal},0^{\intercal},0^{\intercal}\right)^{\intercal}
,
\left(0^{\intercal},(\boldsymbol{v}^{2})^{\intercal},0^{\intercal}\right)^{\intercal}
,
\left(0^{\intercal},0^{\intercal},(\boldsymbol{v}^{3})^{\intercal}\right)^{\intercal}
.

The variation along an eigenvector with type I structure is contributed by all three data views and hence the variation is called joint variation; and type I structure of the eigenvector is referred to as a joint structure.
As for type II structure, two views share a principal component score that is not present in the third view. Hence, an eigenvector of type II structure characterizes variation partially shared by two out of three views and
type II structure is referred to as a partially shared structure.
Lastly, eigenvectors with individual structures only capture variation specific to a single data view. Such a definition is straightforward to be generalized to any number of data views. Notice with
I\geq 4
data views, a type II eigenvector may have
1
to
I-1
blocks of zeros.

Identification of the above three types of structures improves the interpretation of the principal component scores and help us understand
how the data views relate to each other. In particular, it enables us to quantify how much variation of a data view is shared with other data views and how much variation is exclusive to this data view.
In addition to the blockwise sparsity structure that are described above, we also would like to impose within-block variable sparsity. More precisely, we aim to identify relevant variables in each data view that contribute to the variation explained along a particular eigenvector.

We now introduce two types of sparsity index to measure the elementwise and blockwise sparsity level of a vector of dimension
p
. First, for an arbitrary vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we define two index sets as below:

\begin{split}&J_{1}(\boldsymbol{v})=\left\{i:v_{i}\neq 0\right\},\\
&J_{2}(\boldsymbol{v})=\left\{k:\|\boldsymbol{v}^{k}\|_{2}\neq 0\right\},\end{split}

where
J_{1}(\boldsymbol{v})
and
J_{2}(\boldsymbol{v})
refer to the collection of indices corresponding to the elementwise and blockwise nonzero entries of
\boldsymbol{v}
respectively. Further, the cardinalities of
J_{1}(\boldsymbol{v})
and
J_{2}(\boldsymbol{v})
are denoted as
|J_{1}(\boldsymbol{v})|
and
|J_{2}(\boldsymbol{v})|
respectively.

Given
n
independent and identically distributed copies of
\boldsymbol{y}

from the spiked covariance model (
1
),
the aim is to estimate the eigenvectors of the signal covariance
\Sigma_{1}

and also to identify their two levels of sparsity structures: blockwise sparsity and elementwise sparsity.

2.1
Model estimation

The heteroskedasticity in noises across data views in (
2
) leads to one major challenge in the model estimation: the eigenvectors of covariance of the observed data, i.e.,
\Sigma
and those of the signal covariance
\Sigma_{1}
are not necessarily the same. Thus, performing standard PCA or sparsity-inducing PCA directly on the sample covariance matrix might give inconsistent estimates even when the dimension
p
is small compared to the sample size
n
. As a remedy,
[
19
]
propose to replace the diagonal entries of the sample covariance matrix with zeros before performing PCA, which however again might change the targeted principal subspace, i.e., the space spanned by the eigenvectors of the signal covariances.

[
54
]
develop HeteroPCA which estimates the signal principal subspace iteratively and in each iteration
the diagonal entries of the sample covariance matrix are replaced by estimates from the off-diagonals. However, HeteroPCA has two key drawbacks. First, it implicitly requires prior knowledge of the rank of the signal covariance matrix, which is often unknown. Second, HeteroPCA is an iterative algorithm without convergence guarantee.

Our idea is to first denoise the sample covariance matrix, denoted as
\hat{\Sigma}
. Note that the covariance matrix of the
i
th data view is a standard spiked covariance model with homoskedastic noise variance
\sigma_{i}^{2}
.
We adopt the method of bulk eigenvalue matching analysis (BEMA,
[
29
]
) to each individual data view to obtain an estimate of
\sigma_{i}^{2}
, denoted as
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{2}
. Briefly, for a spiked covariance model, BEMA utilizes a number of bulk empirical eigenvalues of the sample covariance in the middle range to estimate the theoretical distribution of sample eigenvalues of the signal covariance and then
the noise estimate
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{2}
can be found by minimizing the sum of squared difference between the bulk empirical eigenvalues and their theoretical limit. Compared to other related approaches (e.g.,
[
22
]
,
[
31
]
), BEMA is appealing because it seems to provide more accurate estimation empirically and its theoretical validity has also been established.

Given
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{2},1\leq i\leq I
, from BEMA, the additive structure in the spiked covariance model (
1
) motivates a straightforward pointwise estimate of
\Sigma_{1}
shown as below,

S=\hat{\Sigma}-\text{blockdiag}(\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}I_{p},\cdots,\hat{\sigma}_{I}^{2}I_{p}).

The estimate
S
can be viewed as a denoised version of the sample covariance
\hat{\Sigma}
and based on
S
, we consider estimating the eigenvectors
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}(1\leq j\leq r
) associated with the signal covariance
\Sigma_{1}
.

We start with estimation of the first eigenvector
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
. Recall that we aim to induce two levels of sparsity in
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
, which can be accommodated by proposing a variance maximization based formulation with a combination of a lasso penalty and a group lasso penalty
[
44
]
. Such a straightforward formulation might not be computationally appealing due to the non-convexity nature of the problem and it would become even more complex for higher-level eigenvector estimation problem which has an additional orthogonality constraint. So we follow the Fantope-based sparse PCA framework (
[
47
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
55
]
), and propose the following optimization problem:

\max_{H\in\mathcal{F}^{1}}\langle S,H\rangle-\lambda\beta\|H\|_{1,1}-\lambda(1-\beta)\|H\|_{1,1}^{\ast},

where
\mathcal{F}^{1}=\{H\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}:0\preceq H\preceq I_{p}\text{ and }\text{tr}(H)=1\}
denotes the Fantope of degree one. Moreover, the first penalty
\|H\|_{1,1}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}|H_{ij}|
is the matrix version of Lasso penalty, and the second penalty
\|H\|_{1,1}^{\ast}=\sum_{k=1}^{I}\sum_{\ell=1}^{I}w_{k\ell}\|H^{k\ell}\|_{2}
is the matrix version of group lasso penalty.
Note that
H^{k\ell}
denotes the
(k,\ell)
th sub block of
H
and
w_{k\ell}
is the weight that is used to adjust for the size of the submatrix
H^{k\ell}
.
The overall penalty is controlled by
\lambda\geq 0
and

\beta
is a number between 0 and 1 and balances the two types of penalties.
When it comes to estimation of higher level eigenvectors, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\ (j\geq 2)
, we use the deflated Fantope constraint
[
11
]
to enforce strict orthogonality between different eigenvectors and the optimization problem is given as follows:

\max_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}}}\langle S,H\rangle-\lambda\beta\|H\|_{1,1}-\lambda(1-\beta)\|H\|_{1,1}^{\ast},

(3)

where

\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}}=\left\{H\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}:\,H\in\mathcal{F}^{1}\text{ and }\langle H,\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}\rangle=0\right\}
is the deflated Fantope and
\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}=\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}^{\top}
is the projection matrix associated with the principal subspace spanned by the first
j-1
eigenvector estimates.
To summarize, the top
r
eigenvector estimates
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j},1\leq j\leq r
are obtained sequentially:

\begin{split}\hat{H}_{j}&=\operatorname{argmax}_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}}}\left\{\langle S,H\rangle-\lambda\beta\|H\|_{1,1}-\lambda(1-\beta)\|H\|_{1,1}^{\ast}\right\},\\
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}&=\text{the first eigenvector of }\hat{H}_{j},\\
\hat{\Pi}_{j}&=\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}+\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}^{\intercal},\end{split}

where
\hat{\Pi}_{0}=0\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
. Note that the tuning parameters
\lambda
and
\beta
don’t have to be the same for all
j
. The optimization problem (
3
) is convex and hence can be solved by existing algorithms with convergence guarantee. We shall describe an algorithm in detail in Section
2.2
.

This sequential formulation is critical because it leads to mutually
orthogonal eigenvector estimates and also allows for different sparsity patterns in each individual eigenvector estimate. In contrast, the principal subspace approach in
[
48
]
restricts all eigenvector estimates to have the same sparsity pattern and hence is not suitable here.

2.2
Estimation algorithm

We use the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM,
[
7
]
) to solve the optimization problem (
3
). First, we rewrite (
3
) by moving the deflated Fantope constraint to the objective function:

-\langle S,H\rangle+\lambda\beta\|H\|_{1,1}+\lambda(1-\beta)\|H\|_{1,1}^{\ast}+\infty\cdot(1-\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{F}_{\Pi}}(H)),

where
\Pi
is an arbitrary but fixed projection matrix, and
\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{F}_{\Pi}}(H)=1
if
H\in\mathcal{F}_{\Pi}
and 0 otherwise. The above problem can be reformulated using variable splitting:

\begin{split}&\min_{H_{1},H_{2}}-\langle S,H_{1}\rangle+\infty\cdot(1-\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{F}_{\Pi}}(H_{1}))+\lambda\beta\|H_{2}\|_{1,1}+\lambda(1-\beta)\|H_{2}\|_{1,1}^{\ast},\\
&\text{ s.t. }H_{1}-H_{2}=0.\end{split}

(4)

The augmented Lagrangian objective function for problem (
4
) is

\begin{split}Q(S;H_{1},H_{2},W)&=-\langle S,H_{1}\rangle+\infty\cdot(1-\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{F}_{\Pi}}(H_{1}))+\lambda\beta\|H_{2}\|_{1,1}+\lambda(1-\beta)\|H_{2}\|_{1,1}^{\ast}\\
&+\langle W,H_{1}-H_{2}\rangle+\frac{\rho_{0}}{2}\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{2}^{2},\end{split}

where
W
is matrix of dual variables and
\rho_{0}>0
is called the penalty parameter. The ADMM algorithm alternates between the primal step, where
Q(S;H_{1},H_{2},W)
is minimized with respect to
H_{i}

(i=1,2)
, and the dual step where the dual variable
W
is updated. We denote the estimates from the previous iteration as
\tilde{H}_{j}

(j=1,2)
and
\tilde{W}
.

In the primal step, since the objective function
Q(S;H_{1},H_{2},W)
is decomposable with respect to
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, they can be updated separately. The update for
H_{1}
is given by

\hat{H}_{1}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{\Pi}}\left\{\tilde{H}_{2}-\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}(\tilde{W}-S)\right\},

(5)

where
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{\Pi}}
is the deflated-Fantope-projection operator, of which the definition and the explicit expression are shown in Lemma
10
. To update
H_{2}
, we need to solve the following subproblem

\min_{H_{2}}\frac{\rho_{0}}{2}\left\|\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\tilde{W}+\hat{H}_{1}-H_{2}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\beta\|H_{2}\|_{1,1}+\lambda(1-\beta)\|H_{2}\|_{1,1}^{\ast},

which also has a closed-form solution due to the fact that these two penalty terms possess a hierarchical structure. Specifically, denote the solution as
\hat{H}_{2}=\text{Prox}\left\{\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\tilde{W}+\hat{H}_{1}\right\}
, and
\hat{H}_{2}
can be obtained via two steps
[
2
]
:

\begin{split}A&=\mathcal{S}_{\frac{\lambda\beta}{\rho_{0}}}\left\{\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\tilde{W}+\hat{H}_{1}\right\},\\
(\hat{H}_{2})^{k\ell}&=\left(1-\frac{\lambda(1-\beta)w_{k\ell}}{\rho_{0}\|A^{k\ell}\|_{2}}\right)_{+}A^{k\ell},\end{split}

(6)

where
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)(|x|-\lambda)_{+}
. In the dual step, the Lagrangian multiplier
W
is updated as
\hat{W}=\tilde{W}+\rho_{0}(\hat{H}_{1}-\hat{H}_{2})
.

Although the global convergence of ADMM algorithm is guaranteed for convex problems, the performance of ADMM is often sensitive to the tuning parameter
\rho_{0}
and
the convergence rate can be slow with a fixed
\rho_{0}
. A locally adaptive ADMM (LA-ADMM) proposed in

[
52
]
runs multiple stages of ADMM in which
at each stage the penalty parameter
\rho_{0}
is dynamically increased by a constant factor
\alpha_{0}>1
and the ADMM is run for a constant number of iterations.
It has been proved theoretically that LA-ADMM improves the computational speed of ADMM. Empirically, we did a small-scale simulation study, which shows that LA-ADMM is 2-8 times faster than ADMM. Hence, LA-ADMM is adopted to accelerate convergence in our simulations and data analysis.

2.3
Tuning parameter selection

In the optimization problem (
3
), the two tuning parameters
\lambda
and
\beta
are associated with the sparsity penalty and need to be selected for each
j
separately.

For estimation of the
j
th eigenvector, a five-fold cross-validation is adopted to determine the best tuning parameters
\lambda^{*}
and
\beta^{*}
. The data is divided into five folds, denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2},...,\mathcal{P}_{5}
. Let
\hat{H}^{(-v)}(\lambda,\beta)
be the solution to (
3
) with tuning parameter
(\lambda,\beta)
and the sample covariance is calculated on data other than
\mathcal{P}_{v}
. Let
S^{v}
be the sample covariance estimated from
\mathcal{P}_{v}
. Then the optimal tuning parameters
(\lambda^{*},\beta^{*})
are chosen by maximizing the cross-validated inner product shown as below:

(\lambda^{*},\beta^{*})=\operatorname{argmax}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{A}_{1},\beta\in\mathcal{A}_{2}}\sum_{v=1}^{5}\hat{e}^{(-v)}(\lambda,\beta)^{\intercal}S^{v}\hat{e}^{(-v)}(\lambda,\beta),

where
\hat{e}^{(-v)}(\lambda,\beta)
is the top eigenvector of
\hat{H}^{(-v)}
and
\mathcal{A}_{1}
,
\mathcal{A}_{2}
are candidate sets for
\lambda
and
\beta
respectively. We set
\mathcal{A}_{2}=\left\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\right\}
, and a sequence between 0 and the 95% quantile of absolute values of off-diagonal elements of
S_{j}=(I-\hat{\Pi}_{j-1})S(I-\hat{\Pi}_{j-1})
is used as
\mathcal{A}_{1}
, following
[
11
]
. The upper bound imposed on
\mathcal{A}_{2}
is sensible as it can be shown a too large
\lambda
will lead to trivial solutions.

3
Theoretical properties

We shall derive the
\ell_{2}
rate of the estimated eigenvectors from SIPCA under appropriate conditions. In addition, we shall establish the sparsity and support recovery properties of the estimated eigenvectors.

3.1
Deterministic theoretical results

In this subsection, we derive
deterministic conditions that will be useful to establish the sparsity of the estimates and derive the upper bounds of the
\ell_{2}
error. The low rank property of the signal matrix, as shown in Assumption
1
, is the key assumption imposed throughout the section. All the propositions in this section imposes neither distributional assumptions on the data nor assumptions on the eigenvalues or sparsity of eigenvectors of the signal matrix, thus the derived conditions and bounds are in the forms of inequalities or functions involving the estimates. In the next subsection, statistical assumptions are imposed, which lead to validation of those deterministic conditions.

We first introduce some assumptions and notations.

Assumption 1
.

For the spiked covariance model in (
1
),
the signal covariance matrix
\Sigma_{1}

has the eigendecomposition
\Sigma_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\gamma_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{\intercal}
, where
r
is a fixed integer,
\gamma_{1}>\gamma_{2}>\cdots>\gamma_{r}>0

are eigenvalues and
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}
is the associated eigenvector for
\gamma_{j}
.

For the optimization problem (
3
),
let
w_{0}=\min_{k,\ell}w_{k\ell}
,
w_{1}=\max_{k,\ell}w_{k\ell}
.
Define for
j=1,\ldots,r-1
, the projection matrices
\Pi_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{k}\boldsymbol{v}_{k}^{\intercal}

and
\hat{\Pi}_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}^{\intercal}
, where
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}
is the estimated
k
th eigenvector from (
3
). Also let
\Pi_{0}=\hat{\Pi}_{0}=0_{p\times p}
. Then for
j=1,\ldots,r-1
, let
\epsilon_{j}=\max(\|\Pi_{j}-\hat{\Pi}_{j}\|_{2},\|\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\|_{2})
, which quantifies the estimation error from the first
j
eigenvectors.
Let
E=S-\Sigma_{1}
be the error matrix, where
S
is the denoised sample covariance in Section
2.1
.
We introduce two useful matrix norms
\|\cdot\|_{\infty,\infty}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\infty,\infty}^{\ast}
:

\|E\|_{\infty,\infty}=\max_{s,t}|E_{st}|,\quad\|E\|_{\infty,\infty}^{\ast}=\max_{k,\ell}\frac{\|(E)^{k\ell}\|_{2}}{w_{k\ell}}.

Note that
\|\cdot\|_{\infty,\infty}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\infty,\infty}^{\ast}
are conjugate norms for
\|\cdot\|_{1,1}
and
\|\cdot\|_{1,1}^{\ast}
, respectively.

Proposition 1

(Deterministic error bound)
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds, and

\min\left(\frac{\|E\|_{\infty,\infty}}{\beta},\frac{\|E\|_{\infty,\infty}^{\ast}}{1-\beta}\right)\leq\lambda.

(7)

Define, for
j=1,\ldots,r
,

\tilde{a}_{j}=2\left(\lambda\beta|J_{1}(\boldsymbol{v}_{j})|+\lambda(1-\beta)w_{1}|J_{2}(\boldsymbol{v}_{j})|+\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\gamma_{k}\epsilon_{k}\right),

and

\begin{split}\tilde{b}_{j}=4\sqrt{2}\left\{\lambda\beta\left(2|J_{1}(\boldsymbol{v}_{j})|+\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}|J_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k})|\right)+\lambda(1-\beta)w_{1}\left(2|J_{2}(\boldsymbol{v}_{j})|+\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}|J_{2}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k})|\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\gamma_{k}\epsilon_{k}\right\}\epsilon_{j-1}+4\gamma_{j}\epsilon_{j-1}^{2}.\end{split}

Then, for
j=1,\ldots,r
,

\|\hat{H}_{j}-\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{\intercal}\|_{2}\leq\frac{2\tilde{a}_{j}}{\gamma_{j}-\gamma_{j+1}}+\sqrt{\frac{2\tilde{b}_{j}}{\gamma_{j}-\gamma_{j+1}}},

(8)

and

\|\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\|_{2}\leq 2\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{2\tilde{a}_{j}}{\gamma_{j}-\gamma_{j+1}}+\sqrt{\frac{2\tilde{b}_{j}}{\gamma_{j}-\gamma_{j+1}}}\right).

(9)

For the leading eigenvector, i.e.,
j=1
, we can make
\epsilon_{0}=0
, thus
\tilde{b}_{1}=0
. Also
\tilde{a}_{1}=2(\lambda\beta|J_{1}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1})|+\lambda(1-\beta)w_{1}|J_{2}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1})|)
. Thus,
inequality (
8
) simplifies to

\frac{\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}}{2}\lVert\hat{H}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}^{\intercal}\rVert_{2}\\
\leq 2\left\{\lambda\beta|J_{1}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1})|+\lambda(1-\beta)w_{1}|J_{2}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1})|\right\}.

Notice that this error bound shows that the
\ell_{2}
error bound of
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1}
does not rely on the sparsity of
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1}
.
In particular, if we consider problem (
3
) with
\beta=1
, we have

\|\hat{H}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}^{\intercal}\|_{2}\leq\frac{4\lambda|J_{1}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1})|}{\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}},

which is the deterministic error bound derived in
[
47
]
. Notice that condition (
7
) can still be used for
\beta=0
or 1 as we use the convention that for any nonnegative number
a
,
a/0=+\infty
.

For the other eigenvectors, the forms of
\tilde{a}_{j}

and
\tilde{b}_{j}
in Proposition
1
involves
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{j-1}
, which quantify the estimation errors from the first
(j-1)
eigenvectors. This is because of the deflation constraint in the optimization problem (
3
), which leads to the accumulation of estimation errors.

Next, we establish in Proposition
2
below that the estimated eigenvectors can be sparse,
a property that can be crucial for deriving the
\ell_{2}
rate of convergence of the estimated eigenvectors.
Moreover, we provide deterministic conditions that guarantee the uniqueness of the solution to the optimization problem in (
3
), a non-trival result as the objective functions in (
3
) is only convex but not strongly convex.

Proposition 2

(Deterministic sparsity control)
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds.
For any fixed
r_{1}\leq r
, let
J_{1}=\cup_{j=1}^{r_{1}}J_{1}(\boldsymbol{v}_{j})
and
J_{2}=\cup_{j=1}^{r_{1}}J_{2}(\boldsymbol{v}_{j})
.
Define

b_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{4[2(\lambda\beta s_{1}+\lambda(1-\beta)s_{2}w_{1})+2\gamma_{k}\epsilon_{k-1}+\gamma_{1}\epsilon_{k-1}^{2}]}{\gamma_{k}-\gamma_{k+1}},\text{ for }j=1,\ldots,r_{1},

where
s_{1}=|J_{1}|
and
s_{2}=|J_{2}|
. Suppose that
\lambda
and
0<\beta\leq 1
satisfy that,
for
1\leq j\leq r_{1}
,

\mathbb{I}(\beta\neq 1)\left\{\|E\|_{\infty,\infty}^{\ast}+b_{j}\left\|(\Sigma_{1})^{J_{2}^{c}J_{2}}\right\|_{2,\infty}^{\ast}\right\}\leq\frac{w_{0}}{w_{1}}\lambda,

(10)

\frac{1}{\beta}\left\{\|E\|_{\infty,\infty}+b_{j}\left\|(\Sigma_{1})_{J_{1}^{c}J_{1}}\right\|_{2,\infty}\right\}\leq\lambda,

(11)

0<\gamma_{j}-\gamma_{j+1}-4\left(\lambda\beta s_{1}+\lambda(1-\beta)s_{2}w_{1}+\gamma_{1}b_{j}\right),

(12)

where

\begin{split}\|(\Sigma_{1})^{J_{2}^{c}J_{2}}\|_{2,\infty}^{\ast}&=\max_{\ell\in J_{2}^{c}}\sqrt{\sum_{k\in J_{2}}\frac{\|(\Sigma_{1})^{k\ell}\|_{2}^{2}}{w_{k\ell}^{2}}},\quad\|(\Sigma_{1})_{J_{1}^{c}J_{1}}\|_{2,\infty}=\max_{t\in J_{1}^{c}}\|(\Sigma_{1})_{J_{1}t}\|_{2}.\end{split}

Then
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}
uniquely exists and satisfies
J_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j})\subseteq J_{1}
,
J_{2}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j})\subseteq J_{2}
, for
j=1,\ldots,r_{1}
.

Remark 1
.

The proof extends that in
[
33
]
. Notice that to prove
J_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j})\subseteq J_{1}
and
J_{2}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j})\subseteq J_{2}
, we may assume that
J_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k})\subseteq J_{1}
and
J_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k})\subseteq J_{2}
for
k=1,\ldots,(j-1)
.

If
r_{1}=r
, then
J_{1}
contains all indices of non-zero elements in the eigenvectors
\{\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{r}\}
while
J_{2}
contains all indices of non-zero blocks in these eigenvectors. It follows that

(\Sigma_{1})^{J_{2}^{c}J_{2}}
consists of only zero elements, i.e.,
\left\|(\Sigma_{1})^{J_{2}^{c}J_{2}}\right\|_{2,\infty}^{\ast}=0
and also

\left\|(\Sigma_{1})_{J_{1}^{c}J_{1}}\right\|_{2,\infty}=0
, which simplify
conditions (
10
) and (
11
) in Proposition
2
.

Next we consider a special case of problem (
3
) with
\beta=1
, i.e., only elementwise penalty is employed.

Corollary 1
.

Consider the optimization problem in (
3
) with
\beta=1
.
Suppose that Assumption
1
holds.
For any fixed
r_{1}\leq r
, let
J_{1}=\cup_{j=1}^{r_{1}}J_{1}(\boldsymbol{v}_{j})
.
Suppose that
\lambda
satisfies, for
1\leq j\leq r_{1}
,

\|E\|_{\infty,\infty}+b_{j}\|(\Sigma_{1})_{J_{1}^{c}J_{1}}\|_{2,\infty}\leq\lambda,

0<\gamma_{j}-\gamma_{j+1}-4\left(\lambda s_{1}+\gamma_{1}b_{j}\right).

Then
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}
uniquely exists and satisfies
J_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j})\subseteq J_{1}
, for
j=1,\ldots,r_{1}
.

If we consider only the leading eigenvector
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
,
the conditions in Corollary
1
will be reduced to:

\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\|E\|_{\infty,\infty}+\frac{8s_{1}}{\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}}\|(\Sigma_{1})_{J_{1}^{c}J_{1}}\|_{2,\infty}\right)\leq 1,

0<\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}-4\lambda s_{1}\left(1+\frac{8\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}}\right),

which are similar to the conditions in
[
33
]
for proving sparsity of estimated principal spaces.

Proposition
2
does not cover the case of problem (
3
) with
\beta=0
, but similar blockwise sparsity results can be obtained by a slight modification of our proof and the detailed proofs are omitted.

Corollary 2
.

Consider the optimization problem in (
3
) with
\beta=0
.
Suppose that Assumption
1
holds.
For any fixed
r_{1}\leq r
, let
J_{2}=\cup_{j=1}^{r_{1}}J_{2}(\boldsymbol{v}_{j})
.
Suppose that
\lambda
satisfies, for
1\leq j\leq r_{1}
,

\|E\|_{\infty,\infty}^{\ast}+b_{j}\left\|(\Sigma_{1})^{J_{2}^{c}J_{2}}\right\|_{2,\infty}^{\ast}\leq\frac{w_{0}}{w_{1}}\lambda,

0<\gamma_{j}-\gamma_{j+1}-4\left(\lambda s_{2}w_{1}+\gamma_{1}b_{j}\right).

Then
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}
uniquely exists and satisfies
J_{2}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j})\subseteq J_{2}
, for
j=1,\ldots,r_{1}
.

Finally, we discuss results on false negative control. Given the estimated projection matrix
\hat{H}_{j}
, we provide conditions so that there are no false negative element/blocks in the estimation of
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}
.

Proposition 3

(Elementwise false negative control)
.

If

\min_{i:v_{ji}\neq 0}v_{ji}^{2}>2\|\hat{H}_{j}-\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{\intercal}\|_{2},

then
J_{1}(\boldsymbol{v}_{j})\subseteq J_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j})
, i.e., there is no false negative element.

Proposition 4

(Blockwise false negative control)
.

If
\min_{k:\,\|\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{k}\|_{2}\neq 0}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{k}\|_{2}^{2}>2\|\hat{H}_{j}-\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{\intercal}\|_{2},

then
J_{2}(\boldsymbol{v}_{j})\subseteq J_{2}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j})
, i.e., there is no false negative block.

3.2
Statistical theoretical results

3.2.1
Assumptions and notations

In this section, we make some additional assumptions in addition to Assumption
1
and introduce notation that will be used in the next two subsections.

For simplicity, we shall assume equal block size
p_{0}=p_{1}=\cdots=p_{I}
and adopt equal block weight
w_{k\ell}=p_{0},\forall k,\ell
. Thus,
w_{0}=w_{1}=p_{0}
.
We derive the statistical properties of the SIPCA estimator in a generic setting, where the elementwise error is bounded with a high probability.

Assumption 2
.

Suppose that the random sample
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{p})
in model (
1
) is sub-Gaussian, i.e., there exists a constant
K>0
such that

\sup_{j}\mathbb{E}[\exp(y_{j}^{2}/K^{2}]\leq 2.

Assume that
\sqrt{\log p/n}=o(1)
,
then under the sub-Guassian assumption, the sample covariance matrix
\hat{\Sigma}
satisfies

\mathbb{P}\left(\|\hat{\Sigma}-\mathbb{E}(\hat{\Sigma})\|_{\infty,\infty}\geq c\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}\right)\geq 1-2p^{-2},

for some absolute constant
c>0
; see, e.g.,
[
46
]
. Moreover, the BEMA estimator
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{2}
satisfies that
\max_{i}\|\hat{\sigma}_{i}-\sigma_{i}^{2}\|=O_{p}(n^{-1})
; see Assumptions in Theorem 1 of
[
29
]
.
It follows that the error matrix
E
satisfies

\|E\|_{\infty,\infty}=O_{p}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}\right).

Note that as we assume equal block size and equal weight,
\|E\|_{\infty,\infty}^{\ast}\leq\|E\|_{\infty,\infty}
. Thus Assumption
2
implies that
\|E\|_{\infty,\infty}^{\ast}=O_{p}(\sqrt{\log p/n})
.

For notation, we let
J_{1}=\cup_{j=1}^{r}J_{1}(\boldsymbol{v}_{j})
and
J_{2}=\cup_{j=1}^{r}J_{2}(\boldsymbol{v}_{j})
,
s_{1}=|J_{1}|
and
s_{2}=|J_{2}|
. To compare,
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
as well as
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
vary with
r_{1}
in Proposition
2
.
For two sequences of positive numbers
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
,

a_{n}\gtrapprox b_{n}
means that there exists a sufficiently large constant
c
such that
a_{n}\geq C_{n}b_{n}
for all sufficiently large
n
and yet still
a_{n}=O(b_{n})
.

3.2.2
Spiked covariance model with strong signals

We first shall consider the scenario that the signal in the data is strong in the sense that the eigenvalues in the signal covariance matrix are proportional to the dimension of the signal covariance matrix; see Assumption
3
.
This scenario includes high-dimensional latent factor models
[
16
]
and functional data models
[
8
]
.

Assumption 3
.

The eigenvalues
\gamma_{j}
s in the signal covariance matrix
\Sigma_{1}
satisfy
\gamma_{j}=\eta_{j}p
for
1\leq j\leq r
and
\eta_{1}>\eta_{2}>\cdots>\eta_{r}>0
are fixed constants.

Assumption
3
usually holds for high-dimensional latent factor data
[
16
]
and functional data
[
8
]
.
Under Assumption
3
,
[
8
]
shows that the sample covariance matrix of sub-Gaussian random vectors with

as the population covariance is consistent in both the scaled Frobenius norm and the scaled operator norm.

Theorem 1

(Strong signal: statistical error bound)
.

Consider the optimization problem (
3
) with
0\leq\beta\leq 1
.
Suppose that Assumptions
1
–
3
hold and
\sqrt{\log p/n}=o(1)
. Suppose also that
\lambda\gtrapprox\sqrt{\log p/n}
. Then

\|\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\|_{2}=O_{p}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}\right),\quad\text{for }j=1,\ldots,r.

Theorem
1
establishes a near-parametric rate of convergence for the estimated eigenvectors. The theorem provides theoretical validation for the work in
[
55
]
, in which multivariate functional data is considered and
\Sigma
is the discretized covariance matrix for multivariate functional data. Indeed, for multivariate functional data, it can be easily verified that Assumption

[

]
.

\beta=1
,
then Theorem

reduces to theoretical results established in
[
11
]
for multivariate functional data. When
\beta=0
, then only blockwise penalty is employed and the above theoretical result is new.

Note that Assumption

might still be too restrictive and can be relaxed. If the eigenvalues of
\Sigma_{1}
actually satisfy
\gamma_{j}=\eta_{j}\tilde{p}
for

and
\eta_{1}>\eta_{2}>\cdots>\eta_{r}>0
, then the bound in Theorem

become

Theorem 2

(Strong signal: sparsity and blockwise false negative control I)
.

Consider the optimization problem (

) with
\beta=0
.
Suppose the same assumptions and conditions as in Theorem

hold.

For any sufficiently large

(ii)

then for any sufficiently large
n
, with high probability,

J_{2}(\boldsymbol{v}_{j})\subseteq J_{2}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j})\quad\text{ for }j=1,\ldots,r.

Theorem 3

(Strong signal: sparsity and elementwise false negative control II)
.

Consider the optimization problem (

, where

3.2.3
Spiked covariance model with weak signals

In this section, we consider the high dimensional spiked covariance model in which the eigenvalues are bounded (Assumption

) and sparsity of eigenvectors is needed

Assumption 4
.

The eigenvalues in the the signal covariance matrix

Theorem 4

(Weak signal: statistical error bound I)

(Weak signal: sparsity and blockwise false negative control I)
.

) with

. Suppose the same assumptions and conditions as in Theorem

(i)

Theorem 6

(Weak signal: statistical error bound II)
.

Consider the optimization problem (

) with

, where

,

, and

Suppose also that

In Theorem

s_{2}p_{0}=O(s_{1})
imply that the signal eigenvectors are sparse elementwise.
Regarding the leading eigenvector estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1}
, the rate presented in Theorem
6
coincides with what has been established in
[
48
]
for the sparse estimate for the projector of the leading one-dimensional principal subspace. However, this rate is suboptimal compared to the optimal minimax rate established for sparse eigenvector and sparse principal subspace estimation in the literature, which is
O_{p}(\sqrt{s_{1}\log p/n})
(
[
9
]
;
[
48
]
).
It has been shown that there is an underlying trade-off between the statistical and the computational efficiency for sparse PCA methods, and no known computationally efficient methods are capable of attaining the minimax rate optimal estimators (
[
47
]
,
[
6
]
). Considering that SIPCA can be solved in polynomial time, the extra factor
\sqrt{s_{1}}
might be unavoidable.

4.1
Simulation settings

We generate zero-mean multivariate normal data using the spiked covariance model in (
1
) with the noise covariance in (
2
).
We consider
I=20
blocks and each block contains
p_{0}=50
elements so that the total dimension
p=1000
.
For the signal covariance,
\Sigma_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{\intercal}
and
the eigenvectors, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{v}_{j},1\leq j\leq 3
, have two levels of sparsity. At the block level,

J_{2}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1})=\left\{1,2,3\right\},J_{2}(\boldsymbol{v}_{2})=\left\{4,5,6\right\},J_{2}(\boldsymbol{v}_{3})=\left\{7,8\right\},

which means that the first three blocks of
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
are nonzero and all others are zero blocks. Similarly, the blockwise supports for
\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{3}
are
\left\{4,5,6\right\}
and
\left\{7,8\right\}
respectively. At the element level, each nonzero block in
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}
have
\gamma\times 100\%
nonzero elements.
To introduce heteroskedasticity into the data,
we follow
[
54
]
to determine
\sigma_{i}^{2},1\leq i\leq I
in the noise covariance. We first generate
I
random variables from the standard uniform distribution:

u_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}\text{Unif}[0,1],1\leq k\leq I
. Then we set
\sigma_{i}^{2}=\sigma^{2}Iu_{i}^{\alpha}/(\sum_{k=1}^{I}u_{k}^{\alpha})
, where
\sigma^{2}=I^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sigma_{i}^{2}
and can be regarded as the average noise level. The parameter
\alpha
delineates the degree of heteroskedasticity, with a larger
\alpha
indicating more heteroskedastic noise. In particular,
\alpha=0
corresponds to homoskedastic noise. In the simulation, we vary
\alpha
over
\left\{0,2,5,8,10,13,15\right\}
. Moreover, we generate data with both weak signal and strong signal. For the former,
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})=(40,20,10)
and for the latter
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})=(400,200,100)
. Finally, the sample size
n
varies over
\left\{200,400\right\}
, and we fix both
\sigma^{2}
and
\gamma
at 0.5.

4.2
Evaluation criteria

The
\ell_{2}
error of the subspace estimators, i.e.,
\|VV^{\intercal}-\hat{V}\hat{V}^{\intercal}\|_{2}/\|VV^{\intercal}\|_{2}
is used to measure the estimation performance for different methods. For SIPCA, PMD and sample PCA, the top three eigenvector estimates are concatenated to form the signal principal subspace estimator
\hat{V}=[\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1},\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{2},\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{3}]
. The algorithms for JIVE, SLIDE and BIDIFAC+ produce an estimation of the signal matrix, of which the top three right singular vectors are extracted to form
\hat{V}
. For iPCA, the loading subspace is estimated by performing SVD on the product of the common score matrix given by the iPCA algorithm and the concatenated observational data matrix.

We also evaluate the performance of SIPCA in terms of elementwise and blockwise support recovery.
For each eigenvector estimate, we calculate the corresponding sensitivity (true positive rate) and specificity (true negative rate) at both the element level and block level.

6
Discussion

This work is motivated by the perceived need for a general and flexible approach with theoretical guarantee to modeling multiview data with potential heteroskedastic noise. We propose a novel sparse PCA method, SIPCA, which allows for flexible control on the sparsity structure of the resulting eigenvectors. Specifically, two levels of sparsity can be induced: blockwise sparsity reflecting the association structures between different data views and elementwise sparsity indicating important variables within each data view. The proposed method can be applied to sparse PCA problems where the noise is either homoskedastic or heteroskedastic. In the scenario where the noise levels for different data views are not identical, SIPCA employs a denoising step to obtain an estimate for the signal covariance, based on which the eigenvector estimates with desired sparsity are extracted. We demonstrate the superiority of SIPCA in a rich set of simulation settings including low to high degrees of noise heteroskedasticity and weak or strong signal strength. Compared to other competitors, SIPCA displays robust performance across all scenarios in terms of the estimation error for the signal principal subspace and the sparsity structure recovery for each individual eigenvector. Theoretical properties of the eigenvector estimate have been established from two perspectives:
\ell_{2}
consistency and support recovery. Specifically, we show that the proposed eigenvector estimate achieves an
\ell_{2}
rate comparable to what has been established in some related work (
[
47
]
,
[
48
]
).

We describe one major limitation of our method. As we mentioned before, it is natural for us to solve (
3
) using ADMM which is a typical polynomial-time algorithm for constrained convex problems. However, the computational efficiency of ADMM has been shown to be low in our simulations despite the fact that it guarantees convergence to the global optima. Even if we implement an accelerated version of the vanilla ADMM
[
52
]
in practice, there is still much room for improvement. This computational issue primarily relates to the full eigendecomposition of a
p\times p
matrix per iteration, which requires high computational cost when the dimensionality is high. Recently,
[
41
]
propose a new gradient-based and projection-free algorithm with a focus on solving Fantope-based formulation for principal subspace estimation
[
47
]
. Their empirical study indicates that the proposed algorithm takes much less time to achieve an estimate with certain level of accuracy compared with the original ADMM. While this algorithm cannot be directly applied to address our objective function (
3
) since it is specifically designed for the optimization task proposed in
[
47
]
, it is still of interest to adapt this new algorithm to accommodate different types of penalties.

\mathbf{u},&k=j.\end{cases}\end{split}

Uniqueness of solution.

Consider the elastic net version of FPS:

\max_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}}}\quad\langle S,H\rangle-\lambda\beta\|H\|_{1,1}-\lambda(1-\beta)\|H\|_{1,1}^{\ast}-\frac{\tau}{2}\|H\|_{2}^{2}.

(33)

As the objective function is strongly concave, the solution of (
33
) is unique. Consider the max-min and min-max forms using the dual variable
Z_{1}\in\mathbb{B}_{p,1}=\{Z\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}:Z=Z^{T},\|Z\|_{\infty,\infty}^{\ast}\leq 1\}
,
Z_{2}\in\mathbb{B}_{p,2}=\{Z\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}:Z=Z^{\intercal},\|Z^{k\ell}\|_{2}\leq w_{k\ell},\forall(k,\ell)\}
:

\displaystyle\min_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}}}\max_{Z_{1}\in\mathbb{B}_{p,1},Z_{2}\in\mathbb{B}_{p,2}}-\langle S,H\rangle+\lambda\beta\langle H,Z_{1}\rangle+\lambda(1-\beta)\langle H,Z_{2}\rangle+\frac{\tau}{2}\|H\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\max_{Z_{1}\in\mathbb{B}_{p,1},Z_{2}\in\mathbb{B}_{p,2}}\min_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}}}\frac{\tau}{2}\left\|H-\frac{1}{\tau}(S-\lambda\beta Z_{1}-\lambda(1-\beta)Z_{2})\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\tau}\|S-\lambda\beta Z_{1}-\lambda(1-\beta)Z_{2}\|_{2}^{2}.

By the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) condition, a triplet
(\hat{H},\hat{Z}_{1},\hat{Z}_{2})\in\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}}\times\mathbb{B}_{p,1}\times\mathbb{B}_{p,2}
is optimal for the above problem if and only if

\displaystyle(\hat{Z}_{1})_{st}=\text{sign}\left(\hat{H}_{st}\right),\quad\forall(s,t)\text{ with }\hat{H}_{st}\neq 0,

\displaystyle(\hat{Z}_{1})_{st}\in[0,1],\quad\forall(s,t)\text{ with }\hat{H}_{st}=0,

\displaystyle(\hat{Z}_{2})^{k\ell}=\frac{w_{k\ell}\hat{H}^{k\ell}}{\|\hat{H}^{k\ell}\|_{2}},\quad\forall(k,\ell)\text{ with }\|\hat{H}^{k\ell}\|_{2}\neq 0,

\displaystyle(\hat{Z}_{2})^{k\ell}\in\left\{U:\|U\|_{2}\leq w_{k\ell}\right\},\quad\forall(k,\ell)\text{ with }\|\hat{H}^{k\ell}\|_{2}=0,

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}}}\left\{\frac{1}{\tau}(S-\lambda\beta\hat{Z}_{1}-\lambda(1-\beta)\hat{Z}_{2})\right\}.

Denote
\tilde{\Sigma}=(I-\hat{\Pi}_{j-1})(S-\lambda\beta\hat{Z}_{1}-\lambda(1-\beta)\hat{Z}_{2})(I-\hat{\Pi}_{j-1})
.
Let
(\hat{H},\hat{Z}_{1},\hat{Z}_{2})
be the modified primal-dual triplet in (
32
). We now show that this triplet is also optimal for the above optimization problem when
\tau
is sufficiently small. By the existence proof,
\lambda_{1}(\tilde{\Sigma})-\lambda_{2}(\tilde{\Sigma})>0
. Then, part 3 of Lemma
12
implies that when

0<\tau\leq\lambda_{1}(\tilde{\Sigma})-\lambda_{2}(\tilde{\Sigma}),

we have

\hat{H}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{F}^{1}}\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\tilde{\Sigma}\right),

which is equivalent to

\hat{H}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}}}\left\{\frac{1}{\tau}(S-\lambda\beta\hat{Z}_{1}-\lambda(1-\beta)\hat{Z}_{2})\right\}

by Lemma
4
, and thus
(\hat{H},\hat{Z}_{1},\hat{Z}_{2})
is indeed an optimal primal-dual triplet for the optimization problem (
33
).

To prove uniqueness of
\hat{H}
as a solution to (
3
), assume that there exists another solution
\hat{H}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}}
such that

\langle S,\hat{H}\rangle-\lambda\beta\|\hat{H}\|_{1,1}-\lambda(1-\beta)\|\hat{H}\|_{1,1}^{\ast}=\langle S,\hat{H}^{\prime}\rangle-\lambda\beta\|\hat{H}^{\prime}\|_{1,1}-\lambda(1-\beta)\|\hat{H}^{\prime}\|_{1,1}^{\ast}.

Because
\hat{H}
is the unique solution to (

,

.
In addition, by the existence proof,

is of rank 1.
Hence,

1=\text{tr}(\hat{H})=\|\hat{H}\|_{2}^{2}<\|\hat{H}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\text{tr}(\hat{H}^{\prime})=1

and we have reached a contradiction.
∎

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}^{\intercal}-\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{\intercal}\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}^{\intercal}-\hat{H}_{j}\rVert_{2}+\lVert\hat{H}_{j}-\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{\intercal}\rVert_{2}.

\displaystyle=1+\operatorname{tr}(\hat{H}_{j}\hat{H}_{j}^{\intercal})-2\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}^{\intercal}\hat{H}_{j}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}.

Note that

\displaystyle\leq 1+\operatorname{tr}(\hat{H}_{j}\hat{H}_{j}^{\intercal})-2\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\hat{H}_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{\lVert(I-\hat{\Pi}_{j-1})\boldsymbol{v}_{j}-\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\rVert_{2}}{\|(I-\hat{\Pi}_{j-1})\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\|_{2}\vee\|\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\|_{2}}

\displaystyle=2\lVert\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\rVert_{2}.

Since
\Pi_{j-1}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}=0
,

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lVert\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=2\lVert(\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}-\Pi_{j-1})\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lVert(\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}-\Pi_{j-1})\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lVert\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}-\Pi_{j-1}\rVert_{op}\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lVert\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}-\Pi_{j-1}\rVert_{2}

and we have proved (a).

We now prove (b). We just need to show that
\langle\hat{\Pi}_{j-1},\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}^{\intercal}\rangle=0
, which follows by the fact that

\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}

is a projection matrix and by the definition of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}
.

We finally prove (c) and (d). By the definition of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}=\frac{(I-\hat{\Pi}_{j-1})\boldsymbol{v}_{j}}{\|I-\hat{\Pi}_{j-1})\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\|_{2}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{v}_{j})\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}}{\|(I-\hat{\Pi}_{j-1})\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\|_{2}}.

The proof is straightforward and hence is omitted.
∎

Lemma 9
.

Let

and
\hat{\Pi}_{j-1}=\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}^{\intercal}

Proof.

The proof is straightforward with Lemma
7
.
∎

Lemma 10
.

(Deflated Fantope projector
[
11
]
)
For any
p\times p
symmetric matrix
A
and projection matrix
\Pi
, the deflated Fantope projection operator is defined as
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}_{\Pi}}(A)\coloneqq\arg\min_{B\in\mathcal{D}_{\Pi}}\|A-B\|_{2}^{2}
. Specifically, let
\Pi=VV^{\intercal}
, where
V
is a
p\times d
matrix with orthonormal columns and let
U
be a
p\times(p-d)
matrix that forms an orthogonal complement basis of
V
. Then

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}_{\Pi}}(A)=U\left[\sum_{i=1}^{p-d}\gamma_{i}^{+}(\theta)\eta_{i}\eta_{i}^{\intercal}\right]U^{\intercal},

where
(\gamma_{i},\eta_{i})_{i=1}^{p-d}
are eigenvalue-eigenvector pairs of
U^{\intercal}AU
:
U^{\intercal}AU=\sum_{i=1}^{p-d}\gamma_{i}\eta_{i}\eta_{i}^{\intercal}
, and
\gamma_{i}^{+}(\theta)=\min(\max(\gamma_{i}-\theta,0),1)
, with
\theta
chosen such that
\sum_{i=1}^{p-d}\gamma_{i}^{+}(\theta)=1
.

Lemma 11
.

(Curvature Lemma
[
47
]
) Let
A
be a symmetric matrix and
E
be the projection onto the subspace spanned by the eigenvectors of A corresponding to its
d
largest eigenvalues
\gamma_{1}\geq\gamma_{2}\geq\ldots
. If
\delta_{A}=\gamma_{d}-\gamma_{d+1}>0
, then

\frac{\delta_{A}}{2}|||E-F|||_{2}^{2}\leq\langle A,E-F\rangle

for all
F
satisfying
0\preceq F\preceq I
and
\text{tr}(F)=d
.

Lemma 12
.

(Basic properties of Fantope projection
[
33
]
)
Let
A
be a symmetric matrix with eigenvalues
\gamma_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\gamma_{p}
and orthonormal eigenvectors
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{p}
.

1.

\max_{H\in\mathcal{F}^{d}}\langle A,H\rangle=\gamma_{1}+\ldots+\gamma_{d}
and the maximum is achieved by the projector of a
d-
dimensional principal subspace of
A
. Moreover, the maximizer is unique if and only if
\gamma_{d}>\gamma_{d+1}
.

2.

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{F}^{d}}(A)=\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}^{+}(\theta)\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{\intercal}
, where
\gamma_{j}^{+}(\theta)=\min(\max(\gamma_{j}-\theta,0),1)
and
\theta
satisfies the equation
\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}^{+}(\theta)=d
.

3.

If
0<\tau\leq\gamma_{d}-\gamma_{d+1}
, then

\arg\max_{H\in\mathcal{F}^{d}}\langle A,H\rangle=\arg\max_{H\in\mathcal{F}^{d}}\langle A,H\rangle-\frac{\tau}{2}\|H\|_{2}^{2}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{F}^{d}}(\tau^{-1}A)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{\intercal},

uniquely.
