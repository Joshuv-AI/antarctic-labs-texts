for the corresponding subarcs of the unit circle.

The
logarithmic energy

I(\mu)
and the
logarithmic potential

U^{\mu}
of a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Sigma)
are defined, respectively, as

I(\mu):=\iint\log\frac{1}{|z-\zeta|}d\mu(z)d\mu(\zeta),

(17)

U^{\mu}(z):=\int\log\frac{1}{|z-\zeta|}d\mu(\zeta).

(18)

In this section we study the following two related problems:

Problem I.
Given
q
and
\beta
, with
0<q<1
,
-1<\beta<1
, determine a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}([-1,1])
that minimizes the energy
I(\mu)
, subject to the constraint
\mu([\beta,1])=q
.

Problem II.

Given
q
and
\theta
, with
0<q<1
,
0<\theta<2\pi
, determine a measure
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
that minimizes the energy
I(\nu)
, subject to the constraint
\nu(A_{\theta})=q
.

The limiting version of the Problems I and II with constraints on the mass at fixed points,
\beta=1
and
\theta=0
, respectively, were already treated in a couple of works. The description of the equilibrium charge distribution of amount
1-q
on the unit circle when a fixed charge amount
q>0
is placed at
t=1
was done in
[
13
]
by M. Lachance, E. B. Saff and R.S. Varga.
A similar problem on an interval was studied in
[
19
]
by E. B. Saff, J.L. Ullman and R.S. Varga, where the the equilibrium charge distribution of amount
1-q_{1}-q_{2}
was determined when a charge amount
q_{1}>0
is placed at
t=1
, and a charge amount
q_{2}>0
is placed at
t=-1
,
q_{1}+q_{2}<1
. Thus, the solution to the Problems I and II, given by Theorems
3.1
and
1.4
below, can be considered as an extension of the results obtained in
[
13
]
and
[
19
]
(with
q_{2}=0
), respectively. We show that in the limiting case, when
\beta\rightarrow 1
in Problem I, our result recovers the one from
[
19
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

(A. Martínez-Finkelshtein, E.B. Saff,
[
15
, Section 4]
)

The measure
\mu^{*}=:\mu^{*}_{q}\in\mathcal{P}([-1,1])
such that

I(\mu^{*})=\inf\{I(\mu):\mu\in\mathcal{P}([-1,1]),\mu([\beta,1])=q\}
is unique and

i) if
q\geq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\beta}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx
, is given by

d\mu^{*}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{|x-\alpha|}}{\pi\sqrt{|(x+1)(x-\beta)(x-1)|}}dx,\;\;\;

(19)

where
x\in[-1,\alpha]\cup[\beta,1]
,
\alpha\leq\beta
, and with
\alpha
determined from the equation

\int_{-1}^{\alpha}\frac{\sqrt{|x-\alpha|}}{\pi\sqrt{|(x+1)(x-\beta)(x-1)|}}dx=1-q;

(20)

ii) if
q\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\beta}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx
, is given by (
19
), where
x\in[-1,\beta]\cup[\alpha,1]
,
\alpha\geq\beta
, and
\alpha
is the solution to the equation

\int_{-1}^{\beta}\frac{\sqrt{|x-\alpha|}}{\pi\sqrt{|(x+1)(x-\beta)(x-1)|}}dx=1-q.

Note that in the trivial case, when
q=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\beta}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx
, we get
\alpha=\beta
and so Theorem
3.1
gives, as expected, the arcsine distribution,
d\mu^{*}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}
.

Corollary 3.2
.

When
\beta\rightarrow 1
, the density function in (
19
) becomes

d\mu^{*}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{|x-\alpha|}}{\pi\sqrt{(x+1)}(1-x)}dx,\;\;\;x\in[-1,\alpha],

where
\alpha
is the solution to the equation

\int_{-1}^{\alpha}\frac{\sqrt{|x-\alpha|}}{\pi\sqrt{(x+1)}(1-x)}dx=1-q.

(21)

This is precisely the measure obtained in
[
19
]
that corresponds to the case
q_{1}=q
,
q_{2}=0
.

Once Theorem
3.1
is verified, the solution to the Problem II is immediate, as we show in Section
3.3
.

3.1
Auxiliary results

We call
\mu\in\mathcal{P}([a,b])
the
unconstrained
equilibrium measure on an interval
[a,b]
if
\mu
minimizes the logarithmic energy
I(\mu)
among all unit measures in
\mathcal{P}([a,b])
. It is well-known (see e.g.
[
18
, Section I.3]
) that

d\mu(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{(x-a)(b-x)}},\;\;\;x\in[a,b].

The measure
\mu^{*}
that minimizes the energy in Problem I can be viewed as the measure of the form

\mu^{*}=(1-q)\mu^{*}_{1}+q\mu^{*}_{2},

where
\mu^{*}_{1}\in\mathcal{P}([-1,\beta])
and
\mu^{*}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}([\beta,1])
. In order to characterize the measure
\mu^{*}
, we refer to
[
18
, Theorem VIII.2.1]
, which states that a measure
\mu
is globally optimal if and only if each component of
\mu
is optimal when the other one is kept fixed and regarded as external field.
Although the theorem is originally formulated for the case of closed sets of positive distance from one another, it is also valid in our setting, when two closed intervals have disjoint interiors.
According to
[
18
, Theorem VIII.2.1]
, there exist constants
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
, such that the potential of the measure
\mu^{*}
satisfies

U^{\mu^{*}}(z)\geq F_{1},\;\;\textnormal{q.e. on}\;[-1,\beta],\;\;\;\;\;U^{\mu^{*}}(z)=F_{1},\;\;\textnormal{q.e. on}\;\textnormal{supp}\>\mu^{*}_{1},

(22)

U^{\mu^{*}}(z)\geq F_{2},\;\;\textnormal{q.e. on}\;[\beta,1],\;\;\;\;\;U^{\mu^{*}}(z)=F_{2},\;\;\textnormal{q.e. on}\;\textnormal{supp}\>\mu^{*}_{2}.

(23)

We remark that a similar reasoning can be applied to characterize the optimal measure
\nu^{*}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
in Problem II.

Our next goal is to show that the measure
\mu^{*}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the measure
\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}
, where
\sigma_{1}
,
\sigma_{2}
are (unconstrained) equilibrium measures on
[-1,\beta]
and
[\beta,1]
, respectively, using the following result of de La Vallée Poussin.

Theorem 3.3
.

(see
[
18
, Theorem IV.4.5.]
)

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two measures of compact support, and let
\Omega
be a domain in which both
U^{\mu}
,
U^{\nu}
are finite and satisfy with some constant
c
the inequality

U^{\mu}(z)\leq U^{\nu}(z)+c,\;\;\;z\in\Omega.

(24)

If
A
is the subset of
\Omega
in which equality holds in (
24
), then
\nu|_{A}\leq\mu|_{A}
; that is, for every Borel set
B\subset A
,
\nu(B)\leq\mu(B)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

The measures
\mu^{*}_{1}
and
\mu^{*}_{2}
that solve Problem I are absolutely continuous with respect to the equilibrium measure
\sigma_{1}
on
[-1,\beta]
and, respectively, equilibrium measure
\sigma_{2}
on
[\beta,1]
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma_{1}\in\mathcal{P}([-1,\beta])
be the (unconstrained) equilibrium measure on
[-1,\beta]
. Then
\sigma_{1}
satisfies

U^{\sigma_{1}}=C,\;\;\textnormal{ on}\;[-1,\beta],

(25)

where
C
is an explicitly known constant.
Combining (
25
) with (
22
), we obtain

U^{\sigma_{1}}=U^{\mu^{*}}+C-F_{1},\;\;\;\textnormal{q.e. on}\;\textnormal{supp}\>\mu^{*}_{1},

U^{\sigma_{1}}\leq U^{\mu^{*}}+C-F_{1},\;\;\;\textnormal{q.e. on}\;[-1,\beta].

(26)

Since
\|\sigma_{1}\|=\|\mu^{*}\|=1
, the principle of domination for logarithmic potentials (see, e.g.
[
18
, Theorem II.3.2]
) implies that the inequality (
26
) holds for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
. By Theorem
3.3
, we have
(1-q)\mu^{*}_{1}\leq\sigma_{1}
. Similarly, we can use the (unconstrained) equilibrium measure
\sigma_{2}\in\mathcal{P}([\beta,1])
on
[\beta,1]
to show that
q\mu^{*}_{2}\leq\sigma_{2}
.
∎

3.2
Proof of Theorem
3.1

Proof.

Step 1.
Determining the support of
\mu^{*}
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}([-1,1])
denote the (unconstrained) equilibrium measure on
[-1,1]
and let
m_{1}:=\sigma([-1,\beta])
,
m_{2}:=\sigma([\beta,1])
. If
q=m_{2}
, then it’s clear that
\mu^{*}=\sigma
and
\textnormal{supp}\;\mu^{*}=[-1,1]
.

We will now assume that
q>m_{2}
(the proof for the case
q<m_{2}
is analogous). We shall show that in this case the support of the minimizer is a union of two intervals,
[-1,\alpha_{0}]\cup[\beta_{0},1]
,
\alpha_{0}<\beta_{0}
. For this purpose, we denote as before the restriction of the measure
\mu^{*}
to
[-1,\beta]
and
[\beta,1]
by
(1-q)\mu^{*}_{1}
and
q\mu^{*}_{2}
, respectively.
First, notice that
U^{\mu^{*}_{2}}(z)
is a convex function on
[-1,\beta)
, which follows immediately from

\frac{d^{2}U^{\mu^{*}_{2}}(z)}{dz^{2}}=\int\frac{d\mu^{*}_{2}(\zeta)}{(z-\zeta)^{2}}\geq 0,\;\;\;\;z\in[-1,\beta).

Since the measure
\mu^{*}_{1}
is the solution to the equilibrium problem on
[-1,\beta]
with the convex external field
U^{\mu^{*}_{2}}(z)
, then
\textnormal{supp}\>\mu^{*}_{1}\cap(-1,\beta)
is an interval (see
[
18
, Theorem IV.1.10]
). By interchanging
\mu^{*}_{1}
and
\mu^{*}_{2}
and repeating the argument, we obtain that
\textnormal{supp}\>\mu^{*}_{2}\cap(\beta,1)
is also an interval. It remains to show that
\alpha_{0}<\beta_{0}
. On the contrary, suppose that
\alpha_{0}=\beta=\beta_{0}
. Then, by (
22
) and (
23
), we have
U^{\mu^{*}}(z)=F_{1}=F_{2}
on
[-1,1]
, which implies
\mu^{*}=\sigma
and contradicts the assumption
q>m_{2}
.

Step 2.
Finding the density function of the minimizing measure.

We are going to apply the standard technique of analyzing the Cauchy transform of the measure
\mu^{*}
(see, for example,
[
18
, Section VIII.5]
). Namely, consider the function

H(z)=\int\frac{d\mu^{*}(\zeta)}{z-\zeta}

(27)

on the Riemann sphere
\mathbb{\overline{C}}
cut along
\textnormal{supp}\>\mu^{*}=[-1,\alpha_{0}]\cup[\beta_{0},1]
. The real part of
H
vanishes on the cut since it is the derivative of the equilibrium potential of
\mu^{*}
and the latter is constant on
\textnormal{supp}\>\mu^{*}
.
Since
H(\overline{z})=\overline{H(z)}
and
H(z)
is continuous on
(-1,\alpha_{0})\cup(\beta_{0},1)
, the function
H^{2}(z)
is analytic on
(-1,\alpha_{0})\cup(\beta_{0},1)
and therefore analytic on
\mathbb{\overline{C}}\setminus\{-1,\alpha_{0},\beta_{0},1\}
. Using Lemma
3.4
one can easily verify that
H^{2}(z)
can have at most simple poles at the points
\{-1,\alpha_{0},\beta_{0},1\}
, and so
H^{2}(z)
is a rational function on
\mathbb{\overline{C}}
. Moreover, because
H^{2}(z)\sim\frac{1}{z^{2}}
when
z\rightarrow\infty
, we must have

H^{2}(z)=\frac{(z-A)(z-B)}{(z+1)(z-\alpha_{0})(z-\beta_{0})(z-1)},\;\;\;A,B\in\mathbb{R},

and thus

H(z)=\bigg(\frac{(z-A)(z-B)}{(z+1)(z-\alpha_{0})(z-\beta_{0})(z-1)}\bigg)^{1/2},

where we take the branch of the square root that is positive for positive
z
.

The function
H(z)
on the upper part of the cut has the form

H(z)=\frac{-i\sqrt{|z-A||z-B|}}{\sqrt{|(z+1)(z-\alpha_{0})(z-\beta_{0})(z-1)|}},\;\;\;z\in\textnormal{supp}\>\mu^{*}.

For
z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\textnormal{supp}\>\mu^{*}
the Cauchy’s formula gives

H(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\textnormal{supp}\>\mu^{*}}\frac{H(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}d\zeta=\frac{1}{\pi i}\int_{\textnormal{supp}\>\mu^{*}}\frac{H(y)}{y-z}dy,

(28)

where the second integral is taken on the upper part of the cut. Consequently, (
27
) together with (
28
) imply

d\mu^{*}(y)=\frac{\sqrt{|y-A||y-B|}}{\pi\sqrt{|(y+1)(y-\alpha_{0})(y-\beta_{0})(y-1)}|}dy.

(29)

Our next goal is to show that in the expression (
29
) of the density function
d\mu^{*}(y)
the constants
A,B
must be equal to
\alpha_{0}
. Indeed, if we assume that
A\neq\alpha_{0}
or
B\neq\alpha_{0}
and then consider for
x\in(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})

\frac{dU^{\mu^{*}}(x)}{dx}=-\frac{1}{\pi}\displaystyle\int\limits_{[-1,\alpha_{0}]\cup[\beta_{0},1]}\frac{1}{x-y}\frac{\sqrt{|y-A||y-B|}}{\sqrt{|(y+1)(y-\alpha_{0})(y-\beta_{0})(y-1)}|}dy,

it’s easy to see that
\frac{dU^{\mu^{*}}(x)}{dx}\rightarrow-\infty
when
x\rightarrow\alpha_{0}^{+}
.
If
\alpha_{0}<\beta
, then this contradicts (
22
), and we conclude that
A=B=\alpha_{0}
. Next, we show that the case
\alpha_{0}=\beta
is impossible under the assumption
q>m_{2}
. To show this, assume the contrary,
\alpha_{0}=\beta
.
Then it implies that
A,B
in (
29
) should be equal to
\beta_{0}
, since otherwise we would have
\frac{dU^{\mu^{*}}(x)}{dx}\rightarrow\infty
when
x\rightarrow\beta_{0}^{-}
, that contradicts (
23
). Thus, the density function in this case is

d\mu^{*}(y)=\frac{\sqrt{|y-\beta_{0}|}}{\pi\sqrt{(y+1)(y-\alpha_{0})(y-1)}}dy,

and, since
\alpha_{0}\leq\beta_{0}
,

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\beta_{0}}^{1}\frac{\sqrt{|y-\beta_{0}|}}{\sqrt{(y+1)(y-\alpha_{0})(y-1)}}dy\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\beta_{0}}^{1}\frac{dy}{\sqrt{(y+1)(y-1)}}=m_{2},

that contradicts the assumption
\mu^{*}([\beta,1])=q>m_{2}
and proves that we necessarily have
\alpha_{0}<\beta
as well as
\beta_{0}=\beta
, that completes the proof.

∎

3.3
Proof of Theorem
1.4

Proof.

For a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}([-1,1])
with density
f(x)
, consider its logarithmic potential

U^{\mu}(z)=\int_{-1}^{1}\log\frac{1}{|z-x|}f(x)dx

(30)

Consider the Joukowski map
z=\Psi(\zeta):=\frac{1}{2}(\zeta+\zeta^{-1})
that maps the exterior of the unit circle, conformally to
\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1]
. We extend continuously the function
\Psi(\zeta)
to the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
and use the same notation for the extension. For
z=\Psi(\zeta)\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1]
the potential (
30
) can be written as

\begin{split}U^{\mu}(z)&=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\log\frac{1}{|\Psi(\zeta)-\Psi(e^{i\psi})|}\widetilde{f}(\psi)d\psi\\
&=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\log\bigg|\frac{\zeta-e^{i\psi}}{\Psi(\zeta)-\Psi(e^{i\psi})}\bigg|\widetilde{f}(\psi)d\psi+\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\log\frac{1}{|\zeta-e^{i\psi}|}\widetilde{f}(\psi)d\psi,\end{split}

(31)

where
x=e^{i\psi}
and
\widetilde{f}
is an even function on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
with
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\widetilde{f}d\psi=1
.
Let us denote
d\nu(e^{i\psi}):=\widetilde{f}(\psi)d\psi
. Then we have

U^{\mu}(z)=h(\zeta)+U^{\nu}(\zeta),\;\;z=\Psi(\zeta)\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1].

Since the function

g(\omega)=\log\bigg|\frac{\omega-\zeta}{\Psi(\omega)-\Psi(\zeta)}\bigg|

is harmonic in
\Delta:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|>1\}
and continuous on the boundary, we have

\begin{split}\underset{\zeta\rightarrow e^{i\varphi}}{\lim}h(\zeta)&=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\log\bigg|\frac{e^{i\varphi}-e^{i\psi}}{\Psi(e^{i\varphi})-\Psi(e^{i\psi})}\bigg|\widetilde{f}(\psi)d\psi\\
&=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\log\frac{1}{|e^{-i\varphi}-e^{i\psi}|}\widetilde{f}(\psi)d\psi+\log 2.\end{split}

(32)

If
U^{\mu}(z)
is continuous in
\mathbb{C}
, then in view of (
32
) we have

U^{\mu}(\Psi(e^{i\varphi}))=\underset{z\rightarrow\Psi(e^{i\varphi})}{\lim}U^{\mu}(z)=\underset{\zeta\rightarrow e^{i\varphi}}{\lim}(h(\zeta)+U^{\nu}(\zeta))=2U^{\nu}(e^{i\varphi})+\log 2,

(33)

where the last equality follows from (
32
) together with the fact that
U^{\nu}(\zeta)=U^{\nu}(\overline{\zeta})
,
\zeta\in\overline{\Delta}
.

Now, if we take
\mu
above to be the measure from (
19
) with
\beta=\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})
and
q\geq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\beta}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx=\frac{\theta}{2\pi}
, then it’s clear that the potential
U^{\mu^{*}}(z)
is continuous in
\mathbb{C}
, and

U^{\mu^{*}}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{S}\log\frac{1}{|z-\Psi(e^{i\psi})|}\frac{\sqrt{|\cos(\psi)-\alpha|}}{\sqrt{|\cos(\psi)-\beta|}}d\psi,

where the set
S:=A_{\theta}\cup\{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}:\arccos(\alpha)\leq\arg\;z\leq 2\pi-\arccos(\alpha)\}
, and
\alpha
is defined by (
8
). Consequently, (
33
) and
[
18
, Theorem VIII.2.1]
imply that the measure
\nu^{*}
, where
d\nu^{*}(e^{i\psi})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\sqrt{|\cos(\psi)-\alpha|}}{\sqrt{|\cos(\psi)-\beta|}}d\psi
, is the solution to the Problem II.
∎
