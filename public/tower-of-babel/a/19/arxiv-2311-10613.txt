Simulating photonic devices with noisy optical elements Preprint: APS/123-QED
Michele Vischi ⋄, Giovanni Di Bartolomeo ⋄, Massimiliano Proietti, Seid Koudia, Filippo Cerocchi, Massimiliano Dispenza, Angelo Bassi

Abstract
Quantum computers are inherently affected by noise. While in the long-term error correction codes will account for noise at the cost of increasing physical qubits, in the near-term the performance of any quantum algorithm should be tested and simulated in the presence of noise. As noise acts on the hardware, the classical simulation of a quantum algorithm should not be agnostic on the platform used for the computation. In this work, we apply the recently proposed noisy gates approach to efficiently simulate noisy optical circuits described in the dual rail framework. The evolution of the state vector is simulated directly, without requiring the mapping to the density matrix framework. Notably, we test the method on both the gate-based and measurement-based quantum computing models, showing that the approach is very versatile. We also evaluate the performance of a photonic variational quantum algorithm to solve the MAX- 2 2 -CUT problem. In particular we design and simulate an ansatz which is resilient to photon losses up to p ∼ 10 − 3 \mathrm{p}\sim 10^{-3} making it relevant for near term applications.

I
Introduction

Photonic quantum devices are very promising potential candidates for achieving quantum advantage
Madsen et al. 2022
;
Zhong et al. 2020
: integrated photonic benefits from low error rates and ease of manipulation, making it eligible for scalable quantum computation
Mezher and Mansfield 2022
;
Maring et al. 2023
. However, like all quantum computing platforms, photonic devices in the NISQ era are affected by noise from various sources, such as photon losses and imperfect indistinguishability of single photons, that can negatively impact the performance of quantum algorithms
Mezher and Mansfield 2022
;
Pont et al. 2022
;
Czerwinski and Czerwinska 2022
. Therefore, studying the noise effects on quantum algorithms and perform accurate classical simulations becomes crucial for their successful deployment
Niedermeier et al. 2023
;
Wright et al. 2022
;
Cheng et al. 2021
. This task can be achieved by employing efficient classical simulation methods based on reliable noise model.

Building on the results presented in
Di Bartolomeo et al. 2023
, we develop the noisy gates approach for optical devices in the dual-rail encoding. The distinctive feature of this approach is that the noise is integrated in the dynamics defining the quantum gates—which turn into noisy gates—offering in this way a superior tool for simulating the behavior of a (unavoidably) noisy quantum device. Here we extend the formalism to second quantization for optical elements
Kok et al. 2006
;
Knill et al. 2000
, and apply it to specific instances of dual-rail encoding, showing that it is a very flexible and efficient framework for simulating noisy optical computers.

We successfully formulate the noisy versions of linear optical elements as phase shifters and beam splitters; we also consider the effects of imperfect single-photon sources, lossy optical guides and detectors as fictitious noisy optical elements that act on photons at the proper step of the circuit. The approach is first tested through simulations of both gate based and measurement based quantum circuits, whereby the same algorithm is expressed in the dual-rail formalism. Then, the approach is applied to a specific variational algorithm solving an optimisation problem, through the study of noise effects on the optimization loop of a variational quantum algorithm.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
we recap the nosy gates formalism. In Sec.
III
we review the main sources of noise in optical devices. Sec.
IV
and
V
present the general derivation of noisy optical elements, imperfect single-photon source effects, lossy optical guides and detectors. In Sec.
VI
we compare our method with previuos existing ones. In Sec.
VII
we report the results of the simulations of X gate and Bell state preparation circuit in the gate based quantum computing framework, of the X gate in the measurement based framework and of a variational quantum algorithm applied to the max 2-cut problem. We conclude with some general remarks and an outlook.

II
The Noisy gates formalism

Noise models for quantum computing typically consists in adding extra gates, mimicking the noise, before and after the standard gates defining a circuit
Benenti et al. 2019
;
Georgopoulos et al. 2021
;
rig 2022
.
The noise gate approach developed in
Di Bartolomeo et al. 2023
instead combines the gate and the noise into a single noisy gate, which represents a more realistic description of the actual functioning of a noisy quantum device. As a matter of fact, this approach proved to offer a better simulation of superconducting quantum computers. We briefly present its building blocks.

Noises affect quantum devices and their effects can be described by the theory of open quantum systems
Breuer et al. 2002
;
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
. The master equation that describes the Markovian evolution of the density matrix of the qubits system in a given environment is

\frac{\!\operatorname{d}\!}{\!\operatorname{d}\!t}\hat{\mathrm{\rho}}_{t}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{\mathrm{H}}_{t},\hat{\rho}_{t}]+\epsilon^{2}\sum_{k}\biggl[\hat{\mathrm{L}}_{k}\hat{\mathrm{\rho}}_{t}\hat{\mathrm{L^{\dagger}}}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{\mathrm{L^{\dagger}}}_{k}\hat{\mathrm{L}}_{k},\hat{\mathrm{\rho}}_{t}\}\biggr],

(1)

where
\hat{\mathrm{H}}_{t}
is the time dependent Hamiltonian realizing a given gate, and
\hat{\mathrm{L}}_{k}
are the Lindblad operators capturing the action of the environment. In principle, in Eq.(
1
) each term
\hat{\mathrm{L}}_{k}
should correspond to a different parameter
\epsilon^{2}_{k}
; however we assume that the noise strengths are of the same order of magnitude, thus for our purposes we consider a single parameter
\epsilon^{2}
.

Given that
\epsilon^{2}
is much weaker than the strength of the Hamiltonian, one usually assumes that the noises are totally independent from the gates dynamics, allowing to formally decouple the two terms in Eq. (
1
). In
Di Bartolomeo et al. 2023
we showed that the assumption underlying this approximation is too tight and one can obtain better results by solving a stochastic differential equation, equivalent to Eq. (
1
), perturbatively to the second order. The approach is the following: we perform a linear stochastic unraveling of the Lindbald equation
Jacobs and Knight 1998
;
Caiaffa et al. 2017
;
Bassi 2003
;
Jacobs 2014
;
Wiseman and Milburn 2009
, in terms of the following stochastic differential equation for the statevector

\!\operatorname{d}\!\ket{\psi_t}=\biggl[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{\mathrm{H}}_{t}\!\operatorname{d}\!t+\sum_{k}\biggl(i\epsilon\hat{\mathrm{L}}_{k}\!\operatorname{d}\!\mathrm{W}_{k}(t)-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\hat{\mathrm{L^{\dagger}}}_{k}\hat{\mathrm{L}}_{k}\!\operatorname{d}\!t\biggr)\biggr]\ket{\psi_t}\,,

(2)

where
\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W}_{k}(t)
are differentials of standard independent Wiener processes, i.e. stochastic infinitesimal increments such that
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W}_{k}(t)\big]=0
and
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W}_{k}(t)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{W}_{k}(t^{\prime})\big]=\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}t
. Eq. (
2
) is an unraveling of the Lindblad equation in the sense that the density matrix obtained by averaging the pure states
\ket{\psi_t}\bra{\psi_t}
over the noise

\hat{\mathrm{\rho}}_{t}=\mathbb{E}\Big[\ket{\psi_t}\bra{\psi_t}\Big],

(3)

is a solution of Eq. (
1
). In this sense, Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
) have the same physical content.

Moreover Eq. (
2
) is linear, thus the solution can be written as

\ket{\psi_t}=\hat{\mathrm{N}}_{g}(t,t_{0})\ket{\psi_{t_{0}}},

(4)

where
\hat{\mathrm{N}}_{g}(t,t_{0})
is a linear operator, implying that it can be interpreted as a noisy quantum gate

In
Di Bartolomeo et al. 2023
we showed how to solve Eq. (
2
) approximately to order
O(\epsilon^{2})
by using perturbative methods for stochastic differential equations
Gardiner et al. 1985
. The resulting expression for the noisy gates is the following

\hat{\mathrm{N}}_{g}(t,t_{0})=\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{g}(t,t_{0})e^{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\hat{\mathrm{D}}(t,t_{0})}e^{i\epsilon\hat{\mathrm{S}}(t,t_{0})},

(5)

where we defined the deterministic operator

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{D}}(t,t_{0})=\sum_{k}\hat{\mathrm{D}}_{k}(t,t_{0}),

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{D}}_{k}(t,t_{0})=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\bigg(\hat{\mathrm{L}}^{\dagger}_{k}(s)\hat{\mathrm{L}}_{k}(s)-\hat{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{k}(s)\bigg)

(7)

and the stochastic one

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{S}}(t,t_{0})=\sum_{k}\hat{\mathrm{S}}_{k}(t,t_{0}),

(8)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{S}}_{k}(t,t_{0})=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d\mathrm{W}_{k}(s)}\hat{\mathrm{L}}_{k}(s).

(9)

We notice that in Eq. (
5
) the Lindblad operators
\hat{\mathrm{L}}_{k}(s)
are in the interaction picture,
\hat{\mathrm{L}}_{k}(s)=\hat{\mathrm{U}}^{\dagger}_{g}(s,t_{0})\hat{\mathrm{L}}_{k}\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{g}(s,t_{0})
, thus since they depend on the gate operator, the noise dynamics is influenced by the unitary evolution realized by
\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{g}
. Moreover, the real part
[\hat{\mathrm{S}}^{\text{\tiny}{R}}_{k}(t,t_{0})]_{ij}
and the imaginary part
[\hat{\mathrm{S}}^{\text{\tiny}{I}}_{k}(t,t_{0})]_{ij}
of the entries of the operators
\hat{\mathrm{S}}_{k}
in Eq. (
9
) are Itô integrals of deterministic functions:
[\hat{\mathrm{S}}^{m}_{k}(t,t_{0})]_{ij}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\!\operatorname{d}\!\mathrm{W}_{k}(s)[\hat{\mathrm{L}}^{m}_{k}(s)]_{ij}
for
m=\text{\tiny}{R},\tiny{I}
. The latter represent Gaussian stochastic processes with means zero
\mathbb{E}\big[[\hat{\mathrm{S}}^{m}_{k}(t,t_{0})]_{ij}\big]=0
, variances
\mathbb{V}\bigl[[\hat{\mathrm{S}}^{m}_{k}(t,t_{0})]_{ij}\bigr]=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\!\operatorname{d}\!s([\hat{\mathrm{L}}^{m}_{k}(s)]_{ij})^{2}
and covariances
\mathbb{E}\bigl[[\hat{\mathrm{S}}^{m}_{k}(t,t_{0})]_{ij}[\hat{\mathrm{S}}^{n}_{k}(t,t_{0})]_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\bigr]=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\!\operatorname{d}\!s[\hat{\mathrm{L}}^{m}_{k}(s)]_{ij}[\hat{\mathrm{L}}^{n}_{k}(s)]_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}
.
Once all the variances and covariances are computed, the stochastic processes can be sampled to generate a single realization of the noisy gate
\hat{\mathrm{N}}_{g}
. By averaging over the number
\mathrm{N}_{\text{\tiny samples}}
of realizations of the final state
\ket{\psi_t}_{k}=\hat{\mathrm{N}}_{g}\ket{\psi_{t_0}}
, one recovers the final density matrix of the system:

\hat{\mathrm{\rho}}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}_{\text{\tiny samples}}}\sum_{k}\ket{\psi_t}_{k}\bra{\psi_t}_{k}.

(10)

It is then sufficient to specify the Hamiltonians, i.e. the unitaries, and the Lindblad operators of a given quantum device to find the expressions of the noisy gates. In the following we specialize to linear optical elements.

III
Noises in dual-rail encoding optical circuits

This work focuses on discrete variable quantum optics
Scully and Zubairy 1999
;
Vogel and Welsch 2006
, in particular on the so called dual-rail encoding of linear optics quantum computing
Burgarth and Giovannetti 2013
;
Burgarth and Bose 2005
, where the computational units are qubits. We consider optical circuits that are essentially made up of single photon sources, single-mode optical guides (also called spatial modes) in which photons propagate, linear optical elements that modify the photons state and single photon detectors. Thus, an optical circuit can be thought as a unitary transformation on
N
modes with symmetry group
SU(N)
, followed by the detection. Since a qubit has a
SU(2)
symmetry, then two spatial optical modes (dual-rail) with a single photon is a natural implementation of a qubit.

The logical states of the qubit are encoded in the two modes Fock states
\ket{0}_{L}=\ket{10}
,
\ket{1}_{L}=\ket{01}
(with this notation we indicate that one of the two modes is occupied by the photon). Thus to represent a system of
M
qubits one needs
2M
modes plus a certain number of additional ancillary modes used to implement the correct unitary transformation.

Although it is believed that optical quantum computing is very promising because of the weak interaction of photons with their surrounding environment, optical quantum circuits are not immune to noise.
We focus on two main sources of noise affecting optical devices: photon losses and non-perfect indistinguishability of single photons
Mezher and Mansfield 2022
;
Pont et al. 2022
.

Photon losses
are particularly daunting in the dual rail encoding, since after a loss the photon state exits from the logical computational states of the single qubit. They can occur at any step of the optical circuit, from the source to optical guides, optical elements and detectors. For each of these steps, one can associate a different loss probability
\mathrm{p}=1-e^{-\Delta t/T}
, where
\Delta t
is the time interval and
T
is a characteristic time. Indeed the state of a photonic mode traveling through a fiber can be expressed as
Czerwinski and Czerwinska 2022
;
Czerwinski and Szlachetka 2022
;
Mielczarek 2018

\hat{\rho}(\Delta t)=\mathrm{p}\ket{0}\bra{0}+(1-\mathrm{p})\ket{1}\bra{1}

(11)

where
\ket{0}
is the state of a single mode without photons and
\ket{1}
is the state of a single mode occupied by the photon. In practice, the value of p depends specifically on the material used for the optical circuit as well as other experimental parameters. In the following, for the purposes of this work, we vary
\mathrm{p}
from
10^{-4}
to
10^{-2}
.

Photons non-perfect indistinguishability
occurs at the stage of photon/qubits generation. Single photons emitted for example by quantum dots
Jacak et al. 2013
are indistinguishable if they have the same wavelength, polarization, temporal and spatial extent. A quantum dot sources indistinguishable photons if it is subject only to radiative decay. If there is any other decoherence process, then sourced photons will be not perfectly indistinguishable. For this reason we simulate indistinguishably using decoherence models.
Typical decoherence processes in semiconductor platforms are depolarization or dephasing
Cogan et al. 2018
;
Schneider et al. 2004

and for this reason somehow photons non-perfect indistinguishability is connected to depolarization/dephasing errors because photons are in turn coupled to an electronic spin state that is susceptible to such errors.

We notice that photon losses and non-perfect indistinguishability lead to incoherent evolution. Here we do not consider coherent errors caused by imperfect calibration of optical elements. The latter can be accounted for using already existing techniques
Pai et al. 2019
. Other sources of error, e.g. cross-talk effects, are beyond the scope of this work and can be added with further developments.

IV
Noisy optical elements

We now apply the theory outlined in the previous sections to build the noisy version of linear optical elements. In particular we focus on phase shifters and beam splitters, showing how to write down their noisy version under the effect of photon loss errors.

IV.1
Noiseless phase shifter and beam splitter

We briefly recap the noiseless expression of optical elements in order to set the main notation used in the following sections.
A phase shifter is an optical element that acts on a single photon mode by changing the phase of the photon state. Its action can be described by the Hamiltonian
Kok et al. 2006
;
Knill et al. 2000

\hat{\mathrm{H}}_{\mathrm{P}}=-\hbar\omega\hat{\mathrm{P}},\qquad\hat{\mathrm{P}}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a},

(12)

where
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a^{\dagger}}
are respectively the annihilation and creation operators of a photon in a single mode. By solving the Schrödinger equation one arrives at the unitary evolution operator
\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{P}}=e^{i\theta\hat{\mathrm{P}}}
,
where
\theta=\omega\cdot(t-t_{0})=\omega\cdot\Delta t
. Thus, the evolution of the annihilation and creation operators is given by

\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{P}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\dagger}=e^{i\theta}\hat{a}^{\dagger},\qquad\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{P}}\hat{a}\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\dagger}=e^{-i\theta}\hat{a}.

(13)

The beam splitter acts on two photon modes
and can be defined through the Hamiltonian
Kok et al. 2006
;
Knill et al. 2000

\hat{\mathrm{H}}_{\mathrm{B}}=-\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\hat{\mathrm{B}}(\phi),\quad\hat{\mathrm{B}}(\phi)=e^{-i\phi}\hat{a}_{0}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}+e^{i\phi}\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{1},

(14)

where the indices of the annihilation and creation operators refer to photon modes 0 and 1.
The unitary evolution operator is
\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{B}}=e^{i\frac{\theta}{2}\hat{\mathrm{B}}(\phi)}
,
with
\theta=\omega\Delta t
; the annihilation/creation operators evolve as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{B}}\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\dagger}=\cos{(\theta/2)}\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}+ie^{-i\phi}\sin{(\theta/2)}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger},

(15)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{B}}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\dagger}=ie^{i\phi}\sin{(\theta/2)}\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}+\cos{(\theta/2)}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger},

(16)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{B}}\hat{a}_{0}\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\dagger}=\cos{(\theta/2)}\hat{a}_{0}-ie^{i\phi}\sin{(\theta/2)}\hat{a}_{1},

(17)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{B}}\hat{a}_{1}\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\dagger}=-ie^{-i\phi}\sin{(\theta/2)}\hat{a}_{0}+\cos{(\theta/2)}\hat{a}_{1}.

(18)

It is possible to express in a compact way these latter transformations by defining unitary matrices that act on vectors of creation operators
(\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger},\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger})^{T}
or annihilation operators
(\hat{a}_{0},\hat{a}_{1})^{T}
. The matrix for creation operators reads

\hat{\mathrm{\mathcal{U}}}_{\mathrm{B}}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos{(\theta/2)}&ie^{-i\phi}\sin{(\theta/2)}\\
ie^{i\phi}\sin{(\theta/2)}&\cos{(\theta/2)}\end{pmatrix},

(19)

while that for annihilation operators is
\hat{\mathrm{\mathcal{U}}}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\dagger}
.

IV.2
Noisy phase shifter

A natural way to describe the effects of photon losses on a given mode
0
in the noisy gates formalism is to consider the system in contact with an environment that absorbs photons. By following the protocol described in Sec.
II
we have to choose the operators in Eq. (
2
): the Hamiltonian operator is the phase shifter Hamiltonian in Eq. (
12
) on mode 0, i.e.
\hat{\mathrm{P}}_{0}=\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{0}
, while we choose
\hat{a}_{0}
as Lindblad operator
\hat{\mathrm{L}}
.
The corresponding expression for the noisy gate reads

\hat{\mathrm{N}}_{0}=\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{0}e^{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}_{0}}{2}\hat{\mathrm{D}}_{0}}e^{i\epsilon_{0}\hat{\mathrm{S}}_{0}},

(20)

where
\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{0}=e^{i\theta\hat{\mathrm{P}}_{0}}
,
\hat{\mathrm{D}}_{0}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{ds}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{0}(s)\hat{a}_{0}(s)-\hat{a}^{2}_{0}(s))
and
\hat{\mathrm{S}}_{0}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d\mathrm{W}(s)}\hat{a}_{0}(s)
.
The time dependent creation operator reads

\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{0}(s)=\hat{\mathrm{U}}^{\dagger}_{0}(s,t_{0})\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{0}\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{0}(s,t_{0})=e^{-i\theta(s,t_{0})}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{0}

(21)

similarly to Eq.(
13
). Thus we can write

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{D}}_{0}=\Delta t\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{0}\hat{a}_{0}+(\mathrm{D}_{C}-i\mathrm{D}_{S})\hat{a}^{2}_{0}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{S}}_{0}=(\mathrm{I}_{C}-i\mathrm{I}_{S})\hat{a}_{0}

(22)

where
\mathrm{D}_{C}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{ds}\cos{2\theta(s,t_{0})}
,
\mathrm{D}_{S}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{ds}\sin{2\theta(s,t_{0})}
,
\mathrm{I}_{C}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d\mathrm{W}(s)}\cos{\theta(s,t_{0})}
,
\mathrm{I}_{S}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d\mathrm{W}(s)}\sin{\theta(s,t_{0})}
.
The latters are Gaussian stochastic processes with means
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{I}_{C}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{I}_{S}\big]=0
, variances
\mathbb{V}\bigl[\mathrm{I}_{C}\bigr]=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\!\operatorname{d}\!s\cos^{2}{\theta(s,t_{0})}
,
\mathbb{V}\bigl[\mathrm{I}_{S}\bigr]=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\!\operatorname{d}\!s\sin^{2}{\theta(s,t_{0})}
and covariance
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{I}_{C}\mathrm{I}_{S}\big]=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\!\operatorname{d}\!s\cos{\theta(s,t_{0})}\sin{\theta(s,t_{0})}
.

However, at this point a difficulty arises because
\hat{\mathrm{N}}_{0}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{0}\hat{\mathrm{N}}^{\dagger}_{0}
cannot be expressed explicitly as a finite function of the creation operator only.

In order to avoid such problem we consider a larger physical system consisting of two photon modes: mode 0 is the physical mode where the phase shifter acts, while mode 1 is a virtual mode that we exploit to model photon losses. The idea is to model the environment as a beam splitter between the two modes, and then trace out the virtual mode. In such a way, the effective action on the system is that the photon is absorbed with some probability. In this new configuration, we choose
\hat{\mathrm{B}}_{01}=\hat{a}_{0}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}+\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{1}
as Lindblad operator
\hat{\mathrm{L}}
; the latter can be obtained from the expression for
\hat{\mathrm{B}}(\phi)
in Eq. (
14
) by setting
\phi=0
. This Lindblad operator acts as a virtual beam splitter that can shift photons from mode 0 to the virtual mode 1.

Following Sec.
II
, the resulting noisy phase shifter is of the form in Eq. (
5
) without the deterministic term due to the fact that the Lindblad operator
\hat{\mathrm{B}}_{01}
that we chose is Hermitian (see also Eq.(
7
)). Then we can write

\hat{\mathrm{N}}_{01}=\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{0}e^{i\epsilon_{0}\hat{\mathrm{S}}_{01}},

(23)

where
\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{0}=e^{i\theta\hat{\mathrm{P}}_{0}}
and
\hat{\mathrm{S}}_{01}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d\mathrm{W}(s)}\hat{\mathrm{B}}_{01}(s)
.

At this point we need to compute the form of the stochastic operator
\hat{\mathrm{S}}_{01}
. We notice that

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{B}}_{01}(s)=\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{0}^{\dagger}(s,t_{0})\hat{\mathrm{B}}_{01}\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{0}(s,t_{0})

(24)

\displaystyle=e^{-i\theta(s,t_{0})\hat{\mathrm{P}}_{0}}\hat{a}_{0}e^{i\theta(s,t_{0})\hat{\mathrm{P}}_{0}}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}+e^{-i\theta(s,t_{0})\hat{\mathrm{P}}_{0}}\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}e^{i\theta(s,t_{0})\hat{\mathrm{P}}_{0}}\hat{a}_{1}

\displaystyle=e^{i\theta(s,t_{0})}\hat{a}_{0}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}+e^{-i\theta(s,t_{0})}\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{1}

\displaystyle=\cos{\theta(s,t_{0})}(\hat{a}_{0}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}+\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{1})+i\sin{\theta(s,t_{0})}(\hat{a}_{0}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}-\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{1})

\displaystyle=\cos{\theta(s,t_{0})}\hat{\mathrm{B}}_{01}-\sin{\theta(s,t_{0})}\hat{\mathrm{C}}_{01}

where in the third line we used Eq.(
13
) with the adjoint unitary evolution and we define
\hat{\mathrm{C}}_{01}=i(\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{1}-\hat{a}_{0}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger})
, which can be obtained from
\hat{\mathrm{B}}(\phi)
in Eq.(
14
) by choosing
\phi=\pi/2
.

Then the stochastic operator reads

\hat{\mathrm{S}}_{01}=\mathrm{I}_{C}\hat{\mathrm{B}}_{01}-\mathrm{I}_{S}\hat{\mathrm{C}}_{01},

(25)

where
\mathrm{I}_{C}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d\mathrm{W}(s)}\cos{\theta(s,t_{0})}
and
\mathrm{I}_{S}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d\mathrm{W}(s)}\sin{\theta(s,t_{0})}
. The latters are the same stochastic integrals defined before.

Finally, by exploiting the fact that at second order in
\epsilon
one has
e^{\epsilon(\hat{\mathrm{A}}+\hat{\mathrm{B}})}=e^{\epsilon\hat{\mathrm{A}}}e^{\epsilon\hat{\mathrm{B}}}
, from Eq. (
23
) we get

\hat{\mathrm{N}}_{01}=\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{0}e^{i\epsilon_{0}\mathrm{I}_{C}\hat{\mathrm{B}}_{01}}e^{-i\epsilon_{0}\mathrm{I}_{S}\hat{\mathrm{C}}_{01}}.

(26)

Through Eq. (
26
) one can compute the evolution of creation and annihilation operators under the action of a noisy phase shifter. As seen before, we can express compactly these transformations with unitary matrices that act on vectors of creation operators. In the case of the noisy phase shifter we have

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{\mathcal{N}}}_{01}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\cos{(\epsilon_{0}\rr{I_{C}})}&i\sin{(\epsilon_{0}\rr{I_{C}})}\\
i\sin{(\epsilon_{0}\rr{I_{C}})}&\cos{(\epsilon_{0}\rr{I_{C}})}\end{pmatrix}\cdot

(27)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\cos{(\epsilon_{0}\rr{I_{S}})}&-\sin{(\epsilon_{0}\rr{I_{S}})}\\
\sin{(\epsilon_{0}\rr{I_{S}})}&\cos{(\epsilon_{0}\rr{I_{S}})}\end{pmatrix}\,,

whereas
\hat{\mathrm{\mathcal{N}}}^{\dagger}_{01}
acts on annihilation operators. In Eq. (
27
) the parameter
\epsilon_{0}
is connected to photon loss probability
\mathrm{p_{0}}
on mode 0 as
\epsilon_{0}=\sqrt{-\log{(1-2\rr{p}_{0})}/2}
where
\mathrm{p}_{0}=(1-e^{-2\Delta t/T})/2
and
\epsilon_{0}^{2}=\Delta t/T
, with
T
the characteristic time of photon losses. We choose such time dependence for
\mathrm{p}_{0}
because by taking the average over the processes
\mathrm{I}_{C}
and
\mathrm{I}_{S}
one has

\mathbb{E}[\sin^{2}(\epsilon\mathrm{I}_{k})]=(1-e^{-2\epsilon_{0}^{2}})/2=\mathrm{p}_{0}
with
k=C,S
. Moreover, we are not interested in the regime in which there is more than 50% of loss probability.

By tracing out the virtual mode we get the expression for the noisy phase shifter

\hat{\mathrm{\mathcal{N}}}_{0}=e^{i\theta}\cos{(\epsilon_{0}\rr{I_{C}})}\cos{(\epsilon_{0}\rr{I_{S}})}

(28)

V
Imperfect single-photon sources, lossy optical guides and detection

As explained in section
III
, photon generation, propagation of photons inside optical guides and detection are also affected by errors.
We now describe how to account for such errors with our formalism.

V.1
Imperfect single-photon sources

As we explained in section
III
a quantum dot undergoing decoherence processes generates non perfect indistinguishable photons. In the case of dual rail encoding, the only relevant property is the photon path, i.e. through which optical guide the photon is traveling. Then, in this setup, the only way to understand whether two photons are distinguishable, if we consider only non-coherent errors, is when the associated qubit state is not completely pure. This can be described by considering depolarizing errors arising at the sources and inherited by the photons.

To model imperfect single-photon sources we apply at the beginning of the optical circuit a layer of fictitious optical elements to each mode that simulate the depolarizing errors. Such optical elements can be built by applying again the noisy gates formalism. Here we focus on single qubit depolarizing channel, but one could straightforwardly extend the approach to multi-qubit depolarizing channel.

Depolarizing for a single spin state can be described by the Lindblad operators
\hat{\mathrm{X}}
,
\hat{\mathrm{Y}}
and
\hat{\mathrm{Z}}
. We can map such operators to operators defined in terms of creation and annihilation operators on the physical modes, thus acting on photons of the circuit. We notice for example that by solving the Schrödinger equation with the beam splitter Hamiltonian in Eq.(
14
) with
\phi=0
and
\theta=\pi
, one gets a unitary on the creation operators that acts as an
\hat{\mathrm{X}}
gate on a qubit in the dual rail encoding (see Eq.(
19
)). Similarly a
\hat{\mathrm{Y}}
is obtained with
\phi=\pi/2
and
\theta=\pi
. The operator
\hat{\mathrm{Z}}
is obtained by solving the Schrödinger equation with the phase shifter Hamiltonian in Eq.(
12
) with
\theta=\pi
applied to mode 1.
For these reasons, to describe depolarizing errors, we can use Eq.(
2
) without Hamiltonian operator and with the Lindblad operators
\hat{\mathrm{B}}_{01}
,
\hat{\mathrm{C}}_{01}
and
\hat{\mathrm{P}}_{1}
.
The resulting noisy gate is:

\hat{\mathrm{N}}^{(\text{dep})}_{01}=e^{i\epsilon_{d}\hat{\mathrm{S}}^{(x)}_{01}}e^{i\epsilon_{d}\hat{\mathrm{S}}^{(y)}_{01}}e^{i\epsilon_{d}\hat{\mathrm{S}}^{(z)}_{01}},

(37)

where
\hat{\mathrm{S}}^{(x)}_{01}=\hat{\mathrm{B}}_{01}\mathrm{W}_{x}(t,t_{0})
,
\hat{\mathrm{S}}^{(y)}_{01}=\hat{\mathrm{C}}_{01}\mathrm{W}_{y}(t,t_{0})
and
\hat{\mathrm{S}}^{(z)}_{01}=\hat{\mathrm{P}}_{1}\mathrm{W}_{z}(t,t_{0})
. The corresponding unitary on the creation and annihilation operators becomes:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{\mathcal{N}}}^{(\text{dep})}_{01}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos{(\epsilon_{d}\rr{W}_x)}&i\sin{(\epsilon_{d}\rr{W}_x)}\\
i\sin{(\epsilon_{d}\rr{W}_x)}&\cos{(\epsilon_{d}\rr{W}_x)}\end{pmatrix}\cdot

(38)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\cos{(\epsilon_{d}\rr{W}_y)}&-\sin{(\epsilon_{d}\rr{W}_{y})}\\
\sin{(\epsilon_{d}\rr{W}_{y})}&\cos{(\epsilon_{d}\rr{W}_{y})}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&e^{i\epsilon_{d}\mathrm{W_{z}}}\end{pmatrix}.

Here the parameter
\epsilon_{d}
has the same definition of the loss parameter but now it is related to depolarizing probability
\mathrm{p}_{d}
. Moreover we stress that, despite the similarity with the noisy gate of lossy optical elements, the effects of Eqs. (
37
), (
38
) are different because modes 0 and 1 are both physical modes, there are not virtual modes.

V.2
Lossy optical guides and detection

In Sec.
IV
we have dealt with optical elements affected by photon losses, however photons inside optical guides and detectors are also subject to this kind of noise. Then, as in the case of noisy phase shifter we can consider two photon modes 0, 1, respectively physical and virtual mode, with Lindblad operator

but this time we use Eq. (

\hat{\mathrm{N}}^{(\text{loss})}_{01}=e^{i\epsilon\hat{\mathrm{S}}_{01}},

(39)

where
\hat{\mathrm{S}}_{01}=\hat{\mathrm{B}}_{01}\mathrm{W}(t,t_{0})
and
\epsilon=\sqrt{-\log{(1-2\rr{p})}/2}
with
\mathrm{p}
the loss probability inside the optical guide or the detector. Consequentially we have:

\hat{\mathrm{\mathcal{N}}}^{(\text{loss})}_{01}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos{(\epsilon\rr{W})}&i\sin{(\epsilon\rr{W})}\\
i\sin{(\epsilon\rr{W})}&\cos{(\epsilon\rr{W})}\end{pmatrix}.

(40)

By tracing out the virtual mode we obtain:

\hat{\mathrm{\mathcal{N}}}^{(\text{loss})}_{0}=\cos{(\epsilon\rr{W})}.

VI
Comparison with other approaches

In the literature, there are two main approaches for simulating photon losses: fixed-loss model
Aaronson and Brod 2016
;
Oszmaniec et al. 2016
;
Oszmaniec and Brod 2018
and beam splitter loss model
Oszmaniec and Brod 2018
;
Brod and Oszmaniec 2020
;
Heurtel et al. 2023a
;
Killoran et al. 2019
. The former one was developed when the number of lost particles can be effectively controlled; indeed it is based on the assumption that initially there are
n
photons and exactly
n-l
of them are lost. The loss is simulated by tracing out
n-l
of the
n
photons
\hat{\rho}^{\prime}=\Tr_{n-l}(\hat{\rho})
and by using the fact that
\Tr_{n-l}(\hat{\mathrm{U}}^{\otimes n}\hat{\rho}\hat{\mathrm{U}}^{\dagger\otimes n})=\hat{\mathrm{U}}^{\otimes n}\Tr_{n-l}(\hat{\rho})\hat{\mathrm{U}}^{\dagger\otimes n}
. Thus, it does not matter whether losses occurred before or after the implemented linear transformation. However, a simulation method that describes a fixed number of lost photons is limited since in real optical circuits this number is a random variable.

In the second approach, the beam splitter loss model is based on the idea of adding a virtual mode to the physical mode and applying a beam splitter (see Eq. (
19
) with
\phi=0
) with a reflectance equal to the loss probability:
\mathrm{R}=\mathrm{p}
. Since the relation between reflectance and the angle
\theta
of a beam splitter is
\cos(\theta/2)=\sqrt{1-\mathrm{R}}
and
\sin(\theta/2)=\sqrt{\mathrm{R}}
, then one gets

\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{1-\mathrm{p}}&i\sqrt{\mathrm{p}}\\
i\sqrt{\mathrm{p}}&\sqrt{1-\mathrm{p}}\end{pmatrix},

(42)

where the probability of losing a photon is exactly
|_{\text{\tiny out}}\!\bra{01}\ket{10}_{\text{\tiny in}}|^{2}=\mathrm{p}
. This approach is very close to what we derived for lossy optical guides and detection in Sec.
V.2
, because Eq. (
40
) is the stochastic version of Eq. (
42
). Indeed, in our case the probability of losing a photon is
|_{\text{\tiny out}}\!\bra{01}\ket{10}_{\text{\tiny in}}|^{2}=\sin^{2}(\epsilon\mathrm{W})
and by taking the average over the Wiener process one has

\mathbb{E}[\sin^{2}(\epsilon\mathrm{W})]=(1-e^{-2\epsilon^{2}})/2=\mathrm{p}
.

This analogy is limited only to lossy optical guides and detectors. Indeed, the stochastic processes appearing in the expressions for noisy optical elements in Eq. (
27
) and (
35
) are not independent from the parameters of the corresponding optical elements. Therefore our approach is not reducible to the beam splitter loss model: as a result of including the noise into the gates, each noisy optical elements has its own lossy behaviour (even if the error probability is the same for all elements). Experimentally the dependence of photon losses on the parameters of optical elements is small and the usual assumption to perform simulations is that photon loss is independent from the angles of beam splitters and phase shifters
Mezher and Mansfield 2022
. However, we already proved in
Di Bartolomeo et al. 2023
, for the case of superconducting devices, that relaxing this assumption, namely that of separating the noise and unitary evolution, provides a more accurate noise simulation tool.

Regarding the treatment of imperfect photon sources, different approaches exist in the literature. In
Osca and Vala 2023
, the authors considered a parametrized quantum dot able to simulate the creation of pairs of entangled photon in a mixed state. Another example is the modeling used in
Per 2022
where an imperfect quantum-dot based single-photon source is modeled by a statistical mixture of Fock states. In this work we deal with single-photon sources thus, following the latter approach, we implemented the stochastic unraveling of a single qubit depolarizing Kraus map as shown in Eq. (
38
), that equivalently outputs a mixed Fock state of the qubits. In general one could use
n
qubits depolarizing channels to take into account multi photon distinguishability
Pont et al. 2022
.

\mathcal{H}(\rho,\sigma)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\big(\sqrt{\rho_{kk}}-\sqrt{\sigma_{kk}}\big)^{2}}\,,

(43)

(

\mathcal{F}=(1-\mathcal{H}^{2})^{2}

The framework of dual rail encoding of linear optics quantum computing is suitable to implement alternative scheme for quantum computation, in particular measurement based quantum computing (MBQC). Such a scheme involves the generation of particular entangled states called cluster states and appropriate single qubit measurement to drive the computation. These two stages involve photon generation, manipulation and detection thus, as for GBQC, MBQC is affected by photon losses and imperfect photon sourcing. To test the robustness of MBQC against such noises, we focus on the realization of X gate.

VII.3
Variational quantum algorithm applied to the max 2-cut problem

We now focus on a VQA algorithm applied to the MAX
2
-CUT problem. The MAX
2
-CUT problem is a well known optimization problem that requires to find a cut dividing the vertices of a graph in two complementary subsets, such that the number of edges crossed by the cut is maximized. Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, with
V=\{1,\dots,N\}
and
E=\{\langle i,j\rangle\}
the sets of vertices
i
and edges
\langle i,j\rangle
, the goal is to maximize the following objective function

\hat{\mathrm{H}}_{C}=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\frac{1}{2}(1-\hat{\mathrm{Z}}_{i}\hat{\mathrm{Z}}_{j}).

(45)

We focus on a graph with
V=\{1,2,3,4\}
and
E=\{\langle 1,2\rangle\,\langle 2,3\rangle\,\langle 3,4\rangle\,\langle 4,1\rangle\}
, thus a square graph with four nodes. The problem is equivalent to minimizing the cost Hamiltonian:

\hat{\mathrm{H}}_{C}=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\hat{\mathrm{Z}}_{i}\hat{\mathrm{Z}}_{j};

(46)

for this cost Hamiltonian, the optimal, or exact, energy is
E_{0}=-4
, corresponding to the cut giving
V_{1}=\{1,3\}
and
V_{2}=\{2,4\}
as complementary subsets of
V
. This is the target energy of the optimization of the variational ansatz.

We choose a specific ansatz
\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{ansatz}(\theta)
for the optimization shown in the bottom of Fig.
5
.
The output of each simulations is a density matrix
\hat{\rho}
, as shown in Eq.
10
. The cost function for the optimization is the energy calculated as
E=\Tr(\hat{\rho}(\theta)\rh{H}_C)
, where
\hat{\rho}(\theta)=\ket{\psi(\theta)}\bra{\psi(\theta)}
and
\ket{\psi(\theta)}=\hat{\mathrm{U}}_{ansatz}(\theta)\ket{0}^{\otimes 4}
. We choose a number of samples
\mathrm{N}_{samples}=500
in Eq.
10
and initial parameters are chosen randomly. The classical optimizer method used to update the parameters is the gradient-free method COBYLA
COB 2023
.

We study the convergence of the optimization in the noiseless case, in the presence of depolarization with
\mathrm{p}=10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2}

\mathrm{p}=10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2}
and under the combined action of depolarization and loss with
\mathrm{p}=10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2}

\mathrm{p}<10^{-4}
the effects of noises on the ansatz circuit are negligible and for

The choice of an appropriate ansatz is a critical factor in determining the VQA model’s performance
Cerezo et al. 2021b
. The motivation behind opting for a carefully balanced ansatz lies in avoiding excessive expressibility, which can potentially lead to challenges during the classical optimization process. An ansatz with overly complex representations may be able to effectively capture the features of the problem’s Hamiltonian. However, this increased expressibility can come at the cost of introducing a large number of parameters and intricate interactions. As a consequence, the cost function gradients may vanish exponentially during classical optimization
Du et al. 2022
. This phenomenon hinders the optimization process, making it difficult for the model to converge efficiently and find satisfactory solutions. In the circuit model of quantum computing, several ansatz designs have been developed to construct parameterized quantum circuits tailored to different tasks and applications
Cerezo et al. 2021b
.

VIII
Conclusions and Outlook

We have extended the noisy gates approach
Di Bartolomeo et al. 2023
to simulate noisy optical circuits, within the second quantization framework (see sections
IV
and
V
), which is the one commonly used in the dual rail encoding for linear optics quantum computing. The simulation method is applicable both to GBQC and MBQC, as shown in sections
VII.1
and
VII.2
. In section
VII.3
we have shown that the simulations are suitable to describe variational problems when noises are present.

When considering quantum computing on linear optics, the situation is not yet as mature as for other quantum platforms. Linear optical quantum computing, which utilizes photons for quantum information processing, faces unique challenges in designing efficient and expressive ansatz circuits. The absence of non-linear interactions in linear optics limits the complexity of quantum computations that can be performed. Consequently, creating ansatz designs that can effectively encode and manipulate quantum information in linear optics remains an ongoing research challenge. MBQC was proposed as a solution to these issues.

For this reason, the next natural target of this work is to understand the practical applicability of a noisy native MBQC algorithm, such as the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)
Farhi et al. 2014
formulated in the MBQC framework for the max K-cut problem in
Proietti et al. 2022
.

Another important goal is to compare our simulations with the output of optical devices available in the cloud, for example the one recently provided by Quandela
Maring et al. 2023
. These targets will be the subject of future research.
