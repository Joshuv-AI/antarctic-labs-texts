On the trace ratio method and Fisher’s discriminant analysis for robust multigroup classification
Giulia Ferrandi, Igor V. Kravchenko, Michiel E. Hochstenbach, M. Rosário Oliveira

Abstract
We compare two different linear dimensionality reduction strategies for the multigroup classification problem: the trace ratio method and Fisher’s discriminant analysis. Recently, trace ratio optimization has gained in popularity due to its computational efficiency, as well as the occasionally better classification results. However, a statistical understanding is still incomplete. We study and compare the properties of the two methods. Then, we propose a robust version of the trace ratio method, to handle the presence of outliers in the data. We reinterpret an asymptotic perturbation bound for the solution to the trace ratio, in a contamination setting. Finally, we compare the performance of the trace ratio method and Fisher’s discriminant analysis on both synthetic and real datasets, using classical and robust estimators.

Introduction

Classification is an important statistical task where an observation is assigned to one of the non-overlapping known groups, based on the statistical properties of the data characterizing these groups. Statistics, machine learning, data science, and pattern recognition are some of the areas that use this family of methods to solve practical problems.
In this paper, we take a closer look at two linear methods for multigroup classification: the trace ratio method and Fisher’s discriminant analysis.
By both theoretical analysis and numerical simulations, we attempt to increase our understanding of the properties of these methods.
We also introduce and study a robust version of the trace ratio method.

Suppose we have a population described by the random vector
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{p})^{T}
, with mean
\expec(\mathbf{X})={\boldsymbol{\mu}}
and covariance
\var(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{\Sigma}
. We make the common assumption that
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is nonsingular.
The population is divided into
g
groups (or classes), modeled by the class variable
Y\in\{1,\dots,g\}
. Denote the expectation of
\mathbf{X}_{j}=(\mathbf{X}\mid Y=j)
by
\expec(\mathbf{X}_{j})={\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}
, and its covariance matrix by
\var(\mathbf{X}_{j})=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}
. The probability of being in group
j
is
p_{j}=P(Y=j)
. Then

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\expec(\mathbf{X})=\expec(\expec(\mathbf{X}\mid Y))=\sum_{j=1}^{g}p_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}.

(1)

Given a new observation
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we would like to assign it to one of the
g
classes, via a so-called
classification rule
, that is built on top of the available data. We are interested in methods where the classification task includes a
linear
dimensionality reduction step, where the data are projected onto a lower-dimensional subspace.

In particular, we study the well-known Fisher’s discriminant analysis (FDA; see, e.g.,
(
Johnson and
Wichern 2007
, Ch. 11)
) and a more recent dimensionality reduction method, the trace ratio method (TR; see, e.g.,
Ngo et al. 2012
). Both strategies aim at finding a lower-dimensional representation of the data where the separation between the groups is maximized in a certain sense. They both involve a measure of distance between and within the groups. This is inspired by the decomposition of the covariance matrix
\var(\mathbf{X})
as

\var(\mathbf{X})=\expec(\var(\mathbf{X}\mid Y))+\var(\expec(\mathbf{X}\mid Y))=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\,p_{j}\,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\,p_{j}\,({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}})\,({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}})^{T},

(2)

where
\mathbf{W}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\,p_{j}\,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}
is the
within covariance
matrix and
\mathbf{B}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\,p_{j}\,({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}})\,({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}})^{T}
the
between covariance
matrix.
If projecting the data leads to a good separation among the groups, a favorable effect on the performance of the classifiers is expected, since the present noise may be damped or filtered.
In addition, the dimension reduction step tends to increase the efficiency of subsequent steps and may help the analyst to interpret the classification output.

The within and between covariance matrices can be estimated in different ways. We start our discussion from the classical estimates in Section
2
, reviewing FDA and the trace ratio method. Next, we are interested in assessing the classification performances of the two methods when robust estimates are used. These are very useful when the data is contaminated, i.e., there is a certain proportion
\varepsilon>0
of outliers that do not follow the distribution of
\mathbf{X}
. As we will observe in Section
5.2
, while some nonlinear classifiers seem to be less affected by outliers, linear classifiers may benefit from the use of robust statistics.

FDA with robust estimates has already been studied mainly by exploring the connection between FDA and linear discriminant analysis (LDA); see, e.g.,
Todorov and Pires 2007
.
Several methods to robustify discriminant analysis have been proposed. The most straightforward proposals suggest employing robust estimators of group means and robust covariance matrices
Todorov and Pires 2007
;
Hawkins and
McLachlan 1997
;
Croux and Dehon 2001
;
Hubert and
Van Driessen 2004
;
Bashir and Carter 2005
.
These methods assume that the number of observations per group, compared to the number of variables, is sufficiently large to guarantee good estimates of the unknown parameters. When we are in the opposite situation (which happens in many practical problems), we say that our data is high-dimensional.
The first attempts to deal with this issue were done by Vanden Branden and Hubert

Vanden Branden and
Hubert 2005
, who provide a robust classifier for high-dimensional data based on SIMCA
(
Wold 1976
)
, and by Pires and Branco
Pires and Branco 2010
who propose robust classifiers based on projection-pursuit. Later on, Hoffmann and coauthors
Hoffmann et al. 2016
suggest a sparse and robust classification method based on partial least squares for binary classification problems, while
Ortner et al. 2020
presents a robust version of optimal scoring, for both the non-sparse and sparse case.

In this paper, we propose a new robust TR method, obtained by exploiting MCD robust estimates of the within and between covariance matrices. The method can deal with high-dimensional data since it uses regularized MCD estimates (MRCD,
Boudt et al. 2020
); however, as we will see in Section
5.3
, when a high number of irrelevant variables has a negative impact on the performance of the estimation methods, even on the robust ones.

We derive theoretical expressions showing that the between and within theoretical covariance matrices, under a certain contamination scenario (cf. Section
3.1
), can be written as the sum of their non-contaminated counterparts and a perturbation term. In the TR case, this formulation allows us to derive an upper bound for the distance between the non-contaminated and the contaminated solutions of the method.

We compare TR and FDA on synthetic and real datasets. Synthetic scenarios consider cases where one method performs better than the others. In this case, FDA and TR are used as classifiers and are compared by means of two different criteria. While the first one is based on the performance of the associated classification rules, the second criterion is related to the proximity between the true solution of one method and the estimated one. Real datasets have been chosen from the UCI
Dua and Graff 2017
and KEEL
Derrac et al. 2015
platforms, and they illustrate the performance of FDA and TR as dimensionality reduction methods, used before the construction of a classifier. In many of these datasets, FDA is as good as or better than TR. Moreover, robust TR shows clear improvements compared to classical TR in several datasets.

In Section

For TR we derive an upper bound quantifying the effect of an infinitesimal percentage of contamination into the TR matrix of coefficients.
In Section

we propose a robust TR estimator. In Section

we carry out several simulation experiments and in Section
6
we explore FDA and TR as dimensionality reduction strategies for real datasets. The main conclusions are presented in Section
7
.

identity matrix by
\mathbf{I}_{k}
.
While

denotes the standard Euclidean norm for vectors and the induced spectral norm for matrices (equal to its largest singular value),

2
FDA, LDA, and TR for multigroup classification

We present and compare FDA
Fisher 1936
with the more recent trace ratio optimization method (see, e.g.,
Ngo et al. 2012
for a review).
We discuss the two methods for the case where classical estimates for the covariance matrices
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{W}
are used.

Let
\mathbf{x}_{j1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{jn_{j}}
represent
n_{j}
realizations of a random sample from
\mathbf{X}_{j}
; the total sample size is
n=\sum_{j=1}^{g}n_{j}
. The sample mean and the sample covariance matrix of group
j
are
\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j}=n_{j}^{-1}\,\sum_{h=1}^{n_{j}}{\mathbf{x}_{jh}}
and
\mathbf{S}_{j}=(n_{j}-1)^{-1}\,\sum_{h=1}^{n_{j}}\,(\mathbf{x}_{jh}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j})\,(\mathbf{x}_{jh}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j})^{T}
, respectively.
The overall sample mean and covariance are then estimated by

\overline{\mathbf{x}}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\tfrac{n_{j}}{n}\,\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j}
and
{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\tfrac{n_{j}-1}{n-g}\,\mathbf{S}_{j}
.
We define the classical between-class scatter matrix,
\mathbf{B}
, and the classical within-class scatter matrix,
\mathbf{W}
, as follows:

\mathbf{B}=n^{-1}\,\sum_{j=1}^{g}n_{j}\,(\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\overline{\mathbf{x}})\,(\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\overline{\mathbf{x}})^{T},\qquad\mathbf{W}=n^{-1}\,\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{h=1}^{n_{j}}\ (\mathbf{x}_{jh}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j})\,(\mathbf{x}_{jh}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j})^{T}.

(3)

To keep the notation simple, we use
\mathbf{B}
(
\mathbf{W})
for both the theoretical and classical (i.e., non-robust) estimated between (within) covariance matrices. It is easy to check that
\mathbf{W}=n^{-1}\,(n-g)\,{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}
.
Let
{\rm rank}(\cdot)
indicate the rank of a matrix. Then
r={\rm rank}(\mathbf{B})\leq\min\{g-1,\,p\}
and
{\rm rank}(\mathbf{W})\leq\min\{n-g,\,p\}
. Throughout the paper, we assume the common situation that there are sufficiently many data points such that
n-g\geq p
. Since
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is assumed to be nonsingular, we also assume that
\mathbf{W}
is also nonsingular, and thus SPD. However, if some variables are highly correlated, numerical singularity of
\mathbf{W}
may occur. One possible way to handle this situation is described in Section
6
.

2.1
Fisher’s discriminant analysis

The original idea of Fisher
Fisher 1936
is to find the linear combination of the data,
\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{x}
, that leads to the highest separability between the groups. By assuming that the population group covariance matrices are equal and of full rank, we look for a projection that maximizes the separation between the groups and minimizes the variability within the groups. This corresponds to finding a projection vector,
\mathbf{v}_{\rm FDA}
, that maximizes a separability index:
\mathbf{v}_{\rm FDA}=\argmax_{\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}}\rho_{\rm FDA}(\mathbf{v})
, where

\rho_{\rm FDA}(\mathbf{v})=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{g}n_{j}\,(\mathbf{v}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\mathbf{v}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{x}})^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{h=1}^{n_{j}}(\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{jh}-\mathbf{v}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j})^{2}}=\frac{\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{v}}.

(4)

This quantity is a
Rayleigh quotient
of the pair
(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{W})
.
Suppose we want to project our data onto a
k
-dimensional subspace spanned by the columns of
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times k}
, with
k\leq r
.
FDA (
4
) can be formalized as a generalized eigenvalue problem; see, e.g.,
(
Johnson and
Wichern 2007
, p. 623)
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{k}>0
be the
k\leq r
largest nonzero eigenvalues of the generalized eigenvalue problem
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}=\lambda\,\mathbf{W}\mathbf{v}
and let
\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k}
be the associated eigenvectors (usually scaled such that
\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}\mathbf{v}_{i}=1
). Then the vector of coefficients
\mathbf{v}_{\rm FDA}
that maximizes (
4
) is given by
\mathbf{v}_{\rm FDA}=\mathbf{v}_{1}
. The
i
th eigenvector is the solution to

\mathbf{v}_{i}=\argmax_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{T}{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}\mathbf{v}=0,\ j<i\\
\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}\end{subarray}}\ \frac{\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{v}}.

(5)

The linear combination
\mathbf{v}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{x}
is called
sample first discriminant
, and likewise the linear combination
\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}
, for
i\leq k
, is the sample
i
th discriminant; Fisher’s discriminants generate a lower-dimensional space whose span optimizes a sequence of separation criteria
\rho_{\rm FDA}(\cdot)
defined in (
4
).

Problem (
5
) is equivalent to finding the first
k
generalized eigenvectors
\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}=[\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k}]
of the pencil
(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{W})
, with scaling
\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{T}{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}=\mathbf{I}_{k}
. Since
\mathbf{W}
and
{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}
are the same matrix up to a multiplicative factor, the generalized eigenvalue problem for FDA is equivalent to finding the solution
\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}
to the constrained optimization problem
(cf., e.g.,
Ngo et al. 2012
)

\max_{\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{W}\,\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{I}_{k}}{\rm tr}(\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}),

(6)

where
{\rm tr}(\cdot)
indicates the trace of a matrix. Another equivalent formulation is (cf.
(
Absil et al. 2009
, Prop. 2.1.1)
)

\max_{\text{\scriptsize rank}(\mathbf{V})=k}{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{W}\,\mathbf{V})^{-1}\,\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V})
,
where the objective function may be viewed as a
generalized Rayleigh quotient
.

2.2
Relation between linear and Fisher’s discriminant analysis

When we assume that all the group covariance matrices are equal, i.e.,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}\equiv\mathbf{\Sigma}
, FDA has a strong connection with LDA, which assumes that, within groups, the random variables have a multivariate normal distribution with equal covariance matrices. The associated classification rule is derived such that it minimizes the
total probability of misclassification
, which is the probability of assigning a given data point to an incorrect class (cf.
(
Johnson and
Wichern 2007
, Result 11.5)
). According to LDA, an observation
\mathbf{x}
is assigned to class
j
if (see, e.g.,
(
Johnson and
Wichern 2007
, p. 630)
)

j=\argmin_{i}\|\mathbf{x}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}}^{2}-2\,\log p_{i},

(7)

and
\|\mathbf{x}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}\|_{\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}}
is the Mahalanobis distance between
\mathbf{x}
and the group mean
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}
.
LDA is also the classifier that maximizes the total probability of correctly assigning an observation (called the accuracy) among all possible classifiers for multivariate normal mixtures with equal covariance matrices.

When
k=r
, LDA with the classical estimates
\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
and
{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}
coincides with projecting a data point
\mathbf{x}
onto
\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
and classifying it to the group with the closest projected mean, according to the Euclidean distance.
This result is reported in Proposition
2
, which is a slightly different formulation of
(
Johnson and
Wichern 2007
, Result 11.6)
. Recall that
\mathbf{B}
has
r\leq\min{\{g-1,p\}}
positive eigenvalues.

Proposition 2
.

Let
[\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}\;\mathbf{V}_{\perp}]
contain the
{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}
-orthogonal generalized eigenvectors of the pencil
(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{W})
, with
\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}=[\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{r}]
and
\mathbf{V}_{\perp}^{T}{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}=\mathbf{0}
. Then only the terms
\|\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{T}(\mathbf{x}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{i})\|^{2}
contribute to the classification. In mathematical terms,

\argmin_{i}\|\mathbf{x}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\|_{{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}^{-1}}^{2}-2\,\log\tfrac{n_{i}}{n}=\argmin_{i}\|\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{T}(\mathbf{x}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{i})\|^{2}-2\,\log\tfrac{n_{i}}{n}.

Proof 2.3
.

Since
{\rm rank}(\mathbf{B})=r
, we have
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}_{\perp}=\mathbf{0}
. In particular,
\mathbf{V}_{\perp}^{T}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-\overline{\mathbf{x}})=\bf 0
for all
i
. This can be deduced from the fact that
0=\text{tr}(\mathbf{V}^{T}_{\perp}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}_{\perp})=\sum_{i=1}^{g}\frac{n_{i}}{n}\,\|\mathbf{V}^{T}_{\perp}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-\overline{\mathbf{x}})\|^{2}
.
Since
{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}^{-1}=\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{T}+\mathbf{V}_{\perp}\mathbf{V}_{\perp}^{T}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\|_{{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{T}(\mathbf{x}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{i})\|^{2}+\|\mathbf{V}_{\perp}^{T}\,[\,(\mathbf{x}-\overline{\mathbf{x}})+(\overline{\mathbf{x}}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{i})\,]\,\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{T}(\mathbf{x}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{i})\|^{2}+\|\mathbf{V}_{\perp}^{T}(\mathbf{x}-\overline{\mathbf{x}})\|^{2},

where the second term in the right-hand side is common to all groups.

From this last proposition, it is easy to see that Rule (
7
) is equivalent to classifying a data point
\mathbf{x}
to the class with the closest projected mean
\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j}
, up to an additive weight related to the class proportions.

For a reduced dimension
k<r
,
there is still a connection between Rule (
7
) and the classification via the Euclidean distance. This requires an estimation of the group means and the common covariance matrix by assuming that the group means lie in a
k
-dimensional space. The result is the following.

Proposition 2.4
.

(
Hastie and
Tibshirani 1996
, Prop. 1)

Consider a family of
g
multivariate normal distributions, with mean vectors
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}
and common covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
. Let
\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}
be the
p\times k
solution to the generalized eigenvalue problem
(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{W})
(
6
), scaled such that
\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{T}{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}=\mathbf{I}_{k}
. Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
be the maximum likelihood estimators of
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}
, with the additional constraint that the centroids
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}
lie in a
k
-dimensional space. Then

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}={\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{T}\,\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{i}+(\mathbf{I}_{p}-{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{T})\,\overline{\mathbf{x}}
and

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}={\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}+(\mathbf{I}_{p}-{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{T})\,\mathbf{B}\,(\mathbf{I}_{p}-{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{T})^{T}.

Moreover,

\|\mathbf{x}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}\|_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{-1}}^{2}=\|\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{T}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\|^{2}+(\mathbf{x}-\overline{\mathbf{x}})^{T}\,(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{-1}-\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{T})\,(\mathbf{x}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}),

where the rightmost term is common to all groups.

We can conclude that classifying a projected data point according to the Euclidean distance from the projected means is equivalent to classifying it in the original space according to the Mahalanobis distance, where
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}
are estimated appropriately based on the rank of
\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}
, i.e., by
\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
and
{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}
when
{\rm rank}(\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA})=r
(Proposition
2
), and by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
when
{\rm rank}(\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA})=k<r
(Proposition
2.4
).
In other words, regardless of the rank of
\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}
,
\mathbf{x}
is assigned to class
j
if

j=\argmin_{i}\|\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{T}(\mathbf{x}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{i})\|^{2}-2\,\log\tfrac{n_{i}}{n},

(8)

where
\frac{n_{i}}{n}
estimates the prior probability
p_{i}
of group
i
.

We remark that the classification rule (
8
) does not require any distributional assumption. In view of this, in general, it is not a Bayesian classification rule and thus does not minimize the total probability of misclassification. Nevertheless, FDA with
k=r
is optimal under the homoscedastic assumption and with multivariate normally distributed data; in this case FDA and LDA coincide.

2.3
Trace ratio optimization

Recently, a different alternative to FDA has been studied (see, e.g.,
(
Fukunaga 1990
, Ch. 10)
and
Park and Park 2008
;
Ngo et al. 2012
;
Shi and Wu 2021
).
The essence is that instead of the trace of a ratio (see Section
2.1
), a
ratio of traces
is considered.
If we consider the projected data
\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{x}
, another possible generalization of
\rho_{\rm FDA}
(
4
) comes from considering the Euclidean distance between the projected vectors. The between covariance matrix of the projected data can be written as a function of the distances between
\mathbf{V}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j}
and
\mathbf{V}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{x}}
(see, e.g.,
Ngo et al. 2012
) in the following way:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{g}n_{j}\,\|\mathbf{V}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\mathbf{V}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{g}n_{j}\,(\mathbf{V}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\mathbf{V}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{x}})^{T}\,(\mathbf{V}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\mathbf{V}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{g}{\rm tr}(n_{j}\,\mathbf{V}^{T}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\overline{\mathbf{x}})\,(\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\overline{\mathbf{x}})^{T}\,\mathbf{V})\ =\ n\ {\rm tr}(\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}).

Similarly, for the common variability within the projected groups we have

\sum_{j=1}^{g}\,\sum_{h=1}^{n_{j}}\,\|\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{jh}-\mathbf{V}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\|^{2}=n\ {\rm tr}(\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{W}\,\mathbf{V}).

The new optimization problem aims at finding the orthogonal projection
\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times k}
that maximizes the trace ratio (TR):

\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}=\argmax_{\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{I}_{k}}\ \rho(\mathbf{V})=\frac{{\rm tr}(\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V})}{{\rm tr}(\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{W}\,\mathbf{V})}.

(9)

Other variants of the problem (
9
) have been considered in
(
Fukunaga 1990
, Ch. 10)
and
Park and Park 2008
;
Ngo et al. 2012
;
Shi and Wu 2021
; these include a different set of constraints on
\mathbf{V}
or consider matrices different from
\mathbf{W}
in the denominator of
\rho
.

In the one-dimensional case, when
k=1
, problems (
4
), (
6
), and (
9
) coincide, up to the scaling of the solution vector. For
k>1
, the trace ratio problem (
9
) does not have an explicit solution and thus relies on iterative approaches; see, e.g.,
(
Jia et al. 2009
;
Ngo et al. 2012
;
Wang et al. 2007
;
Zhao et al. 2013
)
. Still, the authors of
Shi and Wu 2021
report that the TR problem reduces to a least squares problem for the high-dimensional case

p\geq n
, and provide a closed form solution.

The existence of
\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}
is guaranteed under a milder hypothesis on the within covariance matrix. We hereby report
(
Ngo et al. 2012
, Prop. 3.2)
for completeness.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\mathbf{B}
be symmetric and
\mathbf{W}
symmetric positive semidefinite, with
{\rm rank}(\mathbf{W})\geq p-k+1
. Then (
9
) admits a finite maximum.

In particular, Proposition
2.5
implies that for a finite maximum,
k
may be larger than
r={\rm rank}(\mathbf{B})\leq\min\{g-1,p\}
, and
\mathbf{W}
does not need to be positive definite.
Several authors (see, e.g.,
Ngo et al. 2012
) mention the constraint
\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{I}_{k}
for a certain SPD matrix
\mathbf{C}
.
Since the eigenvectors of the pair
(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{W})
are both
\mathbf{B}
-orthogonal and
\mathbf{W}
-orthogonal, we remark that for the convex combination
\mathbf{C}=(1-\gamma_{1})\,\mathbf{B}+\gamma_{1}\mathbf{W}
, with
0<\gamma_{1}\leq 1
such that
\mathbf{C}
is SPD, the TR method is equivalent to a generalized eigenvalue problem (
6
), which renders FDA.

We would like to point out that we may also start Algorithm 1 with the orthogonalized FDA solution
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times k}
.

The global convergence of Algorithm 1 is stated in, e.g.,
(
Zhang et al. 2010
, Thm. 5.1)
. There the uniqueness of the solution
\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}
is also discussed, and it is related to line 3 of Algorithm 1: if there is a gap between the largest
k

\mathbf{B}-\rho(\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR})\,\mathbf{W}
and the
p-k

\{\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}\mathbf{Q}\,:\,\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},\,\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}_{k}\}
.

The stopping criterion may be based on the (relative) error between two consecutive
\rho
-values, or the angle between two consecutive subspaces spanned by the columns of
\mathbf{V}
; in our implementation, we use the first approach.

Unlike FDA, TR does not come with a natural classifier. In
Ngo et al. 2012
, the method of the
k
-nearest neighbors is used to classify new data points in the projected space. We propose to classify a data point to the group with the closest projected sample mean vectors, according to the Euclidean distance and the class proportions:

j=\argmin_{i}\|\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{T}(\mathbf{x}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{i})\|^{2}-2\,\log\tfrac{n_{i}}{n},

as it is done in FDA, although we do not have a connection with the Mahalanobis distance in the original space and the Euclidean distance in the projected space.

2.4
FDA, TR, and the reduced dimension

We now study the solution to FDA and TR, and some relevant quantities, as a function of
k
, the selected reduced dimension.
We recall that for FDA,
1\leq k\leq r={\rm rank}(\mathbf{B})
; since
\mathbf{W}
is assumed nonsingular, the reduced dimension for TR is not restricted, thus
1\leq k\leq p={\rm rank}(\mathbf{W})
.

The FDA solution
\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{(k)}
corresponds to the first
k
generalized eigenvectors of
(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{W})
; therefore it holds
\text{span}(\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{(k)})\subset\text{span}(\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{(k+1)})
for
k=1,\dots,r-1
. If we are interested in choosing the best value for
k
, it is enough to compute the eigenvalue decomposition of
(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{W})
once and retain the desired number of eigenvectors. Another consequence is the following result. For simplicity, we choose
\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{(k)}
to have
\mathbf{W}
-orthogonal eigenvectors (instead of
{\mathbf{S}}_{{\rm pooled}}
-orthogonal).

Proposition 2.6
.

Let the eigenvectors of
(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{W})
be scaled
\mathbf{W}
-orthogonal. Then

{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{(k)})^{T}\,\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{(k)})=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}
,
{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{(k)})^{T}\,\mathbf{W}\,\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{(k)})={\rm tr}(\mathbf{I}_{k})=k
, and therefore both quantities are increasing as a function of
k
.

The trace ratio
\rho(\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{(k)})
for the FDA subspace is equal to
(\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k})/k
, and therefore is a non-increasing function of
k
.

The quantity
{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{(k)})^{T}\,\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}^{(k)})
is mentioned in
(
Johnson and
Wichern 2007
, (11-68))
as a theoretical criterion to choose
k
.

Now we turn our attention to the TR problem.
Algorithm 1 needs the value of
k
in advance; the processes and outputs for different values of
k
are generally unrelated, and therefore we expect
\text{span}(\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})\not\subset\text{span}(\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k+1)})
.
The following example illustrates this statement.

Example 2.7
.

By applying an orthogonal transformation, one may choose the within scatter matrix
\mathbf{W}
to be diagonal without loss of generality.
Therefore, we generate
1000
random problems, where
\mathbf{W}={\rm diag}(1,2,\dots,20)
is fixed, while the between scatter matrix is given by
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{T}
, where
\mathbf{A}
is a
20\times 10
matrix with normally distributed entries, after which
\mathbf{A}
is column centered. The columns represent
10
groups.
For each problem, we run TR with
k=1,2,\dots,8
and compute the angle between two consecutive solutions,
\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)}
and
\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k+1)}
. Figure
1
(a) reports the sorted angles
\angle(\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(2)},\,\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(3)})
for each problem. Their values range from rather small to approximately
\pi/5
, meaning that the two subspaces can be rather different.
In Figure
1
(b), we carry out similar experiments, but now we display the median
\angle(\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)},\,\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k+1)})
over 1000 experiments for various values of
k
.

(a)              (b)

Figure 1
:

and
\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(3)}

over 1000 random problems, for

.

Since
\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)}\not\subset\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k+1)}
, the quantities
{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})^{T}\,\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})
and
{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})^{T}\,\mathbf{W}\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})
do not have an easy expression and interpretation. Nevertheless, we can show that the optimal trace ratio
\rho^{(k)}=\rho(\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})
is a non-increasing function of
k
. To the best of our knowledge, this result is new.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\rho^{(k)}
be the optimal function value of the TR, for a
k
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and the pencil
(\mathbf{B},\,\mathbf{W})
, where
\mathbf{W}
is SPD. Then
\rho^{(k)}
is a non-increasing function of
k
, for
k=1,\dots,p
.

Proof 2.9
.

Let
\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times k}
be the solution to TR with optimal value
\rho^{(k)}
, for
k=1,\dots p-1
. Because of the linearity of the trace, we can write

\rho^{(k+1)}-\rho^{(k)}=\frac{{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k+1)})^{T}\,(\mathbf{B}-\rho^{(k)}\mathbf{W})\,\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k+1)})}{{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k+1)})^{T}\,\mathbf{W}\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k+1)})},

where the denominator is positive.
Denote by
\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{A})
the
i
th largest eigenvalue of a matrix
\mathbf{A}
.
We recall that

0={\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})^{T}\,(\mathbf{B}-\rho^{(k)}\mathbf{W})\,\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{B}-\rho^{(k)}\mathbf{W}).

The first equality comes from the first-order necessary condition for the maximum (see, e.g.,
Ngo et al. 2012
), while the second follows from the fact that
\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)}
contains the
k
largest eigenvectors of
\mathbf{B}-\rho^{(k)}\mathbf{W}
.
Since the sum of the
k
largest eigenvalues of
\mathbf{B}-\rho^{(k)}\mathbf{W}
is zero, the
(k+1)
st largest eigenvalue must be nonpositive:
\lambda_{k+1}(\mathbf{B}-\rho^{(k)}\mathbf{W})\leq 0
. Then

{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k+1)})^{T}(\mathbf{B}-\rho^{(k)}\mathbf{W})\,\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k+1)})\leq\max_{\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{I}_{k+1}}{\rm tr}(\mathbf{V}^{T}(\mathbf{B}-\rho^{(k)}\mathbf{W})\,\mathbf{V})=\lambda_{k+1}(\mathbf{B}-\rho^{(k)}\mathbf{W})\leq 0.

This proves that
\rho^{(k+1)}-\rho^{(k)}\leq 0
.

The monotonicity from this proposition does not need to be strict, in the case of a multiple generalized eigenvalue.
An easy example of this is
\mathbf{B}={\rm diag}(2,4)
and
\mathbf{W}={\rm diag}(1,2)
, when
\rho^{(1)}=\rho^{(2)}=2
.
We also note that, for this result, the hypothesis that
\mathbf{W}
is SPD can be weakened; one only needs that
{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})^{T}\,\mathbf{W}\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})>0
for all
k
.

By rather extensive experimentation, we have reason to believe that
{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})
is a non-decreasing function of
k
, just as for FDA. A proof or counterexample is currently lacking.

Conjecture 2.10
.

Let
\mathbf{B}
be symmetric positive semidefinite and
\mathbf{W}
be SPD, and let
\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)}
be the quantity determined by the trace ratio method.
The quantity
{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})
is a non-decreasing function of
k
.

If this conjecture is true, the following related result follows as well.

Corollary 2.11
.

If Conjecture
2.10
holds, then
{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})^{T}\,\mathbf{W}\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})
is also non-decreasing in
k
.

Proof 2.12
.

This follows from Proposition
2.8
.
In fact, since
\rho^{(k+1)}\leq\rho^{(k)}
,

\frac{{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k+1)})^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k+1)})}{{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})}\leq\frac{{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k+1)})^{T}\,\mathbf{W}\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k+1)})}{{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})^{T}\,\mathbf{W}\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})}.

Therefore, if Conjecture
2.10
holds, since the trace ratio is decreasing, the quantity
{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})^{T}\,\mathbf{W}\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})
increases faster than
{\rm tr}((\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{(k)})
as
k
increases.

From the descriptions of FDA and TR methods, it is clear that any pencil
(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{W})
, with
\mathbf{B}
symmetric and
\mathbf{W}
SPD, estimating the between and the within covariance matrices, may be exploited in the contexts of FDA and TR. This motivates the use of robust estimators in Section
4
and of the true covariance matrices in the simulation study (Section
5
).

Although FDA seems to have a stronger statistical motivation than TR, in Section
5
we illustrate some situations in which TR performs better than FDA in the classification task.

3
Effect of contamination in the within and between covariance matrices

In the following sections, we express the contaminated theoretical within and between covariance matrices as the original matrices plus a perturbation term. These perturbation terms allow us to derive an upper limit of a proximity measure between the TR solution when no contamination is present, and their equivalent under contamination.

3.1
Contaminated theoretical between and within covariance matrices

We model the presence of atypical data as a mixture of two random vectors: the majority of the data comes from a certain (central, clean, or non-contaminated) mixture model
\mathbf{X}
, and, in each group, there is a probability
\varepsilon
for getting data generated by a different distribution, characterizing the atypical scenario. Let
{}^{c}{\mathbf{X}}_{j}
be the contaminating random variable, where
\expec({}^{c}{\mathbf{X}}_{j})={}^{c}{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{j}
and
\var({}^{c}{\mathbf{X}}_{j})={}^{c}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{j}
. Let
\mathbf{Z}_{j}
be the contaminated variable that describes the distribution of group
j
:

\mathbf{Z}_{j}=(1-\Omega_{j})\,\mathbf{X}_{j}+\Omega_{j}\,{}^{c}{\mathbf{X}}_{j},

with
\Omega_{j}\sim{\rm Bernoulli}(\varepsilon)
.
In addition, we assume that
\Omega_{j}
,
\mathbf{X}_{j}
, and
{}^{c}{\mathbf{X}}_{j}
are independent random variables for any value of
j
. Then, we can derive the mean vector and covariance matrix of
\mathbf{Z}_{j}
:

\displaystyle\expec(\mathbf{Z}_{j})

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)\,{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}+\varepsilon\ {}^{c}{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{j}={\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}+\varepsilon\,({}^{c}{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j})={\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}+\varepsilon\,\Delta_{j},\quad{\rm where}\quad\Delta_{j}={}^{c}{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}.

(10)

The covariance matrix of
\mathbf{Z}_{j}
is given by

\displaystyle\var(\mathbf{Z}_{j})

\displaystyle=\expec(\mathbf{Z}_{j}\mathbf{Z}_{j}^{T})-\expec(\mathbf{Z}_{j})\expec(\mathbf{Z}_{j})^{T}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\,\expec({}^{c}{\mathbf{X}}_{j}({}^{c}{\mathbf{X}}_{j})^{T})+(1-\varepsilon)\,\expec(\mathbf{X}_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{j})^{T})

\displaystyle\phantom{MMM}-\varepsilon^{2}\ {}^{c}{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{j}\,({}^{c}{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{j})^{T}-(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\,{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}^{T}-\varepsilon\,(1-\varepsilon)\,\left({}^{c}{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}^{T}+{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}\,({}^{c}{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{j})^{T}\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\ {}^{c}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{j}+(1-\varepsilon)\,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}+\varepsilon\,(1-\varepsilon)\,\left({}^{c}{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{j}\,({}^{c}{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{j})^{T}+{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}^{T}-{}^{c}{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}^{T}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}({}^{c}{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{j})^{T}\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\ {}^{c}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{j}+(1-\varepsilon)\,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}+\varepsilon\,(1-\varepsilon)\,\Delta_{j}\,\Delta_{j}^{T}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}+\varepsilon\left({}^{c}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{j}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}\right)+\varepsilon\,(1-\varepsilon)\,\Delta_{j}\,\Delta_{j}^{T}.

(11)

Let
\mathbf{Z}
be the random vector describing the mixture of the contaminated random vectors
\mathbf{Z}_{j}
.
From (
1
), the expectation of
\mathbf{Z}
is

\expec(\mathbf{Z})=\sum_{j=1}^{g}p_{j}\expec(\mathbf{Z}_{j})={\boldsymbol{\mu}}+\varepsilon\,\overline{\Delta},\quad{\rm where}\quad\overline{\Delta}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}p_{j}\,\Delta_{j}.

(12)

From (
2
) and (
11
), we can compute the contaminated theoretical within covariance matrix:

\mathbf{W}_{Z}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}p_{j}\var(\mathbf{Z}_{j})=\mathbf{W}+\varepsilon\,\sum_{j=1}^{g}p_{j}\,({}^{c}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{j}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j})+\varepsilon\,(1-\varepsilon)\,\sum_{j=1}^{g}p_{j}\,\Delta_{j}\,\Delta_{j}^{T},

(13)

where
\mathbf{W}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}p_{j}\,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}
is the theoretical within covariance matrix. We notice that when
{}^{c}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{j}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}
for all
j
, the contaminated theoretical within covariance matrix is the original
\mathbf{W}
, plus a low-rank matrix.

By using (
2
), we compute the theoretical between covariance matrix of
\mathbf{Z}
by substituting (
10
) and (
12
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{Z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{g}p_{j}\,(\expec(\mathbf{Z}_{j})-\expec(\mathbf{Z}))\,(\expec(\mathbf{Z}_{j})-\expec(\mathbf{Z}))^{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{g}p_{j}\,({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}+\varepsilon\Delta_{j}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}-\varepsilon\,\overline{\Delta})\,({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}+\varepsilon\Delta_{j}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}-\varepsilon\,\overline{\Delta})^{T}.

Let us define
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{j}={\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
. It follows that

\mathbf{B}_{Z}=\mathbf{B}+\varepsilon\sum_{j=1}^{g}p_{j}\,[\boldsymbol{\delta}_{j}(\Delta_{j}-\overline{\Delta})^{T}+(\Delta_{j}-\overline{\Delta})\,\boldsymbol{\delta}_{j}^{T}]+\varepsilon^{2}\,\sum_{j=1}^{g}p_{j}\,(\Delta_{j}-\overline{\Delta})\,(\Delta_{j}-\overline{\Delta})^{T}.

(14)

Now suppose that only the first group has been contaminated. This means that
\Delta_{j}=0
for
j\neq 1
, and
\overline{\Delta}=p_{1}\Delta_{1}
. Exploiting the fact that
\sum_{j=2}^{g}p_{j}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{j}=-p_{1}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{g}p_{j}\,[\boldsymbol{\delta}_{j}\big(\Delta_{j}-\overline{\Delta}\big)^{T}+\big(\Delta_{j}-\overline{\Delta}\big)\boldsymbol{\delta}_{j}^{T}]

\displaystyle=p_{1}\,(1-p_{1})\,(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}\Delta_{1}^{T}+\Delta_{1}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}^{T})-p_{1}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}\Delta_{1}^{T}+\Delta_{1}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}^{T})

\displaystyle=p_{1}\,(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}\Delta_{1}^{T}+\Delta_{1}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}^{T}),

and
\sum_{j=1}^{g}p_{j}\left(\Delta_{j}-\overline{\Delta}\right)\left(\Delta_{j}-\overline{\Delta}\right)^{T}=p_{1}\,(1-p_{1})\,\Delta_{1}\,\Delta_{1}^{T}
.
Then, up to second-order terms in
\varepsilon
, we can write the contaminated covariance matrices as

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{Z}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}+\Delta\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}+\varepsilon\,p_{1}\,[({}^{c}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{1}-\mathbf{\Sigma})+\Delta_{1}\,\Delta_{1}^{T}]+o(\varepsilon),

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{Z}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}\;+\Delta\mathbf{B}\;=\mathbf{B}+\varepsilon\,p_{1}\,(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}\Delta_{1}^{T}+\Delta_{1}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}^{T})+o(\varepsilon).

In general, all the contaminated parameters
\expec(\mathbf{Z}_{j})
,
\var(\mathbf{Z}_{j})
,
\expec(\mathbf{Z})
,
\mathbf{W}_{Z}
, and
\mathbf{B}_{Z}
can be written as their original counterparts (
\expec(\mathbf{X}_{j})
,
\var(\mathbf{X}_{j})
,
\expec(\mathbf{X})
,
\mathbf{W}
, and
\mathbf{B}
, respectively) plus an additional term each, that quantifies the effect of the contamination.
The additional terms are functions of the percentage of contamination,
\varepsilon
, the probability of the contaminated classes,
p_{j}
, the parameters of the uncontaminated (
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}
) and contaminating distribution (
{}^{c}{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{j}
and
{}^{c}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{j}
).

3.2
Perturbation analysis of TR

In Section
3.1
, we have given explicit expressions for the contaminated theoretical between and within covariance matrices, when the contamination happens in one class (
). We have stressed that they are of the form
\mathbf{B}_{Z}=\mathbf{B}+\Delta\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{W}_{Z}=\mathbf{W}+\Delta\mathbf{W}
, where
\|\Delta\mathbf{B}\|
,
\|\Delta\mathbf{W}\|\to 0
when
\varepsilon\to 0
.
We use this formulation to understand how the TR solution,
\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}
, changes when we perturb the pair
(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{W})
to
(\mathbf{B}_{Z},\,\mathbf{W}_{Z})
. A generalization of this problem is addressed by Zhang and Yang
Zhang and Yang 2013
, for arbitrary perturbations such that
\|\Delta\mathbf{B}\|
,
\|\Delta\mathbf{W}\|\to 0
. We report their upper bound for the resulting change in
\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}
.
By
[\Delta\mathbf{B}\ \,\Delta\mathbf{W}]
we denote the
p\times 2p
matrix constructed by appending
\Delta\mathbf{W}
to
\Delta\mathbf{B}
.

Theorem 3.13
.

(
Zhang and Yang 2013
, Thm. 4.1)

Let
\mathbf{B}
be symmetric and
\mathbf{W}
SPD. Let the
p\times k
matrix
\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}
be the solution to (
9
) for the pair
(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{W})
and suppose that there is a gap
\gamma>0
between the
k
th and the
(k+1)
st largest eigenvalues of
\mathbf{B}-\rho(\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR})\,\mathbf{W}
. Then, when
\eta\to 0
, for all
\|[\Delta\mathbf{B}\ \,\Delta\mathbf{W}]\|\leq\eta
, there exists
{}^{c}{\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}}
solution to (
9
) for the pencil
(\mathbf{B}+\Delta\mathbf{B},\,\mathbf{W}+\Delta\mathbf{W})
such that

\|\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{T}-{}^{c}{\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}}{}^{c}{\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{T}}\|\leq\frac{4\sigma}{\gamma}\,\Big(1+\frac{k}{\tau}\,\|\mathbf{W}+\Delta\mathbf{W}\|\Big)+o(\eta),

where
\sigma=\|\Delta\mathbf{B}\|+\rho(\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR})\,\|\Delta\mathbf{W}\|\leq(1+\rho(\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}))\,\eta
, and
\tau
is the sum of the
k
smallest eigenvalues of
\mathbf{W}
.

Note that, as stated in the introduction, we always have
\|\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{T}-{}^{c}{\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}}{}^{c}{\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{T}}\|\leq 1
. This theorem gives an asymptotic upper bound that is linear in
\eta
, for
\eta\to 0
.

When the contamination level tends to zero, i.e.,
\varepsilon\to 0
, we can express
\Delta\mathbf{B}
and
\Delta\mathbf{W}
as in (
). Let us assume that
{}^{c}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{1}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}
. Then

\|\Delta\mathbf{B}\|\leq 2\,\varepsilon\,p_{1}\,\|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}\|\,\|\Delta_{1}\|+o(\varepsilon),\qquad\|\Delta\mathbf{W}\|\leq\,\varepsilon\,p_{1}^{2}\,\|\Delta_{1}\|^{2}+o(\varepsilon).

Thus,

\|[\Delta\mathbf{B}\ \,\Delta\mathbf{W}]\|\leq\,\varepsilon\,p_{1}\,\|\Delta_{1}\|\,(2\,\|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}\|\,+\,p_{1}\,\|\Delta_{1}\|)+o(\varepsilon).

If
p_{1}
,
\|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}\|
, and
\|\Delta_{1}\|
are fixed, then we have just shown that
\|[\Delta\mathbf{B}\ \,\Delta\mathbf{W}]\|\leq\varepsilon\,C
, where
C=p_{1}\,\|\Delta_{1}\|\,(2\,\|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}\|\,+\,p_{1}\,\|\Delta_{1}\|)>0
. If we set
\eta=\varepsilon\,C
in Theorem
3.13
, then
o(\eta)=o(\varepsilon)
. Thus we can write the upper bound for the dissimilarity measure between
\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}
and
{}^{c}{\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}}
:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{T}-{}^{c}{\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}}{}^{c}{\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{T}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\gamma}\,\varepsilon\,p_{1}\,\|\Delta_{1}\|\,\big(2\,\|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}\|+p_{1}\,\rho(\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR})\,\|\Delta_{1}\|\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\cdot\Big(1+\frac{k}{\tau}\,\left(\|\mathbf{W}\|+2\,\varepsilon\,p_{1}^{2}\,\|\Delta_{1}\|^{2}\right)\Big)+o(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\gamma}\,\varepsilon\,p_{1}\,\|\Delta_{1}\|\,\big(2\,\|\delta_{1}\|+p_{1}\,\rho(\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR})\,\|\Delta_{1}\|\big)\,\Big(1+\frac{k}{\tau}\,\|\mathbf{W}\|\Big)+o(\varepsilon),

where in the last step we discard a new
o(\varepsilon)
-term.
Let
\lambda_{p-k+1}(\mathbf{W})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{W})
be the
k
smallest eigenvalues of
\mathbf{W}
; then
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{W})=\|\mathbf{W}\|
and
\tau=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{p-k+i}(\mathbf{W})
. Since
\tau\geq k\,\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{W})
, we can write:

\|\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{T}-{}^{c}{\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}}{}^{c}{\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}^{T}}\|\leq\frac{4}{\gamma}\,\varepsilon\,p_{1}\,\|\Delta_{1}\|\left(2\,\|\delta_{1}\|+p_{1}\,\rho(\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR})\,\|\Delta_{1}\|\right)\,\Big(1+\frac{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{W})}{\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{W})}\Big)+o(\varepsilon).

(16)

The quotient
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{W})/\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{W})
is the condition number of
\mathbf{W}
, which is an indication of the ill-conditioning of
\mathbf{W}
. In the situation of a high condition number, even small perturbations in the
\mathbf{W}
matrix can lead to big changes in the subspaces generated by TR.

The upper limit (
16
) is linear in the distance between
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
, i.e.,
\|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}\|
, but quadratic in
\|\Delta_{1}\|
, the distance between the non-contaminated mean vector and the contaminated mean vector. Thus
\|\Delta_{1}\|
, which is related to the contamination process, is the term that contributes the most to increasing the upper limit of this dissimilarity index. Moreover, the upper limit depends explicitly on the theoretical within covariance matrix,
\mathbf{W}
, but only implicitly on
\mathbf{B}
(via
\rho(\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR})
and
\gamma
), assigning different levels of importance to these two matrices.

A way to control the impact of contamination in the estimated TR and FDA coefficients is to consider robust estimators, which are discussed in the next section.

4
Robust trace ratio and robust Fisher discriminant analysis

In this section, we propose a robust version of the TR algorithm, and also study a robust version of FDA.
Instead of considering the classical estimators of the between and within covariance matrices defined in (
3
), we use their robust versions based on the minimum covariance determinant (MCD) estimators of location and covariance matrix, described in
Rousseeuw and
Driessen 1999
. Let us denote the robust estimators by
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{j}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}_{j}
for the location and the covariance matrix, respectively, of the
j
th group,
j=1,\dots,g
. The global location estimator is defined as
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\tfrac{n_{j}}{n}\,\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{j}
. The robust versions of the between and within scatter matrices are then defined as

\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\tfrac{n_{j}}{n}\,(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}-\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{j})\,(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}-\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{j})^{T},\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\tfrac{n_{j}-1}{n-g}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}_{j}.

(17)

We now propose a robust version of the trace ratio method, which we denote by rTR.
For this method, we determine
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}_{\rm TR}
from Algorithm 1 in Subsection
2.3
, but now using as input the robust estimates of the between and within scatter matrices,
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}
, respectively.

We compare rTR with a robust version of FDA (rFDA), where a robust coefficient matrix,
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}_{\rm FDA}
, is obtained by finding the first
k
generalized eigenvectors of the pencil
(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}},\widetilde{\mathbf{W}})
.
For the classical estimates, the statistical literature usually recommends the derivation of the eigenvectors of the matrix
{\mathbf{W}}^{-1}{\mathbf{B}}
, as can be confirmed in, e.g.,
Johnson and
Wichern 2007
or
Hastie et al. 2009
.
Nevertheless, we note that standard methods working on this pair (such as the ones implemented in the R package
geigen
, see
Hasselman 2019
for details) are numerically preferable over approaches based on the transformed matrix
\mathbf{W}^{-1}\mathbf{B}
(see, e.g.,
(
Golub and Van Loan 2013
, Ch. 8)
). Therefore, we use the function
geigen
from
Hasselman 2019
to derive the classical and robust FDA matrix of coefficients.

The procedure to classify a test set observation,
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, into one of the groups, for both rTR and rFDA, is as follows.

(i)

Project the training set observations on the subspace spanned by
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}
(estimated by rTR or rFDA).

(ii)

Compute the MCD estimates of the group projected locations
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{Uj}

and the covariance matrices, and from these the associated
k\times k
pooled covariance matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{U}
.

(iii)

Compute
\mathbf{u}_{0}=\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{0}
and the Mahalanobis distance between
\mathbf{u}_{0}
and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{Uj}
,
\|\mathbf{u}_{0}-\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{Uj}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{U}^{-1}}
.

(iv)

Assign
\mathbf{x}_{0}
to the group
j
that minimizes the quantity
\|\mathbf{u}_{0}-\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{Uj}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{U}^{-1}}^{2}-2\,\log(n_{j}/n)
.
The second term balances the class prior information in the decision rule.

Step (iv) is equivalent to performing robust LDA over the projected data on the reduced subspace, spanned by a robust estimate of
\mathbf{V}
. The robust LDA is obtained by plugging the MCD estimators of location and covariance in the Mahalanobis distances, computed in the reduced subspace.
This is a robust way to mitigate the potentially harmful effect of outliers in the classification rules based on the Euclidean distance in the projected space. We remark that, in case of high-dimensional data, we may also use regularized MCD
Boudt et al. 2020
or any other robust estimators of the groups location and covariance matrix.

In the next sections, we compare FDA and TR when either classical or robust estimates for the covariance matrices are considered.

5
Simulation study

We first explore TR and FDA as classifiers. Here the dimensionality reduction is embedded in the classifier: first, we build the projection matrix, either
\mathbf{V}_{\rm FDA}
or
\mathbf{V}_{\rm TR}
, and then we classify the projected data points based on the distances from the projected group means.
The simulation scheme presented in this section has two main objectives: (i) compare TR with FDA classical and robust classifiers, under clean and contaminated data, and (ii) analyze the performance of TR and FDA classifiers under the presence of irrelevant variables for the associated classification problem.

5.2
Experimental setting

Given a scenario, for each experiment we generate a training set of
n_{j}=400
data points for each class, and a test set composed by
n_{j}=40
observations per group, according to the parameters defined in Table
1
. For scenario
IV
we also consider
n_{j}=40
for the training set. Training sets and test sets are independently generated. We study the different contamination levels
\varepsilon\in\{0,\,0.05,\,0.10,\,0.15,\,0.20,\,0.25,\,0.30\}
.
We test the performances of the following classifiers.

•

cTR and cFDA: TR and FDA with classical estimators for the covariance matrices. The associated classification rules are described in Section
2
.

•

rTR and rFDA: TR and FDA with robust estimators for the covariance matrices, as described in Section
4
. These are obtained by the MRCD robust location and covariance matrix estimators via the
CovMrcd
routine from the R package
rrcov
, where the parameter
{\sf alpha}=0.75
controls the size of the subsets over which the covariance determinant is minimized (see
(
Hubert and
Van Driessen 2004
;
Todorov and
Filzmoser 2009
)
for further details). The classification rules are described in Section
4
. Given that the MRCD estimates select
75\%
of the data, we expect that they are able to deal with datasets with at most
25\%
of outliers. Finally, we remark that the regularization parameter of the MRCD estimates is chosen adaptively, so that MRCD estimates coincide with MCD estimates when regularization is not needed
Boudt et al. 2020
.

•

tTR and tFDA: theoretical TR and FDA, where the covariance matrices are computed analytically (cf. (
2
)). In this case, no training set is needed. The associated classification rules are described in Section
2
and make use of the theoretical group means.

•

tQDA: quadratic discriminant classification rule (see, e.g.,
Hastie et al. 2009
) for multivariate normal data. The parameters of the distributions are known, so we do not need a training set.

•

AdaBoost: nonlinear classifier (with decision trees as the weak learners)
(
Freund and
Schapire 1997
)
. This is abbreviated by
ab
in the figures. We have selected AdaBoost due to its popularity (and proven performance) in the Machine Learning community.

We remark that tQDA and AdaBoost do not involve any linear dimensionality reduction strategy. When the groups have a multivariate normal distribution with different covariance matrices, tQDA is the optimal classifier, because it maximizes the accuracy (or, equivalently, it minimizes the total probability of misclassification). In scenario
I
, the covariance matrices are all equal, so QDA coincides with LDA, which is the optimal linear classifier.

In cTR, cFDA, rTR, and rFDA, the training set is used to find a projection matrix for each method, while we assess the performances of the classification rules on the test set. Each experiment is repeated
200
times, meaning that for each contamination level
\varepsilon
, we generate
200
training sets and
200
test sets. To get trustful performance measures, the test set is never contaminated.

For each algorithm and scenario, we report the sample accuracy.
We also evaluate the goodness of a sample subspace
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}
compared to its theoretical counterpart
\mathbf{V}
. To do so, we compute the angles between these two subspaces
\angle(\mathbf{V},\widehat{\mathbf{V}})
. The matrices
\mathbf{V}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}
need to have orthogonal columns: these can be obtained from the solution to FDA via a QR decomposition. The solution to TR already has orthogonal columns. Angles compare only subspaces: they indicate the quality of the estimates of the theoretical coefficients obtained with FDA and TR.

Experiments are conducted on the real datasets listed in Table
3
. For each dataset, we discard observations containing missing values, and remove those variables for which the
Q_{n}
is zero (we recall that
Q_{n}

Rousseeuw and Croux 1993
is a high-breakdown point robust estimate of the scale of a random variable). This step aims at removing those variables that are almost constant on the entire dataset. For
penguins
, we combine sex and species to get 6 classes, while we discard the information about the penguins’ home islands. In
ecoli
, we do not consider classes with less than 20 observations.

The experiments consist of two steps: first, we reduce the dimension of the problem from
n\times p
to
n\times k
, for
k=1,\dots,r
, via either classical or robust TR and FDA. Secondly, we use the projected data to build the robust LDA (rLDA) classifier: this is equivalent to computing the robust Mahalanobis distances between points and means in the
k
-dimensional subspace spanned by the chosen dimensionality reduction method, and assigning each data point to its closest group (we refer to Section
4
for more details).

We compute the accuracy to evaluate the performances of rLDA combined with the various dimensionality reduction strategies. Each dataset is split into a training set,
\mathbf{X}_{\rm train}
, and a test set,
\mathbf{X}_{\rm test}
. The training set is used to compute the projection matrix,
\mathbf{V}
, and to estimate the projected group means and pooled covariance matrix for rLDA. The test set data points are then classified according to rLDA. The median accuracy is estimated via 10-fold cross-validation. We remark that each fold contains the classes in the same proportions as the original dataset.

When estimating the covariance matrices, we may have the following problems: the (numerical) singularity of
\mathbf{W}
estimates, and the singularity of one or more individual group covariance matrices. The first issue may be caused by possibly high correlations among the variables. The nullspace of
\mathbf{W}
may intersect the nullspace of
\mathbf{B}
. In FDA (which is a generalized eigenvalue problem) this fact leads to the so-called singular pencils. In TR we may lose the uniqueness of the solution, because for some reduced dimension
k
there may be no gap between the
k
th and the
(k+1)
st eigenvalue of
\mathbf{B}-\rho^{(k)}\mathbf{W}
. To avoid this situation, we compute the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{W}
and project both
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{B}
onto the subspace spanned by the range of
\mathbf{W}
, i.e., by the eigenvectors corresponding to its positive eigenvalues. Numerically, we select only the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{W}
satisfying
\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{W})>10^{-10}\,\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{W})
and the corresponding eigenspace
{\bf\Gamma}
. We solve TR and FDA for the pencil projected onto the span of the eigenvectors

({\bf\Gamma}^{T}\mathbf{B}{\bf\Gamma},\,{\bf\Gamma}^{T}\mathbf{W}{\bf\Gamma})

and thus find a projection matrix
\mathbf{V}
with fewer rows. In the end, this is equivalent to work with
\mathbf{X}_{\rm train}{\bf\Gamma}
as input of the training task; test data is projected accordingly. We adopt the same strategy for the robust covariance matrices
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}
.

The singularity of the individual group covariance matrices can be related to the fact that, for some
j
’s, the group size is smaller than the number of variables
n_{j}<p
. This is not problematic for the classical estimates
\mathbf{S}_{j}
, and, in the worst-case scenario, it results in a singular within covariance matrix, which can be tackled as we have already suggested. As mentioned in Section
4
, in the robust case we use the MRCD estimates
Boudt et al. 2020
for mean and covariance, in place of the MCD estimates. These prevent the determinant of
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}_{j}
from being zero.

When
n_{j}\ll p
, the MRCD estimates could give very different results depending on the selected subset of points. Therefore, we have repeated the same experiments with a different regularization strategy for dimensionality reduction. This consists in centering the groups with respect to their
L_{1}
-median (see, e.g.,
Fritz et al. 2012
) and then computing the MCD estimate of the common covariance matrix. We note that this approach implicitly assumes that all groups share the same covariance matrix. In general, we did not see any improvement compared to the use of MRCD estimates. In some cases, the performance of this other strategy is even less satisfactory.

We mention in passing that we have also compared the rLDA classifier with the classical LDA method. As expected, rLDA results in equal or a better classification performance, except for the
plant shape

For large values of

7
Conclusions

We have studied and tested FDA and TR, two linear dimensionality reduction methods that are based on the within and between covariance matrices. We have shown that, under a particular contamination scenario, the two contaminated covariance matrices can be written as the sum of the clean covariance matrices, and a perturbation term that depends on the contamination level of the data. This allowed us to reinterpret the asymptotic perturbation bounds for the solution to TR
Zhang and Yang 2013
in terms of the parameters of the original variables, the contaminating variables, and the probability of contamination.

We have introduced a new robust version of TR, by running its algorithm with the (regularized) MCD estimates
Boudt et al. 2020
of the between and within covariance matrices. In the simulation study of Section
5
, the performance of the method has been compared with its classical counterpart, FDA, QDA, and AdaBoost. In all scenarios, robust estimates have a good impact on both TR and FDA performances. In some cases, FDA shows a higher accuracy than AdaBoost: this reinforces the idea that a nonlinear model does not necessarily have a higher accuracy than a linear one. We have also studied FDA and TR for an increasing number of irrelevant variables, and noticed that the accuracy of the classification decays for both FDA and TR, even when the covariance matrices are estimated robustly. This decay is faster when the group sizes are much smaller than the number of irrelevant variables.

Finally, we have assessed the performance of FDA and TR as dimensionality reduction methods on real datasets, in combination with robust LDA as classifier. In general, FDA performs as well as or better than TR; in most problems, both methods reach the performance of the full model, where the classifier is trained without any dimensionality reduction; in a few cases, they perform even slightly better. For the tested real datasets, the robust estimators have little impact on the accuracy of FDA, while, in general, a big improvement is observed in the TR method.
