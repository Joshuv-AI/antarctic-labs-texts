Introduction

We consider the one-dimensional stochastic heat equation (SHE) given by

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{x}u(t,x)+\sigma(u(t,x))\dot{W}(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\quad x\in\mathds{R},\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
\dot{W}
represents space-time (Gaussian) white noise on
\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R}
,
u_{0}
is a sufficiently smooth and compactly supported function, and
\sigma:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
is a continuous function with
\sigma(0)=0
. The principal goal is to explore how the nonlinearity
\sigma(u)
influences the support of solutions to (
1.1
). Specifically, we are interested in conditions on
\sigma
under which solutions to (
1.1
) exhibit either full support (i.e. the solution is strictly positive everywhere) or compact support with probability 1.

When
\sigma(u)
is proportional to
u^{\gamma}
near
u=0
for
\gamma>0
, there is an extensive literature that provides a detailed understanding of how the support of the solution is influenced by the value of
\gamma
. In
[
11
]
, Iscoe was among the first to demonstrate that for
\gamma=1/2
, a solution to (
1.1
) exhibits compact support for all
t>0
with probability 1, a property we refer to as the Compact Support Property (CSP). Note that for
\gamma=1/2
,
u
is considered as the density of super-Brownian motion and Iscoe used some properties of super-Brownian motion to show CSP. Following this, Shiga
[
21
]
extended Iscoe’s methodology to establish CSP for (
1.1
) with
\gamma\in(0,1/2)
. On the other hand, Mueller and Perkins
[
17
]
considered the case where
\gamma\in(1/2,1)
. This particular case is more delicate and interesting because this case can be considered as a super-Brownian motion with reduced birth-death rates (i.e. the coefficient of the noise
u^{\gamma}
is effectively less than
u^{1/2}
near
u=0
). By creatively constructing and employing a historical process, they proved that CSP remains valid in this delicate case. Diverging from these approaches, Krylov
[
16
]
used a weak solution framework to (
1.1
) to prove CSP for
\gamma\in(0,1)
. In contrast, Mueller
[
20
]
investigated the case
\gamma\geq 1
, demonstrating that
u(t,x)
remains strictly positive for all
t>0
and
x\in\mathds{R}
with probability 1, thereby indicating the absence of CSP in these cases. This background leads us to the following natural questions:

•

What outcomes can be expected for
\sigma
satisfying
u\ll\sigma(u)\ll u^{\gamma}
when
u
is near zero? In particular, under what conditions can
\sigma
ensure CSP or guarantee strict positivity of solutions to (
1.1
)?

Those questions have previously been addressed within the context of deterministic partial differential equations (PDEs). More precisely, consider

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{x}u(t,x)-\sigma(u(t,x)),\quad(t,x)\in\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\quad x\in\mathds{R}.\end{cases}

(1.2)

where
u_{0}
is a non-negative and continuous function with compact support, and
\sigma:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}_{+}
is a non-decreasing and continuous function with
\sigma(0)=0
and
\sigma(u)>0
for all
u\in\mathds{R}\setminus\{0\}
. In 1974, Kalashnikov
[
12
, Theorem 7]
proved that CSP holds for (
1.2
) if

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{du}{\sqrt{u\sigma(u)}}<+\infty.

(1.3)

Furthermore, in
[
12
, Theorem 11]
, strict positivity of the solution to (
1.2
) is shown to hold when

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{du}{\sigma(u)}=+\infty,

(1.4)

when
u_{0}(x)>0
for all
x\in\mathds{R}
. On the other hand,
[
8
, Remark 2.4]
shows that if

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{du}{\sqrt{u\sigma(u)}}=\infty,

(1.5)

then there exist initial functions
u_{0}
such that the solution
u(t,x)
to (
1.2
) with initial condition
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)
is strictly positive for all
x\in\mathds{R}
and all
t\in[0,t_{0}]
for some
t_{0}>0
. Numerous studies have examined CSP and strict positivity in deterministic PDEs, establishing conditions for these properties (see, e.g.
[
5
,
8
,
9
,
14
]
for related results). However, to the best of our knowledge, literature on conditions for CSP and strict positivity for stochastic heat equations other than the case where
\sigma(u)\approx u^{\gamma}
is extremely limited. This paper aims to bridge this gap by proposing specific conditions on
\sigma
that ensure CSP or strict positivity. We now detail these conditions below.

Assumption 1.1
.

We assume that
\sigma:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}_{+}
is a nondecreasing continuous function with
\sigma(u)\mathds{1}_{u\leq 0}=0
for
u\in\mathds{R}
. We further assume that there exist constants
\mathsf{d}>0
and
L_{\mathsf{d}}>0
such that

(a)

For all
u,v>0
with
u\leq v\leq\mathsf{d}
, we have
0<\frac{\sigma(v)}{v}\leq\frac{\sigma(u)}{u}
,

(b)

|\sigma(u)|\leq L_{\mathsf{d}}|u|
and
|\sigma(u)-\sigma(v)|\leq L_{\mathsf{d}}|u-v|
for all
u,v\geq\mathsf{d}.

Remark 1.2
.

The assumption that
\sigma(u)\mathds{1}_{u\leq 0}=0
is somewhat technical and can be weakened. Throughout the paper, we only deal with nonnegative solutions to (
1.1
) (see Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
1.7
). Therefore, we only need to define
\sigma(u)
for
u\geq 0
and set
\sigma(0)=0
.

We note that Assumption
1.1
guarantees the existence of a (
mild and weak
) solution. However, the uniqueness is a tricky problem (see
[
1
,
18
,
19
]
). Under some additional condition on
\sigma
, we can obtain the uniqueness of a mild solution and the strict positivity of the solution.

Theorem 1.3

(
Well-posedness and Strict positivity).

Suppose
\sigma:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
satisfies Assumption
1.1
and there exist
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
and
v_{1}=v_{1}(\alpha)>0
such that for all
v\in(0,v_{1})

\frac{v}{\sigma(v)}\geq(-\log v)^{-\alpha}.

(1.6)

Let
u_{0}\in C(\mathds{R})
be bounded, nonnegative and not identically zero. Then, we have the following:

(i)

There exists a unique mild solution
u
to (
1.1
), and
u
has a version which is space-time Hölder continuous of regularity
\left(\frac{1}{2}-,\frac{1}{4}-\right)
.

(ii)

For any
T>0
,

\mathds{P}\left(u(t,x)>0\text{ for all }(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathds{R}\right)=1.

To establish Theorem
1.3
, we employ the weak comparison principle. This principle plays a crucial role in our proofs and is also of independent interest.

Theorem 1.4

(
Weak comparison principle).

Let
u^{(1)}
and
u^{(2)}
be two solutions to (
1.1
) with initial data
u^{(1)}_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathds{R})
and
u^{(2)}_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathds{R})
respectively, with
\sigma
satisfying Assumption
1.1
. Suppose that (
1.6
) holds and
u^{(1)}_{0}(x)\geq u^{(2)}_{0}(x)
for all
x\in\mathds{R}
. Then we have for any
T>0

\mathds{P}\left(u^{(1)}(t,x)\geq u^{(2)}(t,x)\text{ for all }(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathds{R}\right)=1.

In particular, the solution
u
in Theorem
1.3
is nonnegative if
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathds{R})
is nonnegative.

Remark 1.5
.

It is worth noting that Mytnik and Perkins
[
18
]
established the pathwise uniqueness when
\sigma(u)
is Hölder continuous with an exponent greater than 3/4. However, the combination of (
1.7
) and (
3.2
) implies that
\sigma(u)
is indeed Hölder continuous with an exponent greater than 3/4, thus ensuring that our condition is stronger than that of Mytnik and Perkins. We point out that our method provides the weak comparison principle and strict positivity whereas they are not shown in
[
18
]
.

Remark 1.6
.

In Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
, the assumptions on the regularity of initial data can be relaxed considerably. Indeed, one can prove that initial functions are allowed to be any measure
\mu
on
\mathds{R}
such that
\int_{\mathds{R}}\exp(-cx^{2})\mu(dx)<\infty
for all
c>0
following the ideas in the proof of
[
4
, Theorem 1.3]
.

We now introduce another condition on
\sigma
that ensures CSP for solutions to (
1.1
). It is important to note that this condition does not guarantee the uniqueness of a weak solution, and indeed, uniqueness is not required for our purposes. We will show that under this condition, any nonnegative weak solution to (
1.1
) exhibits CSP.

Theorem 1.7

(
Existence and Compact support property).

Suppose
\sigma:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
satisfies Assumption
1.1
and there exists
\alpha\in(\frac{5}{2},\infty)
and
v_{2}=v_{2}(\alpha)>0
such that for all
v\in(0,v_{2})

\frac{v}{\sigma(v)}\leq(-\log v)^{-\alpha}.

(1.7)

Let
u_{0}\in C^{1/2}(\mathds{R})
be nonnegative and compactly supported. Let
\tau
be any bounded stopping time. Then, we have the following:

(i)

There exists a stochastically weak solution
u\in C([0,\tau];C_{tem}(\mathds{R}))
to (
1.1
) that is also nonnegative. Additionally, for any weak solution
u\in C([0,\tau];C_{tem}(\mathds{R}))
to (
1.1
) and any
\gamma\in(0,1/4)
, the following holds for every
a>0:

\|\Psi_{a}u\|_{C^{\gamma}([0,\tau]\times\mathds{R})}<\infty\quad\text{almost surely,}

(1.8)

where
\Psi_{a}(x)=\Psi_{a}(|x|)=\frac{1}{\cosh(a|x|)}.

(ii)

For any nonnegative weak solution
u\in C([0,\tau];C_{tem}(\mathds{R}))
to (
1.1
), we have that

\mathds{P}\Big(\text{There exists }R=R(\omega)>0\text{ such that }\mathrm{supp}(u(t,\cdot))\subseteq[-R,R]\Big)=1,

(1.9)

where
\mathrm{supp}(u(t,\cdot))
denotes the support of the solution
u(t,\cdot)
for each
t\geq 0
.

Remark 1.8
.

Theorem
1.3
shows that the solution is strictly positive whenever
\sigma(u)\lesssim u|\log u|^{\alpha}
for all
0\leq u\ll 1
, for
\alpha\in(0,1/4)
. On the other hand, Theorem
1.7
indicates that whenever
\sigma(u)\gtrsim u|\log u|^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha>5/2
, any weak solution is compactly supported. For instance, if
\sigma(u)=u|\log u|^{\beta}|\log\log 1/u|^{\gamma}
for
0\leq u\ll 1
and any
\gamma>0
, we have a strictly positive solution if
\beta\in(0,1/4)
. Conversely, any nonnegative weak solution is compactly supported when
\beta>5/2
. These findings extend the well-established results for
\sigma(u)=u^{\gamma}
and demonstrate their consistency with Theorems
1.3
and
1.7
.

Remark 1.9
.

For the deterministic heat equation (
1.2
), the condition for CSP is given by (
1.3
), which is equivalent to

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{e^{-k}}{\sigma(e^{-k})}}<\infty.

(1.10)

The following heuristic argument provides an intuitive understanding of this condition: Suppose at time
t_{k-1}
,
u(t_{k-1},x)\approx 1
for
x\in[-x_{k-1},x_{k-1}]
. Define
t_{k}
as the time at which the solution, affected only by the negative nonlinear drift
-\sigma
(ignoring the Laplacian), decreases to half of its previous value of
u(t_{k-1},x_{k-1})
. Let
x_{k}
be the spatial point where this decay occurs. Then, considering only the effect of
\sigma
,
t_{k+1}-t_{k}
can be roughly approximated by
e^{-k}/\sigma(e^{-k})
. Due to the heat scaling, the spatial spread
x_{k+1}-x_{k}
can be approximated by
\sqrt{e^{-k}/\sigma(e^{-k})}
. Thus, CSP is expected to hold if
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(x_{k+1}-x_{k})
is finite, as indicated by (
1.10
). While a rigorous proof of this idea is beyond the scope of this remark, it serves as a useful starting point for understanding conditions for CSP and raises the question of whether there exists a parallel condition for the stochastic heat equation (
1.1
). Although Theorems
1.3
and
1.7
do not provide a sharp condition for (
1.1
), by extending the heuristic argument into SPDEs, we might predict that
t_{k+1}-t_{k}\approx\left[e^{-k}/\sigma(e^{-k})\right]^{4}
since
u(\cdot,x)
for each
x\in\mathds{R}
behaves as a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index
1/4
(see
[
13
, Section 3]
). This leads to the proposed critical condition for CSP in (
1.1
):

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{e^{-k}}{\sigma(e^{-k})}\right]^{2}<\infty.

Proof ideas

We now briefly discuss the proofs of Theorems
1.3
and
1.7
, focusing on strict positivity (Theorem
1.3
(ii)) and CSP (Theorem
1.7
(ii)). For the proof of Theorem
1.3
(ii), we adopt the strategy introduced by Mueller
[
20
]
, which involves a mild solution (see Definition
2.1
) consisting of two components: the solution to the deterministic heat equation, which is inherently strictly positive, and the stochastic integral part influenced by noise. Mueller’s approach relies on the observation that if the noise-induced fluctuations are sufficiently small over a short duration, the solution’s strict positivity is maintained. In our analysis, we focus on tightly controlling the influence of noise to ensure strict positivity, which is an improvement over Mueller’s original methodology in that we deal with a more general class of nonlinearities
\sigma
(see Proposition
4.1
).

On the other hand, for the proof of Theorem
1.7
(ii), we follow the strategy by Krylov
[
16
]
, which introduces a framework based on weak solutions (see Definition
2.2
) to establish CSP for
\sigma(u)=u^{\gamma}
. Central to this framework is Lemma 2.1 from
[
16
]
, an analogue of which appears as Lemma
5.2
in this paper. Since Krylov’s work focused on
\sigma(u)=u^{\gamma}
, he was able to use Jensen’s inequality to obtain Lemma 2.1 in
[
16
]
. However, given the broader scope of our
\sigma
beyond
u^{\gamma}
, applying Jensen’s inequality directly proves challenging. To overcome this challenge, our method introduces a novel partitioning of the time interval according to the size of the solution (see Section
5.2
). We examine the behavior of the solution in distinct time intervals: those where the solution exhibits relatively low values and those where it is comparably higher. This approach allows us to formulate Lemma
5.2
effectively, adapting Krylov’s framework to our more general setting.

provides definitions of mild and weak solutions of (

). The proofs of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
are presented in Sections
3
and
4
. Finally, Section

is dedicated to the proof of Theorem

.

Well-posedness and weak comparison principle

Throughout this section, we assume that

\delta\in(0,\mathsf{d})
, we have

|\sigma(u)-\sigma(v)|\leq\left(L_{\mathsf{d}}\vee\delta^{-1}\sigma(\delta)\right)|u-v|+\sigma(\delta),\quad\text{for all }u,v\in\mathds{R}.

(3.1)

Moreover, we have

|\sigma(u)-\sigma(v)|\leq L_{\mathsf{d}}|u-v|+\sigma(|u-v|),\quad\text{for all }u,v\in\mathds{R},

(3.2)

Proof.

We prove the lemma for the case
u\geq v\geq 0
since
\sigma(u)\mathds{1}_{u\leq 0}=0
(see Assumption
1.1
). If
u\geq v\geq\mathsf{d}>\delta
, then (b) of Assumption
1.1
immediately shows that (
3.1
) holds. For the case
u\geq\mathsf{d}\geq v\geq\delta
, we have

\displaystyle|\sigma(u)-\sigma(v)|

\displaystyle\leq\sigma(u)-\sigma(\mathsf{d})+\sigma(\mathsf{d})-\sigma(v)

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mathsf{d}}(u-\mathsf{d})+\frac{\sigma(\mathsf{d})}{\mathsf{d}}\mathsf{d}-\frac{\sigma(v)}{v}v

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mathsf{d}}(u-\mathsf{d})+\frac{\sigma(v)}{v}(d-v)

\displaystyle\leq\left(L_{\mathsf{d}}\vee\frac{\sigma(\delta)}{\delta}\right)(u-v).

In the second line, we have used Assumption
1.1
(b) for the first term. In the third line, we used Assumption
1.1
(a) for the second term. In the last line, we again used Assumption
1.1
(a). For the case
u\geq\mathsf{d}\geq\delta\geq v
, we similarly obtain that

\displaystyle\sigma(u)-\sigma(v)

\displaystyle\leq\sigma(u)-\sigma(\mathsf{d})+\sigma(\mathsf{d})-\sigma(\delta)+\sigma(\delta)-\sigma(v)

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mathsf{d}}(u-\mathsf{d})+\frac{\sigma(\delta)}{\delta}(\mathsf{d}-\delta)+\frac{\sigma(\delta)}{\delta}(\delta-v)

\displaystyle\leq\left(L_{\mathsf{d}}\vee\frac{\sigma(\delta)}{\delta}\right)(u-v).

For the last term in the second line, we have used

\sigma(\delta)-\sigma(v)=\frac{\sigma(\delta)}{\delta}\delta-\frac{\sigma(v)}{v}v\leq\frac{\sigma(\delta)}{\delta}(\delta-v),

due to the monotonicity of
v\mapsto\sigma(v)/v
in Assumption
1.1
(a). The case
\mathsf{d}\geq u\geq v\geq\delta
can be proven similarly, as

\sigma(u)-\sigma(v)=\frac{\sigma(u)}{u}u-\frac{\sigma(v)}{v}v\leq\frac{\sigma(v)}{v}(u-v)\leq\frac{\sigma(\delta)}{\delta}(u-v),

since
\sigma(u)/u\leq\sigma(v)/v\leq\sigma(\delta)/\delta
. For the case
\mathsf{d}\geq u\geq\delta\geq v
, we observe that

\sigma(u)-\sigma(v)\leq\frac{\sigma(u)}{u}u-\frac{\sigma(\delta)}{\delta}\delta+\frac{\sigma(\delta)}{\delta}\delta-\frac{\sigma(v)}{v}v\leq\frac{\sigma(\delta)}{\delta}(u-v),

again owing to the monotonicity of
v\mapsto\sigma(v)/v
. In the final case
\delta\geq u\geq v
, we can simply bound

\sigma(u)-\sigma(v)\leq\sigma(u)\leq\sigma(\delta).

To prove (
3.2
), observe that for all
\mathsf{d}\geq u\geq v

\frac{(u-v)\sigma(u)}{u}\leq\sigma(u-v),\quad\frac{v\sigma(u)}{u}\leq\sigma(v),

which implies that

\sigma(u)-\sigma(v)\leq\sigma(u-v).

If
u\geq\mathsf{d}\geq v
, then we have

\displaystyle\sigma(u)-\sigma(v)

\displaystyle\leq\sigma(u)-\sigma(\mathsf{d})+\sigma(\mathsf{d})-\sigma(v)\ \leq L_{\mathsf{d}}(u-\mathsf{d})+\sigma(\mathsf{d}-v)\ \leq L_{\mathsf{d}}(u-v)+\sigma(u-v)

by Assumption
1.1
. The case
u\geq v\geq\mathsf{d}
is proven immediately by Assumption
1.1
(b).
∎

The existence and uniqueness of a mild solution to (
1.1
) can be established using a classical fixed point argument or a Picard iteration method when
\sigma
is globally Lipschitz continuous. However, in our case, the fact that
\sigma(u)/u
can blow up as
u\to 0
makes these classical methods inapplicable. Lemma
3.1
suggests a potential workaround: by truncating
\sigma(u)
when
u
is small, we can make the truncated
\sigma
globally Lipschitz. More specifically, we consider the following stochastic heat equations: For each integer
m\geq 1
, we consider the following stochastic heat equation:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u^{(m)}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{x}u^{(m)}(t,x)+\sigma^{(m)}(u^{(m)}(t,x))\dot{W}(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R},\\
u^{(m)}(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\quad x\in\mathds{R},\end{cases}

(3.3)

where

\sigma^{(m)}(u):=\sigma(u)\mathds{1}_{u\geq 1/m}+m\sigma(m^{-1})u\mathds{1}_{0\leq u<1/m}\quad\text{for all }u\geq 0,

(3.4)

and
\sigma^{(m)}(u)\mathds{1}_{u\geq 0}=0
. We can follow the same line of the proof of Lemma
3.1
to observe that there exists
m_{0}\geq 1
such that for all
m\geq m_{0}
,

|\sigma^{(m)}(u)-\sigma^{(m)}(v)|\leq(m\sigma(m^{-1})\vee L_{\mathsf{d}})|u-v|\quad\text{for all }u,v\in\mathds{R}.

Note that the only difference from (
3.1
) occurs when
0\leq u,v\leq 1/m
. However, in this case, we get
|\sigma^{(m)}(u)-\sigma^{(m)}(v)|\leq m\sigma(m^{-1})|u-v|
from the definition of
\sigma^{(m)}
in (
3.4
). Without loss of generality, throughout the remainder of this section, we assume that for all
m\geq m_{0}
,

|\sigma^{(m)}(u)-\sigma^{(m)}(v)|\leq m\sigma(m^{-1})|u-v|\quad\text{for all }u,v\in\mathds{R}.

(3.5)

Indeed, if we assume that
m\sigma(m^{-1})\leq L_{\mathsf{d}}
for all
m\geq 1
, then
\sigma^{(m)}
is globally Lipschitz continuous for all
m\geq 1
, which would simplify the proof considerably. We also note that (
3.5
) implies that
\sigma^{(m)}
is globally Lipschitz continuous for each
m\geq m_{0}
. Therefore, for each
m\geq m_{0}
, there exists a unique mild solution to (
3.3
), which is also Hölder continuous in time and space. Moreover, it is known that the weak comparison principle holds for such equations, as demonstrated in the works of Shiga
[
21
]
and Chen and Kim
[
4
]
. The weak comparison principle is crucial for establishing the nonnegativity of solutions and comparing solutions with different initial data. The following lemma, which can be found in
[
21
, Corollary 2.4]
and
[
4
, Theorem 1.1]
, states the weak comparison principle for the solutions of (
3.3
):

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
u^{(m,1)}
and
u^{(m,2)}
be two mild solutions to (
3.3
) with initial data
u^{(1)}_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathds{R})
and
u^{(2)}_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathds{R})
respectively. Suppose that
u^{(1)}_{0}(x)\geq u^{(2)}_{0}(x)
for all
x\in\mathds{R}
. Then, we have

\mathds{P}\left(u^{(m,1)}(t,x)\geq u^{(m,2)}(t,x)\text{ for all }(t,x)\in\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R}\right)=1.

In particular, the solution
u^{(m)}
of (
3.3
) is nonnegative if
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathds{R})
is nonnegative.

To prove the existence and uniqueness of a mild solution to (
1.1
), we first establish moment estimates for the solutions
u^{(m)}
to the truncated equation (
3.3
). These estimates play a key role in showing that the sequence
u^{(m)}
converges as
m\to\infty
. From (
3.5
), we have the following moment estimate for the solution
u^{(m)}
of (
3.3
): For all
p\geq 2
there exists
C=C(\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}},L_{\mathsf{d}})>0
such that for any
T>0

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\|u^{(m)}(t,x)\|_{p}^{p}\leq C^{p}\exp\left(Cm^{4}\sigma^{4}(m^{-1})p^{3}T\right)

(3.6)

(see
[
13
,
4
,
3
]
for instance). Observe that the RHS diverges as
m\to\infty
. However, from the definition of
\sigma^{(m)}
in
3.4
and Assumption
1.1
, there exists
C=C(L_{\mathsf{d}})>0
such that uniformly over all
m\geq 1
, for all
u\in\mathds{R}

|\sigma^{(m)}(u)|\leq C(1+|u|).

(3.7)

From this fact, it is quite standard to deduce that the moments of
u^{(m)}
can be bounded above uniformly in
m\geq 1
. We present the proof for the convenience of the reader.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
u^{(m)}
be a mild solution to (
3.3
) with initial data
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathds{R})
. There exists
C=C(L_{\mathsf{d}},\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}})>0
such that for all
p\geq 2
,
m\geq 1
and
T>0
,

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\|u^{(m)}(t,x)\|^{p}_{p}\leq C^{p}\exp(Cp^{3}T).

Proof.

Let
p\geq 2
and
T>0
. By the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy (BDG) inequality (see
[
13
, Theorem B.1]
), there exists
C=C(L_{\mathsf{d}})>0
such that for all sufficiently large
m\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\|u^{(m)}(t,x)\|_{p}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+Cp\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathds{R}}G^{2}(t-s,y-x)\left(1+\|u^{(m)}(s,y)\|_{p}^{2}\right)dyds

\displaystyle\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+Cp\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-1/2}\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\|u^{(m)}(s,x)\|_{p}^{2}ds,

where in the second inequality we used that for all
x\in\mathds{R}

\int_{\mathds{R}}G^{2}(t-s,y-x)dy\lesssim(t-s)^{-1/2}.

(3.8)

Then, we can apply a version of Gronwall’s inequality (see
[
23
, Corollary 2]
) to obtian that

\|u^{(m)}(t,x)\|_{p}^{2}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}E_{\frac{1}{2}}\left(Cpt^{\frac{1}{2}}\right),

where
E_{\frac{1}{2}}
denotes the Mittag-Leffler function with index
\frac{1}{2}
. Since
E_{\frac{1}{2}}(z)\lesssim\exp(z^{2})
for
z\in\mathds{R}
, we have that

\|u^{(m)}(t,x)\|_{p}^{2}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}\exp\left(C^{2}p^{2}t\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

Next, we aim to show that the sequence of solutions
u^{(m)}
to the truncated equation (
3.3
) converges as
m\to\infty
. We also need to verify that the limit function is indeed a mild solution to the original stochastic heat equation (
1.1
). This step is crucial for proving the existence and uniqueness of a mild solution, as the Lipschitz coefficient of
\sigma^{(m)}
in (
3.5
) may blow up as
m\to\infty
, making the passage to the limit a non-trivial task. However, the assumption (
1.6
) allows us to control the growth of the nonlinear term, which leads to the convergence of the sequence
u^{(m)}
and the uniqueness of a mild solution to (
1.1
).

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
u^{(m)}
be a mild solution to (
3.3
) with initial data
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathds{R})
. Suppose that
\sigma:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
satisfies (
1.6
). Then, for any
T>0
, there exists a unique mild solution
u\in C([0,T]\times\mathds{R};L^{2}(\Omega))
to (
1.1
) such that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\sup_{t\leq T}\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\|u^{(m)}(t,x)-u(t,x)\|_{2}=0.

Proof.

Throughout the proof, we fix a constant
T>0
and assume that
t\in[0,T]
. We first show that for each
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathds{R}
,
\{u^{(m)}(t,x)\}_{m\geq 1}
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{2}(\Omega).
Set

{\bf J}_{m}(t,x):=u^{(m)}(t,x)-u^{(m-1)}(t,x).

By the Itô-Walsh isometry, we have

\displaystyle\|{\bf J}_{m}(t,x)\|_{2}^{2}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathds{R}}G^{2}(t-s,y-x)\|\sigma^{(m)}(u^{(m)}(s,y))-\sigma^{(m-1)}(u^{(m-1)}(s,y))\|_{2}^{2}dyds.

(3.9)

Observe that

\displaystyle\|\sigma^{(m)}(u^{(m)}(s,y))-\sigma^{(m-1)}(u^{(m-1)}(s,y))\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\sigma^{(m)}(u^{(m)}(s,y))-\sigma^{(m)}(u^{(m-1)}(s,y))\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\sigma^{(m)}(u^{(m-1)}(s,y))-\sigma^{(m-1)}(u^{(m-1)}(s,y))\|_{2}^{2}.

From the definition of
\sigma^{(m)}
(see (
3.4
)), we can follow the same line in the proof of Lemma
3.1
to obtain that for all sufficiently large
m\geq 1

\|\sigma^{(m)}(u^{(m)}(s,y))-\sigma^{(m)}(u^{(m-1)}(s,y))\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim m^{2}\sigma^{2}(m^{-1})\|{\bf J}_{m}(s,y)\|_{2}^{2}.

Moreover, by (
3.4
) and Assumption
1.1
, there exists
m_{0}\geq 1
such that for all
m\geq m_{0}

\displaystyle|\sigma^{(m)}(u)-\sigma^{(m-1)}(u)|

\displaystyle\leq|\sigma(u)-(m-1)\sigma((m-1)^{-1})u|\cdot\mathds{1}_{m^{-1}\leq u<(m-1)^{-1}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+|m\sigma(m^{-1})-(m-1)\sigma((m-1)^{-1})|u\cdot\mathds{1}_{0\leq u<m^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq|\sigma((m-1)^{-1})-(m-1)\sigma((m-1)^{-1})m^{-1}|+|m\sigma((m-1)^{-1})-(m-1)\sigma((m-1)^{-1})|\cdot m^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq 2m^{-1}\sigma((m-1)^{-1}).

Therefore, it turns out that

\|\sigma^{(m)}(u^{(m)}(s,y))-\sigma^{(m-1)}(u^{(m-1)}(s,y))\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim m^{2}\sigma^{2}(m^{-1})\|{\bf J}_{m}(s,y)\|_{2}^{2}+m^{-2}\sigma^{2}((m-1)^{-1}).

(3.10)

Let
\mathfrak{J}_{m}(t):=\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\|{\bf J}_{m}(t,x)\|_{2}^{2}
. Then, from (
3.9
) and (
3.10
), there exists
C>0
such that

\mathfrak{J}_{m}(t)\leq C\left(m^{2}\sigma^{2}(m^{-1})\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-1/2}\mathfrak{J}_{m}(s)ds+m^{-2}\sigma^{2}((m-1)^{-1})\right),

where we have used (
3.8
). Then, we use the Grönwall inequality as in the proof of Lemma
3.3
to obtian that for some
C>0
,

\mathfrak{J}_{m}(t)\leq Cm^{-2}\sigma^{2}((m-1)^{-1})E_{\frac{1}{2}}\left(Cm^{2}\sigma^{2}(m^{-1})t^{\frac{1}{2}}\right).

Again using the fact
E_{\frac{1}{2}}(z)\leq\exp(z^{2})
for all
z\in\mathds{R}
, we have

\mathfrak{J}_{m}(t)\leq Cm^{-2}\sigma^{2}((m-1)^{-1})\exp\left(C^{2}m^{4}\sigma^{4}(m^{-1})T\right),

(3.11)

for all
t\in[0,T]
.
To conclude that
u^{(m)}(t,x)
is Cauchy, we will show that
\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(\mathfrak{J}_{m}(t))^{1/2}<\infty
. Specifically, for all
n>m\geq 1
, we have

\|u^{(n)}(t,x)-u^{(m)}(t,x)\|_{2}\leq\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\left(\mathfrak{J}_{k}(t)\right)^{1/2}\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

Now it is clear that to show
\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(\mathfrak{J}_{m}(t))^{1/2}<\infty
is sufficient for the proof. To prove this, we claim that for any constant
c>0
, there exists
m_{1}\geq 1
such that for all
m\geq m_{1}

m^{-1}\sigma((m-1)^{-1})\exp(cm^{4}\sigma^{4}(m^{-1}))\leq m^{-3/2}.

(3.12)

This inequality follows from the assumption (
1.6
). In particular, by (
1.6
), there exist constants
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
and
m_{1}=m_{1}(\alpha)>0
such that for all
m\geq m_{1}

m\cdot\sigma(m^{-1})\leq(\log m)^{\alpha}.

(3.13)

Now we find a constant
m_{2}=m_{2}(\alpha)>m_{1}
such that for all
m\geq m_{2}

\displaystyle m^{-1}\sigma((m-1)^{-1})\exp(cm^{4}\sigma^{4}(m^{-1}))

\displaystyle\leq m^{-2}\log(m)^{\alpha}\cdot\exp(c(m\sigma(m^{-1}))^{4})

\displaystyle\leq m^{-2}(\log m)^{\alpha}\cdot\exp\left(c(\log m)^{4\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\leq m^{-3/2}.

This completes the proof of the claim. Now, from (
3.11
), for any
t\in(0,T]
there exists
C=C(T)>0
such that

(\mathfrak{J}_{m}(t))^{1/2}\leq\frac{C}{m^{3/2}},

(3.14)

which shows that as
m,n\to\infty

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\|u^{(n)}(t,x)-u^{(m)}(t,x)\|_{2}\leq\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\left(\mathfrak{J}_{k}(t)\right)^{1/2}\leq\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\frac{C}{k^{3/2}}\to 0.

(3.15)

This concludes that
\{u^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
is a Cauchy sequence in
C([0,T]\times\mathds{R};L^{2}(\Omega))
. Therefore, for each
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathds{R}
, we can define the
L^{2}-
limit of
u^{(m)}(t,x)
, say
\tilde{u}(t,x)
, which also satisfies
\tilde{u}\in C([0,T]\times\mathds{R};L^{2}(\Omega))
. One can easily verify that
u
is indeed a mild solution to (
1.1
), which is adapted to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t}
. Indeed,
u^{(m)}-u^{(n)}
is equal to
{\bf{N}}^{(m)}-{\bf{N}}^{(n)}
where
{\bf{N}}^{(m)}
is defined as

{\bf{N}}^{(m)}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathds{R}}G(t-s,y-x)\sigma^{(m)}(u^{(m)}(s,y))W(dsdy).

This shows that we can define
{\bf{N}}
by the limit of
{\bf{N}}^{(m)}
in
C([0,T]\times\mathds{R};L^{2}(\Omega))
, which implies that for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathds{R}

\tilde{u}(t,x):=(G(t,\cdot)*u_{0})(x)+{\bf N}(t,x)=\lim_{m\to\infty}u^{(m)}(t,x)

is well-defined. Moreover,
\tilde{u}(t,x)
is adapted to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
as a stochastic process in
C([0,T]\times\mathds{R};L^{2}(\Omega))
. We then can define the Itô-Walsh integral

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathds{R}}G(t-s,y-x)\sigma(\tilde{u}(s,y))W(dsdy).

To justify this, we note that
\tilde{u}
satisfies the uniform boundedness of the second moment of
\sigma(\tilde{u})
. Specifically, by (
3.7
), there exists a constant
C=C(L_{\mathsf{d}},T)>0
such that

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\mathds{E}[\sigma^{2}(\tilde{u}(t,x))]\leq C\left(1+\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\mathds{E}[|\tilde{u}(t,x)|^{2}]\right)<\infty.

This uniform boundedness ensures the existence of the Itô-Walsh integral involving
\sigma(\tilde{u})
.
To conclude that
\tilde{u}
is indeed a mild solution
u
of (
1.1
) (see Definition
2.1
), it remains to show that the above stochastic integral coincides with
{\bf N}(t,x)
for each
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathds{R}
. Once again by (
3.4
), we have that

\displaystyle|\sigma(\tilde{u})-\sigma^{(m)}(u^{(m)})|

\displaystyle\leq|\sigma(\tilde{u})-\sigma^{(m)}(\tilde{u})|+|\sigma^{(m)}(\tilde{u})-\sigma^{(m)}(u^{(m)})|

(3.16)

\displaystyle\leq\sigma(m^{-1})+m\sigma(m^{-1})|\tilde{u}-u^{(m)}|.

This implies that by the Itô-Walsh isometry,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}

\displaystyle\left[\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathds{R}}G(t-s,y-x)\sigma(\tilde{u}(s,y))W(dsdy)-{\bf N}^{(m)}(t,x)\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathds{R}}G^{2}(t-s,y-x)\|\sigma(\tilde{u}(s,y))-\sigma^{(m)}(u^{(m)}(s,y))\|_{2}^{2}dyds.

On the other hand, by (
3.13
) and (
3.15
), there exists
C=C(T)>0
such that for all
(s,y)\in[0,T]\times\mathds{R}

\displaystyle m\sigma(m^{-1})\|\tilde{u}(s,y)-u^{(m)}(s,y)\|_{2}

\displaystyle=m\sigma(m^{-1})\lim_{n\to\infty}\|u^{(n)}(s,y)-u^{(m)}(s,y)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(\log m)^{\alpha}\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{3/2}},

for all sufficiently large
m\geq 1
. Therefore, by the dominated convergence theorem, we have

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathds{R}}G(t-s,y-x)\sigma(\tilde{u}(s,y))W(dsdy)=\lim_{m\to\infty}{\bf N}(t,x),

which shows that
u:=\tilde{u}
is indeed a mild solution of (
1.1
).

The uniqueness of the solution follows in a similar way. Suppose that
u
and
v
are solutions to (
1.1
) with same initial data. Then, by the Itô-Walsh isometry and Lemma
3.1
,

\displaystyle\|u(t,x)-v(t,x)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathds{R}}G^{2}(t-s,y-x)\|\sigma(u(s,y))-\sigma(v(s,y))\|_{2}^{2}dyds

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathds{R}}G^{2}(t-s,y-x)\left(n^{2}\sigma^{2}(n^{-1})\|u(s,y)-v(s,y)\|_{2}^{2}+\sigma^{2}(n^{-1})\right)dyds,

for all sufficiently large
n\geq 1
. As before, we then get

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\|u(t,x)-v(t,x)\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim n^{2}\sigma^{2}(n^{-1})\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-1/2}\cdot\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\|u(s,x)-v(s,x)\|_{2}^{2}ds+\sigma^{2}(n^{-1}).

By the Grönwall inequality, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\|u(t,x)-v(t,x)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{2}(n^{-1})E_{\frac{1}{2}}\left(Cn^{2}\sigma^{2}(n^{-1})T^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{2}(n^{-1})\exp\left(C^{2}n^{4}\sigma^{4}(n^{-1})T\right).

By (
3.13
) and the argument below (
3.13
), we can derive that
\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\|u(t,x)-v(t,x)\|_{2}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. This concludes that
u=v
in
C([0,T]\times\mathds{R};L^{2}(\Omega))
.
∎

From now on, we assume that
u(t,x)
is the unique mild solution of (
1.1
) from Proposition
3.4
. In the next lemma, we present the moment estimates for the solution
u
of (
1.1
), which is a key input for the proofs of the Hölder continuity in time and space and also the strict positivity of
u
.

Lemma 3.5
.

There exist constants
C=C(\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}})>0
and
K=K(L_{\mathsf{d}})>0
such that for any
T>0
,
p\geq 2
, and
\delta\in(0,\mathsf{d}\wedge 1)
,

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\mathds{E}[|u(t,x)|^{p}]\leq C^{p}\exp\left(K\delta^{-4}\sigma^{4}(\delta)p^{3}T\right).

(3.17)

Proof.

Let
\Phi:=\{\Phi(t,x)\}_{(t,x)\in\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R}}
be a random field, and for every
\alpha>0
and
p\geq 2
, define

\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,p}(\Phi):=\sup_{t\geq 0}\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\left(e^{-\alpha t}\|\Phi(t,x)\|_{p}\right).

(3.18)

Note that
\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,p}(u)<\infty
, since by Fatou’s lemma and Lemma
3.3
, we have that for all
x\in\mathds{R}

\|u(t,x)\|_{p}\leq\liminf_{m\to\infty}\|u^{(m)}(t,x)\|_{p}<\infty.

We write
u(t,x)
as

u(t,x)={\bf I}(t,x)+{\bf N}(t,x),\quad\text{for all }(t,x)\in\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R},

where
{\bf I}(t,x):=(G(t,\cdot)*u_{0})(x)
and
{\bf N}(t,x)
is defined as

{\bf N}(t,x):=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathds{R}}G(t-s,y-x)\sigma(u(s,y))W(dsdy).

Let us fix an arbitrary
\delta\in(0,\mathsf{d}\wedge 1)
. From (
3.1
) with
v=0
, we can see that for all
u\in\mathds{R}

|\sigma(u)|\leq\left|(\delta^{-1}\sigma(\delta)\vee L_{\mathsf{d}})u\right|+\sigma(\delta).

(3.19)

From now on, we assume that
\delta^{-1}\sigma(\delta)\geq L_{\mathsf{d}}
, otherwise the proof is much simpler. For
p\geq 2
, we apply the BDG inequality and to get

\displaystyle\|{\bf N}(t,x)\|_{p}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq Cp\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathds{R}}G^{2}(t-s,y-x)\|\sigma(u(s,y))\|_{2}^{p}dyds

(3.20)

\displaystyle\leq Cp\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathds{R}}G^{2}(t-s,y-x)\left(\delta^{-2}\sigma^{2}(\delta)\|u(s,y)\|_{2}^{p}+\sigma^{2}(\delta)\right)dyds

\displaystyle\leq Cp\delta^{-2}\sigma^{2}(\delta)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathds{R}}G^{2}(t-s,y-x)\|u(s,y)\|_{2}^{p}dyds+Cp\sigma^{2}(\delta)t^{1/2},

for some constant
C=C(L_{\mathsf{d}})>0
. We note that
C
depends on
L_{\mathsf{d}}
due to (
3.19
). On the other hand, there exists
C_{1}>0
only depending on
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}
such that

\|{\bf I}(t,x)\|_{p}^{2}\leq C_{1}\quad\text{for all }p\geq 2,(t,x)\in\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R}.

(3.21)

By (
3.20
) and (
3.21
), we see that there exist constants
C^{\prime}_{1}=C^{\prime}_{1}(\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}})>0
and
C_{2}=C_{2}(L_{\mathsf{d}})>0
satisfying

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\|u(t,x)\|_{p}^{2}\leq C^{\prime}_{1}+C_{2}p\left(\sigma^{2}(\delta)t^{1/2}+\delta^{-2}\sigma^{2}(\delta)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathds{R}}G^{2}(t-s,y-x)\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\|u(s,x)\|_{p}^{2}dyds\right).

(3.22)

For the third term on the RHS, by the definition of
\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,p}
(see (
3.18
)), there exists a universal constant
C_{3}>0
such that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathds{R}}G^{2}(t-s,y-x)\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\|u(s,x)\|_{p}^{2}dyds

\displaystyle\leq\left[\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,p}(u)\right]^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathds{R}}e^{2\alpha s}G^{2}(t-s,y-x)dyds

\displaystyle=\left[\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,p}(u)\right]^{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{2\alpha s}(4\pi(t-s))^{-1/2}ds

\displaystyle=\left[\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,p}(u)\right]^{2}e^{2\alpha t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2\alpha r}(4\pi r)^{-1/2}dr

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\left[\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,p}(u)\right]^{2}\alpha^{-1/2},

where we have used the following calculations:

\int_{\mathds{R}}G^{2}(t-s,y-x)^{2}dy=(4\pi(t-s))^{-1/2},

and

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2\alpha r}(4\pi r)^{-1/2}dr\lesssim\alpha^{-1/2}.

Therefore, by multiplying
e^{-2\alpha t}
and taking the supremum over
t\geq 0
, there exists
C_{4}=C_{4}(L_{\mathsf{d}})>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,p}(u)\leq C^{\prime}_{1}+C_{4}p\left(\alpha^{-1/2}\sigma^{2}(\delta)+\alpha^{-1/2}\delta^{-2}\sigma^{2}(\delta)\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,p}(u)\right).

Choose
\alpha=K\delta^{-4}\sigma^{4}(\delta)p^{2}
with
K=K(L_{\mathsf{d}})>0
satisfying

C_{4}p\left[(\alpha^{-1/2}\sigma^{2}(\delta))\vee(\alpha^{-1/2}\delta^{-2}\sigma^{2}(\delta))\right]\leq\frac{1}{4}.

We remark that the constant
K
is independent of
\delta\in(0,\mathsf{d}\wedge 1)
and
p\geq 2
. This ensures that

\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,p}(u)\leq C^{\prime}_{1}+\frac{1}{4}\left(1+\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,p}(u)\right),

which implies

\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,p}(u)\leq C:=\frac{4}{3}\left(C^{\prime}_{1}+\frac{1}{4}\right).

Now it turns out that for all
p\geq 2
and
T>0
,

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\mathds{E}|u(t,x)|^{p}\leq C^{p}\exp\left(K\delta^{-4}\sigma^{4}(\delta)p^{3}T\right).

This concludes the proof of (
3.17
).
∎

We can now provide the proof of Theorem
1.3
(i), which establishes the existence, uniqueness, and Hölder continuity of a mild solution. We will also prove Theorem
1.4
, which states the weak comparison principle.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
(i)
.

The existence and uniqueness of a mild solution follows from Proposition
3.4
. For the Hölder continuity of
u
, the proof follows the same lines of the proof of
[
2
, Theorem 3.1]
with Lemma
3.5
and the fact that
\mathds{E}[\sigma^{2}(u(t,x))]\leq C(1+\mathds{E}[|u(t,x)|^{2}])
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

By Proposition
3.4
, we see that
u^{(m,1)}(t,x)\to u^{(1)}(t,x)
and
u^{(m,2)}(t,x)\to u^{(2)}(t,x)
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
for each
(t,x)\in\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R}
with initial data
u^{(1)}_{0}
and
u^{(2)}_{0}
respectively. In addition,
u^{(1)}
and
u^{(2)}
are both continuous in
\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R}
by Theorem
1.3
(i). Thus, it is clear that Lemma
3.2
completes the proof of Theorem
1.4
.
∎

4
Strict positivity

In this section, our aim is to prove Theorem
1.3
(ii). We start by relaxing the problem into simpler setting. We claim that to prove strict positivity, it is sufficient to consider the solution
u
of the SPDE

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{x}u(t,x)+\sigma(u(t,x))\dot{W}(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R},\\
u(0,x)=\mathds{1}_{[-r,r]}(x),\quad x\in\mathds{R},\end{cases}

(4.1)

where
r>0
. Indeed, since we assumed that
u_{0}
is a nonnegative function in
C(\mathds{R})
which is not identically zero, by the weak comparison principle (Theorem
1.4
), it suffices to consider
u_{0}(x)=c\mathds{1}_{[a,b]}
for any
a,b\in\mathds{R}
and
c>0
. Appealing to the translation
u_{0}(\cdot)\mapsto u_{0}(\cdot-(a+b)/2)
, we can assume that
u_{0}(x)=c\mathds{1}_{[-r,r]}
for some
r>0
. Moreover, we can consider
\bar{u}(t,x):=c\cdot u(t,x)
which is the unique mild solution of (
1.1
) with the initial function
\mathds{1}_{[-r,r]}
and with the nonlinearity
c\cdot\sigma(c^{-1}x)
instead of
\sigma(x)
. Observe that
c\cdot\sigma(c^{-1}\cdot)
satisfies Assumption
1.1
and (
1.6
) if
\sigma
does, with
c\cdot\mathsf{d}
instead of
\mathsf{d}
. In particular, we emphasize that Assumption
1.1
(b) holds with
L_{c\mathsf{d}}=L_{\mathsf{d}}
. Thus, in the rest of the section we only consider the solution
u
of (
4.1
) with the initial function of the form
u_{0}=\mathds{1}_{[-r,r]}
and
\sigma
satisfying Assumption
1.1
and (
1.6
).

4.1
Probability estimates for strict positivity

In what follows, we present the main contribution of this section, which will be iteratively used to prove Theorem
1.3
(ii). The following proposition provides the lower tail estimate for the unique mild solution
u
to (
4.1
) with the initial function
u_{0}=\mathds{1}_{[-r,r]}
for
r>0
, which is a generalization of similar estimates in
[
4
,
20
,
21
]
into a larger class of non-Lipschitz nonlinearity
\sigma
. The main input of the proof is the moment estimate in Lemma
3.5
(see the proof of Lemma
4.4
).

Proposition 4.1
.

Fix
\mathfrak{r}>0
. Let
u
be the unique mild solution to (
4.1
) with an initial condition
u_{0}=\mathds{1}_{[-r,r]}
and satisfy Assumption
1.1
and (
1.6
). For any
T>0
,
M>\mathfrak{r}
,
\eta\in(0,1/4)
, and
\delta\in(0,\mathsf{d}\wedge 1)
, there exists
C=C(T,M,L_{\mathsf{d}},\eta)>0
and
m_{0}=m_{0}(T,M,L_{\mathsf{d}},\eta,\delta,\mathfrak{r})>0
such that for all
m\geq m_{0}
and
r\in[\mathfrak{r},2M]

\displaystyle\mathds{P}

\displaystyle\left(u(s,x)\geq\eta\mathds{1}_{[-r-M/m,r+M/m]}(x)\text{ for all }(s,x)\in\left[\frac{T}{2m},\frac{T}{m}\right]\times\mathds{R}\right)

(4.2)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\exp\left\{-Cm^{1/2}(\log m)^{3/2}\left(\frac{\delta}{\sigma(\delta)}\right)^{2}\right\}.

Remark 4.2
.

We point out that the constants
C
and
m_{0}
in Proposition
4.1
are independent of
r\in[\mathfrak{r},2M]
while
m_{0}
depends on the lower bound
\mathfrak{r}
. The uniform-in-
r
bound (
4.2
) is crucial since we will exploit Proposition
4.1
repeatedly with
r=\mathfrak{r}+\frac{kM}{m}
for
k=0,1,...,m-1
and
m\geq m_{0}
instead of a fixed
r
(see Section
4.2
).

To prove Proposition
4.1
, we consider the unique mild solution of (
4.1
), as in the previous section. Specifically, we work with the following integral equation for
u
:

u(t,x)=(G(t,\cdot)*\mathds{1}_{[-r,r]})(x)+{\bf N}(t,x),\quad\text{for all }(t,x)\in\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R},

where the stochastic term
{\bf N}(t,x)
is defined as

{\bf N}(t,x):=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathds{R}}G(t-s,y-x)\sigma(u(s,y))W(dsdy).

(4.3)

The following two lemmas are essential to prove Proposition
4.1
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Fix
\mathfrak{r}>0
. Let
T,M>0
and
\eta\in(0,1/4)
. There exists a constant
m_{0}=m_{0}(T,M,\eta,\mathfrak{r})\geq 1
such that for all
m\geq m_{0}
and
r\geq\mathfrak{r}
we have

\inf_{s\in[T/(2m),T/m]}(G(s,\cdot)*\mathds{1}_{[-r,r]})(x)\geq 2\eta\mathds{1}_{[-r-M/m,r+M/m]}(x)\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathds{R}.

(4.4)

Proof.

The proof follows similarly as in the proof of
[
4
, Lemma 4.1]
. Let
Z
be a standard normal distribution with the density
G(1,x)
. For the case where
-r-M/m\leq x\leq 0
, for all
s\in[T/(2m),T/m]
, we have that whenever
m>\sqrt{2}M/\mathfrak{r}
,

\displaystyle(G(s,\cdot)*\mathds{1}_{[-r,r]})(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-r}^{r}G(s,x-y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{(x-r)/\sqrt{s}}^{(x+r)/\sqrt{s}}G(1,y)dy

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{P}\left(-\frac{r\sqrt{m}}{\sqrt{T}}\leq Z\leq-\frac{\sqrt{2}M}{\sqrt{mT}}\right).

Similarly, in the case
0\leq x\leq r+M/m
, we have

\displaystyle(G(s,\cdot)*\mathds{1}_{[-r,r]})(x)

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{P}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}M}{\sqrt{mT}}\leq Z\leq\frac{r\sqrt{m}}{\sqrt{T}}\right).

Note that as
m\to\infty
,

\mathds{P}\left(-\frac{\mathfrak{r}\sqrt{m}}{\sqrt{T}}\leq Z\leq-\frac{\sqrt{2}M}{\sqrt{mT}}\right)\wedge\mathds{P}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}M}{\sqrt{mT}}\leq Z\leq\frac{\mathfrak{r}\sqrt{m}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)\to\frac{1}{2}.

Therefore, due to
r\geq\mathfrak{r}
, we can choose
m_{0}=m_{0}(T,M,\eta,\mathfrak{r})\geq 1
large so that (
4.4
) holds for all
m\geq m_{0}
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Fix
\mathfrak{r}>0
. Let
T,\eta>0
,
M>\mathfrak{r}
and
\delta\in(0,\mathsf{d}\wedge 1)
. Then, there exist some constants
C=C(T,M,L_{\mathsf{d}},\eta)>0
and
m_{0}=m_{0}(T,M,L_{\mathsf{d}},\eta,\delta)>0
such that for all
m\geq m_{0}
and
r\in[\mathfrak{r},2M]

\mathds{P}\left(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,y)\in S\end{subarray}}|{\bf N}(s,x)|\geq\eta\right)\leq\exp\left\{-Cm^{1/2}(\log m)^{3/2}\left(\frac{\delta}{\sigma(\delta)}\right)^{2}\right\},

where
S:=\left\{(s,y):0\leq s\leq T/m,|y|\leq r+M/m\right\}
.

Proof.

We begin the proof by applying the BDG inequality,
[
2
, Proposition 4.4]
, and Lemma
3.5
to get that there exists
C_{1}>0
such that for all
p\geq 2
,
\delta\in(0,\mathsf{d}\wedge 1)
and
(s,y),(s^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in S
,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[|{\bf N}(s,y)-{\bf N}(s^{\prime},y^{\prime})|^{p}]

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{p}(|y-y^{\prime}|^{1/2}+|s-s^{\prime}|^{1/4})^{p}\cdot\sup_{(t,x)\in S}\|\sigma(u(t,x))\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{p}{\bf M}_{T,p,\delta}(|y-y^{\prime}|^{1/2}+|s-s^{\prime}|^{1/4})^{p},

where

{\bf M}_{T,p,\delta}:=C^{p}\exp\left\{Kp^{3}\left(\frac{T}{m}\right)\left(\frac{\sigma(\delta)}{\delta}\right)^{4}\right\}.

with some constant
C>0
and the constant
K=K(L_{\mathsf{d}})>0
in Lemma
3.5
. Here, we used the fact that
\|\sigma(u(t,x))\|_{p}^{p}\leq 2^{p}{L_{\mathsf{d}}}^{p}(1+\|u(t,x)\|_{p}^{p})
. On the other hand, for all
\theta\in(0,1-6p^{-1})
and
p>6
, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\sup_{(s,y)\in S}\left|\frac{{\bf N}(s,y)}{(T/m)^{\theta/4}}\right|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\left[\sup_{(s,y)\in S}\left|\frac{{\bf N}(s,y)-{\bf N}(0,y)}{s^{\theta/4}}\right|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\left[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,y),(s^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in S\\
(s,y)\neq(s^{\prime},y^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{{\bf N}(s,y)-{\bf N}(s^{\prime},y^{\prime})}{(|y-y^{\prime}|^{1/2}+|s-s^{\prime}|^{1/4})^{\theta}}\right|^{p}\right],

where we have used that
{\bf N}(0,x)=0
for all
x\in\mathds{R}
. Note that
S\subset[0,3M]^{2}
for all
m\geq 1
and
r\in[\mathfrak{r},2M]
. Then, by the quantitative form of the Kolmogorov continuity theorem (see e.g.
[
13
, Theorem C.6]
), we find that there exists
C_{2}=C_{2}(M,L_{\mathsf{d}})>0
such that for all
p>6
and
m\geq 1

\mathds{E}\left[\sup_{(s,y)\in S}\left|{\bf N}(s,y)\right|^{p}\right]\leq C_{2}^{p}(T/m)^{\frac{\theta p}{4}}\exp\left\{Kp^{3}\left(\frac{T}{m}\right)\left(\frac{\sigma(\delta)}{\delta}\right)^{4}\right\}.

(4.5)

This implies that for all
\eta>0
,

\displaystyle\eta^{-p}\mathds{E}\left[\sup_{(s,y)\in S}\left|{\bf N}(s,y)\right|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\eta^{-p}C_{2}^{p}(T/m)^{\frac{\theta p}{4}}\exp\left\{Kp^{3}\left(\frac{T}{m}\right)\left(\frac{\sigma(\delta)}{\delta}\right)^{4}\right\}

(4.6)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{p\left(\log C_{2}+\log(\eta^{-1})-\frac{\theta}{4}\log\frac{m}{T}\right)+Kp^{3}\left(\frac{\sigma(\delta)}{\delta}\right)^{4}\frac{T}{m}\right\}.

We can choose a sufficiently large
m_{1}:=m_{1}(T,M,L_{\mathsf{d}},\eta)\geq 1
such that for all
m\geq m_{1}

\log C_{2}+\log(\eta^{-1})-\frac{\theta}{4}\log\frac{m}{T}<0.

Notice that we fixed an arbitrary
\theta\in(0,p)
for some
p>6
which will be chosen below , thus we drop the dependence on
\theta
. We find that the function
p\mapsto f(p):=ap^{3}-bp
for
a,b>0
is minimized at
p_{0}=\sqrt{b/(3a)}
and
f(p_{0})=-2(b/3)^{3/2}a^{-1/2}
. Keep this in mind and let

p_{1}=\left(\frac{\frac{\theta}{4}\log(m/T)-\log C_{2}-\log(\eta^{-1})}{3K\left(\frac{\sigma(\delta)}{\delta}\right)^{4}\frac{T}{m}}\right)^{1/2}.

We can take
m_{2}=m_{2}(T,M,L_{\mathsf{d}},\eta,\delta)>0
that guarantees
p_{1}>6
for all
m\geq m_{2}
. With this choice of
p=p_{1}
and by the Chebyshev inequality, we obtain from (
4.6
) that there exists
C=C(T,M,\eta,L_{\mathsf{d}})>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,y)\in S\end{subarray}}|{\bf N}(s,x)|\geq\eta\right)\leq\eta^{-p}\mathds{E}\left[\sup_{(s,y)\in S}\left|{\bf N}(s,y)\right|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{-Cm^{1/2}(\log m)^{3/2}\left(\frac{\delta}{\sigma(\delta)}\right)^{2}\right\}.

We note that
C
and
m_{2}
depend on
L_{\mathsf{d}}
since
K
does. This completes the proof.

∎

The proof of Proposition
4.1
now follows from Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Thanks to Lemma
4.3
, for any
\eta\in(0,1/4)
and all
x\in\mathds{R}

\inf_{s\in[T/(2m),T/m]}(G(s,\cdot)*\mathds{1}_{(-\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{r})})(x)\geq 2\eta\mathds{1}_{(-r-M/m,r+M/m)}(x).

Define

S:=\{(t,x):T/(2m)\leq t\leq T/m,|x|\leq r+M/m\}.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{P}

\displaystyle\Big(u(s,x)<\eta\mathds{1}_{(-r-M/m,r+M/m)}(x)\text{ for some }(s,x)\in[T/(2m),T/m]\times\mathds{R}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\left(\sup_{(s,x)\in S}|{\bf N}(s,x)|\geq\eta\right).

By Lemma
4.4
, we obtain (
4.2
).

∎

4.2
Proof of strict positivity: Theorem
1.3
(ii)

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.3
(ii) (strict positivity of the solution to (
1.1
)).

Proof of Theorem
1.3
(ii)
.

Let
T>0
and
M>\mathfrak{r}>0
be fixed but arbitrary constants. We will prove that

\mathds{P}\left(u(s,x)>0\text{ for all }(s,x)\in\left[\frac{T}{2},T\right]\times\left[-\frac{M}{2},\frac{M}{2}\right]\right)=1,

(4.7)

where
u
is the unique mild solution to (
4.1
) subject to the initial function
u_{0}=\mathds{1}_{[-\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{r}]}
for any fixed
\mathfrak{r}>0
. Since our choices of
T>0
and
M>\mathfrak{r}>0
are arbitrary, by the weak comparison principle (Theorem
1.4
) with the discussion at the beginning of Section
4
, (
4.7
) is sufficient for the proof of Theorem
1.3
(ii).

Let
m_{0}\geq 1
be a large integer which will be specified later (see (
4.9
) below). For any integer
m\geq m_{0}
, we define for
k=0,1,...,m-1

S^{m}_{k}:=\left\{x\in\mathds{R}:|x|\leq\mathfrak{r}+\frac{Mk}{m}\right\}.

For
k\geq 1
, let
w_{k}
be the solution to the following SPDE:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}w_{k}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{x}w_{k}(t,x)+\sigma_{k}(w_{k}(t,x))\dot{W}_{k}(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R},\\
w_{k}(0,x)=\mathds{1}_{S^{m}_{k-1}}(x),\quad x\in\mathds{R},\end{cases}

(4.8)

where
\dot{W}_{k}(s,x):=\dot{W}(s+kT/m,x)
is the time-shifted space-time white noise. Fix
\eta\in(0,1/4\wedge\mathsf{d})
. We set

\sigma_{k}(u):=\eta^{-k}\sigma(\eta^{k}u)\quad\text{for all }u\in\mathds{R}.

Let
v_{k}(t,x)
be a mild solution to (
1.1
) driven by the time-shifted noise
\dot{W}_{k}
starting from
v_{k}(0,x):=\eta^{k}\mathds{1}_{S^{m}_{k-1}}
.

Now let us describe our strategy for proving (
4.7
). First note that
u(s+kT/m,x)\geq v_{k}(s,x)
for all
s\geq 0
and
x\in\mathds{R}
, conditioned on
u(kT/m,x)\geq v_{k}(0,x)=\eta^{k}\mathds{1}_{S^{m}_{k-1}}(x)
for all
x\in\mathds{R}
by the weak comparison principle. Thus, we aim to show that
v_{k}
is strictly positive with high probability in each time interval of length
T/(2m)
inductively for all
k=0,1,...,m-1
(see (
4.9
) below) by applying Proposition
4.1
. However, since the size of the initial condition of
v_{k}
(i.e.
\eta^{k}
) is not fixed as
k
varies, and the constants in Proposition
4.1
depend on it, we instead apply the proposition to the solution
w_{k}
of (
4.8
) which has a fixed-size initial condition
\mathds{1}_{S^{m}_{k-1}}
.

To this end, we first prove that
v_{k}=\eta^{k}w_{k}
by uniqueness (Theorem
1.3
(i)). Thus, we verify that Assumption
1.1
is satisfied by
\sigma_{k}
: The monotonicity of
u\mapsto\sigma_{k}(u)
and
u\mapsto\sigma_{k}(u)/u
follows from that of
\sigma
with
\mathsf{d}
replaced by
\mathsf{d}_{k}:=\eta^{-k}\mathsf{d}
. In addition,
\sigma_{k}(u)\leq L_{\mathsf{d}_{k}}u
with
L_{\mathsf{d}_{k}}=L_{\mathsf{d}}
. Moreover, it is easy to see that the condition (
1.6
) also holds for
\sigma_{k}
uniformly over all
k
. Therefore, by Theorem
1.3
(i), there exists a unique mild solution
w_{k}
of (
4.8
), and by uniqueness we have that
v_{k}(t,x)=\eta^{k}w_{k}(t,x)
.

Next we apply Proposition
4.1
to
w_{k}
for all
k=0,1,...,m-1
. Regarding the assumptions in Proposition
4.1
, we find that replacing
r
by
\mathfrak{r}+(Mk)/m
is valid since we have chosen
M>\mathfrak{r}
to ensure that
r=\mathfrak{r}+(Mk)/m\in[\mathfrak{r},2M]
(see Remark
4.2
), and
\eta<\mathsf{d}\wedge 1\leq\mathsf{d}_{k}\wedge 1
. Then by Proposition
4.1
, there exist constants
C=C(T,M,L_{\mathsf{d}},\eta)\geq 1
and
m_{0}=m_{0}(T,M,L_{\mathsf{d}},\eta,\mathfrak{r})\geq 1
such that for all integers
m\geq m_{0}
and
k=0,1,...,m-1
we have

\displaystyle\mathds{P}

\displaystyle\left(v_{k}(s,x)\geq\eta^{k+1}\mathds{1}_{S^{m}_{k}}(x)\text{ for all }(s,x)\in\left[\frac{T}{2m},\frac{T}{m}\right]\times\mathds{R}\right)

(4.9)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\left(w_{k}(s,x)\geq\eta\mathds{1}_{S^{m}_{k}}(x)\text{ for all }(s,x)\in\left[\frac{T}{2m},\frac{T}{m}\right]\times\mathds{R}\right)\geq 1-g(k),

where
g(k)
is defined as

\displaystyle g(k)

\displaystyle:=\exp\left(-Cm^{1/2}(\log m)^{3/2}\left[\frac{\eta}{\sigma_{k}(\eta)}\right]^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-Cm^{1/2}(\log m)^{3/2}\left[\frac{\eta^{k+1}}{\sigma(\eta^{k+1})}\right]^{2}\right).

By Assumption
1.1
(a), uniformly over all
k=0,1,...,m-1
, we have

\mathfrak{g}(m):=\exp\left(-Cm^{1/2}(\log m)^{3/2}\left[\frac{\eta^{m}}{\sigma(\eta^{m})}\right]^{2}\right)\geq g(k).

(4.10)

Furthermore, by (
1.6
), there exists a constant
C=C(\alpha,\eta)>0
such that

\frac{\eta^{m}}{\sigma(\eta^{m})}=\frac{e^{-m\log\eta^{-1}}}{\sigma(e^{-m\log\eta^{-1}})}\geq Cm^{-\alpha},

for all sufficiently large
m\geq 1
. This leads to an upper bound of
\mathfrak{g}(m)
as follows: There exist constants
C_{1}=C_{1}(T,M,L_{\mathsf{d}},\eta)>0
and
m_{1}=m_{1}(T,M,L_{\mathsf{d}},\eta,\mathfrak{r},\alpha)>0
such that for all
m\geq m_{1}

\mathfrak{g}(m)\leq\exp\left(-C_{1}m^{1/2}(\log m)^{3/2}m^{-2\alpha}\right)=\exp(-C_{1}m^{\bar{\alpha}}(\log m)^{3/2}),

(4.11)

where
\bar{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}-2\alpha>0
.

We define the events

\displaystyle\bf{\displaystyle A}_{k}:=\left\{u(s,x)\geq\eta^{k+1}\mathds{1}_{S^{m}_{k}}(x)\text{ for all }(s,x)\in\left[\frac{(2k+1)T}{2m},\frac{(k+1)T}{m}\right]\times\mathds{R}\right\},\text{ for $k=0,1,...,m-1$},

\displaystyle\bf{\displaystyle B}_{k}:=\left\{u(s,x)\geq\eta^{k+1}\mathds{1}_{S^{m}_{k}}(x)\text{ for all }(s,x)\in\left[\frac{kT}{m},\frac{(2k+1)T}{2m}\right]\times\mathds{R}\right\},\text{ for $k=1,\dots,m-1$},

\displaystyle\bf{\displaystyle B}_{0}:=\left\{u\left(\frac{T}{2m},x\right)\geq\eta\mathds{1}_{S^{m}_{0}}(x)\text{ for all }x\in\mathds{R}\ \right\}.

Note that the decomposition of the events into
{\bf A_{k}}
and
{\bf B_{k}}
is required since we have to wait for an intermidiate time of length
T/2M
to apply Proposition
4.1
iteratively. Then, by (
4.9
) with the weak comparison principle (Theorem
1.4
) and the Markov property of
u
(see
[
7
, Section 9]
) we have that for all
1\leq k\leq m-1

\mathds{P}({\bf A}_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{kT/m})\geq 1-g(k)\geq 1-\mathfrak{g}(m),\quad\text{a.s. on }{\bf A}_{k-1},

since
u(s+kT/m,x)\geq v_{k}(s,x)
for all
(s,y)\in\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R}
, a.s. on
{\bf A}_{k-1}
. We also have

\mathds{P}({\bf A}_{k}\mid{\bf A}_{k-1}\cap\cdots\cap{\bf A}_{0})\geq 1-\mathfrak{g}(m).

Similarly, for
1\leq k\leq m-1
we have

\mathds{P}({\bf B}_{k}\mid{\bf B}_{k-1}\cap\cdots\cap{\bf B}_{0})\geq 1-\mathfrak{g}(m).

and
\mathds{P}({\bf B}_{0})\geq\mathds{P}({\bf A}_{0})\geq 1-\mathfrak{g}(m)
. Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\bigcap_{0\leq k\leq m-1}[{\bf A}_{k}\cap{\bf B}_{k}]\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\left(1-\mathds{P}\left(\bigcap_{0\leq k\leq m-1}{\bf A}_{k}\right)\right)-\left(1-\mathds{P}\left(\bigcap_{0\leq k\leq m-1}{\bf A}_{k}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\mathfrak{g}(m))^{m-1}\mathds{P}({\bf A}_{0})+(1-\mathfrak{g}(m))^{m-1}\mathds{P}({\bf B}_{0})-1

\displaystyle\geq 2(1-\mathfrak{g}(m))^{m}-1.

Let us define

{\bf A}:={\bf A}_{T,M}:=\left\{u(s,x)>0\text{ for all }(s,x)\in\left[\frac{T}{2},T\right]\times\left[-\frac{M}{2},\frac{M}{2}\right]\right\}.

Now it holds that

\displaystyle\mathds{P}({\bf A})

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathds{P}\left(\bigcap_{0\leq k\leq m-1}[{\bf A}_{k}\cap{\bf B}_{k}]\right)\geq\lim_{m\to\infty}2(1-\mathfrak{g}(m))^{m}-1.

The proof of (
4.7
) is completed if we can show

\lim_{m\to\infty}(1-\mathfrak{g}(m))^{m}=1.

This follows immediately from the definition of
\mathfrak{g}(m)
and L’Hopital’s rule.
∎

Compact support property

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem

, demonstrating the compact support property of the solution to (

) under the assumption (
1.7
). The main ingredient for the proof is Lemma

, which states a stochastic version of the maximum principle. We first provide the proof of Theorem
1.7
using Lemma
5.2
and then prove Lemma
5.2
. Throughout this section,

denotes a nonnegative weak solution of (

) under the assumptions in Theorem

.

5.1
Proof of compact support property: Theorem
1.7

For
a>0
, define

\Psi_{a}(x):=\frac{1}{\cosh(ax)}.

(5.1)

The follwing lemma is useful to control the
L^{1}([0,\tau])
-norm of the solution.

Lemma 5.1

(Lemma 3.1 of
[
10
]
)
.

Let
T>0
and let
\tau\leq T
be a bounded stopping time. Let
u\in C([0,\tau];C_{tem}(\mathds{R}))
be a nonnegative weak solution to (
1.1
) satisfying the assumptions in Theorem
1.7
. Suppose that there exist constants
a,H>0
satisfying

\sup_{t\leq\tau}\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}}\Psi_{a}(x)u(t,x)\leq H\quad\text{almost surely},

where
\Psi_{a}
is defined in (
5.1
). Then, we have

\limsup_{R\to\infty}\mathds{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}u(t,R)dt\right]=0.

(5.2)

We now introduce the key lemma (Lemma
5.2
below) which will be used in the proof of Theorem
1.7
. The lemma will be proven in the next section. We denote by
I_{R}
an interval
[-R,R]
for
R>0
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
T>0
and let
\tau\leq T
be a bounded stopping time. Let
u\in C([0,\tau];C_{tem}(\mathds{R}))
be a nonnegative weak solution to (
1.1
) satisfying the assumptions in Theorem
1.7
. Suppose that there exist constants
a,H>0
such that

\|\Psi_{a}u\|_{C^{\gamma}([0,\tau]\times\mathds{R})}\leq H\quad\text{almost surely},

(5.3)

where
\Psi_{a}
is defined in (
5.1
). Furthermore, assume that
\mathrm{supp}(u_{0})\subseteq I_{R_{0}}
for some
R_{0}>0
.
Then, the following hold:

(i)

For every
R>R_{0}\vee 1
,

\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}u(s,R)ds=0\right)=\mathds{P}\Big(u(s,y)=0\quad\text{for all }s\in[0,\tau]\text{ and }y>R\Big).

(ii)

Let
\lambda=\gamma/(1+\gamma)\in(0,1)
. For every
R>R_{0}\vee 1
,
p,q\in(0,\mathsf{d}^{1/\lambda}\wedge 1)
,
r>0
, and
\beta\in(0,1)
, there exists
x\in(r,2r)
such that

\displaystyle\mathds{P}

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,R+x))ds\geq p\right)\leq\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,R))ds\geq q\right)+Cr^{-\frac{3\beta}{2}}\left(\frac{q^{1+\lambda}}{p\sigma(q^{\lambda})}\right)^{\beta},

(5.4)

where
C=C(\beta,\gamma,H,T)>0
.

Remark 5.3
.

One can easily show that the results of Lemma
5.2
also hold for the negative real line with appropriate modifications. Specifically, the following inequalities hold:

\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}u(s,-R)ds=0\right)\leq\mathds{P}\Big(u(s,y)=0\quad\text{for all }s\in[0,\tau]\text{ and }y<-R\Big),

and

\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,-R-x))ds\geq p\right)\leq\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,-R))ds\geq q\right)+Cr^{-\frac{3\beta}{2}}\left(\frac{q^{1+\lambda}}{p\sigma(q^{\lambda})}\right)^{\beta}.

respectively.

We now present the proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

The proof of Theorem
1.7
(i) is precisely the same as that of
[
10
, Theorem 2.9]
. Indeed, Assumption
1.1
implies that we have a universal constant
L>0
such that
|\sigma(u)|\leq L(1+|u|)
. Since
\sigma
is continuous, we can follow the same line in
[
10
, Appendix]
.

In the remainder of the proof, we prove Theorem
1.7
(ii). Fix any
a>0
and define a stopping time

\tau_{n}:=n\,\wedge\,\tau\wedge\,\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\|\Psi_{a}u\|_{C^{\gamma}([0,t]\times\mathds{R})}\geq n\right\}.

By Theorem
1.7
(i), there exists
n_{0}=n_{0}(\omega)>0
almost surely such that
\tau_{n}=\tau
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Henceforth, throughout the proof, we can assume that there exists a constant
H>0
satisfying

\tau\leq H\quad\text{and}\quad\|\Psi_{a}u\|_{C^{\gamma}([0,\tau]\times\mathds{R})}\leq H,

and it is sufficient for the proof of Theorem
1.7
. For
R>0
, we set

\Omega_{R}:=\{\omega\in\Omega:u(\omega,t,x)=0\quad\text{for all }t\leq\tau,\,x>R\}.

In order to prove the theorem, we aim to show that
\mathds{P}(\cup_{R}\Omega_{R})=1
by employing Lemma
5.2
. Once we prove that
\mathds{P}(\cup_{R}\Omega_{R})=1
, we can use the same argument along with Lemma
5.2
and Remark
5.3
to show that

\mathds{P}\Big(\text{there exists $R>0$ such that }u(t,x)=0\text{ for all }t\leq\tau\text{ and }x<-R\Big)=1.

Moreover, since
\Omega_{R_{0}}\subset\Omega_{R_{1}}
for
R_{0}\leq R_{1}
, it suffices to prove that

\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathds{P}(\Omega_{R})=1.

By Lemma
5.2
(i), we have

\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}u(s,R)ds=0\right)\leq\mathds{P}(\Omega_{R}).

From Assumption
1.1
, we observe that

\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,R))ds=0\right)\leq\mathds{P}\left(u(s,R)=0\,\,\text{for all}\,\,s\leq\tau\right)\leq\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}u(s,R)ds=0\right).

Therefore, the proof is completed once we prove

\limsup_{R\to\infty}\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,R))ds>0\right)=0.

(5.5)

Let
m\in\mathds{Z}_{+}
be a sufficiently large positive integer such that
e^{-m}<\mathsf{d}\wedge 1
. Set

r_{k}:=\frac{1}{2(k+1)^{1+c/3}}\quad\text{ and }\quad p_{k}:=e^{-\frac{k+m}{\bar{\gamma}}}\quad\text{for all }k\in\mathds{Z}_{+},

where
c>0
. Here the choice of
c
will be specified later. Let us fix
\beta\in(0,1)
and
x_{0}:=R_{0}+1
where
R_{0}>0
satisfies
\mathrm{supp}(u_{0})\subseteq I_{R_{0}}
. By Lemma
5.2
(ii), there exists
x_{1}\in(x_{0}+r_{0},x_{0}+2r_{0})
such that

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,x_{1}))ds\geq p_{1}\right)\leq\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,x_{0}))ds\geq p_{0}\right)+Cr_{0}^{-\frac{3\beta}{2}}\left(\frac{p_{0}^{1+\lambda}}{p_{1}\sigma(p_{0}^{\lambda})}\right)^{\beta},

where
C=C(\beta,\lambda,\gamma,H)>0
. Now, we proceed the iteration as follows. For
k\geq 2
, take
x_{k-1}
as a new origin and apply Lemma
5.2
(ii) to find
x_{k}\in(x_{k-1}+r_{k-1},x_{k-1}+2r_{k-1})
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,x_{k}))ds\geq p_{k}\right)\leq\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,x_{k-1}))ds\geq p_{k-1}\right)+Cr_{k-1}^{-\frac{3\beta}{2}}\left(\frac{p_{k-1}^{1+\lambda}}{p_{k}\sigma(p_{k-1}^{\lambda})}\right)^{\beta}.

(5.6)

Since
|x_{k}-x_{k-1}|\leq 2r_{k-1}=k^{-1-c/3}
for all
k\geq 1
, there exists
y
such that
x_{k}\rightarrow y
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. From (
5.6
), by summing over
k=1,2,\dots
, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,y))ds>0\right)\leq\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,x_{0}))ds\geq p_{0}\right)+\mathfrak{R}(m),

(5.7)

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}(m)

\displaystyle:=C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(r_{k-1}^{-\frac{3}{2}}\frac{p_{k-1}^{1+\lambda}}{p_{k}\sigma(p_{k-1}^{\lambda})}\right)^{\beta}.

Now note that (
1.7
) implies that there exist constants
\alpha>\frac{5}{2}
and
k_{0}(\alpha)\geq 1
such that for all
k\geq k_{0}

\frac{e^{-k}}{\sigma(e^{-k})}\leq k^{-\alpha}.

(5.8)

On the other hand, observe that

\frac{p_{k-1}}{p_{k}}=e^{1/\lambda}

for all
k\geq 1
. Therefore, we can choose
c=c(\alpha)>0
such that for all
k\geq k_{0}

\frac{p_{k-1}^{1+\lambda}}{p_{k}\sigma(p_{k-1}^{\lambda})}\leq\frac{e^{1/\lambda}}{(k-1+m)^{5/2+c}},

where we applied (
5.8
). Note that
k_{0}
can be chosen independently of
m\geq 1
. Thus, we have an upper bound of
\mathfrak{R}(m)
:

\mathfrak{R}(m)\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e^{1/\lambda}k^{(3+c)/2}}{(k-1+m)^{5/2+c}}\right)^{\beta}.

In addition, for any
c>0
and
\beta\in(0,1)
, we have

\left(\left(\frac{5}{2}+c\right)-\frac{3+c}{2}\right)^{\beta}>1.

Now it follows that for all sufficiently large
m
, we have
\mathfrak{R}(m)<\infty
. Moreover, it is easy to see that
\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathfrak{R}(m)=0
by the dominated convergence theorem. We point out that (
5.7
) also holds for any choice of
x_{0}=R>R_{0}+1
with the same constants
C>0
and
y>0
which are independent of
R
. In other words, for any
R>R_{0}+1
, we have

\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,R+y))ds>0\right)\leq\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,R))ds>e^{-m/\lambda}\right)+\mathfrak{R}(m),

since
p_{0}=e^{-m/\lambda}
. We now send
R\to\infty
to get that

\limsup_{R\to\infty}\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,R))ds>0\right)\leq\limsup_{R\to\infty}\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,R))ds>e^{-m/\lambda}\right)+\mathfrak{R}(m).

(5.9)

Regarding the first term on the right-hand side of (
5.9
), by combining Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
5.1
, we have for any
\delta\in(0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{R\to\infty}\mathds{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,R))ds\right]

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{R\to\infty}\mathds{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}\left[\left(L_{\mathsf{d}}\vee\delta^{-1}\sigma(\delta)\right)u(s,R)+\sigma(\delta)\right]ds\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(\delta)ds\right].

Letting
\delta\to 0
in the last line and applying Chebyshev’s inequality yields

\limsup_{R\to\infty}\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,R))ds>e^{-m/\lambda}\right)=0.

Thus, by taking
m\to\infty
in (
5.9
) and recalling that
\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathfrak{R}(m)=0
, we get

\limsup_{R\to\infty}\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,R))ds>0\right)=0,

which completes the proof.

∎

5.2
Proof of Lemma
5.2

In this section, we prove Lemma
5.2
, which is a key ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.7
. The following lemma presents the estimation of the quadratic variation outside the initial support in the weak formulation (
2.4
). This estimation is obtained using a suitable test function and is controlled by the time integral of the solution
u
at the boundary. The result of this lemma will be used later in the proof of Lemma
5.2
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
T>0
and let
\tau\leq T
be a bounded stopping time. Let
u\in C([0,\tau];C_{tem}(\mathds{R}))
be a nonnegative weak solution to (
1.1
) satisfying the assumptions in Theorem
1.7
. Let
\eta\leq\tau
be a bounded stopping time and let
R>R_{0}\vee 1
where
R_{0}>0
satisfies
\mathrm{supp}(u_{0})\subseteq I_{R_{0}}
. Then, for every
\beta\in(0,1)
, there exists
C=C(\beta)>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{\eta}\int_{R}^{\infty}(x-R)^{2}|\sigma(u(s,x))|^{2}dxds\right)^{\beta/2}\right]\leq C\mathds{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{\eta}u(s,R)ds\right)^{\beta}\right].

(5.10)

Proof.

The proof of this lemma follows the same arguments as those used in the proof of
[
10
, Lemma 4.1]
or
[
16
, Lemma 2.1]
. However, for the sake of completeness, we provide a sketch of the proof here.

For each
n\geq 1
, let
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathds{R})
be a nonnegative even function with
\psi(0)=1
, and define
\psi_{n}(x):=\psi((x-R)/n)
. Then, by choosing the test function
\phi(x):=(x-R)_{+}\psi_{n}(x)
in the definition of a weak solution
u
(see (
2.4
)), we obtain

\displaystyle 0\leq\int_{R}^{\infty}(x-R)\psi_{n}(x)u(t,x)dx=\int_{0}^{t}u(s,R)ds+\mathfrak{A}^{n}_{t}+\mathfrak{M}^{n}_{t},

(5.11)

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}^{n}_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{R}^{\infty}u(s,x)\cdot\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{xx}(x\psi_{n}(x))dxds=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{R}^{\infty}u(s,x)\left[\frac{\psi^{\prime}_{n}(x)+(1+x)\psi^{\prime\prime}_{n}(x)}{2}\right]dx,

and
\mathfrak{M}^{n}_{t}
is a local martingale with quadratic variation

\displaystyle\langle\mathfrak{M}^{n}\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{R}^{\infty}(x-R)^{2}\psi_{n}^{2}(x)\sigma^{2}(u(s,x))dxds.

Note that
\phi
belongs to
C_{c}(\mathds{R})
, but not in
\mathcal{S}
. However, (
5.11
) can be justified by a simple approximation argument (see for instance
[
16
, Lemma 3.1]
). Observe that
|\psi^{\prime}_{n}(x)|\lesssim n^{-1}
and
|\psi^{\prime\prime}_{n}(x)|\lesssim n^{-2}
for all
x\in\mathds{R}
. Thus, we have that almost surely

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t\leq T}|\mathfrak{A}^{n}_{t}|\lesssim\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left(\sup_{t\leq T}\int_{R}^{\infty}(x-R)u(t,x)dx\right)=0.

(5.12)

Here we used the fact that the supremum of the integral on the RHS is finite owing to
[
10
, Lemma 5.1]
. We have obtained that for all
t\leq T

0\leq\int_{0}^{t}u(s,R)ds+|\mathfrak{A}^{n}_{t}|+\mathfrak{M}^{n}_{t}.

This implies that for any stopping time
\bar{\eta}\leq\eta
,

(\mathfrak{M}^{n}_{\bar{\eta}})_{-}\leq\int_{0}^{\bar{\eta}}u(s,R)ds+|\mathfrak{A}^{n}_{\bar{\eta}}|.

Since
\mathfrak{M}^{n}_{t}
is a local martingale, for any fixed
n
, we can choose a sequence of stopping times
\{\eta^{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
such that
\lim_{i\to\infty}\eta^{i}=\infty
and
\mathfrak{M}^{n}_{t\wedge\eta^{i}}
is a martingale on
[0,T]
for each
i\in\mathds{N}
. Then, by the optional stopping time,
\mathfrak{M}^{n}_{t\wedge\eta^{i}\wedge\eta}
is a martingale and
\mathds{E}[\mathfrak{M}^{n}_{t\wedge\eta^{i}\wedge{\bar{\eta}}}]=0
for any
i
and
t\in[0,T]
. This shows that
\mathds{E}[(\mathfrak{M}^{n}_{t\wedge\eta^{i}\wedge{\bar{\eta}}})_{+}]=\mathds{E}[(\mathfrak{M}^{n}_{t\wedge\eta^{i}\wedge{\bar{\eta}}})_{-}]
. Since
u
is nonnegative, we know that
\int_{0}^{t}u(s,R)ds+|\mathfrak{A}^{n}_{t}|
is nondecreasing in
t\in[0,T]
, which ensures that

\mathds{E}\left[|\mathfrak{M}^{n}_{\eta^{i}\wedge\bar{\eta}}|\right]\leq 2\mathds{E}\left[\bar{\mathfrak{A}}^{n}_{\bar{\eta}}\right],

where

\bar{\mathfrak{A}}^{n}_{\bar{\eta}}:=\int_{0}^{\bar{\eta}}u(s,R)ds+|\mathfrak{A}^{n}_{\bar{\eta}}|.

By Fatou’s lemma, we further obtain that

\mathds{E}\left[|\mathfrak{M}^{n}_{{\bar{\eta}}}|\right]\leq 2\mathds{E}\left[\bar{\mathfrak{A}}^{n}_{\bar{\eta}}\right].

Since
{\bar{\eta}}\leq\eta
is arbitrary, we then apply
[
15
, Theorem III.6.8]
to obtain that for any
\beta\in(0,1)
and any stopping time
\eta\leq T
,

\mathds{E}\left[\sup_{\eta\leq T}|\mathfrak{M}^{n}_{\eta}|^{\beta}\right]\leq C_{1}\mathds{E}\left[\sup_{\eta\leq T}|\bar{\mathfrak{A}}^{n}_{\eta}|^{\beta}\right]

for some constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(\beta)>0
. Therefore, by the BDG inequality and letting
n\to\infty,
there exists
C=C(\beta)>0
such that

\mathds{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{\eta}\int_{R}^{\infty}(x-R)^{2}|\sigma(u(s,x))|^{2}dxds\right)^{\beta/2}\right]\leq C\cdot\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathds{E}\left[\sup_{\eta\leq T}|\bar{\mathfrak{A}}^{n}_{\eta}|^{\beta}\right]=C\mathds{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{\eta}u(s,R)ds\right)^{\beta}\right],

where the last equality follows from (
5.12
).
∎

We now present the proof of Lemma
5.2
, which is analogous to Lemma 2.1 in
[
16
]
, as mentioned in Section
1
. However, our proof differs from Krylov’s
[
16
]
since we consider a broader class of
\sigma
than the ones in
[
16
]
(specifically,
\sigma(u)=u^{\gamma}
for
\gamma\in(0,1)
). In particular, we use a novel approach to derive (
5.18
), rather than applying Jensen’s inequality as in
[
16
]
, which relies on partitioning the time interval where the solution remains not too small.

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

Proof of Lemma
5.2
(i)
. We only prove that the probability of the LHS is bounded above by the one of the RHS since the proof of the other direction follows immediately from the fact that
u
is continuous almost surely. Let
p,q\in(0,\mathsf{d}\wedge 1)
,
\beta\in(0,1)
and
R>R_{0}\vee 1
. Define a stopping time

\kappa:=\tau\wedge\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\int_{0}^{t}u(s,R)ds\geq q\right\}.

(5.13)

By Chebyshev’s inequality and (
5.10
) with the stopping time
\eta=\kappa
, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{P}

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{R}^{\infty}(x-R)^{2}|\sigma(u(s,x)))|^{2}dxds\geq p\right)

(5.14)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\left(\kappa<\tau\right)+\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\kappa}\int_{R}^{\infty}(x-R)^{2}|\sigma(u(s,x)))|^{2}dxds\geq p\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}u(s,R)ds\geq q\right)+p^{-\beta/2}C\mathds{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{\kappa}u(s,R)ds\right)^{\beta}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}u(s,R)ds\geq q\right)+C\frac{q^{\beta}}{p^{\beta/2}}

where
C=C(\beta)>0
is the constant given in Lemma
5.4
and we have used the definition of
\kappa
in (
5.13
) in the second inequality. By letting
q\downarrow 0
and then
p\downarrow 0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{R}^{\infty}(x-R)^{2}|\sigma(u(s,x)))|^{2}dxds>0\right)\leq\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}u(s,R)ds>0\right).

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}u(s,R)ds=0\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{R}^{\infty}(x-R)^{2}|\sigma(u(s,x)))|^{2}dxds=0\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\left(u(s,x)=0\quad\text{for all }s\in[0,\tau]\text{ and }x>R\right).

The proof is completed.

Proof of Lemma
5.2
(ii)
. Let us fix
\beta\in(0,1)
, and
p,q\in(0,\mathsf{d}^{1/\lambda}\wedge 1)
. For
R>R_{0}\vee 1
, we define a stopping time
\kappa
as

\kappa:=\tau\wedge\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(u(s,R))ds\geq q\right\},

(5.15)

which differs from the previous definition of
\kappa
in (
5.13
). Let
r>0
and note that

\displaystyle r^{-1}\int_{R+r}^{R+2r}\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,x))ds\geq p\right)dx\leq\mathds{P}(\kappa<\tau)+r^{-1}\int_{R+r}^{R+2r}\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\kappa}\sigma(u(s,x))ds\geq p\right)dx.

It follows from the definition of
\kappa
(see (
5.15
)) that

\displaystyle r^{-1}

\displaystyle\int_{R+r}^{R+2r}\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,x))ds\geq p\right)dx

(5.16)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,R))ds\geq q\right)+r^{-1}\int_{R+r}^{R+2r}\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\kappa}\sigma(u(s,x))ds\geq p\right)dx.

Then, by Chebyshev’s inequality, (
5.10
) with
\eta=\kappa
, and Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle r^{-1}\int_{R+r}^{R+2r}

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\kappa}\sigma(u(s,x))ds\geq p\right)dx

(5.17)

\displaystyle\leq r^{-1}p^{-\beta}\int_{R+r}^{R+2r}\mathds{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{\kappa}\sigma(u(s,x))ds\right)^{\beta}\right]dx

\displaystyle\leq Cr^{-\frac{3\beta}{2}}p^{-\beta}\mathds{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{\kappa}\int_{R}^{\infty}(x-R)^{2}|\sigma(u(s,x))|^{2}dxds\right)^{\beta/2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq Cr^{-\frac{3\beta}{2}}p^{-\beta}\mathds{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{\kappa}u(s,R)ds\right)^{\beta}\right].

for some constant
C=C(T,\beta)>0
. Next, we claim that there exists
C=C(\gamma,H)>0
such that for all
\omega\in\Omega
satisfying (
5.3
), we have

\int_{0}^{\kappa}u(s,R)ds\leq C\frac{q^{1+\lambda}}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}.

(5.18)

If this claim holds, by substituting (
5.18
) into (
5.17
),

\displaystyle r^{-1}\int_{R+r}^{R+2r}\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\kappa}\sigma(u(s,x))ds\geq p\right)dx\leq Cr^{-\frac{3\beta}{2}}\left(\frac{q^{1+\lambda}}{p\sigma(q^{\lambda})}\right)^{\beta},

where
C=C(\beta,\gamma,H,T)>0
. Therefore, from (
5.16
), we can deduce that

\displaystyle r^{-1}

\displaystyle\int_{R+r}^{R+2r}\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,x))ds\geq p\right)dx

(5.19)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma(u(s,R))ds\geq q\right)+Cr^{-\frac{3\beta}{2}}\left(\frac{q^{1+\lambda}}{p\sigma(q^{\lambda})}\right)^{\beta}.

Applying the mean value theorem into (
5.19
), we find a
x\in(r,2r)
to complete the proof of (
5.4
).

For the remainder of the proof, we will prove the claim (
5.18
). Recall that
q<\mathsf{d}^{1/\lambda}\wedge 1
and
\lambda=\gamma/(1+\gamma)\in(0,1)
. Therefore, by Assumption
1.1
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\kappa}u(s,R)\mathds{1}_{u(s,R)\leq q^{\lambda}}ds\leq\frac{q^{\lambda}}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}\int_{0}^{\kappa}\sigma(u(s,R))ds\leq\frac{q^{1+\lambda}}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}.

(5.20)

Additionally, by (
5.13
) and the fact that
u\mapsto\sigma(u)
is nondecreasing, we have

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{\kappa}\sigma(u(s,R))\mathds{1}_{u(s,R)>q^{\lambda}}ds\geq\sigma(q^{\lambda})\int_{0}^{\kappa}\mathds{1}_{u(s,R)>q^{\lambda}}ds.

Therefore, we obtain

\int_{0}^{\kappa}\mathds{1}_{u(s,R)>q^{\lambda}}ds\leq\frac{q}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}.

(5.21)

On the other hand, by the continuity of
u
, we can decompose the open set
\{s\in(0,\kappa):u(s,R)>q^{\lambda}\}
as

\displaystyle\left\{s\in(0,\kappa):u(s,R)>q^{\lambda}\right\}=\dot{\cup}_{j}(s_{q}^{j},t_{q}^{j}),

where
\dot{\cup}
denotes the disjoint union. Observe that again by the continuity of
u(\cdot,R)
, we have
u(s_{q}^{j},R)=q^{\lambda}
. We employ (
5.3
) to proceed as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\kappa}u(s,R)\mathds{1}_{u(s,R)>q^{\lambda}}ds

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\int_{s_{q}^{j}}^{t_{q}^{j}}u(s,R)ds

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left[\int_{s_{q}^{j}}^{t_{q}^{j}}u(s,R)-u(s_{q}^{j},R)ds+\int_{s_{q}^{j}}^{t_{q}^{j}}u(s_{q}^{j},R)ds\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{j}\int_{s_{q}^{j}}^{t_{q}^{j}}|s-s_{q}^{j}|^{\gamma}ds+q^{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\kappa}\mathds{1}_{u(s,R)>q^{\lambda}}ds

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{j}|t_{q}^{j}-s_{q}^{j}|^{1+\gamma}+q^{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\kappa}\mathds{1}_{u(s,R)>q^{\lambda}}ds.

for some constant
C=C(\gamma,H)>0
. Now apply (
5.21
) several times to yield

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\kappa}u(s,R)\mathds{1}_{u(s,R)>q^{\lambda}}ds

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{j}\left|\int_{s_{q}^{j}}^{t_{q}^{j}}ds\right|^{1+\gamma}+\frac{q^{1+\lambda}}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}

(5.22)

\displaystyle\leq C\left|\sum_{j}\int_{s_{q}^{j}}^{t_{q}^{j}}ds\right|^{1+\gamma}+\frac{q^{1+\lambda}}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left|\int_{0}^{\kappa}\mathds{1}_{u(s,R)>q^{\lambda}}ds\right|^{1+\gamma}+\frac{q^{1+\lambda}}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{q}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}\right)^{1+\gamma}+\frac{q^{1+\lambda}}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}.

Recalling that
\lambda=\gamma/(1+\gamma)
, we see that
1-\lambda\gamma^{-1}>\lambda
, which in turn implies that

\left(\frac{q}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}\right)^{1+\gamma}=\frac{q^{1+\lambda}}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}\left(\frac{q^{1-\lambda\gamma^{-1}}}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}\right)^{\gamma}\leq\frac{q^{1+\lambda}}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}\left(\frac{q^{\lambda}}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}\right)^{\gamma},

(5.23)

where we used that
q<1
. Moreover, due to the monotonicity of
u\mapsto u/\sigma(u)
, we can find a constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(\gamma)>0
such that for all
q\in(0,\mathsf{d}^{1/\lambda}\wedge 1)

\left(\frac{q^{\lambda}}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}\right)^{\gamma}\leq C_{1}.

Therefore, by (
5.23
), we have shown that

\left(\frac{q}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}\right)^{1+\gamma}\leq C_{1}\frac{q^{1+\lambda}}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}.

Combining the above inequality with (
5.22
), we prove that there exists
C=C(\gamma,H)
such that

\int_{0}^{\kappa}u(s,R)\mathds{1}_{u(s,R)>q^{\lambda}}ds\leq C\frac{q^{1+\lambda}}{\sigma(q^{\lambda})}.

(5.24)

By (
5.20
) and (
5.24
), we can conclude that (
5.18
) holds uniformly over all
q\in(0,\mathsf{d}^{1/\lambda}\wedge 1)
.
∎
