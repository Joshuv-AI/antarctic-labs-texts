Abstract




Abstract

Dynamic mode decomposition (DMD), which the family of singular-value decompositions (SVD), is a popular tool of data-driven regression. While multiple numerical tests demonstrated the power and efficiency of DMD in representing data (i.e., in the interpolation mode), applications of DMD as a predictive tool (i.e., in the extrapolation mode) are scarce. This is due, in part, to the lack of rigorous error estimators for DMD-based predictions. We provide a theoretical error estimator for DMD extrapolation of numerical solutions to linear and nonlinear parabolic equations. This error analysis allows one to monitor and control the errors associated with DMD-based temporal extrapolation of numerical solutions to parabolic differential equations. We use several computational experiments to verify the robustness of our error estimators and to compare the predictive ability of DMD with that of proper orthogonal decomposition (POD), another member of the SVD family. Our analysis demonstrates the importance of a proper selection of observables, as predicted by the Koopman operator theory. In all the tests considered, DMD outperformed POD in terms of efficiency due to its iteration-free feature. In some of these experiments, POD proved to be more accurate than DMD. This suggests that DMD is preferable for obtaining a fast prediction with slightly lower accuracy, while POD should be used if the accuracy is paramount.

Key words.
Dynamic Mode Decomposition, Koopman operator theory, Reduced Order Model, Nonlinear dynamic system, numerical analysis.

Introduction

Dynamic mode decomposition (DMD)
[
15
]
has recently become a popular tool of data-driven regression. It belongs to the family of singular-value decompositions (SVD) and has its origins in representation of complex fluid flows in terms of their spatial modes and temporal frequencies
[
27
]
. This strategy for representation of spatiotemporal coherent structures has since been used for data diagnostics and related applications including video processing
[
17
]
, interpretation of neural activity measurements
[
3
]
, financial trading
[
19
]
, and forecast of infectious decease spreading
[
22
]
. DMD with control has been developed to extract the input-output characteristics of dynamic systems with external control
[
21
]
. It has also been deployed to learn models of high-dimensional complex systems from data
[
18
,
26
,
35
]
, in the spirit of equation-free simulations
[
12
]
.

DMD is connected to interpretation of nonlinear dynamical systems via the Koopman operator theory
[
25
,
20
]
. The latter provides a bridge between finite-dimensional nonlinear dynamics and infinite-dimensional linear systems by observable functions
[
13
]
. Theoretical studies of the DMD approximation to eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the infinite-dimensional Koopman operator show that the performance of this finite eigen-approximation depends crucially on the choice of observable functions, requiring expert prior knowledge of the underlying dynamics
[
25
,
35
]
. Machine learning techniques have been used to select the observable by identifying relevant terms in the dynamics from data
[
4
,
28
,
34
]
. Extended DMD or EDMD employs regression from a dictionary of observables that spans a subspace of the space of scalar observables
[
35
]
.

Numerical implementations of DMD are also undergoing modifications and enhancements. Under various assumptions on the data, many variants of the standard DMD algorithm were introduced to compute the eigenvalues and DMD modes in more accurate and efficient ways
[
8
,
9
]
. Sparsity-promoting DMD and compressed DMD combine DMD with sparsity techniques and modern theory of compressed sensing
[
5
,
32
]
. Inspired by the applications of DMD in video processing, multi-resolution DMD or mrDMD provides a means for recursive computation of DMD of separate spatiotemporal features at different scales in the data
[
16
]
. The mrDMD approach preserves the translational and rotational invariances, which remains the Achilles heel of many SVD-based methods
[
24
]
.

While multiple numerical tests demonstrated the power and efficiency of DMD in representing data (i.e., for interpolation), applications of DMD as a predictive tool (i.e., for extrapolation) are scarce. This is due, in part, to the lack of rigorous error estimators for DMD-based predictions. The convergence of DMD predictions are reported in
[
10
]
from the numerical perspective and in
[
14
]
from the theoretic perspective. A goal of our analysis is to provide a theoretical error estimator for DMD extrapolation of numerical solutions to linear and nonlinear parabolic equations. We are aware of no other quantitative analysis of the accuracy of DMD predictions. This error analysis allows one to monitor and control the errors associated with DMD-based temporal extrapolation of numerical solutions to parabolic differential equations. That, in turn, would facilitate the design of efficient algorithms for multi-scale/multi-physics simulations.

An alternative way to predict future states of a system relies on reduced-order models (ROMs), which are constructed with the proper orthogonal decomposition (POD)
[
11
,
23
]
. Time integration is still needed to compute future states, but only in a low-dimensional surrogate model. Thus, the computational cost is reduced and future states are predicted using the ROM derived from projecting the dynamics of the full system onto the hyperplane that the POD extracts from data. POD is an SVD-based method that is closely related to the principle component analysis (PCA) and the Karhuen-Loève transform. Recently, the empirical interpolation method (EIM)
[
2
]
and the discrete empirical interpolation method (DEIM)
[
7
]
were combined with POD in order to overcome the difficulty of handling nonlinearities in ROM. Although the POD-EIM/DEIM methods lack error estimation, they have been used in various fields with satisfactory accuracy. While both POD and DMD are based on SVD, they provide two independent approaches to constructing ROMs. It is therefore worthwhile to compare their relative performance in terms of accuracy and efficiency. Advantages of hybridizing the two methods have been demonstrated in several numerical tests
[
1
,
36
]
.

The paper is organized as follows: In
Section

2
, we formulate the DMD algorithm for the linear and nonlinear diffusion equations as a problem set up and provide a brief review of the DMD method and its connection to the Koopman operator theory. Our selection of the observables is also demonstrated with prior knowledge of underlying physics. In
Section

3
, we present our main results in error estimation. Several numerical tests are presented in
Section

4
to verify the error bound and the efficiency of DMD in prediction. DMD and POD are compared in terms of their computational costs and accuracy. We summarize the results with a discussion of applications, challenges and future work in
Section

5
.

Dynamic Mode Decomposition and Koopman Operators

Consider a state variable
u(\mathbf{x},t):\mathcal{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
whose dynamics are governed by parabolic partial differential equation (PDE),

\partial_{t}u=\mathcal{N}(u)+f,\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad t>0,

(2.1)

where
\mathcal{N}
is a linear or nonlinear differential operator representing the internal dynamics in
d
-dimensional space, and the linear or nonlinear source term
f
represents the external source/sink into the system. Discretization of the simulation domain
\mathcal{D}
into
N
elements or nodes (
N\gg 1
) transforms the PDE
Equation

2.1
into either a high-dimensional linear dynamical system

\frac{\text{d}\mathbf{u}}{\text{d}t}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{f},

(2.2)

or a high-dimensional nonlinear dynamical system

\frac{\text{d}\mathbf{u}}{\text{d}t}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbb{u})+\mathbb{f},

(2.3)

where
\mathbf{u}=[u(\mathbf{x}_{1},t),\cdots,u(\mathbf{x}_{N},t)]^{\top}
is the spatial discretization of
u(\mathbf{x},t)
;
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
are linear and nonlinear operators on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, respectively; and
\mathbf{f}
represents the correspondingly discretized source term
f
.

Low-dimensional ROMs are often used to reduce the computational cost of solving the high-dimensional systems
Equations

2.2
and
2.3
. For example, POD has been deployed to construct accurate and efficient ROMs for
Equation

2.2

[
11
,
23
]
. Time evolution of
u(\mathbf{x},t)
needs to be computed but only in a small subspace of the original high-dimensional space. For nonlinear systems
Equation

2.3
, construction of a right ROM using POD becomes more challenging and requires some modifications, such as empirical interpolation method (EIM)
[
2
]
and discrete empirical interpolation method (DEIM)
[
7
]
, whose accuracy cannot be determined a priori. To the best of our knowledge, error estimates of POD-EIM/DEIM are lacking unless the fully resolved solution is available.

The DMD method aims to approximate the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{A}
in
Equation

2.2
and provides an alternative to POD in solving large linear systems. A major advantage of DMD over POD is its equation-free nature, which allows future-state predictions without any computation of further time evolution. For the nonlinear problems
Equation

2.3
, DMD seeks a finite-dimensional approximation of the infinite-dimensional Koopman operator of the nonlinear dynamics. With carefully chosen observables, a ROM can be constructed in the observable space with sufficient accuracy. We briefly review DMD and the related Koopman operator theory in
Sections

2.1
and
2.2
as a set up for the accuracy analysis in
Section

3
.

2.1
Dynamic mode decomposition

Temporal discretization of
Equation

2.2
with time step
\Delta t
yields

\mathbf{u}^{n+1}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\mathbf{u}^{n}+\Delta t\mathbb{f}^{*},\qquad n\geq 0,

(2.4)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
is an
N\times N
matrix and
\mathbb{f}^{*}
is, e.g., interpolation of
\mathbb{f}^{n}
and
\mathbb{f}^{n+1}
. This is rewritten as

\mathbb{u}^{n+1}=\mathbb{K}\mathbb{u}^{n},\qquad n\geq 0,

(2.5)

where
\mathbb{K}
is an
N
-dimensional linear operator. The fully resolved model
Equation

2.4
is advanced by
m
time steps and the resulting temporal snapshots of
\mathbf{u}(t)
is recorded in two matrices:

\mathbb{X}=\begin{bmatrix}|&|&&|\\
\mathbb{u}^{0}&\mathbb{u}^{1}&\cdots&\mathbb{u}^{m-1}\\
|&|&&|\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{X}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}|&|&&|\\
\mathbb{u}^{1}&\mathbb{u}^{2}&\cdots&\mathbb{u}^{m}\\
|&|&&|\end{bmatrix}.

(2.6)

Using these two data sets, one approximates the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\mathbb{K}
using
Algorithm

1
.

Algorithm 1

DMD algorithm on state space
[
15
]

1.

Apply Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)
\mathbb{X}\approx\mathbb{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbb{V}^{*}
, where
\mathbb{U}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times r}
is a unitary matrix,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
is a diagonal matrix with components
\sigma_{k}\geq 0
that are called singular values of
\mathbf{X}
,
\mathbf{V}^{*}
is the conjugate transpose of unitary matrix
\mathbb{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times m}
, and
r
is the truncated rank chosen by certain criteria.

2.

Compute
\tilde{\mathbb{K}}=\mathbb{U}^{*}\mathbb{X}^{\prime}\mathbb{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}
; use it as a low-rank (
r\times r
) approximation of
\mathbb{K}
.

3.

Compute eigendecomposition of
\tilde{\mathbb{K}}
:
\tilde{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{W}=\mathbb{W}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=(\lambda_{k})
are eigenvalues and columns of
\mathbf{W}
are the corresponding eigenvectors.

4.

Eigenvalues of
\mathbb{K}
can be approximated by
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
with corresponding eigenvectors in the columns of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\mathbb{X}^{\prime}\mathbb{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbb{W}
.

Each column of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
in
Algorithm

1
is a DMD mode corresponding to a particular eigenvalue in
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
. With the approximated eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\mathbb{K}
in hand, a solution at the
(n+1)
-th time step (
n>m
) is constructed analytically as

\mathbb{u}_{\text{DMD}}^{n+1}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{n+1}\mathbb{b},\qquad n>m,

(2.7)

where
\mathbb{b}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}\mathbb{u}^{0}
is an
r\times 1
vector representing the initial amplitude of each mode. Notice that no more iteration is needed in the prediction. The solution at any future time is approximated directly with
Equation

2.7
using only information encapsulated in the first
m
temporal snapshots.

2.2
Koopman Operator Theory

The nonlinear dynamical system
Equation

2.3
belongs to a general class of dynamical systems,

\frac{\text{d}\mathbb{u}}{\text{d}t}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{u}),

(2.8)

where the state
\mathbb{u}\in\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is defined on a smooth
N
-dimensional manifold
\mathcal{M}
, and
\mathcal{N}
is a finite-dimensional nonlinear operator. Given a flow map
\mathcal{N}_{t}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
,

\mathcal{N}_{t}(\mathbb{u}(t_{0}))=\mathbb{u}(t_{0}+t)=\mathbb{u}(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+t}\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{u}(\tau))\text{d}\tau,

(2.9)

the corresponding discrete-time dynamical system is described by

\mathbb{u}^{n+1}=\mathcal{N}_{t}(\mathbb{u}^{n}).

(2.10)

Definition 2.1

(Koopman operator
[
15
]
)
.

For nonlinear dynamic system
Equation

2.8
, the Koopman operator
\mathcal{K}
is an infinite-dimensional linear operator that acts on all observable functions
g:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{C}
so that

\mathcal{K}g(\mathbb{u})=g(\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{u})).

(2.11)

For discrete dynamic system
Equation

2.10
, the discrete-time Koopman operator
\mathcal{K}_{t}
is

\mathcal{K}_{t}g(\mathbb{u}^{n})=g(\mathcal{N}_{t}(\mathbb{u}^{n}))=g(\mathbb{u}^{n+1}).

(2.12)

The Koopman operator transforms the finite-dimensional nonlinear problem
Equation

2.10
in the state space into the infinite-dimensional linear problem
Equation

2.12
in the observable space. Since
\mathcal{K}_{t}
is an infinite-dimensional linear operator, it is equipped with infinite eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
and eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
. In practice, one has to make a finite approximation of the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. The following assumption is essential to both a finite-dimensional approximation and the choice of observables.

Assumption 2.1
.

Let
\mathbf{y}
denote a
p\times 1
vector of observables,

\mathbb{y}^{n}=\mathbb{g}(\mathbb{u}^{n})=\begin{bmatrix}g_{1}(\mathbb{u}^{n})\\
\vdots\\
g_{p}(\mathbb{u}^{n})\end{bmatrix},

(2.13)

where
g_{j}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{C}
is an observable function, with
j=1,\cdots,p
. If the chosen observable
\mathbb{g}
is restricted to an invariant subspace spanned by eigenfunctions of the Koopman operator
\mathcal{K}_{t}
, then it induces a linear operator
\mathbb{K}
that is finite-dimensional and advances these eigenobservable functions on this subspace
[
6
]
.

Based on
2.1
, the DMD algorithm can be deployed to approximate the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
\mathbb{K}
using the collected temporal snapshots in the observable space. This DMD strategy is implemented in
Algorithm

2
.

Algorithm 2

DMD algorithm on observable space
[
15
]

0.

Create the data matrices of observables

\mathbb{Y}=\begin{bmatrix}|&|&&|\\
\mathbb{y}^{0}&\mathbb{y}^{1}&\cdots&\mathbb{y}^{m-1}\\
|&|&&|\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{Y}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}|&|&&|\\
\mathbb{y}^{1}&\mathbb{y}^{2}&\cdots&\mathbb{y}^{m}\\
|&|&&|\end{bmatrix}

(2.14)

where each column is given by
\mathbb{y}^{k}=\mathbb{g}(\mathbb{u}^{k})
.

1.

Apply SVD
\mathbb{Y}\approx\mathbb{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbb{V}^{*}
with
\mathbb{U}\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times r},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r},\mathbb{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times m}
, where
r
is the truncated rank chosen by certain criteria.

2.

Compute
\tilde{\mathbb{K}}=\mathbb{U}^{*}\mathbb{X}^{\prime}\mathbb{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}
as a
r\times r
low-rank approximation for
\mathbb{K}
.

3.

Compute eigendecomposition of
\tilde{\mathbb{K}}
:
\tilde{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{W}=\mathbb{W}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
,
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=(\lambda_{k})
.

4.

Reconstruct eigendecomposition of
\mathbb{K}
. Eigenvalues are
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
and eigenvectors are
\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\mathbb{X}^{\prime}\mathbb{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbb{W}
.

5.

Predict future
\mathbb{y}_{\text{DMD}}^{n+1}
as

\mathbb{y}_{\text{DMD}}^{n+1}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\Lambda^{n+1}\mathbb{b},\quad\mathbb{b}=\Phi^{-1}\mathbb{y}^{0}\qquad\mbox{for}\ n>m.

(2.15)

6.

Transform from observables space back to the state space,

\mathbb{u}_{\text{DMD}}^{n}=\mathbb{g}^{-1}(\mathbb{y}_{\text{DMD}}^{n}).

(2.16)

Remark 2.1
.

Connections between the DMD theory and the Koopman spectral analysis under specific conditions on the observables and collected data are established by a theorem in
[
33
]
. This theorem indicates that judicious selection of the observables is critical to success of the Koopman method.

Remark 2.2
.

In general, there is no principled way to select observables without expert knowledge of a dynamical system. Machine learning techniques can be deployed to identify relevant terms in the dynamics from data, which guide selection of the observables
[
28
,
34
,
4
]
.

3.2
Main Results

We rewrite the DMD prediction

\displaystyle\mathbb{u}_{\text{DMD}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{n+1}\mathbb{b}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{n}\mathbb{b}

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\tau}^{n}\|_{2}=

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{u}^{n}-\mathbb{u}^{n-1}-\mathbb{B}\mathbb{u}^{n-1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{u}^{n}-\mathbb{u}^{n-1}-(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}-\mathbb{I}_{N\times N})\mathbb{u}^{n-1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{u}^{n}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}\mathbb{u}^{n-1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|(\mathbb{K}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1})\mathbb{u}^{n-1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{K}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}\|_{F}\|\mathbb{u}^{n-1}\|_{2}.

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{n-m}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}\mathbb{e}^{m}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-m-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{k}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\tau}^{n-k}.

Then

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{e}^{n}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{n-m}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}\|_{F}\|\mathbb{e}^{m}\|_{2}+(n-m)\varepsilon_{m}\max_{0\leq k\leq n-m-1}\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{k}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}\|_{F}

(3.15)

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{n-m}\|_{F}\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}\|_{F}\|\mathbb{e}^{m}\|_{2}+(n-m)\varepsilon_{m}\max_{0\leq k\leq n-m-1}\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{k}\|_{F}\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}\|_{F}.

According to
Lemma

3.2
,
\|\mathbb{e}^{m}\|
is fixed and minimal. Hence, if accuracy of the local truncation error is of
\mathcal{O}((\Delta t)^{q})
, then the global truncation error is of
\mathcal{O}((\Delta t)^{q-1})
.
∎

Theorem

3.2
provides quantitative error bounds of the DMD method with explicit error dependence. In complex simulations, one would not expect the DMD prediction from a local data set to capture the global dynamics accurately. Instead, one can use the error bounds to set up a threshold for DMD prediction limits and combine a resolved algorithm with fast DMD prediction. This would considerably speed up the simulations.

3.3
Application to nonlinear parabolic problems

Consider a general nonlinear reaction-diffusion equation in
d
spatial dimensions

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u=\nabla\cdot[k\psi(u)\nabla u]+f(u),\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad t>0\\
&u(\mathbf{x},0)=u_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D},\end{aligned}\right.

(3.16)

with non-negative functions
k=k(\mathbf{x})
and
\psi=\psi(u)
whose product is diffusion coefficient
D(\mathbf{x},u)=k(\mathbf{x})\psi(u)
. Spatial discretization of
Equation

3.16
leads to the corresponding high-dimensional nonlinear ODE
Equation

2.3
. Its DMD treatment relies on the one’s ability to identify informative observables and requires the prior knowledge of the structure of governing equations such as
Equation

3.16
. Examples in
Sections

4.2
,
4.3
, and
4.4
illustrate the critical role of observable selection in the DMD method.

For
Equation

3.16
, expert knowledge suggests the existence of a function
\eta(u)
such that
\eta^{\prime}(u)=\psi(u)
, which can be constructed via the Kirchhoff transform (e.g.,
[
29
,
30
]
). Then, by chain rule,
Equation

3.16
is rewritten as

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u-\nabla\cdot[k\nabla\eta(u)]=f(u)\\
&u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\end{aligned}\right.

(3.17)

so that the nonlinear diffusion in
u
becomes linear in
\eta
. Spatial discretization of
Equation

3.17
leads to

\frac{\text{d}\mathbb{u}}{\text{d}t}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\boldsymbol{\eta}(\mathbb{u})+\mathbb{F}(\mathbb{u})

(3.18)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
is the same linear operator in
Equation

2.2
. Motivated by the nonlinear observable choice for the nonlinear Schrödinger equation in
[
15
]
, and by the accurate and robust performance of DMD on linear diffusion reported below, we choose the observable

\displaystyle\mathbb{g}=[g_{1}(\mathbb{u}),\cdots,g_{p}(\mathbb{u})],\qquad\mbox{s.t.}\ \ \mathbb{u},\boldsymbol{\eta}(\mathbb{u}),\mathbb{F}(\mathbb{u})\in\text{span}\{g_{1}(\mathbb{u}),\cdots,g_{p}(\mathbb{u})\}.

(3.19)

The reference solution of
Equation

3.16
is obtained by discretizing
Equation

3.18
in time,

\mathbb{u}^{n+1}=\mathbb{u}^{n}+\Delta t\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}+\Delta t\mathbb{F}^{*},

(3.20)

where the superscript
*
denotes linear interpolation between time
t^{n+1}
and
t^{n}
. For the observables in
Equation

3.19
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{u}^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\eta}^{n+1},\mathbb{F}^{n+1}\in\text{span}\{g_{1}(\mathbb{u}^{n+1}),\cdots,g_{p}(\mathbb{u}^{n+1})\},

(3.21)

\displaystyle\mathbb{u}^{n},\boldsymbol{\eta}^{n},\mathbb{F}^{n}\in\text{span}\{g_{1}(\mathbb{u}^{n}),\cdots,g_{p}(\mathbb{u}^{n})\}.

Thus,
Algorithm

1
induces a linear operator denoted by
\mathbb{K}
such that

\mathbb{y}^{n+1}=\mathbb{K}\mathbb{y}^{n},

(3.22)

where
\mathbb{y}^{n}=\mathbb{g}(\mathbb{u}^{n})
defined in
Equation

2.13
. Treating
Equation

3.22
as reference solution, against which we compare the DMD prediction
Equation

2.16
, one gets exactly the same formulae as
Equation

2.5
and
Equation

2.7
but in observable space:

\displaystyle\mathbb{y}^{n+1}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}\mathbb{y}^{n},

(3.23)

\displaystyle\mathbb{y}_{\text{DMD}}^{n+1}=

\displaystyle\Phi\Lambda^{n+1}\mathbb{b}.

So the error analysis in
Section

3
carries on in terms of
\mathbb{y}
.

5
Conclusion and Future outlook

We derived error bounds of DMD predictions for linear and nonlinear parabolic PDEs and verified their accuracy on four computational examples of increasing degree of complexity. Our analysis leads to the following major conclusions.

1.

When combined with an adequate choice observables, the Koopman operator maps the nonlinear underlying dynamics with the linear observable space, where DMD algorithm can be implemented with good accuracy and efficiency.

2.

In the extrapolation (predictive) mode, DMD outperforms other ROM-based method (e.g., POD) in terms of computational efficiency, because it requires no iteration. At the same time, POD has higher predictive accuracy than DMD.

3.

Our error estimator is consistent with previous theoretic understanding of DMD algorithm and the Koopman operator theory. More importantly, it provides a quantitative measure of the accuracy of DMD predictions.

In the follow-up studies we will used our error estimators of DMD predictions to address several challenges in scientific computing:

1.

For PDEs with random coefficients, e.g., for PDE-based models of flow and transport in (randomly) heterogeneous porous media, DMD predictions with quantitative error bounds might provide a means for accelerating computational expensive Monte Carlo and multiscale simulations.

2.

Our error estimators can be used to guide the design of hybrid algorithms that combine DMD predictions with fully resolved solutions of multi-dimensional complex problems.

3.

It might be possible to generalize our results to a broader context of advection-diffusion equations. Multiresolution DMD (mrDMD), instead of DMD, can be used to overcome the translational invariant issues in advection.
