Droplet condensation in the lattice gas with density functional theory
M. Maeritz and M. Oettel

Abstract
A density functional for the lattice gas (Ising model) from fundamental measure theory is applied to the problem of droplet states in three–dimensional, finite systems. Similar to previous simulation studies, the sequence of droplets changing to cylinders and to planar slabs is found upon increasing the average density ρ ¯ \bar{\rho} in the system. Owing to the discreteness of the lattice, additional effects in the state curve for the chemical potential μ ⁡ ( ρ ¯ ) \mu(\bar{\rho}) are seen upon lowering the temperature away from the critical temperature (oscillations in μ ⁡ ( ρ ¯ ) \mu(\bar{\rho}) in the slab portion and spiky undulations in μ ⁡ ( ρ ¯ ) \mu(\bar{\rho}) in the cylinder portion as well as an undulatory behavior of the radius of the surface of tension R s R_{\text{s}} in the droplet region). This behavior in the cylinder and droplet region is related to washed–out layering transitions at the surface of liquid cylinders and droplets. The analysis of the large–radius behavior of the surface tension γ ⁡ ( R s ) \gamma(R_{\text{s}}) gave a dominant contribution ∝ 1 / R s 2 \propto 1/R_{\text{s}}^{2} , although the consistency of γ ⁡ ( R s ) \gamma(R_{\text{s}}) with the asymptotic behavior of the radius–dependent Tolman length seems to suggest a weak logarithmic contribution ∝ ln ⁡ R s / R s 2 \propto\ln R_{\text{s}}/R_{\text{s}}^{2} in γ ⁡ ( R s ) \gamma(R_{\text{s}}) . The coefficient of this logarithmic term is smaller than a universal value derived with field–theoretic methods.



I
Introduction

For the practical description of phase transitions in the nucleation regime, the free energy of a critical nucleus of the newly forming phase plays a prominent role. This is manifest
in classical nucleation theory (CNT) for liquid drop nucleation in a supersaturated gas
where the formation free energy
\Delta F(V_{\text{d}})
is simply described by a sum
of a volume term (proportional to the droplet volume
V_{\text{d}}
in a supersaturated bulk state) and
a surface term
Kashchiev2000
. It is essential in kinetic approaches
where a formation rate of new droplets is proportional to
\exp(-\beta\Delta F(V_{\text{d}}))
and thus inverse exponentially
dependent on the formation free energy (here,
\beta=1/(k_{\text{B}}T)
is the inverse
temperature). However, a critical droplet is at best metastable and it is problematic to associate a free energy
with it since the free energy is an equilibrium concept.

In order to avoid such complications, a thermodynamic method for the estimation of
\Delta F(V_{\text{d}})
has been developed
some years ago
Schrader2009
;
Block2010
;
Troester2012
;
Binder2012
. It is based on the analysis of equilibrium states in a finite volume (box) (with
periodic boundary conditions) in the canonical
ensemble. For a given box size in three dimensions (3D), the variation of the particle number (density)
from the coexistence vapor density
to the coexistence liquid density leads through a sequence of states described by

supersaturated vapor
\to
droplets
\to
liquid cylinders
\to
planar interfaces
\to
vapor cylinders

\to
bubbles
\to
undersaturated liquid
. The excess free energy over the supersaturated state (formation free energy
of droplets and cylinders) can be unambiguously computed since it belongs to an equilibrium state. In a box with fixed size,
the range of droplet radii in the droplet state is limited, and one obtains results for a larger range of droplet radii
by combining the results for different box sizes.

This thermodynamic method has been mainly advanced with simulations. The extraction of a radius–dependent surface tension
for droplets has been shown to be influenced by strong finite–size effects whose analysis leads to the conclusion
that an asymptotic expansion of the surface tension in the inverse radius
1/R_{\text{d}}
should not only contain powers of
1/R_{\text{d}}

but also terms logarithmic in
R_{\text{d}}
. In 3D, the leading term, however, is the term
\propto 1/R_{\text{d}}
containing the
famous “Tolman length” but the next term is of this logarithmic type and varies
\propto\ln R_{\text{d}}/R_{\text{d}}^{2}

Wallace1980
.
The available results in the literature show that the extraction from simulations of the Tolman length and other, sub–leading terms
in the expansion of the surface tension is by no means an easy task
Schrader2009
;
Troester2017
;
Song2019
.
This gives a motivation to use
theoretical tools in applying the thermodynamic method, notably density functional theory (DFT).
For most systems of interest which show liquid-vapor
phase transitions the free energy functional (as the necessary prerequisite) is known only approximately but in contrast to
simulations the extrapolation of droplet surface tensions to large radii is easier.
A previously studied example was the Lennard–Jones fluid where
the simulation results indicate a convergence of the asymptotic Tolman length to the DFT result
Block2010
.
However, the next, subleading term did not agree at all. Most likely this was due to the neglect of translational
entropy of the droplet in the analysis of the simulation results (such a term is absent in DFT calculations where the droplet
is always in the center of the numerical box).

From a computational point of view lattice models are easier, both for the elucidation
of phase transition and wetting phenomena with DFT
Archer2014
;
Archer2017
and for simulations.
The comparable ease of simulations has triggered many studies of droplets in the lattice gas or Ising model. Among these are studies focussing on establishing the droplet condensation/evaporation transition, see e.g. Refs.
Biskup2003
;
Nussbaumer2006
and others looking more in detail on the actual nucleation process
Stauffer1982
;
Ryu2010
;
Schmitz2013
.
Owing to the computational advantage, the problem of the leading and
subleading contributions to the expansion of the surface tension (including the problem of the translational entropy)
was revisited in Ref.
Troester2017
in the context of lattice models, the Ising model (lattice gas) in 2D and an Ising–type model
with three–spin interactions in 3D. Therefore it appears to be a worthwhile exercise to apply DFT methods to the droplet problem
in lattice models for complementary results on the expansion of the surface tension, using also the same type of finite boxes (note that in DFT studies of continuum models usually spherical boxes are employed
Koga1998
;
Block2010
;
Gross2018
).
In the present study we use a recently developed density functional for the lattice gas

Maeritz2021
(much improved compared to the standard mean–field functional) for the description of droplet states in finite
cubic boxes. We find the sequence of states as described above but we also find certain effects of the lattice discreteness
in these states which had not been reported so far. These include the existence of equilibrium, planar interfaces under
tension at chemical potentials off coexistence (described in detail in Ref.
Maeritz2021
) and certain layering effects for the droplet and cylinder states.
The symmetry of the lattice gas shows that the expansion of the surface tension must involve a zero Tolman length,
so the leading coefficients in 3D show a dependence
\propto\ln R_{\text{d}}/R_{\text{d}}^{2}
and
\propto 1/R_{\text{d}}^{2}
.

The paper is structured as follows. In Sec.
II
we recapitulate the essence of the lattice gas functional derived in Ref.
Maeritz2021
as well as the phenomenological theory of droplet states. Sec.
III
shows results for the possible states inside the gas–liquid binodal at three selected temperatures and discusses the asymptotic expansions of the droplet surface tension and a radius–dependent Tolman length. In Sec.
IV
we summarize and conclude our work.

II
Theory

II.1
Density functional theory for the lattice gas

The lattice gas model on a simple cubic lattice is defined
in terms of hard particles with mutual exclusion on the same lattice site and which have nearest–neighbor attractions of strength
-\epsilon
.
It is equivalent to the Ising model on the respective lattices.

We define an ensemble–averaged density
\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})
for lattice gas particles (where the index “c” stands for colloid)
on discrete lattice sites
\boldsymbol{s}
of the lattice.
In DFT, one defines a functional for the grand potential
Evans1979

\displaystyle\Omega[\rho_{\text{c}}]=\mathcal{F}^{\text{id}}[\rho_{\text{c}}]+\mathcal{F}^{\text{ex}}[\rho_{\text{c}}]-\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\mu-V_{\text{ext}}(\boldsymbol{s}))\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})

(1)

which is split into an ideal gas free energy functional
\mathcal{F}^{\text{id}}
, an excess free energy functional
\mathcal{F}^{\text{ex}}
and a remaining part
containing the chemical potential
\mu
and the contribution of a one–body external potential
V_{\text{ext}}(\boldsymbol{s})
. The ideal
gas free energy functional is given by

\displaystyle\beta\mathcal{F}^{\text{id}}[\rho_{\text{c}}]=\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})(\ln\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})-1)

(2)

(where
\beta=1/(k_{\text{B}}T)
is the inverse temperature) and the excess functional is generally unknown.

In Ref.
Maeritz2021
, we have derived an approximate excess functional through the following procedure. First, the attractions
between the particles are viewed as effective depletion interactions between the particles and lattice polymers of length 2 with which the particles interact hard. In 3D, there are 3 species of such polymers and correspond to the orientation
\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}

of the polymer particle on the cubic lattice. The lattice polymers do not interact with each other and hence form a three–component
ideal gas. (In spirit, this is a lattice Asakura–Oosawa (AO) model.) Second,
the partition function of the lattice polymers is reformulated in terms of a partition function for a mixture of polymer clusters,
distinguished by their orientation
\alpha
and the number
n
of polymers they contain. Two clusters of polymers with the same
\alpha

but different
n
cannot occupy the same lattice site, since the simultaneous presence of two clusters with size
n
and
m
corresponds to a cluster of size
n+m
. Therefore one can represent the free energy functional of the polymers in terms
of the functional for a multicomponent hard lattice gas of clusters.
Third, the excess free energy functional of the interacting system
of lattice gas particles and polymer clusters is approximated using lattice fundamental measure theory (FMT).

The results of Ref.
Maeritz2021
are summarized best by first introducing an auxiliary functional
\tilde{\varUpsilon}

for the lattice AO system, i.e. the system of lattice gas
particles with density
\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})
coupled grand–canonically to the polymer clusters with orientation
\alpha
and with a total density
\rho_{\text{pc},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{s})=\sum_{n}\rho_{\text{pc}^{n},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{s})
where
\rho_{\text{pc}^{n},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{s})
is the density of clusters of size
n
and
orientation
\alpha
. The polymer cluster chemical potential
\beta^{-1}\ln\zeta_{\alpha}
is the same for all orientations, which defines an orientation–independent activity
\zeta_{\alpha}=\zeta
:

\displaystyle\beta\tilde{\varUpsilon}[\rho_{\text{c}};\{\rho_{\text{pc},\alpha}\}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta\mathcal{F}^{\text{id}}[\rho_{\text{c}}]+\sum_{\alpha}\beta\mathcal{F}^{\text{id}}[\rho_{\text{pc},\alpha}]+\beta\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\text{AO}}^{\text{ex}}[\rho_{\text{c}},\{\rho_{\text{pc},\alpha}\}]

(3)

\displaystyle-\ln\zeta\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}}\sum_{\alpha}\rho_{\text{pc},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{s})\;.

The activity of the polymer clusters is related to the attraction strength
\epsilon
by

\displaystyle\zeta=\exp(\beta\epsilon)-1\;.

(4)

The lattice FMT expression for the excess free energy
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\text{AO}}^{\text{ex}}[\rho_{\text{c}},\{\rho_{\text{pc},\alpha}\}]
of lattice gas particles and
polymer clusters is given in App.
A
.

Minimization of this lattice AO auxiliary functional with respect to
\rho_{\text{pc},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{s})
gives an effective AO functional,
depending only on the lattice gas particle density profile
\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})
and the attraction strength through
\zeta
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\text{AO}}^{\text{eff}}[\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s});\epsilon(\zeta)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{min}_{\rho_{\text{pc},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{s})}\tilde{\varUpsilon}[\rho_{\text{c}};\{\rho_{\text{pc},\alpha}\}]

(5)

Finally, the full lattice gas functional is obtained by performing a necessary subtraction of a constant and one–body term
in
\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})
and is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{lg}}[\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s});\epsilon(\zeta)]=\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\text{AO}}^{\text{eff}}[\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s});\rho^{\text{res}}_{\text{p}}]+\rho^{\text{res}}_{\text{p}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}}\sum_{\alpha}\left(1-\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\{\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}+\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\alpha}\}}\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})\right)\;.

(6)

Here,
\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\alpha}
is a unit vector on the lattice in
\alpha
–direction and
\rho^{\text{res}}_{\text{p}}=\beta\epsilon=\ln(\zeta+1)
is the reservoir density of polymers (which is the same for all polymer species).
We name this functional the “Highlander functional”, following the nomenclature in Ref.
Cuesta2005
where the idea of using polymer clusters in the treatment of lattice AO models was introduced first.

Note that this functional is superior to the standard mean–field functional, defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{lg}}^{\text{mf}}[\rho_{\text{c}}]=\beta^{-1}\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}}\left(\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})\ln\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})+[1-\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})]\ln[1-\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})]\right)-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\text{n.n.}(\boldsymbol{s})}\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})\;.

(7)

Here, the first term is the exact functional for the hard lattice gas and the second term gives the
contribution from attractions in random phase approximation
Evans1979
.
The summation over lattice points
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
is restricted to nearest neighbors of
\boldsymbol{s}
(
\text{n.n.}(\boldsymbol{s})
). For bulk fluids, the standard mean–field functional gives a phase diagram like the Bragg–Williams approximation and the Highlander functional results in the more precise Bethe–Peierls approximation for that. Planar surface tensions in 3D evaluated with the Highlander functional are close to simulation results
Maeritz2021
.

II.2
Numerical minimization of the Highlander functional

In order to obtain density profile solutions for droplets in finite boxes, one needs
to perform the numerical minimization in 3D of
\Omega=\mathcal{F}_{\text{lg}}-\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rho_{\text{c}}(\mu-V_{\text{ext}})
(with
\mathcal{F}_{\text{lg}}
given in
Eq. (
6
)) with respect to
\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})
. However, the analytic derivative with respect to
\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})

is quite involved, owing to the dependency of
\rho_{\text{pc},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{s})
on
\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})
. It is therefore advisable
to minimize the total particle–polymer grand functional

\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}[\rho_{\text{c}},\{\rho_{\text{pc},\alpha}\}]=\tilde{\Upsilon}-\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})(\mu-V_{\text{ext}}(\boldsymbol{s})){+\rho^{\text{res}}_{\text{p}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}}\sum_{\alpha}\left(1-\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\{\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}+\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\alpha}\}}\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})\right)}

(8)

(with
\tilde{\Upsilon}
given in Eq. (
3
)) with respect to
\rho_{\text{pc},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{s})
and
\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})

simultaneously. The self–consistent equations for the colloid and polymer cluster density profiles
take a form suitable for Picard iteration. They read

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{c}}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z(\boldsymbol{s})\,e^{6\beta\epsilon}\,\frac{\begin{multlined}\left(1-m_{1}(\boldsymbol{s})\right)\left(1-m_{2}(\boldsymbol{s}-\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{x})\right)\left(1-m_{3}(\boldsymbol{s})\right)\left(1-m_{4}(\boldsymbol{s}-\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{y})\right)\\
\\
\cdot\left(1-m_{5}(\boldsymbol{s})\right)\left(1-m_{6}(\boldsymbol{s}-\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{z})\right)\end{multlined}}{\left(1-m_{10}(\boldsymbol{s})\right)^{5}}

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{pc},x}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta\;\frac{\left(1-m_{1}(\boldsymbol{s})\right)\left(1-m_{2}(\boldsymbol{s})\right)}{\left(1-m_{7}(\boldsymbol{s})\right)}

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{pc},y}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta\;\frac{\left(1-m_{3}(\boldsymbol{s})\right)\left(1-m_{4}(\boldsymbol{s})\right)}{\left(1-m_{8}(\boldsymbol{s})\right)}

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{pc},z}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta\;\frac{\left(1-m_{5}(\boldsymbol{s})\right)\left(1-m_{6}(\boldsymbol{s})\right)}{\left(1-m_{9}(\boldsymbol{s})\right)}\qquad.

(13)

Here,
z(\boldsymbol{s})=\exp(\beta[\mu-V_{\text{ext}}(\boldsymbol{s})])
. The definitions of the weighted densities
m_{i}(\boldsymbol{s})
is given in Eq. (
25
). The Picard iterations are done in a standard manner with suitable mixing of old and new density profiles.

For the problem of inhomogeneous states (droplet, cylinder, slab) in the finite box, there is no external potential:
V_{\text{ext}}(\boldsymbol{s})=0
.
For an initial state at a specific density, an excess density over the bulk gas density in an approximately spherical, cylindrical or slablike domain is chosen,
or a density profile from a previous iteration at a slightly different density is used.
The final results are the equilibrated inhomogeneous density profiles
\rho_{\text{c,eq}}(\boldsymbol{s})
and the associated auxiliary
polymer cluster profile
\rho_{\text{pc-eq},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{s})
as well as the total grand potential

\Omega=\tilde{\Omega}[\rho_{\text{c,eq}},\{\rho_{\text{pc-eq},\alpha}\}]
. There are hysteresis effects in the system, i.e. at a specific average density near transition regions,
both a droplet resp. cylinder profile can be found in the droplet–cylinder transition region, or a cylinder resp. slab profile in the cylinder–slab transition region. Reported below are the profiles with the lowest free energy at a specific average density. From droplet profiles surface tensions and droplet radii are extracted
using the phenomenological considerations of the next section.

II.3
Phenomenological theory for the surface tension of droplets

The treatment here is quite standard, see e.g. Ref.
Troester2012
.
We assume a stable state of a droplet in the center of a finite box
\cal V
with volume
V
at chemical potential
\mu
.
The chemical potential is not equal to the chemical potential at liquid–vapor coexistence,
\mu\neq\mu_{\text{coex}}
.
For a homogeneous system, the function
\rho(\mu)
has two solutions near coexistence: for
\mu=\mu_{\text{coex}}
these
are the coexistence densities
\rho_{\text{v,coex}}
(vapor) and
\rho_{\text{l,coex}}
(liquid). For
\mu\neq\mu_{\text{coex}}
we associate with
\rho_{\text{v}}=\rho(\mu)

the solution near
\rho_{\text{v,coex}}
and with
\rho_{\text{l}}=\rho(\mu)
the solution near
\rho_{\text{l,coex}}
. Similarly we define

p_{\text{v}}=p(\mu)
and
p_{\text{l}}=p(\mu)
where
p(\mu)
describes the pressure as function of chemical potential for a bulk system.
The droplet has a total free energy
F
resp. a grand potential
\Omega
and
is characterized by an equilibrium density profile
\rho(\mathbf{r})
. The association of a droplet surface corresponding
to this density profile is arbitrary but two definitions of a surface
{\cal A}_{\text{d}}=\partial{\cal V}_{\text{d}}

(where
{\cal V}_{\text{d}}
is the spherical droplet with volume
V_{\text{d}}
) are common and useful. The first is the notion
of an equimolar surface defined by

\displaystyle\int_{{\cal V}_{\text{d}}}(\rho_{\text{l}}-\rho(\mathbf{r}))d\mathbf{r}=\int_{{\cal V}\backslash{\cal V}_{\text{d}}}(\rho(\mathbf{r})-\rho_{\text{v}})d\mathbf{r}\;.

(14)

The condition is equivalent to the statement that the
total volume can be divided into a homogeneous liquid inside
{\cal V}_{\text{d}}
with density
\rho_{\text{l}}
and a homogeneous
vapor in
{\cal V}\backslash{\cal V}_{\text{d}}
with density
\rho_{\text{v}}
such that the
total number of particles
N=\int_{\cal V}\rho(\mathbf{r})d\mathbf{r}=\rho_{\text{l}}V_{\text{d}}+\rho_{\text{v}}(V-V_{\text{d}})
(no excess adsorption
at the droplet surface).
The drop is assumed to be spherical with radius
R_{\text{e}}
(equimolar radius) which follows from the above condition
as

\displaystyle R_{\text{e}}=\sqrt[3]{\frac{3}{4\pi}\frac{{(\bar{\rho}-\rho_{\text{v}})}\,V}{\rho_{\text{l}}-\rho_{\text{v}}}}\;,

(15)

with the average density
\bar{\rho}=(1/V)\int_{{\cal V}}\rho(\mathbf{r})\,d\mathbf{r}
in the box. For the lattice gas, one has to use
the equilibrium profile
\rho_{\text{c,eq}}(\boldsymbol{s})
and replace the integral by a sum over all lattice points.
The second definition is the so–called surface of tension which rests on a mechanical definition.
First, one defines the surface tension
\gamma
as an excess grand potential

\displaystyle\gamma A_{\text{d}}=\Omega-(-p_{\text{l}}V_{\text{d}}-p_{\text{v}}(V-V_{\text{d}}))\;,

(16)

and from the definition it is clear that the surface tension is not unique but depends on the choice of the
droplet surface. Second, the radius
R_{\text{s}}
of the spherical drop surface of tension is fixed by the Laplace condition

\displaystyle\Delta p=p_{\text{l}}-p_{\text{v}}=\frac{2\gamma}{R_{\text{s}}}\;.

(17)

It is useful to introduce the (radius-dependent) Tolman length by

\displaystyle\delta(R_{\text{s}})=R_{\text{e}}(R_{\text{s}})-R_{\text{s}}\;.

(18)

From the Gibbs adsorption equation, one can derive a differential equation for
\gamma(R_{\text{s}})
, named the
Gibbs–Tolman–Koenig–Buff (GTKB) equation
Tolman1949
:

\displaystyle\frac{d\ln\gamma}{d\ln R_{\text{s}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{2\delta}{R_{\text{s}}}\left[1+\frac{\delta}{R_{\text{s}}}+\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{\delta}{R_{\text{s}}}\right)^{2}\right]}{1+\frac{2\delta}{R_{\text{s}}}\left[1+\frac{\delta}{R_{\text{s}}}+\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{\delta}{R_{\text{s}}}\right)^{2}\right]}

(19)

A solution for
\gamma(R_{\text{s}})
require knowledge of the function
\delta(R_{\text{s}})
, so the GTKB equation should be viewed
as the consistency relation between these two functions in the first place.

We proceed by an
ansatz
for an expansion of
\gamma(R_{\text{s}})
, valid for large radii
Troester2017
:

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma(R_{\text{s}})}{\gamma_{\infty}}=1-\frac{2\delta_{\infty}}{R_{\text{s}}}+\frac{A}{\beta\gamma_{\infty}}\,\frac{\ln(R_{\text{s}}/a)}{R_{\text{s}}^{2}}+\frac{B}{\beta\gamma_{\infty}}\,\frac{1}{R_{\text{s}}^{2}}+\dots

(20)

Here,
\gamma_{\infty}=\gamma(R_{\text{s}}\to\infty)
and likewise
\delta_{\infty}=\delta(R_{\text{s}}\to\infty)
.
Furthermore,
\beta=1/(k_{\text{B}}T)
is the inverse temperature and
a
is a microscopic length, characteristic for the
system (for the lattice gas,
a
is simply the unit cell side length and is put to 1).
This
ansatz
combines a simple
1/R_{\text{s}}
–expansion with capillary–wave droplet shape fluctuations
Wallace1980
, here
the constant
A=-7/(12\pi)\approx-0.19
is universal and can be obtained from integrating over droplet interface fluctuations
using a standard Landau–Ginzburg Hamiltonian
Prestipino2012
;
Prestipino2013
;
Prestipino2014
. Note that the exact value of
a
is unimportant,
using a different value
a^{\prime}
just results in a shift in the nonuniversal constant
B
by
A\ln(a^{\prime}/a)
.
This expansion of
\gamma(R_{\text{s}})
is consistent with the GTKB equation if the following expansion for the
Tolman length holds:

\displaystyle\delta(R_{\text{s}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{\infty}-\frac{A}{\beta\gamma_{\infty}}\,\frac{\ln(R_{\text{s}}/a)}{R_{\text{s}}}+\left(\frac{\frac{1}{2}A-B}{\beta\gamma_{\infty}}+3\delta_{\infty}^{2}\right)\,\frac{1}{R_{\text{s}}}+\dots

(21)

In the lattice gas there is a symmetry between the liquid and the gas phase. This entails a vanishing Tolman length
\delta_{\infty}
.
If furthermore there were no logarithmic term in the expansion, the coefficient for the linear term
\propto-1/R_{\text{s}}

in the Tolman length and the quadratic term
\propto 1/R_{\text{s}}^{2}
in the surface tension would be equal. This will be important below.

III.1
\mu
–equation of state and droplet solutions

Figure 2:
(a)
\mu
–equation of state for the Highlander functional at
\beta\epsilon=1.0

\bar{\rho}=0.155

45^{3}

For a finite system, one can obtain solutions for the full range of average densities
\bar{\rho}
between the coexistence
densities
\rho_{\text{v,coex}}
and
\rho_{\text{l,coex}}
. These define a curve
\mu(\bar{\rho})
(different for each system size) which we call “
\mu
–equation of state”.

At the highest temperature
\beta\epsilon=1.0
for the Highlander functional,
\mu(\bar{\rho})
shows the typical behavior
known from previous work
Schrader2009
;
Schmitz2013
, see Fig.
2
(a). For the average density
\bar{\rho}
slightly larger than
\rho_{\text{v,coex}}
,
the system stays homogeneous and follows the bulk
\mu
–equation of state (shown as vdW–loop in Fig.
2
(a)).
Increasing
\bar{\rho}
, a transition to a droplet state occurs, characterized by a sharp downward jump in
\mu
. A second sharp jump
occurs which marks the transition to a cylinder state, and the third sharp jump marks the transition to a state with two planar interfaces (slab state). In Fig.
2
(b) we show an example for a 2D cut through the midplane of the box of the 3D droplet density profile, where the system size is
45^{3}
and the average density is
\bar{\rho}=0.155

We discuss first the oscillations in the slab portion. There we have two planar interfaces which form
under the constraint of a fixed average density. In Ref.
Maeritz2021
we had investigated one planar interface with such a constraint which
simply translates into a constraint for the equimolar position of the interface.
In the lattice gas model, the properties of the interface are a priori only invariant upon discrete shifts
of the interface. Suppose that through the average density constraint one tries to put a few additional particles into
a system with a free, equilibrium interface. These additional particles can be accomodated by displacing the interface towards the vapor phase or they are added in the bulk, i.e. they change the bulk densities. In general, we find that both mechanisms
occur, and we had found solutions at
chemical potentials
\mu\neq\mu_{\text{coex}}
, i.e., these are solutions for a planar interface off-coexistence. Necessarily these
solutions are found with a periodicity of 1 in the equimolar position of the interface and since the interface position is proportional to the average density in the slab system, the oscillations
in
\mu(\bar{\rho})
result.

These oscillations in the slab portion then also translate to the cylinder portion.
It is reasonable to assume that there are certain relaxed cylindrical droplet states whose radii differ by discrete amounts. If one adds more particles to such a state, they may add to the bulk density away from the cylinder droplet (thus changing
\mu
) or increase the cylinder radius. Depending on which process dominates, changes in
\mu
with
\bar{\rho}
are steeper and lead to the spiky oscillations.

IV
Summary and conclusions

In this paper, we have applied a recently developed density functional for the lattice gas (Ising model) to the problem of droplet states in finite systems. We have found the sequence of droplets to cylinders to planar slabs upon increasing the average density
\bar{\rho}
in the system similar to previous simulation studies. Owing to the discreteness of the lattice, we have seen additional effects in the state curve
\mu(\bar{\rho})
(the
\mu
–equation of state). Upon lowering the temperature away from the critical temperature, we first find oscillations in
\mu(\bar{\rho})
in the slab portion. When decreasing the temperature further, spiky undulations in
\mu(\bar{\rho})
in the cylinder portion are seen and in the droplet region an undulatory behavior of
R_{\text{s}}(\bar{\rho})
(radius of the surface of tension) is found. We could relate this behavior in the cylinder and droplet region to washed–out layering transitions at the surface of liquid cylinders and droplets. The analysis of the large–radius behaviour of the surface tension
\gamma(R_{\text{s}})
gave a dominant contribution
\propto 1/R_{\text{s}}^{2}
, although the consistency of
\gamma(R_{\text{s}})
with the asymptotic behavior of
\delta(R_{\text{s}})
(the radius–dependent Tolman length) through the GTKB equation seems to suggest a weak logarithmic contribution
\propto\ln R_{\text{s}}/R_{\text{s}}^{2}
in
\gamma(R_{\text{s}})
. The coefficient of this logarithmic term is smaller than a universal value derived with field–theoretic methods.

We remark that Ref.
Tovbin2010
utilizes a Bethe–Peierls approach for the lattice gas to calculate numerical solutions for droplets. However, further intermediate approximations are made in Ref.
Tovbin2010
(assumption of radially symmetric density profiles, assumption of
an effective distorted lattice at the droplet interface) such that a comparison is difficult.
While we find a reduction of the surface tension with decreasing radius for all investigated
temperatures, an increase is found in Ref.
Tovbin2010
for low temperatures.

Although the lattice gas (Ising model) has been studied thoroughly with simulations, no particular attention has been paid to the lattice discreteness effects observed here. It would be interesting to check for those in future simulations. In a wider perspective, it would also be desirable to develop DFT and simulation methods further for lattice or continuous systems to achieve agreement for the behavior of
\gamma(R_{\text{s}})
over a wider range of droplet sizes.

Appendix A
The excess free energy functional for the system of lattice gas particles and polymer clusters

The interactions between lattice gas particles and polymer clusters are characterized as follows:
Both the particles (with density
\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})
) and polymer clusters of a certain orientation
\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}

(with density
\rho_{\text{pc},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{s})
) are separately hard lattice gases
(mutual exclusion of particles on one lattice site). Polymer clusters of different orientation do not interact
with each other. The lattice gas particles are excluded from the site of the polymer clusters and one additional site in
\alpha
–direction. Intrinsically, this is a nonadditive model, and the construction of the excess free energy
functional proceeds via lattice FMT
Lafuente2002
;
Lafuente2004
. In 1D, this gives the exact functional
Cuesta2005
.

The construction of lattice FMT functionals proceeds via the following iterative procedure
Lafuente2004
.
First, one finds a maximal set of 0D cavities. A 0D cavity consists of a set of lattice points
for each species with the following property: If one particle of a certain species occupies one
of the points in the set, no other particle will fit in the cavity. The 0D cavity is maximal if
no further points can be added to the set.
The requirement on the excess functional is that it gives
the exact 0D excess free energy for a density distribution, compatible with any such maximal cavity at an arbitrary location.
The exact 0D excess free energy of a cavity with occupation
\eta
is given by
\beta^{-1}\Phi^{\text{0D}}(\eta)
with

\displaystyle\Phi^{\text{0D}}(\eta)=\eta+(1-\eta)\ln(1-\eta)\;.

(24)

Second, the iterative procedure is started as follows: The excess free energy is a sum over
the 0D free energies of all such density distributions. However, when a specific cavity of the maximal set is evaluated with this trial
functional, it will generate the correct 0D free energy plus some residual terms. All these residual terms are explicitly subtracted
in an updated excess free energy. Re–evaluation with a specific cavity may result in further residual terms which need to be subtracted again.
As shown in Ref.
Lafuente2005
, this procedure is guaranteed to terminate with no residual terms and thus the excess functional
has the desired property of giving the exact 0D free energy for any maximal cavity.
In our previous work, Ref.
Maeritz2021
, the construction is performed in detail for the lattice gas in 1D, 2D and 3D.

In 3D, the iteration procedure leads to a set of 10 weighted densities:

\displaystyle m_{1}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle:=\rho_{\text{pc},x}(\boldsymbol{s})+\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle m_{2}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle:=\rho_{\text{pc},x}(\boldsymbol{s})+\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s}+\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{x})

\displaystyle m_{3}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle:=\rho_{\text{pc},y}(\boldsymbol{s})+\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle m_{4}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle:=\rho_{\text{pc},y}(\boldsymbol{s})+\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s}+\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{y})

\displaystyle m_{5}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle:=\rho_{\text{pc},z}(\boldsymbol{s})+\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle m_{6}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle:=\rho_{\text{pc},z}(\boldsymbol{s})+\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s}+\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{z})

\displaystyle m_{7}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle:=\rho_{\text{pc},x}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle m_{8}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle:=\rho_{\text{pc},y}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle m_{9}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle:=\rho_{\text{pc},z}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle m_{10}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle:=\rho_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})\qquad.

(25)

The excess free energy functional can be written with a local free energy density

\displaystyle\beta\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\text{AO}}^{\text{ex}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}}\Phi_{\text{AO,3D}}(\boldsymbol{s})

(26)

and this density
\Phi_{\text{AO,3D}}(\boldsymbol{s})
is given by

\Phi_{\text{AO,3D}}(\boldsymbol{s})=\Phi^{\text{0D}}\left(m_{1}\right)+\Phi^{\text{0D}}\left(m_{2}\right)+\Phi^{\text{0D}}\left(m_{3}\right)+\Phi^{\text{0D}}\left(m_{4}\right)+\Phi^{\text{0D}}\left(m_{5}\right)+\Phi^{\text{0D}}\left(m_{6}\right)\\
-\Phi^{\text{0D}}\left(m_{7}\right)-\Phi^{\text{0D}}\left(m_{8}\right)-\Phi^{\text{0D}}\left(m_{9}\right)-5\,\Phi^{\text{0D}}\left(m_{10}\right)\;.

(27)
