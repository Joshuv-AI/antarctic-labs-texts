Abstract




To the blessed memory of Yuri Ivanovich Manin

1.
introduction

The aim of this note is a study of the Gauss - Manin connection on the chiral de Rham complex.

In the seminal paper
[
G
]
Grothendieck has explained (and introduced the name of) the Gauss - Manin connection, cf.
[
M
]
; his explanation is based on the Cartan homotopy
formula ("formule chère à Cartan"). In
[
T
]
(see also
[
GDT
]
,
[
Ge
]
,
[
DTT
]
) the mechanism of the Cartan homotopy formula was formalized under the name
of
calculus
and generalized to the noncommutative situation.

A calculus is a structure that formalizes the standard relations between the graded Lie algebra of polyvector fields

\Lambda^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{X}
and the de Rham complex
\Omega^{\bullet}_{X}
of a manifold
X
.

The aim of the present note is to propose a chiral version of the calculus (we deal here only with the commutative case, although we believe that a noncommutative version exists as well).

In sect.
2
we propose our version of the classical formalism of Grothendieck and
Katz - Oda
[
KO
]
.
It is a more precise version of the Grothendieck construction.

In sect.
3
we discuss the relative chiral de Rham complex
\Omega_{X/S}^{ch}
for a smooth morphism

f:X\longrightarrow S
of smooth algebraic varieties, the usual chiral de Rham complex [MSV] corresponding to the case
S=*
. In sect.
4
we introduce a chiral version of the calculus algebra which acts on

\Omega_{X/S}^{ch}
. Here a mechanism of anomaly cancellation appears, similar to
the one which gives rise to the usual chiral de Rham.

After passing to the cohomology this action
induces the usual Gauss - Manin connection on the relative cohomology sheaf
R^{\bullet}f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}
.

The main results of this paper are stated and proved in sects.
2
and
4
, and the reader might find it reasonable to skip sect.
3
on first reading. Sect.
3
is our attempt, perhaps not overly successful, to retell a number of well-known results using the language of chiral algebras introduced in
[
BD
]
. The reason we felt this was worth doing is best understood by contemplating the exact sequence (
4.1.1
). This exact sequence represents the Beilinson-Drinfeld way of defining an algebra of chiral differential operators and the degree of clarity it achieves is quite extraordinary, but it does use the technology of
[
BD
]
in earnest – compound pseudo-tensor categories, Lie* algebras and algebroids, etc., the concepts which do not lend themselves easily to treatment by more traditional vertex algebra methods.

2.
calculus

2.1.
Calc 1.

Let
p:X\rightarrow S
be a smooth morphism of smooth algebraic varieties over
\mathbb{C}
. We will be writing
X/S
to indicate that this morphism is fixed
and various standard constructions are understood in the relative sense. For example, the structure sheaf
\mathcal{O}_{X/S}
is automatically a sheaf of
p^{-1}\mathcal{O}_{S}
-modules,
\Omega_{X/S}
will stand for the module of Kähler differentials over
\mathcal{O}_{S}
,
\mathcal{T}_{X/S}
is the algebra of
\mathcal{O}_{S}
-derivations, etc.

Along with the relative tangent bundle
TX/S
, one can consider its superversion,
\Pi TX/S
, obtained from the former declaring that the fibers of
the projection
TX/S\rightarrow X/S
to be odd.

The supervariety
\Pi TX/S
carries an algebra of differential operators,
D_{\Pi TX/S}
, which is filtered,
D_{\Pi TX/S}=\cup_{i\geq 0}D_{\Pi TX/S}^{\leq i}
, and generated by the standard Picard-Lie algebroid:

(2.1.1)

0\rightarrow\Omega^{\bullet}(X/S)\rightarrow D_{\Pi TX/S}^{\leq 1}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{\Pi TX/S}\rightarrow 0,

where the de Rham complex
\Omega^{\bullet}(X/S)
, regarded for the time being only as a supercommutative algebra, is nothing but the structure
sheaf
\mathcal{O}_{\Pi TX/S}
. The canonical action of vector fields on differential forms defines a Lie algebra embedding

(2.1.2)

\mbox{Lie}:\mathcal{T}_{X/S}\rightarrow D_{\Pi TX/S}^{\leq 1},\;\xi\mapsto\mbox{Lie}_{\xi}.

Similarly, we have a morphism of Lie algebra modules

(2.1.3)

\iota:\Pi\mathcal{T}_{X/S}\rightarrow D_{\Pi TX/S}^{\leq 1},\;,

so that

(2.1.4)

[\mbox{Lie}_{\xi},\iota_{\eta}]=\iota_{[\xi,\eta]},\;[\iota_{\xi},\iota_{\eta}]=0\;\forall\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{T}_{X/S}.

We can choose, locally on
X/S
, what in the D-module theory is habitually called a coordinate system, that is, a collection
\{x_{i},\partial_{x_{i}},1\leq i\leq\mbox{dim}\mathcal{T}_{X/S}\}
, where
\{\partial_{x_{i}}\}
is a local basis of
\mathcal{T}_{X/S}
,
\{x_{i}\}\subset\mathcal{O}_{X}
and
\partial_{x_{i}}(x_{j})=\delta_{ij}
. We can further extend it to a coordinate system on
\Pi TX/S
:
\{x_{i},dx_{i},\partial_{x_{i}},\partial_{dx_{i}}\}
, where the differential
dx_{i}
is regarded as an odd function on
\Pi TX/S
linear along the fibers of the projection
\Pi TX/S\rightarrow X/S
, and
\partial_{dx_{i}}
is an odd derivation such that

\partial_{dx_{i}}(dx_{j})=\delta_{ij}
and
\partial_{dx_{i}}(x_{j})=0
. Given this choice, we
can write explicit formulas, which will prove important in the chiral algebra situation:

(2.1.5)

\mbox{Lie}_{f_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}}=f_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}+\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{\alpha}}dx_{\alpha}\partial_{dx_{i}},\;\iota_{f_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}}=f_{i}\partial_{dx_{i}}.

Here and elsewhere, the summation w.r.t. repeated indices will be tacitly assumed.

There is another celebrated element of
\mathcal{T}_{\Pi TX/S}
, the de Rham differential,
d_{X/S}=dx_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}
, and it satisfies

(2.1.6)

[d_{X/S},d_{X/S}]=0,\;[d_{X/S},\iota_{\xi}]=\mbox{Lie}_{\xi},\;[d_{X/S},\mbox{Lie}_{\xi}]=0.

To conclude:

Lemma 2.1.1
.

The maps (
2.1.2
,
2.1.3
) define a morphism of differential graded Lie algebras

\Pi\mathcal{T}_{X/S}\rtimes\mathcal{T}_{X/S}\rightarrow D_{\Pi TX/S}^{\leq 1},

the differential on the right hand side being
[d_{X/S},.]
.

2.2.

Given the morphism
p:X\rightarrow S
, sect.
2.1
, consider a connection

(2.2.1)

\nabla:\mathcal{O}_{X/S}\rightarrow\Omega_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\mathcal{O}_{X/S}

such that

(2.2.2)

\nabla(fg)=f\nabla(g)+g\nabla(f),\nabla(h)=d_{S}h\;\forall f.g\in\mathcal{O}_{X/S},h\in\mathcal{O}_{S},

where
d_{S}h
is the differential of
h
. Requirements (
2.2.2
) say that, geometrically,
\nabla
is a rule that lifts a vector field on
S
to that on
X/S
. Such connections exist at least locally on
X
.

Given
\nabla
, we obtain its curvature

(2.2.3)

R=\frac{1}{2}[\nabla,\nabla].

Choosing, locally, a coordinate system,
\{u_{i},\partial_{u_{i}}\}
, on
S
, we obtain:

\nabla=du_{i}(\partial_{u_{i}}+\xi_{i}),\;R=\frac{1}{2}du_{i}du_{j}(\partial_{u_{i}}\xi_{j}-\partial_{u_{j}}\xi_{i}+[\xi_{i},\xi_{j}]),\;\xi_{i},\xi_{j}\in\mathcal{T}_{X/S}.

We will now extend the scalars from
\mathcal{O}_{S}
to the entire de Rham complex on
S
,
\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}
, thus replacing
\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S}
with

\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S}
. Just as given
\xi\in\mathcal{T}_{X/S}
,
\mbox{Lie}_{\xi}
defines a derivation of

\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S}
, the connection and curvature can be promoted to derivations of
\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S}
, to be denoted
\mbox{Lie}_{\nabla}
and
\mbox{Lie}_{R}
resp.; e.g. in local coordinates
\mbox{Lie}_{\nabla}=du_{i}(\partial_{u_{i}}+\mbox{Lie}_{\xi_{i}})
. The morphism
\iota
, see (
2.1.3
), affords a similar extension, and we obtain derivations such as
\iota_{R}
.

Consider now the operator, in fact a derivation,

D_{\nabla}=\mbox{Lie}_{\nabla}+d_{X/S}-\iota_{R}\in\mbox{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S})

Lemma 2.2.1
.

The pair
(\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S},D_{\nabla})
is a complex, i.e.,

D_{\nabla}^{2}=0.

Proof:

The result follows from the following computation:

\displaystyle 2D_{\nabla}^{2}=[D_{\nabla},D_{\nabla}]=

\displaystyle[\mbox{Lie}_{\nabla},\mbox{Lie}_{\nabla}]+[d_{X/S},d_{X/S}]+[\iota^{ch}_{R},\iota^{ch}_{R}]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2[\mbox{Lie}_{\nabla},d_{X/S}]-2[\mbox{Lie}_{\nabla},\iota_{R}]-2[d_{X/S},\iota_{R}]=

\displaystyle 2\mbox{Lie}_{R}-2\iota_{[\nabla,R]}-2\mbox{Lie}_{R}=0,

which consists in a repeated application of the ‘calculus’ of sect.
2.1
, that is, classical differential-geometric formulas (
2.1.4
,
2.1.6
); one exception is the term
\iota_{[\nabla,R]}
, which occurs thanks to
\iota_{[\nabla,R]}=[\mbox{Lie}_{\nabla},\iota_{R}]
(formula
(
2.1.4
)), and it vanishes thanks to the Bianchi identity
[\nabla,R]=0
.
∎

We will call the complex
(\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S},D_{\nabla})
the

local connection complex
, and denote it
CC(X/S,\nabla)
. It is an example of a
twisted complex
in the sense of Toledo - Tong, cf.
[
TT
]
.

The differential
D_{\nabla}
is not unique, as it depends on a choice of
\nabla
, but various choices give rise to canonically isomorphic complexes.

Lemma 2.2.2
.

The map

\exp{(\iota_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1}})}:(\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S},D_{\nabla_{1}})\rightarrow(\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S},D_{\nabla_{2}})

is an isomorphism of complexes.

Sketch of Proof:

It is clear that the operator
\iota_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1}}\in\mbox{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S})
is nilpotent
and is an even derivation of the algebra
\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S}
. Therefore it defines an automorphism of

\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S}
. What we need to check then is that

\exp{(ad_{\iota_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1},(0)}})}(D_{\nabla_{1}})=D_{\nabla_{2}},

where
ad_{\iota_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1}}}
means the operation of taking the bracket,
[\iota_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1}},.]
. A direct computation, using the various identities as in the proof of Lemma
2.2.2
, will show that

\displaystyle\exp{(ad_{\iota_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1}}})}(D_{\nabla_{1}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{\nabla_{1}}+[\iota_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1}},D_{\nabla_{1}}]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[\iota_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1}},[\iota_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1}},D_{\nabla_{1}}]]=D_{\nabla_{2}}.\;\qed

2.3.
Comparison with Katz-Oda.

The complex
(\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S},D_{\nabla})
is filtered,

F^{p}(\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S})=\Omega^{\geq p}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S}

and the first term of the corresponding spectral sequence

E_{1}:\ \Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes H^{\bullet}_{DR}(X/S,\nabla)\overset{\nabla_{GM}}{\longrightarrow}\Omega^{\bullet+1}_{S}\otimes H^{\bullet}_{DR}(X/S,\nabla)

obviously coincides with that of
\Omega^{\bullet}_{X}
introduced in
[
KO
]
, for any
\nabla
, thus reproducing the Katz-Oda construction
[
KO
]
of the Gauss-Manin connection. Cf. also
[
SV
]
.

2.4.
Globalization.

We will now show that Lemmas
2.2.1
and
2.2.2
imply the existence of a globally defined complex; such a complex is not unique, but different such complexes are canonically isomorphic.

The variety
S
being smooth, it can be covered by open subsets
S_{\alpha}
étale over
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, hence each having a coordinate system
\{u_{i},\partial_{u_{i}}\}
,
\partial_{u_{i}}(u_{j})=\delta_{ij}
. The morphism
p:X\rightarrow S
being smooth, the preimage
p^{-1}(S_{\alpha})
has an open cover
\{U_{\alpha\beta}\}
so that each
U_{\alpha\beta}
has a coordinate system
\{\{u_{i},x_{j},\partial_{u_{i}},\partial_{x_{j}}\}
. This means that over each
U_{\alpha\beta}
, the restriction

\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S}|_{U_{\alpha\beta}}
regarded as a sheaf over
S_{\alpha}
carries a connection,

\nabla_{\alpha\beta}
.

To streamline the notation: we have a sheaf
\Omega\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{def}}}{{=}}\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S}
on
X
and an open
cover
\{U_{i}\}
of
X
; on each restriction
\Omega_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{def}}}{{=}}\Omega|_{U_{i}}
we have chosen a connection
\nabla_{i}
hence a differential
D_{\nabla_{i}}:\Omega_{i}\rightarrow\Omega_{i}
, Lemma
2.2.1
. According to Lemma
2.2.2
, over the intersection
U_{i}\cap U_{j}
there is an
isomorphism

G_{ji}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{def}}}{{=}}\exp{(\iota_{\nabla_{j}-\nabla_{i}})}:(\Omega_{i},D_{\nabla_{i}})\rightarrow(\Omega_{j},D_{\nabla_{j}}).

It is clear that on triple intersections we have the consistency condition:

G_{ki}=G_{kj}\circ G_{ji}.

Therefore, having fixed a cover
\{U_{i}\}
and a bunch of connections
\{\nabla_{i}\}
, which we will denote simply by
\nabla
, we obtain a new globally defined complex,
\Omega_{\nabla}
, by tearing the original

\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S}
apart and regluing the pieces
\{(\Omega_{i},\nabla_{i})\}
using
\{G_{ij}\}
as transition functions. This proves the existence.

Two distinct such complexes,
\Omega_{\nabla}
and
\Omega_{\nabla^{\prime}}
(attached to distinct collections
\nabla
and
\nabla^{\prime}
) are canonically isomorphic. To see this, assume, as we can, that both are defined using the same open cover
\{U_{i}\}
. Then the collection of isomorphisms

\exp{(\iota_{\nabla^{\prime}_{i}}-\iota_{\nabla_{i}})}:\Omega_{\nabla}|_{U_{i}}\rightarrow\Omega_{\nabla^{\prime}}|_{U_{i}}

well defines an isomorphism of complexes
\Omega_{\nabla}\rightarrow\Omega_{\nabla^{\prime}}
.

We will call the resulting glued complex defined up to a canonical isomorphism the
global connection complex
, and denote it
CC(X/S)
; it gives rise to the Gauss - Manin connection on the relative cohomology.

3.
the relative chiral de rham complex

3.1.

Let
C
be a smooth algebraic curve over
\mathbb{C}
,
D_{C}
the algebra of differential operators on
C
,
\mbox{Mod}^{l}(D_{C})
,
\mbox{Mod}^{r}(D_{C})
the category of left (right, resp.)
D_{C}
-modules.

For a finite set
I
, we let
C^{I}
be the Cartesian product, and for a surjection
\pi^{(J/I)}:J\rightarrow I
we denote by
\Delta^{(J/I)}
the diagonal map
C^{I}\hookrightarrow C^{J}
; if
I
is a point, then we write
\Delta^{(J)}:C\hookrightarrow C^{J}
.

Given an
I
-family of
D_{C}
-modules,
\{M_{i}\}
, left or right, we have a
D_{C^{I}}
-module, left or right resp.,
\boxtimes_{I}M_{i}
, and if
\{M_{i}\}\subset\mbox{Mod}^{r}(D_{C})
we obtain

\Delta^{(J/I)}_{\ast}(\boxtimes_{I}M_{i})\in\mbox{Mod}^{r}(D_{C^{J}}),

where
\Delta^{(J/I)}_{*}
is the standard direct image functor:

\Delta^{(J/I)}_{\ast}(\boxtimes_{I}M_{i})=(\boxtimes_{I}M_{i})\otimes_{D_{C^{I}}}D_{C^{I}\rightarrow C^{J}}.

The
D_{C^{J}}
-module structure of
\Delta^{(J/I)}_{\ast}(\boxtimes_{I}M_{i})
becomes apparent locally where we can include
a local coordinate system on
C^{I}
into that on
C^{J}
. For example, if
C=\mathbb{C}
with coordinate
z
and, for simplicity,
I
is a point, then for any
j_{0}\in J

\Delta^{(J)}_{\ast}(M)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}M[\partial_{z_{j}},\;j\neq j_{0}],

and the action of
D_{C^{J}}
is determined by the following requirements:
\mathcal{O}_{C^{J}}
acts on
M
via the projection

\mathbb{C}[z_{j},j\in J]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[z]
,
z_{j}\mapsto z
, the action of
\partial_{j}
,
j\neq j_{0}
is obvious, and
\partial_{z_{j_{0}}}
acts as

\partial_{z}-\sum_{j\neq j_{0}}\partial_{z_{j}}
; of course, the action of
\partial_{z}
and
\mathbb{C}[z]
on
M
is the tautological one.

This means that
\Delta^{(J)}_{\ast}(M)
is the space of maps
\mathcal{O}_{C^{J}}\rightarrow\Delta^{(J)}_{\bullet}
that are differential operators in the directions transversal to the diagonal which suggests a convenient "functional" realization so popular in the vertex algebra theory, e.g.

(3.1.1)

\Delta^{(J)}_{\ast}(M)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}M[(z_{j}-z_{j_{0}})^{-1},\;j\neq j_{0}]\bigwedge_{j\neq j_{0}}\frac{dz_{j}}{z_{j}-z_{j_{0}}}.

The element of
\Delta^{(J)}_{\ast}(M)
that is represented by
m\otimes\prod_{j\neq j_{0}}\partial_{z_{j}}^{k_{j}}/k_{j}!
or, equivalently, by

m\otimes\bigwedge_{j\neq j_{0}}dz_{j}/(z_{j}-z_{j_{0}})^{-k_{j}-1}
maps

f\mapsto m\cdot\prod_{j\neq j_{0}}\partial_{z_{j}}^{k_{j}}/k_{j}!f|_{z_{j}=z_{j_{0}}}

Of course, there is nothing special in the choice of coordinates
\{z_{j}-z_{j_{0}},z_{j_{0}},j\neq j_{0}\}
. We can choose any system of generators of the diagonal ideal

\{h_{j}\}
,
j\in J^{\prime}
, which will define a coordinate system
\{h_{j},z_{j_{o}}\}
for any
j_{0}
, and obtain

\Delta^{(J)}_{\ast}(M)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}M[h_{j}^{-1},\;j\in J^{\prime}]\bigwedge_{j\in J^{\prime}}\frac{dh_{j}}{h_{j}}.

The function that an element
m\otimes\bigwedge_{j\in J^{\prime}}dh_{j}/h_{j}^{-k_{j}-1}
represents is as follows: restrict a function
f
to the formal neighborhood of the diagonal, write it as a power series in
h_{j}
,
j\in J^{\prime}
, with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}[z_{j_{0}}]
, then
m\otimes\bigwedge_{j\in J^{\prime}}dh_{j}/h_{j}^{-k_{j}-1}
will pick the coefficient of
\prod_{j\in J^{\prime}}h_{j}^{k_{j}}
applied to
m
.

3.2.

In the case of left
D_{C}
-modules, we will be interested not so much in a similar standard direct image functor as in the functor

\widehat{\Delta}^{(J/I)}_{*}
defined in
[
BD
]
, sect. 3.5,
to be the right adjoint to the usual inverse image functor
\Delta^{(J/I)*}
. One has

(3.2.1)

\Gamma(U,\widehat{\Delta}^{(J/I)}_{*}M)=\mbox{Hom}(\Delta^{(J/I)*}D_{U},M).

A receptacle for an OPE map is defined by localization:

\widetilde{\Delta}^{(J/I)}_{*}M=j_{*}^{(J/I)}\mathcal{O}_{U^{(J/I)}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{C^{J}}}\widehat{\Delta}^{(J/I)}_{*}M,

where
j^{(J/I)}:U^{(J/I)}\rightarrow C^{J}
is the complement to diagonals that contain
\Delta^{(J/I)}(C^{I})\subset C^{J}
.

If
C
is the line
\mathbb{C}
,
I=\{1,2,3,...,n\}
, then
M
can be replaced with the space of global sections and (
3.2.1
) suggests a realization

\widehat{\Delta}^{(I)}_{*}M\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}pr^{*}_{i_{0}}M[[(z_{i}-z_{i_{0}}),\;i\neq i_{0}]],

where
pr_{i_{0}}:C^{I}\rightarrow C
is the projection number
i_{0}
. Of course, there are
n
such realizations, one for each value of
i_{0}
, but they are pairwise canonically identified, as
D
-modules, by a formal application of the Taylor expansion:

(3.2.2)

m(z_{i_{0}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(z_{i_{0}}-z_{i_{1}})^{n}}{n!}\partial_{z_{i_{1}}}^{n}m(z_{i_{1}}),

which is nothing but Grothendieck’s way of defining a
D
-module structure.
Here
m(z_{i_{j}})
means not a function of
z_{i_{j}}
, but
m\in M
regarded as a section of
pr_{i_{j}}^{*}M
, the convention we will be using repeatedly.

By the same token, we can say that
\widetilde{\Delta}^{(I)}_{*}M
is an
I
-tuple of modules

pr^{*}_{i_{0}}M[[(z_{i}-z_{i_{0}}),\;i\neq i_{0}]][(z_{i}-z_{j})^{-1},\;i\neq j],\;i_{0}\in I,

along with pairwise identifications defined by (
3.2.2
).

3.3.

Beilinson and Drinfeld
[
BD
]
promote
\mbox{Mod}^{r}(D_{C})
to a pseudo-tensor category with the following spaces of *-operations:

P^{*}_{I}(\{M_{i}\},N)=Hom_{D_{C^{I}}}(\boxtimes_{I}M_{i},\Delta^{(I)}_{*}N).

Such operations can be composed: given a surjection
\pi:J\rightarrow I
, a
J
-family of objects
\{L_{j}\}
, an
I
-family of operations

\phi_{i}\in P^{*}_{J_{i}}(\{L_{j}\},M_{i})
, where
J_{i}=\pi^{-1}(i)
, and
\psi\in P^{*}_{I}(\{M_{i}\},N)
, the composition
\psi(\{\phi_{i}\})\in P^{*}_{J}(\{L_{j}\},N)
is defined to be the following composite map

\boxtimes_{J}L_{j}=\boxtimes_{I}\boxtimes_{J_{i}}L_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\boxtimes_{I}\phi_{i}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\boxtimes_{I}\Delta^{(J_{i})}_{*}M_{i}=\Delta^{(J/I)}_{*}(\boxtimes_{I}M_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta^{(J/I)}_{*}(\psi)}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Delta^{(J/I)}_{*}\circ\Delta^{(I)}_{*}N\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}\Delta^{(J)}_{*}N.

By definition,
[
BD
]
, a Lie* algebra is a Lie algebra object in this pseudo-tensor category; that is to say, a Lie* algebra is a pair of a
D_{C}
-module
L
and an operation
[.,.]\in P^{*}_{2}(\{L,L\},L)
, a Lie* bracket, which is anticommutative and satisfies the Jacobi identity. Let us now see what this means.

3.4.

We will be exclusively working in the situation where
C=\mathbb{C}
and all the structure elements are translation-invariant. This means that a left
D_{\mathbb{C}}
-module is of the form
V\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}}
for some vector space
V
, the
D_{\mathbb{C}}
-module structure is determined by a linear map
\partial:V\rightarrow V
so that
\partial_{z}(v\otimes f(z))=\partial v\otimes f(z)+v\otimes f(z)^{\prime}
. Any right
D_{\mathbb{C}}
-module is
V\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\Omega_{\mathbb{C}}

for some left
D_{\mathbb{C}}
-module
V
as above with action
(v\otimes f(z)\,dz)\partial_{z}=-\partial v\otimes f(z)\,dz-v\otimes f(z)^{\prime}\,dz
. We will write
v\partial
for
-\partial v
.

Similarly, being an
\mathcal{O}_{C\times C}
-morphism, a binary operation

[.,.]:\;V\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\Omega_{\mathbb{C}}\boxtimes V\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\Omega_{\mathbb{C}}\rightarrow\Delta^{(\{1,2\})}_{*}(V\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\Omega_{\mathbb{C}})

is determined by its restriction to the fiber
Vdz_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}Vdz_{2}
, and its translation-invariance means this restriction has the form

[adz_{1},bdz_{2}]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{(n)}b(z_{2})(z_{1}-z_{2})^{-n-1}dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2},\;

where
a_{(n)}b\in V
,
a_{(n)}b=0
for all
n
large enough; we are using the functional realization (
3.1.1
). Hence
[.,.]
is determined by an infinite family of products
{}_{(n)}:V\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}V\rightarrow V
.

Being a
D_{C\times C}
-morphism means the following 2 equalities of elements of
\mbox{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)
:

(3.4.1)

(\partial a)_{(n)}=[\partial,a_{(n)}]=-na_{(n-1)}.

Let us spell out the anticommutativity and the Jacobi identity in this setting.

Anticommutativity:

If

[a(z_{1})dz_{1},b(z_{2})dz_{2}]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{(n)}b(z_{2})(z_{1}-z_{2})^{-n-1}dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2},\;

then

\displaystyle[b(z_{2})dz_{2},a(z_{1})dz_{1}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{(n)}a(z_{1})(z_{2}-z_{1})^{-n-1}dz_{2}\wedge dz_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{j}}{j!}\partial^{j}(b_{(n)}a)(z_{2})(z_{2}-z_{1})^{-n-1}dz_{2}\wedge dz_{1},

(the last equality is the result of an application of (
3.2.2
).)
Comparing the coefficients in front of
(z_{1}-z_{2})^{-n-1}
we see that

\displaystyle[a(z_{1})dz_{1},b(z_{2})dz_{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-[b(z_{2})dz_{2},a(z_{1})dz_{1}]\Leftrightarrow

(3.4.2)

\displaystyle a_{(n)}b

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n+1}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\partial^{j}(b_{(n+j)}a)\mbox{ for all }n,

the identity long known in vertex algebra theory as
skew-commutativity.

Jacobi:

The definition of the composition of *-operations, sect.
3.3
, gives

\displaystyle[a(z_{1})dz_{1},[b(z_{2})dz_{2},c(z_{3})dz_{3}]]=[a(z_{1})dz_{1},\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}b_{(m)}c(z_{3})(z_{2}-z_{3})^{-m-1}dz_{2}\wedge dz_{3}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}a_{(n)}(b_{(m)}c)(z_{3})(z_{1}-z_{3})^{-n-1}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{-m-1}dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}\wedge dz_{3};

similarly,

[b(z_{2})dz_{2},[a(z_{1})dz_{1},c(z_{3})dz_{3}]]=\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}b_{(m)}(a_{(n)}c)(z_{3})(z_{1}-z_{3})^{-n-1}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{-m-1}dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}\wedge dz_{3};

and

[[a(z_{1})dz_{1},b(z_{2})dz_{2}],c(z_{3})dz_{3}]=\sum_{s,t=0}^{\infty}(a_{(s)}b)_{(t)}c(z_{3})(z_{1}-z_{2})^{-s-1}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{-t-1}dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}\wedge dz_{3}.

We will show that the Jacobi identity,

[a(z_{1})dz_{1},[b(z_{2})dz_{2},c(z_{3})dz_{3}]]-[b(z_{2})dz_{2},[a(z_{1})dz_{1},c(z_{3})dz_{3}]]=[[a(z_{1})dz_{1},b(z_{2})dz_{2}],c(z_{3})dz_{3}],

amounts to the well-known
Borcherds commutator formula
:

(3.4.3)

[a_{(n)},b_{(m)}]=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}{n\choose s}(a_{(s)}b)_{(n+m-s)};

in fact, we will do this in two different ways.

Guided by sect.
3.1
, we need to show that (
3.4.3
) is equivalent to the corresponding differential operators being equal to each other.
One way
to do this is to choose an arbitrary test function, say
(z_{1}-z_{3})^{n}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{m}
, expand it in powers of the chosen coordinate system,
\{(z_{1}-z_{3}),(z_{2}-z_{3}),z_{3}\}
for the first two expression and
\{(z_{1}-z_{2}),(z_{2}-z_{3}),z_{3}\}
for the third one, and then take the residues. We obtain:

[a(z_{1})dz_{1},[b(z_{2})dz_{2},c(z_{3})dz_{3}]]
sends
(z_{1}-z_{3})^{n}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{m}
to
a_{(n)}(b_{(m)}c)
;

[b(z_{2})dz_{2},[a(z_{1})dz_{1},c(z_{3})dz_{3}]]
sends
(z_{1}-z_{3})^{n}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{m}
to
b_{(m)}(a_{(n)}c)
; and

[[a(z_{1})dz_{1},b(z_{2})dz_{2}],c(z_{3})dz_{3}]
sends
(z_{1}-z_{3})^{n}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{m}
to
\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}{n\choose s}(a_{(s)}b)_{(n+m-s)}
; this is because we have to re-expand as follows:

(z_{1}-z_{3})^{n}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{m}=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}{n\choose s}(z_{1}-z_{2})^{s}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{n+m-s}.

That the 1st value minus the 2nd equals the 3rd for all
m,n
is the content of (
3.4.3
).

Allternatively,
and more straightforwardly, let us compare the differential operators themselves. As explained in the end of sect.
3.1
, this amounts to choosing the coordinate systems, the same we have just chosen, and the corresponding dual bases of the tangent sheaf; these are
\{\partial_{z_{1}},\partial_{z_{2}},\partial_{z_{1}}+\partial_{z_{2}}+\partial_{z_{3}}\}
for the first two expressions and
\{\partial_{z_{1}},\partial_{z_{1}}+\partial_{z_{2}},\partial_{z_{1}}+\partial_{z_{2}}+\partial_{z_{3}}\}
for the third.

We obtain

[a(z_{1})dz_{1},[b(z_{2})dz_{2},c(z_{3})dz_{3}]]=\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}a_{(n)}(b_{(m)}c)(z_{3})dz_{3}\otimes\frac{\partial_{z_{1}}^{n}}{n!}\frac{\partial_{z_{2}}^{m}}{m!},

[b(z_{2})dz_{2},[a(z_{1})dz_{1},c(z_{3})dz_{3}]]=\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}b_{(m)}(a_{(n)}c)(z_{3})dz_{3}\otimes\frac{\partial_{z_{1}}^{n}}{n!}\frac{\partial_{z_{2}}^{m}}{m!},

and

[[a(z_{1})dz_{1},b(z_{2})dz_{2}],c(z_{3})dz_{3}]=\sum_{s,t=0}^{\infty}(a_{(s)}b)_{(t)}c(z_{3})dz_{3}\otimes\frac{\partial_{z_{1}}^{s}}{s!}\frac{(\partial_{z_{1}}+\partial_{z_{2}})^{t}}{t!}.

Comparing coefficients in front of
\partial_{z_{1}}^{n}\partial_{z_{2}}^{m}/n!m!
we again obtain (
3.4.3
).
∎

Remark.

A curious reader might enjoy seeing yet another, a third, proof of (

). One needs to take the third term of the Jacobi identity,

\displaystyle\{\partial^{(n)}_{x_{i}}(z_{1})dz_{1},x^{(m)}_{j}(z_{2})dz_{2}\}

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}{n+m\choose n}\delta_{ij}\frac{dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{n+m+1}},

\displaystyle\{\partial_{dx_{i}}^{(n)}(z_{1})dz_{1},dx^{(m)}_{j}(z_{2})dz_{2}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}{n+m\choose n}\delta_{ij}\frac{dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{n+m+1}},

and more generally, as a repeated application of the Leibniz identity (

\displaystyle\{P(z_{1})dz_{1},Q(z_{2})dz_{2}\}=

In the superversion of the above formula

and

3.9.

The concept of a coisson algebra
V
is a quasiclassical limit of that of a chiral algebra, a.k.a. a vertex algebra in the translation-invariant situation. By definition,
[
BD
]
,
a chiral algebra is a Lie algebra object of
\mbox{Mod}^{r}(D_{C})
with a pseudo-tensor structure defined by means of the spaces of chiral operations,

P^{ch}_{I}(\{M_{i}\},N)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{def}}}{{=}}\mbox{Hom}_{D_{C^{I}}}(j_{*}^{(I)}j^{(I)*}(\boxtimes_{I}M_{i}),\Delta^{(I)}_{*}N),

where
j^{(I)}:U^{(I)}\hookrightarrow C^{I}
is the embedding of the open subset
U^{(I)}=\{(z_{i})\mbox{ s.t. }z_{i_{1}}\neq z_{i_{2}}\mbox{ if }i_{1}\neq i_{2}\}
, cf. sect.
3.3
; it also contains a unit,
\Omega_{C}\rightarrow V
, whose definition we will skip.

A chiral bracket on
M\in\mbox{Mod}^{r}(D_{C})
is then a mophism

[.,.]:j_{*}^{(2)}j^{(2)*}M\boxtimes M\rightarrow\Delta^{(2)}_{*}M,

which is antisymmetric and satisfies the Jacobi identity.

Computationally more convenient than a chiral bracket is the concept of an operator product expansion, OPE for short. It is easy to understand,
[
BD
]
, 3.5, that
a morphism
j_{*}^{(2)}j^{(2)*}M\boxtimes M\rightarrow\Delta^{(2)}_{*}M

is the same thing as a morphism of left
D_{C^{2}}
-modules

\circ:M^{l}\boxtimes M^{l}\rightarrow\widetilde{\Delta}^{(2)}_{*}M^{l},

where
M^{l}=M\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{C}}\Omega_{C}^{-1}
and
\widetilde{\Delta}^{(2)}_{*}M^{l}
is the
D_{C^{2}}
-module that was defined in sect.
3.1
.

In the translation-invariant setting, where
M=V\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\Omega_{\mathbb{C}}
,
M^{l}=V\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}}
, the OPE is determined by its restriction to the fiber:

(3.9.1)

pr_{1}^{*}V\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}pr_{2}^{*}V\ni a\otimes b\mapsto a(z_{1})\circ b(z_{2})=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{(n)}b(z_{2})(z_{1}-z_{2})^{-n-1}\in V((z_{1}-z_{2})),

and the
\mathbb{C}[\partial_{z_{1}},\partial_{z_{2}}]
-linearity is equivalent to the familiar identity (
3.4.1
), except now it is valid for all
n
, positive and negative:

(3.9.2)

(\partial a)_{(n)}=[\partial,a_{(n)}]=-na_{(n-1)},\;n\in\mathbb{Z}.

The corresponding chiral bracket is as follows

(3.9.3)

\displaystyle\frac{adz_{1}\otimes bdz_{2}}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{M}}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\sum_{n=-M}^{\infty}a_{(n)}b(z_{2})(z_{1}-z_{2})^{-n-1-M}dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle V((z_{1}-z_{2}))dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}\mbox{ mod }V[[z_{1}-z_{2}]]dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}.,

which, by the way is an illustration of how the OPE point of view is computationally convenient.

The anticommutativity and the Jacobi identity of a chiral bracket are equivalent to the commutativity and associativity of the OPE
\circ
,
[
BD
]
, 3.5.10.

In terms of products
(n)
, commutativity of
\circ
, equivalently anticommutativity of
[.,.]
, means the familiar identity (
3.4.2
), except now it is valid for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, not necessarily positive. The Jacobi identity for
[.,.]
is not easy to write down explicitly because of the poles, harder at least than the computation in sect.
3.4
. Even defining the concept of associativity for
\circ
requires an effort. We have

\displaystyle a(z_{1})\circ(b(z_{2})\circ c(z_{3}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n}a_{(n)}(b_{(m)}c)(z_{3})(z_{1}-z_{3})^{-n-1}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{-m-1}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle V((z_{2}-z_{3}))((z_{1}-z_{3})),

\displaystyle(a(z_{1})\circ b(z_{2}))\circ c(z_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s,t}((a_{(s)}b)_{(t)}c)(z_{3})(z_{1}-z_{2})^{-s-1}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{-t-1}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle V((z_{1}-z_{2}))((z_{2}-z_{3})),

two expansions which belong to different spaces. The OPE
\circ
is called
associative
if they are the Laurent series expansions, in their respective domains, of one and the same element of the localization
V[[z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}]][(z_{1}-z_{2})^{-1},(z_{1}-z_{3})^{-1},(z_{2}-z_{3})^{-1}]
. For example, the associativity demands that in order to obtain
a(z_{1})\circ(b(z_{2})\circ c(z_{3}))
, one has to expand
1/(z_{1}-z_{2})
as this:

\frac{1}{z_{1}-z_{2}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(z_{2}-z_{3})^{n}}{(z_{1}-z_{3})^{n+1}}.

None of this is very explicit. One obtains an explicit formula – the celebrated Borcherds identity – if one passes to the de Rham cohomology or, equivalently, integrates. This can be done even at the level of a Lie* algebra, the point where we start; we will return to the chiral algebra case in sect.
3.11
.

3.10.

If
M
is a right
D_{C}
-module, then one considers its de Rham cohomology
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{dR}(C,M)=M/M\mathcal{T}_{C}
.

Given a morphism
M\boxtimes M\rightarrow\Delta^{(2)}_{*}M
, by taking the de Rham cohomology along the 1st copy of
C
, one obtains a map

\mathcal{H}^{1}_{dR}(C,M)\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}M\rightarrow M,

and by taking the de Rham cohomology over
C\times C
one obtains a map

\mathcal{H}^{1}_{dR}(C,M)\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{H}^{1}_{dR}(C,M)\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{1}_{dR}(C,M).

If
M
is a Lie* algebra (in particular, a chiral algebra), then the latter map is a Lie algebra bracket and the former an action of (the sheaf of Lie algebras)

\mathcal{H}^{1}_{dR}(C,M)
on
M
.

For example, in the translation-invariant setting the answer for the space of sections of
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{dR}(C,V((z))dz)
over a formal disc is as follows

\mbox{Lie}(V)=V((z))dz/\langle(mz^{m-1}a+z^{m}\partial a)dz,\;a\in V,m\in\mathbb{Z}\rangle

The chiral bracket formula (
3.9.3
) shows that the action map is as follows

(3.10.1)

\displaystyle\mbox{Lie}(V)\otimes V((z))dz

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle V((z))dz

\displaystyle\overline{f(z_{1})a(z_{1})dz_{1}}\otimes g(z_{2})b(z_{2})dz_{2}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}f^{(j)}(z_{2})g(z_{2})(a_{(j)}b)(z_{2})dz_{2}.

(Indeed, by construction we have to write

f(z_{1})a(z_{1})dz_{1}\otimes g(z_{2})b(z_{2})dz_{2}\mapsto\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{(j)}b(z_{2})\frac{f(z_{1})g(z_{2})}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{j+1}}dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}

and then take the residue
\mbox{Res}_{z_{1}=z_{2}}f(z_{1})dz_{1}/(z_{1}-z_{2})^{j+1}
.)

Further integrating w.r.t.
z_{2}
we obtain the Lie bracket formula

(3.10.2)

\displaystyle\mbox{Lie}(V)\otimes\mbox{Lie}V

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\mbox{Lie}(V)

\displaystyle\overline{f(z_{1})a(z_{1})dz_{1}}\otimes\overline{g(z_{2})b(z_{2})dz_{2}}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\overline{f^{(j)}(z_{2})g(z_{2})(a_{(j)}b)(z_{2})dz_{2}}.

To see what this really means denote by
v_{(n)}=\overline{z^{n}vdz}
for any
v\in V
. The fact that (
3.10.2
) defines a Lie algebra structure and (
3.10.1
) this Lie algebra action means the following identity

(3.10.3)

[a_{(n)},b_{(m)}]c=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}{n\choose s}(a_{(s)}b)_{(n+m-s)}c,\;\forall a,b,c\in V,

which is familiar to us and was proved in sect.
3.4
only if
m,n\geq 0
from a slightly different perspective.

In the case of the coisson algebra

, which was defined in sect.

,

,

all the other feasible pairwise brackets being 0. This follows from (
3.10.2
) applied to the formulas recorded in sect.
3.7

In the case of

3.11.

An obvious embedding
M\boxtimes M\hookrightarrow j_{*}^{(2)}j^{(2)*}M\boxtimes M
means that a chiral bracket

j_{*}^{(2)}j^{(2)*}M\boxtimes M\rightarrow\Delta^{(2)}_{*}M
determines a Lie* bracket
M\boxtimes M\rightarrow\Delta^{(2)}_{*}M
, by restriction. Each chiral algebra is therefore a Lie* algebra, and so formulas such as (
3.10.1
–
3.10.3
) apply, in particular, to a chiral algebra. We will now write down an identity, which generalizes (
3.10.3
) and follows from a full-fledged chiral Jacobi identity.

We let

[.,.]\in P^{ch}_{2}(\{V\otimes\Omega_{C},V\otimes\Omega_{C}\},V\otimes\Omega_{C})
be a chiral bracket, consider the composition
[.,[.,.]]
, evaluate it on

a(z_{1})\otimes b(z_{2})\otimes c(z_{3})(z_{1}-z_{3})^{n}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{m}(z_{1}-z_{2})^{l})
, and then ‘‘Jacobiate’’ it. Denote the result by

Jac(a(z_{1})\otimes b(z_{2})\otimes c(z_{3})(z_{1}-z_{3})^{n}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{m}(z_{1}-z_{2})^{l})
. The Jacobi identity says

Jac(a(z_{1})\otimes b(z_{2})\otimes c(z_{3})(z_{1}-z_{3})^{n}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{m}(z_{1}-z_{2})^{l})=0.

Writing this down as it is is an onerous task, similar to but harder than the computation that proved (
3.4.3
). We will restrict ourselves to this equality written down modulo
\partial_{z_{1}},\partial_{z_{2}}
, that is,

Jac(a(z_{1})\otimes b(z_{2})\otimes c(z_{3})(z_{1}-z_{3})^{n}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{m}(z_{1}-z_{2})^{l})\equiv 0\mbox{ mod }\partial_{z_{1}},\partial_{z_{2}}.

By definition, this is a statement to the effect that a certain element of
V\otimes\Omega_{C}
(in fact of
V
, in translation-invariant setting) that depends on
a,b,c\in V
and
n,m,l\in\mathbb{Z}
is 0. It is easiest to organize the computation of this element as a repeated integration. Using the
[a,[b,c]]-[b,[a,c]]=[[a,b],c]
format of the Jacobiator the desired identity becomes, in the notation of (
3.9.1
,
3.9.3
)

\displaystyle\iint_{|z_{1}-z_{3}|>|z_{2}-z_{3}|}dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}((z_{1}-z_{3})^{n}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{m}(z_{1}-z_{2})^{-l})a(z_{1})\circ(b(z_{2})\circ c(z_{3}))

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\iint_{|z_{2}-z_{3}|>|z_{1}-z_{3}|}dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}((z_{1}-z_{3})^{n}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{m}(z_{1}-z_{2})^{-l})b(z_{2})\circ(a(z_{1})\circ c(z_{3}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint_{|z_{2}-z_{3}|>|z_{1}-z_{2}|}dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}((z_{1}-z_{3})^{n}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{m}(z_{1}-z_{2})^{-l})(a(z_{1})\circ(b(z_{2}))\circ c(z_{3}).

In this formula, the expression under the integration sign indicates the choice of the variables and of the Laurent series expansion; e.g. in the 1st integral the variables are
z_{1}-z_{3},z_{2}-z_{3}
and

\frac{1}{z_{1}-z_{2}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(z_{2}-z_{3})^{j}}{(z_{1}-z_{3})^{j+1}};

in the 2nd, the variables are
z_{1}-z_{3},z_{2}-z_{3}
, but

\frac{1}{z_{1}-z_{2}}=-\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(z_{1}-z_{3})^{j}}{(z_{2}-z_{3})^{j+1}};

and in the last, the variables are
z_{1}-z_{2},z_{2}-z_{3}
and

\frac{1}{z_{1}-z_{3}}=-\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{j}}{(z_{2}-z_{3})^{j+1}}.

Integration means computing the residue, that is, the coefficient of
dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}/(z_{1}-z_{3})(z_{2}-z_{3})
for the first two terms, and of

dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}/(z_{1}-z_{2})(z_{2}-z_{3})

It is convenient at this point to do what is usually done – introduce a field:

(3.11.1)

V\ni a\mapsto Y(a,z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{(m)}z^{-m-1}\in\mbox{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)((z,z^{-1}))

and rewrite the last identity as follows:

\displaystyle\iint_{|z_{1}-z_{3}|>|z_{2}-z_{3}|}dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}((z_{1}-z_{3})^{n}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{m}(z_{1}-z_{2})^{-l})Y(a,z_{1}-z_{3})Y(b,z_{2}-z_{3})c)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\iint_{|z_{2}-z_{3}|>|z_{1}-z_{3}|}dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}((z_{1}-z_{3})^{n}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{m}(z_{1}-z_{2})^{-l})Y(b,z_{2}-z_{3})Y(a,z_{1}-z_{3})c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint_{|z_{2}-z_{3}|>|z_{1}-z_{2}|}dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2}((z_{1}-z_{3})^{n}(z_{2}-z_{3})^{m}(z_{1}-z_{2})^{-l})Y(Y(a,z_{1}-z_{2})b,z_{2}-z_{3})c).

The case
l=0
of this identity gives the

formula:

(3.11.2)

[a_{(n)},b_{(m)}]c=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}{n\choose s}(a_{(s)}b)_{(n+m-s)}c,\forall m,n\in\mathbb{Z},

familiar to us, (

where we let

\displaystyle Y(a,z)_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}a_{(-m-1)}z^{m},\;Y(a,z)_{-}=\sum_{m=-1}^{-\infty}a_{(-m-1)}z^{m},

\displaystyle Y(a,z)_{+}Y(b,z)+Y(b,z)Y(a,z)_{-}.

For example, (

The case where

Thanks to Corollary
4.1.3
, we know how to lift these to an operator acting on
\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{ch}(X/S)
: we define

\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\nabla}=du_{i}(\partial_{u_{i}}+\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\xi_{i}})\mbox{ and }

\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\nabla,(0)}=du_{i}(\partial_{u_{i}}+\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\xi_{i},(0)});

\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{R}=\frac{1}{2}du_{i}du_{j}(\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\partial_{u_{i}}\xi_{j}}-\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\partial_{u_{j}}\xi_{i}}+\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{[\xi_{i},\xi_{j}]})\mbox{ and }

\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{R,(0)}=\frac{1}{2}du_{i}du_{j}(\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\partial_{u_{i}}\xi_{j,(0)}}-\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\partial_{u_{j}}\xi_{i,(0)}}+\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{[\xi_{i},\xi_{j}],(0)}),

and note that thanks to Lemma
4.1.3
, definition (
4.2.1
) implies

(4.2.2)

\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{R,(0)}=\frac{1}{2}[\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\nabla,(0)},\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\nabla,(0)}].

Consider
D^{ch}_{\nabla}\in\mbox{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{ch}(X/S))
defined by

D^{ch}_{\nabla}=\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\nabla,(0)}+d_{X/S,(0)}-\iota^{ch}_{R,(0)}.

Lemma 4.2.1
.

(cf. Lemma
2.2.1
.) The pair
(\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{ch}(X/S),D_{\nabla})
is a complex, that is,

(D^{ch}_{\nabla})^{2}=0.

Proof:

\displaystyle 2(D^{ch}_{\nabla})^{2}=[D^{ch}_{\nabla},D^{ch}_{\nabla}]=

\displaystyle[\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\nabla,(0)},\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\nabla,(0)}]+[d_{X/S,(0)},d_{X/S,(0)}]+[\iota^{ch}_{R,(0)},\iota^{ch}_{R,(0)}]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2[\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\nabla,(0)},d_{X/S,(0)}]-2[\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\nabla,(0)},\iota^{ch}_{R,(0)}]-2[d_{X/S,(0)},\iota^{ch}_{R,(0)}]=

\displaystyle 2\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{R,(0)}-2\iota^{ch}_{[\nabla,R],(0)}-2\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{R,(0)}=0.

A quick guide to this computation: in the last line, the 1st occurrence of
\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{R,(0)}
is via
\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{R,(0)}=[\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\nabla,(0)},\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\nabla,(0)}]
, which is (
4.2.2
), the second is via

\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{R,(0)}=[d_{X/S,(0)},\iota^{ch}_{R,(0)}]
, which is a consequence of (
4.1.6
); finally,
\iota^{ch}_{[\nabla,R],(0)}
appears because it equals
[\mbox{Lie}^{ch}_{\nabla,(0)},\iota^{ch}_{R,(0)}]
according to Lemma
4.1.3
and vanishes because
[\nabla,R]=0
, which is the Bianchi identity. The vanishing of the rest of the summands is more or less clear.
∎

We will call the complex
(\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{ch}(X/S),D_{\nabla})
the
local chiral
connection complex
and denote it
CC^{ch}(X/S,\nabla)
.

The choice of
\nabla
is arbitrary, but different such complexes are canonically isomorphic.

Lemma 4.2.2
.

(cf. Lemma
2.2.2
.)

The map

\exp{(\iota^{ch}_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1},(0)})}:(\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{ch}(X/S),D_{\nabla_{1}})\rightarrow(\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{ch}(X/S),D_{\nabla_{2}})

is an isomorphism of complexes.

Sketch of Proof:

It is clear that the operator
\iota^{ch}_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1},(0)}\in\mbox{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{ch}(X/S))
is nilpotent
and, as we have once mentioned, a derivation of the chiral bracket; hence it defines an automorphism of the vertex algebra.
What we need to check then is that

\exp{(ad_{\iota^{ch}_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1},(0)}})}(D^{ch}_{\nabla_{1}})=D^{ch}_{\nabla_{2}},

where
ad_{\iota^{ch}_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1},(0)}}
means the operation of taking the bracket,
[\iota^{ch}_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1},(0)},.]
. A direct but little appetizing computation, using the various identities as in the proof of Lemma
4.2.2
, will show that

\displaystyle\exp{(ad_{\iota^{ch}_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1},(0)}})}(D^{ch}_{\nabla_{1}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D^{ch}_{\nabla_{1}}+[\iota^{ch}_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1},(0)},D^{ch}_{\nabla_{1}}]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[\iota^{ch}_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1},(0)},[\iota^{ch}_{\nabla_{2}-\nabla_{1},(0)},D^{ch}_{\nabla_{1}}]]=D^{ch}_{\nabla_{2}}.

4.3.
Globalization.

The above made computations have a number of consequences.

(1) Corollary
4.1.3
says that the natural action of the Lie algebra of vector fields on the ordinary de Rham complex lifts to that on the chiral de Rham complex
\Omega^{ch}(X/S)
if
X/S
is sufficiently ‘‘small’’ (that is, allows a global coordinate system.) A standard argument
that goes under the Gelfand-Kazhdan formal geometry moniker implies that for each smooth
X/S
over
S
there is a (sheaf of) differential graded vertex algebra
\Omega^{ch}_{X/S}
, along with an embedding of a commutative differential algebra

\Omega^{\bullet}_{X/S}\hookrightarrow\Omega^{ch}_{X/S}
. The assignment
X/S\mapsto\Omega^{ch}_{X/S}
is functorial in
X/S
. All of this was originally proved
[
MSV
]
by explicitly writing down the universal transition functions that define
\Omega^{ch}_{X/S}
( only in the absolute setting which, however, hardly makes any difference.) The crucial formula (
4.1.3
) appeared in
[
MS
]
.

(2) Furthermore, we have, keeping to our assumptions on
X/S
,

Theorem 4.3.1
.

There is a sheaf of differential vertex algebras on
X/S
that is locally isomorphic to the one defined in Lemma
4.2.1
with transition functions as in Lemma
4.2.2
. Different such complexes are canonically isomorphic.

Proof
is a repetition, practically verbatim, of the argument from sect.
2.4
. We pick an open cover
\{U_{i}\}
as in sect.
2.4
and choose a connection
\nabla_{i}
over each
p(U_{i})\subset S
. By Lemma
4.2.1
, this gives us a complex

((\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{ch}_{X/S})|_{U_{i}},D_{\nabla_{i}})
. Let us for the sake of brevity re-denote

\Omega^{ch}_{i}=(\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{ch}_{X/S})|_{U_{i}}.

On the intersections, we have isomorphisms, Lemma
4.2.2
,

G_{ji}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{def}}}{{=}}\exp{(\iota^{ch}_{\nabla_{j}-\nabla_{i},(0)})}:\Omega^{ch}_{i}|_{U_{i}\cap U_{j}}\rightarrow\Omega^{ch}_{j}|_{U_{i}\cap U_{j}}.

It is clear that

G_{ki}=G_{kj}\circ G_{ji}.

Therefore, having fixed a cover
\{U_{i}\}
and connections
\{\nabla_{i}\}
, we obtain a new complex,
\Omega^{ch}_{\nabla}
, by tearing the original

\Omega^{\bullet}_{S}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{S}}\Omega^{ch}_{X/S}
apart and re-gluing the pieces
\{(\Omega_{i},\nabla_{i})\}
using
\{G_{ij}\}
as transition functions.

Two distinct such complexes,
\Omega^{ch}_{\nabla}
and
\Omega^{ch}_{\nabla^{\prime}}
, are canonically isomorphic. To see this, assume, as we can, that both are defined using the same open cover
\{U_{i}\}
. Then the collection of isomorphisms

\exp{(\iota^{ch}_{\nabla^{\prime}_{i}-\nabla_{i},(0)})}:\Omega^{ch}_{\nabla}|_{U_{i}}\rightarrow\Omega^{ch}_{\nabla^{\prime}}|_{U_{i}}

well defines an isomorphism of complexes
\Omega^{ch}_{\nabla}\rightarrow\Omega^{ch}_{\nabla^{\prime}}
.
∎

We will call the sheaf of differential vertex algebras from the above theorem the
global chiral connection complex
and denote it
CC^{ch}(X/S)
.
