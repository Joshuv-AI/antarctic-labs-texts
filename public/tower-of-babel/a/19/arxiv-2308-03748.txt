The Erdős distinct subset sums problem, considered one of the oldest conjectures of Erdős

by considering the sum of the squares of the elements
[

. Erdős has posed this question multiple times and even offered monetary prize for its proof or disproof

]
.
The lower bound on the largest element

has subsequently been improved by a constant factor

]
.
The current best known lower bound
\left(\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}-o(1)\right)\frac{2^{n}}{\sqrt{n}}
is due to Dubroff, Fox and Xu
[
7
]
; an alternative proof is given by Steinerberger
[
16
]
.
In the other direction, starting with a construction by Conway and Guy
[
6
]
, where they produced a construction with

, better constructions have been obtained

[
14
,
3
,

]
.
The best construction so far is the one by Bohman
[

]
, where he produced a construction with

Here he studied variations of the Conway-Guy sequence. These sequences are derived from differences between an element and the smaller ones in an increasing sequence
(u_{n})_{n}
which is exponential:
S=\{u_{n}-u_{1},u_{n}-u_{2},\ldots,u_{n}-u_{n-1}\}.

As such, most of the elements in the set have roughly the same magnitude.
This implies that two subsets of different sizes have a different subset-sum in most cases.
For two subsets with the same size, it is sufficient to have a different subset-sum modulo

.

Motivated by this, we consider the Erdős distinct subset sums problem in a modular setting.
An

A set

The asymptotic modular Erdős distinct subset sums conjecture

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let us define the sumset generating function

g_{A}(x)=\prod_{i}(1+x^{a_{i}})

and let
\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{t}\}=\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{N\mathbb{Z}}\setminus S(A)
be the set of
t
numbers that cannot be expressed as the sum of a subset of
A
. Then we have

g_{A}(\omega_{N})+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\omega_{N}^{s_{i}}=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\omega_{N}^{i}=0.

Hence, by the triangle inequality, we have

t\geq\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{t}\omega_{N}^{s_{i}}\right\rvert=\left\lvert g_{A}(\omega_{N})\right\rvert=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert 1+\omega_{N}^{a_{i}}\right\rvert=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{2+2\cos\left(\frac{a_{i}}{N}\cdot 2\pi\right)}.

Now, if
\max{A}<(1-\varepsilon)\frac{N}{3}
, then using
\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{3}+\frac{2\pi}{3}x\right)<\frac{1}{2}-x-\frac{x^{2}}{2}
for
0<x<1
, we have for every
i\in[n]
that

\displaystyle\sqrt{2+2\cos\left(\frac{a_{i}}{N}\cdot 2\pi\right)}

\displaystyle>\sqrt{2+2\cos\left(\frac{1-\varepsilon}{3}\cdot 2\pi\right)}=\sqrt{2-2\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{3}+\frac{2\varepsilon\pi}{3}\right)}>1+\varepsilon.

This would imply
t>(1+\varepsilon)^{n}
, contradicting the assumption.

We now construct the sets showing that the constant
c=\frac{1}{3}
cannot be improved.

Let
n\gg k\gg 1
be two integers, and consider
N=2^{n}+2^{k}+1
such that
3\mid(N-1)
, and so
3\mid 2^{n-k}+1
.
Let

, where

\displaystyle a_{i}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &2^{i-1},&1\leq i\leq n-k,\\
&2^{i-1-(n-k)}(2^{n-k}+1),&n-k<i\leq n.\end{aligned}\right.

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{i}\leq a_{j+1}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}<N
,
B
is sumset-distinct module
N
.
Let
\lambda=\frac{N-1}{3}
, then
\gcd(\lambda,N)=\gcd(N-1,N)=1
. By Lemma

, we know that
\lambda B
is also sumset-distinct modulo
N
.
Let
A
be the set obtained from

by restricting all elements to

\mathrm{res}(x)=\min\{x\pmod{N},N-x\pmod{N}\}
.
Since

, it is not hard to see that for

, either
\lambda a_{i}<\frac{N}{3}+\frac{N}{2^{k}}\pmod{N}

If

.
Thus, we conclude that

.
∎

We first prove the “only if” direction for

.

Proof for
N=2^{n}+1
.

Let us fix one element
\lambda\in A
and consider
A^{\prime}:=\lambda^{-1}A=\{\lambda^{-1}a\mid a\in A\},

where
\lambda^{-1}

is the inverse of
\lambda
in
\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{N\mathbb{Z}}.

Since
\gcd(\lambda,N)=1,
we have that
\gcd(\lambda^{-1},N)=1,
and so
A^{\prime}
is also sumset-distinct modulo
N
by
2.2
.
We note that by definition,
1
belongs to
A^{\prime}
and there is exactly one residue
x
modulo
N
that is not part of

Furthermore, it is evident that for any

y,y+1\not\equiv x\pmod{N},
then exactly one of the two belongs to
S(A^{\prime}\setminus\{1\}).

By writing the residues as the elements
x+1-N
up to
x-1
, we can deduce that
S(A^{\prime}\setminus\{1\})\pmod{N}
contains exactly the even numbers within the interval from
x+1-N
up to
x-1
.
Consequently, we can express
A^{\prime}=\{1,a_{2},a_{3},\ldots,a_{n}\}
, where each

is an even number within the interval
[x+1-N,x-1]
.
We now present three elementary observations:

•

the sum of any set of negative numbers in
A^{\prime}\setminus\{1\}=\{a_{2},a_{3},\ldots,a_{n}\}
is an even negative number,

•

since
N
is odd,
S(A^{\prime}\setminus\{1\})
is not congruent modulo

to any even number between
x-2N
and
x-N
,

•

for every
2\leq i\leq n
, we have
\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert<N
.

Combining these observations, we can conclude that
S(A^{\prime}\setminus\{1\})
does not contain any element smaller than
x+1-N.
Similarly, no element in
S(A^{\prime}\setminus\{1\})
is larger than
x-1.

Consequently,
S(A^{\prime})
is precisely equal to the interval
[x+1-N,x-1]
. A corollary of this is that the sum of all negative numbers in
A^{\prime}
results in the smallest number,
x+1-N
, and the sum of all positive numbers in
A^{\prime}
equals

.
By replacing the negative numbers in

with their additive inverse, we obtain

, which is still sumset-distinct modulo
N
due to
2.1
. Additionally, the sum of all elements in

equals

and so

It remains to show that

Suppose we know the

smallest elements of

are

, for some

, then since

is the smallest element in

that cannot be expressed as a sum of any subset of these

∎

Proof of
1.6
.

Recall that
\mathrm{res}(x)=\min\{x\pmod{N},N-x\pmod{N}\}
is the element between
0
and
\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}
equivalent to
x
or
-x
modulo
N.

For every
\lambda\in[N]
for which
\gcd(\lambda,N)=1,
we consider

R(\lambda)=\{\mathrm{res}(\lambda),\mathrm{res}(2\lambda),\ldots,\mathrm{res}(2^{n-1}\lambda)\}
as a representative set for all the sets of the form
\{\pm\lambda,\pm 2\lambda,\ldots\pm 2^{n-1}\lambda\}.

We note that
R(2\lambda)=R(\lambda)
since
\mathrm{res}(2^{n}\lambda)=\mathrm{res}(-\lambda)
, as a consequence of
2^{n}\equiv-1\pmod{N}
.
This implies that
R(\lambda)=R(\pm 2^{i}\lambda)
for every
0\leq i\leq n-1.

On the other hand, if
\lambda^{\prime}\not\in\{\pm\lambda,\pm 2\lambda,\ldots\pm 2^{n-1}\lambda\}
, as elements in
\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{N\mathbb{Z}}
, then
R(\lambda)\not=R(\lambda^{\prime})
and furthermore these sets are disjoint.
There are
\phi(N)
choices for
\lambda
, and thus
\frac{\phi(N)}{2n}
different representative sets
R(\lambda).

Every set
R(\lambda)
is associated with
2^{n}
different sets of the form
\{\pm\lambda,\pm 2\lambda,\ldots\pm 2^{n-1}\lambda\}.

We conclude that there are exactly
\frac{2^{n}\phi{(N)}}{2n}
sumset-distinct sets modulo

∎

Finally, we prove the case

and begin by presenting a lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose

is a sumset-distinct set modulo
N=2^{n}+t
, where
t
is an odd positive integer, and
A
has cardinality

.
In this case, the missing elements from

can be divided into a singleton

and
\frac{t-1}{2}
pairs

such that

Proof.

Let

be the sum of all elements in

.
For every subset

Therefore, an element

We note that no subset

∎

Proof for
N=2^{n}+3
.

According to
5.2
, the three missing numbers in the sumset-distinct set modulo

form an arithmetic progression of length
3.

We first argue that the common difference of these three numbers is relatively prime to

Claim 5.3
.

Let
A
be a sumset-distinct set modulo
N=2^{n}+3
, and let
x,y,z
be the three missing elements in
S(A)
such that
y+z=2x
. Then we have
\gcd(x-y,N)=1.

Proof of claim.

Suppose to the contrary that there is a prime divisor
p\mid\gcd(x-y,N)
.
Since
N=2^{n}+3
is not divisible by
2
or
3
, we have
p>3
.
Furthermore, all three elements
x,y
, and

are congruent modulo

Thus, among the residue classes modulo

,

contains
\frac{N}{p}-3
elements from the one containing

, and exactly
\frac{N}{p}
elements from every other residue class.
Let
A=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\}
and
A_{i}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{i}\}.

Then we have
S(A_{i+1})=S(A_{i})\cup\left(S(A_{i})+a_{i+1}\right).

Let the number of elements in

that are congruent to

By definition, we have
x_{n,j}\equiv\frac{N}{p}\pmod{3}
for every
j\in[p]
.
Using induction, we shall prove that for a fixed

x_{i,j}\pmod{3}
is the same for every

and not equal to zero. This suffices to conclude the proof as some of the

values are clearly equal to zero, a contradiction.

Assuming the statement is true for

to
n
, we will prove that it is also true for
i
. Note first that as
S(A_{i+1})=S(A_{i})\cup\left(S(A_{i})+a_{i+1}\right)
, by definition, we have

x_{i+1,j}=x_{i,j}+x_{i,j-a_{i+1}},

where the index

is considered modulo

as well.
It is important to note that, by
5.1
,
p\nmid a_{i+1}.
So we can consider these equations with variables

for

and constants
x_{i+1,j}
,
and rewrite the system of linear equations as follows:

\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&1&1&0&\dots&0&0\\
\dots&\dots&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&\dots&1&1\\
1&0&0&0&\dots&0&1\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{i,a_{i+1}}\\
x_{i,2a_{i+1}}\\
x_{i,3a_{i+1}}\\
\dots\\

x_{i+1,3a_{i+1}}\\
\dots\\

The coefficient matrix in this case is a

-matrix.
Since

Consequently, in

there exists a unique solution.
However, since all the constants

,

, are the same by the induction hypothesis, this implies that all the variables

are the same and equal to

modulo 3.
∎

Let

be the common difference of

and

, which is coprime with

according to

.
Therefore,

contains all elements in

except for three consecutive integers

and

All three integers are nonzero (since

.
Let us consider the binary representation

Now, we define

It follows that

By

, we can conclude that the set

is derived from a set

by possibly switching the signs of some elements, and one can even conclude that

, i.e.

∎

Proof of

.

If

contains

Therefore, it is sufficient to prove the characterization assuming that

Let

be a sumset-distinct set modulo

which does not contain

Let

and

be the two missing elements in

Suppose

Claim 6.1
.

s_{1}-s_{2}\not\equiv N/2\pmod{N}.

Proof of claim.

Let
g_{A}(x)=\prod_{i}(1+x^{a_{i}})
.
Then we have

∎

Since
2^{n}>2^{n-1}+1,

S(A)
contains odd elements.
This implies that

contains at least one odd element. By induction, we can conclude that

contains an equal number of odd and even sums. Consequently,

and

have different parities. Next, we will prove that

contains exactly one odd element.

.

A
contains precisely one odd element.

Proof of claim.

Assume that
A
contains at least two odd numbers, with

being one of them.

and

have the same residue modulo

Let

be defined analogously.
Since

Since

and the two elements

Denoting the single odd element of

,

∎

7
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we introduced a modular setting of the Erdős distinct subset sums conjecture, and proved it asymptotically.
One may also investigate if a similar result holds for sets that are almost sumset-distinct modulo
N
, i.e. sets having at least
(1-o(1))2^{n}
different subset sums.

We also characterise the sumset-distinct sets modulo
N=2^{n}+t
of cardinality
n
, for
0\leq t\leq 3.
For larger
t
, we suggest the following problems.

•

For every
t\geq 1,
is the number of sumset-distinct sets modulo
N=2^{n}+t
of cardinality
n
bounded by
O_{t}(N^{2+v_{2}(t)})
?

•

Given
t\geq 1
, let
n
be sufficiently large. If
t
is odd, is every sumset-distinct set modulo
N=2^{n}+t
of cardinality
n
a ‘perturbation’ of the powers of
2
?

•

If
t
is even and
n
sufficiently large, is it true that every sumset-distinct set modulo
N=2^{n}+t
of cardinality
n
either contains
N/2
, or contains exactly one odd number, or is a ‘perturbation’ of the powers of
2
?

Here are two ways to construct perturbations of the powers of
2
.

•

A=\{2^{0}+p_{0},2^{1}+p_{1},\ldots,2^{n-1}+p_{n-1}\}
, with
p_{i}\geq 0
,
p_{n-1}<t/2
and
p_{i+1}\geq\sum_{j=0}^{i}p_{j}
for all
i\geq 0
,

•

A=\{a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1},2^{i},2^{i+1},\ldots,2^{n-2},2^{n-1}\}
with
v_{2}(a_{j})=j
for every
0\leq j\leq i-1
.

We remark that the condition that
n
is sufficiently large is necessary. For
N=2^{5}+4
, the set
\{1,6,11,13,15\}
is sumset-distinct modulo
N
, but it is not of the above form.

In
Appendix

A
it is additionally proven that many of the constructions of the first kind belong to different equivalence classes.
It might be tempting to guess that for odd
t
and
n
large enough, every sumset-distinct set modulo
N=2^{n}+t
is in the equivalence class of a set for which the sum of elements is bounded by
N
. This turns out to be false, as explained in
Appendix

B
.

Appendix

Appendix A
Perturbations of powers of
2
that are sumset-distinct

We start with two families of sets that are sumset-distinct modulo
2^{n}+t
. Additionally, we can prove that many of these sets belong to distinct equivalence classes.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
r\geq 1
be a fixed value, and set
t=2r+3
.
Let us consider a set
A=\{2^{0}+p_{0},2^{1}+p_{1},\ldots,2^{n-1}+p_{n-1}\}
, which is a sumset-distinct sets modulo
2^{n}+t
. This holds for any choice of
p_{i}\geq 0
that satisfies
the condition
p_{i+1}\geq\sum_{j=0}^{i}p_{j}
for all
i\geq 0
, and
r\geq p_{n-1}
.
For sufficiently large
n
, each set of this form belongs to a distinct equivalence class.

Proof.

Note that the given conditions imply that
t>2r\geq\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}p_{j}
.
It is now sufficient to observe that the sum of all elements is bounded by
2^{n}-1+t
, and no two different subsets can have equal sums. This is a consequence of the fact that
a_{i+1}>\sum_{j=0}^{i}a_{j}
holds for every
i\geq 0
, where
a_{j}=2^{j}+p_{j}.

Next, we proceed to prove that two distinct sets of the given form,
A=\{a_{1},a_{2}\ldots,a_{n}\}=\{2^{0}+p_{0},2^{1}+p_{1},\ldots,2^{n-1}+p_{n-1}\}
and
B=\{2^{0}+q_{0},2^{1}+q_{1},\ldots,2^{n-1}+q_{n-1}\}
, belong to different equivalence classes when
n\geq t>2(r+1)
.
Assume, without loss of generality, that the number of
p_{i}
values equal to zero is at least the number of
q_{i}
values equal to zero.
Let
j_{0}
be the smallest index for which
q_{j_{0}}>0
.
Since
r\geq p_{n-1}\geq\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}p_{j}
, there are at most
r+1
occurrences of zero among the
p_{i}
values.
Since
n>2(r+1)
, we can conclude that more than half of the
p_{i}
values(and similarly for
q_{i}
) are zeros. Therefore, we have
j_{0}>\frac{n}{2}>r+1
.
If
A
and
B
belong to the same equivalence class, there must exist a value
\lambda\in B
such that
\{\lambda a_{1},\pm\lambda a_{2},\ldots,\pm\lambda a_{n}\}
is equal to
B
.
When
\lambda=2^{s}
for some value of
1\leq s\leq j_{0}-1,
it would imply that
2^{j_{0}}\in B
, leading to a contradiction.
Therefore, we must have
\lambda=2^{j}+q_{j}
for some
j\geq j_{0}.

Now, considering
2^{n-j-1}\lambda=2^{n-1}+2^{n-j-1}q_{j}
, we need it to be equal to
\pm\left(2^{n-1}+q_{n-1}\right)\pmod{N}
.
The latter implies that
2^{n-j}\lambda=-\left(2^{n}+2q_{n-1}\right)\pmod{N},
specifically equal to
t-2q_{n-1}
.
However, this value is an odd number that lies strictly between
1
and
2^{r+2}
, and therefore, it does not belong to
B.

∎

For

it appears that the examples in

Example A.2

A set

Appendix B
Sumset-disctinct sets could have large total sum

Our exact results for

Proposition B.1
.

For

and

We will prove the validity of the statement for the sets

First, we will prove that the sets in the class are sumset-distinct modulo

.

Claim B.2
.

The set
A=\{3,6,12,24,2^{4},2^{5},\ldots,2^{n-2},2^{n-1}\}
is sumset-distinct modulo

.

Proof of claim.

Note that
s(A)=2^{n}+29.

The numbers have different

x
and

.
Since

, it must be a multiple of
3
.
The elements not involved, summing to

, should be multiples of
3
, or equal to

.
However, since

, this is impossible.
∎

Next, we prove that no representative of this equivalence class has a sumset(sum of elements) smaller than

Claim B.3

The set

Recall
\mathrm{res}(x)=\min\{x\pmod{N},N-(x\pmod{N})\}
, where

.
We need to prove that for every

\sum_{i=0}^{n-5}\mathrm{res}(2^{i}\lambda)+\sum_{i=1}^{4}\mathrm{res}\left(\frac{3}{2^{i}}\lambda\right)\geq N.

According to

For

Therefore, we can focus on the cases where either

Case 1:
1\leq\lambda\leq 16
