Abstract
. The Stone-Weierstrass Theorem for compact Hausdorff spaces is a basic result of functional analysis with far-reaching consequences. We introduce an equational logic ⊨ Δ \vDash_{\Delta} associated with an infinitary variety Δ \Delta and show that the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem is a consequence of the Beth definability property of ⊨ Δ \vDash_{\Delta} , stating that every implicit definition can be made explicit. Further, we define an infinitary propositional logic ⊢ Δ \vdash_{\Delta} by means of a Hilbert-style calculus and prove a strong completeness result whereby the semantic notion of consequence associated with ⊢ Δ \vdash_{\Delta} coincides with ⊨ Δ \vDash_{\Delta} .



1.
Introduction

Weierstrass’ Approximation Theorem states that any continuous real-valued function defined on a closed real interval can be uniformly approximated by polynomials. In 1937, Marshall Stone proved a vast generalisation of this theorem
[
35
]
; nowadays known as the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem for compact Hausdorff spaces, this is a fundamental result of functional analysis with far-reaching consequences.

Let
X
be a non-empty compact Hausdorff space and let
\C(X,\mathbb{R})
be the collection of all continuous functions
X\to\mathbb{R}
, where we denote by
\mathbb{R}
the set of real numbers equipped with the usual Euclidean topology.
The set
\C(X,\mathbb{R})
is equipped with the
uniform metric

\varrho\colon\C(X,\mathbb{R})\times\C(X,\mathbb{R})\to[0,\infty)
given by

\forall\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{g}\in\C(X,\mathbb{R}),\ \ \varrho(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{g})\coloneqq\sup_{x\in X}{|\mathfrak{f}(x)-\mathfrak{g}(x)|}.

The Stone-Weierstrass Theorem provides sufficient conditions for a subset
G\subseteq\C(X,\mathbb{R})
to be dense in the topology induced by the uniform metric. Recall that a subset
G
of
\C(X,\mathbb{R})
is said to
separate the points of
X
if, for any two distinct points
x,y\in X
, there exists
\mathfrak{f}\in G
such that
\mathfrak{f}(x)\neq\mathfrak{f}(y)
. The Stone-Weierstrass Theorem can be phrased as follows:

Theorem 1.1

(cf.
[
35
, Theorem 82]
)
.

Let
X
be a non-empty compact Hausdorff space and let
G
be a subset of
\C(X,\mathbb{R})
satisfying the following properties:

(i)

G
separates the points of
X
;

(ii)

G
contains the constant function of value
1
;

(iii)

\mathfrak{f}\in G
and
r\in\mathbb{R}
imply
r\cdot\mathfrak{f}\in G
;

(iv)

if
\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{g}\in G
, then
\mathfrak{f}+\mathfrak{g}\in G
and
\max{\{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{g}\}}\in G
.

Then
G
is dense in
\C(X,\mathbb{R})
in the topology induced by the uniform metric.

Remark 1.2
.

For a nice exposition of the previous result, along with a proof relying on the closure of the set
G
under lattice-theoretic operations, see
[
36
]
. The version stated above, involving the operations
+
and
\max
, seems to be more widespread in analysis (see, e.g.,
[
17
, Theorem 7.29]
).

On the other hand, Beth definability is a strong property that a logic may, or may not, satisfy. Informally, it states that every property that can be defined implicitly admits an explicit definition. Beth’s theorem, proved in 1953, states that first-order logic has the Beth definability property
[
3
]
. This result sheds light on a phenomenon that occurs frequently in the mathematical practice. For instance, a real closed field
F
(i.e., a field elementarily equivalent to
\mathbb{R}
) admits a unique total order that turns it into an ordered field. This order is given by the first-order formula

\forall x,y\in F\ (x\leqslant y\,\leftrightarrow\,\exists z\,(y-x=z^{2})).

Beth’s theorem tells us that this is no coincidence: whenever a property can be described in a unique way, there is a first-order formula explicitly defining it.
In the context of algebraic logic, the Beth definability property has been extensively investigated, cf.
[
16
, §5.6]
or
[
18
,
7
]
. For a precise definition in our setting, see Section
4
.

The aim of this paper is to show that the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem for compact Hausdorff spaces can be seen as a consequence of the Beth definability property for a certain equational logic
\vDash_{\Delta}
, thus obtaining a “logical proof” of the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem.
(In fact, the Beth definability property for
\vDash_{\Delta}
can be regarded as an equivalent form of the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem, cf. Remark
4.10
.)
The logic
\vDash_{\Delta}
is the equational consequence relation associated with the variety
\Delta
of
\delta
-algebras introduced in
[
26
]
. The latter is not a variety in the usual sense,
à la
Birkhoff, in that its signature contains an operation symbol of countably infinite arity. Each member of
\Delta
has a reduct of MV-algebra, the algebraic counterpart to Łukasiewicz infinite-valued propositional logic
\L_{\infty}

[
23
,
24
]
, and so
\vDash_{\Delta}
is an infinitary extension of
\L_{\infty}
(this extension is, in fact, conservative: every equation between MV-algebraic terms that holds in all
\delta
-algebras must hold in all MV-algebras).

This paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we provide the necessary background on Abelian
\ell
-groups (which capture the structure of the algebras of functions
\C(X,\mathbb{R})
), MV-algebras, and the infinitary variety
\Delta
of
\delta
-algebras. Further, we recall the Cignoli-Dubuc-Mundici adjunction between MV-algebras and the dual of compact Hausdorff spaces, and its restriction to the variety
\Delta
. The logic
\vDash_{\Delta}
associated with
\Delta
is introduced in Section
3
, where some of its main properties are established. In Section
4
, we prove that
\vDash_{\Delta}
has the Beth definability property and show how the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem can be deduced from it. Finally, in Section
5
a logic
\vdash_{\Delta}
is introduced by means of a Hilbert-style calculus, and shown to be strongly complete with respect to a
[0,1]
-valued semantics which, up to a translation between terms and equations, coincides with the consequence relation
\vDash_{\Delta}
.

2.
Preliminaries and background

2.1.
Abelian
\ell
-groups and MV-algebras

An
Abelian
\ell
-group
is an Abelian group
G
, written additively,
equipped with a lattice order
\leqslant
invariant under translations, i.e.,

\forall a,b,c\in G,\ \ a\leqslant b\ \Longrightarrow\ a+c\leqslant b+c.

We say that
G
is
unital
if it is equipped with a distinguished element
u\in G
(the
unit
) such that, for each
a\in G
, there is an
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a\leqslant nu
. A prime example of unital Abelian
\ell
-group is
(\mathbb{R},1)
, where
\mathbb{R}
is the additive group of real numbers equipped with the usual total order. More generally, for any topological space
X
, the set
\C(X,\mathbb{R})
of all continuous
\mathbb{R}
-valued functions on
X
is an Abelian
\ell
-group with respect to pointwise operations. The constant function
1_{X}\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
of value
1
is a unit for
\C(X,\mathbb{R})
.

Remark 2.1
.

Throughout this paper, the expression
\C(X,\mathbb{R})
will refer to either the structure of unital Abelian
\ell
-group, or that of topological space with the topology induced by the uniform metric. It will always be clear from the context which structure we are considering.

Let us denote by
\lGr
the category of unital Abelian
\ell
-groups and
unital
\ell
-homomorphisms
, i.e. functions that are both lattice and group homomorphisms and preserve the unit. Subobjects in
\lGr
, i.e. sublattice subgroups containing the unit, are called
unital
\ell
-subgroups
.

Given an arbitrary unital Abelian
\ell
-group
(G,u)
, we can equip its
unit interval

\Gamma(G,u)\coloneqq\{a\in G\mid 0\leqslant a\leqslant u\}

with the operations

\displaystyle a\oplus b\coloneqq(a+b)\wedge u\ \text{ and }\ \neg a\coloneqq u-a.

(When the choice of the unit is clear from the context, we write
\Gamma(G)
instead of
\Gamma(G,u)
.)
The tuple
(\Gamma(G),\oplus,\neg,0)
carries the structure of an MV-algebra.

An
MV-algebra
is an algebra
(A,\oplus,\neg,0)
, in the algebraic language
\mathbb{L}_{MV}\coloneqq\{\oplus,\neg,0\}
of type
(2,1,0)
, satisfying the following conditions for all
a,b\in A
:
2
2

2

The axiomatisation presented here is redundant: it was shown in
[
20
]
that commutativity of the monoid operation
\oplus
follows from the other axioms.

(i)

(A,\oplus,0)
is a commutative monoid

(ii)

\neg
is an involution, i.e.
\neg\neg a=a

(iii)

a\oplus\neg 0=\neg 0

(iv)

\neg(\neg a\oplus b)\oplus b=\neg(\neg b\oplus a)\oplus a

While Boolean algebras are the algebraic counterpart to classical propositional logic, MV-algebras are the algebraic counterpart to Łukasiewicz infinite-valued propositional logic
\L_{\infty}
. For more details, we refer the interested reader to
[
11
,
30
]
.

The operation
\oplus
should be regarded as a strong disjunction, and the involution
\neg
plays the role of a negation which allows us to define a strong conjunction by

a\odot b\coloneqq\neg(\neg a\oplus\neg b).

It will be useful to define a further connective:

\displaystyle a\ominus b\coloneqq a\odot\neg b.

We denote by
\MV
the category of MV-algebras and
MV-homomorphisms
, i.e. functions preserving
\oplus
,
\neg
, and
0
.
Any MV-algebra has an underlying structure of distributive lattice bounded below by
0
and above by
1\coloneqq\neg 0
. Binary joins (also known as weak disjunctions) are given by

a\vee b=\neg(\neg a\oplus b)\oplus b.

Thus, item
(iv)
above states that
a\vee b=b\vee a
.
Binary meets (also known as weak conjunctions) are given by the De Morgan condition
a\wedge b=\neg(\neg a\vee\neg b)
.
Boolean algebras are precisely those MV-algebras that satisfy the law of excluded middle
a\vee\neg a=1

[
9
, Theorems 1.16 and 1.17]
.

The
standard MV-algebra
is the real unit interval
[0,1]
with neutral element
0
in which the operations
\oplus
and
\neg
are defined, respectively, by

\displaystyle a\oplus b\coloneqq\min{\{1,a+b\}}

(the connective
\oplus
is frequently referred to as
truncated addition
) and

\displaystyle\neg a\coloneqq 1-a.

The derived operations
\odot
and
\ominus
are then interpreted as
a\odot b=\max{\{0,a+b-1\}}
and
a\ominus b=\max{\{0,a-b\}}
(
\ominus
is often called
truncated subtraction
).
The underlying lattice order of this MV-algebra coincides with the total order that
[0,1]
inherits from the real numbers, cf.
[
11
, pp. 9–10]
. When we
refer to
[0,1]
as an MV-algebra, we always mean the structure just described.
Note that the standard MV-algebra
[0,1]
coincides with
\Gamma(\mathbb{R},1)
, the unit interval of the unital Abelian
\ell
-group
(\mathbb{R},1)
. Similarly, for any topological space
X
, the unit interval of the Abelian
\ell
-group
\C(X,\mathbb{R})
with unit
1_{X}
can be identified with the MV-algebra

\displaystyle\C(X,[0,1])\coloneqq\{\mathfrak{f}\colon X\to[0,1]\mid\mathfrak{f}\,\text{ is continuous}\},

where
[0,1]
is equipped with the Euclidean topology and the MV-algebraic operations of
\C(X,[0,1])
are defined pointwise.

Every unital
\ell
-homomorphism
(G,u)\to(G^{\prime},u^{\prime})
between unital Abelian
\ell
-groups restricts to an MV-homomorphism
\Gamma(G,u)\to\Gamma(G^{\prime},u^{\prime})
. In fact, this assignment yields a functor
\Gamma\colon\lGr\to\MV
.
In 1986, Mundici showed that
\Gamma
is an equivalence of categories.

3

3

We shall only need that
\Gamma
is full and faithful, hence it reflects isomorphisms. Cf. the proof of Theorem
1.1
on page
4
. This fact is easier to prove and corresponds to Propositions 3.4 and 3.5 in
[

]
.
For a proof of this fact, and an explicit description of the quasi-inverse functor, see
[

]
or
[

.

Theorem 2.2

(Mundici’s Equivalence)
.

\Gamma\colon\lGr\to\MV
is an equivalence of categories.

2.2.
Ideal theory in MV-algebras

As in the case of rings, quotients (or congruences) of MV-algebras can be described in terms of ideals. An
ideal
of an MV-algebra
A
is a subset of
A
that contains
0
, is downwards closed in the lattice order of
A
, and is closed under
\oplus
.
The ideal
\langle S\rangle
generated by a non-empty subset
S\subseteq A
can be described as follows (cf.
[
11
, Lemma 1.2.1]
):

(1)

\displaystyle\langle S\rangle=\{a\in A\mid a\leqslant s_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus s_{n}\ \text{for some}\ s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\in S\}.

Given an ideal
I\subseteq A
, the corresponding congruence is

\displaystyle{\equiv_{I}}\coloneqq\{(a,b)\in A\times A\mid\textrm{d}(a,b)\in I\},

where
d
is the derived operation

(2)

\textrm{d}(a,b)\coloneqq(a\ominus b)\oplus(b\ominus a)

known as
Chang’s distance
. When interpreted in the MV-algebra
[0,1]
, Chang’s distance coincides with the usual Euclidean distance. Just observe that, for all
x,y\in[0,1]
,

\textrm{d}(x,y)=\min\{\max\{0,x-y\}+\max\{0,y-x\},1\}=\begin{cases}y-x\ \ \text{if $x\leqslant y$}\\
x-y\ \ \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\hskip-8.00003pt=|x-y|.

We write
A/I
for the quotient algebra
A/{\equiv_{I}}
. Conversely, the ideal associated with a congruence
\equiv
on
A
is

I_{\equiv}\coloneqq\{a\in A\mid a\equiv 0\}.

This yields a bijective correspondence between ideals of
A
and congruences on
A

[
11
, Proposition 1.2.6]
.

A
maximal ideal
of an MV-algebra
A
is an ideal
\mathfrak{m}
of
A
that is proper (i.e.,
\mathfrak{m}\subsetneq A
) and not strictly contained in any other proper ideal of
A
. The set of maximal ideals of
A
is denoted by
\Maxi(A)
. As a consequence of Zorn’s Lemma, an MV-algebra is non-trivial (i.e., it has two distinct elements) precisely when
\Maxi(A)\neq\emptyset

[
11
, Corollary 1.2.15]
.
An MV-algebra is
simple
if it has no proper ideal distinct from
\{0\}
. Up to isomorphism, the simple MV-algebras are precisely the subalgebras of
[0,1]

[
11
, Theorem 3.5.1]
. An MV-algebra
A
is
semisimple
if it is a subdirect product of simple MV-algebras; equivalently, if
\bigcap{\Maxi(A)}=\{0\}

[
11
, Proposition 3.6.1]
.

Maximal ideals of an MV-algebra
A
can also be described in terms of MV-homomorphisms
A\to[0,1]
. Using the fact that the standard MV-algebra
[0,1]
is simple, it is not difficult to see that
h^{-1}(0)\in\Maxi(A)
for any MV-homomorphism
h\colon A\to[0,1]

[
11
, Proposition 1.2.16]
. This yields a function

\Bbbk\colon\hom_{\MV}(A,[0,1])\to\Maxi(A),\ \ \Bbbk(h)\coloneqq h^{-1}(0).

For the converse direction, we appeal to Hölder’s Theorem (for a modern proof for
\ell
-groups, see
[
6
, §2.6]
; for the MV-algebraic version, cf.
[
11
, Theorem 3.5.1]
):

Theorem 2.3

(Hölder’s Theorem)
.

For any MV-algebra
A
and maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}\in\Maxi(A)
, there exists a unique homomorphism of MV-algebras
h_{\mathfrak{m}}\colon A\to[0,1]
such that
\Bbbk(h_{\mathfrak{m}})=\mathfrak{m}
.

2.3.
The Cignoli-Dubuc-Mundici adjunction

We recall from
[
12
]
the Cignoli-Dubuc-Mundici adjunction between the category
\MV
of MV-algebras and MV-homomorphisms and the dual of the category
\KH
of compact Hausdorff spaces and continuous maps.

This dual adjunction is induced by the dualising object
[0,1]
, regarded as either a compact Hausdorff space or an MV-algebra.
Given an MV-algebra
A
, equip the set of homomorphisms
\hom_{\MV}(A,[0,1])
with the subspace topology induced by the product topology of
[0,1]^{A}
. The ensuing space is denoted by
\Max{A}
and referred to as the
maximal spectrum
of
A
. A routine argument, exploiting the fact that the MV-algebraic operations of
[0,1]
are continuous with respect to the Euclidean topology, shows that
\Max{A}
is closed in the product topology of
[0,1]^{A}
. Thus,
\Max{A}
is a compact Hausdorff space.

In the MV-algebraic literature (see e.g.
[
30
]
), the space
\Max{A}
is usually studied by means of a different but equivalent representation. Let us endow the set of maximal ideals
\Maxi(A)
with the
hull-kernel
topology having as a basis of opens the sets of the form

O_{a}\coloneqq\{\mathfrak{m}\in\Maxi(A)\mid a\notin\mathfrak{m}\}

for
a\in A
. The space
\Maxi(A)
is compact and Hausdorff, see
[
30
, Proposition 4.15]
. In fact, the function
\Bbbk\colon\Max{A}\to\Maxi(A)
, which is a bijection by Theorem
2.3
, is a homeomorphism. For the continuity of
\Bbbk
note that, for any
a\in A
,

\Bbbk^{-1}(O_{a})=\{h\in\Max{A}\mid h(a)\neq 0\}

which is open in
\Max{A}
because it coincides with the preimage of
(0,1]
under the (continuous) projection
\Max{A}\to[0,1]
,
h\mapsto h(a)
. As every continuous bijection between compact Hausdorff spaces is a homeomorphism, the spaces
\Max{A}
and
\Maxi(A)
are homeomorphic.

The next lemma provides a characterisation of the closed subsets of
\Max{A}
(for a proof, see
[
30
, §4.4]
). For any subset
S\subseteq A
, define

(3)

\displaystyle\V(S)\coloneqq\{h\in\Max{A}\mid h(a)=0\ \text{for all}\ a\in S\}.

If
a\in A
, we write
\V(a)
as a shorthand for
\V(\{a\})
.

Lemma 2.4
.

The following statements hold for any MV-algebra
A
and subsets
S,S^{\prime}\subseteq A
.

(a)

\V(S)\subseteq\V(S^{\prime})
if, and only if,
\langle S\rangle\supseteq\langle S^{\prime}\rangle
.

(b)

The closed subsets of
\Max{A}
are precisely those of the form
\V(I)
for some ideal
I\subseteq A
.

For every MV-homomorphism
k\colon A\to B
, the function

\Max{k}\colon\Max{B}\to\Max{A},\ \ h\mapsto h\circ k

is continuous because
(\Max{k})^{-1}(\V(I))=\V(\langle k(I)\rangle)
for every ideal
I
of
A
.
We get a functor

\displaystyle\Max\colon\MV\to\KH^{\rm op}.

Conversely, given a compact Hausdorff space
X
, let
\C(X,[0,1])
be the MV-algebra of all continuous functions
X\to[0,1]
with the pointwise operations of the standard MV-algebra. If
\mathfrak{f}\colon X\to Y
is a morphism in
\KH
, it is easily seen that the induced map

\displaystyle\C{\mathfrak{f}}\colon\C(Y,[0,1])\to\C(X,[0,1]),\ \ \mathfrak{g}\mapsto\mathfrak{g}\circ\mathfrak{f}

is a morphism in
\MV
. We can thus regard
\hom_{\KH}(-,[0,1])
as a functor

\displaystyle\C\colon\KH^{\rm op}\to\MV.

For every compact Hausdorff space
X
there is a continuous map

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{X}\colon X\to\Max{\C(X,[0,1])},\ \ x\mapsto(\C(X,[0,1])\xrightarrow{\ev_{x}}[0,1],\ \mathfrak{f}\mapsto\mathfrak{f}(x)).

Moreover, for every MV-algebra
A
, there is an MV-homomorphism

\displaystyle\eta_{A}\colon A\to\C(\Max{A},[0,1]),\ \ a\mapsto(\Max{A}\xrightarrow{\ev_{a}}[0,1],\ h\mapsto h(a)).

To improve readability, we will write
\widehat{a}
instead of
\ev_{a}
. Thus, for all
a\in A
,

(4)

\displaystyle\widehat{a}\colon\Max{A}\to[0,1]

is the continuous map defined by
\widehat{a}(h)\coloneqq h(a)
.

Denoting by
{\rm Id}_{{C}}
the identity functor on a category
{C}
, it is not difficult to see that
\varepsilon_{X}
and
\eta_{A}
yield natural transformations
\varepsilon\colon\Max\circ\C\to{\rm Id}_{\KH^{\rm op}}
and
\eta\colon{\rm Id}_{\MV}\to\C\circ\Max
, respectively.

Theorem 2.5

(Cignoli-Dubuc-Mundici Adjunction)
.

The natural transformations
\eta
and
\varepsilon
are the unit and counit, respectively, of an adjunction
\Max\dashv\C\colon\KH^{\rm op}\to\MV
. Furthermore, the following statements hold:

(a)

For every
X
in
\KH
, the component
\varepsilon_{X}\colon X\to\Max{\C(X,[0,1])}
of the counit is a homeomorphism, i.e. the functor
\C
is full and faithful.

(b)

For every
A
in
\MV
, the component
\eta_{A}\colon A\to\C(\Max{A},[0,1])
of the unit is injective if, and only if,
A
is semisimple.

The adjunction
\Max\dashv\C
was introduced in
[
12
]
.
For a proof of items
(a)
and
(b)
, see e.g.
[
30
, Theorem 4.16]
.

2.4.
The variety
\Delta

Any adjunction restricts to an equivalence between the full subcategories defined by the fixed objects, i.e. those objects for which the components of the unit and counit, respectively, are isomorphisms. In the case of the Cignoli-Dubuc-Mundici adjunction

\Max\dashv\C\colon\KH^{\mathrm{op}}\to\MV,

as the counit is a natural isomorphism by Theorem
2.5
(a)
, we see that there exists a full subcategory of
\MV
dually equivalent to
\KH
. In
[
26
]
, the variety
\Delta
of
\delta
-algebras was defined and shown to be isomorphic to a full subcategory of
\MV
dually equivalent to
\KH
. However, the proof of the duality between
\Delta
and
\KH
given there relies on the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem. In this section we recall some facts about
\delta
-algebras that do not depend on the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem.

Consider the algebraic language
\mathbb{L}_{\Delta}\coloneqq\{\delta,\oplus,\neg,0\}
of type
(\omega,2,1,0)
.

3.
The logic
\vDash_{\Delta}

The purpose of this section is to introduce the infinitary equational logic
\vDash_{\Delta}
and prove its main properties: the compactness and local deduction theorems, and some of their consequences. Every Birkhoff variety
\mathcal{V}
of finitary algebras comes with an associated logic, namely the equational consequence
\vDash_{\mathcal{V}}
. See, e.g.,
[
27
, §2]
. This concept makes sense also for varieties of infinitary algebras in the sense of Słomiński
[
33
]
. We shall spell this out in the case of the variety
\Delta
.

To improve readability, write
\mathbb{L}
instead of
\mathbb{L}_{\Delta}
for the language of
\delta
-algebras. Given a (possibly infinite) set of propositional variables
\overline{x}
, let
\mathcal{T}(\overline{x})
denote the
algebra of
\mathbb{L}
-terms in the variables
\overline{x}
. In other words,
\mathcal{T}(\overline{x})
is the
absolutely free
\mathbb{L}
-algebra over
\overline{x}
. As in the case of varieties of Birkhoff algebras, the free
\delta
-algebra over
\overline{x}
, denoted by
\mathcal{F}(\overline{x})
, can be constructed in a canonical way as a quotient

\uprho\colon\mathcal{T}(\overline{x})\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{F}(\overline{x}).

(This is true for any variety of infinitary algebras, provided there is a cardinal
\kappa
such that all operations have arity smaller than
\kappa
, cf.
[
33
, Chapter III]
.) If
A
is an
\mathbb{L}
-algebra and
f\colon\overline{x}\to A
is a function, also called an
assignment
of the variables
\overline{x}
in
A
, we denote by

\widetilde{f}\colon\mathcal{T}(\overline{x})\to A

the unique
\mathbb{L}
-homomorphism extending
f
.

An
\mathbb{L}
-equation
in the variables
\overline{x}
is a pair
(s,t)\in\mathcal{T}(\overline{x})\times\mathcal{T}(\overline{x})
of
\mathbb{L}
-terms; we shall use the more suggestive notation
s\approx t
for the equation
(s,t)
.
Arbitrary
\mathbb{L}
-equations will be denoted by
\sigma
, and sets of
\mathbb{L}
-equations by
\Sigma
,
\Gamma
, or
\Pi
. To emphasize that the variables of an
\mathbb{L}
-term,
\mathbb{L}
-equation, or set of
\mathbb{L}
-equations, are contained in
\overline{x}
, we write
s(\overline{x}),\sigma(\overline{x})
, or
\Sigma(\overline{x})
. Further, for convenience of notation, we drop reference to the language
\mathbb{L}
when speaking of
\mathbb{L}
-terms,
\mathbb{L}
-equations, and
\mathbb{L}
-homomorphisms.
Given a set of equations
\Sigma(\overline{x})
, an
\mathbb{L}
-algebra
A
and an assignment
f\colon\overline{x}\to A
, we define

A,f\models\Sigma\ \text{ if, and only if, }\ \Sigma\subseteq\ker{\widetilde{f}}

where
\ker{\widetilde{f}}\coloneqq\{(s,t)\in\mathcal{T}(\overline{x})\times\mathcal{T}(\overline{x})\mid\widetilde{f}(s)=\widetilde{f}(t)\}
is the kernel of
\widetilde{f}
.
If
A,f\models\Sigma
, we say that
\Sigma
is
satisfied
in
A
with respect to the variable assignment
f
.
For any set of equations
\Sigma(\overline{x})\cup\{\sigma(\overline{x})\}
, define
\Sigma\vDash_{\Delta}\sigma
if and only if for every
A\in\Delta
and assignment
f\colon\overline{x}\to A
,

A,f\models\Sigma\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ A,f\models\sigma.

Finally, given a set of equations
\Sigma(\overline{x})\cup\Gamma(\overline{x})
, the equational consequence relation
\vDash_{\Delta}
is defined by
\Sigma\vDash_{\Delta}\Gamma
if, and only if,
\Sigma\vDash_{\Delta}\sigma
for every
\sigma\in\Gamma
.

As with groups, every equation
s\approx t
in the language of
\delta
-algebras is equivalent to one of the form
s^{\prime}\approx 0
. Just observe that, for any
A\in\Delta
and all elements
a,b\in A
,

a=b\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \textrm{d}(a,b)=0

where
d
is Chang’s distance. By applying the involution
\neg
, we can also transform the equation
s\approx t
into an equivalent one of the form
s^{\prime\prime}\approx 1
.
Furthermore, since every member of
\Delta
embeds into a power of
[0,1]
by Theorem
2.8
(c)
, we see that

(6)

\Sigma\vDash_{\Delta}\sigma\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \text{for every assignment $f\colon\overline{x}\to[0,1]$},\ [0,1],f\models\Sigma\ \ \text{entails}\ \ [0,1],f\models\sigma.

In other words, the logic
\vDash_{\Delta}
is complete with respect to valuations in
[0,1]
(algebraically:
[0,1]
generates the variety
\Delta
).

An important observation is that the logic
\vDash_{\Delta}
is compact because the corresponding maximal spectra are topologically compact (cf. Lemma
3.1
below). We refer to an arbitrary set of equations
\Sigma(\overline{x})
as a
theory
, and say that
\Sigma(\overline{x})
is
satisfiable
if there exist a non-trivial algebra
A\in\Delta
(i.e., a
\delta
-algebra with at least two distinct elements) and an assignment
f\colon\overline{x}\to A
such that
A,f\models\Sigma
. Recalling from Section
2.2
that
A
is non-trivial if, and only if, it admits a maximal ideal, we see that
\Sigma
is satisfiable precisely when there exists an assignment
f\colon\overline{x}\to[0,1]
satisfying
[0,1],f\models\Sigma
. Here and throughout this paper, by an
ideal
of a
\delta
-algebra we understand an ideal of the underlying MV-algebra.

Lemma 3.1

(Compactness)
.

Let
\Sigma(\overline{x})
be any theory. Then
\Sigma(\overline{x})
is satisfiable if, and only if, all its finite subsets are satisfiable.

Proof.

For the non-trivial direction, suppose that every finite subset of
\Sigma(\overline{x})
is satisfiable. We can assume without loss of generality that

\Sigma(\overline{x})=\{s_{i}\approx 0\mid i\in I\}

for a set of terms
\{s_{i}(\overline{x})\mid i\in I\}
. With the notation in (
3
), each term
s_{i}
yields a closed subset
\V(\uprho(s_{i}))
of
\Max{\mathcal{F}(\overline{x})}
, where
\uprho\colon\mathcal{T}(\overline{x})\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{F}(\overline{x})
is the canonical quotient. We claim that

\{\V(\uprho(s_{i}))\mid i\in I\}

has the finite intersection property. For any finite subset
I_{0}\subseteq I
, the theory

\Sigma_{0}\coloneqq\{s_{i}\approx 0\mid i\in I_{0}\}

is satisfiable, hence there exists an assignment
f\colon\overline{x}\to[0,1]
satisfying
[0,1],f\models\Sigma_{0}
. Denoting by
h\colon\mathcal{F}(\overline{x})\to[0,1]
the unique homomorphism extending
f
, we have

h(\uprho(s_{i}))=\widetilde{f}(s_{i})=0\ \text{ for every $i\in I_{0}$},

i.e.,
h\in\bigcap{\{\V(\uprho(s_{i}))\mid i\in I_{0}\}}
. It follows that
\{\V(\uprho(s_{i}))\mid i\in I\}
has the finite intersection property and so, as the space
\Max{\mathcal{F}(\overline{x})}
is compact,
\bigcap{\{\V(\uprho(s_{i}))\mid i\in I\}}\neq\emptyset
. Therefore, there exists a homomorphism
k\colon\mathcal{F}(\overline{x})\to[0,1]
such that
k(\uprho(s_{i}))=0
for every
i\in I
. We get
[0,1],k\circ\uprho_{\restriction\overline{x}}\models\Sigma
, showing that the theory
\Sigma(\overline{x})
is satisfiable.
∎

Lemma 3.3

(Local Deduction Theorem)
.

Let
\Sigma(\overline{x})
be a theory, and
s(\overline{x}),t(\overline{x})
two terms such that
\Sigma\cup\{s\approx 1\}\vDash_{\Delta}t\approx 1
. Then there is a
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\Sigma\vDash_{\Delta}(s^{k}\to t)\approx 1
.

Proof.

The same proof as for Łukasiewicz logic (cf.
[
11
, Propositions 4.2.9 and 4.6.4]
), hinging on the ideal theory in MV-algebras, applies here mutatis mutandis.
We spell out the details for the sake of completeness. Assume without loss of generality that

\Sigma(\overline{x})=\{u_{i}\approx 0\mid i\in I\}

for some set of terms
\{u_{i}(\overline{x})\mid i\in I\}
and let
h\colon\mathcal{F}(\overline{x})\to[0,1]
be any homomorphism such that
h(\uprho(s))=0
and
h(\uprho(u_{i}))=1
for all
i\in I
.
Then,

[0,1],h\circ\uprho_{\restriction\overline{x}}\models\Sigma\cup\{s\approx 1\}

and, because
\Sigma\cup\{s\approx 1\}\vDash_{\Delta}t\approx 1
, we see that
[0,1],h\circ\uprho_{\restriction\overline{x}}\models t\approx 1
. In other words,
h(\uprho(t))=1
. Applying the involution
\neg
, we obtain

\V(\{\uprho(u_{i})\mid i\in I\}\cup\{\neg\uprho(s)\})\subseteq\V(\neg\uprho(t)).

In view of Lemma

,
\neg\uprho(t)
belongs to the ideal of
\mathcal{F}(\overline{x})
generated by
{\{\uprho(u_{i})\mid i\in I\}}\cup\{\neg\uprho(s)\}
. By equation (
1
), there exist
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}\in\{\uprho(u_{i})\mid i\in I\}
and a

such that

\displaystyle\leqslant\varphi_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\varphi_{n}\oplus k(\neg\uprho(s))

\displaystyle=\varphi_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\varphi_{n}\oplus\neg(\uprho(s)^{k})

\displaystyle=\uprho(s)^{k}\to(\varphi_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\varphi_{n}),

\uprho(s)^{k}\odot\neg\uprho(t)\leqslant\varphi_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\varphi_{n}

belongs to the ideal of

, and

\uprho(s)^{k}\to\uprho(t)=\neg\uprho(s)^{k}\oplus\uprho(t)=\neg(\uprho(s)^{k}\odot\neg\uprho(t))\in g^{-1}(1).

We conclude that

\widetilde{f}(s^{k}\to t)=(g\circ\uprho)(s^{k}\to t)=g(\uprho(s)^{k}\to\uprho(t))=1,

i.e.
A,f\models(s^{k}\to t)\approx 1

to
h(\uprho(x))

\nu
is clearly a bijection. Further, for any
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}\in[0,1]

\displaystyle\nu^{-1}([\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}])

\displaystyle=\{h\in\Max{\mathcal{F}(x)}\mid\epsilon\leqslant h(\uprho(x))\leqslant\epsilon^{\prime}\}

which is a closed subset of

We now state and prove a Robinson’s Joint Consistency Theorem for the logic
\vDash_{\Delta}
, which will allow us to prove a useful interpolation result in Corollary

below. (In fact, the desired interpolation result follows from the special case of the Joint Consistency Theorem where the two theories share the same set of propositional variables.)

Theorem 3.5
.

For any two theories
\Sigma_{1}(\overline{x},\overline{y}),\Sigma_{2}(\overline{y},\overline{z})
, the union
\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}
is satisfiable if, and only if, there is no term
s(\overline{y})
such that
\Sigma_{1}\vDash_{\Delta}s\approx 0
and
\Sigma_{2}\vDash_{\Delta}s\approx 1
.

Proof.

If either
\Sigma_{1}
or
\Sigma_{2}
are unsatisfiable, there is nothing to prove. Hence, assume
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}
are satisfiable.
Clearly, if the union
\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}
is satisfiable, there is no term
s(\overline{y})
satisfying
\Sigma_{1}\vDash_{\Delta}s\approx 0
and
\Sigma_{2}\vDash_{\Delta}s\approx 1
. Hence, suppose that
\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}
is unsatisfiable.

Let us assume first that
\overline{x}=\overline{z}=\emptyset
, and so
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
are theories in the variables
\overline{y}
.
By Lemma
3.1
, there exists a finite subset
\{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}\}\subseteq\Sigma_{2}
such that
\Sigma_{1}\cup\{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}\}
is unsatisfiable. Suppose that each equation
\sigma_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, is of the form
t_{i}\approx 1
for some term
t_{i}(\overline{y})
, and define the term

u(\overline{y})\coloneqq t_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge t_{n}.

Then
\Sigma_{1}\cup\{u\approx 1\}\vDash_{\Delta}0\approx 1
.
In view of Lemma
3.3
, there exists a
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\Sigma_{1}\vDash_{\Delta}(u^{k}\to 0)\approx 1
, that is
\Sigma_{1}\vDash_{\Delta}\neg(u^{k})\approx 1
. Therefore,
s\coloneqq u^{k}
satisfies
\Sigma_{1}\vDash_{\Delta}s\approx 0
. Further,
\Sigma_{2}\vDash_{\Delta}u\approx 1
entails
\Sigma_{2}\vDash_{\Delta}s\approx 1
.

For the general case, let us assume without loss of generality that

\Sigma_{1}=\{s_{i}(\overline{x},\overline{y})\approx 0\mid i\in I\}\ \text{ and }\ \Sigma_{1}=\{t_{j}(\overline{y},\overline{z})\approx 0\mid j\in J\}.

Let
S_{1}\coloneqq\{\uprho(s_{i})\mid i\in I\}\subseteq\mathcal{F}(\overline{x},\overline{y})
and
S_{2}\coloneqq\{\uprho(t_{j})\mid j\in J\}\subseteq\mathcal{F}(\overline{y},\overline{z})
. By Proposition
3.4
,
\V(S_{1})
and
\V(S_{2})
can be identified with closed subsets of the Tychonoff cubes

and
[0,1]^{\overline{y},\overline{z}}
, respectively. When
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
are regarded as theories in the variables
\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z}
, the corresponding closed subsets of
[0,1]^{\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z}}
are the cylinders
\V(S_{1})\times[0,1]^{\overline{z}}
and
[0,1]^{\overline{x}}\times\V(S_{2})
. As
\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}
is unsatisfiable, these two cylinders are disjoint. But then

\pi_{\overline{y}}(\V(S_{1})\times[0,1]^{\overline{z}})\cap\pi_{\overline{y}}([0,1]^{\overline{x}}\times\V(S_{2}))=\emptyset,

where
\pi_{\overline{y}}\colon[0,1]^{\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z}}\twoheadrightarrow[0,1]^{\overline{y}}
is the projection. Just observe that, if

is both in
\pi_{\overline{y}}(\V(S_{1})\times[0,1]^{\overline{z}})
and
\pi_{\overline{y}}([0,1]^{\overline{x}}\times\V(S_{2}))
, then there exist
a\in[0,1]^{\overline{x}}
and

such that
\{(a,b)\}\times[0,1]^{\overline{z}}\subseteq\V(S_{1})\times[0,1]^{\overline{z}}
and
[0,1]^{\overline{x}}\times\{(b,c)\}\subseteq[0,1]^{\overline{x}}\times\V(S_{2})
. So,

is in the intersection of the cylinders
\V(S_{1})\times[0,1]^{\overline{z}}
and
[0,1]^{\overline{x}}\times\V(S_{2})
, a contradiction.

Now, since
\pi_{\overline{y}}(\V(S_{1})\times[0,1]^{\overline{z}})
is a closed subset of
[0,1]^{\overline{y}}
, there exists a set

such that
\pi_{\overline{y}}(\V(S_{1})\times[0,1]^{\overline{z}})=\V(T_{1})
. Similarly,
\pi_{\overline{y}}([0,1]^{\overline{x}}\times\V(S_{2}))=\V(T_{2})
for some set

. Define the theories

\widetilde{\Sigma}_{1}(\overline{y})\coloneqq\{s^{\prime}(\overline{y})\approx 0\mid s^{\prime}\in\uprho^{-1}(T_{1})\}\ \text{ and }\ \widetilde{\Sigma}_{2}(\overline{y})\coloneqq\{t^{\prime}(\overline{y})\approx 0\mid t^{\prime}\in\uprho^{-1}(T_{2})\}.

Because

is unsatisfiable. By the first part of the proof, there exists a term
s(\overline{y})
such that
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{1}\vDash_{\Delta}s\approx 0
and
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{2}\vDash_{\Delta}s\approx 1

, it is not difficult to see that

for every

; similarly,
\Sigma_{2}\vDash_{\Delta}\sigma
for every

\Sigma_{1}\vDash_{\Delta}s\approx 0
and
\Sigma_{2}\vDash_{\Delta}s\approx 1
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Corollary 3.7

Let
A\in\Delta

Let
\overline{x}

4.
The Beth definability property

In this section we prove that the logic
\vDash_{\Delta}
has the Beth definability property, asserting that implicit definability is equivalent to explicit definability. We then derive the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem from the Beth definability property of
\vDash_{\Delta}
.

Definition 4.1
.

Consider a set of variables
\overline{x}
, a variable
y
not in
\overline{x}
, and a theory
\Sigma(\overline{x},y)
. For any variable
z
, write
\Sigma(\overline{x},z)
for the theory obtained from
\Sigma(\overline{x},y)
by replacing
y
by
z
. We say that
\Sigma

implicitly defines

y
over
\overline{x}
if, for every variable
z
,

\Sigma(\overline{x},y)\cup\Sigma(\overline{x},z)\vDash_{\Delta}y\approx z.

Further,
\Sigma

explicitly defines

y
over
\overline{x}
if there exists a term
s_{y}(\overline{x})
such that

\Sigma(\overline{x},y)\vDash_{\Delta}y\approx s_{y}.

The meaning of implicit definability is that any assignment
f\colon\overline{x}\to A
into an algebra
A\in\Delta
can be extended to at most one assignment
g\colon\overline{x},y\to A
satisfying
A,g\models\Sigma
. On the other hand, an explicit definition
s_{y}
of
y
witnesses the fact that the interpretation of
y
in a model of
\Sigma(\overline{x},y)
is completely determined by the interpretation of
\overline{x}
. Clearly, if
\Sigma
explicitly defines
y
over
\overline{x}
, then it implicitly defines
y
over
\overline{x}
. The Beth definability property states that the converse holds as well.

Definition 4.2
.

The logic
\vDash_{\Delta}
has the
Beth definability property
if, whenever a theory
\Sigma(\overline{x},y)
implicitly defines
y
over
\overline{x}
, then
\Sigma(\overline{x},y)
explicitly defines
y
over
\overline{x}
.

Remark 4.3
.

The definition of Beth definability property given above is the direct specialisation of the classical notion for first-order logic, see e.g.
[
10
, p. 90]
, obtained by replacing relation symbols by propositional variables. This definition, employed e.g. in
[
16
, §5.6]
or
[
25
]
, corresponds to the
singleton
Beth property in abstract algebraic logic
[
18
]
.

Following Proposition
3.4
, throughout this section we identify a Tychonoff cube
[0,1]^{\overline{x}}
with the maximal spectrum
\Max{\mathcal{F}(\overline{x})}
. In fact, it follows from the proof of this proposition that the map
\Max{\mathcal{F}(\overline{x})}\to[0,1]^{\overline{x}}
sending a homomorphism
h\colon\mathcal{F}(\overline{x})\to[0,1]
to
h\circ\uprho_{\restriction\overline{x}}\colon\overline{x}\to[0,1]
is a homeomorphism.

A particular class of implicit definitions is obtained as follows. Fix a set
\overline{x}
, a closed subset
X\subseteq[0,1]^{\overline{x}}
, and a continuous function
\mathfrak{f}\colon X\to[0,1]
. If
y
is a variable not in
\overline{x}
, then the graph of
\mathfrak{f}
can be identified with a closed subset of
[0,1]^{\overline{x},y}\cong\Max{\mathcal{F}(\overline{x},y)}
. Hence, by Lemma
2.4
(b)
, the graph of
\mathfrak{f}
is homeomorphic to
\V(J_{\mathfrak{f}})
for some ideal
J_{\mathfrak{f}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}(\overline{x},y)
. Define the theory

(9)

\Sigma_{\mathfrak{f}}(\overline{x},y)\coloneqq\{s(\overline{x},y)\approx 0\mid s\in\uprho^{-1}(J_{\mathfrak{f}})\},

i.e.
\Sigma_{\mathfrak{f}}\coloneqq\Sigma\llbracket J_{\mathfrak{f}}\rrbracket
with the notation in (
8
). Note that an assignment
g\colon\overline{x},y\to[0,1]
satisfies
[0,1],g\models\Sigma_{\mathfrak{f}}
if, and only if, it lies on the graph of
\mathfrak{f}
when regarded as a point of
[0,1]^{\overline{x},y}
. Thus,
\Sigma_{\mathfrak{f}}
implicitly defines
y
over
\overline{x}
because the graph of
\mathfrak{f}
is a functional relation:

Lemma 4.4
.

The theory
\Sigma_{\mathfrak{f}}
implicitly defines
y
over
\overline{x}
.

Proof.

Consider an assignment
f\colon\overline{x}\to A
with
A\in\Delta
, and assume that
g\colon\overline{x},y\to A
is an assignment extending
f
and satisfying
A,g\models\Sigma_{\mathfrak{f}}
. We show that
g
is the only assignment of the variables
\overline{x},y
with these properties.

If
A
is the trivial algebra, then this is clearly true. Hence, let us suppose that
A
is non-trivial. We can assume without loss of generality that
A=[0,1]
.
If
g^{\prime}\colon\overline{x},y\to[0,1]
is another assignment extending
f
and satisfying
[0,1],g^{\prime}\models\Sigma_{\mathfrak{f}}
, and
\pi\colon[0,1]^{\overline{x},y}\twoheadrightarrow[0,1]^{\overline{x}}
is the projection map, we get
\pi(g)=\pi(g^{\prime})
because both
g
and
g^{\prime}
extend
f
. Therefore, since
g
and
g^{\prime}
belong to the graph of
\mathfrak{f}
, which is a functional relation, it must be
g=g^{\prime}
.

By definition, the theory

(Ł2)
. To conclude that
\alpha\in\Theta^{\vdash}
, it remains to prove that
\gamma\in\Theta^{\vdash}
.

If
\widetilde{\beta}_{i}
is the formula associated with
t_{\beta_{i}}
, then it is not difficult to see that
\vdash_{\Delta}\beta_{i}\to\widetilde{\beta}_{i}
, and so
\widetilde{\beta}_{i}\in\Theta^{\vdash}
for all
i\in\omega
. Hence, by the
\delta
-rule, also
\delta(\langle\widetilde{\beta}_{i}\rangle)\in\Theta^{\vdash}
. Now, for any two terms
s,t
corresponding to formulas
\alpha_{s}
and
\alpha_{t}
, respectively, the term
s\odot t
corresponds to the formula
\neg(\neg\neg\alpha_{s}\to\neg\alpha_{t})
. We claim that
\neg(\neg\neg\alpha_{s}\to\neg\alpha_{t})\in\Theta^{\vdash}
whenever
\alpha_{s},\alpha_{t}\in\Theta^{\vdash}
. An easy inductive argument then shows that
\gamma\in\Theta^{\vdash}
.

Suppose that
\alpha_{s},\alpha_{t}\in\Theta^{\vdash}
and note that, for any
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbf{Form}
,

(13)

\displaystyle\vdash_{\Delta}(\alpha\to(\beta\to\gamma))\to(\beta\to(\alpha\to\gamma))

(14)

\displaystyle\vdash_{\Delta}(\alpha\to\neg\beta)\to(\beta\to\neg\alpha).

(The same proof as for Łukasiewicz logic applies here, mutatis mutandis, cf.
[
11
, Proposition 4.3.4]
.) Equation (
13
), together with
\vdash_{\Delta}(\neg\neg\alpha\to\neg\beta)\to(\neg\neg\alpha\to\neg\beta)
, entails
\vdash_{\Delta}\neg\neg\alpha\to((\neg\neg\alpha\to\neg\beta)\to\neg\beta)
and so, by (
14
),
\vdash_{\Delta}\neg\neg\alpha\to(\beta\to\neg(\neg\neg\alpha\to\neg\beta))
. As
\vdash_{\Delta}\alpha\to\neg\neg\alpha
, we get

\vdash_{\Delta}\alpha\to(\beta\to\neg(\neg\neg\alpha\to\neg\beta)).

Therefore,
\alpha_{s},\alpha_{t}\in\Theta^{\vdash}
entails
\neg(\neg\neg\alpha_{s}\to\neg\alpha_{t})\in\Theta^{\vdash}
.

Now, for the general case, let
\Theta=\{\beta_{i}\mid i\in I\}
be an arbitrary set of formulas. We claim that there exists a countable subset
\widetilde{\Theta}\subseteq\Theta
such that
\Theta^{\vDash}\subseteq\widetilde{\Theta}^{\vDash}
. It then follows from the argument above that

\Theta^{\vDash}\subseteq\widetilde{\Theta}^{\vDash}\subseteq\widetilde{\Theta}^{\vdash}\subseteq\Theta^{\vdash}.

Consider the set

S\coloneqq\V(\{\neg\uprho(t_{\beta_{i}})\mid i\in I\})\subseteq\Max{\mathcal{F}(\overline{x})}

where
\overline{x}\coloneqq\mathbf{Var}
is the countable set of propositional variables from which the formulas of
\vdash_{\Delta}
are built. By Proposition
3.4
,
S
can be identified with a closed subset of the Tychonoff cube
[0,1]^{\overline{x}}
. Setting
S_{i}\coloneqq\V(\neg\uprho(t_{\beta_{i}}))
for each
i\in I
, we get
S=\bigcap_{i\in I}{S_{i}}
.
Recall that any second-countable space is Lindelöf (see e.g.
[
37
, Theorem 16.9]
) and any subspace of a second-countable space is also second-countable, so any subspace of a second-countable space is Lindelöf. In particular, since
[0,1]^{\overline{x}}
is second-countable, the complement
S^{\complement}
of
S
is Lindelöf. Hence there exists a countable subset
J\subseteq I
such that
S^{\complement}\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in J}{S_{i}^{\complement}}
, and so

(15)

\bigcap_{i\in J}{S_{i}}\subseteq S.

Note that the points of
S
(respectively, of
\bigcap_{i\in J}{S_{i}}
) are in bijection with the assignments
f\colon\overline{x}\to[0,1]
that satisfy
[0,1],f\models t_{\beta_{i}}\approx 1
for all
i\in I
(respectively, for all
i\in J
). Thus, equation (
15
) entails that
\{t_{\beta_{i}}\approx 1\mid i\in J\}\vDash_{\Delta}\{t_{\beta_{i}}\approx 1\mid i\in I\}
. Upon setting
\widetilde{\Theta}\coloneqq\{\beta_{i}\mid i\in J\}
, an application of Lemma
5.1
(a)
yields
\Theta^{\vDash}\subseteq\widetilde{\Theta}^{\vDash}
.
∎

Let us remark that Łukasiewicz logic
\L_{\infty}
is complete—but
not
strongly complete—with respect to its usual (Bolzano-Tarski)
[0,1]
-valued semantics. This problem can be rectified by considering a different notion of model for sets of
\L_{\infty}
-formulas, giving rise to a
differential semantics
of Łukasiewicz logic
[
31
]
. This contrasts with Theorem
5.4
, which establishes the strong completeness of
\vdash_{\Delta}
with respect to its
[0,1]
-valued semantics.

Remark 5.5
.

Although we have defined formulas starting from a countably infinite set of propositional variables, the results in this section can be generalised to uncountable sets of variables. The proof of the Completeness theorem remains unchanged, as a single formula
\alpha
can only use countably many variables. We briefly indicate how to adapt the proof of the Strong Completeness theorem. Let
\Theta=\{\beta_{i}\mid i\in I\}
be any set of formulas with variables in a (possibly uncountable) set
\overline{y}
. For the interesting direction, we must prove that for any formula
\alpha
with variables in
\overline{y}
,
\alpha\in\Theta^{\vDash}
entails
\alpha\in\Theta^{\vdash}
. Suppose that
\alpha\in\Theta^{\vDash}
. It suffices to show that
\alpha\in\widetilde{\Theta}^{\vDash}
for a countable subset
\widetilde{\Theta}\subseteq\Theta
, for then the set of variables appearing in either
\alpha
or one of the formulas in
\widetilde{\Theta}
is countable, and so Theorem
5.4
yields
\alpha\in\widetilde{\Theta}^{\vdash}\subseteq\Theta^{\vdash}
.

Denote by
\overline{x}
the countable subset of
\overline{y}
consisting of the variables appearing in
\alpha
, and let
\pi\colon[0,1]^{\overline{y}}\twoheadrightarrow[0,1]^{\overline{x}}
be the projection map. Set
S_{i}\coloneqq\V(\neg\uprho(t_{\beta_{i}}))\subseteq[0,1]^{\overline{y}}
for each
i\in I
, and
T\coloneqq\V(\neg\uprho(t_{\alpha}))\subseteq[0,1]^{\overline{x}}
. Reasoning as in the proof of the Strong Completeness theorem, we see that
\alpha\in\Theta^{\vDash}
implies

\bigcap_{i\in I}{S_{i}}\subseteq\pi^{-1}(T).

Because
[0,1]^{\overline{x}}
is second-countable,
T^{\complement}
is Lindelöf. By
[
13
, Theorem 3.8.8]
, the preimage
\pi^{-1}(T^{\complement})=\pi^{-1}(T)^{\complement}
is also Lindelöf. Hence, there exists a countable subset
J\subseteq I
such that
\pi^{-1}(T)^{\complement}\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in J}{S_{i}^{\complement}}
, and so

\bigcap_{i\in J}{S_{i}}\subseteq\pi^{-1}(T).

It follows easily that
\alpha\in\widetilde{\Theta}^{\vDash}
where
\widetilde{\Theta}\coloneqq\{\beta_{i}\mid i\in J\}
, as desired.

6.
Conclusion

In
[
35
, p. 467]
, Stone observes that the proof of Weierstrass’ Approximation Theorem can be divided into two parts. The first part, of which he provides a generalisation, can be regarded, in his words, as “algebraico-topological”, while he refers to the second part as the “analytical kernel” of the proof. On the other hand, Banaschewski showed in
[
1
]
that even this analytical kernel can be proved by algebraic means in the setting of
f
-rings. In a sense, Banaschewski fully brought out the
algebraic
content of the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem, thus concluding a process started by Stone himself.

In this paper, we have exposed the
logical
content of the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem by showing that it can be ultimately regarded as an equivalent form of the Beth definability property of the logic
\vDash_{\Delta}
(see Remark
4.10
).
In the same spirit, other properties of compact Hausdorff spaces admit a translation into properties of the logic
\vDash_{\Delta}
, and vice versa. For instance, the Joint Consistency Theorem for
\vDash_{\Delta}
yields a Urysohn’s Lemma for Tychonoff cubes (Remark
3.6
), and it is not difficult to see that the fact that any continuous map between compact Hausdorff spaces is closed implies the deductive interpolation property of
\vDash_{\Delta}
(in fact, even the right uniform deductive interpolation property
[
15
]
).
