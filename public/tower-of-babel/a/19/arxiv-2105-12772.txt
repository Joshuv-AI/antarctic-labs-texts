Residually finite lattices in PU ⁡ ( 2 , 1 ) ~ \widetilde{\PU(2,1)} and fundamental groups of smooth projective surfaces
Matthew Stover, Domingo Toledo

Abstract
This paper studies residual finiteness of lattices in the universal cover of PU ⁡ ( 2 , 1 ) \PU(2,1) and applications to the existence of smooth projective varieties with fundamental group a cocompact lattice in PU ⁡ ( 2 , 1 ) \PU(2,1) or a finite covering of it. First, we prove that certain lattices in the universal cover of PU ⁡ ( 2 , 1 ) \PU(2,1) are residually finite. To our knowledge, these are the first such examples. We then use residually finite central extensions of torsion-free lattices in PU ⁡ ( 2 , 1 ) \PU(2,1) to construct smooth projective surfaces that are not birationally equivalent to a smooth compact ball quotient but whose fundamental group is a torsion-free cocompact lattice in PU ⁡ ( 2 , 1 ) \PU(2,1) .



To Gopal Prasad in celebration of his 75th birthday

Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to study residual finiteness of lattices in the universal cover of
\PU(2,1)
and applications to the existence of smooth projective varieties with fundamental group a cocompact lattice in
\PU(2,1)
or a finite covering of it. This follows a general theme used by the second author
[
53
]
, and in a different way by Nori (unpublished) and Catanese–Kollár
[
1
]
, to build smooth projective varieties with fundamental group that is not residually finite. First, we prove that certain lattices in the universal cover of
\PU(2,1)
are residually finite. To our knowledge, these are the first such examples. We then use residually finite central extensions of torsion-free lattices in
\PU(2,1)
to construct smooth projective surfaces that are not birationally equivalent to a smooth compact ball quotient but whose fundamental group is a torsion-free cocompact lattice in
\PU(2,1)
.

Let
G
be an adjoint simple Lie group of hermitian type. Then
\mathbb{Z}\leq\pi_{1}(G)
, and the universal cover
\widetilde{G}
of

fits in a central extension

1\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow\widetilde{G}\longrightarrow\overline{G}\longrightarrow 1

for a certain finite cover
\overline{G}
of

[
35
]
that any lattice
\Gamma<G
is residually finite. However,
\widetilde{G}
is not linear and so it is not clear whether or not the preimage
\widetilde{\Gamma}
of
\Gamma
in
\widetilde{G}
is residually finite. When
\Gamma
is an arithmetic subgroup of an absolutely simple, simply connected algebraic group
\mathbf{G}
defined over a number field
K
and
\Gamma
has the congruence subgroup property, Deligne
[
14
]
showed that the preimage
\widetilde{\Gamma}
of
\Gamma
in
\widetilde{G}=\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbb{R})
need not be residually finite. If

is a generator for
\mathbb{Z}\leq\pi_{1}(\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R}))<\widetilde{G}
and

is the order of the
absolute metaplectic kernel

, Deligne proved that

maps trivially to every finite quotient of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
. See work of Prasad and Rapinchuk
[
39

for a precise description of the connection between residual finiteness of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
and

and for a complete description of

. One then sees that the preimage of
\Gamma
in any connected
d

\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
with

also cannot be residually finite.

There are many known examples of lattices in
\PU(n,1)
without the congruence subgroup property, and it is widely expected to fail for all such lattices. Consequently, the following question seems interesting in contrast with the situation in higher-rank.

Question 1

Let

We briefly recall that

denote complex hyperbolic

\widehat{\Gamma}\backslash\SU(n,1)/\SU(n),

where

We also note another motivation for Question
1
from geometric group theory. If there is a cocompact lattice

in
\widetilde{G}
that is not residually finite, then, as in Deligne’s work
[

]
, one could find a lattice in a finite cover of
\PU(n,1)
that is not residually finite. This would be a finite central extension of a cocompact lattice in
\PU(n,1)

It is known in the case

that Question

has a positive answer. In other words, lattices in the universal cover of
\PU(1,1)\cong\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
are both residually finite and linear. Very briefly, the most well-known proof goes as follows. First, passing to a subgroup of finite index, we can assume that
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is the fundamental group of the unit tangent bundle of a hyperbolic surface. Either geometrically or using a presentation for
\widetilde{\Gamma}
, one can produce a homomorphism from
\widetilde{\Gamma}
to a two-step nilpotent group (in fact, an integer Heisenberg group) for which the restriction to the center of

is also residually finite. See

The first aim of this paper is to construct the first examples of lattices in

2
Residual finiteness

Here, we collect some basic facts about residual finiteness. Recall that a group
\Gamma
is residually finite if, given any nontrivial
\gamma\in\Gamma
, there exists a finite group
F
and a homomorphism
\rho:\Gamma\to F
so that
\rho(\gamma)
is not the identity element of
F
. Finitely generated linear groups are residually finite by a famous theorem of Malcev
[
35
]
(also see
[
34
, §2.1]
). We begin with a basic and well-known fact about residual finiteness; see for instance
[
34
, Lem. 2.1.3]
for a proof.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
\Gamma
is a finitely generated group and
\Delta\leq\Gamma
is a finite index subgroup. Then
\Gamma
is residually finite if and only if
\Delta
is residually finite.

We will also need the following simple lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
n\geq 2
, suppose that

1\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}/n\longrightarrow\Gamma_{n}\overset{\pi_{n}}{\longrightarrow}\Gamma\longrightarrow 1

is a central exact sequence. If
\Gamma_{n}
is residually finite, then there exists a finite index subgroup
\Gamma^{\prime}\leq\Gamma
that admits a section to
\Gamma_{n}
under
\pi_{n}
. Consequently,
\Gamma_{n}
has a finite index subgroup that is isomorphic to a finite index subgroup of
\Gamma
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\Gamma_{n}
is residually finite and that
\sigma
is a generator for
\ker(\pi_{n})
. For each
1\leq j\leq n-1
there exists a finite group
F_{j}
and a homomorphism
\rho_{j}:\Gamma_{n}\to F_{j}
so that
\rho(\sigma^{j})
is nontrivial. The product homomorphism

\rho=\left(\rho_{1}\times\cdots\times\rho_{n-1}\right):\Gamma_{n}\longrightarrow\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}F_{j}

then has the property that
\rho(\sigma^{j})
is nontrivial for each
1\leq j\leq n-1
. If
\Gamma^{\prime}=\ker(\rho)
, then
\Gamma^{\prime}\cap\langle\sigma\rangle=\{1\}
. Since
\langle\sigma\rangle=\ker(\pi_{n})
, we have that
\pi_{n}|_{\Gamma^{\prime}}
is an isomorphism onto its image. This proves the lemma.
∎

Corollary 2.3
.

For any
n\geq 1
, let
\Gamma<\SU(n,1)
be a finitely generated subgroup. Then there is a finite index subgroup
\Gamma^{\prime}\leq\Gamma
so that projection
\SU(n,1)\to\PU(n,1)
restricts to an isomorphism on
\Gamma^{\prime}
. In particular, every lattice in
\SU(n,1)
contains a finite index subgroup isomorphic to a lattice in
\PU(n,1)
.

Proof.

Since
\SU(n,1)
is linear,
\Gamma
is residually finite. Let
\sigma
be a generator for the intersection of
\Gamma
with the center of
\SU(n,1)
. The center of
\SU(n,1)
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/(n+1)
, so
\langle\sigma\rangle
is cyclic. Therefore if
\overline{\Gamma}
denotes the image of
\Gamma
in
\PU(n,1)
, we have a central exact sequence

1\longrightarrow\langle\sigma\rangle\longrightarrow\Gamma\longrightarrow\overline{\Gamma}\longrightarrow 1

to which Lemma
2.2
applies, giving the desired
\Gamma^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that
\Gamma
is a residually finite group and that

1\longrightarrow Z\longrightarrow\widetilde{\Gamma}\longrightarrow\Gamma\longrightarrow 1

is an extension. If there is a homomorphism
\rho:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to H
onto a residually finite group
H
so that
\rho|_{Z}
is injective, then
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is residually finite.

Proof.

Given a nontrivial element
\alpha\in\widetilde{\Gamma}
, if the projection
\overline{\alpha}
of
\alpha
to
\Gamma
is nontrivial, then there is a map from
\Gamma
to a finite group where the image of
\overline{\alpha}
is nontrivial, hence there is a homomorphism from
\widetilde{\Gamma}
to a finite group so that the image of
\alpha
is nontrivial. If
\overline{\alpha}
is trivial, then
\alpha\in Z
. Then
\rho(\alpha)\in H
is nontrivial, and there is a finite quotient of
H
, which we consider as a finite quotient of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
, where the image of
\alpha
is nontrivial. This proves that
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is residually finite.
∎

Finitely generated nilpotent groups are known to be residually finite. By Lemma
2.1
, this follows from the fact that finitely generated nilpotent groups contain a torsion-free subgroup of finite index
[
27
, Thm. 3.21]
combined with Malcev’s famous results that finitely generated torsion-free nilpotent groups are linear
[
36
]
and that linear groups are residually finite
[
35
]
. The following is then a special case of Lemma
2.4
.

Corollary 2.5
.

Suppose that
\Gamma
,
\widetilde{\Gamma}
, and
Z
are as in Lemma
2.4
. If
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is finitely generated and there is a nilpotent quotient
\mathcal{N}
of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
so that
Z
injects into
\mathcal{N}
, then
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is residually finite.

We return to the case of interest for this paper, namely where
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is a central extension of a finitely generated residually finite group
\Gamma
by
\mathbb{Z}
. Corollary
2.5
implies that to prove
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is residually finite, it suffices to find a nilpotent quotient of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
into which
\mathbb{Z}
injects. We also want to study the central exact sequence obtained by reducing the central element of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
modulo an integer
d
. It is not immediately clear that residual finiteness of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
implies residual finiteness of each
\mathbb{Z}/d
central extension, only infinitely many of the extensions. Using separability properties of finitely generated nilpotent groups, we can prove the stronger statement:

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose that
\Gamma
is a finitely generated residually finite group and that

1\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow\widetilde{\Gamma}\longrightarrow\Gamma\longrightarrow 1

is a central exact sequence associated with
\phi\in H^{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
. For
d\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\phi_{d}\in H^{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}/d)
be the reduction of
\phi
modulo
d
and

1\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}/d\longrightarrow\Gamma_{d}\longrightarrow\Gamma\longrightarrow 1

be the associated central exact sequence given by reducing the kernel
\langle\sigma\rangle
of
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
modulo
d
. Suppose that there is a nilpotent quotient
\mathcal{N}
of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
that is injective on
\langle\sigma\rangle
. Then
\Gamma_{d}
is residually finite for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be a generator for the kernel of
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
and
\rho:\Gamma\to\mathcal{N}
be a nilpotent quotient such that
S=\langle\rho(\sigma)\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}
. Since
\sigma
is central in
\widetilde{\Gamma}
,
\rho(\sigma)
is central in
\mathcal{N}
. In particular,
S_{d}=\langle\rho(\sigma^{d})\rangle
is a normal subgroup of
\mathcal{N}
for every natural number
d
.

Malcev proved that finitely generated nilpotent groups are
subgroup separable

[
37
]
, i.e., for every subgroup
H\leq\mathcal{N}
, there is a collection
\{\mathcal{N}_{i}\}
of finite index subgroups of
\mathcal{N}
so that
H=\bigcap\mathcal{N}_{i}
. See
[
34
, §2.3]
for more basic facts about subgroup separability. Note that generally a group is subgroup separable if this condition holds for all
finitely generated
subgroups
H
, but finitely generated nilpotent groups are Noetherian, hence all subgroups are finitely generated.

By
[
42
, Lem. 4.6]
, for each
d\in\mathbb{N}
we can find a finite index subgroup
\mathcal{N}_{d}
of
\mathcal{N}
so that
\mathcal{N}_{d}\cap S=S_{d}
. Since
S_{d}
is normal in
\mathcal{N}
, we can replace
\mathcal{N}_{d}
by the intersection of all its conjugates in
\mathcal{N}
and assume that
\mathcal{N}_{d}
is normal in
\mathcal{N}
. Then
F_{d}=\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{N}_{d}
is a finite quotient of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
such that the image of
\langle\sigma\rangle
in
F_{d}
is isomorphic to
S/(S\cap\mathcal{N}_{d})\cong\mathbb{Z}/d
. This means that
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to F_{d}
factors through the projection
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma_{d}
, and moreover that
\Gamma_{d}\to F_{d}
is injective on the central
\mathbb{Z}/d
quotient of
\langle\sigma\rangle
. Corollary
2.5
then implies that
\Gamma_{d}
is residually finite, which completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

3
Structure of
\SU(n,1)
and its universal cover

This section gives precise coordinates, including an Iwasawa decomposition, for
\SU(n,1)
and its universal cover. These coordinates will be used to describe a concrete procedure for lifting finitely presented subgroups of
\SU(n,1)
to
\widetilde{\SU(n,1)}
. Lastly, we study a certain homogeneous space for each group that will be fundamental for this lifting procedure. We start with some preliminaries on the universal cover
\widetilde{\U(n)}
of the unitary group
\U(n)
.

3.1
Coordinates on
\widetilde{\U(n)}

The universal cover
\widetilde{\U(n)}
of
\U(n)
is identified with
\SU(n)\times\mathbb{R}
under the universal covering map

(g,t)\mapsto e^{\pi it}g.

Composing this with the isomorphism
g\mapsto(g,\det(g)^{-1})
between
\U(n)
and

Coordinates on

Consider

With notation as above,

First, note that

∎

4
Lifting presentations

In this section, fix a finitely presented group
\Gamma<G=\SU(n,1)
. We assume for simplicity that
\Gamma
contains the center
Z(G)\cong\mathbb{Z}/(n+1)
of
G
, which is always the case when
\Gamma
is the pullback to
\SU(n,1)
of a lattice in
\PU(n,1)
, and let
\widehat{z}
be a generator for
Z(G)
. Without loss of generality, we can assume
\widehat{z}
is a generator for
\Gamma
, and so
\Gamma
is given by generators and relations

\Gamma=\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{d},\widehat{z}~|~\mathcal{R}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{R}_{e}\rangle.

We now describe a procedure for presenting the central extension

1\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow\widetilde{\Gamma}\longrightarrow\Gamma\longrightarrow 1

obtained by pulling
\Gamma
back to the universal cover
\widetilde{G}
of
G
.

Lifting generators

Since
G
is connected, we can write
g_{i}=\exp_{G}(v_{i})
for some
v_{i}
in the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of
G
. This defines an embedded path:

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}:[0,1]

\displaystyle\longrightarrow G

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}(s)

\displaystyle=\exp_{G}(sv_{i})

Notice that
g_{i}^{-1}=\exp_{G}(-v_{i})
. Then

\displaystyle\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}:[0,1]

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\widetilde{G}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}(s)

\displaystyle=\exp_{\widetilde{G}}(sv_{i})

is an embedded path in
\widetilde{G}
with
\widetilde{g}_{i}=\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}(1)
a lift of
g_{i}
and
\widetilde{g}_{i}^{-1}=\exp_{\widetilde{G}}(-v_{i})
.

Let
z
be a generator for the center of
\widetilde{G}
that projects to
\widehat{z}\in G
. Then
z^{n+1}
is a generator for
\pi_{1}(G)
. Our generators for
\widetilde{\Gamma}
are
\widetilde{g}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{g}_{d}
and
z
.

Lifting relations

Now, let
\mathcal{R}_{i}
be a relation for
\Gamma
. There is no loss of generality in considering
\mathcal{R}_{i}
as being of the form

g_{i_{f}}^{\epsilon_{1,f}}\cdots g_{i_{1}}^{\epsilon_{i,1}}=I_{n+1}\in G

for
\epsilon_{i,j}\in\{\pm 1\}
and
f=f(i)
. This determines a piecewise-defined loop
\sigma_{i}:[0,1]\to G
based at the identity by

\sigma_{i}(s)=\begin{cases}\gamma_{i_{1}}(\epsilon_{i_{1}}fs)&s\in[0,\frac{1}{f}]\\
\gamma_{i_{j}}(\epsilon_{i,j}(fs-j+1))g_{i_{j-1}}^{\epsilon_{i,j-1}}\cdots g_{i_{1}}^{\epsilon_{i,1}}&s\in[\frac{j-1}{f},\frac{j}{f}]\\
\gamma_{i_{f}}(\epsilon_{i,f}(fs-f+1))g_{i_{f-1}}^{\epsilon_{i,f-1}}\cdots g_{i_{1}}^{\epsilon_{i,1}}&s\in[\frac{f-1}{f},1]\end{cases}

where
1\leq j\leq f-1
. Using Lemma
3.6
, we can compute the element
r_{i}=[\sigma_{i}]\in\mathbb{Z}\cong\pi_{1}(G)
defined by
\sigma_{i}
using the winding number of the loop
\pi_{n+1}(\sigma_{i}(s)z_{0})
around
0
in
\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\{0\}
. Notice that
\sigma_{1}(s)\in\SL_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})
, so the path
\sigma_{i}(s)z_{0}
is explicitly computed using matrix multiplication in
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
.

Uniqueness of path lifting implies that the lift of
\sigma_{i}
to
\widetilde{G}
based at the identity is given by
z^{(n+1)r_{i}}
for
r_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
the winding number of
\pi_{n+1}\circ\sigma_{i}
around
0\in\mathbb{C}
. Thus our lifted relations
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{i}
for
\widetilde{\Gamma}
are:

\widetilde{g}_{i_{f}}^{\epsilon_{1,f}}\cdots\widetilde{g}_{i_{1}}^{\epsilon_{i,1}}\widetilde{z}^{-(n+1)r_{i}}=1_{\widetilde{G}}.

This presents
\widetilde{\Gamma}
as the group with generators
\{\widetilde{g}_{i}\}
and relations
\{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{i}\}
, possibly with the additional relation that
z
is central.

Lemma 4.1
.

With notation as above,

\widetilde{\Gamma}=\langle\widetilde{g}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{g}_{d},z~|~\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{e},z~\mathrm{central}\rangle.

Proof.

Let
L
be the abstract group with the given presentation. The above shows that each relation for
L
holds in
\widetilde{\Gamma}
, hence we have a homomorphism
L\to\widetilde{\Gamma}
. On the other hand,
\langle z\rangle
is the kernel of the projection
L\to\Gamma
, which factors through
\widetilde{\Gamma}
. Thus, the kernel of
L\to\widetilde{\Gamma}
is contained in
\langle z\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}
. However,
\langle z\rangle
maps isomorphically to the center of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
, so

.
∎

5
Residual finiteness of central extensions and the cup product

Let
\Gamma
be the fundamental group of a Riemann surface
\Sigma
of genus
g\geq 2
. The fundamental group
\widetilde{\Gamma}
of its unit tangent bundle
T^{1}\Sigma
fits into a central exact sequence

1\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow\widetilde{\Gamma}\longrightarrow\Gamma\longrightarrow 1

whose Euler class
e\in H^{2}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
is the first Chern class of the canonical bundle. We can equivalently say that the Euler class of the bundle is a nonzero multiple of the Kähler form
\omega
on
\Sigma
. Lastly,
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is isomorphic to the preimage of
\Gamma<\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
in its universal cover
\widetilde{\PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

It is well-known that
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is residually finite, and the most common proof is to show that
\widetilde{\Gamma}
admits a homomorphism onto an integral Heisenberg group such that a generator for the center of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
maps to an element of infinite order. See
[
13
, §IV.48]
for details. It is not hard to prove by hand using reductions modulo primes, that integral Heisenberg groups are residually finite, and recall from §
2
that in fact all finitely generated nilpotent groups are residually finite. Since
\Gamma
is linear, and hence residually finite, residual finiteness of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
follows from Corollary
2.5
.

The purpose of this section is to present a broad generalization of this result, and its strategy of proof, based on a proposition that is perhaps best attributed to Sullivan (e.g., see
[
2
, Ch. 3, §1]
, in particular Remark 3.4). In what follows it will be convenient to adopt the convention that a
k
-step nilpotent group has length
at most

k
. Our most general result is the following.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
X
be a closed aspherical manifold with fundamental group
\Gamma
and

U(1)\longrightarrow Y\longrightarrow X

be a principal
\U(1)
bundle with Euler class
\omega\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
that has infinite additive order, equivalently, its image
\omega\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Q})
is not zero. If
\widetilde{\Gamma}=\pi_{1}(Y)
and
z
denotes a generator for
\pi_{1}(U(1))<\widetilde{\Gamma}
, then the image of
z
in the maximal two-step nilpotent quotient of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
has infinite order if and only if
\omega
is in the image of the map

c_{\mathbb{Q}}:\bigwedge\nolimits^{2}H^{1}(X,\mathbb{Q})\to H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Q})

given by evaluation of the cup product.

Remark 5.2
.

The aspherical assumption on
X
is only to conclude using the standard homotopy exact sequence that
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is indeed a central extension of
\Gamma
by
\mathbb{Z}
. Our methods also allow one to study the cases where any of our hypotheses on
X
and
Y
fail to hold, but we do not pursue these directions since this would take us too far afield from our applications to ball quotients.

Before embarking on the proof, we record some corollaries. The first explains why Theorem
5.1
is exactly what we need in order to implement the proof of residual finiteness of
\pi_{1}(T^{1}\Sigma)
for more general circle bundles over aspherical manifolds.

Corollary 5.3
.

With
X
,
Y
,
\Gamma
, and
\widetilde{\Gamma}
as in the statement of Theorem
5.1
, suppose that
\Gamma
is residually finite. Then residual finiteness of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
can be detected in a two-step nilpotent quotient of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
via Corollary
2.5
if and only if the Euler class of
Y\to X
is in the image of the cup product map
c_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Proof.

Since
\Gamma
is a finitely generated residually finite group, Corollary
2.5
says that it suffices find a nilpotent quotient of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
where the generator
z
for
\pi_{1}(\U(1))
maps to an element of infinite order. Taking the maximal two-step quotient of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
, the corollary then follows from Theorem
5.1
.
∎

The following is then a consequence of Theorem
5.1
and Corollary
2.5
.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
X
be an aspherical compact Kähler manifold with residually finite fundamental group for which the Kähler class
\omega\in H^{2}(X)
is in the image of the cup product map
\bigwedge\nolimits^{2}H^{1}(X,\mathbb{Q})\to H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Q})
. If
Y
is a principal
\U(1)
bundle over
X
with Euler class a nonzero multiple of
\omega
, then
\pi_{1}(Y)
is residually finite.

Proof.

The case where
X
is a Riemann surface of genus
g\geq 2
is the classical case explained at the beginning of this section. When
X
is a torus,
\pi_{1}(Y)
is a finitely generated nilpotent group, so again the result is known. If
X
has complex dimension
n\geq 2
, then
\omega^{n}\in H^{2n}(X,\mathbb{Q})
is nontrivial, hence
\omega
has infinite order in
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Q})
. Therefore, Corollary
5.3
applies to prove that
\pi_{1}(Y)
is residually finite.
∎

Note that Theorem
1.2
is the special case of Corollary
5.4
where
X
is a smooth compact ball quotient. Also recall that smooth compact ball quotients are smooth projective varieties, so the real cohomology class of the Kähler form is always (up to normalization, of course) rational.

Before proving Theorem
5.1
, we define some notation and give some preliminary results. To start, we only assume that
X
is a closed manifold with fundamental group
\Gamma
. Let
\Delta
denote the derived subgroup of
\Gamma
. The earliest reference we could find to the following fact was in work of Sullivan

51
]
, who commented that its proof follows from “a certain amount of soul searching classical algebraic topology”. Also see
[
2
, Ch. 3]
. We use a formulation given by Beauville
[
5
]
.

Proposition 5.5

(Cor. 1(2)
[
5
]
)
.

Let
X
be a connected space homotopic to a CW complex with fundamental group
\Gamma
and
\Delta
be the derived subgroup of

7.2
Finite maps to
\mathbb{P}^{N}

This section refines the well-known connection between very ample line bundles and maps to projective spaces. Critical for our purposes is that one can produce a
finite
map, and we include the proof of this fact for completeness.

Lemma 7.1
.

Suppose that
X
is an
n
-dimensional smooth projective variety and
\eta\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is the first Chern class of a very ample line bundle on
X
. Then there exists a finite map

\alpha:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}

so that
\alpha^{*}([H])=\eta
, where
[H]\in H^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathbb{Z})
is the Poincaré dual to a hyperplane.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{0}:X\to\mathbb{P}^{N}
,
N>n
, be an embedding of
X
in
\mathbb{P}^{N}
determined by a basis for the space of global sections of the very ample line bundle. Then
\alpha_{0}^{*}([H_{0}])=\eta
, where
[H_{0}]\in H^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{N},\mathbb{Z})
is the hyperplane class. See
[
22
, §1.4]
for a standard reference.

Now, choose
p_{0}\in\mathbb{P}^{N}
so that
p_{0}\notin\alpha_{0}(X)
and the generic hyperplane
H_{0}\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
though
p_{0}
is transverse to
\alpha_{0}(X)
. Then
\alpha_{0}^{-1}(H_{0})
is Poincaré dual to
\alpha_{0}^{*}([H_{0}])=\eta
. Let

\pi_{0}:\mathbb{P}^{N}\smallsetminus\{p_{0}\}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{N-1}

be projection centered at
p_{0}
and
\alpha_{1}=\pi_{0}\circ\alpha_{0}
. Then, by construction,
\alpha_{1}
is a finite map. Since
\pi_{0}^{-1}
determines a correspondence between hyperplanes in
\mathbb{P}^{N-1}
and hyperplanes in
\mathbb{P}^{N}
through
p_{0}
, we have that

\alpha_{1}^{-1}(H_{1})=\alpha_{0}^{-1}(H_{0})

for all hyperplanes
H_{1}\subset\mathbb{P}^{N-1}
, where
H_{0}=\pi_{0}^{-1}(H_{1})
. Thus, for a general
H_{1}\subset\mathbb{P}^{N-1}
, we see that
\alpha_{1}^{-1}(H_{1})
is Poincaré dual to
\eta
, i.e.,
\alpha_{1}^{*}([H_{1}])=\eta
.

We now iterate this procedure to obtain
\alpha_{j}:X\to\mathbb{P}^{N-j}
. As long as
N-j>n
, we can find a point
p_{j}\notin\alpha_{j}(X)
that allows us to construct
\alpha_{j+1}
. Note that the composition of finite maps is finite, so each
\alpha_{j}
is a finite map. This ends with
\alpha=\alpha_{N-n}:X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
, which is the desired map.
∎

must be nontrivial (in fact infinite by Remark

), completing the proof of both theorems except for the statement that

is not birational to any ball quotient.

Suppose

is a compact, smooth quotient.of
\mathbb{B}^{2}
. Observe that
Z
has no rational curves, that is, every holomorphic map

is constant. Note that the same is true for
M^{\prime}
, because, by construction,
M^{\prime}
has a finite map to a ball quotient

. We use the following fact (see
[

, Thm. VI.1.9.3]
):

Lemma 7.6
.

Suppose
X,Y
are smooth projective varieties, suppose
Y
has no rational curves, and
f:X\dashrightarrow Y
is a rational map. Then
f
is everywhere defined.

Finally, suppose
f:M^{\prime}\dashrightarrow Z
is a birational map. Applying Lemma
7.6
to
f
and
f^{-1}
we see that they are both everywhere defined, so
f
is biholomorphic, which is impossible. Thus
M^{\prime}
and
Z
cannot be birational. This completes the proofs of part 3 of Theorem
7.4
and Theorem
1.4
.

To give a concrete example of how one can use the methods of this section, we now prove Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be the fundamental group of a fake projective plane
X
whose canonical bundle

is divisible by three. The case

of Theorem
7.4
applied to the preimage
\widetilde{\Gamma}
of
\Gamma
in the universal cover of
\PU(2,1)
provides a smooth complex surface

whose fundamental group is the central
\mathbb{Z}/3
extension of
\Gamma
isomorphic to the preimage of
\Gamma
in
\SU(2,1)
. Our assumption on

implies that this lift splits, so
\pi_{1}(S)\cong\mathbb{Z}/3\times\Gamma
(e.g., see
[
32

).

It follows that there is a degree

with fundamental group

Remark 7.7
.

We briefly explain how one can determine the Chern numbers of the surfaces in Theorem
1.5
. Let
X
be a fake projective plane such that
K_{X}
is divisible by
3
. We need to replace
K_{X}
with a very ample line bundle with the same first Chern class as
K_{X}
modulo
3
. Using Reider’s theorem as in
[
9
, Thm. 2.1]
, one easily sees that
nK_{X}
is very ample for all

, and it has the same Chern class modulo
3
as
K_{X}
, since
K_{X}
is divisible by
3
. In fact, Catanese and Keum prove that one can take
2K_{X}
for many fake projective planes
[
9
, Thm. 1.1]
.

Proceeding with our construction, we take
S_{n}
to be the surface with fundamental group
\pi_{1}(X)\times\mathbb{Z}/3
built above. The construction of
S_{n}
as a fiber product implies that it is the
(\mathbb{Z}/3)^{2}
branched cover of
X
over three general members
D_{1},D_{2},D_{3}
of the linear system
|nK_{X}|
. Note that “general” here is only needed to ensure that the triple intersection is empty. We compute the genus of
D_{j}
by adjunction:

\displaystyle 2(g-1)

\displaystyle=K_{X}D_{j}+D_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=nK_{X}^{2}+n^{2}K_{X}^{2}

\displaystyle=9n(n+1)

Each
D_{j}
meets another of the curves
D_{k}
in
D_{j}D_{k}=9n^{2}
points, hence each
D_{j}
contains
18n^{2}
singular points and there are
27n^{2}
singular points in total.

Branching of

has order
3
over each nonsingular point of

and order
9
over the singular points. For example, following
[
4
, p. 16-17]
, we can then compute the Chern numbers of

by:

\displaystyle c_{1}^{2}(S_{n})

\displaystyle=9\left(K_{X}+3\left(1-\frac{1}{3}\right)nK_{X}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=27\,\left((2n+1)K_{X}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=243\,(2n+1)^{2}

\displaystyle c_{2}(S_{n})

\displaystyle=9\left(3-3\left(1-\frac{1}{3}\right)(-9n(n+1)-18n^{2})-27n^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{9}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=81\,(10n^{2}+6n+1)

Now, let
S_{n}^{\prime}
be the étale
\mathbb{Z}/3
cover of
S_{n}
with
\pi_{1}(S_{n}^{\prime})\cong\pi_{1}(X)<\pi_{1}(S_{n})
. Note that the Chern numbers of
S_{n}^{\prime}
are three times those of
S_{n}
. This determines a sequence of surfaces
S_{n}^{\prime}
,
n\geq 1
, where
S_{1}^{\prime}=X
, maybe
S_{2}^{\prime}
is not defined if

is not very ample, and for

we have:

c_{1}^{2}(S_{n}^{\prime})/c_{2}(S_{n}^{\prime})=\frac{3(2n+1)^{2}}{10n^{2}+6n+1}\overset{n\to\infty}{\xrightarrow{\hskip 19.91684pt}}\frac{6}{5}

Note that Troncoso and Urzúa
[

]
produce examples of surfaces

with
\pi_{1}(Y_{\alpha})\cong\pi_{1}(X)
and

dense in
[1,3]
, whereas we only obtain the limit point
1.2
.
