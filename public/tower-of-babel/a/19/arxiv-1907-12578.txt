Walls and asymptotics for Bridgeland stability conditions on threefolds
Marcos Jardim, Antony Maciocia

Abstract




Abstract.
We consider Bridgeland stability conditions for threefolds conjectured by Bayer–Macrì–Toda in the case of Picard rank
1
. We study the differential geometry of numerical walls, characterizing when they are bounded, discussing possible intersections and showing that they are essentially regular. Next, we prove that walls within a certain region of the upper half plane that parameterize geometric stability conditions must always intersect the curve given by the vanishing of the slope function and, for a fixed value of
s
, have a maximum turning point there. We then use these facts to prove that Gieseker semistability is equivalent to a strong form of asymptotic semistability along a class of paths in the upper half plane, and we show how to find large families of walls. We illustrate how to compute all of the walls and describe the Bridgeland moduli spaces for the Chern character
(2,0,-1,0)
on complex projective
3
-space in a suitable region of the upper half plane.

Keywords.
Bridgeland stability, 3-folds, walls, asymptotic stability, higher triangulations, instantons

1. Introduction

Bridgeland’s notion of stability conditions on triangulated categories, introduced in
[
Bri07
]
and
[
Bri08
]
, provides a new set of tools to study moduli spaces of sheaves on smooth projective varieties. Such tools have been successfully applied by many authors first to the study of sheaves on surfaces, see for example
[
AM16
,
AB13
,
BM14a
,
BM14b
,
Fey16
,
Fey17
,
FL21
,
MM13
,
YY14
]
, and more recently on threefolds (especially
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
); see for instance
[
GHS18
,
MS20
,
Sch20a
,
Schi20b
]
. One way to study moduli spaces of sheaves using Bridgeland stability spaces is to restrict attention to the so-called geometric stability conditions parameterized by (a subset of) the upper half plane
\mathbb{H}
. Once we know that the moduli space of Bridgeland-stable objects is asymptotically given by the Gieseker semistable moduli space along an unbounded path, we can try to locate all the points where the moduli space changes along this path (these isolated points are called walls) and compute the change to the moduli space. Eventually, we might reach a point where the Bridgeland space is empty, and then we can reverse our steps to reconstruct the Gieseker moduli space.

For surfaces, this is a fairly well-understood process. In that case, it is known that the geometric stability space is non-empty, that there only finitely many walls in
\mathbb{H}
away from the
\beta
-axis which are nested semicircles centered along the horizontal axis and that Bridgeland stability is asymptotic to (twisted) Gieseker stability. Furthermore, there is an effective algorithm to find all such walls for a given Chern character, and then we can carry out the process above to recover the moduli space of semistable sheaves. One approach to finding walls in this case is to observe that every wall for a given Chern character
v
intersects a special curve which we will denote by
\Theta_{v}
in this paper, given by the vanishing locus of the slope function
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(v)
, and we can then restrict our attention to finding walls along
\Theta_{v}
.

The whole process becomes much more complicated for threefolds. We can still use the
2
-dimensional construction, but it does not produce full stability conditions, and it is unable to detect sufficient features of the Gieseker moduli spaces since the latter does not coincide, in general, with the asymptotic moduli space.

The first step to improve this was made possible by a number of results guaranteeing the existence of Bridgeland stability conditions on the derived category of sheaves on different types of threefolds, based on the pioneering work of Bayer, Macrì and Toda
[
BMT14
]
. Their idea is to start with the surface case and tilt again. This provides a full stability condition, and the family of moduli spaces is considerably more refined than the one provided by the first tilt. Even though there is no general result which shows that their construction works for all smooth threefolds, it is known to work for a wide variety of relevant examples:
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}

[
Mac14
]
, smooth quadric threefolds,
cf.

[
Sch14
]
, abelian threefolds,
cf.

[
BMS16
,
MP16
]
, Fano threefolds with Picard rank
1
,
cf.

[
Li19b
]
, more general Fano threefolds,
cf.

[
BMS+17
,
Piy17
]
, and smooth quintic threefolds,
cf.

[
Li19a
]
. More precisely, the geometric stability conditions constructed by Bayer, Macrì and Toda via the generalized
Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality proposed in
[
BMT14
]
depend on three real parameters
(\alpha,\beta,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
. For each of these, we have an abelian category
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta}
and a slope function
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}
which allows us to test the stability of objects of
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta}
. There are, however, known counterexamples (see
[
Sch17
]
and
[
MS19
]
) where the generalized Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality fails.

The goal of this paper is to advance on the other two stages of the process outlined above, namely the understanding of the structure of walls and that of asymptotic stability. We only consider the case where
X
is a smooth projective threefold of Picard rank
1
over an algebraically closed field of characteristic
0
. This means that we can view our Chern classes (and their twists) as purely numerical vectors of the form
v=(v_{0},v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
.

In order to study walls, we start by providing a uniform way to define the slope functions and their differences in terms of skew-symmetric functions. We go on to consider a number of general properties of numerical
\lambda
-walls (defined as the locus where two
\lambda
-slopes are equal) in Section
4.1
. They are, in general, quartic curves, possibly unbounded and not connected.

In our first main result, we find a simple characterization of those numerical
\lambda
-walls which are bounded, and we show that unbounded walls satisfy a version of Bertram’s nested wall theorem. Given numerical Chern characters
v
,
u
and
u^{\prime}
, we define
\delta_{01}(u,v):=u_{0}v_{1}-u_{1}v_{0}
and an equivalence relation
u\sim_{v}u^{\prime}
which is essentially that the
\lambda
-walls for
v
corresponding to
u
and
u^{\prime}
are the same; see (
4.9
) for a precise definition. We also remark that when
v
is a numerical Chern character satisfying the Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality
v_{1}^{2}-2v_{0}v_{2}\geq 0
and
v_{0}\neq 0
, the curve
\Theta_{v}
allows us to divide the upper half plane
\mathbb{H}
into four regions (see Figure
1
for an example and Section
3
for details).

Main Theorem 1
.

Suppose
v_{0}\neq 0
and
u\nsim_{v}u^{\prime}
.

(1)

The numerical
\lambda
-wall for
v
corresponding to
u
is bounded if and only if
\delta_{01}(u,v)\neq 0
.

(2)

If
\,\delta_{01}(u,v)=0=\delta_{01}(u^{\prime},v)
, then the numerical
\lambda
-walls corresponding to
u
and
u^{\prime}
do not intersect.

(3)

If
\,\delta_{01}(u,v)\neq 0
and
\operatorname{ch}_{\leq 2}(u)=\operatorname{ch}_{\leq 2}(u^{\prime})
, then the numerical
\lambda
-walls for
v
corresponding to
u
and
u^{\prime}
only intersect on
\Theta_{v}
.

(4)

An unbounded numerical
\lambda
-wall for
v
does not intersect
\Theta_{v}
, and its
unbounded connected components are contained in
R^{0}_{v}
.

The different parts of Main Theorem
1
are proved in various results contained in Section
4
.

The four regions of the plane as defined by the hyperbola
\Theta_{v}
and the vertical line
\{\beta=\mu(v)\}
when
v
is a numerical Chern character satisfying the Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality
v_{1}^{2}-2v_{0}v_{2}\geq 0
and
v_{0}\neq 0
. In this picture, we used
\operatorname{ch}_{\leq 2}(v)=(2,-1,-5/2)
.

The third stage, determining the asymptotics, is not well known. In
[
BMT14
, Section 6]
, it is shown that the large volume limit as
\alpha\to\infty
for
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{H}
gives a polynomial stability condition, but the converse and other directions were not considered. However, unlike the
2
-dimensional case, we cannot assume the walls are bounded in all directions. In fact, it is easy to check that as
s\to 0
, the walls are unbounded, and it is theoretically possible that the number of walls is infinite.

This means that the large volume limit is more subtle than for surfaces. We need, therefore, to be more careful about what we mean by asymptotic stability, which we define precisely in Definition
7.1
. We use a strong form of such asymptotic stability which effectively includes finiteness of the number of walls for a given object. We also need to be careful to specify the curve along which we are considering the asymptotics. To this end, we introduce the notion of
unbounded

\Theta^{\pm}
-
curve
, which is essentially a curve which is asymptotically either to the left or to the right of all
\Theta
-curves; see Definition
5.9
.

To help set this up, we also consider what would happen for surfaces, in Section
5
. In our case, we look at so-called
\nu
-stability for threefolds, which mimics stability for surfaces given by the first tilt on the category of coherent sheaves, by re-proving results about the large volume limit without the assumption that the walls are bounded.

In order to describe the asymptotics, we need to understand how the stability of an object varies along curves. For
\nu
-stability, it turns out that stable objects can only be destabilized once along inward-moving curves (which cross
\nu
-walls only once). It turns out that this also holds for
\lambda
-stability outside the
\nu
-wall. See Theorem
7.8
for the details. Accomplishing such a task requires an understanding of the geometry of
\lambda
-walls. For the
\nu
-walls, this was simple because they were circles, and the key property is that they cross the
\Theta_{v}
-curve at their maximum. To understand the similar properties of
\lambda
-walls, we need to understand their differential properties in a similar way. We do this is Section
6
. Unlike
\nu
-walls,
\lambda
-walls need not be regular, and we carefully study when regularity fails. When
s=1/3
, it is straightforward to prove that the walls are regular except if they happen to cross a very special point (on
\Gamma_{u,1/3}
and its
\nu
-wall), in which case there is a cusp. For other values of
s
, it is much harder. It turns out to be easier to study the differential properties of the
2
-dimensional wall where we allow
s
to vary, which we call
\Sigma_{u,v}
, regarded as a real algebraic quartic surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We show that
\Sigma_{u,v}
is regular except at some exceptional points and for exceptional
u
and
v
(see Theorem
6.7
for the details).

Moreover, there is again a special curve, here denoted by
\Gamma_{v,s}
, which is defined as the vanishing locus of the slope function
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}(v)
. When
s=1/3
, a numerical
\lambda
-wall for
v
crosses
\Gamma_{v,1/3}
at its maximum point (just as a
\nu
-wall crosses
\Theta_{v}
at its maximum) and the associated
\nu
-wall at its minimum point. This imposes large constraints on the possible numerical
\lambda
-walls when
s=1/3
. We also show that for
s\geq 1/3
, any wall existing for one value of
s
must also exist for all
s
. When
s<1/3
, we show that any wall existing for
s
exists for all value less than that of
s
.

The key conclusion is the following; see also Theorem
6.17
. The proof uses key differential-geometric information about
\Sigma_{u,v}
such as its Gauss and mean curvatures.

Main Theorem 2
.

Suppose a real numerical Chern character
v
satisfies the Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality and
v_{0}\neq 0
. Any connected bounded component of a numerical
\lambda
-wall in
R^{-}_{v}
for some
s\geq 1/3
intersects
\Gamma^{-}_{v,s}
.

Although the same statement is not true for unbounded walls, we can describe the explicit conditions
u
must satisfy so that the wall corresponding to
u
intersects
\Gamma_{v,s}
.

We are then finally in position to prove, in Section
7
, that strong asymptotic stability is equivalent to Gieseker stability. Our results can be summarized as follows.

Main Theorem 3
.

Let
v
be a numerical Chern character with
v_{0}\neq 0
and satisfying the Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality; fix
s\geq 1/3
.

(1)

If
\gamma
is an unbounded
\Theta^{-}
-curve, then an object
E\in D^{b}(X)
is asymptotically
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}
-
(
semi
)
stable along
\gamma
if and only if
E
is a Gieseker
(
semi
)
stable sheaf.

(2)

If
\gamma
be an unbounded
\Theta^{+}
-curve, then an object
E\in D^{b}(X)
is asymptotically
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}
-
(
semi
)
stable along
\gamma
if and only if
E^{\vee}
is a Gieseker
(
semi
)
stable sheaf.

Duals of Gieseker semistable sheaves can be described via a technical lemma of independent interest which characterizes duals of torsion-free sheaves on threefolds (see Proposition
2.15
for the details). We also emphasize that more is true for the special curve
\Gamma_{v,s}
, which is an example of an unbounded
\Theta^{-}
-curve: the first part of the previous statement holds for every
s>0
.

The study of the differential geometry of
\lambda
-walls is also useful to help locate them. Here again there is a complication. For
\nu
-walls (or walls for surfaces), a point on an actual
\nu
-wall corresponds to an actual destabilizing sub-object
F
of an object
E
which is
\nu
-stable on one side of the wall and
\nu
-unstable on the other. This is then always the case at all points along the numerical
\nu
-wall. In other words, if a portion of a numerical
\nu
-wall is an actual
\nu
-wall, then the whole numerical
\nu
-wall is an actual
\nu
-wall. It follows that to locate actual
\nu
-walls, it suffices to look along, say, a vertical line from the limiting centre or along
\Theta_{v}
; we do this in two examples, one in Section
8.1
and the other in Lemma
8.4
.

The situation for
\lambda
-walls is quite different. While we have proved that numerical
\lambda
-walls to the left of
\Theta_{v}
must intersect
\Gamma_{v,s}
, that need not be true for actual
\lambda
-walls. However, the largest actual
\lambda
-wall (if it exists) must cross
\Gamma_{v,s}
, and this allows us to find
\lambda
-walls by working along
\Gamma_{v,s}
from infinity.

We work out a complete example for the case of the Chern character
v=(2,0,-1,0)
on
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
. This is done for the region to the left of
\Theta_{v}
in Proposition
8.8
. The key step is to first find what we call
pseudo-walls
. These are given by destabilizing objects which satisfy the Bogomolov–Gieseker and generalized Bogomolov–Gieseker inequalities. This approach to finding
\lambda
-walls complements Schmidt’s method
[
Sch20a
, Theorem 6.1]
, which is to observe that, essentially, the
\lambda
-walls crossing
\Theta_{v}
must also cross their associated
\nu
-wall at the same point.

We close the paper with a number of examples for the case of
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
in Section
8
. First we consider the case of the ideal sheaf of a line; it illustrates one of two typical situations in which the
\Gamma_{v,s}
curve intersects
\Theta_{v}
. In this case, we show that there must exist a vanishing
\lambda
-wall containing the intersection point (see Theorem
4.22
), and we illustrate this by constructing such a wall for this example. We also consider the ideal sheaf of a point for which
\Gamma_{v,s}
and
\Theta_{v}
do not cross and provide some additional stability information for our final example, which is the null correlation sheaves on
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
. In this case, we show there are no
\nu
-walls. We exhibit a number of methods to illustrate the various phenomena which can occur and to locate walls, and we show that there is effectively a single wall to the left of
\Theta_{v}
. These cases are also considered from a different point of view using Bridgeland stability in
[
SS21
]
.

Throughout the paper we make very full use of the triangulated structure of the derived category of coherent sheaves and especially the octahedral axiom. We use a higher-dimensional variant of the octahedral axiom, which is key to understanding how objects vary in the heart
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta}
along paths in the upper half plane. We illustrate this in Section
2.3
using Paul Balmer’s description of the objects of
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta}
.

2. Background material and notation

Let
X
be an irreducible, non-singular projective variety of dimension
3
over an algebraically closed field of characteristic
0
with
\Pic(X)={\mathbb Z}
. Fix an ample generator
L
of
\Pic(X)
, and write
\ell=c_{1}(L)
. Our assumptions mean that each object
A\in D^{\rm b}(X)
has a well-defined numerical Chern character

\operatorname{ch}(A):=(\operatorname{ch}_{0}(A)\ell^{3},\operatorname{ch}_{1}(A)\cdot\ell^{2},\operatorname{ch}_{2}(A)\cdot\ell,\operatorname{ch}_{3}(A))\in{\mathbb Z}\times{\mathbb Z}\times\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb Z}\times\frac{1}{6}{\mathbb Z}.

Abusing notation, we will simply write
\operatorname{ch}_{i}(A)
for
\operatorname{ch}_{i}(A)\cdot\ell^{3-i}
. We will refer to an element of
\mathbb{R}^{4}=\mathbb{R}\otimes K_{\text{num}}(X)
as a
real numerical Chern character
and an element
v
of
{\mathbb Z}\times{\mathbb Z}\times\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb Z}\times\frac{1}{6}{\mathbb Z}
as a Chern character when there is an object
A\in D^{\rm b}(X)
such that
v=\operatorname{ch}(A)
. We write the components as
v=(v_{0},v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
corresponding to the Chern characters of objects so that the underlying real Chern character
v
which is the numerical Chern character
\operatorname{ch}(A)
of an object of
D^{\rm b}(X)
satisfies
v_{i}=\operatorname{ch}_{i}(A)
.

Given
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, recall the definition of the
twisted Chern character

\operatorname{ch}^{\beta}(A):=\exp(-\beta)\cdot\operatorname{ch}(A)=(\operatorname{ch}_{0}^{\beta}(A),\operatorname{ch}_{1}^{\beta}(A),\operatorname{ch}_{2}^{\beta}(A),\operatorname{ch}_{3}^{\beta}(A))
. So

\displaystyle\operatorname{ch}_{0}^{\beta}(A)

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{ch}_{0}(A);

\displaystyle\operatorname{ch}_{1}^{\beta}(A)

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{ch}_{1}(A)-\beta\operatorname{ch}_{0}(A);

\displaystyle\operatorname{ch}_{2}^{\beta}(A)

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{ch}_{2}(A)-\beta\operatorname{ch}_{1}(A)+\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}\operatorname{ch}_{0}(A);

\displaystyle\operatorname{ch}_{3}^{\beta}(A)

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{ch}_{3}(A)-\beta\operatorname{ch}_{2}(A)+\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}\operatorname{ch}_{1}(A)-\frac{1}{6}\beta^{3}\operatorname{ch}_{0}(A).

Recall that the
\mu
-slope of a coherent sheaf
E\in\mathcal{C}oh(X)
is defined as follows:

\mu(E):=\begin{cases}\operatorname{ch}_{1}(E)/\operatorname{ch}_{0}(E)&\textrm{if }E\textrm{ is torsion-free},\\
+\infty&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In addition, we also define

\mu^{+}(E):=\max\{\mu(F)\mid F\hookrightarrow E\text{ is a non-zero subsheaf }\}\quad\text{and}

\mu^{-}(E):=\min\{\mu(G)\mid E\twoheadrightarrow G\text{ is a non-zero quotient }\}.

As usual,
E
is said to be
\mu
-(semi)stable if every non-zero subsheaf
F\hookrightarrow E
satisfies
\mu(F)<(\leq)\penalty\ \mu(E/F)
. So
E
is
\mu
-semistable if and only if

\mu^{+}(E)=\mu(E)
or, equivalently,
\mu^{-}(E)=\mu(E)
.

2.1.
\boldsymbol{\nu}
-stability

Given
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, consider the following torsion pair on
\mathcal{C}oh(X)
:

\mathcal{T}_{\beta}:=\{E\in\mathcal{C}oh(X)\penalty\ |\penalty\ \textrm{every non-zero quotient }E\twoheadrightarrow G\textrm{ satisfies }\mu(G)>\beta\}\quad{\rm and}

\mathcal{F}_{\beta}:=\{E\in\mathcal{C}oh(X)\penalty\ |\penalty\ \textrm{every non-zero subsheaf }F\hookrightarrow E\textrm{ satisfies }\mu(F)\leq\beta\}.

Tilting on
(\mathcal{F}_{\beta},\mathcal{T}_{\beta})
, one obtains an abelian subcategory
\mathcal{B}^{\beta}:=\langle\mathcal{F}_{\beta}[1],\mathcal{T}_{\beta}\rangle
of
D^{\rm b}(X)
, which is the heart of a t-structure on
D^{\rm b}(X)
.

For
B\in D^{\rm b}(X)
, let
{\mathcal{H}}^{p}(B)
denote cohomology with respect to
\mathcal{C}oh(X)
. Observe that the objects of
\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
are those
B\in D^{\rm b}(X)
such that:

•

{\mathcal{H}}^{p}(B)=0
for
p\neq-1,0
;

•

{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal{F}_{\beta}
; and

•

{\mathcal{H}}^{0}(B)\in\mathcal{T}_{\beta}
.

In particular, from the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{\beta}
, the sheaf
{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(B)
must be torsion-free.

Introducing a new parameter
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, one considers a group homomorphism

Z_{\alpha,\beta}^{\rm tilt}\colon K_{\rm num}(X)\to\mathbb{C},

called a
central charge
, given by

(2.1)

Z_{\alpha,\beta}^{\rm tilt}(B):=-\left(\operatorname{ch}_{2}^{\beta}(B)-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\operatorname{ch}_{0}(B)\right)+\sqrt{-1}\operatorname{ch}_{1}^{\beta}(B),

whose corresponding slope function is

(2.2)

\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(B):=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\operatorname{ch}_{2}^{\beta}(B)-\alpha^{2}\operatorname{ch}_{0}(B)/2}{\operatorname{ch}_{1}^{\beta}(B)}&\textrm{ if }\operatorname{ch}_{1}^{\beta}(B)\neq 0,\\
+\infty&\text{if }\operatorname{ch}_{1}^{\beta}(B)=0.\end{cases}

In addition, we also define

\nu_{\alpha,\beta}^{+}(B):=\max\{\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(F)\penalty\ |\penalty\ F\hookrightarrow B\textrm{ in }\mathcal{B}^{\beta},\ F\neq 0\}\quad{\rm and}

\nu_{\alpha,\beta}^{-}(B):=\min\{\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(G)\penalty\ |\penalty\ B\twoheadrightarrow G\textrm{ in }\mathcal{B}^{\beta},\ G\neq 0\}.

An object
B\in D^{\rm b}(X)
is said to be
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-(semi)stable if
B\in\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
and every non-zero sub-object
F\hookrightarrow B
within
\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
satisfies
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(F)<(\leq)\penalty\ \nu_{\alpha,\beta}(B/F)
. Note that
E
is
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-semistable if and only if
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}^{+}(E)=\penalty\ \nu_{\alpha,\beta}(E)
or, equivalently,
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}^{-}(E)=\penalty\ \nu_{\alpha,\beta}(E)
.

Every
\mu
-semistable sheaf and every
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-semistable object
B\in\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
satisfies the usual Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality, which in our situation is purely numerical, see
[
BMT14
, Corollary 7.3.2]
:

(2.3)

Q^{\rm tilt}(B):=\operatorname{ch}_{1}(B)^{2}-2\operatorname{ch}_{0}(B)\operatorname{ch}_{2}(B)\geq 0.

In addition, for certain choices of
X
, every
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-semistable object
B\in\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
also satisfies the following
generalized Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality
:

(2.4)

\begin{split}Q_{\alpha,\beta}(B)&=\alpha^{2}Q^{\rm tilt}(B)+4(\operatorname{ch}^{\beta}_{2}(B))^{2}-6\operatorname{ch}^{\beta}_{1}(B)\operatorname{ch}^{\beta}_{3}(B)\\
&=Q^{\rm tilt}(B)(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})+\left(6\operatorname{ch}_{0}(B)\operatorname{ch}_{3}(B)-2\operatorname{ch}_{1}(B)\operatorname{ch}_{2}(B)\right)\beta+4\operatorname{ch}_{2}(B)^{2}-6\operatorname{ch}_{1}(B)\operatorname{ch}_{3}(B)\\
&\geq 0,\end{split}

originally proposed in
[
BMT14
, Conjecture 1.3.1]
. This inequality was proved to hold for all Fano and abelian threefolds with Picard rank
1
, see
[
Li19b
]
and
[
BMS16
,
MP16
]
, respectively, and for the quintic threefold; see
[
Li19a
]
. We assume from now on that
X
is such that the generalized Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality (
2.4
) holds for all
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-semistable objects.

Let
\mathbb{H}:=\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}
, thought of as the upper half plane, with coordinates denoted by
(\alpha,\beta)
. We will want to consider the slope function as a function of
\alpha
and
\beta
, and to this end it is convenient to define the following function on
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{H}
:

(2.5)

\rho_{v}(\alpha,\beta)=v_{2}-v_{1}\beta+\frac{1}{2}v_{0}(\beta^{2}-\alpha^{2}),

which coincides with the numerator of
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(B)
when
v=\operatorname{ch}(B)
. To simplify the notation, we define
\rho_{B}(\alpha,\beta):=\rho_{\operatorname{ch}(B)}(\alpha,\beta)
for objects
B\in D^{\rm b}(X)
.

Note that the pair
(\mathcal{B}^{\beta},Z^{\rm tilt}_{\alpha,\beta})
is a
weak stability condition
in
D^{\rm b}(X)
, in the sense of
[
Tod10
, Section 2]
, for all pairs
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}
. In practical terms, this gives the following.

Proposition 2.1
.

Fix
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. If
B\in\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
, then
\operatorname{ch}^{\beta}_{1}(B)\geq 0
, with equality only if
\rho_{B}(\alpha,\beta)\geq 0
for all
\alpha>0
.

2.2.
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
-stability

The next step is to consider the following torsion pair on
\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\alpha,\beta}:=\{E\in\mathcal{B}^{\beta}\mid\text{every non-zero quotient }E\twoheadrightarrow G\text{ satisfies }\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(G)>0\}\quad\text{ and}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\beta}:=\{E\in\mathcal{B}^{\beta}\mid\text{every non-zero sub-object }F\hookrightarrow E\text{ satisfies }\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(F)\leq 0\}.

Tilting on
(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\beta},\mathcal{T}_{\alpha,\beta})
, one obtains a new abelian subcategory

\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta}:=\langle\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\beta}[1],\mathcal{T}_{\alpha,\beta}\rangle
of
D^{\rm b}(X)
, which
is also the heart of a t-structure on
D^{\rm b}(X)
.

One then introduces a third parameter
s>0
in order to define a family of central charges

Z_{\alpha,\beta,s}\colon K_{\rm num}(X)\to\mathbb{C}

as follows, for
A\in\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta}
:

(2.6)

Z_{\alpha,\beta,s}(A):=-\operatorname{ch}_{3}^{\beta}(A)+\big(s+1/6\big)\alpha^{2}\operatorname{ch}_{1}^{\beta}(A)+\sqrt{-1}\left(\operatorname{ch}_{2}^{\beta}(A)-\alpha^{2}\operatorname{ch}_{0}(A)/2\right),

whose corresponding slope function is

(2.7)

\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}(A):=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\operatorname{ch}_{3}^{\beta}(A)-\big(s+1/6\big)\alpha^{2}\operatorname{ch}_{1}^{\beta}(A)}{\operatorname{ch}_{2}^{\beta}(A)-\alpha^{2}\operatorname{ch}_{0}(A)/2}&\textrm{if }\operatorname{ch}_{2}^{\beta}(A)-\alpha^{2}\operatorname{ch}_{0}(A)/2\neq 0,\\
+\infty&\textrm{if }\operatorname{ch}_{2}^{\beta}(A)-\alpha^{2}\operatorname{ch}_{0}(A)/2=0.\end{cases}

Remark 2.2
.

We could also consider a more general central charge, whose real part is

-\operatorname{ch}^{\beta}_{3}(A)+b\operatorname{ch}^{\beta}_{2}(A)+a\operatorname{ch}^{\beta}_{1}(A)

for parameters
b\in\mathbb{R}
and
a\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
; see
[
BMS16
, Lemma 8.3]
and
[
Piy17
]
. However, we will only consider the special case where
b=0
, while
a=\alpha^{2}(s+1/6)
.

An object
A\in D^{\rm b}(X)
is said to be
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}
-(semi)stable if
A\in\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta}
and every non-zero sub-object
F\hookrightarrow A
within
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta}
satisfies
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}(F)<(\leq)\penalty\ \lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}(B/F)
.

For further reference, we define for each real numerical Chern character
v\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
the following function on
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{H}
:

(2.8)

\tau_{v,s}(\alpha,\beta)=v_{3}-v_{2}\beta+\frac{1}{2}v_{1}\beta^{2}-\frac{1}{6}v_{0}\beta^{3}-\left(s+\frac{1}{6}\right)(v_{1}-v_{0}\beta)\alpha^{2},

which coincides with the numerator of
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}(A)
when
v=\operatorname{ch}(A)
. Again, we define
\tau_{A,s}(\alpha,\beta):=\tau_{\operatorname{ch}(A),s}(\alpha,\beta)
for objects
A\in D^{\rm b}(X)
, and we will also write
\tau_{v}(\alpha,\beta,s)=\tau_{v,s}(\alpha,\beta)
.
Note that, when non-zero, the denominator of
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}(A)
is
\rho_{A}(\alpha,\beta)
.

Finally, a direct consequence of the generalized Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality (
2.4
) is that
(\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta},Z_{\alpha,\beta,s})
is a (numerical) stability condition, in the sense of
[
BMT14
, Definition 2.1.1]
, for every triple
(\alpha,\beta,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
. In practical terms, this yields the following.

Proposition 2.3
.

Fix
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{H}
. If
A\in\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta}
is non-zero, then
\rho_{A}(\alpha,\beta)\geq 0
, with equality only if
\tau_{A,s}(\alpha,\beta)>0
for every
s\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

The generalized Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality can also be used to prove a form of the support property for
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}
-semistability. In the case we are considering where the Picard rank is
1
, we can state it as follows.

Proposition 2.4

(
cf.

[
BMS16
, Theorem 8.7]
)
.

Suppose
X
is a smooth threefold with Picard rank
1
such that the generalized Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality (
2.4
) holds for all
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-semistable objects. If
E\in\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta}
is
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}
-semistable, then
Q_{\alpha,\beta}(E)\geq 0
.

Remark 2.5
.

This implies that
(\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta},Z_{\alpha,\beta,s})
is a (full) Bridgeland stability condition on
D^{\rm b}(X)
.

Remark 2.6
.

Observe that
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}
-semistable objects may not satisfy the usual Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality. For example, on
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
, the object
A:={\mathcal{O}}_{X}[2]\oplus{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(1)
is
\lambda_{1/2\sqrt{1+6s},1/2,s}
-semistable for every
s>0
, but
Q^{\rm tilt}(A)=-1
.

When we come to do more detailed computations, it will also be useful to have a more uniform notation for the various functions of
v\in K_{\rm num}(X)
introduced above; more precisely, we define the following:

(2.9)

\displaystyle\operatorname{ch}^{\alpha,\beta}_{0}(v):=v_{0},

\displaystyle\operatorname{ch}^{\alpha,\beta}_{2}(v):=\rho_{v}(\alpha,\beta),

\displaystyle\operatorname{ch}^{\alpha,\beta}_{1}(v):=\operatorname{ch}^{\beta}_{1}(v),

\displaystyle\operatorname{ch}^{\alpha,\beta}_{3}(v):=\tau_{v,1/3}(\alpha,\beta).

By convention, we set
\operatorname{ch}_{i}^{\alpha,\beta}(v)=0
for
i\notin\{0,1,2,3\}
.
Alternatively, one can also define

\operatorname{ch}^{\alpha,\beta}(v)=\operatorname{Re}\,\bigl(\exp(-\beta-\sqrt{-1}\alpha)\cdot v\bigr).

The reason for setting
s=1/3
will become clearer in Section
4.2
, but one technical reason is that the partial derivatives of
\operatorname{ch}^{\alpha,\beta}_{i}(v)
with respect to
\alpha
and
\beta
behave very well; more precisely,

(2.10)

\partial_{\alpha}\operatorname{ch}_{i}^{\alpha,\beta}(v)=-\alpha\operatorname{ch}_{i-2}^{\alpha,\beta}(v)\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{\beta}\operatorname{ch}_{i}^{\alpha,\beta}(v)=-\operatorname{ch}^{\alpha,\beta}_{i-1}(v).

Note that
\tau_{v,s}(\alpha,\beta)=\operatorname{ch}^{\alpha,\beta}_{3}(v)-\alpha^{2}(s-1/3)\operatorname{ch}^{\alpha,\beta}_{1}(v)
.

We also introduce

(2.11)

\begin{gathered}\Delta_{ij}(\alpha,\beta):=\operatorname{ch}^{\alpha,\beta}_{i}(u)\operatorname{ch}^{\alpha,\beta}_{j}(v)-\operatorname{ch}^{\alpha,\beta}_{j}(u)\operatorname{ch}^{\alpha,\beta}_{i}(v)\quad\text{and}\\
\delta_{ij}(u,v):=\operatorname{ch}_{i}(u)\operatorname{ch}_{j}(v)-\operatorname{ch}_{j}(u)\operatorname{ch}_{i}(v)=\Delta_{ij}(0,0).\end{gathered}

In particular, note that
\Delta_{10}(\alpha,\beta)=\delta_{10}(u,v)
.

The following is an easy exercise.

Lemma 2.7
.

Fix real numerical Chern characters
u
and
v
.

(1)

We have
\Delta_{01}\Delta_{23}+\Delta_{02}\Delta_{31}+\Delta_{12}\Delta_{03}=0
.

(2)

The partial derivatives of

(3)

Assume either
\operatorname{ch}^{\alpha,\beta}_{i}(u)\neq 0
or
\operatorname{ch}^{\alpha,\beta}_{i}(v)\neq 0
. Then, for any

\Delta_{ij}(\alpha,\beta)=0=\Delta_{ik}(\alpha,\beta)

\Delta_{jk}(\alpha,\beta)=0
.

(4)

The following are equivalent:

(a)

and

(

.

(b)

.

(c)

We have

.

Note that

means that

and

are proportional as vectors.

In what follows, we will use

to denote the set of

-semistable objects with Chern character

; Piyaratne and Toda proved in
[

]
that

has the structure of an algebraic stack, locally of finite type over

. Determining whether

2.3. The second tilt category

We will now collect some useful facts about the objects in the second tilt category
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta}
. Much of the following is well known and is easy to deduce in various
ad hoc
ways, but we give a novel treatment using higher octahedra which is of independent interest (the idea first appeared in

[

]
). We will henceforth drop the

from the notation
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta}
. Recall that
{\mathcal{H}}^{i}
denotes cohomology in
\mathcal{C}oh(X)
and
{\mathcal{H}}^{i}_{\mathcal{B}}

\mathcal{B}^{\beta}

Suppose

for some

(2.12)

\begin{gathered}A_{11}[1]\to A_{1}\to A_{10},\\
A_{01}[1]\to A_{0}\to A_{00},\\
A_{1}[1]\to A\to A_{0}.\end{gathered}

Because these triangles intersect, we can arrange them into a diagram as follows:

(2.13)

\textstyle{A_{01}}
\textstyle{A_{0}[-1]}
\textstyle{A_{1}[1]}
\textstyle{A_{10}[1]}
\textstyle{A_{00}[-1]}
\textstyle{C}
\textstyle{\widetilde{A}[1]}
\textstyle{A_{01}[1]}
\textstyle{A}
\textstyle{C^{\prime}}
\textstyle{A_{0}}

\textstyle{A_{1}[2]}
\textstyle{A_{10}[2].}

Here the squiggly arrows

mean

shifting by
[-n]
and

and

\begin{gathered}A_{1}[1]\to C\to A_{01}[1]\to A_{1}[2],\\

A_{10}[1]\to\widetilde{A}[1]\to A_{01}[1]\to A_{10}[2],\\

4

by

with factors

,

and

Observe that

and, in particular,

Lemma 2.8
.

If
A\in\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta}
for some
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{H}
, then
{\mathcal{H}}^{-2}(A)
must be a reflexive sheaf.

Proof.

Assume
{\mathcal{H}}^{-2}(A)\simeq A_{11}
is not reflexive and

. Then
T\to A_{11}[1]
is a monomorphism in
\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
. Since
A_{11}[1]
is a
\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
-sub-object of

, it must have
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}^{+}(A_{11})\leq 0
. However,
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(T)=+\infty
, providing a contradiction.
∎

The following fact will also be useful later on.

Lemma 2.9
.

If
A\in\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta}
for some

satisfies

, then
{\mathcal{H}}^{p}(A)\simeq{\mathcal{H}}^{p}_{\beta}(A)
for

Proof.

Chasing through the seven triangles listed in display (

, which is the same as

by the sequences in display (

We will need to consider short exact sequences in

.

2.4. Duals of semistable sheaves

Given an object
A\in D^{\rm b}(X)
, we denote its derived dual by

{\mathcal{H}}^{j}(E^{\vee})={\mathcal{E}}{\it xt}^{j+2}(E,{\mathcal{O}}_{X})
for

and
{\mathcal{H}}^{j}(E^{\vee})=0
otherwise. If

\mathcal{C}oh(X)

(2.18)

0\to E\to E^{**}\to Q_{E}\to 0,\quad Q_{E}:=E^{**}/E,

where

. Note that

; letting

be the maximal

, define
T_{E}:=Q_{E}/Z_{E}
. Let
E^{\prime}
be the kernel of the composed epimorphism
E^{**}\twoheadrightarrow Q_{E}\twoheadrightarrow T_{E}
; it fits into the following short exact sequence in
\mathcal{C}oh(X)

2.5. Refining notions of stability for sheaves

We complete this section by introducing a stability condition in

which interpolates between
\mu

The Hilbert polynomial of a coherent sheaf

on
X
with respect to the polarization

P_{E}(t):=\chi(E\otimes L^{\otimes t})=\operatorname{ch}_{0}(E)\chi(L^{\otimes t})+\operatorname{ch}_{1}(E)x_{2}(t)+\operatorname{ch}_{2}(E)x_{1}(t)+\operatorname{ch}_{3}(E),

where

x_{2}(t):=\left(\frac{1}{2}t^{2}L^{2}+t\operatorname{td}_{1}(X)\cdot L+\operatorname{td}_{2}(X)\right),\quad x_{1}(t):=\left(tL+\operatorname{td}_{1}(X)\right),

and the
\operatorname{td}_{j}(X)
denote the Todd classes of
X
. If

is another coherent free sheaf on

, we define, following
[
Rud97
, Section 2]

\Lambda(E,F):=\bigl(\delta_{10}(E,F),\delta_{20}(E,F),\delta_{30}(E,F),\delta_{21}(E,F),\delta_{31}(E,F),\delta_{32}(E,F)\bigr),

where
\delta_{ij}(E,F):=\delta_{ij}(\operatorname{ch}(E),\operatorname{ch}(F))
following the notation introduced in display (
2.11
).

We remark that a coherent sheaf
E
is Gieseker (semi)stable if and only if every proper, non-trivial subsheaf
F\hookrightarrow E
satisfies
\Lambda(E,F)>\penalty\ (\geq)\penalty\ 0
in the lexicographic order. For instance, assume
E
is torsion-free, and let
F\hookrightarrow E
be a proper subsheaf; letting

denote the reduced Hilbert polynomial of the sheaf

, we have

p_{E}(t)-p_{F}(t)=\dfrac{1}{\operatorname{ch}_{0}(E)\operatorname{ch}_{0}(F)}\bigl(\delta_{10}(E,F)x_{2}(t)+\delta_{20}(E,F)x_{1}(t)+\delta_{30}(E,F)\bigr),

so

is Gieseker (semi)stable if and only if

\bigl(\delta_{10}(E,F),\delta_{20}(E,F),\delta_{30}(E,F)\bigr)>\penalty\ (\geq)\penalty\ 0

in the lexicographic order. Similarly, a torsion-free sheaf
E
is Gieseker (semi)stable if and only if every quotient sheaf
E\twoheadrightarrow G
satisfies

\bigl(\delta_{10}(E,G),\delta_{20}(E,G),\delta_{30}(E,G)\bigr)<\penalty\ (\leq)\penalty\ 0

in the lexicographic order.

In what follows, it will be important to consider another notion of stability for torsion-free sheaves, which is equivalent to the notion of stability in the category
\mathcal{C}oh_{3,1}(X)
in the sense of
[

-

3.2. The
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
-curve

The next step is to understand the vanishing locus of
\lambda
-slope for a given numerical Chern vector
v
and to study its relative position with respect to
\Theta_{v}
.

Definition 3.4
.

For any real numerical Chern character
v
and
s\geq 0
, we define the curve

\Gamma_{v,s}:=\{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{H}\penalty\ \mid\penalty\ \tau_{v,s}(\alpha,\beta)=0\}.

In addition, we set
\Gamma_{v,s}^{\bullet}:=\Gamma_{v,s}\cap R_{v}^{\bullet}
, with
\bullet=-,0,+
.

The curve
\Gamma_{v,s}
is given explicitly by

(3.3)

v_{0}\beta^{3}-3v_{1}\beta^{2}+\left(6v_{2}-(6s+1)v_{0}\alpha^{2}\right)\beta+\left((6s+1)v_{1}\alpha^{2}-6v_{3}\right)=0.

If
v_{0}\neq 0
, equation (
3.3
) is cubic on
\beta
; hence
\Gamma_{v,s}
has at most three connected components. In fact, there exists an
\alpha_{0}>0
such that

\Gamma_{v,s}\cap\{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{H}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \alpha>\alpha_{0}\}

has exactly three connected components. Two of these components are asymptotic to lines
\alpha\sqrt{6s+1}=\pm(\beta-v_{1}/v_{0})
and therefore lie in the regions
R^{\pm}_{v}
; these are the curves
\Gamma_{v,s}^{\pm}
in Definition
3.4
above. The third component, namely
\Gamma_{v,s}^{0}
, either coincides with or is asymptotic to the vertical line
\beta=v_{1}/v_{0}
.

Example 3.5
.

When
v=(m,0,-n,0)
for some
m,n>0
, equation (
3.3
) simplifies to

\left(m\beta^{2}-(6s+1)m\alpha^{2}-6n\right)\beta=0,

so
\Gamma_{v,s}
consists of exactly three components:
\Gamma^{\pm}_{v,s}
are the two branches of the hyperbola

\beta^{2}-(6s+1)\alpha^{2}=6n/m
, and
\Gamma^{0}_{v,s}
coincides with the vertical axis
\{\beta=0\}
.

In the limit
s\to 0
, also assuming that the Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality
v_{1}^{2}\geq 2v_{0}v_{2}
holds for
v
, the curves
\Gamma^{\pm}_{v,s=0}
are co-asymptotic with the two branches of the hyperbola
\Theta_{v}
.

It turns out that the position of the different branches of the cubic curve
\Gamma_{v,s}
relative to the two branches of the hyperbola
\Theta_{v}
has an important geometric meaning, which is unravelled in the following two statements.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
v
be a real numerical Chern character with
v_{0}\neq 0
satisfying the Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality (
2.3
).
If, for each
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{H}
, there is an object
E\in\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta}
with
\operatorname{ch}(E)=v
, then
\Gamma_{v,s}
consists of exactly three connected components:

\Gamma^{-}_{v,s}\sqcup\Gamma^{0}_{v,s}\sqcup\Gamma^{+}_{v,s}

and

(3.4)

q(E):=3v_{1}^{2}v_{2}^{2}-6v_{1}^{3}v_{3}+18v_{0}v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}-8v_{0}v_{2}^{3}-9v_{0}^{2}v_{3}^{2}\geq 0.

Proof.

The hypotheses imply that the three asymptotic branches
\Gamma^{\bullet}_{v,s}
remain in their respective regions
R^{\bullet}_{v}
for
\bullet=+,-,0
all the way down to the horizontal axis
\{\alpha=0\}
, and so we have three distinct irreducible components, as given.

Recall that a cubic equation admits three distinct real roots exactly when its discriminant is positive. Regarding equation (
3.3
) as a cubic in
\beta
, an easy computation gives its discriminant as (
108
times)

(3.5)

\begin{split}q_{\alpha}(E):=&\;\alpha^{6}v_{0}^{4}(6s+1)^{3}+9\alpha^{4}v_{0}^{2}(6s+1)^{2}\left(v_{1}^{2}-2v_{0}v_{1}\right)\\
&+27\alpha^{2}(6s+1)\left(v_{1}^{2}-2v_{0}v_{1}\right)^{2}\\
&+27\left(3v_{1}^{2}v_{2}^{2}-6v_{1}^{3}v_{3}+18v_{0}v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}-8v_{0}v_{2}^{3}-9v_{0}^{2}v_{3}^{2}\right).\end{split}

Setting
\alpha=0
yields (
3.4
). Finally, we need to know that
q_{\alpha}
can only change sign once as
\alpha
varies. To see that, observe that

\frac{dq_{\alpha}(E)}{d\alpha^{2}}=3(6s+1)\bigl(v_{0}^{2}(6s+1)\alpha^{2}+3(v_{1}^{2}-2v_{0}v_{2})\bigr)^{2}>0.

So as a cubic in
\alpha^{2}
,

has a single point of inflection and so can change sign at most once. Note that, since
v_{1}^{2}\geq 2v_{0}v_{1}
, the point of inflection does not occur for any
\alpha>0
.
∎

We also make use of the following.

Proposition 3.8

Let

Proof.

This is easiest to see by considering the sign of

4. Walls

4.1.
\boldsymbol{\nu}
-walls

Given a real numerical Chern character
v
, a curve
\Xi_{u,v}\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}
is called a
numerical
\nu
-wall
for
v
if there is a real numerical Chern character
u
such that

\Xi_{u,v}:=\{t_{u,v}(\alpha,\beta)=0\},\quad{\rm where}

(4.1)

t_{u,v}(\alpha,\beta):=\rho_{u}(\alpha,\beta)\operatorname{ch}_{1}^{\beta}(v)-\rho_{v}(\alpha,\beta)\operatorname{ch}_{1}^{\beta}(u)=\Delta_{21}(\alpha,\beta);

compare with the notation introduced in display (
2.11
). Note that this coincides with the numerator of the difference
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(u)-\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(v)
, so its vanishing locus is precisely where it changes sign. Observe that numerical
\nu
-walls are invariant as subsets of the upper half plane under the changes of coordinates
u\mapsto\kappa v+u
,
u\mapsto\zeta u
and
(u,v)\mapsto(v,u)
for any real constants
\kappa
and
\zeta\neq 0
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose
v_{0}>0
,
v
satisfies the Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality and
u=(0,1,x,y)
for real
x
and
y
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\Xi_{u,v}
is empty.

(2)

For all real
\kappa
,
\kappa v+u
satisfies the Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality.

(3)

(x-\mu(v))^{2}\leq\mu(v)^{2}-2v_{2}/v_{0}
.

(4)

\delta_{02}(u,v)^{2}\leq 4\delta_{01}(u,v)\delta_{12}(u,v)
.

Proof.

The Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality for
\kappa v+u
as a polynomial in
\kappa
is

(4.2)

\kappa^{2}Q^{\mathrm{tilt}}(v)+2\kappa(v_{1}-v_{0}x)+1.

The condition in (
3
) is the discriminant condition for (
4.2
) divided by
v_{0}^{2}
. This shows that (
2
) and (
3
) are equivalent. The condition that
\Xi_{u,v}=\varnothing
is that
\Delta_{21}(0,\beta)=0
has at most one solution. Explicitly, this is

(4.3)

\beta^{2}v_{0}/2-\beta v_{0}x+v_{1}x-v_{2},

and the discriminant condition for this is
v_{0}^{2}x^{2}\leq 2v_{0}(v_{1}x-v_{2})
, which is equivalent to the condition in (
3
). Finally,
\delta_{02}(u,v)^{2}-4\delta_{01}(u,v)\delta_{12}(u,v)=v_{0}^{2}x^{2}-2v_{0}(v_{1}x-v_{2})
establishes the equivalence of (
4
).
∎

The structure of numerical
\nu
-walls was described in
[
Mac14
]
for the case of projective surfaces, but the same results also hold for projective threefolds; see for instance
[
Sch20a
, Theorem 3.3]
and
[
MS17
, Remark 9.2]
. Precisely, numerical
\nu
-walls are non-intersecting semicircles centered along the horizontal axis and cross the hyperbola
\Theta_{v}
at their maximum point or the vertical line
M_{v}
. The semicircles tend to a fixed point
(0,C_{0})
on the
\beta
-axis as the radius tends to zero.

Definition 4.2
.

A numerical
\nu
-wall
\Xi_{u,v}
is called an
actual
\nu
-wall
if for some
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\in\Xi_{u,v}
, there are an object
B\in\mathcal{B}^{\beta_{0}}
with
\operatorname{ch}(B)=v
and a
\nu_{\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}}
-semistable sub-object
F\hookrightarrow B
with
\operatorname{ch}(F)=u
such that the quotient
E/F
is also
\nu_{\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}}
-semistable.

It then follows that the same property holds for every point of
\Xi_{u,v}
; see
[
Sch20a
, Theorem 3.3]
. Actual
\nu
-walls are
locally finite
(that is, any compact subset of the upper half plane intersects only finitely many numerical
\nu
-walls), see
[
MS17
, Lemma 6.23]
, and there are
C_{\rm max}\in\mathbb{R}
and
R_{\rm max}>0
such that every numerical
\nu
-wall is contained in the semicircle centered at
(0,C_{\rm max})
with radius
R_{\rm max}
. In particular, there is an
\overline{\alpha}>0
for which there are only finitely many actual
\nu
-walls above the horizontal line
\{\alpha=\overline{\alpha}\}
. However, actual
\nu
-walls may accumulate towards the point
\Theta_{v}\cap\{\alpha=0\}
; see
[
Mea12
]
and
[
YY14
]
for examples on abelian surfaces.

Another observation is that, since
\nu
-walls are nested, there can be at most two vanishing
\nu
-walls for a given Chern character
v
, one on each side of the vertical line
M_{v}
.

The following technical result, which will be useful in Section 8, follows immediately from Lemma
4.1
.
For a Chern character
v
, we let

Y_{v}=\{\gamma\mid\gamma=\delta_{02}(u,v)/\delta_{01}(u,v)\text{ for some actual $\nu$-wall given by $u$}\}.

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose
\operatorname{ch}(E)=v
and
\operatorname{ch}(F)
are two numerical Chern characters which satisfy the Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality,
v_{0}>0
and
\delta_{01}(u,v)\neq 0
.
If
F\hookrightarrow E\twoheadrightarrow G
is a short exact sequence in
\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
for
\beta<\mu(E)
, then if
\delta_{02}(u,v)/\delta_{01}(u,v)\notin Y_{v}
, we have

\delta_{01}(u,v)\delta_{12}(u,v)\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad|\delta_{02}(u,v)|\leq 2\sqrt{\delta_{01}(u,v)\delta_{12}(u,v)}.

For example, if a particular Chern character
v
admits no actual
\nu
-walls, then any Chern character of a sub-object in
\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
must satisfy those inequalities.

4.2.
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
-walls

Given in a real numerical Chern character
v
, a curve
\Upsilon_{u,v,s}\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}
is called a
numerical
\lambda
-wall
for
v
if there is a real numerical Chern character
u
such that

\Upsilon_{u,v,s}:=\{f_{u,v,s}(\alpha,\beta)=0\},\quad{\rm where}

(4.4)

f_{u,v,s}(\alpha,\beta):=\tau_{u,s}(\alpha,\beta)\rho_{v}(\alpha,\beta)-\tau_{v,s}(\alpha,\beta)\rho_{u}(\alpha,\beta).

Note that this coincides with the numerator of the difference
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}(u)-\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}(v)
, so its vanishing locus is precisely where it changes sign. Comparing with the notation introduced in display (
2.11
), we remark that

f_{u,v,s}(\alpha,\beta)=\Delta_{32}-\alpha^{2}(s-1/3)\Delta_{21};

thus, in particular,
f_{u,v,1/3}(\alpha,\beta)=\Delta_{32}(\alpha,\beta)
.

More explicitly, using the notation from display (
2.11
)

\delta_{ij}=\delta_{ij}(u,v)=\operatorname{ch}_{i}(u)\operatorname{ch}_{j}(v)-\operatorname{ch}_{j}(u)\operatorname{ch}_{i}(v),

we have

(4.5)

\begin{split}f_{u,v,s}(\alpha,\beta)=&\;\dfrac{6s+1}{12}\delta_{10}\alpha^{4}\\
&+\left(\dfrac{3s-1}{6}\delta_{10}\beta^{2}-\dfrac{3s-1}{3}\delta_{20}\beta+\dfrac{6s+1}{6}\delta_{21}-\dfrac{1}{2}\delta_{30}\right)\alpha^{2}\\
&+\left(\dfrac{1}{12}\delta_{10}\beta^{4}-\dfrac{1}{3}\delta_{20}\beta^{3}+\dfrac{1}{2}(\delta_{30}+\delta_{21})\beta^{2}-\delta_{31}\beta+\delta_{32}\right).\end{split}

Numerical
\lambda
-walls that are bounded as subsets of
\mathbb{H}
have a simple numerical characterization.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
v
be a real numerical Chern character with
v_{0}\neq 0
and satisfying the Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality. A numerical
\lambda
-wall
\Upsilon_{u,v,s}
for
v
is bounded if and only if
\delta_{10}(u,v)\neq 0
.

Notice that if
\Upsilon_{u,v,s}
is bounded for some value of the parameter
s
, then it is bounded for every value of
s
.

Proof.

Assuming
\delta_{10}(u,v)\neq 0
, turn
f_{u,v,s}(\alpha,\beta)
into a homogeneous polynomial
of degree 4 by adding a new variable
\gamma
; let us denote this new function by

\overline{f_{u,v,s}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
. The set

\{\overline{f_{u,v,s}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=0\}
can be regarded as a hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}{\mathbb{P}^{2}}
,
and
\Upsilon_{u,v,s}
is bounded if and only if

\{\overline{f_{u,v,s}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=0\}
does not intersect the line at infinity

\{\gamma=0\}
.

Comparing with equation (
4.5
), note that

\overline{f_{u,v,s}}(\alpha,\beta,0)=\dfrac{\delta_{10}}{12}\left(6s\alpha^{2}(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})+(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})^{2}\right).

Since the right-hand side vanishes if and only if
\alpha=\beta=0
, we conclude that
\{\overline{f_{u,v,s}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=0\}
does not intersect the line at infinity
\{\gamma=0\}
; hence
\Upsilon_{u,v,s}
is bounded.

Next, if
\delta_{10}(u,v)=0
and
\delta_{20}(u,v)\neq 0
, then the homogenisation of
f_{u,v,s}(\alpha,\beta)
yields a polynomial
\overline{f_{u,v,s}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
of degree 3. Comparing with equation (
4.5
), note that

(4.6)

\overline{f_{u,v,s}}(\alpha,\beta,0)=-\dfrac{\delta_{20}}{3}\left((3s-1)\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}\right)\beta,

so
\overline{f_{u,v,s}}(1,0,0)=0
; thus
\Upsilon_{u,v,s}
is not bounded.

Finally, if
\delta_{10}(u,v)=\delta_{20}(u,v)=0
, then
f_{u,v,s}(\alpha,\beta)
becomes a constant multiple of
\rho_{u}(\alpha,\beta)=\rho_{v}(\alpha,\beta)
; hence the curve
\Upsilon_{u,v,s}
coincides with the hyperbola
\Theta_{v}
, which is not bounded.
∎

Next, we observe an interesting relation between a numerical

-wall

and its associated numerical

-wall

: if one intersects the hyperbola

, then so does the other.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
v
be a real numerical Chern character satisfying the Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality and
v_{0}\neq 0
. If a numerical
\lambda
-wall
\,\Upsilon_{u,v,s}
intersects
\Theta_{v}
at a point
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
for which an object
A
with

Definition 5.1

Theorem 5.2
.

Let

be an object with
\operatorname{ch}_{0}(B)\neq 0

(1)

\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-
(

)

\mu_{\leq 2}

(

)

(2)

\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-
(
semi
)
stable along a path
\gamma(t)=(\alpha(t),\beta(t))
such that
\beta(t)>\mu(B)
for

if and only if

\mu_{\leq 2}
-
(

Theorem

is not new, though its statement and the second part, especially, are different from previous versions. The proof of item (
1
) is essentially the same as
[
Bri08
, Proposition 14.2]
, which covers the case of

[

, Proposition 2.5]

and the arguments in its proof in the subsequent sections, we include a full proof here. Our proof is longer than previous ones for two reasons. Firstly, we have a stronger form of asymptotic stability, and secondly we do not assume there are only finitely many
\nu
-walls above a certain horizontal line. Although the latter fact is true, we want to illustrate how it can be avoided, since it is not known for
\lambda
-stability. If we do assume the
\nu
-walls are bounded above, then it follows that the
t_{1}
of the definition of asymptotic
\nu
-stability can be chosen uniformly in

, that is,
t_{1}
only depends on
\operatorname{ch}_{\leq 2}(E)
.

The study of asymptotic
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-stability is considerably simplified by the following observation.

Lemma 5.3
.

(1)

If
B\in\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
for all
\beta\ll 0
, then
B\in\mathcal{C}oh(X)
.

(2)

Suppose
E
is a
\mu
-semistable sheaf, and suppose there is some
(\alpha,\beta)
with
\beta<\mu(E)
which lies on an actual
\nu
-wall so that there are a
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-semistable object
F
and a monomorphism
F\hookrightarrow E
in
\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
. Then this is the only
\nu
-wall for
\beta<\mu(E)
at which

is destabilized.

Proof.

If
{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(B)\neq 0

\operatorname{ch}_{1}^{\beta}({\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(B)[1])=-\operatorname{ch}_{1}({\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(B))+\operatorname{ch}_{0}({\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(B))\beta<0\quad\text{for }\beta\ll 0,

so
B
cannot lie in
\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
for any
\beta\ll 0
.

For the second item, the idea is that
E
must be
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-stable above the
\nu
-wall defined by the exact sequence. The local finiteness of the actual
\nu
-walls ensures that
E
is
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-stable either immediately below or immediately above this wall. But we can show that the latter holds, as follows. First observe that
\delta_{01}(E,F)\leq 0
. This is because if we split the
\mathcal{C}oh(X)
sequence

0\to{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(G)\to F\to E\to{\mathcal{H}}^{0}(G)\to 0,

where
G=E/F
in
\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
, via

, then
\mu(K)\leq\mu(E)
by the
\mu
-semistability of
E
while
\mu({\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(G))\leq\beta<\mu(F)
and so
\mu(F)<\mu(K)\leq\mu(E)
. We use Lemma
2.7
(
2
) to compute the partial derivatives, which satisfy

\partial_{\alpha}\bigl(\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(E)-\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(F)\bigr)=\partial_{\alpha}\frac{\Delta_{21}(E,F)}{\operatorname{ch}^{\beta}_{1}(E)\operatorname{ch}^{\beta}_{1}(F)}=-\frac{\alpha\delta_{01}(E,F)}{\operatorname{ch}_{1}^{\beta}(E)\operatorname{ch}_{1}^{\beta}(F)}\geq 0

as
E
is
\mu
-semistable. Consequently,
E
cannot be destabilized on another wall outside of this
\nu
-wall because it must be stable immediately above any wall and unstable immediately below the wall. But on a path between two adjacent walls, it cannot be both stable and unstable.

To complete the proof, observe that
F\hookrightarrow E\twoheadrightarrow G
remains a short exact sequence in
\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
at all points inside the
\nu
-wall corresponding to
F
.
∎

We will see that a similar statement holds for
\beta>\mu(E)
. So, for a given

, there is at most one actual
\nu
-wall destabilizing it on each side of the vertical line
M_{v}
. We can then reduce the proof of Theorem

to the study of asymptotic

-stability along horizontal lines. To this end, we define

(5.1)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\overline{\alpha}}^{-}

\displaystyle:=\{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{H}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \alpha=\overline{\alpha},\beta<0\}\quad\text{and}\quad

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\overline{\alpha}}^{+}

\displaystyle:=\{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{H}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \alpha=\overline{\alpha},\beta>0\}.

5.1. Asymptotics along

The first part of Theorem

Lemma 5.4

If

5.2. Unbounded
\boldsymbol{\Theta^{*}}
-curves

It is tempting to think that if

(respectively,

First we prove a technical lemma which allows us to turn Proposition

about the existence of asymptotic Harder–Narasimhan filtrations into a more precise bound on

so long as we constrain the unbounded curve we move along. To this end, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 5.9
.

A path
\gamma\colon(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{H}
with
\gamma(t)=(\alpha(t),\beta(t))
is called
an unbounded
\Theta^{-}
-curve
if
\lim_{t\to\infty}\beta(t)=-\infty
and
for all
v
such that

\nu_{\gamma(t)}(v)>0
for all
t
sufficiently large. Equivalently, the curve is asymptotically bounded by
\Theta^{-}_{v}
;
i.e.

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)}{\dot{\beta}(t)}>-1.

We define the
dual curve

\gamma^{*}
by
\gamma^{*}(t)=(\alpha(t),-\beta(t))
.
Similarly, we define
\gamma
to be
an unbounded
\Theta^{+}
-curve
if
\lim_{t\to\infty}\beta(t)=+\infty
and for all
v
such that
v_{\leq 1}\neq(0,0)
,
\nu_{\gamma(t)}(v)<0
for all
t
sufficiently large.

In particular,
\gamma
is an unbounded
\Theta^{-}
-curve if and only if
\gamma^{*}
is an unbounded
\Theta^{+}
-curve.

Remark 5.10

If
v_{0}=0
while

\nu_{\gamma(t)}(v)=v_{2}/v_{1}-\beta(t)

When

is satisfied, for example, if there is an

such that for all

\alpha(t)^{2}<\beta(t)^{2}(1-\epsilon)
: we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}\dfrac{\nu_{\gamma(t)}(v)}{-\beta(t)}>\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{v_{2}-\beta(t)v_{1}+\beta(t)^{2}\epsilon v_{0}/2}{-v_{1}\beta(t)+v_{0}\beta(t)^{2}}=\dfrac{\epsilon}{2}>0.

So again this is positive for all
t\gg 0
.

Example 5.11
.

Note that, since
s>0
,
\alpha^{2}<\beta^{2}(1-\epsilon)
sufficiently far along

Let

Using Remark

6. The differential geometry of surface walls

Definition 6.1

Let

and

f(\alpha,\beta,s)
or just

In addition, we will denote by

(without the parameter

in the subscript) the surface

Our aim in this section is to explore some of the differential-geometric properties of

with a view to understanding finiteness properties of
\lambda
-walls. We will assume
v
is a fixed real numerical Chern character. Note that, by (

), we can assume
u_{0}=v_{0}
. It turns out to be best to consider the two cases

and
u_{0}\neq 0\neq v_{0}

Generically,

can have components of dimensions

,
1
or

Lemma 6.2
.

At the points of intersection
\Gamma_{u,s}\cap\Gamma_{v,s}
,
\Theta_{u}\cap\Theta_{v}
and
\Gamma_{v,s}\cap\Theta_{v}

is

-dimensional.

In other words, a surface wall
\Sigma_{u,v}
does have

Proof.

Note that each pair of curves divides a small ball around their intersection into four regions; otherwise, the (algebraic) curves must coincide. They cannot coincide except possibly for the Theta curves, but then
\Sigma_{u,v}=\Theta_{u}\times\mathbb{R}
. Then for a small arc around the intersection point in two of the regions, the function

is positive on one curve and negative on the other, and so must vanish at some point in the region for each arc sufficiently close to the intersection point. Combining this with Lemma
4.11
, we see that
\Sigma_{u,v}
is

-dimensional in a neighbourhood of the point.
∎

Remark 6.11

Theorem 6.12
.

Let

be a non-empty connected component of
\,\Sigma_{u,v}
.

(1)

First observe that we may assume boundedness away from

Then

Lemma 6.15

If a numerical

Proof.

Remark 7.11
.

Just as for Proposition

, we can deduce from Lemma

The following will be useful in Section

Proposition 7.12

Let

be a Gieseker stable sheaf with

, and let

be an actual
\lambda

Let

7.2. Asymptotics along
\boldsymbol{\Lambda_{\overline{\alpha}}^{-}}

Next, we study objects that are asymptotically

-semistable along a horizontal line

, or

\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}
-semistable

, which is the region to the left of

(see Definition

). Then we can use Theorem

. We establish the following result.

Theorem 7.13
.

Let
v
be a Chern character of an object of

satisfying

and the Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality (
2.3
). For each

and each

is asymptotically
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}
-semistable along

if and only if

is a Gieseker semistable sheaf.

The strategy of the proof is very similar to the one used in Theorem
7.3
, though with different calculations; we include them here for the sake of completeness. Indeed, note that

(7.6)

\lim_{\beta\to-\infty}\frac{-1}{\beta}\nu_{\overline{\alpha},\beta}(F)=\begin{cases}1/2&\text{if }\operatorname{ch}_{0}(F)\neq 0,\\
1&\text{if }\operatorname{ch}_{0}(F)=0\text{ and }\operatorname{ch}_{1}(F)\neq 0.\end{cases}

As before, the claim follows from Proposition
5.13
(
1
); alternatively, it can also be proved in the same manner as Lemma
7.4
above.

Lemma 7.14
.

Fix
\overline{\alpha}>0
. If there is a
\beta_{0}<0
such that
E\in\mathcal{A}^{\overline{\alpha},\beta}
for every
\beta<\beta_{0}
, then
E\in\mathcal{C}oh(X)
.

The next step is to understand the difference in
\lambda
-slopes; note that

(7.7)

\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}(F)-\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}(E)=\dfrac{f_{u,v}(\alpha,\beta)}{\rho_{u}(\alpha,\beta)\rho_{v}(\alpha,\beta)}

with
u=\operatorname{ch}(F)
and
v=\operatorname{ch}(E)
. The full expression of the numerator is given by equation (
4.5
); the numerator is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{u}(\alpha,\beta)\rho_{v}(\alpha,\beta)=\dfrac{1}{4}\operatorname{ch}_{0}(F)\operatorname{ch}_{0}(E)\beta^{4}-\dfrac{1}{2}(\operatorname{ch}_{1}(F)\operatorname{ch}_{0}(E)+\operatorname{ch}_{1}(E)\operatorname{ch}_{0}(F))\beta^{3}

\displaystyle+\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{ch}_{0}(F)\operatorname{ch}_{0}(E)\alpha^{2}+\operatorname{ch}_{1}(F)\operatorname{ch}_{1}(E)+\dfrac{1}{2}(\operatorname{ch}_{0}(F)\operatorname{ch}_{2}(E)+\operatorname{ch}_{2}(F)\operatorname{ch}_{0}(E))\right)\beta^{2}

\displaystyle+(\textrm{ terms of lower order in }\beta).

With these formulas at hand, we can establish the

part of Theorem
7.13
. First we prove a version of Lemma
7.6
along
\Lambda^{-}_{\overline{\alpha}}
.

Lemma 7.15
.

For any fixed
s>0
and
\overline{\alpha}>0
, if
E
is a Gieseker stable sheaf, then there is no monomorphism
F\hookrightarrow E
in
\mathcal{A}^{\overline{\alpha},\beta}
with
\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}(F)\geq\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}(E)
for all
\beta<{\overline{\beta}}
.

Proof.

If
F\hookrightarrow E\twoheadrightarrow G
is a short exact sequence in
\mathcal{A}^{\overline{\alpha},\beta}
for every
\beta<\beta_{0}
, then Lemma
7.14
implies that both
F
and
G
are sheaves. We first have that

\lim_{\beta\to-\infty}\left(\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}(F)-\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}(E)\right)=\dfrac{1}{3}\dfrac{\delta_{10}(F,E)}{\operatorname{ch}_{0}(F)\operatorname{ch}_{0}(E)}\leq 0

since
\delta_{10}(F,E)\leq 0
by hypothesis. If
\delta_{10}(F,E)=0
, then also

\lim_{\beta\to-\infty}(-\beta)(\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}(F)-\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}(E))=\dfrac{4}{3}\dfrac{\delta_{20}(F,E)}{\operatorname{ch}_{0}(F)\operatorname{ch}_{0}(E)}\leq 0

because
\delta_{20}(F,E)\leq 0
. Finally, if
\delta_{20}(F,E)
also vanishes, then

\lim_{\beta\to-\infty}\beta^{2}(\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}(F)-\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}(E))=4\dfrac{\delta_{30}(F,E)}{\operatorname{ch}_{0}(F)\operatorname{ch}_{0}(E)}\leq 0

since
\delta_{30}(F,E)\leq 0
, with equality holding if and only if
\delta_{30}(F,E)=0
as well, which implies that
\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}(F)=\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}(E)
for every
\overline{\alpha}
,
\beta
and
s
. It follows that
E
is asymptotically
\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}
-(semi)stable, as desired.
∎

Now we extend Theorem
7.8
to
\Lambda^{-}_{\overline{\alpha}}
. The trick is to reduce to the case of
\Gamma^{-}_{v,s}
.

Lemma 7.16
.

Suppose
s\geq 1/3
, and suppose
E
is a Gieseker semistable sheaf with
\operatorname{ch}(E)=v
. Then there is a
{\overline{\beta}}
such that for all
\beta<{\overline{\beta}}
, there are no actual
\lambda
-walls intersecting
\Lambda^{-}_{\overline{\alpha}}
. Furthermore, there are no more actual
\lambda
-walls to the left of the actual
\nu
-wall for

in
R^{-}_{v}
.

Proof.

Suppose
W_{u,v,s}
is an actual
\lambda
-wall intersecting
\Lambda^{-}_{\overline{\alpha}}
at a point
P=(\overline{\alpha},\beta_{0})
. By the choice of
{\overline{\beta}}
, we can assume
P\in R^{L}_{v,s}
. By Theorem
6.12
. any numerical
\lambda
-wall
W_{u,v,s}
for
s\geq 1/3
remains a numerical
\lambda
-wall at

, and, since it is bounded, each component must intersect

at its maxima. Theorem
7.8
tells us that this wall must destabilize to the right. If

is not actual as it crosses

, it ends on another actual
\lambda
-wall. Repeating, we have a piecewise path of walls

which must all destabilize to the right, and so the path has increasing

. But the actual
\lambda
-wall component

crossing

must be the unique wall by Theorem

. So there cannot be another such wall to the left of

{\overline{\beta}}=\beta_{0}
.

For the last sentence, observe that if the wall crosses
\Lambda^{-}_{\overline{\alpha}}
again, then there must be a piecewise smooth curve of actual
\lambda
-walls which intersects

at a point

-wall above

.

These two lemmas then immediately imply the following.

Lemma 7.17
.

For any
s\geq 1/3
and
\overline{\alpha}>0
, if
E
is a Gieseker
(
semi
)
stable sheaf, then

is asymptotically
\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}
-
(

)

We now consider the converse.

Lemma 7.18
.

For any
s>0
and
\overline{\alpha}>0
, if
E
is asymptotically
\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}
-
(
semi
)

is a Gieseker
(

)

Proof.

If
E
is asymptotically
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}
-(semi)stable along
\alpha=\overline{\alpha}
, then Lemma
7.14
implies that
E
is a sheaf.

If
E
is not torsion-free, let
F\hookrightarrow E
be its maximal torsion subsheaf. If
T\hookrightarrow F
is a subsheaf of dimension
1
, then
T\hookrightarrow E
is a morphism in
{\mathcal{A}}^{\overline{\alpha},\beta}
for
\beta\ll 0
since

for every

\operatorname{ch}_{0}(T)=\operatorname{ch}_{1}(T)=0

\lim_{\beta\to-\infty}\dfrac{-1}{\beta}\left(\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}(T)-\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}(E)\right)=\dfrac{2}{3}>0

\lambda
-semistability. If
\operatorname{ch}(T)=(0,0,0,e)

clearly destabilizes

as well.

So now assume

has pure dimension 2, and let

be its maximal
\hat{\mu}
-semistable subsheaf. Remark

for

\lim_{\beta\to-\infty}\dfrac{-1}{\beta}\left(\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}(T)-\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}(E)\right)=\dfrac{1}{6}>0,

It follows that

\lim_{\beta\to-\infty}\left(\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}(F)-\lambda_{\overline{\alpha},\beta,s}(E)\right)=\dfrac{1}{3}\dfrac{\delta_{10}(F,E)}{\operatorname{ch}_{0}(F)\operatorname{ch}_{0}(E)}\geq 0;

7.3. Asymptotics along general unbounded
\boldsymbol{\Theta^{-}}
-curves

We can now extend our results to any unbounded
\Theta^{-}
-curve (recall Definition
5.9
).

Theorem 7.19
.

Let
\gamma
be an unbounded
\Theta^{-}
-curve, and fix
s\geq 1/3
. An object
E\in D^{b}(X)
is asymptotically
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}
-
(
semi
)

\gamma
if and only if
E
is Gieseker
(
semi
)
stable.

Proof.

We consider the stable case first, and we deduce the semistable case by inducting on the lengths of the Jordan–Hölder filtrations.

Suppose
E
is Gieseker stable. Then Lemma
7.16
as
\overline{\alpha}
varies implies that there is at most a single destabilizing piecewise smooth curve of actual
\lambda
-walls from the
\beta
-axis, and it cannot be unbounded in
R^{-}_{v}
. So the piecewise curve must either cross the
\beta
-axis again or cross
\Theta^{-}_{v}
. In either case, there is a
{\overline{\beta}}
such that
E
is
\lambda_{\beta,\alpha,s}
-stable in
\{\beta<{\overline{\beta}}\}\cap R^{-}_{v}
and so asymptotically
\lambda
-stable along any curve in this region.

Conversely, suppose that
E
is asymptotically
\lambda
-stable along
\gamma
and not Gieseker stable. Then for all
t\gg 0
,
E
is not
\lambda
-stable along
\Lambda^{-}_{\alpha(t)}
. Let
t_{0}
be the least
t\in\mathbb{R}
such that
E
is
\lambda
-semistable in
\mathcal{A}^{\gamma(t)}
for all
t<t_{0}
. By increasing
t_{0}
if necessary, we may assume that for each
t>t_{0}
, there is some
F_{t}
such that
F_{t}
destabilizes
E
at some
\beta=\beta_{1}<\beta(t)
along
\Lambda^{-}_{\alpha(t)}
and such that
\nu^{-}_{\gamma(t)}(F_{t})>0
(by the assumption that
\gamma
is a
\Theta^{-}
-curve and Lemma
5.12
). So there must be a wall
W_{\operatorname{ch}(E),\operatorname{ch}(F_{t}),s}
which crosses the vertical line
\beta=\beta_{1}
. But that wall must cross either
\Lambda^{-}_{\alpha(t)}
at
\beta<\beta_{1}
or
\Im(\gamma)
at
\gamma(t^{\prime})
for some

. Since it remains an actual
\lambda
-wall, the latter possibility cannot happen by the assumption on
t_{0}
. The former possibility means that there must be another
F^{\prime}_{t}
which destabilizes at some
(\alpha(t),\beta_{2})
with
\beta_{2}<\beta_{1}
. Repeating, we have an infinite sequence of such destabilizers, which contradicts Lemma
7.16
.
∎

Remark 7.20
.

We expect that the assumption
s\geq 1/3
is unnecessary. But there are instances of walls which vanish as
s
increases. These could occur to the left of our piecewise destabilizing wall. For any
s
, we would conjecture that there are only finitely many of these, and then there would be a Gieseker chamber containing a finite number of small regions where

is
\lambda
-unstable.

We can now deduce asymptotic conditions along unbounded curves in
R^{+}_{v}
. Using the notion of unbounded
\Theta^{-}
-curve
\gamma
and of its dual curve

proposed in Definition

, we state the following.

.

Let
\gamma
be an unbounded
\Theta^{-}
-curve. Then

\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}
-
(

is asymptotically
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}
-
(
semi
)

By Proposition
5.13
, we know that there is a
t_{1}
such that

for all

. If
F\hookrightarrow E
destabilizes

for all

, then the proposition also shows that there is some

such that

and

are in
\mathcal{A}^{\gamma^{*}(t)}

\displaystyle\leq 0,

8.3. Torsion-free sheaves with Chern character
\mathbf{(2,0,-1,0)}

Our first step towards the classification of
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}
-semistable objects with Chern character
v=(2,0,-1,0)
on
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
near the curve
\Gamma_{v,s}^{-}
is the classification of
\mu
-semistable torsion-free sheaves with this Chern character.

Below,
p
and
L
, respectively, denote a point and a line in
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
. Recall also that a torsion-free sheaf
E
on
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
is called a
null correlation sheaf
if it sits in the exact sequence

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-1)\to{}^{1}_{{\mathbb{P}^{3}}}(1)\to E\to 0;

see
[
Ein82
]
. Note that
\operatorname{ch}(E)=(2,0,-1,0)
. A locally free null correlation sheaf is called a null correlation bundle; non–locally free
null correlation sheaves satisfy a sequence of the form

0\to E\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{\oplus 2}\to\mathcal{O}_{L}(1)\to 0

for some line
L\subset{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
E
be a
\mu
-semistable torsion-free sheaf on
X={\mathbb{P}^{3}}
with
\operatorname{ch}(E)=(2,0,-1,0)
.

(1)

If
E
is locally free, then
E
is a null correlation bundle; in particular,
E
is
\mu
-stable.

(2)

If
E
is properly torsion-free, then
E
is strictly
\mu
-semistable, and it is given by one of the following extensions:

(a)

0\to I_{L}\to E\to I_{p}\to 0
for
p\in L
with non-trivial extension (in particular,
E
is a null correlation sheaf, and it is
\mu_{\leq 2}
-stable),

(b)

0\to I_{p}\to E\to I_{L}\to 0
for arbitrary
p
and
L
(in particular,
E
is not
\mu_{\leq 2}
-semistable, and it has no global sections),

(c)

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\to E\to I_{\tilde{L}}\to 0
, where
\tilde{L}
is a
1
-dimensional scheme satisfying the sequence
0\to\mathcal{O}_{p}\to\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{L}}\to\mathcal{O}_{L}\to 0
for arbitrary
p
and
L
(in particular,
E
is not
\mu_{\leq 2}
-semistable, and it has a global section).

Note that
{\rm Ext}^{1}(I_{L},I_{p})=0
when
p\notin L
.

Proof.

If
E
is locally free, then
[
Cha84
, Lemma 2.1]
implies that
E
is
\mu
-stable; the fact that every
\mu
-stable rank
2
bundle
E
with
c_{2}(E)=1
on
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
is a null correlation bundle is proved in
[
OSS80
, 4.3.2, p. 363]
.

If
E
is not locally free, then
E^{**}
is a
\mu
-semistable rank
2
reflexive sheaf with
\operatorname{ch}_{1}(E^{**})=0
, and either
\operatorname{ch}_{2}(E^{**})=0
or
\operatorname{ch}_{2}(E^{**})=1
; in both cases,
E^{**}
is strictly
\mu
-semistable (
cf.

[
Cha84
, Lemma 2.1]
), so
E
is strictly
\mu
-semistable. The first case forces
E^{**}={\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{\oplus 2}
, while, in the second case,
E^{**}
must be a properly reflexive sheaf
S_{L}
given by the sequence

(8.3)

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\to S_{L}\to I_{L}\to 0;

note that
\operatorname{ch}(S_{L})=(2,0,-1,1)
.

We start by analysing the first case, that is,
E^{**}={\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{\oplus 2}
. We have that
\operatorname{ch}(Q_{E})=(0,0,1,0)
, where
Q_{E}:=E^{**}/E
; note that
h^{0}(E)=0,1
. Again, two possibilities follow: either
Q_{E}
has pure dimension 1, in which case
Q_{E}=\mathcal{O}_{L}(1)
, or
Q_{E}
satisfies a sequence of the form

0\to Z\to Q_{E}\to O_{L}(k)\to 0,

where
Z
is a
0
-dimensional sheaf and
k-1=-h^{0}(Z)<0
because
\operatorname{ch}_{3}(O_{L}(k))=-\operatorname{ch}_{3}(Z)
; since we have
h^{0}(O_{L}(k))>0
, we must have
k=0
and thus
Z=\mathcal{O}_{p}
.

The first possibility, namely
Q_{E}=\mathcal{O}_{L}(1)
, leads to the sequence

0\to E\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{\oplus 2}\to\mathcal{O}_{L}(1)\to 0

and therefore yields the sheaves described in item (
2
)(
2a
) since
I_{L}
is the kernel of the composition
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{\oplus 2}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{L}(1)
.
In addition, notice that these are the null correlation sheaves. Checking that these sheaves are
\mu_{\geq 2}
-stable is a simple exercise.

The second possibility leads to the sequence

0\to E\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{\oplus 2}\to\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{L}}\to 0

and therefore yields the sheaves described in item (
2
)(
2c
).

Finally, if
E^{**}=S_{L}
, then
Q_{E}=\mathcal{O}_{p}
, and the sequence
0\to E\to S_{L}\to\mathcal{O}_{p}\to 0
together with the sequence (
8.3
) lead to the sequence in item (
2
)(
2b
).
∎

It is important to notice that non-trivial extensions like the ones in items (
2
)(
2b
) and (
2
)(
2c
) of Proposition
8.1
do exist. Let us first consider the extension of an ideal sheaf of a line by an ideal sheaf of a point.

Lemma 8.2
.

If
p
is a point and
L
is a line in
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
, then

\dim\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(I_{L},I_{p})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}3\quad\text{if}\penalty\ p\notin L,\\
5\quad\text{if}\penalty\ p\in L.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

First, apply the functor
\operatorname{Hom}(-,I_{p})
to the exact sequence
0\to I_{L}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\to\mathcal{O}_{L}\to 0
to conclude that
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(I_{L},I_{p})\simeq\operatorname{Ext}^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{L},I_{p})
. Applying the functor
\operatorname{Hom}(\mathcal{O}_{L},-)
to the exact sequence
0\to I_{p}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\to\mathcal{O}_{p}\to 0
, we obtain

(8.4)

0\to\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{L},\mathcal{O}_{p})\to\operatorname{Ext}^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{L},I_{p})\to H^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{L}(-4))\to\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{L},\mathcal{O}_{p})\to 0.

Note that
\operatorname{Ext}^{i}(\mathcal{O}_{L},\mathcal{O}_{p})=H^{0}({\mathcal{E}}{\it xt}^{i}(\mathcal{O}_{L},\mathcal{O}_{p}))
. If
p\notin L
, then
{\mathcal{E}}{\it xt}^{i}(\mathcal{O}_{L},\mathcal{O}_{p})=0
for every
i\geq 0
; thus
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(I_{L},I_{p})\simeq H^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{L}(-4))
, which completes the proof of the first claim.

If
p\in L
, then one can check that

h^{0}({\mathcal{E}}{\it xt}^{i}(\mathcal{O}_{L},\mathcal{O}_{p}))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}2\quad\text{for}\penalty\ i=1,\\
1\quad\text{\rm for}\penalty\ i=2.\end{array}\right.

Comparing with the exact sequence in display (
8.4
), we obtain the second part of the statement.
∎

Next we consider the sheaves in item (
2
)(
2c
) of Proposition
8.1
.

Lemma 8.3
.

If
\,\tilde{L}
is the
1
-dimensional scheme described in item (
2
)(
2c
) of Proposition
8.1
, then we have
\dim\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(I_{\tilde{L}},{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}})=1
.

Proof.

Note that

\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(I_{\tilde{L}},{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}})\simeq H^{2}(I_{\tilde{L}}(-4))^{*}\simeq H^{2}(I_{L}(-4))^{*},

where the first isomorphism is given by Serre duality, and the second follows from the sequence
0\to I_{\tilde{L}}\to I_{L}\to\mathcal{O}_{p}\to 0
.

The resolution of the ideal sheaf
I_{L}
yields the cohomology sequence

0\to H^{2}(I_{L}(-4))\to H^{3}({\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-6))\to H^{3}({\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-5))^{\oplus 2}\to H^{3}(I_{L}(-4))\to 0.

However,
H^{3}(I_{L}(-4))\simeq\operatorname{Hom}(I_{L},{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}})^{*}
; thus
h^{2}(I_{L}(-4))=1
.
∎

Now let
\mathcal{G}
denote the Grassmanian of lines in
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
. Recall that a line
L\in\mathcal{G}
is called a
jumping line
for a
\mu
-semistable torsion-free sheaf
E
with
\operatorname{ch}_{1}(E)=0
on
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
if
E\otimes\mathcal{O}_{L}=\mathcal{O}_{L}(-a)\oplus\mathcal{O}_{L}(a)
for some
a>0
; we denote by
\mathcal{J}(E)
the set of jumping lines for
E
, as a subset of
\mathcal{G}
.

If
E
is a null correlation sheaf, then
\mathcal{J}(E)
is a divisor of degree 1 in
\mathcal{G}
. Therefore, for each null correlation sheaf
E
and
L\in\mathcal{J}(E)
, there exists an epimorphism
E\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{L}(-1)
whose kernel is a Gieseker semistable sheaf
K
with
\operatorname{ch}(K)=(2,0,-2,2)
.

Such sheaves have been previously considered by Miró-Roig and Trautmann in
[
MRT94
, Section 1.5]
; they showed that the family of sheaves
K
defined by exact sequences of the form

0\to K\to E\to\mathcal{O}_{L}(-1)\to 0,

where
E
is a null correlation sheaf and
L\in\mathcal{J}(E)
, define a locally closed
8
-dimensional subscheme of the full moduli space of Gieseker semistable sheaves on
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
with Chern character equal to
(2,0,-2,2)
. In addition, this moduli space, which we will simply denote by
\mathcal{Z}
, is an irreducible projective variety of dimension 13, and every such sheaf satisfies an exact sequence of the form

(8.5)

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-2)^{\oplus 2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\to}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{\oplus 4}(-1)\to K\to 0,

with
A
a
4\times 2
matrix of linear forms in four variables. Combining this with the presentation of
\mathcal{O}_{L}(-1)

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-3)\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-2)^{\oplus 2}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-1)\to\mathcal{O}_{L}(-1)\to 0,

we have a presentation for any Gieseker stable sheaf of Chern character
(2,0,-1,0)
:

(8.6)

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-3)\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-2)^{\oplus 4}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-1)^{\oplus 5}\to E\to 0.

Our next goal in this section is the classification of
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-stable objects for the Chern character
v=(-2,0,1,0)
. First, we show that there are no
\nu
-walls.

Lemma 8.4
.

For
v=\pm(2,0,-1,k)
, there are no actual
\nu
-walls for any
k
.

Proof.

We first suppose
\beta<0
. Then we may assume
E
is a Gieseker semistable sheaf (to find the biggest wall). Suppose
F
is a sub-object of
E
in
\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
with
\nu_{\beta}(F)=\nu_{\beta}(E)
and
\operatorname{ch}(F)=(r,x,y/2,z/6)
. Then
r\geq 1
and
x\leq 0
. By
[
Mac14
, Corollary 2.8]
, every
\nu
-wall must cross
\beta=\mu(E)-\sqrt{\Delta(E)/r(E)^{2}}=-1
(at the bottom of
\Theta_{v}
), and so
0<x+r<2
and then
x=1-r
. We look for
\nu
-walls along
\Theta^{-}_{v}
. Then
\alpha^{2}=\beta^{2}-1
. Now
\operatorname{ch}_{2}^{\alpha,\beta}(F)=y/2-\beta(1-r)+r/2=0
. So
y=2\beta(1-r)-r
. If
r\geq 2
, then
y\geq r-1
as
\beta<-1
. From the Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality for
F
, we have
(r-1)^{2}\geq ry\geq r(r-1)
, which is impossible. So
r=1
. Then
y=-1
, but
x=0
, and
(1,0,-1/2,z/6)
is not the Chern character of a sheaf.

Now we assume
\beta>0
. Then
E
is the dual of a Gieseker semistable sheaf. Suppose
0\to F\to E\to G\to 0
is a short exact sequence in
\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
with
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(F)=\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(E)=\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(G)
for some
\alpha
. If
E
is a sheaf, then so is
G
, and
G^{\vee}\in\mathcal{B}^{\beta}
with
\nu_{\alpha,-\beta}(G^{\vee})=-\nu_{\alpha,-\beta}(G)
. But then
F^{\vee}
is also a sheaf, and so
E^{\vee}
is not
\nu_{\alpha,-\beta}
-stable, but this contradicts the last paragraph. If
E
is not a sheaf, then
\mathcal{H}^{0}(G)
is a quotient of
\mathcal{O}_{L}(1)
, and since we may assume
G
is
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-semistable, it follows that condition (
6
) of Proposition
2.15
holds and
\mathcal{H}^{-1}(G)
is reflexive (any torsion sheaf
T
with a map
T[1]\to\mathcal{H}^{-1}(G)\to G
injects). Since
S=0
in Proposition
2.15
, it follows that
G
is the dual of a torsion-free sheaf, and again
F^{\vee}
is a sheaf. So we still have a contradiction.
∎

Lemma
8.4
has two interesting applications. First we recover the following stronger version of a well-known fact.

Proposition 8.5
.

If
k>0
is an integer, then there are no
\mu_{\leq 2}
-semistable torsion-free sheaves of Chern character
(2,0,-1,k)
on
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
.

Proof.

Note that
q(v)=8(2-9k^{2})<0
. Then if there are
\mu_{\leq 2}
-semistable torsion-free sheaves, there must exist an actual vanishing
\nu
-wall by Theorem
4.22
. But there are no
\nu
-walls.
∎

Note that
S_{L}
in the proof of Proposition
8.1
is an example of a
\mu
-semistable reflexive sheaf of Chern character
(2,0,-1,1)
. In addition, let
E
be a null correlation bundle, and let
\{p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\}
be distinct points in
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
; the kernel of an epimorphism
E\twoheadrightarrow\oplus_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{O}_{p_{i}}
provides an example of a
\mu
-stable torsion-free sheaf with Chern character
(2,0,-1,-k)
for any
k>0
.

One can also provide a complete description of
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-stable objects with Chern character
(2,0,-1,0)
.

Proposition 8.6
.

Given any
\alpha>0
, an object
B\in\mathcal{B}^{\beta}({\mathbb{P}^{3}})
with
\operatorname{ch}(B)=(2,0,-1,0)
is
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-stable if and only if

(1)

B
is a null correlation sheaf, when
\beta<0
;

(2)

B^{\vee}[-1]
is a null correlation sheaf, when
\beta>0
.

Proof.

Theorem
5.2
and Proposition
8.1
tell us that the asymptotically
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-stable objects on each side of the
\alpha
-axis are precisely the ones described above. Since there are no actual
\nu
-walls, such objects are
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-stable everywhere.
∎

Note that it also follows from Proposition
8.5
that if
k>0
, then there are no
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
-objects with Chern character
(2,0,-1,k)
. We can also deduce that from the fact that
Q_{\alpha,\beta}({2,0,-1,k})=4+4\alpha^{2}+4\beta^{2}+12\beta k
, which vanishes for
k>0
in the region
\beta<0
; since there are no non-vertical
\nu
-walls, this is impossible.

8.4.
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
-walls and stability for the Chern character
\mathbf{(2,0,-1,0)}

We now turn to the classification of actual
\lambda
-walls corresponding to Gieseker stable
objects and the description of the
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta,s}
-semistable objects with Chern character

v=(2,0,-1,0)
near the curve
\Gamma^{-}_{v,s}
.

Note that the fact that both
I_{L}
and
I_{p}
are
\lambda_{1/\sqrt{6s+1},-2,s}
-semistable for every
s>0
, checked in Sections
8.1
and
8.2
, implies that the sheaves of described in items (
2
)(
2a
) and (
2
)(
2b
) of Proposition

are also
\lambda_{1/\sqrt{6s+1},-2,s}
-semistable for every
s>0
. For this case, we will set
s=1/3
for simplicity.

We first observe that there is a vanishing wall through the point
P
given by
\beta=-2
and
\alpha^{2}=1/3
on
\Gamma^{-}_{v,1/3}
given by
F={\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-1)
. Theorem
7.8
and Lemma
8.4
then imply that this is the largest actual
\lambda
-wall for such Gieseker stable sheaves, and it suffices to find all of the walls through
P
. So in what follows, we set
\beta=-2
and
\alpha^{2}=1/3
. One strategy to find all of the walls would be to guess some based on prior knowledge, and then check whether both the sub-object
F
and the quotient object
G
are semistable, thus guaranteeing that the suspected wall is an actual one. However, we shall try to deduce the walls by solving the inequalities to locate solutions directly to illustrate the techniques one might use to do this.

For
E
with
\operatorname{ch}(E)=(2,0,-1,0)
, we consider a short exact sequence in
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,\beta}

0\to F\to E\to G\to 0

with
\lambda_{\alpha,\beta}(F)=\lambda_{\alpha,\beta}(E)
along
\Gamma^{-}_{v,s}
. We let
\operatorname{ch}(F)=(r,x,y/2,z/6)
for integers
r,x,y,z
. Since
\chi(F)=r+11x/6+y+z/6
is an integer, we must have
6|(z-x)
. In fact, at our point
P
,
\chi(F)\propto\operatorname{ch}_{3}^{P}(u)=0
, so this holds automatically.

We have
\operatorname{ch}^{\alpha,\beta}_{2}(E)=\frac{2}{3}\beta^{2}
,

Lemma 8.7

,

Remark 8.9
.

Note that the problem of eliminating walls when
x\leq-2
and
r=1
is actually a quadratic programming problem. To see this, note that

z=-2\beta^{2}x+3\beta y+3\beta-3x,

and so

Q_{P}(F)=3\beta^{2}+\frac{10}{3}\beta^{2}x^{2}+2x^{2}-2\beta^{3}x-6\beta x-4\beta xy+y^{2}+\frac{5}{3}\beta^{2}y+y,

which we can rewrite as

F_{t}(\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{v}A_{t}\mathbf{v}^{T}-\mathbf{c}\mathbf{v}^{T}

subject to the inequalities

\displaystyle 4\leq\gamma<\lambda/2<\mu/4,

\displaystyle-2-\gamma\leq y\leq\gamma,

\displaystyle\lambda-\mu/3<({y+1})/2<\lambda+\mu/3,

where
\mathbf{v}=(\lambda,\mu,\gamma,y)=(\beta x,\beta^{2},x^{2},y)
,

A_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}10(1-t)/3&-1&0&-2\\
-1&0&5t/3&5/6\\
0&5t/3&0&0\\
-2&5/6&0&1\end{pmatrix}

and
c=(6,-3,-2,-1)
. Then we need to show there is a real value of
t
for which
F_{t}(\mathbf{v})<0
given the constraints. In fact, numerical methods show that the “local” maximum value of
F(\mathbf{v})
when
t=1
is negative even without the additional non-linear constraint
\gamma\mu=\lambda^{2}
. But
A_{t}
is not definite, and so this need not be a global maximum.
