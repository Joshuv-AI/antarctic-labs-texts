Simulating short GRB jets in realistic late binary neutron star merger environments
Matteo Pais, Tsvi Piran, Kenta Kiuchi (木内 建太), Masaru Shibata (柴田 大)

Abstract
The electromagnetic emission and the afterglow observations of the binary neutron star merger event GW 170817A confirmed the association of the merger with a short gamma-ray burst (sGRB) harboring a narrow ( 5 5 – 10 ∘ 10^{\circ} ) and powerful ( 10 49 10^{49} – 10 50 ​ erg 10^{50}~\mathrm{erg} ) jet. Using the 1 second-long neutrino-radiation-GR-MHD simulation of coalescing neutron stars of Kiuchi et al. [62] , and following the semi-analytical estimates of Pais et al. [109] we inject a narrow, powerful, unmagnetized jet into the post-merger phase. We explore different opening angles, luminosities, central engine durations, and times after the merger. We explore early ( 0.1 ​ s 0.1~\mathrm{s} following the merger) and late ( 1 ​ s 1~\mathrm{s} ) jet launches; the latter is consistent with the time delay of ≈ 1.74 ​ s \approx 1.74~\mathrm{s} observed between GW 170817 and GRB 170817A. We demonstrate that the semi-analytical estimates correctly predict the jets’ breakout and collimation conditions. When comparing our synthetic afterglow light curves to the observed radio data of GW170807, we find a good agreement for a 3 × 10 49 3\times 10^{49} ergs jet launched late with an opening angle in the range ≃ 5 ∘ \simeq 5^{\circ} – 7 ∘ 7^{\circ} .

II
Setup

II.1
The BNS environment data

We import our initial conditions describing the ejecta surrounding the remnant BH into
pluto
from the fully 3D general relativistic neutrino-radiation MHD simulation of a BNS merger described in greater detail in
Kiuchi et al. [63]
and
Kiuchi et al. [62]
.
In these simulations, the neutron star is modeled with the SFHo equation of state
[
135
]
, and the binary is composed of a
1.2~\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
and a
1.5~\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
neutron star, with a chirp mass consistent with the one observed in GW 170817.
We employ the
lorene
library
[
42
]
to construct a quasi-equilibrium configuration of the irrotational BNS with the initial orbital frequency of
GM_{0}\Omega_{0}/c^{3}=0.025
, where
M_{0}=2.7~\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
is the total mass, and with an orbital eccentricity of
\mathcal{O}(10^{-3})
.
The entire run lasts one full second and is the longest simulation of a BNS merger in the literature.
The merger and the subsequent BH formation occur at
\approx 0.015~\mathrm{s}
and
\approx 0.032~\mathrm{s}
, respectively, where
t=0
is the start time of the simulation.

A fraction of neutron star matter is dynamically ejected by the tidal interaction and shock heating in the merge phase and shows a fast tail with a terminal velocity up to
\simeq 0.96
c
[
30
]
. A high-mass neutron star (HMNS) is formed and survives for
\sim 20~
ms after the merger before collapsing to a BH. It has a non-axisymmetric density structure and exerts a gravitational torque on the plasma, transporting angular momentum outwards.
The torque leads to the formation of a torus of mass
\simeq 0.05~\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, which surrounds a newly formed spinning BH of mass
\simeq 2.55~\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
and a dimensionless spin of
\simeq 0.65
.

The poloidal magnetic field is initialized with a magnetar-like strength of
10^{15}~\mathrm{G}
, and the Kelvin-Helmholtz instability at the onset of the merger amplifies it further
[
114
,
66
,
61
,
65
,
5
,
6
,
110
,
4
,
3
,
67
]
.
Non-axis-symmetric dynamo-driven MRI contributes to the enhancement of the magnetic field. Still, even at this level, the magnetic field is irrelevant to the overall dynamical evolution.
The magnetic winding and radial motion of the fluid enhance the magnetic field in the torus until the electromagnetic energy saturates at
\sim 1\%
of the internal energy of the torus. At
t\approx 0.04~\mathrm{s}
after the merger, the magnetic field energy is maximal and then decreases. At all stages, the magnetic field energy is subdominant to the total energy budget (
\sim 10^{49}~\mathrm{erg}
vs
\sim 10^{51}~\mathrm{erg}
, see
Kiuchi et al. [62]
).
The subdominant magnetic field energy allows us to neglect the magnetic fields in our work, which focuses on jet propagation.

At
t=0.1~\mathrm{s}
, a thick torus of roughly
300~\mathrm{km}
in radius surrounds the compact object, and a funnel structure has already formed with its distinctive features of low density and low pressure.
The torus expands outward due to MRI-driven turbulent viscosity transporting angular momentum, and as it expands, it cools adiabatically.
A part of the torus matter is also ejected due to this MRI-driven turbulence as the post-merger ejecta after the neutrino cooling in the torus becomes inefficient. This occurs at
t\approx 0.3~\mathrm{s}
after the merger.
At around
t\approx 1~\mathrm{s}
, the post-merger ejecta has a typical velocity of around 0.1
5
c, slower than the average value of 0.2 c reported in literature
[
130
, see]
. This is largely due to the post-merger ejecta that is launched at the outskirt of the massive torus formed around the black hole. This turbulent viscosity-driven post-merger ejection still lasts
\simeq 1~\mathrm{s}
.

At
t=1~\mathrm{s}
the thick torus surrounding the BH has a size of
\simeq 750~\mathrm{km}
of radius and a comparable thickness, with an average density of
\rho_{\mathrm{torus}}>10^{6}~\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{3}
.
A funnel with a half-opening angle of roughly
10^{\circ}
and an average density below
3\times 10^{3}~\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{3}
exists along the polar axis.
As the simulation progresses in time, the entire system expands in size. The total mass of the ejecta at
t=1~\mathrm{s}
is approximately
1.5\times 10^{-2}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
.
Fig.
1
shows the initial conditions of the simulation at
t=1.06~\mathrm{s}
after the merger, in which the presence of two polar funnels with low density and low pressure is noticeable.

The post-merger secular ejecta expand in a “numerical vacuum" of circumbinary medium with a uniform pressure of
1.35\times 10^{12}~\mathrm{erg}/\mathrm{cm}^{3}
(in order to avoid spurious random motion) and a density of
\rho\simeq 0.17~\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{3}
.
This results in a mass surrounding the merger of approximately
1.2\times 10^{-3}~\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
enclosed in a radius of
R_{\mathrm{max}}=1.5\times 10^{5}~\mathrm{km}
, which is an order of magnitude less than the mass of the ejecta.
The fast tail of the dynamical ejecta stretching to 0.96 c gradually slows down due to interaction with the external density that occupies the "numerical vacuum” (see
Kiuchi et al. [62]
and
Fujibayashi et al. [30]
for more details) leaving behind only slower, mildly-relativistic material.
Apart from influencing the low-mass, the very high-velocity tail of the unbound material, this artificial vacuum doesn’t significantly impact the ejecta’s expansion within this simulation box. Hence, it does not influence our results.

II.2
Computational setup

We construct our 2.5D initial conditions from the BNS simulation by cylindrically averaging the original 3D data (details of importing the BNS simulation data into our calculations, using
pluto
, are described in Appendix
A
).
The original cylindrical average of the output is imported and re-gridded into
pluto
(details of the computational grid can be found in Appendix
A.2
).
After the data import, we evolve the matter distribution within the code and, with a user-defined boundary prescription, we insert a jet into the environment through a nozzle of a radius
r_{\mathrm{j}}
at the excision height
z_{0}
, imposing a reflective boundary elsewhere along
z=z_{0}
(and an axis-symmetric boundary along
r=0
).
Our choice of the boundary condition at
z_{0}
does not influence the outcome of our simulations since the velocity of our injected outflow is much larger than the velocity of the ejecta. In addition, due to the strong power of the jet, the newly formed cocoon quickly dominates the ejecta structure in the innermost part of the ejecta.

We perform our simulations using the massively parallel multidimensional relativistic magneto-hydrodynamic code
pluto
(v4.4.2)
1
1

1

This is the latest version available during the making of this paper.

[
87
]
.
The code uses a finite-volume, shock-capturing scheme that integrates a system of conservation laws. The flow quantities are discretized on a rectangular computational grid enclosed by a boundary.
We use the special relativistic hydrodynamics module in 2.5D cylindrical coordinates (i.e., a 2D grid in cylindrical geometry in which
v_{\phi}
is also integrated and evolved with periodic boundary conditions).
We performed our simulations using a linear reconstruction scheme combined with a second-order Runge-Kutta time stepping.
We also force the code to reconstruct the 4-velocity vectors at each time step.
We chose a Taub-Matthews equation of state
[
88
]
, which accounts for both relativistic and non-relativistic material, i.e.,
\gamma=5/3
for non-relativistic material and
\gamma=4/3
for relativistically moving matter.

The choice of a 2.5D simulation over 3D has implications for both the jet propagation and the development of hydrodynamic turbulence in the flow.
A direct effect of the dimensionality is the direction of the turbulent cascade.
While in 3D, the cascade goes from large to small scales, in 2D, the opposite is true, as shown by
Kraichnan [69]
, resulting in a flow organizing itself in large eddies and generating different turbulent structures that affect the mixing of the jet material with the ejecta.
Since a 2.5D simulation lacks a degree of freedom, the jet is forced to propagate, staying on-axis.
This results in a numerical effect called “plug", which consists of matter stalling over the jet and affecting its expansion.
Conversely, in 3D simulations, this feature is absent, and the jet, while carving its way out, wobbles due to flow instabilities and interaction with the surrounding ejecta.
When 2D and 3D runs are compared, the difference is not too stark
[
50
]
.

II.3
Jet injection

We inject a jet with constant luminosity
L_{\mathrm{j}}
, operating for an engine time
t_{\mathrm{e}}
with an initial bulk Lorentz factor
\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{j}}
, a density
\rho_{\mathrm{j}}
and a dimensionless specific enthalpy
h_{\mathrm{j}}
.
The hot jet is injected through a nozzle parallel to the
z-
axis with a radius of
r_{\mathrm{j}}
and with an initial opening angle,
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}
, which also determines the initial Lorentz factor via
\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{j}}\simeq 1/(1.4\theta_{\mathrm{j}})
(see details on this injection method at
Mizuta & Ioka 90
and
Harrison et al. 50
).
We place the nozzle at an initial excision height of
z_{0}=r_{\mathrm{j}}/\theta_{\mathrm{j}}
.
In all our simulations, we chose
r_{\mathrm{j}}=10~\mathrm{km}
, a size comparable to the Schwarzschild radius of the central BH, allowing sufficient mesh coverage.
The head cross section is given by
\Sigma_{\mathrm{j}}=\pi r_{\mathrm{j}}^{2}
.
This determines
\rho_{\mathrm{j}}
as:

\rho_{\mathrm{j}}=\dfrac{L_{\mathrm{j}}}{\Sigma_{\mathrm{j}}h_{\mathrm{j}}\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{j}}^{2}\beta_{0,\mathrm{j}}\mathrm{c}^{3}}\ ,

(1)

with
\beta_{0,\mathrm{j}}=\sqrt{1-1/\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{j}}^{2}}
.
This choice of the specific dimensionless enthalpy determines the terminal Lorentz factor of the system, i.e.
\Gamma_{\infty}\simeq h_{\mathrm{j}}\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{j}}
.
The jet’s pressure is given by
P_{\mathrm{j}}=h_{\mathrm{eff}}{\rho_{\mathrm{j}}\mathrm{c}^{2}}
, where
h_{\mathrm{eff}}=\left(5h_{\mathrm{j}}-\sqrt{9h_{\mathrm{j}}^{2}+16}\right)/8
is derived from Taub-Matthews EOS. We choose
h_{\mathrm{j}}=100
, which gives
h_{\mathrm{eff}}\simeq 25
.
For details regarding the computational grid used in
pluto
, we refer to Appendix
A.2
.
At the same time, a more detailed description of the jet injection is found in Appendix
B.1
.

Results

III.1
Collimation criterion

In
Pais et al. [109]
, we showed how the ambient pressure counterbalances the Poynting flux of a heavily magnetized jet, assuming transverse equilibrium for the magnetic field.
In our case, the jet is not magnetized, and an equation of state with
\gamma=4/3
describes the relativistic pressure injected. So, we cannot strictly apply the same criteria for collimation as described in our previous work.
In particular, if
P_{\mathrm{j}}\simeq(h_{\mathrm{j}}-1)\rho_{\mathrm{j}}\mathrm{c}^{2}/4
for hot jets and if
\rho_{\mathrm{j}}
is given by Eq. (
1
), then the equilibrium condition with ambient pressure reads:

P_{\mathrm{ej}}\simeq\dfrac{L_{\mathrm{j}}}{4\pi r^{2}\Gamma^{2}\mathrm{c}}\ ,

(2)

where
P_{\mathrm{ej}}(R,z)
is the local ejecta pressure, and
\Gamma=\Gamma(R)
is the Lorentz factor of the flow.

For a relativistic jet confined by a non-static medium along the cross-sectional radius
R
of the jet, the scaling
\Gamma\propto R
holds
[
16
]
, which is the same scaling found when transverse equilibrium for the magnetic field is reached for magnetically dominated jets.
In
Pais et al. [109]
, we derived the collimation curves for the simulation data of
Kiuchi et al. [62]
, varying the luminosity of the jet smoothly, determining the corresponding equilibrium contours between the jet pressure and the ambient pressure, and calculating the opening angle of these contours with the assumption of a quasi-conical shape for the jet.
For the increase in the luminosity, the increase in the jet opening angle was obtained, giving a functional relation between these two quantities.
Since we use the same background data from the BNS merger as the initial condition for our jet injection, we can fit the collimation curve
L_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{coll}}
as a function of the angle
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}
with a power law
\simeq L_{0}\times\theta_{\mathrm{j}}^{4}
.
Thus, under the assumption that the luminosity is constant over time and given an angle
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}
, the corresponding collimation energy is:

E_{\mathrm{coll}}(\theta_{\mathrm{j}})=\int_{0}^{t_{\mathrm{e}}}L_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{coll}}(\theta_{\mathrm{j}})\mathrm{d}t\approx 3.6\times 10^{49}~\left(\dfrac{\theta_{\mathrm{j}}}{0.1~\mathrm{rad}}\right)^{4}\left(\dfrac{t_{\mathrm{e}}}{1~\mathrm{s}}\right)~\mathrm{erg}\ .

(3)

We remark that this formula is an approximation, and its prefactor is tailored to the specific energy distribution of the BNS simulation we are using. Since the luminosity is assumed to be a constant, the collimation energy as a function of the jet angle scales linearly with the engine time.
Analyzing the simulations, we report our fit in Fig.
2
as a black dashed line over the colored collimation curve lines.

III.2
Breakout criterion

Gottlieb & Nakar [40]
estimate the breakout criterion for a jet surrounded by anisotropic, fast-moving ejecta.
Their condition for a jet of luminosity
L_{\mathrm{j}}
reads:

E_{\mathrm{j}}\equiv L_{\mathrm{j}}\times t_{\mathrm{e}}>150\left[\left(\dfrac{t_{\mathrm{d}}}{t_{\mathrm{e}}}\right)^{2}+2\right]E_{\mathrm{ej}}(<\theta_{\mathrm{j}})\times\theta_{\mathrm{j}}^{2}\ ,

(4)

where
t_{\mathrm{d}}
is the delay time between the merger and the jet launch,
t_{\mathrm{e}}
is the engine time of the jet,
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}
is the jet opening angle, and
E_{\mathrm{ej}}(<\theta_{\mathrm{j}})
is the energy of the ejecta within
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}
.
We see immediately that if
t_{\mathrm{d}}\gg t_{\mathrm{e}}
, it becomes energetically difficult for a jet to break out from the ejecta.
If we now express
E_{\mathrm{ej}}(<\theta)
as a function of
\theta
, then we fit the data from the BNS simulation as
E_{\mathrm{ej}}(<\theta_{\mathrm{j}})\simeq E_{0}\times\theta_{\mathrm{j}}^{k}
(where
k\approx 2)
, and if we express
E_{\mathrm{j}}
as
L_{\mathrm{j}}\times t_{\mathrm{e}}
, we get a third-order polynomial in
t_{\mathrm{e}}
, which gives us an approximate estimate of the minimal engine time for a jet to break out as a function of
L_{\mathrm{j}}
,
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}
and
t_{\mathrm{d}}
.
This estimate, for typical jet parameters, reads:

t_{\mathrm{e}}\simeq 0.1~\mathrm{s}~\left(L_{\mathrm{j},50}^{-1}\theta_{\mathrm{j},5}^{4}+L_{\mathrm{j},50}^{-1/3}\theta_{\mathrm{j},5}^{4/3}t^{2/3}_{\mathrm{d,0}}\right)\ ,

(5)

where
L_{\mathrm{j},50}=L_{\mathrm{j}}/(10^{50}\mathrm{erg}/\mathrm{s})
,
\theta_{\mathrm{j},5}=\theta_{\mathrm{j}}/5^{\circ}
, and
t_{\mathrm{d,0}}=t_{\mathrm{d}}/(1\,\mathrm{s})
.
Since
t_{\mathrm{e}}\lesssim 1\,\mathrm{s}
for sGRB jets and the jet is likely to be narrowly collimated, the first term of Eq. (
5
) can be neglected.
This second term agrees with the estimates given by Eq. (31) of
Nakar [101]
, even though it was derived under some restrictive conditions that do not hold here.

III.4
Initially wide jets

As a last step in our analysis, we address the collimation effect of the ambient medium on a jet initialized with a very wide angle, i.e.,
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}=35^{\circ}
, and a luminosity of
L_{\mathrm{j}}=10^{51}~
erg/s.
The results are reported in Fig.
9
.
We plotted two different moments of the simulation:
25~
ms after injection and
150~
ms after injection.
The collimating effect of the surrounding matter is clearly seen now. The ambient pressure of the ejecta can redirect the material’s flux going sideways and collimate the jet to a much smaller opening angle in a few tens of milliseconds. A characteristic cusp-shaped collimation shock is seen at
t=25
ms. As expected, this leads to an initially cylindrical jet in the region past the collimation shock (see also at
t=25
ms).
These results are consistent with Eq. (
2
) and subsequent Fig.
2
from which we see that the luminosity of the jet is not sufficient to maintain a wide opening angle; thus, the resulting jet will be collimated to a smaller angle.

The jet later expands sideways to
\sim~10^{\circ}
, filling the post-merger environment’s low density-low pressure polar cavity.
This cavity has an opening angle of
\simeq 10^{\circ}
, resulting in the jet quickly filling it and propagating with a reduced opening angle, presenting a quasi-cylindrical structure in its evolution (second row).

In Fig.
10
, we show two simulations with an initial jet opening angle of
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}=20^{\circ}
with two different luminosities:
10^{51}~\mathrm{erg}/\mathrm{s}
and
5.4\times 10^{51}~\mathrm{erg}/\mathrm{s}
. The latter value is chosen according to Eq. (
3
) for the collimation energy for the selected opening angle. The first case presents again a wide jet collimated to a smaller angle by the ejecta pressure; the second is a more energetic jet that expands almost conically at later times.

This has a nontrivial implication: the only way to have very wide jets in this post-BNS merger environment is to inject a large amount of energy in a very short amount of time, i.e., the jet would require a high luminosity (
>10^{52}~\mathrm{erg}/\mathrm{s}
) to achieve a wide opening angle and counteract the presence of the strong ambient pressure.
Thus, it is reasonable to suppose that the jet coming out from the post-BNS merger is relatively narrow (
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}\leq 10^{\circ}
) and in line with the observations of the afterglow of GW 170817
[
104
]
.

III.5
The angular distribution

The angular distribution of the total energy of the outflow (the jet, the cocoon, and the ejecta) has significant implications for the EM signals: the prompt gamma rays that arose from the cocoon in the case of GRB 170817A, the observed afterglow that arose first from the cocoon and then from the jet, and a possible late radio afterglow arising from the rest of the ejecta.

Fig.
11
depicts the differential isotropic equivalent energy distribution for four different runs with
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}=[3,5,6.8,10,20]^{\circ}
, and two luminosities
L_{\mathrm{j}}=10^{50}~\mathrm{erg}/\mathrm{s}
and
10^{51}~\mathrm{erg}/\mathrm{s}
, taken at
t_{\mathrm{e}}=300~\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s}
. For each luminosity, the runs have all the same total energy
E_{\mathrm{j}}(t_{\mathrm{e}})=3\times 10^{49}~\mathrm{erg}
and
3\times 10^{50}~\mathrm{erg}
, respectively.
As expected, the narrower the opening angle, the more collimated the energy distribution is. For the
10^{50}~\mathrm{erg}/\mathrm{s}
runs, the effect of the collimation by the ejecta is evident since the fit shows a smaller opening angle than the initial one, and the curve looks closer to a standard Gaussian. Overall, we find a core with rather sharp edges surrounded by a lower energy cocoon material (from the jet up to
\sim 50^{\circ}
–
60^{\circ}
and ejecta material from
\sim 50^{\circ}
–
60^{\circ}
to the equatorial plane).

We fit these curves with a generalized Gaussian function:

E_{\Omega}(t,\theta)=\dfrac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}\Omega}\equiv\dfrac{1}{2\pi\sin\theta}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}\theta}=E_{0}(t)\exp\left[-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{\theta}{\theta_{\mathrm{c}}(t)}\right)^{s(t)}\right]\ ,

(6)

where
\theta_{\mathrm{c}}
is the angular spread of the core of the jet,
E_{0}(t)
is proportional to the total energy of the jet at the time
t
, and
s
is a shape parameter that depends on time. This fit is suitable for the core region that is dominated by the jet and has a rather steep profile.
We notice that the more the jet evolves, the more
\theta_{\mathrm{c}}
saturates to
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}
, the shape parameter
s
stabilizes to a constant value, and
E_{0}
increases following the same time evolution of
E_{\mathrm{j}}=L_{\mathrm{j}}\times t
.
The jet’s core angle
\theta_{\mathrm{c}}
is in excellent agreement with the jet’s initial opening angle within the error, and the wider the opening angle, the higher the jet’s shape parameter is (indicating a flatter distribution within
\theta\leq\theta_{\mathrm{c}}\simeq\theta_{\mathrm{j}}
).
A more in-depth overview of the parameters regulating the jet shape and their correlations can be found in Appendix
D.3
.

IV
Summary and Conclusions

We presented a set of 2.5D special relativistic simulations of unmagnetized relativistic jets launched into a realistic post-BNS merger environment based on the neutrino radiation GR-MHD simulation by
[
62
]
that was evolved for more than 1 second after the merger.
We explored a variety of configurations for relativistic jets launched in proximity of a compact object and expanded in a post-merger environment.
We verified the semi-analytical prediction presented in
[
109
]
about the jet escape and collimation and validated our results for a jet launched at
0.1
s and
1
s after the merger.
We summarise our findings as follows:

•

The low-pressure, low-density polar funnel that arises in the BNS simulations makes it easier for the jet to emerge from the ejecta.

•

For wide jets, the ambient pressure of the ejecta counterbalances the pressure of the jet while it propagates and collimates it.

•

We confirm the predictions derived by
[
40
]
for a minimum engine operative time for the jet to escape. The numerical simulations verified that the predicted engine time
t_{\mathrm{e}}
allows the jet to escape from the system after the shutdown of the central engine driving it.

•

We explored the conditions of a late jet launch at
t_{\mathrm{d}}=1~\mathrm{s}
and an early jet launch at
t_{\mathrm{d}}=0.1~\mathrm{s}
, finding a significantly shorter engine time was required to escape in case of an early launch at
t_{\mathrm{d}}=0.1~\mathrm{s}
, i.e.,
0.01-0.05~\mathrm{s}
vs.
0.1-0.3~\mathrm{s}
for a launch with a delay of
t_{\mathrm{d}}=1~\mathrm{s}
.

•

We verified that the choice of the enthalpy (and thus of the terminal Lorentz factor) does not influence our results as long as the jet is hot.

•

We tested the effect of the ejecta thermal pressure in collimating the jet injecting two very wide jets (
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}=20^{\circ}\simeq 0.35~\mathrm{rad}
and
35^{\circ}\simeq 0.6~\mathrm{rad}
) and following their evolution in the ejecta.
We showed how the dense inner ejecta can collimate the jet to an opening angle of about
10^{\circ}
in a few tens of milliseconds while the jet is still located close to the compact object where it is surrounded by dense ejecta matter.

•

We compared our synthetic light curves generated from the energy profile of our simulations with the observed afterglow data of GW170817 at
5.5~\mathrm{GHz}
.
Our isotropic equivalent energy distributions are characterized by sharp edges around the jet’s opening angle and decline faster than a Gaussian.
Nevertheless, the resulting synthetic light curves can match radio data for collimated jets with
E_{\mathrm{j}}=3\times 10^{49}~\mathrm{erg}/\mathrm{s}
,
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}=6.8^{\circ}
(corresponding to
\theta_{\mathrm{c}}=(3.8\pm 0.3)^{\circ}
) and a viewing angle of
\theta_{\mathrm{obs}}=18^{\circ}
. In contrast, for smaller (larger) jets’ opening angles, the light curve’s peak becomes too narrow (wide).
These values are roughly in agreement with those obtained by fitting jet models to the light curve
[
53
,
34
,
72
,
137
]
.
The more powerful
E_{\mathrm{j}}=3\times 10^{50}~\mathrm{erg}/\mathrm{s}
jets are uncollimated (i.e., they roughly maintain their original opening angles). These jets decline or rise too steeply and do not match the radio data well.

•

These calculations that follow the jet propagation in the ejecta enable us to break the degeneracy between the total energy of the jet,
E_{\mathrm{j}}
, and the external density,
n_{0}
that exists in ad hoc fittings of jet profiles to the light curve
[
104
, e.g.]
.

The novelty of this work is that we can study the jet launch much later than previous works due to the 1-second-long evolved initial conditions, which tracked the ejecta’s expansion into the circumbinary environment.
As we have seen, it is more difficult for the jet to break out from the ejecta in case of a late launch, thus putting stronger constraints on the jet engine.
This is important, at least in the context of modeling GW 170817A, as it has been suggested that there was about a 1-sec delay between the merger and the jet launch
[
59
,
41
,
102
]
in that event.

Because of this long duration, there is an external decelerating shock wave propagating outwards at a velocity of
0.1~
c in the artificially imposed “numerical vacuum" that has a floor value for the density of
\approx 0.17~\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{3}
. This does not influence our main concerns here: jet collimation and breakout. However, it influences the fine details of the shock breakout from the ejecta that is needed in case one wishes to calculate the signature of the cocoon breakout - which was, most likely, the cause of the observed
\gamma
-rays in GRB 170817A
[
59
,
41
,
102
]
.

Modeling the observed sGRB detected in GRB 170817A would require following up the shocked cocoon until it catches up with the low mass very-high velocity component of the ejecta so that the optical depth of the shock is of order
\tau\simeq 1
. This would require a longer run of our simulations, which would take at least 2 seconds. However, for a realistic simulation, the external “numerical vacuum" in such a case should be much lower so that it does not slow down the fast component of the ejecta.
Namely, the circumbinary atmosphere surrounding the post-merger ejecta has to have a mass smaller than the fast-moving ejecta (i.e.,
M_{\mathrm{atmosphere}}<10^{-4}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) within the simulation box of about
10^{11}-10^{12}
cm. We leave this analysis to future work. Additional future work would involve exploring the propagation of a Poynting flux-dominated jet in this ejecta.

In this work, we have demonstrated that for jet luminosities of
10^{50}
–
10^{51}
erg/s an engine time
t_{\mathrm{e}}
of
0.1
–
0.3~\mathrm{s}
are required for a late launch (
t_{\mathrm{d}}=1~\mathrm{s}
) and
t_{\mathrm{e}}\sim 0.01
–
0.05~\mathrm{s}
in the case of an early launch with a time delay of
t_{\mathrm{d}}=0.1~\mathrm{s}
.
Recalling that the jet in GW 170817 was less powerful compared to typical sGRBs, we note that a longer duration would be required for less powerful jets to break out.
The jet coming out from the system is narrowly collimated.
The collimation is supported by the narrow funnel in the initial outflow configuration and does not exceed an opening of
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}\lesssim 10^{\circ}
. A wide jet injected into the ejecta is quickly collimated to a smaller opening angle.

Appendix B
Detailed jet setup and energy calculation

B.1
Jet injection

The jet is characterized by four parameters: luminosity
L_{\mathrm{j}}
, opening angle
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}
, enthalpy
h_{\mathrm{j}}
, and the jet engine duration
t_{\mathrm{e}}
.
In our setup, we inject the jet cylindrically through a nozzle such that the initial Lorentz factor
\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{j}}
is related to the initial choice of the jet opening angle via
\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{j}}\simeq 1/(1.4\theta_{\mathrm{j}})

[
90
]
.
The following stress-energy tensor gives the energy density of a relativistic, unmagnetized fluid:

T^{\mu\nu}=\rho hc^{2}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}+Pg^{\mu\nu}

(B1)

where
u^{\mu}
is the 4-velocity and
g^{\mu\nu}
is the metric tensor (which coincides with the Minkowski space-time in our case). In the laboratory frame, where
u^{\mu}=\Gamma(1,\bm{\beta})
, the energy flux of the fluid along z assumes the form
4
4

4

Some authors, like
Tominaga [141]
, subtract the rest energy density of the fluid measured in the lab frame from this quantity.
:

T^{0z}=\rho hc^{2}\Gamma^{2}\beta_{z}\quad.

(B2)

The jet is injected through a nozzle with radius
r_{\mathrm{j}}
and area
\Sigma_{0z}\equiv\Sigma_{\mathrm{j}}=\pi r^{2}_{\mathrm{j}}
.
At the nozzle, we inject a flux of energy per unit time such that:

L_{\mathrm{j}}=\int_{0}^{r_{\mathrm{j}}}T_{\mathrm{j}}^{0z}\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{0z}=\rho_{\mathrm{j}}h_{\mathrm{j}}c^{2}\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{j}}^{2}\beta_{0,\mathrm{j}}\Sigma_{\mathrm{j}}\ ,

(B3)

where we imposed a top-hat profile of the jet (i.e., the properties of the jet do not vary with the position within the nozzle radius).
From Eq. (
B3
), we can derive the value for
\rho_{\mathrm{j}}
.
The expression of the enthalpy links pressure and density:

h=1+\varepsilon+P/\rho
, where
\varepsilon(P,\rho)
is the internal specific energy density of the fluid. The explicit expression for
\varepsilon(P,\rho)
depends on the choice of the EOS. In the case of a perfect fluid, we have
\rho\varepsilon=P/(\gamma-1)
, where the adiabatic factor
\gamma=4/3
for a relativistic fluid, such that
\rho hc^{2}=\rho c^{2}+4P
. In the case of a Taub-Matthews EOS for a Synge gas, we use the approximation showed in
Mignone & McKinney [88]
where the product
\rho c^{2}h
is expressed by

\rho hc^{2}=\dfrac{5}{2}P+\sqrt{\rho^{2}c^{4}+\dfrac{9}{4}P^{2}}

(B4)

Inverting Eq. (
B4
) and since we fix the enthalpy when we inject the jet, we find the following expression for the pressure:

P=\dfrac{1}{8}\left(5h-\sqrt{9h^{2}+16}\right)\rho c^{2}=h_{\mathrm{eff}}\rho c^{2}

(B5)

which reduces to
\rho hc^{2}/4
for a relativistic gas.
Since we inject our jet vertically through the nozzle, the only non-zero component of
u^{\mu}
is
\beta_{z}
. Since we fix the value of the luminosity
L_{\mathrm{j}}
and the enthalpy
h_{\mathrm{j}}
the quantities injected at the excision height
z_{0}
and for
r\leq r_{\mathrm{j}}
are:

\beta_{r,\mathrm{j}}=\beta_{\phi,\mathrm{j}}=0

(B6)

\beta_{z,\mathrm{j}}\equiv\beta_{0,\mathrm{j}}=\sqrt{1-1/\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{j}}^{2}}

(B7)

\rho_{\mathrm{j}}=\dfrac{L_{j}}{\Sigma_{\mathrm{j}}h_{\mathrm{j}}\Gamma^{2}_{0,\mathrm{j}}\beta_{z,\mathrm{j}}c^{3}}

(B8)

P_{\mathrm{j}}=h_{\mathrm{eff}}\rho_{\mathrm{j}}c^{2}

(B9)

Eqs. (
B6
)–(
B9
) describe the jet injection at the nozzle and are constant in time by the entire duration of the engine activity as long as
L_{\mathrm{j}}=\mathrm{const.}
, otherwise
\rho_{\mathrm{j}}
and
P_{\mathrm{j}}
will vary according to the time dependency of
L_{\mathrm{j}}
.

Additionally, we enforce that, after
q
time steps, the increase in the total energy over time follows the temporal law we specified for the jet luminosity. To do so, we measure the total energy added to the system up to that point on top of the ejecta energy of the initial conditions (i.e.,
E_{\mathrm{added}}=E_{\mathrm{measued}}-E_{\mathrm{ej}})
, and we compare it to the total energy we expect up to that moment integrating the luminosity function of our choice, i.e.
E(t)=\int_{0}^{t}L_{\mathrm{j}}(t)\mathrm{d}t
. For a constant luminosity, the energy added to the system is trivial
E(t)=L_{\mathrm{j}}\times t
.
If the energy in the system does not match the expected energy, we re-adjust iteratively the value of the jet pressure (and thus the jet density) at the injection point according to the following scheme:

P_{\mathrm{j},n+q}=\begin{cases}(1+x)P_{\mathrm{j},n}&\textrm{if~$E_{\mathrm{added}}<E(t)$}\\
P_{\mathrm{j},n}&\textrm{if~$E_{\mathrm{added}}=E(t)$}\\
P_{\mathrm{j},n}/(1+x)&\textrm{if~$E_{\mathrm{added}}>E(t)$}\\
\end{cases}

(B10)

where
P_{\mathrm{j},n}
is the jet pressure at the nozzle at the current time step
n
, while
n+q
indicates the
q
-th time step after
n
. The quantity
x=10^{-3}
is a small correction we give to the pressure at each time step to match the desired total energy of the system up to that point. In our simulations, we found that
q=500
was sufficient to control the pressure value over time.

B.2
Energy calculation

Eq. (
B1
) expresses the fluid’s total energy density. If we plug the definition of specific dimensionless enthalpy
h=1+\varepsilon/c^{2}+P/\rho c^{2}
, we can rewrite it as:

T^{00}=\rho c^{2}\Gamma^{2}+(\rho\varepsilon+P)\Gamma^{2}-P=Dc^{2}(\Gamma-1)+Dc^{2}+(\rho\varepsilon+P)\Gamma^{2}-P

(B11)

where we introduced the laboratory density
D=\rho\Gamma
. Writing the total energy density in this way allows us to distinguish different contributions:

e_{\mathrm{kin}}=Dc^{2}(\Gamma-1)

(B12)

e_{\mathrm{int}}=(\rho\varepsilon+P)\Gamma^{2}-P=(h-1)\rho c^{2}\Gamma^{2}-P=\left(\dfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\Gamma^{2}-1\right)P

(B13)

which are the kinetic and internal energy of the fluid in the lab frame, respectively. In case of a Taub-Matthews EOS we can still find an effective adiabatic factor
\gamma
that allows writing Eq. (
B13
) in the form shown and, generalizing, we can write
\gamma/(\gamma-1)=(h-1)\rho c^{2}/P

[
88
]
.
Integrating Eqs. (
B12
) and (
B13
) over the entire volume gives the total energy of the relativistic fluid in the simulation measured in the laboratory frame.

Appendix D
Afterglow modeling

D.1
Framework

The synchrotron emission of the GRB afterglow is modeled after
Sari et al. [127]
and
Granot et al. [44]
.

The received flux is given by the volume integral:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(T,\theta_{\mathrm{obs}})

\displaystyle=\left.\dfrac{1}{4\pi D^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\phi\int_{0}^{\theta_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}\theta\sin\theta\dfrac{R^{2}\Delta R~\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\nu^{\prime}}}{\Gamma^{2}(1-\beta\mu)^{2}}\right|_{t=T+\mu R/c}

(D1)

\displaystyle\simeq\left.\dfrac{1}{4\pi D^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\theta_{\mathrm{max}}}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\phi\mathrm{d}\theta\sin\theta R^{3}~\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\nu^{\prime}}}{12\Gamma^{4}(1-\beta_{\mathrm{sh}}\mu)(1-\beta\mu)^{2}}\right|_{t=T+\mu R/c}

where we assumed that the observed radiation is produced by a thin shell of width
\Delta R\simeq R/[12\Gamma^{2}(1-\beta_{\mathrm{sh}}\mu)]\ll R_{\mathrm{sh}}
, smaller than the size of the shock radius. In the formula,
D
is the distance from the source,
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\nu^{\prime}}
is
the comoving frame emissivity, and
\mu(\theta,\phi)
is the angle between observer direction and local velocity, defined as:

\mu=\cos\theta\cos\theta_{\mathrm{obs}}+\sin\theta\sin\theta_{\mathrm{obs}}\cos\phi

(D2)

Furthermore, the integral is calculated over different emission times, which depend on the position
(\theta,\phi)
.
The implicit formula

t=T+\dfrac{\mu}{c}R(t)

(D3)

relates the laboratory time
t
at each position
(\theta,\phi)
to a given observer time
T
. Solving it we obtain
t=t(T,\theta,\phi)
for fixed
\theta_{\mathrm{obs}}
. The functional form of
R(t)
is found taking some approximations: we assume that the jet expands in a cold and homogeneous interstellar medium;
the jet is assumed to be axisymmetric, the energy of the blast wave is angle-dependent, and each segment expands spherically with a portion of the total energy given by the local isotropic equivalent energy
E_{\Omega}\equiv\mathrm{d}E(\theta)/\mathrm{d}\Omega=E_{0}f(\theta)
.

Figure 15:
Comparison between the formula used in
van Eerten et al. [144]
for the shock proper velocity and our broken power law expressed by Eq. (
D4
). The curves are plotted in dimensionless time
t_{*}
and are scale-free. The dashed vertical lines indicate the two transition times
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
between the relativistic, trans-relativistic, and the trans-relativistic and Newtonian regimes, respectively. The horizontal dotted lines represent the values of
u_{\mathrm{sh}}
at the transition times. Our formula interpolates the two asymptotic regimes for
t_{*}\rightarrow 0
and
t_{*}\rightarrow\infty
with a softer power law between the two with
u\propto t^{-0.9}
.

Each shock segment is described by an ultra-relativistic blast wave, which gradually decelerates. We distinguish three regimes: i) R:
relativistic
(
\Gamma\beta>1
), in which the evolution of the (proper) velocity of the shock is described by the self-similar solution of
Blandford & McKee [14]
; ii) TR:
trans-relativistic
(
0.1\lesssim\Gamma\beta\leq 1
), which marks the gradual passage from a relativistic blast wave to a Newtonian one and. iii) NR:
non-relativistic
(
\Gamma\beta\lesssim 0.1
), in which the shock dynamics is described by the Sedov-Taylor solution
[
140
,
128
]
.

Unlike
van Eerten et al. [144]
, we do not combine the two self-similar solutions by summing their squares under the square root. Instead, we use a broken power law for the proper velocity to describe the three phases of the shock radius evolution.
The self-similar solution for
\Gamma\beta
reads

(\Gamma\beta)_{\mathrm{sh}}\equiv u_{\mathrm{sh}}=\begin{cases}C_{\mathrm{BM}}t^{-3/2}&\quad\mathrm{(R)},\quad\ \ \mathrm{if}\ \ \Gamma\beta>1\\
C_{\mathrm{BM}}^{3/5}t^{-9/10}&\quad\mathrm{(TR)},\quad\mathrm{if}\ \ 0.12\lesssim\Gamma\beta\leq 1\\
C_{\mathrm{ST}}t^{-3/5}&\quad\mathrm{(NR)},\quad\mathrm{if}\ \ \Gamma\beta\lesssim 0.12\\
\end{cases}

(D4)

where

C_{\mathrm{BM}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{17E_{\Omega}}{8\pi n_{0}m_{\mathrm{p}}c^{5}}}\quad,\quad C_{\mathrm{ST}}=\dfrac{2}{5}\times 1.15\left(\dfrac{E_{\Omega}}{n_{0}m_{\mathrm{p}}c^{5}}\right)^{1/5}

(D5)

are the coefficients of the Blandford-McKee and the Sedov-Taylor self-similar solutions, respectively.

The trans-relativistic (TR) regime is obtained by modifying the Blandford-McKee solution raised to the power of
3/5
, which imposes a different slope to make the transition to a non-relativistic regime smoother.
The transition times between the different regimes are found imposing
(\Gamma\beta)_{\mathrm{R}}=(\Gamma\beta)_{\mathrm{TR}}
, and
(\Gamma\beta)_{\mathrm{TR}}=(\Gamma\beta)_{\mathrm{NR}}
. We find

t_{\mathrm{R}\rightarrow\mathrm{TR}}=C_{\mathrm{BM}}^{2/3}\quad,\quad t_{\mathrm{TR}\rightarrow\mathrm{NR}}=C_{\mathrm{BM}}^{2}C_{\mathrm{ST}}^{-10/3}

(D6)

We can rewrite the formulas more concisely, introducing a characteristic time scale for the blast wave segment such that

t=\alpha t_{*}\quad,\quad R=\alpha cR_{*}\ ,

(D7)

where

\alpha(\theta)=\left(\dfrac{E_{\Omega}}{n_{0}m_{\mathrm{p}}c^{5}}\right)^{1/3}=337~\mathrm{d}\left(\dfrac{E_{0}f(\theta)}{10^{51}~\mathrm{erg}}\right)^{1/3}\left(\dfrac{n_{0}}{1~\mathrm{cm}^{-3}}\right)^{-1/3}\ ,

(D8)

which depends on
\theta
via the local isotropic energy.
We can rewrite the coefficients in Eq. (
D5
) as
C_{\mathrm{BM},*}=\sqrt{17/8\pi}
and
C_{\mathrm{ST},*}=0.46
, and the transition times as:

\displaystyle t_{1,*}=t_{\mathrm{R}\rightarrow\mathrm{TR},*}=\left(\dfrac{17}{8\pi}\right)^{1/3}\simeq 0.878\quad,

(D9)

\displaystyle t_{2,*}=t_{\mathrm{TR}\rightarrow\mathrm{NR},*}=\dfrac{17}{8\pi}\left(\dfrac{2}{5}\times 1.15\right)^{-10/3}\simeq 9\ .

Substituting, these values in Eq. (
D4
) correspond to the values of the proper velocity:

u_{\mathrm{R}\rightarrow\mathrm{TR}}=1\quad,\quad u_{\mathrm{TR}\rightarrow\mathrm{NR}}=C_{\mathrm{ST}}^{3}C_{\mathrm{BM}}^{-6/5}\simeq 0.123

(D10)

A comparison between the proper velocity formula used by
van Eerten et al. [144]
and our approximation is shown in Fig.
15
.

The Lorentz factor of the fluid is related to the Lorentz factor of the shock via the jump conditions, giving
u=u_{\mathrm{sh}}/\sqrt{2}
in the relativistic and trans-relativistic regimes, and
u=3u_{\mathrm{sh}}/4
in the non-relativistic regime.
The shock radius is given by

R_{\mathrm{sh}}(t)=c\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{\mathrm{sh}}(\tilde{t})\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}\quad\longrightarrow\quad R_{\mathrm{sh},*}(t_{*})=\int_{0}^{t_{*}}\beta_{\mathrm{sh}}(\tilde{t})\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}

(D11)

with
\beta_{\mathrm{sh}}=u_{\mathrm{sh}}/\sqrt{1+u_{\mathrm{sh}}^{2}}
. For each regime, the integration is analytical and yields to

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{sh},\mathrm{R},*}={}_{2}F_{1}\left(\dfrac{1}{3},\dfrac{1}{2},\dfrac{4}{3};-\dfrac{t_{*}^{3}}{C_{\mathrm{BM}}^{2}}\right)t_{*},

(D12)

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{sh},\mathrm{TR},*}=10C_{\mathrm{BM}}^{3/5}~{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-\dfrac{1}{18},\dfrac{1}{2},\dfrac{17}{18},-\dfrac{C_{\mathrm{BM}}^{6/5}}{t_{*}^{9/5}}\right)t_{*}^{1/10}

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{sh},\mathrm{NR},*}=C_{\mathrm{ST}}t_{*}^{2/5}

where
{}_{2}F_{1}(\dots)
is Gauss’ hypergeometric function. The integral in Eq. (
D11
) can be broken into the individual contributions of the terms expressed by Eq. (
D12
) for the different regimes:

R_{\mathrm{sh},*}=\begin{cases}R_{\mathrm{sh},\mathrm{R},*}(t_{*})&t_{*}\leq t_{1,*}\\
R_{\mathrm{sh},\mathrm{R},*}(t_{1,*})+\left.R_{\mathrm{sh},\mathrm{TR},*}\right|^{t_{*}}_{t_{1,*}}&t_{1,*}<t_{*}\leq t_{2,*}\\
R_{\mathrm{sh},\mathrm{R},*}(t_{1,*})+\left.R_{\mathrm{sh},\mathrm{TR},*}\right|^{t_{2,*}}_{t_{1,*}}+\left.R_{\mathrm{sh},\mathrm{NR},*}\right|^{t_{*}}_{t_{2,*}}&t_{2,*}<t_{*}\end{cases}

where
\left.x(t)\right|^{b}_{a}=x(b)-x(a)
. Substituting the constants, we find:

R_{\mathrm{sh},*}=\begin{cases}R_{\mathrm{sh},\mathrm{R},*}(t_{*})&\ \ \ \qquad t_{*}\leq t_{1,*}\\
R_{\mathrm{sh},\mathrm{TR},*}(t_{*})-8.176&t_{1,*}<t_{*}\leq t_{2,*}\\
R_{\mathrm{sh},\mathrm{NR},*}(t_{*})+1.8&t_{2,*}<t_{*}\end{cases}

(D13)

This method helps to quickly find the numerical root for
t
in Eq. (
D3
), which can be easily rewritten as
t_{*}=T_{*}+\mu R_{*}(t_{*})
, with
T_{*}=T/\alpha
. When the scale
\alpha(\theta)
becomes very small (i.e. for
\theta\gg\theta_{\mathrm{j}}
and
f(\theta)\ll 1
), then, at later times,
T_{*}
becomes larger than
R_{*}(t_{*})
and we can impose
t\simeq T
to speed up the calculations.

The shocked medium is characterized by its comoving number density
n^{\prime}
, internal energy density
e_{i}^{\prime}
, and magnetic field strength
B^{\prime}
. Those are given as follows

Sari et al. [127]
:

n^{\prime}=4\Gamma n_{0}

(D14)

e_{i}^{\prime}=(\Gamma-1)n^{\prime}m_{\mathrm{p}}c^{2}=4\Gamma(\Gamma-1)n_{0}m_{\mathrm{p}}c^{2}

(D15)

B^{\prime}=\sqrt{8\pi\varepsilon_{\mathrm{B}}e_{i}^{\prime}}

(D16)

where
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{B}}
is a parameter that expresses the energy fraction of the shocked matter going into the magnetic field.

The non-thermal electrons generating the synchrotron emission are described by an energy spectrum with a single power-law
p
, and a constant fraction
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}
of the shock energy is assumed to go into these accelerated electrons.
The synchrotron spectrum is approximated by a broken power law, with a characteristic frequency
\nu^{\prime}_{\mathrm{m}}
and a cooling frequency
\nu^{\prime}_{\mathrm{c}}
.
These are given by:

\nu_{\mathrm{m}}^{\prime}=\dfrac{3}{16}\left[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}\left(\dfrac{p-2}{p-1}\right)\dfrac{m_{\mathrm{p}}}{m_{\mathrm{e}}}(\Gamma-1)\right]^{2}\dfrac{q_{\mathrm{e}}B^{\prime}}{m_{\mathrm{e}}c}

(D17)

\nu_{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}=\dfrac{3}{16}\left[\dfrac{3m_{\mathrm{e}}c\Gamma}{4\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{B}}e_{i}^{\prime}t}\right]^{2}\dfrac{q_{\mathrm{e}}B^{\prime}}{m_{\mathrm{e}}c}

(D18)

where
q_{\mathrm{e}}
is the electron charge and
\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}=8\pi q_{\mathrm{e}}^{4}/(3m_{\mathrm{e}}^{2}c^{4})
is the Thomson cross section.
The emissivity
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\nu^{\prime}}
, in case of slow cooling (
\nu^{\prime}_{\mathrm{m}}<\nu^{\prime}_{\mathrm{c}}
) is given by:

\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\nu^{\prime}}=\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\nu^{\prime},\mathrm{peak}}\begin{cases}(\nu^{\prime}/\nu^{\prime}_{\mathrm{m}})^{1/3}\quad&\nu^{\prime}<\nu^{\prime}_{\mathrm{m}}\\
(\nu^{\prime}/\nu^{\prime}_{\mathrm{m}})^{(1-p)/2}\quad&\nu^{\prime}_{\mathrm{m}}\leq\nu^{\prime}<\nu^{\prime}_{\mathrm{c}}\\
(\nu^{\prime}_{\mathrm{c}}/\nu^{\prime}_{\mathrm{m}})^{(1-p)/2}(\nu^{\prime}/\nu^{\prime}_{\mathrm{c}})^{-p/2}\quad&\nu^{\prime}_{\mathrm{c}}\leq\nu^{\prime}\end{cases}

(D19)

where
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\nu^{\prime},\mathrm{peak}}
is the peak emissivity
[
44
]
:

\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\nu^{\prime},\mathrm{peak}}\simeq 0.88\times\dfrac{256}{27}\left(\dfrac{p-1}{3p-1}\right)\dfrac{q_{\mathrm{e}}^{2}}{m_{e}c^{2}}n^{\prime}B^{\prime}\ .

(D20)

Finally, the rest-frame frequency
\nu^{\prime}
is connected to the observer frequency
\nu
by:

\nu^{\prime}=\Gamma(1-\beta\mu)\nu

(D21)

D.2
Degeneracy relations

The afterglow light curves are subject to degeneracy relations for the peak position and the peak height, derived from the following scaling laws
[
105
,
104
]
:

T_{\mathrm{peak}}\propto E_{0}^{1/3}n_{0}^{-1/3}(\theta_{\mathrm{obs}}-\theta_{\mathrm{j}})^{2}

(D22)

\mathcal{F}_{\nu,\mathrm{peak}}\propto E_{0}n^{\frac{p+1}{4}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}^{p-1}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{B}}^{\frac{p+1}{4}}\theta_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-2p}\nu^{\frac{1-p}{2}}D^{-2}

(D23)

for a fixed observer time
T
. Here,
E_{0}=E_{\Omega}(\theta=0)
. Additionally, the scaling for
\theta_{\mathrm{obs}}
becomes more precise as long as
\theta_{\mathrm{obs}}\gg\theta_{\mathrm{j}}
.
We verified the scaling relations by numerically integrating the light curves for several viewing angles, from
\theta_{\mathrm{obs}}=14^{\circ}
to
60^{\circ}
at steps of
1^{\circ}
, and for four different opening angles of the jet, i.e.
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}=[3,5,6.8,10]^{\circ}
.
We show the results of the peak time and the peak flux as a function of the viewing angle in Fig.
16
. From the fitting lines we see that, for a ratio
\chi=\theta_{\mathrm{obs}}/\theta_{\mathrm{j}}
sufficiently larger than unity, the scaling relation is a power law with slope
\simeq 2.2
(instead of
2
in Eq. (
D22
)) for
T_{\mathrm{peak}}
and a slope of
\simeq-2.4p
for
\mathcal{F}_{\nu,\mathrm{peak}}
(instead of
-2p
from Eq. (
D23
)).
Furthermore, the afterglow peak tends to be wide for
\chi\simeq 1-3
and narrower for
\chi\gg 1
until the shape converges to a certain curve.
Combining Eqs. (
D22
) and (
D23
), and taking into account the corrections above for the scaling with respect to
\theta_{\mathrm{obs}}
, we get the generalized scaling relations for the peak time and the peak flux:

\displaystyle\dfrac{p+5}{4}\log\left(\dfrac{\tilde{n}_{0}}{n_{0}}\right)+\dfrac{p+1}{4}\log\left(\dfrac{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{B}}}{{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{B}}}}\right)+(p-1)\log\left(\dfrac{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{e}}}{{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}}}\right)=

(D24)

\displaystyle=(3l_{1}+l_{2}p)\log\left(\dfrac{\tilde{\theta}_{\mathrm{obs}}}{{\theta_{\mathrm{obs}}}}\right)+3l_{1}\log\left(\dfrac{1-1/\tilde{\chi}}{{1-1/\chi}}\right)

with
l_{1}\simeq 2.2
and
l_{2}\simeq 2.4
.
If the observer’s angle does not change, the second term goes to 0, and the previous equation reduces to the scaling relation of
Takahashi & Ioka [138]
.
We remark that, even if the peak time and peak flux coincide for different viewing angles, the previous equation does not define a family of perfectly superposing curves since the afterglow light curve tends to spread the more the observer gets closer to the jet’s opening angle.
This is visible from the third panel of Fig.
16
where we measured the time
\Delta T
between the peak time
T_{\mathrm{peak}}
and the asymptotic decay of the light curve (i.e., the observer time when the flux decreases by roughly 10% from its peak value), which roughly follows the power law scaling of a spreading jet
[
43
]
.

D.3
Jets described by a generalized Gaussian and their time evolution

The integral of the energy per solid angle of a jet described by Eq. (
6
) is rather complicated and is analytical only for specific integer or fractional values of
s
; thus, it is better to approximate the integrated function using a Taylor series expansion of the result around
\theta_{\mathrm{c}}=0
, as it follows:

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{j}}

\displaystyle=2\pi E_{0}\int_{0}^{\theta_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}\theta\sin\theta\exp\left[-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{\theta}{\theta_{\mathrm{c}}}\right)^{s}\right]

(D25)

\displaystyle=2\pi E_{0}\theta_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}(c_{1}-c_{2}\theta_{\mathrm{c}}^{2})+\mathcal{O}(\theta_{\mathrm{c}}^{6})

Expressing
\sin\theta
as a Taylor series around
\theta_{\mathrm{c}}=0
and integrating term-by-term (assuming that
\theta_{\mathrm{max}}\gg\theta_{\mathrm{c}}
) we find the following expression for the integral:

E_{\mathrm{j}}(\theta_{\mathrm{c}},s)=\dfrac{2\pi E_{0}\theta^{2}_{\mathrm{c}}}{s}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\dfrac{\theta_{\mathrm{c}}^{2n}4^{\frac{n+1}{s}}}{(2n+1)!}\Gamma\left(\dfrac{2n+2}{s}\right),

(D26)

where
\Gamma(x)
is Gauss’ Gamma function in this context.
We find that the first two terms
c_{1}(s)
and
c_{2}(s)
have the following exact forms:

c_{1}(s)=\dfrac{4^{1/s}}{s}\Gamma\left(\dfrac{2}{s}\right)\quad,\quad c_{2}(s)=\dfrac{4^{2/s}}{6s}\Gamma\left(\dfrac{4}{s}\right)\ .

(D27)

For
s\rightarrow\infty
, the edges of the jet become infinitely sharp, and the Gamma functions of the coefficients become
\simeq s/(2n+2)
. The jet shape degenerates into a Heaviside step function
E_{0}\Theta(\theta_{c}-\theta)
, thus the integral of Eq. (
D25
), dropping a factor
2\pi
, will give exactly
1-\cos(\theta_{\mathrm{c}})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{j+1}\frac{1}{2j!}\theta_{\mathrm{c}}^{2j}=\frac{1}{2!}\theta_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}-\frac{1}{4!}\theta_{\mathrm{c}}^{4}+\ldots
, which are the asymptotic limits of the coefficients
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
.

In Fig.
17
, we show the time evolution of the fitting parameters for four values of the opening angle
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}=[3,5,6.8,10]^{\circ}
and fixed
L_{\mathrm{j}}=10^{51}~\mathrm{erg}/\mathrm{s}
.
We took snapshots from
25~\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s}
to
450~\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s}
of continuous jet injection, at
\Delta t=25~\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s}
from each other.
We cut off the rest of the slow ejecta in each snapshot and fitted the on-axis peak with a generalized Gaussian.
We used Eqs. (
D25
) and (
D27
) to calculate the total energy under the fitting function and compared it to the jet energy for each given time.
We see that for all the runs but one (
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}=10^{\circ}
), the result matches the expected jet energy very well.
The discrepancy at larger angles can be explained considering that there is a non-negligible amount of energy outside the peak, thus giving less than expected energy.
The value of
\theta_{\mathrm{c}}
grows in the first 250 ms of injection to roughly stabilize around the value of the jet’s opening angle
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}
. Also, the shape parameter of the Gaussian seems to converge to a certain value at later times, around
5
. However, due to its wide angular spread, the run with
\theta_{\mathrm{j}}=10^{\circ}
seems to jump to higher values (flatter central distribution). Not surprisingly,
\theta_{\mathrm{c}}
and the shape parameters are linearly correlated at later times, as shown in the bottom-right panel of the figure.
From these data, we can conclude that taking the energy distribution
E_{\Omega}
too early might strongly affect the final result when the synthetic light curves are calculated since its parameters tend to stabilize at later times.
