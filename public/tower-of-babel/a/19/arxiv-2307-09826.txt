On Rota-Baxter vertex operator algebras
Chengming Bai, Li Guo, Jianqi Liu, Xiaoyan Wang

Abstract
. Derivations play a fundamental role in the definition of vertex (operator) algebras, sometimes regarded as a generalization of differential commutative algebras. This paper studies the role played by the integral counter part of the derivations, namely Rota-Baxter operators, in vertex (operator) algebras. The closely related notion of dendriform algebras is also defined for vertex operator algebras. It is shown that the classical relations among dendriform algebras, associative algebras and Rota-Baxter algebras are preserved for their vertex algebra analogs.

1.
Introduction

This paper studies Rota-Baxter operators on vertex operator algebras (VOAs), as an integral counterpart of the derivations which play an essential role in VOAs. The closely related dendriform algebras for VOAs are also studied.

1.1.
Vertex operator algebras and derivations

Vertex algebras and VOAs, introduced by Borcherds in
[
11
]
and Frenkel, Lepowsky and Meurman in
[
21
]
respectively, were developed in conjunction with conformal field theory, “monstrous moonshine”, string theory and infinite-dimensional Lie algebras, with applications in geometric Langlands program. See
[
21
,
29
,
22
,
23
,
43
]
.

On the other hand, the study of differential algebras, defined to
be an associative algebra equipped with a derivation, has its
origin in Ritt’s algebraic approach to differential
equations
[
40
]
and further developed by Kolchin
[
30
]
.
Since then, the subject has evolved into a vast area involving
differential aspects of Galois theory, algebraic geometry,
computational algebra and logic (see for example
[
39
]
). More
generally, differential algebras with weights were introduced as
an abstraction of the differential quotients whose limits define
the derivation in analysis
[
25
]
.

A vertex algebra in general can be viewed as a
generalization of a commutative differential algebra. In fact, any
commutative differential unital algebra
(A,D,1)
is naturally a
vertex algebra (cf.
[
11
]
). More generally, the notion of vertex
algebras, as well
as the related notion of vertex algebras without vacuum (cf.
[
28
]
), can be equivalently formulated
in terms of a “weakly commutative vertex operator” equipped with a special
derivation. See
[
29
,
28
,
32
,
36
]
and Theorem
2.3

and Proposition
2.6
. On the other hand, the study of derivations of vertex (operator) algebras has drawn much attention. The derivations of VOAs naturally give rise to automorphisms of VOAs (cf.
[
20
,
14
]
), which is an important notion that relates to many subbranches like the moonshine conjecture, orbifold theory, and quantum Galois theory, etc., see
[
21
,
18
,
15
]
. Huang observed in
[
26
]
that the set of derivations of a VOA with coefficients in an ordinary module can be identified with the first-order cohomology of VOAs, similar to the case of Lie algebras and associative algebras. The structure of the derivation algebras of strongly rational VOAs was determined by Dong and Griess in
[
14
]
. They also proved in
[
14
]
that the derivation algebra of a strongly rational VOA
V
generates the connected component of the automorphism group of
V
.

1.2.
Vertex operator algebras and Rota-Baxter operators

Given the close relationship between derivations and vertex (operator)
algebras, it is natural to study the role played by integrations
in vertex (operator) algebras. The integral counterpart of the differential
algebra (with a weight) is the Rota-Baxter algebra, whose study originated from the 1960 work of
G. Baxter
[
10
]
in probability and can be tracked back
further under the disguise of a linear
transformation
[
13
,
42
]
. For Lie algebras, they were
rediscovered in the 1980s as the operator forms of the classical
Yang-Baxter equation (CYBE) and modified Yang-Baxter
equation. To further develop the operator forms of the
CYBE
[
41
]
, the more general notion of a relative Rota-Baxter
operator was introduced by Kupershmidt who called it an

\mathcal{O}
-operator.
[
31
]

Rota-Baxter operators have been defined on a very wide range of algebraic structures, and indeed on algebraic operads
[
38
]
. Given the importance of VOAs and Rota-Baxter algebras, it is a natural question to investigate the possibility of combining the theories of Rota-Baxter operators and VOAs. However, due to the complexity of VOAs, this has not been carried out.

The purpose of this paper is to define, on VOAs, the closely related notions of differential operators, Rota-Baxter operators and dendriform algebras. As it turns out, in
a special case, the axiom of Rota-Baxter operators on VOAs coincides with X. Xu’s definition of
R
-matrices for VOAs
[
44
]
, as a vertex operator algebra analog of the
r
-matrices
for Lie algebras as solutions to the CYBE. In a separate work

[
5
]
, we introduced the tensor form of the CYBE for VOAs.

A closely related notion to the (relative) Rota-Baxter associative
algebra is the dendriform algebra, introduced by Loday from the
periodicity of
K
-theory and plays an essential
role of splitting of the associativity. In fact, the (tri)dendriform algebra can be
naturally derived from Rota-Baxter algebra. It can further be
characterized by (relative) Rota-Baxter algebras
in terms of representations (see
[
7
]
for example). Since a
vertex algebra can be viewed as a generalization of both the commutative differential algebra and the Lie algebra, it is natural to investigate the analogs of these
structures and their relations with (relative) Rota-Baxter
operators in the context of vertex (operator) algebras.

2.1.
The definition of vertex (operator) algebras

We briefly recall the background on VOAs that will be needed in the sequel and refer the readers to

Definition 2.1
.

A
vertex algebra
is a triple
(V,Y,{\bf 1})
consisting of a vector space
V
,
a linear map
Y
called the
vertex operator
or the state-field correspondence:

\displaystyle Y:

\displaystyle V\to(\mbox{End}\,V)[[z,z^{-1}]],\quad a\mapsto Y(a,z)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a_{n}z^{-n-1}\ \ \ \ (a_{n}\in\mbox{End}\,V),

and a distinguished element
{\bf 1}\in V
called the
vacuum vector
, satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

(Truncation property) For any given
a,b\in V
,
a_{n}b=0
when
n
is sufficiently large.

(ii)

(Vacuum property)
Y(\mathbf{1},z)=\mathrm{Id}_{V}
.

(iii)

(Creation property) For
a\in V
, we have
Y(a,z){\bf 1}\in V[[z]]
and
\lim\limits_{z\to 0}Y(a,z){\bf 1}=a
.

(iv)

(The Jacobi identity) For
a,b\in V
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle{z^{-1}_{0}\delta\left(\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{z_{0}}\right)Y(a,z_{1})Y(b,z_{2})-z^{-1}_{0}\delta\left(\frac{-z_{2}+z_{1}}{z_{0}}\right)Y(b,z_{2})Y(a,z_{1})}\displaystyle{=z_{2}^{-1}\delta\left(\frac{z_{1}-z_{0}}{z_{2}}\right)Y(Y(a,z_{0})b,z_{2})}.

Here
\delta(x):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}x^{n}
is the formal delta function.

The following equivalent characterization of the Jacobi identity is useful in our later discussions (cf.
[
32
]
; see also
[
20
]
).

Theorem 2.2
.

A vertex algebra
(V,Y,\mathbf{1})
satisfies the following properties.

(i)

(
weak commutativity
)
For
a,b\in V
, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(1)

(z_{1}-z_{2})^{k}Y(a,z_{1})Y(b,z_{2})=(z_{1}-z_{2})^{k}Y(b,z_{2})Y(a,z_{1}).

(ii)

(
weak associativity
)
For
a,b,c\in V
, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}

(
depending on
a
and
c
)
such that

(2)

(z_{0}+z_{2})^{k}Y(Y(a,z_{0})b,z_{2})c=(z_{0}+z_{2})^{k}Y(a,z_{0}+z_{2})Y(b,z_{2})c.

Moreover, if
Y:V\rightarrow(\mathrm{End}V)[[z,z^{-1}]]
is a linear map that satisfies the truncation property, then the Jacobi identity of
Y
in the definition of a vertex algebra is equivalent to the weak commutativity together with the weak associativity.

Let
(V,Y,\mathbf{1})
be a vertex algebra. Define a translation operator
D:V\rightarrow V
by letting

Da:=a_{-2}\mathbf{1},

for all
a\in V
. Then
(V,Y,D,\mathbf{1})
satisfies the
D
-derivative property
:

(3)

Y(Da,z)=\frac{d}{dz}Y(a,z),

the
D
-bracket derivative property
:

(4)

[D,Y(a,z)]=\frac{d}{dz}Y(a,z),

and the
skew-symmetry
formula:

(5)

Y(a,z)b=e^{zD}Y(b,-z)a,\quad a,b\in V.

Equalities (
3
) and (
4
) together are called the
D
-translation invariance property
.
On the other hand, a vertex algebra also has the following equivalent condition (cf.
[
32
]
).

Theorem 2.3
.

Consider a triple
(V,Y,\mathbf{1})
consisting of a vector space
V
, a
linear map
Y:V\to(\mathrm{End}V)[[z,z^{-1}]]
and a distinguished
vector
\mathbf{1}
. The triple is a vertex algebra if and
only if it satisfies the truncation, vacuum and creation
properties (
i
)–(
iii
), the weak
commutativity (
1
), and allows a derivation
D
that
satisfies the
D
-bracket derivative property (
4
).

Our later discussion needs the following weaker notions of vertex algebras introduced in
[
36
]
.

Definition 2.4
.

A
vertex Leibniz algebra

(V,Y)
is a vector space
V
equipped with a linear map
Y:V\rightarrow(\mathrm{End}V)[[z,z^{-1}]]
, satisfying the truncation property and the Jacobi identity.

As a special case, a subspace
U
of a vertex algebra
(V,Y,\mathbf{1})
is a vertex Leibniz subalgebra with respect to the restricted vertex operator
Y|_{U}
if it satisfies
a_{n}b\in U
, for all
a,b\in U
, and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. A related notion is the vertex algebra without vacuum (see
[
28
]
):

Definition 2.5
.

A
vertex algebra without vacuum
is a vector space
V
, equipped with a linear map
Y:V\rightarrow(\mathrm{End}V)[[z,z^{-1}]]
and a linear operator
D:V\rightarrow V
satisfying the truncation property, the Jacobi identity, the
D
-derivative property (
3
), and the skew-symmetry (

). We denote a vertex algebra without vacuum by
(V,Y,D)
.

The following fact is proved by Huang and Lepowsky in
[
28
]
; see also
[
36
]
:

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
V
be a vector space, equipped with a linear map
Y:V\rightarrow(\mathrm{End}V)[[z,z^{-1}]]
, satisfying the truncation property. If
D:V\rightarrow V
is another linear map that satisfies the

-bracket derivative property (
4

1

Definition 2.7
.

A
vertex operator algebra (VOA)
is a quadruple
(V,Y,\mathbf{1},\omega)
, where

•

(V,Y(\cdot,z),\mathbf{1})
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded vertex algebra:
V=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}V_{n},

such that
{\bf 1}\in V_{0}
,
\text{dim}V_{n}<\infty

, and
V_{n}=0
for

•

{\omega}\in V_{2}

and for which we denote
Y(\omega,z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}L(n)z^{-n-2}
, so
L(n)=\omega_{n+1}
,

. It satisfies the following additional conditions.

(5)

(The Virasoro relation)

[L(m),L(n)]=(m-n)L(m+n)+\frac{1}{12}(m^{3}-m)\delta_{m+n,0}c,

where

(6)

(

-derivation property)

(7)

(

L(0)v=nv
, for all

n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

A VOA

is said to be of the

V=V_{0}\oplus V_{+}

V_{0}=\mathbb{C}\mathbf{1}

V_{+}=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}V_{n}

We also need the notion of modules over VOAs (cf.

Let

(i)

(ii)

(Vacuum property)

(iii)

(The Jacobi identity) For

\displaystyle z_{0}^{-1}\delta\left(\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{z_{0}}\right)Y_{W}(a,z_{1})Y_{W}(b,z_{2})u-z_{0}^{-1}\delta\left(\frac{-z_{2}+z_{1}}{z_{0}}\right)Y_{W}(b,z_{2})Y_{W}(a,z_{1})u

\displaystyle=z_{2}^{-1}\delta\left(\frac{z_{1}-z_{0}}{z_{2}}\right)Y_{W}(Y(a,z_{0})b,z_{2})u.

A weak

.

An

is an admissible

Let

2.2.
The
\lambda
-derivations on VOAs

Recall that a derivation on a VOA
(V,Y,\mathbf{1},\omega)
is a linear map

d:V\rightarrow V
satisfying
d\mathbf{1}=0
,
d\omega=0
, and

d(a_{n}b)=(da)_{n}b+a_{n}(db)
for all
a,b\in V
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
(cf.

[
14
,
26
]
). We introduce the following generalized notion of
derivations with weights for VOAs.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
(V,Y,\mathbf{1})
be a vertex algebra, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
be a fixed complex number. A linear map
d:V\rightarrow V
is called a
weak
\lambda
-derivation
of
V
if it satisfies

(6)

d(Y(a,z)b)=Y(da,z)b+Y(a,z)db+\lambda Y(da,z)db,\quad a,b\in V.

In other words,

d(a_{m}b)=(da)_{m}b+a_{m}(db)+\lambda(da)_{m}(db),
for all
a,b\in V,m\in\mathbb{Z}.

Let
(V,Y,\mathbf{1},\omega)
be a VOA. A

\lambda
-derivation
on
V
is a weak
\lambda
-derivation
d:V\rightarrow V
such that
d\mathbf{1}=0
and
d\omega=0
. The space of
\lambda
-derivations on
V
is denoted by
\mathrm{Diff}_{\lambda}(V)
.

In particular, for a
\lambda
-derivation
d:V\rightarrow V
on a VOA
(V,Y,\mathbf{1},\omega)
, since
d\omega=0
, by (
6
) we have

d(Y(\omega,z)b)=Y(d\omega,a)+Y(\omega,z)db+\lambda Y(d\omega,z)db
, which implies

d(Y(\omega,z)b)=Y(\omega,z)db
and hence
dL(-1)b=L(-1)db
for
b\in V.

Thus, the
\lambda
-derivation
d
is automatically compatible with the derivation
D=L(-1)
on the VOA:
dL(-1)=L(-1)d
.

By Definition
2.9
, it is easy to see that a
0
-differential operator is just a derivation on
V
. i.e.,
\mathrm{Diff}_{0}(V)=\mathrm{Der}(V)
. The
1
-derivations have a nice correspondence with the automorphisms on
V
. Recall that an endomorphism of
V
is a linear map
\phi:V\rightarrow V
such that

(7)

\phi(Y(a,z)b)=Y(\phi(a),z)\phi(b),

\phi(\mathbf{1})=\mathbf{1}
, and
\phi(\omega)=\omega
(cf.
[
20
]
). The space of endomorphisms on
V
is denoted by
\mathrm{End}(V)
.

Lemma 2.10
.

If
(V,Y,\mathbf{1},\omega)
is a simple VOA,
then
\mathrm{End}(V)
is a division algebra over
\mathbb{C}
, with the unit group
\mathrm{Aut}(V)
.

Proof.

Let
\phi\in\mathrm{End}_{V}(V)
and
\phi\neq 0
. It suffices to show that
\phi
is an automorphism. First we note that
\ker\phi
is an ideal of
V
: for
u\in\ker\phi
,
a\in V
, and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have
\phi(a_{m}u)=\phi(a)_{m}\phi(u)=0
by (
7
). Since
\phi\neq 0
, we have
\ker\phi=0
, and
\phi
is injective.
Moreover, for
a\in V_{n}
, since
\phi(\omega)=\omega
, we have
L(0)\phi(a)=\phi(\omega)_{1}\phi(a)=\phi(L(0)a)=n\phi(a)
. It follows that
\phi(V_{n})\subseteq V_{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\dim V_{n}<\infty
, we have
\phi|_{V_{n}}:V_{n}\rightarrow V_{n}
is a linear isomorphism. Thus,
\phi
is an automorphism.
∎

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
(V,Y,\mathbf{1},\omega)
be a simple VOA. Then the map
\alpha:\mathrm{Diff}_{1}(V)\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(V):d\mapsto d+\mathrm{Id}_{V}
is a bijection.

Proof.

Since
d\mathbf{1}=0
and
d\omega=0
, we have
(d+\mathrm{Id}_{V})(\mathbf{1})=\mathbf{1}
and
(d+\mathrm{Id}_{V})(\omega)=\omega
. Moreover,

\displaystyle(d+\mathrm{Id})(Y(a,z)b)

\displaystyle=Y(da,z)b+Y(a,z)db+Y(da,z)db+Y(a,z)b

\displaystyle=Y(da+a,z)b+Y(a+da,z)db

\displaystyle=Y((d+\mathrm{Id})(a),z)(d+\mathrm{Id})(b).

Thus
d+\mathrm{Id}_{V}\in\mathrm{End}_{V}(V)
. But
d+\mathrm{Id}_{V}\neq 0
, since
otherwise
d=-\mathrm{Id}_{V}
does not satisfy
d\mathbf{1}=0
. Hence
\alpha(d)=d+\mathrm{Id}_{V}
is in
(\mathrm{End}_{V}(V))^{\times}=\mathrm{Aut}(V)
, in view of
Lemma
2.10
.

On the other hand, for
\phi\in\mathrm{Aut}(V)
, we have
(\phi-\mathrm{Id})(\mathbf{1})=0
and
(\phi-\mathrm{Id})(\omega)=0
, and

\displaystyle Y((\phi-\mathrm{Id})(a),z)b+Y(a,z)(\phi-\mathrm{Id})(b)+Y((\phi-\mathrm{Id})(a),z)(\phi-\mathrm{Id})(b)

\displaystyle=Y(\phi(a),z)b-Y(a,z)b+Y(a,z)\phi(b)-Y(a,z)b+Y(\phi(a),z)\phi(b)-Y(a,z)\phi(b)

\displaystyle\ \ -Y(\phi(a),z)b+Y(a,z)b

\displaystyle=(\phi-\mathrm{Id})(Y(a,z)b).

Thus,
\phi-\mathrm{Id}
is in
\mathrm{Diff}_{1}(V)
. Clearly,
\phi\mapsto\phi-\mathrm{Id}_{V}
is the inverse of
\alpha
. Hence
\alpha
is a bijection.
∎

3.
Rota-Baxter operators on vertex algebras

In this section, we introduce various types of Rota-Baxter operators on vertex algebras and the notion of Rota-Baxter vertex (operator) algebras. We will give a few examples of Rota-Baxter vertex algebras, some of which generalize the classical Rota-Baxter associative algebras and the Rota-Baxter Lie algebras.

3.1.
Definition and first properties of Rota-Baxter operators on vertex algebras

Recall that a
Rota-Baxter (associative) algebra
of weight
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
(cf.
[
24
]
) is an associative algebra
(R,\cdot)
, equipped with a linear map
P:R\rightarrow R
, called the
Rota-Baxter operator (RBO)
, satisfying

P(a)\cdot P(b)=P(P(a)\cdot b)+P(a\cdot P(b))+\lambda P(a\cdot b),\quad a,b\in R.

Similarly, a
Rota-Baxter Lie algebra
of weight
\lambda
is a Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\cdot,\cdot])
equipped with a linear map
P:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}
such that

[P(a),P(b)]=P([P(a),b])+P([a,P(b)])+\lambda P([a,b]),\quad a,b\in\mathfrak{g}.

The Rota-Baxter operator can be defined over various other algebraic structures
[

8

The vertex operator

can be viewed as a generating Laurent series of the infinitely many component binary operations

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(V,Y,\bf{1})
be a vertex algebra,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
be a fixed complex number, and

(i)

(Pa)_{m}(Pb)=P(a_{m}(Pb))+P((Pa)_{m}b)+\lambda P(a_{m}b),\quad a,b\in V.

We denote the set of

(ii)

of weight

is a linear map

) for every

. In other words,

satisfies the following condition:

Y(Pa,z)Pb=P(Y(Pa,z)b)+P(Y(a,z)Pb)+\lambda P(Y(a,z)b),\quad a,b\in V.

We denote the set of RBOs on
V
by
\mathrm{RBO}(V)=\bigcap_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{RBO}(V)(m)
.

(iii)

An (
m
-)ordinary RBO

on

is called

if

, where

is the translation operator:
Da=a_{-2}\mathbf{1}
.

(iv)

Let

be a VOA, and let

be an (
m
-)ordinary RBO.

is called

. Degree zero RBOs are called

.

A

is a vertex algebra

of weight

Proof of Proposition

\displaystyle Y(Pt_{m},z)Pt_{n}

\displaystyle=Y(t_{m+1},z)t_{n+1}=\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{(m+n+2-j)!}{(m+1-j)!(n+1)!j!}t_{m+n+2-j}z^{j},

\displaystyle P(Y(Pt_{m},z)t_{n})

\displaystyle=P(Y(t_{m+1},z)t_{n})=P\bigg(\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{(m+n+1-j)!}{(m+1-j)!n!j!}t_{m+n+1-j}z^{j}\bigg)

\displaystyle P(Y(t_{m},z)Pt_{n})

\displaystyle=P(Y(t_{m},z)t_{n+1})=P\bigg(\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{(m+n+1-j)!}{(m-j)!(n+1)!j!}t_{m+n+1-j}z^{j}\bigg)

\displaystyle h^{1}(-n_{1})\dots h^{k}(-n_{k})\alpha(-1)^{m}\mathbf{1}

3.3.
Properties and further examples of RBVAs

The next theorem generalizes a basic property of RBOs (see

[
24
, Theorem 1.1.13]
) and provides a systematic way to produce
examples of RBVAs.

Theorem 3.16
.

Let
(V,Y,\mathbf{1})
be a vertex algebra and
P:V\rightarrow V
a linear map. Then
P
is an idempotent RBO of weight
-1
, if and only if
V
admits a decomposition:
V=V^{1}\oplus V^{2}
into a direct sum of vertex Leibniz subalgebras
V^{1}
and
V^{2}
, and
P:V\rightarrow V^{1}
is the projection map onto
V^{1}
:

P(a^{1}+a^{2})=a^{1},\quad a^{1}\in V^{1},a^{2}\in V^{2}.

In particular,
V^{1}=P(V)
and
V^{2}=(\mathrm{Id}-P)(V)
.

Proof.

Let
P:V\rightarrow V
be an idempotent RBO of weight
-1
. Then the idempotency gives the direct sum
V=P(V)\oplus(\mathrm{Id}-P)(V)
. Further,
V^{1}=P(V)\subseteq V
is closed under the vertex operator
Y
, since by (
9
), we have

(Pa)_{n}(Pb)=P(a_{n}P(b)+P(a)_{n}b-a_{n}b)\in P(V),\quad a,b\in V.

Hence
(V^{1},Y|_{V^{1}})
is a vertex Leibniz subalgebra of
V
. By Proposition
3.10
,
V^{2}=(\mathrm{Id}-P)(V)
is also a vertex Leibniz subalgebra.

Conversely, suppose that
V
has a decomposition
V=V^{1}\oplus V^{2}
into vertex Leibniz subalgebras, and
P:V\rightarrow V^{1}
is the projection. Then
P
is idempotent. Further, for
a=a^{1}+a^{2}
and
b=b^{1}+b^{2}
in
V
, with
a^{i},b^{i}\in V^{i}
for
i=1,2
, we have

\displaystyle P(a)_{n}P(b)

\displaystyle=a^{1}_{n}b^{1},\quad P((Pa)_{n}b)=P(a^{1}_{n}b^{1}+a^{1}_{n}b^{2})=a^{1}_{n}b^{1}+P(a^{1}_{n}b^{2}),

\displaystyle P(a_{n}P(b))

\displaystyle=P(a^{1}_{n}b^{1}+a^{2}_{n}b^{1})=a^{1}_{n}b^{1}+P(a^{2}_{n}b^{1}),

\displaystyle P(a_{n}b)

\displaystyle=P(a^{1}_{n}b^{1}+a^{1}_{n}b^{2}+a^{2}_{n}b^{1}+a^{2}_{n}b^{2})=a^{1}_{n}b^{1}+P(a^{1}_{n}b^{2})+P(a^{2}_{n}b^{1}).

It follows that
P(a)_{n}P(b)=P((Pa)_{n}b)+P(a_{n}P(b))-P(a_{n}b)
, for all
a,b\in V
. Thus
P:V\rightarrow V
is an idempotent RBO of weight
-1
.
∎

Example 3.17
.

Let

\displaystyle Y(\alpha(-1)\mathbf{1},z)=\alpha(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha(n)z^{-n-1},

There is another general construction of ordinary RBOs on vertex algebras. First, we have the following lemma whose proof is straightforward.

Lemma 3.18
.

Let
(V,Y,\mathbf{1})
be a vertex algebra, and
(A,\cdot,1)
be a commutative unital associative algebra. Let
\hat{V}:=V\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}A
. Extend
Y:V\rightarrow\mathrm{End}(V)[[z,z^{-1}]]
to

(24)

\hat{Y}:\hat{V}\rightarrow\mathrm{End}(\hat{V})[[z,z^{-1}]],\quad\hat{Y}(a\otimes f,z)(b\otimes g):=Y(a,z)b\otimes f\cdot g,\quad a,b\in V,f,g\in A.

Then
(\hat{V},\hat{Y},\mathbf{1}\otimes 1)
is a vertex algebra, and the translation operator
\hat{D}:\hat{V}\rightarrow\hat{V}
is given by
\hat{D}(a\otimes f)=a_{-2}\mathbf{1}\otimes f=D(a)\otimes f
.

Proposition 3.19
.

With the notations as above, let
(V,Y,\mathbf{1})
be a vertex algebra, and
(A,\cdot,1,P)
be a commutative unital Rota-Baxter algebra. Let

(25)

\hat{P}:\hat{V}\rightarrow\hat{V},\quad\hat{P}(a\otimes f):=a\otimes P(f),\quad a\otimes f\in\hat{V}.

Then
(\hat{V},\hat{Y},\mathbf{1}\otimes 1,\hat{P})
is an Rota-Baxter vertex algebra of weight
\lambda
.

Proof.

By (
24
) and (
25
), we have

\displaystyle\hat{P}\left(\hat{Y}(\hat{P}(a\otimes f),z)b\otimes g\right)

\displaystyle=\hat{P}\left(Y(a,z)b\otimes P(f)\cdot g\right)=Y(a,z)b\otimes P(P(f)\cdot g),

\displaystyle\hat{P}\left(\hat{Y}(a\otimes f,z)\hat{P}(b\otimes g)\right)

\displaystyle=\hat{P}\left(Y(a,z)b\otimes f\cdot P(g)\right)=Y(a,z)b\otimes P(f\cdot P(g)),

\displaystyle\lambda\hat{P}\left(\hat{Y}(a\otimes f,z)b\otimes g\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\hat{P}\left(Y(a,z)b\otimes f\cdot g\right)=Y(a,z)b\otimes\lambda P(f\cdot g),

\displaystyle\hat{Y}\left(\hat{P}(a\otimes f),z\right)\hat{P}(b\otimes g)

\displaystyle=\hat{Y}\left(a\otimes P(f),z\right)b\otimes P(g)=Y(a,z)b\otimes P(f)\cdot P(g),

for all
a,b\in V
, and
f,g\in A
. Since
P(f)\cdot P(g)=P(P(f)\cdot g)+P(f\cdot P(g))+\lambda P(f\cdot g)
, it follows that
\hat{P}:\hat{V}\rightarrow\hat{V}
is a RBO of the vertex algebra
\hat{V}
of weight
\lambda
.
∎

Although it is generally not easy to classify all the Rota-Baxter operators on a given VOA, we can determine all the homogeneous Rota-Baxter operators of non-positive degree on certain CFT-type VOAs (as defined in Definition

Lemma 3.21
.

Let
(V,Y,\mathbf{1},\omega)
be a VOA of CFT-type, and

be a homogeneous RBO of weight
\lambda\neq 0

and degree

. Then we have
P(\mathbf{1})=0
or
P(\mathbf{1})=-\lambda\mathbf{1}
. Furthermore,
P^{2}+\lambda P=0
.

Proof.

Since
V_{n}=0
for
n<0
,
V_{0}=\mathbb{C}\mathbf{1}
, and
PV_{0}\subseteq V_{N}
for some
N\leq 0
, we have
P(\mathbf{1})=\mu\mathbf{1}
for some
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
. Recall that
\mathbf{1}_{-1}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
. Then by (
9
), we have

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{1})_{-1}P(\mathbf{1})=P(P(\mathbf{1})_{-1}\mathbf{1})+P(\mathbf{1}_{-1}P(\mathbf{1}))+\lambda P(\mathbf{1}_{-1}\mathbf{1}),

yielding
\mu^{2}\mathbf{1}=\mu^{2}\mathbf{1}+\mu^{2}\mathbf{1}+\lambda\mu\mathbf{1}.

Hence
\mu
is either
0
or
-\lambda
. i.e.,
P(\mathbf{1})=0
or
-\lambda\mathbf{1}
. Furthermore, again by (
9

(26)

P(a)_{-1}P(\mathbf{1})=P(P(a)_{-1}\mathbf{1})+P(a_{-1}P(\mathbf{1}))+\lambda P(a_{-1}\mathbf{1}),\quad a\in V.

If
P(\mathbf{1})=0

∎

Proposition 3.22
.

Let
(V,Y,\mathbf{1},\omega)
be a VOA of CFT-type, and
P:V\rightarrow V
be a homogeneous RBO of weight
\lambda\neq 0
and degree

V=V^{1}\oplus V^{2}
, where

and
V^{2}

P(\mathbf{1})=0

P(\mathbf{1})=-\lambda\mathbf{1}

V^{2}_{0}=0

Since

\lambda\neq 0

3.10

Definition 3.24

\displaystyle Y^{\star_{P}}(Da,z)b

for

4.
Dendriform vertex algebras

In this section, we study the dendriform structure under the framework of vertex algebras, which has an intimate relation with the Rota-Baxter operators on vertex algebras. Since the usual dendriform axioms are the splitting axioms of associativity, while the key axiom of a vertex algebra is the Jacobi identity, we take the viewpoint that the Jacobi identity is the combination of weak associativity together with the
D
-bracket derivative property and skew-symmetry (cf.
[
28
,
34
]
), and define our dendriform structures gradually. In particular, we show that our definitions preserve the usual properties of a dendriform algebra, and they give rise to some new kinds of Jacobi identities (see Theorem
4.11
).

4.1.
Dendriform field and vertex algebras

A
dendriform algebra
is a vector space
A
over a field
k
, equipped with two binary operators
\prec
and
\succ
, satisfying

(30)

\displaystyle(x\prec y)\prec z

\displaystyle=x\prec(y\prec z+y\succ z),

(31)

\displaystyle(x\succ y)\prec z

\displaystyle=x\succ(y\prec z),

(32)

\displaystyle(x\prec y+x\succ y)\succ z

\displaystyle=x\succ(y\succ z),\quad x,y,z\in A.

Given a dendriform algebra
(A,\prec,\succ)
, the product

(33)

x\star y:=x\prec y+x\succ y,\quad x,y\in A.

is associative. Furthermore, Rota-Baxter algebras give rise to dendriform algebras.
For instance, an Rota-Baxter algebra
(R,P)
of weight
0
defines a dendriform algebra
(R,\prec_{P},\succ_{P})

[
2
]
, where

(34)

x\prec_{P}y=xP(y),\qquad x\succ_{P}y=P(x)y,\quad x,y\in R.

The associative analog of vertex algebras is the notion of field
algebras
[
9
]
, or the nonlocal vertex
algebras
[
34
]
. A
field algebra

(V,Y,\mathbf{1},D)
is a vector space
V
, equipped with a linear map
Y:V\rightarrow(\mathrm{End}V)[[z,z^{-1}]]
, a distinguished vector

\mathbf{1}
, and a linear map
D:V\rightarrow V
, satisfying the truncation
property, the vacuum and creation properties, the
D
(-bracket)
derivative properties (
3
) and (
4
), and the weak
associativity (
1
).

Since the axioms of a dendriform are extracted from the
associativity axiom, the weak associativity axiom (
2
) of
a field algebra is enough for our purpose. We introduce the
following notion, which can be viewed as a weaker version of both
field algebras and vertex Leibniz algebras.

Definition 4.1
.

A
field Leibniz algebra
is a vector space
V
, equipped with a linear map
Y:V\rightarrow(\mathrm{End}V)[[z,z^{-1}]]
, satisfying the truncation property and the weak associativity (
2
). We denote a field Leibniz algebra by
(V,Y)
.

An
ordinary Rota-Baxter operator (ordinary RBO)
on a field Leibniz algebra
(V,Y)
of weight
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is a linear map
P:V\rightarrow V
, satisfying the compatibility (
9
).

By a similar argument as the proof of Proposition
2.6
(cf.
[
28
]
), together with Lemma 2.7 in
[
36
]
, we can derive the following relation between the field Leibniz algebras and the vertex algebras without vacuum.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
(V,Y)
be a field Leibniz algebra. Suppose that there exists a linear map
D:V\rightarrow V
satisfying the
D
-bracket derivative property (
4
) and the skew-symmetry (
5
). Then
(V,Y,D)
is a vertex algebra without vacuum.

Recall the notions of vertex Leibniz algebras and vertex algebras without vacuum in
Definitions
2.4
and
2.5
, respectively. It is clear that we have the following embedding of categories.

(35)

\text{ vertex\ alg.\ without\ vacuum}\subset\text{ vertex\ Leibniz\ alg.}\subset\text{ field\ Leibniz\ alg.}

Inspired by the axioms (
30
)–(
32
) of dendriform algebras and the weak-associativity (
2
), we expect to decompose the vertex operator
Y(\cdot,z)
into a sum of two operators:
Y(\cdot,z)=Y_{\prec}(\cdot,z)+Y_{\succ}(\cdot,z),
whose properties are consistent with both the Rota-Baxter type axiom and the weak associativity axiom. Furthermore, we want the embedding of categories (
35
) to be reflected as well. This leads us to the following definition.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
V
be a vector space, and

\displaystyle Y_{\prec}(\cdot,z):V\rightarrow\mathrm{Hom}(V,V((z))),\ a\mapsto Y_{\prec}(a,z),

\displaystyle Y_{\succ}(\cdot,z):V\rightarrow\mathrm{Hom}(V,V((z))),\ a\mapsto Y_{\succ}(a,z)

be two linear operators associated with a formal variable
z
. For simplicity, we denote
Y_{\prec}(\cdot,z)
by
\cdot\prec_{z}\cdot
, and
Y_{\succ}(\cdot,z)
by
\cdot\succ\cdot
, respectively, and write

Y_{\prec}(a,z)b=a\prec_{z}b,\quad Y_{\succ}(a,z)b=a\succ_{z}b,\quad a,b\in V.

(i)

A triple
(V,\prec_{z},\succ_{z})
is called a
dendriform field algebra
if for
a,b,c\in V
, there exists some
N\in\mathbb{N}
depending on
a
and
c
, satisfying

(36)

\displaystyle(z_{0}+z_{2})^{N}(a\prec_{z_{0}}b)\prec_{z_{2}}c

\displaystyle=(z_{0}+z_{2})^{N}a\prec_{z_{0}+z_{2}}(b\succ_{z_{2}}c+b\prec_{z_{2}}c),

(37)

\displaystyle(z_{0}+z_{2})^{N}(a\succ_{z_{0}}b)\prec_{z_{2}}c

\displaystyle=(z_{0}+z_{2})^{N}a\succ_{z_{0}+z_{2}}(b\prec_{z_{2}}c),

(38)

\displaystyle(z_{0}+z_{2})^{N}(a\succ_{z_{0}}b+a\prec_{z_{0}}b)\succ_{z_{2}}c

\displaystyle=(z_{0}+z_{2})^{N}a\succ_{z_{0}+z_{2}}(b\succ_{z_{2}}c).

(ii)

A triple
(V,\prec_{z},\succ_{z})
is called a
dendriform vertex Leibniz algebra
if it is a dendriform field algebra and satisfies the following additional conditions: for
a,b,c\in V
, there exists some
N\in\mathbb{N}
depending on
a
and
b
, such that

(39)

\displaystyle(z_{1}-z_{2})^{N}a\succ_{z_{1}}(b\prec_{z_{2}}c)

\displaystyle=(z_{1}-z_{2})^{N}b\prec_{z_{2}}(a\succ_{z_{1}}c+a\prec_{z_{1}}c),

(40)

\displaystyle(z_{1}-z_{2})^{N}a\succ_{z_{1}}(b\succ_{z_{2}}c)

\displaystyle=(z_{1}-z_{2})^{N}b\succ_{z_{2}}(a\succ_{z_{1}}c).

(iii)

Let
D:V\rightarrow V
be a linear map. A quadruple
(V,\prec_{z},\succ_{z},D)
is called a
dendriform vertex algebra (without vacuum)
if
(V,\prec_{z},\succ_{z})
is a dendriform field algebra, and
D
,
\prec_{z}
, and
\succ_{z}
satisfy the following compatibility properties:

(41)

\displaystyle e^{zD}(a\prec_{-z}b)=b\succ_{z}a\quad\mathrm{and}\quad e^{zD}(a\succ_{-z}b)=b\prec_{z}a;

(42)

\displaystyle D(a\prec_{z}b)-a\prec_{z}(Db)=\frac{d}{dz}(a\prec_{z}b)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad D(a\succ_{z}b)-a\succ_{z}(Db)=\frac{d}{dz}(a\succ_{z}b).

Remark 4.4
.

By the definition above, any dendriform vertex Leibniz algebra is automatically a dendriform field algebra. Later, we will see that the equations (
36
)–(
38
) are the underlying axioms of the weak associativity (
2
), while the equations (
39
) and (
40
) are the underlying axioms of the weak commutativity (
1
).

We will also show that the equations (
36
)–(
38
)
are equivalent with equations (
39
) and (
40
) if
there exists
D:V\rightarrow V
satisfying (
41
) and
(
42
) (Proposition
4.8
). Thus
any dendriform vertex algebra is also a dendriform vertex Leibniz
algebra (Corollary
4.9
), and we have a similar embedding of
categories as (
35
):

(43)

\text{dendriform\ vertex\ alg.}\subset\text{dendriform\ vertex\ Leibniz\ alg.}\subset\text{dendriform\ field\ alg}.

We can regard the equality (
41
) as the analog of skew-symmetry (
5
) satisfied by the partial operators
\prec_{z}
and
\succ_{z}
. The equality (
42
) can be viewed as the
D
-bracket derivative property (
4
) for the partial operators. Similar to Lemma 2.7 in
[
36
]
, we have the following result.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
V
be a vector space, equipped with two operators
\prec_{z},\succ_{z}:V\times V\rightarrow V((z))
and a linear operator
D:V\rightarrow V
, satisfying the skew-symmetry (
41
). Then the
D
-bracket derivative property (
42
) is equivalent to the following
D
-derivative property.

(44)

(Da)\prec_{z}b=\frac{d}{dz}(a\prec_{z}b),\quad\quad(Da)\succ_{z}b=\frac{d}{dz}(a\succ_{z}b),\quad a,b\in V.

In particular, a dendriform vertex algebra
(V,\prec_{z},\succ_{z},D)
can be defined as a dendriform field algebra
(V,\prec_{z},\succ_{z})
that satisfies (
41
) and (
44
).

Proof.

Similar to the proof of Lemma 2.7 in
[
36
]
, for
a,b\in V
, we have

\displaystyle(Da)\prec_{z}b-\frac{d}{dz}(a\prec_{z}b)

\displaystyle=e^{zD}b\succ_{-z}Da-De^{zD}(b\succ_{z}a)-e^{zD}\frac{d}{dz}(b\succ_{-z}a)

\displaystyle=e^{zD}\left(b\succ_{-z}Da-D(b\succ_{-z}a)-\frac{d}{dz}(b\succ_{-z}a)\right),

\displaystyle(Da)\succ_{z}b-\frac{d}{dz}(a\succ_{z}b)

\displaystyle=e^{zD}b\prec_{-z}Da-De^{zD}(b\prec_{z}a)-e^{zD}\frac{d}{dz}(b\prec_{-z}a)

\displaystyle=e^{zD}\left(b\prec_{-z}Da-D(b\prec_{-z}a)-\frac{d}{dz}(b\prec_{-z}a)\right).

Thus, (
42
) is equivalent to (
44
).
∎

The axioms of the dendriform algebra are closely related to the properties of Rota-Baxter operators. As their vertex algebra analogs, the following theorem shows that an RBVA of weight
\lambda
gives rise to a dendriform field algebra, and an RBVA of weight
0
gives rise to a dendriform vertex algebra.

Let

Let

(

(

(V,\prec_{z},\succ_{z},D)
be a dendriform vertex algebra, and let
(V,Y,D)
be the associated vertex algebra without vacuum, where
Y
is given by (
47
):
Y(a,z)b=a\prec_{z}b+a\succ_{z}b
. Let
W=V
, and define

(56)

Y_{W}:V\rightarrow(\mathrm{End}W)[[z,z^{-1}]],\quad Y_{W}(a,z)b:=a\succ_{z}b,\quad a\in V,b\in W.

Then
(W,Y_{W})
is a module over
(V,Y,D)
.

Proof.

By Definition
4.3
, clearly
Y_{W}
satisfies the truncation
property. By the Jacobi identity (
53
) of

(V,\prec_{z},\succ_{z},D)
, (
56
) and
(
47
), we have

\displaystyle z^{-1}_{0}\delta\left(\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{z_{0}}\right)Y_{W}(a,z_{1})Y_{W}(b,z_{2})c-z^{-1}_{0}\delta\left(\frac{-z_{2}+z_{1}}{z_{0}}\right)Y_{W}(b,z_{2})Y_{W}(a,z_{1})c=z^{-1}_{2}\delta\left(\frac{z_{1}-z_{0}}{z_{2}}\right)Y_{W}(Y(a,z_{0})b,z_{2})c,

for
a,b\in V,c\in W=V
. Finally, by Lemma
4.5
, we have

Y_{W}(Da,z)b=(Da)\succ_{z}b=\frac{d}{dz}a\succ_{z}b,\quad a\in V,b\in W.

Thus,
(W,Y_{W})
is a module over the vertex algebra without vacuum
(V,Y,D)
, according to Definition
4.15
.
∎

Remark 4.17
.

Since we define
Y_{W}
by one of the partial operator
\succ_{z}
in (
56
), it is natural to consider the vertex operator
\mathcal{Y}
defined by the other partial operator
\mathcal{Y}(a,z)b=a\prec_{z}b
. By the skew-symmetry (
41
), we have

(57)

\mathcal{Y}(a,z)b=a\prec_{z}b=e^{zD}(b\succ_{-z}a)=e^{zD}Y_{W}(b,-z)a=Y_{WV}^{W}(a,z)b,

where
Y_{WV}^{W}
is defined by the skew-symmetry formula (see Section 5 in
[
20
]
).
i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}=Y_{WV}^{W}
. It is easy to see that the Jacobi identities (
52
) and (
54
) correspond to the following equation.

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle z^{-1}_{0}\delta\left(\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{z_{0}}\right)Y_{W}(a,z_{1})Y_{WV}^{W}(b,z_{2})c-z^{-1}_{0}\delta\left(\frac{-z_{2}+z_{1}}{z_{0}}\right)Y_{WV}^{W}(b,z_{2})Y(a,z_{1})c

\displaystyle=z^{-1}_{2}\delta\left(\frac{z_{1}-z_{0}}{z_{2}}\right)Y_{WV}^{W}(Y_{W}(a,z_{0})b,z_{2})c,\quad a,b,c\in V=W.

Moreover,
Y_{WV}^{W}(Da,z)b=(Da)\prec_{z}b=\frac{d}{dz}a\prec_{z}b
by Lemma
4.5
. Thus, if the vertex algebra without vacuum
(V,Y,D)
is an underlying structure of some VOA
(V,Y,\mathbf{1},\omega)
, with
D=L(-1)
, then
Y_{WV}^{W}(a,z)b=a\prec_{z}b
is an intertwining operator of type
{\binom{W}{W\;V}}
.

Corollary 4.18
.

Let
(V,Y,\mathbf{1},\omega)
be a VOA. Assume that the underlying vertex algebra without vacuum structure
(V,Y,D=L(-1))
is induced from a dendriform vertex algebra structure
(V,\prec_{z},\succ_{z},D)
by Theorem
4.7
. Let
(W,Y_{W})
be the weak
V
-module given by Proposition
4.16
. Then the identity map
T=\mathrm{Id}:W\rightarrow V
is a relative RBO.

Proof.

By (
47
), (
56
), (
57
), and the assumption that
T=\mathrm{Id}
, we have

\displaystyle Y(Tu,z)Tv

\displaystyle=u\prec_{z}v+u\succ_{z}v=Y_{W}(u,z)v+Y_{WV}^{W}(u,z)v

\displaystyle=T(Y_{W}(Tu,z)v)+T(Y_{WV}^{W}(u,z)Tv),\quad u,v\in W.

So
T=\mathrm{Id}
is a relative RBO as defined in (
15
)
∎

Conversely, we have the following relation between dendriform vertex algebras and vertex algebra without vacuum, which gives a characterization of the dendriform vertex algebras.

Proposition 4.19
.

Let
V
be a vector space, equipped with linear maps
\prec_{z},\succ_{z}:V\rightarrow\mathrm{Hom}(V,V((z)))
, and
D:V\rightarrow V
. Define
Y(a,z)b:=a\prec_{z}b+a\succ_{z}b
for all
a,b\in V
as in (
47
). Then
(V,\prec_{z},\succ_{z},D)
is a dendriform vertex algebra if and only if the following conditions are satisfied.

(i)

(V,Y,D)
is a vertex algebra without vacuum.

(ii)

\prec_{z}
,
\succ_{z}
, and
D
satisfy the equations (
41
) and (
42
).

(iii)

(V,\succ_{z},D)
defines a module structure of
(V,Y,D)
on
V
itself.

Proof.

If
(V,\prec_{z},\succ_{z},D)
forms a dendriform vertex algebra, then (i), (ii), and (iii) follows from Theorem
4.7
, Definition
4.3
, and Proposition
4.16
, respectively. Conversely, since
\succ_{z}:V\rightarrow\mathrm{End}(V)[[z,z^{-1}]]
defines a module structure, we have the Jacobi identity:

\displaystyle z^{-1}_{0}\delta\left(\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{z_{0}}\right)a\succ_{z_{1}}(b\succ_{z_{2}}c)-z^{-1}_{0}\delta\left(\frac{-z_{2}+z_{1}}{z_{0}}\right)b\succ_{z_{2}}(a\succ_{z_{1}}c)

\displaystyle=z^{-1}_{2}\delta\left(\frac{z_{1}-z_{0}}{z_{2}}\right)(Y(a,z_{0})b)\prec_{z_{2}}c

\displaystyle=z^{-1}_{2}\delta\left(\frac{z_{1}-z_{0}}{z_{2}}\right)(a\succ_{z_{0}}b+a\prec_{z_{0}}b)\prec_{z_{2}}c,\hskip 9.24994pta,b,c\in V.

Hence
(V,\prec_{z},\succ_{z},D)
is a quadruple satisfying
(
41
), (
42
), and (
53
). Then by
Corollary
4.14
,
(V,\prec_{z},\succ_{z},D)
is a
dendriform field vertex algebra.
∎

However, for dendriform vertex Leibniz algebras, since we do not have the skew-symmetry property, the left and right module actions cannot be combined into one action. The notion of left module over a vertex Leibniz algebra was introduced in
[
36
]
(see Definition 2.15 there). Inspired by the Jacobi identities in Theorem
4.11
and Proposition
4.16
, we introduce the notion of bi-modules over a vertex Leibniz algebra as follows.

Definition 4.20
.

Let
(V,Y)
be a vertex Leibniz algebra. A
bi-module over
(V,Y)
is a triple
(W,Y_{W},Y_{WV}^{W})
, where
W
is a vector space, and

Y_{W}:V\rightarrow\mathrm{Hom}(W,W((z))),\quad Y_{WV}^{W}:W\rightarrow\mathrm{Hom}(V,W((z)))

are linear operators, satisfying the following axioms:

(58)

\begin{split}&z^{-1}_{0}\delta\left(\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{z_{0}}\right)Y_{W}(a,z_{1})Y_{WV}^{W}(b,z_{2})c-z^{-1}_{0}\delta\left(\frac{-z_{2}+z_{1}}{z_{0}}\right)Y_{WV}^{W}(b,z_{2})Y(a,z_{1})c\\
&=z^{-1}_{2}\delta\left(\frac{z_{1}-z_{0}}{z_{2}}\right)Y_{WV}^{W}(Y_{W}(a,z_{0})b,z_{2})c,\hskip 9.24994pta,c\in V,b\in W,\end{split}

(59)

\begin{split}&z^{-1}_{0}\delta\left(\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{z_{0}}\right)Y_{W}(a,z_{1})Y_{W}(b,z_{2})c-z^{-1}_{0}\delta\left(\frac{-z_{2}+z_{1}}{z_{0}}\right)Y_{W}(b,z_{2})Y_{W}(a,z_{1})c\\
&=z^{-1}_{2}\delta\left(\frac{z_{1}-z_{0}}{z_{2}}\right)Y_{W}(Y(a,z_{0})b,z_{2})c,\hskip 9.24994pta,b\in V,c\in W,\end{split}

(60)

\begin{split}&z^{-1}_{0}\delta\left(\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{z_{0}}\right)Y_{WV}^{W}(a,z_{1})Y(b,z_{2})c-z^{-1}_{0}\delta\left(\frac{-z_{2}+z_{1}}{z_{0}}\right)Y_{W}(b,z_{2})Y_{WV}^{W}(a,z_{1})c\\
&=z^{-1}_{2}\delta\left(\frac{z_{1}-z_{0}}{z_{2}}\right)Y_{WV}^{W}(Y_{WV}^{W}(a,z_{0})b,z_{2})c,\hskip 9.24994ptb,c\in V,a\in W.\end{split}

In particular,
(W,Y_{W})
is a (left) module over the vertex Leibniz algebra
(V,Y)
as in
[
36
]
, in view of (
59
).

Proposition 4.21
.

Let
(V,\prec_{z},\succ_{z})
be a dendriform vertex Leibniz algebra, and let
(V,Y)
be the associated vertex Leibniz algebra, where
Y
is given by (
47
):
Y(a,z)b=a\prec_{z}b+a\succ_{z}b
. Let
W=V
, and define
Y_{W}
and
Y_{WV}^{W}
by

(61)

Y_{W}(a,z)b:=a\succ_{z}b,\quad Y_{WV}^{W}(b,z)a:=b\prec_{z}a,\quad\forall a\in V,b\in W.

Then
(W,Y_{W},Y_{WV}^{W})
is a bi-module over
(V,Y)
.

Proof.

We use the equivalent definition of dendriform vertex Leibniz algebra in Corollary
4.13
. By our definition (
61
), it is clear that (
58
), (
59
), and (
60
) are equivalent to (
52
), (
53
), and (
54
), respectively. Therefore,
(W,Y_{W},Y_{WV}^{W})
is a bi-module over
(V,Y)
by Definition
4.20
.
∎

Similar to Proposition
4.19
, we have the following characterization of the dendriform vertex Leibniz algebras.

Proposition 4.22
.

Let
V
be a vector space, equipped with linear maps
\prec_{z},\succ_{z}:V\rightarrow\mathrm{Hom}(V,V((z)))
. Let
Y(a,z)b=a\prec_{z}b+a\succ_{z}b
for all
a,b\in V
. Then
(V,\prec_{z},\succ_{z})
forms a dendriform vertex Leibniz algebra if and only if

(i)

(V,Y)
forms a vertex Leibniz algebra, and

(ii)

(V,\succ_{z},\prec_{z})
forms a bi-module of the vertex Leibniz algebra
(V,Y)
.

Proof.

If
(V,\prec_{z},\succ_{z})
forms a dendriform vertex Leibniz algebra, then (i) follows from Theorem
4.7
, (ii) follows from Proposition
4.21
. Conversely, by our assumption and Definition
4.20
, equations (
58
)–(
60
) translates to equations (
52
)–(
54
). Then by Corollary
4.13
,
(V,\prec_{z},\succ_{z})
is a dendriform vertex Leibniz algebra.
∎

Remark 4.23
.

For dendriform field algebras, we can follow the same routine and introduce a notion of bi-modules over a field Leibniz algebra, which is coherent with equations (
36
)–(
38
). Then the axioms of dendriform field algebras can also be characterized by the bi-module axioms.
