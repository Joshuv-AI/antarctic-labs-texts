A mean-field game of market-making against strategic traders This work benefits from the financial support of the Chaire Deep Finance and Statistics. The authors would like to thank Idris Kharroubi, Gilles Pagès and Huyên Pham for insightful discussions on the subject.
Bastien Baldacci, Philippe Bergault, Dylan Possamaï

Abstract
We design a market-making model à la Avellaneda and Stoikov 2008 in which the market-takers act strategically, in the sense that they design their trading strategy based on an exogenous trading signal. The market-maker chooses her quotes based on the average market-takers’ behaviour, modelled through a mean-field interaction. We derive, up to the resolution of a coupled HJB–Fokker–Planck system, the optimal controls of the market-maker and the representative market-taker. This approach is flexible enough to incorporate different behaviours for the market-takers and takes into account the impact of their strategies on the price process. Keywords: market-making, algorithmic trading, mean-field games

Introduction

On financial markets, a market-maker is usually defined as a liquidity provider. In a nutshell, it is a market participant who provides bid and ask (
i.e.
buy and sell) prices on one or several assets. The market-maker makes profit by earning the price difference between her buy and sell orders, called the bid–ask spread. She faces the following optimisation problem: being rarely executed at a very high spread, or being often executed at a very low spread. In the first case, the marked-to-market gain will be high, and conversely in the second case. On most of the order-driven markets, such as equities, the market-making activity is provided by a small number of high-frequency trading firms which are the counterpart of the vast majority of the transactions, see for example
Megarbane et al. 2017

[
42
]
. They quote almost continuously bid and ask prices at the best limits, and their strategies are mostly based on either their superior proprietary technology, or on arbitrage strategies on cross-listed securities based on microstructure signals, see for instance
Baldacci and Manziuk 2020

[
3
]
. On quote-driven markets, such as for many fixed income products and single stock options, the liquidity-takers, who have a fair price in mind, first request a price for a given transaction size of the asset to a market-maker, often called dealer. The dealer answers by providing a price for the corresponding size, which is then accepted or rejected by the client. It is common that, similarly to order-driven markets, a small number of dealers provide the vast majority of liquidity to a large set of clients. On some specific products, such as options on single stocks, there can even be a monopolistic situation where there is a single liquidity provider

Coming back to the present paper, our main goal is to address the problem of a market-maker providing the vast majority of the liquidity on an underlying asset. The market-maker faces a very high number of traders, or market-takers, with possibly different behaviours such as momentum or mean reverting strategies. Each market-taker acts strategically in the sense that they send request to the market-maker with a fair price in mind, which results from their own optimisation, depending on their inventory and their trading signal. The market-maker proposes quotes based on her inventory process, and the behaviour of her counterparts, which are aggregated through a mean-field game. The problem thus takes the form of a mean-field game with controlled jumps: the intensities of the order arrivals of all market participants are averaged through a single point process, and the market-maker increases her execution rate if her quote is close to the fair price resulting from the mean-field equilibrium of the minor players. Based on the mean-field equilibrium between the traders, the market-maker computes the average fair price of the mean-field. If the fair bid price of the minor players is higher than her ask quote, for instance, the intensity of arrival orders at the ask for the market-maker will decrease and conversely. We characterise completely the problem by a system of Fokker–Planck equations for the mean-field game associated to the minor players, and a (decoupled) HJB equation for the major player, which can be efficiently solved via classic numerical methods on grids. Up to the resolution of these PDEs, we derive the optimal strategies of both the representative market-taker, and the market-maker. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work on optimal market-making taking into account the strategic behaviour of market-takers. We believe that the use of mean-field games to represent the interaction between market-maker and market-takers can lead to substantial improvement of the usual optimal market-making models, without increasing drastically the numerical complexity. The numerical results with a set of reasonable market parameters exhibit some interesting features. The probability distribution of the market-takers’ inventory shifts with the exogenous trading signal and the market-maker’s strategy is adjusted with respect to this flow. Even with an empty inventory, the market-takers prefer to have a non-zero signal as it will allow them to make more profit over the trading horizon: the worst-case for a market-taker is a flat signal. Moreover, when market-takers have a very volatile signal, the distribution of inventories remains symmetric around
\displaystyle 0
but with two ‘bumps’ representing their incapacity to react rapidly to a change of signal. These results enable to enhance the seminal work of
Avellaneda and Stoikov 2008

[
2
]
in the sense that the behaviour of market-takers is not fixed as a function of the quotes of the market-maker only, but as a function of their own view on the price process as well as their current inventory. This is of particular importance as it leads to a more accurate estimation of the PnL of the strategy of the market-maker. Moreover, our model also provides the optimal strategy of an informed market-taker, which is absent in
Avellaneda and Stoikov 2008

[
2
]
.

Some motivation: one market-maker facing several market-takers

\displaystyle N

2.1
Framework

2.3
The problem of the market-takers

Each market-taker manages his inventory process
\displaystyle q^{i}
,
\displaystyle i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, which is defined as the difference between the number of filled bid and ask requests

\displaystyle\displaystyle q_{t}^{i}:=N_{t}^{i,b}-N_{t}^{i,a},\;\forall t\in[0,T].

(2.6)

We define the dynamics of the cash process of the
\displaystyle i
-th trader
\displaystyle X^{i}

\displaystyle\displaystyle X^{i}_{t}:=X^{i}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}P^{a}(S_{s},\delta_{s}^{i,a})\mathrm{d}N^{i,a}_{s}-\int_{0}^{t}P^{b}(S_{s},\delta_{s}^{i,b})\mathrm{d}N^{i,b}_{s},\;t\in[0,T].

The
\displaystyle i

\displaystyle b^{i}

\displaystyle t

As before, we can see that the problem boils down to

\displaystyle\displaystyle V^{i}(\delta^{0},\delta^{-i})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sup_{\delta^{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{N}^{i}(\delta^{0},\delta^{-i})}\mathbb{E}^{\delta^{0},\delta^{i}\otimes_{i}\delta^{-i}}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\bigg(\delta^{i,a}_{t}\Lambda^{m}(\delta^{i,a}_{t})+\delta^{i,b}_{t}\Lambda^{m}(\delta^{i,b}_{t})+b^{i}_{t}q^{i}_{t}

With

\displaystyle\delta^{-i}

\displaystyle b^{i}

2.4
Limitations of the finite game model and mean-field limit

In this section, we described an agent-based model where
\displaystyle N
market-takers trade against one market-maker. Solving this multi-agent problem boils down to the resolution of a system of Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equations, where the state variables are the inventory processes of the market-maker and the
\displaystyle N
market-takers. This system of
\displaystyle N+1
HJB equations of dimension
\displaystyle N+2
(that is, the time, the inventories of the
\displaystyle N
market-takers and the inventory of the market-maker) is obviously intractable in practice for a large number of market-takers.

In order to propose a tractable framework for the optimal market-making problem with strategic market-takers, we propose a mean-field game approach. The market-maker does not face
\displaystyle N
strategic market-takers but infinitely many of them in a mean-field interaction, which can be thought as the averaged behaviour of the market-takers. In the next section, we present rigorously the mean-field limit of the
\displaystyle N
-players model, and the corresponding optimisation problems of the market-maker and the mean-field agent.

We see in (
2.5
) and (
2.3
) that the empirical distribution of the controls of the market-takers appears in both problems. Though we do not pursue rigorous proofs in that direction, a typical reasoning using propagation of chaos (see for instance
Bayraktar and Chakraborty 2021

[
9
]
) leads to conjecture that as
\displaystyle N
goes to
\displaystyle+\infty
, the controls
\displaystyle(\delta^{i,b},\delta^{i,a})_{i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}}
chosen by the market-takers will only intervene through their distribution, which we denote by
\displaystyle\nu
,
i.e.

\displaystyle\nu=(\nu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
denotes the probability flow representing the distribution of those controls on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For
\displaystyle t\in[0,T]
, we denote by
\displaystyle\nu^{b}_{t}
and
\displaystyle\nu^{a}_{t}
the first and second marginal laws of
\displaystyle\nu_{t}
, respectively.

Propagation of chaos also tells us that at the limit, we should expect that the point processes associated to each market-taker will become independent, so that it is reasonable to consider that the intensities of
\displaystyle N^{0,b}
and
\displaystyle N^{0,a}
will respectively converge towards

A\exp\bigg(-\frac{k}{\sigma}\bigg(\delta^{0,b}_{t}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\nu^{a}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)\bigg)\bigg),\;\text{and}\;A\exp\bigg(-\frac{k}{\sigma}\bigg(\delta^{0,a}_{t}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\nu^{b}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)\bigg)\bigg),\;t\in[0,T].

The intensities of the processes
\displaystyle N^{i,b}
and
\displaystyle N^{i,a}
of a given market-taker do not change, as they do not depend on the controls of the other market-takers

\lambda^{i,b,\delta^{i}}_{t}=A^{m}\exp\bigg(-\frac{k^{m}}{\sigma}\delta^{i,b}_{t}\bigg),\;\lambda^{i,a,\delta^{i}}_{t}=A^{m}\exp\bigg(-\frac{k^{m}}{\sigma}\delta^{i,a}_{t}\bigg),\;t\in[0,T].

In the next section, we rigorously introduce the mean-field version of the problem.

\displaystyle\lambda^{m,b,\delta^{m}}_{t}=\mathds{1}_{\{q^{m}_{t-}<\tilde{q}^{m}\}}{\Lambda}(\delta^{m,b}_{t})

and

\displaystyle\lambda^{m,a,\delta^{m}}_{t}=\mathds{1}_{\{q^{m}_{t-}>-\tilde{q}^{m}\}}{\Lambda}(\delta^{m,a}_{t})
,
\displaystyle t\in[0,T]
.

4.2
The Markovian optimisation problems

With the same notations as before, the optimisation problem of the market-maker is now given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{V}(\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}}},\Pi)&=\sup_{\delta\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}(\Pi)}{\mathbb{E}}^{\delta,\delta^{m},{\cal V}(\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}}},\Pi)}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\bigg(\delta^{b}_{s}\mathds{1}_{\{q_{s-}<\tilde{q}\}}{\Lambda}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},a}(s,z,y,\Pi_{s})\Pi_{s}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}z),\delta^{b}_{s}\bigg)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\delta^{a}_{s}\mathds{1}_{\{q_{s-}>-\tilde{q}\}}{\Lambda}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},b}(s,z,y,\Pi_{s})\Pi_{s}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}z),\delta^{a}_{s}\bigg)-\phi\sigma^{2}(q_{s})^{2}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+q_{s}\kappa\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\cal L}\big(y,\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},b}(s,z,y,\Pi_{s}),\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},a}(s,z,y,\Pi_{s})\big)\Pi_{s}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}z)\bigg)\mathrm{d}s\Bigg],\end{split}

(4.1)

for given
\displaystyle(\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}}},\Pi)\in({\cal D}^{m})^{2}\times{\cal D}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Similarly, the optimisation problem of the representative market-taker is now

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{V}^{m}(\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}}},\Pi)&=\sup_{\delta^{m}\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}^{m}(\Pi)}{\mathbb{E}}^{\delta,\delta^{m},{\cal V}(\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}}},\Pi)}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\bigg(\delta^{m,b}_{s}\mathds{1}_{\{q^{m}_{s-}<\tilde{q}^{m}\}}\Lambda^{m}(\delta^{m,b}_{s})+\delta^{m,a}_{s}\mathds{1}_{\{q^{m}_{s-}>-\tilde{q}^{m}\}}\Lambda^{m}(\delta^{m,a}_{s})+b^{m}_{s}q^{m}_{s}\\
&\qquad\qquad-\gamma\sigma^{2}(q^{m}_{s})^{2}+q^{m}_{s}\kappa\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\cal L}\big(y,\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},b}(s,z,y,\Pi_{s}),\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},a}(s,z,y,\Pi_{s})\big)\Pi_{s}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}z)\bigg)\bigg)\mathrm{d}s\Bigg],\end{split}

(4.2)

for a given
\displaystyle\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}}}\in({\cal D}^{m})^{2}
and
\displaystyle\Pi\in{\cal D}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. We now define a solution to the mean-field game in the Markovian case.

Definition 4.1
.

A solution of the above game in the Markovian case is given by functions
\displaystyle\bar{\delta}^{\star}=(\bar{\delta}^{\star,b},\bar{\delta}^{\star,a})\in{\cal D}^{2}
,
\displaystyle\bar{\delta}^{m,\star}=(\bar{\delta}^{m,\star,b},\bar{\delta}^{m,\star,a})\in({\cal D}^{m})^{2}
,
\displaystyle\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},\star}=(\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},\star,b},\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},\star,a})\in({\cal D}^{m})^{2}
, and a probability flow
\displaystyle\Pi^{\star}\in{\cal D}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, such that

\displaystyle(i)

the control
\displaystyle\delta^{\star}_{t}:=\bar{\delta}^{\star}(t,q_{t-},\Pi^{\star}_{t}),\;\forall t\in[0,T]
, reaches the supremum in the definition of
\displaystyle\tilde{V}(\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},\star},\Pi^{\star});

\displaystyle(ii)

the control
\displaystyle\delta^{m,\star}_{t}:=\bar{\delta}^{m,\star}(t,b^{m}_{t-},q^{m}_{t-},\Pi^{\star}_{t}),\;\forall t\in[0,T]
, reaches the supremum in the definition of
\displaystyle\tilde{V}^{m}(\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},\star},\Pi^{\star});

\displaystyle(iii)

\displaystyle\bar{\delta}^{m,\star}=\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},\star};

\displaystyle(iv)

\displaystyle\Pi^{\star}_{t}
is the joint distribution of
\displaystyle q^{m}_{t}
and
\displaystyle b^{m}_{t}
under
\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}^{\delta^{\star},\delta^{m,\star},{\cal V}(\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},\star},\Pi^{\star})}
for Lebesgue–almost every
\displaystyle t\in[0,T]
.

.

. This is because the probability flow

appearing in

4.3
The master equation

We define the function
\displaystyle H:[-\delta_{\infty},\delta_{\infty}]\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
as
\displaystyle H(d,z,\tilde{z})=d+\tilde{z}-z,

and the function
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}:[0,T]\times\llbracket-\tilde{q},\tilde{q}\rrbracket\times[-\delta_{\infty},\delta_{\infty}]^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times({\cal D}^{m})^{2}\times{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(t,q,d_{1},d_{2},z,\tilde{z}_{1},\tilde{z}_{2},\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}}},\Pi_{t})

\displaystyle\displaystyle:=\mathds{1}_{\{q<\tilde{q}\}}{\Lambda}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},a}(t,z,y,\Pi_{t})\Pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}z),d_{1}\bigg)H\big(d_{1},z,\tilde{z}_{1}\big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\mathds{1}_{\{q>-\tilde{q}\}}{\Lambda}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},b}(t,z,y,\Pi_{t})\Pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}z),d_{2}\bigg)H\big(d_{2},z,\tilde{z}_{2}\big).

(4.3)

This function corresponds to the Hamiltonian of the market-maker’s problem: it accounts for the changes in the value function at each trade, as well as the marked-to-market gain associated with each trade. The first and second terms of the sum correspond respectively to the bid and ask side of the market-maker. We next introduce the function
\displaystyle{\cal K}:\llbracket-\tilde{b},\tilde{b}\rrbracket\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\cal K}(b^{m},z,\tilde{z}_{1},\tilde{z}_{2})=\mathds{1}_{\{b^{m}<\tilde{b}\}}\bar{\lambda}^{+}(\tilde{z}_{1}-z)+\mathds{1}_{\{b^{m}>-\tilde{b}\}}\bar{\lambda}^{-}(\tilde{z}_{2}-z),

corresponding to the infinitesimal generator of
\displaystyle b^{m}
. We define the function
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{m}:[0,T]\times\llbracket-\tilde{b},\tilde{b}\rrbracket\times\llbracket-\tilde{q}^{m},\tilde{q}^{m}\rrbracket\times[-\delta_{\infty},\delta_{\infty}]^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{m}(q^{m},d_{1},d_{2},z,\tilde{z}_{1},\tilde{z}_{2})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\{q^{m}<\tilde{q}^{m}\}}\Lambda^{m}(d_{1})H(d_{1},z,\tilde{z}_{1})+\mathbf{1}_{\{q^{m}>-\tilde{q}^{m}\}}\Lambda^{m}(d_{2})H(d_{2},z,\tilde{z}_{2}).

(4.4)

As for the market-maker, this function corresponds to the Hamiltonian of the representative market-taker’s problem.

Given the control function
\displaystyle\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}}}=(\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},b},\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},a})\in({\cal D}^{m})^{2}
of the market-takers, if we denote by
\displaystyle p:[0,T]\times\llbracket-\tilde{q}^{m},\tilde{q}^{m}\rrbracket\times\llbracket-\tilde{b},\tilde{b}\rrbracket\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
the function such that
\displaystyle p(t,\cdot,\cdot)
represents the probability mass function of the distribution
\displaystyle\Pi_{t}
, then
\displaystyle p
verifies, at least formally, the following Fokker–Planck equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}p(t,q^{m},b^{m})-\mathds{1}_{\{q^{m}>-\tilde{q}^{m}\}}\Lambda^{m}\big(\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},b}(t,b^{m},q^{m}-1,p(t,\cdot,\cdot)\big)p(t,q^{m}-1,b^{m})-\bar{\lambda}^{+}\mathds{1}_{\{b^{m}>-\tilde{b}\}}p(t,q^{m},b^{m}-1)\\
&\quad-\bar{\lambda}^{-}\mathds{1}_{\{b^{m}<\tilde{b}\}}p(t,q^{m},b^{m}+1)-\mathds{1}_{\{q^{m}<\tilde{q}^{m}\}}\Lambda^{m}\big(\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},a}(t,b^{m},q^{m}+1,p(t,\cdot,\cdot))\big)p(t,q^{m}+1,b^{m})\\
&\quad+\bigg(\mathds{1}_{\{q^{m}<\tilde{q}^{m}\}}\Lambda^{m}\big(\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},b}(t,b^{m},q^{m},p(t,\cdot,\cdot))\big)+\mathds{1}_{\{q^{m}>-\tilde{q}^{m}\}}\Lambda^{m}\big(\bar{\delta}^{{\text{\rm MF}},a}(t,b^{m},q^{m},p(t,\cdot,\cdot))\big)\\
&\quad+\bar{\lambda}^{-}\mathds{1}_{\{b^{m}>-\tilde{b}\}}+\bar{\lambda}^{+}\mathds{1}_{\{b^{m}<\tilde{b}\}}\bigg)p(t,q^{m},b^{m})=0,\;(t,q^{m},b^{m})\in(0,T]\times\llbracket-\tilde{q}^{m},\tilde{q}^{m}\rrbracket\times\llbracket-\tilde{b},\tilde{b}\rrbracket,\\
&p(0,q^{m},b^{m})=P_{0}(q^{m},b^{m}),\;(q^{m},b^{m})\in\llbracket-\tilde{q}^{m},\tilde{q}^{m}\rrbracket\times\llbracket-\tilde{b},\tilde{b}\rrbracket,\end{aligned}\right.

(4.5)

where
\displaystyle P_{0}
denotes the probability mass function of the initial inventories of the market-takers and their initial signals at time
\displaystyle 0
. Notice that the supremum in the Hamiltonian (
4.3
) is reached at
\displaystyle(d^{b},d^{a}):=\big(\bar{d}(z-\tilde{z}^{1}),\bar{d}(z-\tilde{z}^{2})\big),

and the supremum in the Hamiltonian (
4.4
) is reached at
\displaystyle(d^{m,b},d^{m,a}):=\big(\bar{d}^{m}(z-\tilde{z}^{1}),\bar{d}^{m}(z-\tilde{z}^{2})\big),

where
\displaystyle\bar{d}:p\in\mathbb{R}\longmapsto\sigma/k+p,
and
\displaystyle\bar{d}^{m}:p\in\mathbb{R}\longmapsto\sigma/k^{m}+p
. Finally, we arrive to the following master equation for our problem at the equilibrium

\left\{\begin{aligned} 0&=\partial_{t}W(t,q)-\phi\sigma^{2}q^{2}\\
&\quad+q\kappa\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\cal L}\Big(y,\bar{d}^{m}\big(W^{m}(t,z,y)-W^{m}(t,z,y+1)\big),\bar{d}^{m}\big(W^{m}(t,z,y)-W^{m}(t,z,y-1)\big)\Big)p(t,y,z)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}z\\
&\quad+\mathcal{H}\Big(t,q,\bar{d}\big(W(t,q)-W(t,q+1)\big),\bar{d}\big(W(t,q),W(t,q-1)\big),W(t,q),W(t,q+1),W(t,q-1),\bar{\delta}^{m},p(t,\cdot,\cdot)\Big),\\

&\quad+q^{m}\kappa\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\cal L}\Big(y,\bar{d}^{m}\big(W^{m}(t,z,y)-W^{m}(t,z,y+1)\big),\bar{d}^{m}\big(W^{m}(t,z,y)-W^{m}(t,z,y-1)\big)\Big)p(t,y,z)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}z\\
&\quad+\mathcal{H}^{m}\Big(q^{m},\bar{d}^{m}\big(W^{m}(t,q^{m},b^{m})-W^{m}(t,q^{m}+1,b^{m})\big),\bar{d}^{m}\big(W^{m}(t,q^{m},b^{m}),W^{m}(t,q^{m}-1,b^{m})\big),\\
&\qquad\qquad\;\;\;W^{m}(t,q^{m},b^{m}),W^{m}(t,q^{m}+1,b^{m}),W^{m}(t,q^{m}-1,b^{m})\Big),\\
0&=\partial_{t}p(t,q^{m},b^{m})-\mathds{1}_{\{q^{m}>-\tilde{q}^{m}\}}\Lambda^{m}\Big(\bar{d}^{m}\big(W^{m}(t,q^{m}-1,b^{m})-W^{m}(t,q^{m},b^{m})\big)\Big)p(t,q^{m}-1,b^{m})\\
&\quad-\mathds{1}_{\{q^{m}<\tilde{q}^{m}\}}\Lambda^{m}\Big(\bar{d}^{m}\big(W^{m}(t,q^{m}+1,b^{m})-W^{m}(t,q^{m},b^{m})\big)\Big)p(t,q^{m}+1,b^{m})\\
&\quad-\bar{\lambda}^{+}\mathds{1}_{\{b^{m}>-\tilde{b}\}}p(t,q^{m},b^{m}-1)+\bar{\lambda}^{-}\mathds{1}_{\{b^{m}<\tilde{b}\}}p(t,q^{m},b^{m}+1)\\
&\quad+\bigg(\mathds{1}_{\{q^{m}<\tilde{q}^{m}\}}\Lambda^{m}\Big(\bar{d}^{m}\big(W^{m}(t,q^{m},b^{m})-W^{m}(t,q^{m}+1,b^{m})\big)\Big)\\
&\quad+\mathds{1}_{\{q^{m}>-\tilde{q}^{m}\}}\Lambda^{m}\Big(\bar{d}^{m}\big(W^{m}(t,q^{m},b^{m})-W^{m}(t,q^{m}-1,b^{m})\big)\Big)+\bar{\lambda}^{-}\mathds{1}_{\{b^{m}>-\tilde{b}\}}+\bar{\lambda}^{+}\mathds{1}_{\{b^{m}<\tilde{b}\}}\bigg)p(t,q^{m},b^{m}),\end{aligned}\right.

(4.6)

where
\displaystyle\bar{\delta}^{m}=\big(\bar{\delta}^{m,b},\bar{\delta}^{m,a}\big)\in({\cal D}^{m})^{2}
is the function such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bar{\delta}^{m,b}(t,b^{m},q^{m},p(t,\cdot,\cdot))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\bar{d}^{m}\big(W^{m}(t,q^{m},b^{m})-W^{m}(t,q^{m}+1,b^{m})\big),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bar{\delta}^{m,a}(t,b^{m},q^{m},p(t,\cdot,\cdot))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\bar{d}^{m}\big(W^{m}(t,q^{m},b^{m})-W^{m}(t,q^{m}-1,b^{m})\big),

and where
\displaystyle p
is the flow of probability mass functions associated with
\displaystyle\Pi
, with initial and terminal conditions

\displaystyle\displaystyle p(0,q^{m},b^{m})=P_{0}(q^{m},b^{m}),\;W(T,q)=W^{m}(T,q^{m},b^{m})=0,\;\text{for $\displaystyle(b^{m},q^{m},q)\in\llbracket-\tilde{b},\tilde{b}\rrbracket\times\llbracket-\tilde{q}^{m},\tilde{q}^{m}\rrbracket\times\llbracket-\tilde{q},\tilde{q}\rrbracket$.}

(4.7)

The master equation (

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{\Pi}\in C\big([0,T],\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Q}^{m}\times\mathcal{B})\big)
be a flow of probability measures on
\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{m}\times\mathcal{B}
. We denote by
\displaystyle\tilde{p}
the associated probability mass functions. Assume that the coupled Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} 0&=\partial_{t}\tilde{W}(t,q)-\phi\sigma^{2}q^{2}\\
&\quad+q\kappa\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\cal L}\Big(y,\bar{d}^{m}\big(\tilde{W}^{m}(t,z,y)-\tilde{W}^{m}(t,z,y+1)\big),\bar{d}^{m}\big(\tilde{W}^{m}(t,z,y)-\tilde{W}^{m}(t,z,y-1)\big)\Big)\tilde{p}(t,y,z)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}z\\
&\quad+\mathcal{H}\Big(t,q,\bar{d}\big(\tilde{W}(t,q)-\tilde{W}(t,q+1)\big),\bar{d}\big(\tilde{W}(t,q),\tilde{W}(t,q-1)\big),\tilde{W}(t,q),\tilde{W}(t,q+1),\tilde{W}(t,q-1),\bar{\delta}^{m},\tilde{p}(t,\cdot,\cdot)\Big),\\
0&=\partial_{t}\tilde{W}^{m}(t,q^{m},b^{m})-\gamma\sigma^{2}(q^{m})^{2}+b^{m}q^{m}+{\cal K}\Big(b^{m},\tilde{W}^{m}(t,q^{m},b^{m}),\tilde{W}^{m}(t,q^{m},b^{m}+1),\tilde{W}^{m}(t,q^{m},b^{m}-1)\Big)\\
&\quad+q^{m}\kappa\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\cal L}\Big(y,\bar{d}^{m}\big(\tilde{W}^{m}(t,z,y)-\tilde{W}^{m}(t,z,y+1)\big),\bar{d}^{m}\big(\tilde{W}^{m}(t,z,y)-\tilde{W}^{m}(t,z,y-1)\big)\Big)\tilde{p}(t,y,z)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}z\\
&\quad+\mathcal{H}^{m}\Big(q^{m},\bar{d}^{m}\big(\tilde{W}^{m}(t,q^{m},b^{m})-\tilde{W}^{m}(t,q^{m}+1,b^{m})\big),\bar{d}^{m}\big(\tilde{W}^{m}(t,q^{m},b^{m}),\tilde{W}^{m}(t,q^{m}-1,b^{m})\big),\\
&\qquad\qquad\;\;\tilde{W}^{m}(t,q^{m},b^{m}),\tilde{W}^{m}(t,q^{m}+1,b^{m}),\tilde{W}^{m}(t,q^{m}-1,b^{m})\Big),\end{aligned}\right.

with terminal condition
\displaystyle\tilde{W}(T,.)=\tilde{W}^{m}(T,\cdot,\cdot)=0
has a classical solution
\displaystyle(\tilde{W},\tilde{W}^{m})
once continuously differentiable in time. We introduce the following feedback controls

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{\delta}^{\star,b}(t,q,\tilde{p}(t,\cdot,\cdot))=\frac{\sigma}{k}+\tilde{W}(t,q)-&\tilde{W}(t,q+1),\;\bar{\delta}^{\star,a}(t,q,\tilde{p}(t,\cdot,\cdot))=\frac{\sigma}{k}+\tilde{W}(t,q)-\tilde{W}(t,q-1),\\

\bar{\delta}^{m,\star,a}(t,b,q^{m},\tilde{p}(t,\cdot,\cdot)&)=\frac{\sigma}{k^{m}}+\tilde{W}^{m}(t,q^{m},b)-\tilde{W}^{m}(t,q^{m}-1,b).\end{split}

(4.8)

Then the functions
\displaystyle\tilde{W}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{W}^{m}
correspond respectively to the value functions of the market-maker and the market-takers associated with the flow of probability measures
\displaystyle\tilde{\Pi},
in the sense that

\tilde{W}(0,q_{0})=\tilde{V}(\bar{\delta}^{m},\tilde{\Pi}),\;\text{\rm and}\;\tilde{W}^{m}(0,q^{m}_{0},b^{m}_{0})=\tilde{V}^{m}(\bar{\delta}^{m},\tilde{\Pi}),

and the controls given in closed-loop by (
4.8

From these two results , we immediately get the following corollary, which provides us with a Markovian equilibrium.

Corollary 4.5
.

Consider the solution
\displaystyle\big(W,W^{m},p\big)
to the master equation (
4.6
) with terminal conditions (
4.7
) defined in
Theorem

4.3
. We introduce the associated feedback controls as in
Theorem

4.4

\begin{split}\bar{\delta}^{\star,b}(t,q,p(t,\cdot,\cdot))=\frac{\sigma}{k}+W(t,q)-&W(t,q+1),\;\bar{\delta}^{\star,a}(t,q,p(t,\cdot,\cdot))=\frac{\sigma}{k}+W(t,q)-\tilde{W}(t,q-1),\\
\bar{\delta}^{m,\star,b}(t,b,q^{m},p(t,\cdot,\cdot)&)=\frac{\sigma}{k^{m}}+W^{m}(t,q^{m},b)-W^{m}(t,q^{m}+1,b),\\
\bar{\delta}^{m,\star,a}(t,b,q^{m},p(t,\cdot,\cdot)&)=\frac{\sigma}{k^{m}}+W^{m}(t,q^{m},b)-W^{m}(t,q^{m}-1,b).\end{split}

(4.9)

These feedback controls and the flow
\displaystyle\Pi\in\mathcal{D}^{p}
constitute a Markovian equilibrium in the sense of
Definition

4.1
.

Remark 4.6
.

Corollary

4.5
gives us an equilibrium of our mean-field game. The presence of controlled point processes in the problem makes the uniqueness of the equilibrium hard to prove, as classical techniques do not apply here. This matter is left for a future, more general work. However, the absence of numerical instability in our numerous experiments leads us to believe that the equilibrium is indeed unique.

6
Conclusion

In this article, we tackled the problem of a market-maker on a single underlying asset facing strategical market-takers. The market-maker choses her quotes based on the behaviour of the mean-field of market-takers whose strategy is determined through an exogenous trading signal. We derive the system of HJB–Fokker–Planck equations driving this optimization problem and show existence of a solution through a fixed point argument. As the HJB equations of the market-maker and the mean-field of market-takers are decoupled, numerical resolution is simplified.

We illustrate the results of our model using a set of realistic market parameters and draw some conclusions. As expected, the probability distribution of the market-takers’ inventory shifts with the exogenous trading signal and the strategy of the market-makers are adjusted with respect to this flow of probability. Finally, a more volatile exogenous signal leads to a change in inventory distribution for the market-takers: as they cannot react fast enough to a change of signal, they are not able to get back to an empty inventory at the same speed when the signal goes to zero.

This framework can be enhanced by introducing more complex trading signals and deeper interactions between market-maker and market-takers, at the cost of an increasing numerical complexity. In particular, one could add a common noise into the signals dynamics, and make the intensity function
\displaystyle\Lambda^{m}
of the market-takers dependent on the behaviour of the market-maker, but the problem then becomes numerically intractable, and theoretically much more complex to study.
