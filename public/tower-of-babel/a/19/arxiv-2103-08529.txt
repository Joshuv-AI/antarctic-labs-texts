Learning in Markets: Greed Leads to Chaos but Following the Price is Right
Yun Kuen Cheung, Stefanos Leonardos, Georgios Piliouras

Abstract
We study learning dynamics in distributed production economies such as blockchain mining, peer-to-peer file sharing and crowdsourcing. These economies can be modelled as multi-product Cournot competitions or all-pay auctions (Tullock contests) when individual firms have market power, or as Fisher markets with quasi-linear utilities when every firm has negligible influence on market outcomes. In the former case, we provide a formal proof that Gradient Ascent (GA) can be Li-Yorke chaotic for a step size as small as Θ ⁡ ( 1 / n ) \Theta(1/n) , where n n is the number of firms. In stark contrast, for the Fisher market case, we derive a Proportional Response (PR) protocol that converges to market equilibrium. The positive results on the convergence of the PR dynamics are obtained in full generality, in the sense that they hold for Fisher markets with any quasi-linear utility functions. Conversely, the chaos results for the GA dynamics are established even in the simplest possible setting of two firms and one good, and they hold for a wide range of price functions with different demand elasticities. Our findings suggest that by considering multi-agent interactions from a market rather than a game-theoretic perspective, we can formally derive natural learning protocols which are stable and converge to effective outcomes rather than being chaotic.

Our contribution.

Motivated by the above, our aim is to study the behavior of learning dynamics in production economies from a theoretical perspective. Our research goals are 1) to establish formal mathematical arguments that explain the irregular behavior of

Informally, a dynamical system is Li-Yorke chaotic if there are uncountably many pairs of trajectories which get arbitrarily close together (but never intersect) and move apart indefinitely. When two trajectories are very close to each other, they become essentially indistinguishable due to the precision limitation inherent with the environment or computer. In other words, we cannot tell which of the two trajectories will be realized in the future — this is exactly what
unpredictable
means. A primary reason for the chaos to arise is that each firm uses its own market power to strategically influence the price. When all firms make such strategic manipulations simultaneously, they aggregately drive prices up and down without proper control.

While the previous technique does not lead to a formal proof of Li-Yorke chaos in the case of Best Response (BR) dynamics, we formalize the (in)-stability properties of the latter via eigenvalue analysis of a first-order linear approximation of the non-linear dynamical system. Here, instability refers to abrupt changes in the long term behavior of the dynamics in response to small perturbations of the systems’ parameters (e.g., firms costs).

Since robustness is an essential property in distributed production economies both from a normative and a descriptive perspective,
the above results provide a convincing argument against the use of
game-theoretically motivated
protocols. This brings us to our second goal which is to seek learning protocols that result in stable outcomes.

Our main result in this direction is to propose a
market-motivated
Proportional Response (PR) algorithm and show that it is stable and robust: from any initial condition, the PR update rule converges to the market equilibrium of an ensuing Fisher market that captures production economies, namely Fisher market with quasi-linear utility functions. The protocol is simple and can be run by each firm independently using only local and observable (market level) information, which makes it particularly suitable for these distributed settings. It can be interpreted as a naturally motivated adaptive algorithm from a firm’s perspective: in each round, each firm appropriates a certain amount of money, and invests it to the productions of different goods in proportion to the revenues received from selling them in the previous round.

One necessary assumption to establish this result is that as economies grow larger, firms have a negligible influence on aggregate outputs (prices or total exerted efforts). However, we formally argue that in the distributed production economy setting, market equilibria are approximate Nash equilibria. This finding is in line with the largeness concept in
[
25
]
, who showed that when markets grow
large
, they become asymptotically efficient even under agents’ strategic behaviors. This implies that the assumption of diminished influence on outcomes does not significantly affect the equilibrium outcome of the system.
However, it does have important implications from a technical perspective. In particular, by modeling production economies as Fisher markets, we can leverage their
Eisenberg-Gale convex-program formulation

[

to draw direct analogue between our PR algorithm and standard optimization methods like mirror descent. This allows us to apply tools from optimization theory and provides a principled approach to derive proofs of convergence.

Connection between TC and FM.

The crucial difference between TC and FM is that in TC, prices are determined endogenously as a function of
\mathbf{b}

\mathbf{b}

The above suggest that if there is an algorithm that converges to the market equilibrium spending (our

Gradient Ascent Dynamics and Li-Yorke Chaos.

To establish our chaos results of the GA dynamics in CC (hence, also in TC),
we consider a CC with one good and
n
firms. Since there is only one good, we omit the subscript
j=1
and use the shorthand
\alpha\equiv\alpha_{1}
to denote the marginal cost of producing the good (recall from

2

it is more convenient to use the quantities of the good produced, i.e., the variables
x_{i}=b_{i1}/\alpha

Without loss of generality, let
v_{1}=1

Then the utility of firm

u_{i}(\mathbf{x})=x_{i}/(\sum_{k}x_{k})-\alpha x_{i}

The

Assuming that the initial point is

Thus, a symmetric GA dynamic is essentially one-dimensional, and its trajectory can be represented by the sequence
\{x_{1}^{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

generated by the GA update rule:

\textstyle x_{1}^{t+1}\leftarrow x_{1}^{t}+\eta\cdot\left(\frac{n-1}{n^{2}x_{1}^{t}}-\alpha\right).

Definition 5

(Li-Yorke Chaos)
.

A discrete time dynamical system
\left(x^{t}\right)_{t\in\mathbb{N}}

for a continuous update rule

k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a periodic point
\hat{x}\in X
with period

S\subset X
that is
scrambled

x\neq x^{\prime}\in S
it holds that
\lim\inf_{t\to\infty}|f^{t}\left(x\right)-f^{t}\left(x^{\prime}\right)|=0<\lim\sup_{t\to\infty}|f^{t}\left(x\right)-f^{t}\left(x^{\prime}\right)|
.

Theorem 6

(Li-Yorke Chaos in
n
-Player CC/TC)
.

Consider a symmetric GA dynamic with
n
firms and marginal cost
\alpha>0
.
Then for any step-size
\eta\geq 3(n-1)/n^{2}\alpha^{2}
, the essentially-one-dimensional dynamical system (
4
) is Li-Yorke chaotic.

This theorem applies with isoelastic price function. In Section
5
, we consider a larger family of price functions and prove that Li-Yorke chaos also occurs in the corresponding symmetric GA dynamics. We also present theoretical and empirical evidences that instability arises when the GA rule is replaced by the Best Response rule.

Remark
.

In practice, firms may choose to use a large step-size in a myopic, greedy approach to profit maximization.
Given that chaos occurs with a vanishingly small step-size
\Theta\left(1/n\right)
as the number of firms increases (cf.
Theorem

6
),
our result is practically relevant for distributed production economies in which many small firms are involved.
Stability results should be possible for smaller step sizes, however, such step sizes are not particularly interesting from a practical perspective. Finally, the presence of a centralised planner who may enforce small step sizes is a rather unnatural assumption for the settings and applications that we consider.

Best Response Dynamics.

We conclude by revisiting the well-studied Best Response (BR) dynamics and formally establish that they can be unstable even in the simplest setting of two firms and one good. The general BR update rule is

Conclusions

The current work brings together multi-agent learning with optimization, market theory and chaos theory. Our findings suggest that by considering production economies from a market rather than a game-theoretic perspective, we can formally derive a natural learning protocol (PR) which is stable and converges to effective outcomes rather than being chaotic (GA). Due to its simple form and mild informational requirements, PR can be used to study real-world multi-agent settings from an AI perspective. Since distributed production economies capture many important applications (blockchain, peer-to-peer networks, crowdsourcing), our contributions are significant both for theoretical and practical purposes.

with equality holds whenever

for

.

(ii)

or equivalently if

, then

v_{ij}/p^{\#}_{j}\leq 1,\quad\forall j\in M,

with equality whenever
b_{ij}^{\#}>0
for

Proof.

We will show that the solution to (SH) yields a market equilibrium, by showing that the first-order conditions for the objective function of (SH)
are the same as the first order conditions for each firm’s individual utility
u_{i}\left(\mathbf{b}\right)
as given in the objective function of (NE).
From the market equilibrium perspective, the contribution
b_{ij}
of each firm

is negligible when compared to the aggregate effort
p_{j}=\sum_{i\in N}b_{ij}
contributed to contest
j\in M
by all firms
i\in N
and hence
p_{j}
can be thought as constant and be independent of
b_{ij}
. This implies that differentiating
u_{i}\left(\mathbf{b}\right)
with respect to
b_{ij}
yields

\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{ij}}u_{i}\left(\mathbf{b}\right)=\frac{v_{ij}}{p_{j}}-1.

By the same reasoning as in Proposition
10
, we obtain the first-order conditions in the statement of Proposition
12
.

It remains to show that the solution yields the same first order conditions and hence that its solution corresponds to a market equilibrium. To proceed, let
\phi\left(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w}\right)
with
\left(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w}\right)=\left(\left(b_{ij}\right)_{i\in N,j\in M},\left(w_{i}\right)_{i\in N}\right)
, denote the objective function of (SH), i.e.,

\displaystyle\phi\left(b,w\right):=\,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{ij}\ln{v_{ij}}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\ln{p_{j}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}.

Taking the partial derivative of

with respect to

\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{ij}}\phi\left(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w}\right)=\ln{v_{ij}}-\ln{p_{j}}-1=\ln{\frac{v_{ij}}{p_{j}}}-1,

where the fraction
v_{ij}/p_{j}
denotes the marginal utility and

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial b_{ij}^{2}}\phi\left(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w}\right)=-\frac{1}{p_{j}}<0.

The shows that
\phi\left(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w}\right)
is concave in
\mathbf{b}_{i}
for all
\mathbf{b}_{-i}
. Hence, the first-order conditions can be now formulated as follows.

Case 1:

w^{\#}_{i}=0
, or equivalently
\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{ij}^{\#}=K_{i}
. In this case, at
\mathbf{b}^{\#}

such that

(a)

if

(b)

if
b_{ij}=0

\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{ij}}\phi\left(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w}\right)\leq\tilde{C}_{i}

The reasoning is the same as in the proof of Proposition

Case 2:

w^{\#}_{i}>0

, in this case, we have that

(a)

if
b_{ij}>0

\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{ij}}\phi\left(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w}\right)=-1

(b)

if

\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{ij}}\phi\left(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w}\right)\leq-1

To see this, rewrite

and observe that

which is equivalent to the statement in (a). Statement (b) follows by the same reasoning with the additional assumption that now

is on the boundary of the feasible region. ∎

A.3
Proof of
Proposition

3
: Market Equilibria are Approximate Nash Equilibria

Using the Nash and market equilibrium characterizations from
Propositions

10
and
12
, we can now prove
Proposition

3
.

Step 1.

Note that
v_{ij}/p^{\#}_{j}-1
is the marginal utility return per unit of spending on good
j

(the
-1
occurs because we need to subtract the budget spent in quasi-linear utility).
At a market equilibrium
\mathbf{p}^{\#}
, for each firm
i
with quasi-linear utility, it only produces goods
j
that maximizes
v_{ij}/p^{\#}_{j}-1
,
and its attained utility is thus
\left(\max_{j}\left\{\frac{v_{ij}}{p^{\#}_{j}}\right\}-1\right)K_{i}=(\beta_{i}-1)K_{i}
.

Step 2.

Recall that
p^{\#}_{j}=\sum_{k}b^{\#}_{kj}
. Let
i
be any firm. Since
b_{ij}^{\#}\leq K_{i}
,
\sum_{k\neq i}b^{\#}_{kj}=p^{\#}_{j}-b_{ij}^{\#}\geq p^{\#}_{j}-K_{i}\geq(1-\delta)p^{\#}_{j}
.

Step 3.

Now, consider the situation when all firms except
i
have fixed their spendings at
\mathbf{b}^{\#}_{-i}
.
For any spending
\mathbf{b}_{i}
of firm
i
, the component about good
j
in
u_{i}^{\mathsf{CC}}
is
v_{ij}\cdot\frac{b_{ij}}{b_{ij}+\sum_{k\neq i}b^{\#}_{kj}}-b_{ij}
.
Thus,

\frac{\partial u_{i}^{\mathsf{CC}}}{\partial b_{ij}}=v_{ij}\cdot\frac{\sum_{k\neq i}b^{\#}_{kj}}{(b_{ij}+\sum_{k\neq i}b^{\#}_{kj})^{2}}-1\leq\frac{v_{ij}}{\sum_{k\neq i}b^{\#}_{kj}}-1.

Due to Step 2, we have
\frac{\partial u_{i}^{\mathsf{CC}}}{\partial b_{ij}}\leq\frac{v_{ij}}{(1-\delta)p^{\#}_{j}}-1
for any feasible
\mathbf{b}_{i}
that satisfies
\sum_{j}b_{ij}\leq K_{i}
.

Step 4.

Due to Step 3, when all firms except
i
have fixed their spendings at
\mathbf{b}^{\#}_{-i}
, no matter how firm
i
spends its budget, its maximum possible utility is either zero (when its spends nothing),
or
\max_{j}\left\{\frac{v_{ij}}{(1-\delta)p^{\#}_{j}}-1\right\}\cdot K_{i}=\left(\frac{\beta_{i}}{1-\delta}-1\right)K_{i}
.

Step 5.

If
\beta_{i}>1
, then by comparing the results of Step 1 and Step 4, firm
i
can improve its utility by a ratio of at most
\frac{\frac{\beta_{i}}{1-\delta}-1}{\beta-1}
.

If
\beta_{i}<1
and
\frac{\beta_{i}}{1-\delta}\leq 1
, then firm
i
spends nothing at both market equilibrium and in the situation depicted by Steps 3 and 4, so its utility cannot be improved.

However, if
\beta_{i}<1
but
\frac{\beta_{i}}{1-\delta}>1
, then firm
i
spends nothing at market equilibrium, thus attaining utility zero, but it will spend some money in the situation depicted by Steps 3 and 4, yielding a positive utility. This forces
\mathbf{b}^{\#}
not be a
\delta^{\prime}
-NE for any finite
\delta^{\prime}
.

Summarizing the three cases discussed in Step 5, Proposition
3
follows.

B.1
Proof of

Recall that the function

with domain

is

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{p}):=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{ij}\ln{v_{ij}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\ln{p_{j}}.

As we have discussed in

since they are functions of

via the equalities

To prove

0\leq F(\mathbf{b}^{\prime})-F(\mathbf{b})-\left\langle\nabla F(\mathbf{b}),\mathbf{b}^{\prime}-\mathbf{b}\right\rangle\leq\mathrm{KL}\left(\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\|\mathbf{b}\right).

To proceed, let for convenience

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(b_{ij}^{\prime}-b_{ij}\right)\ln{v_{ij}}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(p_{j}^{\prime}\ln p_{j}^{\prime}-p_{j}\ln p_{j}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(w^{\prime}_{i}-w_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(p_{j}^{\prime}\ln p_{j}^{\prime}-p_{j}\ln p_{j}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left((K_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{ij}^{\prime})-(K_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{ij})\right)

\sum_{i}b_{ij}^{\prime}=p^{\prime}_{j}

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(p_{j}^{\prime}\ln p_{j}^{\prime}-p_{j}\ln p_{j}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}(p_{j}-p^{\prime}_{j})-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\ln p_{j})(p^{\prime}_{j}-p_{j})

B.2
Deriving the
PR-QLIN
Algorithm from Mirror Descent

The Mirror Descent update rule for the objective function
F
in (
SH
) is (cf.
Algorithm

2
)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{b}^{t+1},\mathbf{w}^{t+1})=\,

\displaystyle\argmin_{(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w})\in C}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(w_{i}-w_{i}^{t})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(b_{ij}-b_{ij}^{t}\right)\left(1-\ln{\frac{v_{ij}}{p_{j}^{t}}}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{KL}(\mathbf{b}_{i}\|\mathbf{b}_{i}^{t})\right\}.

Since
\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{ij}+w_{i}
is a constant in the domain
C
, we may ignore any term that does not depend on
\mathbf{b}
and
\mathbf{w}
, and any positive constant factor in the objective function and simplify the above update rule to

\displaystyle(\mathbf{b}^{t+1},\mathbf{w}^{t+1})=\argmin_{(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w})\in C}\left\{-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{ij}\left(\ln{\frac{v_{ij}}{p_{j}^{t}}}-\ln\frac{b_{ij}}{b_{ij}^{t}}+1\right)\right\}=:\argmin_{(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w})\in C}\overline{F}(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w}).

We have that
\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{i}}\overline{F}(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w})=0
and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{ij}}\overline{F}(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w})=\ln{\frac{b_{ij}}{b_{ij}^{t}}}-\ln{\frac{v_{ij}}{p_{j}^{t}}}\,.

As before, for each fixed
i
, the values of

for all
j
are identical. In other words, there exists
c_{i}>0
such that

b_{ij}=c_{i}\cdot\frac{v_{ij}b_{ij}^{t}}{p_{j}^{t}}\,.

There are two cases which depend on

as follows

•

If
S^{t}_{i}\geq K_{i}
, then for each

we set
b_{ij}^{t+1}=K_{i}\cdot\frac{v_{ij}b_{ij}^{t}}{p_{j}^{t}}/S^{t}_{i}
, and

.
At this point, we have

, so the optimality condition is satisfied.

•

if

, then for each

b_{ij}^{t+1}=\frac{v_{ij}b_{ij}^{t}}{p_{j}^{t}}
,
and

.
At this point,
\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{ij}}\overline{F}(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w})=0=\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{i}}\overline{F}(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w})
, so the optimality condition is again satisfied.

C.1
Best Response Dynamics

While the previous technique does not allow us to establish Li-Yorke chaos in the case of Best Response (BR) Dynamics, we complement the existing empirical results of
[
41
]
and
[
50
]
with a formal proof of the stability properties of the BR dynamics via eigenvalue analysis of a first order linear approximation of the original non-linear system. Due to the first order approximation, the boundary cases are not precise, however, qualitatively, the result is robust. Recall, that for TC with isoelastic demand, the BR dynamics take the form

x_{i}^{t+1}\leftarrow\sqrt{x_{-i}^{t}/\alpha_{i}}-x_{-i}^{t},\,\,\text{for }i=1,2\text{ and }t\in\mathbb{N}.

(8)

Proposition 15
.

Under the Best Response (BR) update rule, (
8
), the evolution of the sequence
\left(x^{t},y^{t}\right)
of effort level pairs around the unique equilibrium depends on the value of the ratio
r:=\alpha/\beta
of the abilities of the two firms relative to the points
r_{0}:=3+2\sqrt{2}
and
r_{0}^{-1}

•

If
r_{0}^{-1}<r<r_{0}
, the effort levels are spiralling inwards towards the unique equilibrium (stable spiral focus).

•

If
r=r_{0}^{-1}
or
r=r_{0}
, the effort levels cycle around the unique equilibrium effort level (neutral center).

•

If
r<r_{0}^{-1}
or
r>r_{0}
, the effort levels are spiralling outwards away from the unique equilibrium effort level (unstable spiral focus).

An instantiation of the result of
Proposition

15
is shown in
Figure

7
.

Proof.

The unique resting point of the best response dynamics is given by the simultaneous solutions of the equations in equation (
8
) which yields

\left(x^{*},y^{*}\right)=\left(v\beta,v\alpha\right)/\left(\alpha+\beta\right)^{2}.

The stability of the dynamical system (
8
) around the equilibrium
\left(x^{*},y^{*}\right)
can be determined by the eigenvalues of the Jacobian of the system at the equilibrium point,

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\left.\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}x^{t+1}\left(x,y\right)&\frac{\partial}{\partial y}x^{t+1}\left(x,y\right)\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}y^{t+1}\left(x,y\right)&\frac{\partial}{\partial y}y^{t+1}\left(x,y\right)\end{pmatrix}\right|_{\left(x,y\right)=\left(x^{*},y^{*}\right)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\frac{1}{2r}\left(r-1\right)\\
\frac{1}{2}\left(r-1\right)&0\end{pmatrix},

which yields the characteristic equation

\det\left(J-\lambda I\right)=\lambda^{2}+\frac{1}{4r}\left(r-1\right)^{2},

and hence, the complex conjugate eigenvalues
\lambda_{1,2}
with absolute value
|\lambda_{1}|=|\lambda_{2}|=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}|r-1|
. This implies that the dynamics oscillate around the equilibrium with the exact behavior depending on whether the absolute value of
\lambda_{1,2}
is less than, equal to or larger than
1
. In particular,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}|r-1|=1

\displaystyle\iff r^{2}-6r+1=0\iff\left(r-r_{0}\right)\left(r-r_{0}^{-1}\right)=0,

with
r_{0}:=3+2\sqrt{2}
, which concludes the proof.
∎
