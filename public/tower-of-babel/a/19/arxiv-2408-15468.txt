A Young-type integration on self-similar sets in intervals
Takashi MARUYAMA, Tatsuki SETO

Abstract
. We introduce a generalization of the Young integration on self-similar sets defined in a closed interval and give a sufficient condition of its integrability. We also prove integration by substitution, integration by parts and term-by-term integration and give examples of the properties.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 26A42; Secondary 28A80.

Introduction

Integration is used in a wide range of fields.
One representative integration is the integration by the Lebesgue measure, which serves as a fundamental ingredient in expanding on the theory of the Riemann integration.
The Lebesgue integration has abundant applications for sets with positive measures, such as open sets or their closure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
On the other hand, the Lebesgue integration on a set with the Lebesgue measure
0

cannot have favorable applications since the Lebesgue integration of any functions on such a set is trivial.
Examples of a set of the measure
0
can be found in fractal sets.
Indeed, the middle-third Cantor set, a typical example of fractal sets,
has the measure
0
,
which motivates one to seek other types of integration for fractal sets.

For fractal sets, some works employ the Hausdorff measure to define integration on fractal sets. In the context of noncommutative geometry, there are many studies that relate the Dixmier trace with the Hausdorff probability measure on self-similar sets; see for example
[
2
,
3
,
5
,
8
]
.
There is also a work
[
4
]
which introduces single and double integrals using Hausdorff measure and conducts numerical comparison of the integrands with the ground-truth value.

Apart from the integrations mentioned above, the Young integration
[
11
]
is another generalization of the Riemann integration on
1
-dimensional smooth compact manifolds.
The Young integral is defined as (the limit of) the Riemann sum of the point-wise difference of functions on subdivisions of a given interval
I
.
As a corollary of the convergence of the limit,
the Young integration on
I
induces a bilinear functional from the product space
C^{\alpha}(I)\times C^{\beta}(I)
of the Hölder continuous classes for
0<\alpha,\beta\leq 1
with
\alpha+\beta>1
to the complex numbers

Y\colon C^{\alpha}(I)\times C^{\beta}(I)\to\mathbb{C}.

The Young integration on an interval can be also extended on a circle.

The definition of the Young integration would fit a class of fractal sets such as self-similar sets defined by iterated function systems (IFSs) built on a closed interval (resp. a circle) since IFSs generate the union of contracted intervals (resp. circles).
Indeed, there is a study
[
7
]
that
defines a cyclic cocycle based on the Young integration on
the union of infinitely many closed curves or circles
by
introducing a new class of self-similar sets; note that the class does not contain self-similar sets in intervals such as the middle-third Cantor set.
In a talk
[
10
]
, a variant of the Young integration was defined on a new class of algebra of functions,
that is the pullback of the continuous and bounded variation class on a unit circle by
the Cantor function restricted on the middle-third Cantor set.
There is also a study
[
9
]
which generalizes the result of
[
10
]
to “two dimension”.
While the literature
[
9
,
10
]
defined a Young-type integration on non-differentiable spaces, they assume to use structures of smooth manifolds by connecting the disconnected components of self-similar sets with the Cantor function.
In other words, the integration is not the one natively defined on self-similar sets.

In the present paper, we define and study a Young-type integration on a class of self-similar sets in an interval
I
.
The class includes intervals and the middle-third Cantor set as examples.
As opposed to the literature
[
9
,
10
]
, the definition of our integration solely relies on the information of IFS.

Let
(I,S,\{f_{s}\})
be IFS on
I
and

K
the self-similar set of
(I,S,\{f_{s}\})
.
We define a variant of the Riemann sum
\phi_{n}(f,g)
on
\cup_{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}}(f_{s_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{s_{n}})(I)
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and functions
f,g
on
K
.
Taking the limit
\phi(f,g)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\phi_{n}(f,g)
as
n\to\infty
, and we define a Young-type integration on
K
; see for the details in Section
2
.
We show the integrability condition of the Hölder continuous functions in Section
3
,
in which we also give various examples of integrable pairs and their corresponding integral, and non-integrable pairs in Section
3.2
and
3.3
.
In Section
3.2
, we need generalizations of the Cantor function; the definition and properties of the generalizations
are explained in Appendix
B
.
We further investigate properties of our integration:
integration by substitution (Section
4
),
integration by parts (Section
5
) and
term-by-term integration (Section
6
).
Here, we outline our integration by substitution
since the property reveals an explicit connection of our integration to the variant of the Young integration studied in
[
10
]
.
Let
\mathcal{L}^{(i)}=\left(I^{(i)},S,\{f^{(i)}_{s}\}\right)
be IFS on an interval
I^{(i)}

and
K^{(i)}
the associated self-similar set of
\mathcal{L}^{(i)}
for
i=1,2
.
If a permutation of
S
with a condition induces a map
T\colon K^{(1)}\to K^{(2)}
,
we can prove integration by substitution

\phi^{(1)}(f\circ T,g\circ T)=\pm\phi^{(2)}(f,g)
, where the sign
\pm
depends only on the permutation but does not coincide with the signature.
Note that the condition of permutations can not be removed; see Section
4.4
.
In particular, if
K^{(1)}
equals the middle-third Cantor set and
K^{(2)}
equals the unit interval
[0,1]
,
the idea using
the pullback of functions on an interval by the Cantor function introduced in
[
10
]
can be interpreted as an example of integration by substitution of our integration when the permutation equals identity; see Section
4.2
.
In Section
4.2
, we also explain the concrete idea introduced in the talk
[
10
]
for the sake of readability.

We note that in order to generalize our results to higher dimension,
we may use the Fredholm module introduced in
[
8
]
and need more general methods.
We will leave the study for future work.

We firstly recall the Fredholm module

2.
Definition of a Young-type integration

In this section, we define a Young-type integration
on
K

and prove a vanishing result for Hölder continuous functions with a sufficiently large Hölder exponent. Let
(C(\{a,b\}),\ell^{2}(\{a,b\}),F_{I})
be the Fredholm module on
I=\left[a,b\right]
(or
I=(a,b)
).
We define

M_{I}=-\dfrac{1}{b-a}[F_{I},x]=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}.

For the simplicity, we omit the subscript
I

if there is no confusion.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any continuous function
f,g\in C(\{a,b\})
, we have

\mathrm{Tr}(f[F,g]M)=(f(a)+f(b))(g(b)-g(a)).

Proof.

Since we have

[F,g]=(g(b)-g(a))\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{bmatrix},

we obtain the lemma.
∎

Lemma
2.1
indicates that
the trace of an operator

f[F,g]M
gives
rise to a discretized version of the Young integration on intervals.
See also Appendix
A
for the definition of the Young integration on an interval.

From now on, we denote

\mathrm{Tr}_{I}(f[F,g]M)=\mathrm{Tr}(f[F_{I},g]M_{I})

for an bounded interval
I
and two functions
f,g
on
\partial I
.
We define the integrability of a Young-type integration
on
K
.

Definition 2.2
.

For any functions
f
and
g
on
K
and

n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

\phi_{n}(f,g)=\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\mathrm{Tr}_{I_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}}(f[F,g]M).

We call a pair
(f,g)
is integrable on
K

if the sequence
\{\phi_{n}(f,g)\}
is convergent.
We denote the limit by
\phi(f,g)
and call it the integral of
(f,g)
on
K
.

We first show a class of functions such that the integral vanishes.
Denote by
C^{\alpha}(K)
the
\alpha
-Hölder continuous functions on
K
and set

|f|_{\alpha}=\sup_{x\neq y}\dfrac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\quad(f\in C^{\alpha}(K)).

Theorem 2.3
.

For any functions
f\in C(K)
and
g\in C^{\beta}(K)
with
\beta>\dim_{S}(K)
,
we have
\phi(f,g)=0
.

Proof.

If
\beta>\dim_{S}(K)
, we have

\displaystyle|\phi_{n}(f,g)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}|\mathrm{Tr}_{f_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}(I)}(f[F,g]M)|\leq\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}|f(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})+f(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})||g(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})-g(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})|

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f\||g|_{\beta}\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}|b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}-a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}|^{\beta}=2(b-a)^{\beta}\|f\||g|_{\beta}\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}r_{s_{i}}^{\beta}

\displaystyle=2(b-a)^{\beta}\|f\||g|_{\beta}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}r_{s}^{\beta}\right)^{n}\to 0\quad(n\to\infty).

∎

We note that
our integration on intervals
does not coincide
with either of the Young integration or the Lebesgue integration.

Example 2.4
.

We first consider the correspondence of our integration to the Young integration; see Appendix
A
for the Young integration.
Let
I=[a,b]
be the closed interval and we set

f_{0}(x)=\dfrac{1}{2}(x-a)+a\ \text{and}\ f_{1}(x)=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(x-b\right)+b\quad(x\in I).

Then a tuple
(I,\{0,1\},\{f_{0},f_{1}\})

defines an IFS and the self-similar set of the IFS equals
I
.
Take two functions
f,g
on
I
such that the Stieltjes integral
Y(f,g)

exists in Riemann sense.
By the definition of our integral,
the pair
(f,g)
is integrable
and we have

\phi(f,g)=2\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dg(x).

On the other hand, let
f
be the Dirichlet function on
I
.
Then a pair
(f,x)
is integrable and its integral satisfies
\phi(f,x)=2
,
but the Stieljes integral
Y(f,x)
does not exist.
Therefore, our integral is different from the both of the Young integration and Lebesgue integration.

3.
Convergence of integration

In this section, we prove a sufficient condition for the existence of the limit of
\phi_{n}
in Theorem
3.2
.
We also calculate examples of our integration.

3.1.
Sufficient condition for the integrability

In order to prove a sufficient condition for the integrability,
we firstly prove an easy lemma.
Set
\Delta_{I}f=f(b)-f(a)
for any function
f
on
\partial I=\{a,b\}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
I=[a,b]
,
x\in(a,b)
,
A=[a,x]
and
B=[x,b]
.

(1)

For any function
g
on
\{a,b,x\}
, we have

[F_{I},g]-[F_{A},g]=[F_{B},g]

in
M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

(2)

For any function
f,g
on
\{a,b,x\}
, we have

\operatorname{Tr}_{A}(f[F,g]M)+\operatorname{Tr}_{B}(f[F,g]M)=\operatorname{Tr}_{I}(f[F,g]M)+\Delta_{A}f\Delta_{B}g-\Delta_{B}f\Delta_{A}g.

Proof.

(1)

The following easy calculation
proves this part.

\displaystyle[F_{I},g]-[F_{A},g]

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}&g(b)-g(a)\\
-(g(b)-g(a))&\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}&g(x)-g(a)\\
-(g(x)-g(a))&\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}&g(b)-g(x)\\
-(g(b)-g(x))&\end{bmatrix}=[F_{B},g].

(2)

Denote by
P_{i}
the projection on to
the
i
-th component of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
By (1), we have

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\operatorname{Tr}_{A}(f[F,g]M)+\operatorname{Tr}_{B}(f[F,g]M)-\operatorname{Tr}_{I}(f[F,g]M)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}(f(a)P_{1}[F_{A},g]M)+\operatorname{Tr}(f(x)P_{2}[F_{A},g]M)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\operatorname{Tr}(f(x)P_{1}[F_{B},g]M)+\operatorname{Tr}(f(b)P_{2}[F_{B},g]M)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\operatorname{Tr}(f(a)P_{1}[F_{I},g]M)-\operatorname{Tr}(f(b)P_{2}[F_{I},g]M)f

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{Tr}(f(a)P_{1}[F_{B},g]M)-\operatorname{Tr}(f(b)P_{2}[F_{A},g]M)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\operatorname{Tr}(f(x)P_{2}[F_{A},g]M)+\operatorname{Tr}(f(x)P_{1}[F_{B},g]M)

\displaystyle=(f(x)-f(a))\operatorname{Tr}(P_{1}[F_{B},g]M)-(f(b)-f(x))\operatorname{Tr}(P_{2}[F_{A},g]M)

\displaystyle=(f(x)-f(a))(f(b)-f(x))-(f(b)-f(x))(f(x)-f(a))

\displaystyle=\Delta_{A}f\Delta_{B}g-\Delta_{B}f\Delta_{A}g.

This proves our lemma.

∎

By recursively applying Lemma
3.1
(2),
we can generalize Lemma
3.1
(2)
for any number of pieces of divisions of
I
.
For example, let
A=[a,x]
,
B=[x,y]
and
C=[y,b]
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\operatorname{Tr}_{A}(f[F,g]M)+\operatorname{Tr}_{B}(f[F,g]M)+\operatorname{Tr}_{C}(f[F,g]M)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{A\cup B}(f[F,g]M)+\operatorname{Tr}_{C}(f[F,g]M)+\Delta_{A}g\Delta_{b}g-\Delta_{B}f\Delta_{A}g

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{A\cup B\cup C}(f[F,g]M)+\Delta_{A}f\Delta_{B}g+\Delta_{A\cup B}f\Delta_{C}g-\Delta_{B}f\Delta_{A}g-\Delta_{C}f\Delta_{A\cup B}g.

For the proof of a sufficient condition for the integrability, we define another sequence on
\cup_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\cup_{l=0}^{N-2}J_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$},l}
:

\displaystyle\psi_{n+1}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\sum_{l=0}^{N-2}\ \mathrm{Tr}_{J_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$},l}}(f[F,g]M).

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
f\in C^{\alpha}(K)
and
g\in C^{\beta}(K)
with

\alpha+\beta>\dim_{S}(K)
.
The pair
(f,g)
is integrable on
K

if and only if
the series
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi_{n}(f,g)
converges.

Proof.

In a manner similar to Section
1
, we denote
(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$},k)=(s_{1},\dots,s_{n},k)\in S^{\times(n+1)}
for
\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\in S^{\times n}
and
k\in S
.
For the simplicity, we will use notations
I_{k}=I_{(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$},k)}
and
J_{l}=J_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$},l}
as long as it is clear what they mean. Then,
by recursively applying Lemma
3.1
(2), we get

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\operatorname{Tr}_{I_{(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$},k)}}(f,[F,g]M)+\sum_{l=0}^{N-2}\operatorname{Tr}_{J_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$},l}}(f,[F,g]M)-\operatorname{Tr}_{I_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}}(f,[F,g]M)

\displaystyle=\left(\Delta_{I_{0}}f\Delta_{J_{0}}g+\Delta_{I_{0}\cup J_{0}}f\Delta_{I_{1}}g+\cdots+\Delta_{I_{0}\cup\dots\cup J_{N-2}}f\Delta_{I_{N-1}}g\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\left(\Delta_{J_{0}}f\Delta_{I_{0}}g+\Delta_{I_{0}}f\Delta_{I_{0}\cup J_{0}}g+\cdots+\Delta_{I_{N-1}}f\Delta_{I_{0}\cup\cdots\cup J_{N-2}}g\right).

With this expression, we have

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\phi_{n+1}(f,g)-\phi_{n}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\operatorname{Tr}_{I_{(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$},k)}}(f,[F,g]M)-\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\operatorname{Tr}_{I_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}}(f,[F,g]M)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\left(\Delta_{I_{0}}f\Delta_{J_{0}}g+\Delta_{I_{0}\cup J_{0}}f\Delta_{I_{1}}g+\cdots+\Delta_{I_{0}\cup\dots\cup J_{N-2}}f\Delta_{I_{N-1}}g\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\left(\Delta_{J_{0}}f\Delta_{I_{0}}g+\Delta_{I_{0}}f\Delta_{I_{0}\cup J_{0}}g+\cdots+\Delta_{I_{N-1}}f\Delta_{I_{0}\cup\cdots\cup J_{N-2}}g\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\psi_{n+1}(f,g).

Then we have

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\phi_{n+m}(f,g)-\phi_{n}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\left(\phi_{n+r+1}(f,g)-\phi_{n+r}(f,g)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times(n+r)}}\left(\Delta_{I_{0}}f\Delta_{J_{0}}g+\Delta_{I_{0}\cup J_{0}}f\Delta_{I_{1}}g+\cdots+\Delta_{I_{0}\cup\dots\cup J_{N-2}}f\Delta_{I_{N-1}}g\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times(n+r)}}\left(\Delta_{J_{0}}f\Delta_{I_{0}}g+\Delta_{I_{0}}f\Delta_{I_{0}\cup J_{0}}g+\cdots+\Delta_{I_{N-1}}f\Delta_{I_{0}\cup\cdots\cup J_{N-2}}g\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\psi_{n+r}(f,g).

By the Hölder continuity of
f,g
, we have

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\left|\Delta_{I_{0}}f\Delta_{J_{0}}g+\Delta_{I_{0}\cup J_{0}}f\Delta_{I_{1}}g+\cdots+\Delta_{I_{0}\cup\dots\cup J_{N-2}}f\Delta_{I_{N-1}}g\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\Delta_{I_{0}}f\Delta_{J_{0}}g\right|+\left|\Delta_{I_{0}\cup J_{0}}f\Delta_{I_{1}}g\right|+\cdots+\left|\Delta_{I_{0}\cup\dots\cup J_{N-2}}f\Delta_{I_{N-1}}g\right|

\displaystyle\leq|f|_{\alpha}|g|_{\beta}(2N+1)(b-a)\prod_{i=1}^{N}r_{s_{i}}^{\alpha+\beta}

and

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\left|\Delta_{J_{0}}f\Delta_{I_{0}}g+\Delta_{I_{0}}f\Delta_{I_{0}\cup J_{0}}g+\cdots+\Delta_{I_{N-1}}f\Delta_{I_{0}\cup\cdots\cup J_{N-2}}g\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\Delta_{I_{0}}f\Delta_{J_{0}}g\right|+\left|\Delta_{I_{0}}f\Delta_{I_{0}\cup J_{0}}g\right|+\cdots+\left|\Delta_{I_{N-1}}f\Delta_{I_{0}\cup\cdots\cup J_{N-2}}g\right|

\displaystyle\leq|f|_{\alpha}|g|_{\beta}(2N+1)(b-a)\prod_{i=1}^{N}r_{s_{i}}^{\alpha+\beta}.

Therefore, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha+\beta>\dim_{S}(K)
, we have

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\left|\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times(n+r)}}\left(\Delta_{I_{0}}f\Delta_{J_{0}}g+\Delta_{I_{0}\cup J_{0}}f\Delta_{I_{1}}g+\cdots+\Delta_{I_{0}\cup\dots\cup J_{N-2}}f\Delta_{I_{N-1}}g\right)\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\left.\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times(n+r)}}\left(\Delta_{J_{0}}f\Delta_{I_{0}}g+\Delta_{I_{0}}f\Delta_{I_{0}\cup J_{0}}g+\cdots+\Delta_{I_{N-1}}f\Delta_{I_{0}\cup\cdots\cup J_{N-2}}g\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2|f|_{\alpha}|g|_{\beta}(2N+1)(b-a)\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times(n+r)}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}r_{s_{i}}^{\alpha+\beta}

\displaystyle=2|f|_{\alpha}|g|_{\beta}(2N+1)(b-a)\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}r_{s}^{\alpha+\beta}\right)^{n+r}

\displaystyle=2|f|_{\alpha}|g|_{\beta}(2N+1)(b-a)\left(\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}r_{s}^{\alpha+\beta}\right)^{n}\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}r_{s}^{\alpha+\beta}\right)^{r}

\displaystyle\to 0\quad(n\to\infty).

Therefore,
the sequence
\{\phi_{n}(f,g)\}
is Cauchy
if and only if

\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\psi_{n+r}(f,g)\to 0
(
n\to\infty
).
Thus we get the conclusion.

∎

(2)

For any

Example 3.5

We have

\displaystyle\phi(x^{m},c)=\begin{cases}\,2&for $m=0$,\\
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\left(-\frac{1}{3}\right)^{r}\binom{m}{r}\phi(x^{r},c)&for $m\colon$odd,\\

Proof.

We now prove our claims for

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we take the union of boundary points of
I_{n,k}
for all

(1)

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(x^{m},c)

\displaystyle(3^{n}-a)^{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{m}(-1)^{r}\binom{m}{r}(3^{n})^{m-r}a^{r}=\sum_{r=0}^{m}(-1)^{r}\binom{m}{r}3^{nm-nr}a^{r},

\displaystyle\frac{S_{n}^{(m)}}{2^{n}\cdot 3^{nm}}

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{n-1}^{(m)}}{2^{n}\cdot 3^{nm}}+\sum_{r=0}^{m}(-1)^{r}\binom{m}{r}3^{nm-nr}\frac{S_{n-1}^{(r)}}{2^{n}\cdot 3^{nm}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\cdot 3^{m}}\frac{S_{n-1}^{(m)}}{2^{n-1}\cdot 3^{m(n-1)}}+\sum_{r=0}^{m}(-1)^{r}\binom{m}{r}\frac{1}{2\cdot 3^{r}}\frac{S_{n-1}^{(r)}}{2^{n-1}\cdot 3^{r(n-1)}}.

1

\displaystyle\phi(x^{m},c)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\cdot 3^{m}}\phi(x^{m},c)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r=0}^{m}\left(-\frac{1}{3}\right)^{r}\binom{m}{r}\phi(x^{r},c)

4.
Integration by substitution

4.1.
Statement of integration by substitution

We study integration by substitution for our integration
by using a modified version of isomorphism
of self-similar structure of IFS;
see also
[
6
, Definition 1.3.2]
.
Recall that our self-similar structures satisfying
the images
f_{s}(I)
are ordered from left to right with respect to the indices
s
, that is,

a=a_{0}<b_{0}\leq a_{1}<b_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{N-1}<b_{N-1}=b.

Set

S^{(1)}=S^{(2)}=\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}
.
Let
\mathcal{L}^{(i)}=\left(I^{(i)},S^{(i)},\{f^{(i)}_{s}\}_{s\in S^{(i)}}\right)
be IFS on an interval
I^{(i)}=\left[a^{(i)},b^{(i)}\right]

and
K^{(i)}
the associated self-similar set of
\mathcal{L}^{(i)}
for
i=1,2
.
There exists the unique
continuous surjection
\pi^{(i)}\colon\Sigma(S^{(i)})\to K^{(i)}
,
where
\Sigma(S^{(i)})=(S^{(i)})^{\mathbb{N}}

is the shift space with symbols
S^{(i)}
;
see also
[
6
, Definition 1.3.3]
.
For a permutation
\rho\colon S^{(1)}\to S^{(2)}
,
we set
\iota_{\rho}(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots)=\rho(s_{1})\rho(s_{2})\cdots\in\Sigma(S^{(2)})

for
s_{1}s_{2}\cdots\in\Sigma(S^{(1)})
.
We assume that

T_{\rho}:=\pi^{(2)}\circ\iota_{\rho}\circ(\pi^{(1)})^{-1}

is a well-defined
map
from
K^{(1)}
to
K^{(2)}
.

Theorem 4.1

(Integration by substitution)
.

Let
\phi^{(i)}
be our integration on
K^{(i)}
.
For any
functions
f,g
on
K^{(2)}
such that
the pair
(f,g)
is integrable on
K^{(2)}
,
we have the following.

(1)

Assume that
\rho
preserves
0
and
N-1
, respectively.
Then a pair
(f\circ T_{\rho},g\circ T_{\rho})
is integrable on
K^{(1)}

and we have
\phi^{(1)}(f\circ T_{\rho},g\circ T_{\rho})=\phi^{(2)}(f,g)
.

(2)

Assume that
\rho
flips
0
and
N-1
.
Then a pair
(f\circ T_{\rho},g\circ T_{\rho})
is integrable on
K^{(1)}

and we have
\phi^{(1)}(f\circ T_{\rho},g\circ T_{\rho})=-\phi^{(2)}(f,g)
.

Proof.

Take
\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}=(s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{n})\in(S^{(i)})^{\times n}
.
By the definition of
\pi^{(i)}
, we have

\displaystyle\pi^{(i)}(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}00\dots)

\displaystyle=f^{(i)}_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}(a^{(i)})=a^{(i)}_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}

and

\displaystyle\pi^{(i)}(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}(N-1)(N-1)\dots)

\displaystyle=f^{(i)}_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}(b^{(i)})=b^{(i)}_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}.

The arguments are derived as follows:

(1)

If
\rho
satisfies
\rho(0)=0
and
\rho(N-1)=N-1
,
we have

\displaystyle T_{\rho}(a^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})

\displaystyle=(\pi^{(2)}\circ\iota_{\rho}\circ(\pi^{(1)})^{-1})(a^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})

\displaystyle=(\pi^{(2)}\circ\iota_{\rho})(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}00\dots)

\displaystyle=\pi^{(2)}(\rho(s_{1})\rho(s_{2})\cdots\rho(s_{n})00\cdots)

\displaystyle=a^{(2)}_{\rho(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$})}

and

\displaystyle T_{\rho}(b^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})

\displaystyle=(\pi^{(2)}\circ\iota_{\rho}\circ(\pi^{(1)})^{-1})(b^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})

\displaystyle=(\pi^{(2)}\circ\iota_{\rho})(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}(N-1)(N-1)\cdots)

\displaystyle=\pi^{(2)}(\rho(s_{1})\rho(s_{2})\cdots\rho(s_{n})(N-1)(N-1)\cdots)

\displaystyle=b^{(2)}_{\rho(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$})},

where we denote
\rho(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$})=(\rho(s_{1}),\cdots,\rho(s_{n}))
.
Thus we have

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\ \phi^{(1)}_{n}(f\circ T_{\rho},g\circ T_{\rho})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\left((f\circ T_{\rho})(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}^{(1)})+f\circ T_{\rho}(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}^{(1)})\right)\left((g\circ T_{\rho})(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}^{(1)})-(g\circ T_{\rho})(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}^{(1)})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\left(f(a_{\rho(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$})}^{(2)})+f(b_{\rho(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$})}^{(2)})\right)\left(g(b_{\rho(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$})}^{(2)})-g(a_{\rho(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$})}^{(2)})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\left(f(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}^{(2)})+f(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}^{(2)})\right)\left(g(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}^{(2)})-g(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}^{(2)})\right)

\displaystyle=\phi^{(2)}_{n}(f,g)

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
This proves (1).

(2)

If
\rho
satisfies
\rho(0)=N-1
and
\rho(N-1)=0
,
we have

\displaystyle T_{\rho}(a^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})

\displaystyle=(\pi^{(2)}\circ\iota_{\rho}\circ(\pi^{(1)})^{-1})(a^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})

\displaystyle=(\pi^{(2)}\circ\iota_{\rho})(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}00\cdots)

\displaystyle=\pi^{(2)}(\rho(s_{1})\rho(s_{2})\cdots\rho(s_{n})(N-1)(N-1)\cdots)

\displaystyle=b^{(2)}_{\rho(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$})}

and

\displaystyle T_{\rho}(b^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})

\displaystyle=(\pi^{(2)}\circ\iota_{\rho}\circ(\pi^{(1)})^{-1})(b^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})

\displaystyle=(\pi^{(2)}\circ\iota_{\rho})(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}(N-1)(N-1)\cdots)

\displaystyle=\pi^{(2)}(\rho(s_{1})\rho(s_{2})\cdots\rho(s_{n})00\cdots)

\displaystyle=a^{(2)}_{\rho(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$})}.

Thus we have

\phi^{(1)}_{n}(f\circ T_{\rho},g\circ T_{\rho})=-\phi^{(2)}_{n}(f,g)

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
This proves (2).

∎

4.2.
Moriyoshi and Natsume’s example

We here recall a Young-type integration introduced by Moriyoshi and Natsume
[
10
]
with our notation,
which is the first example of a Young-type integration on
CS
,
and we clarify the integration is given as an example of Theorem
4.1
.
Let
c\colon CS\to\mathbb{R}
be the restriction on
CS

of the Cantor function.
In

[
10
]
, they essentially states that for any functions

f,g\in C^{BV}
, where
C^{BV}
is the continuous functions of bounded variation on
I=[0,1]
,
the pair
(f\circ c,g\circ c)
is integrable and the following holds:

(2)

\phi(f\circ c,g\circ c)=2\int_{0}^{1}f(x)dg(x),

where the right hand side is the Young integration on
I
.
They also stated that
\phi
satisfies the cyclic condition
when
CS
is replaced with its quotient space
CS/\{0,1\}
.
The induced cyclic
1
-cochain equals
0
on
c^{\ast}C^{Lip}(I/\{0,1\})

and defines a non-trivial cyclic
1
-cocycle on
c^{\ast}C^{BV}(I/\{0,1\})
.

We next show that equation (
2
) can be considered as an example of Theorem
4.1
.
Let
\mathcal{L}^{(1)}=\left(I,\{0,1\},\{f^{(1)}_{0},f^{(1)}_{1}\}\right)

be the IFS of
CS
described at the end of Section
1
and

\mathcal{L}^{(2)}=\left(I,\{0,1\},\{f^{(2)}_{0},f^{(2)}_{1}\}\right)

the IFS of
I
described in Example
2.4
.
Because of the equations

\displaystyle f^{(1)}_{0}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{2a_{i}}{3^{i}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{2a_{i}}{3^{i+1}}\ \text{ and }\ f^{(1)}_{1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{2a_{i}}{3^{i}}\right)=\dfrac{2}{3}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{2a_{i}}{3^{i+1}}

for
a_{i}=0
or
1
,
we have

f^{(1)}_{s}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{2a_{i}}{3^{i}}\right)=\dfrac{2s}{3}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{2a_{i}}{3^{i+1}}\quad(s=0,1).

Then, we get

\pi^{(1)}(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2s_{i}}{3^{i}}\quad(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots\in\Sigma(\{0,1\})).

Similarly, we have

\pi^{(2)}(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{s_{i}}{2^{i}}\quad(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots\in\Sigma(\{0,1\})).

Recall that the set
CS
is identified with the set

\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2s_{i}}{3^{i}}\,;\,s_{i}=0,1\right\}.

Thus, we have

T_{\mathrm{id}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2s_{i}}{3^{i}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{s_{i}}{2^{i}},

which means nothing but
T_{\mathrm{id}}=c
.
Therefore, we have

\phi^{(1)}(f\circ c,g\circ c)=\phi^{(1)}(f\circ T_{\mathrm{id}},g\circ T_{\mathrm{id}})=\phi^{(2)}(f,g)

when the pair
(f,g)
is integrable on
I
by Theorem
4.1
(1).
For example, when
f
and

are continuous and in Wiener classes of order
p
and
q
, respectively,
with
1/p+1/q>1
,
the Stieltjes integral
Y(f,g)
exists in the Riemann sense; see Theorem
A.2
.
Then, the pair
(f,g)
is integrable and we get

\phi^{(1)}(f\circ c,g\circ c)=\phi^{(1)}(f\circ T_{\mathrm{id}},g\circ T_{\mathrm{id}})=\phi^{(2)}(f,g)=2\int_{0}^{1}f(x)dg(x).

Note that the equation (
2
) is the case for
p=q=1
.

Remark 4.2

By the arguments of this section,

4.3.
Examples on an Interval

In this section, we study other examples of Theorem
4.1
, in which IFSs have different similarity ratio.
Let

\mathcal{L}^{(1)}=\left(I,\{0,1\},\{f^{(1)}_{0},f^{(1)}_{1}\}\right)

be the IFS of
K^{(1)}=I
described in Example
2.4

and

\mathcal{L}^{(2)}=\left(I,\{0,1\},\{f^{(2)}_{0},f^{(2)}_{1}\}\right)

the IFS of
K^{(2)}=I
such that

f^{(2)}_{0}(x)=\dfrac{1}{3}x\quad\text{and}\quad f^{(2)}_{1}(x)=\dfrac{2}{3}x+\dfrac{1}{3}.

When we denote
d_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{i}s_{k}-1
for

s_{1}s_{2}\dots\in\Sigma(\{0,1\})
, we have

\displaystyle f^{(2)}_{0}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{2^{d_{i}}}{3^{i}}s_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{2^{d_{i}}}{3^{i+1}}s_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad f^{(2)}_{1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{2^{d_{i}}}{3^{i}}s_{i}\right)=\dfrac{1}{3}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{2^{d_{i}+1}}{3^{i+1}}s_{i}.

Thus we have

f^{(2)}_{s}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{2^{d_{i}}}{3^{i}}s_{i}\right)=\dfrac{s}{3}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{2^{s+d_{i}}}{3^{i+1}}s_{i}.

Therefore, we have

\pi^{(2)}(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2^{d_{i}}}{3^{i}}s_{i}.

Next, we prove that
T_{\rho}
(
\rho=\mathrm{id}
or transposition
(0\,1)
) induces a well-defined map from
K^{(1)}
to
K^{(2)}
.
For the purpose, it is only to show that we have

\pi^{(2)}(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}1000\cdots)=\pi^{(2)}(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}0111\cdots)\quad(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}).

It is clear by the following calculation:

\displaystyle\pi^{(2)}(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}1000\cdots)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{2^{d_{i}}}{3^{i}}s_{i}+\dfrac{2^{d_{n}+1}}{3^{n+1}}

\displaystyle\pi^{(2)}(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}0111\cdots)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{2^{d_{i}}}{3^{i}}s_{i}+\sum_{i=n+2}^{\infty}\dfrac{2^{d_{n}+i-n-1}}{3^{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{2^{d_{i}}}{3^{i}}s_{i}+\dfrac{2^{d_{n}-1}}{3^{n}}\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}\dfrac{2^{i}}{3^{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{2^{d_{i}}}{3^{i}}s_{i}+\dfrac{2^{d_{n}+1}}{3^{n+1}}.

Then we have a well-defined map

T_{\mathrm{id}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{s_{i}}{2^{i}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2^{d_{i}}}{3^{i}}s_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad T_{(0\,1)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{s_{i}}{2^{i}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2^{i-d_{i}-2}}{3^{i}}(1-s_{i}).

Next, we show that
two pairs
(1\circ T_{\mathrm{id}},x\circ T_{\mathrm{id}})

and
(1\circ T_{(0\,1)},x\circ T_{(0\,1)})

are integrable on
K^{(1)}
.
By definition, we have

\displaystyle(x\circ T_{\mathrm{id}})(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})-(x\circ T_{\mathrm{id}})(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2^{d_{n}+i-n}}{3^{i}}=\dfrac{2^{d_{n}}}{3^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2^{i}}{3^{i}}=\dfrac{2^{d_{n}+1}}{3^{n}}

and

\displaystyle(x\circ T_{(0\,1)})(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})-(x\circ T_{(0\,1)})(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2^{i-d_{n}-2}}{3^{i}}-\dfrac{2^{n-(d_{n}+1)}}{3^{n}}.

Since the number of
s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}000\cdots
such that
d_{i}+1=r

equals
\binom{n}{r}
,
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\left((x\circ T_{\mathrm{id}})(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})-(x\circ T_{\mathrm{id}})(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}\dfrac{2^{r}}{3^{n}}=1

\displaystyle\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\left((x\circ T_{(0\,1)})(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})-(x\circ T_{(0\,1)})(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}\dfrac{2^{n-r}}{3^{n}}=-1.

Therefore, we have

\phi^{(1)}_{n}(1\circ T_{\mathrm{id}},x\circ T_{\mathrm{id}})=2

and

\phi^{(1)}_{n}(1\circ T_{(0\,1)},x\circ T_{(0\,1)})=-2

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Taking
n\to\infty
, we know that two pairs
(1\circ T_{\mathrm{id}},x\circ T_{\mathrm{id}})

and
(1\circ T_{(0\,1)},x\circ T_{(0\,1)})

are integrable on
K^{(1)}

and its limit equals
2
and
-2
, respectively.
This implies the followings, which are examples of Theorem
4.1
:

\displaystyle\phi^{(1)}(1\circ T_{\mathrm{id}},x\circ T_{\mathrm{id}})

\displaystyle=2=2\int_{0}^{1}dx=\phi^{(2)}(1,x),

\displaystyle\phi^{(1)}(1\circ T_{(0\,1)},x\circ T_{(0\,1)})

\displaystyle=-2=-2\int_{0}^{1}dx=-\phi^{(2)}(1,x).

4.4.
A permutation for which integration by substitution does not hold

In order to study a permutation for which integration by substitution does not hold,
we deal with a 5-adic Cantor set
CS_{2}
; see Appendix
B
.
Let

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{(1)}=\mathcal{L}^{(2)}=\left(I,S=\{0,1,2\},\{f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}\}\right)

CS_{2}=K^{(1)}=K^{(2)}

Similar to the calculation in
Section

,
4.2

we have

\displaystyle\pi(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}000\cdots)

\displaystyle=f_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}(0)=a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}

\displaystyle\pi(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}111\cdots)

\displaystyle=f_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}(1/2)=\dfrac{a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}+b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}}{2}

\displaystyle\pi(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}222\cdots)

\displaystyle=f_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}(1)=b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}

for
\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}=(s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{n})\in S^{\times n}

We also have

\pi(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2s_{i}}{5^{i}}\quad(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots\in\Sigma(S)).

Since the map
\pi:\Sigma(S)\to K
is bijective,
the composition
T_{\rho}=\pi\circ\iota_{\rho}\circ\pi^{-1}

induces a well-defined map for any permutation
\rho\colon S\to S
.

We study the case for the transposition
\rho=(1\,2)
.
In this case, we have

\displaystyle T_{\rho}(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})

\displaystyle=\pi(\rho(s_{1})\rho(s_{2})\cdots\rho(s_{n})000\cdots)=a_{\rho(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$})}

and

\displaystyle T_{\rho}(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})

\displaystyle=\pi(\rho(s_{1})\rho(s_{2})\cdots\rho(s_{n})111\cdots)=a_{\rho(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$})}+\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2}{5^{i}}.

Let us define
g=c_{2,1/3}

.
Then we have

\displaystyle(g\circ T_{\rho})(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})-(g\circ T_{\rho})(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{3^{i}}=\dfrac{1}{2\cdot 3^{n}}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\phi^{(1)}_{n}(1\circ T_{\rho},g\circ T_{\rho})

\displaystyle=2\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\left((g\circ T_{\rho})(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})-(g\circ T_{\rho})(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\dfrac{1}{3^{n}}=1.

This implies that a pair
(1\circ T_{\rho},g\circ T_{\rho})

is integrable on
K^{(1)}
and its integral satisfies

\phi^{(1)}(1\circ T_{\rho},g\circ T_{\rho})=1
.
By Theorem
3.4
(1), we have

\phi^{(1)}_{n}(1\circ T_{\rho},g\circ T_{\rho})=\dfrac{1}{2}\phi^{(2)}_{n}(1,g)\quad(=1\neq 0).

On the other hand,
we next define

h\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2s_{i}}{5^{i}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-1)^{s_{i}}s_{i}}{3^{i}}

on

\displaystyle h(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})-h(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{(-1)^{s_{i}}s_{i}}{3^{i}}+2\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{3^{i}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{(-1)^{s_{i}}s_{i}}{3^{i}}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{3^{i}}=\dfrac{1}{3^{n}}

\displaystyle(h\circ T_{\rho})(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})-(h\circ T_{\rho})(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{(-1)^{\rho(s_{i})}\rho(s_{i})}{3^{i}}-\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{3^{i}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{(-1)^{\rho(s_{i})}\rho(s_{i})}{3^{i}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{3^{i}}=-\dfrac{1}{2\cdot 3^{n}},

we have

\displaystyle\phi^{(2)}_{n}(1,h)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\left(h(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})-h(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})\right)=2

and

\displaystyle\phi^{(1)}_{n}(1\circ T_{\rho},h\circ T_{\rho})

\displaystyle=2\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\left((h\circ T_{\rho})(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})-(h\circ T_{\rho})(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})\right)=-1.

This indicates
that the pair
(1,h)
(resp.
(1\circ T_{\rho},h\circ T_{\rho})

is integrable on
K^{(2)}
(resp.
K^{(1)}
)
and its integral satisfies

\phi^{(2)}(1,h)=2

(resp.
\phi^{(1)}(1\circ T_{\rho},h\circ T_{\rho})=-1
).
Therefore, we have

\phi^{(1)}(1\circ T_{\rho},h\circ T_{\rho})=-\dfrac{1}{2}\phi^{(2)}(1,h)\quad(=-1\neq 0).

The above examples show that
there is not a constant

dependent only on

that

valid.

5.
Integration by parts

Our integration also inherits integration by parts.
This property also has
examples for integrable pairs
(f,g)
(see Example
5.2
).

Theorem 5.1

(Integration by Parts)
.

For any functions
f
and
g
on
K
, we have

\phi_{n}(1,fg)=\phi_{n}(f,g)+\phi_{n}(g,f)
.
If two of
(1,fg)
,
(f,g)
and
(g,f)
are integrable,
the rest is also integrable and
we have
\phi(1,fg)=\phi(f,g)+\phi(g,f)
.

Proof.

\phi_{n}(1,fg)
can be written as follows:

(3)

\displaystyle\phi_{n}\left(1,fg\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\left[F,fg\right]M\right)=\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\left(f\left[F,g\right]+\left[F,f\right]g\right)M\right).

Note that, for any interval
I=[a,b]
,
we have the following relation:

\displaystyle gM_{I}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}g(a)&0\\
0&g(b)\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}g(b)&0\\
0&g(a)\end{bmatrix}=M_{I}\begin{bmatrix}g(b)&0\\
0&g(a)\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle(\ref{middle_fg})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\operatorname{Tr}\left(f\left[F,g\right]M\right)+\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\left[F,f\right]M\begin{bmatrix}g(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})&0\\
0&g(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\phi_{n}\left(f,g\right)+\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\begin{bmatrix}g(b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})&0\\
0&g(a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}})\end{bmatrix}\left[F,f\right]M\right)

\displaystyle=\phi_{n}\left(f,g\right)+\phi_{n}\left(g,f\right).

∎

By Theorem
5.1
,
we obtain as follows.

Example 5.2
.

Let
l,m\geq 0
be integers.
Then we have

\phi(x^{l},x^{m}c)=\phi(x^{l+m},c).

Proof.

By Theorem
5.1
(2), we have

\phi_{n}(1,x^{m}c)=\phi_{n}(x^{m},c)+\phi_{n}(c,x^{m}).

By Example
3.5
,
\phi_{n}(x^{m},c)
converges to
\phi(x^{m},c)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. On the other hand,
\phi_{n}(c,x^{m})\to 0
by Theorem
2.3
because
x^{m}
is a Lipschitz function. Hence,
\phi_{n}(1,x^{m}c)
converges to
\phi(x^{m},c)
.
Moreover, we obtain

\phi_{n}(1,x^{l+m}c)=\phi_{n}(x^{l},x^{m}c)+\phi_{n}(x^{m}c,x^{l}).

Since
\phi_{n}(x^{m}c,x^{l})\to 0
(
n\to\infty
), we obtain

\phi(x^{l},x^{m}c)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\phi_{n}(x^{l},x^{m}c)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\phi_{n}(1,x^{l+m}c)=\phi(x^{l+m},c).

∎

6.
Term-by-term Integration

6.1.
Term-by-integration for self-similar set

We prove
a theorem of term-by-term integration.

Theorem 6.1

(Term-by-term Integration)
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
and
0<\beta<1

such that
\alpha+\beta>\dim_{S}(K)
.
Suppose that uniformly bounded sequenses
\{f_{m}\}\subset C^{\alpha}(K)
and
\{g_{m}\}\subset C^{\beta}(K)
satisfy the following properties:

(1)

f_{m}\to f
and
g_{m}\to g
(
m\to\infty
) uniformly on
K
.

(2)

(f,g)
is integrable.

(3)

A series

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\psi_{n}(f_{m},g_{m})|

converges uniformly in
m
.

Then we have

\lim_{m\to\infty}\phi(f_{m},g_{m})=\phi(f,g)
.

Proof.

By the multilinearity of
\phi
,
it suffices to show that

\phi(f_{m},g_{m})
converges to
0

when one of
f_{m}
,
g_{m}
converges to
0
.

Let
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
.
As is the proof of Theorem
3.2
,
we have

\displaystyle\phantom{=}|\phi(f_{m},g_{m})-\phi_{n}(f_{m},g_{m})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r=n}^{\infty}|\phi_{r+1}(f_{m},g_{m})-\phi_{r}(f_{m},g_{m})|

\displaystyle\leq 2|f|_{\alpha}|g|_{\beta}(2N+1)(b-a)\sum_{r=n}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}r_{s}^{\alpha+\beta}\right)^{r}+\sum_{r=n}^{\infty}|\psi_{r+1}(f_{m},g_{m})|

\displaystyle=2|f|_{\alpha}|g|_{\beta}(2N+1)\left(\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}r_{s}^{\alpha+\beta}\right)^{n}\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}r_{s}^{\alpha+\beta}\right)^{r}+\sum_{r=n}^{\infty}|\psi_{r+1}(f_{m},g_{m})|

Thus, for any
\epsilon>0
,
there exists
n_{0}>0
such that we have

|\phi(f_{m},g_{m})-\phi_{n}(f_{m},g_{m})|<\epsilon

for any
n\geq n_{0}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then, the above calculations imply

\displaystyle|\phi(f_{m},g_{m})|

\displaystyle\leq|\phi(f_{m},g_{m})-\phi_{n}(f_{m},g_{m})|+|\phi_{n}(f_{m},g_{m})|

\displaystyle<\epsilon+4N^{n}\|f_{m}\|\|g_{m}\|

for any
n\geq n_{0}
.
Therefore, we have

\limsup_{m\to\infty}|\phi(f_{m},g_{m})|\leq\epsilon.

This completes the proof.
∎

6.2.
Necessity of (
3
) in Theorem

We give examples in which term-by-term integration does not hold when a sequence of functions does not meet (

) in Theorem

We first set

for any

; see Definition

By Theorem

,
g_{m}\to c_{1,1/2}
as

uniformly on
CS_{1}
.

Let
f_{m}=1
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, the pair
(f,g)
of the respective limit of
\{f_{m}\}
and
\{g_{m}\}
is integrable, see Section

. However, the series
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\psi_{n}(f_{m},g_{m})|

converges but not uniformly in

, which concludes that

\{f_{m}\}
and
\{g_{m}\}
do not meet Theorem

(3).
In fact, since we have

\displaystyle\psi_{n+1}(f_{m},g_{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\mathrm{Tr}_{J_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$},0}}([F,g_{m}]M)=2\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}(g_{m}(a_{(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$},1)})-g_{m}(b_{(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$},0)}))

\displaystyle=2\left(1-\dfrac{m}{2m+1}\right)^{2}\sum_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}}\left(\dfrac{m}{2m+1}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=2\left(1-\dfrac{m}{2m+1}\right)^{2}\left(\dfrac{2m}{2m+1}\right)^{n},

the series
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\psi_{n}(f_{m},g_{m})|

converges for any
m\in\mathbb{N}

but not uniformly in
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
On the other hand, by Theorem
3.4
,
the limit

\lim_{m\to\infty}\phi(f_{m},g_{m})=0

does not coincide with

\phi(f,g)=2
.
Therefore,
the assumption (3) in Theorem
6.1

is essential in term-by-term integration.

7.
A comment on non-continuous case

The Young integration introduced in
[
11
]
is defined on the space of Wiener class
W_{p}
.
The class
W_{p}
is a set of functions whose
p
-variation is bounded, and in general can include non-continuous functions.
Our integral can be calculated
for some non-continuous functions as follows:
Set

\displaystyle h(x)=\begin{cases}$0$&for $0\leq x<\frac{1}{3}$,\\
$1$&for $\frac{1}{3}\leq x\leq 1$\end{cases}

on
CS
.
The function
h
is obviously non-continuous at
x=\frac{1}{3}
,
and for each
\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\in S^{\times n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{I_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}}(h[F,h]M)=\begin{cases}$1$&for $s_{1}=0$ \text{and} $s_{2}=\dots=s_{n}=1$,\\
$0$&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Therefore, because of
\phi_{n}(h,h)=1
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
we can conclude that the pair
(h,h)
is integrable and
we have
\phi(h,h)=1
.

To our knowledge there is no proper definition of
“
p
-variation” for the Cantor set yet.
We leave the extension of our integration to the setting of ‘Wiener class’ for the future study.

Appendix A
The Young integration on the interval

We recall the definition of the Young integration and its existence by referring to
[
11
]
. Let
I
be an interval
[a,b]
.

Definition A.1
.

Let
f,g:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. We say that the Stieltjes integral

Y(f,g)=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dg(x)

exists in the Riemann sense with the value
J
, if the sum
\sum_{r=1}^{N}f(\xi_{r})\left(g(x_{r})-g(x_{r-1})\right)
, in which
a=x_{0}\leq\xi_{1}\leq x_{1}\leq\cdots\leq x_{N-1}\leq\xi_{N}\leq x_{N}=b
, differs from
J
by at most
\epsilon_{\delta}
in modulus, when all
[x_{r-1},x_{r}]
have lengths less than
\delta
, where
\epsilon_{\delta}\rightarrow 0
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
.

We associate with
f(x):I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and with
p>0
,
\delta>0
, the quantity

V_{p}^{(\delta)}(f)=V_{p}^{(\delta)}(f;a,b)=\sup_{|\chi|\leq\delta}\left(\sum_{r}\left|f(x_{r})-f(x_{r-1})\right|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

for all subdivisions
\chi
of
[a,b]
into parts
[x_{r-1},x_{r}]
of lengths each less than
\delta
. We write
V_{p}(f)
as the value of
V_{p}^{(\delta)}(f)
for
\delta
exceeding
b-a
and call
V_{p}(f)

the mean variation of order

p
. We shall say that
f
belongs to the Wiener class
W_{p}

if
V_{p}(f)
is finite.
Note that if
f
is
1/p
-Hölder continuous function on
I
then
we have
f\in W_{p}
.

Theorem A.2

(Existence on Stieltjes integral)
.

Let
f\in W_{p}
and
g\in W_{q}
. If
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}>1
and
f,g
have no common discontinuities, their Stieltjes integral exists in the Riemann sense.

Appendix B
Generalization of the Cantor function on the
(2k+1)
-adic Cantor set

For a positive integer
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
s\in S=\{0,1,\dots k\}
,
we set

f_{s}(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{x}$})=\dfrac{x}{2k+1}+\dfrac{2s}{2k+1}\quad(x\in\mathbb{R}).

Then we call the self-similar set
CS_{k}
of the IFS
\left(I=[0,1],S,\{f_{s}\}_{s\in S}\right)
the
(2k+1)
-adic Cantor set.
By definition, we have
\dim_{S}(CS_{k})=\log_{(2k+1)}(k+1)
.
It is easy to show that any element
x\in CS_{k}
is given by the formula

x=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2s_{i}}{(2k+1)^{i}}\quad(s_{i}\in S).

In particular, we have

a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{2s_{i}}{(2k+1)^{i}}\quad\text{and}\quad b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}=a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}+\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2k}{(2k+1)^{i}}\quad(\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}\in S^{\times n}).

See Section
1
for the definition of
a_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}
and
b_{\mbox{$\boldsymbol{s}$}}
.
We want to generalize the Cantor function on the middle-third Cantor set
CS=CS_{1}
to
CS_{k}
.
The following definition is almost the same as the one in
[
1
]
, but slightly different.

Definition B.1
.

For
0<p<1
, we set

c_{k,p}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2s_{i}}{(2k+1)^{i}}\right)=\dfrac{1-p}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}s_{i}p^{i-1}\quad(s_{i}\in S).

A straightforward calculation implies the following basic properties:

Proposition B.2
.

For
0<p<1
, we have the followings:

(1)

c_{1,1/2}=c
, the Cantor function on the middle-third Cantor set.

(2)

c_{k,1/(2k+1)}=x|_{CS_{k}}
.

(3)

c_{k,p}(0)=0
.

(4)

c_{k,p}\left(\frac{1}{(2k+1)^{n}}\right)=p^{n}

for any non-negative integer
n=0,1,2,\dots
.
In particular, we have
c_{k,p}(1)=1
.

Moreover,
c_{k,p}
satisfies the following properties:

Theorem B.3
.

For
0<p<1
,

c_{k,p}
is a
\log_{(2k+1)}(p^{-1})
-Hölder continuous function
on
CS_{k}
.

Proof.

Take any
x,y\in CS_{k}

with
x=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{2s_{i}}{(2k+1)^{i}}

and
y=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{2t_{i}}{(2k+1)^{i}}

such that

\dfrac{1}{(2k+1)^{n+1}}<|x-y|\leq\dfrac{1}{(2k+1)^{n}}

for a non-negative integer
n
.
Then we have
s_{i}=t_{i}
(
i=1,2,\dots n
) and
s_{n+1}=t_{n+1}
, and

\displaystyle|c_{k,p}(x)-c_{k,p}(y)|

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1-p}{k}\left|\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}(s_{i}-t_{i})p^{i-1}\right|\leq(1-p)\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}p^{i-1}

\displaystyle=p^{n}=p^{-1}\left(\dfrac{1}{(2k+1)^{n+1}}\right)^{\log_{(2k+1)}(p^{-1})}

\displaystyle\leq p^{-1}|x-y|^{\log_{(2k+1)}(p^{-1})}.

This implies that the function
c_{k,p}
is a Hölder continuous function
with the Hölder exponent
\log_{(2k+1)}(p^{-1})
.
∎

Theorem B.4
.

For any fixed
0<p_{0}<1
,

c_{k,p}
converges to
c_{k,p_{0}}
as
p\to p_{0}

uniformly on
CS_{k}
.

Proof.

Fix any
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that
\epsilon_{0}<p_{0}<1-\epsilon_{0}
.
Let
\epsilon>0
such that
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
.
For any
x=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{2s_{i}}{(2k+1)^{i}}\in CS_{k}

and
0<p<1
such that
|p-p_{0}|<\epsilon
,
we have

\displaystyle|c_{k,p}(x)-c_{k,p_{0}}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}s_{i}|(1-p)p^{i}-(1-p_{0})p_{0}^{i}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(|p^{i}-p_{0}^{i}|+|p^{i+1}-p_{0}^{i+1}|\right)

\displaystyle=|p-p_{0}|\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}p^{k}p_{0}^{i-1-k}+\sum_{k=0}^{i}p^{k}p_{0}^{i-k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2|p-p_{0}|\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}i(p_{0}+\epsilon_{0})^{i-1}

\displaystyle\leq 2|p-p_{0}|\dfrac{1}{(1-(p_{0}+\epsilon_{0}))^{2}}

\displaystyle<\dfrac{2\epsilon}{(1-(p_{0}+\epsilon_{0}))^{2}}.

This proves the theorem.
∎
