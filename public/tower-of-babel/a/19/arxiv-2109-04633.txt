A Fixed-point Theorem for Horn Formula Equations
Stefan Hetzl Johannes Kloibhofer

Abstract
We consider constrained Horn clause solving from the more general point of view of solving formula equations. Constrained Horn clauses correspond to the subclass of Horn formula equations. We state and prove a fixed-point theorem for Horn formula equations which is based on expressing the fixed-point computation of a minimal model of a set of Horn clauses on the object level as a formula in first-order logic with a least fixed point operator. We describe several corollaries of this fixed-point theorem, in particular concerning the logical foundations of program verification, and sketch how to generalise it to incorporate abstract interpretations.



1
Introduction

Constrained Horn clauses are a versatile and practical formalism for representing and solving
a variety of problems in program verification and model checking
[
5
,
18
]
.
In this paper we approach constrained Horn clause solving from a theoretical point of view.
In logic, related problems have a long history:
solving Boolean equations goes back to the 19th century and was already investigated
in
[
33
]
, see
[
31
]
for a comprehensive textbook.
Solving Boolean equations is closely related to Boolean unification, a subject of thorough study in
computer science, see, e.g.,
[
27
]
for a survey.
The generalisation of this problem from propositional to first-order logic has been made explicit
as early as
[
3
,
4
]
.
Solving a formula equation in first-order logic is closely related to second-order quantifier elimination,
a problem with applications in a variety of areas in computer science, e.g., databases or common-sense
reasoning
[
15
]
.
A seminal work on second-order quantifier elimination and basis of several algorithms still in use today
is Ackermann’s
[
2
]
.
See
[
36
]
for a recent survey of this area of related problems.

Solving constrained Horn clauses is closely related to solving formula equations.
In fact, constrained Horn clauses correspond to a natural class of formula
equations which we will call Horn formula equations.
This relationship allows for an elegant theoretical description of the connections between
problems considered from Ackermann to contemporary verification.

Fixed-point theorems play an important role for solving equations in many areas of mathematics.
In recursion theory, a subject with close ties to verification, the recursion theorem guarantees the
existence of a solution of a system of recursion equations by computing a fixed point.
But also in areas quite remote from verification similar constructions can be found, as in the use of Banach’s
fixed-point theorem in the proof of the
Picard-Lindelöf theorem on the unique solvability of ordinary differential equations.
In constrained Horn clause solving we have a comparable situation: it is a well-known result from
logic programming and constraint logic programming that every set of Horn clauses has a unique minimal model
(in the sense of identifying a model with the ground atoms true in it) and that this minimal model
can be computed as the fixed-point of an operator induced by the clause set
[
34
,
23
]
.

In this paper we formulate and prove a fixed-point theorem for Horn formula equations which (essentially)
makes the construction of the minimal model explicit
in the logic
.
Expressing this construction will be achieved by using FO[LFP], first-order logic with a least fixed-point operator,
thus providing a canonical solution for any Horn formula equation.
The fixed-point theorem has a number of applications:
it helps to explain, at least from a theoretical point of view, the efficacy of interpolation for Horn
clause solving and invariant generation.
Moreover, as a simple corollary one can obtain the expressibility of the
weakest precondition and the strongest postcondition, and thus the partial correctness of an imperative
program in FO[LFP].
As another corollary it allows a generalisation of a result by
Ackermann
[
2
]
on second-order quantifier-elimination in a direction different
from the recent generalisation
[
35
]
.
A result from a recently introduced approach to inductive theorem proving with tree grammars described
in
[
11
]
on generating a proof with induction based on partial information about
that proof can be obtained from our fixed-point theorem as another straightforward corollary.
Last, but not least, an abstract form of the fixed-point theorem, stated here as Conjecture
24
, would
permit to considerably simplify the proof of the decidability of affine formula
equations
[
20
]
.

In Section
2
we relate constrained Horn clause solving with solving formula equations.
The fixed-point theorem is stated and proved in detail in Section
3
.
Section
4
describes some of its applications to the foundations of program verification.
In Section
5
we sketch how to generalise our fixed-point theorem to accommodate
abstract interpretation based on Galois connections.
This paper is an improved presentation of some of the main results of the second authors master’s
thesis
[
26
]
.

2
Constrained Horn clauses and formula equations

We use standard notation from logic.
In particular, for a first-order language
\mathcal{L}
, an
\mathcal{L}
-structure
\mathcal{M}
, an
\mathcal{L}
-formula
\varphi
,
and an interpretation
I
of the free variables of
\varphi
in
\mathcal{M}
we write
\mathcal{M},I\models\varphi
to
express that
\varphi
is true in
\mathcal{M}
under the interpretation
I
(in the usual sense of Tarski semantics).
Occasionally we will, in a slight abuse of notation, also allow elements of
\mathcal{M}
or relations over
\mathcal{M}
to
appear on the right-hand side of
\models
by which we intend to denote truth in
\mathcal{M}
under
an accordingly modified interpretation
I
.
We write
\models\varphi
to express that
\varphi
is a valid formula.
Individual variables will be denoted by lowercase Latin letters
x,y,z,u,v\ldots
.
Predicate variables will be denoted by uppercase Latin letters
X,Y,\ldots
.
If
X
is a
k
-ary predicate variable and
\chi
is a first-order formula with the free variables

v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
we write
[X\backslash\chi]
for the substitution of
X
by
\chi
inserting the
i
-th
argument of an
X
-atom for
v_{i}
.
We write
[X_{1}\backslash\chi_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\backslash\chi_{n}]
for the
simultaneous substitution of
X_{i}
by
\chi_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
A substitution
[X_{1}\backslash\chi_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\backslash\chi_{n}]
is
called
first-order substitution
if
\chi_{1},\ldots,\chi_{n}
are first-order formulas.
The logical symbol
\bot
is a nullary predicate constant which is false in all structures.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a first-order language and let
T
be an
\mathcal{L}
-theory.
A
constrained Horn clause
is an
\mathcal{L}
-formula of the form

\varphi\land X_{1}(\overline{t_{1}})\land\cdots\land X_{n}(\overline{t_{n}})\rightarrow Y(\overline{s})
or

\varphi\land X_{1}(\overline{t_{1}})\land\cdots\land X_{n}(\overline{t_{n}})\rightarrow\bot

where
\overline{t_{1}},\ldots,\overline{t_{n}},\overline{s}
are
tuples of first-order terms of appropriate arity and
\varphi
is a first-order formula, i.e., a formula not containing
predicate variables.
Note that a constrained Horn clause is allowed to (and typically does) contain free individual variables which,
as usual in clause logic, are treated as universally quantified.
A finite set
S
of constrained Horn clauses is considered as the conjunction of these clauses and is thus
logically equivalent to a formula of the form
\forall^{*}\bigwedge_{C\in S}C
where
\forall^{*}
denotes
the universal closure w.r.t. individual variables.
We are interested in solving a given finite set of constrained Horn clauses.
There are different notions of solvability in the literature which we will discuss in detail below.

In this paper we embed constrained Horn clauses in the more general framework of formula equations.
In the context of logical formulas, we consider an equation to be

\text{an $\mathcal{L}$-formula}\ \varphi_{1}\leftrightarrow\varphi_{2}\ \text{containing predicate variables $\overline{X}=X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}$.}

(1)

A solution of (
1
) is a first-order substitution
[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\chi}]

s.t.
\models(\varphi_{1}\leftrightarrow\varphi_{2})[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\chi}]
.
A solution in
T
has to satisfy
T\models(\varphi_{1}\leftrightarrow\varphi_{2})[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\chi}]
instead.
Since the concept of solution in
T
subsumes that of solution (by setting
T=\emptyset
) we will only use the
former.
We can simplify equations to instead considering

\text{an $\mathcal{L}$-formula}\ \varphi\ \text{containing predicate variables $\overline{X}=X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}$.}

(2)

Then, as a solution in
T
, we ask for a first-order substitution
[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\chi}]
s.t.
T\models\varphi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\chi}]
.
Note that every instance of (
1
) is an instance of (
2
) by letting
\varphi
be
\varphi_{1}\leftrightarrow\varphi_{2}

and every instance of (
2
) is an instance of (
1
) by letting
\varphi_{1}
be
\varphi
and

\varphi_{2}
be
\top
.
Moreover, it will be notationally useful to explicitly indicate the predicate variables by
existential quantifiers.
Consequently we define:

Definition 1
.

A
formula equation
is a closed
\mathcal{L}
-formula
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
where

\varphi
contains only first-order quantifiers.
A
solution
of
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
in
T
is a first-order substitution

[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\chi}]
s.t.
T\models\varphi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\chi}]
.

The problem of computing a solution to a formula equation given as input will be denoted as FEQ in the sequel.
A formula equation
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
is called
valid
if it is a valid second-order formula
and
satisfiable
if it is a satisfiable second-order formula.
If
S
is a set of constrained Horn clauses in the predicate variables
\overline{X}=X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, then

\exists\overline{X}\forall^{*}\bigwedge_{C\in S}C
will be called
Horn formula equation
.
Thus constrained Horn clauses correspond to existential second order Horn logic, which also plays a
significant role in finite model theory, see
[
16
]
.

There are different notions of solvability for constrained Horn clauses in the literature:

satisfiability
of
[
18
]
is satisfiability of a Horn formula equation,

semantic solvability
of
[
32
]
is validity of a Horn formula equation, and

syntactic solvability
of
[
32
]
is solvability of a Horn formula equation.
In this paper we will primarily be interested in this last notion: solvability of a Horn formula equation.
Every solvable formula equation is valid and every valid formula equation is satisfiable but neither of
the converse implications are true as the following example shows.

Example 2
.

If
\varphi
is a first-order formula which is satisfiable but not valid and does not contain
X

then, trivially,
\exists X\,\varphi
is a formula equation which is satisfiable but not valid.

Towards an example for a valid but unsolvable Horn formula equation we work in the first-order language

\mathcal{L}=\{0/0,s/1\}
.
Let
A_{1}
be
\forall x\,s(x)\neq 0
and let
A_{2}
be
\forall x\forall y\,(s(x)=s(y)\rightarrow x=y)

and consider the formula

A_{1}\land A_{2}\rightarrow\exists X\exists Y\forall u\,\Big(X(0)\land Y(s(0))\land(X(u)\rightarrow Y(s(u)))\land(Y(u)\rightarrow X(s(u)))\land\neg(X(u)\land Y(u))\Big)

which, up to some simple logical equivalence transformations, is a Horn formula equation
\Phi
.
Now
\Phi
is valid since, in a model
\mathcal{M}
of
A_{1}\land A_{2}
, interpreting
X
by

\{s^{2n}(0)\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
Y
by
\{s^{2n+1}(0)\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
makes the remaining
formula true.

For unsolvability suppose that
\Phi
has a solution
[X\backslash\chi(u),Y\backslash\psi(u)]
,
then, since the standard model
\mathbb{N}
in the language
\mathcal{L}
satisfies
A_{1}\land A_{2}
, we would have

\mathbb{N}\models\chi(0)\land\psi(s(0))\land\forall u\,(\chi(u)\rightarrow\psi(s(u)))\land\forall u\,(\psi(u)\rightarrow\chi(s(u)))\land\forall u\,\neg(\chi(u)\land\psi(u)),

in particular
\chi
would be a definition of the even numbers.
However, the theory of
\mathbb{N}
in
\mathcal{L}
admits quantifier elimination
[
12
, Theorem 31G]

which has the consequence that the
L
-definable sets in
\mathbb{N}
are the finite and co-finite
subsets of
\mathbb{N}

[
12
, Section 3.1, Exercise 4]
and thus we obtain a contradiction to
\chi
being
a definition of the even numbers (which is neither finite nor co-finite).

Solving formula equations (FEQ) is closely related to the problem of second-order quantifier elimination (SOQE):
given a formula
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
where
\varphi
contains only first-order quantifiers find
a first-order formula
\psi
s.t.
\models\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi
.
The relationship between FEQ and SOQE often hinges on a third problem: second-order quantifier elimination
by a witness (WSOQE): given a formula
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
where
\varphi
contains only first-order
quantifiers find a first-order substitution
[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\chi}]

s.t.
\models\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi\leftrightarrow\varphi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\chi}]
,
see
[
36
]
for more details.
This complex of problems has a long history in logic and a wealth of applications in computer science,
see the textbook
[
15
]
on second-order quantifier elimination.
A number of algorithms for second-order quantifier elimination have been developed, for example:
The SCAN algorithm introduced in
[
14
]
(tries to) compute(s) a first-order formula equivalent to
\exists X\,\varphi

for a conjunctive normal form
\varphi
by forming the closure of
\varphi
under constraint resolution which
only resolves on
X
-literals.
The DLS algorithm has been introduced in
[
9
]
and consists essentially of
formula rewriting steps tailored to allow application of Ackermann’s lemma (which instantiates a predicate
variable provided some conditions on the polarity of its occurrences are met).

3
The fixed-point theorem

It is well-known that a set of Horn clauses has a minimal model and that it can be obtained as
least fixed point of an operator induced by the clause set.
In order to integrate this insight seamlessly into the framework of formula equations we will express
it on the object level by representing this least fixed-point by means of an explicit
least fixed-point operator.
An adequate tool to that end is first-order logic with least fixed points, FO[LFP], which plays an
important role in finite model theory and descriptive complexity
[
22
]
.

In order to introduce FO[LFP] we first define, by induction on a formula
\varphi
, what it means
for a predicate variable
X
to occur positively (negatively) in
\varphi
:

1.

If
\varphi
is an atom of the form
X(\overline{t})
then
X
occurs positively in
\varphi
.

2.

X
occurs positively (negatively) in
\neg\varphi
iff
X
occurs negatively (positively) in
\varphi
.

3.

For
\circ\in\{\land,\lor\}
,
X
occurs positively (negatively) in
\varphi\circ\psi
iff

X
occurs positively (negatively) in
\varphi
or
X
occurs postively (negatively) in
\psi
.

4.

X
occurs positively (negatively) in
\varphi\rightarrow\psi
iff
X
occurs positively (negatively)
in
\psi
or
X
occurs negatively (positively) in
\varphi
.

5.

For
Q\in\{\forall,\exists\}
,
X
occurs positively (negatively) in
Qx\,\varphi
iff

X
occurs positively (negatively) in
\varphi
.

Example 3
.

X
occurs positively but not negatively in
\forall u\,(P(u)\rightarrow X(u))
.

X
occurs both positively and negatively in
\forall u\,(X(u)\rightarrow X(s(u)))
.

FO[LFP] is first-order logic, augmented with a least fixed point operator
\mathrm{lfp}
which allows
to add new predicates to the logic that have the form
[\mathrm{lfp}_{X}\,\varphi(X,\overline{u})]
where

\varphi
is a formula in which
X
occurs only positively
and the length of
\overline{u}
is the arity of
X
.
Then
\varphi
defines a monotone function
F_{\varphi}:\mathcal{P}(M)^{k}\to\mathcal{P}(M)^{k}
and since
the power set lattice
\mathcal{P}(M)^{k}
is complete, the Knaster-Tarski theorem applies and the
least fixed point of
F_{\varphi}
is well-defined.
The predicate
[\mathrm{lfp}_{X}\,\varphi(X,\overline{u})]
is interpreted as that least fixed point.

Example 4
.

Working in the language
\{E/2\}
of graphs, let
X
be a binary predicate variable and define

\varphi(X,u,v)\quad\equiv\quad u=v\lor\exists w(X(u,w)\wedge E(w,v)).

As
X
occurs only positively in
\varphi
we can form
[\mathrm{lfp}_{X}\,\varphi(X,u,v)]
and
observe that
[\mathrm{lfp}_{X}\,\varphi(X,u,v)](a,b)
is true iff there is a path from
a
to
b
.

In this paper we will consider first-order logic with an operator for simultaneous least fixed points
(which corresponds to introducing mutual recursion).
Then we require a tuple
\Phi=(\varphi_{i}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},\overline{u_{i}}))_{i=1}^{n}
of formulas containing the

X_{i}
only positively where the length of
\overline{u_{i}}
is the arity
k_{i}
of
X_{i}
.
For a structure
\mathcal{M}
with domain
M
we define

\displaystyle F_{i}:\quad M^{k_{1}}\times\cdots\times M^{k_{n}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow M^{k_{i}},

\displaystyle(R_{1},\ldots,R_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto\{\overline{a}\in M^{k_{i}}\mid\mathcal{M}\models\varphi_{i}(R_{1},\ldots,R_{n},\overline{a})\}.

and the operator
F_{\Phi}=(F_{1},...,F_{n}):M^{k_{1}}\times\cdots\times M^{k_{n}}\to M^{k_{1}}\times\cdots\times M^{k_{n}}
.
Since the
X_{i}
occur only positively in the
\varphi_{j}
, the operator
F_{\Phi}
is monotone and, again,
the Knaster-Tarski theorem applies.
Thus we obtain new predicates
[\mathrm{lfp}_{X_{i}}\Phi]
for
i=1,\ldots,n
which are interpreted by the
i
-th
component of the least fixed point of the operator
F_{\Phi}
.
For more details, the reader is referred to
[
8
]
.

Let
\exists X_{1}\cdots\exists X_{n}\,\psi
be a Horn formula equation.
We distinguish three different types of clauses in
\psi
:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{crl}(B)&\varphi&\rightarrow X_{i_{0}}(\overline{s}),\\
(I)&\varphi\land X_{i_{1}}(\overline{t_{1}})\land\cdots\land X_{i_{m}}(\overline{t_{m}})&\rightarrow X_{i_{0}}(\overline{s}),\\
(E)&\varphi\land X_{i_{1}}(\overline{t_{1}})\land\cdots\land X_{i_{m}}(\overline{t_{m}})&\rightarrow\bot,\end{array}

where the constraint
\varphi
is a formula in
\mathcal{L}
not containing a predicate variable,

m\geq 1
,
\overline{t_{1}},..,\overline{t_{m}},\overline{s}
are
tuples of first-order terms in
\mathcal{L}
of appropriate arity and
i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Note that free variables
\overline{y}
may occur in the formulas
\varphi
and the terms

\overline{s},\overline{t_{1}},\ldots,\overline{t_{m}}
.
We call the first base clauses, the second induction clauses, and the third end clauses.
The idea now is to build an inductive relation from the base and induction clauses for every
formula variable.
For
j=1,\ldots,n
let
B_{j}
and
I_{j}
be the sets of clauses of the form (B) and (I), respectively,
where
i_{0}=j
.
In order to abbreviate notation we write
\iota:=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})
and
\tau:=(\overline{t_{1}},\ldots,\overline{t_{m}})
.
A clause in
I_{j}
is determined by the tuple
(\varphi,\iota,\tau,\overline{s})
, thus we write

(\varphi,\iota,\tau,\overline{s})
for the clause

\varphi\land X_{i_{1}}(\overline{t_{1}})\land\cdots\land X_{i_{m}}(\overline{t_{m}})\rightarrow X_{j}(\overline{s})

in
I_{j}
.
Analogously we write
(\varphi,\overline{s})
for the clause
\varphi\rightarrow X_{j}(\overline{s})
in
B_{j}
.

Definition 5
.

Let
\exists X_{1}\cdots\exists X_{n}\,\psi
be a Horn formula equation.
Define the
n
-tuple
\Phi_{\psi}=(\varphi_{j}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},\overline{x_{j}}))_{j=1}^{n}
where, for
j=1,\ldots,n
,

\varphi_{j}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},\overline{x_{j}})\quad\text{is}\quad\exists\overline{y}\left(\bigvee_{(\varphi,\overline{s})\in B_{j}}\left(\varphi\land\overline{x_{j}}=\overline{s}\right)\lor\bigvee_{(\varphi,\iota,\tau,\overline{s})\in I_{j}}\left(\varphi\land\bigwedge_{k=1}^{m}X_{i_{k}}(\overline{t_{k}})\land\overline{x_{j}}=\overline{s}\right)\right)

where
\overline{y}
are the free variables of the clauses in
B_{j}\cup I_{j}
and
\overline{x_{j}}
is a
tuple of variables s.t.
|\overline{x_{j}}|
equals the arity of
X_{j}
.

From the point of view of (constraint) logic programming, the above tuple of formulas is a first-order definition
of the operator
T_{P}
induced by
\exists\overline{X}\,\psi
when considered as a constraint logic program
P
,
see, e.g.,
[
23
]
.
Note that
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
only occur positively in
\Phi_{\psi}
, hence we can introduce the simultaneous
fixed-point formulas
[\mathrm{lfp}_{X_{j}}~\Phi_{\psi}]
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\exists X_{1}\cdots\exists X_{n}\,\psi
be a Horn formula equation and
\mu_{j}:=[\mathrm{lfp}_{X_{j}}~\Phi_{\psi}]

for
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, then

1.

\models\exists\overline{X}\,\psi\leftrightarrow\psi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\mu}]
and

2.

if
\mathcal{M}\models\psi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{R}]

for some structure
\mathcal{M}
and relations
R_{1},\ldots,R_{n}
in
\mathcal{M}
, then

\mathcal{M}\models\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}\rightarrow R_{j})
.

Proof.

The right-to-left direction of
1
is clear.
For the left-to-right direction we first observe that the formulas
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}
satisfy all
clauses in (B) and (I), i.e., for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
we have

\displaystyle\models

\displaystyle\forall\overline{y}\,(\varphi\rightarrow\mu_{j}(\overline{s})),

\displaystyle\forall(\varphi,\overline{s})\in B_{j},

\displaystyle\models

\displaystyle\forall\overline{y}\,(\varphi\land\mu_{i_{1}}(\overline{t_{1}})\land\cdots\land\mu_{i_{m}}(\overline{t_{m}})\rightarrow\mu_{j}(\overline{s})),

\displaystyle\forall(\varphi,\iota,\tau,\overline{s})\in I_{j}.

To see this let
\mathcal{M}
be a structure and
\overline{a}
s.t.
\mathcal{M},[\overline{y}:=\overline{a}]\models\varphi\land\mu_{i_{1}}(\overline{t_{1}})\land\cdots\land\mu_{i_{m}}(\overline{t_{m}})
,
then, as
(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
is a fixed point of
F_{\Phi}
, we have

\mathcal{M},[\overline{y}:=\overline{a}]\models\mu_{j}(\overline{s})
.
The argumentation is analogous for clauses of the form (B).

Now let
\mathcal{M}
be a structure s.t.
\mathcal{M}\models\exists\overline{X}\,\psi
.
Let
R_{1},\ldots,R_{n}
be relations in
\mathcal{M}
s.t.
\mathcal{M}\models\psi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{R}]
.
Then for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\models

\displaystyle\forall\overline{y}\,(\varphi\rightarrow R_{j}(\overline{s})),

\displaystyle\forall(\varphi,\overline{s})\in B_{j},

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\models

\displaystyle\forall\overline{y}\,(\varphi\land R_{i_{1}}(\overline{t_{1}})\land\cdots\land R_{i_{m}}(\overline{t_{m}})\rightarrow R_{j}(\overline{s})),

\displaystyle\forall(\varphi,\iota,\tau,\overline{s})\in I_{j}.

(4)

Assume
\overline{a_{j}}\in F_{\Phi}(R_{1},\ldots,R_{n})_{j}
.
Then there either exists
(\varphi,\overline{s})\in B_{j}
s.t.
\mathcal{M}\models\exists\overline{y}\,(\varphi\land\overline{a_{j}}=\overline{s})

or there exists
(\varphi,\iota,\tau,\overline{s})\in I_{j}
s.t.

\mathcal{M},[\overline{X}:=\overline{R}]\models\exists\overline{y}\,(\varphi\land\bigwedge_{k=1}^{m}X_{i_{k}}(\overline{t_{k}})\land\overline{a_{j}}=\overline{s})
.
We assume the latter, the proof for the former is analogous.
Thus let
\overline{a}
be s.t.

\mathcal{M},[\overline{X}:=\overline{R},\overline{y}:=\overline{a}]\models\varphi\land\bigwedge_{k=1}^{m}X_{i_{k}}(\overline{t_{k}})\land\overline{x_{j}}=\overline{s}.

From (
4
) we obtain
\mathcal{M},[\overline{y}:=\overline{a}]\models R_{j}(\overline{s})
and thus
\mathcal{M}\models R_{j}(\overline{a_{j}})
.

Hence
F_{\Phi}(R_{1},\ldots,R_{n})\subseteq(R_{1},\ldots,R_{n})
and as
(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})

is the least fixed point of
F_{\Phi}
we obtain
\mathcal{M}\models\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}\rightarrow R_{j})
.

For all clauses in (E) we have

\mathcal{M},[\overline{X}:=\overline{R}]\models\forall\overline{y}\,(\varphi\land X_{i_{1}}(\overline{t_{1}})\land\cdots\land X_{i_{m}}(\overline{t_{m}})\rightarrow\bot),

and therefore, as
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
occur only negatively in this formula, we obtain

\mathcal{M},[\overline{X}:=\overline{\mu}]\models\forall\overline{y}\,(\varphi\land X_{i_{1}}(\overline{t_{1}})\land\cdots\land X_{i_{m}}(\overline{t_{m}})\rightarrow\bot).

Thus
\mathcal{M},[\overline{X}:=\overline{\mu}]
satisfies all clauses in
\psi
and we conclude that

\mathcal{M}\models\psi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\mu}]
.
For
2
. we get
\mathcal{M}\models\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}\rightarrow R_{j})
analogously
to the proof of
1
.
∎

Theorem 7

(Fixed-Point theorem)
.

Let
\exists X_{1}\cdots\exists X_{n}\,\psi
be a valid Horn formula equation
and let
\mu_{j}=[\mathrm{lfp}_{X_{j}}~\Phi_{\psi}]
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Then:

1.

\models\psi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\mu}]
,

2.

If
\models\psi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\chi}]
for FO[LFP]-formulas

\chi_{1},\ldots,\chi_{n}
, then
\models\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}\rightarrow\chi_{j})
.

Proof.

Follows immediately from Lemma
6
.
∎

We now turn to dual and linear Horn formula equations.
A dual constrained Horn clause is an
L
-formula of the form

\varphi\land X(\overline{t})\rightarrow Y_{1}(\overline{s_{1}})\lor\cdots\lor Y_{n}(\overline{s_{n}})
or

\varphi\rightarrow Y_{1}(\overline{s_{1}})\lor\cdots\lor Y_{n}(\overline{s_{n}})
where
\overline{t},\overline{s_{1}},\ldots,\overline{s_{n}}
are tuples of first-order terms of appropriate arity and
\varphi
is a
first-order formula, i.e., a formula not containing predicate variables.
A
dual Horn formula equation
is a formula equation of the form

\exists\overline{X}\forall\overline{y}\,\bigwedge_{i=1}^{m}H_{i}
, where
H_{i}
is a constrained
dual Horn clause for

.
A
linear Horn formula equation
is a formula equation that is both Horn and dual Horn.

For a formula
\psi
we define
\psi^{D}
as
\psi[X_{1}\backslash\neg X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\backslash\neg X_{n}]

where
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are all predicate variables occurring in
\psi
.
Note that
\models\psi\leftrightarrow(\psi^{D})^{D}
for all formulas
\psi
.
Moreover, note that
\models\exists\overline{X}\,\psi\leftrightarrow\exists\overline{X}\,\psi^{D}
where
\overline{X}=X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}

are all predicate variables occurring in
\psi
.
If
\exists\overline{X}\,\psi
is a Horn formula equation, then
\exists\overline{X}\,\psi^{D}
is logically
equivalent to a dual Horn formula equation and if
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
is a dual Horn formula
equation, then
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi^{D}
is logicall equivalent to a Horn formula equation.
Note that dualisation of a (dual) Horn formula equation interchanges (B)- and (E)-clauses.

Example 8
.

Consider the constrained Horn clauses

\psi\quad\equiv\quad X(a)\land(X(u)\land X(v)\rightarrow Y(f(u,v)))\land(Y(w)\rightarrow\bot).

The dualisation of
\psi
is

\psi^{D}\quad\equiv\quad\neg X(a)\land(\neg X(u)\land\neg X(v)\rightarrow\neg Y(f(u,v)))\land(\neg Y(w)\rightarrow\bot)

which is logically equivalent to the dual constrained Horn clauses

(X(a)\rightarrow\bot)\land(Y(f(u,v))\rightarrow X(u)\lor X(v))\land Y(w).

We can now prove the following result which is dual to Lemma

Lemma 9

Let
\exists X_{1}\cdots\exists X_{n}\,\psi

\nu_{j}:=\neg[\mathrm{lfp}_{X_{j}}~\Phi_{\psi^{D}}]

1.

\models\exists\overline{X}\,\psi\leftrightarrow\psi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\nu}]

and

2.

if

,

\mathcal{M}

\mathcal{M}\models\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}(R_{j}\rightarrow\nu_{j})

Proof.

Let
\mu_{j}:=[\mathrm{lfp}_{X_{j}}~\Phi_{\psi^{D}}]
for
j=1,\ldots,n
.
For
1
. note that, since
\exists\overline{X}\,\psi
is a dual Horn formula

\exists\overline{X}\,\psi^{D}
is logically equivalent to a Horn formula equation. An application of Lemma

/

\models\exists\overline{X}\,\psi^{D}\leftrightarrow\psi^{D}[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\mu}]

Since

For

\mathcal{M}\models\psi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{R}]

\mathcal{M}

and relations

Theorem 10

(Dual Horn fixed-point theorem)
.

Let
\exists X_{1}\cdots\exists X_{n}\psi

and let
\nu_{j}=\neg[\mathrm{lfp}_{X_{j}}~\Phi_{\psi^{D}}]
for

1.

\models\psi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\nu}]
and

2.

if
\models\psi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\chi}]
for FO[LFP]-formulas

\chi_{1},\ldots,\chi_{n}
, then
\models\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}(\chi_{j}\rightarrow\nu_{j})
.

Proof.

Follows immediately from Lemma

.
∎

Note that the operator induced by
\Phi_{\psi^{D}}
is not the dual operator of the one induced by
\Phi_{\psi}

For the case of linear Horn formula equations we obtain:

Theorem 11

(Linear Horn fixed-point theorem)
.

Let
\exists X_{1}\cdots\exists X_{n}\,\psi
be a valid linear Horn formula equation,
let
\mu_{j}=[\mathrm{lfp}_{X_{j}}~\Phi_{\psi}]
and
\nu_{j}=\neg[\mathrm{lfp}_{Y_{j}}~\Phi_{\psi^{D}}]

1.

\models\psi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\mu}]

Theorem
11
/
2
. shows that solving a linear Horn formula

two tuples of FO[LFP]-formulas we seek to find a tuple of first-order, i.e.,
fixed-point free

At least from a theoretical point of view this result helps to explain the efficacy of interpolation-based

[

The relationship between interpolation and Horn clauses has also been studied by

17
]
.

4
Applications to program verification

In this section we will describe some direct applications of our fixed-point theorem to
the foundations of program verification.
As an exemplary framework we will consider the Hoare calculus for a simple imperative programming
language as in
[
37
]
.
We fix the first-order language of arithmetic
\mathcal{L}=\{0,1,+,-,\cdot,\leq\}
.
The set of programs is defined by

\displaystyle p::=\textbf{skip}~|~x:=t~|~p_{0};p_{1}~|~\textbf{if}~B~\textbf{then}~p_{0}~\textbf{else}~p_{1}~|~\textbf{while}~B~\textbf{do}~p_{0},

where
t
is an
\mathcal{L}
-term,
B
a quantifier-free first-order formula in
\mathcal{L}

and
x
is a program variable.

The denotational semantics of programs is defined as usual based on a set of states
\Sigma
:
we write
C(p)
for the partial function from
\Sigma
to
\Sigma
that is the denotational
semantics of the program
p
.
A Hoare triple is written as
\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\}
.
For the purposes of this paper we fix the program variables to taking values in the integers and thus
we can work in the standard model
\mathbb{Z}
.
We write
\sigma\models\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\}
if
\mathbb{Z},\sigma\models\varphi
implies
that
\mathbb{Z},C(p)(\sigma)\models\psi
and
\models\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\}

if
\sigma\models\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\}
for all
\sigma\in\Sigma
.
The Hoare calculus can be defined as usual, see, e.g.
[
37
]
.
We write
\vdash\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\}
if
\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\}
is provable
in the Hoare calculus.
We can then consider the verification condition of a Hoare triple as a Horn formula
equation as follows:

Definition 12
.

The
verification condition
of a Hoare triple
\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\}
,
written
\mathrm{vc}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})
, is a formula equation

\exists\overline{I}\forall^{*}\,\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})
,
where
\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})
is defined by structural induction on
p
as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{\varphi\}\textbf{skip}\{\psi\})

\displaystyle=\varphi\rightarrow\psi

(1)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{\varphi\}x_{j}:=t\{\psi\})

\displaystyle=\varphi\rightarrow\psi[x_{j}\backslash t]

(2)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{\varphi\}p_{0};p_{1}\{\psi\})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{\varphi\}p_{0}\{I\})\land\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{I\}p_{1}\{\psi\})

(3)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{\varphi\}\textbf{if}~B~\textbf{then}~p_{0}~\textbf{else}~p_{1}\{\psi\})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{\varphi\land B\}p_{0}\{\psi\})\land\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{\varphi\land\neg B\}p_{1}\{\psi\})

(4)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{\varphi\}\textbf{while}~B~\textbf{do}~p_{0}\{\psi\})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{I\land B\}p_{0}\{I\})\land(\varphi\rightarrow I)\land(I\land\neg B\rightarrow\psi)

(5)

where, in clauses (3) and (5),
I
is a fresh predicate variable which does not appear in

\varphi
nor in
\psi
.
Then
\mathrm{vc}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})=\exists\overline{I}\forall^{*}\,\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})
is
obtained from
\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})
by
existential quantification of all predicate variables in
\forall^{*}\,\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})
.

Note that this is an purely syntactic definition, thus we can define
\mathrm{vc}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})

analogously for a program
p
and second-order formulas
\varphi,\psi
.
We then obtain the following completeness result which characterises Hoare provability by truth of a formula
equation.

Theorem 13
.

Let
\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\}
be a Hoare triple.
Then
\vdash\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\}
iff
\mathbb{Z}\models\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\}
iff
\mathbb{Z}\models\mathrm{vc}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})
.

Proof Sketch.

\vdash\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\}
iff
\mathbb{Z}\models\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\}
is soundness and completeness
of the Hoare calculus.
The implication from
\vdash\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\}
to
\Rightarrow\quad\mathbb{Z}\models\mathrm{vc}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})

is proved by a straightforward induction on the structure of the Hoare proof of
\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\}

The implication from
\mathbb{Z}\models\mathrm{vc}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})
to
\mathbb{Z}\models\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\}
is proved
by translating the semantics.
∎

As a corollary we can now obtain the statement that a partial correctness assertion of an imperative program
is expressible as a formula in FO[LFP], a point also made in
[
6
]
for existential least
fixed-point logic.

Corollary 14
.

Let
\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\}
be a Hoare triple and let
\overline{\mu}
be the solution of

\mathbb{Z}\models\mathrm{vc}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})

\mathbb{Z}\models\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})[\overline{I}\backslash\overline{\mu}]

A second corollary is based on the observation that

\mathrm{vc}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})
is a linear Horn formula equation (which can be shown
by a straightforward induction).
Therefore we can apply Theorem
11
to the verification condition and obtain:

Corollary 15
.

Let
\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\}
be a Hoare triple,
then
\exists I_{1}\cdots\exists I_{n}\,\pi\equiv\mathrm{vc}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})

is a linear Horn formula equation.
Let
\pi^{D}
be the dual formula of
\pi
with predicate variables
K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}
.
Assume
\mathbb{Z}\models\mathrm{vc}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})
and let

\mu_{j}=[\mathrm{lfp}_{I_{j}}~\Phi_{\pi}]
and
\nu_{j}=\neg[\mathrm{lfp}_{K_{j}}~\Phi_{\pi^{D}}]

Then

1.

\mathbb{Z}\models\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})[\overline{I}\backslash\overline{\mu}]

\mathbb{Z}\models\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})[\overline{I}\backslash\overline{\nu}]

If
\mathbb{Z},[\overline{I}:=\overline{R}]\models\tilde{\mathrm{vc}}(\{\varphi\}p\{\psi\})

, then
\mathbb{Z}\models\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}((\mu_{j}\rightarrow R_{j})\land(R_{j}\rightarrow\nu_{j}))
.

This corollary shows that finding first-order formulas as loop invariants is equivalent to
an interpolation problem in the sense of finding a fixed-point free interpolant.
Just as Theorem
11
does for linear Horn clauses, this corollary contributes

[
28
]
.

As a third corollary we will show that the weakest precondition and the strongest postcondition
are expressed by the least and greatest solutions
\mu
and
\nu

based on the verification condition.

Definition 16
.

Let

\varphi,\psi

\mathcal{L}
.
The
weakest precondition

Let

For a formula

5
Towards an abstract fixed-point theorem

Abstract interpretation, originally introduced in
[
7
]
, is one of the most important techniques
in static analysis and software verification.
Since many verification techniques could successfully be generalised from programs to the logical level
of constrained Horn clauses it is also natural to expect this possibility for abstract interpretation.
And indeed, abstract interpretations have been used in tools for solving Horn clauses
[
21
,
24
]
.
In this section we briefly outline how we expect this generalisation to apply to our fixed-point theorem.
Proving the main statement of this section, Conjecture
24
, is currently work-in-progress.

An application we have in mind is the following: in the recent article
[
20
]

the decidability of the existence of affine invariants for programs with affine assignments
(essentially due to Karr
[
25
]
) has been generalised
to formula equations of the form
\exists\overline{X}\forall^{*}\varphi
for
\varphi
being a quantifier-free
formula.
The essential difference between the proof in
[
20
]
and
Theorem
7
is that in
[
20
]
the fixed point is formed in the lattice of affine subspaces and not in
the power set lattice.
We can allow for this possibility as follows.

Definition 20
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=(A,\subseteq)
and
\mathcal{B}=(B,\sqsubseteq)
be two partially ordered sets.
A
Galois connection
between
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
consists of two functions

\alpha:A\rightarrow B
and
\gamma:B\rightarrow A
, s.t. for all
X\in A

and
Y\in B
:

\displaystyle X\subseteq\gamma(Y)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\alpha(X)\sqsubseteq Y.

Definition 21
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a language.
A
model abstraction
is a pair
(\mathcal{M},G)
, where
\mathcal{M}
is an
\mathcal{L}
-structure and

G=(\mathcal{V}_{k},\alpha_{k},\gamma_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of triples, s.t. for all

k\in\mathbb{N}:

\mathcal{V}_{k}=(V_{k},\sqsubseteq)
is a complete lattice and

\alpha_{k}:\mathcal{P}(M^{k})\rightarrow V_{k}
and
\gamma_{k}:V_{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(M^{k})
form a Galois-connection
between
(\mathcal{P}(M^{k}),\subseteq)
and
\mathcal{V}_{k}
.

Example 22
.

Consider the language
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aff}}=(0,1,+,(c)_{c\in\mathbb{Q}})
where the intended interpretation of the
unary function symbol
c
for
c\in\mathbb{Q}
is multiplication with
c
.
Let
G_{{\mathrm{aff}}}=(({\mathrm{Aff}~}\mathbb{Q}^{k},\subseteq),{\mathrm{aff}}_{k},{\mathrm{id}}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
{\mathrm{Aff}~}\mathbb{Q}^{k}
is the set of all affine
subspaces of
\mathbb{Q}^{k}
,
{\mathrm{aff}}_{k}
maps every subset of
\mathbb{Q}^{k}
to its affine hull and
{\mathrm{id}}_{k}
is the
embedding of
{\mathrm{Aff}~}\mathbb{Q}^{k}
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Q}^{k})
.
Then
(\mathbb{Q},G_{{\mathrm{aff}}})
is a model abstraction.

We will now introduce the semantics of model abstractions.
We will interpret FO[LFP]- and second-order-formulas
\varphi
by defining a satisfaction relation

(\mathcal{M},G)\models_{\mathrm{a}}\varphi
.
The crucial difference between
\models_{\mathrm{a}}
and standard Tarski semantics
\models
will be that
second-order quantifiers and the least fixed-point operator will not be interpreted in the power set
of the domain but in
G
instead (for the appropriate arity).
This restriction of the domain of second-order quantification is reminiscent of (but different from)
Henkin semantics of second- and higher-order logic
[
19
]
.

Definition 23
.

The defining clauses for first-order atoms, propositional connectives, and first-order
quantifiers for
\models_{\mathrm{a}}
are identical to those for
\models
.
For formulas of the form
\exists X\,\psi
, where
X
is a
k

(\mathcal{M},G)\models_{a}\exists X\psi\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\exists S\in V_{k}:(\mathcal{M},G)\models_{a}\psi[X\backslash\gamma_{k}(S)],

and analogously for formulas of the form
\forall X\psi
.
The semantics of the
\mathrm{lfp}
-operator is defined as follows.
Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be formula variables with
X_{i}
having arity
k_{i}
, let

\Phi=(\varphi_{i}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},\overline{u_{i}}))_{i=1}^{n}
be a tuple of formulas
s.t.
|\overline{u_{i}}|=k_{i}
and
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
occur only positively in
\varphi_{i}

for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Let

\displaystyle F_{i}^{\#}:~V_{k_{1}}\times\cdots\times V_{k_{n}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow V_{k_{i}}

\displaystyle(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto\alpha_{k_{i}}\circ F_{i}(\gamma_{k_{1}}(Y_{1}),\ldots,\gamma_{k_{n}}(Y_{n})),

where
F_{i}
is defined as in Section
3
and let
F_{\Phi}^{\#}=(F_{1}^{\#},\ldots,F_{n}^{\#})
.
Then

(\mathcal{M},G)\models_{\mathrm{a}}[\mathrm{lfp}_{X_{i}}~\Phi](\overline{a})\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\overline{a}\in\gamma_{k_{i}}(\mathrm{lfp}(F_{\Phi}^{\#})_{i}).

Note that the semantics of the least fixed-point operator is well-defined:
We already know that
F_{i}
is a monotone operator and
\alpha_{k_{i}}
and
\gamma_{k_{i}}
are monotone
as they form a Galois connection for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Thus
F_{i}^{\#}
is monotone for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and therefore
F_{\Phi}^{\#}
is monotone as
well. As
\mathcal{V}_{k}
is a complete lattice for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we can use the Knaster-Tarski theorem
to obtain the least fixed point of
F_{\Phi}^{\#}
.

Conjecture 24

(Abstract fixed-point theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a language and
(\mathcal{M},G)
be a model abstraction.
Let
\exists X_{1}\cdots\exists X_{n}\,\psi
be a Horn
formula equation and let
\mu_{j}=[\mathrm{lfp}_{X_{j}}~\Phi_{\psi}]
for
j=1,\ldots,n
.
Then:

1.

(\mathcal{M},G)\models_{a}\exists\overline{X}\,\psi\leftrightarrow\psi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\mu}]
and

2.

if
(\mathcal{M},G)\models_{a}\psi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\chi}]
for
FO[LFP]-formulas
\chi_{1},\ldots,\chi_{n}
, then
(\mathcal{M},G)\models_{a}\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}\left(\alpha_{j}\rightarrow\chi_{j}\right)
.

Example 25
.

In continuation of Example
22
consider an
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aff}}
formula equation
\exists\overline{X}\,\forall^{*}\psi
where
\psi
is a quantifier-free formula.
We want to decide whether there are formulas
\overline{\chi}
s.t.
\mathbb{Q}\models\psi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\chi}]

and
\chi_{1},\ldots,\chi_{n}
are conjunctions of affine equations, i.e., they define affine subspaces of

\mathbb{Q}^{k}
in the model
\mathbb{Q}
.
As in
[
20
]
we reduce the solvability of
\exists\overline{X}\forall^{*}\,\psi
to
solvability of one of its finitely many projections which are Horn formula equations.
Let
\exists\overline{X}\forall^{*}\,\varphi
be one of them, then an application
of Conjecture
24
yields a tuple of FO[LFP]-formulas
\overline{\mu}
s.t.
(\mathbb{Q},G_{{\mathrm{aff}}})\models_{a}\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi\leftrightarrow\varphi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\mu}]
.
Since all lattices
{\mathrm{Aff}~}\mathbb{Q}^{k}
have finite height we can compute fixed-point free formulas
\overline{\chi}

equivalent to
\overline{\mu}
from
\overline{\mu}
and therefore
(\mathbb{Q},G_{{\mathrm{aff}}})\models_{a}\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi\leftrightarrow\varphi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\chi}]
.
Now
\varphi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\chi}]
is a first-order formula and hence
(\mathbb{Q},G_{{\mathrm{aff}}})\models_{a}\varphi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\chi}]

iff
\mathbb{Q}\models\varphi[\overline{X}\backslash\overline{\chi}]
.
The latter statement can now be checked by a decision procedure for linear arithmetic.

6
Conclusion

We have shown a fixed-point theorem for Horn formula equations and applied it to dual Horn formula equations
and linear Horn formula equations.
The proof of this result essentially consists of expressing the construction of a minimal model
of a set of Horn clauses, which is familiar from logic programming, on the object level as a formula
in first-order logic with least fixed points, thus providing
a canonical solution to a Horn formula equation in FO[LFP].

Note that Theorem
7
applies equally to constraints
\varphi
being FO[LFP]-formulas.
It therefore shows that FO[LFP], in contrast to first-order logic, has the property of being
closed under solving Horn formula equations.
It thus shows that in FO[LFP] validity and solvability of Horn formula equations coincide.
This is in contrast to formula equations in first-order logic, cf. Example
2
.

Explicit fixed-point operators have been employed for second-order quantifier elimination
in the
\mathrm{DLS}^{*}
algorithm introduced in
[
30
,
10
]
.
In this algorithm Ackermann’s lemma is generalised to a fixed-point lemma that covers additional situations.
While the
\mathrm{DLS}^{*}
algorithm as such will fail on Horn formula equations due to its priority
of shifting universal quantifiers inwards, the fixed-point lemma of the
\mathrm{DLS}^{*}
algorithm
could be used for solving Horn formula equations with a single variable when combined with
a different preprocessing.
However, for Horn formula equations with more than one predicate variable it would result
in solutions with iterated fixed points.
In this sense our fixed-point theorem can be considered a generalisation of the fixed-point lemma
of
[
30
]
.
On the other hand the fixed-point lemma of
[
30
]
is not restricted to
Horn formula equations.

These fixed-point theorems contribute to our theoretical understanding of the logical foundations of
constrained Horn clause solving and software verification:
Theorem
11
and Corollary
15
help to explain, from a theoretical
point of view, the efficacy of interpolation for Horn clause solving and invariant generation respectively.
Moreover, as corollary to our fixed point theorem we have obtained the expressibility of the
weakest precondition and the strongest postcondition, and thus the partial correctness of an imperative
program in FO[LFP].

As shown in
[
26
]
, our fixed-point theorem has a number of further
corollaries in a variety of application areas: it allows a generalisation of a result by
Ackermann
[
2
]
on second-order quantifier-elimination in a direction different
from the recent generalisation
[
35
]
of that result.
It allows to obtain a result on the generation of a proof with induction based on partial information
about that proof shown in
[
11
]
as straightforward corollary.
Last, but not least, an abstract form of the fixed-point theorem, Conjecture
24
, would
permit to considerably simplify the proof of the decidability of affine formula
equations given in
[
20
]
.

In conclusion, we believe that it is fruitful to consider constrained Horn clause solving from the more general
point of view of solving formula equations.
On the theoretical level this perspective uncovers connections to a number of topics such as
second-order quantifier elimination and results such as Ackermann’s
[
2
]
.
On the practical level it suggests to study the applicability of algorithms such as DLS and SCAN for
constrained Horn clauses and vice versa, that of algorithms for constrained Horn clause solving for
applications of second-order quantifier elimination.
