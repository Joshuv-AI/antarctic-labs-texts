On Spatial Conditioning of the Spectrum of Discrete Random Schrödinger Operators
Pierre Yves Gaudreau Lamarre, Promit Ghosal, Yuchen Liao

Abstract
. Consider a random Schrödinger-type operator of the form H := − H X + V + ξ H:=-H_{X}+V+\xi acting on a general graph 𝒢 = ( 𝒱 , ℰ ) \mathscr{G}=(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{E}) , where H X H_{X} is the generator of a Markov process X X on 𝒢 \mathscr{G} , V V is a deterministic potential with sufficient growth (so that H H has a purely discrete spectrum), and ξ \xi is a random noise with at-most-exponential tails. We prove that the eigenvalue point process of H H is number rigid in the sense of Ghosh and Peres [ 26 ] ; that is, the number of eigenvalues in any bounded domain B ⊂ ℂ B\subset\mathbb{C} is determined by the configuration of eigenvalues outside of B B . Our general setting allows to treat cases where X X could be non-symmetric (hence H H is non-self-adjoint) and ξ \xi has long-range dependence. Our strategy of proof consists of controlling the variance of the trace of the semigroup e − t ​ H \mathrm{e}^{-tH} using the Feynman-Kac formula.

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathscr{G}=(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{E})
be a countably infinite connected graph
with uniformly bounded degrees and a distinguished vertex
0\in\mathscr{V}
, which we call the root.
For example,
\mathscr{G}
could be the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
any semiregular tessellation/honeycomb of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that includes the
origin, or a much more general graph.

In this paper, we are interested in the spectral theory of
random Schrödinger-type operators of the form

Hf(v)=-H_{X}f(v)+\big(V(v)+\xi(v)\big)f(v),\qquad v\in\mathscr{V},~f:\mathscr{V}\to\mathbb{R},

where we assume that

(1)

H_{X}
is the infinitesimal generator of some continuous-time
Markov process
X
on
\mathscr{G}
(which need not be symmetric);

(2)

\xi:\mathscr{V}\to\mathbb{R}
is a random noise (which may have long-range
dependence); and

(3)

V:\mathscr{V}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is a deterministic potential with sufficient growth
at infinity (as measured by the size of
V(v)
as
v
grows farther away from
the root), ensuring that
H
has a purely discrete spectrum.

More specifically, we are interested in studying the
spatial conditioning
of the spectrum
of
H
, i.e., understanding the random configuration of
H
’s eigenvalues in some domain

B\subset\mathbb{C}
conditional on the configuration of eigenvalues outside of
B
.
As a first step in this direction, we establish that under general assumptions on
H_{X}
,
\xi
, and
V
,

H
’s spectrum is
number rigid
in the sense of Ghosh and Peres
[
26
]
; that is,
the number of eigenvalues of
H
in bounded domains
B\subset\mathbb{C}

is a measurable function of the configuration of
H
’s eigenvalues outside of
B

(we point to Definition
3.3
for a precise definition).
To the best of our knowledge, ours is the first work to study the occurrence of
such a phenomenon in the spectrum of random Schrödinger operators acting on discrete spaces.

The spectral theory of differential operators (including non-self-adjoint operators; e.g.,

[
1
,
6
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
18
,
28
,
33
,
35
]
) is among the
most promiment research programs in mathematical physics; see, for instance,

[
27
,
42
]
. In particular, starting from the pioneering
work of Anderson
[
4
]
, the study of Schödinger
operators perturbed by irregular noise has attracted a lot of attention; we refer
to
[
3
,
11
]
for general introductions to the subject. A particularly active program in this direction
is the work on
Anderson localization
, which concerns the appearance of
pure point spectrum and eigenfunction decay; see the survey articles
[
29
,
32
,
41
]

for more details.

In contrast to localization and similar questions, in this paper we investigate the
transport
of spectral information
from one region to another,
whereby observing the configuration of
H
’s eigenvalues in some domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}

allows to recover nontrivial information about the spectrum in
D
’s complement.
Such questions of spatial conditioning in general point processes
have long been of interest due to their
natural applications in mathematics and physics; see, e.g.,
[
5
,
30
]
.
In recent years, there has been a renewed interest in such investigations coming from
the seminal work of Ghosh and Peres
[
26
]
on
rigidity and tolerance
,
culminating in a now active field of research (e.g.,

[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
37
]
;
see also
[
2
]
).
In
[
20
]
, we studied the occurrence of number rigidity in the
spectrum of a class of random Schrödinger operators on one-dimensional continuous space.
In this paper, we study a similar problem for discrete random Schrödinger operators.

1.2.
Outline of Main Results

Let
\mathsf{d}
denote the graph distance on
\mathscr{G}
.
For every
v\in\mathscr{V}
, we use
\mathsf{c}_{n}(v)
,
n\geq 0
, to denote
v
’s coordination sequence
in
\mathscr{G}
; that is, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathsf{c}_{n}(v)
is the number of vertices
u\in\mathscr{V}
such that
\mathsf{d}(u,v)=n
. Stated informally,
our main result is as follows:

Theorem 1.1

(Informal Statement)
.

Suppose that there exists
d\geq 1
such that

(1.1)

\displaystyle\sup_{v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathsf{c}_{n}(v)=O(n^{d-1})\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Under mild technical assumptions on the Markov process
X
and
the noise
\xi
,
there exists a constant
d/2\leq\alpha\leq d
(which, apart from
d
, depends on the
the range of the covariance in
\xi
) such that if
V(v)
grows
faster than
\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{\alpha}
as
\mathsf{d}(0,v)\to\infty
,
then
the eigenvalue point process of
H
is number rigid.

See Theorems
3.16

and
3.17
for a formal statement.
Our technical assumptions are stated in Assumptions
3.8

and
3.12
; roughly speaking,
our assumptions are that

(1)

the jump rates of
X
(which may be site-dependent) are uniformly bounded; and

(2)

the tails of
\xi
are not worse than exponential.

In particular, our assumptions allow for
X
to be non-symmetric
(hence, the operator
H
need not be self-adjoint) and for
\xi

to have a variety of covariance structures, including long-range
dependence.

Remark 1.2
.

The constant
d
in (
1.1
), which quantifies the growth rate of the number of vertices,
can be thought of as the
dimension
of
\mathscr{G}
(or, at least, an upper bound of the dimension).
To illustrate this, if
\mathscr{G}
is for example
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
or a semiregular tessellation of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
then it is easy to see that
cn^{d-1}\leq\mathsf{c}_{n}(v)\leq Cn^{d-1}
for some
C,c>0
. More generally,
the constant
d
is closely related to the
intrinsic dimension
of
\mathscr{G}
, which is the minimal number

k
such that
\mathscr{G}
can be embedded in
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. We refer to, e.g.,
[
34
,
36
]
for more details.

Remark 1.3
.

In Theorem
3.18
, we provide concrete examples showing
that the growth lower bound of
\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{\alpha}
that we impose on
V
to
get rigidity is the best general sufficient condition that can be obtained with our proof method.
The question of whether or not this is actually necessary for rigidity is addressed in
Section
1.4.1
.

1.3.
Proof Strategy and Previous Results

Our method to prove number rigidity follows the general scheme introduced by Ghosh and Peres
in
[
26
]
: Let
\mathcal{X}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\delta_{\lambda_{k}}
be a point process on
\mathbb{C}
.
As per
[
26
, Theorem 6.1]
, for any bounded set
B\subset\mathbb{C}
, if there exists
a sequence of functions
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that, as
n\to\infty
,

(1)

f_{n}\to 1
uniformly on
B
, and

(2)

the variance of the linear statistics

\int f_{n}~\mathrm{d}\mathcal{X}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}f_{n}(\lambda_{k})
vanish,

then
\mathcal{X}(B)
is measurable
with respect to the configuration of
\mathcal{X}
outside of
B
.

One of the main difficulties involved with carrying out the above program lies in
the computation of upper bounds for the variances of linear statistics
\mathbf{Var}[\int f~\mathrm{d}\mathcal{X}]
.
For this reason, much of the previous literature on number rigidity exploits special
properties that make the computations more manageable, such as
determinantal/Pfaffian or other inetegrable structure
[
7
,
10
,
21
,
25
,
26
]
, translation
invariance and hyperuniformity
[
19
,
24
]
, and finite dimensional approximations
[
39
]
.

Among those works, the only result that is related to the spectrum of random Schrödinger operators
is the proof of rigidity of the Airy-2 point process in
[
7
]
. Thanks to the work of Edelman,
Ramírez, Rider, Sutton, and Virág
[
16
,
38
]
, this implies that the spectrum
of the
stochastic Airy operator
with parameter
\beta=2
is number rigid. Given that the
method of proof in
[
7
]
relies crucially on special algebraic structure only present in that
one particular case, however, the result cannot be extended to general Schrödinger operators.

More recently, in
[
20
]
we proposed to study number rigidity in
the spectrum of random Schrödinger operators using a new
semigroup method
: Given that
the exponential functions
\mathrm{e}_{n}(z):=\mathrm{e}^{-z/n}
converge uniformly to 1 on any bounded
set as
n\to\infty
, in order to prove number rigidity of any point process, it suffices to prove
that
\mathbf{Var}[\int\mathrm{e}_{n}~\mathrm{d}\mathcal{X}]\to 0
(though the requirement that
\int\mathrm{e}_{n}~\mathrm{d}\mathcal{X}

is finite imposes strong conditions on
\mathcal{X}
).
If
\mathcal{X}
happens to be
the eigenvalue point process of a random Schrödinger operator
H
, then
\int\mathrm{e}_{n}~\mathrm{d}\mathcal{X}

is the trace of the operator
\mathrm{e}^{-H/n}
. Thus, in order to prove the number rigidity of the
spectrum of any random Schrödinger operator
H
, it suffices to prove that

\lim_{t\to 0}\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[\mathrm{e}^{-tH}]\big]=0.

The reason why this is a particularly attractive strategy to prove number rigidity of general
random Schrödinger operators is that, thanks to the Feynman-Kac formula, there
exists an explicit probabilistic representation of the semigroup
(\mathrm{e}^{-tH})_{t>0}
in terms
of elementary stochastic processes, making the variance
\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[\mathrm{e}^{-tH}]\big]

amenable to computation.

In
[
20
]
, this strategy was used to prove number rigidity
for a class of random Schrödinger operators acting on one-dimensional continuous
space (i.e., an interval of the form
I=(a,b)
with
-\infty\leq a<b\leq\infty
). In this paper,
we apply the same methodology to prove number rigidity for a general class of discrete
random Schrödinger operators.

Despite the fact that the general strategy of proof used in the present paper is the same as

[
20
]
, the differences between the two settings are such that
virtually none of the work carried out in
[
20
]
can be directly
extended to the present paper. For example:

(1)

Since we consider operators acting on general graphs
\mathscr{G}
,
the treatment of the geometry of the space on which our operators are
defined requires a much more careful analysis than that carried out in

[
20
]
. In particular (as per Remark

1.2
), in this paper we uncover
that the dimension of the space plays an important role in the proof
of rigidity using the semigroup method.

(2)

In
[
20
]
, we only consider Schrödinger
operators whose kinetic energy operator is the standard Laplacian and whose noise
is a Gaussian process. As a result, the operators considered therein are all self-adjoint
and upper bounds of
\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[\mathrm{e}^{-tH}]\big]
can mostly be reduced to the analysis
of self-intersection local times of standard Brownian motion.
In contrast, in this paper we allow for much more general generators
H_{X}
and noises
\xi
.
Most notably, the assumptions of this paper allow for non-self-adjoint operators, which
increases the technical difficulties involved (e.g., Sections
5

and
6
).

1.4.
Future Directions

Given that our main theorems apply to a very general class of operators,
the results of this paper provide substantial evidence of the universality
of number rigidity in discrete random Schrödinger operators.
That being said, we feel that our results raise a number of interesting
follow-up questions. We now discuss three such directions.

1.4.1.
New Methods

It is natural to wonder if the growth condition
V(v)\gg\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{\alpha}

that we impose on the potential to get number rigidity is close to optimal. As we
show in Theorem
3.18
, our main result is optimal in the sense that
we can find concrete examples of operators such that

(1.2)

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to 0}\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[\mathrm{e}^{-tH}]\big]>0

when
V(v)\asymp\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{\alpha}
. That being said, the vanishing of the variance of the
trace of the semigroup is only a sufficient condition for number rigidity, and, in fact,
it was observed in
[
20
, Proposition 2.27]
that there
exists at least one random Schrödinger operator whose spectrum is known to
be number rigid and such that (
1.2
)
holds. For example, the following simple question appears to be outside the scope
of the methods used in this paper:

Problem 1.4
.

Suppose that
X
is the simple symmetric random walk on
\mathscr{G}=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
that
V(v)=\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{\delta}
for some
\delta>0
, and that
\big(\xi(v)\big)_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}

are i.i.d. standard Gaussians (or any other simple distribution). Is
H
’s spectrum always number rigid in this case?

More specifically, given that
\mathsf{c}_{n}(v)\asymp n^{d-1}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
our main theorem only implies number rigidity in the above when
\delta>d/2
.
We expect that solving Problem
1.4

will require developing new methods to study number rigidity in random
Schrödinger operators.

1.4.2.
The Mechanism of Rigidity

Our main result implies that for every bounded measurable set
B\subset\mathbb{C}
,
there exists a deterministic function
\mathcal{N}_{B}
such that
the identity

\text{number of $H$'s eigenvalues in $B$}=\mathcal{N}_{B}(\text{configuration of $H$'s eigenvalues outside $B$})

holds with probability one. That being said, the argument that we use to prove the
existence of
\mathcal{N}_{B}

gives little information on its exact form. In other words, the precise nature
of the mechanism that makes the number of eigenvalues in
B
a deterministic function of the configuration
on the outside remains largely unknown. In light of this, an interesting future direction for investigation would be
along the following lines:

Problem 1.5
.

Let
B\subset\mathbb{C}
be a “simple” bounded subset of the complex plane
(e.g., a closed or open ball).
Does
\mathcal{N}_{B}
admit an explicit representation?

We point to Remark
6.4
for more details on the construction of
\mathcal{N}_{B}
.

1.4.3.
Spatial Conditioning Beyond Number Rigidity

When
H
’s spectrum is number rigid, we know that if we condition

H
on having a specific eigenvalue configuration outside of a bounded set

B
, then
H
’s spectrum inside of
B
is a point process with a fixed total number
of points. It would be interesting to see if more can be learned about
the conditional distribution of the eigenvalues in
B
. For instance, the
following problem (related to the notion of tolerance introduced in

[
26
]
) might be a good starting point:

Problem 1.6
.

Suppose that, after conditioning on the outside configuration,

H
has
M\in\mathbb{N}
random eigenvalues in some bounded
set
B\subset\mathbb{C}
. Let
\Lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
be the random
vector whose components are the random eigenvalues of
H
in
B

(conditional on the configuration outside
B
),
taken in a uniformly random order. What is the support of
\Lambda
’s
probability distribution on the set
B^{M}
?

2.
Proof Outline

In this section, we present a sketch of the proof of
our main theorem in two simple special cases.
We take this opportunity to explain how our technical
assumptions arise in our computations.
For simplicity of exposition, we assume in this outline that
\mathscr{G}
is the integer lattice

\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(i.e.,
(u,v)\in\mathscr{E}
if and only if
\|u-v\|_{\infty}=1
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the
usual
\ell^{\infty}
norm),
X
is the simple
symmetric random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and
\xi
is a centered stationary Gaussian process
with covariance function

\gamma(v):=\mathbf{E}[\xi(v)\xi(0)],\qquad v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

As alluded to in the introduction (and proved in Section
6
),
to prove that the eigenvalue point process of
H
is number rigid, it suffices to show that

\mathrm{Tr}[\mathrm{e}^{-tH}]
’s variance vanishes as
t\to 0
.
According to the Feynman-Kac formula, we have that

\mathrm{Tr}[\mathrm{e}^{-tH}]=\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathbf{E}_{X}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}V\big(X(s)\big)+\xi\big(X(s)\big)~\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathbf{1}_{\{X(t)=X(0)\}}\bigg|X(0)=v\right],

where
\mathbf{E}_{X}
means that we are only averaging with respect to the randomness in the path of
X
,
and we assume that
X
is independent of the noise
\xi
.
In order to ensure that
\mathrm{e}^{-tH}
is trace class (or even bounded) in the general case,
we assume that
\mathscr{G}
has uniformly bounded degrees; see Section
6.1

for more details.

Our first step in the analysis of
\mathrm{Tr}[\mathrm{e}^{-tH}]
is to note that if

t
is small, then the probability that there exists some
0\leq s\leq t
such that
X(s)\neq X(0)

is close to zero (i.e.,
1-\mathrm{e}^{-t}\sim t
). Thus, by working only with the complement of this event,
we have that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[\mathrm{e}^{-tH}]\approx\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-tV(v)-t\xi(v)}.

A rigorous version of this heuristic is carried out in
the proof of Lemma
4.6
.
The latter relies on controlling how far
X
can travel from its initial value
X(0)
after a small time
(e.g., the tail bound (
4.21
)), which itself depends on the
assumptions that the jump rates of
X
are uniformly bounded.

Our second step is to identify the leading order asymptotics in the variance
of the expression on the right-hand side of (
2.1
).
In the special case where
\xi
is a stationary Gaussian process with covariance
\gamma
,
an application of Tonelli’s theorem yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{Var}\left[\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-tV(v)-t\xi(v)}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-tV(u)-tV(v)}\mathbf{Cov}[\mathrm{e}^{-t\xi(u)},\mathrm{e}^{-t\xi(v)}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-tV(u)-tV(v)}\mathrm{e}^{t^{2}\gamma(0)}\left(\mathrm{e}^{t^{2}\gamma(u-v)}-1\right)

(2.2)

\displaystyle\approx t^{2}\,\sum_{u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-tV(u)-tV(v)}\gamma(u-v),

where the last line follows from a Taylor expansion.
A bound of this type can be achieved in the general case thanks to our assumption that

\xi
’s tails are not worse than exponential. We refer to Proposition
4.2

for the general form of the variance formula. See Lemmas

4.3
and
4.4

for quantitative bounds on the vanishing of the covariance of the exponential random field
\mathrm{e}^{-t\xi}

as
t\to 0
in terms of the strength of
\xi
’s covariance.

Our third and final step is to identify conditions such that the quantity

(2.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-tV(u)-tV(v)}\gamma(u-v)

does not blow up at a faster rate than
t^{-2}
as
t\to 0
. As advertised
in our informal statement, this depends on the growth rate of the potential
V

and the decay rate (if any) of the covariance
\gamma
at infinity. To give an
illustration of how this is carried out in this paper, we consider the two simplest
(and most extreme) cases of covariance structure:

(1)

\big(\xi(v)\big)_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
are i.i.d., i.e.,
\gamma(v)=0
whenever
v\neq 0
; and

(2)

\big(\xi(v)\big)_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
are all equal to each other, i.e.,
\gamma(v)=\gamma(0)
for all
v\in\mathscr{V}
.

The quantity (
2.3
) then becomes

\sum_{u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-tV(u)-tV(v)}\gamma(u-v)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\gamma(0)\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-2tV(v)}&\text{i.i.d. case,}\\
\displaystyle\gamma(0)\left(\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-tV(v)}\right)^{2}&\text{all equal case.}\end{cases}

If we assume that
V(v)\gg\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
,
then for any
\theta>0
we have that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta tV(v)}\ll\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta t\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{\alpha}}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}\mathsf{c}_{n}(0)\mathrm{e}^{-\theta tn^{\alpha}},

where we recall that
\mathsf{c}_{n}(0)
denotes for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
the number of vertices in
\mathscr{G}
such that
\mathsf{d}(0,v)=n
.
For the
d
-dimensional integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, it is easy to check that there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\mathsf{c}_{n}(0)\leq Cn^{d-1}

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, whence (
2.4
) yields

(2.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta tV(v)}\ll\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}n^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta tn^{\alpha}}\approx\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta tx^{\alpha}}~\mathrm{d}x=O(t^{-d/\alpha}).

Summarizing our argument so far in (
2.1
)–(
2.5
),
we are led to the
t\to 0
asymptotic

\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[\mathrm{e}^{-tH}]\big]\ll\begin{cases}t^{2-d/\alpha}&\text{i.i.d. case,}\\
t^{2-2d/\alpha}&\text{all equal case.}\end{cases}

Thus, the eigenvalue point process of
H
is proved to be number rigid if
V(v)\gg\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{d/2}
in the i.i.d case
and
V(v)\gg\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{d}
in the all equal case. If
\gamma
has a less extreme decay rate
(such as
\gamma(v)=O(\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{-\beta})
as
\mathsf{d}(0,v)\to\infty
for some
\beta>0
), then the eigenvalue point process of
H
is number rigid if
V(v)\gg\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{\alpha}
for some
d/2\leq\alpha\leq d
, where the exact value
of
\alpha
depends on
\gamma
’s decay rate. We refer to Theorems
3.16
and
3.17

for the details.

3.
Main Results

3.1.
Basic Definitions and Notations

We begin by introducing basic/standard notations
that will be used throughout the paper.

Notation 3.1

(Function Spaces)
.

We use
\ell^{p}(\mathscr{V})
to denote the space of real-valued
absolutely
p
-summable (or bounded if
p=\infty
) functions on
\mathscr{V}
; we
denote the associated norm by
\|\cdot\|_{p}
.
We use
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
to denote the inner
product on
\ell^{2}(\mathscr{V})
.
Given a subset
\mathscr{U}\subset\mathscr{V}
, we denote

\ell^{p}_{\mathscr{U}}(\mathscr{V}):=\{f\in\ell^{p}(\mathscr{V}):f(u)=0\text{ for every }u\in\mathscr{U}\}.

Notation 3.2

(Operator Theory)
.

Given a linear operator
T
on
\ell^{2}_{\mathscr{U}}(\mathscr{V})
(or a dense domain
D(T)\subset\ell^{2}_{\mathscr{U}}(\mathscr{V})
), we use
\sigma(T)
to denote its spectrum,
and
\sigma_{p}(T)\subset\sigma(T)
to denote its point spectrum. If
T
is bounded, we denote its operator norm by

\|T\|_{\mathrm{op}}:=\sup_{f\in\ell^{2}_{\mathscr{U}}(\mathscr{V}),~\|f\|_{2}=1}\|Tf\|_{2}.

We use
\mathfrak{R}(z,T):=(T-z)^{-1}

to denote the resolvent of
T
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(T)
.
If
\lambda
is an isolated eigenvalue of
T
, then we let

m_{a}(\lambda,T):=\dim\left(\mathrm{rg}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma_{\lambda}}\mathfrak{R}(z,T)~\mathrm{d}z\right)\right)

denote the algebraic multiplicity of
\lambda
, where
\dim
denotes the dimension of a linear space,

\mathrm{rg}
denotes the range of an operator, and

\Gamma_{\lambda}
denotes a Jordan curve that encloses
\lambda
and excludes the remainder
of the spectrum of
T
.

Definition 3.3

(Rigidity)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\delta_{\lambda_{k}}
be an infinite point process on
\mathbb{C}
.
We say that
\mathcal{X}
is real-bounded below by a random variable
\omega\in\mathbb{R}

if
\Re(\lambda_{k})\geq\omega
almost surely for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
We say that such a point process is number rigid if for every Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{C}

such that
B\subset(-\infty,\delta]+\mathrm{i}[-\tilde{\delta},\tilde{\delta}]

for some
\delta,\tilde{\delta}>0
, the random variable
\mathcal{X}(B)
is measurable with
respect to the completion (under the law of the point process
\mathcal{X}
) of the sigma algebra generated by the set

\big\{\mathcal{X}(A):A\subset\mathbb{C}\text{ is Borel and }B\cap A=\varnothing\big\}.

Remark 3.4
.

In previous works in the literature, it is most common to define number rigidity
as the requirement that
\mathcal{X}(B)
is measurable with respect to the configuration in
\mathbb{C}\setminus B

for every bounded Borel set
B
. This is in part due to the fact that most point processes
that have been proved to be number rigid thus far are such that
\mathcal{X}(B)=\infty
almost surely
whenever
B
is unbounded.

That being said, the fact that we are considering the spectrum of Schrödinger
operators whose potentials have a strong growth at infinity means that we are considering
eigenvalue point processes that are real-bounded below, in which case a more general
notion of number rigidity makes sense. We note that a similarly generalized notion of rigidity appeared
in the work of Bufetov on the stochastic Airy operator in
[
7
, Proposition 3.2]
.

3.2.
Markov Process

Next, we introduce the Markov processes on the graph

\mathscr{G}
that generate
our random operators, as well as some of the notions we
need to describe them. We recall that
\mathscr{G}=(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{E})
is a countably infinite
connected graph with uniformly bounded degrees and a root
0\in\mathscr{V}
.

Definition 3.5

(Markov Process)
.

Let
\Pi:\mathscr{V}\times\mathscr{V}\to[0,1]
be a matrix such that

(1)

\Pi
is stochastic, that is, for every
u\in\mathscr{V}
,

\sum_{v\in\mathscr{V}}\Pi(u,v)=1;

(2)

\Pi(v,v)=0
for all
v\in\mathscr{V}
; and

(3)

If
(u,v)\not\in\mathscr{E}
, then
\Pi(u,v)=\Pi(v,u)=0
.

Let
q:\mathscr{V}\to(0,\infty)
be a positive vector and let

X:[0,\infty)\to\mathscr{V}
denote the continuous-time Markov process on
\mathscr{V}

defined as follows. If
X
is in
state
u\in\mathscr{V}
, it waits for a random time with
an exponential distribution with rate
q(u)
, and then jumps
to another state
v\neq u
with probability
\Pi(u,v)
, independently of the wait time.
Once at the new state,
X
repeats this procedure independently of all previous jumps.

Remark 3.6
.

We note that condition (3) in the above definition implies that

X
is a Markov process on the graph
\mathscr{G}
, in the sense that
jumps can only occur between vertices that are connected by edges.

Notation 3.7
.

For every
v\in\mathscr{V}
, we use

X^{v}
to denote the process
X

conditioned on the starting point
X(0)=v
.
We use
\mathbf{P}^{v}
to denote the
law of
X^{v}
, and
\mathbf{E}^{v}
to denote expectation
with respect to
\mathbf{P}^{v}
.

We assume throughout that the Markov process
X

and the graph
\mathscr{G}
satisfy the following.

Assumption 3.8

(Graph Geometry and Jump Rates)
.

The following two conditions hold:

(1)

There exists constants
d\geq 1
and
\mathfrak{c}>0
such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\sup_{v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathsf{c}_{n}(v):=\sup_{v\in\mathscr{V}}|\{u\in\mathscr{V}:\mathsf{d}(u,v)=n\}|\leq\mathfrak{c}\,n^{d-1}\qquad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\},

recalling that
\mathsf{d}
is the graph distance in
\mathscr{G}
, that is,
\mathsf{d}(u,v)

is the length of the shortest path (in terms of number of edges) connecting

u
and
v
, and with the convention that
\mathsf{d}(v,v)=0
for all
v\in\mathscr{V}
.

(2)

X
has uniformly bounded jump rates, that is,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}:=\sup_{v\in\mathscr{V}}q(v)<\infty.

Remark 3.9
.

We note that the assumption (
3.1
)
simultaneously takes care of the requirement that
\mathscr{G}
has uniformly
bounded degrees (since
\mathsf{c}_{1}(v)=\deg(v)
) and of the asymptotic
growth rate (
1.1
) stated in our informal theorem.

3.3.
Feynman-Kac Kernel

We are now in a position to introduce the central objects of study of this paper,
namely, the Feynman-Kac semigroups of the Schrödinger operators we
are interested in.

Notation 3.10

(Local Time)
.

For every
t\geq 0
, we let
L_{t}:\mathscr{V}\to[0,t]
denote the local time of
X
:

L_{t}(v):=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{X(s)=v\}}~\mathrm{d}s,\qquad v\in\mathscr{V}.

Definition 3.11

(Potential and Noise)
.

Let
V:\mathscr{V}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a deterministic function,
and let
\xi:\mathscr{V}\to\mathbb{R}
be a random function.
We denote the set

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathscr{Z}:=\{v\in\mathscr{V}:V(v)=\infty\}.

Throughout, we make the following assumptions on the noise and potential.

Assumption 3.12

(Potential Growth and Noise Tails)
.

There exists
\alpha>0
such that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\liminf_{\mathsf{d}(0,v)\to\infty}\frac{V(v)}{\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{\alpha}}=\infty.

Moreover,
\xi
satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

\mathbf{E}[\xi(v)]=0
for every
v\in\mathscr{V}
.

(2)

There exists
\mathfrak{m}>0
such that
for every
p\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\sup_{v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}[|\xi(v)|^{p}]\leq p!\mathfrak{m}^{p}.

In the sequel, it will be useful to characterize noises in terms
of the decay rate of their covariances. For this purpose, we make
the following definition.

Definition 3.13

(covariance decay)
.

We say that
\xi
has covariance decay of order (at least)
\beta>0
if there
exists a constant
\mathfrak{C}>0
such that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\big|\mathbf{E}[\xi(u)\xi(v)]\big|\leq\mathfrak{C}\,\big(\mathsf{d}(u,v)+1\big)^{-\beta}

for every
u,v\in\mathscr{V}
, and such that

(3.6)

\displaystyle\big|\mathbf{E}[\xi(u)\xi(v)\xi(w)]\big|\leq\mathfrak{C}\,\min_{a,b\in\{u,v,w\}}\big(\mathsf{d}(a,b)+1\big)^{-\beta}

for every
u,v,w\in\mathscr{V}
.

Definition 3.14

(Feynman-Kac Kernel)
.

Define the Feynman-Kac kernel

(3.7)

\displaystyle K_{t}(u,v):=\mathbf{E}^{u}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L_{t},V+\xi\rangle}\mathbf{1}_{\{X(t)=v\}}\right],\qquad u,v\in\mathscr{V},

where we assume that
X
is independent of
\xi
,
and that
\mathbf{E}^{v}
denotes the expectation with respect to
the Markov process
X^{v}
, conditional on
\xi
.
We denote the trace of
K_{t}
as

\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]:=\sum_{v\in\mathscr{V}}K_{t}(v,v).

Remark 3.15
.

In the above definition,
we use the convention that
\mathrm{e}^{-\infty}:=0
whenever
V(v)=\infty
,
in particular,
K_{t}(u,v)=0
whenever
u\in\mathscr{Z}
or
v\in\mathscr{Z}
.

3.4.
Main Results: Variance Upper Bound and Rigidity

We now state our main results. First, we have the following sufficient condition
for the vanishing of the variance of the trace of
K_{t}
as
t\to 0
:

Theorem 3.16
.

Suppose that Assumptions
3.8
and
3.12
hold.
In order to have

\lim_{t\to 0}\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]=0,

it is sufficient that the constant
\alpha
in (
3.3
)
satisfies the following:

(1)

if
\xi
has covariance decay of order
\beta>0
, then

(3.8)

\displaystyle\alpha\begin{cases}\geq d/2&\text{when }\beta>d,\\
>d/2&\text{when }\beta=d,\\
\geq d-\beta/2&\text{when }\beta<d;\end{cases}

(2)

otherwise,
\alpha\geq d
.

As a consequence of the above theorem, we have the following result,
which states some properties of the infinitesimal generator of
K_{t}
, including number rigidity.

Theorem 3.17
.

Suppose that Assumptions
3.8
and
3.12
hold,
and that we take the constant
\alpha
in (
3.3
) as in Theorem
3.16
.
The following conditions hold almost surely.

(1)

For every
t>0
,
K_{t}
is a trace class linear operator
on
\ell^{2}_{\mathscr{Z}}(\mathscr{V})
. There exists a random variable
\omega\leq 0

such that
\|K_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\omega t}
for all
t>0
.

(2)

The family of operators

(K_{t})_{t>0}
is a strongly continuous semigroup
on
\ell^{2}_{\mathscr{Z}}(\mathscr{V})
.

(3)

The infinitesimal generator

(3.9)

\displaystyle H:=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{K_{0}-K_{t}}{t}

is closed on some dense domain
D(H)\subset\ell^{2}_{\mathscr{Z}}(\mathscr{V})
,
and its action on functions is given by the following matrix:

(3.10)

\displaystyle H(u,v):=\begin{cases}-q(u)\Pi(u,v)&\text{if }u\neq v\text{ and }u,v\not\in\mathscr{Z},\\
q(u)+V(u)+\xi(u)&\text{if }u=v\text{ and }u\not\in\mathscr{Z},\\
0&\text{if }u\in\mathscr{Z}\text{ or }v\in\mathscr{Z}.\end{cases}

(So, if
f\in D(H)
, then
f(v)=0
for every
v\in\mathscr{Z}
.)

In particular, almost surely,

H
has a pure point spectrum without accumulation point,
and the eigenvalue point process (counting algebraic multiplicities)

(3.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{H}:=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(H)}m_{a}(\lambda,H)\,\delta_{\lambda}

is real-bounded below by
\omega

and number rigid in the sense of Definition
3.3
.

3.5.
Questions of Optimality

In this section, we study the optimality of the growth assumptions
we make on
V
in Theorem
3.16
by considering three
counterexamples.

Theorem 3.18
.

Suppose that
X
is the nearest-neighbor symmetric random walk on the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
that
V(v):=\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{\delta}
for some
\delta>0
, and that
\xi
is a centered stationary Gaussian
process whose covariance function
\gamma(v):=\mathbf{E}[\xi(v)\xi(0)]
is nonnegative.
If one of the following conditions hold:

(1)

\delta\leq d/2
and
\gamma(v)=\mathbf{1}_{\{v=0\}}
;

(2)

\delta\leq d-\beta/2
for some
0<\beta<d
, and there exists a constant
\mathfrak{L}>0

such that
\gamma(v)\geq\mathfrak{L}\big(\mathsf{d}(0,v)+1\big)^{-\beta}
for every
v\in\mathscr{V}
; or

(3)

\delta\leq d
and
\inf_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\gamma(v)>\mathfrak{L}
for some constant
\mathfrak{L}>0
;

then we have the variance lower bound

\liminf_{t\to 0}\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]>0.

Thus, given that
\mathsf{c}_{n}(v)\asymp n^{d-1}
as
n\to\infty
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
if one is interested in providing a general sufficient condition for number rigidity on graphs
using semigroups, then Theorem
3.16
is essentially the optimal result
one could hope for.

Remark 3.19
.

An examination of the proof of Theorem
3.18

reveals that similar lower bounds can be proved for more general
examples with little effort; we restrict our attention to this elementary setting for
simplicity of exposition.

4.
Proof of Theorem
3.16

Throughout this section, we suppose that Assumptions

3.8
and
3.12
hold.
This section is organized as follows: In Section
4.1
, we outline the
main steps of the proof of Theorem
3.16
.
That is, we state a number of technical propositions and lemmas,
which we then use to prove Theorem
3.16
. Then, in Sections

4.2
–
4.6
, we prove the technical results stated
Section
4.1
, thus wrapping-up the proof
of Theorem
3.16
.

4.1.
Proof Outline

4.1.1.
Step 1. Variance Formula and First Bound

We begin with some notations.

Notation 4.1
.

Let us denote by
(\Omega_{\xi},\mathbf{P}_{\xi})
the probability space on which
\xi

is defined. Let
Y
be any random element that is independent of
\xi
,
and let
F
be any measurable function. We denote the random variable

\mathbf{E}_{\xi}\big[F(\xi,Y)\big]:=\int_{\Omega_{\xi}}F(x,Y)~\mathrm{d}\mathbf{P}_{\xi}(x);

that is,
\mathbf{E}_{\xi}
is the conditional expectation with respect to

\xi
, given
Y
. Then, for measurable functions
F
and
G
,
we denote the random variable

\mathbf{Cov}_{\xi}\big[F(\xi,Y),G(\xi,Y)\big]:=\mathbf{E}_{\xi}\big[F(\xi,Y)G(\xi,Y)\big]-\mathbf{E}_{\xi}\big[F(\xi,Y)\big]\mathbf{E}_{\xi}\big[G(\xi,Y)\big].

Our main tool in the proof of Theorem
3.16
is the following variance formula:

Proposition 4.2
.

For every
u,v\in\mathscr{V}
,
we let
X^{u}
and
\tilde{X}^{v}
be independent
copies of the Markov process
X
started from
u
and
v
respectively.
We assume that
X^{u}
and
\tilde{X}^{v}
are independent of the
noise
\xi
, and
we denote their local times as

L^{u}_{t}(w):=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{u}(s)=w\}}~\mathrm{d}s\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{L}^{v}_{t}(w):=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tilde{X}^{v}(s)=w\}}~\mathrm{d}s

for all
w\in\mathscr{V}
. It holds that

\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]=\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\mathbf{Cov}_{\xi}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L^{u}_{t},\xi\rangle},\mathrm{e}^{-\langle\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},\xi\rangle}\right]\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{u}(t)=u,\tilde{X}^{v}(t)=v\}}\right].

The proof of this proposition, which we provide in Section
4.2
below,
is essentially a direct consequence of the definition of
K_{t}
in (
3.7
).
In order to find sufficient conditions for
\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]\to 0
as
t\to 0
using this formula, it is
convenient to control the contributions coming from
V
and
\xi
separately. To this end,
we use Hölder’s inequality, as well as the elementary fact that
\mathbf{1}_{E}\leq 1
for every event
E
,
which yields

\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\mathbf{Cov}_{\xi}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L^{u}_{t},\xi\rangle},\mathrm{e}^{-\langle\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},\xi\rangle}\big]\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{u}(t)=u,\tilde{X}^{v}(t)=v\}}\Big]\\
\leq\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{Cov}_{\xi}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L^{u}_{t},\xi\rangle},\mathrm{e}^{-\langle\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},\xi\rangle}\Big]^{2}\Big]^{1/2}

for every fixed
u,v\in\mathscr{V}
.
Then, by summing both sides of the above inequality over
u,v\in\mathscr{V}
,
we obtain our first upper bound for the variance:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]\leq\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{Cov}_{\xi}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L^{u}_{t},\xi\rangle},\mathrm{e}^{-\langle\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},\xi\rangle}\Big]^{2}\Big]^{1/2}.

4.1.2.
Step 2. Controlling the Contributions from
\xi
and
V

We now state the technical results that we use to control the right-hand side of (
4.1
).
Our first such result is as follows:

Lemma 4.3
.

Recall the definition of the constant
\mathfrak{m}>0
in (
3.4
).
There exists a constant
C_{1}>0
(which only depends on
\mathfrak{m}
) such that
for every
t<1/C_{1}
, one has

\displaystyle\sup_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{Cov}_{\xi}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L^{u}_{t},\xi\rangle},\mathrm{e}^{-\langle\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},\xi\rangle}\big]^{2}\Big]^{1/2}\leq C_{1}t^{2}.

The proof of Lemma
4.3
, which we provide in Section
4.4
,
follows from estimating expectations of the form
\mathbf{E}^{v}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\langle L_{t},\xi\rangle}\big]

using our assumption that
\xi
’s tails are not worse than exponential (i.e., (
3.4
)).
Next, we have the following result, which provides a tighter decay rate
in the case where
\xi
has covariance decay:

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose that
\xi
has covariance decay of order
\beta
,
as per Definition
3.13
.
Recall the definitions of the constants
\mathfrak{q}
,
\mathfrak{m}
, and
\mathfrak{C}

in Assumption
3.8
(3), (
3.4
),
(
3.5
), and (
3.6
).
There exists a constant
C_{2}>0

(which only depends on
\mathfrak{q}
,
\mathfrak{m}
,
\mathfrak{C}
, and
\beta
)
such that for every
t<1/C_{2}
and
u,v\in\mathscr{V}
, one has

\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{Cov}_{\xi}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L^{u}_{t},\xi\rangle},\mathrm{e}^{-\langle\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},\xi\rangle}\right]^{2}\right]^{1/2}\leq C_{2}\left(t^{2}\big(\mathsf{d}(u,v)+1\big)^{-\beta}+t^{4}\right).

Lemma
4.4
is proved in Section
4.5
.
The proof of this lemma is rather more subtle than that of Lemma
4.3
,
and depends on a careful control of how much
X^{u}
and
\tilde{X}^{v}
deviate from their
respective starting points
u
and
v
. We note that the uniform upper bound on
the jump rates of
X
in Assumption
3.8
(3) is crucial for this lemma.

Remark 4.5
.

The proofs of Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4
both rely on some elementary formulas and estimates of the moment generating functions of the noises and their covariances, which will be stated and proved in Section
4.3
.

With Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4

in hand, it now only remains to control the contribution of the potential
V
in (
4.1
).
For this, we have the following result:

Lemma 4.6
.

Recall the definition of
d\geq 1
and
\mathfrak{c}>0
in (
3.1
).
Suppose that we can find some constants
\kappa,\mu>0
such that

(4.2)

\displaystyle V(v)\geq\big(\kappa\,\mathsf{d}(0,v)\big)^{\alpha}-\mu,\qquad v\in\mathscr{V}.

Then, there exists a constant
C_{3}>0

(which only depends on
\alpha
,
\beta
,
d
, and
\mathfrak{c}
)
such that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to 0}t^{2d/\alpha}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}\leq C_{3}\kappa^{-2d};

(4.4)

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to 0}t^{(2d-\beta)/\alpha}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}\big(\mathsf{d}(u,v)+1\big)^{-\beta}\leq C_{3}\kappa^{-2d+\beta}

for every
0<\beta<d
; and

(4.5)

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to 0}t^{d/\alpha}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}\big(\mathsf{d}(u,v)+1\big)^{-\beta}\leq C_{3}\kappa^{-d}

for every
\beta>d
.

Lemma
4.6
, which is proved in Section
4.6
,
follows the strategy outlined in (
2.4
) and (
2.5
):
The first step of the proof of Lemma
4.6
relies on a rigorous
implementation of the intuition that, for very small
t>0
, one expects that

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\big]^{1/2}\approx\mathrm{e}^{-tV(u)-tV(v)}.

This once again relies on controlling how much
X^{u}
and
\tilde{X}^{v}
deviate from their starting points.
Once a quantitative version of (
4.6
) is established,
we can then use (
4.2
), which allows to control

\mathbf{E}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\big]^{1/2}

in terms of quantities that only depend on the geometry of
\mathscr{G}
(more precisely, the graph distance).
We then wrap up the proof of the lemma by using the upper bound on the coordination sequences in
(
3.1
), in similar fashion to (
2.5
).

4.1.3.
Step 3. Conclusion of Proof

We now combine the technical results stated above to conclude the proof of Theorem

3.16
.
By applying Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4

to our upper bound (
4.1
), we get that for every
t<1/C_{1}
, one has

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]\leq C_{1}t^{2}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2},

and if
\xi
has covariance decay of order
\beta>0
, then for every
t<1/C_{2}
, one has

(4.8)

\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]\leq C_{2}t^{2}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}\big(\mathsf{d}(u,v)+1\big)^{-\beta}\\
+C_{2}t^{4}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}.

Thanks to our growth assumption in (
3.3
), for any choice of
\kappa>0
, we know that there exists
a large enough
\mu>0
so that (
4.2
) holds. We may then complete the proof
of Theorem
3.16
by an application of Lemma
4.6
. We do this on a case-by-case
basis:

Suppose first that
\xi
has covariance decay of order
0<\beta<d
and that
\alpha\geq d-\beta/2>d/2
. Then,
the fact that
2-(2d-\beta)/\alpha\geq 0
implies by (
4.4
) that

\limsup_{t\to 0}t^{2}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}\big(\mathsf{d}(u,v)+1\big)^{-\beta}\\
=\limsup_{t\to 0}t^{2-(2d-\beta)/\alpha}t^{(2d-\beta)/\alpha}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}\big(\mathsf{d}(u,v)+1\big)^{-\beta}\leq C_{3}\kappa^{-2d+\beta};

and the fact that
4-2d/\alpha>0
implies by (
4.3
) that

(4.9)

\limsup_{t\to 0}t^{4}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}\\
=\limsup_{t\to 0}t^{4-2d/\alpha}t^{2d/\alpha}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}=0.

Combining this with (
4.8
) implies that

\limsup_{t\to 0}\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]\leq C_{2}C_{3}\kappa^{-2d+\beta},

where we recall that
C_{2},C_{3}>0
do not depend on
\kappa
or
\mu
. Since
(
4.2
) holds for any choice of
\kappa>0
, we can take

\kappa\to\infty
, which then yields
\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]\to 0
as
t\to 0
.

Next, suppose that
\xi
has covariance decay of order
\beta=d
and that
\alpha>d/2
.
We note that this implies that
\xi
also has correlation
decay of order
\tilde{\beta}
for any choice of
0<\tilde{\beta}<d
.
Since
\alpha>d/2
implies that
2d-2\alpha<d
, we can choose
\tilde{\beta}
close enough to

d
so that

2d-2\alpha<\tilde{\beta}
, which we can rearrange into
2>(2d-\tilde{\beta})/\alpha
.
Thus, (
4.4
) implies that

\limsup_{t\to 0}t^{2}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}\big(\mathsf{d}(u,v)+1\big)^{-\beta}\\
=\limsup_{t\to 0}t^{2-(2d-\tilde{\beta})/\alpha}t^{(2d-\tilde{\beta})/\alpha}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}\big(\mathsf{d}(u,v)+1\big)^{-\tilde{\beta}}=0.

Combining this with (
4.9
), we directly prove
that
\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]\to 0
as
t\to 0
in this case.

Suppose now that
\xi
has covariance decay of order
\beta>d
and that
\alpha\geq d/2
.
Then, the fact that
2-d/\alpha\geq 0
implies by (
4.5
) that

\limsup_{t\to 0}t^{2}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}\big(\mathsf{d}(u,v)+1\big)^{-\beta}\\
=\limsup_{t\to 0}t^{2-d/\alpha}t^{d/\alpha}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}\big(\mathsf{d}(u,v)+1\big)^{-\beta}\leq C_{3}\kappa^{-d};

and the fact that
4-2d/\alpha\geq 0
implies by (
4.3
) that

\limsup_{t\to 0}t^{4}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}\\
=\limsup_{t\to 0}t^{4-2d/\alpha}t^{2d/\alpha}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}\leq C_{3}\kappa^{-2d}.

Combining this with (
4.8
) and taking
\kappa\to\infty
then implies that

\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]\to 0
as
t\to 0
.

Finally, consider the general case where we simply assume that
\alpha\geq d
.
Then,
2-2d/\alpha\geq 0
, and thus (
4.3
) implies that

\limsup_{t\to 0}t^{2}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}\\
=\limsup_{t\to 0}t^{2-2d/\alpha}t^{2d/\alpha}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\Big]^{1/2}\leq C_{3}\kappa^{-2d}.

Since the constants
C_{1},C_{3}>0
are independent of
\kappa
and
\mu
, combining this with (
4.7
)
and taking
\kappa\to\infty
then implies that
\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]\to 0
as
t\to 0
in this case.
This then completes the proof of Theorem
3.16
.

4.2.
Proof of Proposition
4.2

Since the random walk
X
is assumed independent of
\xi
, by applying Fubini’s theorem
to the definition of
K_{t}
in (
3.7
),
we have that

\mathbf{E}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]=\sum_{v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}^{v}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L_{t},V\rangle}\mathbf{E}_{\xi}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L_{t},\xi\rangle}\right]\mathbf{1}_{\{X(t)=v\}}\right],

where we recall the definition of
\mathbf{E}_{\xi}
in Notation
4.1
.
Taking the square of this expression, we then get once again by Fubini’s theorem that

\mathbf{E}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]^{2}=\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\mathbf{E}_{\xi}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L^{u}_{t},\xi\rangle}\right]\mathbf{E}_{\xi}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},\xi\rangle}\right]\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{u}(t)=u,\tilde{X}^{v}(t)=v\}}\right].

Thanks to (
3.7
), it is easy to check that

\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]^{2}=\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}_{\xi}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V+\xi\rangle}\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{u}(t)=u,\tilde{X}^{v}(t)=v\}}\right].

Taking the expectation of this expression using Fubini’s theorem then leads to

\mathbf{E}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]^{2}\big]=\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\mathbf{E}_{\xi}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},\xi\rangle}\right]\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{u}(t)=u,\tilde{X}^{v}(t)=v\}}\right].

The proof of Proposition
4.2
is then simply a matter of subtracting

\mathbf{E}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]^{2}
from the above expression for
\mathbf{E}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]^{2}\big]
,
and using the definition of
\mathbf{Cov}_{\xi}
in Notation
4.1
.

4.3.
Auxiliary results on estimates of moment generating functions

Before discussing the proofs of Lemma
4.3
and Lemma
4.4
in the next two subsections, we list here two simple propositions concerning the tail behaviors of the moment generating functions of the noises and their covariances. The first result is a straightforward consequence of Taylor expansions and Assumption
3.12
on the tails of the noises.

Proposition 4.7
.

Under Assumption
3.12
,
for every finitely-supported deterministic functions
f,g:\mathscr{V}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\|f+g\|_{1},\|f\|_{1},\|g\|_{1}\leq 1/2\mathfrak{m}
,
it holds that

(4.10)

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbf{E}\big[\mathrm{e}^{\langle f,\xi\rangle}\big]-1\Big|\leq 2\mathfrak{m}^{2}\|f\|_{1}^{2}

and

(4.11)

\displaystyle\big|\mathbf{Cov}\big[\mathrm{e}^{\langle f,\xi\rangle},\mathrm{e}^{\langle g,\xi\rangle}\big]\Big|\leq 2\mathfrak{m}^{2}\big(\|f+g\|_{1}^{2}+\|f\|_{1}^{2}+\|g\|^{2}_{1}\big)+4\mathfrak{m}^{4}\|f\|_{1}^{2}\|g\|_{1}^{2}.

For every deterministic function
f:\mathscr{V}\to\mathbb{R}
, it follows from
a straightforward Taylor expansion of the exponential that

(4.12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\langle f,\xi\rangle}\right]=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p!}\sum_{v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}[\xi(v_{1})\cdots\xi(v_{p})]f(v_{1})\cdots f(v_{p}),

with the convention that the term with
p=0
above is equal to one.
Firstly, since
\mathbf{E}[\xi(v)]=0
for all
v
,
the term corresponding to
p=1
in (
4.12
) is zero.
Secondly, thanks to our moment growth assumption
\mathbf{E}[|\xi(v)|^{p}]\leq p!\mathfrak{m}^{p}
, for every
p\geq 2
we have that

\left|\sum_{v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}[\xi(v_{1})\cdots\xi(v_{p})]f(v_{1})\cdots f(v_{p})\right|\\
\leq\sum_{v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}[|\xi(v_{1})|^{p}]^{1/p}\cdots\mathbf{E}[|\xi(v_{p})|^{p}]^{1/p}|f(v_{1})|\cdots|f(v_{p})|\leq p!\big(\mathfrak{m}\|f\|_{1}\big)^{p}.

Thus, if
\|f\|_{1}\leq 1/2\mathfrak{m}
, then we have that

\Big|\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\langle f,\xi\rangle}\right]-1\Big|\leq\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}(\mathfrak{m}\|f\|_{1})^{p}=\frac{(\mathfrak{m}\|f\|_{1})^{2}}{1-\mathfrak{m}\|f\|_{1}}\leq 2(\mathfrak{m}\|f\|_{1})^{2}.

As for the claim regarding the covariance, for any two random variables
Y
and
Z
,
we have by the triangle inequality that

|\mathbf{Cov}[Y,Z]|=|\mathbf{E}[YZ]-\mathbf{E}[Y]\mathbf{E}[Z]|\\
\leq|\mathbf{E}[YZ]-1|-|\mathbf{E}[Y]-1||\mathbf{E}[Z]-1|+|1-\mathbf{E}[Y]|+|1-\mathbf{E}[Z]|

Thus, whenever
\|f+g\|_{1},\|f\|_{1},\|g\|_{1}\leq 1/2\mathfrak{m}
,
it follows from (
4.10
) that

\Big|\mathbf{Cov}\big[\mathrm{e}^{\langle f,\xi\rangle},\mathrm{e}^{\langle g,\xi\rangle}\big]\Big|\leq 2\mathfrak{m}^{2}\big(\|f+g\|_{1}^{2}+\|f\|_{1}^{2}+\|g\|^{2}_{1}\big)+4\mathfrak{m}^{4}\|f\|_{1}^{2}\|g\|_{1}^{2},

as desired.
∎

In cases where we need a more precise control on the covariance, we have the following power series expansion:

Proposition 4.8
.

Suppose that Assumption
3.12
holds.
For any two finitely supported deterministic functions
f,g:\mathscr{V}\to\mathbb{R}
,
one has

\mathbf{Cov}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\langle f,\xi\rangle},\mathrm{e}^{\langle g,\xi\rangle}\right]=\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{A}_{p}(f,g)}{p!},

where, for every
p\geq 2
, we denote

(4.13)

\mathcal{A}_{p}(f,g):=\sum_{v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}\in\mathscr{V}}\Bigg(\sum_{m=1}^{p-1}{p\choose m}\mathbf{Cov}[\xi(v_{1})\cdots\xi(v_{m}),\xi(v_{m+1})\cdots\xi(v_{p})]\\
\cdot f(v_{1})\cdots f(v_{m})g(v_{m+1})\cdots g(v_{p})\Bigg).

Using the same Taylor expansion as in (
4.12
),
we get, on the one hand,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\langle f+g,\xi\rangle}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p!}\sum_{v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}[\xi(v_{1})\cdots\xi(v_{p})]\big(f(v_{1})+g(v_{1})\big)\cdots\big(f(v_{p})+g(v_{p})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p!}\sum_{v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}\in\mathscr{V}}\sum_{m=0}^{p}{p\choose m}\mathbf{E}[\xi(v_{1})\cdots\xi(v_{p})]f(v_{1})\cdots f(v_{m})g(v_{m+1})\cdots g(v_{p}),

and on the other hand

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\langle f,\xi\rangle}\right]\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\langle g,\xi\rangle}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m_{1}!m_{2}!}\Bigg(\sum_{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m_{1}+m_{2}}\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}[\xi(v_{1})\cdots\xi(v_{m_{1}})]\mathbf{E}[\xi(v_{m_{1}+1})\cdots\xi(v_{m_{1}+m_{2}})]

\displaystyle\hskip 180.67499pt\cdot f(v_{1})\cdots f(v_{m_{1}})g(v_{m_{1}+1})\cdots g(v_{m_{1}+m_{2}})\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{p}\frac{1}{m!(p-m)!}\Bigg(\sum_{v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}[\xi(v_{1})\cdots\xi(v_{m})]\mathbf{E}[\xi(v_{m+1})\cdots\xi(v_{p})]

\displaystyle\hskip 180.67499pt\cdot f(v_{1})\cdots f(v_{m})g(v_{m+1})\cdots g(v_{p})\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p!}\sum_{v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}\in\mathscr{V}}\Bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{p}{p\choose m}\mathbf{E}[\xi(v_{1})\cdots\xi(v_{m})]\mathbf{E}[\xi(v_{m+1})\cdots\xi(v_{p})]

\displaystyle\hskip 180.67499pt\cdot f(v_{1})\cdots f(v_{m})g(v_{m+1})\cdots g(v_{p})\Bigg).

We then get the result by subtracting these two expressions.
∎

4.4.
Proof of Lemma
4.3

By definition of local time,
\|L_{t}^{u}\|_{1}=\|\tilde{L}_{t}^{v}\|_{1}=t
,
as well as
\|L_{t}^{u}+\tilde{L}_{t}^{v}\|_{1}=2t
.
Thus, by (
4.11
) in Proposition
4.7
, if
t<1/4\mathfrak{m}
, then
we have for any
u,v\in\mathscr{V}
that

\left|\mathbf{Cov}_{\xi}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L^{u}_{t},\xi\rangle},\mathrm{e}^{-\langle\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},\xi\rangle}\big]\right|\leq 2\mathfrak{m}^{2}\big(4t^{2}+t^{2}+t^{2}\big)+4\mathfrak{m}^{4}t^{4}=12\mathfrak{m}^{2}t^{2}+4\mathfrak{m}^{4}t^{4}.

Since the right-hand side of this inequality is not random, the result then follows
by noting that
t^{4}\leq t^{2}
when
t\leq 1
and taking
C_{1}:=\max\{1,4\mathfrak{m},12\mathfrak{m}^{2},4\mathfrak{m}^{4}\}
.

4.5.
Proof of Lemma
4.4

For every
u,v\in\mathscr{V}
and
t>0
, let us denote by

\mathfrak{D}^{u,v}_{t}:=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\in\mathscr{V}\\
L_{t}^{u}(a),\tilde{L}_{t}^{v}(b)\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mathsf{d}(a,b)

the distance between the ranges of
X^{u}
and
\tilde{X}^{v}
up to time
t
.
In Section
4.5.1
below we prove the following crude version of Lemma
4.4
:
For every
t<\min\{1,1/4\mathfrak{m}\}
and
u,v\in\mathscr{V}
,

(4.14)

\displaystyle\left|\mathbf{Cov}_{\xi}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L_{t}^{u},\xi\rangle},\mathrm{e}^{-\langle\tilde{L}_{t}^{v},\xi\rangle}\right]\right|\leq 2\mathfrak{C}t^{2}(\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{u,v}+1)^{-\beta}+64\mathfrak{m}^{4}t^{4}.

With this in hand, by Minkowski’s inequality, we have that

(4.15)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{Cov}_{\xi}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L_{t}^{u},\xi\rangle},\mathrm{e}^{-\langle\tilde{L}_{t}^{v},\xi\rangle}\right]^{2}\right]^{1/2}\leq 2\mathfrak{C}t^{2}\mathbf{E}\big[(\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{u,v}+1)^{-2\beta}\big]^{1/2}+64\mathfrak{m}^{4}t^{4}

for every
t<\min\{1,1/4\mathfrak{m}\}
and
u,v\in\mathscr{V}
.

Next, we control
\mathfrak{D}^{u,v}_{t}
in terms of

\mathsf{d}(u,v)
. We do this in two cases. Suppose first that
\mathsf{d}(u,v)<16
.
In this case, we have the trivial bound

\mathbf{E}\big[(\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{u,v}+1)^{-2\beta}\big]^{1/2}\leq 1\leq 17^{\beta}\big(\mathsf{d}(u,v)+1\big)^{-\beta},

which, when combined with (
4.15
), yields

(4.16)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{Cov}_{\xi}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L_{t}^{u},\xi\rangle},\mathrm{e}^{-\langle\tilde{L}_{t}^{v},\xi\rangle}\right]^{2}\right]^{1/2}\leq 2\cdot 17^{\beta}\mathfrak{C}t^{2}\big(\mathsf{d}(u,v)+1\big)^{-\beta}+64\mathfrak{m}^{4}t^{4}

for every
t<\min\{1,1/4\mathfrak{m}\}
and
u,v\in\mathscr{V}
such that
\mathsf{d}(u,v)<16
.

Suppose then that
\mathsf{d}(u,v)\geq 16
.
For any
u,v\in\mathscr{V}
and
t>0
, we introduce the event

E^{u,v}_{t}:=\left\{\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\mathsf{d}\big(X^{u}(s),u\big)\leq\frac{\mathsf{d}(u,v)}{4}\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\mathsf{d}\big(\tilde{X}^{v}(s),v\big)\leq\frac{\mathsf{d}(u,v)}{4}\right\}.

With this in hand, given that
(\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{u,v}+1)^{-\beta}\leq 1
and
\sqrt{x+y}\leq\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}
for all
x,y\geq 0
,

\mathbf{E}\big[(\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{u,v}+1)^{-2\beta}\big]^{1/2}\leq\mathbf{E}\big[(\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{u,v}+1)^{-2\beta}\mathbf{1}_{E^{u,v}_{t}}\big]^{1/2}+\mathbf{P}\big[(E^{u,v}_{t})^{c}\big]^{1/2}.

For any outcome in the event
E^{u,v}_{t}
, we have by the triangle inequality that

\mathsf{d}\big(X^{u}(s),\tilde{X}^{v}(\tilde{s})\big)\geq\mathsf{d}(u,v)-\mathsf{d}\big(X^{u}(s),u\big)-\mathsf{d}\big(\tilde{X}^{v}(\tilde{s}),v\big)\geq\frac{\mathsf{d}(u,v)}{4}

for every
0\leq s,\tilde{s}\leq t
. In particular, this means that

\mathfrak{D}^{u,v}_{t}\mathbf{1}_{E^{u,v}_{t}}\geq\mathsf{d}(u,v)/4
.
In Section
4.5.2
below, we prove that
if
t<\min\{4/\mathfrak{q},1/4\mathfrak{q}\mathrm{e}\}
and
\mathsf{d}(u,v)\geq 16
,
then

(4.17)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\big[(E^{u,v}_{t})^{c}\big]^{1/2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\mathfrak{q}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2}t^{2}}{16}.

Combining these bounds with (
4.15
), we are led to

(4.18)

\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{Cov}_{\xi}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L_{t}^{u},\xi\rangle},\mathrm{e}^{-\langle\tilde{L}_{t}^{v},\xi\rangle}\right]^{2}\right]^{1/2}\\
\leq 2\cdot 4^{\beta}\mathfrak{C}t^{2}\big(\mathsf{d}(u,v)+1\big)^{-\beta}+\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\mathfrak{q}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2}\mathfrak{C}}{8}+64\mathfrak{m}^{4}\right)t^{4}

for all
t<\min\{1,1/4\mathfrak{m},4/\mathfrak{q},1/4\mathfrak{q}\mathrm{e}\}
and
u,v\in\mathscr{V}
such that
\mathsf{d}(u,v)\geq 16
.

With (
4.16
) and (
4.18
) in hand,
in order to prove Lemma
4.4
, it only remains
to establish (
4.14
) and (
4.17
).
We do this in the next two subsections.

4.5.1.
Proof of (
4.14
)

Our main tool to prove (
4.14
) consists of the power series expansion
proved in Proposition
4.13
:

(4.19)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Cov}_{\xi}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L_{t}^{u},\xi\rangle},\mathrm{e}^{-\langle\tilde{L}_{t}^{v},\xi\rangle}\right]=\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{A}_{p}(-L^{u}_{t},-\tilde{L}^{v}_{t})}{p!},

where the terms
\mathcal{A}_{p}
are defined in (
4.13
).
Thanks to our moment growth assumptions in (
3.4
), for every
p\geq 4
and
1\leq m\leq p-1
, we have that

\displaystyle\big|\mathbf{Cov}[\xi(v_{1})\cdots\xi(v_{m}),\xi(v_{m+1})\cdots\xi(v_{p})]\big|

\displaystyle\leq\big|\mathbf{E}[\xi(v_{1})\cdots\xi(v_{p})]\big|+\big|\mathbf{E}[\xi(v_{1})\cdots\xi(v_{m})]\mathbf{E}[\xi(v_{m+1})\cdots\xi(v_{p})]\big|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}[|\xi(v_{1})|^{p}]^{1/p}\cdots\mathbf{E}[|\xi(v_{p})|^{p}]^{1/p}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbf{E}[|\xi(v_{1})|^{m}]^{1/m}\cdots\mathbf{E}[|\xi(v_{m})|^{m}]^{1/m}\mathbf{E}[|\xi(v_{m+1})|^{p-m}]^{1/(p-m)}\cdots\mathbf{E}[|\xi(v_{p})|^{p-m}]^{1/(p-m)}

\displaystyle\leq p!\mathfrak{m}^{p}+m!(p-m)!\mathfrak{m}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq 2p!\mathfrak{m}^{p}.

Therefore, by combining (
4.13
)
with the fact that
\sum_{m=0}^{p}{p\choose m}=2^{p}
, one has

\frac{|\mathcal{A}_{p}(-L_{t}^{u},-\tilde{L}_{t}^{v})|}{p!}\leq 2\mathfrak{m}^{p}\sum_{m=1}^{p-1}{p\choose m}\|L_{t}^{u}\|_{1}^{m}\|\tilde{L}_{t}^{v}\|_{1}^{p-m}\leq 2(2\mathfrak{m}t)^{p}.

Next, if
\xi
has covariance decay of order
\beta
, then
(
3.5
) implies that

|\mathcal{A}_{2}(-L_{t}^{u},-\tilde{L}_{t}^{v})|\leq\sum_{w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathscr{V}}\big|\mathbf{Cov}[\xi(w_{1}),\xi(w_{2})]\big|L_{t}^{u}(w_{1})\tilde{L}_{t}^{v}(w_{2})\\
\leq\mathfrak{C}(\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{u,v}+1)^{-\beta}\|L_{t}^{u}\|_{1}\|\tilde{L}_{t}^{v}\|_{1}\leq\mathfrak{C}t^{2}(\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{u,v}+1)^{-\beta}.

and similarly (
3.6
) implies that

|\mathcal{A}_{3}(-L_{t}^{u},-\tilde{L}_{t}^{v})|\leq\mathfrak{C}t^{3}(\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{u,v}+1)^{-\beta}.

At this point if we take
t<\min\{1,1/4\mathfrak{m}\}
, then
t^{3}\leq t^{2}
,
and thus it follows from the expansion (
4.19
)
and the estimates above that

5.
Spectral Mapping and Multiplicity

A crucial aspect of the proof of Theorem
3.17

is the ability to relate exponential linear statistics of the eigenvalue point process
(
3.11
) to the trace of
K_{t}
via the identities

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]=\sum_{\mu\in\sigma(K_{t})\setminus\{0\}}m_{a}(\mu,K_{t})\,\mu=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(H)}m_{a}(\lambda,H)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}\in(0,\infty).

Though we expect that such a result is known (or at least folklore) in the operator theory
community, we were not able to locate any reference that contains all of the precise statements
that we need to prove (
5.1
).
(This is especially so since the level of generality in this paper allows for non-self-adjoint
operators.)
As such, our purpose in this section
is to provide a general criterion for an identity of the form (
5.1
)
to hold (as well as a few more properties), which we then use in Section
6

to wrap up the proof of Theorem
3.17
.

We begin this section with a definition:

Definition 5.1
.

We say that a linear operator
T
on
\ell^{2}_{\mathscr{Z}}(\mathscr{V})

is finite-dimensional if there exists a finite set
\mathscr{U}\subset\mathscr{V}

such that
T(u,v)=0
whenever
(u,v)\not\in\mathscr{U}\times\mathscr{U}
.
In particular, if we enumerate the set
\mathscr{U}=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{|\mathscr{U}|}\}
, then
T

has the same spectrum as the
|\mathscr{U}|\times|\mathscr{U}|
matrix
M_{T}
with entries

(5.2)

\displaystyle M_{T}(i,j):=T(u_{i},u_{j}),\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq|\mathscr{U}|.

The result that we prove in this section is as follows:

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
(T_{t})_{t>0}
be a strongly continuous semigroup of trace class operators on
\ell^{2}_{\mathscr{Z}}(\mathscr{V})

such that
\|T_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\omega t}
for some
\omega<0
, and
let
G
be its infinitesimal generator.
The following holds:

(1)

G
is closed and densely defined on
\ell^{2}_{\mathscr{Z}}(\mathscr{V})
.

(2)

\sigma(G)=\sigma_{p}(G)
, and
\Re(\lambda)\geq\omega
for all
\lambda\in\sigma(G)
.

(3)

For every
t>0
,
\sigma(T_{t})\setminus\{0\}=\{\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}:\lambda\in\sigma(G)\}
.

Moreover, if there exists a sequence of finite-dimensional
operators
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

(5.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\mathfrak{R}(z,G_{n})-\mathfrak{R}(z,G)\|_{\mathrm{op}}=0

for at least one
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(G)
and such that

(5.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\mathrm{e}^{-tG_{n}}-T_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=0,

then for every
t>0
and
\mu\in\sigma(T_{t})\setminus\{0\}
,

(5.5)

\displaystyle m_{a}(\mu,T_{t})=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(G):~\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}=\mu}m_{a}(\lambda,G).

As a direct consequence of the above proposition, we have that

\mathrm{Tr}[T_{t}]=\sum_{\mu\in\sigma(T_{t})\setminus\{0\}}m_{a}(\mu,T_{t})\,\mu=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(G)}m_{a}(\lambda,G)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}\in\mathbb{C}

for all
t>0
, which is precisely the kind of statement that we are looking for.
The remainder of this section is now devoted to the proof of Proposition
5.2
.

5.1.
Step 1. Closed Generator and Spectral Mapping

We begin with the more straightforward aspects of the statement
of Proposition
5.2
, namely, items (1)–(3).
Since
(T_{t})_{t>0}
is strongly continuous and
\|T_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\omega t}
,
it follows from the Hille-Yosida theorem (e.g.,
[
17
, Chapter II, Corollary 3.6]

is closed and densely defined on
\ell^{2}_{\mathscr{Z}}(\mathscr{V})

Moreover,
\Re(\lambda)\geq\omega
for every
\lambda\in\sigma(G)
.
Given that the

are trace class, we know that
\sigma(T_{t})=\sigma_{p}(T_{t})
and that

\mathrm{Tr}[T_{t}]=\sum_{\mu\in\sigma(T_{t})\setminus\{0\}}m_{a}(\mu,T_{t})\mu\in\mathbb{C}

by Lidskii’s theorem
(e.g.,
[
40
, Sections 3.6 and 3.12]
). Next,
by the spectral mapping theorem (e.g.,
[
17
, Chapter IV, (3.7) and (3.16)]

we know that for every
t>0
,

(5.6)

\displaystyle\big\{\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}:\lambda\in\sigma(G)\big\}\subset\sigma(T_{t})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\big\{\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}:\lambda\in\sigma_{p}(G)\big\}=\sigma_{p}(T_{t})\setminus\{0\}.

In particular,
\sigma(G)=\sigma_{p}(G)
, concluding the proof of
Proposition
5.2
(1)–(3).

5.2.
Step 2. Multiplicities in Finite Dimensions

It now remains to prove (
5.5
).
Before we prove this result, we first prove the corresponding statement
in finite dimensions, namely:

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
T
be a finite-dimensional linear operator on
\ell^{2}_{\mathscr{Z}}(\mathscr{V})

and
F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be an analytic function.
For every
\mu\in\sigma\big(F(T)\big)=F\big(\sigma(T)\big)
, one has

m_{a}\big(\mu,F(T)\big)=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(T):~F(\lambda)=\mu}m_{a}(\lambda,T).

Applying this to the exponential map and the operators
G_{n}
, we are led to
the fact that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
t>0
, and
\mu\in\sigma(\mathrm{e}^{-tG_{n}})
one has

(5.7)

\displaystyle m_{a}(\mu,\mathrm{e}^{-tG_{n}})=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(G_{n}):~\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}=\mu}m_{a}(\lambda,G_{n}).

It suffices to prove the result with
T
replaced by
M_{T}

and
F(T)
replaced by
F(M_{T})
, where
M_{T}
is the
matrix defined in (
5.2
).
Let
M_{T}=PJP^{-1}
be
M_{T}
’s Jordan canonical form. That is,

J
is the direct sum of
M_{T}
’s Jordan blocks, and in particular the number of times
any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
appears on
J
’s diagonal is equal to

m_{a}(\lambda,M_{T})
. By the standard analytic functional calculus for matrices,
we know that
F(M_{T})=PF(J)P^{-1}
, where
F(J)
is the direct sum
of
M_{T}
’s transformed Jordan blocks, wherein any
k\times k
Jordan block
of the form

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\lambda&1\\
&\lambda&1\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&\lambda&1\end{array}\right]

is transformed into the upper triangular matrix

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}F(\lambda)&F^{\prime}(\lambda)&F^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)/2&\cdots&F^{(k-1)}(\lambda)/(k-1)!\\
&F(\lambda)&F^{\prime}(\lambda)&\cdots&F^{(k-2)}(\lambda)/(k-2)!\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
&&&\ddots&F^{\prime}(\lambda)\\
&&&&F(\lambda)\end{array}\right].

Given that the characteristic polynomial of
F(M_{T})
is the same as that of
F(J)
,
this readily implies the result.
∎

5.3.
Step 3. Passing to the Limit

We now complete the proof of Proposition
5.2

by arguing that the identity (
5.7
) persists
in the large
n
limit.
Thanks to (
5.3
)
and (
5.4
), we know that we have the convergences

G_{n}\to G
and
\mathrm{e}^{-tG_{n}}\to T_{t}
for every

in the generalized sense of Kato
(see
[
31
, Chapter IV, (2.9), (2.20) and p. 206]
for a definition of convergence in the
generalized sense, and
[
31
, Chapter IV, Theorems 2.23 a) and 2.25]
for a proof
that norm-resolvent and norm convergence implies convergence in the generalized sense).
As shown in
[
31
, Chapter IV, Theorem 3.16]
(see also
[
31
, Chapter IV, Section 5]

for a discussion specific to the context of isolated eigenvalues),
convergence in the generalized sense implies the following spectral continuity results:

Notation 5.4
.

In what follows, we use
B(z,r)
to denote the closed ball in the complex plane centered at
z\in\mathbb{Z}

and with raduis
r>0
.

Corollary 5.5
.

For every
\lambda\in\sigma(G)
, if
\varepsilon>0
is such that
\sigma(G)\cap B(\lambda,\varepsilon)=\{\lambda\}
,
then there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
large enough so that

(5.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(G_{n})\cap B(\lambda,\varepsilon)}m_{a}(\tilde{\lambda},G_{n})=m_{a}(\lambda,G)

whenever
n\geq N
.

Conversely, for every
t>0
and
\mu\in\sigma(T_{t})\setminus\{0\}
, if
\varepsilon>0
is such that
\sigma(T_{t})\cap B(\mu,\varepsilon)=\{\mu\}
,
then there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
large enough so that

(5.9)

\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{\mu}\in(\mathrm{e}^{-tG_{n}})\cap B(\mu,\varepsilon)}m_{a}(\tilde{\mu},\mathrm{e}^{-tG_{n}})=m_{a}(\mu,T_{t})

whenever
n\geq N
.

We are now ready to prove (
5.2
).
We first show that for every
t>0
and
\mu\in\sigma(T_{t})\setminus\{0\}
, the set
\{\lambda\in\sigma(G):~\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}=\mu\}

is finite. Suppose by contradiction that this is not the case. Then, for any integer
M>0
, we can
find at least
M
distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M}\in\sigma(G)
such that
\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda_{i}}=\mu
.
By taking a small enough
\varepsilon>0
and large enough
N\in\mathbb{N}
, a combination of
(
5.7
) and (
5.9
) yields

(5.10)

\displaystyle m_{a}(\mu,T_{t})=\sum_{\tilde{\mu}\in\sigma(\mathrm{e}^{-tG_{N}})\cap B(\mu,\varepsilon)}m_{a}(\tilde{\mu},\mathrm{e}^{-tG_{N}})=\sum_{\tilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(G_{N}):~\mathrm{e}^{-t\tilde{\lambda}}\in B(\mu,\varepsilon)}m_{a}(\tilde{\lambda},G_{N}).

Since
z\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{-tz}
is continuous, we can take
\delta>0
small enough so that

(1)

if
\tilde{\lambda}\in B(\lambda_{i},\delta)
for some
1\leq i\leq M
, then
\mathrm{e}^{-t\tilde{\lambda}}\in B(\mu,\varepsilon)
; and

(2)

\sigma(G)\cap B(\lambda_{i},\delta)=\{\lambda_{i}\}
for every
1\leq i\leq M
.

Thus, up to increasing the value of
N
if necessary, an application of (
5.8
)
to the right-hand side of (
5.10
) then gives

(5.11)

\displaystyle m_{a}(\mu,T_{t})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{\tilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(G_{N})\cap B(\lambda_{i},\delta)}m_{a}(\tilde{\lambda},G_{N})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}m_{a}(\lambda_{i},G)\geq M.

Since
M
was arbitrary, this implies that
m_{a}(\mu,T_{t})=\infty
. Since
T_{t}
is trace class
this cannot be the case, hence
we conclude that
\{\lambda\in\sigma(G):~\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}=\mu\}
is finite.

By repeating the argument leading up to (
5.11
),
but this time letting
M
be equal to the number of eigenvalues in the set

\{\lambda\in\sigma(G):~\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}=\mu\}
, we obtain that

m_{a}(\mu,T_{t})\geq\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(G):~\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}=\mu}m_{a}(\lambda,G).

We now proceed to prove the reverse inequality. Recall that
\{\lambda\in\sigma(G):~\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}=\mu\}
contains finitely many elements. Denote them by
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M}
for some
M\in\mathbb{N}
.
Thanks to (
5.8
), we can find
a small enough
\varepsilon>0
and large enough

such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}m_{a}(\lambda_{i},G)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tilde{\lambda}\in\cup^{M}_{i=1}\sigma(G_{N})\cap B(\lambda_{i},\varepsilon)}m_{a}(\tilde{\lambda},G_{N})=\sum_{\tilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(G_{N})\cap\big(\cup^{M}_{i=1}B(\lambda_{i},\varepsilon)\big)}m_{a}(\tilde{\lambda},G_{N}).

Then, by (
5.7
), one has

(5.12)

\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(G_{N})\cap\big(\cup^{M}_{i=1}B(\lambda_{i},\varepsilon)\big)}m_{a}(\tilde{\lambda},G_{N})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{\mu}\in\sigma(e^{-tG_{N}})\\
\tilde{\mu}\in e^{-t}(\cup_{i=1}^{M}B(\lambda_{i},\varepsilon))\end{subarray}}m(\tilde{\mu},e^{-tG_{N}}),

where we use
\mathrm{e}^{-t}(B)
to denote the image of a set
B\subset\mathbb{C}
through the exponential map
z\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{-tz}
.
Since the exponential map is open and
\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda_{i}}=\mu
for all
1\leq i\leq M
, we can find a small enough
\delta>0

such that
B(\mu,\delta)\subset e^{-t}(\cup_{i=1}^{M}B(\lambda_{i},\varepsilon))
and
\sigma(T_{t})\cap B(\mu,\delta)=\{\mu\}
.
As a result we get

(5.13)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}m_{a}(\lambda_{i},G)\geq\text{r.h.s. of \eqref{eq:RevIneq2}}\geq\sum_{\tilde{\mu}\in\sigma(e^{-tG_{N}})\cap B(\mu,\delta)}m_{a}(\tilde{\mu},e^{-tG_{N}}).

At this point, up to increasing
N
if necessary an application of
(
5.9
) then yields

\sum_{i=1}^{M}m_{a}(\lambda_{i},G)\geq\sum_{\tilde{\mu}\in\sigma(e^{-tG_{N}})\cap B(\mu,\delta)}m_{a}(\tilde{\mu},e^{-tG_{N}})=m_{a}(\mu,T_{t}),

thus concluding the proof of (
5.5
)
and Proposition
5.2
.

6.
Proof of Theorem
3.17

In this section, we prove Theorem
3.17
.
We suppose throughout that Assumptions
3.8
and
3.12
hold.
We begin with a notation:

Notation 6.1
.

Throughout this proof,
we denote
X
’s transition semigroup by

\Pi_{t}(u,v)=\mathbf{P}^{u}[X(t)=v],\qquad t\geq 0,~u,v\in\mathscr{V}.

6.1.
Step 1. Boundedness

Our first step in the proof is to show that, almost surely,
K_{t}
is a bounded linear operator
on
\ell^{2}_{\mathscr{Z}}(\mathscr{V})
with
\|K_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{\omega t}

for every
t>0
for some
\omega<0
. As is typical in Schrödinger semigroup theory,
this relies on controlling the minimum of the random potential
V+\xi
. To this end, we have the following
result:

Lemma 6.2
.

Define the random variable

(6.1)

\displaystyle\omega_{0}:=\inf_{v\in\mathscr{V}}\big(V(v)+\xi(v)\big).

\omega_{0}>-\infty
almost surely.

Thanks to (
3.3
), it suffices to prove that

(6.2)

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left(\inf_{v\in\mathscr{V}:~\mathsf{d}(0,v)\leq n}\frac{\xi(v)}{\log n}\right)>-\infty\qquad\text{almost surely}.

By a union bound and Markov’s inequality, for every
\theta,\lambda>0
,

\mathbf{P}\Big(\inf_{v\in\mathscr{V}:~\mathsf{d}(0,v)\leq n}\xi(v)\leq-\lambda\Big)\leq\sum_{v\in\mathscr{V}:~\mathsf{d}(0,v)\leq n}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\lambda}\mathbf{E}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\xi(v)}\big].

On the one hand, thanks to (
3.1
), we have that

|\{v\in\mathscr{V}:\mathsf{d}(0,v)\leq n\}|\leq\mathfrak{c}\sum_{m=1}^{n}m^{d-1}\leq\mathfrak{c}+\mathfrak{c}\int_{1}^{n}x^{d-1}~\mathrm{d}x\leq Cn^{d}

for some constant
C>0
. On the other hand, thanks to the moment bound (
3.4
),
there exists a
\theta>0
small enough so that

\sup_{v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\xi(v)}\big]<\infty.

Combining these two observations, we conclude that there exists
\tilde{C},\theta>0
such that

\mathbf{P}\Big(\inf_{v\in\mathscr{V}:~\mathsf{d}(0,v)\leq n}\xi(v)\leq-\lambda\Big)\leq\tilde{C}n^{d}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\lambda},\qquad\lambda>0.

If we take
\lambda=\lambda(n)=c\log n
for large enough
c>0
,
then
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\tilde{C}n^{d}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\lambda(n)}<\infty
;
hence (
6.2
) holds by the
Borel-Cantelli lemma.
∎

As a direct application of Lemma
6.2
,
we have the inequality
K_{t}(u,v)\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{0}t}\Pi_{t}(u,v)
for every
u,v\in\mathscr{V}
,
where we take
\omega_{0}
as in (
6.1
).
In particular,
\|K_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{0}t}\|\Pi_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
.
Given that
\omega_{0}>-\infty
almost surely by Lemma
6.2
,
it suffices to prove that
\Pi_{t}
is bounded with
\|\Pi_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-t\omega_{1}}

for some constant
\omega_{1}\leq 0
. We now prove this.

Note that for every
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathscr{V})
, we have by Jensen’s inequality that

\|\Pi_{t}f\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}^{v}\big[f\big(X(t)\big)\big]^{2}\leq\sum_{v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}^{v}\big[f\big(X(t)\big)^{2}\big]=\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\Pi_{t}(v,u)f(u)^{2},

from which we conclude that

\|\Pi_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\sqrt{\sup_{u\in\mathscr{V}}\sum_{v\in\mathscr{V}}\Pi_{t}(v,u)}.

If we define the matrix

H_{X}(u,v):=\begin{cases}-q(u)\Pi(u,v)&\text{if }u\neq v\\
q(u)&\text{if }u=v\end{cases},\qquad u,v\in\mathscr{V}

(i.e., the Markov generator of
X
), then we can write

\sum_{v\in\mathscr{V}}\Pi_{t}(v,u)=\sum_{v\in\mathscr{V}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-t)^{n}H_{X}^{n}(v,u)}{n!}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\sum_{v\in\mathscr{V}}|H_{X}^{n}(v,u)|.

Noting that

\sup_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}|H^{n}_{X}(u,v)|\leq\|H^{n}_{X}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|H_{X}\|^{n}_{\mathrm{op}},

for every
u,v\in\mathscr{V}
, we have the bound

|H_{X}^{n}(v,u)|\leq\|H_{X}\|^{n}_{\mathrm{op}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathsf{d}(u,v)\leq n\}}.

By (
3.1
), for any
u\in\mathscr{V}
,
the number of
v\in\mathscr{V}
such that
(u,v)
is an edge is bounded by
\mathfrak{c}
.
Thus, the number of
v\in\mathscr{V}
such that
\mathsf{d}(u,v)\leq n
is crudely bounded by
\mathfrak{c}^{n}
.
Consequently,

\|\Pi_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\leq\sup_{u\in\mathscr{V}}\sum_{v\in\mathscr{V}}\Pi_{t}(v,u)\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(t\mathfrak{c}\|H_{X}\|_{\mathrm{op}})^{n}}{n!}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{c}\|H_{X}\|_{\mathrm{op}}t}.

Thus, it now suffices to prove that
\|H_{X}\|_{\mathrm{op}}<\infty
.

Recall that, by assumption,
\mathfrak{q}:=\mathrm{sup}_{u\in\mathscr{V}}q(u)<\infty
. For every
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathscr{V})
,

\|H_{X}f\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathfrak{q}^{2}\sum_{u\in\mathscr{V}}\left(\sum_{v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{1}_{\{(u,v)\in\mathscr{E}\}}f(v)\right)^{2}\leq\mathfrak{q}^{2}2^{\mathfrak{c}}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{1}_{\{(u,v)\in\mathscr{E}\}}f(v)^{2},

where the last inequality comes from the fact that

(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{\mathfrak{c}})^{2}\leq 2^{\mathfrak{c}}(x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{\mathfrak{c}}^{2}),\qquad x_{i}\in\mathbb{R},

and that, by (
3.1
),
for every
v\in\mathscr{V}
there are at most
\mathfrak{c}
vertices
u

such that
(u,v)\in\mathscr{E}
.
Using once again this last observation, we have that

\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{1}_{\{(u,v)\in\mathscr{E}\}}f(v)^{2}\leq\mathfrak{c}\|f\|_{2}^{2},

from which we conclude that

\|H_{X}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\leq\mathfrak{q}^{2}2^{\mathfrak{c}}\mathfrak{c},

as desired.

6.2.
Step 2. Continuity of the Semigroup

We now prove the almost-sure strong continuity and semigroup property.
Since
X
is Markov and local time is additive, the semigroup property is trivial. We now prove strong continuity.
Let
C_{0,\mathscr{Z}}(\mathscr{V})
denote the set of functions
f:\mathscr{V}\to\mathbb{R}
that are finitely supported
on
\mathscr{V}\setminus\mathscr{Z}
.
Since
C_{0,\mathscr{Z}}(\mathscr{V})
is dense in
\ell^{2}_{\mathscr{Z}}(\mathscr{V})
and a semigroup of bounded linear
operators is strongly continuous if and only if it is weakly continuous
(e.g.,
[
17
, Chapter I, Theorem 5.8]
), it suffices to prove that

\langle f,K_{t}g-g\rangle\to 0
as
t\to 0
for every
f,g\in C_{0,\mathscr{Z}}(\mathscr{V})
.
For every
g\in C_{0,\mathscr{Z}}(\mathscr{V})
, we know that

\lim_{t\to 0}g\big(X(t)\big)\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L_{t},V+\xi\rangle}=g\big(X(0)\big)\mathbf{1}_{\{X(0)\not\in\mathscr{Z}\}}=g\big(X(0)\big)\qquad\text{almost surely}.

By the definition of
\omega_{0}
, it follows that
\langle L_{t},V+\xi\rangle\geq\omega_{0}t
which implies that

\big|g\big(X(t)\big)\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L_{t},V+\xi\rangle}\big|\leq\|g\|_{\ell^{\infty}}\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{0}t}.

Since the right-hand side of this inequality is independent of
X
, it follows from dominated
convergence that

\lim_{t\to 0}K_{t}g(v)=\lim_{t\to 0}\mathbf{E}^{v}\left[g\big(X(t)\big)\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L_{t},V+\xi\rangle}\right]=g(v)\qquad\text{almost surely}

for every
v\in\mathscr{V}
. Finally, given that for every
v\in\mathscr{V}
, we have

\big|f(v)\big(K_{t}g(v)-g(v)\big)\big|\leq\|f\|_{\ell^{\infty}}\|g\|_{\ell^{\infty}}(\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{0}t}+1)\mathbf{1}_{\{f(v)\neq 0\}},

which is summable in
v
whenever
f\in C_{0,\mathscr{Z}}(\mathscr{V})
,
we obtain
\langle f,K_{t}g-g\rangle\to 0
as
t\to 0
by dominated convergence.

6.3.
Step 3. Trace Class

By the semigroup property, for every
t>0
, we can write

K_{t}
as the product
K_{t/2}K_{t/2}
. Thus, given that
the product of any two Hilbert-Schmidt operators is trace class
(e.g.,
[
40
, Theorem 3.7.4]
),
it suffices to prove that, almost surely,
K_{t}
is Hilbert-Schmidt
for all
t>0
, that is,

\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}K_{t}(u,v)^{2}<\infty.

By (
6.2
),
there exists finite random variables
\kappa,\mu>0
that only depend on
\xi
such that

V(v)+\xi(v)\geq\big(\kappa\mathsf{d}(0,v)\big)^{\alpha}-\mu,\qquad v\in\mathscr{V}

almost surely. Therefore, it suffices to prove the result with
K_{t}
replaced by the kernel

\tilde{K}_{t}(u,v):=\mathrm{e}^{\mu t}\mathbf{E}^{u}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L_{t},(\kappa\mathsf{d}(0,\cdot))^{\alpha}\rangle}\mathbf{1}_{\{X(t)=v\}}\right],\qquad u,v\in\mathscr{V}.

By Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\tilde{K}_{t}(u,v)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{2\mu t}\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}^{u}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L_{t},(\kappa\mathsf{d}(0,\cdot))^{\alpha}\rangle}\mathbf{1}_{\{X(t)=v\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{2\mu t}\sum_{u\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}^{u}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L_{t},(\kappa\mathsf{d}(0,\cdot))^{\alpha}\rangle}\right].

At this point, the same argument used in (
4.21
), (
4.6.1
), and (
4.24
)
implies that there exists some finite constant
C_{\kappa,t}>0
(which depends on
\kappa
and
t
) such that

\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\tilde{K}_{t}(u,v)^{2}\leq C_{\kappa,t}\mathrm{e}^{2\mu t}\sum_{u\in\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{e}^{-2t(\kappa\mathsf{d}(0,u))^{\alpha}}.

Then, writing the above sum as

\sum_{u\in\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{e}^{-2t(\kappa\mathsf{d}(0,u))^{\alpha}}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathsf{c}_{n}(0)\mathrm{e}^{-2t(\kappa n)^{\alpha}},

this is easily seen to be finite for all
t>0
by (
3.1
).

6.4.
Step 4. Infinitesimal Generator

We now prove the properties of the generator
H
,
except for number rigidity of its spectrum, which is relegated to the next (and final) step of the proof.
That
K_{t}
’s generator
is of the form (
3.10
) follows
from the straightforward computation that for every
u,v\in\mathscr{V}\setminus\mathscr{Z}
,

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\{u=v\}}-K_{t}(u,v)}{t}=H(u,v)\qquad\text{almost surely}

(indeed, recall that by definition of the process
X
,
\Pi_{t}(u,v)=q(u)\Pi(u,v)t+o(t)
as
t\to 0

whenever
u\neq v
, and that
K_{t}(u,v)=0
if
u\in\mathscr{Z}
or
v\in\mathscr{Z}
).

Almost surely,
(K_{t})_{t>0}
is a strongly continuous semigroup of trace class operators and
\|K_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\omega t}
.
Therefore, by Proposition
5.2
(1)–(3), the following holds almost surely:

(1)

H
is closed and densely defined on
\ell^{2}_{\mathscr{Z}}(\mathscr{V})
.

(2)

\sigma(H)=\sigma_{p}(H)
, and
\Re(\lambda)\geq\omega
for all
\lambda\in\sigma(H)
.

(3)

For every

,
\sigma(K_{t})\setminus\{0\}=\{\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}:\lambda\in\sigma(H)\}
.

It now remains to establish the trace identity (
5.1
), which is crucial
in our proof of rigidity. The fact that
\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]

is a positive real number follows from the fact that

\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]=\sum_{v\in\mathscr{V}}K_{t}(v,v)

and that
K_{t}(u,v)\in[0,\infty)
for all
u,v\in\mathscr{V}
. To prove the remainder of (
5.1
),
as per Proposition
5.2
, we need to find a sequence of finite-dimensional
operators that converge to
H
and
K_{t}
in the sense of (
5.3
)
and (
5.4
).

To this end, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let us denote the subset

\mathscr{V}_{n}:=\{v\in\mathscr{V}:\mathsf{d}(0,v)\leq n\}\subset\mathscr{V}.

Given that
\mathscr{G}
has uniformly bounded degrees, this must be finite. Thus,
the operators

H_{n}(u,v):=H(u,v)\mathbf{1}_{\{(u,v)\in\mathscr{V}_{n}\}},\qquad u,v\in\mathscr{V}

are finite-dimensional in the sense of Definition
5.1
.
More specifically,
H_{n}
is the restriction of
H
to the set
\mathscr{V}_{n}

with Dirichlet boundary on
\mathscr{V}\setminus\mathscr{V}_{n}
. In particular,
if for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we denote the hitting time

\tau_{n}:=\inf_{t\geq 0}\big\{t\geq 0:X(t)\not\in\mathscr{V}_{n}\big\},

Then
\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{n}}
is the integral operator on
\ell^{2}_{\mathscr{Z}}(\mathscr{V})
with kernel

(6.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{n}}(u,v)=\mathbf{E}^{u}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L_{t},V+\xi\rangle}\mathbf{1}_{\{X(t)=v\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{n}>t\}}\right].

The proof of (
5.1
) is now a matter of establishing the following result:

Lemma 6.3
.

Almost surely, it holds that

(6.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\mathfrak{R}(z,H_{n})-\mathfrak{R}(z,H)\|_{\mathrm{op}}=0

for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(z)<\omega
and

(6.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\mathrm{e}^{-tG_{n}}-K_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=0

for every
t>0
.

Given that
0\leq\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{n}}(u,v)\leq K_{t}(u,v)
for all
u,v\in\mathscr{V}
,
it is easy to see that
\|\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{n}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|K_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\omega t}

for all
t>0
almost surely. In particular, any
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(z)<\omega

is in the resolvent set of
H_{n}
and
H
for all
n
. Consequently, it follows
from
[
17
, Chapter II, Theorem 1.10]
that

\|\mathfrak{R}(z,H_{n})-\mathfrak{R}(z,H)\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\left\|\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{tz}(\mathrm{e}^{-tG_{n}}-K_{t})~\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{tz}\|\mathrm{e}^{-tG_{n}}-K_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}~\mathrm{d}t,

where the last inequality follows from
[
15
, Chapter II, Theorem 4 (ii)]
.
Given that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{tz}\|\mathrm{e}^{-tG_{n}}-K_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}~\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{tz}\big(\|\mathrm{e}^{-tG_{n}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|K_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\big)~\mathrm{d}t\leq 2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{t(z-\omega)}~\mathrm{d}t<\infty

whenever
\Re(z)<\omega
, we get that (
6.4
) is
a consequence of (
6.5
) by an application of the dominated convergence theorem.

Let us then prove (
6.5
).
Since the Hilbert-Schmidt norm dominates the operator norm, it suffices to prove that

(6.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\big(\mathrm{e}^{-tG_{n}}(u,v)-K_{t}(u,v)\big)^{2}=\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}^{u}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L_{t},V+\xi\rangle}\mathbf{1}_{\{X(t)=v\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{n}\leq t\}}\right]^{2}

vanishes as
n\to\infty
for all
t>0
almost surely. By Hölder’s inequality,
the right-hand side of (
6.6
) is bounded above by

\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}^{u}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L_{t},V+\xi\rangle}\mathbf{1}_{\{X(t)=v\}}\right]\mathbf{P}^{u}[\tau_{n}\leq t].

By mimicking our proof that
K_{t}
is trace class, we know that

\sum_{u,v\in\mathscr{V}}\mathbf{E}^{u}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-2\langle L_{t},V+\xi\rangle}\mathbf{1}_{\{X(t)=v\}}\right]<\infty

for every
t>0
almost surely. Thus, by dominated convergence, it suffices to prove that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}^{u}[\tau_{n}\leq t]=0

for every
u\in\mathscr{V}
and
t>0
. Noting that

\mathbf{P}^{u}\left[\max_{0\leq s\leq t}\mathsf{d}\big(0,X(s)\big)>n\right]\leq\mathbf{P}^{u}\left[\max_{0\leq s\leq t}\mathsf{d}\big(u,X(s)\big)>n-\mathsf{d}(0,u)\right]

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
by the triangle inequality,
this follows directly from
the tail bound (
4.21
).
∎

6.5.
Step 5. Rigidity

It now only remains to prove that the point process
(
3.11
)
is number rigid in the sense of Definition
3.3
. The proof of
this amounts to a minor modification of the argument in
[
26
, Theorem 6.1]
(see also

[
20
, Proposition 2.2]
).

Let
B\subset\mathbb{C}
be a Borel set such that

B\subset(-\infty,\delta]+\mathrm{i}[-\tilde{\delta},\tilde{\delta}]

for some
\delta,\tilde{\delta}>0
.
Thanks to the trace identity (
5.1
),
almost surely,
we can write

\mathcal{X}_{H}(B)=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(H)\cap B}m_{a}(\lambda,H)

as the sum of the following three terms:

(6.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(H)}m_{a}(\lambda,H)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}-\mathbf{E}\left[\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(H)}m_{a}(\lambda,H)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}\right]=\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]-\mathbf{E}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big],

(6.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(H)\cap B}m_{a}(\lambda,H)\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}\right),

(6.9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(H)}m_{a}(\lambda,H)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}\right]-\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(H)\setminus B}m_{a}(\lambda,H)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}.

Since we choose the exponent
\alpha
in the same way as
Theorem
3.16
, (
6.7
) converges to zero as
t\to 0

almost surely along a subsequence.
Next, we have that (
6.8
) is bounded above
in absolute value by

\mathcal{X}_{H}(B)\sup_{\zeta\in[\omega,\delta]+\mathrm{i}[\alpha,\beta]}|1-\mathrm{e}^{-t\zeta}|,

where we recall that
\omega
is the random lower bound on the real part
of the points in
\mathcal{X}_{H}
.
Since
\mathcal{X}_{H}
is real-bounded below and
B\subset(-\infty,\delta]+\mathrm{i}[-\tilde{\delta},\tilde{\delta}]
,

\mathcal{X}_{H}(B)<\infty
almost surely. Thus, (
6.8
)
converges to zero almost surely as
t\to 0
.
Thus,
\mathcal{X}_{H}(B)
is the almost sure limit of (
6.9
)
as
t\to 0
, along a subsequence.
Given that (
6.9
)
is measurable with respect
to the configuration of points outside of
B
for every
t
and that the almost-sure limit of measurable functions is measurable (assuming the sigma algebra is complete),
we conclude that
\mathcal{X}_{H}(B)
is measurable with respect
to the configuration outside of
B
. This then concludes the proof
of number rigidity, and thus of Theorem
3.17
.

Remark 6.4
.

Referring back to the point raised in Section
1.4.2
,
we see that the function denoted
\mathcal{N}_{B}
therein satisfies the relation

(6.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{B}\big(\sigma(H)\setminus B\big)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\mathbf{E}\left[\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(H)}m_{a}(\lambda,H)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}\lambda}\right]-\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(H)\setminus B}m_{a}(\lambda,H)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}\lambda}\right)

with probability one, where
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sparse enough
sequence that vanishes in the large
n
limit. In particular, understanding the
precise form of
\mathcal{N}_{B}
relies, among other things, on understanding
how the divergences of the two terms inside the limit on the right-hand side of
(
6.10
) somehow cancel out as
n\to\infty
.

7.
Proof of Theorem
3.18

7.1.
Step 1. General Lower Bound

We begin by providing a lower bound for
\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]

in the general setting of the statement of
Theorem
3.18
. This bound will then be shown to remain positive
as
t\to 0
in the cases labelled (1)–(3).

Recalling that
\gamma
is the positive definite covariance function of
\xi
,
if we denote the semi-inner-product

\langle f,g\rangle_{\gamma}:=\sum_{u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f(u)\gamma(u-v)g(v),\qquad f,g:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},

then our assumption that
\gamma
is nonnegative implies that
\langle f,g\rangle_{\gamma}\geq 0

whenever
f
and
g
are nonnegative. In particular, we have that

(7.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Cov}_{\xi}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L^{u}_{t},\xi\rangle},\mathrm{e}^{-\langle\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},\xi\rangle}\big]=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}\langle L_{t}^{u},L_{t}^{u}\rangle_{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\tilde{L}_{t}^{v},\tilde{L}_{t}^{v}\rangle_{\gamma}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\langle L_{t}^{u},\tilde{L}_{t}^{v}\rangle_{\gamma}}-1\right)\geq 0.

For every
u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
t>0
, denote the event

J_{t}(u,v):=\{L^{u}_{t}=t\mathbf{1}_{u}\text{ and }\tilde{L}^{v}_{t}=t\mathbf{1}_{v}\}
.
Clearly,
J_{t}(u,v)\subset\{X^{u}(t)=u,\tilde{X}^{v}(t)=v\}
, and
by independence of
X^{u}
and
\tilde{X}^{v}
,

(7.2)

\displaystyle\inf_{u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathbf{P}[J_{t}(u,v)]=\inf_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathbf{P}^{v}[X(s)=v\text{ for every }s\leq t]^{2}\geq\mathrm{e}^{-2t}.

We now combine (
7.1
)
and (
7.2
) to lower bound the variance of
\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]
:
By Proposition
4.2
, we may write

\displaystyle\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-\langle L^{u}_{t}+\tilde{L}^{v}_{t},V\rangle}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}\langle L_{t}^{u},L_{t}^{u}\rangle_{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\tilde{L}_{t}^{v},\tilde{L}_{t}^{v}\rangle_{\gamma}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\langle L_{t}^{u},\tilde{L}_{t}^{v}\rangle_{\gamma}}-1\right)\mathbf{1}_{J_{t}(u,v)}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-tV(u)-tV(v)}\mathrm{e}^{t^{2}\gamma(0)}\left(\mathrm{e}^{t^{2}\gamma(u-v)}-1\right)\mathbf{P}[J_{t}(u,v)]

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{e}^{-2t+t^{2}\gamma(0)}\sum_{u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-tV(u)-tV(v)}\left(\mathrm{e}^{t^{2}\gamma(u-v)}-1\right)

(7.3)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-2t+t^{2}\gamma(0)}\sum_{u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{d}(0,u)^{\delta}-t\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{\delta}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{t^{2}\gamma(u-v)}-1\right),

where the first line comes from (
7.1
) and
the fact that
\mathbf{E}[Y]\geq\mathbf{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_{E}]
for any nonnegative random variable
Y

and event
E
, the second line comes from the definition of the event
J_{t}(u,v)
,
the third line comes from (
7.2
),
and the last line comes from the assumption on
V
stated in Theorem
3.18
.
As
\mathrm{e}^{-2t+t^{2}\gamma(0)}\to 1
as
t\to 0
, we obtain our general lower bound:

(7.4)

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to 0}\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]\geq\liminf_{t\to 0}\sum_{u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{d}(0,u)^{\delta}-t\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{\delta}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{t^{2}\gamma(u-v)}-1\right).

We now prove that the right-hand side of (
7.4
) is positive in cases (1)–(3).

7.2.
Step 2. Three Examples

Suppose first that
\delta\leq d/2
and
\gamma(v)=\mathbf{1}_{\{v=0\}}
.
On the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, it is easy to see that there exists a
constant
C>0
such that
\mathsf{c}_{n}(0)\geq Cn^{d-1}
.
Therefore,
by an application of (
7.4
), followed by
the inequality
\mathrm{e}^{x}-1\geq x
for all
x\geq 0
and a Riemann sum, we have that

\liminf_{t\to 0}\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]\geq\liminf_{t\to 0}\left(\mathrm{e}^{t^{2}}-1\right)\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-2t\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{\delta}}\geq\liminf_{t\to 0}t^{2}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}\mathsf{c}_{n}(0)\mathrm{e}^{-2tn^{\delta}}\\
\geq C\liminf_{t\to 0}t^{2-d/\delta}t^{1/\delta}\sum_{n\in t^{1/\delta}\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}n^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-2n}\geq C\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-2x}~\mathrm{d}x>0.

Next, suppose that
\delta\leq d-\beta/2
and that

\gamma(v)\geq\mathcal{L}\big(\mathsf{d}(0,v)+1\big)^{-\beta}
for some
0<\beta<d
and
\mathcal{L}>0
. Then,
(
7.4
), the triangle inequality,
and the same arguments as in the previous case yield

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to 0}\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{t\to 0}\sum_{u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{d}(0,u)^{\delta}-t\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{\delta}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{L}t^{2}(\mathsf{d}(u,v)+1)^{-\beta}}-1\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{L}\liminf_{t\to 0}t^{2}\sum_{u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{d}(0,u)^{\delta}-t\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{\delta}}\big(\mathsf{d}(0,u)+\mathsf{d}(0,v)+1\big)^{-\beta}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\liminf_{t\to 0}t^{2}\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}\mathsf{c}_{m}(0)\mathsf{c}_{n}(0)\,\mathrm{e}^{-tm^{\delta}-tn^{\delta}}(m+n+1)^{-\beta}

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{L}C^{2}\liminf_{t\to 0}t^{2-2(d-1)/\delta+\beta/\delta}\sum_{m,n\in t^{1/\delta}\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}(mn)^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-m^{\delta}-n^{\delta}}(m+n+t^{\delta})^{-\beta}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}C^{2}\liminf_{t\to 0}t^{2-2(d-\beta/2)/\delta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(xy)^{d-1}}{(x+y)^{\beta}}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{\delta}-y^{\delta}}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y>0.

Finally, suppose that
\delta\leq d
and
\inf_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\gamma(v)>\mathcal{L}>0
. In this case we obtain that

\liminf_{t\to 0}\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathrm{Tr}[K_{t}]\big]\geq\liminf_{t\to 0}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{L}t^{2}}-1\right)\sum_{u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{d}(0,u)^{\delta}-t\mathsf{d}(0,v)^{\delta}}\\
\geq\mathcal{L}C^{2}\liminf_{t\to 0}t^{2}\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}n^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-2tn^{\delta}}\right)^{2}=\mathcal{L}C^{2}\liminf_{t\to 0}t^{2-2d/\delta}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-2x}~\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}>0,

thus concluding the proof.
