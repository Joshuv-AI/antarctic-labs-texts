Eric Swartz, Nicholas J. Werner

1.
Introduction

In this article, rings are assumed to be associative, but need not be commutative nor have a multiplicative identity. Our goal is to study the ways in which a ring
R
can be expressed as a union of proper subrings. Here, we use subring in the weakest sense:
S\subseteq R
is a subring when
S
is an additive group and is closed under multiplication. In particular,
S
need not contain a multiplicative identity, even if
R
itself is unital. A
cover
of
R
is a collection
\mathcal{C}
of proper subrings of
R
such that
R=\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{C}}S
. When such a cover exists, we say that
R
is
coverable
, and define the
covering number

\sigma(R)
to be the cardinality of a minimal cover. If
R
is not coverable, we set
\sigma(R)=\infty
.

The origin of this problem comes from group theory, where there is an analogous notion of a cover (by subgroups) for a noncyclic group, and covering problems have a long and fascinating history. That no group is the union of exactly two proper subgroups is an easy exercise (and, in fact, a Putnam Competition problem from 1969; see
[
13
, Chapter 3, Exercise 15]
). The earliest known consideration of covering numbers of groups was perhaps by Scorza
[
30
]
, who proved that a group is the union of three proper subgroups if and only if it has a quotient isomorphic to the Klein
4
-group; a nice proof of this result, which has been rediscovered a number of times through the years, can be found in
[
1
]
.

Problems related to covering numbers of groups have received considerable attention in recent years. Cohn
[
8
]
proved that there exists a group with covering number
p^{n}+1
for every prime
p
and positive integer
n
and conjectured that every solvable group has a covering number of this form. This conjecture was proven by Tomkinson
[
34
]
, who also proved that there is no group with covering number equal to 7. Covering numbers have been determined for various families of simple and almost simple groups (see, for instance,
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
17
,
24
,
31
]
). As noted above, no group has covering number 7, and other natural numbers exist that are not the covering number of a group (the next smallest examples being 11
[
10
]
and 19
[
14
]
). All the integers
N\leqslant 129
that are not the covering number of a group were determined in
[
15
]
, which also contains a good summary of the history and recent developments of the related work for groups. It is not known whether there are infinitely many positive integers that are not the covering number of a group.

A natural question then arises: what can be said about covering numbers of other algebraic structures? The covering number of a vector space by proper subspaces was determined by Khare
[
19
]
(see also
[
6
]
). The 2014 article by Kappe
[
18
]
provides a survey of recent research, but there have been some notable results since its publication. Gagola III and Kappe
[
12
]
proved that for every integer
n>2
, there exists a loop whose covering number is exactly
n
, and Donoven and Kappe
[
11
]
proved that the covering number of a finite semigroup that is not a group and not generated by a single element is always two. Furthermore, they showed that for each
n\geqslant 2
, there exists an inverse semigroup whose covering number (by inverse subsemigroups) is exactly
n
. Recently, the covering number of modules by proper submodules was studied in
[
20
]
and
[
16
]
.

The related question for rings has also received significant attention, especially in the last decade. Lucchini and Maróti
[
23
]
determined all rings with covering number equal to three. However, as noted by Kappe
[
18
, p. 86]
, “the solution is less simple than the group case,” and a ring has covering number three if and only if it has a homomorphic image isomorphic to one of five rings. In light of this result, Kappe
[
18
, p. 87]
further notes that a characterization of rings which are the union of exactly four proper subrings “does not seem to be a feasible problem for rings.” The difficulty of this problem is further illuminated in recent work by Cohen
[
7
]
, in which a strategy is presented to classify unital rings with a given covering number and a partial classification is given of unital rings whose covering number is four. Moreover, for each integer
n
,
3\leqslant n\leqslant 12
, examples are known of finite rings (with unity) that have covering number
n
, and no integers have thus far been ruled out from being the covering number of a ring. Indeed, Kappe
[
18
, p. 87]
further writes, “An interesting question would be if there are integers
n>2
that are not the covering number of a ring.” Other recent works on this problem include
[
5
,
9
,
27
,
32
,
35
]
.

The goal of this paper is to provide a general method to calculate
\sigma(R)
for any ring
R
with a finite covering number, and consequently to determine all integers that occur as the covering number of a ring. Our main result (Theorem
1.3
) allows one to describe the covering numbers of all unital rings. A variation on this theorem (Theorem
1.5
, Theorem
1.6
) can be used to compute the covering number of any nonunital ring. Thus, we are able to characterize all the positive integers that are the covering number of a ring, and prove that the set of such integers has density 0 (Corollary
1.9
).

Our approach to these problems is based on the following easy observation: if
R
has a two-sided ideal
I
such that
R/I
admits a cover
\mathcal{C}
, then we may form a cover of
R
by taking the inverse images of the subrings in
\mathcal{C}
under the natural homomorphism
R\to R/I
. Thus,
\sigma(R)\leqslant\sigma(R/I)
. Of capital importance are those rings for which the inequality
\sigma(R)\leqslant\sigma(R/I)
is strict for all
I\neq\{0\}
.

Definition 1.1
.

A ring
R
is said to be
\sigma
-elementary
if
\sigma(R)<\sigma(R/I)
for every nonzero two-sided ideal
I
of
R
. Note that a
\sigma
-elementary ring
R
must be coverable, since
\sigma(R)<\sigma(\{0\})=\infty
.

Observe that if
\sigma(R)
is finite, then either
R
is
\sigma
-elementary, or
R
has a proper,
\sigma
-elementary residue ring with the same covering number. Moreover, if an integer
n
occurs as the covering number of a ring, then there exists a
\sigma
-elementary ring
R
such that
\sigma(R)=n
. Thus, many questions about covering numbers of rings can be reduced to the case of
\sigma
-elementary rings.

In a recent paper
[
33
]
, the authors introduced a new family of
\sigma
-elementary rings, called rings of AGL-type (see Definition
1.2
) and determined their covering numbers. In Theorem
1.3
below, we will prove that rings of AGL-type, along with three other infinite families of rings (which have been studied previously), provide a complete list of all
\sigma
-elementary rings with unity. Furthermore, we give formulas for the covering numbers of each family of
\sigma
-elementary rings.

In order to state Theorem
1.3
, we must introduce some notation. For a prime power
q
,
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is the finite field of order
q
, and
M_{n}(q)
is the
n\times n
matrix ring with entries from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The Jacobson radical of a ring
R
is denoted by
\mathscr{J}(R)
, and when
R
contains unity, the unit group of
R
is
R^{\times}
. When
R
is a commutative ring and
M
is an
R
-module, the idealization of
R
and
M
, denoted
R(+)M
, is the ring

R(+)M:=\{(r,m)\mid r\in R,m\in M\},

with multiplication
(r_{1},m_{1})\cdot(r_{2},m_{2})=(r_{1}r_{2},r_{1}m_{2}+r_{2}m_{1})
. Of particular importance is the idealization
\mathbb{F}_{q}(+)\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with the 2-dimensional vector space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
. This ring can be represented as the following ring of
3\times 3
matrices:

\mathbb{F}_{q}(+)\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}\cong\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b&c\\
0&a&0\\
0&0&a\end{pmatrix}:a,b,c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\right\}.

Next, we give some definitions from
[
33
]
related to rings of AGL-type.

Definition 1.2
.

Given two powers
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
of a prime
p
, we define
q_{1}\otimes q_{2}
to be the order of the field compositum of
\mathbb{F}_{q_{1}}
and
\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}}
, which is
\mathbb{F}_{q_{1}}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}}
. Observe that when
q_{1}=p^{d_{1}}
and
q_{2}=p^{d_{2}}
, we have
q_{1}\otimes q_{2}=p^{\text{lcm}(d_{1},d_{2})}
.

Let
n\geqslant 1
and let
q=q_{1}\otimes q_{2}
. We define
A(n,q_{1},q_{2})
to be the following subring of
M_{n+1}(q)
:

A(n,q_{1},q_{2}):=\left(\text{\begin{tabular}[]{c|c}$M_{n}(q_{1})$&$M_{n\times 1}(q)$\\
\hline\cr$0$&$\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}}$\end{tabular} }\right),

and call this a ring of
AGL-type
. The construction and terminology for
A(n,q_{1},q_{2})
were inspired by representations of the affine general linear group
{\rm AGL}(n,q)
, which is isomorphic to a subgroup of
A(n,q,q)^{\times}
.

The formulas to compute
\sigma(R)
require some accessory functions. For a prime power
q=p^{d}
, we take
{\rm Irr}(p,d)
to be the set of all monic irreducible polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
of degree
d
. We define

\tau(q):=\begin{cases}p,&\text{ if }d=1,\\
|{\rm Irr}(p,d)|+1,&\text{ if }d>1.\end{cases}

We let
\omega
denote the prime omega function, which counts the number of distinct prime divisors of a natural number. Note that
\omega(1)=0
. For
q=p^{d}
, we define

\nu(q):=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if }d=1,\\
\omega(d),&\text{ if }d>1,\end{cases}

which counts the number of maximal subrings of the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The variation in the definitions between
\omega
and
\nu
is due to the fact that when
q=p
,
\{0\}
is the only maximal subring of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
; but, when
q>p
, the maximal subrings of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
are exactly the maximal subfields of prime index. Finally, we let
\binom{n}{k}_{q}
denote the
q
-binomial coefficient, which counts the number of
k
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
:

\binom{n}{k}_{q}=\frac{(q^{n}-1)(q^{n-1}-1)\cdots(q^{n-(k-1)}-1)}{(q^{k}-1)(q^{k-1}-1)\cdots(q-1)}.

We can now state our first main result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
R
be a
\sigma
-elementary ring with unity. Then, one of the following holds.

(1)

If
R
is commutative and semisimple, then for some prime power
q=p^{d}
,
R\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\tau(q)}\mathbb{F}_{q}
and

\sigma(R)=\tau(q)\nu(q)+d\binom{\tau(q)}{2}.

(2)

If
R
is commutative but not semisimple, then for some prime power
q
,
R\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}(+)\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
and

\sigma(R)=q+1.

(3)

If
R
is noncommutative and semisimple, then for some prime power
q
and integer
n\geqslant 2
,
R\cong M_{n}(q)
and

\sigma(R)=\frac{1}{a}\prod_{k=1,\\
a\nmid k}^{n-1}(q^{n}-q^{k})+\sum_{k=1,\\
a\nmid k}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{n\choose k}_{q},

where
a
is the smallest prime divisor of
n
.

(4)

If
R
is noncommutative and not semisimple, then
R\cong A(n,q_{1},q_{2})
and one of the two cases below holds. Let
q=q_{1}\otimes q_{2}=q_{1}^{d}
, and if
n\geqslant 2
, then let
a
be the smallest prime divisor of
n
.

(i)

n=1
and
(q_{1},q_{2})\neq(2,2)
or
(4,4)
. In this case,
\sigma(R)=q+1
.

(ii)

n\geqslant 3
,
d<n-(n/a)
, and
(n,q_{1})\neq(3,2)
. In this case,

\sigma(R)=q^{n}+\binom{n}{d}_{q_{1}}+\omega(d).

Portions of Theorem
1.3
have been proved in earlier papers. The classification of commutative unital
\sigma
-elementary rings was done in
[
32
]
, and the covering numbers of the rings in parts (1) and (2) were determined in
[
35
]
. The formula for the covering number of
M_{n}(q)
is due to Crestani, Lucchini, and Maróti
[
9
,
23
]
. Since
M_{n}(q)
is simple, it is clearly
\sigma
-elementary, and the fact that these are the only noncommutative semisimple
\sigma
-elementary rings follows from the classification of covering numbers for finite semisimple rings done in
[
27
]
. The determination of when
A(n,q_{1},q_{2})
is
\sigma
-elementary, and, in that event, the computation of its covering number, were completed in
[
33
]
. The content of the present paper is a proof that any
\sigma
-elementary unital ring falls into one of the four classes listed in Theorem
1.3
.

While Theorem
1.3
handles the situation where
R
contains unity, it leaves open the problem of determining which integers are covering numbers of nonunital rings. This more general case can be reduced to the unital case by considering covers of
R
in which every subring contains
1_{R}
.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
R
be a ring with unity. If
R
can be covered by unital subrings (i.e. those containing
1_{R}
), then the
unital covering number

\sigma_{u}(R)
is defined to be the cardinality of a minimal cover by unital subrings. We say
R
is
\sigma_{u}
-elementary
if
\sigma_{u}(R)<\sigma_{u}(R/I)
for every nonzero two-sided ideal
I
of
R
. Note that a
\sigma_{u}
-elementary ring necessarily contains unity, and admits a cover by unital subrings.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
R
be a ring without unity that has a finite cover. Then, there exists a ring
R^{\prime}
with unity such that
\sigma(R)=\sigma_{u}(R^{\prime})
. Thus, every covering number of a nonunital ring by subrings occurs as the covering number of a unital ring by unital subrings.

Via Theorem
1.5
, we can describe the covering numbers of all rings (with or without unity) by classifying
\sigma_{u}
-elementary rings. Our classification is very similar to that of unital
\sigma
-elementary rings, which was given in Theorem
1.3
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
R
be a
\sigma_{u}
-elementary ring.

(1)

If
R
is
\sigma
-elementary, then
R
is one of the rings listed in Theorem
1.3
, with the exception that
R\not\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Moreover, any ring
R\not\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{F}_{p}
listed in Theorem
1.3
is
\sigma_{u}
-elementary.

(2)

If
R
is not
\sigma
-elementary, then either
R\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p+1}\mathbb{F}_{p}
with
\sigma_{u}(R)=p+\binom{p}{2}
, or
R\cong A(1,2,2)
with
\sigma_{u}(R)=3
.

In particular, if
R
is
\sigma_{u}
-elementary, then
\sigma_{u}(R)=\sigma(R)
, and the integers that are covering numbers of rings with unity are the same as the integers that are unital covering numbers of rings with unity.

We remark that Theorem
1.6
is likely of independent interest: for many, the definition of a ring includes the existence of a multiplicative identity, and subrings likewise are required to contain this unital element; see, e.g.,
[
21
, Chapter II]
. (Those who adopt this convention often refer to a ring without unity as an
rng
.) In this setting, the only covers would indeed be covers by unital subrings.

In light of these results, any integer that is the covering number of a ring (with or without unity) or unital covering number of a ring with unity can be computed by the formulas in Theorem
1.3
.

Corollary 1.7
.

Any integer that is the covering number or unital covering number of a ring occurs as the covering number of a ring with unity.

This, in turn, allows us to answer Question 1.2 from
[
32
]
:

Corollary 1.8
.

There does not exist an associative ring with covering number
13
.

Even more, Theorems
1.3
,
1.5
, and
1.6
allow us to prove that there are infinitely many integers that are not covering numbers (or unital covering numbers) of rings. In fact, almost all integers are not covering numbers of rings.

Corollary 1.9
.

Let
\mathscr{E}(N):=\{m:m\leqslant N,\sigma(R)=m\text{ for some ring }R\}.

Then, for all
N\geqslant 5
,

\frac{N}{\log N}<|\mathscr{E}(N)|<\frac{144N}{\log N},

where
\log N
denotes the binary (base
2
) logarithm of
N
. In particular, we have
|\mathscr{E}(N)|=\Theta(N/\log(N))
and

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|\mathscr{E}(N)|}{N}=0.

This paper is organized as follows. For the majority of the article, we will focus on rings with unity. In Section
2
, we collect a number of background results that will be used in the rest of the article. Section
3
lays the groundwork for the proof of Theorem
1.3
by studying the Peirce decomposition of the Jacobson radical of a
\sigma
-elementary ring and providing numerical bounds of covering numbers in certain cases. Section
4
is dedicated to proving that most terms in the Peirce decomposition of the Jacobson radical of a
\sigma
-elementary ring must in fact be
\{0\}
(so, a
\sigma
-elementary ring cannot be “too far” from being semisimple), and Section
5
in turn places severe restrictions on the direct summands of the semisimple residue of a
\sigma
-elementary ring. This portion of the work culminates in Section
6
, where we use the results of the previous sections to prove Theorem
1.3
.

In Section
7
, we deal with the general case where rings need not contain a multiplicative identity. After proving Theorem
1.5
, we concentrate on proving Theorem
1.6
, which is accomplished using the methods developed in earlier sections. Finally, in a short post-script (Section
8
), we employ the formulas for
\sigma(R)
in Theorem
1.3
to prove Corollary
1.9
.

2.
Frequently used results

In this section, we summarize a number of results that will be referenced frequently later in the paper. The first lemma is elementary, and will be taken for granted throughout the article.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
R
be a ring with unity.

(1)

R
is coverable if and only if
R
cannot be generated (as a ring) by a single element.

(2)

If
R
is noncommutative, then
R
is coverable.

(3)

For any two-sided ideal
I
of
R
, a cover of
R/I
can be lifted to a cover of
R
. Hence,
\sigma(R)\leqslant\sigma(R/I)
.

(4)

If each proper subring of
R
is contained in a maximal subring, then we may assume that any minimal cover of
R
consists of maximal subrings.

By Lemma
2.1
(4), maximal subrings of
R
are important in forming minimal covers. The next lemma provides conditions under which a maximal subring is guaranteed to be part of every cover of a ring.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
R
be a coverable ring, and let
\mathcal{C}
be a minimal cover of
R
.

(1)

[
35
, Lemma 2.1]
If
M
is a maximal subring of
R
and
M\notin\mathcal{C}
, then
\sigma(M)\leq\sigma(R)
.

(2)

[
32
, Lemma 2.2]
Let
S
be a proper subring of
R
such that
R=S\oplus I
for some two-sided ideal
I
of
R
. If
\sigma(R)<\sigma(R/I)
, then
S\subseteq T
for some
T\in\mathcal{C}
. If, in addition,
S
is a maximal subring of
R
, then
S\in\mathcal{C}
.

As shown in
[
35
, Theorem 2.2]
, when
R
is a direct sum of rings,
\sigma(R)
can be determined from the covering numbers of the summands of
R
as long as all maximal subrings of
R
respect the direct sum decomposition. While not explicitly stated in
[
35
]
, the analogous result for unital maximal subrings and unital coverings of
R
is also true, and can be proved in the same manner as
[
35
, Theorem 2.2]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

[
35
, Theorem 2.2]

Let
R=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}R_{i}
for rings
R_{1},\ldots,R_{t}
. Assume that each maximal subring
M
of
R
has the form

M=R_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus R_{i-1}\oplus M_{i}\oplus R_{i+1}\oplus\cdots\oplus R_{t}

for some
1\leqslant i\leqslant t
. Then,
\sigma(R)=\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant t}\{\sigma(R_{i})\}
. Furthermore, if each
R_{i}
is a unital ring and each unital maximal subring has the above form (with
M_{i}
itself unital), then
\sigma_{u}(R)=\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant t}\{\sigma_{u}(R_{i})\}
.

From results of Neumann
[
26
, Lemma 4.1, 4.4]
and Lewin
[
22
, Lemma 1]
, it is known that if
R
has a finite covering number, then there exists a two-sided ideal
I
of
R
of finite index such that
\sigma(R)=\sigma(R/I)
. Hence, the calculation of covering numbers reduces to the case of finite rings. In fact, a much stronger reduction is possible. Recall that
\mathscr{J}(R)
denotes the Jacobson radical of a ring
R
.

Lemma 2.4
.

[
32
, Theorem 3.12]

Let
R
be a (unital, associative) ring such that
\sigma(R)
is finite. Then, there exists a two-sided ideal
I
of
R
such that
R/I
is finite;
R/I
has characteristic
p
;
\mathscr{J}(R/I)^{2}=\{0\}
; and
\sigma(R/I)=\sigma(R)
.

Thus, for unital rings with finite covers, the computation of
\sigma(R)
reduces to the case where
R
is finite of characteristic
p
and
\mathscr{J}(R)^{2}=\{0\}
. When
R
is finite,
R/\mathscr{J}(R)
is semisimple, i.e., it is isomorphic to a direct sum of matrix rings over finite fields. The Wedderburn-Malcev Theorem
[
28
, Sec. 11.6, Cor. p. 211]
,
[
25
, Thm. VIII.28]
(sometimes called the Wedderburn Principal Theorem) provides a more detailed description of the structure of
R
, and how it relates to
R/\mathscr{J}(R)
.

Theorem 2.5
.

(Wedderburn-Malcev Theorem)
Let
R
be a finite ring with unity of characteristic
p
. Then, there exists an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-subalgebra
S
of
R
such that
R=S\oplus\mathscr{J}(R)
, and
S\cong R/\mathscr{J}(R)
as
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebras. Moreover,
S
is unique up to conjugation by elements of
1+\mathscr{J}(R)
.

Let
\mathscr{S}(R)
be the set of all semisimple complements to
\mathscr{J}(R)
in
R
.
By Theorem
2.5
, all such complements are conjugate. So, for any
S\in\mathscr{S}(R)
, we have
R=S\oplus\mathscr{J}(R)
, and
S\cong R/\mathscr{J}(R)
.

The decomposition given by Theorem
2.5
allows us to characterize all of the maximal subrings of
R
.

Lemma 2.6
.

[
32
, Theorem 3.10]

Let
R
be a finite ring with unity of characteristic
p
, let
M
be a maximal subring of
R
, and let
J=\mathscr{J}(R)
.

(1)

J\subseteq M
if and only if
M
is the inverse image of a maximal subring of
R/J
.

(2)

J\not\subseteq M
if and only if
M=S\oplus\mathscr{J}(M)
, where
S\in\mathscr{S}(R)
and
\mathscr{J}(M)=M\cap J
is an ideal of
R
that is maximal among the subideals of
R
contained in
J
.

3.
Peirce decomposition and bounds

We now turn our attention to proving that any
\sigma
-elementary unital ring falls into one of the four classes given in Theorem
1.3
.
Throughout this section, we will assume that
R
is a
\sigma
-elementary unital ring with
\sigma(R)
finite. By Lemma
2.4
, we can assume that
R
is finite of characteristic
p
and has Jacobson radical
J=\mathscr{J}(R)
with
J^{2}=\{0\}
. Moreover, using Theorem
2.5
, we have
R=S\oplus J
, where
S\cong R/J
is a semisimple complement to
J
in
R
. The set of all such complements is denoted by
\mathscr{S}(R)
. For the remainder of this section, we fix
S\in\mathscr{S}(R)
. Then, for some
N\geqslant 1
,
S=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{N}S_{i}
, where each
S_{i}
is a simple ring. For each
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
, let
S_{i}=M_{n_{i}}(q_{i})
, where
n_{i}\geqslant 1
and
q_{i}
is a power of
p
. Finally, let
e_{i}=1_{S_{i}}
, so that
e_{1},\ldots,e_{N}
are orthogonal idempotents such that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}e_{i}
is the unity of
R
.

Apply a two-sided Peirce decomposition to
J
using the idempotents
e_{1},\ldots,e_{N}
. This gives
J=\bigoplus_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}e_{i}Je_{j}
, where each
e_{i}Je_{j}
is an (
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
)-bimodule. If
S_{i}=M_{n_{i}}(F_{i})
and
S_{j}=M_{n_{j}}(F_{j})
, then (
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
)-bimodules are equivalent to modules over
M_{n_{i}n_{j}}(F)
, where
F=F_{i}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}F_{j}
is the compositum of
F_{i}
and
F_{j}
. In particular, we may speak of the length of
e_{i}Je_{j}
as an (
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
)-bimodule, i.e., the number of simple (
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
)-bimodules in a direct sum decomposition of
e_{i}Je_{j}
. Note that any simple (
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
)-bimodule has order
|F|^{n_{i}n_{j}}
.

Definition 3.1
.

For all
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N
, let
J_{ij}:=e_{i}Je_{j}
. If
J_{ij}\neq\{0\}
, then let
\lambda_{ij}
be the length of
J_{ij}
as an (
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
)-bimodule. If
J_{ij}=\{0\}
, then we take
\lambda_{ij}=0
.

Recall that when
q_{i}
and
q_{j}
are powers of
p
, we define
q_{i}\otimes q_{j}
to be the order of
\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}\mathbb{F}_{q_{j}}
. If
q_{i}=p^{d_{i}}
and
q_{j}=p^{d_{j}}
, then
q_{i}\otimes q_{j}=p^{\text{lcm}(d_{i},d_{j})}
. Note also that we have
M_{n_{i}}(q_{i})\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}M_{n_{j}}(q_{j})\cong M_{n_{i}n_{j}}(q_{i}\otimes q_{j})
for any positive integers
n_{i}
and
n_{j}
. Thus,
|J_{ij}|=(q_{i}\otimes q_{j})^{n_{i}n_{j}\lambda_{ij}}
.

We remark that the rings in parts (2) and (4) of Theorem
1.3
may occur as subrings of
R
, and can be described using the notation and parameters we have defined. If
n_{k}=1
and
\lambda_{kk}=2
for some
1\leqslant k\leqslant N
, then
S_{k}\oplus J_{kk}\cong\mathbb{F}_{q_{k}}(+)\mathbb{F}_{q_{k}}^{2}
. If
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N
with
i\neq j
,
n_{j}=1
, and
\lambda_{ij}=1
, then
S_{i}\oplus S_{j}\oplus J_{ij}\cong A(n_{i},q_{i},q_{j})
is a ring of AGL-type.

As we now show, any (
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
)-bimodule inside of
J
is a two-sided ideal of
R
, and has an ideal complement in
J
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N
.

(1)

Let
X\subseteq J
be an (
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
)-bimodule. Then,
X\subseteq J_{ij}
,
X
is a two-sided ideal of
R
, and
X
has a two-sided ideal complement in
J
.

(2)

Let
I\subseteq J
be a two-sided ideal of
R
. Then,
I
has a two-sided ideal complement in
J
.

(3)

Let
I\subseteq J
be a two-sided ideal of
R
and let
Z=J\cap Z(R)
. For all
x\in J
,
S\oplus I=S^{1+x}\oplus I
if and only if
x\in I+Z
. Hence, the number of conjugates of
S\oplus I
in
R
is equal to
|J:(I+Z)|
.

(4)

For each
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
,
R
has a residue ring isomorphic to
S_{i}\oplus J_{ii}
. For all
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N
with
i\neq j
,
R
has a residue ring isomorphic to
S_{i}\oplus S_{j}\oplus J_{ij}
.

Proof.

(1) Since
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
are the identities of
S_{i}
and
S_{j}
, respectively,
X=e_{i}Xe_{j}\subseteq J_{ij}
. To show that
X
is a two-sided ideal of
R
, let
r\in R
and
x\in X
. Then,
r=s+y
for some
s\in S
and
y\in J
. Since
J^{2}=\{0\}
,
rx=sx
. Next, write
s=\sum_{k=1}^{N}s_{k}
, where each
s_{k}\in S_{k}
. Since the idempotents
e_{1},\ldots,e_{N}
are orthogonal, we have
se_{i}=s_{i}e_{i}
. So,
rx=sx=s(e_{i}x)=s_{i}x\in X
. The proof that
xr\in X
is similar. Finally, since
S_{i}
and
S_{j}
are simple rings,
J
is a semisimple (
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
)-bimodule. So, there is an (
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
)-bimodule
\widehat{X}\subseteq J
such that
J=X\oplus\widehat{X}
, and by the work above,
\widehat{X}
is also a two-sided ideal of
R
.

(2) Apply the Peirce decomposition to
I
to get
I=\bigoplus_{i,j}e_{i}Ie_{j}
. Then, each
e_{i}Ie_{j}
is an (
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
)-bimodule contained in
J
. By part (1), each
e_{i}Ie_{j}
has an ideal complement, and therefore
I
does as well.

(3) Note that since
J^{2}=\{0\}
,
(1+x)^{-1}=1-x
for all
x\in J
. So,
s^{1+x}=(1-x)s(1+x)=s+sx-xs
for all
s\in S
and all
x\in J
. Certainly, if
x\in I+Z
, then
S^{1+x}\subseteq S\oplus I
. Conversely, assume that
x\in J
is such that
S\oplus I=S^{1+x}\oplus I
. By (2), there is an ideal complement
\widehat{I}
of
I
in
J
. Express
x
as
x=\alpha+\beta
, where
\alpha\in I
and
\beta\in\widehat{I}
. Then, for all
s\in S
,

sx-xs=(s\alpha-\alpha s)+(s\beta-\beta s),

and
sx-xs\in I
because
s^{1+x}\in S\oplus I
. It follows that
s\beta-\beta s\in I\cap\widehat{I}=\{0\}
. Thus,
\beta
is central in
R
and
x\in I+Z
.

(4) We will prove the claim regarding
S_{i}\oplus S_{j}\oplus J_{ij}
. The proof of the other statement is similar. Let
T=S_{i}\oplus S_{j}\oplus J_{ij}
, and let
\widehat{S}_{ij}=\bigoplus_{k\neq i,k\neq j}S_{k}
, which is a direct sum complement to
S_{i}\oplus S_{j}
in
S
. Let
\widehat{J}_{ij}
be an ideal complement to
J_{ij}
in
J
, which exists by part (2). Since the idempotents
e_{1},\ldots,e_{N}
are orthogonal,
\widehat{S}_{ij}
and
T
annihilate one another. Hence,
\widehat{S}_{ij}\oplus\widehat{J}_{ij}
is a two-sided ideal of
R
, and
R/(\widehat{S}_{ij}\oplus\widehat{J}_{ij})\cong T
.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Assume that
R
is
\sigma
-elementary. Let
M
be a maximal subring of
R
that does not contain
J
. Then,
M
is contained in every minimal cover of
R
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.6
,
M=S^{\prime}\oplus I
, where
S^{\prime}\in\mathscr{S}(R)
and
I
is a maximal subideal of
J
. By Lemma
3.2
,
I
has an ideal complement
\widehat{I}
in
J
. Thus,
R=M\oplus\widehat{I}
. Since
R
is
\sigma
-elementary,
M
must be contained in every minimal cover of
R
by Lemma
2.2
.
∎

Proposition
3.3
(often in conjunction with Lemma
2.2
) will be used to produce lower bounds on the covering number of a
\sigma
-elementary ring. The remainder of this section contains various bounds on the covering numbers of
R
and its subrings.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N
with
i\neq j
, and let
T=S_{i}\oplus S_{j}\oplus J_{ij}
.

(1)

J_{ij}\cap Z(T)=\{0\}
.

(2)

Let
I
be a subideal of
J_{ij}
. Then, for all
x\in J_{ij}
,
(S_{i}\oplus S_{j})\oplus I=(S_{i}\oplus S_{j})^{1+x}\oplus I
if and only if
x\in I
.

(3)

\sigma(R)\leqslant\sigma(T)
.

Proof.

For (1), there is nothing to prove if
J_{ij}=\{0\}
, so assume there exists
x\in J_{ij}\setminus\{0\}
. Then,
e_{i}x=x
and
xe_{i}=(xe_{j})e_{i}=0
. Hence,
x\in Z(T)
if and only if
x=0
. Part (2) now follows from part (1) and Lemma
3.2
(3), and part (3) follows from Lemma
3.2
(4).
∎

The next two lemmas are generalizations of
[
33
, Proposition 3.3]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N
and let
q=q_{i}\otimes q_{j}
. Assume that
J_{ij}\neq\{0\}
, and let
T=S_{i}\oplus S_{j}\oplus J_{ij}
. Then,
\sigma(T)\leqslant(q^{n_{i}n_{j}+1}-1)/(q-1)
.

Proof.

For convenience, let
S=S_{i}\oplus S_{j}
and
J=J_{ij}
. First we argue that we may assume
J
is a simple (
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
)-bimodule. Indeed, if
J
is not simple, then let
X\subseteq J
be a simple (
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
)-bimodule. By Lemma
3.2
,
X
has an ideal complement
\widehat{X}
in
J
. Since
T/\widehat{X}\cong S_{i}\oplus S_{j}\oplus X
, we get
\sigma(T)\leqslant\sigma(T/\widehat{X})=\sigma(S_{i}\oplus S_{j}\oplus X)
. Hence, we may assume that
J
is simple.

For each nonzero
x\in J
, define
C_{S}(x):=\{s\in S:sx=xs\}
. Then, each
C_{S}(x)
is a subring of
S
. By Lemma
3.4
(1),
C_{S}(x)\subsetneq S
for all nonzero
x\in J
, so
C_{S}(x)\oplus J
is a proper subring of
T
for all
x\neq 0
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathscr{S}(T)\cup\{C_{S}(x)\oplus J:x\in J,x\neq 0\}
. We will show that
\mathcal{C}
is a cover of
T
. Suppose
t\in T\backslash\bigcup_{x\in J\setminus\{0\}}C_{S}(x)\oplus J
; that is, suppose
t\in s+J
for some
s\in S\backslash\bigcup_{x\in J\setminus\{0\}}C_{S}(x)
.

We claim that
s+J=s^{1+J}
. We know that
s^{1+x}=s+sx-xs
for all
x\in J
, so
s^{1+J}\subseteq s+J
. Furthermore,
s^{1+x_{1}}=s^{1+x_{2}}
if and only if
s^{1+(x_{1}-x_{2})}=s
. Since
s\notin C_{S}(x)
for all
x\neq 0
, this means that
s^{1+x_{1}}=s^{1+x_{2}}
if and only if
x_{1}=x_{2}
. Hence,
|s^{1+J}|=|J|=|s+J|
, and so
s+J=s^{1+J}
. Thus,

t\in s+J=s^{1+J}\subseteq\bigcup_{S^{\prime}\in\mathscr{S}(T)}S^{\prime},

and
\mathcal{C}
is a cover of
T
.

Finally, note that
C_{S}(cx)=C_{S}(x)
whenever
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. This means that there are at most
(|J|-1)/(q-1)=(q^{n_{i}n_{j}}-1)/(q-1)
subrings
C_{S}(x)
of
S
. Since
J
is a simple
(S_{i},S_{j})
-bimodule,
|J|=q^{n_{i}n_{j}}
. Therefore,

\sigma(T)\leqslant|J|+\frac{|J|-1}{q-1}=\frac{q^{n_{i}n_{j}+1}-1}{q-1},

as required.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N
such that
i\neq j
and
n_{j}=1
, and let
q=q_{i}\otimes q_{j}
. If
J_{ij}
is a simple (
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
)-bimodule and
R
is
\sigma
-elementary, then
q^{n_{i}}+1\leqslant\sigma(R)
. If, in addition,
n_{i}=1
, then
R=S_{i}\oplus S_{j}\oplus J_{ij}
and
\sigma(R)=q+1
.

Proof.

Assume that
J_{ij}
is simple and
R
is
\sigma
-elementary. Let
T=S_{i}\oplus S_{j}\oplus J_{ij}
. Then, for each
x\in J_{ij}
,
(S_{i}\oplus S_{j})^{1+x}
is a maximal subring of
T
. By Lemma
3.4
,
J_{ij}\cap Z(T)=\{0\}
, so there are
|J_{ij}|=q^{n_{i}}
distinct conjugates of
S_{i}\oplus S_{j}
in
T
.

Let
\widehat{S}_{ij}=\bigoplus_{k\neq i,k\neq j}S_{k}
and let
\widehat{J}_{ij}
be an ideal complement to
J_{ij}
in
J
. Then, for any conjugate
S^{\prime}
of
S_{i}\oplus S_{j}
in
T
, we have

R=(S^{\prime}\oplus\widehat{S}_{ij}\oplus\widehat{J}_{ij})\oplus J_{ij}.

Since
J_{ij}
is simple, each subring
S^{\prime}\oplus\widehat{S}_{ij}\oplus\widehat{J}_{ij}
is maximal. On the one hand, because
R
is
\sigma
-elementary, each such subring is contained in every minimal cover of
R
by Lemma
2.2
.
So,
q^{n_{i}}\leqslant\sigma(R)
. On the other hand, the union of all the conjugates
S^{\prime}
cannot cover
T
. To see this, let
A\subseteq T
be the intersection of all
q^{n_{i}}
conjugates of
S_{i}\oplus S_{j}
. Then, the union of all the conjugates covers at most

|J_{ij}|\big(|S_{i}\oplus S_{j}|-|A|\big)+|A|=|T|-|A|\big(|J|-1\big)

elements of
T
. It follows that the union of all the subrings
S^{\prime}\oplus\widehat{S}_{ij}\oplus\widehat{J}_{ij}
cannot cover
R
. Hence,
q^{n_{i}}+1\leqslant\sigma(R)
.

Lastly, by Lemmas
3.4
and
3.5
,
\sigma(R)\leqslant\sigma(T)\leqslant(q^{n_{i}+1}-1)/(q-1)
. When
n_{i}=1
, this forces
\sigma(R)=q+1
and
R=T
because
R
is
\sigma
-elementary.
∎

Recall the definitions for
\text{Irr}(p,d)
and
\tau(q)
stated prior to Theorem
1.3
. It is shown in
[
35
, Theorem 3.5]
that a direct sum
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}\mathbb{F}_{q}
of copies of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is coverable if and only if
t\geqslant\tau(q)
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
q=p^{d}
, let
t\geqslant\tau(q)
, and let
T=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}\mathbb{F}_{q}
. If
d=1
, then
\sigma(T)=\tfrac{1}{2}(p^{2}+p)
. If
d\geqslant 2
, then
\sigma(T)\leqslant q^{2}/(2d)
.

Proof.

As stated in Theorem
1.3
(1), we have

\sigma(T)=\tau(q)\nu(q)+d\displaystyle\binom{\tau(q)}{2},

where
\nu(q)
is equal to 1 if
d=1
, and is equal to the number of prime divisors of
d
otherwise. Via this formula, it is clear that
\sigma(T)=p+\binom{p}{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}(p^{2}+p)
when
d=1
. So, assume that
d\geqslant 2
. Then,
\nu(q)\leqslant\tfrac{d}{2}
, and

(3.8)

\sigma(T)\leqslant\tau(q)\cdot\tfrac{d}{2}+\tfrac{d}{2}\cdot\tau(q)\cdot(\tau(q)-1)=\tfrac{d}{2}\cdot\tau(q)^{2}.

Now, it is well known that
|\text{Irr}(p,d)|\leqslant p^{d}/d
, with equality if and only if
d=1
. When
d\geqslant 2
,
|\text{Irr}(p,d)|<p^{d}/d
, and hence
\tau(q)\leqslant q/d
. Combining this with (
3.8
) produces the desired upper bound for
\sigma(T)
.
∎

4.
Restrictions on
J

Maintain the notation given at the start of Section
3
. Our goal in this section is to prove that when
R
is
\sigma
-elementary, most of the bimodules
J_{ij}
must be zero. In essence, this means that a
\sigma
-elementary ring is “almost” semisimple. We will first examine the bimodules
J_{ij}
with
i\neq j
, and then consider the (
S_{k}
,
S_{k}
)-bimodules
J_{kk}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N
with
i\neq j
. Assume that
J_{ij}\neq\{0\}
and
R
is
\sigma
-elementary. Then, the following hold:

(1)

J_{k\ell}=\{0\}
for all
1\leqslant k,\ell\leqslant N
such that
k\neq\ell
and
(k,\ell)\neq(i,j)
.

(2)

J_{ij}
is a simple (
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
)-bimodule.

(3)

At least one of
S_{i}
or
S_{j}
is a field.

Proof.

Throughout, let
T_{ij}=S_{i}\oplus S_{j}\oplus J_{ij}
and
q=q_{i}\otimes q_{j}
.

(1) Let
I_{ij}
be a maximal subideal of
J_{ij}
. If
S^{\prime}
is a conjugate of
S_{i}\oplus S_{j}
in
T_{ij}
, then
S^{\prime}\oplus I_{ij}
is a maximal subring of
T_{ij}
. Let
\widehat{J}_{ij}
be an ideal complement to
J_{ij}
in
J
, and let

\widehat{S}_{ij}:=\bigoplus_{m\neq i,m\neq j}S_{m},

which is a direct sum complement to
S_{i}\oplus S_{j}
in
S
. Then, for any conjugate
S^{\prime}
of
S_{i}\oplus S_{j}
in
T_{ij}
,

(4.2)

(S^{\prime}\oplus\widehat{S}_{ij})\oplus(I_{ij}\oplus\widehat{J}_{ij})

is a maximal subring of
R
. The number of such subrings is equal to the number of distinct maximal subrings of
T_{ij}
of the form
S^{\prime}\oplus I_{ij}
. Since the size of a simple
(S_{i},S_{j})
-bimodule is
q^{n_{i}n_{j}}
, by Lemma
3.4
(2),
R
contains
|J_{ij}:I_{ij}|=q^{n_{i}n_{j}}
maximal subrings of the form in (
4.2
).

Suppose that
J_{k\ell}\neq\{0\}
for some
(k,\ell)\neq(i,j)
. The arguments of the previous paragraph can be applied to
S_{k}\oplus S_{\ell}\oplus J_{k\ell}
. Employing the analogous notation and letting
q^{\prime}=q_{k}\otimes q_{\ell}
, we see that
R
contains
(q^{\prime})^{n_{k}n_{\ell}}
maximal subrings of the form

(4.3)

(S^{\prime\prime}\oplus\widehat{S}_{k\ell})\oplus(I_{k\ell}\oplus\widehat{J}_{k\ell}),

where
S^{\prime\prime}
is a conjugate of
S_{k}\oplus S_{\ell}
. Moreover, no ring of the form of (
4.2
) can equal a ring of the form of (
4.3
), because
I_{ij}\oplus\widehat{J}_{ij}\neq I_{k\ell}\oplus\widehat{J}_{k\ell}
. By Proposition
3.3
,

(4.4)

q^{n_{i}n_{j}}+(q^{\prime})^{n_{k}n_{\ell}}\leqslant\sigma(R).

Now, by Lemmas
3.4
and
3.5
,

(4.5)

\sigma(R)<\sigma(T_{ij})\leqslant\dfrac{q^{n_{i}n_{j}+1}-1}{q-1}.

Thus, by (
4.4
) and (
4.5
),
q^{n_{i}n_{j}}+(q^{\prime})^{n_{k}n_{\ell}}<(q^{n_{i}n_{j}+1}-1)/(q-1)
. This gives

(q^{\prime})^{n_{k}n_{\ell}}<\dfrac{q^{n_{i}n_{j}+1}-1}{q-1}-q^{n_{i}n_{j}}=\dfrac{q^{n_{i}n_{j}}-1}{q-1}<q^{n_{i}n_{j}}.

However, we also have
\sigma(R)<\sigma(S_{k}\oplus S_{\ell}\oplus J_{k\ell})
. Working as above, this leads to
q^{n_{i}n_{j}}<(q^{\prime})^{n_{k}n_{\ell}}
, a contradiction.

(2) Suppose that
\lambda_{ij}\geqslant 2
. Then,
R
still contains maximal subrings as in (
4.2
). But,
J_{ij}
contains at least two distinct maximal subideals, so we have at least two choices for
I_{ij}
. Thus,
R
contains at least
2q^{n_{i}n_{j}}
maximal subrings of the form of (
4.2
). Appealing again to Lemma
3.5
, we have

2q^{n_{i}n_{j}}\leqslant\sigma(R)<\sigma(T_{ij})\leqslant\dfrac{q^{n_{i}n_{j}+1}-1}{q-1}.

But then,
2q^{n_{i}n_{j}}(q-1)<q^{n_{i}n_{j}+1}-1
, which is impossible. Hence,
\lambda_{ij}=1
and
J_{ij}
is simple.

(3) Suppose that both
S_{i}
and
S_{j}
are noncommutative, i.e., that
n_{i}\geqslant 2
and
n_{j}\geqslant 2
. As in part (1), we obtain
q^{n_{i}n_{j}}\leqslant\sigma(R)
by considering maximal subrings of the form in (
4.2
). Without loss of generality, assume that
n_{i}\leqslant n_{j}
. Since
R
is
\sigma
-elementary,
\sigma(R)<\sigma(R/J)\leqslant\sigma(S_{i})
. But,

\sigma(S_{i})=\sigma(M_{n_{i}}(q_{i}))<q^{n_{i}^{2}}\leqslant q^{n_{i}n_{j}}

a contradiction. Thus, at least one of
S_{i}
or
S_{j}
must be a field.

∎

Remark 4.6
.

Theorem
4.1
holds mutatis mutandis for unital maximal subrings and unital coverings of
R
. This fact will be used later in Section
7
when we examine
\sigma_{u}
-elementary rings.

It remains possible that
R
is
\sigma
-elementary and
J_{ii}\neq\{0\}
for some
i
. Indeed, this case cannot be excluded entirely, because
\mathbb{F}_{q}(+)\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
is
\sigma
-elementary and has a nonzero radical. However, we can restrict the possibilities for
J_{ii}
in this case as well. First, we show that if
R
is
\sigma
-elementary and
S_{i}
is noncommutative, then
J_{ii}=\{0\}
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
and let
T=S_{i}\oplus J_{ii}
. Assume that
n_{i}\geqslant 2
and
J_{ii}\neq\{0\}
. Then,
|J_{ii}\cap Z(T)|=q^{\lambda_{ii}}
.

Proof.

For convenience, let
S_{i}=M_{n}(q)
,
J=J_{ii}
, and
\lambda=\lambda_{ii}
. As in
[
28
, Section 11.8]
,
J
decomposes as a direct sum
\bigoplus_{k=1}^{\lambda}X_{k}
of simple (
M_{n}(q)
,
M_{n}(q)
)-bimodules
X_{1},\ldots,X_{\lambda}
. Each
X_{k}
is equivalent to a simple
M_{n^{2}}(q)
module—hence has order
q^{n^{2}}
—and the isomorphism type of
T
is determined entirely by
\lambda
. Thus, we can represent each
X_{k}
(additively) as a copy of
M_{n}(q)
, and represent
T
in block matrix form as

T=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A&x_{1}&\cdots&x_{\lambda}\\
0&A&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&\\
0&\cdots&0&A\end{pmatrix}:A\in M_{n}(q),x_{k}\in X_{K}\text{ for }1\leqslant k\leqslant\lambda\right\}.

Via this representation, we see that
x\in J\cap Z(T)
if and only if
x=\sum_{k}x_{k}
and each
x_{k}
is an
n\times n
scalar matrix. Thus,
|J\cap Z(T)|=q^{\lambda}
.∎

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
and let
T=S_{i}\oplus J_{ii}
. Assume that
n_{i}\geqslant 2
.

(1)

T
is coverable, and
\sigma(T)=\sigma(M_{n_{i}}(q_{i}))
.

(2)

If
R
is
\sigma
-elementary, then
J_{ii}=\{0\}
.

Proof.

As in earlier proofs, we will drop some subscripts for readability. Let
n=n_{i}
,
q=q_{i}
, and
\lambda=\lambda_{i}
.

(1) The ring
T
is coverable because it is noncommutative. For the covering number of
T
, we use induction on
\lambda
. If
\lambda=0
, then
T=M_{n}(q)
and we are done. So, assume that
\lambda\geqslant 1
and that the covering number of
T^{\prime}:=M_{n}(q)\oplus J^{\prime}
is
\sigma(M_{n}(q))
whenever
J^{\prime}
is an (
M_{n}(q)
,
M_{n}(q)
)-bimodule of length
\lambda-1
.

Consider
M:=S_{i}\oplus I
, where
I
is a maximal
T
-subideal of
J_{ii}
. Note that
I
has length
\lambda-1
, so
\sigma(M)=\sigma(M_{n}(q))
and
|J_{ii}:I|=q^{n^{2}}
. On the one hand,
I
has an ideal complement in
J_{ii}
, so
M
occurs as a residue ring of
T
and
\sigma(T)\leqslant\sigma(M)
. On the other hand, by Lemmas
3.2
(3) and
4.7
, the number of conjugates of
M
in
T
is

|J_{ii}:(I+(J_{ii}\cap Z(T)))|=|J_{ii}:I|\cdot\frac{|I\cap Z(T)|}{|J_{ii}\cap Z(T)|}=q^{n^{2}}\cdot\frac{q^{\lambda-1}}{q^{\lambda}}=q^{n^{2}-1}.

By
[
33
, Lemma 3.4]
,
\sigma(M)\leqslant q^{n^{2}/2}
, so any minimal cover of
T
excludes some conjugate of
M
. Hence,
\sigma(M)\leqslant\sigma(T)
by Lemma
2.2
(1).

(2) Suppose that
R
is
\sigma
-elementary, but
J_{ii}\neq\{0\}
. Let
I
be a maximal subideal of
J_{ii}
, let
\widehat{J}_{ii}
be an ideal complement for
J_{ii}
in
J
, and let
\widehat{S}_{i}=\bigoplus_{k\neq i}S_{k}
. Then,
S_{i}\oplus I
is maximal in
T
, so

M:=(S_{i}\oplus\widehat{S}_{i})\oplus(I\oplus\widehat{J}_{ii})

is a maximal subring of
R
. As noted earlier, the number of conjugates of
S_{i}\oplus I
in
T
is
q^{n^{2}-1}
. So,
R
contains
q^{n^{2}-1}
such maximal subrings
M
. Since

\sigma(R)<\sigma(S_{i})=\sigma(M_{n}(q))<q^{n^{2}-1},

every minimal cover of
R
excludes at least one
M
. By Lemma
2.2
(1),
\sigma(M)\leqslant\sigma(R)
. But,
M
occurs as a residue ring of
R
, so
\sigma(R)<\sigma(M)
, a contradiction.
∎

Next, we consider
J_{kk}
when
S_{k}
is a field. We will prove that for
R
to be
\sigma
-elementary in this case, it is necessary that
\lambda_{kk}\geqslant 2
; and, if
J_{ij}\neq\{0\}
for some
i\neq j
, then
J_{kk}=\{0\}
whenever
S_{k}
is isomorphic to
S_{i}
or
S_{j}
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
1\leqslant k\leqslant N
. Assume that
S_{k}
is a field and that
J_{kk}
is a simple (
S_{k}
,
S_{k}
)-bimodule.

(1)

R
contains a unique maximal subring that does not contain
J_{kk}
.

(2)

\sigma(R)=\sigma(R/J_{kk})
and
R
is not
\sigma
-elementary.

Proof.

(1) First, we note that
J_{kk}\subseteq Z(R)
. Indeed, suppose that
x\in J_{kk}
and
s\in S
. Write
s=\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}
, where each
s_{i}\in S_{i}
. Then,
sx=s_{k}x
. But,
S_{k}\oplus J_{kk}
is commutative, so
s_{k}x=xs_{k}=xs
.

Next, let
\widehat{J}_{kk}
be an ideal complement for
J_{kk}
in
J
, and let
T=S\oplus J_{kk}
. Then,
T
is a maximal subring of
R
because
J_{kk}
is simple. Let
T^{\prime}
be any maximal subring of
R
that does not contain
J_{kk}
. By Lemma
2.6
,
T^{\prime}=S^{1+y}\oplus I
for some
y\in J
and some maximal subideal
I
of
J
.

We claim that
I=\widehat{J}_{kk}
. To see this, apply the Peirce decomposition to
I
to get

I=(e_{k}Ie_{k})\oplus\Big(\bigoplus_{(i,j)\neq(k,k)}e_{i}Ie_{j}\Big)=(I\cap J_{kk})\oplus(I\cap\widehat{J}_{kk}).

Since
J_{kk}
is simple and
J_{kk}\not\subseteq I
,
I\cap J_{kk}=\{0\}
. Then, by maximality,
I=\widehat{J}_{kk}
.

Now, let
\alpha\in J_{kk}
and
\beta\in\widehat{J}_{kk}
be such that
y=\alpha+\beta
. Since
\alpha
is central, conjugation by
1+\alpha
fixes elements of
S
pointwise. Thus,

S^{1+y}=(S^{1+\alpha})^{1+\beta}=S^{1+\beta}\subseteq S\oplus\widehat{J}_{kk}

and so
T^{\prime}=T
.

(2) As in part (1), let
\widehat{J}_{kk}
be the ideal complement for
J_{kk}
in
J
and let
T:=S\oplus\widehat{J}_{kk}
be the unique maximal subring of
R
that does not contain
J_{kk}
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a minimal cover of
R
, and let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
be all the maximal subrings in
\mathcal{C}
that contain
J_{kk}
.

Note that
R=T\oplus J_{kk}
. Let
a\in T
, let
x\in J_{kk}\setminus\{0\}
, and consider
a+x
. Since
S_{k}\subseteq T
and
J_{kk}
is simple, if
x\in T
then
S_{k}\cdot x=J_{kk}\subseteq T
, a contradiction. So,
x\notin T
, and hence
a+x\notin T
as well. Consequently,
a+x\in\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{m}A_{\ell}
. This holds for all
a\in T
, so
T+x\subseteq\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{m}A_{\ell}
. Recalling that
J_{kk}\subseteq A_{\ell}
for each
\ell
, we see that
\{A_{1}/J_{kk},\ldots,A_{m}/J_{kk}\}
is a cover of
R/J_{kk}\cong T
. Thus,
\sigma(R/J_{kk})\leqslant m\leqslant\sigma(R)
, and so in fact
\sigma(R)=\sigma(R/J_{kk})
.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

We note that Lemma
4.9
holds mutatis mutandis for unital maximal subrings and unital coverings of

. As with Theorem
4.1
, this will become relevant in Section

.

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N
such that
i\neq j
. Assume that
J_{ij}
is a simple (
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
)-bimodule,
S_{j}
is a field, and
R
is
\sigma
-elementary. Then, for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant N
, if
S_{k}\cong S_{j}
, then
J_{kk}=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Assume
1\leqslant k\leqslant N
is such that
S_{k}\cong S_{j}
, and suppose that
J_{kk}\neq\{0\}
. Since
R
is
\sigma
-elementary,
J_{kk}
cannot be simple by Lemma
4.9
. So,
\lambda_{kk}\geqslant 2
.

Now,
S_{k}\oplus J_{kk}
is a commutative ring. Since
\lambda_{kk}\geqslant 2
and

, the ring
S_{k}\oplus J_{kk}
has

as a residue ring. By
[

, Theorem 4.8(2)]
,

Moreover,

occurs as a residue ring of

by Lemma

(4), so

. However, letting

and applying Lemma

yields

which is a contradiction. Thus,

.
∎

Based on our work so far, it remains possible that

and

for some field

that is isomorphic to neither

nor

. Ultimately, this case is ruled out by our classification of

-elementary rings in Theorem

. Rather than attack this case directly, however, we merely note that

is always a commutative ring. The conclusion that

will be reached later by referencing the classification of commutative

-elementary rings from

]
.

5.

Continue to use the notations of Sections
3
and
4
. At this point, we know that in a
\sigma
-elementary ring, the radical has a restricted structure. There is at most one pair
(i,j)
with

and

, and if

, then

must be a field not isomorphic to

or

. We can also place restrictions on the direct summands of

. In Proposition

, we show that if
S_{i}
is noncommutative, then
S_{j}\not\cong S_{i}
for all

. A closely related result holds for a field

(Proposition

): if
J_{ij}\neq\{0\}
with

and

is a field, then
S_{k}\not\cong S_{i}
for all

.

We begin by recalling some results from
[
27
]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
A=M_{n}(q)
.

(1)

[
27
, Proposition 4.2]
Let
\phi
be an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-automorphism of
A
. Then,
M:=\{a+\phi(a):a\in A\}
is a maximal subring of
A\oplus A
, and
M\cong A
.

(2)

[
27
, Lemma 5.5]
If
n\geqslant 2
, then the number of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-automorphisms of

is greater than
\sigma(A)
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
. Assume that
J_{ij}=J_{ji}=\{0\}
for all
j\neq i
. Then,
S_{i}\oplus J_{ii}
is a two-sided ideal of
R
.

Proof.

Let
R_{1}:=S_{i}\oplus J_{ii}
and

R_{2}:=\Big(\bigoplus_{j\neq i}S_{j}\Big)\oplus\Big(\bigoplus_{(j,k)\neq(i,i)}J_{jk}\Big),

so that
R=R_{1}\oplus R_{2}
. By assumption,
J_{ij}=\{0\}=J_{ji}
for all
j\neq i
. So,

R_{2}=\Big(\bigoplus_{j\neq i}S_{j}\Big)\oplus\Big(\bigoplus_{j\neq i,k\neq i}J_{jk}\Big).

This means that
r_{1}r_{2}=0=r_{2}r_{1}
for all
r_{1}\in R_{1}
and
r_{2}\in R_{2}
. It follows that
R_{1}
is a two-sided ideal of
R
.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N
such that
n_{i}\geqslant 2
. If
R
is
\sigma
-elementary, then
S_{i}\not\cong S_{j}
for all
j\neq i
.

Proof.

Assume that
R
is
\sigma
-elementary, but there exists
j\neq i
such that
S_{j}\cong S_{i}
. By Theorem
4.1
, there is at most one pair
(k,\ell)
with
k\neq\ell
and
J_{k\ell}\neq\{0\}
, and we must have
J_{ij}=J_{ji}=\{0\}
because
S_{i}
and
S_{j}
are noncommutative. Also,
J_{ii}=J_{jj}=\{0\}
by Proposition
4.8
. It follows that either
J_{ik}=J_{ki}=\{0\}
for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant N
, or
J_{jk}=J_{kj}=\{0\}
for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant N
. Assume without loss of generality that the latter condition holds. Then,
S_{j}
is a two-sided ideal of
R
by Lemma
5.2
.

For any
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebra isomorphism
\phi:S_{i}\to S_{j}
, let
M_{\phi}
be the maximal subring
M_{\phi}:=\{a+\phi(a):a\in S_{i}\}
of
S_{i}\oplus S_{j}
. Note that
M_{\phi_{1}}=M_{\phi_{2}}
if and only if
\phi_{1}=\phi_{2}
. Let

T:=\Big(\bigoplus_{k\neq i,k\neq j}S_{k}\Big)\oplus M_{\phi}\oplus J,

which is maximal subring of
R
. Furthermore, since
M_{\phi}\cong S_{i}\cong S_{j}
, we have

T\cong\Big(\bigoplus_{k\neq j}S_{k}\Big)\oplus J\cong R/S_{j},

and so
\sigma(R)<\sigma(R/S_{j})=\sigma(T)
.

Now, let
m
be the number of maximal subrings of
R
isomorphic to
T
. Then,
m
is greater than or equal to the number of choices for
M_{\phi}
, which by Lemma
5.1
(2) is greater than
\sigma(M_{n_{i}}(q_{i}))=\sigma(S_{i})
. Since
R
has a residue ring isomorphic to
S_{i}
, we have
\sigma(R)<\sigma(S_{i})<m
. Hence, any minimal cover of
R
omits some maximal subring

isomorphic to
T
. Applying Lemma

(1), we have
\sigma(T)=\sigma(T^{\prime})\leqslant\sigma(R)
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Ruling out fields isomorphic to
S_{j}
is more difficult. By

, Theorem 3.5]
,

is coverable if and only if

, where

is as defined in the introduction. When

is not coverable, it is generated (as a ring) by a single element
\alpha=\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}\alpha_{\ell}
. By
[

, Proposition 3.4]
,
\alpha
generates
T
if and only if each
\alpha_{\ell}
is a primitive element for
\mathbb{F}_{q}
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
(if

, then any nonzero element of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
is primitive) and each
\alpha_{\ell}
has a distinct minimal polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

We also require knowledge about the maximal subrings of

. These subrings were classified in
[

.

.

[
27
, Definition 4.3]

Let

.

[

.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N
with
i\neq j
. Assume that
J_{ij}\neq\{0\}
and
S_{j}
is a field. If
R
is
\sigma
-elementary, then
S_{k}\not\cong S_{j}
for all
k\neq i,j
.

Proof.

Assume that
R
is
\sigma
-elementary. By Theorem
4.1
,
J_{ij}
is a simple (
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
)-bimodule. Let
\mathscr{T}:=\{1\leqslant k\leqslant N:k\neq i,k\neq j,S_{k}\cong S_{j}\}
. Suppose that
\mathscr{T}\neq\varnothing
, let
t=|\mathscr{T}|
, and let
T=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathscr{T}}S_{k}
. From Theorem
4.1
, we know that
J_{k\ell}=J_{\ell k}=\{0\}
whenever
k\in\mathscr{T}
and
\ell\neq k
. Moreover, by Proposition
4.11
,
J_{kk}=\{0\}
for all
k\in\mathscr{T}
. Hence, by Lemma
5.2
, each
S_{k}
—and therefore
T
—is a two-sided ideal of

.

Let
q_{j}=p^{d_{j}}
and
q=q_{i}\otimes q_{j}
. If
n_{i}=1
, then
R=S_{i}\oplus S_{j}\oplus J_{ij}
by Lemma
3.6
, and there is nothing to prove because
\mathscr{T}=\varnothing
. So, we may assume that
n_{i}\geqslant 2
.

We break the proof into two cases, depending on whether
t<\tau(q_{j})-1
or
t\geqslant\tau(q_{j})-1
. The latter case is easier, so we will deal with that first.

When
t\geqslant\tau(q_{j})-1
,
S_{j}\oplus T\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t+1}\mathbb{F}_{q_{j}}
is coverable, and
\sigma(R)<\sigma(S_{j}\oplus T)
since
S_{j}\oplus T
occurs as a residue ring of

. By Lemma
3.7
,

(5.7)

\sigma(R)<\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{2}(p^{2}+p),&d_{j}=1\\
q_{j}^{2}/(2d_{j}),&d_{j}\geqslant 2\end{cases}.

However, by Lemma
3.6
,
q_{j}^{2}+1\leqslant q^{n_{i}}+1\leqslant\sigma(R)
. This is not compatible with (
5.7
), since neither
p^{2}+1<\tfrac{1}{2}(p^{2}+p)
nor
q_{j}^{2}+1<q_{j}^{2}/(2d_{j})
is a valid inequality.

For the remainder of the proof, assume that
t<\tau(q_{j})-1
. Define
\widehat{T}
to be
\widehat{T}:=\bigoplus_{\ell\notin\mathscr{T}}S_{\ell}
. Then,
R=(\widehat{T}\oplus J)\oplus T
.

We will sort the maximal subrings of
R
into three classes
\mathcal{C}_{1}
,
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{3}
. Let
\mathcal{C}_{1}
be the collection of all maximal subrings of
R
that contain
T
. Note that these maximal subrings are all inverse images of maximal subrings of
R/T
.

Next, suppose
M\subseteq R
is maximal with
T\not\subseteq M
. If
J\not\subseteq M
, then
M=S^{\prime}\oplus I
for some
S^{\prime}\in\mathscr{S}(R)
and some maximal subideal
I\subseteq J
. However, as noted earlier,
J_{k\ell}=J_{\ell k}=\{0\}
for all
1\leqslant\ell\leqslant N
and all
k\in\mathscr{T}
. So, the action of
T
on
J
is trivial. It follows that
T\subseteq S^{\prime}
for all
S^{\prime}\in\mathscr{S}(R)
, and hence
J
must be contained in
M
.

Since
J\subseteq M
and
T\not\subseteq M
,
M
is the inverse image of a maximal subring of
R/J
that does not contain
T
. In light of Lemma
5.5
, we can partition such subrings into two classes. Use a bar to denote passage from
R
to
R/J
. Let
\mathcal{C}_{2}
be the set of all subrings of the form
\widehat{T}\oplus J\oplus M^{\prime}
, where

is maximal in

. Subrings in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
are those where

is not linked to any

,

. Finally, let
\mathcal{C}_{3}
be the collection of maximal subrings where
\overline{S_{j}}
is linked to some
\overline{S_{k}}
with
k\in\mathscr{T}
.

Now, since
t<\tau(q_{j})
,

is not coverable. Next, we will show that for all

, we can find
\alpha\in T
such that
\alpha
generates

(as a ring), and

\mathcal{C}_{2}
or
\mathcal{C}_{3}
. This is easy to see with regard to

as above, if

, then
\alpha\in M^{\prime}
for some maximal subring

of

. But, this is impossible because

generates
T

For

\widehat{S}_{j}
be an additive complement to

in
\widehat{T}
. Write

and

. Since

,

is less than or equal to the number of irreducible polynomials in

of degree

. So, for each

, we can find
\alpha_{k}\in S_{k}
such that
\alpha_{k}
generates
S_{k}
; each
\alpha_{k}
has a distinct minimal polynomial over

; and no
\alpha_{k}
has the same minimal polynomial as

. Then, for every

. We conclude that

is linked to some

.

We have shown that for every

. Now, consider a minimal cover

of

Proof of Theorem

We now have all the ingredients necessary to prove Theorem

-elementary ring with unity. The idea of the proof is to write

Proposition 6.2

Assume

.

We may assume that

7.
Rings without unity and covers by unital subrings

Theorem
1.3
provides a complete classification of
\sigma
-elementary rings with unity, which in turn allows us to describe all the integers that occur as covering numbers of such rings. We now examine two related covering problems. First, what can be said of covering numbers for rings without a multiplicative identity? Second, how do our results change if we insist that each subring in a cover of a unital ring
R
must contain
1_{R}
? As shown in Theorem
1.5
, these two problems are closely related, and an answer to the second question provides an answer to the first.

Recall the definitions of
\sigma_{u}
and
\sigma_{u}
-elementary given in Definition
1.4
. It is clear that parts (3) and (4) of Lemma
2.1
carry over to coverings of
R
by unital subrings: for any two-sided ideal of
R
, we have
\sigma_{u}(R)\leqslant\sigma_{u}(R/I)
; and when
R
is coverable by unital subrings that are contained in maximal subrings, we may assume that each subring in a minimal cover is maximal. Going forward, we will make free and frequent use of these properties.

It is well known that any ring without unity can be embedded in a larger ring that contains a multiplicative identity. Using this construction, we will connect covers of rings without unity to covers of unital rings by unital subrings and prove Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

We first proceed as in
[
32
, Section 3]
and reduce to the case where
R
is finite and has prime power order. By results of Neumann
[
26
, Lemma 4.1,4.4]
and Lewin
[
22
, Lemma 1]
,
R
contains a two-sided ideal
I
of finite index such that
\sigma(R)=\sigma(R/I)
. Hence, we may assume that
R
is finite. Next, the Chinese Remainder Theorem still applies to finite rings without identity, so
R\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}R_{i}
, where each
R_{i}
is a ring and
|R_{i}|=p_{i}^{n_{i}}
for some distinct primes
p_{1},\ldots,p_{t}
. Lemma
2.3
holds for rings without unity, so
\sigma(R)=\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant t}\{\sigma(R_{i})\}
. Thus, we may assume that
|R|=p^{n}
for some prime
p
and some
n\geqslant 1
.

Now, as in
[
32
, Lemma 3.2, Proposition 3.3]
, one may prove that
pR
is contained in every maximal subring of
R
. It follows that
\sigma(R)=\sigma(R/pR)
, and hence we may assume that
pR=\{0\}
. From here, we will embed
R
in a unital ring
R^{\prime}
that has characteristic
p
.

Let
R^{\prime}:=\mathbb{F}_{p}\times R
with multiplication given by the rule:

(n_{1},r_{1})(n_{2},r_{2}):=(n_{1}n_{2},n_{1}r_{2}+n_{2}r_{1}+r_{1}r_{2}).

Notice that, for any
(n,r)\in R^{\prime}
,
(1,0)(n,r)=(n,r)(1,0)=(n,r)
, so
R^{\prime}
has a multiplicative identity. We claim that
\sigma_{u}(R^{\prime})=\sigma(R)
.

Indeed, let
\mathcal{C}
be a minimal cover of
R
by subrings. Given
S\in\mathcal{C}
, define
\mathbb{F}_{p}\times S:=\{(n,s)\in R^{\prime}:s\in S\}
, which is a unital subring of
R^{\prime}
, and is proper in
R^{\prime}
because
S\subsetneq R
. Let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}:=\{\mathbb{F}_{p}\times S:S\in\mathcal{C}\}
. If
(n,r)\in R^{\prime}
, then
r\in S
for some
S\in\mathcal{C}
, so
(n,r)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\times S\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. Hence,
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is a cover of
R^{\prime}
by unital subrings, and so
\sigma_{u}(R^{\prime})\leqslant|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}|=|\mathcal{C}|=\sigma(R).

Next, let
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
be a minimal cover of
R^{\prime}
by unital subrings, which we now know must exist. Let
\rho:R^{\prime}\to R
be the projection map defined by
\rho((n,r)):=r
. The map
\rho
is not multiplicative, but
\rho(S^{\prime})
is a proper subring of
R
for any
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
. To see this, let
r_{1},r_{2}\in\rho(S^{\prime})
. Then,
(n_{1},r_{1}),(n_{2},r_{2})\in S^{\prime}
for some
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Because
(1,0)\in S^{\prime}
, both
(n_{1},0)
and
(n_{2},0)
are in
S^{\prime}
. Thus,
S^{\prime}
contains both
(0,r_{1})
and
(0,r_{2})
, and hence
r_{1}+r_{2},r_{1}r_{2}\in\rho(S^{\prime})
. Moreover,
\rho(S^{\prime})\neq R
, because if not, then
\{0\}\times R\subseteq S^{\prime}
and
(1,0)\in S^{\prime}
, which implies that
S^{\prime}=R^{\prime}
.

Finally, given
r\in R
, there exists
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
such that
(0,r)\in S^{\prime}
. Hence,
r\in\phi(S^{\prime})
and
\{\phi(S^{\prime}):S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\}
is a cover of
R
of size at most
|\mathcal{U}^{\prime}|
. Consequently,
\sigma(R)\leqslant|\mathcal{U}^{\prime}|=\sigma_{u}(R)
, completing the proof.
∎

In light of Theorem
1.5
, determining the covering number of any ring (with or without unity) reduces to the case of a unital ring

, for which we may consider either covers by subrings (which need not contain

) or covers by unital subrings. These problems can in turn be solved by classifying
\sigma
-elementary and
\sigma_{u}
-elementary rings. The classification of

-elementary rings has been done in Theorem

-elementary rings, with the greatest discrepancy occurring in the commutative semisimple case.

Lemma 7.1

Let

\sigma(R)\leqslant\sigma_{u}(R)

Proof.

Note that every unital subring is a subring (but not conversely).

, define
J_{ij}:=e_{i}Je_{j}
to be the
(S_{i},S_{j})
-bimodule obtained via a Peirce decomposition of
J
. Note that
J=\bigoplus_{i,j}J_{ij}
. Let
\lambda_{ij}
be the length of
J_{ij}
as an
(S_{i},S_{j})
-bimodule; if
J_{ij}=\{0\}
, then we take
\lambda_{ij}=0
.

As in Section
4
, our strategy is to show that in a
\sigma_{u}
-elementary ring, most of the bimodules
J_{ij}
must be zero. Much of the work done earlier in this paper still holds when stated only for unital subrings or
\sigma_{u}
-elementary rings. As noted earlier, Theorem
4.1
, and Lemma
4.9
hold mutatis mutandis for unital maximal subrings and
\sigma
-elementary rings (see Remarks
4.6
and
4.10
). In particular, we have the following structural results on a
\sigma_{u}
-elementary ring
R
.

Lemma 7.5
.

Let
R
be as in Notation
7.4
.

(1)

If
R
is
\sigma_{u}
-elementary, then there is at most one pair
(i,j)
with
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N
and
J_{ij}\neq\{0\}
. For this pair
(i,j)
, if
J_{ij}\neq\{0\}
, then
\lambda_{ij}=1
.

(2)

For all
1\leqslant k\leqslant N
, if
S_{k}
is a field and
\lambda_{kk}=1
, then
R
is not
\sigma_{u}
-elementary.

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
R
be a
\sigma_{u}
-elementary ring that is not
\sigma
-elementary.

(1)

There exists a two-sided ideal
I
of
R
such that
J\subseteq I
and
R/I\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

(2)

\sigma(R)=p+\binom{p}{2}
.

(3)

If
R
has a residue ring isomorphic to
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p+1}\mathbb{F}_{p}
, then
R=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p+1}\mathbb{F}_{p}
and
\sigma_{u}(R)=p+\binom{p}{2}
.

Proof.

(1) Since
R
is not
\sigma
-elementary, it projects nontrivially onto a
\sigma
-elementary ring
R/I
such that
\sigma(R)=\sigma(R/I)
. By Proposition
7.3
, all
\sigma
-elementary rings are
\sigma_{u}
-elementary except for
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{F}_{p}
. If
R/I\not\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{F}_{p}
, then
\sigma_{u}(R/I)=\sigma(R/I)=\sigma(R)\leqslant\sigma_{u}(R)
, a contradiction to
R
being
\sigma_{u}
-elementary. Thus, there exists an ideal
I
of
R
such that
R/I\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
Since
R/I
is semisimple,
J\subseteq I
.

(2) With
I
as in part (1), we have
\sigma(R)=\sigma(R/I)=p+\binom{p}{2}
.

(3) Assume that
R
has a residue ring isomorphic to
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p+1}\mathbb{F}_{p}
. By Lemma
7.2
,
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p+1}\mathbb{F}_{p}
is
\sigma_{u}
-elementary and has unital covering number
p+\binom{p}{2}
. By Lemma
7.1
and part (2),
p+\binom{p}{2}=\sigma(R)\leqslant\sigma_{u}(R).
Since
R
is
\sigma_{u}
-elementary, we must have
R=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p+1}\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
∎

Using Lemma
7.6
, we can impose another restriction on
R
and assume that
R
has a residue ring isomorphic to
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{F}_{p}
, but does not have a residue ring isomorphic to
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p+1}\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Thus, we may assume that
S
has the form

(7.7)

S=\Big(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}S_{i}\Big)\oplus\Big(\bigoplus_{i=p+1}^{N}S_{i}\Big),

where
S_{i}\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant p
, and
S_{i}\not\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}
for all
p+1\leqslant i\leqslant N
.

Lemma 7.8
.

Let
S
be as in (
7.7
) and let
M
be a maximal unital subring of
S
.

(1)

If
M
is Type
\Pi_{1}
, then
M
contains
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}S_{i}
.

(2)

If
M
does not contain
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}S_{i}
, then
M
is Type
\Pi_{2}
with
S_{i}
linked to
S_{j}
for some
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant p
.

Proof.

(1) Assume that
M
is Type
\Pi_{1}
. Then, for some
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
,

M=S_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus S_{i-1}\oplus M_{i}\oplus S_{i+1}\oplus\cdots\oplus S_{N},

where
M_{i}
is a maximal subring of
S_{i}
. Suppose that
1\leqslant i\leqslant p
. Then,
M_{i}=\{0\}
because
S_{i}\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}
. But then,
1_{R}\notin M
, a contradiction. So,
i\geqslant p+1
, and
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}S_{i}\subseteq M
.

(2) If
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}S_{i}
is not a subring of
M
, then by part (1)
M
must be Type
\Pi_{2}
. In this case, the only way that
M
will fail to contain
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}S_{i}
is if
S_{i}
is linked to
S_{j}
for some
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant p
.
∎

Proposition 7.9
.

Let
R
be as Notation
7.4
, and let
S
be as in (
7.7
). Assume that
R
is
\sigma_{u}
-elementary, but not
\sigma
-elementary.

(1)

\lambda_{ii}=0
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant p
.

(2)

J_{ij}=\{0\}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant p
and all
p+1\leqslant j\leqslant N
.

Proof.

(1) Suppose first that
\lambda_{ii}\geqslant 2
for some
1\leqslant i\leqslant p
. Then,
S_{i}\oplus J_{ii}\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}(+)\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\lambda_{ii}}
(the idealization of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
with the
\lambda_{ii}
-dimensional vector space
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\lambda_{ii}}
), which projects onto
\mathbb{F}_{p}(+)\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
. By Lemma
3.2
(4),
R
has a residue ring isomorphic to
\mathbb{F}_{p}(+)\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
. Using this and Lemmas
7.1
and
7.6
, we get

p+\textstyle\binom{p}{2}\leqslant\sigma_{u}(R)\leqslant\sigma_{u}(\mathbb{F}_{p}(+)\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2})=p+1.

Since
R
is
\sigma_{u}
-elementary, this forces
R\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}(+)\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
. But, this contradicts the fact that
R
is not
\sigma
-elementary.

So, we must have
\lambda_{ii}\leqslant 1
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant p
. However, by Lemma
7.5
(2), if
\lambda_{ii}=1
for any
1\leqslant i\leqslant p
, then
R
is not
\sigma_{u}
-elementary. We conclude that
\lambda_{ii}=0
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant p
.

(2) Suppose by way of contradiction that
J_{k\ell}\neq\{0\}
for some
1\leqslant k\leqslant p
and
p+1\leqslant\ell\leqslant N
. By Lemma
7.5
(1), there is at most one pair
(i,j)
with
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N
and
J_{ij}\neq\{0\}
. Thus,
(k,\ell)
is unique, and
J_{k\ell}
is a simple
(S_{k},S_{\ell})
-bimodule.

Assume without loss of generality that
k=p
. Let
T=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p-1}S_{i}\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p-1}\mathbb{F}_{p}
. We claim that
T
is a two-sided ideal of
R
. To see this, note that by part (1),
J_{ii}=\{0\}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant p-1
. Hence,
J=J_{p\ell}\oplus\big(\bigoplus_{i=p+1}^{N}J_{ii}\big)
. Let
\widehat{T}=\big(\bigoplus_{i=p}^{N}S_{i}\big)\oplus J
. Then,
R=T\oplus\widehat{T}
, and elements from
T
and
\widehat{T}
mutually annihilate one another. Thus,
T
is an ideal of
R
. Moreover, the action of
1+J
on
T
is trivial, so
T
is contained in
S^{\prime}
for all
S^{\prime}\in\mathscr{S}(R)
. We will show that
\sigma_{u}(R)=\sigma_{u}(R/T)
, which contradicts the fact that
R
is
\sigma_{u}
-elementary.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{1}
be the set of all unital maximal subrings of
R
that contain
T
, and let
\mathcal{C}_{2}
be the set of all unital maximal subrings that do not. Let
M\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
, and suppose that
J\not\subseteq M
. Then, by
[
32
, Theorem 3.10]
,
M=S^{\prime}\oplus I
for some
S^{\prime}\in\mathscr{S}(R)
and some maximal subideal
I\subseteq J
. But then,
T\subseteq S^{\prime}\subseteq M
, a contradiction. So, each subring in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
contains
J
. Use a bar to denote passage to
R/J\cong S
. By Lemma
7.8
,
\overline{M}
is Type
\Pi_{2}
with
\overline{S}_{i}
linked to
\overline{S}_{j}
for some
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant p
, and this is true for every
M\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
.

From here, we will show that for each
a\in\widehat{T}\cong R/T
, there exists
\alpha\in T
such that
a+\alpha
is in a maximal subring containing
T
. Let
a\in\widehat{T}
, and write
a
as
a=\alpha_{p}+b
, where
\alpha_{p}\in S_{p}
and
b\in\big(\bigoplus_{i=p+1}^{N}S_{i}\big)\oplus J
. Note that
\alpha_{p}
is equal to an element of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p-1}
be all of the elements of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
that are not equal to
\alpha_{p}
. Considering
\alpha_{i}
as an element of
S_{i}
for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant p-1
, we take
\alpha:=\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}\alpha_{i}\in T
.

Certainly,
a+\alpha
lies in some unital maximal subring
M
of
R
. Suppose that
M\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
. As noted above,
\overline{M}
is Type
\Pi_{2}
with
\overline{S}_{i}
linked to
\overline{S}_{j}
for some
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant p
. Since the only automorphism of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
is the identity, this means that
\overline{\alpha_{i}}=\overline{\alpha_{j}}
. However, this is impossible, because
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p}
are all distinct elements of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Thus,
a+\alpha
lies in some subring in
\mathcal{C}_{1}
.

To complete the proof, let
\mathcal{C}
be a minimal cover of
R
by unital subrings, and let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{C}_{1}
. Then, each subring in
\mathcal{A}
contains
T
, and their images in
R/T
cover
R/T\cong\widehat{T}
. Hence,
\sigma_{u}(R/T)\leqslant|\mathcal{A}|\leqslant\sigma_{u}(R)
, which contradicts the fact that
R
is
\sigma_{u}
-elementary.
∎

Proposition 7.10
.

Let
R
be as described prior to Lemma
7.6
, and let
S
be as in (
7.7
). Assume that
R
is
\sigma_{u}
-elementary, but not
\sigma
-elementary. Then,

R=\Big(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}S_{i}\Big)\oplus\Big(\bigoplus_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant p}J_{ij}\Big).

Proof.

By Proposition
7.9
, we know that
J_{ij}=\{0\}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant p
and
p+1\leqslant j\leqslant N
. Decompose
R
as
R=R_{1}\oplus R_{2}
, where

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle=\Big(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}S_{i}\Big)\oplus\Big(\bigoplus_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant p}J_{ij}\Big),\text{ and }

\displaystyle R_{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(\bigoplus_{i=p+1}^{N}S_{i}\Big)\oplus\Big(\bigoplus_{p+1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}J_{ij}\Big).

We claim that all unital maximal subrings of
R
respect this decomposition. That is, if
M
is a unital maximal subring of
R
, then either
M=M_{1}\oplus R_{2}
with
M_{1}
maximal in
R_{1}
, or
M=R_{1}\oplus M_{2}
with
M_{2}
maximal in
R_{2}
. When
J\not\subseteq M
, this can be shown just as in the proof of Proposition
6.2
. When
J\subseteq M
,
M=T\oplus J
for some maximal subring
T
of
S
. In this case, the only way that
M
would not respect the direct sum decomposition of
R
is if
T
were Type
\Pi_{2}
with
S_{i}
linked to
S_{j}
for some
1\leqslant i\leqslant p
and
p+1\leqslant j\leqslant N
. However, this is impossible, because
S_{j}\not\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}
for all
p+1\leqslant j\leqslant N
.

Since every maximal subring of
R
has the form
M_{1}\oplus R_{2}
or
R_{1}\oplus M_{2}
, we can apply Lemma
2.3
, which yields
\sigma_{u}(R)=\min\{\sigma_{u}(R_{1}),\sigma_{u}(R_{2})\}
. Since
R
is
\sigma_{u}
-elementary, this means that either
R=R_{1}
or
R=R_{2}
. Because only
R_{1}
has
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{F}_{p}
as a residue ring, we conclude that
R=R_{1}
.
∎

Proposition 7.11
.

Let
R
be a
\sigma_{u}
-elementary ring that is not
\sigma
-elementary, and such that
R\not\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p+1}\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Then,
R\cong A(1,2,2)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
7.6
,
R
has no residue ring isomorphic to
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p+1}\mathbb{F}_{p}
. By Proposition
7.10
,
R=\big(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{F}_{p}\big)\oplus J
, and
J\neq\{0\}
because
R
is coverable by unital subrings. By Proposition
7.9
,
J_{ii}=\{0\}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant p
. Moreover, by Lemma
7.5
(1), there is a unique pair
(i,j)
with
i\neq j
such that
J_{ij}\neq\{0\}
, and
\lambda_{ij}=1
.

Thus,
S_{i}\oplus S_{j}\oplus J_{ij}\cong A(1,p,p)
, which has unital covering number
p+1
. By
3.2
,
A(1,p,p)
occurs as a residue ring of
R
. Thus,
p+\binom{p}{2}\leqslant\sigma_{u}(R)\leqslant p+1
, which forces
p=2
and
\sigma_{u}(R)=3
. Since
R
is
\sigma_{u}
-elementary, we must have
R=S_{i}\oplus S_{j}\oplus J_{ij}\cong A(1,2,2)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Apply Proposition
7.3
, Lemma
7.6
, and Proposition
7.11
. The covering numbers for
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p+1}\mathbb{F}_{p}
and
A(1,2,2)
were found in Lemma
7.2
.
∎

8.
Bounds on the number of integers that are covering numbers of rings

This section is dedicated to the proof of Corollary
1.9
. Recall that

\mathscr{E}(N):=\{m:m\leqslant N,\sigma(R)=m\text{ for some ring }R\}.

Let
\log x
denote the binary (base 2) logarithm. Our goal is to show that
|\mathscr{E}(N)|
is bounded above by
cN/\log N
for some positive constant
c
. Rather than attempting to find an optimal value for
c
, we will be content with a value that is easy to verify.

Let
\pi
be the prime counting function, which counts the number of primes less than or equal to a given positive number. The following bounds on
\pi(x)
will be useful in later estimates.

Lemma 8.1
.

For all
x>1
,
\pi(x)<2x/\log x
. For all
x>5
,
x/\log x<\pi(x)
.

Proof.

Let
\ln x
denote the natural logarithm. By
[
29
, Corollary 1]
, we have
\pi(x)<(1.25506x)/(\ln x)
when
x>1
, and
\pi(x)>x/\ln(x)
when
x\geqslant 17
. The remaining cases for the lower bound follow from inspection.
∎

Let
\Pi
be the prime power counting function, i.e.,

\Pi(x):=|\{m\in\mathbb{N}:2\leqslant m\leqslant x\text{ and }m\text{ is a prime power}\}|.

Using Lemma
8.1
, it is not difficult to get an upper bound on
\Pi(x)
.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
x>1
. Then,
\Pi(x)<8x/\log x
.

Proof.

Let
\ell:=\lfloor\log x\rfloor
. Then,

\displaystyle\Pi(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}\pi(x^{1/r})=\pi(x)+\sum_{r=2}^{\ell}\pi(x^{1/r})\leqslant\pi(x)+(\ell-1)\pi(x^{1/2})

\displaystyle\leqslant\dfrac{2x}{\log x}+(\ell-1)\dfrac{2x^{1/2}}{(1/2)\log(x)}\leqslant\dfrac{2x}{\log x}+4x^{1/2}<\dfrac{8x}{\log x}.

∎

Using Lemma
8.2
, we can provide bounds on the number of integers that are expressible in terms of the formulas given in the statement of Theorem
1.3
.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
N>1
be a natural number.

(1)

The number of integers
m
,
2\leqslant m\leqslant N
, that can be expressed as
\tau(q)\nu(q)+d\binom{\tau(q)}{2}
for some prime power
q=p^{d}
is less than
8N/\log N
.

(2)

The number of integers
m
,
2\leqslant m\leqslant N
, that can be expressed as
q+1
for some prime power
q=p^{d}
is less than
8N/\log N
.

(3)

The number of integers
m
,
2\leqslant m\leqslant N
, that can be expressed as

\frac{1}{a}\prod_{k=1,\\
a\nmid k}^{n-1}(q^{n}-q^{k})+\sum_{k=1,\\
a\nmid k}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\binom{n}{k}_{q},

where
q
is some prime power,
n\geqslant 2
,
a
is the smallest prime divisor of
n
, and
\binom{n}{k}_{q}
is the
q
-binomial coefficient is less than
56N/\log N
.

(4)

Let
N>1
be a natural number. The number of integers
m
,
2\leqslant m\leqslant N
, that can be expressed as

q^{n}+\binom{n}{d}_{q_{1}}+\omega(d),

where
q
and
q_{1}
are prime powers,
q=q_{1}^{d}
,
n\geqslant 3
, and
d<n
is less than
72N/\log N
.

Proof.

The first two parts follow from the fact that there is one such integer
m
of the desired form for each prime power. Hence, the number of such integers
m
is bounded above by
\Pi(N)
, which is less than
8N/\log N
by Lemma
8.2
.

For (3), let

P:=\frac{1}{a}\prod_{k=1,\\
a\nmid k}^{n-1}(q^{n}-q^{k})\;\text{ and }\;S:=\sum_{k=1,\\
a\nmid k}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\binom{n}{k}_{q}.

We first note that
N\geqslant P+S\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}q^{2}
, from which we conclude that
q\leqslant\sqrt{2N}
, and there at most
\Pi(\sqrt{2N})
choices for
q
.

Next, by
[
33
, Lemma 3.31]
we have
P\geqslant q^{n(n-(n/a)-1)}
, so

N\geqslant P+S>q^{n(n-(n/a)-1)}\geqslant q^{n(n-2)/2}\geqslant q^{(n-2)^{2}/2}.

It follows that
(n-2)^{2}<2\log N
and
n<2+\sqrt{2\log N}
. Since there is exactly one integer expressible as
P+S
for each pair
(q,n)
, the number of such integers at most
N
having this form is bounded above by

\Pi(\sqrt{2N})\cdot(2+\sqrt{2\log N})<\frac{8\sqrt{2N}(2+\sqrt{2\log N})}{(1/2)\log(2N)}<\frac{56N}{\log N}.

Finally, for (4), since
q^{3}\leqslant q^{n}<N
, there are at most
\Pi(\sqrt[3]{N})
choices for
q
, and, since
2^{n}\leqslant q^{n}<N
and
d<n
, there are at most
\log N
choices for
n
for a fixed
q
, and at most
\log N
choices for
d
given a fixed
n
. Hence, the number of integers expressible in the desired form is bounded above by the number of triples
(q,n,d)
, which is bounded above by

\Pi(\sqrt[3]{N})(\log N)^{2}<\frac{8\sqrt[3]{N}(\log N)^{2}}{(1/3)\log N}<\frac{72N}{\log N}.

∎

We now have what we need to prove Corollary
1.9
.

Proof of Corollary
1.9
.

The upper bound of
144N/\log N
follows from Lemma
8.3
. For the lower bound, we note that by Theorem
1.3
(2), every integer of the form
q+1
, where
q
is a prime power, is a covering number of a ring with unity. Thus,
|\mathscr{E}(N)|\geqslant\Pi(N)-1
, and for
N\geqslant 5
we have
\Pi(N)-1\geqslant\pi(N)>N/\log N
.
∎
