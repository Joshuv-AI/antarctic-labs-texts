Nonnegative Polynomials and Moment Problems on Algebraic Curves
Lorenzo Baldi * and Grigoriy Blekherman † \dagger and Rainer Sinn ‡ \ddagger

Abstract
. The cone of nonnegative polynomials is of fundamental importance in real algebraic geometry, but its facial structure is understood in very few cases. We initiate a systematic study of the facial structure of the cone of nonnegative polynomials P \pos on a smooth real projective curve X X . We show that there is a duality between its faces and totally real effective divisors on X X . This allows us to fully describe the face lattice in case X X has genus one. We compute the Carathéodory number of the dual moment cone P ∨ \pos^{\vee} for an elliptic normal curve X X , which measures the complexity of quadrature rules of measures supported on X X . Interestingly, the topology of the real locus of X X influences the Carathéodory number of P ∨ \pos^{\vee} . We apply our results to truncated moment problems on affine cubic curves, where we deduce sharp bounds on the flat extension degree.

1.
Introduction

Providing certificates of positivity for real nonnegative polynomials is a fundamental challenge in real algebraic geometry, dating back to the works of Hilbert
[
26
]
and Artin
[
3
]
on the existence of sums of squares representations. Over the last century, these results, now called Positivstellensätze, have been extensively studied and generalized, see e.g.
[
35
]
. However, the geometric properties of the set of nonnegative polynomials are less understood. The polynomials of degree
2d
nonnegative on a variety
X
form a convex cone, which we denote by
\pos_{X,2d}
; its face structure is fully known only in very few cases
[
44
,
32
]
: degree
2d
univariate polynomials (or equivalently, quadratic polynomials on the rational normal curve), quadratic polynomials on
\mathbb R^{n}
and quartic polynomials on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
In this work, we initiate a systematic study of the facial structure of the cones of nonnegative polynomials on a real projective curve
X
, and in particular of the extreme rays of these cones. For elliptic normal curves, we provide a full description of the face lattice.

While the cone of nonnegative polynomials is a central object in real algebraic geometry, its dual convex cone
\pos_{X,2d}^{\vee}
plays an important role in real analysis. In the analysis literature,
\pos_{X,2d}^{\vee}
is called the moment cone
[
43
]
. It corresponds to the convex cone of linear functionals, acting on polynomials of degree
2d
, that can be written as integration with respect to a measure supported on
X
. We leverage our understanding of the cone of nonnegative polynomials on elliptic normal curves to find the Carathéodory number of the dual moment cone, refining results of
[
17
]
. Interestingly, we find that the Carathéodory number depends on the topology of the real locus: smooth totally real, genus one curves can have either one or two connected components, and the Carathéodory number is different in these two cases.

Our main results deal with projective curves, whereas truncated moment problems are usually studied in the affine setting. We apply projective study to investigate the truncated moment problem on smooth affine plane cubic curves. Such truncated moment problems have been studied for specific rational curves, e.g. in
[
21
,
47
]
. We provide sharp degree bounds for flat extension, improving the known results and completing the picture for the truncated moment problem on smooth cubic planar curves.

1.1.
Main results and related works

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}=\mathbb P^{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a real projective curve, and denote the real locus of
X
by
X(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathbb P^{n}(\mathbb{R})
.
We work with totally real curves, i.e. curves such that
X(\mathbb{R})
is Zariski dense in
X
. Our main object of study is the cone of nonnegative forms on
X
of degree
2d
. By replacing
X
with the
d
-th Veronese embedding
\nu_{n,d}(X)
we may restrict ourselves to analyzing nonnegative quadratic polynomials on real curves, and thus we define

\pos_{X,2}\coloneqq\big\{\,q\mid\deg q=2\text{ and for all }A\in X(\mathbb{R}),\ q(A)\geq 0\,\big\}

to be the convex cone of quadratic forms nonnegative on
X(\mathbb{R})
.
\pos_{X,2}
is a closed, pointed convex cone in the real vector space
R_{2}
of quadratic forms on
X
.
In
Section

3
, we study the face structure of
\pos_{X,2}
. The cone
\pos_{X,2}
has one zero-dimensional face: the origin. One-dimensional faces of
\pos_{X,2}
are its extreme rays. To any positive dimensional face
\mathrm{F}
we associate a unique totally real effective divisor
\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})
, which we call the
face divisor
of
\mathrm{F}
, as follows (we refer to
Section

3.2
for more details). The face divisor is equal to half the real part of the divisor
\mathrm{div}q
, for any quadric
q
in the relative interior of
\mathrm{F}
. In the converse direction, to any totally real effective divisor
D
we associate the face
\mathrm{F}_{D}
consisting of all quadratic forms
q\in\pos_{X,2}
such that
\mathrm{div}q\geq 2D
.The above allows us to define a two maps
\Phi
and
\Psi
between faces
\mathrm{F}\subset\pos
and totally real effective divisors on
X
:
\Phi
which sends a positive dimensional face of
\pos_{X,2}
to its face divisor
\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})
, and the map
\Psi
which sends a totally real effective divisor
D
to its associated face
\mathrm{F}_{D}
.

Theorem A

(see
Theorem

3.2.5
and
Theorem

3.2.6
)
.

Let
(\mathcal{F}\setminus\{\,\{0\}\,\},\subset)
be the poset of positive-dimensional faces of
\pos_{X,2}
, ordered by inclusion. Then

\Phi
and
\Psi
form a Galois connection between
(\mathcal{F}\setminus\{\,\{0\}\,\},\,\subset\,)
and the face divisors
(\imaginary\Phi,\leq)
. This means that, for all positive dimensional faces
\mathrm{F}\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\{\,\{0\}\,\}
and face divisors
D\in\imaginary\Phi
:

\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})\leq D\iff\mathrm{F}\supseteq\mathrm{F}_{D}

Moreover, we have
(\Psi\circ\Phi)(\mathrm{F})=\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})}=\mathrm{F}
for all
\mathrm{F}\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\{\,\{0\}\,\}
.

The study of the face lattice of
\pos
thus reduces to understanding the following crucial question:
which totally real effective divisors are face divisors of some face
\mathrm{F}
of
\pos
?
In
Section

3.3
, we study the dimension of the faces of
\pos
using Riemann-Roch. In
Section

4
, we focus in particular on the extreme rays of
\pos_{X,2d}
, which we denote by
\mathcal{E}(\pos_{X,2d})
. We introduce a map
\double\colon\mathcal{E}(\pos)\to\jac
from the set of extreme rays to the Jacobian
\jac
of the curve (see
Section

4.1
), and focus in
Section

4.2
on the set
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{rr}}(\pos_{X,2d})
of extreme rays spanned by forms with only real zeroes on
X
. We characterize the image of
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{rr}}(\pos_{X,2d})
under
\double
as the set of positive 2-torsion points in
J(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
(see
Definition

4.2.4
), which is an elementary abelian group of order
2^{g}
, and study its fibers.

Theorem B

(see
Corollary

4.2.10
)
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real smooth irreducible curve of genus
g
. With the previous notations, for all sufficiently large
d
,

\double(\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{rr}}(\pos_{X,2d}))=\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{g}

Moreover,
\double(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot q_{1})=\double(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot q_{2})
if and only if there exists
g\in\mathbb{R}(X)
such that
q_{1}=g^{2}q_{2}
.

Theorem

B
has surprising consequences for the representation of positive polynomials, as it predicts the existence of forms
q\in\pos_{X,2d}
such that
q^{2k+1}
is not a sum of squares for all
k\in\mathbb{N}

[
4
]
. Using
Theorem

B
, we investigate in
Section

5
the case of genus one curves, when we have
\mathcal{E}(\pos_{X,2d})=\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{rr}}(\pos_{X,2d})
(see also
[
30
]
). The special case of plane cubics, which was our original motivation, is described using elementary techniques in
Section

A.1
.
More generally, the complete facial structure of
\pos_{X,2d}
for elliptic normal curves is described in
Theorem

5.1.2
.

Extreme rays of positivity cones have been previously investigated in the literature. In
[
15
]
, the authors provide several examples of extreme rays (that are not sums of squares) of the nonnegative cone for the case
X=\mathbb P^{n}
. Additional examples can be found in
[
39
]
. We refer to
[
44
]
for the description of Hilbert’s cases and other results of local nature. The case of ternary sextics was studied recently in
[
31
]
, where the authors characterize the sets of nine points in the projective plane that admit a nonnegative, extreme ternary sextic vanishing on them.
But these results are only partial, and before
Theorem

5.1.2
a complete description of the faces of the nonnegative cone was known to be possible only when the nonnegative cone coincides with the sums of squares cone.

In
Section

6
, we move to the study of the dual cone
\pos_{X,2}^{\vee}
, which consists of linear functionals in the dual space to quadrics on
X
, that can be expressed as integration with respect to a measure supported on
X(\mathbb{R})
. Equivalently,
\pos^{\vee}
is the convex hull of the set of point evaluations of quadrics at the real points
X(\mathbb{R})
of
X
. The
Carathéodory number

\mathcal{C}_{X,2}
of
\pos_{X,2}^{\vee}
is the minimal natural number
\mathcal{C}_{X,2}
such that any linear functional
L\in\pos_{X,2}^{\vee}
is conic sum of at most
\mathcal{C}_{X,2}
point evaluations. This important quantity has been studied from different perspectives, and it is equivalent to determining the minimal number of nodes in a quadrature rule, or the maximal rank in a symmetric tensor decomposition with nonnegative coefficients. Our study of the nonnegative cone on elliptic normal curves allows us to determine the Carathéodory number of
\pos_{X,2}^{\vee}
, or more generally of
\pos_{X,2d}^{\vee}
, which remarkably depends on the topology of the real locus of the curve.

Theorem C

(see
Theorem

6.1.4
)
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real elliptic normal curve.

(i)

If
X(\mathbb{R})
is connected, then
\mathcal{C}_{X,2}=\deg X=n+1
.

(ii)

If
X(\mathbb{R})
is disconnected, then
\mathcal{C}_{X,2}=\deg X+1=n+2
.

The proof of
Theorem

C
, developed in
Section

6.1
is inspired by Hilbert’s proof that every nonnegative ternary quartic is a sum of at most three squares (see
[
9
]
for a modern exposition). It is natural to ask if the same proof technique can be applied to curves of higher genus as well.

Exact results concerning Carathéodory numbers are rare in the literature. The rational univariate case is e.g. completely solved in
[
43
, Cor. 9.16]
. To the best of our knowledge
Theorem

C
is the first complete description for non-rational curves, and the first time where the topology of the real supporting set is shown to play a key role in the study of Carathéodory numbers.
Theorem

C
distinguishes between the two possibilities left open from
[
17
, Th. 4.8]
.
Asymptotic estimates for Carathéodory numbers on affine curves and for higher dimensional cases have been recently studied in
[
40
,
18
,
17
]
.

We can also interpret the Carathéodory number as the rank of a real symmetric tensor decomposition with nonnegative coefficients, using forms supported on the Veronese embedding of the curve (we refer to
Section

6.2
for more details). The similarities and differences between the complex case, real case, and real case with nonnegative coefficients have been investigated in
[
38
,
10
,
1
]
.

We now describe our results on the truncated moment problem, contained in
Section

7
. We switch from the projective setting to the affine one, as it is more common in the analysis literature, and focus on the planar case.
Let then
X
be an affine real cubic plane curve, whose projectivization is smooth.
The
moment problem
asks whether a liner functional
L
can be written as integration with respect to a measure supported on
X(\mathbb{R})
. Unlike in the projective case, this is not equivalent to
L
lying in the dual cone
\pos_{X,\leq 2d}^{\vee}
, since the cone of linear functionals that are representable by measures is not always closed. The most effective criterion for solving this problem is the
flat extension
criterion, which can be stated as follows. Given a linear functional
L
acting on polynomials on degree
\leq 2d
, we say that
\widetilde{L}
, acting on polynomials of degree
\leq 2d+2
, is a flat extension of
L
if:

(i)

L
is the restriction of
\widetilde{L}
to polynomials of degree
\leq 2d
; and

(ii)

the bilinear forms
(p,q)\mapsto L(pq)
and
(p,q)\mapsto\widetilde{L}(pq)
(defined respectively on polynomials of degree
\leq d
and
\leq d+1
) have the same rank.

The flat extension criterion states that if
L
has a flat extension, then
L\in\pos_{X,\leq 2d}^{\vee}
.

Leveraging our projective study of the Carathéodory numbers, we show that for plane cubics the moment problem is conveniently described via the existence of an
almost flat extension
of
L
. This is an extension
\widetilde{L}
of
L
to degree
\leq 2d+4
, where the rank of the associate bilinear form is allowed to grow by one. We refer to
Section

7.1
for more details. Note that the existence of an almost flat extension guarantees a flat extension either between degree
2d
and
2d+2
, or between degree
2d+2
or
2d+4
.

Theorem D

(see
Proposition

7.2.1
)
.

Let
X(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the real locus of a totally real affine plane cubic, whose projectivization
\overline{X}
is smooth, and let
L
be a linear functional acting on
\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{\leq 2d}\big/\mathcal{I}(X)_{\leq 2d}
. Then
L
is a moment linear functional if and only if
L
admits an almost flat extension.

The proof of
Theorem

D
is given in
Section

7.2
. We provide refined versions of this result in
Theorems

7.2.2
and
7.2.4
, which take into account the topology of the curve in analogy to the projective case.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first solution of the moment problem for irreducible, nonrational curves.

The truncated moment problem for plane cobic curves was previously studied, e.g., in
[
21
,
47
,
49
]
for the union of parallel lines. All these works deal with (unions of) rational curves, and the authors are able to characterize membership in the moment cone with the flat extension condition. The flat extension condition is not sufficient in our genus one case, and to overcome this problem we introduce the notion of almost flat extension. Moreover, we remark that these results apply for linear functionals
L\in\pos_{X,\leq 2d}^{\vee}
for large enough
d
, while
Theorem

D
applies for any
d\geq 1
. A complete solution of the truncated moment problem for plane cubics has recently appeared in
[
29
]
, extending
Theorem

D
to singular and reducible curves.

In this work, we restricted ourselves to studying positivity cones inside
\mathbb{R}[X]_{2d}
, to keep the article accessible also to researchers in functional analysis. However, analogous results apply for the convex cones of nonnegative sections in
H^{0}(X,L\otimes L)
, where
L=\mathcal{O}_{X}(D)
is an (ample, real) line bundle on
X
. This is the point of view taken e.g. in
[
30
]
and in
[
11
, Sec. 5]
.

2.
Preliminaries and notations

2.1.
Real algebraic curves

We refer to
[
34
]
for the basics of real algebraic geometry, which we briefly recall in this section. In the following, varieties are always to be understood as
reduced
,
(geometrically) irreducible
and
smooth
.

An
\mathbb{R}
-variety
is a pair
(X,\sigma)
, where
X
is an (abstract) complex algebraic variety and
\sigma
is an anti-regular (or anti-holomorphic) involution on
X
, see
[
34
, Def. 2.1.12]
. We will often omit
\sigma
from the notation. The
real locus
of the
\mathbb{R}
-variety
(X,\sigma)
, denoted
X(\mathbb{R})
, is the set of fixed points for
\sigma
, i.e.
X(\mathbb{R})\coloneqq\{\,x\in X\colon\sigma(x)=x\,\}
. We denote by
\mathbb{C}(X)
the
\mathbb{C}
-algebra of rational functions and by
\mathbb{R}(X)
the
\mathbb{R}
-algebra of real rational functions on
X
. We say that an
\mathbb{R}
-variety is
totally real
if
X(\mathbb{R})
is Zariski dense in
X
(these are called varieties with
enough real points
in
[
34
]
).

Concretely, we are interested in
real algebraic projective subvarieties
, which we call
real varieties
for short.
These are subvarieties
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}=\mathbb P^{n}(\mathbb{C})
of the projective space, which are invariant under the natural involution of
\mathbb P^{n}
, i.e. the coordinate-wise conjugation. Equivalently, real varieties
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
are given as the zero locus of (finitely many) real
n
-variate homogeneous polynomials
\{\,p_{1},\dots,p_{a}\}\subset\mathbb{R}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]=\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]
.
The real homogeneous coordinate ring is
\mathbb{R}[X]=\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]/I
, where
I=\mathcal{I}(X)
is the ideal of (real) polynomials vanishing on
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
.
\mathbb{R}[X]
is a graded ring, with the grading induced by
\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]=\mathbb{R}[\mathbb P^{n}]
. We denote by
R_{k}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}[X]_{k}=\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{k}/I_{k}
the
k
-graded part, i.e. the
\mathbb{R}
-vector space of homogeneous real polynomials (or
forms
) of degree
k
on
X(\mathbb{R})
.
The field of real rational functions on
X
is
\mathbb{R}(X)=\quot(\mathbb{R}[U])
, where
U\subset X
is an open real affine variety and
\mathbb{R}[U]
is the coordinate ring of
U
.

If
\dim X=1
(dimension as a complex algebraic variety), we say that
(X,\sigma)
(resp.
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
) is a real curve (resp. a real projective curve). When
X
is totally real, the real locus
X(\mathbb{R})
of a real curve is a real differentiable manifold of dimension
1
.

A
(Weil) divisor

D=\sumop\displaylimits_{A\in X}a_{A}A
on a curve
X
is an element of the free abelian group on the points of
X
. This means that a divisor is a formal combination of points
A\in X
with integer coefficients
a_{A}\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
a_{A}=0
for all but finitely many
A\in X
. We denote by
(\Div{X},+)
the abelian group of divisors on
X
. A divisor
D=\sumop\displaylimits_{A\in X}a_{A}A\in\Div X
is called
effective
if
a_{A}\geq 0
for all
A
. The
support
of
D
, denoted
\supp D
, is the set of
A\in X
such that
a_{A}\neq 0
.

We say that a divisor
D=\sumop\displaylimits_{A\in X}a_{A}A
on a real curve
X
is
real
if it is invariant under
\sigma
, i.e. if
\sumop\displaylimits_{A\in X}a_{A}\,A=\sumop\displaylimits_{A\in X}a_{A}\,\sigma(A)
. We say that
D=\sumop\displaylimits_{A\in X}a_{A}\,A
is
totally real
if the support of
D
is contained in
X(\mathbb{R})
, i.e. if
a_{A}=0
for all
A\in X\setminus X(\mathbb{R})
. We are particularly interested in totally real effective divisors, i.e. divisors with nonnegative integer coefficients whose support is included in
X(\mathbb{R})
. Given a divisor
D=\sumop\displaylimits_{A\in X}a_{A}A
, we write
D_{\mathbb{R}}=\sumop\displaylimits_{A\in X(\mathbb{R})}a_{A}A
and
D_{\mathbb{C}}=\sumop\displaylimits_{A\in X\setminus X(\mathbb{R})}a_{A}A
. Then
D
is totally real if and only if
D=D_{\mathbb{R}}
.

2.2.
Convex geometry

We refer to
[
41
,
6
]
for the basics of convex geometry.

Given a finite dimensional vector space
V
, we call
\mathrm{C}\subset V
a
convex cone
if
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot\mathrm{C}\subset\mathrm{C}
and
\mathrm{C}+\mathrm{C}\subset\mathrm{C}
. Given
B\subset V
, we denote by
\cone(B)
the smallest convex cone containing
B
, called the
conic hull
of
B
. We say that a convex cone
C
is
closed
if it is closed as a subset of
V
with the Euclidean topology. We say that
C
is
pointed
if
C\cap-C=\{0\}
. In this paper, we will mostly work with convex cones inside
R_{2}=\mathbb{R}[X]_{2}
, the real vector space of quadratic forms restricted to a totally real projective curve
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
.

A
face
of a convex cone
\mathrm{C}
is a convex cone
\mathrm{F}\subset\mathrm{C}
such that, for all
a,b\in\mathrm{C}
,
a+b\in\mathrm{F}
implies
a\in\mathrm{F}
and
b\in\mathrm{F}
. The
dimension
of a face
\mathrm{F}
, denoted by
\dim\mathrm{F}
, is the dimension of the smallest vector subspace
W\subset V
containing
\mathrm{F}
, called the
linear span
of
\mathrm{F}
. Faces of dimension one are called
extreme rays
of
\mathrm{C}
. A closed pointed convex cone is equal to the conic hull (or convex hull) of its extreme rays, see e.g.
[
41
, Th. 18.5]
.

The
relative interior
of a face
\mathrm{F}
, denoted
\relint\mathrm{F}
, is the interior of
\mathrm{F}
when regarded as a subset of its linear span, equipped with the Euclidean topology. If, given two faces
\mathrm{F}_{1},\mathrm{F}_{2}\subset\mathrm{C}
, there exists
a\in\relint\mathrm{F}_{1}\cap\relint\mathrm{F}_{2}
, then
\mathrm{F}_{1}=\mathrm{F}_{2}
(see
[
41
, Th. 18.1.2]
).

Given a real vector space
V
, we denote by
V^{*}\coloneqq\hom_{\mathbb{R}}(V,\mathbb{R})
the dual space of
\mathbb{R}
-linear functions. If
\mathrm{C}\subset V
is a convex cone,
\mathrm{C}^{\vee}\coloneqq\{\,L\in V^{*}\colon L(V)\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\,\}
is the
dual cone
to
\mathrm{C}
. A face
\mathrm{F}\subset\mathrm{C}
is called
exposed
if
\mathrm{F}=L^{-1}(0)\cap C
for some
L\in\mathrm{C}^{\vee}
.
Conic duality
states that, given a
closed
convex cone
\mathrm{C}
, we have
(\mathrm{C}^{\vee})^{\vee}=\mathrm{C}
.

2.3.
Nonnegative forms and the moment problem

We refer to
[
8
,
11
,
43
]
for the study of cones of nonnegative forms and their dual cones.

In the following, we consider a totally real projective subvariety
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
.
If
k=2d
is even, every
q\in R_{2d}=\mathbb{R}[X]_{2d}
has a well-defined sign on every
A\in X(\mathbb{R})
. We write
q(A)\geq 0
(resp.
q(A)\leq 0
) if the sign of
q
at
A
is
0
or positive (resp. negative). If the form
q
is such that
q(A)\geq 0
for all
A\in X(\mathbb{R})
, we say that
q
is
nonnegative
. We define:

\pos_{X,2d}\coloneqq\big\{\,q\in R_{2d}\mid\forall A\in X(\mathbb{R})\ q(A)\geq 0\,\big\}=\big\{\,q\in R_{2d}\mid q\text{ is nonnegative}\,\big\}

\Sigma_{X,2d}\coloneqq\left\{\,\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}^{2}\in R_{2d}\mid r\in\mathbb{N},\ p_{1},\dots p_{r}\in R_{d}\,\right\}

The elements of
\Sigma_{X,2d}
are called
sums of squares
forms.
Clearly, every sum of squares is nonnegative, i.e.
\Sigma_{X,2d}\subset\pos_{X,2d}
. When clear from the context, we will write
\pos\coloneqq\pos_{X,2d}
and
\Sigma\coloneqq\Sigma_{X,2d}
.
\pos
and
\Sigma
are full dimensional, closed, pointed convex cones in the vector space
R_{2d}
, see
[
11
]
.

The dual convex cone
\pos^{\vee}
can be characterised as follows. Denote by
\widehat{X}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
the affine cone over
X(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathbb P^{n}(\mathbb{R})
, and by
\mathbb{S}^{n}
the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. For every
x\in\widehat{X}(\mathbb{R})
, denote by
\mathrm{ev}_{x}\in R_{2d}^{*}

the point evaluation at
x
, i.e.
\mathrm{ev}_{x}(q)\coloneqq q(x)
for
q\in R_{2d}
. Then:

\pos^{\vee}=\pos_{X,2d}^{\vee}=\cone\left(\mathrm{ev}_{A}\colon A\in\widehat{X}(\mathbb{R})\right)

see
[
11
]
. We will often replace
\widehat{X}(\mathbb{R})
by
{X}(\mathbb{R})
, writing
\mathrm{ev}_{A}
for
A\in X(\mathbb{R})
, identifying
A
with any of its affine representatives in
\widehat{X}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathbb{S}^{n}
.
The
moment problem
is the problem of determining, given
L\in R_{2d}^{*}
, whether
L\in\pos^{\vee}
or
L\notin\pos^{\vee}
.

If
L\in\pos^{\vee}
, we define the
Carathéodory number
of
L
as:

\mathcal{C}_{X,2d}(L)\coloneqq\min\big\{\,r\in\mathbb{N}\mid\exists x_{1},\dots x_{r}\in\widehat{X}(\mathbb{R})\text{ s.t. }L\in\cone(\mathrm{ev}_{x_{1}},\dots,\mathrm{ev}_{x_{r}})\,\big\}

and the
Carathéodory number
of
\pos^{\vee}
as

\mathcal{C}_{X,2d}\coloneqq\max\big\{\,\mathcal{C}_{X,2d}(L)\colon L\in\pos^{\vee}\,\big\}

The main goal of this article is to study in detail the convex cones
\pos_{X,2d}
,
\pos_{X,2d}^{\vee}
, to determine the Carathéodory number
\mathcal{C}_{X,2d}
when
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
is a totally real elliptic normal curve, and finally to apply these results to solve the moment problem on affine plane cubics.

3.
The convex cone of nonnegative forms on projective curves

In the following,
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
is a smooth, irreducible, totally real projective curve. We want to study the convex cone
\pos_{X,2d}\subset R_{2d}=\mathbb{R}[X]_{2d}
of nonnegative forms on
X(\mathbb{R})
of degree
2d
.
We can restrict to the case
d=1
, since
\mathbb{R}[X]_{2d}\cong\mathbb{R}[\nu_{n,d}(X)]_{2}
, where
\nu_{n,d}(X)
denotes the
d
-th Veronese embedding of
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
.

We then fix
\pos=\pos_{X,2}
. Our goal is to understand the relationship between faces
\mathrm{F}\subset\pos
and totally real effective divisors
D\in\Div X
. Recall that, given any divisor
D\in\Div(X)
, we can uniquely write
D=D{R}+D{C}
, where
\supp D_{\mathbb{R}}\subset X(\mathbb{R})
and
\supp D{C}\subset X\setminus X(\mathbb{R})
, and a divisor
D
is totally real if and only if
D=D_{\mathbb{R}}
.

3.1.
Bivariate forms

Before developing the general theory, we describe the special case of
\mathbb P^{1}
. For a bivariate form
q\in\mathbb{R}[x_{0},x_{1}]_{2d}
, we denote
\mathrm{div}q
its divisor of zeroes and write
\mathrm{div}q=(\mathrm{div}q){R}+(\mathrm{div}q){C}
, where the support of
(\mathrm{div}q){R}
and
(\mathrm{div}q){C}
is included in
\mathbb P^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathbb P^{1}\setminus\mathbb P^{1}(\mathbb{R})
respectively.

Example 3.1.1

(see also
[
44
, Prop. 1.4.4]
)
.

Let
\pos=\pos_{\mathbb P^{1},2d}
be the convex cone of nonnegative bivariate homogeneous polynomials, or bivariate forms, of degree
2d
. Every nonnegative
q\in\pos
has all zeroes with even multiplicity on
\mathbb P^{1}(\mathbb{R})
(otherwise
q
would change sign).
We therefore have
(\mathrm{div}q){R}=2D
, for some totally real effective divisor
D
of degree
\leq d
.
We can determine the faces of
\pos
by specifying real zeroes on
\mathbb P^{1}
, as follows: given a totally real effective divisor
D
of degree
\leq d
, we define

\mathrm{F}_{D}\coloneqq\left\{\,q\in\pos\mid\mathrm{div}q\geq 2D\,\right\}

These are faces of
\pos
. By divisibility properties of univariate polynomials, every divisor
D
imposes
2\deg D
conditions in the
2d+1
dimensional space of bivariate forms
\mathbb{R}[x_{0},x_{1}]_{2d}
. More precisely, we have

\dim\mathrm{F}_{D}=2(d-\deg D)+1
, as we show in the following.

•

If
D=0
is the empty divisor, then
\mathrm{F}_{D}=\pos
, and we want to show that
\dim\pos=2d+1
. Therefore we only need to show that
\pos
has nonempty interior. But this is clear, as e.g. the form
x_{0}^{2d}+x_{1}^{2d}
is strictly positive on
\mathbb P^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, and it can be perturbed in any direction while remaining strictly positive.

•

If
D=A
for some
A\in\mathbb P^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then
\mathrm{F}_{D}=\mathrm{F}_{A}
consists on nonnegative forms vanishing on
A
. This imposes two conditions on form of degree
2d
, and
\mathrm{F}_{D}
is full dimensional in its
2(d-1)+1
linear span given by

V_{D}\coloneqq\{\,q\in\mathbb{R}[x_{0},x_{1}]_{2d}\colon\mult_{A}(q)\geq 2\,\}
.
For instance, if
A=(a:b)
, then
the form
(bx_{0}-ax_{1})^{2}(x_{0}^{2d-2}+x_{1}^{2d-2})
lies in the relative interior of
\mathrm{F}_{D}
, as it vanishes exactly with multiplicity
2
at
A
on all
\mathbb P^{1}(\mathbb{R})
:
\mathrm{div}((bx_{0}-ax_{1})^{2}(x_{0}^{2d-2}+x_{1}^{2d-2})){R}=2A
.

•

In general, if
D
is a totally real effective divisor with
\deg D\leq d
, then
\mathrm{F}_{D}
is full dimensional in
V_{D}=\{\,q\in\mathbb{R}[x_{0},x_{1}]_{2d}\colon\mathrm{div}q\geq 2D\,\}
, which is a
2(d-\deg D)+1
dimensional vector space. A point in the relative interior of
\mathrm{F}_{D}
can be constructed as follows. If
D=A_{1}+\ldots+A_{k}
for points
A_{i}=(a_{i}:b_{i})\in\mathbb P^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, set
f=\prodop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{k}(b_{i}x_{0}-a_{i}x_{1})^{2}
. Then
f(x_{0}^{2d-2k}+x_{1}^{2d-2k})
lies in the relative interior of
\mathrm{F}_{D}\subset V_{D}
.

All the faces of
\pos
are of the form
\mathrm{F}_{D}
for some totally real effective divisor
D
. Indeed, if
\mathrm{F}\subset\pos
is a face, then pick
q\in\relint\mathrm{F}
, and write
(\mathrm{div}q){R}=2D
. It is then possible to show that
q\in\relint\mathrm{F}_{D}
, and therefore
\mathrm{F}=\mathrm{F}_{D}
from
[
41
, Cor. 18.1.2]
(see also
Lemma

3.2.1
).

In the next sections, we will generalize
Example

3.1.1
to arbitrary totally real projective curves
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
.

3.2.
Faces and divisors

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a smooth, irreducible, totally real projective curve. For
0\neq q\in\mathbb{C}[X]
we consider the divisor of
q
, see e.g.
[
45
, p. 152]
, denoted
\mathrm{div}q
.
This divisor encodes the intersection points (with multiplicity) of
X
inside
\mathbb P^{n}
with the hypersurface defined by any
Q\in\mathbb C[\mathbf{x}]
such that
Q+I(X)=q
in
\mathbb C[X]
:
\mathrm{div}q=\sumop\displaylimits_{A\in X}\mult_{A}(Q)\,A
.
We have
\deg(\mathrm{div}q)=\sumop\displaylimits_{A\in X}\mult_{A}(Q)=\deg q\cdot\deg X
.

We now consider
q\in\pos\subset R_{2}=\mathbb{R}[X]_{2}
. Since
q
does not change sign on
X(\mathbb{R})
, it does not change sign on any connected component of
X(\mathbb{R})
. Therefore, if
q
has a zero at
A\in X(\mathbb{R})
, the multiplicity of
q
at
A
is even. This implies that
(\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{R}}=2D
for some effective totally real divisor
D
.

We now show that any non-zero face
\mathrm{F}\subset\pos
determines a unique totally real effective divisor
D
on
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
.

Lemma 3.2.1
.

Let
\{0\}\neq\mathrm{F}\subset\pos
be a face. Then there exists a unique totally real effective divisor
D\subset\Div(X)
such that, for all
q
in the relative interior of
\mathrm{F}
,
(\mathrm{div}q){R}=2D
.

Proof.

For
A\in X(\mathbb{R})
, let
\mu_{A}=\min\{\,\mult_{A}(q)\colon q\in\mathrm{F}\,\}/2
. Note that, since
\mathrm{F}\neq\emptyset
, we have
\mu_{A}\neq 0
only for finitely many
A\in X(\mathbb{R})
(any quadratic form
q\in\mathbb{C}[X]
,
q\neq 0
, only vanishes at finitely many points on
X
). We show that, for all
q\in\mathrm{F}
and
A\in X(\mathbb{R})
,
\mult_{A}(q)>2\mu_{A}
implies
q\notin\relint\mathrm{F}
. So we define the totally real effective divisor
D\coloneqq\sumop\displaylimits_{A\in X(\mathbb{R})}\mu_{A}\,A
. The following argument then shows
(\mathrm{div}q){R}=2D
for all
q\in\relint\mathrm{F}
as claimed.

Consider the vector space
V_{A}=\{\,q\in R_{2}\colon\mult_{A}(q)\geq 2\mu_{A}\,\}
. By definition,
\mathrm{F}\subset V_{A}
.
We construct a linear functional
L\colon V_{A}\to\mathbb{R}
as follows.
Consider a local parameter
t
at
A\in X(\mathbb{R})
, and expand
q\in V_{A}
locally around
A
so that
q=a_{q}t^{2\mu_{A}}+\dots
, where the dots represent higher order terms in
t
. We define
L(q)=a_{q}
. Since every
q\in\mathrm{F}
is nonnegative on
X(\mathbb{R})
we conclude
L(q)\geq 0
for all
q\in\mathrm{F}
, i.e.
L\in\mathrm{F}^{\vee}\subset V_{A}^{*}
. By definition of
\mu_{A}
, there exists a form
g\in\mathrm{F}
with
\mult_{A}(g)=2\mu_{A}
, which implies
L(g)>0
. So
L
defines a supporting hyperplane to
F
containing any
q\in F
with
\mult_{A}(q)>2\mu_{A}
, showing
q\notin\relint\mathrm{F}
.
∎

Since the divisor
(\mathrm{div}q){R}
is equal for all
q\in\relint\mathrm{F}
by
Lemma

3.2.1
we can make the following definition.

Definition 3.2.2
.

Given a face
\{\,\{0\}\,\}\neq\mathrm{F}\subset\pos
, we define the
face divisor
of
\mathrm{F}
as the unique totally real divisor
\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})
such that
(\mathrm{div}q){R}=2\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})
for all
q\in\relint\mathrm{F}
.

We have found a way to go from faces to divisors. We can also go in the other direction, as follows.

Definition 3.2.3
.

Given a totally real effective divisor
D
, we define

\mathrm{F}_{D}\coloneqq\{q\in P\colon\mathrm{div}(q)\geq 2D\}

the face of
\pos
consisting of quadratic forms vanishing on
X(\mathbb{R})
with at least twice the multiplicity given by
D
. We call
F_{D}
the
face associated to the divisor
D
.

Lemma 3.2.4
.

\mathrm{F}_{D}
is a face of
\pos
.

Proof.

Since
V_{D}=\{q\in\mathbb{R}[X]\colon\mathrm{div}(q)\geq 2D\}
is a linear space, we conclude that
F_{D}
is a convex cone contained in
\pos
. To show that it is a face, pick
q_{1},q_{2}\in\pos
with
q_{1}+q_{2}\in\mathrm{F}_{D}
. So we have
\mult_{A}(q_{1}+q_{2})\geq\mult_{A}(2D)
for all
A\in X(\mathbb{R})
. Since
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are nonnegative on
X(\mathbb{R})
, the valuation
\nu_{A}(q_{1}+q_{2})
is equal to
\min\{\nu_{A}(q_{1}),\nu_{A}(q_{2})\}
so that we also get
\mult_{A}(q_{1})\geq\mult_{A}(2D)
and
\mult_{A}(q_{2})\geq\mult_{A}(2D)
for all
A\in X(\mathbb{R})
. Therefore
q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathrm{F}_{D}
, showing that
\mathrm{F}_{D}
is a face.
∎

Denote by
\mathcal{F}
the set of faces of
\pos
and by
\Div_{\geq 0}(X(\mathbb{R}))
the set of totally real effective divisors. We define

\displaystyle\Phi\colon\mathcal{F}\setminus\{\,\{0\}\,\}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\Div_{\geq 0}(X(\mathbb{R}))

\displaystyle\mathrm{F}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})

\displaystyle\Psi\colon\Div_{\geq 0}(X(\mathbb{R}))\supset\imaginary(\Phi)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathrm{F}_{D}

\Phi
and
\Psi
are, by definition, two order-reversing maps between the poset of (nonzero) faces
\mathcal{F}\setminus\{\,\{0\}\,\}
and the face divisors
\imaginary(\Phi)\subset\Div_{\geq 0}(X(\mathbb{R}))
. Here, we order
\mathcal{F}
by inclusion and
\imaginary(\Phi)
by the usual order on
\Div(X)
defined as such:
\sumop\displaylimits_{P\in X}a_{P}P\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{P\in X}b_{P}P
if
a_{P}\leq b_{P}
for all
P\in X
.

We now show that every face can be realized as the face associated to a totally real effective divisor. In other words, we prove that
\Psi
is surjective.

Theorem 3.2.5
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real curve and
\pos=\pos_{X,2}
the convex cone of nonnegative quadrics. For any face
\mathrm{F}\subset\pos
,
F\neq\{0\}
the following statements hold.

(i)

\mathrm{F}=\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})}=(\Psi\circ\Phi)(\mathrm{F})
;

(ii)

The linear span of
\mathrm{F}
is
V_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})}=\{\,q\in R_{2}\colon\mathrm{div}q\geq 2\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})\,\}
;

(iii)

\relint\mathrm{F}=\{\,q\in\pos\colon(\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{R}}=2\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})\,\}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})
be the face divisor of
\mathrm{F}
. For any
g\in\relint\mathrm{F}
, we have
(\mathrm{div}g){R}=2\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})
by definition. We now show that if
(\mathrm{div}g){R}=2\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})
for some
g\in\pos
then
g
lies in the relative interior of
\mathrm{F}
.

Consider the vector space
V=V_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})}=\{\,q\in R_{2}\colon(\mathrm{div}q)\geq 2\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})\,\}
. Then we have
\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})}\subset V
by definition. We now show that any
g\in P
with
(\mathrm{div}g){R}=2\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})
belongs to the interior of
\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})}
in
V
, which in particular implies that
V
is the linear span of
\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(F)}
.
Since
(\mathrm{div}g){R}=2\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})
we have
g\in\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})}
.
We only need to show that for any
q\in V
there is an
\varepsilon>0
such that
g+\varepsilon q\in\pos
, i.e., that
g+\varepsilon q\geq 0
on
X(\mathbb{R})
. This follows from compactness of
X(\mathbb{R})
.
We switch to the affine cone so that
g
and
q
are actual functions on
\widehat{X}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathbb{S}^{n}
.
For every
A\in\supp\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})
let
x_{A}\in\widehat{X}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathbb{S}^{n}
be an affine representative. Since the multiplicity of
g
at
A
is smaller or equal to the multiplicity of
q
at
A
, and since
g
is nonnegative, there is an open neighborhood
U_{A}
of
x_{A}
in
\widehat{X}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathbb{S}^{n}
and an
\varepsilon>0
such that
g+\varepsilon q\geq 0
on
U_{A}
. Since
g(-x)=g(x)
and
g
vanishes only at finitely many points in
X(\mathbb{R})
, there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that
g+\varepsilon q\geq 0
on
U\coloneqq\bigcupop\displaylimits_{A\in\supp\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})}(U_{A}\cup-U_{A})
.
Next, since all the zeroes of
g
belong to
U
, we conclude that
g
is strictly positive on the compact set
K\coloneqq(\widehat{X}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathbb{S}^{n})\setminus U
. Since
q
is bounded on
K
and
g
has a strictly positive minimum of
K
we get
g+\varepsilon q>0
on
K
for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
. Overall, we conclude that there is an
\varepsilon>0
such that
g+\varepsilon q
is nonnegative on
X(\mathbb{R})
, showing that
g
is in the relative interior of
\mathrm{F}
.

This also implies that any interior point of
\mathrm{F}
is an interior point of
\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})}
, and thus by
[
41
, Cor. 18.1.2]
we have
\mathrm{F}=\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})}
. Moreover, the linear span of
\mathrm{F}
is
V
.

Above, we have shown the inclusion
\relint\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})}\supset\{\,q\in\pos\colon(\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{R}}=2\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})\,\}
. We now prove the reverse inclusion as well.
For
f\in\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})}\setminus\{\,q\in\pos\colon(\mathrm{div}f)_{\mathbb{R}}=2\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})\,\}
there exists a real point
A\in\supp\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})
such that
\mult_{A}(q)>\mult_{A}(\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F}))
. Consider then
L\colon V_{A}\to\mathbb{R}
as in the proof of
Lemma

3.2.1
. By definition we have
L(f)=0
and
L(g)>0
for all
g\in\{\,q\in\pos\colon(\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{R}}=2\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})\,\}\supset\relint\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})}
, concluding the proof.
∎

Theorem

3.2.5
allows us to describe the duality between faces and face divisors precisely, as follows.

Theorem 3.2.6
.

The functions

\displaystyle\Phi\colon\mathcal{F}\setminus\{\,\{0\}\,\}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\Div_{\geq 0}(X(\mathbb{R}))

\displaystyle\mathrm{F}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})

\displaystyle\Psi\colon\Div_{\geq 0}(X(\mathbb{R}))\supset\imaginary(\Phi)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathrm{F}_{D}

form a Galois connection between the positive dimensional faces
\mathcal{F}\setminus\{\,\{0\}\,\}
, ordered by inclusion, and the set of face divisors
\imaginary(\Phi)\subset\Div_{\geq 0}(X(\mathbb{R}))
. This means that, for all
F\in\mathcal{F}
and
D\in\imaginary\Phi
we have

\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})\leq D\iff\mathrm{F}\supseteq\mathrm{F}_{D}.

Proof.

Let
\mathrm{F}
and
D
be such that
\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})\leq D
. By definition this implies
\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})}\supset\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F}_{D})}
. From
Lemma

3.2.4
we deduce that
\mathrm{F}_{D}
is a face of
\pos
, while from
Theorem

3.2.5

\mathrm{F}_{D}=\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F}_{D})}
and
\mathrm{F}=\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})}
. Therefore
\mathrm{F}=\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})}\supset\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F}_{D})}=\mathrm{F}_{D}
, as we wanted.

For the converse implication, let
D=\mathrm{div}(\widetilde{\mathrm{F}})\in\imaginary\Phi
and
\mathrm{F}\supset\mathrm{F}_{D}=\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\widetilde{\mathrm{F}})}
. This implies that
\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})\leq\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\widetilde{\mathrm{F}}_{1})})=\mathrm{div}(\widetilde{\mathrm{F}})=D
, concluding the proof.
∎

Theorem

3.2.5
implies that
\Psi
is surjective. On the other hand,
\Phi
is not.
Characterizing the image of
\Phi
is difficult, and it is naturally connected to existence of nonnegative quadrics, as we now show.

Lemma 3.2.7
.

Let
D
be a totally real effective divisor. The following are equivalent:

(i)

D
is a face divisor, i.e.,
D\in\imaginary(\Phi)
;

(ii)

D=\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F}_{D})
;

(iii)

There exists
q\in\pos
such that
(\mathrm{div}q){R}=2D
.

Proof.

For
(i)\implies(ii)
, we have to prove
\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})=\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})})
, which follows from
Theorem

3.2.5
.
The converse implication
(ii)\implies(i)
is trivial.

(ii)\implies(iii)
follows by taking
q\in\relint\mathrm{F}_{D}
because we have
(\mathrm{div}q){R}=2\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F}_{D})=2D
for such a
q
.
The final implication
(iii)\implies(ii)
follows from the fact that
(\mathrm{div}q){R}=2D
implies
q\in\relint\mathrm{F}_{D}
, and thus
D=\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F}_{D})
.
∎

Studying the set
\imaginary\Phi
is challenging. The following example, for instance, shows that
\imaginary(\Phi)
is in general not a lower set in
\Div_{\geq 0}(X(\mathbb{R}))
, i.e. for
D,D^{\prime}\in\Div_{\geq 0}(X(\mathbb{R}))
,
D\in\imaginary\Phi
and
D^{\prime}\leq D\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.75pt\kern-4.99998pt$\displaystyle\n@tch@r$\hss}{\implies}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.75pt\kern-4.99998pt$\textstyle\n@tch@r$\hss}{\implies}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 2.625pt\kern-4.18054pt$\scriptstyle\n@tch@r$\hss}{\implies}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.875pt\kern-3.68057pt$\scriptscriptstyle\n@tch@r$\hss}{\implies}}}D^{\prime}\in\imaginary\Phi
.

Example 3.2.8
.

Consider the projective closure
X\subset\mathbb P^{2}
of the affine plane quartic defined by

2(1-x_{1}^{2}-2x_{2}^{2})(1-2x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})-(4x_{1}^{2}-1)(4x_{2}^{2}-1)=0

Its real locus consists of four disjoint ovals, symmetric with respect to the
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
axis. Setting
a=\sqrt{1-\sqrt{112}/14}
, we can verify that
X(\mathbb{R})
intersects the quadric
q=a^{2}-x_{1}^{2}
in the four points
(\pm a,\pm\sqrt{1-3\sqrt{7}/14})
with multiplicity
2
, and that
q
is nonnegative on
X(\mathbb{R})
. If we denote these four points by
A_{1},\dots,A_{4}
and we define
D=A_{1}+\dots+A_{4}
, we therefore have that
\mathrm{div}q=(\mathrm{div}q){R}=2D
, and thus
D\in\imaginary\Phi
.

We now set
D^{\prime}=A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}
. To show that
\imaginary\Phi
is not a lower set in the totally real effective divisors, we prove that
D^{\prime}\notin\imaginary\Phi
. Equivalently, we want to show that there does not exist
q^{\prime}\in\pos
s.t.
(\mathrm{div}q^{\prime}){R}=2D^{\prime}
.
Let
q^{\prime}\in R_{2}
be a quadric such that
\mathrm{div}q^{\prime}\geq 2D
. It follows from the Cayley-Bacharach theorem (e.g.
[
19
, Th. CB7]
, applied to the hypersurfaces
X
and
q=0
with
\Gamma^{\prime}=2D^{\prime}
and
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}=2P_{4}
) that
q^{\prime}
vanishes with multiplicity
2
at
P_{4}
, which implies that
\mathrm{div}q^{\prime}\geq(\mathrm{div}q^{\prime})_{\mathbb{R}}\geq 2D
. Therefore, every nonnegative quadric vanishing at
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}
vanishes also at
P_{4}
, showing that
D^{\prime}=P_{1}+P_{2}+P_{3}\notin\imaginary\Phi
. Thus
\imaginary\Phi
is not a lower set.

3.3.
Dimension of faces

In this section, we use the Riemann-Roch theorem (see e.g.
[
45
]
) to compute dimensions of faces of the cone of nonnegative quadrics on
X
. We denote
\mathcal{L}(D)=\left\{\,f\in\mathbb{C}(X)\mid D+\mathrm{div}(f)\geq 0\,\right\}
the Riemann-Roch spaces, and
we write
\ell(D)
for
\dim{C}(\mathcal{L}(D))
. The Riemann-Roch Theorem states
\ell(D)=\deg(D)+1-g+\ell(K-D)
, where
g
is the genus of
X
and
K
is a
canonical divisor
of
X
.
For this computation to be reflected in the chosen embedding
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
of our curve, we assume that the curve is
projectively normal
, which means that the linear systems
|dH|
for any
d\in\mathbb{N}
are complete, where
H
is a hyperplane in
\mathbb P^{n}
. Concretely this means that, for any divisor
D\in\Div(X)
that is linearly equivalent to the zero divisor
\mathrm{div}g
of a form
g\in\mathbb{C}[X]_{d}
, there exists a form
h\in\mathbb{C}[X]_{d}
with
D=\mathrm{div}h
.

We can now investigate, in the projectively normal case, the dimension of faces
\mathrm{F}\in\mathcal{F}
.

Lemma 3.3.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real curve, and let
\{0\}\neq\mathrm{F}\subset\pos
be a nonzero face.
If
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
is projectively normal and
q\in\relint\mathrm{F}
, then
\dim\mathrm{F}=\ell((\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{C}})
.

Proof.

Since
D=\mathrm{div}(\mathrm{F})\in\imaginary\Phi
we have
D=(\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{R}}
for any
q\in\relint\mathrm{F}
by
Theorem

3.2.5
. As in
Theorem

3.2.5
we write
V_{D}=\{\,p\in R_{2}\colon\mathrm{div}p\geq 2D\,\}
. For any
q\in\relint\mathrm{F}
consider the map:

V_{D}\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}((\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{C}}),\quad f\longmapsto f/q.

This is clearly well-defined and injective, and it is surjective since
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
is projectively normal. Therefore
\dim V_{D}=\ell((\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{C}})
. By
Theorem

3.2.5
we have
\dim\mathrm{F}=\ell((\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{C}})
.
∎

Thanks to
Lemma

3.3.1
and
Theorem

3.2.5
, when we consider a totally real projectively normal curve
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
, we only need to consider nonnegative quadrics with a large number of real zeroes, in order to understand the dimension of all the small dimensional faces
\mathrm{F}\subset\pos
. We make this statement precise in the following proposition for the extreme rays, i.e. for faces of dimension one.

Proposition 3.3.2
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real projectively normal curve of genus
g
.
If
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot q
is an extreme ray of
\pos
, then
\deg((\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{R}})\geq 2\deg X-g
. In particular, if all the real zeroes of
q
have multiplicity two, then
q
has at least
\deg X-g/2
real zeroes.

Proof.

If
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot q
is an extreme ray of
\pos
, then combining
Lemma

3.3.1
and Riemann’s inequality we have:

1=\ell((\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{C}})\geq\deg((\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{C}})-g+1

This implies that:

2\deg X-\deg((\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{R}})=\deg((\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{C}})\leq g

concluding the proof.
∎

4.
Extreme rays of the nonnegative cone

We study the
extreme rays
of the cone
P
of nonnegative quadrics, which we denote by
\mathcal{E}(P)
.
The complete description of the extreme rays of
\pos
is difficult for curves of arbitrary genus. In the following, we will mostly focus on real rooted extreme rays, i.e. those that they are defined by nonnegative quadrics with only real zeroes. We use

\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{rr}}(\pos)\coloneqq\{\,\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot q\in\mathcal{E}(\pos)\mid\mathrm{div}q=(\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{R}}\,\}

to denote the set of such extreme rays.
Example

4.0.1
below shows that, in general,
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{rr}}(\pos)\subsetneq\mathcal{E}(\pos)
. However, for genus one curves,
Proposition

3.3.2
implies that the extreme rays are all real rooted. We study them further in
Sections

5
,
6
and
7
.

Example 4.0.1
.

Let
X(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathbb P^{2}(\mathbb{R})
be the plane sextic defined by
h=x_{1}^{6}+x_{2}^{6}-x_{0}^{6}=0
and let
q=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-x_{0}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}]_{2}\cong\mathbb{R}[X]_{2}
. The curve
X
is a smooth plane curve of degree
6
and hence has genus
g=10
from the genus-degree formula.
Proposition

3.3.2
implies that any nonnegative quadric defining an extreme ray of
\pos
has between
2\deg X-g=2
and
12
real zeroes, counted with multiplicity.

The curves
h
and
q
intersect with multiplicity two in the real points
A_{1}=(1:0:1)
,
A_{2}=(1:0:-1)
,
A_{3}=(1:1:0)
and
A_{4}=(1:-1:0)
, and with multiplicity two in the complex conjugate points
A_{5}=(0:1:i)
and
A_{6}=\sigma(A_{5})=(0:1:-i)
. In other words,
(\mathrm{div}q){R}=2(A_{1}+\dots+A_{4})
and
(\mathrm{div}q){C}=2(A_{5}+\sigma(A_{5}))
.

The curve
X(\mathbb{R})
is connected, and
q
is nonnegative on
X(\mathbb{R})
.
By
Proposition

3.3.2
, to show that
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot q
is an extreme ray of
\pos
it is sufficient to verify that
\ell((\mathrm{div}q){C})=\ell(2(A_{5}+\sigma(A_{5}))=1
. This can be done using the Cayley-Bacharach theorem, see e.g.
[
19
, Th. CB7]
or using any computer algebra system. Alternatively, one can directly verify that the double vanishing at

imposes enough conditions to obtain a one-dimensional family of solutions.

Notice in particular that

2\deg X-g=2<\deg(\mathrm{div}q){R}=8<2\deg X=12

showing that

is an extreme ray defined by a quadric with a nonmaximal number of real zeroes, and not attaining the lower bound of the inequality in
Proposition

3.3.2
.

4.1.
Extreme rays and the Jacobian

We propose a general framework to study
\mathcal{E}(\pos)
, based on the properties of the Jacobian of a real curve
X
, for which we refer the reader to
[
24
,
46
,
5
]
.

Let
(X,\sigma)
be a smooth irreducible real curve of genus
g
. For every
f\in\mathbb{C}(X)\setminus\{\,0\,\}
, we can consider the divisor
\mathrm{div}f
of poles and zeroes. Such divisors form a normal subgroup of
\Div(X)
, and the quotient
\Cl{(X)}
is called the
divisor class group
.
So two divisors
D
and
D^{\prime}
represent the same class
[D]=[D^{\prime}]\in\Cl{(X)}
if and only if there exists
f\in\mathbb{C}(X)\setminus\{0\}
such that
D=D^{\prime}+\mathrm{div}f
.
We denote
\Cl^{d}(X)
the subgroup of divisor classes of degree
d
.
\Cl^{0}{(X)}
is isomorphic to the Jacobian variety
\jac=\jac(X)\cong\mathbb{C}^{g}/\Lambda
, which is a (complex)
g
-torus. There is a natural action of
\sigma
on
\jac
,
which gives
\jac
the structure of
\mathbb{R}
-variety.
As usual, we denote by
\jac(\mathbb{R})
the real part of
J
, i.e. the fixed points of
\sigma
. Concretely, a divisor class
[D]
is in
\Cl^{0}(X)(\mathbb{R})\cong\jac(\mathbb{R})
if the divisor
\sigma(D)
is linearly equivalent to
D
, which implies
[\sigma(D)]=[D]
.

We define a map
\mathcal{E}(\pos)\to\jac(\mathbb{R})
. Since
X
is smooth and totally real, we have
X(\mathbb{R})=S_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup S_{r}
, where
S_{i}
is diffeomorphic to a circle for all
i
, and
0<r\leq g+1
by Harnack’s inequality. Furthermore, there exist smooth, simple loops

R_{1},\dots,R_{m}\subset X
such that deleting these loops and the real locus
S_{1},\dots,S_{r}
from the Riemann surface
X(\mathbb{C})
disconnects it:
X(\mathbb{C})\setminus(\bigcupop\displaylimits_{i}S_{i}\cup\bigcupop\displaylimits_{j}R_{j})=Y_{1}\sqcup Y_{2}
is disconnected, with
\sigma(R_{j})=R_{j}
,
\sigma(Y_{1})=Y_{2}
and
\sigma(Y_{2})=Y_{1}
. We refer the reader to
[
5
, Sec. 2]
for more details. The action of
\sigma
on
R_{j}
has no fixed points, and we can write
R_{j}=T_{j,1}\sqcup T_{j,2}
, with
\sigma(T_{j,1})=T_{j,2}
,
\sigma(T_{j,2})=T_{j,1}
and each
T_{j,k}
is diffeomorphic to
[0,1)
. These choices allow us to split divisors
D
supported outside
X(\mathbb{R})
into two parts. If
\supp(D)\cap X(\mathbb{R})=\emptyset
, write
\widetilde{D}
for the unique divisor supported on
Y_{1}\cup T_{1,1}\cup\dots\cup T_{m,1}
such that
D=\widetilde{D}+\sigma(\widetilde{D})
. If
H
denotes the hyperplane divisor, we can then define the map

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}\colon\mathcal{E}(\pos)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\jac

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{\geq}\cdot q

\displaystyle\longmapsto[\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{div}(q))_{\mathbb{R}}+\widetilde{(\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{C}}}-H]\eqqcolon\mathfrak{D}(q)

Notice that the map is well-defined, since the degree of the divisor
\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{div}(q))_{\mathbb{R}}+\widetilde{(\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{C}}}-H
is zero, and that
\mathfrak{D}(q)+\sigma(\mathfrak{D}(q))=0
by definition. The operation
\widetilde{\cdot}
, and hence
\mathfrak{D}
, depend on the (non-unique) choice of
R_{1},\dots,R_{m}
. A related construction, independent of such a choice but not refined enough for our purposes, has been studied in
[
42
, Sec. 2]
. Notice that, if
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot q\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{rr}}(\pos)
, then
\double(q)=[\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{div}(q))_{\mathbb{R}}-H]
.

4.2.
Real rooted extreme rays

A full understanding of
\mathcal{E}(\pos)
will require studying the image
\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{E}(\pos))
in detail. We begin by investigating
\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{rr}}(\pos))
. For this, we are interested in the
2
-torsion points of
\jac(\mathbb{R})
.

Definition 4.2.1
.

We denote
\jac_{2}=\{\,\alpha\in\jac\colon 2\alpha=0\,\}
the subgroup of
\jac
consisting of
2
-torsion points, and we denote the
real
2
-torsion points
by
\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}\coloneqq\jac_{2}\cap\jac(\mathbb{R})
.

It is known that the number of real
2
-torsion points depends only on the genus
g
of
X
and the number of connected components
r
of
X(\mathbb{R})
.

Lemma 4.2.2

(
[
24
, § 5]
)
.

If
(X,\sigma)
is a totally real curve of genus
g
such that
X(\mathbb{R})
has
r>0
connected components, then
\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{g+r-1}
.

Among all real
2
-torsion points, we are interested in those defined by nonnegative rational functions.

Lemma 4.2.3
.

Let
\alpha\in\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}
and let
D,D^{\prime}
be divisors representing
\alpha
. If
f,f^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}(X)
are such that
\mathrm{div}f=2D
and
\mathrm{div}f^{\prime}=2D^{\prime}
, then there exists
g\in\mathbb{R}(X)
such that
f^{\prime}=fg^{2}
. In particular, if
f\geq 0
on
X(\mathbb{R})
(wherever it is defined), then
f^{\prime}\geq 0
on
X(\mathbb{R})
(wherever it is defined).

Proof.

Let
D,D^{\prime},f,f^{\prime}
and
\alpha
be as in the statement. As
[D]=\alpha=[D^{\prime}]
, there exists
g\in\mathbb{R}(X)
such that
D+\mathrm{div}g=D^{\prime}
.
Hence
\mathrm{div}f^{\prime}=2D^{\prime}=2(D+\mathrm{div}g)=\mathrm{div}fg^{2}
. Therefore, up to a multiplicative constant,
f^{\prime}=fg^{2}
.
∎

Definition 4.2.4
.

We say that
\alpha\in\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}
is
positive
if, for any divisor
D
representing
\alpha
, there exist
f\in\mathbb{R}(X)
such that
f\geq 0
on
X(\mathbb{R})
(wherever it is defined) and
\mathrm{div}f=2D
(see
Lemma

4.2.3
). We denote the subgroup of positive
2
-torsion points by
\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
.

We show that the number of positive
2
-torsion points
\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
depends only on the genus of
X
, and not on the topology of
X(\mathbb{R})
: see
[
23
, § 5]
for a related discussion.

Lemma 4.2.5
.

If
(X,\sigma)
is a totally real curve of genus
g
, then
\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{g}
.

Proof.

Write
X(\mathbb{R})=Y_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup Y_{r}
as the disjoint union of its connected components. For all
\alpha=[D]\in\jac(X)_{2}
, we can choose
f_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}(X)
such that
\mathrm{div}f=2D
and
f\geq 0
on
Y_{1}
. The sign of any such
f_{\alpha}
on
Y_{2},\dots,Y_{r}
only depends on
\alpha
because for any two rational functions
f
and
f^{\prime}
such that
\mathrm{div}(f)=2D
and
\mathrm{div}(f^{\prime})=2D^{\prime}
and
[D]=[D^{\prime}]=\alpha\in\jac(X)
differ by a square, see the proof of
Lemma

4.2.3
. We can therefore define the map
\jac(X)_{2}\to\{\,\pm 1\,\}^{r-1}
, which associates to every
\alpha
the tuple of signs of
f_{\alpha}
on
Y_{2},\dots,Y_{r}
. This is a well-defined group morphism, whose kernel is equal to
\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
. The morphism is surjective (see
[
23
, § 5]
or
[
30
]
), and thus
Lemma

4.2.2
implies
\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{g}
.
∎

We are now ready to show how positive
2
-torsion points can be used to characterize totally real divisors that arise as intersections of nonnegative quadrics on
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
with the maximal number of real zeroes.

Proposition 4.2.6
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real smooth irreducible projectively normal curve. Let
[H]
be the hyperplane divisor class, and let
D\in\Div_{\geq 0}(X(\mathbb{R}))
be an effective totally real divisor such that
\deg D=\deg X
and
[2D]=[2{H}]
. Then the following are equivalent.

(i)

There exists a quadric
q\in R_{2}
such that
2D=(\mathrm{div}q)=(\mathrm{div}q){R}
.

(ii)

[D-{H}]\in\jac(\mathbb{R})
is a
2
-torsion point.

Moreover,
q
is nonnegative if and only if
[D-{H}]\in\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
.

Proof.

For
(i)\implies(ii)
, consider the rational function
q/\ell^{2}
, where
\ell
is a linear form. Then
\mathrm{div}(q/\ell^{2})=2D-2{\mathrm{div}\ell}
.
If
q\in\pos
, then
q/\ell^{2}
is nonnegative on
X(\mathbb{R})
and thus
[D-{H}]{=[D-\mathrm{div}\ell]}\in\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
.

For
(ii)\implies(i)
, as
[D-{H}]\in\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}
there exists
f\in\mathbb{R}(X)
and
E\in\absolutevalue{E}
such that
\mathrm{div}f=2D-2{E}
. Therefore
2D=\mathrm{div}f\ell^{2}
for some linear form
\ell
, and by projective normality there exists
q\in R_{2}
such that
2D=\mathrm{div}q=(\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{R}}
.
Furthermore, if
[D-{H}]\in\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
then
f
is nonnegative on
X(\mathbb{R})
and thus
q\in\pos
.
∎

Theorem 4.2.7
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real smooth irreducible projectively normal curve. Let
[H]
be the hyperplane divisor class, and let
D\in\Div_{\geq 0}(X(\mathbb{R}))
be a divisor of degree
\deg D=\deg X
with
[2D]=[2{H}]
.
Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathrm{F}_{D}\subset\pos
is an extreme ray of
\pos
;

(ii)

[D-{H}]
is a positive
2
-torsion point of the Jacobian.

Proof.

Combine
Proposition

4.2.6
and
Theorem

3.2.5
.
∎

Therefore, the existence of a nonnegative quadric with a maximal number of real zeroes can be read from the positive
2
-torsion points in the Jacobian. However, to be able to apply
Lemma

4.2.5
to count their number, we need to be sure that the torsion points are totally real, i.e. that their divisor classes can be represented using only points of
X(\mathbb{R})
.

Definition 4.2.8
.

Let
X
be an real curve. We say that a real divisor class
\beta\in\Cl^{k}(X)
is
totally real
if there exists
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}\in X(\mathbb{R})
such that
\beta=[A_{1}+\dots+A_{k}]
. We denote by
N(X)
the smallest
k
such that all real divisor classes in
\Cl^{k}{X}
are totally real.

The study of the invariant
N(X)
, called
totally real divisor threshold
, was initiated by Scheiderer in
[
42
]
;
[
42
, Th. 2.7]
shows that
N(X)
is finite for every real curve
X
. The papers
[
27
,
36
,
5
]
) give bounds on
N(X)
depending on the topological type of the curve and on its intrinsic metric properties. The fact that
N(X)<+\infty
allows us to describe
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{rr}}(\pos_{X,2d})
in sufficiently high degree
2d
.

Lemma 4.2.9
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real smooth irreducible projectively normal curve, and denote
[H]
the hyperplane divisor class.
For every
d\in\mathbb{N}
, and for every positive
2
-torsion point
\alpha\in\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
such that

\alpha+[d{H}]
is totally real, there exist a real rooted extreme ray
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot q_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{rr}}(\pos_{X,2d})
such that
\double(q_{\alpha})=\alpha
.

Proof.

Since
\alpha
is a positive
2
-torsion point, there exists
f\in\mathbb{R}(X)
nonnegative on
X(\mathbb{R})
such that
\mathrm{div}f=2D
and
\alpha=[D]
. As
\alpha+[d{H}]=[D+d{H}]
is totally real by hypothesis, there exists a totally real divisor
D^{\prime\prime}
such that
[D+d{H}]=[D^{\prime\prime}]
. Moreover, since the embedding is projectively normal and
[2D^{\prime\prime}]=[2d{H}]
, there exists
q_{\alpha}\in R_{2}
such that
\mathrm{div}q_{\alpha}=(\mathrm{div}q_{\alpha}){R}=2D^{\prime\prime}
. Finally, since
\alpha\in\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
, we have (up to a global sing change)
q_{\alpha}\geq 0
on
X(\mathbb{R})
, i.e.
q_{\alpha}\in\pos_{X,2d}
. From
Theorem

4.2.7
we conclude that
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot q_{\alpha}
is an extreme ray of
\pos_{X,2d}\cong\pos_{\nu_{n,d}(X),2}
and, by construction, we have
\double(q_{\alpha})=\alpha
.
∎

Corollary 4.2.10
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real smooth irreducible curve of genus
g
. For all sufficiently large
d
,
\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{rr}}(\pos_{X,2d}))=\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{g}
. Moreover,
f_{1},f_{2}
defining real rooted extreme rays of
\pos_{X,2d}
satisfy
\mathfrak{D}(f_{1})=\mathfrak{D}(f_{2})
if and only if there exists
g\in\mathbb{R}(X)
such that
f_{1}=g^{2}f_{2}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\pos_{X,2d}\cong\pos_{\nu_{n,d}(X),2}
, and if
[H]
is the hyperplane class on
X
, then
[dH]
is the hyperplane class of
\nu_{n,d}(X)
. Moreover, if
d
is big enough,
\nu_{n,d}(X)
is projectively normal and

d\cdot\deg X=\deg\nu_{n,d}(X)\geq N(X),

see
Definition

4.2.8
. This implies that for every
\alpha\in\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
, the divisor class
\alpha+[d{H}]
is totally real. Therefore, from
Lemma

4.2.9
we deduce that for all
\alpha\in\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
there exist
q_{\alpha}\in\pos_{X,2d}
real rooted and generating an extreme rays such that
\double(q_{\alpha})=\alpha
. We recall from
Lemma

4.2.5
that
\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{g}
, and conclude by applying
Lemma

4.2.3
that
\mathfrak{D}(f_{1})=\mathfrak{D}(f_{2})
if and only if there exists
g\in\mathbb{R}(X)
such that
f_{1}=g^{2}f_{2}
.
∎

Corollary

4.2.10
highlights the difference between sums of squares and nonnegative forms. Indeed, if the sums of squares and nonnegative cones are equal, then they have the same extreme rays. But all sums of squares extreme rays correspond only to the zero of the Jacobian, which is a positive
2
-torsion point. If the genus is at least one, then for all sufficiently large
d
there are positive
2
-torsion points which correspond to non-sums of squares extreme rays, and thus we have
\Sigma_{X,2d}\subsetneq\pos_{X,2d}
. This is a particular case of
[
11
, Th. 1.1]
. For a more detailed description in the genus one case, see
Theorem

5.1.2
.

5.
Nonnegative forms on genus one curves

5.1.
Elliptic normal curves

We now consider in detail curves of genus
1
. The case of plane cubic curves, which inspired our work, is detailed in elementary terms in
Proposition

A.1.1
.

Definition 5.1.1
.

An
elliptic normal curve
is a non-degenerate, smooth projective curve
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
of genus one such that
\deg X=n+1
.

Recall that every elliptic normal curve is automatically projectively normal, see e.g.
[
25
, Ex. IV.4.2]
. Using this fact it is possible to show that the Veronese embedding of an elliptic normal curve is again an elliptic normal curve: therefore we can restrict to the case of quadratic forms, as we did in
Section

3
.
If the genus one curve
X
is totally real, then
X(\mathbb{R})
has either one or two connected components, and the invariant
N(X)
, introduced in
Definition

4.2.8
, is always equal to one
[
27
,
36
]
.

We use these facts to fully describe the face lattice of
\pos_{X,2}
for an alliptic normal curve

.

Theorem 5.1.2
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real elliptic normal curve, and denote
[H]
the hyperplane divisor class. If we denote
\{\,O,T\,\}=\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
the positive
2
-torsion points, then all the proper faces of
\pos_{X,2}
can be described as follows:

(i)

\mathrm{F}_{D}
, where
D
is a totally real effective divisor of degree
1\leq\deg D\leq n
. In this case, we have
\dim\mathrm{F}_{D}=2(n+1-\deg D)
.

(ii)

\mathrm{F}_{D}
, where
D
is a totally real effective divisor of degree
n+1
such that
{[D-H]}=O\in\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
. In this case,
\mathrm{F}_{D}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot q
is an extreme ray of both
\Sigma=\Sigma_{X,2}
and
\pos
.

(iii)

\mathrm{F}_{D}
, where
D
is a totally real effective divisor of degree
n+1
such that
{[D-H]}=T\in\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
. In this case,
\mathrm{F}_{D}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot q
is an extreme ray of
\pos
, but it is not in
\Sigma=\Sigma_{X,2}
.

In particular, we have
\mathcal{E}(\pos_{X,2})=\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{rr}}(\pos_{X,2})
.

Proof.

From
Theorem

3.2.5
, all the faces of
\pos
are of the form
\mathrm{F}_{D}
for some totally real effective divisor
D
. If
\deg D>\deg X=n+1
, then
\mathrm{F}_{D}=\{\,0\,\}
.

From
Theorem

4.2.7
, if
\deg D=n+1
then
\mathrm{F}_{D}
is an extreme ray if and only if
[D-{H}]
is a positive
2
-torsion point of the Jacobian. Recall that
N(X)=1
from
[
27
,
36
]
, since
X(\mathbb{R})
has either
1=g
or
2=g+1
connected components. Moreover
X
is projectively normal, hence every positive
2
-torsion point determines a family of extreme rays of
\pos
, see
Corollary

4.2.10
.

If
[D-{H}]=O\in\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
, then there exist
f\in\mathbb{R}(X)
and
E\in\absolutevalue{H}
such that
D={E}+\mathrm{div}f
, and thus by projective normality there exists
\ell\in R_{1}=\mathbb{R}[X]_{1}
such that
\mathrm{div}\ell=D
. Therefore
\mathrm{div}\ell^{2}=2D
and
\mathrm{F}_{D}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot\ell^{2}
is an extreme ray of both
\Sigma
and
\pos
. If
[D-{H}]=T\in\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
, then
D
is not the divisor of a linear form, and thus the extreme ray
\mathrm{F}_{D}
is not generated by a square. This implies that
\mathrm{F}_{D}
is not contained in
\Sigma
.

We now study higher-dimensional faces. We first show that, if
D
is a totally real effective divisor of degree
1\leq\deg D\leq n
, then
D\in\imaginary(\Phi)
, i.e. there exists
q\in\pos
such that
(\mathrm{div}q){R}=D
(see
Lemma

3.2.7
). Assume that
\deg D=n=\deg X-1
. Using the isomorphism between
X
and its Jacobian, we can always find
A_{1}\neq A_{2}\in X(\mathbb{R})
such that
D+A_{1},D+A_{2}\in\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
. From
Theorem

4.2.7
, there exists
q_{1},q_{2}\in\pos
such that
\mathrm{div}q_{i}=(\mathrm{div}q_{i}){R}=D+A_{i}
. Therefore
q_{1}+q_{2}
is nonnegative and vanishes exactly on

, i.e.
(\mathrm{div}(q_{1}+q_{2})){R}=2D
, showing that

. A similar argument works for all totally real divisors of degree smaller than
n
.

The dimension of
\mathrm{F}_{D}
can then be deduced using
Lemma

3.3.1
and the Riemann-Roch theorem, as follows. Since
D\in\imaginary\Phi
, then
Lemmas

3.2.7
and
3.2.1
imply that for all
q\in\relint\mathrm{F}_{D}
we have
(\mathrm{div}q){R}=2D
. It follows from
Lemma

3.3.1
that
\dim\mathrm{F}_{D}=\ell((\mathrm{div}q){C})
for all
q\in\relint\mathrm{F}_{D}
. Using the Riemann-Roch theorem and the fact that
1\leq\deg(\mathrm{div}q){C}=2(n+1-\deg D)
, we have:

\ell((\mathrm{div}q){C})=\deg(\mathrm{div}q){C}+\ell(K-(\mathrm{div}q){C})=2(n+1-\deg D)

concluding the proof.
∎

Theorem

5.1.2
completely describes the facial structure of the cone of nonnegative quadrics on an elliptic normal curve. Using the fact that the Veronese embedding of an elliptic normal curve is an elliptic normal curve,

5.1.2
also describes all faces of

. This is the first complete description of the facial structure of the nonnegative cone beyond Hilbert’s cases
[
44
]
. It is therefore the first non-rational example where such a description is shown.

We also remark that we do not explicitly see the distinction between the two possible topologies of

in

, as the theorem applies to both the connected and disconnected case. In particular, we are not using the real
2
-torsion points of the Jacobian which are not positive. Such points exist when

is disconnected, see

4.2.2

.

Auxiliary lemmas

We conclude the section presenting two useful lemmas about the signature signs of quadrics on connected components of elliptic normal curves, that will be needed in

.

Lemma 5.2.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real elliptic normal curve whose real locus has two connected components. If
Y\subset X(\mathbb{R})
is a connected component, then there exists
q\in\pos_{Y,2}\setminus\pos_{X,2}
(i.e.
q
is nonnegative on
Y
but changes sign on
X(\mathbb{R})
) such that
\mathrm{div}q=(\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{R}}=2D
, with
\supp(D)
consist of
\deg X=n+1
distinct points of
Y
.

Proof.

Fix
A_{0}\in X(\mathbb{R})
. Let
Y\subset X(\mathbb{R})
be a connected component, and let
A\in Y
be the (unique) point such that
[A-A_{0}]=\alpha\in\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}\setminus\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
(such a point exists since genus one curves are isomorphic to their Jacobian). Therefore, it follows from
[
22
, Problem 5.41]
that there exists
q_{A}\in R_{2}
such that
\mathrm{div}q_{A}=2(n+1)A
. As
[A-A_{0}]
is not a positive
2
-torsion point,
q_{A}
changes sign on
X(\mathbb{R})
, and we may assume that
q_{A}\in\pos_{Y,2}\setminus\pos_{X,2}
. Moving one pair of points at a time along
Y
, it is possible to continuously perturb the
(n+1)
-tuple
(A,\dots,A)
to
(A_{1},\dots,A_{n+1})\in Y^{n+1}
, in such a way the
A_{i}
’s are pairwise distinct and
A_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus A_{n+1}=A
.
Therefore there exists
q\in\pos_{Y,2}\setminus\pos_{X,2}
, perturbation of
q_{A}
, such that
\mathrm{div}q=2(A_{1}+\dots+A_{n+1})
, concluding the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.2.2
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real elliptic normal curve whose real locus has two connected components. If
X(\mathbb{R})=Y_{1}\sqcup Y_{2}
and
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}\in Y_{1}
, then there exists
q\in\mathbb R_{2}
such that:

(i)

(\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{R}}=2(B_{1}+\dots+B_{n})
;

(ii)

q
is nonnegative on
Y_{1}
;

(iii)

q
is strictly negative on
Y_{2}
.

Proof.

Consider the non-positive
2
-torsion points
\{\,T_{2},T_{3}\,\}=\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}\setminus\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
, and define
A_{i}=T_{i}\ominus B_{1}\ominus\dots\ominus B_{n}
for
i=2,3
. Therefore there exist
q_{2},q_{3}\in R_{2}
such that
\mathrm{div}q_{i}=(\mathrm{div}q_{i}){R}=2(B_{1}+\dots+B_{n}+A_{i})
for
i=2,3
. As
T_{2},T_{3}
are non-positive
2
-torsion points, then
q_{2},q_{3}
change sign on
X(\mathbb{R})
. In particular, we can assume that
q_{2},q_{3}
are nonnegative on
Y_{1}
and nonpositive on
Y_{2}
. We then define
q=q_{1}+q_{2}
. Notice that:

(i)

q
vanishes with multiplicity two at
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
, since both
q_{2}
and
q_{3}
vanish there with multiplicity two;

(ii)

q
in nonnegative on
Y_{1}
, since both
q_{2}
and
q_{3}
are nonnegative on
Y_{1}
;

(iii)

As
A_{2}\neq A_{3}
,
q
does not vanish at any point of
X(\mathbb{R})
except

, and
q
is strictly negative on
Y_{2}
.

This concludes the proof.
∎

6.
Carathéodory numbers for genus one curves

We refer to
Section

2.3
for the notations and terminology used in this section.
We investigate the Carathéodory number
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{X,2}
of
\pos^{\vee}=\pos_{X,2}^{\vee}
, where
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
is a totally real elliptic normal curve. We show that the Carathéodory number depends on the topology of the real locus
X(\mathbb{R})
, using a technique inspired by Hilbert’s proof on ternary quartics (see
[
9
]
for a modern exposition).

Using standard conic duality, we can use
Theorem

5.1.2
to describe linear functionals
L\in\partial\pos^{\vee}
on the boundary of the moment cone.
Indeed, if
L\in\partial\pos^{\vee}
then by conic duality there exists
q\in\pos
such that
L(q)=0
.
More precisely,
Theorem

5.1.2
implies the following corollary.

Corollary 6.0.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real elliptic normal curve. Let
\{\,O,T\,\}=\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
be the positive
2
-torsion points. We have

\displaystyle\partial\pos^{\vee}=

\displaystyle\left\{\,\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\,\mathrm{ev}_{A_{i}}\mid 1\leq k\leq n,\ A_{1},\dots,A_{k}\in X(\mathbb{R}),\ \lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\,\right\}

\displaystyle\sqcup\left\{\,\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n+1}\lambda_{i}\,\mathrm{ev}_{A_{i}}\mid\ A_{1},\dots,A_{n+1}\in X(\mathbb{R})\textnormal{ distinct},\ \lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n+1}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0},\ A_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus A_{n+1}=O\,\right\}

\displaystyle\sqcup\left\{\,\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n+1}\lambda_{i}\,\mathrm{ev}_{A_{i}}\mid\ A_{1},\dots,A_{n+1}\in X(\mathbb{R})\textnormal{ distinct},\ \lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n+1}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0},\ A_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus A_{n+1}=T\,\right\}

6.1.
Critical values and boundary points

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real elliptic normal curve. For
Y\subset X(\mathbb{R})
a connected component of
X(\mathbb{R})
, let
\pos_{Y,2d}\subset R_{2d}
be the cone of nonnegative forms on
Y
. As
Y
is not (Euclidean) dense in
X(\mathbb{R})
, we have
\pos_{Y,2d}\supsetneq\pos_{X,2d}
and
\pos_{Y,2d}^{\vee}\subsetneq\pos_{X,2d}^{\vee}
.

We will use the following notation:

•

Z\coloneqq v_{n,2}(X(\mathbb{R}))
;

•

If
S
is any set,

\cone_{k}(S)\coloneqq\left\{\,\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}s_{i}\colon s_{i}\in S,\ \lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\,\right\}

denotes the set of conic combinations of points of
S
which use at most
k
points from
S
.

As we have
v_{n,2}(A)\cong\mathrm{ev}_{A}\in R_{2}^{*}
, then we can characterize the Carathéodory number as:

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{X,2}=\min\left\{\,k\in\mathbb{N}\mid\cone_{k}(Z)=\pos^{\vee}\,\right\}

Lemma 6.1.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real elliptic normal curve. Then
n+1=\deg X\leq\mathcal{C}_{X,2}\leq\deg X+1=n+2
.

Proof.

The proof is a consequence of the following standard argument, see e.g.
[
17
, Th. 4.8]
.

Let
L=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathrm{ev}_{A_{i}}
be such that the
A_{i}
are distinct and
A_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus A_{n+1}\in\jac(\mathbb{R})_{2}^{+}
.
Then
L\in\partial(\pos^{\vee})
from
Corollary

6.0.1
. We deduce e.g. from
[
43
, Prop. 18.12]
that
\mathcal{C}(L)=n+1
, and thus
\mathcal{C}\geq n+1
.
Corollary

6.0.1
shows also that
\mathcal{C}(L)\leq n+1
for all
L\in\partial\pos^{\vee}
. Now, let
L\in\interior(\pos^{\vee})
. Since
\pos^{\vee}
is pointed and closed, for all
A\in X(\mathbb{R})
there exists
\lambda_{A}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
L-\lambda_{A}\mathrm{ev}_{A}\in\partial\pos^{\vee}
. By the above,
\mathcal{C}(L-\lambda\mathrm{ev}_{A})\leq n+1
holds for all
A\in X(\mathbb{R})
, and thus
\mathcal{C}(L)\leq n+2
.
∎

An equivalent result to
Lemma

6.1.1
was proven in
[
17
, Th. 4.8]
for general affine curves with compact real locus, and considering forms of sufficiently high degree
2d
.

Lemma

6.1.1
shows that there are only two possibilities for the Carathéodory number: In the following, we show that the exact value depends on the topology of
X(\mathbb{R})
. For the proof, we will use the following simple observation.

Lemma 6.1.2
.

Let
A\subset B\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be such that
A
is closed,
\interior A\neq\emptyset
and
B
is convex. Then
A=B
if and only if
\partial A\subset\partial B
.

Proof.

Clearly, if
A=B
then
\partial A=\partial B
.

For the converse implication, we show that
A\neq B
implies
\partial A\subsetneq\partial B
. Let
x\in\interior A\subset\interior B
and
y\in B\setminus A
. Consider now the segment between
x
and
y
. Since
B
is convex, this segment is contained in
B
, and all the points in the relative interior of the segment belong to the interior of
B
, see e.g.
[
41
, Th. 6.1]
. As
A
is closed,
x\in\interior A
and
y\in B\setminus A
, we can find
z
in the relative interior of the segment such that
z\in\partial A
. Therefore,
z\in\partial A\cap\interior B
, proving that
\partial A\subsetneq\partial B
.
∎

We are now ready to determine the Carathéodory number in the case of connected curves, or more generally for the moment cone on connected components of the real locus.

Proposition 6.1.3
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real elliptic normal curve, and let
Y
be a connected component of
X(\mathbb{R})
. Then
\pos_{Y,2}^{\vee}=\cone_{n+1}(v_{n,2}(Y))
, or, in other words,
\mathcal{C}_{Y,2}=\deg X=n+1
.

Proof.

Let
Z=v_{n,2}(Y)
. Our goal is to apply
Lemma

6.1.2
to
\cone_{n+1}(Z)\subset\pos_{Y,2}^{\vee}
, to show that they are equal. First, notice that
\pos_{Y,2}^{\vee}
is convex and that
\cone_{n+1}(Z)
is closed. Second,
\cone_{n+1}(Z)
has nonempty interior. Indeed, the proof of
[
13
, Th. 2]
, which shows that the smallest typical rank (with arbitrary real coefficients) is equal to the complex generic rank, can be generalized to the case of nonnegative coefficients (see also
[
38
, Lem. 37]
). As
n+1
is the complex generic rank (see
[
33
]
and also
Section

6.2
), the above implies that
\cone_{n+1}(Z)
has nonempty interior.

Therefore, in order to apply
Lemma

6.1.2
we only need to show that
\partial\cone_{n+1}(Z)\subset\partial(\pos_{Y,2})^{\vee}
. For this, we proceed similarly to Hilbert’s proof that every ternary quartic is a sum of at most three squares, see e.g.
[
9
]
. To make the analogy clearer, we identify the space
R_{2}^{*}
with
\mathcal{I}(X)^{\perp}\subset\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{2}
using the apolar or Bombieri-Weil inner product
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{BW}
, see
Section

6.2
for the definition and more details. If
B=(B_{0},\dots,B_{n}),v=(v_{0},\dots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
\ell_{B}=B_{0}x_{0}+\dots+B_{n}x_{n}
, then
\langle\ell_{B}^{2},q\rangle_{BW}=\mathrm{ev}_{B}(q)=q(B)
and
\langle 2\ell_{v}\ell_{B},q\rangle_{BW}=\partial_{v}q(B)
for all
q\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{2}
.

We can therefore consider the maps

\displaystyle\phi\colon(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})^{n+1}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{2}

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n+1})

\displaystyle\longmapsto\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n+1}\ell_{A_{i}}^{2}

\displaystyle\psi\colon\widehat{Y}^{n+1}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{I}(X)_{2}^{\perp}\cong R_{2}^{*}

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n+1})

\displaystyle\longmapsto\sumop\displaylimits_{i=0}^{n}\ell_{A_{i}}^{2}

By definition,
\psi
is the restriction of
\phi
to the affine cone over
Y
. Our goal is to show that
\partial\cone_{3}(Z)\cong\partial\imaginary\psi
is included in
\partial(\pos_{Y,2})^{\vee}
. The differential of
\phi
is well-known (see e.g
[
9
, Lem. 2.2]
), and therefore, by restriction we have:

\displaystyle\differential\psi_{\mathbf{A}}\colon\mathrm{T}_{\mathbf{A}}(\widehat{Y}^{n+1})\cong\mathrm{T}_{A_{1}}\widehat{Y}\times\dots\times\mathrm{T}_{A_{n+1}}\widehat{Y}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathrm{T}_{\psi(\mathbf{A})}(\mathcal{I}(X)_{2}^{\perp})\cong\mathcal{I}(X)_{2}^{\perp}

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\dots v_{n+1})

\displaystyle\longmapsto 2\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n+1}\ell_{v_{i}}\ell_{A_{i}}

Notice that
\dim\mathrm{T}_{\mathbf{A}}(\widehat{Y})^{n+1}=\dim\mathcal{I}(X)_{2}^{\perp}=2n+2
, and therefore
\differential\psi_{\mathbf{A}}
is a square linear map. In particular, if
\psi(\mathbf{A})\in\partial\imaginary\psi
then
\differential\psi_{\mathbf{A}}
does not have full rank. Using again the Bombieri-Weil inner product, this is equivalent to the fact that there exists
q\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{2}\setminus\mathcal{I}(X)_{2}^{\perp}
such that
\langle\ell_{v_{i}}\ell_{A_{i}},q\rangle_{BW}=0
for all
\mathbf{v}
.
Setting
v_{i}=A_{i}
(this is possible since
\widehat{Y}
is a cone), we have
\langle\ell_{A_{i}}^{2},q\rangle_{BW}=q(A_{i})=0
for all
i=1,\dots n+1
. Letting
v_{i}
vary in the tangent space, we see that
q
double vanishes at
A_{1},\dots,A_{n+1}
.

There are then two cases.

(i)

If the
A_{i}
’s are distinct,
q
(now seen as a nonzero element of
R_{2}=\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{2}\big/\mathcal{I}(X)_{2}
) is uniquely determined by the double vanishing at
A_{1},\dots,A_{n+1}
, i.e.
\mathrm{div}q=(\mathrm{div}q)_{\mathbb{R}}=2(A_{1}+\dots+A_{n+1})
. Since
Y
is connected,
q
does not change sign on
Y
, and therefore we can take
q
nonnegative on
Y
. By conic duality, this means that
\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathrm{ev}_{A_{i}}\cong\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n+1}\ell_{A_{i}}^{2}\in\partial(\pos_{Y,2})^{\vee}
.

(ii)

If the
A_{i}
’s are not distinct, we can always find a linear form
\ell\in R_{1}
vanishing on all of them, and we can take
q=\ell^{2}
. As in the previous point, since
q=\ell^{2}
is nonnegative on
Y
we have
\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathrm{ev}_{A_{i}}\cong\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n+1}\ell_{A_{i}}^{2}\in\partial(\pos_{Y,2})^{\vee}
.

The above two cases cover all the possible
\mathbf{A}
such that
\psi(\mathbf{A})\in\partial\imaginary\psi\cong\partial\cone_{n+1}(Z)
. We have then shown that
\partial\cone_{n+1}(Z)\subset\partial(\pos_{Y,2})^{\vee}
. We can therefore use
Lemma

6.1.2
to conclude that
\cone_{n+1}(Z)=(\pos_{Y,2})^{\vee}
.
In other words, we have proven that
\mathcal{C}_{Y,2}=n+1
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 6.1.4
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real elliptic normal curve. Then:

(i)

if
X(\mathbb{R})
is connected, then
\mathcal{C}_{X,2}=\deg X=n+1
;

(ii)

if
X(\mathbb{R})
is disconnected, then
\mathcal{C}_{X,2}=\deg X+1=n+2
.

Moreover the set of
L\in\pos^{\vee}
such that
\mathcal{C}(L)=\mathcal{C}_{X,2}
has nonempty interior.

Proof.

Assume that
X(\mathbb{R})
is connected. Then the result follows directly from
Proposition

6.1.3
.

Now assume that
X(\mathbb{R})
is disconnected, and set
Z=\nu_{n,2}(X(\mathbb{R}))
. By
Lemma

6.1.1
we only need to show that
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{X,2}>n+1
, or in other words that

\cone_{n+1}(\nu_{n,2}(X(\mathbb{R})))=\cone_{n+1}(Z)\subsetneq\pos_{X,2}^{\vee}=\pos^{\vee}.

While in the proof of
Proposition

6.1.3
we have shown that
\partial\cone_{n+1}(Z)\subset\partial(\pos_{Y,2}^{\vee})
if
X(\mathbb{R})
is connected, hereafter we want to show that
\partial\cone_{n+1}(Z)\subsetneq\partial(\pos_{X,2}^{\vee})
, i.e. that there exists
L\in\partial\cone_{n+1}(Z)\cap\interior(\pos_{X,2}^{\vee})
. This imples that
\cone_{n+1}(Z)\subsetneq\pos_{X,2}
, which is our claim. In order to prove the existence of
L\in\partial\cone_{n+1}(Z)\cap\interior(\pos_{X,2}^{\vee})
, we are going to use the fact that if

6.2.
Discussion: Carathéodory numbers and Waring decompositions

We hereafter summarize the equivalence between the computation of the Carathéodory numbers and the minimal rank of a certain Waring-type decomposition.

We briefly recall the
apolar
or
differential
or
Bombieri-Weil
inner product
[
28
]
.
Let
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{R}
. The inner product
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{BW}
on
\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x}]_{k}=\mathbb{K}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]_{k}
is defined on monomials
\mathbf{x}^{\alpha},\mathbf{x}^{\beta}
(and extended by linearity) as:

\langle\mathbf{x}^{\alpha},\mathbf{x}^{\beta}\rangle_{BW}\coloneqq\frac{1}{k!}{\partial_{\alpha}\,\mathbf{x}^{\beta}}(0)

One can show that, if
A=(A_{0},\dots,A_{k})\in\mathbb{K}^{n+1}
and
f\in\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x}]_{k}
, then
\langle\ell_{A}^{k},f\rangle_{BW}=f(A)=\mathrm{ev}_{A}(f)
, where
\mathrm{ev}_{A}\colon\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x}]_{k}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the usual point evaluation and
\ell_{A}=A_{0}x_{0}+\dots+A_{n}x_{n}
. Now let
Z\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a smooth, non-degenerate algebraic variety. If
L\in(\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x}]/I(Z))_{k}^{*}\cong I(Z)_{k}^{\perp}\subset\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x}]_{k}
,
A_{i}\in Z(\mathbb{K})
and
a_{i}\in\mathbb{K}
, then:

L=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\,\mathrm{ev}_{A_{i}}\iff f_{L}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\,\ell_{A_{i}}^{k}

where
f_{L}\in I(Z)^{\perp}\subset\mathbb{K}[Z]
is the unique polynomial representing
L=\langle f_{L},\,\cdot\,\rangle_{BW}
using the Riesz representation theorem. If
Z=\mathbb P^{n}
, such a decomposition of a form as a sum of powers of linear forms is called a
Waring decomposition
of rank
r
over
\mathbb{K}
(assuming that the
A_{i}
’s are pairwise distinct), and the minimal such
r
is called the
Waring rank
over
\mathbb{K}
of
f
. More generally, when
Z\subset\mathbb P^{n}
is a non-degenerate algebraic variety, such a minimal
r
is called the

Z
-rank of
f
over
\mathbb{K}
. If we write
Z=v_{n,2d}(X)
for the second Veronese embedding, the Carathéodory number
\mathcal{C}_{X,2d}(L)
corresponds to the
nonnegative

Z
-rank of
f_{L}
, i.e. to the
Z
-rank over
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, see e.g.
[
38
,
10
,
1
]
. We say that a nonnegative
Z
-rank
r
is
typical
if the set of forms of nonnegative
Z
-rank equal to
r
has nonempty interior. Using this terminology,
Corollary

6.1.5
can be rephrased as follows.

Corollary 6.2.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real elliptic normal curve and denote
Z=\nu_{n,2d}(X)
. Then, for linear functionals in the moment cone
\pos_{X,2d}^{\vee}
:

(i)

if
X(\mathbb{R})
is connected, then the maximal nonnegative
Z
-rank is
d(n+1)
;

(ii)

if
X(\mathbb{R})
is disconnected, then the maximal nonnegative
Z
-rank is
d(n+1)+1
.

Furthermore, the maximal nonnegative
Z
-rank is always typical.

Let us also remark on another connection between our study of Carathéodory numbers and secant varieties. The proof of
Theorem

6.1.4
relies on the study of the boundary points of the set of linear functionals obtained using at most
n+1
point evaluations. In particular, we study the tangent space at the boundary, which is of a smaller dimension than the expected one. In the language of tensor decomposition, those points define what is called
Terraccini locus
. For more information and precise definitions on the Terraccini locus, we refer the reader to the recent work
[
2
]
and references therein.

We finally state some lemmas on the geometry of the representations of linear functionals as a sum of point evaluations. They will be used in
Section

7
to exclude the points at infinity from the allowed point evaluations. Their proofs are mostly applications of standard techniques from projective and convex geometry, and are provided in
Appendix

B
. The first lemma is a particular case of
[
14
, Prop. 5.2]
.

Lemma 6.2.2
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be an elliptic normal curve,
Z=v_{n,2d}(X)
, and

L=\mathrm{ev}_{A_{1}}+\dots+\mathrm{ev}_{A_{d(n+1)}}\ \in\left(\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}]_{2d}\big/I(X)_{2d}\right)^{*}

If
A_{1},\dots,A_{d(n+1)}
are distinct and
A_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus A_{d(n+1)}
is not a
2
-torsion point of
X
, then
L

has two distinct representations of
Z
-rank (over
\mathbb{C}
) equal to
d(n+1)
.

We now adapt
Lemma

6.2.2
to the
nonnegative

Z
-rank: in
Lemma

6.2.3
we treat the generic case, while in
Lemma

6.2.4
we consider special cases.

Lemma 6.2.3
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real elliptic normal curve and
Z=v_{n,2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))
.
All linear functions
L\in\cone_{d(n+1)}(Z)\setminus\partial\cone_{d(n+1)}(Z)
(which is a Euclidean dense subset of
\cone_{d(n+1)}(Z)
) admit exactly two different representations:

L=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{d(n+1)}a_{i}\,\mathrm{ev}_{A_{i}}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{d(n+1)}b_{i}\,\mathrm{ev}_{B_{i}}

with
a_{i},b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
A_{i},B_{i}\in X(\mathbb{R})
.

Lemma 6.2.4
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real elliptic normal curve and
Z=v_{n,2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))
.
All linear functionals
L\in\partial\cone_{d(n+1)}(Z)\setminus\cone_{d(n+1)-1}(Z)
admit at most two representations as in
Lemma

6.2.3
.

In the previous lemmas, we focused on representations using

points, showing that they are generically finitely many. On the other hand, when we use

admits infinitely many representations.
More precisely, we can show that every linear functional in the relative interior of
\pos_{X,2d}^{\vee}
has a

Lemma 6.2.5
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real elliptic normal curve and let

. Then

has a
2
-dimensional family of representations using

We now use

to show that there exist representations of linear functionals in

whose atoms avoid finitely many points in

Lemma 6.2.6
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{n}
be a totally real elliptic normal curve and
let

Remark 6.2.7
.

We make some remarks on the previous results.

(i)

In the proof of

, we are implicitly using the fact that, if

.

(ii)

Assuming that

is equal to the nonnegative

: in general, the rank of

(iii)

7.
The moment problem for plane cubics

In this section, we consider
affine
genus one curves, and see how the results in
Section

6
can be exploited to solve the
moment problem
on such curves. In particular, we show how the topology of the real locus and the number of points at infinity can be used to characterize the
flat extension
degree. In the following, affine curves and their real loci will be denoted by
X
and
X(\mathbb{R})
respectively, while their projective closures will be denoted by
\overline{X}
and
\overline{X}(\mathbb{R})
.

In recent years, the case of
plane
curves has been particularly investigated, see
[
21
,
48
,
49
,
47
,
7
]
. For ease of presentation and comparison, we restrict ourselves to the same case. i.e. we consider a smooth totally real affine plane cubic
X(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

7.1.
Flat extensions and almost flat extensions

We now briefly introduce the relevant definitions and notations for this section.

Let
X(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a totally real affine plane cubic.
Given

L\in R_{\leq 2d}^{*}=\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq 2d}^{*}=\left(\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{\leq d}\big/I(X)_{\leq d}\right)^{*}

we can consider the bilinear form

\displaystyle M_{d}(L)\colon R_{\leq d}\times R_{\leq d}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(q_{1},q_{2})

\displaystyle\longmapsto L(q_{1}q_{2})

Equivalently,
M_{d}(L)
is the linear map:

\displaystyle M_{d}(L)\colon R_{\leq d}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow R_{\leq d}^{*}

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle\longmapsto L\circ{m}_{q}

where
{m}_{q}\colon R_{\leq d}\to R_{\leq 2d}
denotes the multiplication by
q\in R_{\leq d}
.
We call the matrix representing
M_{d}(L)
(with respect to any basis of
R_{\leq d}
) the
moment matrix
of
L
. We refer to
[
43
]
for more details. These matrices are also known as
Catalecticant matrices

[
28
]
(in the case
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
) or
Hankel matrices

[
37
]
.
We also notice that
\rank M_{d}(L)\leq\dim R_{\leq d}=3d
.

Similarly to the homogeneous case, we denote

\pos_{X,\leq 2d}=\{\,q\in R_{\leq 2d}\colon q(A)\geq 0\text{ for all }A\in X(\mathbb{R})\,\}.

Notice that, if
L\in\pos_{X,\leq 2d}^{\vee}
, then the moment matrix
M_{d}(L)
is positive semidefinite.

As
R_{2d}\cong R_{\leq 2d}
by homogenization, we also have
\pos_{X,\leq 2d}\cong\pos_{\overline{X},2d}
and
\pos_{X,\leq 2d}^{\vee}\cong\pos_{\overline{X},2d}^{\vee}
. To emphasize the fact that we are working in an affine setting, hereafter we use the notations
R_{\leq 2d}
and
\pos_{X,\leq 2d}
.

We denote
\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))
the
moment cone
, i.e. the convex cone of linear functionals acting on polynomials of degree
\leq 2d
which are induced by a Borel measure supported on
X(\mathbb{R})
. Using the Richter-Tchakaloff theorem
[
43
, Th. 1.24]
, we can describe
\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))
using only conic sums of point evaluations at
X(\mathbb{R})
:

\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))=\cone(\mathrm{ev}_{A}\colon A\in X(\mathbb{R}))

If
X(\mathbb{R})
is compact then
\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))=\pos_{X,2d}^{\vee}
. On the other hand, if
X(\mathbb{R})
is not compact, then
\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))
is not closed and the inclusion
\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))\subsetneq\pos_{X,\leq 2d}^{\vee}
is proper: this is always the case for plane cubics
X(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

We are now ready to recall the notion of flat extension.

Definition 7.1.1
.

A
positive flat extension
of
L\in R_{\leq 2d}^{*}
is any
\widetilde{L}\in R_{\leq 2d+2}^{*}
such that:

(i)

\widetilde{L}\mid_{{R_{\leq 2d}}}=L
;

(ii)

\rank M_{d+1}(\widetilde{L})=\rank M_{d}(L)
;

(iii)

M_{d+1}(\widetilde{L})
is positive semidefinite.

Testing the existence of a flat extension is the standard algorithmic tool to verify if
L
is a moment linear functional or not, see e.g.
[
16
,
43
,
37
]
.
Our definition of flat extension is slightly different from others present in the literature for the case of curves (see e.g.
[
21
]
), in terms of how we enforce the atoms of any representation of
L
to lie on
X(\mathbb{R})
. We refer to
Remark

7.1.4
for a comparison.

The rank of the flat extension determines the number of atoms needed to represent
L
as conic combination of point evaluations on
X(\mathbb{R})
, see e.g.
[
43
, Th. 17.36]
.
Moreover, if a flat extension is found, then the flat extension can be further extended from degree
d+1
to an arbitrary degree. Notice also that, since
\widetilde{L}\mid_{{R_{\leq 2d}}}=L
, the matrix
M_{d}(L)
can be identified with the matrix
M_{d}(\widetilde{L})
, which is a submatrix of
M_{d+1}(\widetilde{L})
.

In other words, the rank of a flat extension gives the (affine) Carathéodory number of a linear functional. We define the Carathéodory number for the affine plane curve
X
as in the projective case, as follows:

\mathcal{C}_{X,\leq 2d}\coloneqq\min\left\{\,k\in\mathbb{N}\mid\cone_{k}(\nu_{2,d}(X(\mathbb{R})))=\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))\,\right\}

Note that we are not allowed to use atoms at infinity, i.e. point evaluations from
\overline{X}(\mathbb{R})\setminus X(\mathbb{R})
.

In the following, we will also need a slight generalization of
Definition

7.1.1
.

Definition 7.1.2
.

An
almost flat extension
of
L\in R_{\leq 2d}
is any
\widetilde{L}\in R_{\leq 2d+4}^{*}
such that:

(i)

\widetilde{L}\mid_{{R_{\leq 2d}}}=L
;

(ii)

\rank M_{d+2}(\widetilde{L})\leq\rank M_{d}(L)+1
;

(iii)

M_{d+2}(\widetilde{L})
is positive semidefinite.

Remark 7.1.3
.

If
\widetilde{L}
is a flat extension of
L
, then
\widetilde{L}
can be further extended to an almost flat extension of
L
. More precisely, if
L
admits a flat extension
\widetilde{L}
, then
\widetilde{L}\in R_{\leq 2d+2}^{*}
admits a flat extension
\widetilde{\widetilde{L}}\in R_{\leq 2d+4}^{*}
such that:

\rank M_{d+2}(\widetilde{\widetilde{L}})=\rank M_{d+1}(\widetilde{L})=\rank M_{d}(L).

On the contrary, if
L
does not admit a flat extension, but it admits an almost flat extension
\widetilde{L}
, we necessarily have:

\rank M_{d+2}(\widetilde{L})=\rank M_{d+1}(\widetilde{L})=\rank M_{d}(L)+1.

In
Section

7.2
we show that we can characterize solutions to the moment problem on plane cubics using almost flat extensions.

Remark 7.1.4
.

We compare our definitions with the ones existing in the moment literature, e.g. in
[
21
, Th. 1.1]
. The main difference is that, instead of working with all the monomials up to a certain degree and imposing extra conditions for the representing measure to be supported on the plane curve, we directly work in the quotient
R_{\leq 2d}=\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{\leq 2d}\big/I(X)_{\leq 2d}
. We also speak of the flat extension of a linear functional, instead of a flat extension of the corresponding moment matrix.
Concretely, a
p-pure
moment matrix in
[
21
, Th. 1.1]
corresponds to a positive definite moment matrix in this manuscript, and a
positive, recursively generated flat moment matrix extension
in
[
21
, Th. 1.1]
simply corresponds to our flat extension.

7.2.
The moment problem for plane cubics

We are now ready to show how to solve the moment problem, i.e. how to characterize membership
L\in\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))
, using almost flat extensions, see

7.1.2
.

Proposition 7.2.1
.

Let
X(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

\overline{X}
is smooth. Given
L\in R_{\leq 2d}^{*}

(i)

L\in\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))

(ii)

l
has an almost flat extension
\widetilde{L}\in R_{\leq 2d+4}

Proof.

(i)\implies(ii)
Recall that, by homogenization,
\pos_{X,\leq 2d}\cong\pos_{\overline{X},2d}
. Therefore, given
L\in\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))\subset\pos_{X,\leq 2d}^{\vee}
, it follows from
Corollary

6.1.5
that we can always write

L=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{3d+1}a_{i}\,\mathrm{ev}_{A_{i}}

where, for all

,
a_{i}\geq 0
and
A_{i}
belongs to
\overline{X}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathbb P^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the real locus of the projective closure
\overline{X}
of
X
. From

6.2.6
, we deduce that there exists a representation for which
A_{i}\in X(\mathbb{R})
(we are excluding the finitely many points at infinity).
Therefore
L
admits the almost flat extension
\widetilde{L}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{3d+1}a_{i}\,\mathrm{ev}_{A_{i}}\in R_{2d+4}^{*}
.

(i)\implies(ii)
If
L
admits an almost flat extension
\widetilde{L}
, then either
\rank M_{d+1}(\widetilde{L})=\rank M_{d}(L)
or
\rank M_{d+2}(\widetilde{L})=\rank M_{d+1}(\widetilde{L})
. From the flat extension theorem, we deduce that, in both cases,
L
can be represented as an atomic measure using point evaluations in
X(\mathbb{R})
, which implies
L\in\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))
.
∎

We showed in
Proposition

7.2.1
that, extending the degree by
4
and considering an almost flat extension, it is possible to characterize whether
L\in\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))
or not. A natural question then arises: is it necessary to consider almost flat extensions, or can we replace them with ordinary flat extensions? In the following, we are going to show how the answer to this question depends on the topology of
X(\mathbb{R})
and the number of points at infinity of the projective closure
\overline{X}(\mathbb{R})
.

Theorem 7.2.2
.

Let
X
be an affine, smooth, totally real plane cubic. Assume that
X(\mathbb{R})
is connected and that the projective closure
\overline{X}(\mathbb{R})
contains only one real point at infinity. Then, given
L\in R_{\leq 2d}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

L\in\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))
;

(ii)

L
has a positive flat extension
\widetilde{L}\in R_{\leq 2d+2}^{*}
.

Moreover, the affine and projective Carathéodory numbers are equal:
\mathcal{C}_{X,\leq 2d}=\mathcal{C}_{\overline{X},2d}=3d
.

Proof.

For
(ii)\implies(i)
, we can proceed as in
Proposition

7.2.1
.

For
(i)\implies(ii)
, it follows from
Theorem

6.1.4
that
L
can be written using at most
3d
points from
\overline{X}(\mathbb{R})
. We now show that at least one such representation has all points in the affine part
X(\mathbb{R})
.

If
L\in\partial\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))
, it follows e.g. from
[
43
, Prop. 18.12]
that
L
has a unique representation as a sum of point evaluations in
X(\mathbb{R})
. This unique representation uses at most
3d
point evaluations, and thus we can construct a flat extension of
L
using such a representation.

If
L\in\interior\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))
, then
Lemma

6.2.3
implies that
L
admits two different representations as sum of
3d
point evaluations. Such distinct representations cannot share any point, and therefore the fact that
\overline{X}(\mathbb{R})
has a unique point at infinity implies that one of the two uses only points in
X(\mathbb{R})
. We can therefore construct a flat extension for
L
from this representation with only points in
X(\mathbb{R})
.

The fact that
\mathcal{C}_{X,\leq 2d}=\mathcal{C}_{\overline{X},2d}
follows from the previous points, as the existence of representations using points from
\overline{X}(\mathbb{R})
implies that existence of representations using points from
X(\mathbb{R})
with the same number of atoms.
∎

We now construct an example which illustrates that
\overline{X}(\mathbb{R})
having a unique point at infinity is a necessary assumption in
Theorem

7.2.2
.

Example 7.2.3
.

Let
\widetilde{X}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the real locus of the affine totally real plane cubic, and assume that
\widetilde{X}(\mathbb{R})
is connected. Let
L\in\interior\mathcal{M}_{2d}(\widetilde{X}(\mathbb{R}))\subset\pos_{\widetilde{X},\leq 2d}^{\vee}
.

We deduce from
Lemma

6.2.3
that
L
admits exactly two distinct representations

L=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{3d}a_{i}\,\mathrm{ev}_{\widetilde{A}_{i}}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{3d}b_{i}\,\mathrm{ev}_{\widetilde{B}_{i}}

using
3d
points form the projective closure of
\widetilde{X}(\mathbb{R})
.
Now consider the (real) line between
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
, and apply a projective linear change of coordinates in such a way the line between
\widetilde{A}_{1}
and
\widetilde{B}_{1}
is sent to the line at infinity.
Let
X(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the affine real locus of
\widetilde{X}(\mathbb{R})
under this change of coordinates.

We have
L\in\interior\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))=\interior\mathcal{M}_{2d}(\widetilde{X}(\mathbb{R}))
, and as before
L
admits only two representations

L=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{3d}a_{i}\,\mathrm{ev}_{A_{i}}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{3d}b_{i}\,\mathrm{ev}_{B_{i}}\in\pos_{\widetilde{X},\leq 2d}^{\vee}=\pos_{X,\leq 2d}^{\vee}

where
A_{i},B_{i}
denote the images of
\widetilde{A}_{i},\widetilde{B}_{i}
after the change of coordinates above. Notice that both representations use a point at infinity, i.e.
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
respectively, by construction.

This shows that
L\in\interior\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))
cannot be represented using
3d
evaluations at points of the affine curve
X(\mathbb{R})
, and thus
L
does not admit a flat extension. Therefore, we cannot remove the condition that
\overline{X}(\mathbb{R})
has a unique point at infinity from
Theorem

7.2.2
, to conclude that the flat extension condition is sufficient.
Notice also that the above discussion implies that
\mathcal{C}_{X,\leq 2d}(L)=3d+1
, but
\mathcal{C}_{\overline{X},2d}=3d
, and thus
\mathcal{C}_{\overline{X},2d}<\mathcal{C}_{X,\leq 2d}
. It is therefore not possible to compute the affine Carathéodory numbers using solely the projective information.

We conjecture that such pathological behavior happens for all affine plane cubics with at least two real points at infinity.

We now turn our attention to the disconnected case.

Theorem 7.2.4
.

Let
X(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the affine real locus of a totally real plane cubic, whose projectivization
\overline{X}
is smooth. Assume that
X(\mathbb{R})
has two connected components. Then, given
L\in R_{\leq 2d}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

L\in\mathcal{M}_{2d}(X(\mathbb{R}))
;

(ii)

L
has a positive almost flat extension
\widetilde{L}\in R_{\leq 2d+4}^{*}
.

The almost flat extension condition cannot be replaced by flat extension in (ii). Moreover, the affine and projective Carathéodory numbers are equal:
\mathcal{C}_{X,\leq 2d}=\mathcal{C}_{\overline{X},2d}=3d+1
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

7.2.1
, we only need to show that there exists

which does not admit a flat extension. For this, it is sufficient to show that
\mathcal{C}_{\overline{X},2d}(L)>3d
. By
Theorem

6.1.4
, such an
L
exists when
X(\mathbb{R})
has two connected components. This also implies that
\mathcal{C}_{X,\leq 2d}=\mathcal{C}_{\overline{X},2d}=3d+1
.
∎

We notice that the solution of the moment problem proposed in
Theorem

7.2.4
with the almost flat extension condition is much more complicated than the usual flat extension one, exploited in
Theorem

7.2.2
and
[
21
,
48
,
49
,
47
,
7
]
. However, this increased complexity cannot be avoided, as it is a direct consequence of the higher Carathéodory number.

Appendix A
Plane cubic curves

We begin with the example of nonnegative quadrics on cubic plane curves, where we illustrate our results in detail and provide explicit certificates on nonnegativity obtained by a direct computation.

A.1.
Plane cubics

Plane cubics are the first example of genus one curves. For their basic properties, we refer e.g. to
[
45
,
22
]
. In this case, the analysis of
\pos_{X,2}
can be performed explicitly and completely.

Plane cubics are curves in
\mathbb P^{2}
defined as the zero locus of a form of degree three. In the following, we always assume that our plane cubics are smooth, irreducible, and totally real.
Recall that the group law of a plane cubic
X
is induced by the isomorphism
between the plane cubic and the Jacobian:

\displaystyle\varphi_{P_{0}}:X

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\Cl^{0}X\cong\jac

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle\longrightarrow[P-P_{0}]

Hereafter, we consider
P_{0}=O
to be a smooth real inflection point,
so that our plane cubic has equation (Weierstrass form):

h=x_{2}^{2}x_{0}-\left(x_{1}-a_{1}x_{0}\right)\left(x_{1}-a_{2}x_{0}\right)\left(x_{1}-a_{3}x_{0}\right)=0

with
a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
.
X
is non-singular if and only if

a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
’s are distinct.

Denote
\oplus
and
\ominus
the group operations in
X
. Then the point at infinity
O
is the identity,
and

T_{1}=\left(1:a_{1}:0\right),T_{2}=\left(1:a_{2}:0\right),T_{3}=\left(1:a_{3}:0\right)

are
the
3
nontrivial
2
-torsion points for
(X,\oplus)
(see also
Definition

4.2.1
).

Since we are assuming that
X
is totally real, then at least one of the
a_{i}
is real,
say
a_{1}
, while
a_{2}
and
a_{3}
can be either:

(i)

Complex conjugates
a_{2}=\overline{a_{3}}
,
and in this case
X(\mathbb{R})
is connected in the Euclidean topology; or

(ii)

both real (and distinct, if
X
is smooth),
and in this case
X(\mathbb{R})

has two connected components. In such a case, we will always assume without loss of generality that
a_{1}<a_{2}<a_{3}
.

These are the two possible topologies for a genus one totally real curve. In the first case, there are only two real
2
-torsion points,
O
and
T_{1}
, while in the second case there are four. This is consistent with
Lemma

4.2.2
.

We focus on quadratic forms on
X
, and we start by analyzing the extreme rays of
\pos_{X,2}
, which are given by forms with the maximal possible number of real zeroes. Given a quadric
q\in\mathbb{R}[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}]_{2}\cong\mathbb{R}[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}]_{2}/(h)_{2}=\mathbb{R}[X]_{2}
, we denote
\mathrm{div}q=h\cdot q
the intersection divisor of
q
and
h
(or of
q
and
X=\mathcal{V}(h)
), see e.g.
[
22
]
. If
q
is nonnegative on
X(\mathbb{R})
, then
q
has zeroes on
X(\mathbb{R})
with even multiplicity only. By Bézout’s theorem, there are at most three such zeroes. We then deduce from
[
22
, Problem 5.41]
that, given
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}\in X(\mathbb{R})
, there exists
q\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{2}
such that
\mathrm{div}q=2(A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3})
if and only
A_{1}\oplus A_{2}\oplus A_{3}
is a 2-torsion point for
(X,\oplus)
.

If
X(\mathbb{R})
is connected, then
\mathrm{div}q=2(A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3})
implies that
q
is nonnegative.
On the other hand, if
X(\mathbb{R})
is disconnected then
\mathrm{div}q=2(A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3})
does not imply that
q
is nonnegative. We now show that in the disconnected case, whether
q
is nonnegative or not depends on the
2
-torsion point
A_{1}\oplus A_{2}\oplus A_{3}
.

If
A_{1}\oplus A_{2}\oplus A_{3}=O
, then there exists a linear form
\ell\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{1}
such that
\mathrm{div}\ell=A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}
, and thus
q=a\,\ell^{2}
for some
a\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. This shows that
q
is a sum of squares and thus nonnegative.

We now deal with the other cases. In the following, we denote
\ell_{A_{1},A_{2}}
the line passing through
A_{1},A_{1}\in X(\mathbb{R})
, and
\ell_{A}
the line tangent to
A\in X(\mathbb{R})
. Recall from the definition of the group law that the third intersection point of
\ell_{A_{1},A_{2}}
with
X(\mathbb{R})
is equal to
\ominus A_{1}\ominus A_{2}
, i.e.
\mathrm{div}\ell_{A_{1},A_{2}}=A_{1}+A_{2}+(\ominus A_{1}\ominus A_{2})
.
Assume that
A_{1}\oplus A_{2}\oplus A_{3}=T_{i}
for some
i\in\{\,1,2,3\,\}
(recall that we have chosen indices in such a way
a_{1}<a_{2}<a_{3}
). A direct computation shows that:

(1)

\mathrm{div}q=\mathrm{div}\frac{\ell_{A_{1},A_{2}}^{2}\ell_{A_{1}\oplus A_{2},A_{3}}^{2}}{\ell_{\ominus A_{1}\ominus A_{2},A_{1}\oplus A_{2}}^{2}\ell_{T_{i}}^{2}}\ell_{O}\ell_{T_{i}}

and since
X
is irreducible, the two expressions agree (up to a positive constant) on
X(\mathbb{R})
. Therefore,
q
is nonnegative if and only if
\ell_{O}\ell_{T_{i}}
is nonnegative. For
i=2,3
it is easy to show that
\ell_{O}\ell_{T_{i}}
changes sign on
X(\mathbb{R})
, and thus
q
is not nonnegative. On the other hand, for
i=1
we have:

(2)

(\ell_{T_{1}}^{2}+(a_{2}-a_{1})(a_{3}-a_{1})\ell_{O}^{2})\,\ell_{O}\ell_{T_{1}}=x_{2}^{2}\ell_{O}^{2}+(a_{2}+a_{3}-2a_{1})\ell_{O}^{2}\ell_{T_{1}}^{2}

which shows that
\ell_{O}\ell_{T_{1}}
, and thus
q
, is nonnegative on
X(\mathbb{R})
.

In conclusion,
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}
define a quadric
q
such that
\mathrm{div}q=2(A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3})
which is nonnegative if and only if:

(i)

either
A_{1}\oplus A_{2}\oplus A_{3}=O
, and in this case
q=\ell^{2}
is a square;

(ii)

or
A_{1}\oplus A_{2}\oplus A_{3}=T
, where
T
is the unique nontrivial real
2
-torsion point such that
\ell_{O}\ell_{T}
is nonnegative on
X(\mathbb{R})
.

These quadrics span extreme rays of
\pos=\pos_{X,2}
since they have the maximal number of real zeroes: there are therefore two families of extreme rays defined by the two positive
2
-torsion points
O
and
T
. This is consistent with
Lemma

4.2.5
.

We now study other faces. In particular, recall from
Theorem

3.2.5
that all the faces
\mathrm{F}\subset\pos
are of the form
\mathrm{F}=\mathrm{F}_{A_{1}+\dots+A_{k}}
, with
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}\in X(\mathbb{R})
.

If
k\geq 4
, then
\mathrm{F}_{A_{1}+\dots+A_{k}}=\{\,0\,\}
by Bézout’s theorem. For
k=3
, we deduce from the above discussion that either
\dim\mathrm{F}_{A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}}=1
, when the
A_{i}
’s are in special position, otherwise
\mathrm{F}_{A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}}=\{\,0\,\}
.

For
k=2
, we can consider the two additional points
B_{1}=O\ominus A_{1}\ominus A_{2}
and
B_{2}=T\ominus A_{1}\ominus A_{2}
. Then
\mathrm{F}_{A_{1}+A_{2}+B_{1}}
and
\mathrm{F}_{A_{1}+A_{2}+B_{2}}
are extreme rays, and
\mathrm{F}_{A_{1}+A_{2}}=\cone(\mathrm{F}_{A_{1}+A_{2}+B_{1}},\mathrm{F}_{A_{1}+A_{2}+B_{2}})
has dimension
2
. Similarly, for
k=1
we can show that

\dim\mathrm{F}_{A_{1}}=4
.
In conclusion, we proved the following.

Proposition A.1.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb P^{2}
be a smooth totally real plane cubic, and denote
\pos=\pos_{X,2}
the convex cone of nonnegative quadratic forms. Let
T
be the unique nontrivial
2
-torsion point of
(X,\oplus)
such that
\ell_{O}\ell_{T}
is nonnegative. Then all the proper faces of
\pos
are the following:

(i)

\mathrm{F}_{A}
for
A\in X(\mathbb{R})
, and we have
\dim\mathrm{F}_{A}=4
;

(ii)

\mathrm{F}_{A_{1}+A_{2}}
for
A_{1},A_{2}\in X(\mathbb{R})
, and we have
\dim\mathrm{F}_{A_{1}+A_{2}}=2
.

(iii)

\mathrm{F}_{A_{1}+A_{2}+B_{1}}
with
A_{1},A_{2}\in X(\mathbb{R})
and
B_{1}=O\ominus A_{1}\ominus A_{2}
; in this case
\mathrm{F}_{A_{1}+A_{2}+B_{1}}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot\ell^{2}
is an extreme ray of the cone of sums of squares and nonnegative quadrics.

(iv)

\mathrm{F}_{A_{1}+A_{2}+B_{2}}
with
A_{1},A_{2}\in X(\mathbb{R})
and
B_{2}=T\ominus A_{1}\ominus A_{2}
; in this case
\mathrm{F}_{A_{1}+A_{2}+B_{1}}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot q
is an extreme ray of the nonnegative quadrics and
q
is not a sum of squares.

In particular, notice that all the extreme rays of
\pos
arise from quadrics with the maximal possible number of real zeroes, and all higher dimensional faces have the expected dimension, equal to
6=\dim\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{2}
minus two times the number of common zeroes of the quadrics in the face.
Similar elementary considerations can be applied for higher degree nonnegative forms.

Let us also remark that
Equations

1
and
2
can be combined to give explicit certificates of nonnegativity for all the extreme rays of
\pos
. Indeed, if
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot q
is an extreme ray of
\pos
, then
Equations

1
and
2
imply that there exists
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that:

q=\alpha\,\frac{\ell_{A_{1},A_{2}}^{2}\ell_{A_{1}\oplus A_{2},A_{3}}^{2}\left(x_{2}^{2}\ell_{O}^{2}+(a_{2}+a_{3}-2a_{1})\ell_{O}^{2}\ell_{T_{1}}^{2}\right)}{\ell_{\ominus A_{1}\ominus A_{2},A_{1}\oplus A_{2}}^{2}\ell_{T_{i}}^{2}\left(\ell_{T_{1}}^{2}+(a_{2}-a_{1})(a_{3}-a_{1})\ell_{O}^{2}\right)}\text{ on }X(\mathbb{R})

where
X
is a totally real plane cubic given by the equation in Weierstrass form

x_{2}^{2}x_{0}-\left(x_{1}-a_{1}x_{0}\right)\left(x_{1}-a_{2}x_{0}\right)\left(x_{1}-a_{3}x_{0}\right)=0

The above Artin-type certificates for extreme rays, can be combined in convex combinations to provide explicit certificates of nonnegativity for all nonnegative quadrics
q\in\pos
. We also remark that such certificates do not have the smallest possible degree: indeed, in
[
12
, Th. 1.1]
is shown the existence of certificates with a denominator of degree two and numerator of degree four. On the other hand, the certificate given in
Equation

2
is of minimal possible degree.

From
Corollary

6.1.5
, we deduce that
\imaginary\psi=\pos_{X,2}^{\vee}
, which is a
2(n+1)
-dimensional convex cone. We want to show that, for
L\in\interior\pos_{X,2}^{\vee}
,
\dim\psi^{-1}(L)
has dimension two.

As in the proof of
Proposition

6.1.3
, we notice that
\differential\psi_{\mathbf{A}}
is not surjective if and only if there exists a double vanishing quadratic form
q\in R_{2}
at
\mathbf{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n+2})
.
As
\deg\mathrm{div}q=\deg q\cdot\deg X=2(n+1)
, this can happen if and only if the
A_{i}
’s are not distinct and they admit a double vanishing quadraitc form through them. Therefore, if the
A_{i}
’s are distinct then
\mathbf{A}
is a regular point of
\psi
, and

\dim\psi^{-1}(\psi(\mathbf{A}))=\dim\widehat{X}(\mathbb{R})^{n+2}-\dim R_{2}^{*}=2

It is then sufficient to show that every linear functional in the relative interior of
\pos_{X,2}^{\vee}
is the image of a regular point of
\psi
. We proceed as in the proof of
Lemma

6.1.1
: let
L\in\interior\pos_{X,2}^{\vee}
. For all
A\in\widehat{X}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathbb{S}^{n}
, there exists
\lambda=\lambda(A)\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that

L-\lambda^{2}\mathrm{ev}_{A}=L-\lambda^{2}\ell_{A}^{2}=q-\ell_{\lambda A}^{2}\in\partial\pos_{X,2}^{\vee}

and
L-\ell_{\lambda A}^{2}
can be represented in a unique way as a sum of
n+1
point evaluations (see the proof of
Lemma

6.1.1
). Therefore, if the
n+1
points are distinct and different from
A
, then
L
is the image of a regular point of
\psi
, as desired. If the
n+1
points and
A
are not distinct for all
A\in X(\mathbb{R})
, then
L
would admit a representation as a sum of
n+1
point evaluations using every
A\in X(\mathbb{R})
, contradicting
Lemmas

6.2.3
and
6.2.4
. Thus
L
has a representation using
n+2
distinct point evaluations, and it is the image of a regular point of
\psi
, as desired.
∎

We note that the proof of
Lemma

6.2.5
can be simplified if
X(\mathbb{R})
is connected. Indeed, in this case all the linear functionals in the relative interior of
\pos_{X,2}^{\vee}
are regular values of
\psi
, and we can immediately conclude that all the fibers have dimension two. On the other hand, if
X(\mathbb{R})
is disconnected then
\psi
has critical values in the interior of
\pos_{X,2}^{\vee}
, and the proof cannot be easily simplified.
