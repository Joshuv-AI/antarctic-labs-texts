Abstract
. In this note we prove uniqueness of the critical point for positive solutions of elliptic problems in bounded planar domains: we first examine the Poisson problem − Δ ​ u = f ⁡ ( x , y ) -\Delta u=f(x,y) finding a geometric condition involving the curvature of the boundary and the normal derivative of f f on the boundary to ensure uniqueness of the critical point. In the second part we consider stable solutions of the nonlinear problem − Δ ​ u = f ⁡ ( u ) -\Delta u=f(u) in perturbation of convex domains.



1.
Introduction and main results

In this note we are interested in investigating the number of critical points of solutions of elliptic equations. Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a smooth and bounded domain and consider the following problem

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=f\left((x,y),u\right)&\text{in }\Omega\\
u>0&\text{in }\Omega\\
u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
f:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function.
It is known that the number of critical points of solutions of the previous problem is strongly influenced by the function
f
and by the geometry of the domain
\Omega
. In this context the literature is too wide to report all the result, so we just focus on some of them which are closest to the interest of this paper (see
[
17
,
13
]
for a recent survey on the topic in the case
f=f(u)
and the monograph
[
14
]
).

The first fundamental result has been proved for the solution of the torsion problem, i.e.
f\equiv 1
by Makar-Limanov
[
18
]
: uniqueness of critical point holds if
\Omega
is convex. The same is true for the first eigenfunction,
f(u)=\lambda u
, see
[
3
,
1
]
.

Under symmetry assumptions the well known results of Gidas, Ni and Nirenberg
[
10
]
gives the uniqueness of the critical point for general
f
.

Uniqueness of critical point can also be proved for solutions of the Schrödinger eigenvalue problem

-\Delta u+V(x)u=\lambda u,

with
V
convex,
\lambda>0
. It follows from the results in
[
16
,
5
]
, still in convex domains (their result holds also for more general equations).

If we consider the Poisson problem
-\Delta u=f(x,y)
, as a consequence of a result by Kennington in
[
15
]
one can prove uniqueness of the critical point if
\Omega
is convex and if
f
is
\beta
-concave, i.e.
f^{\beta}
is concave, for some
\beta>1
.

The last result we want to recall is due to Cabré and Chanillo
[
4
]
. They show that if the curvature of
\partial\Omega
is striclty positive and
u
is a semi-stable solution of the nonlinear problem
-\Delta u=f(u)
, then
u
has exactly one critical point (the result has been extended to the case of non negative curvature in
[
8
]
). Remember that
u
is said to be a
(semi-)stable
solution if the linearized operator at
u
is (nonnegative) positive definite, i.e. if for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
one has

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}-\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u)|\varphi|^{2}>(\geq)0,

or equivalently if the first eigenvalue of the linearized operator
-\Delta-f^{\prime}(u)
in
\Omega
is (nonnegative) positive.

All the results mentioned before hold in convex domain and it is known that, in general, we cannot expect uniqueness of the critical point in non convex domains, as showed by the case of the torsion problem in a dumbell domain (see for instance
[
22
]
). Then it is natural to ask whether it is possible to recover the uniqueness in (possibly) non convex domain, under suitable assumptions.

In the first part of this paper we examine the Poisson problem

(1.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=f(x,y)&\text{in }\Omega\\
u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
f:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a smooth, bounded (and simply connected) domain. We have the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume
f>0
in
\overline{\Omega}
and

(1.2)

\Delta(\log f)=0,\quad\text{in }\Omega,

and

(1.3)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\nu}+\mathfrak{K}f\geq 0,\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

where
\nu
is the outnormal unit vector to
\partial\Omega
and
\mathfrak{K}
is its curvature.
If
u
is the solution of problem
1.1
, then it has a unique nondegenerate critical point
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\Omega
.

Remark 1.2
.

(1)

Theorem
1.1
holds even if
\Omega
is not convex. For instance, if we consider
f(x,y):=e^{2x}
then equation
1.2
is trivially satisfied while equation
1.3
is satisfied if
\Omega
is such that

(1.4)

\nu_{x}\geq-\mathfrak{K},\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

where we write
\nu:=(\nu_{x},\nu_{y})
. Hence, we can find a non convex domain
\Omega
such that
1.4
is satisfied, see Figure
1
.

(2)

Let us point out that we cannot drop assumption
1.2
or
1.3
, otherwise we can lose the uniqueness of the critical point: see Remark
2.4
and Remark
2.5
for the details.

(3)

The preceding theorem can be seen as a generalization of Makar-Limanov’s result in
[
18
]
for the torsion problem. Indeed, for
f\equiv 1
and
\Omega
convex it is easy to see that assumptions
1.2
and
1.3
are trivially satisfied.

The idea of the proof is the following: thanks to the assumptions
1.2
and
1.3
we can construct an invertible conformal map that maps
\Omega
to a bounded and convex domain where we are able to estimate the number of critical points of the composition of our solution with the inverse of the conformal map.

In the second part of this paper, we want to investigate the nonlinear problem

(1.5)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=f(u)&\hbox{in }\Omega\\
u>0&\hbox{in }\Omega\\
u=0&\hbox{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\Omega
is still a smooth bounded and convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth nonlinearity.

We are interested in the number of critical points of solutions of the preceding problem when
\Omega
is close to be a convex domain.

We recall that if
\Omega
is convex, we know that a semi-stable solution
u
of
1.5
admits exactly one critical point which turns out to be nondegenerate, see
[
4
,
8
]
. Then we can ask what happens if we consider domains which are (possibly) non convex, but close to a convex one.

First of all let us recall that for a convex domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with no empty interior, we can find a Lipschitz function
\chi_{\Omega}:\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\to(0+\infty)
such that

\Omega=\Set{tP(\left(1+\chi_{\Omega}(P)\right)}{P\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1},\quad t\in[0,1)},

where up to a translation and a dilatation we assumed without loss of generality that
B_{1}(\mathbf{0})\subseteq\Omega
. Furthermore, if we assume
\Omega
to be of class
\mathcal{C}^{k}
then
\chi_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{C}^{k}(\mathbb{S}^{N-1})
.

Hence let us give the following definition of convergence of a family of smooth sets to a smooth and convex one.

Definition 1.1
.

Given a bounded and convex set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
of class
\mathcal{C}^{k}
and with no empty interior, we say that the family
(\Omega_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
of bounded sets of class
\mathcal{C}^{k}

converges to the convex set

\Omega
for
\varepsilon\to 0
- and we write
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\to\Omega
for
\varepsilon\to 0
- if there exists a family of functions
(\chi_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}})_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{k}(\mathbb{S}^{N-1})
such that

\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\Set{tP(\left(1+\chi_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(P)\right)}{P\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1},\quad t\in[0,1)},

and

\left\|\chi_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}-\chi_{\Omega}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}(\mathbb{S}^{N-1})}\to 0,\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

Here again we assumed, without loss of generality, that
B_{1}(\mathbf{0})\subseteq\Omega
.

Now, let us fix a smooth and convex domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with
B_{1}(\mathbf{0})\subseteq\Omega
and consider a family of domains
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
that are smooth and such that
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\to\Omega
for
\varepsilon\to 0
, say at least in
\mathcal{C}^{4}
sense, according to the preceding definition.

The following result holds true.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
u_{\varepsilon}
be a semi-stable solution the following problem

(1.6)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u_{\varepsilon}=f(u_{\varepsilon})&\text{ in }\ \Omega_{\varepsilon}\\
u_{\varepsilon}>0&\text{ in }\ \Omega_{\varepsilon}\\
u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{ on }\ \partial\Omega_{\varepsilon},\end{cases}

with
f(0)\geq 0
and assume that
\left\|u_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\leq C
for some
C>0
.
Then
u_{\varepsilon}
has a unique nondegenerate critical point
P_{\varepsilon}\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. Moreover
P_{\varepsilon}\to P_{c}
where
P_{c}\in\Omega
is the unique critical point of a semi-stable solution
u
of problem
1.5
in
\Omega
.

Remark 1.4
.

As will be explained in details in Remark
3.1
, let us point out that the hypothesis that
\left\|u_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}
is uniformly bounded is always satisfied if, for instance, we assume that the nonlinearity has the form
f(u)=\lambda g(u)
,
g
is smooth and satisfies

(1.7)

\displaystyle g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\,\hbox{ is increasing and convex},

(1.8)

\displaystyle g(0)>0,

and
\lambda\in(0,\lambda^{*}(\Omega))
, with
\lambda^{*}(\Omega)>0
. We recall that in this setting it is well known that there exists
\lambda^{*}(\Omega)>0
such that for all
\lambda\in(0,\lambda^{*}(\Omega))
the problem

(1.9)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\lambda g(u)&\hbox{in }\Omega\\
u>0&\hbox{in }\Omega\\
u=0&\hbox{on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

admits a stable solution, see for instance
[
2
,
6
,
19
]
and the references therein.

Remark 1.5
.

Observe that if the limit domain is convex, but unbounded, then the result of Theorem
1.3
does not hold. Indeed, it is possible to build a family of domains which converges to the strip
\mathcal{S}:=\mathbb{R}\times(-1,1)
and such that the corresponding solutions have an arbitrary large (finite) number of critical points. More precisely for any
\lambda\in(0,\lambda^{*}(-1,1))
and for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a family of smooth and bounded domains
\widetilde{\Omega}_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

(i)

\widetilde{\Omega}_{\varepsilon}
is star-shaped with respect to and interior point;

(ii)

\widetilde{\Omega}_{\varepsilon}
locally converges to the strip
\mathcal{S}
for
\varepsilon\to 0
, i.e. for all compact sets
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
it holds
\lvert K\cap(\mathcal{S}\Delta\widetilde{\Omega}_{\varepsilon})\rvert\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
;

(iii)

\lambda^{*}(\widetilde{\Omega}_{\varepsilon})\geq\lambda^{*}(-1,1)
for
\varepsilon
small enough;

(iv)

if
\widetilde{u}_{\varepsilon}
is the stable solution of problem
1.9
in
\widetilde{\Omega}_{\varepsilon}
then
\widetilde{u}_{\varepsilon}
has at least
k
maximum points.

This fact has been proved in
[
11
]
for the torsion problem
f\equiv 1
and generalized to any
f=\lambda g
, with
g
satisfying
1.7
and
1.8
in
[
7
]
.

To prove Theorem

we show that
u_{\varepsilon}
converges to

2.
The Poisson problem

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
.
Until the end of the section we identify
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\mathbb{C}
and we write
z:=x+iy
.

Assume
f:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
be positive and such that assumptions
1.2
and
1.3
are satisfied. Then the following proposition holds true.

Proposition 2.1
.

There exists a holomorphic function
T:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
such that

(i)

\lvert T^{\prime}\rvert^{2}=f
in
\Omega
,

(ii)

T(\Omega)
is bounded and convex,

(iii)

there exists a holomorphic function
\tau:T(\Omega)\to\mathbb{C}
such that
\tau=T^{-1}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume
\mathbf{0}\in\Omega
.
Since
\log f
is harmonic in
\Omega
by
1.2
and
\Omega
is simply connected, we can find a holomorphic function
w:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\Re(w)=\frac{1}{2}\log f.

Moreover, since
f>0
in
\overline{\Omega}
,
w
is holomorphic up to the boundary of
\Omega
.
Hence, also the function
\mathfrak{t}:=e^{w}
is holomorphic in
\overline{\Omega}
and if we decompose it by modulus and principal argument we have

\mathfrak{t}=\lvert\mathfrak{t}\rvert e^{i\Theta}.

Finally

\frac{1}{2}\log f=\Re(w)=\Re\left(\log\mathfrak{t}\right)=\Re\left(\log(\lvert\mathfrak{t}\rvert e^{i\Theta})\right)=\log\lvert\mathfrak{t}\rvert,

which yields to

(2.1)

\lvert\mathfrak{t}\rvert^{2}=f.

Then, since
\Omega
is simply connected and
\mathfrak{t}
is holomorphic, we can define
T
as the primitive of
\mathfrak{t}
such that
T(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}
. Clearly
T
is holomorphic and
2.1
implies
\lvert T^{\prime}\rvert^{2}=f
, proving
(i)
.

To prove
(ii)
note that since
T
is continuous up to the boundary of
\Omega
we have that
\lvert T\rvert
is bounded and then
T(\Omega)
is.
To show the second claim we recall that the curvature
\widetilde{\mathfrak{K}}
of the boundary of
T(\Omega)
, in
\zeta\in\partial T(\Omega)
is given by (see
[
20
, equation (23) pag. 234]
)

(2.2)

\widetilde{\mathfrak{K}}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{\lvert T^{\prime}(z)\rvert}\left(\Im\left(\frac{\textsl{t}(z)T^{\prime\prime}(z)}{T^{\prime}(z)}\right)+\mathfrak{K}(z)\right),

where
z\in\partial\Omega
satisfies
T(z)=\zeta
and
\textsl{t}(z)
is the unit tangent vector to
\partial\Omega
in
z
. Here, since
\Omega
is simply connected then its boundary
\partial\Omega
is a Jordan curve and we assume it is orientated is such a way that the winding number satisfies

W_{\partial\Omega}(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{d\mathbf{z}}{\mathbf{z}-z}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }z\in\Omega\\
0&\text{if }z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\Omega}.\end{cases}

Thanks to this convention the unit tangent vector
t
is uniquely determined by the orientation of
\partial\Omega
.
If we write
T:=h+ig
we have

T^{\prime}=h_{x}-ih_{y},\quad\text{and}\quad T^{\prime\prime}=h_{xx}-ih_{xy},

and then, writing
\textsl{t}=x_{\textsl{t}}+iy_{\textsl{t}}
, one has

\displaystyle\Im\left(\frac{\textsl{t}T^{\prime\prime}}{T^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Im\left(\frac{(x_{\textsl{t}}+iy_{\textsl{t}})(h_{xx}-ih_{xy})}{h_{x}-ih_{y}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Im\left(\frac{x_{\textsl{t}}h_{xx}+y_{\textsl{t}}h_{xy}+i(-x_{\textsl{t}}h_{xy}+y_{\textsl{t}}h_{xx})}{h_{x}-ih_{y}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{\textsl{t}}(h_{y}h_{xx}-h_{x}h_{xy})+y_{\textsl{t}}(h_{y}h_{xy}+h_{x}h_{xx})}{h_{x}^{2}+h_{y}^{2}}

(2.3)

\displaystyle=\frac{y_{\textsl{t}}(h_{x}h_{xx}+h_{y}h_{xy})-x_{\textsl{t}}(h_{x}h_{xy}+h_{y}h_{yy})}{h_{x}^{2}+h_{y}^{2}}.

Taking into account

\displaystyle\partial_{x}f

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}(h_{x}^{2}+h_{y}^{2})=2h_{x}h_{xx}+2h_{y}h_{xy},

\displaystyle\partial_{y}f

\displaystyle=\partial_{y}(h_{x}^{2}+h_{y}^{2})=2h_{x}h_{xy}+2h_{y}h_{yy},

equation
2.3
becomes

\Im\left(\frac{\textsl{t}T^{\prime\prime}}{T^{\prime}}\right)=\frac{y_{\textsl{t}}f_{x}-x_{\textsl{t}}f_{y}}{2f}=\frac{\nu\cdot\nabla f}{2f},

where
\nu=(y_{\textsl{t}},-x_{\textsl{t}})
. Finally, the previous equation and
2.2
imply

\lvert T^{\prime}\rvert\widetilde{\mathfrak{K}}=\Im\left(\frac{\textsl{t}T^{\prime\prime}}{T^{\prime}}\right)+\mathfrak{K}=\frac{f_{\nu}+2\mathfrak{K}f}{2f}\geq 0,

where the last inequality holds true by
1.3
. Then
\widetilde{\mathfrak{K}}\geq 0
and
T(\Omega)
is convex.

Since
T
is proper,
T^{\prime}\not=0
and
T(\Omega)
is simply connected,
[
12
, Theorem B]
tells us that
T
is invertible. Finally, the inverse is holomorphic by the Open Mapping Theorem and
(iii)
follows.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

In particular
T
is a conformal map, indeed
\lvert T^{\prime}\rvert^{2}=f>0
in
\overline{\Omega}
and then
\Omega
and
T(\Omega)
are conformally equivalent.

.

The function
T
can be written in a more explicit way by setting

T^{\prime}(z)=\mathfrak{t}(x+iy):=\frac{1}{\overline{\mathfrak{t}(\mathbf{0})}}f\left(\frac{x+iy}{2},\frac{y-ix}{2}\right).

See

.

We can now prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let us denote
\Lambda:=T(\Omega)
with coordinates
\zeta:=\xi+i\eta
and set

v(\xi,\eta):=u(\tau(\xi,\eta)),

where we recall that
\tau=T^{-1}
. If we write
\tau:=\varphi+i\psi
we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\xi}v

\displaystyle=u_{x}\varphi_{\xi}+u_{y}\psi_{\xi},

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta}v

\displaystyle=u_{x}\varphi_{\eta}+u_{y}\psi_{\eta},

and

\displaystyle\partial_{\xi\xi}v

\displaystyle=u_{xx}\varphi_{\xi}^{2}+2u_{xy}\varphi_{\xi}\psi_{\xi}+u_{yy}\psi_{\xi}^{2}+u_{x}\varphi_{\xi\xi}+u_{y}\psi_{\xi\xi},

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta\eta}v

\displaystyle=u_{xx}\varphi_{\eta}^{2}+2u_{xy}\varphi_{\eta}\psi_{\eta}+u_{yy}\psi_{\eta}^{2}+u_{x}\varphi_{\eta\eta}+u_{y}\psi_{\eta\eta}.

Hence by the Cauchy-Riemann equations one has

\displaystyle\Delta v

\displaystyle=u_{xx}\lvert\nabla\varphi\rvert^{2}+2u_{xy}\nabla\varphi\nabla\psi+u_{yy}\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^{2}+u_{x}\Delta\varphi+u_{y}\Delta\psi=\Delta u\lvert\tau^{\prime}\rvert^{2}=-f\lvert\tau^{\prime}\rvert^{2},

and then by
(i)
of Proposition
2.1
we get

-\Delta v=f\lvert T^{\prime}\rvert^{-2}=1,

that is
v
is the solution of the torsion problem in
\Lambda
, i.e.

\begin{cases}-\Delta v=1&\text{in }\Lambda\\
v=0&\text{on }\partial\Lambda.\end{cases}

By
[
18
, Theorem 1]
,
v
has a unique nondegenerate critical point
(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\in\Lambda
and then
(x_{0},y_{0}):=\tau(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\in\Omega
is the unique critical point of
u
.
To show the nondegeneracy of
(x_{0},y_{0})
, since
u_{x}(x_{0},y_{0})=u_{y}(x_{0},y_{0})=0
one has

\displaystyle\partial_{\xi\xi}v(\tau(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}))

\displaystyle=u_{xx}\varphi_{\xi}^{2}+2u_{xy}\varphi_{\xi}\psi_{\xi}+u_{yy}\psi_{\xi}^{2},

\displaystyle\partial_{\xi\eta}v(\tau(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}))

\displaystyle=u_{xx}\varphi_{\xi}\varphi_{\eta}+u_{xy}(\varphi_{\xi}\psi_{\eta}+\varphi_{\eta}\psi_{\xi})+u_{yy}\psi_{\xi}\psi_{\eta},

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta\eta}v(\tau(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}))

\displaystyle=u_{xx}\varphi_{\eta}^{2}+2u_{xy}\varphi_{\eta}\psi_{\eta}+u_{yy}\psi_{\eta}^{2}.

Then

\displaystyle\partial_{\xi\xi}v\partial_{\eta\eta}v

\displaystyle=u_{xx}^{2}\varphi_{\xi}^{2}\varphi_{\eta}^{2}+2u_{xx}u_{xy}\varphi_{\xi}^{2}\varphi_{\eta}\psi_{\eta}+u_{xx}u_{yy}\varphi_{\xi}^{2}\psi_{\eta}^{2}+

\displaystyle+2u_{xx}u_{xy}\varphi_{\xi}\varphi_{\eta}^{2}\psi_{\xi}+4u_{xy}^{2}\varphi_{\xi}\varphi_{\eta}\psi_{\xi}\psi_{\eta}+2u_{xy}u_{yy}\varphi_{\xi}\psi_{\xi}\psi_{\eta}^{2}+

\displaystyle+u_{xx}u_{yy}\varphi_{\eta}^{2}\psi_{\xi}^{2}+2u_{xy}u_{yy}\varphi_{\eta}\psi_{\xi}^{2}\psi_{\eta}+u_{yy}^{2}\psi_{\xi}^{2}\psi_{\eta}^{2},

and

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\xi\eta}v\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=u_{xx}^{2}\varphi_{\xi}^{2}\varphi_{\eta}^{2}+u_{xy}^{2}(\varphi_{\xi}\psi_{\eta}+\varphi_{\eta}\psi_{\xi})^{2}+u_{yy}^{2}\psi_{\xi}^{2}\psi_{\eta}^{2}+2u_{xx}u_{yy}\varphi_{\xi}\varphi_{\eta}\psi_{\xi}\psi_{\eta}+

\displaystyle+2u_{xx}u_{xy}\varphi_{\xi}^{2}\varphi_{\eta}\psi_{\eta}+2u_{xx}u_{xy}\varphi_{\xi}\varphi_{\eta}^{2}\psi_{\xi}+2u_{xy}u_{yy}\varphi_{\xi}\psi_{\xi}\psi_{\eta}^{2}+2u_{xy}u_{yy}\varphi_{\eta}\psi_{\xi}^{2}\psi_{\eta}.

Finally we have that

v_{\xi\xi}v_{\eta\eta}-v_{\xi\eta}^{2}=(u_{xx}u_{yy}-u_{xy}^{2})(\varphi_{\xi}\psi_{\eta}-\varphi_{\eta}\psi_{\xi})^{2},

and since
(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})
is nondegenerate, the same holds true for
(x_{0},y_{0})
.
∎

2.1.
Final remarks

We conclude this section by showing that if at least one between
1.2
and
1.3
does not hold, then Theorem
1.1
may fail.

Remark 2.4
.

If
f>0
in
\overline{\Omega}
, satisfies
1.2
, but does not satisfy
1.3
then the solution of the Poisson problem
1.1
can have more than one critical point.

Indeed in
[
11
]
it is shown that for any
\delta>0
, there exists a star-shaped domain
\Omega:=\Omega(\delta)
such that the solution of the torsion problem, i.e.
f\equiv 1
, admits at least two critical points. Moreover one has
\mathfrak{K}_{|\partial\Omega}\geq-\delta
and it is negative somewhere. Then
f>0
in
\overline{\Omega}
,
1.2
is satisfied but
1.3
is not.

Remark 2.5
.

If
f>0
in
\overline{\Omega}
, satisfies
1.3
, but does not satisfy
1.2
then the solution of the Poisson problem
1.1
can have more than one critical point.

Indeed, as a consequence of
[
9
, Theorem 1.1]
, one has that if
p>1
is large enough there exists a solution
u
of the following Hénon problem

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=(x^{2}+y^{2})^{\alpha}\lvert u\rvert^{p}&\text{in }B\\
u>0&\text{in }B\\
u=0&\text{on }\partial B,\end{cases}

with
\alpha>0
,
B:=B_{1}(\mathbf{0})
, and there exist
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in B
such that

\max_{B\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{2}B_{2\delta}(Q_{i})}u\leq\frac{\sqrt{e}}{4},\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{B_{\delta}(Q_{i})}u\geq\frac{\sqrt{e}}{2},\quad i=1,2,

for some
0<\delta<\frac{\dist(Q_{1},Q_{2})}{4}
. Then let
v
be the solution of the torsion problem in
B
with Dirichlet boundary conditions and set

u_{\varepsilon}:=u+\varepsilon v,\quad 0<\varepsilon<\frac{\sqrt{e}}{4\left\|v\right\|_{\infty}},

which solves

\begin{cases}-\Delta u_{\varepsilon}=f_{\varepsilon}(x,y)&\text{in }B\\
u_{\varepsilon}>0&\text{in }B\\
u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\partial B,\end{cases}

with
f_{\varepsilon}(x,y):=(x^{2}+y^{2})^{\alpha}\lvert u\rvert^{p}+\varepsilon>0
in
\overline{B}
. Then
u=0
on
\partial B
implies

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial f_{\varepsilon}}{\partial\nu}+\mathfrak{K}f_{\varepsilon}=0+\varepsilon>0,\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

that is
1.3
is satisfied. Moreover, it is easy to see that
u_{\varepsilon}
admits at least one critical point in each
B_{\delta}(Q_{i})
for
i=1,2
.

Now let
Q\in B_{\delta}(Q_{1})\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
be a critical point for
u
such that
u(Q)\geq\frac{\sqrt{e}}{2}
. Then one has

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}(Q

\displaystyle={f_{\varepsilon}(Q)\Delta f_{\varepsilon}(Q)-\lvert\nabla f_{\varepsilon}(Q)\rvert^{2}}

\displaystyle=-p\lvert Q\rvert^{6\alpha}\lvert u(Q)\rvert^{3p-1}+4\varepsilon\alpha^{2}\lvert Q\rvert^{2(\alpha-1)}\lvert u(Q)\rvert^{p}-\varepsilon p\lvert Q\rvert^{4\alpha}\lvert u(Q)\rvert^{2p-1}<0,

for

3.
The nonlinear problem

In this section we prove Theorem
1.3
. Hence, let
\Omega
be a fixed bounded and convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and assume
B_{1}(\mathbf{0})\subseteq\Omega
. Let
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
be the family of smooth domains converging to
\Omega
as
\varepsilon\to 0
and
u_{\varepsilon}
be the solution of problem
1.6
, as in Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Since
\Omega
is convex we know that if
u
is a semi-stable solution of problem
1.5
in
\Omega
, then it admits a unique nondegenerate critical point, which we denote by
P_{c}
.
Hence, it is enough to show that for all multi-indices
\alpha
with
\lvert\alpha\rvert\leq 2
it holds

(3.1)

\sup_{\overline{\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap\Omega}}\left|D^{\alpha}\big(u_{\varepsilon}-u\big)\right|\to 0,\quad\text{for }\varepsilon\to 0.

Indeed, if
P_{\varepsilon}\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is a critical point for
u_{\varepsilon}
, then
P_{\varepsilon}\to P_{c}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and it is a nondenerate maximum thanks to
3.1
. Then uniqueness follows from the convergence to
P_{c}
and the nondegeneracy.

We prove
3.1
through several steps.

Step 1: there exists
C>0
such that
\left\|u_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{H^{2}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\leq C
.

From classical regularity theory one has

\left\|u_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{H^{m+2}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\leq C(\Omega_{\varepsilon})\left(\left\|f(u_{\varepsilon})\right\|_{H^{m}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}+\left\|u_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\right),

but from the convergence of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
to
\Omega
one can see that
C(\Omega_{\varepsilon})
does not really depend on
\varepsilon
. Then for
m=0
using the assumption
\left\|u_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\leq C
we get the desired claim.

Step 2:
u_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup u
in
H^{1}(\Omega^{\rho})
, where
u
is a semi-stable solution of problem
1.5
in
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\rho}:=\set{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}{(\frac{x}{\rho},\frac{y}{\rho})\in\Omega}
, for some
\rho>1
.

First of all from the convergence of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
to
\Omega
we can find
\rho>1
such that
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\Omega^{\rho}
.
Since
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
are smooth we can consider
u_{\varepsilon}
defined in
\Omega^{\rho}
by means of zero extension outside
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
and with a little abuse of notation we still denote such an extension by
u_{\varepsilon}
.
Then from the previous step we have
u_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup u
in
H^{1}(\Omega^{\rho})
. Then it is easy to see that by means of the dominated convergence theorem it holds

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla\varphi=\int_{\Omega}f(u)\varphi,\quad\text{for all }\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega),

and
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
in trace sense. Finally for any
\xi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
again the dominated convergence theorem gives

\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla\xi\rvert^{2}-\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u)\xi^{2}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla\xi\rvert^{2}-\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u_{\varepsilon})\xi^{2}\right)\geq 0.

Hence we proved that
u
is a semi-stable solution of problem
1.5
in
\Omega
.

Step 3: end of the proof

From the convergence of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
to
\Omega
we can find
r>0
and
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{k}\in\partial\Omega
such that

\displaystyle\partial\Omega\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}B_{r}(Q_{i}),\quad\text{and}\quad\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}B_{r}(Q_{i}),

\displaystyle\Omega\cap B_{2r}(Q_{i})=\set{(x,y)\in B_{2r}(Q_{i})}{y>\Gamma^{i}(x)},

\displaystyle\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap B_{2r}(Q_{i})=\set{(x,y)\in B_{2r}(Q_{i})}{y>\Gamma^{i}(x)+\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{i}(x)},

where the last two relations hold up to a rotation and where
\Gamma^{1},\dots,\Gamma^{k},\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{1},\dots,\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{k}
are smooth functions such that

\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{i}\to 0\quad\text{in }\mathcal{C}^{4},\quad\text{for all }i=1,\dots,k.

Now, let us fix
i=1
: up to a translation we can assume
Q_{1}=\mathbf{0}
and consider

\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,y):=u_{\varepsilon}(x,y+\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x)),\quad\text{for all }(x,y)\in\Omega\cap B_{2r},

where we omitted the index
i
and
B_{2r}:=B_{2r}(\mathbf{0})
.
Then one has
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}=0
on
\partial\Omega\cap B_{2r}
and since
\gamma_{\varepsilon}\to 0
in
\mathcal{C}^{2}
it holds

\sup_{\overline{\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap\Omega\cap B_{2r}}}\left|D^{\alpha}\big(u_{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}\big)\right|\to 0,\quad\text{for }\varepsilon\to 0,

for all multi-indices
\alpha
, with
\lvert\alpha\rvert\leq 2
. Moreover, we have

\begin{cases}-\Delta(u-\bar{u}_{\varepsilon})=h_{\varepsilon}&\text{in }\Omega\cap B_{2r}\\
u-\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega\cap B_{2r},\end{cases}

where

h_{\varepsilon}:=f(u)-f(\bar{u}_{\varepsilon})-2\partial_{xy}u_{\varepsilon|(x,y+\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x))}\dot{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}-\partial_{yy}u_{\varepsilon|(x,y+\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x))}\dot{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}^{2}-\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon|(x,y+\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x))}\ddot{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}.

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, by means of the mean value theorem and taking into account that
u
,
u_{\varepsilon}
and in turn
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}
are uniformly bounded, we have
\lvert f(u)-f(\bar{u}_{\varepsilon})\rvert\leq C\lvert u-\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}\rvert
, then

\left\|h_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{H^{m}(\Omega\cap B_{2r})}\leq C\left(\left\|u-\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{H^{m}(\Omega\cap B_{2r})}+\left\|u_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{H^{m+2}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\left\|\gamma_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{m+2}}\right).

Iterating the argument in
Step 1
, we can find
C>0
such that
\left\|u_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{H^{m+2}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\leq C
and

\left\|u-\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\cap B_{2r})}\leq\left\|u-u_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\cap B_{2r})}+C\left\|\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}-u_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\cap B_{2r})}\to 0,\quad\text{for }\varepsilon\to 0,

thanks to the compact embedding of
H^{1}(\Omega^{\rho})
in
L^{2}(\Omega^{\rho})
. Then classical boundary regularity theory gives
\left\|u-\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2}(\Omega\cap B_{r})}\to 0
for
\varepsilon\to 0
and in turn

\sup_{\overline{\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap\Omega\cap B_{r}}}\left|D^{\alpha}\big(u_{\varepsilon}-u\big)\right|\to 0,\quad\text{for }\varepsilon\to 0,

for all multi-indices
\alpha
, with
\lvert\alpha\rvert\leq 2
. To complete the proof of
3.1
it is enough to repeat the argument for all
i=1,\dots,k
and use interior regularity estimates taking into account that
\lvert-\Delta(u-u_{\varepsilon})\rvert\leq C\lvert u-u_{\varepsilon}\rvert
, for some
C>0
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

Here we show that if we assume that the nonlinearity has the form
f(u)=\lambda g(u)
,
g
is smooth and satisfies
1.7
and
1.8
, that are

\displaystyle g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\,\hbox{ is increasing and convex},

\displaystyle g(0)>0,

and
\lambda\in(0,\lambda^{*}(\Omega))
, then there exists
C>0
such that
\left\|u_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\leq C
.

First of all note that, since
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\to\Omega
then
\lambda^{*}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})\to\lambda^{*}(\Omega)
and then
\lambda<\lambda^{*}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})
for
\varepsilon
small enough.

Remember that from the convergence of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
to
\Omega
we can find
\rho>1
such that
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\Omega^{\rho}=\set{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}{(x/\rho,y/\rho)\in\Omega}
. Moreover under this set of assumptions one has
\lambda^{*}(\Omega^{\rho})>\lambda
for
\varepsilon
small enough. Hence if we consider the stable solution
u^{\rho}
of
1.5
in
\Omega^{\rho}
- using the convexity of
g
- we have

\begin{cases}-\Delta(u_{\varepsilon}-u^{\rho})=\lambda\left(g(u_{\varepsilon})-g(u^{\rho})\right)\leq\lambda g^{\prime}(u_{\varepsilon})(u_{\varepsilon}-u^{\rho})&\hbox{in}\ \Omega_{\varepsilon}\\
u_{\varepsilon}-u^{\rho}\leq 0&\hbox{on}\ \partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}\end{cases}

and then from the stability of
u_{\varepsilon}
we can apply the maximum principle to deduce
u_{\varepsilon}\leq u^{\rho}\leq\left\|u^{\rho}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\rho})}
in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.
