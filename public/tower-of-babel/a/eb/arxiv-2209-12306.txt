Abstract
. Frieze groups are discrete subgroups of the full group of isometries of a flat strip. We investigate here the dynamics of specific architected materials generated by acting with a frieze group on a collection of self-coupling seed resonators. We demonstrate that, under unrestricted reconfigurations of the internal structures of the seed resonators, the dynamical matrices of the materials generate the full self-adjoint sector of the stabilized group C ∗ C^{\ast} -algebra of the frieze group. As a consequence, in applications where the positions, orientations and internal structures of the seed resonators are adiabatically modified, the spectral bands of the dynamical matrices carry a complete set of topological invariants that are fully accounted by the K-theory of the mentioned algebra. By resolving the generators of the K-theory, we produce the model dynamical matrices that carry the elementary topological charges, which we implement with systems of plate resonators to showcase several applications in spectral engineering. The paper is written in an expository style.

However, if the topological charges are found to be different, then the topology of the spectrum must change during the deformations and, as such, some of the spectral gaps must close, regardless how hard we try to avoid this phenomenon. This supplies a very simple, robust and practical principle for resonant spectrum engineering of metamaterials, because it supplies the means to close and open spectral gaps on demand, without fine-tuning. Furthermore, it gives us insight into the dynamical features that can be achieved with a material from a given a class and in a given set of experimental conditions, in the sense that all such materials deliver wave-channels that carry a finite number of topological charges that are conserved when bands collide or split as a result of continuous deformations of the material. In fact, if one is interested only in dynamical effects that are robust against continuous deformations of the materials, referred to here as topological dynamics, then all such dynamical effects can be reproduced by stacking elementary models that carry the fundamental topological charges.

2.
A Class of Architected Materials

A strip is a quasi one-dimensional slice of the Euclidean plane, such as
{\mathcal{S}}:={\mathbb{R}}\times[-1,1]
, equipped with the inherited Euclidean metric.
Its complete group of isometries is generated by continuous translations along the long axis and reflections relative to the horizontal and vertical axes. This continuous group of isometries accepts seven distinct discrete subgroups, the frieze groups
[
18
]
. This section supplies a brisk review of the frieze groups and introduces our specific algorithm that creates architected materials using actions of the frieze groups. The section also analyzes the dynamical matrices of these materials.

2.2.
The class of architectures and the assumed experimental conditions

The protocol that defines the architected materials we want to investigate consists of the following steps:

P1.

Start with a finite number
N_{s}
of seeding discrete resonators, placed in a strip at desired non-overlapping locations and with desired orientations;

P2.

Apply a plane transformation from a chosen frieze group to each of the seed resonators.

P3.

Replenish the seed resonators and repeat step two for all the plane transformations contained in the frieze group.

P4.

Adjust the seeds if steps 1-3 result in overlapings and repeat the process.

Remark 2.1
.

There are several observations to be made about
our
class of materials. Firstly, the resulting architectures are symmetric w.r.t. the frieze group engaged by the protocol. However, as stated in our last rule, we will be avoiding those particular seed configurations that lead to overlappings and, as such, the seeds are always placed at general Wyckoff positions (i.e. those points of the strip that are fixed only by the action of the identity operation of the frieze group). Of course, since the number of seeds can be arbitrarily large, a finite number of them can assemble into a super-seed with center at a special Wyckoff position. Still, this is a special situation because the site group of that Wyckoff position acts freely on the local modes. The bottom line is that, by choice, there are symmetric architectures that are not included in
our
class of materials. If we insist in including those, then the algebra of dynamical matrices, for those particular cases, drops from a group to a groupoid
C^{\ast}
-algebra
[
42
]
.

\Diamond

We also need to specify the experimental conditions. For this, note that any of the proposed architected materials is completely determined by the shape, position, orientation and internal structures of the seeding resonators. We will assume that the configuration space defined by the factors we just listed is fully and unrestrictidely explored during the experimental applications, within the bounds imposed by the protocol P1-P4.

2.3.
Dynamical matrices

The resonators can be steel plates embedded in an elastic medium, acoustic cavities, or magnetized solid bodies (see Example 2.6 in
[
42
]
,
[
44
]
and section

, where

corresponds to the seed resonator on which the transformation

was applied. We emphasize that this is a consequence of our decision to exclude any overlap of the resonators. Each resonator carries a finite number

As a result, these observations and measurements are insensitive to the Euclidean transformations applied on the resonators and this enables us to choose the bases for the internal spaces of resonators in a coherent fashion. Specifically, for the seed resonator
i
, we choose once and for all a basis for its space of internal resonant modes, which we denote by
|\alpha,i,e\rangle
, with
\alpha=\overline{1,K}
and
e
the neutral element of the frieze group
F
. When the seed resonator is acted with a plane transformation
f
from the frieze group and placed to its rightful configuration in the architectures, the internal space can be rendered in the basis
|\alpha,i,f\rangle
,
\alpha=\overline{1,K}
, which coincides with the modes
|\alpha,i,e\rangle
when observed from the intrinsic frame.

Remark 2.2
.

This coherent labeling of the modes always exist for our special class of materials. For atomic systems, however, the orbitals are always observed and quantified in the laboratory frame, hence quite differently from what we are proposing here for classical resonators.

\Diamond

The collective dynamics of a pattern of resonators takes place inside the Hilbert space
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\otimes\ell^{2}(F)
,
N=N_{s}\,K
, spanned by the vectors
\xi\otimes|f\rangle
with
\xi\in{\rm Spann}\{|\alpha,i\rangle,\ \alpha=\overline{1,K},\ i=\overline{1,N_{s}}\}\simeq{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
and
f\in F
. Given our convention, a plane transformation from the frieze group acts as

T_{f^{\prime}}(\xi\otimes|f\rangle)=\xi\otimes|f^{\prime}f\rangle.

(2.3)

It is important to note that the vector
\xi
is not acted on by the transformation. Now, like any bounded operator over
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\otimes\ell^{2}(F)
, a dynamical matrix assumes the generic form

H=\sum_{f,f^{\prime}\in F}w_{f,f^{\prime}}\otimes|f\rangle\langle f^{\prime}|,\quad w_{f,f^{\prime}}\in M_{N}({\mathbb{C}}),

(2.4)

where
M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
denotes the algebra of
N\times N
matrices with complex entries. As an experimental fact, the coupling matrix
w_{f,f^{\prime}}
becomes weaker as the distance between a pair
(f,f^{\prime})
of resonators is increased and in fact the coupling matrix cannot be experimentally resolved beyond a certain separation distance. Thus, for typical experiments, it is justified to assume that there is a finite number of terms in Eq.
2.4
involving one
f\in F
.

If the physical processes involved in the coupling of resonators are Galilean invariant, then the coupling matrices must satisfy the following symmetry constraints

w_{g\cdot f,g\cdot f^{\prime}}=w_{f,f^{\prime}},\quad\forall\ f,f^{\prime},g\in F.

(2.5)

It is quite important to understand that this is not an assumption but rather a physical reality. Indeed, let there be some underlying Galilean invariant physical laws
{\mathcal{L}}\mapsto\{w_{f,f^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}})\}
that associate to each admissible architecture
{\mathcal{L}}
a family of coupling matrices. Then, if
g
is any Euclidean transformation, we must have

w_{g\cdot f,g\cdot f^{\prime}}(g\cdot{\mathcal{L}})=w_{f,f^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}}),

(2.6)

because any instrumentation rigidly attached to the frame of the resonators will not be able to sense the Euclidean drift
g
of the entire platform. Now, if
g
belongs to the frieze group that produced the pattern in the first place, then
g\cdot{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{L}}
and the Eq. (
2.5
) follows.

Remark 2.3
.

As opposed to atomic systems, no additional conjugation with a representation of
g
on the space
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
is needed in Eq. (
2.5
), because the pair of resonators and their resonant modes are rigidly rotated by
g
and the modes are observed and quantified from frames rigidly attached to the resonators. Another way to justify the absence of such conjugation is via the specific action (
2.3
) of the plane transformations on our basis.

\Diamond

As a direct consequence of Eq. (
2.5
), any dynamical matrix over one of our symmetric patterns can be always reduced to the following particular form

H=\sum_{f,f^{\prime}\in F}w_{1,f^{-1}\cdot f^{\prime}}\otimes|f\rangle\langle f^{\prime}|.

(2.7)

This is already a strong indication that the dynamical matrices of our patterns of resonators form a sub-algebra of the bounded operators over
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\otimes\ell^{2}(F)
. Computing this sub-algebra is the subject of the next section.

Remark 2.4
.

The algebra
{\mathbb{B}}\big(\ell^{2}(F)\big)
of bounded operators over
\ell^{2}(F)
is very large and it has a trivial K-theory. Thus, in order to predict topological phenomena in the dynamics of the resonators, it is paramount to demonstrate that the algebra of dynamical matrices is actually a separable

-subalgebra of

g\in G
, which form an orthonormal basis

\langle g,g^{\prime}\rangle=\delta_{g,g^{\prime}},\quad g,g^{\prime}\in G.

(3.5)

The left action of
{\mathbb{C}}G
on itself can be extended to the action of a bounded operator on
\ell^{2}(G)
, and this supplies the left regular representation
\pi_{L}
of
{\mathbb{C}}G
inside the algebra
{\mathbb{B}}\big(\ell^{2}(G)\big)
of bounded operators. Specifically,

\pi_{L}(q)|g\rangle=\sum_{g^{\prime}\in G}\alpha_{g^{\prime}}|g^{\prime}g\rangle=\sum_{g^{\prime}\in G}\alpha_{g^{\prime}g^{-1}}|g^{\prime}\rangle,\quad q\in{\mathbb{C}}G,\quad g\in G,

(3.6)

where
q=\sum_{g^{\prime}\in G}\alpha_{g^{\prime}}g^{\prime}
with
\alpha_{g^{\prime}}\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
The completion of
{\mathbb{C}}G
with respect to the norm

\|q\|:=\|\pi_{L}(q)\|_{{\mathbb{B}}(\ell^{2}(G))}

(3.7)

supplies the reduced group
C^{\ast}
-algebra
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
of
G
.

Remark 3.1
.

As we shall see, the stabilization of this group algebra represents the space where the dynamical matrices live and are deformed. The norm (
3.7
) puts a topology on this space, which gives a rigorous meaning to the wording “continuous deformations” used up to this point in an intuitive but very imprecise mode.

\Diamond

Remark 3.2
.

For any state on a
\ast
-algebra, there are left and right GNS representations. The right GNS representation
\pi_{R}
corresponding to
{\mathcal{T}}
has the same Hilbert representation space, but the elements act from the right (see Eq. (
3.11
)).

\Diamond

Remark 3.3
.

The following detail is worth mentioning. If the group
G
is finite, then

{\mathcal{T}}(q)=\frac{1}{|G|}{\rm Tr}\big(\pi_{L}(q)\big)=\frac{1}{|G|}{\rm Tr}\big(\pi_{R}(q)\big),

(3.8)

which can be seen straight from (
3.6
).

\Diamond

Since we only deal here with discrete amenable groups, the reduced and full group
C^{\ast}
-algebras coincide. This is important because the relation between the group and its
C^{\ast}
-algebra is functorial only for the latter. So, in the present context, we have the following simple but essential statement:

Proposition 3.4

(
[
45
]
)
.

Let
G
and
H
be discrete amenable groups and
\rho:G\to H
be a group morphism. Then
\rho
lifts to a
C^{\ast}
-algebra morphism
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)\to C^{\ast}_{r}(H)
, extending the map between group algebras

{\mathbb{C}}G\ni\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}\,g\mapsto\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}\,\rho(g)\in{\mathbb{C}}H.

(3.9)

3.2.
Symmetry and structure of dynamical matrices

As we have seen, the group
G
acts on itself via an action either from the left or from the right. The symmetry operations associated to the group elements are implemented via the left-regular representation:

U:G\to{\mathbb{B}}\big(\ell^{2}(G)\big),\quad U_{g}|g^{\prime}\rangle:=\pi_{L}(g)|g^{\prime}\rangle=|gg^{\prime}\rangle.

(3.10)

The right regular representation of the group algebra acts as

\pi_{R}(q)|g^{\prime}\rangle:=\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}|g^{\prime}g^{-1}\rangle=\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g^{-1}g^{\prime}}|g\rangle,\quad q\in{\mathbb{C}}G,\quad g^{\prime}\in G.

(3.11)

Since the left and right group actions commute, we automatically have that

U_{g}^{\ast}\,\pi_{R}(q)\,U_{g}=\pi_{R}(q),\quad q\in C^{\ast}_{r}(G),\quad g\in G.

(3.12)

Hence, the group
C^{\ast}
-algebra supplies models that are naturally symmetric w.r.t. the group
G
, via a mechanism that rests entirely on the associative property of the group multiplication.

Remark 3.5
.

The following simple observation establishes a useful link between projections and representations. Suppose
p
is a projection from
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
. Then
\pi_{R}(p)\ell^{2}(G)
is an invariant subspace for the group symmetries

U_{g}\big[\pi_{R}(p)\ell^{2}(G)\big]=\pi_{R}(p)\ell^{2}(G),\quad\forall\ g\in G.

(3.13)

As such,
g\mapsto\pi_{L}(g)\pi_{R}(p)
supplies a representation of
G
on the subspace
\pi_{R}(p)\ell^{2}(G)
. This simple observation will be exploited here quite often.

\Diamond

We now specialize the discussion to the case when
G
is the frieze group generating a class of architected materials. The right regular representations of
C^{\ast}_{r}(F)
,

\pi_{R}\Big(\sum_{f}\alpha_{f}\,f\Big)=\sum_{f,f^{\prime}\in F}\alpha_{f^{-1}f^{\prime}}|f\rangle\langle f^{\prime}|,

(3.14)

can be naturally extended to a representation of the algebra
M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})\otimes C^{\ast}_{r}(F)
over the Hilbert space
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\otimes\ell^{2}(F)
. Specifically, an element from
M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})\otimes C^{\ast}_{r}(F)
takes the same form
\sum_{f}\alpha_{f}\,f
, but with coefficients
\alpha_{f}
from
M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
, and the extension of the right regular representation is

\pi_{R}\Big(\sum_{f}\alpha_{f}\,f\Big)=\sum_{f,f^{\prime}\in F}\alpha_{f^{-1}f^{\prime}}\otimes|f\rangle\langle f^{\prime}|.

(3.15)

Similarly, the group symmetries
U_{f}
can be lifted as
I\otimes U_{f}
on the Hilbert space
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\otimes\ell^{2}(F)
by letting
F
act trivially on
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
.

Now, comparing the generic form Eq. (
2.7
) of the dynamical matrices for Galilean invariant theories with the right regular representation (
3.15
), we see that the two coincide if we take
w_{f,f^{\prime}}=\alpha_{f^{-1}f^{\prime}}
. Furthermore, when this correspondence is applied on elements from
{\mathbb{C}}F
, it supplies dynamical matrices with finite coupling ranges. The important conclusion is that
all
dynamical matrices of the patterns of resonators proposed by us can be generated from the right regular representation of
M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})\otimes C^{\ast}_{r}(F)
, for some finite but otherwise arbitrary
N
, and, reciprocally, the dynamical matrices that can be engineered in a laboratory densely sample the self-adjoint sector of
{\mathbb{K}}\otimes C^{\ast}_{r}(F)
. Furthermore, we can be sure that our allowed deformations of the physical systems, which involve changing the shapes, positions and orientations, as well as the internal structures of the seeding resonators, take place inside the stabilization of the
C^{\ast}
-algebra
C^{\ast}_{r}(F)
.

Remark 3.6
.

Throughout, we will reserve the symbols
\pi_{R}
,
{\mathcal{T}}
and
U
for the objects introduced in this section, making sure that the group
G
can be clearly read off from the context. In fact, since the left regular representation is only used in tandem with
U
, we simplify the notation for the right regular representation to
\pi
.

\Diamond

For the practical purposes of this paper, it is useful to merge the general discussion and the specifics of the frieze groups presented in subsection
2.1
. Using the uniform notation introduced there, the elements of
M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})\otimes C^{\ast}_{r}(F)
can be presented as norm-convergent infinite series

\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\sum_{\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{2}}w(n,\alpha)u^{n}v^{\alpha},

(3.16)

with coefficients

from
M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
. Given the rule (
3.11
), the right regular representation of an element

as in Eq. (

) has matrix elements

\langle i,\big(u^{m}v^{\beta}\big)\cdot\big(u^{n}v^{\alpha}\big)^{-1}|\pi(h)|j,u^{m}v^{\beta}\rangle=w_{ij}(n,\alpha).

(3.17)

By sampling all allowed values of the labels

,

,
(n,\alpha)
and

, Eq. (

) returns all the matrix elements of the element

.

4.
Topological dynamics by
K
-theory

In
K_{0}
-theory, two projections from
{\mathbb{K}}\otimes C^{\ast}_{r}(F)
are declared equivalent if one can find a continuous family of projections inside the same algebra, interpolating between the two projections. Note that, during this interpolation, the number of engaged local degrees of freedom can change and, as such, the mentioned interpolations are rightfully called stable homotopies. Almost tautologically, the equivalence class
[P]_{0}
of a projection
P\in{\mathbb{K}}\otimes C^{\ast}_{r}(F)
defines the complete topological invariant associated to the projection relative to stable homotopies. Any projection from
{\mathbb{K}}\otimes C^{\ast}_{r}(F)
derives from the spectral projection of a dynamical matrix for a material from the class introduced in section
2
and, reciprocally, any projection from
{\mathbb{K}}\otimes C^{\ast}_{r}(F)
can serve as the band projection of a properly designed material from the same class. Thus, the complete topological invariants associated to the band projections of dynamical matrices are account for by the
K_{0}
-theory of
C^{\ast}_{r}(F)
. The latter accepts a natural abelian semigroup structure and its enveloping group often admits a small number of generators. As already advertised in our introductory remarks, by identifying these generators, one gains complete knowledge and control of the dynamical features supported by our class of architected materials that are robust against the allowed deformations of the materials.

The goal of this section is to advertise the Baum-Connes machinery
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
, which is one of the most efficient tools available for mapping the
K
-theories of group
C^{\ast}
-algebras. This will help us to map the isomorphism classes of the
K_{0}
-groups of the frieze group algebras. Additionally, we will collect results scattered in the literature in order to supply concrete presentations of the generators
K_{0}
-groups.

Remark 4.1
.

In this work we will not deal at all with
K_{1}
-theories, which become relevant if the so called chiral symmetry is imposed on the dynamical matrices
[
47
]
. This, however, will require a completely different set of experimental conditions.

On a similar note, it would generally appear natural to classify materials which are symmetric under a group action using (twisted) equivariant K-theory
[
27
]
, where representatives come from Hamiltonians that are symmetric under a (projective) representation of the symmetry group and the equivalence relation is modified to equivariant homotopy. In this work, non-equivariant K-theory is the correct choice since the invariance under the frieze group is merely an automatic consequence of the protocol that generates the architected material. However, the finer classification of equivariant K-theory would become relevant if one could control the experimental setup such as to impose symmetry also under some representation of the frieze group which acts non-trivially on the internal modes of the resonators.

\Diamond

4.1.

-theories by Baum-Connes machinery

The Baum-Connes assembly map
\mu
makes the connection

K_{0}^{F}(\underline{E}F)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mu}}{{\to}}K_{0}\big(C_{r}^{\ast}(F)\big)

(4.1)

[

Our first task is to decide on a model for
\underline{E}F
. The full group
{\rm Iso}({\mathcal{S}})
of isometries of the strip is the Lie group given by the semidirect product of the Lie group

generated by the two reflections against the two axes of the strip. It has a finite number of connected components. Then, according to
[
7
]
[p. 8], the space of the left cosets

and also for any of its discrete subgroups. Therefore,

can be chosen as
{\mathbb{R}}

Our next task is to compute the abelian groups

and the main tool here is the equivariant Chern character
[

39

[

(4.3)

where

is a set of representative members: one for each conjugacy class for each finite order element in
F
.

X^{\gamma}
is the space of fix points of

under the action of
\gamma
and
Z(\gamma)
is the centralizer of
\gamma
. The groups appearing on the right are the ordinary homology groups of spaces with constant coefficients in
{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Remark 4.3
.

As already stressed in our introduction, the tensoring with

{\mathbb{Q}}
and the K-homology groups can be taken with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

\Diamond

Based on the above observation and the fact that

(4.4)

14
]
[Th. 23.1.2] to
C^{\ast}_{r}(\mathrm{Dih}_{\infty}\times\mathrm{Dih}_{1})\simeq C^{\ast}_{r}(\mathrm{Dih}_{\infty})\otimes C^{\ast}_{r}(\langle e,v_{2}\rangle)
.

5.
Algorithm for computing the K-theoretic labels

Given a band projection
\pi(p)
, its class in
K_{0}
-theory can be expressed in one of the bases
\{p_{a}\}
listed in the previous section,
[p]_{0}=\sum_{a}n_{a}[p_{a}]_{0}
,
n_{a}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. From general considerations, we can be sure that two band projections fall into the same topological class if their K-theoretic labels
\{n_{a}\}
all coincide. Our goal for this section is to supply practical algorithms for computing the K-theoretic labels of a band projection, generated by an arbitrary dynamical matrix. For this, we employ Kasparov’s bivariant K-theory and in particular Kasparov’s product
[
36
,
37
]
for guidance and as a convenient book-keeping instrument, as well as to illustrate a model calculation that can be repeated for other group
C^{\ast}
-algebras. We use standard notation,
e.g.
as in
[
14
]
, and a brief review of the concepts is supplied below.

5.1.
General considerations

For ungraded
C^{\ast}
-algebras
A
and
B
, the elements of Kasparov’s abelian group
KK_{0}(A,B)
can be presented as the homotopy class of a pair of morphisms
\bar{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})
from
A
to
{\rm End}^{\ast}\big(\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}},B)\big)
with the property that
\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}
takes values in
{\mathbb{K}}\otimes B

[
21
]
. In particular,
KK_{0}({\mathbb{C}},A)
coincides with the K-group
K_{0}(A)
and any morphism
\psi:A\to B
supplies an element of
KK_{0}(A,B)
. Furthermore, if
A
,
B
,
C
are
C^{\ast}
-algebras, there is an associative product

KK_{0}(A,B)\times KK_{0}(B,C)\to KK_{0}(A,C)

(5.1)

with quite exceptional properties
[
22
]
. In particular, if
\psi:A\to B
and
\psi^{\prime}:B\to C
are
C^{\ast}
-algebra morphisms, then

[\psi]\times[\psi^{\prime}]=[\psi^{\prime}\circ\psi].

(5.2)

This particular case will actually cover all applications considered here.

Now, let
F
be any of the frieze groups. In the KK-language, our task is to construct
all
pairings of the type

KK_{0}\big({\mathbb{C}},C^{\ast}_{r}(F)\big)\times KK_{0}\big(C^{\ast}_{r}(F),{\mathbb{C}}\big)\to KK_{0}({\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{C}})\simeq{\mathbb{Z}},

(5.3)

such that

[p_{a}]_{0}\times[\bar{\phi}_{b}]=\Lambda\,\delta_{a,b},\quad\Lambda\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\times},

(5.4)

where
\{[p_{a}]\}
is one of the K-theoretic bases listed in the previous section. If
[p]_{0}=\sum_{a}n_{a}[p_{a}]_{0}
,
n_{a}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, is the decomposition of the
K_{0}
-class of a projection in such basis, then

\Lambda^{-1}\,[p]_{0}\times[\bar{\phi}_{a}]=n_{a}

(5.5)

and this will supply an algorithm to map the K-theoretic labels
\{n_{a}\}
, hence, all the topological invariants associated to a projection
p\in{\mathbb{K}}\otimes C_{r}^{\ast}(F)
.

Our strategy is to construct the pairings (
5.3
) in two steps,

\Big(KK\big({\mathbb{C}},C^{\ast}_{r}(F)\big)\times KK\big(C^{\ast}_{r}(F),C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F})\big)\Big)\times KK\big(C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F}),{\mathbb{C}}\big)\to{\mathbb{Z}},

(5.6)

where
\tilde{F}
is a finite group. The reasoning behind the stated strategy is that the pairings in the big parentheses land in
KK\big({\mathbb{C}},C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F})\big)
and the pairings
KK\big({\mathbb{C}},C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F})\big)\times KK\big(C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F}),{\mathbb{C}}\big)
are essentially known and computable once the representation theory of the finite group
\tilde{F}
is resolved. Hence, the task reduces to finding appropriate Kasparov cycles from
KK\big(C^{\ast}_{r}(F),C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F})\big)
. As we shall see, this cycle will take the form of a simple morphism.

5.2.
Explicit pairings

As we have seen in section
2
, all frieze groups can be derived from generators and relations. In particular, they all have
{\mathbb{Z}}
, generated by the element
u
in our notation, as a normal subgroup.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
F
be any of the frieze groups and let
k{\mathbb{Z}}
be the subgroup of
F
generated by
u^{k}
for some fixed
k\geq 2
. Then
k{\mathbb{Z}}
is a normal subgroup of
F
.

Proof.

We can treat all frieze groups at once by using the conventions stated in subsection
2.1
. Indeed, given the multiplication rule (
2.1
), we have

(u^{n}v^{\alpha})^{-1}u^{k}(u^{n}v^{\alpha})=v^{\alpha}u^{k}v^{\alpha}=u^{(-1)^{\alpha_{1}}k}\in k{\mathbb{Z}},

(5.7)

which shows that, indeed,
k{\mathbb{Z}}
is indeed a normal subgroup.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

There exist the group morphisms
F\to F/k{\mathbb{Z}}
from the infinite frieze groups to finite groups. The latter correspond to adding the relation
u^{k}=1
to the defining relations of the frieze groups.

Remark 5.3
.

The above statements will also be relevant for our numerical applications. Indeed, the quotient
F/k{\mathbb{Z}}
supplies a finite group and a canonical morphism
F\to F/k{\mathbb{Z}}
, which deliver convenient finite size approximations for the infinite lattice models (see subsection
6.2
).

\Diamond

According to Proposition
3.4
, these morphisms lift to morphisms
\varphi_{k}:C^{\ast}_{r}(F)\to C^{\ast}_{r}(F/k{\mathbb{Z}})
of
C^{\ast}
-algebras, hence they supply Kasparov cycles

[\varphi_{k}]\in KK\big(C^{\ast}_{r}(F),C^{\ast}_{r}(F/k{\mathbb{Z}})\big).

For elements from
\ell^{1}(F)
, the morphism
\varphi_{k}
can be written down explicitly as

\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\sum_{\alpha}c(n,\alpha)u^{n}v^{\alpha}\mapsto\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\sum_{\alpha}\Big(\sum_{r\in k{\mathbb{Z}}}c(m+r,\alpha)\Big)\tilde{u}^{m}\tilde{v}^{\alpha},

(5.8)

where
\tilde{u}
and
\tilde{v}_{j}
are the classes of
u
and
v_{j}
’s in
F/k{\mathbb{Z}}
. Below is an alternative characterization of the morphism
\varphi_{k}
:

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}_{k}
be the canonical trace on
{\mathbb{C}}F/k{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then
{\mathcal{T}}_{k}\circ\varphi_{k}
supplies a positive trace on
{\mathbb{C}}F
. Let
({\mathcal{H}}_{k},\pi_{k})
be the right GNS representation of
C^{\ast}_{r}(F)
for this tracial state. Then the image
\pi_{k}({\mathbb{C}}F)
in
{\mathbb{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{k})
coincides with the right regular representation of
C^{\ast}_{r}(F/k{\mathbb{Z}})
. Hence,
\varphi_{k}
can be identified with the map
\pi_{k}
.

Proof.

First, let us point out that the trace
{\mathcal{T}}_{k}
generates the right regular representation of
C^{\ast}_{r}(F/k{\mathbb{Z}})
and, since
\varphi_{k}
is surjective,
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}
coincide with
\ell^{2}(F/k{\mathbb{Z}})
, the Hilbert space of the right regular representation. Then a direct computation shows that
\pi_{k}(f)=\pi\big(\varphi_{k}(f)\big)
for any
f\in{\mathbb{C}}F
, where
\pi
is the right regular representation of
F/k{\mathbb{Z}}
.
∎

For us, the relevant finite group is
\tilde{F}:=F/2{\mathbb{Z}}
. Now, given a finite group
G
, any of its finite-dimensional representations supply a morphism
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)\to{\mathbb{K}}
, hence a cycle from
KK\big(C^{\ast}_{r}(G),{\mathbb{C}}\big)
. On the other hand, the
K_{0}
-theory of
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
can be read off from the ring of representations of the group
G

[
40
]
[Th. 3.2]. Then we have canonical pairings
KK\big({\mathbb{C}},C^{\ast}_{r}(G)\big)\times KK\big(C^{\ast}_{r}(G),{\mathbb{C}}\big)\to{\mathbb{Z}}
, which in the present context work as follows:

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
F
be any of the frieze groups and
\tilde{F}
be as above. If
\tilde{p}
is a projection from
M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})\otimes C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F})
, then the map

\tilde{F}\ni\tilde{f}\mapsto\left.U_{\tilde{f}}\right|_{\pi(\tilde{p})[{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\otimes\ell^{2}(\tilde{F})]}

(5.9)

supplies a representation of
\tilde{F}
by linear maps on the invariant sub-space
\pi(\tilde{p})[{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\otimes\ell^{2}(\tilde{F})]
. Let
\{\rho_{a}\}_{a=\overline{1,|\tilde{F}|}}
be a complete set of irreducible representations of
\tilde{F}
. Then
\{\rho_{a}\}
extend to morphisms from
{\mathbb{K}}\otimes C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F})
to
{\mathbb{K}}
that generate a basis for Kasparov’s group
KK\big(C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F}),{\mathbb{C}}\big)
. Furthermore, the pairings

\tilde{n}_{a}:=[\tilde{p}]_{0}\times[\rho_{a}]=\frac{1}{|\tilde{F}|}\sum_{\tilde{f}\in\tilde{F}}{\rm Tr}\big(U_{\tilde{f}}\pi(\tilde{p})\big)\,{\rm Tr}\big(\rho_{a}(\tilde{f})\big)\in{\mathbb{Z}},

(5.10)

supply a full set of
K
-theoretic labels for
\tilde{p}
.
Above, “
\times
” refers to the Kasparov product

KK\big({\mathbb{C}},C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F})\big)\times KK\big(C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F}),{\mathbb{C}}\big)\to KK({\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{C}})\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}.

(5.11)

Proof.

Any projection
\tilde{p}
can be seen as a
C^{\ast}
-algebra morphism from
{\mathbb{C}}
to
M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})\otimes C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F})
. Indeed, the map

{\mathbb{C}}\ni c\mapsto c\,\tilde{p}\in M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})\otimes C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F})

(5.12)

preserve multiplication precisely because of the idempotency of
\tilde{p}
,

c_{1}c_{2}\mapsto(c_{1}\tilde{p})(c_{2}\tilde{p})=c_{1}c_{2}\,\tilde{p}^{2}=c_{1}c_{2}\,\tilde{p}.

(5.13)

We denote this morphism by the same symbol
\tilde{p}
. We claim that Eq. (
5.10
) is nothing else but the trace of
({\rm id}\otimes\rho_{a})\circ\tilde{p}
. According to our observation at the beginning of the previous subsection,
({\rm id}\otimes\rho_{a})\circ\tilde{p}
encodes the Kasparov product
[\tilde{p}]_{0}\times[\rho_{a}]
, which lands in
KK_{0}({\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{C}})
, and the trace applied on
({\rm id}\otimes\rho_{a})\circ\tilde{p}
is simply the isomorphism mapping
KK_{0}({\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{C}})
to
{\mathbb{Z}}
. Thus, the proof is complete if we can confirm the above claim. Now, suppose first that
\tilde{p}
is from
C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F})
. Then, if
\tilde{p}=\sum_{\tilde{f}}\tilde{p}_{\tilde{f}}\,\tilde{f}
, the coefficients of this expansion are given by (see Remark
3.3
)

\tilde{p}_{\tilde{f}}={\mathcal{T}}(\tilde{f}^{-1}\,\tilde{p})=\frac{1}{|\tilde{F}|}{\rm Tr}\big(U_{\tilde{f}}\,\pi(\tilde{p})\big).

(5.14)

More generally, if
\tilde{p}
is from
M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})\otimes C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F})
, then the coefficients
\tilde{p}_{\tilde{f}}
are from
M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
and
\tilde{p}_{\tilde{f}}=({\rm id}\otimes{\mathcal{T}})(\tilde{f}^{-1}\tilde{p})
. Consequently,

{\rm Tr}(\tilde{p}_{\tilde{f}})=\frac{1}{|\tilde{F}|}{\rm Tr}\big(U_{\tilde{f}}\,\pi(\tilde{p})\big).

(5.15)

Then

{\rm Tr}\Big(({\rm id}\otimes\rho_{a})(\tilde{p})\Big)={\rm Tr}\Big(\sum_{\tilde{f}\in\tilde{F}}\tilde{p}_{\tilde{f}}\otimes\rho_{a}(\tilde{f})\Big)=\frac{1}{|\tilde{F}|}\sum_{\tilde{f}\in\tilde{F}}{\rm Tr}(U_{\tilde{f}}\pi(\tilde{p})\big){\rm Tr}\big(\rho_{a}(\tilde{f})\big),

(5.16)

and this completes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

The representations
\rho_{a}
generate a complete set of mutually orthogonal central projections
[
49
]
[pg. 50]

\tilde{p}_{a}=\tfrac{d_{a}}{|\tilde{F}|}\sum_{\tilde{f}\in\tilde{F}}{\rm Tr}\big(\rho_{a}(\tilde{f})\big)\,\tilde{f}^{-1},\quad d_{a}={\rm dim}\,\rho_{a},

(5.17)

for which

C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F})=\bigoplus_{a}\tilde{p}_{a}C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F})\tilde{p}_{a}\simeq\bigoplus_{a}\rho_{a}(C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F}))=\bigoplus_{a}M_{d_{a}}({\mathbb{C}})

(5.18)

and a basis of
K_{0}(C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F}))
is obtained by choosing a rank-
1
-projection from each block. The K-theoretic labels
\tilde{n}_{a}
in Eq. (
5.3
) are the expansion coefficients of a projection with respect to such a basis.

\Diamond

Remark 5.7
.

The right side of Eq. (
5.10
) returns the power
n_{a}
in the decomposition
\oplus_{a}\;\rho_{a}^{n_{a}}
of the representation (
5.9
) in the irreducible representations. The Kasparov groups and product are used here to place these algebraic relations in a topological context. Indeed, when phrased as above, the pairings communicate much more, specifically, that the
K
-theoretic labels are invariant against continuous stable deformations of
\tilde{p}
. The Kasparov groups and product also supplies a very convenient book keeping instrument. Lastly, they enable us to place this and the following computations in a framework that can supply guidance in many other situations.

\Diamond

Now, according to Proposition
5.4
,
\varphi_{2}
lands exactly in the right regular representation of
\tilde{F}
. Hence
\varphi_{2}
can be seen as a cycle from
KK\big(C^{\ast}_{r}(F),C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F})\big)
. The following statement, together with the explicit checks of the assumptions, carried out in the next subsection, assures us that
\varphi_{2}
is the actual sought cycle, mentioned in our discussion of the general strategy:

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
F
be any of the frieze groups and
\{p_{a}\}
be the basis of the
K_{0}
-group of
C^{\ast}_{r}(F)
, as listed in the previous section. If there is a complete set of irreducible representations
\{\rho_{b}\}
of
\tilde{F}=F/2{\mathbb{Z}}
such that the map,

K_{0}\big(C^{\ast}_{r}(F)\big)\ni[p_{a}]_{0}\mapsto\big\{[p_{a}]_{0}\times[\varphi_{2}]\times[\rho_{b}]\big\}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times|\tilde{F}|},

(5.19)

is injective, then we can find a set of representations
\{\chi_{a}\}
and corresponding classes
\{[\chi_{a}]\}\subset KK\big(C^{\ast}_{r}(\tilde{F}),{\mathbb{C}}\big)
resolving the K-theoretic labels, in the sense that

[p_{a}]_{0}\times[\varphi_{2}]\times[\chi_{b}]=\Lambda\delta_{a,b},\quad\Lambda\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\times}.

(5.20)

Consequently, the full set of K-theoretic labels of a projection
p
from
{\mathbb{K}}\otimes C^{\ast}_{r}(F)
can be computed as

n_{a}=\frac{1}{\Lambda|\tilde{F}|}\sum_{\tilde{f}\in\tilde{F}}{\rm Tr}\big(U_{\tilde{f}}\varphi_{2}(p)\big)\,{\rm Tr}\big(\chi_{a}(\tilde{f})\big)\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

(5.21)

Proof.

Consider the matrix
{\rm R}
with integer entries
{\rm R}_{ba}=\big([p_{a}]_{0}\times[\varphi_{2}]\big)\times[\rho_{b}]
, describing a homomorphism
K_{0}(C_{r}^{*}(F))\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}^{{\mathcal{N}}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{\lvert\tilde{F}\rvert}
, where
{\mathcal{N}}
is the dimension of the corresponding
K_{0}
-group. By assumption on the injectivity,
{\rm R}
has a left inverse
M
with rational entries and, as such, we can find
\Lambda\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\times}
and a matrix
\tilde{M}
with integer entries such that
\sum_{c}\tilde{M}_{bc}{\rm R}_{ca}=\Lambda\,\delta_{ab}
. Setting

\tilde{\chi}_{b}(f)=\Lambda^{-1}\sum_{c}{\rm Tr}(\rho_{c}(f))\tilde{M}_{bc}=\sum_{c}{\rm Tr}(\rho_{c}(f))M_{bc},

(5.22)

then, for any projection
p\sim p_{a}^{\otimes n_{a}}
, one has

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\tilde{F}|}\sum_{\tilde{f}\in\tilde{F}}{\rm Tr}\big(U_{\tilde{f}}\varphi_{2}(p)\big)\,\tilde{\chi}_{b}(\tilde{f})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\tilde{F}|}\sum_{\tilde{f}\in\tilde{F}}n_{a}{\rm Tr}\big(U_{\tilde{f}}\varphi_{2}(p_{a})\big)\,\tilde{\chi}_{b}(\tilde{f})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\tilde{F}|}\sum_{\tilde{f}\in\tilde{F}}n_{a}{\rm Tr}\big(U_{\tilde{f}}\varphi_{2}(p_{a})\big)\,\tilde{\chi}_{b}(\tilde{f})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\tilde{F}|}\sum_{\tilde{f}\in\tilde{F}}n_{a}{\rm Tr}\big(U_{\tilde{f}}\varphi_{2}(p_{a})\big)\,\sum_{c}{\rm Tr}(\rho_{c}(f))M_{bc}

\displaystyle=\sum_{c}n_{a}{\rm R}_{ca}M_{bc}=n_{a}\delta_{ab}.

Lastly, if we set
\chi_{b}=\oplus_{c}\rho_{c}^{\tilde{M}_{bc}}
as the desired representations, then

\tilde{\chi}_{b}(\tilde{f})=\Lambda^{-1}\,{\rm Tr}\big(\chi_{b}(\tilde{f})\big)

(5.23)

and the statement follows. ∎

6.
Numerical Experiments

6.1.
The generating dynamical matrices

One refers to a set of dynamical matrices as generating models if their band projections supply a complete basis of the
K_{0}
-group. Of course, one can can always start from
h_{j}=-p_{j}
with
\{p_{j}\}
a basis of the
K_{0}
-group, but sometime this fails because
p_{j}
’s may involve an infinite number of coefficients in their expansion,
i.e.
they don’t have finite range. The latter is essential for laboratory implementations of the models. While this will not be the case here, let us recall that, in such cases, one simply truncates the series, making sure that the truncation does not close the spectral gap. This opening paragraph serves as a brief reminder of the well known fact that a successful computation of the
K_{0}
-group and of its generators is equivalent to finding all relevant topological models supported by the algebra of dynamical matrices. Any other dynamical matrix can be deformed into a stack of elementary models from this generating set.

The present context is special because all model Hamiltonians can be generated directly from the basis of the
K_{0}
-groups. For example, for the group
p2mm
, the generating dynamical matrices are
h_{j}=-p_{j}
, where

h_{1}=-e,\ h_{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}(v_{1}-e),\ h_{3}=\tfrac{1}{2}(uv_{1}-e),\ h_{4}=-\tfrac{1}{2}(e+v_{2})

(6.1)

and

h_{5}=\tfrac{1}{4}(v_{1}v_{2}-v_{1}-v_{2}+e),\ h_{6}=\tfrac{1}{4}(uv_{1}v_{2}-uv_{1}-v_{2}+e)

(6.2)

6.2.
Numerical algorithm for the K-theoretic labels

We generate finite-size models that can be handled on a computer from the group algebra
C^{\ast}_{R}(F/k{\mathbb{Z}})
, for some large even integer
k
. As we already stated, the subgroup
k{\mathbb{Z}}
is normal in
F
, hence the quotient of a self adjoint element
h
from
F
to
F/k{\mathbb{Z}}
preserves the resolvent spectrum

{\mathbb{R}}\setminus{\rm Spec}(h)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}\setminus{\rm Spec}(h\cdot k{\mathbb{Z}}).

(6.4)

In other words, the spectral gaps of
h
are not contaminated by our finite volume approximations.

e^{-2|f\cdot 0-f^{\prime}\cdot 0|}|f\rangle\langle f^{\prime}|
,

with a coupling strength that decays exponentially with the distance between their centers. The values of the six topological invariants associated with the bottom spectral band are displayed in each panel.

First, we need to acknowledge a few practical book keeping details. The group
F/k{\mathbb{Z}}
is manipulated on the computer by mapping it in a convenient model group. This model group is simply a subgroup of the permutation group of
|F/k{\mathbb{Z}}|
objects. For
F=p2mm

u^{n}v_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}v_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\mapsto f_{j},\quad j=4n+2\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1}+1,\quad n=\overline{0,k-1},\quad\alpha_{j}=0,1,

which gives rise to a map
F/k{\mathbb{Z}}\to\{1,2,\ldots,|F/k{\mathbb{Z}}|\}
. Then the group table can be encoded in a two-index array

(j,j^{\prime})\mapsto{\rm Prod}_{j,j^{\prime}}\in\{1,\ldots,|F/k{\mathbb{Z}}|\},

(6.6)

by using the rule (
2.1
). Obviously, the relation stated in Eq. (
6.6
) assigns a unique permutation to each element
f_{j}\in F/k{\mathbb{Z}}
, acting as

f_{j}(j^{\prime})={\rm Prod}_{j,j^{\prime}},\quad j^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,|F/k{\mathbb{Z}}|\}.

(6.7)

The basis
|i,f\rangle
of the Hilbert space corresponding to the right regular representation of
F/k{\mathbb{Z}}
is labeled using the scheme

|i,f_{j}\rangle\mapsto|w\rangle,\quad w=i+N_{s}j,\quad i=\overline{1,N_{s}},\quad j=\overline{1,|F/k{\mathbb{Z}}|},

(6.8)

and the matrix elements of the representations
\pi(f)
are stored using the rule (
3.17
), which is applied using the labels and the array

introduced above. In both rules (
2.1
) and (

), the sums involving the index

are computed modulo

. By setting

, the above schemes can and are applied to the group
\tilde{F}
too.

Now, the first challenge for us is how to implement the map
\varphi_{2}
. The solution rests on using the spectral projections of the reduced element

:

P_{j}=\tfrac{1}{k}\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}e^{-\imath 2\pi jn/k}\pi(u)^{n},\quad j=\overline{0,k-1}.

(6.9)

Then we define
\varphi_{2}
by projecting

F/k{\mathbb{Z}}\ni f\mapsto\varphi_{2}(f)=\Pi\pi(f)\Pi,\quad{\rm\Pi}:=P_{0}\oplus P_{k/2}.

(6.10)

Eq. (
6.10
) is justified by the following relations:

\pi(u^{m})\Pi=\Pi\pi(u^{m})=\pi(u)^{m\,{\rm mod}2}\,\Pi,\quad\pi(v_{j})\Pi=\Pi\pi(v_{j}),

which enable us to evaluate

(6.12)

and confirm that Eq.
6.10

5.8

\tilde{u}:=\Pi\pi(u)\Pi,\quad\tilde{v}_{j}:=\Pi\pi(v_{j})\Pi.

The second and last challenge is how to evaluate the trace

. Switching the order of the operators inside the trace, we have

{\rm Tr}\big(\varphi_{2}(p)U_{\tilde{f}}\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}\sum_{\tilde{f}^{\prime}\in\tilde{F}}\langle i,\tilde{f}^{\prime}|\varphi_{2}(p)U_{\tilde{f}}|i,\tilde{f}^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}\sum_{\tilde{f}^{\prime}\in\tilde{F}}\langle i,\tilde{f}^{\prime}|\varphi_{2}(p)|i,\tilde{f}\tilde{f}^{\prime}\rangle.

(6.14)

Next, we connect with what was said above and represent the elements
\tilde{f}^{\prime}
of
\tilde{F}
as
\Pi f
with

from

, in which case,

{\rm Tr}\big(U_{\tilde{f}}\varphi_{2}(p)\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}\sum_{f\in F/k{\mathbb{Z}}}\langle i,f|\Pi\pi(p)\Pi|i,\tilde{f}f\rangle,

(6.15)

where the sum is taken over all elements of
F/k{\mathbb{Z}}
. Here,

is seen as an element of
F/k{\mathbb{Z}}
via the canonical section of the morphism
F/k{\mathbb{Z}}\to F/2{\mathbb{Z}}
. To verify that the normalization constants are properly fixed, we have checked that

, the dimension of the right regular representation of
\tilde{F}
when
N_{s}
is set to one.

Topological spectral flows with plate resonators

In this section, we illustrate how the discrete models discussed so far can be implemented with physical resonating structures. Fig.
7.1

p2mm

7.1

As such, the collective dynamics of the plates in the regime of weak couplings and in a frequency domain around the frequency of this particular mode can be accurately quantified using a discrete model with one resonant mode per resonator.

Couplings between the resonators are implemented by thin metal bridges that respect the
p2mm
symmetry. The strength of these couplings can be controlled by the position of these bridges: A bridge placed closer to a node (anti-node) of the resonant mode produces a weaker (stronger) coupling (see the mode’s spatial profile in Fig.
7.1

E

(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3},E_{1},E_{2})

7.1

Remark 7.1
.

Among other things, the above K-theoretic labels assures us that actually
K_{i}
together with
K_{0}=1
can serve as an alternative generating set of model dynamical matrices. In many respects, this set is more natural than the one we originally considered.

\Diamond

Each of the resonating structures seen in Fig.
7.2
corresponds to a point in the 5-dimensional parameter space
(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3},E_{1},E_{2})
. Based on the K-theoretic labels computed above, we predict that, no matter how we interpolate between two of these five points, we cannot avoid the closing of the gap in the resonant spectrum. This phenomenon should be extremely robust because the K-theory predicts that the gap closing cannot be avoided even if the structure is connected to external frames, provided this connection does not change the topological class of the initial and final resonating structures. Furthermore, we conjecture that the topological spectral flows are robust against disorder.

The five topologically distinct resonating structures from Fig.

(7.1)
