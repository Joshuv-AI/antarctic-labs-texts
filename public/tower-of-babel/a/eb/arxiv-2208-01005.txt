Abstract
We revisit parallel-innermost term rewriting as a model of parallel computation on inductive data structures and provide a corresponding notion of runtime complexity parametric in the size of the start term. We propose automatic techniques to derive both upper and lower bounds on parallel complexity of rewriting that enable a direct reuse of existing techniques for sequential complexity. The applicability and the precision of the method are demonstrated by the relatively light effort in extending the program analysis tool AProVE and by experiments on numerous benchmarks from the literature.

Term Rewriting and Innermost Runtime Complexity

We assume basic familiarity with term rewriting (see, e.g.,

[
11
]
) and recall standard definitions to fix notation.
As customary for analysis of runtime complexity of rewriting,
we consider terms as

without sharing of subtrees.

We first define
Term Rewrite Systems
and
Innermost Rewriting
.

\mathcal{T}(\Sigma,\mathcal{V})
denotes the set of
terms
over a
finite signature
\Sigma
and the set of variables
\mathcal{V}
.
For a term
t

size

\lvert t\rvert

is defined by:
(a)

\lvert t\rvert=1

(b)
if
t=f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})

\lvert t\rvert=1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert t_{i}\rvert
.
The set
\mathcal{P}\!\mathit{os}(t)
of
the
positions
of
t
is
defined by:
(a) if
t\in\mathcal{V}
, then
\mathcal{P}\!\mathit{os}(t)=\{\varepsilon\}
,
and (b) if
t=f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
, then

\mathcal{P}\!\mathit{os}(t)=\{\varepsilon\}\cup\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq n}\{i\pi\mid\pi\in\mathcal{P}\!\mathit{os}(t_{i})\}
.
The position
\varepsilon
is
the
root position

of term
t
.
If
t=f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
,

\mathrm{root}(t)=f
is the
root symbol
of
t
.
The
(strict) prefix order

>
on positions is
the strict partial order given by:

\tau>\pi
iff there exists
\pi^{\prime}\neq\varepsilon
such that
\pi\pi^{\prime}=\tau
.
Two positions
\pi
and
\tau
are

\pi>\tau
nor
\pi=\tau
nor
\tau>\pi
hold.
For
\pi\in\mathcal{P}\!\mathit{os}(t)

is the subterm of
t

at position
\pi
, and we write

for the term that results
from

A substitution
\sigma
is a mapping from

to

\mathit{Dom}(\sigma)=\{x\in\mathcal{V}\mid\sigma(x)\neq x\}

We write

\sigma(x)=x

We extend substitutions to terms by

\sigma(f(t_{1},\ldots,f_{n}))=f(\sigma(t_{1}),\ldots,\sigma(t_{n}))
.
We may write

\sigma(t)
.

For a term
t
,
\mathcal{V}(t)
is the set of variables
in

t
.
A
term rewrite system (TRS)

\mathcal{R}
is a set of rules

\{\ell_{1}\to r_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}\to r_{n}\}

with

\ell_{i},r_{i}\in\mathcal{T}(\Sigma,\mathcal{V})

\ell_{i}\not\in\mathcal{V}

The

Here,

If no proper subterm of

.

\Sigma_{d}^{\mathcal{R}}=\{f\mid f(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n})\to r\in\mathcal{R}\}

For a relation

We wish to provide static bounds on the
length of the longest
rewrite sequence from terms of a specific size.
Here we
use innermost evaluation strategies,
which closely correspond to call-by-value strategies used in many
programming languages.
We focus
on rewrite sequences that start with
basic terms
,
corresponding to function calls where a function is applied to data
objects. The
resulting
notion of complexity for term
rewriting is known as
innermost runtime complexity
.

Definition 1
(Innermost Runtime Complexity
\mathrm{irc}

[
27
,
38
]
)

The
derivation height
of a term
t
wrt a relation
\to
is the
length of the longest sequence of
\to
-steps
from
t
:

\Dh(t,\to)=\sup\{e\mid\exists\,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}(\Sigma,\mathcal{V}).\;t\to^{e}t^{\prime}\}
where
\to^{e}
is the
e^{\textrm{th}}

iterate of
\to
.
If
t
starts an infinite
\to
-sequence, we write
\Dh(t,\to)=\omega
.
Here,
\omega
is the smallest infinite ordinal, i.e.,

\omega>n
holds for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

A term
f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})
is
basic (for a TRS
\mathcal{R}
)

iff
f\in\Sigma_{d}^{\mathcal{R}}
and
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\in\mathcal{T}(\Sigma_{c}^{\mathcal{R}},\mathcal{V})
.

\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{basic}}^{\mathcal{R}}
is the set of basic terms
for a TRS
\mathcal{R}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
the
innermost runtime complexity
function
is

\mathrm{irc}_{\mathcal{R}}(n)=\sup\{\Dh(t,{\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}})\mid t\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{basic}}^{\mathcal{R}},\lvert t\rvert\leq n\}
.
For all
P\subseteq\mathbb{N}\cup\{\omega\}
,
\sup\,P
is the
least upper bound of
P
, where
\sup\,\emptyset=0
.

to analyse

[

]
, originally designed to find upper bounds
for(sequential) innermost runtime complexity. A central
idea is to group all function calls by a rewrite rule
together

rather than to separate them (as with DPs for proving termination

[

). We use

to represent these

Definition 2
(Sharp Terms
\mathcal{T}^{\sharp}
)

For every
f\in\Sigma_{d}
, we introduce a fresh symbol

f^{\sharp}
of the same arity, called a
sharp symbol
.
For a term
t=f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
with

f\in\Sigma_{d}
, we define
t^{\sharp}=f^{\sharp}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
.
For all other terms
t
, we define
t^{\sharp}=t
.

\mathcal{T}^{\sharp}=\{t^{\sharp}\mid t\in\mathcal{T}(\Sigma,\mathcal{V}),\mathrm{root}(t)\in\Sigma_{d}\}
denotes the set of
sharp terms
.

To
get an upper bound for sequential complexity, we
“count” how often
each rewrite rule is used. The idea is
that when a rule
\ell\to r
is used,
the cost (i.e., number of rewrite steps for the evaluation)
of the function call to the instance of
\ell

is 1 + the sum
of the costs of all the function calls in the resulting instance of
r
,
counted separately in some fixed order.
To group
k
function calls together, we use “compound symbols”

\mathsf{Com}_{k}
of arity
k
, which intuitively represent the sum of
the runtimes of their arguments.

Definition 3
(Dependency Tuple, DT
[
38
]
)

A
dependency tuple (DT)
is a rule of the form

s^{\sharp}\to\mathsf{Com}_{n}(t^{\sharp}_{1},\ldots,t^{\sharp}_{n})

where
s^{\sharp},t^{\sharp}_{1},\ldots,t^{\sharp}_{n}\in\mathcal{T}^{\sharp}
.
Let
\ell\to r
be a rule with
\mathcal{P}\!\mathit{os}_{d}(r)=\{\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n}\}

and
\pi_{1}\gtrdot\ldots\gtrdot\pi_{n}
for a total order
\gtrdot

(e.g., lexicographic order) on positions.
Then
\mathit{DT}(\ell\to r)=\ell^{\sharp}\to\mathsf{Com}_{n}(r|^{\sharp}_{\pi_{1}},\ldots,r|^{\sharp}_{\pi_{n}})
.

is used as a characterisation of DTs

Definition 5

[

Let
\mathcal{R}

be a set of DTs,
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{D}

Then

\langle\mathit{DT}(\mathcal{R}),\mathit{DT}(\mathcal{R}),\mathcal{R}\rangle

canonical DT problem
for
\mathcal{R}

For a DT problem
\langle\mathcal{D},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{R}\rangle
, the set
\mathcal{D}

contains all DTs that can be used in chain trees.

\mathcal{S}
contains the DTs whose complexity remains to be
analysed.
\mathcal{R}
contains the rewrite rules for evaluating
the arguments of DTs. Here we focus on simplifying
\mathcal{S}
(thus
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{R}
are fixed during the process)
but techniques to simplify
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{R}
are available as well

]
.

Let

Then we have

If

\mathrm{irc}_{\mathcal{R}}(n)=\mathrm{irc}_{\langle\mathit{DT}(\mathcal{R}),\mathit{DT}(\mathcal{R}),\mathcal{R}\rangle}(n)
.

In practice, the focus

\mathcal{S}=\emptyset
: we always have
\mathrm{irc}_{\langle\mathcal{D},\emptyset,\mathcal{R}\rangle}(n)=0
.
To simplify and finally solve DT problems in an incremental fashion,
complexity analysis techniques called
DT processors
are used. A
DT processor takes a DT problem as input and returns a (hopefully
simpler) DT problem as well as an asymptotic complexity bound as an
output. The largest asymptotic complexity bound returned over this
incremental process is then also an upper bound for
\mathrm{irc}_{\mathcal{R}}(n)

[
38
, Corollary 21]
.

The
reduction pair processor using polynomial interpretations

[
38
]

applies a restriction
of polynomial interpretations to
\mathbb{N}

[
35
]
to infer
upper bounds on the number of times that DTs can occur
in a chain tree for terms of size at most
n
.

Definition 6
(Polynomial Interpretation, CPI)

A
polynomial interpretation

{\mathcal{P}ol}
maps
every
n
-ary function symbol to a polynomial with variables

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and coefficients from
\mathbb{N}
.

{\mathcal{P}ol}
extends
to terms via
{\mathcal{P}ol}(x)=x
for

x\in\mathcal{V}
and
{\mathcal{P}ol}(f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}))={\mathcal{P}ol}(f)({\mathcal{P}ol}(t_{1}),\ldots,{\mathcal{P}ol}(t_{n}))
.

{\mathcal{P}ol}
induces an order
\succ_{\mathcal{P}ol}
and a quasi-order

\succsim_{\mathcal{P}ol}
over terms where

s\succ_{\mathcal{P}ol}t
iff
{\mathcal{P}ol}(s)>{\mathcal{P}ol}(t)

and

s\succsim_{\mathcal{P}ol}t
iff
{\mathcal{P}ol}(s)\geq{\mathcal{P}ol}(t)

for all instantiations of variables with natural numbers.

A
complexity polynomial interpretation (CPI)

{\mathcal{P}ol}

is a polynomial interpretation where:

{\mathcal{P}ol}(\mathsf{Com}_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))=x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}
, and
for all

f\in\Sigma_{c}
,

{\mathcal{P}ol}(f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))=a_{1}\cdot x_{1}+\dots+a_{n}\cdot x_{n}+b

for some
a_{i}\in\{0,1\}
and
b\in\mathbb{N}
.

The restriction for CPIs regarding constructor symbols
enforces that the interpretation of a constructor term
t
(as an argument
of a term for which a chain tree is constructed) can
exceed its size
\lvert t\rvert
only by at most a constant factor.
This is crucial for soundness.
Using a CPI, we can now define and state correctness of the
corresponding reduction pair processor

[
38
, Theorem 27]
.

Theorem 2.3
(Reduction Pair Processor with CPIs

[
38
]
)

Let
\langle\mathcal{D},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{R}\rangle
be a DT problem,
let
\succsim
and
\succ
be induced by a CPI
{\mathcal{P}ol}
.
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be the maximal degree of all polynomials

{\mathcal{P}ol}(f^{\sharp})
for all
f\in\Sigma_{d}
.
Let
\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{R}\subseteq{\succsim}
.
If
\mathcal{S}\cap{\succ}\neq\emptyset
,
the reduction pair processor returns the DT problem

\langle\mathcal{D},\mathcal{S}\setminus{\succ},\mathcal{R}\rangle

and the complexity
\mathcal{O}(n^{k})
.
Then the reduction pair processor is sound.

3
Finding Upper Bounds for Parallel Complexity

In this section we present our main contribution: an application of the
DT framework from innermost runtime complexity to
parallel-innermost rewriting
.

The notion of parallel-innermost rewriting dates back at least
to
[
41
]
. Informally, in a parallel-innermost
rewrite step, all innermost redexes are rewritten simultaneously.
This corresponds to executing all function calls in parallel using a
call-by-value strategy on a machine with unbounded parallelism
[
16
]
.
In the literature
[
40
]
, this strategy is also known as
“max-parallel-innermost rewriting”.

Definition 7
(Parallel-Innermost Rewriting
[
20
]
)

A term
s

rewrites innermost in parallel
to
t
with a TRS
\mathcal{R}
,
written
s\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}t
, iff
s\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}^{+}}t
, and either
(a)
s\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}t
with
s
an innermost redex, or
(b)
s=f(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
,
t=f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
, and for all

1\leq k\leq n
either
s_{k}\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}t_{k}
or
s_{k}=t_{k}
is a normal form.

Definition 8
(Parallel-Innermost Runtime Complexity

For

31

12

of a function on a machine with unbounded parallelism (“wall time”), corresponding

In contrast,
\mathrm{irc}_{\mathcal{R}}
would describe the

(“CPU time”).

In the following, given a TRS
\mathcal{R}

fully automatically.
Of course, an upper bound for (sequential)

\mathrm{pirc}_{\mathcal{R}}

We will now introduce

Regardless of which of these two calls has the higher cost,

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\Dh(\mathsf{size}(\mathsf{Tree}(v,l,r))\sigma,\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}})\\

In the DT setting, we could
introduce a new symbol
\mathsf{ComPar}_{k}

that explicitly expresses that its arguments
are evaluated in parallel. This symbol would
then be interpreted as the maximum
of its arguments in an extension of

\displaystyle\mathsf{size}^{\sharp}(\mathsf{Tree}(v,l,r))

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{Com}_{2}(\mathsf{ComPar}_{2}(\mathsf{size}^{\sharp}(l),\mathsf{size}^{\sharp}(r)),\mathsf{plus}^{\sharp}(\mathsf{size}(l),\mathsf{size}(r)))

Although automation of the search for

Dependency Tuples and also adapt all existing techniques

This is why we have chosen the following alternative approach,
which is equally powerful on theoretical level and
enables immediate reuse of existing techniques in the DT framework.
Equivalently to the above, we can “factor in” the cost of
calling
\mathsf{plus}
into the maximum function:

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\Dh(\mathsf{size}(\mathsf{Tree}(v,l,r))\sigma,\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}})\\

Intuitively, this would correspond to evaluating

\mathsf{plus}(\ldots,\ldots)

twice, in two parallel threads of execution,
which costs the same amount of (wall) time as evaluating

\mathsf{plus}(\ldots,\ldots)

once.
We can represent this maximum of the execution times of two threads
by introducing
two
DTs for our recursive
\mathsf{size}
-rule:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsf{size}^{\sharp}(\mathsf{Tree}(v,l,r))&\to&\mathsf{Com}_{2}(\mathsf{size}^{\sharp}(l),\mathsf{plus}^{\sharp}(\mathsf{size}(l),\mathsf{size}(r)))\\
\mathsf{size}^{\sharp}(\mathsf{Tree}(v,l,r))&\to&\mathsf{Com}_{2}(\mathsf{size}^{\sharp}(r),\mathsf{plus}^{\sharp}(\mathsf{size}(l),\mathsf{size}(r)))\end{array}

To express the cost of a concrete rewrite sequence, we would
non-deterministically choose the DT that
corresponds to the “slower thread”.

In other words,
when a rule
\ell\to r
is used,
the cost of the function call to the instance of
\ell

is 1 + the sum
of the costs of the function calls in the resulting instance of
r

that are in structural dependency with each other
.
The actual cost of the function call to the instance of
\ell
in a concrete
rewrite sequence is the
maximum
of all the possible costs
caused by such
chains
of structural dependency
(based on the prefix order
>
on positions of defined
function symbols in
r
).
Thus,
structurally independent
function calls are considered in
separate DTs, whose non-determinism models the parallelism
of these function calls.

The notion of
structural dependency
of function calls is captured by

Def. 9
. Basically, it comes from the fact that a term
cannot be evaluated before all its subterms have been reduced to normal forms
(innermost rewriting/
call by value

This induces a “happens-before” relation for the computation

[

]

Definition 9
(Structural Dependency,
\mathit{MSDC}

For positions

for a term

\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{k}\in\mathcal{P}\!\mathit{os}_{d}(t)
and
\pi_{1}>\ldots>\pi_{k}
.
Here
\pi_{i}

structurally depends on

\pi_{j}
in
t
iff
j<i
.
A structural dependency chain
\langle\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k}\rangle

for a term
t
is

iff

k=0
and
\mathcal{P}\!\mathit{os}_{d}(t)=\emptyset
, or
k>0
and

\forall\pi\in\mathcal{P}\!\mathit{os}_{d}(t)\,.\,\pi\ngtr\pi_{1}\wedge(\pi_{1}>\pi\Rightarrow\pi\in\{\pi_{2},\ldots,\pi_{k}\})
.
We write
\mathit{MSDC}(t)
for the set of all maximal structural

t
.

Note that
\mathit{MSDC}(t)\neq\emptyset

if
\mathcal{P}\!\mathit{os}_{d}(t)=\emptyset

Due to the structural dependencies, these calls do not happen in parallel
(so call
k+1
to
\mathsf{plus}
must wait for call
k
).

Example 10

Note that
\mathrm{pirc}_{\mathcal{R}}(n)
can be asymptotically lower than
\mathrm{irc}_{\mathcal{R}}(n)
, for instance for the TRS
\mathcal{R}
with the following rules:

\begin{array}[]{rcl@{\hspace*{5ex}}|@{\hspace*{5ex}}rcl}\mathsf{doubles}(\mathsf{Zero})&\to&\mathsf{Nil}\hfil\hskip 21.52771pt\vrule\hskip 21.52771pt&\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{Zero})&\to&\mathsf{Zero}\\
\mathsf{doubles}(\mathsf{S}(x))&\to&\mathsf{Cons}(\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{S}(x)),\mathsf{doubles}(x))\hfil\hskip 21.52771pt\vrule\hskip 21.52771pt&\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{S}(x))&\to&\mathsf{S}(\mathsf{S}(\mathsf{d}(x)))\end{array}

The upper bound
\mathrm{irc}_{\mathcal{R}}(n)\in\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
is tight:
from

\mathsf{doubles}(\mathsf{S}(\mathsf{S}(\ldots\mathsf{S}(\mathsf{Zero})\ldots)))
, we get
linearly many calls to the linear-time function
\mathsf{d}
on arguments
of size linear in the start term.
However, the Parallel Dependency Tuples in this example are:

\begin{array}[]{rcl@{\hspace*{5ex}}|@{\hspace*{5ex}}rcl}\mathsf{doubles}^{\sharp}(\mathsf{Zero})&\to&\mathsf{Com}_{0}\hfil\hskip 21.52771pt\vrule\hskip 21.52771pt&\mathsf{d}^{\sharp}(\mathsf{Zero})&\to&\mathsf{Com}_{0}\\
\mathsf{doubles}^{\sharp}(\mathsf{S}(x))&\to&\mathsf{Com}_{1}(\mathsf{d}^{\sharp}(\mathsf{S}(x)))\hfil\hskip 21.52771pt\vrule\hskip 21.52771pt&\mathsf{d}^{\sharp}(\mathsf{S}(x))&\to&\mathsf{Com}_{1}(\mathsf{d}^{\sharp}(x))\\
\mathsf{doubles}^{\sharp}(\mathsf{S}(x))&\to&\mathsf{Com}_{1}(\mathsf{doubles}^{\sharp}(x))\hfil\hskip 21.52771pt\vrule\hskip 21.52771pt\end{array}

Then the following polynomial
interpretation, which orients all DTs with
\succ
and all
rules from
\mathcal{R}
with
\succsim
, proves
\mathrm{pirc}_{\mathcal{R}}(n)\in\mathcal{O}(n)
:

{\mathcal{P}ol}(\mathsf{doubles}^{\sharp}(x_{1}))={\mathcal{P}ol}(\mathsf{d}(x_{1}))=2x_{1},{\mathcal{P}ol}(\mathsf{d}^{\sharp}(x_{1}))=x_{1},{\mathcal{P}ol}(\mathsf{doubles}(x_{1}))={\mathcal{P}ol}(\mathsf{Cons}(x_{1},x_{2}))={\mathcal{P}ol}(\mathsf{Zero})={\mathcal{P}ol}(\mathsf{Nil})=1,{\mathcal{P}ol}(\mathsf{S}(x_{1}))=1+x_{1}
.

Interestingly enough, Parallel Dependency Tuples also allow us to
identify TRSs that have
no
potential for parallelisation
by parallel-innermost rewriting.

Theorem 3.3
(Absence of Parallelism by PDTs)

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a TRS
such that for all rules
\ell\to r\in\mathcal{R}
,

|\mathit{MSDC}(r)|=1
.
Then:
(a)
\mathit{PDT}(\mathcal{R})=\mathit{DT}(\mathcal{R})
;
(b) for all basic terms
t_{0}
and rewrite sequences

t_{0}\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}t_{1}\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}t_{2}\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}\dots
, also

t_{0}\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}t_{1}\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}t_{2}\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}\dots
holds (i.e., from basic terms,

\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}
and
\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}
coincide);
(c)
\mathrm{pirc}_{\mathcal{R}}(n)=\mathrm{irc}_{\mathcal{R}}(n)
.

Thus, for TRSs
\mathcal{R}
where

Thm. 3.3
applies,
no rewrite rule
can introduce
parallel redexes, and
specific analysis techniques for

\mathrm{pirc}_{\mathcal{R}}

are not needed.

4
From Parallel DTs to Innermost Rewriting

As we have seen in the previous section, we can transform a TRS
\mathcal{R}
with
parallel-innermost rewrite relation to a DT problem whose
complexity provides an upper bound of
\mathrm{pirc}_{\mathcal{R}}
(or,
for
confluent
\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}
, corresponds exactly to
\mathrm{pirc}_{\mathcal{R}}
).
However,
DTs are only one of many
available techniques
to find bounds for
\mathrm{irc}_{\mathcal{R}}
. Other techniques
include, e.g.,
Weak Dependency Pairs
[
27
]
,
usable replacement maps
[
28
]
,
the Combination Framework
[
8
]
,
a transformation to complexity
problems for integer transition systems
[
37
]
,
amortised complexity analysis
[
36
]
,
or techniques for finding
lower
bounds
[
23
]
.
Thus, can we benefit also from other
techniques for (sequential) innermost complexity to analyse parallel
complexity?

In this section, we answer the question in the affirmative, via a
generic transformation from Dependency Tuple problems back to rewrite
systems whose innermost complexity can then be analysed using
arbitrary existing techniques.

We use

relative rewriting
,
which allows for labelling some of the rewrite rules such that
their use does not contribute to the derivation height of a term.
In other words, rewrite steps with these rewrite rules are
“for free” from the perspective of complexity.
Existing state-of-the-art tools like
AProVE

[
25
]

and
TcT

[
9
]
are
able to find bounds on (innermost) runtime complexity of such
rewrite systems.

Definition 11
(Relative Rewriting)

For two TRSs
{\mathcal{R}_{1}}
and
{\mathcal{R}_{2}}
,
{\mathcal{R}_{1}}/{\mathcal{R}_{2}}
is a

relative TRS
.
Its
rewrite relation

\to_{{\mathcal{R}_{1}}/{\mathcal{R}_{2}}}
is

\to^{*}_{{\mathcal{R}_{2}}}\circ\to_{{\mathcal{R}_{1}}}\circ\to^{*}_{{\mathcal{R}_{2}}}
, i.e., rewriting with

{\mathcal{R}_{2}}
is allowed before and after each
{\mathcal{R}_{1}}
-step.
We define the
innermost
rewrite relation by

s\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{{\mathcal{R}_{1}}/{\mathcal{R}_{2}}}}t
iff

s\to^{*}_{{\mathcal{R}_{2}}}s^{\prime}\to_{{\mathcal{R}_{1}}}s^{\prime\prime}\to^{*}_{{\mathcal{R}_{2}}}t
for some terms
s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}
such that the
proper subterms of the
redexes of each step with
\to_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}
or
\to_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}
are in normal form
wrt
{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\cup{\mathcal{R}_{2}}
.

The set
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{basic}}^{{\mathcal{R}_{1}}/{\mathcal{R}_{2}}}
of basic terms for a
relative TRS
{\mathcal{R}_{1}}/{\mathcal{R}_{2}}
is

\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{basic}}^{{\mathcal{R}_{1}}/{\mathcal{R}_{2}}}=\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{basic}}^{{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\cup{\mathcal{R}_{2}}}
.
The notion of innermost runtime complexity extends to
relative TRSs in the natural way:

\mathrm{irc}_{{\mathcal{R}_{1}}/{\mathcal{R}_{2}}}(n)=\sup\{\Dh(t,{\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{{\mathcal{R}_{1}}/{\mathcal{R}_{2}}}}})\mid t\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{basic}}^{{\mathcal{R}_{1}}/{\mathcal{R}_{2}}},\lvert t\rvert\leq n\}

The rewrite relation
\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{{\mathcal{R}_{1}}/{\mathcal{R}_{2}}}}
is essentially
the same as
\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\cup{\mathcal{R}_{2}}}}
, but only steps using
rules from
{\mathcal{R}_{1}}
count towards the complexity;
steps
using rules from
{\mathcal{R}_{2}}
have no cost.
This can be useful, e.g., for representing that built-in functions
from programming languages modelled as recursive functions
have constant cost.

Example 11

Consider a variant of
Ex. 1
where

\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{S}(x),y)\to\mathsf{S}(\mathsf{plus}(x,y))
is moved
to
{\mathcal{R}_{2}}
,
but all other rules are elements of
{\mathcal{R}_{1}}
.
Then

{\mathcal{R}_{1}}/{\mathcal{R}_{2}}
would provide a modelling of the
\mathsf{size}
function that is
closer to the Rust function from
Sect. 1
.
Let
\mathsf{S}^{n}(\mathsf{Zero})
denote the term obtained by
n
-fold application
of
\mathsf{S}
to
\mathsf{Zero}
(e.g.,
\mathsf{S}^{2}(\mathsf{Zero})=\mathsf{S}(\mathsf{S}(\mathsf{Zero}))
).
Although
\Dh(\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{S}^{n}(\mathsf{Zero}),\mathsf{S}^{m}(\mathsf{Zero})),\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\cup{\mathcal{R}_{2}}}})=n+1
, we would then get
\Dh(\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{S}^{n}(\mathsf{Zero}),\mathsf{S}^{m}(\mathsf{Zero})),\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{{\mathcal{R}_{1}}/{\mathcal{R}_{2}}}})=1
, corresponding to a machine model where the
time of evaluating addition for integers is constant.

Note the similarity of a relative TRS and a Dependency Tuple
problem: only certain rewrite steps count towards the
analysed complexity.
We make use of this observation for the following transformation.

Definition 12
(Relative TRS for a Dependency Tuple Problem,
\delta
)

Let
\langle\mathcal{D},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{R}\rangle
be a Dependency Tuple problem.
We define the corresponding relative TRS

\delta(\langle\mathcal{D},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{R}\rangle)=\mathcal{S}/((\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S})\cup\mathcal{R})
.

In other words, we omit the information that steps with our
dependency tuples can happen only on top level (possibly below
constructors
\mathsf{Com}_{n}
, but above
\mathrel{\smash{\rightarrow}_{\mathcal{R}}}
steps). (As we shall see in
Thm. 4.2
, this information can be recovered.)

The following example is taken from the

Termination Problem Data Base (TPDB)

[

,
a collection of examples used at the
annual
Termination and Complexity Competition (termCOMP)

[
26

]
(see also

):

Example 14
(Confluence of
\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}
does not Imply Confluence of

\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}
)

To see that we cannot prove confluence of
\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}
just by
using a standard off-the-shelf tool for
confluence analysis of innermost or full rewriting
[
17
]
, consider the TRS

\mathcal{R}=\{\mathsf{a}\to\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{b},\mathsf{b}),\mathsf{a}\to\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{b},\mathsf{c}),\mathsf{b}\to\mathsf{c},\mathsf{c}\to\mathsf{b}\}
. For this TRS, both
\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}
and
\mathrel{\smash{\rightarrow}_{\mathcal{R}}}
are confluent.
However,
\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}
is not confluent: we can rewrite both

\mathsf{a}\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{b},\mathsf{b})
and
\mathsf{a}\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{b},\mathsf{c})
,
yet there
is no term
v
such that
\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{b},\mathsf{b})\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}v
and

\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{b},\mathsf{c})\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}v
. The reason is that the only possible rewrite
sequences with
\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}
from these terms are

\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{b},\mathsf{b})\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{c},\mathsf{c})\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{b},\mathsf{b})\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}\dots

and

\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{b},\mathsf{c})\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{c},\mathsf{b})\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{b},\mathsf{c})\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}\dots
,
with no terms in common.

Conjecture 1

If
\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}
is confluent, then
\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}
is confluent.

Confluence means:
if a term
s
can be rewritten to two
different terms
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
in 0 or more steps,
it is
always possible to rewrite
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
in 0 or more steps
to
a
term
u
.
For

\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\>\>\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow\hskip-16.0pt{\parallel}\hskip 8.0pt}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}
, the redexes that get rewritten are
fixed: all
innermost redexes simultaneously.
Thus,

s
can
rewrite to two
different
terms
t_{1}
and
t_{2}

only if at least one of these redexes can be rewritten in two
different ways using

\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}
.

Towards a sufficient criterion for confluence of parallel-innermost
rewriting, we introduce the following standard definition:

Definition 13
(Non-Overlapping)

A TRS
\mathcal{R}
is
non-overlapping
iff for any two rules

\ell\to r,u\to v\in\mathcal{R}
where variables have been renamed apart
between the rules, there is no position
\pi
in
\ell
such that

\ell|_{\pi}\notin\mathcal{V}

and the terms
\ell|_{\pi}
and
u
unify.

A sufficient criterion that a given redex has a unique result from a
rewrite step is given in the following.

Lemma 1
(
[
11
]
, Lemma 6.3.9)

If a TRS
\mathcal{R}
is non-overlapping,

s\mathrel{\smash{\rightarrow}_{\mathcal{R}}}t_{1}
and

s\mathrel{\smash{\rightarrow}_{\mathcal{R}}}t_{2}
with the redex of both rewrite steps at the same
position, then
t_{1}=t_{2}
.

With the above reasoning, this lemma directly gives us a sufficient criterion for confluence of

parallel-innermost
rewriting.

6
Related Work, Conclusion, and Future Work

Related work.

We provide pointers to work on automated analysis of (sequential) innermost runtime complexity of
TRSs at the start of
Sect. 4
.
We now focus on
automated techniques
for
complexity analysis of
parallel/concurrent computation.

Our
notion of
parallel complexity follows a large
tradition of static
cost analysis
, notably
for
concurrent programming. The two notable
works
[
4
,
5
]
address
async/finish programs where tasks are explicitly launched.
The authors propose several metrics such as the total number of spawned
tasks (in any execution of the program) and
a notion of
parallel complexity that is roughly the same as ours. They provide
static analyses that build on
techniques for estimating costs
of imperative languages with functions calls
[
3
]
, and/or
recurrence equations.
Recent approaches for the Pi
Calculus
[
12
,
13
]
compute
the

span
(our parallel complexity)
through a new typing system. Another type-based calculus for the same
purpose has been proposed with session types
[
18
]
.

For logic programs, which – like TRSs – express an implicit parallelism,
parallel complexity can be inferred
using recurrence solving
[
32
]
.

The tool
RAML

[
30
]

derives bounds on the worst-case evaluation cost of
first-order functional programs with list and pair
constructors as well as pattern matching and both sequential and
parallel composition
[
31
]
.
They use two typing derivations with specially annotated types, one for the
work

and one for the
depth
(parallel complexity).
Our
setting is more flexible wrt the shape of user-defined
data structures (we allow for tree constructors of arbitrary arity),
and our analysis
deals with both data structure and control in an integrated manner.

Conclusion and future work.

We have defined parallel-innermost
runtime complexity for TRSs and proposed an approach to its
automated analysis.
Our approach allows for finding both upper and lower bounds and builds
on existing techniques and tools. Our experiments on the TPDB indicate that our
approach is practically usable, and we are
confident that it captures the potential parallelism of programs with pattern matching.

Parallel rewriting is a topic of active research, e.g., for GPU-based
massively parallel rewrite engines
[
19
]
. Here our work could
be useful to determine which functions to evaluate on the GPU.
More generally, parallelising
compilers which need to determine which function calls should be
compiled into parallel code may benefit from an analysis of
parallel-innermost runtime complexity such as ours.

DTs have been used
[
44
]
in runtime complexity analysis of

Logically Constrained TRSs (LCTRSs)

[
33
]
, an extension of
TRSs by built-in data types from SMT theories
(integers, arrays, …).
This work could be extended to parallel rewriting.
Moreover, analysis of

derivational complexity

[
29
]

of parallel-innermost term rewriting can be a promising direction. Derivational complexity
considers the length of rewrite sequences from arbitrary start terms, e.g.,
\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{d}(\dots(\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{S}(\mathsf{Zero})))\dots))
in
Ex. 10
,
which can have longer derivations than basic terms of the same size.
Finally, towards
automated parallelisation
we aim to
infer
complexity bounds wrt term

height

(terms = trees!), as suggested in
[
6
]
.

, which carries over to
\ell
and
r
. And if
\ell\to r\in\mathcal{D}
, then
\ell
and
r
have type

\beta
. To see that
\ell
and
r
are well typed, consider that every
term
\ell
has the shape
f^{\sharp}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
, where
f^{\sharp}

has result type
\beta
and expects all arguments to have type
\alpha
,
while all
s_{i}
contain only subterms of type
\alpha
. Similarly,

r
has the shape
\mathsf{Com}_{k}(f^{\sharp}_{1}(t_{1,1},\ldots,t_{1,n_{1}}),\ldots,f^{\sharp}_{k}(t_{k,1},\ldots,t_{k,n_{k}}))
.

\mathsf{Com}_{k}
has result type
\beta
and expects all arguments to have
type
\beta
. This is the case since all
f_{i}^{\sharp}
have result type

\beta
. An all
f_{i}^{\sharp}
, which are right below the root, expect
their arguments
t_{i,j}
to have result type
\alpha
. This is the
case by construction.

In the following, we consider basic terms that
are well typed according to
\Theta
as start terms.
For our relative TRS
\mathcal{S}/((\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S})\cup\mathcal{R})
,
we have
the following
two kinds of well-typed basic terms that we need to consider:

Case 1:

t=f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
with
f\in\Sigma_{d}
and
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in\mathcal{T}(\Sigma_{c},\mathcal{V})
. This term and all its subterms are of type

\alpha
. Thus, this term can be rewritten by rules from
\mathcal{R}
, but not
by rules from
\mathcal{D}
(and
\mathcal{S}
), which all have type
\beta
. As
rewriting preserves the type of terms,
t
is a normal form wrt the relations

\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\Theta(\mathcal{S}/((\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S})\cup\mathcal{R}))}}
and

\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{S}/((\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S})\cup\mathcal{R})}}
, and

\Dh(t,\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{S}/((\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S})\cup\mathcal{R})}})=0
.
Since
\mathit{Cplx}_{\langle\mathcal{D},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{R}\rangle}(s)\geq 0

regardless of
s
, the claim follows for this case.

Case 2:

t=f^{\sharp}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
with
f\in\Sigma_{d}
and

t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in\mathcal{T}(\Sigma_{c},\mathcal{V})
.
If
t
is a normal form, there is no tree, and

\Dh(t,\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{S}/((\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S})\cup\mathcal{R})}})=0=\mathit{Cplx}_{\langle\mathcal{D},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{R}\rangle}(t)
.

Otherwise, we can convert any
{\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{S}\cup((\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S})\cup\mathcal{R})}}}={\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{R}}}}
rewrite
sequence to a
(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{R})
-chain tree
T
for
t
such that

\Dh(t,\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{S}/((\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S})\cup\mathcal{R})}})=|T|_{\mathcal{S}}
,
including any rewrite sequence that witnesses

\Dh(t,\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{S}/((\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S})\cup\mathcal{R})}})

in the following way:

As
t
is a basic term, the first step in the rewrite sequence
rewrites at the root of the term. Since only rules from

\mathcal{D}
are applicable to terms with
f^{\sharp}
at the root,
this step uses a DT
s^{\sharp}\to\mathsf{Com}_{k}(\ldots)
from
\mathcal{D}
.
With
\sigma
as the used matcher for the rewrite step,
we obtain the root node
(s^{\sharp}\to\mathsf{Com}_{k}(\ldots)\mid\sigma)
.

Now assume that we have a partially constructed chain tree
T^{\prime}
for the
rewrite sequence so far, which we have represented up until
the term
s
that resulted from a
\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{D}}}
step.

If there are no further
\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{D}}}
steps in the rewrite sequence,
we have completed our chain tree
T=T^{\prime}
since the remaining
\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}

suffix of the rewrite sequence does not contribute
to
\Dh(t,\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{S}/((\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S})\cup\mathcal{R})}})

(only steps using rules from
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
are counted).

Otherwise, our remaining rewrite sequence has the shape

s\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}^{*}}u\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{D}}}v\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{R}}^{m}}\dots

for some
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\omega\}

The step
u\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{D}}}v
takes place at position
\pi
,
using the DT
p^{\sharp}\to\mathsf{Com}_{l}(q^{\sharp}_{1},\ldots,q^{\sharp}_{l})\in\mathcal{D}
and the matcher
\mu
.

We reorder the rewrite steps

s\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}^{*}}u
by advancing all
\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}}
steps
at positions
\tau>\pi
, yielding

s\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R},>\pi}^{*}}s^{\prime}\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R},\not>\pi}^{*}}u
.
Here
\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R},>\pi}}

\mathcal{R}
at a position

, and

\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.0pt}{\scriptsize$\mathsf{i}\>$}}}{{\smash{\rightarrow}}}}_{\mathcal{R},\not>\pi}}

\mathcal{R}

Now we change our remaining rewrite sequence to
