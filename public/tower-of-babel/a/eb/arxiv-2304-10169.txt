The critical window in activated random walk on the complete graph
Antal A. Járai, Christian Mönch, Lorenzo Taggi

Abstract
We consider an elementary model for self-organised criticality, the activated random walk on the complete graph. We introduce a discrete time Markov chain as follows. At each time step we add an active particle at a random vertex and let the system stabilise following the activated random walk dynamics, obtaining a particle configuration with all sleeping particles. Particles visiting a boundary vertex are removed from the system. We characterise the support of the stationary distribution of this Markov chain, showing that, with high probability, the number of particles concentrates around the value ρ c ​ N + a ​ N ​ log ⁡ N \rho_{c}N+a\sqrt{N\log N} within a critical window of size at most o ⁡ ( N ​ log ⁡ N ) o(\sqrt{N\log N}) , where N N is the number of vertices. Due to the mean-field nature of the model, we are able to determine precisely the critical density ρ c = λ 1 + λ \rho_{c}=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda} , where λ \lambda is the sleeping rate, as well as the constant a = λ 1 + λ a=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{1+\lambda} characterising the lower order shift. Our approach utilises results about super-martingales associated with activated random walk that are of independent interest. Keywords and phrases. Self-organized criticality, Interacting particle systems, Abelian networks, Absorbing-state phase transition. MSC 2010 subject classifications. Primary 82C22; Secondary 60K35, 82C26.



1
Introduction and main results

Self-organized criticality (SOC) is a property of dynamical systems that have a critical point as an attractor. The concept was put forward by
Bak, Tang and Wiesenfeld
[
3
]
, and has been applied across different fields to explain a variety of physical phenomena.

Together with models for ‘sandpile’-type dynamics, like the Abelian sandpile, the stochastic sandpile model, and the Bak-Sneppen evolution model, the activated random walk (ARW) is one of the models which has been proposed to provide a mathematically rigorous description of SOC
[
10
,
22
]
. It is a conservative particle system with two types of particles, active (A) and sleeping (S). Active particles perform a continuous time simple random walk on a graph. In addition, to each active particle is associated an independent Poisson clock with rate
\lambda
. Whenever a particle’s clock rings, the particle turns into the S-state if it does not share its location with another active particle, in which case nothing happens. Each S-particle does not move and switches into the A-state whenever its location is visited by another A-particle. Consequently, any configuration with only S-type particles is an absorbing state for the induced Markovian dynamics.

As highlighted in the recent survey of Levine and Silvestri
[
19
]
,
in contrast with the ‘sandpile’-type dynamics of the original example proposed in
[
3
]
, to which
a lot of attention has been given
[
9
,
16
]
, the ARW model
appears to have better universality properties, and therefore can serve as a more suitable
model of SOC. Indeed, key features of ARW are believed to be independent of the underlying lattice
or details of initial conditions, one rigorous example being the remarkable result of
Rolla, Sidoravicius and Zindy
[
23
]
showing that stabilisability properties
only depend on the particle density.

Previous research on the activated random walk model mostly considers the dynamics on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
or, more generally, on infinite vertex-transitive graphs. In this case the model does not exhibit self-organised critical behaviour, instead it undergoes an ordinary
absorbing-state phase transition.
More precisely, at time zero, take the particle configuration to be distributed according to a product of Poisson distributions with parameter
\rho\in(0,\infty)
, the
particle density.
If the particle density is below a certain critical threshold,
\rho_{c}=\rho_{c}(\lambda)
, the system fixates, i.e. each vertex gets visited finitely many times almost surely. Instead, if the particle density is above the critical threshold, each vertex gets visited infinitely many times almost surely.

Significant efforts have been devoted to establishing various properties of the critical curve
\rho_{c}=\rho_{c}(\lambda)
on various graphs, such as non-triviality
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
,
continuity
[
29
]
, universality
[
23
]
, that it is strictly below one for each
\lambda\in(0,\infty)

[
1
,
4
,
13
,
14
,
21
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
, and to provide some asymptotic bounds when taking the limits
\lambda\rightarrow 0
or
\lambda\rightarrow\infty

[
1
,
2
]
.

In order to observe self-organised critical behaviour, however, one should consider the activated random walk model in a different setting, namely on a large finite subset of a graph under the addition of active particles. More precisely, active particles are added to a random vertex of the set and particles are absorbed at the boundary of this set during relaxation. Every time a new active particle is added, one waits until the system globally stabilises, i.e. all particles fall asleep or reach the boundary, where they are removed from the system. (This is called the
wired chain
in the terminology of
[
19
]
). If the average density of particles is too large, then the addition of the active particle causes an intense activity, and a huge number of particles is absorbed at the boundary. On the other hand, if the average density of particles is too small, then the addition of the active particle does not activate many other particles, thus mass tends to accumulate. With this carefully designed mechanism, the model is attracted to a stationary state with an average density that should approach
\rho_{c}
, as the finite volume exhausts the graph,
\rho_{c}
being the point at which the infinite system displays critical behaviour.

The present paper provides a rigorous description of this mechanism for the activated random walk model on the complete graph and provides a characterisation of the support of the invariant distribution of the corresponding Markovian dynamics. We show that the number of particles concentrates with high probability around a value
\rho_{c}N+a\sqrt{N\log N}
, staying within a small critical window of length at most
o(\sqrt{N\log N})
, where
N
is the number of vertices in the underlying graph. We also determine the critical value
\rho_{c}=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}
of the particle density on the complete graph, and the lower order shift
a=\sqrt{\lambda}/(1+\lambda)
. As far as we know, together with the case of totally asymmetric jumps on
\mathbb{Z}

[
7
, Theorem 1]
, this is the only case in which the value of the critical density for the activated random walk model is known
exactly
. In addition, the presence of the lower order shift, that we also calculate, is a feature that should be present for ARW on finite subsets of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as well. The shift plays an important role in the ARW dynamics, and we believe that the coarse-graining argument we develop in this paper to study it gives useful insight into a potential spatial analogue in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, at least at a heuristic level. In sufficiently high dimensions one can expect
\rho_{c}
to be a perturbation of
\lambda/(1+\lambda)
and the dynamics to behave similarly to that on the complete graph. We note that an analogous shift in density for the Abelian sandpile on the complete graph and on finite subsets of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is also present
[
12
,
15
]
. Indeed, an infinitesimal super-criticality appears to be a general feature of SOC, see the detailed study of the forest-fire model on the complete graph by Ráth and Tóth
[
20
]
.

Let us now define the ARW Markov chain formally. We use
\mathcal{G}_{N+1}=(V_{N+1},E_{N+1})
to denote the complete graph with
N+1
vertices, with a distinguished vertex
\partial V_{N+1}\in V_{N+1}
playing the role of the
boundary
. We further write
\mathring{\mathcal{G}}_{N+1}=(\mathring{V}_{N+1},\mathring{E}_{N+1})
for the sub-graph of
\mathcal{G}_{N+1}
induced by the non-boundary vertices, which has
N
vertices. The dynamics take place in
\mathcal{G}_{N+1}=(V_{N+1},E_{N+1})
. A particle configuration can be represented as
\xi\in\Sigma:=\{0,s\}^{{\mathring{V}_{N+1}}}
, where
\xi(x)=s
denotes the presence of a sleeping particle at
x
,
while
\xi(x)=0
means that the vertex
x
is empty.

We initiate the system in an arbitrary configuration of only sleeping particles,
\xi_{0}\in\Sigma
. At each time step
n\geq 1
, we add an active particle to a vertex uniformly chosen in
\mathring{V}_{N+1}

(if this vertex already hosts a sleeping particle, the latter is activated) and then let the system evolve according to the continuous time activated random walk dynamics defined above. Whenever an active particle jumps to the boundary vertex, it instantaneously disappears from the system. After an almost surely finite time we obtain a stable configuration, i.e. all particles in
\mathring{V}_{N+1}
are in the S-state
and some particles may possibly have left the set
\mathring{V}_{N+1}
and have disappeared from the system by jumping to the boundary vertex. We identify such a stable particle configuration with an element
\xi_{n}\in\Sigma.

The sequence of random variables
\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots
thereby defined is a discrete time Markov chain on the finite configuration space
\Sigma
.
It is straightforward to see, that the chain is irreducible and aperiodic and thus converges to a stationary distribution,
\mu=\mu^{(N)}
,
which does not depend on the initial particle configuration.

Our main results, Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
below, give a detailed characterisation of the support of the stationary distribution
\mu
. Note that although Theorem
1.2
supersedes Theorem
1.1
, we prefer to state both, for two reasons. On the one hand, Theorem
1.1
is easier to prove, and on the other hand, the upper bound on the support provided by Theorem
1.1
is used as input in the proof of the more precise bounds of Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 1.1

(Law of large numbers)
.

There exists some sequence
(\alpha_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
depending only on
\lambda
and satisfying
\alpha_{N}=O(\sqrt{N\log N})
, such that,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mu^{(N)}\Big[\rho_{c}N-\alpha_{N},\rho_{c}N+\alpha_{N}\Big]=1,

where

\rho_{c}=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}.

In other words, independently of the initial number of particles
at time zero (which may be much larger or smaller than
\rho_{c}N
),
the system converges to a critical state in which the number of particles
concentrates

around the value

\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}N

staying within a window of size at most
O(\sqrt{N\log N})
, and thus much smaller than the number of particles.

Our second theorem characterises the lower order shift in the number of sleeping particles more precisely and in particular shows that, at stationarity, the particle density is slightly
above

\rho_{c}
.

Theorem 1.2

(Lower order shift)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mu^{(N)}\Big[\rho_{c}N+(a-\varepsilon)\sqrt{N\log N},\rho_{c}N+(a+\varepsilon)\sqrt{N\log N}\Big]=1,

where

a=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{1+\lambda}.

The scaling window constant
\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{1+\lambda}
has in fact an interpretation in terms of moderate deviations of an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process that appears as scaling limit of appropriately recentered particle counts in our model. We provide the details of this connection in Section
6
.

We would like to mention that dynamics similar to the ones we study in the proofs of Theorem
1.1
and
1.2
have recently been considered in
[
6
,
18
]
in order to provide bounds on the mixing time of the system.

2
The induced chain of particle counts

In order to characterise the stationary distribution
\mu=\mu^{(N)}
of the Markov chain
\xi=(\xi_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, we first introduce a new ‘microscopic’ chain
(\eta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, which has the same stationary distribution as
\xi
. We then argue that
\eta
can be reduced to a simple bivariate process that keeps only track of particle counts but not of their locations.

The Markov chain
(\eta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is defined as follows: The dynamics take place on
\mathcal{G}_{N+1}
. Particles are either
asleep (S-particles)
or
active (A-particles)
. The state of the system at time
t
,
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, is denoted by
\smash{\eta_{t}\in\{0,s,1\}^{\mathring{V}_{N+1}}}
, where
\eta_{t}(x)=1

(resp.
\eta_{t}(x)=s
) if there is an active (resp. sleeping) particle at vertex
x
, while
\eta_{t}(x)=0
if there is no particle at
x
. Note that there is at most one particle per vertex, which might be an S-particle or an A-particle. The single parameter that moderates the active-sleep-transition is the
sleeping rate

\lambda>0
. The initial configuration is given by
\eta_{0}=(1,\dots,1)
, i.e. there is an active particle at each vertex.

Update dynamics for
\eta
.
To update the system from
\eta_{t}
to
\eta_{t+1}
we use the following rules: If the configuration
\eta_{t}
contains no A-particle, then
\eta_{t+1}=\eta_{t}
and we say that
stabilisation
has occurred. If there is at least one A-particle, then we choose a site
x
occupied by an A-particle uniformly at random. With probability
\smash{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}
, the particle falls immediately asleep, we set
\eta_{t+1}(x)=s
and
\eta_{t+1}(y)=\eta_{t}(y),y\neq x
.
With probability
\smash{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}
, the chosen particle moves according to a simple random walk on
\mathcal{G}_{N+1}
until it hits an unoccupied vertex or the boundary.
If the boundary is hit, the particle is removed from the system.
If an unoccupied site
y
is hit, then we set
\eta_{t+1}(y)=1
, i.e. the particle remains there. In either case, all occupied sites that were visited by the moving particle and contain an S-particle change their status from
s
to
1
, i.e. the sleeping particles on the path of the chosen active particle are woken up to obtain
\eta_{t+1}
.

Reduction to particle counts.
We now associate to each realisation of the interacting particle system two variables which count the total number of particles,
X_{t}
, and the total number of active particles
Y_{t}
. More precisely, these variables are defined as follows,

X_{t}:=\sum\limits_{x\in\mathring{V}_{N+1}}\mathbf{1}\{\eta_{t}(x)\neq 0\},\quad Y_{t}:=\sum\limits_{x\in\mathring{V}_{N+1}}\mathbf{1}\{\eta_{t}(x)=1\},\quad t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Thus, the total number of S-particles at time
t
is
X_{t}-Y_{t}
and the total number of empty non-boundary vertices is
N-X_{t}
.
Note that due to the complete symmetry of our model,
(X,Y)
encodes the behaviour of
\eta
up to a trivial labelling operation: if
\eta^{\prime}_{t}
is obtained from
(X_{t},Y_{t})
by uniformly assigning to each particle a site (without replacement), then
\smash{\eta^{\prime}_{t}\overset{d}{=}\eta_{t}}
. All our results about
\mu
are obtained in the following sections by carefully analysing the behaviour of
(X,Y)
. The crucial connection between
(X,Y)
and the stationary distribution of
\xi
is formalised in Lemma
2.1
below. Namely, if we start with a full system of
N
active particles and stabilise, then the stabilised state is stationary. Here and throughout the remainder of the article, we denote by
P_{N}
and
E_{N}
the law and expectation, respectively, of the Markov chain
(X,Y)
on
\mathcal{G}_{N+1}
and set
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma((X_{s},Y_{s}),0\leq s\leq t)
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The stationary distribution
\mu=(\mu_{k}^{(N)})_{0\leq k\leq N}
of
(\xi_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
has the representation

\mu_{k}=P_{N}(X_{T^{+}}=k|(X_{0},Y_{0})=(N,N)),\quad 0\leq k\leq N,

where

T^{+}=\inf\{t\geq 0:\,Y_{t}=0\}.

Proof.

Invariance of
\mu
follows from the Abelian property. Consider the configuration in which each vertex in
\mathring{V}_{N+1}
contains an A-particle and add an extra particle at a uniformly chosen vertex.
Stabilise in two different ways: first, we can start with stabilising only
the extra particle, which will eventually leave the system, as all
vertices are occupied by an active particle. Subsequently, we stabilise the
N
active sites,
which results in the distribution
\mu
. Second, disregard the extra particle,
and stabilize the rest, and subsequently stabilise with the extra
particle present. The resulting distribution corresponds to the distribution
\mu
evolved by one step of
(\xi_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. Hence
\mu
is invariant for the
one step evolution of
(\xi_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
from deviation bounds

Unless stated otherwise, we assume that
P_{N}(X_{0}=N,Y_{0}=N)=1
from now on. Our next result shows, that stabilisation occurs after at most
O(N^{2})
steps with very high probability. For any
\rho\in(0,1)
define the stopping time

\tau_{\rho}=\inf\{t\geq 0:\,X_{t}\leq\rho N\}.

Let further
(\varepsilon_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
denote a fixed sequence satisfying

\sqrt{\frac{{(\log N)_{+}}}{{N}}}\leq\varepsilon_{N}\leq\min\{1-\rho_{c},1/100\},\quad N\in\mathbb{N}.

Proposition 3.1

(Upper bound on stabilisation time)
.

Fix
\delta\in(1-\rho_{c},1)
. There exist constants
c_{1},C_{1}
, depending only on
\delta
and
\lambda
, such that

P_{N}(T^{+}\wedge\tau_{\rho_{c}-\varepsilon_{N}}>\delta(1+\lambda)N^{2})\leq C_{1}\textup{e}^{-c_{1}N}.

Proof.

Suppose that
X_{t}>(\rho_{c}-\varepsilon_{N})N
and
Y_{t}\geq 1
. The active particle that is
selected for a potential move can leave the system on its first steps with
probability
\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{1}{N}
, hence we have

P_{N}(X_{t+1}=X_{t}-1\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t})\geq\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{1}{N}

as long as
t<T^{+}\wedge\tau_{\rho_{c}-\varepsilon_{N}}
. Therefore, the time it takes for either

Y_{t}
to reach
0
, or
X_{t}
to reach
(\rho_{c}-\varepsilon_{N})N
, is
stochastically smaller than the time it takes to accumulate

(1-(\rho_{c}-\varepsilon_{N}))N
successes in a sequence of independent
Bernoulli trials with probability of success

\smash{p=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{1}{N}}
. Since

1-(\rho_{c}-\varepsilon_{N})=1-\rho_{c}+\varepsilon_{N}<\frac{\delta+1-\rho_{c}}{2}<\delta,

it follows that the probability that more than

\delta(1+\lambda)N^{2}
trials are needed decays exponentially in
N
, and the statement follows.
∎

We measure the deviation of
(X_{t},Y_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
from the straight line
(x-y)/(N-y)=\rho_{c}
through the quantity

S_{t}=Y_{t}-\ell(X_{t}),\quad t\geq 0,

with
\ell(x)=\ell_{N}(x)=(1+\lambda)x-\lambda N
. To obtain tail bounds for
\mu
, we control deviations of
(S_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
using the next two results.

Proposition 3.2

(Lower deviations of
(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
)
.

Fix
\delta\in(1-\rho_{c},1)
. There exist
c_{2}=c_{2}(\delta,\lambda)>0
and
C_{2}=C_{2}(\delta,\lambda)<\infty
such that

P_{N}(S_{t}\geq-2\varepsilon_{N}N\text{ for all }0\leq t\leq\delta(1+\lambda)N^{2})\geq 1-C_{2}\,N^{4}e^{-c_{2}\varepsilon_{N}^{2}N}.

Proposition 3.3

(Upper deviations of
(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
)
.

Fix
\delta\in(1-\rho_{c},1)
. There exist
c_{3}=c_{3}(\delta,\lambda)>0
and
C_{3}=C_{3}(\delta,\lambda)<\infty
such that

P_{N}(S_{t}\leq 2\varepsilon_{N}N\text{ for all }\tau_{\rho_{c}-\varepsilon_{N}/(1+\lambda)}\leq t\leq\delta(1+\lambda)N^{2})\geq 1-C_{3}\,N^{4}e^{-c_{2}\varepsilon_{N}^{2}N}.

We discuss the derivation of Propositions
3.2
in Section
5
. The proof of Proposition
3.3
is very similar and superseded by the results of Section
7
. We state Proposition
3.3
here to allow for a short proof of Theorem
1.1
, a self-contained proof of the latter result is available in an earlier version of the present article It is straightforward to deduce Theorem
1.1
from the bounds given in Propositions
3.1
–
3.3
:

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Fix
\delta\in(1-\rho_{c},1)
. We may apply Proposition
3.1
to obtain stabilisation before time
\delta(1+\lambda)N^{2}
with probability tending to
1
. The upper tail bound now follows immediately from the definition of
(S_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
and Proposition
3.2
, if we choose
\varepsilon_{N}
proportional to
C\sqrt{\log N/N}
for sufficiently large
C
. The lower tail bound follows similarly from Proposition
3.3
upon observing that
S_{T^{+}}>2\varepsilon_{N}N
can only occur if
T^{+}>\tau_{\rho_{c}-\varepsilon_{N}}
.
∎

4
Transition probabilities for the particle count chain

Our main technical tool for the remainder of the paper are the following formulae for the transition probabilities of
(X,Y)
. For any (random or deterministic) sequence
(a_{n},n\in\mathbb{Z})
we use the notation

\Delta a_{n}=a_{n+1}-a_{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},

for the forward difference operator.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
0\leq y\leq x\leq N
, we have that

\displaystyle\Pi_{k}^{(0)}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=P_{N}\Big(\Delta Y_{t}\geq k,\,\Delta X_{t}=0\,\big|\,X_{t}=x,Y_{t}=y\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\,\frac{N+1}{N}\,\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\Big(\frac{x-y-i}{N+2-(y+i)}\Big)\,\frac{N+1-x}{N+2-x},

where
1\leq k\leq x-y
, as well as

\begin{split}\Pi_{0}^{(0)}(x,y)&:=P_{N}\Big(\Delta Y_{t}\geq 0,\,\Delta X_{t}=0\,\big|\,X_{t}=x,Y_{t}=y\Big)\\
&=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\,\frac{N+1}{N}\,\frac{N+1-x}{N+2-x}-\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{1}{N},\end{split}

as well as

\displaystyle\Pi_{k}^{(-1)}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=P_{N}\Big(\Delta Y_{t}\geq k-1,\,\Delta X_{t}=-1\,\big|\,X_{t}=x,Y_{t}=y\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\,\frac{N+1}{N}\,\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\Big({\frac{x-y-i}{N+2-(y+i)}}\Big)\,{\frac{1}{N+2-x}},

where
0\leq k\leq x-y
.

Proof.

We call a vertex hosting one (or more during updates) active particle an ‘A-vertex’, a vertex hosting an S-particle an ‘S-vertex’, and a vertex hosting at least one particle a ‘filled vertex’. The ‘sleeping set’ is the set of S-vertices in
\mathring{V}_{N+1}
(at a given time
t
).

Deriviation of the formula for
\Pi_{k}^{(0)}(x,y)
.

An active particle is drawn uniformly at random. We move the particle until it either reaches one empty vertex or it reaches the boundary
\partial V_{N+1}
. The event defining
\Pi_{k}^{0}(x,y)
occurs if and only if the A-particle visits
k
distinct sites of the sleeping set at least once and, after that, reaches an empty site rather than reaching the boundary. We now compute the probability of this event.

(1)

Probability that the moving particle hits one vertex of the sleeping set.
First, the probability that the A-particle reaches one vertex before reaching the boundary or an empty vertex is

\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\,\,\,\,\frac{X_{t}-Y_{t}}{N+2-Y_{t}}\frac{N+1}{N}.

(4.1)

This expression is the sum of two terms: The first term is the probability that the moving particle jumps immediately to the sleeping set,

\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\,\,\frac{X_{t}-Y_{t}}{N},

(4.2)

the second term is the probability that it first jumps to one or more A-vertices and, after that, it jumps to the sleeping set rather than reaching the boundary or an empty vertex

\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\,\,\frac{Y_{t}-1}{N}\,\,\frac{X_{t}-Y_{t}}{N+1-(Y_{t}-1)}=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\,\,\frac{Y_{t}-1}{N}\,\,\frac{X_{t}-Y_{t}}{N+2-Y_{t}}.

(4.3)

Here, the product of the first two factors is the probability that at the first step the particle jumps to a vertex hosting an A-particle and the third factor is the probability that, after having jumped over an arbitrary number of vertices hosting an A-particle, it jumps to the sleeping set rather than reaching the boundary or an empty site.
The sum of (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) gives (
4.1
).

(2)

Probability that moving A-particle hits a second vertex of the sleeping set.
At this point the A-particle is on a vertex which was hosting an S-particle in
\eta_{t}
and which now hosts two active particles, thus
Y_{t}
vertices host at least one active particle (one of them hosts two active particles) and
X_{t}-Y_{t}-1
vertices host one sleeping particle. The probability that the A-particle will leave the set of vertices hosting one active particle by jumping to a vertex hosting one sleeping particle rather than reaching the boundary or an empty site is

\frac{X_{t}-Y_{t}-1}{N+1-Y_{t}},

(4.4)

where the numerator is the current number of vertices hosting a sleeping particle and the denominator is the number of vertices which host one sleeping particle, are empty or the boundary vertex.

(3)

Probability that the A-particle hits a third vertex of the sleeping set.
Now, the active particle
is on a vertex which was hosting an S-particle in
\eta_{t}
and which now hosts two active particles, thus
Y_{t}+1
vertices host at least one active particle (one of them hosts two active particles) and
X_{t}-Y_{t}-2
vertices host one sleeping particle. Similarly to (
4.4
), the
probability that the A-particle will leave the set of vertices hosting one active particle by jumping to a vertex hosting one sleeping particle rather than reaching the boundary or an empty vertex is,

\frac{X_{t}-Y_{t}-2}{N+1-(Y_{t}+1)}.

(4.5)

(4)

Iteration.
Iteratively, we obtain that

P_{N}\Big(Y_{t+1}>Y_{t}+k,\,\,X_{t+1}=X_{t}\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\Big)\\
=\,\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\,\,\frac{N+1}{N}\,\,\frac{X_{t}-Y_{t}}{N+2-Y_{y}}\,\,\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Big(\frac{X_{t}-Y_{t}-i}{N+2-(Y_{t}+i)}\Big)\,\,\frac{N+1-X_{t}}{N+2-X_{t}},

(4.6)

where the first three factors are given by (
4.1
), the product of
k
terms corresponding to the third factor is obtained iterating (
4.4
) and (
4.5
)
k
times, and the last factor is the probability that the last jump is to an empty vertex rather than the boundary. This concludes the calculation of
\Pi_{k}^{(0)}(x,y)
.

Deriviation of the formula for
\Pi_{k}^{(-1)}(x,y)
.

The event defining
\Pi_{k}^{(0)}(x,y)
occurs if and only if the same sequence of events as in the previous consideration occurs, with the difference that the last jump is to
\partial V_{N+1}
rather than to an empty vertex. We immediately obtain the stated formula for
\Pi_{k}^{(-1)}(x,y)
.
∎

5
Drift bounds: upper deviations of the total particle count

We now examine the evolution of
(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with the goal to prove Proposition
3.2
. We use the notation

\pi^{(j)}_{k}(x,y)=-\Delta\Pi_{k-1}^{(j)}(x,y),\quad-1\leq k\leq x-y,j\in\{-1,0\},

for the increment distribution of
(X,Y)
in the proofs of this section. Note that

\pi^{(0)}_{-1}(x,y)=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda},\quad\text{ whenever }y>x.

Our goal is to show, that if
S_{t}
is small, then it has a considerable drift upwards in the next step, resulting in reduction of the number of S-particles with high probability. Define the event

L^{(N)}=\{-2\varepsilon_{N}N\leq S_{t}\leq-\varepsilon_{N}N\}.

The following lemma provides bounds on the product terms in Lemma
4.1
on the event
L^{(N)}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
N
is sufficiently large, then we have for every
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
on the event
L^{(N)}

\frac{X_{t}-Y_{t}-i}{N+2-Y_{t}-i}\geq\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+\frac{1}{4(1+\lambda)}\varepsilon_{N},\quad\text{ for }0\leq i\leq\varepsilon_{N}N/2.

(5.1)

Proof.

Assume
-2\varepsilon_{N}N\leq S_{t}\leq-\varepsilon_{N}N
.
Then from the definition of
S_{t}
we have

-\varepsilon_{N}N\geq S_{t}=(1+\lambda)(Y_{t}-X_{t})+\lambda(N-Y_{t})\geq(1+\lambda)(Y_{t}-X_{t})+\lambda(X_{t}-Y_{t})=Y_{t}-X_{t}.

(5.2)

Hence

X_{t}-Y_{t}\geq\varepsilon_{N}N>\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{N}N.

(5.3)

The same inequalities also imply that

(1+\lambda)(X_{t}-Y_{t})\geq\lambda(N-Y_{t})+\varepsilon_{N}N.

(5.4)

From this we obtain

(1+\lambda)(X_{t}-Y_{t}-i)\geq\lambda(N-Y_{t}-i)-i+\varepsilon_{N}N=\lambda(N+2-Y_{t}-i)-i-2\lambda+\varepsilon_{N}N.

Hence

\begin{split}\frac{X_{t}-Y_{t}-i}{N+2-Y_{t}-i}&\geq\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\,\frac{\varepsilon_{N}N-i-2\lambda}{N+2-Y_{t}-i}.\end{split}

Using now that

\varepsilon_{N}N-2\lambda<\varepsilon_{N}N\leq X_{t}-Y_{t}\leq N-Y_{t}<N+2-Y_{t},

the restriction
0\leq i\leq\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{N}N
allows us to replace
i
by its largest possible value
to give the inequality:

\begin{split}\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{\varepsilon_{N}N-i-2\lambda}{N+2-Y_{t}-i}&\geq\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\,\frac{\varepsilon_{N}N-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{N}N-2\lambda}{N+2-Y_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{N}N}=\frac{1}{2(1+\lambda)}\varepsilon_{N}\,\frac{1-\frac{4\lambda}{\varepsilon_{N}N}}{1+\frac{2}{N}-\frac{Y_{t}}{N}-\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{2}}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2(1+\lambda)}\varepsilon_{N}\,\frac{1-\frac{4\lambda}{\varepsilon_{N}N}}{1+\frac{2}{N}}\geq\frac{1}{4(1+\lambda)}\varepsilon_{N},\end{split}

when
N
is sufficiently large. Therefore (
5.1
) is proved.
∎

Lemma
5.1
enables us to provide a bound on the instantaneous drift of
S
.

Lemma 5.2
.

There exist some constant
h>0
such that for every
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

on the event
L^{(N)}
we have

E_{N}\big[\exp\big(-h\varepsilon_{N}\Delta S_{t}\big)\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\big]\leq 1

for all sufficiently large
N
.

Proof.

Rewrite the statement in the form

1-E_{N}\Big[e^{-h_{N}(S_{t+1}-S_{t})}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\Big]\geq 0,

where
h_{N}=h\varepsilon_{N}
.
Then the left hand side equals

\begin{split}\sum_{k=0}^{X_{t}-Y_{t}}\left(1-e^{-h_{N}(\lambda+k)}\right)\pi_{k}^{(-1)}(X_{t},Y_{t})+\sum_{k=0}^{X_{t}-Y_{t}}\left(1-e^{-h_{N}k}\right)\pi_{k}^{(0)}(X_{t},Y_{t})+\left(1-e^{h_{N}}\right)\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda},\end{split}

(5.5)

so our aim is to show that this expression is non-negative. The last term we write as

\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{N+1}{N}\Big\{-\lambda(e^{h_{N}}-1)\frac{N}{N+1}\Big\}.

(5.6)

Writing

1-e^{-h_{N}k}=(1-e^{-h_{N}})+(e^{-h_{N}}-e^{-2h_{N}})+\dots+(e^{-h_{N}(k-1)}-e^{-h_{N}k}),

the second sum in (
5.5
) can be written

\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{X_{t}-Y_{t}}e^{-(k-1)h_{N}}(1-e^{-h_{N}})\Pi_{k}^{(0)}(X_{t},Y_{t})=(e^{h_{N}}-1)\sum_{k=1}^{X_{t}-Y_{t}}e^{-kh_{N}}\Pi_{k}^{(0)}(X_{t},Y_{t}).\end{split}

(5.7)

Similarly, writing

\begin{split}1-e^{-h_{N}(\lambda+k)}&=(1-e^{-h_{N}\lambda})+(e^{-h_{N}\lambda}-e^{-h_{N}(\lambda+k)})\\
&=(1-e^{-h_{N}\lambda})+e^{-h_{N}\lambda}\,\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\left(e^{-h_{N}(\ell-1)}-e^{-h_{N}\ell}\right),\end{split}

the first sum in (
5.5
) is

\begin{split}&(1-e^{-h_{N}\lambda})\Pi^{(-1)}_{0}(X_{t},Y_{t})+\sum_{k=1}^{X_{t}-Y_{t}}e^{-(k-1)h_{N}}(1-e^{-h_{N}})e^{-h_{N}\lambda}\Pi_{k}^{(-1)}(X_{t},Y_{t})\\
&\quad=(1-e^{-h_{N}\lambda})\Pi^{(-1)}_{0}(X_{t},Y_{t})+e^{-h_{N}\lambda}(e^{h_{N}}-1)\sum_{k=1}^{X_{t}-Y_{t}}e^{-kh_{N}}\Pi_{k}^{(-1)}(X_{t},Y_{t}).\end{split}

(5.8)

Adding the two expressions in (
5.8
) and (
5.7
), and abbreviating

x:=X_{t}
and
y:=Y_{t}
, we get the expression

\begin{split}&(1-e^{-h_{N}\lambda})\Pi^{(-1)}_{0}(x,y)+(e^{h_{N}}-1)\sum_{k=1}^{x-y}e^{-kh_{N}}\left[\Pi_{k}^{(0)}(x,y)+e^{-h_{N}\lambda}\Pi_{k}^{(-1)}(x,y)\right]\\
&\quad=(1-e^{-h_{N}\lambda})\Pi^{(-1)}_{0}(x,y)+(e^{h_{N}}-1)\sum_{k=1}^{x-y}e^{-kh_{N}}\left[\Pi_{k}^{(0)}(x,y)+\Pi_{k}^{(-1)}(x,y)\right]\\
&\quad\qquad-(e^{h_{N}}-1)(1-e^{-h_{N}\lambda})\sum_{k=1}^{x-y}e^{-kh_{N}}\Pi_{k}^{(-1)}(x,y).\end{split}

Using Lemma
4.1
,
this equals

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{N+1}{N}\Big\{(1-e^{-h_{N}\lambda})\frac{1}{N+2-x}+(e^{h_{N}}-1)\sum_{k=1}^{x-y}e^{-kh_{N}}\Big[\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{x-y-i}{N+2-y-i}\Big]\\
&\quad\qquad-(e^{h_{N}}-1)(1-e^{-h_{N}\lambda})\frac{1}{N+2-x}\sum_{k=1}^{x-y}e^{-kh_{N}}\Big[\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{x-y-i}{N+2-y-i}\Big]\Big\}.\end{split}

(5.9)

The common positive factor
(1+\lambda)^{-1}\frac{N+1}{N}
present in (
5.6
) and
(
5.9
) we will be able to ignore from now on.

We will show that the sum of the first and third terms inside the braces is positive,
and that the sum of the second term inside the braces and the term in (
5.6
)
is also positive. This implies the claim immediately.

We start with analysing the second term inside the braces. Recalling
h_{N}=h\varepsilon_{N}
,
let us now require that
h>0
be small enough such that

e^{-h\varepsilon_{N}}(\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{4(1+\lambda)})\geq\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{8(1+\lambda)},

and note that such a choice of such
h>0
is possible independently of
N
.
Then from the inequality (
5.1
) of Lemma
5.1
,
for
1\leq k\leq\varepsilon_{N}N/2
we have

\begin{split}e^{-kh_{N}}\Big[\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{x-y-i}{N+2-y-i}\Big]&\geq\Big(e^{-h_{N}}\Big[\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{4(1+\lambda)}\Big]\Big)^{k}\geq\Big(\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{8(1+\lambda)}\Big)^{k}.\end{split}

Summing over
k
and recalling (
5.3
) gives the lower bound

\displaystyle(e^{h_{N}}-1)\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\varepsilon_{N}N/2\rfloor}\Big(\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{8(1+\lambda)}\Big)^{k}

(5.10)

\displaystyle\geq(e^{h_{N}}-1)\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{8(1+\lambda)}\Big)^{k}-\exp(-c\,\varepsilon_{N}N)\Big]

\displaystyle\geq(e^{h_{N}}-1)\Big[\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}\,\frac{1}{1-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}-\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{8(1+\lambda)}}-\exp(-c\,\varepsilon_{N}N)\Big]

\displaystyle\geq(e^{h_{N}}-1)\lambda\Big[\frac{1}{1-\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{8}}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\,\exp(-c\,\varepsilon_{N}N)\Big]

\displaystyle\geq(e^{h_{N}}-1)\lambda\Big[1+\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{8}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\,\exp(-c\,\varepsilon_{N}N)\Big]

\displaystyle\geq(e^{h_{N}}-1)\lambda\Big[1+\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{16}\Big],

where the last inequality follows for
N
sufficiently large. The sum of the last expression
and the expression inside the braces in (
5.6
) is at least

(e^{h_{N}}-1)\lambda\varepsilon_{N}/16>0
.

It is left to consider the first and third terms inside the braces in (
5.9
).
Regarding the third term in (
5.9
) we write

\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{x-y}e^{-kh_{N}}\Big[\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{x-y-i}{N+2-y-i}\Big]\leq\sum_{k=1}^{x-y}e^{-kh_{N}}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-kh_{N}}=\frac{e^{-h_{N}}}{1-e^{-h_{N}}}=\frac{1}{e^{h_{N}}-1}.\end{split}

This implies that the sum of the first and third term is non-negative.
∎

We are now ready to prove Proposition
3.2
.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

For
1\leq s<t
, we introduce the following event:

A_{s,t}=\left\{\parbox{256.0748pt}{$-\varepsilon_{N}N-1\leq S_{s}\leq-\varepsilon_{N}N$, and
for all $s<r<t$ we have $-2\varepsilon_{N}N\leq S_{r}\leq-\varepsilon_{N}N$
and $S_{t}<-2\varepsilon_{N}N$}\right\}.

Since the downward steps of
Y_{t}
are at most of size
1
, we have
that on the event in the statement of the proposition, there
must exist
1\leq s<t\leq\xi(1+\lambda)N^{2}
such that

A_{s,t}
occurs. This implies that

P_{N}\left[\text{$S_{t}<-2\varepsilon_{N}N$ for some
$0\leq t\leq\xi(1+\lambda)N^{2}$}\right]\leq\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq\xi(1+\lambda)N^{2}}P_{N}\left(A_{s,t}\right).

The number of terms is
O(N^{4})
, hence it is enough to upper bound
each summand by
\exp(-c\varepsilon_{N}^{2}N)
. Let us fix
h>0
satisfying the
conclusion of Lemma
5.2
. Using Markov’s inequality in the
second step, we have

\begin{split}P_{N}[A_{s,t}]&\leq P_{N}[S_{t}-S_{s}\leq-\varepsilon_{N}N+1,\,-2\varepsilon_{N}N\leq S_{r}\leq-\varepsilon_{N}N\text{ for all $s\leq r<t$}]\\
&=P_{N}\big[e^{-h_{N}(S_{t}-S_{s})}\geq e^{-h_{N}}\,e^{h_{N}\varepsilon_{N}N},\,-2\varepsilon_{N}N\leq S_{r}\leq-\varepsilon_{N}N\text{ for all $s\leq r<t$}\big]\\
&\leq C\,\exp(-h_{N}\varepsilon_{N}N)\,E_{N}\big[e^{-h_{N}(S_{t}-S_{s})}\,\mathbf{1}\{-2\varepsilon_{N}N\leq S_{r}\leq-\varepsilon_{N}N\text{ for all $s\leq r<t$}\}\big]\\
&=C\,\exp(-h_{N}\varepsilon_{N}N)\,E_{N}\Big[\prod_{r=s}^{t-1}\big(e^{-h_{N}(S_{r+1}-S_{r})}\,\mathbf{1}\{-2\varepsilon_{N}N\leq S_{r}\leq-\varepsilon_{N}N\}\big)\Big]\\
&=C\,\exp(-h_{N}\varepsilon_{N}N)\,E_{N}\Big[\prod_{r=s}^{t-1}E_{N}\big[e^{-h_{N}(S_{r+1}-S_{r})}\,\mathbf{1}\{-2\varepsilon_{N}N\leq S_{r}\leq-\varepsilon_{N}N\}\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}_{r}\big]\Big]\\
&\leq C\,\exp(-h_{N}\varepsilon_{N}N)\leq C\exp(-h\varepsilon_{N}^{2}N).\end{split}

This completes the proof.
∎

6
Heuristic derivation of the scaling window constant from moment bounds

6.1
Scaling limit heuristics

We now provide the idea behind the appearance of the scaling window constant

a={\sqrt{\lambda}}/(1+\lambda)
in Theorem
1.2
. The proof of Theorem
1.2
in Section
7

is formally independent of the discussion in this subsection but we believe that an illustration of the idea behind the proof benefits the reader.

Propositions
3.2
and
3.3
as well as the proofs in the previous section suggest that
(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is mean reverting. Indeed, under appropriate rescaling,
(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
converges
to an Orstein-Uhlenbeck process, that is, the process
R
solving the SDE

dR_{s}=-R_{s}ds+\sqrt{2}dB_{s},

where
(B_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{R}}
denotes standard Brownian motion. The idea is now, to extrapolate the validity of this scaling limit to mesoscopic time-scales on which
R
is likely to make a moderate deviation of size
\sqrt{\log N}
.

Recall that

S_{t}=Y_{t}-(1+\lambda)X_{t}+\lambda N=(Y_{t}-X_{t})+\lambda(N-X_{t}),\quad t\in\mathbb{N}.

(6.1)

We have the following estimates for the conditional expectation and variance of
(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in the critical region (recall that
\Delta S_{t}=S_{t+1}-S_{t}
):

Lemma 6.1
.

Assuming that

X_{t}=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}N+b(1+o(1))\sqrt{N\log N},

with
b>0
, we have

\begin{split}E_{N}[\Delta S_{t}\,|\,X_{t},Y_{t}]&=-\frac{S_{t}}{N}-\frac{2\lambda}{N}-\frac{S_{t}}{N}O\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\log N}}{N^{1/2}}\Big)+O\big(\sqrt{\log N}{N^{3/2}}\big)\\
&=-\frac{S_{t}}{N}(1\pm o(1))+O(N^{-1}),\end{split}

(6.2)

as
N\to\infty
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Under the assumption of Lemma
6.1
, we also have

\mathrm{Var}[\Delta S_{t}\,|\,X_{t},Y_{t}]=2\lambda+o(1),

(6.3)

as
N\to\infty
.

In view of (
6.1
) and the two previous Lemmas, consider now for some
t_{0}
such that
X_{t_{0}}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}N=\Theta(\sqrt{N\log N})
, the rescaling

R^{(N)}_{s}=\frac{S(\lfloor t_{0}+sN\rfloor)}{\sqrt{\lambda N}},\quad s\geq 0.

Applying Lemmas
6.1
and
6.2
, with
\Delta s=N^{-1}
we have

\begin{split}E_{N}[R^{(N)}_{s+\Delta s}-R^{(N)}_{s}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{s}]&=-R^{(N)}_{s}\Delta s+o(\Delta s);\\
\mathrm{Var}[R^{(N)}_{s+\Delta s}-R^{(N)}_{s}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{s}]&=2\Delta s+o(\Delta s).\end{split}

It can be proved using standard methods (see for example
[
11
, Section 8.7]
),
that as long as
s
is restricted to a finite interval

[0,K]
, the sequence of processes
\smash{(R^{(N)}_{s})_{s\in[0,K]}}
converges
as
N\to\infty
to the standard Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. We are only going to use this fact to lend support to
the following two observations (recall that
a=\sqrt{\lambda}/(1+\lambda)
):

(I)

Given
\varepsilon>0
and
b>a+\varepsilon
assume that

t_{0}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}N=b(1+o(1))\sqrt{N\log N},\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{t_{0}}=O(1).

Let

T=\inf\{t>t_{0}:Y_{t}=0\}.

Then

P_{N}[T>(b-a-\varepsilon)N\sqrt{N\log N}]=1-o(1),\quad\text{as $N\to\infty$.}

(6.4)

(II)

Given
\varepsilon>0
and
a-2\varepsilon\leq b^{\prime}\leq a-\varepsilon
, assume that

t^{\prime}_{0}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}N=b^{\prime}(1+o(1))\sqrt{N\log N},\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{t^{\prime}_{0}}=O(1).

Let

T^{\prime}=\inf\{t>t^{\prime}_{0}:Y_{t}=0\}.

Then

P_{N}[T^{\prime}>\varepsilon N\sqrt{N\log N}]=o(1),\quad\text{as $N\to\infty$.}

(6.5)

Argument for (I).

The event in (
6.4
) is implied by the event

\min\{S_{t}:t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}+(b-a-\varepsilon)N\sqrt{N\log N}\}>-M\sqrt{\lambda N},

(6.6)

where

M:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}(1+\lambda)(a+\varepsilon)\sqrt{\log N}=(1+\Theta(\varepsilon))\sqrt{\log N}.

In terms of the rescaled process, the event in (
6.6
) corresponds to

\min\{R^{(N)}_{s}:0\leq s\leq(b-a-\varepsilon)\sqrt{N\log N}\}>-M.

(6.7)

We let

T_{-M}:=\inf\{s\geq 0:R_{s}\leq-M\},

and replace, as a heuristic, (
6.7
) with the event

T_{-M}>(b-a-\varepsilon)\sqrt{N\log N}.

The Laplace transform of
T_{-M}
was computed by Darling and Siegert
[
8
]
(see also

[
5
]
). Putting

\hat{f}_{-M}(\nu)=E_{N}[\exp(-\nu T_{-M})],

they found that

\hat{f}_{-M}(\nu)=\frac{D_{-\nu}(0)}{\exp(M^{2}/4)D_{-\nu}(-M)},

(6.8)

where
D_{\nu}(\cdot)
is a parabolic cylinder function known as the Weber function
[
30
, Section 16.5]
.

Let us consider this Laplace transform with
\nu=((b-a-\varepsilon)\sqrt{N\log N})^{-1}
, which tends to

0
as
N\to\infty
. The value of
D_{-\nu}(0)
is known to be
[
30
, Section 16.5]

D_{-\nu}(0)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})2^{-\nu/2}}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\nu}{2})}=1+o(1).

The asymptotic expansion of
D
for fixed
\nu
and large arguments is also known, and to leading
order, as
M\to\infty
, the denominator in (
6.8
) is given by
[
30
, Section 16.52]
:

\begin{split}\exp(M^{2}/4)D_{-\nu}(-M)&\sim(-M)^{-\nu}(1+o(1))-\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\Gamma(\nu)}e^{-\nu\pi i}\exp(M^{2}/2)(-M)^{\nu-1}(1+o(1)).\end{split}

(6.9)

The first term is

\exp(\nu\pi i+\nu\log M)(1+o(1))=\exp(o(1))=1+o(1).

The second term is

\begin{split}&-\mathrm{const}\,\nu(1+o(1))e^{\pi i}\exp(\frac{M^{2}}{2}+\log M(\nu-1))(1+o(1))\\
&\quad=\mathrm{const}\exp(\log\nu+\frac{(1+\Theta(\varepsilon))^{2}\log N}{2}+o(\log\log N))\\
&\quad=\mathrm{const}\exp(-\frac{\log N}{2}+O(\log\log N)+\frac{(1+\Theta(\varepsilon))^{2}\log N}{2})=\Omega(\log N).\end{split}

It follows that
\hat{f}_{-M}(\nu)\to 0
, as
N\to\infty
. Hence

T_{-M}\gg\nu^{-1}
.

Argument for (II).

The event in (
6.5
) implies the event (allowing
Y_{t}
to become negative):

\min\{S_{t}:t^{\prime}_{0}\leq t\leq t^{\prime}_{0}+\varepsilon N\sqrt{N\log N}\}>-M^{\prime}\sqrt{\lambda N},

(6.10)

where

M^{\prime}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}(1+\lambda)(a-\varepsilon)\sqrt{\log N}=(1-\Theta(\varepsilon))\sqrt{\log N}.

In terms of the rescaled process, the event in (
6.10
) corresponds to

\min\{R^{(N)}_{s}:0\leq s\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{N\log N}\}>-M^{\prime}.

(6.11)

Again, as a heuristic, we replace the event in (
6.11
) with the event

T_{-M^{\prime}}>\varepsilon\sqrt{N\log N}.

We consider the Laplace transform with
\nu^{\prime}=(\varepsilon\sqrt{N\log N})^{-1}
that again goes to
0
, so the first term in asymptotics (
6.9
) is again
1+o(1)
.
This time the second term in (
6.9
) is negligible:

\begin{split}&-\mathrm{const}\nu^{\prime}(1+o(1))e^{\pi i}\exp(\frac{(M^{\prime})^{2}}{2}+\log M^{\prime}(\nu^{\prime}-1))(1+o(1))\\
&\quad=\mathrm{const}\exp(\log\nu^{\prime}+\frac{(1-\Theta(\varepsilon))^{2}\log N}{2}+o(\log\log N))\\
&\quad=\mathrm{const}\exp(-\frac{\log N}{2}+O(\log\log N)+\frac{(1-\Theta(\varepsilon))^{2}\log N}{2})\to 0.\end{split}

It follows that
\hat{f}_{-M^{\prime}}(\nu^{\prime})\to 1
, as
N\to\infty
. Hence

T_{-M^{\prime}}\ll(\nu^{\prime})^{-1}
.

6.2
Formal justification of conditional moment bounds

We now provide proofs for Lemmas
6.1
and
6.2
. We will need the following elementary identities:

Lemma 6.3
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
m>n
we have

\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{n(n-1)\dots(n-\ell+1)}{m(m-1)\dots(m-\ell+1)}=\frac{m+1}{m-n+1}-1=\frac{n}{m-n+1},

(6.12)

\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\ell\frac{n(n-1)\dots(n-\ell+1)}{m(m-1)\dots(m-\ell+1)}=\frac{n(m+1)}{(m-n+1)(m-n+2)},

(6.13)

and, provided that
e^{-\theta}n/m
is bounded away from
1
,

\begin{split}&\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}e^{-\ell\theta}\frac{n(n-1)\dots(n-\ell+1)}{m(m-1)\dots(m-\ell+1)}\\
&\quad=\frac{n}{m-n+1}-\theta\frac{n(m+1)}{(m-n+1)(m-n+2)}+O(|\theta|^{2}).\end{split}

(6.14)

Proof.

We begin by showing (
6.12
). Adding the
\ell=0
term (equal to
1
) to the summation, we have the sum

\begin{split}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\frac{n!}{(n-\ell)!}\frac{(m-\ell)!}{m!}&=\frac{1}{\binom{m}{n}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\binom{m-\ell}{n-\ell}\\
&=\frac{1}{\binom{m}{n}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\binom{m-n+\ell}{\ell}\\
&=\frac{\binom{m+1}{n}}{\binom{m}{n}}\\
&=\frac{m+1}{m-n+1}.\end{split}

Here the penultimate step uses a well-known identity for binomial coefficients that is easy to verify. Moving to the proof of (
6.13
), we obtain by similar manipulations

\begin{split}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\ell\frac{n!}{(n-\ell)!}\frac{(m-\ell)!}{m!}&=\frac{1}{\binom{m}{n}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\ell\binom{m-\ell}{n-\ell}\\
&=\frac{1}{\binom{m}{n}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}(n-\ell)\binom{m-n+\ell}{\ell}\\
&=\frac{n\binom{m+1}{n}}{\binom{m}{n}}-\frac{1}{\binom{m}{n}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\ell\binom{m-n+\ell}{\ell}\\
&=\frac{n(m+1)}{m-n+1}-\frac{1}{\binom{m}{n}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(m-n+1)\binom{m-n+\ell}{\ell-1}\\
&=\frac{n(m+1)}{m-n+1}-\frac{m-n+1}{\binom{m}{n}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\binom{m-n+1+\ell}{\ell}\\
&=\frac{n(m+1)}{m-n+1}-\frac{(m-n+1)\binom{m+1}{n-1}}{\binom{m}{n}}\\
&=\frac{n(m+1)}{m-n+1}-\frac{(m-n+1)(m+1)n}{(m-n+1)(m-n+2)}\\
&=\frac{n(m+1)}{(m-n+1)(m-n+2)}.\end{split}

Finally, the asymptotic formula (
6.14
) follows from (
6.12
) and (
6.13
) by Taylor expansion around
\theta=0
, followed by observing that the
second derivative can be bounded uniformly in terms of the sum

\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\ell^{2}e^{-\theta\ell}\frac{n!}{(n-\ell)!}\frac{(m-\ell)!}{m!}<\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\ell^{2}(e^{-\theta}n/m)^{\ell}.

The latter remains bounded under the condition on
e^{-\theta}n/m
.
∎

assuming Lemmas
7.4
(ii),
7.5
and Proposition
7.6
(ii).

We may assume without loss of generality that
\varepsilon<1
and that
a+1<A
.
Let

\bar{s}_{0}:=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,:\,X_{t}<\rho_{c}N+{(2A+1)}\sqrt{N\log N}\}.

If
\bar{s}_{0}=\infty
, then almost surely the process has
stabilized with more than
\rho_{c}N+{A}\sqrt{N\log N}
particles
and the
green
absorbing configuration does not contribute to the probability to be bounded.
Assume now that
\bar{s}_{0}<\infty
.
It follows from Proposition
3.2
and the choice of
A
in the proof of
Theorem
1.1
that with probability tending to
1
, we have

S_{\bar{s}_{0}}\geq-(1+\lambda)A\sqrt{N\log N}.

In the remainder of the proof we assume that this event occurs. Consequently,

Y_{\bar{s}_{0}}=S_{\bar{s}_{0}}+(1+\lambda)\hat{X}_{\bar{s}_{0}}\sqrt{N\log N}\geq(1+\lambda)A\sqrt{N\log N}>(1+\lambda)(a+\varepsilon)\sqrt{N\log N},

(7.7)

where
\hat{X}_{t}=\frac{X_{t}-\rho_{c}N}{\sqrt{N\log N}},t\in\mathbb{N}.

Let us define the stopping time

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}_{1}:=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:\,S_{t}\leq 4\lambda+2\}.

We deduce from (
7.7
) that taking

\bar{t_{2}}=\bar{s}_{0}^{\prime}:=\bar{s_{0}}+\bar{\sigma}_{1}(\bar{s}_{0})

the conditions of Proposition
7.6
(ii) are satisfied.

We now inductively define a sequence of stopping times

\bar{s}^{\prime}_{0},\dots,\bar{s}^{\prime}_{R}
, with a random

0\leq\bar{R}\leq N^{1/2}\log^{3}N
, and a notion of ‘success’/‘failure’, as follows.
The induction has been initiated with the definition of
\bar{s}^{\prime}_{0}
. Assuming
\bar{s}^{\prime}_{i}
has been
defined for some
i\geq 0
, we consider whether either

\sigma_{2}(\bar{s}^{\prime}_{i})\wedge\tilde{\tau}(\bar{s}^{\prime}_{i})>N\log^{3}N
or
\sigma_{2}(\bar{s}^{\prime}_{i})\wedge\tilde{\tau}(\bar{s}^{\prime}_{i})<N\log^{-2}N
occurs. If yes, we set
R=i
,
and declare that failure occurred.
Furthermore, on the event
\tau(\bar{s}^{\prime}_{i})<\sigma_{2}(\bar{s}^{\prime}_{i})
we set
R=i
and say that failure occurred. On the event
\tau(\bar{s}^{\prime}_{i})>\sigma_{2}(\bar{s}^{\prime}_{i})
,
we define
\bar{s}^{\prime}_{i+1}:=\bar{s}^{\prime}_{i}+\sigma_{2}(\bar{s}^{\prime}_{i})+\bar{\sigma}_{1}(\sigma_{2}(\bar{s}^{\prime}_{i}))
. If
\hat{X}_{\bar{s}_{i+1}}
reaches a value smaller than
a+\varepsilon
,
we stop the process with
R=i+1
and declare that success occurred. Otherwise, the bound of Lemma
7.5
can be applied at the
time
\bar{t}_{2}=\bar{s}^{\prime}_{i+1}
.

As in the proof of Theorem
7.1
, we use the weak law of large numbers to deduce that, for any function
f(N)\to\infty
, we have
that during
Nf(N)
steps the
X
-coordinate moves to the left by
(1\pm o(1))f(N)
with
probability going to
1
. It follows from this that if by step
I=N^{1/2}\log^{3}N
neither success nor failure occurred, then we would have

{X_{\bar{s}^{\prime}_{I}}}\leq\rho_{c}N+{(2A+1)}\sqrt{N\log N}-\frac{1+o(1)}{N}N^{1/2}\log^{3}N\frac{N}{\log^{2}N}\leq\rho_{c}N+(a+\varepsilon)\sqrt{N\log N},

a contradiction.
By Proposition
7.6
(ii) and Lemmas
7.4
and
7.5
, the probability that the process stops with a failure at some
0\leq R<N^{1/2}\log^{3}N
is
at most

N^{1/2}\log^{3}N\big[CN^{-1/2-\eta_{2}}+CN^{-c\log N}\big]=o(1).

On the event of success, we have that
(X,Y)
did not hit
\{(x,0),x\in\mathbb{N}\}
at a point
(x_{0},0)
with

x_{0}\geq\rho_{c}N+(a+\varepsilon)\sqrt{N\log N}
, so the theorem is proved.
∎

7.2
Hitting time and exit time estimates

In Section
7.2.1
we prove Lemmas
7.3
and
7.4
via general martingale arguments. Sections
7.2.2
and
7.2.3
provide auxiliary estimates.
Section
7.2.4
gives useful stochastic comparisons for the
Y
process. In Section

we use these to prove Lemma
7.5
.

7.2.1
Martingale arguments

Proof of Lemma
7.3
.

Let
\sigma_{1}
and
t_{1}
be defined as in the statement of the lemma.
Due to the Strong Markov Property, we may replace
(X_{0},Y_{0})
by the value of
(X_{t_{1}},Y_{t_{1}})
,
and replace
\sigma_{1}(t_{1})
by
\sigma_{1}
. Note that then necessarily
S_{0}>4\lambda+2
.
Define

U_{t}=\log S_{\sigma_{1}\wedge t},\quad t\geq 0,

and note that
U_{t}
is well-defined and positive for all
t\geq 0
, because on the event

S_{t}>4\lambda+2
we have
S_{t+1}>1
.

We show that
U_{t}
is a supermartingale, and give an upper bound on its drift.
On the event
\{\sigma_{1}>t\}
we have

\begin{split}E_{N}[U_{t+1}-U_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}]&=E_{N}\Big[\log\Big(\frac{S_{t+1}}{S_{t}}\Big)\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\Big]\\
&=E_{N}\Big[\log\Big(1+\frac{S_{t+1}-S_{t}}{S_{t}}\Big)\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\Big]\\
&\leq E_{N}\Big[\frac{S_{t+1}-S_{t}}{S_{t}}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\Big]\\
&=\frac{1}{S_{t}}E_{N}[S_{t+1}-S_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}].\end{split}

(7.8)

Recalling the result of Lemma
6.1
we have

\begin{split}E_{N}[S_{t+1}-S_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq-\frac{S_{t}}{N}+o(N^{-1}).\end{split}

When
S_{t}>4\lambda+2
, and
N
is sufficiently large, the right hand side of the last expression gives

\begin{split}E_{N}[S_{t+1}-S_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq-\frac{1}{2}\frac{S_{t}}{N}.\end{split}

Substituting this back into (
7.8
) we get

\begin{split}E_{N}[U_{t+1}-U_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq-\frac{1}{2N}.\end{split}

Define for each
t\geq 0

V_{t}=U_{t}+{\frac{\sigma_{1}\wedge t}{2N}},

which is a non-negative supermartingale. The Optional Stopping Theorem gives that

E_{N}[V_{\sigma_{1}\wedge{(4N\log N)}}]\leq E_{N}[V_{0}]=E_{N}[U_{0}]=E_{N}[\log(S_{0})]\leq\log N.

Since
U_{t}>0
for all
t\geq 0
, the left hand side of the previous expression satisfies

\begin{split}E_{N}[V_{{\sigma_{1}\wedge(4N\log N)}}]&=E_{N}[U_{\sigma_{1}\wedge(4N\log N)}]+\frac{1}{2N}E_{N}[\sigma_{1}\wedge(4N\log N)]\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2N}E_{N}[\sigma_{1}\wedge{(4N\log N)}].\end{split}

We conclude
E_{N}[\sigma_{1}\wedge{(4N\log N)}]\leq 2N\log N
,
and deduce using Markov’s inequality that

P_{N}[\sigma_{1}>4N\log N]\leq\frac{1}{2}.

To finish the proof, we iterate this bound
\frac{1}{4}\log^{2}N
times
using the Strong Markov Property to get

P_{N}[\sigma_{1}>N\log^{3}N]\leq\exp(-\log 2(1/4)\log^{2}N)=N^{-c\log N}.

∎

Proof of Lemma
7.4
.

We prove parts (i) and (ii) simultaneously, only assuming that
X_{t_{2}}=\rho_{c}N+\Theta(\sqrt{N\log N})
.

Recall the definitions of
t_{2}
,
\sigma_{2}
and
\tau
in the statement of the lemma. By the Strong Markov Property, we may replace
(X_{0},Y_{0})
by the value of
(X_{t_{2}},Y_{t_{2}})
, and replace
\sigma_{2}(t_{2})
and

\tau(t_{2})
in the statement by
\sigma_{2}
and
\tau
, respectively.

We define for each
t\geq 0
the process

V_{t}:=S_{t}^{2}-\lambda t.

Using the result of Lemma
6.2
, we see that
for large enough
N
we have

\begin{split}E_{N}[V_{t+1}-V_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}]&=-\lambda+E_{N}\big[(S_{t+1}-S_{t})^{2}\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\big]+2E_{N}[(S_{t+1}-S_{t})S_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}]\\
&=-\lambda+2\lambda+o(1)-\frac{S_{t}^{2}}{N}(1+o(1))+O(|S_{t}|/N)\\
&\geq 0,\end{split}

where the last inequality holds if
N
is sufficiently large, implying that
V
is a submartingale in this case.
It is easy to see that there is a constant
C_{1}>0
such that

S_{{\sigma_{2}\wedge\tau\wedge t}}^{2}\leq C_{1}N\log N

for all
t\geq 0
and
N\geq 1
. It follows from the Optional Stopping Theorem that

\begin{split}0&\leq S_{0}^{2}=E_{N}[V_{0}]\leq E_{N}[V_{\sigma_{2}\wedge\tau\wedge\frac{2C_{1}}{\lambda}N\log N}]\\
&=E_{N}[S_{\sigma_{2}\wedge\tau\wedge\frac{2C_{1}}{\lambda}N\log N}^{2}]-\lambda E_{N}[\sigma_{2}\wedge\tau\wedge\frac{2C_{1}}{\lambda}N\log N]\\
&\leq C_{1}N\log N-\lambda E_{N}[\sigma_{2}\wedge\tau\wedge\frac{2C_{1}}{\lambda}N\log N].\end{split}

Rearranging the previous inequality and using the Markov inequality we obtain that

P_{N}[\sigma_{2}\wedge\tau>\frac{2C_{1}}{\lambda}N\log N]\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Iterating this bound
{\lambda\log^{2}N/2C_{1}}
times, using the Markov property leads to

P_{N}[\sigma_{2}\wedge\tau>N\log^{3}N]\leq N^{-c\log N}.

∎

7.2.2
No large jumps

It will be useful to be able to ignore very large upwards jumps of
Y_{t}
, and the
next simple lemma states the required estimate for this. Let

M=M_{N}:=\log^{2}N.

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
t_{2}
be a stopping time satisfying the condition

\rho_{C}N+(a-2\varepsilon)\sqrt{N\log N}\leq X_{t_{2}}\leq\rho_{c}N+{(2A+1)}\sqrt{N\log N}.

There exists some constant
c=c(\lambda)>0
such that for any
t\geq t_{2}

P_{N}[\text{$\Delta Y_{t}>M$ for some $t_{2}\leq t\leq t_{2}+\sigma_{2}(t_{2})\wedge\tau(t_{2})\wedge N\log^{3}N$}\,\bigm|\,{\mathcal{F}_{t_{2}}}]\leq N^{-c\log N}.

Proof.

From the formulas for the transition probabilities in Lemma
4.1
we obtain

\begin{split}&P_{N}[Y_{t+1}-Y_{t}>M\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}]\\
&=\Pi^{(0)}_{M+1}+\Pi^{{(-1)}}_{M+2}\\
&\leq\Pi^{(0)}_{M+1}+\Pi^{{(-1)}}_{M+1}\\
&=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{N+1}{N}\prod_{i=0}^{M}\frac{X_{t}-Y_{t}-i}{N+2-Y_{t}-i}\Big[\frac{1}{N+2-X_{t}}+\frac{N+1-X_{t}}{N+2-X_{t}}\Big]\\
&\leq\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{N+1}{N}\Big(\frac{X_{t}}{N+2}\Big)^{M+1}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{N+1}{N}\Big(\frac{X_{t_{2}}}{N+2}\Big)^{M+1}\\
&\leq C(\lambda)e^{-c(\lambda)M}\\
&=C(\lambda)N^{-c(\lambda)\log N}.\end{split}

The statement now follows by a union bound.
∎

7.2.3
An auxiliary estimate

Recall that

\hat{x}=\hat{x}_{N}=\frac{x-\rho_{c}N}{\sqrt{N\log N}},\quad x\in\mathbb{N},

(7.9)

and further set
N^{\prime}=N/\log^{2}N
and
x_{*}=x-\log^{2}N
.

Lemma 7.8
.

The exists a finite constant
C
, such that for any
t,N\in\mathbb{N}

P_{N}[X_{t+N^{\prime}}<x_{*}\,|\,X_{t}=x,\,Y_{t}=y]\leq CN^{-\log N}.

Proof.

For
x-\log^{2}N\leq x^{\prime}\leq x
we have

\begin{split}\Pi^{(-1)}_{0}(x^{\prime},y)&=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{N+1}{N}\frac{1}{N+2-x^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{N}(1+o(1)),\end{split}

where we used that

N+2-x^{\prime}=N-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}N-\hat{x}\sqrt{N\log N}-O(\log^{2}N)+2=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}N(1-o(1)).

Thus the probability in the statement is bounded by the probability that a binomial random variable
B
with
N^{\prime}
trials and probability of success
(1+o(1))/N
exceeds
\log^{2}N
.
The latter probability can be bounded

\begin{split}P_{N}[B>\log^{2}N]&=P_{N}[e^{\theta B}>e^{\theta\log^{2}N}]\\
&\leq N^{-\theta\log N}\Big(1+\frac{(e^{\theta}-1)(1+o(1))}{N}\Big)^{N/\log^{2}N}\\
&\leq N^{-\theta\log N}\exp\Big(\frac{(e^{\theta}-1)(1+o(1))}{\log^{2}N}\Big)\end{split}

for any
\theta>0
. Choosing
\theta=1
completes the proof.
∎

7.2.4
Stochastic comparisons

Let us write

\nu_{k}(x,y)=\pi^{(0)}_{k}(x,y)+\pi^{(-1)}_{k+1}(x,y)=P_{N}[\Delta Y_{t}=k\,|\,X_{t}=x,\,Y_{t}=y],\quad k\geq-1.

Lemma 7.9
.

We have that the distribution
(\nu_{k}(x,y))_{k\geq-1}
is stochastically decreasing in
y
and stochastically increasing in
x
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
4.1
, for
\ell\geq 1
, we have

\begin{split}&P_{N}[Y_{t+1}-Y_{t}\geq\ell\,|\,X_{t}=x,\,Y_{t}=y]=\Pi^{(0)}_{\ell}(x,y)+\Pi^{{(-1)}}_{\ell+1}(x,y)\\
&\quad=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{N+1}{N}\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{x-y-i}{N+2-y-i}\Big[\frac{x-y-\ell}{N+2-y-\ell}\frac{1}{N+2-x}+\frac{N+1-x}{N+2-x}\Big]\\
&\quad=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{N+1}{N}\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{x-y-i}{N+2-y-i}\Big[\frac{x-y-\ell+(N+2-y-\ell)(N+1-x)}{(N+2-y-\ell)(N+2-x)}\Big]\\
&\quad=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{N+1}{N}\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{x-y-i}{N+2-y-i}\Big[\frac{x-(N+2)+(N+2-y-\ell)(N+2-x)}{(N+2-y-\ell)(N+2-x)}\Big]\\
&\quad=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{N+1}{N}\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{x-y-i}{N+2-y-i}\Big[\frac{(N+1-y-\ell)(N+2-x)}{(N+2-y-\ell)(N+2-x)}\Big]\\
&\quad=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{N+1}{N}\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{x-y-i}{N+2-y-i}\Big[1-\frac{1}{N+2-y-\ell}\Big].\end{split}

Here, if
y
is increased, the right hand expression decreases. Also, if
x
is increased, the expression increases.

In order to complete the monotonicity claim, it remains to observe that the comparison also holds for
\ell=0
. When
\ell=0
, the
\Pi^{(0)}_{0}
contribution contains an extra term
-\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{1}{N}
, which however, being independent of
x
and
y
, does not affect the comparison.
∎

Now recall that
x_{*}=x-\log^{2}N
, and let us introduce the notations

\begin{split}y_{*}^{+}&=\ell(x)+2-\lambda=(1+\lambda)x-\lambda N+2-\lambda\\
&=(1+\lambda)\Big(\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}N+\hat{x}\sqrt{N\log N}\Big)-\lambda N+2-\lambda\\
&=(1+\lambda)\hat{x}\sqrt{N\log N}+2-\lambda;\end{split}

and

\begin{split}y_{*}^{-}&=\ell(x_{*})-\lambda=(1+\lambda)x_{*}-\lambda N-\lambda\\
&=(1+\lambda)\Big(\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}N+\hat{x}\sqrt{N\log N}-\log^{2}N\Big)-\lambda N-\lambda\\
&=(1+\lambda)\hat{x}\sqrt{N\log N}-(1+\lambda)\log^{2}N-\lambda.\end{split}

Lemma 7.10
.

(i) For
x^{\prime}\leq x
and
y\geq y_{*}^{+}
we have that

(\nu_{k}(x^{\prime},y))_{k\geq-1}
is stochastically dominated by
(\nu_{k}(x,y_{*}^{+}))_{k\geq-1}
.
Also, for sufficiently large
N
we have

\sum_{k\geq-1}k\nu_{k}(x,y_{*}^{+})<0.

(ii) For
x_{*}\leq x^{\prime}\leq x
and
y\leq y_{*}^{-}
we have that

(\nu_{k}(x^{\prime},y))_{k\geq-1}
is stochastically larger than
(\nu_{k}(x_{*},y_{*}^{-}))_{k\geq-1}
.
Also, for sufficiently large
N
we have

\sum_{k\geq-1}k\nu_{k}(x_{*},y_{*}^{-})>0.

Proof.

(i) The claimed stochastic domination follows immediately from Lemma
7.9
. Computing the mean, we have

\begin{split}E_{N}[Y_{t+1}-Y_{t}\,|\,X_{t}=x,\,Y_{t}=y_{*}^{+}]&=E_{N}[(S_{t+1}-S_{t})-(\ell(X_{t+1})-\ell(X_{t}))\,|\,X_{t}=x,\,Y_{t}=y_{*}^{+}]\\
&=-\frac{S_{t}}{N}(1+o(1))-\frac{2\lambda}{N}+o(N^{-1})+\frac{1+\lambda}{N}(1+o(1))\\
&=-\frac{y_{*}^{+}-\ell(x)}{N}(1+o(1))+\frac{1-\lambda}{N}+o(N^{-1})\\
&=-\frac{1+o(1)}{N}<0.\end{split}

(ii) Again, the stochastic domination claim follows immediately from Lemma
7.9
. Computing the mean, we have

\begin{split}E_{N}[Y_{t+1}-Y_{t}\,|\,X_{t}=x_{*},\,Y_{t}=y_{*}^{-}]&=E_{N}[(S_{t+1}-S_{t})-(\ell(X_{t+1})-\ell(X_{t}))\,|\,X_{t}=x_{*},\,Y_{t}=y_{*}^{-}]\\
&=-\frac{S_{t}}{N}(1+o(1))-\frac{2\lambda}{N}+o(N^{-1})+\frac{1+\lambda}{N}(1+o(1))\\
&=-\frac{y_{*}^{-}-\ell(x_{*})}{N}(1+o(1))+\frac{1-\lambda}{N}+o(N^{-1})\\
&=\frac{1+o(1)}{N}>0.\end{split}

∎

7.2.5
Lower bound on the exit time

U_{-}(1),U_{-}(2),\dots

(ii) that we can
couple
(Y_{r}:s\leq r\leq\frac{N}{\log^{2}N})
to
U_{-}(1),U_{-}(2),\dots

in such a way that the event in (
7.16
) implies the events

\begin{split}&Y_{s}=y_{*}^{-}\quad\text{ and }\quad y_{*}^{-}\geq\max_{0\leq r^{\prime}\leq\frac{N}{\log^{2}N}-s}\left[Y(s)+U_{-}(1)+\dots+U_{-}(r^{\prime})\right]\\
&\quad\text{ and }\quad\max_{0\leq r^{\prime}\leq\frac{N}{\log^{2}N}-s}\left[Y_{s}+U_{-}(1)+\dots+U_{-}(r^{\prime})\right]<y_{*}^{-}-N^{1/2}.\end{split}

(7.17)

Dropping the requirement on the minimum, and
using that
U_{-}^{\prime}(r^{\prime})<U_{-}(r^{\prime})
(due to Lemma
7.9
(ii)), the events
in (
7.17
) for sufficiently large
N
imply the event

\max_{0\leq r^{\prime}\leq\frac{N}{\log^{2}N}-s}-\left[U_{-}^{\prime}(1)+\dots+U_{-}^{\prime}(r^{\prime})\right]>N^{1/2}.

(7.18)

From the above we obtain that

\begin{split}P_{N}[\tau<N/\log^{2}N]&\leq\sum_{1\leq s\leq N/\log^{2}N}P_{N}\Big[\max_{0\leq r^{\prime}\leq\frac{N}{\log^{2}N}}-\left[U_{-}^{\prime}(1)+\dots+U_{-}^{\prime}(r^{\prime})\right]>N^{1/2}\Big].\end{split}

(7.19)

We can again apply a maximal inequality for i.i.d. variables (see
[
17
, Corollary A.2.7]
)
that gives that the right hand side of (
7.19
) is at most

\frac{N}{\log^{2}N}\exp\Big(-\frac{\log^{2}N}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big)\exp\Big(O\Big(\frac{\log^{4}N}{\sqrt{N}}\Big)\Big)=O(N^{-c\log N}).

∎

7.3
Estimates on the absorption time

In this section we prove Proposition
7.6
(i)–(ii), by coupling the evolution of
Y_{t}

to a coarse-grained process that can be represented as a birth-and-death Markov chain. We then study the required hitting probability of the birth-and-death chain by estimating its resistances. Almost the same coupling will be used in the upper and lower bounds on the support,
so we provide a setup that applies in either situation.

7.3.1
Coupling with a coarse-grained process

We first make some preparations to define the coupling. We want control the probability that
Y_{t}

hits
0
while
X_{t}-\rho_{c}N
is either in the interval

[(a-3\varepsilon)\sqrt{N\log N},(a-\varepsilon)\sqrt{N\log N}]
, or in the interval
[(a+\varepsilon)\sqrt{N\log N},{(2A+1)}\sqrt{N\log N}]
.

The overall idea of the argument is as follows. We give upper and lower bounds on the upwards drift of
Y
,
and use this to approximate the evolution of
Y
by that of a coarse-grained nearest neighbour
process on scale
N^{3/8}
. The scale
N^{3/8}
is such
that the drift of
Y
does not vary significantly during a displacement of this order, so we
may accurately estimate the probabilities that
Y
exits an interval of length
2N^{3/8}
at
either end.
On the other hand, the scale
N^{3/8}
is large enough so that jumps of size at most
M

do not matter for the estimates and the course grained process is indeed a nearest neighbour
walk.
1
1

1

Any choice

extra care.

Then, using resistances, we estimate exit probabilities for
the coarse-grained process, and use this to bound the probability that

hits
0
during the
time period of interest.

For each step of the construction, we first give the details in the case of the lower bound on the support (i.e. when

7.3.2
Resistance estimate

In this section we obtain an estimate of
f_{k}
and
\bar{f}_{k}
, and thereby of the resistance functions
r
and
\bar{r}
, respectively.
Consider the moment-generating function

\begin{split}\phi_{k}(\theta)&=E_{N}[\exp(\theta(Z^{\prime}_{s+1}-Z^{\prime}_{s}))],\quad Z^{\prime}_{s}\in I_{k}.\end{split}

Since the drift is small, we will be looking for a
\theta_{k}^{*}
with small absolute value
such that
\phi_{k}(\theta_{k}^{*})=1
, and hence produces a martingale. It is useful to deduce the
order of smallness by writing

\frac{X_{t_{2}}-y_{k}^{*}}{N-y_{k}^{*}}=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+\delta_{k},

where
|\delta_{k}|
is small. This is justified by

\begin{split}\frac{X_{t_{2}}-y_{k}^{*}}{N-y_{k}^{*}}&=\frac{\rho_{c}(N-y_{k}^{*})+\hat{X}_{t_{2}}\sqrt{N\log N}-(1-\rho_{c})y_{k}^{*}}{N-y_{k}^{*}}\\
&=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{z_{k}^{*}}{N-y_{k}^{*}}+O(N^{-1}),\end{split}

and

\frac{|z_{k}^{*}|}{N-y_{k}^{*}}\leq(1+o(1))\frac{|k+\frac{3}{2}|N^{3/8}}{N}\leq(1+o(1))\frac{(1+\lambda)\hat{X}_{t_{2}}\sqrt{N\log N}}{N}=O(\sqrt{\log N/N}).

Lemma 7.15
.

(i) There exists a value
0\not=d_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
such that with
\theta_{k}^{*}=d_{k}\delta_{k}
we have

\phi_{k}(\theta_{k}^{*})=1
, and such that

\theta^{*}_{k}=\frac{1+\lambda}{\lambda}\delta_{k}+\delta_{k}O(N^{-3/8}\log N).

(ii) The process
U_{s}=\exp(\theta^{*}_{k}(Z^{\prime}_{s}-Z^{\prime}_{s_{0}}))
,
s\geq s_{0}
, is a martingale.

Proof.

(i) In this proof, we write
x=X_{t_{2}}
,
\hat{x}=\hat{X}_{t_{2}}
,
y=y_{k}^{*}
and

z=z_{k}^{*}
for short.
Applying Lemma
6.4
to the increment
Z^{\prime}_{s+1}-Z^{\prime}_{s}
we have

\begin{split}\phi_{k}(\theta)&=1+\theta\Big[\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}-\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{x-y}{N-x}\Big]\\
&\quad\qquad+\theta^{2}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{x-y}{N-x}+\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{(x-y)(N-y)}{(N-x)(N-x)}\Big]+O(|\theta|N^{-1})+O(|\theta|^{3}).\end{split}

Here

\begin{split}\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{x-y}{N-x}&=\frac{x-y}{N-y}\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{N-y}{N-x}=\Big(\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+\delta_{k}\Big)\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{N-y}{N-\rho_{c}N-\hat{x}\sqrt{N\log N}}\\
&=\Big(\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+\delta_{k}\Big)\frac{N-y}{N-(1+\lambda)\hat{x}\sqrt{N\log N}}\\
&=\Big(\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+\delta_{k}\Big)\frac{1-\frac{y}{N}}{1-\frac{(1+\lambda)\hat{x}\sqrt{N\log N}}{N}}\\
&=\Big(\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+\delta_{k}\Big)\Big(1+\frac{(1+\lambda)\hat{x}\sqrt{N\log N}-y}{N}+O(\log N/N)\Big)\\
&=\Big(\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+\delta_{k}\Big)\Big(1+(1+\lambda)\delta_{k}\frac{N-y}{N}+O(\log N/N)\Big)\\
&=\Big(\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+\delta_{k}\Big)\Big(1+(1+\lambda)\delta_{k}+O(\log N/N)\Big)\\
&=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+(1+\lambda)\delta_{k}+O(\delta_{k}^{2})+O(\log N/N).\end{split}

Note that in the course of the above calculation we saw that

\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{N-y}{N-x}=1+(1+\lambda)\delta_{k}+O(\log N/N).

Hence with
\theta=d\delta_{k}
we get on the one hand

\begin{split}\theta\Big[\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}-\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{x-y}{N-x}\Big]=d\delta_{k}\left[-(1+\lambda)\delta_{k}\right]+O(|\delta_{k}|^{3})+O(|\delta_{k}|\log N/N);\end{split}

on the other hand

\begin{split}&\theta^{2}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{x-y}{N-x}+\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{(x-y)(N-y)}{(N-x)(N-x)}\Big]\\
&\quad=d^{2}\delta_{k}^{2}\Big[\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}\frac{N-y}{N-x}+O(\delta_{k})+O(\log N/N)\Big]\\
&\quad=d^{2}\delta_{k}^{2}\left[\lambda+O(\delta_{k})+O(\log N/N)\right]\\
&\quad=\lambda d^{2}\delta_{k}^{2}+O(|\delta_{k}|^{3})+O(|\delta_{k}|\log N/N).\end{split}

This yields

\begin{split}\phi_{k}(\theta)&=1+d[\lambda d-(1+\lambda)]\delta_{k}^{2}+O(|\delta_{k}|^{3})+O(|\delta_{k}|\log N/N).\end{split}

We show that all the error terms are
\delta_{k}^{2}O(N^{-3/8}\log N)
. Indeed, we first
note that

\delta_{k}=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{(k+\frac{3}{2})N^{3/8}}{N}+O(N^{-1}),

which means that

cN^{-5/8}\leq\delta_{k}\leq CN^{-1/2}\sqrt{\log N},

and

cN^{-5/4}\leq\delta_{k}^{2}\leq CN^{-1}\log N;

(see the technical note following (
7.21
)).
From this we have

\begin{split}N^{-1}\log N\delta_{k}&=N^{-5/8}N^{-3/8}\log N\delta_{k}\leq\delta_{k}^{2}O(N^{-3/8}\log N);\\
\delta_{k}^{3}&=\delta_{k}^{2}\delta_{k}\leq\delta_{k}^{2}O(N^{-1/2}\sqrt{\log N})=\delta_{k}^{2}O(N^{-3/8}\log N).\end{split}

Consequently, we obtain the equation

0=d[\lambda d-(1+\lambda)]+\varepsilon(d),

where
\varepsilon(d)=O(N^{-3/8}\log N)
and is a continuous function of
d
.
Hence there is a root
d
within
O(N^{-3/8}\log N)
of
(1+\lambda)/\lambda
, proving statement (i).

(ii) This follows immediately from statement (i).
∎

Analogous considerations apply to

\begin{split}\bar{\phi}_{k}(\theta)&=E_{N}[\exp(\theta(\bar{Z}^{\prime}_{s+1}-\bar{Z}^{\prime}_{s}))],\quad\bar{Z}^{\prime}_{s}\in I_{k}.\end{split}

This time we can write

\frac{X_{t_{2}}-\bar{y}_{k}^{*}}{N-\bar{y}_{k}^{*}}=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}+\bar{\delta}_{k},

where
\bar{\delta}_{k}
satisfies

\begin{split}\bar{\delta}_{k}&=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{\bar{z}_{k}^{*}}{N-\bar{y}_{k}^{*}}+O(N^{-1}),\end{split}

and

\frac{|\bar{z}_{k}^{*}|}{N-\bar{y}_{k}^{*}}\leq(1+o(1))\frac{|k-\frac{3}{2}|N^{3/8}}{N}=O(\sqrt{(\log N)/N}).

With these definitions the following lemma follows by the same proof as Lemma
7.15
.

Lemma 7.16
.

(i) There exists a value
0\not=\bar{d}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
such that with
\bar{\theta}_{k}^{*}=\bar{d}_{k}\bar{\delta}_{k}
we have

\bar{\phi}_{k}(\bar{\theta}_{k}^{*})=1
, and such that

\bar{\theta}^{*}_{k}=\frac{1+\lambda}{\lambda}\bar{\delta}_{k}+\bar{\delta}_{k}O(N^{-3/8}\log N),\quad k\in\{-K^{-},\dots,\bar{K}^{+}\}.

(ii) The process

,

, is a martingale.

Lemma 7.17
.

(i) We have

\begin{split}\frac{1-f_{k}}{f_{k}}&=\exp\Big(\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{k+\frac{3}{2}}{N^{1/4}}+O(MN^{-3/8})\Big),\quad\quad k\in\{-K^{-},\dots,K^{+}\}.\end{split}

(7.25)

(ii) We have

\begin{split}\frac{1-\bar{f}_{k}}{\bar{f}_{k}}&=\exp\Big(\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{k-\frac{3}{2}}{N^{1/4}}+O(MN^{-3/8})\Big),\quad\quad k\in\{-K^{-},\dots,\bar{K}^{+}\}.\end{split}

(7.26)

Proof.

We only spell out in detail the proof of (i), since the proof of (ii) follows a very similar argument.
Apply the optional stopping theorem to the martingale
U
and the stopping time
\rho^{\prime}
. Then

\begin{split}1=E_{N}[U_{s_{0}}]=f_{k}E_{N}[U_{\rho^{\prime}}\,|\,Z^{\prime}_{\rho^{\prime}}\geq(k+1)N^{3/8}]+(1-f_{k})E_{N}[U_{\rho^{\prime}}\,|\,Z^{\prime}_{\rho^{\prime}}\leq(k-1)N^{3/8}].\end{split}

(7.27)

Here the right hand side can be estimated as

\begin{split}&=f_{k}\exp\big(\theta^{*}_{k}[(k+1)N^{3/8}+O(M)]-\theta^{*}_{k}[kN^{3/8}+O(M)]\big)\\
&\qquad\quad+(1-f_{k})\exp\big(\theta^{*}_{k}[(k-1)N^{3/8}+O(M)]-\theta^{*}_{k}[kN^{3/8}+O(M)]\big)\\
&=f_{k}\exp\big(\theta^{*}_{k}[N^{3/8}+O(M)]\big)+(1-f_{k})\exp\big(\theta^{*}_{k}[-N^{3/8}+O(M)]\big).\end{split}

We obtain

\begin{split}f_{k}&=\frac{1-\exp\big(\theta^{*}_{k}[-N^{3/8}+O(M)]\big)}{\exp\big(\theta^{*}_{k}[N^{3/8}+O(M)]\big)-\exp\big(\theta^{*}_{k}[-N^{3/8}+O(M)]\big)}\\
1-f_{k}&=\frac{\exp\left(\theta^{*}_{k}[N^{3/8}+O(M)]\right)-1}{\exp\left(\theta^{*}_{k}[N^{3/8}+O(M)]\right)-\exp\left(\theta^{*}_{k}[-N^{3/8}+O(M)]\right)}\\
\frac{1-f_{k}}{f_{k}}&=\frac{\exp\left(\theta^{*}_{k}[N^{3/8}+O(M)]\right)-1}{1-\exp\left(\theta^{*}_{k}[-N^{3/8}+O(M)]\right)}\\
&=:\frac{e^{w_{k}+\eta}-1}{1-e^{-w_{k}}}.\end{split}

We rewrite the last expression into

\begin{split}\frac{e^{\frac{w_{k}+\eta}{2}}\left(e^{\frac{w_{k}+\eta}{2}}-e^{-\frac{w_{k}+\eta}{2}}\right)}{e^{-\frac{w_{k}}{2}}\left(e^{\frac{w_{k}}{2}}-e^{-\frac{w_{k}}{2}}\right)}&=e^{w_{k}+\frac{\eta}{2}}\frac{\sinh\left(\frac{w_{k}}{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}\right)}{\sinh\left(\frac{w_{k}}{2}\right)}\\
&=e^{w_{k}+\frac{\eta}{2}}\frac{\sinh\left(\frac{w_{k}}{2}\right)\cosh\left(\frac{\eta}{2}\right)+\cosh\left(\frac{w_{k}}{2}\right)\sinh\left(\frac{\eta}{2}\right)}{\sinh\left(\frac{w_{k}}{2}\right)}\\
&=e^{w_{k}+\frac{\eta}{2}}\left[\cosh\left(\frac{\eta}{2}\right)+\sinh\left(\frac{\eta}{2}\right)\coth\left(\frac{w_{k}}{2}\right)\right]\\
&=e^{w_{k}+\frac{\eta}{2}}\left[1+O(\eta^{2})+O(\eta w_{k}^{-1})\right].\end{split}

Here

\eta w_{k}^{-1}=O(MN^{-3/8}),

wheras

\begin{split}w_{k}&=\theta^{*}_{k}(N^{3/8}+O(M))=d\delta_{k}(N^{3/8}+O(M))=\frac{1+\lambda}{\lambda}\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\frac{k+\frac{3}{2}}{N^{5/8}}(N^{3/8}+O(M))\\
&=\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{k+\frac{3}{2}}{N^{1/4}}\left[1+O(MN^{-3/8})\right]=\Omega(N^{-1/4}).\end{split}

Thus the above calculation gives

\begin{split}\frac{1-f_{k}}{f_{k}}&=\exp\left(w_{k}+O(\eta w_{k}^{-1})\right)=\exp\Big(\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{k+\frac{3}{2}}{N^{1/4}}+O(MN^{-3/8})\Big),\end{split}

(7.28)

as required.
∎

Proof of Proposition
7.6
(i).

Recall the coupling which has been defined before Lemma
7.13
.
We have

P_{0}[{{T}^{\circ}}_{K^{+}+1}<{{T}^{\circ}}_{-K^{-}-1}]=\frac{\mathcal{R}((-K^{-}-1)\leftrightarrow 0)}{\mathcal{R}((-K^{-}-1)\leftrightarrow(K^{+}+1))},

(7.29)

where
\mathcal{R}
denotes effective resistance.
We evaluate the resistance in the denominator as follows:

\begin{split}&\mathcal{R}((-K^{-}-1)\leftrightarrow(K^{+}+1))=\sum_{p=-K^{-}-1}^{K^{+}}\prod_{r=-K^{-}}^{p}\frac{1-g_{r}}{g_{r}}\\
&={\sum_{p=-K^{-}-1}^{K^{+}}\prod_{r=-K^{-}}^{p}\Big(\frac{1-f_{r}}{f_{r}}-O(N^{-c\log N})\Big)}\\
&\quad=\sum_{p=-K^{-}-1}^{K^{+}}\exp\Big(\frac{1}{\lambda}N^{-1/4}\sum_{r=-K^{-}}^{p}\Big[(r+\frac{3}{2})+O(MN^{-1/8})\Big]\Big)\\
&\quad=\sum_{p=-K^{-}-1}^{K^{+}}\exp\Big(\frac{1}{\lambda}N^{-1/4}\sum_{r^{\prime}=0}^{p+K^{-}}\Big[(r^{\prime}-K^{-}+\frac{3}{2})+O(MN^{-1/8})\Big]\Big)\\
&\quad=\sum_{p=-K^{-}-1}^{K^{+}}\exp\Bigg(\frac{1}{2\lambda}N^{-1/4}\Big[(p+K^{-})(p+K^{-}+1)\\
&\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad-K^{-}(p+K^{-}+1)+(\frac{3}{2}+O(MN^{-1/8})(p+K^{-}+1)\Big]\Bigg)\\
&\quad=\sum_{p=-K^{-}-1}^{K^{+}}\exp\Big(\frac{1}{2\lambda}N^{-1/4}\Big[p(p+K^{-}+1)+\Big(\frac{3}{2}+O(MN^{-1/8}\Big)(p+K^{-}+1)\Big]\Big)\\
&\quad=N^{1/8}\sum_{p=-K^{-}-1}^{K^{+}}N^{-1/8}\exp\Big(\frac{1}{2\lambda}(pN^{-1/8})(pN^{-1/8}+1)+B\Big)\end{split}

where the last display defines the
B
term,
which is such that
B=O(N^{-\frac{1}{8}})
uniformly in
p\in[-K^{-}-1,K^{+}]
.
Using Rieman sums, the sum on the right hand side within a factor
1+o(1)
satisfies the following bounds:

\begin{split}\sum_{p=-K^{-}-1}^{K^{+}}N^{-1/8}\exp\Big(\frac{1}{2\lambda}(pN^{-1/8})(pN^{-1/8}+{1})\Big)&\leq\int_{-1}^{(K^{+}+1)N^{-1/8}}\exp\Big(\frac{1}{2\lambda}x(x+1)\Big)dx\\
\sum_{p=-K^{-}-1}^{K^{+}}N^{-1/8}\exp\Big(\frac{1}{2\lambda}(pN^{-1/8})(pN^{-1/8}+{1})\Big)&\geq\int_{-1}^{K^{+}N^{-1/8}}\exp\Big(\frac{1}{2\lambda}x(x+1)\Big)dx.\end{split}

(7.30)

Change variables to

y=\frac{x+1/2}{\sqrt{2\lambda}},\qquad x=\sqrt{2\lambda}y-\frac{1}{2},

so that

\frac{x(x+1)}{2\lambda}=\frac{(\sqrt{2\lambda}y-\frac{1}{2})(\sqrt{2\lambda}y+\frac{1}{2})}{2\lambda}=y^{2}-\frac{1}{8\lambda}.

This yields the integral

\begin{split}&\sqrt{2\lambda}\int_{-1/2\sqrt{2\lambda}}^{(K^{+}N^{-1/8}+1/2)/\sqrt{2\lambda}}\exp\Big(\Big[y-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\lambda}}\Big]\Big[y+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\lambda}}\Big]\Big)dy\\
&\quad=\sqrt{2\lambda}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{8\lambda}\Big)\int_{-1/2\sqrt{2\lambda}}^{(K^{+}N^{-1/8}+1/2)/\sqrt{2\lambda}}\exp(y^{2})dy.\end{split}

The asymptotics

\int_{0}^{L}\exp(y^{2})dy\sim\frac{1}{2L}\exp(L^{2}),\quad\text{as $L\to\infty$}

are well-known (and easy to verify using l’Hospital’s rule). Hence
we get

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}((-K^{-}-1)\leftrightarrow(K^{+}+1))=O(1)\frac{N^{1/8}}{K^{+}N^{-1/8}}\exp\Big(\frac{1}{2\lambda}(1+\lambda)^{2}\hat{X}_{t_{2}}^{2}\log N\Big).\end{split}

Similarly, we get

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}((-K^{-}-1)\leftrightarrow 0)={\Theta(1)}N^{1/8}.\end{split}

This yields

P_{0}[{{T}^{\circ}}_{K^{+}+1}<{{T}^{\circ}}_{K^{-}-1}]={\Theta(1)}\hat{X}_{t_{2}}\sqrt{\log N}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2\lambda}(1+\lambda)^{2}\hat{X}_{t_{2}}^{2}\log N\Big).

(7.31)

Since
\hat{X}_{t_{2}}\leq a-\varepsilon=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{1+\lambda}-\varepsilon
, the last expression is

\begin{split}\geq c\exp\Big(-\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\eta_{1}(\varepsilon)\Big)\log N\Big)=cN^{-1/2+\eta_{1}(\varepsilon)}.\end{split}

Recall the definition of
\tau
and
\sigma_{2}
in the statement and
the coupling which has been defined before Lemma
7.13
. We deduce from Lemma
7.13
that

\displaystyle P_{N}(\tau<\sigma_{2})

\displaystyle\geq P_{N}(\{\tau<\sigma_{2}\}\cap\{\mbox{coupling successful}\})

\displaystyle\geq P_{N}(\{\exists r\leq\hat{T}\,:\,Y_{\rho_{r}}\leq 0\mbox{ and }\rho_{r}<\sigma_{2}\}\cap\{\mbox{coupling successful}\})

\displaystyle\geq P_{N}(\{\hat{T}_{K^{+}+1}<\hat{T}_{K^{-}-1}\}\cap\{\mbox{coupling successful}\})

\displaystyle\geq c_{1}N^{-\frac{1}{2}+\eta_{1}(\varepsilon)}-3N^{-c\log N}\geq c_{3}N^{-\frac{1}{2}+\eta_{1}(\varepsilon)},

where for the first inequality we used Lemma
7.4
and
Lemma
7.7

to upper bound the probability that the coupling is unsuccessful,
for the third inequality we used Lemma
7.13
,
and the last inequality holds for some positive constant
c_{3}
and every positive integer
N
.
∎
