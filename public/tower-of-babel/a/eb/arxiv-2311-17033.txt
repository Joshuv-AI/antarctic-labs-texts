Harmonic Functions on Four Dimensions
William Johnston, Sara Moore, and Rebecca G. Wahl

Abstract
. This paper develops theory for a newly-defined bicomplex hyperbolic harmonic function with four real-dimensional inputs, in a way that generalizes the connection between real harmonic functions with two real-dimensional inputs and complex analytic functions. For example, every bicomplex hyperbolic harmonic function appears as this paper’s newly-defined hyperbolic real part of a bicomplex analytic function, just as every real harmonic function with two real-dimensional inputs is the real part of a complex analytic function. In addition, this connection produces a unique (up to additive constant) and newly-defined hyperbolic harmonic conjugate function, just as every real harmonic function has a unique (up to additive constant) real harmonic conjugate. Finally, the paper determines a bicomplex Poisson kernel function that produces a corresponding integral representation for bicomplex harmonic functions, one that generalizes the complex harmonic function Poisson integral representation.

A powerful so-called hyperbolic norm evolves from consideration of bicomplex numbers with real idempotent components. This norm equals the Euclidean norm when applied to complex numbers, but it outputs moduli for noncomplex numbers that are in general not real. Hence its properties form a generalization of the standard defining properties of a norm, obtained by replacing the nonnegative real numbers by a partially ordered set (a poset) of bicomplex numbers called the nonnegative hyperbolic numbers
\mathbb{H}^{+}
, which the following definition describes. To distinguish it from a norm that has the standard properties, mathematicians sometimes call the hyperbolic norm a poset-valued norm.

Definition 1

(Hyperbolic Numbers)
.

The hyperbolic numbers

\mathbb{H}=\{x+ijy:\ x,y\in\mathbb{R}\}=\{\eta_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\eta_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}:\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\ \}

\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}
. The subset of nonnegative hyperbolic numbers is

\mathbb{H}^{+}=\{\eta_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\eta_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}:\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\geq 0\}
.

For any
\zeta=\zeta_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\zeta_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}\in\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}
, the hyperbolic norm is defined as

|\zeta|_{{}_{\mathbb{H}}}=|\zeta_{1}|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+|\zeta_{2}|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
,

which is an element of
\mathbb{H}^{+}
and satisfies
|\zeta\cdot\omega|_{{}_{\mathbb{H}}}=|\zeta|_{{}_{\mathbb{H}}}\cdot|\omega|_{{}_{\mathbb{H}}}
for any
\zeta,\omega\in\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}

A partial ordering on
\mathbb{H}^{+}

\zeta=\eta_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\eta_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}

\eta_{1}<\psi_{1}
and
\eta_{2}<\psi_{2}

B_{{}_{\mathbb{H}}}(c,R)=\{\zeta:|\zeta-c|_{{}_{\mathbb{H}}}<_{{}_{\mathbb{H}}}R\}

where the center

and the hyperbolic radius

2.
Bicomplex Holomorphic, Harmonic, and Hyperbolic Harmonic Functions

This section defines bicomplex holomorphic and harmonic functions and develops a relationship between the two types, one that generalizes the corresponding relationship between complex holomorphic and harmonic functions. We start with the concept of holomorphicity. Again appealing to the idempotent decomposition, for
\zeta=\zeta_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\zeta_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}\subset\Omega
, we say that
F(\zeta)=f_{1}(\zeta_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+f_{2}(\zeta_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
is
\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}
-holomorphic on an open region
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}\oplus\Omega_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
if and only if
f_{1}(\zeta_{1})
and
f_{2}(\zeta_{2})
are complex-valued holomorphic functions on
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, respectively. In this case, for
\zeta\in\Omega
, the following well-known (cf.
[
11
, p. 144]
) identity results:
F^{\prime}(\zeta)=f_{1}^{\prime}(\zeta_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+f_{2}^{\prime}(\zeta_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
.

Throughout this section, the notation
\nabla^{2}
denotes the complex Laplacian – the sum of second partial derivatives – so that
\nabla^{2}f(z)=\nabla^{2}f(x+iy)=\nabla^{2}f(x,y)=f_{xx}(x,y)+f_{yy}(x,y)
. For functions with complex outputs,
\nabla^{2}
is applied to both the real and imaginary parts of
f
. This Laplacian extends to bicomplex functions via the following new definition:

Definition 2
.

For a
\mathbb{BC}
-function
F(\zeta)=F(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})=f_{1}(\zeta_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+f_{2}(\zeta_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
, we define
the bicomplex Laplacian, where
\zeta_{1}=x_{1}+iy_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}=x_{2}+iy_{2}
, as

\nabla^{2}_{\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}}F(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})=\nabla^{2}f_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\nabla^{2}f_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}.

This four-dimensional Laplacian is much different from the standard four-dimensional Laplacian given in rectangular coordinates as

\nabla^{2}f(x,y,s,t)=f_{xx}(x,y,s,t)+f_{yy}(x,y,s,t)+f_{ss}(x,y,s,t)+f_{tt}(x,y,s,t)
.

The form of the bicomplex Laplacian motivates us to define a
\mathbb{BC}
-function
F(\zeta)=F(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})=f_{1}(\zeta_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+f_{2}(\zeta_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
as twice continuously differentiable when both
f_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})
and
f_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})
are twice continuously differentiable. The bicomplex Laplacian then naturally defines a bicomplex harmonic function as follows.

Definition 3
.

A bicomplex,
\mathbb{BC}
-harmonic function is a twice continuously differentiable bicomplex function with bicomplex Laplacian equal to 0.

The following important results follow immediately.

Theorem 1
.

\nabla^{2}_{\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}}F(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})=0
if and only if
\nabla^{2}f_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})=0
and
\nabla^{2}f_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})=0
.

Corollary 1
.

If
F(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})
is a
\mathbb{BC}
-harmonic function, then
f_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})
and
f_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})
are complex harmonic functions.

The notion of a bicomplex hyperbolic harmonic function generalizes that of a real harmonic function, and it is founded on the important set of hyperbolic bicomplex numbers
\mathbb{H}=\{\eta_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\eta_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}:\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\ \}.

Definition 4
.

A bicomplex hyperbolic function is a bicomplex function

F(\zeta)=f_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+f_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}

that has both
f_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})
and
f_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})
real. A hyperbolic harmonic function
F(\zeta)
is a bicomplex hyperbolic function that is also bicomplex harmonic.

An important decomposition of a bicomplex function
F(\zeta)
separates it into two pieces that are the natural generalization of the real and imaginary parts of a complex function. We call it the hyperbolic decomposition of
F(\zeta)
. To see how, write
\zeta_{1}=x_{1}+iy_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}=x_{2}+iy_{2}
and put
F
into its idempotent representation
F(\zeta)=f_{1}(\zeta_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+f_{2}(\zeta_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
. Now write the complex functions
f_{i}
in terms of their real and imaginary parts as

f_{i}(\zeta_{i})=u_{i}(x_{i},y_{i})+iv_{i}(x_{i},y_{i})
for
i=1,2
.

Then

\displaystyle F(\zeta)

\displaystyle=[u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})+i\ v_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})]\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+[u_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})+i\ v_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})]\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}

\displaystyle=[u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}]+i[v_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+v_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}].

Each of
u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
and
v_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+v_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
is a bicomplex hyperbolic function. In the special case when
F(\zeta)
is a
\mathbb{BC}-
holomorphic function, then
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are complex holomorphic, and so
u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})
,
u_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})
,
v_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})
and
v_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})
are real-valued harmonic functions. Therefore in this case, each of
u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
and
v_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+v_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
is a hyperbolic harmonic function. The next definition then follows naturally.

Definition 5
.

Write a bicomplex function
F(\zeta)
in terms of its hyperbolic decomposition as described immediately above, where

(1)

F(\zeta)=[u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}]+i[v_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+v_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}]

Then the hyperbolic real part of
F
is defined as

u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
,

which is a bicomplex hyperbolic function. In this case, we write

\mathbb{H}-
{\rm Re}[F]=u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
.

Correspondingly, the hyperbolic imaginary part of
F
is defined as

v_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+v_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
,

and we write

\mathbb{H}-
\mbox{\rm Im}[F]=v_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+v_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
.

In the special case when
F(\zeta)
is a bicomplex holomorphic function on a bicomplex region
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}\oplus\Omega_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
, both its hyperbolic real and imaginary parts are bicomplex harmonic, and they define the hyperbolic harmonic real and imaginary parts of
F(\zeta)
, respectively.

Note the hyperbolic decomposition in Definition 5 is truly a generalization of the decomposition of a complex function into its real and imaginary parts. To see this explicitly, consider a bicomplex function
F(\zeta)
for
\zeta
any complex number; i.e.
\zeta=z=z\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+z\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
, so that in the decomposition
x_{1}=x_{2}
and
y_{1}=y_{2}
. When the output for such complex inputs is also complex, then the idempotent decomposition for the complex holomorphic function
F(z)
has equal complex functions in both idempotent components. In this case, then,
u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})=u_{2}(x_{1},y_{1})
and
v_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})=v_{2}(x_{1},y_{1})
. Hence
u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}=u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})
and
v_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+v_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}=v_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})
The result is

F(z)=u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})+iv_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})
,

which is the usual decomposition of a complex function into its harmonic real and imaginary parts.

Given any bicomplex hyperbolic harmonic function
u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
on a bicomplex region
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}\oplus\Omega_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
, we importantly form what we call the “hyperbolic conjugate function”

u_{1}^{*}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}^{*}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
.

How are
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
defined? First, take
u_{1}^{*}(x_{1},y_{1})
as the real-valued harmonic conjugate function of
u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})
, so that

f_{1}(z_{1})=f_{1}(x_{1}+iy_{1})=f_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\equiv u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})+i\ u_{1}^{*}(x_{1},y_{1})

is complex holomorphic on
\Omega_{1}
. Similarly take
u_{2}^{*}(x_{2},y_{2})
as the real-valued harmonic conjugate function of
u_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})
, so that

f_{2}(z_{2})=f_{2}(x_{2}+iy_{2})=f_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\equiv u_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})+i\ u_{2}^{*}(x_{2},y_{2})

is complex holomorphic on
\Omega_{2}
. Then

F(\zeta)\equiv f_{1}(z_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+f_{2}(z_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}

is bicomplex holomorphic on
\Omega
. Its hyperbolic decomposition is

(2)

F(\zeta)\equiv[u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}]+i\ [u_{1}^{*}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}^{*}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}],

which shows that its hyperbolic imaginary part
\mathbb{H}-
\mbox{\rm Im}[F]
is the hyperbolic conjugate function of its
\mathbb{H}-
{\rm Re}[F]
.

The fact that every bicomplex hyperbolic harmonic function has a corresponding hyperbolic conjugate function is the foundation for the following theorem’s proof. The theorem generalizes what most mathematicians consider one of the most important basic result of complex analysis, describing the relationship between real harmonic functions and complex analytic functions.

Theorem 2
.

Each hyperbolic harmonic function on an open region
\Omega
appears as the hyperbolic real part
\mathbb{H}-
{\rm Re}[F]
of a bicomplex function
F
holomorphic on
\Omega
and has a unique (up to bicomplex hyperbolic constant) bicomplex conjugate function. Conversely, every bicomplex function
F
, holomorphic on an open region
\Omega
has both
\mathbb{H}-
{\rm Re}[F]
and
\mathbb{H}-
\mbox{\rm Im}[F]
that are hyperbolic harmonic on
\Omega
, where
\mathbb{H}-
\mbox{\rm Im}[F]
is the hyperbolic conjugate function of
\mathbb{H}-
{\rm Re}[F]
.

Proof.

The construction that led to equation (2) proves each hyperbolic harmonic function on
\Omega
appears as the hyperbolic real part of a bicomplex function holomorphic on
\Omega
. The fact that, in equation (2), the real-valued harmonic conjugates
u_{1}^{*}
and
u_{2}^{*}
are uniquely determined up to an additive real constant proves the hyperbolic conjugate function is determined up to a bicomplex hyperbolic constant.

Conversely, for any such bicomplex holomorphic function
F
, the functions

u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
and
v_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+v_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}

that appear in equation (1) are each a hyperbolic harmonic function on
\Omega
, which proves the result. ∎

3.
The Bicomplex Poisson Integral Representation

This section develops a Poisson integral representation for bicomplex hyperbolic harmonic functions. It generalizes the familiar Poisson integral representation for real harmonic functions. In the case where the function is harmonic on the upper half-plane
\Pi^{+}=\{(x,y):y>0\}
, that representation is as follows (cf.
[
6
, p. 258]
).

Theorem 3
.

(The complex Poisson integral representation.) Every piecewise continuous and bounded function
u(t)
on
\mathbb{R}
extends to a function
u(x,y)
on
\Pi^{+}
so that the restriction of
u(x,y)
to
\mathbb{R}
is
u(t)
at every point
t
of continuity and the restriction of
u(x,y)
to
\Pi^{+}
is harmonic. Moreover, the following integral formula produces the harmonic function
u(x,y)
:

u(x,y)=\
\displaystyle\frac{y}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{u(t)}{(x-t)^{2}+y^{2}}
\,dt,\ \ (x,y)\in\Pi^{+}

Here,
P(x,y)=\,
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{y}{x^{2}+y^{2}}
is called the complex Poisson kernel function.

Example

(
1).

(Use of the complex Poisson kernel function.) Let
u(t)=u(1,0)=-1
if
t<0
and
u(t,0)=1
if
t>0
. The Poisson integral formula from Theorem 3 gives (cf.
[
6
, p. 259]
)

u(x,y)=\
\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}
\tan^{-1}
\displaystyle(\frac{x}{y})
.

The graph of
u(x,y)
follows.

These results generalize, building upon the items in the following definition.

Definition 6
.

(i) The bicomplex hyperbolic upper half-plane is
\Pi^{+}_{\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}}=\Pi^{+}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}\oplus\Pi^{+}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
.

(ii) For
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, the bicomplex Poisson kernel function on
\Pi^{+}_{\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}}
is

P_{\mathbb{BC}}(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2})=P(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+P(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}=\frac{1}{\pi}\left[\frac{y_{1}}{x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}}\cdot\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\frac{y_{2}}{x_{2}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}}\cdot\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}\right].

(iii) A given bicomplex hyperbolic function

u(t_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+t_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})=u_{1}(t_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}(t_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
for
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}

is bicomplex piecewise continuous and bounded when each of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are piecewise continuous and bounded over
\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 4
.

(The bicomplex Poisson integral representation.) Every bicomplex piecewise continuous and bounded function
u(t_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+t_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})=u_{1}(t_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}(t_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
for
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
extends to a bicomplex function
u(\zeta)=u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
on the hyperbolic upper half-plane
\Pi^{+}_{\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}}
so that the restriction of
u(\zeta)
to
\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}
is
u(t_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+t_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})
at every point
t_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+t_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
of continuity and the restriction of
u(\zeta)
to
\Pi^{+}_{\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}}
is hyperbolic harmonic. Moreover, the following integral formula produces the hyperbolic harmonic function
u(\zeta)
:

u(x,y)=\
\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
P_{{}_{\mathbb{BC}}}(x_{1}-t,y_{1},x_{2}-t,y_{2})[u_{1}(t)\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}(t)\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}]\,dt,\ \ (x,y)\in\Pi^{+}

As defined above,

P_{{}_{\mathbb{BC}}}(x_{1}-t,y_{1},x_{2}-t,y_{2})=P(x_{1}-t,y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+P(x_{2}-t,y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}

is the bicomplex Poisson kernel, where in each idempotent component
P(x-t,y)
is the complex Poisson kernel.

Proof.

Apply the complex Poisson integral formula to each individual component of

u(t_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+t_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})=u_{1}(t_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}(t_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
,

obtaining real harmonic extension functions
u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})
and
u_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})
, respectively on
\Pi^{+}
, as follows.

\displaystyle u(\zeta)

\displaystyle=u_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+u_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}

Conclusion

Theorems 2 and 4 form a rather surprisingly attained generalization of the well-known and mathematically beautiful results about analytic complex functions: (i) any real harmonic function over a domain can be generated as either a real and/or imaginary part of a complex analytic function; and (ii) the Poisson integral formula shows how harmonic functions on a domain are completely determined by their boundary values. These elegant theories relate real functions on two real variables to complex functions on one complex variable. Theorems 2 and 4 step up the dimensionality to 4D, relating a hyperbolic function on four real variables to a bicomplex function on one bicomplex variable.

The astute reader might rightly state, “There are no known applications of these results to the physical world in which we live.” Correct. But the mathematics, not only beautiful in its own right, might very well turn out to have applications in our four-dimensional space-time universe. And of course, the vast majority of theoretical mathematics, even for functions, is discovered without a known application. When an application exists, it typically turns out to be realized much later than the theory’s birth. It is exciting to imagine what such applications might be in this case. For example, we all know that the mathematical explanation of Einstein’s general relativity, put in terms of covariant and contravariant tensors, is brutally difficult. Might bicomplex functions describe this theory of four dimensional transformations of space-time in a much more elegant and easily understood manner? This paper shows conclusively that complex function theory can be generalized into higher dimensions using bicomplex functions, which hopefully motivates the mathematical community toward such new investigations.
