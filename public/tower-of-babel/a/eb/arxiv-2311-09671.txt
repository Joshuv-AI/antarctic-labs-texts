Robust Contrastive Learning With Theory Guarantee
Lam TranVinAI Research, Ngoc N. TranVanderbilt University, Hoang PhanNew York UniversityAnh BuiMonash UniversityTung PhamVinAI ResearchToan TranVinAI ResearchDinh PhungMonash UniversityTrung LeMonash University

Abstract
Contrastive learning (CL) allows us to create meaningful features without any label information. In the first phase, CL approaches learn the features, which are then classified by a linear classifier that has been learned from labeled data. While existing theoretical works have studied the connection between the supervised loss in the second phase and the unsupervised loss in the first phase to explain why the unsupervised loss can support the supervised loss, there has been no theoretical examination of the connection between the unsupervised loss in the first phase and the robust supervised loss in the second phase, which can shed light on how to establish an effective unsupervised loss in the first phase. To fill this gap, our paper develops rigorous theories to identify which components in the supervised loss can aid the robust supervised loss. Finally, we conduct experiments to verify our findings. All code used in this work is available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/rosa .

Introduction

Contrastive learning (CLR) has become an essential technique in self-supervised learning (SSL) where positive and negative examples are created for each given anchor example
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
. By using CLR, a feature extractor can learn to align representations of the anchors and their positive examples while contrasting those of the anchors and their negative ones. A pioneering work in this area is SimCLR
[
2
]
, which proposed an efficient technique to train a feature extractor with contrastive learning. In SimCLR, positive examples are created using random data augmentation techniques sampled from a pool
\mathcal{T}
of data augmentations, while negative examples are simply sampled from the data distribution. Subsequently, the InfoNCE loss is employed to train a feature extractor by aligning the representations of positive pairs while contrasting those of negative examples.

The vulnerability of neural networks to imperceptibly small perturbations
[
5
]
has posed a significant challenge in deploying them for safety-critical applications, such as autonomous driving. Researchers have proposed various studies to enhance the robustness of trained networks against adversarial attacks
[
6
,
7
]
, random noise
[
8
]
, and corruptions
[
9
]
. In particular, the robustness of contrastive learning (CL) has been empirically investigated in previous works
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
. Specifically, these works explored adversarial examples generated by slightly modifying the InfoNCE loss to obtain robust unsupervised features for the second phase. Although these studies achieved promising empirical robust accuracies in the second phase, they still need to address why improving the robustness in the first phase using the InfoNCE loss can enhance the robustness in the second phase from a theoretical perspective.

The success of SimCLR and other CL techniques
[
13
,
14
]
has spurred interest in exploring the theoretical foundations of CL with the InfoNCE loss. Prior works
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
have inspired several recent studies, including
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
. For example,
[
15
]
investigated the connection between the general classification loss and unsupervised loss in binary classification using Rademacher complexity. In contrast,
[
16
]
empirically and theoretically explored the distribution of representations on the unit sphere, finding that positive examples and their anchors remain close even as the representations become more uniformly distributed. More recently,
[
17
]
extended these findings to multi-class classification and suggested that reducing the gap between the two losses is possible if the pool of data augmentations
\mathcal{T}
satisfies the intra-class connectivity condition. However, none of these works have yet investigated the robustness of CL.

In this work, we aim to provide a theoretical understanding of the robustness of CL. Our focus is on connecting the supervised adversarial loss in the second phase with the unsupervised loss in the first phase. We demonstrate that incorporating adversarial training in the first phase by creating adversarial examples that attack the InfoNCE loss can improve the robustness in the second phase, and provide a theoretical justification for this phenomenon. Additionally, we show that utilizing a global view and enhancing the sharpness of the feature extractor
[
18
]
can also strengthen the robustness in the second phase. Finally, we conduct empirical experiments to validate our theoretical findings.

To summarize, our contributions in this paper include

•

We rigorously develop theories to bound the adversarial loss in the second phase, identifying the crucial factors from the first phase that contribute to the robustness. Our work is the first to address this theoretical problem.

•

Our theoretical findings highlight several important insights: (i) Minimizing the InfoNCE loss on the benign examples proves beneficial in enhancing both the robust and natural accuracies during the second phase. (ii) Utilizing sharpness-aware minimization (SAM)
[
18
]
on the InfoNCE loss of the benign examples offers further improvement in both robust and natural accuracies during the second phase. (iii) Employing adversarial attacks on the InfoNCE loss and pushing the resulting adversarial examples away from their corresponding benign examples globally assists in enhancing the robust accuracies during the second phase. It is noteworthy that our findings in (i) and (ii) contrast with the outcomes observed in standard adversarial training, where minimizing the benign loss can enhance natural accuracies but may compromise robust accuracies
[
7
,
19
]
. Finally, we conduct experiments to validate our findings.

2.2
Self-supervised Learning

Standard SSL
. Self-supervised learning is a machine learning paradigm that aims to extract meaningful representations from input data without relying on human annotations. Recent advances in self-supervised learning for visual data, including methods such as MoCo
[
1
]
, SimCLR
[
2
]
, BYOL
[
42
]
, SimSiam
[
43
]
, and SwAV
[
44
]
, have demonstrated the effectiveness of these representations in various downstream tasks such as image classification, object detection, and instance segmentation. The underlying principle of these methods is to learn representations that remain invariant to different data augmentations by maximizing the similarity of representations derived from different augmented versions of an image. However, this process can potentially lead to mode collapse
[
45
]
, whereby the network can capture a few modes/presentations of the original data distribution, thus resulting in suboptimal performance. To overcome this challenge, several methods have been proposed to learn more informative representations, such as the aforementioned MoCo and SimCLR methods.

Contrastive methods are commonly used to create positive and negative pairs through the use of data augmentations and InfoNCE loss, which aims to align positive pairs while spreading negative pairs apart
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
. However, these methods often require comparisons between each image and many others to achieve optimal results. In more recent works such as BYOL
[
42
]
and SimSiam
[
43
]
, only positive pairs are used in the loss function, with a ”predictor” network learning to output predictions that align with the stop-gradient projections of an existing model. In contrast, SwAV
[
44
]
does not directly compare image features, but instead assigns augmentations from the same image to clusters and enforces consistency between these clusters.

In contrastive learning methods, positive samples are commonly generated through augmentation of the same images, while negative samples are chosen from the remaining data. However, an issue arises when negative samples sharing the same label as the anchor sample are included in the selection process. To address this issue, a partial solution has been proposed by Chuang et al.
[
13
]
, which introduces a distribution over the negative samples to correct potential biases and improve the quality of the learned representations. Building upon this work, Robinson et al.
[
14
]
have further improved the sampling process by incorporating the similarity between the negative samples and the anchor into the distribution, resulting in negative samples that are closer to the anchor and thus enhancing the effectiveness of the training process.

Robust SSL.

Machine learning models have demonstrated a concerning susceptibility to adversarial perturbations
[
46
,
5
]
. Although undetectable by human perception, these perturbations can lead to erroneous outcomes. Consequently, the vulnerability of machine learning models to such attacks poses a significant safety risk when applied to real-world systems.
One of the promising directions to tackle this issue is to develop a robust feature extractor
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
47
,
48
,
49
]
. By training a feature extractor capable of disregarding adversarial perturbations and generating representations similar to those of benign inputs, we can establish a strong foundation for building robust classifiers even with a simple linear classifier on top of the feature extractor.

[
10
,
11
,
12
]
parallelly proposed a robust self-supervised learning method that aims to robustify the feature extractor by using adversarial training with adversarial examples which maximize the InfoNCE loss. While sharing the same principle of maximizing feature consistency under different augmented views, three methods were different in how to construct the positive/negative sets.
In
[
10
]
, the adversarial examples were considered
as
positive samples in relation to the benign anchor.
On the other hand,
[
11
]
utilized two adversarial examples for each benign anchor, resulting doubled computation costs.
In the context of a supervised setting,
[
47
,
48
]
proposed a similar regularization technique which leverage both global information (i.e., class identity) and local information (i.e., adversarial/benign identity) to reduce the divergence between the representations of adversarial and benign examples.

Theory of SSL.
Driven by promising empirical results in contrastive learning, several studies have explored this learning paradigm from a theoretical perspective
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
. More specifically,
[
15
]
established a connection between supervised and unsupervised losses by using Rademacher complexity in the context of binary classification. Moreover,
[
16
]
examined the distribution of latent representations over the unit sphere and found that these representations tend to be uniformly distributed, encouraging alignment between positive examples and their anchors. Most recently,
[
17
]
relaxed the conditional independence assumption in
[
15
]
by introducing a milder condition, established a connection between supervised and unsupervised losses, and developed a new theory of augmentation overlap for contrastive learning.

3
Problem Formulation and Notions

In this section, we present the problem formulation of self-supervised learning and the notions used in our following theory development. We consider an
M
-class classification problem with the label set

\mathcal{Y}=\left\{1,2,...,M\right\}
. Given a class
c\in\mathcal{Y}
,
the class-condition distribution for this class has the density function

p_{c}\left(x\right)=p\left(x\mid y=c\right)
where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

specifies a data example. Therefore, the entire data distribution
has the form

p_{\text{data}}\left(x\right)=\sum_{c=1}^{M}\pi_{c}p\left(x\mid y=c\right)=\sum_{c=1}^{M}\pi_{c}p_{c}\left(x\right),

where
\pi_{c}=\mathbb{P}\left(y=c\right),c\in\mathcal{Y}
is a class
probability.

The distribution of positive pairs over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

is formulated as

p_{\text{pos}}\left(x,x^{+}\right)=\sum_{c=1}^{M}\pi_{c}p_{c}\left(x\right)p_{c}\left(x^{+}\right).

It is worth noting that with the above equality,
p_{\mathrm{pos}}\left(x,x^{+}\right)

is relevant to the probability that
x,x^{+}\sim p_{\text{data}}

possess the same label. Particularly, to form a positive pair
\left(x,x^{+}\right)
,
we first sample a class
c\sim\text{Cat}\left(\pi\right)
from the
categorical distribution with
\pi=\left[\pi_{c}\right]_{c=1}^{M}
,
and then sample
x,x^{+}\sim p_{c}
.

The general unsupervised InfoNCE loss over the entire data and positive
pair distributions is denoted as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{un}}}^{\text{un}}\left(\theta,p_{\text{pos}}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\left(x,x^{+}\right)\sim p_{\text{pos}},x_{1:K}^{-}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}p_{\text{data}}}\Biggl[-\log\frac{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x\right)\cdot f_{\theta}\left(x^{+}\right)}{\tau}\big\}}{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x\right)\cdot f_{\theta}\left(x^{+}\right)}{\tau}\big\}+\frac{\beta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x\right)\cdot f_{\theta}\left(x_{k}^{-}\right)}{\tau}\big\}}\Biggr],

(1)

where
f_{\theta}
with
\theta\in\Theta
is a feature extractor, the operation
f_{\theta}(x)\cdot f_{\theta}(\widetilde{x})
presents the inner product,
\tau>0

is the temperature variable,
K
is the number of used negative examples, and
\mathcal{D}_{\text{un}}
denotes the distribution over
z=\left[x,x^{+},\left[x_{k}^{-}\right]_{k=1}^{K}\right]

with
\left(x,x^{+}\right)\sim p_{\text{pos}},x_{1:K}^{-}\sim p_{\text{data}}
.
Note that
\beta\geq 0
is a parameter and setting
\beta=K
recovers
the original formula of contrastive learning.

It is our ultimate goal to minimize the general unsupervised InfoNCE loss.
However, in reality, we work with a specific training set
\mathcal{S}=\Big\{z_{i}=\Big[x_{i},x_{i}^{+},\big[x_{ik}^{-}\big]_{k=1}^{K}\Big]\Big\}_{i=1}^{N}

where
z_{1:N}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{un}}
. The empirical unsupervised InfoNCE
loss over
\mathcal{S}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\text{un}}\left(\theta,p_{\text{pos}}\right)=-\frac{1}{N}\times\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\frac{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x_{i}\right)\cdot f_{\theta}\left(x_{i}^{+}\right)}{\tau}\big\}}{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x_{i}\right)\cdot f_{\theta}\left(x_{i}^{+}\right)}{\tau}\big\}+\frac{\beta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x_{i}\right)\cdot f_{\theta}\left(x_{ik}^{-}\right)}{\tau}\big\}}.

SSL aims to minimize the empirical unsupervised InfoNCE loss
over a specific training set
\mathcal{S}
to learn an optimal feature extractor
f_{\theta^{*}}
, used in
the second phase for training a linear classifier on top of its extracted features. Given a feature extractor

f_{\theta}
and a linear classifier that parameterized by a weight matrix
W
,
we define the general loss induced by this couple as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{sup}}}^{\text{sup}}\left(\theta,W\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\left(x,y\right)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{sup}}}\left[\tau_{\text{CE}}\left(Wf_{\theta}\left(x\right),y\right)\right],

where
\mathcal{D}_{\text{sup}}\left(x,y\right)=\pi_{y}p_{y}\left(x\right)

is the data-label distribution and
\tau_{\text{CE}}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)

is the
\tau
-temperature cross-entropy loss (i.e., softmax with a temperature
\tau
applied to logits before computing the cross-entropy loss).

Given the fact that we aim to train the optimal linear classifier
in the second phase, we define the optimal general loss over all weight
matrices
W
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{sup}}}^{\text{sup}}\left(\theta\right)=\min_{W}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{sup}}}^{\text{sup}}\left(\theta,W\right).

We now present the losses for the robust SSL. Given an attack
\mathbf{a}
(i.e., FGSM
[
5
]
, PGD
[
6
]
, TRADES
[
7
]
, and AutoAttack
[
50
]
) and a weight matrix
W
, we denote
a_{W,\theta}(x)\in B_{\epsilon}(x)=\{x^{\prime}:\|x^{\prime}-x\|\leq\epsilon\}
as the adversarial example obtained by using
\mathbf{a}
to attack the second phase classifier, formed by
f_{\theta}
and
W
, on a benign example
x
with a label
y
. We define the adversarial loss using the adversary
\mathbf{a}
to attack
W
and
f_{\theta}
as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(W,\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}\left[\tau_{\mathrm{CE}}\left(Wf_{\theta}\left(a_{W,\theta}\left(x\right)\right),y\right)^{1/2}\right].

(2)

Our next step is to minimize the above adversarial loss to define the best adversarial loss corresponding to the most robust linear classifier when using the adversary
\mathbf{a}
to attack the classifier in the second phase:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)=\min_{W}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(W,\theta,\mathbf{a}\right).

(3)

Proposed Framework

4.1
Theory Development

We first develop necessary theories to tackle the optimization problem of the adversarial loss
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{adv}}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}(\theta,\mathbf{a})
. Let us denote
p_{\text{adv}}^{a_{W,\theta}}=a_{W,\theta}\#p_{\text{data}}
as the adversarial distribution formed by using the attack transformation
a_{W,\theta}
to transport the data distribution
p_{\text{data}}
. It is obvious by the definition that
p_{\text{adv}}^{a_{W,\theta}}
consists of adversarial examples
a_{W,\theta}(x)
with the benign example
x\sim p_{\text{data}}
. In addition, we define
q_{\text{data}}=f_{\theta}\#p_{\text{data}}
and
q_{\text{adv}}^{a_{W,\theta}}=f_{\theta}\#p^{a_{W,\theta}}_{\text{adv}}
as the benign and adversarial distributions on the latent space, respectively.

The following theorem indicates the upper bounds for the adversarial loss of interest
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{adv}}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}(\theta,\mathbf{a})
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Consider the adversarial loss
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{adv}}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}(\theta,\mathbf{a})
.

i) We have the first upper bound on the data space

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)\leq\min_{W}\Big\{\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{sup}}\left(\theta,W\right)^{1/2}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{sup}}\left(\theta,W\right)^{1/2}D_{v}\left(p_{\mathrm{adv}}^{a_{W,\theta}},p_{\mathrm{data}}\right)^{1/2}\Big\}.

(4)

ii) We have the second upper bound on the latent space

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)\leq\min_{W}\Big\{\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{sup}}\left(\theta,W\right)^{1/2}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{sup}}\left(\theta,W\right)^{1/2}D_{v}\left(q_{\mathrm{adv}}^{a_{W,\theta}},q_{\mathrm{data}}\right)^{1/2}\Big\}.

(5)

Here we note that
D_{v}
represents a
f
-divergence with the convex function
v(t)=(t-1)^{2}
.

Both bounds in Theorem
B.1
have the same form but they are defined on the data and latent spaces, respectively. Additionally, the upper bound in (
18
) reveals that to minimize the adversarial loss for the adversary
\mathbf{a}
, we need to search the feature extractor
f_{\theta}
and the weight matrix
W
for minimizing the supervised loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{sup}}\left(\theta,W\right)
and the divergence
D_{v}\left(q_{\text{adv}}^{a_{W,\theta}},q_{\text{data}}\right)
between the benign and adversarial distributions on the latent space.

We now go further to discover which losses should be involved in the first phase to minimize the upper bound in (
18
). To this end, we denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\left\{a\left(\cdot\mid x\right)\,\text{with\,$x\sim p_{\text{data}}$}:a\left(\cdot\mid x\right)\text{ has a support set over }B_{\epsilon}\left(x\right)\right\},

\displaystyle q^{a}

\displaystyle=f_{\theta}\#(a\#p_{\text{data}})\text{ for $a\in\mathcal{A}$}.

where
B_{\epsilon}(x)
specifies the ball with the radius
\epsilon
around
x
.
\mathcal{A}
consists of the stochastic map
a(\cdot\mid x)\text{ with }x\sim p_{\text{data}}
, working on the ball
B_{\epsilon}(x)
, which can be
considered as the set of all possible attacks. By its definition, the stochastic map
p_{\text{adv}}^{a_{W,\theta}}
can be represented by an element in
\mathcal{A}
as long as
a_{W,\theta}\in B_{\epsilon}(x)
. The following theorem assists us in converting the upper bound in (
18
) to the one directly in the phase 1.

Theorem 4.2
.

The adversarial loss
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{adv}}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}(\theta,\mathbf{a})
can be further upper-bounded by

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)\leq\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)+A\left(K,\beta\right)\right]^{1/2}\big[1+\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}D_{v}\left(q^{a},q_{\mathrm{data}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big],

(6)

where
A(K,\beta)=-O\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\big)-\log\beta-O\big(\frac{1}{\beta}\big)
.

Inequality (
26
) indicates that to achieve

\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)
, we can alternatively minimize its upper-bound which is relevant to

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta\right)
. Unfortunately,
minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p_{\text{pos}}\right)

directly is intractable due to the unknown general distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}
.
The following theorem resolves this issue and also signifies the concept
of sharpness for the feature extractor
f_{\theta}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Under mild conditions, with the probability at least
1-\delta
over
the random choice of
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}^{N}
, we have
the following inequality

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)\leq\max_{\theta^{\prime}:\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|<\rho}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Big[\frac{T}{2}\log\Big(1+\frac{\|{\theta}\|^{2}}{T\sigma^{2}}\Big)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{L^{2}}{8}+2L+O\big(\log(N+T)\big)\Big],

where
L=\frac{2}{\tau}+\log(1+\beta)
,
T
is the number of parameters
in
\theta
, and
\sigma=\rho[\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{\log\left(N\right)}]^{-1}
.

We note that the proof in
[
18
]
invoked the McAllester PAC-Bayesian generalization bound
[
51
]
, hence only applicable to the 0-1 loss in the binary classification setting. Ours is the first work that proposes and devises sharpness-aware theory for feature extractor in the context of SSL. Additionally, the proof of our theory employs the PAC-Bayesian generalization bound
[
52
]
to deal with the more general InfoNCE loss. Eventually, by leveraging Theorems
B.2
and
B.3
, we reach the following theorem.

Theorem 4.4
.

Under mild conditions, with the probability at least
1-\delta
over
the random choice of
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}^{N}
, we have the following inequality

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)\leq\Big[\max_{\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|\leq\rho}\mathcal{L}_{S}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)+B(T,N,\delta,\|\theta\|)+A\left(K,\beta\right)\Big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big[1+\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}D_{v}\left(q^{a},q_{\mathrm{data}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big],

(7)

where we have defined

B(T,N,\delta,\|\theta\|)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Big[\frac{T}{2}\log\Big(1+\frac{\|\theta\|^{2}}{T\sigma^{2}}\Big)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{L^{2}}{8}+2L+O\left(\log\left(N+T\right)\right)\Big].

Remark 4.5
.

Inequality (
34
) shows that minimizing the adversarial loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)
with respect to
\theta
requires minimizing the sharpness-aware unsupervised InfoNCE loss:
\max_{\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|\leq\rho}\mathcal{L}_{S}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta\right)
and the divergence:

\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}D_{v}\left(q^{a},q_{\mathrm{data}}\right)
. While the sharpness-aware unsupervised InfoNCE loss is straightforward, the divergence term
\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}D_{v}\left(q^{a},q_{\text{data}}\right)
requires further derivations, which will be clarified in the next section.

We now develop another upper bound for the adversarial loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)
. Given a stochastic map
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we define the hybrid positive
p_{\mathrm{pos}}^{a}(x,x^{+})
distribution induced by
a
as

p_{\text{pos}}^{a}(x,x^{+})=\sum_{c=1}^{M}\pi_{c}p_{c}\left(x\right)p_{c}^{a}\left(x^{+}\right),
where
p^{a}_{c}=a\#p_{c}
.

Additionally, by its definition, the hybrid positive distribution
p_{\text{pos}}^{a}(x,x^{+})
consists of the positive pairs of a benign example
x
with the label
c
and an adversarial example
x^{+}
formed by applying the stochastic map
a
to an another benign example
x^{\prime}
. The following theorem gives us another upper bound on the the adversarial loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)
.

Theorem 4.6
.

The adversarial loss
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{adv}}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}(\theta,\mathbf{a})
can be further upper-bounded by

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)\leq\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Big\{\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p^{a}_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)+\Big(1+\exp\big\{\frac{2}{\tau}\big\}\Big)D_{u}\left(q^{a},q_{\mathrm{data}}\right)\Big\}+A\left(K,\beta\right),

(8)

where
A(K,\beta)=-O\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\big)-\log\beta-O\big(\frac{1}{\beta}\big)
,
D_{u}
is a
f
-divergence with the convex function
u(t)=|t-1|
, and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p^{a}_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)
is similar to
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)
in Eq. (
14
) except that the positive pairs
(x,x^{+})\sim p^{a}_{\mathrm{pos}}
.

Remark 4.7
.

Theorem
B.6
indicates that we need to find the adversary
a
that maximizes the InfoNCE loss over the hybrid adversarial positive distribution
p^{a}_{\text{pos}}
and the divergence on the latent space
D_{u}(q^{a},q_{\text{data}})
. This allows us to create strong adversarial examples that cause high InfoNCE values (i.e.,
z^{a}=f_{\theta}(x_{a})
is locally far from
z=f_{\theta}(x)
), while the distribution of adversarial examples is globally far from the that of benign examples on the latent space. Eventually, the feature extractor
f_{\theta}
is updated to minimize both the local distances and the global divergence.

Additionally, the adversarial InfoNCE term
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p^{a}_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)
has the form of

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(f_{\theta},p_{\text{pos}}^{a}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\left(x,x^{+}\right)\sim p_{\text{pos}}^{a},x_{1:K}^{-}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}p_{\text{data}}}\Biggl[-\log\frac{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x\right).f_{\theta}\left(x^{+}\right)}{\tau}\big\}}{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x\right).f_{\theta}\left(a\left(x^{+}\right)\right)}{\tau}\big\}+\frac{\beta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x\right).f_{\theta}\left(x_{k}^{-}\right)}{\tau}\big\}}\Biggr]

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt=\mathbb{E}_{\left(x,x^{+}\right)\sim p_{\text{pos}},x_{1:K}^{-}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}p_{\text{data}}}\Biggl[-\log\frac{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x\right).f_{\theta}\left(a\left(x^{+}\right)\right)}{\tau}\big\}}{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x\right).f_{\theta}\left(x^{+}\right)}{\tau}\big\}+\frac{\beta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x\right).f_{\theta}\left(x_{k}^{-}\right)}{\tau}\big\}}\Biggr],

(9)

which means that given the positive pair
(x,x^{+})
having the same label, we find the adversarial example
a(x^{+})
for
x^{+}
that maximizes the InfoNCE loss as in Eq. (
39
). Note that according to Eq. (
35
), we actually need to seek adversarial examples
a(x^{+})
to globally maximize a
f
-divergence between the benign and adversarial distributions.

Leveraging the upper bounds in Theorems
B.4
and
B.6
, we arrive the final upper bound stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.8
.

For any
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
, the adversarial loss
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{adv}}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}(\theta,\mathbf{a})
can be further upper-bounded by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)\leq\lambda\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Big\{\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p^{a}_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)+\Big(1+\exp\big\{\frac{2}{\tau}\big\}\Big)D_{u}\left(q^{a},q_{\mathrm{data}}\right)\Big\}+\lambda A\left(K,\beta\right)

\displaystyle+(1-\lambda)\Big[\max_{\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|\leq\rho}\mathcal{L}_{S}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)+B(T,N,\delta,\|\theta\|)+A\left(K,\beta\right)\Big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big[1+\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}D_{v}\left(q^{a},q_{\mathrm{data}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big].

(10)

Remark 4.9
.

We summarize our theoretical findings as follows:

Minimizing the InfoNCE loss on the benign examples (i.e.,
\mathcal{L}_{S}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)
) assists us in lowering down the adversarial loss in the second phase. Moreover, the sharpness-aware minimization for the InfoNCE loss on the benign examples (i.e.,
\max_{\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|\leq\rho}\mathcal{L}_{S}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)
) also supports us in reducing the adversarial loss in the second phase.

Minimizing the InfoNCE loss on the adversarial examples formed by attacking the benign examples on the InfoNCE loss (i.e.,
\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p^{a}_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)
) helps to strengthen the adversarial robustness in the second phase. Specifically, according to our theory, the adversarial examples are found by maximizing the InfoNCE loss and the global divergence to their corresponding benign examples (i.e.,
\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left\{\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p^{a}_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)+\left(1+\exp\left\{\frac{2}{\tau}\right\}\right)D_{u}\left(q^{a},q_{\mathrm{data}}\right)\right\}+A\left(K,\beta\right)
. In our implementation, we relax
D_{u}
to the Jensen-Shannon divergence
D_{JS}
in order to use generative adversarial network (GAN)
[
53
]
for quantifying the global divergence.

4.2
Practical Method

This section explains how to harvest the developed theories to conduct a practical method for robust self-supervised learning. The main objective is to demonstrate that the finding terms from the theories assist us in improving the robustness of self-supervised learning.

Given a mini-batch
B=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{b}\}
of the anchor examples, we apply random transformations from a pool of transformation
\mathcal{T}
to create the corresponding positive pairs
(\tilde{x}_{1},\tilde{x}_{1}^{+})
,
(\tilde{x}_{2},\tilde{x}_{2}^{+})
,…,
(\tilde{x}_{b},\tilde{x}_{b}^{+})
where
\tilde{x}_{i}=t(x_{i})
and
\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}=t^{\prime}(x_{i})
with
t,t^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{T}
.

Hinted by Eq. (
39
), we formulate the adversarial InfoNCE loss term
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p^{a}_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)
as

\displaystyle\max_{\forall i:f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)_{a}\in B_{\epsilon}\left(f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)\right)}\bigg\{-\frac{1}{b}\times\sum_{i=1}^{b}\log\frac{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}\right).f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)_{a}}{\tau}\big\}}{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}\right).f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)_{a}}{\tau}\big\}+\frac{\beta}{K}\sum_{k\neq i}\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}\right).f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{k}\right)}{\tau}\big\}}\bigg\},

where we denote
f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)_{a}
as the adversarial example of
f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)
within its
\epsilon
-ball.

We relax
D_{u}\left(q^{a},q_{\mathrm{data}}\right)
to
D_{JS}\left(q^{a},q_{\mathrm{data}}\right)
so that GAN
[
53
]
can be applied to realize it. With the support of the discriminator
D
, the divergence of interest can be rewritten as
[
53
]

D_{JS}\left(q^{a},q_{\text{data}}\right)=\min_{\theta}\max_{D}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{b}\log D\left(f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{b}\log D\left(1-f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)_{a}\right)\Big\}+\text{const}.

(11)

Therefore, we can combine the local and global views to generate adversarial examples as

\displaystyle\max_{\forall i:f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)_{a}\in B_{\epsilon}\left(f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)\right)}

\displaystyle\bigg\{-\frac{1}{b}\times\sum_{i=1}^{b}\log\frac{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}\right).f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)_{a}}{\tau}\big\}}{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}\right).f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)_{a}}{\tau}\big\}+\frac{\beta}{K}\sum_{k\neq i}\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}\right).f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{k}\right)}{\tau}\big\}}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{\text{global}}

\displaystyle\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{b}\log D\left(f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{b}\log D\left(1-f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)_{a}\right)\bigg\}\bigg\},

(12)

where
\lambda_{\text{global}}\geq 0
is a parameter to trade off between the local and global views. Note that in (
12
), we indeed push the batch adversarial examples
f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)_{a}
globally far away from the batch of benign examples
f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}\right)
by maximizing the log-likelihood.

Moreover, the discriminator
D
is updated to distinguish the batch adversarial examples
f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)_{a}
and the batch of benign examples
f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}\right)
by maximizing the log-likelihood as in (
11
). Moreover, given the batch of adversarial examples, we update the feature extractor
f_{\theta}
as

\min_{\theta}\bigg\{\frac{-1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\log\frac{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}\right).f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)_{a}}{\tau}\big\}}{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}\right).f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)_{a}}{\tau}\big\}+\frac{\beta}{K}\sum_{k\neq i}\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}\right).f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{k}\right)}{\tau}\big\}}+\lambda_{\text{global}}\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{b}\log D\left(f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)\right)\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{b}\log D(1-f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)_{a})\bigg\}-\frac{\lambda_{\text{benign}}}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\log\frac{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}\right).f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+}\right)}{\tau}\big\}}{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}(\tilde{x}_{i}).f_{\theta}(\tilde{x}_{i}^{+})}{\tau}\big\}+\frac{\beta}{K}\sum_{k\neq i}\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}\right).f_{\theta}\left(\tilde{x}_{k}\right)}{\tau}\big\}}\bigg\},

(13)

where we incorporate the InfoNCE loss on the benign examples with the trade-off parameter
\lambda_{\text{benign}}
. Note that we minimize the log-likelihood term w.r.t.
\theta
to move the batch of adversarial examples closer to the batch of benign examples.

Finally, we apply sharpness-aware minimization (SAM)
[
18
]
to the InfoNCE loss on the benign examples. The only difference in computation is that we find the perturbed model
\theta^{a}
that maximizes the InfoNCE loss on the benign examples and use the gradient at
\theta^{a}
for this term when updating
\theta
in a stochastic gradient descent manner as in (
13
). It is worth noting that the first two terms in (
13
) are optimized normally used their gradients at
\theta
.

For the contrastive learning process, we follow the settings generally used in existing works

[
18

6

for final classification evaluation.

6
Discussion

6.1
Broader Impact

Our surprising result on the effect of weighting benign loss term, to the best of our knowledge, is a first. As we combine these losses and receive improvements on both clean and robust accuracy, it is counter-intuitive to the existing notion that there always exist a tradeoff between the two values. Delving into this phenomenon could be an interesting future research direction, which would help us further understand the nature of adversarial vulnerabilities.

6.2
Limitations

As with any adversarial training process, the time needed to train a model increases significantly. This additional cost is doubled if we really want to squeeze out the highest accuracy, by regenerating adversarial examples right before training our discriminator instead of reusing which was used to train the feature extractor. Furthermore, a GAN-style training is prone to convergence issues. However, we have not encountered such problems, possibly due to the extremely simple discriminator used.

7
Conclusion

Contrastive learning (CL) is a powerful technique that enables the creation of meaningful features without relying on labeled information. In the initial phase of CL, the focus lies on learning these features, which are subsequently classified using a linear classifier trained on labeled data. Although previous theoretical studies have explored the relationship between the supervised loss in the second phase and the unsupervised loss in the first phase, aiming to explain how the unsupervised loss supports the supervised loss, no theoretical investigation has examined the connection between the unsupervised loss in the first phase and the
robust
supervised loss in the second phase. Such an analysis can provide valuable insights into establishing an effective unsupervised loss in the initial phase. To address this research gap, our paper develops rigorous theories to identify the specific components within the supervised loss that contribute to the robustness of the supervised loss. Lastly, we conduct a series of experiments to validate our theoretical findings.

Appendix A
Problem Formulation and Notions

In this section, we present the problem formulation of self-supervised learning and the notions used in our following theory development. We consider an
M
-class classification problem with the label set

\mathcal{Y}=\left\{1,2,...,M\right\}
. Given a class
c\in\mathcal{Y}
,
the class-condition distribution for this class has the density function

p_{c}\left(x\right)=p\left(x\mid y=c\right)
where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

specifies a data example. Therefore, the entire data distribution
has the form

p_{\text{data}}\left(x\right)=\sum_{c=1}^{M}\pi_{c}p\left(x\mid y=c\right)=\sum_{c=1}^{M}\pi_{c}p_{c}\left(x\right),

where
\pi_{c}=\mathbb{P}\left(y=c\right),c\in\mathcal{Y}
is a class
probability.

The distribution of positive pairs over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

is formulated as

p_{\text{pos}}\left(x,x^{+}\right)=\sum_{c=1}^{M}\pi_{c}p_{c}\left(x\right)p_{c}\left(x^{+}\right).

It is worth noting that with the above equality,
p_{\mathrm{pos}}\left(x,x^{+}\right)

is relevant to the probability that
x,x^{+}\sim p_{\text{data}}

possess the same label. Particularly, to form a positive pair
\left(x,x^{+}\right)
,
we first sample a class
c\sim\text{Cat}\left(\pi\right)
from the
categorical distribution with
\pi=\left[\pi_{c}\right]_{c=1}^{M}
,
and then sample
x,x^{+}\sim p_{c}
.

The general unsupervised InfoNCE loss over the entire data and positive
pair distributions is denoted as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{un}}}^{\text{un}}\left(\theta,p_{\text{pos}}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\left(x,x^{+}\right)\sim p_{\text{pos}},x_{1:K}^{-}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}p_{\text{data}}}\Biggl[-\log\frac{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x\right)\cdot f_{\theta}\left(x^{+}\right)}{\tau}\big\}}{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x\right)\cdot f_{\theta}\left(x^{+}\right)}{\tau}\big\}+\frac{\beta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x\right)\cdot f_{\theta}\left(x_{k}^{-}\right)}{\tau}\big\}}\Biggr],

(14)

where
f_{\theta}
with
\theta\in\Theta
is a feature extractor, the operation
f_{\theta}(x)\cdot f_{\theta}(\widetilde{x})
presents the inner product,
\tau>0

is the temperature variable,
K
is the number of used negative examples, and
\mathcal{D}_{\text{un}}
denotes the distribution over
z=\left[x,x^{+},\left[x_{k}^{-}\right]_{k=1}^{K}\right]

with
\left(x,x^{+}\right)\sim p_{\text{pos}},x_{1:K}^{-}\sim p_{\text{data}}
.
Note that
\beta\geq 0
is a parameter and setting
\beta=K
recovers
the original formula of contrastive learning.

It is our ultimate goal to minimize the general unsupervised InfoNCE loss.
However, in reality, we work with a specific training set
\mathcal{S}=\Big\{z_{i}=\Big[x_{i},x_{i}^{+},\big[x_{ik}^{-}\big]_{k=1}^{K}\Big]\Big\}_{i=1}^{N}

where
z_{1:N}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{un}}
. The empirical unsupervised InfoNCE
loss over
\mathcal{S}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\text{un}}\left(\theta,p_{\text{pos}}\right)=-\frac{1}{N}\times\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\frac{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x_{i}\right)\cdot f_{\theta}\left(x_{i}^{+}\right)}{\tau}\big\}}{\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x_{i}\right)\cdot f_{\theta}\left(x_{i}^{+}\right)}{\tau}\big\}+\frac{\beta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\big\{\frac{f_{\theta}\left(x_{i}\right)\cdot f_{\theta}\left(x_{ik}^{-}\right)}{\tau}\big\}}.

SSL aims to minimize the empirical unsupervised InfoNCE loss
over a specific training set
\mathcal{S}
to learn an optimal feature extractor
f_{\theta^{*}}
, used in
the second phase for training a linear classifier on top of its extracted features. Given a feature extractor

f_{\theta}
and a linear classifier that parameterized by a weight matrix
W
,
we define the general loss induced by this couple as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{sup}}}^{\text{sup}}\left(\theta,W\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\left(x,y\right)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{sup}}}\left[\tau_{\text{CE}}\left(Wf_{\theta}\left(x\right),y\right)\right],

where
\mathcal{D}_{\text{sup}}\left(x,y\right)=\pi_{y}p_{y}\left(x\right)

is the data-label distribution and
\tau_{\text{CE}}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)

is the
\tau
-temperature cross-entropy loss (i.e., softmax with a temperature
\tau
applied to logits before computing the cross-entropy loss).

Given the fact that we aim to train the optimal linear classifier
in the second phase, we define the optimal general loss over all weight
matrices
W
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{sup}}}^{\text{sup}}\left(\theta\right)=\min_{W}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{sup}}}^{\text{sup}}\left(\theta,W\right).

We now present the losses for the robust SSL. Given an attack
\mathbf{a}
(i.e., FGSM
[
5
]
, PGD
[
6
]
, TRADES
[
7
]
, and AutoAttack
[
50
]
) and a weight matrix
W
, we denote
a_{W,\theta}(x)\in B_{\epsilon}(x)=\{x^{\prime}:\|x^{\prime}-x\|\leq\epsilon\}
as the adversarial example obtained by using
\mathbf{a}
to attack the second phase classifier, formed by
f_{\theta}
and
W
, on a benign example
x
with a label
y
. We define the adversarial loss using the adversary
\mathbf{a}
to attack
W
and
f_{\theta}
as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(W,\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}\left[\tau_{\mathrm{CE}}\left(Wf_{\theta}\left(a_{W,\theta}\left(x\right)\right),y\right)^{1/2}\right].

(15)

Our next step is to minimize the above adversarial loss to define the best adversarial loss corresponding to the most robust linear classifier when using the adversary
\mathbf{a}
to attack the classifier in the second phase:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)=\min_{W}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(W,\theta,\mathbf{a}\right).

(16)

Theory Development

We first develop necessary theories to tackle the optimization problem of the adversarial loss
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{adv}}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}(\theta,\mathbf{a})

p_{\text{adv}}^{a_{W,\theta}}=a_{W,\theta}\#p_{\text{data}}
as the adversarial distribution formed by using the attack transformation

to transport the data distribution
p_{\text{data}}

p_{\text{adv}}^{a_{W,\theta}}
consists of adversarial examples

q_{\text{data}}=f_{\theta}\#p_{\text{data}}
and
q_{\text{adv}}^{a_{W,\theta}}=f_{\theta}\#p^{a_{W,\theta}}_{\text{adv}}
as the benign and adversarial distributions on the latent space, respectively.

The following theorem indicates the upper bounds for the adversarial loss of interest
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{adv}}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}(\theta,\mathbf{a})

Theorem B.1
.

\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{adv}}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}(\theta,\mathbf{a})

i) We have the first upper bound on the data space

(17)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)\leq\min_{W}\Big\{\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{sup}}\left(\theta,W\right)^{1/2}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{sup}}\left(\theta,W\right)^{1/2}D_{v}\left(q_{\mathrm{adv}}^{a_{W,\theta}},q_{\mathrm{data}}\right)^{1/2}\Big\}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x^{-}}\Big[\exp\Big\{\frac{f_{\theta}(x)\cdot f_{\theta}(x^{-})}{\tau}\Big\}\Big]

\displaystyle:=\alpha_{x}

\displaystyle\exp\Big\{\frac{f_{\theta}(x)\cdot f_{\theta}(x^{+})}{\tau}\Big\}

\displaystyle:=Z.

The inner part of the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{(x,x^{+})\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}},x_{1:K}^{-}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}
is written as

\displaystyle\log\big(\beta\alpha_{x}\big)-\log\big(\beta(Y_{n}+\alpha_{x})+Z\big)

\displaystyle=-\log\frac{\beta Y_{n}+\beta\alpha_{x}+Z}{\beta\alpha_{x}}=-\log\Big(1+\frac{Z}{\beta\alpha_{x}}+\frac{Y_{n}}{\alpha_{x}}\Big)

\displaystyle=-\log\big(1+\frac{Z}{\beta\alpha_{x}}\big)-\log\Big(1+\frac{Y_{n}/\alpha_{x}}{1+Z/(\beta\alpha_{x})}\Big)

Thus, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(x,x^{+})\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}},x_{1:K}^{-}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}\Big[-\log\big(1+\frac{Z}{\beta\alpha_{x}}\big)-\log\Big(1+\frac{Y_{n}/\alpha_{x}}{1+Z/(\beta\alpha_{x})}\Big)\Big]=\mathbb{E}_{(x,x^{+})\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}}}\Big[-\log\big(1+\frac{Z}{\beta\alpha_{x}}\big)\Big]+

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(x,x^{+})\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}},x_{1:K}^{-}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}\Big[-\log\Big(1+\frac{Y_{n}/\alpha_{x}}{1+Z/(\beta\alpha_{x})}\Big)\Big].

We also have
\exp(-1/\tau)\leq\exp\Big\{\frac{f_{\theta}(x)\cdot f_{\theta}(x^{-})}{\tau}\Big\}\leq\exp(1/\tau)
. It follows that
\exp(-1/\tau)\leq Z,\alpha_{x}\leq\exp(1/\tau)
. Then we deduce bounds for other quantities

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\exp(-2/\tau)\leq\frac{Z}{\beta\alpha_{x}}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\exp(1/\tau)\exp(1/\tau)=\frac{1}{\beta}\exp(2/\tau)

\displaystyle\exp(-2/\tau)\leq\frac{Y_{n}+\alpha_{x}}{\alpha_{x}}\leq\exp(2/\tau)

\displaystyle\exp(-2/\tau)-1\leq\frac{Y_{n}}{\alpha_{x}}\leq\exp(2/\tau)-1

\displaystyle\exp(-2/\tau)-1\leq\frac{Y_{n}}{\alpha_{x}}+\frac{Z}{\beta\alpha_{x}}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\exp(2/\tau)+\exp(2/\tau)-1

\displaystyle\frac{Y_{n}/\alpha_{x}}{1+Z/(\beta\alpha_{x})}\geq\frac{\exp(-2/\tau)-1}{1+\frac{1}{\beta}\exp(-2/\tau)}

In both terms inside the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{(x,x^{+})\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}},x_{1:K}^{-}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}
, we have the form
\log(1+t)
.

For
t>0
, we use the inequality

\displaystyle\log(1+t)\leq t.

For
-1<t<0
we have

\displaystyle\big|\log(1+t)\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\log\frac{1}{1-|t|}\big|=\big|\log(1+|t|+|t|^{2}+\ldots)\big|\leq|t|+|t|^{2}+\ldots+|t|^{m}+\ldots=|t|\frac{1}{1-|t|}.

Use these inequalities for
\log(1+t)
, we get

\displaystyle\Big|-\log\Big(1+\frac{Y_{n}/\alpha_{x}}{1+Z/(\beta\alpha_{x})}\Big)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\frac{Y_{n}/\alpha_{x}}{1+Z/(\beta\alpha_{x})}\Big|\times\frac{1}{1+\frac{\exp(-2/\tau)-1}{1+\frac{1}{\beta}\exp(-2/\tau)}}=\Big|\frac{Y_{n}/\alpha_{x}}{1+Z/(\beta\alpha_{x})}\Big|\times\frac{1+\frac{1}{\beta}\exp(-2/\tau)}{(1+1/\beta)\exp(-2/\tau)}

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\frac{Y_{n}/\alpha_{x}}{1+Z/(\beta\alpha_{x})}\Big|\exp(2/\tau).

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:K}^{-}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}|Y_{n}|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:K}^{-}}[Y_{n}^{2}]}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\exp(2/\tau)-\exp(-2/\tau)}{K}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{(x,x^{+})\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}},x_{1:K}^{-}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}\Big[-\log\Big(1+\frac{Y_{n}/\alpha_{x}}{1+Z/(\beta\alpha_{x})}\Big)\Big]\right|\leq\mathbb{E}_{(x,x^{+})\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}}}\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:K}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}\Big|\frac{Y_{n}/\alpha_{x}}{1+Z/(\beta\alpha_{x})}\Big|\times\exp(2/\tau)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}\exp(2/\tau)\times\sqrt{\frac{\exp(2/\tau)-\exp(-2/\tau)}{K}}=O(K^{-1/2}).

For the other term inside
\mathbb{E}_{(x,x^{+})\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}},x_{1:K}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(x,x^{+})\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}}}\Big[-\log\big(1+\frac{Z}{\beta\alpha_{x}}\big)\Big]

is a constant and negative, its absolute value is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(x,x^{+})\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}}}\Big[\log\big(1+\frac{Z}{\beta\alpha_{x}}\big)\Big]\leq\mathbb{E}_{(x,x^{+})\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}}}\frac{Z}{\beta\alpha_{x}}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\exp(2/\tau).

For a lower bound of
\log(1+t)
, we have

\displaystyle e^{\frac{1}{1+t}-1}\geq\frac{1}{1+t}\Rightarrow e^{\frac{1}{t+1}}\geq\frac{e}{1+t}\Rightarrow(1+t)e^{\frac{1}{1+t}}\geq e\Rightarrow(1+t)\geq e^{\frac{t}{1+t}}\Rightarrow\log(1+t)\geq\frac{t}{1+t}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(x,x+)\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}}}\Big[\log\big(1+\frac{Z}{\beta\alpha_{x}}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{(x,x+)\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}}}\Big[\frac{Z/(\beta\alpha_{x})}{1+Z/(\beta\alpha_{x})}\Big]\geq\mathbb{E}_{(x,x+)\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}}}\Big[\frac{Z}{\beta\alpha_{x}}\Big]\times\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{\beta}\exp(2/\tau)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\exp(-2\tau)}{1+\frac{1}{\beta}\exp(2/\tau)}.

Together, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{un}}}^{\text{un}}(\theta,p_{pos})-\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{un}}}^{\text{un}}(\theta,p_{pos})=\mathbb{E}_{(x,x^{+})\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}}}\Big[\log\big(1+\frac{Z}{\beta\alpha_{x}}\big)\Big]+O\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\big)=O\big(\frac{1}{\beta}\big)+O\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\big).

Last step:
We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{sup}}(\theta,\bar{W})\geq\inf_{W}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{sup}}(\theta,W)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{sup}}(\theta).

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{sup}}(\theta)\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}(\theta,p_{pos})-O\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\Big)-\log(\beta)-O\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}\Big).

(2)
It appears that

D_{v}\left(q_{\text{adv}}^{a_{\bar{W},\theta}},q_{\text{data}}\right)\leq\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}D_{v}\left(q^{a},q_{\text{data}}\right),

(31)

because
a_{\bar{W},\theta}\in\mathcal{A}
.

By combining (
27
), (
28
), and (
31
), we reach the conclusion.

∎

Inequality (
26
) indicates that to achieve

\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)
, we can alternatively minimize its upper-bound which is relevant to

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta\right)
. Unfortunately,
minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p_{\text{pos}}\right)

directly is intractable due to the unknown general distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}
.
The following theorem resolves this issue and also signifies the concept
of sharpness for the feature extractor
f_{\theta}
.

Theorem B.3
.

Under mild conditions, with the probability at least
1-\delta
over
the random choice of
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}^{N}
, we have
the following inequality

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)\leq\max_{\theta^{\prime}:\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|<\rho}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta^{\prime}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Big[\frac{T}{2}\log\Big(1+\frac{\|{\theta}\|^{2}}{T\sigma^{2}}\Big)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{L^{2}}{8}+2L+O\big(\log(N+T)\big)\Big],

where
L=\frac{2}{\tau}+\log(1+\beta)
,
T
is the number of parameters
in
\theta
, and
\sigma=\rho[\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{\log\left(N\right)}]^{-1}
.

Proof.

Given
z=\Big[x,x^{+},\big[x_{k}^{-}\big]_{k=1}^{K}\Big]
where
(x,x^{+})\sim p_{\mathrm{pos}}
,
x_{1:K}^{-}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}p_{\mathrm{data}}
. We recall the loss function is

\displaystyle\ell(f_{\theta}(x))

\displaystyle=-\log\frac{\exp\Big\{\frac{f_{\theta}(x)\cdot f_{\theta}(x^{+})}{\tau}\Big\}}{\exp\Big\{\frac{f_{\theta}(x)\cdot f_{\theta}(x^{+})}{\tau}\Big\}+\frac{\beta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\Big\{\frac{f_{\theta}(x)\cdot f_{\theta}(x_{k}^{-})}{\tau}\Big\}}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\exp\Big\{\frac{f_{\theta}(x)\cdot f_{\theta}(x^{+})}{\tau}\Big\}+\frac{\beta}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\Big\{\frac{f_{\theta}(x)\cdot f_{\theta}(x_{k}^{-})}{\tau}\Big\}}{\exp\Big\{\frac{f_{\theta}(x)\cdot f_{\theta}(x^{+})}{\tau}\Big\}}

\displaystyle\leq\log\frac{e^{1/\tau}+\beta e^{1/\tau}}{e^{-1/\tau}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\tau}+\log(1+\beta).

We use the PAC-Bayes theory for
P=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{P}^{2}\mathbb{I}_{T})
and
Q=\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2}\mathbb{I}_{T})
are the prior and posterior distributions, respectively.

By using the bound in
[
52
]
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}(\theta,Q)\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{un}}(\theta,Q)+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big[\mathsf{KL}(Q\|P)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\Psi(\beta,N)\Big],

where we have defined

\displaystyle\Psi(\beta,N)=\log\mathbb{E}_{P}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}^{N}}\Big[\exp\Big\{\beta\big(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}(\theta)-\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{un}}(\theta)\big)\Big\}\Big]

Since the loss function is bounded by
L
, we have

\displaystyle\Psi(\beta,N)\leq\frac{\beta^{2}L^{2}}{8N}.

Thus, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}(\theta,Q)\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{un}}(\theta,Q)+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big[\mathsf{KL}(Q\|P)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{\beta^{2}L^{2}}{8N}\Big].

(32)

By Cauchy inequality,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\Big[\mathsf{KL}(Q\|P)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{\beta^{2}L^{2}}{8N}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\Big[\mathsf{KL}(Q\|P)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\Big]+\frac{\beta L^{2}}{8N}\geq\frac{\sqrt{\mathsf{KL}(Q\|P)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}}}{\sqrt{2N}}\times L

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sqrt{\mathsf{KL}(Q\|P)}}{\sqrt{2N}}\times L.

(33)

Since
P
and
Q
are Gaussian distribution, the KL divergence between
Q
and
P
is equal to

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}(Q\|P)=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\frac{T\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}}{\sigma_{P}^{2}}-T+T\log\frac{\sigma_{P}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big],

where
T
is the number of coordinate of
\theta
. Let us consider the KL divergence term
\mathsf{KL}(Q\|P)
as a function of
\sigma_{P}^{2}
, then its derivative with respect to
\sigma_{P}^{2}
is equal to

\displaystyle T\frac{1}{\sigma_{P}^{2}}-\frac{T\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}}{\sigma_{P}^{4}}=\frac{T}{\sigma_{P}^{2}}\Big[1-\frac{\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}/T}{\sigma_{P}^{2}}\Big],

which is equal to zero when
\sigma_{P}^{2}=\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}/T
. Thus

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}(Q\|P)\geq\frac{T}{2}\log\big(1+\frac{\|\theta\|^{2}}{T\sigma^{2}}\big).

Together with (
33
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\Big[\mathsf{KL}(Q\|P)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{\beta^{2}L^{2}}{8N}\Big]\geq\frac{L}{\sqrt{2N}}\sqrt{\frac{T}{2}\log\big(1+\frac{\|\theta\|^{2}}{T\sigma^{2}}\big)}\geq L.

when
\|\theta\|^{2}\geq T\sigma^{2}\Big[\exp\frac{4N}{T}-1\Big]
. Since the loss function
\ell
is bounded by
L
, if
\|\theta\|^{2}\geq T\sigma^{2}\Big[\exp\frac{4N}{T}-1\Big]
, then the RHS of (
32
) is already greater than
L
. Therefore, we only need to consider the case that
\|\theta\|^{2}\leq T\sigma^{2}\Big[\exp\frac{4N}{T}-1\Big]
.

We need to specify
P
in advance, since it is a prior distribution. However, we do not know in advance the value of
\theta
that affect the KL divergence term. Hence, we build a family of distribution
P
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}=\Big\{P_{j}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{P_{j}}^{2}\mathbb{I}_{T}):\sigma_{P_{j}}^{2}=c\exp\big(\frac{1-j}{T}\big),c=\sigma^{2}\big(1+\exp\frac{4N}{T}\big),j=1,2,\ldots\Big\}

Set
\delta_{j}=\frac{6\delta}{\pi^{2}j^{2}}
, the below inequality holds with probability at least
1-\delta_{j}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}(\theta,Q)\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{un}}(\theta,Q)+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big[\mathsf{KL}(Q\|P_{j})+\log\frac{1}{\delta_{j}}+\frac{\beta^{2}L^{2}}{8N}\Big].

Thus, with probability
1-\delta
the above inequalities hold for all
P_{j}
. We choose

\displaystyle j^{*}=\left\lfloor 1+T\log\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}\big(1+\exp\{4N/T\}\big)}{\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}/T}\right)\right\rfloor.

Since
\frac{\|\theta\|^{2}}{T}\leq\sigma^{2}\big[\exp\frac{4N}{T}-1\big]
, we get
\sigma^{2}+\frac{\|\theta\|^{2}}{T}\leq\sigma^{2}\exp\frac{4N}{T}
, thus
j^{*}
is well-defined. We also have

\displaystyle T\log\frac{c}{\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}/T}\leq j^{*}\leq 1+T\log\frac{c}{\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}/T}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle\log\frac{c}{\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}/T}\leq\frac{j^{*}}{T}\leq\frac{1}{T}+\log\frac{c}{\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}/T}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{T}+\log\frac{\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}/T}{c}\leq\frac{-j^{*}}{T}\leq\log\frac{\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}/T}{c}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle e^{-1/T}\frac{\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}/T}{c}\leq e^{-j^{*}/T}\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}/T}{c}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}+\frac{\|\theta\|^{2}}{T}\leq ce^{\frac{1-j^{*}}{T}}\leq e^{\frac{1}{T}}\Big(\sigma^{2}+\frac{\|\theta\|^{2}}{T}\Big)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}+\frac{\|\theta\|^{2}}{T}\leq\sigma_{P_{j^{*}}}^{2}\leq e^{\frac{1}{T}}\Big(\sigma^{2}+\frac{\|\theta\|^{2}}{T}\Big).

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}(Q\|P_{j^{*}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\frac{T\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}}{\sigma_{P_{j^{*}}}^{2}}-T+T\log\frac{\sigma_{Pj^{*}}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big]\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big[\frac{T\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}/T}-T+T\log\frac{e^{1/T}\big(\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}/T\big)}{\sigma^{2}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big[1+T\log\big(1+\frac{\|\theta\|^{2}}{T\sigma^{2}}\big)\Big].

For the term
\log\frac{1}{\delta_{j^{*}}}
, use the inequality
\log(1+e^{t})\leq 1+t
for
t>0
,

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{\delta_{j^{*}}}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{(j^{*})^{2}\pi^{2}}{6\delta}=\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\Big(\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\Big)+2\log(j^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}+2\log\Big(1+T\log\frac{\sigma^{2}\big(1+\exp(4N/T)\big)}{\sigma^{2}+\|\theta\|^{2}/T}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}+2\log\Big(1+T\log\big(1+\exp(4N/T)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}+2\log\Big(1+T\big(1+\frac{4N}{T}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}+\log(1+T+4N).

Choosing
\beta=\sqrt{N}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\Big[\mathsf{KL}(Q\|P_{j^{*}})+\log\frac{1}{\delta_{j^{*}}}+\frac{\beta^{2}L^{2}}{8N}\Big]\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Big[\frac{1}{2}+\frac{T}{2}\log\Big(1+\frac{\|\theta\|^{2}}{T\sigma^{2}}\Big)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}+6\log(N+T)\Big]+\frac{L^{2}}{8\sqrt{N}}

Since
\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|^{2}
is
T
chi-square distribution, for any positive
t
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|^{2}-T\sigma^{2}\geq 2\sigma^{2}\sqrt{Tt}+2t\sigma^{2}\big)\big)\leq\exp(-t).

By choosing
t=\frac{1}{2}\log(N)
, with probability
1-N^{-1/2}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\log(N)+T\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{2}\sqrt{2T\log(N)}\leq T\sigma^{2}\Big(1+\sqrt{\frac{\log(N)}{T}}\Big)^{2}.

By setting
\sigma=\rho\times\big(\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{\log(N)}\big)^{-1}
, we have
\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|^{2}\leq\rho^{2}
. Hence, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}\Big(\theta^{\prime},\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2}\mathbb{I}_{T})\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2}\mathbb{I}_{T})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}}\big[f_{\theta^{\prime}}\big]=\int_{\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|\leq\rho}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}}\big[f_{\theta^{\prime}}\big]d\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2}\mathbb{I}_{T})+\int_{\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|>\rho}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}}\big[f_{\theta^{\prime}}\big]d\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2}\mathbb{I}_{T})

\displaystyle\leq\Big(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Big)\max_{\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|\leq\rho}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}(\theta^{\prime})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}L

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|_{2}\leq\rho}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}(\theta^{\prime})+\frac{2L}{\sqrt{N}}.

Together,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}(\theta,p_{\mathrm{pos}})\leq\max_{\theta^{\prime}:\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|<\rho}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{un}}(\theta,p_{\mathrm{pos}})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Bigg[\frac{T}{2}\log\Big(1+\frac{\|\theta\|^{2}}{T\sigma^{2}}\Big)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}+O\Big(\log(N+T)\Big)+\frac{L^{2}}{8}+2L\Bigg].

∎

We note that the proof in
[
18
]
invoked the McAllester PAC-Bayesian generalization bound
[
51
]
, hence only applicable to the 0-1 loss in the binary classification setting. Ours is the first work that proposes and devises sharpness-aware theory for feature extractor in the context of SSL. Additionally, the proof of our theory employs the PAC-Bayesian generalization bound
[
52
]
to deal with the more general InfoNCE loss. Eventually, by leveraging Theorems
B.2
and
B.3
, we reach the following theorem.

Theorem B.4
.

Under mild conditions, with the probability at least
1-\delta
over
the random choice of
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}^{N}
, we have the following inequality

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)\leq\Big[\max_{\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|\leq\rho}\mathcal{L}_{S}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta^{\prime},p_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)+B(T,N,\delta,\|\theta\|)+A\left(K,\beta\right)\Big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big[1+\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}D_{v}\left(q^{a},q_{\mathrm{data}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big],

(34)

where we have defined

B(T,N,\delta,\|\theta\|)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Big[\frac{T}{2}\log\Big(1+\frac{\|\theta\|^{2}}{T\sigma^{2}}\Big)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{L^{2}}{8}+2L+O\left(\log\left(N+T\right)\right)\Big].

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of Theorems
B.2
and
B.3
.
∎

Remark B.5
.

Inequality (
34
) shows that minimizing the adversarial loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)
with respect to
\theta
requires minimizing the sharpness-aware unsupervised InfoNCE loss:
\max_{\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|\leq\rho}\mathcal{L}_{S}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta\right)
and the divergence:

\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}D_{v}\left(q^{a},q_{\mathrm{data}}\right)
. While the sharpness-aware unsupervised InfoNCE loss is straightforward, the divergence term
\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}D_{v}\left(q^{a},q_{\text{data}}\right)
requires further derivations, which will be clarified in the next section.

We now develop another upper bound for the adversarial loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)
. Given a stochastic map
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we define the hybrid positive
p_{\mathrm{pos}}^{a}(x,x^{+})
distribution induced by
a
as

p_{\text{pos}}^{a}(x,x^{+})=\sum_{c=1}^{M}\pi_{c}p_{c}\left(x\right)p_{c}^{a}\left(x^{+}\right),
where
p^{a}_{c}=a\#p_{c}
.

Additionally, by its definition, the hybrid positive distribution
p_{\text{pos}}^{a}(x,x^{+})
consists of the positive pairs of a benign example
x
with the label
c
and an adversarial example
x^{+}
formed by applying the stochastic map
a
to an another benign example
x^{\prime}
. The following theorem gives us another upper bound on the the adversarial loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)
.

Theorem B.6
.

The adversarial loss
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{adv}}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}(\theta,\mathbf{a})
can be further upper-bounded by

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sup}}}^{\mathrm{adv}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)\leq\left[\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left\{\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{un}}^{un}\left(\theta,p_{\text{pos}}^{a}\right)+\left(\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\tau}\right\}+\frac{1}{\tau}\right)D_{u}\left(q^{a},q_{\text{data}}\right)\right\}+A\left(K,\beta\right)\right]^{1/2},

(35)

where
A(K,\beta)=-O\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\big)-\log\beta-O\big(\frac{1}{\beta}\big)
,
D_{u}
is a
f
-divergence with the convex function
u(t)=|t-1|
, and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p^{a}_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)
is similar to
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{un}}}^{\mathrm{un}}\left(\theta,p_{\mathrm{pos}}\right)
in Eq. (
14
) except that the positive pairs
(x,x^{+})\sim p^{a}_{\mathrm{pos}}
.

Proof.

We start with

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{sup}}}^{\text{adv}}\left(W,\theta,\mathbf{a}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{sup}}}\left[\tau_{\mathrm{CE}}\left(Wf_{\theta}\left(a_{W,\theta}\left(x\right)\right),y\right)^{1/2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\tau_{\mathrm{CE}}\left(Wf_{\theta}\left(a_{W,\theta}\left(x\right)\right),y\right)^{1/2}p_{\text{data}}\left(x,y\right)dxdy

\displaystyle=

Appendix C
Algorithm

We present the algorithm for our Global-Local Adversarial Self-Supervised Learning in Algorithm
1
. Briefly, it contains four main steps: First, two augmentation views of the original mini-batch of clean images are created. Second, adversarial images corresponding to one of the two views are generated. This step realizes the local and global views when generating adversaries: maximize the InfoNCE loss on the adversarial and the other view of benign examples; and minimize the discriminator loss, i.e. Binary Cross Entropy loss, on the adversaries. The discriminator loss minimization makes the model know that the adversaries are fake data, thus further forces them to be distinguished from their clean counterparts in the latent space. Third, freeze the parameters of the main network and update those of the discriminator such that the model can distinguish the adversaries and the clean samples, i.e. minimize two corresponding BCE losses. Finally, freeze the discriminator’s parameters, update those of the main network such that the InfoNCE loss is minimized and the discriminator losses are maximized. This step ensures that the learnt main model can capture the invariance between adversarial and clean samples, thus becomes more robust against adversarial pertubations.

D.1
Implementation Details

As mentioned in the main paper, to take into account the global divergence between adversarial examples and their benign counterparts, we use generative adversarial network, GAN
[
53
]
. Specifically, we additionally use a discriminator which takes the output of the projection head as input, and returns the probability that the given sample is clean. We thus choose an one-layer perceptron
128\times 1
for its architecture. Moreover, the discriminator’s objective is to quantify the global divergence between adversaries and clean examples, thus we choose its loss function to be the Binary Cross Entropy loss. The labels for benign and adversarial examples are
\boldsymbol{1}
and
\boldsymbol{0}
respectively, where
\boldsymbol{1}
(
\boldsymbol{0}
) is a vector with all the elements are
1
(
0
).

When applying SAM to the benign term loss, our optimization procedure is as follows: First, compute the gradient of other terms in the total loss with respect to the current model parameters and save it as
g_{other}
. Second, zero out current gradient then follow the procedure of SAM to compute the SAM gradient of only the benign term,
g_{benign}
. Then average
g_{other}
and
g_{benign}
to get the final gradient update.
