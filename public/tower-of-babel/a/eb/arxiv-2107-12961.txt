Abstract
. We define the notion of isosingular loci of algebraic varieties, following the analytic case first studied by Ephraim. In particular, we give a partial extension of his main result in arbitrary characteristic and a full extension assuming characteristic 0 0 . One of the main obstructions in the positive characteristic case is the non-separability of the orbit map associated to the contact group, as first observed by Greuel and Pham for isolated singularities.

Let
X
be a complex analytic space and
p\in X
. In
[
2
]
the
isosingular locus
of
X
at
p
was defined to be the subset of
X
with a prescribed singularity type, that is,

\iso(X,p)=\{q\in X\mid X_{q}\simeq X_{p}\},

where
X_{p}
and
X_{q}
denote the analytic germs of
X
at
p
and
q
. The main result of
[
2
]
asserts the following:

Theorem

(
[
2
, Theorem 0.2, Observation 2.5]
)
.

For a complex analytic space
X
and
p\in X
the set
\iso(X,p)
is locally closed and smooth as a (reduced) analytic subspace of
X
. Furthermore, there exists a germ of an analytic space
Y_{y}
such that
X_{p}\simeq Y_{y}\times\iso(X,p)_{p}
, with
Y_{y}
having the additional property that there does not exist an isomorphism of germs
Y_{y}\simeq Y^{\prime}_{y^{\prime}}\times{\mathbb{C}}_{0}
.

Generalizations of this result to the relative case of morphisms between analytic spaces were obtained in
[
8
,
9
]
. We call an analytic germ
(X,p)

harmonic
if
\iso(X,p)=\iso(\Sing X,p)
. In
[
4
]
the classical Mather–Yau theorem (see
[
10
]
) was extended from the isolated singularity case to general harmonic singularities. Results in a very similar spirit appeared recently in the preprint
[
3
]
.

In this paper, our main goal is to study isosingular loci of an algebraic variety
X
over an arbitrary algebraically closed field
k
. As we do not have the analytic topology at our disposal, we will replace it by considering the corresponding
formal neighborhoods
instead. That is, for any
x\in X(k)
the
isosingular locus
of
X
at
x
is defined to be the set

\iso(X,x):=\{x^{\prime}\in X\mid\widehat{X}_{x^{\prime}}\simeq\widehat{X}_{x}\},

where
\widehat{X}_{x}
denotes the formal completion of
X
along the singleton
\{x\}
. Note that any isomorphism of such formal completions induces an isomorphism of residue fields and thus
\iso(X,x)\subset X(k)
. Our first main result is an extension of the first part of the above theorem in
[
2
]
.

Theorem A
.

Let
X
be a variety over an algebraically closed field
k
.

(1)

For each
x\in X(k)
the subset
\iso(X,x)
is locally closed in
X(k)
(endowed with the Zariski topology).

(2)

Denote by
X^{(x)}
the unique reduced subscheme of
X
whose
k
-points agree with
\iso(X,x)
. Then
X^{(x)}
is smooth.

The proof follows the analytic case and uses the action of the contact group
\mathcal{K}
as well as a version of Artin’s celebrated approximation result in
[
1
]
. As for remaining part of
[
2
, Theorem 0.2]
, assuming characteristic
0
we obtain the following result:

Theorem B
.

Let
X
be a variety over an algebraically closed field of characteristic
0
. For each
x\in X(k)
there exists a scheme
Y
of finite type over
k
, a point
y\in Y(k)
and an isomorphism

\widehat{X}_{x}\simeq\widehat{(X^{(x)})}_{x}\times\widehat{Y}_{y},

such that
\widehat{Y}_{y}
has
no smooth factors
, that is, there does not exist an isomorphism
\widehat{Y}_{y}\simeq\widehat{Z}_{z}\times\widehat{({\mathbb{A}}^{1})}_{0}
.

The assertion of
B
fails in positive characteristics, as can be seen in the example of the Whitney umbrella
f=x^{p}+y^{p}z
in characteristic

, c.f.
Example

2.6
. Let us mention that the proof of

assumption in two separate places. First, it makes use of the Nagata–Zariski-Lipman criterion (see

), which expresses the existence of smooth factors in terms of the existence of

. The second important assumption which holds only in characteristic

is that the orbit map associated to the (truncated) contact group

is separable for all

[
6
,
5
]
for certain isolated singularities. As we will see in
Example

, the results of
Section

2
give new examples of this phenomenon, including the singularity

mentioned above. Moreover, it prompts the following question:

Question C
.

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field and
0\in X\subset{\mathbb{A}}^{N}
a hypersurface singularity given by
f\in k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}]
. Assume that there exists
\beta_{0}>0
such that the orbit map
\mathcal{K}_{\beta}\to o(f_{\beta})
is separable for

(see

Note that we restrict the question to the hypersurface case as even then we do not know of any counterexample.

Conventions

Throughout this paper we always assume
X
to be an algebraic variety, that is, a separated scheme of finite type over an algebraically closed field
k
. For any point
x\in X(k)
we denote by
\widehat{X}_{x}
the formal neighborhood of

at
x

\widehat{X}_{x}

1.
Contact equivalence and proof of

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field. We start by repeating the main definition: for any point
x\in X(k)
the
isosingular locus
of
X
at
x
is defined to be the set

\iso(X,x):=\{x^{\prime}\in X\mid\widehat{X}_{x}\simeq\widehat{X}_{x^{\prime}}\}.

If

is smooth at

\iso(X,x)
is clearly an open subset of

Remark 1.1
.

Let us briefly discuss the situation when

is not algebraically closed. Assume
\charK k=0
and consider the Whitney umbrella

defined by

in
{\mathbb{A}}^{3}_{k}

\Sing X
is just given by the

\widehat{X}_{x}
is isomorphic to the union of two hyperplanes if and only if

Since both

is affine, given by polynomials

Let

\det(\frac{\partial\varphi_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}(0))_{i,j\leq N}\in R^{*}

Follows from considering the maps on the associated graded rings.

Lemma 1.4

For each
\beta\geq 0

For the first assertion note that the underlying space of

and that we have open immersions
\GL_{n}(\mathcal{O}_{N,\beta})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{A}}^{n^{2}M}
and

The main idea behind the use of the contact group in the proof of
A
is that two points of
X
have isomorphic formal neighborhoods if and only if the respective Taylor expansions of
f
at those points lie in the same orbit of
\mathcal{K}
. We want to review this classical argument in our setting.
First, let
\gamma:X\to\mathcal{O}_{N}^{n}
be the morphism defined as follows: for each
k
-algebra
R
the map
\gamma(R)
is given by

a\in X(R)\subset R^{N}\mapsto f(\underline{x}+a)\in R[[\underline{x}]].

In an analogous way we obtain morphisms
\gamma_{\beta}:X\to\mathcal{O}_{N,\beta}^{n}
for
\beta\geq 0
.

Lemma 1.5
.

Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in X(k)
. Then
\widehat{X}_{x_{1}}\simeq\widehat{X}_{x_{2}}
if and only if
\gamma(x_{2})\in o(\gamma(x_{1}))
.

Proof.

We may assume that
x_{1}=0
and
x_{2}=a\in k^{N}
. Then

\widehat{\mathcal{O}_{X,x_{2}}}\simeq k[[\underline{x}]]/(f(\underline{x}+a)).

The existence of an isomorphism
\widehat{\mathcal{O}_{X,x_{1}}}\simeq\widehat{\mathcal{O}_{X,x_{2}}}
is equivalent to the existence of an isomorphism
\varphi\in\Aut_{k}(k[[\underline{x}]])
such that the following equality of ideals holds:

(\varphi(f(\underline{x})))=(f(\underline{x}+a)).

To proceed we make use of a trick of Mather:

Lemma 1.6
.

Let
A,B\in k^{n\times n}
. Then there exists
C\in k^{n\times n}
such that
C(1-AB)+B
is invertible.

Proof.

Let
r=\rk(B)
and choose a basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i}
for
k^{n}
such that
Be_{1},\ldots,Be_{r}
are linearly independent and
Be_{i}=0
for
i>r
. Let
e^{\prime}_{r+1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{n}
be such that
Be_{i},e^{\prime}_{j}
form a basis. Then
C
is the matrix representing the linear map given by
e_{i}\mapsto 0
for
i\leq r
and
e_{i}\mapsto e^{\prime}_{i}
for
i>r
.
∎

Write
R=k[[\underline{x}]]
and
\mathfrak{m}=(\underline{x})
. The above equality of ideals implies the existence of
A,B\in R^{n\times n}
with
A\cdot f(\varphi(\underline{x}))=f(\underline{x}+a)
and
B\cdot f(\underline{x}+a)=f(\varphi(\underline{x}))
. Then, by
Lemma

1.6
, there exists
C\in k^{n\times n}
with
D=C(1-AB)+B\in R^{n\times n}
invertible modulo
\mathfrak{m}
, which implies that
D
is invertible in
R^{n\times n}
. Clearly,
D\cdot f(\underline{x}+a)=f(\varphi(\underline{x}))
.
∎

Note the contact group
\mathcal{K}
is not of finite type and neither is the scheme

1.4

we make use of a variant of Artin’s celebrated approximation results, which is commonly referred to as

Theorem 1.7

(
[
1

)

Let
k
be a field. For each tuple
(n,m,d,\alpha)
of non-negative integers there exists a
\beta\geq 0
satisfying the following property: let
\underline{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
\underline{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
be two sets of variables and

with
\deg(f_{i})\leq d

\bar{y}=(\bar{y}_{1},\ldots,\bar{y}_{m})\in k[\underline{x}]^{m}

f(\underline{x},\bar{y}(\underline{x}))\equiv 0\mod(\underline{x})^{\beta}.

Then there exist algebraic power series

with

\bar{y}\equiv y(\underline{x})\mod(\underline{x})^{\alpha}.

.

Let

Remark 1.10
.

Let us mention that the isosingularity loci

do not give a stratification of

in general, since
X
might have infinitely many points with distinct singularities. Consider the classical example by Whitney

defined by

. For each point

the associated tangent cone is a union of four planes, which have a well-defined cross-ratio depending on

. As any formal isomorphism induces a linear isomorphism of tangent cones we see that
\iso(X,x)=\{x\}

Let

, which in turn implies that
X^{(x)}
is smooth at
x
.
∎

2.
The proof of
B
and separability of the orbit map

This section is devoted to the proof of
B
, which will involve studying the orbit map
\mathcal{K}\to o(f)
. As both sides are non-Noetherian schemes of infinite dimension, we first need to introduce the right notion of smoothness in this setting.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
k
be a ring and
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a local
k
-algebra. We say that
R
is
formally smooth
over
k
if for every
k
-algebra
C
with nilpotent ideal
J\subset C
and every diagram

\textstyle{R\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\bar{\psi}}
\scriptstyle{\psi}
\textstyle{C/J}
\textstyle{k\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

such that
\bar{\psi}(\mathfrak{m}^{n})=0
in
C/J
there exists a diagonal arrow
\psi:R\to C
making the diagram commute.

Note that this is equivalent to saying that
(R,\mathfrak{m})
considered as a topological ring with respect to its
\mathfrak{m}
-adic topology is formally smooth in the sense of
[
7
, (19.3.1)]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
and
(S,\mathfrak{n})
be local
k
-algebras with
R/\mathfrak{m}=S/\mathfrak{n}=k
.

(1)

Assume
R=\varinjlim_{n}R_{n}
with
\{(R_{n},\mathfrak{m}_{n})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
a direct system of local
k
-algebras smooth over
k
. Then
R
is formally smooth over
k
.

(2)

Assume
\varphi:R\to S
is a local map and
R
,
S
are formally smooth over
k
. If the induced cotangent map
T^{*}\varphi:\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^{2}\to\mathfrak{n}/\mathfrak{n}^{2}
is injective, then there exists a retraction
\widehat{S}\to\widehat{R}
of the completion map
\widehat{\varphi}:\widehat{R}\to\widehat{S}
.

Compare the second assertion with the fact that any submersion between smooth manifolds allows for a local section.

Proof.

Let us start by proving (1). Since
R_{n}
is smooth over
k
, it is in particular formally smooth over
k
. By
[
7
, (19.5.4)]
this is equivalent to the natural map

\Sym_{k}(\mathfrak{m}_{n}/\mathfrak{m}_{n}^{2})\to\gr(R_{n})

being a bijection. The colimit of these maps is given by

\Sym_{k}(\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^{2})\to\gr(R)

and thus this map is a bijection, which, again by
[
7
, (19.5.4)]
, implies that
R
is formally smooth over
k
.

To prove (2), let
x_{j}\in S
,
j\in J
, be elements whose images form a basis for
\mathfrak{n}/\mathfrak{n}^{2}
and such that for
I\subset J
the images of
x_{i}
,
i\in I
, form a basis for the subspace
\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^{2}
. Then the bijection
\Sym_{k}(\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^{2})\simeq\gr(R)
from before induces an isomorphism

k[[x_{i}\mid i\in I]]:=\varprojlim_{n}k[x_{i}\mid i\in I]/(x_{i}\mid i\in I)^{n}\to\widehat{R}

and similarly for
\widehat{S}
. In particular, the map
\widehat{\varphi}
is given by the inclusion

k[[x_{i}\mid i\in I]]\hookrightarrow k[[x_{j}\mid j\in J]],

which has a obvious retraction defined by
x_{j}\mapsto 0
for
j\in J\setminus I
.
∎

Let us now go back to the situation of
B
, that is,
X
is a variety over an algebraically closed field
k
and for
x\in X(k)
we let
X^{(x)}
be the associated isosingularity scheme. We keep the notation of the last section. Our main result will establish the existence of “enough” regular derivations on
\widehat{\mathcal{O}_{X,x}}
. Recall that a derivation
d\in\Der_{k}(k[[\underline{x}]])
is called
regular
if there exists
g\in k[[\underline{x}]]
with
d(g)\in k[[\underline{x}]]^{*}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Assume that there exists
\beta_{0}>0
such that the orbit map
\mathcal{K}_{\beta}\to o(f_{\beta})
is separable for all
\beta\geq\beta_{0}
. Then, for every tangent vector
a\in T_{x}X^{(x)}
there exists a regular derivation
d_{a}\in\Der_{k}(k[[\underline{x}]])
satisfying
d_{a}(f)\subset(f)
and
d_{a}(x)=a
.

Proof.

Write
Z_{\beta}:=o(f_{\beta})
and let
\beta\geq\beta_{0}
. By
Section

1
,
\mathcal{K}_{\beta}
and
Z_{\beta}
are nonsingular varieties over an algebraically closed field and thus the orbit map
\mathcal{K}_{\beta}\to Z_{\beta}
is generically smooth. Since it is
\mathcal{K}_{\beta}
-equivariant (and the action of
\mathcal{K}_{\beta}
on both sides is obviously transitive) this implies that it is smooth everywhere. In particular, the tangent map
T_{1}\mathcal{K}_{\beta}\to T_{f_{\beta}}Z_{\beta}
is surjective for all
\beta\geq\beta_{0}
. Setting
Z:=o(f)\subset\mathcal{O}^{n}
, we get that
T_{1}\mathcal{K}\to T_{f}Z
is surjective. As both
\mathcal{K}
and
Z
are colimits of schemes smooth over
k
, by
Lemma

2.2
the morphism of formal schemes

\Phi:\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{1}\to\widehat{Z}_{f}

admits a section
\widetilde{\Psi}:\widehat{Z}_{f}\to\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
. Write
\Psi
for the composition of the map
\gamma:\widehat{(X^{(x)})}_{x}\to\widehat{Z}_{f}
with
\widetilde{\Psi}
; this gives a factorization of
\gamma
as

\textstyle{\widehat{(X^{(x)})}_{x}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Psi}
\textstyle{\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Phi}
\textstyle{\widehat{Z}_{f}.}

Let us analyze what this factorization means for the tangent map of
\gamma
. We may assume for convenience’s sake that
x=0
. To proceed we consider the functorial description of formal neighborhoods via
test rings

(A,\mathfrak{m})
, that is,
A
is a local
k
-algebra with
A/\mathfrak{m}=k
and
\mathfrak{m}^{n}=0
for some
n
. Let
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})
be an
A
-point of
\widehat{(X^{(x)})}_{x}
, that is,
a_{i}\in\mathfrak{m}
and
f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})=0
. The map
\Psi(A)
is given by

a\mapsto(M(a),\varphi(a)),\>M(a)\in\GL_{n}(A[[\underline{x}]]),\varphi(a)\in\Aut_{k}(A[[\underline{x}]]),

where
M(a)\equiv 1\mod\mathfrak{m}
and
\varphi(a)\equiv\underline{x}\mod\mathfrak{m}
. Composing with
\Phi(A)
gives
\gamma(A)
and thus the identity

(2a)

f(\underline{x}+a)=M(a)\cdot f(\varphi(a)).

To compute
T_{0}\gamma
we take
A=k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^{2})
and let
a=\widetilde{a}\varepsilon
with
\widetilde{a}\in k^{N}
. Taking Taylor expansions on both sides of (
2a
) gives

f(\underline{x})+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(\underline{x})\cdot\widetilde{a}\varepsilon=(1+\widetilde{M}(\widetilde{a})\varepsilon)(f(\underline{x})+\frac{\partial f}{\partial\underline{x}}(\underline{x})\cdot\widetilde{\varphi}(\widetilde{a})\varepsilon),

with
\widetilde{M}(\widetilde{a})\in k[[\underline{x}]]^{n\times n}
and
\widetilde{\varphi}(\widetilde{a})\in k[[\underline{x}]]^{n}
. Simplifying yields

(2b)

\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(\underline{x})\cdot(\widetilde{a}-\widetilde{\varphi}(\widetilde{a}))=\widetilde{M}(\widetilde{a})\cdot f(\underline{x}).

Define
d:=\frac{\partial}{\partial\underline{x}}\cdot(\widetilde{a}-\widetilde{\varphi}(\widetilde{a}))\in\Der_{k}(k[[\underline{x}]])
. As
\underline{x}+\widetilde{\varphi}(\widetilde{a})\in\Aut_{k}(k[[\underline{x}]])
, it follows that
d(0)=\widetilde{a}
and by (
2b
) we have
d(f)\subset(f)
.
∎

Now the proof of
B
follows from
Lemma

2.3
together with the following variant of the classical Nagata–Zariski–Lipman criterion:

Theorem 2.4
.

Assume that
k
is of characteristic
0
and
X
is a variety over

. Let
X^{\prime}\subset X
be a subvariety which is smooth of dimension
m
at a point

. Assume that for a choice of local coordinates

for
X^{\prime}
at
x
the associated derivations
dx^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,dx^{\prime}_{m}
lift to derivations on

. Then

\widehat{X}_{x}\simeq\widehat{(X^{\prime})}_{x}\times\widehat{Y}_{y},

for some variety
Y
and

.

Proof.

See for example
[
11
, Theorem 30.1]
.
∎

Proof of
B
.

Assume
\charK k=0
, then the orbit map
\mathcal{K}_{\beta}\to o(f_{\beta})
is separable since it is dominant. Thus
Lemmas

2.3
and
2.4
together imply that

\widehat{X}_{x}\simeq\widehat{(X^{(x)})}_{x}\times\widehat{Y}_{y},

for some variety

and

. Using

2.5
below it follows that
\widehat{Y}_{q}
itself has no smooth factors.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
X
be a scheme of finite type over an algebraically closed field
k
and let
x\in X(k)
. Assume that
\widehat{X}_{x}\simeq\widehat{Y}_{y}\times\widehat{({\mathbb{A}}^{m})}_{0}
. Then
\dim_{x}X^{(x)}\geq m
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

1.12
we can find a common étale neighborhood
U
for
x\in X
and
y^{\prime}=(y,0)\in Y\times{\mathbb{A}}^{m}
. Clearly
\iso(Y\times{\mathbb{A}}^{m},y^{\prime})\supset\{y\}\times{\mathbb{A}}^{m}
and thus we are done using
Lemma

1.13
.
∎

We now want to discuss the existence of a decomposition as in
B
for
k
of positive characteristic. As the following example shows, this fails in general.

Example 2.6
.

Let
k
be of characteristic
p>0
and
X
be the Whitney umbrella given by
x^{p}+y^{p}z
in
{\mathbb{A}}^{3}_{k}
. We claim that
\widehat{X}_{0}
is isomorphic to
\widehat{X}_{x}
, where
x=(0,0,t)
for some
t\neq 0
. As
k
is algebraically closed, there exists
s\in k
with
s^{p}=t
. Consider now the change of coordinates
\varphi
given by

x\mapsto x+ys,\>y\mapsto y,\>z\mapsto z.

Then
\varphi(x^{p}+y^{p}z)=x^{p}+y^{p}(z+t)
and thus
\widehat{X}_{0}\simeq\widehat{X}_{x}
. In particular, we have that
\iso(X,0)
is just the
z
-axis.

We claim that
\widehat{X}_{0}
has no smooth factors and sketch the argument here. By an extension of
Theorem

2.4
(see for example
[
11
, Exercise 30.1]
) it is sufficient to show that there does not exist a regular continuous
Hasse–Schmidt derivation

D\in\Der_{k}^{\infty}(\widehat{\mathcal{O}_{X,0}})
. That is, there does not exist a map
D:\widehat{\mathcal{O}_{X,0}}\to\widehat{\mathcal{O}_{X,0}}[[t]]
of
\widehat{\mathcal{O}_{X,0}}
-algebras which is continuous for the respective adic topologies and such that there exists an element
g\in\widehat{\mathcal{O}_{X,0}}
with
g(0)=0
and
D(g)
invertible. To that avail, suppose such a map
D
is given by

D(x)=\sum_{i\geq 0}\widetilde{x}_{i}t^{i},\>D(y)=\sum_{i\geq 0}\widetilde{y}_{i}t^{i},\>D(z)=\sum_{i\geq 0}\widetilde{z}_{i}t^{i},

satisfying
\widetilde{x}_{0}=x
and so on. Applying
D
to the equation
x^{p}+y^{p}z=0
yields a system of equations for
\widetilde{x}_{i},\widetilde{y}_{i},\widetilde{z}_{i}
. For simplicity we will give them explicitly only in the case
p=2
:

\displaystyle y^{2}\widetilde{z}_{1}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\widetilde{x}_{1}^{2}+y^{2}\widetilde{z}_{2}+\widetilde{y}_{1}^{2}z

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\ldots

From the first equation it follows that
\widetilde{z}_{1}=0
and from the second that
\widetilde{x}_{1}(0)=0
. Now suppose that
\widetilde{y}_{1}
is invertible. Then the second equation gives

z=-\frac{y^{2}\widetilde{z}_{2}+\widetilde{x}_{1}^{2}}{\widetilde{y}_{1}^{2}}.

Note that the right hand side has order
\geq 2
, which gives a contradiction. Thus it follows that for any
g\in\widehat{\mathcal{O}_{X,0}}
with
g(0)=0
we have that
D(g)
is not invertible.

One of the main assumptions in the proof of
B
was the separability of the orbit map
\mathcal{K}_{\beta}\to o(f_{\beta})
for
\beta\gg 0
. As observed already in
[
6
, Example 2.9]
, this fails in positive characteristics for general isolated singularities. The example provided there was the cusp singularity
f=y^{2}+x^{3}
for
\charK(k)=2
. While obviously not applicable to the case of isolated singularities,
Lemma

2.3
can be used to construct related examples where the separability of the orbit map fails.

Example 2.7
.

Let
k
be algebraically closed with
\charK k=2
and consider the deformation
\widetilde{f}=x^{2}+y^{3}+zy^{2}\in k[x,y,z]
of the cusp singularity
f=x^{2}+y^{3}
. Set
X=V(\widetilde{f})\subset{\mathbb{A}}^{3}
; we claim that
X^{(0)}=V(x,y)\simeq{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
. If
t\neq 0
and
x=(0,0,t)
, then an isomorphism between
\widehat{X}_{0}
and
\widehat{X}_{x}
is given by the map

x\mapsto x+ys,\>y\mapsto y,\>z\mapsto z,

where
s\in k
with
s^{2}=t
. However, there does not exist
d\in\Der_{k}(k[[x,y,z]])
satisfying
d(\widetilde{f})\subset(\widetilde{f})
and
d(0)=(0,0,1)
, as can be verified with an argument similar to the one in
Example

2.6
. Therefore, by
Lemma

2.3
the orbit map
\mathcal{K}_{\beta}\to o(\widetilde{f}_{\beta})
is inseparable for infinitely many
\beta>0
.

Note that the same argument also works for
f=x^{p}+y^{p}z
and
\charK k=p
, as in
Example

2.6
.

As mentioned in the introduction, these examples prompt the question whether inseparability of the orbit map is the main obstruction to extending
B
to positive characteristics. We expect a further investigation into this problem to shed more light on the formal structure of singularities for
\charK k=p
.
