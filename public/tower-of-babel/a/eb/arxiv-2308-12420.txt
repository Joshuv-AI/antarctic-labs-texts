Evolution of ESG-focused DLT Research: An NLP Analysis of the Literature
Walter Hernandez Cruz, Kamil Tylinski, Alastair Moore, Niall Roche, Nikhil Vadgama, Horst Treiblmaier, Jiangbo Shangguan, Jiahua Xu, Paolo Tasca

Abstract
Emerging technologies, such as Distributed Ledger Technology, face growing scrutiny for their environmental impact, especially when it comes to the energy use of the Proof of Work consensus mechanism and broader Environmental, Social, and Governance considerations. Yet, much of the existing systematic literature reviews of Distributed Ledger Technology rely on the limited analyses of citations, abstracts, and keywords, failing to fully capture the field’s complexity and Environmental, Social, and Governance concerns. To address these challenges, we analyze the full text of 24,539 publications using Natural Language Processing with our manually labeled Named Entity Recognition dataset of 39,427 entities for Distributed Ledger Technology. This method identifies 505 key publications connecting Distributed Ledger Technology and Environmental, Social, and Governance domains, providing a more comprehensive and nuanced understanding of the field. Our combined Natural Language Processing and temporal graph analysis reveals critical trends in Distributed Ledger Technology evolution and Environmental, Social, and Governance impacts, including the pivotal role of research in cryptography and peer-to-peer networks, Bitcoin’s persistent impact on research and environmental concerns (a Lindy effect ), Ethereum’s influence on Proof of Stake and smart contracts adoption, and a shift towards energy-efficient consensus mechanisms. Our contributions include the first Distributed Ledger Technology-specific Named Entity Recognition dataset, addressing the scarcity of high-quality labeled Natural Language Processing data for blockchain research; a methodology integrating Natural Language Processing and temporal graph analysis for interdisciplinary literature review at large scale; and the first Natural Language Processing-driven Distributed Ledger Technology literature review emphasizing Environmental, Social, and Governance aspects.

Introduction

Emerging technologies are facing increased scrutiny regarding their energy consumption and broader ecological impacts, including the use of vital resources such as water, precious metals, and synthetic compounds
[
1
,
2
]
. This growing environmental awareness necessitates evaluating technological advancements through their ecological footprint, with Distributed Ledger Technology being no exception. While Distributed Ledger Technology offers promising features like record immutability and decentralization, it also presents challenges, particularly the high energy consumption of certain consensus algorithms. For instance, Bitcoin’s Proof of Work
[
3
]
, designed to prevent Sybil attacks where malicious actors pretend to be multiple users, has significant energy requirements.

The rapid evolution and interest in Distributed Ledger Technology has led to a growing number of publications and intensified its ecological footprint scrutiny, yet persistent misconceptions about its energy consumption patterns have complicated objective assessment.
Additionally, the complexity of Distributed Ledger Technology extends well beyond environmental considerations. Distributed Ledger Technology spans breakthroughs in security, cryptography, network design, and diverse applications, leading to a rapidly expanding body of research. This makes traditional systematic reviews and manual literature analysis, often relying on citation metrics, abstract analyses, and database term searches, increasingly difficult, and may fail to capture the field’s full scope.

We address this complexity by turning to Natural Language Processing, a sub-field of Artificial Intelligence and linguistics. Natural Language Processing offers powerful tools to examine the growing body of Distributed Ledger Technology literature, from academic articles to industry whitepapers. Among its various applications, we focus on Named Entity Recognition to identify specific Distributed Ledger Technology technologies (e.g., Proof of Work, Proof of Stake) and their Environmental, Social, and Governance implications (such as

energy consumption

and

computational power

) within our dataset. This approach allows us to understand the shifts in research across different Distributed Ledger Technology technologies and their environmental impacts.

Building on precedents from the biomedical field, where ontologies or hierarchical taxonomies are used to create Named Entity Recognition datasets for Natural Language Processing-driven literature mining and review

, we have successfully transferred these methods to the Distributed Ledger Technology domain. Unlike previous systematic literature reviews that rely on citation measures, abstract analyses, keywords, or repeated term searches in databases, our approach examines the entire body of each publication. This allows us to capture all relevant entities under top-level categories representing the whole Distributed Ledger Technology field, removing the need for exhaustive database term searches to generate a dataset that represents the Distributed Ledger Technology field and enabling us to work at scale. By mapping each publication’s tokens to the hierarchical Distributed Ledger Technology taxonomy from

As a result, we compile a more inclusive dataset that reflects the Distributed Ledger Technology field’s overall evolution while preserving the methodological rigor demonstrated in biomedical literature studies.

Our research makes the following contributions:

•

Creating a manually labeled Named Entity Recognition dataset composed of 39,427 named entities for twelve top-level Distributed Ledger Technology’s taxonomy categories. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first Named Entity Recognition dataset explicitly designed for Distributed Ledger Technology. Our dataset aims to address the Natural Language Processing data scarcity issue in blockchain research. By making it openly available, we help overcome common hurdles like high costs of labeling and complex access of Natural Language Processing labeled data in the Distributed Ledger Technology field.

Two-Tier analysis strategy

Our analysis follows a two-tier approach to maximize the use of available data:

1.
Broad analysis using metadata
: Keyword extraction
[

[

We initially analyze the complete corpus of over 60,000 papers using their metadata (titles, keywords, etc.), regardless of full-text availability. This approach allows us to construct a comprehensive keywords graph that captures the broad evolution of the Distributed Ledger Technology field. While many papers (38,413) were behind paywalls or otherwise not easily inaccessible, their metadata still contributes to our understanding of the field’s development.

2.
Deep analysis using full text
.
For the subset of 24,539 papers where full text was available through open-access journals, conferences, or public repositories, we conduct a more detailed analysis. We narrow the number of publications for our analysis by fine-tuning a transformer-based language model for a Named Entity Recognition task for ESG/DLT content density corpus filtering. This method allows us to categorize technologies in Distributed Ledger Technology, such as different

Temporal graph and named entities analysis

The final stage involves temporal graph and named entities analysis to track the evolution of Distributed Ledger Technology technologies, and their Environmental, Social, and Governance implications over time. This combines insights from the broad metadata analysis and the detailed full-text examination, providing a comprehensive view of the field’s development.

3.2
NER task for literature mining

We considered using Large Language Models for our Named Entity Recognition task. An unsupervised approach like zero-shot Named Entity Recognition with Large Language Models would have been more cost-effective in terms of not requiring manually labeled data. However, at the time of this writing, domain-specific Named Entity Recognition tasks often perform better with supervised learning models than with current Large Language Models
[
62
,
63
]
. This performance difference arises from fundamental architectural distinctions: Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers-based models generally outperform decoder-only Large Language Models, such as OpenAI’s ChatGPT and Google Deepmind’s Gemini, on token-level tasks like Named Entity Recognition, due to their bidirectional encoder architecture
[
64
]
. Specifically, Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers processes text by considering both left and right context simultaneously
[
64
]
, enabling it to capture fine-grained contextual information around each token. This is a crucial capability for Named Entity Recognition, where entity recognition often depends on the full contextual surrounding of a word.

In contrast, decoder-only architectures, like Generative Pre-trained Transformer, process text unidirectionally (left to right)
[
65
,
66
]
, limiting their ability to capture the complete contextual information necessary for accurate entity recognition. While decoder-only architectures excel at open-ended question answering, they struggle to match Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers-based models’ Named Entity Recognition performance
[
62
,
63
]
, which currently represents the state of the art for Named Entity Recognition tasks
4
4

4

https://paperswithcode.com/task/named-entity-recognition-ner
. Therefore, we adopted a supervised learning approach, fine-tuning and benchmarking several Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers-base models, selecting the final model based on its performance and efficiency at inference. This approach provides more granularity for literature mining of an entire publication body without the hallucination issues of Large Language Models (i.e., creation of irrelevant or inconsistent content), although the trade-off is the requirement of the costly manual labeling of the Named Entity Recognition dataset.

We applied text resampling for overlapping named entities that could fit into multiple categories, such as belonging to

Discussion

5.1
Transferability and adaptability of the methodology

5.1.1
Methodology framework transferability

Our methodology shows versatility in analyzing emerging technologies and their interdisciplinary implications. While we apply it to Distributed Ledger Technology
11
11

11

Glossary
explains Distributed Ledger Technology and some its key terms in more detail
and Environmental, Social, and Governance, the framework’s structure makes it adaptable to various technological domains and their intersections, such as Artificial Intelligence in healthcare or renewable energy applications in urban development, to name a few.

Methodological framework

The methodology comprises five phases (
Figure 1
). Initially, we establish a comprehensive corpus through data collection by selecting seed papers that represent seminal publications at domain intersections of interest via citation analysis and expert consultation, then systematically expanding this by traversal up to two levels of the seed paper’s references (
subsection 3.1
). The second phase focuses on data labeling by first developing taxonomies that capture the domain intersections terminology, which serves as a guideline for labeling an Named Entity Recognition dataset (
subsubsection 3.2.1
and
subsubsection 3.2.2
). This labeled dataset then supports the third phase in which it is used to fine-tune Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers-based models for an Named Entity Recognition task (
subsection 3.2
), benchmarking and selecting the best-performing model (
subsection 4.2
). The fourth phase applies the selected fine-tuned model to filter the corpus based on the named entities identified (
subsubsection 3.2.3
). Finally, we conduct a manual literature analysis and temporal graph analysis to derive insights that track the evolution of concepts, technologies, and their interconnections.

Framework application example

To demonstrate the framework’s transferability, consider its application to Artificial Intelligence in healthcare research. The process would begin by identifying influential papers at the Artificial Intelligence-healthcare intersection, followed by traversal of the references from these papers to build a comprehensive corpus. A specialized taxonomy would integrate medical terminology with Artificial Intelligence concepts, enabling dataset labeling for model fine-tuning. This fine-tuned model with the labeled dataset would then facilitate identification of specific medical Artificial Intelligence technologies and their clinical applications. Finally, a manual literature review using temporal graph analysis, named entities, and citation network analysis would reveal the evolution of medical Artificial Intelligence research and its healthcare impact.

5.1.2
Methodological advantages over other approaches

Recent developments in Large Language Models, such as Google DeepMind Gemini’s demonstration of a systematic literature review, where terms like
“Chip”
and
“CRISPR-Cas9”
are searched in publications’ titles and abstracts to filter them

However, Gemini faces limitations like potential hallucinations that could undermine it for Named Entity Recognition tasks and filtering of publications. Although supervised learning approaches outperform current Large Language Models for Named Entity Recognition tasks

In contrast to current Large Language Model approaches, our methodology (

This supervised learning approach provides a more structured and verifiable framework for literature analysis, making it particularly valuable for the systematic examination of emerging technical fields and their intersections with other domains. The methodology’s emphasis on supervised learning and domain-specific training ensures greater accuracy and reliability (see

5.3
Limitations

Our literature review faces limitations, including potential biases in seed paper selection and a time lag in capturing recent publications, which may affect the comprehensiveness of our analysis. For instance, the choice of XRP’s 2018 whitepaper
[
126
]
over the more cited 2014 edition
[
127
]
could underestimate its influence in the citation network. Similarly, recent works such as the 2018 Hedera whitepaper
[
128
]
are omitted due to unavailable citation data.

Building the citation network predominantly from pre-2020 seed papers introduces a bias toward older publications, potentially overlooking newer research yet to achieve recognition (
6(a)
). While our methodology could theoretically filter citations to seed papers based on content density, our review focused solely on references within the seed papers, possibly limiting the thematic breadth.

Regarding our Named Entity Recognition dataset performance, there is an expected level of noise
[
129
,
130
,
131
]
that we tried to reduce by enforcing interlabeler consistency (see
subsubsection 3.2.2
). Our dataset, with 80 full-text manually labeled papers, is smaller than many general-purpose Named Entity Recognition datasets
[
132
,
69
,
133
,
134
]
but is comparable to those in specialized domains like materials science
[
135
,
136
]
.

Our analysis contains 24,539 openly accessible publications from an initial corpus of 63,083 references (
Figure 2
). While this reliance on publicly available research introduces potential sampling limitations, several factors mitigate concerns about representativeness and comprehensiveness. First, empirical evidence demonstrates that open-access publications achieve broader academic engagement
[
137
,
138
,
139

and higher media attention
[
140
]
, suggesting they effectively capture significant research developments and may influence more research trends. Second, although only approximately 18% of journal articles are currently openly available
[
137
]
, authors of high-quality research increasingly seem to favor hybrid open-access journals
[
141
]
. Third, the Distributed Ledger Technology field’s rapid evolution has elevated non-traditional literature, particularly whitepapers and industry publications, as crucial sources of technological developments. Seminal works such as the Bitcoin
[
3
]
, Ethereum
[

]
, PPCoin
[
109
]

Our methodology addresses these considerations through two complementary approaches: (1) incorporating non-traditional academic literature to capture contemporary technological developments, and (2) leveraging metadata from paywalled publications in our broader analysis of the Distributed Ledger Technology field evolution (
subsubsection 5.2.1
). This two-tier analytical approach (

Also, it could enable relationship mapping between Distributed Ledger Technology concepts, supporting the construction of knowledge graphs. Finally, the dataset could contribute to the development of automated systematic literature review systems, particularly valuable given the rapid evolution of the Distributed Ledger Technology field. These potential applications emphasize our Named Entity Recognition dataset’s versatility and value beyond this paper.

6
Conclusion

This paper addresses the scarcity of labeled Natural Language Processing data for blockchain research by developing an Named Entity Recognition dataset for the Environmental, Social, and Governance/Distributed Ledger Technology domains intersection from public sources. We conducted a systematic literature analysis to demonstrate its utility and provide future research possibilities. Our analysis reveals the critical intersections of topics like Cryptography, Peer-to-Peer, Proof of Work, Proof of Stake, and smart contracts in the evolution of Distributed Ledger Technology. We observed Bitcoin’s persistent dominance in research (a

Lindy effect

). Ethereum’s significant impact on the adoption of smart contracts and Proof of Stake as an energy-efficient alternative to Proof of Work. These insights, coupled with the growing focus on energy-efficient consensus mechanisms, highlight the field’s rapid adaptation to technological and environmental challenges.

We believe our work represents a step towards leveraging and facilitating the use of Natural Language Processing to research rapidly evolving fields like Distributed Ledger Technology, enabling researchers, policymakers, and industry stakeholders to stay informed and make data-driven decisions.
