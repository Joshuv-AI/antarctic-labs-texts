Abstract
Unsourced random access (URA) is a recently proposed communication paradigm attuned to machine-driven data transfers. In the original URA formulation, all the active devices share the same number of bits per packet. The scenario where several classes of devices transmit concurrently has so far received little attention. An initial solution to this problem takes the form of group successive interference cancellation, where codewords from a class of devices with more resources are recovered first, followed by the decoding of the remaining messages. This article introduces a joint iterative decoding approach rooted in approximate message passing. This framework has a concatenated coding structure borrowed from the single-class coded compressed sensing and admits a solution that offers performance improvement at little added computational complexity. Our findings point to new connections between multi-class URA and compressive demixing. The performance of the envisioned algorithm is validated through numerical simulations.



I
Introduction and Background

Machine-type communication (MTC) is poised to increase sharply over the coming decades
[
1
,
2
]
.
This poses a great challenge to the foundation on which wireless infrastructures are relying,
as MTC often produces short packets and fleeting connections.
This results in a
constantly changing collection of devices wishing to transmit bursty data.
In this context, it becomes impractical to adopt the enrollment-estimation-scheduling paradigm, as the cost of acquiring side information on the channel state becomes prohibitive.
This reality is reflected in the current interest in grant-free schemes and uncoordinated wireless access.

Another aspect of coordinated system that is hard to maintain in MTC is the implicit agreement between an access point and an active device regarding the MAC layer configuration.
This consideration serves as a motivation behind the unsourced random access (URA) model introduced by Polyanskiy
[
3
]
.
A distinguishing feature of this approach is that active devices must share a same encoding function.
The signal sent by a device therefore depends solely on the message payload, and not on its identity.
If a device wishes to be recognized, then it must embed an identifier within its message.
Altogether, the URA model captures several fundamental aspects of MTC: this has led to
a rapid growth of literature on the topic

In such situations, devices from distinct groups may wish to communicate messages of different lengths and they may have varying power budgets.
This reality can be accommodated by allowing group-based encoding and separating groups in signal space

II
System Model and Encoding Process

In this section, we begin by describing the two-class URA architecture.
Consider the scenario in which
K_{1}

K_{2}

Our proposed algorithm loosely parallels coded compressed sensing (CCS).

III
Demixing and Decoding Algorithm

Having established the encoding for our multi-class URA problem, we now turn our attention to the decoding process.
Conceptually, the receiver seeks to recover
\mathbf{s}_{1}
and
\mathbf{s}_{2}

The first task can be viewed as support recovery through
compressive demixing, and the second objective corresponds to message disambiguation.
As in
[

The building blocks for our algorithm include BP on factor graphs
[

]
and approximate message passing (AMP) applied to non-separable functions
[

]

III-B

Approximate Message Passing (AMP)

In this section, we explain the component of the decoding algorithm associated with the inner code.
The mathematical structure in (
3
) hints at several demixing algorithms found in the literature to address similar tasks
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
.
Yet, as mentioned in the introduction, a distinguishing feature of our setting lies in the very large dimensions of the raw index vectors.
Much like in the case of CCS, this precludes the direct application of existing demixing methods.
As such, we strive to create schemes that harness lessons from the past while also admitting a computationally tractable implementation.
Specifically, we wish to leverage insights from demixing and transpose them into an AMP framework, which has recently been adapted to the standard URA formulation
[
8
,
9
]
.
The appeal of AMP-based solutions stems, partly, from their low-complexity and mathematical tractability
[
29
,
30
]
.

To conform to the structure of typical AMP formulations, we rewrite the received signal in (
3
) as

\begin{split}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}\mathbf{s}_{1}\\
d_{2}\mathbf{s}_{2}\end{bmatrix}+\mathbf{z}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{1}&\mathbf{A}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}\mathbf{s}_{1}\\
d_{2}\mathbf{s}_{2}\end{bmatrix}+\mathbf{z}.\end{split}

(8)

where
d_{g}\mathbf{A}_{g}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{g}
.
In this abstraction, matrix
\mathbf{A}
has independent, zero-mean Gaussian entries.
The variance of these entries is selected as to normalize the 2-norm of every column,
\mathbb{E}\left\|\mathbf{A}_{:,\iota}\right\|^{2}=1
.
Constant
d_{g}
is a non-negative amplitude; it accounts for the transmit power employed by devices in each group.
We emphasize that (
8

[
18
]
, albeit with an additional noise term.
In its current form, this representation invites a composite iterative AMP decoder that alternates between (
9
) and (
10

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{(t)}=\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}\mathbf{s}_{1}^{(t)}\\
d_{2}\mathbf{s}_{2}^{(t)}\end{bmatrix}+\frac{\mathbf{z}^{(t-1)}}{n}\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(t-1)}\left(\mathbf{r}^{(t-1)}\right)

(9)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{s}_{1}^{(t+1)}\\
\mathbf{s}_{2}^{(t+1)}\end{bmatrix}=\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(t)}\left(\mathbf{r}^{(t)}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}^{(t)}\left(\mathbf{r}_{1}^{(t)}\right)\\
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}^{(t)}\left(\mathbf{r}_{2}^{(t)}\right)\end{bmatrix},

(10)

where the
effective observation

\mathbf{r}^{(t)}
is given by

\mathbf{r}^{(t)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{r}_{1}^{(t)}\\
\mathbf{r}_{2}^{(t)}\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{z}^{(t)}+\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}\mathbf{s}_{1}^{(t)}\\
d_{2}\mathbf{s}_{2}^{(t)}\end{bmatrix}.

(11)

The iterative process is initiated with
\mathbf{s}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{z}^{(0)}=\mathbf{y}
.
Equation (
9
) is the computation of the

The second equation is a state update, which is performed through denoising using function
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(t)}(\cdot)
.
The remainder of this section is devoted to defining and justifying the denoising functions.

An astounding property of AMP is that, under certain conditions, the effective observation is asymptotically distributed as the true state plus Gaussian noise.
For the problem at hand, in the limit where the system gets larger,
\mathbf{r}^{(t)}
becomes approximately distributed as

\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}\mathbf{s}_{1}\\
d_{2}\mathbf{s}_{2}\end{bmatrix}+\tau_{t}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t},

(12)

where the entries in
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}
are independent
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
random variables and
\tau_{t}
is a deterministic quantity.
This phenomenon underlies the rationale behind our denoising functions.

The first ingredient of our denoiser is the posterior mean estimate (PME) of Fengler et al.
[
21
]
, a low-complexity estimator that encourages sparsity in
\mathbf{s}^{(t)}
.
Their work advocates an element-wise estimate of the form

\hat{s}_{g}\left(q,r,\tau\right)=\frac{q\exp\left(-\frac{\left(r-d_{g}\right)^{2}}{2\tau^{2}}\right)}{q\exp\left(-\frac{\left(r-d_{g}\right)^{2}}{2\tau^{2}}\right)+(1-q)\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2\tau^{2}}\right)}

(13)

where
q
is the prior probability of entry
s
being equal to one.
As its name suggests,
\hat{s}_{g}\left(q,r,\tau\right)
is equal to the mean of
s
under the aforementioned Gaussian approximation
r\sim d_{g}s+\tau\zeta
.
This approach performs well for the single-class URA problem, especially in view of its low complexity.

The PME denoiser found in
[
21
]
employs uninformative priors based on the known sparsity level of
\mathbf{s}
.
The approach was subsequently improved after realizing that priors can be enhanced by performing one round of
BP on the factor graph of the outer LDPC code
[
22
]
.
Specifically, in this latter case,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\ell}
is initialized based on the PME of the corresponding entries in
\mathbf{s}
.
This vector estimate is then refined using (
4

5

Finally, the distribution afforded by (
6

Details are available in
[
22

Conceptually, the enhanced version provides a means to embed the local structure of the outer code in the AMP iterate.
Accordingly, it can improve performance significantly while adding little to the computational complexity of the CCS approach.

The dynamic PME denoiser can be adapted to the current context, with its multiple classes.
We propose the following candidate implementation.
As in (
10

\boldsymbol{\eta}_{g}^{(t)}(\mathbf{r}_{g})\quad g\in\{1,2\}.

(14)

These functions are the dynamic PME denoiser defined by

\boldsymbol{\eta}_{g}^{(t)}(\mathbf{r}_{g})=\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{g,1}(\mathbf{r}_{g},\tau_{t})\cdots\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{g,L_{g}}(\mathbf{r}_{g},\tau_{t}).

Furthermore, individual entries are given by

\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{g,\ell}(\mathbf{r}_{g},\tau_{t})=\left(\hat{s}_{g}\left(\mathbf{q}(\ell,k),\mathbf{r}(\ell,k),\tau_{t}\right):k\in 0,\ldots,m_{g}-1\right),

where

are the

An important subtlety in extending previous results to the current, more elaborate scenario is that
\tau_{t}
is shared by the two parallel denoisers
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}^{(t)}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}^{(t)}
.
It must therefore be computed (or evaluated) through the joint state evolution, leading to

\begin{split}\tau_{t+1}^{2}=\sigma^{2}+\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left\|\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(t)}\left(\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}\mathbf{s}_{1}\\
d_{2}\mathbf{s}_{2}\end{bmatrix}+\tau_{t}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}\right)-\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}\mathbf{s}_{1}\\
d_{2}\mathbf{s}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\right\|^{2},\end{split}

where
\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1}\mathbf{I},d_{2}\mathbf{I})
accounts for the signal amplitudes and
\sigma^{2}
is the noise variance in (
1
).
In practice,
\tau_{t}
is often approximated by
\tau_{t}^{2}\approx\|\mathbf{z}^{(t)}\|^{2}/n
.
A second nuance in AMP for multi-class URA is the computation of the Onsager correction.
To get the proper form, we note that

\operatorname{div}\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(t)}(\mathbf{r})=d_{1}\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}^{(t)}(\mathbf{r}_{1})+d_{2}\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}^{(t)}(\mathbf{r}_{2}).

(16)

Adapting results from
[
7
, Prop. 8]
, we then obtain

\operatorname{div}\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(t)}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{\tau_{t}^{2}}\left(\big\|\mathbf{D}^{2}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(t)}(\mathbf{r})\big\|_{1}-\big\|\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(t)}(\mathbf{r})\big\|^{2}\right).

(17)

This compact form takes advantage of the identities

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(t)}(\mathbf{r})\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\big\|d_{1}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}^{(t)}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\big\|^{2}+\big\|d_{2}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}^{(t)}(\mathbf{r}_{2})\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbf{D}^{2}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(t)}(\mathbf{r})\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\big\|d_{1}^{2}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}^{(t)}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\big\|_{1}+\big\|d_{2}^{2}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}^{(t)}(\mathbf{r}_{2})\big\|_{1}.

These results crucially assume that the number of
BP iterations on each LDPC factor graph is strictly less than its shortest cycle.
The computation of the Onsager correction would become more involved otherwise.
In practice, this is not an issue because every AMP composite step entails the initiation of the local observations followed by a single round of
BP on each graph.
Expression (
17
) can be substituted as the Onsager term in (
9

III-C

Disambiguation

Once the AMP algorithm has converged, the messages embedded within
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{1}
and
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{2}
still need to be disentangled.
The presence of the LDPC-based message passing within the denoising functions encourages local consistency, but may fall short of decoding.
To address this issue, it suffices to run a version of the outer decoding algorithm first introduced in
[
7
]
.
Starting from root sections, the algorithm keeps track of all possible paths.
A message is added to the output list when it fulfills all the factors of the outer code.
This step is somewhat standard, as it is done separately for
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
.
Additional details can be found in
[
7
,
9
]
.

IV
Practical Considerations and Performance

As mentioned above, AMP is a powerful conceptual framework for the design and analysis of efficient algorithms.
Still, in practice, it can be beneficial to deviate slightly from theoretical underpinnings, especially for exceedingly large spaces.
Certain decisions can greatly reduce computational complexity.

IV-A

Implementation Details

One modification commonly found in the CCS literature is to create
\mathbf{A}
by sampling rows of a large Hadamard matrix (except the row composed of all ones), rather than generate it randomly.
Empirical evidence suggests that this does not significantly affect performance, while circumventing the need to store a large matrix.
Furthermore, the matrix multiplication steps in (
9
) and (
11
) can then be performed using the fast Walsh-Hadamard transform.

For the problem at hand, we could create
\mathbf{A}
in a similar manner.
Yet, inspecting (
11
) and (
14
), we gather that
\mathbf{r}_{1}^{(t)}
and
\mathbf{r}_{2}^{(t)}
are employed separately within the denoiser.
It therefore suffices to produce these sub-vectors independently.
Thus,
\mathbf{A}_{1}
and
\mathbf{A}_{2}
can be generated in a disjoint fashion, possibly each through the sampling of a matrix amenable to fast transform techniques.
This approach retains the structural properties of the problem, and lowers the computational load slightly.
Nevertheless, it is important that
\mathbf{A}_{1}
and
\mathbf{A}_{2}
maintain good incoherence properties.
Natural candidates for this step include standard Hadamard matrices, but also extend to matrices constructed using second-order Reed-Muller functions
[
31
]
.
These latter matrices feature good correlation properties and they have appeared in the context of single-class CCS in the past
[
6
]
, although their use therein is quite different.

IV-B

Numerical Simulations

We compare the performance of the proposed multi-class approach against two baselines for multi-class CCS.
The first implementation executes (group)
treating interference as noise
(TIN).
That is, a single-class recovery algorithm for
\mathcal{C}_{1}
is applied to
\mathbf{y}
; similar steps are followed for
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, again using
\mathbf{y}
as the input.
This scheme would arise naturally if the two device groups were assigned to distinct access points that are not cooperating, but nevertheless share a same spectral band.
The second multi-class approach is group
successive interference cancellation
(SIC).
Therein, a single-class recovery algorithm for
\mathcal{C}_{1}
is applied to
\mathbf{y}
, which yields estimate

However, we emphasize two important aspects of our findings.

V
Conclusion

In this paper, an extension of coded compressed sensing to the multi-class unsourced random access scenario is studied.
A joint decoding approach is proposed to concurrently decode messages emanating from multiple classes of users, leveraging insights from compressed demixing.
Within the framework of approximate message passing (AMP), the computational complexity of the proposed scheme is the same as that of alternate implementations such as
treating interference as noise
and
successive interference cancellation
.
However, these other schemes perform poorly compared to concurrent multi-class decoding.
The ability to decode classes of device jointly with essentially no added complexity is surprising and should be embraced.
Our findings provide a blueprint for generic demixing in very high dimensional spaces, which forms a promising avenue for future research.
