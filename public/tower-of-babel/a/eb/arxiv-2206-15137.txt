A Generalization of Zwegers’ 𝝁 \boldsymbol{\mu} -Function According to the 𝒒 \boldsymbol{q} -Hermite–Weber Difference Equation
Genki SHIBUKAWA and Satoshi TSUCHIMI

Abstract
We introduce a one parameter deformation of the Zwegers’ μ \mu -function as the image of q q -Borel and q q -Laplace transformations of a fundamental solution for the q q -Hermite–Weber equation. We further give some formulas for our generalized μ \mu -function, for example, forward and backward shift, translation, symmetry, a difference equation for the new parameter, and bilateral q q -hypergeometric expressions. From one point of view, the continuous q q -Hermite polynomials are some special cases of our μ \mu -function, and the Zwegers’ μ \mu -function is regarded as a continuous q q -Hermite polynomial of “ − 1 -1 degree”.

Introduction

Throughout this paper, let
\mathbb{Z}
denote the set of integers and let
\mathbb{C}
denote the set of complex numbers,

\displaystyle\vartheta_{11}(u,\tau)=\vartheta_{11}(u):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}{\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}(n+\frac{1}{2})(u+\frac{1}{2})+\pi{\rm i}(n+\frac{1}{2})^{2}\tau}=-{\rm i}q^{\frac{1}{8}}{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}u}\big(q,{\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}u},q{\rm e}^{-2\pi{\rm i}u};q\big)_{\infty},

\displaystyle\theta_{q}(x):=(q,-x,-q/x)_{\infty}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}x^{n}q^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}},

and for appropriate complex numbers

\displaystyle{}_{r}\phi_{s}\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\\

\displaystyle{}_{r}\psi_{s}\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\\

where

\displaystyle(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})_{n}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{r};q)_{n}:=(a_{1};q)_{n}\cdots(a_{r};q)_{n},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\infty\}.

Mock theta functions first appeared in Ramanujan’s last letter to Hardy in 1920.
In this letter, Ramanujan told Hardy that he had discovered a new class of functions which he called mock theta functions.
Mock theta functions are functions that have asymptotic behavior similar to “theta functions” (i.e., modular forms) at roots of unity but are not “theta functions”, and Ramanujan gave 17 examples of mock theta functions.
Some typical examples are as follows:

\displaystyle f_{0}(q)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n^{2}}}{(-q;q)_{n}},\qquad\phi(q)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n^{2}}}{\big({-}q^{2};q^{2}\big)_{n}},\qquad\psi(q)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n^{2}}}{\big(q;q^{2}\big)_{n}}.

Later, Andrews and Hickerson gave a detailed definition of the mock theta function
[
2
]
. For more background on mock theta functions, see, for example,
[
1
,
10
]
.

Ramanujan’s mock theta functions are represented as linear combinations of specializations of the universal mock theta function:

g_{3}(x;q):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n(n-1)}}{(x)_{n}\big(x^{-1}q\big)_{n}}

and some
q
-infinite products, which have come to be known as the mock theta conjectures.
For example
[
5
, Appendix A]
,

\displaystyle f_{0}(q)=-2q^{2}g_{3}\big(q^{2};q^{10}\big)+\frac{\big(q^{5};q^{5}\big)_{\infty}\big(q^{5};q^{10}\big)_{\infty}}{\big(q;q^{5}\big)_{\infty}\big(q^{4};q^{5}\big)_{\infty}}.

Such identities were proved by Hickerson
[
11
]
.
More fundamentally, the universal mock theta function
g_{3}(x;q)
can be written in the form
[
12
, Theorem 3.1]

\displaystyle q^{-\frac{1}{24}}x^{\frac{3}{2}}g_{3}(x;q)=\frac{q^{\frac{1}{3}}\big(q^{3};q^{3}\big)_{\infty}^{3}}{(q;q)_{\infty}\vartheta_{11}(3u,3\tau)}+q^{-\frac{1}{6}}x\mu(3u,\tau,3\tau)+q^{-\frac{2}{3}}x^{2}\mu(3u,2\tau,3\tau),

where
\mu
is the
\mu
-function defined by Zwegers as follows
[
23
]

\displaystyle\mu(u,v;\tau):=\frac{{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}u}}{\vartheta_{11}(v)}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{(-1)^{n}{\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}nv}q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}}{1-{\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}u}q^{n}}.

For convenience, we also use the following multiplicative notation of the
\mu
-function:

\displaystyle\mu(x,y;q)=\mu(x,y):=\frac{{\rm i}q^{-\frac{1}{8}}\sqrt{xy}}{\theta_{q}(-y)}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{(-1)^{n}y^{n}q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}}{1-xq^{n}}.

If we substitute
x={\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}u}
and

, then
\mu(x,y;q)=\mu(u,v;\tau)

Zwegers showed that the
\mu
-function satisfies a transformation law like Jacobi forms by adding an appropriate non-holomorphic function to the

-function
[

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}(u,v;\tau+1)={\rm e}^{-\frac{\pi{\rm i}}{4}}\tilde{\mu}(u,v;\tau),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}\left(\frac{u}{\tau},\frac{v}{\tau};-\frac{1}{\tau}\right)=-{\rm i}\sqrt{-{\rm i}\tau}{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}\frac{(u-v)^{2}}{\tau}}\tilde{\mu}(u,v;\tau),

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}(u,v;\tau):=\mu(u,v;\tau)+\frac{{\rm i}}{2}R(u-v;\tau),\qquad E(x):=2\int_{0}^{x}{\rm e}^{-\pi z^{2}}{\rm d}z,

\displaystyle R(u;\tau):=\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}}\big\{{\rm sgn}(\nu)-E\big((\nu+a)\sqrt{2t}\big)\big\}(-1)^{\nu-\frac{1}{2}}{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}\nu^{2}\tau-2\pi{\rm i}\nu u},

and

,

.
This result was a pioneering work in the study of mock modular forms (see
[

Thus, the
\mu
-function is very important for the study of mock theta functions.

On the other hand, the

q

For example, let us recall the well-known Kronecker formula

\displaystyle k(x,y):=\frac{1}{1-x}\,{{}_{1}\psi_{1}}\left(\begin{matrix}x\\

Thus the
\mu
-function is an interesting object deeply studied.
But, it seems that there are many problems to be clarified further.
For example, why the
\mu
-function is a two-variable function, whereas the universal mock theta function
g_{3}(x,q)
is a one-variable function (i.e., what is the origin of the extra variable of the
\mu
-function?).
Also, why the factors
{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}u}
in the numerator and
\vartheta_{11}(v)
in the denominator are needed.
Further, where the translation formula (
1.3
) comes from.
Some explanation has been given for these questions.
For example, Zwegers gives constructions of mock theta functions as indefinite theta series, of which
\mu
-function is a special case, and these naturally have theta functions in denominators which are explainable from the geometry of the quadratic forms. Also Zwegers’ two-variable
\tilde{\mu}
-function is decomposed into a sum of meromorphic Jacobi form and one-variable Maaß–Jacobi form
[
21
]
(see also
[
5
, Theorem 8.18]
).
However, as we will show in this paper, one can give another answer from the view of
q
-special functions.

By using the pseudo-periodicity equation (
1.2
) and a bit of calculation, we obtain the following second-order
q
-difference equation for the
\mu
-function:

\displaystyle\left[T_{x}^{2}-q^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(1-\frac{x}{y}q\right)T_{x}-\frac{x}{y}q\right]\mu(x,y)=0,\qquad T_{x}f(x):=f(qx).

(1.18)

This
q
-difference equation (
1.18
) coincides with the specialization
a=q
and the transformation
x\mapsto\frac{x}{y}
of the following equation essentially:

\displaystyle\big[T_{x}^{2}-(1-xq)\sqrt{a}T_{x}-xq\big]f(x)=0,

(1.19)

which is the transformation
a\mapsto\frac{q}{a}
,
u(xq)=\theta_{q}\big({-}\frac{\sqrt{a}}{xq}\big)f(x)
of the
q
-Hermite–Weber equation:

\displaystyle\big[axT_{x}^{2}+(1-x)T_{x}-1\big]u(x)=0.

The equation (
1.19
), which we also call the “
q
-Hermite–Weber equation”, is a typical example of the second-order
q
-difference equation of the Laplace type:

\displaystyle\big[(a_{0}+b_{0}x)T_{x}^{2}+(a_{1}+b_{1}x)T_{x}+(a_{2}+b_{2}x)\big]u(x)=0.

The
q
-difference equations of the Laplace type have long been studied.
For example, the
q
-difference equation satisfied by Heine’s hypergeometric function
{}_{2}\phi_{1}
:

\displaystyle\big[(c-abqx)T_{x}^{2}-(c+q-(a+b)qx)T_{x}+q(1-x)\big]f(x)=0,

(1.20)

which is the most popular and master class in the following hierarchy of the second order
q
-difference equations, was discovered in the 19th century.
The
q
-Hermite–Weber equation (
1.19
) is one of some equations in the hierarchy obtained by taking some limits of
q
-difference equation (
1.20
) (for example, see
[
16
, Figure 2]
).

However, a systematic study of the global analysis of degenerate Laplace types had to wait for J.P. Ramis, J. Sauloy and C. Zhang’s work
[
18
]
in the 21st century.
They introduced some
q
-Borel and
q
-Laplace transformations:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{+}(f)(\xi):=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}q^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\xi^{n},\qquad\mathcal{L}^{+}(f)(x,\lambda):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{f(\lambda q^{n})}{\theta_{q}(\lambda q^{n}/x)},

(1.21)

for the formal series

f(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{n}\in\mathbb{C}\llbracket x\rrbracket,

which play a fundamental role in the study of the Laplace type
q
-difference equations.
In particular, C. Zhang
[
22
]
obtained some connection formulas for the
q
-convergent hypergeometric series
{}_{2}\phi_{0}(a,b;q,x)
by applying these transformations.
By considering the degeneration of Zhang’s result, C. Zhang and Y. Ohyama
[
17
]
gave some connection formulas for the
q
-Hermite series
{}_{1}\phi_{1}(a;0;q,x)
.

In view of the story discussed above, we introduce the following generalization of the
\mu
-function.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\alpha
,
a
be complex parameters such that
u-\alpha\tau,v\in\mathbb{C}\backslash(\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\tau)
, and such that
xa^{-1},y\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{q^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
.
We define
\mu(u,v;\alpha)
or
\mu(x,y;a)
as the following series:

\displaystyle\mu(u,v;\alpha,\tau)=\mu(u,v;\alpha):=\frac{{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}\alpha(u-v)}}{\vartheta_{11}(v)}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{n}{\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}(n+\frac{1}{2})v}q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\frac{\big({\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}u}q^{n+1}\big)_{\infty}}{\big({\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}u}q^{n-\alpha+1}\big)_{\infty}},

\displaystyle\mu(x,y;a,q)=\mu(x,y;a):=-{\rm i}q^{-\frac{1}{8}}\frac{(x/y)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}}{\theta_{q}(-y)}\frac{(x)_{\infty}}{(x/a)_{\infty}}{}_{1}\psi_{2}\left(\begin{matrix}x/a\\
0,x\end{matrix};q,y\right).

In particular, we have
\mu(u,v;0)=-{\rm i}q^{-\frac{1}{8}}
and
\mu(u,v;1)=\mu(u,v)
.

If we substitute

,

and

The definition of

is equal to the composition of the

-Borel and

-Laplace transformations of the formal solution

-\end{matrix};q,{\frac{x}{a}}\right)

\displaystyle=\big(r{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}(u-v)}q,r{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}(u-v)}q\big)_{\infty}\mu(u,v)-{\rm i}rq^{\frac{7}{8}}\,{{}_{3}\phi_{2}}\left(\begin{matrix}q,r{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}(u-v)}q,r{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}(u-v)}q\\
0,0\end{matrix};q,q\right).\!\!\!

(3.15)

The conclusion follows from (
3.15
) and Andrews’ formula
[
8
, p. 298, Exercise 10.8]
:

\displaystyle{{}_{3}\phi_{2}}\left(\begin{matrix}a,b,c\\
d,e\end{matrix};q,x\right)=\frac{(ax,b,c)_{\infty}}{(x,d,e)_{\infty}}\Phi^{(1)}\left(\begin{matrix}x;d/b,e/c\\
ax\end{matrix};q;b,c\right).

(3.16)

Finally, we prove (
1.38
).
From (
1.37
), we have

\displaystyle S(r)=\big(r{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}(u-v)}q,r{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}(u-v)}q\big)_{\infty}

\displaystyle\hphantom{S(r) =}{}\times\left\{\mu(u,v)-\frac{{\rm i}rq^{\frac{7}{8}}}{1-q}\Phi^{(1)}\left(\begin{matrix}q;0,0\\
q^{2}\end{matrix};q;r{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}(u-v)}q,r{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}(u-v)}q\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\hphantom{S(r)}{}=\big(r{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}(u-v)}q,r{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}(u-v)}q\big)_{\infty}

\displaystyle\hphantom{S(r) =}{}\times\left\{\mu(u,v)-\frac{{\rm i}q^{\frac{7}{8}}}{1-q}\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(q)_{n}}{(q)_{k}(q)_{n-k}(q^{2})_{n}}{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}(2k-n)(u-v)}q^{n}r^{n+1}\right\}

\displaystyle\hphantom{S(r)}{}=\big(r{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}(u-v)}q,r{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}(u-v)}q\big)_{\infty}\left\{\mu(u,v)-\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{{\rm i}q^{\frac{7}{8}}H_{n}(\cos{\pi(u-v)}\mid q)}{(q)_{n+1}}q^{n}r^{n+1}\right\}.

From Theorem
1.6
,
H_{n}(x\mid q):={\rm i}q^{\frac{1}{8}}\mu(u,v;-n)
, so (
1.38
) holds.
∎

By re-expanding the generating function
S(r)
, we obtain the following relationship between the function
\mu(u,v;k+1)
and the continuous
q
-Hermite polynomials.

Corollary 3.6
.

For non-negative integers
k
, we have

\displaystyle\mu(u,v;k+1)=\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{q^{l}}{(q)_{l}}F_{k-l+1}(\cos\pi(u-v)\mid q)\mu(u,v;1-l),

(3.17)

where

\displaystyle F_{n+1}(\cos\pi w\mid q):={}

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}nw}\,{}_{1}\phi_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}q^{-n}\\
0\end{matrix};q,{\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}w}q\right)\frac{(-1)^{n}q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}}{(q)_{n}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-\frac{\pi{\rm i}w}{2\tau}}\frac{(q)_{\infty}}{(q^{-n})_{n}}J_{\frac{w}{\tau}}^{(2)}\big(2{\rm i}{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}n\tau};q\big).

For positive integers
m
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m}\mu{(u,v;k+1)}\frac{H_{m-k}(\cos{\pi(u-v)}\mid q)}{(q)_{m-k}}q^{m-k}=-\frac{{\rm i}q^{\frac{7}{8}}H_{m-1}(\cos{\pi(u-v)}\mid q)}{(q)_{m}}.

(3.18)

Proof.

From the Taylor expansion of the
q
-exponential function
[
8
, equation (II.2)]

(x)_{\infty}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{(q)_{n}}(-1)^{n}q^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}x^{n},

we have

\displaystyle\big({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta}rq,{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\theta}rq\big)_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-qr)^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{q^{\binom{k}{2}}q^{\binom{n-k}{2}}}{(q)_{k}(q)_{n-k}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}(n-2k)\theta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}{}_{1}\phi_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}q^{-n}\\
0\end{matrix};q,{\rm e}^{2{\rm i}\theta}q\right)\frac{(-1)^{n}q^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}}{(q)_{n}}({\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\theta}rq)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}F_{n+1}(\cos\pi(u-v)\mid q)r^{n}.

By expanding (
1.38
) and comparing the coefficient in (
1.38
), we obtain (
3.17
).
To prove (
3.18
), we divide by both sides of (
1.38
) by
\big({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta}rq,{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\theta}rq\big)_{\infty}
;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(r{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}(u-v)}q,r{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}(u-v)}q)_{\infty}}S(r)=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{\mu(u,v;1-m)}{(q)_{m}}q^{m}r^{m}.

(3.19)

By the generating function of continuous

-Hermite polynomial (

), the left-hand side of (

) is equal to

\sum_{m\geq 0}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\mu{(u,v;k+1)}\frac{H_{m-k}(\cos{\pi(u-v)}\mid q)}{(q)_{m-k}}q^{m-k}r^{m}.

Then we obtain the conclusion (

∎

4
Modular transformations related to
\boldsymbol{\mu(u,v;k,\tau)}

In this section, again let
k
be a positive integer.
We state that
\mu(u,v;k,\tau)
is essentially equivalent to the original
\mu
-function with respect to the properties of a real-analytic Jacobi form.

Proposition 4.1
.

We define a modified
\mu(u,v;k,\tau)
by

\displaystyle\tilde{\nu}(u,v;k,\tau):=\frac{\mu(u,v;k+1,\tau)}{F_{k+1}(\cos\pi(u-v)\mid q)}+\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}R_{k+1}(u-v;\tau),

where

R_{k+1}(u;\tau):=R(u;\tau)-2q^{-\frac{1}{8}}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\frac{q^{l}}{(q)_{l}}\frac{F_{k-l+1}(\cos\pi u\mid q)}{F_{k+1}(\cos\pi u\mid q)}H_{l-1}(\cos\pi u\mid q).

We have

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}(u,v;\tau)=\tilde{\nu}(u,v;k,\tau).

Therefore, the following transformations hold;

\displaystyle\tilde{\nu}(u,v;k,\tau+1)={\rm e}^{-\frac{\pi{\rm i}}{4}}\tilde{\nu}(u,v;k,\tau),

(4.1)

\displaystyle\tilde{\nu}\left(\frac{u}{\tau},\frac{v}{\tau};k,-\frac{1}{\tau}\right)=-{\rm i}\sqrt{-{\rm i}\tau}{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}\frac{(u-v)^{2}}{\tau}}\tilde{\nu}(u,v;k,\tau).

(4.2)

Proof.

From equation (
3.17
) of Corollary
3.6
,

\displaystyle\mu(u,v;\tau)-\frac{\mu(u,v;k+1,\tau)}{F_{k+1}(\cos\pi(u-v)\mid q)}

\displaystyle\qquad{}={\rm i}q^{-\frac{1}{8}}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\frac{q^{l}}{(q)_{l}}\frac{F_{k-l+1}(\cos\pi(u-v)\mid q)}{F_{k+1}(\cos\pi(u-v)\mid q)}H_{l-1}(\cos\pi(u-v)\mid q).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}(u,v;\tau)=\mu(u,v;\tau)+\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}R(u-v;\tau)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tilde{\mu}(u,v;\tau)}{}=\frac{\mu(u,v;k+1,\tau)}{F_{k+1}(\cos\pi(u-v)\mid q)}+\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}R(u-v;\tau)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tilde{\mu}(u,v;\tau)=}{}+{\rm i}q^{-\frac{1}{8}}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\frac{q^{l}}{(q)_{l}}\frac{F_{k-l+1}(\cos\pi(u-v)\mid q)}{F_{k+1}(\cos\pi(u-v)\mid q)}H_{l-1}(\cos\pi(u-v)\mid q)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tilde{\mu}(u,v;\tau)}{}=\frac{\mu(u,v;k+1,\tau)}{F_{k+1}(\cos\pi(u-v)\mid q)}+\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}R_{k+1}(u-v;\tau).

∎

Remark 4.2
.

T. Matsusaka also mentions this proposition
[
15
]
.

5
Remarks for further studies

In this paper, from the point of view of the analysis of one-variable linear
q
-difference equations of the Laplace type, we have studied a generalization of Zwegers’
\mu
-function
\mu(x,y;a)
which satisfies the
q
-Hermite–Weber equation (
1.19
).

On the other hand, the generalized
\mu
-function
\mu(x,y;a)
is a two-variable function originally.
More precisely,
\mu(x,y;a)
is closely related to the
q
-Appell hypergeometric function
\Phi^{(1)}
(
1.39
) and its
q
-difference system which we call
q
-Appell difference system:

\displaystyle[(1-T_{x})(1-c/qT_{x}T_{y})-x(1-aT_{x}T_{y})(1-b_{1}T_{x})]\Phi(x,y)=0,

\displaystyle[(1-T_{y})(1-c/qT_{x}T_{y})-y(1-aT_{x}T_{y})(1-b_{2}T_{y})]\Phi(x,y)=0,

\displaystyle[x(1-T_{y})(1-b_{1}T_{x})-y(1-T_{x})(1-b_{2}T_{y})]\Phi(x,y)=0.

(5.1)

The first and second equations are essentially equivalent to Gasper–Rahman
[
8
, Exercises 10.12(i) and (ii)]
which have some small typos (the terms
(c/q-a)f(qx,qy)
in (i) and (ii) should be
(c/q-ax)f(qx,qy)
and
(c/q-ay)f(qx,qy)
).
The third equation (
5.1
) is a reducible factor of the following
q
-difference equation:

\displaystyle(1-T_{x})[(1-T_{y})(1-c/qT_{x}T_{y})-y(1-aT_{x}T_{y})(1-b_{2}T_{y})]\Phi(x,y)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}-(1-T_{y})[(1-T_{x})(1-c/qT_{x}T_{y})-x(1-aT_{x}T_{y})(1-b_{1}T_{x})]\Phi(x,y)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=(1-aT_{x}T_{y})[x(1-T_{y})(1-b_{1}T_{x})-y(1-T_{x})(1-b_{2}T_{y})]\Phi(x,y)=0

and we easily show that
\Phi^{(1)}
satisfies (
5.1
).

First,
\Phi^{(1)}
appears in the expression of
\mu(x,y;a)
.
In Theorem
1.4
, by dividing the bilateral sum of
q
-hypergeometric series
{}_{2}\psi_{2}
and
{}_{0}\psi_{2}
into two parts, positive and negative;

\displaystyle{}_{2}\psi_{2}\left(\begin{matrix}x/a,y/a\\

\displaystyle\hphantom{{}_2\psi_2\left(\begin{matrix}x/a,y/a\\
0,0\end{matrix};q,a\right)=}{}+{}_{2}\phi_{2}\left(\begin{matrix}0,q\\
aq/x,aq/y\end{matrix};q,-\frac{aq^{2}}{xy}\right),

\displaystyle{}_{0}\psi_{2}\left(\begin{matrix}-\\
x,y\end{matrix};q,\frac{xy}{a}\right)=\frac{1}{(1-x)(1-y)}{}_{1}\phi_{2}\left(\begin{matrix}q\\

we rewrite Theorem

\displaystyle{\rm i}q^{\frac{1}{8}}\mu(x,y;a)={\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}\alpha(u-v)}\left\{\frac{xy}{a}\frac{(a,q,a/x,a/y)_{\infty}}{\theta_{q}(-y)\theta_{q}(-x/a)}{}_{3}\phi_{2}\left(\begin{matrix}xq/a,yq/a,q\\

\displaystyle[x(1-T_{y})-y(1-T_{x})]\Phi(x,y)=0.

(5.2)

Theorem 5.1
.

The function

\nu(x,y;a):={\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}\alpha(u-v)}\frac{\theta_{q}(-ay)}{\theta_{q}(-y)}\mu(ax,ay;a)

-difference equation (

\displaystyle(1-aT_{x}T_{y})\nu(x,y;a)=0,

(5.3)

\displaystyle[x(1-T_{y})-y(1-T_{x})]\nu(x,y;a)=0.

(5.4)

Proof.

To prove the first and second equations of (

), it is enough to show (
5.3

By simple calculation, we have

\displaystyle aT_{x}T_{y}\nu(x,y;a)=a\frac{\theta_{q}(-ayq)}{\theta_{q}(-yq)}\mu(axq,ayq;a)=a\frac{-a^{-1}y^{-1}}{-y^{-1}}\frac{\theta_{q}(-ay)}{\theta_{q}(-y)}\mu(x,y;a)=\nu(x,y;a).

Here, the second equality follows from

1.28

Another equation (

) is proved as follows:

\displaystyle(xT_{y}-yT_{x})\nu(x,y;a)=x{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}\alpha(u-v-\tau)}\frac{\theta_{q}(-ayq)}{\theta_{q}(-yq)}\mu(ax,ayq;a)

\displaystyle\hphantom{(xT_y-yT_x)\nu(x,y;a)=}{}-y{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}\alpha(u-v+\tau)}\frac{\theta_{q}(-ay)}{\theta_{q}(-y)}\mu(axq,ay;a)

\displaystyle\hphantom{(xT_y-yT_x)\nu(x,y;a)}{}={\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}\alpha(u-v)}\frac{\theta_{q}(-ay)}{\theta_{q}(-y)}\big\{(-y\mu(ax,ay;a)+\sqrt{xy}\mu(ax,ay;a/q))

\displaystyle\hphantom{(xT_y-yT_x)\nu(x,y;a)=}{}-(-x\mu(ax,ay;a)+\sqrt{xy}\mu(ax,ay;a/q))\big\}

\displaystyle\hphantom{(xT_y-yT_x)\nu(x,y;a)}{}=(x-y)\nu(x,y;a).

and
