Abstract
. We present stability and error analysis for algebraic flux correction schemes based on monolithic convex limiting. For a continuous finite element discretization of the time-dependent advection equation, we prove global-in-time existence and the worst-case convergence rate of 1 2 \frac{1}{2} w. r. t. the L 2 L^{2} error of the spatial semi-discretization. Moreover, we address the important issue of stabilization for raw antidiffusive fluxes. Our a priori error analysis reveals that their limited counterparts should satisfy a generalized coercivity condition. We introduce a limiter for enforcing this condition in the process of flux correction. To verify the results of our theoretical studies, we perform numerical experiments for simple one-dimensional test problems. The methods under investigation exhibit the expected behavior in all numerical examples. In particular, the use of stabilized fluxes improves the accuracy of numerical solutions and coercivity enforcement often becomes redundant.



1.
Introduction

Algebraic flux correction (AFC) schemes were proposed in
[
17
,
24
]
and have recently become an active research area (
e. g.,

[
1
,
7
,
14
,
19
,
29
]
).
These methods provide a robust framework guaranteeing that discrete maximum principles hold and/or that entropy conditions are satisfied in the case of nonlinear equations. Nonlinear AFC approaches combine a high order baseline scheme, such as the Galerkin finite element discretization, with a provably bound-preserving low order approximation.
In this manner, global and/or local constraints can be imposed on the values of AFC solutions resulting from discretization of various partial differential equations (PDEs).

The focus of most efforts dealing with AFC schemes was on the development of numerical algorithms, while theoretical aspects have only recently started to attract significant interest.
Barrenechea, John and Knobloch
[
3
]
were the first to show solvability of the nonlinear problem and prove a convergence rate of
\frac{1}{2}
for stationary convection-diffusion-reaction equations.
In their subsequent work
[
4
]
, they derived a sharper, first order error estimate under the assumption that the limiter is
linearity preserving
.
Unfortunately, the proof technique that was used to obtain this
superconvergence result relies on the presence of diffusive terms.
Lohmann
[
25
]
extended the analysis of AFC schemes to
the case without diffusive terms and obtained similar theoretical results for
linear hyperbolic problems, again with a provable rate of
\frac{1}{2}
.
Other theoretically investigated aspects of AFC procedures include their connection to edge-based diffusion
[
2
]
, proofs of invariant domain preservation for the low order method
[
7
]
, and a study of nonlinear solvers
[
13
]
.
The recent work of Jha and Ahmed
[
12
]
presents the first theoretical study of AFC schemes for parabolic convection-diffusion-reaction equations. Their analysis of the fully discrete problem for the backward Euler time discretization combined with the Galerkin space discretization
is, again, not readily applicable to the hyperbolic case.

This work presents semi-discrete stability and error analysis of AFC schemes for finite element discretizations of the time-dependent linear advection equation. To cure the oscillatory behavior of the continuous Galerkin method, we stabilize the antidiffusive fluxes using high order dissipation. Flux correction is performed using Kuzmin’s
[
21
]
monolithic convex limiter with a modification that we propose to enforce a generalized coercivity condition. The use of this condition as a criterion for flux limiting is motivated by our theoretical analysis. We prove that the nonlinear semi-discrete scheme is stable and its spatial accuracy
w. r. t.
L^{2}
norm is at least
\frac{1}{2}
for linear finite elements and general meshes. In practice, second order superconvergence can be expected for smooth solutions and uniform meshes, as shown in the 1D numerical examples of this paper and, for instance, in
[
1
,
12
,
26
]
.

The structure of this article is as follows. We begin with the formulation of the continuous problem and review the standard discretization procedure using a finite element method. The next section deals with construction of monolithic AFC schemes, including the aspects of high order stabilization and coercivity enforcement in the process of flux correction. Following the description of numerical algorithms, a section on numerical analysis presents the main theoretical outcomes of this effort. Finally, we report the results of numerical experiments for 1D test problems
and make some concluding remarks.

2.
Model problem and finite element discretization

Let
(0,T)
be a finite time interval and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{1,2,3\}
a polytopal domain with Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
.
Advection of a scalar quantity
u=u(t,\boldsymbol{x})

by a prescribed velocity field
\boldsymbol{a}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)^{d})

in
\Omega
is modeled by the initial boundary-value problem

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{a}u)=\;

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\text{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega,

(2.1b)

\displaystyle u=\;

\displaystyle u^{\textup{in}}

\displaystyle\text{ on }(0,T)\times\Gamma_{-},

(2.1c)

\displaystyle u=\;

\displaystyle u_{0}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\{0\}\times\Omega.

The inlet
\Gamma_{-}
and the complementary part

\Gamma_{+}
of
\partial\Omega

are defined by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{-}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}}=\Gamma_{-}(t)=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega:\boldsymbol{a}(t,\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{x})<0\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{+}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}}=\Gamma_{+}(t)=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega:\boldsymbol{a}(t,\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{x})\geq 0\},

where
\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the outward unit normal
\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{x})
to
\partial\Omega
. The inflow and outflow parts of
\partial\Omega
may change as time proceeds, but the possible dependence on
t
is suppressed in the notation.
In this work, the velocity is assumed to be solenoidal,
i. e.,

\nabla\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{a}=0
. Therefore, the conservative form
\nabla\!\cdot\!(\boldsymbol{a}u)
of the advective term is equivalent to the non-conservative form
\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\nabla u
.
For analytical purposes, we require that the data satisfy
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega)\cap C(\overline{\Omega})
and
u^{\textup{in}}\in L^{2}(0,T;C(\overline{\Gamma_{-}}))
.

At each time instant
t\in(0,T)
, the weak solution
u(t,\cdot)
to (
2.1
) is an element of the
graph space

V\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}}=\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega):\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\nabla v\in L^{2}(\Omega)\}
,
[
5
, Def. 2.1]
.
The weak formulation of (
2.1
) reads: Find

u\in C(0,T;V)\cap C^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
such that

u(0,\cdot)=u_{0}
in
\Omega
and

(2.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}+a(u,w)=\;

\displaystyle b(w)\qquad\forall w\in V,\penalty\ t\in(0,T),

(2.3)

\displaystyle a(u,w)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}}=\;

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w\nabla\!\cdot\!(\boldsymbol{a}u)\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}-\int_{\Gamma_{-}}wu\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\,\textup{d}\sigma,

(2.4)

\displaystyle b(w)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}}=\;

\displaystyle-\int_{\Gamma_{-}}wu^{\textup{in}}\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\,\textup{d}\sigma.

We refer to
[
5
, Sec. 3.1.1]
for
a discussion on further theoretical properties of the continuous problem.
Instead, we focus on the spatial discretization of (
2.1
).
For a fixed discretization parameter
h>0
, let
\mathcal{K}_{h}=\{K^{1},\dots,K^{E}\}
be a triangulation of
\Omega
satisfying
\diam{K^{e}}\leq h
for all
e=1,\dots,E
.
For simplicity, we restrict our analysis to the case of simplicial elements and assume that the mesh has no hanging nodes.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{1}(K)
be the space of linear polynomials on
K
. Then

\displaystyle V_{h}=\{v_{h}\in C(\bar{\Omega}):v_{h}|_{K}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(K)\penalty\ \forall K\in\mathcal{K}_{h}\}

is a finite-dimensional subspace of
V
. Any
v_{h}\in V_{h}

can be written as

\displaystyle v_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}\varphi_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}),

where
\varphi_{i}\in V_{h}
are piecewise linear Lagrange basis functions such that
\varphi_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})=\delta_{ij}
for all
i,j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. The degrees of freedom
v_{i}=v_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
represent the values of
v_{h}
at the mesh vertices
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. In particular, a finite element approximation
u_{h}
to the solution of (
2.2
) is uniquely determined by its values at the vertices. The semi-discrete version of
problem (
2.2
) reads: Find
u_{h}\in C^{1}(0,T;V_{h})
such that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w_{h}\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial t}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}+a(u_{h},w_{h})=\;

\displaystyle b(w_{h})\qquad\forall w_{h}\in V_{h},\penalty\ t\in(0,T)

and
u_{h}(0,\cdot)=\mathrm{I}_{h}u_{0}
, where
\mathrm{I}_{h}
is a suitable projection operator.
Since
u_{0}
is assumed to be continuous, we can use the interpolation operator

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}_{h}:C(\overline{\Omega})\rightarrow V_{h},\qquad\mathrm{I}_{h}w=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\varphi_{i}\quad\forall w\in C(\overline{\Omega}).

Remark 2.1
.

To ensure conservation of mass
\int_{\Omega}u_{h}(0,\cdot)\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\int_{\Omega}u_{0}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}
, an
L^{2}
-type projection can be used instead
of pointwise interpolation. The standard
L^{2}
projection may, however,
produce an approximation
u_{h}(0,\cdot)
that violates maximum principles
and/or exhibits spurious oscillations. For a conservative and
bound-preserving initialization of
u_{h}
, one can use a lumped-mass or
FCT-constrained
L^{2}
projection
[
22
]
.

To obtain an evolution equation for the time-dependent
degree of freedom
u_{i}(t)
, we test with
w_{h}=\varphi_{i}
in
the semi-discrete weak form (
2.5
) of the advection
equation. This choice of
w_{h}
yields

(2.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[m_{ij}\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{j}}{\mathrm{d}t}+a_{ij}u_{j}\right]=b_{i}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}}=\int_{\Gamma_{-}}\varphi_{i}u^{\textup{in}}|\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,\textup{d}\sigma,

\displaystyle m_{ij}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}}=\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x},\qquad a_{ij}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}}=\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{i}\,\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}+\int_{\Gamma_{-}}\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}|\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,\textup{d}\sigma.

In the formulas for
a_{ij}
and
b_{i}
, we use the assumption that

\boldsymbol{a}
is solenoidal and the fact that
\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}<0
on
\Gamma_{-}

by definition.
The sum on the left hand side of (
2.6
)
reduces to that over the nodal
stencil

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{i}=\{j\in\{1,\dots,N\}:m_{ij}\neq 0\}

because
m_{ij}=0
and
a_{ij}=0
for all
j\notin\mathcal{N}_{i}
.

Unfortunately, the standard Galerkin discretization (
2.6
)
exhibits unsatisfactory convergence behavior both in theory and in practice.
For advection problems with smooth initial data, optimal
convergence behavior can be achieved by adding stabilization terms
to (
2.5
);
see
[
30
, Sec. 14.3.1–2]
. However, even stabilized
versions of (
2.5
)
tend to produce undershoots/overshoots in the vicinity
of steep gradients. The lack of stabilization
allows local errors to grow and spread throughout the domain. These shortcomings of the Galerkin discretization can be cured using the methods presented in the following section.

3.
Algebraic flux correction tools

The poor performance of standard finite element discretizations and stabilization techniques can be attributed to the lack of direct control over the properties of the resulting sparse matrices, which ultimately determine the qualitative behavior of numerical solutions. The concept of algebraic flux correction offers a direct way to enforce discrete maximum principles. The AFC methodology traces its origins to
[
17
,
24
]
and provides a general framework for algebraic manipulations of high order (semi-)discretizations. Similarly to other nonlinear high-resolution schemes for hyperbolic problems, the key idea is to blend a high order
target scheme
with a provably bound-preserving
low order method
. The latter is constructed by adding a discrete diffusion (graph Laplacian) operator to the discrete transport operator of the baseline scheme. At a subsequent correction step, limited antidiffusive fluxes are used to remove unnecessary artificial diffusion in smooth regions.

In the remainder of this section, we introduce the main components of the nonlinear AFC methods that we study in this work. First, we present the monolithic convex limiting algorithm developed in
[
21
]
. Next, we discuss the recommendable use of high order stabilization for antidiffusive fluxes that define the target scheme. Finally, we derive a new limiting procedure for enforcing a coercivity condition, which we later need to prove stability and obtain an a priori error estimate.

3.1.
Standard monolithic convex limiting

To derive a low order method from the matrix form
M_{C}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}t}+Au=b
of (
2.6
),
the consistent mass matrix
M_{C}=(m_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{N}
is approximated by its lumped counterpart
M_{L}=(m_{i}\delta_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{N}
, where

m_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{ij}
.
Furthermore, the discrete advection operator

A=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{N}
is replaced by
A-D
, where

D=(d_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{N}
is a discrete
diffusion operator defined by

(3.1)

\displaystyle d_{ij}=\begin{cases}\max\{|a_{ij}|,|a_{ji}|\}&\mbox{if }i\neq j,\\
-\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\setminus\{i\}}d_{ik}&\mbox{if }i=j.\end{cases}

Note that these manipulations are conservative because the matrices

M_{C}-M_{L}
and
D
represent graph Laplacian operators.

The semi-discrete form of the low order method is given by

(3.2)

\displaystyle m_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}(d_{ij}-a_{ij})u_{j}+b_{i}.

Recalling the definitions of
a_{ij}
and
b_{i}
in (
2.6
),
we find that

\displaystyle b_{i}-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}a_{ij}u_{j}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{-}}\varphi_{i}(u^{\textup{in}}-u_{i})|\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,\textup{d}\sigma-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j}\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{i}\,\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\backslash\{i\}}(u_{j}-u_{i})\int_{\Gamma_{-}}\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}|\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,\textup{d}\sigma.

Since the basis functions
\varphi_{j}

form a partition of unity, we
have

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{i}\,\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{i}\,\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\nabla\Big(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{j}\Big)\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}=0.

It follows that

b_{i}-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}a_{ij}u_{j}=\int_{\Gamma_{-}}\varphi_{i}(u^{\textup{in}}-u_{i})|\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,\textup{d}\sigma-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\backslash\{i\}}a_{ij}(u_{j}-u_{i}).

Using this representation and the zero row sum
property of the artificial diffusion operator
D
, we reformulate (
3.2
) as follows:

(3.3)

m_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\setminus\{i\}}(d_{ij}-a_{ij})(u_{j}-u_{i})+\int_{\Gamma_{-}}\varphi_{i}(u^{\textup{in}}-u_{i})|\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,\textup{d}\sigma.

If
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\Gamma_{-}
, the integral over
\Gamma_{-}
reduces to that over
\Gamma_{i}^{-}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}}=\Gamma_{-}\cap\supp\varphi_{i}
, where
\supp
denotes the support of a function.
For
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\notin\Gamma_{-}
, this integral is zero, therefore we set
\Gamma_{i}^{-}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}}=\emptyset
in this case.

We say that
u_{i}
is a
local extremum
if
u_{i}=u_{i}^{\min}
or
u_{i}=u_{i}^{\max}
for

\displaystyle u_{i}^{\min}=\min\{\min_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j},\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Gamma_{i}^{-}}u^{\textup{in}}(\boldsymbol{x})\},\quad u_{i}^{\max}=\max\{\max_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j},\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Gamma_{i}^{-}}u^{\textup{in}}(\boldsymbol{x})\}.

The
right hand side of (
3.3
) is nonpositive for
u_{i}=u_{i}^{\max}

and nonnegative for
u_{i}=u_{i}^{\min}
. This implies that
a local maximum cannot increase, while a local minimum cannot decrease.
Hence, (
3.3
) belongs to the family of

local extremum diminishing
(LED) methods.

Remark 3.1
.

The LED scheme (
3.3
) is, indeed, a low order approximation to
(
2.6
). The diffusive fluxes
d_{ij}(u_{j}-u_{i})
introduce an
h^{1/2}
consistency error on general meshes
[
3
,
25
]
.

In the AFC literature, (
3.3
) with
d_{ij}
defined by
(
3.1
) is referred to as the
Rusanov scheme

or
algebraic Lax–Friedrichs method
. For theoretical
analysis purposes, (
3.3
) can be written as
[
7
]

(3.4)

\displaystyle m_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\setminus\{i\}}2d_{ij}(\bar{u}_{ij}-u_{i})+\int_{\Gamma_{-}}\varphi_{i}(u^{\textup{in}}-u_{i})|\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,\textup{d}\sigma

using the
bar states

(3.5)

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{ij}=\frac{u_{i}+u_{j}}{2}-\frac{a_{ij}(u_{j}-u_{i})}{2d_{ij}},

which satisfy
\min\{u_{i},u_{j}\}\leq\bar{u}_{ij}\leq\max\{u_{i},u_{j}\}

by (
3.1
). If discretization in time is performed
using an explicit strong stability preserving (SSP) Runge-Kutta
method, each forward Euler stage produces a convex combination
of the states
u_{i}
and
\bar{u}_{ij}
, provided the time step is chosen
sufficiently small
[
7
,
21
]
. It follows that
the fully discrete counterpart of (
3.2
)
satisfies a local discrete maximum principle.

Remark 3.2
.

The
discrete upwinding
method
[
17
,
24
]

uses the artificial diffusion coefficients

d_{ij}=\max\{a_{ij},0,a_{ji}\}
for
j\neq i
. This definition
of
d_{ij}
also ensures the LED property for linear advection
problems and is slightly less dissipative than (
3.1
).
However, it does not generally guarantee that the bar states
(
3.5
) stay between
u_{i}
and
u_{j}
.

To construct a high order LED approximation, we
insert antidiffusive fluxes
f_{ij}=-f_{ji}
into (
3.4
)
and consider the AFC scheme

(3.6)

\displaystyle m_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\setminus\{i\}}\left[2d_{ij}(\bar{u}_{ij}-u_{i})+f_{ij}\right]+\int_{\Gamma_{-}}\varphi_{i}(u^{\textup{in}}-u_{i})|\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,\textup{d}\sigma.

Note that the Galerkin approximation (
2.6
) is recovered if we use

(3.7)

\displaystyle f_{ij}=d_{ij}(u_{i}-u_{j})+m_{ij}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}-\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{j}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right).

In practice, one needs to approximate the time derivatives appearing in (
3.7
).
This issue will be discussed in detail in the next section. For now, let
\dot{u}_{h}\in V_{h}
be a suitable approximation to
\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{h}}{\mathrm{d}t}
and consider

(3.8)

f_{ij}=d_{ij}(u_{i}-u_{j})+m_{ij}(\dot{u}_{i}-\dot{u}_{j}).

To ensure preservation of local bounds, the
monolithic convex limiting
(MCL) approach
[
21
]
replaces the so-defined target flux
f_{ij}
by

(3.9)

\displaystyle f_{ij}^{*}=\begin{cases}\min\{f_{ij},2d_{ij}(u_{i}^{\max}-\bar{u}_{ij}),2d_{ij}(\bar{u}_{ji}-u_{j}^{\min})\}&\mbox{if }f_{ij}\geq 0,\\
\max\{f_{ij},2d_{ij}(u_{i}^{\min}-\bar{u}_{ij}),2d_{ij}(\bar{u}_{ji}-u_{j}^{\max})\}&\mbox{if }f_{ij}\leq 0.\\
\end{cases}

This modification can be interpreted as multiplication of
f_{ij}
by a correction factor
\alpha_{ij}\in[0,1]
, satisfying the symmetry condition
\alpha_{ij}=\alpha_{ji}
.
The flux-corrected version of the target scheme
(
3.6
) uses

f_{ij}^{*}=\alpha_{ij}f_{ij}

instead of
f_{ij}
. It is easy to verify that the limited bar states

(3.10)

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{ij}^{*}=\bar{u}_{ij}+\frac{f_{ij}^{*}}{2d_{ij}}

stay in the range
[u_{i}^{\min},u_{i}^{\max}]
for
f_{ij}^{*}
defined by (
3.9
). Thus the bound-preserving property of the MCL scheme

(3.11)

\displaystyle m_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\;

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\setminus\{i\}}2d_{ij}(\bar{u}_{ij}^{*}-u_{i})+\int_{\Gamma_{-}}\varphi_{i}(u^{\textup{in}}-u_{i})|\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,\textup{d}\sigma

can be shown in the same way as for the low order method; see
[
21
]
.

Substituting (
3.10
) into (
3.11
) and recalling that
(
3.4
) is an equivalent form of (
3.2
), we obtain

m_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=b_{i}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}(d_{ij}-a_{ij})u_{j}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\setminus\{i\}}f_{ij}^{*}.

This representation of (
3.11
)
can be used for practical implementation purposes. For

f_{ij}^{*}=\alpha_{ij}f_{ij}
with
f_{ij}
defined by
Eq.

3.8
,
it becomes

\displaystyle m_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\;b_{i}-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}a_{ij}u_{j}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\setminus\{i\}}(1-\alpha_{ij})d_{ij}(u_{j}-u_{i})

(3.12)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\setminus\{i\}}\alpha_{ij}m_{ij}(\dot{u}_{i}-\dot{u}_{j}).

The fact that the correction factors
\alpha_{ij}
depend on the numerical solution
u_{h}
has been suppressed in our notation so far.
We make this dependence apparent in the definition of the
nonlinear forms
[
3
]

(3.13)

\displaystyle d_{h}(v_{h};w_{h},z_{h})=\;

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\setminus\{i\}}(1-\alpha_{ij}(v_{h}))d_{ij}(w_{i}-w_{j}),

(3.14)

\displaystyle m_{h}(v_{h};w_{h},z_{h})=\;

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\setminus\{i\}}\alpha_{ij}(v_{h})m_{ij}(w_{i}-w_{j})

such that (
3.12
) holds for all
i=1,\dots,N
if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}m_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle+a(u_{h},w_{h})+d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},w_{h})

(3.15)

\displaystyle=b(w_{h})+m_{h}(u_{h};\dot{u}_{h},w_{h})\quad\forall w_{h}\in V_{h}.

This semi-discrete weak form of
the advection equation has the structure required for
finite element analysis of AFC schemes.

To derive a priori error estimates for (
3.1
) in

Section

4
, we need to show the validity of
the generalized coercivity condition (GCC)

(3.16)

\frac{\gamma h}{\lambda}m_{h}(u_{h};\dot{u}_{h},\dot{u}_{h})\leq(1-\gamma)d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},u_{h})-m_{h}(u_{h};\dot{u}_{h},u_{h}),

where
\gamma\in(0,1)
is independent of
h

and
\lambda=

\|\mathbf{a}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
is
the maximum velocity. Note that

d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},u_{h})\geq 0
for any
u_{h}\in V_{h}
(see

[
3
,
25
]
or

Lemma

4.6
below). Hence, condition
(
3.16
) holds for any
\gamma\in(0,1)
in the
case
\dot{u}_{h}\equiv 0
, i.e., for the lumped-mass
version of the target scheme. However, violations of
(
3.16
) are possible for other choices of
\dot{u}_{h}
.
Therefore, the validity of GCC may need to
be enforced using the modification of
MCL that we propose in
Section

3.3
.

Remark 3.3
.

As we show in
Lemma

4.4
below,
a(u_{h},u_{h})\geq 0
for
any
u_{h}\in V_{h}
. The GCC criterion (
3.16
) could be made
less restrictive by adding
a(u_{h},u_{h})
on the right hand side.
However, the Galerkin part
a(u_{h},u_{h})
is small in the
case of linear advection and can be negative for nonlinear
conservation laws, which we are planning to consider in the
future. For that reason, we do not include
a(u_{h},u_{h})

in (
3.16
).

3.2.
High order stabilization

The fluxes
f_{ij}
defined by (
3.7
) correspond
to the standard continuous Galerkin method which
tends to produce spurious ripples and spread local errors.
This unsatisfactory behavior cannot be cured by the flux
limiting procedure because the local bounds of the inequality
constraints are too wide to filter out small-scale oscillations.
In the literature on finite volume methods, centered
flux approximations
are commonly stabilized using second order artificial
viscosity in shock regions and fourth order background
dissipation elsewhere
[
9
,
10
,
32
,
28
]
.
This approach traces its origins to the classical Jameson-Schmidt-Turkel
(JST) scheme

[
11
]
.

Algebraic flux correction schemes for continuous
finite elements can also be configured to use
fluxes
f_{ij}
that include a high order dissipative
component
[
20
,
23
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
.
As shown in
[
18
,
21
,
25
]
, the
desired stabilization effect can be achieved by using a
smoothed approximation to the time derivatives
that appear in (
3.7
).
In this work, we define the fluxes
(
3.8
)
using approximate time derivatives of the form

(3.17)

\dot{u}_{i}=\dot{u}_{i}^{A}+\dot{u}_{i}^{D},

where

\dot{u}_{i}^{A}=\frac{1}{m_{i}}\left(b_{i}-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}a_{ij}u_{j}\right)

is the advective
flux potential
corresponding to the lumped-mass
version of the Galerkin scheme (
2.6
). The dissipative flux potential

(3.18)

\dot{u}_{i}^{D}=\frac{\omega}{m_{i}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\backslash\{i\}}d_{ij}(u_{j}-u_{i})

stabilizes
\dot{u}_{i}^{A}
using the artificial diffusion coefficients
d_{ij}

of the low order method.
The amount of high order stabilization
can be adjusted using the parameter
\omega\in[0,1]
. We use

\omega=1
by default.

Remark 3.4
.

Antidiffusive fluxes of AFC schemes based on discontinuous Galerkin (DG)
methods (as proposed, e.g., in

[
1
,
8
,
14
,
29
]
)
do not require stabilization of
\dot{u}_{i}^{A}
via
\dot{u}_{i}^{D}

because DG discretizations of the
linear advection equation are inherently stable.

To motivate the use of (
3.18
) and construct a
coercive high order stabilization operator, we consider the
alternative definition

(3.19)

\dot{u}_{i}^{D}=\omega\frac{\beta_{i}}{m_{i}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}m_{il}\beta_{l}\Delta_{l}(u_{h})\qquad

of
\dot{u}_{i}^{D}
in terms of the discrete Laplacians

\Delta_{l}(u_{h})=\frac{1}{m_{l}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{l}\backslash\{l\}}m_{lk}(u_{k}-u_{l})

and the scaling factors

\beta_{l}=\sqrt{\frac{\max_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{l}\backslash\{l\}}d_{lk}}{\min_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{l}\backslash\{l\}}m_{lk}}}.

Note that definition (
3.19
)
is similar to (
3.18
)
but uses a mass-weighted average
of discrete Laplacians instead of a single discrete Laplacian. The
parameters
\beta_{l}
are defined so that the physical
units are correct and (
3.18
)
coincides with the lumped-mass version of (
3.19
) for
linear advection with constant velocity on uniform meshes in 1D.

Suppose that no limiting is performed (i.e., all correction factors

\alpha_{ij}
are set to 1) and the advective potentials
\dot{u}_{i}^{A}=0

are used (e.g., because only the steady-state solution is of interest).
Then the dissipative fluxes
m_{ij}(\dot{u}_{i}^{D}-\dot{u}_{j}^{D})
with

\dot{u}_{i}^{D}
defined by (
3.19
)
transform the lumped-mass Galerkin discretization into the stabilized
target scheme

\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}m_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}+a(u_{h},w_{h})+s_{h}(u_{h},w_{h})=b(w_{h})\quad\forall w_{h}\in V_{h}.

High order stabilization is represented by the bilinear form
(cf.
[
28
]
)

\displaystyle s_{h}(w_{h},z_{h})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\backslash\{i\}}m_{ij}(\dot{w}_{j}^{D}-\dot{w}_{i}^{D})

\displaystyle=\omega\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\backslash\{i\}}m_{ij}\bigg(\frac{\beta_{j}}{m_{j}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{j}}m_{jk}\beta_{k}\Delta_{k}(w_{h})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad-\frac{\beta_{i}}{m_{i}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}m_{il}\beta_{l}\Delta_{l}(w_{h})\bigg)

\displaystyle=\omega\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\beta_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}\Delta_{j}(\varphi_{i})\sum_{k=1}^{N}m_{jk}\beta_{k}\Delta_{k}(w_{h})

\displaystyle=\omega\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\beta_{j}\Delta_{j}(z_{h})\sum_{k=1}^{N}m_{jk}\beta_{k}\Delta_{k}(w_{h})

\displaystyle=\omega(\Delta_{h}^{\beta}(z_{h}),\Delta_{h}^{\beta}(w_{h}))_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where
\Delta_{h}^{\beta}(z_{h})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\beta_{j}\Delta_{j}(z_{h})
and

\Delta_{j}(z_{h})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}\Delta_{j}(\varphi_{i})
with

\Delta_{j}(\varphi_{i})=\frac{1}{m_{j}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{j}\backslash\{j\}}m_{jk}(\delta_{ki}-\delta_{ji})=\begin{cases}\frac{m_{ji}}{m_{j}}&\mbox{if}\ j\neq i,\\
-\frac{1}{m_{i}}\sum\limits_{l\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\backslash\{i\}}m_{il}&\mbox{if}\ j=i.\end{cases}

In view of the above, the biharmonic operator
s_{h}(w_{h},z_{h})

introduces fourth order background dissipation and
is coercive. However, the bilinear form induced by the
combined contribution
m_{ij}(\dot{u}_{i}-\dot{u}_{j})

of the advective and dissipative flux potentials is
generally not coercive and may require further correction
after bound-preserving flux limiting.

3.3.
Coercivity enforcement via limiting

Adopting the simpler definition (
3.18
) of

\dot{u}_{i}^{D}
, we limit the stabilized
target fluxes (
3.8
) in a manner which ensures
coercivity and enables us to obtain an
a priori error estimate in
Section

4
.
Splitting the flux

f_{ij}
into
f_{ij}^{D}=d_{ij}(u_{i}-u_{j})
and

f_{ij}^{M}=m_{ij}(\dot{u}_{i}-\dot{u}_{j})
, we first use
the standard MCL formula (
3.9
) to limit
f_{ij}^{D}
. This
step produces
f_{ij}^{*}=\alpha_{ij}f_{ij}^{D}
such that

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{ij}^{*}

\displaystyle=\bar{u}_{ij}+\frac{f_{ij}^{*}}{2d_{ij}}=\bar{u}_{ij}+\alpha_{ij}\frac{u_{i}-u_{j}}{2}\in[u_{i}^{\min},u_{i}^{\max}].

At the next correction step, we use MCL to prelimit the fluxes

\displaystyle\dot{f}_{ij}=\text{minmod}(f_{ij}^{M},f_{ij}^{M}+f_{ij}^{D}-f_{ij}^{*}),

which we define using the minmod function

\displaystyle\text{minmod}(a,b)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}}=\begin{cases}\min(a,b)&\text{if }ab>0,\\
\max(a,b)&\text{if }ab<0,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The limited counterparts
\dot{f}_{ij}^{*}
of the fluxes
\dot{f}_{ij}

are calculated using formula
(
3.9
) with
\bar{u}_{ij}^{*}
and
\bar{u}_{ji}^{*}

instead of
\bar{u}_{ij}
and
\bar{u}_{ji}
. This
limiting strategy guarantees that the flux-corrected bar states satisfy

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{ij}^{**}

\displaystyle=\bar{u}_{ij}^{*}+\frac{\dot{f}_{ij}^{*}}{2d_{ij}}=\bar{u}_{ij}^{*}+\dot{\alpha}_{ij}\frac{m_{ij}(\dot{u}_{i}-\dot{u}_{j})}{2d_{ij}}\in[u_{i}^{\min},u_{i}^{\max}],

where
\dot{\alpha}_{ij}:=0
for
\dot{f}_{ij}^{M}=0
and

\dot{\alpha}_{ij}:=\dot{f}_{ij}/f_{ij}^{M}
otherwise. The use
of the minmod function in the definition of
\dot{f}_{ij}

guarantees that
\dot{\alpha}_{ij}\in[0,1]
.

To enforce (
3.16
), we now seek correction factors
\dot{\alpha}^{\pm}\in[0,1]
and

(3.20)

\dot{\alpha}_{ij}^{-}=\begin{cases}\dot{\alpha}_{ij}&\mbox{if}\ (\dot{u}_{i}-\dot{u}_{j})(u_{j}-u_{i})\geq 0,\\
\dot{\alpha}^{-}\dot{\alpha}_{ij}&\mbox{if}\ (\dot{u}_{i}-\dot{u}_{j})(u_{j}-u_{i})<0,\end{cases}

such that the generalized coercivity condition

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma h}{\lambda}m_{h}(u_{h};\dot{\alpha}^{+}\dot{u}_{h},\dot{\alpha}^{+}\dot{u}_{h})\leq(1-\gamma)d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},u_{h})-m_{h}(u_{h};\dot{\alpha}^{+}\dot{u}_{h},u_{h})

(3.21)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=(1-\gamma)d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},u_{h})-\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\backslash\{i\}}\dot{f}_{ij}^{**}

holds for the fluxes
\dot{f}_{ij}^{**}=\dot{\alpha}^{+}\dot{\alpha}_{ij}^{-}m_{ij}(\dot{u}_{i}-\dot{u}_{j})
and nonlinear forms

(3.22)

\displaystyle d_{h}(v_{h};w_{h},z_{h})=\;

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\backslash\{i\}}(1-\alpha_{ij}(v_{h}))d_{ij}(w_{i}-w_{j}),

(3.23)

\displaystyle m_{h}(v_{h};w_{h},z_{h})=\;

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\backslash\{i\}}\dot{\alpha}_{ij}^{-}(v_{h})m_{ij}(w_{i}-w_{j}).

Note that
(
3.22
) has the same structure as (
3.13
)
but the correction factors
\alpha_{ij}
are generally different because
they are calculated using MCL for the target flux
f_{ij}^{D}
rather than for

f_{ij}=f_{ij}^{D}+f_{ij}^{M}
. Similarly, the nonlinear form (
3.23
) differs
from (
3.14
) in the definition of the correction factors
and of the corresponding target fluxes.

The limiting criterion (
3.3
) implies that
(
3.23
) is coercive in the generalized sense of
condition (
3.16
) for the
time derivative approximation
\dot{u}_{h}^{*}=\dot{\alpha}^{+}\dot{u}_{h}

such that
m_{h}(u_{h};\dot{u}_{h}^{*},z_{h})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\backslash\{i\}}\dot{f}_{ij}^{**}
. The modified
MCL scheme is
bound preserving because the bar states associated with
the final limited fluxes
f_{ij}^{**}=\alpha_{ij}f_{ij}^{D}+\dot{\alpha}^{+}\dot{\alpha}_{ij}^{-}f_{ij}^{M}
stay in the admissible range
[u_{i}^{\min},u_{i}^{\max}]
for
\dot{\alpha}^{+}\dot{\alpha}_{ij}^{-}\in[0,\dot{\alpha}_{ij}]
.

To calculate the correction factors
\dot{\alpha}^{+}
and

\dot{\alpha}^{-}
for (
3.20
), we define

\displaystyle P^{+}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\dot{\alpha}_{ij}m_{ij}\max\{0,(\dot{u}_{i}-\dot{u}_{j})(u_{j}-u_{i})\},

\displaystyle P^{-}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\dot{\alpha}_{ij}m_{ij}\min\{0,(\dot{u}_{i}-\dot{u}_{j})(u_{j}-u_{i})\},

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\dot{\alpha}_{ij}m_{ij}(\dot{u}_{i}-\dot{u}_{j})^{2},\qquad D=d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h};u_{h}).

For
\dot{\alpha}^{-}=0
, inequality (
3.3
) holds if

\dot{\alpha}^{+}P^{+}+(1-\gamma)D\geq(\dot{\alpha}^{+})^{2}\gamma Q
. To ensure the
validity of this sufficient condition, we choose

(3.24)

\dot{\alpha}^{+}=\min\left\{1,\frac{P^{+}}{2\gamma Q}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{P^{+}}{2\gamma Q}\right)^{2}+\frac{(1-\gamma)D}{\gamma Q}}\right\}.

In general, a sufficient condition for (
3.3
) to
hold is given by

(3.25)

(1-\gamma)D+\dot{\alpha}^{+}(P^{+}+\dot{\alpha}^{-}P^{-})\geq(\dot{\alpha}^{+})^{2}\gamma Q.

Given
\dot{\alpha}^{+}
defined by (
3.24
), we can enforce
this constraint using

(3.26)

\dot{\alpha}^{-}=\min\left\{1,\frac{(\dot{\alpha}^{+}\gamma Q-P^{+})\dot{\alpha}^{+}-(1-\gamma)D}{\dot{\alpha}^{+}P^{-}}\right\}.

It is easy to verify that
(\dot{\alpha}^{+}\gamma Q-P^{+})\dot{\alpha}^{+}\leq 0
by
definition of
\dot{\alpha}^{+}
.

In our 1D numerical experiments, the use of
(
3.18
) with
\omega=1
was found to be enough for condition
(
3.3
) to hold without the need for additional limiting.
The proposed algorithm produced
\dot{\alpha}^{\pm}=1

for
\gamma\leq 0.4
on uniform meshes. While the use of
\dot{\alpha}^{\pm}<1

was, indeed, necessary to enforce coercivity on perturbed meshes,
the results were just slightly more diffusive than those obtained
with the standard MCL scheme. In contrast to that, the complete
deactivation of high order stabilization by using

\omega=0
in (
3.18
) required significant coercivity
corrections, resulting in lower overall accuracy
compared to
\omega=1
.

4.
Stability and error analysis

In this section, we show boundedness of
u_{h}
and derive an a priori estimate for the error
u-u_{h}
. Specifically, we prove that the error behaves as
h^{1/2}
. We formulate two main theorems in
Section

4.1
. Auxiliary results for theoretical analysis of AFC schemes are presented in
Section

4.2
,
and proofs of the two theorems follow in
Section

4.3
.

4.1.
Statement of main results

Recall that the MCL solution
u_{h}
satisfies (
3.1
) and is initialized by
u_{h}(0,\cdot)=\mathrm{I}_{h}u_{0}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that the solution
u_{h}
of
semi-discrete problem (
3.1
) satisfies the generalized
coercivity condition (
3.16
). If this is not the case, we
enforce (
3.16
) with
\dot{\alpha}^{+}\dot{u}_{h}
in lieu of

\dot{u}_{h}
using the strategy presented in
Section

3.3
.

The following stability result implies existence of
u_{h}
at any time
t\in(0,T)
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that the coercivity condition (
3.16
) is satisfied for the solution
u_{h}
of (
3.1
) and the associated function
\dot{u}_{h}
. Then there exists a constant
C=C(T,\boldsymbol{a},u^{\textup{in}},u_{0})
such that

(4.1)

\|u_{h}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C.

Further analysis yields an a priori estimate for the error
u-u_{h}
:

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{K}_{h})_{h>0}
be a shape- and contact-regular family of triangulations.
Suppose that the coercivity condition (
3.16
) is satisfied for
solutions
u_{h}
of (
3.1
) on

\mathcal{K}_{h}
and that the analytical solution
u
of (
2.2
)
is in

H^{1}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
.
Then there exists a constant
C=C(T,\boldsymbol{a},\gamma)>0
such that the following a priori estimate holds

(4.2)

\|u-u_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq Ch\int_{0}^{T}\left(|u|^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+|\partial_{t}u|^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\,\textup{d}t.

Consequently, the sequence of AFC approximations
u_{h}(t,\cdot)
converges to
u(t,\cdot)
at least as fast as
h^{1/2}
tends to zero.

4.2.
Auxiliary results

We begin by reviewing some standard results in finite element analysis. In particular, we recall an inverse inequality and an interpolation error estimate. To prepare the ground for proving Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
, we also present some auxiliary results regarding the properties of the nonlinear forms that appear in (
3.1
).

Lemma 4.1

(Inverse inequality,
[
5
, Lem. 1.44]
)
.

For any shape- and contact-regular family of triangulations
(\mathcal{K}_{h})_{h>0}
, there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
h
, such that

\displaystyle|v_{h}|_{H^{1}(K)}\leq Ch^{-1}\|v_{h}\|_{L^{2}(K)}

for all
v_{h}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(K)
,
K\in\mathcal{K}_{h}
.

Lemma 4.2

(Interpolation error,
[
6
, Ex. 1.111]
)
.

For any shape-regular family of triangulations
(\mathcal{K}_{h})_{h>0}
, there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
h
, such that the estimate

\displaystyle\|v-\mathrm{I}_{h}v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+h|v-\mathrm{I}_{h}v|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{2}|v|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}

holds for all
v\in H^{2}(\Omega)
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{K}_{h})_{h>0}
be a shape- and contact-regular family of triangulations. Then there exist constants
C_{1,2}>0
, independent of
h
, such that

\displaystyle m_{ij}\leq C_{1}h^{d},\qquad d_{ij}\leq C_{2}h^{d-1}\qquad\forall i,j\in\{1,\dots,N\}.

Proof.

The first inequality is obviously true.
The validity of the
second one is shown by invoking definition (
3.1
) combined with
Lemma

4.1
and the discrete trace inequality
[
5
, Lem. 1.46]
; see also
[
25
, p. 116]
.
∎

Next, we take a closer look at the bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

The bilinear form (
2.3
) satisfies

\displaystyle a(v_{h},v_{h})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}v_{h}^{2}|\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,\textup{d}\sigma\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h},

and is thus positive semi-definite on
V_{h}
.

Proof.

Integrating by parts, using the assumption that

\nabla\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{a}=0
and the identity
v_{h}\nabla v_{h}=\frac{1}{2}\nabla v_{h}^{2}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle a(v_{h},v_{h})=\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}v_{h}^{2}\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\,\textup{d}\sigma-\int_{\Gamma_{-}}v_{h}^{2}\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\,\textup{d}\sigma=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}v_{h}^{2}|\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,\textup{d}\sigma.

∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
v\in H^{2}(\Omega)
and
w_{h}\in V_{h}
. Then there exist constants
C_{1,2}>0
, independent of
h
, such that

\displaystyle a(v-\mathrm{I}_{h}v,w_{h})\leq C_{1}\|w_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C_{2}h^{2}|v|^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

Using Young’s inequality we find that

\displaystyle a(v-\mathrm{I}_{h}v,w_{h})=\;

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w_{h}\,\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\nabla(v-\mathrm{I}_{h}v)\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}+\int_{\Gamma_{-}}w_{h}(v-\mathrm{I}_{h}v)|\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,\textup{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(\|w_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+|v-\mathrm{I}_{h}v|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\;

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(h\|w_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{-})}^{2}+\frac{1}{h}\|v-\mathrm{I}_{h}v\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{-})}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle C_{1}\|w_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C_{2}h^{2}|v|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

where the last step follows from the discrete and continuous trace inequalities
[
5
, Lem 1.46 and 1.49]
combined with
Lemma

4.2
.
∎

Let us now analyze the nonlinear forms

and
m_{h}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

For arbitrary
v_{h},w_{h},z_{h}\in V_{h}
, the nonlinear forms (

\displaystyle d_{h}(v_{h};w_{h},w_{h})\geq\;

\displaystyle 0,\qquad m_{h}(v_{h};w_{h},w_{h})\geq 0,

\displaystyle d_{h}(v_{h};w_{h},w_{h})\,d_{h}(v_{h};z_{h},z_{h}),

Positive semi-definiteness of

was shown in
[

. For the proof of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

[

Virtually the same arguments apply to our definition of

∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
v_{h}\in V_{h}
, and
\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}

\displaystyle|v_{i}-v_{j}|\leq Ch^{1-d/2}|v_{h}|_{H^{1}(K)}.

See

∎

Lemma 4.8

(
[
3
, Lem. 7.3]
)
.

Let
(\mathcal{K}_{h})_{h>0}
be a shape- and contact-regular family of triangulations. Then there exist constants
C_{1,2}>0
, independent of
h
, such that the estimates

\displaystyle d_{h}(v_{h},\mathrm{I}_{h}w;\mathrm{I}_{h}w)\leq C_{1}h\|w\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\quad m_{h}(v_{h},\mathrm{I}_{h}w;\mathrm{I}_{h}w)\leq C_{2}h^{2}\|w\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

hold for all
v_{h}\in V_{h}
,
w\in C(\overline{\Omega})
.

Proof.

Combining
Lemmas

4.7
and
4.3
, we obtain

\displaystyle d_{h}(v_{h},\mathrm{I}_{h}w;\mathrm{I}_{h}w)\leq C_{1}h|\mathrm{I}_{h}w|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2},\quad m_{h}(v_{h},\mathrm{I}_{h}w;\mathrm{I}_{h}w)\leq C_{2}h^{2}|\mathrm{I}_{h}w|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

By
Lemma

4.2
, the semi-norm
|\mathrm{I}_{h}w|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
satisfies

\displaystyle|\mathrm{I}_{h}w|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\;

\displaystyle|\mathrm{I}_{h}w-w|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+|w|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

(4.3)

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle Ch|w|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+|w|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C\|w\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}.

The inequalities to be verified follow from these estimates.
∎

4.3.
Proofs of the main theorems

We are now in a position to prove the main theoretical results stated in
Section

4.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

4.1
.

We test (
3.1
) with
w_{h}=u_{h}
, which produces

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}m_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}+a(u_{h},u_{h})+d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},u_{h})-m(u_{h};\dot{u}_{h},u_{h})=b(u_{h}).

Employing (
3.16
) on the left and Young’s inequality on the right, we find that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}

\displaystyle\frac{m_{i}}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}(u_{i})^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t}+a(u_{h},u_{h})+\gamma d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},u_{h})+\frac{\gamma h}{\lambda}m_{h}(u_{h};\dot{u}_{h},\dot{u}_{h})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Gamma_{-}}u_{h}u^{\textup{in}}|\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,\textup{d}\sigma\leq\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Gamma_{-}}u_{h}^{2}|\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,\textup{d}\sigma+\int_{\Gamma_{-}}(u^{\textup{in}})^{2}|\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,\textup{d}\sigma.

By
Lemma

4.4
, the term depending on
u_{h}^{2}
can be compensated
by
\frac{1}{2}a(u_{h},u_{h})
on the left hand side. Integration in time yields

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}u_{i}^{2}(T)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\mbox{$\Big[a(u_{h},u_{h})+2\gamma d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},u_{h})+\frac{2\gamma h}{\lambda}m_{h}(u_{h};\dot{u}_{h},\dot{u}_{h})\Big]\,\textup{d}t$}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}(u_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))^{2}+2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma_{-}}(u^{\textup{in}})^{2}|\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,\textup{d}\sigma\,\textup{d}t.

The integral on the left is nonnegative by
Lemmas

4.4
and
4.6
. Thus we have shown stability
w. r. t.
the lumped
L^{2}(\Omega)
-norm

\displaystyle\|v_{h}\|_{h}=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}v_{i}^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Stability
w. r. t.
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
follows from the fact that these norms are equivalent with constants independent of
h

[
15
, Rem. 6.16]
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

4.2
.

This proof borrows ideas from
[
31
]
, where a new compact proof of a standard error estimate is presented for the advection equation discretized with discontinuous finite elements.

Since
V_{h}
is a subspace of
V
, we can subtract (
3.1
) from (
2.2
) to show that the error
u-u_{h}
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w_{h}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle{}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}m_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}+a(u-u_{h},w_{h})

(4.4)

\displaystyle=d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},w_{h})-m_{h}(u_{h};\dot{u}_{h},w_{h})\qquad\forall w_{h}\in V_{h}.

Splitting
u-u_{h}
into the interpolation error
\theta\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}}=u-\mathrm{I}_{h}u

and the remainder
\varrho_{h}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.63612pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathrm{I}_{h}u-u_{h}
, we test (
4.4
) with
w_{h}=\varrho_{h}
. This yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{h}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle{}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varrho_{i}m_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}+a(\varrho_{h},\varrho_{h})

(4.5)

\displaystyle=-a(\theta,\varrho_{h})+d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},\varrho_{h})-m_{h}(u_{h};\dot{u}_{h},\varrho_{h}).

Using the decompositions
u=\theta+\varrho_{h}+\mathrm{I}_{h}u-\varrho_{h}
and

u_{h}=\mathrm{I}_{h}u-\varrho_{h}
, we reorganize the time derivative terms as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{h}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varrho_{i}m_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{h}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}+\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{h}\frac{\partial\varrho_{h}}{\partial t}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle{}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\varrho_{i}m_{ij}\left[\frac{\partial(\mathrm{I}_{h}u)_{j}}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial\varrho_{j}}{\partial t}\right]-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varrho_{i}m_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left[(\mathrm{I}_{h}u)_{i}-\varrho_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{h}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\|\varrho_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle{}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varrho_{i}m_{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[(\mathrm{I}_{h}u)_{j}-(\mathrm{I}_{h}u)_{i}-(\varrho_{j}-\varrho_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{h}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\|\varrho_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle{}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(\varrho_{i}-\varrho_{j})m_{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[(\mathrm{I}_{h}u)_{j}-(\mathrm{I}_{h}u)_{i}-(\varrho_{j}-\varrho_{i})\right].

We use this identity in (
4.5
). Invoking the coercivity
condition (
3.16
), the interpolation error estimate (
4.2
), Young’s
inequality, as well as
Lemmas

4.1
,
4.6
,
4.5
,
4.2
,
4.7
,
4.3
and
4.8
, we obtain the estimates

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}

\displaystyle\|\varrho_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{m_{ij}}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\varrho_{i}-\varrho_{j})^{2}+a(\varrho_{h},\varrho_{h})

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{h}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(\varrho_{i}-\varrho_{j})m_{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[(\mathrm{I}_{h}u)_{i}-(\mathrm{I}_{h}u)_{j}\right]

\displaystyle{}-a(\theta,\varrho_{h})+d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},\mathrm{I}_{h}u)-\gamma d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},u_{h})

\displaystyle{}-[(1-\gamma)d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},u_{h})-m_{h}(u_{h};\dot{u}_{h},u_{h})]-m_{h}(u_{h};\dot{u}_{h},\mathrm{I}_{h}u)

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\varrho_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{2}\|\partial_{t}\theta\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+Ch^{2}\sum_{e=1}^{E}|\varrho_{h}|_{H^{1}(K^{e})}|\partial_{t}\mathrm{I}_{h}u|_{H^{1}(K^{e})}

\displaystyle+Ch^{2}|u|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C\|\varrho_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},u_{h})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}d_{h}(u_{h};\mathrm{I}_{h}u,\mathrm{I}_{h}u)-\gamma d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},u_{h})-\frac{\gamma h}{\lambda}m_{h}(u_{h},\dot{u}_{h},\dot{u}_{h})

\displaystyle{}+\frac{\gamma h}{2\lambda}m_{h}(u_{h};\dot{u}_{h},\dot{u}_{h})+\frac{\lambda}{2\gamma h}m_{h}(u_{h};\mathrm{I}_{h}u,\mathrm{I}_{h}u)

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle C\|\varrho_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+Ch^{4}|\partial_{t}u|^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+Ch^{2}\|\partial_{t}u\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle{}+Ch^{2}|u|^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}-\frac{\gamma}{2}d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},u_{h})+\frac{Ch}{2\gamma}\|u\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle{}-\frac{\gamma h}{2\lambda}m_{h}(u_{h};\dot{u}_{h},\dot{u}_{h})+\frac{C\lambda}{2\gamma h}h^{2}\|u\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Let us now move all terms with the negative sign to the left hand side of the inequality, integrate in time, and exploit the fact that
\varrho_{h}(0)\equiv 0
for
u_{h}(0,\cdot)=\mathrm{I}_{h}u_{0}
. The result of these manipulations is given by

\displaystyle\|\varrho_{h}

\displaystyle(T)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}m_{ij}(\varrho_{i}-\varrho_{j})^{2}(T)

\displaystyle{}+\int_{0}^{T}2a(\varrho_{h},\varrho_{h})+\gamma d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},u_{h})+\frac{\gamma h}{\lambda}m_{h}(u_{h};\dot{u}_{h},\dot{u}_{h})\,\textup{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\|\varrho_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\,\textup{d}t+Ch\int_{0}^{T}\left(\|u\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}u\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)\,\textup{d}t.

Applying Grönwall’s inequality, we find that

\|\varrho_{h}(T)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{1/2}
, owing to
Lemmas

4.4
and
4.6
.
The statement of
Theorem

4.2
follows from
Lemma

4.2
and the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\|u(T)-u_{h}(T)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|\theta(T)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\varrho_{h}(T)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{2}+Ch^{1/2}.

∎

Remark 4.3
.

Kučera and Shu
[
16
]
show that the use of Grönwall’s inequality can be avoided in a convergence proof for the advection equation discretized with discontinuous Galerkin methods. This may also be the case for our proof of
Theorem

4.2
. However, adapting the ideas developed in
[
16
]

to our problem is beyond the scope of this effort.

5.
Numerical examples

Having discussed all theoretical aspects of the proposed methods, we now assess their performance for simple one-dimensional test problems.
In all cases, the velocity is given by
a=1
. Thus the analytical solution to (
2.1
) is
u(x,t)=u_{0}(x-t)
.
To distinguish between
different spatial discretizations, we use the following acronyms:

•

GC: Galerkin method (
2.6
) with the consistent mass matrix,

•

GS: Galerkin method stabilized by low order time derivatives,
i.e., unlimited target scheme (
3.6
) using the flux potentials (
3.17
),(
3.18
) with
\omega=1
in the fluxes (
3.8
).

•

LF: algebraic Lax–Friedrichs method (
3.2
),

•

MC-L: monolithic convex limiting (
3.11
) using low order time derivatives (
3.17
),(
3.18
) with
\omega=1
in the fluxes (
3.8
),

•

MC-0: monolithic convex limiting (
3.11
) using flux potentials
\dot{u}_{h}\equiv 0
, i.e., full mass lumping, in the fluxes (
3.8
),

•

CE: coercivity-enforcing version of MC-L, as proposed in
Section

3.3
, with coercivity constant
\gamma=0.4
.

In all examples, explicit SSP Runge–Kutta methods are used for temporal discretization.

Snapshots of numerical solutions illustrating the qualitative behavior of
different methods are presented in
Section

5.1
. The ability
of these methods to deliver the theoretically proven convergence
rates is verified in the numerical experiments of
Section

5.2
.

5.1.
Advection of discontinuous and smooth profiles

We consider
\Omega=(0,1)
with periodic boundaries. The analytical solution at any time
t\in\mathbb{N}
coincides with the initial condition
[
8
]

(5.1)

\displaystyle u_{0}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if }0.2\leq x\leq 0.4,\\
\exp(10)\exp(\frac{1}{0.5-x})\exp(\frac{1}{x-0.9})&\mbox{if }0.5<x<0.9,\\

which features two discontinuities as well as a
C^{\infty}

Given a uniform mesh
\mathcal{K}_{h}
with vertices

Let

in the case

The failure of GC to converge properly can be blamed on the use of time steps that are too large for this approach. If
\nu
is set to

, we observe second order convergence for GC on all meshes employed in this study. All other EOCs are at least as high as the theory predicts. The assumptions of

4.2
are always satisfied for the LF method (because all correction factors are set to zero), for the MC-0 scheme (because the generalized coercivity condition holds trivially), and for the CE version which is designed to enforce (
3.16
) in the process of flux correction. If condition (
3.16
) holds for the GS or MC-L fluxes without additional limiting,

4.2
is applicable as well. In simulations on
perturbed meshes, we observed occasional violations of (

) for MC-L and the use of
\dot{\alpha}^{\pm}<1
in the CE version. On uniform meshes, condition (
3.16
) was never violated for MC-L or CE with
\dot{\alpha}^{\pm}=1
.

The actual convergence rates of all methods under investigation are, in fact, higher than
h^{1/2}
. The EOC of the LF method gradually approaches 1 as the mesh is refined. This is to be expected because LF is equivalent to the first order upwind method for linear advection on uniform 1D meshes. The MCL-0 convergence rates stagnate around 1.67, while GS, MC-L, and CE exhibit second order convergence already on coarse meshes. Since a locally bound-preserving scheme can be at most second order accurate
[
33
]
, the behavior of MC-L and CE is optimal. The lack of second order superconvergence for MC-0 can again be attributed to numerical dispersion due to mass lumping.

The fact that some EOCs in
Tables

3
,
3
and
3
are significantly higher than the provable order
\frac{1}{2}
is not surprising, since we have not made any assumptions on the choice of correction factors
\alpha_{ij}
other than condition
Eq.

3.16
. In fact, first order convergence could be shown under the assumption that the overall correction factors, defined by

(5.3)

\displaystyle\alpha(u_{h})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{d_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},u_{h})}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}d_{ij}(u_{i}-u_{j})^{2}},

(5.4)

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}(u_{h})

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{h}(u_{h};\dot{u}_{h},\dot{u}_{h})}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}m_{ij}(\dot{u}_{i}-\dot{u}_{j})^{2}},

behave as
O(h)
. If this is the case, we can gain an additional power
of
h
after applying Young’s inequality in the proof of
Theorem

4.2
.
Similarly to the generalized coercivity condition, it is easy to verify
the validity of (
5.3
) and (
5.4
)
a posteriori
.
However, it is not so easy to enforce these conditions if they are
violated, because this may require undesirable relaxation of
local bounds. Moreover, the
O(h)
behavior of
\alpha(u_{h})
and

\dot{\alpha}(u_{h})
cannot be guaranteed for arbitrary

u_{h}\in V_{h}
. Nevertheless, the fact that the EOC of many AFC schemes
can be as high as 2 in practice justifies the quest for new
limiting criteria and procedures that provably guarantee
at least first order convergence for hyperbolic problems.

6.
Conclusions

The presented research
appears to be the first theoretical investigation of semi-discrete AFC schemes for evolutionary hyperbolic problems. To obtain an a priori error estimate with convergence rate
\frac{1}{2}
, we formulated a generalized coercivity condition and enforced it using a modification of the monolithic convex limiting procedure. As shown numerically, coercivity enforcement has no appreciable negative impact on the accuracy of flux-corrected finite element approximations. Moreover, our numerical examples indicate that the original MCL scheme is essentially coercive if the antidiffusive fluxes are stabilized, e.g., using a low order approximation to the nodal time derivatives.

Of course, the implications of generalized coercivity conditions and performance of limiting procedures based on such conditions require additional numerical studies in the multidimensional case. Furthermore, it is worth investigating if raw antidiffusive fluxes can be stabilized in such a way that coercivity corrections become unnecessary.

It is hoped that the ideas presented in this work can be used for analysis of fully discrete problems and extended to nonlinear conservation laws, hopefully even systems like the Euler equations. Other interesting avenues to explore in future studies include analysis of AFC schemes for other target discretizations, such as discontinuous Galerkin methods and/or higher order finite elements. Moreover, the aspects of inexact numerical integration may need to be taken into account. We invite the interested reader to participate in these research endeavors.
