Central-surface-densities correlation in general MOND theories
Mordehai Milgrom

Abstract
It is shown that the foundational axioms of MOND alone predict a strong correlation between a bulk measure of the baryonic surface density, Σ B \Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B} , and the corresponding dynamical one, Σ D \Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D} , of an isolated, self-gravitating object, such as a galaxy. The correlation is encapsulated by its high- and low- Σ B \Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B} behaviors. For Σ B ≫ Σ M ≡ a 0 / 2 ​ π ​ G \Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\gg\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\equiv a_{0}/2\pi G ( Σ M \Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M} being the critical MOND surface density) one has Σ D ≈ Σ B \Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\approx\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B} . More specifically, their difference – which would be interpreted as the contribution of dark matter – is Σ p = Σ D − Σ B ∼ Σ M ≪ Σ B \Sigma_{p}=\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}-\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\sim\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\ll\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B} . In the deep-MOND limit, Σ B ≪ Σ M \Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\ll\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M} , one has Σ D ∼ ( Σ M ​ Σ B ) 1 / 2 \Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\sim(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B})^{1/2} . This is a primary prediction of MOND, shared by all theories that embody its basic tenets. Sharper correlations, even strict algebraic relations, Σ D ​ ( Σ B ) \Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}) , are predicted in specific MOND theories, for specific classes of mass distribution – e.g., pure discs, or spherical systems – and for specific definitions of the surface densities. I proceed to discuss such tighter correlations for the central surface densities of axisymmetric galactic systems, Σ B 0 \Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B} and Σ D 0 \Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D} . Past work has demonstrated such relations for pure discs in the AQUAL and QUMOND theories. Here I consider them in broader classes of MOND theories. For most observed systems, Σ D 0 \Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D} cannot be determined directly at present, but, in many cases, a good proxy for it is the acceleration integral 𝒢 ≡ ∫ 0 ∞ g r ​ d ​ ln ⁡ r \mathcal{G}\equiv\int_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}g_{r}d\ln~r , where g r g_{\scriptscriptstyle r} is the radial acceleration along a reflection-symmetry plane of a system, such as a disc galaxy. 𝒢 \mathcal{G} can be determined directly from the rotation curve. I discuss the extent to which 𝒢 \mathcal{G} is a good proxy for Σ D 0 \Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D} , and how the relation between them depends on system geometry, from pure discs, through disc-plus-bulge ones, to quasispherical systems.

II
Definitions and procedures

In modified-gravity theories of nonrelativistic MOND, the acceleration of test particles in a gravitational field of a density
\rho({\bf r})
is given by a gradient of the MOND potential,
\phi({\bf r})
,

\ddot{\bf r}={\bf g}=-\vec{\nabla}\phi.

(3)

Unlike the Newtonian potential
\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle N}({\bf r})
, which is the solution of the Poisson equation
\Delta\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle N}=4\pi G\rho
, and gives rise to the Newtonian acceleration field,
{\bf g}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
,
\phi
solves some MOND equation that
incorporates the basic tenets of MOND.

The predicted MOND acceleration field,
{\bf g}
, should be identified with the
observed
acceleration field. So, one can defines a dynamical density distribution

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\equiv-(4\pi G)^{-1}\vec{\nabla}\cdot{\bf g}.

(4)

This auxiliary density would be required in Newtonian dynamics to reproduce the MOND gravitational field via the Poisson equation.
The difference,

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle p}({\bf r})=\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle D}({\bf r})-\rho({\bf r}),

(5)

is called the phantom density
milgrom86
, which in the context of a Newtonian interpretation of the observed (MOND) acceleration field has to be attributed to the putative dark matter.

Some MOND results can be described, for convenience, in terms of this phantom density. Some examples of such a device can be found in Refs.
milgrom86
;
ms08
;
oria21
.

Here, we are interested in the “central surface densities” of the baryons, of the phantom mass, and the total, dynamical one,
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B},~\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle P},~\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
, respectively, of an isolated galactic system.
7
7

7

When the system is not isolated, in that it is falling in some external field, the internal dynamics is modified due to the MOND external-field effect.
This concept is relevant for an axisymmetric object – such as an idealized disc galaxy, or an axisymmetric pressure-supported galaxy – and is defined as the column density
of the respective component along the symmetry axis, taken as the
z
axis. It is called so since it is the projected central surface density when the system is viewed along the axis.
We consider systems that are also reflection symmetric about a plane perpendicular to the
z
axis, taken as the
x-y
plane, which is many times a good approximation.
Then

\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(z)dz=-\frac{1}{2\pi G}\int_{0}^{\infty}\vec{\nabla}\cdot{\bf g}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}(z)dz,~~~~\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}(z)dz=-\frac{1}{2\pi G}\int_{0}^{\infty}\vec{\nabla}\cdot{\bf g}(z)dz,

(6)

where superscript “0” on

indicates density on the
z

II.1
Determining the central surface densities

\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
is directly measured – at least in principle, and not always easily – as the deprojected central surface density of the baryons, with contributions from stars and gas, etc.
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
includes the contribution of phantom matter (by MOND), or of dark matter (according to this paradigm). This cannot be detected (in MOND), and has not been detected (as dark matter); so, to measure
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
we would need to map the acceleration field in some cylindrical volume around the symmetry axis, so
\vec{\nabla}\cdot{\bf g}
can be determined there.

In spherical systems, it is enough to measure the radial acceleration profile, since we then have from Eq. (
6
)

\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}=\frac{1}{2\pi G}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2g_{\scriptscriptstyle r}}{r}+\frac{dg_{\scriptscriptstyle r}}{dr}\right)~dr=\frac{1}{2\pi G}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{2g_{\scriptscriptstyle r}}{r}dr=\frac{\kappa}{2\pi G}\mathcal{G},

(7)

where

\mathcal{G}\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{\scriptscriptstyle r}~d{\rm ln}~r,

(8)

and
\kappa=2
.
Here and below I take inward accelerations,
g_{\scriptscriptstyle r}
, to be positive.

The “acceleration integral”,
\mathcal{G}
, a certain average of the radial acceleration, plays an important role in the present context.

In deriving Eq. (
7
), I used
g_{\scriptscriptstyle r}(\infty)=0
, and I also assumed that
g_{\scriptscriptstyle r}(0)=0
, otherwise the integral diverges at least logarithmically at the origin.
8
8

8

The acceleration does not vanish at the origin if the density diverges at least as fast as
1/r
there, in which case
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
indeed diverges.

\mathcal{G}
can be measured with various degrees of accuracy for quasispherical systems, such as elliptical galaxies or galaxy clusters, using methods detailed in the literature, such as hot gas hydrostatics, or weak lensing. Such systems tend, however, to have
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\gg\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
and they would probe the “less interesting” region of high accelerations.

II.1.1
Disc galaxies – using rotation curves

Our main concern here is with disc galaxies, because they offer the most extensive and most accurate sample for studying the CSDC.
As we shall see below, the predictions made by modified-gravity MOND theories concern the phantom and dynamical column densities along the symmetry axis.
However, except perhaps for the Milky Way,

9

The Milky Way also has

where we can, in principle, use test particles away from the plane to map the potential field (using, e.g., tidal streams; see, for example, Ref.
ibata23

g_{r}(r)=V^{2}(r)/r

As another example of mass models with controllable degree of flatness,

Relations like Eq. (

To avoid possible confusion, note that when we discuss such relations in the context of MOND (which is nonlinear) they should always be understood to apply not to the baryonic surface density of the system, but to the dynamical density distribution predicted by MOND – baryonic-plus-phantom. Hence, in the present context, such surface densities are designated with a subscript “D.”
Thus, such relations are not additive with respect to the baryonic components of the mass distribution. Given a baryonic distribution, we first have to determine the MOND, dynamical distribution, identify different components of it, and then apply such relations.

For a system made of several components with different

\mathcal{G}=\sum_{i}\mathcal{G}_{i}

\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}=\sum_{i}\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle Di}^{0}

\bar{\kappa}=\mathcal{G}^{-1}\sum_{i}\kappa_{i}\mathcal{G}_{i}=\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\left[\sum_{i}\frac{\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle Di}^{0}}{\kappa_{i}}\right]^{-1}.

(12)

When testing MOND predictions, the contributions of the different phantom components to

III
What has been done to date

From the MOND vantage point, the first statement relevant to our discussion was made when it was pointed in Ref.
brada99
that according to MOND, the gravitational acceleration produced by the fictitious dark matter cannot much exceed
a_{0}
. This can be stated, alternatively, as “according to MOND, the central surface density of the phantom halo cannot much exceed
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
”.
This follows from relations such as Eq.(
7
), which shows that the characteristic surface density in units of
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
is some average over the acceleration in units of
a_{0}
.
Reference
brada99
based their derivation on either AQUAL, or the algebraic formulation, whereby
\mu(g/a_{0}){\bf g}={\bf g}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
.

Induced by the prediction in Ref.
brada99
, Ref.
ms05
then used a sample of disc galaxies and fitted their rotation curves with baryons plus a cored-isothermal-sphere, dark-mater halo. It showed (in their Fig. 4) that the product of the “halo” central density and its core radius is bounded by, and concentrated strongly near a value of
\approx 10^{2}M_{\odot}~{\rm pc}^{-2}\approx\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
. This translates into the maximum acceleration of the model halo being somewhat below
a_{0}
, in agreement with the prediction of Ref.
brada99
.
The product itself is approximately the central surface density of the “halo” as defined in Eq. (
6
). So the findings of Ref.
ms05
are tantamount to the distribution of halo central surface densities of their galaxy sample, being narrowly peaked near
\approx\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
. This sample was, however, lacking galaxies deep in the MOND limit.

Other studies have found similar results, without noting that these vindicate a MOND prediction. For example, like Ref.
ms05
, Ref.
spano08
fitted rotation curves of a sample of disc galaxies including a cored-isothermal-sphere halo model, while Ref.
donato09
fitted another set of rotation curves with Burkert model halos (which is also characterized by a central density and a core radius). Both found for their samples, a distribution of the “halo” central surface densities that is strongly peaked at a value of
\approx 140M_{\odot}~{\rm pc}\approx\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
.

On the theoretical side, using the algebraic relation
\mu(g/a_{0}){\bf g}={\bf g}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
, Ref.
ms08
calculated the phantom surface-density profiles – in a plane perpendicular to the axisymmetry axis – for a point mass, for two equal masses of different separations, and for a thin rod of various lengths [in units of their MOND radius,
r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}=(MG/a_{0})^{1/2}
]; all these for different choices of
\mu(x)
.
The corresponding values of
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle P}
for all these models can be read from the figures in Ref.
ms08
(given there in units of
a_{0}/G=2\pi\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
).

All the above results concern only the high-surface-density end of the CSDC, predicted to give
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle P}=\gamma\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
, with
\gamma=O(1)
.

In Ref.
milgrom09c
, I considered the problem in some generality, using AQUAL, with the observational results of Refs.
ms05
;
spano08
;
donato09
in mind. As noted above, those studies found a quasiuniversal value of
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle P}\approx\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
for the galaxy samples they employed, as MOND predicts for high-acceleration systems (see Sec.
IV.1
) below.
However, I noted that MOND does not predict a universal value of
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle P}
. This is the case only for high-surface-density systems, but for
low-surface-density ones, one predicts a correlation of the form
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle P}\propto(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\Sigma_{b})^{1/2}
, where
\Sigma_{b}
is some measure of the average surface density (not the central surface density, which I use in the present paper). The proportionality factor was estimated to be of the order of a few and to depend on the exact form of the interpolating function and on the exact mass distribution. The apparent quasiuniversality was attributed to the lack of truly low-surface-density galaxies in the sample studied. It was shown, with a few examples of such galaxies, that their values of
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle P}
indeed fall much below
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
, and are consistent with the MOND predictions for the low-surface-density regime.

By far the most extensive observational study of the correlation was described in Ref.
lelli16
(see also Ref.
swaters14

lelli16

The proxy for the baryonic central surface density was the stellar value, which may underestimate the baryonic value somewhat because of the contribution of the galactic gas.
12
12

12

In most cases gas is extended and, even when important globally, contributes little at the center; however, there are gas-dominated galaxies where the gas contribute materially to
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
.
The proxy for the dynamical central surface density was the acceleration integral, using Eq. (
7

\kappa=1

If one wishes to learn from the correlation about dark-matter halos, then a value of
\kappa

\kappa

lelli16
, underestimates
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
only a little.

While Ref.

\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}

\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle P}
, which is harder to separate from
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}

\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle P}
contributes very little to
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}

\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\approx\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}

In a related study, Ref.

Next came a derivation of a universal
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}-\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
algebraic relation for pure baryonic discs predicted by AQUAL and QUMOND, which I describe next.

III.1
Predictions of AQUAL and QUMOND for pure discs

In Ref.
milgrom16
I proved an exact result that holds in AQUAL and QUMOND, stating that the central dynamical surface density of a
pure disc
is a unique function of the baryonic central surface density:

\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}=\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\mathcal{S}(\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}/\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}),~~~~~~\mathcal{S}(Y)=\int_{0}^{Y}\nu(Y^{\prime})dY^{\prime},

(13)

and
\nu(Y)
is the interpolating function of the respective theory.
14
14

14

The interpolating function
\mu(X)
that appears in AQUAL is represented here by
\nu(Y)
defined such that if
Y=X\mu(X)
, then
X=Y\nu(Y)
.

The contributions to
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
of the disc (baryonic plus phantom),
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}^{\scriptscriptstyle 0,disc}
, and the bulge (all phantom),
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}^{\scriptscriptstyle 0,sph}
, are

\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}^{\scriptscriptstyle 0,disc}=\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\nu(Y)Y,~~~~~~~\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}^{\scriptscriptstyle 0,sph}=\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\int_{0}^{Y}[\nu(Y^{\prime})-\nu(Y)]dY^{\prime},~~~~~Y=\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}/\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}.

(14)

\nu
has the asymptotes
\nu(Y)\rightarrow Y^{-1/2}
for
Y\ll 1
, and
\nu(Y)\rightarrow 1
for
Y\gg 1
.

In the high-central-surface-density limit,
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\gg\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
, the integral picks up most of the contribution from
y\gg 1
, so
\mathcal{S}(y)\rightarrow y
in this limit, hence,
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\rightarrow\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
. In the opposite limit, the integrand is well approximated by
Y^{-1/2}
, and we have
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\rightarrow 2(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B})^{1/2}
.
It is also quite interesting to consider the finer prediction for the separate contribution,
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle P}
, of the phantom halo.
Since
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}=\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\int_{0}^{\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}/\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}}dy
, we have

\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle P}=\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}-\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}=\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\int_{0}^{\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}/\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}}[\nu(Y)-1]dY.

(15)

Solar-System constraints imply that
\nu(Y)
has to approach 1 fast as
Y\gg 1
. So the asymptotic value of
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle P}

is
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\int_{0}^{\infty}[\nu(Y)-1]dY
, which should converge fast. The integral is of order unity. For example, for the choice of
\nu(Y)=Y^{-1/2}
for
Y\leq 1
and
\nu(Y)=1
for
Y\geq 1
, we get
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle P}\rightarrow 2\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
.

In the DML branch of the relation (
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\ll\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
), we have
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}=2(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B})^{1/2}\gg\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
. We see from Eq. (
14
) that in this limit half of the contribution to
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
comes from
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}^{\scriptscriptstyle 0,disc}
, and half from
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}^{\scriptscriptstyle 0,sph}
.

Are these exact and universal (disc-independent) relations in AQUAL and QUMOND predicted in a wider range of MG theories?
It turns out that they are not quite, as we shall see below.

IV
Predictions of the basic tenets alone

Now that we have the machinery in place, and before we consider more precise and detailed predictions of the CSDC, I show how the basic tenets of MOND predict a correlation between baryonic and dynamical surface densities. This makes the correlation in its rough form a primary prediction of MOND. Predictions of exact details of the correlation, such as the values of the numerical coefficients that appear, do depend, however, on the specific theory, on the details of the mass distribution, and on the concrete definition of the surface densities.

The basic tenets of MOND inform us in detail only on the two limits of the theory, but not on the exact way in which the interpolation between them occurs in various theories, and for different phenomena. As regards the transition itself, because
a_{0}
is the only new dimensioned constant, and we assume that no dimensionless parameters appear that differ much from unity, the transition is predicted to occur around
a_{0}
, in acceleration, and within a range of that order; viz. the asymptotic behaviors are already good approximations below
a_{0}/q
and above
qa_{0}
, for
q\not\gg 1
.
As a result, we can hope to derive from the basic tenets alone only the asymptotic limits of a correlation, but be assured that these limits apply – each in its own regime – almost everywhere except for a region of order
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
around
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}=\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
.

For the arguments here to hold, we need to apply them to baryonic surface-density attributes,
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
, that are a good measure of the overall or average Newtonian acceleration of the system, such as the average surface density, because then
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}/\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
is a good indicator of whether we are in the MOND or the Newtonian regime.

IV.1
Newtonian limit

A system of mass
M
and characteristic size
R
, with
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\sim M/\pi R^{2}\gg\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
, is well contained within its MOND radius
r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}=(MG/a_{0})^{1/2}
; namely,
R\ll r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
. Whichever way we measure accelerations, and whether we are dealing with MG or modified inertia (MI) theories, the accelerations within
\sim r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
are predicted to be Newtonian.
15
15

15

In MI theories we have to assume that the orbits of the test-particle probes do not take them far outside
r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
.
Hence the phantom density picks up contributions only from
r\gtrsim r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
. In this region we already have approximate spherical symmetry, and the average, or central, phantom surface density,
\Sigma_{p}\sim g(r_{\scriptscriptstyle M})/2\pi G\sim\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
, as can be seen, e.g., from Eq. (
7
).

IV.2
Deep-MOND limit

Consider a density distribution
\rho({\bf r})
such that the system is fully in the deep-MOND regime. Generate from it a two-parameter family of density distributions

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}({\bf r})=\alpha\lambda^{-3}\rho(\lambda^{-1}{\bf r}),

(16)

whereby all sizes are stretched by a factor
\lambda
and all masses are multiplied by a factor
\alpha
. Scale invariance of the DML, and dimensional arguments, imply
milgrom09a
;
milgrom14
that all kinematic accelerations,
{\bf a}
(which are the same as the MOND gravitational accelerations,
{\bf g}
) that appear in solutions of the theory for
\rho
, scale to
\alpha^{1/2}\lambda^{-1}{\bf a}
and
\alpha^{1/2}\lambda^{-1}{\bf g}
, respectively, in the solutions for
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}
.
16
16

16

To see this, note that the scale invariance of the DML implies that only the combination
\mathcal{A}_{0}=Ga_{0}
can appear in a DML expression for the MOND accelerations. A change of units must leave any valid expression valid. Change the units of mass such that the values of masses
m\rightarrow\alpha m
, the length units such that
\ell\rightarrow\lambda\ell
, and the units of time so that
t\rightarrow\lambda\alpha^{-1/4}t
. Since the only dimensioned constant of the theory,
\mathcal{A}_{0}
, remains unchanged under this scaling, the scaled solutions satisfy the DML equations with the correct value of
\mathcal{A}_{0}
. The kinematic accelerations then indeed scale as
\alpha^{1/2}\lambda^{-1}
.

Masses scale as
m\rightarrow\alpha m
; dynamical densities scale as
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle D}({\bf r})\rightarrow\alpha^{1/2}\lambda^{-2}\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle D}(\lambda^{-1}{\bf r})
(not as
\alpha\lambda^{-3}
like
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}
itself). All dynamical surface densities scale as
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\rightarrow\alpha^{1/2}\lambda^{-1}\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
(at the appropriately scaled positions).
On the other hand,
any baryonic surface density scales as
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\rightarrow\alpha\lambda^{-2}\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
. Thus, with any definition of some
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
and
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
, the dimensionless, scale-invariant, and mass-scaling-invariant quantity (again, calculated at the scaled positions)

\eta(\lambda,\alpha)=\frac{\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}}{(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B})^{1/2}}

(17)

is, in fact, independent of
\lambda
or
\alpha
, as long as
\lambda
is not as small, or
\alpha
is not as large, as to bring the system out of the DML.

To recapitulate this important result:
It follows from the basic tenets of MOND alone that in any given MOND theory that embody them, the DML value of
\eta
is constant within any such two-parameter family of galaxies.
For example, all thin exponential (pure) disc galaxies in the DML, or all DML galaxies described by a Plummer model, etc. should have the same value of
\eta
.

This fact holds for any choice of
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
and
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
, even if they are unrelated, for example, the dynamical central surface density and the baryonic surface density at the radius
containing 90 percent of the projected mass. However, with arbitrary choices of the two surface densities,
\eta
is not a useful ratio to consider, because it could be very large or very small and could depend very strongly on the family
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}^{\scriptscriptstyle\alpha}
.

A similar statement is correct if we replace
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
in the nominator of
\eta
by any measure of the MOND (measured) accelerations, such as
\mathcal{G}
(or
\mathcal{G}/2\pi G
if we want the ratio to remain dimensionless) because they scale in the same way as
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
under the scaling of Eq. (
16
).

To proceed with the argument, and produce a significant correlation between the two quantities, encompassing all density distributions, with
\eta
of order unity, we want to choose the two surface densities so that they are physically related; i.e., the dynamical and baryonic analogs of each other. In particular, we choose their definition such that in the Newtonian limit
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
goes to
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
– for instance, they can be the central surface densities, or the average surface densities,
\langle\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\rangle
and
\langle\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\rangle
. In this way we can also bring to bear the second MOND tenet, that of the “correspondence principle” in the Newtonian limit.

Given the generating density distribution
\rho({\bf r})
, there is a demarcation line in the
\lambda-\alpha
plane,
\alpha\propto\lambda^{2}
, where
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}=\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
. Much above the line the corresponding system is Newtonian, and much below it, it is in the DML. In the former region,
\eta\rightarrow(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}/\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M})^{1/2}
, since we chose the definitions of
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
and
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
so that
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\rightarrow\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
in this limit. And, as we saw, in the latter region
\eta
is some constant (for the family). The essence of our argument is that the dimensionless
\eta
cannot make a big jump when going from one side to the other. So, the constant, DML value cannot be very different from the extrapolation of the Newtonian
\eta
values to the demarcation line. The latter extrapolate to
\eta=1
on the dividing line
17
17

17

In specific examples that we have, the actual value of
\eta
on the dividing line is, in fact, somewhat larger than one, and the DML values of
\eta
vary roughly between 2 for discs and
\sim 4
for spheres.
; so the DML constant value must be
\eta=O(1)
. This follows from the tenet saying that the two limiting expressions of
\eta
hold approximately to “factor of order unity,” down to the transition.

Since this has to hold for all choices of
\rho({\bf r})
, we have, generally,

\eta=O(1)
in the DML for all bounded mass distributions.

This establishes a general CSDC. Despite the impression that such a predicted CSDC can be rather loose, with large scatter, one has to note that the span of
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
values to which we apply it in practice can be several orders of magnitude; so the predicted correlation would still be quite strong.

The arguments here and in Sec.
IV.1
assume that the ratio of the baryonic surface density we employ to
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
is a good measure of whether we are in the DML or the Newtonian regime. This would be the case for global measures of the surface density, such as
\langle\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\rangle
, or the surface density at the half-mass radius.
Beyond these general arguments that use essentially only the basic tenets, but predict a correlation with possibly large scatter, we would like, if possible, to get more refined predictions, at a cost of being, perhaps, less general, in being theory dependent, and dependent on the exact definitions of the surface densities involved.
This leads us to consider the central surface densities in the correlation, because, as I said above,
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
is both easier to measure and to calculate in a given theory.

In many disc galaxies,
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
is close to a global measure of the surface density (e.g., for exponential discs), and for these galaxies the above arguments apply to the central surface densities, as well.
However, in principle,
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
may be quite different from the average surface density of the galaxy, and hence its ratio to
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
is not a good MOND-vs-Newtonian-dynamics indicator.
For example, consider a disc galaxy with a concentric, circular hole around its center, whereby
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}=0
. This remains so all along the above
\lambda,~\alpha
sequence; so the above argument does not work in this case.
Nonetheless, the exact relation (
13
) holds for this case in AQUAL and QUMOND, and it dictates that
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}=0
, as well, for all values of
\lambda
and
\alpha
.
18
18

18

In this case, there is a region of negative phantom density along the
z
-axis
milgrom86
, which results in
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle P}
vanishing. There is also a region around the center where the acceleration integral,
\mathcal{G}
, picks negative contributions (the radial accelerations point outward). The Newtonian rotation curve would then give
\mathcal{G}=0
. But the observed (MOND) rotation curve will generally give
\mathcal{G}\not=0
.
\mathcal{G}
is then not useful for determining
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
, since in this case the effective
\bar{\kappa}=0
in Eq. (
12
) is very different from unity.
This is a result that we could not have derived based on the above general argument, but requires reference to the specific theory.

With these caveats and subtleties in mind, I now proceed to discuss the predictions of more concrete theories.

V
Generalized theories

In this section, I describe the classes and subclasses of theories that I will use to generate predictions of a CSDC.
I shall consider in some detail only MG theories, with some comments on the not-less-promising class of MI theories. The reason for this preference it twofold. First, we do have concrete, full-fledged, and self-consistent examples of MG MOND theories, while we are still lacking such well-developed MI theories.
Second, as I explain in Sec.
V.3
below, the concept of phantom density, and hence of a dynamical surface density, is not well defined in MI theories.

Nevertheless, we do have some general results for a certain class of MI theories, which I discuss briefly in Sec.
V.3

V.1
General class of modified-gravity MOND theories

The class of MG, MOND theories – more general than AQUAL and QUMOND – that I consider here in some more detail are governed by a Lagrangian density

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{G}+\rho(\frac{1}{2}{\bf v}^{2}-\phi),

(18)

whose gravitational part is

\mathcal{L}_{G}=-\frac{a_{0}^{2}}{4\pi G}F\left(\frac{\vec{\nabla}\phi}{a_{0}},\frac{\vec{\nabla}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}{a_{0}},\frac{\vec{\nabla}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}{a_{0}},...\right).

(19)

(Note that only the first derivatives of the potentials appear.)
They are a subclass of the more general theories described in detail in Ref.
milgrom14b
.

Of the scalar potentials
\phi,~\psi_{1},~\psi_{2},...
, which are the gravitational degrees of freedom, only
\phi
couples directly to the matter density. Variation over the matter degrees of freedom gives the equation of motion
\ddot{\bf r}=-\vec{\nabla}\phi
, identifying
\phi
as the MOND potential, which dictates accelerations of test particles.

F
is a dimensionless function that depends on the potentials through the scalar products,
\vec{\nabla}\phi\cdot\vec{\nabla}\psi_{i}
, and
\vec{\nabla}\psi_{i}\cdot\vec{\nabla}\psi_{j}
, for
j\geq i
.

The field equations are

a_{0}^{2}\vec{\nabla}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\vec{\nabla}\phi}\right)=4\pi G\rho=-\vec{\nabla}\cdot{\bf g}_{\scriptscriptstyle N},~~~~~~\vec{\nabla}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\vec{\nabla}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle i}}\right)=0.

(20)

A useful property of this general class of theories, which I employ below, is that for one-dimensional systems, with spherical, cylindrical, or plane symmetry, the MOND acceleration,
{\bf g}
, is a universal function of the Newtonian acceleration,
{\bf g}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
– a function unique to the theory, but independent of the specific one-dimensional symmetry, or the specific mass distribution. We thus have

{\bf g}=\nu(g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}/a_{0}){\bf g}_{\scriptscriptstyle N},~~~~~{\bf g}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}=-\frac{GM(r){\bf r}}{r^{\scriptscriptstyle 3}}.

(21)

To see this, apply Gauss’s theorem to the field equations in Eq. (
20
) for constant-
r
surfaces, where
r
is the coordinate that underlies the one-dimensional symmetry. All the acceleration-vector arguments of
F
are collinear and have only an
r
component; so they can be replaced by
\vec{\nabla}\phi\rightarrow g=|d\phi/dr|
,

\vec{\nabla}\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle i}\rightarrow g_{\scriptscriptstyle i}=|d\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle i}/dr|
, and all scalar products can be replaced by products of the
g
s.
F
then becomes a function of
\chi\equiv g/a_{0}
and
\chi_{i}\equiv g_{\scriptscriptstyle i}/a_{0}
,
F=\hat{F}(\chi,\chi_{1},\chi_{2},...)
, and the field equations become algebraic equations between
\chi,~\chi_{i}

\frac{\partial\hat{F}}{\partial\chi}(\chi,\chi_{1},\chi_{2},...)=\frac{g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}}{a_{0}},

(22)

\frac{\partial\hat{F}}{\partial\chi_{i}}(\chi,\chi_{1},\chi_{2},...)=0.

(23)

Equations (
23
) are used to solve for all the
g_{\scriptscriptstyle i}/a_{0}
as functions of
g/a_{0}
, substituted in Eq. (
22
), which is then inverted to give Eq. (
21
). The possibility to make all these inversions is a necessary requirement from
F
, for the theory to predict gravitational fields uniquely.
19
19

19

In AQUAL, for example, this is the requirement of ellipticity of the field equations, which requires
x\mu(x)
to be monotonic.

Equation (
21
) defines the “one-dimensional interpolating function”,
\nu(Y)
.

After some general results that follow for all the theories in the class, I shall concentrate, for more detailed calculations, on a yet smaller subclass – the tripotential MOND (TRIMOND) theories discussed in Ref.
milgrom23
.
While TRIMOND theories – with AQUAL and QUMOND as special cases – form a subclass, with a Lagrangian density of the type (
19
), the generalizations of QUMOND presented in Ref.
milgrom23a
are examples of MG theories that are not of this type, because their Lagrangians depend also on higher derivatives of the auxiliary potentials. These latter theories do not predict the algebraic relation (
21
) for one-dimensional systems.

Finally, as shown in Ref.
milgrom14b
, all the theories in this class predict the DML virial relation for self-gravitating, isolated system of (pointlike) masses
m_{p}
,

\sum_{p}{\bf r}_{p}\cdot{\bf K}_{p}=-(2/3)(Ga_{0})^{1/2}[(\sum_{p}m_{p})^{3/2}-\sum_{p}m_{p}^{3/2}],

(24)

where,
{\bf r}_{p}
are the positions of the masses, and
{\bf K}_{p}
are the forces they are subject to.
A special case is the deep-MOND two-body force for arbitrary masses,

{\rm K}(m_{1},m_{2},\ell)=\frac{2}{3}\frac{(a_{0}G)^{1/2}}{\ell}[(m_{1}+m_{2})^{3/2}-m_{1}^{3/2}-m_{2}^{3/2}]

(25)

(
\ell
is the distance between the masses).
Another important corollary is the DML mass-velocity-dispersion relation

\sigma^{2}=\frac{2}{3}(MGa_{0})^{1/2}[1-\sum_{p}(m_{p}/M)^{3/2}],

(26)

where
\sigma^{2}=M^{-1}\sum_{p}m_{p}{\bf v}_{p}^{2}
(velocities are in the rest frame of the center of mass), and
M=\sum_{p}m_{p}
is the total mass. This reduces to
\sigma^{4}=\frac{4}{9}MGa_{0}
for systems made of many masses, with
m_{p}\ll M
.

In particular, all these theories predict the same value of the
Q
parameter, defined in Ref.
milgrom12
, which was proposed as a possible discriminator between MOND theories.

After considering in Sec.
VI
some general prediction of this class of theories, I derive more specific ones for the TRIMOND subclass, which I now recap briefly.

V.2
TRIMOND recap

Following is a summary of the aspects and equations of TRIMOND from
Ref.
milgrom23
that we shall be needing here.

The TRIMOND Lagrangian density is

\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{8\pi G}[2\vec{\nabla}\phi\cdot\vec{\nabla}\psi-a_{0}^{2}\mathcal{F}(x,y,z)]+\rho(\frac{1}{2}{\bf v}^{2}-\phi),

(27)

where

x\equiv(\vec{\nabla}\psi)^{2}/a_{0}^{2},~~~y\equiv(\vec{\nabla}\varphi)^{2}/a_{0}^{2},~~~z=2\vec{\nabla}\psi\cdot\vec{\nabla}\varphi/a_{0}^{2},

(28)

and
\mathcal{F}
is a dimensionless function satisfying the basic tenets of MOND.

This is a special case of the Lagrangian density (
19
),
special in two important regards:
(a) It involves only three gravitational potentials.
(b) The MOND potential,
\phi
, couples directly to only one of the auxiliary potentials,
\psi
, and that linearly. This results in
\psi
being the Newtonian field, and is straightforwardly solved for.
As a result, unlike the general case, TRIMOND theories do not require solving coupled equations.

Acceleration of test bodies is given by

\ddot{\bf r}_{i}=-\vec{\nabla}\phi({\bf r}_{i}),

(29)

where
\phi
is the MOND gravitational potential. The first of Eq. (
20
) becomes here

\Delta\psi=4\pi G\rho,

(30)

and the two additional field equations are

\vec{\nabla}\cdot(\mathcal{F}_{y}\vec{\nabla}\varphi)+\vec{\nabla}\cdot(\mathcal{F}_{z}\vec{\nabla}\psi)=0,

(31)

\Delta\phi=\vec{\nabla}\cdot(\mathcal{F}_{x}\vec{\nabla}\psi)+\vec{\nabla}\cdot(\mathcal{F}_{z}\vec{\nabla}\varphi)\equiv 4\pi G\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle D},

(32)

where
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
is the dynamical density (baryonic plus phantom).

\psi
thus equals the Newtonian potential, and is solved for from Eq. (
30
). It is then substituted in Eq. (
31
), which becomes a nonlinear, second order equation in
\varphi
. Solving for it, and substituting both in the right-hand side of Eq. (
32
), we get a Poisson equation for
\phi
.

AQUAL is a special case with
\mathcal{F}_{x}=0,~\mathcal{F}_{z}=\epsilon
, a constant.
QUMOND is a special case gotten for the choice
\mathcal{F}_{y}=\mathcal{F}_{z}=0
.

As a subclass of the above class of MG theories, for systems with one-dimensional symmetry, TRIMOND theories predict the algebraic relation (
21
) between the MOND and Newtonian accelerations.
In such cases, the field equations (
31
) and (
32
) can be stripped of the divergences, and become algebraic (putting, here,
a_{0}=1
),

\mathcal{F}_{y}(g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2},g_{\scriptscriptstyle\varphi}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2},2g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}g_{\scriptscriptstyle\varphi})g_{\scriptscriptstyle\varphi}+\mathcal{F}_{z}(g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2},g_{\scriptscriptstyle\varphi}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2},2g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}g_{\scriptscriptstyle\varphi})g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}=0,

(33)

from which
g_{\scriptscriptstyle\varphi}
can be solved for as a function of
g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
.
\mathcal{F}
has to be such that the solution for
g_{\scriptscriptstyle\varphi}
exists and is unique; the ellipticity of Eq. (
31
) should ensure this.
Equation (
32
) gives, in the same vein,

g=\mathcal{F}_{x}(g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2},g_{\scriptscriptstyle\varphi}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2},2g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}g_{\scriptscriptstyle\varphi})g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}+\mathcal{F}_{z}(g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2},g_{\scriptscriptstyle\varphi}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2},2g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}g_{\scriptscriptstyle\varphi})g_{\scriptscriptstyle\varphi}.

(34)

Substituting the
g_{\scriptscriptstyle\varphi}
values gotten from Eq. (
33
), we then get an equation of the form (
21
).

The Newtonian limit for
a_{0}\rightarrow 0
requires that in this limit
\mathcal{F}_{z}\rightarrow\epsilon
,

20
20

20

VI.2
Kuzmin-inspired disc-plus-bulge systems

I shall discuss the CSDC for pure discs in Sec.
VI.3

We can, however, derive some exact results in the class of theories described in Sec.
V.1
for a family of disc-plus-bulge mass distributions. Such exact results are important anchors for some of our general discussion, even if they are derived only for toy examples.
The results for these mass models are the same in all the theories in the class, and are expressed only in terms of their one-dimensional interpolating function
\nu(Y)

These models are constructed based on an idea that generalizes the construction of the Kuzmin disc, as described in Ref.
brada95

I use these galaxy models here to test how well the acceleration integral
\mathcal{G}
actually measures the dynamical central surface density
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}

To construct the baryonic models, start with a spherical density distribution
\rho_{s}(R)
with accumulated mass
M(R)
.
For the models, the Newtonian potential above the
x-y
, symmetry plane is that of our spherical mass centered on the
z
axis at
z=-h
. The potential below the symmetry plane is a reflection of the one above. The Newtonian potential satisfies the Poisson equation
outside the symmetry plane, with the (baryonic) density

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(r,z)=\rho_{s}(R),~~~~~~R=[r^{2}+(z+h)^{2}]^{1/2}

(48)

as a source (using cylindrical coordinates
r,~z
); this density distribution constitutes our model baryonic bulge. The Newtonian acceleration field outside the disc is

{\bf g}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}=-\frac{GM(R)}{R^{3}}(r,z+h).

(49)

The
z

{\bf g}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
just above the disc is

g_{Nz}^{+}(r)=-\frac{GM(\bar{R})h}{\bar{R}^{3}},~~~~~\bar{R}=(r^{2}+h^{2})^{1/2},

(50)

and just below the disc
g_{Nz}^{-}(r)=-g_{Nz}^{+}(r)
. This jump implies that, for the Poisson equation to be satisfied everywhere, the mass distribution has to be supplemented with a thin disc of (baryonic in our context) surface density

\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}^{\scriptscriptstyle disc}(r)=-\frac{1}{2\pi G}g_{Nz}^{+}(r)=\frac{hM(\bar{R})}{2\pi\bar{R}^{3}}.

(51)

If
\rho_{s}(R)
is nonincreasing, then so is
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}^{\scriptscriptstyle disc}(r)
.
The resulting mass distribution is a disc, and a bulge made of the two caps of the mass distribution
M(R)
.
Since we want the bulge mass to be finite,
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}^{\scriptscriptstyle disc}(r)
goes asymptotically to the Kuzmin surface density
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle B}^{\scriptscriptstyle disc}(r)\propto\bar{R}^{-3}
.
For example, for a sphere of radius
R_{0}
and constant density
\rho_{0}
, we get a disc of constant surface density
2h\rho_{0}/3
up to

, and a Kuzmin disc beyond this, plus a constant-density lenslike bulge.

26

26

If we start with the center of the sphere above the plane, we end up with an hourglass-shaped bulge.

Despite the complexity of the MG theories described in Sec.
V.1

The mass distribution and the Newtonian field above the plane are spherical with respect to the center at

For this solution

VI.3
The CSDC for pure discs

We saw that AQUAL and QUMOND predict a universal – i.e., disc independent –

VII
Summary

The CSDC is a correlation between the dynamical central surface density,
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
, of an axisymmetric and plane-symmetric, galactic system, and the baryonic central surface density,
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
.
Various versions of the CSDC emerge from various MOND starting points.

I have emphasized, in the first place, that the notions of dynamical density and dynamical surface density are defined only in modified-gravity theories. In these, the Newtonian potential is modified into a MOND potential, from which one can define a dynamical density: the density that would source the MOND potential in a Poisson equation.
In modified-inertia versions of MOND there is no gravitational potential defined, and so no dynamical density is defined. However, since in actual tests one uses proxies for the dynamical surface density, and these may be well defined in MI theories, the CSDC may emerge in the MI context in some circumstances.

I showed that from MOND’s defining tenets alone follows a CSDC in a basic form: The underlying tenets pertain to the behavior of the theory at high and low accelerations, but do not specify the interpolation scheme between these behaviors, and how it takes place in different phenomena.
31
31

31

The only assumption about the interpolation is that it occurs within a range of order
a_{0}
around
a_{0}
; so that the theory does not involve dimensionless constants
\gg 1
or
\ll 1
.
So, they can predict only the high- and low-surface-density ends of the CSDC.

At the high end,
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\gg\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
, where
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
is the MOND critical surface density, it is predicted that the phantom surface density

\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle P}\equiv\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}-\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\rightarrow\gamma\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
, with
\gamma=O(1)
that depends on the theory, but not on the system, because in this limit the contribution to
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle P}
comes from far outside the system, where it can be thought of as a point mass, whose structure is immaterial here.

At the other end,
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\ll\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
, it is predicted that
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}=\eta(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B})^{1/2}
, with
\eta=O(1)
that does not depend on the mass or overall size of the system, but that can depend on dimensionless attributes of the system, such as whether we have a sphere, a pure disc, or a combination, or generally on ratios of scale-length attributes of the system.

This makes a rough CSDC, as described above, a primary prediction of MOND that is shared by all MOND theories that embody the basic axioms.

Specific MOND theories can predict more constrained forms of a CSDC, especially if we also consider specific system geometries.
For example, the AQUAL and QUMOND theories predict for pure discs a universal (disc-independent), tight algebraic relation
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}(\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B})
.

As other examples, I considered the detailed predictions of a large class of modified-gravity theories that include AQUAL, QUMOND, and TRIMOND as special cases. They all coincide in their predictions for one-dimensional systems, such as spherical systems or near-disc dynamics. Such predictions then hinge only on the so-called one-dimensional interpolating function – a function of the Newtonian acceleration of the system.

Importantly, though, and in contradistinction from the special cases of AQUAL and QUMOND, these theories do not predict a universal relation for pure discs, with the exception of the Kuzmin disc for which they all make the same prediction of a tight algebraic CSDC.
In particular, the value of
\eta
that describes the low-
\Sigma^{0}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
limit can depend somewhat on the exact disc model, and on the exact theory.
Such details of the CSDC are thus secondary MOND predictions, as they do not follow from the basic tenets alone.
