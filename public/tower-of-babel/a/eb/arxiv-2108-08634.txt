q-analogues of multiple zeta values and the formal double Eisenstein space
Henrik Bachmann

Abstract
. In this survey article, we discuss the algebraic structure of q q -analogues of multiple zeta values, which are closely related to derivatives of Eisenstein series. Moreover, we introduce the formal double Eisenstein space, which generalizes the formal double zeta space of Gangl, Kaneko, and Zagier. Using the algebraic structure of q q -analogues of multiple zeta values, we will present a realization of this space. As an application, we will obtain purely combinatorial proofs of identities among (quasi-)modular forms.

1.
Introduction

In this note, we discuss the algebraic structure of certain
q
-analogues of multiple zeta values and their connection to modular forms. The purpose of this survey article is to give a self-contained summary of some of the results obtained in
[
B1
]
and
[
B2
]
and to motivate the works in progress
[
BB
]
,
[
BKM
]
and
[
BIM
]
. Some parts of those results were also announced in
[
BBK
]
. In particular, we give an explanation of the formal double Eisenstein space, which will be introduced and discussed in detail in
[
BKM
]
and which can be seen as a generalization of the formal double zeta spaces introduced in
[
GKZ
]
. We will see that understanding the algebraic structure of
q
-analogues of multiple zeta values allows us to write down an explicit realization of this space. As an application of this, we will give some purely combinatorial proofs of identities among (quasi-)modular forms such as the Ramanujan differential equations.

Multiple zeta values are defined for
k_{1}\geq 2,k_{2},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1
by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\zeta(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})=\sum_{m_{1}>\cdots>m_{r}>0}\frac{1}{m_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots m_{r}^{k_{r}}}\,.

These values are known to satisfy a collection of relations called the double shuffle relations. For example, in smallest depth and
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 2
these are given by

(1.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\zeta(k_{1})\zeta(k_{2})&=\zeta(k_{1},k_{2})+\zeta(k_{2},k_{1})+\zeta(k_{1}+k_{2})\\
&=\sum_{j=2}^{k_{1}+k_{2}-1}\left(\binom{j-1}{k_{1}-1}+\binom{j-1}{k_{2}-1}\right)\zeta(j,k_{1}+k_{2}-j)\,.\end{split}

The first one, called the stuffle product, is a direct consequence of the definition (
1.1
). The second one, called the shuffle product, can be proven either by using an iterated integral expression for multiple zeta values or by using partial fraction decomposition. Motivated by the relations (
1.2
) Gangl, Kaneko and Zagier defined in
[
GKZ
]
the
formal double zeta space
for
k\geq 1
by the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{k}=\mathchoice{\text{\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{$\big\langle Z_{k},Z_{k_{1},k_{2}},P_{k_{1},k_{2}}\mid k_{1}+k_{2}=k,k_{1},k_{2}\geq 1\big\rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}$}\Big/\lower 4.30554pt\hbox{$\eqref{eq:dzrel}$}}}{\big\langle Z_{k},Z_{k_{1},k_{2}},P_{k_{1},k_{2}}\mid k_{1}+k_{2}=k,k_{1},k_{2}\geq 1\big\rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}\,/\,\eqref{eq:dzrel}}{\big\langle Z_{k},Z_{k_{1},k_{2}},P_{k_{1},k_{2}}\mid k_{1}+k_{2}=k,k_{1},k_{2}\geq 1\big\rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}\,/\,\eqref{eq:dzrel}}{\big\langle Z_{k},Z_{k_{1},k_{2}},P_{k_{1},k_{2}}\mid k_{1}+k_{2}=k,k_{1},k_{2}\geq 1\big\rangle_{\mathbb{Q}}\,/\,\eqref{eq:dzrel}}\,,

which is spanned by formal symbols
Z_{k},Z_{k_{1},k_{2}},P_{k_{1},k_{2}}
satisfying the relations

(1.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}P_{k_{1},k_{2}}&=Z_{k_{1},k_{2}}+Z_{k_{2},k_{1}}+Z_{k_{1}+k_{2}}\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{1}+k_{2}-1}\left(\binom{j-1}{k_{1}-1}+\binom{j-1}{k_{2}-1}\right)Z_{j,k_{1}+k_{2}-j}\,.\end{split}

In
[
GKZ
]
different realizations of this space are given, where for a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space
A
a realization of
\mathcal{D}_{k}
in
A
is defined as an element in
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{D}_{k},A)
. By (
1.2
) together with some regularization one can show that for
A=\mathbb{R}
we obtain a realization of
\mathcal{D}_{k}
by

(1.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z_{k}&\longmapsto\begin{cases}\zeta(k)&k\geq 3\\
0&k=1,2\end{cases}\,,\\
Z_{k_{1},k_{2}}&\longmapsto\begin{cases}\zeta(k_{1},k_{2})&k_{1}\geq 2\\
-\zeta(k_{2},1)-\zeta(k_{2}+1)&k_{1}=1,k_{2}\geq 2\\
0&k_{1}=k_{2}=1\end{cases}\,,\\
P_{k_{1},k_{2}}&\longmapsto\begin{cases}\zeta(k_{1})\zeta(k_{2})&k_{1},k_{2}\neq 1\\
0&k_{1}=1\vee k_{2}=1\end{cases}\,.\end{split}

The Riemann zeta values
\zeta(k)
also appear as the constant term of the Eisenstein series defined for
k\geq 2
by

(1.5)

\displaystyle G_{k}(\tau)=\zeta(k)+\frac{(-2\pi i)^{k}}{(k-1)!}\sigma_{k-1}(n)q^{n}\,,

where
\tau\in\mathbb{H}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid\operatorname{Im}(z)>0\}
,
q=e^{2\pi i\tau}
and
\sigma_{k-1}(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d^{k-1}
.
For even
k\geq 4
these are the first non-trivial examples of modular forms of weight
k
for the full modular group. In
[
GKZ
]
it was observed, that the relations (
1.2
) can be lifted to the space of holomorphic functions, such that the Riemann zeta values gets replaced by the Eisenstein series and the double zeta values by so-called double Eisenstein series
G_{k_{1},k_{r}}
. In other words they showed that for
k\geq 4
there exists a realization of
\mathcal{D}_{k}
in the space of holomorphic functions in the upper half plane with
Z_{k}\mapsto G_{k}
(where
G_{1}
is given by (
1.5
) without the constant term),
Z_{k_{1},k_{2}}\mapsto G_{k_{1},k_{r}}
and

(1.6)

\displaystyle P_{k_{1},k_{2}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto G_{k_{1}}G_{k_{2}}+\frac{\delta_{k_{1},2}}{2k_{2}}G^{\prime}_{k_{2}}+\frac{\delta_{k_{2},2}}{2k_{1}}G^{\prime}_{k_{1}}\,.

Taking the constant term of this realization gives the multiple zeta value realization (
1.4
).
But in contrast to (
1.4
) the image of
P_{k_{1},k_{2}}
in (
1.6
) is not given exactly by the product of the depth one objects, since also derivatives of Eisenstein series appear. This has to do with the fact that
G_{2}
is not modular, and it is one of the motivations for the formal double Eisenstein space, which will be introduced in
[
BKM
]
and which we will explain at the end of this note. In this space, we will also consider ”formal derivatives” of our objects. As it will turn out, this gives an analogue of the formal double zeta space, which is better suited for Eisenstein series in the sense that we will find a realization of this space where the formal symbol of the product gets mapped exactly to the product of Eisenstein series. It can also be seen as a generalization of the work of Gangl, Kaneko, Zagier from modular forms to quasi-modular forms.

2.
q
-analogues of multiple zeta values

This section recalls the
q
-analogues of multiple zeta values, which were introduced in
[
B1
]
. Parts of the results are contained in
[
BK
]
and detailed information can be found in
[
B2
]
. Most of the time we will only consider the depth two case, but everything done in this section also has a generalization to arbitrary depths.

Definition 2.1
.

A
modified q-analogue of weight
k
of a complex number
c\in\mathbb{C}
is a q-series
f\in\mathbb{C}[[q]]
, such that

\displaystyle\lim_{q\rightarrow 1}(1-q)^{k}f=c\,.

Here and in the following by
q\rightarrow 1
, we always mean the limit of
q
on the real axis with
0<q<1
. Usually, our objects are just formal
q
-series in
\mathbb{Q}[[q]]
, but all appearing
q
-series in this note can also be seen as holomorphic functions in the unit disc (
|q|<1
) or as holomorphic functions in the upper half-plane by setting
q=e^{2\pi i\tau}
. We will not distinguish these three points of view in the following.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
f(\tau)=a_{0}+\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}q^{n}
be a modular form of weight
k
.Then
f
is a modified q-analogue of
(2\pi i)^{k}a_{0}
.

Proof.

If
f
is modular of weight
k
then
f(-\frac{1}{\tau})=\tau^{k}f(\tau)
, i.e.

\displaystyle\lim_{q\rightarrow 1}(1-q)^{k}f(q)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}((2\pi i\tau)^{k}+O(\tau^{k+1}))f(\tau)=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}(2\pi i)^{k}f\left(-\frac{1}{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow i\infty}(2\pi i)^{k}f(\tau)=(2\pi i)^{k}a_{0}\,.

∎

In particular the normalized Eisenstein series

(2.1)

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{k}(\tau):=\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{k}}G_{k}(\tau)=-\frac{B_{k}}{2k!}+\frac{1}{(k-1)!}\sum_{n>0}\sigma_{k-1}(n)q^{n}

are modified
q
-analogue (of weight
k
) of
\zeta(k)
for even
k\geq 4
. But we will see that we have for any
k\geq 2

\displaystyle\lim_{q\rightarrow 1}(1-q)^{k}\underbrace{\frac{1}{(k-1)!}\sum_{n>0}\sigma_{k-1}(n)q^{n}}_{g(k):=}=\zeta(k)\,.

In the following we will consider a multiple version of the
q
-series
g(k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>0\\
n>0\end{subarray}}\frac{n^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}q^{mn}
.

Definition 2.3
.

For
k_{1},\dots k_{r}\geq 1
we define the
q
-series
g(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})\in\mathbb{Q}[[q]]
by

\displaystyle g(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0\\
n_{1},\dots,n_{r}>0\end{subarray}}\frac{n_{1}^{k_{1}-1}}{(k_{1}-1)!}\dots\frac{n_{r}^{k_{r}-1}}{(k_{r}-1)!}q^{m_{1}n_{1}+\dots+m_{r}n_{r}}\,.

Notice that for
r=1
these are, up to the constant term, exactly the Eisenstein series
\widetilde{G}_{k}
. Moreover, they are modified q-analogues of multiple zeta values.

Proposition 2.4
.

For
k_{1}\geq 2
,
k_{2},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1
the
g(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})
are modified q-analogues of
\zeta(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})
, i.e.

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{q\rightarrow 1}(1-q)^{k_{1}+\dots+k_{r}}g(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})=\zeta(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})\,.

Proof.

First rewrite

\displaystyle g(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})=\sum_{m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0}\frac{P_{k_{1}}(q^{m_{1}})}{(1-q^{m_{1}})^{k_{1}}}\dots\frac{P_{k_{r}}(q^{m_{r}})}{(1-q^{m_{r}})^{k_{r}}}\,,

where the polynomials
P_{k}
are defined by
\frac{P_{k}(X)}{(1-X)^{k}}:=\sum_{n>0}\frac{n^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}X^{k-1}
. These are, up to a normalization, the
Eulerian polynomials
and they satisfy
P_{k}(1)=1
from which we get after using some elementary convergence criteria that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{q\rightarrow 1}(1-q)^{k_{1}+\dots+k_{r}}g(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})=\lim\limits_{q\rightarrow 1}\sum_{m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\left(\frac{1-q}{1-q^{m_{j}}}\right)^{k_{j}}P_{k_{j}}(q^{m_{j}})=\sum_{m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\frac{1}{m_{j}^{k_{j}}}\,.

∎

Proposition 2.5
.

For
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 1
we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle g(k_{1})g(k_{2})=g(k_{1},k_{2})+g(k_{2},k_{1})+g(k_{1}+k_{2})+\sum_{j=1}^{k_{1}+k_{2}-1}\lambda^{j}_{k_{1},k_{2}}\,g(j)\,,

where the rational numbers
\lambda^{j}_{k_{1},k_{2}}
are given by

(2.3)

\displaystyle\lambda^{j}_{k_{1},k_{2}}=\left((-1)^{k_{1}-1}\binom{k_{1}+k_{2}-1-j}{k_{2}-j}+(-1)^{k_{2}-1}\binom{k_{1}+k_{2}-1-j}{k_{1}-j}\right)\frac{B_{k_{1}+k_{2}-j}}{(k_{1}+k_{2}-j)!}\,.

Proof.

The proof uses the generating function
L_{m}(X)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{P_{k}(q^{m})}{(1-q^{m})^{k}}X^{k}=\frac{e^{X}q^{m}}{1-e^{X}q^{m}}
. By direct calculation one then checks that

\displaystyle L_{m}(X)L_{m}(Y)=\frac{1}{e^{X-Y}-1}L_{m}(X)+\frac{1}{e^{Y-X}-1}L_{m}(Y),

and uses
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}
to show

\displaystyle\frac{P_{k_{1}}(q^{m})}{(1-q^{m})^{k_{1}}}\frac{P_{k_{2}}(q^{m})}{(1-q^{m})^{k_{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{k_{1}+k_{2}}(q^{m})}{(1-q^{m})^{k_{1}+k_{2}}}+\sum_{j=1}^{k_{1}+k_{2}-1}\lambda^{j}_{k_{1},k_{2}}\frac{P_{j}(q^{m})}{(1-q^{m})^{j}}\,.

The statement then follows since

\displaystyle g(k_{1})g(k_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{1}>0}\frac{P_{k_{1}}(q^{m_{1}})}{(1-q^{m_{1}})^{k_{1}}}\sum_{m_{2}>0}\frac{P_{k_{2}}(q^{m_{2}})}{(1-q^{m_{2}})^{k_{2}}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{m_{1}>m_{2}>0}+\sum_{m_{2}>m_{1}>0}+\sum_{m_{1}=m_{2}>0}\right)\frac{P_{k_{1}}(q^{m_{1}})}{(1-q^{m_{1}})^{k_{1}}}\frac{P_{k_{2}}(q^{m_{2}})}{(1-q^{m_{2}})^{k_{2}}}\,.

∎

Except for the lower weight terms the product (
2.2
) looks exactly like the stuffle product
\zeta(k_{1})\zeta(k_{2})=\zeta(k_{1},k_{2})+\zeta(k_{2},k_{1})+\zeta(k_{1}+k_{2})
of zeta values. Notice that this formula for zeta values follows from Proposition
2.4
for
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 2
by multiplying (
2.2
) with
(1-q)^{k_{1}+k_{2}}
and then taking the limit
q\rightarrow 1
since the lower weight terms vanish. So a natural question is if there is also an analogue of the shuffle product for the
q
-series
g
. Before answering this question we consider the operator
q\frac{d}{dq}
(which corresponds to
\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{d\tau}
for
q=e^{2\pi i\tau}
).

\displaystyle q\frac{d}{dq}g_{k}(q)=q\frac{d}{dq}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>0\\
n>0\end{subarray}}\frac{n^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}q^{mn}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>0\\
n>0\end{subarray}}\frac{mn^{k}}{(k-1)!}q^{mn}\,.

We see that after taking the derivative we also have a
m
appearing in the numerator. Moreover if we would take the
d
-th derivative we would get

\displaystyle\left(q\frac{d}{dq}\right)^{d}g_{k}(q)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>0\\
n>0\end{subarray}}\frac{m^{d}n^{k+d-1}}{(k-1)!}q^{mn}\,.

This leads us to define
g
in a more general way.

Definition 2.6
.

For
k_{1},\dots k_{r}\geq 1,d_{1},\dots,d_{r}\geq 0
define the
q
-series

\displaystyle g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{d_1,\dots,d_r}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0\\
n_{1},\dots,n_{r}>0\end{subarray}}\frac{m_{1}^{d_{1}}n_{1}^{k_{1}-1}}{(k_{1}-1)!}\dots\frac{m_{r}^{d_{r}}n_{r}^{k_{r}-1}}{(k_{r}-1)!}q^{m_{1}n_{1}+\dots+m_{r}n_{r}}\,.

We say that this has
weight

k_{1}+\dots+k_{r}+d_{1}+\dots+d_{r}
and
depth

r
.

Clearly these generalize the
q
-series in Definition
2.3
since we have

\displaystyle g(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})=g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{0,\dots,0}\,.

Notice that again for
r=1
these
q
-series are essentially the derivatives of Eisenstein series since for
k>d
we have

\displaystyle g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k}{d}=\frac{(k-d-1)!}{(k-1)!}\left(q\frac{d}{dq}\right)^{d}\widetilde{G}_{k-d}+\delta_{d,0}\frac{B_{k}}{2k!}\,.

With the same idea as before it is easy to see that we have

\displaystyle q\frac{d}{dq}g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{d_1,\dots,d_r}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}k_{j}g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots.k_j+1,\dots,k_r}{d_1,\dots,d_j+1,\dots,d_r}\,.

But the main reason for introducing this more general definition is not that we can talk about derivatives, but rather we will see that this enables us to define an analogue of the shuffle product. Before doing this we will see that the formula for the stuffle product (Proposition
2.4
) generalizes easily.

Proposition 2.7

(Stuffle product analogue)
.

For
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 1,d_{1},d_{2}\geq 0
we have

(2.4)

\displaystyle g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1}{d_1}g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_2}{d_2}=

\displaystyle g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,k_2}{d_1,d_2}+g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_2,k_1}{d_2,d_1}+g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1+k_2}{d_1+d_2}+\sum_{j=1}^{k_{1}+k_{2}-1}\lambda^{j}_{k_{1},k_{2}}\,g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{j}{d_1+d_2}\,,

where the
\lambda^{j}_{k_{1},k_{2}}
are given by (
2.3
).

Proof.

Similar proof as Proposition
2.5
since the exponents of the
m_{j}
just add up.∎

Let
\operatorname{Part}_{r}(N)
denote the set of partitions of
N
made out of
r
different parts. For example,
11=4+4+1+1+1
is a partition of
N=11
made out of
r=2
different parts
(m_{1},m_{2})=(4,1)
with multiplicities
(n_{1},n_{2})=(2,3)
, i.e.
N=m_{1}n_{1}+m_{2}n_{2}
. Any element
\lambda\in\operatorname{Part}_{r}(N)
can be represented by a Young diagram

\lambda=
m_{1}
m_{2}
m_{r-1}
m_{r}
n_{1}
n_{2}
n_{r-1}
n_{r}

where
N=m_{1}n_{1}+\dots+m_{r}n_{r}
and
m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0
,
n_{1},\dots,n_{r}>0
. In particular we can write the coefficient of
q^{N}
in the
q
-series
g
as a sum over all elements in
\operatorname{Part}_{r}(N)

\displaystyle g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{d_1,\dots,d_r}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}>\dots>m_{r}>0\\
n_{1},\dots,n_{r}>0\end{subarray}}\underbrace{\frac{m_{1}^{d_{1}}n_{1}^{k_{1}-1}}{(k_{1}-1)!}\dots\frac{m_{r}^{d_{r}}n_{r}^{k_{r}-1}}{(k_{r}-1)!}}_{f(\lambda):=}q^{m_{1}n_{1}+\dots+m_{r}n_{r}}=\sum_{N>0}\left(\sum_{\lambda\in\operatorname{Part}_{r}(N)}f(\lambda)\right)q^{N}\,,

where
f:\operatorname{Part}_{r}(N)\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
sends a partition
\lambda
, given as above, to a polynomial in the
m_{j}
and
n_{j}
depending on the
k_{j}
and
d_{j}
. On
\operatorname{Part}_{r}(N)
we have an involution given by the conjugation
\rho
of Young diagrams, which correspond to reflecting the Young diagram along the diagonal

\lambda=
m_{1}
m_{2}
m_{r-1}
m_{r}
n_{1}
n_{2}
n_{r-1}
n_{r}
\rho
=\rho(\lambda)
n_{1}+\dots+n_{r}
n_{1}+\dots+n_{r-1}
n_{1}+n_{2}
n_{1}
m_{r}
m_{r-1}-m_{r}
m_{2}-m_{3}
m_{1}-m_{2}

Since we sum over all elements in
\operatorname{Part}_{r}(N)
we obtain

\displaystyle g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{d_1,\dots,d_r}=\sum_{N>0}\left(\sum_{\lambda\in\operatorname{Part}_{r}(N)}f(\lambda)\right)q^{N}=\sum_{N>0}\left(\sum_{\lambda\in\operatorname{Part}_{r}(N)}f(\rho(\lambda))\right)q^{N}\,.

The
f(\rho(\lambda))
is again a polynomial in
m_{1},\dots,m_{r},n_{1},\dots,n_{r}
and therefore the right-hand side of above equation can be written again as a linear combination of the
q
-series
g
in the same depth and weight. We call the linear relation obtained from this
partition relation
. These linear relations can be written done explicitly in all depths, and we give the explicit formula for depth one and two in the following.

Proposition 2.8

(Partition relation)
.

i)

For
k\geq 1,d\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k}{d}=\frac{d!}{(k-1)!}g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{d+1}{k-1}\,.

ii)

For
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 1,d_{1},d_{2}\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,k_2}{d_1,d_2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq a\leq d_{1}\\
0\leq b\leq k_{2}-1\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{b}}{a!\,b!}\frac{d_{1}!}{(k_{1}-1)!}\frac{(d_{2}+a)!}{(k_{2}-1-b)!}\,\,g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{d_2+1+a,\,d_1+1-a}{k_2-1-b,\,k_1-1+b}\,.

Example 2.9
.

Combining the stuffle product (Proposition
2.7
) and the partition relation (Proposition
2.8
) gives us another way of expressing the product of two
q
-series
g
:

\displaystyle g(2)g(3)

\displaystyle=g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{2}{0}g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{3}{0}\overset{\text{Prop.}\ref{prop:partrel}}{=}\frac{1}{2}g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1}g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{1}{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Prop.}\ref{prop:stufflebig}}{=}\frac{1}{2}\left(g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{1,1}{1,2}+g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{1,1}{2,1}+g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{2}{3}-g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{1}{3}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Prop.}\ref{prop:partrel}}{=}g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{2,3}{0,0}+3g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{3,2}{0,0}+6g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{4,1}{0,0}+3g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{4}{1}-3g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{4}{0}.

Using
q\frac{d}{dq}g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{3}{0}=3g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{4}{1}
we obtain

\displaystyle g(2)g(3)

\displaystyle=g(2,3)+3g(3,2)+6g(4,1)-3g(4)+q\frac{d}{dq}g(3)\,.

This formula looks similar to the shuffle product of the corresponding zeta values, which is given by

\displaystyle\zeta(2)\zeta(3)=\zeta(2,3)+3\zeta(3,2)+6\zeta(4,1)\,.

But now we do not just have an additional lower weight term
3g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{4}{0}
, but also the term
3g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{4}{1}

3.
Formal double Eisenstein space

In this section, we will introduce the formal double Eisenstein space which will be discussed in more detail in
[
BKM
]
. This space will be spanned by formal symbols satisfying the same relations as the
q

Definition 3.1
.

We define for

the

(3.1)

The reason why this space can be seen as a generalization of the formal double zeta space

is that we have the following embedding of

in

Proposition 3.2
.

For all
k\geq 1
the following gives a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear map
\mathcal{D}_{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{k}

\displaystyle Z_{k}

\displaystyle\longmapsto G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k}{0}-\delta_{k,2}G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{2}{0}\,,

\displaystyle\quad Z_{k_{1},k_{2}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,k_2}{0,0}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta_{k_{2},1}G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1}{1}-\delta_{k_{1},1}G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_2}{1}+\delta_{k_{1},2}G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_2+1}{1}\right)\,,

\displaystyle P_{k_{1},k_{2}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto P\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,k_2}{0,0}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta_{k_{1},2}G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_2+1}{1}+\delta_{k_{2},2}G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1+1}{1}\right)-\delta_{k_{1}\cdot k_{2},1}G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{2}{0}\,.

Proof.

We just need to check that this map is well-defined. This can be done by applying this map to the defining equation (
1.3
) of
\mathcal{D}_{k}
and then observe that, after rearranging some terms and using
G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{2}{0}=G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1}
, the result is exactly (
3.1
) with
d_{1}=d_{2}=0
.
∎

4.1

Theorem 4.1
.

For all
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 1
with
k=k_{1}+k_{2}\geq 4
even we have

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\left(\binom{k_{1}+k_{2}}{k_{2}}-(-1)^{k_{1}}\right)G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k}{0}=&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=2\\
j\text{even}\end{subarray}}^{k-2}\left(\binom{k-j-1}{k_{1}-1}+\binom{k-j-1}{k_{2}-1}-\delta_{j,k_{1}}\right)P\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{j,k-j}{0,0}\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\left(\binom{k-3}{k_{1}-1}+\binom{k-3}{k_{2}-1}+\delta_{k_{1},1}+\delta_{k_{2},1}\right)G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k-1}{1}\,.\end{split}

Proof.

By
[
B2

the following relation in the formal double zeta space holds

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\binom{k_{1}+k_{2}}{k_{2}}-(-1)^{k_{1}}\right)Z_{k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=2\\
j\text{even}\end{subarray}}^{k-2}\left(\binom{k-j-1}{k_{1}-1}+\binom{k-j-1}{k_{2}-1}-\delta_{j,k_{1}}\right)P_{j,k-j}\,.

Applying the linear map from Proposition
3.2
to this gives exactly (
4.1
).
∎

Corollary 4.2

i)

\displaystyle G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k-1}{1}=\frac{k+1}{2}G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k}{0}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+k_{2}=k\\

ii)

For all even

\displaystyle\frac{(k+1)(k-1)(k-6)}{12}G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k}{0}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+k_{2}=k\\

Example 4.3
.

i)

We have

\displaystyle G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{8}{0}=\frac{6}{7}G_{4}^{2},\,\quad G\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{10}{0}=\frac{10}{11}P\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{4,6}{0,0}\,.
