Abstract
. We find the position-momentum decomposition of the quantum operators of the classic Meixner random variables. The position-momentum decomposition involves translation operators, which are used to give a new characterization of the Meixner random variables.



Key words and phrases: joint quantum and semi-quantum operators, number operator,
second quantization operator, position and momentum operators, translation and dilations operators,
Meixner random variables.

MSC 2020: 42C05, 62P05, 05E35

1.
Introduction

The field of orthogonal polynomials is a rich and
classic area of investigation that have occupied the minds of many
mathematicians in the last two centuries.
People have employed various tools like continuous fraction expansions,
generating functions, re-normalization techniques,
and combinatorial methods to understand the orthogonal polynomials
generated by a random variable (or equivalently,
by its probability distribution),
having finite moments of all orders.
These investigations focused on the concrete form of
the orthogonal polynomials for various classic random variables.
An equivalent way of studying the underlying orthogonal structure
of the unital algebra generated by a random variable,
having finite moments of all orders,
that is more operatorial in nature,
and somehow follows the philosophy of quantum mechanics,
has been developed in the last decades by analyzing
the quantum operators: creation, preservation, and annihilation
operators.
A particular class of random variables that is well suited
to this operatorial treatment consists of the classic
Meixner random variables which include the shifted and re-scaled
Gaussian, Poisson, gamma, negative binomial, two parameter hyperbolic,
and binomial distributions.
This class is well suited to this treatment due to the
Lie algebra structure enjoyed by the quantum operators,
that means the fact that the commutator of any two quantum operators
can be expressed as a linear combination of the quantum,
number, and identity operators.
There are two fundamental operators:
the multiplication by
X
, also called the
position
operator,
and the differentiation operator
D=d/dx
,
also called the
momentum
operator.
Every quantum operator and each linear operator
that maps the space
F
, of all random variables of the form
P(X)
,
where
P
is a polynomial and
X
is a given random variable,
having finite moments of all orders, into itself, can be expressed
uniquely as sum (series) of terms of the form
A_{n}(X)D^{n}
,
that means, compositions of powers of the position operator and
powers of the momentum operator.
In these compositions, the position “factors” are always placed
at the left of the momentum “factors”.
The importance of the decomposition into
a sum (series) of compositions of position and momentum operators
relies on the Heisenberg commutation relation:

(1.1)

\left[D,X\right]=I,

where
I
is the identity operator.

These facts can be extended to the multi-dimensional case,
where one random variable
X
is replaced
by a finite family of random variables

X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}
,
defined on the same probability space,
and having finite moments of all orders.
On the surface, the random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}
commute,
but beneath the surface there are hidden the quantum operators:
creation, preservation, and annihilation,
which are defined in the terms of the orthogonal spaces and
compositions of the multiplication operators
by
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}
(called the position operators)
and projections onto these orthogonal spaces.
These quantum operators do not commute,
and the non-zero commutators
between any two of these quantum operators
make the study of quantum probability interesting.
In classical probability
a family of random variables is determined
by their joint probability distribution.
But in quantum probability a family of random variables
is characterized by the concrete form of their joint quantum operators,
the number operator and the second quantization operators of constants
times the identity operator,
which ultimately lead to a concrete form of
the projection operators on the orthogonal spaces
which are generated by the family of random variables.

This paper is dedicated to the study of
the classical Meixner random variables
along with quantum probability.
As we will see, their quantum operators will be expressed
in terms of translation operators.
Moreover, we will derive a characterization of these random variables
in terms of their quantum operators.
We will not address the problem of knowing the second quantization
operator of a constant times the identity operator in this paper.
Moreover, we will derive a characterization of these random variables
in terms of these quantum operators.

The paper is structured as follows.
In section 1 we present a brief background of quantum,
semi-quantum and number operators.
In section 2 we focus on Meixner random variables,
and find the concrete form of the operators described in Section 1.
In section 3 we characterize the Meixner random variables
using the forms found in Section 2.

2.
Background

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}
be
d\geq 1
real valued random variables,
defined on a common probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
.
We assume that these random variables have finite moments
of all orders, that means, for all
i\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}

and all
p>0
, we have:

(2.1)

E\left[\left|X_{i}\right|^{p}\right]=\int_{\Omega}\left|X_{i}(\omega)\right|^{p}dP(\omega)<\infty.

Let
F
be the space of all random variables of the form

f(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d})
,
where
f
is a polynomial of
d
variables with complex coefficients.
For all
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}

let
F_{n}
be the subspace of
F
consisting of
all random variables of the form
f(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d})
,
where
f
is a polynomial of degree at most
n
.
Since the random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}
have finite
moments of all orders, we have:

(2.2)

\mathbb{C}\cong F_{0}\subseteq F_{1}\subseteq F_{2}\subseteq\dotsb\subseteq F\subseteq L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P).

Because of finite dimensionality
F_{n}
is a closed subspace of

L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
For convenience we set
F_{-1}=\{0\}
(the null space).

For
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
we define

(2.3)

G_{n}=F_{n}\ominus F_{n-1},

that means,
G_{n}
is the orthogonal complement of
F_{n-1}
in
F_{n}
.
Then obviously,

(2.4)

F_{n}=G_{0}\oplus G_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus G_{n}.

We call
G_{n}
the
n
th homogenous chaos space
generated by

X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}
, and a random variable
f(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d})

in
G_{n}
a
homogenous polynomial random variable
of degree
n

(here the word “homogenous” does not have the classic meaning
that all terms have the same degree).

It is essential for us to regard

X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}
not as random variables,
but as the multiplication operators

(2.5)

X_{i}:f(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d})\mapsto X_{i}f(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}).

Thus,
X_{i}
becomes a linear operator from
F
into itself.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
3
, Theorem 1]
,
[
1
, Lemma 2.1]
)
.

Let
i\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}

and
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
The image of
G_{n}
by the multiplication operator
X_{i}
is
orthogonal to
G_{k}
for all
k\neq n-1,n,n+1
,
that is,
X_{i}G_{n}\perp G_{k}
.

Since
X_{i}F_{n}\subseteq F_{n+1}
and
G_{n}\subseteq F_{n}
,
we see from (
2.4
) that

X_{i}G_{n}\subseteq G_{0}\oplus G_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus G_{n+1}
.
Then with the help of Lemma
2.1
we obtain

(2.6)

X_{i}G_{n}\subseteq G_{n-1}\oplus G_{n}\oplus G_{n+1}.

Thus, for each homogenous polynomial random variable of degree
n
,

f\in G_{n}
, there exist unique
f_{i,n-1}\in G_{n-1}
,

f_{i,n}\in G_{n}
, and
f_{i,n+1}\in G_{n+1}

such that:

(2.7)

X_{i}f=f_{i,n-1}\oplus f_{i,n}\oplus f_{i,n+1},

where
\oplus
stands for the orthogonal sum.

According to (
2.7
)
we define three operators
D_{n}^{-}(i),D_{n}^{0}(i)
and
D_{n}^{+}(i)
by

(2.8)

D_{n}^{-}(i)f=f_{i,n-1},\qquad D_{n}^{0}(i)f=f_{i,n},\qquad D_{n}^{+}(i)f=f_{i,n+1}.

Then we have

(2.9)

D_{n}^{-}(i):G_{n}\to G_{n-1},\qquad D_{n}^{0}(i):G_{n}\to G_{n},\qquad D_{n}^{+}(i):G_{n}\to G_{n+1}.

The operator
D_{n}^{-}(i)
decreases the degree of
a homogenous polynomial random variable by
1
unit
and is called an
annihilation operator
.
The operator
D_{n}^{0}(i)
does not change the degree of
a homogenous polynomial random variable
and is called a
preservation operator
.
Finally,
D_{n}^{+}(i)
increases the degree of
a homogenous polynomial random variable by
1
unit
and is called a
creation
operator.
With these notations Lemma
2.1
can be written as

Lemma 2.2
.

For all
i\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}

we have

(2.10)

X_{i}|_{G_{n}}=D_{n}^{-}(i)+D_{n}^{0}(i)+D_{n}^{+}(i),

where
X_{i}|_{G_{n}}
stands for the restriction of the
multiplication operator
X_{i}
to
G_{n}
.

We extend the definition of the creation, preservation,
and annihilation operators to the space
F
of
all polynomial random variables in the following manner.
For all
f\in F
there exist unique
f_{0}\in G_{0}
,

f_{1}\in G_{1}
,
f_{2}\in G_{2}
,
\cdots
, with only finitely many
of them being not zero, such that

(2.11)

f=f_{0}\oplus f_{1}\oplus f_{2}\oplus\cdots.

We define the
creation operator

a^{+}(i)
by

(2.12)

a^{+}(i)f=D_{0}^{+}(i)f_{0}+D_{1}^{+}(i)f_{1}+D_{2}^{+}(i)f_{2}+\dotsb.

Similarly, the
preservation operator

a^{0}(i)
and

annihilation operator

a^{-}(i)
are defined by

(2.13)

\displaystyle a^{0}(i)f

\displaystyle=D_{0}^{0}(i)f_{0}+D_{1}^{0}(i)f_{1}+D_{2}^{0}(i)f_{2}+\dotsb,

(2.14)

\displaystyle a^{-}(i)f

\displaystyle=D_{0}^{-}(i)f_{0}+D_{1}^{-}(i)f_{1}+D_{2}^{-}(i)f_{2}+\dotsb,

respectively.
The operators
a^{+}(i),a^{0}(i),a^{-}(i)
,
i\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}
,
are generally called the
joint quantum operators

associated to the random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}
.
Now Lemma
2.2
can be
written as follows.

Lemma 2.3
.

For all
i\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}
we have

(2.15)

X_{i}=a^{-}(i)+a^{0}(i)+a^{+}(i),

where the domain of these operators is taken to be
the space
F
of all polynomial random variables

f(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d})
.

We have thus defined the quantum operators,
first separately on each homogenous chaos space

G_{n}
for
n\geq 0
,
and then extended them in a linear way
to the space
F
of all polynomial random variables of

X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}
.
We now mention an alternative way to define
the quantum operators directly on
F
.

For each
n\geq 0
let
\bar{P}_{n}
denote
the projection operator of
L^{2}(\Omega
,
P)
onto
G_{n}
,
and
P_{n}
the restriction of
\bar{P}_{n}
to
F
.
The identity operator of
F
is denoted by by
I
.
It follows from Lemma
2.1
that

P_{m}X_{i}P_{n}=0
for any non-negative integers
m
and
n

such that
|m-n|\geq 2
and
1\leq i\leq d
.
Then, for each
1\leq i\leq d
we have

\displaystyle X_{i}

\displaystyle=IX_{i}I

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}P_{m}\bigg)X_{i}\bigg(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|m-n|\leq 1}P_{m}X_{i}P_{n}

(2.16)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n+1}X_{i}P_{n}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}X_{i}P_{n}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P_{n}X_{i}P_{n-1}.

Each sum of the last expression corresponds to
a quantum operator of index
i
, namely,

(2.17)

\displaystyle a^{+}(i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n+1}X_{i}P_{n},

(2.18)

\displaystyle a^{0}(i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}X_{i}P_{n},

(2.19)

\displaystyle a^{-}(i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P_{n}X_{i}P_{n-1}.

Moreover, from (
2.17
) and (
2.19
) we see that

(2.20)

(a^{+}(i))^{\star}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(P_{n+1}X_{i}P_{n}\right)^{\star}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}X_{i}P_{n+1}=a^{-}(i)

and from (
2.18
)

(2.21)

(a^{0}(i))^{\star}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(P_{n}X_{i}P_{n}\right)^{\star}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}X_{i}P_{n}=a^{0}(i).

The above formulas must be interpreted carefully,
since we speak about adjoints of unbounded operators.
To be cautious,
we say that
a^{-}(i)
is the polynomial dual of
a^{+}(i)
,
while
a^{0}(i)
is polynomially self-dual,
which means that for any pair of polynomial random variables

f,g\in F
we have

(2.22)

\langle a^{+}(i)f,g\rangle=\langle f,a^{-}(i)g\rangle

and

(2.23)

\langle a^{0}(i)f,g\rangle=\langle f,a^{0}(i)g\rangle,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes
the inner product in
L^{2}(\Omega,P)
.

For all
i\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}

we define the
semi-annihilation operator
of index
i
by

(2.24)

U_{i}=a^{-}(i)+\frac{1}{2}\,a^{0}(i)

and the
semi-creation operator
of index
i
by

(2.25)

V_{i}=a^{+}(i)+\frac{1}{2}\,a^{0}(i).

The following assertion is immediate from
Lemma
2.3
.

Lemma 2.4
.

For all
i\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}
we have

(2.26)

X_{i}=U_{i}+V_{i}.

Since
a^{+}(i)
is the polynomial dual of
a^{-}(i)

and
a^{0}(i)
is polynomial self-dual,
we conclude that
V_{i}
is the polynomial dual of
U_{i}

for all
i\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}
.
We call
U_{i}
and
V_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}
,
the
joint semi-quantum operators

associated to
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}
.

Another very important operator is the
number operator

N:F\to F
defined by

(2.27)

N=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}nP_{n},

or equivalently by

(2.28)

Nf=nf,\qquad f\in G_{n},\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}.

When
d=1
, we start with just random variable
X=X_{1}

and, omitting the subscript
1
we write

a^{+}
,
a^{0}
,
a^{-}
,
V
and
U

for the creation, preservation, annihilation, semi-creation and
semi-annihilation operators, respectively.
In that case
F_{n}=F_{n-1}+\mathbb{C}x^{n}

for all
n\geq 0
and the codimension of
F_{n-1}

in
F_{n}
is at most
1
.
Thus, the dimension of
G_{n}=F_{n}\ominus F_{n-1}
is at most
1

for all
n\geq 0
.
Consequently, if
G_{n}\neq 0
,
then there exists a unique polynomial random variable
f_{n}(X)

in
G_{n}
, having the leading coefficient (the coefficient of
X^{n}
)
equal to
1
.
Due to this fact, the quantum decomposition
X=a^{+}+a^{0}+a^{-}
becomes
the classical recursive relation among monic orthogonal polynomials,
namely, for all
n\geq 0
there exists real numbers
\alpha_{n}

and
\omega_{n}
such that

(2.29)

Xf_{n}(X)=f_{n+1}(X)+\alpha_{n}f_{n}(X)+\omega_{n}f_{n-1}(X).

The numbers
\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
and
\{\omega_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}

(
\omega_{0}
is not defined since
f_{-1}(X)=0
)
are called the
Szegő-Jacobi parameters
of
X
.
We thus come to

(2.30)

\displaystyle a^{+}f_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=f_{n+1}(X),

(2.31)

\displaystyle a^{0}f_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{n}f_{n}(X),

(2.32)

\displaystyle a^{-}f_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=\omega_{n}f_{n-1}(X).

Remark 2.5
.

A similar idea of semi-quantum operators in case of
d=1

appeared in
[
10
]
, where the adjacency matrix
of a Hamming graph was decomposed into a sum of
“semi-quantum operators” and its asymptotic spectral
distribution was determined.
Later the “semi-quantum operators” was replaced with
the “full” quantum decomposition, see e.g.,
[
9
]
.

3.
Universal Commutation Relationships

In what follows, for any pair of linear operators
A
and
B

we define the commutator by

(3.1)

[A,B]=AB-BA,

provided both compositions
AB
and
BA
make sense.

It is very important to understand the commutation relations
satisfied by the quantum, semi-quantum and number operators
associated to a given family of random variables

X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}
having finite moments of all orders.
Some of those commutation relationships are specific
to a particular family random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}
,
and the first moments determine completely the random variables
in the sense of joint moments.
On the other hand, there is a set of commutation relationships
that is common for all finite families of random variables.
These are called the
universal commutation relationships

[
8
]
.

Theorem 3.1

(Universal Commutators Theorem
[
8
]
)
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}
be random variables defined on
a common probability space and having finite moments of all orders.
Let
a^{+}(i),a^{0}(i),a^{-}(i)
,
V_{i},U_{i}
and
N
be their
joint creation, preservation, annihilation, semi-creation,
semi-annihilation, and number operators, where
i\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}
.
Then, for all
i\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}
we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle[N,a^{+}(i)]=a^{+}(i),

(3.3)

\displaystyle[N,a^{0}(i)]=0,

(3.4)

\displaystyle[a^{-}(i),N]=a^{-}(i),

(3.5)

\displaystyle[N,V_{i}]=a^{+}(i),

(3.6)

\displaystyle[U_{i},N]=a^{-}(i),

(3.7)

\displaystyle[N,X_{i}]=V_{i}-U_{i}=a^{+}(i)-a^{-}(i).

The above commutation relationships can be combined
with the rules of “commutative probability”,
i.e.,
X_{i}X_{j}=X_{j}X_{i}
for all
i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}
.
These rules, established in
[
1
,
2
,
14
]
,
are as follows: for all
i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}
we have

(3.8)

\displaystyle[a^{+}(i),a^{+}(j)]=0,

(3.9)

\displaystyle[a^{0}(i),a^{+}(j)]=[a^{0}(j),a^{+}(i)],

(3.10)

\displaystyle[a^{-}(i),a^{+}(j)]+[a^{0}(i),a^{0}(j)]+[a^{+}(i),a^{-}(j)]=0,

(3.11)

\displaystyle[a^{-}(i),a^{0}(j)]=[a^{-}(j),a^{0}(i)],

(3.12)

\displaystyle[a^{-}(i),a^{-}(j)]=0,

(3.13)

\displaystyle[X_{i},V_{j}]=[X_{j},V_{i}],

(3.14)

\displaystyle[U_{i},X_{j}]=[U_{j},X_{i}],

(3.15)

\displaystyle[X_{i},V_{j}]^{\star}=[X_{i},V_{j}],

(3.16)

\displaystyle[U_{i},X_{j}]^{\star}=[U_{i},X_{j}].

4.
Position-Momentum Decomposition of Linear Operators
Defined on
F

Let
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}]
be the algebra of polynomials
with complex coefficients in
d
variables
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}
.

Definition 4.1
.

For any
\vec{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{d})\in(\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\})^{d}
, we define the

\vec{\alpha}
-position operator

X^{\vec{\alpha}}:\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}]\to\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}]
by

(4.1)

X^{\vec{\alpha}}f\left(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}\right)=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}),

and
\vec{\alpha}
-momentum operator

D^{\vec{\alpha}}:\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}]\to\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}]
by

(4.2)

D^{\vec{\alpha}}f\left(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}\right)=\frac{\partial^{\alpha_{1}}}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}}\frac{\partial^{\alpha_{2}}}{\partial x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}}\cdots\frac{\partial^{\alpha_{d}}}{\partial x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}}f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}).

The position operator
X^{\vec{\alpha}}
becomes naturally
a multiplication operator on the space
F
of polynomial random
variables as a composition of the multiplication
operators by
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}
.
On the other hand, the momentum operator
D^{\vec{\alpha}}

may not be well-defined as an operator from
F
into itself.
In fact, if the joint probability distribution
\mu
of

X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}
is supported on the algebraic variety

V=\{\vec{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\mid f_{0}(\vec{x})=0\}

for some polynomial
f_{0}(x_{1}
,
x_{2}
,
\dots
,
x_{d})\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}]
,
then we have
g(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d})=h(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d})

holds almost surely for any pair of polynomials
g
and
h

such that
f_{0}\mid(g-h)
,
but
D^{\vec{\alpha}}g(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d})
may not
be almost surely equal to

D^{\vec{\alpha}}h(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d})

since
f_{0}\nmid(D^{\vec{\alpha}}g-D^{\vec{\alpha}}h)
may happen.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
k
be a fixed integer and
T:F\to F
a linear operator.
We say that
T
is
k
-faithful to the gradation

\{F_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
if

(4.3)

TF_{n}\subseteq F_{n+k},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

Here we tacitly understand that
F_{n+k}=\{0\}
if
n+k<0
.

Theorem 4.3

(
[
8
]
)
.

If
T
is
k
-faithful,
then there exists a unique family of polynomial random variables

\{A_{\vec{\alpha}}(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d})\}_{\vec{\alpha}\in(\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\})^{d}}
with

(4.4)

\deg(A_{\vec{\alpha}})\leq|\vec{\alpha}|+k,\qquad\vec{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{d})\in({\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\})^{d},

where
|\vec{\alpha}|=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\dotsb+\alpha_{d}
is
the length of
\vec{\alpha}
and
\deg(A_{\vec{\alpha}})

denotes the degree of
A_{\vec{\alpha}}
,
such that

(4.5)

T(f(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}))=\sum_{\vec{\alpha}\in({\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\})^{d}}A_{\vec{\alpha}}(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d})(D^{\vec{\alpha}}f)(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d})

for all
f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}]
.
The uniqueness of
\{A_{\vec{\alpha}}(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}\}_{\alpha\in(\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\})^{d}}
is
understood in the sence of
\mu
-almost sure,
where
\mu
is the joint probability distribution of

X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}
.

For convenience formula (
4.5
) is
written as

(4.6)

T=\sum_{\vec{\alpha}\in{{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}}^{d}}A_{\vec{\alpha}}D^{\vec{\alpha}},

which is called the
position-momentum decomposition
of
T
.

Remark 4.4
.

For a given polynomial random variable
Y\in F

there is ambiguity of choosing a polynomial
f

such that
Y=f(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d})
,
\mu
-a.s.
Once such a polynomial
f
is chosen the same polynomial
must be used for all the terms in the right-hand side of
formula (
4.5
)).

For
\vec{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d}),\vec{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})\in(\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\})^{d}

we write
\vec{\alpha}\preceq\vec{\beta}
if

\alpha_{i}\leq\beta_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq d
.
We then write
\vec{\alpha}\prec\vec{\beta}

if
\vec{\alpha}\preceq\vec{\beta}

and
\vec{\alpha}\neq\vec{\beta}
.
For a polynomial
f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d})
let

m_{i}
be the highest power at which
x_{i}
appears in
f

and set
\vec{m}=(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{d})\in(\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\})^{d}
.
Then formula (
4.5
) becomes

(4.7)

T(f\left(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}\right))=\sum_{\vec{\alpha}\preceq\vec{m}}A_{\vec{\alpha}}(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d})(D^{\vec{\alpha}}f)(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}).

In fact, if we differentiate
f
with respect to
one of the variables
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}
more times than
its highest power, we get
0
.
Thus (
4.5
)
is reduced to (
4.7
).
The above argument is based on the form of
(
4.7
),
where the “position factors” contained
in the multiplication operators

A_{\vec{\alpha}}(\vec{X}^{\vec{\alpha}})

is placed to the left of all the “momentum factors”

D^{\vec{\alpha}}
.
We also note that
a mixed composition of position and momentum operators

x_{1}
,
\dots
,
x_{d}
,
\partial/\partial x_{1}
, …,
\partial x_{d}
, that do not obey the left-position right-momentum order
is always rearranged by using the famous Heisenberg
commutation relations:

(4.8)

\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}},x_{j}\right]=\delta_{i,j}I,\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq d,

where
\delta_{i,j}
denotes the Kronecker symbol
and I the identity operator.

For more detailed information on
the position-momentum decomposition,
see
[
8
]
.

5.
Position-Momentum Decomposition of Meixner Random Variables

We start with the classical Meixner random variables.

Definition 5.1
.

A real-valued random variable
X
having finite moments of all orders
is called a
classical Meixner random variable
if
its Szegő-Jacobi parameters are of the form:

(5.1)

\alpha_{n}=\alpha n+\alpha_{0},\qquad\omega_{n}=\beta n^{2}+(t-\beta)n,\qquad n\geq 1,

where
\alpha
,
\alpha_{0}
,
\beta
, and
t
are real constants
satisfying one of the following conditions:

(1)

\beta\geq 0
and
t\geq 0
;

(2)

\beta<0
and
t\in-{\mathbb{N}}\beta
.

If
t=0
, then
\omega_{1}=0
and hence
X
becomes
a constant random variable.
This case being not interesting, we only consider the case of
t>0
.
We may also assume that
\alpha\geq 0

since otherwise, we can replace
X
by
-X
.

We will follow the following steps:

Step 1.
Find the commutator
[U
,
X]
between
the semi-annihilation operator
U
and multiplication operator by
X
.
For all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
if
f_{n}(X)
denotes the orthogonal polynomial of degree
n

with leading coefficient equal to
1
, we have

\displaystyle[U,X]f_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=[U,U+V]f_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=[U,V]f_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=\left[a^{-}+\frac{1}{2}a^{0},a^{+}+\frac{1}{2}a^{0}\right]f_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=\left[a^{-},a^{+}\right]f_{n}(X)+\frac{1}{2}\left[a^{-},a^{0}\right]f_{n}(X)+\frac{1}{2}\left[a^{0},a^{+}\right]f_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=a^{-}a^{+}f_{n}(X)-a^{+}a^{-}f_{n}(X)+\frac{1}{2}\left[a^{-}a^{0}f_{n}(X)-a^{0}a^{-}f_{n}(X)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\left[a^{0}a^{+}f_{n}(X)-a^{+}a^{0}f_{n}(X)\right]

\displaystyle=a^{-}f_{n+1}(X)-a^{+}\omega_{n}f_{n-1}(X)+\frac{1}{2}\left[a^{-}\alpha_{n}f_{n}(X)-a^{0}\omega_{n}f_{n-1}(X)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\left[a^{0}f_{n+1}(X)-a^{+}\alpha_{n}f_{n-1}(X)\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\omega_{n+1}-\omega_{n}\right)f_{n}(X)+\frac{1}{2}\omega_{n}\left(\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{n-1}\right)f_{n-1}(X)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha_{n+1}-\alpha_{n}\right)f_{n+1}(X).

For
n\geq 1
we have

(5.2)

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\alpha(n+1)+\alpha_{0}-\alpha n-\alpha_{0}=\alpha,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\omega_{n+1}-\omega_{n}

\displaystyle=\beta(n+1)^{2}+(t-\beta)(n+1)-\beta n^{2}-(t-\beta)n=2\beta n+t.

Thus, for all
n\geq 1
we obtain:

\displaystyle[U,X]f_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=(2\beta n+t)f_{n}(X)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha\omega_{n}f_{n-1}(X)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha f_{n+1}(X)

\displaystyle=(2\beta N+tI)f_{n}(X)+\frac{\alpha}{2}a^{-}f_{n}(X)+\frac{\alpha}{2}a^{+}f_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=(2\beta N+tI)f_{n}(X)+\frac{\alpha}{2}(a^{-}+a^{+})f_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=(2\beta N+tI)f_{n}(X)+\frac{\alpha}{2}(X-a^{0})f_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=(2\beta N+tI)f_{n}(X)+\frac{\alpha}{2}(X-\alpha N-\alpha_{0}I)f_{n}(X).

The last formula is also true for
f_{0}(X)=1
since

\displaystyle[U,X]f_{0}(X)

\displaystyle=UXf_{0}(X)-XUf_{0}(X)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}a^{0}+a^{-}\right)\left[f_{1}(X)+\alpha_{0}f_{0}(X)\right]-X\left(\frac{1}{2}a^{0}+a^{-}\right)f_{0}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}f_{1}(X)+\frac{\alpha_{0}^{2}}{2}f_{0}(X)+\omega_{1}f_{0}(X)-X\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}f_{0}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}f_{1}(X)+\frac{\alpha_{0}^{2}}{2}f_{0}(X)+\omega_{1}f_{0}(X)-\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}(f_{1}(X)+\alpha_{0}f_{0}(X))

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}f_{1}(X)+\omega_{1}f_{0}(X)

\displaystyle=tf_{0}(X)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(X-\alpha_{0}\right)

and
Nf_{0}(X)=0f_{0}(X)=0
.
Thus, we obtain

(5.4)

[U,X]=\frac{\alpha}{2}X+\frac{4\beta-\alpha^{2}}{2}N+\frac{2t-\alpha\alpha_{0}}{2}I.

We set

(5.5)

\Delta=\alpha^{2}-4\beta,\qquad\tau=2t-\alpha\alpha_{0}.

Then (
5.4
) becomes

(5.6)

[U,X]=\frac{\alpha}{2}X-\frac{\Delta}{2}N+\frac{\tau}{2}I.

We consider two cases.

Case 1.

\Delta=0
.
We obtain

(5.7)

[U,X]=\frac{\alpha}{2}X+\frac{\tau}{2}I,

which means that the commutator
[U,X]
is a linear combination of

X
and
I
.
After
[
14
]
we call such a random variable is called

1
-Meixner random variable
.

Case 2.

\Delta\neq 0
.
Using the universal commutator theorem,
we compute the following double commutator:

\displaystyle[[U,X],X]

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\alpha}{2}X-\frac{\Delta}{2}N+\frac{\tau}{2}I,X\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Delta}{2}[N,X]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Delta}{2}\left(V-U\right)

(5.8)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Delta}{2}\left(X-2U\right).

After
[
14
]
such a random variable is called

2
-Meixner random variables
.

Step 2.

Find the position-momentum decomposition of
the semi-quantum operators
U
and
V
.
Indeed, let the position-momentum decomposition of
U
be

(5.9)

U=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}(X)D^{n},

where for all

is a polynomial of degree at most
n
,
since
U
maps
F_{n}
into
F_{n}
.

Case 1.

\Delta=0
.
Substituting
U
by its position-momentum decomposition in

5.7

(5.10)

\frac{\alpha}{2}X+\frac{\tau}{2}I=[U,X]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left[A_{n}(X)D^{n},X\right].

Here we can interchange the sum with the commutator,
since we apply the operators
A_{n}(X)D^{n}
only
to polynomial random variables
f(X)\in F
,
and for each fixed
f
, for all
n>N
,
where
N
is the degree of
f
, we have
D^{n}=0
.
Thus after a while we are adding only zero terms.
Applying the Leibniz commutator rule:

(5.11)

[B_{1}B_{2}\cdots B_{k},C]=\sum_{i=1}^{k}B_{1}\cdots B_{i-1}[B_{i},C]B_{i+1}\cdots B_{k},

we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2}X+\frac{\tau}{2}I

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left[A_{n}(X)D^{n},X\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\{[A_{n}(X),X]D^{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{n}(X)D^{i-1}[D,X]D^{n-i}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\{0D^{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{n}(X)D^{i-1}ID^{n-i}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nA_{n}(X)D^{n-1}

(5.12)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+1)A_{n+1}(X)D^{n}.

We can write the above formula as

(5.13)

\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}X+\frac{\tau}{2}I\right)D^{0}+0D^{1}+0D^{2}+\cdots=A_{1}(X)D^{0}+2A_{2}(X)D^{1}+3A_{3}(X)D^{3}+\cdots.

Using the uniqueness of the position-momentum decomposition,
we can equate the left-position coefficients of
the corresponding right-momentum factors
D^{n}

from the two sides of the above equality, and obtain:

(5.14)

\displaystyle A_{1}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}X+\frac{\tau}{2}I,

(5.15)

\displaystyle nA_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=0,\qquad n\geq 2.

The left-position coefficient
A_{0}(X)
can be
obtained from the fact that,
for the constant polynomial
f_{0}=1
we have:

U1=\left(a^{-}+\frac{1}{2}a^{0}\right)1\\
=\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}1

and

(5.16)

U1=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}(X)D^{n}1=A_{0}(X)D^{0}1=A_{0}(X)1.

Thus, we obtain:

(5.17)

A_{0}(X)=\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}.

Therefore, the position-momentum quantum decomposition of the

1
-Meixner random variable with parameters
\alpha
,
\alpha_{0}
,
and
t
(remember that
\beta=\alpha^{2}/4
), is

(5.18)

U=\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}I+\frac{1}{2}(\alpha X+\tau)D.

In view of
V=X-U
,
we conclude that the position-momentum decomposition of
V
is
given by

(5.19)

V=\left(X-\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}\right)I-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha X+\tau I)D.

Case 2.

\Delta\neq 0
.
Substituting
U
by its position-momentum decomposition
in (
5.8
), we obtain

(5.20)

\left[\left[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}(X)D^{n},X\right],X\right]=-\frac{\Delta}{2}\left(X-2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}(X)D^{n}\right).

Using the Leibniz commutator formula, we obtain

(5.21)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[nA_{n}(X)D^{n-1},X\right]=-\frac{\Delta}{2}\left\{X-2A_{0}(X)\right\}I+\Delta\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}(X)D^{n}.

Using the Leibniz commutator formula again, we obtain

(5.22)

\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}n(n-1)A_{n}(X)D^{n-2}=-\frac{\Delta}{2}\left\{X-2A_{0}(X)\right\}I+\Delta\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}(X)D^{n},

which, after making the change of variable
n\mapsto n-2

in the left sum, becomes:

(5.23)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+2)(n+1)A_{n+2}(X)D^{n}=-\frac{\Delta}{2}\left\{X-2A_{0}(X)\right\}I+\Delta\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}(X)D^{n}.

Equating the left-position coefficients of the corresponding
right-momentum factors
D^{n}
, for
n\geq 0

we obtain the recursive formula:

(5.24)

A_{n+2}(X)=\frac{\Delta}{(n+2)(n+1)}\left(A_{n}(X)-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{n,0}X\right),

where
\delta_{p,q}
denotes the Kronecker symbol.
Iterating the recursive relation (
5.24
),
we obtain

(5.25)

\displaystyle A_{2n}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta^{n}}{(2n)!}\left(A_{0}(X)-\frac{1}{2}X\right)

(5.26)

\displaystyle A_{2n+1}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta^{n}}{(2n+1)!}A_{1}(X),

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
The left-position coefficients
A_{0}(X)
and
A_{1}(X)
can be
found by applying
U
to
f_{0}(X)=1
and

f_{1}(X)=X-\alpha_{0}
.
We have, as before

(5.27)

U1=\frac{1}{2}a^{0}1=\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}1

and

(5.28)

U1=A_{0}(X)1,

which means that
A_{0}(X)=\alpha_{0}/2
.
Moreover, we have

\displaystyle Uf_{1}(X)

\displaystyle=a^{-}f_{1}(X)+\frac{1}{2}a^{0}f_{1}(X)

\displaystyle=\omega_{1}f_{0}(X)+\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}f_{1}(X)

\displaystyle=(\beta\cdot 1^{2}+(t-\beta)1)1+\frac{\alpha\cdot 1+\alpha_{0}}{2}(X-\alpha_{0})

(5.29)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha+\alpha_{0}}{2}(X-\alpha_{0})+t

and also

\displaystyle Uf_{1}(X)

\displaystyle=(A_{0}(X)I+A_{1}(X)D)(X-\alpha_{0})

(5.30)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}(X-\alpha_{0})+A_{1}(X).

From (
5.29

(5.31)

A_{1}(X)=\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(X-\alpha_{0}\right)+t.

It follows from (

(5.32)

\displaystyle A_{2n}(X)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\Delta^{n}}{(2n)!}(X-\alpha_{0})

(5.33)

\displaystyle A_{2n+1}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta^{n}}{(2n+1)!}\left[\frac{\alpha}{2}(X-\alpha_{0})+t\right].

Let
\delta

\Delta

\Delta=\delta^{2}

Since

Thus we obtain the position-momentum decomposition of

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{2n}(X)D^{2n}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{2n+1}(X)D^{2n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}I-\frac{1}{2}(X-\alpha_{0})\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Delta^{n}}{(2n)!}D^{2n}+\left[\frac{\alpha}{2}(X-\alpha_{0})+t\right]\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Delta^{n}}{(2n+1)!}D^{2n+1}.

With each complex number

We then see by the Taylor formula that the position-momentum

(5.37)

\mathcal{T}_{-\delta}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\delta)^{n}}{n!}D^{n}=\exp(-\delta D).

Adding and subtracting both sides of (

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\delta^{2n}}{(2n)!}D^{2n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}I-\frac{1}{4}(X-\alpha_{0})\cdot 0+\frac{1}{2}(\alpha X+\tau)D

of the

-Meixner random variables.

\displaystyle=X-\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}I+\frac{1}{4}(X-\alpha_{0})(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}+\mathcal{T}_{-\delta}-2I)-\frac{1}{4\delta}(\alpha X+\tau)(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}-\mathcal{T}_{-\delta}).

Find the position-momentum decomposition of the number operator

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=(X-\alpha_{0})\lim_{\delta\to 0}\left\{\frac{1}{2\delta}(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}-\mathcal{T}_{-\delta})\right\}-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha X+\tau)\lim_{\delta\to 0}\left\{\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}+\mathcal{T}_{-\delta}-2I)\right\}

(5.50)

\displaystyle=(X-\alpha_{0})D-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha X+\tau)D^{2}.

Step 4.

Find the position-momentum decomposition of the quantum operators

find first

On the other hand, since

Using

In view of

a^{-}
and
a^{+}
.
Thus, using formulas (
5.40
),
(
5.41
) and (
5.53
),
we obtain

(5.54)

a^{-}=U-\frac{1}{2}a^{0}.

We have:

\displaystyle a^{-}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}I-\frac{1}{4}(X-\alpha_{0})(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}+\mathcal{T}_{-\delta}-2I)+\frac{1}{4\delta}(\alpha X+\tau)(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}-\mathcal{T}_{-\delta})

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\alpha}{4\delta}(X-\alpha_{0})(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}-\mathcal{T}_{-\delta})+\frac{\alpha}{4\delta^{2}}(\alpha X+\tau)(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}+\mathcal{T}_{-\delta}-2I)-\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}I

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau+\alpha\alpha_{0}}{4\delta}(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}-\mathcal{T}_{-\delta})+\frac{\alpha_{0}\delta^{2}+\alpha\tau}{4\delta^{2}}(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}+\mathcal{T}_{-\delta}-2I)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\alpha^{2}-\delta^{2}}{4\delta^{2}}X(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}+\mathcal{T}_{-\delta}-2I).

Since
\tau+\alpha\alpha_{0}=2t
and
\alpha^{2}-\delta^{2}=4\beta
,
the last expression becomes:

(5.55)

a^{-}=\frac{t}{2\delta}(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}-\mathcal{T}_{-\delta}t)+\frac{\alpha_{0}\delta^{2}+\alpha\tau}{4\delta^{2}}(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}+\mathcal{T}_{-\delta}-2I)+\frac{\beta}{\delta^{2}}X(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}+\mathcal{T}_{-\delta}-2I).

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle a^{+}

\displaystyle=V-\frac{1}{2}a^{0}

\displaystyle=X-\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}I+\frac{1}{4}(X-\alpha_{0})(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}+\mathcal{T}_{-\delta}-2I)-\frac{1}{4\delta}(\alpha X+\tau)(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}-\mathcal{T}_{-\delta})

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\alpha}{4\delta}(X-\alpha_{0})(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}-\mathcal{T}_{-\delta})+\frac{\alpha}{4\delta^{2}}(\alpha X+\tau)(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}+\mathcal{T}_{-\delta}-2I)-\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}I

\displaystyle=X-\frac{\alpha}{2\delta}X(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}-\mathcal{T}_{-\delta})+\frac{\alpha^{2}+\delta^{2}}{4\delta^{2}}X(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}+\mathcal{T}_{-\delta}-I)

(5.56)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\alpha\alpha_{0}-\tau}{4\delta}(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}-\mathcal{T}_{-\delta})+\frac{\alpha\tau-\alpha_{0}\delta^{2}}{4\delta^{2}}(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}+\mathcal{T}_{-\delta}-I)-\alpha_{0}I.

We now introduce the
\delta
-momentum operator
defined by

(5.57)

D_{\delta}=\begin{cases}(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}-I)/\delta,&\text{if $\delta\neq 0$},\\
D&\text{if $\delta=0$}.\end{cases}

We have

(5.58)

D_{\delta}(F_{n})\subseteq F_{n-1},\qquad n\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\},

which means that
D_{\delta}
is a
(-1)
-faithful operator
with respect to the gradation
\{F_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
.
Moreover,
D_{\delta}
is a
purely momentuous operator

in the sense that its position-momentum decomposition
contains no non-constant left-position coefficients
A_{n}(X)
.
Indeed,

(5.59)

D_{\delta}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\delta^{n-1}}{n!}D^{n}.

With this notation the annihilation operator of
X
can be written as

(5.60)

a^{-}=\frac{t}{2}(D_{\delta}+D_{-\delta})+\frac{\alpha_{0}\delta^{2}+\alpha\tau}{4\delta}(D_{\delta}-D_{-\delta})+\frac{\beta}{\delta}X(D_{\delta}-D_{-\delta}).

If
\beta=0
, (
5.60
) becomes

(5.61)

a^{-}=\frac{t}{2}(D_{\delta}+D_{-\delta})+\frac{\alpha_{0}\delta^{2}+\alpha\tau}{4\delta}(D_{\delta}-D_{-\delta}),

which means that
a^{-}
is a purely momentuous operator and
is a linear combination of translation operators
whose coefficients add up to zero.
The above observation motivates us to study the following

Problem 5.2
.

Find all random variables
X
having finite moments of all orders
such that its annihilation operator is of the form:

(5.62)

a^{-}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mathcal{T}_{d_{i}},

for some real constants
c_{i}
and
d_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq s
,
satisfying the necessary condition:

(5.63)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=0.

Without loss of generality we may assume
that
c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n}
are all different from zero
and
d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n}
are different each other.

Before solving this problem, let us explain
why the condition (
5.63
) is necessary.
Since
a^{-}
decreases the degree of a polynomial random variable
by
1
-unit, the degree of
a^{-}X^{m}
must be at most
m-1

for all
m\geq 0
.
Therefore the coefficient of
X^{m}
in

(5.64)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mathcal{T}_{d_{i}}X^{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}(X+d_{i})^{m}

must be zero.
This is equivalent to condition (
5.63
).

Going back to Problem
5.2
,
let us assume that
a^{-}
has the form (
5.62
).
We may assume that it has the mean
0
after suitably shifting
X

since a shift does not affect the annihilation operator
a^{-}

Keeping in mind that
a^{0}1=E{X}1=0
,
for all
m\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle E[X^{m}]

\displaystyle=\left\langle X^{m}1,1\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle(a^{+}+a^{0}+a^{-})X^{m-1}1,1\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle X^{m-1}1,a^{-}1\right\rangle+\left\langle X^{m-1}1,a^{0}1\right\rangle+\left\langle a^{-}X^{m-1}1,1\right\rangle

\displaystyle=0+0+\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mathcal{T}_{d_{i}}X^{m-1}1,1\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\left\langle(X+d_{i})^{m-1}1,1\right\rangle

(5.65)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}E[(X+d_{i})^{m-1}].

Claim 1.

There exists a constant
k\geq 1
such that

(5.66)

|E[X^{m}]|\leq k^{m}\cdot m!,\qquad m\geq 0.

Indeed, from the obvious limit

(5.67)

\lim_{p\to\infty}\frac{|d_{i}|^{p}}{p!}=0,\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,

we see that there exists a constant
A\geq 1
such that

(5.68)

\frac{|d_{i}|^{p}}{p!}\leq A,\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,\qquad p\geq 0.

We set

(5.69)

k=\max\left\{A\sum_{i=1}^{n}|c_{i}|,1\right\}.

We now prove (
5.66
) by induction on
n
.
Since
k\geq 1
, inequality (
5.66
) is true
for
m=0
.
Let
M\geq 1
and assume that inequality
(
5.66
) is true for all
m\leq M-1
.
Using the recursive relation (
5.65
), we obtain

\displaystyle|E[X^{M}]|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}E[(X+d_{i})^{M-1}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}|c_{i}|\binom{M-1}{m}|d_{i}|^{M-1-m}|E[X^{m}]|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}|c_{i}|\binom{M-1}{m}\cdot\frac{|d_{i}|^{M-1-m}}{(M-1-m)!}\cdot|E[X^{m}]|\cdot(M-1-m)!\,.

Using the induction hypothesis, we obtain

\displaystyle|E[X^{M}]|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}|c_{i}|\binom{M-1}{m}\cdot A\cdot k^{m}m!\cdot(M-1-m)!

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}|c_{i}|(M-1)!Ak^{m}.

Note from
k\geq 1
that
k^{m}\leq k^{M-1}
for
for all
1\leq m\leq M-1
.
Then we have

\displaystyle|E[X^{M}]|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}|c_{i}|(M-1)!Ak^{M-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}M|c_{i}|(M-1)!Ak^{M-1}

\displaystyle=k^{M-1}M!\left(A\sum_{i=1}^{n}|c_{i}|\right)

(5.70)

\displaystyle\leq k^{M}M!\,,

as desired.

Claim 2.

For all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
we have

(5.71)

E[|X|^{m}]\leq(2k)^{m}m!.

Indeed, since
X
is a real-valued random variable,
we have
|X|^{2}=X^{2}
.
Using Jensen inequality for the concave function

f(x)=\sqrt{x}
(
x\geq 0
) and
the inequality (
5.66
),
we have

\displaystyle E[|X|^{m}]

\displaystyle=E[\sqrt{X^{2m}}]\leq\sqrt{E[X^{2m}]}\leq\sqrt{k^{2m}(2m)!}

\displaystyle=k^{m}m!\sqrt{\binom{2m}{m}}\leq k^{m}m!\sqrt{(1+1)^{2m}}=(2k)^{m}m!,

as desired.

Multiplying both sides of (
5.65
) by
t^{m-1}/(m-1)!

and summing up for
m=1
to
\infty
,
we obtain

(5.72)

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}E[X^{m}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}E[(X+d_{i})^{m-1}].

We know that the above power series converges
for
t
in a neighborhood of
0
.
By the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem
with (
5.71
)
we can interchange the series and expectation
to obtain

(5.73)

E[X\exp(tX)]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}E[\exp(t(X+d_{i}))],

where
t
is in a neighborhood of
0
.
Let
\varphi(t)=E[\exp(tX)]
be the Laplace transform of
X
.
Applying the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem again,
we conclude that

(5.74)

\frac{d\varphi}{dt}=\varphi(t)\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\exp(d_{i}t).

We have two cases.

Case 1.

d_{i}\neq 0
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.
Since
\varphi(0)=E[\exp(0X)]=1
,
the solution of the differential equation (
5.74
) is
given by

(5.75)

\varphi(t)=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{c_{i}}{d_{i}}\left(e^{d_{i}t}-1\right)\right)=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{c_{i}}{d_{i}}\left(e^{d_{i}t}-d_{i}t-1\right)\right),

where
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=0
is taken into account.
Then we have

(5.76)

\varphi(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(\frac{c_{i}}{d_{i}}\left(e^{d_{i}t}-d_{i}t-1\right)\right).

On the other hand,
letting
Y
be a Poisson random variable with mean
\lambda>0

and
\gamma
a real constant,
we consider
Z=\gamma(Y-\lambda)
.
Then, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle E[\exp(tZ)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{\gamma(k-\lambda)t}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}e^{-\lambda}

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda\gamma t-\lambda}\exp(\lambda e^{\gamma t})

(5.77)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\lambda(e^{\gamma t}-\gamma t-1)\right).

Since according to formula (
5.76
)
the Laplace transform
\varphi(t)
can be written
as a product of those of the above shifted
and re-scaled Poisson random variables,
we conclude that there exist
n
independent
Poisson random variables
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{n}
with means

c_{1}/d_{1}
,
c_{2}/d_{2}
,
\dots
,
c_{n}/d_{n}
, such that

(5.78)

X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}Y_{i}.

Since the mean (parameter)
\lambda
of
a Poisson random variable must be positive,
we have necessarily
c_{i}/d_{i}>0
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.
Hence
c_{i}
and
d_{i}
have the same sign for each
1\leq i\leq n
.

Case 2.

d_{i_{0}}=0
happens for some

i_{0}\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.
The solution of the differential equation (
5.74
)
with initial condition
\varphi(0)=1
is given by

\displaystyle\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(c_{i_{0}}t+\sum_{i\neq i_{0}}\frac{c_{i}}{d_{i}}\left(e^{d_{i}t}-1\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{i\neq i_{0}}\frac{c_{i}}{d_{i}}\left(e^{d_{i}t}-d_{i}t-1\right)\right)

(5.79)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\neq i_{0}}\exp\left(\frac{c_{i}}{d_{i}}\left(e^{d_{i}t}-d_{i}t-1\right)\right),

where
\sum_{i\neq i_{0}}c_{i}=-c_{i_{0}}
is taken into account.
Thus

(5.80)

X=\sum_{i\neq i_{0}}d_{i}Y_{i}\,,

where
Y_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n
,
i\neq i_{0}
,
are independent Poisson random variables with means
c_{i}/d_{i}
.

Since we have initially shifted the random variable
X
to have
the mean equal to zero, we can say that the answer to the problem
of describing the random variables having finite moments of all orders,
whose annihilation operator is a linear combination of translation
operators, is that those random variables are all shifted linear combinations of independent Poisson random variables.

Upon closing this paper we note that
formula (
5.55
) can be used
to recover all the six types of Meixner random variables,
by centering first the random variable
X

and then using the formula:

\displaystyle E[X^{m}]

\displaystyle=\langle X^{m}1,1\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(a^{+}+a^{0}+a^{-})X^{m-1}1,1\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle X^{m-1}1,a^{-}1\rangle+\langle X^{m-1}1,a^{0}1\rangle+\langle a^{-}X^{m-1}1,1\rangle

\displaystyle=0+0+\langle a^{-}X^{m-1}1,1\rangle

(5.81)

\displaystyle=\langle a^{-}X^{m-1}1,1\rangle.

Substituting
a^{-}
by its position-momentum decomposition
(
5.55
),
we obtain first a recursive formula for the moments of
X
as before,
then a differential equation for the Laplace transform of
X
.
Solving this differential equation and inverting the Laplace transform,
we obtain the well-known classification of
the classical Meixner random variables as below.

(1)

\alpha=\beta=0
.
In this case
X
is a
Gaussian
random variable,
i.e., a continuous random variable with the density function

f_{X}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\,\exp\bigg(-\frac{(x-\alpha_{0})^{2}}{2t}\bigg).

(2)

\beta=0
and
\alpha\neq 0
.
In this case
X
is a shifted and re-scaled
Poisson

random variable and its distribution is given by

\mu_{X}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\,e^{-\lambda}\delta_{\alpha(k-\lambda)+\alpha_{0}},

where
\lambda=t/\alpha^{2}
.

(3)

\beta>0
and
\alpha^{2}>4\beta
.
Then
X
is a shifted
Pascal (negative binomial)

random variable with the distribution given by

\mu_{X}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(r+k)}{k!\Gamma(r)}\,p^{r}(1-p)^{k}\delta_{k-[2t/(\alpha+d)]+\alpha_{0}},

where

d=\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-4\beta},\qquad p=\frac{2d}{\alpha+d}\,,\qquad r=\frac{t}{\beta}\,.

(4)

\beta>0
and
\alpha^{2}=4\beta
.
In this case
X
is a shifted and re-scaled

Gamma
random variable with shift parameter

2t/\alpha
and scaling parameter
\alpha/2
, i.e.,
its density function is given by

f_{X}(x)=\frac{2^{2t/\alpha}}{\alpha^{2t/\alpha}\,\Gamma(2t/\alpha)}\,x^{(2t/\alpha)-1}e^{-2x/\alpha}1_{(0,\infty)}(x).

(5)

\beta>0
and
\alpha^{2}<4\beta
.
In this case,
up to translation
X
is a
two parameter hyperbolic secant

random variable whose density function is given by

f_{X}(x)=ce^{2\theta x/\gamma}\left|\Gamma(k+ix\gamma)\right|^{2},

where

\gamma=\sqrt{4\beta-\alpha^{2}}\,,\qquad k=\frac{2t}{r\gamma}\,,\qquad\gamma+i\alpha=re^{i\theta},\quad-\frac{\pi}{2}<\theta<\frac{\pi}{2}\,.

(6)

\beta<0
. Then
t\in-{\mathbb{N}}\beta
.
In this case, up to a shifting and re-scaling

X
is a
binomial
random variable
whose distribution is given by

\mu_{X}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}\delta_{k},

where

n=-\frac{t}{\beta}\,,\qquad p=\frac{1\pm\sqrt{c/(4+c)}}{2}\,,\qquad c=-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta}\geq 0.
