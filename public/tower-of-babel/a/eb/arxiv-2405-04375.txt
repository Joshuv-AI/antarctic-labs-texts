Coherent distributions: Hilbert space approach and duality
Egor Kravchenko

Abstract
Let X X be a Bernoulli random variable with the success probability p p . We are interested in tight bounds on 𝔼 ⁡ [ f ⁡ ( X 1 , X 2 ) ] \mathbb{E}[f(X_{1},X_{2})] , where X i = 𝔼 ⁡ [ X | ℱ i ] X_{i}=\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{F}_{i}] and ℱ i \mathcal{F}_{i} are some sigma-algebras. This problem is closely related to understanding extreme points of the set of coherent distributions. A distribution on [ 0 , 1 ] 2 [0,1]^{2} is called coherent if it can be obtained as the joint distribution of ( X 1 , X 2 ) (X_{1},X_{2}) for some choice of ℱ i \mathcal{F}_{i} . By treating random variables as vectors in a Hilbert space, we establish an upper bound for quadratic f f , characterize f f for which this bound is tight, and show that such f f result in exposed coherent distributions with arbitrarily large support. As a corollary, we get a tight bound on cov ⁡ ( X 1 , X 2 ) \mathrm{cov}\,(X_{1},X_{2}) for p ∈ [ 1 / 3 , 2 / 3 ] p\in[1/3,\,2/3] . To obtain a tight bound on cov ⁡ ( X 1 , X 2 ) \mathrm{cov}\,(X_{1},X_{2}) for all p p , we develop an approach based on linear programming duality. Its generality is illustrated by tight bounds on 𝔼 ⁡ [ | X 1 − X 2 | α ] \mathbb{E}[|X_{1}-X_{2}|^{\alpha}] for any α > 0 \alpha>0 and p = 1 / 2 p=1/2 .

Introduction

A distribution
\mu
on the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
is called coherent if there exists a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, a Bernoulli random variable
X
defined on this space, and two subalgebras
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}\subset\mathcal{F}
such that
\mu
is a joint distribution of a random vector
(X_{1},X_{2})
,

where
{X_{i}=\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{F}_{i}]}
. We denote the class of all these distributions by
\mathcal{C}
and its subset corresponding to fixed expectation
\mathbb{E}[X]=p
by
\mathcal{C}_{p}
.

The set of coherent distributions
\mathcal{C}
admits the following interpretation. Two experts make predictions about a random variable
X
, which they do not observe directly. The information available to expert
i
is captured by a sigma-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{i}
. Accordingly,
X_{i}
is expert
i
’s best prediction for
X
given their information. Thus, the set
\mathcal{C}
is the set of all possible distributions of experts’ predictions, and the set
\mathcal{C}_{p}
corresponds to experts attributing a prior probability
p
to
\{X=1\}
.

The key question about coherent distributions — attracted substantial attention in probability and economic literature — is how different experts’ predictions can be.
This question boils down to understanding
\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{p}
maximizing
\int f(x_{1},x_{2})d\mu
, where
f
is a certain measure of discrepancy. This problem turns out to be challenging, and the optimal
\mu
is known for only a few particular objectives
f
, e.g.,

f=(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}
was analyzed by
(
Cichomski, 2020
;
Arieli, Babichenko, Sandomirskiy, and
Tamuz, 2020
)
and
f=-(x_{1}-1/2)(x_{2}-1/2)
corresponding to covariance minimization, by
(
Arieli, Babichenko, Sandomirskiy, and
Tamuz, 2020
)
for
p=1/2
.
In all the cases where the optimal
\mu
is known, it has a simple form and contains at most
4
points in its support.

Geometrically, maximization of
\int f(x_{1},x_{2})d\mu
over
\mathcal{C}_{p}
corresponds to maximizing a linear functional over a convex set
(
Dubins and Pitman, 1980
)
. By Bauer’s principle, the maximum is attained at an extreme point. Together with the simplicity of known solutions, this led to a conjecture that extreme points of
\mathcal{C}_{p}
are atomic distributions with at most
4
points in the support
(
Burdzy and Pitman, 2019
)
.
This conjecture was refuted by
Arieli, Babichenko, Sandomirskiy, and
Tamuz (2020)
and
T.Zhu (2022)
, who constructed extreme
\mu
with an arbitrarily large finite support;
Cichomski and
Osȩkowski (2023b)
demonstrated the existence of non-atomic extreme points.
Despite this progress in understanding extreme points of
\mathcal{C}_{p}
, no examples of objectives leading to non-elementary optima
\mu
are known; in particular, it is not known whether the constructed extreme points are exposed.

The existence of exposed points with non-atomic support remains an open question. While our first theorem demonstrates that exposed

Theorem 1.1
.

Let

f(x_{1},x_{2})=\alpha(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}+\beta(x_{1}+x_{2}-2p)^{2}.

If
\alpha<0
or
2\beta>\alpha
, then the following identity holds:

\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{p}}\int f(x_{1},x_{2})d\mu=\max\left(0,4\beta,\alpha+\beta\right)\cdot p(1-p).

Otherwise, if
\alpha\geqslant\max(0,2\beta)
, then the following bound holds:

\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{p}}\int f(x_{1},x_{2})d\mu\leq p(1-p)\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\alpha-\beta}.

Moreover, in this case, the equality is achieved if and only if there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that at least one of the following three conditions is satisfied:

1.

\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\alpha}=N
and
(2-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\beta})p+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\beta}\geqslant 1\geqslant(2-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\beta})p.

2.

\frac{2(\alpha-\beta)-(\alpha-2\beta)p}{\alpha}=N
and
(2-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\beta})p+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\beta}>1
.

3.

\frac{2(\alpha-\beta)-(\alpha-2\beta)(1-p)}{\alpha}=N
and
(2-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\beta})p<1
.

In the case of equality, the optimum is attained at a ladder distribution with
2N-1
points in the support if condition
1
does not hold, and at a convex combination of two such distributions if it does hold.

Theorem
1.1
relies on a Hilbert-space reinterpretation of the problem introduced by
(
Arieli, Babichenko, Sandomirskiy, and
Tamuz, 2020
;
Cichomski, 2020
)
. The idea is to interpret
X
as an element of
L_{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and
X_{i}
as its orthogonal projections. A quadratic
f
can be expressed through a scalar product, and thus the value of the optimization is bounded by a maximum of some functional expressed through scalar products of projections of
X
. Not all orthogonal projections of
X
correspond to conditional expectations, so this bound may not be tight. Theorem
1.1
exhaust all quadratic
f
for which this approach provides the exact optimum.

Theorem
1.1
gives us a tight lower bound on
\mathrm{cov}(X_{1},X_{2})
but not for all
p
. Indeed, for
f(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{4}(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(x_{1}+x_{2}-2p)^{2}=-(x_{1}-p)(x_{2}-p)
, maximizing
\int f(x_{1},x_{2})d\mu
corresponds to minimizing
\mathrm{cov}(X_{1},X_{2})
. The condition
1
from Theorem
1.1
simplifies to
{\frac{2}{3}\geqslant p\geqslant\frac{1}{3}}
, so the bound is tight only for such those
p
.

To find a tight lower bound on
\mathrm{cov}(X_{1},X_{2})
for
p
outside of
[1/3,\,2/3]
we develop another approach, based on interpreting the problem of maximizing
\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1},X_{2}]
as a linear program.
By the duality theorem, the value of the primal problem is bounded by the value of the dual.
Hence, the optimality of a candidate solution to the primal problem can be verified by constructing a candidate solution of the dual so that the values of the two objectives coincide.
We show how to construct a primal-dual pair of solutions and obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f(x_{1},x_{2})=-(x_{1}-p)(x_{2}-p)
. Then

\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{p}}\int f(x_{1},x_{2})d\mu=\min_{(X_{1},X_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{p}}(\mathrm{cov}(X_{1},X_{2}))=\begin{cases}-\left(\frac{p(1-p)}{1+p}\right)^{2}&p\in\left(0,\frac{1}{3}\right)\\
-\frac{1}{8}p(1-p)&\text{$p\in\left[\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}\right]$}\\
-\left(\frac{p(1-p)}{2-p}\right)^{2}&p\in\left(\frac{2}{3},1\right).\end{cases}

For notational convenience, we write
(X_{1},X_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{p}
, whenever we want to indicate, that the distribution of the random vector
(X_{1},X_{2})
belongs to
\mathcal{C}_{p}
.

Our linear programming duality method is not specific to the covariance or even quadratic objectives. We illustrate the generality by establishing a tight upper bound for
\mathbb{E}\{|X_{1}-X_{2}|^{\alpha}\}
. The bound for
\alpha\in(0,\alpha_{0}]
, where
\alpha_{0}=2.25751\dots
, was established by
Arieli, Babichenko, and
Sandomirskiy (2023)
as

\max_{(X_{1},X_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{{1}/{2}}}\mathbb{E}[|X_{1}-X_{2}|^{\alpha}]=2^{-\alpha}.

While we get a bound for all
\alpha>0
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
f(x_{1},x_{2})=|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}
where
\alpha>0
and
p=1/2
. Then

\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{\frac{1}{2}}}\int f(x_{1},x_{2})d\mu=\begin{cases}2^{-\alpha}&\text{ for }\alpha<\alpha_{0}\\
2\cdot\left(\frac{3\alpha+1-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{2\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\frac{-(\alpha+1)+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{\alpha-1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}&\text{ for }\alpha\geqslant\alpha_{0},\end{cases}

where
\alpha_{0}=2.25751\dots
is the point, at which the function
2\cdot\left(\frac{3\alpha+1-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{2\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\frac{-(\alpha+1)+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{\alpha-1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}
equals
2^{-\alpha}
.

The asymptotic behaviour of
\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{\frac{1}{2}}}\int|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}d\mu
was studied by
Cichomski and
Osȩkowski (2023a)
, Whose approach is more combinatorial. For large
\alpha
, the expression in Theorem
1.3
simplifies (see Lemma
3.9
for details), and we obtain
\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{\frac{1}{2}}}\int|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}d\mu\simeq\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{2}{e}
as
\alpha\to\infty
, recovering the result of
Cichomski and
Osȩkowski (2023a)

Besides, Theorem
1.3
provides a comprehensive answer to the open question formulated by
Burdzy and Pitman (2019)
, who asked what is a maximum of
\mathbb{E}[|X_{1}-X_{2}|^{\alpha}]
for
({X_{1},X_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{1/2}}
.

2
Hilbert space approach and proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section, we show how to bound
\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha(X_{1}-X_{2})^{2}+\beta(X_{1}+X_{2}-2p)^{2}\right]
for coherent random vectors
(X_{1},X_{2})

via a Hilbert space approach, study when the resulting bound is tight and obtain Theorem
1.1
as a corollary.

The idea behind the Hilbert space approach originated in
(
Cichomski, 2020
;
Arieli, Babichenko, Sandomirskiy, and
Tamuz, 2020
)
and is as follows.
Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, and
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{L}_{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be the corresponding Hilbert space with the inner product
{\langle Y,Z\rangle=\mathbb{E}[YZ]}.
Let
X
be a Bernoulli random variable with the success probability
p
, defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, where
p
is a fixed parameter. Suppose
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}
are sub sigma algebras of the sigma algebra
\mathcal{F}
, then we define random variables
X_{1},X_{2}
as
{X_{i}=\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{F}_{i}]}
.

Initially, we center random variables
X,X_{1},X_{2}
. Let
{\hat{X}=X-p}
and
{\hat{X}_{i}=X_{i}-p=\mathbb{E}[\hat{X}|\mathcal{F}_{i}]}
. Then we consider them as vectors in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Random variables
\hat{X}_{i}
are expectations of the random variable
X
, so, as vectors,
\hat{X}_{i}
are projections of
\hat{X}
, which means that the angle between vectors
{\hat{X}_{i}}
and
{\hat{X}-\hat{X}_{i}}
equals
\frac{\pi}{2}
. So the end of the vector
\hat{X}_{i}
lies on a sphere with diameter
\hat{X}
. This leads to the following observation.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{L}_{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and
S\subset\mathcal{H}
be a sphere with diameter
X
. Then

\max_{(X_{1},X_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{p}}\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha(X_{1}-X_{2})^{2}+\beta(X_{1}+X_{2}-2p)^{2}\right]\leqslant\sup_{\hat{X}_{1},\hat{X}_{2}\in S}\left(\alpha\|\hat{X}_{1}-\hat{X}_{2}\|^{2}+\beta\|\hat{X}_{1}+\hat{X}_{2}\|^{2}\right).

The expression
\alpha\|\hat{X}_{1}-\hat{X}_{2}\|^{2}+\beta\|\hat{X}_{1}+\hat{X}_{2}\|^{2}
depends only on the mutual arrangement of vectors
\hat{X},\hat{X}_{1},\hat{X}_{2}
, so it is sufficient to consider only a sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the maximum instead of the supremum.

Lemma 2.1

(
Cichomski (2020)
;
Arieli, Babichenko, Sandomirskiy, and
Tamuz (2020)
)
.

Let
{S_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}}
be a sphere with diameter
\|X\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\sqrt{p(1-p)}
and containing the point
0
. Then

\max_{(X_{1},X_{2})\in\mathfrak{F}}\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha(X_{1}-X_{2})^{2}+\beta(X_{1}+X_{2}-2p)^{2}\right]\leqslant\max_{x_{1},x_{2}\in S_{2}}\left(\alpha\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}+\beta\|x_{1}+x_{2}\|^{2}\right).

We prove Lemma
2.1
in Appendix
D
for the reader’s convenience. Our goal is to characterize when the bound from Lemma
2.1
is tight.

First, we calculate the expression
\displaystyle\max_{x_{1},x_{2}\in S_{2}}\left(\alpha\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}+\beta\|x_{1}+x_{2}\|^{2}\right)
via the standard Lagrange multipliers method, obtaining the following lemma proved in Appendix
E
:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
S_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be the sphere built on a vector
w
as a diameter. Then

\max_{x_{1},x_{2}\in S_{2}}\left(\alpha\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}+\beta\|x_{1}+x_{2}\|^{2}\right)=\begin{cases}\|w\|^{2}\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\alpha-\beta}&\text{if }\alpha\geqslant\max(0,2\beta)\\
\|w\|^{2}\cdot\max\left(0,4\beta,\alpha+\beta\right)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Moreover, in the case
\alpha>0\geqslant\beta
, the value
\|w\|^{2}\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\alpha-\beta}
is reached on those and only those pairs of vectors
x_{1},x_{2}\in S
such that
x_{1}+x_{2}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\beta}w
.

Combining this lemma with lemma
2.1
, we obtain the following upper bound

on
\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha(X_{1}-X_{2})^{2}+\beta(X_{1}+X_{2}-2p)^{2}\right]
.

Corollary 2.3
.

The following identity holds

\max_{(X_{1},X_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{p}}\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha(X_{1}-X_{2})^{2}+\beta(X_{1}+X_{2}-2p)^{2}\right]\leqslant\begin{cases}\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\alpha-\beta}\cdot p(1-p)&\text{if }\alpha\geqslant\max(0,2\beta)\\
\max\left(0,4\beta,\alpha+\beta\right)\cdot p(1-p)&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

Moreover, in the case
\alpha\geqslant\max(0,2\beta)
, the value
\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\alpha-\beta}\cdot p(1-p)
is reached on those and only those pairs of random variables
X_{1},X_{2}
, such that
(X_{1}-p)+(X_{2}-p)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\beta}(X-p)
.

This method provides us with an upper bound but does not say anything about whether this bound is tight or not. The values
0,4\beta p(1-p),(\alpha+\beta)\cdot p(1-p)
can be easily achieved:

•

Let
X_{1}=X_{2}=\mathbb{E}[X]=p
. Then
\mathbb{E}\big[\alpha(X_{1}-X_{2})^{2}+\beta(X_{1}+X_{2}-2p)^{2}\big]=0
.

•

Let
X_{1}=X_{2}=X
. Then
\mathbb{E}\big[\alpha(X_{1}-X_{2})^{2}+\beta(X_{1}+X_{2}-2p)^{2}\big]=4\beta p(1-p)
.

•

Let
X_{1}=X,X_{2}=\mathbb{E}[X]=p
. Then
\mathbb{E}\big[\alpha(X_{1}-X_{2})^{2}+\beta(X_{1}+X_{2}-2p)^{2}\big]=(\alpha+\beta)\cdot p(1-p)
.

Thus, this bound is tight if the condition
\alpha>\max(0,2\beta)
is not satisfied. The case
\alpha>\max(0,2\beta)
is more challenging. In this case, the bound is tight if and only if there exists a pair of random variables
(X_{1},X_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{p}
such that
(X_{1}-p)+(X_{2}-p)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\beta}(X-p)
. Let’s consider this situation more closely.

We start with a simple proposition.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
A
be a Borel subset of
\mathbb{R}
. If
A\subset(0,+\infty)
, then

\mathbb{P}\Big(X_{1}\in A,X=1\Big)=0\Longrightarrow\mathbb{P}\Big(X_{1}\in A,X=0\Big)=0,

and if
A\subset(-\infty,1)
, then

\mathbb{P}\Big(X_{1}\in A,X=0\Big)=0\Longrightarrow\mathbb{P}\Big(X_{1}\in A,X=1\Big)=0.

Proof.

We will prove only the first statement; the proof of the second is identical and thus omitted.
To begin, note that
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in A,X=1)=\mathbb{E}[X\mathds{1}_{X_{1}\in A}]
. The random variable
\mathds{1}_{X_{1}\in A}
is measurable with respect to the sigma algebra
\mathcal{F}_{1}
, so

\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in A,X=1)=\mathbb{E}[X\cdot\mathds{1}_{X_{1}\in A}]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\cdot\mathds{1}_{X_{1}\in A}|\mathcal{F}_{1}]\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{F}_{1}]\mathds{1}_{X_{1}\in A}\big]=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}\mathds{1}_{X_{1}\in A}].

If
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in A,X=1)=0
then
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}\mathds{1}_{X_{1}\in A}]=0
, so
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in A)=0
. On the other hand,
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in A,X=0)\leqslant\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=0)=0
.
∎

Let
a
be a positive real number and there exist coherent random variables
(X_{1},X_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{p}
, such that
(X_{1}-p)+(X_{2}-p)=a(X-p)
, then
X_{1}+X_{2}=aX+(2-a)p
. This means that if
X=0
, the distribution of
(X_{1},X_{2})
is concentrated on a line
X_{1}+X_{2}=(2-a)p
and if
X=1
, the distribution of
(X_{1},X_{2})
is concentrated on a line
X_{1}+X_{2}=a+(2-a)p
. The following proposition states that if we find set
I
, such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in I,X=1)=0
, then sets
I+ka
for all integer
k\geqslant 0
have the same property.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
I
be a Borel subset of a half-infinite interval
\Big(\max\big(0,(2-a)p-1\big),+\infty\Big)
, such that

\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in I,X=1)=0.

Then

\mathbb{P}\Big(X_{1}\in(I+ka),X=1\Big)=0

for all integer
k\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

We prove this proposition via induction on
k
. The base for
k=0
is given in the statement, so we only have to discuss the induction step. Let
\mathbb{P}\big(X_{1}\in(I+ka),X=1\big)=0
, then by Proposition
2.4
, we have

\displaystyle 0=\mathbb{P}\Big(X_{1}\in(I+ka),X=0\Big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(X_{2}\in\big((2-a)p-(I+ka)\big),X=0\Big).

From the statement of the proposition we have
I>(2-a)p-1
, so
(2-a)p-I<1
and we can apply the Proposition
2.4
again.

0=\mathbb{P}\Big(X_{2}\in\left((2-a)p-(I+ka)\right),X=1\Big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(X_{1}\in\big(I+(k+1)a\big),X=1\Big).

∎

Thus, we want to find a set
I
, such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in I,X=1)=0
. Generally, there are three cases of how lines
X_{1}+X_{2}=a+(2-a)p
and
X_{1}+X_{2}=(2-a)p
are arranged.

•

The first case: both lines
X_{1}+X_{2}=(2-a)p
and
X_{1}+X_{2}=a+(2-a)p
are above the line
X_{1}+X_{2}=1
. This condition is equivalent to
(2-a)p-1>0
.

We have
X_{2}\leqslant 1
almost surely, so

0=P\Big(X_{2}\in(1,a+1),X=1\Big)=P\Big(X_{1}\in\big((2-a)p-1,(2-a)p-1+a\big),X=1\Big).

Then if we let
I_{k}=\Big((2-a)p-1,(2-a)p-1+a\Big)
, by the Proposition
2.5
, we obtain
{P(X_{1}\in I_{k},X=1)}
and, by the Proposition
2.4
,
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in I_{k},X=0)=0.

Also, note that

0=\mathbb{P}\big(X_{2}=1+a,X=1\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(X_{1}=(2-a)p-1,X=1\big)

and, by the Proposition
2.4
,
\mathbb{P}\big(X_{1}=(2-a)p-1,X=0\big)=0
.

Thus we proved that distribution
\mu
of
(X_{1},X_{2})
is concentrated on points of the

form
{\Big(ka+(2-a)p-1,1-(k-1)a\Big)}
for integer
k\geqslant 1
and
\Big(ka+(2-a)p-1,1-ka\Big)
for integer
k\geqslant 1
. Consider
k=\lceil\frac{2-(2-a)p}{a}\rceil

Theorem 2.6
.

Let

If
\alpha<0
or
2\beta>\alpha
, then the following identity holds:

\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{p}}\int f(x_{1},x_{2})d\mu=\max\left(0,4\beta,\alpha+\beta\right)\cdot p(1-p).

Otherwise, if
\alpha\geqslant\max(0,2\beta)
, then the following bound holds:

\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{p}}\int f(x_{1},x_{2})d\mu\leq p(1-p)\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\alpha-\beta}.

Moreover, in this case, the equality is achieved if and only if there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that at least one of the following three conditions is satisfied:

1.

\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\alpha}=N
and
(2-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\beta})p+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\beta}\geqslant 1\geqslant(2-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\beta})p.

2.

\frac{2(\alpha-\beta)-(\alpha-2\beta)p}{\alpha}=N
and
(2-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\beta})p+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\beta}>1
.

3.

\frac{2(\alpha-\beta)-(\alpha-2\beta)(1-p)}{\alpha}=N
and
(2-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\beta})p<1
.

In the case of equality, the optimum is attained at a ladder distribution with
2N-1
points in the support if condition
1
does not hold, and at a convex combination of two such distributions if it does hold.

A lower bound for
\mathrm{cov}(X_{1},X_{2})

was found in

. In both papers, authors rely on a version of the Hilbert space approach

1.1
extends the result to

and provides the optimal distribution without guessing.

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
p
be a real number in
(0,1)
. If
p
lies in the interval
[\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}]
, then

\min_{(X_{1},X_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{p}}\mathrm{cov}(X_{1},X_{2})=-\frac{1}{8}p(1-p).

If
p
lies outside the interval
[\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}]
, then the strict inequality holds:

\min_{(X_{1},X_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{p}}\mathrm{cov}(X_{1},X_{2})>-\frac{1}{8}p(1-p).

Proof.

Rewrite
\mathrm{cov}(X_{1},X_{2})
as a quadratic function of
X_{1},X_{2}

\mathrm{cov}(X_{1},X_{2})=\mathbb{E}\big\{(X_{1}-p)(X_{2}-p)\big\}=-\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\big\{(X_{1}-X_{2})^{2}-(X_{1}+X_{2}-2p)^{2}\big\}.

Then we substitute

.
\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\alpha}=2\in\mathbb{N}

\frac{-\beta}{\alpha-\beta}\leqslant\frac{\alpha-2\beta}{\alpha-\beta}p\leqslant 1
. This means that

, which is
\frac{1}{3}\leqslant p\leqslant\frac{2}{3}.

3
Linear programming approach

The Hilbert space approach is only applicable to quadratic functionals, yet even for these cases, it provides a tight bound only for a limited range of parameters. To find a tight bound in other situations, we need to use other techniques. This section discusses how linear programming duality helps us with our problem.

We rely on the basic finite-dimensional duality. The following lemma shows that our infinite-dimensional optimization problem can be approximated with a finite-dimensional optimization problem by reducing to only finite subalgebras. An algebra is called finite if it contains only a finite number of sets. We prove the following lemma in Appendix
F
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
X
be a bounded random variable defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. For subalgebras
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}
of algebra
\mathcal{F}
, we define random variables
X_{i}=\mathbb{E}\left[X|\mathcal{F}_{i}\right]
for
i=1,2
. Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a set of all subalgebras of
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathfrak{B}
be a set of all finite subalgebras of
\mathcal{F}
.

If a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is Lipschitz

1
1

1

For the sake of definiteness, we consider the regular Euclidean metric in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\|(x,y)\|=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
.
, then

\sup_{\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}\in\mathfrak{A}}\mathbb{E}f(X_{1},X_{2})=\sup_{\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}\in\mathfrak{B}}\mathbb{E}f(X_{1},X_{2}).

This lemma says that it is enough to find the upper bound of
\mathbb{E}f(X_{1},X_{2})
only for atomic distributions of
(X_{1},X_{2})
with a finite number of atoms. Let

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1},X_{2}\ |X=1\right)=\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\alpha_{i,j}\delta_{\{X_{1}=a_{i},X_{2}=a_{j}\}}\\
&\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1},X_{2}\ |X=0\right)=\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\beta_{i,j}\delta_{\{X_{1}=a_{i},X_{2}=a_{j}\}},\end{split}

where
\delta
is the Dirac measure. Without loss of generality, for every
i
, there is at least one nonzero value among
\alpha_{i,j}
and
\beta_{i,j}
. Then, by Bayes’ theorem, we have:

\begin{split}&a_{i}=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1}=a_{i}|\ X=1\right)=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1}=a_{i},X=1\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1}=a_{i}\right)}=\frac{p\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i,j}}{p\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i,j}+(1-p)\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{i,j}},\\
&a_{j}=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{2}=a_{j}|\ X=1\right)=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X_{2}=a_{j},X=1\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(X_{2}=a_{j}\right)}=\frac{p\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i,j}}{p\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i,j}+(1-p)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i,j}}.\end{split}

These equations can be expressed as linear constraints on
\alpha_{i,j}
and
\beta_{i,j}
.

\begin{split}&(1-a_{i})p\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i,j}-a_{i}(1-p)\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{i,j}=0,\\
&(1-a_{j})p\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i,j}-a_{j}(1-p)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i,j}=0.\end{split}

(1)

Note that constraints
1
are satisfied even if, for some
i
, all
\alpha_{i,j}
and
\beta_{i,j}
are zero for every
j
.
The only remaining constraints on
\alpha
and
\beta
are as follows:

\begin{split}\alpha_{i,j}&\geqslant 0,\\
\beta_{i,j}&\geqslant 0,\\
\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\alpha_{i,j}&=1,\\
\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\beta_{i,j}&=1.\end{split}

(2)

The objective function can also be rewritten as a linear function of
\alpha_{i,j},\beta_{i,j}
:

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1},X_{2})]=\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}f(a_{i},a_{j})(p\alpha_{i,j}+(1-p)\beta_{i,j}).\end{split}

(3)

Now, we can solve this linear program numerically and find a distribution at which the objective function is very close to its optimal value. By the strong duality theorem, the optimal value of the objective function in the primal linear program equals the optimal value of the objective function in the dual problem. So, to prove that some value of the objective function in the primal linear program is optimal, it is enough to identify variables in the dual linear program such that the dual objective achieves identical value.

Let us write down the dual problem. The
2n
constraints of the form (
1
) turn into
2n
variables
\mu_{i},\nu_{i}
. Constraints (
1
) are equations, so
\mu_{i},\nu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. Constraints of the form (
2
) become variables
\gamma,\delta\in\mathbb{R}
. Variables
\alpha_{i,j},\beta_{i,j}
turn into constraints.

\begin{split}\mu_{i}(1-a_{i})p+\nu_{j}(1-a_{j})p+\gamma&\geqslant pf(a_{i},a_{j}),\\
-\mu_{i}a_{i}(1-p)-\nu_{i}a_{j}(1-p)+\delta&\geqslant(1-p)f(a_{i},a_{j}).\end{split}

(4)

And the objective function becomes
\gamma+\delta\to\min.

Note that conditions (
4
) can be rewritten as:

\begin{split}&\gamma\geqslant p\cdot\max_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\Big\{f(a_{i},a_{j})-\mu_{i}(1-a_{i})-\nu_{j}(1-a_{j})\Big\},\\
&\delta\geqslant(1-p)\max_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\Big\{f(a_{i},a_{j})+\mu_{i}a_{i}+\nu_{j}a_{j}\Big\}.\end{split}

So the dual linear program takes the following form.

Problem 3.2
.

For a given number
p\in[0,1]
, a function
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, and a sequence
(a_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, minimize the expression

p\cdot\max_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\Big\{f(a_{i},a_{j})-\mu_{i}(1-a_{i})-\nu_{j}(1-a_{j})\Big\}+(1-p)\max_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\Big\{f(a_{i},a_{j})+\mu_{i}a_{i}+\nu_{j}a_{j}\Big\}

over all sequences
(\mu_{i})_{i=1}^{n},(\nu_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
of real numbers.

To find
\displaystyle\sup_{(X_{1},X_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{p}}\mathbb{E}f(X_{1},X_{2})
, we want to find the supremum among the minimum values. Consider the following problem:

Problem 3.3
.

For a given number
p\in[0,1]
and a function
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, maximize the expression,

\min_{(\mu_{i})_{i=1}^{n},(\nu_{i})_{i=1}^{n}}\Bigg\{p\cdot\max_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\Big\{f(a_{i},a_{j})-\mu_{i}(1-a_{i})-\nu_{j}(1-a_{j})\Big\}+(1-p)\max_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\Big\{f(a_{i},a_{j})+\mu_{i}a_{i}+\nu_{j}a_{j}\Big\}\Bigg\}

over all numbers
n\in\mathbb{N}
and sequences
(a_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
of numbers in
[0,1]
. We call the supremum obtained from this maximization problem the value of the problem.

Thus, combining strong duality theorem and Lemma
3.1
we obtain the following lemma:

Lemma 3.4
.

The value of Problem
3.3
equals
\displaystyle\sup_{(X_{1},X_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{p}}\mathbb{E}f(X_{1},X_{2})
.

We will find
\mu_{i}
and
\nu_{i}
as functions of
a_{i}
, so consider the continuous analogue of the problem
3.3
:

Problem 3.5
.

For a given number
p\in[0,1]
and function
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, minimize the expression

p\cdot\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\Big\{f(x,y)-g(x)(1-x)-h(y)(1-y)\Big\}+(1-p)\cdot\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\Big\{f(x,y)+g(x)x+h(y)y\Big\}

(5)

over all functions

. We call the infimum obtained from this minimization problem the value of the problem.

The following lemma states that the values of Problem
3.3
and Problem
3.5

G

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
f
be a continuous function, then the value of Problem
3.3
equals the value of Problem
3.5

As a result, the value of Problem
3.5
equals to the
\displaystyle\sup_{(X_{1},X_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{p}}\mathbb{E}f(X_{1},X_{2})

Then we introduce two simplifications of this problem which are proven in Appendices

Lemma 3.7
.

If

Let

\displaystyle\gamma\geqslant-(x-p)(y-p)-g(x)(1-x)-g(y)(1-y)

{\gamma\geqslant 2\delta+p^{2}+2p-3(\delta+p^{2})^{\frac{2}{3}}},

so we let
\gamma=2\delta+p^{2}+2p-3(\delta+p^{2})^{\frac{2}{3}}
. The simplest way to define function
g(x)
for
x\leqslant x_{0}
is to define it as a linear function. To satisfy (
7
), it should be a function
g(x)=x_{0}-p-\frac{x}{2}
. then for the function
g
to be continuous we let
x_{0}=\sqrt{2(\delta+p^{2})}
. Then the problem turns into the problem of minimizing
p\gamma+(1-p)\delta
that is a function of one variable
\delta
. The minimum reaches for
\delta=\left(\frac{2p}{p+1}\right)^{3}-p^{2}
. Eventually,
p\gamma+(1-p)\delta=-\left(\frac{p(1-p)}{p+1}\right)^{2}
. Proof that such function
g
satisfies the inequalities (
6
),(
7
) is a technical routine.

To obtain the lower bound for
p\in[\frac{2}{3},1)
, we notice that
{\mathrm{cov}(X_{1},X_{2})=\mathrm{cov}(1-X_{1},1-X_{2})}
, and
1-X_{i}
are conditional expectations of the random variable
1-X
with mean
{1-p\in(0,\frac{1}{3}]}
. Therefore, for
p\in[\frac{2}{3},1)
, the lower bound on
\mathrm{cov}(X_{1},X_{2})
is
-\left(\frac{p(1-p)}{2-p}\right)^{2}
.

3.2
Tight bound on
\mathbb{E}|X_{1}-X_{2}|^{\alpha}

The problem of finding a tight upper bound for
\mathbb{E}\{|X_{1}-X_{2}|^{\alpha}\}
for
\alpha>0
was initially stated in
(
Burdzy and Pitman, 2019
)
, and it was solved there for
\alpha=1
using the bound for
\mathbb{E}\max(X_{1},X_{2})
. Then, it was independently solved in
(
Arieli, Babichenko, Sandomirskiy, and
Tamuz, 2020
)
and
(
Cichomski, 2020
)
for
\alpha\in(0,2]
using the Hilbert space approach. Lately, it was solved in
(
Arieli, Babichenko, and
Sandomirskiy, 2023
)
for
\alpha\in(0,\alpha_{0}]
, where
\alpha_{0}=2.25751\dots
using linear programming duality. In these cases, the upper bound is
2^{-\alpha}
, but, as stated in
(
Cichomski and
Osȩkowski, 2023a
)
, the asymptotic for
\alpha\to\infty
differs a lot. This section establishes a tight upper bound for all
\alpha\in[0,+\infty)
.

Theorem
1.3
.

Let
f(x_{1},x_{2})=|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}
where
\alpha>0
and
p=1/2
. Then

\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{\frac{1}{2}}}\int f(x_{1},x_{2})d\mu=\begin{cases}2^{-\alpha}&\text{ for }\alpha<\alpha_{0}\\
2\cdot\left(\frac{3\alpha+1-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{2\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\frac{-(\alpha+1)+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{\alpha-1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}&\text{ for }\alpha\geqslant\alpha_{0},\end{cases}

where
\alpha_{0}=2.25751\dots
is the point, at which the function
2\cdot\left(\frac{3\alpha+1-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{2\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\frac{-(\alpha+1)+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{\alpha-1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}
equals
2^{-\alpha}
.

3.2.1
Optimal distribution

Fix
p=\frac{1}{2}
. There are two candidates for optimal distribution.

The first candidate is a trivial distribution
X_{1}=X,X_{2}=\frac{1}{2}
. In this case
{\mathbb{E}\big[|X_{1}-X_{2}|^{\alpha}\big]=2^{-\alpha}}
.
This distribution is known to be optimal for
\alpha\leqslant\alpha_{0}
.

To guess the optimal distribution for big
\alpha
we rely on numerical simulations. We guessed it via solving the primal linear program numerically for various
\alpha
and found that the optimal distribution of
(X,X_{1},X_{2})
usually concentrates on six points
(1,a,1),(1,1,a),(1,1-a,1-a),(0,0,1-a),(0,1-a,0),(0,a,a)
for some
a
. Random variables
X_{1},X_{2}
are conditional expectations of
X
, so, by Bayes’ theorem, this distribution should be as follows

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(X=1,X_{1}=a,X_{2}=1\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(X=1,X_{1}=1,X_{2}=a\right)=\frac{a}{2(a+1)}\\
&\mathbb{P}\left(X=1,X_{1}=1-a,X_{2}=1-a\right)=\frac{1-a}{2(a+1)}\\
&\mathbb{P}\left(X=0,X_{1}=0,X_{2}=1-a\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(X=0,X_{1}=1-a,X_{2}=0\right)=\frac{a}{2(a+1)}\\
&\mathbb{P}\left(X=0,X_{1}=a,X_{2}=a\right)=\frac{1-a}{2(a+1)}.\end{split}

(8)

It is easy to check that
X_{i}
defined as in (
8
) are conditional expectations of
X
for all
{a\in[0,1]}
.
For this distribution,
\mathbb{E}|X_{1}-X_{2}|=(1-a)^{\alpha}\cdot\frac{2a}{a+1}
, so it is only left to maximize this expression over
a
. The optimal value is
a=\frac{-(\alpha+1)+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{2\alpha}
, and for this
a
we have

\mathbb{E}\big[|X_{1}-X_{2}|^{\alpha}\big]=2\cdot\left(\frac{3\alpha+1-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{2\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\frac{-(\alpha+1)+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{\alpha-1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}.

(9)

Note that
2\cdot\left(\frac{3\alpha+1-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{2\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\frac{-(\alpha+1)+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{\alpha-1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}\geqslant 2^{-\alpha}
exactly for
\alpha\geqslant\alpha_{0}
, so we claim that this distribution is optimal for big
\alpha\geqslant\alpha_{0}
.

3.2.2
Upper bound

We check that one of the two distributions described in the previous subsection gives the optimal value to
\mathbb{E}\big[|X_{1}-X_{2}|^{\alpha}\big]
, depending on the value of
\alpha
.

Let
\mathrm{opt}(\alpha)
be defined as follows:

\mathrm{opt}(\alpha)=\begin{cases}2^{-\alpha}&\text{ for }\alpha<\alpha_{0},\\
2\cdot\left(\frac{3\alpha+1-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{2\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\frac{-(\alpha+1)+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{\alpha-1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}&\text{ for }\alpha\geqslant\alpha_{0}.\end{cases}

By Lemma
3.8
, to prove an upper bound, it is sufficient to find a function
s:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
such that
s(x)+s(1-x)=0
and
\forall x,y\in[0,1]

|x-y|^{\alpha}+s(x)x+s(y)y\leqslant\text{opt}(\alpha).

(10)

We rely on the heuristic that inequality (
10
) turns into equality for
x=0,y\geqslant y_{0}
. This means that for
y\geqslant y_{0}
,

y^{\alpha}+s(y)y=\text{opt}(\alpha),

so

s(y)=\frac{\text{opt}(\alpha)-y^{\alpha}}{y}.

We are looking for a function
s(x)
under the condition
s(x)+s(1-x)=0
, so for
x\leqslant 1-y_{0}
, we have

s(x)=\frac{(1-x)^{\alpha}-\text{opt}(\alpha)}{1-x}.

Then for
y\geqslant y_{0}\geqslant 1-y_{0}\geqslant x
(
10
) turns into

(y-x)^{\alpha}+x\frac{\text{opt}(\alpha)-(1-x)^{\alpha}}{1-x}-y^{\alpha}\leqslant 0.

(11)

For
x=0
, the LHS equals
0
, so its derivative with respect to
x
should be non-positive at the point
x=0
, thus

-\alpha y^{\alpha-1}+1-\text{opt}(\alpha)\leqslant 0.

The function
-\alpha y^{\alpha-1}+1-\text{opt}(\alpha)
monotonically decreases for
y
, so we obtain a lower bound

y_{0}\geqslant\left(\frac{1-\text{opt}(\alpha)}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}

and we let

y_{0}=\max\left(\left(\frac{1-\text{opt}(\alpha)}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}},\frac{1}{2}\right).

For
x,y\leqslant 1-y_{0}
, we should check the inequality

(y-x)^{\alpha}+x\cdot\frac{(1-x)^{\alpha}-\text{opt}(\alpha)}{1-x}+y\cdot\frac{(1-y)^{\alpha}-\text{opt}(\alpha)}{1-y}\leqslant\text{opt}(\alpha).

For
x=y
, it turns into

\frac{2x\cdot(1-x)^{\alpha}}{1+x}\leqslant\text{opt}(\alpha).

(12)

The function
\frac{2x\cdot(1-x)^{\alpha}}{1+x}
has a maximum for
x\in[0,1]
at the point
x_{0}
such that

0=\left(\frac{2x\cdot(1-x)^{\alpha}}{1+x}\right)^{\prime}\Bigg|_{x=x_{0}}=-\frac{2x_{0}(1-x_{0})^{\alpha-1}(\alpha x_{0}^{2}+\alpha x_{0}+x_{0}-1)}{(x+1)^{2}},

so
\alpha x_{0}^{2}+(\alpha+1)x_{0}-1=0
. Solving the quadratic equation, we get
x_{0}=\frac{-(\alpha+1)+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{2\alpha}
. Then (
12
) turns into

2\cdot\left(\frac{3\alpha+1-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{2\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\frac{-(\alpha+1)+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{\alpha-1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}\leqslant\text{opt}(\alpha),

The only difference between cases
\alpha<\alpha_{0}
and
\alpha\geqslant\alpha_{0}
is that for the case
\alpha\geqslant\alpha_{0}
the inequality (
12
) turns into equality for
x=y=x_{0}
, while for
\alpha<\alpha_{0}
it remains an inequality.

All we have to do is extend the function
s(x)
to
x\in(1-y_{0},y_{0})
, and we set it equal to
0
there. Thus we obtain the function

s(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{(1-x)^{\alpha}-\text{opt}(\alpha)}{1-x}&x\leqslant\min(1-(1-\text{opt}(\alpha))^{\alpha-1},\frac{1}{2}),\\
\frac{\text{opt}(\alpha)-x^{\alpha}}{x}&x\geqslant\max((1-\text{opt}(\alpha))^{\alpha-1},\frac{1}{2}),\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proving that this function
s(x)
satisfies the inequality (
10
) is routine. We checked it via a Mathematica code, presented in Appendix
K
.

3.3
Asymptotic behavior of
\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{\frac{1}{2}}}\int|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}d\mu
as
\alpha\to\infty
.

The asymptotic behavior of
\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{\frac{1}{2}}}\int|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}d\mu
was found by
Cichomski and
Osȩkowski (2023a)
as
\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{2}{e}
for large
\alpha
. Theorem
1.3
allows us to check this result by direct computation.

Lemma 3.9
.

Cichomski and
Osȩkowski (2023a)

The following identity holds:

\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\alpha\cdot\Big(\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{\frac{1}{2}}}\int|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}d\mu\Big)=\frac{2}{e}.

Proof.

By Theorem
1.3
, for
\alpha>2.25751\dots
we have

\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{\frac{1}{2}}}\int|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}d\mu=2\cdot\left(\frac{3\alpha+1-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{2\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\frac{-(\alpha+1)+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{\alpha-1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}.

(13)

To begin, we compute the limit of the second multiplier of the right side of (
13
). Note that

\begin{split}&\frac{3\alpha+1-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{2\alpha}=1+\frac{3\alpha+1-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{2\alpha}=\\
&1+\frac{-4\alpha}{2\alpha\cdot(\alpha+1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1})}=1-\frac{2}{\alpha+1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}.\end{split}

Where the expression
\frac{2}{\alpha+1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}
tends to
0
, as
\alpha
tends to
\infty
. So

\begin{split}\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\left(\frac{3\alpha+1-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{2\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}&=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{2}{\alpha+1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha+1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{2}\cdot\frac{2\alpha}{{\alpha+1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}}}=\\
&e^{-\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{2\alpha}{{\alpha+1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}}}=\frac{1}{e}.\end{split}

(14)

Then we compute the limit of the last multiplier of the right side of (
13
) multiplied by
\alpha

\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\alpha\cdot\frac{-(\alpha+1)+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}{\alpha-1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1}}=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{\alpha\cdot 4\alpha}{(\alpha-1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1)}\cdot(\alpha+1+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+6\alpha+1})}=1.

(15)

Combining the equality (
13
) with (
14
) and (
15
) we obtain

\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\alpha\cdot\Big(\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{\frac{1}{2}}}\int|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}d\mu\Big)=2\cdot\frac{1}{e}\cdot 1=\frac{2}{e}.

∎

\begin{split}&p\cdot\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\Big\{f(x,y)-g(x)(1-x)-h(y)(1-y)\Big\}+(1-p)\cdot\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\Big\{f(x,y)+g(x)x+h(y)y\Big\}\geqslant\\
&p\cdot\sup_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\Big\{f(a_{i},a_{j})-g(a_{i})(1-a_{i})-h(a_{j})(1-a_{j})\Big\}+(1-p)\cdot\sup_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\Big\{f(a_{i},a_{j})+g(a_{i})a_{i}+h(a_{j})a_{j}\Big\}.\end{split}

Thus,

V_{2}+\varepsilon\geqslant p\cdot\sup_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\Big\{f(a_{i},a_{j})-g(a_{i})(1-a_{i})-h(a_{j})(1-a_{j})\Big\}+(1-p)\cdot\sup_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\Big\{f(a_{i},a_{j})+g(a_{i})a_{i}+h(a_{j})a_{j}\Big\},

and we obtain that
V_{2}+\varepsilon\geqslant V_{1}
, so
V_{2}\geqslant V_{1}
. Now we aim to prove the opposite inequality.

The function
f
is continuous on the square
[0,1]^{2}
, so it is uniformly continuous. Fix
\varepsilon>0
, then there exists
\delta>0
, such that

\forall x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in[0,1]\ |x_{1}-x_{2}|+|y_{1}-y_{2}|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})|<\varepsilon.

Fix a sequence
(a_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
. Without loss of generality we may assume that there is a member of the sequence
(a_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
in every interval
{\left(x-\frac{\delta}{4},x+\frac{\delta}{4}\right)\subset[0,1]}
and that
0=a_{1}<a_{2}<\dots<a_{n}=1
.

Consider the function
g(x)
which equals
\mu_{i}
at the interval
[a_{i},a_{i+1})
and equals
\mu_{n}
at the point
1
. Analogously, construct the function
h(x)
by sequence
\nu_{i}
. Note that if
a_{i}\leqslant x<a_{i+1}
then
|x-a_{i}|<|a_{i}-a_{i+1}|\leqslant\frac{\delta}{2}
. So, if
a_{i}\leqslant x<a_{i+1}
and
a_{j}\leqslant y<a_{j+1}
, then we have

\begin{split}&V_{2}\leqslant p\Big(f(x,y)-g(x)(1-x)-h(y)(1-y)\Big)+(1-p)\Big(f(x,y)+g(x)x+g(y)y\Big)\leqslant\\
&p\Big(f(a_{i},a_{j})+\varepsilon-\mu_{i}(1-a_{i})-\nu_{j}(1-a_{j})\Big)+(1-p)\Big(f(a_{i},a_{j})+\varepsilon+\mu_{i}a_{i}+\nu_{j}a_{j}\Big).\end{split}

So
V_{2}\leqslant V_{1}+\varepsilon
, and consequently
V_{2}\leqslant V_{1}
. Thus
V_{2}=V_{1}
.

Proof of Lemma
3.8

Proof.

Lemma
3.7
states that the infimum over all functions
g,h:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
of

\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\Big\{f(x,y)-g(x)(1-x)-h(y)(1-y)\Big\}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\Big\{f(x,y)+g(x)x+h(y)y\Big\}

equals the infimum over only function
g,h
such that
g=h
. Let this infimum equals
A
.

Note that the sum of two supremums is not less then the supremum of the sum, so

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\Big\{f(x,y)+h(x)x+h(y)y\Big\}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\{f(x,y)-h(x)(1-x)-h(y)(1-y)\}\leqslant

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\Big\{\frac{1}{2}\cdot\Big(f(x,y)+h(x)x+h(y)y\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\Big(f(1-x,1-y)-h(1-x)x-h(1-y)y\Big)\Big\}.

If we denote
s(x)=\frac{1}{2}h(x)-\frac{1}{2}h(1-x)
, then
s(x)+s(1-x)=0
and we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\left\{p\Big(f(x,y)+h(x)x+h(y)y\Big)+(1-p)\Big(f(1-x,1-y)-h(1-x)x-h(1-y)y\Big)\right\}=

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\Big\{f(x,y)+s(x)x+s(y)y\Big\}.

Thus,

A\leqslant\inf_{s(x)+s(1-x)=0}\Bigg\{\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\Big\{f(x,y)+s(x)x+s(y)y\Big\}\Bigg\}.

On the other hand, if function
S
satisfies the condition
s(x)+s(1-x)=0
, then

\displaystyle A\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\Big\{f(x,y)-s(x)(1-x)-s(y)(1-y)\Big\}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\Big\{f(x,y)+s(x)x+s(y)y\Big\}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\Big\{f(x,y)+s(1-x)(1-x)+s(1-y)(1-y)\Big\}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\Big\{f(x,y)+s(x)x+s(y)y\Big\}=

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\Big\{f(x,y)+s(x)x+s(y)y\Big\}.

Thus,

A=\inf_{s(x)+s(1-x)=0}\Bigg\{\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\Big\{f(x,y)+s(x)x+s(y)y\Big\}\Bigg\}.

Proof of lower bound in section
3.1.2

Proof.

We have the following designations:

\begin{split}\delta&=\left(\frac{2p}{p+1}\right)^{3}-p^{2},\\
\gamma&=2\delta+p^{2}+2p-3(\delta+p^{2})^{\frac{2}{3}},\\
x_{0}&=\sqrt{2(\delta+p^{2})},\\
g(x)&=\frac{\delta+p^{2}}{x}-p\text{ for $x\geqslant x_{0}$},\\
g(x)&=x_{0}-p-\frac{x}{2}\text{ for $x\leqslant x_{0}$}.\end{split}

And we want to proof two following inequalities for
x,y\in[0,1]
:

\displaystyle\gamma\geqslant-(x-p)(y-p)-g(x)(1-x)-g(y)(1-y),

(21)

\displaystyle\delta\geqslant-(x-p)(y-p)+g(x)x+g(y)y.

(22)

Note that function
g(x)
is differentiable on
[0,1]
. The function
-(x-p)(y-p)
is also differentiable, so we have to check the inequalities (
21
), (
22
) only for
x,y\in\{0,1\}
and
x,y
such that derivatives are zeros.

First, we check (
22
).

•

For
x\geqslant x_{0}
, we have
\big(-(x-p)(y-p)+g(x)x\big)^{\prime}=-y
. Then for
y=0
the inequality (
22
) turns to

\delta\geqslant p(y-p)+\delta+p^{2}-py=\delta.

•

For
x\leqslant x_{0}
, we have
\big(-(x-p)(y-p)+g(x)x\big)^{\prime}=-(y-p)+(x_{0}-p)-x
, so
x+y=x_{0}
. Then the inequality (
22
) turns to

\delta\geqslant-xy-p^{2}+x_{0}(x+y)-\frac{x^{2}}{2}-\frac{y^{2}}{2}=x_{0}^{2}-\frac{x_{0}^{2}}{2}-p^{2}=\delta.

•

For
x=0,y=0
the inequality (
22
) turns to
\delta\geqslant-p^{2}
.

•

For
x=0,y=1
the inequality (
22
) turns to
\delta\geqslant p(1-p)+\delta+p^{2}-p=\delta
.

•

For
x=1,y=1
the inequality (
22
) turns to
\delta\geqslant-(1-p)^{2}+2\delta+2p^{2}-2p=\delta+\left(\frac{2p}{p+1}\right)^{3}-1
.

Then we check (
21
).

•

For
x\geqslant x_{0}
we have
\big(-(x-p)(y-p)-g(x)(1-x)\big)^{\prime}=\frac{\delta+p^{2}}{x^{2}}-y
.

•

For
x\leqslant x_{0}
we have
\big(-(x-p)(y-p)-g(x)(1-x)\big)^{\prime}=x_{0}+\frac{1}{2}-(x+y)
.

Thus if both partial derivatives are zeros, we have three cases:

•

If
x\geqslant x_{0},y\geqslant x_{0}
, then
x^{2}y=xy^{2}=\delta+p^{2}
. So

) turns to

\displaystyle\gamma\geqslant 2p+p^{2}+2\delta-xy-\frac{\delta+p^{2}}{x}-\frac{\delta+p^{2}}{y}=\gamma.

•

then we have
x+y=x_{0}+\frac{1}{2},x^{2}y=\delta+p^{2}
. Then the inequality (
21
) turns into

\left(\frac{2p}{p+1}\right)^{3}-3\left(\frac{2p}{p+1}\right)^{2}+\sqrt{2\left(\frac{2p}{p+1}\right)^{3}}\geqslant y\left(\frac{y}{2}-x\right).

(23)

If

, then
x_{0}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
. So

. This means that
\frac{y}{2}-x+xy\leqslant 0
, so we can bound
\frac{y^{2}}{2}-xy\leqslant-x^{2}y=-\left(\frac{2p}{p+1}\right)^{3}

\frac{2p}{p+1}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
, so

\displaystyle 2\left(\frac{2p}{p+1}\right)^{3}-3\left(\frac{2p}{p+1}\right)^{2}+\sqrt{2\left(\frac{2p}{p+1}\right)^{3}}\geqslant 2\left(\frac{2p}{p+1}\right)^{3}-3\left(\frac{2p}{p+1}\right)^{2}+\frac{2p}{p+1}=

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{2p}{p+1}\left(\frac{2p}{p+1}-1\right)\left(2\cdot\frac{2p}{p+1}-1)\right)\geqslant 0.

(25)

•

If
x,y<x_{0}
, then
x=y=\frac{x_{0}+\frac{1}{2}}{2}
. But
x_{0}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
, so
x=y\geqslant x_{0}
, which leads us to a contradiction.

•

For
x=1,y=1
the inequality (
21
) turns to
\gamma\geqslant-(1-p)^{2}
, what follows from

\displaystyle 0\leqslant 2\left(\frac{2p}{p+1}\right)^{3}-3\left(\frac{2p}{p+1}\right)^{2}+1=\left(\frac{2p}{p+1}-1\right)^{2}\cdot\left(2\left(\frac{2p}{p+1}\right)+1\right).

For

) turns to

. It can be rewritten as

what we have already proven in (

).

•

For
x=0,y=0
the inequality (

) turns to
\gamma\geqslant-p^{2}-2x_{0}+2p
