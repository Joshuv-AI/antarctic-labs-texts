WildWood: a new Random Forest algorithm
Stéphane Gaïffas, Ibrahim Merad, Yiyang Yu

Abstract
We introduce WildWood (WW), a new ensemble algorithm for supervised learning of Random Forest (RF) type. While standard RF algorithms use bootstrap out-of-bag samples to compute out-of-bag scores, WW uses these samples to produce improved predictions given by an aggregation of the predictions of all possible subtrees of each fully grown tree in the forest. This is achieved by aggregation with exponential weights computed over out-of-bag samples, that are computed exactly and very efficiently thanks to an algorithm called context tree weighting. This improvement, combined with a histogram strategy to accelerate split finding, makes WW fast and competitive compared with other well-established ensemble methods, such as standard RF and extreme gradient boosting algorithms.



1
Introduction

This paper introduces WildWood (WW), a new ensemble method of Random Forest (RF) type
(
Breiman 2001
)
.
The main contributions of the paper and the main advantages of WW are as follows.
Firstly, we use out-of-bag samples (trees in a RF use different bootstrapped samples) very differently than what is done in standard RF
(
Louppe 2014
;
Biau and
Scornet 2016
)
.
Indeed, WW uses these samples to compute an aggregation of the predictions of all possible subtrees of each tree in the forest, using aggregation with exponential weights
(
Catoni 2004
)
.
This leads to much improved predictions: while only leaves contribute to the predictions of a tree in standard RF, the full tree structure contributes to predictions in WW.
An illustration of this effect is given in Figure
1
on a toy binary classification example, where we can observe that subtrees aggregation leads to improved and regularized decision functions for each individual tree and for the forest.

Subtrees aggregation improves trees predictions, as illustrated by smoother decision functions in the top compared with the bottom, improving overall predictions of the forest (last column).

We further illustrate in Figure

that each tree becomes a stronger learner, and that excellent performance can be achieved even when WW uses few trees, granting better interpretability of the solutions as a side benefit.
Indeed, reducing the number of trees is a known way to obtain explainable models
(
Zhou

;
Johansson et al. 2011
)
.
A remarkable aspect of WW is that this improvement comes only at a small computational cost, thanks to a technique called “context tree weighting”, used in lossless compression or online learning to aggregate all subtrees of a given tree
(
Willems
et al. 1995
;
Willems 1998
;
Helmbold and
Schapire 1997
;
Catoni 2004
;
Mourtada
et al. 2021
)
.
Also, the predictions of WW do not rely on MCMC approximations required with Bayesian variants of RF
(
Chipman
et al. 1998
;
Denison
et al. 1998
;
Chipman
et al. 2010
;
Taddy
et al. 2011
)
, which is a clear distinction from such methods.

Secondly, WW uses feature binning (“histogram” strategy), similarly to extreme gradient boosting (EGB) libraries such as XGBoost
(
Chen and
Guestrin 2016
)
, LightGBM
(
Ke et al. 2017
)
and CatBoost
(

;
Dorogush

)
.
This strategy helps accelerate computations in WW compared with standard RF algorithms, that typically require to sort features locally in nodes and try a larger number of splits

)
.

Related works.

Tree based decision models appeared as a supervised learning tool in the 1960s
(
Morgan and
Sonquist 1963
;
Messenger and
Mandell 1972
;
Quinlan 1986
)
. Their use was promoted by the in-depth practical and theoretical study of
Breiman
et al. 1984
. The key idea of forming tree ensembles led to the well-known Random Forest algorithm
(
Ho 1995
;
Breiman 2001
)
which became one of the most popular supervised learning algorithms thanks to its ease of use, robustness to hyperparameters
(
Biau and
Scornet 2016
;
Probst
et al. 2019
)
and applicability to a wide range of domains. Recent examples include
predictive medicine
(
Subasi
et al. 2017
;
Alghatani et al. 2021
)
, intrusion detection
(
Chen
et al. 2021
)
, car safety
(
Tavakoli et al. 2021
)
, differential privacy
(
Patil and
Singh 2014
)
and COVID-19
(
She
et al. 2020
)
to cite but a few.

The RF algorithm sparked considerable interest and benefited from a rich literature of methodological developments
(
Geurts
et al. 2006
;
Criminisi
et al. 2012
;
Athey
et al. 2018
)
. In particular, RF was adapted for various tasks including the computation of prediction intervals
(
Roy and
Larocque 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Calviño 2020
)
, online learning
(
Lakshminarayanan et al. 2014
;
Mourtada
et al. 2021
)
and quantile regression
(
Meinshausen 2006
)
. A theoretical framework was progressively built around random forests to study their statistical properties such as consistency
(
Biau
et al. 2008
;
Biau 2012
;
Scornet
et al. 2015
)
, control over bias and variance
(
Arlot and
Genuer 2014
;
Genuer 2012
)
and feature importance assessment
(
Louppe
et al. 2013
;
Kazemitabar et al. 2017
)
. These works often consider purely random trees (splits are not optimized using training data) which are more amenable to theoretical analysis. More recent works introduced “honest” trees which are built using disjoint samples from those used for prediction
(
Athey and
Imbens 2016
)
. Honest forests were considered and proven consistent for causal inference tasks by
Wager and
Athey 2018
.

By essence, decision trees are universal approximators which lends them strong expressive power but also makes them prone to overfitting, hence the need for regularization. This was addressed in early works using pruning methods such as CCP, REP or MEP
(
Quinlan 1986
;
Breslow and
Aha 1997
)
. Although they are fairly effective at reducing tree overfitting, these methods are mostly based on heuristics so that little is known about their theoretical properties.
A form of soft-pruning was proposed by
Buntine 1992
and referred to as
tree smoothing
.
The latter efficiently computes predictions as approximate Bayesian posteriors over the set of possible prunings, however, the associated complexity is of the order of the tree-size, which makes the computation of predictions slow. An alternative form of regularization called “shrinkage” was proposed in
(
Hastie and
Pregibon 1990
;
Agarwal
et al. 2022
)
and consists in averaging predictions between leaf nodes and their ancestors. Although a parallel can be drawn between such methods and kernel Ridge regression, their theoretical support remains limited and does not allow to claim improved performance. Recent studies suggest that tree depth limitation and randomness of the tree building process bring an implicit regularization effect which improves performance on low signal-to-noise ratio datasets
(
Mentch and
Zhou 2020
;
Zhou and
Mentch 2021
)
.

In
(
Mourtada
et al. 2021
)
, an improvement of Mondrian Forests
(
Lakshminarayanan et al. 2014
)
is introduced for online learning, using subtrees aggregation with exponential weights, which is particularly convenient in the online learning setting.
However,
Mourtada
et al. 2021
considers only the online setting, with purely random trees, leading to poor performances compared with realistic decision trees.

Extreme boosting algorithms are another family of tree ensemble methods.
XGBoost
(
Chen and
Guestrin 2016
)
provides an extremely popular scalable tree boosting system which has been widely adopted in industry.
LightGBM
(
Ke et al. 2017
)
employed the “histogram strategy” for faster split finding, together with clever downsampling and features grouping algorithms in order to achieve high performance in reduced computation times.
CatBoost
(
Probst
et al. 2019
)
is another boosting library which pays particular attention to categorical features using target encoding, while addressing the potential bias issues associated to such an encoding.

In WW, we combine several ideas from the literature into a novel high performing RF algorithm.
We use a subtrees aggregation mechanism similar to
(
Mourtada
et al. 2021
)
for batch learning in a different way: we exploit the bootstrap, one of the key ingredients of RF, which provides in-the-bag and out-of-bag samples, to perform aggregation with exponential weights leading to considerably improved predictions. In addition, we employ the histogram strategy
(
Ke et al. 2017
)
in order to grow decision trees much more efficiently. Finally, we implement an improved split finding method along categorical features which makes for better predictions.

Limitations.

Our implementation of WW is still evolving and is not yet at the level of maturity of state-of-the-art EGB libraries such as
Chen and
Guestrin 2016
;
Ke et al. 2017
;
Probst
et al. 2019
.
It does not outperform such strong baselines, but proposes an improvement of RF algorithms, and gives an interesting balance between performance and computational efficiency.

2
WildWood: a new Random Forest algorithm

We consider batch supervised learning, where data comes as a set of i.i.d training samples
(x_{i},y_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
with vectors of numerical or categorical features
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
.
Our aim is to design a RF predictor
\hat{g}(\>\cdot\>;\boldsymbol{\Pi})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat{f}(\>\cdot\>;\Pi_{m}):\mathcal{X}\to\hat{\mathcal{Y}}

computed from training samples, where
\hat{\mathcal{Y}}
is the prediction space.
Such a RF computes the average of
M
randomized trees predictions
\hat{f}(\>\cdot\>;\Pi_{m})
following the principle of bagging
(
Breiman 1996
;
Oza and
Russell 2001
)
, with
\boldsymbol{\Pi}=(\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{M})
where
\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{M}
are i.i.d realizations of a random variable corresponding to bootstrap and feature subsampling (see Section
2.1
below).
Each tree is trained independently of each other, in parallel.
In what follows we describe only the construction of a single tree and omit from now on the dependence on
m=1,\ldots,M
.

Feature binning.

The split finding strategy described in Section
2.2
below works on binned features.
While this technique is of common practice in EGB libraries
(
Chen and
Guestrin 2016
;
Ke et al. 2017
;
Prokhorenkova et al. 2018
)
, we are not aware of an implementation of it for RF. Note that binning may affect predictive performance by making split thresholds less accurate. However, the experimental performance from EGB libraries (see also Section
4
) suggests that this effect is minor.
The input
n\times d
matrix
\boldsymbol{X}
of features is transformed into another same-size matrix of “binned” features denoted
\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{bin}}
.
To each input feature
j=1,\ldots,d
is associated a set
B_{j}=\{1,\ldots,b_{j}\}
of bins, where
b_{j}\leqslant b_{\max}
with
b_{\max}
a hyperparameter corresponding to the maximum number of bins a feature can use (default is
b_{\max}=256
similarly to
Ke et al. 2017
, so that a single byte can be used for entries of
\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{bin}}
).
When a feature is continuous, it is binned into
b_{\max}
bins using inter-quantile intervals.
If it is categorical, each modality is mapped to a bin whenever
b_{\max}
is larger than its number of modalities, otherwise sparsest modalities end up binned together.
If a feature
j
contains missing values, its rightmost bin in
B_{j}
is used to encode them
(in such case, later in split, we do not loop only left to right (along bin order), but right to left as well, in order to compare splits that put missing values on the left or on the right).
After binning, each column satisfies
\boldsymbol{X}_{\bullet,j}^{\text{bin}}\in B_{j}^{n}
.

2.1
Random decision trees

Let
C=\prod_{j=1}^{d}B_{j}
be the binned feature space.
A random decision tree is a pair
(\mathcal{T},\Sigma)
, where
\mathcal{T}
is a finite ordered binary tree and
\Sigma
contains information about each node in
\mathcal{T}
, such as split information.
The tree is random and its source of randomness
\Pi
comes from the bootstrap and feature subsampling as explained below.

Finite ordered binary trees.

A finite ordered binary tree
\mathcal{T}
is represented as a finite subset of the set
\{0,1\}^{*}=\bigcup_{n\geqslant 0}\{0,1\}^{n}
of all finite words on
\{0,1\}
.
The set
\{0,1\}^{*}
is endowed with a tree structure (and called the complete binary tree): the empty word
\mathtt{root}
is the root, and for any
\mathbf{v}\in\{0,1\}^{*}
, the left (resp. right) child of
\mathbf{v}
is
\mathbf{v}0
(resp.
\mathbf{v}1
).
We denote by
\mathrm{intnodes}(\mathcal{T})=\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{T}:\mathbf{v}0,\mathbf{v}1\in\mathcal{T}\}
the set of its interior nodes and by
\mathrm{leaves}(\mathcal{T})=\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{T}:\mathbf{v}0,\mathbf{v}1\not\in\mathcal{T}\}
the set of its leaves, both sets are disjoint and the set of all nodes is
\mathrm{nodes}(\mathcal{T})=\mathrm{intnodes}(\mathcal{T})\cup\mathrm{leaves}(\mathcal{T})
.

Splits and cells.

The split
\sigma_{\mathbf{v}}=(j_{\mathbf{v}},t_{\mathbf{v}})\in\Sigma
of each
\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{intnodes}(\mathcal{T})
is characterized by its dimension
j_{\mathbf{v}}\in\{1,\dots,d\}
and a non-empty subset of bins
t_{\mathbf{v}}\subsetneq\{1,\ldots,b_{j_{\mathbf{v}}}\}
.
We associate to each
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{T}
a cell
C_{\mathbf{v}}\subseteq C
which is defined recursively:
C_{\mathtt{root}}=C
and for each
\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{intnodes}(\mathcal{T})
we define

C_{\mathbf{v}0}:=\{x\in C_{\mathbf{v}}:x_{j_{\mathbf{v}}}\in t_{\mathbf{v}}\}\quad\text{and}\quad C_{\mathbf{v}1}:=C_{\mathbf{v}}\setminus C_{\mathbf{v}0}.

When
j_{\mathbf{v}}
corresponds to a continuous feature, bins have a natural order so that
t_{\mathbf{v}}
is encoded by a bin threshold
s_{\mathbf{v}}\in B_{j_{\mathbf{v}}}
as
t_{\mathbf{v}}=\{1,2,\ldots,s_{\mathbf{v}}\}
; while for a categorical split, the set
t_{\mathbf{v}}
may be any non-trivial subset of
B_{j_{\mathbf{v}}}
.
By construction,
(C_{\mathbf{v}})_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{leaves}(\mathcal{T})}
is a partition of
C
.

Bootstrap and feature subsampling.

Let
I=\{1,\ldots,n\}
be the training samples indices.
The randomization
\Pi
of the tree uses bootstrap: it samples uniformly at random, with replacement, elements of
I
corresponding to in-the-bag (
\mathtt{itb}
) samples.
If we denote as
I_{\mathtt{itb}}
the indices of unique
\mathtt{itb}
samples, we can define the indices of out-of-bag (
\mathtt{oob}
) samples as
I_{\mathtt{oob}}=I\setminus I_{\mathtt{itb}}
.
A standard argument shows that
\mathbb{P}[i\in I_{\mathtt{itb}}]=1-(1-1/n)^{n}\rightarrow 1-e^{-1}\approx 0.632
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
, known as the 0.632 rule
(
Efron and
Tibshirani 1997
)
.
The randomization
\Pi
uses also feature subsampling: each time we need to find a split, we do not try all the features
\{1,\ldots,d\}
but only a subset of them of size
d_{\max}
, chosen uniformly at random.
This follows what standard RF algorithms do
(
Breiman 2001
;
Biau and
Scornet 2016
;
Louppe 2014
)
, with the default
d_{\max}=\sqrt{d}
.

2.2
Split finding on histograms

For
K
-class classification, when looking for a split for some node
\mathbf{v}
, we compute the node’s “histogram”

\mathrm{hist}_{\mathbf{v}}[j,b,k]=\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{itb}}:x_{i}\in C_{\mathbf{v}}}\mathbf{1}_{x_{i,j}=b,y_{i}=k}
for each sampled feature
j
, each bin
b
and label class
k
seen in the node’s samples (actually weighted counts to handle bootstrapping and sample weights).
Of course, one has
\mathrm{hist}_{\mathbf{v}}=\mathrm{hist}_{\mathbf{v}0}+\mathrm{hist}_{\mathbf{v}1}
, so that we don’t need to compute two histograms for siblings
\mathbf{v}0
and
\mathbf{v}1
, but only a single one.
Then, we loop over the set of non-constant (in the node) sampled features
\{j:\#\{b:\sum_{k}\mathrm{hist}_{\mathbf{v}}[j,b,k]\geqslant 1\}\geqslant 2\}
and over the set of non-empty bins
\{b:\sum_{k}\mathrm{hist}_{\mathbf{v}}[j,b,k]\geqslant 1\}
to find a split, by comparing standard impurity criteria computed on the histogram’s statistics, such as gini or entropy for classification and variance for regression.

Bin order and categorical features.

The order of the bins used in the loop depends on the type of the feature.
If it is continuous, we use the natural order of bins.
If it is categorical and the task is binary classification (labels in
\{0,1\}
) we use the bin order that sorts
\mathrm{hist}_{\mathbf{v}}[j,b,1]/\sum_{k=0,1}\mathrm{hist}_{\mathbf{v}}[j,b,k]
with respect to
b
, namely the proportion of labels
1
in each bin.
This allows to find the optimal split with complexity
O(b_{j}\log b_{j})
, see Theorem 9.6 in
(
Breiman
et al. 1984
)
, the logarithm coming from the sorting operation, while there are
2^{b_{j}-1}-1
possible splits.
This trick is used by EGB libraries as well, using an order of
\text{gradient}/\text{hessian}
statistics of the loss considered
(
Chen and
Guestrin 2016
;
Ke et al. 2017
;
Prokhorenkova et al. 2018
)
.
For
K
-class classification with
K>2
, we consider two strategies:
(1) one-versus-rest, where we train
MK
trees instead of
M
, each tree trained with a binary one-versus-rest label, so that trees can find optimal categorical splits and
(2) heuristic, where we train
M
trees and where split finding uses
K
loops over bin orders that sort
\mathrm{hist}_{\mathbf{v}}[j,b,k]/\sum_{k^{\prime}}\mathrm{hist}_{\mathbf{v}}[j,b,k^{\prime}]
(w.r.t
b
) for
k=0,\ldots,K-1
.
The former strategy generally yields better predictions but at a higher computational cost, while the latter is more efficient but also more prone to overfitting.

Split requirements.

Nodes must hold at least one
\mathtt{itb}
and one
\mathtt{oob}
sample to apply aggregation with exponential weights, see Section
2.3
below.
A split is discarded if it leads to children with less than
n_{\text{min-leaf}}

\mathtt{itb}
or
\mathtt{oob}
samples and we do not split a node with less than
n_{\text{min-split}}

\mathtt{itb}
or
\mathtt{oob}
samples.
These hyperparameters only weakly impact WW’s performances and sticking to default values (
n_{\text{min-leaf}}=1
and
n_{\text{min-split}}=2
, following
scikit-learn
’s
(
Louppe 2014
;
Pedregosa
et al. 2011
)
) is usually enough (see Section
11
below).

Related works on categorical splits.

In
Coppersmith
et al. 1999
, an interesting characterization of an optimal categorical split for multiclass classification is introduced, but no efficient algorithm is, to the best of our understanding, available for it.
A heuristic algorithm is proposed therein, but it requires to compute, for each split, the top principal component of the covariance matrix of the conditional distribution of labels given bins, which is computationally too demanding for an RF algorithm intended for large datasets.
Regularized target encoding is shown in
Pargent et al. 2021
to perform best when compared with many alternative categorical encoding methods.
Catboost
(
Prokhorenkova et al. 2018
)

uses target encoding, which replaces feature modalities by label statistics, so that a natural bin order can be used for split finding.
To avoid overfitting on uninformative categorical features, a debiasing technique uses random permutations of samples and computes the target statistic of each element based only on its predecessors in the permutation.
However, for multiclass classification, target encoding is influenced by the arbitrarily chosen ordinal encoding of the labels.
LightGBM
(
Ke et al. 2017
)
uses a one-versus-rest strategy, which is also one of the approaches used in WW for categorical splits on multiclass tasks.
For categorical splits, where bin order depends on labels statistics, WW does not use debiasing as in
Prokhorenkova et al. 2018
, since aggregation with exponential weights computed on
\mathtt{oob}
samples allows to deal with overfitting.

Tree growth stopping.

We do not split a node and make it a leaf if it contains less than
n_{\text{min-split}}

\mathtt{itb}
or
\mathtt{oob}
samples.
The same applies when a node’s impurity is not larger than a threshold
\varepsilon
(
\varepsilon=0
by default).
When only leaves or non-splittable nodes remain, the growth of the tree is stopped.
Trees grow in a depth-first fashion so that childs
\mathbf{v}0
and
\mathbf{v}1
have memory indexes larger than their parent
\mathbf{v}
(as required by Algorithm
1
below). In practice, trees are grown to full depth in WildWood (i.e. we take
n_{\text{min-split}}=2
) since we can only benefit from additional splits and do not need to worry about overfitting since it is prevented by the subtrees aggregation we present now.

Prediction function: aggregation with exponential weights

Node and subtree prediction.

We define
\mathbf{v}_{T}(x)\in\mathrm{leaves}(T)
as the leaf of
T
containing
x\in C
.
The prediction of a node
\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{nodes}(\mathcal{T})
and of a subtree
T\subset\mathcal{T}
is given by

\hat{y}_{\mathbf{v}}=h((y_{i})_{i\in I_{\mathtt{itb}}\;:\;x_{i}\in C_{\mathbf{v}}})\quad\text{ and }\quad\hat{y}_{T}(x)=\hat{y}_{\mathbf{v}_{T}(x)},

(1)

where
h:\cup_{n\geqslant 0}\mathcal{Y}^{n}\to\hat{\mathcal{Y}}
is a generic “forecaster” used in each cell and where a subtree prediction is that of its leaf containing
x
.
A standard choice for regression (
\mathcal{Y}=\hat{\mathcal{Y}}=\mathbb{R}
) is the empirical mean forecaster

\hat{y}_{\mathbf{v}}=\frac{1}{n_{\mathbf{v}}}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{itb}}\;:\;x_{i}\in C_{\mathbf{v}}}y_{i},

(2)

where
n_{\mathbf{v}}=|\{i\in I_{\mathtt{itb}}\;:\;x_{i}\in C_{\mathbf{v}}\}|
.
For
K
-class classification with
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,\ldots,K\}
and
\hat{\mathcal{Y}}=\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
, the set of probability distributions over
\mathcal{Y}
, a standard choice is a Bayes predictive posterior with a prior on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
equal to the Dirichlet distribution
\mathsf{Dir}(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)
, namely the
Jeffreys prior
on the multinomial model
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
, which leads to

\hat{y}_{\mathbf{v}}(k)=\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)+\alpha}{n_{\mathbf{v}}+\alpha K},

(3)

for any
k\in\mathcal{Y}
, where
n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)=|\{i\in I_{\mathtt{itb}}:x_{i}\in C_{\mathbf{v}},y_{i}=k\}|
.
By default, WW uses
\alpha=1/2
(the
Krichevsky-Trofimov
forecaster
(
Tjalkens
et al. 1993
)
), but one can perfectly use any
\alpha>0
, so that all the coordinates of
\hat{y}_{\mathbf{v}}
are positive.
This is motivated by the fact that WW uses as default the log loss to assess
\mathtt{oob}
performance for classification, which requires an arbitrarily chosen clipping value for zero probabilities.
Different choices of
\alpha
only weakly impact WW’s performance, as illustrated in Appendix
11
.
We use
\mathtt{oob}
samples to define the cumulative losses of the predictions of all
T\subset\mathcal{T}

L_{T}=\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}\ell(\hat{y}_{T}(x_{i}),y_{i}),

(4)

where
\ell:\hat{\mathcal{Y}}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a loss function.
For regression problems, a default choice is the quadratic loss
\ell(\hat{y},y)=(\hat{y}-y)^{2}
while for multiclass classification, a default is the log-loss
\ell(\hat{y},y)=-\log\hat{y}(y)
, where
\hat{y}(y)\in(0,1]
when using (
3
), but other loss choices are of course possible.

Prediction function.

Let
x\in C
.
The prediction function
\hat{f}
of a tree
\mathcal{T}
in WW is given by

\hat{f}(x)=\frac{\sum_{T\subset\mathcal{T}}\pi(T)e^{-\eta L_{T}}\hat{y}_{T}(x)}{\sum_{T\subset\mathcal{T}}\pi(T)e^{-\eta L_{T}}}\quad\text{with}\quad\pi(T)=2^{-\|T\|},

(5)

where the sum is over all subtrees
T
of
\mathcal{T}
rooted at
\mathtt{root}
, where
\eta>0
is a temperature parameter and
\|T\|
is the number of nodes in
T
minus its number of leaves that are also leaves of
\mathcal{T}
.
A default choice for the temperature is
\eta=1
for the log-loss (see in particular Corollary
1
in Section
3
below), but it can also be tuned through hyperoptimization, although we do not observe strong performance gains, see Appendix
11
below.

The prediction function (
5
) corresponds to an exponentially weighted average over the predictors
\hat{y}_{T}(x)
for
T\subset\mathcal{T}
rooted at
\mathtt{root}
based on
\mathtt{oob}
performance and the branching process prior
\pi
with branching probability
1/2
.
The latter being expressly chosen for its fitness within this paradigm.
This definition falls within the framework of PAC-Bayesian theory
(
McAllester 1998
;
McAllester 1999
;
Catoni 2007
)
with theoretical guarantee of the oracle inequality for
\hat{f}
on
\mathtt{oob}
(see Theorem
2
below).
This aggregation procedure can be understood as a
non-greedy way to prune trees
: the weights depend not only on the quality of one single split but also on the performance of each subsequent split.

Naively computing
\hat{f}
from Equation (
5
) is computationally and memory-wise infeasible for a large
\mathcal{T}
, since it involves a sum over all
T\subset\mathcal{T}
rooted at
\mathtt{root}
and requires one weight for each
T
.
Indeed, the number of subtrees of a minimal tree that separates
n
points is exponential in the number of nodes, and hence
exponential in
n
.
However, it turns out that one can compute
\hat{f}
exactly and very efficiently thanks to the prior choice
\pi
together with an adaptation of
context tree weighting

(
Willems
et al. 1995
;
Willems 1998
;
Helmbold and
Schapire 1997
;
Catoni 2004
)
.

Theorem 1
.

The prediction function (
5
) can be written as
\hat{f}(x)=\hat{f}_{\mathtt{root}}(x)
,
where
\hat{f}_{\mathtt{root}}(x)
satisfies the recursion

\hat{f}_{\mathbf{v}}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{w_{\mathbf{v}}}{w^{\mathrm{den}}_{\mathbf{v}}}\hat{y}_{\mathbf{v}}+\Big(1-\frac{1}{2}\frac{w_{\mathbf{v}}}{w^{\mathrm{den}}_{\mathbf{v}}}\Big)\hat{f}_{\mathbf{v}a}(x)

(6)

for
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}a\in\mathtt{path}(x)

(
a\in\{0,1\}
)
the path in
\mathcal{T}
going from
\mathtt{root}
to
\mathbf{v}_{\mathcal{T}}(x)
,
where
w_{\mathbf{v}}:=\exp(-\eta L_{\mathbf{v}})
with
L_{\mathbf{v}}:=\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}:x_{i}\in C_{\mathbf{v}}}\ell(\hat{y}_{\mathbf{v}},y_{i})
and where
w^{\mathrm{den}}_{\mathbf{v}}
are weights satisfying the recursion

w^{\mathrm{den}}_{\mathbf{v}}=\begin{cases}w_{\mathbf{v}}&\text{ if }\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{leaves}(\mathcal{T}),\\
\frac{1}{2}w_{\mathbf{v}}+\frac{1}{2}w^{\mathrm{den}}_{\mathbf{v}0}w^{\mathrm{den}}_{\mathbf{v}1}&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

(7)

The proof of Theorem
1
is given in Section
6
below, a consequence of this Theorem being a very efficient method to compute
\hat{f}(x)
described in Algorithms
1
and
2
below.
Algorithm
1
computes the weights
w^{\mathrm{den}}_{\mathbf{v}}
using the fact that trees in WW are grown in a depth-first fashion, so that we can loop
once
, leading to a
O(|\mathrm{nodes}(\mathcal{T})|)
complexity in time and in memory usage, over nodes from a data structure that respects the parenthood order.
Direct computations can lead to numerical over- or under-flows (many products of exponentially small or large numbers are involved), so Algorithm
1
works recursively over the logarithms of the weights (line 6 uses a log-sum-exp function that can be made overflow-proof).

Algorithm 1

Computation of
\log(w^{\mathrm{den}}_{\mathbf{v}})
for all
\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{nodes}(\mathcal{T})
.

1:

Inputs:

\mathcal{T}
,
\eta>0
and losses
L_{\mathbf{v}}
for all
\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{nodes}(\mathcal{T})
.
Nodes from
\mathrm{nodes}(\mathcal{T})
are stored in a data structure
\mathtt{nodes}
that respects parenthood order: for any
\mathbf{v}=\mathtt{nodes}[i_{\mathbf{v}}]\in\mathrm{intnodes}(\mathcal{T})
and children
\mathbf{v}a=\mathtt{nodes}[i_{\mathbf{v}a}]
for
a\in\{0,1\}
, we have
i_{\mathbf{v}a}>i_{\mathbf{v}}
.

2:

for

\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{reversed}(\mathtt{nodes})

do

3:

if

\mathbf{v}
is a leaf
then

4:

Put
\log(w^{\mathrm{den}}_{\mathbf{v}})\leftarrow-\eta L_{\mathbf{v}}

5:

else

6:

Put
\log(w^{\mathrm{den}}_{\mathbf{v}})\leftarrow\log(\frac{1}{2}e^{-\eta L_{\mathbf{v}}}+\frac{1}{2}e^{\log(w^{\mathrm{den}}_{\mathbf{v}0})+\log(w^{\mathrm{den}}_{\mathbf{v}1})})

7:

end

if

8:

end

for

9:

return
The set of log-weights
\{\log(w^{\mathrm{den}}_{\mathbf{v}}):\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{nodes}(\mathcal{T})\}

Algorithm
1
is applied once
\mathcal{T}
is fully grown, so that WW is ready to produce predictions using Algorithm
2
below.
Note that hyperoptimization of
\eta
or
\alpha
, if required, does not need to grow
\mathcal{T}
again, but only to update
w^{\mathrm{den}}_{\mathbf{v}}
for all
\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{nodes}(\mathcal{T})
with Algorithm
1
, making hyperoptimization of these parameters particularly efficient.

Algorithm 2

Computation of
\hat{f}(x)
for any
x\in C
.

1:

Inputs:
Tree
\mathcal{T}
, losses
L_{\mathbf{v}}
and log-weights
\log(w^{\mathrm{den}}_{\mathbf{v}})
computed by Algorithm
1

2:

Find
\mathbf{v}_{\mathcal{T}}(x)\in\mathrm{leaves}(\mathcal{T})
(the leaf containing
x
) and put
\mathbf{v}\leftarrow\mathbf{v}_{\mathcal{T}}(x)

3:

Put
\hat{f}(x)\leftarrow\hat{y}_{\mathbf{v}}
(the node
\mathbf{v}
forecaster, such as (
2
) for regression or (
3
) for classification)

4:

while

\mathbf{v}\neq\mathtt{root}

do

5:

Put
\mathbf{v}\leftarrow\mathrm{parent}(\mathbf{v})

6:

Put
\alpha\leftarrow\frac{1}{2}\exp(-\eta L_{\mathbf{v}}-\log(w^{\mathrm{den}}_{\mathbf{v}}))

7:

Put
\hat{f}(x)\leftarrow\alpha\hat{y}_{\mathbf{v}}+(1-\alpha)\hat{f}(x)

8:

end

while

9:

return
The prediction
\hat{f}(x)

The recursion used in Algorithm
2
has a complexity
O(|\mathtt{path}(x)|)
which is the complexity required to find the leaf
\mathbf{v}_{\mathcal{T}}(x)
containing
x\in C
: Algorithm
2

only increases by a factor
2
the prediction complexity of a standard RF (in order to go down to
\mathbf{v}_{\mathcal{T}}(x)
and up again to
\mathtt{root}
along
\mathtt{path}(x)
).
More details about the construction of Algorithms
1
and
2
can be found in Section
6
below.

3
Theoretical guarantees

This section proposes some theoretical guarantees on the subtrees aggregation (
5
) used in WW.
We say that a loss function
\ell
is
\eta
-exp-concave for some
\eta>0
whenever
z\mapsto\exp(-\eta\ell(z,y))
is concave for any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
We consider a fully-grown tree
\mathcal{T}
computed using
\mathtt{itb}
samples and the set of
\mathtt{oob}
samples
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}
on which
L_{T}
is computed using (
4
), and we denote
n_{\mathtt{oob}}:=|I_{\mathtt{oob}}|
.

Theorem 2

(Oracle inequality)
.

Assume that the loss function
\ell
is
\eta
-exp-concave.
Then
,
the prediction function
\hat{f}
given by (
5
) satisfies the oracle inequality

\frac{1}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}\ell(\hat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})\leqslant\inf_{T\subset\mathcal{T}}\bigg\{\frac{1}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}\ell(\hat{y}_{T}(x_{i}),y_{i})+\frac{\log 2}{\eta}\frac{\|T\|}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}+1}\bigg\},

where the infimum is over any subtree
T\subset\mathcal{T}
and where we recall that
\|T\|
is the number of nodes in
T
minus its number of leaves that are also leaves of
\mathcal{T}
.

Theorem
2
proves that, for a general loss function, the prediction function of WW is able to perform nearly as well as the best
oracle
subtree
T\subset\mathcal{T}
on
\mathtt{oob}
samples, with a
O(\|T\|/n_{\mathtt{oob}})
rate which is optimal for model-selection oracle inequalities
(
Tsybakov 2003
)

(
\|T\|=O(\log N_{\mathcal{T}})
with a number of “experts”
N_{\mathcal{T}}=|\{T:T\subset\mathcal{T}\}|
for a well-balanced
\mathcal{T}
).
Let us stress again that, while finding an oracle
\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{T\subset\mathcal{T}}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}\ell(\hat{y}_{T}(x_{i}),y_{i})
is computationally infeasible, since it requires to try out all possible subtrees, WW’s prediction function (
5
) comes at a cost comparable to that of a standard Random Forest, as explained in Section
2.3
above.

The proof of Theorem
2
is given in Section
7
below and relies on techniques from PAC-Baysesian theory
(
McAllester 1998
;
McAllester 1999
;
Catoni 2007
)
.
The arguments hinge upon the mixability of the loss, a consequence of its exp-concavity, as well as the Donsker-Varadhan variational formula.
Note that the statement concerns only the performance of a single tree and not the whole forest. Indeed, extrapolating the benefit brought by the exponential weights’ aggregation within each tree to the forest itself remains a difficult open problem even though it is intuitive enough that improved individual trees should enhance the overall performance.
Compared with
Mourtada
et al. 2021
about online learning, our proof differs in that we do not use results specialized to online learning such as
Vovk 1998
nor online-to-batch conversion
(
Cesa-Bianchi et al. 2004
)
.
Note that Theorem
2
does not address the generalization error, since it would require to study the generalization error of the random forest itself (and of the fully grown tree
\mathcal{T}
), which is a topic way beyond the scope of this paper, and still a very difficult open problem: recent results
(
Genuer 2012
;
Arlot and
Genuer 2014
;
Scornet
et al. 2015
;
Scornet 2016a
;
Scornet 2016b
;
Mourtada et al. 2020
)
only study stylized versions of RF (called purely random forests).

Consequences of Theorem
2
are Corollary
1
for the log-loss (classification) and Corollary
2
for the least-squares loss (regression).

Corollary 1

(Classification)
.

Consider
K
-class classification
(
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,\ldots,K\}
)
and consider the prediction function
\hat{f}
given by (
5
)
,
where node predictions are given by (
3
) with
\alpha=1/2

(
WW’s default
),
where
\ell
is the log-loss and where
\eta=1
.
Then
,
we have

\frac{1}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}\ell(\hat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})\leqslant\inf_{T\subset\mathcal{T}}\Big\{\frac{1}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}\ell(g_{T}(x_{i}),y_{i})+\frac{K+4\log 2-1}{4}\frac{\|T\|+1}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}\Big\},

where
g_{T}
is any constant function on the leaves of
T
.

Corollary 2

(Regression)
.

Consider regression with
\mathcal{Y}=[-B,B]
for some
B>0
and the prediction function
\hat{f}
given by (
5
)
,
where node predictions are given by (
2
), where
\ell
is the least-squares loss and where
\eta=1/(8B^{2})
.
Then
,
we have

\frac{1}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}\ell(\hat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})\leqslant\inf_{T\subset\mathcal{T}}\Big\{\frac{1}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}\ell(g_{T}(x_{i}),y_{i})+8(\log 2)B^{2}\frac{\|T\|}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}\Big\},

where
g_{T}
is any function constant on the leaves of
T
.

The proofs of Corollaries
1
and
2
are given in Section
7
.
These corollaries motivate the default hyperparameter values of
\eta
, in particular
\eta=1
for classification.

5
Conclusion

We introduced WildWood, a new Random Forest algorithm for batch supervised learning.
Tree predictions in WildWood are aggregation with exponential weights of the predictions of all subtrees, with weights computed on bootstrap out-of-bag samples.
This leads to improved predictions in each individual tree, at a small computational cost, since WildWood’s prediction complexity is similar to that of a standard Random Forest.
Moreover, thanks to the histogram strategy, WildWood’s implementation is competitive with strong baselines including popular extreme boosting libraries, both in terms of performance and training times.
Note also that WildWood has few hyperparameters to tune and that the performances obtained with default hyperparameters are usually good enough in our experiments.

WildWood’s implementation is still evolving and many improvements coming with future updates are planned, including the computation of feature importance, GPU training, distributed training (we only support single-machine training for now), among other enhancements that will further improve performances and accelerate computations.
Room for improvement in WildWood comes from the fact that the overall forest prediction is a simple arithmetic mean of each tree prediction, while we could perform also exponentially weighted aggregation between trees.
Future works include a WildWood-based implementation of isolation-forest
(
Liu
et al. 2008
)
, using the same subtrees aggregation mechanism with the log loss for density estimation, to propose a new algorithm for outliers detection.

More precisely, we will use the following lemma

several times to efficiently compute sums of products.
Let us recall that
\mathrm{nodes}(\mathcal{T})
stands for the set of nodes of

Lemma 1
.

Let

be an arbitrary function and define
G:\mathrm{nodes}(\mathcal{T})\to\mathbb{R}
as

G(\mathbf{v})=\sum_{T\subset\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{v}}}2^{-\|T\|}\prod_{\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{leaves}(T)}g(\mathbf{v}^{\prime}),

(8)

where the sum over
T\subset\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{v}}
means the sum over all subtrees
T
of
\mathcal{T}
rooted at
\mathbf{v}
.
Then
,

G(\mathbf{v})
can be computed recursively as follows

G(\mathbf{v})=\begin{cases}g(\mathbf{v})&\text{ if }\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{leaves}(\mathcal{T})\\
\frac{1}{2}g(\mathbf{v})+\frac{1}{2}G(\mathbf{v}0)G(\mathbf{v}1)&\mathrm{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for each node
\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{nodes}(\mathcal{T})

For the sake of completeness, we include a proof of this statement.

Proof.

First, let us notice that the case
\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{leaves}(\mathcal{T})
is straightforward since there is only one pruning
T
of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{v}}
which satisfies

(recall that

is the number of internal nodes and leaves in
T

minus
the number of leaves in
T
that are also leaves of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{v}}
).
For the second case, we can expand

by taking into account the pruning which only leaves
\mathbf{v}
as a leaf, the rest of the prunings can be expressed through pairs of prunings

and

\displaystyle G(\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}g(\mathbf{v})+\sum_{T_{0}\subset\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{v}0}}\sum_{T_{1}\subset\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{v}1}}2^{-(1+\|T_{0}\|+\|T_{1}\|)}\prod_{\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\in T_{0}}g(\mathbf{v}0\mathbf{v}^{\prime})\prod_{\mathbf{v}{{}^{\prime\prime}}\in T_{1}}g(\mathbf{v}1\mathbf{v}{{}^{\prime\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}g(\mathbf{v})+\frac{1}{2}\Big(\sum_{T_{0}\subset\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{v}0}}2^{-\|T_{0}\|}\prod_{\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\in T_{0}}g(\mathbf{v}0\mathbf{v}^{\prime})\Big)\cdot\Big(\sum_{T_{1}\subset\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{v}1}}2^{-\|T_{1}\|}\prod_{\mathbf{v}{{}^{\prime\prime}}\in T_{1}}g(\mathbf{v}1\mathbf{v}{{}^{\prime\prime}})\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}g(\mathbf{v})+\frac{1}{2}G(\mathbf{v}0)G(\mathbf{v}1).

This concludes the proof of Lemma

The proof of Theorem
2
is partly inspired from the proof of Theorem 2 in
Dalalyan and
Tsybakov 2008
, that we generalize to exp-concave losses, while only least-squares regression is considered therein.
Let
E
be a measurable space and
P,Q
be probability measures on it.
The Kullback-Leibler divergence between
P
and
Q
is defined by

\mathrm{KL}(P,Q)=\int_{E}\log\Big(\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}\Big)\mathrm{d}P

whenever
P
is absolutely continuous with respect to
Q
and equal to
+\infty
otherwise.
Also, if
h:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function such that
\int_{E}h\mathrm{d}P
is well-defined on
\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,+\infty\}
, we introduce

P_{h}:=\frac{h}{\int_{E}h\mathrm{d}P}\cdot P,

the probability measure on
E
with density
h/\int h\mathrm{d}P
with respect to
P
.
A classical result is the Donsker-Varadhan variational formula
(
Donsker and
Varadhan 1976
)
, which is at the core of the proofs of many PAC-Bayesian theorems
(
McAllester 1999
;
Catoni 2007
)
and that we use here as well in the proof of Theorem
2
.
It states that

\log\Big(\int_{E}\exp(h)\mathrm{d}Q\Big)+\mathrm{KL}(P,Q)-\int h\mathrm{d}P=\mathrm{KL}(P,Q_{\exp(h)})

(23)

holds for any probability measures
P
and
Q
on
E
and any measurable function
h:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
This entails in particular that

\log\Big(\int_{E}\exp(h)\mathrm{d}Q\Big)=\sup_{P}\Big\{\int h\mathrm{d}P-\mathrm{KL}(P,Q)\Big\},

where the supremum is over all probability measures on
E
, and where the supremum is achieved for
P=Q_{\exp(h)}
whenever the term on the left-hand side is finite.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Recall that the
\mathtt{oob}
loss of a subtree
T\subset\mathcal{T}
is given by

L_{T}=\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}\ell(\hat{y}_{T}(x_{i}),y_{i})

and let us introduce

p_{T}=\frac{\pi(T)\exp(-\eta L_{T})}{\sum_{T^{\prime}}\pi(T^{\prime})\exp(-\eta L_{T^{\prime}})}

(24)

for any subtree
T\subset\mathcal{T}
.
First, we use the fact that
\ell
is a
\eta
-exp-concave loss function, hence
\eta
-mixable, see Section 3.3 from
Cesa-Bianchi and
Lugosi 2006
, which entails, since
p_{T}
is a probability measure over the set of all subtrees
T\subset\mathcal{T}
, that

\ell\Big(\sum_{T}p_{T}\hat{y}_{T}(x_{i}),y_{i}\Big)\leqslant-\frac{1}{\eta}\log\Big(\sum_{T}p_{T}\exp(-\eta\ell(\hat{y}_{T}(x_{i}),y_{i}))\Big),

where the sums over
T
are over all subtrees
T\subset\mathcal{T}
.
Now, summing this inequality over
i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}
and using the convexity of the log-sum-exp function leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}\ell\Big(\sum_{T}p_{T}\hat{y}_{T}(x_{i}),y_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant-\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}\log\Big(\sum_{T}p_{T}\exp(-\eta\ell(\hat{y}_{T}(x_{i}),y_{i}))\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant-\frac{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}{\eta}\log\Big(\sum_{T}p_{T}\exp\Big(-\frac{\eta}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}\ell(\hat{y}_{T}(x_{i}),y_{i})\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}{\eta}\log\Big(\sum_{T}p_{T}\exp\Big(-\frac{\eta}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}L_{T}\Big)\Big).

By plugging the definition of
p_{T}
into the previous expression, and by introducing
\rho(T):=\eta L_{T}/n_{\mathtt{oob}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{S}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}\ell(\hat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})

\displaystyle\leqslant-\frac{1}{\eta}\log\Big(\sum_{T}\pi(T)\exp\big(-(n_{\mathtt{oob}}+1)\rho(T)\big)\Big)+\frac{1}{\eta}\log\Big(\sum_{T}\pi(T)\exp\big(-n_{\mathtt{oob}}\rho(T)\big)\Big).

The Hölder inequality implies that

\sum_{T}\pi(T)\exp\big(-n_{\mathtt{oob}}\rho(T)\big)\leqslant\Big(\sum_{T}\pi(T)\exp\big(-(n_{\mathtt{oob}}+1)\rho(T)\big)\Big)^{n_{\mathtt{oob}}/(n_{\mathtt{oob}}+1)},

thus

\frac{S}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}\leqslant-\frac{1}{\eta(n_{\mathtt{oob}}+1)}\log\Big(\sum_{T}\pi(T)\exp\big(-(n_{\mathtt{oob}}+1)\rho(T)\big)\Big).

Using (
23
) with
h(T)=-(n_{\mathtt{oob}}+1)\rho(T)
and
Q=\pi
, we have

\displaystyle\log\Big(\sum_{T}\pi(T)\exp

\displaystyle\big(-(n_{\mathtt{oob}}+1)\rho(T)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{T}P(T)(n_{\mathtt{oob}}+1)\rho(T)-\mathrm{KL}(P,\pi)+\mathrm{KL}(P,\pi_{\exp(h)})

for any probability measure
P
over the set of subtrees of
\mathcal{T}
.
This leads to

\frac{1}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}\ell(\hat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})\leqslant\frac{1}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}\sum_{T}P(T)L_{T}+\frac{1}{\eta(n_{\mathtt{oob}}+1)}\mathrm{KL}(P,\pi)

for any
P
, since
\mathrm{KL}(P,\pi_{\exp(h)})\geqslant 0
. So for the particular choice
P=\delta_{T}
(the Dirac mass at
T
) for any subtree
T\subset\mathcal{T}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}\ell(\hat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}L_{T}+\frac{1}{\eta(n_{\mathtt{oob}}+1)}\log\big(\pi(T)^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}}L_{T}+\frac{\log 2}{\eta}\frac{\|T\|}{n_{\mathtt{oob}}+1},

which concludes the proof of Theorem
2
.

.
We consider the Krichevsky-Trofimov estimator

\hat{y}(k)=\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)+1/2}{n_{\mathbf{v}}+K/2}

where
n_{\mathbf{v}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)
and the log loss
\ell(y^{\prime},y)=-\log y^{\prime}(y)
.
Then, the inequality

\sum_{i}\ell(\hat{y},y_{i})-\inf_{p}\sum_{i}\ell(p,y_{i})\leqslant\frac{K-1}{2}

holds.

Proof.

We know that the optimal
p
is given by
p_{k}=n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)/n_{\mathbf{v}}
.
Indeed, it is the solution to the following constrained convex optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{p}\;-\sum_{k=1}^{K}n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)\log p_{k}\quad\text{ subject to }\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}=1,

where we consider non-negativity to be already enforced by the objective and imposing
p_{k}\leqslant 1
is redundant with the constraint
\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}=1
.
We can write the Lagrangian function as

L(p,\lambda)=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)\log p_{k}+\lambda\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}-1\Big)

and one can check the KKT conditions when taking
p_{k}=n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)/n_{\mathbf{v}}
and
\lambda=n_{\mathbf{v}}
.
Since we are dealing with a convex problem with linear constraints, this is a sufficient optimality condition.
Straightforward computations give

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\ell(\hat{y},y_{i})-\inf_{p}\sum_{i}\ell(p,y_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}-n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)\log(\hat{y}(k))-\sum_{k=1}^{K}-n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)\log p_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}-n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)\log\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)+1/2}{n_{\mathbf{v}}+K/2}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}-n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)\log\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)}{n_{\mathbf{v}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}-n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)\Big(\log\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}}{n_{\mathbf{v}}+K/2}+\log\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)+1/2}{n_{\mathbf{v}}}-\log\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)}{n_{\mathbf{v}}}\Big)

\displaystyle=-n_{\mathbf{v}}\log\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}}{n_{\mathbf{v}}+K/2}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}-n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)\Big(\log\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)+1/2}{n_{\mathbf{v}}}-\log\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)}{n_{\mathbf{v}}}\Big)

\displaystyle=n_{\mathbf{v}}\log\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}+K/2}{n_{\mathbf{v}}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)\log\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)}{n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)+1/2}.

Now, using the concavity of the logarithm gives

\sum_{i}\ell(\hat{y},y_{i})-\inf_{p}\sum_{i}\ell(p,y_{i})\leqslant n_{\mathbf{v}}\log\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}+K/2}{n_{\mathbf{v}}}+n_{\mathbf{v}}\log\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)}{n_{\mathbf{v}}}\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)}{n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)+1/2}\Big),

and the fact that
x\mapsto x/(x+1/2)
is non-decreasing and
n_{\mathbf{v}}(k)\leqslant n_{\mathbf{v}}
leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\ell(\hat{y},y_{i})-\inf_{p}\sum_{i}\ell(p,y_{i})

\displaystyle\leqslant n_{\mathbf{v}}\log\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}+K/2}{n_{\mathbf{v}}}+n_{\mathbf{v}}\log\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}}{n_{\mathbf{v}}+1/2}

\displaystyle=n_{\mathbf{v}}\log\frac{n_{\mathbf{v}}+1/2+(K-1)/2}{n_{\mathbf{v}}+1/2}

\displaystyle=n_{\mathbf{v}}\log\Big(1+\frac{K-1}{2n_{\mathbf{v}}+1}\Big)\leqslant\frac{K-1}{2}.

This concludes the proof of Lemma
2
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1
.

The log-loss is trivially
1
-exp-concave, so that we can choose
\eta=1
.
Following Theorem
2
, it remains to bound the regret of the tree forecaster
T
with respect to the optimal labeling of its leaves.
For classification and the log loss, we use Lemma
2
to obtain

\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}:x_{i}\in C_{\mathbf{v}}}\ell(\hat{y}_{T}(x_{i}),y_{i})-\inf_{p}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}:x_{i}\in C_{\mathbf{v}}}\ell(p,y_{i})\leqslant\frac{K-1}{2}

for any subtree
T
and any
\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{leaves}(T)
.
Now, summing over
\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{leaves}(T)
, of cardinality
(\|T\|+1)/2
, leads to

\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}\ell(\hat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})-\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}}\ell(g_{T}(x_{i}),y_{i})\leqslant\|T\|\log 2+\frac{(K-1)(\|T\|+1)}{4},

which concludes the proof of Corollary
1
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
2
.

The square loss is
1/(8B^{2})
-exp-concave on
[-B,B]
, see
Cesa-Bianchi and
Lugosi 2006
, so we can choose
\eta=1/(8B^{2})
.
Following Theorem
2
,
it remains to bound the regret of the tree forecaster
T
with respect to the optimal labeling of its leaves.
For regression with the least-squares loss, since we use the empirical mean forecaster (
2
), we have

\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}:x_{i}\in C_{\mathbf{v}}}\ell(\hat{y}_{T}(x_{i}),y_{i})-\inf_{b}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathtt{oob}}:x_{i}\in C_{\mathbf{v}}}\ell(b,y_{i})=0

for any subtree
T
and any leaf
\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{leaves}(T)
.
The rest of the proof follows that of Corollary
1
.
∎
