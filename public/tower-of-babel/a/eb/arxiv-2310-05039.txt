Uncertainty relations revisited 1 Note: Dedicated to Daniel M. Greenberger on the occasion of his 90th birthday.
Berthold-Georg Englert

Abstract
Introductory courses on quantum mechanics usually include lectures on uncertainty relations, typically the inequality derived by Robertson and, perhaps, other statements. For the benefit of the lecturers, we present a unified approach — well suited for undergraduate teaching — for deriving all standard uncertainty relations: those for products of variances by Kennard, Robertson, and Schrödinger, as well as those for sums of variances by Maccone and Pati. We also give a brief review of the early history of this topic and try to answer why the use of variances for quantifying uncertainty is so widespread, while alternatives are available that can be more natural and more fitting. It is common to regard the states that saturate the Robertson inequality as “minimum uncertainty states” although they do not minimize the variance of one observable, given the variance of another, incompatible observable. The states that achieve this objective are different and can be found systematically.

2
Measures of uncertainty

A measure of uncertainty quantifies the fuzziness associated with the
probability distribution of a random variable, such as the values of a
measured property of a quantum system.
While there is no unique way of quantifying uncertainty, all uncertainty
measures must have the following three properties:

U1

They are well-defined and assign a nonnegative
number to every distribution of the random variable;

U2

they acquire the minimal value (usually
=0
) in all
limits of a sharp distribution (only one value of the random
variable occurs), and only then;

U3

they are concave: a convex sum of distributions
cannot have an uncertainty measure less than the corresponding
average of the uncertainty measures for the ingredient
distributions.

(1)

There is also a desirable fourth property:

U4

A measure of uncertainty should
reach its largest value (finite or infinite)
in the limit of a uniform distribution.

(2)

These properties are closely analogous to those required of measures for path
knowledge in interferometers
[
6
,
7
]
.
Note that property
U2
excludes measures that assign minimal uncertainty to
distributions with nonzero probabilities for more than one value; and that the
“should reach” clause in property
U4
allows measures that are maximal
for nonuniform distributions.

One important example for which this clause matters is the
variance

\var(X)=\Min_{x}\Bigl\{{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{(X-x)^{2}}\right\rangle}\Bigr\}={\left\langle\vphantom{|}{X^{2}}\right\rangle}-{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{X}\right\rangle}^{2}

(3)

for a real-valued random variable
X
; it has properties
U1
,
U2
, and
U3
,
but is usually largest for a nonuniform distribution.
In Eq. (
3
), all real reference values
x

participate in the competition.
It is natural to restrict the
x
values to the proper values of
X
, and then
we obtain another permissible measure, the
smallest squared distance

\ssd(X)=\var(X)+\bigl({\left\langle\vphantom{|}{X}\right\rangle}-\check{x}\bigr)^{2}\,,

(4)

where
\check{x}
is the proper value closest to
{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{X}\right\rangle}
.
Many more ways of quantifying uncertainty are available,
and one should choose the uncertainty measure that fits the context.

In the spirit of
\ssd(\ )
, there are, in particular, the measures based on
minimizing a Monge-type cost,
\mtc(\ )
, for converting the actual
probability distribution into a sharp one,

\mtc(X)=\Min_{x}\Bigl\{\bigl\langle c(x,X)\bigr\rangle\Bigr\}\,,

(5)

where
c(x,x^{\prime})
is the cost associated with allocating the probability of
finding the proper value
x^{\prime}
to that of finding
x
; we have

c(x,x^{\prime})=(x-x^{\prime})^{2}
in Eq. (
4
).
The minimization ensures property
U3
, and

c(x,x^{\prime})\geq 0\quad\mbox{with ``$=$'' if $x=x^{\prime}$ and only then}

(6)

guarantees property
U2
.
This approach was explored by Werner and collaborators, see Refs.
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]

and works cited there.

Yet another procedure favors entropic measures that do not change when the
proper values of
X
are permuted, such as a
Shannon-type entropy for the probabilities of a discrete observable or the
binned probabilities of a continuous observable

[
12
,
13
,
14
]
.
While entropic measures of this kind possess property
U4
and find applications
in quantum information science (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
), they do not
serve the same purpose as (the square root of) the variance or a Monge-type
cost, namely quantifying our uncertainty about, say, the position by a
distance measure.

3
Why variances?

It appears that the common practice — almost without exceptions — of
measuring an uncertainty in quantum mechanics by a variance originates in a
first-day plausible choice that no one questioned.
In Heisenberg’s ground-breaking 1927 paper on how to understand the
then-new quantum mechanics
[
1
]
, there are two relations
between uncertainty products and Planck’s constant
h
,

\text{first:}\ q_{1}p_{1}\sim h\,,\quad\text{later:}\ q_{1}p_{1}=\frac{h}{2\pi}\,.

(7)

The first is an intuitive, semi-quantitative, order-of-magnitude statement
about “the precision
q_{1}
with which the position is known
and the precision
p_{1}
with which the momentum is determinable;’’
1
1

1

This is not an exact quote but a paraphrase of Wheeler’s and Zurek’s
translation of Heisenberg’s words.

a microscope with its finite resolution and the recoil of the Compton effect
are mentioned in this context.
While this seems to put
q_{1}
and
p_{1}
on different footing,
the supporting evidence of the later equality has them on equal footing
because Heisenberg employs for this purpose a wave function that gives
gaussian probability densities
{\propto\!\exp\bigl(-(q-q^{\prime})^{2}/q_{1}^{2}\bigr)}
and

{\propto\!\exp\bigl(-(p-p^{\prime})^{2}/p_{1}^{2}\bigr)}
for the position
Q
and the
momentum
P
, respectively.

Heisenberg did not offer a general definition of
q_{1}
and
p_{1}
for
arbitrary wave functions and seemed to trust that his intuition would tell him
how to quantify uncertainty in any given situation.
What would Heisenberg’s 1927 intuition have told him about a position
distribution with two well-separated narrow peaks?
We don’t know.
It is, however, not plausible that he would have chosen the variance for
quantifying the position uncertainty in this situation.

Still in 1927, Kennard worked out the now-common textbook examples for which
Heisenberg’s equations of motion are linear and, therefore, easily solved

[
2
]
.
He mathematized Heisenberg’s
q_{1}p_{1}
product by defining both factors as the
square roots of doubled variances and derived the inequality

q_{i}p_{i}\geq\frac{h}{2\pi}\,,

(8)

which states the “slightly generalized law of indeterminacy by Heisenberg”
for “every pair of canonical variables”
(analogs of position and momentum).
Except for the doubling, Kennard’s definition became the accepted notion of
uncertainty immediately:
Weyl used it in his 1928 book, then Condon, Robertson, and Schrödinger in
their papers of 1929 and 1930

[
16
,
17
,
3
,
4
]
.
Since none of these authors justified the use of variances — Robertson
simply invokes “statistical usage” — this must have
been an utterly natural choice for them and their contemporaries.

While the use of variances for measuring uncertainty can be questioned, there
is a context in which variances appear naturally, namely in the no-change
probability when a small change is induced by a unitary transformation,

\mathopen{\boldsymbol{\Big|}}{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{\mathrm{e}^{\mbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{i}\epsilon X$}}}\right\rangle}\mathclose{\boldsymbol{\Big|}}^{2}=1-\epsilon^{2}\var(X)+\cdots\,,

(9)

where the ellipsis stands for contributions of order
\epsilon^{4}
or higher
powers.
None of the classical papers on the subject mentions this, however, and so we
conclude that the use of variances was not motivated by Eq. (
9
).

In the terminology of Ref.
[
9
]
, the inequalities by
Kennard, Robertson, and Schrödinger are statements about the

preparation uncertainty
, not about the

error-disturbance uncertainty
or the
measurement uncertainty
.
The well-known works by Arthurs and Kelly
[
18
]
, and by Ozawa

[
19
]
concern the latter.
In this paper, we deal only with the preparation uncertainty.

The initial steps taken by Heisenberg and Kennard and followed
by others gave the center stage to inequalities obeyed by the product of two
variances.
Of course, it was clear early on that there can be vanishing variances for
observables with discrete eigenvalues
[
17
]
, in which case
inequalities for sums of the two variances are more useful.
Judging by the wikipedia article on the topic
[
20
]
, the
inequalities derived by Maccone and Pati in 2014
[
5
]
are
regarded as standard in this context.

4
The standard inequalities

The inequalities for products of variances are usually demonstrated as
consequences of the Cauchy–Bunyakovsky–Schwarz inequality, whereas Maccone and
Pati employed much more advanced tools when establishing their inequalities for
sums.
Contrary to the impression this might create, the two kinds of uncertainty
relations are not profoundly different.
In fact, all standard inequalities — for products or sums of
variances — follow from the elementary fact that an operator maps a ket onto
a linear combination of this ket and a second, perpendicular ket.

We consider a quantum system, whose state is described by the generic
unit-length ket
{\left|{\ }\right\rangle}
and its adjoint bra
{\left\langle{\ }\right|}
, and two real physical
properties symbolized by the hermitian operators
A
and
B
.
While the following considerations have easy generalizations for mixed states,
we simplify the presentation by the use of pure states.
We then have
{{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{A}\right\rangle}={\left\langle{\ }\right|}A{\left|{\ }\right\rangle}}
, for instance.

For any two complex numbers
a
and
b
— with metrical dimensions such that

aA+bB
is meaningful — the identity

(aA+bB)\,{\left|{\ }\right\rangle}={\left|{\ }\right\rangle}\,\bigl(a{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{A}\right\rangle}+b{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{B}\right\rangle}\bigr)+{\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\perp$}}\right\rangle}\,\gamma

(10)

defines the product
{\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\perp$}}\right\rangle}\,\gamma
of the unit-length ket
{\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\perp$}}\right\rangle}

and the complex coefficient
\gamma
, with
{\left\langle{\ }{|}{\mbox{\scriptsize$\perp$}}\right\rangle}=0

\displaystyle\mathopen{\boldsymbol{|}}\gamma\mathclose{\boldsymbol{|}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{\boldsymbol{|}}a\mathclose{\boldsymbol{|}}^{2}\var(A)+\mathopen{\boldsymbol{|}}b\mathclose{\boldsymbol{|}}^{2}\var(B)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\mbox{}+2\Real{\left({a^{*}b}\right)}\cor(A,B)+2\Imag{\left({a^{*}b}\right)}\,{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}$}[A,B]}\right\rangle}\,,

(11)

where
{[A,B]=AB-BA}
is the commutator and

\cor(A,B)=\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{(AB+BA)}\right\rangle}-{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{A}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{B}\right\rangle}

(12)

is the correlation of
A
and
B

With judicious choices for
a
and

and the observation that

\mathopen{\boldsymbol{|}}\gamma\mathclose{\boldsymbol{|}}^{2}\geq\mathopen{\boldsymbol{\big|}}{\left\langle{\smash{\mbox{\scriptsize$\perp$}^{\prime}}}\right|}\,(aA+bB)\,{\left|{\ }\right\rangle}\mathclose{\boldsymbol{\big|}}^{2}\geq 0

{{\left\langle{\mbox{\scriptsize$\perp$}^{\prime}}{|}{\ }\right\rangle}=0}

Heisenberg never spoke of the

while others used “uncertainty principle” quite early — and the term stuck.
In his 1929 paper

Eq. (

Robertson’s way of using “uncertainty principle” later in 1929

[

suggests that this was established terminology, and he

[

[

do

For us, the inequalities obeyed by uncertainty measures are

uncertainty relations
, and we use the term “uncertainty principle”
with the limited, and perhaps rudimentary, meaning that there are no physical
pairs of uncertainty values in the vicinity of the bottom-left corner
in plots such as the ones in Figs.
1
–
6
in the following
sections if the uncertainties refer to incompatible observables —
observables that do not have one or more common eigenstates.
This is in line with Heisenberg’s profound insight that, as a matter of
principle, one cannot have precise knowledge of two such observables:
We can know either one of the properties with great precision —
with a small uncertainty or no uncertainty at all, that is —
but there is no situation in which we know both properties accurately on the
quantum scale.

Having thus clarified what the “uncertainty principle” means for us, we
submit that this is poorly chosen terminology as it is not a
physical principle in the usual sense.
While the principle of relativity, the entropy principle in statistical physics,
the principle of complementarity in quantum kinematics,
the equivalence principle in general relativity, the gauge principle in
quantum field theory, and the various action principles are the starting
points of whole branches of physics or impose profound constraints, the
uncertainty principle plays no such role.

The standard inequalities in
Sec.
4
, taken together, serve
the purpose of confirming that the uncertainty principle is respected by each
pair of incompatible observables if we measure uncertainties by variances.
When other measures are used, corresponding inequalities apply.

Stationary uncertainty states

Figures

and
2

The central question

For two hermitian observables,
A
and
B
, what are the values
available for the pairs of their variances?

(26)

A partial answer is given by finding the smallest value of
\var(A)
for a
given value of
\var(B)
and
vice versa
.
It is obvious that the Robertson states are useless here;
in fact, it is unclear which purpose they serve.
3
3

The full answer to the central question requires knowledge of the

(i)  For the variances of two hermitian observables,

The familiar tools of variational calculus establish that the auxiliary
question (ii) is answered by the states that solve the nonlinear eigenvalue
equation

\Bigl(\alpha\bigl(A-{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{A}\right\rangle}\bigr)^{2}+\beta\bigl(B-{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{B}\right\rangle}\bigr)^{2}\Bigr)\,{\left|{\ }\right\rangle}={\left|{\ }\right\rangle}\,\bigl(\alpha\var(A)+\beta\var(B)\bigr)

are real Lagrange parameters.

question (i); among them are the pairs on the border of the accessible region,
the answer to the central question.

8
Convex hull

The smallest eigenvalues for
\alpha,\beta>0
in Eq. (
28
) provide

u(\alpha,\beta)=\Min_{\rho}\bigl\{\alpha\var(A)+\beta\var(B)\bigr\}\,,

(29)

where the minimization is over all statistical operators
\rho
.
We note that, owing to property
U3
, it is enough to consider pure states

{\rho={\left|{\ }\right\rangle}{\left\langle{\ }\right|}}
for the minimization in Eq. (
29
);
and that all pairs
(x,y)
with
{\alpha x+\beta y\geq u(\alpha,\beta)}
are in
the (lower) convex hull of the region occupied by the physical

{(x,y)=\bigl(\var(A),\var(B)\bigr)}
pairs.

More specifically, a pair
(x,y)
is on the border of the convex hull if

x=\Max_{\alpha,\beta>0}\biggl\{\frac{u(\alpha,\beta)-\beta y}{\alpha}\biggr\}\quad\mbox{or}\quad y=\Max_{\alpha,\beta>0}\biggl\{\frac{u(\alpha,\beta)-\alpha x}{\beta}\biggr\}\,.

(30)

Since
u(\alpha,\beta)
is homogeneous,

u(\alpha,\beta)=\alpha u(1,\beta/\alpha)=\beta u(\alpha/\beta,1)\,,

(31)

Eq. (
30
) are essentially statements of a Legendre transformation if
the partial derivatives are defined.
If so, then

x=\frac{\partial u(\alpha,\beta)}{\partial\alpha}\,,\quad y=\frac{\partial u(\alpha,\beta)}{\partial\beta}

(32)

give a pair on the border of the convex hull.
Alternatively, one recognizes an application of the Hellmann–Feynman theorem
in Eq. (
32
).

We further note that establishing
{u(\alpha,\beta)>0}
for just one

\alpha,\beta
pair implies that there is a finite-size rectangular triangle
with the right angle at
{(x,y)=(0,0)}
that contains no

\bigl(\var(A),\var(B)\bigr)
pairs.
This demonstrates the uncertainty principle (in the sense of Sec.
5
)
for the
A,B
pair under consideration.

9.1
Position and momentum — a singularity

For the Heisenberg pair of
{(A,B)=(Q,P)}

\bigl(\var(Q),\var(P)\bigr)

\bigl(\ssd(Q),\ssd(P)\bigr)

Equation (

and we find

\var(Q)\var(P)=\frac{1}{4}\hbar^{2}(2n+1)^{2}\quad\mbox{with $n=0,1,2,\dots$}

(33)

The minimization in Eq. (
29

(34)

The physical region is convex here; its border is also the border of the convex
hull.
On the border, we have
{n=0}
in Eq. (
33

(
22

This is the singular situation in which the Robertson inequality is the
Kennard inequality, and the Robertson states identify the border.

To appreciate this singularity, we note that the ket of a Robertson state, a
solution of Eq. (
24

\Bigl(\mathopen{\boldsymbol{|}}a\mathclose{\boldsymbol{|}}^{2}\bigl(A-{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{A}\right\rangle}\bigr)^{2}+\mathopen{\boldsymbol{|}}b\mathclose{\boldsymbol{|}}^{2}\bigl(B-{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{B}\right\rangle}\bigr)^{2}\Bigr)\,{\left|{\ }\right\rangle}=-\mathrm{i}[A,B]\,{\left|{\ }\right\rangle}\Imag{\left({a^{*}b}\right)}\,,

(35)

which differs in structure from Eq. (
28

\mathrm{i}[A,B]
is a
number (multiple of the identity).
This is the case only for Heisenberg pairs of the position-momentum kind.

Qutrit — complementary pair of unitary observables

Two components of the angular momentum vector operator are not complementary

Instead, a natural pair of complementary observables for the qutrit are Weyl’s

3
unitary operators
U
and
V

[
24

32

with the matrix representations

U\mathbin{\hat{=}}{\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\
0&\ \mathrm{e}^{\mbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{i}2\pi/3$}}&0\\
0&0&\mathrm{e}^{\mbox{\footnotesize$-\mathrm{i}2\pi/3$}}\end{array}\right)}\,,\quad V\mathbin{\hat{=}}{\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&1&0\\

\mathrm{e}^{\mbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{i}2\pi/3$}}

smallest probabilities in the trio

for

(47)

and analogously for
V
; the factor of
\frac{3}{2}
is for normalization:

{0\leq\ssd(U)\leq 1}
.

9.6
Period-
N
Weyl pairs

More generally than the period-2 pair
\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y}
for the qubit and
the period-3 pair
U,V
for the qutrit, we have the Weyl pair of complementary
period-
N
unitary operators
U,V
for a
N
-dimensional degree of freedom

[
24
,
32
]
.
The two orthonormal eigenstate bases are unbiased,

{\left\langle{u_{k}}{|}{v_{l}}\right\rangle}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathrm{e}^{\mbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{i}2\pi kl/N$}}\,,

(50)

where
{u_{k}=\mathrm{e}^{\mbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{i}2\pi k/N$}}}
and
{v_{l}=\mathrm{e}^{\mbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{i}2\pi l/N$}}}
are the eigenvalues
with
{k,l=0,1,2\dots,N-1}
.
Then

c\bigl(u_{k},u_{k^{\prime}}\bigr)=\frac{N}{N-1}\bigl(1-\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}\bigr)\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ c\bigl(v_{l},v_{l^{\prime}}\bigr)=\frac{N}{N-1}\bigl(1-\delta_{l,l^{\prime}}\bigr)

(51)

specify particularly simple Monge-type costs,

\mtc(U)=\frac{N}{N-1}\Bigl(1-\Max_{k}\Bigl\{{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{\delta(U,u_{k})}\right\rangle}\Bigr\}\Bigr)

(52)

and analogously for
\mtc(V)
.
This is an example of an uncertainty measure that reaches its largest value
for the uniform distribution only.
For
{N=2}
, this gives
\frac{1}{2}\ssd(\sigma_{x})
and
\frac{1}{2}\ssd(\sigma_{y})
of
Sec.
9.4
;
for
{N=3}
, it gives
\ssd(U)
and
\ssd(V)
of Sec.
9.5
.
For any
N
value, we have a convex set of physical

(x,y)=\bigl(\mtc(U),\mtc(V)\bigr)

u(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{N}{N-1}{\left(\alpha+\beta-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}-\frac{2N-4}{N}\alpha\beta}\,\right)}\,.

(53)

This border is the arc of the ellipse

\frac{(N-1)^{2}}{N}\biggl(\frac{N}{N-1}-x-y\biggr)^{2}+\frac{N-1}{N}(x-y)^{2}=1

(54)

that connects
(x,y)=(0,1)
and
(x,y)=(1,0)
; see Sec. 6.2 in

[
7
]
.
For
N=2
, it is the quarter circle
{(1-x)^{2}+(1-y)^{2}=1}
(cf. Fig.
3

{N\to\infty}
, it is the straight line

{x+y=1}
.

9.7
Rotor variables

The quantum rotor has a periodic position (azimuth) with bras

{{\left\langle{\varphi}\right|}={\left\langle{\varphi+2\pi}\right|}}

that we picture as points on a circle, and a discrete momentum (orbital
angular momentum for the symmetry axis of the circle) with kets
{\left|{\ell}\right\rangle}

for
{\ell=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\dots}
;
the continuous
\varphi
basis and the discrete
\ell
basis are unbiased

[
33
, Sec. 1.1.9]
,

{\left\langle{\varphi}{|}{\ell}\right\rangle}=\mathrm{e}^{\mbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{i}\ell\varphi$}}\,.

(55)

We associate a unitary observable
E
with the quantum number
\varphi
and a
hermitian observable
L
with the quantum number
\ell
,

{\left\langle{\varphi}\right|}E=\mathrm{e}^{\mbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{i}\varphi$}}{\left\langle{\varphi}\right|}\,,\quad L{\left|{\ell}\right\rangle}={\left|{\ell}\right\rangle}\ell\,.

(56)

We use
\ssd(L)
of Eq. (
4
) for
L
and, in analogy with
Sec.
9.5
, we measure the uncertainty of
E
by the smallest
squared distance that goes with the chordal distance between points on the
circle,

\ssd(E)=\Min_{\varphi}\Bigl\{1-\Real{\left({{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{E}\right\rangle}\mathrm{e}^{\mbox{\footnotesize$-\mathrm{i}\varphi$}}}\right)}\Bigr\}=1-\mathopen{\boldsymbol{|}}{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{E}\right\rangle}\mathclose{\boldsymbol{|}}\,,

(57)

scaled such that
{0\leq\ssd(E)\leq 1}
.
This is a Monge-type cost with

{c\Bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\mbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{i}\varphi$}},\mathrm{e}^{\mbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{i}\varphi^{\prime}$}}\Bigr)=1-\cos(\varphi-\varphi^{\prime})}
in
Eq. (
5
);
it is another example of an uncertainty measure that is largest for
the uniform distribution, but there are other distributions as well for which

{{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{E}\right\rangle}=0}
.

The stationary uncertainty states are solutions of

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\Biggl(\alpha\biggl(1-\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}\bigl(E^{\dagger}\mathrm{e}^{\mbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{i}\phi$}}+E\mathrm{e}^{\mbox{\footnotesize$-\mathrm{i}\phi$}}\bigr)\biggr)+\beta(L-l)^{2}\Biggr)\,{\left|{\ }\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left|{\ }\right\rangle}\,\bigl(\alpha\ssd(E)+\beta\ssd(L)\bigr)\,,

(58)

where
{{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{E}\right\rangle}=\mathopen{\boldsymbol{|}}{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{E}\right\rangle}\mathclose{\boldsymbol{|}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{i}\phi$}}}
and
l
is the integer
nearest to
{\left\langle\vphantom{|}{L}\right\rangle}
.
The periodic wave function

{\psi(\varphi)={\left\langle{\varphi}{|}{\ }\right\rangle}=\psi(\varphi+2\pi)}
obeys a
Mathieu-type equation (see
C
), from which we obtain

u(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{4}\beta\be_{0}(8\alpha/\beta)\,,

(59)

where
\be_{0}(\ )
is the zeroth characteristic value of the Mathieu equation
with the notational conventions of
[
34
]
.
This gives the blue border of the convex set of the physical

{\bigl(\ssd(E),\ssd(L)\bigr)}
pairs in Fig.
6
.
The other stationary values are located on the lines associated with

u_{n}(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{4}\beta\be_{n}(8\alpha/\beta)\quad\mbox{with $n=1,2,3,\dots$}

(60)

in analogy with Eqs. (
30
) and (
32
).
Similar to Eq. (
33

10
Summary

We recalled the early history of uncertainty relations, and traced the
widespread use of variances as measures of uncertainty to Kennard’s
mathematization of Heisenberg’s observation.
The unified approach by which we derived all standard uncertainty relations
can be taught in undergraduate courses.

Further, we noted that calling the states that saturate Robertson’s inequality
“minimum uncertainty states” is misleading as the challenge of minimizing
the variance of one observable, given the variance of another, is met by other
states.
We also discussed other measures of uncertainty, in particular the smallest
squared distance and the Monge-type cost.
The position-momentum pair of linear motion, the qubit,
the spin-1 angular momentum, the qutrit, the Weyl pairs
of period-
N
observables, and the rotor provided illustrating examples,
which — so we hope — will be useful when teaching quantum mechanics.
