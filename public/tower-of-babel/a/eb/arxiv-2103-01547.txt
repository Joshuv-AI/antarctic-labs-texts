Tachyonic and Plasma Instabilities of η \eta -Pairing States Coupled to Electromagnetic Fields
Naoto Tsuji, Masaya Nakagawa, Masahito Ueda

Abstract
Cooper pairs featuring a nonzero center-of-mass crystal momentum 𝑸 = ( π , π , … ) \boldsymbol{Q}=(\pi,\pi,\dots) and an off-diagonal long-range order ( η \eta -pairing states) constitute exact eigenstates of a Hubbard model [C. N. Yang, Phys. Rev. Lett. 63 , 2144 (1989)]. Here we show that the η \eta -pairing states are rendered dynamically unstable via coupling to dynamical electromagnetic fields. The instability is caused by “tachyonic” electromagnetic fields and unstable plasma modes for attractive and repulsive interactions, respectively. The typical time scale of the growth of the instability is of the order of femtoseconds for electron systems in solids, which places a strict bound on the lifetime of the η \eta -pairing states. The decay of the η \eta -pairing states leads to enhanced light emission with frequencies shifted from the Hubbard interaction strength for the repulsive case and unattenuated electromagnetic penetration for the attractive case.



Introduction.
—
A search for long-lived non-thermal excited states that support
macroscopic long-range order
is a modern challenge in nonequilibrium condensed matter physics.
If such a state exists, a rich variety of possibilities arises
that could extend the landscape of long-range order
in solid-state materials.
In fact, there have been a number of experimental reports on the possible existence
of nonequilibrium long-range order,
including light-induced
Fausti et al. 2011
;
Kaiser et al. 2014
;
Hu et al. 2014
;
Mitrano et al. 2016
;
Cantaluppi et al. 2018
;
Buzzi et al. 2020
;
Bud
or quench-induced
Oike et al. 2018
superconductivity (see also
Niwa et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2020
), photoinduced ferromagnetism
Matsubara et al. 2007
;
McLeod et al. 2020

and charge density waves
Stojchevska et al. 2014
;
Vaskivskyi et al. 2015
;
Sun et al. 2018
.
While the experimental progress is underway, the theoretical understanding
is yet to be made.
A major challenge is that an analysis of excited states in quantum many-body systems often requires approximations
that render the conclusion on the existence of such a state highly nontrivial.

A very exception to this situation is the
\eta
-pairing states,
which are known to be exact eigenstates of a Hubbard model
as revealed by C. N. Yang
Yang 1989
.
The
\eta
-pairing states exhibit a number of remarkable features.
In particular, they have an off-diagonal long-range order (ODLRO) in arbitrary dimensions
even though their eigenenergies lie much higher than the ground state.
This is to be contrasted
with finite-temperature thermal states, which cannot show ODLROs in one and two dimensions
due to the Mermin-Wagner theorem. The non-thermal nature of the
\eta
-pairing states has also been discussed recently
in the context of quantum many-body scars
Vafek et al. 2017
;
Mark and Motrunich 2020
;
Moudgalya et al. 2020
.

The presence of such a non-thermal state suggests that the
\eta
-pairing states
with an ODLRO
(and hence superconductivity) might be realized in nonequilibrium situations. Recent theoretical studies have
demonstrated that this is indeed possible in several different setups, including
periodic
Kitamura and Aoki 2016
;
Peronaci et al. 2020
;
Cook and Clark 2020
;
Tindall et al. 2021
and
pulsed
Kaneko et al. 2019
;
Kaneko et al. 2020
;
Werner et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2020
electric-field drives,
dissipation engineering
Diehl et al. 2008
;
Kraus et al. 2008
,
spin-dependent dephasing
Bernier et al. 2013
;
Tindall et al. 2019
,
and spontaneous light emission
Nak
.
These mechanisms will work for the Hubbard model with or without coupling to an external bath,
which may be realized in electrically neutral ultracold atoms trapped in an optical lattice.

In view of applications to real materials,
one cannot ignore the coupling of electrons to
dynamical electromagnetic fields
, since electrons have electric charges.
This point is crucial for the stability of the
\eta
-pairing states supported by the long lifetime of doublons.
If doublons decay into single particles or lose their momenta, they induce local electric currents due to charge transfer,
which then generate dynamical electromagnetic fields.
The effect of the latter feedbacks to electrons, and causes collective modes of electromagnetic fields,
which accelerate the relaxation of doublons.
Such a dynamical instability
deserves careful scrutiny in view of growing attention in nonequilibrium superconductivity.

In this Letter, we study the dynamics of the
\eta
-pairing states in the Hubbard model coupled to dynamical electromagnetic fields.
Our approach is based on the exact solution of the electromagnetic response function (or the Meissner kernel)

K^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

with full momentum (
\boldsymbol{q}
) and frequency (
\omega
) dependences.
In contrast, previous studies have focused on the static and uniform limit (i.e.,
\boldsymbol{q}=\omega=0
)
Su et al. 1991
;
Su et al. 1992
;
Kaneko et al. 2020
.
As we will see, the momentum and frequency dependences play a pivotal role in dynamical instabilities of
\eta
-pairing states.
Combining the obtained results with the Maxwell equations,
we rigorously prove the existence of the “tachyonic” and plasma instabilities for attractively and repulsively interacting systems,
respectively. The time scale of the growth of the instability is surprisingly short,
being of the order of femtoseconds or even shorter than that for ordinary materials.
This puts a severe constraint on the lifetime of the
\eta
-pairing states in electron systems.
Finally, we discuss that
the decay of the
\eta
-pairing states leads to intense light emission with frequencies shifted from the interaction strength in the repulsive case,
and unattenuated penetration of electromagnetic fields in the attractive case.

\eta
pairing in the Hubbard model.—

We consider the Hubbard model on a
d
-dimensional cubic lattice subject to the periodic boundary condition with the Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=-t_{h}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma}(c_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{j\sigma}+\mbox{H.c.})+U\sum_{i}n_{i\uparrow}n_{i\downarrow}-\frac{U}{2}\sum_{i\sigma}n_{i\sigma},

(1)

where
t_{h}
(
>0
) is the hopping amplitude,
c_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}
is a creation operator of an electron
at site
i
with spin
\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow
,

\langle ij\rangle
represents a pair of nearest-neighbor lattice sites,

U
is the on-site interaction strength,
and
n_{i\sigma}=c_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{i\sigma}
is the particle-number operator.
Since we fix the total number of electrons throughout this Letter, the last term in Eq. (
1

We set the lattice constant
a=1
and the Planck constant
\hbar=1
unless otherwise noted.

The Hubbard model (
1
) has the spin SU(2) symmetry
together with the “hidden”
\eta
SU(2) symmetry,
which altogether form the symmetry of
{\rm SU(2)}\times{\rm SU(2)}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}\simeq{\rm SO(4)}

Yang and Zhang 1990
.
The existence of
\eta
-pairing states as the exact eigenstates of the Hubbard model essentially relies on
this fact. To see the
\eta
symmetry, we define the
\eta
operators,

\eta^{+}:=\sum_{j}e^{i\boldsymbol{Q}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}c_{j\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{j\downarrow}^{\dagger}
,

\eta^{-}:=(\eta^{+})^{\dagger}
, and

\eta^{z}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}(n_{j\uparrow}+n_{j\downarrow}-1)
,
where
\boldsymbol{Q}=(\pi,\pi,\dots)
is the momentum at the Brillouin-zone corner,
and
\boldsymbol{R}_{j}
is the position vector of lattice site
j
. The
\eta
operators satisfy the ordinary su(2) algebra,
i.e.,
[\eta^{+},\eta^{-}]=2\eta^{z}
and
[\eta^{z},\eta^{\pm}]=\pm\eta^{\pm}
.
From direct calculations, one can confirm that they all commute with the Hamiltonian (
1

[H,\eta^{\alpha}]=0
(
\alpha=\pm,z
).

Using the
\eta
operators, one can construct Yang’s
\eta
-pairing states.
The simplest one is

\displaystyle|\psi_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{N}}}(\eta^{+})^{\frac{N}{2}}|0\rangle,

(2)

where
N
is the number of electrons which is assumed to be an even integer,

\mathcal{N}_{N}
is the normalization constant (such that
\langle\psi_{N}|\psi_{N}\rangle=1
),
and
|0\rangle
is the vacuum state.
The
\eta
-pairing state

consists of
\frac{N}{2}
doublons having momentum
\boldsymbol{Q}
.
Since
\eta^{+}
commutes with
H
(
1
),

|\psi_{N}\rangle
(
2
) is indeed the exact eigenstate of
H
with the eigenenergy
E_{N}=0

in arbitrary dimensions.
The state
|\psi_{N}\rangle
(
2
) has the ODLRO

\frac{1}{2}\langle\psi_{N}|(c_{i\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{i\downarrow}^{\dagger}c_{j\downarrow}c_{j\uparrow}+\mbox{H.c.})|\psi_{N}\rangle=e^{i\boldsymbol{Q}\cdot(\boldsymbol{R}_{i}-\boldsymbol{R}_{j})}C_{M,N}

Yang 1989

(
C_{M,N}:=\frac{\frac{N}{2}(M-\frac{N}{2})}{M(M-1)}
and
M
is the number of lattice sites),
which saturates the upper bound of ODLRO
Yang 1962
;
Nak
.
Physically,
|\psi_{N}\rangle
(
2
) corresponds to the condensate of
spin-singlet Cooper pairs with the center-of-mass momentum
\boldsymbol{Q}
.

Electromagnetic response of
\eta
-pairing states.—

We study the electromagnetic response of the
\eta
-pairing state
|\psi_{N}\rangle
(
2
)
within the linear-response regime. We focus on the three-dimensional case (
d=3
).
However, most of the results in the present Letter can straightforwardly be extended to other dimensions.
The response of the current against an external electromagnetic field with momentum
\boldsymbol{q}
and frequency
\omega
is given by

j^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)=-K^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)A_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
(
\mu,\nu=x,y,z
),
where
K^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
is the Meissner kernel
Schrieffer 1983

and
A_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
is the vector potential.

In general, the kernel
K^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
consists of the paramagnetic and diamagnetic components
Schrieffer 1983
.
In the case of
\eta
-pairing states, the diamagnetic component vanishes exactly, since it is proportional to
the kinetic energy
kin
,
which vanishes for the
\eta
-pairing states. This is in stark contrast to ordinary superconductors,
in which perfect diamagnetism arises from the diamagnetic component of the Meissner kernel.
In the
\eta
-pairing states, the paramagnetic component takes over the role of the diamagnetic one
in ordinary superconductors.

The paramagnetic component is given by the Kubo formula,

\displaystyle K_{\rm para}^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)

\displaystyle=-i\theta(t)\langle\psi_{N}|[J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t),J^{\nu}(0,0)]|\psi_{N}\rangle,

(3)

where
\theta(t)
is the unit-step function (
\theta(t)=1
for
t\geq 0
and
\theta(t)=0
otherwise),
and
J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)
is the local current operator at site
j
and time
t
in the Heisenberg picture.
The local current
J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},0)=J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j})
is expressed explicitly as

J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j})=-iet_{h}\sum_{\sigma}(c_{j+\mu,\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{j\sigma}-c_{j\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{j+\mu,\sigma})
,
where
e
is the electric charge, and
j+\mu
represents the nearest-neighbor site of
j
in the
\mu
direction.

We can evaluate Eq. (
3
) exactly for arbitrary
\boldsymbol{R}_{j}
and
t

using the following algebraic relations:

[J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}),\eta^{\pm}]=:\pm 2J_{\eta}^{\mu\pm}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j})
,

[J_{\eta}^{\mu\pm}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}),\eta^{\pm}]=0
, and

[J_{\eta}^{\mu\pm}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}),\eta^{\mp}]=\pm J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j})
.
They allow us to reduce the
N
-particle correlation function (
3
) to that of the vacuum state
sup
,

\displaystyle K_{\rm para}^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)

\displaystyle=-4i\theta(t)C_{M,N}[\langle 0|J_{\eta}^{\mu-}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(0,0)|0\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\langle 0|J_{\eta}^{\nu-}(0,0)J_{\eta}^{\mu+}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)|0\rangle].

(4)

In this way, the
N
-particle problem reduces to the two-particle problem, which is exactly solvable
Essler et al. 2005
.

We further decompose the kernel into the transverse and longitudinal components.
Without loss of generality, we assume that the momentum
\boldsymbol{q}
of the vector potential points in the
z
direction.
The transverse component is defined as
K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega):=K^{\mu\mu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
(
\mu=x,y
),
while the longitudinal one is
K^{\parallel}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega):=K^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
.
The kernel does not have the off-diagonal components (
K^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)=0
for
\mu\neq\nu
)
sup
.
In the two-particle dynamics involved in Eq. (
4
), the center-of-mass momentum
and the relative coordinates in the
x
and
y
directions of the two particles are conserved.
In addition, for the transverse components the two particles never sit at the same site, making the dynamics effectively noninteracting.
These observations lead us to an analytical solution for the transverse component
sup
,

\displaystyle K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=-8ie^{2}t_{h}C_{M,N}\left[f_{q}\big(\tfrac{\omega+U}{t_{h}}\big)-f_{q}\big(\tfrac{\omega-U}{t_{h}}\big)\right],

\displaystyle f_{q}(x)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\frac{i{\rm sgn}(x)}{\sqrt{x^{2}-16\sin^{2}\frac{q}{2}}}&{\rm for}\;|x|>4\sin\frac{q}{2};\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{16\sin^{2}\frac{q}{2}-x^{2}}}&{\rm for}\;|x|<4\sin\frac{q}{2},\end{cases}

(5)

where
q

(>0)
is the
z
component of
\boldsymbol{q}
.
The longitudinal component does not have such a compact expression, but can be evaluated exactly
in a similar manner
sup
.

The exact solution (
5

reveals a number of important properties of the
\eta
-pairing states.
By taking the limit
\lim_{\boldsymbol{q}\to 0}\lim_{\omega\to 0}K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)=\frac{16e^{2}t_{h}^{2}}{U}C_{M,N}=:\frac{1}{\pi}D_{s}

Dynamical instability of
\eta
-pairing states.—

Now, let us study the dynamics of electromagnetic fields coupled to the
\eta
-pairing states for
U>0
.
To this end, we consider the Maxwell equation in the Lorenz gauge,

-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}\boldsymbol{A}+q^{2}\boldsymbol{A}=\mu_{0}\boldsymbol{j}

-pairing states,

j^{\mu}=-K^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)A_{\nu}

-pairing states.
We focus on the transverse mode, whose energy dispersion is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}-q^{2}

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega).

Summary and outlook.—

We have shown that Yang’s
\eta
-pairing states have the intrinsic plasma instability for
U>0
and the tachyonic instability for
U<0

when the system is coupled to electromagnetic fields.
The time scales of both of these instabilities are of the order of femtoseconds,
which puts a strong constraint on the realization of the
\eta
-pairing states in real materials.
The decay of the
\eta
-pairing states leads to enhanced light emission with characteristic frequencies
shifted from the Hubbard interaction
U
for the repulsive case, and unattenuated penetration of electromagnetic fields for the attractive case.
While we have focused on the simplest form of the
\eta
-pairing eigenstates (
2
),
we expect that similar instabilities might exist for more general states having unpaired particles (at least if they are dilute enough).
Stabilizing the
\eta
-pairing states coupled to electromagnetic fields is an interesting open problem,
which merits further studies.

I
I. Derivation of the electromagnetic response function

In this section, we describe how to analytically evaluate the electromagnetic response function (Meissner kernel)
[Eq. (
3
) in the main text],

\displaystyle K^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)

\displaystyle=-i\theta(t)\langle\psi_{N}|[J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t),J^{\nu}(0,0)]|\psi_{N}\rangle,

(S1)

at arbitrary lattice coordinate
\boldsymbol{R}_{j}
and time
t

for Yang’s
\eta
-pairing state
|\psi_{N}\rangle
[Eq. (
4
)] in the Hubbard model.

I.1
A. Reduction to the two-particle correlation function

The first step is to reduce the correlation function of
N
particles (
S1
) to
that of two particles by shifting all the
\eta
operators in
|\psi_{N}\rangle

to the left of the current operators using the commutation relation

\displaystyle[J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}),\eta^{+}]

\displaystyle=-2iet_{h}e^{i\boldsymbol{Q}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}(c_{j+\mu\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{j\downarrow}^{\dagger}+c_{j\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{j+\mu\downarrow}^{\dagger}).

(S2)

For convenience, we define the operator that appeared on the right-hand side of Eq. (
S2
) as

\displaystyle J_{\eta}^{\mu+}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j})

\displaystyle:=-iet_{h}e^{i\boldsymbol{Q}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}(c_{j+\mu\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{j\downarrow}^{\dagger}+c_{j\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{j+\mu\downarrow}^{\dagger}).

(S3)

With this definition, we can write

\displaystyle[J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}),\eta^{+}]

\displaystyle=2J_{\eta}^{\mu+}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}).

(S4)

Since
J_{\eta}^{\mu+}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j})
only involves creation operators,
J_{\eta}^{\mu+}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j})
commutes with
\eta^{+}
.
Therefore, we can repeatedly use the relation (
S4
) to obtain

\displaystyle[J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}),(\eta^{+})^{n}]

\displaystyle=2n(\eta^{+})^{n-1}J_{\eta}^{\mu+}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j})

(S5)

for
n=1,2,\dots
. By using Eq. (
S5
),
we can evaluate the current-current correlation function as

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{N}|J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J^{\nu}(0,0)|\psi_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{N}}}\langle\psi_{N}|J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J^{\nu}(0,0)(\eta^{+})^{\frac{N}{2}}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{N}}}N\langle\psi_{N}|J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)(\eta^{+})^{\frac{N}{2}-1}J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(0)|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{N}}}N\langle\psi_{N}|(\eta^{+})^{\frac{N}{2}-1}J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(0)|0\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{N}}}N(N-2)\langle\psi_{N}|(\eta^{+})^{\frac{N}{2}-2}J_{\eta}^{\mu+}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(0)|0\rangle.

(S6)

Then, straightforward calculations show that

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{N}|(\eta^{+})^{\frac{N}{2}-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{N}}}\frac{(\frac{N}{2})!(M-1)!}{(M-\frac{N}{2})!}\langle 0|\eta^{-},

(S7)

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{N}|(\eta^{+})^{\frac{N}{2}-2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{N}}}\frac{(\frac{N}{2})!(M-2)!}{2(M-\frac{N}{2})!}\langle 0|(\eta^{-})^{2},

(S8)

which can be used to rewrite the correlation function (
S6
) as

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{N}|J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J^{\nu}(0,0)|\psi_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{\mathcal{N}_{N}}\frac{(\frac{N}{2})!(M-1)!}{(M-\frac{N}{2})!}\langle 0|\eta^{-}J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(0)|0\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{N(N-2)}{\mathcal{N}_{N}}\frac{(\frac{N}{2})!(M-2)!}{2(M-\frac{N}{2})!}\langle 0|(\eta^{-})^{2}J_{\eta}^{\mu+}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(0)|0\rangle.

(S9)

To further simplify the expression, we use the commutation relations,

\displaystyle[\eta^{-},J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j})]

\displaystyle=2J_{\eta}^{\mu-}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}),

(S10)

\displaystyle[\eta^{-},J_{\eta}^{\mu+}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j})]

\displaystyle=-J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}),

(S11)

where
J_{\eta}^{\mu-}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}):=[J_{\eta}^{\mu+}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j})]^{\dagger}
.
Thus, the correlation function becomes

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{N}|J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J^{\nu}(0,0)|\psi_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2N}{\mathcal{N}_{N}}\frac{(\frac{N}{2})!(M-1)!}{(M-\frac{N}{2})!}\langle 0|J_{\eta}^{\mu-}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(0)|0\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{N(N-2)}{\mathcal{N}_{N}}\frac{(\frac{N}{2})!(M-2)!}{2(M-\frac{N}{2})!}\langle 0|\eta^{-}J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(0)|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2N(\frac{N}{2})!(M-2)!}{(M-\frac{N}{2}-1)!\mathcal{N}_{N}}\langle 0|J_{\eta}^{\mu-}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(0)|0\rangle.

(S12)

Here, let us recall that the normalization constant for the
\eta
-pairing state is explicitly given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\frac{N}{2})!M!}{(M-\frac{N}{2})!}.

(S13)

We use Eq. (
S12
) to reduce the current-current correlation function of
N
particles to that of two particles,

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{N}|J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J^{\nu}(0,0)|\psi_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=4C_{M,N}\langle 0|J_{\eta}^{\mu-}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(0)|0\rangle,

(S14)

where

\displaystyle C_{M,N}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\frac{N}{2}(M-\frac{N}{2})}{M(M-1)}.

(S15)

Equation (
S14
) leads to Eq. (
4
) in the main text.

The technique used here (i.e., reduction of
N
-particle to few-particle correlation functions) can be applied
not only to the electromagnetic response function (
S1
) but also to arbitrary correlation functions
constructed from few-body operators.

I.2
B. Evaluation of the two-particle dynamics

In the previous subsection, we have shown that the
N
-particle correlation function (
S1
)
can be reduced to the two-particle correlation function (
S14
).
Since the two-particle problem in the Hubbard model is exactly solvable, we can evaluate the two-particle correlation function exactly.
Here we describe the details of the evaluation.

First, we Fourier transform Eq. (
S14
) to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j}e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}\langle\psi_{N}|J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J^{\nu}(0,0)|\psi_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{M}C_{M,N}\langle 0|J_{\eta}^{\mu-}(\boldsymbol{q},t)J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(\boldsymbol{q})|0\rangle,

(S16)

where

\displaystyle J_{\eta}^{\mu+}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j}e^{-i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}J_{\eta}^{\mu+}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}),

(S17)

\displaystyle J_{\eta}^{\mu-}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle:=[J_{\eta}^{\mu+}(\boldsymbol{q})]^{\dagger}.

(S18)

Acting
J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(\boldsymbol{q})
on the vacuum state, we obtain

\displaystyle J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(\boldsymbol{q})|0\rangle=-iet_{h}\sum_{j}e^{i(\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q})\cdot\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}(c_{j+\nu\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{j\downarrow}^{\dagger}+c_{j\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{j+\nu\downarrow}^{\dagger})|0\rangle

\displaystyle=-iet_{h}\sum_{j}e^{i(\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q})\cdot\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}(|\boldsymbol{R}_{j}+\boldsymbol{e}_{\nu},\boldsymbol{R}_{j}\rangle+|\boldsymbol{R}_{j},\boldsymbol{R}_{j}+\boldsymbol{e}_{\nu}\rangle),

(S19)

where we have introduced the notation
|\boldsymbol{R}_{j},\boldsymbol{R}_{k}\rangle:=c_{j\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{k\downarrow}^{\dagger}|0\rangle

to represent a two-particle state with
\uparrow
spin at site
j
and
\downarrow
spin at site
k
,
and
\boldsymbol{e}_{\nu}
is the unit vector in the
\nu
direction.

Since the system has the (discrete) translation symmetry, the center-of-mass (crystal) momentum of two particles under consideration is conserved.
Let us define the translation operator
T_{\mu}
that shifts two particles by one lattice site in the
\mu
direction.
We express the eigenstates of
T_{\mu}
in terms of the center-of-mass momentum
\boldsymbol{K}
and the relative coordinate
\boldsymbol{r}

of the two particles as

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{K},\boldsymbol{r}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{j}e^{i\boldsymbol{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}|\boldsymbol{R}_{j}+\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{R}_{j}\rangle,

(S20)

which satisfies
T_{\mu}|\boldsymbol{K},\boldsymbol{r}\rangle=e^{-i\boldsymbol{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}}|\boldsymbol{K},\boldsymbol{r}\rangle
.
Using the eigenstates (
S20
),
J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(\boldsymbol{q})|0\rangle
(
S19
) can be written as

\displaystyle J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(\boldsymbol{q})|0\rangle

\displaystyle=-iet_{h}\sqrt{M}(|\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q},+\boldsymbol{e}_{\nu}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+e^{-i(\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q})\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\nu}}|\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q},-\boldsymbol{e}_{\nu}\rangle),

(S21)

which has the center-of-mass momentum
\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q}
.
The action of the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) in the main text on the state
|\boldsymbol{K},\boldsymbol{r}\rangle
(
S20
) is given by

\displaystyle H|\boldsymbol{K},\boldsymbol{r}\rangle

\displaystyle=-t_{h}\sum_{\mu}[(1+e^{i\boldsymbol{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}})|\boldsymbol{K},\boldsymbol{r}+\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+(1+e^{-i\boldsymbol{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}})|\boldsymbol{K},\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}\rangle]

\displaystyle\quad+U(\delta_{\boldsymbol{r},0}-1)|\boldsymbol{K},\boldsymbol{r}\rangle.

(S22)

If we define operators
\Delta_{\mu}^{\pm}
that shift the relative coordinate of two particles by
\pm\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}
,
the Hamiltonian can be represented in the Hilbert subspace of two particles with the center-of-mass momentum
\boldsymbol{K}
as

\displaystyle H(\boldsymbol{K})

\displaystyle=-t_{h}\sum_{\mu}[(1+e^{i\boldsymbol{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}})\Delta_{\mu}^{+}+(1+e^{-i\boldsymbol{K}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}})\Delta_{\mu}^{-}]

\displaystyle\quad+U(\delta_{\boldsymbol{r},0}-1).

(S23)

Using the representation (
S23
), the correlation function (
S16
) can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{j}e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}\langle\psi_{N}|J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J^{\nu}(0,0)|\psi_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{M}C_{M,N}\langle 0|J_{\eta}^{\mu-}(\boldsymbol{q})e^{-iH(\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q})t}J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(\boldsymbol{q})|0\rangle.

(S24)

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\boldsymbol{q}
is parallel to the
z
direction [
\boldsymbol{q}=(0,0,q)
].
Then,

\displaystyle H(\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=-t_{h}[(1-e^{-iq})\Delta_{z}^{+}+(1-e^{iq})\Delta_{z}^{-}]

\displaystyle\quad+U(\delta_{\boldsymbol{r},0}-1).

(S25)

Hence, the two-particle dynamics that we have to consider is essentially a one-dimensional problem,
in which the relative coordinates
r_{x}
and
r_{y}
are conserved.
Below, we decompose the electromagnetic response function into the transverse component

K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega):=K^{\mu\mu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
(
\mu=x,y
)
and the longitudinal one
K^{\parallel}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega):=K^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
.
The off-diagonal components are absent, i.e.,
K^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)=0
for
\mu\neq\nu
,
since the state
e^{-iH(\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q})t}J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(\boldsymbol{q})|0\rangle
never has an overlap
with the state
J_{\eta}^{\mu+}|0\rangle
for
\mu\neq\nu
.

I.3
C. Transverse component

For the transverse component, the two-particle state
J_{\eta}^{\nu+}(\boldsymbol{q})|0\rangle
(
S21
)
has the relative coordinates,
(r_{x},r_{y})\neq(0,0)
. Since
r_{x}
and
r_{y}
are conserved during the time evolution,
the two particles do not sit on the same site. Therefore, they do not interact with each other, and the dynamics
becomes effectively noninteracting. The correlation function (
S16
) now reads

\displaystyle\sum_{j}e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}\langle\psi_{N}|J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J^{\mu}(0,0)|\psi_{N}\rangle\quad(\mu=x,y)

\displaystyle=4e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}\sum_{s=\pm}\langle\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q},s\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}|e^{-iH(\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q})t}|\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q},s\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}\rangle,

(S26)

where

\displaystyle H(\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=-t_{h}[(1-e^{-iq})\Delta_{z}^{+}+(1-e^{iq})\Delta_{z}^{-}]-U

(S27)

is the noninteracting Hamiltonian which can be diagonalized by Fourier transformation
with respect to the relative coordinate
r_{z}
. The result is

\displaystyle\sum_{j}e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}\langle\psi_{N}|J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J^{\mu}(0,0)|\psi_{N}\rangle\quad(\mu=x,y)

\displaystyle=8e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}\frac{e^{iUt}}{M}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}e^{2it_{h}(\cos k_{z}-\cos(k_{z}+q))t}

\displaystyle=8e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}e^{iUt}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{dk_{z}}{2\pi}e^{2it_{h}(\cos k_{z}-\cos(k_{z}+q))t}

\displaystyle=8e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}e^{iUt}J_{0}\left(4t_{h}\sin\frac{q}{2}t\right),

(S28)

where
J_{0}(x)
is the zeroth-order Bessel function of the first kind.
We thus obtain the transverse component of the electromagnetic response function as

\displaystyle K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},t)

\displaystyle=-8i\theta(t)e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}(e^{iUt}-e^{-iUt})

\displaystyle\quad\times J_{0}\left(4t_{h}\sin\frac{q}{2}t\right).

(S29)

Using the integral formula for the Bessel function (
a>0
),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dte^{i\omega t}J_{0}(at)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{i{\rm sgn}(\omega)}{\sqrt{\omega^{2}-a^{2}}}&\mbox{for}\;|\omega|>a;\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^{2}-\omega^{2}}}&\mbox{for}\;|\omega|<a,\end{cases}

(S30)

we obtain

\displaystyle K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)=-8ie^{2}t_{h}C_{M,N}\left[f_{q}\left(\frac{\omega+U}{t_{h}}\right)-f_{q}\left(\frac{\omega-U}{t_{h}}\right)\right],

(S31)

where

\displaystyle f_{q}(x)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\frac{i{\rm sgn}(x)}{\sqrt{x^{2}-16\sin^{2}\frac{q}{2}}}&\mbox{for}\;|x|>4\sin\frac{q}{2};\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{16\sin^{2}\frac{q}{2}-x^{2}}}&\mbox{for}\;|x|<4\sin\frac{q}{2}.\end{cases}

(S32)

This is the final result for the transverse component in Eq. (
5
) in the main text.

Transverse component
K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
of the electromagnetic response function
for the
\eta
-pairing states in the Hubbard model in units of
4e^{2}t_{h}C_{M,N}
.
(a), (b): Real (a) and imaginary (b) parts of
K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
for

.
(c), (d): Real (c) and imaginary (d) parts of

for

.

In Fig.
S1
, we plot
K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
for
U/t_{h}=2
and
6
.
The imaginary part of
K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
represents the absorption (emission) of light for the
\eta
-pairing states.
When a doublon decays by emitting light with momentum
\boldsymbol{q}
, quasiparticles with momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
and
-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q}

are created. As discussed above, these particles never interact with each other, so that the total energy of the two particles is given by

-2t_{h}\sum_{\mu}[(\cos k_{\mu})+\cos(-k_{\mu}+Q_{\mu}-q_{\mu})]=-4t_{h}\sin\frac{q}{2}\sin(k_{z}+\frac{q}{2})
.
The energy of quasiparticles ranges from
-4t_{h}\sin\frac{q}{2}
to
4t_{h}\sin\frac{q}{2}
.
Since the energy of a single doublon is
U
, the condition for light emission to take place is

|\omega-U|<4t_{h}\sin\frac{q}{2}
. This is exactly the condition of
{\rm Im}\,K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)>0
(for
U/t_{h}>4
).
The gap for the electromagnetic response function closes at
q=\pi
when
U/t_{h}=4
.

I.4
D. Asymptotic behavior at long distance

Here we derive the asymptotic behavior of the transverse electromagnetic response function (
S31
) at long distance
and low frequency,
which is related to Pippard’s coherence length.
In the low-frequency limit, the kernel is given by

\displaystyle K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega=0)

\displaystyle=16e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}\begin{cases}\frac{{\rm sgn}(U)}{\sqrt{U^{2}-16t_{h}^{2}\sin^{2}\frac{q}{2}}}&\mbox{for}\;|U|>4t_{h}\sin\frac{q}{2};\\
0&\mbox{for}\;|U|<4t_{h}\sin\frac{q}{2}.\end{cases}

(S33)

We consider the problem in two distinct regimes:
|U|>4t_{h}
and
|U|<4t_{h}
.

In the first case (
|U|>4t_{h}
),
the Fourier transform of
K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega=0)
(
S33
) to real space in the
z
direction reads

\displaystyle K^{\perp}(j,\omega=0)

\displaystyle=16e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{dq}{2\pi}e^{iqj}\frac{{\rm sgn}(U)}{\sqrt{U^{2}-16t_{h}^{2}\sin^{2}\frac{q}{2}}}.

(S34)

Precisely speaking, Eq. (
S34
) shows the electromagnetic response function
for
q_{x}=q_{y}=0
,
R_{z}=j
, and
\omega=0
. The asymptotic behavior of
K^{\perp}(j,\omega=0)
for
j\gg 1

is qualitatively similar to that of
K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},\omega=0)
(i.e., the full Fourier transform of
K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega=0
)
in all directions) for
|\boldsymbol{R}_{j}|\gg 1
, since the dominant contribution in

K^{\perp}(j,\omega=0)=\sum_{R_{x},R_{y}}K^{\perp}(R_{x},R_{y},R_{z}=j,\omega=0)
for
j\gg 1
arises near
R_{x}\sim R_{y}\sim 0
.
In particular, if
K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},\omega=0)
decays exponentially for
|\boldsymbol{R}_{j}|\gg 1
, then
K^{\perp}(j,\omega=0)
(
S34
) also decays exponentially for
j\gg 1
with the same correlation length.

The integral in Eq. (
S34
) can be evaluated analytically as

\displaystyle K^{\perp}(j,\omega=0)

\displaystyle=16e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}\frac{{\rm sgn}(U)}{\sqrt{U^{2}-16t_{h}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{{}_{3}F_{2}(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2},1;1-j,1+j;\tfrac{1}{1-U^{2}/16t_{h}^{2}})}{\Gamma(1-j)\Gamma(1+j)},

(S35)

where
{}_{3}F_{2}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3};b_{1},b_{2};z)
is the generalized hypergeometric function
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 1995
,
and
\Gamma(z)
is the gamma function. The explicit expression (
S35
), however, does not directly
tell us about the long-distance behavior. Hence we take a different approach.

Figure S2:
Contour for the integral (
S39
) in the complex plane depicted by the dashed circle,
which can continuously be deformed to the solid curve without crossing the branch cuts
shown by the wavy lines.

The kernel (
S34
) can be written as

\displaystyle K^{\perp}(j,\omega=0)

\displaystyle=4e^{2}t_{h}C_{M,N}{\rm sgn}(U)I_{j}(u),

(S36)

where

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\frac{|U|}{4t_{h}}

(S37)

is the normalized interaction strength, and

\displaystyle I_{j}(u)

\displaystyle:=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{dq}{2\pi}e^{-iqj}\frac{1}{\sqrt{u^{2}-\sin^{2}\frac{q}{2}}}

(S38)

for
u>1
.
By putting
z=e^{-iq}
, we transform the integral (
S38
) to a complex contour integral,

\displaystyle I_{j}(u)

\displaystyle=\oint\frac{dz}{2\pi i}z^{j-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{u^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{2}(z+z^{-1}))}}

\displaystyle=2\oint\frac{dz}{2\pi i}\frac{z^{j-\frac{1}{2}}}{\sqrt{z^{2}+2(2u^{2}-1)z+1}},

(S39)

where the contour is taken to be the circle around the origin with unit radius
|z|=1
(dashed curve in Fig.
S2
).
In the case of
|U|>4t_{h}
, we have
u>1
. The roots of the quadratic polynomial in the denominator of (
S39
) are given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{\pm}

\displaystyle=1-2u^{2}\pm\sqrt{(2u^{2}-1)^{2}-1},

(S40)

which are real numbers. These roots satisfy
\alpha_{-}<-1<\alpha_{+}<0
and
\alpha_{+}\alpha_{-}=1
. Using
\alpha_{\pm}
,
we can write the contour integral (
S39
) as

\displaystyle I_{j}(u)

\displaystyle=2\oint\frac{dz}{2\pi i}\frac{z^{j-\frac{1}{2}}}{\sqrt{(z-\alpha_{+})(z-\alpha_{-})}}.

(S41)

In Fig.
S2
, we show the branch cuts that we adopt in evaluating Eq. (
S41
)
by wavy lines. With this configuration, the contour can be smoothly deformed
to the solid curve in Fig.
S2
without crossing the branch cuts,
where the integral is evaluated as

\displaystyle I_{j}(u)

\displaystyle=2(e^{i(j-\frac{1}{2})\pi+i\pi}-e^{i(j-\frac{1}{2})\pi})

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{\alpha_{+}}^{0}\frac{dx}{2\pi i}\frac{(-x)^{j-\frac{1}{2}}}{\sqrt{(x-\alpha_{+})(x-\alpha_{-})}}

\displaystyle=-4e^{i(j-\frac{1}{2})\pi}\int_{\alpha_{+}}^{0}\frac{dx}{2\pi i}\frac{(-x)^{j-\frac{1}{2}}}{\sqrt{(x-\alpha_{+})(x-\alpha_{-})}}.

(S42)

The asymptotic behavior of
I_{j}(u)
can be read off as follows: for
j\gg 1
,
the function
\frac{(-x)^{j-\frac{1}{2}}}{\sqrt{x-\alpha_{+}}}
in the integrand has a concentrated contribution near
x=\alpha_{+}
,
while
\frac{1}{\sqrt{x-\alpha_{-}}}
is a smooth function in
(\alpha_{+},0]
. Therefore, one can replace

\frac{1}{\sqrt{x-\alpha_{-}}}
by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{-}}}
in the integrand of Eq. (
S42
), obtaining

\displaystyle I_{j}(u)

\displaystyle\approx-4e^{i(j-\frac{1}{2})\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{-}}}\int_{\alpha_{+}}^{0}\frac{dx}{2\pi i}\frac{(-x)^{j-\frac{1}{2}}}{\sqrt{x-\alpha_{+}}}

(S43)

for
j\gg 1
. The rest of the integral can be evaluated as

\displaystyle I_{j}(u)

\displaystyle\approx-4e^{i(j-\frac{1}{2})\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{-}}}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(j+\frac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(j+1)}(-\alpha_{+})^{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{-}}}\frac{\Gamma(j+\frac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(j+1)}\alpha_{+}^{j}.

(S44)

Using Stirling’s formula, we obtain the asymptotic form of
I_{j}(u)
as

\displaystyle I_{j}(u)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{-}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{j}}\alpha_{+}^{j}.

(S45)

Thus, the transverse electromagnetic response function behaves
in the long distance (
j\to\infty
) as

\displaystyle K^{\perp}(j,\omega=0)

\displaystyle\approx\mbox{cosnt.}\times\frac{\alpha_{+}^{j}}{\sqrt{j}}.

(S46)

Since
|\alpha_{+}|<1
,
K^{\perp}(j,\omega=0)
decays exponentially in space
with Pippard’s coherence length
\xi
defined by

\displaystyle K^{\perp}(j,\omega=0)

\displaystyle\approx\mbox{const.}\times\exp\left(-\frac{j}{\xi}\right).

(S47)

Physically,
\xi
represents the length scale over which a response against a local perturbation of electromagnetic fields propagates
in space (Pippard’s nonlocal electrodynamics
Schrieffer 1983
).

From the result (
S46
),
\xi
is identified as

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle=-\frac{a}{\ln|\alpha_{+}|}=\frac{a}{\cosh^{-1}(2u^{2}-1)},

(S48)

which does not depend on the doublon density
\rho
.
At
|U|=4t_{h}=:U_{c}
, the coherence length diverges as

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle\propto\frac{1}{|U-U_{c}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

(S49)

This is exactly the point where the electromagnetic gap closes at
q=\pi
.

In the second case (
|U|<4t_{h}
), the Meissner kernel in the low-frequency limit is
expressed as

\displaystyle K^{\perp}(j,\omega=0)

\displaystyle=4e^{2}t_{h}C_{M,N}{\rm sgn}(U)I_{j}(u)

(S50)

with

\displaystyle I_{j}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{u\geq|\sin\frac{q}{2}|}\frac{dq}{2\pi}e^{-iqj}\frac{1}{\sqrt{u^{2}-\sin^{2}\frac{q}{2}}}

(S51)

for
u<1
. Similarly to the first case, we put
z=e^{-iq}
to rewrite the integral (
S51
) as

\displaystyle I_{j}(u)

\displaystyle=2\int_{C}\frac{dz}{2\pi i}\frac{z^{j-\frac{1}{2}}}{\sqrt{(z-\alpha_{+})(z-\alpha_{-})}},

(S52)

where the roots in the denominator are given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{\pm}

\displaystyle=1-2u^{2}\pm i\sqrt{1-(2u^{2}-1)^{2}},

(S53)

and the contour
C
is taken to be the arc of the circle with the unit radius connecting
\alpha_{-}
and
\alpha_{+}

(dashed curve in Fig.
S3
). We choose the branch cuts in the integrand of Eq. (
S52
)
as shown by wavy lines in Fig.
S3
.

Following the steepest descent method, we deform the contour from
C
to the solid lines in Fig.
S3

without crossing the branch cuts, where we put
z=r\alpha_{\pm}
. Now the integral (
S52
) can be
evaluated as

\displaystyle I_{j}(u)

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dr}{2\pi i}\alpha_{+}\frac{(r\alpha_{+})^{j-\frac{1}{2}}}{\sqrt{(r\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{+})(r\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{-})}}

\displaystyle\quad-2\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dr}{2\pi i}\alpha_{-}\frac{(r\alpha_{-})^{j-\frac{1}{2}}}{\sqrt{(r\alpha_{-}-\alpha_{+})(r\alpha_{-}-\alpha_{-})}}.

(S54)

For
j\gg 1
, the function
\frac{r^{j-\frac{1}{2}}}{\sqrt{r\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{+}}}
in the first integral
is dominantly contributed from a region near
r=1
, which allows us to replace

\frac{1}{\sqrt{r\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{-}}}
by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{-}}}
in the integrand.
A similar approximation can be applied to the second term. Taking care of the branch cuts, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{j}(u)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2(\alpha_{+}^{j}e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}}-\alpha_{-}^{j}e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}})}{\sqrt{|\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{-}|}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dr}{2\pi i}\frac{r^{j-\frac{1}{2}}}{\sqrt{1-r}}.

(S55)

If we define
\alpha_{\pm}=:e^{\pm i\varphi}
, the integral (
S51
) can be approximated as

\displaystyle I_{j}(u)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2\sin(j\varphi+\frac{\pi}{4})}{\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{|\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{-}|}}\frac{\Gamma(j+\frac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(j+1)}.

(S56)

Using Stirling’s formula, the asymptotic form of
I_{j}(u)
for
j\gg 1
is given by

\displaystyle I_{j}(u)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2\sin(j\varphi+\frac{\pi}{4})}{\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{|\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{-}|}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{j}}.

(S57)

Therefore, the kernel decays in a long distance according to a power law as

\displaystyle K^{\perp}(j,\omega=0)

\displaystyle\approx\mbox{const.}\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{j}}.

(S58)

This means that the coherence length diverges (
\xi=\infty
) for
|U|<4t_{h}
.

I.5
E. Longitudinal component

The longitudinal component of the electromagnetic response function is defined by

K^{\parallel}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega):=K^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
, where we take
\boldsymbol{q}\parallel\boldsymbol{e}_{z}
.
The
zz
component of the current-current correlation function (
S14
) reads

\displaystyle\sum_{j}e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}\langle\psi_{N}|J^{z}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)J^{z}(0,0)|\psi_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=4e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}(\langle\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q},+\boldsymbol{e}_{z}|-e^{-iq}\langle\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q},-\boldsymbol{e}_{z}|)

\displaystyle\quad\times e^{-iH(\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q})t}(|\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q},+\boldsymbol{e}_{z}\rangle-e^{iq}|\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q},-\boldsymbol{e}_{z}\rangle),

(S59)

where
H(\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q})
is given in Eq. (
S25
).
During the time evolution, the relative coordinate of two particles changes only in the
z
direction.
Therefore, what we need to solve is essentially a one-dimensional two-particle problem,
which can be solved exactly in the spirit of the Bethe ansatz
Essler et al. 2005
.
Here we do not go into details of analytical solutions,
since we can easily diagonalize the Hamiltonian (
S25
) numerically for a large system size.

II
II. Charge conservation

In the Hubbard model [Eq. (
1
) in the main text], electric charge is conserved due to the charge
U(1)
symmetry.
This imposes a nontrivial constraint on the electromagnetic response function
Schrieffer 1983
.
To see this, we introduce the four-vector form of the electromagnetic response function defined by

\displaystyle K^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=-i\theta(t)\langle\psi_{N}|[J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t),J^{\nu}(0,0)]|\psi_{N}\rangle

(S62)

(\mu,\nu=0,x,y,z)
, where
J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j})=(c\rho(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}),\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}))
is the four-vector current,
and

\displaystyle\rho(\boldsymbol{R}_{j})

\displaystyle=e\sum_{\sigma}c_{j\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{j\sigma}

(S63)

is the local density operator. In the following, we use the metric convention
\eta^{\mu\nu}={\rm diag}(-,+,+,+)
.
The linear response in the four-vector form reads
J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)=-K^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)A_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
,
where
A_{\nu}=\eta_{\nu\lambda}A^{\lambda}=(-\frac{\phi}{c},\boldsymbol{A})
and
\phi
is the scalar potential.

II.1
A. Charge response function

The charge response function for the
\eta
-pairing state is given by

\displaystyle K^{00}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)

\displaystyle=-ic^{2}\theta(t)\langle\psi_{N}|[\rho(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t),\rho(0,0)]|\psi_{N}\rangle.

(S64)

As before, the density-density correlation function for
N
particles
can be reduced to the two-particle correlation function. To see this, we define local
\eta
operators,

\displaystyle\eta^{+}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j})

\displaystyle:=e^{i\boldsymbol{Q}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}c_{j\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{j\downarrow}^{\dagger},

(S65)

\displaystyle\eta^{-}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j})

\displaystyle:=e^{-i\boldsymbol{Q}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}c_{j\downarrow}c_{j\uparrow}.

(S66)

They satisfy the following commutation relations:

\displaystyle[\rho(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}),\eta^{\pm}]

\displaystyle=\pm 2\eta^{\pm}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}),

(S67)

\displaystyle[\eta^{\pm}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}),\eta^{\pm}]

\displaystyle=0,

(S68)

\displaystyle[\eta^{\pm}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}),\eta^{\mp}]

\displaystyle=\pm(\rho(\boldsymbol{R}_{j})-1).

(S69)

Applying the above relations iteratively, we can reduce the
N
-particle density-density correlation function to

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{N}|\rho(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)\rho(0,0)|\psi_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=4e^{2}C_{M,N}\langle 0|\eta^{-}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)\eta^{+}(0,0)|0\rangle+4e^{2}\frac{\frac{N}{2}(\frac{N}{2}-1)}{M(M-1)}.

(S70)

From this result, we can evaluate the charge response function as

\displaystyle K^{00}(\boldsymbol{q},t)

\displaystyle=-4i\theta(t)c^{2}e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}

\displaystyle\quad\times[\langle\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r}=0|e^{-iH(\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q})t}|\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r}=0\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\langle\boldsymbol{Q}+\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r}=0|e^{iH(\boldsymbol{Q}+\boldsymbol{q})t}|\boldsymbol{Q}+\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r}=0\rangle],

(S71)

where

\displaystyle H(\boldsymbol{Q}\pm\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=-t_{h}((1-e^{\pm iq})\Delta_{z}^{+}+(1-e^{\mp iq})\Delta_{z}^{-})

\displaystyle\quad+U(\delta_{\boldsymbol{r},0}-1).

(S72)

Thus, the problem reduces to solving the two-particle dynamics in the one-dimensional Hubbard model,
which can be diagonalized numerically or analytically with the Bethe ansatz method.

K^{00}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
is related to
K^{\parallel}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
due to the symmetry constraint.
Compared with
K^{\parallel}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
, the contribution of the low-energy sidebands is enhanced in
K^{00}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
,
which can also be understood from the symmetry constraint [Eq. (
S79
)].
In the low-momentum limit, the charge response function vanishes,

\displaystyle\lim_{\boldsymbol{q}\to 0}K^{00}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=0,

(S73)

since
H(\boldsymbol{Q}\pm\boldsymbol{q})=0
for
q\to 0
and
\boldsymbol{r}=0
.

II.2
B. Symmetry constraint

Here we see how the symmetry puts a constraint on the electromagnetic response functions
Schrieffer 1983
.
Our starting point is the continuity equation,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho(\boldsymbol{R}_{j},t)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\mu=x,y,z}(J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j})-J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}-\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu})),

(S74)

which is a direct consequence of the U(1) symmetry of the Hubbard model.
After Fourier transformation, the relation becomes

\displaystyle-i\omega\rho(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\mu=x,y,z}(e^{\frac{i}{2}\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}}-e^{-\frac{i}{2}\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}})J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega).

(S75)

Taking the expectation value with respect to
|\psi_{N}\rangle
and
substituting the electromagnetic response function (
S62
) in Eq. (
S75
), we obtain

\displaystyle-i\frac{\omega}{c}\sum_{\nu=0,x,y,z}K^{0\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)A_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\mu=x,y,z}(e^{\frac{i}{2}\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}}-e^{-\frac{i}{2}\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}})\sum_{\nu=0,x,y,z}K^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)A_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega).

(S76)

Since the above relation must hold for arbitrary
A_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
, we conclude that

\displaystyle i\frac{\omega}{c}K^{0\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)-\sum_{\mu=x,y,z}(e^{\frac{i}{2}\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}}-e^{-\frac{i}{2}\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}})K^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=0.

(S77)

We assume
\boldsymbol{q}\parallel\boldsymbol{e}_{z}
without loss of generality. Then the relation becomes

\displaystyle K^{0\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{2c\sin\frac{q}{2}}{\omega}K^{z\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega).

(S78)

Due to Onsager’s reciprocity relation, we also have
K^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)=K^{\nu\mu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
.
Therefore,
K^{00}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
and
K^{\parallel}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
are related to each other through

\displaystyle K^{00}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{4c^{2}\sin^{2}\frac{q}{2}}{\omega^{2}}K^{\parallel}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega).

(S79)

To summarize, all the components of the electromagnetic response function
K^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
can be
expressed in terms of
K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
and
K^{\parallel}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
as

\displaystyle K^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\frac{4c^{2}\sin^{2}\frac{q}{2}}{\omega^{2}}K^{\parallel}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)&0&0&\frac{2c\sin\frac{q}{2}}{\omega}K^{\parallel}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)\\
0&K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)&0&0\\
0&0&K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)&0\\
\frac{2c\sin\frac{q}{2}}{\omega}K^{\parallel}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)&0&0&K^{\parallel}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)\end{pmatrix}.

(S80)

One can check that the symmetry constraint (
S78
) is consistent with gauge invariance
in the Hubbard model.

III
III. Dynamical electromagnetic fields

In this section, we give a detailed description of dynamical electromagnetic fields coupled to the
\eta
-pairing states.
We start with the Maxwell equations,

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{E}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{0}}\rho,

(S81)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\boldsymbol{E}+\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{B}=0,

(S82)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{B}=0,

(S83)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\boldsymbol{B}-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{E}=\mu_{0}\boldsymbol{j},

(S84)

where
\boldsymbol{E}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
are electric and magnetic fields,
and
\rho
and
\boldsymbol{j}
are the charge density and current, respectively.
As usual, we introduce the scalar potential
\phi
and the vector potential
\boldsymbol{A}
through

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\phi-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{A},

(S85)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}

\displaystyle=\nabla\times\boldsymbol{A}.

(S86)

In the following, we adopt the Lorenz gauge:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\phi+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{A}

\displaystyle=0.

(S87)

Then, the equations for
\phi
and
\boldsymbol{A}
become

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\phi-\nabla^{2}\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{0}}\rho,

(S88)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\boldsymbol{A}-\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{A}

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}\boldsymbol{j}.

(S89)

To solve these equations, we assume plane-wave solutions,

\displaystyle\phi(\boldsymbol{r},t)

\displaystyle=\phi_{0}e^{-i\omega t+i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}},

(S90)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r},t)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}_{0}e^{-i\omega t+i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}},

(S91)

with frequency
\omega
and momentum
\boldsymbol{q}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume
\boldsymbol{q}\parallel\boldsymbol{e}_{z}
.
Combining the linear-response relation
j^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)=-K^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)A_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

and the gauge condition (
S87
),
we obtain

\displaystyle-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}\phi+q^{2}\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{0}}K^{00}(q,\omega)\frac{\phi}{c}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{0}}K^{0z}(q,\omega)A_{z},

(S92)

\displaystyle-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}A^{x}+q^{2}A^{x}

\displaystyle=-\mu_{0}K^{xx}(q,\omega)A_{x},

(S93)

\displaystyle-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}A^{y}+q^{2}A^{y}

\displaystyle=-\mu_{0}K^{yy}(q,\omega)A_{y},

(S94)

\displaystyle-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}A^{z}+q^{2}A^{z}

\displaystyle=-\mu_{0}K^{zz}(q,\omega)A_{z}+\mu_{0}K^{z0}(q,\omega)\frac{\phi}{c},

(S95)

\displaystyle\frac{\omega}{c^{2}}\phi-qA^{z}

\displaystyle=0.

(S96)

Note that we use the metric
\eta^{\mu\nu}={\rm diag}(-,+,+,+)
to write down the above equations.
One can see that the transverse (
A^{x},A^{y}
) and longitudinal (
A^{z}
) components are decoupled.

In the low-energy and long-wavelength limit, the above field equation can be derived from an effective Lagrangian density

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm eff}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\mu_{0}}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}-V_{\rm eff}(A^{\mu})

(S97)

with an effective potential

\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}(A^{\mu})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}m^{2}c^{2}A_{\mu}A^{\mu},

(S98)

where
F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}
is the field strength, and
m
is the effective mass of the electromagnetic field
corresponding to

\displaystyle m^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{0}}{c^{2}}\lim_{q\to 0}\lim_{\omega\to 0}K^{\perp}(q,\omega)=\frac{\mu_{0}}{\pi c^{2}}D_{s}.

(S99)

Thus, the squared mass of the electromagnetic field is proportional to the Meissner weight.
In ordinary situations (
D_{s}>0
), the electromagnetic field acquires a positive squared mass
due to the Anderson-Higgs mechanism. In the opposite case (
D_{s}<0
),
the electromagnetic field becomes “tachyonic” with a negative squared mass.

III.1
A. Transverse mode

In order for the transverse modes to exist (
A^{x},A^{y}\neq 0
), the dispersion must satisfy

\displaystyle q^{2}-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\mu_{0}K^{\perp}(q,\omega),

(S100)

which corresponds to Eq. (
6
) in the main text. While the condition (
S100
) gives
a complicated nonlinear relation between
\omega
and
q
,
the situation becomes simplified at low momentum.

In the case of
U>0
, Eq. (
S100
) reduces to

\displaystyle-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}

\displaystyle=-8\mu_{0}e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}\left(\frac{1}{\omega+U}-\frac{1}{\omega-U}\right)

(S101)

in the limit of
q\to 0
[see Eq. (
S61
)]. The solution for
\omega^{2}
is given by

\displaystyle\omega^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(U^{2}\pm\sqrt{U^{4}-64\mu_{0}c^{2}e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}U}\right).

(S102)

In order for
\omega
to take a real value, the interaction strength
U
must satisfy

\displaystyle U^{3}\geq 64\mu_{0}c^{2}e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N},

(S103)

which is exactly the condition derived in the main text.
In the thermodynamic limit (
M,N\to\infty
with
N/M
being fixed),

C_{M,N}
approaches
\rho(1-\rho)
, where
\rho:=(N/2)/M
is the doublon density.
In this limit, the condition (
S103
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{U}{t_{h}}\geq 4\left(\frac{\mu_{0}c^{2}e^{2}\rho(1-\rho)}{t_{h}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}.

(S104)

One can see that a wide range of the parameter region shows a dynamical instability of the electromagnetic field
coupled to the
\eta
-pairing state in the long-wavelength limit.
The real part of the frequency at
q\to 0
is given by

\displaystyle{\rm Re}\,\omega

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\sqrt{64\mu_{0}c^{2}e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}U}+U^{2}},

(S105)

which is proportional to
U^{1/4}
at small
U
.
The imaginary part of the frequency at
q\to 0
is given by

\displaystyle{\rm Im}\,\omega

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\sqrt{64\mu_{0}c^{2}e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}U}-U^{2}}

(S106)

for
U^{3}<64\mu_{0}c^{2}e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}
.

More generally, if we take into account arbitrary
\boldsymbol{q}
modes, we can prove that a dynamical instability exists
for all
U
and
\rho
. First, we observe that
K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
in Eq. (
S31
)
takes a real value if and only if
|\omega+U|>4t_{h}\sin\frac{q}{2}
and
|\omega-U|>4t_{h}\sin\frac{q}{2}
.
Let us first consider the case of
0<\omega<U-4t_{h}\sin\frac{q}{2}
.
Using the result for
K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
in Eq. (
S31
), the dispersion relation (
S100
)
can be written as

\displaystyle q^{2}-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}

\displaystyle=-8\mu_{0}e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{(\omega+U)^{2}-16t_{h}^{2}\sin^{2}\frac{q}{2}}}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\frac{1}{\sqrt{(\omega-U)^{2}-16t_{h}^{2}\sin^{2}\frac{q}{2}}}\right\},

(S107)

which is negative definite. Therefore, we have
\omega>cq
.
For the other case of
\omega>U+4t_{h}\sin\frac{q}{2}
, the condition (
S100
) reads

\displaystyle q^{2}-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}

\displaystyle=-8\mu_{0}e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{(\omega+U)^{2}-16t_{h}^{2}\sin^{2}\frac{q}{2}}}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.-\frac{1}{\sqrt{(\omega-U)^{2}-16t_{h}^{2}\sin^{2}\frac{q}{2}}}\right\},

(S108)

which is positive definite since
(\omega+U)^{2}-16t_{h}^{2}\sin^{2}\frac{q}{2}>(\omega-U)^{2}-16t_{h}^{2}\sin^{2}\frac{q}{2}
.
Therefore, we have
\omega<cq
.

This means that there must always be a region in

In the attractive case (
U<0
), the mode equation in the long-wavelength limit is similarly given by Eq. (

).
The solution for

is the same as Eq. (

).
Hence the frequency becomes imaginary for
\boldsymbol{q}\to 0
. The inverse of the imaginary part of

is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\rm Im}\,\omega}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{U^{4}-64\mu_{0}c^{2}e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}U}-U^{2}\right)}}.

(S109)

In the attractive case, the electromagnetic field is dynamically unstable against the
\boldsymbol{q}=0
mode.
This corresponds to the fact that the electromagnetic field has a negative squared mass.
Physically, the electromagnetic field with a long wavelength penetrates deeply inside the
\eta
pairing state,
transferring the kinetic energy of doublons to the electromagnetic field.
The time scale of the growth of this instability is determined by Eq. (
S109
).

Combining the arguments for the two cases (
U>0
and
U<0
), we have established that
the electromagnetic field coupled to the
\eta

and
\rho
.

The mode equation for the longitudinal components can be derived from Eqs. (

),
(

), and (

qa\ll 1
).

\displaystyle q^{2}-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\mu_{0}K^{\parallel}(q,\omega)\left(1-\frac{c^{2}q^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\right),

(S110)

where we have used the relation (
S80
).
In the limit of

\displaystyle-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\mu_{0}\lim_{q\to 0}K^{\parallel}(q,\omega).

IV
IV. Magnetic properties of tachyonic superconductors

In this section, we describe static magnetic properties of tachyonic superconductors realized as the
\eta
-pairing states
in the Hubbard model with
U<0
.
As shown in the main text and in the preceding section, the tachyonic superconductors are dynamically unstable.
Here we focus on the response of the
\eta
-pairing states against static magnetic fields within the linear-response regime,
and do not consider their decay dynamics.

The static magnetic field
\boldsymbol{B}
obeys the following Maxwell equations:

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{B}

\displaystyle=0,

(S112)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\boldsymbol{B}

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}\boldsymbol{j}.

(S113)

We introduce a static vector potential
\boldsymbol{A}
as
\boldsymbol{B}=\nabla\times\boldsymbol{A}
.
If we take the Coulomb gauge (
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{A}=0
), the equation for
\boldsymbol{A}
becomes

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{A}

\displaystyle=-\mu_{0}\boldsymbol{j}.

(S114)

To solve the equation, we assume a plane-wave form,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}_{0}e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}},

(S115)

with amplitude
\boldsymbol{A}_{0}
and wave number
\boldsymbol{q}
.
Without loss of generality, we choose
\boldsymbol{q}=q\boldsymbol{e}_{z}
.
We apply the linear-response theory to the
\eta
-pairing states to obtain the equations for
\boldsymbol{A}_{0}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

(S116)

\displaystyle-q^{2}A_{0}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=-\mu_{0}j^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}\sum_{\nu}K^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega=0)A_{0\nu}\quad(\mu,\nu=x,y,z).

(S117)

From Eq. (
S116
), we find
A_{0}^{z}=0
.
In order for the solution
A_{0}^{\mu}\neq 0
to exist,
q
must satisfy a nonlinear equation,

\displaystyle q^{2}

\displaystyle=-\mu_{0}K^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega=0)

\displaystyle=-16\mu_{0}e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}

\displaystyle\quad\times\begin{cases}\frac{{\rm sgn}(U)}{\sqrt{U^{2}-16t_{h}^{2}\sin^{2}\frac{q}{2}}}&\mbox{for}\;|U|>4t_{h}\sin\frac{q}{2};\\
0&\mbox{for}\;|U|<4t_{h}\sin\frac{q}{2},\end{cases}

(S118)

where we have used the result (
S31
).

One can immediately see that a real solution for Eq. (
S118
) does not exist for
U>0
.
This is nothing but the Meissner effect; that is, a magnetic field cannot propagate freely into superconductors.
In fact, the magnetic field decays exponentially in space with the penetration depth

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{U}{16\mu_{0}e^{2}t_{h}^{2}C_{M,N}}}.

(S119)

On the other hand, when
U<0
a real solution is possible as is clear from the graphical illustration of Eq. (
S118
) in Fig.
S9
.
Thus, a magnetic field
can
penetrate into tachyonic superconductors without decay.

\pm q_{1}

V. Spontaneous light emission

In this section, we evaluate the rate of spontaneous light emission for
\eta
-pairing states in the Hubbard model with
U>0
.
In the repulsive case, doublons can decay spontaneously into pairs of single particles by emitting light with frequency
\omega=U
.
After emitting light, the
\eta
-pairing state
|\psi_{N}\rangle
is transformed into

\displaystyle J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{q}=0)|\psi_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{N}}}N(\eta^{+})^{\frac{N}{2}-1}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}v^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})c_{\boldsymbol{k}\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{k}\downarrow}^{\dagger}|0\rangle,

(S124)

where
v^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})=\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}}{\partial k^{\mu}}=2t_{h}\sin k^{\mu}
is the group velocity
(
\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}=-2t_{h}\sum_{\mu=x,y,z}\cos k^{\mu}
is the band dispersion).

Let us define a one-doublon-broken state
Yang 1989

\displaystyle|\zeta_{N,\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{N,\boldsymbol{a}}}}(\eta^{+})^{\frac{N}{2}-1}\eta_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{+}|0\rangle,

(S125)

where
\mathcal{N}_{N,\boldsymbol{a}}
is the normalization constant
(such that
\langle\zeta_{N,\boldsymbol{a}}|\zeta_{N,\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}
),

\boldsymbol{a}
represents a lattice-site coordinate, and

\displaystyle\eta_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{+}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}}c_{\boldsymbol{k}\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{k}\downarrow}^{\dagger}.

(S126)

The state
|\zeta_{N,\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle
consists of
\frac{N}{2}-1
doublons with momentum
\boldsymbol{Q}
and
two unpaired particles with the lattice spacing
\boldsymbol{a}
. One can show that
|\zeta_{N,\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle
with
\boldsymbol{a}\neq 0
is
an exact eigenstate of the Hubbard model [Eq. (
1
) in the main text] with the eigenenergy
-U
.
At
\boldsymbol{a}=0
, we have
|\zeta_{N,\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle=|\psi_{N}\rangle
.
Using
|\zeta_{N,\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle
, we can write the one-photon emitted state (
S124
) as

\displaystyle J^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{q}=0)|\psi_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=e\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{N}_{N,\boldsymbol{a}}}{\mathcal{N}_{N}}}\frac{N}{M}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}v^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\neq 0}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}}|\zeta_{N,\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle.

(S127)

Therefore, all the states that are accessible by one-photon emission are covered by the eigenstates

|\zeta_{N,\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle
(
\boldsymbol{a}\neq 0
).

The rate of spontaneous emission is given by Einstein’s A coefficient
Loudon 2000
:

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega^{3}}{3\pi\varepsilon_{0}c^{3}\hbar}\sum_{\mu}|\langle e|P^{\mu}|\sigma\rangle|^{2},

(S128)

where
P^{\mu}
is the polarization operator,
|\sigma\rangle
is an initial state, and
|e\rangle

is a one-phonon emitted state. In the present case, we take
|\sigma\rangle=|\psi_{N}\rangle
and
|e\rangle=|\zeta_{N,\boldsymbol{a}}\rangle
.
Since
\frac{d}{dt}P^{\mu}=J^{\mu}
, the rate
\Gamma
is rewritten as

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega}{3\pi\varepsilon_{0}c^{3}\hbar}\sum_{\mu}\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}}|\langle\zeta_{N,\boldsymbol{a}}|J^{\mu}|\psi_{N}\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}\omega}{3\pi\varepsilon_{0}c^{3}\hbar}\sum_{\mu}\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}}\frac{\mathcal{N}_{N,\boldsymbol{a}}}{\mathcal{N}_{N}}\frac{N^{2}}{M^{2}}\left|\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}v^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k})e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}}\right|^{2}.

(S129)

Using Eq. (
S13
) and

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{N,\boldsymbol{a}}

\displaystyle=\frac{M(M-2)!(\frac{N}{2}-1)!}{(M-\frac{N}{2}-1)!}\quad(\boldsymbol{a}\neq 0),

(S130)

we obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{8e^{2}\omega t_{h}^{2}a^{2}dC_{M,N}}{3\pi\varepsilon_{0}c^{3}\hbar^{3}}M,

(S131)

where
d
is the dimension of the system.
One can see that the rate
\Gamma
is proportional to the system size, which is natural because
the doublon decay can take place at any lattice site with equal probability.

For ordinary three-dimensional materials, we substitute
t_{h}=
1[eV],
\omega=U=
1[eV],
a=
1[Å],
\rho=0.5
,
and
d=3
in Eq. (
S131
), obtaining

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma}{M}

\displaystyle=2.3\times 10^{7}\;[{\rm s}^{-1}].

(S132)
