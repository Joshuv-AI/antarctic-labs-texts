On the Arrow of Time and Organized Complexity in the Universe
Tatsuaki Okamoto

Abstract
There is a widespread assumption that the universe in general, and the biosphere on Earth in particular, is becoming more complex over time. This study formulates this assumption as a macroscopic law, the law of increasing complexity . It hypothesizes that this macroscopic law emerges in certain open systems, such as the observable universe and the biosphere on Earth. There are two types of complexity: disorganized and organized. The complexity associated with this assumption is organized complexity. To formulate this law, we utilized a quantitative definition of the complexity of organized matters, termed organized complexity (OC), which is defined for probability distributions. We represent any object of complexity as the source of its observed value, which is a probability distribution, allowing us to treat various objects in a unified manner. We need observation systems to represent objects for this formulation. We introduce an order relation between observation systems to demonstrate that the complexity of an object possesses a generic property, one that does not depend on any specific observation system. This study develops a methodology to formulate the arrow of time in terms of increasing organized complexity for open systems ; in contrast, the second law of thermodynamics formulated the arrow of time in terms of increasing disorganized complexity for isolated systems. We then apply this hypothesis to the fine-tuning problem of the universe, where the fundamental physical constants appear to be fine-tuned for life on Earth. Our new explanation of the fine-tuning problem, based on this hypothesis, posits that the fundamental physical constants are fine-tuned for the emergence of the law of increasing complexity in the universe. This explanation is considered more plausible than those based on the thoughts of their fine-tuning for life on Earth or the anthropic principle.

I.2
Fine-Tuning Problem and Explanations

The fundamental physical laws incorporate various physical constants, including the gravitational constant, the speed of light, Planck’s constant, and the elementary charge.
However, there is yet to be an
answer to how these physical constants came to take on their currently measured values.
The only thing that can be said now is that these values were selected by chance,
though a future “theory of everything” may answer this question.

A strange thing here is that the physical constants of our universe appear to have been set to values
conducive to the creation of life on Earth
in such an intricately balanced way that they could hardly be a product of chance

;
Davies07

For example, there is a constant that determines the magnitude of the nuclear force, called the strong force, that binds elementary particles in the atomic nucleus.
Another constant determines the magnitude of the electromagnetic force,
which binds electrons to the atomic nucleus and creates repulsion between protons.
If the values of these two constants were to deviate even slightly and upset the balance between them, for example, if
the electromagnetic force were to be slightly stronger,
atoms but hydrogen could not exist, which would mean no carbon and, by extension,
no life on Earth.
Conversely, if the strong force were to be slightly stronger,
hydrogen would disappear, which would also mean no life on Earth.
Many examples of such physical constants that appear to be fine-tuned for life are known

;
Davies07
;
SmiSmiVer18
;
Davies03

The

questions why the
physical constants appear to have been fine-tuned for life on Earth.

There are many explanations for this problem using

The weak anthropic principle states that
the (observed) physical laws and constants in the universe
must be consistent with the observer’s existence, such as (human) life.
It explains the fine-tuning problem by asserting that
the physical constants we observed must be fine-tuned for us or (human) life.
However, this principle is a tautological statement regarding the observation selection effects.

The multiverse hypothesis posits the existence of numerous universes,
each with different values of the fundamental physical constants.
It explains the fine-tuning problem by suggesting that a universe from among numerous
universes that just happened to have physical constants appropriate
for (human) life would have to be our universe.
It states that (human) life exists in this universe due to selection bias and provides support for the weak anthropic principle.

The top-down cosmology proposes
that the universe’s initial conditions consisted of
a superposition of many possible initial conditions,
only a tiny fraction of which contributed to the universe we see today.
This theory offers an anthropic explanation for the fine-tuning problem and
provides support for the weak anthropic principle.

There are many variants of cosmic designers,
such as extra-universal aliens or intelligent entities who designed the universe,
program simulators of the universe, and
some supernatural agent or God.
The explanations for the fine-tuning problem from the cosmic designers
rely on a teleological argument, similar to the strong anthropic principle.

In summary,
the weak anthropic principle is tautological.
However, extra-universal hypotheses, such as
the multiverse hypothesis and top-down cosmology, provide support for the weak anthropic principle
by invoking selection bias.
The cosmic designers hypothesis would suggest the strong
anthropic principle through teleological arguments.
Hence, all explanations tie with the anthropic principle.
This is because
we suppose that this universe is fine-tuned for “life” on Earth.
Now we have a question:

Is this universe fine-tuned for “life”?

Some arguments exist about whether this universe is fine-tuned for “life”

Friederich21
.
Many physicists and cosmologists regarding this problem
seem to support not so much that this universe is fine-tuned for “life”;
rather this is fine-tuned for “the building blocks and environments that life requires”

SmiSmiVer18
;
Davies03
.
A caution
Adams19
is raised that the range of parameters for which the universe would have been habitable is quite considerable.
In addition, the universe could have been more, rather than less, life-friendly
if some parameters of the physical laws were to change slightly.
Our universe may just be habitable rather than maximally life-supporting.
Even the weak anthropic principle states that
this universe is consistent with life, but it does not mean that it is optimally fine-tuned for life.
Our universe may be more fine-tuned for something else than life.
We may be biased to assume that all possible kinds of complex organizations
in the universe will resemble life on Earth.
The creation of life on Earth is just one phenomenon in the universe.
Numerous other phenomena could occur, such as the formation of diverse complex organizations beyond our current imagination. Then, the universe could be fine-tuned for
“the building blocks and environments required for life” and unknown complex organizations as well as life.
Is the universe fine-tuned for
any more plausible objective than life
?

We then have a question.

Can we explain the fine-tuning problem
using a more plausible objective

II.1
Complexity Definition for Any Objects

Objects

The objects of complexity encompass everything in our surroundings, including
materials, planets, stars, galaxies, life, the biosphere, humans, and human societies.
Our recognition of the existence of anything relies solely on observation.
For instance, we recognize the existence of distant or microscopic objects through observations by devices such as telescopes, microscopes, specialized observation apparatuses, and electronic instruments.
While we can directly interact with objects through touch, our brains ultimately perceive them as electronic nerve signals transmitted from the sensory organs of our five senses via the nervous system.
Therefore, all objects of complexity are perceived as the outcome of observations made by various instruments and devices, including the sensory organs of humans. In other words, we can represent any object as the result of its observation.

When observing microphenomena in quantum mechanics,
the observed value is randomly selected according to a specific probability distribution associated with the quantum state. The reception and measurement of radio signals almost always involve the presence of noise.
Chaos theory posits that fluctuations in the initial conditions can result in diverse phenomena exhibiting characteristics
similar to those of random systems.
Even such a quasi-probabilistic system can be effectively modeled as
a probabilistic system if the initial conditions are subject to noise-induced fluctuations.
In general, an observed value, expressed as a deterministic sequence,
is randomly selected according to a particular probability distribution,
which is called a source.
Hence, an observed value (deterministic sequence) is
inherently associated with its underlying
source (probability distribution)

Okamoto22
.

When representing an object of complexity
as the outcome of its observation,
we should represent it by its source (probability distribution)
rather than by a specific observed value (deterministic sequence)
randomly drawn from that source.
Hence, hereafter, we represent any object as the source of its observed value, which is a probability distribution.
Note that a deterministic sequence can be considered a special case of a probability distribution
(where only one value occurs with probability 1, and all others
with probability 0)
when it is not an observed value but the source of an observed value.

Complexity definition

There are two types of complexity: disorganized and
organized
Weaver48
;
Okamoto22
.
Disorganized complexity is characterized by randomness
and exemplified by systems with numerous elements haphazardly distributed. Entropy quantifies the degree of disorganized complexity.
In contrast, organized complexity is exemplified by systems such as stars, planets, galaxies, living organisms, the biosphere,
and human societies, where elements are interconnected within a system, forming an organic whole through their relations. Clearly, the complexity associated with the assumption presented in Section I.A is organized complexity.

To define the organized complexity of a source or a probability distribution,
such as the source of observed data, we employ a quantitative definition of the complexity of organized matters,
termed
organized complexity
(OC)
Okamoto22
.
Roughly, the organized complexity (OC) for a probability distribution
X
is defined
by the shortest size of a stochastic automaton form of circuit, the oc-circuit, for simulating
the distribution
X

That is, the OC of
X
is the size of the shortest description for simulating
X

This complexity definition is well-suited for our purposes as it is defined for probability distributions (the source of observed data)
and fulfills most criteria for the quantitative definition of organized complexity
Okamoto22
.

Observation systems

We must employ an observation system to represent an object as the source of an observed value.
This system, given the object, outputs an observed value (a deterministic sequence) along with
its corresponding source (a probability distribution).
While the observed value is directly measurable, the source, though not directly visible, is inherently determined by the observation system when it produces the observed value.
Determining the source or probability distribution from observed data is a challenging problem, but it has been extensively studied within the framework of model selection theory in information theory and statistics.
Notable examples include
MDL (minimum description length)
Rissanen84
;
Rissanen89

and AIC (Akaike information criterion)

The objective in this context is to identify the most probable and concise model (i.e., the source or probability distribution) that best represents the collected data.
However, this study does not focus on the methods for source identification.
Instead, we assume that the source of an object’s observed value is implicitly provided by the observation system.

For instance, consider an observation system that monitors Earth’s biosphere using a vast array of diverse instruments and devices.
This system outputs a collection of observed values, each paired with its corresponding source.
This collection consists of individual outputs from each instrument or device, rather than centrally aggregated data within the observation system.
In other words, the observation system lacks internal data transmission.

We can refine our understanding of the past universe through more accurate inferences by leveraging a wealth of observational data collected using advanced astronomical instruments.

II.3
Two Cases of Complexity Increasing

This law states that within the universe, there exist open systems in which organized complexity increases over time.
Because this law may emerge in numerous open systems within the universe,
it suggests two potential scenarios:
(1) The average (or total) complexity of the (observable) universe is increasing, and
(2) The complexity of the most (or highly) complex systems within the universe is increasing.
This hypothesis posits that these two scenarios occur. That is,
(1) The total complexity of the observable universe is increasing.
(2) The complexity of a highly complex system within the universe –
the Earth’s biosphere – is also increasing.

II.4
Consistency with the Law of Increasing Entropy

The macroscopic law of increasing complexity is not
inherently inconsistent with the law of increasing entropy.
This is because the law of increasing complexity applies to
open systems, while the law of increasing entropy applies to
isolated systems. Furthermore, both laws emerge from the same fundamental physical laws governing the universe.

II.5
Physical Laws and Computation Theory

Mathematics serves as the language for describing physical laws and theories.
Various forms of mathematics, including novel approaches, have been developed to provide suitable frameworks for describing physical laws.

It may seem unusual that the law of increasing complexity
presented here utilizes
a computational concept,
the

, given that it addresses
physical phenomena within the universe.
However, computation theory is a rigorously mathematical discipline

Just as various mathematical frameworks are employed
to describe physical laws,
computation theory provides a suitable framework for describing this macroscopic law.

III
Explanation of the Fine-Tuning Problem Using the Hypothesis

We will address the question given in Section I.B using the hypothesis introduced in Section II.B.

III.1
New Explanation

When referring to the observable universe as this universe, the hypothesis can be succinctly stated as:
“the law of increasing complexity emerges in this universe.”

Macroscopic phenomena, such as the law of increasing complexity,
emerge from the fundamental physical laws in this universe.
Therefore,
this hypothesis means that
“the law of increasing complexity
emerges from the fundamental physical laws
in this universe.”

If the fundamental physical laws were to change,
any macroscopic phenomena and laws emergent from them would
necessarily be affected.
Consequently, any alteration to the physical constants within the fundamental physical laws would likely prevent the emergence of the law of increasing complexity.

With this in mind,
our new explanation of the
fine-tuning problem, based on this hypothesis, posits that
“the fundamental physical laws are configured so that the
law of increasing complexity emerges in this universe.”

In other words,

[
Our explanation of the fine-tuning problem
]

The fundamental physical constants
are fine-tuned for the emergence of the law of increasing complexity in this universe.

Based on the law of increasing complexity,
we can consider two distinct types of universes.
The first type is a universe where the law of increasing complexity emerges, characterized by richness, diversity, and an abundance of order.
Our universe exemplifies this type.
The second type is a universe where the law of increasing complexity does not emerge, resulting in a bounded level of complexity.
Such a universe exhibits limited order and structure, potentially resembling
a chaotic state or heat death.

Let us examine an example illustrating the relation between
the fundamental physical constants and
the law of increasing complexity.
We will now revisit the example from Section I.B concerning the delicate balance between the nuclear force and
the electromagnetic force.
If the values of these constants were to deviate slightly, disrupting this delicate balance, for example,
if the electromagnetic force were to be slightly stronger,
no atoms but hydrogen could form.
This would preclude the formation of more complex structures and order,
potentially leading to a chaotic state or heat death. As a consequence, the law of increasing complexity would fail to emerge.
Conversely, if the strong force were to be slightly stronger,
hydrogen would disappear, resulting in a universe dominated by heavier atoms. This would significantly limit molecular diversity
(e.g., the absence of water, methane, ammonia, and hydride including
acid such as amino acid and nucleic acid as well as
diverse high molecular compound).
There would be no more stars and galaxies than we currently observe
(99% of matter within the current universe is hydrogen).
Consequently, only limited order and structure would arise, hindering the emergence of the law of increasing complexity.

Therefore, we hypothesize that the observed values of these physical constants are crucial for the emergence of the law of increasing complexity (although further rigorous investigation is necessary to validate this conclusion).

III.2
Advantage

Our explanation is grounded in the objectively formulated emergent phenomenon,
the law of increasing complexity,
which utilizes the mathematically defined notion of the organized complexity (OC),
and does not rely on subjective or unscientific ideas such as the anthropic principle or teleological arguments.

This explanation suggests a natural
emergent relationship between fundamental and macroscopic laws.
The emergence of life is just one phenomenon among many within this universe.
However, the emergence of this macroscopic law represents a more fundamental and profound property of this universe,
paving the way for the emergence of life, the blocks and environments
required for life, and a diverse array of complex organizations.
It is more plausible that the universe is fine-tuned for a fundamental property
rather than a specific phenomenon.
In other words,

the emergence of this macroscopic law
is a more plausible objective for the universe’s fine-tuning than the specific emergence of life
.
Therefore, this explanation addresses the question posed in Section I.B.

If we could find an (approximate) reduction
of this macroscopic law to fundamental physical laws
as shown in Section II.F, it would concretely evaluate the conditions of fundamental physical constants
so that this macroscopic law emerges.
Such a reduction would clarify whether the physical constants are
optimally fine-tuned for the emergence of this macroscopic law.

Under the multiverse hypothesis, we can consider the two types of universes as previously described: one where the law of increasing complexity emerges and another where it does not.
If the physical constants of a newly formed universe were randomly determined under the multiverse hypothesis, the law of increasing complexity would likely fail to emerge in most universes. In other words, a universe
where this law does emerge,
resulting in a rich and varied universe with abundant order,
rarely exists.
In such universes, the physical constants are fine-tuned for this law
as observed in our universe.

IV
Conclusion

This paper formulates the assumption that the universe in general,
and the biosphere on Earth in particular, is becoming more complex over time
as a macroscopic law,
the law of increasing complexity
.
We present the hypothesis that this macroscopic law
emerges in certain open systems, such as the observable universe
and the biosphere on Earth.
We then apply this hypothesis to the
fine-tuning
problem of the fundamental physical constants.
This hypothesis explains the fine-tuning problem
by proposing that the fundamental physical constants
are fine-tuned for the emergence of the macroscopic law of increasing complexity.

A crucial remaining problem is to (approximately) reduce this macroscopic law
to fundamental physical laws.
Such an (approximate) reduction
would validate this hypothesis
and concretely evaluate the conditions of fundamental physical constants
so that this law emerges.
