Approximation Theory of Total Variation Minimization for Data Completion
Jian-Feng Cai, Jae Kyu Choi, Ke Wei

Abstract
Total variation (TV) minimization is one of the most important techniques in modern signal/image processing, and has wide range of applications. While there are numerous recent works on the restoration guarantee of the TV minimization in the framework of compressed sensing, there are few works on the restoration guarantee of the restoration from partial observations. This paper is to analyze the error of TV based restoration from random entrywise samples. In particular, we estimate the error between the underlying original data and the approximate solution that interpolates (or approximates with an error bound depending on the noise level) the given data that has the minimal TV seminorm among all possible solutions. Finally, we further connect the error estimate for the discrete model to the sparse gradient restoration problem and to the approximation to the underlying function from which the underlying true data comes.

Introduction

Total variation (TV) minimization is one of the most important techniques in modern signal/image processing, and it has wide range of applications in denoising, deblurring, and inpainting
[
2
,
19
,
22
]
. In this paper, we consider the restoration from partial observations, which is to restore the underlying data
{\boldsymbol{f}}
from a given partial entrywise observation

by solving

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{g}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}{\boldsymbol{f}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}[{\boldsymbol{k}}],&{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}},\\
\text{unknown},&{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}\setminus{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}.\end{array}\right.

In
Section

1

,

) where the underlying true data

is defined, and

is a set of indices where
{\boldsymbol{g}}
is reliable. Note that this paper involves both functions and their discrete counterparts. We shall use regular characters to denote functions and use bold-faced characters to denote their discrete analogies. For example, we use
u
as an element in a function space, while we use
\boldsymbol{u}
to denote its corresponding discretized version (the type of discretization will be made clear later).

The partial observation
{\boldsymbol{g}}
can be part of sound, images, time-varying measurement values and sensor data, and the goal is to fill-in the missing region
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\setminus{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
from the measurement
{\boldsymbol{g}}

[
14
]
. Related tasks include e.g.
[
9
,
10
,
13
,
24
,
32
]
for image inpainting,
[
11
,
16
,
17

for matrix completion,
[

,

for regression in machine learning,
[
36
]
for surface reconstruction in computer graphics, and
[

,
23
]
for miscellaneous applications. Nevertheless, we forgo to give a detailed survey on these various applications and the interested reader should consult the references mentioned above for details. Instead, assuming that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\leq\eta^{2}\leq\mathrm{Var}({\boldsymbol{g}}):=\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}|}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\left|{\boldsymbol{g}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]-\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}|}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{l}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}{\boldsymbol{g}}[{\boldsymbol{l}}]\right|^{2}<\infty,

(1.3)

we focus on the following constrained TV minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{u}}~\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{1}~~~\text{subject to}~~\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}|}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\left|\boldsymbol{u}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]-{\boldsymbol{g}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]\right|^{2}\leq\eta^{2}.

(1.4)

In

is defined as

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\left|\boldsymbol{u}[{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}]-\boldsymbol{u}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]\right|,

In this paper, we will consider the following random setting. For

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\left\{0,1,\cdots,N-1\right\}^{d}

(1.6)

with
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Omega},~~|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}|=m,~~{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}~\text{is uniformly random chosen from all $m$-subsets of}~\boldsymbol{\Omega}.

(1.7)

To simplify the discussion, we only consider the regular

dimensional hypercubic grid
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. Note, however, that it is not difficult to generalize our analysis to the arbitrary
d
dimensional hyperrectangular grid. Then we denote by

the density of the pixels available. Notice that we assume that the measurement
{\boldsymbol{g}}
and the error
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
are given and fixed, even though the error
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
can be viewed as a particular realization of some random variables, e.g. the i.i.d. Gaussian noise. Hence, the only random variable in our setting is the data set
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
which is uniformly drawn from all
m
-subsets of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. Such a missing data restoration from randomly sampled pixels frequently occur when part of pixels is randomly missing due to e.g. the unreliable communication channel
[
26
]
, and/or the corruption by a salt-and-pepper noise
[
12
,
23
]
.

The focus of this paper is to study the approximation property of the above TV minimization problem
Eq.

1.4
. To do this, we assume that the underlying true data
{\boldsymbol{f}}
satisfies
\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{1}<\infty
and
{\boldsymbol{f}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]\in[0,M]
,
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, for some constant
M\geq 1
. The first condition enforces some regularity on
{\boldsymbol{f}}
and the second condition enforces the boundedness of each pixel value. In addition, since the measurement
{\boldsymbol{g}}
can be “clipped” due to e.g. the hardware limitation
[
46
]
, we further assume that
{\boldsymbol{g}}
satisfies
{\boldsymbol{g}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]\in[0,M]

{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
as well. Let
\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
be a solution to
Eq.

1.4
. Notice that, as a discrete analogy of
[
24

, it is not hard to verify that

1.4
admits at least one solution with the minimal total variation seminorm subject to the constraint. In addition, it is obvious that, if
\rho=1
(i.e.
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}
) and
\eta=0
,
{\boldsymbol{f}}
is the unique solution. Hence, we are interested in analyzing what happens when
\rho<1
. More precisely, we will show that, under some mild assumptions, the error between
\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
and

satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{\Omega}|}\left\|\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{\ell_{2}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})}^{2}\leq C\rho^{-1/2}|\boldsymbol{\Omega}|^{-\beta}\left(\log_{2}|\boldsymbol{\Omega}|\right)^{3/2}+\frac{16}{3}\eta^{2}

(1.8)

with probability at least
1-|\boldsymbol{\Omega}|^{-1}
. In

is a constant related to the regularity of

, and

(or equivalently

by solving

m
partial observation

.

In the literature, the restoration guarantees of TV minimization has been extensively studied in the various signal/image restoration tasks. For instance, the authors in
[
18
]
asserted the restoration guarantee of
\boldsymbol{u}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
with
s
-sparse gradient from
m
noiseless Fourier samples drawn uniformly and randomly. Briefly speaking, as long as the number of samples
m
satisfies
m\gtrsim s\ln N
,
\boldsymbol{u}
can be exactly restored with high probability by solving the TV minimization with the equality constraint on the Fourier samples. Later, the authors in
[
40
,
41
]
further extended the result to the
d\geq 2
dimensional TV image restoration problems. More precisely, the authors present the connection between the TV seminorm and the compressibility under the Haar wavelet (e.g.
[
29
,
39
]
) to modify a restricted isometry property (RIP) of a random sensing matrix under the Haar wavelet representation. Unlike the works in
[
40
,
41
]
with the RIP condition, the authors in
[
15
]
present a restoration guarantee based on the nullspace condition of a Gaussian sensing matrix. Briefly speaking, the authors use the so-called “Escape through the Mesh” theorem (e.g.
[
27
]
) to estimate the Gaussian width (e.g.
[
44
]
) of a cone
specified by the null space property (NSP) condition. Finally, apart from the aforementioned works on the random sensing matrices, the restoration guarantees for Fourier samples have also been extensively studied in
[
2
,
38
,
42
]
under various sampling strategies. While these aforementioned restoration guarantees are mainly in the context of compressed sensing, to the best of our knowledge, there are few works on the error analysis of the missing data restoration from partial measurements.

The analysis in this paper is different from the aforementioned previous works. First of all, our analysis does not require explicit sparse gradient. Notice that the “sensing matrix” of
Section

1
does not satisfy the RIP condition or the NSP condition for the sparse restoration. Instead, we assume the bounded TV seminorm to impose some mild regularity condition of the underlying image, rather than the sparse gradient, so our analysis is not restricted to the data with sparse gradient. In fact, our analysis mostly follows the direction of
[
14
]
on the approximation property of frame based missing data restoration. More precisely, we also use the combination of the uniform law of large numbers, which is standard in classical empirical processes and statistical learning theory, and an estimation for its involved covering number of a hypothesis space of the solution. Nevertheless, we further mention that our error analysis uses a new estimation for covering number. In other words, even though we also use the special structure of the set and the max-flow min-cut theorem in graph theory to estimate covering number, we improve the estimate in
[
14
, Theorem 2.4]
by relaxing the constraint of the radius to an arbitrarily small one. As a consequence, our analysis is not limited to
Eq.

1.4
. In fact, with the aid of our new estimate for covering number, it is not difficult to extend our analysis to the analysis of tight frame based missing data restoration in
[
14
]
with a slight modification.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In
Section

2
, we give our main results of approximation analysis for TV minimization
Eq.

1.4
. In
Sections

3
and
4
, we illustrate the applications of our main results. More precisely, we estimate the error of piecewise constant data restoration from random samples in
Section

3
. In
Section

4
, we estimate the error of TV image inpainting, and further connect the error analysis for the discrete problem

1.4
to the two dimensional

BV
functions
[
7
]
. Finally, all technical proofs are postponed to
Section

5
, and
Section

6
concludes this paper with some future directions.

Approximation error of TV minimization

In this section, we give the approximation error analysis of the TV minimization model

with the anisotropic TV seminorm

defined as

1.5
. Specifically, let
\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
be the solution to

1.4
, and we derive the explicit formulation of

1.8
. Recall that

is a data set which is uniformly randomly chosen from all

To begin with, we need to define an appropriate hypothesis space. Hence, we present
Lemma

2.1
related to the discrete maximum principle (e.g.
[
5
]
) to characterize the solution
\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume that
{\boldsymbol{g}}\in[0,M]^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
in
Section

1
is a nonconstant vector which satisfies
Eq.

1.3
. For a solution
\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
to
Eq.

1.4
, we have

\displaystyle 0\leq\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]\leq M~~~~~\text{for all}~~~{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}.

(2.1)

Proof 1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
be a solution to
Eq.

1.4
. It is obvious that, if we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}({\boldsymbol{g}})=\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}|}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\left|{\boldsymbol{g}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]-{\mathcal{E}}\left({\boldsymbol{g}}\right)\right|^{2}=\eta^{2}~~~\text{with}~~~{\mathcal{E}}\left({\boldsymbol{g}}\right)=\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}|}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}{\boldsymbol{g}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}],

then the constant vector
\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]={\mathcal{E}}\left({\boldsymbol{g}}\right)
for all
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is the minimizer of
Eq.

1.4
with
Eq.

2.1
. Hence, it suffices to prove that
Eq.

2.1
holds when

\displaystyle\eta^{2}<\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}|}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\left|{\boldsymbol{g}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]-{\mathcal{E}}\left({\boldsymbol{g}}\right)\right|^{2}.

In this case, since we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}|}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\left|{\boldsymbol{g}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]-c\right|^{2}\geq\mathrm{Var}({\boldsymbol{g}})>\eta^{2},

for any constant
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
Eq.

1.4
does not admit a constant vector as a minimizer. For each
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, we define
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]&\text{if}&\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]\leq M\\
M&\text{if}&\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]>M.\end{array}\right.

Obviously, we have
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]\leq M
. In addition, we can write
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]=G(\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}])
for
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, where
G:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is defined as

\displaystyle G(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}x&\text{if}&x\leq M_{{\boldsymbol{g}}}\\
M&\text{if}&x>M.\end{array}\right.

Notice that this
G:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
1
:

\displaystyle\left|G(x)-G(y)\right|\leq\left|x-y\right|,

(2.6)

and takes
{\boldsymbol{g}}
as the fixed point:
G({\boldsymbol{g}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}])={\boldsymbol{g}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]
for
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
.

From
Eq.

2.6
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]-{\boldsymbol{g}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]\right|=\left|G(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}])-G({\boldsymbol{g}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}])\right|\leq\left|\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]-{\boldsymbol{g}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]\right|,

which means that
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
satisfies the constraint:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}|}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\left|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]-{\boldsymbol{g}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]\right|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}|}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\leq\left|\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]-{\boldsymbol{g}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]\right|^{2}\leq\eta^{2}.

In addition,
Eq.

2.6
also gives us

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\left|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}]-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\left|\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}]-\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]\right|=\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\right\|_{1}.

Since
\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
is a minimizer of
Eq.

1.4
,
\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\right\|_{1}
should be the minimum discrete total variation subject to the constraint. Hence, it follows that
\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\right\|_{1}=\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\right\|_{1}
, and in particular, by
1
,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}=\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
. If not, then
\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}_{0}]>M=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}_{0}]
for some
{\boldsymbol{k}}_{0}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, which leads to a contradiction that
\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\right\|_{1}<\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\right\|_{1}
. Hence, it must be that
\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]\leq M
for all
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. For the lower bound, we use the same argument by considering
{\boldsymbol{g}}\mapsto-{\boldsymbol{g}}
, i.e. noting that
-\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
is a minimizer of
Eq.

1.4
. This completes the proof.
\square

With the aid of

\displaystyle{\mathscr{M}}=\left\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\ell_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}):\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{1},~\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}|}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\left|\boldsymbol{u}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]-{\boldsymbol{g}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]\right|^{2}\leq\eta^{2},~\boldsymbol{u}\in[0,M]^{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\right\},

as an involved hypothesis space. In

is the anisotropic TV defined as

is defined as

. In addition, since

\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{1}

Our error analysis relies on the capacity of an involved set. Notice that there are numerous tools including VC dimension
[
47
]
,

]

Definition 2.2
.

Let
{\mathscr{M}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
and

{\mathcal{N}}({\mathscr{M}},r)
is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}\left({\mathscr{M}},r\right)=\inf\left\{K\in{\mathbb{N}}:\exists~\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{u}_{K}\in{\mathscr{M}}~~\text{s.t.}~~{\mathscr{M}}\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{K}\left\{\boldsymbol{u}\in{\mathscr{M}}:\left\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{j}\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})}\leq r\right\}\right\}.

With this idea of covering number, we present the first relation between the solution
\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
of

1.4
and the underlying true image
{\boldsymbol{f}}
. Specifically, we estimate the probability of the event

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{\Omega}|}\left\|\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{\ell_{2}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})}^{2}\leq\epsilon+\frac{16}{3}\eta^{2}

for an arbitrary
\epsilon>0
in terms of the covering number of the hypothesis space
{\mathscr{M}}
defined as
Eq.

2.7
. Since the proof is exactly the same as
[
14
, Theorem 2.3]
, we omit the proof.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
{\mathscr{M}}
be defined as
Eq.

2.7
and
\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
be a solution to
Eq.

1.4
. Then for an arbitrary
\epsilon>0
, the following inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{\Omega}|}\left\|\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{\ell_{2}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})}^{2}\leq\epsilon+\frac{16}{3}\eta^{2}\right\}\geq 1-{\mathcal{N}}\left({\mathscr{M}},\frac{\epsilon}{12M}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{3m\epsilon}{256M^{2}}\right)

holds. In

Hence, our error estimate will be completed if we can bound the covering number in

. This leads us to relax

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}}=\left\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\ell_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}):\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{1},~\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})}\leq M\right\}.

With this

[

, Theorem 2.4]
, our estimate is also based on the quantized total variation minimization (e.g.

]

]
. In this paper, we improve

Theorem 2.4

With the aid of

{\boldsymbol{g}}\in[0,M]^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}

This section is devoted to the connection of the total variation image inpainting (e.g.

function from which a discrete image comes. In what follows, we restrict our discussions for the real-valued function of two variables (

14

All functions we consider are defined on the square domain
\Omega=[0,1)^{2}
, and we assume for simplicity that
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is a
2^{J}\times 2^{J}
cartesian grid defined as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\left\{0,1,\cdots,2^{J}-1\right\}^{2}\simeq 2^{-J}{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\cap\Omega.

In other words, we implicitly identify a
2^{J}\times 2^{J}
grid
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
with a
2^{J}\times 2^{J}
discrete mesh of
\Omega
. Note, however, that it is not difficult to extend our discussion to the generic regular square grid
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\simeq N^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\cap\Omega

\Omega
with the ones on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with fundamental period of each variable to be
1
.

Recall that a function
u\in BV(\Omega)
(is of bounded variation) if
u\in L_{1}(\Omega)
and its distributional first order derivative is a Radon measure. To simplify the notation, we use
\nabla u
to denote such a measure. We define the total variation (TV) of

by

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(u)=\int_{\Omega}{\mathrm{d}}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{1}

(4.1)

with
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1}
being the
\ell_{1}
norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

[
6
]

To begin with, let
\varphi=1_{\Omega}
. We assume that, for each
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{f}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]=2^{J}\left\langle f,\varphi_{J,{\boldsymbol{k}}}\right\rangle,

(4.2)

where
\varphi_{J,{\boldsymbol{k}}}=2^{J}\varphi(2^{J}\cdot-{\boldsymbol{k}})

{\mathbf{Q}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}:=\prod_{j=1}^{2}[2^{-J}k_{j},2^{-J}(k_{j}+1))

Given the discrete samples
{\boldsymbol{f}}
on
\boldsymbol{\Omega}

\displaystyle f_{J}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}}{\boldsymbol{f}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]\phi(2^{J}\cdot-{\boldsymbol{k}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\left\langle f,\varphi_{J,{\boldsymbol{k}}}\right\rangle\phi_{J,{\boldsymbol{k}}}

5.1
Proof of
Theorem

2.4

Theorem

2.4
is to estimate the covering number. The proof follows the line similar to
[
14
, Theorem 2.4]
. However, since we improve
[
14
, Theorem 2.4]
by relaxing the constraint of the radius
r
, we include the detailed proof for the sake of completeness. Notice that it is obvious that
{\mathscr{M}}\subseteq\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}}
and
{\mathscr{N}}\left({\mathscr{M}},r\right)\leq{\mathscr{N}}\left(\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}},r\right)
where
\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}}
is defined as
Eq.

2.10
. Hence, it suffices to bound the covering number
{\mathscr{N}}\left(\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}},r\right)
. In addition, it is easy to see that if there exists a finite set
F\subseteq\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}}
such that

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}}\subseteq\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{q}\in F}\left\{\boldsymbol{u}:\left\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{q}\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})}\leq r\right\},

we have
{\mathscr{N}}\left(\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}},r\right)\leq\left|F\right|
. What we need now is to construct an appropriate set
F
by exploiting the specific structure of
\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}}
, so that
\left|F\right|
has an appropriate upper bound.

For this purpose, let
\kappa=\lceil 2M/r\rceil
, and we define

\displaystyle{\mathscr{R}}=\left\{-\kappa r/2,(-\kappa+1)r/2,\cdots,\kappa r/2\right\}.

(5.1)

By
[
14
, Lemma 4.4]
, for each
\boldsymbol{u}\in\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}}
, there exists
Q(\boldsymbol{u})\in{\mathscr{R}}^{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{u}-Q(\boldsymbol{u})\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})}\leq r/2~~~\text{and}~~~\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\left(Q(\boldsymbol{u})\right)\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{1}.

Let

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}=\left\{\boldsymbol{q}\in\ell_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}):\boldsymbol{q}=Q(\boldsymbol{u})~~\text{for some}~~\boldsymbol{u}\in\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}}\right\}\subseteq{\mathscr{R}}^{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}.

Notice that for each
\boldsymbol{q}\in\widetilde{F}
, there may be more than one
\boldsymbol{u}\in\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}}
such that
\boldsymbol{q}=Q(\boldsymbol{u})
.

For each
\boldsymbol{q}\in\widetilde{F}
, choose
\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\in\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}}
such that
\left\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{q}}-\boldsymbol{q}\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})}\leq r/2
, and define
F=\left\{\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{q}}:\boldsymbol{q}\in\widetilde{F}\right\}
. For an arbitrary
\boldsymbol{u}\in\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}}
, there exists
\boldsymbol{q}\in\widetilde{F}
such that
\left\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{q}\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})}\leq r/2
. This implies

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})}\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{q}\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})}+\left\|\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})}\leq r,

by the definition of
\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{q}}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}}\subseteq\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\in F}\left\{\boldsymbol{u}:\left\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})}\leq r\right\}~~\text{and}~~{\mathscr{N}}\left(\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}},r\right)\leq\left|F\right|\leq\left|\widetilde{F}\right|.

Thus, the covering number
{\mathscr{N}}\left(\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}},r\right)
is bounded by any upper bound of
\left|\widetilde{F}\right|
. Notice that each
\boldsymbol{q}\in\widetilde{F}
is uniquely determined by
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{q}
and
\boldsymbol{q}[1,\cdots,1]
. Since
\widetilde{F}
is a subset of
{\mathscr{R}}^{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
, there are
2\kappa+1
choices for
\boldsymbol{q}[1,\cdots,1]
. It remains to count the number of choices in
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{q}
. Define

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\widetilde{F}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{q}:\boldsymbol{q}\in\widetilde{F}\right\}.

Then we need to bound
\left|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\widetilde{F}\right|
.

To do this, we first consider the uniform upper bound of
\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{q}\right\|_{1}
for
\boldsymbol{q}\in\widetilde{F}
. By the definition of
\widetilde{F}
and
[
14
, Lemma 4.4]
, for each
\boldsymbol{q}\in\widetilde{F}
, there exists
\boldsymbol{u}\in\widetilde{{\mathscr{M}}}
such that
\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{q}\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{1}
. Since
\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{1}\leq C_{{\boldsymbol{f}}}\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right|^{b}
for some
b\in[0,1]
by assumption, we further have

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{q}\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{1}\leq C_{{\boldsymbol{f}}}\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right|^{b}.

Hence, for all
\boldsymbol{q}\in\widetilde{F}
, we have
\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{q}\right\|_{1}\leq Kr/2
, where

\displaystyle K=\left\lceil\frac{2C_{{\boldsymbol{f}}}\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right|^{b}}{r}\right\rceil.

In addition, since
\boldsymbol{q}\in{\mathscr{R}}^{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
, each element of
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{q}
has to be a multiple of
r/2
, which means that the range of
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{q}
is a subset of
\left\{-Kr/2,-(K-1)r/2,\cdots,Kr/2\right\}^{d}
. Recall that there are
R=d\left(\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right|-\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right|^{(d-1)/d}\right)
elements in
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{q}
. Hence, the bound of
\left|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\widetilde{F}\right|
can be estimated by the number of possible integer solutions of the following inequality

\displaystyle\left|x_{1}\right|+\left|x_{2}\right|+\cdots+\left|x_{R-1}\right|+\left|x_{R}\right|\leq K.

That is,

\displaystyle\left|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\widetilde{F}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{s=1}^{\min\{k,R\}}2^{s}\binom{R}{R-s}\binom{k-1}{s-1}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{k=1}^{K}2^{k}\sum_{s=1}^{\min\{k,R\}}\binom{R}{R-s}\binom{k-1}{s-1}=1+\sum_{k=1}^{K}2^{k}\binom{R+k-1}{R-1}

\displaystyle\leq\binom{R+K-1}{R-1}\sum_{k=0}^{K}2^{k}\leq 2^{K+1}\binom{R+K-1}{K}\leq 2\left[2\left(R+K-1\right)\right]^{K}.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{F}\right|\leq\left(4\kappa+2\right)\left[2\left(R+K-1\right)\right]^{K}.

In other words, using
r\geq\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right|^{-a}
with
a\geq 1-b
and
K-1\leq 2C_{{\boldsymbol{f}}}|\boldsymbol{\Omega}|^{a+b}
, we have

\displaystyle\ln{\mathcal{N}}\left({\mathscr{M}},r\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2C_{{\boldsymbol{f}}}\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right|^{b}}{r}\ln\left(2\left(R+K-1\right)\right)+\ln\left(\frac{8M}{r}+2\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2C_{{\boldsymbol{f}}}\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right|^{b}}{r}\left[\ln\left(2d\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right|+4C_{{\boldsymbol{f}}}\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right|^{a+b}\right)+\ln\frac{10M}{r}\right]\leq\frac{2C_{{\boldsymbol{f}}}\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right|^{b}}{r}\ln\left(\left(d+2C_{{\boldsymbol{f}}}\right)20M\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right|^{2a+b}\right)

where we use the fact that
a+b\geq 1
from the choice of
a
and
b
in the final inequality. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\ln{\mathcal{N}}\left({\mathscr{M}},r\right)\leq\frac{40(2a+b)MC_{{\boldsymbol{f}}}\left(d+2C_{{\boldsymbol{f}}}\right)\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right|^{b}}{r}\log_{2}\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right|,

and this completes the proof.

\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right|=2^{2J}
. In addition, since
f(\boldsymbol{x})\in[0,M]
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega
,
{\boldsymbol{f}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]\in[0,M]
for
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. What is left is to determine
C_{{\boldsymbol{f}}}
and
b\in[0,1)
such that
\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{1}\leq C_{{\boldsymbol{f}}}2^{2bJ}
. From
Eq.

4.2
, we have

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{f}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}]-{\boldsymbol{f}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]=2^{J}\left\langle f,\varphi_{J,{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}}\right\rangle-2^{J}\left\langle f,\varphi_{J,{\boldsymbol{k}}}\right\rangle=2^{J}\left(\left\langle f,\varphi_{J,{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}}-\varphi_{J,{\boldsymbol{k}}}\right\rangle\right).

Then, since we have

\displaystyle 1_{[0,1)}(\cdot-1)-1_{[0,1)}=-\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}x}B_{2}(\cdot-1),

the direct computations gives

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{f}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}]-{\boldsymbol{f}}[{\boldsymbol{k}}]=\left\langle\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{j}},\widetilde{\varphi}_{j,J,{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}}\right\rangle,

where
\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}
is defined as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varphi}_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})=B_{2}(x_{1})1_{[0,1)}(x_{2})~~~\text{and}~~~\widetilde{\varphi}_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})=1_{[0,1)}(x_{1})B_{2}(x_{2}).

Together with the fact that
\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}(\cdot+{\boldsymbol{k}})
forms a partition of unity for each
j=1,2
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{1}\leq 2^{J}\int_{\Omega}{\mathrm{d}}\left\|\nabla f\right\|_{1}=2^{J}\mathrm{TV}(f).

By setting
C_{{\boldsymbol{f}}}=\mathrm{TV}(f)
and
b=1/2
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{2J}}\left\|\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{\ell_{2}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})}^{2}\leq\frac{64}{3}M^{2}\left(4+3\sqrt{5(4a+1)\mathrm{TV}(f)\left(1+\mathrm{TV}(f)\right)}\right)\rho^{-1/2}2^{-J/2}\sqrt{8J^{3}}+\frac{16}{3}\eta^{2}

for some
a\geq 1/2
. Hence, we establish
Eq.

4.5
by setting

\displaystyle\widetilde{c}=\frac{128}{3}M^{2}\left(4+3\sqrt{5(4a+1)\mathrm{TV}(f)\left(1+\mathrm{TV}(f)\right)}\right)\sqrt{2},

with probability at least
1-2^{-2J}
.

For
Eq.

4.7
, notice that

\displaystyle\left\|u_{J}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}-f\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leq\left\|u_{J}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}-f_{J}\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|f_{J}-f\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}.

More precisely,

\displaystyle\left\|u_{J}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}-f\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 2\left(\left\|u_{J}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}-f_{J}\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|f_{J}-f\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right).

By
Eq.

5.11
in
Lemma

5.11
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|u_{J}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}-f_{J}\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\frac{4}{2^{2J}}\left\|\boldsymbol{u}^{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{\ell_{2}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})}^{2}

In addition, by
Eq.

4.4
in
Theorem

4.8
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|f_{J}-f\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\left(16+4\pi^{1/2}\right)\mathrm{TV}(f)M2^{-J}.

Hence, we obtain
Eq.

4.7
with probability at least
1-2^{-2J}
by setting

\displaystyle C_{1}=8\widetilde{c},~~C_{2}=\frac{128}{3},~~\text{and}~~C_{3}=\left(32+8\pi^{1/2}\right)\mathrm{TV}(f)M,

all of which are independent of
J
,
\rho
, or
\eta
. This completes the proof.
\square

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we establish an approximation property of total variation minimization from incomplete data. Our error analysis is based on the combination of the uniform law of large numbers and the estimation for its involved covering number of a hypothesis space of the solution. Finally, we further connect our error analysis to the approximation of data with a sparse gradient and the approximation of underlying two dimensional
BV
functions. For the future work, we plan to establish an approximation from the data on the graph via a graph total variation (e.g.
[
45
]
). We may also consider the approximation analysis of the nonlocal total variation (e.g.
[
51
]
) for the missing data restoration.
