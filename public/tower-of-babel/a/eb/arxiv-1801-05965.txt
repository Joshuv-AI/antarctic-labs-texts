The Complexity of Combinations of Qualitative Constraint Satisfaction Problems
Manuel Bodirsky, Johannes Greiner

Abstract
. The CSP of a first-order theory T T is the problem of deciding for a given finite set S S of atomic formulas whether T ∪ S T\cup S is satisfiable. Let T 1 T_{1} and T 2 T_{2} be two theories with countably infinite models and disjoint signatures. Nelson and Oppen presented conditions that imply decidability (or polynomial-time decidability) of CSP ⁡ ( T 1 ∪ T 2 ) \Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2}) under the assumption that CSP ⁡ ( T 1 ) \Csp(T_{1}) and CSP ⁡ ( T 2 ) \Csp(T_{2}) are decidable (or polynomial-time decidable). We show that for a large class of ω \omega -categorical theories T 1 , T 2 T_{1},T_{2} the Nelson-Oppen conditions are not only sufficient, but also necessary for polynomial-time tractability of CSP ⁡ ( T 1 ∪ T 2 ) \Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2}) (unless P=NP).

1.
Introduction

Two independent proofs of the finite-domain constraint satisfaction tractability conjecture have recently been published by Bulatov and Zhuk
[
Bul17
,
Zhu17
]
, settling the Feder-Vardi dichotomy conjecture. In contrast, the computational complexity of constraint satisfaction problems over infinite domains cannot be classified in general
[
BG08
]
.
However, for a restricted class of constraint satisfaction problems that strictly contains all finite-domain CSPs and captures the vast majority of the problems studied in
qualitative reasoning
(see the survey article
[
BJ17
]
) there also is a tractability conjecture (see
[
BPP14
,
BOP18
,
BP16
,
BKO+17
]
).
The situation is similar to the situation
for finite-domain CSPs before Bulatov and Zhuk: there is a formal condition which provably implies NP-hardness, and the conjecture is that every other CSP in the class is in P.

For finite domain CSPs, it turned out that only few fundamentally different algorithms were needed to complete the classification; the key in both the solution of Bulatov and the solution of Zhuk
was a clever combination of the existing algorithmic ideas. An intensively studied method for obtaining (polynomial-time) decision procedures for infinite-domain CSPs is the Nelson-Oppen combination method; see, e.g.,
[
BS01
,
NO79
]
.
The method did not play any role for the classification of finite-domain CSPs, but is extremely powerful for combining algorithms for infinite-domain CSPs.

In order to conveniently state what type of combinations of CSPs can be studied with the Nelson-Oppen method, we slightly generalise
the notion of a CSP. The classical definition is to fix an infinite structure
\mathfrak{B}
with finite relational signature
\tau
; then
\Csp(\mathfrak{B})
is the computational problem of deciding whether a given finite set of
atomic
\tau
-formulas
(i.e., formulas of the form
x_{1}=x_{2}
or of the form
R(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
for
R\in\tau
and variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
) is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}
.
Instead of fixing a
\tau
-structure
\mathfrak{B}
, we fix a
\tau
-theory
T
(i.e.,
a set of first-order
\tau
-sentences).
Then
\Csp(T)
is the computational problem of deciding for a given finite set
S
of atomic
\tau
-formulas whether

T\cup S
has a model. Clearly, this is a generalisation of the classical definition since
\Csp(\mathfrak{B})
is the same as
\Csp(\Th(\mathfrak{B}))
where
\Th(\mathfrak{B})
is the
first-order theory of
\mathfrak{B}
, i.e., the set of all first-order sentences that hold in
\mathfrak{B}
.
The definition for theories is
strictly
more expressive (we give an example in Section
2
that shows this).

Let
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
be two theories
with disjoint finite relational signatures

\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
.
We are interested in the question when

\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
can be solved in polynomial time; we refer to this problem as
the
combined CSP
for
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
. Clearly, if
\Csp(T_{1})

or
\Csp(T_{2})

is NP-hard, then
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
is NP-hard, too. Suppose now that
\Csp(T_{1})

and
\Csp(T_{2})

can be solved in polynomial-time.
In this case, there are examples where
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
is in P, and
examples where
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
is NP-hard. Even if we know the complexity of
\Csp(T_{1})
and of
\Csp(T_{2})
, a classification of the complexity of
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
for arbitrary
theories
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
is too ambitious (see Section
5
for a formal justification).
But such a classification
should be feasible
at least for the mentioned class of infinite-domain CSPs for which the tractability conjecture applies.

1.1.
Qualitative CSPs

The idea of
qualitative formalisms
is
that reasoning tasks (e.g., about space and time) is not performed with absolute numerical values, but rather with
qualitative
predicates (such as
within
,
before
, etc.).
There is no universally accepted definition in the literature that defines what a
qualitative CSP
is, but a proposal has been made in
[
BJ17
]
; the central mathematical property
for this proposal is
\omega
-categoricity
.
A theory is called
\omega
-categorical

if it has up to isomorphism only one countable model.
A structure is called
\omega
-categorical
if and only if its first-order theory is
\omega
-categorical.
Examples are
({\mathbb{Q}};<)
, Allen’s Interval Algebra, and more generally all homogeneous
structures with a finite relational
signature
(a structure
\mathfrak{B}
is called
homogeneous
if all isomorphisms between finite substructures
can be extended to an automorphism; see
[
Hod93
,
Bod12
]
).
The class of CSPs
for
\omega
-categorical theories
arguably coincides with the class of CSPs for
qualitative formalisms

studied e.g. in temporal and spatial reasoning; see
[
BJ17
]
.

For an
\omega
-categorical theory
T
,
the complexity of
\Csp(T)
can be studied using the universal-algebraic approach that led to the proof of the Feder-Vardi dichotomy conjecture. A
signature
is a set of function and relation symbols together with an arity for each symbol. Let
\tau
be a signature. A
\tau
-structure

\mathfrak{B}
is a set
B
together with an
n
-ary function on
B
for each
n
-ary function symbol in
\tau
and an
n
-ary relation over
B
for each
n
-ary relation symbol in
\tau
.
All signatures of structures in this article are assumed to be countable.
One of the central concepts for the universal-algebraic approach is the concept of a
polymorphism
of a structure
\mathfrak{B}
,
i.e., a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{B}^{k}
to
\mathfrak{B}
for some
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
It is known that the polymorphisms of a finite structure
\mathfrak{B}
fully capture the complexity of
\Csp(\mathfrak{B})
up to P-time reductions (in fact, up to Log-space reductions; see
[
GGZ18
]
for a collection of survey articles about the complexity of CSPs), and the same is true for structures
\mathfrak{B}
with an
\omega
-categorical theory
[
BN06
]
.
In order to understand when we can apply the universal-algebraic approach to study the complexity of
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
, we need to understand the following fundamental question.

Question 1:
Suppose that
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are theories with disjoint finite relational signatures
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
. When is there an
\omega
-categorical
(\tau_{1}\cup\tau_{2})
-theory
T

such that
\Csp(T)
equals
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
, i.e., for all sets
S
of atomic
(\tau_{1}\cup\tau_{2})
-formulas,
we have that
S\cup T
is satisfiable if and only if
S\cup(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
is satisfiable?

Note that
\omega
-categorical theories are
complete
, i.e., for every first-order sentence
\phi
either
T
implies
\phi

or
T
implies
\neg\phi
.
In general, it is not true that
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
equals
\Csp(T)
for a complete theory
T
(we present an example in Section
2
).

Question 1 appears to be very difficult in general, in particular when considering the work of Braunfeld
[
Bra19
]
in the context of Proposition
2
.
However, we present
a broadly applicable condition for
\omega
-categorical theories

T_{1}
and
T_{2}
with infinite models that implies the existence of an
\omega
-categorical theory
T
such that
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
equals
\Csp(T)
(Proposition
1.3
below).
The theory
T
that we construct has many useful properties. In particular
T_{1}\cup T_{2}\subseteq T
and

(1)

if
\phi_{1}(\bar{x})
is a
\tau_{1}
-formula
and
\phi_{2}(\bar{x})
is a
\tau_{2}
-formula, both with free variables
\bar{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, then

T\models\exists\bar{x}(\phi_{1}(\bar{x})\wedge\phi_{2}(\bar{x})\wedge\bigwedge_{i<j}x_{i}\neq x_{j})
if and only if

T_{1}\models\exists\bar{x}(\phi_{1}(\bar{x})\wedge\bigwedge_{i<j}x_{i}\neq x_{j})
and
T_{2}\models\exists\bar{x}(\phi_{2}(\bar{x})\wedge\bigwedge_{i<j}x_{i}\neq x_{j})
;

(2)

For every
(\tau_{1}\cup\tau_{2})
-formula
\phi
there exists a conjunction of
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
formulas that is equivalent to
\phi
modulo
T
.

In fact,
T
is uniquely given by these three properties (up to equivalence of theories; see Lemma
2
) and again
\omega
-categorical, and we call it the
generic combination of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
.
Let
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
be two
\omega
-categorical relational structures whose first-order theories have a generic combination
T
; then we call the (up to isomorphism unique) countably infinite model of
T
the

generic combination of
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
.

1.2.
The Nelson-Oppen Criterion

Let
T_{1},T_{2}
be theories with disjoint finite signatures
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
and suppose that
\Csp(T_{1})
is in P and
\Csp(T_{2})
is in P.
Nelson and Oppen gave sufficient conditions
for
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
to be solvable
in polynomial time, too. Their conditions are:

(1)

Both
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are
stably infinite
:
a
\tau
-theory
T
is called
stably infinite

if for every quantifier-free
\tau
-formula
\phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
,
if
\phi
is satisfiable over
T
, then
there also exists an
infinite
model
\mathfrak{A}

and elements
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
such that

\mathfrak{A}\models\phi(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
.

(2)

for
i=1
and
i=2
,
the signature
\tau_{i}
contains a binary relation symbol
\neq_{i}
that denotes the disequality relation, i.e.,
T_{i}
implies the sentence

\forall x,y\,(x\neq_{i}y\Leftrightarrow\neg(x=y))
;

(3)

Both
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are
convex
(here we follow established terminology). A
\tau
-theory
T
is called
convex
if for every finite set
S
of atomic
\tau
-formulas
the set

T\cup S\cup\{x_{1}\neq y_{1},\dots,x_{m}\neq y_{m}\}
is satisfiable whenever

T\cup S\cup\{x_{j}\neq y_{j}\}
is satisfiable for each
j\leq m
.

The assumption that a relation symbol denoting the disequality relation is part of the signatures
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
is often implicit in the literature treating the Nelson-Oppen method. It would be interesting to explore when it can be dropped, but we will not pursue this question here.
The central question of this article is the following.

Question 2.
In which settings are the Nelson-Oppen
conditions (and in particular, the convexity condition) not only sufficient, but also necessary for polynomial-time tractability of the combined CSP?

Again, for general
theories
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, this is a too ambitious research goal;
but we will study it for generic combinations
of
\omega
-categorical theories
T_{1},T_{2}
with infinite models.
In this setting,
the first condition that both
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are stably infinite is trivially satisfied.
The third condition on
T_{i}
,
convexity, is
equivalent to the existence of a
binary injective polymorphism of the
(up to isomorphism unique) countably infinite model of
T_{i}
(see Theorem
3
). We mention that binary injective polymorphisms played an important role in several recent infinite-domain complexity classifications
[
BK08
,
BJP17
,
KP17
]
.

A permutation group

We call a tuple

For a fixed

To simplify the presentation of our result and its proof, we introduce the following shortcut.

A countably infinite

We now state our main result for Question 2. The proof can be found in Section

Theorem
\thethm
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}
be the generic combination of two countably infinite
\omega
-categorical structures
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
with
Property
J
and finite relational signature such that
\Csp(\mathfrak{B}_{1})
and
\Csp(\mathfrak{B}_{2})
are in P. Then one of the following applies:

•

\Th(\mathfrak{B}_{1})
or
\Th(\mathfrak{B}_{2})
is not convex; in this case,
\Csp(\mathfrak{B})
is NP-hard.

•

Each of
\Th(\mathfrak{B}_{1})
and
\Th(\mathfrak{B}_{2})
is convex, and
\Csp(\mathfrak{B})
is in P.

In other words, either the Nelson-Oppen conditions apply, and
\Csp(\mathfrak{B})
is in P,
or otherwise
\Csp(\mathfrak{B})
is NP-complete.

Again, the easiest examples for structures satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
1.3
come from
(\mathbb{Q},<)
(see Section

). The CSPs for first-order reducts of
(\mathbb{Q},<)
(see Section

and their computational complexity has been classified

.
There are many interesting polynomial-time tractable temporal CSPs that have non-convex theories, which makes
temporal CSPs a particularly interesting class for understanding the situation where the Nelson-Oppen conditions do not apply.
Generic combinations of temporal CSPs are isomorphic to first-order reducts of the

introduced in
[

]
and studied in
[

]
;

[

]
for simpler structures than the random permutation) is out of reach for the current methods. In principle, the method of canonical functions (see
[

) can be applied but leads to a huge amount of cases and seems combinatorially intractable at the moment.

Outline of the article

The rest of the article is organized as follows. In Section
2
we introduce generic combinations, study existence and uniqueness and prove a result that will help us to prove satisfiability of conjunctions of formulas.
Afterwards, in Section

2.
Generic Combinations

We already mentioned that our definition of CSPs for theories is a strict generalisation of
the notion of CSPs for structures,
and this will be clarified by the following proposition which is an immediate
consequence of Proposition 2.4.6 in
[
Bod12
]
.

Proposition
\thethm
.

Let
T
be a first-order theory with finite relational signature. Then there exists a structure
\mathfrak{B}
such that

\Csp(\mathfrak{B})=\Csp(T)
if and only if

T
has the
Joint Homomorphism Property (JHP)
, that is, for any two models
\mathfrak{A}
,
\mathfrak{B}
of
T
there exists a model
\mathfrak{C}
of
T
such that both
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
homomorphically map to
\mathfrak{C}
.

Example
\thethm
.

A simple example of two theories
T_{1},T_{2}
with the JHP such that
T_{1}\cup T_{2}
does not have the JHP is given by

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vcentcolon=}\{\forall x,y\;((O(x)\wedge O(y))\Rightarrow x=y)\}

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vcentcolon=}\{\forall x.\;\neg(P(x)\wedge Q(x))\}

Suppose for contradiction that
T_{1}\cup T_{2}
has the JHP.
Note that

\displaystyle T_{1}\cup T_{2}\cup\{\exists x(O(x)\wedge P(x))\}\quad\text{and }\quad T_{1}\cup T_{2}\cup\{\exists y(O(y)\wedge Q(y))\}

are satisfiable. The JHP implies that

T_{1}\cup T_{2}\cup\{\exists x(O(x)\wedge P(x)),\exists y(O(y)\wedge Q(y))\}

has a model

\mathfrak{A}
, so
\mathfrak{A}
has elements

u,v
satisfying

O(u)\wedge O(v)\wedge P(u)\wedge Q(v)
.
Since
\mathfrak{A}\models T_{1}

we must have
u=v
,
and so
\mathfrak{A}
does not satisfy the sentence

\forall x.\;\neg(P(x)\wedge Q(x))
from
T_{2}
,
a contradiction.

For general theories
T_{1},T_{2}
even the question whether
T_{1}\cup T_{2}

has the JHP might be
a difficult question.
But if both
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are
\omega
-categorical with a countably infinite model that
does not have algebraicity
,
then
T_{1}\cup T_{2}
always has the JHP
(a consequence of Lemma
2
below).
A structure
\mathfrak{B}
(and its first-order theory)
does not have algebraicity
if for all first-order
formulas
\phi(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

and all elements
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in B
the set
\{a_{0}\in B\mid\mathfrak{B}\models\phi(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\}

is either infinite or contained in
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
; otherwise, we say that
the structure
has algebraicity
.

Next, we will introduce
strong amalgamation
which has a tight link to algebraicity.
The
age
of a relational
\tau
-structure
\mathfrak{B}
is the class of all finite
\tau
-structures that embed into
\mathfrak{B}
.
A class
\mathcal{K}
of structures has the
amalgamation property
if for all

and embeddings

[
[

If

is the age of a countable structure

,
then

has no algebraicity if and only if

has the strong amalgamation property.

The significance of strong amalgamation in the theory of

Note that if a structure

Let

Lemma
\thethm
.

Let

\omega

\displaystyle\text{ for all }k\in{\mathbb{N}},\bar{a},\bar{b}\in B^{k}\text{ injective}\colon

\displaystyle\quad\Aut(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{1}})\bar{a}\cap\Aut(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{2}})\bar{b}\neq\emptyset\quad\text{and}

(1)

\displaystyle\text{ for all }k\in{\mathbb{N}},\bar{a}\in B^{k}\text{ injective}\colon

\displaystyle\quad\Aut(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{1}})\bar{a}\cap\Aut(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{2}})\bar{a}=\Aut(\mathfrak{B})\bar{a}\,.

(2)

Proof.

\mathfrak{B}_{i}

Hence, the

∎

Note that properties

motivates the following definition.

Definition
\thethm

(Generic Combination)
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{2}

be countably infinite
\omega
-categorical structures with disjoint relational signatures

and
\tau_{2}

In later proofs we will need means to prove satisfiability of conjunctions of formulas in the presence of parameters. This is facilitated by the following lemma. For tuples

we will use

for

Lemma
\thethm
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}
be the generic combination of the structures
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
with relational signatures
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
respectively. Let
B
be the domain of
\mathfrak{B}
and
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in B^{n}
injective tuples and
\bar{c}\in B^{m}
such that all entries of
\bar{c}
are distinct from all entries of
\bar{a}
and
\bar{b}

\displaystyle\Aut_{\bar{c}}(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{1}})\bar{a}\cap\Aut_{\bar{c}}(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{2}})\bar{b}\neq\emptyset\qquad\text{and}

(3)

\displaystyle\Aut_{\bar{c}}(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{1}})\bar{a}\cap\Aut_{\bar{c}}(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{2}})\bar{a}=\Aut_{\bar{c}}(\mathfrak{B})\bar{a}.

(4)

Proof.

There exists
\bar{d}\in\Aut(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{1}})(\bar{c},\bar{a})\cap\Aut(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{2}})(\bar{c},\bar{b})
by Property (
1
) of generic combination. Notice that we did not require
\bar{c}
to be injective. We can nevertheless apply Property (
1
) by removing duplicate entries in
\bar{c}
, applying the property to the injective version and reintroducing the duplicates afterwards. In particular,
(d_{1},\dotsc,d_{m})\in\Aut(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{1}})\bar{c}\cap\Aut(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{2}})\bar{c}=\Aut(\mathfrak{B})\bar{c}
by Property (
2
) of generic combination. Hence, there exists
\gamma\in\Aut(\mathfrak{B})
such that
\gamma((d_{1},\dotsc,d_{m}))=\bar{c}
. Furthermore, there exist
\alpha\in\Aut(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{1}}),\beta\in\Aut(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{2}})
such that
\alpha((\bar{c},\bar{a}))=\bar{d}=\beta((\bar{c},\bar{b}))
. Hence,
\gamma\circ\alpha\in\Aut_{\bar{c}}(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{1}})
and
\gamma\circ\beta\in\Aut_{\bar{c}}(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{2}})
and
(\gamma\circ\alpha)\bar{a}=(\gamma\circ\beta)\bar{b}\in\Aut_{\bar{c}}(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{1}})\bar{a}\cap\Aut_{\bar{c}}(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{2}})\bar{b}
.

For equation (
4
),

is trivial and

is implied by Property (
2
) of generic combination as follows.
Let
\bar{d}\in\Aut_{\bar{c}}(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{1}})\bar{a}\cap\Aut_{\bar{c}}(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{2}})\bar{a}
. Then

(\bar{c},\bar{d})\in\Aut(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{1}})(\bar{c},\bar{a})\cap\Aut(\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{2}})(\bar{c},\bar{a})
and therefore
(\bar{c},\bar{d})\in\Aut(\mathfrak{B})(\bar{c},\bar{a})
. Hence, there exists
\gamma\in\Aut(\mathfrak{B})
such that
\gamma(\bar{c},\bar{a})=(\bar{c},\bar{d})
, i.e.,
\bar{d}\in\Aut_{\bar{c}}(\mathfrak{B})\bar{a}
.
∎

Lemma

can be used to show the uniqueness of generic combinations.

Lemma
\thethm
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
be countable
\omega
-categorical relational structures.
Then up to isomorphism, there is at most one generic combination of
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{B}
and
\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}
be two generic combinations of
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
. We prove that
\mathfrak{B}
and
\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}
are isomorphic by a back-and-forth argument. For this, we inductively extend a bijection
\alpha
between finite subsets of
\mathfrak{B}
and
\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}
such that
\alpha
preserves all first-order
\tau_{1}
-formulas and
\tau_{2}
-formulas.
The empty map
\alpha
trivially preserves all first-order formulas.
Both structures satisfy the same first-order
\tau_{1}
-formulas without free variables and
\tau_{2}
-formulas without free variables, since they are models of

\Th(\mathfrak{B}_{1})\cup\Th(\mathfrak{B}_{2})
.
Now suppose that we have already constructed
\alpha
for the finite substructure of
\mathfrak{B}
induced by the elements of the tuple
\bar{b}\in B^{n}
and want to extend
\alpha
to another element

of
\mathfrak{B}
.
By the
\omega
-categoricity
of

, for
i=1
and
i=2
, there exists an element

of
\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}
such that

satisfies the same formulas in
\mathfrak{B}^{\tau_{i}}
as

(\alpha(\bar{b}),c_{i})
satisfies in
(\mathfrak{B}^{\prime})^{\tau_{i}}

By Property (
3

there exists

and

We now prove Proposition
1.3

n
-transitive for all

.

Proof of Proposition
1.3
.

If both
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and

do not have algebraicity then

-categorical generic combination follows from Lemma

.
If on the other hand one of them, say
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
, is
n
-transitive for all

is isomorphic to a first-order reduct of

. The generic combination

can then easily be seen to be a first-order expansion of
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
.

We prove the converse direction by contradiction. Let
\mathfrak{B}
be the generic combination of the
\tau_{1}
-structure
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and the
\tau_{2}
-structure
\mathfrak{B}_{2}

By symmetry between
\mathfrak{B}_{1}

3.
On the Necessity of the Nelson-Oppen Conditions

In this section we prove that for theories with
Property
J
the conditions of
Nelson and Oppen are not only sufficient, but also necessary for the polynomial-time tractability of generic combinations (unless P = NP). In particular, we prove Theorem
1.3
from the introduction.
In order to avoid an excess of superscripts we will drop the bar above tuples in this section. We need the following characterisation of
convexity of
\omega
-categorical theories.

{thmC}
[
[
Bod12
, Lemma 6.1.3]
]

Let
\mathfrak{B}
be a countably infinite
\omega
-categorical relational structure and let
T
be its first-order theory.
Then the following are equivalent.

•

T
is convex;

•

\mathfrak{B}
has a binary injective polymorphism.

Moreover, if
\mathfrak{B}
contains the relation
\neq
,
these conditions are also equivalent to the following.

•

If
S
is a finite set of atomic
\tau
-formulas
such that

S\cup T\cup\{x_{1}\neq y_{1}\}
is satisfiable and
S\cup T\cup\{x_{2}\neq y_{2}\}

is satisfiable, then

T\cup S\cup\{x_{1}\neq y_{1},x_{2}\neq y_{2}\}
is satisfiable, too.

The construction of the binary injective polymorphism, which is at the core of this theorem, first finds an injective homomorphism from a finite substructure of
\mathfrak{B}^{2}
to
\mathfrak{B}
and then uses compactness of first-order logic to expand its domain to
\mathfrak{B}^{2}
.

An operation
f\colon B^{k}\to B
is called
essentially unary
if there exists an
i\leq k
and a function
g\colon B\to B
such that
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=g(x_{i})
for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in B
. The operation
f
is called
essential
if it is not essentially unary.
Hence, if a function
f\colon B^{2}\rightarrow B
is essential then there exist
p,q,r,s\in B^{2}
such that
p_{2}=q_{2}
and
r_{1}=s_{1}
and

f(p_{1},p_{2})\neq f(q_{1},q_{2})
and
f(r_{1},r_{2})\neq f(s_{1},s_{2})
.
The tuples
(p,q)
and
(r,s)
are called
witnesses
for the essentiality of
f
.

The following fact is well-known. We give a short proof to illustrate how it follows from known results.

Proposition
\thethm
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}
be an infinite
\omega
-categorical structure with finite relational signature containing the relation
\neq
and such that all polymorphisms of
\mathfrak{B}
are essentially unary. Then
\Csp(\mathfrak{B})
is NP-hard.

Proof.

Let
E
be the set of all relations (on the domain of
\mathfrak{B}
) that have a first-order definition over the empty signature.
If all polymorphisms of
\mathfrak{B}
are essentially unary and preserve
\neq
, they preserve all relations in
E
. Therefore, all relations in
E
are pp-definable in
\mathfrak{B}
(see
[
BN06
]
, Theorem 4). In this case, there is a finite signature reduct of
\mathfrak{B}
which has an NP-hard CSP (see
[
BK08
]
, Theorem 1).
∎

Hence, it suffices to show that the existence of an essential polymorphism of the generic combination of two countably infinite
\omega
-categorical structures
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
implies the existence of a binary injective polymorphism. The key technical result is the following proposition.

Proposition
\thethm
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}
be the generic combination of two

\neq
to the signature. Then
\mathfrak{B}
is 2-set-transitive by Lemma
4
. Furthermore, a cross prevention formula for
\mathfrak{B}
is

\phi(x,y,u,v)\mathrel{\vcentcolon\equiv}\exists a,b\big(E(x,a,b)\AND E(u,a,b)\AND E(x,u,a)\AND E(y,v,b)\big).

This formula describes a tetrahedron between
a,b,x,u
, except that the edge
E(x,u,b)
is replaced by
E(y,v,b)
. It is easy to check that both
\phi(x,y,u,v)\AND x=y
and
\phi(x,y,u,v)\AND u=v
allow solutions
s_{1},s_{2}
respectively, such that
s_{1}(x,u,v)
and
s_{2}(x,y,u)
are injective but
\phi(x,y,u,v)\AND x=y\AND u=v
is unsatisfiable. Furthermore,
\Age(\mathfrak{B})
has the strong amalgamation property and therefore,
\mathfrak{B}
does not have algebraicity and hence it has
Property
J
by Proposition
2
.

The example can be generalized to work with any complete 3-uniform hypergraph on
n
vertices for
n\geq 4
instead of a tetrahedron (i.e.,
n=4
).

To give examples for Theorem
1.3
that are not covered by the Nelson-Oppen combination criterion we need structures with
Property
J
, with polynomial-time tractable CSP, and without convexity. Here is one such structure and an ostensibly harmless combination partner:

Example
\thethm
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
be the relational structure
({\mathbb{Q}};\neq,<,R_{\mi})

where
R_{\mi}
is defined as in Example
4
. Let
\mathfrak{B}_{2}\mathrel{\vcentcolon=}({\mathbb{Q}};\prec)
where
\prec
also denotes the strict order of the rationals
(we chose a different symbol than
<
to make the signatures disjoint).
It is easy to see that
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
satisfy
the assumptions of Proposition
1.3
,
so they have a generic combination
\mathfrak{B}
.

From Example
4
we know that
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
has
Property
J
and both have polynomial-time tractable CSPs.
Notice that
R_{\mi}
prevents binary injective polymorphisms, i.e., it ensures that
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
does not have a convex theory (see Theorem
3
). To apply Theorem
1.3
we need a symbol for the disequality relation in
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
. Let
\not\approx
be another symbol for the disequality relation on
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
. Then
\mathfrak{B}_{1}\ast(\mathbb{Q},\not\approx,\prec)
has the same CSP as
\mathfrak{B}_{1}\ast\mathfrak{B}_{2}
, because
\not\approx
and
\neq
will denote the same relation in
\mathfrak{B}_{1}\ast(\mathbb{Q},\not\approx,\prec)
. Hence, Theorem
1.3
implies that the CSP of
\mathfrak{B}_{1}\ast\mathfrak{B}_{2}
is NP-complete (and we invite the reader to find an NP-hardness proof without using our theorem).

This example shows how expansion of the signature, application of Theorem
1.3
, and subsequent reduction of the signature can extend the scope of Theorem
1.3
.

Concluding this section, we would like to state the following corollary.

Corollary
\thethm
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
be
two first-order expansions of
({\mathbb{Q}};<,\neq)
with finite relational signatures. Rename the relations of
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
so that
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
have disjoint signatures.
Then
\Csp(\Th(\mathfrak{B}_{1})\cup\Th(\mathfrak{B}_{2}))
is in P if

\Csp(\mathfrak{B}_{1})
and
\Csp(\mathfrak{B}_{2})
are in P and if both
\Th(\mathfrak{B}_{1})
and
\Th(\mathfrak{B}_{2})
are convex.
Otherwise,
\Csp(\Th(\mathfrak{B}_{1})\cup\Th(\mathfrak{B}_{2}))
is NP-hard.

This follows from Proposition
1.3
which characterises the existence of a generic combination of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, and from Theorem
1.3
which classifies the computational complexity of the generic combination.

5.
Difficulties for a General Complexity Classification

Let
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
be
\omega
-categorical theories with disjoint finite relational signatures such that

\Csp(T_{1})
is in P and
\Csp(T_{2})
is in P.
The results in this section suggest that
in general we cannot hope to get a classification
of the complexity of
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
. We use the result from
[
BG08
]

that there are homogeneous directed graphs
\mathfrak{B}
such that
\Csp(\mathfrak{B})
is undecidable.
There are even homogeneous directed graphs

\mathfrak{B}

such that
\Csp(\mathfrak{B})
is
coNP-intermediate
, i.e.,
in coNP, but neither coNP-hard nor in P
[
BG08
]
(unless P = coNP).
All of the homogeneous graphs
\mathfrak{B}
used in
[
BG08
]
can be described
by specifying a set of finite tournaments
\mathcal{T}
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be the class of all finite directed loopless graphs
\mathfrak{A}
such that no tournament from
\mathcal{T}
embeds into
\mathfrak{A}
. It can be checked
that
\mathcal{C}
is a strong amalgamation class;
the Fraïssé-limits of those classes are called the

Henson digraphs
.

Proposition
\thethm
.

For every Henson digraph
\mathfrak{B}

there exist
\omega
-categorical convex theories
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
with disjoint finite relational signatures such that

\Csp(T_{1})
is in P,
\Csp(T_{2})
is in P, and

\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
is polynomial-time Turing equivalent to
\Csp(\mathfrak{B})
.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
be the structure obtained from
\mathfrak{B}
by adding a new element
a
and adding the tuple
(a,a)
to the edge relation
E
of
\mathfrak{B}
.
Clearly,
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
is
\omega
-categorical and
\Csp(\mathfrak{B}_{1})
is in P.
Let
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
be
(\mathbb{N},\neq)
. Let
T_{i}
be the theory of
\mathfrak{B}_{i}
, for
i=1
and
i=2
. We first present a polynomial-time reduction from
\Csp(\mathfrak{B})
to
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
.

First observe that
x\mapsto a
is the only solution to the instance
\left\{E(x,x)\right\}
. Hence, given an instance
S
of
\Csp(\mathfrak{B})
with variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, we can give the following to an algorithm for
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
:

S^{*}\mathrel{\vcentcolon=}\left\{E(x_{0},x_{0}),\,x_{0}\neq x_{1},\dotsc,x_{0}\neq x_{n}\right\}\cup S

If there is a solution
s
for
S^{*}
in
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
, then the values
s(x_{1}),\dotsc,s(x_{n})
are distinct from
a
and therefore
s|_{\left\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n}\right\}}
is a solution for
S
in
\mathfrak{B}
. Likewise, if there is a solution
s
for
S
in
\mathfrak{B}
, then
s
and
x_{0}\mapsto a
gives a solution for
S^{*}
in
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
.

Next, we give a polynomial-time reduction from
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
to
\Csp(\mathfrak{B})
. Consider an instance
S
of
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
and assume without loss of generality that there is no conjunct of the form
x=y
(substitute
y
with
x
otherwise and revert the substitution after the algorithm otherwise). Now we consider the directed graph
(V;R)
where
V
is the set of variables occurring in the instance
S
and
R
is the set of pairs
(x_{i},\,x_{j})
such that
E(x_{i},x_{j})
is in
S
.

Let
C_{1},\dots,C_{n}
be the connected components of
(V;R)
where two nodes count as connected if there is some edge between them, regardless of its direction. Furthermore define
D\mathrel{\vcentcolon=}\left\{(x_{i},x_{j})\mid x_{i}\neq x_{j}\text{ is a constraint in }S\right\}
and label all
C_{k}
where
\left\{E(x_{i},x_{j})\in S\mid x_{i},x_{j}\in C_{k}\right\}
has no solution in
\mathfrak{B}
with
a
. The algorithm proceeds as follows: If there exists
(x_{i},x_{j})\in D
such that both
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
are in components labeled with
a
, then reject. Accept the instance otherwise.

To see that this is correct, notice that a connected component labeled
a
can only be satisfied by setting all variables in that component to
a
. Therefore, all rejected cases must be unsatisfiable.

Now, consider an instance
S^{\prime}
of
\Csp(\mathfrak{B})
obtained by this reduction. There is a solution to the instance iff no tournament
G_{F}\in\mathcal{T}
can be embedded in the graph
G_{S^{\prime}}
given by the edge constraints in
S^{\prime}
. If there is an embedding
f\colon G_{F}\hookrightarrow G_{S^{\prime}}
for some
G_{F}\in\mathcal{T}
, then either
E(x_{i},x_{j})
or
E(x_{j},x_{i})
is in
S^{\prime}
for all variables
x_{i},x_{j}
in the image of
f
. Hence, if we started with an additional constraint
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
(for two variables
x_{i},x_{j}
with
i\neq j
), removing this constraint would never make an otherwise unsatisfiable instance satisfiable, because the same
G_{F}
would still be embeddable. Therefore, any satisfiable instance of
\Csp(\mathfrak{B})
is also satisfiable when arbitrary additional
\neq
-constraints between different variables are enforced.
Hence, to construct a solution for the non-rejected cases of
S
, we take an injective solution
s
for the union of all unlabeled
C_{k}
of
S^{\prime}
such that
a
is not in the image of
s
and map all other variables to
a
. It is easy to check that this satisfies all constraints in
S
.
∎

We mention that another example of two theories such that
\Csp(T_{1})
and
\Csp(T_{2})
are decidable but
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
is not can be found in
[
BGN+06
]
.
