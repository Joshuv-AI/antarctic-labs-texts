Aerodynamic Risk Assessment using Parametric, Three-Dimensional Unstructured, High-Fidelity CFD and Adaptive Sampling
Runda Ji, Qiqi Wang

Abstract
We demonstrate an adaptive sampling approach for computing the probability of a rare event for a set of three-dimensional airplane geometries under various flight conditions. We develop a fully automated method to generate parameterized airplanes geometries and create volumetric mesh for viscous CFD solution. With the automatic geometry and meshing, we perform the adaptive sampling procedure to compute the probability of the rare event. We show that the computational cost of our adaptive sampling approach is hundreds of times lower than a brute-force Monte Carlo method.

Introduction

Numerical simulations have been widely applied in aerodynamic shape design and optimization for decades.
Such numerical tools provide us more underlying physics with higher resolution, and most importantly, they are more affordable compared to wind tunnel tests.
However, the key restriction for such simulations is the computational time, drastically limiting the number of configurations/designs could be tested and analyzed within the required time-limit.
The entire workflow was so time-consuming that the users have to use naive Monte Carlo method because they did not have the capability/time to run multiple sampling iterations.
Unfortunately, if the total number of sampling points is limited, naive Monte Carlo method in high dimensional sampling space cannot allocate adequate sampling points within a specific region, and hence when estimating high-impact low-probability events, high stochastic error is almost inevitable for naive Monte Carlo method.

The idea of allocating sampling points adaptively was originated while characterizing the operability limits of a supersonic combustion engine to determine the safe operation region.
[
1
]

Extra sampling points were allocated in the uncertain region between the safe operation and unstart of the engine.
[
2
]

When performing an optimization under uncertainty, introducing a surrogate model can greatly reduce the computational cost, but the results are less accurate than the original model.
[
3
,
4
]

Rather than using the approximations from surrogate models to directly replace real simulations, such approximations can be used to guide the allocation of real simulations.
Following this idea, we present an adaptive sampling procedure to accurately estimate the probability of a rare event in this article.

Specifically, we introduce three stochastic parameters in our parameterized transport airliner geometry: aspect ratio, sweep and dihedral angle of the wings.
Meanwhile, there are three freestream parameters: angle of attack, side-slip angle and the Mach number.
The parameterized geometries are first generated via Engineering Sketch Pad (ESP)
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, then meshed through Pointwise,
[
8
]
and
finally the flow solutions are computed using Flow360.
In this paper, we focus on estimating the probability of exceeding a critical lift coefficient, as an example of estimating the probability of a rare event.
Once the lift coefficients are calculated, we build a linear regression to model the relationship between the lift coefficient and the geometric/freestream parameters.
This regression model is further applied to divide the stochastic parameters into multiple strata, while the optimal number of additional cases in each stratum is determined by two factors:
First, the probability a sample lies in this stratum.
Second, the conditional probability the rare event happens given the sample lies in this stratum.
After determining the optimal distribution of additional cases, we simulate additional 99 cases.
Combining the lift coefficients obtained from 100 preliminary and additional 99 solutions, we efficiently and accurately estimate the probability of achieving a high-lift coefficient under stochastic geometric and freestream parameters.

For clarity, we summarize the adaptive sampling procedure as follows:

1.

simulate 100 preliminary cases and calculate the real objective functions (lift coefficients)
\mathcal{J}(w)=C_{L}

2.

construct linear regression model based on the preliminary flow solutions

3.

generate a lot (10,000,000) of stochastic parameters
w
, and calculate the linearly approximated objective function
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)

4.

define the strata based on the linear approximation
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)

5.

estimate the probability
P_{i}^{(1)}
that a sample lies in the
i^{\text{th}}
stratum
S_{i}

6.

estimate the conditional probability
P_{i}^{(2)}
that the real objective function
\mathcal{J}(w)>0.9
given the sample lies in the
i^{\text{th}}
stratum
S_{i}

7.

determine the optimal number of additional cases
N_{i}
in each stratum

8.

run additional cases according to the distribution of
N_{i}

9.

re-calculate the conditional probability
P_{i}^{(2)}
based on the 100 preliminary plus the 99 additional flow solutions

10.

estimate
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>0.9)
and its variance based on
P_{i}^{(1)}
and
P_{i}^{(2)}

Optimizing the Allocation of Samples: Theory

In this section, we will elaborate the theory of adaptive sampling approach and further derive the optimized distribution for sampling points.

3.1
Linear Regression of the Preliminary Flow Solutions

Since we want to accurately estimate the probability of
C_{L}>0.9
, we set the lift coefficient as our objective function
\mathcal{J}(w)=C_{L}
.
Once the objective function is determined, we perform a linear regression between the objective function
\mathcal{J}(w)
and the stochastic parameter vector

w=(\text{aspect ratio},\text{sweep},\text{dihedral},\alpha,\beta,\text{Mach})
.

For the 100 preliminary cases, the parameters are evenly distributed in the sampling space.
We first generate 10 geometries, and then for each geometry we run 10 simulations under 10 different freestream conditions.
The real
\mathcal{J}(w)=C_{L}
obtained from the flow simulations versus the linearly approximated
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)
is plotted as follows:

Figure 7:
Real objective function
\mathcal{J}(w)
versus the linear approximation
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)
. The black solid line indicates the exact approximation, the dash line indicates the
\pm 4\sigma
range, where
\sigma
is the root mean square deviation of the linear regression. The horizontal and vertical dotted lines show
\mathcal{J}(w)=0.9
and
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)=0.9
, respectively.

We are interested in estimating
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>0.9)
, so we set the critical lift coefficient
\mathcal{J}_{\text{critical}}=\mathcal{C}=0.9
.
Note that we have
\mathcal{J}(w)<0.9
for all 100 preliminary cases, which indicates the rare event does not happen in these preliminary cases, and hence we need to run additional simulations clustering around
\mathcal{J}(w)=0.9
.

3.2
Definition of Strata

In this paper, we use "strata" to mathematically describe the allocation of sampling points.
Each stratum is a set of stochastic parameters
w
, such that the corresponding linear approximations
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)
fall in a certain range.

S_{i}=\{w|a<\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)<b\}

(1)

For example, a possible way to define 1,000 strata in
(0,1)
is

\begin{split}S_{1}&=\{w|0<\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)<0.001\}\\
S_{2}&=\{w|0.001<\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)<0.002\}\\
S_{3}&=\{w|0.002<\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)<0.003\}\\
&\cdots\end{split}

(2)

Note that the construction of strata is arbitrary and we need to adjust the size and location of the strata based on our problem, i.e. we are not using equation (
2
) to define the strata in the following discussions.

3.3
The Minimization Problem

The goal of accurately estimating the probability of achieving a high lift coefficient can be converted in to a minimization problem,

\min_{N_{i}}\bigg\{\text{Var}\big[\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C})\big]\bigg\}

(3)

where
N_{i}
is the number sampling points within the
i^{\text{th}}
stratum.

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C})
can be re-written as,

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}\mathbb{P}(w\in S_{i})\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C}|w\in S_{i})

(4)

where
\mathbb{P}(w\in S_{i})
is the probability of a sample lies in the
i^{\text{th}}
stratum,
while
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C}|w\in S_{i})
is the conditional probability that the real objective function
\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C}
under the condition that
w\in S_{i}
,
and
N_{S}
is the total number of strata.

3.4
Probability of a sample lies in the
i^{\text{th}}
stratum

To begin with, let us consider the first term
\mathbb{P}(w\in S_{i})
, which can be estimated by,

\mathbb{P}(w\in S_{i})\approx P_{i}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{N_{A}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{A}}I_{w_{k}\in S_{i}}

(5)

where
N_{A}
is the total number of linearly approximation
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)
, in our project we set
N_{A}=10,000,000
.
For the
k^{\text{th}}
sample,

I_{w_{k}\in S_{i}}=\begin{cases}1,&\mbox{if }w_{k}\in S_{i}\\
0,&\mbox{if }w_{k}\notin S_{i}\\
\end{cases}

(6)

The biased sample variance of
P_{i}^{(1)}
can be written as,

\begin{split}\mathbf{Var}\big[P_{i}^{(1)}\big]&=\mathbf{Var}\bigg[\frac{1}{N_{A}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{A}}I_{w_{k}\in S_{i}}\bigg]\\
&=\bigg(\frac{1}{N_{A}}\bigg)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{A}}\mathbf{Var}\big[I_{w_{k}\in S_{i}}\big]\end{split}

(7)

where for each
w_{k}\in S_{i}
,

\mathbf{Var}\big[I_{w_{k}\in S_{i}}\big]=P_{i}^{(1)}\big(1-P_{i}^{(1)}\big)

(8)

Hence we have,

\mathbf{Var}\big[P_{i}^{(1)}\big]=\frac{P_{i}^{(1)}\big(1-P_{i}^{(1)}\big)}{N_{A}}

(9)

Meanwhile the unbiased population variance can be written as,

\begin{split}\big(s_{i}^{(1)}\big)^{2}&=\frac{N_{A}}{N_{A}-1}\frac{P_{i}^{(1)}\big(1-P_{i}^{(1)}\big)}{N_{A}}\\
&=\frac{P_{i}^{(1)}\big(1-P_{i}^{(1)}\big)}{N_{A}-1}\\
\end{split}

(10)

When the total number of linear approximation
N_{A}
is large, both
\mathbf{Var}\big[P_{i}^{(1)}\big]
and
\big(s_{i}^{(1)}\big)^{2}
are negligible,
which indicates we may reasonably assume that
P_{i}^{(1)}
is a constant rather than a random variable in further calculation.

3.5
Conditional probability

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C}|w\in S_{i})
is the conditional probability that the real objective function
\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C}
under the condition
w\in S_{i}
,

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C}|w\in S_{i})\approx P_{i}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{i}}I_{\mathcal{J}(w_{k}^{i})>\mathcal{C}}

(11)

As for the
k^{\text{th}}
sample in the
i^{\text{th}}
stratum
w_{k}^{i}
,

I_{\mathcal{J}(w_{k}^{i})>\mathcal{C}}=\begin{cases}1,&\mbox{if }\mathcal{J}(w_{k}^{i})>\mathcal{C}\\
0,&\mbox{if }\mathcal{J}(w_{k}^{i})<\mathcal{C}\\
\end{cases}

(12)

The biased sample variance of
P_{i}^{(2)}
can be written as,

\begin{split}\mathbf{Var}\big[P_{i}^{(2)}\big]&=\mathbf{Var}\bigg[\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{i}}I_{\mathcal{J}(w_{k}^{i})>\mathcal{C}}\bigg]\\
&=\frac{1}{N_{i}}\mathbf{Var}\big[I_{\mathcal{J}(w_{k}^{i})>\mathcal{C}}\big]\\
&=\frac{P_{i}^{(2)}\big(1-P_{i}^{(2)}\big)}{N_{i}}\\
\end{split}

(13)

Meanwhile the unbiased population variance can be written as,

\begin{split}\big(s_{i}^{(2)}\big)^{2}&=\frac{N_{i}}{N_{i}-1}\frac{P_{i}^{(2)}\big(1-P_{i}^{(2)}\big)}{N_{i}}\\
&=\frac{P_{i}^{(2)}\big(1-P_{i}^{(2)}\big)}{N_{i}-1}\\
\end{split}

(14)

3.6
The Optimized Distribution

Assuming
P_{i}^{(1)}
are constants, the biased sample variance of the estimated probability of the rare event can be written as,

\begin{split}\mathbf{Var}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}P_{i}^{(1)}P_{i}^{(2)}\bigg]&=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}\big(P_{i}^{(1)}\big)^{2}\mathbf{Var}\big[P_{i}^{(2)}\big]\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}\big(P_{i}^{(1)}\big)^{2}\frac{P_{i}^{(2)}\big(1-P_{i}^{(2)}\big)}{N_{i}}\\
\end{split}

(15)

In total, we have
N_{S}
strata:
S_{1},S_{2},S_{3}\cdots S_{N_{S}}
, denote the number of samples in the
i^{\text{th}}
stratum
S_{i}
as
N_{i}

f(N_{1},N_{2},N_{3}\cdots N_{N_{S}})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}\big(P_{i}^{(1)}\big)^{2}\frac{P_{i}^{(2)}\big(1-P_{i}^{(2)}\big)}{N_{i}}

(16)

The restriction is given by,

g(N_{1},N_{2},N_{3}\cdots N_{N_{S}})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}N_{i}-N=0

(17)

where
N
is the total number of flow simulations we need to run.
The Lagrangian function can be constructed as,

\mathcal{L}=f(N_{1},N_{2},N_{3}\cdots N_{N_{S}})+\lambda g(N_{1},N_{2},N_{3}\cdots N_{N_{S}})

(18)

where
\lambda
is the Lagrange multiplier.

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial N_{i}}&=\big(P_{i}^{(1)}\big)^{2}\frac{P_{i}^{(2)}\big(1-P_{i}^{(2)}\big)}{-N_{i}^{2}}+\lambda\\
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\lambda}&=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}N_{i}-N=0\end{split}

(19)

which gives the number of sampling points
N_{i}
in the
i^{\text{th}}
stratum,

N_{i}\propto P_{i}^{(1)}\sqrt{P_{i}^{(2)}\big(1-P_{i}^{(2)}\big)}

(20)

Once we know
P_{i}^{(1)}
and
P_{i}^{(2)}
, we can easily determine the optimal number of sampling points for each stratum.
Therefore, the key is to numerically estimate
P_{i}^{(1)}
and
P_{i}^{(2)}
.

4
Optimizing the Allocation of Samples: Implementation

4.1
Estimating the probability a sample lies in the
i^{\text{th}}
stratum

We generated
N_{A}=10,000,000
stochastic parameters and then compute the linear approximation
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)
for each
w
.
As aforementioned, the probability that a stochastic parameter vector
w
lies in
S_{i}
was estimated via

P_{i}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{N_{A}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{A}}I_{w_{k}\in S_{i}}

(21)

4.2
Estimating the conditional probability

P_{i}^{(2)}
is the conditional probability that the real objective function
\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C}
under the condition that
w\in S_{i}

P_{i}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{i}}I_{\mathcal{J}(w_{k}^{i})>\mathcal{C}}

(22)

Since each stratum
S_{i}
is defined based on the linear approximation
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)
,
P_{i}^{(2)}
could be interpreted as an evaluation of our linear approximation.
Specifically, if our linear approximation were 100% accurate, then we would have,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C}|\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)<\mathcal{C})&=0\\
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C}|\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)>\mathcal{C})&=1\\
\end{split}

(23)

Although our linear regression cannot be 100% accurate, it does offer us a rough estimation on the real objective function
\mathcal{J}(w)
.
Specifically, if we already known the linear approximation
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)
for a given parameter vector
w
, then the real objective function
\mathcal{J}(w)
is most likely to lie in
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)\pm 4\sigma
(see figure
7
).
If we further extend the range to
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)\pm 6\sigma
or even to
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)\pm 10\sigma
, then the real objective function
\mathcal{J}(w)
should lie in the predicted range.
In another word, if our linear regression is somehow accurate, then it is very unlikely that
\mathcal{J}(w)
falls beyond
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)\pm 10\sigma
, i.e.

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C}|\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)<\mathcal{C}-10\sigma)&\rightarrow 0\\
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C}|\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)>\mathcal{C}+10\sigma)&\rightarrow 1\\
\end{split}

(24)

Hence, rather than evenly distributing 1,000 strata from 0 to 1, we need to focus on
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)\in(\mathcal{C}-10\sigma,\mathcal{C}+10\sigma)
.
In order to quantitatively estimate the conditional probability
P_{i}^{(2)}
we assume the error between
\mathcal{J}(w)
and
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)
has a Laplace distribution.

\mathcal{J}(w)=\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)+\epsilon\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ where }\hskip 14.22636pt\epsilon\sim\text{Laplace}(\mu,b)

(25)

the probability density function is given by,

f(x|\mu,b)=\frac{1}{2b}\exp\bigg(-\frac{|x-\mu|}{b}\bigg)

(26)

the mean
\mu=0
and the variance
2b^{2}
should be identical to
\sigma^{2}
, i.e.
b=\sigma/\sqrt{2}
.
Consider a infinitesimal small stratum
S
defined as,

S=\{w|a-\delta<\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)<a+\delta\}

(27)

where
a
can be interpreted the "midpoint" of stratum
S
.
The conditional probability can be estimated as,

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C}|w\in S)\approx\int_{\mathcal{C}-a}^{+\infty}f(x|\mu,b)dx=F(+\infty)-F(\mathcal{C}-a)

(28)

where the cumulative distribution function
F(x)
is given by,

F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(u)du=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\text{sgn}(x-\mu)\bigg[1-\exp\bigg(-\frac{|x-\mu|}{b}\bigg)\bigg]

(29)

Thus,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C}|w\in S)&\approx 1-\bigg\{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\text{sgn}(\mathcal{C}-a-\mu)\bigg[1-\exp\bigg(-\frac{|\mathcal{C}-a-\mu|}{b}\bigg)\bigg]\bigg\}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\text{sgn}(\mathcal{C}-a)\bigg[1-\exp\bigg(-\frac{|\mathcal{C}-a|}{\sigma/\sqrt{2}}\bigg)\bigg]\end{split}

(30)

where we used
\mu=0
and
b=\sigma/\sqrt{2}
.

If
a>\mathcal{C}
i.e.
\mathcal{C}-a<0
, we have
S
to the right of
\mathcal{C}

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C}|w\in S)&=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\bigg[1-\exp\bigg(\frac{\mathcal{C}-a}{\sigma/\sqrt{2}}\bigg)\bigg]\\
&=1-\frac{1}{2}\exp\bigg(\frac{\mathcal{C}-a}{\sigma/\sqrt{2}}\bigg)\end{split}

(31)

If
a<\mathcal{C}
i.e.
\mathcal{C}-a>0
, we have
S
to the left of
\mathcal{C}
(majority of the sampling points)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C}|w\in S)&=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\bigg[1-\exp\bigg(\frac{a-\mathcal{C}}{\sigma/\sqrt{2}}\bigg)\bigg]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\exp\bigg(\frac{a-\mathcal{C}}{\sigma/\sqrt{2}}\bigg)\end{split}

(32)

Following this idea, we generated 100 strata within
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)\in(\mathcal{C}-10\sigma,\mathcal{C}+10\sigma)
.
Combine with another 2 strata
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)\in(-\infty,\mathcal{C}-10\sigma)
and
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)\in(\mathcal{C}+10\sigma,+\infty)
, we have
N_{S}=102
strata in total.

where

Hence we only need to estimate

utilizing our 199 flow solutions.

The biased sample variance is given by,

\begin{split}\mathbf{Var}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}P_{i}^{(1)}P_{i}^{(2)}\bigg]&=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}\big(P_{i}^{(1)}\big)^{2}\mathbf{Var}\big[P_{i}^{(2)}\big]=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}\big(P_{i}^{(1)}\big)^{2}\frac{P_{i}^{(2)}\big(1-P_{i}^{(2)}\big)}{N_{i}}\\

5.1
Comparison with Naive Monte Carlo method

In order to achieve similar level of variance, i.e. width of confidence interval, how many samples do we need if we use naive Monte Carlo method?
Denote the total number of sampling points as
N_{A}
, the estimated probability of achieving high lift coefficient can be written as,

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C})\approx\frac{1}{N_{A}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{A}}I_{\mathcal{J}(w_{k})>\mathcal{C}}

(38)

where,

I_{\mathcal{J}(w_{k})>\mathcal{C}}=\begin{cases}1,&\mbox{if }\mathcal{J}(w_{k})>\mathcal{C}\\
0,&\mbox{if }\mathcal{J}(w_{k})<\mathcal{C}\\
\end{cases}

(39)

The biased sample variance of
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C})
is given by,

\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C})\big]=\mathbf{Var}\bigg[\frac{1}{N_{A}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{A}}I_{\mathcal{J}(w_{k})>\mathcal{C}}\bigg]=\frac{1}{N_{A}}\ \mathbf{Var}\big[I_{\mathcal{J}(w_{k})>\mathcal{C}}\big]

(40)

where,

\mathbf{Var}\big[I_{\mathcal{J}(w_{k})>\mathcal{C}}\big]=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C})\big(1-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C})\big)

(41)

Thus,

\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C})\big]=\frac{1}{N_{A}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C})\big(1-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C})\big)

(42)

which gives,

N_{A}=\frac{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C})\big(1-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C})\big)}{\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C})\big]}

(43)

Since we want
\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{J}(w)>\mathcal{C})\big]\approx\text{5.191024e-08}
,

N_{A}\approx 40,852

(44)

In order to achieve the same level of variance obtained by the adaptive sampling approach, we will have to run 40,852 simulations in total when using the naive Monte Carlo method.
However, in the adaptive approach, we only simulated 199 cases in total: 100 preliminary cases plus 99 additional cases.
Therefore, the adaptive sampling approach we proposed is over 200 time faster compared to the naive Monte Carlo method, while providing the exactly same level of accuracy.

6.1
Hybrid Model

If we directly use the distribution of
\sqrt{P_{i}^{(2)}\big(1-P_{i}^{(2)}\big)}
in figure

N_{i}
for the second adaptive iteration, we will have all additional sampling points clustered in

S_{11}
and
S_{12}

\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)=0.9
will never get sampled.
Therefore, we introduce a hybrid model to mix the probability estimated from the flow solutions (observations) with the probability analytically calculated from the Laplace distribution (predictions).
Specifically,

•

when
N_{i}=0
, we have to fully depend on the Laplace distribution (prediction).

•

when
N_{i}
is large enough, we can 100% trust the probability estimated from the flow solutions (observations).

•

as for those intermediate
N_{i}
, we mix the prediction with the observations. The weight is determined by the confidence we have on the observations. Specifically, the more observations we have in stratum
S_{i}
, the more we trust the
P_{i}^{(2)}
estimated from such observations and less rely on the prediction.

Hence we introduce,

r_{i}=\frac{N_{i}}{N_{\text{confident}}}

(49)

For instance, we set
N_{\text{confident}}=10
, i.e. if we have 10 (or more) samples per stratum, then we can 100% trust the
P_{i}^{(2)}
estimated from the flow solutions.

P_{i,\text{mix}}^{(2)}=r_{i}P_{i,\text{observation}}^{(2)}+(1-r_{i})P_{i,\text{prediction}}^{(2)}

(50)

By introducing this idea of mixing the prediction with observations, we will be able to sample every stratum after running adequate adaptive sampling iterations.

7
Conclusion

This article presents an adaptive sampling approach for accurately estimating the probability of a rare event.
As an example, the adaptive sampling approach was implemented to estimate the probability of exceeding a critical lift coefficient for a set of parameterized geometries,
where the uncertainty was prescribed by 6 stochastic geometric and freestream parameters.
100 preliminary cases were first simulated.
Based on the solutions of these preliminary cases, a linear regression model was built and further applied to split the stochastic parameters into multiple strata.
The sampling space of stochastic geometric and freestream parameters was divided into 102 different strata, with 100 strata clustered around
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(w)=0.9
.
The adaptive sampling approach provided the optimized distribution of additional cases, such that the variance of the estimator was minimized.
Based on the lift coefficients calculated from 100 preliminary and 99 additional flow solutions, the probability of achieving a high lift coefficient was accurately estimated.
It has been shown that the adaptive sampling approach is hundreds of times more efficient than the brute-force Monte Carlo method, and the performance could be further improved by running multiple adaptive sampling iterations with less sampling points per iteration.
