How likely are Snowball episodes near the inner edge of the habitable zone?
R. Wordsworth

Abstract
Understanding when global glaciations occur on Earth-like planets is a major challenge in climate evolution research. Most models of how greenhouse gases like CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}} evolve with time on terrestrial planets are deterministic, but the complex, nonlinear nature of Earth’s climate history motivates study of non-deterministic climate models. Here a maximally simple stochastic model of CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}} evolution and climate on an Earth-like planet with an imperfect CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}} thermostat is investigated. It is shown that as stellar luminosity is increased in this model, the decrease in the average atmospheric CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}} concentration renders the climate increasingly unstable, with excursions to a low-temperature state common once the received stellar flux approaches that of present-day Earth. Unless climate feedbacks always force the variance in CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}} concentration to decline rapidly with received stellar flux, this means that terrestrial planets near the inner edge of the habitable zone may enter Snowball states quite frequently. Observations of the albedos and color variation of terrestrial-type exoplanets should allow this prediction to be tested directly in the future.

[
34
]
. Today, Earth is still our only confirmed example of a habitable planet, so its climate and chemistry continues to drive our understanding of habitability in general. One of the most influential models of long-term

evolution on Earth is the carbonate-silicate weathering feedback

[

cycle through geologic time remains a highly active area of research, and a number of processes likely cause Earth’s carbon cycle to deviate significantly from the standard weathering feedback
[

13

Accurate estimates of temperature and atmospheric
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
in Earth’s deep-time history are difficult to obtain, but there is no evidence for secular warming of the climate over the last 4 Gy

with time seems almost certain. However, this decline has been far from monotonic: current anthropogenic emissions aside, the variations in Earth’s surface temperature and atmospheric
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}

Motivated by these observations and previous modeling efforts, the purpose of this note is to construct a simple stochastic model of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
evolution, and to apply it to terrestrial-type planets. The model is intentionally semi-empirical, rather than mechanistic, because many of the processes that affect Earth’s
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
levels remain so uncertain. As will be shown, the transition to a stochastic view of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
evolution leads to qualitatively different conclusions compared to the deterministic picture.

Surface temperature evolution in the model is represented as

C\frac{dT}{dt}=\frac{1}{4}F[1-A(T)]-OLR

(1)

where
T
is surface temperature,
C
is the heat capacity of the ocean-atmosphere system (here in J/m
2
/K),
F
is incident stellar flux, and
OLR
is the outgoing longwave radiation at the top of the atmosphere, which we will take to be a function of
T
and the molar concentration of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
in the atmosphere. Internal climate variability, which would add a stochastic term to (
1

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
cycle.

The aim here is to point out general model features rather than to make precise predictions, so we linearize the OLR around Earth’s preindustrial surface temperature
T_{0}=288
K and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
molar concentration
f_{{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}},0}=280
ppmv

OLR\approx OLR_{0}+a(T-T_{0})-b\log(f_{{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}}}/f_{{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}},0}).

(2)

Here
a=2
W/m
2
/K following
[
1
]
, and
b=5.35
W/m
2
is the radiative forcing coefficient for
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}

[
31
]
. Logarithmic dependence of OLR on
f_{{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}}}
is a reasonable approximation in the 10 to
10^{5}
ppmv
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
and 280 to 290 K temperature range, although the value of
b
begins to increase at high
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
concentrations.

Setting
F=F_{0}+\Delta F
and noting that
\frac{1}{4}F_{0}(1-A_{0})=S_{0}=OLR_{0}
, where
F_{0}=1366
W/m
2
and
A_{0}=0.3
are Earth’s present-day received solar flux and albedo, respectively, we can write the time evolution of the temperature deviation from the baseline state
x=T-T_{0}
as

C\frac{dx}{dt}=\Delta S-\frac{1}{4}F\Delta A-ax+b\log y

(3)

where
y=f_{{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}}}/f_{{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}},0}
,
\Delta A=A(T)-A_{0}
and
\Delta S=\Delta F(1-A_{0})/4

[
1
]
.
\Delta A
is quite hard to assess in general due to cloud effects, but its dependence on surface ice coverage acts to accelerate Snowball transitions as the transition temperature is approached. As the main aim here is to assess the likelihood of a Snowball transition as a function of atmospheric
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
concentration, rather than to study the Snowball state itself, it can be safely set to zero. In addition, the climate achieves thermal balance far more rapidly than atmospheric
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
levels change, so we set
{dx}/{dt}=0
. This allows the temperature deviation from the present-day Earth value to be written as

x=\frac{\Delta S+b\log y}{a}.

(4)

Next, we incorporate
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
evolution. The evolution of the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
molar concentration
y
with time is modeled as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with an offset term
\chi

[
18
]
. For a given timestep
dt
, this means that the increment in
y
is

dy=-\tau^{-1}(y-\chi)dt+gdW

(5)

Here
g
is a constant and
dW
represents a Wiener process such that for every timestep
dt
,
dW
is equal to a value taken from a gaussian distribution with variance
dt
.
\tau
is a timescale that determines how rapidly
y
is drawn back to the mean value (either by carbonate-silicate weathering feedbacks, or some other process). At each timestep,
y
is set to
-y
if
y<0
, ensuring that
y
always remains positive-valued.

Equation (
5
) provides an inherently non-deterministic representation of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
evolution, with a linear restoration term that prevents unbounded growth in the probability distribution for
y
with time. From any starting condition, the system evolves towards a statistically steady state on a timescale
\tau
. Once a steady state is reached, the probability density function for
y
has the form

q(y)=Q\mathrm{e}^{-(y-\chi)^{2}/2\sigma_{y}^{2}}

(6)

where the standard deviation
\sigma_{y}=\sqrt{\tau/2}g
and the normalization factor

Q=\frac{2}{\sigma_{y}\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{1}{1+\mathrm{erf}[\chi/\sqrt{2}\sigma_{y}]}

(7)

ensure

\int_{0}^{\infty}q(y)dy=1.

(8)

Because
y
can only take positive values, the mean of this distribution is

\overline{y}=\int_{0}^{\infty}yq(y)dy=\chi+\sigma_{y}^{2}q_{0}.

(9)

where
q_{0}=q(0)
. The distribution variance is

V=\int_{0}^{\infty}(y-\overline{y})^{2}q(y)dy=\sigma_{y}^{2}(1-\overline{y}q_{0}).

(10)

Note that when
\chi>>\sigma_{y}
,
\overline{y}=\chi
and
V=\sigma_{y}^{2}
.

Converting (
5
) into a statement about the probability of a Snowball transition under given conditions requires the parameter
\chi
to be determined. Given the lack of evidence for secular warming of Earth over the last 4 Gy as the Sun’s luminosity has increased, the simplest assumption we can make is that
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
feedbacks set
\chi
at a value that yields
\overline{T}=T_{0}
(and hence
\overline{x}=0
) on long timescales. Taking the time mean of (
3
) and assuming separation of timescales between slow (
>100
My) evolution of
\Delta S
and more rapid stochastic
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
fluctuations yields

\overline{\log y}=-\Delta S/b=S_{0}\left(1-\alpha\right)/b

(11)

where
\alpha=F/F_{0}
is the stellar flux received relative to Earth’s present-day received flux.
\chi
is then calculated for use in (
5

\Phi(\chi)=\overline{\log y}-\int_{0}^{\infty}\log yq(y,\chi)dy

(12)

numerically. Situations where
\Phi(\chi)
has no root for
\chi>0
occur at low
\chi/\sigma_{y}
values, but this is of little practical significance here, because the planet enters a Snowball state before they are reached.

Because temperature depends directly on the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
concentration, a second probability density function
p(x)
for the temperature deviation
x
can be written as

p(x)=q[y(x)]\left|\frac{dy}{dx}\right|.

(13)

We can rearrange (
4
) in terms of
y

6

p(x)=cQ\mathrm{e}^{cx-\Delta S/b-\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{cx-\Delta S/b}-\chi)^{2}/\sigma_{y}^{2}}.

(14)

where
c=a/b
. Given (
13
), we can also write
q(f_{{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}}})=q(y)/f_{{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}},0}
and
p(T)=p(x)
. The function

is asymmetric, with rapid decline at high
x

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}

The model presented here is extremely simple and empirical. However, it is arguably at least as justified for exoplanet habitability modeling as the many other more sophisticated deterministic models of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
evolution that currently exist. It is of course possible that variance in
f_{{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}}}
does always decrease rapidly enough as stellar luminosity increases to prevent Snowball transitions. However, even if
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
variance has decreased on Earth since the Neoproterozoic, it is not at all obvious based on our current understanding of the carbon cycle that this trend will continue to hold in the future, or apply in general to Earth-like exoplanets.

The long-term
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
source in the carbon cycle (volcanism) behaves largely independently of climate until Venus-like atmospheric pressures are reached, with possible modest
positive
feedbacks due to couplings between sea level and the rate of mid-ocean ridge volcanism
[
17
]
. The
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
weathering sink has a temperature dependence, but this can readily become saturated because of local physical weathering rate limits, even when the global climate is temperate. Earth’s history in the Phanerozoic
[
29
,
28
]
and Neoproterozoic
[
16
]
indicates that the effects of tectonic processes and continental drift on
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
variability are both extremely important. Large Igneous Province (LIP) eruptions, which have appeared intermittently throughout Earth history, are capable of supplying huge quantities of weatherable basalt to the surface and hence drawing down large quantities of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
, even at the cooler equatorial temperatures expected near a Snowball transition. Indeed, weathering associated with LIPs likely played a major role in the first Neoproterozoic Snowball Earth transition
[
6
]
.

The final major source of complexity in Earth’s
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
cycle is the biosphere. It is plausible, although certainly not guaranteed, that life itself can rapidly reduce
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
variance as stellar luminosity increases, which would be a clear example of a ‘Gaian’ feedback
[
24
]
. Surface weathering by land plants is an important part of the modern carbon cycle, and it has been suggested that weathering feedbacks involving C
3

f_{{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}}}
values to 100-200 ppmv on Earth over the last 24 My
[
32
]
. However, positive biogeochemical ocean feedbacks are a plausible explanation for the ice-age
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
oscillations observed over the last 800,000 ky
[
36
]
, and coal formation in the Carboniferous may have brought Earth closer to a Snowball than at any time since the Neoproterozoic
[
8
]
. Even during the last glacial maximum ca. 20,000 years ago, estimates of global mean temperature
[
38
]
suggest only a few Kelvins of additional cooling could have been sufficient to push Earth into a Snowball state. As our current era of anthropogenic global warming makes clear, when the starting atmospheric
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
inventory is small, even relatively small changes in exchange rates with other reservoirs in the system have the capacity to cause sudden and dramatic shifts in climate.

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
on exoplanets has been proposed
