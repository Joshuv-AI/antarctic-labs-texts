Abstract




Abstract:
Working over an arbitrary base scheme
S
, we define the canonical quadratic pair on the Clifford algebra associated to an Azumaya algebra with quadratic pair. Given an Azumaya algebra
\mathcal{A}
with quadratic pair, i.e., with an orthogonal involution and a semi-trace, its associated Clifford algebra’s canonical involution is only orthogonal in certain cases, namely when
\deg(\mathcal{A})
is divisible by
8
or when both
2=0
over
S
and
\deg(\mathcal{A})
is divisible by
4
. When
\deg(\mathcal{A})\geq 8
, our definition of the canonical quadratic pair on the Clifford algebra is extended from previous work of Dolphin and Quéguiner-Mathieu, who worked over fields of characteristic
2
. When
\deg(\mathcal{A})=4
, we show that no canonical quadratic pair exists.

Keywords: Clifford Algebras, Quadratic Pairs, Quadratic Forms, Involutions, Azumaya Algebras.

MSC 2020: Primary 15A66, 11E81. Secondary 14F20, 16H05, 16W10, 20G35.

Introduction

In The Book of Involutions (
[
KMRT
]
), the final chapters are dedicated to the phenomenon of
triality
which arises from the order three symmetries of the Dynkin diagram
D_{4}
. However, these final chapters assume that the base field is of characteristic different from
2
, an assumption which is avoided throughout the rest of the book. The assumption that the characteristic is not
2
is common when studying quadratic forms and related objects, such as symmetric bilinear forms and central simple algebras with orthogonal involution, since in this case there is the well known bijective correspondence between quadratic forms and their polar symmetric bilinear forms. However, this correspondence does not hold when the characteristic is
2
, and so to handle this case the authors of
[
KMRT
]
introduce and develop the notion of a
quadratic pair
. Given a central simple algebra
A
over a field
\mathbb{F}
of any characteristic, a quadratic pair on
A
is
(\sigma,f)
where
\sigma
is an orthogonal involution and
f\colon\Sym(A,\sigma)\to\mathbb{F}
is a linear function satisfying
f(a+\sigma(a))=\Trd_{A}(a)
for all
a\in A
. Here
\Sym(A,\sigma)
are the symmetric elements of
A
and
\Trd_{A}
is the reduced trace. Such functions
f
are called
semi-traces
. These pairs
(\sigma,f)
play the role of orthogonal involutions in characteristic
2
, for example the group of algebra automorphism of
A
which preserve
\sigma
may fail to be smooth, but those which preserve
(\sigma,f)
will be a smooth semisimple group of type
D
.

The exposition of triality in
[
KMRT
]
(assuming characteristic not
2
) relies on the fact that for a central simple algebra of degree
8
with orthogonal involution
(A,\sigma)
of trivial discriminant, the Clifford algebra decomposes as a Cartesian product
\mathrm{Cl}(A,\sigma)\cong(B,\sigma_{B})\times(C,\sigma_{C})
of two central simple algebras of degree
8
, each with an orthogonal involution of trivial discriminant. However, when discussing triality in characteristic
2
, the algebra
A
will have a quadratic pair, say
(A,\sigma,f)
, and the appropriate objects to appear on the right should be algebras with quadratic pairs as well,
(B,\sigma_{B},f_{B})
and
(C,\sigma_{C},f_{C})
. Therefore, one should also have a quadratic pair on the Clifford algebra. Triality in characteristic
2
was avoided by the authors of
[
KMRT
]
since, in their words, “we did not succeed in giving a rational definition of the quadratic pair on
\mathrm{Cl}(A,\sigma,f)
”
[
KMRT
, p. 571]
. Recently in
[
DQ21
]
, Dolphin and Quéguiner-Mathieu gave a rational definition of the canonical quadratic pair on the Clifford algebra when working over a field of characteristic
2
. Their construction uses an element
a\in A
with
\Trd_{A}(a)=1
and defines the quadratic pair using the image of
a
under the canonical mapping
c\colon A\to\mathrm{Cl}(A,\sigma,f)
.

In this paper, we generalize the construction of
[
DQ21
]
to define the canonical quadratic pair on the Clifford algebra when working over an arbitrary base scheme
S
. The notion of quadratic pair was generalized to the setting over a scheme in
[
CF15
]
, and then further studied in
[
GNR
]
. In this setting we work primarily with sheaves on the category
\Sch_{S}
of schemes over
S
equipped with the fppf topology. The role of the base field is played by the sheaf of global sections
\mathcal{O}
, which sends a scheme
T\mapsto\mathcal{O}_{T}(T)
, and central simple algebras are replaced by Azumaya algebras, which are sheaves locally isomorphic to finite rank matrix algebras over
\mathcal{O}
. Unlike in the setting over fields, or even over rings, an Azumaya algebra
\mathcal{A}
may fail to have a global section
a\in\mathcal{A}(S)
for which
\Trd_{\mathcal{A}}(a)=1
, and therefore some modification of the definition from
[
DQ21
]
is needed.

1.A.
Setting over a Scheme

We work over an arbitrary fixed base scheme

[

1.B.
Quadratic Forms and Quadratic Triples

For the theory of quadratic forms over rings we refer to
[
Knu91
]

[

]

[

A

is a natural transformation

\mathcal{O}
–module

\mathcal{O}

The pair

A bilinear form

\displaystyle b^{*}\colon\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{M}^{*}

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\mapsto b(m,\underline{\hskip 8.61108pt})

is regular. A regular bilinear form has an adjoint involution
\sigma\colon\mathcal{E}\hskip-1.72218pt\textit{n\hskip-0.86108ptd}_{\mathcal{O}}(\mathcal{M})\to\mathcal{E}\hskip-1.72218pt\textit{n\hskip-0.86108ptd}_{\mathcal{O}}(\mathcal{M})

for all appropriate sections
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
and
B\in\mathcal{E}\hskip-1.72218pt\textit{n\hskip-0.86108ptd}_{\mathcal{O}}(\mathcal{M})
. A regular bilinear form also induces an isomorphism

(2)

\displaystyle\varphi_{b}\colon\mathcal{M}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}}\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{E}\hskip-1.72218pt\textit{n\hskip-0.86108ptd}_{\mathcal{O}}(\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle m_{1}\otimes m_{2}

If
\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\Ker(\Id-\sigma)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\hskip-1.72218pt\textit{k\hskip-0.86108pte\hskip-1.29167ptw}_{(\mathcal{A},\sigma)}

\displaystyle=\Ker(\Id+\sigma)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\hskip-2.15277pt\textit{y\hskip-1.29167ptm\hskip-0.86108ptd}_{(\mathcal{A},\sigma)}

f(a+\sigma(a))=\Trd_{\mathcal{A}}(a)

for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
. This is also referred to as a
quadratic pair

(\sigma,f)
on
\mathcal{A}
. The function
f
is called a
semi-trace
. A morphism of quadratic triples
\varphi\colon(\mathcal{A},\sigma,f)\to(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},f^{\prime})
is a morphism
\varphi\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
between the Azumaya algebras such that
\varphi\circ\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}\circ\varphi
and
f^{\prime}\circ\varphi=f
.

By
[
GNR
, 3.6(iii)]
, if
\sigma
is an orthogonal involution, then the submodules
\mathcal{S}\hskip-2.15277pt\textit{y\hskip-1.29167ptm}_{(\mathcal{A},\sigma)}
and
\mathcal{A}\hskip-0.43057pt\textit{$\ell$\hskip-1.29167ptt}_{(\mathcal{A},\sigma)}
are mutually orthogonal with respect to the trace form
(a,b)\mapsto\Trd_{\mathcal{A}}(ab)
on
\mathcal{A}
. Therefore, any element
\lambda\in(\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{A}\hskip-0.43057pt\textit{$\ell$\hskip-1.29167ptt}_{(\mathcal{A},\sigma)})(S)
gives a well defined map
\Trd_{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda\cdot\underline{\hskip 8.61108pt})\colon\mathcal{S}\hskip-2.15277pt\textit{y\hskip-1.29167ptm}_{(\mathcal{A},\sigma)}\to\mathcal{O}
. By
[
GNR
, 4.18]
, given a quadratic triple
(\mathcal{A},\sigma,f)
, there exists a unique element
\lambda_{f}\in(\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{A}\hskip-0.43057pt\textit{$\ell$\hskip-1.29167ptt}_{(\mathcal{A},\sigma)})(S)
such that
f=\Trd_{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda_{f}\cdot\underline{\hskip 8.61108pt})
.

If
(\mathcal{M},q)
is a regular quadratic module, then by
[
GNR
, 4.4]
it corresponds to a unique quadratic triple
(\mathcal{E}\hskip-1.72218pt\textit{n\hskip-0.86108ptd}_{\mathcal{O}}(\mathcal{M}),\sigma_{q},f_{q})
where
\sigma_{q}
is the adjoint involution of
b_{q}
and the semi-trace satisfies

f_{q}(\varphi_{b}(m\otimes m))=q(m)

for all
m\in\mathcal{M}
. Conversely, let
b\colon\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{O}
be a regular symmetric bilinear form with adjoint involution
\sigma_{b}
. If
(\mathcal{E}\hskip-1.72218pt\textit{n\hskip-0.86108ptd}_{\mathcal{O}}(\mathcal{M}),\sigma_{b},f)
is a quadratic triple then it corresponds to a unique quadratic form

q_{f}(m)=f(\varphi_{b}(m\otimes m))

on
\mathcal{M}
and the polar of
q_{f}
is
b
. The triple
(\mathcal{E}\hskip-1.72218pt\textit{n\hskip-0.86108ptd}_{\mathcal{O}}(\mathcal{M}),\sigma_{q},f_{q})
is called the
adjoint
of
(\mathcal{M},q)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be an
\mathcal{O}
–algebra which is locally a Cartesian product of Azumaya algebras. An
orthogonal involution
on
\mathcal{B}
will be an order two anti-automorphism of
\mathcal{B}
which acts trivially on the center, is therefore locally a product of involutions on each Azumaya algebra, and those local involutions are orthogonal. A
weakly-symplectic
or
symplectic involution
on
\mathcal{B}
is defined similarly. Let
(\mathcal{B},\sigma)
be such an algebra with orthogonal involution. A linear map

f\colon\mathcal{S}\hskip-2.15277pt\textit{y\hskip-1.29167ptm}_{(\mathcal{B},\sigma)}\to\mathcal{O}
satisfying
f(b+\sigma(b))=\Trd_{\mathcal{B}}(b)
for all
b\in\mathcal{B}
will also be called a
semi-trace
. The triple
(\mathcal{B},\sigma,f)
will be called an
algebra with semi-trace
. Our terminology differs slightly from
[
DQ21
]
, where
(\sigma,f)
would also be called a quadratic pair on
\mathcal{B}
despite
\mathcal{B}
not being an Azumaya
\mathcal{O}
–algebra. Such an algebra with semi-trace will locally be a cartesian product of quadratic triples. The process of defining a semi-trace using an element
\lambda\in\mathcal{B}/\mathcal{A}\hskip-0.43057pt\textit{$\ell$\hskip-1.29167ptt}_{(\mathcal{B},\sigma)}
detailed above works analogously here by virtue of local considerations.

1.C.
Clifford Algebras over Schemes

We recall the definition of the Clifford algebras associated to a quadratic form
(\mathcal{M},q)
and to a quadratic triple
(\mathcal{A},\sigma,f)
over our base scheme
S
from
[
CF15
, 4.2]
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},q)
be an
\mathcal{O}
–module with a quadratic form and let
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{M})
be the tensor
\mathcal{O}
–algebra. Then, the
Clifford algebra
of
(\mathcal{M},q)
is

\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell(\mathcal{M},q)=\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{M})/\mathcal{I}

where
\mathcal{I}
is the subsheaf of two-sided ideals generated by elements of the form
m\otimes m-q(m)
.

Analogous to the theory of Clifford algebras over fields or rings,
\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell(\mathcal{M},q)
decomposes into even and odd components, denoted
\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell_{0}(\mathcal{M},q)
and
\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell_{1}(\mathcal{M},q)
respectively. It also has a submodule isomorphic to
\mathcal{M}
which is the image of the map
m\mapsto m
, and a canonical involution
\underline{\sigma}
which is the identity on
\mathcal{M}
. This involution also restricts to the even and odd portions of the Clifford algebra and is denoted
\underline{\sigma}_{0}
and
\underline{\sigma}_{1}
respectively. By
[
CF15
, 4.2.0.7]
, if
U\in\Aff_{S}
, then
\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell(\mathcal{M},q)(U)\cong\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{M}(U),q(U))
where the right hand side is the Clifford algebra over the ring
\mathcal{O}(U)
as in
[
Knu91
, IV.1.1.2]
.

Next, let
(\mathcal{A},\sigma,f)
be a quadratic triple over
S
. Let
\underline{\mathcal{A}}
be the
\mathcal{O}
–module underlying
\mathcal{A}
and let
\mathcal{T}(\underline{\mathcal{A}})
be the tensor algebra. Since
\mathcal{A}
is an Azumaya
\mathcal{O}
–algebra, the enveloping map
\varphi_{e}
of (
1
) is an algebra isomorphism, so in particular gives an
\mathcal{O}
–module isomorphism

\underline{\varphi_{e}}\colon\underline{\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}}\underline{\mathcal{A}}\xrightarrow{\sim}\underline{\mathcal{E}\hskip-1.72218pt\textit{n\hskip-0.86108ptd}_{\mathcal{O}}(\underline{\mathcal{A}})}.

Now, for
T\in\Sch_{S}
and a section
u\in(\underline{\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}}\underline{\mathcal{A}})(T)
, the map

\displaystyle\underline{\mathcal{A}}|_{T}

\displaystyle\mapsto\underline{\mathcal{A}}|_{T}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto\underline{\varphi_{e}}(u)|_{T}(\sigma(x))

is an
\mathcal{O}|_{T}
–module morphism and is therefore equal to
\underline{\varphi_{e}}(v)
for some unique
v\in(\underline{\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}}\underline{\mathcal{A}})(T)
. Setting
\sigma_{2}(u)=v
defines an order two automorphism
\sigma_{2}\colon\underline{\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}}\underline{\mathcal{A}}\to\underline{\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}}\underline{\mathcal{A}}
.

At this point we deviate slightly from the exposition in
[
CF15
]
, but utilize an equivalent construction.

Lemma 1.2
.

Let
u\in\underline{\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}}\underline{\mathcal{A}}
with
\sigma_{2}(u)=u
. Then
(\underline{\varphi_{e}}(u))(a)=0
for all appropriate sections
a\in\mathcal{A}\hskip-0.43057pt\textit{$\ell$\hskip-1.29167ptt}_{(\mathcal{A},\sigma)}
.

Proof.

Since
a\in\mathcal{A}\hskip-0.43057pt\textit{$\ell$\hskip-1.29167ptt}_{(\mathcal{A},\sigma)}
, it is locally of the form
a^{\prime}-\sigma(a^{\prime})
. We can then compute that
(\underline{\varphi_{e}}(u))(a)
is locally of the form

\displaystyle(\underline{\varphi_{e}}(u))(a^{\prime}-\sigma(a^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=(\underline{\varphi_{e}}(u))(a^{\prime})-(\underline{\varphi_{e}}(u))(\sigma(a^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=(\underline{\varphi_{e}}(u))(a^{\prime})-(\underline{\varphi_{e}}(\sigma_{2}(u)))(a^{\prime})=0

and therefore
(\underline{\varphi_{e}}(u))(a)=0
as claimed.
∎

This means that for every
u\in\underline{\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}}\underline{\mathcal{A}}
with
\sigma_{2}(u)=u
, the map
\underline{\varphi_{e}}(u)
descends to a well defined function

\phi_{u}\colon\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{A}\hskip-0.43057pt\textit{$\ell$\hskip-1.29167ptt}_{(\mathcal{A},\sigma)}\to\underline{\mathcal{A}}.

We use these functions to define the Clifford algebra.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\sigma,f)
be a quadratic triple over
S
and let
\lambda_{f}\in(\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{A}\hskip-0.43057pt\textit{$\ell$\hskip-1.29167ptt}_{(\mathcal{A},\sigma)})(S)
be the unique element corresponding to
f
as in
[
GNR
, 4.18]
. The
Clifford algebra
of
(\mathcal{A},\sigma,f)
is

\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell(\mathcal{A},\sigma,f)=\frac{\mathcal{T}(\underline{\mathcal{A}})}{\mathcal{J}_{1}(\sigma,f)+\mathcal{J}_{2}(\sigma,f)}

where

(i)

\mathcal{J}_{1}(\sigma,f)
is the subsheaf of two-sided ideals generated by all sections of the form
s-f(s)
for
s\in\mathcal{S}\hskip-2.15277pt\textit{y\hskip-1.29167ptm}_{(\mathcal{A},\sigma)}
, and

(ii)

\mathcal{J}_{2}(\sigma,f)
is the subsheaf of two-sided ideals generated by all sections of the form
u-\phi_{u}(\lambda_{f})
where
u\in\underline{\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}}\underline{\mathcal{A}}
with
\sigma_{2}(u)=u
.

We have a composition of canonical
\mathcal{O}
–module maps

\mathcal{A}\hookrightarrow\underline{\mathcal{A}}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{T}(\underline{\mathcal{A}})\to\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell(\mathcal{A},\sigma,f),

which we denote by

(3)

c\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell(\mathcal{A},\sigma,f)

and call the
canonical mapping
. The image of
c
generates
\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell(\mathcal{A},\sigma,f)
as an algebra. The Clifford algebra is also equipped with a canonical involution
\underline{\sigma}
which is defined by
\underline{\sigma}(c(a))=c(\sigma(a))
. The following theorem is a key fact.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
CF15
, 4.2.0.14]
)
.

If
(\mathcal{M},q)
is a regular quadratic module of constant even rank, then there is an isomorphism of algebras with involution

\Phi\colon(\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell_{0}(\mathcal{M},q),\underline{\sigma}_{0})\xrightarrow{\sim}(\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell(\mathcal{E}\hskip-1.72218pt\textit{n\hskip-0.86108ptd}_{\mathcal{O}}(\mathcal{M}),\sigma_{q},f_{q}),\underline{\sigma_{q}})

This isomorphism is induced by the isomorphism of modules
\varphi_{b}\colon\mathcal{M}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}}\mathcal{M}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{E}\hskip-1.72218pt\textit{n\hskip-0.86108ptd}_{\mathcal{O}}(\mathcal{M})
of (
2
).

The Clifford algebra construction is functorial. Given a homomorphism of quadratic triples
(\mathcal{A},\sigma,f)\to(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},f^{\prime})
there is a homomorphism of algebras with involution

(4)

\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell(\varphi)\colon(\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell(\mathcal{A},\sigma,f),\underline{\sigma})\to(\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},f^{\prime}),\underline{\sigma^{\prime}})

defined by
\mathbf{C}(\varphi)(c(a))=c^{\prime}(\varphi(a))
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
where
c\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell(\mathcal{A},\sigma,f)
and
c^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},f^{\prime})
are the respective canonical mappings.

In what follows, we will use the Clifford algebra of a hyperbolic quadratic form for calculations. We outline the construction of this algebra and some of its key properties in the following example.

Example 1.5
.

We let
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{O}^{n}
be the free
\mathcal{O}
–module of rank
n
, and set
\mathbb{H}(\mathcal{V})=\mathcal{V}\oplus\mathcal{V}^{*}
. Let
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
be the standard basis of
\mathcal{V}
, and
\{v_{1}^{*},\ldots,v_{n}^{*}\}
the dual basis of
\mathcal{V}^{*}
. We define a hyperbolic quadratic form on
\mathbb{H}(\mathcal{V})
by setting
q_{2n}(x+g)=g(x)
for
x\in\mathcal{V}
and
g\in\mathcal{V}^{*}
. This is a regular quadratic form. The Clifford algebra
\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell(\mathbb{H}(\mathcal{V}),q_{2n})
is generated by the elements
v_{i}
and
v_{j}^{*}
subject to the relations

\displaystyle v_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle(v_{i}^{*})^{2}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle v_{i}v_{j}

\displaystyle=-v_{j}v_{i}

\displaystyle v_{i}^{*}v_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle=-v_{j}^{*}v_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle v_{i}v_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle=1-v_{i}^{*}v_{i}

\displaystyle v_{i}v_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle=-v_{j}^{*}v_{i}

for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
with
i\neq j
.

Let
\wedge\mathcal{V}
be the exterior algebra of
\mathcal{V}
, and let
\wedge_{0}\mathcal{V}
and
\wedge_{1}\mathcal{V}
be the even and odd parts respectively. We define two families of endomorphisms of
\wedge\mathcal{V}
. For
x\in\mathcal{V}
, let
\ell_{x}\colon\wedge\mathcal{V}\to\wedge\mathcal{V}
be the left multiplication by
x
. For
g\in\mathcal{V}^{*}
, define
d_{g}\colon\wedge\mathcal{V}\to\wedge\mathcal{V}
by

d_{g}(x_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge x_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-1)^{i+1}g(x_{i})\cdot x_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\hat{x_{i}}\wedge\ldots\wedge x_{k}

where hat denotes omission. Then by
[
CF15
, 4.2.0.10]
(which follows from
[
Knu91
, IV.2.1.1]
), there is an isomorphism of algebras

\displaystyle\Phi\colon\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell(\mathbb{H}(\mathcal{V}),q_{2n})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{E}\hskip-1.72218pt\textit{n\hskip-0.86108ptd}_{\mathcal{O}}(\wedge\mathcal{V})

\displaystyle v_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto\ell_{v_{i}}

\displaystyle v_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle\mapsto d_{v_{i}^{*}}

which restricts to an isomorphism

(5)

\Phi_{0}\colon\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell_{0}(\mathbb{H}(\mathcal{V}),q_{2n})\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{E}\hskip-1.72218pt\textit{n\hskip-0.86108ptd}_{\mathcal{O}}(\wedge_{0}\mathcal{V})\times\mathcal{E}\hskip-1.72218pt\textit{n\hskip-0.86108ptd}_{\mathcal{O}}(\wedge_{1}\mathcal{V}).

To track the canonical involution across this isomorphism, we equip
\wedge\mathcal{V}
with its own bilinear form following
[
KMRT
, pg.90]
. Let
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
be the set of integers from
1
to
n
, and then for
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}\subseteq[n]
, written in increasing order, we set
v_{I}=v_{i_{1}}\wedge v_{i_{2}}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{i_{k}}
. These
v_{I}
form a basis of
\wedge\mathcal{V}
. Now, for
x\in\wedge\mathcal{V}
, let
\overline{x}
be the element with the wedge factors reversed, i.e.,
\overline{x_{1}\wedge x_{2}\wedge\ldots\wedge x_{k}}=x_{k}\wedge\ldots\wedge x_{2}\wedge x_{1}
and extended linearly. For
I\subseteq[n]
we have that

\overline{v_{I}}=(-1)^{\frac{(|I|-1)|I|}{2}}v_{I}=\begin{cases}v_{I}&|I|\equiv 0,1\pmod{4}\\
-v_{I}&|I|\equiv 2,3\pmod{4}.\end{cases}

Let
\pi\colon\wedge\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{O}
be the map coming from the projection onto the submodule spanned by
e_{[n]}
. Define a bilinear form
b_{\wedge}\colon\wedge\mathcal{V}\times\wedge\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{O}
by

b_{\wedge}(x,y)=\pi(\overline{x}\wedge y).

Lemma 1.6
.

We have the following formula for the bilinear form
b_{\wedge}
.

b_{\wedge}\left(\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\alpha_{I}v_{I},\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\beta_{I}v_{I}\right)=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}(-1)^{(\Sigma I)-|I|}\alpha_{I}\beta_{I^{c}}.

where
\Sigma I=\sum_{i\in I}i
and
\alpha_{I},\beta_{I}\in\mathcal{O}
are coefficients.

Proof.

First, we argue that
\overline{v_{I}}\wedge v_{I^{c}}=(-1)^{(\Sigma I)-|I|}v_{[n]}
. Let
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
, written in increasing order. Then

\overline{v_{I}}\wedge v_{I^{c}}=v_{i_{k}}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{i_{1}}\wedge v_{I^{c}}.

If we commute the leading factor
v_{i_{k}}
to the right until it is in the correct position, it must pass exactly those
v_{\ell}
(either in
\overline{v_{I}}
or in
v_{I^{c}}
) for which
\ell<i_{k}
. Therefore, this process produces a factor of
(-1)^{i_{k}-1}
and we have

\overline{v_{I}}\wedge v_{I^{c}}=(-1)^{i_{k}-1}\overline{v_{I\backslash\{v_{i_{k}}\}}}\wedge v_{I^{c}\cup\{v_{i_{k}}\}}.

Repeating this process for all factors in
v_{I}
, we obtain

\overline{v_{I}}\wedge v_{I^{c}}=(-1)^{\sum_{i\in I}(i-1)}v_{[n]}=(-1)^{\Sigma I-|I|}v_{[n]}.

Now we may simply compute.

\displaystyle b_{\wedge}\left(\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\alpha_{I}v_{I},\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\beta_{I}v_{I}\right)

\displaystyle=\pi\left(\left(\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\alpha_{I}\overline{v_{I}}\right)\wedge\left(\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\beta_{I}v_{I}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\pi\left(\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\alpha_{I}\beta_{I^{c}}\overline{v_{I}}\wedge v_{I^{c}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}(-1)^{(\Sigma I)-|I|}\alpha_{I}\beta_{I^{c}}.

In the second equality, we both expanded the product of the two sums and dropped any term for which
\pi(\overline{v_{I}}\wedge v_{J})=0
, which are those where
J\neq I^{c}
. This justifies the claimed formula.
∎

Lemma 1.7
.

We have the following.

(i)

b_{\wedge}
is symmetric when
n\equiv 0,1\pmod{4}
and it is alternating when
n\equiv 2,3\pmod{4}
.

(ii)

b_{\wedge}
is regular.

(iii)

Since
b_{\wedge}
is regular, it has an adjoint involution
\tau
. This involution corresponds to
\underline{\sigma}
via
\Phi
, i.e.,
\tau=\Phi\circ\underline{\sigma}\circ\Phi^{-1}
.

Proof.

(i)
: Using
1.6
but reversing the arguments yields

\displaystyle b_{\wedge}\left(\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\beta_{I}v_{I},\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\alpha_{I}v_{I}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}(-1)^{(\Sigma I)-|I|}\beta_{I}\alpha_{I^{c}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}(-1)^{(\Sigma I^{c})-|I^{c}|}\alpha_{I}\beta_{I^{c}}.

Hence we need to compare the coefficients
(-1)^{(\Sigma I)-|I|}
and
(-1)^{(\Sigma I^{c})-|I^{c}|}
. Their product is

(-1)^{(\Sigma I)-|I|}\cdot(-1)^{(\Sigma I^{c})-|I^{c}|}=(-1)^{\Sigma[n]-n}=(-1)^{\Sigma[n-1]}=(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}

which is
1
if
n\equiv 0,1\pmod{4}
and is
-1
if
n\equiv 2,3\pmod{4}
. Therefore, when
n\equiv 0,1\pmod{4}
the coefficients agree and
b_{\wedge}
is symmetric.

Now assume
n\equiv 2,3\pmod{4}
. The expression

b_{\wedge}\left(\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\alpha_{I}v_{I},\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\alpha_{I}v_{I}\right)=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}(-1)^{(\Sigma I)-|I|}\alpha_{I}\alpha_{I^{c}}

will contain the terms
(-1)^{(\Sigma I)-|I|}\alpha_{I}\alpha_{I^{c}}
and
(-1)^{(\Sigma I^{c})-|I^{c}|}\alpha_{I^{c}}\alpha_{I}
in pairs. By the computation above, these are negatives of one another and hence cancel. Thus we see that
b_{\wedge}
is alternating in this case.

(ii)
: Pick any order on the basis
\{v_{I}\mid I\subseteq[n]\}
of
\wedge\mathcal{V}
. Once again using the formula of
1.6
, we see that the bilinear form
b_{\wedge}
will be given by
b(x,y)=x^{T}By
where we view
x
and
y
as column vectors and where
B=[\beta_{IJ}]
is the matrix with entries

\beta_{IJ}=\begin{cases}0&J\neq I^{c}\\
(-1)^{\Sigma I-|I|}&J=I^{c}.\end{cases}

This matrix
B
is invertible, so
b_{\wedge}
is regular.

(iii)
: To verify that
\tau=\Phi\circ\underline{\sigma}\circ\Phi^{-1}
, we only need to show that
\tau
fixes the elements
\ell_{v_{i}},d_{v_{i}^{*}}\in\mathcal{E}\hskip-1.72218pt\textit{n\hskip-0.86108ptd}_{\mathcal{O}}(\wedge\mathcal{N})
since the defining property of
\underline{\sigma}
is that it acts as the identity on
\mathbb{H}(\mathcal{V})\subseteq\mathcal{C}\hskip-1.29167pt\ell(\mathbb{H}(\mathcal{V}),q_{2n})
. First, we see that

b(x,\ell_{v_{i}}(y))=\pi(\overline{x}\wedge(v_{i}\wedge y))=\pi(\overline{(v_{i}\wedge x)}\wedge y)=b(\ell_{v_{i}}(x),y)

and so
\tau(\ell_{v_{i}})=\ell_{v_{i}}
as desired. Second, to verify that
\tau(d_{v_{i}^{*}})=d_{v_{i}^{*}}
it suffices to only consider basis elements of
\wedge\mathcal{V}
. Let
J,K\subseteq[n]
. If
J\cup K\neq[n]
or
J\cap K\neq\{i\}
, then it is clear that both

b_{\wedge}(v_{J},d_{v_{i}^{*}}(v_{K}))=0\text{ and }b_{\wedge}(d_{v_{i}^{*}}(v_{J}),v_{K})=0.

Now assume that
J\cup K=[n]
and
J\cap K=\{i\}
. Let
J=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{p}=i,\ldots,j_{r}\}
and
K=\{k_{1},\ldots,k_{q}=i,\ldots,k_{s}\}
. Then

\displaystyle\overline{v_{J}}\wedge d_{v_{i}^{*}}(v_{K})

\displaystyle=\overline{v_{J}}\wedge(-1)^{q+1}v_{J^{c}}=(-1)^{q+1}(-1)^{\Sigma J-|J|}v_{[n]},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\overline{d_{v_{i}^{*}}(v_{J})}\wedge v_{K}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{p+1}\overline{v_{K^{c}}}\wedge v_{K}=(-1)^{p+1}(-1)^{\Sigma K^{c}-|K^{c}|}v_{[n]}.

Due to the assumptions on
J
and
K
, we have that
\Sigma K^{c}=\Sigma J-i
and
|K^{c}|=|J|-1
. Furthermore, by counting the number of elements of
[n]
less than
i
in
J
and
K
, we get that
i-1=(p-1)+(q-1)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle(-1)^{p+1}(-1)^{\Sigma K^{c}-|K^{c}|}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{p+1}(-1)^{\Sigma J-|J|-(i-1)}=(-1)^{(p+1)-(i-1)}(-1)^{\Sigma J-|J|}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{q+1}(-1)^{\Sigma J-|J|}

and hence the above two expressions are equal. In summary,
b_{\wedge}(v_{J},d_{v_{i}^{*}}(v_{K}))=b_{\wedge}(d_{v_{i}^{*}}(v_{J}),v_{K})
for all
J,K\subseteq[n]
, and so
\tau(d_{v_{i}^{*}})=d_{v_{i}^{*}}
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Next, let

1.E.
Twisting via Torsors

Let
\mathbf{G}\colon\Sch_{S}\to\Grp
be any sheaf of groups. A
\mathbf{G}
–torsor
is a sheaf of sets
\mathcal{P}\colon\Sch_{S}\to\Grp
with a right
\mathbf{G}
–action, i.e., a natural transformation
\mathcal{P}\times\mathbf{G}\to\mathcal{P}
such that

(i)

for all
T\in\Sch_{S}
, the map
\mathcal{P}(T)\times\mathbf{G}(T)\to\mathcal{P}(T)
defines a simply transitive right
\mathbf{G}(T)
–action on
\mathcal{P}(T)
, and

(ii)

there exists a cover
\{T_{i}\to S\}_{i\in I}
over which
\mathcal{P}(T_{i})\neq\O
for all
i\in I
.

As
(ii)
above hints, a torsor
\mathcal{P}
may have
\mathcal{P}(T)=\O
for some

and in this case the map
\O\times\mathbf{G}(T)\to\O
vacuously gives a simply transitive action of
\mathbf{G}(T)
on
\O
. A morphism of
\mathbf{G}
–torsors is a natural transformation of sheaves which is equivariant with respect to the right
\mathbf{G}
–actions. Any morphism of torsors must be an isomorphism. The

is
\mathbf{G}
itself viewed as a sheaf of sets with right action coming from right multiplication.

We will use contracted products with respect to torsors. If
\mathcal{P}
is a
\mathbf{G}
–torsor and
\mathcal{F}
is another sheaf of sets equipped with a left action of

, then the contracted product as defined in
[

, 2.2.2.9]

\mathcal{P}\wedge^{\mathbf{G}}\mathcal{F}=((\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{F})/\sim)^{\sharp},

where the

\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{F}
modulo the equivalence relation
(p\cdot g,f)\sim(p,g\cdot f)
for all appropriate sections
g\in\mathbf{G}
,
p\in\mathcal{P}
, and
f\in\mathcal{F}
. We call
\mathcal{P}\wedge^{\mathbf{G}}\mathcal{F}
the

\mathcal{F}
by
\mathcal{P}
.

Two sheaves

on
\Sch_{S}
are called

of one another if there exists a cover

such that

for all

. In this case, the sheaf of internal isomorphisms

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\hskip-1.50696pt\textit{s\hskip-0.6458pto\hskip-0.6458ptm}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G})\colon\Sch_{S}

\displaystyle\to\Sets

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\mapsto\Isom(\mathcal{F}|_{T},\mathcal{G}|_{T})

is an
\mathbf{Aut}(\mathcal{F})
–torsor. Furthermore,
\mathcal{I}\hskip-1.50696pt\textit{s\hskip-0.6458pto\hskip-0.6458ptm}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G})\wedge^{\mathbf{Aut}(\mathcal{F})}\mathcal{F}\cong\mathcal{G}
. We will primarily use this in the context of quadratic triples. For example, if
(\mathcal{A},\sigma,f)
is a quadratic triple of constant rank

, then the sheaf of quadratic triple isomorphisms

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{I}\hskip-1.50696pt\textit{s\hskip-0.6458pto\hskip-0.6458ptm}((\mathcal{E}\hskip-1.72218pt\textit{n\hskip-0.86108ptd}_{\mathcal{O}}(\mathbb{H}(\mathcal{V})),\sigma_{q_{2n}},f_{q_{2n}}),(\mathcal{A},\sigma,f))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(xx^{*}-yy^{*})v_{k}v_{k}^{*}-v_{k}^{*}v_{k}(x^{*}x-y^{*}y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(xx^{*}-yy^{*})v_{k}v_{k}^{*}-(1-v_{k}v_{k}^{*})(1-xx^{*}-1+yy^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(xx^{*}-yy^{*})v_{k}v_{k}^{*}+(xx^{*}-yy^{*})-v_{k}v_{k}^{*}(xx^{*}-yy^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(xx^{*}-yy^{*})v_{k}v_{k}^{*}+(xx^{*}-yy^{*})-(xx^{*}-yy^{*})v_{k}v_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle xx^{*}-yy^{*}

and therefore
