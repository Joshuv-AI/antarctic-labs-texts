Rate Constant Matrix Contraction Method for Stiff Master Equations with Detailed Balance
Satoru Iwata, Taihei Oki, Shinsaku Sakaue

Abstract
This paper considers master equations for Markovian kinetic schemes that possess the detailed balance property. Chemical kinetics, as a prime example, often yields large-scale, highly stiff equations. Based on chemical intuitions, Sumiya et al. (2015) presented the rate constant matrix contraction (RCMC) method that computes approximate solutions to such intractable systems. This paper aims to establish a mathematical foundation for the RCMC method. We present a reformulated RCMC method in terms of matrix computation, deriving the method from several natural requirements. We then perform a theoretical error analysis based on eigendecomposition and discuss implementation details caring about computational efficiency and numerical stability. Through numerical experiments on synthetic and real kinetic models, we validate the efficiency, numerical stability, and accuracy of the presented method. Keywords : Chemical kinetics, differential equations, quasi-steady-state approximation, rate constant matrix contraction method

Introduction

A
master equation
is a system of first-order ordinary differential equations (ODEs) describing the time evolution of the quantity or probability distribution on a finite number of states.
This paper focuses on master equations for
Markovian kinetic schemes
, which are linear ODEs written in the form of

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=Kx(t),

(2)

where
x(t)
is an
n
-dimensional vector whose
i
th component
x_{i}(t)
represents the quantity or probability of occupying state
i
at time
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
K
is an
n\times n
matrix whose
(i,j)
entry
K_{ij}
with
i\neq j
is a non-negative value called the (
transition
)
rate constant
from state
j
to state
i
.
Each diagonal entry in
K
is the minus of the sum of off-diagonals in the column to which the diagonal belongs, i.e.,
K_{jj}=-\sum_{i\neq j}K_{ij}
.
This equality implies that the equation satisfies the
conservation law
, i.e.,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}(t)
is preserved throughout time evolution.
The matrix
K
is called a
rate constant matrix
.

A master equation (
2
) is said to satisfy the
detailed balance condition
if there exists a positive vector
\pi=\mathopen{\left\lparen\vphantom{{\pi_{i}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}\pi_{i}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{\pi_{i}}}\right\rparen}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle K_{ij}\pi_{j}=K_{ji}\pi_{i}

(3)

holds for each pair of
i
and
j
.
The condition (
3
) implies
K\pi=\mathbf{0}
.
If the system is irreducible (i.e., the supporting graph of
K
is (strongly) connected),
\pi
is a
stationary
(or
equilibrium
)
distribution
, which is a unique distribution such that
x(t)
converges to
\pi
as
t\to\infty
regardless of the initial distribution
p={(p_{i})}_{i}=x(0)
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}
, as expounded in
[
1
]
.

Master equations with detailed balance often arise from a monomolecular chemical kinetics simulation of reaction path networks.
Within this context, the states represent energetically stable atomic configurations in the potential energy surface, called
equilibrium states
(EQs).
If the system is described by a canonical ensemble, the rate constant
K_{ij}
from state
j
to state
i
with
i\neq j
is given by

\displaystyle K_{ij}=\Gamma\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{h}\exp\mathopen{\left\lparen\vphantom{{-\frac{E_{\text{$i$--$j$}}-E_{j}}{RT}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}-\frac{E_{\text{$i$--$j$}}-E_{j}}{RT}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{-\frac{E_{\text{$i$--$j$}}-E_{j}}{RT}}}\right\rparen},

(4)

where
k_{\mathrm{B}}
is the Boltzmann constant,
h
is the Planck constant,
R
is the gas constant,
T
is the temperature,
\Gamma
is the transmission coefficient, and
E_{j}
and
E_{\text{$i$--$j$}}=E_{\text{$j$--$i$}}
are the potential energies of equilibrium state
j
and the transition state between equilibrium states
i
and
j
, respectively
[
20
]
.
The rate constant matrix
K
determined by (
4
) satisfies the detailed balance condition (
3
) with the stationary distribution
\pi
given by

\displaystyle\pi_{i}=\frac{\exp\mathopen{\big\lparen}-\frac{E_{i}}{RT}\mathclose{\big\rparen}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\mathopen{\big\lparen}-\frac{E_{j}}{RT}\mathclose{\big\rparen}},

(5)

which is called the
Boltzmann distribution
.
Rate constant matrices derived from the microcanonical ensemble also satisfy the detailed balance condition; see
[
21
]
.

Chemical kinetics simulation often yields stiff master equations due to a wide range of time scales of reactions.
While the analytic solution of (
2
) can be readily expressed as
x(t)=\mathrm{e}^{tK}p
for any rate constant matrix
K
and initial distribution
p
, solving stiff ODEs numerically has been recognized as a difficult task.
A classical remedy for the stiffness issue in kinetics simulation is to apply
quasi-steady-state approximation
(QSSA), introduced in
[
3
]
.
Given a subset
S\subseteq[n]\coloneqq\mathopen{\left\{\vphantom{{1,\dotsc,n}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}1,\dotsc,n\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{1,\dotsc,n}}\right\}}
of states that are regarded to be “steady,” i.e.,
\dot{x}_{i}(t)\approx 0
for
i\in S
, the QSSA replaces the left-hand side
\dot{x}_{i}(t)
of (
2
) with
0
for every
i\in S
.
In other words, the QSSA constrains the solution to a subspace determined from
S
, which we call the
QSSA subspace
.
The approximate equation is solved as a differential-algebraic equation (DAE), or one can eliminate the algebraic variables
x_{i}\;(i\in S)
to get a new master equation of smaller size.
While QSSA is conceptually clear and easy to compute, the conservation law is no longer valid in the approximate DAE.

In recent years, large-scale, highly stiff master equations have emerged with the development of automatic chemical reaction pathway search techniques such as the anharmonic downward distortion following (ADDF) method
[
18
]
and the single-component artificial force induced reaction (SC-AFIR) method
[
17
]
.
To address such intractable equations, Sumiya et al.
[
20
,
21
]
presented the
rate constant matrix contraction
(RCMC) method for systems described by the canonical or microcanonical ensemble.
Based on QSSA, the RCMC method maintains a set
S\subseteq[n]
of states regarded to be steady, which monotonically grows from the empty set.
For each
S
, the method computes a vector
q={(q_{i})}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
that approximates the solution
x(t)
at time
t
when the states in
S
and
T\coloneqq[n]\setminus S
can be regarded to be steady and transient, respectively.
The output
q
preserves the component sum, i.e.,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}
holds, and the method runs fast for large-scale, stiff equations because it does not rely on numerical integration, unlike ordinary numerical methods.
The RCMC method has been successfully applied to the kinetics-based navigation in the SC-AFIR method
[
19
]
.
The RCMC method was, however, designed based on chemical intuition, and its mathematical validity and properties have not been discussed thoroughly.

Contributions.

This paper improves the RCMC method by establishing mathematical foundations, summarized as follows.

First, we reformulate the RCMC method in terms of matrix computations.
The reformulated method applies to any master equation (
2
) whose rate constant matrix
K
satisfies the detailed balance condition (
3
).
We describe the method by means of matrix operations, while all calculation formulas in the original form
[
20
,
21
]
are written with direct reference to the matrix entries.
In our reformulation, the output
q
of the method is expressed as a matrix-vector multiplication
Vp
, where
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a given initial value and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a matrix approximating
\mathrm{e}^{tK}
that can be explicitly constructed from the rate constant matrix
K
and the set
S
of steady states.
We observe that the matrix
V
can be determined from natural requirements coming from properties of
\mathrm{e}^{tK}
and the QSSA.
Furthermore, we propose a variant of
V
, which is another natural approximation of
\mathrm{e}^{tK}
.
The methods with original and modified
V
are referred to as Types A and B, respectively.
While the matrix
V
of Type A is easier to compute, the matrix
V
of Type B is mathematically more tractable as it is indeed the orthogonal projection onto the QSSA subspace.

Second, we theoretically analyze the error between the approximate solution
q=Vp
and the exact solution
x(t)=\mathrm{e}^{tK}p
.
Here, the error is measured in terms of the
\pi
-norm
; see
Section

2.1
for definition.
The derived error bounds depend on the spectral radii of matrices obtained from
K
and
S
, as well as the coefficients in the expansion of
p
with respect to an eigenbasis of
K
.
Roughly speaking,
q
becomes a good approximation to
x(t)
if
p
consists of eigenvectors of
K
with eigenvalues far from the spectral radii.
We give a better and simpler bound for Type B than for Type A.
Our error estimation for Type B leads to a principled way to determine the optimal reference time
t^{*}
such that
q
should be regarded as an approximation of
x(t^{*})
.

Third, based on our reformulation and subsequent error analysis, we discuss implementation details of the RCMC method.
We provide efficient implementations for calculating the optimal reference time
t^{*}
and for the incremental updates of the LU decomposition within the algorithm.
We also address concerns regarding numerical stability.

Finally, we conduct numerical experiments using one synthetic dataset and two actual chemical reaction networks to demonstrate the efficiency, numerical stability, and accuracy of the RCMC method.
We observe that the RCMC method of both Types A and B using double precision is capable of producing approximate solutions within mere seconds.
The experimental results substantiate that the actual errors are within our theoretical bounds, subject to issues arising from the machine epsilon.
Furthermore, we compare three methods for calculating the optimal reference time
t^{*}
and analyze the errors using
\pi
- and
L_{\infty}
-norms.

Previous and Related Work.

The RCMC method was originally introduced in
[
20
]
as a method to reduce the master equation (
2
) with the rate constant matrix derived from the canonical ensemble (
4
) into a smaller system by combining repeated applications of QSSA and an operation similar to
lumping

[
11
,
24
]
, which is a classical reduction method for (generally nonlinear) master equations by grouping several states into a single state.
The RCMC method in the aforementioned form of computing approximate solutions was subsequently proposed in
[
21
]
, inheriting the spirit of lumping in its design.

QSSA is classified as a reduction method for generally nonlinear master equations based on timescale analysis.
Other representative examples of methods based on timescale analysis include
intrinsic low-dimensional manifold
(ILDM) method
[
14
,
15
]
and
computational singular perturbation
(CSP)
[
13
]
.
Both methods rely on the eigendecomposition of the rate constant matrix (or the Jacobian matrix for nonlinear systems), which is prohibitive for large-scale, highly stiff systems we are concerned with.
In contrast, the RCMC method can be executed without performing the eigendecomposition, while our error analysis depends on it.
See
[
22
, Chapter 7]
for a comprehensive survey of reduction techniques for kinetics equations.

2.1
Rate Constant Matrices

Let
K\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be an
n\times n
matrix satisfying the following conditions:

(RCM1)

K_{ij}\geq 0
for
i\neq j
,

(RCM2)

\mathbf{1}^{\top}_{n}K=\mathbf{0}^{\top}
,

(RCM3)

the detailed balance condition (
3
) holds for some
\pi\in\Delta_{n}^{\circ}
.

We call such
K
and
\pi
a
rate constant matrix
and a
stationary distribution
, respectively (see
[
24
]
).
A matrix satisfying
(RCM1)
is called a
Metzler matrix

[
8
]
or an
essentially non-negative matrix

[
23
]
.
Setting

\displaystyle\Pi\coloneqq\diag(\pi),

(6)

we can simply write
(RCM3)
as
K\Pi=\Pi K^{\top}
, i.e.,
K
is a self-adjoint matrix with respect to the
\pi
-inner product
in the following sense.

For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define the
\pi
-inner product
by

\displaystyle{\mathopen{\left\langle\vphantom{{a,b}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}a,b\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{a,b}}\right\rangle}}_{\pi}\coloneqq a^{\top}\Pi^{-1}b.

(7)

The
\pi
-inner product induces the
\pi
-norm

{\mathopen{\left\|\vphantom{{a}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}a\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{a}}\right\|}}_{\pi}\coloneqq\sqrt{{\mathopen{\left\langle\vphantom{{a,a}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}a,a\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{a,a}}\right\rangle}}_{\pi}}
.
Unless otherwise stated, we regard
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as a metric vector space equipped with the
\pi
-inner product.
Similar arguments to the standard inner product hold for basic notions such as adjoint matrices and positive definiteness in this space.
Specifically, the
adjoint matrix
of
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the matrix
A^{*}
such that
{\mathopen{\left\langle\vphantom{{a,Ab}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}a,Ab\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{a,Ab}}\right\rangle}}_{\pi}={\mathopen{\left\langle\vphantom{{A^{*}a,b}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}A^{*}a,b\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{A^{*}a,b}}\right\rangle}}_{\pi}
for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and is explicitly written as
A^{*}=\Pi A^{\top}\Pi^{-1}
.
A matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is
self-adjoint
if
A=A^{*}
, i.e.,
A\Pi=\Pi A^{\top}
.
Thus,
(RCM3)
is nothing but a statement that
K
is self-adjoint.
See
Sections

A.1
and
A.2
for details of adjoint matrices and positive semi-definiteness on the
\pi
-inner product space.

Taking the transpose on both sides of
(RCM2)
, we get
\mathbf{0}=K^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{n}=\Pi^{-1}K\Pi\mathbf{1}_{n}=\Pi^{-1}K\pi
and thereby
K\pi=\mathbf{0}
.
Conversely, a self-adjoint matrix
K\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
satisfying
K\pi=\mathbf{0}
meets
(RCM2)
.
Thus,
\mathbf{1}^{\top}_{n}K=\mathbf{0}^{\top}
and
K\pi=\mathbf{0}
are equivalent under
(RCM3)
.

The eigenvalues of a self-adjoint matrix are real.
A self-adjoint matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is called
positive semi-definite
if
{\mathopen{\left\langle\vphantom{{a,Aa}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}a,Aa\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{a,Aa}}\right\rangle}}_{\pi}
is non-negative for
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, or equivalently, all eigenvalues of
A
are non-negative.
It follows from the Gershgorin circle theorem that a rate constant matrix
K
has non-positive eigenvalues, i.e.,
K
is
negative semi-definite
.
Let
0=\lambda_{1}\geq\dotsb\geq\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
K
and
u_{k}
an eigenvector corresponding to
\lambda_{k}
for
k\in[n]
.
Since
K
is self-adjoint, we can take
u_{1},\dotsc,u_{n}
such that
{\mathopen{\left\langle\vphantom{{u_{k},u_{\ell}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}u_{k},u_{\ell}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{u_{k},u_{\ell}}}\right\rangle}}_{\pi}
is
1
if
k=\ell
and
0
otherwise.
We can set
u_{1}=\pi
by
K\pi=\mathbf{0}
.
Note that
\pi
satisfies
{\mathopen{\left\|\vphantom{{\pi}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}\pi\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{\pi}}\right\|}}_{\pi}^{2}=\pi^{\top}\Pi^{-1}\pi=\mathbf{1}^{\top}_{n}\pi=1

See
Section

A.2
for details on the semi-definiteness of self-adjoint matrices.

The minus of
K
is a
Z-matrix
, i.e., every off-diagonal entry is non-negative.
In addition,
-K
has non-negative eigenvalues.
Such a matrix is called an

[
2
, Chapter 6]
.
Principal submatrices of an M-matrix are M-matrices again, and the inverse of a nonsingular M-matrix is a non-negative matrix.
Therefore,

holds for

The matrix

is a (weighted)

Conversely, given a graph Laplacian matrix

and

by

.

2.2
Master Equations with Detailed Balance

Consider the master equation (
2
) associated with a rate constant matrix
K
with stationary distribution
\pi
.
Letting
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an initial value
x(0)
, the solution
x(t)
of (
2
) can be analytically represented by
x(t)=\mathrm{e}^{tK}p
, where
\mathrm{e}^{tK}\coloneqq\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{d!}{(tK)}^{d}
.
The matrix exponential
\mathrm{e}^{tK}
enjoys the following properties:

(E1)

\mathrm{e}^{tK}\geq O
for
t\geq 0
,

(E2)

\mathbf{1}^{\top}_{n}\mathrm{e}^{tK}=\mathbf{1}^{\top}_{n}
,

(E3)

\mathrm{e}^{tK}
is self-adjoint.

We can directly derive
(E2)
and
(E3)
from
(RCM2)
and
(RCM3)
, respectively.
Property
(E1)
follows from the general fact that the matrix exponential of a Metzler matrix is non-negative
[
23
, Section 8.2]
.
Note that
(E2)
is equivalent to
\mathrm{e}^{tK}\pi=\pi
under
(E3)
, which can be shown in the same way as the equivalence of
(RCM2)
and
K\pi=\mathbf{0}
under
(RCM3)
.
These properties on
\mathrm{e}^{tK}
give rise to the following well-known properties on the solution
x(t)
of (
2
).
We here describe a short proof to clarify from which property of
\mathrm{e}^{tK}
each claim is derived.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
x(t)
be the solution of (
2
) with
x(0)=p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
If
p\in\Delta_{n}
, then
x(t)\in\Delta_{n}
for all
t\geq 0
.
If
p=\pi
, then
x(t)=\pi
for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

By
(E1)
, we have
x(t)=\mathrm{e}^{tK}p\geq\mathbf{0}
for
p\geq\mathbf{0}
and
t\geq 0
.
By
(E2)
, it holds that
\mathbf{1}^{\top}_{n}x(t)=\mathbf{1}^{\top}_{n}\mathrm{e}^{tK}p=\mathbf{1}^{\top}_{n}p=1
if
\mathbf{1}^{\top}_{n}p=1
.
By
(E2)
and
(E3)
, we have
\mathrm{e}^{tK}\pi=\pi
, which implies
x(t)=\mathrm{e}^{tK}\pi=\pi
for
p=\pi
.
∎

Another important property of (
2
) is the
linearity
of solutions.
That is, if
x(t)
and
y(t)
are the solutions of (
2
) with initial values
x(0)=p
and
y(0)=q
, respectively, then
ax(t)+by(t)
is the solution of (
2
) with initial value
ap+bq
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.
We also remark that the eigenvalues of
\mathrm{e}^{tK}
with
t\geq 0
are in
[0,1]
because
K
is negative semi-definite.

2.3
Quasi-Steady-State Approximation

QSSA, originated in the work of Bodenstein
[
3
]
, is a classical tool for reducing the number of variables and stiffness in a (generally nonlinear) master equation.
We here describe the method specialized to our linear setting (
2
).

Suppose that we have a bipartition
\mathopen{\left\{\vphantom{{S,T}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}S,T\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{S,T}}\right\}}
of
[n]
separating the components of
x(t)
into
fast variables

x_{S}(t)
and
slow variables

x_{T}(t)
.
With this bipartition, the master equation (
2
) can be displayed as

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{x}_{S}(t)&=K_{SS}x_{S}(t)+K_{ST}x_{T}(t),\\
\dot{x}_{T}(t)&=K_{TS}x_{S}(t)+K_{TT}x_{T}(t).\end{aligned}\right.

(8)

Now,
x_{S}(t)
is approximately regarded as “steady state,” i.e.,
\dot{x}_{S}(t)\approx\mathbf{0}
.
The QSSA replaces the left-hand side
\dot{x}_{S}(t)
of the first equation in (
8
) with
\mathbf{0}
.
Namely, letting
\bar{x}(t)
be a new variable vector, we can formulate the QSSA of (
8
) as

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbf{0}&=K_{SS}\bar{x}_{S}(t)+K_{ST}\bar{x}_{T}(t),\\
\dot{\bar{x}}_{T}(t)&=K_{TS}\bar{x}_{S}(t)+K_{TT}\bar{x}_{T}(t).\end{aligned}\right.

(9)

The system (
9
) consists of purely algebraic equations and differential equations.
Such a system of equations is called a
differential-algebraic equation
(DAE).
The algebraic equations constrain the solution to the
QSSA subspace

\displaystyle W\coloneqq\mathopen{\big\{}{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathrel{\big|}K_{SS}y_{S}+K_{ST}y_{T}=\mathbf{0}\mathclose{\big\}}.

(10)

If
K_{SS}
is nonsingular,
\bar{x}_{S}(t)
can be determined from
\bar{x}_{T}(t)
by

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{S}(t)=-K_{SS}^{-1}K_{ST}\bar{x}_{T}(t).

(11)

Substituting (
11
) back into the differential equation in (
9
), we obtain a differential equation involving only
\bar{x}_{T}(t)
:

\displaystyle\dot{\bar{x}}_{T}(t)=\mathopen{\big\lparen}K_{TT}-K_{TS}K_{SS}^{-1}K_{ST}\mathclose{\big\rparen}\bar{x}_{T}(t).

(12)

The coefficient matrix
D\coloneqq K_{TT}-K_{TS}K_{SS}^{-1}K_{ST}
in (
12
) is called the
Schur complement
of
K_{SS}
in
K
.
The Schur complement
D
is again a rate constant matrix in the sense that it satisfies
(RCM1)
–
(RCM3)
with stationary distribution proportional to
\pi_{T}
.
Hence, QSSA can be applied repeatedly.
From the property of the Schur complement, the equation obtained by applying the QSSA twice with
S=I
and
J
coincides with the single application of the QSSA with
S=I\cup J
for any disjoint subsets
I,J\subseteq[n]
.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we have explored mathematical aspects of the RCMC method.
We have reformulated the RCMC method in terms of matrix computation and conducted theoretical error analysis.
Building on this foundation, we have also discussed its efficient and numerically stable implementations.
Numerical experiments with both synthetic and real-world datasets have demonstrated computational efficiency and numerical stability, and validated the derived error bounds.
Potential future work includes extending the RCMC method to nonlinear master equations, such as those involving bimolecular reactions.

Vector Spaces with Diagonal Metric

This section summarizes basic results on the real vector space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with the
\pi
-inner product (
7
), where
\pi={(\pi_{i})}_{i\in[n]}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
.
We let
\Pi\coloneqq\diag(\pi)
.

A.1
Adjointness

The
adjoint matrix
of
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the unique matrix
A^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that
{\mathopen{\left\langle\vphantom{{a,Ab}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}a,Ab\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{a,Ab}}\right\rangle}}_{\pi}={\mathopen{\left\langle\vphantom{{A^{*}a,b}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}A^{*}a,b\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{A^{*}a,b}}\right\rangle}}_{\pi}
holds for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The adjoint matrix
A^{*}
is explicitly written as
A^{*}=\Pi A^{\top}\Pi^{-1}
by

\displaystyle{\mathopen{\left\langle\vphantom{{a,Ab}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}a,Ab\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{a,Ab}}\right\rangle}}_{\pi}=a^{\top}\Pi^{-1}Ab=\mathopen{\big\lparen}\Pi A^{\top}\Pi^{-1}a\mathclose{\big\rparen}^{\top}\Pi^{-1}b={\mathopen{\big\langle}\Pi A^{\top}\Pi^{-1}a,b\mathclose{\big\rangle}}_{\pi}.

(111)

It holds that
{(A_{1}A_{2})}^{*}=A_{2}^{*}A_{1}^{*}
for
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\mathopen{\left\lparen\vphantom{{A^{-1}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}A^{-1}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{A^{-1}}}\right\rparen}^{*}=\mathopen{\left\lparen\vphantom{{A^{*}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}A^{*}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{A^{*}}}\right\rparen}^{-1}
for nonsingular
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

A matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is called
self-adjoint
if
A^{*}=A
, i.e.,
A\Pi=\Pi A^{\top}
.
If
A
is self-adjoint,
B\coloneqq\Pi^{-\frac{1}{2}}A\Pi^{\frac{1}{2}}
is a symmetric matrix.
Here, for a non-negative diagonal matrix
D=\diag\mathopen{\left\lparen\vphantom{{d_{1},\dotsc,d_{n}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}d_{1},\dotsc,d_{n}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{d_{1},\dotsc,d_{n}}}\right\rparen}\geq O
, we let
D^{\frac{1}{2}}\coloneqq\diag\mathopen{\left\lparen\vphantom{{\sqrt{d_{1}},\dotsc,\sqrt{d_{n}}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}\sqrt{d_{1}},\dotsc,\sqrt{d_{n}}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{\sqrt{d_{1}},\dotsc,\sqrt{d_{n}}}}\right\rparen}
.
Let
B=V\Lambda V^{\top}
be an eigendecomposition of
B
, where
V
is an orthogonal matrix (i.e.
VV^{\top}=V^{\top}V=I_{n}
) and
\Lambda=\diag(\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{n})
is a diagonal matrix.
Letting
U\coloneqq\Pi^{\frac{1}{2}}V
, we have
A=\Pi^{\frac{1}{2}}V\Lambda V^{\top}\Pi^{-\frac{1}{2}}=U\Lambda U^{-1}
.
Thus,
A
and
B
have the same eigenvalues.
In particular, eigenvalues of self-adjoint matrices are real.
See also
[
6
, Chapter IV]
.

Let
u_{1},\dotsc u_{n}
be the column vectors of
U
, i.e.,
u_{k}
is an eigenvector of
A
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
.
The matrix
U
satisfies
U^{\top}\Pi^{-1}U=V^{\top}V=I_{n}
, which means that
{\mathopen{\left\langle\vphantom{{u_{k},u_{\ell}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}u_{k},u_{\ell}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{u_{k},u_{\ell}}}\right\rangle}}_{\pi}
is
1
if
k=\ell
and
0
otherwise for
k\in[n]
.
Namely, adjoint matrices have orthonormal eigenbases.
If we expand
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to the eigenbasis as
a=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}u_{k}
with
c_{1},\dotsc,c_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, for any
\ell\in[n]
, it holds that

\displaystyle{\mathopen{\left\langle\vphantom{{u_{\ell},a}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}u_{\ell},a\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{u_{\ell},a}}\right\rangle}}_{\pi}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}{\mathopen{\left\langle\vphantom{{u_{\ell},u_{k}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}u_{\ell},u_{k}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{u_{\ell},u_{k}}}\right\rangle}}_{\pi}=c_{\ell},

(112)

and hence we have
a=\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\mathopen{\left\langle\vphantom{{u_{k},a}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}u_{k},a\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{u_{k},a}}\right\rangle}}_{\pi}u_{k}
.

A.2
Positive Semi-definiteness

A self-adjoint matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is called
positive semi-definite
if
{\mathopen{\left\langle\vphantom{{a,Aa}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}a,Aa\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{a,Aa}}\right\rangle}}_{\pi}\geq 0
for
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

if
{\mathopen{\left\langle\vphantom{{a,Aa}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}a,Aa\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{a,Aa}}\right\rangle}}_{\pi}>0
for
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathopen{\left\{\vphantom{{\mathbf{0}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}\mathbf{0}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{\mathbf{0}}}\right\}}
.
Similarly,
A
is called
negative semi-definite
if
-A
is positive semi-definite and
negative definite
if
-A
is positive definite.
The definiteness are equivalent to those of
B\coloneqq\Pi^{-\frac{1}{2}}A\Pi^{\frac{1}{2}}
by

\displaystyle{\mathopen{\left\langle\vphantom{{a,Aa}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}a,Aa\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{a,Aa}}\right\rangle}}_{\pi}=a^{\top}\Pi^{-1}Aa=a^{\top}\Pi^{-\frac{1}{2}}B\Pi^{-\frac{1}{2}}a=b^{\top}Bb,

(113)

where
b=\Pi^{-\frac{1}{2}}a
.
Since
A
and
B
have the same eigenvalues,
A
is positive (resp. negative) semi-definite if and only if its eigenvalues are non-negative (resp. non-positive) and is positive (resp. negative) definite if and only if its eigenvalues are positive (resp. negative).

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a positive semi-definite matrix with eigendecomposition
U\Lambda U^{-1}
.
The
square root
of
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is defined as
A^{\frac{1}{2}}\coloneqq U\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}U^{-1}
, which is the unique positive semi-definite matrix whose square is
A
.
If
A
is positive definite, so is
A^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

For self-adjoint
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, let
A\preceq B
mean that
B-A
is positive semi-definite.
The binary relation
\preceq
forms a partial order on the self-adjoint matrices compatible with addition, called the
Löwner order
.
As with the usual case of symmetric matrices, the Löwner order satisfies the following properties.

•

For nonsingular self-adjoint matrices
A,B
, if
A\preceq B
, then
B^{-1}\preceq A^{-1}
.

•

A\preceq B
implies
P^{*}AP\preceq P^{*}BP
for any
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

A.3

Since the metric
\Pi^{-1}
is dinagonal, we can consider the “subspace” of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
indexed by

.
Let
\mathbb{R}^{I}
be the vector space having coordinates indexed by
I\subseteq[n]
.
We call
\mathbb{R}^{I}
a

of
\mathbb{R}^{n}

The space

f(t)
due to (
155
).
Differentiating (
155
) by
t
, we get

\displaystyle\dot{f}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{\left\lparen\vphantom{{\frac{a}{{\lambda^{*}(t)}^{2}}-\frac{1}{b}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}\frac{a}{{\lambda^{*}(t)}^{2}}-\frac{1}{b}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{\frac{a}{{\lambda^{*}(t)}^{2}}-\frac{1}{b}}}\right\rparen}\dot{\lambda}^{*}(t).

(156)

Similarly, differentiating (
154
) by
t
, we obtain

\displaystyle(\lambda^{*}(t)+t\dot{\lambda}^{*}(t))\mathrm{e}^{t\lambda^{*}(t)}=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{\left\lparen\vphantom{{-\frac{a\dot{\lambda}^{*}(t)}{{\lambda^{*}(t)}^{2}}-\frac{\dot{\lambda}^{*}(t)}{b}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}-\frac{a\dot{\lambda}^{*}(t)}{{\lambda^{*}(t)}^{2}}-\frac{\dot{\lambda}^{*}(t)}{b}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{-\frac{a\dot{\lambda}^{*}(t)}{{\lambda^{*}(t)}^{2}}-\frac{\dot{\lambda}^{*}(t)}{b}}}\right\rparen}

(157)

and

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}^{*}(t)=-\frac{{\lambda^{*}(t)}^{2}}{t\lambda^{*}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{\left\lparen\vphantom{{\frac{a}{\lambda^{*}(t)}+\frac{\lambda^{*}(t)}{b}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}\frac{a}{\lambda^{*}(t)}+\frac{\lambda^{*}(t)}{b}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{\frac{a}{\lambda^{*}(t)}+\frac{\lambda^{*}(t)}{b}}}\right\rparen}\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda^{*}(t)}}.

(158)

Let
t^{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a stationary point of
f(t)
, i.e.,
\dot{f}(t^{*})=0
.
By (
157
),
\dot{f}(t^{*})=0
is equivalent to
\dot{\lambda}^{*}(t^{*})=0
or
\frac{a}{{\lambda^{*}(t^{*})}^{2}}-\frac{1}{b}=0
.
If
\dot{\lambda}^{*}(t^{*})=0
, it holds that
\lambda^{*}(t^{*})=0
from (
158
), which contradicts
\lambda^{*}(t^{*})<0
.
Thus,
\frac{a}{{\lambda^{*}(t^{*})}^{2}}-\frac{1}{b}=0
must hold, i.e.,
\lambda^{*}(t^{*})=-\sqrt{ab}
.
Substituting this into (
154
), we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-t^{*}\sqrt{ab}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{\left\lparen\vphantom{{-\frac{a}{\sqrt{ab}}+\frac{\sqrt{ab}}{b}+1}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}-\frac{a}{\sqrt{ab}}+\frac{\sqrt{ab}}{b}+1\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{-\frac{a}{\sqrt{ab}}+\frac{\sqrt{ab}}{b}+1}}\right\rparen}=\frac{1}{2}

(159)

and thus

\displaystyle t^{*}=\frac{\ln 2}{\sqrt{ab}}.

(160)

Finally, by direct calculation, we can check that

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}^{*}(t^{*})

\displaystyle=\frac{ab}{2\sqrt{\frac{a}{b}}+\ln 2}>0,

(161)

\displaystyle\ddot{f}(t^{*})

\displaystyle=-\frac{a\dot{\lambda}^{*}(t^{*})}{{\lambda^{*}(t^{*})}^{3}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{\left\lparen\vphantom{{\frac{a}{{\lambda^{*}(t^{*})}^{2}}-\frac{1}{b}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}\frac{a}{{\lambda^{*}(t^{*})}^{2}}-\frac{1}{b}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{\frac{a}{{\lambda^{*}(t^{*})}^{2}}-\frac{1}{b}}}\right\rparen}\ddot{\lambda}^{*}(t^{*})=-\frac{a\dot{\lambda}^{*}(t^{*})}{{\lambda^{*}(t^{*})}^{3}}>0.

(162)

Thus,
t^{*}
is a local minimum of
f(t)
, and is indeed the global minimum since it is the unique stationary point of
f(t)
with
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

Appendix E
Steady-states Selection and MAP Inference for DPPs

The max-diagonal strategy for steady-states selection in
Algorithm

1
is closely related to the
maximum a posteriori
(MAP)
inference for determinantal point processes
(DPPs)
[
16
]
as follows.

If
S^{(k)}
could be chosen independently from
S^{(k-1)}
, the steady-states selection boiled down to the task of finding a bipartition
\mathopen{\left\{\vphantom{{S,T}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}S,T\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{S,T}}\right\}}
of
[n]
with
|S|=k
such that the error of the RCMC method becomes smaller.
Roughly speaking, the results in
Section

4
claim that the accuracy of the RCMC method improves when
\sigma(K_{SS})
is large and
\rho(D)
with
D=K_{TT}-K_{TS}K_{SS}^{-1}K_{ST}
is small.
Namely, it is desirable to choose
S\subseteq[n]
with
|S|=k
such that all the eigenvalues of
K_{SS}
and
D
are large and small, respectively, in terms of the absolute value.

Since
\det K_{SS}\det D=\det K
, using
\mathopen{\left|\vphantom{{\det K_{SS}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}\det K_{SS}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{\det K_{SS}}}\right|}
in place of
\sigma(K_{SS})
and
\frac{1}{\rho(D)}
, one can model this task as a problem of finding
S\subseteq[n]
with
|S|=k
such that
\mathopen{\left|\vphantom{{\det K_{SS}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}\det K_{SS}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{\det K_{SS}}}\right|}
is maximized, or equivalently,
f(S)\coloneqq\ln{\mathopen{\left|\vphantom{{\det K_{SS}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}\det K_{SS}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{\det K_{SS}}}\right|}}
is the largest.
This problem is known as the
maximum a posteriori
(MAP)
inference for determinantal point processes
(DPPs) if
K
is symmetric positive (negative) semi-definite
[
5
,
16
]
.
Since
K
is written as
-L\Pi^{-1}
with a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix
L
, it holds that

\displaystyle f(S)=\ln\det L_{SS}-\sum_{i\in S}\ln\pi_{i}.

(163)

The function
g(S)\coloneqq\ln\det L_{SS}
is known to be
submodular
, i.e.,
g(i\mathbin{|}X)\geq g(i\mathbin{|}Y)
holds for all
Y\subseteq[n]
,
X\subseteq Y
, and
i\in[n]\setminus Y
, where
g(i\mathbin{|}S)\coloneqq g(S\cup\mathopen{\left\{\vphantom{{i}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}i\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{i}}\right\}})-g(S)
for
S\subseteq[n]
and
i\in[n]
.
By (
163
),
f(S)
is also submodular, and thus the problem of maximizing
f(S)
is a special case of submodular function maximization under a cardinality constraint.

A standard approach to submodular function maximization with a cardinality constraint is the
greedy algorithm
: starting from
S^{(0)}=\emptyset
, one chooses
j^{(k)}\in\argmax\mathopen{\big\{}{f\mathopen{\big\lparen}j\mathbin{|}S^{(k-1)}\mathclose{\big\rparen}}\mathrel{\big|}j\in[n]\setminus S\mathclose{\big\}}
and let
S^{(k)}\coloneqq S^{(k-1)}\cup\mathopen{\big\{}j^{(k)}\mathclose{\big\}}
for
k=1,2,\dotsc
.
Indeed, as shown in
[
5
]
,
f\mathopen{\big\lparen}j\mathbin{|}S^{(k-1)}\mathclose{\big\rparen}
coincides with the logarithm of
\mathopen{\big|}D_{jj}^{(k-1)}\mathclose{\big|}
by

\displaystyle f\mathopen{\big\lparen}j\mathbin{|}S^{(k-1)}\mathclose{\big\rparen}

\displaystyle=\ln{\mathopen{\big|}\det K_{S^{(k-1)}\cup\mathopen{\left\{\vphantom{{j}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}j\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{j}}\right\}},S^{(k-1)}\cup\mathopen{\left\{\vphantom{{j}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}j\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{j}}\right\}}}\mathclose{\big|}}-\ln{\mathopen{\left|\vphantom{{\det K_{S^{(k-1)}S^{(k-1)}}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}\det K_{S^{(k-1)}S^{(k-1)}}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{\det K_{S^{(k-1)}S^{(k-1)}}}}\right|}}

(164)

\displaystyle=\ln{\mathopen{\left|\vphantom{{\frac{\det K_{S^{(k-1)}\cup\mathopen{\left\{\vphantom{{j}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}j\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{j}}\right\}},S^{(k-1)}\cup\mathopen{\left\{\vphantom{{j}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}j\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{j}}\right\}}}}{\det K_{S^{(k-1)}S^{(k-1)}}}}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}\frac{\det K_{S^{(k-1)}\cup\mathopen{\left\{\vphantom{{j}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}j\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{j}}\right\}},S^{(k-1)}\cup\mathopen{\left\{\vphantom{{j}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}j\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{j}}\right\}}}}{\det K_{S^{(k-1)}S^{(k-1)}}}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{\frac{\det K_{S^{(k-1)}\cup\mathopen{\left\{\vphantom{{j}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}j\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{j}}\right\}},S^{(k-1)}\cup\mathopen{\left\{\vphantom{{j}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}j\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{j}}\right\}}}}{\det K_{S^{(k-1)}S^{(k-1)}}}}}\right|}}

(165)

\displaystyle=\ln{\mathopen{\big|}D_{jj}^{(k-1)}\mathclose{\big|}},

(166)

meaning that
Algorithm

1
is nothing but an implementation of the greedy algorithm for maximizing
f(S)
.
Therefore, the max-diagonal strategy is natural from the viewpoint of error analysis as well.

Appendix F
Incremental Update of LU Decomposition for
M

Let
K\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a rate constant matrix and
S=S^{(k)}
and
T=T^{(k)}
those defined in
Algorithm

1
.
In the RCMC method of Type B, we solve a linear system with the coefficient matrix

\displaystyle M^{(k)}\coloneqq{}

\displaystyle\Omega^{(k)}{\Omega^{(k)}}^{*}=I_{T}+K_{TS}K_{SS}^{-2}K_{ST},

(167)

where

\displaystyle\Omega^{(k)}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}-K_{TS}K_{SS}^{-1}&I_{T}\end{pmatrix}.

(168)

In the discussions below, we show that one can update a Cholesky factor of
M^{(k-1)}
into that of
M^{(k)}
by performing the
rank-one update
of a Cholesky decomposition of a matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
.
Here, the rank-one update of a Cholesky decomposition of a positive definite matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
means a task to compute a Cholesky decomposition of
A+vv^{\top}
for given vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
using a Cholesky decomposition of
A
.
Efficient algorithms that run in
\mathrm{O}\mathopen{\big\lparen}|T|^{2}\mathclose{\big\rparen}=\mathrm{O}(n^{2})
time are available for this problem
[
9
]
.

Let
G^{(k)}
be a Cholesky factor of
M^{(k)}
with rows and columns indexed by
T
and
[|T|]=[n-k]
, respectively.
As with the matrix
C^{*}
in
Section

5.1
, the adjoint matrix
{G^{(k)}}^{*}
means
{G^{(k)}}^{\top}\Pi_{T}^{-1}
.
By technical reasons, we assume that the rows of
G^{(k)}
are ordered as
j^{(k+1)},j^{(k+2)},\dotsc
, i.e., one needs to compute the entire sequence of
j^{(1)},j^{(2)},\dotsc
before calculating the output vectors
p^{(1)},p^{(2)},\dotsc
, which is possible since
j^{(k)}
can be determined without referencing
M^{(k)}
.

Initially, we can take
G^{(0)}=\Pi^{\frac{1}{2}}
by
M^{(0)}=I_{n}
.
Consider the update at the
k
th iteration.
Let
E=E^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{T^{(k)}\times T^{(k-1)}}
be the matrix defined by (
B.2
).
By (
B.2
),
M^{(k)}
can be written as

\displaystyle M^{(k)}=\Omega^{(k)}{\Omega^{(k)}}^{*}=E\Omega^{(k-1)}{\Omega^{(k-1)}}^{*}{E}^{*}=EM^{(k-1)}E^{*}=EG^{(k-1)}{G^{(k-1)}}^{*}E^{*}.

(169)

Letting
T=T^{(k)}
,
j=j^{(k)}
, and
J\coloneqq\mathopen{\left\{\vphantom{{2,\dotsc,n-k}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}2,\dotsc,n-k\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{{2,\dotsc,n-k}}\right\}}
, we have

\displaystyle EG^{(k-1)}=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{D^{(k-1)}_{Tj}}{D^{(k-1)}_{jj}}&I_{T}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}G_{j1}^{(k-1)}&\mathbf{0}^{\top}\\
G_{T1}^{(k-1)}&G_{TJ}^{(k-1)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}v&G_{TJ}^{(k-1)}\end{pmatrix},

(170)

where

\displaystyle v\coloneqq G_{T1}^{(k-1)}-\frac{G_{j1}^{(k-1)}}{D^{(k-1)}_{jj}}D^{(k-1)}_{Tj}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}.

(171)

By (
169
) and (
170
), it holds that

\displaystyle M^{(k)}=\begin{pmatrix}v&G_{TJ}^{(k-1)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v^{*}\\
{G_{TJ}^{(k-1)}}^{*}\end{pmatrix}=G_{TJ}^{(k-1)}{G_{TJ}^{(k-1)}}^{*}+vv^{*}

(172)

with
v^{*}\coloneqq v^{\top}\Pi_{T}^{-1}
.
The equation (
172
) means that
G^{(k)}
is obtained as the rank-one update of
G_{TJ}^{(k-1)}{G_{TJ}^{(k-1)}}^{*}
for
v
.
Analyzing numerical stability on the rank-one update is left for further investigation.
