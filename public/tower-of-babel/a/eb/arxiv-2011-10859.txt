Representing an integer as the sum of a prime and the product of two small factors
ROGER BAKER, GLYN HARMAN

Abstract
. Let ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 . We show that every large integer n n may be written in the form n = p + a ​ b , n=p+ab\,, where a , b ≤ n 1 2 − δ a,b\leq n^{\frac{1}{2}-\delta} for a positive absolute constant δ \delta , and a ​ b ≤ n 0.55 + ϵ ab\leq n^{0.55+\epsilon} . This sharpens a result of Heath-Brown [ 10 ] . The improvement depends on a lower bound version of Bombieri’s theorem in short intervals. In establishing such a result we shall need to “intersect” two lower bound prime-detecting sieves, and we give a more general discussion on this point which may have further applications. Introduction

Answering a question raised by M. Car, Heath-Brown
[

n=p+ab

(1.1)

where
p

\max(a,b)\leq p^{\frac{1}{2}-\delta}

(1.2)

(where
\delta

ab\leq n^{\theta}

(1.3)

where
\theta<1

[

\theta
may be assigned any value
>\frac{3}{4}
.

Recently Lü and Ren
[
13
]
claimed a sharpening of the above result, requiring only
\theta>\frac{3}{5}
in (
1.3
). Unfortunately, there is a substantial error in the last paragraph of
[
13
]
.

In the present paper the following theorem is proved.

Theorem 1.1
.

Given
\epsilon>0
,
every large integer
n
can be written in the form (
1.1
) with
a,b
positive integers subject to (
1.2
) and

ab\leq n^{0.55+\epsilon}.

The key auxiliary result that we use is given below as Theorem 1.2. We require some notation. Let
x
be a large positive number.
Write
{\cal L}=\log x
and
\rho(n)
for the indicator function of the primes. Given an arithmetic function
f(n)

E_{f}(y,h;q,a)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle y-h<n\leq y}\\
{\scriptstyle n\equiv a\bmod{q}}\end{subarray}}f(n)\,-\,\frac{h}{\phi(q)h_{0}}\sum_{y-h_{0}<n\leq y}f(n),

where
\frac{1}{2}x<y\leq x
and

.
Constants implied by the
\ll

\epsilon

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\theta=0.55+\epsilon

\lambda(n)
with the following properties.

(i) We have

\lambda(n)\leq\rho(n)\quad(2\leq n\leq x),

\text{\rm if}\ \lambda(n)\neq 0\ \ \text{\rm then}\ \ p|n\Rightarrow p\geq x^{\frac{1}{10}}.

(ii) If

\sum_{y-h_{0}<n\leq y}\lambda(n)\gg h_{0}{\cal L}^{-1}.

(iii) Let
A>0
,
Q=x^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}}{\cal L}^{-C}

Then

\sum_{q\leq Q}\max_{(a,q)=1}\max_{h\leq x^{\theta}}\max_{y\sim x}\left|E_{\lambda}(y,h;q,a)\right|\ll x^{\theta}{\cal L}^{-A}.

(1.4)

P(z)=\prod_{p<z}p\qquad\text{and}\quad\psi(n,z)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\ (n,P(z))=1\\

Proof.

2
Mean-Values of Dirichlet Polynomials

Throughout sections 2 and 3 we suppose that

{\cal L}^{A+B}<Q\leq Q_{0}.

(2.1)

For
N,M,K\geq 1
, a character
\chi(\cdot)
and a complex variable
s
, we write

N(s,\chi)=\sum_{n\asymp N}b_{n}n^{-s}\chi(n),\quad M(s,\chi)=\sum_{m\asymp M}a_{m}m^{-s}\chi(m),

K(s,\chi)=\sum_{k\asymp K}k^{-s}\chi(k)

for Dirichlet polynomials, where

a_{m}\ll d(m)^{B},\qquad b_{n}\ll d(n)^{B}.

(2.2)

For a fixed
Q>{\cal L}^{A+B}
and
x^{2}\geq T\geq 1
we write, for
1\leq u<\infty
,

||N||_{u}=\left(\sum_{q\sim Q}{\sum_{\chi\bmod{q}}\kern-6.0pt}^{*}\int_{(T-1)/2}^{T}\left|N\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+it,\chi\right)\right|^{u}dt\right)^{1/u}.

We also write

||N||_{\infty}=\sup_{(t,q,\chi)}\left|N\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+it,\chi\right)\right|

where the supremum is taken over all primitive characters
\chi\pmod{q},q\sim Q
and
\frac{1}{2}(T-1)\leq t\leq T
.

It is convenient to write (see Lemma 3.2 below for the application)

\Psi(T)=\min\left(x^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}},x^{\frac{1}{2}}T^{-1}\right).

Here, and throughout the rest of the paper,
\theta=0.55+\epsilon
.

Lemma 2.1
.

We have

||N||_{2}^{2}\ll\left(N+Q^{2}T\right)\sum_{n\asymp N}|b_{n}|^{2}n^{-1}.

Proof.

See
[
14
, Theorem 7.1]
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let

M(s,\chi),N(s,\chi)

be defined as above and

MN\asymp x
.
Suppose that

\max(M,N)\ll x^{\theta}{\cal L}^{-2A-B}.

(2.3)

Then

\Psi(T)||MN||_{1}\ll Qx^{\theta}{\cal L}^{-A}.

(2.4)

Proof.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, Lemma 2.1, and (
2.2
) we have

\Psi(T)||MN||_{1}\ll\Psi(T)\left(Q^{2}T+M\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(Q^{2}T+N\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}{\cal L}^{B}.

(2.5)

For
0\leq\beta\leq 1
, we observe that

\max_{T\geq 1}\Psi(T)T^{\beta}=x^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}}\left(x^{1-\theta}\right)^{\beta}.

Thus, in estimating the right hand side of (
2.5
) we may suppose that
T=x^{1-\theta}
. Now

x^{1-\theta}(MN)^{\frac{1}{2}}{\cal L}^{B}\ll x^{\theta}{\cal L}^{B}\ll Qx^{\theta}{\cal L}^{-A}

from (
2.1
);

\displaystyle x^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}}QT^{\frac{1}{2}}\max(M,N)^{\frac{1}{2}}{\cal L}^{B}

\displaystyle\ll x^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}}Qx^{\frac{1}{2}(1-\theta)}x^{\frac{1}{2}\theta}{\cal L}^{-A-B}{\cal L}^{B}

\displaystyle=Qx^{\theta}{\cal L}^{-A};

and

\displaystyle x^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}}Q^{2}x^{1-\theta}{\cal L}^{B}

\displaystyle\ll x^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}}Qx^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}}{\cal L}^{-A-B}x^{(1-\theta)}{\cal L}^{B}

\displaystyle=Qx^{\theta}{\cal L}^{-A}.

This gives a satisfactory estimate for the right-hand side of (
2.5
) and proves (
2.4
).
∎

.

.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
K(s,\chi),M(s,\chi)
and
N(s,\chi)
be defined as above.
Suppose that
KMN\asymp x,Q\leq x^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}}{\cal L}^{-A-B}
and

M\ll x^{0.55},\quad N\ll x^{0.275},\quad MN\ll x^{0.775}.

(2.6)

Then

\Psi(T)||KMN||_{1}\ll Qx^{\theta}{\cal L}^{-A}.

Proof.

For small
Q
this would follow from
[
2
, Lemma 10]
. We give a full proof of the result here for completeness. We consider two cases.

Case 1.

K\leq QT
. We apply Hölder’s inequality, Lemmas 2.1 and 2.3 in conjunction with (
2.2
) to give

\begin{split}\Psi(T)||&KMN||_{1}\leq\Psi(T)||M||_{2}||N||_{4}||K||_{4}\\
&\ll\Psi(T)\left(Q^{2}T+M\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(Q^{2}T+N^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(Q^{2}T+Q^{4}T^{-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}{\cal L}^{B}.\end{split}

(2.7)

Write
S
for the sum of all the terms obtained by multiplying out the last expression, except for
\Psi(T)(MN)^{\frac{1}{2}}QT^{-\frac{1}{4}}
. In estimating
S
we may take
T=x^{1-\theta}
. Now

S\ll{\cal L}^{B}x^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}}\left(Q^{2}x^{1-\theta}+Qx^{\frac{1}{2}(1-\theta)}M^{\frac{1}{2}}+Q^{\frac{3}{2}}x^{\frac{3}{4}(1-\theta)}N^{\frac{1}{2}}+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{4}(1-\theta)}(MN)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right).

Since
Q\leq x^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}}{\cal L}^{-A-B}
, we have

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{B}x^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}}Q^{2}x^{1-\theta}\ll Qx^{\theta}{\cal L}^{-A},

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{B}x^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}}Qx^{\frac{1}{2}(1-\theta)}M^{\frac{1}{2}}\ll Q{\cal L}^{B}x^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon},

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{B}x^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{3}{2}}x^{\frac{3}{4}(1-\theta)}N^{\frac{1}{2}}\ll Q{\cal L}^{B}x^{\theta-\frac{1}{4}\epsilon},

and

{\cal L}^{B}x^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{4}(1-\theta)}(MN)^{\frac{1}{2}}\ll Q{\cal L}^{B}x^{\theta-\frac{1}{4}\epsilon}.

This gives a satisfactory estimate for
S
. For the term
\Psi(T)(MN)^{\frac{1}{2}}QT^{-\frac{1}{4}}
we use
T\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\Psi(T)(MN)^{\frac{1}{2}}QT^{-\frac{1}{4}}

\displaystyle\ll x^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{4}(1+\theta)}Q

\displaystyle\ll Qx^{\theta}{\cal L}^{-A}

since
\frac{1}{4}(1+\theta)<\frac{1}{2}
. This covers Case 1.

Case 2.

K>QT
. From Lemma 2.4,

\displaystyle\Psi(T)||KMN||_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\Psi(T)||M||_{2}||N||_{2}||K||_{\infty}

\displaystyle\ll\Psi(T)\left(Q^{2}T+M\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(Q^{2}T+N\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}{\cal L}^{B}.

Since

\left(Q^{2}T+N\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\ll\left(Q^{2}T+N^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(Q^{2}T\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}

the result follows as in Case 1. This completes the proof of Lemma 2.5.
∎

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
b>0,T>1

E(u)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\ 0<u<1\\

Then

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{b-iT}^{b+iT}\frac{u^{s}}{s}\,ds=E(u)+O\left(\frac{u^{b}}{T|\log u|}\right).

(3.1)

Proof.

This is
[

We note that, by standard procedures using the above, we can derive results of the form (

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
x^{0.45}\leq M\leq x^{0.55}
and
MN\asymp x
. Write

f(k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle mn=k}\\
{\scriptstyle m\asymp M,n\asymp N}\end{subarray}}a_{m}b_{n}

with

subject to (
2.2
). Then (
1.5
) holds for

Proof.

We first reduce the “edge effects” by noting that we can restrict
y
and
y-h
to values
n+\frac{1}{2},n\in\mathbb{N}
with a maximum error

\sum_{q\sim Q}{\sum_{\chi\bmod{q}}\kern-6.0pt}^{*}\max_{n\ll x}\tau(n)^{B}\ll Q^{2}x^{\epsilon},

which is certainly a negligible error.
It then follows from Lemma 3.1 that

\displaystyle E_{f}(y,h;\chi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y-h<k\leq y}f(k)\chi(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\frac{1}{2}-iT}^{\frac{1}{2}+iT}M(s,\chi)N(s,\chi)\frac{y^{s}-(y-h)^{s}}{s}ds+O\left(V_{y,h}\right),

where

V_{y,h}=\sum_{k\asymp x}\tau(k)^{B}\frac{1}{T\min(|\log(y/k)|,|\log((y-h)/k)|)}\,.

A simple calculation gives

\max_{\frac{1}{2}x\leq y\leq x}\,\max_{h\leq x^{\theta}}V_{y,h}\ll\frac{x^{1+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon}}{T}\,.

If we take
T=x
the error is again negligible from the above.

Next we note that

\frac{y^{s}-(y-h)^{s}}{s}=\int_{y-h}^{y}u^{s-1}du\ll hx^{-\frac{1}{2}},

and

\frac{y^{s}-(y-h)^{s}}{s}\ll\frac{x^{\frac{1}{2}}}{|s|}.

It follows that, for
t\sim T
, we have

\frac{y^{s}-(y-h)^{s}}{s}\ll\Psi(T).

This demonstrates the significance of our choice for
\Psi(T)
.
It therefore remains to prove that, with

\sum_{q\sim Q}{\sum_{\chi\bmod{q}}\kern-6.0pt}^{*}\max_{1\leq T\leq x}\Psi(T){\cal L}\int_{\frac{1}{2}(T-1)}^{T}|M(s,\chi)N(s,\chi)|dt\ll Qx^{\theta}{\cal L}^{-A}.

This follows immediately from Lemma 2.2 and completes the proof.

4
Some Further Notation

Given an arithmetic function
f(n)
we write
\mathbb{f}
to mean the sequence
f(k),k\in[\tfrac{1}{2}x,x)\cap\mathbb{N}
. So, for example,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
means the sequence
\lambda(k),\frac{1}{2}x\leq k<x
.
We recall the definition of a
sublinear functional

{\cal M}
defined over the set of all real sequences. That is, given sequences

\mathbb{w},\mathbb{v}
,
we have

{\cal M}(c\mathbb{w})=|c|{\cal M}(\mathbb{w})\ \ (c\in\mathbb{R}),\qquad{\cal M}(\mathbb{w}+\mathbb{v})\leq{\cal M}(\mathbb{w})+{\cal M}(\mathbb{v})\,.

We then define a sequence
\bf w
to be
{\cal M}
-regular
if it satisfies

{\cal M}(\mathbb{w})\ll 1.

Here we assume the parameter
x\rightarrow\infty
and the implied constant is independent of
x
.
For a set
\Phi
of sublinear functionals, a sequence
\mathbb{w}
is said to be
\Phi
-regular
if
\mathbb{w}
is
{\cal M}
-regular for each
{\cal M}\in\Phi
.

Example 1.
Let
A>0
; let
Q_{0}(A)=x^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}}{\cal L}^{-A-B},\eta=\eta(Q)=x^{-\theta}Q^{-1}{\cal L}^{A}
and

{\cal M}_{Q}({\bf w})=\eta\sum_{q\sim Q}{\sum_{\chi\bmod{q}}\kern-6.0pt}^{*}\max_{h\leq x^{\theta}}\,\max_{y\sim x}\left|E_{w}(y,h,\chi)\right|\,.

We put

\Phi_{1}=\left\{{\cal M}_{Q}:{\cal L}^{A+B}\leq Q\leq Q_{0}\right\}\,,\quad\Phi_{2}=\left\{{\cal M}_{Q}:1\leq Q\leq{\cal L}^{A+B}\right\}\,.

Then (1.4) of Theorem 2 is the requirement that
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
be both
\Phi_{1}
-regular and
\Phi_{2}
-regular.

In the following we write
\mathcal{B}
for the set of integers in the interval
(y-h_{0},y)
for some
y\sim x
,

Example 2.
Let
{\cal A}\subset\mathcal{B}
and let
\sigma
be an approximation to
|{\cal A}||\mathcal{B}|^{-1}
. We wish to show that
{\cal A}
contains primes. Let

{\cal M}({\bf w})=\frac{{\cal L}^{2}}{\sigma|\mathcal{B}|}\left|\sum_{k\in{\cal A}}w(k)-\sigma\sum_{k\in\mathcal{B}}w(k)\right|\,.

Suppose an
{\cal M}
-regular sequence
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
satisfies
\lambda(k)\leq\rho(k)
and

\sum_{k\in\mathcal{B}}\lambda(k)\gg\sum_{k\in\mathcal{B}}\rho(k)\,.

(4.1)

Then we have

\left|\sum_{k\in{\cal A}}\lambda(k)-\sigma\sum_{k\in\mathcal{B}}\lambda(k)\,\right|\ll\frac{\sigma|\mathcal{B}|}{{\cal L}^{2}},

and, from the prime number theorem for the slightly shortened interval

(y-h_{0},y)
,

\sigma\sum_{k\in\mathcal{B}}\lambda(k)\gg\frac{\sigma|\mathcal{B}|}{{\cal L}}\,.

Consequently

\sum_{k\in{\cal A}}\rho(k)\geq\sum_{k\in{\cal A}}\lambda(k)\gg\sigma|\mathcal{B}|{\cal L}^{-1}\,,

and so
{\cal A}
contains primes.

By the sublinearity definition, we can thus establish lower bounds for the number of primes in sets by constructing
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
as above using a linear combination of a bounded number of regular sequences. In particular, to prove Theorem 1.2, we need to show that the
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
we construct is a combination of sequences that are regular for both
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
. We now briefly sketch the process developed in
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
for constructing a lower bound sieve
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
. Let us only consider
k>\frac{1}{2}x
. Then, for any value
z_{0}
,

\rho(k)=\psi(k,x^{\frac{1}{2}})=\psi(k,z_{0})-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle z_{0}\leq p<x^{\frac{1}{2}}}\\
{\scriptstyle k=p\ell}\end{subarray}}\psi(\ell,p)\,,

using Buchstab’s identity. We choose
z_{0}
to be the largest value with
\psi(k,z_{0})
being regular.
Now we may apply Buchstab’s identity a bounded number of times – indeed we want an
even
(say
K
) number of times – to give

\rho(k)=\psi(k,z_{0})-f_{1}(k)-f_{3}(k)-\ldots-f_{K-1}(k)+g_{2}(k)+\ldots+g_{K}(k)

for certain functions
f_{j},g_{j}
defined as sums of the
\psi
function over
j+1
variables which multiply together to give
k
. By their definition, these functions are non-negative. Now, suppose each
f_{j}(k)
is regular, and we split
g_{j}
into a regular term and a (possibly non-existent) term which is not regular:
g_{j}(k)=r_{j}(k)+s_{j}(k)
, say. Then the function

\lambda(k)=\psi(k,z_{0})-f_{1}(k)-f_{3}(k)-\ldots-f_{K-1}(k)+r_{2}(k)+\ldots+r_{K}(k)

(4.2)

is certainly regular by the sublinearity definition. Since we have only discarded non-negative terms, we have
\lambda(k)\leq\rho(k)
. The challenge that remains is to carry out the above process in such a way that (
4.1
) holds. When successful, we shall call
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
a
non-trivial lower bound prime-detecting sieve
for whatever
{\cal M}
or family of
{\cal M}
is under consideration. Clearly our ultimate challenge is to find such a
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
for Example 1, regular for both families
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
defined there.

Given a sublinear functional
{\cal M}
(or family
\Phi
of such), we call the process leading to (
4.2
) a
permissible decomposition for

{\cal M}
(respectively,
\Phi
)
. We write
{\mathfrak{D}}
with or without a subscript for a permissible decomposition. Now, if (
4.1
) holds with
\lambda(k)\leq\rho(k)
, then clearly

\sum_{k\in\mathcal{B}}\lambda(k)=(1-d)\sum_{k\in\mathcal{B}}\rho(k)\,

for some
d\in(0,1)
. By what we have written so far,
d
might vary somewhat with
x
, but we shall show later that in practice
d=\delta+o(1)
for a fixed
\delta\in(0,1)
. We write this as
\delta({\mathfrak{D}})
to show that it depends essentially on the decomposition process (equivalently we could have defined it as
\delta(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
).

5
Intersecting Two Prime-Detecting Sieves

When constructing a prime detecting sieve as outlined in
[
8
, Chapters 3, 5–11]
, one first garners the necessary arithmetical information (what is commonly know as Type I & II information, although there are sub-varieties of these types that appear as well), and then one applies Buchstab’s identity to decompose a sum over primes in a given set,
{\cal A}
, into multiple sums. Alternatively, and equivalently, as in the previous section we use Buchstab’s identity applied to
\rho(k)
summed over the set of interest, which often means we weight
\rho(k)
with auxiliary functions like
k^{-s},\chi(k)
or
e(\alpha\ell k)
.

Suppose
K
is an even integer, and
\zeta>0
(but not arbitrarily small) is given. Write
\xi=x^{\zeta}
.
For
j\leq K<\zeta^{-1}
write

E_{j}=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}:\alpha_{i}\geq\zeta\ (1\leq i\leq j),\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{j}\leq 1\}.

We write
\mathbf{p}_{j}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{j})
where

p_{i}=x^{\alpha_{i}},1\leq i\leq j,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in E_{j}
, and

\Pi_{j}=\prod_{1\leq i\leq j}p_{j}\,.

Given a Jordan measurable subset
{\cal F}
of
E_{j}
we may thus write

\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in{\cal F}}

to indicate the summation ranges for
\mathbf{p}_{j}
. In practice,
{\cal F}
will always be the interior of a polyhedron (or union of finitely many polyhedrons).
When we apply Buchstab’s identity in a straightforward way we will have summation ranges satisfying

p_{j}<p_{j-1}<\ldots<p_{1},\qquad{p_{r}}^{2}(p_{1}\ldots p_{r-1})<x,\ \text{for}\ \ 2\leq r\leq j.

We write
\mathcal{H}_{j}
for the set of all possible values of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}
in this case.

When we sketched out a decomposition in the previous section we assumed we would always decompose the “inner variable” counted with the
\psi
notation using Buchstab’s identity. This is not always the most efficient course of action. Instead one can apply what is known as a rôle-reversal. We describe the simplest case of this technique (see also
[
8
, §3.5]
). Suppose
{\cal F}
is a Jordan measurable region of
\mathcal{H}_{2}
and suppose we have a term to consider of the form

w(k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\in{\cal F}}\\
{\scriptstyle k=\Pi_{2}\ell}\end{subarray}}\psi(\ell,p_{2})\,.

Instead of applying Buchstab’s identity to
\psi(\ell,p_{2})
, we rewrite
{\bf w}
as

w(k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\in{\cal F}}\\
{\scriptstyle k=n_{1}p_{2}\ell}\end{subarray}}\psi(\ell,p_{2})\psi\left(n_{1},\left(\frac{x}{\ell p_{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

(5.1)

where
n_{1}=x^{\alpha_{1}}
, and apply Buchstab’s identity to

\psi\left(n_{1},\left(\frac{x}{\ell p_{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\,.

Clearly one could alternatively have applied this process to decompose
p_{2}
as well or instead of
p_{1}
. The possibilities become rather complicated at this point (consider the working in
[
2
, pp.32-41]
).
We will first set up the sieve machinery in the case where there are no rôle-reversals. We shall need to take these into account later to prove our main theorems, but the general result we prove initially is not valid when rôle-reversals are used. For the purposes of clarity it may help the reader to see the simplest case first.

Our first task is to describe the decomposition process in a way that will help develop the method for intersecting two sieves. Let
\Phi
be a set of sublinear functionals, and our desire is to construct
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
which is
\Phi
-regular.
Now, given any Jordan-measurable set
R_{j}\subset E_{j}
we write
R_{j}^{*}
for the set of all

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}\in E_{t},t\geq j
whose coordinates can be partitioned into
j
sets (none of which is empty) such that if we form the vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}
by summing the
\alpha_{i}
in the
j
sets, then
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in R_{j}
. Suppose there are sets
R_{j}
and functions
z(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})\geq\xi
such that if we write

{\cal R}=\bigcup_{j}R_{j}^{*}

then the sequence

v_{k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle k=\Pi_{t}\ell}\\
{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}\in V_{t}\cap{\cal R}}\end{subarray}}\psi(\ell,z^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}))

is
\Phi
-regular for every Jordan-measurable subset
V_{t}\subset E_{t}
and all functions
z^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t})\leq z(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t})
. If
U_{j}
is a Jordan-measurable subset of
E_{j}\cap{\cal R}
then we say
U_{j}
is a
Type I
\Phi
-regular domain
.
Let
U_{j}
be such a Type I
\Phi
-regular domain with associated
z(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})
.
Write

w_{k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle k=\Pi_{j}\ell p}\\
{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in U_{j}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{z^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})\leq p<z(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})}\psi(\ell,p)\,.

Then, by Buchstab’s identity, for any
z^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})<z(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})
we have

w_{k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle k=\Pi_{j}\ell}\\
{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in U_{j}}\end{subarray}}\psi(\ell,z(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}))-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle k=\Pi_{j}\ell}\\
{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in U_{j}}\end{subarray}}\psi(\ell,z^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}))\,,

and so
{\bf w}
is
\Phi
-regular. For future reference we call this
Property
{\cal R}
.

Results such as these usually follow from what the second-named author has called
The Fundamental Theorem
: see
[
8
, Theorems 3.1, 5.2]
. By the method of proof, if we get regularity with values
z(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t})
, we also get regularity for any
z^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t})\in[2,z(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t})]
. In our present context, for
\Phi_{1}
-regularity, Lemma 3.3 gives

R_{2}=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\in E_{2}:\alpha_{1}\leq 0.55,\ \alpha_{2}\leq 0.275,\ \alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}\leq 0.775\},

with
z(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})\equiv x^{\frac{1}{10}}
.
We would thus take
\zeta={\frac{1}{10}}
in this case. We write, in general,
z(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})=x^{\zeta(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})}
, and note that
\zeta(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})
will be piece-wise linear in each coordinate by the methods used (for example, see
[
8
, p.153, Diagram 7.3]
) and
\zeta
would be the infimum of all
\zeta(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})
.

Now, if we use rôle-reversals, we might have in our sums instead of
p_{i}
a “sieved-variable”
n_{i}
all of whose prime factors exceed
g(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t})=x^{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t})}
where
\gamma(\cdot)
is piecewise linear in each variable. That is we are counting
n_{i}
with a weight
\psi(n_{i},g(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}))
. We can do this by breaking up the sum into multiple sums over appropriate domains.
We remark that we can replace as many prime variables
p_{i}
as we wish by this process. We illustrate this by considering
(
5.1
). The variable
\ell
there has all its prime factors exceeding
p_{2}
so, for some integer
t<\zeta^{-1}
,

w(k)=\sum_{3\leq h\leq t}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\in{\cal F}}\\
{\scriptstyle k=n_{1}p_{2}p_{3}\cdots p_{h}}\end{subarray}}\psi\left(n_{1},\left(\frac{x}{p_{2}p_{3}\cdots p_{h}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

Here the variables
p_{j},j\geq 3
satisfy
p_{h}>p_{h-1}>\ldots>p_{3}>p_{2}
. We can rewrite
w(k)
then as

w(k)=\sum_{3\leq h\leq t}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{h}\in{\cal F}_{h}}\\
{\scriptstyle k=np_{2}p_{3}\cdots p_{h}}\end{subarray}}\psi\left(n,\left(\frac{x}{p_{2}p_{3}\cdots p_{h}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

for certain regions
{\cal F}_{h}\in E_{h}
. For example,

{\cal F}_{2}=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}:(1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1})\in{\cal F},\alpha_{2}>\alpha_{1}\}\,,

and

{\cal F}_{3}=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{3}:(1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{3},\alpha_{1})\in{\cal F},\alpha_{3}>\alpha_{2}>\alpha_{1}\}\,.

Similarly, but with one crucial difference, given any Jordan-measurable set
S_{j}\subset E_{j}
we define
S_{j}^{*}
to be the set of all

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}\in E_{t},t\geq j
whose coordinates can be partitioned into
j+1
sets (at most one of which is empty) such that if we form the vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}
by summing the
\alpha_{i}
in
j
of the (non-empty) sets, then
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in S_{j}
. Suppose there are sets
S_{j}
such that if we write

{\cal S}=\bigcup_{j}S_{j}^{*}

then the sequence

v_{k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle k=\Pi_{t}}\\
{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}\in V_{t}\cap{\cal S}}\end{subarray}}1

is
\Phi
-regular for every Jordan-measurable subset
V_{t}\subset E_{t}
. If
U_{j}
is a Jordan-measurable subset of
E_{j}\cap{\cal S}
then we say
U_{j}
is a
Type II
\Phi
-regular domain
. This corresponds to Type II (and possible more complicated variants) information in the usual description of this sieve method. For
\Phi_{1}
we have just the one set
S_{1}=[0.45,0.55]
by Lemma 3.2. So, in this case,
{\cal S}
is comprised of all regions in
j
dimensions,
1\leq j\leq\zeta^{-1}
, contained in
E_{j}
where a sum of a subset of the coordinates lies in
S_{1}
. This is just the union of a finite number of polyhedra. It follows from our definition of
{\cal S}
that the sequence

w_{k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle k=n_{1}\cdots n_{t}}\\
{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}\in V_{t}\cap{\cal S}}\end{subarray}}c_{1}(n_{1})\cdots c_{t}(n_{t})

is
\Phi
-regular for any Jordan-measurable subset
V_{t}\subset E_{t}
, where each
c_{j}(n)
is either
\rho(n)
or
\psi(n,g(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}))
for some function
g(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t})=x^{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t})}
where
\gamma(\cdot)
is piecewise linear in each variable. Let us illustrate this with the simplest case.
Suppose we investigate

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in V_{j}\cap{\cal S}}\\
{\scriptstyle k=\Pi_{j}\ell}\end{subarray}}\psi(\ell,g(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})).

This can be written as the sum of

sequences corresponding to

.
Each sequence is
\Phi
-regular from the definition of

, because
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}
belongs to a Jordan-measurable region (guaranteed by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in V_{j}
and the subsequent coordinates only have piecewise linear restrictions).

Consider now how the decomposition is framed to construct a lower bound sieve. We always start with

\rho(k)=\psi(k,z_{0})-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle z_{0}\leq p_{1}<x^{\frac{1}{2}}}\\
{\scriptstyle k=p_{1}\ell}\end{subarray}}\psi(\ell,z(\alpha_{1}))+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle z(\alpha_{1})\leq p_{2}<p_{1}<x^{\frac{1}{2}}}\\
{\scriptstyle k=\Pi_{2}\ell}\end{subarray}}\psi(\ell,p_{2}).

(5.2)

The

\Phi

to conform with (

We note that the final sum, which for future reference we shall call

We have thus written

{\scriptstyle k=\Pi_{2}\ell}\end{subarray}}\psi(\ell,p_{2})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\in{\cal F}_{3}}\\
{\scriptstyle k=\Pi_{2}\ell}\end{subarray}}\psi(\ell,z(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}))-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\in{\cal F}_{3}}\\
{\scriptstyle\zeta(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})\leq\alpha_{3}<\alpha_{2}}\\
{\scriptstyle k=\Pi_{3}\ell}\end{subarray}}\psi(\ell,z(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{3}))+\Sigma_{4}(k)\,,

say, where the first two sequences on the right hand side above are

.

Finally, we write
{\cal F}_{2}={\cal F}\setminus\left({\cal F}_{1}\cup{\cal F}_{3}\right)
. We then have the term

s_{2}^{\prime}(k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\in{\cal F}_{2}}\\
{\scriptstyle k=\Pi_{2}\ell}\end{subarray}}\psi(\ell,p_{2})

which at first sight we might discard as a positive non-regular term. However, it is possible to rewrite

as the combination of sequences written as sums over two, three or more primes:

s_{2}^{2}(k)+s_{2}^{3}(k)+s_{2}^{4}(k)+\ldots=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\in{\cal F}_{2}}\\
{\scriptstyle k=\Pi_{2}p_{3}}\end{subarray}}1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{3}\in{\cal J}_{3}}\\
{\scriptstyle k=\Pi_{3}p_{4}}\end{subarray}}1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{4}\in{\cal J}_{4}}\\
{\scriptstyle k=\Pi_{4}p_{5}}\end{subarray}}1\ldots,

for certain sets

. The sequence

must be discarded. But we can split each

and

where

is regular.
We then have

r_{2}(k)=v_{2}(k)+\sigma_{2}(k)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\in{\cal F}_{3}}\\
{\scriptstyle k=\Pi_{2}\ell}\end{subarray}}\psi(\ell,z(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}))

in the notation of (

We have now given enough notation and explanation to state and prove a general result on intersecting lower bound prime-detecting sieves.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
{\mathfrak{D}}_{1},{\mathfrak{D}}_{2}
be two decompositions (constructed as above with no rôle-reversals) which are permissible for the families of sublinear functionals
\Xi_{1},\Xi_{2}
respectively. Then we can define a decomposition
{\mathfrak{D}}_{3}={\mathfrak{D}}_{1}\cap{\mathfrak{D}}_{2}
which is permissible for
\Xi_{1}\cup\Xi_{2}
, and which satisfies

\delta({\mathfrak{D}}_{3})\leq\delta({\mathfrak{D}}_{1})+\delta({\mathfrak{D}}_{2}).

(5.3)

Corollary
.

Given the conditions of the theorem with
\delta({\mathfrak{D}}_{1})+\delta({\mathfrak{D}}_{2})<1
, then we can construct a non-trivial lower bound prime-detecting sieve
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
which is both
\Xi_{1}
and
\Xi_{2}
regular.

Proof of Theorem 5.1.

For simplicity we suppose that
{\cal J}_{i}\cap{\cal S}=\emptyset
for every
i
at each stage for both decompositions. This does not change the principle involved in the proof – it just simplifies the details of describing the discarded sums at each stage.
Let
{\cal R}_{1},{\cal R}_{2},{\cal S}_{1},{\cal S}_{2}
correspond to
{\mathfrak{D}}_{1},{\mathfrak{D}}_{2}
respectively.
Our first task is to define
{\mathfrak{D}}_{3}={\mathfrak{D}}_{1}\cap{\mathfrak{D}}_{2}
. We do this inductively as follows.
We let
z_{0,j}
and
z_{j}(\alpha_{1})
correspond to
{\mathfrak{D}}_{j}
. We then put

z_{0,3}=\min_{j=1,2}z_{0,j},\qquad z_{3}(\alpha_{1})=\min_{j=1,2}z_{j}(\alpha_{1})\,.

Now modify
{\mathfrak{D}}_{1},{\mathfrak{D}}_{2}
by replacing
z_{0,j}
and
z_{j}(\alpha_{1}),j=1,2
in their definition with the values for
j=3
. Of course, in some circumstances we may not have modified them at all, but in general the regions
{\cal F}^{(j)}
corresponding to our construction for each decomposition will have increased in size to a new
{\cal F}^{(3)}
. Consider the region
{\cal F}^{(3)}\setminus{\cal F}^{(1)}
. For the sake of argument, suppose that
z_{1}(\alpha_{1})>z_{2}(\alpha_{1})
for
\alpha_{1}\in{\cal I}
. This means that a new region

\{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}):\alpha_{1}\in{\cal I},\zeta_{2}(\alpha_{1})<\alpha_{2}<\zeta_{1}(\alpha_{1})\}

has appeared in the extended
{\mathfrak{D}}_{1}
decomposition. This increases the range of summation for the variable
\alpha_{2}
. However, by what we called
Property
{\cal R}
, the sum over this new range is a
\Xi_{1}
-regular sequence. We get a similar result for
{\cal F}^{(3)}\setminus{\cal F}^{(2)}
. The crucial point is that increasing the summation domain does not disrupt the regularity property of the resulting sequence. So in the following we can redefine
{\cal F}_{1}^{(1)}
to be
{\cal F}_{1}^{(1)}\cup({\cal F}^{(3)}\setminus{\cal F}^{(1)})
, and similarly for
{\cal F}_{1}^{(2)}
.

In
{\mathfrak{D}}_{3}
we discard sums over the region
{\cal F}_{2}^{(1)}\cup{\cal F}_{2}^{(2)}
. The sum over
{\cal F}_{1}^{(1)}\cap{\cal F}_{1}^{(2)}
is clearly regular for both
\Xi_{1}
and
\Xi_{2}
: call this term
u_{2}(k)
. Buchstab’s identity can be applied twice more for
{\cal F}_{3}^{(1)}\cap{\cal F}_{3}^{(2)}
leading to terms
v_{2}(k)-f_{3}(k)+\Sigma_{4}(k)
, say. Write
w_{2}(k)=u_{2}(k)+v_{2}(k)
. That only leaves the cases
{\cal F}_{1}^{(i)}\cap{\cal F}_{3}^{(j)},\ i\neq j
to discuss. Without loss of generality consider
{\cal F}_{1}^{(1)}\cap{\cal F}_{3}^{(2)}
. In this case we work in
{\mathfrak{D}}_{3}
just as we would in
{\mathfrak{D}}_{2}
(that is, applying Buchstab’s identity twice more) giving the same shape sums. The important point to observe is that each of these sums is still regular for
\Xi_{1}
. To see this, simply note that in each of the new sums we still have
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
unchanged. Here we have observed that, keeping the first two coordinates fixed,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\in{\cal S}_{1}\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{3}\in{\cal S}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{4}\in{\cal S}_{1}
. This is a trivial consequence of our definition of a Type II
\Phi
-regular domain.
We have thus started constructing our lower bound sieve
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
which is regular for both
\Xi_{1}
and
\Xi_{2}
. We have
\lambda(k)\leq\rho(k)
with

\lambda(k)=\psi(k,z_{0,3})-f_{1}(k)+w_{2}(k)-f_{3}(k)+\Sigma_{4}(k),

where the first
4
terms on the right hand side above are regular for both
\Xi_{1}
and
\Xi_{2}
. As in our previous discussion we can treat
\Sigma_{4}
similarly and continue to the case
K_{3}=\max(K_{1},K_{2})
by induction. At each stage
\delta_{j}^{(3)}\leq\delta_{j}^{(1)}+\delta_{j}^{(2)}
. Summing over
j
then gives (
5.3
) as required.
∎

Now, if rôle-reversals came into play, we can instantly see a problem might arise over a new type of region, even proceeding beyond
j=2
, where one applies Buchstab twice more in
{\mathfrak{D}}_{1}
in a straightforward manner but uses a rôle reversal in
{\mathfrak{D}}_{2}
, before applying Buchstab twice. We are thus jumping straight from a two dimensional region to a six dimensional one. Our philosophy would suggest we discard those parts of sums which are non-negative and not regular in the individual cases. This immediately gets complicated, because in mixing the two decompositions we then have to remove several sums, not all of which are non-negative. We illustrate this as follows.

Suppose at the
j=2
stage we have the term

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\in{\cal F}}\\
{\scriptstyle k=p_{1}p_{2}\ell}\end{subarray}}\psi(\ell,p_{2})

which is regular for neither
\Phi_{1}
nor
\Phi_{2}
. Further suppose, that for
\Phi_{1}
we can apply Buchstab twice more, and for
\Phi_{2}
we rewrite it as per (
5.1
). This leads to the following two decompositions, where for simplicity (and since we are demonstrating that the result cannot be true in general) we shall assume that
z
is fixed, for certain regions for the variables:

\sum_{k=\Pi_{2}\ell}\psi(\ell,z)-\sum_{k=\Pi_{3}\ell}\psi(\ell,z)+\sum_{k=\Pi_{4}\ell}\psi(\ell,p_{4})\,,

(5.4)

\sum_{k=np_{2}\ell}\psi(\ell,p_{2})\psi(n,z)-\sum_{k=np_{2}p_{5}\ell}\psi(\ell,p_{2})\psi(n,z)+\sum_{k=np_{2}p_{5}p_{6}\ell}\psi(\ell,p_{2})\psi(n,p_{6})\,.

(5.5)

We would expect to split the final sum in each of (
5.4
), (
5.5
) into a regular term and a term which must be discarded. Suppose that the discarded sum in (
5.4
) corresponds to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{4}\in{\cal K}
. We would then have to split the variable
\ell
in each of the three terms in (
5.5
) into sums matching those in (
5.4
) and discard terms such as a sum,say
\Sigma^{\prime}
, over (we state the most complicated)

np_{2}p_{3}p_{4}p_{5}p_{6}\ell
with
np_{5}p_{6}=x^{\alpha_{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{4}\in{\cal K}
. Of course, at this point we are discarding some sums which could be negative (corresponding to the middle term in (
5.5
)). However, on reversing Buchstab’s identity it is clear that overall the discarded term is non-negative (indeed, it is
\delta_{2}^{(1)}
), and so the procedure is legitimate. It is a different matter, though, when we come to discard that part of the final sum in (
5.5
) over a region not belonging to
{\cal S}_{2}
, say we write this as
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{4}}^{\prime}\in{\cal K}^{\prime}
(
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{4}}^{\prime}
corresponding to the variables
p_{2},\ell,p_{5},p_{6}
). We split into three cases with the variable
\ell
unchanged, or decomposed as
\ell p_{3}
or
\ell p_{3}p_{4}
. If we had not discarded
\Sigma^{\prime}
we could reverse Buchstab’s identity and verify that the new discarded sum is overall non-negative (indeed, it would have been
\delta_{2}^{(2)}
). However, we cannot rule out that we are discarding negative terms and so our proof collapses.

We can put this argument another way. Let

\sigma(k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle k=np_{2}p_{3}p_{4}p_{5}p_{6}\ell}\\
{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{4}\in{\cal K}}\\
{\scriptstyle{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{4}}^{\prime}\in{\cal K}^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\psi(\ell,p_{2})\psi(n,p_{6})\,.

Then, in general, we would have to assume that
\sigma(k)
is regular for neither
\Phi_{1}
nor
\Phi_{2}
(though for some examples there might be a reason for it to be regular). Now, if we were constructing a lower bound
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
(regular for both
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
) for
\rho(k)+\sigma(k)
, our method would work. But
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is no longer a lower bound prime-detecting sieve as it possibly gives a positive count for the numbers for which
\sigma(k)
is non-zero. We cannot retrieve the situation by subtracting off this term, because, since
\sigma(k)
is not regular,
\lambda(k)-\sigma(k)
cannot be regular.

6
Application to Theorem 1.2

Before proving Theorem 1.2 we first state a more general result which allows us to have rôle-reversals in one of the decompositions.
The trick, of course, is to ensure the problem that we described at the end of the last section cannot occur. To this end we establish the following result.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
{\mathfrak{D}}_{1},{\mathfrak{D}}_{2}
be two decompositions which are permissible for the families of sublinear functionals
\Xi_{1},\Xi_{2}
respectively, and
{\mathfrak{D}}_{1}
involves no rôle-reversals. Assume for
{\mathfrak{D}}_{1}
we apply Buchstab no more than 4 times, and at the
K=2
stage there is a region
{\cal F}_{1}
, sums over which are
\Xi_{1}
-regular, a region
{\cal F}_{2}
which must be discarded, and a region
{\cal F}_{3}
where Buchstab can be applied twice more. Suppose further that
{\cal F}_{3}={\cal F}_{4}\cup{\cal F}_{5}
where sums over variables in
{\cal F}_{4}
are
\Xi_{2}
-regular (possibly using rôle-reversals) and, however the sums are treated over
{\cal F}_{5}
for
{\mathfrak{D}}_{2}
, there are no rôle-reversals. Then (
5.3
) holds.

Proof.

We only need to consider the two new cases introduced not covered by Theorem 5.1. First of all, there may be a subset, say
{\cal G}
of
{\cal F}_{1}
, where rôle-reversals are applied in the
{\mathfrak{D}}_{2}
decomposition. This is easily dealt with, since, however the variables in
k=p_{1}p_{2}\ell
are broken up, we can still combine them at any stage to give variables of the same size as
p_{1},p_{2},\ell
. For
{\cal F}_{4}
no obstacle arises since we would only be discarding a four dimensional region in
{\mathfrak{D}}_{1}
and we have all the sums remaining
\Xi_{2}
-regular.
∎

We can apply the above theorem directly to our problem with
\Xi_{j}=\Phi_{j},j=1,2
. We have described the regions
{\cal F}_{j},1\leq j\leq 3
in the previous section. To cover
{\mathfrak{D}}_{2}
and the corresponding
\Phi_{2}
-regularity, consider the situation described in
[
2
]
which gives

\delta({\mathfrak{D}}_{2})<0.01
when
\theta=0.55+\epsilon
. Here rôle-reversals are crucial. Looking at the proof, though, we notice that sums with
0.4\leq\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}\leq 0.46
are
\Phi_{2}
-regular
[
2
, Lemma 13]
. Also, if
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}<0.4
, no rôle-reversals are used (see
[
2
, p.37–38]
). We can thus apply our theorem with
\delta({\mathfrak{D}}_{2})<0.01
. Also,
\delta({\mathfrak{D}}_{1})
is the sum of the two integrals

\int_{0.275}^{0.45}\int_{0.55-\alpha_{1}}^{\min(\alpha_{1},(1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})/2)}\omega\left(\frac{1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{2}}\right)\frac{d\alpha_{2}d\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}^{2}\alpha_{1}}\,;

\int_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{4}\in{\cal G}}\omega\left(\frac{1-\alpha_{1}-\ldots\alpha_{4}}{\alpha_{4}}\right)\frac{d\alpha_{4}\ldots d\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{4}^{2}\ldots\alpha_{1}}\,.

Here
{\cal G}
is the region with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\in{\cal F}_{3},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{4}\in\mathcal{H}_{4},\alpha_{4}\geq 0.1
, and no sum of three or all four of

\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{4}
is between
0.45
and
0.55
.

Calculations give the first integral as
<0.71
. The second integral would have been
<0.16
even before removing sums of variables between
0.45
and
0.55
. With those removals the integral is
<0.02
. So, we get a prime detecting lower bound sieve with a deficit
<\frac{3}{4}
for
\Phi_{1}\cup\Phi_{2}
, which establishes Theorem 2.

7
Proof of Theorem 1.1

This is not very different from the proof of Heath-Brown’s result
[
10
]
, so we shall be brief.

Lemma 7.1
.

We have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle n-2y<p\leq n-y}\\
{\scriptstyle v|n-p}\end{subarray}}1\ll\frac{y}{\phi(v)}\log{y/v}

for
y\geq 3v,n\geq 2y
.

Proof.

See
[
5
, Theorem 3.7]
.
∎

Lemma 7.2
.

When
(n,d)=1
we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle u\leq E}\\
{\scriptstyle d|u,(u,n)=1}\end{subarray}}\frac{u}{\phi(u)}=CE\frac{\omega(d)}{d}f(n)+O\left((ED)^{\frac{1}{2}}d(n)\phi(d)^{-1}\right)

where

C=\prod_{p}\left(1+\frac{1}{p(p-1)}\right)

and
\omega,f
are multiplicative functions given by

\omega(p^{e})=\frac{p^{2}}{p^{2}-p+1},\quad f(p^{e})=\frac{(p-1)^{2}}{p^{2}-p+1}\,.

Proof.

This is
[
10
, Lemma 3]
.
∎

Proof of Theorem 1.1.
Let
y=\frac{1}{2}n^{\theta}
and
Q=yn^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\log n)^{-2C}
where
C
is as in Theorem 1.2 with
A=4
. We shall estimate

\#\{(p,u,v):n=p+uv,y\leq uv<2y,Q<u\leq 2Q,(u,q)=1\}

in two different ways. We have

N\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle n=k+uv}\\
{\scriptstyle\rm{(7.1)}}\end{subarray}}\lambda(k)

with
\lambda
as in Theorem 1.2 with
n
in place of
x
, where (7.1) denotes the set of conditions

y\leq uv<2y,\quad Q<u\leq 2Q,\quad(u,n)=1.

(7.1)

It is an easy deduction from Theorem 1.2 that

N\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle Q<u\leq 2Q}\\
{\scriptstyle(u,n)=1}\end{subarray}}\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle n-2y<k\leq n-y}\\
{\scriptstyle u|n-k}\end{subarray}}\lambda(k)\gg\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle Q<u\leq 2Q}\\
{\scriptstyle(u,n)=1}\end{subarray}}\frac{y}{\phi(u)\log n}\,.

On taking
d=1
in Lemma 7.2, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle Q<u\leq 2Q}\\
{\scriptstyle(u,n)=1}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\phi(u)}

\displaystyle\gg\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle Q<u\leq 2Q}\\
{\scriptstyle(u,n)=1}\end{subarray}}\frac{u}{\phi(u)}

\displaystyle\gg f(n)+O\left(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}d(n)\right)

\displaystyle\gg f(n),

since
f(n)\gg\phi(n)/n
and
Q\gg n^{0.05}
. It follows that

N\gg\frac{f(n)y}{\log n}\,.

(7.2)

We now take
\delta
to be a small positive constant, and assume there are no solutions of
n=p+ab
for which
1\leq a,b\leq n^{\frac{1}{2}-\delta}
and
ab<2y
, and estimate
N
from above. Using the argument in
[
10
]
, we find that

N\ll\frac{Q}{\log n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle F\leq v<4F}\\
{\scriptstyle(v,nK)=1}\end{subarray}}\frac{v}{\phi(v)}

(7.3)

where
F=\frac{1}{2}yQ^{-1}
and

K=\prod_{q_{1}\leq p<q_{2}}p,\quad q_{1}=2yn^{\delta-\frac{1}{2}}Q^{-1},\quad q_{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}n^{\frac{1}{2}-\delta}Q^{-1}.

Following Heath-Brown’s application of the Selberg sieve method in
[
10
]
, we obtain the bound

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle F\leq v<4F}\\
{\scriptstyle(v,nK)=1}\end{subarray}}\frac{v}{\phi(v)}\leq\frac{3CFf(n)}{G}+O\left(n^{\frac{3}{7}}\right)

(7.4)

where, with

g(m)=\prod_{p|m}(p-\omega(p))^{-1},

we have

G=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle m<n^{\frac{1}{6}}}\\
{\scriptstyle(m,n)=1,m|K}\end{subarray}}\mu^{2}(m)g(m)\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\scriptstyle q_{1}\leq p<n^{\frac{1}{6}}}\\
{\scriptstyle p\nmid n}\end{subarray}}g(p).

Thus

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{q_{1}\leq p<n^{\frac{1}{6}}}\frac{1}{p}-\sum_{p|n}\frac{1}{q_{1}}

\displaystyle=-\log 6+\log\log n-\log\log q_{1}+O\left((\log q_{1})^{-1}+q_{1}^{-1}\log n\right).

Now

\displaystyle q_{1}

\displaystyle=2(\log n)^{2C}n^{\delta},

\displaystyle\log q_{1}

\displaystyle=\delta\log n+O(\log\log n),

\displaystyle\log\log q_{1}

\displaystyle=\log\delta+\log\log n+O\left((\log n)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right).

It follows that

G\geq-\log(6\delta)+O\left((\log n)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\geq-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\delta

(7.5)

if
\delta
is sufficiently small. We now see from (
7.3
), (
7.4
), (
7.5
), that

N\ll\frac{yf(n)}{\log n\log(1/\delta)},

which contradicts (
7.2
) if
\delta
is chosen to be sufficiently small. This completes the proof of Theorem 1.1.
