Quantum formalism on the plane: POVM-Toeplitz quantization, Naimark theorem and linear polarisation of the light
Roberto Beneduci a , Emmanuel Frion b , Jean-Pierre Gazeau c and Amedeo Perri d

Abstract
. We investigate two aspects of the elementary example of POVMs on the Euclidean plane, namely their status as quantum observables and their role as quantizers in the integral quantization procedure. The compatibility of POVMs in the ensuing quantum formalism is discussed, and a Naimark dilation is found for the quantum operators. The relation with Toeplitz quantization is explained. A physical situation is discussed, where we describe the linear polarization of the light with the use of Stokes parameters. In particular, the case of sequential measurements in a real bidimensional Hilbert space is addressed. An interpretation of the Stokes parameters in the framework of unsharp or fuzzy observables is given. Finally, a necessary condition for the compatibility of two dichotomic fuzzy observables which provides a condition for the approximate joint measurement of two incompatible sharp observables is found.

Introduction

Positive operator-valued measures (POVMs) play a central role in the Hilbert space formulation of quantum mechanics. Indeed, observables are represented by positive operator-valued measures, states are represented by positive trace-class operators with trace one and observables and states are in statistical duality (see next section). 2.
General setting

What is an observable? In standard quantum mechanics an observable is represented by a self-adjoint operator but it has been argued
[
54
,
55
,
56
,
57
,
58
,
1
,
59
]
that the choice of self-adjoint operators as representative of observables is too restrictive. It has been suggested that an observable is better represented by a POVM of which the spectral measures are particular examples. Before we give motivation for such a generalization of the concept of observable, let us recall the main definitions and properties of POVMs
[
27
]
. In the following, the set of positive trace-class self-adjoint operators with trace one is denoted by
S(\mathcal{H})
.

2.1.
Definition of a POVM

Definition 2.1
.

A normalized Positive-Operator Valued measure (POVM) is a map
F:\mathcal{B}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{L}^{+}_{s}(\mathcal{H})
from the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of a topological space
\Omega
to the space of linear positive self-adjoint operators such that:

(2.1)

\displaystyle F\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\Delta_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}F(\Delta_{n})\;,

(2.2)

\displaystyle F(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\;,

where
\{\Delta_{n}\}
is a countable family of disjoint sets in
\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
and the series converges in the weak operator topology. The POVM is said to be real if
\Omega=\mathbb{R}
. A projection-valued measure (PVM) is a POVM such that
F(\Delta)
is a projection operator for every
\Delta\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
.

Spectral measures are real PVMs. By the spectral theorem, spectral measures are in a one-to-one correspondence with self-adjoint operators. Therefore, if we use POVMs to represent observables, the set of observables represented by self-adjoint operators is just a subset of the set of observables.

The motivation for such an extension is rooted in the statistical nature of the measurement process. It is well known that by repeating the measurement
M
of a system
S
in the state
\rho\in S(\mathcal{H})
we, in general, obtain different results (that we suppose contained in a topological space
\Omega
). On the other hand, by repeating the measurement
M
several times, we observe a statistical regularity, which is encoded in the statistical distribution
f^{M}_{\rho}
of the measurement outcomes, and depends only on the state of the system. In other words, we have a map
\rho\mapsto f^{M}_{\rho}
ensuring the existence of regularities of a statistical nature. Note that this is a necessary condition for the existence of the scientific enterprise. In its turn, the statistical distribution
f_{\rho}^{M}
is represented by a probability measure
\mu^{M}_{\rho}:\mathcal{B}(\Omega)\to[0,1]
. At this point of our argument, it is better to specify that the state of the system is defined by the procedure we followed to prepare the system before the measurement, and to recall that the space
S(\mathcal{H})
of the states of a given system is a convex space (this corresponds to the existence of mixed states). For example, a filtering operation ensures that if a beam of electrons passes through the filter, the electrons in the beam must immediately afterward have positions within a restricted range of values.

The previous analysis can be summarized by saying that the measurement process
M
defines a map from “states” to “probability measures,”
\rho\mapsto\mu^{M}_{\rho}
. Moreover, if the state is given by a convex combination of states,
\rho=t\rho_{1}+(1-t)\rho_{2}
, the measurement outcomes are given by a convex combination of probability measures,
t\mu^{M}_{1}+(1-t)\mu^{M}_{2}
. In mathematical terms, the map
\rho\mapsto\mu^{M}_{\rho}
is affine. Note that the previous argument is of a general nature. It applies both to classical and quantum formalism (in mechanics, optics, …). In the classical case, for example, the states are represented by probability measures on the phase space
\Gamma
, and the measurements are affine maps that transform probability measures on
\Gamma
into probability measures on the space of possible outcomes of the measurement process,
\Omega

[
56
]
. Actually, the scheme we just described applies to any physical model where the states of the system define a convex set (see
[
1
]
chapter 23). In order to introduce the concept of observable in this scheme, it is sufficient to note that different measurements can correspond to the same affine map. In this case, we say that the measurements are equivalent and divide the set of measurement into equivalence classes. An observable is an equivalent class of measurements. The same can happen with states: two states
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
are said to be equivalent if they cannot be separated by the set of measurements,
i.e.
, if
\mu_{1}^{M}=\mu_{2}^{M}
for every measurement
M
.

Now, let us focus on the particular case of the quantum framework where, to each system, a complex and separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is associated and the states are represented by density operators,
i.e.
positive, bounded self-adjoint operators with trace
1
. We have the following characterization of the measurement process:

Theorem 2.1

(Holevo,
[
56
]
)
.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between POVMs
F:\mathcal{B}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{L_{s}^{+}(H)}
and affine maps
S(\mathcal{H})\mapsto\mathcal{M}_{+}(\Omega)
from states to probability measures which is given by
\mu(\Delta)=Tr(\rho F(\Delta))
,
\Delta\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
,
\rho\in S(\mathcal{H})
,
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(\Omega)
.

Note that equivalent measurements are represented by the same POVM and this establishes a one-to-one map between observables and POVMs. Moreover,
\mu_{\rho}^{M}=Tr[F(\Delta)\rho]
establishes a statistical duality between observables (POVMs) and states (density operators)
[
1
]
.

2.2.
Naimark theorem and compatibility of POVMs

In the previous section, we have seen that observables are represented by POVMs and that observables represented by spectral measures (self-adjoint operators) define a subset of the space of observables. Now, we recall Naimark’s dilation theorem which ensures that every POVM is the projection of a projector-valued measure defined in an extended Hilbert space.

Theorem 2.2

(Naimark,
[
60
]
)
.

Let
F:\mathcal{B}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{L}^{+}_{s}(\mathcal{H})
be a POVM in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Then, there is an extended Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{+}
and a projector-valued (PV) measure
E^{+}:\mathcal{B}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{L}_{s}(\mathcal{H}^{+})
such that

PE^{+}(\Delta)\psi=F(\Delta)\psi,\quad\psi\in\mathcal{H},\quad\Delta\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega)\,,

where
P
is the projection operator onto
\mathcal{H}
.

Naimark’s theorem provides a necessary and sufficient condition for the compatibility of two POVMs.

Definition 2.2
.

Two POVMs
F_{1}:\mathcal{B}(\Omega_{1})\to\mathcal{L}^{+}_{s}(\mathcal{H})
and
F_{2}:\mathcal{B}(\Omega_{2})\to\mathcal{L}^{+}_{s}(\mathcal{H})
are compatible if there is a third POVM
F:\mathcal{B}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})\to\mathcal{L}^{+}_{s}(\mathcal{H})
of which
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are the marginals, i.e.,

F_{1}(\Delta_{1})=F(\Delta_{1}\times\Omega_{2}),\quad F_{2}(\Delta_{2})=F(\Omega_{1}\times\Delta_{2}).

We recall that the symbol
\mathcal{B}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})
denotes the product
\sigma
-algebra generated by the family of sets
\{\Delta_{1}\times\Delta_{2}\,\,:\,\,\Delta_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega_{1}),\,\Delta_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega_{2})\}
.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
27
]
)
.

Two POVMs
F_{1}:\mathcal{B}(\Omega_{1})\to\mathcal{L}^{+}_{s}(\mathcal{H})
and
F_{2}:\mathcal{B}(\Omega_{2})\to\mathcal{L}^{+}_{s}(\mathcal{H})
are compatible if and only if there are two Naimark extensions
E^{+}_{1}:\mathcal{B}(\Omega_{1})\to\mathcal{L}_{s}(\mathcal{H}^{+})
and
E^{+}_{2}:\mathcal{B}(\Omega_{2})\to\mathcal{L}_{s}(\mathcal{H}^{+})
such that
[E^{+}_{1},E^{+}_{2}]=0
.

3.
An outline of integral quantization and semi-classical portraits

In this section, we recall the fundamental role played by POVMs as quantizers of classical models of physical systems,
i.e.
in the building of self-adjoint operators and their subsequent PV measures
[
29
,
61
]
.

3.1.
Quantization map

To define a quantization procedure for functions defined on a set
X
(
e.g.
a phase space), the map from elements in a “classical” vector space to operators in some Hilbert space must fulfill basic requirements. A minimal program should meet the four following criteria
[
62
,
63
]
:

(1)

Linearity
. Quantization is a linear map
f\mapsto A_{f}
:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}:\mathcal{C}(X)\mapsto\mathcal{A}({\mathcal{H}})\,,\qquad\mathfrak{Q}(f)=A_{f}\,,

where

•

\mathcal{C}(X)
is a vector space of complex or real-valued functions
f(x)

on a set
X
,
i.e.
a “classical” mathematical model,

•

\mathcal{A}({\mathcal{H}})
is a vector space of linear operators in some real or complex Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
,
i.e.
, a “quantum” mathematical model, notwithstanding the question of common domains in the case of unbounded operators.

(2)

Unity
. The map (
3.1
) is such that the function
f=1
is mapped to the identity operator
\mathbbm{1}
on
{\mathcal{H}}
.

(3)

Reality
. A real function
f
is mapped to a self-adjoint operator
A_{f}
in
{\mathcal{H}}
or, at least, a symmetric operator (in the infinite-dimensional case).

(4)

Covariance
. Defining the action of a symmetry group G on X by
\left(g,x\right)\in G\times X
such as
\left(g,x\right)\mapsto g\cdot x\in X
, there is a unitary representation
U
of
G
such that
A_{T(g)f}=U(g)A_{f}U(g^{-1})
, with
(T(g)f)(x)=f\left(g^{-1}\cdot x\right)
.

In the remainder of this paper, we suppose
{\mathcal{H}}
be a finite Hilbert space. Therefore, the quantized objects
A_{f}
are matrices
[
64
]
. Note that the previous requirements are minimal. In this work, we make use of POVMs to define the quantization procedure, and it is therefore necessary to consider additional minimal structures, namely a measure
\nu
on
X
, the
\sigma
-algebra defined in (
2.1
), and local compactness. POVMs can be used in conjunction with integral quantization to fulfill the first three above requirements. However, covariance needs some additional structure
[
65
,
61
]
. In particular, Euclidean POVMs provide covariance, as we will see in section
5.2
.

The integral quantization of a function
f(x)
on the measure space
(X,\nu)
is defined by the linear map

(3.2)

f\mapsto A_{f}=\int_{X}\,{\sf M}(x)\,f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,,

where the family of matrices
{\sf M}(x)
solves the identity as

(3.3)

X\ni x\mapsto\mathsf{M}(x)\,,\quad\int_{X}\,{\sf M}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\mathbbm{1}\,.

If the matrices
{\sf M}(x)
are non-negative, the quantum operator related to the characteristic function on
\Delta
,
A(\chi_{\Delta})
, defines a POVM . Indeed, the restriction to
\Delta
of the quantization map (
3.2
)

(3.4)

\displaystyle F(\Delta):=A(\chi_{\Delta})=\int_{X}\,{\sf M}(x)\,\chi_{\Delta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\int_{\Delta}\,{\sf M}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\;,

solves the identity (
2.2
) by definition. Therefore, we have shown two key roles of POVMs; they are the mathematical representatives of observables and they provide a quantization procedure.

3.2.
Semi-classical portraits

A quantum model for a system must have a classical or semi-classical counterpart. We say there exists a
dequantization
map acting on an operator
A_{f}
to give back the original
f(x)
, in general with some corrections. This procedure gives a
semi-classical portrait
of the original function
f
, often denoted
\check{f}(x)
(Husimi and Wigner functions
[
66
]
, lower (Lieb)
[
67
]
or covariant (Berezin)
[
68
]
symbols). Given two families
{\sf M}_{\mathrm{a}}(x)
(for “analysis”) and
{\sf M}_{\mathrm{r}}(x)
(for “reconstruction") resolving the identity in the sense of (
3.3
),
we here generalize the definition of these lower symbols in the following way:

(3.5)

A_{f}\mapsto\check{f}(x):=\mathrm{tr}(\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{r}}(x)\,A_{f})=\int_{X}\,\mathrm{tr}(\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{r}}(x){\sf M}_{\mathrm{a}}(x^{\prime}))\,f(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x^{\prime})\,,

Choosing
\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{a}}(x)=\rho_{\mathrm{a}}(x)
and
\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{r}}(x)=\rho_{\mathrm{r}}(x)
to be density matrices, the map
x^{\prime}\mapsto\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{\mathrm{r}}(x)\rho_{\mathrm{a}}(x^{\prime}))
defines a probability
distribution
\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{\mathrm{r}}(x)\rho_{\mathrm{a}}(x^{\prime}))
on the measure space
(X,\mathrm{d}\nu(x^{\prime}))
, as it is easily proved by multiplying the resolution of the unity (
3.3
) with
\rho_{\mathrm{r}}(x)
and tracing the result. Thus, the expectation value of the operator
A_{f}
,
\check{f}(x)
, is built from the analysis POVMs based on the
\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{a}}(x)
’s and given by:

(3.6)

f(x)\mapsto\check{f}(x)=\int_{X}f(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{\mathrm{r}}(x)\rho_{\mathrm{a}}(x^{\prime}))\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x^{\prime})\,.

The map (
3.6
) represents in general a regularization of the original, possibly extremely singular,
f
. Standard cases are for
\rho_{\mathrm{a}}=\rho_{\mathrm{r}}
, in particular for rank-one density matrices (coherent states).

3.3.
POVMs and quantum measurement

We base the content of this section on
[
61
]
, in which the analogy of the present formalism with quantum measurement was discussed.
In quantum mechanics, a physically relevant
A_{f}
is a self-adjoint operator whose expectation value is the “unsharp” representation
[
56
,
1
]

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left(\rho_{m}A_{f}\right)=\int_{X}f(x)\,\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{m}\rho(x))\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,.

In compliance with the presentation given in Section
2.1
, the operator

\rho_{m}=\sum_{i}q_{i}|\kern 1.29167pt\phi_{i}\kern 1.29167pt\rangle\langle\kern 1.29167pt\phi_{i}\kern 1.29167pt|

is here the density matrix describing the mixing of the pure states
|\kern 1.29167pt\phi_{i}\kern 1.29167pt\rangle
, each associated with the outcome probability
q_{i}
. On the other hand, the expectation value (
3.7
) of any quantum observable stems from the probability distribution
\mathrm{tr}\left(\rho_{m}\rho(x)\right)
. We note that, unlike in (
3.6
),
\rho_{m}
does not depend on any variable. Since the family of density operators used to construct our quantization procedure can provide the choice
\rho_{m}=\rho_{r}(x_{0})
for some
x_{0}
, we obtain a quantum measurement based on the pair of POVMs
\left(\rho_{m},\rho(x)\right)
, which is equivalent to the pair
\left(\rho_{r},\rho_{a}\right)
introduced in (
3.6
). We also note that the unsharp representation can be associated with the “sharp” representation

(3.8)

\mathrm{tr}\left(\rho_{m}A_{f}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lambda\,\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{m}\,\mathrm{d}E_{f}(\lambda))\;,

coming from the self-adjoint operator
A_{f}
spectral decomposition

(3.9)

A_{f}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lambda\,\mathrm{d}E_{f}(\lambda)\;,

with spectral measure
\mathrm{d}E_{f}(\lambda)
. While the sharp representation is directly associated to the PV spectral measure
E_{f}
, a family
\rho(x)
resolving the identity is needed in the unsharp representation. However, classical and quantum spectra can be considered identical, regardless of the differences between their PV measure and POVMs. Finally, we observe that both the quantum operator
A_{f}
and its semi-classical counterpart (
3.6
) involve probability distributions with respect to some POVMs. Therefore, quantifying an object and measuring it are done through a sole procedure.

4.
Integral quantization, Toeplitz quantization and Naimark dilation

The so-called Berezin-Klauder-Toeplitz quantization (see
[
69
]
for a clear presentation of it
1
1

1

We stress that the quantization procedure presented in
[
69
]
is based on group action while our presentation does not.
), and in particular coherent state (CS) quantization, is one kind of integral quantization. In this section, we describe their relationship, which will allow us to find the Naimark dilation of our quantum operators.

The construction of family of operators solving the identity in (
3.3
) in the CS case is implemented along the following scheme. Let us equip the set
X
with another measure
\mu
and consider the Hilbert space
L^{2}(X,\mathrm{d}\mu(x))
of complex-valued functions on
X
which are Lebesgue square integrable with respect to
\mu
. One then chooses an orthonormal set

\mathcal{O}
of functions
\phi_{i}(x)
(set aside the question of evaluation map in their respective equivalence classes), satisfying the finiteness and positiveness conditions

(4.1)

0<\mathcal{N}(x)=\sum_{i}|\phi_{i}(x)|^{2}<\infty\quad(\mathrm{a.e.})\,,

and a “companion” Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
(the space of quantum states) with orthonormal basis
\{|e_{i}\rangle\}
in one-to-one correspondence
\{|e_{i}\rangle\leftrightarrow\phi_{i}\}
with the elements of
\mathcal{O}
. There results a family
\mathcal{C}
of unit vectors
|x\rangle
in
\mathcal{H}
, named, in a generalised sense, coherent states (CS). These vectors are labelled by elements of
X
and resolve the identity operator in
\mathcal{H}
:

(4.2)

X\ni x\mapsto|x\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}(x)}}\sum_{i}\overline{\phi_{i}(x)}|e_{i}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}\,.

(4.3)

\langle x|x\rangle=1\,,\quad\int_{X}\,|x\rangle\langle x|\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}}\,,\quad\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\mathcal{N}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,.

We now explain the meaning of the CS quantization within this framework. Let us denote by
\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}}
the orthogonal projector mapping
L^{2}(X,\mathrm{d}\mu(x))
onto the sub-Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{o}}
, which is the closure of the linear span of the elements of
\mathcal{O}
. Clearly,
\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}}
is the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{o}}
. Now, we identify
\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{o}}\equiv\mathcal{H}
through a unitary map
U_{\mathfrak{o}}
such that we have

(4.4)

U_{\mathfrak{o}}|e_{i}\rangle=\phi_{i}\,.

Let
f(x)
be a function defining the multiplication operator in
L^{2}(X,\mathrm{d}\mu(x))
:

(4.5)

M_{f}:\phi\mapsto f\,\phi\,.

Proposition 4.1
.

(Toeplitz quantization)
We have the identity

(4.6)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}}M_{f}\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}}\phi_{i}=U_{\mathfrak{o}}A_{f}U^{-1}_{\mathfrak{o}}\phi_{i}\;,\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{N}\;,

where
A_{f}
is the CS quantisation of
f
as

(4.7)

A_{f}=\int_{X}\,f(x)\,|x\rangle\langle x|\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,.

Proof.

We will compare the respective matrix elements of the left- and right-hand sides of (
4.6
) with respect to the basis
\{|e_{i}\rangle\}
of
\mathcal{H}
. Let us start with the action of
U_{\mathfrak{o}}A_{f}U^{-1}_{\mathfrak{o}}
on
\phi_{i}
:

\displaystyle U_{\mathfrak{o}}A_{f}U^{-1}_{\mathfrak{o}}\phi_{i}

\displaystyle=U_{\mathfrak{o}}A_{f}|e_{i}\rangle=U_{\mathfrak{o}}\int_{X}\,f(x)\,|x\rangle\langle x|e_{i}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)

\displaystyle=U_{\mathfrak{o}}\sum_{jk}\int_{X}\,f(x)\,\overline{\phi_{j}}(x)\,\phi_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,|e_{j}\rangle\langle e_{k}|e_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=U_{\mathfrak{o}}\sum_{j}\int_{X}\,f(x)\,\overline{\phi_{j}}(x)\,\phi_{i}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,|e_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(A_{f}\right)_{ji}\,U_{\mathfrak{o}}|e_{j}\rangle=\sum_{j}\left(A_{f}\right)_{ji}\,\phi_{j}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}}M_{f}\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}}\phi_{i}=\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}}M_{f}\phi_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(\phi_{j},f\phi_{i}\right)_{L^{2}}\,\phi_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\phi_{j}\,\int_{X}\overline{\phi_{j}}(x)\,f(x)\,\phi_{i}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(A_{f}\right)_{ji}\,\phi_{j}\,.

\square

Note that
M_{\Delta}:=M_{\chi_{\Delta}}
defines a projection-valued measure such that
M_{f}=\int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}M_{x}
. Indeed, let
g\in L^{2}(X,\mathrm{d}\mu(x))
and
\mu_{g}(\cdot):=\int_{(\cdot)}|g(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\left(g|M_{\chi_{(\cdot)}}g\right)_{L^{2}}
be the corresponding measure of which
|g|^{2}
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative (
\mathrm{d}\mu_{g}(x)=|g(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)
). Then, for every
g\in L^{2}(X,\mathrm{d}\mu(x))
,

\displaystyle\int f(y)\,\mathrm{d}\left(g|M_{\chi_{(\cdot)}}g\right)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int f(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{g}(y)=\int f(y)|g(y)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)

\displaystyle=\left(g|fg\right)_{L^{2}}=\left(g|M_{f}g\right)_{L^{2}}\,.

where
\mathrm{d}\left(g|M_{\chi_{(\cdot)}}g\right)_{L^{2}}
denotes integration with respect to the measure
\left(g|M_{(\cdot)}g\right)_{L^{2}}
.
Moreover,
M_{(\cdot)}:=

M_{\chi_{(\cdot)}}
is a Naimark dilation of the POVM
U_{\mathfrak{o}}A_{\Delta}U^{-1}_{\mathfrak{o}}:=

U_{\mathfrak{o}}A_{\chi_{\Delta}}U^{-1}_{\mathfrak{o}}=\int_{\Delta}U_{\mathfrak{o}}|x\rangle\langle x|U^{-1}_{\mathfrak{o}}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)
since, by proposition
4.1
,
\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}}M_{\chi_{\Delta}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}}\phi_{i}=U_{\mathfrak{o}}A_{\chi_{\Delta}}U^{-1}_{\mathfrak{o}}\phi_{i}
.

In other words, and with slight abuse of language, every operator
A_{f}=\int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}A_{x}
is the projection of the operator
M_{f}=\int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}M_{x}
where
\mathrm{d}A_{x}
denotes integration with respect to the POVM
A_{\chi_{\Delta}}
and
\mathrm{d}M_{x}
denotes integration with respect to the PV measure
M_{\chi_{\Delta}}
. In its turn,
M_{\chi_{\Delta}}
is the Naimark’s dilation of
A_{\chi_{\Delta}}
.

An alternative construction of families of
X
-labelled coherent states rests upon unitary transports of one unit-norm state
|\psi\rangle
picked in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Let
\{|e_{i}\rangle\}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
and let
U(x)
be a family of
X
-labelled unitary operators on
\mathcal{H}
which obey square-integrability and orthonormality on
(X,\nu)
in the sense given in the following.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\psi
be arbitrarily picked in
\mathcal{H}
. The family of
X
-labelled unit-norm states in
\mathcal{H}
defined by

(4.8)

X\ni x\mapsto|x\rangle_{\psi}:=U(x)|\psi\rangle\,,

solves the identity in
\mathcal{H}
with respect to the measure
\nu
on
X
,

(4.9)

\int_{X}|x\rangle_{\psi}{}_{\psi}\langle x|\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\mathbbm{1}\,,

if and only if the
X
-labelled family of unitary operators
U(x)
obeys square-integrability and orthonormality on
(X,\nu)
in the following sense

(4.10)

\displaystyle\delta_{ik}

\displaystyle=\int_{X}U_{ji}(x)\,\overline{U_{jk}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\quad\forall\,j\,,

(4.11)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\left[U_{ji}(x)\,\overline{U_{lk}}(x)+U_{li}(x)\,\overline{U_{jk}}(x)\right]\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\quad\forall\,j,l\,,\ j\neq l\,,

where
U_{ij}(x):=\langle e_{i}|U(x)|e_{j}\rangle
.

Proof.

By expanding
\psi=\sum_{i}a_{i}\,|e_{i}\rangle
, we have,

|x\rangle_{\psi}=U(x)|\psi\rangle=\sum_{i}a_{i}\,U(x)\,|e_{i}\rangle=\sum_{ij}a_{i}\,U_{ji}(x)\,|e_{j}\rangle\,,

and so

\displaystyle|x\rangle_{\psi}{}_{\psi}\langle x|

\displaystyle=\sum_{ijkl}a_{i}\bar{a}_{k}\,U_{ji}(x)\,\overline{U_{lk}}(x)\,|e_{j}\rangle\langle e_{l}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{ik}a_{i}\bar{a}_{k}\,\sum_{j}U_{ji}(x)\,\overline{U_{jk}}(x)\,|e_{j}\rangle\langle e_{j}|+\sum_{ik}a_{i}\bar{a}_{k}\,\sum_{j\neq l}U_{ji}(x)\,\overline{U_{lk}}(x)\,|e_{j}\rangle\langle e_{l}|\,.

We now integrate the previous expression and impose (
4.9
). From the completeness of the basis,
\sum_{j}|e_{j}\rangle\langle e_{j}|=\mathbbm{1}
, and linear independence of the operators
|e_{j}\rangle\langle e_{l}|
, we infer

(4.12)

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\sum_{ik}a_{i}\bar{a}_{k}\,\int_{X}U_{ji}(x)\,\overline{U_{jk}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\quad\forall\,j\,,

(4.13)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{ik}a_{i}\bar{a}_{k}\,\int_{X}U_{ji}(x)\,\overline{U_{lk}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\quad\forall\,j,l,\ j\neq l\,,

for any unit-norm complex sequence
(a_{i})\in\ell^{2}
. Let us consider the two operators
\mathrm{M}^{j}
and
\mathrm{M}^{j,l}
,
j\neq l
, with matrix elements with respect to the basis
\{e_{i}\}
,

(4.14)

\mathrm{M}^{j}_{ki}=\int_{X}U_{ji}(x)\,\overline{U_{jk}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,,\quad\mathrm{M}^{j,l}_{ki}=\int_{X}U_{ji}(x)\,\overline{U_{lk}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,.

The operator
\mathrm{M}^{j}
is Hermitian and the condition (
4.12
) means that its mean value obeys
\langle\psi|\mathrm{M}^{j}|\psi\rangle=1
for any normalised
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
. There results that
\mathrm{M}^{j}=\mathbbm{1}
, and so

\int_{X}U_{ji}(x)\,\overline{U_{jk}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\delta_{ik}\quad\forall\,j\,.

The operator
\mathrm{M}^{j,l}
obeys
\left(\mathrm{M}^{j,l}\right)^{{\dagger}}=\overline{\mathrm{M}}^{l,j}
, and so the operator
\mathrm{M}^{j,l}+\mathrm{M}^{l,j}
is Hermitian. The condition (
4.13
) means that its mean value obeys
\langle\psi|\mathrm{M}^{j,l}+\mathrm{M}^{l,j}|\psi\rangle=0
for any normalised
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
. There results that
\mathrm{M}^{j,l}+\mathrm{M}^{l,j}=\mathbb{0}
, and so

\int_{X}\left[U_{ji}(x)\,\overline{U_{lk}}(x)+U_{li}(x)\,\overline{U_{jk}}(x)\right]\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=0\quad\forall\,j,l\,,\ j\neq l\,.

\square

The above result extends to density operators.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\rho
be a density operator on
\mathcal{H}
, and
\rho(x):=U(x)\,\rho\,U^{{\dagger}}(x)
its unitary transport
U(x)
which has square-integrability and orthonormality properties (
4.10
) and (
4.11
). We then have the resolution of the identity:

(4.15)

\int_{X}\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\mathbbm{1}\,.

Proof.

From the decomposition of
\rho
in a sum of rank-one projectors,

\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\,|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|\,,\quad\|\psi_{i}\|^{2}=1\,\quad\sum_{i}p_{i}=1\,,\quad 0\leq p_{i}\leq 1\,,

we have

\rho(x)=\sum_{i}p_{i}\,|x\rangle_{\psi_{i}}{}_{\psi_{i}}\langle x|\,.

By applying (
4.9
) to each CS projectors in this sum, we obtain

\int_{X}\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\sum_{i}p_{i}\,\mathbbm{1}=\mathbbm{1}\,.

\square

Let us now understand the Toeplitz quantization within this alternative approach. Implementing the construction (
4.8
) with the choice
\psi=e_{j}

and

U^{{\dagger}}(x)
, we obtain

(4.16)

|x\rangle_{e_{j}}=\sum_{i}\overline{U_{ji}}(x)\,|e_{i}\rangle\,.

We denote by
\mathcal{O}_{j}=\{U_{ji}\}
the orthonormal system in
L^{2}(X,\mathrm{d}\nu(x))
formed by the functions
U_{ji}(x)
at fixed
j
according to the condition (
4.10
), and by
\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}_{j}}
the corresponding orthogonal projector mapping
L^{2}(X,\mathrm{d}\nu(x))
onto the sub-Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{o}_{j}}
which is the closure of the linear span of the elements of
\mathcal{O}_{j}
. With the identification (
4.4
),
i.e.

U_{\mathfrak{o}_{j}}|e_{i}\rangle=U_{ji}
, relation (
4.6
) holds under the form,

(4.17)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}_{j}}M_{f}\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}_{j}}U_{ji}=U_{\mathfrak{o}_{j}}A^{(j)}_{f}U^{-1}_{\mathfrak{o}_{j}}U_{ji}\,,\quad A^{(j)}_{f}=\int_{X}f(x)\,|x\rangle_{e_{j}}{}_{e_{j}}\langle x|\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\,.

We now extend this result to the integral quantization with a density operator
\rho
on
\mathcal{H}
. We choose the orthonormal basis
\{|e_{i}\rangle\}
of the latter as made of the eigenstates of
\rho
(with possible null eigenvalue),

(4.18)

\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\,|e_{i}\rangle\langle e_{i}|\,,\quad\sum_{i}p_{i}=1\,,\quad 0\leq p_{i}\leq 1\,.

Clearly we have,

(4.19)

A^{(\rho)}_{f}=\int_{X}f(x)\,\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\sum_{j}p_{j}\int_{X}f(x)\,|x\rangle_{e_{j}}{}_{e_{j}}\langle x|\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\sum_{j}p_{j}A^{(j)}_{f}\,.

By linearity, from (
4.17
) we infer

(4.20)

\sum_{j}p_{j}\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}_{j}}M_{f}\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}_{j}}=\sum_{j}p_{j}U_{\mathfrak{o}_{j}}A^{(j)}_{f}U^{-1}_{\mathfrak{o}_{j}}\,.

Let us complete (
4.10
) by imposing the full orthonormality constraint on the matrix elements
U_{ij}(x)
:

(4.21)

\displaystyle\delta_{jl}\delta_{ik}

\displaystyle=\int_{X}U_{ji}(x)\,\overline{U_{lk}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\quad\forall\,i,j,k,l\,.

It follows that
\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}_{j}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}_{l}}=0

\forall j\neq l
. Moreover, (
4.19
) becomes the direct sum

(4.22)

A^{(\rho)}_{f}=\bigoplus_{j}p_{j}A^{(j)}_{f}\,.

Defining the direct sum of unitary operators
U_{\mathfrak{o}}=\bigoplus_{j}U_{\mathfrak{o}_{j}}
we write (
4.20
) as

(4.23)

\bigoplus_{j}p_{j}\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}_{j}}M_{f}\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{o}_{j}}=\bigoplus_{j}p_{j}U_{\mathfrak{o}_{j}}A^{(j)}_{f}U_{\mathfrak{o}_{j}}^{-1}=U_{\mathfrak{o}}A^{(\rho)}_{f}U^{-1}_{\mathfrak{o}}

where we recover an analogous result about Naimark dilation in the case of density matrices.

5.
Integral POVM quantization of fonctions on the circle

Let us now describe the integral quantization for building observables acting on the two-dimensional real Hilbert space,
i.e.
, the Euclidean plane. In this section, we give an overview of the material developed in
[
65
]
on the integral quantization of functions on the unit circle. We describe rays in the Euclidean plane, and real POVMs built from density matrices in dimension 2. We then discuss the integral quantization procedure based on the group of rotations SO
(2)
.

5.1.
Euclidean plane with Dirac notations

First, a quantum state can be decomposed into a sum of pure states, each identified with a unit vector. In the specific case of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the polar angle
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
is associated with the unit vector
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\phi}
. This unit vector corresponds to the pure state
|\phi\rangle:=\left|\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\phi}\right\rangle
. In particular, the two axes of the Euclidean plane can be described as a “horizontal orientation" state
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\imath}}=|0\rangle
and a “vertical orientation" state
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}=\left|\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right\rangle
. The plane is depicted in figure
1
.

Figure 1.
The Euclidean plane and its unit vectors viewed as pure quantum states in Dirac ket notations.

Therefore, a pure state in the horizontal-vertical representation can be decomposed as

(5.1)

|\phi\rangle=\cos{\phi}\,|0\rangle+\sin{\phi}\,\left|\frac{\pi}{2}\right\rangle\,,\quad\langle 0|\phi\rangle=\cos\phi\,,\qquad\left\langle\frac{\pi}{2}\right|\phi\rangle=\sin\phi\,,

with the associated probability distributions
\phi\mapsto\cos^{2}\phi
and
\phi\mapsto\sin^{2}\phi
on the unit circle equipped with the measure
\mathrm{d}\phi/\pi
.
One also have the unsharp
unit circle
representation of a pure state given by the trigonometric function

(5.2)

\langle\eta|\phi\rangle=\cos(\phi-\eta)\,.

with associated probability distribution families

(5.3)

\eta\mapsto\mathcal{P}_{\phi}(\eta)=|\langle\eta|\phi\rangle|^{2}=\cos^{2}(\phi-\eta)\,,\quad\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathcal{P}_{\phi}(\eta)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\eta}{\pi}=1\,.

To
|\phi\rangle
corresponds the orthogonal projector
E_{\phi}=|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|
(also called pure state),

(5.4)

\displaystyle E_{\phi}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos{\phi}\\
\sin{\phi}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos{\phi}&\sin{\phi}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos^{2}{\phi}&\cos{\phi}\sin{\phi}\\
\cos{\phi}\sin{\phi}&\sin^{2}{\phi}\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{R}(\phi)|0\rangle\langle 0|\mathcal{R}(-\phi)\,,

where
\mathcal{R}(\phi)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\phi&-\sin\phi\\
\sin\phi&\cos\phi\end{pmatrix}
.
In contrast to pure states the mixed states are defined by two-dimensional density matrices of the form

(5.5)

\rho:=\mathsf{M}(a,b)=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
b&1-a\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad 0\leq a\leq 1\,,\quad\Delta:=\det\rho=a(1-a)-b^{2}\geq 0\,.

From the spectral decomposition of
\rho

(5.6)

\rho=\lambda E_{\phi}+(1-\lambda)E_{\phi+\pi/2}\,,

we only have to consider the upper half-plane,
i.e.
,
0\leq\phi\leq\pi
. The highest eigenvalue of
\rho
is
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\Delta})
, such that
1/2\leq\lambda\leq 1
.
Defining
r:=2\lambda-1
,
0\leq r\leq 1
,
a real density matrix takes the form

(5.7)

\displaystyle\rho=\left(\frac{1+r}{2}\right)E_{\phi}+\left(\frac{1-r}{2}\right)E_{\phi+\pi/2}\;,

and, in polar coordinates
(r,\phi)
, we obtain

(5.8)

\rho\equiv\rho_{r,\phi}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}+\frac{r}{2}\mathcal{R}(\phi)\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\mathcal{R}(-\phi)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}+\frac{r}{2}\cos 2\phi&\frac{r}{2}\sin 2\phi\\
\frac{r}{2}\sin 2\phi&\frac{1}{2}-\frac{r}{2}\cos 2\phi\end{pmatrix}\,.

In particular, we retrieve the projector on the unit vector,
\rho_{1,\phi}=E_{\phi}
.

Note that the parameter
r
encodes the distance of
\rho
to the pure state
E_{\phi}
while
1-r
measures the degree of “mixing"
[
65
]
. Its statistical interpretation is given by the von Neumann entropy defined as

(5.9)

S_{\rho}:=-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\,\ln\rho)=-\lambda\ln\lambda-(1-\lambda)\ln(1-\lambda)=-\frac{1+r}{2}\ln\frac{1+r}{2}-\frac{1-r}{2}\ln\frac{1-r}{2}\,.

As a function of
\lambda\in[1/2,1]
,
S_{\rho}
is non-negative, concave and symmetric with respect to its maximum value
\log 2
at
\lambda=1/2
, which corresponds to
r=0
,
i.e.
,
\rho_{0}\equiv\mathbbm{1}/2
, which describes the state of completely random orientations.

5.2.
Integral POVM quantization

The measure space
(X,\mathrm{d}\nu(x))
for the Euclidean plane is the unit circle with its uniform (Lebesgue) measure:

(5.10)

X=\mathbb{S}^{1}\,,\quad\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\pi}\,,\quad\phi\in[0,2\pi)\,.

\mathbb{S}^{1}
is here viewed as the abelian group SO
(2)
of rotations in the plane. As a consequence, the density operator (
5.8
) is manifestly covariant under rotation

(5.11)

\rho_{r,\phi_{0}}(\phi)=\mathcal{R}\left(\phi\right)\rho_{r,\phi_{0}}\mathcal{R}\left(-\phi\right)=\rho_{r,\phi_{0}+\phi}\,,\quad 0\leq\phi<2\pi\,.

Additionally, one easily proves by elementary integration on matrix elements that this family solves the identity

(5.12)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\rho_{r,\phi+\phi_{0}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\pi}=\mathbbm{1}\,.

This property allows to apply the procedure (
3.2
) to quantize a function (or distribution)
f(\phi)
on the circle. Its quantum counterpart is the 2
\times
2 matrix operator

(5.13)

\begin{split}f\mapsto A_{f}&=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(\phi)\rho_{r,\phi+\phi_{0}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\pi}=\begin{pmatrix}\langle f\rangle+\frac{r}{2}C_{c}\left(R_{\phi_{0}}f\right)&\frac{r}{2}C_{s}\left(R_{\phi_{0}}f\right)\\
\frac{r}{2}C_{s}\left(R_{\phi_{0}}f\right)&\langle f\rangle-\frac{r}{2}C_{c}\left(R_{\phi_{0}}f\right)\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\langle f\rangle\,I+\frac{r}{2}\left[C_{c}\left(R_{\phi_{0}}f\right)\,\sigma_{3}+C_{s}\left(R_{\phi_{0}}f\right)\,\sigma_{1}\right]\,,\end{split}

where
\langle f\rangle:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(\phi)\,\mathrm{d}\phi
is the average of
f
on the unit circle and
R_{\phi_{0}}(f)(\phi):=f(\phi-\phi_{0})
. The symbols
C_{c}
and
C_{s}
are for the cosine and sine doubled angle Fourier coefficients of
f
,

(5.14)

C_{c}(f)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(\phi)\cos 2\phi\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\pi}\,,\quad C_{s}(f)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(\phi)\sin 2\phi\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\pi}\,.

We observe that the quantisation of the components
(\cos(\phi-\phi_{0}),\sin(\phi-\phi_{0}))
of the density matrix eigenvectors give
0
. Let us denote by
N_{\phi_{0}}
the space of functions or distributions (in a certain sense)
T(\phi)
on the circle which obey

(5.15)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}T(\phi)\rho_{r,\phi+\phi_{0}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\pi}=0\,.

Such a space includes the orthogonal in
L_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathrm{d}\phi/\pi)
to the
3
-dimensional subspace spanned by the functions
1
,
\cos 2\phi
, and
\sin 2\phi
. Thus, the operator
A_{f}
is the
\rho_{r,\phi+\phi_{0}}
-quantization of
f
modulo any element of
N_{\phi_{0}}
. Actually, by identifying the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the real span
V_{3}
of the orthonormal set in
L_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathrm{d}\phi/\pi)
,

(5.16)

V_{3}=\mathrm{Span}\left\{e_{0}(\phi):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},e_{1}(\phi):=\cos 2\phi,e_{2}(\phi):=\sin 2\phi\right\}\,,

the linear map (
5.13
) yields a non-commutative version of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In particular, we have

(5.17)

\displaystyle A_{e_{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbbm{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\,,

(5.18)

\displaystyle A_{e_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{2}[\cos 2\phi_{0}\,\sigma_{3}+\sin 2\phi_{0}\,\sigma_{1}]\equiv\frac{r}{2}\sigma_{2\phi_{0}}\,,

(5.19)

\displaystyle A_{e_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{2}[-\sin 2\phi_{0}\,\sigma_{3}+\cos 2\phi_{0}\,\sigma_{1}]\equiv\frac{r}{2}\sigma_{2\phi_{0}+\pi/2}\,,

with the non-zero commutation rule

(5.20)

\left[A_{e_{1}},A_{e_{2}}\right]=-\frac{r^{2}}{2}\tau_{2}\,,\quad\tau_{2}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

In the above we have associated with the unit vector
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\phi}
(
5.1
) the symmetric matrix,

(5.21)

\sigma_{\phi}:=\cos\phi\,\sigma_{3}+\sin\phi\,\sigma_{1}\equiv\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\phi}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\phi&\sin\phi\\
\sin\phi&-\cos\phi\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{R}(\phi)\,\sigma_{3}\,,

where we have introduced the vector notation
\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\sigma_{3}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\imath}}+\sigma_{1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}
. Note the commutation rule

(5.22)

\left[\sigma_{\phi},\sigma_{\phi^{\prime}}\right]=2\sin(\phi-\phi^{\prime})\tau_{2}\,.

Also note that the
3
matrices
\sigma_{1}
,
\tau_{2}
,
\sigma_{3}
span the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}
(2,
\mathbb{R}
), while
\tau_{2}
is precisely the generator of rotations in the plane:

(5.23)

\mathcal{R}(\phi)=e^{\tau_{2}\phi}\,.

The spectral decomposition of the quantum observable
\sigma_{2\phi}
precisely involves its eigen-orientations
\left|\phi\right\rangle
(eigenvalue
1
) and
\left|\phi+\frac{\pi}{2}\right\rangle
(eigenvalue
-1
),

(5.24)

\sigma_{2\phi}=\left|\phi\right\rangle\left\langle\phi\right|-\left|\phi+\frac{\pi}{2}\right\rangle\left\langle\phi+\frac{\pi}{2}\right|=E_{\phi}-E_{\phi+\pi/2}\,.

In agreement with (
5.6
), the density operator (
5.8
) is expressed in terms of
\sigma_{2\phi}
as

(5.25)

\rho_{r,\phi}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}+r\sigma_{2\phi}\right)\,.

Finally, note that the covariance requirement emerges from the rotational invariance of the density operator (
5.11
)

(5.26)

\mathcal{R}\left(\theta\right)\,A_{f}\,\mathcal{R}\left(-\theta\right)=A_{R_{\theta}(f)}\,.

An interesting question concerns the existence of functions
f(\phi)
in
V_{3}
yielding density matrices through the quantization map (
5.13
). Solving this problem amounts to find
f(\phi)=a_{0}+a_{2}\cos 2\phi+b_{2}\sin 2\phi
such that
A_{f}=\rho_{s,\theta}
. Solutions are given from the relations

(5.27)

a_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\,,\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}r\left(a_{2}\cos 2\phi_{0}-b_{2}\sin 2\phi_{0}\right)=s\cos 2\theta\\
r\left(a_{2}\sin 2\phi_{0}+b_{2}\cos 2\phi_{0}\right)=s\sin 2\theta\end{array}\right.\,,\quad 0\leq r\,,\,s\leq 1\,.

Putting
a_{2}=l\cos 2\theta_{0}
and
b_{2}=l\sin 2\theta_{0}
with
l>0
, we obtain from above,

(5.28)

l=\frac{s}{r}\,,\quad\theta_{0}=\theta-\phi_{0}\,,

which yields the solution

(5.29)

f(\phi)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{s}{r}\,\cos 2(\phi+\phi_{0}-\theta)\,.

The representation of a given mixed state as a continuous superposition of mixed states as

(5.30)

\rho_{s,\theta}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left[\frac{1}{2}+\frac{s}{r}\,\cos 2\phi\right]\,\rho_{r,\phi+\theta}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\pi}\,,

which is convex for

, provides one more illustration of a
typical property of quantum-mechanical ensembles in comparison with their classical counterparts.
Note that if
r\geq 2s
holds,

is a probability distribution and (

) can also be viewed as the average of these mixed states.

Quantization and symbol calculus

Here, we go forward in the algebraic and geometrical interpretation of the quantisation map (

Three basic matrices, namely the identity

generate the Jordan algebra

2

2

(xy)x^{2}=x(yx^{2})

6.
An example of quantum measurement of orientations: polarization of light and Stokes parameters

We now wish to describe the relationship between POVMs and light polarization within the context of integral quantization, which was briefly presented in
[
70
]
. Let us first introduce the notion of polarization, and explain how it can be related to density matrices. As was pointed out in the preface of the textbooks
[
71
,
72
]
, polarization is a fundamental characteristic of the transverse wave that is light.
There exists a well-known physical interpretation of
2\times 2
real or complex density matrices in terms of the so-called Stokes parameters for the polarization of light, see for instance
[
73
]
and references therein. The significance and measurements of such parameters is clearly explained in
[
74
]
. We follow here the presentation given by Landau and Lifshitz in Section 50 of
[
75
]
, where is introduced the polarization “tensor” for a quasi-monochromatic plane wave propagating along the
z
-axis with the following notations involving the three Pauli matrices:

(6.1)

\left(\rho_{\alpha\beta}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1+\xi_{3}&\xi_{1}-\mathsf{i}\xi_{2}\\
\xi_{1}+\mathsf{i}\xi_{2}&1-\xi_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\xi_{i}\sigma_{i}\right)\,,

with the Stokes parameters

(6.2)

\xi_{1}=r\sin 2\phi\,,\quad\xi_{2}=A\,,\quad\xi_{3}=r\cos 2\phi\,.

The labels
\alpha
,
\beta
run over the coordinates
(x,y)
in the plane orthogonal to the
z
-axis. In (
6.2
) the parameter
0\leq r\leq 1
characterizes the degree of maximal linear polarization whilst
-1\leq A\leq 1
characterizes the circular polarization of the beam.

Figure 2.
A quasi-monochromatic plane wave propagates to the right along the
z
-axis. The electric field, as the real part of its complex description, lies in the
x-y
plane.

Let us explain the physical origin of the polarization tensor (
6.1
). In its complex description, the electric field reads

(6.3)

\overrightarrow{\mathcal{E}}(t)=\overrightarrow{\mathcal{E}_{0}}(t)\,e^{\mathsf{i}\omega t}=\mathcal{E}_{x}\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\imath}}+\mathcal{E}_{y}\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}=\left(\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\right)\,,

where
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{E}_{0}}(t)
has slow temporal variation and
\omega
is the mean frequency.
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{E}_{0}}
determines the polarization of light. The latter is measured through Nicol prisms, or other devices, by measuring the intensity of the light yielded by mean values of quadratic expressions of field components, which are proportional to
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\mathcal{E}_{\beta}
,
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}_{\beta}
and their respective complex conjugates. Now,
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\mathcal{E}_{\beta}=\mathcal{E}_{0\alpha}\mathcal{E}_{0\beta}e^{2\mathsf{i}\omega t}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\ast}_{\alpha}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}_{\beta}=\mathcal{E}^{\ast}_{0\alpha}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}_{0\beta}e^{-2\mathsf{i}\omega t}
have rapidly oscillating factors and so have null temporal average
\langle\cdot\rangle_{t}
. It results that the properties of a partially polarized light are completely described by the tensor

(6.4)

J_{\alpha\beta}:=\left\langle\mathcal{E}_{0\alpha}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}_{0\beta}\right\rangle_{t}\,.

The quantity
J=\sum_{\alpha}J_{\alpha\alpha}=\left\langle|\mathcal{E}_{0x}|^{2}\right\rangle_{t}+\left\langle|\mathcal{E}_{0y}|^{2}\right\rangle_{t}
determines the intensity of the wave, obtained from the measurement of the energy flux transported by the wave. Since this quantity does not concern the properties of polarization of the wave, one has eventually to deal with the normalized tensor (
6.1
),
i.e.
,

(6.5)

\rho_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{J_{\alpha\beta}}{J}\,.

The light is said completely polarized when the complex amplitude
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
is time-independent, and so is equal to its time average. Then, the polarization tensor factorizes as

(6.6)

\left(\rho_{\alpha\beta}\right)=\frac{1}{J}\begin{pmatrix}|\mathcal{E}_{0x}|^{2}&\mathcal{E}_{0x}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}_{0y}\\
\mathcal{E}^{\ast}_{0x}\mathcal{E}_{0y}&|\mathcal{E}_{0y}|^{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{E}_{0x}/\sqrt{J}\\
\mathcal{E}_{0y}/\sqrt{J}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{E}^{\ast}_{0x}/\sqrt{J}&\mathcal{E}^{\ast}_{0y}/\sqrt{J}\\
\end{pmatrix}\,,

i.e.
, is the orthogonal projector along
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
, and, in quantum terms, a pure state. This case should be put in regard to the other extreme case, namely the non-polarized or natural light, for which all directions in the
x-y
plane are equivalent:

(6.7)

\left(\rho_{\alpha\beta}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,.

In the general case and with
\xi_{i}
coordinates,
\det\left(\rho_{\alpha\beta}\right)=\frac{1}{4}\left(1-\xi_{1}^{2}-\xi_{2}^{2}-\xi_{3}^{2}\right)\equiv\frac{1}{4}\left(1-P^{2}\right)
, where
0\leq P\leq 1
is called the degree of polarization, from
P=0
(random polarization) to
P=1
(total polarization). Another extreme case holds with circular polarization. Then
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{E}_{0}}
is constant and
\mathcal{E}_{0y}=\pm\mathsf{i}\mathcal{E}_{0x}
, which gives
\left(\rho_{\alpha\beta}\right)=(1/2)(\mathbbm{1}\pm\mathsf{i}\sigma_{2})
. As a consequence, the parameter
-1\leq A\leq 1
in (
6.2
) is interpreted as the degree of circular polarization, with
A=1
(resp.
A=-1
) for right (resp. left) circular polarization, and
A=0
for linear polarization.

This distinction between linear and circular polarizations corresponds to the decomposition of (
6.1
) into its symmetric and antisymmetric parts:

(6.8)

\left(\rho_{\alpha\beta}\right)=\rho_{r,\phi}+\frac{A}{2}\sigma_{2}=\frac{1+r}{2}E_{\phi}+\frac{1-r}{2}E_{\phi+\pi/2}+\frac{A}{2}\sigma_{2}\,,

where we have introduced our own notations in (
5.6
) and (
5.8
). Hence, the light can be viewed as the superposition of two waves which are incoherent and elliptically polarized, with similar and orthogonal polarization ellipses.
Within the context of the present paper, circular polarization is not considered. We then put
A=0
, ultimately reducing the polarization tensor to our density matrix.

Let us now describe the interaction polarizer-partially linear polarized light as an example of simple quantum measurements. Here, we follow and generalize the example presented by Peres in
[
76
]
. Two planes and their tensor product are under consideration. The first one is the Hilbert space on which act the states
\rho^{M}_{s,\theta}
of the polarizer viewed as an orientation
pointer
. We note that the action of the generator of rotations
\tau_{2}=-\mathsf{i}\sigma_{2}
on these states corresponds to a
\pi/2
rotation :

(6.9)

\tau_{2}\rho^{M}_{s,\theta}\tau_{2}^{-1}=-\tau_{2}\rho^{M}_{s,\theta}\tau_{2}=\rho^{M}_{s,\theta+\pi/2}\,.

The second plane is the Hilbert space on which act the partially linearized polarization states
\rho^{L}_{r,\phi}
of the plane wave crossing the polarizer. As was explained above, the spectral decomposition of the latter corresponds to the incoherent superposition of two completely linearly polarized waves

(6.10)

\rho^{L}_{r,\phi}=\frac{1+r}{2}\,E_{\phi}+\frac{1-r}{2}\,E_{\phi+\pi/2}\,.

The pointer is designed to detect the orientation in the plane determined by the angle
\phi
.
The interaction pointer-system generating a measurement whose time duration is the interval
I_{M}=(t_{M}-\eta,t_{M}+\eta)
centred at
t_{M}
is described by the (pseudo-) Hamiltonian operator

(6.11)

\widetilde{H}_{\mathrm{int}}(t)=\,g^{\eta}_{M}(t)\tau_{2}\otimes\rho^{L}_{r,\phi}\,,

where
g^{\eta}_{M}
is a Dirac sequence with support in
I_{M}
,
i.e.
,

\lim_{\eta\to 0}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,f(t)\,g^{\eta}_{M}(t)=f(t_{M})\,.

The operator (
6.11
) is the tensor product of an antisymmetric operator (
i.e.
, or pseudo-Hamiltonian, see
[
65
]
) for the pointer with an operator for the system which is symmetric (
i.e.
Hamiltonian). Nevertheless, the operator
U(t,t_{0})
defined for
t_{0}<t_{M}-\eta
as

(6.12)

U(t,t_{0})=\exp\left[\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,g^{\eta}_{M}(t^{\prime})\,\tau_{2}\otimes\rho^{L}_{r,\phi}\right]=\exp\left[G_{M}^{\eta}(t)\,\tau_{2}\otimes\rho^{L}_{r,\phi}\right]\,,

with
G_{M}^{\eta}(t)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\,g^{\eta}_{M}(t^{\prime})
, is an evolution operator. Hence, as soon as
t>t_{M}+\eta
,
G_{M}^{\eta}(t)=1
, and from the general formula involving an orthogonal projector
P
,

(6.13)

\exp(\theta\tau_{2}\otimes P)=\mathcal{R}(\theta)\otimes P+\mathbbm{1}\otimes(\mathbbm{1}-P)\,,

we obtain

(6.14)

U(t,t_{0})=\mathcal{R}\left(G_{M}^{\eta}(t)\,\frac{1+r}{2}\right)\otimes E_{\phi}+\mathcal{R}\left(G_{M}^{\eta}(t)\,\frac{1-r}{2}\right)\otimes E_{\phi+\pi/2}\,.

For
t_{0}<t_{M}-\eta
and
t>t_{M}+\eta
, we finally obtain

(6.15)

U(t,t_{0})=\mathcal{R}\left(\frac{1+r}{2}\right)\otimes E_{\phi}+\mathcal{R}\left(\frac{1-r}{2}\right)\otimes E_{\phi+\pi/2}\,.

One easily checks that
U(t,t_{0})U(t,t_{0})^{{\dagger}}=U(t,t_{0})^{{\dagger}}U(t,t_{0})=\mathbbm{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1}
.
After having prepared the polarizer in the state
\rho^{M}_{s_{0},\theta_{0}}
, the action of (
6.15
) on the initial state
\rho^{M}_{s_{0},\theta_{0}}\otimes\rho^{L}_{r_{0},\phi_{0}}
reads for
t>t_{M}+\eta

\displaystyle U(t,t_{0})\,\rho^{M}_{s_{0},\theta_{0}}\otimes\rho^{L}_{r_{0},\phi_{0}}\,U(t,t_{0})^{{\dagger}}=

\displaystyle\rho^{M}_{s_{0},\theta_{0}+\frac{1+r}{2}}\otimes\frac{1+r_{0}\cos 2(\phi-\phi_{0})}{2}\,E_{\phi}

\displaystyle+\rho^{M}_{s_{0},\theta_{0}+\frac{1-r}{2}}\otimes\frac{1-r_{0}\cos 2(\phi-\phi_{0})}{2}\,E_{\phi+\pi/2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\left(\mathcal{R}(r)+s_{0}\sigma_{2\theta_{0}+1}\right)\otimes r_{0}\sin 2(\phi-\phi_{0})\,E_{\phi}\tau_{2}

(6.16)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}\left(\mathcal{R}(-r)+s_{0}\sigma_{2\theta_{0}+1}\right)\otimes r_{0}\sin 2(\phi-\phi_{0})\,\tau_{2}E_{\phi}\,.

As expected from the standard theory of quantum measurement, this formula indicates that the probability for the pointer to rotate by
\frac{1+r}{2}
, corresponding to the polarization along the orientation
\phi
, is

(6.17)

\mathrm{Tr}\left[\left(U(t,t_{0})\,\rho^{M}_{s_{0},\theta_{0}}\otimes\rho^{L}_{r_{0},\phi_{0}}\,U(t,t_{0})^{{\dagger}}\right)\left(\mathbbm{1}\otimes E_{\phi}\right)\right]=\frac{1+r_{0}\cos 2(\phi-\phi_{0})}{2}\,,

whereas it is

(6.18)

\mathrm{Tr}\left[\left(U(t,t_{0})\,\rho^{M}_{s_{0},\theta_{0}}\otimes\rho^{L}_{r_{0},\phi_{0}}\,U(t,t_{0})^{{\dagger}}\right)\left(\mathbbm{1}\otimes E_{\phi+\pi/2}\right)\right]=\frac{1-r_{0}\cos 2(\phi-\phi_{0})}{2}\,,

for the perpendicular orientation
\phi+\pi/2
and the pointer rotation by
\frac{1-r}{2}
. For the completely linear polarization of the light, i.e.
r_{0}=1
, we recover the familiar Malus laws,
\cos^{2}(\phi-\phi_{0})
and
\sin^{2}(\phi-\phi_{0})
respectively.

7.
Compatibility of POVMs

We now argue that the physical situation where two measurements are made one after another is described by a dichotomic POVM. We then recall the necessary conditions for the compatibility of two dichotomic POVMs, and what form these conditions take in the Euclidean plane.

7.1.
POVMs arising from the measurement of polarization

It is well known
[
77
]
that the statistics of the sequential measurement of two incompatible observables is described by a POVM. Let us analyze the case of the sequential measurement of two incompatible polarization observables.
Suppose an incident light ray is polarized according to the state
\rho
and is sent toward a first vertical polarizer (
\pi/2
) and subsequently toward a second oblique (
\pi/4
) polarizer. The light ray is then detected by a light detector. Suppose the state of the detector is
D=1
if it detects a light ray and
D=0
if it does not. What is the probability that the light ray triggers the detector (in which case
D=1
and the polarization of the transmitted light ray is
\pi/4
)?

We first note that the probability that the light ray exits the first polarizer is
Tr\left(\rho E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\right)
where
\rho
is an initial state and
E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}=|\frac{\pi}{2}\rangle\langle\frac{\pi}{2}|
. If the light ray has passed the first polarizer, its state is

\rho^{\prime}{}=\frac{E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\rho E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}}{Tr\left(E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\rho\right)}\;.

Then, the probability that it passes the second polarizer is
Tr\left(\rho^{\prime}{}E_{\frac{\pi}{4}}\right)
. Therefore, the probability that the state of the detector is
D=1
at the end of the measurement process (the light ray is transmitted) is

(7.1)

\displaystyle p\left(D=1\right)=Tr\left(\rho E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\right)Tr\left(\rho^{\prime}{}E_{\frac{\pi}{4}}\right)=Tr\left(\rho E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}E_{\frac{\pi}{4}}E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}Tr\left(\rho E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\right).

Note that
E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}E_{\frac{\pi}{4}}E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}
is an effect,
i.e.
a symmetric operator
E
such that
0\leq E\leq\mathbbm{1}
, but not a projection operator (it is a multiple of a projection operator).
Now, the probability that the light ray passes the first polarizer but does not pass the second is

(7.2)

\displaystyle Tr\left(\rho E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\right)Tr\left[\rho^{\prime}{}\left(\mathbbm{1}-E_{\frac{\pi}{4}}\right)\right]=Tr\left[\rho E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\left(\mathbbm{1}-E_{\frac{\pi}{4}}\right)E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\right]=\frac{1}{2}Tr\left(\rho E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\right).

The probability that, at the end of the measurement process, the detector is in the state
D=0
is

(7.3)

\displaystyle p(D=0)=Tr(\rho E_{0})+\frac{1}{2}Tr(\rho E_{\frac{\pi}{2}})=Tr\left[\rho\left(E_{0}+\frac{1}{2}E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\right)\right].

Note that
p(D=1)+p\big(D=0\big)=1
. Again,
E_{0}+\frac{1}{2}E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}
is an effect but not a projection operator. The measurement process is then described by the dichotomic POVM

(7.4)

\displaystyle F=\left\{\frac{1}{2}E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\;,\;E_{0}+\frac{1}{2}E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\right\}\;,

which is not the spectral measure corresponding to a self-adjoint operator.

If we analyze the sequential measurement where the light ray first passes the oblique polarizer and then the vertical one, we obtain that the overall measurement process is characterized by the POVM
\left\{\frac{1}{2}E_{\frac{\pi}{4}},\mathbbm{1}-\frac{1}{2}E_{\frac{\pi}{4}}\right\}
which is different from the previous one describing the measurement process with the light ray passing first through the vertical polarizer. The two measurement procedures give different results. That is explained by the incompatibility of the measurement of the vertical and the oblique polarization and is analogous to the Heisenberg microscope experiment where a sequential measure of the position and momentum is realized.

7.2.
Dichotomic POVMs

A dichotomic POVM
F
is a pair
F=\{A,\mathbbm{1}-A\}
with
A
an effect.
We consider two dichotomic POVMs
F
and
F^{\prime}
, and look for necessary and sufficient conditions for their compatibility. We show that every dichotomic observable
F
is the fuzzy version of a sharp observable
E
;
F
can also be interpreted as an approximation of
E
. Then, inspired by
[
1
]
, we answer the following question: given two incompatible sharp observables
E
and
E^{\prime}
, is it possible to define two compatible fuzzy versions
F
and
F^{\prime}
of
E
and
E^{\prime}
respectively whose joint measurement can be interpreted as the approximate joint measurement of
E
and
E^{\prime}
? First we recall the main definitions and theorems concerning the compatibility of POVMs that we adapt to the case of a real, bidimensional Hilbert space.
Two dichotomic POVMs
F_{1}=\{A_{1},\mathbbm{1}-A_{1}\}
and
F_{2}=\{A_{2},\mathbbm{1}-A_{2}\}
are compatible if there is a third POVM
F=\{G_{11},G_{00},G_{10},G_{01}\}
of which
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are the marginals,
i.e.
,

(7.5)

\displaystyle A_{1}=G_{11}+G_{10},\,\,A_{2}=G_{11}+G_{01},

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}-A_{1}=G_{00}+G_{01},\,\,I-A_{2}=G_{00}+G_{10}\;.

A necessary and sufficient condition for the compatibility
[
58
]
of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
is the existence of a positive operator
G_{11}
such that

(7.6)

\begin{split}&G_{11}\leq A_{1},A_{2}\;,\\
&A_{1}+A_{2}-G_{11}\leq\mathbbm{1}\;.\end{split}

Indeed, if
G_{11}
exists, we can define the effects

(7.7)

\displaystyle G_{00}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}-A_{1}-A_{2}+G_{11}\geq 0\;,

(7.8)

\displaystyle G_{10}

\displaystyle=A_{1}-G_{11}\geq 0\;,

(7.9)

\displaystyle G_{01}

\displaystyle=A_{2}-G_{11}\geq 0\;,

which satisfy (
7.5
).

Now we translate the above compatibility conditions in the present context where an observable is represented by the dichotomic observable corresponding to a symmetric operator of the kind defined below

(7.10)

A(\alpha,\phi,r)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{r}{2}\cos 2\phi&\frac{r}{2}\sin 2\phi\\
\frac{r}{2}\sin 2\phi&\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{r}{2}\cos 2\phi\end{pmatrix}=\alpha\rho_{\frac{r}{\alpha},\phi}\,.

with eigenvalues
\frac{\alpha}{2}\pm\frac{r}{2}
. The condition
0\leq A\leq I
then becomes
r\leq\alpha\leq 2-r
(so that
0\leq\alpha\leq 2
and
0\leq r\leq 1
) which ensures that
A\left(\alpha,\phi,r\right)
is an effect. Note that there are two meanings we can give to matrices of the form (
7.10
): if
\alpha=r=1
, they are density operators which can be used to represent pure states or sharp observables; in the case
\alpha=1
,
r\neq 1
, they can represent mixed states or fuzzy (unsharp) observables; in the general case
\alpha,r\neq 1
they represent fuzzy unbiased observables (see below). We recall that in the context of the linear polarisation of the light as described by (
6.5
), the parameter
\alpha
may be interpreted as the intensity of the light while
r=\xi_{1}^{2}+\xi_{3}^{2}=P
provides a measure of the degree of polarization. That is relevant in order to give an interpretation of the fuzzy observables as approximations of the sharp observables and of the joint measurability of fuzzy observables as the approximate joint measurability of incompatible sharp polarization observables (see below).

The dichotomic observable corresponding to
A(\alpha,\phi,r)
is
\big\{F_{+}=A(\alpha,\phi,r),\,F_{-}=\mathbbm{1}-A(\alpha,\phi,r)\big\}
and can be interpreted as the randomization of the dichotomic PVM
\big\{E_{+}=A(1,\phi,1),\,E_{-}=\mathbbm{1}-A(1,\phi,1)\big\}
through the Markov kernel
\mu(+,+)=\frac{\alpha+r}{2}
,
\mu(+,-)=\frac{\alpha-r}{2}
,
\mu(-,+)=1-\mu(+,+)
,
\mu(-,-)=1-\mu(+,-)
. Indeed,
F_{+}=\mu(+,+)E_{+}+\mu(+,-)E_{-}
and
F_{-}=\mu(-,+)E_{+}+\mu(-,-)E_{-}
.

As an example, we can consider the POVMs
\left\{\frac{1}{2}E_{\frac{\pi}{2}},\mathbbm{1}-\frac{1}{2}E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\right\}
and
\left\{\frac{1}{2}E_{\frac{\pi}{4}},\mathbbm{1}-\frac{1}{2}E_{\frac{\pi}{4}}\right\}
that we found in section
7.1
which represent sequential measurements. In particular, we have that
\frac{1}{2}E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}=A\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
,
\frac{1}{2}E_{\frac{\pi}{4}}=A\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{1}{2}\right)
. They are not compatible as can be seen by showing that there is no symmetric positive operator satisfying conditions (
7.6
). Note also that
\left\{\frac{1}{2}E_{\frac{\pi}{2}},\mathbbm{1}-\frac{1}{2}E_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\right\}
is the randomization of the PVM
\left\{E_{+}=A\left(1,\frac{\pi}{2},1\right),\,E_{-}=\mathbbm{1}-A\left(1,\frac{\pi}{2},1\right)\right\}
through the Markov kernels
\mu(+,+)=\frac{1}{2}
,
\mu(+,-)=0
,
\mu(-,+)=\frac{1}{2}
,
\mu(-,-)=1
. The same holds for the POVM
\left\{\frac{1}{2}E_{\frac{\pi}{4}},\mathbbm{1}-\frac{1}{2}E_{\frac{\pi}{4}}\right\}
which is the randomization of the PVM
\left\{E_{+}=A\left(1,\frac{\pi}{4},1\right),\,E_{-}=\mathbbm{1}-A\left(1,\frac{\pi}{4},1\right)\right\}
through the same Markov kernel.

Let

F_{1}=\{A(\alpha_{1},\phi_{1},r_{1}),\mathbbm{1}-A(\alpha_{1},\phi_{1},r_{1})\}

and

F_{2}=\{A(\alpha_{2},\phi_{2},r_{2}),\mathbbm{1}-A(\alpha_{2},\phi_{2},r_{2})\}

be two dichotomic POVMs. We look for conditions for the compatibility of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. An analogous problem has been dealt with in Ref.
[
58
,
1
]
. The present case can be considered as the real version of the qubit case analyzed in
[
1
]
, chapter 14. If circular polarization were included, the relevant Hilbert space would be
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and the formalism would coincide with the one in
[
1
]
.

First, we note that to each symmetric operator
A(\alpha,\phi,r)
there corresponds a vector
v=(r,\phi)
in the unit closed disc. We then have

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=A(\alpha_{1},\phi_{1},r_{1})\mapsto v_{1}=(r_{1},\phi_{1})\;,

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=A(\alpha_{2},\phi_{2},r_{2})\mapsto v_{2}=(r_{2},\phi_{2})\;,

\displaystyle G_{11}

\displaystyle=A(\alpha,\phi,r)\mapsto v=(r,\phi)\;.

Moreover, using (
7.5
)

\displaystyle A_{1}-G_{11}

\displaystyle=A(\alpha_{1},\phi_{1},r_{1})-A(\alpha,\phi,r)=G_{10}=A(\alpha_{1}-\alpha,r_{10},\phi_{10})\;,

\displaystyle A_{2}-G_{11}

\displaystyle=A(\alpha_{2},\phi_{2},r_{2})-A(\alpha,\phi,r)=G_{01}=A(\alpha_{2}-\alpha,r_{01},\phi_{01})\;,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}-A_{1}-A_{2}+G_{11}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}-A(\alpha_{1},\phi_{1},r_{1})-A(\alpha_{2},\phi_{2},r_{2})+A(\alpha,\phi,r)

\displaystyle=G_{00}=A(1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}+\alpha,r_{00},\phi_{00})\;,

and consequently, we can define the vectors

\displaystyle v_{10}

\displaystyle=(r_{10},\phi_{10})=v_{1}-v\;,

\displaystyle v_{01}

\displaystyle=(r_{01},\phi_{01})=v_{2}-v\;,

\displaystyle v_{00}

\displaystyle=(r_{00},\phi_{00})=v-v_{1}-v_{2}\;.

This can be seen by a simple application of Carnot’s theorem for triangles. Therefore, the respective eigenvalues imply that the compatibility between POVMs is achieved if

(7.11)

\displaystyle\|v\|

\displaystyle=r\leq\alpha

\displaystyle(\text{positivity of}\,\,G_{11})\;,

\displaystyle\|v_{1}-v\|

\displaystyle=r_{10}\leq\alpha_{1}-\alpha

\displaystyle(\text{positivity of}\,\,G_{10})\;,

\displaystyle\|v_{2}-v\|

\displaystyle=r_{01}\leq\alpha_{2}-\alpha

\displaystyle(\text{positivity of}\,\,G_{01})\;,

\displaystyle\|v-v_{1}-v_{2}\|

\displaystyle=r_{00}\leq 2-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}+\alpha

\displaystyle(A_{1}+A_{2}-G_{11}\leq\mathbbm{1})\;.

We then have

(7.12)

\displaystyle\|v_{1}+v_{2}\|+\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|\leq\alpha+(2-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}+\alpha)+(\alpha_{1}-\alpha)+(\alpha_{2}-\alpha)=2

which is a necessary condition for the compatibility. That is exactly the same condition obtained in
[
58
]
for the case of a two-dimensional complex Hilbert space of which the real case we are considering is a particular case. However, the condition is not sufficient, as can be seen by noting that the two dichotomic observables introduced in the previous subsection,
i.e.
,
A\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
and
A\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{1}{2}\right)
, satisfy the necessary condition but are not compatible as we remarked above. Note also that in the case of unbiased dichotomic observables
A(1,\phi_{1},r_{1})
,
A(1,\phi_{2},r_{2})
, the condition is also sufficient. Indeed, one can choose
v=\frac{v_{1}+v_{2}}{2}
and
\alpha=1-\frac{|v_{1}-v_{2}|}{2}
which, thanks to condition (
7.12
), ensures that the inequalities (
7.11
) are satisfied.

In order to give an interpretation of both the fuzzy observable
\{A(\alpha,\phi,r),\mathbbm{1}-A(\alpha,\phi,r)\}
and the necessary condition (
7.12
), we can resort again to the Stokes parameters. In section
6
, we have shown that the quantity
P=\xi_{1}^{2}+\xi_{3}^{2}=r
provides a measure of the degree of linear polarization; with
P
varying between
1
(total polarization) and
0
(random polarization). In the present framework, the same quantity is interpreted as a measure of the fuzziness of the polarization measurement. For example, a measurement of polarization represented by the PVM
E=\big\{E_{+}=A(1,\phi,1),\,E_{-}=\mathbbm{1}-A(1,\phi,1)\big\}
(sharp observable) corresponds to a sharp measurement of polarization in the direction defined by
\phi
while a measurement represented by the POVM
F=\big\{F_{+}=A(1,\phi,r),\,F_{-}=\mathbbm{1}-A(1,\phi,r)\big\}
,
r<1
(unsharp observable) corresponds to a fuzzy measurement; the fuzziness depending on
r
which measures the degree with which
F
approximates
E
. The POVM
\hat{F}=\big\{F_{+}=A(\alpha,\phi,r),\,F_{-}=\mathbbm{1}-A(\alpha,\phi,r)\big\}
is a fuzzy version of
E
as well and the degree of approximation depends both on
\alpha
and
r
. If two sharp observables
E=\big\{E_{+}=A(1,\phi,1),\,E_{-}=\mathbbm{1}-A(1,\phi,1)\big\}
and
E^{\prime}=\big\{E_{+}=A(1,\phi^{\prime},1),\,E_{-}=\mathbbm{1}-A(1,\phi^{\prime},1)\big\}
are not compatible, they could be approximated by two compatible fuzzy versions
F=\big\{F_{+}=A(1,\phi,r),\,F_{-}=\mathbbm{1}-A(1,\phi,r)\big\}
and
F^{\prime}=\big\{F_{+}=A(1,\phi^{\prime},r^{\prime}),\,F_{-}=\mathbbm{1}-A(1,\phi^{\prime},r^{\prime})\big\}
. Equation (
7.12
) gives a necessary and sufficient condition for the joint measurability of
F
and
F^{\prime}
to be connected to the degree of fuzziness one has to add in the measurement process; the latter being measured by
r
and
r^{\prime}
. In the case we use fuzzy observables
F=\big\{F_{+}=A(\alpha,\phi,r),\,F_{-}=\mathbbm{1}-A(\alpha,\phi,r)\big\}
and
F^{\prime}=\big\{F_{+}=A(\alpha^{\prime},\phi^{\prime},r^{\prime}),\,F_{-}=\mathbbm{1}-A(\alpha^{\prime},\phi^{\prime},r^{\prime})\big\}
, with
\alpha\neq 1
and/or
\alpha^{\prime}\neq 1
, to approximate
E
and
E^{\prime}
, condition (
7.12
) is only necessary.

8.
Conclusions

We considered in this work the role of real Positive Operator-Valued Measures as quantizers of a physical system as well as their status as quantum observables in finite dimension. We focused in particular in dimension 2, which is the lowest one for which we can develop a non-trivial quantum formalism. The starting point, after having recalled the definition of POVMs, was to lay down some necessary and sufficient conditions for two POVMs to be compatible. We then emphasized that POVMs, besides their role as observables, can also provide a quantization scheme in the framework of integral quantization. In particular, a first result emerged when considering Toeplitz quantization as an integral quantization, where we showed that Naimark theorem is valid in our case.

We continued the study by restraining ourselves to integral quantizations of functions on the unit circle and their actions on real two-dimensional systems, and where we supposed non-negative POVMs,
i.e.
density matrices. In this case, the quantization map gives a non-commutative version of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that can be identified as a Fourier subspace, and the density matrix (
5.8
) is particularly simple. Quantizing a function on this subspace gives the simple
2
x
2
matrix representation (
5.13
), which allows for a simple non-commutative version of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The most general functions living in this Fourier space were found in (
5.29
). They were subsequently used to prove that a mixed state can be represented as a continuous superposition of mixed state, which is a typical quantum mechanical behavior. Applying Naimark theorem made possible the identification between the quantum operator of a POVM and its density matrix.

A physical illustration of the previous results was made by using light polarization. Remembering the construction of the polarization tensor (
6.8
), we showed that it reduces to the general form of our two-dimensional density matrices when circular polarization is neglected. Then, the interaction between a polarizer and the system was described during a quantum measurement, where we showed that the probability to obtain a given polarization is given by the evolution equation (
6
).

The case of sequential measurements was analysed at last. It was shown that two measurements in which a light ray goes first through an oblique polarizer before passing through a vertical polarizer is described by a dichotomic POVM, while a measurement in the reverse order is described by another dichotomic POVM, showing the incompatibility of the measurement procedures. We finally searched for the necessary condition (
7.12
) for the compatibility of two dichotomic POVMs in a real bidimensional Hilbert space. In the last section, we related the density matrix’s parameters to the Stokes parameters defining a polarization tensor for linearly polarized light. It turns out that we can identify the degree of mixing of a density matrix to the fuzziness of a quantum observable. We concluded that compatibility conditions of two POVMs can be expressed in terms of Stokes parameters.

Appendix A
Quantum orientations in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

A.1.
Pure and mixed states

We now generalize to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
a part of the material developed at length in the previous sections. A vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\boldsymbol{v}
(Euclidean geometry) or
|\boldsymbol{v}\rangle
(Dirac or Hilbertian notation). Thus a pure state is represented by an element of
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, and a mixed state by the general density matrix

(A.1)

\rho\equiv\rho_{\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\phi}}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbbm{1}+\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\,\mathsf{D}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\,{}^{t}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\,,

with notations similar to (
5.8
). The diagonal matrix
\mathbbm{1}/n\equiv\rho_{\mathrm{rm}}
which appears in this expression describes the full random mixing.
The symbol
\mathsf{D}
stands for the diagonal matrix

(A.2)

\mathsf{D}(\boldsymbol{\eta})=\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{1}&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\dots&\eta_{n}\end{pmatrix}\,,

where the vector
\boldsymbol{\eta}={}^{t}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\cdots,\eta_{n})
lies in the simplex defined by the conditions

(A.3)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}=0\,,\quad-\frac{1}{n}\leq\eta_{i}\leq 1-\frac{1}{n}\,,

i.e.
, the intersection of the hyperplane normal to the first diagonal and containing the origin with the unit hypercube shifted by the vector
{}^{t}(-1/n,-1/n,\dotsc,-1/n)
. The rotation matrix
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
and the symbol
\boldsymbol{\phi}
stands for the set of
n(n-1)/2
(angular) parameters of the group SO
(n)
.
Thus, the manifold of density matrices (
A.1
) is real with dimension equal to
n(n+2)/2
.

The representation of the SO
(n)
group composition law is written here as

(A.4)

\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime})=\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi}\circ\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime})\;.

We adopt the Euler parametrisation based on the descending sequence
\mathrm{SO}(n)\supset\mathrm{SO}(n-1)\supset\cdots\supset\mathrm{SO}(2)

[
78
]
. First, for
1\leq k\leq n-1
, we denote
\mathcal{R}_{k}(\phi)
the rotation by the angle
\phi
in the
\left(x_{k},x_{k+1}\right)
-plane. Next, we introduce the successive products of such elementary rotations:

(A.5)

\mathcal{R}^{(k)}\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(k)}\right)=\mathcal{R}_{1}(\phi_{1}^{k})\,\mathcal{R}_{2}(\phi_{2}^{k})\,\cdots\mathcal{R}_{k}(\phi_{k}^{k})\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(k)}=\left(\phi_{1}^{k},\phi_{2}^{k},\dotsc,\phi_{k}^{k}\right)\,.

For each
1\leq k\leq n-1
, the Euler angles
\phi_{j}^{k}
,
1\leq j\leq k
vary as follows

(A.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0\leq\phi_{1}^{k}<2\pi&\\
0\leq\phi_{j}^{k}<\pi&j\neq 1\end{array}\right.\,.

Then any rotation
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi})
of the group SO
(n)
can be written
[
78
]
as the product

(A.7)

\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi})=\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathcal{R}^{(k)}\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(k)}\right)\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\phi}=\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(1)},\dotsc,\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(n-1)}\right)\,.

The non-normalized invariant (Haar) measure on SO
(n)
reads in terms of the above
n(n-1)/2
Euler angular variables:

(A.8)

\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}=\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\,\sin^{j-1}\phi_{j}^{k}\,\mathrm{d}\phi_{j}^{k}\,,

from which we obtain the volume of SO
(n)

(A.9)

\mathrm{Vol}\left(\mathrm{SO}(n)\right)=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Area}\left(\mathbb{S}^{i-1}\right)\,,\quad\mathrm{Area}\left(\mathbb{S}^{i-1}\right)=2\frac{\pi^{i/2}}{\Gamma(i/2)}\,.

Finally, note that the spectral decomposition of
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\phi}}
is easily deduced from (
A.1
) by using the orthonormal set of column vectors of the rotation matrix
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi})
:

(A.10)

\rho_{\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\phi}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}+\eta_{i}\right)\,|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{i}|\,,\quad\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi})=(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\dotsc,\boldsymbol{v}_{n})\,.

A.2.
SO
(n)
covariant integral quantisation

Let us choose a set of
(n+2)(n-1)/2
parameters
(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})
. The covariant integral quantisation of functions (or distributions) on the SO
(n)
manifold is based on the resolution of the identity provided by the rotational transport of the matrix density
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}}
, similarly as what we have seen in the two-dimensional case (
5.12
). The resolution of identity reads

(A.11)

\mathbbm{1}=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}}{c_{n}}\,\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\,\rho_{\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}}\,{}^{t}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi})=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}}{c_{n}}\,\rho_{\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\phi}\circ\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}}\,,

where
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}
is the Haar measure on SO
(n)
and
\boldsymbol{\phi}\circ\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}
stands for the group composition law in the parameter space. Equality (
A.11
) is validated by Schur’s Lemma. Indeed, due to the invariance of the Haar measure, the right-hand side commutes with the (fundamental)
n
-dimensional unitary irreducible representation
\boldsymbol{\beta}\mapsto\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
of SO
(n)
. The constant
c_{n}
is calculated by taking the trace on both sides,

(A.12)

c_{n}=\frac{\mathrm{Vol}\left(\mathrm{SO}(n)\right)}{n}\,.

Note that the resolution of the identity could be also proved by hand using the orthonormality between matrix elements of the fundamental (symmetric) UIR of SO
(n)
,
\boldsymbol{\phi}\mapsto\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi})
,

(A.13)

\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}}{c_{n}}\,\mathcal{R}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\,\mathcal{R}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\phi})=\delta_{ii^{\prime}}\,\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\,,

which, with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}=0
, entails

(A.14)

\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}}{c_{n}}\,\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\,\mathsf{D}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\,{}^{t}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi})=0_{n\times n}\,.

From (
A.11
), we can derive the quantisation of a real-valued function (or distribution)
f(\boldsymbol{\phi})
on SO
(n)
. It is precisely given by the map

(A.15)

\begin{split}f(\boldsymbol{\phi})\mapsto A_{f}&=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}}{c_{n}}\,f(\boldsymbol{\phi})\,\rho_{\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\phi}\circ\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}}=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}}{c_{n}}\,f\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}\circ(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})^{-1}\right)\,\rho_{\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\phi}}\\
&=\langle f\rangle+\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}}{c_{n}}\,f\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}\circ(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})^{-1}\right)\,\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\,\mathsf{D}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\,{}^{t}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\\
&\equiv\langle f\rangle+\mathsf{N}_{f}\,.\end{split}

In the above equation,
\langle f\rangle
denotes the mean value of
f
on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
,

(A.16)

\langle f\rangle=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}}{\mathrm{Vol}\left(\mathrm{SO}(n)\right)}\,f(\boldsymbol{\phi})\,.

We find the elements of the matrix
\mathsf{N}_{f}
using the decomposition of the tensor product

(A.17)

\mathcal{R}\otimes\mathcal{R}=\oplus_{\boldsymbol{t}}\mathrm{T}^{\boldsymbol{t}}\;,

as a (finite) direct sum of UIR’s
\mathrm{T}^{\boldsymbol{t}}
of SO
(n)
(
\boldsymbol{t}
is a multi-index). Actually, the range of this multi-index is quite narrow in our case. From the Peter-Weyl theorem for continuous functions on a compact group
[
79
]
, this expansion reads on the level of matrix elements of
\mathcal{R}
:

(A.18)

\mathcal{R}_{il}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\,\mathcal{R}_{jl}(\boldsymbol{\phi})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J}}C^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{il,jl,\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J}}\mathrm{T}^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J}}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\,,

where the
C^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{il,jl,\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J}}
are Clebsch-Gordan coefficients and
\boldsymbol{I}
,
\boldsymbol{J}
, are multi-indices. The range of
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is obviously quite limited.
On the other hand, with minimal assumptions on
f
,
e.g.
square integrability on SO
(n)
, and from the completeness of the
\mathrm{T}^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J}}(\boldsymbol{\phi})
’s, one can Fourier expand
f
as

(A.19)

f(\boldsymbol{\phi})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J}}f^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J}}\mathrm{T}^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J}}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\,,

and eventually,

(A.20)

f\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}\circ(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})^{-1}\right)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{K}}f^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J}}\mathrm{T}^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{K}}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\,\mathrm{T}^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{K}}\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)\,,

With (
A.18
) and (
A.20
) in hand, and from the orthogonality of the
\mathrm{T}^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J}}(\boldsymbol{\phi})
’s, the matrix elements of
\mathsf{N}_{f}
read as

(A.21)

\left[\mathsf{N}_{f}\right]_{ij}=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\eta_{l}\sum_{\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{K}}f^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J}}\,C^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{il,jl,\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J}}\,\mathrm{T}^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{K}}\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)\,.

The covariance of the quantisation map (
A.15
) means

(A.22)

\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\,A_{f}\,{}^{t}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=A_{\mathrm{R}(\boldsymbol{\beta})f}\,,\quad(\mathrm{R}(\boldsymbol{\beta})f)(\boldsymbol{\phi}):=f\left((\boldsymbol{\beta})^{-1}\circ\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)\,.

Let us determine the function
f=1/n+g
such that
A_{f}
is the density matrix
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
,
i.e.
,

(A.23)

\rho_{\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}}{c_{n}}\,\left(\frac{1}{n}+g(\boldsymbol{\phi})\right)\,\rho_{\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\phi}\circ\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}}\,.

Taking the trace on both sides implies that

(A.24)

\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}\,g(\boldsymbol{\phi})=0\,,\quad\mbox{{i.e.}},\quad\langle g\rangle=0\,.

Using (
A.11
), (
A.14
), and (
A.24
) makes (
A.23
) equivalent to

(A.25)

\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\,\mathsf{D}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\,{}^{t}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}}{c_{n}}\,g(\boldsymbol{\phi})\,\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi}\circ\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})\,\mathsf{D}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\,{}^{t}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\phi}\circ\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})\,.

Using the SO
(n)
-covariance, one gets for each
1\leq l\leq n

(A.26)

\frac{\zeta_{l}}{\eta_{l}}=\sum_{i,j}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}}{c_{n}}\,g\left((\boldsymbol{\beta})^{-1}\circ\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)\,\mathcal{R}_{il}(\boldsymbol{\phi}\circ\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})\,\mathcal{R}_{jl}(\boldsymbol{\phi}\circ\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})\,.

Using the reduction formula (
A.18
), expanding
g(\boldsymbol{\phi})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J}}g^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J}}\mathrm{T}^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J}}(\boldsymbol{\phi})
as in (
A.19
), using the SO
(n)
representation law and the orthogonality of the corresponding matrix elements yields the linear system for the components
g^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J}}
:

(A.27)

\frac{\zeta_{l}}{\eta_{l}}=\sum_{i,j,\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{K},\boldsymbol{I}^{\prime}}g^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\boldsymbol{I}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{J}}\,C^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{il,jl,\boldsymbol{I}\boldsymbol{J}}\mathrm{T}^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\boldsymbol{I}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{J}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\,\mathrm{T}^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\boldsymbol{K},\boldsymbol{J}}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})\,.

Let us impose in our choice of
g
the relations

(A.28)

\sum_{i,j}g^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\boldsymbol{I}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{J}}\,C^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{il,jl,\boldsymbol{I}\boldsymbol{J}}=h^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{l,\boldsymbol{I}}\delta_{\boldsymbol{I}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{J}}\,.

We next choose
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}=\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{-1}
. We finally end with the following values of
\eta_{l}
:

(A.29)

\eta_{l}=\frac{\zeta_{l}}{\sum_{\boldsymbol{I},\boldsymbol{t}}h^{\boldsymbol{t}}_{l,\boldsymbol{I}}}\,.

Finally, let us consider the
n^{2}
-dimensional Lie algebra
\mathfrak{gl}(n,\mathbb{R})
and its Cartan decomposition into antisymmetric and symmetric matrices,

(A.30)

\mathfrak{gl}(n,\mathbb{R})=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}\,,

it is easy to prove that the quantisation (
A.15
) maps real-valued functions into the
n(n+1)/2
-dimensional space
\mathfrak{p}
of symmetric matrices,
i.e.
, the quantum observables, while
\mathfrak{k}\simeq\mathfrak{so}(n)
is the
n(n-1)/2
-dimensional Lie algebra of SO
(n)
. In particular, any element
X
in the sub-algebra
\mathfrak{k}
may play the role of a (pseudo)-Hamiltonian as a generator of the evolution (
i.e.
unitary)
operator
e^{tX}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
