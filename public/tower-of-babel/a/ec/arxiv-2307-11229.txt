Abstract
. We study the Landau-de Gennes Q-tensor model of liquid crystals subjected to an electric field and develop a fully discrete numerical scheme for its solution. The scheme uses a convex splitting of the bulk potential, and we introduce a truncation operator for the Q-tensors to ensure well-posedness of the problem. We prove the stability and well-posedness of the scheme. Finally, making a restriction on the admissible parameters of the scheme, we show that up to a subsequence, solutions to the fully discrete scheme converge to weak solutions of the Q-tensor model as the time step and mesh are refined. We then present numerical results computed by the numerical scheme, among which we show that it is possible to simulate the Fréedericksz transition with this scheme.



1.
Introduction

Liquid crystals are a state of matter with properties intermediate to those of solids and liquids. They flow like liquids, but they also possess properties of solids. Namely, their molecules have an orientation as in crystals. These materials generally consist of elongated molecules that can be pictured as rods which align along a common direction due to intermolecular forces
[
29
,
26
,
27
]
. This microscopic structure affects the mechanical response of the material to stress and strain at the macroscopic level. This makes liquid crystals useful for many engineering applications. For example, there are liquid crystals that react to electric fields and polarize the light going through them. This is taken advantage of in liquid crystal displays (LCDs), smart glasses, and other technologies
[
7
,
6
,
17
]
.
The goal of this work is to introduce and analyze a numerical scheme for a model of liquid crystal subjected to an electric field. The convergence proof will also demonstrate existence of weak solutions of the underlying system of partial differential equations (PDEs). The model that we consider here is based on the Landau and de Gennes Q-tensor theory of liquid crystal dynamics
[
29
,
10
]
.

In the Q-tensor model, the orientation of liquid crystals is described by a symmetric, trace-free
d\times d
matrix
Q
which is assumed to minimize the Landau-de Gennes free energy

E_{LG}(Q)=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{F}_{B}(Q)+\mathcal{F}_{E}(Q)+\mathcal{F}_{e}(Q),

in equilibrium. Here
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d=2,3
is the spatial domain in which the liquid crystal molecules lie,
\mathcal{F}_{e}
is the electrostatic energy density which we discuss below,
\mathcal{F}_{B}
is a bulk potential, and
\mathcal{F}_{E}
is the elastic energy given by

\mathcal{F}_{B}(Q)=\frac{a}{2}\operatorname{tr}(Q^{2})-\frac{b}{3}\operatorname{tr}(Q^{3})+\frac{c}{4}(\operatorname{tr}(Q^{2}))^{2}+A_{0},\quad\mathcal{F}_{E}(Q)=\frac{L_{1}}{2}|\nabla Q|^{2}+\frac{L_{2}}{2}|\operatorname{div}Q|^{2}+\frac{L_{3}}{2}\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\partial_{i}Q_{jk}\partial_{k}Q_{ji},

where
a,b,c,A_{0},L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}
are constants with
c,L_{1}>0
;
L_{2}+L_{3}\geq 0
. Here
(\operatorname{div}Q)_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{j}Q_{ij}
and
|\nabla Q|^{2}=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}(\partial_{k}Q_{ij})^{2}
. We use
|\cdot|
to denote the Euclidean vector norm, the Frobenius norm for matrices, and corresponding generalizations for higher order tensors. Having
c>0
ensures that the bulk potential is bounded from below
[
33
]
, and
A_{0}
ensures
\mathcal{F}_{B}(Q)\geq 0
. We further assume that
\Omega
is a bounded, simply connected domain with Lipschitz boundary. For the clarity of exposition, we will also assume the one-constant approximation
[
29
]
, i.e., that
L_{1}:=L>0
and
L_{2}=L_{3}=0
. The results of this paper can be extended to nonzero
L_{2}
and
L_{3}
in a straightforward manner, but this assumption simplifies our presentation.

Now non-equilibrium situations are described via the gradient flow

(1.1)

Q_{t}=M\left(L\Delta Q-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{B}(Q)}{\partial Q}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{e}(Q)}{\partial Q}\right),

where
M>0
is a constant. Here, we have

\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{B}(Q)}{\partial Q}=aQ-b\left(Q^{2}-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}(Q^{2})I\right)+c\operatorname{tr}(Q^{2})Q.

The gradient flow above will be coupled to another equation related to the electric potential. We turn now to its derivation and the derivation of
\mathcal{F}_{e}
.

1.1.
Electrostatic Energy Density Derivation

Liquid crystals interact with externally applied electric fields or self-induce an internal electric field due to dielectric and spontaneous polarization effects. We let
D
be the electric displacement field and
E
be the electric field. One may use Maxwell’s equations to find the electric field in
\Omega
, which in this case with no free charges are given by

(1.2)

\begin{split}\operatorname{div}D&=0,\\
\operatorname{curl}E&=0.\end{split}

As the time scale of the electromagnetic waves is much faster than the one of the liquid crystal molecule motion, it makes sense to consider the stationary Maxwell equations. The equations for the magnetic field can be included also, however, they decouple if the liquid crystal material is not susceptible to magnetic fields and for this reason, we will not consider them here.
Since
\Omega
is simply connected, it follows that we may write
E=\nabla u
, where
u
is a scalar potential. In the setting of nematic liquid crystals, we have

D=\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon E+P_{s},

where
\varepsilon=\Delta\varepsilon^{\ast}Q+\overline{\varepsilon}I
and

P_{s}=\overline{e}\operatorname{div}Q

is the spontaneous polarization to the leading order
[
21
]
. Defining the constants
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{0}\overline{\varepsilon}
,
\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon_{0}\Delta\varepsilon^{\ast}
, and
\varepsilon_{3}=\overline{e}
, we can rewrite
D
as

D=\varepsilon_{1}\nabla u+\varepsilon_{2}Q\nabla u+\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}Q,

so with (
1.2
) we obtain

(1.3)

\operatorname{div}(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla u+\varepsilon_{2}Q\nabla u+\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}Q)=0.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\varepsilon_{1}>0
in the following.
Then the electrostatic energy density is given by

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{e}(Q)&=-\int(\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon E+P_{s})\cdot\,d{E}=-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{0}(\varepsilon E)\cdot E-P_{s}\cdot E\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{0}(\Delta\varepsilon^{\ast}Q+\overline{\varepsilon}I)\nabla u\cdot\nabla u-\overline{e}\operatorname{div}(Q)\cdot\nabla u\\
&=-\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{2}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{2}\nabla u^{\top}Q\nabla u-\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}(Q)\cdot\nabla u.\end{split}

Then (
1.1
) becomes

Q_{t}=M\left(L\Delta Q-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{B}(Q)}{\partial Q}+\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{2}\left[\nabla u\nabla u^{\top}-\frac{1}{d}|\nabla u|^{2}I\right]-\varepsilon_{3}\left[\nabla^{2}u-\frac{1}{d}\Delta uI\right]\right),

and the coupled system for the Q-tensor and the electric field with (
1.3
) becomes

(1.4a)

\displaystyle Q_{t}

\displaystyle=M\left(L\Delta Q-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{B}(Q)}{\partial Q}+\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{2}\left[\nabla u\nabla u^{\top}-\frac{1}{d}|\nabla u|^{2}I\right]-\varepsilon_{3}\left[\nabla^{2}u-\frac{1}{d}\Delta uI\right]\right),

(1.4b)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\operatorname{div}(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla u+\varepsilon_{2}Q\nabla u+\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}Q),

with suitable boundary conditions. The elliptic equation in (
1.4b
) is only coercive (and thus solvable given
Q
) if

(1.5)

|\varepsilon_{1}|>|\varepsilon_{2}||Q|,

for almost every
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
. From a physical perspective, the eigenvalues of
Q
should be in the range
[-1/d,2/d]
(see, for example, the derivation of the
Q
-tensor model in the book by Virga
[
29
]
). If this is indeed the case, then under the condition
d|\varepsilon_{1}|>2|\varepsilon_{2}|
, the elliptic equation (
1.4b
) is coercive and solvable.
For the pure gradient flow (without electric field), it has been shown for smooth solutions that if the eigenvalues of the Q-tensor are initially within a certain range, this is preserved for the time evolution
[
32
,
8
]
. Those results also apply in the more general case of the Beris-Edwards system (liquid crystal Q-tensor coupled to fluid flow). However, it is unknown whether similar estimates can be proved for the system (
1.4
), and in fact, it is not even clear that
Q
would remain bounded. Our numerical experiments in Section
4
indicate that
Q
may not stay within the range
[-1/d,2/d]
in some cases and may grow without bound.
Thus it is unclear whether (
1.4
) is a practical model for liquid crystal dynamics under the influence of an electric field. Therefore, to ensure well-posedness of our problem, we introduce the truncation operator
\mathcal{T}_{R}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
defined by

(1.6)

\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)_{ij}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{\epsilon}(Q_{ij}-y)\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{R}(y)\,dy,

where
\varphi_{\epsilon}
is a standard mollifier and

(1.7)

\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{R}(y)=\begin{cases}\frac{R}{d}\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}&\text{if }|y|>R/d-\epsilon\\
y&\text{if }|y|\leq R/d-\epsilon.\end{cases}

Note that
\mathcal{T}_{R}
is smooth and
\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)_{ij}=\pm R/d
if
\lvert Q_{ij}\rvert>R/d
. Also note that the derivative of
\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)_{ij}
is bounded between
0
and
1
.
\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)
is a local operator with respect to its argument
Q
. Now we obtain the modified elliptic equation

(1.8)

\operatorname{div}(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla u+\varepsilon_{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\nabla u+\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}Q)=0

and the modified electrostatic energy density

(1.9)

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{e}(Q)=-\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{2}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{2}\nabla u^{\top}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\nabla u-\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}(Q)\cdot\nabla u,

where
R>0
is some parameter. Note that it follows from the definition that
|\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)|_{F}\leq R
. This way, we can ensure that the elliptic equation remains solvable under constraints on
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
that depend on
R
.

Now to determine the evolution PDE for the Q-tensor, we compute the variational derivative of this modified electrostatic energy,

\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{e}(Q)\,dx.

Letting
\phi:\Omega\times[0,T)\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be smooth and compactly supported in
\Omega
, we compute

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{d\delta}\right\lvert_{\delta=0}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{e}(Q+\delta\phi)\,dx

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{d}{d\delta}\right\lvert_{\delta=0}\int_{\Omega}\left(-\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{2}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{2}\nabla u^{\top}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q+\delta\phi)\nabla u-\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}(Q+\delta\phi)\cdot\nabla u\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(-\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{2}\nabla u^{\top}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)}{\partial Q}\odot\phi\right)\nabla u-\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}(\phi)\cdot\nabla u\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(-\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)}{\partial Q}\odot\nabla u\nabla u^{\top}\right):\phi+\varepsilon_{3}\nabla^{2}u:\phi\right)\,dx.

Here we denoted by
\odot
the Hadamard product
(A\odot B)_{ij}=A_{ij}B_{ij}
.
We will also denote
\mathcal{P}(Q):=\frac{\partial\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)}{\partial Q}
. Note that
\mathcal{P}
depends on the parameter
R
which is omitted in the notation for simplicity.
Thus, the complete system of equations, along with boundary conditions, is given by

(1.10a)

\displaystyle Q_{t}=M\left(L\Delta Q-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{B}(Q)}{\partial Q}+\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{2}\left[\mathcal{P}(Q)\odot\nabla u\nabla u^{\top}-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathcal{P}(Q)\odot\nabla u\nabla u^{\top}\right)I\right]\right.\hskip 21.52771pt

\displaystyle\left.-\varepsilon_{3}\left[\nabla^{2}u-\frac{1}{d}\Delta uI\right]\right)

(1.10b)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla u+\varepsilon_{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\nabla u+\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}(Q))=0

(1.10c)

\displaystyle u=g\text{ on }\partial\Omega

(1.10d)

\displaystyle Q=q\text{ on }\partial\Omega.

Here
g=g(t,x)
and
q=q(x)
are the Dirichlet boundary conditions for
u
and
Q
respectively.
The elliptic equation (
1.10b
) can be derived as the variational derivative of the electrostatic energy with respect to
u
.

To the best of our knowledge, existence and uniqueness of solutions for this system is unknown. Due to the nonlinearities, smooth solutions may not be expected in general. We therefore proceed to define weak solutions: Fix
T>0
and assume that
g
admits an extension
\tilde{g}:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
with
\tilde{g}\in L^{\infty}([0,T];H^{1}(\Omega))\cap W^{1,2}([0,T];H^{1}(\Omega))
. Further assume that
q
admits an extension
\tilde{q}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with
\tilde{q}\in(H^{1}(\Omega))^{d\times d}
which is trace-free and symmetric. Also, assume that
Q_{0}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\in(H^{1}(\Omega))^{d\times d}
takes values in the symmetric, trace-free
d\times d
matrices. Denote

(A)_{S}:=\frac{A+A^{\top}}{2}

the symmetric part of a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Then we define weak solutions as follows:

Definition 1.1
.

A pair
(Q,u)
with
Q:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
trace-free and symmetric and
u:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is called a weak solution of (
1.10
) if

\tilde{Q}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)),\quad\tilde{Q}_{t}\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega),\quad\tilde{u}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)),

with
Q=\tilde{Q}+\tilde{q}
and
u=\tilde{u}+\tilde{g}
, and

(1.11a)

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}Q:\Phi_{t}\,dx\,dt+\int_{\Omega}Q_{0}(x):\Phi(0,x)\,dx\\
&=ML\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla Q:\nabla\Phi\,dx\,dt+M\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{B}(Q)}{\partial Q}:\Phi\,dx\,dt\\
&\hskip 43.05542pt-\frac{M\varepsilon_{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{P}(Q)\odot\nabla u\nabla u^{\top}-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{P}(Q)\odot\nabla u\nabla u^{\top})I\right):\Phi\,dx\,dt,\\
&\hskip 43.05542pt-M\varepsilon_{3}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla u\cdot\operatorname{div}((\Phi)_{S})-\frac{1}{d}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\tr\Phi\right)\,dx\,dt\\
\end{split}

(1.11b)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla u+\varepsilon_{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\nabla u+\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}Q)\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx\,dt=0

for all smooth
\Phi=(\Phi_{ij})_{ij=1}^{d}:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\psi:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
which are compactly supported within
[0,T)\times\Omega
.

In this work, we will construct a fully discrete numerical scheme based on a finite element discretization in space for system (
1.10
). We will prove uniform stability with respect to the discretization parameters and in the case
\varepsilon_{3}=0
(effect of polarization is zero), convergence to a weak solution as in Definition
1.1
as the discretization parameters vanish. Thus, the convergence proof also implies the existence of a weak solution to (
1.10
) when
\varepsilon_{3}=0
. The case with nonzero polarization introduces additional mathematical challenges and we therefore leave it for future research.

Related works

There have been a couple of numerical works investigating the dynamics of liquid crystals under the influence of external fields. Most rigorous results with error and/or convergence analyses consider equilibrium situations or explicit solutions in simplified situations, e.g.,
[

,
2
,

,

,

,

]
. Most works for nonequilibrium situations are mainly experimental, without stability or convergence guarantees,
[

,

,

,

,

,

]
.
An exception is
[

]
, however in that model, the electric field is given and not computed through a PDE like in (
1.10
), and in
[
30
]
, a convergent numerical scheme for the director field model by Oseen and Frank with an elliptic equation for the electric field was designed and analyzed.

1.3.
Outline

The structure of this article is as follows: In Section
2
, we present the numerical scheme for (
1.10
) and prove basic properties such as well-posedness, energy-stability, and preservation of the symmetry and trace-free constraint of
Q
at the discrete level. In Section
3
, we show that the scheme converges up to a subsequence to a weak solution of (
1.10
) when
\varepsilon_{3}=0
. Then in the last section, Section
4
, we present numerical experiments for the scheme. We illustrate that numerically, the truncation operator
\mathcal{T}_{R}
is necessary for certain boundary conditions for the elliptic equation, since in this case the norms of the approximations of
Q
grow over time without bound, which would cause the elliptic equation to become ill-posed. One of the experiments also shows that with this model, it is possible to simulate the Fréedericksz transition
[
12
]
, a phase transition commonly observed in liquid crystals that interact with electric or magnetic fields.

2.
The numerical scheme

We start by introducing the numerical scheme to discretize (
1.10
). To obtain a suitable time discretization, we will use convex splitting for the bulk potential.

2.1.
Convex Splitting

We rewrite the Landau-de Gennes bulk potential
\mathcal{F}_{B}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\mathcal{F}_{B}(Q)=\frac{a}{2}\langle Q,Q\rangle_{F}-\frac{b}{3}\langle Q^{2},Q\rangle_{F}+\frac{c}{4}(\langle Q,Q\rangle_{F})^{2}+A_{0},

where we used
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{F}
to denote the Frobenius inner product:
\langle A,B\rangle_{F}:=\tr(A^{\top}B)
.
When
Q
is symmetric, this is equal to

\mathcal{F}_{B}(Q)=\frac{a}{2}\tr(Q^{2})-\frac{b}{3}\tr(Q^{3})+\frac{c}{4}(\tr(Q^{2}))^{2}+A_{0}.

The following lemma is similar to Lemma 2.4 in
[
33
]
. We modify its proof here to make its statement more precise.

Lemma 2.1

(Convex Splitting of
\mathcal{F}_{B}
)
.

We can write this bulk potential as

\mathcal{F}_{B}(Q)=\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q)-\mathcal{F}_{2}(Q),

where

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q)&=\frac{\beta_{1}}{2}\langle Q,Q\rangle_{F}-\frac{b}{3}\langle Q^{2},Q\rangle_{F}+\frac{\beta_{2}}{4}(\langle Q,Q\rangle_{F})^{2}+A_{0},\\
\mathcal{F}_{2}(Q)&=\frac{\beta_{1}-a}{2}\langle Q,Q\rangle_{F}+\frac{\beta_{2}-c}{4}(\langle Q,Q\rangle_{F})^{2}.\end{split}

If
\beta_{1}\geq\max\{|b|,a\}
and
\beta_{2}\geq\max\{|b|,c\}
, then
\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q)
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}(Q)
are convex functions.

Proof.

We begin by showing that
\mathcal{F}_{2}
is convex by showing its Hessian is positive semidefinite. In what follows, we will use Einstein summation convention. We have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{2}}{\partial Q_{ij}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{1}-a}{2}(\langle Q,E_{ij}\rangle_{F}+\langle E_{ij},Q\rangle_{F})+\frac{\beta_{2}-c}{2}\langle Q,Q\rangle_{F}(\langle Q,E_{ij}\rangle_{F}+\langle E_{ij},Q\rangle_{F})

\displaystyle=(\beta_{1}-a)Q_{ij}+(\beta_{2}-c)Q_{ks}Q_{ks}Q_{ij}.

Thus we have the entries of the Hessian are given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}_{2}}{\partial Q_{mn}\partial Q_{ij}}

\displaystyle=(\beta_{1}-a)\delta_{im}\delta_{jn}+(\beta_{2}-c)(2\delta_{km}\delta_{sn}Q_{ks}Q_{ij}+\delta_{im}\delta_{jn}Q_{ks}Q_{ks})

\displaystyle=(\beta_{1}-a)\delta_{im}\delta_{jn}+(\beta_{2}-c)(2Q_{mn}Q_{ij}+\delta_{im}\delta_{jn}Q_{ks}Q_{ks}).

Multiplying this expression by
x_{mn}
and
x_{ij}
and summing over
i,j,m,n
, we have

\displaystyle x_{mn}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}_{2}}{\partial Q_{mn}\partial Q_{ij}}x_{ij}

\displaystyle=(\beta_{1}-a)x_{mn}x_{mn}+(\beta_{2}-c)(2Q_{mn}x_{mn}Q_{ij}x_{ij}+x_{mn}x_{mn}Q_{ks}Q_{ks})\geq 0,

where in the last inequality we have used
\beta_{1}\geq a
and
\beta_{2}\geq c
. This shows that
\mathcal{F}_{2}(Q)
is convex. Now we show that
\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q)
is convex. We begin by computing

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}}{\partial Q_{ij}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{1}}{2}(\langle E_{ij},Q\rangle_{F}+\langle Q,E_{ij}\rangle_{F})-\frac{b}{3}\left(\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial Q_{ij}}Q^{2},Q\right\rangle_{F}+\langle Q^{2},E_{ij}\rangle_{F}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 43.05542pt+\frac{\beta_{2}}{2}\langle Q,Q\rangle_{F}(\langle E_{ij},Q\rangle_{F}+\langle Q,E_{ij}\rangle_{F})

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}Q_{ij}-\frac{b}{3}(Q_{ik}Q_{jk}+Q_{ki}Q_{kj}+Q_{ik}Q_{kj})+\beta_{2}Q_{ks}Q_{ks}Q_{ij},

where we have used

\frac{\partial(Q^{2})_{ks}}{\partial Q_{ij}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial Q_{ij}}Q_{k\ell}Q_{\ell s}=\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}Q_{\ell s}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{js}Q_{k\ell}=\delta_{ik}Q_{js}+\delta_{js}Q_{ki}

to simplify the last line. Now computing the entries of the Hessian, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}_{1}}{\partial Q_{mn}\partial Q_{ij}}

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}\delta_{im}\delta_{jn}-\frac{b}{3}\bigg(\delta_{im}\delta_{kn}Q_{jk}+\delta_{jm}\delta_{kn}Q_{ik}+\delta_{km}\delta_{in}Q_{kj}\bigg.

\displaystyle\hskip 21.52771pt\bigg.+\delta_{km}\delta_{jn}Q_{ki}+\delta_{im}\delta_{kn}Q_{kj}+\delta_{km}\delta_{jn}Q_{ik}\bigg)+\beta_{2}(2\delta_{km}\delta_{sn}Q_{ks}Q_{ij}+\delta_{im}\delta_{jn}Q_{ks}Q_{ks})

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}\delta_{im}\delta_{jn}-\frac{b}{3}(\delta_{im}Q_{jn}+\delta_{jm}Q_{in}+\delta_{in}Q_{mj}+\delta_{jn}Q_{mi}+\delta_{im}Q_{nj}+\delta_{jn}Q_{im})

\displaystyle\hskip 21.52771pt+\beta_{2}(2Q_{mn}Q_{ij}+\delta_{im}\delta_{jn}Q_{ks}Q_{ks}).

Multiplying this by
x_{mn}
and
x_{ij}
and summing over
i,j,m,n
gives

\displaystyle x_{mn}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}_{1}}{\partial Q_{mn}\partial Q_{ij}}x_{ij}

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}x_{mn}x_{mn}-\frac{b}{3}\bigg(x_{mn}x_{mj}Q_{jn}+x_{mn}x_{im}Q_{in}+x_{mn}x_{nj}Q_{mj}\bigg.

\displaystyle\hskip 86.11084pt+\bigg.x_{mn}x_{in}Q_{mi}+x_{mn}x_{mj}Q_{nj}+x_{mn}x_{in}Q_{im}\bigg)

\displaystyle\hskip 21.52771pt+\beta_{2}(2Q_{mn}x_{mn}Q_{ij}x_{ij}+x_{mn}x_{mn}Q_{ks}Q_{ks}).

Note that

\displaystyle x_{mn}Q_{jn}x_{mj}=\sum_{m}\sum_{n}\sum_{j}x_{mn}Q_{jn}x_{mj}=\sum_{j,n}Q_{jn}\sum_{m}x_{mn}x_{mj}=\sum_{j,n}Q_{jn}B_{jn}\leq\rvert Q\lvert_{F}\lvert B\rvert_{F},

where
B_{jn}:=\sum_{m}x_{mn}x_{mj}
and where we have applied the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality once more for
B
, we have

\displaystyle\lvert B\rvert_{F}=\left(\sum_{j,n}B_{jn}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{j,n}\left(\sum_{m_{1}}x_{m_{1}n}^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{m_{2}}x_{m_{2}j}^{2}\right)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{n,m_{1}}x_{m_{1}n}^{2}\sum_{j,m_{2}}x_{m_{2}j}^{2}\right)^{1/2}=\sum_{m,n}x_{mn}^{2}=x_{mn}x_{mn},

and thus we have

\lvert x_{mn}Q_{jn}x_{mj}\rvert\leq\lvert Q\rvert_{F}x_{mn}x_{mn}.

We can similarly bound the other terms of this form by
\lvert Q\rvert_{F}x_{mn}x_{mn}
. Thus, using this bound and dropping the term
2Q_{mn}x_{mn}Q_{ij}x_{ij}\geq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle x_{mn}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{F}_{1}}{\partial Q_{mn}\partial Q_{ij}}x_{ij}

\displaystyle\geq\beta_{1}x_{mn}x_{mn}-2\lvert b\rvert\lvert Q\rvert_{F}x_{mn}x_{mn}+\beta_{2}x_{mn}x_{mn}\lvert Q\rvert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\lvert b\rvert x_{mn}x_{mn}(1-\lvert Q\rvert_{F})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

since
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\geq\lvert b\rvert
. This shows that
\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q)
is convex, as desired.
∎

2.2.
Definition of the Numerical Scheme

Now we are in a position to define the numerical scheme. To discretize in time, we will use a fully implicit discretization and for the spatial discretization, we will use finite elements. We let
\Delta t>0
and denote
t^{n}:=n\Delta t
. Also,
N\in\mathbb{N}
is such that
T=t^{N}
for a given final time of computation
T>0
. Then we let
\mathfrak{T}_{h}=\{K_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
be a quasi-uniform triangulation of
\Omega
with mesh size
h
. Each
K
is a closed subset of
\Omega
. We assume for simplicity that
\Omega
is a convex polygon/polyhedron so that we do not need to deal with the approximation of curved boundaries. When
\Omega
is 2-dimensional, each
K
is a triangle, while when
\Omega
is 3-dimensional they are tetrahedrons. We define the spaces

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{h}

\displaystyle=\{f_{h}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}):\left.(f_{h})_{ij}\right\rvert_{K}\in P_{1}(K)\text{ for every }K\in\mathfrak{T}_{h},\,1\leq i,j\leq d\},

(2.1b)

\displaystyle\mathbb{Y}_{h}

\displaystyle=\{v_{h}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega):\left.v_{h}\right\rvert_{K}\in P_{1}(K)\text{ for every }K\in\mathfrak{T}_{h}\}.

We let
\tilde{g}^{n}=\tilde{g}(\Delta tn)
and denote by
\tilde{g}_{h}^{n}
the projection of the extension of the boundary data
\tilde{g}^{n}
onto the subspace of
H^{1}(\Omega)
which is piecewise linear on each
K\in\mathfrak{T}_{h}
. We analogously define
\tilde{q}_{h}
to be the projection of the extension of the boundary data
\tilde{q}
onto the subspace of
(H^{1}(\Omega))^{d\times d}
which is piecewise linear on each
K\in\mathfrak{T}_{h}
. Now the fully discrete formulation of the numerical scheme is as follows:

Find
\tilde{Q}_{h}^{n}\in\mathbb{X}_{h}
and
\tilde{u}_{h}^{n}\in\mathbb{Y}_{h}
for
n=1,\dots,N
such that

(2.2a)

\begin{split}-\int_{\Omega}D_{t}^{+}Q_{h}^{n}:\Phi_{h}\,dx&=ML\int_{\Omega}\nabla Q_{h}^{n+1/2}:\nabla\Phi_{h}\,dx+M\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q_{h}^{n+1})}{\partial Q}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{2}(Q_{h}^{n})}{\partial Q}\right):\Phi_{h}\,dx\\
&-\frac{M\varepsilon_{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left((\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{h}^{n}\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla u_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})_{S}-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{h}^{n}\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla u_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})I\right):\Phi_{h}\,dx\\
&-M\varepsilon_{3}\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla u_{h}^{n+1/2}\cdot\operatorname{div}((\Phi_{h})_{S})-\frac{1}{d}\nabla u_{h}^{n+1/2}\cdot\nabla\operatorname{tr}\Phi_{h}\right)\,dx,\end{split}

(2.2b)

\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla u_{h}^{n}+\varepsilon_{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}^{n})\nabla u_{h}^{n}+\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}(Q_{h}^{n}))\cdot\nabla\psi_{h}\,dx=0

for all
\Phi_{h}\in\mathbb{X}_{h}
and
\psi_{h}\in\mathbb{Y}_{h}
, where
Q_{h}^{n}=\tilde{Q}_{h}^{n}+\tilde{q}_{h}
and
u_{h}^{n}=\tilde{u}_{h}^{n}+\tilde{g}_{h}^{n}
. Here
D_{t}^{+}
is the discrete time derivative defined by

D_{t}^{+}Q_{h}^{n}=\frac{Q_{h}^{n+1}-Q_{h}^{n}}{\Delta t},

and the superscript
n+1/2
is used to denote the average of approximations at time steps
n
and
n+1
:

Q^{n+1/2}_{h}=\frac{Q^{n}_{h}+Q^{n+1}_{h}}{2},\quad u^{n+1/2}_{h}=\frac{u^{n}_{h}+u^{n+1}_{h}}{2},

and
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{h}^{n}
is understood as
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{h}^{n}=\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}^{n+1},Q_{h}^{n})
, where

(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{h}^{n})_{ij}=\begin{cases}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q^{n+1}_{h})_{ij}-\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}^{n})_{ij}}{(Q^{n+1}_{h})_{ij}-(Q^{n}_{h})_{ij}}&\text{if }(Q_{h}^{n+1})_{ij}-(Q^{n}_{h})_{ij}\neq 0\\
\frac{\partial\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}^{n})_{ij}}{\partial Q}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We will sometimes make the dependence of
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{n}_{h}
on
Q^{n+1}_{h}
and
Q^{n}_{h}
more explicit by instead writing
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q^{n+1}_{h},Q^{n}_{h})
.
Before proving stability and well-posedness of the above scheme, we will show that it preserves the trace-free and symmetry properties of
Q_{h}^{n}
. To do so, we require the map
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{X}_{h}\times\mathbb{Y}_{h}\to\mathbb{X}_{h}\times\mathbb{Y}_{h}
,
\hat{x}=\mathcal{L}x

\{\tilde{Q}_{h}^{n+1},\tilde{u}_{h}^{n+1}\}\xmapsto{\mathcal{L}}\{\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1},\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1}\},

where the quantities with hats solve

(2.3a)

\begin{split}&-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1}+\tilde{q}_{h}-Q_{h}^{n}}{\Delta t}:\Phi_{h}\,dx\\
&=ML\int_{\Omega}\frac{\nabla\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1}+\nabla\tilde{q}_{h}+\nabla Q_{h}^{n}}{2}:\nabla\Phi_{h}\,dx+M\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q_{h}^{n+1})}{\partial Q}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{2}(Q_{h}^{n})}{\partial Q}\right):\Phi_{h}\,dx\\
&-\frac{M\varepsilon_{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left((\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}^{n+1},Q_{h}^{n})\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})_{S}-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}^{n+1},Q_{h}^{n})\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})I\right):\Phi_{h}\,dx\\
&-\frac{M\varepsilon_{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left((\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}^{n+1},Q_{h}^{n})\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})_{S}-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}^{n+1},Q_{h}^{n})\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})I\right):\Phi_{h}\,dx\\
&-M\varepsilon_{3}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1}+\nabla u_{h}^{n}}{2}\cdot\operatorname{div}((\Phi_{h})_{S})-\frac{1}{d}\frac{\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1}+\nabla u^{n}}{2}\cdot\nabla\operatorname{tr}\Phi_{h}\right)\,dx\\
&-\frac{M\varepsilon_{3}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{n+1}\cdot\operatorname{div}((\Phi_{h})_{S})-\frac{1}{d}\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{n+1}\cdot\nabla\operatorname{tr}\Phi_{h}\right),\end{split}

(2.3b)

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1}+\varepsilon_{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}^{n+1})\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1})\cdot\nabla\psi_{h}\,dx\\
&=-\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{n+1}+\varepsilon_{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}^{n+1})\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{n+1}+\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}(Q_{h}^{n+1}))\cdot\nabla\psi_{h}\,dx\end{split}

for all
\Phi_{h}\in\mathbb{X}_{h}
and
\psi_{h}\in\mathbb{Y}_{h}
, where
Q_{h}^{n+1}=\tilde{Q}_{h}^{n+1}+\tilde{q}_{h}
.

We will prove that this map is well defined in Theorem
2.7
, but first we will use the map to prove the following result, which implies a symmetry and trace-free preservation property of scheme (
2.2
) needed for proving solvability of the scheme:

Theorem 2.2

(Trace-Free and Symmetry Preservation Generalized)
.

If
Q_{h}^{n}
is trace-free and symmetric and
\lambda\in(0,1]
, we have that every
\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1}
which satisfies
\lambda^{-1}\widehat{x}=\mathcal{L}\widehat{x}
is trace-free and symmetric. In particular, for
\lambda=1
, this shows that solutions
Q_{h}^{n+1}
to (
2.2
) are trace-free and symmetric.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in(0,1]
and let
\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1}
satisfy
\lambda^{-1}\widehat{x}=\mathcal{L}\widehat{x}
. We begin by showing that
\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1}
is symmetric. Take
\Phi_{h}=\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1}-(\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top}:=V_{h}^{n+1}
in (
2.3a
). Note that
V_{h}^{n+1}
is skew-symmetric. Writing
\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1}=\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1}+\tilde{q}_{h}
, it follows that

\int_{\Omega}\left((\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1},Q_{h}^{n})\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})_{S}-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1},Q_{h}^{n})\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})I\right):V_{h}^{n+1}\,dx=0

since
(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1},Q_{h}^{n})\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})_{S}-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1},Q_{h}^{n})\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})I
is symmetric and
V_{h}^{n+1}
is skew-symmetric. For the same reason,

\int_{\Omega}\left((\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1},Q_{h}^{n})\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})_{S}-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1},Q_{h}^{n})\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})I\right):V_{h}^{n+1}\,dx=0.

Furthermore, we have

\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1}+\nabla u_{h}^{n}}{2}\cdot\operatorname{div}((V_{h}^{n+1})_{S})-\frac{1}{d}\frac{\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1}+\nabla u_{h}^{n}}{2}\cdot\nabla\operatorname{tr}V_{h}^{n+1}\right)\,dx=0

and

\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{n+1}\cdot\operatorname{div}((V_{h}^{n+1})_{S})-\frac{1}{d}\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{n+1}\cdot\nabla\operatorname{tr}V_{h}^{n+1}\right)\,dx=0

since
(V_{h}^{n+1})_{S}=0
and
\operatorname{tr}V_{h}^{n+1}=0
. We also have

\int_{\Omega}\tilde{q}_{h}:V_{h}^{n+1}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{q}_{h}:\nabla V_{h}^{n+1}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}Q_{h}^{n}:V_{h}^{n+1}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\nabla Q_{h}^{n}:\nabla V_{h}^{n+1}\,dx=0

because
\tilde{q}_{h}
and
Q_{h}^{n}
are symmetric and
V_{h}^{n+1}
is skew-symmetric. Using these facts again gives

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{2}(Q_{h}^{n})}{\partial Q}:V_{h}^{n+1}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\left((\beta_{1}-a)Q_{h}^{n}+(\beta_{2}-c)\operatorname{tr}((Q_{h}^{n})^{2})Q_{h}^{n}\right):V_{h}^{n+1}\,dx=0.

Thus we have

(2.4)

\frac{1}{\lambda\Delta t}\int_{\Omega}\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1}:V_{h}^{n+1}\,dx+\frac{ML}{2\lambda}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1}:\nabla V_{h}^{n+1}\,dx+M\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})}{\partial Q}:V_{h}^{n+1}\,dx=0.

Now because
V_{h}^{n+1}
is skew-symmetric and
\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})}{\partial Q}\right)^{\top}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}((\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})}{\partial Q}
, we also have

(2.5)

-\frac{1}{\lambda\Delta t}\int_{\Omega}(\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top}:V_{h}^{n+1}\,dx-\frac{ML}{2\lambda}\int_{\Omega}\nabla(\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top}:\nabla V_{h}^{n+1}\,dx-M\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}((\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})}{\partial Q}:V_{h}^{n+1}\,dx=0.

Adding the previous two equations together gives

(2.6)

\frac{1}{\lambda\Delta t}\|V_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}+\frac{ML}{2\lambda}\|\nabla V_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}+M\left\langle\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})}{\partial Q}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}((\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})}{\partial Q},\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1}-(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top}\right\rangle=0,

where we have used
V_{h}^{n+1}=\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1}-(\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top}=\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1}-(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top}
since
\tilde{q}_{h}^{n+1}
is symmetric.
And since
\mathcal{F}_{1}
is convex, it follows that

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})}{\partial Q}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}((\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})}{\partial Q},\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1}-(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})}{\partial Q},\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1}-(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top}\right\rangle-\left\langle\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}((\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})}{\partial Q},\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1}-(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq-(\mathcal{F}_{1}((\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})-\mathcal{F}_{1}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1}))-(\mathcal{F}_{1}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})-\mathcal{F}_{1}((\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{\top}))

\displaystyle=0.

Thus

\frac{1}{\lambda\Delta t}\|V_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}+\frac{ML}{2\lambda}\|\nabla V_{h}^{n+1}\|^{2}\leq 0,

which implies that
V_{h}^{n+1}\equiv 0
so that
\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1}
is symmetric.

We now show that
\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1}
is trace-free. Taking
\Phi_{h}=\operatorname{tr}(\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1})I
as a test function in (
2.3a
), we have

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\lambda\Delta t}\int_{\Omega}\lvert\operatorname{tr}\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1}\rvert^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\hskip 21.52771pt=ML\int_{\Omega}\frac{\lambda^{-1}\nabla\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1}+\nabla\tilde{q}_{h}+\nabla Q_{h}^{n}}{2}:\nabla(\operatorname{tr}(\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1})I)\,dx+M\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})}{\partial Q}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{2}(Q_{h}^{n})}{\partial Q}\right):\operatorname{tr}(\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1})I\,dx

\displaystyle\hskip 21.52771pt=\frac{ML}{2\lambda}\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla\operatorname{tr}(\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1})\rvert^{2}\,dx+M\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})}{\partial Q}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{2}(Q_{h}^{n})}{\partial Q}\right):\operatorname{tr}(\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1})I\,dx,

where for the last equality we used
\nabla A:\nabla(\operatorname{tr}(B)I)=\nabla\operatorname{tr}(A)\cdot\nabla\operatorname{tr}(B)
and the fact that
\tilde{q}_{h}
and
Q_{h}^{n}
are trace-free. The remaining terms involving
\nabla u^{n}_{h}
and
\nabla u^{n+1}_{h}
cancel out due to the structure of the test function. Then computing the second integral above, we have

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{2}(Q_{h}^{n})}{\partial Q}:\operatorname{tr}(\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1})I\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\left((\beta_{1}-a)Q_{h}^{n}+(\beta_{2}-c)\operatorname{tr}((Q_{h}^{n})^{2})Q_{h}^{n}\right):\operatorname{tr}(\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1})I\,dx=0

since
\operatorname{tr}(Q_{h}^{n})=0
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})}{\partial Q}:\operatorname{tr}(\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1})I\,dx

\displaystyle\hskip 21.52771pt=\int_{\Omega}\left(\beta_{1}\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1}-b((\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{2}-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}((\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{2})I)+\beta_{2}\operatorname{tr}((\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{2})\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1}\right):\operatorname{tr}(\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1})I\,dx

\displaystyle\hskip 21.52771pt=\int_{\Omega}\left(\beta_{1}\lvert\operatorname{tr}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})\rvert^{2}+\beta_{2}\operatorname{tr}((\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{2})\lvert\operatorname{tr}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})\rvert^{2}\right)\,dx,

where we have used
\operatorname{tr}(\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1})=\operatorname{tr}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})
since
\operatorname{tr}(\tilde{q}_{h})=0
. Using this fact again, it follows that

\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda\Delta t}+M\beta_{1}+M\beta_{2}\operatorname{tr}((\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{2})\right)\lvert\operatorname{tr}(\overline{Q}^{n+1})\rvert^{2}\,dx+\frac{ML}{2\lambda}\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla\operatorname{tr}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})\rvert^{2}\,dx=0.

Now since
\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1}
is symmetric, we have that
\operatorname{tr}((\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})^{2})=\lvert\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1}\rvert_{F}^{2}\geq 0
. We also have
\lambda,\beta_{1},\beta_{2}>0
. Thus,

\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{\Omega}\lvert\operatorname{tr}(\overline{Q}_{h}^{n+1})\rvert^{2}\,dx=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{\Omega}\lvert\operatorname{tr}(\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1})\rvert^{2}\,dx\leq 0,

which shows that
\operatorname{tr}(\widehat{Q}_{h}^{n+1})\equiv 0
, as desired.
∎

Note that it follows from this result that
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{n}_{h}
is also symmetric. Next, we derive a uniform energy estimate for the scheme.

Theorem 2.3

(Energy Stability)
.

Assume that
\Delta t<\min\{\frac{L}{2d|\varepsilon_{3}|},\frac{\varepsilon_{1}-|\varepsilon_{2}|R}{3(\varepsilon_{1}+|\varepsilon_{2}|R^{2})}\}
and
\varepsilon_{1}>|\varepsilon_{2}|R
. Then the approximations defined by the scheme (
2.2
) satisfy for all
N\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle M\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{F}_{B}(Q_{h}^{N})\,dx+\frac{1}{4}\left(ML-2d\Delta tM|\varepsilon_{3}|\right)\|\nabla Q_{h}^{N}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\frac{M}{6}\left(\varepsilon_{1}-|\varepsilon_{2}|R\right)\|\nabla u_{h}^{N}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+t^{N}\operatorname{exp}(Ct^{N}))\left(\|\nabla Q_{h}^{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\mathcal{F}_{B}(Q_{h}^{0})+(1+R)\|\nabla u_{h}^{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+C(1+t^{N}\operatorname{exp}(Ct^{N}))\Delta t\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\|D_{t}^{+}\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\|\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{N}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\operatorname{exp}(Ct^{N})\Delta t\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\|\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

for some constant
C>0
not depending on
\Delta t
or
h
.

Proof.

Averaging (
2.2b
) at times
n
and
n+1
gives

(2.7)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla u_{h}^{n+1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{2}(\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q^{n+1}_{h})\nabla u_{h}^{n+1}+\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}^{n})\nabla u_{h}^{n})+\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}(Q^{n+1/2}_{h}))\cdot\nabla\psi_{h}\,dx=0.\end{split}

Recall that
\tilde{u}_{h}^{n}=u_{h}^{n}-\tilde{g}_{h}^{n}
. Then
\tilde{u}^{n}_{h}=0
on
\partial\Omega\times[0,T]
. Take
\psi_{h}=D_{t}^{+}\tilde{u}^{n}_{h}
to get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla u^{n+1/2}_{h}\cdot D_{t}^{+}\nabla u_{h}^{n}-\varepsilon_{1}\nabla u_{h}^{n+1/2}\cdot D_{t}^{+}\nabla\tilde{g}^{n}_{h}+\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{2}(\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}^{n+1})\nabla u_{h}^{n+1}+\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}^{n})\nabla u^{n}_{h})\cdot D_{t}^{+}\nabla u_{h}^{n}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 43.05542pt-\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{2}D_{t}^{+}(\nabla\tilde{g}^{n}_{h})\cdot(\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q^{n+1}_{h})\nabla u_{h}^{n+1}+\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}^{n})\nabla u_{h}^{n})

\displaystyle\hskip 64.58313pt\Big.+\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}(Q_{h}^{n+1/2})\cdot D_{t}^{+}\nabla u^{n}_{h}-\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}(Q_{h}^{n+1/2})\cdot D_{t}^{+}\nabla\tilde{g}^{n}_{h}\Big)\,dx

so that

(2.8)

\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{2}D_{t}^{+}|\nabla u^{n}_{h}|^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{2}(\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}^{n+1})\nabla u_{h}^{n+1}+\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}^{n})\nabla u^{n}_{h})\cdot D_{t}^{+}\nabla u_{h}^{n}+\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}(Q_{h}^{n+1/2})\cdot D_{t}^{+}\nabla u_{h}^{n}\right)\,dx\\
=\int_{\Omega}\left(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla u_{h}^{n+1/2}\cdot D_{t}^{+}\nabla\tilde{g}^{n}_{h}+\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{2}(\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}^{n+1})\nabla u_{h}^{n+1}+\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q^{n}_{h})\nabla u_{h}^{n})\cdot D_{t}^{+}\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{n}+\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}(Q_{h}^{n+1/2})\cdot D_{t}^{+}\nabla\tilde{g}^{n}_{h}\right)\,dx.

Now take
\Phi_{h}=D_{t}^{+}Q^{n}_{h}
, which is zero on the boundary, as a test function in (
2.2a
) and note that
(D_{t}^{+}Q^{n}_{h})_{S}=D_{t}^{+}Q^{n}_{h}
since
Q^{n}_{h}
and
Q_{h}^{n+1}
are symmetric. Symmetry also implies that
(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{n}_{h}\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla u_{h}^{n+1})^{\top})_{S}:D_{t}^{+}Q_{h}^{n}=\tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{n}_{h}\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla u_{h}^{n+1})^{\top}:D_{t}^{+}Q^{n}_{h}
. Now use convexity of
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}
and use that
D_{t}^{+}Q^{n}_{h}
is trace-free to get

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}|D_{t}^{+}Q^{n}_{h}|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}\frac{ML}{2}D_{t}^{+}|\nabla Q^{n}_{h}|^{2}\,dx+M\int_{\Omega}D_{t}^{+}\mathcal{F}_{B}(Q_{h}^{n})\,dx

\displaystyle\hskip 12.91663pt-\frac{M\varepsilon_{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{n}_{h}\odot\nabla u^{n}_{h}(\nabla u^{n+1}_{h})^{\top}:D_{t}^{+}Q^{n}_{h}\,dx-M\varepsilon_{3}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u^{n+1/2}_{h}:\operatorname{div}D_{t}^{+}Q^{n}_{h}\,dx.

Thus,

(2.9)

which proves the result.

.
∎

2.3.
Solvability of the scheme

In this section, we will show that solutions to the scheme (
2.2
) exist. Before doing so, we require the following lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Then the entries of
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(A,B)
are between
0
and
1
inclusive.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{T}_{R}
is the mollification of the entrywise truncation function

\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{R}(y)=\begin{cases}\frac{R}{d}\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}&\text{if }|y|>R/d-\epsilon\\
y&\text{if }|y|\leq R/d-\epsilon.\end{cases}

So it is clear that
\frac{\partial\mathcal{T}_{R}(B)_{ij}}{\partial Q}
is bounded between
0
and
1
, and the slope of any secant line is between
0
and
1
. So by definition of
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
, the entries of
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(A,B)
are bounded between
0
and
1
.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Then
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(\cdot,B)
is Lipschitz.

Proof.

Recall the definition of
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
,

\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q,B)_{ij}=\begin{cases}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)_{ij}-\mathcal{T}_{R}(B)_{ij}}{Q_{ij}-B_{ij}}&\text{if }Q_{ij}\neq B_{ij}\\
\frac{\partial\mathcal{T}_{R}(B)_{ij}}{\partial Q}&\text{if }Q_{ij}=B_{ij}.\end{cases}

Clearly
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
is continuous. To show that
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
is Lipschitz, we first show that its derivative is continuously differentiable. For
Q_{ij}\neq B_{ij}
, since
\mathcal{T}_{R}
is smooth, by the quotient rule we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q,B)_{ij}}{\partial Q_{ij}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Q_{ij}-B_{ij}}\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)_{ij}}{\partial Q_{ij}}-\frac{\mathcal{T}_{R}(B)_{ij}-\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)_{ij}}{B_{ij}-Q_{ij}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Q_{ij}-B_{ij}}\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)_{ij}}{\partial Q_{ij}}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{T}_{R}(B)_{ij}}{\partial Q_{ij}}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(B)_{ij}}{\partial Q_{ij}^{2}}(Q_{ij}-B_{ij})+o(Q_{ij}-B_{ij})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Q_{ij}-B_{ij}}\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)_{ij}}{\partial Q_{ij}}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{T}_{R}(B)_{ij}}{\partial Q_{ij}}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(B)_{ij}}{\partial Q_{ij}^{2}}+\frac{o(Q_{ij}-B_{ij})}{Q_{ij}-B_{ij}}.

As
Q_{ij}\to B_{ij}
, we see this has limit
\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(B)_{ij}}{\partial Q_{ij}^{2}}
. Now applying the limit definition of the derivative and again writing Taylor approximations, we see that for
Q_{ij}=B_{ij}
, we also have

\frac{\partial\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q,B)_{ij}}{\partial Q_{ij}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(B)_{ij}}{\partial Q_{ij}^{2}}.

Thus
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
is continuously differentiable. Now using the fact that
\mathcal{T}_{R}
is bounded and the fact that the derivative of
\mathcal{T}_{R}
has compact support, we see that the derivative
\partial\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q,B)_{ij}/\partial Q_{ij}
goes to
0
as
\lvert Q_{ij}\rvert\to\infty
. Combining these facts, we conclude that
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
has bounded derivative and is Lipschitz, as desired.
∎

We now prove that solutions to the scheme exist using the Leray-Schauder fixed point theorem.

Theorem 2.7

(Existence of Solutions)
.

Assume
\tilde{Q}_{h}^{n}\in\mathbb{X}_{h}
and
\tilde{u}_{h}^{n}\in\mathbb{Y}_{h}
are given, and let
Q_{h}^{n}=\tilde{Q}_{h}^{n}+\tilde{q}_{h}
and
u_{h}^{n}=\tilde{u}_{h}^{n}+\tilde{g}_{h}^{n}
. Assume that
\varepsilon_{1}>|\varepsilon_{2}|R+|\varepsilon_{3}|
and
\lvert\varepsilon_{3}\rvert<\frac{L}{12}
. Then the scheme (
2.2
) admits solutions for all
n=1,2,\dots,N
.

Proof.

Consider the map
\hat{x}=\mathcal{L}x
given by (
2.3
). We now show that
\mathcal{L}
satisfies the hypotheses of the Leray-Schauder fixed point theorem to assert the existence of a solution.

–

Well-posedness.
We must show that the operator
\mathcal{L}
is well defined. We begin by solving the elliptic equation (
2.3b
). This equation has a solution for
\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1}
due to the Lax-Milgram theorem.

The integral on the left hand side of (
2.3b
) defines a bilinear form
B:H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
, and the right hand side defines a linear form
F:H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
. We show that
B
is continuous and coercive and that
F
is bounded from which it will follow by the Lax-Milgram theorem that a unique solution exists given
Q^{n+1}_{h}
. To show continuity, we apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to obtain

(2.19)

\begin{split}&\left\lvert\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1}+\varepsilon_{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}^{n+1})\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1})\cdot\nabla\psi_{h}\,dx\right\rvert\\
&\hskip 43.05542pt\leq\varepsilon_{1}\|\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla\psi_{h}\|_{L^{2}}+|\varepsilon_{2}|\|\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}^{n+1})\nabla u_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla\psi_{h}\|_{L^{2}}\\
&\hskip 43.05542pt\leq(\varepsilon_{1}+R|\varepsilon_{2}|)\|\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla\psi_{h}\|_{L^{2}}\\
&\hskip 43.05542pt\leq(\varepsilon_{1}+R|\varepsilon_{2}|)\|\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}\|\psi_{h}\|_{H^{1}_{0}},\end{split}

so the bilinear form is continuous on

(2.20)

\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1}+\varepsilon_{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}^{n+1})\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1})\cdot\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1}\,dx\geq(\varepsilon_{1}-R|\varepsilon_{2}|)\|\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq C(\varepsilon_{1}-|\varepsilon_{2}|R)\|\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1}\|_{H_{0}^{1}},

by the Poincaré inequality, which shows that the bilinear form is coercive since
C(\varepsilon_{1}-|\varepsilon_{2}|R)>0
.

We now show that the linear form is bounded. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

(2.21)

\begin{split}&\left\lvert\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{n+1}+\varepsilon_{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}^{n+1})\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{n+1}+\varepsilon_{3}\operatorname{div}(Q_{h}^{n+1}))\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx\right\rvert\\
&\hskip 21.52771pt\leq\left((\varepsilon_{1}+|\varepsilon_{2}|R)\|\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}+|\varepsilon_{3}|\sqrt{d}\|\nabla Q_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}\right)\|\nabla\psi_{h}\|_{H_{0}^{1}}.\end{split}

Then since
\tilde{g}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, we have that the linear form is bounded. Thus, the Lax-Milgram lemma gives that there is a unique solution
\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1}
to (
2.3b
). Given
\widehat{u}_{h}^{n+1}
, a similar argument shows that a unique
\widehat{Q}^{n+1}_{h}
exists for (
2.3a
).

\mathcal{L}
maps bounded sets into bounded sets.

Continuity.
We now show that the operator
\mathcal{L}
is continuous. Because
\mathcal{L}
is independent of the input
\tilde{u}_{h}^{n+1}

\mathcal{L}
is continuous with respect to
\tilde{Q}_{h}^{n+1}

in

is well-posed, there are

\mathcal{L}
is independent of this input). Let
(\widehat{Q},\widehat{u})=\mathcal{L}(Q,\tilde{u})

again arbitrary. Then to show
\mathcal{L}

\widehat{Q}_{k}\to\widehat{Q}

\widehat{u}_{k}\to\widehat{u}

H^{1}(\Omega)

First, because
\mathcal{L}
maps bounded sets into bounded sets, we have that

is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
because the sequence
\{\tilde{Q}_{k}\}_{k}
is bounded in
H^{1}(\Omega)
. Now subtract the elliptic equations corresponding to

and to
\nabla\widehat{u}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{1}(\nabla\widehat{u}_{k}-\nabla\widehat{u})+\varepsilon_{2}(\mathcal{T}_{R}(\tilde{Q}_{k}+\tilde{q}_{h})\nabla\widehat{u}_{k}-\mathcal{T}_{R}(\tilde{Q}+\tilde{q}_{h})\nabla\widehat{u}))\cdot\nabla\psi_{h}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{2}(\mathcal{T}_{R}(\tilde{Q}_{k}+\tilde{q}_{h})-\mathcal{T}_{R}(\tilde{Q}+\tilde{q}_{h}))\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{n+1}+\varepsilon_{3}(\operatorname{div}(\tilde{Q}_{k}+\tilde{q}_{h})-\operatorname{div}(\tilde{Q}+\tilde{q}_{h})))\cdot\nabla\psi_{h}\,dx.

Then take
\psi_{h}=\nabla\widehat{u}_{k}-\nabla\widehat{u}
. We get

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}\|\nabla\widehat{u}_{k}-\nabla\widehat{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\varepsilon_{2}\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\widehat{u}_{k}-\nabla\widehat{u})^{\top}\mathcal{T}_{R}(\tilde{Q}_{k}+\tilde{q}_{h})(\nabla\widehat{u}_{k}-\nabla\widehat{u})\,dx}_{I}

\mathcal{T}_{R}

are bounded to obtain the upper bound

\displaystyle\left\|(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(\tilde{Q}_{k}+\tilde{q}_{h},Q_{h}^{n})\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla\widehat{u}_{k})^{\top})_{S}-(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(\tilde{Q}+\tilde{q}_{h},Q_{h}^{n})\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla\widehat{u})^{\top})_{S}\right\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\widehat{u}_{k}-\nabla\widehat{u}\|_{L^{2}}+C\|\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(\tilde{Q}_{k}+\tilde{q}_{h},Q_{h}^{n})-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(\tilde{Q}+\tilde{q}_{h},Q_{h}^{n})\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\widehat{u}_{k}-\nabla\widehat{u}\|_{L^{2}}+C^{\prime}\|\tilde{Q}_{k}-\tilde{Q}\|_{L^{2}},

where we have applied the Lipschitz property of
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
. Then since
\widehat{u}_{k}\to\widehat{u}
in
H^{1}
as previously shown and
\tilde{Q}_{k}\to\tilde{Q}
in
H^{1}
, we have

\left\|(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(\tilde{Q}_{k}+\tilde{q}_{h},Q_{h}^{n})\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla\widehat{u}_{k})^{\top})_{S}-(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(\tilde{Q}+\tilde{q}_{h},Q_{h}^{n})\odot\nabla u_{h}^{n}(\nabla\widehat{u})^{\top})_{S}\right\|_{L^{2}}\to 0

as
k\to\infty
. By similar computations, we have
III\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.
We also see that

IV=\frac{M\lvert\varepsilon_{3}\rvert}{2}\left\|\nabla\widehat{u}_{k}-\nabla\widehat{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}\|\operatorname{div}(\widehat{Q}_{k}-\widehat{Q})\|_{L^{2}}\to 0

since
\nabla\widehat{u}_{k}\to\nabla\widehat{u}
in
L^{2}
, as previously shown.
Combining all of the limits we have computed gives

\frac{1}{\Delta t}\|\widehat{Q}_{k}-\widehat{Q}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{ML}{2}\|\nabla\widehat{Q}_{k}-\nabla\widehat{Q}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\to 0

as
k\to\infty
. In particular,
\widehat{Q}_{k}\to\widehat{Q}
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
, and as previously computed, we also have
\widehat{u}_{k}\to\widehat{u}
. This shows that
\mathcal{L}
is continuous, as desired.

–

Compactness
Compactness of the operator
\mathcal{L}
follows since it is continuous and maps bounded sets into bounded sets in finite dimensional space.

Thus, we can apply the Leray-Schauder fixed point theorem to conclude the proof.
∎

3.
Convergence

We now show that up to a subsequence, the scheme (
2.2
) converges to a weak solution of (
1.11
) when
\varepsilon_{3}=0
and under some conditions for
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
(that were needed for stability and well-posedness in the previous section). To do so, we define piecewise constant interpolants in time:

(3.1a)

Q_{h,\Delta t}(t,x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}Q_{h}^{n}(x)\chi_{S_{n}}(t),

(3.1b)

u_{h,\Delta t}(t,x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}u_{h}^{n}(x)\chi_{S_{n}}(t),

where
\chi_{A}
is the characteristic function of a set
A
and
S_{n}=[n\Delta t,(n+1)\Delta t)
. We will need the following version of the Aubin-Lions-Simon lemma to conclude pre-compactness of
\{Q_{h,\Delta t}\}
:

Lemma 3.1

(Pre-Compactness of
\{Q_{h,\Delta t}\}
)
.

Assume that
\{Q_{h,\Delta t}\}\subset L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
and
\{D_{t}^{+}Q_{h,\Delta t}\}\subset L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)
uniformly in
\Delta t,h>0
sufficiently small. Then the sequence
\{Q_{h,\Delta t}\}
is precompact in
L^{2}
, that is,

Q_{h,\Delta t}\to Q\quad\text{in }L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)

up to a subsequence.

Proof.

We define piecewise linear interpolations in time:

\widehat{Q}_{h,\Delta t}(t,x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\chi_{S_{n}}(t)\left(Q_{h}^{n}(x)+\frac{Q_{h}^{n+1}(x)-Q_{h}^{n}(x)}{\Delta t}(t-n\Delta t)\right),

where
S_{n}=[n\Delta t,(n+1)\Delta t)
. Then if
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<T
, we have (omitting spatial dependence)

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\widehat{Q}_{h,\Delta t}(t)\,dt\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\|\widehat{Q}_{h,\Delta t}(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\,dt\leq 3\Delta t\sum_{n=0}^{N}\|Q_{h}^{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C,

where
C>0
is some constant and where the last inequality follows since
\{Q_{h,\Delta t}\}\subset L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
. Now
H^{1}(\Omega)
is compactly embedded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, so the set
\left\{\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\widehat{Q}_{h,\Delta t}(t)\,dt\right\}_{(h,\Delta t)}
is relatively compact in
L^{2}(\Omega)
for all
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<T
.
Now

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T-\Delta t}\lvert\widehat{Q}_{h,\Delta t}(t+\Delta t)-\widehat{Q}_{h,\Delta t}(t)\rvert^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T-\Delta t}\left(\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\lvert\partial_{s}\widehat{Q}_{h,\Delta t}(s)\rvert\,ds\right)^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\Delta t\int_{0}^{T-\Delta t}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\lvert\partial_{s}\widehat{Q}_{h,\Delta t}(s)\rvert^{2}\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta t,

where the second inequality is Hölder’s inequality, and the last inequality follows since
\{D_{t}^{+}Q_{h,\Delta t}\}\subset L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)
uniformly in
\Delta t,h>0
. Thus, we have that
\|\widehat{Q}_{h,\Delta t}(\cdot+\Delta t)-\widehat{Q}_{h,\Delta t}(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(0,T-\Delta t;L^{2}(\Omega))}\to 0
as
\Delta t\to 0
, uniformly in
h>0
. It follows from Theorem 1 in
[
25
]
that the family
\{\widehat{Q}_{h,\Delta t}\}_{(h,\Delta t)}
is pre-compact in
L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)
.

We now show that
\widehat{Q}_{h,\Delta t}
and
Q_{h,\Delta t}
have the same limit. We have

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{Q}_{h,\Delta t}-Q_{h,\Delta t}\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\chi_{S_{n}}(t)\frac{Q_{h}^{n+1}(x)-Q_{h}^{n}(x)}{\Delta t}(t-n\Delta t)\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\Delta t\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\chi_{S_{n}}D_{t}^{+}Q_{h}^{n}\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dt\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\Delta t\left(\Delta t\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\int_{\Omega}\lvert D_{t}^{+}Q_{h}^{n}\rvert^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta t,

where we have used Corollary
2.4
. So as
\Delta t\to 0
, we see that
\widehat{Q}_{h,\Delta t}
and
Q_{h,\Delta t}
have the same limits. Thus,
\{Q_{h,\Delta t}\}_{(h,\Delta t)}
is precompact in
L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)
, as desired.
∎

Using this lemma and assuming some restrictions on
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},
and
\varepsilon_{3}
, we now show that the scheme converges to a weak solution as the mesh is refined.

Theorem 3.2

(Convergence to Weak Solutions)
.

Assume that
\Delta t<\frac{\varepsilon_{1}-|\varepsilon_{2}|R}{3(\varepsilon_{1}+|\varepsilon_{2}|R^{2})}
, that
\varepsilon_{1}>\lvert\varepsilon_{2}\rvert R
, and
\varepsilon_{3}=0
. Then the sequence
\{(Q_{h,\Delta t},u_{h,\Delta t})\}
of approximations computed by the scheme (
2.2
) converge up to a subsequence to a weak solution of (
1.11
).

Proof.

From Theorem
2.3
, the Poincaré inequality, the fact that
\|\tilde{q}_{h}\|_{H^{1}},\|\tilde{g}_{h}(t)\|_{H^{1}}\leq C
uniformly in
h
, and Corollary
2.4
, we have

(3.2)

\{Q_{h,\Delta t}\}\subset L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),\quad\{u_{h,\Delta t}\}\subset L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),\quad\{D_{t}^{+}Q_{h,\Delta t}\}\subset L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)

uniformly in
\Delta t,h>0
sufficiently small. Then the Banach-Alaoglu theorem implies weak star convergence of subsequences as
\Delta t,h\to 0
, for simplicity denoted
\{(Q_{h},u_{h})\}_{h>0}
,

(3.3a)

\displaystyle Q_{h}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}Q\text{ in }L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\quad u_{h}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}u\text{ in }L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

(3.3b)

\displaystyle D_{t}^{+}Q_{h}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\partial_{t}Q\text{ in }

\displaystyle L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega).

The bounds (
3.2
) allow us to apply the result of Lemma
3.1
,

(3.4)

Q_{h}\to Q\text{ in }L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)

up to a subsequence, for simplicity still denoted
Q_{h}
. Because
Q_{h}
converges to
Q
in
L^{2}
, there is a subsequence which converges almost everywhere, and so we see that
Q
must be trace-free and symmetric, as the
Q_{h}
are trace-free and symmetric. In an abuse of notation, we will denote by
\Pi_{h}
the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection from
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
to
\mathbb{X}_{h}
and also the
L^{2}
-projection from
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
to
\mathbb{Y}_{h}
. Then this projection satisfies

(3.5)

\lvert\Pi_{h}v-v\rvert_{H^{1}}\overset{h\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0

for
v\in H^{s}(\Omega)
or
v\in H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
,
s>1
. Furthermore,
\|\Pi_{h}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H^{1}(\Omega);H^{1}(\Omega))}
is uniformly bounded in
h>0
(see
[
4
]
or
[
11
, Lemma 11.18]
). Then for a given
\Phi,\psi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,T]\times\Omega)
, taking
\Pi_{h}\Phi
and
\Pi_{h}\psi
to be test functions in (
2.2
), we obtain (omitting dependences on
x
)

(3.6a)

\begin{split}&-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}D_{t}^{+}Q_{h}:\Pi_{h}\Phi\,dx\,dt\\
&=ML\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\nabla Q_{h}(t+\Delta t)+\nabla Q_{h}(t)}{2}:\nabla\Pi_{h}\Phi\,dx\,dt\\
&+M\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q_{h}(t+\Delta t))}{\partial Q}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{2}(Q_{h}(t))}{\partial Q}\right):\Pi_{h}\Phi\,dx\,dt\\
&-\frac{M\varepsilon_{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left((\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}(t+\Delta t),Q_{h}(t))\odot\nabla u_{h}(t)(\nabla u_{h}(t+\Delta t))^{\top})_{S}\right.\\
&\hskip 86.11084pt\left.-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}(t+\Delta t),Q_{h}(t))\odot\nabla u_{h}(t)(\nabla u_{h}(t+\Delta t))^{\top})I\right):\Pi_{h}\Phi\,dx\,dt,\end{split}

(3.6b)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla u_{h}+\varepsilon_{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h})\nabla u_{h})\cdot\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi\,dx\,dt=0.

We first consider the elliptic equation. We have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi\,dx\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{h}\cdot(\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi-\nabla\psi)\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla u_{h}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi(t)-\nabla\psi(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,dt+\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u_{h}-\nabla u)\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert.

Then the second term above goes to
0
as
\Delta t,h\to 0
by weak star convergence of
\nabla u_{h}
. Now since
\|\nabla u_{h}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
is essentially bounded in
[0,T]
uniformly in
\Delta t,h>0
and
\|\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi(t)-\nabla\psi(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
is bounded uniformly in
h>0
, we have by (
3.5
) and Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem that the first term also goes to
0
as
\Delta t,h\to 0
and so

(3.7)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi\,dx\,dt\longrightarrow\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx\,dt

as
\Delta t,h\to 0
.

Now for the second term in the elliptic equation, applying the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h})\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi\,dx\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\nabla u\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h})-\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q))\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert}_{I}

\displaystyle\hskip 43.05542pt+\underbrace{\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi\,dx\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\nabla u\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert}_{II}.

Estimating
I
, we note that
(\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h})-\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q))\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi=\tr((\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi)^{\top}(\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h})-\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q))\nabla u_{h})
and use the cyclic property of the trace followed by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fact that
\mathcal{T}_{R}
is Lipschitz to obtain

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h})-\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)):\nabla u_{h}(\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi)^{\top}\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h})-\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\|\nabla u_{h}(\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi)^{\top}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\|Q_{h}-Q\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\|\nabla u_{h}(\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi)^{\top}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}.

Then because
\nabla u_{h}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
and
\Pi_{h}\psi\in C_{c}([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega})
is uniformly bounded in

, we have

\|\nabla u_{h}(\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi)^{\top}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\leq CT^{1/2}\esssup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\nabla u_{h}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<\infty.

Thus by strong convergence of
Q_{h}
to
Q
in
L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)
, we have
I\to 0
. Then to estimate
II
, we have

\displaystyle II

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi\,dx\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\nabla u\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\nabla u_{h}\cdot(\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi-\nabla\psi)\,dx\,dt\right\rvert+\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)(\nabla u_{h}-\nabla u)\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert.

The first term above is shown to converge to
0
by the same argument as for the first term in the elliptic equation using the fact that
\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)
is bounded. The second term converges to
0
due to the weak star convergence of
\nabla u_{h}
to
\nabla u
. Thus
II\to 0
and so we obtain

(3.8)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h})\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla\Pi_{h}\psi\,dx\,dt\to\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\nabla u\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx\,dt.

And so combining (
3.6b
), (
3.7
), and (
3.8
), we have

(3.9)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla u+\varepsilon_{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\nabla u)\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx\,dt=0.

Since it follows from the energy inequality that
u\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
, and smooth functions are dense in
H^{1}
, this identity holds for any
\psi\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
.

For what follows, we will need to show that
\nabla u_{h}\to\nabla u
in
L^{2}
. To do this, take

in (

) and

in (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla u_{h}+\varepsilon_{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h})\nabla u_{h})\cdot\nabla(u_{h}-\tilde{g}_{h})\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla u+\varepsilon_{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\nabla u)\cdot\nabla(u-\tilde{g})\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle=0.

Subtracting these two equations gives

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(\varepsilon_{1}(\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}-\lvert\nabla u_{h}\rvert^{2})+\varepsilon_{2}(\nabla u^{\top}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\nabla u-\nabla u_{h}^{\top}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h})\nabla u_{h})\right)\,dx\,dt\\

For the left hand side, we first have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon_{2}(\nabla u^{\top}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\nabla u-\nabla u_{h}^{\top}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h})\nabla u_{h})\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon_{2}\left(\nabla u^{\top}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)(\nabla u-\nabla u_{h})-(\nabla u-\nabla u_{h})^{\top}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h})(\nabla u-\nabla u_{h})\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 51.6665pt\left.+\nabla u^{\top}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h})(\nabla u-\nabla u_{h})+\nabla u^{\top}(\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)-\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}))\nabla u_{h}\right)\,dx\,dt.

\displaystyle|V|

\displaystyle|VI|

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\tilde{g}^{\top}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\nabla u-\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{\top}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h})\nabla u_{h})\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{g}^{\top}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)(\nabla u-\nabla u_{h})\,dx\,dt\right\rvert+\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{g}^{\top}(\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)-\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h}))\nabla u_{h}\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle\hskip 43.05542pt+\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\tilde{g}^{\top}-\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}^{\top})\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h})\nabla u_{h}\,dx\,dt\right\rvert.

The first of these converges to
0
by weak convergence of
\nabla u_{h}
. The second term converges to
0
by the same argument as the analogous term dealt with before. The third term is bounded by
\|\nabla\tilde{g}-\nabla\tilde{g}_{h}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\|\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q_{h})\nabla u_{h}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}
and hence goes to
0
as
\Delta t,h\to 0
.
It follows that all the terms

-
VI
in (

) converge to zero as

up to subsequence.
Combining this with (
3.12
), we have that

(3.13)

\begin{split}0&\geq\lim_{\Delta t,h\to 0}\left\{\varepsilon_{1}\|\nabla u-\nabla u_{h}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}^{2}-\lvert\varepsilon_{2}\rvert R\|\nabla u-\nabla u_{h}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}^{2}\right\},\end{split}

which implies that
\|\nabla u-\nabla u_{h}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\to 0
as
\Delta t,h\to 0
since
\varepsilon_{1}>\lvert\varepsilon_{2}\rvert R
.

We turn now to estimates for (
3.6a
). First, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}D_{t}^{+}Q_{h}:\Pi_{h}\Phi\,dx\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}Q:\Phi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}D_{t}^{+}Q_{h}:(\Pi_{h}\Phi-\Phi)\,dx\,dt\right\rvert+\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(D_{t}^{+}Q_{h}-\partial_{t}Q):\Phi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert.

The second of these terms converges to
0
by weak star convergence of
D_{t}^{+}Q_{h}
to
\partial_{t}Q
. For the first term, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}D_{t}^{+}Q_{h}:(\Pi_{h}\Phi-\Phi)\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\|D_{t}^{+}Q_{h}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\|\Pi_{h}\Phi-\Phi\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}.

Then by the Poincaré inequality and since
\lvert\Pi_{h}\Phi(t)-\Phi(t)\rvert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\to 0
for each
t
as
\Delta t,h\to 0
and is uniformly bounded by a constant, we have by Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem and the fact that
D_{t}^{+}Q_{h}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}
that that
\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}D_{t}^{+}Q_{h}:(\Pi_{h}\Phi-\Phi)\,dx\,dt\right\rvert\to 0
as
\Delta t,h\to 0
. Thus,

(3.14)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}D_{t}^{+}Q_{h}:\Pi_{h}\Phi\,dx\,dt\to\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}Q:\Phi\,dx\,dt

as
\Delta t,h\to 0
. By a similar argument, we obtain

(3.15)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\nabla Q_{h}(t)}{2}:\nabla\Pi_{h}\Phi\,dx\,dt\to\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla Q:\nabla\Phi\,dx\,dt

as
\Delta t,h\to 0
. Then for
\Delta t
sufficiently small (since
\Phi
has compact support), we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\nabla Q_{h}(t+\Delta t)}{2}:\nabla\Pi_{h}\Phi(t)\,dx\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\nabla Q}{2}:\nabla\Phi(t)\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\nabla Q_{h}(t)}{2}:\nabla\Pi_{h}\Phi(t-\Delta t)\,dx\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\nabla Q}{2}:\nabla\Phi(t)\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\nabla Q_{h}(t)}{2}:(\nabla\Pi_{h}\Phi(t-\Delta t)-\nabla\Phi(t))\,dx\,dt\right\rvert+\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\nabla Q_{h}-\nabla Q}{2}:\nabla\Phi(t)\,dx\,dt\right\rvert.

The second term above converges to
0
by weak star convergence of
\nabla Q_{h}
. The first term we bound by

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\nabla Q_{h}(t)}{2}:(\nabla\Pi_{h}\Phi(t-\Delta t)-\nabla\Phi(t))\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla Q_{h}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\|\nabla\Pi_{h}\Phi(\cdot-\Delta t)-\nabla\Phi\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}.

Now we have

\|\nabla\Pi_{h}\Phi(\cdot-\Delta t)-\nabla\Phi\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\leq\|\nabla\Pi_{h}\Phi(\cdot-\Delta t)-\nabla\Pi_{h}\Phi\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}+\|\nabla\Pi_{h}\Phi-\nabla\Phi\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)},

and the second term on the right hand side converges to
0
, as before. For the first term, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla\Pi_{h}\Phi(\cdot-\Delta t)-\nabla\Pi_{h}\Phi\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq CT^{1/2}\esssup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\nabla\Pi_{h}\Phi(t-\Delta t)-\nabla\Pi_{h}\Phi(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq CT^{1/2}\esssup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\Pi_{h}\Phi(t-\Delta t)-\Pi_{h}\Phi(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq CT^{1/2}\|\Pi_{h}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H^{1}(\Omega);H^{1}(\Omega))}\esssup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\Phi(t-\Delta t)-\Phi(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq CT^{1/2}\|\Pi_{h}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H^{1}(\Omega);H^{1}(\Omega))}o_{\Delta t\to 0}(1)

where we have used that
\Phi
is continuously differentiable.
Thus we see that this bound converges to
0
as
\Delta t\to 0
, as
\|\Pi_{h}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H^{1}(\Omega);H^{1}(\Omega))}
is uniformly bounded in
h>0
. Combining this with the previous bounds and the fact that
\|\nabla Q_{h}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}
is uniformly bounded in
h>0
, we obtain

(3.16)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\nabla Q_{h}(t+\Delta t)}{2}:\nabla\Pi_{h}\Phi(t)\,dx\,dt\to\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla Q:\nabla\Phi(t)\,dx\,dt

and conclude

(3.17)

ML\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\nabla Q_{h}(t+\Delta t)+\nabla Q_{h}(t)}{2}:\nabla\Pi_{h}\Phi\,dx\,dt\to ML\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla Q:\nabla\Phi\,dx\,dt

as
\Delta t,h\to 0
. Now we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{2}(Q_{h})}{\partial Q}:\Pi_{h}\Phi\,dx\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{2}(Q)}{\partial Q}:\Phi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left[(\beta_{1}-a)Q_{h}:\Pi_{h}\Phi+(\beta_{2}-c)\operatorname{tr}(Q_{h}^{2})Q_{h}:\Pi_{h}\Phi-(\beta_{1}-a)Q:\Phi-(\beta_{2}-c)\operatorname{tr}(Q^{2})Q:\Phi\right]\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq(\beta_{1}-a)\underbrace{\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(Q_{h}:\Pi_{h}\Phi-Q:\Phi)\,dx\,dt\right\rvert}_{I}+(\beta_{2}-c)\underbrace{\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{tr}(Q_{h}^{2})Q_{h}:\Pi_{h}\Phi-\operatorname{tr}(Q^{2})Q:\Phi)\,dx\,dt\right\rvert}_{II}.

Similar to arguments above, we have
I\to 0
as
\Delta t,h\to 0
. For
II
, we have

\displaystyle II

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{tr}(Q_{h}^{2})Q_{h}:(\Pi_{h}\Phi-\Phi)\,dx\,dt\right\rvert}_{I^{\prime}}+\underbrace{\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{tr}(Q_{h}^{2})Q_{h}:\Phi-\operatorname{tr}(Q^{2})Q:\Phi)\,dx\,dt\right\rvert}_{II^{\prime}}.

Because
\nabla Q_{h}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
uniformly in
\Delta t,h>0
, we have that
Q_{h}\in L^{6}([0,T]\times\Omega)
by the Sobolev embedding theorem, so
\operatorname{tr}(Q_{h}^{2})Q_{h}
is bounded in
L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)
. It follows as in our previous arguments that
I^{\prime}\to 0
as
\Delta t,h\to 0
. Then for
II^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle II^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{tr}(Q_{h}^{2})Q_{h}-\operatorname{tr}(Q_{h}Q)Q_{h}+\operatorname{tr}(Q_{h}Q)Q_{h}-\operatorname{tr}(Q^{2})Q_{h}+\operatorname{tr}(Q^{2})Q_{h}-\operatorname{tr}(Q^{2})Q):\Phi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|Q_{h}-Q|\left(|Q|^{2}+|Q_{h}|^{2}\right)|\Phi|dxdt

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert Q_{h}-Q\right\rVert_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\left(\left\lVert Q\right\rVert_{L^{4}([0,T]\times\Omega)}^{2}+\left\lVert Q_{h}\right\rVert_{L^{4}([0,T]\times\Omega)}^{2}\right)\left\lVert\Phi\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\Omega)}.

Since
\Phi
is bounded,
Q
and
Q_{h}
are bounded in
L^{4}
, and
Q_{h}\to Q
in
L^{2}
, we have that
II^{\prime}\to 0
and we conclude that
II\to 0
and thus

(3.18)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{2}(Q_{h})}{\partial Q}:\Pi_{h}\Phi\,dx\,dt\to\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{2}(Q)}{\partial Q}:\Phi\,dx\,dt

as
\Delta t,h\to 0
.

Now for
\Delta t
sufficiently small, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q_{h}(t+\Delta t))}{\partial Q}:\Pi_{h}\Phi\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q_{h})}{\partial Q}:\Pi_{h}\Phi(t-\Delta t)\,dx\,dt

so that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q_{h}(t+\Delta t))}{\partial Q}:\Pi_{h}\Phi\,dx\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q)}{\partial Q}:\Phi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q_{h})}{\partial Q}:\Pi_{h}\Phi(t-\Delta t)\,dx\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q)}{\partial Q}:\Phi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q_{h})}{\partial Q}:(\Pi_{h}\Phi(t-\Delta t)-\Pi_{h}\Phi(t))\,dx\,dt\right\rvert}_{I}+\underbrace{\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q_{h})}{\partial Q}:(\Pi_{h}\Phi-\Phi)\,dx\,dt\right\rvert}_{II}

\displaystyle\hskip 43.05542pt+\underbrace{\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q)}{\partial Q}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q_{h})}{\partial Q}\right):\Phi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert}_{III}.

Now for

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{h}\Phi(t-\Delta t)-\Pi_{h}\Phi(t)\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq CT^{1/2}\|\Pi_{h}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H^{1}(\Omega);H^{1}(\Omega))}\Delta t,

as argued before. Thus,

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q_{h})}{\partial Q}\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\|\Pi_{h}\Phi(t-\Delta t)-\Pi_{h}\Phi(t)\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\to 0

as

. For

, we have

\displaystyle II

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q_{h})}{\partial Q}\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\|\Pi_{h}\Phi-\Phi\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\to 0

\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q_{h})/\partial Q

and
\|\Pi_{h}\Phi-\Phi\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\to 0

\displaystyle III

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{1}\underbrace{\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(Q-Q_{h}):\Phi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert}_{I^{\prime}}+\lvert b\rvert\underbrace{\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(Q^{2}-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}(Q^{2})I-Q_{h}^{2}+\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}(Q_{h}^{2})I):\Phi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert}_{II^{\prime}}

. Also

\displaystyle II^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|Q-Q_{h}|(|Q|+|Q_{h}|)|\Phi|dxdt

, as

as
\Delta t,h\to 0
. It follows that

(3.19)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q_{h}(t+\Delta t))}{\partial Q}:\Pi_{h}Q\,dx\,dt\to\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q)}{\partial Q}:\Phi\,dx\,dt

as
\Delta t,h\to 0
.

\Phi_{h}=\Pi_{h}\Phi
, we now consider

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left((\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}(t+\Delta t),Q_{h}(t))\odot\nabla u_{h}(t)(\nabla u_{h}(t+\Delta t))^{\top})_{S}\right):\Phi_{h}\,dx\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{P}(Q)\odot\nabla u(\nabla u)^{\top})_{S}:\Phi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert.

We will show that this quantity converges to zero as
\Delta t,h\to 0
, from which it will follow that the trace term associated with it in (
3.6a
) also converges to the desired quantity by a very similar argument. We can also ignore the symmetric part in the above expression, for if we can show convergence without the symmetric part, the corresponding transpose will also converge as we can just move the transpose onto
\Phi_{h}
. We have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}(t+\Delta t),Q_{h}(t))\odot\nabla u_{h}(t)\nabla u_{h}(t+\Delta t)^{\top}):\Pi_{h}\Phi\,dx\,dt\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 43.05542pt\left.-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{P}(Q)\odot\nabla u_{h}(t)\nabla u_{h}(t+\Delta t)^{\top}):\Pi_{h}\Phi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}(t+\Delta t),Q_{h}(t))_{ij}-\mathcal{P}(Q)_{ij})\frac{\partial u_{h}(t)}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial u_{h}(t+\Delta t)}{\partial x_{j}}\Pi_{h}\Phi_{ij}\right\rvert\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C(\Phi)\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\left\|(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}(t+\Delta t),Q_{h}(t))_{ij}-\mathcal{P}(Q)_{ij})\frac{\partial u_{h}(t)}{\partial x_{i}}\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\left\|\frac{\partial u_{h}(t+\Delta t)}{\partial x_{j}}\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}.

By strong convergence of

\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}(\cdot+\Delta t),Q_{h})_{ij}\to\mathcal{P}(Q)_{ij}
pointwise a.e. in

\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}(\cdot+\Delta t),Q_{h})_{ij}\to\mathcal{P}(Q)_{ij}

\delta

\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}(\cdot+\Delta t),Q_{h})_{ij}\to\mathcal{P}(Q)_{ij}

\displaystyle\left\|(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}(t+\Delta t),Q_{h}(t))_{ij}-\mathcal{P}(Q)_{ij})\frac{\partial u_{h}(t)}{\partial x_{i}}\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}(t+\Delta t),Q_{h}(t))_{ij}-\mathcal{P}(Q)_{ij})\frac{\partial u_{h}(t)}{\partial x_{i}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\omega_{\delta})}+2\left\|(1-\chi_{\omega_{\delta}})\frac{\partial u_{h}(t)}{\partial x_{i}}\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}.

\delta

\nabla u_{h}

\nabla u

\delta\to 0

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}(t+\Delta t),Q_{h}(t))\odot\nabla u_{h}(t)\nabla u_{h}(t+\Delta t)^{\top}):\Pi_{h}\Phi\,dx\,dt\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 43.05542pt\left.-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{P}(Q)\odot\nabla u_{h}(t)\nabla u_{h}(t+\Delta t)^{\top}):\Pi_{h}\Phi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert\to 0

as
\Delta t,h\to 0
. As in previous arguments, we also have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{P}(Q)\odot\nabla u_{h}(t)\nabla u_{h}(t+\Delta t)^{\top}):\Pi_{h}\Phi\,dx\,dt\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 43.05542pt\left.-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{P}(Q)\odot\nabla u_{h}(t)\nabla u_{h}(t+\Delta t)^{\top}):\Phi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert\to 0.

Then we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{P}(Q)\odot\nabla u_{h}(t)\nabla u_{h}(t+\Delta t)^{\top}):\Phi\,dx\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{P}(Q)\odot\nabla u(t)\nabla u_{h}(t+\Delta t)^{\top}):\Phi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\mathcal{P}(Q)_{ij}\Phi_{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}(u_{h}(t)-u(t))\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}u_{h}(t+\Delta t)\right\rvert\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\left\|\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}(u_{h}-u)\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\left\|\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}u_{h}(\cdot+\Delta t)\Phi_{ij}\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)},

which converges to
0
by
L^{2}
convergence of
\nabla u_{h}
to
\nabla u
and because
\Phi_{ij}
has compact support so that the norm of
\partial_{x_{j}}u_{h}(\cdot+\Delta t)\Phi_{ij}
is well defined. Now we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{P}(Q)\odot\nabla u(t)\nabla u_{h}(t+\Delta t)^{\top}):\Phi\,dx\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{P}(Q)\odot\nabla u\nabla u^{\top}):\Phi\,dx\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\mathcal{P}(Q)_{ij}\Phi_{ij}\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}(u_{h}(t+\Delta t)-u(t))\right\rvert\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\left\|\Phi_{ij}\left(\frac{\partial u_{h}(\cdot+\Delta t)}{\partial x_{j}}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\left(\left\|\Phi_{ij}\left(\frac{\partial u_{h}(\cdot+\Delta t)}{\partial x_{j}}-\frac{\partial u(\cdot+\Delta t)}{\partial x_{j}}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 129.16626pt\left.+\left\|\Phi_{ij}\left(\frac{\partial u(\cdot+\Delta t)}{\partial x_{j}}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\right),

which converges to
0
by
L^{2}
convergence of
\nabla u_{h}
to
\nabla u
and by continuity of
L^{2}
shifts. Thus, we have

(3.20)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left((\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(Q_{h}(t+\Delta t),Q_{h}(t))\odot\nabla u_{h}(t)(\nabla u_{h}(t+\Delta t))^{\top})_{S}\right):\Phi_{h}\,dx\,dt\to\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{P}(Q)\odot\nabla u(\nabla u)^{\top})_{S}:\Phi\,dx\,dt

as
\Delta t,h\to 0
, as desired.

Combining all the estimates above, we see that in the limit, it holds that

(3.21a)

\begin{split}&-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}Q:\Phi\,dx\,dt\\
&=ML\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla Q:\nabla\Phi\,dx\,dt+M\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{1}(Q)}{\partial Q}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{2}(Q)}{\partial Q}\right):\Phi\,dx\,dt\\
&\hskip 43.05542pt-\frac{M\varepsilon_{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{P}(Q)\odot\nabla u\nabla u^{\top}-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{P}(Q)\odot\nabla u\nabla u^{\top})I\right):\Phi\,dx\,dt,\end{split}

(3.21b)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{1}\nabla u+\varepsilon_{2}\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)\nabla u)\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx\,dt=0.

Integrating the
\partial_{t}Q
term by parts shows that
(Q,u)
is a weak solution of (

), as desired.

\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)
as

\mathcal{T}_{R}(Q)_{ij}=Q_{ij}\widehat{H}\left(\frac{R}{2}-Q_{ij}\right)\widehat{H}\left(\frac{R}{2}+Q_{ij}\right)+\frac{R}{2}\widehat{H}\left(Q_{ij}-\frac{R}{2}\right)-\frac{R}{2}\widehat{H}\left(-Q_{ij}-\frac{R}{2}\right),

where
\widehat{H}
is a smooth approximation of the Heaviside function and is given by

\widehat{H}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan(5x)+\frac{1}{2}.

Furthermore, we compute
\mathcal{P}(Q)
using the exact derivative of
\mathcal{T}_{R}
instead of a discrete approximation so that the implementation could utilize automatically computed Newton iterations.

For our numerical experiments, we take

a=-0.3,\quad b=-4,\quad c=4,\quad M=1,\quad\beta_{1}=8,\quad\beta_{2}=8.

Furthermore, take
\Omega=[-0.5,0.5]^{2}
with a
30\times 30
mesh. Other parameters and the boundary and initial conditions differ for the various experiments. We first consider an example with constant initial director angle. In the second experiment, we show the effect of increasing the electric field magnitude. After that, the third experiment demonstrates the need for the truncation operator. We end with an experiment showing that our model captures a phenomenon in liquid crystals known as the Fréedericksz transition.

4.4.
Fréedericksz transition

We now demonstrate a phenomenon in liquid crystals known as the Fréedericksz transition. This transition is a change in behavior of the liquid crystal directors in which the directors align according to the anchoring of the boundary conditions for sufficiently small electric fields, and the directors align according to the electric field for sufficiently large electric fields
[
12
]
. We can think of this transition as a sort of competition between the boundary conditions and the electric field.

Here we will use the same parameters as in the previous experiments, and take

\tilde{g}(t,x,y)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }t<0.5\\
10x/3&\text{if }0.5\leq t<1\\
20x/3&\text{if }1\leq t<1.5\\
10x&\text{if }t\geq 1.5,\end{cases}

and

Q_{0}(x,y)=\bm{\textrm{d}}\bm{\textrm{d}}^{\top}-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\bm{\textrm{d}}\bm{\textrm{d}}^{\top})I,\quad\text{where}\quad\bm{\textrm{d}}=(0,1).

We take
Q
to be
\begin{bmatrix}-0.5&0\\
0&0.5\end{bmatrix}
on the boundary so that the initial condition is continuous at
\partial\Omega
.

We see with the definition of
\tilde{g}
that the electric field becomes stronger at times
t=0.5
,
t=1
, and
t=1.5
. The results of our simulation are shown in Figure
6
. We see that for
t\leq 1.5
, the directors are all vertical, which is the orientation associated with the boundary condition. Once the electric potential is larger at time
t=1.5
, we see that the directors change so that a circular region in the middle of the domain has directors oriented horizontally, which is parallel to the gradient of the electric potential. Thus we observe that for large electric potentials, the directors align with the electric field, but for small electric potentials, the liquid crystals orient according to the boundary condition. We investigate this further in Figure
7
.

To produce this figure, we choose the extension of the electric potential boundary to be
s\tilde{g}(t,x,y)/10
, where
s
is the parameter called “Electric Field Strength” on the axes. The plot gives the average absolute value of the director angles at time
t=2
using this boundary extension. We see that there is a value of the strength
s
below which the directors align vertically, that is, the same direction as the boundary condition for
Q
. For larger values of the strength
s
, we see that the directors begin to align in directions other than the vertical direction. Thus, our model reproduces the Fréedericksz transition.
