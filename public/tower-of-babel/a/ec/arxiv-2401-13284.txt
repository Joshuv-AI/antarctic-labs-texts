On the number of real forms of a complex variety
Gerard van der Geer, Xun Yu

Abstract
. We give a bound on the number of weighted real forms of a complex variety with finite automorphism group, where the weight is the inverse of the number of automorphisms of the real form. We give another bound involving the (isomorphism class of a) Sylow 2 2 -subgroup and as an application we give bounds on real forms of plane curves.



1.
Introduction

If
X
is a complex variety then a real form of
X
is a variety
Y
defined over

{\rm Spec}\;{\mathbb{R}}
such that
X\cong Y\times_{{\rm Spec}\;{\mathbb{R}}}{\rm Spec}\;{\mathbb{C}}
as
complex varieties.
Recently there has been a lot of activity dealing with complex varieties
with infinitely many real forms. In this note we deal with
quasi-projective varieties with finite automorphism group and show a formula
for the number of real forms. More precisely,
for a variety over a finite field with a finite automorphism group there is a mass formula that
says that the weighted sum of its twists equals
1
.
Here we derive an analogue of this
for the real forms of a complex algebraic variety.
We give also another bound
involving the (isomorphism class of a) Sylow
2
-subgroup of the automorphism group.

As an application we give a bound
on the number of real forms of plane curves.
Natanzon
[
Na78
]
proved that a smooth projective curve
X
of genus
g\geq 2

has at most
2(\sqrt{g}+1)
non-isomorphic real forms with at least one real point,
and this bound is attained for infinitely many odd genera
g
. Gromadzki and Izquierdo
[
GI98
]

proved that if
g
is even, then
X
has at most four non-isomorphic real forms
with at least one real point. Our result shows
that the number of non-isomorphic real forms of smooth plane curves is
uniformly bounded independent of their genera and independent of having a real point or not. In particular, a smooth
projective curve with at least nine non-isomorphic real forms cannot be a plane curve.

2.
A bound on the number of weighted real forms

Let
G:={\rm Gal}({\mathbb{C}}/{\mathbb{R}})=\{{\rm id}_{\mathbb{C}},c\}
, where
c
is complex conjugation
z\mapsto\bar{z}
.
Let
X
be a complex quasi-projective variety and
\pi:X\rightarrow{\rm Spec\,}{\mathbb{C}}
the structure morphism.
A
real form
of
X
is a scheme
Y
over
{\rm Spec\,}{\mathbb{R}}
such that

X\cong Y\times_{{\rm Spec\,}{\mathbb{R}}}{\rm Spec\,}{\mathbb{C}}

over
{\rm Spec\,}{\mathbb{C}}
. Two real forms are equivalent if they are isomorphic over
{\rm Spec\,}{\mathbb{R}}
.
The set of equivalence classes of real forms of
X
is denoted by
{\rm RF}(X)
.

A
real structure
of
X
is an anti-holomorphic involution

\sigma:X\to X,

that is,
\sigma
is an automorphism over
{\rm Spec\,}{\mathbb{R}}
with
\sigma^{2}={\rm id\hskip 0.43057pt}_{X}
and

\pi\circ\sigma=c\circ\pi
.
Two real structures
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
on
X
are said to be equivalent
if
\sigma^{\prime}=h^{-1}\circ\sigma\circ h
for some
h\in{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
.
We denote by
{\rm RS}(X)
the set of real structures
and by
\overline{\rm RS}(X)
the set of equivalence classes of real structures of
X
.
There is a
1
-
1
correspondence between
\overline{\rm RS}(X)
and
{\rm RF}(X)

given by associating to
\sigma
the quotient variety
X/\langle\sigma\rangle
, obtained by Galois descent
(see
[
Se02
, Ch.III,1.3]
).
An automorphism
f\in{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
descends to an automorphism of

X/\langle\sigma\rangle
over
{\rm Spec\,}{\mathbb{R}}
if and only if
f\circ\sigma=\sigma\circ f
.
Thus, as abstract groups, we may identify
{\rm Aut}(X/\langle\sigma\rangle/{\mathbb{R}})
with the centralizer

C_{{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})}(\sigma):=\{f\in{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})\mid f\circ\sigma=\sigma\circ f\}.

From now on, we fix a real form
Y_{0}
and an identification

X=Y_{0}\times_{{\rm Spec}\;{\mathbb{R}}}{\rm Spec}\;{\mathbb{C}}
.
Clearly,

\sigma_{0}:={\rm id}_{Y_{0}}\times c:X\rightarrow X

is a real structure of
X
. Then
G
acts on
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
by

f\mapsto c\cdot f:=\sigma_{0}\circ f\circ\sigma_{0}

for any
f\in{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
.
A
1-cocycle
of
G
in
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
is a map
s\mapsto a_{s}

of
G
to
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
such that

a_{st}=a_{s}\circ(s\cdot a_{t})\;\;\text{\rm for all $s,t\in G$}\,.

Since
G
is a cyclic group of order
2
generated by
c
,
we may naturally identify the set
Z^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))
of such
1
-cocycles with a subset of
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})

Z^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))=\{f\in{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})\mid f\circ(c\cdot f)={\rm id}_{X}\}.

Two
1
-cocycles
f
and
f^{\prime}
are
cohomologous

if there exists
h\in{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
such that

f^{\prime}=h^{-1}\circ f\circ(c\cdot h).

This is an equivalence relation in
Z^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))
, and the quotient set is the

first cohomology set

{\rm H}^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))
, see
[
Se02
]
.
The map
f\mapsto f\circ\sigma_{0}
defines a bijection
Z^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))\leftrightarrow{\rm RS}(X)

and induces a bijection
{\rm H}^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))\leftrightarrow\overline{\rm RS}(X)
.
Thus we have bijections

{\rm H}^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))\longleftrightarrow\overline{\rm RS}(X)\longleftrightarrow{\rm RF}(X)\,.

A mass formula for curves defined over finite fields was given in
[
GV92
, Proposition 5.1]
.
The following theorem gives an analogue for real forms of an algebraic variety.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
X/{\mathbb{C}}
be a quasi-projective algebraic variety with real form
Y_{0}
.
Suppose
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
is a finite group. Then we have

\sum_{Y\in{\rm RF}(X)}\frac{1}{\#\;{\rm Aut}(Y/{\mathbb{R}})}=\frac{\#\;Z^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))}{\#\;{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})}\leq 1\,.

Moreover, if
{\rm Aut}(X/{{\mathbb{C}}})
is not abelian the inequality is strict.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of
[
GV92
, Proposition 5.1]
. The group
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})

has a (right) action on
Z^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))
given by

Z^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))\times{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow Z^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})),\;(f,h)\mapsto h^{-1}\circ f\circ(c\cdot h).

The orbits correspond to the cohomology classes in
{\rm H}^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))
.
The stabilizer of an element
f\in Z^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))
is equal to

\{h\in{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})\mid(f\circ\sigma_{0})\circ h=h\circ(f\circ\sigma_{0})\}\,,

which can be identified with the automorphism group of the real form

Y=X/\langle f\circ\sigma_{0}\rangle
corresponding to the real structure
f\circ\sigma_{0}
of
X
.
Thus, by counting the cardinalities of the orbits, we find

\#\;Z^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))=\sum_{Y\in{\rm RF}(X)}\frac{\#\;{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})}{\#\;{\rm Aut}(Y/{\mathbb{R}})}\,.

Dividing by
\#{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
delivers the desired formula.
Since
Z^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))
is identified with a subset of
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
,
the inequality follows.

The obstruction for the product
fg
of two elements
f,g\in Z^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))\subseteq{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})

to lie in
Z^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))
is the commutator:

fg\in Z^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))
if and only if
f\,g\,c(f)\,c(g)=f\,g\,f^{-1}g^{-1}
is trivial.
The equality
Z^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))={\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
implies that
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
is abelian and
c
is the map

f\mapsto f^{-1}
on
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
. This shows the inequality for nonabelian
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

Suppose
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
is a finite group and
Y
is a real form of
X
.
If
{\rm Aut}(Y/{\mathbb{R}})=\{{\rm id}_{Y}\}
, then
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
is abelian and
\#{\rm RF}(X)=1
.

3.
A bound using a
2
-Sylow subgroup

Because of the condition
f\circ(c\cdot f)={\rm id}_{X}
for
Z^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{{\mathbb{C}}}))
we can often give a better bound.
Indeed, the number of real forms of
X
with finite
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
is bounded by the cardinality of the
first cohomology set of
G
in some Sylow
2
-subgroup of
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
is finite. Then the following statements hold:

(1) There is a Sylow
2
-subgroup
H\subseteq{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
such that
H
is a
G
-subgroup of
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
;

(2) For such
H
we have
\#\;{\rm RF}(X)\leq\#\;{\rm H}^{1}(G,H)
.

Proof.

Let
K:={\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})\rtimes\langle\sigma_{0}\rangle
. By the Sylow Theorem, there is a Sylow
2
-subgroup
S<K

such that
\sigma_{0}\in S
. Let
H:=S\cap{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
. Clearly
H
is a Sylow
2
-subgroup of
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})

and
H\cup H\sigma_{0}=S
. Then, we have
S=H\rtimes\langle\sigma_{0}\rangle
, which implies that
H
is a

G
-subgroup of
{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
and it proves (1).

We may identify the set of
1
-cocycles of
G
in
H
with a subset of
H

Z^{1}(G,H)=\{f\in H\mid f\circ(c\cdot f)={\rm id}_{X}\}.

We define a map

[formula]

\varphi:{\rm H}^{1}(G,H)\longrightarrow\overline{\rm RS}(X),\;[f]\mapsto[f\circ\sigma_{0}]\,.

Indeed,
\varphi
is well-defined. If
[f]=[f^{\prime}]\in{\rm H}^{1}(G,H)
, then there is an element
h\in H

such that
h^{-1}f\,(c\cdot h)=f^{\prime}
, that is,
h^{-1}f\sigma_{0}h\sigma_{0}=f^{\prime}
.
Thus, we have
h^{-1}f\sigma_{0}h=f^{\prime}\sigma_{0}
and
[f\sigma_{0}]=[f^{\prime}\sigma_{0}]\in\overline{\rm RS}(X)
.
In fact,
\varphi
factors via the natural map
{\rm H}^{1}(G,H)\to{\rm H}^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))
.

Next we show that
\varphi
is surjective. Let
[\sigma]\in\overline{\rm RS}(X)
.
Since
\sigma\in K\setminus{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
is of order
2
, by the Sylow theorem again, there exists
\alpha\in K

such that
\sigma^{\prime}:=\alpha\sigma\alpha^{-1}\in S
.
Note that
\sigma^{\prime}\sigma_{0}\in H
and

[\sigma^{\prime}]=\varphi([\sigma^{\prime}\sigma_{0}])\in{\rm Im}(\varphi)
.
So it suffices to show
[\sigma]=[\sigma^{\prime}]\in\overline{\rm RS}(X)
.
If
\alpha\in{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
, then clearly
[\sigma^{\prime}]=[\sigma]\in\overline{\rm RS}(X)
.
On the other hand, if
\alpha\notin{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
, then
\alpha=\beta\sigma_{0}
for
some
\beta\in{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
. Then

\sigma^{\prime}=\beta\sigma_{0}\sigma\sigma_{0}\beta^{-1}=(\beta\sigma_{0}\sigma)\sigma(\beta\sigma_{0}\sigma)^{-1},

which implies
[\sigma^{\prime}]=[\sigma]\in\overline{\rm RS}(X)
since
\beta\sigma_{0}\sigma\in{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
.

By the surjectivity of
\varphi
and the equality

\#{\rm RF}(X)=\#\overline{\rm RS}(X)
,
we have
\#\;{\rm RF}(X)\leq\#\;{\rm H}^{1}(G,H)
. This completes the proof of
(2)
.
∎

In the setting of Proposition
3.1

the Sylow
2
-subgroup
H
acts on
Z^{1}(G,H)
via
f\mapsto h^{-1}fc\cdot h

and the space of orbits is
H^{1}(G,H)
. We then count weighted orbits to get the following result.

Theorem 3.2
.

With
H\subset{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
a Sylow
2
-subgroup preserved by
G
, we have

\sum_{[Y]\in{\rm H}^{1}(G,H)}\frac{1}{\#({\rm Aut}(Y/{{\mathbb{R}}})\cap H)}=\frac{\#Z^{1}(G,H)}{\#H}\leq 1\,.

Moreover, if
H
is not abelian then the inequality is strict.

Proof.

The stabilizer of
f\in Z^{1}(G,H)

is
\{h\in H:h^{-1}f\sigma_{0}h=f\sigma_{0}\}
and can be identified with

{\rm Aut}(Y/{{\mathbb{R}}})\cap H
with
Y
the real form
X/\langle f\sigma_{0}\rangle
.
Counting the orbits of
Z^{1}(G,H)
thus yields the weighted number of real forms.
The inequality for non-abelian
H
follows in exactly the same manner as in the proof of Theorem
2.1
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

The sum in Theorem
3.2
is over elements
[Y]\in{\rm H}^{1}(G,H)
, not over

[Y]\in{\rm H}^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))
; but the map

\varphi:{\rm H}^{1}(G,H)\to{\rm H}^{1}(G,{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}}))\cong\overline{\rm RS}(X)
defined in (1) is surjective. Thus we have an inequality

\sum_{[Y]\in R}\frac{1}{\#({\rm Aut}(Y/{{\mathbb{R}}})\cap H)}\leq\frac{\#Z^{1}(G,H)}{\#H}\,.

with
R\subset{\rm H}^{1}(G,H)
a subset such that
\varphi_{|R}:R\to\overline{\rm RS}(X)

is a bijection. If one has information on the cardinality of the fibres of
\varphi

one can sharpen Proposition
3.1
(2) and Theorem

3.2
. A description of the fibres is given in

[
Se02
, Ch. I, §5.4. Cor. 2]
).

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
X
be a complex quasi-projective variety with finite automorphism group and
Y
a real form.
If
\#{\rm Aut}(Y/{\mathbb{R}})
is odd, then
\#{\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
is odd and
\#{\rm RF}(X)=1
.

Proof.

The left hand side of the equality in Theorem
3.2
has only one term, hence
we see
\#Z^{1}(G,H)=\#H
and
\#H^{1}(G,H)=1
. Since the product of two elements of

Z^{1}(G,H)
lies in
Z^{1}(G,H)
we see
as at the end of the proof of Theorem
2.1
that
H
is abelian and
c(h)=h^{-1}
for all
h\in H
.
But each element of order
2
of
H
defines an element of
Z^{1}(G,H)=H^{1}(G,H)
, hence
H
is trivial.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

If
Z
is a complex quasi-projective variety with finite

{\rm Aut}(Z/{\mathbb{C}})
of odd order, then
Z
has at most one real form,
up to isomorphism over
{\rm Spec\,}{\mathbb{R}}
.

4.
An application: real forms of plane curves

We prove a bound on the number of real forms of a plane curve. We start with remarks
on the cohomology of
G
-sets.

Let
H
be a finite group and with automorphism group
{\rm Aut}(H)
and let
\phi:G\rightarrow{\rm Aut}(H)

be a group homomorphism. Then we may view
H
as a
G
-group via
\phi
and the first cohomology set
{\rm H}^{1}(G,H)
is defined as in
[
Se02
]
.
We sometimes denote
{\rm H}^{1}(G,H)
by
{\rm H}^{1}(G,H_{\phi})
to emphasize the underlying action is
\phi
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
H
be a finite group. We use
m(H)
to denote the maximal cardinality of

{\rm H}^{1}(G,H_{\phi})
when
\phi
runs over all possible group homomorphisms
\phi:G\rightarrow{\rm Aut}(H)
.
We denote the minimal number of generators of a finite group
H
by
l(H)
.

Since
G\cong{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
, the set of group homorphisms from
G
to
{\rm Aut}(H)
corresponds
bijectively to the set of elements in
{\rm Aut}(H)
of order at most
2
.
If two homomorphisms
\phi_{i}:G\rightarrow{\rm Aut}(H)

(i=1,2)

have the property that
\phi_{1}(c)
and
\phi_{2}(c)
are conjugate in
{\rm Aut}(H)
, then

\#\;{\rm H}^{1}(G,H_{\phi_{1}})=\#\;{\rm H}^{1}(G,H_{\phi_{2}}).

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
H
be a finite abelian group. Then
m(H)=2^{l(H_{2})}
, where
H_{2}
is a Sylow
2
-subgroup of
H
.

Proof.

For the trivial action of
G
on
H
the cardinality of

{\rm H}^{1}(G,H_{\rm triv})
is equal to the number of conjugacy classes of elements
of order at most
2
in
H
.
The group
H_{2}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/d_{1}\mathbb{Z}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}/d_{r}\mathbb{Z}
,
where
d_{i}
divides
d_{i+1}
for each
1\leq i\leq r-1
,
and the elements of order at most
2
are obtained by taking the elements of order at most
2
in each
\mathbb{Z}/d_{i}\mathbb{Z}
.
This implies
\#\;{\rm H}^{1}(G,H_{\rm triv})=2^{l(H_{2})}
.
Thus we have the lower bound
m(H)\geq 2^{l(H_{2})}
.

Let
\phi:G\rightarrow{\rm Aut}(H)
be a group homomorphism. Since
H
is abelian we write
H
additively, and then

Z^{1}(G,H)=\{f\in H\mid\;0=c\cdot f+f=(c+1)\cdot f\}

is a subgroup of
H
, where
c\cdot x=\phi(c)(x)
for any
x\in H
.
Moreover,
{\rm H}^{1}(G,H)=Z^{1}(G,H)/K
, where
K=\{(c-1)h\mid h\in H\}
. The quotient group
Z^{1}(G,H)/K

is of exponent at most
2
; in fact, if
f\in Z^{1}(G,H)
, then
2f=(c-1)(-f)\in K
.
We denote by
(Z^{1}(G,H)/K)_{2}
(resp.
Z^{1}(G,H)_{2}
) a Sylow
2
-subgroup of
Z^{1}(G,H)/K
(resp.
Z^{1}(G,H)
). Note that
Z^{1}(G,H)_{2}
is mapped surjectively to
(Z^{1}(G,H)/K)_{2}
under the natural projection
Z^{1}(G,H)\rightarrow Z^{1}(G,H)/K
.
Then we have

l(Z^{1}(G,H)/K)=l((Z^{1}(G,H)/K)_{2})\leq l(Z^{1}(G,H)_{2})\leq l(H_{2})\,.

Thus,
\#\;{\rm H}^{1}(G,H)=\#\;Z^{1}(G,H)/K=2^{l(Z^{1}(G,H)/K)}\leq 2^{l(H_{2})}

and this gives the upper bound
m(H)\leq 2^{l(H_{2})}
. We conclude
m(H)=2^{l(H_{2})}
.
∎

Recall that a subgroup
K
of a group
H
is called a
characteristic subgroup

if
\varphi(K)=K
for every
\varphi\in{\rm Aut}(H)
. Clearly, a characteristic subgroup is a normal subgroup.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
K
be a characteristic subgroup of a finite group
H
. Then

m(H)\leq m(K)\,m(H/K).

Proof.

Let
\phi:G\rightarrow{\rm Aut}(H)
be a group homomorphism.
Then
H
is a
G
-group and
K
is a
G
-subgroup of
H

since
K
is a characteristic subgroup.
The resulting exact sequence of cohomology sets

{\rm H}^{1}(G,K)\rightarrow{\rm H}^{1}(G,H)\rightarrow{\rm H}^{1}(G,H/K)

implies by
[
Se02
, Chapter I, Section 5.5, Corollary 2]
that

\#\;{\rm H}^{1}(G,H)\leq m(K)\#\;{\rm H}^{1}(G,H/K)\leq m(K)\,m(H/K)\,.

From this, we conclude that
m(H)\leq m(K)\,m(H/K).

∎

Using this lemma, we can compute
m(D_{2n})

for the dihedral group
D_{2n}
of order
2n
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
n\geq 3
. Then

m(D_{2n})=\begin{cases}2&n\equiv 1\;(\bmod\;2)\\
4&n\equiv 0\;(\bmod\;2).\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

If
n
is odd (resp. even), then the number of conjugacy classes of elements of
H=D_{2n}
of
order at most
2
is
2
(resp. 4), which implies
m(H)\geq 2
(resp.
4
).
Let
K
denote the unique cyclic subgroup of
H
of order
n
.
Since
K
is a characteristic subgroup, Lemma
4.3
implies
that
m(H)\leq m(K)\,m(H/K)=2m(K)
. Then
m(K)=1
(resp.
2
) by Lemma
4.2
.
From this we have
m(H)\leq 2
(resp.
4
). Thus, we have
m(H)=2
(resp.
4
) if
n
is odd (resp. even).
∎

Example 4.5
.

Consider the quaternion group

H=Q_{8}=\{\pm 1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\}
.
It is known that
{\rm Aut}(Q_{8})
is isomorphic to the symmetric group

\mathfrak{S}_{4}
, which has exactly three conjugacy classes of elements of order at most
2
.
Their representatives give three homomorphisms

\phi_{t}:G\rightarrow{\rm Aut}(Q_{8})
(
t=1,2,3
) with
\phi_{1}(c)={\rm id}_{Q_{8}}
, and
\phi_{2}(c)
,
\phi_{3}(c)
given by

\phi_{2}(c):\;i\mapsto i,j\mapsto-j,k\mapsto-k,\qquad\phi_{3}(c):i\mapsto j,j\mapsto i,k\mapsto-k.

A computation shows
\#\;{\rm H}^{1}(G,{H}_{\phi_{t}})=2,3,1
for
t=1,2,3
respectively.
In particular,
m(Q_{8})=3>2=
the number of conjugacy classes of elements order at most
2
in
Q_{8}
.

Badr and Bars proved that
({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{2}
cannot act faithfully on any smooth
plane curve of degree
d=5
. By a similar argument, the same result holds for any odd
d\geq 5
.

Lemma 4.6

(cf.
[
BB16
, Lemma 10]
)
.

The group
({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{2}
is not isomorphic to a subgroup of the automorphism group of any smooth plane curve of odd degree
d\geq 5
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
H\subset{\rm GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
be a finite subgroup of order
2^{s}
,
s\geq 3
. Let
\pi:{\rm GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow{\rm PGL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
be the natural projection map. If
\pi(H)
is isomorphic to a dihedral group of order
2^{s-1}
, then
m(H)\leq 4
.

Proof.

A finite group of order
8
is one of the following groups:

{\mathbb{Z}}/8{\mathbb{Z}}
,
{\mathbb{Z}}/4{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
,
D_{8}
,
Q_{8}
,
({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{3}
. Since
({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{3}

is not a subgroup of
{\rm GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
, we have

H\cong

{\mathbb{Z}}/4{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
,
D_{8}
,
Q_{8}
if
s=3
. Then
m(H)\leq 4

by Lemmas
4.2
and
4.4
and Example
4.5
.
From now on, we assume
s\geq 4
. By
[
NPT08
, Theorem 4 (4)-(a)-(ii)]
,
H

is conjugate (in
{\rm GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
) to one of the following three groups:

\langle i\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{2^{s-1}}&0\\
0&\xi_{2^{s-1}}^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}\rangle,\;\langle\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{2^{s-1}}&0\\
0&\xi_{2^{s-1}}^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\rangle,\;\langle\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{2^{s-1}}&0\\
0&\xi_{2^{s-1}}^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}\rangle.\,,

where
\xi_{n}
indicates a primitive
n
th root of unity.
From this, we see that
H
has a unique cyclic subgroup of order
2^{s-1}
, say
N
.
Then
N
is a characteristic subgroup of
H
with
H/N\cong{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
. Thus, by Lemma
4.3
we find
m(H)\leq 4
.
∎

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
X
be a smooth plane curve of degree
d\geq 4
. Then

\#{\rm RF}(X)\leq\begin{cases}2&d\equiv 1\,(\bmod\;2)\\
4&d\equiv 2\;(\bmod\;4)\\
8&d\equiv 0\;(\bmod\;4)\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
H={\rm Aut}(X/{\mathbb{C}})
and let
H_{2}
be a Sylow
2
-subgroup of
H
. We use the results of Harui

[
Ha19
]
on automorphism groups of plane curves.

(1) Suppose
d
is odd. Then it suffices to show
m(H)\leq 2
. By
[
Ha19
, Theorem 2.3]
,
we only need to consider five cases: (a-i), (a-ii), (b-i), (b-ii), (c) as listed there.

(a-i)
H
is a cyclic group. Then
m(H)\leq 2
by Lemma
4.2
.

(a-ii)
H
fits in the following exact sequence

1\rightarrow N\rightarrow H\rightarrow K\rightarrow 1,

where
N
is a cyclic group of order dividing
d
and
K
is isomorphic to one of the
following groups:
{\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}}
,
D_{2n}
,
\mathfrak{A}_{4}
,
\mathfrak{S}_{4}
,
\mathfrak{A}_{5}
. Thus,
H
and
K
have
isomorphic Sylow
2
-subgroups. Lemma
4.6
does not allow
({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{2}
as a subgroup of
H
.
So
K
is either cyclic or
D_{2n}
with odd
n
and thus
H_{2}
is a cyclic group.
By Lemma
4.2
, we have
m(H)\leq m(H_{2})\leq 2
.

(b-i)
H
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
{\rm Aut}(F_{d})\cong({\mathbb{Z}}/d{\mathbb{Z}})^{2}\rtimes\mathfrak{S}_{3}
,
where
F_{d}
is the Fermat curve defined by
x^{d}+y^{d}+z^{d}=0
. Since
d
is odd,

H_{2}
is of order at most
2
. Then
m(H)\leq m(H_{2})\leq 2
.

(b-ii)
H
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
{\rm Aut}(C_{d})
, where
C_{d}
is the
Klein curve defined by
xy^{d-1}+yz^{d-1}+zx^{d-1}=0
. Since
{\rm Aut}(C_{d})
is
of odd order
3(d^{2}-3d+3)
(
[
Ha19
, Proposition 3.5]
), the group
H
is of odd order and
m(H)=1
.

(c)
H
is isomorphic to one of the following
6
groups:

\mathfrak{A}_{5}
,
{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{7})
,
\mathfrak{A}_{6}
,
H_{216}
,
({\mathbb{Z}}/3{\mathbb{Z}})^{2}\rtimes{\mathbb{Z}}/4{\mathbb{Z}}
,
({\mathbb{Z}}/3{\mathbb{Z}})^{2}\rtimes Q_{8}
.
Here
H_{216}
is the Hessian group of order
216
, and the last two groups are subgroups of
H_{216}
.

By Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
, we infer that
m((\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})^{2}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z})=2
. For
H=\mathfrak{A}_{5}
,
{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{7})
,
\mathfrak{A}_{6},H_{216}
,
(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})^{2}\rtimes Q_{8}
, the automorphism group
{\rm Aut}(H)
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{S}_{5}
,
{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{7})\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathfrak{S}_{6}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
,
H_{216}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
,
H_{216}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
respectively. Moreover, the number of conjugacy classes of elements of order at most
2
in
{\rm Aut}(H)
is equal to
3
,
3
,
4
,
3
,
3
respectively. Then we conclude that
m(H)=2
by a computation as the one in Example
4.5
. We give the details for the case
H=\mathfrak{A}_{5}
. Note that we have the isomorphism
\psi:\mathfrak{S}_{5}\rightarrow{\rm Aut}(\mathfrak{A}_{5})
given by
\psi(\alpha)(\beta)=\alpha\beta\alpha^{-1}
for any
\alpha\in\mathfrak{S}_{5}
,
\beta\in\mathfrak{A}_{5}
. Then
{\rm Aut}(\mathfrak{A}_{5})
has three conjugacy classes of elements of order at most
2
which are represented by
\psi(\alpha_{1})
,
\psi(\alpha_{2})
,
\psi(\alpha_{3})
, where
\alpha_{1}=(1)
,
\alpha_{2}=(12)(34)
,
\alpha_{3}=(12)
. These representatives give three homomorphisms
\phi_{t}:G\rightarrow{\rm Aut}(\mathfrak{A}_{5})
with
\phi_{t}(c)=\psi(\alpha_{t})
(
t=1,2,3
). Clearly, we have
\#{\rm H}^{1}(G,H_{\phi_{1}})=\#{\rm H}^{1}(G,H_{\rm triv})=2
. Since the automorphism
\psi(\alpha_{2})
is an inner automorphism given by the conjugation action of the element
\alpha_{2}
in
H=\mathfrak{A}_{5}
, by
[
Se02
, Ch. I, Proposition 35 bis]
, we have
\#{\rm H}^{1}(G,H_{\phi_{2}})=\#{\rm H}^{1}(G,H_{\rm triv})=2
. For
\#{\rm H}^{1}(G,H_{\phi_{3}})
, we observe that
H_{2}:=\{(1),(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}
is a Sylow
2
-subgroup of
H
preserved by
G
and a computation shows that
Z^{1}(G,H_{2})=\{(1),(12)(34)\}
, which implies
\#{\rm H}^{1}(G,H_{\phi_{3}})\leq\#{\rm H}^{1}(G,H_{2})\leq\#Z^{1}(G,H_{2})=2
. Therefore, we conclude that
m(\mathfrak{A}_{5})=2
. The cases
H={\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{7})
,
\mathfrak{A}_{6},H_{216}
,
(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})^{2}\rtimes Q_{8}
can be treated in a similar way.

(2) Suppose
d=4k+2
,
k\geq 1
. We apply
[
Ha19
, Theorem 2.3]
again.
Similar to (1), for cases (a-i), (b-ii), (c), we have
m(H)\leq 2
.
Then we only need to consider cases (a-ii) and (b-i).

(a-ii) We consider the subgroup of
{\rm PGL}(3,{\mathbb{C}})
consisting of elements
represented by matrices of the form

\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
,
A\in{\rm GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
.
Under identification of this group with
{\rm GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
, the natural projection

\pi:{\rm GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow{\rm PGL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
coincides with the map

\rho:{\rm PBD(2,1)}\rightarrow{\rm PGL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
in
[
Ha19
, Theorem 2.3]
.
Then
H
can be viewed as a subgroup of
{\rm GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
and it fits in the following exact sequence

1\rightarrow N\rightarrow H\xrightarrow{{}^{\pi|H}}K\rightarrow 1,

where
K\subset{\rm PGL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
is isomorphic to one of the following groups:

{\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}}
,
D_{2n}
,
\mathfrak{A}_{4}
,
\mathfrak{S}_{4}
,
\mathfrak{A}_{5}
. Since the order
|N|
of

divides
d

(see
[
Ha19
, Lemma 3.8]
),
we have
4\nmid|N|
. If
2\nmid|N|
, then
H_{2}\cong K_{2}
and we have
m(H)\leq m(H_{2})=m(K_{2})\leq 4
.
Thus, we only need to consider the case where
2
divides
|N|
. Then
H_{2}
fits in the following exact sequence

1\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\rightarrow H_{2}\xrightarrow{{}^{\pi|H_{2}}}K_{2}\rightarrow 1\,.

Clearly,
K_{2}
is either cyclic or a dihedral group. If
K_{2}
is cyclic,
then
H_{2}
is abelian and
m(H_{2})\leq 4
. So we may assume
K_{2}\cong D_{2^{s}}

for some
s\geq 2
. Then by Lemma
4.7
we have
4\geq m(H_{2})\geq m(H)
.

(b-i)
H
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
({\mathbb{Z}}/d{\mathbb{Z}})^{2}\rtimes\mathfrak{S}_{3}
.
Since a Sylow
2
-subgroup of
({\mathbb{Z}}/d{\mathbb{Z}})^{2}\rtimes\mathfrak{S}_{3}
is isomorphic to
D_{8}
,
the group
H_{2}
is a subgroup of
D_{8}
, which implies that
m(H)\leq m(H_{2})\leq 4
.

This completes the proof for
d\equiv 2\;(\bmod\;4)
.

(3) Suppose
d=4k
,
k\geq 1
. We claim that
m(M)\leq 8
for any finite subgroup
M
in
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{C})
. If
M
is abelian, then we may assume that
M
consists of diagonal matrices, which implies that
M
is generated by
2
elements and
m(M)\leq 4
by Lemma
4.2
. If
M
is not abelian, then the center
Z(M)
of
M
is a cyclic group consisting of scalar matrices and the quotient group

is isomorphic to a finite subgroup in
{\rm PGL}(2,\mathbb{C})
(i.e.,

,
\mathfrak{A}_{4}
,
\mathfrak{S}_{4}
,
\mathfrak{A}_{5}

, we infer that

Next we apply
[
Ha19
, Theorem 2.3]
as in (1) and (2). Similar to (1), for cases (a-i), (c), we have
m(H)\leq 2
. Then we only need to consider cases (a-ii), (b-i), (b-ii).

(a-ii) As in (2),
H
can be viewed as a subgroup of
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{C})
. Then

by the previous claim.

(b-i) As in (1),
H
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
{\rm Aut}(F_{d})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{2}\rtimes\mathfrak{S}_{3}
. Consider the finite subgroup
M_{1}\subset{\rm PGL}(3,\mathbb{C})
generated by the following matrices:
{\rm diag}(\xi_{d},1,1)
,
{\rm diag}(1,\xi_{d},1)
,
\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
. Here
\xi_{d}
is a primitive
d
th root of unity. Clearly,

contains a Sylow
2

{\rm Aut}(F_{d})
and

. Thus, a Sylow
2
-subgroup of

. Then by Proposition
3.1

.

(b-ii) As in (1), if

.

This completes the proof for

∎

Remark 4.9
.

Recently Sasaki
[
Sa23
]
computed the number of real forms of
all Fermat hypersurfaces of degree at least
3
. In particular, for Fermat curves
F_{d}

he showed that
\#\;{\rm RF}(F_{d})=2
(resp.
3
) if
d
is odd (resp. even).
Thus, our bound in Theorem
4.8
is optimal for odd
d
.
For even
d\geq 6
we can prove by a computation similar to the one in
[
Sa23
]

that the curve
X_{d}
defined by
x^{d}+y^{d}+z^{d}+y^{2}z^{d-2}=0
has
4
real forms.
In fact,
{\rm Aut}(X_{d}/{\mathbb{C}})
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}/d{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
and it is
generated by
(x:y:z)\mapsto(\xi_{d}x:y:z)
and
(x:y:z)\mapsto(x:-y:z)
with

\xi_{d}
a primitive
d
th root of unity. This implies that our
bound in Theorem
4.8
for
d\equiv 2\,(\bmod\,4)
is optimal.
For
d\equiv 0\,(\bmod\,4)
, we speculate that the optimal bound is
6
instead of
8
.
