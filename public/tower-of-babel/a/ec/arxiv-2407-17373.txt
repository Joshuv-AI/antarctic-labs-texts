A Particle Consensus Approach to solving nonconvex-nonconcave min-max problems
Giacomo Borghi, Hui Huang, Jinniao Qiu

Abstract
. We propose a zero-order optimization method for sequential min-max problems based on two populations of interacting particles. The systems are coupled so that one population aims to solve the inner maximization problem, while the other aims to solve the outer minimization problem. The dynamics are characterized by a consensus-type interaction with additional stochasticity to promote exploration of the objective landscape. Without relying on convexity or concavity assumptions, we establish theoretical convergence guarantees of the algorithm via a suitable mean-field approximation of the particle systems. Numerical experiments illustrate the validity of the proposed approach. In particular, the algorithm is able to identify a global min-max solution, in contrast to gradient-based methods, which typically converge to possibly suboptimal stationary points.



Keywords:
Nonconvex-nonconcave, min-max problems, consensus-based optimization, derivative-free optimization, sequential games

AMS subject classifications:
65C35; 65K05; 90C56; 35Q90; 35Q83

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

The min-max optimization problem, also known as the saddle point problem, represents a compelling yet complex category of challenges. The objective here is to determine a value for the argument that minimizes the objective function’s value even in the worst-case scenario within a particular class of objective functions. This approach was initially introduced in the realm of multi-player zero-sum games in game theory
[
62
]
. Such problems are widespread in various domains, including mathematics, biology, social sciences, and especially in economics
[
55
]
. In recent years, min-max optimization has also seen broad applications in signal processing, such as distributed processing
[
13
]
, robust transceiver design, fair resource allocation
[
50
]
, and communication strategies in the presence of jammers
[
31
]
. Moreover, this optimization technique has gained prominence in modern machine learning contexts, including generative adversarial networks (GANs)
[
32
]
, fair machine learning
[
51
]
, adversarial training
[
52
]
, and multi-agent reinforcement learning
[
57
]
. For an overview of further applications and challenges, see
[
59
]
.

To initiate our discussion, consider the scenario involving two agents. The first agent aims to minimize the objective function
\mathcal{E}~:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
, whereas the second agent’s goal is to maximize it, namely

(1.1)

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{1}}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{2}}}\mathcal{E}(x,y).

In this context,
x
and
y
denote the optimization variables, with
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
representing feasible sets. The objective function
\mathcal{E}
may exhibit non-convexity with respect to
x
and non-concavity with respect to
y
. Specifically, within the framework of GANs,
x
symbolizes the parameters of a generator, whereas
y
corresponds to those of a discriminator. Here,
\mathcal{E}(\cdot,\cdot)
serves as the cost function for the generator, that is equal to the negative of the discriminator’s cost function. In adversarial training,
x
is the weight of the neural network that one aims to train while
y
models possible adversarial attacks, and
\mathcal{E}(\cdot,\cdot)
the loss function.

When min-max problem is defined in a symmetrical way, namely

(1.2)

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{E}(x,y)=\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{E}(x,y)\,,

a well-known notion of optimality originating from game theory is the one of Nash equilibria (also referred to as saddle points)
[
56
]
, where neither of the players has anything to gain by changing only his own strategy.
This concept is formalized within the following definition
[
65
]
:

Definition 1.1
.

A point
(x^{*},y^{*})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
is called Nash equilibrium or saddle point of a function
\mathcal{E}
if it holds

\mathcal{E}(x^{*},y)\leq\mathcal{E}(x^{*},y^{*})\leq\mathcal{E}(x,y^{*})\quad\text{for all }(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\,.

In other words, we have simultaneously:

x^{*}\in\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{E}(x,y^{*}),\quad y^{*}\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{E}(x^{*},y)\,.

According to
[
44
]
, the symmetrical property (
1.2
) is a sufficient and necessary condition for the existence of Nash equilibrium
(x^{*},y^{*})
in closed convex domains. This is corresponding to simultaneous games, where each player chooses its action without the knowledge of the action chosen by the other player, so both players act simultaneously. While in sequential games, there is an intrinsic order (
\min_{x}\max_{y}
and
\max_{y}\min_{x}
) that players take their actions. Actually, GANs and adversarial training are in fact sequential games in their standard formulations, where Nash equilibria may not exist
[
21
]
. For example, in the formulation of GANs, one first find the optimal parameters of the discriminator
y
based on the parameters of the generator
x
, and then optimize over
x
.
In this case, we first introduce the upper envelope functions:

(1.3)

\overline{\mathcal{E}}(x)=\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{E}(x,y)\,,

which is allowed to take value
+\infty
.
Then it leads to the following solution concept
[
65
]
:

Definition 1.2
.

Point
(x^{*},y^{*})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
is a global min-max solution to (
1.1
) if

(1.4)

x^{*}\in\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\overline{\mathcal{E}}(x)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad y^{*}=y^{*}(x^{*})\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{E}(x^{*},y)\,,

in other words, for all
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
it holds

(1.5)

\mathcal{E}(x^{*},y)\leq\mathcal{E}(x^{*},y^{*})=\overline{\mathcal{E}}(x^{*})\leq\overline{\mathcal{E}}(x)\,.

Similarly one can use the lower envelope function
\underline{\mathcal{E}}(y):=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{E}(x,y)
to define a global max-min solution to a max-min problem
\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{E}(x,y)
.

To better understand the definitions, we introduce the following examples. The first example considers a simple bilinear (hence convex-concave) objective function:

Example 1.1
.

Consider the bilinear objective function

\mathcal{E}(x,y):=xy,\quad(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\,.

It is easy to verify that global min-max points are
\{0\}\times{\mathbb{R}}
while global max-min points are
{\mathbb{R}}\times\{0\}
. Taking the intersection we obtain the unique global saddle point
(0,0)
.

In the next example, the objective function
\mathcal{E}
possesses both global min-max and max-min points, yet it lacks a saddle point.

Example 1.2
.

Consider the bivariate function

\mathcal{E}(x,y)=x^{4}/4-x^{2}/2+xy,\quad(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\,.

One can check that global min-max points occur at
\{0\}\times{\mathbb{R}}
with a value
0
, whereas the global max-min points are located at
(\pm 1,0)
with values of
-1/4
. Nonetheless, there are no saddle points.

Although the concept of global min-max is clearly defined in Definition
1.2
, it encounters significant challenges when applied to nonconvex-nonconcave scenarios. The primary issue is the lack of an efficient algorithm for finding a global minimizer
x^{*}
of a non-convex function
\overline{\mathcal{E}}(x)
. This challenge can be partially addressed by aiming for a local minimizer or a stationary point. Moreover, once
x^{*}
is determined, finding a global maximizer
y^{*}
for the non-concave function
\mathcal{E}(x^{*},y)
is NP-hard. While it may be tempting to settle for a local solution, doing so compromises the initial notion of optimality for
x^{*}
. Therefore, there is a pressing need for a new method to effectively address these complexities arising from nonconvex-nonconcave problems. In contrast with
[
42
]
where a consensus-based method is introduced for finding global saddle points satisfying Definition
1.1
, this paper aims to propose a new method for finding global min-max solutions that only meet the criteria of Definition
1.2
. This method is especially relevant where the condition of symmetry, as in (
1.2
), may not hold, as illustrated by Example
1.2
.

1.2.
Literature review

Due to the aforementioned applications in Machine Learning, there is a renewed interest in the development of solvers for nonconvex-nonconcave minmax problems. The Gradient Descent Ascent (GDA) algorithm is a first order algorithms which couples a descent dynamics in the
x
direction, with an ascent one in
y
one. This can be done simultaneously (single-loop algorithm), or by alternating the two steps (double-loops). While being suitable for convex-concave problems
[
17
]
, GDA fails to convergence even in simple problems like example
1.1
, see e.g.
[
30
]
. More generally, in
[
40
,
48
]
, the authors investigate the convergence properties of gradient-based methods in the nonconvex-nonconcave settings proving existence of cycles or attractors that are not global min-max solutions in the sense of Definition
1.2
. Many improved variants of GDA have been proposed and analyzed to mitigate this effects see, for example,
[
30
,
66
,
63
]
to name a few. We note that gradient-based methods aim to converge to stationary solutions which are not necessary global minmax solutions, unless the objective function satisfies additional assumptions such as the two-sided Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality condition
[
64
]
. For additional details on the relation between (local) minimax solutions, stationary points, and Nash equilibria, we refer to
[
45
]
.

Alternative to GDA-like dynamics, gradient-free (zero-order) methods aim to solve (
1.1
) by only relying on the function evaluations. This is, for instance, the scenario where the attacker, in the GAN framework, has only access to the model’s output.
This can be done, for example, via a randomized gradient estimation
[
49
]
, model approximations of the objective function
[
53
]
, or evolutonary algorithms
[
19
,
36
,
47
,
15
]
. The last class comprises metaheuristic startegies
[
3
,
28
]
where a set of particles explore the search space via deterministic and random interactions. Authors in
[
47
,
15
]
, in particular, adapt the particle-swarm evolution
[
46
]
, while in
[
19
,
36
]
the genetic algorithm paradigm
[
39
]
is used. We note that, despite their popularity in the field of non-smooth optimization, such evolutionary methods typically lack convergence guarantees.

Our approach will be inspired by the framework of consensus-based optimization (CBO), initially introduced by
[
8
,
58
]
. CBO belongs to the family of global optimization methodologies, which leverages systems of interacting particles to achieve consensus around global minimizers of the cost functions. As part of the broader class of metaheuristics, CBO orchestrates interactions between local improvement procedures and global strategies, utilizing both deterministic and stochastic processes. This interplay ultimately results in an efficient and robust procedure for exploring the solution space of the cost functions.
A distinctive advantage of the CBO approach is its gradient-free nature, making it particularly appealing for optimizing cost functions that are either non-smooth or where the computation of derivatives is overly burdensome.

In response to a wide range of applications, the foundational CBO model has been extended and refined to accommodate diverse scenarios. These enhancements include the incorporation of memory aspects or gradient information
[
60
,
61
,
18
]
, and the integration of momentum mechanisms
[
14
]
. Moreover, CBO has been adapted to address challenges in global optimization on compact manifolds
[
22
,
35
]
, to tackle general constraints
[
6
,
12
]
, to optimize functions with multiple minimizers
[
7
,
27
]
, to solve multi-objective problems
[
4
,
5
]
, and to sample from specific distributions
[
9
]
. Further applications of CBO have spanned high-dimensional machine learning issues
[
10
,
23
]
, asset allocation tasks
[
1
]
, clustered federated learning
[
11
]
, and more recently, non-convex multiplayer games
[
16
]
. Finally, during the preparation of this work, we observed that the authors in
[
37
]
—before their work was posted online—independently developed a CBO dynamic similar to ours for bi-level optimization problems. However, their focus was primarily on the numerical aspects, employing a multi-time scaling approach without providing proof of global convergence.

1.3.
Contribution

In this paper, we propose a zero-order, consensus-based method aimed at identifying global min-max solutions that comply with Definition
1.2
. The algorithm employs two sets of interacting particles,
N
in the
x
direction, and
N
in the
y
direction, which are coupled together to specifically solve the sequential min-max problem (
1.1
).
The particle system is described via a system of
2N
-dimensional SDEs.

To study the large-time behavior of the dynamics, we propose a mean-field approximation that reduces the system to only two mean-field SDEs of Mckean–Vlasov type. Inspired by
[
25
]
we establish convergence towards global min-max problems under specific assumptions on the objective function. We assume, in particular, the uniqueness of the solution and the growth conditions around it for the objective function. Our analysis does not require any convexity/concavity assumption, nor even differentiability of
\mathcal{E}
.

We test the proposed algorithm on benchmark min-max problems which are nonconvex-nonconcave, namely the ”Bilinearly-coupled”
[
33
]
, the
”Forsaken”
[
40
]
, and the ”Sixth-order polynomial” problem
[
63
]
. While state-of-the-art gradient-based methods converges to sub-optimal stationary points
[
66
]
, the proposed CBO method successfully computes optimal solutions in the sense of Definition
1.2
, see Figure
2
. Some numerical tests are also conducted to investigate how different parameters of the algorithms affect the convergence properties.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we illustrate the particle system and its mean-field approximation. Section
3
is devoted to the theoretical analysis of the mean-field dynamics and its convergence properties. Results on benchmark problems are presented in Section
4
, while some technical aspects of the proofs are presented in the Appendix.

The particle dynamics

For simplicity, we take
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
as the extensions to cases with
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
having different dimensions are straightforward. Specifically, our CBO dynamic for min-max problem is a collection of
2N

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-valued particles
\{(X_{t}^{i},Y_{t}^{i})\}_{i\in[N]}
satisfying the following Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs)

(2.1a)

\displaystyle dX_{t}^{i}=-\lambda_{1}(X_{t}^{i}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{N,X}))dt+\sigma_{1}D(X_{t}^{i}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{N,X}))dB_{t}^{X,i}\,,\quad i\in[N]:=\{1,\dots,N\}\,,

(2.1b)

\displaystyle dY_{t}^{i}=-\lambda_{2}(Y_{t}^{i}-Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{N,Y},X_{t}^{i}))dt+\sigma_{2}D(Y_{t}^{i}-Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{N,Y},X_{t}^{i}))dB_{t}^{Y,i}\,,\quad i\in[N],

where

(2.2)

Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{N,Y},X_{t}^{i})=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y\omega_{-\beta}^{\mathcal{E}}(X_{t}^{i},y)\rho^{N,Y}(t,dy)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega_{-\beta}^{\mathcal{E}}(X_{t}^{i},y)\rho^{N,Y}(t,dy)},\quad\rho^{N,Y}(t,dy)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{Y_{t}^{i}}dy\,,

and

(2.3)

X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{N,X})=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{N,Y},x))\rho^{N,X}(t,dx)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{N,Y},x))\rho^{N,X}(t,dx)},\quad\rho^{N,X}(t,dx)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{t}^{i}}dx\,.

Here
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}>0
are drift and diffusion parameters,
\{B_{t}^{m,i}\}_{m=X,Y;i\in[N]}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
are independent standard Brownian motions, and
D(X):=\diag(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})
(anisotropic), or
D(X):=|X|
(isotropic), for all
X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. However, in the subsequent analysis, we will specifically focus on the anisotropic case, and let
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\lambda
and
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}=\sigma
for simplicity.
The system is complemented with identically and independently distributed (i.i.d.) initial data
\{(X_{0}^{i},Y_{0}^{i})\}_{i\in[N]}
with respect to the common law
f_{0}
.
The first term on the right hand-side of (
2.1
) is a drift that pulls the particles towards a current consensus point
\left(X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{N,X}),Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{N,Y},X_{t}^{i})\right)
, in which
\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}
is a weight function defined as

(2.4)

\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,y):=\exp\left(-\alpha\mathcal{E}(x,y)\right)\,,\quad\text{for all }(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,.

The introduction of the second stochastic term in (
2.1
) is intended to promote exploration of the energy landscape of the objective function. When the consensus is achieved, meaning
(X^{i},Y^{i})=\left(X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho^{N,X}),Y_{\beta}(\rho^{N,Y},X^{i})\right)
, both the drift and diffusion terms vanish. The choice of the weight function (
2.4
) comes from the well-known Laplace’s principle
[
54
,
20
]
, which states that for any probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there holds for any fixed
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

(2.5)

\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,y)\mu(dx)\right)\right)=\inf\limits_{x\in\rm{supp}(\mu)}\mathcal{E}(x,y)\,.

Then the intuition behind the dynamics stems from Laplace’s principle, which implies that for any fixed
x
, it holds that
Y_{\beta}(\rho^{Y},x)\to\arg\max\limits_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathcal{E}(x,y)=:\overline{y}(x)
as
\beta\to\infty
. This leads to
\overline{\mathcal{E}}(x)=\mathcal{E}(x,\overline{y}(x))\approx\mathcal{E}(x,Y_{\infty}(\rho^{Y},x))
.
Meanwhile, we have that

X_{\alpha,\beta=\infty}(\rho^{X})=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,Y_{\infty}(\rho^{Y},x))\rho^{X}(dx)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,Y_{\infty}(\rho^{Y},x))\rho^{X}(dx)}\approx\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,\overline{y}(x))\rho^{X}(dx)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,\overline{y}(x))\rho^{X}(dx)}=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\omega_{\alpha}^{\overline{\mathcal{E}}}(x)\rho^{X}(dx)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega_{\alpha}^{\overline{\mathcal{E}}}(x)\rho^{X}(dx)}.

Applying Laplace’s principle once more, one would expect that
X_{\alpha=\infty,\beta=\infty}\approx\arg\min\overline{\mathcal{E}}(x)=x^{*}
and at the same time
Y_{\infty}(\rho^{Y},x^{*})=\overline{y}(x^{*})=y^{*}
.

The theoretical analysis of the convergence behavior for a CBO method can be approached from two distinct perspectives. The first approach entails a direct examination of the behavior at the microscopic level of individual particles, as detailed in
[
34
]
, through an analysis of a particle system with common noise. Conversely, the approach embraced in this paper pivots to an examination at the macroscopic scale, concentrating on the mean-field equation that emerges as the number
N
of particles approaches infinity. This macroscopic or mean-field approach has been effectively applied in a series of prior studies
[
24
,
26
,
25
,
41
]
.
Indeed, as the number of particles
N\to\infty
, the mean-field limit
[
43
,
29
]
result would imply that our CBO particle dynamic (
2.1
) well approximates solutions of the following mean-field kinetic Mckean–Vlasov type equations

(2.6a)

\displaystyle d\overline{X}_{t}=-\lambda(\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X}))dt+\sigma D(\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X}))dB_{t}^{X},

(2.6b)

\displaystyle d\overline{Y}_{t}=-\lambda(\overline{Y}_{t}-Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y},\overline{X}_{t}))dt+\sigma D(\overline{Y}_{t}-Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y},\overline{X}_{t}))dB_{t}^{Y}\,,

where

(2.7)

Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y},\overline{X}_{t})=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y\omega_{-\beta}^{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{X}_{t},y)\rho^{Y}(t,dy)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega_{-\beta}^{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{X}_{t},y)\rho^{Y}(t,dy)},\quad\rho^{Y}(t,dy)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,dx,dy)\,,

and

(2.8)

X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X})=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y},x))\rho^{X}(t,dx)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y},x))\rho^{X}(t,dx)},\quad\rho^{X}(t,dx)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,dx,dy)\,,

with
f(t,x,v)
being the distribution of
(\overline{X}_{t},\overline{V}_{t})
at time
t
, which makes the set of equations (
2.6
) nonlinear.

The current paper centers on investigating the convergence of the proposed CBO variant in the context of finding global min-max solutions. The well-posedness results regarding the CBO particle system (
2.1
) and its mean-field dynamics (
2.6
) have been omitted from this work. However, it is worth noting that these results closely resemble the well-posedness theorems presented in
[
42
, Theorem 3, Theorem 6]
, where a CBO dynamic is introduced for two player zero-sum games with the goal of finding saddle points satisfying Definition
1.1
. Moreover, in the following we shall assume the solution to (
2.6
) has the regularity
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}_{t}|^{4}+|\overline{Y}_{t}|^{4}]<\infty
for any time horizon
T>0
, which can be guaranteed by the assumption on the initial data
\mathbb{E}[|X_{0}|^{4}+|Y_{0}|^{4}]<\infty
.

The main objective of this work is to establish the convergence of the mean-field dynamics
(\overline{X}_{t},\overline{Y}_{t})
in (
2.6
) to the global min-max point
(x^{*},y^{*})
as
t
and
\alpha
approach infinity. Inspired by
[
25
]
, we introduce the variance functions as follows:

(2.9)

\mathcal{V}^{X}(t):=\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}_{t}-x^{*}|^{2}]\,,\text{and}\quad\mathcal{V}^{Y}(t):=\mathbb{E}[|\overline{Y}_{t}-y^{*}|^{2}]\,,\quad\text{for}\ t>0\,.

By analyzing the decay behavior of the (cumulative) variance function
\mathcal{V}(t):=\mathcal{V}^{X}(t)+\mathcal{V}^{Y}(t)
we establish the convergence of the CBO dynamics to the global min-max point. Specifically, we demonstrate that the function
V(t)
decays exponentially, achieving any desired level of accuracy
\varepsilon>0
with a decay rate controllable through the parameters of the CBO method.

3.
Global convergence in mean-field law

In this section, we present our main result about the global convergence in mean-field law for objective functions satisfying the following conditions. Our proof methodology draws upon the approaches
found in
[
25
]
, yet it introduces a novel perspective
through the application of our new CBO model.

Assumption 1
.

Throughout this section, the objective function
\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
satisfies

(1)

there exist some constants
\underline{C}_{\mathcal{E}}
and
\overline{C}_{\mathcal{E}}
such that

(3.1)

\underline{C}_{\mathcal{E}}\leq\mathcal{E}(x,y)\leq\overline{C}_{\mathcal{E}}\quad\mbox{ for any }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

(2)

there exists some
L_{\mathcal{E}}>0
such that for any
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
it holds that

(3.2)

|\mathcal{E}(x_{1},y_{1})-\mathcal{E}(x_{2},y_{2})|\leq L_{\mathcal{E}}(1+|x_{1}|+|x_{2}|+|y_{1}|+|y_{2}|)(|x_{1}-x_{2}|+|y_{1}-y_{2}|)\,.

(3)

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exists some
\overline{y}(x)
such that

(3.3)

\overline{y}(x)=\arg\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathcal{E}(x,y)\,.

Especially
\overline{y}(x^{*})=y^{*}
and
x^{*}=\arg\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\overline{\mathcal{E}}(x)=\arg\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathcal{E}(x,\overline{y}(x))
. Moreover there exists some constants
\overline{c}_{1},\overline{c}_{2}>0
such that

(3.4)

\displaystyle|\overline{y}(x_{1})-\overline{y}(x_{2})|\leq\overline{c}_{1}|x_{1}-x_{2}|\quad\mbox{ for any }x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(3.5)

\displaystyle|\overline{y}(x)|\leq\overline{c}_{2}\quad\mbox{ for any }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

(4)

there exist
\mathcal{E}_{\infty},\eta,\nu,R_{0}>0
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(3.6)

Remark 3.1
.

In the above assumption, condition
(i)
states that the objective function is bounded from above and below. This is achievable by considering the function

instead, which is bounded and retains the same min-max solution as

. Condition

requires

to be locally Lipschitz.

is tailored to min-max problems, necessitating that the optimized function

be Lipschitz continuous and uniformly bounded, aligning with the maximum theorem
[

, Theorem 3.6]
. It is noteworthy that our numerical experiments suggest the possibility of relaxing or even eliminating condition
(iii)

are standard in the analysis of CBO methods (c.f.
[

,
11
,
16
,

) and pertain to the tractability of the landscape of
\mathcal{E}
around
(x^{*},y^{*})
and in the farfield. The assumption does not mandate any convexity-concavity or differentiability requirements; for example, one may check the nonconvex–nonconcave nondifferentiable function
\mathcal{E}(x,y)=-(y-(1+x^{2})^{-1}\sin(x))^{2}(1+(y-(1+x^{2})^{-2}\sin(x))^{2})^{-1}+(1+\sin^{2}x)^{-1}|\tanh x|
for

Next we recall the variance functions

(3.12)

\mathcal{V}^{X}(t)=\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}_{t}-x^{*}|^{2}]\mbox{ and }\mathcal{V}^{Y}(t)=\mathbb{E}[|\overline{Y}_{t}-y^{*}|^{2}].

Then one has the following lemma for a differential inequality of variance functions.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
satisfy Assumption
1
(i)-(ii), and we assume that
\lambda,\sigma
satisfy

2\lambda-\sigma^{2}>0
. Then the variance function
\mathcal{V}(t)=\mathcal{V}^{X}(t)+\mathcal{V}^{Y}(t)
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{V}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle\leq-(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})\mathcal{V}(t)+2(\lambda+\sigma^{2})(\mathcal{V}(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(|x^{*}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X})|+\mathbb{E}[|y^{*}-Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y},\overline{X}_{t})|]\right)

(3.13)

\displaystyle\quad+\sigma^{2}\left(|x^{*}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X})|^{2}+\mathbb{E}[|y^{*}-Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y},\overline{X}_{t})|^{2}]\right)\,.

Proof.

Applying Itô’s formula gives that

(3.14)

\displaystyle d|\overline{X}_{t}-x^{*}|^{2}=2\lambda(\overline{X}_{t}-x^{*})d\overline{X}_{t}+\sigma^{2}|\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X})|^{2}dt\,,

which implies

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{V}^{X}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-2\lambda\mathbb{E}[(\overline{X}_{t}-x^{*})\cdot(\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X}))]+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X})|^{2}]

\displaystyle=-2\lambda\mathcal{V}^{X}(t)-2\lambda\mathbb{E}[(\overline{X}_{t}-x^{*})\cdot(x^{*}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X}))]+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X})|^{2}]

(3.15)

\displaystyle\leq-(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})\mathcal{V}^{X}(t)+2(\lambda+\sigma^{2})(\mathcal{V}^{X}(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}|x^{*}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X})|+\sigma^{2}|x^{*}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X})|^{2}\,.

Similarly one also has

(3.16)

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{V}^{Y}(t)}{dt}\leq-(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})\mathcal{V}^{Y}(t)+2(\lambda+\sigma^{2})(\mathcal{V}^{Y}(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}[|y^{*}-Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y},\overline{X}_{t})|]+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}[|y^{*}-Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y},\overline{X}_{t})|^{2}]\,.

This completes the proof.
∎

The main idea underpinning our main convergence result consists of showing that

(3.17)

\frac{d\mathcal{V}(t)}{dt}\leq-\frac{1}{2}(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})\mathcal{V}(t)\,,

which formally requires that

\displaystyle 2(\lambda+\sigma^{2})(\mathcal{V}(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(|x^{*}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X})|+\mathbb{E}[|y^{*}-Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y},\overline{X}_{t})|]\right)

(3.18)

\displaystyle+\sigma^{2}\left(|x^{*}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X})|^{2}+\mathbb{E}[|y^{*}-Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y},\overline{X}_{t})|^{2}]\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})\mathcal{V}(t)\,.

This condition is fulfilled by ensuring that
|x^{*}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X})|^{2}+\mathbb{E}[|y^{*}-Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y},\overline{X}_{t})|^{2}]
remains sufficiently small. To establish this, we shall utilize a quantitative estimate of the Laplace principle.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let Assumption
1
(i)-(v) hold. For each

and

(3.19)

\displaystyle|\overline{y}(x)-Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y},x)|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{(2q)^{\nu}}{\eta}+\frac{\exp\left(-\beta q\right)}{\rho_{t}^{Y}(\{y:y\in B_{r}(\overline{y}(x))\})}\int|y-\overline{y}(x)|\rho_{t}^{Y}(dy)\,.

.

Such

1

(v)

and a suitably large value for

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{r}\geq r>0
, and using Jensen’s inequality one can deduce

\displaystyle|\overline{y}(x)-Y_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y},x)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{\widetilde{r}}(\overline{y}(x))}|y-\overline{y}(x)|\frac{\omega_{-\beta}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,y)\rho_{t}^{Y}(dy)}{\|\omega_{-\beta}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,\cdot)\|_{L_{1}(\rho_{t}^{Y})}}

(3.20)

\displaystyle+\int_{(B_{\widetilde{r}}(\overline{y}(x)))^{c}}|x-\overline{y}(x)|\frac{\omega_{-\beta}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,y)\rho_{t}^{Y}(dy)}{\|\omega_{-\beta}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,\cdot)\|_{L_{1}(\rho_{t}^{Y})}}\,.

The first term is bounded by
\widetilde{r}
since

for all

. Moreover, it follows from Markov’s inequality that

\displaystyle\|\omega_{-\beta}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,\cdot)\|_{L_{1}(\rho_{t}^{Y})}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\exp\left(\beta(\mathcal{E}(x,\overline{y}(x))-\mathcal{E}_{r}(x))\right)\rho_{t}^{Y}\left(\{y:\exp(\beta\mathcal{E}(x,y))\geq\exp\left(\beta(\mathcal{E}(x,\overline{y}(x))-\mathcal{E}_{r}(x))\right)\}\right)

\displaystyle\exp\left(\beta(\mathcal{E}(x,\overline{y}(x))-\mathcal{E}_{r}(x))\right)\rho_{t}^{Y}(\{y:\mathcal{E}(x,y)\geq\mathcal{E}(x,\overline{y}(x))-\mathcal{E}_{r}(x)\})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\exp\left(\beta(\mathcal{E}(x,\overline{y}(x))-\mathcal{E}_{r}(x))\right)\rho_{t}^{Y}(\{y:y\in B_{r}(\overline{y}(x))\})\,.

Hence for the second term we have

\displaystyle\int_{(B_{\widetilde{r}}(\overline{y}(x)))^{c}}|y-\overline{y}(x)|\frac{\omega_{-\beta}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,y)\rho_{t}^{Y}(dy)}{\|\omega_{-\beta}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,\cdot)\|_{L_{1}(\rho_{t}^{Y})}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\exp\left(\beta(\mathcal{E}(x,\overline{y}(x))-\mathcal{E}_{r}(x))\right)\rho_{t}^{Y}(\{y:y\in B_{r}(\overline{y}(x))\})}\int_{(B_{\widetilde{r}}(\overline{y}(x)))^{c}}|y-\overline{y}(x)|\omega_{-\beta}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,y)\rho_{t}^{Y}(dy)

\displaystyle\widetilde{r}+\frac{\exp\left(\beta(\sup_{y\in(B_{\widetilde{r}}(\overline{y}(x)))^{c}}\mathcal{E}(x,y)+\mathcal{E}_{r}(x)-\mathcal{E}(x,\overline{y}(x)))\right)}{\rho_{t}^{Y}(\{y:y\in B_{r}(\overline{y}(x))\})}\int|y-\overline{y}(x)|\rho_{t}^{Y}(dy)\,.

\displaystyle\widetilde{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q+\mathcal{E}_{r}(x))^{\nu}}{\eta}\geq\frac{\mathcal{E}_{r}(x)^{\nu}}{\eta}=\frac{\left(\sup_{y\in B_{r}(\overline{y}(x))}\left|\mathcal{E}(x,y)-\mathcal{E}(x,\overline{y}(x))\right|\right)^{\nu}}{\eta}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{y\in B_{r}(\overline{y}(x))}|y-\overline{y}(x)|=r

We now validate the performance of the proposed CBO dynamics (

To obtain the actual numerical algorithm, we discretize the SDE particle system via explicit Euler–Maruyama scheme

\displaystyle X_{(k+1)}^{i}=X_{(k)}^{i}-\lambda\Delta t(X_{(k)}^{i}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{(k)}^{N,X}))+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}D(X_{(k)}^{i}-X_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{(k)}^{N,X}))B_{(k)}^{X,i}\,,\quad i\in[N]\,,

The error is computed as the squared norm from the (closest) global minimax point

|(X^{i^{*}},Y^{j^{*}})-(x^{*},y^{*})|^{2}.

The particles positions are randomly initialized at the border of the search space
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
to avoid the possibility of initializing particles close the global solutions.

We recall that the proposed convergence analysis for the particles dynamics has been carried out by relying on the mean-field approximation (
2.6
) which is expected to be exact as
N\to\infty
. Therefore, the finite-particle system introduces an additional approximation error to the convergence established in Theorem
3.4
. From Figure
4a
we note that the error consistently decreases as the number of particles per population
N
increases. The improvement is substantial up until approximately
N=120
particles, and it reaches a plateau when more than
N=10^{2}
particles per population are used. It also interesting to notice the interquantile range decreases as
N
increases. This is consistent with the mean-field limit assumption, which assume that the particles’ empirical measure approaches a deterministic distribution as the number of particles
N
approaches infinity.

Other parameters that play a central role in the convergence result Theorem
3.4
are
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
which enter the dynamics via the computation of the consensus points (
2.2
) and (
2.3
). In particular, we recall from the quantitative Laplace principle Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
that they both need to be large, in relation to the objective function growth rates around the solutions. For Problems A and B, using larger values of
\alpha,\beta
indeed increases the algorithm accuracy, while for Problem C, values larger than
10^{5}
lead to poor results, see Figure
4b
. We conjecture this may be due to the well-know trade off for CBO algorithm, between the parameter
\alpha
(and
\beta
in this case) and the accuracy in the mean-field approximation. Specifically, as
\alpha,\beta

increases, the particle system is expected to diverge form the mean-field model, with possible lose of the theoretical convergence guarantees
[
22

25
,
29
]
.
