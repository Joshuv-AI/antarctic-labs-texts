Stationary fluctuations for the facilitated exclusion process
Clément Erignoux, Linjie Zhao

Abstract
. We derive the stationary fluctuations for the Facilitated Exclusion Process (FEP) in one dimension in the symmetric, weakly asymmetric and asymmetric cases. Our proof relies on the mapping between the FEP and the zero-range process, and extends the strategy in [ 10 ] , where hydrodynamic limits were derived for the FEP, to its stationary fluctuations. Our results thus exploit works on the zero-range process’s fluctuations [ 17 , 19 ] , but we also provide a direct proof in the symmetric case, for which we derive a sharp estimate on the equivalence of ensembles for the FEP’s stationary states.



1.
Introduction

The facilitated exclusion process (FEP) was first introduced in
[
24
]
in the physics community, indicating the existence of a new universality class of nonequilibrium phase transitions in the presence of a conserving field. In the FEP, particles can jump if and only if their target neighboring site is empty (exclusion rule) and another neighboring site is occupied (the facilitated rule). Particles with an occupied neighboring site are referred to as
active
. As a result of the facilitated rule, the FEP is ultimately absorbed in some frozen configuration (all particles are isolated) if the particle density is below a critical value
\rho_{c}
depending on the dimension, and can stay active for unbounded times otherwise. Over the past years, much progress has been made in dimension one, where the critical value is
\rho_{c}:=1/2
. In a series of papers
[
5
,
6
,
10
]
, the authors and their collaborators have shown that the macroscopic behaviors of the FEP are described by parabolic, resp. hyperbolic, Stefan problems when the process is symmetric, resp. asymmetric. The above results correspond to law of large numbers for the empirical density of the process and are called hydrodynamic limits in the literature
[
22
]
.

A natural question that arises once the hydrodynamic limit of the FEP is established is to consider fluctuations for the process, which play the role of the central limit theorem for interacting particle systems. Very recently, Barraquand
et al.
[
3
]
investigated this issue when the process is weakly asymmetric and starts from step-like initial distributions. The specific form of the initial distribution allows them to map the process into the simple exclusion process on the half-line with particle creation (and no annihilation) at the origin. Different from their setting, we let the process start from its stationary measure and prove central limit theorems for the density fluctuation fields in the following three cases: symmetric, weakly asymmetric and asymmetric. Roughly speaking, we show in this article that

(i)

in the symmetric case, the density fluctuation fields converge to the solution of a stochastic heat equation;

(ii)

in the weakly asymmetric case with weak asymmetry of order
N^{-1}
, where
N
is the scaling parameter, the density fluctuation fields converge to the solution of a stochastic Burgers-like equation;

(iii)

in the asymmetric case, the density fluctuation fields translates along characteristics of the corresponding hydrodynamic equation up to time
N^{\gamma}
for
\gamma<4/3
.

In the proof of the symmetric case, we obtain a sharp estimate on the equivalence of ensembles (see Proposition
5.5
) for the FEP, improving the bound in
[
5
]
, which is of independent interest. The latter is essential to derive the so-called Boltzmann-Gibbs principle (see Proposition
5.4
), which plays an important role when considering fluctuations from hydrodynamic limits.

The mathematical novelty involved in proving the above results and the techniques in this article are the following:

—

In
[
10
]
, the authors together with M. Simon proved hydrodynamic limits for the FEP and FZRP, both in the symmetric and asymmetric cases by using a classical mapping between the FEP and the facilitated zero-range process (FZRP). In the present article, we extend the mapping technique to investigate the fluctuations of FEP. We remark that when the FEP starts from its stationary measure, the FEP can be mapped to the classical rate one zero-range process (ZRP), which is not degenerate. Despite this, we still need to give sharp and uniform estimates on the positions of empty sites in order to express the density fluctuation field of the FEP as that of the ZRP, see Section
4
for details. We also need new non-trivial identities (see. (
3.9
)) between the two process’s invariant states. Once this is done, the derivation of macroscopic fluctuations for the FEP follow from previous results on the ZRP
[
17
,
19
]
. Moreover, we believe the techniques used in this article should also work for non-stationary fluctuations of FEP, but we leave this question as future work.

—

Over the last ten years, much efforts have been made to understand the weak Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) universality conjecture, which states that for weakly asymmetric systems with weak asymmetry of order
N^{-1}
and with only one conservation law, the density fluctuation fields converge to the solution of the stochastic Burgers equation. Since the seminal work of
[
18
]
, this has been proven to be true for a large class of models
[
19
,
9
]
. The second contribution of the article is to provide another example of a degenerate system that validates the weak KPZ universality conjecture.

1.1.
Related references.

The FEP has been widely explored in recent years after being introduced in
[
24
]
. In the physics literature, its critical behavior, namely its critical density and critical exponents have been investigated in different dimensions in
[
4
,
8
,
23
]
. In
[
13
]
the authors found the phenomenon of jump continuity at the leading edge of rarefaction waves, which is quite different from asymmetric simple exclusion. In the mathematics literature, the stationary states of the facilitated exclusion, either in the continuous or discrete time setting, have been studied in
[

,
14
,
15
,
16
]
. Limit theorems have also been proved in
[
2
]
for the position of the rightmost particle starting from step initial condition. We underline that the results mentioned above are concentrated on dimension one, and few rigorous results are known in higher dimensions.

The mapping between exclusion processes and the zero-range processes is well known, and has been put to use in varied contexts. It is used for instance in
[
21
]
in order to prove a central limit theorem for a tagged particle in the asymmetric simple exclusion process; in
[
12
]
, the weakly asymmetric zero-range process with a

[

, the authors used the mapping when considering non-equilibrium fluctuations for the rate one zero-range process; more precisely, they proved a non-equilibrium version of the Boltzmann-Gibbs principle of the zero-range process, from which the non-equilibrium fluctuations follow directly. Compared with
[
11
]
we use the mapping in the opposite direction and map the two processes at the level of density fluctuation fields.

2.
Notation and results

2.1.
Facilitated exclusion process

Let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}N\in\mathbb{N}}
be the scaling parameter for our process. The
facilitated exclusion process on
\mathbb{Z}
is a Markov process on the set of configurations
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Sigma:=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}}
. A
configuration

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta\in\Sigma}
is sequences of
0
’s and
1
’s indexed by
\mathbb{Z}
, namely
\eta_{x}=1
if and only if site
x\in\mathbb{Z}
is occupied by a particle.
The infinitesimal generator ruling the evolution in time of this Markov process is given by
\mathscr{L}_{N}
, which acts on local functions
f:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
as

(2.1)

\mathscr{L}_{N}f(\eta):=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\big(p_{N}c_{x,x+1}(\eta)+q_{N}c_{x+1,x}(\eta)\big)\big(f(\eta^{x,x+1})-f(\eta)\big),

where

(2.2)

p_{N}=\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}N^{2}+N^{\gamma}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad q_{N}=\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}N^{2},

where
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}\in\{0,1\}
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
, and
\eta^{x,x^{\prime}}
denotes the configuration obtained from
\eta
by swapping the values at sites
x
and
x^{\prime}
,

\eta_{z}^{x,x^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}\eta_{x^{\prime}}&\mbox{ if }z=x,\\
\eta_{x}&\mbox{ if }z=x^{\prime},\\
\eta_{z}&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

The jump rates
c_{x,x^{\prime}}(\eta)
in (
2.1
) encode two dynamical constraints:

(i)

the
exclusion rule
, which imposes no more than one particle at each site,

(ii)

the
facilitated rule
, a kinetic constraint which asks for a neighboring occupied site in order for a particle to jump to the other neighboring empty site.

More precisely, we define

(2.3)

c_{x,x+1}(\eta)=\eta_{x-1}\eta_{x}(1-\eta_{x+1}),\quad c_{x+1,x}(\eta)=(1-\eta_{x})\eta_{x+1}\eta_{x+2}.

In this article, we consider the following two cases:

—

the
weakly asymmetric
case where
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}=1
and
\gamma\leqslant 3/2
;

—

the
asymmetric case
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}=0
and
\gamma<4/3
.

Note that letting
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}=1
and
\gamma=-\infty
, we obtain the
symmetric
case, which is therefore considered as a special case of the weakly asymmetric one.

Let us now recall some results from previous work
[
5
,
6
]
: because of the kinetic constraint (ii), the facilitated exclusion process displays a
phase transition
at the critical density
\rho_{c}=\frac{1}{2}
. Indeed, pairs of neighboring empty sites cannot be created by the
dynamics, because to do so would require an isolated particle jumping out. Therefore, if at initial time the density of particles
\rho
is bigger than
\rho_{c}
(at least half of the sites are occupied), then particles will perform random jumps
in the microscopic system until there are no longer two neighboring empty sites. Similarly, if
initially
\rho<\rho_{c}
(at least half of the sites are empty), particles will perform random jumps until all particles can no longer move because they are surrounded by empty sites. The particle configurations can therefore be divided into three categories:

—

the
ergodic
configurations, where all empty sites are isolated, namely:

\eta
is
ergodic
if, for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\eta_{x}+\eta_{x+1}\geqslant 1
;
We denote, for any connected set
B\subset\mathbb{Z}
, by

(2.4)

\mathcal{E}_{B}:=\left\{\eta\in\{0,1\}^{B},\;\eta_{x}+\eta_{x+1}\geqslant 1\;\forall(x,x+1)\in B\right\}

the set of ergodic configurations on
B
.

—

the
frozen
configurations, where all particles are isolated, namely:

\eta
is
frozen
if, for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\eta_{x}+\eta_{x+1}\leqslant 1
; Similarly, for a box
B\subset\mathbb{Z}
, we denote by

\mathcal{F}_{B}:=\left\{\eta\in\{0,1\}^{B},\;\eta_{x}+\eta_{x+1}\leqslant 1\;\forall(x,x+1)\in B\right\}

the set of ergodic configurations on
B
.

—

the
transient
configurations, which are the remaining ones, those which are neither ergodic, nor frozen. They are called transient in
[
5
,
6
]
because starting from a transient configuration, restricting the dynamics to a finite box
B
, the microscopic process will locally evolve towards either the ergodic or frozen components on
B
in an a.s. finite number of jumps.

Note in particular that the alternated configurations, where each particle is surrounded by empty sites and vice-versa, are critical, since their density is exactly
\frac{1}{2}
, and it is convenient for them to be considered both frozen
and
ergodic: they are indeed frozen (no particle is allowed jump in them), and because they have probability non-zero under the grand canonical distribution of the process, whose support we refer to as the
ergodic component
, it is natural to see them as ergodic as well.

As a consequence, the infinite volume invariant measures of the facilitated process are not independent products of homogeneous Bernoulli
measures (as in the standard Simple Exclusion Process for instance). Instead, they are given for
\rho>\frac{1}{2}
,
\ell\geqslant 1
, by the measures

(2.5)

\pi_{\rho}\Big(\eta_{|\Lambda_{\ell}}={\sigma}\Big)={\bf{1}}_{\{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{\ell}\}}(1-\rho)\left(\frac{1-\rho}{\rho}\right)^{\ell-1-p}\left(\frac{2\rho-1}{\rho}\right)^{2p-\ell+1-{\sigma(1)-\sigma(\ell)}},

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Lambda_{\ell}:=\{1,\dots,\ell\}}
for
\ell\geqslant 1
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{E}_{\ell}:=\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda_{\ell}}}
denotes the set of configurations which are ergodic on
\Lambda_{\ell}
, and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p=p(\ell,\sigma):=\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{\ell}}\sigma_{x}}
is the number of particles in
\Lambda_{\ell}
. On can think of these measures as product Bernoulli distributions conditioned to being in the ergodic component. Furthermore, they are translation invariant, and one can easily check that under
\pi_{\rho}
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\rho}}(\eta_{0})=\rho
. The formula above, however, is not always very convenient to compute local function’s expectations. For this reason, we describe below in (
3.9
) an alternative construction for
\pi_{\rho}.

Consider as initial distribution the infinite volume stationary measure
\pi_{\rho}
, and the process
\eta(t)
started from
\pi_{\rho}
and with generator
\mathscr{L}_{N}
on
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{Z}}
. We denote by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{P}_{\rho}^{N}}
its distribution, and by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{N}}
the corresponding expectation.

2.2.
Stationary fluctuations field

Denote by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{S}:=\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})}
the Schwartz space of functions with fast decaying derivatives on
\mathbb{R}
, and by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}
its dual space, namely the space of tempered distributions. Further denote by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime})}
the set of càdlàg trajectories on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. We now define the density fluctuation field trajectory
({\mathcal{Y}}^{N}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}\in D([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
of the process
\eta
, acting on smooth compactly supported functions
G\in\mathcal{S}
as

(2.6)

{\mathcal{Y}}^{N}_{t}(G)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\overline{\eta}_{x}(t)G\big(\tfrac{x}{N}-tvN^{\gamma-1}\big),

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{\eta}_{x}(t)=\eta_{x}(t)-\rho}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}v:=v(\rho)}
is the average macroscopic speed of asymmetric particles in the stationary state, namely

(2.7)

v(\rho):=\frac{d}{d\rho}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\rho}}(\eta_{x-1}\eta_{x}(1-\eta_{x+1}))=\frac{d}{d\rho}\frac{(1-\rho)(2\rho-1)}{\rho}=\frac{1-2\rho^{2}}{\rho^{2}}.

Note that because of the asymmetry, mass moves rightwards at a velocity of order
O(N^{\gamma-1})
, which is the reason for shifting in time the test function by
vtN^{\gamma-1}
.
In what follows, we will use the notation

(2.8)

v_{N}=vN^{\gamma-1},

for the macroscopic velocity of particles in the system in its stationary state.

We denote by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{Q}_{\rho}^{N}:=\mathbb{P}_{\rho}^{N}\circ({\mathcal{Y}}^{N})^{-1}}
the pushforward of
\mathbb{P}_{\rho}^{N}
through the mapping

{\mathcal{Y}}^{N}:\{\eta(t),\;t\geqslant 0\}\mapsto\{{\mathcal{Y}}^{N}_{t},\;t\geqslant 0\},

that is,
\mathbb{Q}_{\rho}^{N}
is the distribution of the stationary fluctuation field
{\mathcal{Y}}^{N}_{t}
when
\eta
is stationary state
\mathbb{P}_{\rho}^{N}
. In the asymmetric and weakly asymmetric cases mentioned above, we are now ready to define the limiting processes and introduce our main results.

2.3.
Diffusion coefficient and compressibility

We start by introducing the relevant macroscopic quantities to appear in the macroscopic fluctuation field of the FEP. The hydrodynamic behavior of the symmetric FEP (see
[
6
]
) is characterized by the Stefan problem

\partial_{t}\rho=\partial_{u}^{2}\big\{a(\rho){\bf 1}_{\{\rho\geqslant 1/2\}}\big\}=\partial_{u}\big\{D(\rho){\bf 1}_{\{\rho\geqslant 1/2\}}\partial_{u}\rho\big\},

where
a(\rho)
average density of active particles under
\pi_{\rho}
and
D(\rho)
the diffusion coefficient,

(2.9)

a(\rho):=\pi_{\rho}(\eta_{x}=1\mid\eta_{x-1}=1)=\frac{2\rho-1}{\rho}\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad D(\rho)=a^{\prime}(\rho)=\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}.

We further define the conductivity

(2.10)

\sigma(\rho):=\pi_{\rho}(c_{0,1})=\frac{(2\rho-1)(1-\rho)}{\rho}.

and the compressibility as

(2.11)

\chi(\rho):=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}Cov_{\pi_{\rho}}(\eta_{0},\eta_{x})=\rho(1-\rho)(2\rho-1).

Identities (
2.9
) and (
2.10
) are direct applications of the explicit formula (
2.5
). We prove in Appendix
A.2
this explicit formula (
2.11
) for the compressibility. Note that, as expected, the Einstein (fluctuation-dissipation) relation
\sigma=D\chi
holds.

2.4.
Stationary fluctuations

In order to introduce our main results, we start by choosing
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}=1
and
\gamma\leqslant 3/2
in (
2.2
) to consider the weakly asymmetric case. We denote by
K_{t}(u,v)
the one-dimensional heat kernel

(2.12)

K_{t}(u,v):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}e^{\frac{(u-v)^{2}}{4t}},

we now introduce the notion of solution to the stochastic heat equation for the case
\gamma<3/2
.

Definition 2.1

(Solution to the stochastic heat equation)
.

We say that
Y:=(Y_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
taking values a.s. in
C([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
is a stationary solution to the stochastic heat equation

(2.13)

\partial_{t}Y_{t}=D(\rho)\partial_{u}^{2}Y_{t}+\sqrt{2\sigma(\rho)}\partial_{u}\dot{\mathcal{W}}_{t}

if it is a stationary generalized Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
with mean
0
and covariance given by

\mathbb{E}(Y_{t}(G)Y_{s}(H))=\chi(\rho)\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}G(u)K_{(t-s)D(\rho)}(u,v)H(v)dudv=\chi(\rho)\langle T_{t-s}G,H\rangle,

for
G,H\in\mathcal{S}
and
s\leqslant t
, where
T_{t}
is the semi-group associated with the self-adjoint operator
D(\rho)\partial_{u}^{2}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the
L^{2}
inner product on
\mathbb{R}
.

We will use the following result, which states that solutions to the stochastic heat equation are solution to a martingale problem.

Proposition 2.1

(Characterization of solutions to (
2.13
))
.

Fix a stochastic process
Y
taking values a.s. in
C([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
, and assume that for any
G\in\mathcal{S}
,

M_{t}(G):=Y_{t}(G)-Y_{0}(G)-D(\rho)\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}(\partial_{u}^{2}G)ds

and

N_{t}(G):=\big[M_{t}(G)\big]^{2}-2t\sigma(\rho)\|\partial_{u}G\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

are both integrable martingales w.r.t.
Y
’s natural filtration, and that for any
t\geqslant 0
,

(2.14)

\mathbb{E}(Y_{t}(G)Y_{t}(H))=\chi(\rho)\langle G,H\rangle.

Then,
Y
is a stationary solution to the stochastic heat equation in the sense of Definition
2.1
.

In the case
\gamma=3/2
, a non-linear contribution coming from the asymmetric jumps appears in the fluctuation regime. To define the proper limiting equation, we need to introduce further definitions. First, define

\iota(u)=(1/2)\mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}(u),

and let
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
be a mollifier on
\mathbb{R}
, namely a non-negative compactly supported function such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(u)du=1
. We then define

\varphi_{\varepsilon}(u)=\varepsilon^{-1}\varphi(u/\varepsilon)\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\iota_{\varepsilon}(u)=\varepsilon^{-1}\iota(u/\varepsilon).

We now approximate the dirac measure in
u
by the smooth convolution
\delta_{u,\varepsilon}=\varphi_{\varepsilon^{3}}*\iota_{\varepsilon}(\cdot-u)\in\mathcal{S}
, straightforward computations show that

\|\delta_{0,\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\varepsilon^{-1/2}\|\delta_{0,\varepsilon}-\iota_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=0.

We now introduce a necessary
L^{2}
condition in the case where
\gamma=3/2
.

Definition 2.2

(
L^{2}
Energy condition)
.

Fix a process
Y
taking values a.s. in
C([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
, and for
0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant T
, we define
A_{s,t}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
by

A_{s,t}^{\varepsilon}(G)=\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Y_{s^{\prime}}(\delta_{u,\varepsilon})^{2}\partial_{u}G(u)du\,ds^{\prime}.

We say that
Y

satisfies the
L^{2}
energy condition
if for any
G\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
,
A_{s,t}^{\varepsilon}(G)
is Cauchy in
L^{2}
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, and the limit in
L^{2}
does not depend on the mollifier
\varphi
.
We then denote by
A_{s,t}=A_{s,t}[Y]
the limit of
A_{s,t}^{\varepsilon}
.

Note that we do not know a priori whether
A_{0,t}
is time-differentiable, therefore we do not give an analogous definition to
2.1
for solutions to the stochastic Burgers equation. Instead, we directly use the characterization of solutions in terms of martingales, analogous to Proposition
2.1
.

Definition 2.3

(Solution to the stochastic Burgers equation)
.

We say that a stochastic process
Y
taking values a.s. in
C([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
is a stationary solution to the stochastic Burgers equation

(2.15)

\partial_{t}Y_{t}=D(\rho)\partial_{u}^{2}Y_{t}+\frac{1}{2}D^{\prime}(\rho)\partial_{u}Y_{t}^{2}+\sqrt{2\sigma(\rho)}\partial_{u}\dot{\mathcal{W}}_{t}.

if

i)

For any
t>0
, and
G,H\in\mathcal{S}
,

(2.16)

\mathbb{E}(Y_{t}(G)Y_{t}(H))=\chi(\rho)\langle G,H\rangle.

ii)

Y
satisfies the
L^{2}
energy condition (cf. Definition
2.2
), so that for any
t\geqslant 0
, the tempered distribution
A_{0,t}[Y]\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is well-defined.

iii)

For any
G\in\mathcal{S}
,

M_{t}(G):=Y_{t}(G)-Y_{0}(G)-D(\rho)\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}(\partial_{u}^{2}G)ds+\frac{1}{2}D^{\prime}(\rho)A_{0,t}(G)

N_{t}(G):=\big[M_{t}(G)\big]^{2}-2t\sigma(\rho)\|\partial_{u}G\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

are both integrable martingales w.r.t.
Y
’s natural filtration.

We are now in a position to state our main result, which derives the fluctuation field in the weakly asymmetric case.

Theorem 2.2

(Weakly asymmetric case)
.

For
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}=1
, the FEP’s fluctuation field
\{{\mathcal{Y}}^{N}_{t},\;0\leqslant t\leqslant T\}
introduced in (
2.6
) converges in the uniform topology on
D\big([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\big)
, as
N\to\infty
to a process
\{Y_{t},\;0\leqslant t\leqslant T\}
, which is

i)

solution to the stochastic heat equation in the sense of Proposition
2.1
for
\gamma<3/2
,

ii)

solution to the stochastic Burgers equation in the sense of Definition
2.3
for
\gamma=3/2
.

This convergence is to be understood as a weak uniform convergence of the distribution of
\mathcal{Y}_{N}
to that of
Y
.

To give a more explicit description of this convergence, by Skorokhod’s representation theorem, in both cases one can build the limiting process
\{Y_{t},\;0\leqslant t\leqslant T\}
on the same probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},{\mathbb{P}})
as the facilitated exclusion process
\{\eta_{t},0\leqslant t\leqslant T\}
, and have that for any
G\in\mathcal{S}

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}|{\mathcal{Y}}^{N}_{t}(G)-Y_{t}(G)|=0\qquad{\mathbb{P}}\mbox{-a.s.}.

Note that this result does not seem to explicitly depend on the value of
\gamma
, except through the condition
\gamma=3/2
or
\gamma<3/2
, which may seem strange since the particle’s motion does. This is natural, however, because we are looking at the scaling limit of the moving field
{\mathcal{Y}}^{N}_{t}
, which translates at a
\gamma
-dependent speed.

We now consider the (totally) asymmetric FEP, and let
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}=0
and
\gamma<4/3
in (
2.2
), in which case we have the following result.

Theorem 2.3

(Totally asymmetric case)
.

For
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}=0
and
\gamma<4/3
, the FEP’s fluctuation field
\{{\mathcal{Y}}^{N}_{t},\;0\leqslant t\leqslant T\}
introduced in (
2.6
) converges in the weak topology on
D\big([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\big)
, as
N\to\infty
to a stationary Gaussian process
Y
with covariance

\mathbb{E}(Y_{t}(G)Y_{s}(H))=\chi(\rho)\langle G,H\rangle.

In other words, the limiting process
Y_{t}=\sqrt{2\sigma(u)}\partial_{u}\mathcal{W}_{t}
is a time-integrated space-time white noise, or, equivalently, the space derivative of a
(2,1)
-Brownian sheet.

Once again, the limiting process does not depend on the asymmetry exponent
\gamma
, because the field
{\mathcal{Y}}^{N}_{t}
we are looking at is taken in a moving frame that does depend on
\gamma
.

The proof of Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
is the purpose of Section
4
. In order to prove these two results, we will rely on a classical mapping to an attractive zero-range process, already exploited to derive the FEP’s hydrodynamic limit in both symmetric and weakly asymmetric cases, and described in details in Section
3
. The derivation of the stationary fluctuations field’s scaling limit then follows from a regularity estimate on the mapping in the stationary state (cf. Proposition
4.1
below), together with the derivation of stationary fluctuation already obtained in the weakly asymmetric case in
[
19
]
and in the totally asymmetric case in
[
17
]
.

In Section
5
, we then offer a direct, alternative proof of Theorem
2.2
in the symmetric case
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}=1
,
\gamma=-\infty
. The latter relies on a sharp bound (of order
\ell^{-1}\log^{2}\ell
) for the equivalence of ensembles (cf. Proposition
5.5
below) which is interesting in its own right since it significantly improves on the one obtained so far in
[
5
, Proposition 6.9, p. 697]
(of order
\ell^{-1/4}
).

3.
Mapping to the constant rate zero-range process

We start by describing a classical mapping between the facilitated exclusion process and a zero-range process. In the ergodic component
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{Z}}
(cf. (
2.4
)), we have no ergodicity issues, and this mapping can be built straightforwardly to the unconstrained, constant-rate zero-range process.

3.1.
Static mapping

Recall the definition (
2.4
) of the ergodic component
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{Z}}
, and fix an ergodic configuration
\eta\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{Z}}
. Let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X_{0}=X_{0}(\eta)\leqslant 0}
be the position, of the first empty site in
\eta
to the left of (or at) the origin, meaning that
\eta_{X_{0}}=0
and
\eta_{x}=1
for
X_{0}<x\leqslant 0
. For any
y>0
(resp.
y<0
), we define
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X_{y}}
as the position of the
y
-th empty site to the right (resp. to the left) of
X_{0}
. We then define

(3.1)

\omega_{y}:=X_{y+1}-X_{y}-2.

Recall
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}}
, and note that
\omega_{y}\in\mathbb{N}
, so that
\omega\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{Z}}
defines a zero-range configuration, because
\eta
was assumed to be ergodic. Of course, the mapping
\eta\mapsto\omega
is not
1
-to-
1
, since knowledge of the position of the initial empty site
X_{0}
is necessary to revert the construction.

Figure 3.1.
Representation of the mapping
\Pi
between a FEP ergodic configurations
\eta
and zero-range configuration
\omega

Instead, denote by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Pi:\eta\mapsto(\omega,X_{0})}
, the mapping represented in Figure
3.1
which, with an ergodic configuration
\eta
, associates the zero-range configuration
\omega\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{Z}}
built through (
3.1
) and the position of the first empty site left of the origin. It is straightforward to show that the mapping
\Pi
is a bijection between the FEP’s ergodic configuration
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{Z}}
, and the set

(3.2)

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbb{Z}}:=\{(\omega,X_{0})\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}\;|\;-\omega_{0}-1\leqslant X_{0}\leqslant 0\}.

The reverse mapping
\Pi^{-1}
is easily defined; choose a zero-range configuration
\omega\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{Z}}
and an integer
-\omega_{0}-1\leqslant X_{0}\leqslant 0
. The associated ergodic configuration
\eta
is then built by placing an empty site in
\eta
at site
X_{0}
, and then choosing the consecutive positions
X_{y}
of empty sites in
\eta
according to
\omega
and (
3.1
). We then have as wanted
\eta=\Pi^{-1}(\omega,X_{0})
.

3.2.
Dynamic mapping

We now consider a dynamical version of the mapping above : to do so, consider a trajectory
\{\eta(t),\;t\geqslant 0\}
of the FEP starting from an ergodic configuration
\eta(0)\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{Z}}
. Let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X_{0}(0)}
be the position of the first empty site in
\eta(0)
to the left of (or at) the origin at the initial time. Then, as before we define for any
y>0
(resp.
y<0
),
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}X_{y}(0)}
is the position of the
y
-th empty site to the right (resp. to the left) of
X_{0}(0)
. We keep track of each trajectory
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\{X_{y}(t),\;t\geqslant 0\}}
of the
y
-th empty site for
y\in\mathbb{Z}
in
\{\eta(t),\;t\geqslant 0\}
.
Since the jumps are nearest neighbor, the orders of the empty sites are preserved along the evolution of the process,
i.e.
for any
t\geqslant 0
,

\ldots<X_{-1}(t)<X_{0}(t)<X_{1}(t)<\ldots.

We then define as in the static case

(3.3)

\omega_{y}(t):=X_{y+1}(t)-X_{y}(t)-2\quad\mbox{ for }y\in\mathbb{Z}.

In order not to confuse with the static mapping, we denote
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Pi^{\star}[\eta]}
this dynamic mapping between trajectories, meaning that
\Pi^{\star}[\eta](t)=\omega(t)
. Note that we do not have in general that
\Pi^{\star}[\eta](t)=\Pi(\eta(t))
, unless the tagged empty site that was at time
0
the first left of the origin is still the first left of the origin at time
t
.

It is straightforward to show that if
\eta
is a FEP,
\{\omega(t),\;t\geqslant 0\}=\Pi^{\star}[\eta]
evolves as the classical zero-range process with generator
\mathscr{L}^{\mbox{\tiny{zr}}}_{N}
, which acts on local functions
f:\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{Z}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

(3.4)

\mathscr{L}^{\mbox{\tiny{zr}}}_{N}f(\omega)=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{y}\geqslant 1\}}\big\{p_{N}\big(f(\omega^{y,y+1})-f(\omega)\big)+q_{N}\big(f(\omega^{y,y-1})-f(\omega)\big)\big\}.

Since we use different letters for exclusion (
\eta
) and zero-range (
\omega
) configurations, without confusion, we also denote
\omega^{y,y\pm 1}
the zero-range configuration obtained from
\omega
after a particle jumps from
y
to
y\pm 1
,

\omega_{y^{\prime}}^{y,y\pm 1}=\begin{cases}\omega_{y}-1&\mbox{ if }y^{\prime}=y,\\
\omega_{y}+1&\mbox{ if }y^{\prime}=y\pm 1,\\
\omega_{y^{\prime}}&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Given a distribution
\nu
on
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{Z}}
, we denote by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\bf P}^{N}_{\nu}}
the distribution of the zero-range process started from the initial state
\omega(0)\sim\nu
and driven by the generator
\mathscr{L}^{\mbox{\tiny{zr}}}_{N}
and by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\bf E}^{N}_{\nu}}
the corresponding expectation.

3.3.
Stationary states

We now consider the stationary states for the zero-range process. We define the (grand-canonical) equilibrium distributions for the generator
\mathscr{L}^{\mbox{\tiny{zr}}}_{N}
as the product distributions with marginals in
\omega_{y}
given by geometric distributions with parameter the active density
a(\rho)
(cf. (
2.9
)), namely

(3.5)

\mu_{\rho}(\omega_{y}=k)=a(\rho)^{k}(1-a(\rho)),\;k\geqslant 0.

Define

(3.6)

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha(\rho):=\frac{a(\rho)}{1-a(\rho)}}=\frac{2\rho-1}{1-\rho},

one easily obtains the identity
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\rho}}(\omega_{y})=\alpha(\rho),
and that
\mu_{\rho}
is stationary for
\mathscr{L}^{\mbox{\tiny{zr}}}_{N}
. Further denote by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}}
the distribution on
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbb{Z}}
(defined in (
3.2
)) such that under
\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}
,

—

the zero-range configuration
\omega_{\mid\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}
is distributed as
\mu_{\rho}
, defined by (
3.5
), everywhere except at the origin.

—

At the origin,
\omega_{0}
is distributed, independently from the rest of the configuration, as

(3.7)

\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}(\omega_{0}=k)=(k+2)a(\rho)^{k}\rho(1-a(\rho))^{2}

—

the distribution of
X_{0}
conditionally to
\omega
is uniform in
\{-\omega_{0}-1,\dots,0\}
.

In other words, for any
(\omega,x)\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbb{Z}}
, we have

(3.8)

\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}(\omega,x)=\rho(1-a(\rho))\mu_{\rho}(\omega)

We claim that the stationary state
\pi_{\rho}
for the FEP can be built through the mapping, by

(3.9)

\pi_{\rho}=\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}\circ\Pi\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}=\pi_{\rho}\circ\Pi^{-1}.

Note that through this mapping identity, sites in the zero-range process are associated with
clusters of particles in the FEP,
namely consecutive particles preceded by an empty site.
Given the apparent breaking of translation invariance in the definition of the central cluster
(\omega_{0})
in
\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}
, it is not a priori clear that
\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}\circ\Pi
would yield a translation invariant distribution. The core of the argument is that the central particle cluster, the one of the origin, needs the shifted distribution (
3.7
) to account for the multiple ways it can be placed at the origin, since
X_{0}\sim Unif(\{-\omega_{0}-1,\dots,0\})
. The proof of identity (
3.9
) is given in Appendix
A.1
, where we given an alternative construction for
\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}:=\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}\circ\Pi
, and prove that the latter is translation invariant, and satisfies (
2.5
).

It might also appear counter-intuitive that the stationary state
\pi_{\rho}
for the FEP dynamics is mapped to a distribution
\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}
whose marginal in
\omega
is
not stationary
for the mapped zero-range dynamics (due to the loss of translation invariance at the origin in
\omega
). This is to be expected however, because the mapped dynamics must be considered instead on the pair
(\omega,X_{0})
, and jumps at the origin have effects on both components, so that the term coming from the displacement of the tagged empty site offsets the loss of stationarity of the zero-range due to the lack of stationarity at the origin. This statement is explored in more detail in Appendix
A.3
.

The core of the arguments amounts to the following : to build
\pi_{\rho}
, one has to put i.i.d. particle clusters (meaning sets of consecutive particles followed by an empty site) with geometric number of particles distributed as
1+\mu_{\rho}(\cdot)
, (i.e.
1+\omega
) and then randomly shift the configuration by choosing the origin (i.e.
X_{0}
). If the origin is empty, then one can simply build i.i.d. clusters and everything works as expected. If the origin is occupied, however, one needs to choose “at random” an occupied site, and choosing an occupied site “at random” biases towards larger particle clusters, because the latter contain by definition more particles. In other words, building geometric clusters of particles, and then choosing one particle uniformly among those, the distribution of the cluster of the chosen particle is no longer going to be geometrically distributed, and its distribution is instead going to be the tilted distribution (
3.7
).

In what follows, we will use the following result, which can be straightforwardly proved using the zero-range process’s attractiveness.

Proposition 3.1

(Discrepancies due to the distortion at the origin)
.

There exists a coupling
\mathbb{Q}
between two trajectories
\omega
,
\xi
of the rate
1
zero-range process such that

\omega(0)\sim\mu_{\rho}
,
\xi(0)\sim\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}
, and such that

\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}|\xi_{y}(t)-\omega_{y}(t)|=|\xi_{0}(0)-\omega_{0}(0)|=\xi_{0}(0)-\omega_{0}(0)\qquad\mathbb{Q}-\mbox{a.s.}

In other words, there exists a coupling between two zero-range processes such that the total number of discrepancies at any time between the two is equal to the number of discrepancies initially at the origin.

We do not detail the proof of this result, it is a direct consequence of our choice of initial distributions, and the classical so-called
basic coupling
, which we now briefly describe. Because the initial distributions are identical everywhere except at the origin, we can choose
\omega_{y}(0)=\xi_{y}(0)
for any
y\neq 0
. At the origin, we biased the distribution
\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}
towards large clusters, we can therefore couple
\omega
and
\xi
in such a way that
\omega_{0}(0)\leqslant\xi_{0}(0)
. Because the constant rate zero-range process is attractive, we then endow each site with i.i.d. Poisson clock, and each time the clock rings on a site in which both
\omega
and
\xi
have particles, a particle performs the same jump in the two configurations. If instead, only
\xi
has a particle, we make it jump normally in
\xi
and nothing happens in
\omega
. Under this basic coupling, it is straightforward to show that both
\omega
and
\xi
are constant rate zero-range processes, and that Proposition
3.1
holds.

Because it yields a tight control of discrepancies, Proposition
3.1
will allow us to assume that the mapped zero-range process is initially in a stationary state as well rather than
\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}
, and therefore to prove the mapping estimates we need in the stationary state, as well as use the stationary fluctuations results obtained in
[
17
,
19
]
.

3.4.
Position of the tagged empty site in the dynamic mapping

Given a trajectory
\omega:=\{\omega(t),\;t\geqslant 0\}
of the zero-range process, we denote by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}J^{\rm ZR}_{x,x+1}(t)\in\mathbb{Z}}
the particle current going through
(x,x+1)
in
[0,t]
, namely the total particle number going through the edge
(x,x+1)
before time
t
. It can be formally written as

(3.10)

J^{\rm ZR}_{x,x+1}(t)=\sum_{y\geqslant x+1}\big\{\omega_{y}(t)-\omega_{y}(0)\big\}.

It is straightforward to show that in the stationary state, its expectation is given by

(3.11)

{\bf E}_{\mu_{\rho}}^{N}\left(J^{\rm ZR}_{x,x+1}(t)\right)=tN^{\gamma}\mu_{\rho}(\omega_{0}\geqslant 1)=tN^{\gamma}\frac{2\rho-1}{\rho}.

As before, we denote by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{J^{\rm ZR}_{x,x+1}}(t):=J^{\rm ZR}_{x,x+1}(t)-tN^{\gamma}(2\rho-1)/\rho}
the corresponding centered variable.

Consider now a trajectory
\{\eta(t),\;t\geqslant 0\}
of the FEP, and
\{\omega(t),\;t\geqslant 0\}=\Pi^{\star}[\eta]
the mapped zero-range trajectory introduced in Section
3.2
. Then, the position
X_{0}(t)
of the tagged empty site at time
t
can then be expressed in terms of the mapped zero-range process
\omega
through the identity

(3.12)

X_{0}(t)=-J^{\rm ZR}_{-1,0}(t)+X_{0}(0).

We now give an estimate of the variance of stationary current going through the origin, that will be used repeatedly in the proof of Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
.

Lemma 3.2
.

There exists a constant
C
independent of
N
such that

(3.13)

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{N}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\overline{J^{\rm ZR}_{-1,0}}(t)^{2}\Big]\leqslant C(\log N)^{3}\Big[N^{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}N^{4/3}\Big]=O\big(N^{3/2}(\log N)^{3}\big).

Proof.

Because we need an estimate that is uniform in time, the proof of this estimate is rather technical.
We first get back to the zero-range stationary case. First note that according to identity (
3.9
),

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{N}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\overline{J^{\rm ZR}_{-1,0}}(t)^{2}\Big]={\bf E}^{N}_{\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\overline{J^{\rm ZR}_{-1,0}}(t)^{2}\Big],

where as before
{\bf E}^{N}_{\nu}
is the expectation w.r.t. the distribution of the zero-range process started from the initial state
\omega(0)\sim\nu
. The right-hand side above is the expectation of the current going through
(-1,0)
for a zero-range process started from the state
\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}
characterized in (
3.9
). According to Proposition
3.1
above, since the current discrepancy is less than the total particle discrepancy, we obtain that

\;\left|\;\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{N}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\overline{J^{\rm ZR}_{-1,0}}(t)^{2}\Big]-{\bf E}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\overline{J^{\rm ZR}_{-1,0}}(t)^{2}\Big]\;\right|\;\leqslant|{\bf E}^{N}_{\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}}(\omega_{0}(0))-{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}(\omega_{0}(0))|^{2}:=C(\rho)

is at most bounded by a constant, so that it is enough to prove (
3.13
) starting from the zero-range’s stationary distribution
\mu_{\rho}
.

We now consider the stationary zero-range process
\omega
initially distributed as
\mu_{\rho}
. For
\ell>0
, let

G_{\ell}(u)=\Big(1-\frac{u}{\ell}\Big)^{+}\mathbf{1}_{\{u\geqslant 0\}}.

Then,

(3.14)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{\ell}\big(\tfrac{x}{N}\big)\big[\overline{\omega}_{x}(t)-\overline{\omega}_{x}(0)\big]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{\ell}\big(\tfrac{x}{N}\big)\big[\overline{J^{\rm ZR}_{x-1,x}}(t)-\overline{J^{\rm ZR}_{x,x+1}}(t)\big]\\
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\overline{J^{\rm ZR}_{-1,0}}(t)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x=0}^{N\ell}\frac{1}{N\ell}\overline{J^{\rm ZR}_{x,x+1}}(t),

where
\overline{J^{\rm ZR}_{x,x+1}}(t)
was introduced after (
3.11
). Once again, we define

\overline{g}(k)={\bf 1}_{\{k\geqslant 1\}}-\frac{2\rho-1}{\rho}.

For
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,

M_{x,x+1}(t)=\overline{J^{\rm ZR}_{x,x+1}}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}N^{2}[\overline{g}(\omega_{x}(s))-\overline{g}(\omega_{x+1}(s)]+N^{\gamma}\overline{g}(\omega_{x}(s))ds

are independent
{\bf P}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}
-martingales with quadratic variation

(3.15)

\langle M_{x,x+1}\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}N^{2}+N^{\gamma})g(\omega_{x}(s))+\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}N^{2}g(\omega_{x+1}(s))ds=O(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}N^{2}+N^{\gamma}).

In particular, (
3.14
) rewrites

(3.16)

\overline{J^{\rm ZR}_{-1,0}}(t)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{\ell}\big(\tfrac{x}{N}\big)\big[\overline{\omega}_{x}(t)-\overline{\omega}_{x}(0)\big]\\
+\frac{1}{N\ell}\sum_{x=0}^{N\ell}M_{x,x+1}(t)+\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}N}{\ell}\int_{0}^{t}\big[\overline{g}(\omega_{0}(s))-\overline{g}(\omega_{N\ell+1}(s))\big]ds+\frac{N^{\gamma-1}}{\ell}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=0}^{N\ell}\overline{g}(\omega_{x}(s))ds.

We first bound the last line in (
3.16
). For the martingale term, by Doob’s inequality and (
3.15
),

(3.17)

{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Big(\frac{1}{N\ell}\sum_{x=0}^{N\ell}M_{x,x+1}(t)\Big)^{2}\Big]\leqslant 4{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\Big(\frac{1}{N\ell}\sum_{x=0}^{N\ell}M_{x,x+1}(T)\Big)^{2}\Big]\leqslant C\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}N+N^{\gamma-1}}{\ell}.

For the last two terms in (
3.16
), which are time integrals, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and since both
\overline{g}(\omega_{x}(s))
and
\overline{\omega}_{x}(s)
are bounded in
L^{2}({\bf P}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}})
,

(3.18)

{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Big(\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}N}{\ell}\int_{0}^{t}\big[\overline{g}(\omega_{0}(s))-\overline{g}(\omega_{N\ell+1}(s))\big]ds\Big)^{2}\Big]\leqslant CT^{2}\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}N^{2}}{\ell^{2}},

(3.19)

{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Big(\frac{N^{\gamma-1}}{\ell}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=0}^{N\ell}\overline{g}(\omega_{x}(s))ds\Big)^{2}\Big]\leqslant CT^{2}\frac{N^{2\gamma-1}}{\ell}.

Now, we bound the first term on the right side of (
3.16
). Clearly,

(3.20)

{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\Big(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{\ell}\big(\tfrac{x}{N}\big)\overline{\omega}_{x}(0)\Big)^{2}\Big]\leqslant CN\ell.

It remains to bound

(3.21)

{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Big(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{\ell}\big(\tfrac{x}{N}\big)\overline{\omega}_{x}(t)\Big)^{2}\Big].

The main issue to estimate this quantity lies in the time supremum inside the expectation. Without it, one would straightforwardly obtain a bound of order
O(N\ell)
since the zero-range process is stationary. Below, we will show that (
3.21
) is of order
O\big(N(\log N)^{3}\ell\big)
. To this end, we first divide the time interval
[0,T]
into small intervals of size
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{N}:=N^{-100}
. More precisely, let
t_{i}=iT\varepsilon
for
0\leqslant i\leqslant 1/\varepsilon
. Note that in order for the set of configurations
\{\omega(t_{i}),0\leqslant i\leqslant 1/\varepsilon\}
to be different than
\{\omega(t),0\leqslant t\leqslant T\}
, there must have been an interval
[t_{i},t_{i+1}[
in which at least two particle jumps occurred. In particular, we bound (
3.21
) by

(3.22)

{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant i\leqslant\varepsilon^{-1}}\Big(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{\ell}\big(\tfrac{x}{N}\big)\overline{\omega}_{x}(t_{i})\Big)^{2}\Big]+{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Big(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{\ell}\big(\tfrac{x}{N}\big)\overline{\omega}_{x}(t)\Big)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{A_{N}}\Big],

where
A_{N}
is the event

(3.23)

A_{N}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{1/\varepsilon-1}\Big\{\mbox{at least two particle jumps occurred in the box}\{-1,\dots,N\ell+1\}\\
\mbox{during the time interval }[t_{i},t_{i}+1[\Big\}.

Since for any fixed
i
, the number of jumps in the box
[-1,N\ell+1]
during the time interval
[t_{i},t_{i+1}]
is bounded by a Poisson random variable of parameter
T\varepsilon N^{2}(N\ell+1)=O(N^{-97}\ell)
, we have

{\bf P}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}(A_{N})\leqslant C\varepsilon^{-1}\big(N^{-97}\ell\big)^{2}=O\big(N^{-94}\ell^{2}\big).

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we bound the second term in (
3.22
) by

{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Big(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{\ell}\big(\tfrac{x}{N}\big)\overline{\omega}_{x}(t)\Big)^{4}\Big]^{1/2}{\bf P}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\big(A_{N}\big)^{1/2}\\
\leqslant\Big\{\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{\ell}\big(\tfrac{x}{N}\big)^{4}{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Big(\overline{\omega}_{x}(t)\Big)^{4}\Big]\Big\}^{1/2}{\bf P}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\big(A_{N}\big)^{1/2}.

A very rough bound shows that

(3.24)

{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\omega_{0}(t)^{4}\Big]\leqslant CN^{8}.

Indeed, let
N_{0,T}
be the number of particles that are initially outside of the box
[-2N^{2}T,2N^{2}T]
and visit the origin before time
T
. Then,

{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\omega_{0}(t)^{4}\Big]\leqslant C\Big({\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\Big(\sum_{|y|\leqslant 2N^{2}T}\omega_{y}(0)\Big)^{4}\Big]+{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[N_{0,T}^{4}\Big]\Big)\leqslant C\Big(N^{8}+{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[N_{0,T}^{4}\Big]\Big).

Dividing the sets
\mathbb{Z}\backslash[-2N^{2}T,2N^{2}T]
into

(3.25)

\bigcup_{k\geqslant 2}\big([(-k-1)N^{2}T,-kN^{2}T]\cup[kN^{2}T,(k+1)N^{2}T]\big),

and since a particle travels at speed at most
N^{2}
, standard large deviation arguments yield

{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[N_{0,T}^{4}\Big]\leqslant C\sum_{k\geqslant 1}N^{8}e^{-CkN^{2}}\leqslant CN^{8}e^{-CN^{2}},

which proves (
3.24
). Thus, although this bound is certainly not optimal, the second term in (
3.22
) by is finally bounded by
CN^{-40}\ell^{2}
.

We now turn to the first term in (
3.22
). Define the event

(3.26)

B_{N}=\Big\{\sup_{0\leqslant i\leqslant 1/\varepsilon}\Big|\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{\ell}\big(\tfrac{x}{N}\big)\overline{\omega}_{x}(t_{i})\Big|>N^{1/2}(\log N)^{3/2}\ell^{1/2}\Big\}.

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and (
3.24
), we write

\displaystyle{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant i\leqslant 1/\varepsilon}\Big(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{\ell}\big(\tfrac{x}{N}\big)\overline{\omega}_{x}(t_{i})\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leqslant N(\log N)^{3}\ell+{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant i\leqslant 1/\varepsilon}\Big(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{\ell}\big(\tfrac{x}{N}\big)\overline{\omega}_{x}(t_{i})\Big)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{B_{N}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leqslant N(\log N)^{3}\ell+{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant i\leqslant 1/\varepsilon}\Big(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{\ell}\big(\tfrac{x}{N}\big)\overline{\omega}_{x}(t_{i})\Big)^{4}\Big]^{1/2}{\bf P}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}(B_{N})^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant N(\log N)^{3}\ell+CN^{5}\ell{\bf P}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}(B_{N})^{1/2}.

Using the stationarity of the zero-range process and a standard large deviation principle, by union bound,

{\bf P}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}(B_{N})\leqslant\frac{C}{\varepsilon}e^{-C(\log N)^{3}}.

Thus, the first term in (
3.22
) is bounded by
CN(\log N)^{3}\ell
for
N
large enough, which finally yields

(3.27)

{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Big(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{\ell}\big(\tfrac{x}{N}\big)\overline{\omega}_{x}(t)\Big)^{2}\Big]=O(N^{-40}\ell^{2}+N\ell(\log N)^{3}).

Putting together Equations (
3.17
), (
3.18
), (
3.19
), (
3.20
), (
3.27
), and by the elementary inequality
(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k})^{2}\leqslant n\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}
, we obtain,

{\bf E}^{N}_{\mu_{\rho}}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\overline{J^{\rm ZR}_{-1,0}}(t)^{2}\Big]\leqslant C\Big(N^{-40}\ell^{2}+N(\log N)^{3}\ell+\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}N+N^{\gamma-1}}{\ell}+\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}N^{2}}{\ell^{2}}+\frac{N^{2\gamma-1}}{\ell}\Big).

We then prove the first bound in (
3.13
) by taking
\ell=N^{1/3}
if
\gamma\leqslant 4/3
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}=1
, and by taking
\ell=N^{\gamma-1}
otherwise. For the second bound in (
3.13
), we simply use our assumption that
\gamma\leqslant 3/2
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Note that to bound (
3.21
), the main ingredient is a large deviation principle for the sum
\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{\ell}\big(\tfrac{x}{N}\big)\overline{\omega}_{x}(t)
, and the time-uniform bound turns out to have an
O((\log N)^{3})
correction compared with the one without the supremum inside the expectation. This technique holds as soon as the quantity to estimate has exponentially decaying tails (in particular when a large deviations principle holds) and result will be used repeatedly in the following section. Since the argument will always be analogous, we will not always detail the time uniform estimate, and instead refer to the proof of Lemma
3.2
. To be quite explicit, except for Martingales, for which Doob’s inequality yields the wanted result, the scheme to obtain a time-uniform estimate will always be the following

1.

Divide the time interval into a polynomial number (e.g.
1/\varepsilon:=N^{100}
) of small time intervals
[t_{i},t_{i}+1[
. By observing all the
\omega(t_{i})
, one actually observes all the
\{\omega(t),\;0\leqslant t\leqslant T\}
, unless two jumps occurred in one of the small time intervals.

2.

Since the process in a box of size
K
jumps at rate
O(KN^{2})
, if
K=O(N^{2})
, the latter happens with arbitrarily small probability, by letting
\varepsilon
be as small as wanted.

3.

Then, the supremum over the
t_{i}
’s is obtained by stationarity and union bound, which makes us loose a factor
1/\varepsilon
, which is not a problem if the probability that the relevant quantity is too large vanishes exponentially in
N
.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigg[-\rho G\Big(\tfrac{X_{y}(t)}{N}-tv_{N}\Big)+\sum_{x=X_{y}(t)+1}^{X_{y+1}(t)-1}(1-\rho)G\big(\tfrac{x}{N}-tv_{N}\big)\bigg]

(4.2)

We first deal with the second term on the right side in (

), and show that it vanishes in

. In what follows, we define

(4.3)

Proposition 4.1
.

For any smooth function
G\in\mathcal{S}
with compact support,

(4.4)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{N}_{\rho}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Big|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{x=X_{y}(t)+1}^{X_{y+1}(t)-1}\Big[G\big(\tfrac{x}{N}-tv_{N}\big)-G\big(\tfrac{X_{y}(t)}{N}-tv_{N}\big)\Big]\Big|\Big]=0.

Proof.

Denote

M_{G}(y,t)=\sup_{X_{y}(t)\leqslant x\leqslant X_{y+1}(t)}|G^{\prime}\big(\tfrac{x}{N}-tv_{N}\big)|.

By Taylor’s expansion, since for any
y
, the number of terms in the sum over

is
\omega_{y}(t)+1
(cf. (
3.3
))

(4.5)

\mathbb{E}^{N}_{\rho}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Big|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{x=X_{y}(t)+1}^{X_{y+1}(t)-1}\Big[G\big(\tfrac{x}{N}-tv_{N}\big)-G\big(\tfrac{X_{y}(t)}{N}-tv_{N}\big)\Big]\Big|\Big]\\
\leqslant\mathbb{E}^{N}_{\rho}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Big|\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}(\omega_{y}(t)+1)^{2}M_{G}(y,t)\Big|\Big].

The next step is to restrict the support of the sum over
y
. Recall (
4.3
) and define

\Gamma_{N}:=\{y\in\mathbb{Z}\;:\;|y-y_{N}|<N^{4/3}\},

so that

(4.6)

\mathbb{E}^{N}_{\rho}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Big|\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}(\omega_{y}(t)+1)^{2}M_{G}(y,t)\Big|\Big]\\
\leqslant\mathbb{E}^{N}_{\rho}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{N}}(\omega_{y}(t)+1)^{2}M_{G}(y,t)\Big]+\mathbb{E}^{N}_{\rho}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{N}^{c}}(\omega_{y}(t)+1)^{2}M_{G}(y,t)\Big]\\
\leqslant\frac{\|G^{\prime}\|_{\infty}}{N^{3/2}}\mathbb{E}^{N}_{\rho}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{N}}(\omega_{y}(t)+1)^{2}\Big]+\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{N}^{c}}\mathbb{E}^{N}_{\rho}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}(\omega_{y}(t)+1)^{8}\Big]^{1/4}\mathbb{E}^{N}_{\rho}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}M_{G}(y,t)^{4/3}\Big]^{3/4},

by Hölder inequality. Note that

\mathbb{E}^{N}_{\rho}\Big[\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{N}}(\omega_{y}(t)+1)^{2}\Big]\leqslant CN^{4/3}

and that the sum inside the expectation satisfies a large deviations principle. Following the same steps as in the proof of Lemma
3.13
(see Remark
3.3
), we obtain that the first term on the right-hand side in (
4.6
) is
O\big(N^{4/3-3/2}(\log N)^{3}\big)
and therefore vanishes as
N\to\infty
. We now consider the second sum. First observe that

(4.7)

\mathbb{E}^{N}_{\rho}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}(\omega_{0}(t)+1)^{8}\Big]\leqslant\mathbb{E}^{N}_{\rho}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\sum_{|y|\leqslant(\log N)^{2}}(\omega_{y}(t)+1)^{8}\Big]\leqslant C(\log N)^{8},

where in the second inequality we use Remark
3.3
again. Thus, the second term on the right-hand side in (
4.6
) is bounded by

\frac{C(\log N)^{2}}{N^{3/2}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{N}^{c}}\mathbb{E}^{N}_{\rho}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}M_{G}(y,t)^{4/3}\Big]^{3/4}.

Denote by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}A:=A(G)>0}
the size of
G
’s support, meaning that
G
vanishes on the set
\mathbb{R}\setminus[-A,A]
. Since
M_{G}(y,t)=0
if
|\tfrac{X_{y}(t)}{N}-tv_{N}|>A
and
|\tfrac{X_{y+1}(t)}{N}-tv_{N}|>A
, using (
4.5
) and a union bound, we obtain that the expectation in (
4.4
) is bounded from above by

(4.8)

\frac{C(\rho,G)(\log N)^{2}}{N^{3/2}}\sum_{y\mbox{ \tiny or }y-1\in\Gamma_{N}^{c}}\mathbb{P}^{N}_{\rho}\big(\inf_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}|\tfrac{X_{y}(t)}{N}-tv_{N}|\leqslant A\big)^{3/4}+O(N^{-1/6}(\log N)^{3})

We now need to show that for any
y\notin\Gamma_{N}
,
\tfrac{X_{y}(t)}{N}-v_{N}t
is w.h.p. outside of the support of
G
.

Fix
y\in\mathbb{Z}
, using (
3.3
) and (
3.12
), we obtain

(4.9)

X_{y}(t)=\sum_{y^{\prime}=1}^{y}[\omega_{y^{\prime}-1}(t)+2]+X_{0}(t)=\sum_{y^{\prime}=1}^{y}[\omega_{y^{\prime}-1}(t)+2]-J^{\rm ZR}_{-1,0}(t)+X_{0}(0).

Recall from (
4.3

.. Using (
3.11

4.2

\displaystyle\tfrac{X_{y}(t)}{N}-v_{N}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left[\sum_{y^{\prime}=1}^{y}\omega_{y^{\prime}-1}(t)+2y-J^{\rm ZR}_{-1,0}(t)+X_{0}(0)\right]-v_{N}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left[\sum_{y^{\prime}=1}^{y}\overline{\omega}_{y^{\prime}-1}(t)-\overline{J^{\rm ZR}_{-1,0}}(t)\right]-tv^{\prime}_{N}+\frac{2y+X_{0}(0)}{N}+\frac{y}{N}\frac{2\rho-1}{1-\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left[\sum_{y^{\prime}=1}^{y}\overline{\omega}_{y^{\prime}-1}(t)-\overline{J^{\rm ZR}_{-1,0}}(t)+X_{0}(0)+\frac{y-y_{N}}{(1-\rho)}\right].

For
y\in\Gamma_{N}^{c}
, shorten
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{y}:=|y-y_{N}|/(1-\rho)}\geqslant N^{4/3}
. Since

X_{0}(0)\geqslant\frac{\overline{y}-NA}{3},\quad\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\;\left|\;\sum_{y^{\prime}=1}^{y}\overline{\omega}_{y^{\prime}-1}(t)\;\right|\;\geqslant\frac{\overline{y}-NA}{3}\quad\mbox{ or }\quad\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\;\left|\;\overline{J^{\rm ZR}_{-1,0}}(t)\;\right|\;\geqslant\frac{\overline{y}-NA}{3}.

p_{y}:=\mathbb{P}_{\rho}^{N}\left(X_{0}(0)\geqslant\frac{\overline{y}-NA}{3}\right)=O(e^{-c(\overline{y}-NA)})

for some positive constant

for some positive constant

. Note that because of the non-stationarity at the origin,

is not a stationary Facilitated Zero-Range process. However, thanks to Proposition

q_{y}:={\mathbb{P}}_{\rho}^{N}\left(\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\;\left|\;\sum_{y^{\prime}=1}^{y}\overline{\omega}_{y^{\prime}-1}(t)\;\right|\;\geqslant\frac{\overline{y}-NA}{3}\right)\\
={\bf P}_{\mu_{\rho}}^{N}\left(\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\;\left|\;\sum_{y^{\prime}=1}^{y}\overline{\omega}_{y^{\prime}-1}(t)\;\right|\;\geqslant\frac{\overline{y}-NA}{3}\right)=O\big(N^{4}y^{6}e^{-c(\overline{y}-NA)^{2}/|y|}+y^{-4}\big),

where in the last step, we divide the time interval

We split the exponent depending on whether

. If

\frac{(\overline{y}-NA)^{2}}{|y|}\geqslant\frac{(N^{4/3}-NA)^{2}}{2y_{N}}\geqslant cN^{8/3-\gamma}\geqslant cN

since

is assumed to be less than

q_{y}\leqslant N^{4+6\gamma}e^{-cN}{\bf 1}_{\{y\in B_{N}\}}+N^{4}y^{6}e^{-c\overline{y}}{\bf 1}_{\{y\in\Gamma_{N}^{c}\setminus B_{N}\}}+y^{-4}.

Since

\mathbb{P}^{N}_{\rho}\big(\inf_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}|\tfrac{X_{y}(t)}{N}-tv_{N}|\leqslant A\big)\leqslant p_{y}+q_{y}+r_{y},

Summing this identity over

and multiplying by

yields that the first term in (

) vanishes as

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}A>0}

Then, the above lemma follows immediately from the following two results.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any test function
G

(4.20)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{N}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Big|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{N}(G)}\overline{\omega}_{y}(t)\big[G\big(\tfrac{X_{y}(t)}{N}-v_{N}t\big)-G\big(\tfrac{y}{N(1-\rho)}-tv^{\prime}_{N}\big)\big]\Big|\Big]=0.

.

For any test function

R_{N,T}(G):=\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Big|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y\notin\Gamma_{N}(G)}\overline{\omega}_{y}(t)\big[G\big(\tfrac{X_{y}(t)}{N}-v_{N}t\big)-G\big(\tfrac{y}{N(1-\rho)}-tv^{\prime}_{N}\big)\big]\Big|

Proof of Lemma

.

For

Summing by parts, we bound the absolute value in (

\Big|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{N}(G)}\overline{\omega}_{y}(t)\Delta_{y}^{\ell}(G)\Big|+\Big|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{N}(G)}\overline{\omega}^{\ell}_{y}(t)\big[G\big(\tfrac{X_{y}(t)}{N}-tv_{N}\big)-G\big(\tfrac{y}{N(1-\rho)}-tv^{\prime}_{N}\big)\big]\Big|,

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{\omega}^{\ell}_{y}(t)=(2\ell+1)^{-1}\sum_{|z-y|\leqslant\ell}\overline{\omega}_{z}(t)}
. By (

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{N}\big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\overline{\omega}_{y}(t)^{2}\big]^{1/2}\leqslant C(\log N)^{4}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{N}\big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\overline{\omega}^{\ell}_{y}(t)^{2}\big]^{1/2}\leqslant C(\log\ell)^{3}/\sqrt{\ell},

we obtain that the expectation in (

(4.22)

\frac{C(\log N)^{4}}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{N}(G)}\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{N}\big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Delta_{y}^{\ell}(G)^{2}\big]^{1/2}\\
+\frac{C(\log\ell)^{3}}{\sqrt{N\ell}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{N}(G)}\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{N}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Big(G\big(\tfrac{X_{y}(t)}{N}-tv_{N}\big)-G\big(\tfrac{y}{N(1-\rho)}-tv^{\prime}_{N}\big)\Big)^{2}\Big]^{1/2}.

(a+b+c)^{2}\leqslant 3a^{2}+3b^{2}+3b^{2}

+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{4}}\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{N}\left(\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}[X_{0}(t)-X_{\ell}(t)]^{4}\right)\right)+O(\ell^{4}/N^{4}).

Rewrite

Since, by Remark

5.
Proof by sharp estimates

In this section, we give an alternative proof of Theorem
2.2
which does not rely on the mapping to the zero-range process. Doing so, we obtain a sharper estimate (in
O(\log^{2}\ell/\ell)
on the equivalence of ensembles for the FEP than the one previously obtained in
[
5
]
(in
O(\ell^{-1/4})
) to derive the supercritical hydrodynamic limit. This estimate is the main argument needed to derive equilibrium fluctuations, and we feel it is interesting on its own, which is the reason why we give this alternative proof.

Theorem 5.1

(Symmetric case)
.

For
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}=1
,
\gamma=-\infty
, the FEP’s fluctuation field

(5.1)

{\mathcal{Y}}^{N}_{t}(G)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\overline{\eta}_{x}(t)G(x/N),

converges in the weak uniform topology on
D\big([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\big)
, as
N\to\infty
to a process
\{Y_{t},\;0\leqslant t\leqslant T\}
, which is solution to the stochastic heat equation in the sense of Proposition
2.1
, meaning that for any
G\in\mathcal{S}
,

M_{t}(G):=Y_{t}(G)-Y_{0}(G)-D(\rho)\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}(\partial_{u}^{2}G)ds

N_{t}(G):=\big[M_{t}(G)\big]^{2}-2t\sigma(\rho)\|\partial_{u}G\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

are both integrable martingales w.r.t.
Y
’s natural filtration, and that for any
t\geqslant 0
,

(5.2)

\mathbb{E}(Y_{t}(G)Y_{t}(H))=\chi(\rho)\langle G,H\rangle.

First note that in the stationary state, direct calculation immediately yields (
5.2
). Furthermore, for any compactly supported test functions
G:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, by Dynkin’s martingale formula,

{\mathcal{M}}^{N}_{t}(G):=\mathcal{Y}^{N}_{t}(G)-\mathcal{Y}^{N}_{0}(G)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{L}_{N}\mathcal{Y}^{N}_{s}(G)ds

is a martingale with quadratic variation given by

\langle{\mathcal{M}}^{N}_{\cdot}(G)\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{L}_{N}\mathcal{Y}^{N}_{s}(G)^{2}-2\mathcal{Y}^{N}_{s}(G)\mathscr{L}_{N}\mathcal{Y}^{N}_{s}(G)ds.

Direct calculations yield

\mathscr{L}_{N}\mathcal{Y}^{N}_{s}(G)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}[\tau_{x}h(\eta(s))-a(\rho)]\partial_{u}^{2,N}G(x/N),

where
h(\eta)=\eta_{-1}\eta_{0}+\eta_{0}\eta_{1}-\eta_{-1}\eta_{0}\eta_{1}
,
a(\rho)
is
h
’s average under
\pi_{\rho}
defined in (
2.9
) and
\partial_{u}^{2,N}G
is a discrete approximation of
G
’s laplacian,

\partial_{u}^{2,N}G(x/N)=N^{2}\left[G((x+1)/N)+G((x-1)/N)-2G(x/N)\right].

Furthermore,

\langle{\mathcal{M}}^{N}_{\cdot}(G)\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{x,x+1}(\eta_{s})[\partial_{u}^{N}G(x/N)]^{2}ds.

where similarly

\partial_{u}^{N}G(x/N)=N\left[G((x+1)/N)-G(x/N)\right].

Note that by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and from the exponential decay of correlations under the measure
\pi_{\rho}
, one straightforwardly obtains the following result :

Lemma 5.2
.

For any compactly supported test functions
G:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle{\mathcal{M}}^{N}_{\cdot}(G)\rangle_{t}=t\sigma(\rho)||\partial_{u}G||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\quad\text{in $L^{2}(\pi_{\rho})$},

where
\sigma(\rho)
is defined in (
2.10
).

Indeed, to prove the above result, one just note that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{{\rho}}^{N}\big[\langle{\mathcal{M}}^{N}_{\cdot}(G)\rangle_{t}\big]=t\sigma(\rho)||\partial_{u}G||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

and the variance of
\langle{\mathcal{M}}^{N}_{\cdot}(G)\rangle_{t}
is bounded by a constant multiple of

\frac{t^{2}}{N^{2}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}[\partial_{u}^{N}G(x/N)]^{4},

which has order
O(N^{-1})
and thus vanishes as
N\rightarrow\infty
. Following classical estimates (see
[
25
]
for example), it is not hard to show the following result.

Lemma 5.3
.

The sequence
\{\mathcal{Y}^{N}_{t},0\leqslant t\leqslant T\}
is tight with respect to the weak uniform topology of
D([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
.

The main ingredient to derive the equilibrium fluctuations for the FEP is the Boltzmann-Gibbs principle, whose proof will be adapted from
[
22
]
. For any local function
\psi:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, denote

\widetilde{\psi}(\rho)=E_{\pi_{\rho}}[\psi].

Proposition 5.4

(Boltzmann-Gibbs principle)
.

For any local function of the configuration
\psi
, any compactly supported smooth function
G
, and any
t>0
, we have

(5.3)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{N}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}ds\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}G(x/N)\tau_{x}V_{\psi}(\eta(s))\right)^{2}\right]=0,

where

V_{\psi}(\eta)=\psi(\eta)-\widetilde{\psi}(\rho)-\widetilde{\psi}^{\prime}(\rho)(\eta_{0}-\rho),

and
\tau_{x}\psi(\eta)=\psi(\eta_{\cdot-x})
denotes the translation of
\psi
by
x
.

By Proposition
5.4
and Lemma
5.2
, for any limit
Y_{\cdot}
of
\mathcal{Y}^{N}_{\cdot}
and any compactly supported test functions

,

Y_{t}(G)-Y_{0}(G)-D(\rho)\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}(\partial_{u}^{2}G)ds

is a martingale with quadratic variation

which proves Theorem
5.1
. We now prove Proposition

5.1.
Boltzmann-Gibbs Principle

In this section, we prove Proposition
5.4
. Since it is the main case of interest, let us assume
\psi
depends only on the values of
\eta_{0}
and
\eta_{\pm 1}
. The proof is divided into several steps, and it can be straightforwardly adapted when
\psi
depends on the value of
\eta
in a finite box

.

Step 1
. Fix
\ell>0
, which will goes to infinity after
N\rightarrow\infty
. For
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, fix points
x_{i}<y_{i}
in
\mathbb{Z}
such that
x_{0}=0
, and for
i\in\mathbb{Z}

y_{i}-x_{i}=\ell-1,\qquad y_{i}+3=x_{i+1}.

Let
A_{i}=\{x_{i},\dots,y_{i}\}
. Then, the length of the interval
A_{i}
is
\ell
for each
i
. Denote

A_{i}^{o}=\{x_{i}+1,\dots,y_{i}-1\},\qquad B_{i}=\{y_{i},\dots,x_{i+1}\}.

Fix some point
z_{i}\in A_{i}^{o}
. Then, we rewrite the sum inside the expectation in (
5.3
) as

(5.4)

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}G(x/N)\tau_{x}V_{\psi}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{x\in B_{i}}G(x/N)\tau_{x}V_{\psi}+\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{x\in A_{i}^{o}}\big(G(x/N)-G(z_{i}/N)\big)\tau_{x}V_{\psi}\\
+\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}G(z_{i}/N)\sum_{x\in A_{i}^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}.

For the first term above, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the invariance of the measure
\pi_{\rho}
,

(5.5)

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{N}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}ds\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{x\in{B}_{i}}G(x/N)\tau_{x}V_{\psi}(\eta_{s})\right)^{2}\right]\leqslant t^{2}E_{\pi_{\rho}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{x\in{B}_{i}}G(x/N)\tau_{x}V_{\psi}(\eta)\right)^{2}\right]\\
=\frac{t^{2}}{N}\sum_{i\neq i^{\prime}}{\rm Cov}_{\pi_{\rho}}\left[\sum_{x\in{B}_{i}}G(x/N)\tau_{x}V_{\psi}(\eta),\sum_{x\in{B}_{i^{\prime}}}G(x/N)\tau_{x}V_{\psi}(\eta)\right]\\
+\frac{t^{2}}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}E_{\pi_{\rho}}\left[\left(\sum_{x\in{B}_{i}}G(x/N)\tau_{x}V_{\psi}(\eta)\right)^{2}\right].

Since
|B_{i}|=4
, it is easy to see that there exists a constant
C=C(G,\psi)
such that the second term on the right hand side is bounded by
Ct^{2}/\ell
, which vanishes in the limit as
\ell\rightarrow\infty
. Since the distance between the two boxes
B_{i}
and
B_{i^{\prime}}
is of order
|i-i^{\prime}|\ell
, by the exponential decay of correlations of the measure
\pi_{\rho}
(
cf.

[
5
, Corollary 6.6]
), there exists a constant
C
independent of
N
such that the first term in the last inequality is bounded by

\frac{t^{2}}{N}\sum_{i\neq i^{\prime}}E_{\pi_{\rho}}\left[\Big|\sum_{x\in{B}_{i}}G(x/N)\tau_{x}V_{\psi}(\eta)\Big|\right]E_{\pi_{\rho}}\left[\Big|\sum_{x\in{B}_{i^{\prime}}}G(x/N)\tau_{x}V_{\psi}(\eta)\Big|\right]e^{-C\ell|i-i^{\prime}|}.

We may bound the above term by

\frac{Ct^{2}}{N}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}=-CN/\ell}^{CN/\ell}e^{-C\ell|i-i^{\prime}|}\leqslant\frac{Ct^{2}}{\ell}

for some constant
C=C(G,\psi)
.

For the contribution of the second term in (
5.4
), by smoothness of the function
G
, there exists a constant
C=C(G,\psi)
such that

\Big|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{x\in A_{i}^{o}}\big(G(x/N)-G(z_{i}/N)\big)\tau_{x}V_{\psi}\Big|\leqslant\frac{C\ell}{\sqrt{N}},

which vanishes as
N\rightarrow\infty
.

Step 2.
It remains to deal with the contribution of third term in (
5.4
). For any finite set
F\subset\mathbb{Z}
, denote by
\eta^{F}
the empirical density of particles in the set
F
in configuration
\eta
,

\eta^{F}=\frac{1}{|F|}\sum_{x\in F}\eta_{x}.

For
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, define

\widetilde{V}_{\psi,i}(\eta)=E_{\pi_{\rho}}\Big[\sum_{x\in A_{i}^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}\big|\eta^{A_{i}},\eta_{x_{i}-1},\eta_{y_{i}+1}\Big].

Then, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

(5.6)

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{N}\left[\Big(\int_{0}^{t}ds\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}G(z_{i}/N)\sum_{x\in A_{i}^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}(\eta(s))\Big)^{2}\right]\\
\leqslant 2\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{N}\left[\Big(\int_{0}^{t}ds\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}G(z_{i}/N)\Big\{\sum_{x\in A_{i}^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}(\eta(s))-\widetilde{V}_{\psi,i}(\eta(s))\Big\}\Big)^{2}\right]\\
+2\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{N}\left[\Big(\int_{0}^{t}ds\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}G(z_{i}/N)\widetilde{V}_{\psi,i}(\eta(s))\Big)^{2}\right].

We first prove the second term above converges to zero as
N\rightarrow\infty,\ell\rightarrow\infty
. Shorten

A=A_{0}=\{0,\dots,\ell-1\},

and
\widetilde{V}_{\psi}=\widetilde{V}_{\psi,0}
. By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the exponential decay of correlations of the measure
\pi_{\rho}
(
cf.

[
5
, Corollary 6.6]
), there exists a constant
C=C(G)
such that

(5.7)

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{N}\left[\Big(\int_{0}^{t}ds\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}G(z_{i}/N)\widetilde{V}_{\psi,i}(\eta(s))\Big)^{2}\right]\\
\leqslant Ct^{2}\ell E_{\pi_{\rho}}\Big[\Big(E_{\pi_{\rho}}\Big[\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{x\in A^{o}}\tau_{x}\psi\big|\eta^{A},\eta_{x_{0}-1},\eta_{y_{0}+1}\Big]-\widetilde{\psi}(\eta^{A})\Big)^{2}\Big]\\
+Ct^{2}\ell E_{\pi_{\rho}}\Big[\big(\widetilde{\psi}(\eta^{A})-\widetilde{\psi}(\rho)-\widetilde{\psi}^{\prime}(\rho)(\eta^{A}-\rho)\big)^{2}\Big]+\mathcal{O}(\ell^{-1}).

Fix
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
\rho-\varepsilon_{0}>1/2
and that
\rho+\varepsilon_{0}<1
. We bound the first term above by

Ct^{2}\ell\Big\{E_{\pi_{\rho}}\Big[\Big(E_{\pi_{\rho}}\Big[\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{x\in A^{o}}\tau_{x}\psi\big|\eta^{A},\eta_{x_{0}-1},\eta_{y_{0}+1}\Big]-\widetilde{\psi}(\eta^{A})\Big)^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{|\eta^{A}-\rho|<\varepsilon_{0}}\Big]+P_{\pi_{\rho}}\Big(|\eta^{A}-\rho|\geqslant\varepsilon_{0}\Big)\Big\}.

According to Corollary

, under

,
\{\eta_{x}\}_{x\geqslant 0}
is an ergodic Markov chain with finite state space
\{0,1\}
, and with transition probability

By

, for any

C

Together with Proposition

Ct^{2}\Big[(\log\ell)^{4}/\ell+\ell e^{-C\ell}\Big].

By Taylor’s expansion and the above large deviation estimates, the second term in (

Ct^{2}\ell E_{\pi_{\rho}}\big[(\eta^{A}-\rho)^{4}\big]\leqslant Ct^{2}\ell\Big[E_{\pi_{\rho}}\big[(\eta^{A}-\rho)^{4}\mathbbm{1}_{|\eta^{A}-\rho|<\ell^{-1/3}}\big]+P_{\pi_{\rho}}\Big(|\eta^{A}-\rho|>\ell^{-1/3}\Big)\Big]\\
\leqslant Ct^{2}\Big[\ell^{-1/3}+\ell^{5/3}e^{-C\ell^{1/3}}\Big].

Step 3.
To conclude the proof, it remains to show

(5.8)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{N}\left[\Big(\int_{0}^{t}ds\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}G(z_{i}/N)\Big\{\sum_{x\in A_{i}^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}(\eta(s))-\widetilde{V}_{\psi,i}(\eta(s))\Big\}\Big)^{2}\right]=0.

By Kipnis-Varadhan’s inequality (see e.g.
[
22
, Proposition A1.6.1, p333]
), the above term is bounded by

(5.9)

20t\sup_{f\in L^{2}(\pi_{\rho})}\Big\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}G(z_{i}/N)E_{\pi_{\rho}}\Big[\Big\{\sum_{x\in A_{i}^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}-\widetilde{V}_{\psi,i}\Big\}f\Big]-E_{\pi_{\rho}}\big[f(-\mathscr{L}_{N})f\big]\Big\}.

To make notations short, let
f_{i}=\tau_{x_{0}-x_{i}}f
. Define
\overline{A}=A\cup\{x_{0}-1,y_{0}+1\}
. For
\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{\overline{A}}
, denote

\overline{f}(\sigma)=E_{\pi_{\rho}}[f|\eta_{|\overline{A}}=\sigma].

Since
\pi_{\rho}
is translation invariant, and
\sum_{x\in A^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}-\widetilde{V}_{\psi}
depends only on the values of
(\eta_{x},x\in\overline{A})
,

(5.10)

E_{\pi_{\rho}}\Big[\Big\{\sum_{x\in A_{i}^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}-\widetilde{V}_{\psi,i}\Big\}f\Big]=E_{\pi_{\rho}}\Big[\Big\{\sum_{x\in A^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}-\widetilde{V}_{\psi}\Big\}f_{i}\Big]=E_{\pi_{\rho}}\Big[\Big\{\sum_{x\in A^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}-\widetilde{V}_{\psi}\Big\}\overline{f_{i}}\Big].

We now project on hyperplanes with fixed number of particles in
A=\{x_{0}\dots,y_{0}\}
. However,
\pi_{\rho}
is not constant on such a set because the probability to see a local configuration depends on its boundary values. For this reason, aside from conditionning to the number of particles in
A
, we also condition to fixed boundary values; For any triplet
\overline{k}=(k,a,b)\in\{0,\dots,\ell\}\times\{0,1\}^{2}
, define

\Sigma_{A,\overline{k}}=\left\{\eta\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}},\;\sum_{z\in A}\eta_{z}=k,\;\eta_{x_{0}-1}=a,\;\eta_{y_{0}+1}=b\right\},

the set of configurations on
\overline{A}
with
k

A
and with boundary states
(a,b)
.
We can now rewrite the last term in the right-hand side of (

\sum_{\overline{k}}m_{\overline{k}}E_{A,\overline{k}}\Big[\Big\{\sum_{x\in A^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}-\widetilde{V}_{\psi}\Big\}\overline{f_{i}}\Big],

where
m_{\overline{k}}=\pi_{\rho}(\Sigma_{A,\overline{k}})
, and for any

defined on
\{0,1\}^{\overline{A}}

E_{A,\overline{k}}[g]=E_{\pi_{\rho}}\big[\,g(\eta_{\mid\overline{A}})\,|\,\eta\in\Sigma_{A,\overline{k}}\big].

For any
\overline{k}=(k,a,b)\in\{0,\dots,\ell\}\times\{0,1\}^{2}
and any function

, define

as

\mathscr{L}_{A,\overline{k}}g(\eta)=\sum_{z=x_{0}}^{y_{0}}c_{x,x+1}(\eta)(g(\eta^{x,x+1})-g(\eta))

with the convention that
\eta_{x_{0}-1}=a,\eta_{y_{0}+1}=b
. Note tha the generator does not in fact depend on the number
k
of particles in
A
, just on the boundary states
a
and
b

\pi_{\rho}
only charges ergodic configurations, we claim that

is in the range of
\mathscr{L}_{A,\overline{k}}
, i.e. that there exists a function
g
on
\Sigma_{A,\overline{k}}
such that

\sum_{x\in A^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}-\widetilde{V}_{\psi}=\mathscr{L}_{A,\overline{k}}g.

The claim follows directly from the fact that
E_{A,\overline{k}}\big[\sum_{x\in A^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}-\widetilde{V}_{\psi}\big]=0
and from the following observations:

(i)

the codimension of
\mathscr{L}_{A,\overline{k}}
’s range is one in the space of functions on
\Sigma_{A,\overline{k}}
. Indeed, if
\mathscr{L}_{A,\overline{k}}g=0
, then

implying that

is constant on
\Sigma_{A,\overline{k}}
by ergodicity. In particular, the dimension of the kernel of
\mathscr{L}_{A,\overline{k}}
is one, which proves the claim.

(ii)

The set of mean-
0

1

\mathscr{L}_{A,\overline{k}}
. In particular, the range of
\mathscr{L}_{A,\overline{k}}
is the set of mean-
0
functions.

Then, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, for any
\gamma>0

E_{A,\overline{k}}\Big[\Big\{\sum_{x\in A^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}-\widetilde{V}_{\psi}\Big\}\overline{f_{i}}\Big]\leqslant\gamma E_{A,\overline{k}}\Big[\Big\{\sum_{x\in A^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}-\widetilde{V}_{\psi}\Big\}(-\mathscr{L}_{A,\overline{k}})^{-1}\Big\{\sum_{x\in A^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}-\widetilde{V}_{\psi}\Big\}\Big]\\
+\gamma^{-1}E_{A,\overline{k}}\Big[\overline{f_{i}}(-\mathscr{L}_{A,\overline{k}})\overline{f_{i}}\Big].

Therefore, we may bound the first term in (

) by

(5.11)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}G(z_{i}/N)E_{\pi_{\rho}}\Big[\Big\{\sum_{x\in A_{i}^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}-\widetilde{V}_{\psi,i}\Big\}f\Big]\\
\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma G(z_{i}/N)\sum_{\overline{k}}m_{\overline{k}}E_{A,\overline{k}}\Big[\Big\{\sum_{x\in A^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}-\widetilde{V}_{\psi}\Big\}(-\mathscr{L}_{A,\overline{k}})^{-1}\Big\{\sum_{x\in A^{o}}\tau_{x}V_{\psi}-\widetilde{V}_{\psi}\Big\}\Big]\\
+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma^{-1}G(z_{i}/N)\sum_{\overline{k}}m_{\overline{k}}E_{A,\overline{k}}\Big[\overline{f_{i}}(-\mathscr{L}_{A,\overline{k}})\overline{f_{i}}\Big].

It is also easy to see that there exists a constant

such that

By convexity and translation invariance of the Dirichlet form,

N^{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{\overline{k}}m_{\overline{k}}E_{A,\overline{k}}\Big[\overline{f_{i}}(-\mathscr{L}_{A,\overline{k}})\overline{f_{i}}\Big]\leqslant E_{\pi_{\rho}}\big[f(-\mathscr{L}_{N})f\big].

Taking

Lemma 5.8

Observe that

a_{m+1}=\frac{1+\tfrac{m+1}{(1-\rho)\ell}}{1+\tfrac{m+1}{\rho\ell}}(a_{m}+1)-1=\frac{1+\tfrac{m+1}{(1-\rho)\ell}}{1+\tfrac{m+1}{\rho\ell}}a_{m}+(m+1)\frac{\tfrac{1}{(1-\rho)\ell}-\tfrac{1}{\rho\ell}}{1+\tfrac{m+1}{\rho\ell}}.

Since

In particular,

a_{m}\leqslant C(\rho)\Big[\Big(\frac{\rho(2-\rho)}{(1-\rho)(1+\rho)}\Big)^{m}+\frac{m}{\ell}\Big].

Since

\sum_{m=M\log\ell}^{\ell-1}\Big\{\prod_{n=1}^{m}\frac{1-\rho+\tfrac{n}{\ell}}{\rho+\tfrac{n}{\ell}}-\Big(\frac{1-\rho}{\rho}\Big)^{m}\Big\}=\sum_{m=M\log\ell}^{\ell-1}a_{m}\Big(\frac{1-\rho}{\rho}\Big)^{m}\\
\leqslant C(\rho)\sum_{m=M\log\ell}^{\ell-1}\Big(\frac{1-\rho}{\rho}\Big)^{m}\Big[\Big(\frac{\rho(2-\rho)}{(1-\rho)(1+\rho)}\Big)^{m}+\frac{m}{\ell}\Big]\leqslant C(\rho)\Big(\big(\tfrac{2-\rho}{1+\rho}\big)^{M\log\ell}+\ell^{-1}\Big)\leqslant\frac{C(\rho)}{\ell}.

The case
\rho=1
is easier and could be proved in the same way. This concludes the proof.

2.5
). We first show that
\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}
is translation invariant. To do so, fix a local configuration
\sigma
on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Lambda}=\{-\ell,\dots,\ell\}
, we can safely assume that there is at least an empty site to the left of the origin, and two empty sites to the right of the origin. Otherwise, we can simply derive the value of
\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\eta_{|\Lambda}=\sigma)
by extending
\sigma
on a larger set
\Lambda
. As a consequence of this assumption, the origin and site
1
’s clusters are both fully contained in
\Lambda,
where we call a
particle cluster
an empty site followed by all its consecutive particles. Two cases can arise: either
\sigma_{1}=1
, in which case the origin and site
1
are in the same cluster. Then, since the position of the origin in the cluster is chosen uniformly, we have by construction

\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\eta_{|\Lambda}=\sigma)=\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\eta_{|\tau_{-1}\Lambda}=\sigma_{\cdot+1}),

where we defined
\tau_{-1}\Lambda=\{-1-\ell,\ell-1\}
. The other possibility is that
\sigma_{1}=0
, in which case by translating the event
\{\eta_{|\Lambda}=\sigma\}
by
-1
, we change the origin’s cluster. For this reason, we need to compute explicitly the probability of obtaining
\sigma
. In
\sigma
, denote by
X_{0}:=-\omega^{\sigma}_{0}-2
the position of the first empty site left of the origin,
X_{1}=1
by assumption is the position of the first empty site right of the origin, and
X_{2}=\omega^{\sigma}_{1}+3
the position of the second empty site to the right of the origin. By construction, to obtain
\sigma
under
\widetilde{\mu}_{\rho}
, we need

1)

To choose the right size for the central cluster (the one of the origin), which occurs with probability

p_{1}:=(\omega^{\sigma}_{0}+2)a(\rho)^{\omega^{\sigma}_{0}}\rho(1-a(\rho))

given by (
3.7
).

2)

To choose the right position for the central cluster, which occurs with probability
p_{2}:=1/(\omega^{\sigma}_{0}+2)
.

3)

To build the next cluster right of the origin according to the Markovian construction, which then occurs with geometric probability

p_{3}:=\mu_{\rho}(\omega_{1}=\omega^{\sigma}_{1})=a(\rho)^{\omega^{\sigma}_{1}}(1-a(\rho))

given by (
3.5
).

4)

To build, by the Markovian construction, the rest of the configuration
\sigma
outside of those two clusters, which occurs with probability denote
p_{4}:=p_{4}(\sigma)
.

With these notations, we can now write

(A.3)

\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\eta_{|\Lambda}=\sigma)=p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}p_{4}.

Similarly, to obtain
\sigma_{\cdot+1}
in the translated box
\tau_{-1}\Lambda
, we need

1)

To choose the right size for the central cluster, which has now changed because of the translation, this occurs with probability

q_{1}:=(\omega^{\sigma}_{1}+2)a(\rho)^{\omega^{\sigma}_{1}}\rho(1-a(\rho))

2)

To choose the right position for the central cluster, which occurs with probability
q_{2}:=1/(\omega^{\sigma}_{1}+2)
.

3)

To build the cluster left of the origin according to the Markovian construction, which now occurs with probability

q_{3}:=\mu_{\rho}(\omega_{-1}=\omega^{\sigma}_{0})=a(\rho)^{\omega^{\sigma}_{0}}(1-a(\rho)).

given by (
3.5
).

4)

To build by the Markovian construction the rest of the configuration
\sigma
outside of those two clusters, which occurs with probability denote
q_{4}:=p_{4}(\sigma)

We obtain

(A.4)

\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\eta_{|\tau_{-1}\Lambda}=\sigma_{\cdot+1})=q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4}=\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\eta_{|\Lambda}=\sigma),

which proves that
\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}
is translation invariant since
\Lambda
is of arbitrary size.

By translation invariance, the average density
\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}}(\eta_{0})
is the average density of an arbitrary cluster, which is

(A.5)

\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}}(\eta_{0})=\frac{1+\frac{a(\rho)}{1-a(\rho)}}{2+\frac{a(\rho)}{1-a(\rho)}}=\frac{1}{2-a(\rho)}=\rho.

We now prove that
\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}
also satisfies the explicit formula (
2.5
) for
\pi_{\rho}
. To do so, we are going to use the translation invariance of
\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}
, and the fact that very far from the origin, the central cluster has no influence, and the configuration’s construction is Markovian. For this purpose, choose
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, an ergodic configuration
\sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}
on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Lambda}:=\Lambda_{\ell}(x)=\{x+1,\dots,x+\ell\}
, and denote by

\displaystyle n_{\circ\bullet}

\displaystyle:=card\{z\in\Lambda\setminus\{x+\ell\},\;\sigma_{z}=0,\;\sigma_{z+1}=1\},

\displaystyle n_{\bullet\circ}

\displaystyle:=card\{z\in\Lambda\setminus\{x+\ell\},\;\sigma_{z}=1,\;\sigma_{z+1}=0\},

\displaystyle n_{\bullet\bullet}

\displaystyle:=card\{z\in\Lambda\setminus\{x+\ell\},\;\sigma_{z}=\sigma_{z+1}=1\}.

Since
\sigma
was assumed ergodic,
n_{\circ\bullet}+n_{\bullet\circ}+n_{\bullet\bullet}=\ell-1
. Furthermore, denoting by
p=\sum_{z\in\Lambda}\sigma_{z}
the number of particles in
\sigma
,
\ell-p
is its number of empty sites, therefore

n_{\circ\bullet}=\ell-p-(1-\sigma_{x+\ell})\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad n_{\bullet\circ}=\ell-p-(1-\sigma_{x+1}).

In other words, the number of
\circ\bullet
is
\ell-p
except if the last site is empty, in which case it is
\ell-p-1
, and similarly for
\bullet\circ
. From those identities, we finally obtain

n_{\bullet\bullet}=2p-\ell+1-\sigma_{x+1}-\sigma_{x+\ell}.

Fix now
x\gg 1
, unless the central cluster reaches
x
, which occurs with probability exponentially small in
x
, we can use the markovian construction of
\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}
. Since
\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\eta_{x+1}=\sigma_{x+1})=\rho^{\sigma_{x+1}}(1-\rho)^{1-\sigma_{x+1}}
according to (
A.5
) and translation invariance, we obtain using the transition rates (
A.1
), (
A.2
)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\eta_{\mid\Lambda}=\sigma)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\eta_{\mid\Lambda\setminus\{x+1\}}=\sigma_{\mid\Lambda\setminus\{x+1\}})\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\eta_{x+1}=\sigma_{x+1})+O(e^{-cx})

\displaystyle=1^{n_{\circ\bullet}}(1-a(\rho))^{n_{\bullet\circ}}a(\rho)^{n_{\bullet\bullet}}\rho^{\sigma_{x+1}}(1-\rho)^{1-\sigma_{x+1}}+O(e^{-cx}).

Letting
x\to\infty
and using
\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}
’s translation invariance, we recover (
2.5
) and obtain as wanted that
\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}=\pi_{\rho}
.

Thanks to (
3.9
), we also have the following result.

Corollary A.1
.

Under
\pi_{\rho}
, both
(\eta_{x})_{x\geqslant 0}
and
(\eta_{-x})_{x\geqslant 0}
are distributed as homogeneous Markov chains, started from
\eta_{0}\sim Bernoulli(\rho)
, and with transition probabilities given by (
A.1
) and (
A.2
). However, these two Markov chains are
not
independant.

Proof.

For any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
, by construction,
{\mathbb{P}}(\eta_{x_{0}}=1)=\rho
, and the distribution of
(\eta_{x_{0}+x})_{x\geqslant 0}
converges as
x_{0}\to\infty
to that of a homogeneous Markov chain with transition probabilities given by (
A.1
) and (
A.2
), because the influence of the initial cluster’s construction vanishes. But by translation invariance, this distribution does not depend on
x_{0}
, which proves the corollary.

A.2.
Compressibility

We prove here for the sake of completeness identity (
2.11
) for the equilibrium compressibility for the FEP, namely

\chi(\rho)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}Cov_{\pi_{\rho}}(\eta_{0},\eta_{x})=\rho(1-\rho)(2\rho-1).

To prove it, it is convenient to consider the construction
\widetilde{\pi}_{\rho}
for
\pi_{\rho}
obtained in Section
A.1
rather than (
2.5
). We start by writing by translation invariance of
\pi_{\rho}
that

(A.6)

\chi(\rho)=\lim_{y\to\infty}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}Cov_{\pi_{\rho}}(\eta_{y},\eta_{x+y})=\rho(1-\rho)+2\lim_{y\to\infty}\sum_{x\geqslant 1}Cov_{\pi_{\rho}}(\eta_{y},\eta_{x+y}).

Because we send
y
to
\infty
, we do not need to take into account the central cluster, so that the distribution of
(\eta_{x+y})_{x\geqslant 0}
, in the limit
y\to\infty
is that of a Markov chain with transition probabilities given by (
A.1
) and (
A.2
). Consider therefore the distribution
\nu_{\rho}
of a markov chain
(\eta_{x})_{x\geqslant 0}
with transition probabilities given by (
A.1
) and (
A.2
).

We now define

g_{x}=\nu_{\rho}(\eta_{0}\eta_{x}=1),\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad h_{x}=g_{x}-\rho^{2}=Cov_{\nu_{\rho}}(\eta_{0},\eta_{x}),

and straightforward computation yield
h_{1}=\rho(a(\rho)-\rho)=-(1-\rho)^{2}
. We then write

g_{x+1}=\nu_{\rho}(\eta_{0}\eta_{x}\eta_{x+1}=1)+\nu_{\rho}(\eta_{0}(1-\eta_{x})\eta_{x+1}=1).

Because
\nu_{\rho}
only charges the ergodic component, the second term in the right-hand side is equal to
\nu_{\rho}(\eta_{0}(1-\eta_{x})=1)=\rho-g_{x}
, whereas the first is
\nu_{\rho}(\eta_{0}\eta_{x}\eta_{x+1}=1)=a(\rho)g_{x}.
This straightforwardly yields

h_{x+1}=-\frac{1-\rho}{\rho}h_{x}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad h_{x}=\rho(1-\rho)\left(\frac{\rho-1}{\rho}\right)^{x},

so that using (
A.6
), we obtain

\chi(\rho)=\rho(1-\rho)+2\sum_{x\geqslant 1}h_{x}=\rho(1-\rho)(2\rho-1)

as wanted.

A.3.
Stationary states and mapping

In this section, we comment on why the mapped distribution
\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}(\omega=\cdot)
defined in Section
3.3
is
not
a stationary state for the zero-range generator (
3.4
). The reason is straightforward, because we need to consider instead the generator
\widehat{\mathscr{L}}^{\mbox{\tiny{zr}}}_{N}
on the mapped pair
(\omega,X_{0})
. More precisely, given a FEP trajectory
\eta=\{\eta(t),\;t\geqslant 0\}
the process
(\omega,X_{0})(t):=\Pi(\eta(t))
defined by the mapping
\Pi
defined in Section
3.1
is a Markov process with generator
\widehat{\mathscr{L}}^{\mbox{\tiny{zr}}}_{N}
defined on functions
f
on
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbb{Z}}
(cf.
3.2
) as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathscr{L}}^{\mbox{\tiny{zr}}}_{N}f(\omega,x)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}^{\mbox{\tiny{zr}},\star}_{N}f(\cdot,x)(\omega)

\displaystyle+\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{-1}\geqslant 1\}}p_{N}\Big\{f(\omega^{-1,0},x-1)-f(\omega,x)\Big\}+\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{1}\geqslant 1\}}q_{N}\Big\{f(\omega^{1,0},x)-f(\omega,x)\Big\}

\displaystyle+\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{0}\geqslant 1\}}p_{N}\Big\{f(\tau_{-1}(\omega^{0,1}),0)\mathbf{1}_{\{x=-\omega_{0}-1\}}+f(\omega^{0,1},x)\mathbf{1}_{\{x>-\omega_{0}-1\}}-f(\omega,x)\Big\}

\displaystyle+\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{0}\geqslant 1\}}q_{N}\Big\{f(\tau_{1}(\omega^{0,-1}),-\omega_{-1}-2)\mathbf{1}_{\{x=0\}}+f(\omega^{0,-1},x+1)\mathbf{1}_{\{x<0\}}-f(\omega,x)\Big\}

where
\mathscr{L}^{\mbox{\tiny{zr}},\star}_{N}
is the zero-range generator defined in (
3.4
), except that all jumps to and from the origin are suppressed, and
(\tau_{x}\omega)_{y}=\omega_{y-x}
is the configuration translated by
x
. Note that the contribution of
\mathscr{L}^{\mbox{\tiny{zr}},\star}_{N}
does not affect the position of
x
, since the tagged empty site in the exclusion configuration only changes when an
\omega
-particle jumps over the edge
(-1,0)
, or when an
\omega
-particle jumps from site
0
to site
1
and an empty site is at the origin in
\eta
.

By applying the mapping back and forth, it is then straightforward to show that, for any function
f(\omega)
of the zero-range configuration,

\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}}(\widehat{\mathscr{L}}^{\mbox{\tiny{zr}}}_{N}f)=0,

so that the distribution
\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}
is invariant w.r.t. the generator
\widehat{\mathscr{L}}^{\mbox{\tiny{zr}}}_{N}
on
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
However, projected on
\omega
,
\widehat{\mathscr{L}}^{\mbox{\tiny{zr}}}_{N}
is not the generator of the constant rate zero-range process, but rather the modified generator

\widehat{\mathscr{L}}^{\mbox{\tiny{zr}}}_{N}f(\omega)=\mathscr{L}^{\mbox{\tiny{zr}}}_{N}f(\omega)\\
+\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{0}\geqslant 1,\;x=-\omega_{0}-1\}}p_{N}\Big\{f(\tau_{-1}(\omega^{0,1}))\mathbf{1}_{\{x=-\omega_{0}-1\}}-f(\omega^{0,1})\Big\}+\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{0}\geqslant 1,\;x=0\}}q_{N}\Big\{f(\tau_{1}(\omega^{0,-1}))-f(\omega^{0,-1})\Big\},

where the two last terms account for the configuration translation when the origin’s cluster changes because an empty site jumped over the exclusion edge
(0,1)
. Note that obviously, the generator above is not a Markov generator on the set of zero-range configurations
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{Z}}
, since it is defined in parts by the external variable
x
. This identity shows why the mapped stationary distribution
\widehat{\mu}_{\rho}
is
not
invariant w.r.t. the generator of the constant rate zero-range process.
