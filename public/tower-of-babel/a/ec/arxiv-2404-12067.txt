Subordinators and generalized heat kernels: Random time change and long time dynamics
N. Ajaber, A. Alshehri, H. Altamimi, M. Majdoub, E. Mliki

Abstract
. This paper focuses on studying the long-time dynamics of the subordination process for a range of linear evolution equations, with a special emphasis on the fractional heat equation. By treating inverse subordinators as random time variables and employing the subordination principle to solve forward Kolmogorov equations, we explore the behavior of the solutions over extended periods. We provide a detailed description of the specific classes of subordinators suitable for conducting asymptotic analysis. Our findings not only extend existing research, but also enhance the results previously presented in [ 9 , 10 ] .

35R11, 35B40, 40E05

1.
Introduction

This paper aims to investigate the long-term behavior of solutions for a specific class of linear evolution equations that emerge from random time changes utilizing various subordinators, such as
gamma
and
alpha
stable subordinators. We explore how these subordinators, denoted as
\mathbf{S}=\left\{\mathbf{S}_{t}\,;t\geq 0\right\}
, influence the dynamics of the solutions over extended time periods.

More specifically, our study focuses on examining the behavior of the subordinated solution, denoted as
v(x,t)
, for some evolution partial differential equations (PDEs) as time progresses. We analyze the large-time dynamics of the subordination process by convolving the solution with the density function
G_{t}
associated with the subordinator
\mathbf{S}
. This is accomplished through the analysis of the Cesàro mean of the subordinated solution. By examining the Cesàro mean, we gain insights into the long-term behavior and dynamics of the subordination process.

Understanding the behavior of the subordination process is of considerable significance in various physical models. Specifically, it has the potential to contribute to the development of valuable models that capture the concept of biological time in the evolution of species and ecological systems. By gaining insights into this process, we can enhance our understanding of complex biological phenomena and improve our ability to analyze ecological dynamics. This understanding can pave the way for advances in fields such as population dynamics, epidemiology, and ecological modeling, ultimately leading to more accurate predictions and informed decision making in real-world scenarios. For further explanations and detailed discussions on the topics covered, see
[
14
,
16
]
.

Let us explain now the mathematical framework of the problem. Consider the linear evolution equation

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial_{t}v(x,t)&=\mathcal{A}\,v(x,t)\quad\mbox{ in }\quad\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,\infty),\\
v(x,0)&=\varphi(x)\quad\mbox{ in }\quad\mathbb{R}^{N},\end{array}\right.

where
\mathcal{A}
is a Markov generator that acts on the functions
v(x,t)
. Assuming that
(
1.1
)
has a solution
v
such that
v(x,\cdot)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, we introduce the subordination of
v(x,t)
by a density function
G_{t}(\tau)
as follows:

(1.2)

v^{E}(x,t):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\,v(x,\tau)G_{t}(\tau)\,d\tau,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\;t\geq 0.

According to
[
3
]
, we know that
v^{E}(x,t)
is the solution of the general fractional differential equation

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{matrix}\left(\mathbb{D}^{(k)}_{t}v^{E}\right)(x,t)=\mathcal{A}\,v^{E}(x,t)\\
v^{E}(x,0)=\varphi(x).\end{matrix}\right.

Here
\mathbb{D}^{(k)}_{t}
is the differential-convolution operator given by

(1.4)

\left(\mathbb{D}^{(k)}_{t}g\right)(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{t}\,\mathbf{k}(t-s)\left(g(s)-g(0)\right)\,ds,

where
\mathbf{k}\in L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is a non-negative kernel.

The main focus of this paper is to characterize the specific classes of subordinators that facilitate the derivation of time asymptotics for generalized fractional dynamics. In particular, our objective is to investigate the long-term behavior of
v^{E}(x,t)
, the solution of interest. To achieve this, we introduce the concept of Cesàro mean for
v^{E}(x,t)
:

(1.5)

\mathbf{M}_{t}(v^{E}(x,t)):=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\,v^{E}(x,s)\,ds.

Clearly, from (
1.2
) one can see that

(1.6)

\mathbf{M}_{t}(v^{E}(x,t))=\int_{0}^{\infty}\,v(x,\tau)\mathbf{M}_{t}\left(G_{t}(\tau)\right)\,d\tau.

Our approach in this study is grounded in Laplace transform techniques, the Feller-Karamata-Tauberian theorem, and the integrability of the solution over time
v(x,t)
. This method can be applied to various models, including the fractional heat equation. In particular, our approach yields satisfactory results for dimensions
N\geq 2
. However, for the one-dimensional case, the obtained result is somewhat weaker. For further investigations and related studies, we recommend referring to
[
9
,
10
]
.

We consider a broad class of admissible kernels, denoted
0\leq\mathbf{k}\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, which are characterized in terms of their Laplace transforms
\mathcal{K}(\lambda)
as follows.

(1.7)

\lambda\mathcal{K}(\lambda)\underset{\lambda\to 0^{+}}{\longrightarrow}\,0,

and, for some
\varrho\geq 0
,

(1.8)

L(x):=x^{-\varrho}\,\mathcal{K}\left(x^{-1}\right)\quad\mbox{is a slowly varying function}.

An example that is well known and commonly used is given by

(1.9)

\mathbf{k}(s)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{s^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\mu(\alpha)d\alpha,

where
\mu:[0,1]\to(0,\infty)
is a continuous function. For further properties and asymptotic behavior of kernels defined by (
1.9
), we refer the reader to
[
11
]
. In particular,
[
11
]
provides detailed information on the Laplace transform of
\mathbf{k}
as follows:

(1.10)

\mathcal{K}(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{1}\lambda^{\alpha-1}\mu(\alpha)\,d\alpha.

In this case, it is worth noting that the slowly varying function
L
is defined as follows:

(1.11)

L(x)=\int_{0}^{1}x^{-\alpha}\mu(\alpha)d\alpha.

Below, we provide a list of fundamental examples of kernel classes that will be utilized in our analysis.

1
)

The
\theta
-stable subordinator class (
{\bf C}_{1}
):

\mathcal{K}(\lambda)=\lambda^{\theta-1},\quad 0<\theta<1.

2
)

The distributed order derivatives class (
{\bf C}_{2}
):

\mathcal{K}(\lambda)\;\underset{\lambda\to 0^{+}}{\sim}\;C\lambda^{-1}\left(-\ln\lambda\right)^{-\kappa},\quad C,\kappa>0.

3
)

The inverse Gamma subordinator class (
{\bf C}_{3}
):

\mathcal{K}(\lambda)=\frac{\sqrt{b}}{\lambda}\left(2\sqrt{2\lambda+a}-\sqrt{a}\right),\quad a\geq 0,\;\;b>0.

4
)

The Gamma subordinator class (
{\bf C}_{4}
):

\mathcal{K}(\lambda)=\frac{a}{\lambda}\ln\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{b}\right),\quad a,b>0.

5
)

The tempered stable subordinator class (
{\bf C}_{5}
):

\mathcal{K}(\lambda)=\frac{(\lambda+\beta)^{\theta}-\beta^{\theta}}{\lambda},\quad\beta>0.

Remark 1.1
.

(i)

All classes (
{\bf C}_{i}
),
1\leq i\leq 5
, satisfy assumption (
1.7
) except for (
{\bf C}_{3}
) with
a>0
. It is worth noting that when
a=0
in (
{\bf C}_{3}
), we obtain the class (
{\bf C}_{1}
) as a special case with
\theta=1/2
.

(ii)

The hypothesis (
1.8
) holds for all classes (
{\bf C}_{i}
),
1\leq i\leq 5
, with specific values of
\varrho
. For (
{\bf C}_{1}
), we have
\varrho=1-\theta
and
L(x)=1
, for (
{\bf C}_{2}
), we have
\varrho=1
and
L(x)=C\left(\ln x\right)^{-\kappa}
, for (
{\bf C}_{3}
) we have
\varrho=1
and
L(x)=\sqrt{b}\left(2\sqrt{2/x+a}-\sqrt{a}\right)
, for (
{\bf C}_{4}
), we have
\varrho=0
and
L(x)=ax\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{bx}\right)
and for (
{\bf C}_{5}
), we have
\varrho=0
and
L(x)=x\left(\left(1/x+\beta\right)^{\theta}-\beta^{\theta}\right)
.

Henceforth, we will consistently represent the Lebesgue norm in
L^{p}
, applicable to both space and time variables, as
\|\cdot\|_{p}
for
1\leq p\leq\infty
.
The following statement presents our first main result:

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that the Markov generator
\mathcal{A}
satisfies the condition that for any
0\leq\varphi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, the solution
v
of (
1.1
) is non-negative, and
v(x,\cdot)\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
.
Moreover, if we assume that the conditions (
1.7
) and (
1.8
) are fulfilled, then:

(1.12)

\mathbf{M}_{t}(v^{E}(x,t))\,\underset{t\to\infty}{\backsim}\,\frac{\|v(x)\|_{1}}{\Gamma(\varrho+1)}\,\bigg(t^{-1}\mathcal{K}\left(t^{-1}\right)\bigg).

Here,
v^{E}(x,t)
represents the subordination of the solution
v(x,t)
as defined in (
1.2
).

Remark 1.2
.

(i)

The asymptotic behavior result, stated as (
1.12
), can be alternatively expressed as

(1.13)

\mathbf{M}_{t}(v^{E}(x,t))\,\underset{t\to\infty}{\backsim}\,\frac{\|v(x)\|_{1}}{\Gamma(\varrho+1)}\,\left(\int_{0}^{1}t^{-\alpha}\mu(\alpha)d\alpha\right),

where the function
\mu
is given by (
1.9
).

(ii)

If we assume
\lambda\mathcal{K}(\lambda)\underset{\lambda\to 0^{+}}{\longrightarrow}\,\ell>0
instead of (
1.7
), then the requirement
v(x,\cdot)\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
can be omitted. In that case, the conclusion (
1.12
) remains valid, provided that
\|v(x)\|_{1}
is replaced with

\|v(x)\|_{1,\ell}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\,{\rm e}^{-\ell\tau}\,v(x,\tau)\,d\tau.

(iii)

Our primary contribution is encapsulated in Theorem
1.1
, where we employ a unified approach to investigate a broad class of operators and kernels.

For specific choices of kernel classes, Theorem
1.1
provides the following asymptotic estimates.

Corollary 1.1
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1

(
except for class
(
\mathbf{C}_{3}
)
where the assumption
v(x,\cdot)\in L^{1}
is omitted
)
, the long-time behavior of the Cesàro mean of
v^{E}(x,t)
as

is expressed as:

(1.14)

\begin{split}\mathbf{M}_{t}(v^{E}(x,t))\,\underset{t\to\infty}{\backsim}\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}\frac{\|v(x)\|_{1}}{\Gamma(2-\theta)}\,t^{-\theta}&\text{if}\quad\mathbf{k}\in\;(\mathbf{C}_{1})\\
\mathtt{C}\,\|v(x)\|_{1}\,\left(\ln t\right)^{-\kappa}&\text{if}\quad\mathbf{k}\in\;(\mathbf{C}_{2})\\
2\sqrt{ab}\,\|v(x)\|_{1,\sqrt{ab}}&\text{if}\quad\mathbf{k}\in\;(\mathbf{C}_{3})\\

2.2.
Subordinators

A subordinator is a process with stationary and independent non-negative increments starting at zero.
Subordinators are a special class of Lévy processes taking values in
[0,\infty)
and their sample
paths are non-decreasing; this is a type of stochastic process that is used to model random phenomena that have jumps (see
[
4
,
5
]
for more details). Let
\mathbf{S}=\left\{\mathbf{S}_{t},\;t\geq 0\right\}
be a subordinator. The infinite divisibility of the law of
\mathbf{S}
implies that
its Laplace transform can be expressed in the form

\displaystyle E(e^{-\lambda\mathbf{S}_{t}})=e^{-t\Phi(\lambda)}=e^{-t\lambda\mathcal{K}(\lambda)},\quad\lambda\geq 0,

where
\Phi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
, called the Laplace exponent, is
a Bernstein function
[
17
]
.
Such a Laplace exponent
\Phi
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Phi(\lambda)=\int_{(0,\infty)}(1-e^{-\lambda\tau})\,\sigma(d\tau)<\infty,

which is known as the Lévy-Khintchine formula for the subordinator
\mathbf{S}
. Where
\sigma
are a measure on the positive real half-line such that

\displaystyle\int_{(0,\infty)}(1\land\tau)\,\sigma(d\tau)<\infty.

The kernel
\mathbf{k}
is related to the subordinator
\mathbf{S}
via the Lévy measure
\sigma
, namely if we set

\mathbf{k}(t)=\sigma((t,\infty)),\quad t\geq 0.

For any
\lambda\geq 0
we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}d\sigma(s)\,ds=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{s}e^{-\lambda t}dt\,d\sigma(s)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\Phi(\lambda)=\mathcal{K}(\lambda).

Denote by
E
the inverse process of the subordinator
\mathbf{S}
,

E_{t}:=\mbox{inf}\{s\geq 0;\,\mathbf{S}_{t}\geq t\}.

The marginal density of
E_{t}
is the function
G_{t}(\tau)
, more precisely

G_{t}(\tau)\,d\tau=\partial_{\tau}P(E_{t}\leq\tau)=\partial P(\mathbf{S}\geq t).

2.2.1.
\theta
-stable subordinator

Let
\mathbf{k}
be the kernal corresponding to the Caputo-Djrbashian fractional derivative
\mathbb{D}^{(\theta)}_{t}
of order
\theta\in(0,1).
Then its Laplace transform is given by

\mathcal{K}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\theta)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,{\rm e}^{-\lambda t}\,t^{-\theta}\,dt=\lambda^{\theta-1},

where
\mathbf{k}(t)=\frac{t^{-\theta}}{\Gamma(1-\theta)}.

It is easy to verify that (
1.7
) holds for
\mathcal{K}.

2.2.2.
Gamma Subordinator

Let
\mathbf{k}
be the kernal given by

\mathbf{k}(t)=a\Gamma(0,bt),\quad a,b>0,

where
\Gamma(\nu,x):\displaystyle{\int_{x}^{\infty}t^{\nu-1}e^{-t}\,dt}
is the upper incomplete Gamma function.
The Laplace transform of
\mathbf{k}
is given by

\mathcal{K}(\lambda)=\frac{a}{\lambda}\ln\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{b}\right),\;\;a,b,\lambda>0.

It is easy to see that (
1.7
) holds for
\mathcal{K}.

2.2.3.
Inverse Gamma Subordinator

Let
a\geq 0
and
b>0
be given and definite the kernel
\mathbf{k}
by

\mathbf{k}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{b}{2\pi}}\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{t}}e^{-\frac{at}{2}}-\sqrt{2a\pi}(1-\mbox{erf}(z))\right),\quad z:=\sqrt{\frac{at}{2}},

where
\mbox{erf}(z):=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{z}e^{-t^{2}}\,dt
is the error function.
The Laplace transform of
\mathbf{k}
is given by

\mathcal{K}(\lambda)=\frac{\sqrt{b}}{\lambda}\left(2\sqrt{2\lambda+a}-\sqrt{a}\right),\quad a\geq 0,\;\;b,\lambda>0.

The assumption (
1.7
) holds for
\mathcal{K}
when the value
0
in (
1.7
) is replaced with
\sqrt{ab}
.

2.2.4.
Tempered Stable Subordinator

Let us consider a tempered stable subordinator
D_{\beta}(t)
with index
\theta\in(0,1)
. The density function of
D_{\beta}(t)
can be expressed as:

f_{\beta}(x,t)={\rm e}^{-\beta\,x+\beta^{\theta}t}\,f(x,t),\quad\beta>0,

where
\beta>0
and
f(x,t)
represents the density of the
\theta
-stable subordinator. It is worth noting that
f_{\beta}(x,t)
possesses finite moments and exhibits infinite divisibility. However, it is not self-similar.

Moreover, we can obtain the Laplace transform of the density function
f_{\beta}(x,t)
, given by:

\mathcal{L}_{x}\left(f_{\beta}(x,t)\right)={\rm e}^{-t\left((\lambda+\beta)^{\theta}-\beta^{\theta}\right)}={\rm e}^{-t\lambda\mathcal{K}(\lambda)}.

For further details and explanations, refer to
[
12
,
15
]
.

2.3.
Fractional Heat Semigroup

Consider the linear homogeneous fractional heat equation

(2.16)

\partial_{t}v+(-\Delta)^{\mathsf{s}}v=0,\quad v(0)=\varphi.

It is well known that the operator
(-\Delta)^{\mathsf{s}}
,
\mathsf{s}>0
serves as the generator of a semi-group
e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\mathsf{s}}}
, whose kernel
\mathscr{E}_{\mathsf{s}}
is smooth, radial, and adheres to the scaling property:

(2.17)

\mathscr{E}_{\mathsf{s}}(x,t)=t^{-\frac{N}{2\mathsf{s}}}\mathscr{E}_{\mathsf{s}}(t^{-\frac{1}{2\mathsf{s}}}x,1):=t^{-\frac{N}{2\mathsf{s}}}\mathscr{K}_{\mathsf{s}}\left(t^{-\frac{1}{2\mathsf{s}}}x\right),

where

(2.18)

\mathscr{K}_{\mathsf{s}}(x)=(2\pi)^{-N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\,e^{ix\cdot\xi}\,e^{-|\xi|^{2\mathsf{s}}}\,d\xi.

Therefore, the solution to (
2.16
) can be formally obtained through convolution as

(2.19)

v(x,t)=\left(e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\mathsf{s}}}\right)\varphi(x)=\bigg(t^{-\frac{N}{2\mathsf{s}}}\mathscr{K}_{\mathsf{s}}\left(t^{-\frac{1}{2\mathsf{s}}}\cdot\right)\ast\varphi\bigg)(x),

whenever this representation is meaningful. The explicit expression of the kernel
\mathscr{E}_{\mathsf{s}}
is known for particular cases
\mathsf{s}=1
and
\mathsf{s}=1/2
. When
\mathsf{s}=1
we obtain the standard Gaussian kernel:

(2.20)

\mathscr{E}_{1}(x,t)=(4\pi t)^{-N/2}\,{\rm e}^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4t}},

with profile
\mathscr{K}_{1}(x)=(4\pi)^{-N/2}\,{\rm e}^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4}}.
For
\mathsf{s}=1/2
, we get the well known Poisson kernel

(2.21)

\mathscr{E}_{1/2}(x,t)=\frac{\Gamma(N+1/{2})\,t}{\pi^{\frac{2N+1}{2}}\big(t^{2}+|x|^{2}\big)^{\frac{N+1}{2}}},

with profile
\mathscr{K}_{1/2}(x)=\frac{\Gamma(N+1/{2})}{\pi^{\frac{2N+1}{2}}\big(1+|x|^{2}\big)^{\frac{N+1}{2}}}
. Here, the symbol
\Gamma
stands for the Gamma function.

While the explicit form of
\mathscr{K}_{\mathsf{s}}
is not known for every
\mathsf{s}\in(0,1)
, we do have a positivity property, as stated below.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
N\geq 1
and
\mathsf{s}\in(0,1)
. Then for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we have

(2.22)

{c_{\mathsf{s}}}\,\left(1+|x|\right)^{-N-2\mathsf{s}}\,\leq\,\mathscr{K}_{\mathsf{s}}(x)\leq C_{\mathsf{s}}\,\left(1+|x|\right)^{-N-2\mathsf{s}},

where
c_{\mathsf{s}},\,C_{\mathsf{s}}
are positive constant depending only on
N
and
\mathsf{s}
.
As a result, we get
\mathscr{K}_{\mathsf{s}}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for any
1\leq p\leq\infty
.

The proof of Lemma
2.1
can be found, for instance, in
[
1
, p. 395]
. See also
[
7
, Theorem 2.1, p. 263]
. It’s worth noting that the crux of the proof relies on the formula

(2.23)

\mathscr{K}_{\mathsf{s}}(x)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\,(4\pi t)^{-N/2}\,{\rm e}^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4t}}\chi(t)\,dt,

where
\chi(t)
represents the inverse Laplace transform of
0<\lambda\mapsto{\rm e}^{-\lambda^{\mathsf{s}}}
. It is noteworthy that the positivity of the kernel
\mathscr{K}_{\mathsf{s}}
was asserted in
[
7
, p. 263]
without providing a proof.

Remark 2.1
.

As indicated by
[
7
, Theorem 2.1]
, the constant
c_{\mathsf{s}}
in the lower bound of (
2.22
) approaches
0
as
\mathsf{s}
approaches
1
. Specifically, the lower bound estimate in (
2.22
) becomes invalid when
\mathsf{s}=1
due to the exponential decay of
\mathscr{E}_{\mathsf{s}}(\cdot,t)
.

By using (
2.17
), (
2.19
), and (
2.22
), we can easily establish both a lower and an upper bound for
v(x,t)
as follows.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
N\geq 1
,
\mathsf{s}\in(0,1)
and
0\leq\varphi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. For any compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, there exists a constant
C=C(K,\varphi,N)>1
, such that

(2.24)

\frac{1}{C}t^{-\frac{N}{2\mathsf{s}}}\,\leq\,v(x,t)\,\leq\,{C}t^{-\frac{N}{2\mathsf{s}}},\quad t\geq 1,\,\,x\in K.

By utilizing (
2.19
), Lemma
2.1
, and Young’s inequality, we readily derive the subsequent
L^{p}-L^{q}
estimate.

Proposition 2.2
.

For
N\geq 1
and
0<\mathsf{s}<1
, there exists a positive constant
C=C(N,\mathsf{s})
such that for all
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty
, the following inequality holds:

(2.25)

\|e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\mathsf{s}}}\varphi\|_{q}\leq C\,t^{-\frac{N}{2\mathsf{s}}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}\,\|\varphi\|_{p},\quad t>0,\;\;\varphi\in L^{p}.

By exploiting the property that
e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\mathsf{s}}}
forms a
C_{0}
-semigroup, we derive a result analogous to the one established in
[
13
, Lemma 2.1]
for
\mathsf{s}=1
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose
0<\mathsf{s}<1
,
1\leq q<r\leq\infty
, and let
\delta=\frac{N}{2\mathsf{s}}\left(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{r}\right)
. If
\varphi\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, then the following holds:

(2.26)

\lim_{t\to 0}\,t^{\delta}\,\left\|e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\mathsf{s}}}\varphi\right\|_{L^{r}}=0.

The asymptotic behavior of the solution
v

Theorem 2.1
.

[

Consider

2.4.
Karamata’s Tauberian theorem

The Feller-Karamata Tauberian theorem is a powerful result in mathematical analysis that establishes a connection between the behavior of a function and its Laplace transform. See
[
6
, Sec. 1.7]
and
[
8
, XIII, Sec. 1.5]
for a general statement. Below, we present a simplified version of the Feller-Karamata Tauberian theorem that adequately serves our purpose.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
U:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a monotone non-decreasing right-continuous function satisfying
\mathcal{L}(U)(\lambda)<\infty
for all
\lambda>0
. Then, the following assertions are equivalent:

(2.29)

U(t)\sim\frac{Ct^{\rho}}{\Gamma(\rho+1)}L(t)\quad\text{as}\quad t\to\infty,

(2.30)

\mathcal{L}(U)(\lambda)\sim C\lambda^{-1-\rho}L\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\quad\text{as}\quad\lambda\to 0^{+}.

Here,
C>0
,
\rho\geq 0
, and
L
represents a slowly varying function.

Remark 2.2
.

(i)

In essence, Theorem
2.2
establishes a direct connection between the asymptotic tendencies of
U(t)
as
t
approaches infinity and the corresponding behavior of its Laplace transform as
\lambda
tends to
0
. The constants
C
,
\rho
, and the slowly varying function
L
capture the precise quantitative relationship between the two asymptotic behaviors.

(ii)

The theorem’s validity can be confirmed for the function
U(t)=\frac{t^{\rho}}{\Gamma(\rho+1)}
, where
\rho\geq 0
, resulting in
L(t)=1
a clearly slowly varying function. By choosing
U(t)=\frac{t^{\rho}}{\Gamma(\rho+1)}
it becomes evident that
\mathcal{L}(U)(\lambda)=\lambda^{-1-\rho}
.

The following asymptotic behavior of integral uses the incomplete Gamma function and it is needed in the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Consider
0<\theta\leq 1
, and define

\mathbf{F}(\varepsilon)=\int_{1}^{\infty}\,\tau^{-\theta}{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\tau}\,d\tau,\quad\varepsilon>0.

Then, the following asymptotic holds

(2.31)

\mathbf{F}(\varepsilon)\,\sim\,\varepsilon^{\theta-1}\,\Gamma(1-\theta)\quad\text{as}\quad\varepsilon\to 0.

3.
Proof of the main result

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
. First, recall a crucial relation between the Laplace transform of the kernel
k

and the density
G_{t}(\tau)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The
t-
Laplace transform of
G_{t}(\tau)
is given by

(3.32)

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-\lambda t}G_{t}(\tau)\,dt=\mathcal{K}(\lambda){\rm e}^{-\tau\lambda\mathcal{K}(\lambda)},

where
\mathcal{K}(\lambda)
is the Laplace transform of the kernel
k
.

The proof of the above Lemma can be found in
[
9
, Lemma 2.2]
for instance.

Let
0\leq\varphi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, and
v
the non-negative solution of (
1.1
). It follows that the subordination
v^{E}
of
v
by the density
G_{t}(\tau)
given by (
1.2
) is also non-negative. Let us define

(3.33)

\mathbf{U}(t)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\,v^{E}(x,s)\,ds=t\mathbf{M}_{t}(v^{E}(x,s)).

Here,
\mathbf{M}_{t}
is the Cesàro mean given by (
1.6
).
By using the formula (
3.32
), we obtain that

(3.34)

\frac{\lambda\,w(\lambda)}{\mathcal{K}(\lambda)}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-\tau\lambda\mathcal{K}(\lambda)}v(x,\tau)\,d\tau,

where
w(\lambda):=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{U})(\lambda)
denotes the Laplace transform of
\mathbf{U}
.

Thanks to (
1.7
) and the fact that
v(x,\cdot)\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
, the Dominate Convergence Theorem yields

(3.35)

\underset{\lambda\to 0^{+}}{\lim}\left(\frac{\lambda w(\lambda)}{\mathcal{K}(\lambda)}\right)=\int_{0}^{\infty}v(x,\tau)\,d\tau=\|v(x)\|_{1}.

Hence,

(3.36)

w(\lambda)\,\underset{\lambda\to 0^{+}}{\sim}\,\|v(x)\|_{1}\,\frac{\mathcal{K}(\lambda)}{\lambda}.

Referring to (
1.8
), the above asymptotic formula (
3.36
) can be expressed alternatively as

(3.37)

w(\lambda)\,\underset{\lambda\to 0^{+}}{\sim}\,\|v(x)\|_{1}\,\lambda^{-1-\varrho}\,L\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right).

Here, as stated in (
1.8
),
\varrho\geq 0
and
L
is a slowly varying function (SVF) at infinity.
Due to the non-negativity of
v^{E}
, we see that
\mathbf{U}
is a continuous non-decreasing function. Hence, we can apply Feller-Karamata-Tauberian theorem and obtain that

(3.38)

\mathbf{M}_{t}(v^{E}(x,t))\,\underset{t\to\infty}{\sim}\,\frac{\|v(x)\|_{1}}{\Gamma(\varrho+1)}t^{\varrho-1}L(t).

From the hypotheses (
1.8
) we easily derive (
1.12
). This finishes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

3.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

The proof of Theorem
1.3
relies on the utilization of the incomplete Gamma function (Lemma
2.2
) and Proposition
2.24
. By employing Lemma
2.2
, Proposition
2.24
, and decomposing the integral as follows:

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-\tau\lambda\mathcal{K}(\lambda)}v(x,\tau)\,d\tau=\int_{0}^{1}{\rm e}^{-\tau\lambda\mathcal{K}(\lambda)}v(x,\tau)\,d\tau+\int_{1}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-\tau\lambda\mathcal{K}(\lambda)}v(x,\tau)\,d\tau,

(3.41)

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-\tau\lambda\mathcal{K}(\lambda)}v(x,\tau)\,d\tau\,\asymp\,\left(\lambda\mathcal{K}(\lambda)\right)^{\frac{1-2\mathsf{s}}{2\mathsf{s}}}\Gamma\left(\frac{2\mathsf{s}-1}{2\mathsf{s}}\right),

w(\lambda)\,\asymp\,\lambda^{\frac{1}{2\mathsf{s}}-2}\,\left(\mathcal{K}(\lambda)\right)^{\frac{1}{2\mathsf{s}}}.

Now, using (

(3.43)

where

(3.44)

\mathbf{U}(t)\asymp\frac{t^{1+\frac{\varrho-1}{2s}}}{\Gamma(1+\varrho)}\,\left(L(t)\right)^{\frac{1}{2\mathsf{s}}}.

The proof of Theorem

U(t)=t\mathbf{M}_{t}(v^{E}(x,t))
