Persistence of Morse decompositions over grid resolution for maps and time series Thanks: This work was supported by a grant from the Simons Foundation (282398, JW)
Jim Wiseman

Abstract
. We can approximate a continuous map f : X → X f:X\to X of a compact metric space by discretizing the space into a grid. Through either the map itself or a time series, f f induces a multivalued grid map ℱ {\mathcal{F}} . The dynamical properties of ℱ {\mathcal{F}} depend on the resolution of the grid, and we study the persistence of these properties as we change the resolution. In particular, we look at the persistence of Morse decompositions, at both the global (Morse graph) and local (individual Morse set) levels, using several notions of persistence – graph structure, persistent homology, and mixing properties.

1.
Introduction

In order to understand the dynamics of a continuous map
f:X\to X
of a compact metric space, we can create a finite discretization of the space, then use a computer to create a multivalued map
{\mathcal{F}}
on the discretization to approximate
f
.
The relationship between the dynamics of
f
and those of
{\mathcal{F}}
is an active area of research (see, for example,
[
43
,
42
,
30
,
1
,
33
,
26
,
27
,
41
]
), and as the computational technology improves, understanding this relationship becomes increasingly important.
This is true whether the map
{\mathcal{F}}
is constructed directly, from the action of
f
on the elements of the discretization, or indirectly, from a sample of the dynamics (a time series).

We will discretize the space using a grid (defined in Section
2
). The behavior of the map
{\mathcal{F}}
depends on the resolution of the grid, perhaps especially in the case of maps reconstructed from time series. This is discussed in
[
10
]
: too coarse a grid will give only a very rough approximation of
f
, while too fine a grid can isolate each individual data point. As stated there, it is an interesting problem to understand this dependence of the dynamics on grid resolution in the spirit of persistent topology, as in
[
22
,
13
,
20
]
.

This paper studies persistence of Morse decompositions as a step in that direction. A Morse decomposition (
[
15
]
) is a collection of invariant sets of recurrent points (Morse sets), such that all other points in the space move from one Morse set to another. This notion is very useful in computational dynamics (see, for example,
[
30
,
5
,
10
,
8
,
9
,
18
,
25
,
31
,
38
]
). The persistent homology of Morse sets has been studied in
[
47
]
(zero-dimensional persistence for vector fields) and more generally in
[
20
]
. In
[
20
]
, the authors use Alexandrov topology to study the persistent homology of the Morse sets (where the persistence can be over different kinds of parameters). In this paper we take a different approach, looking at the Morse decomposition more broadly, including the connections between the different Morse sets (the Morse graph) and the dynamics on the individual Morse sets.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we discuss grids on
X
and the multivalued grid maps induced by the map
f:X\to X
. We define recurrence and Morse decompositions in Section
3
, and define and prove persistence for grid maps and Morse decompositions in Section
4
. We discuss Morse graphs and persistence of the global dynamics in Section
5
, and local persistence of individual Morse sets in Section
6
. Finally, in Section
7
, we apply our results to grid maps generated by time series.

3.
Morse decompositions

There are many notions of recurrence for dynamical systems (
[
1
,
3
]
), but recurrence is simpler for maps
{\mathcal{F}}:\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{G}
. We say that a grid element
G
is
recurrent
if there is a nontrivial orbit from
G
to itself. Grid elements
G
and
H
are equivalent if there are orbits from
G
to
H
and from
H
to
G
. This gives an equivalence relation on the recurrent set. The equivalence classes correspond exactly to the nontrivial strongly connected components of the graph
{\mathcal{F}}
.

Definition 3.1

(
[
39
]
)
.

A
Morse decomposition

{\mathcal{M}}
for a closed multivalued map
{\mathcal{F}}:\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{G}
is a collection of invariant sets
{\mathcal{S}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
, called Morse sets, with a partial order
\succeq
such that for any bi-infinite orbit
\{G_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
, either there exists an
{\mathcal{S}}_{j}
such that
G_{i}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{j}
for all
i
, or there exist integers
i^{-}<i^{+}
and Morse sets
{\mathcal{S}}_{j}\succ{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
such that
G_{i}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{j}
for all
i\leq i^{-}
and
G_{i}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
for all
i\geq i^{+}
.
That is, every complete orbit either is contained in one of the Morse sets, or begins in one Morse set and ends in another, lower Morse set.

The equivalence classes of the recurrent set (the strongly connected components of
{\mathcal{F}}
) are the Morse sets of the finest Morse decomposition
{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{G}}
(
[
20
]
*Thm. 4.1).
The partial order
\succeq
is given by
{\mathcal{S}}_{j}\succeq{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
if there is an orbit going from
{\mathcal{S}}_{j}
to
{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
. We observe that in this case, if there is an orbit from
{\mathcal{S}}_{j}
to
{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
and an orbit from
{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
to
{\mathcal{S}}_{l}
, then there is an orbit from
{\mathcal{S}}_{j}
to
{\mathcal{S}}_{l}
, because there are orbits between any two points within a Morse set. This differs from the case of Morse decompositions for maps, where there can be an orbit from one Morse set to another, and from the second to a third, but not from the first to the third.

on each set as well. On each
{\mathcal{S}}_{j}
,
{\mathcal{F}}
induces the restriction map
{\mathcal{F}}_{j}:{\mathcal{S}}_{j}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{S}}_{j}

{\mathcal{F}}_{j}
is the induced subgraph of
{\mathcal{F}}
on the vertices in
{\mathcal{S}}_{j}

We define

, the
augmented Morse decomposition
, to be the Morse decomposition along with the induced maps,

{\mathfrak{M}}=({\mathcal{M}},\{{\mathcal{F}}_{j}:{\mathcal{S}}_{j}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{S}}_{j}\})=(\{{\mathcal{S}}_{j}\},\succeq,\{{\mathcal{F}}_{j}:{\mathcal{S}}_{j}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{S}}_{j}\}).

4.
Persistence

Let
\{\mathcal{G}_{k}\}
be a collection of grids for
X
, partially ordered by refinement. Our goal is to show that this collection gives persistence of augmented Morse decompositions, in the following sense, which we will make precise.

For a given grid
\mathcal{G}_{k}
, with associated minimal multivalued map
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k}}
, denote by

{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k}}=({\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k}},\{{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{j}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{j}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{j}\})=(\{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{j}\},\succeq^{\mathcal{G}_{k}},\{{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{j}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{j}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{j}\})

the corresponding finest augmented
Morse decomposition, arising from the strongly connected components of
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k}}
. Then for every
\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}
, there is an appropriately defined morphism
{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}}:{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}}\to{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}}
that respects the Morse sets, the partial order, and the induced maps. If
\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}
, then
{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}}\circ{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}}={\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}}
.

We will proceed as follows. It is straightforward to show that grid refinement gives persistence of the minimal multivalued maps associated to the grids. It then follows that the multivalued map persistence induces persistence of the augmented Morse decompositions. (There are somewhat analogous results, using covers instead of grids, in
[
43
]
.)

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
be grids, and
{\mathcal{F}}:\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{G}
and
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}:\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
be multivalued maps. A (single-valued) map
h:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
is a
grid map morphism
if
h({\mathcal{F}}(G))\subset{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}(h(G))
for all
G\in\mathcal{G}
. Equivalently,
h
is a directed graph homomorphism from
{\mathcal{F}}
to
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
f:X\to X
be a continuous map of a compact metric space, and let
\{\mathcal{G}_{k}\}
be a collection of grids for
X
, partially ordered by refinement. For each
k
, let
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k}}:\mathcal{G}_{k}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{G}_{k}
be the minimal multivalued map associated to
f
on
\mathcal{G}_{k}
.
Then for every
\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}
, there is a morphism
h_{k1}^{k2}:\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}\to\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}
, and if
\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}
, then
h_{k_{2}}^{k_{3}}\circ h_{k_{1}}^{k_{2}}=h_{k_{1}}^{k_{3}}
.

Proof.

Take grids
\mathcal{G}<\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
. Since
\mathcal{G}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, by definition for any
G\in\mathcal{G}
there is a
G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
such that
G\subset G^{\prime}
; define
h
by
h(G):=G^{\prime}
. If
H\in{\mathcal{F}}(G)
, then
H\cap f(G)\neq\emptyset
. Since
G\subset h(G)
and
H\subset h(H)
, we have
h(H)\cap f(h(G))\neq\emptyset
, so
h(H)\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}(h(G))
. Since
H
was an arbitrary element of
{\mathcal{F}}(G)
, we have
h({\mathcal{F}}(G))\subset{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}(h(G))
. That
h_{k_{2}}^{k_{3}}\circ h_{k_{1}}^{k_{2}}=h_{k_{1}}^{k_{3}}
follows from the definition; the composition of inclusions is inclusion.
∎

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
be grids, and
{\mathcal{F}}:\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{G}
and
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}:\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
multivalued maps. Let
{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{G}}=(\{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}\},\succeq^{\mathcal{G}})
and
{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}=(\{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}_{j}\},\succeq^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}})
be the corresponding finest Morse decompositions, arising from the strongly connected components of
{\mathcal{F}}
and
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
. A map
{\bar{h}}:\{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}\}\to\{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}_{j}\}
is a
Morse decomposition morphism
if
{\bar{h}}({\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j})\succeq^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}{\bar{h}}({\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{k})
whenever
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}\succeq^{\mathcal{G}}{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{k}
. Let
{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{G}},\{{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}\})
and
{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}=({\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}},\{{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}_{j}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}_{j}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}_{j}\})
be the corresponding augmented Morse decompositions. An
augmented Morse decomposition morphism

{\mathfrak{h}}:{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathcal{G}}\to{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}
is a pair
({\bar{h}},\{h_{j}\})
, where
{\bar{h}}
is a morphism between
{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{G}}
and
{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}
, and for each
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}
,
h_{j}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}\to{\bar{h}}({\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j})
is a grid map morphism.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\{\mathcal{G}_{k}\}
be a collection of grids, partially ordered by refinement, with maps
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k}}:\mathcal{G}_{k}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{G}_{k}
. Then persistence for grid maps induces persistence for the corresponding finest augmented Morse decompositions. That is, if for every
\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}
, there is a morphism
h_{k1}^{k2}:\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}\to\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}
such that
h_{k_{2}}^{k_{3}}\circ h_{k_{1}}^{k_{2}}=h_{k_{1}}^{k_{3}}
for any
\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}
, then for every
\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}
, there is a morphism
{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}}:{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}}\to{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}}
such that
{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}}\circ{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}}={\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}}
for any
\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}
.

Proof.

This is essentially the fact that directed graph homomorphisms preserve strong connectedness. Take grids
\mathcal{G}<\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
and maps
{\mathcal{F}}:\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{G}
and
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}:\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, with morphism
h:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
. Then any
{\mathcal{F}}
-orbit
(G_{0}=G,G_{1},\ldots,G_{n}=H)
from
G
to
H\in\mathcal{G}
gives an
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
-orbit
(h(G_{0})=h(G),h(G_{1}),\ldots,h(G_{n})=h(H))
from
h(G)
to
h(H)\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
. Thus if
G
is recurrent for
{\mathcal{F}}
, then
h(G)
is recurrent for
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
, and
if
G
and
H
are in the same Morse set
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}
, then
h(G)
and
h(H)
are in the same Morse set
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}_{j^{\prime}}
,
and we can define
{\bar{h}}
by
{\bar{h}}({\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}):={\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}_{j^{\prime}}
, where
h(G)\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}_{k}
for all
G\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}
. Furthermore, if there is an
{\mathcal{F}}
-orbit from

{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}
to
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{k}
, then there is an
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
-orbit from
{\bar{h}}({\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j})
to
{\bar{h}}({\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{k})
, and so
{\bar{h}}({\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j})\succeq^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}{\bar{h}}({\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{k})

whenever
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}\succeq^{\mathcal{G}}{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{k}
. Thus
{\bar{h}}
is a Morse decomposition morphism.

For each
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}
, we can now define
h_{j}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}\to{\bar{h}}({\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j})
as the restriction of
h
to
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}
.

The identity
{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}}\circ{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}}={\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}}
for any
\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}
follows from the definition.
∎

As an immediate corollary, we have persistence over grid refinement for the finest Morse decompositions.

.

Let

be a continuous map of a compact metric space, and let
\{\mathcal{G}_{k}\}
be a collection of grids for

, partially ordered by refinement. For each

, let
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k}}:\mathcal{G}_{k}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{G}_{k}
be the minimal multivalued map associated to

on
\mathcal{G}_{k}
. Then for every
\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}
, there is a morphism
{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}}:{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}}\to{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}}
such that
{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}}\circ{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}}={\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}}
for any
\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}

Remark 4.8
.

It may seem more natural to have morphisms going the other way, from the objects corresponding to the coarser grid to those corresponding to the finer grid, in particular so that we can take finer and finer grids. One obvious way to achieve this is to take cohomology instead when we look at homology in the following sections.

More generally,
there are essentially two reasons that we do not get grid map morphisms
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{G}
for
\mathcal{G}<\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
. First, a
G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
can correspond to multiple elements of
\mathcal{G}
, and second, an edge in
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
may not correspond to an edge in
\mathcal{G}
. We can address both of these issues by looking at a kind of dual graph to
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}}
,
({\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}})^{*}
. The vertices of
({\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}})^{*}
are the subsets of the vertices of
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}}
, and there is an edge from
\{G_{1},\ldots,G_{m}\}
to
\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}\}
if for all
i
and
j
there is no edge
G_{i}\to H_{j}
in
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}}
. Then there is a directed graph homomorphism
({\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}})^{*}\to({\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}})^{*}
for
\mathcal{G}<\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, which gives persistence, as discussed in this section.

A disadvantage of this approach is that the vertex set is very large, but we can take advantages of symmetries to greatly reduce the computational complexity.
The details of these dual graphs and the relation of their properties, such as Morse decompositions, to the properties of
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}}
will be the subject of future work.

5.1.
Morse graphs

For a given grid

5.3.
Persistent homology

The merge tree is an easy way to measure the changes in the Morse graphs, but it loses information about the gradient-like structure since it does not take into account the edges of the graphs. A simple way to do that is to count the cycles in the graph. (Note that while a Morse graph is acyclic as a directed graph, it may have cycles as an undirected graph.)

Cycles are concatenations of forward and backward orbit segments. The most dynamically significant cycles are created when there are two (directed) paths of the same length from a vertex
v_{i}
to a vertex
v_{j}
. We can count these by looking at the (directed) adjacency matrix
A
for the Morse graph
{\mathsf{M}}
. (Since
{\mathsf{M}}
is acyclic,
A
will be nilpotent.) The
i,j
entry of
A^{m}
gives the number of (directed) paths from
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
, so if it is greater than 1, then there is a cycle: follow one path from
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
, then the other backwards from
v_{j}
back to
v_{i}
. Unfortunately, this method can overcount the number of such cycles. If we have two paths of length
m_{1}
from
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
and two of length
m_{2}
from
v_{j}
to
v_{k}
, then
A^{m_{1}+m_{2}}
will detect four paths from
v_{i}
to
v_{k}
, implying multiple cycles, when in fact there are just combinations of the two existing cycles. We can address this overcounting algebraically, in homology.

Homology for graphs is particularly simple:
H_{0}
counts the connected components, and
H_{1}
counts the cycles algebraically. (See
[
35
]
*§VII.3.) For simplicity, we will use
\mathbb{R}
coefficients. Persistence for Morse graphs induces persistence for homology:

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
f:X\to X
be a continuous map of a compact metric space, and let
\{\mathcal{G}_{k}\}
be a collection of grids for
X
, partially ordered by refinement. For each
k
, let
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k}}:\mathcal{G}_{k}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{G}_{k}
be the minimal multivalued map associated to
f
on
\mathcal{G}_{k}
. Then for every
\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}
, there are homomorphisms
(\mathsf{h}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}})_{*}:H_{*}({\mathsf{M}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}})\to H_{*}({\mathsf{M}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}})
such that
(\mathsf{h}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}})_{*}\circ(\mathsf{h}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}})_{*}=(\mathsf{h}_{\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}})_{*}
for any
\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}
.

As we saw in Proposition
5.4
, passing from a more refined grid
\mathcal{G}
to a coarser grid
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
can change
{\mathsf{M}}^{\mathcal{G}}
to
{\mathsf{M}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}
by adding edges, adding vertices, or merging vertices.
We now examine the effect of each change on homology. We first make some definitions for a Morse graph
{\mathsf{M}}
. For a given vertex
v
, call the set of vertices above it
A(v):=\{u:u\succ v\}
, the vertices below
B(v)):=\{u:u\prec v\}
, and the incomparable vertices
N(v):=\{u:u\not\gtreqqless v\}
. For a pair of vertices
v\succ v^{\prime}
, call the set of vertices in between
I(v,v^{\prime}):=\{u:v\succ u\succ v^{\prime}\}=B(v)\cap A(v^{\prime})
. Let
a(v)=\#(A(v))
and
b(v)=\#(B(v))
.

Recall that when we merge two vertices
v
and
v^{\prime}
, we also merge the relevant edges (for example, edges
u\to v
and
u\to v^{\prime}
are merged).

Theorem 5.6
.

For grids
\mathcal{G}<\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, the graph
{\mathsf{M}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}
can be obtained from
{\mathsf{M}}^{\mathcal{G}}
by a series of the following operations.

(1)

Adding a vertex (with no edges).

(2)

Adding an edge
v\to v^{\prime}
, where
v
and
v^{\prime}
are incomparable,
v
is maximal in
N(v^{\prime})
(that is,
A(v)\cap N(v^{\prime})=\emptyset)
and
v^{\prime}
is minimal in
N(v)
(that is,
B(v^{\prime})\cap N(v)=\emptyset
).

(3)

Merging two vertices
v
and
v^{\prime}
, such that
v\succ v^{\prime}
, there are no intermediate vertices (that is,
I(v,v^{\prime})=\emptyset
), and
u\succ u^{\prime}
for all
u\in A(v^{\prime})
and
u^{\prime}\in B(v)
.

Proof.

If coarsening the grid adds a vertex with edges, we can clearly add the vertex first, then add the edges.

If it adds an edge
u\to u^{\prime}
, we claim that
u
and
u^{\prime}
must be incomparable. If
u\succ u^{\prime}
, then there is already an edge
u\to u^{\prime}
, and we cannot add one. If
u^{\prime}\succ u
, then adding an edge
u\succ u^{\prime}
means that the corresponding Morse sets are now in the same equivalence class, and thus the vertices are merged. So
u
and
u^{\prime}
must be incomparable. Since
{\mathsf{M}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}
is a Morse graph, adding an edge from
u\to u^{\prime}
implies that the resulting graph will have edges from every vertex in
\{u\}\cup A(u)
to every vertex in
\{u^{\prime}\}\cup B(u^{\prime})
. We can add these edges iteratively, starting with an edge
v\to v^{\prime}
that does not force the addition of any other edges (that is,
v
is maximal in
N(v^{\prime})
and
v^{\prime}
is minimal in
N(v)
), then continuing with the resulting graph. We claim that we can find such an edge by picking
v
to be a maximal element of
(\{u\}\cup A(u))\cap N(u^{\prime})
and
v^{\prime}
to be a minimal element of
(\{u^{\prime}\}\cup B(u^{\prime}))\cap N(v)
. Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that there exists a
y\in A(v)\cap N(v^{\prime})
. Then
y
is not in
N(u^{\prime})
(that would contradict maximality of
v
), so
y
is comparable to
u^{\prime}
but not to
v^{\prime}
. Since
u^{\prime}\succeq v^{\prime}
, that means that
u^{\prime}\succ y
, so we have
u^{\prime}\succ y\succ v\succeq u
, meaning that
u
and
u^{\prime}
are comparable, which is a contradiction. Thus
A(v)\cap N(v^{\prime})=\emptyset
. And since
B(v^{\prime})\subset\{u^{\prime}\}\cup B(u^{\prime})
, the minimality of
v^{\prime}
implies that
B(v^{\prime})\cap N(v)=\emptyset
.

We can merge two incomparable vertices
v
and
u
by first adding an edge from
v
to
u
. So we can assume that
v\succ u
. If
I(v,u)\neq\emptyset
, then, since
{\mathsf{M}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}
is a Morse graph, merging
v
and
u
into one vertex also merges everything in
I(v,u)
with
v
. We can perform these mergers iteratively, starting by verging
v
with a maximal member
v^{\prime}
of
I(v,u)
, that is, one with
I(v,v^{\prime})=\emptyset
, continuing with the resulting graph by merging the new vertex
v\sim v^{\prime}
with a maximal member of
I(v\sim v^{\prime},u)
, and so on.

Merging
v
and
v^{\prime}
forces
u\succ u^{\prime}
for all
u\in A(v^{\prime})
and
u^{\prime}\in B(v)
(we already have
w\succ w^{\prime}
for all
w\in A(v)
and
w^{\prime}\in B(v^{\prime})
, since
v\succ v^{\prime}
). If any such edge

does not already exist, that is,
u\not\succ u^{\prime}
, then
u\not\gtreqqless u^{\prime}
: if
u^{\prime}\succ u
, then we would have
v\succ u^{\prime}\succ u\succ v^{\prime}
, contradicting the assumption that there are no intermediate vertices between
v
and
v^{\prime}
. So, by adding edges as in (2) before merging, we can assume that

for all
u\in A(v^{\prime})
and
u^{\prime}\in B(v)
.

For (2), we observe that since

Assume first that

and

are in different connected components. Then adding the edge

\dim H_{0}

Now consider the case with
v
and
v^{\prime}
in the same connected component. Then adding the edge

from

to

and

The Morse graph helps us to understand the gradient-like behavior of the system. To understand the recurrent behavior, we study the dynamics on individual Morse sets. (The Conley index can rigorously relate these multivalued map dynamics to the dynamics of the underlying system; see, for example,

Let
\mathcal{G}<\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
be two grids, and
{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{G}},\{{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}\})

(\{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{j}\},\succeq^{\mathcal{G}})

As we pass from

Just the mapping

Proposition 6.1
.

h({\mathcal{S}})
is an

Proof.

This follows from the fact that

We need to show that

h

.

When the mapping
h:{\mathcal{S}}\to{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
is not surjective,
h({\mathcal{S}})\subsetneq{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
, we see a change in the recurrent dynamics as we pass from
\mathcal{G}
to
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
.
To better understand these changes in the size and shape of the Morse set, we can look at the persistent homology. In
[
20
]
, the authors define persistent homology for Morse sets as finite topological spaces, in part to address the issue that in the grid setting, different Morse sets can have nonempty intersection. Since we are studying Morse sets individually, we can also use cubical homology
[
29
,
40
]
.

Even when passing from
{\mathcal{F}}
to
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
does not increase the size of the Morse set, that is, even when
h({\mathcal{S}})={\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
, it can still change the dynamics by growing the image,
h({\mathcal{F}}(G))\subsetneq{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}(h(G))
for some
G\in{\mathcal{S}}
. Equivalently, there are edges in
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}\to{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
that do not come from edges in
{\mathcal{F}}:{\mathcal{S}}\to{\mathcal{S}}
. (This will not happen for the minimal multivalued map associated to
f:X\to X
, since there is an
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
-edge
G^{\prime}\to H^{\prime}
if and only if there is an
{\mathcal{F}}
-edge
G\to H
for some
G\in h^{-1}(G^{\prime})
and
H\in h^{-1}(H^{\prime})
, but it can occur for other methods of defining multivalued grip maps, discussed in Section
7
.)

So, for the rest of this section, we assume that
h:{\mathcal{S}}\to{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
is surjective. We cannot compare
{\mathcal{F}}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}\to{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{F}}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}\to{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
directly, since they act on different spaces. To understand the change in dynamics, we compare
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}\to{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
to
\bar{\mathcal{F}}:{\mathcal{S}}\to{\mathcal{S}}
, the map induced by
{\mathcal{F}}
on
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
.

More precisely, we define
\bar{\mathcal{F}}:=h\circ{\mathcal{F}}\circ h^{-1}
. Thus, thinking of
\bar{\mathcal{F}}
as a graph, we have an edge
G^{\prime}\to H^{\prime}
if and only if
{\mathcal{F}}
has an edge
G\to H
for some
G
and
H
such that
h(G)=G^{\prime}
and
h(H)=H^{\prime}
. We observe that
\bar{\mathcal{F}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
, that is,
\bar{\mathcal{F}}(G^{\prime})\subset{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}(G^{\prime})
for all
G^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
.

Since the Morse sets correspond to the strongly connected components of the multivalued map considered as a graph, the dynamics on any Morse set are transitive, meaning that for any
G
,
H\in{\mathcal{S}}
, there is an
n>0
such that
H\subset{\mathcal{F}}^{n}(G)
. Thus passing from
{\mathcal{F}}
to
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
will not cause a radical change in the dynamics; they will still be transitive. However, it can cause one important change. Recall that
{\mathcal{F}}
is mixing if there exists an
n>0
such that for any
G
,
H\in{\mathcal{S}}
and any
m\geq n
,
H\subset{\mathcal{F}}^{m}(G)
(
[
32
]
*§4.5). Any mixing map is transitive, but not vice versa. If
{\mathcal{F}}
is transitive but not mixing, then we can partition
{\mathcal{S}}
into subsets
{\mathcal{S}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
for some
d
, where
{\mathcal{F}}
cyclically permutes the sets
{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
(
{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{S}}_{i})={\mathcal{S}}_{i+1\mod d}
)
and the restriction of the
d
th power of
{\mathcal{F}}
to each
{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
,
{\mathcal{F}}^{d}|_{{\mathcal{S}}_{i}}:{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\to{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
, is mixing. If

is the minimal such integer, we call

the period of
{\mathcal{F}}
,

, and say that
{\mathcal{F}}
is
d
-periodic (so a mixing map is 1-periodic). We see in Figure

that passing from

can change the dynamics from non-mixing to mixing.

7.
Time series

In applications, we may not have complete information about the map
f:X\to X
, or it may be too difficult to compute.
We can also get multivalued grid maps from time series, either from sampling the phase space directly or from observations. Reconstructing dynamics from time series is a very active area of research. See, for example,
[
2
,
23
,
10
,
36
,
45
,
34
,
44
,
24
,
7
]
. In particular, in
[
38
]
the authors create a Morse decomposition for the time series map.

7.1.
Observations

We largely follow the presentation in
[
38
]
.
Let
\pi:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a continuous map. We think of
\pi
as an observation of the state of the system. It can measure
m
different scalar quantities, or a single quantity
\pi_{0}
at
m
time steps:

\pi(x)=(\pi_{0}(x),\pi_{0}(f(x)),\ldots,\pi_{0}(f^{m-1}(x))).

Our data consist of observations of a finite number of finite orbit segments. More precisely, let
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
be a set of initial points in
X
. For each
x_{i}
, we take observations from the first
N_{i}
points on the orbit of
x_{i}
, and define
y_{i}^{j}:=\pi(f^{j}(x_{i}))
for
0\leq j\leq N_{i}-1
. Let
{\mathbf{y}}_{i}=(y_{i}^{0},\ldots,y_{i}^{N-i-1})
denote the observations from initial point
x_{i}
, and
O=\{{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\}
the set of all observations.

Now take a grid
\mathcal{G}
on
\pi(X)\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We define the multivalued map
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{O}:\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{G}
associated to
O
on
\mathcal{G}
by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{O}(G)=\{H\in\mathcal{G}:\

there exist
{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\in O
and
j
,
0\leq j<N_{i}-1
,

\displaystyle\text{such that $y_{i}^{j}\in G$, $y_{i}^{j+1}\in H$}\}.

As with the minimal multivalued map associated to
f
in Section
2
, we trim any stranded vertices from the graph associated to
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{O}
.

We can define a Morse decomposition for
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{O}
just as before: Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{O}
be the finest Morse decomposition associated to
O
, corresponding to the strongly connected components of
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{O}
.
We now show that we get persistence over grid refinement. We begin with the following analogue of Proposition
4.2
.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
O
be a set of observations, and let
\{\mathcal{G}_{k}\}
be a collection of grids for
\pi(X)
, partially ordered by refinement. For each
k
, let
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{O}:\mathcal{G}_{k}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{G}_{k}
be the multivalued map associated to
O
on
\mathcal{G}_{k}
.
Then for every
\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}
, there is a morphism
h_{k1}^{k2}:\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}\to\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}
, and if
\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}<\mathcal{G}_{k_{3}}
, then
h_{k_{2}}^{k_{3}}\circ h_{k_{1}}^{k_{2}}=h_{k_{1}}^{k_{3}}
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Proposition
4.2
. Take grids
\mathcal{G}<\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
. Since
\mathcal{G}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, by definition for any
G\in\mathcal{G}
there is a
G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
such that
G\subset G^{\prime}
; define
h
by
h(G):=G^{\prime}
. If
y_{i}^{j}\in G
and
y_{i}^{j+1}\in H
, then
y_{i}^{j}\in h(G)
and
y_{i}^{j+1}\in h(H)
. Thus we have
h({\mathcal{F}}_{O}(G))\subset{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}_{O}(h(G))
. It follows from the definition that
h_{k_{2}}^{k_{3}}\circ h_{k_{1}}^{k_{2}}=h_{k_{1}}^{k_{3}}
.
∎

.

Let
O
be a set of observations, and let
\{\mathcal{G}_{k}\}
be a collection of grids for

, partially ordered by refinement. For each

This follows from Proposition
7.1
and Lemma
4.5

As a consequence, all of the results on global and local persistence from Sections
5
and
6
apply to the maps

7.2.
Sampled dynamics

Instead of taking observations, we can also gather information about the map
f:X\to X
by sampling the phase space directly.
Abstractly, in our setting, the two approaches are essentially the same, but they are conceptually different ways of looking at the dynamics, so we treat them separately. We generally follow the presentation in
[
36
]
.

Our data consist of a sample of the dynamics, in the form of pairs of points,
D:=\{(x_{i},y_{i}):y_{i}=f(x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{K}
. For a grid
\mathcal{G}
on
X
, we define the multivalued map
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{D}:\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{G}
associated to
D
on
\mathcal{G}
by

{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{D}(G)=\{H\in\mathcal{G}:\text{ there exists $(x_{i},y_{i})\in D$ with $x_{i}\in G$, $y_{i}\in H$}\}.

We proceed just as we did with the observations. We trim any stranded vertices from the graph associated to
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{D}
.
We define
{\mathcal{M}}_{D}
to be the finest Morse decomposition associated to
D
, corresponding to the strongly connected components of
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{D}
.
And we get persistence over grid refinement.

7.3.
Generalizations

In applications, the time series data may be noisy, and we may not want to construct grid maps with images corresponding to every data point, as we do above. Instead, we may require that they meet some type of frequency threshold. We discuss two such methods, and their relation to persistence.

One such method is discussed in
[
20
]
. Translating their approach slightly to our setting, of a grid
\mathcal{G}
and sampled data points
D=\{(x_{i},y_{i}):y_{i}=f(x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{K}
, they define functions
n(G,H):=\#\{x_{i}:x_{i}\in G,y_{i}\in H\}
and
n_{max}(\mathcal{G}):=\max_{G,H\in\mathcal{G}}\{n(G,H)\}
. They then define the grid map
{{\widehat{\mathcal{F}}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{D}}:\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{G}
by

{{\widehat{\mathcal{F}}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{D}}(G):=\{H:n(G,H)>\mu\cdot n_{max}(\mathcal{G})\},

for some threshold parameter
\mu
. (They then take the convex hull, but we can ignore that for our purposes.)

For a fixed threshold
\mu
, this method will not give persistence over grid refinement. If
\mathcal{G}<\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
and
H\in{{\widehat{\mathcal{F}}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{D}}(G)
, it will not necessarily be
true that
h(H)\in{{\widehat{\mathcal{F}}}^{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}_{D}}(h(G))
. Let
M=M(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{G}^{\prime}):=\max_{G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}\#(h^{-1}(G^{\prime}))
, the maximum number of
\mathcal{G}
-grid elements any
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
-grid element is subdivided into.
Then
n_{max}(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})
can be as great as
M^{2}\cdot n_{max}(\mathcal{G})
, if there are elements
G^{\prime}
,
H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
such that
n(G,H)=n_{max}(\mathcal{G})
for all
G\in h^{-1}(G^{\prime})
and
H\in h^{-1}(H^{\prime})
. At the same time, there can be
G^{\prime}
,
H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
such that
n(G^{\prime},H^{\prime})=n(G,H)
for some
G\in h^{-1}(G^{\prime})
and
H\in h^{-1}(H^{\prime})
, if there are no data points going from any other element of
h^{-1}(G^{\prime})
to any other element of
h^{-1}(H^{\prime})
. Thus
n(G,H)>\mu\cdot n_{max}(\mathcal{G})
does not imply
n(h(G),h(H))>\mu\cdot n_{max}(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})
.

However, if we can choose the threshold
\mu
to depend on the grids
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
(and
not
on the data
D
), we can get persistence. Since
n(h(G),h(H))\geq n(G,H)
and
n_{max}(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})\leq M^{2}\cdot n_{max}(\mathcal{G})
, if we choose a threshold
\mu(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})\leq\frac{\mu(\mathcal{G})}{M^{2}}
, we will get persistence.

Morita et al. present another method in
[
38
]
. They define
v(G):=\#\{x_{i}:x_{i}\in G\}
and the transition probability
t(G,H):=\frac{n(G,H)}{v(G)}
. They then define the grid map
{{\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{D}}:\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{G}
by

{{\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}}^{\mathcal{G}}_{D}}(G):=\{H:t(G,H)\geq\lambda\cdot t(H,G)\text{ and }n(G,H)\geq\mu\},

for some threshold parameters
\lambda
and
\mu
. This definition will not give persistence, and there is no way to get persistence by choosing thresholds based solely on the grids
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
: we can have
t(G,H)>t(H,G)
, but
\frac{t(h(G),h(H))}{t(h(H),h(G))}
arbitrarily small, if there are many data points going from the other elements of
h^{-1}(H)
to the other elements of
h^{-1}(G)
. However,
we may be able to get persistence if we construct thresholds based on characteristics of the data as well as of the grids. This is not necessarily an unreasonable requirement, given that in practice we may want to choose refinements based on the density of the data points anyway.

Both of these results can be generalized. Characterizing persistent methods for constructing grid maps from time series will be the subject of future work.
