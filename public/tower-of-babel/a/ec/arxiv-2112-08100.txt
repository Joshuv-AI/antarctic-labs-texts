Abstract
. In 1991, Roth introduced a natural generalization of rank metric codes, namely tensor codes. The latter are defined to be subspaces of r r -tensors where the ambient space is endowed with the tensor rank as a distance function. In this work, we describe the general class of tensor codes and we study their invariants that correspond to different families of anticodes. In our context, an anticode is a perfect space that has some additional properties. A perfect space is one that is spanned by tensors of rank 1. Our use of the anticode concept is motivated by an interest in capturing structural properties of tensor codes. In particular, we indentify four different classes of tensor anticodes and show how these gives different information on the codes they describe. We also define the generalized tensor binomial moments and the generalized tensor weight distribution of a code and establish a bijection between these invariants. We use the generalized tensor binomial moments to define the concept of an i i -tensor BMD code, which is an extremal code in relation to an inequality arising from them. Finally, we give MacWilliams identities for generalized tensor binomial moments.

1.
Introduction

There are well established connections between linear block codes and 3-tensors, in particular the bilinear complexity of a 3-tensor can be related to the parameters of an associated linear code
[
4
]
. More recently, matrix codes have been studied in the context of 3-tensors
[
5
,
7
]
.
In
[
14
,
15
]
, Roth studied
tensor codes
, that is, linear spaces of
r
-tensors in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, where the ambient space is endowed with the tensor rank as a distance function. Such codes are natural generalizations of rank metric codes.
In
[
6
]
, new invariants of coding theory were introduced, namely the
i
-th generalized rank weights. Matrix codes that are extremal in respect of a related bound, called
i
-binomial moment determined (
i
-BMD), all have the same
i
-th generalized rank weights. These new invariants can be used to distinguish inequivalent codes.
In
[
12
]
, the generalized rank weights were defined using anticodes and a Wei-duality result was derived for linear matrix codes.

Building on these initial papers, we generalize in two directions. Firstly, we study the general class of tensor codes and secondly we consider different anticodes related to the tensor rank and their corresponding invariants. In our context, an anticode is a
perfect space
that has some additional properties. A perfect space is one that is spanned by tensors of rank
1
. We identify four classes of different anticodes, namely the perfect spaces and three proper subtypes called
closure-type
,
Delsarte-type
and
Ravagnani-type
anticodes. We associate with each anticode type a sequence of generalized tensor weights. We remark that for the case
r=2
, i.e. for which the tensors codes are matrix codes, both the Delsarte-type and Ravagnani-type anticodes coincide and are the same as those defined in
[
12
]
, while the
j
-th generalized tensor weights corresponding to the perfect spaces are the the same as those defined in
[
7
]
.

Our use of the anticode concept is motivated by an interest in capturing structural properties of tensor codes. As such, we define these objects in terms of tensor products. What we observe is a gradation in information about the parameters and properties of tensor codes. For example, the first generalized tensor weight associated with the
perfect spaces
corresponds exactly to the tensor rank distance of the code while the
k
-th generalized tensor weight associated with the perfect spaces corresponds to the tensor rank of the code itself as an
(r+1)
-tensor, where
k
denotes the dimension of the code. These invariants give the most information on the code. As we progress through the types, from perfect spaces, through the closure-type to the Delsarte and Ravagnani-type anticodes and their associated invariants, we observe a loss of information about the code but we see decreasing complexity of computing these invariants, with the most noticeable difference occurring between the invariants for the perfect spaces and those of the closure-type anticodes. On the other hand, the Ravagnani-type anticodes are the only ones that exhibit a natural duality, similar to what can be observed in the matrix case (
r=2
). We compare these invariants through numerous examples.

We remark on some important motivations on this topic. There are some significant departures from the matrix case for codes that are linear spaces of
r
-tensors for
r\geq 3
. Firstly, the ‘usual’ Singleton bound is not sharp in general. While a strict improvement on this bound is obtained in
[
14
]
for the codes in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n\times n}
, only asymptotic bounds for higher order tensors are known. Moreover, it is not known if extremality with respect to this bound is an invariant of code duality. Compare this to the case of MRD codes, whose duals are also MRD and whose rank weight distributions are determined. We remark further that invariants associated with the closure-type anticodes not only provide a good distinguisher between inequivalent tensor codes, they are also a new invariant in the case of matrix codes.

This paper is organised as follows. In Section 2 we give preliminary results. In Section 3 we define what is meant by a tensor code, extending the definition originally given in
[
14
]
. We define the closure of a tensor code and describe its properties. In Section 4, we introduce the concept of a tensor anticode and define the perfect space anticodes, closure-type, Delsarte-type, and the Ravagnani-type anticodes. We characterize these families and show the relations between them. In Section 5 we define the
j
-th generalized tensor weight associated with each type of anticode, and their corresponding dual weights, should they exist. We describe their properties and show how these different invariants offer different information on the codes they describe. In Section 6 we define the generalized tensor binomial moments and the generalized tensor weight distribution of a code and establish a bijection between these invariants. We also establish MacWilliams identities for generalized binomial moments, from which those for generalized tensor weight distributions follow. Particular formulations of such identities for
r=2
are derived in
[
6
,
9
,
13
]
. We use the generalized tensor binomial moments to define the concept of an
i
-tensor BMD code (an
i
-TBMD code), which is an extremal code in relation to an inequality arising from the binomial moments. We summarize the different characterisations and properties of the collections of anticodes considered in this paper in the appendix.

Preliminaries and Notation

Throughout the paper, we let
q
be a prime power and let
\mathbb{F}
be the tensor product space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{r}}
for some integers
r,n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}
such that
r,n_{1}\geq 2
. We will assume that
n_{1}=\min\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}\}
and that
n_{r}=\max\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}\}
. We let
n:=\prod_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}
. Finally, for a positive integer
i
we denote by
[i]
the set
\{1,\ldots,i\}
.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that
U\in\mathbb{F}
is a
rank-
1
(or
simple
)
tensor
if it can be expressed as
u^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes u^{(r)}
, for some
u^{(i)}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
.

Definition 2.2
.

The
rank
of
X\in\mathbb{F}
is defined to be

\textup{rk}(X):=\min\left\{R\in\mathbb{Z}:X=\sum_{s=1}^{R}u_{s}^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{s}^{(r)}\right\}.

That is,
\textup{rk}(X)
is the least integer
\ell
such that
X
can be written as sum of
\ell
simple tensors.

The rank induces a metric on
\mathbb{F}
, i.e. the function

:\mathbb{F}\times\mathbb{F}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}:(X,Y)\longmapsto\textup{rk}(X-Y),

is a distance function (see
[
15
]
for further details).

It is well-known that if
\{x_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,x_{n_{i}}^{(i)}\}
is a basis of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}}
, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, then a basis of
\mathbb{F}
is

\left\{\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}x_{j_{i}}^{(i)}:1\leq j_{i}\leq n_{i},1\leq i\leq r\right\}.

In particular, we have
\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\mathbb{F}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}}\right)=n
. An
r
-tensor
X\in\mathbb{F}
can be represented as the map

X:[n_{1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{r}]\longmapsto\mathbb{F}_{q}

given by
X=(X_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}}:1\leq j_{i}\leq n_{i},1\leq i\leq r)
. Therefore, the map

:\mathbb{F}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{r}}:X=\sum_{s=1}^{\textup{rk}(X)}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}x_{s}^{(i)}\longmapsto\left(X_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}}=\sum_{s=1}^{\textup{rk}(X)}x_{j_{i},s}^{(i)}\right)_{1\leq j_{i}\leq n_{i},1\leq i\leq r}

where,
x_{s}^{(i)}=(x_{j_{i},s}^{(i)}:1\leq j_{i}\leq n_{i})
for any
i\in[r]
, is an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-isomorphism. In particular, we can identify
\mathbb{F}
with
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{r}}
and represent an
r
-tensor as an
r
-dimensional array.

We briefly recall some basic definitions on posets and lattices (a standard reference is
[
16
, Chapter 3]
). A
partially ordered set
(or
poset
) is a pair
(\mathscr{P},\leq)
where
\mathscr{P}
is a non-empty set and
\leq
is an binary relation satisfying the three axioms of
reflexivity
,
antisymmetry
and
transitivity
. With an abuse of notation, in the following we denote by
\mathscr{P}
the poset
(\mathscr{P},\leq)
. Let
x,y\in\mathscr{P}
, we say that
x
and
y
are
comparable
if
x\leq y
or
y\leq x
, otherwise we say that they are
incomparable
. Moreover, we use the standard notation
x<y
to mean
x\leq y
and
x\neq y
. An
upper bound
of
x
and
y
is an element
u\in\mathscr{P}
satisfying
x\leq u
and
y\leq u
. A
least upper bound
(or
join
) of
x
and
y
is the element
u\in\mathscr{P}
such that
u\leq v
for any other upper bound
v
of
x
and
y
. If such element exists then it is unique and it is denoted by
x\vee y
. Dually, we can define the
greatest upper bound
(or
meet
)
x\wedge y
of
x
and
y
, if it exists. If
\mathscr{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{P}_{r}
are posets then the (
direct
)
product
of
\mathscr{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{P}_{r}
is defined to be the poset
\mathscr{P}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathscr{P}_{r}
on the set
\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}):x_{i}\in\mathscr{P}_{i},i\in[r]\}
such that
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})\leq(y_{1},\ldots,y_{r})
in
\mathscr{P}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathscr{P}_{r}
if
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
in
\mathscr{P}_{i}
, for all
i\in[r]
. The
Möbius function
of
\mathscr{L}
is defined recursively as

\mu_{\mathscr{P}}(x,y)=\begin{cases}1&\textup{ if }x=y,\\
\displaystyle-\sum_{x\leq z<y}\mu_{\mathscr{P}}(x,z)&\textup{ if }x<y,\\
0&\textup{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Remark 2.3
.

One can check that the Möbius function of the subspace poset
\mathscr{P}
of a vector spaces over a field
\mathbb{K}
depends only on the dimension of their elements. In particular, for any
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathscr{P}
such that
x_{1}\leq y_{1}
,
x_{2}\leq y_{2}
,
\dim_{\mathbb{K}}(x_{1})=\dim_{\mathbb{K}}(x_{2})
and
\dim_{\mathbb{K}}(y_{1})=\dim_{\mathbb{K}}(y_{2})
, we have
\mu_{\mathscr{P}}(x_{1},y_{1})=\mu_{\mathscr{P}}(x_{2},y_{2})
. In the following, we use the notation
\mu_{\mathscr{P}}(\dim_{\mathbb{K}}(x_{1}),\dim_{\mathbb{K}}(y_{1}))
to mean
\mu_{\mathscr{P}}(x_{1},y_{1})
.

Proposition 2.4

(Möbius Inversion Formula)
.

Let
\mathscr{P}
a poset such that for every element
z\in\mathscr{P}
the
principal order ideal
generated by
z
, i.e. the set
\{x\leq z:x\in\mathscr{P}\}
, is finite. Let
f,g:\mathscr{P}\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}
, where
\mathbb{K}
is a field. For all
y\in\mathscr{P}
, we have

g(y)=\sum_{x\leq y}f(x)\qquad\textup{ if and only if }\qquad f(y)=\sum_{x\leq y}\mu(x,y)g(x).

We say that the poset
\mathscr{P}
is
locally finite
if for all
x,y\in\mathscr{P}
the sets
\{z\in\mathscr{P}:x\leq z\leq y\}
and
\{z\in\mathscr{P}:x<z<y\}
are finite (or empty).

Proposition 2.5

(The Product Theorem)
.

Let
\mathscr{P}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathscr{P}_{r}
be the product of the locally finite posets
\mathscr{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{P}_{r}
. If
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})\leq(y_{1},\ldots,y_{r})
in
\mathscr{P}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathscr{P}_{r}
then

\mu_{\mathscr{P}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathscr{P}_{r}}((x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}),(y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}))=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{\mathscr{P}_{i}}(x_{i},y_{i}).

A
lattice

\mathscr{L}
is a poset that contains the join and the meet of every pair of its elements. Every finite lattice has a minimum and a maximum element, denoted by
0_{\mathscr{L}}
and
1_{\mathscr{L}}
respectively. A subset
\mathscr{M}
of
\mathscr{L}
is a
sublattice
if is closed under the operations of
\vee
and
\wedge
in
\mathscr{L}
.

-binomial coefficients (the reader is referred to

.

Let

\begin{bmatrix}{a}\\

Let

3.
Tensor codes

Roth introduced the class of tensor codes, as a generalisation of rank-metric codes, in
[
14
,
15
]
and for the case
n_{1}=\cdots=n_{r}
.
We will consider this class of codes, which are subspaces of
\mathbb{F}
, without the constraint that the
n_{i}
are all equal.

Definition 3.1
.

A
tensor code
is a
k
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathbb{F}
. We define the
maximum rank

\textup{maxrk}(\mathcal{C})
as
\textup{maxrk}(\mathcal{C}):=\max\{\textup{rk}(C):C\in\mathcal{C}\}
. The
minimum
(
tensor rank
)
distance
of a non-zero code
\mathcal{C}
is
d(\mathcal{C}):=\min\{\textup{rk}(C):C\in\mathcal{C},c\neq 0\}
. The
tensor rank

\textup{trk}(\mathcal{C})
of
\mathcal{C}
is the minimum dimension of a perfect space containing
\mathcal{C}
.

We refer to
n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{r},k,d(\mathcal{C})
as the
code parameters
of
\mathcal{C}
and we say that
\mathcal{C}
is an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-
[n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{r},k,d(\mathcal{C})]
code. Throughout the paper, we let
\mathcal{C}
be an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-
[n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{r},k,d(\mathcal{C})]
code and we write
d
instead of
d(\mathcal{C})
. For any
i
, we denote by
\cdot
the dot product on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}}
, defined in the usual way as
x\cdot y=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}x_{j}y_{j}
for
x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}}
.
We furthermore denote by
*
the non-degenerate bilinear form defined by:

\begin{array}[]{cccc}*:&\mathbb{F}\times\mathbb{F}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{F}_{q}\\
&\displaystyle\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}u^{(i)},\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}v^{(i)}\right)&\longmapsto&\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(u^{(i)}\cdot v^{(i)}\right).\end{array}

Remark 3.2
.

One can check that, for all
X,Y\in\mathbb{F}
, we have

X*Y=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\cdots\sum_{j_{r}=1}^{n_{r}}X_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{r}}Y_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{r}}.

Definition 3.3
.

We define the
dual
of
\mathcal{C}
to be

\mathcal{C}^{\perp}:=\{X\in\mathbb{F}:X*C=0\textup{ for all }C\in\mathcal{C}\}.

We summarize some basic facts on the dual tensor code.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}\leq\mathbb{F}
be codes. The following hold.

(1)

\left(\mathcal{C}^{\perp}\right)^{\perp}=\mathcal{C}
.

(2)

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\mathcal{C}^{\perp}\right)=n-\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\mathcal{C}\right)
.

(3)

(\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{D})^{\perp}=\mathcal{C}^{\perp}+\mathcal{D}^{\perp}
.

In the remainder, unless explicitly stated,
\mathcal{C}\leq\mathbb{F}
will denote a tensor code of dimension
k
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and minimum distance
d
. We now define the
closure
of
\mathcal{C}
. As the reader will see, this corresponds to a minimal
anticode
containing
\mathcal{C}
.
Before giving a formal definition, let us state what the closure is for a matrix code.
To this end, let
r=2
. For each
C\in\mathcal{C}
, denote by
\textup{rowsp}(C)
and
\textup{colsp}(C)
the vector spaces generated by the rows and the columns of
C
, respectively,
and define the closure of
\left<C\right>_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
to be
\textup{colsp}(C)\otimes\textup{rowsp}(C)
.
More generally, we define
\textup{cl}(\mathcal{C})=\textup{colsp}(\mathcal{C})\otimes\textup{rowsp}(\mathcal{C})
, where

\textup{rowsp}(\mathcal{C})=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\textup{rowsp}(C)\qquad\textup{ and }\qquad\textup{colsp}(\mathcal{C})=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\textup{colsp}(C).

We remark that the closure of a matrix code does not require knowledge of its tensor rank. However, in our first definition of the closure, we will express it in terms of the decomposition of codewords as rank-1 tensors.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
C=\sum_{j=1}^{\textup{rk}(C)}c_{j}^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes c_{j}^{(r)}\in\mathcal{C}
be a codeword. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, we define the subspace

\textup{cl}(C)^{(i)}:=\left<c_{j}^{(i)}:j\in\{1,\ldots,\textup{rk}(C)\}\right>_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\leq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}}
. The
closure
of

is the tensor space

\textup{cl}(\mathcal{C}):=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}\textup{cl}(\mathcal{C})^{(i)}\qquad\textup{ where }\qquad\textup{cl}(\mathcal{C})^{(i)}:=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\textup{cl}(c)^{(i)}

for any

. For a tensor
X\in\mathbb{F}
we write
\textup{cl}(X)
to mean
\textup{cl}\left(\left<X\right>_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\right)
.

Clearly, every tensor code is contained in its own closure.

4.
Theory of Anticodes

Inspired by the work in
[
12
]
, in this section we develop the general theory of anticodes for tensor codes. We then define different classes of anticodes and we study their properties. In the following, we say that
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathbb{F}
is
perfect
if
\mathcal{C}
is generated by simple tensors, (c.f.
[
2
]
).

Definition 4.1
.

We say that
A\leq\mathbb{F}
is a (
tensor
)
anticode
if
A
is perfect. If
\mathcal{A}
is a collection of anticodes of
\mathbb{F}
we write
\mathcal{A}_{a}
to denote the set of all anticodes of
\mathcal{A}
of dimension
a
, where
a\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
.
If
\{A^{\perp}:A\in\mathcal{A}\}
is a collection of anticodes, then we define the collection of
dual
(
tensor
)
anticodes
of
\mathcal{A}
to be
\overline{\mathcal{A}}:=\{A^{\perp}:A\in\mathcal{A}\}
.

We now define the different classes of anticodes that we will consider in this paper.

Definition 4.2
.

We define the following.

(1)

The family of
perfect spaces
is

\mathcal{A}^{\textup{ps}}:=\{A\leq\mathbb{F}:A\textup{ is perfect}\}
.

(2)

The family of
closure-type anticodes
is

\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}:=\{A:A\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{ps}}\mid A=\textup{cl}(A)\}
.

(3)

The family of
Delsarte-type anticodes
is

\mathcal{A}^{\textup{D}}:=\{A:A\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}\mid A^{\perp}\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}\textup{ and }A^{(p)}=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{p}}\textup{ for some }p\in[r]\textup{ such that }n_{p}=n_{r}\}
.

(4)

The family of
Ravagnani-type anticodes
is

\mathcal{A}^{\textup{R}}:=\{A:A\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}\mid A^{\perp}\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}\textup{ and }A^{(p)}=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{p}}\textup{ for any }p\in[r]\textup{ such that }n_{p}\neq n_{1}\}
,

if
n_{1}<n_{r}
, and

\mathcal{A}^{\textup{R}}:=\{A:A\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}\mid A^{\perp}\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}\textup{ and }A^{(p)}=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{p}}\textup{ for some }p\in[r]\textup{ such that }n_{p}=n_{1}\}
,

if
n_{1}=n_{2}=\cdots=n_{r}
.

Clearly,
\mathcal{A}^{R},\mathcal{A}^{D}\subset\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}\subset\mathcal{A}^{\textup{ps}}.

The choice of these families of anticodes is motivated by the fact that they allow us to define invariants for tensor codes that extend the theory of invariants for matrix codes in the rank-metric.
For example, the definition of the
generalized tensor ranks
of a matrix code as defined in
[
7
, Section 6]
, relies on the notion of perfect spaces (the collection
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{ps}}
), although this was not explicitly stated there. Moreover, we will show that the Delsarte-type and the Ravagnani-type anticodes extend the theory of Delsarte optimal anticodes for rank-metric codes (see
[
12
]
).

Notation 4.3
.

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots r\}
and let
\phi_{i}:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}}
be a map. We define

\phi:\mathbb{F}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}:\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}X^{(i)}\longmapsto\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}\phi_{i}\left(X^{(i)}\right).

Clearly, if
\phi_{i}
is an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-isomorphism for each
i
then
\phi
is an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear isometry with respect to the tensor rank. Let
1\leq j<s\leq r
and suppose that
n_{j}=n_{s}
. Define the map

\tau_{j,s}:\mathbb{F}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}:\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}X^{(i)}\longmapsto\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{j-1}X^{(i)}\right)\otimes X^{(s)}\otimes\left(\bigotimes_{i=j+1}^{s-1}X^{(i)}\right)\otimes X^{(j)}\otimes\left(\bigotimes_{i=s+1}^{r}X^{(i)}\right).

That is,
\tau_{j,s}
is the map that interchanges
X^{(j)}
and
X^{(s)}
in
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}X^{(i)}
. It is easy to see that
\tau_{j,s}
is an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear isometry of
\mathbb{F}
.

We remark that
\tau_{j,s}
corresponds to the transposition of matrices for the case
r=2
.

Remark 4.4
.

Observe that the set
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{ps}}
of perfect spaces is closed under the isometries defined in Notation
4.3
. As the reader will see in the remainder of this section, all the classes of anticodes defined in Definition
4.1
are closed under these isometries. This fact will easily follow from the characterizations of the anticodes we now provide.

The following example shows that the dual (the orthogonal complement) of a perfect space is not perfect in general. This implies that the set
\overline{\mathcal{A}^{\textup{ps}}}
is not defined.

Example 4.5
.

Let
A\leq\mathbb{F}_{3}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}
be the perfect space of dimension
5
generated by

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&1&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&2&1\\
1&2&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0&2\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\

One can check that the dual of
\mathcal{C}
is the

A^{\perp}=\left<\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\

which is clearly not perfect.

Remark 4.6
.

Observe that
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{ps}}
is a lattice where the partial order relation is given by the inclusion of subspaces. Denote by
\vee_{\textup{ps}}
and
\wedge_{\textup{ps}}
the operations of join and meet, respectively, in
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{ps}}
. More in detail, let
A,B\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{ps}}
, we have that
A\vee_{\textup{ps}}B
is the vector space sum
A+B
and
A\wedge_{\textup{ps}}B
is the space
\left<X\in A\cap B:\textup{rk}(X)=1\right>_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
. Clearly,
A\vee_{\textup{ps}}B
and
A\wedge_{\textup{ps}}B
are both perfect spaces.

We now characterize the families of anticodes
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}
,
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{D}}
, and
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{R}}
. The following result is an immediate consequence of the definition of the closure of a tensor code (Definition
3.5
) and the fact that the tensor product of a collection of vector spaces is perfect.

Proposition 4.7
.

The following holds.

\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}=\left\{\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}A^{(i)}:A^{(i)}\leq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}}\textup{ for all }i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\right\}.

Notation 4.8
.

Let
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
. We denote by
\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}})
the lattice of subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}}
where, for any subspaces
A,B
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}}
, we define the join of
A
and
B
to be
A+B
and their meet to be
A\cap B
. For any
A\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}
we denote by
A
the unique subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}}
such that
A=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}A^{(i)}
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}
. We have
B\leq A
if and only if, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
,
B^{(i)}\leq A^{(i)}
in
\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}})
.

Proof.

Since
A,B\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}
then
A=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}A^{(i)}
and
B=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}B^{(i)}
for some
A^{(i)},B^{(i)}\leq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}}
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
. Clearly, if
B^{(i)}\leq A^{(i)}
in
\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}})
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
then
B\leq A
. It remains to prove the other implication. Suppose
B\leq A
and observe that
B
has a basis of simple tensors
C:=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}c^{(i)}
where, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
,
c^{(i)}
is an element of a basis for
B^{(i)}
. Since
B\leq A
then
c
is also an element of
A
. This implies that, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
,
c^{(i)}
is also an element of
A^{(i)}
. The statement follows.
∎

Proposition 4.10
.

\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}
is a sublattice of
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{ps}}
.

Proof.

We claim that
A\wedge_{\textup{ps}}B=A\cap B
.
It is sufficient to show that
A\cap B\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}
. We have

A\cap B=\left<\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}x^{(i)}:x^{(i)}\in A^{(i)}\textup{ and }x^{(i)}\in B^{(i)}\textup{ for all }i\in[r]\right>_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}\left(A^{(i)}\cap B^{(i)}\right)

which shows that
A\cap B
is an element of
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}
. Therefore
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}
is closed under the operation of
\wedge_{\textup{ps}}
. It is easy to see that for any
A,B\in A^{\textup{cl}}
, we have
A\vee_{\textup{ps}}B=A+B
and that the latter is a closure-type anticode. This shows that
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}
is also closed under the operation of
\vee_{\textup{ps}}
and therefore
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}
is a sublattice of
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{ps}}.

∎

Corollary 4.11
.

The closure of
\mathcal{C}
is the minimal element of
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}
containing
\mathcal{C}
, that is,

\textup{cl}(\mathcal{C})=\bigcap\{A\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}:\mathcal{C}\leq A\}.

Proof.

First of all, observe that
\textup{cl}(\mathcal{C})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}\textup{cl}(\mathcal{C})^{(i)}\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}
by definition of the closure. If
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}
then the result is immediate. We assume
\mathcal{C}\notin\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}
in the remainder of the proof. Suppose that there exists a closure-type anticode
A\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}
such that
\mathcal{C}<A<\textup{cl}(\mathcal{C})
, write
A=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}A^{(i)}
. By Lemma
4.9
, there exists
u\in\textup{cl}(\mathcal{C})^{(i)}
such that
u\notin A^{(i)}
, for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
. This leads to the contradiction

A\cap\mathcal{C}=\left\{C\in\mathcal{C}:c^{(i)}\notin\left<u\right>_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\right\}\lneq\mathcal{C}.

since, by definition of the closure, we have that there exists at least one element
C
of
\mathcal{C}
such that
c^{(i)}\in\left<u\right>_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

In the next theorem we give a characterization of the closure-type dual anticodes. As a consequence, we have that the set of closure-tye dual anticodes
\overline{\mathcal{A}}^{\textup{cl}}
is defined.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
A=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}A^{(i)}\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}
. We have

A^{\perp}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{j}}\right)\otimes\left(A^{(i)}\right)^{\perp}\otimes\left(\bigotimes_{j=i+1}^{r}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{j}}\right).

In particular,
A^{\perp}
is a perfect space.

Proof.

For ease of notation, in the remainder of this proof, we denote by
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
the spaces

\left(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{j}}\right)\otimes A^{(i)}\otimes\left(\bigotimes_{j=i+1}^{r}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{j}}\right)\qquad\textup{ and }\qquad\left(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{j}}\right)\otimes\left(A^{(i)}\right)^{\perp}\otimes\left(\bigotimes_{j=i+1}^{r}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{j}}\right)

respectively, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
. We claim that
X_{i}^{\perp}=Y_{i}
. It is immediate to check that
Y_{i}\subseteq X_{i}^{\perp}
. Moreover, by a dimension argument, we get

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(Y_{i}\right)=(n_{i}-\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(A_{i}\right))\prod_{\tiny\begin{matrix}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{matrix}}^{r}n_{j}=n-\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(A_{i}\right)\prod_{\tiny\begin{matrix}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{matrix}}^{r}n_{j}=n-\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(X_{i}\right)=\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(X_{i}^{\perp}\right)

where the last equality follows from Lemma
3.4
(
2
). This implies the claim. Finally, since
A=\bigcap_{i=1}^{r}X_{i}
, by Lemma
3.4
(
3
), we have
A^{\perp}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}Y_{i}
. That
A^{\perp}
is perfect follows immediately from the fact that
A^{\perp}
is a sum of tensor spaces.
∎

As an immediate consequence of this theorem we have the following characterization of the set
\overline{\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}}
of closure-type dual anticodes.

Corollary 4.13
.

The set
\overline{\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}}
is defined. In particular, we have

\overline{\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{j}}\right)\otimes A^{(i)}\otimes\left(\bigotimes_{j=i+1}^{r}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{j}}\right):A^{(i)}\leq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}}\textup{ for all }i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\right\}.

Proposition 4.14
.

Let
A,B\in\overline{\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}}
. Define
A\vee_{\overline{\textup{cl}}}B
to be
A+B
and
A\wedge_{\overline{\textup{cl}}}B
to be the sum of the elements in
\{C\in\mathcal{B}:C\in A\cap B\}
where

\mathcal{B}:=\left\{\left(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{j}}\right)\otimes A^{(i)}\otimes\left(\bigotimes_{j=i+1}^{r}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{j}}\right):A^{(i)}\leq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}}\textup{ for all }i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\right\}.

We have that
\overline{\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}}
with the operation of
\vee_{\overline{\textup{cl}}}
and
\wedge_{\overline{\textup{cl}}}
is a lattice.

Proof.

It is immediate from the definition that the operations in the statement are well-defined.
∎

The following example demonstrates that
\overline{\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}}
is not a sublattice of

.

Example 4.15
.

Consider the following dual closure-type anticodes of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{3}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{2}^{3}

A:=\left<(0,1,0),(0,0,1)\right>_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{2}^{3}+\mathbb{F}_{2}^{3}\otimes\left<(1,0,0),(0,1,1)\right>_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}\quad\textup{ and }\quad B:=\left<(1,0,0)\right>_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{2}^{3}.

One can check that

A\wedge_{\textup{ps}}B=\left<(1,0,0)\otimes(1,0,0),(1,0,0)\otimes(0,1,1)\right>_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}\neq 0_{\textup{ps}}=0_{\overline{\textup{cl}}}=A\wedge_{\overline{\textup{cl}}}B

where
\wedge_{\textup{ps}}
and
\wedge_{\overline{\textup{cl}}}
, and
0_{\textup{ps}}
and

denote the operation of

and the zero element in the lattices
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{ps}}
and

respectively.

In the following result we give a characterization of the set
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{D}}
.

Theorem 4.16
.

Let
P:=\{i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}:n_{i}=n_{r}\}
. We have

\mathcal{A}^{\textup{D}}=\bigcup_{p\in P}\left\{\left(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{j}}\right)\otimes A^{(i)}\otimes\left(\bigotimes_{j=i+1}^{r}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{j}}\right):A^{(i)}\leq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}},i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\setminus\{p\}\right\}.

Proof.

It was shown in Corollary
4.13
that
\overline{\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}}
is a collection of anticodes and a characterization of it was given. Therefore, an equivalent definition of
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{D}}
is

\mathcal{A}^{\textup{D}}=\{A:A\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}\cap\overline{\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}}\textup{ and }A^{(p)}=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{p}}\textup{ for some }p\in P\}.

It is not hard to check that, by the first part of the proof of Theorem
4.12
,
\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}\cap\overline{\mathcal{A}^{\textup{cl}}}
is the set of all the perfect spaces of the form

Corollary 4.17

Theorem 4.18

Proof.

Let

\mathbb{F}&\textup{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(1)

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a collection of anticodes. For each

j
-th generalized tensor weight
with respect to
\mathcal{A}
is defined to be:

t_{j}(\mathcal{C}):=\min\left\{\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(A\right):A\in\mathcal{A}\mid\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\mathcal{C}\cap A\right)\geq j\right\}.

Furthermore, if
\overline{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\textup{ps}}
then we define the
j
-th generalized dual tensor weight
to be

s_{j}(\mathcal{C}):=\min\left\{\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(A\right):A\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}\mid\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\mathcal{C}\cap A\right)\geq j\right\}.

In the following, we write
t_{j}
,
s_{j}
,
t_{j}^{\perp}
and
s_{j}^{\perp}
instead of
t_{j}(\mathcal{C})
,
s_{j}(\mathcal{C})
,
t_{j}\left(\mathcal{C}^{\perp}\right)
and
s_{j}\left(\mathcal{C}^{\perp}\right)
respectively, for any
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. As we observed in the previous section, in some cases
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
is a collection of anticodes, while in others it is not. Particular formulations of the following result for
r=2
can be read in
[
7
, Proposition 6.3]
and
[
12
, Theorem 30]
. These statements follow directly from Definitions
4.2
and
5.1
.

Proposition 5.2
.

The following hold.

(1)

d\leq t_{1}
and if
\overline{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\textup{ps}}
then
d\leq s_{1}
.

(2)

\textup{trk}(\mathcal{C})\leq t_{k}
and if

then
\textup{trk}(\mathcal{C})\leq s_{k}
.

(3)

for any

.

(4)

s_{j}\leq s_{j+1}
for any

if

.

(5)

t_{j}^{\textup{ps}}\leq t_{j}^{\textup{cl}}\leq t_{j}^{\textup{D}}\leq t_{j}^{\textup{R}}

.

(6)

t_{j}^{\textup{D}}=s_{j}^{\textup{D}}

Proposition 5.3

The following hold.

(2)

(3)

Proposition 5.4

The following hold for

(2)

and such that
\hat{A}\leq A
. We want to show that
\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\hat{A}\cap\mathcal{C}\right)\geq j
. Since
\hat{A}\leq A
we have
\hat{A}\cap\mathcal{C}=\hat{A}\cap(A\cap\mathcal{C})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\hat{A}\cap\mathcal{C}\right)

\displaystyle=\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\hat{A}\cap(A\cap\mathcal{C})\right)

\displaystyle=\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\hat{A}\right)+\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(A\cap\mathcal{C}\right)-\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\hat{A}+(A\cap\mathcal{C})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\hat{A}\right)+\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(A\cap\mathcal{C}\right)-\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(A\right)

\displaystyle\geq\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(A\right)-\frac{n}{n_{1}}+j+\frac{n}{n_{1}}-\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(A\right),

which implies the statement.

(2)

Let
h:=\left\lfloor\frac{n_{1}}{n}(k-j)\right\rfloor
. It follows from (
1
) that
t_{j}^{\textup{R}}+\ell\frac{n}{n_{1}}\leq t_{j+\ell\frac{n}{n_{1}}}^{\textup{R}}
for each
\ell\in[h]
and therefore
t_{j}^{\textup{R}}+h\frac{n}{n_{1}}\leq n
.
The statement now follows.

(3)

If
A\in\mathcal{A}^{\textup{R}}
is such that
\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(A\cap\mathcal{C}\right)\geq j
then
\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(A\right)\geq j
and so (
3
) holds.∎

Theorem 6.4
.

Let

be a family of anticodes whose members form a lattice and let

The following hold for all

(2)

\displaystyle W_{b}^{(j)}(\mathcal{C})=\sum_{a=0}^{b}\mu(a,b)B_{a}^{(j)}(\mathcal{C})\;\left|\left\{(A^{\prime},A)\in\mathcal{A}_{b}\times\mathcal{A}_{a}:A\leq A^{\prime}\right\}\right|

Proof.

(3)

A\leq A^{\prime}\end{matrix}}\mu(\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(A\right),\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(A^{\prime}\right))B_{A}^{(j)}.

We prove the two statement independently.

(1)

By Equation (

\displaystyle B_{a}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{a}}B_{A}^{(j)}=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{a}}\sum_{\tiny\begin{matrix}A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}\\
A^{\prime}\leq A\end{matrix}}W_{A^{\prime}}^{(j)}

Lemma 6.5

Theorem 6.6
