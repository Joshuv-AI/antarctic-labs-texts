Open EFT for Interacting Fermions from Holography
Godwin Martin, Shivam K. Sharma

Abstract
We initiate the study of an open EFT for finite-temperature holographic systems with interacting fermions. In particular, we do this for Yukawa interactions in the bulk using the real-time formalism (grSK geometry). From the bulk perspective, this study corresponds to Yukawa scattering against a black hole, incorporating the effects of Hawking radiation. We derive a field theory in the exterior of the black hole and thus develop a Witten diagrammatic understanding of the scattering processes. This allows the explicit evaluation of boundary Schwinger-Keldysh (SK) correlators to arbitrary order, at tree-level in the bulk. Here we present explicitly the SK generating functional up to four-point functions. Finally, we represent the correlators in a column-vector representation that manifests microscopic unitarity and thermality, with all the statistical factors neatly folded in.

2
Influence Functional and grSK geometry

As discussed in the introduction, we aim to construct an open EFT for a
system
of interacting bosonic and fermionic degrees of freedom coupled to a holographic
thermal bath
. A way of obtaining such an EFT was first given by Feynman and Vernon
Feynman:1963fq
, sometimes dubbed as the
Influence functional formalism
. The basic idea is to double the degrees of freedom to describe the non-unitary evolution of the
system
, which arises by integrating out the
bath
.

The set-up we have is the following: consider a single system degree of freedom
\mathfrak{X}
coupled with many bath degrees of freedom
X_{i}
. The total action of the combined unitary theory is

S_{\text{tot}}[\mathfrak{X},X_{i}]=\ S_{\text{s}}[\mathfrak{X}]+S_{\text{b}}[X_{i}]+S_{\text{s-b}}[\mathfrak{X},X_{i}]\ .

(1)

where
S_{\text{s}}
,
S_{\text{b}}
and
S_{\text{s-b}}
are parts of the action describing the system, the bath and the interaction between them respectively. Integrating out the bath degrees of freedom
X_{i}
, we obtain the non-unitary theory for the system. In the influence functional language, the path integral heuristically becomes

\int\mathcal{D}\mathfrak{X}\,\mathcal{D}X_{i}\,\exp\bigg(iS_{\text{tot}}[\mathfrak{X},X_{i}]\bigg)\mapsto\int\mathcal{D}\mathfrak{X}_{{}_{\text{R}}}\,\mathcal{D}\mathfrak{X}_{{}_{\text{L}}}\exp\bigg(iS_{\text{s}}[\mathfrak{X}_{{}_{\text{R}}}]-iS_{\text{s}}[\mathfrak{X}_{{}_{\text{L}}}]+iS_{\text{IF}}[\mathfrak{X}_{{}_{\text{R}}},\mathfrak{X}_{{}_{\text{L}}}]\bigg)\ ,

(2)

where
\mathfrak{X}_{{}_{\text{R}}}
and
\mathfrak{X}_{{}_{\text{L}}}
are two copies of system degrees of freedom and
S_{\text{IF}}[\mathfrak{X}_{{}_{\text{R}}},\mathfrak{X}_{{}_{\text{L}}}]
is the
influence phase
, containing the influence of the bath on the system.

The influence phase generates the real-time correlators of the operator
\mathcal{O}(X_{i})
coupled to the system degree of freedom
\mathfrak{X}
.

3
Yukawa theory in grSK

We will now consider one of the simplest theories involving Dirac spinors, i.e., Yukawa theory. For simplicity, here we will be concerned with a massless Dirac spinor interacting with a massless scalar through the Yukawa interaction. Furthermore, both of these will be minimally coupled to the grSK spacetime, which itself will be non-dynamical.

Yukawa interactions in the bulk are described by the action

S=S_{\rm free}+S_{\rm int}\ ,

(10)

where
S_{\rm free}
is the sum of the free massless Dirac action and the free massless scalar action,
and
S_{\rm int}
is the Yukawa interaction term defined as

S_{\rm int}=-i\oint\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\zeta\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d}x\sqrt{-g}\ \lambda\phi\bar{\Psi}\Psi\ .

(11)

Here
\lambda
is the Yukawa coupling constant that controls the strength of the coupling between the Dirac spinor
\Psi
and the scalar
\phi
. The symbol
g
denotes the determinant of the grSK metric. The explicit expression of the free part of the above action will be described in detail below.

Before proceeding to understand this action and its consequences, we will first focus on the free part. We will describe scalar and spinor propagation in the grSK geometry without any interaction terms between them. This will also allow us to set up necessary notation for the later sections. Later in this section, as well as in the next section, we will use this notation to describe the Yukawa interaction in detail.

3.1
Free scalar theory

We start with scalar propagation in the grSK geometry, i.e., we consider the scalar part of the free action given above. We will see how to solve the Klein-Gordon equation that comes from varying this action. The important lesson here is that once we have one solution to the Klein-Gordon equation, we then can use the time-reversal involution
Loganayagam:2020eue
of the grSK geometry to generate another independent solution. We will see this in more detail soon.

The free scalar field is described by the action

S_{\phi}=\ -\oint\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\zeta\int\mathrm{d}^{d}x\sqrt{-g}\ \frac{1}{2}g^{MN}\partial_{M}\phi\partial_{N}\phi\ ,

(12)

where
g^{MN}
is the metric describing the grSK spacetime, and
g
is the determinant of this metric. Varying the above action, we obtain the equation of motion

\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{M}\left(\sqrt{-g}g^{MN}\partial_{N}\right)\phi=0\ .

(13)

The above partial differential equation can be reduced to a second-order ordinary differential equation in the radial coordinate by passing to the Fourier domain in the boundary coordinates. In other words, if we decompose the field
\phi
as

\phi(\zeta,x)\equiv\int_{k}e^{ikx}\phi(\zeta,k)\ ,\qquad\int_{k}\equiv\int\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\ ,

(14)

then we obtain the equation of motion

\mathbb{D}_{+}\left(r^{d-1}\mathbb{D}_{+}\phi\right)+\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}r^{d-1}\left(f|\mathbf{k}|^{2}-(k^{0})^{2}\right)\phi=0\ ,

(15)

where
\mathbb{D}_{+}
is the radial derivative operator defined as

\mathbb{D}_{+}\equiv\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\zeta}+\frac{\beta k^{0}}{2}\ .

(16)

Note that we use the same symbol for the field in the real domain as well as the Fourier domain. This should not cause any confusion since the arguments of the field will differentiate between them.

The above second-order ordinary differential equation has two independent solutions. The solution
G^{\rm in}(\zeta,k)
that satisfies the boundary conditions

G^{\rm in}(\zeta,k)\Big|_{\zeta=0}=1=G^{\rm in}(\zeta,k)\Big|_{\zeta=1}

(17)

is called the
ingoing boundary-to-bulk
Green function. Given this ingoing solution, we can generate another independent solution, the
outgoing boundary-to-bulk
Green function, by using the time-reversal involution of the grSK geometry

G^{\rm out}(\zeta,k)=e^{-\beta k^{0}\zeta}G^{\rm in}(\zeta,-k)\ .

(18)

With these two independent solutions, the most general solution of the Klein-Gordon equation satisfying the boundary conditions

\phi(\zeta,k)\Big|_{\zeta=0}=J_{{}_{\text{L}}}(k)\ ,\quad\phi(\zeta,k)\Big|_{\zeta=1}=J_{{}_{\text{R}}}(k)\ ,

(19)

takes the form

\phi(\zeta,k)=-G^{\rm in}(\zeta,k)J_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k)+e^{\beta k^{0}}G^{\rm out}J_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k)\ ,

(20)

where
J_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}
and
J_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}
are the future and the past sources defined as

\begin{split}J_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k)&\equiv-(1+n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}})J_{{}_{\text{R}}}(k)+n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}}J_{{}_{\text{L}}}(k)\ ,\\
J_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k)&\equiv-n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}}\left[J_{{}_{\text{R}}}(k)-J_{{}_{\text{L}}}(k)\right]\ ,\end{split}

(21)

respectively. This form of the solution with the ingoing and the outgoing propagators was first explored in
Son:2009vu
and later detailed for the grSK geometry in
Chakrabarty:2019aeu
;
Jana:2020vyx
.
The symbol
n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}}
denotes the Bose-Einstein statistical factor

n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}}\equiv\frac{1}{e^{\beta k^{0}}-1}\ .

(22)

We will also later find it convenient to write the above solution in the right-left basis of the sources as

\begin{split}\phi(\zeta,k)&\equiv\ g_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,k)\,J_{{}_{\text{R}}}(k)-g_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta,k)\,J_{{}_{\text{L}}}(k)\ ,\end{split}

(23)

where
g_{{}_{\text{R}},{}_{\text{L}}}
denote the boundary-to-bulk Green functions from the right and the left AdS boundaries, which in terms of the ingoing and the outgoing Green functions take the form

\begin{split}&g_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,k)\equiv\left(1+n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}}\right)\left[G^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)-G^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k)\right]\ ,\\
&g_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta,k)\equiv n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}}\left[G^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)-e^{\beta k^{0}}G^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k)\right]\ .\end{split}

(24)

Free Dirac theory

Next, we come to free Dirac spinor propagation in the grSK geometry. The free Dirac action takes the form

S_{\Psi}=\ i\oint\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\zeta\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d}x\sqrt{-g}\,\bar{\Psi}\Gamma^{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle A$}}D_{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle A$}}\Psi+i\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,\bar{\Psi}\mathbb{P}_{-}\Psi\Bigg|_{\zeta=0}^{\zeta=1}\ .

;
Henneaux:1998ch
.

Varying the above action with respect to
\overline{\Psi}
gives us the Dirac equation

\Gamma^{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle A$}}D_{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle A$}}\Psi=0\ .

(27)

Passing to the Fourier domain in the boundary coordinates, as we did for the scalar field in Eq. (
14
), the Dirac equation becomes a first-order ordinary differential equation in the radial coordinate:

\begin{split}\left\{\frac{\mathbb{T}(\zeta)}{\sqrt{f}}\partial_{\zeta}+\mathbb{\Gamma}\left(\beta k^{0}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\zeta}\ln f\right)-\frac{\beta}{2}\Gamma^{(i)}k_{i}\right\}\left(r^{d/2}\Psi(\zeta,k)\right)=0\ .\end{split}

(28)

Here
\mathbb{\Gamma}
and
\mathbb{T}
are linear combinations of bulk gamma matrices defined as

\mathbb{\Gamma}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(\Gamma^{(\zeta)}+\Gamma^{(v)}\right)\ ,\quad\mathbb{T}(\zeta)\equiv\sqrt{f}\mathbb{\Gamma}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{f}}\mathbb{\Gamma}\ .

(29)

In the case of Dirac spinors as well, we would like to follow the same line of logic as we did for scalars to find the solutions. But here there are new important features we have to keep in mind. Primarily, the equation of motion is now a first-order equation. Secondly, the field we are solving for is a spinor, i.e., it has a spinorial index.

Due to the latter feature, the boundary conditions of the bulk field are slightly more complicated. This is because in general dimensions, the boundary spinor and the bulk spinor do not have the same number of components. If
\psi_{{}_{\text{R}}}
and
\psi_{{}_{\text{L}}}
are spinors on the right and the left boundary respectively, the boundary conditions on the bulk spinor
\Psi
take the form

\lim_{\zeta\to 0}r^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathbb{P}_{+}S_{{}_{0}}\Psi(\zeta,k)=\mathbb{P}_{+}S_{{}_{0}}\psi_{{}_{\text{L}}}(k)\ ,\qquad\qquad\lim_{\zeta\to 1}r^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathbb{P}_{+}S_{{}_{0}}\Psi(\zeta,k)=\mathbb{P}_{+}S_{{}_{0}}\psi_{{}_{\text{R}}}(k)\ .

(30)

Here
S_{0}
is a constant rectangular matrix defined in
Loganayagam:2020eue
. Notice that since the Dirac equation is first-order in derivatives, the double Dirichlet boundary conditions can be imposed only after projecting with an appropriate projection matrix, which in the present case is
\mathbb{P}_{{}_{+}}
.

We can now proceed as in the scalar case by first introducing the ingoing boundary-to-bulk Green function
S^{\rm in}
, which is a solution of the Dirac equation and satisfies the boundary conditions

\lim_{\zeta\to 0}r^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathbb{P}_{+}S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)=\mathbb{P}_{+}S_{{}_{0}}=\lim_{\zeta\to 1}r^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathbb{P}_{+}S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)\ .

(31)

Given the ingoing Green function, once again the time-reversal involution of the grSK geometry produces outgoing Green function
S^{\rm out}
given by

S^{\rm out}(\zeta,k)\equiv e^{-\beta k^{0}\zeta}\mathbb{T}(\zeta)S^{\rm in}(\zeta,-k)\ .

(32)

The most general solution of the Dirac equation satisfying the boundary conditions in Eq.(
30
) can then be written as

\Psi(\zeta,k)=\ -S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)\psi_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k)-e^{\beta k^{0}}S^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k)\psi_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k)\ .

(33)

Here
\psi_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k)
and
\psi_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k)
are the boundary sources in the past-future basis defined as

\psi_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k)\equiv\ n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}\left[\psi_{{}_{\text{R}}}(k)-\psi_{{}_{\text{L}}}(k)\right]-\psi_{{}_{\text{R}}}(k)\ ,\qquad\psi_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k)\equiv\ n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}\left[\psi_{{}_{\text{R}}}(k)-\psi_{{}_{\text{L}}}(k)\right]\ ,

(34)

where
n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}
is the Fermi-Dirac statistical factor

n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}\equiv\frac{1}{e^{\beta k^{0}}+1}\ .

(35)

We will also find it useful to recast the above solution in the right-left basis of the boundary sources:

\Psi(\zeta,k)\equiv s_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,k)\ \psi_{{}_{\text{R}}}(k)-s_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta,k)\ \psi_{{}_{\text{L}}}(k)\

(36)

where
s_{{}_{\text{R}}}
and
s_{{}_{\text{L}}}
denote the boundary-to-bulk Green functions from the right and the left AdS boundaries, respectively. In terms of the ingoing and the outgoing Green functions, these take the form

\begin{split}&s_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,k)\equiv\ \left(1-n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}\right)\left[S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)-S^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k)\right]\ ,\\
&s_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta,k)\equiv\ -n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)-\left(1-n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}\right)S^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k)\ .\end{split}

(37)

The Dirac conjugate solutions can also be arranged in the same form as in Eq.(
33
). Thus, the conjugate solutions have a very similar structure as those given above. We refer the reader to Appendix (
A
) for more details.

3.3
Yukawa theory

We now have all the basic setup and notation we require to discuss Yukawa theory in the grSK spacetime. The action we consider is the sum of the free actions we have described in the previous subsections as well as the Yukawa interaction term:

S=S_{\phi}+S_{\Psi}-i\oint\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\zeta\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d}x\sqrt{-g}\ \lambda\phi\bar{\Psi}\Psi\ .

(38)

Varying the above action gives Klein-Gordon and Dirac equations that are sourced by terms linear in the Yukawa coupling:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle B$}}\left(\sqrt{-g}g^{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle A$}\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle B$}}\partial_{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle A$}}\phi\right)=i\lambda\bar{\Psi}\Psi\ \qquad\text{and}\qquad\Gamma^{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle A$}}D_{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle A$}}\Psi=\lambda\phi\Psi\ .

(39)

These equations can be solved perturbatively in the Yukawa coupling
\lambda
. In other words, we can express the solutions as

\Psi=\Psi_{(0)}+\lambda\Psi_{(1)}+\lambda^{2}\Psi_{(2)}+\ldots\ ,

(40)

\phi=\phi_{(0)}+\lambda\phi_{(1)}+\lambda^{2}\phi_{(2)}+\ldots\ .

(41)

The leading-order solutions
\Psi_{(0)}
and
\phi_{(0)}
are just the free theory solutions that we have seen in detail in the previous subsections. We will take these solutions to satisfy the GKPW boundary conditions we require. Thus the higher-order terms in the solutions will be normalisable on both the boundaries. To obtain the higher-order solutions, we will now need to develop a perturbation theory to solve sourced Klein-Gordon and Dirac equations.

We will solve this problem using the Green function technique. First we define the binormalisable bulk-to-bulk Green functions. These are objects that are normalisable on both the boundaries of the grSK spacetime and satisfy the equations of motion with a Dirac-delta source in the bulk. More precisely, the scalar binormalisable Green function
\mathbb{G}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)
satisfies

\left(-\Box+m^{2}\right)\mathbb{G}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)=\frac{\delta(\zeta-\zeta_{0})}{\sqrt{-g}}\ ,

(42)

where
\delta(\zeta-\zeta_{0})
denotes the radial delta function on the grSK contour. The above differential equation for the massless case, can be explicitly written as

\mathbb{D}_{+}\left(r^{d-1}\mathbb{D}_{+}\mathbb{G}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)\right)+\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}r^{d-1}\left(f|\mathbf{k}|^{2}-(k^{0})^{2}\right)\mathbb{G}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\frac{i\beta}{2}\delta(\zeta-\zeta_{0})\ ,

(43)

supplemented with the following boundary conditions

\lim_{\zeta\to 0}\mathbb{G}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)=\lim_{\zeta\to 1}\mathbb{G}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)=0\ .

(44)

Similarly, the spinor bulk-to-bulk Green function
\mathbb{S}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)
is a square matrix in the bulk-spinor space that satisfies

(-\Gamma^{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle A$}}D_{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle A$}}+m)\mathbb{S}(X|X^{\prime})=\frac{\delta^{D}(X-X^{\prime})}{\sqrt{-g}}\mathbb{1}\ .

(45)

Written explicitly for the massless case, we have

\begin{split}\left\{\frac{\mathbb{T}(\zeta)}{\sqrt{f}}\partial_{\zeta}+\mathbb{\Gamma}\left(\beta k^{0}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\zeta}\ln f\right)-\frac{\beta}{2}\Gamma^{(i)}k_{i}\right\}\left(r^{d/2}\mathbb{S}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)\right)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\mathbb{1}\frac{\delta(\zeta-\zeta_{0})}{r^{d/2}f}\ ,\end{split}

(46)

where
\mathbb{1}
is the identity in the bulk spinor space, along with the boundary conditions

\lim_{\zeta\to 0}r^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathbb{P}_{+}S_{{}_{0}}\mathbb{S}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)=0\ ,\qquad\qquad\lim_{\zeta\to 1}r^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathbb{P}_{+}S_{{}_{0}}\mathbb{S}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)=0\ .

(47)

An explicit derivation of the scalar bulk-to-bulk Green function can be found in
Loganayagam:2024mnj
. As for the spinor bulk-to-bulk Green function, explicit expressions in terms of the boundary-to-bulk Green functions can be found in Appendix (
B
), where we derive the Green function along similar lines as in
Faulkner:2013bna
, using the technique of Wronskians.

With the bulk-to-bulk Green functions in our hands, we can immediately write down expressions for the higher-order terms in the perturbative expansion of the solutions. For all
n>0
, we have

\Psi_{(n)}(\zeta,k)=\oint_{\zeta}\ \mathbb{S}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)\mathfrak{J}_{(n)}(\zeta_{0},k)\ ,\quad\text{and}\quad\phi_{(n)}(\zeta,k)=\oint_{\zeta}\ \mathbb{G}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)\mathbb{J}_{(n)}(\zeta_{0},k)\ ,

(48)

where
\mathfrak{J}_{(n)}(\zeta_{0},k)
and
\mathbb{J}_{(n)}(\zeta_{0},k)
are sources for the
n
th order term in the solutions. These can be read off from the equations of motion. For example, at first order we have

\begin{split}\mathfrak{J}_{(1)}(\zeta_{0},k)&\equiv{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\int_{k_{1}}\int_{k_{2}}(2\pi)^{d}\delta^{(d)}(k_{1}+k_{2}-k)\phi_{(0)}(\zeta_{0},k_{1})\Psi_{(0)}(\zeta_{0},k_{2})\ ,\\
\mathbb{J}_{(1)}(\zeta_{0},k)&\equiv{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}i\int_{k_{1}}\int_{k_{2}}(2\pi)^{d}\delta^{(d)}(k_{1}+k_{2}-k)\overline{\Psi}_{(0)}(\zeta_{0},k_{1})\Psi_{(0)}(\zeta_{0},k_{2})\ .\end{split}

(49)

Similarly, at second-order,

\begin{split}\mathfrak{J}_{(2)}(\zeta_{0},k)&\equiv{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\int_{k_{1}}\int_{k_{2}}(2\pi)^{d}\delta^{(d)}(k_{1}+k_{2}-k)\left[\phi_{(0)}(\zeta_{0},k_{1})\Psi_{(1)}(\zeta_{0},k_{2})+\phi_{(1)}(\zeta_{0},k_{1})\Psi_{(0)}(\zeta_{0},k_{2})\right]\ ,\\
\mathbb{J}_{(2)}(\zeta_{0},k)&\equiv{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}i\int_{k_{1}}\int_{k_{2}}(2\pi)^{d}\delta^{(d)}(k_{1}+k_{2}-k)\left[\overline{\Psi}_{(0)}(\zeta_{0},k_{1})\Psi_{(1)}(\zeta_{0},k_{2})+\overline{\Psi}_{(1)}(\zeta_{0},k_{1})\Psi_{(0)}(\zeta_{0},k_{2})\right]\ .\end{split}

(50)

4
Yukawa theory and exterior EFT from grSK

We now come to the influence phase for Yukawa theory. In the previous section, we have detailed how to obtain the solutions to the bulk equations of motion to arbitrary orders in the Yukawa coupling constant. Substituting these solutions into the action will then give us the tree-level influence phase up to any desired order.

We will arrange this expansion with the notation

S_{\text{on-shell}}=S_{(2)}+\lambda S_{(3)}+\lambda^{2}S_{(4)}+\lambda^{3}S_{(5)}+\ldots\ ,

(51)

where
S_{\text{on-shell}}
is the full on-shell action. On the RHS, we have employed a notation wherein the subscripts indicate the number of boundary sources in the term. Thus, for example,
S_{(2)}
is quadratic in the boundary sources and takes the form given in Eq. (
137
).

The term linear in the coupling
\lambda
is also cubic in the boundary sources and takes the form

S_{(3)}=-i\int_{k_{1,2,3}}\oint_{\zeta}\overline{\Psi}_{(0)}(\zeta,k_{1})\phi_{(0)}(\zeta,k_{2})\Psi_{(0)}(\zeta,k_{3})\ .

(52)

An explicit derivation of this can be found in Appendix
D
.
Here we have introduced a useful notation for the boundary momentum integrals, that we will employ throughout the rest of this note,

\int_{k_{1,2,\ldots,n}}\equiv\int\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d}k_{1}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d}k_{2}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\ldots\int\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d}k_{n}}{(2\pi)^{d}}(2\pi)^{d}\delta^{d}(k_{1}+k_{2}+\ldots+k_{n})\ .

(53)

The reader will notice that the three-point influence phase can be directly obtained from the free solutions in the usual Witten-diagram manner with the diagrams drawn on the full grSK contour. We will soon perform the monodromy integrals and see how this answer can be arranged in terms of Witten diagrams on one copy of the spacetime (instead of two, as in the grSK prescription).

The next correction, i.e., the term quadratic in the interaction parameter
\lambda
, which is quartic in the boundary sources, takes the form

\begin{split}S_{(4)}=\ &{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\frac{1}{2}\int_{k_{1,2,3,4}}\oint_{\zeta_{1}}\oint_{\zeta_{2}}\overline{\Psi}_{(0)}(\zeta_{2},k_{1})\Psi_{(0)}(\zeta_{2},k_{2})\mathbb{G}(\zeta_{2}|\zeta_{1},k_{3}+k_{4})\overline{\Psi}_{(0)}(\zeta_{1},k_{3})\Psi_{(0)}(\zeta_{1},k_{4})\\
&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}-i\int_{k_{1,2,3,4}}\oint_{\zeta_{1}}\oint_{\zeta_{2}}\phi_{(0)}(\zeta_{2},k_{1})\overline{\Psi}_{(0)}(\zeta_{2},k_{2})\mathbb{S}(\zeta_{2}|\zeta_{1},k_{3}+k_{4})\phi_{(0)}(\zeta_{1},k_{3})\Psi_{(0)}(\zeta_{1},k_{4})\ .\end{split}

(54)

Once again, an explicit derivation of the above results can be found in Appendix
D
.

We have seen above the three-point and the four-point influence phase written as integrals over the full grSK contour. We will now perform these monodromy integrals. To this end, let us focus on one particular term in the three-point influence phase. It will be clear from this discussion how the other terms can be similarly computed. We focus on the term with two future sources and one past source, each of which can be a scalar, a spinor, or its conjugate. Thus we expect three terms at the end. Starting from Eq. (
52
) and extracting the desired term, we have

\begin{split}S_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}&=-i\int_{k_{1,2,3}}\oint_{\zeta}\Bigg\{\left[-\overline{\psi}_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k_{1})\overline{S}^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k_{1})\right]\left[-G^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k_{2})J_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k_{2})\right]\left[-e^{\beta k^{0}_{3}}S^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k_{3})\psi_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k_{3})\right]\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt+\left[-\overline{\psi}_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k_{1})\overline{S}^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k_{1})\right]\left[e^{\beta k^{0}_{2}}G^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k_{2})J_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k_{2})\right]\left[-S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k_{3})\psi_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k_{3})\right]\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt+\left[-e^{\beta k^{0}_{1}}\overline{\psi}_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k_{1})\overline{S}^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k_{1})\right]\left[-G^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k_{2})J_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k_{2})\right]\left[-S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k_{3})\psi_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k_{3})\right]\Bigg\}\ .\end{split}

(55)

The ingoing Green function is analytic on the grSK contour and simply goes for a ride in the monodromy integral. The only non-analyticities in the above expression come from the outgoing Green functions. These come from the factors of
\sqrt{f}
and
e^{-\beta k^{0}\zeta}
. Noting that
\sqrt{f}
flips in sign while going from one branch of the contour to the other, and
\zeta
jumps by unity, we get

\oint_{\zeta}e^{-\beta k^{0}\zeta}f^{j/2}\mathcal{F}(\zeta)=\int_{\rm ext}e^{-\beta k^{0}\zeta}f^{j/2}\mathcal{F}(\zeta)\times\begin{cases}\frac{-1}{1+n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}}}&\text{for}\ j=0\\
\frac{-1}{1-n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}}&\text{for}\ j=1\end{cases}\ ,

(56)

where
\mathcal{F}(\zeta)
is an analytic function and we have chosen to write the integral over the left branch of the grSK contour with
\int_{\rm ext}
defined as

\int_{\rm ext}\equiv\int_{r_{h}}^{r_{c}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\ r^{d-1}\ .

(57)

Using this result, we obtain

\begin{split}S_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}&=-i\int_{k_{1,2,3}}\int_{\rm ext}\bigg\{\frac{1}{n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k_{3}^{0}}}\left[\overline{\psi}_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k_{1})\overline{S}^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k_{1})\right]\left[G^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k_{2})J_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k_{2})\right]\left[S^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k_{3})\psi_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k_{3})\right]\\
&\hskip 79.6678pt-\frac{1}{n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k_{2}^{0}}}\left[\overline{\psi}_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k_{1})\overline{S}^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k_{1})\right]\left[G^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k_{2})J_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k_{2})\right]\left[S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k_{3})\psi_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k_{3})\right]\\
&\hskip 79.6678pt+\frac{1}{n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k_{1}^{0}}}\left[\overline{\psi}_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k_{1})\overline{S}^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k_{1})\right]\left[G^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k_{2})J_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k_{2})\right]\left[S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k_{3})\psi_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k_{3})\right]\bigg\}\ ,\end{split}

(58)

Note also that none of the functions in the integrand have poles at the horizon (which can be gleaned from the explicit expressions of the Green functions in
Jana:2020vyx
;
Loganayagam:2020eue
), and thus there are no horizon-localised contributions.

We can now perform similar computations for all the other terms in the three-point influence phase. The term with only future sources vanishes, i.e.,
S_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}=0
, which is simply the SK collapse condition at the three-point level. This is because the integrand is completely analytic and thus the monodromy integral vanishes. Similarly, the term with all past sources also vanishes, i.e.,
S_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}=0
, which is the KMS condition at the three-point level. This is because each term has an exponential factor, all of which multiply to give unity by momentum conservation, and a factor of
f
which is analytic around
r=r_{h}
. Therefore, the only other non-vanishing term in the three-point influence phase is the term with two past sources. This term looks like

\begin{split}S_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}&=-i\int_{k_{1,2,3}}\int_{\rm ext}\bigg\{-\frac{1}{n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k_{2}^{0}+k_{3}^{0}}}\left[\overline{\psi}_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k_{1})\overline{S}^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k_{1})\right]\left[G^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k_{2})J_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k_{2})\right]\left[S^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k_{3})\psi_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k_{3})\right]\\
&\hskip 85.35826pt+\frac{1}{n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k_{1}^{0}+k_{3}^{0}}}\left[\overline{\psi}_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k_{1})\overline{S}^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k_{1})\right]\left[G^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k_{2})J_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k_{2})\right]\left[S^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k_{3})\psi_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k_{3})\right]\\
&\hskip 85.35826pt-\frac{1}{n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k_{1}^{0}+k_{2}^{0}}}\left[\overline{\psi}_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k_{1})\overline{S}^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k_{1})\right]\left[G^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k_{2})J_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k_{2})\right]\left[S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k_{3})\psi_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k_{3})\right]\bigg\}\ .\end{split}

(59)

Thus the full three-point influence phase takes the form

S_{(3)}=S_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}+S_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}\ .

(60)

We can compute the higher point contributions to the influence phase using similar arguments as above. Rather than illustrating explicit calculations, we will introduce a series of diagrammatic Feynman rules. Through these rules, the reader can determine the on-shell action at any desired order.

When statistical factors are functions of sum over momenta of two (more generally even) fermionic legs, one effectively gets a bosonic statistical factor.

Column vector representation of Yukawa influence phase

In this section, we will express the three-point and the four-point Yukawa influence phase in a convenient notation, i.e., the
column vector
representation. The column vector representation naturally gives the spectral functions in the real-time formalism
Chaudhuri:2018ihk
. This is not surprising as real-time correlators admit Källén-Lehmann-like spectral representations
Evans:1990hy
;
Evans:1991ky
. It has been very useful in perturbative techniques, computation of transport coefficients, etc.
Henning:1993gh
;
Carrington:2006xj
.

The column vector representation manifestly incorporates the microscopic unitarity and the thermality of the system as shown in
Chaudhuri:2018ihk
. Here, the authors showed how thermal bosonic Out-of-Time-Ordered Correlators (OTOCs) can be organised in spectral representations using column vectors. While for scalar holographic theories, it has been shown that the same results can be obtained from the bulk
Loganayagam:2024mnj
, the generalisation to include spinors has not been done so far. From a boundary perspective, we anticipate the existence of a similar representation for fermions. Here, we aim to demonstrate that this expectation is indeed supported by the bulk side as well.

We now turn our attention to the explicit construction of the column vector representation. In this representation, boundary scalar sources are expressed as

\begin{split}&J_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k)\equiv\mathbf{J}(k)\cdot e_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}^{{}_{(1)}}(\bar{k})\ ,\qquad J_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k)\equiv\mathbf{J}(k)\cdot e_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}^{{}_{(1)}}(\bar{k})\ ,\end{split}

(69)

where we have employed the notation
\bar{k}\equiv-k
to avoid clutter, and the symbol
\mathbf{J}(k)
denotes the array of boundary scalar sources:

\mathbf{J}(k)\equiv\begin{pmatrix}J_{{}_{\text{R}}}(k)&\quad-J_{{}_{\text{L}}}(k)\end{pmatrix}\ .

(70)

Furthermore,
e_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k)
and
e_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k)
are column vectors of the form

e_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}^{{}_{(1)}}(k)\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1+n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}}\\
1+n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}}\end{pmatrix}\ ,\quad\text{and}\quad e_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}^{{}_{(1)}}(k)\equiv\begin{pmatrix}n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}}\\
1+n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}}\end{pmatrix}\ .

(71)

Similarly, we now introduce the column vector representation for spinors. In column vector notation, the boundary spinor sources are

\begin{split}&\psi_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k)\equiv\boldsymbol{\psi}(k)\cdot f_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}^{{}_{(1)}}(\bar{k})\ ,\qquad\psi_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k)\equiv\boldsymbol{\psi}(k)\cdot f_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}^{{}_{(1)}}(\bar{k})\ ,\\
&\overline{\psi}_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}(k)\equiv\boldsymbol{\overline{\psi}}(k)\cdot f_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}^{{}_{(1)}}(\bar{k})\ ,\qquad\overline{\psi}_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}(k)\equiv\boldsymbol{\overline{\psi}}(k)\cdot f_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}^{{}_{(1)}}(\bar{k})\ ,\end{split}

(72)

where
\boldsymbol{\psi}(k)
and
\boldsymbol{\overline{\psi}}(k)
are arrays of boundary spinor sources defined as

\boldsymbol{\psi}(k)\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{{}_{\text{R}}}(k)&\quad-\psi_{{}_{\text{L}}}(k)\end{pmatrix}\ ,\qquad\boldsymbol{\overline{\psi}}(k)\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\psi}_{{}_{\text{R}}}(k)&\quad-\overline{\psi}_{{}_{\text{L}}}(k)\end{pmatrix}\ .

(73)

Here again,
f_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}^{{}_{(1)}}
and
f_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}^{{}_{(1)}}
are two-dimensional column vectors whose explicit expressions are

f_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}^{{}_{(1)}}(k)\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1-n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}\\
1-n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}\end{pmatrix}\ ,\quad f_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}^{{}_{(1)}}(k)\equiv\begin{pmatrix}-n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}\\
1-n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}\end{pmatrix}\ .

(74)

For convenience, we also define a second set of column vectors as

\begin{split}e_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}^{{}_{(2)}}(k)&\equiv e^{-\beta k^{0}}e_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}}}^{{}_{(1)}}(k)\ ,\qquad e_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}^{{}_{(2)}}(k)\equiv e^{-\beta k^{0}}e_{\bar{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle F$}}}^{{}_{(1)}}(k)\ ,\\

(75)

Note that the column vectors for spinors can be obtained from the scalar column vectors by replacing the Bose-Einstein factor

with the negative of the Fermi-Dirac factor
-n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}

e^{-\beta k^{0}}
by

6
Discussion

In this work, we have constructed an open effective field theory for a finite-temperature holographic system comprising interacting fermions. This was achieved by employing the gravitational dual of the Schwinger-Keldysh formalism. While this formalism had been previously used for free fermions
Loganayagam:2020eue
;
Loganayagam:2020iol
, our contribution lies in its application to interacting fermions for the first time.

In the bulk, our framework derives an exterior field theory in a black hole background. The setup in the bulk involves a massless scalar and a massless Dirac field with a Yukawa interaction in the AdS black hole background. Additionally, we have developed Witten diagrammatics for this exterior field theory which allows us to write down the Schwinger-Keldysh correlators of the boundary theory. We have provided explicit expressions for these correlators up to four-point functions, at tree level in the bulk, using Witten diagrams. However, the Witten diagrammatics we provide can be used to compute the tree-level influence phase to arbitrary orders in the Yukawa coupling constant.

Another contribution of our work is the derivation of the column vector representation for fermions. This representation facilitates the concise expression of correlators wherein the Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) conditions and the Schwinger-Keldysh collapse conditions become manifest.

This work has many clear future directions. One of them is to understand quantum electrodynamics (QED) in the gravitational Schwinger-Keldysh geometry. Now that we understand how to deal with fermions interacting with scalars, this will be the next natural step. The gauge field of QED can be decomposed into (designer) scalars as in
Ghosh:2020lel
, and the QED vertex will reduce to interactions between these scalars and the fermions. Thus, it will be interesting to see how our work generalises to the case of designer scalars, and thus gauge fields. We intend to explore this question in the future.

Physically, in the bulk, this corresponds to the question of photons and matter scattering against each other in a black hole background. More precisely, how does the Hawking radiation of the black hole, comprising photons and matter, affect the scattering of particles thrown in from the boundary? This question becomes particularly interesting in the case of charged black holes
Loganayagam:2020iol
, since now the black hole couples to fields not only gravitationally but also electromagnetically.

Another future direction is to study the divergences present in the many bulk integrals in this work. An interesting question would be to ask whether the prescription of source-renormalisation, first detailed in
Jana:2020vyx
, becomes important in the present case. This will be an independent check of the source-renormalisation prescription.

Throughout this work, we have restricted ourselves to computing the bulk on-shell action, which is equivalent to the tree-level influence phase. It would be interesting to explore the loop contributions in the bulk. These loop corrections correspond to the large-
N
corrections in the boundary field theory. We have some preliminary results in this direction which we hope to present soon.

It is also interesting to ask how much of the analysis presented here generalises to the case of de Sitter spacetime. In the spirit of
Sleight:2021plv
;
Schaub:2023scu
;
Bhattacharya:2023twz
, we ask what our analysis teaches us about cosmological correlators in the Schwinger-Keldysh formalism.

Following this discussion on scalars, we will come to a detailed derivation of the spinor bulk-to-bulk propagator in the grSK geometry.

Scalar bulk-to-bulk propagator

The scalar bulk-to-bulk propagator
\mathbb{G}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)
normalised to unity at the left and the right boundary (henceforth binormalisable) is given by

\mathbb{G}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\frac{e^{\beta k^{0}\zeta_{0}}}{1+n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}}}\frac{g_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta_{>},k)g_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta_{<},k)}{K^{\text{in}}(k)-K^{\text{in}}(-k)}\ .

(93)

Here the symbols
\zeta_{<}
and
\zeta_{>}
are defined by

\zeta_{<}\equiv\Bigl\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\zeta&\text{if $\zeta$ comes before $\zeta_{0}$ on the grSK contour}\\
\zeta_{0}&\text{if $\zeta_{0}$ comes before $\zeta$ on the grSK contour}\ ,\end{array}

(94)

and

\zeta_{>}\equiv\Bigl\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\zeta&\text{if $\zeta$ comes after $\zeta_{0}$ on the grSK contour}\\
\zeta_{0}&\text{if $\zeta_{0}$ comes after $\zeta$ on the grSK contour}\ .\end{array}

(95)

Since the bulk-to-bulk propagator has to solve the free Klein-Gordon equation away from the sources, it is clear that it has to be proportional to the boundary-to-bulk propagators. Left and right-normalisability then fixes this combination of the boundary-to-bulk propagators to be
g_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta_{>},k)g_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta_{<},k)
. The other factors in the above expression must be fixed using the discontinuity condition coming from the delta function source in the Klein-Gordon equation. Here,
K^{\text{in}}
is the retarded boundary two-point correlator, obtained by taking the boundary limit of the ingoing boundary-to-bulk propagator as shown in
Ghosh:2020lel
.

Apart from the binormalisable bulk-to-bulk propagator, we also require the retarded and the advanced bulk-to-bulk propagators, given by

\begin{split}\mathbb{G}_{\text{ret}}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)&=\frac{e^{\beta k^{0}\zeta_{0}}}{1+n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}}}\frac{G^{\text{in}}(\zeta_{>},k)g_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta_{<},k)}{K^{\text{in}}(k)-K^{\text{in}}(-k)}\ ,\\
\mathbb{G}_{\text{adv}}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)&=\frac{e^{\beta k^{0}\zeta_{0}}}{1+n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}}}\frac{G^{\text{out}}(\zeta_{>},k)g_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta_{<},k)}{K^{\text{in}}(k)-K^{\text{in}}(-k)}\ .\end{split}

(96)

These are both propagators that are normalisable at the left boundary. Furthermore,
\mathbb{G}_{\text{ret}}
is analytic in the upper half-plane (UHP) of frequency of
k^{0}
, and
\mathbb{G}_{\text{adv}}
is analytic in the lower half-plane (LHP) frequency of
k^{0}
. We will now present an argument to show these analyticity properties.

We begin by detailing the analyticity properties of the ingoing and the outgoing boundary-to-bulk propagators. The boundary-to-bulk propagators,
G^{\text{in}}
and
G^{\text{out}}
, following

G^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)=K^{\text{in}}(k)\widetilde{G}^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)\ ,\qquad G^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k)=K^{\text{in}}(-k)\widetilde{G}^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k)\ ,

(97)

where
\widetilde{G}^{\text{in}}
and
\widetilde{G}^{\text{out}}
are generically regular

9

This is easy to see in
d=2
, from the explicit expressions of these propagators provided in
Jana:2020vyx
.

k^{0}
. Here we have used the intuition that the ingoing propagator should have poles only at quasinormal frequencies
Birmingham:2001pj
. It also highlights the fact that
G^{\text{in}}
is analytic in the UHP of
k^{0}
.

Coming to the retarded and the advanced bulk-to-bulk propagators in Eq. (

), as far as analyticity in the

plane is concerned, we can replace
\left[K^{\text{in}}(k)-K^{\text{in}}(-k)\right]
by

. This leads to

\mathbb{G}_{\text{ret}}(k)=K^{\text{in}}(k)\widetilde{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{ret}}(k)\ ,\qquad\mathbb{G}_{\text{adv}}(k)=K^{\text{in}}(-k)\widetilde{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{adv}}(k)\ ,

(98)

where
\widetilde{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{ret}}
and

\mathbb{G}_{\text{ret}}

\mathbb{G}_{\text{adv}}

Spinor bulk-to-bulk propagators

We now come to the spinor bulk-to-bulk propagators. The computation of the spinor bulk-to-bulk propagator will require the evaluation of a quantity
\mathfrak{W}(\zeta,k)
which satisfies
\partial_{\zeta}\mathfrak{W}(\zeta,k)=0
, i.e., the analogue of the Wronskian of a second-order ODE. We will, therefore, spend some time in computing this quantity explicitly and then use it to compute the spinor bulk-to-bulk propagator.

Derivation of the conserved quantity

Consider
\overline{\Psi}_{2}
and
\Psi_{1}
, which solve the conjugate Dirac equation and the Dirac equation, given by

\left(r^{d/2}\overline{\Psi}_{2}(\zeta,k)\right)\left\{\overleftarrow{\partial}_{\zeta}\frac{\mathbb{T}(\zeta)}{\sqrt{f}}+\mathbb{\Gamma}\left(\beta k^{0}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\zeta}\ln f\right)-\frac{\beta}{2}\Gamma^{(i)}k_{i}\right\}=0\ ,

(99)

and

\left\{\frac{\mathbb{T}(\zeta)}{\sqrt{f}}\partial_{\zeta}+\mathbb{\Gamma}\left(\beta k^{0}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\zeta}\ln f\right)-\frac{\beta}{2}\Gamma^{(i)}k_{i}\right\}\left(e^{-\beta k^{0}\zeta}\mathbb{T}(\zeta)r^{d/2}\Psi_{1}(\zeta,-k)\right)=0\ ,

(100)

respectively.
Note that if
\Psi_{1}(\zeta,k)
is a solution of the Dirac equation, then so is its time reversal
e^{-\beta k^{0}\zeta}\mathbb{T}(\zeta)r^{d/2}\Psi_{1}(\zeta,-k)
, which is what we have used in the second equation above. Multiplying Eq. (
99
) from the right by
\sqrt{f}e^{-\beta k^{0}\zeta}r^{d/2}\Psi_{1}(\zeta,-k)
, Eq. (
100
) from the left by
\sqrt{f}r^{d/2}\overline{\Psi}_{2}(\zeta,k)\mathbb{T}(\zeta)
and adding, we obtain
10
10

10

Here, we have used the bulk gamma matrix identities

\Gamma^{(i)}+\mathbb{T}(\zeta)\Gamma^{(i)}\mathbb{T}(\zeta)=0\ ,\quad\text{and}\quad\sqrt{f}\left[\mathbb{\Gamma}+\mathbb{T}(\zeta)\mathbb{\Gamma}\mathbb{T}(\zeta)\right]=\mathbb{T}(\zeta)\ .

(101)

\begin{split}&\partial_{\zeta}\left\{r^{d/2}\overline{\Psi}_{2}(\zeta,k)\mathbb{T}(\zeta)e^{-\beta k^{0}\zeta}r^{d/2}\Psi_{1}(\zeta,-k)\right\}\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt+\left(\beta k^{0}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\zeta}\ln f\right)\left\{r^{d/2}\overline{\Psi}_{2}(\zeta,k)\mathbb{T}(\zeta)e^{-\beta k^{0}\zeta}r^{d/2}\Psi_{1}(\zeta,-k)\right\}=0\ ,\end{split}

(102)

which integrates to

\partial_{\zeta}\mathfrak{W}(\zeta,k)=0\ ,\quad\text{where}\quad\mathfrak{W}(\zeta,k)\equiv\sqrt{f}r^{d/2}\overline{\Psi}_{2}(\zeta,k)\mathbb{T}(\zeta)r^{d/2}\Psi_{1}(\zeta,-k)\ .

(103)

Note that this conserved quantity contains the boundary sources. To construct a bulk-to-bulk propagator we would require a quantity independent of boundary sources. So, we now define a square matrix
\mathbb{W}(\zeta,k)
in the boundary spinor space where we strip off the boundary sources from this conserved quantity
\mathfrak{W}(\zeta,k)
, given by

\mathbb{W}[\bar{s}_{2}(\zeta,k),s_{1}(\zeta,k)]\equiv\sqrt{f}r^{d/2}\bar{s}_{2}(\zeta,-k)\mathbb{T}(\zeta)r^{d/2}s_{1}(\zeta,k)\ .

(104)

Here,
\bar{s}_{2}(\zeta,k)
and
s_{1}(\zeta,k)
are the conjugate boundary-to-bulk and boundary-to-bulk propagators respectively.

This boundary spinor-space matrix in the basis of ingoing and outgoing solutions takes the form

\begin{split}\lim_{\zeta\to 0,1}\mathbb{W}[\overline{S}^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k),S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)]&=\lim_{\zeta\to 0,1}\sqrt{f}r^{d/2}\overline{S}^{\text{in}}(\zeta,-k)\mathbb{T}(\zeta)r^{d/2}S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)\\
&=\lim_{\zeta\to 0,1}r^{d/2}\overline{S}^{\text{in}}(\zeta,-k)(\mathbb{P}_{{}_{+}}-\mathbb{P}_{{}_{-}})r^{d/2}S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)\\
&=-\left\{\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(k)+\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(-k)\right\}\ ,\end{split}

(105)

where we have used Eq. (
31
), Eq. (
87
) and have also defined the boundary retarded two-point correlator
\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(k)
as

\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(k)\equiv\lim_{\zeta\to 0,1}\overline{S}_{0}\mathbb{P}_{{}_{-}}r^{d/2}S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)=-\lim_{\zeta\to 0,1}r^{d/2}\overline{S}^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)\mathbb{P}_{{}_{+}}S_{0}\ .

(106)

By similar computations, we find the other combinations:

\begin{split}&\mathbb{W}[\overline{S}^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k),S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)]=-\left\{\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(k)+\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(-k)\right\}=-\mathbb{W}[\overline{S}^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k),S^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k)]\ ,\\
&\mathbb{W}[\overline{S}^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k),S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)]=0=\mathbb{W}[\overline{S}^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k),S^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k)]\ .\end{split}

(107)

Similar expressions for the conserved quantity corresponding to the right and the left boundary-to-bulk propagators can be derived from the above equations using Eq. (
37
) and Eq. (
92
). Explicitly, we have

\begin{split}&\mathbb{W}[\bar{s}_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,k),s_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,k)]=0=\mathbb{W}[\bar{s}_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta,k),s_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta,k)]\ ,\\
&\mathbb{W}[\bar{s}_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,k),s_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta,k)]=n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}\left\{\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(k)+\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(-k)\right\}=e^{-\beta k^{0}}\mathbb{W}[\bar{s}_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta,k),s_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,k)]\ .\end{split}

(108)

Determining the spinor bulk-to-bulk propagator

Let us now take a look at the differential equation satisfied by the spinorial bulk-to-bulk propagator (Eq. (
46
)). Away from the point
\zeta=\zeta_{0}
, it is simply the free Dirac equation and therefore in these regions, the bulk-to-bulk propagator has to be proportional to the boundary-to-bulk propagators. Keeping in mind the boundary conditions in Eq. (
47
), we write an ansatz for the bulk-to-bulk propagator as

\begin{split}\mathbb{S}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)&=\left[{s}_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,k)M_{<}(\zeta_{0},k)\bar{s}_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta_{0},-k)\right]\Theta_{{}^{\text{SK}}}(\zeta<\zeta_{0})\\
&\hskip 1.42271pt+\left[{s}_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta,k)M_{>}(\zeta_{0},k)\bar{s}_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta_{0},-k)\right]\Theta_{{}^{\text{SK}}}(\zeta>\zeta_{0})\ .\end{split}

(109)

Here, the symbol
\Theta_{{}^{\text{SK}}}
denotes a Heaviside theta function on the grSK contour defined as

\begin{split}&\Theta_{{}^{\text{SK}}}(\zeta<\zeta_{0})\equiv\Bigg\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\zeta&\text{if $\zeta$ comes before $\zeta_{0}$ on the grSK contour}\\
\zeta_{0}&\text{if $\zeta_{0}$ comes before $\zeta$ on the grSK contour}\end{array}\ ,\\
&\Theta_{{}^{\text{SK}}}(\zeta>\zeta_{0})\equiv 1-\Theta_{{}^{\text{SK}}}(\zeta<\zeta_{0})\ .\end{split}

(110)

The symbols
M_{<}(\zeta_{0},k)
and
M_{>}(\zeta_{0},k)
denote square matrices in the boundary spinor space which we will soon determine using the jump conditions at
\zeta=\zeta_{0}
. It is easy to see that the above ansatz is a square matrix in the bulk spinor space and also that it satisfies the binormalisable boundary conditions given in Eq. (
47
).

The jump condition is obtained by integrating the delta-sourced Dirac equation in Eq.(
46
) around the point
\zeta=\zeta_{0}
. Explicitly, we have

\begin{split}\frac{i\beta r}{2}r^{d/2}\Gamma^{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle A$}}D_{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle A$}}\mathbb{S}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)&=\left\{\frac{\mathbb{T}(\zeta)}{\sqrt{f}}\partial_{\zeta}+\mathbb{\Gamma}\left(\beta k^{0}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\zeta}\ln f\right)-\frac{\beta}{2}\Gamma^{(i)}k_{i}\right\}\left(r^{d/2}\mathbb{S}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)\right)\\
&={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\mathbb{1}\frac{\delta(\zeta-\zeta_{0})}{r^{d/2}f}\ ,\end{split}

(111)

where we have used the fact that
\sqrt{-g}=\frac{i\beta}{2}r^{d+1}f
.
Integrating the above ODE from
\zeta=\zeta_{0}^{-}
to
\zeta=\zeta_{0}^{+}
, we arrive at the jump condition

\frac{\mathbb{T}(\zeta)}{\sqrt{f}}r^{d/2}\mathbb{S}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)\Bigg|_{\zeta=\zeta_{0}^{-}}^{\zeta=\zeta_{0}^{+}}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\frac{\mathbb{1}}{r_{0}^{d/2}f(\zeta_{0})}\ .

(112)

Using the ansatz in Eq. (
109
), the above jump condition takes the form

\sqrt{f}r^{d}\mathbb{T}(\zeta)\left\{{s}_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta,k)\ M_{>}(\zeta,k)\ \bar{s}_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,-k)-{s}_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,k)\ M_{<}(\zeta,k)\ \bar{s}_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta,-k)\right\}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\mathbb{1}\ .

(113)

Notice now that pre-multiplying and post-multiplying the above equation with appropriate boundary-to-bulk propagators, we obtain simple equations involving the conserved quantity that we computed in Eq. (
104
) and Eq. (
108
). Pre-multiplying by
r^{d/2}\bar{s}_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta,-k)
and post-multiplying by
\sqrt{f}\mathbb{T}r^{d/2}s_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,k)
, we obtain
M_{<}(\zeta,k)
. Similarly, pre-multiplying by
r^{d/2}\bar{s}_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,-k)
and post-multiplying by
\sqrt{f}\mathbb{T}r^{d/2}s_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta,k)
, we obtain
M_{>}(\zeta,k)
. The explicit expressions are as follows.

M_{<}(\zeta,k)=\frac{1}{(1-n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}})\left\{\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(k)+\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(-k)\right\}}\ ,\quad M_{>}(\zeta,k)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\frac{1}{n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}\left\{\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(k)+\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(-k)\right\}}\ .

(114)

Substituting these results in the ansatz in Eq. (
109
), we obtain

\begin{split}\mathbb{S}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)&=\left[{s}_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,k)\frac{1}{(1-n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}})\left\{\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(k)+\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(-k)\right\}}\bar{s}_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta_{0},-k)\right]\Theta_{{}^{\text{SK}}}(\zeta<\zeta_{0})\\
&\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\left[{s}_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta,k)\frac{1}{n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}\left\{\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(k)+\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(-k)\right\}}\bar{s}_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta_{0},-k)\right]\Theta_{{}^{\text{SK}}}(\zeta>\zeta_{0})\ .\end{split}

(115)

We take a moment to also note that the
\mathbb{P}_{-}
projected boundary limits of the above propagator take the form

\lim_{\zeta\to 0}r^{\frac{d}{2}}\overline{S}_{0}\mathbb{P}_{-}\mathbb{S}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)=\overline{s}_{{}_{\text{L}}}(\zeta_{0},k)\ ,\qquad\lim_{\zeta\to 1}r^{\frac{d}{2}}\overline{S}_{0}\mathbb{P}_{-}\mathbb{S}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)=\overline{s}_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta_{0},k)\ .

(116)

Retarded and advanced spinor bulk-to-bulk propagators

Now that we have the binormalisable bulk-to-bulk propagator, we can also construct bulk-to-bulk propagators that have specific causal properties, i.e., the retarded and the advanced bulk-to-bulk propagators. These propagators are defined to be normalisable at the left boundary, and analytic in the upper-half and the lower-half of the frequency plane respectively. Here, we will not provide a derivation of these propagators, as it is similar to the computation of the binormalisable bulk-to-bulk propagator given above. We just quote the results:

\begin{split}\mathbb{S}_{\text{ret}}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)&={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\left[{s}_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,k)\frac{1}{(1-n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}})\left\{\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(k)+\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(-k)\right\}}\overline{S}^{\text{out}}(\zeta_{0},-k)\right]\Theta_{{}^{\text{SK}}}(\zeta<\zeta_{0})\\
&\quad+\left[S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)\frac{1}{n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}\left\{\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(k)+\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(-k)\right\}}\bar{s}_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta_{0},-k)\right]\Theta_{{}^{\text{SK}}}(\zeta>\zeta_{0})\ ,\end{split}

(117)

and

\begin{split}\mathbb{S}_{\text{adv}}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)&={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\left[{s}_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,k)\frac{1}{(1-n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}})\left\{\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(k)+\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(-k)\right\}}\overline{S}^{\text{in}}(\zeta_{0},-k)\right]\Theta_{{}^{\text{SK}}}(\zeta<\zeta_{0})\\
&\quad+\left[S^{\text{out}}(\zeta,k)\frac{1}{n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}\left\{\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(k)+\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(-k)\right\}}\bar{s}_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta_{0},-k)\right]\Theta_{{}^{\text{SK}}}(\zeta>\zeta_{0})\ .\end{split}

(118)

Now, we show how these propagators satisfy all the required properties. First of all, we note that the right boundary-to-bulk propagator
s_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,k)
satisfies

\lim_{\zeta\rightarrow 0}r^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathbb{P}_{+}{s}_{{}_{\text{R}}}(\zeta,k)=0\ .

(119)

This implies that the retarded and the advanced bulk-to-bulk propagators satisfy

\lim_{\zeta\rightarrow 0}r^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathbb{P}_{+}\mathbb{S}_{\text{ret}}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)=0\ ,\qquad\lim_{\zeta\rightarrow 0}r^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathbb{P}_{+}\mathbb{S}_{\text{adv}}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)=0\ .

(120)

Next, we have to show that the above propagators have the desired analyticity properties on the complex frequency plane. To do this, we will employ a similar set of arguments as the one used in the case of the scalar bulk-to-bulk propagator. To begin with, we use the intuition that the ingoing boundary-to-bulk propagator
S^{\text{in}}(\zeta,k)
has the same pole structure, i.e., the quasinormal poles, on the complex frequency plane as the boundary retarded correlator
\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(k)
. We can then use the same trick we used for the case of the scalar retarded and advanced propagators to conclude that
\mathbb{S}_{\text{ret}}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)
, has the same poles as
\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(k)
and is analytic in the upper half of the frequency plane. Similarly,
\mathbb{S}_{\text{adv}}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)
is analytic in the lower half of the frequency plane.

Here, we point out that the binormalisable spinor bulk-to-bulk propagator can be written as a linear combination of the retarded and the advanced bulk-to-bulk propagators as

\mathbb{S}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)=\ n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}\mathbb{S}_{\text{ret}}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)+(1-n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}})\mathbb{S}_{\text{adv}}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)\ .

(121)

This relation can be easily obtained by replacing the corresponding propagators, and
n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}}
with
-n^{{}_{\text{FD}}}_{k^{0}}
, from the corresponding relation for scalar bulk-to-bulk propagators
Loganayagam:2024mnj
:

\mathbb{G}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)=\ -n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}}\mathbb{G}_{\text{ret}}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)+(1+n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{k^{0}})\mathbb{G}_{\text{adv}}(\zeta|\zeta_{0},k)\ .

(122)

Appendix C
Monodromy integrals around the grSK contour

The computation of the four-point exchange diagrams that we have detailed in the main text requires performing integrals of the form

\mathscr{I}_{\mathbb{G}}\equiv\oint_{\zeta_{1}}\oint_{\zeta_{2}}\ [f(\zeta_{1})]^{\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}}[f(\zeta_{2})]^{\frac{\alpha_{2}}{2}}e^{-\beta k^{0}_{1}\zeta_{1}}e^{-\beta k^{0}_{2}\zeta_{2}}\mathbb{G}(\zeta_{2}|\zeta_{1},p)\mathscr{F}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})\ ,

(123)

and

\mathscr{I}_{\mathbb{S}}\equiv\oint_{\zeta_{1}}\oint_{\zeta_{2}}\ [f(\zeta_{1})]^{\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}}[f(\zeta_{2})]^{\frac{\alpha_{2}}{2}}e^{-\beta k^{0}_{1}\zeta_{1}}e^{-\beta k^{0}_{2}\zeta_{2}}\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{2}(\zeta_{2})\mathbb{S}(\zeta_{2}|\zeta_{1},p)\mathfrak{a}_{1}(\zeta_{1})\ .

(124)

Here
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are integers,
\mathscr{F}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})
is a scalar analytic function on the complex
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
planes, and
\mathfrak{a}_{1}(\zeta)
and
\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{2}(\zeta)
are analytic functions on the
r
plane that has the spinor structure of a spinor and a conjugate spinor boundary-to-bulk propagator respectively.

The double-discontinuity integrals above reduce to integrals purely on one copy of the black hole exterior. That is, the monodromy around the grSK branch cut can be explicitly computed and we can write the answer for the contour integral as an integral over the real
r
line. In this appendix, we will show this in detail for the second integral above, i.e., the one involving the spinor bulk-to-bulk propagator,
\mathscr{I}_{\mathbb{S}}
.

On the other hand, the double discontinuity integral of the scalar bulk-to-bulk propagator has been computed in
Loganayagam:2024mnj
. Here the authors were concerned only with scalar interactions and thus do not include the factors of
\sqrt{f}
we have above. Nevertheless, including the new factors of
\sqrt{f}
is relatively straightforward and we just present the result here.

\begin{split}\mathscr{I}_{\mathbb{G}}=\int_{\rm ext_{1}}\int_{\rm ext_{2}}[f(\zeta_{1})]^{\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}}[f(\zeta_{2})]^{\frac{\alpha_{2}}{2}}e^{-\beta k^{0}_{1}\zeta_{1}}e^{-\beta k^{0}_{2}\zeta_{2}}\mathbb{G}_{\rm DD}(\zeta_{2}|\zeta_{1},p)\mathscr{F}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})\ ,\end{split}

(125)

where
\int_{\rm ext}
denotes the integral over the exterior of the left copy of the black bole, defined in Eq. (
57
). The symbol
\mathbb{G}_{\rm DD}(\zeta_{2}|\zeta_{1},p)
denotes the double-discontinuity given by

\begin{split}\mathbb{G}_{\rm DD}(\zeta_{2}|\zeta_{1},p)&\equiv\frac{-n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{p^{0}}}{\left(1+\frac{1}{(-)^{\alpha_{1}}e^{\beta(k_{1}^{0}-p^{0})}-1}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{(-)^{\alpha_{2}}e^{\beta k_{2}^{0}}-1}\right)}\mathbb{G}_{\text{ret}}(\zeta_{2}|\zeta_{1},p)\\
&+\frac{1+n^{{}_{\text{BE}}}_{p^{0}}}{\left(1+\frac{1}{(-)^{\alpha_{1}}e^{\beta k_{1}^{0}}-1}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{(-)^{\alpha_{2}}e^{\beta(k_{2}^{0}+p^{0})}-1}\right)}\mathbb{G}_{\text{adv}}(\zeta_{2}|\zeta_{1},p)\ .\end{split}

(126)

Here
\mathbb{G}_{\text{ret}}(\zeta_{1}|\zeta_{1},p)
and
\mathbb{G}_{\text{adv}}(\zeta_{1}|\zeta_{1},p)
are the retarded and the advanced bulk-to-bulk propagators respectively, given in Eq. (
96
). From the above result, note that the temperature-dependent factors outside the propagators are all either Fermi-Dirac or Bose-Einstein statistical factors, depending on the parity of
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
. In particular, note that for
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=0
, we obtain only Bose-Einstein factors and that the result matches the purely scalar theory result in
Loganayagam:2024mnj
.

We now come to the double-discontinuity involving the spinor bulk-to-bulk propagator. Before proceeding to perform the monodromy integrals, we take a moment to note that exactly as in the purely scalar case, here too there are no pole contributions from the horizon. This might seem surprising in the case of fermions since there are factors of
1/\sqrt{f}
that can multiply to give horizon poles of the form
1/f
. This does not happen because the factors of
1/\sqrt{f}
in all our computations only come from the outgoing propagator. In particular, from the time-reversal matrix. Since the time-reversal matrix is idempotent (squares to identity), there can never be factors of
1/f
in the calculation, thus avoiding the question of horizon-pole contributions.

Accounting for the monodromy across the branch cut, the double-discontinuity of the spinor bulk-to-bulk propagator takes the form

\begin{split}\mathscr{I}_{\mathbb{S}}=\int_{\text{ext}_{1}}\int_{\text{ext}_{2}}\ [f(\zeta_{1})]^{\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}}[f(\zeta_{2})]^{\frac{\alpha_{2}}{2}}e^{-\beta k^{0}_{1}\zeta_{1}}e^{-\beta k^{0}_{2}\zeta_{2}}\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{2}(\zeta_{2})\mathbb{S}_{{}_{\text{DD}}}(\zeta_{2}|\zeta_{1},p)\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{1}(\zeta_{1})\ ,\end{split}

(127)

where
\mathbb{S}_{{}_{\text{DD}}}(\zeta_{2}|\zeta_{1},p)

\begin{split}\mathbb{S}_{{}_{\text{DD}}}(\zeta_{2}|\zeta_{1},p)&\equiv\mathbb{S}(\zeta_{2}|\zeta_{1},p)-(-)^{\alpha_{1}}e^{-\beta k^{0}_{1}}\mathbb{S}(\zeta_{2}|\zeta_{1}+1,p)\\

\begin{split}\mathbb{S}_{{}_{\text{DD}}}(\zeta_{2}|\zeta_{1},p)&=S^{\text{in}}(\zeta_{2},p)\frac{1}{\left\{\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(p)+\mathfrak{K}_{\rm ret}(-p)\right\}}\left[c_{1}\overline{S}^{\text{in}}(\zeta_{1},-p)-c_{2}\overline{S}^{\text{out}}(\zeta_{1},-p)\right]\\
