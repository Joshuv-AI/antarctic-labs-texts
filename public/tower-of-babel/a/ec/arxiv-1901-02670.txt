Let
\Delta:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}
where
\mathbb{C}
is the complex plane. We denote by
\mathcal{B}
the class of all analytic functions
w(z)
in
\Delta

w(0)=0
and
|w(z)|<1
, and denote by
\mathcal{A}
the class of all functions that are analytic and normalized in
\Delta
. The subclass of
\mathcal{A}
consisting of univalent functions in
\Delta
is denoted by
\mathcal{S}
. For functions
f
and
g
belonging to the class
\mathcal{A}
, we say that
f
is subordinate to
g
in the unit disk
\Delta

f(z)\prec g(z)
or
f\prec g

In particular, if

is univalent function in
\Delta

f(z)\prec g(z)\Leftrightarrow f(0)=g(0)\quad{\rm and}\quad f(\Delta)\subset g(\Delta).

Denote by
\mathcal{S}^{*}
and
\mathcal{K}
the set of all starlike and convex functions in
\Delta
, respectively.
A function
f\in\mathcal{A}
is said to be close-to-convex, if there exists
a convex function

and
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
such that

{\rm Re}\left\{e^{i\delta}\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{g^{\prime}(z)}\right\}>0\quad(z\in\Delta).

The functions class which satisfy the last condition was introduced by Kaplan in
[
6
]
and we denote by
\mathcal{CK}
. It is clear that if we take
g(z)\equiv z
in the class
\mathcal{CK}
, then we have the Noshiro-Warschawski class as follows

\mathcal{C}:=\left\{f\in\mathcal{A}:\exists\delta\in\mathbb{R};{\rm Re}\left\{e^{i\delta}f^{\prime}(z)\right\}>0,\,z\in\Delta\right\}.

By the basic Noshiro-Warschawski lemma
[
1
, §2.6]
, we have
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{S}
.

Here, we recall from
[

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\beta\in[0,1)
and
\alpha\in(-\pi,\pi]
. If
f\in\mathcal{A}
belongs to the class
\mathcal{R}(\alpha,\beta)
, then

{\rm Re}\{f^{\prime}(z)\}>\beta\quad(0\leq\beta<1).

This means that
\mathcal{R}(\alpha,\beta)\subset\mathcal{C}(\beta)
.

Proof.

Let
f^{\prime}(z)\neq 0
for
z\neq 0
and
p(z)
be defined by

p(z)=\frac{1}{1-\beta}\left(f^{\prime}(z)-\beta\right)\quad(0\leq\beta<1).

Then
p(z)
is analytic in
\Delta
,
p(0)=1
and

f^{\prime}(z)+\frac{1+e^{i\alpha}}{2}zf^{\prime\prime}(z)=(1-\beta)[p(z)+(1+e^{i\alpha})zp^{\prime}(z)/2]+\beta=\phi(p(z),zp^{\prime}(z);z),

where
\phi(r,s;z):=(1-\beta)[r+(1+e^{i\alpha})s/2]
.
Since
f\in\mathcal{R}(\alpha,\beta)
, we define the set
\Omega_{\beta}
as follows:

(2.2)

\{\phi(p(z),zp^{\prime}(z);z):z\in\Delta\}\subset\{w:{\rm Re}\,\{w\}>\beta\}=:\Omega_{\beta}.

For all real
\rho
and
\sigma
, that
\sigma\leq-(1+\rho^{2})/2
, we get

\displaystyle{\rm Re}\{\phi(i\rho,\sigma;z)\}

\displaystyle={\rm Re}\left\{(1-\beta)[i\rho+(1+e^{i\alpha})\sigma/2]\right\}

\displaystyle=(1-\beta)(1+\cos\alpha)\sigma/2+\beta

\displaystyle\leq\beta-\frac{(1-\beta)}{4}(1+\cos\alpha)(1+\rho^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\beta.

This shows that
{\rm Re}\{\phi(p(z),zp^{\prime}(z);z)\}\not\in\Omega_{\beta}
. Thus by Lemma
1.3
, we get
{\rm Re}\{p(z)\}>0
or
{\rm Re}\{f^{\prime}(z)\}>\beta
. This means that
f\in\mathcal{C}(\beta)
and concluding the proof.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let

and
\alpha\in(-\pi,\pi]

Proof.

Let the function
f\in\mathcal{A}
belongs to the class
\mathcal{R}(\alpha,\beta)
where
\beta\in[0,1)
and
\alpha\in(-\pi,\pi]
. Define the function

as

(2.3)

\textsl{p}(z):=\frac{1}{1-\beta}\left(\frac{f(z)}{z}-\beta\right).

Since
f\in\mathcal{A}
, easily seen that

is analytic in
\Delta
and
\textsl{p}(0)=1
. The equation (
2.3
), with a simple calculation implies that

(2.4)

f^{\prime}(z)=\beta+(1-\beta)\textsl{p}(z)+(1-\beta)z\textsl{p}^{\prime}(z)

and

(2.5)

f^{\prime\prime}(z)=2(1-\beta)\textsl{p}^{\prime}(z)+(1-\beta)z\textsl{p}^{\prime\prime}(z).

Now, from (
2.4
) and multiplying (
2.5
) by

, we get

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(z)+\frac{1+e^{i\alpha}}{2}zf^{\prime\prime}(z)

\displaystyle\quad=\beta+(1-\beta)\textsl{p}(z)+[(2+e^{i\alpha})(1-\beta)]z\textsl{p}^{\prime}(z)+(1-\beta)\frac{1+e^{i\alpha}}{2}z^{2}\textsl{p}^{\prime\prime}(z)

\displaystyle\quad=\psi(\textsl{p}(z),z\textsl{p}^{\prime}(z),z^{2}\textsl{p}^{\prime\prime}(z);z),

where

\psi(r,s,t;z)=\beta+(1-\beta)r+[(2+e^{i\alpha})(1-\beta)]s+(1-\beta)\frac{1+e^{i\alpha}}{2}t.

Since
f\in\mathcal{R}(\alpha,\beta)
we consider the following inclusion relation

\{\psi(\textsl{p}(z),z\textsl{p}^{\prime}(z),z^{2}\textsl{p}^{\prime\prime}(z);z):z\in\Delta\}\subset\Omega_{\beta},

where
\Omega_{\beta}
is defined in (
2.2
). Let
\rho,\sigma,\mu
and
\nu
be real numbers such that

(2.6)

\sigma\leq-\frac{1}{2}(1+\rho^{2}),~\mu+\nu\leq 0\quad{\rm and}\quad 2\mu+\nu\leq 0.

From (
2.6
) and by
[
9
]
(see also
[
10
, Theorem 2.3b]

\psi(i\rho,\sigma,\mu+i\nu;z)=\beta+(1-\beta)i\rho+[(2+e^{i\alpha})(1-\beta)]\sigma+(1-\beta)\frac{1+e^{i\alpha}}{2}(\mu+i\nu),

we get

\displaystyle\quad{\rm Re}\{\psi(i\rho,\sigma,\mu+i\nu;z)\}

\displaystyle=\beta+(1-\beta)(2+\cos\alpha)\sigma+\frac{1-\beta}{2}[\mu(1+\cos\alpha)-\nu\sin\alpha]

\displaystyle\leq\beta-(1-\beta)(1+\rho^{2})(1+\cos\alpha)+\frac{1-\beta}{2}[\mu(1+\cos\alpha)-\nu\sin\alpha]

\displaystyle=F(\alpha,\beta,\rho)+G(\alpha,\beta,\mu),

where

F(\alpha,\beta,\rho):=\beta-(1-\beta)(1+\rho^{2})(1+\cos\alpha)

It is easy to see that

Therefore we obtain

2.3

This completes the proof.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\phi_{b}(z)
be defined by (
1.1
) for

. Then
\phi_{b}(\Delta)=\Omega_{b}
where

\Omega_{b}:=\{w\in\mathbb{C}:0<{\rm Re}\{w\}<2b\}.

Proof.

If
b=1
, then we have
0<{\rm Re}\{\phi_{b}(z)\}={\rm Re}\{1+z\}<2
. For
b>1/2
and
b\neq 1
,
the function
\phi_{b}(z)
does not have any poles in
\overline{\Delta}
and is analytic in
\Delta
. Thus looking for the
\min\{{\rm Re}\{\phi_{b}(z)\}:~|z|<1\}
it is sufficient to consider it on the boundary
\partial\phi_{b}(\Delta)=\{\phi_{b}(e^{i\varphi}):\varphi\in[0,2\pi]\}
.
A simple calculation gives us

{\rm Re}\left\{\phi_{b}(e^{i\varphi})\right\}=\frac{(1/b)(1+\cos\varphi)}{1+2(1/b-1)\cos\varphi+(1/b-1)^{2}}\quad(\varphi\in[0,2\varphi]).

So we can see that
{\rm Re}\left\{F_{\alpha}(z)\right\}
is well defined also for
\varphi=0
and
\varphi=2\pi
. Define

h(x)=\frac{(1/b)(1+x)}{1+2(1/b-1)x+(1/b-1)^{2}}\quad(-1\leq x\leq 1).

Thus for
b>1/2
and
b\neq 1
, we have
h^{\prime}(x)>0
. Therefore, we get

0=h(-1)\leq h(x)\leq h(1)=2b.

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{A}
be a member of the class
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}(b)
where
b>1/2
and
\alpha\in(-\pi,\pi]
. Then

0<{\rm Re}\{f^{\prime}(z)\}<2b\quad(z\in\Delta).

Proof.

Let us
f\in\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}(b)
. Then by Lemma
1.1
, Lemma
2.1
and by definition of the subordination principle we have

(2.7)

0<{\rm Re}\left\{f^{\prime}(z)+\frac{1+e^{i\alpha}}{2}zf^{\prime\prime}(z)\right\}<2b\quad(z\in\Delta,b>1/2,-\pi<\alpha\leq\pi).

First, we assume that

{\rm Re}\left\{f^{\prime}(z)+\frac{1+e^{i\alpha}}{2}zf^{\prime\prime}(z)\right\}>0.

Then by Corollary
2.2
we have
{\rm Re}\{f^{\prime}(z)\}>0
.
Now we let

{\rm Re}\left\{f^{\prime}(z)+\frac{1+e^{i\alpha}}{2}zf^{\prime\prime}(z)\right\}<2b.

Put
\xi=2b
and so
\xi>1
. Let
f^{\prime}(z)\neq 0
for
z\neq 0
. Consider

q(z)=\frac{1}{1-\xi}\left(f^{\prime}(z)-\xi\right)\quad(\xi>1,z\in\Delta).

Then
q(z)
is analytic in
\Delta
and
q(0)=1
. A simple check gives us

f^{\prime}(z)+\frac{1+e^{i\alpha}}{2}zf^{\prime\prime}(z)=(1-\xi)[q(z)+(1+e^{i\alpha})zq^{\prime}(z)/2]+\xi=\eta(q(z),zq^{\prime}(z);z),

where
\eta(x,y;z)=(1-\xi)[x+(1+e^{i\alpha})y/2]+\xi
. Now we define

\{\eta(q(z),zq^{\prime}(z);z):z\in\Delta\}\subset\{w:{\rm Re}\{w\}<\xi\}=:\Omega_{\xi}.

Again with a simple calculation we deduce that

\displaystyle{\rm Re}\{\eta(i\rho,\sigma;z)\}

\displaystyle={\rm Re}\left\{(1-\xi)[i\rho+(1+e^{i\alpha})\sigma/2]\right\}

\displaystyle=(1-\xi)(1+\cos\alpha)\sigma/2+\xi

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(\xi-1)}{4}(1+\cos\alpha)(1+\rho^{2})+\xi\quad(-\sigma\geq(1+\rho^{2})/2)

\displaystyle\geq\xi.

This shows that
{\rm Re}\{\eta(i\rho,\sigma;z)\}\not\in\Omega_{\xi}
and therefore
{\rm Re}\{q(z)\}>0
, or equivalently
{\rm Re}\{f^{\prime}(z)\}<\xi
. This is the end of proof.
∎

Let

. Then from the definition of subordination and by Lemma

3.
The radius of univalence of 2-th section sum of
f\in\mathcal{R}(\alpha,\beta)

In this section, we obtain the radius of univalence of 2-th section sum of
f\in\mathcal{R}(\alpha,\beta)
. We recall that
the Taylor polynomial
s_{k}(z)=s_{k}(f)(z)
of
f
defined by

s_{k}(z)=s_{k}(f)(z)=z+a_{2}z^{2}+\cdots+a_{k}z^{k},

is called the
k-th\,section/partial
sum of
f
. In
[
18
]
, proved that every section
s_{k}(z)
of a
f\in\mathcal{S}
is univalent in the disk
|z|<1/4
and the number
1/4
is best possible as the second partial sum of the Koebe function
k(z)=z/(1-z)^{2}
shows. Next, we find the radius of univalence of the 2-th section sum of
f\in\mathcal{R}(\alpha,\beta)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

The 2-th section sum of
f\in\mathcal{R}(\alpha,\beta)
is univalent in the disc

|z|<\frac{\sqrt{10+6\cos\alpha}}{4(1-\beta)}\quad(-\pi<\alpha\leq\pi,0\leq\beta<1).

The number
\frac{\sqrt{10+6\cos\alpha}}{4(1-\beta)}
cannot be replaced by a greater one.

Proof.

Let
f(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}\in\mathcal{R}(\alpha,\beta)
and
s_{2}(z)=z+a_{2}z^{2}
be its second section. By a simple calculation and since
|a_{2}|\leq\frac{2(1-\beta)}{\sqrt{10+6\cos\alpha}}
we have

{\rm Re}\{s_{2}^{\prime}(z)\}={\rm Re}\{1+2a_{2}z\}\geq 1-2|a_{2}||z|\geq 1-\frac{4(1-\beta)|z|}{\sqrt{10+6\cos\alpha}},

which is positive provided
|z|<\frac{\sqrt{10+6\cos\alpha}}{4(1-\beta)}
. Therefore
s_{2}(z)
is close-to-convex (univalent) in the disk
|z|<\frac{\sqrt{10+6\cos\alpha}}{4(1-\beta)}
. To show that this bound is sharp, we consider the function
f_{x}
defined by (
2.1
). The second partial sum
s_{2}(f_{x})(z)
of
f_{x}
is
z+\frac{4(1-\beta)}{2(3+e^{i\alpha})}z^{2}
. Thus we get

s_{2}^{\prime}(z)=1+\frac{4(1-\beta)}{(3+e^{i\alpha})}z.

Hence
{\rm Re}\{s_{2}^{\prime}(z)\}=0
when
z=-\frac{(3+e^{i\alpha})}{4(1-\beta)}
. This completes the proof.
∎
