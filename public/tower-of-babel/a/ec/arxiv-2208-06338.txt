Zilber–Pink in a product of modular curves assuming multiplicative degeneration
Christopher Daw, Martin Orr

Abstract
. We prove the Zilber–Pink conjecture for curves in Y ​ ( 1 ) n Y(1)^{n} whose Zariski closure in ( ℙ 1 ) n (\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n} passes through the point ( ∞ , … , ∞ ) (\infty,\ldots,\infty) , going beyond the asymmetry condition of Habegger and Pila. Our proof is based on a height bound following André’s G-functions method. The principal novelty is that we exploit relations between evaluations of G-functions at unboundedly many non-archimedean places.

1.
Introduction

One of the central problems of unlikely intersections is the
Zilber–Pink conjecture
for Shimura varieties:

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let
V
be an irreducible subvariety of a Shimura variety
S
. If
V
is not contained in a special subvariety of positive codimension, then the intersection of
V
with the union of all special subvarieties of
S
of codimension greater than
\dim(V)
is not Zariski dense in
V
.

An immediate corollary of Conjecture
1.1
is the André–Oort conjecture: the special subvarieties of
S
are characterised among the irreducible subvarieties of
S
as those containing a Zariski dense set of special points. A proof of the André–Oort conjecture was recently announced by Pila, Shankar and Tsimerman
[
PST
]
, who, using work of Esnault and Groechenig, give height bounds for special points, thereby completing a new strategy of Binyamini, Schmidt and Yafaev
[
BSY23
]
.

In
[
HP12
]
, Habegger and Pila made the first progress on Conjecture
1.1
beyond the André–Oort conjecture, proving it for so-called “asymmetric curves” in
Y(1)^{n}
.
Here
Y(1)
denotes the level-one modular curve, which is isomorphic to the affine line
\mathbb{A}^{1}
.
An irreducible curve
C\subset Y(1)^{n}
is called
asymmetric
if the restrictions of the coordinate functions on
Y(1)^{n}
to
C
have distinct degrees, up to at most one exceptional pair of coordinates, and ignoring coordinates whose restrictions to
C
are constant.
Habegger and Pila’s theorem was as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

[
HP12
, Theorem 1]

Let
C
be an irreducible asymmetric curve in
Y(1)^{n}
defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
If
C
is not contained in a special subvariety of positive codimension, then the intersection of
C
with the union of all special subvarieties of
Y(1)^{n}
of codimension at least
2
is finite.

Removing the asymmetry condition in
Theorem

1.2
has been a major challenge.
The main result of this article is
a proof of
1.1
for a different class of curves in
Y(1)^{n}
,
namely, those that “intersect infinity.”
We say that an irreducible curve in
Y(1)^{n}\cong\mathbb{A}^{n}

intersects infinity
if its Zariski closure in
(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
contains the point
(\infty,\ldots,\infty)
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
C
be an irreducible curve in
Y(1)^{n}
which intersects infinity and is defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
If
C
is not contained in a special subvariety of positive codimension, then the intersection of
C
with the union of all special subvarieties of
Y(1)^{n}
of codimension at least
2
is finite.

The class of curves which intersect infinity neither contains nor is contained in the class of asymmetric curves.
Examples of curves in
Y(1)^{n}
which intersect infinity but are not asymmetric are given by lines in
\mathbb{A}^{n}
which are not parallel to any coordinate hyperplane.

In fact, by combining
Theorem

1.3
with the ideas about “modular Mordell–Lang” from
[
HP12
]
, we may prove the Zilber–Pink conjecture in full for lines in
Y(1)^{n}
.
(We thank an anonymous referee for suggesting this theorem.)

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
C
be a line in
Y(1)^{n}\cong\mathbb{A}^{n}
which is defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
If
C
is not contained in a special subvariety of positive codimension, then the intersection of
C
with the union of all special subvarieties of
Y(1)^{n}
of codimension at least
2
is finite.

If a line in
Y(1)^{n}
is not parallel to any coordinate hyperplane and is contained in any hyperplane defined by setting two coordinates equal to one another, then it is not contained in any special subvariety of positive codimension.
Hence we obtain the following concrete example of a new case of the Zilber–Pink conjecture included in
Theorem

1.3
, where
\Phi_{M}(X,Y)\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]
denotes the classical modular polynomial.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
a,b
be distinct, non-zero algebraic numbers.
Then there are only finitely many
z\in\mathbb{C}
for which there exist positive integers
M
,
N
such that the following equation holds:

\Phi_{M}(z,z+a)=\Phi_{N}(z,z+b)=0.

Proof.

Let
C
denote the line in
Y(1)^{3}
defined by
x_{2}=x_{1}+a
and
x_{3}=x_{1}+b
.
Then
C
intersects infinity and is not contained in a special subvariety of positive codimension.
The curves in
Y(1)^{3}
defined by
\Phi_{M}(x_{1},x_{2})=\Phi_{N}(x_{1},x_{3})=0
are special subvarieties, so the theorem follows from
Theorem

1.3
applied to
C
.
∎

As well as proving
Theorem

1.3
, the aim of this article is also to
demonstrate a new method of proving lower bounds for Galois orbits
, the most difficult aspect of the Zilber–Pink conjecture.
We use a method of André
[
And89
, Ch. X]
for studying fibres with large endomorphism rings in a family of abelian varieties, based on G-functions.
Recently, this method has been used to obtain bounds for Galois orbits in other cases of the Zilber–Pink conjecture
[
BM21
]
,
[
DO21
]
,
[
DO22
]
.
André’s method has also been applied by Papas in variations of Hodge structure, going beyond families of abelian varieties
[
Papb
]
.

The key new contribution of this article is to use relations between evaluations of G-functions
at all non-archimedean places
to obtain lower bounds for Galois orbits, and thus new cases of the Zilber–Pink conjecture.
Previous applications used only archimedean evaluations of G-functions (except
[
And95
]
, which exploited only a fixed finite number of non-archimedean places).
Ignoring non-archimedean places restricted the large endomorphism rings for which the method could prove Galois orbit bounds, preventing the method being applied to
Y(1)^{n}
, or to intersections with
E^{2}
curves in the moduli space of abelian surfaces in
[
DO22
]
.

This article’s method can be generalised to other cases of the Zilber–Pink conjecture for curves in moduli spaces of abelian varieties, subject to a condition generalising the “intersects infinity” condition in
Theorem

1.3
.
This condition corresponds to multiplicative degeneration of the corresponding family of abelian varieties as in
[
And89
, X, Theorem 1.3]
.
The method can also be used to prove versions of
Theorem

1.3
with some variations of this degeneration condition, but removing it entirely is challenging.

1.A.
Height bounds and bounds for Galois orbits

For a precise description of special subvarieties in
Y(1)^{n}
, see
[
HP12
, Section 2.1]
.
Informally, a
special subvariety
is a subvariety of
Y(1)^{n}
defined by a system of equations of two types:

(1)

\Phi_{M}(x_{i},x_{j})=0
for some indices
i,j\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}
;

(2)

x_{i}=a
for some index
i\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}
and some CM
j
-invariant
a\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.

A
strongly special subvariety
of
Y(1)^{n}
is a subvariety defined by equations of type (i) only.

In
[
HP12
, Theorem 2]
, the variant of
Theorem

1.2
which dealt with intersections between
C
and non-strongly special subvarieties was proved, without the asymmetry restriction.
Thus in order to prove
Theorem

1.3
, it suffices to restrict our attention to strongly special subvarieties.

Furthermore, in
[
HP12
]
, the asymmetry condition was used only in proving the lower bound for Galois orbits
[
HP12
, Lemma 4.2]
.
Thus, in this article, we only need to prove a new lower bound for Galois orbits of intersections between strongly special subvarieties and a curve which intersects infinity.
Theorem

1.3
then follows via the Pila–Zannier method, exactly as in
[
HP12
]
.
Our lower bound for Galois orbits is as follows.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
n\geq 3
and let
C\subset Y(1)^{n}
be an irreducible algebraic curve defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
that intersects infinity and is not contained in a special subvariety of positive codimension.
Let
\Sigma
denote the set of points of
C
with at least two CM coordinates.
There exist positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
with the following property.
Let
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}
with
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
,
i_{3}\neq i_{4}
and
\{i_{1},i_{2}\}\neq\{i_{3},i_{4}\}
.
For all points
s=(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{n})\in C(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})\setminus\Sigma
satisfying

\Phi_{M}(s_{i_{1}},s_{i_{2}})=\Phi_{N}(s_{i_{3}},s_{i_{4}})=0

for some positive integers
M
and
N
,
we have

[\mathbb{Q}(s):\mathbb{Q}]\geq{c_{\ref*{C:galois-bound-mult}}}\max\{M,N\}^{c_{\ref*{C:galois-bound-exp}}}.

The statement of Theorem
1.6
is the same as
[
HP12
, Lemma 4.2]
, except that the “asymmetric curve” condition has been replaced by “intersects infinity” (and we always impose the condition
\{i_{1},i_{2}\}\neq\{i_{3},i_{4}\}
, which is not really a change from
[
HP12
, Lemma 4.2]
because the condition of intersecting infinity implies that no coordinate function is constant on
C
).

Habegger and Pila deduce
[
HP12
, Lemma 4.2]
from isogeny estimates of Masser–Wüstholz type and a height bound: after a suitable relabelling of the coordinates,

(1)

h(s_{i_{1}})\leq c_{3}\max\{1,\log(M)\}.

Here, and throughout the paper,
h:\mathbb{A}^{n}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the (logarithmic) height defined in
[
HP12
, Section 2.2]
.
This height bound
[
HP12
, (17)]
, which is essentially the same as
[
Hab10
, Thm. 1.1]
, is obtained by comparing heights of coordinates on
C
and Faltings heights, with a key input being the asymmetry condition.

Habegger conjectured that the height bound (
1
) should hold without requiring the asymmetry condition, and indeed that it should only require two coordinates related by one modular polynomial (as opposed to the two relations between three or four coordinates in
Theorem

1.6
):

Conjecture 1.7
.

[
Hab10
, Conjecture]

Let
C\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}
be an irreducible algebraic curve defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
that is not special.
There exists a positive constant
c_{4}
such that, for all
s=(s_{1},s_{2})\in C(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
satisfying
\Phi_{M}(s_{1},s_{2})=0
for some positive integer
M
, we have

h(s)\leq c_{\ref*{C:habegger-conj-mult}}\max\{1,\log(M)\}.

The majority of this article is dedicated to proving a height bound, somewhat weaker than
1.7
, in the case of curves that intersect infinity. We shall prove the following height bound or, more precisely, a technical variant of it.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
C\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
and
\Sigma
be as in
Theorem

1.6
(in particular,
C
intersects infinity). For every
\epsilon>0
, there exist constants
c_{5}
and
c_{6}
such that, for all

,
M
,
N
as in
Theorem

1.6
,
we have

h(s)<c_{\ref*{C:andre-bound-mult}}\max\{[\mathbb{Q}(s):\mathbb{Q}]^{c_{\ref*{C:andre-bound-exp}}},M^{\epsilon}\}.

The bound in
Theorem

1.8
is weaker than
c_{\ref*{C:habegger-conj-mult}}\max\{1,\log(M)\}
in

, but it is sufficient to deduce

1.6
in the same way as

, Lemma 4.2]
follows from (

).
Note that, unlike
1.7
and
[

, Thm. 1.1]
, we require two modular relations in

.
Two modular relations are required to “eliminate

” in the relations between archimedean periods (see

).

We will not prove or use

1.8
directly, but rather a variant applying to a suitable finite cover of

.

1.B.
Comparison with previous applications of André’s method

As in
[
And89
, Ch. X]
, our proof of
Theorem

1.8
is based on studying the relative periods of a family of abelian varieties
\mathcal{A}
over the curve
C
.
The Taylor series of some of these period functions (the “locally invariant periods”) around
a point where
\mathcal{A}
degenerates to a torus (in the case of
Theorem

1.8
, this point is
(\infty,\dotsc,\infty)
) are power series with special Diophantine properties, called G-functions.

At points
s\in C
where the corresponding abelian variety
\mathcal{A}_{s}
has additional endomorphisms, there are polynomial relations between the evaluations at
s
of these G-functions.

Theorem

1.8
follows using a theorem of Bombieri: if there is a polynomial relation between the evaluations of a set of G-functions at a
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
-point
s
, valid at all places for which
s
lies inside the radius of convergence of the G-functions, and not coming from a “trivial” relation between the G-functions themselves, then the height of
s
is bounded in terms of the degree of the polynomial relation.

In
[
And89
]
, and in previous applications of
[
And89
]
to Galois orbit bounds for the Zilber–Pink conjecture, only the archimedean evaluations of G-functions were interpreted as periods.
In order to apply Bombieri’s theorem without finding relations between non-archimedean evaluations of G-functions, the extra endomorphisms of
\mathcal{A}_{s}
were used to ensure that
s
lies outside the radius of convergence of the G-functions at all non-archimedean places.
This is the reason for the condition appearing in
[
And89
]
that
\End(\mathcal{A}_{s})
does not inject into
\End(\mathbb{G}_{m}^{g})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}\cong\mathrm{M}_{g}(\mathbb{Q})
(where
g=\dim(\mathcal{A}_{s})
).

On the other hand, in
Theorem

1.3
, the exceptional abelian varieties
\mathcal{A}_{s}
have endomorphism rings which inject into
\mathrm{M}_{4}(\mathbb{Q})
.
For example, a product of non-CM elliptic curves
E_{1}\times E_{2}\times E_{3}\times E_{4}
, where there are isogenies
E_{1}\to E_{2}
and
E_{3}\to E_{4}
, has endomorphism algebra
\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathbb{Q})\times\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathbb{Q})
.
Thus, in order to apply Bombieri’s theorem, we need relations valid at non-archimedean places.

Relations between non-archimedean evaluations of G-functions were used in
[
And95
, Théorème 1]
. However in this result, as in
[
And89
]
, the global relation needed for Bombieri’s theorem was obtained as a product of relations, one for each place. Thus in order to control the degree of this global relation, it was necessary to limit the number of non-archimedean places at which the G-functions could converge (by imposing an integrality condition at other places).
Our construction of relations instead directly constructs a polynomial relation simultaneously valid at all non-archimedean places, based on the Tate uniformisation of elliptic curves in a family degenerating to a torus.
At archimedean places, we are still using the previous approach of taking a product of local relations, one for each place.

Since the first preprint of this article became public, several other papers have appeared, all building on the aforementioned approach to Zilber–Pink using relations between G-functions. These include the paper
[
Urb
]
of Urbanik and the papers
[
Papb
,
Pap24
,
Papa
]
of Papas, as well as our sequel to this article
[
DO
]
. For a detailed survey of the literature to date, see
[
DO
, Section 1.D]
.

1.C.
Effectivity

Our height bound
Theorem

1.8
and Galois bound
Theorem

1.6
can be made effective.
The main work required to do this would be to determine the size and global radius of the G-functions
F_{\lambda}
and
G_{\lambda}
in equation (
27
) and the size and singularities of the associated differential operator.

As previously mentioned, the Zilber–Pink conjecture in the case of intersections between curves
C\subset Y(1)^{n}
and
non-strongly special
subvarieties was already proved by
[
HP12
, Theorem 2]
, without any condition on the curve
C
.
When the special subvariety is defined by one CM coordinate and one pair of isogenous coordinates, the underlying Galois bound
[
HP12
, Lemma 4.4]
was ineffective due to its dependence on an estimate for Siegel zeros.
(The remaining case, namely the André–Oort conjecture for a curve in
Y(1)^{n}
, is known effectively by a different method, due to Kühne
[
Küh12
]
.)
Our proof of
Theorem

1.6
could be adapted to apply to intersections with non-strongly special subvarieties, provided that
C
intersects infinity, thus leading to an effective Galois bound for such cases – the bound on CM discriminants would come from a height bound as in
Theorem

1.8
and an estimate of Masser–Wüstholz type.

Note that the full strength of this paper’s ideas are not required to deal with intersections with non-strongly special subvarieties in
Y(1)^{n}
, because having one CM coordinate is sufficient to prevent such intersections being
p
-adically close to infinity for any
p
.
Hence, a height bound can be proved for such intersections using only relations between archimedean periods, using the methods of
[
And89
, Ch. X]
.

Thus, if the relevant point-counting step of the Pila–Zannier strategy were to be made effective, that could be combined with Galois bounds using the techniques of this article, to obtain an effective proof of the Zilber–Pink conjecture for curves in
Y(1)^{n}
which intersect infinity.

2.
Preliminaries

2.A.
Embeddings of a number field

Let
K
be a number field.
We define an
embedding
of
K
to be a homomorphism
K\to\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{C}_{p}
for some prime
p
.
If
\iota
is an embedding of
K
, we write
\mathbb{C}_{\iota}
for the complete algebraically closed field which is the target of
\iota
. We write
D(0,r,\mathbb{C}_{\iota})
for the open disc in
\mathbb{C}_{\iota}
of radius
r
, centred at
0
.

If
s\in K
, we write
s^{\iota}=\iota(s)\in\mathbb{C}_{\iota}
.
Similarly, if
S
is an algebraic object over
K
(in particular, a polynomial, a power series, an algebraic variety or a morphism of algebraic varieties), then we write
S^{\iota}
for the base change of
S
by
\iota\colon K\to\mathbb{C}_{\iota}
.
This superscript notation sometimes carries the connotation that
S^{\iota}
should be interpreted as an analytic, rather than algebraic, object over
\mathbb{C}_{\iota}
: in particular, if
f\in K[\![X]\!]
then
f^{\iota}
denotes the analytic function obtained by evaluating
f
on its disc of convergence in
\mathbb{C}_{\iota}
.

We have sought to minimise the use of rigid geometry in this paper (it is essential only in section
4.B
).
Nevertheless, whenever we wish to refer specifically to the complex or rigid analytification of an algebraic variety (after base change to
\mathbb{C}_{\iota}
), we denote this by the superscript
{\iota\textrm{-}\mathrm{an}}
.

We normalise the absolute value on
\mathbb{C}_{\iota}
so that it extends the standard archimedean or
p
-adic absolute value on
\mathbb{Q}
.

2.B.
Non-archimedean power series

Let
f(X)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}X^{n}\in K[\![X]\!]
be a power series, where
(K,\lvert\cdot\rvert)
is a complete field equipped with an absolute value.
We write
R(f)
for the radius of convergence of
f
.
Recall that

R(f)=\frac{1}{\limsup_{n\to\infty}\lvert a_{n}\rvert^{1/n}}.

When
(K,\lvert\cdot\rvert)
is non-archimedean, we also define

R^{\dagger}(f)=1/\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}\lvert a_{n}\rvert^{1/n}=\inf\{\lvert\rho\rvert:\rho\in K^{\times},f(\rho^{-1}X)-f(0)\in\mathcal{O}_{K}[\![X]\!]\}.

Note that we ignore
a_{0}
in the definition of
R^{\dagger}
.

Clearly
R^{\dagger}(f)\leq R(f)
.
It is easy to prove that if

R(f)>0
, then
R^{\dagger}(f)>0
.

The quantity
R^{\dagger}(f)
is useful for understanding composition of power series over non-archimedean fields.
We note the following bound, which can be seen as a consequence of the non-archimedean mean value theorem:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(K,\lvert\cdot\rvert)
be a non-archimedean complete field equipped with an absolute value.
Let
g\in K[\![X]\!]
and
x\in K
.
If
\lvert x\rvert\leq R^{\dagger}(g)
, then

\lvert g(x)-g(0)\rvert\leq R^{\dagger}(g)^{-1}\lvert x\rvert.

Proof.

Let
g(X)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}X^{n}
.
Note that
R^{\dagger}(g)=\inf_{n\geq 1}\lvert b_{n}\rvert^{-1/n}
.
Hence, for each
n\geq 1
,
R^{\dagger}(g)^{n}\leq\lvert b_{n}\rvert^{-1}
.
Therefore, for each
n\geq 1
,

(2)

\lvert b_{n}x^{n}\rvert\leq\lvert b_{n}\rvert\lvert x\rvert R^{\dagger}(g)^{n-1}\leq R^{\dagger}(g)^{-1}\lvert x\rvert\cdot\lvert b_{n}\rvert R^{\dagger}(g)^{n}\leq R^{\dagger}(g)^{-1}\lvert x\rvert\cdot 1.

Because the absolute value is non-archimedean, this implies that
\lvert g(x)-g(0)\rvert\leq R^{\dagger}(g)^{-1}\lvert x\rvert
.
∎

The following lemma is obvious from the perspective of rigid geometry (it is a statement about composing morphisms of rigid open discs).
However we have given a proof in order to minimise this paper’s dependence on rigid geometry.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(K,\lvert\cdot\rvert)
be a non-archimedean complete field equipped with an absolute value.
Let
f,g\in K[\![X]\!]
with
R(f)\geq 1
and
g(0)=0
.
Then
R(f\circ g)\geq R^{\dagger}(g)
and, for all
x\in K
satisfying
\lvert x\rvert<R^{\dagger}(g)
, we have
f(g(x))=(f\circ g)(x)
.

Proof.

Let
f(X)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}X^{n}
and
g(X)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}X^{n}
.

Let
x\in K
with
\lvert x\rvert<R^{\dagger}(g)
.
Let
M_{\lvert x\rvert}(g)=\sup_{n\geq 1}\lvert b_{n}x^{n}\rvert
.
By inequality (
2
) from the proof of
Lemma

2.1
and by our hypotheses, we have

M_{\lvert x\rvert}(g)\leq R^{\dagger}(g)^{-1}\lvert x\rvert<1\leq R(f).

Since also
\lvert x\rvert<R(g)
, the conditions of
[
Rob00
, 6.1.5, Theorem]
are satisfied. Therefore
R(f\circ g)>\lvert x\rvert
and
f(g(x))=(f\circ g)(x)
.
∎

Over
\mathbb{C}
, composition of power series is described by the following classical result:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f,g\in\mathbb{C}[\![X]\!]
with
g(0)=0
.
Let

s=\sup\{r\leq R(g):\lvert g(x)\rvert<R(f)\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{C}\text{ with }\lvert x\rvert<r\}.

Note that if
R(f)>0
and
R(g)>0
, then
s>0
.
Then
R(f\circ g)\geq s
and, for all
x\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying
\lvert x\rvert<s
, we have
f(g(x))=(f\circ g)(x)
.

2.C.
G-functions

Definition
.

[
And89
, p. 1]

A
G-function
is a power series
y(X)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}X^{n}
whose coefficients
a_{n}
belong to a number field
K
, which satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

there exists

Lemma 2.9

Let

. There exists a finite set

such that, for all

and all

at

at
\xi

Let

\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[X][Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}]
generated by the (homogeneous) functional relations between

satisfying: each homogeneous component of

(Here “homogeneous” means homogeneous as a polynomial in

with coefficients in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[X]

, the ideal of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[X][Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}]

For any polynomial ring

3.

3.A.
The “
1/j

Let

and let

.
Let

\mathcal{E}=\bigl\{([x:y:z],s)\in\mathbb{P}^{2}\times S:\\

This is the
S
-smooth locus of an elliptic scheme
\pi\colon\mathcal{E}\to S
.
Indeed, the fibres of
\mathcal{E}
over

are elliptic curves and
\mathcal{E}_{s}
has

-invariant

.
The fibre
\mathcal{E}_{0}

Therefore, the standard Weierstrass group law makes

into an
S
-group scheme, with

.

We obtain an invariant holomorphic differential form on

3.B.

Let

.

We shall use
[

Following these references, we will work mostly with explicit power series. We will require rigid geometry only in equation (

[

Proof.

The power series

Furthermore,

It follows that

3.D.
Periods

In order to define a power series

under the archimedean embedding

For each

Lemma 3.3
.

is the complex evaluation of a power series

Proof.

Let

-adic evaluation of

4.
Period relations for isogenous points

In this section, we show how isogenies between elliptic curves over a number field
\hat{K}
lead to polynomial relations between corresponding evaluations of
F
and
G
.
We construct these relations separately for archimedean and non-archimedean embeddings of
\hat{K}
.

Definition
.

If
N
is a non-zero integer, we write

for the number of positive divisors of

.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\hat{K}
be a number field.
Let

,

be positive integers.
Let

be points such that:

(1)

of degree

defined over
\hat{K}
;

(2)

there is an isogeny
\mathcal{E}_{s_{3}}\to\mathcal{E}_{s_{4}}
of degree

defined over
\hat{K}
.

Then:

(1)

There exists a non-zero homogeneous polynomial
P_{\infty}\in\hat{K}[Y_{1},Z_{1},\dotsc,Y_{4},Z_{4}]
of degree at most
2[\hat{K}:\mathbb{Q}]
such that, for every archimedean embedding
\iota
of
\hat{K}
satisfying

for all

, we have

Furthermore,
P_{\infty}
is not in the ideal
\langle Y_{1}-Y_{3},Z_{1}-Z_{3}\rangle
.

(2)

There exists a non-zero homogeneous polynomial
P_{fin}\in\hat{K}[Y_{1},Y_{2}]
of degree at most
2d(M)
such that, for every non-archimedean embedding
\iota
of
\hat{K}
satisfying
\lvert s_{i}^{\iota}\rvert<1
for
i=1,2
, we have

P_{fin}^{\iota}(F^{\iota}(s_{1}^{\iota}),F^{\iota}(s_{2}^{\iota}))=0.

Proof.

(1) Let
P_{\infty}
be the product of the polynomials
P_{\iota}
given by
Proposition

4.4
, as
\iota
runs through the archimedean embeddings
\iota
of
\hat{K}
.

(2) Let
P_{fin}
be the product of the polynomials
P_{m}
from
Proposition

4.7
(applied to
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
), as
m
runs through the divisors (positive and negative) of

.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

For each archimedean embedding
\iota
,

implies that
\lvert s^{\iota}\rvert<\min(R(F^{\iota}),R(G^{\iota}))
. Hence the evaluations in

Note that the relations at non-archimedean embeddings involve only
F
, not
G
.
This enables us to construct the relation without knowing a geometric interpretation of the
p
-adic evaluations of
G
.
It also saves us the effort of estimating

which would otherwise be needed to control the set on which

4.1
(2) makes sense.

The non-archimedean relations involve only
s_{1},s_{2}
, not
s_{3},s_{4}
.
This is not important, except that it avoids the need to assert that
P_{fin}\not\in\langle Y_{1}-Y_{3},Z_{1}-Z_{3}\rangle
.

We may need to apply

4.1
with

.
The assertion that

in

is needed to ensure that

does not collapse to

upon making the substitution

,

.

Remark 4.3
.

The polynomial
P_{\infty}
is obtained by finding a relation for each archimedean embedding of
\hat{K}
(

4.4
), then multiplying these together. There are
[\hat{K}:\mathbb{Q}]
archimedean embeddings, leading to a bound for

.

The polynomial

is similarly constructed as a product, where one of the factors gives a relation between evaluations of

at each non-archimedean embedding of

(

).
However, there are infinitely many non-archimedean embeddings, so it is important that the relations at non-archimedean embeddings are not independent of each other: instead

gives a finite collection of polynomials (of controlled size) whose members give relations between evaluations of

at every non-archimedean embedding where

and

are small enough.

4.A.

To obtain a period relation at an archimedean embedding of

, we require two isogenies

and
\mathcal{E}_{s_{3}}\to\mathcal{E}_{s_{4}}
.
A single isogeny gives a relation whose coefficients involve

, not just algebraic numbers. A second isogeny gives a second such relation, allowing us to eliminate

.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\hat{K}
be a number field.
Let
M
,
N
be positive integers.
Let
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4}\in S^{*}(\hat{K})
with the following properties:

(1)

there is an isogeny
\mathcal{E}_{s_{1}}\to\mathcal{E}_{s_{2}}
of degree

defined over
\hat{K}
;

(2)

there is an isogeny
\mathcal{E}_{s_{3}}\to\mathcal{E}_{s_{4}}
of degree

defined over
\hat{K}
.

Then, for each archimedean embedding
\iota
of
\hat{K}
satisfying

for all

, there exists a non-zero polynomial
P_{\iota}\in\hat{K}[Y_{1},Z_{1},\dotsc,Y_{4},Z_{4}]
, homogeneous of degree
2
, such that

P_{\iota}^{\iota}(F^{\iota}(s_{1}^{\iota}),G^{\iota}(s_{1}^{\iota}),\dotsc,F^{\iota}(s_{4}^{\iota}),G^{\iota}(s_{4}^{\iota}))=0.

Furthermore,
P_{\iota}
is not in the ideal
\langle Y_{1}-Y_{3},Z_{1}-Z_{3}\rangle

Proof.

Let
\iota
be an embedding
\hat{K}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying
s_{i}^{\iota}\in\Delta_{S}
for all
i=1,2,3,4
.
To reduce notation, we omit mention of
\iota
for the rest of this proof.

We consider first the pair
s_{1}
,
s_{2}
, obtaining an inhomogeneous relation of degree at most
2
between the
\iota
-adic evaluations of

whose value is a rational multiple of
1/2\pi i
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{E}_{s_{1}}\to\mathcal{E}_{s_{2}}
be an isogeny of degree

.
Note that
f^{*}(\omega_{s_{2}})\in\Omega^{1}(\mathcal{E}_{s_{1}}/\hat{K})
, which is a 1-dimensional
\hat{K}
-vector space, so there is an element

such that

(12)

f^{*}(\omega_{s_{2}})=a\omega_{s_{1}}.

There are also

such that

where

denotes the class of a differential form in

Since
[\omega_{s}]
,

form a symplectic basis of

Looking at homology, there are
p,q,r,s\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\displaystyle f_{*}(\gamma_{s_{1}})

\displaystyle=p\gamma_{s_{2}}+r\delta_{s_{2}},

\displaystyle f_{*}(\delta_{s_{1}})

\displaystyle=q\gamma_{s_{2}}+s\delta_{s_{2}}.

Here
\det\left(\begin{matrix}p&q\\

Since

, and so on for

,
s_{3}
,
s_{4}
.
Hence, using equations such as

\int_{\gamma_{s_{1}}}f^{*}(\omega_{s_{2}})=\int_{f_{*}(\gamma_{s_{1}})}\omega_{s_{2}},

we obtain the period relations

(13)

\left(\begin{matrix}a&0\\
b&d\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}F(s_{1})&F^{*}(s_{1})\\
G(s_{1})&G^{*}(s_{1})\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}F(s_{2})&F^{*}(s_{2})\\
G(s_{2})&G^{*}(s_{2})\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}p&q\\
r&s\end{matrix}\right).

Multiplying on the left by the row vector
(-G(s_{2}),F(s_{2}))
and using (

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}\bigl(-aG(s_{2})+bF(s_{2}),dF(s_{2})\bigr)\left(\begin{matrix}F(s_{1})&F^{*}(s_{1})\\

\displaystyle=\bigl(-G(s_{2})F(s_{2})+F(s_{2})G(s_{2}),-G(s_{2})F^{*}(s_{2})+F(s_{2})G^{*}(s_{2})\bigr)\left(\begin{matrix}p&q\\

\displaystyle=\bigl(0,1/(2\pi i)\bigr)\left(\begin{matrix}p&q\\

Multiplying on the right by the column vector

between the

,

,

,

vanishes at
(F(s_{1}),G(s_{1}),\dotsc,F(s_{4}),G(s_{4}))

5.
Proof of lower bound for Galois orbits

In this section we prove the height bound
Theorem

1.8
and deduce the Galois orbits bound

1.6
.
The proof follows the strategy of
[

, Ch. X]
, using the polynomial relations between periods of fibres with exceptional isogenies obtained in
Theorem

4.1
.
In particular, the work of finding relations between non-archimedean evaluations of

was already carried out in
Theorem

4.1
(2).
Thus the only new work in this section needed to make use of non-archimedean evaluations of G-functions is to pay attention to non-archimedean convergence of power series.

In order to be confident that the argument is valid with non-archimedean evaluations, we have written the full argument in detail. This includes expanding the remark on
[

, p. 202]
about finite ramification at

when choosing the local parameter, via

.
To help the reader follow the essential structure of the argument, we have included in Section

a summary for a special case in which many of the technical difficulties do not arise.

We found Papas’s paper
[

]
very helpful when writing this section, especially for describing the functional relations using André’s Normal Monodromy Theorem

]
, which is simpler than the approach in
[

, X, Lemma 3.3]
.

Please note that the notation

in this section will refer to a collection of base points in the curve

(as defined two paragraphs above

).
This differs from section

5.B.
Outline of technical issues in the general case

The proof of a special case of
Theorem

1.8
in Section
5.A
made use of two properties of the morphism

: the only zero of
x
was at
s_{0}=(\infty,\dotsc,\infty)
and

was a local parameter at

.
A morphism with these properties must have degree

is a rational curve.

In order to prove

when

is not rational, we will replace
\overline{C}
by another curve

, equipped with a surjective morphism

and with a finite set of points

mapping to

. We will choose

so that there is a morphism

which vanishes only at

and which is a local parameter at each of

.
This comes as close as we can to the properties of

used in Section

, at the cost of having finitely many degeneration points

instead of the single point

.

If

and

is small for some embedding

of

, then

is close to one of the

,

might be close to

for different

depending on the embedding

. We therefore have to look for relations between locally invariant periods of the semiabelian schemes

near each of

(where

denotes the reciprocal of a coordinate on

, composed with the map

Working with periods around different degeneration points is awkward, especially when it comes to understanding functional relations between the period functions. We therefore impose an extra condition on

: for every

has an automorphism such that

Their locally invariant periods around

After the technical setup summarised above, we will finally be able to complete the proof by finding global relations between evaluations of these G-functions at exceptional points along the lines explained in Section

.

5.D.

We wish to pull back the

It is easier to compare periods of a larger number of semiabelian schemes over a neighbourhood of a single point, so we will instead consider the semiabelian schemes

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
S
be a connected normal noetherian scheme with geometric generic point
\bar{\eta}
.
Let
A
,
B
be abelian schemes over
S
.
Suppose that there exists an isogeny
\phi\colon A_{\bar{\eta}}\to B_{\bar{\eta}}
.
Then, for every algebraically closed field
\overline{K}
and every geometric point
\bar{s}\in S(\overline{K})
, there exists an isogeny
\phi_{\bar{s}}\colon A_{\bar{s}}\to B_{\bar{s}}
of the same degree as
\phi
.

Proof.

Let
\ell
be a prime different from the characteristic of
\overline{K}
.
Let
S[\ell^{-1}]=S\times_{\Spec(\mathbb{Z})}\Spec(\mathbb{Z}[\ell^{-1}])
, so that
\bar{s}
factors through
S[\ell^{-1}]
.
Let

\rho_{\ell}\colon\pi_{1}^{et}(S[\ell^{-1}])\to\GL(T_{\ell}(A\times_{S}B)_{\bar{\eta}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}}\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})

denote the
\ell
-adic monodromy representation, and let
G_{\ell}
denote the Zariski closure of the image of
\rho_{\ell}
.
Let
S^{\prime}
be the finite étale cover of

corresponding to the subgroup
\rho_{\ell}^{-1}(G_{\ell}^{\circ})\subset\pi_{1}^{et}(S[\ell^{-1}])
.

Let
\eta^{\prime}
denote the generic point of
S^{\prime}
.
By
[
GP23
, Lemma 2.8 and Lemma 3.4]
, the base-change maps

are bijective (the core of the proof of this is
[
FC90
, Prop. I.2.7]
).
Hence
\phi\colon A_{\bar{\eta}}\to B_{\bar{\eta}}
is the base change of a homomorphism
\phi^{\prime}\colon A_{S^{\prime}}\to B_{S^{\prime}}
.
By
[
GP23
, Lemma 3.6]
,

is an isogeny.

Choose a geometric point

which lifts
\bar{s}
.
Note that

is equal to the specialisation of

at
\bar{s}^{\prime}
, and similarly for

.
Hence, the specialisation
\phi^{\prime}_{\bar{s}^{\prime}}
of

is an isogeny

.

Since

is a locally free finite

-group scheme,
\deg(\phi^{\prime}_{\bar{s}^{\prime}})=\deg(\phi)
.
∎

5.E.
Neighbourhoods

It is well-known that, if
x
is a local parameter at a point
s_{0}
on an algebraic curve
C
over a number field
K
, then for every embedding
\iota\colon K\to\mathbb{C}_{\iota}
,
x^{\iota}
restricts to an analytic isomorphism between a sufficiently small neighbourhood of
s_{0}^{\iota}
in
C^{\iota}(\mathbb{C}_{\iota})
and an open disc in
\mathbb{C}_{\iota}
.
The following lemma is a formal statement of the properties we shall require of these analytic isomorphisms.
A key point for us is that the discs can be taken to have radius at least
1
for almost all embeddings
\iota
; since this fact is perhaps not so well-known as the rest, we have written out a proof of the lemma.

In the subsequent sections of the paper, we will only use the neighbourhood
U_{\iota,1}
.
However, the full collection of open neighbourhoods

are needed in the proof of the properties of

.
In particular, (iii) is used in the proof that

is injective, and this in turn is used in the proof of (v).

Definition
.

Let
C
be an algebraic curve over a field
K
and let

be a smooth
K
-point. Let
\mathfrak{m}
denote the maximal ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{C,s}

\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{C,s}
denote the
\mathfrak{m}
-adic completion of

. Let

denote a local parameter for
C
at
s
and let

denote the unique isomorphism of topological
K
-algebras which satisfies
\Tay(x)=X
. For any

which is regular at
s
, we refer to

as the

of
f
around

in terms of

.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
C_{0}
be a smooth algebraic curve over a number field

.
Let

be a rational function of degree

, such that

has
\ell

.
Then for each embedding
\iota
of

, there exists a real number

and open sets

with the following properties:

(1)

for almost all embeddings
\iota
of

;

(2)

;

(3)

for each
\iota
, the sets

are pairwise disjoint and

is equal to the preimage under

and

For an archimedean embedding

and

satisfying (ii)–(v) is classical.
We can ignore finitely many embeddings in (i), so for the rest of this proof we consider only non-archimedean embeddings.

Let

be the smooth projective model of

.
Embed
\overline{C_{0}}
in some projective space

.
After applying a linear automorphism of

(regarded as an open subset of

).
Let

.
Let

denote the coordinate functions.

Let

denote the finite set of

-points of

.

Let

around

For each non-archimedean embedding

\displaystyle=\min\bigl(\{1\}

.
Let

Since

5.H.
Global relations

Let
\mathcal{G}
denote the set of G-functions defined in (

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=\{F_{\lambda},G_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}.

In order to control the radii within which global relations have to hold, we shall add an additional G-function

Choose

\lvert\zeta^{\iota}\rvert\leq r_{\iota}
for all embeddings

of

.
(By (

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
M
,
N
be positive integers.
Let
s\in C_{0}^{*}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
be a point such that there exist
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}
such that
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
,
i_{3}\neq i_{4}
,
\{i_{1},i_{2}\}\neq\{i_{3},i_{4}\}
and:

(1)

there exists an isogeny
\mathcal{A}_{i_{1},s}\to\mathcal{A}_{i_{2},s}
of degree
M
;

(2)

there exists an isogeny
\mathcal{A}_{i_{3},s}\to\mathcal{A}_{i_{4},s}
of degree

.

Then there exists a non-zero global relation between the evaluations of the G-functions
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
at

of degree at most
288\ell[K(s):\mathbb{Q}]+2\ell d(MT^{2})
, where

is defined at the end of section

.
This relation does not involve

.

Proof.

The proof has two parts: first we construct a polynomial

, then we prove that
Q
is a global relation.

Let
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}
be the indices appearing in the statement of the lemma.
The hypotheses
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
,
i_{3}\neq i_{4}
,
\{i_{1},i_{2}\}\neq\{i_{3},i_{4}\}
imply that either
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}
are distinct, or that one of
i_{1},i_{2}
is equal to one of
i_{3},i_{4}
and there are no other equalities between the indices. In the latter case,
assume without loss of generality that
i_{1}=i_{3}
.

The strategy is as follows.
For each embedding
\hat{\iota}
of
K(s)
,
if
\lvert x^{\iota}(s^{\hat{\iota}})\rvert
is small, then for some
k\in\{1,\dotsc,\ell\}
, the point
\sigma_{k}^{-1}(s)^{\hat{\iota}}
is close to

.
Consequently
\hat{\iota}
-adic locally invariant periods of the elliptic curves
\mathcal{A}^{\hat{\iota}}_{[k,i_{j}],\sigma_{k}^{-1}(s)}
can be interpreted as evaluations of the G-functions
F_{[k,i_{j}]}
and
G_{[k,i_{j}]}
at
x(s)^{\hat{\iota}}
.
The isogeny
\mathcal{A}_{i_{1},s}\to\mathcal{A}_{i_{2},s}
from hypothesis (i) gives rise to an isogeny
\mathcal{A}_{[k,i_{1}],\sigma_{k}^{-1}(s)}\to\mathcal{A}_{[k,i_{2}],\sigma_{k}^{-1}(s)}
, and similarly for
i_{3},i_{4}
(after replacing
K(s)
by a controlled extension
\hat{K}
).
Hence, via
Theorem

4.1
, we get an
\hat{\iota}
-adic relation between the evaluations of
F_{[k,i_{j}]}
and
G_{[k,i_{j}]}
at
x(s)
.

In the above outline of the strategy, the index
k
depends on the embedding
\hat{\iota}
.
Therefore, we need to apply
Theorem

4.1
once for each
k
and multiply the resulting relations together to obtain a global relation.

For each

and

, write

.

By the definition of

and of

, there exists an isogeny

of degree dividing

.
By

5.4
, this implies that there exists an isogeny

of the same degree.
Composing with isomorphisms coming from fibre product diagrams (in particular,

), we obtain an isogeny

of degree dividing

.
We similarly obtain an isogeny

of degree dividing

.
By hypothesis, we have an isogeny
\mathcal{A}_{i_{1},s}\to\mathcal{A}_{i_{2},s}
of degree

We thus obtain a chain of isogenies

has degree dividing

.

Similarly, we obtain an isogeny

.

By

,

and

are each defined over extensions of

of degree at most

.
Thus we can choose an extension

of degree at most

and

.

Applying

4.1
to the isogenies

and

, we obtain non-zero homogeneous polynomials

of

5.I.
Functional relations

Our aim in this section is to prove that there are no non-zero (homogeneous) functional relations between the G-functions
\{F_{\lambda},G_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}\cup\{H\}
.
For this, it suffices to consider the evaluations of
F_{\lambda}
,
G_{\lambda}
and
H
at a single archimedean embedding
\iota
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the fibre product over
C_{0}
of the semiabelian schemes
\mathcal{A}_{\lambda}
for
\lambda\in\Lambda
.
Let
\mathcal{A}^{*}=\mathcal{A}|_{C_{0}^{*}}
and let
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}:\mathcal{A}^{*}\to C_{0}^{*}
denote the structure morphism.
Because the elements of
\Lambda
lie in distinct classes for the equivalence relation
\sim
, the factors of
\mathcal{A}_{\bar{\eta}}
are pairwise non-isogenous elliptic curves, so the generic Mumford–Tate group of
\mathcal{A}^{*\iota}
is

\displaystyle\left\{(g_{\lambda})\in\mathbf{GL}_{2}^{\Lambda}:\det(g_{\lambda})\text{ is the same for all }\lambda\in\Lambda\right\}.

Let
V
denote a simply connected open subset of
U_{\iota,1}^{*}\subset C_{0}^{*\iota}(\mathbb{C})
.
The local system
R_{1}\pi_{\lambda*}^{\iota}(\mathbb{Q})|_{V}
is trivial and has rank
2
, so we may choose a section
\delta_{\iota,\lambda}\in R_{1}\pi_{\lambda*}^{\iota}(\mathbb{Q})(V)
such that
\gamma_{\iota,\lambda}
and
\delta_{\iota,\lambda}
form an oriented basis for
R_{1}\pi_{\lambda*}^{\iota}(\mathbb{Q})|_{V}
.

We define holomorphic functions on
V
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\lambda,1,1}(s)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma_{\iota,\lambda,s}}\omega^{\iota}_{\lambda,s},\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\lambda,1,2}(s)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\delta_{\iota,\lambda,s}}\omega^{\iota}_{\lambda,s},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\lambda,2,1}(s)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma_{\iota,\lambda,s}}\eta^{\iota}_{\lambda,s},\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\lambda,1,2}(s)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\delta_{\iota,\lambda,s}}\eta^{\iota}_{\lambda,s},

forming the full period matrix of the elliptic curve
\mathcal{A}_{\lambda,s}^{\iota}

From (
