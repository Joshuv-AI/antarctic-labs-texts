In-and-Out: Algorithmic Diffusion for Sampling Convex Bodies
​​​ Yunbum Kook Santosh S. Vempala Matthew S. Zhang

Abstract
We present a new random walk for uniformly sampling high-dimensional convex bodies. It achieves state-of-the-art runtime complexity with stronger guarantees on the output than previously known, namely in Rényi divergence (which implies TV, 𝒲 2 \mathcal{W}_{2} , KL, χ 2 \chi^{2} ). The proof departs from known approaches for polytime algorithms for the problem — we utilize a stochastic diffusion perspective to show contraction to the target distribution with the rate of convergence determined by functional isoperimetric constants of the target distribution.

Introduction

Generating random samples from a high-dimensional convex body is a basic algorithmic problem with myriad connections and applications. The core of the celebrated result of
[
DFK91
]
, giving a randomized polynomial-time algorithm for computing the volume of a convex body, was the first polynomial-time algorithm for uniformly sampling convex bodies.
In the decades since, the study of sampling has led to a long series of improvements in its algorithmic complexity
[
LS90
,
LS93
,
KLS97
,
LV06
,
CV18
]
, often based on uncovering new mathematical/geometric structure, establishing connections to other fields (e.g., functional analysis, matrix concentration) and developing new tools for proving isoperimetric inequalities and analyzing Markov chains. With the proliferation of data and the increasing importance of machine learning, sampling has also become an essential algorithmic tool, with applications needing samplers in very high dimension, e.g.,
scientific computing
[
CV16
,
HCT+17
,
KLSV22
]
, systems biology
[
LNP12
,
TSF+13
]
, differential privacy
[
MT07
,
Mir17
]
and machine learning
[
BCJ+19
,
Sta20
]
.

Samplers for convex bodies are based on Markov chains (see Sec. §
1.4
for a summary). Their analysis is based on bounding the
conductance
of the associated Markov chain, which in turn bounds the mixing rate. Analyzing the conductance requires combining delicate geometric arguments with (Cheeger) isoperimetric inequalities for convex bodies. An archetypal example of the latter is the following:
for any measurable partition
S_{1},S_{2},S_{3}
of a convex body
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\operatorname{vol}(S_{3})\geq\frac{d(S_{1},S_{2})}{C_{\mathcal{K}}}\min\{\operatorname{vol}(S_{1}),\operatorname{vol}(S_{2})\}\,,

where
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is the (minimum) Euclidean distance, and
C_{\mathcal{K}}
is an isoperimetric constant of the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{K}
. (The KLS conjecture posits that
C_{\mathcal{K}}=O(1)
for any convex body
\mathcal{K}
in
isotropic position
, i.e., under the normalization that a random point from
\mathcal{K}
has identity covariance). The coefficient
C_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}
is bounded by the Poincaré constant of the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{K}
(and they are in fact asymptotically equal). The classical proof of conductance uses geometric properties of the random walk at hand to reduce the analysis to a suitable isoperimetric inequality (see e.g.,
[
LS93
,
Vem05
]
). The end result is a guarantee on the number of steps after which the total variation distance (TV distance) between the current distribution and the target is bounded by a desired error parameter.
This framework has been widely used and effective in analyzing an array of candidate samplers, e.g.,
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}

[
KLS97
]
,
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}

[
Lov99
,
LV06
]
,
\mathsf{Riemannian\ Hamiltonian\ Monte\ Carlo}

[
LV18
]
etc.

A different approach, studied intensively over the past decade, is based on
diffusion
. The basic idea is to first analyze a continuous-time diffusion process, typically modeled by a
stochastic differential equation
(SDE), and then show that a suitable time-discretization of the process, sometimes together with a Metropolis filter, converges to the desired distribution efficiently.
A major success along this line is the
\mathsf{Unadjusted\ Langevin\ Algorithm}
and its variants, studied first in
[
BGHM95
]
. These algorithms have strong guarantees for sampling “nice” distributions
[
DT12
,
Dal17
,
DMM19
,
VW23
]
, such as ones that are strongly logconcave, or more generally distributions satisfying isoperimetric inequalities, while also obeying some smoothness conditions. The analysis of these algorithms is markedly different from the conductance approach, and typically yields guarantees in stronger metrics such as the
\mathsf{KL}
-divergence.

Our starting point is the following question:

Can diffusion-based approaches be used for the problem of sampling convex bodies?

Despite remarkable progress, thus far, constrained sampling problems have evaded the diffusion approach, except as a high-level analogy (e.g., the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
can be viewed as a discretization of Brownian motion, but this alone does not suggest a route for analysis) or with significantly worse convergence rates (e.g.,
[
BDMP17
,
BEL18
]
).

Our main finding is a simple diffusion-based algorithm that can be mapped to a stochastic process such that the rate of convergence is bounded directly by an appropriate functional inequality for the target distribution. As a consequence, for the first time, we obtain clean end-to-end guarantees in the Rényi divergence (which implies guarantees in other well-known quantities such as
\mathcal{W}_{2},\mathsf{TV},\mathsf{KL},\chi^{2}
etc.), while giving state-of-the-art runtime complexity for sampling convex bodies (e.g.,
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
or
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}

[
LS93
,
KLS97
]
).
Besides being a stronger guarantee on the output, Rényi divergence is of particular interest for differential privacy
[
Mir17
]
. Perhaps most interesting is that our proof approach is quite different from prior work on convex body sampling. In summary,

•

The guarantees hold for the
q
-Rényi divergences while matching the rates of previous work (prior work only had guarantees in the TV distance).

•

The analysis is simple, modular, and easily extendable to several other settings.

Algorithm 1

\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}

It might be illuminating for the reader to compare this algorithm to the well-studied
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
(Algorithm
2
); each proposed step is a uniform random point in a fixed-radius ball around the current point, and is accepted only if the proposed point is in the body
\mathcal{K}
. In contrast, each iteration of
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}

Each successful iteration of the algorithm, i.e., one that is not declared “Failure”, can be called a
proper
step. We will see that the number of proper steps is directly bounded by isoperimetric constants (such as Poincaré and log-Sobolev) of the target distribution. In fact, this holds quite generally without assuming the convexity of
\mathcal{K}
. The implementation of an iteration is based on rejection sampling (Line
3
), and our analysis of the efficiency of this step relies crucially on the convexity of
\mathcal{K}
. This is reminiscent of the
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
in the literature on convex body sampling (Algorithm
3
), which is used as a tool to analyze proper steps of the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
. We refer the reader to §
A

This simple algorithm can be interpreted as a composition of “flows” in the space of measures. This view will allow us to use tools from stochastic analysis.
In particular, we shall demonstrate how to interpret the two steps of one iteration of
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
as alternating
forward
and
backward
heat flows.

We begin by defining an augmented probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

\pi(x,y)\propto\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2h}\,\lvert x-y\rvert^{2}\bigr)\,\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}}(x)\,.

We denote by
\pi^{X},\pi^{X|Y}(\cdot\,|\,y)
the marginal distribution of its first component (i.e., conditional distribution given the second component), and similarly denote by
\pi^{Y},\pi^{Y|X}(\cdot\,|\,x)
for the second component.
In particular, the marginal in the first component
\pi^{X}
is the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{K}
. Sampling from such a joint distribution to obtain the marginal on
X
(say), can be more efficient than directly working only with
\pi^{X}
. This idea was utilized in Gaussian cooling
[
CV18
]
and later as the restricted Gaussian Oracle (RGO)
[
LST21
,
CCSW22
]
.

Under this notation, Algorithm
1
without the failure condition corresponds to a Gibbs sampling scheme from the two marginals of
\pi(x,y)
.
To be precise, Line
2
and Line
3
correspond to sampling

y_{i+1}\sim\pi^{Y|X}(\cdot\,|\,x_{i})=\mathcal{N}(x_{i},hI_{d})\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad x_{i+1}\sim\pi^{X|Y}(\cdot\,|\,y_{i+1})=\mathcal{N}(y_{i+1},hI_{d})|_{\mathcal{K}}\,.

It is well known that such a Gibbs sampling procedure will ensure the desired stationarity of
\pi(x,y)
.
We implement the latter step through rejection sampling; if the number of trials in Line
3
hits the threshold
N
, then we halt and declare
failure
of the algorithm.
Note that the accepted point in Line
3
, conditional on success (i.e., number of attempts is at most the cap), is still exactly
\pi^{X|Y=y_{i+1}}
. However, conditioning on “no failure over all
T
iterations” (or equivalently “re-run-until-success”) re-weights trajectories, so the final output law of the capped INO is not exactly the same as the INO without the cap; see §
D
for details. Nonetheless, it is
\eta
-close to the intended target
\pi^{X}
pointwise when the overall failure probability is
\eta
.

Practical implementation via restart.

In practice, one would restart the algorithm if it declares failure before completing the target number of iterations. This yields an always-terminating implementation whose output law equals that of Algorithm
1
conditioned on success. The expected number of restarts is at most
(1-\eta)^{-1}
, so the expected total query complexity increases by a multiplicative factor of only
(1-\eta)^{-1}\leq 2
for
\eta\leq 1/2
.

Remark 1
.

We note that
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
is exactly the
proximal
sampling scheme
[
LST21
,
CCSW22
,
FYC23
]
for uniform distributions. The proximal sampler with a target density proportional to
\exp(-V)
considers an augmented distribution
\pi(x,y)\propto\exp(-V(x)-\frac{1}{2h}\,\lvert x-y\rvert^{2})
and then repeats the following two steps: (1)
y_{i+1}\sim\pi^{Y|X=x_{i}}=\mathcal{N}(x_{i},hI_{d})
and then (2)
x_{i+1}\sim\pi^{X|Y=y_{i+1}}
.
Naïvely, the proximal sampler is implemented by performing rejection sampling, with the Gaussian centered at the minimizer of
\log\pi^{\cdot|Y=y_{i+1}}
as the proposal. Realizing this would require a projection oracle (to
\mathcal{K}
), which is only known to be implementable with
O(d^{2})
membership queries.
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
completely avoids the need for a projection oracle.

Our computational model is the classical general model for convex bodies

Definition 1

(Convex body oracle)
.

A

, a number

Definition 2

(Warmness)

A distribution

\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq M\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x)
.

Theorem 3

(Succinct version of Theorem

)
.

For any given
\eta,\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
,

, and any convex body

such that
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
, starting from an

\widetilde{O}(qd^{2}\Lambda\log^{2}\nicefrac{{M}}{{\eta\varepsilon}})

Even though our guarantee is in the much stronger “metric” of

Remark 2

((Warm-start generation))
.

While the assumption of warmness for the initialization may seem strong at the outset, for well-rounded convex bodies (
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\lvert\cdot\rvert^{2}]\leq C^{2}d
for some constant
C
), it is possible to generate an
O(1)
warm-start with complexity
\widetilde{O}(d^{3})
. We refer readers to
[

,
KZ25
,

]

We note that for

\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi^{\mathcal{K}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\leq\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{cov}\pi^{\mathcal{K}})=\mathbb{E}[\lvert X-\mathbb{E}X\rvert^{2}]\leq D^{2}\,.

The above guarantee in the Rényi divergence immediately provides
\mathcal{W}_{2},\mathsf{TV},\mathsf{KL}
, and
\chi^{2}
guarantees as special cases.
For two distributions
\mu

, we have

1.

\mathsf{KL}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)=\lim_{q\downarrow 1}\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\mathcal{R}_{q^{\prime}}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)=\log\sup\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}
for

.

2.

2\,\lVert\mu-\pi\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}^{2}\leq\mathsf{KL}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\log(\chi^{2}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)+1)=\mathcal{R}_{2}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)
.

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\pi)\leq 2C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\,\mathsf{KL}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)

.

4.

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\pi)\leq 2C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\,\chi^{2}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)

[

]
and
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{\mathcal{K}})\lesssim\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi^{\mathcal{K}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\log d
.

Outline of analysis.

We summarize our proof strategy below, which requires us to demonstrate two facts: (i) The current distribution should converge to the uniform distribution, (ii) within each iteration of the algorithm, the failure probability and the expected number of rejections should be small enough.

•

We need to demonstrate that the corresponding Markov chain is rapidly mixing.
Here, we use the heat flow perspective to derive mixing rates under any suitable divergence measure (such as
\mathsf{KL}
,
\chi^{2}
, or
\mathcal{R}_{q}
). This extends known results for the unconstrained setting
[
CCSW22
]
. To summarize the proof, by considering instead the solutions after small time
t
, we invoke known contraction results from
[
CCSW22
]
and then use a continuity argument to conclude the proof.

•

We show that the number of rejections in Line
3
over the entire execution of the algorithm is bounded with high probability. To do this, we apply a detailed argument involving local conductance and the convexity of
\mathcal{K}
, which relies on techniques from
[
BNN13
]
. For this step, we show that with the appropriate choice of variance
h=\widetilde{\Theta}(d^{-2})
and threshold
N=\widetilde{\Theta}(T\eta^{-1})
, the entire algorithm succeeds with probability
1-\eta
. The expected number of rejections is polylogarithmic.

While each individual component resembles pre-existing work in the literature, in their synthesis we will demonstrate how to interleave past relevant developments in theoretical computer science, optimal transport, and functional analysis.
This yields elegant and surprisingly simple proofs, as well as stronger results.

1.3
Discussion

No need to be lazy.

Previous uniform samplers like the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
are made
lazy
(i.e., with probability
1/2
, it does nothing), to ensure convergence to the target stationary distribution. However, our algorithm does not need this, as our sampler is shown to directly contract towards the target.

Unified framework.

We remark that Theorem
2
places the previously known mixing guarantees for the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk},\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
in a unified framework.
Existing tight guarantees for the
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
are in TV distance and based on the log-Sobolev constant, assuming an oracle for implementing each step
[
LV24
]
.
The known convergence guarantees of the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
(see §
A
for details), namely the mixing time of
\widetilde{O}(Md^{2}D^{2}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
for TV distance, are for the composite algorithm [
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}+
rejection sampling]. Here, the
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
records only the accepted steps of the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
, so its stationary distribution differs slightly from the uniform distribution (and can be corrected with a post-processing step).
On the other hand,
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
actually converges to
\pi^{\mathcal{K}}
without any adjustments and achieves stronger Rényi divergence bounds in the same asymptotic complexity.
Our analysis shows that the mixing guarantee is determined by isoperimetric constants of the target (Poincaré or log-Sobolev).

Effective step size.

The
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
’s largest possible step size is of order
1/\sqrt{d}
(see §
A
) to keep the rejection probability bounded by a constant.
This bound could also be viewed as an “effective” step size of
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
, since the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,hI)
is concentrated around
\sqrt{hd}
and we will set the variance
h
of
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
to
\widetilde{O}(d^{-2})
, so we have
\sqrt{hd}\approx 1/\sqrt{d}
.

What has really changed?

\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
has clear similarities to both
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
and
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
.
What then are the changes that allow us to use continuous-time interpolation?
One step of the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
is [random step (
y\in B_{\delta}(x)
)
+
Metropolis-filter (accept if
y\in\mathcal{K}
)].
This filtering is an abrupt discrete step, and it is unclear how to control contraction. It could be replaced by a step of the
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
(
x\sim\text{Unif}(B_{\delta}(y)\cap\mathcal{K})
). Then, each iteration of
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
can be viewed as a Gaussian version of a
\mathsf{Ball\ walk^{\prime}s\ proposal}+\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
algorithm.

How can we compare
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
with the
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}?
Iterating speedy steps leads to a biased distribution.
One step of (a Gaussian version of) the
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
can be understood as a step of backward heat flow. Therefore, if one can control the isoperimetric constants of the biased distribution along the trajectory of the backward flow, then contraction of the
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
toward the biased distribution will follow from the simultaneous backward analysis.

Subsequent work.

The ideas and methods of this paper have led to subsequent progress. First,
[
KZ25
]
shows that the output guarantee can be strengthened to
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
under LSI, using
\widetilde{O}(d^{2}R^{2}\operatorname{polylog}\frac{1}{\varepsilon\eta})
queries.
Second, the diffusion-based approach is extended to general logconcave sampling in
[
KV25b
]
, with the query complexity of
\widetilde{O}(d^{2}\,(R^{2}\vee 1)\operatorname{polylog}\frac{1}{\varepsilon\eta})
for a
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
guarantee, beyond the uniform distribution. Lastly, the
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-warmness requirement has been relaxed to significantly weaker
\mathcal{R}_{\widetilde{O}(1)}
-warmness in
[
KV25a
]
, improving the query complexity of warm-start generation from
\widetilde{O}(d^{2}R^{2})
to
\widetilde{O}(d^{2}R^{3/2}\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/4})
.

1.4
Related work

Sampling from constrained logconcave distributions is a fundamental task arising in many fields. Uniform sampling with convex constraints is its simplest manifestation, which was first studied as a core subroutine for a randomized volume-computation algorithm
[
DFK91
]
. Since then, this fundamental problem has been studied for over three decades
[
LS90
,
LS93
,
KLS97
,
LV06
,
BEL18
,
BDMP17
]
. We review these algorithms, grouping them under three categories — geometric random walks, structured samplers, and diffusion-type samplers. Below,
\mathcal{K}
is convex.

Geometric random walks.

We discuss two geometric random walks –
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}

[
LS93
,
KLS97
]
and
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}

[
Smi84
,
Lov99
]
. The
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
is a simple metropolized random walk; it draws
y
uniformly at random from a ball of radius
\delta
centered at a current point
x
, and moves to
y
if
y\in\mathcal{K}
and stays at
x
otherwise. In the literature, the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
actually refers to a composite algorithm consisting of [
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}+
rejection sampling], where the
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
records only the accepted steps of the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
(see §
A
for details).
The step size
\delta
should be set to
O(d^{-1/2})
to avoid stepping outside of
\mathcal{K}
.

[
KLS97
]
showed that the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
needs
\widetilde{O}(Md^{2}D^{2}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
membership queries to be
\varepsilon
-close to
\pi^{\mathcal{K}}
in
\mathsf{TV}
, where
D
is the diameter of
\mathcal{K}
, and the warmness parameter
M
measures the closeness of the initial distribution to the target uniform distribution
\pi^{\mathcal{K}}
.

\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
is another zeroth-order algorithm that needs
no step size
; it picks a uniform random line
\ell
passing a current point, and move to a uniform random point on
\ell\cap\mathcal{K}
.

[
LV06
]
shows that, if we define the second moment as
R^{2}:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\mathcal{K}}}[\lvert X-\mathbb{E}X\rvert^{2}]
, then
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
requires
O(d^{2}R^{2}\log\frac{M}{\varepsilon})
iterations. Notably, this algorithm has a poly-logarithmic dependence on
M
as opposed to the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
. Chen and Eldan
[
CE25
]
further showed that
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
mixes in
\widetilde{O}(M^{11}d^{2}/\varepsilon^{11})
times for isotropic convex bodies.

Both algorithm are affected by skewed shape of
\mathcal{K}
(i.e., large
D
or
R
), so these samplers are combined with pre-processing step called
rounding
. This procedure finds a linear transformation that makes the geometry of
\mathcal{K}
less skewed and so more amenable to sampling. In literature, there exists a randomized algorithm
[
JLLV21
]
that rounds
\mathcal{K}
and generates a good warm start (i.e.,
M=O(1)
), with the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
used as a core subroutine. We refer readers to
[
KZ24
]
for a streamlined proof, with
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
used as a main sampler.
This algorithm takes up
\widetilde{O}(d^{3.5})
queries in total, and from a well-rounded position with a good warm start, the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
only needs
\widetilde{O}(d^{2}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
queries to sample from
\pi^{\mathcal{K}}
.

Structured samplers.

The aforementioned samplers based on geometric random walks require
only
access to the membership oracle of the convex body
without
any additional structural assumptions. The alternate paradigm of
geometry-aware sampling
attempts to exploit the
structure
of convex constraints, with the aim of expediting the convergence of the resultant sampling schemes. One common assumption is to make available a
self-concordant barrier function

\phi
which has regularity on its high-order derivatives and blows up when approaching the boundary
\partial\mathcal{K}
. The Hessian of
\phi
encodes the local geometry of the constraint, and the samplers often work directly with
\nabla^{2}\phi
.

The first canonical example of such a zeroth-order sampler is
\mathsf{Dikin\ walk}
used when
\mathcal{K}
is given by
m
linear constraints
[
KN12
]
; it draws a uniform sample from an ellipsoid (characterized by
\nabla^{2}\phi
) of fixed radius around a current point, and is often combined with a Metropolis adjustment.
[
KN12
]
shows that
\mathsf{Dikin\ walk}
mixes in
O(md\log\frac{M}{\varepsilon})
steps, although each iteration is slightly more expensive than one membership query. This algorithm requires no rounding, but still needs a good warm-start, which can be achieved by an annealing-type algorithm using
\widetilde{O}(md)
iterations of
\mathsf{Dikin\ walk}

[
KV24
]
.

\mathsf{Riemannian\ Hamiltonian\ Monte\ Carlo}
is a structured sampler that exploits the first-order information of the potential (i.e.,
\nabla\log(1/\pi)
)
[
GC11
]
; its proposal is given as the solution to the Hamilton’s ODE equation, followed by the Metropolis-filter. In the linear-constraint setting above, it requires
O(md^{2/3}\log\frac{M}{\varepsilon})
many iterations to achieve
\varepsilon
-close distance to
\pi^{\mathcal{K}}

[
LV18
]
. This sampler is further analyzed for practical ODE solvers
[
KLSV23
]
and for more sophisticated self-concordant barriers
[
GKV24
]
.

Similarly,
\mathsf{Mirror\ Langevin}

[
ZPFP20
,
Jia21
,
AC21
,
LTVW22
]
is a class of algorithms which converts the constrained problem into an unconstrained one obtained by considering the pushforward of the constrained space by
\nabla\phi
. The algorithm can also be metropolized
[
SWW24
]
. The best known rate for this algorithm is
\widetilde{O}(d\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
under some strong assumptions on
\phi
.

Diffusion-based samplers.

Samplers based on discretizations of Itô diffusions, stochastic processes which rapidly mix to
\pi
in continuous time, have long been used for sampling without constraints
[
BGHM95
,
DT12
,
Dal17
,
Che25
]
. While the underlying stochastic processes generalize easily to constrained settings, the discretization analysis relies crucially on the smoothness of the target distribution. This is clearly impossible to achieve in the constrained setting, so some techniques are required to circumvent this difficulty. These algorithms, however, generalize easily to the more general problem of sampling from distributions of the form
\tilde{\pi}^{X}\propto e^{-f}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}}
, by incorporating first-order information from
f
.

The first approach for adapting diffusion-based samplers
[
BEL18
,
Leh23
]
iterates a two-step procedure. First, a random step is taken, with
x_{k+1/2}\sim\mathcal{N}(x_{k},2hI_{d})
for some appropriately chosen step
h
,
2
2

2

A gradient step can be added in the more general case, for sampling from
\tilde{\pi}^{X}
.
and then project it to
\mathcal{K}
, i.e.,
x_{k+1}=\mathsf{proj}_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{k+1/2})
. The complexity is given in terms of queries to a
projection oracle
, each call to which can be implemented with a polynomial number of membership oracle queries; a total of
\widetilde{O}(\nicefrac{{d^{2}D^{3}}}{{\varepsilon^{4}}})
queries are needed to be
\varepsilon
-close in
\mathcal{W}_{2}
to
\pi^{X}
. Another approach, which uses an algorithmically designed “soft” penalty instead of a projection, was proposed in
[
GHZ24
]
, and achieves a rate estimate of
\widetilde{O}(\nicefrac{{d}}{{\varepsilon^{10}}})
.

A second approach, suggested by
[
BDMP17
]
, considers a different proximal scheme, which performs a “soft projection” onto
\mathcal{K}
, by taking steps like
\mathcal{N}((1-h\lambda^{-1})x_{k}+h\,\mathsf{proj}_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{k}),2hI_{d})
. It is called Moreau–Yosida regularized Langevin, named after an analogous regularization scheme for constrained optimization. This scheme also relies on access to a projection oracle for
\mathcal{K}
, and quantifies their query complexity accordingly. Their final rate estimate is
\widetilde{O}(\nicefrac{{d^{5}}}{{\varepsilon^{6}}})
to be
\varepsilon
-close in
\mathsf{TV}
distance to
\pi^{X}
.

Observing the prior work integrating diffusion-based sampling with convex constraints, the dependence on the key parameters
d,\varepsilon
, while polynomial, are many orders worse than the rates for zeroth-order samplers such as
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
and
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
. In contrast, our analysis not only recovers but in some sense surpasses the known rates for
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
and
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
, while harmonizing well with the continuous-time perspective of diffusions.

Proximal schemes for sampling.

The Gibbs sampling scheme used in this paper was inspired by the restricted Gaussian oracle introduced in
[
LST21
]
(in turn inspired by Gaussian cooling
[
CV18
]
), which alternately iterates between a pure Gaussian step, and a “proximal” step (which we elaborate in our exposition). This scheme was given novel interpretations by
[
CCSW22
]
, which showed that it interpolates the forward and backward heat flows, in the sense defined by
[
KP23
]
. The backward heat flow itself is intimately related to stochastic localization schemes, invented and popularized in
[
Eld13
,
Che21
]
.

This formulation proved surprisingly powerful, allowing many existing rates in unconstrained sampling to be recovered from a relatively simple analysis. This was further extended by
[
FYC23
]
to achieve the current state-of-the-art rate in unconstrained sampling. Finally,
[
GLL+23
]
suggests that this could be applied to tackle some constrained problems.
However, the assumptions in this final mentioned work are not compatible with the uniform sampling problem on general convex bodies.

Unless otherwise specified, we will use
\lvert\cdot\rvert
for the
2
-norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We write
a=O(b)

a\leq cb

c>0

a\gtrsim b,a=\Omega(b)
for

To quantify the convergence rate, we recall common notions of divergence between distributions.

Definition 8

(Distance and divergence)
.

For two measures
\mu,\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
total variation
distance between them is defined by

\lVert\mu-\nu\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}:=\sup_{B\in\mathcal{F}}\,\lvert\mu(B)-\nu(B)\rvert\,,

where
\mathcal{F}
is the collection of all measurable subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}\,
.
The
2
-
Wasserstein distance
is given by

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim\gamma}[\lvert X-Y\rvert^{2}]\,,

where
\Gamma
is the set of all couplings between
\mu,\nu
.
Next, we define the
f
-divergence
of
\mu
towards
\nu
with
\mu\ll\nu
(i.e.,
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu
) as, for some convex function
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
with
f(1)=0
and
f^{\prime}(\infty)=\infty
,

D_{f}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu):=\int f\bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,.

The
\mathsf{KL}
-divergence
arises when taking
f(x)=x\log x
, the
\chi^{q}
-divergence
when taking
f(x)=x^{q}-1
, and the
q
-Rényi divergence
is given by

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu):=\frac{1}{q-1}\log\bigl(\chi^{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)+1\bigr)\,.

We recall two important functional inequalities of a distribution.

Definition 9
.

We say that a probability measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies a
Poincaré inequality
(PI) with parameter
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\nu)
if for all smooth functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\operatorname{var}_{\nu}f\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\nu)\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\lvert\nabla f\rvert^{2}]\,,

where
\operatorname{var}_{\nu}f\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\lvert f-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}f\rvert^{2}]
.

The Poincaré inequality is implied by the log-Sobolev inequality.

Definition 10
.

We say that a probability measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies a
log-Sobolev inequality
(LSI) with parameter
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)
if for all smooth functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\mathsf{Ent}_{\nu}(f^{2})\leq 2C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\lvert\nabla f\rvert^{2}]\,,

where
\mathsf{Ent}_{\nu}(f^{2}):=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f^{2}\log f^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f^{2}]\log(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f^{2}])
.
Equivalently, for any probability measure
\mu
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\mu\ll\nu
,

\mathsf{KL}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)\leq\frac{C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)}{2}\,\mathsf{FI}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)\,,

where
\mathsf{FI}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu):=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\lvert\nabla\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\rvert^{2}]
is the
Fisher information
of
\mu
with respect to
\nu
.

We state two important lemmas which are needed for our proofs. The first is the data-processing inequality (DPI) for Rényi divergence and
f
-divergence, given below.

Lemma 11

(Data-processing inequality)
.

For measures
\mu,\nu
, Markov kernel
P
,
f
-divergence
D_{f}
, and
q\geq 1
, it holds that

D_{f}(\mu P\mathbin{\|}\nu P)\leq D_{f}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu P\mathbin{\|}\nu P)\leq\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)\,.

Functional inequalities allow us to show exponential contraction of various divergences, through the following helpful inequality.

Lemma 12

(Grönwall)
.

Suppose that
u,g:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
are two continuous functions, with
u
being differentiable on
[0,T]
and satisfying

u^{\prime}(t)\leq g(t)\,u(t)\qquad\text{for all }t\in[0,T]\,.

Then,

u(t)\leq\exp\Bigl(\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\Bigr)\,u(0)\qquad\text{for all }t\in[0,T]\,.

Analysis

We begin this section by proving the stationarity of the target
\pi^{X}
.

Lemma 13
.

\pi^{X}
is stationary under iterations of
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
.

Proof.

Note that the transition kernel of the forward and backward step is
\pi^{Y|X=\cdot}
and
\pi^{X|Y=\cdot}
, respectively. Hence, the forward step brings
\pi^{X}
to
\pi^{Y}
as seen in

\int\pi^{Y|X}(\cdot\,|\,x)\,\pi^{X}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int\pi^{X,Y}(x,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}x=\pi^{Y}\,.

Also, the backward step brings
\pi^{Y}
to
\pi^{X}
:

\int\pi^{X|Y}(\cdot\,|\,y)\,\pi^{Y}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\int\pi^{X,Y}(\cdot,y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\pi^{X}\,.

Therefore,
\pi^{X}
is stationary under each iteration of
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
.
∎

Our analysis for
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
consists of two parts: (1) demonstrating its mixing, i.e., how many
outer
iterations are needed to be sufficiently close to the uniform distribution, and (2) quantifying the failure probability and wasted steps in Line
3
.

For (1), we collect in §
3.1
some important implications of functional inequalities, e.g., the Poincaré and log-Sobolev inequalities, for the uniform distribution. Then in §
3.2
, we exploit the flow perspective of the algorithm to obtain the mixing guarantees. To this end, we revisit the proofs for the contraction results of forward heat flows in
[
CCSW22
,
KO25
]
.

Theorem 14
.

Let
\mu^{X}_{k}
be the law of the
k
-th output of
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
with initial distribution
\mu_{0}^{X}
and step size
h>0
.
Let
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}
be the (
10
) constant of the uniform distribution
\pi^{X}
over
\mathcal{K}
.
Then, for any
q\geq 1
,

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{k}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})\leq\frac{\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{0}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})}{(1+h/C_{\mathsf{LSI}})^{k/q}}\,.

For
C_{\mathsf{PI}}
the (
9
) constant of
\pi^{X}
,

\chi^{2}(\mu^{X}_{k}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})\leq\frac{\chi^{2}(\mu^{X}_{0}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})}{(1+h/C_{\mathsf{PI}})^{k}}\,.

Furthermore, for any
q\geq 2
,

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{k}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})\leq\begin{cases}\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{0}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})-\frac{k\log(1+h/C_{\mathsf{PI}})}{q}&\text{if }k\leq\frac{q}{2\log(1+h/C_{\mathsf{PI}})}\,\bigl(\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{0}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})-1\bigr)\,,\\
(1+h/C_{\mathsf{PI}})^{-(k-k_{0})/q}&\text{if }k\geq k_{0}:=\lceil\frac{q}{2\log(1+h/C_{\mathsf{PI}})}\,\bigl(\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{0}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})-1\bigr)\rceil\,.\end{cases}

The result reduces the problem of obtaining a mixing guarantee to that of demonstrating a functional inequality on the target distribution. For this, it is not strictly necessary that
\mathcal{K}
be convex.

As for (2), convexity of
\mathcal{K}
is crucial this time unlike (1). We show in §

that the failure probability remains under control by taking a suitable variance

Lemma 15

The bias of conditioning on success can be bounded as follows.

Lemma 16

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{m}^{\textup{cap}}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{m}}

Proposition 1

(General de Bruijn identity,
[
KO25
]
)

Let
\mu

\partial_{t}\chi^{q}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})=-\frac{q(q-1)}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}\bigl[\bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}}\bigr)^{q}\bigl|\nabla\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}}\bigr|^{2}\bigr]=-\frac{q-1}{2}\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}}\Bigr\|^{q}_{L^{q}(\nu_{t})}\mathsf{RF}_{q}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})\,.

Using the general de Bruijn identity with

\partial_{t}\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})=\frac{1}{q-1}\,\frac{\partial_{t}\chi^{q}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})}{1+\chi^{q}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})}=\frac{1}{q-1}\,\frac{\partial_{t}\chi^{q}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})}{\lVert\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}}\rVert^{q}_{L^{q}(\nu_{t})}}=-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathsf{RF}_{q}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})\,.

(3.1)

We also obtain
\partial_{t}\chi^{2}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})=-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}[\lvert\nabla\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}}\rvert^{2}]

Lemma 22

(Contraction under functional inequalities)

Let

\frac{\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)}{(1+t/C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\nu))^{1/q}}&\text{if }\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)<1\,.\end{cases}

Proof.

It is well-known that (

3.3
Failure probability and wasted steps

We begin by defining a Gaussian version of

Definition 23

The local conductance

Then the expected number of trials until the first success of Line

Naïve analysis for expected number of trials.

This suggests that one should consider the algorithm as having “failed” if the number of trials exceeds some threshold.

Refined analysis under a failure condition.

Going forward, we assume an

Lemma 25

For a convex body

\pi^{Y}(\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c})\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2h}+\delta d\bigr)\,.

Proof.

For

containing

\displaystyle\pi^{Y}(\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K})}\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c}}\int_{\mathcal{K}}\frac{\exp(-\frac{1}{2h}\lvert y-x\rvert^{2})}{(2\pi h)^{d/2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K})}\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c}}\int_{H(y)}\frac{\exp(-\frac{1}{2h}\lvert y-x\rvert^{2})}{(2\pi h)^{d/2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K})}\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c}}\int_{d(y,\mathcal{K})}^{\infty}\frac{\exp(-\frac{z^{2}}{2h})}{\sqrt{2\pi h}}\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}y\,.

(3.2)

Let us denote the tail probability of the

\mathsf{T}(s):=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{N}(0,h)}(Z\geq s)=1-\Phi(h^{-1/2}s)\,,

By the co-area formula and integration by parts,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c}}\int_{d(y,\mathcal{K})}^{\infty}\frac{\exp(-\frac{1}{2h}z^{2})}{\sqrt{2\pi h}}\,\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\delta}^{\infty}\mathsf{T}(s)\operatorname{vol}(\partial\mathcal{K}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\Bigl[\underbrace{\mathsf{T}(s)\int_{0}^{s}\operatorname{vol}(\partial\mathcal{K}_{z})\,\mathrm{d}z}_{\eqqcolon\mathsf{F}}\Bigr]_{\delta}^{\infty}+\int_{\delta}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi h}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{s^{2}}{2h}\bigr)\int_{0}^{s}\operatorname{vol}(\partial\mathcal{K}_{z})\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}s\,.

Proof of Lemma

.

For
\mu_{h}:=\mu*\mathcal{N}(0,hI_{d})

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{h}}[(1-\ell)^{N}]

\mathrm{d}\mu/\mathrm{d}\pi^{X}\leq M

\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}/\mathrm{d}(\pi^{X})_{h}=\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}/\mathrm{d}\pi^{Y}\leq M
(as easily checked by the definition of convolution), it follows that

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{h}}[(1-\ell)^{N}]\leq M\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}[(1-\ell)^{N}]\,.

Then,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\underbrace{(1-\ell)^{N}\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{Y}}_{\eqqcolon\mathsf{A}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c}}\mathsf{A}+\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell\geq N^{-1}\log(3mM/\eta)]}\mathsf{A}+\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell<N^{-1}\log(3mM/\eta)]}\mathsf{A}

\displaystyle\leq\pi^{Y}(\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c})+\int_{[\ell\geq N^{-1}\log(3mM/\eta)]}\exp(-\ell N)\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{Y}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell<N^{-1}\log(3mM/\eta)]}\frac{\ell(y)}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K})}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{t^{2}}{2c}+t\bigr)+\frac{\eta}{3mM}+\frac{\log(3mM/\eta)}{N}\,\frac{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K}_{\delta})}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K})}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{t^{2}}{2c}+t\bigr)+\frac{\eta}{3mM}+\frac{e^{t}}{N}\,\log\frac{3mM}{\eta}\,,

where we used

.

,

, and

, we can bound the last line by

. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{h}}[(1-\ell)^{N}]\leq M\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}[(1-\ell)^{N}]\leq\frac{\eta}{m}\,.

be the minimum of the threshold

.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl(\frac{1}{\ell}\wedge N\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{Y}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}}\frac{1}{\ell}\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{Y}+N\pi^{Y}(\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c})=\frac{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K}_{\delta})}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K})}+N\pi^{Y}(\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq e^{t}+N\exp\bigl(-\frac{t^{2}}{2c}+t\bigr)\leq\log^{3}Z+3\log^{4}Z=O\bigl(\log^{4}\frac{mM}{\eta}\bigr)\,.

Therefore, the expected number of trials per step is

\mathcal{K}
.

be the law of

and
\mu_{m}^{\textup{cap}}
be the law of
X
conditioned on
\mathsf{Succ}
. Then, by Bayes’ rule,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{m}^{\textup{cap}}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{m}}(x)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(X=x\,|\,\mathsf{Succ})}{\mathbb{P}(X=x)}=\frac{\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{Succ}\,|\,X=x)\,\mathbb{P}(X=x)}{\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{Succ})\,\mathbb{P}(X=x)}\leq\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{Succ})}\leq\frac{1}{1-\eta}\,.

Lastly, using the weak triangle inequality for Rényi divergence,

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{m}^{\textup{cap}}\,\|\,\pi)\leq\frac{q}{q-1}\,\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mu_{m}^{\textup{cap}}\,\|\,\mu_{m})+\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{m}\,\|\,\pi)\leq\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{m}\,\|\,\pi)+\frac{q}{q-1}\log\frac{1}{1-\eta}\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

Functional inequalities

We provide full details on functional inequalities omitted in §

.
We use

Cheeger and PI constants.

The
Cheeger isoperimetric constant

C_{\mathsf{Ch}}(\mu)
measures how large surface area a measurable subset with larger volume has, defined by

C_{\mathsf{Ch}}(\mu):=\inf_{S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\mu^{+}(S)}{\min(\mu(S),\mu(S^{c}))}\,,

where the infimum is taken over all measurable subsets
S
, and
\mu^{+}(S)
is the Minkowski content of
S
under
\mu
defined as, for
S^{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in X:d(x,S)<\varepsilon\}
,

\mu^{+}(S):=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mu(S^{\varepsilon})-\mu(S)}{\varepsilon}\,.

[
Che70
]
established
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\mu)\lesssim C_{\mathsf{Ch}}^{-2}(\mu)
4
4

4

The opposite direction
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\mu_{\mathsf{LC}})\gtrsim C_{\mathsf{Ch}}^{-2}(\mu_{\mathsf{LC}})
also holds for logconcave distributions due to
[
Led94
]
.
, and then
[
KLS95
]
showed that for covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\mu}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[(\cdot-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}X)(\cdot-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}X)^{\mathsf{T}}]
,

C_{\mathsf{Ch}}(\mu_{\mathsf{LC}})\gtrsim\frac{1}{(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\mathsf{LC}}}[\lvert X-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\mathsf{LC}}}X\rvert^{2}])^{1/2}}=\frac{1}{(\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{\mu_{\mathsf{LC}}})^{1/2}}\,.

(B.1)

This immediately leads to
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\lesssim(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\lvert X-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}X\rvert^{2}])^{1/2}\leq D^{2}
for the uniform distribution
\pi
over a convex body
\mathcal{K}
with diameter
D>0
.

Kannan et al. proposed the
KLS conjecture
in the same paper, which says that

C_{\mathsf{Ch}}(\mu_{\mathsf{LC}})\gtrsim\frac{1}{\lVert\Sigma_{\mu_{\mathsf{LC}}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{1/2}}\,.

While the original result in
[
KLS95
]
ensures

for an isotropic logconcave distribution (due to

Following a line of work

which implies that

Log-Cheeger and LSI constants.

Just as the Cheeger and PI constants are related above, there are known connections between LSI and

constants. The log-Cheeger constant

of a distribution

C_{\mathsf{logCh}}(\mu):=\inf_{S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mu(S)\leq\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\mu^{+}(S)}{\mu(S)\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\mu(S)}}}\,.

[

Lemma 29

(Decay along forward/backward heat flows)
.

Let
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0},(\nu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the laws of the solutions to (
C.4
) starting at
\mu_{0},\nu_{0}
respectively.
Assume that
\mu_{t}
and
\nu_{t}
has sufficient regularity and fast tail decay so that we can discard boundary terms in integration by parts and differentiate under the integral sign.
Then, for any differentiable function
g
,

\partial_{t}g\bigl(D_{f}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})\bigr)=-\frac{1}{2}\,g^{\prime}\bigl(D_{f}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})\bigr)\times\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\bigl\langle\nabla\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr),\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr\rangle\,.

Proof.

The case where
g\neq\mathsf{id}
is an application
of the chain rule, so it suffices to take
g=\mathsf{id}
and simply
differentiate an
f
-divergence.

For brevity, we drop the variable
x
from functions involved and proceed by differentiating under the integral sign and discarding boundary terms in integration by parts:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}D_{f}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})

\displaystyle=\int\Bigl\{\bigl(f\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\,\partial_{t}\nu_{t}+\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\bigl(\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)^{\prime}\nu_{t}\Bigr\}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int\Bigl\{\partial_{t}\nu_{t}\Bigl(\bigl(f\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)-\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\Bigr)+\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\,\partial_{t}\mu_{t}\Bigr\}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\underset{(i)}{=}\int\bigl[-\nabla\cdot(b_{t}\nu_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta\nu_{t}\bigr]\Bigl(\bigl(f\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)-\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\bigl[-\nabla\cdot(b_{t}\mu_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta\mu_{t}\bigr]\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

where in
(i)
we substitute the F-P equation from (
C.4
).
Integrating by parts (i.e.,
\int f\operatorname{div}(\mathbf{G})=-\int\langle\nabla f,\mathbf{G}\rangle

for a real-valued function
f
and vector-valued function
\mathbf{G}
),
we have that

\int\bigl[-\nabla\cdot(b_{t}\nu_{t})\bigr]\bigl(f\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int\bigl\langle b_{t}\nu_{t},\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\nabla\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

(C.5)

On the other hand, we have that

-\int\bigl[-\nabla\cdot(b_{t}\nu_{t})\bigr]\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}x=-\int\bigl\langle b_{t}\nu_{t},\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\,\nabla\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)+\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\nabla\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

The second term cancels with the RHS of (
C.5
). We have a similar
cancellation for the
\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta\nu_{t}
term:

\int\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta\nu_{t}\,\bigl(f\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x=-\int\frac{1}{2}\,\bigl\langle\nabla\nu_{t},\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\nabla\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

and

-\int\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta\nu_{t}\,\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int\frac{1}{2}\,\bigl\langle\nabla\nu_{t},\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\,\nabla\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)+\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\nabla\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

Combining these, we are left with

\displaystyle\int\bigl[-\nabla\cdot(b_{t}\nu_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta\nu_{t}\bigr]\Bigl(\bigl(f\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)-\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int\bigl\langle b_{t}\nu_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\,\nabla\nu_{t},\nabla\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int\bigl\langle b_{t}\mu_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\,\mu_{t}\nabla\log\nu_{t},\nabla\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

Finally, we note that

\displaystyle\int\bigl[-\nabla\cdot(b_{t}\mu_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\mu_{t}\bigr]\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int\bigl\langle b_{t}\mu_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\,\nabla\mu_{t},\nabla\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int\bigl\langle b_{t}\mu_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\,\mu_{t}\nabla\log\mu_{t},\nabla\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

Putting it all together, noticing that the drift terms cancel, we
are left with

\partial_{t}D_{f}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})=-\int\frac{1}{2}\,\bigl\langle\mu_{t}\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}},\nabla\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x=-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\bigl\langle\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}},\nabla\bigl(f^{\prime}\circ\frac{\mu_{t}}{\nu_{t}}\bigr)\bigr\rangle\,,

which completes the proof.

Appendix D
Bias from failure

By computing the one-step distribution, we can note that
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
conditioned on success has a bias. To see this, consider
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
with threshold
N
and the initial measure
X_{1}\sim\mu
. Then, for a measurable set
A
and the success event
S
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{2}\in A\mid S)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}(X_{2}\in A,S)}{\mathbb{P}(S)}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(S)}\int\mathbb{P}(X_{2}\in A,X_{1}=x,S)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(S)}\int\mathbb{P}(X_{2}\in A,S\mid X_{1}=x)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(S)}\int\mathbb{P}(X_{2}\in A,S,Y_{2}=y\mid X_{1}=x)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(S)}\int\mathbb{P}(X_{2}\in A,S\mid X_{1}=x,Y_{2}=y)\,\gamma(y-x)\,\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(S)}\int\mathbb{P}(X_{2}\in A,S\mid Y_{2}=y)\,\gamma(y-x)\,\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(S)}\int\mathbb{P}(S\mid Y_{2}=y)\mathbb{P}(X_{2}\in A\mid S,Y_{2}=y)\,\gamma(y-x)\,\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(S)}\int\bigl(1-\bigl(1-\ell(y)\bigr)^{N}\bigr)\,\pi^{X|Y=y}(A)\,\gamma(y-x)\,\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\bigl(1-\bigl(1-\ell(y)\bigr)^{N}\bigr)\,\pi^{X|Y=y}(A)\,\gamma(y-x)\,\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y}{1-\mathbb{E}_{\mu*\gamma}[(1-\ell)^{N}]}\,.

On the other hand,
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
without cap (i.e.,
N=\infty
) satisfies

\mathbb{P}(X_{2}\in A)=\int\pi^{X|Y=y}(A)\,\gamma(y-x)\,\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\,.

Thus, trajectories are weighted differently in the two distributions.
