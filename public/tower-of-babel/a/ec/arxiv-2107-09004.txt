Derived analytic geometry for ℤ \mathbb{Z} -valued functions Part I - Topological properties
Federico Bambozzi, Tomoki Mihara

Abstract
We study the Banach algebras C ​ ( X , R ) \text{\rm C}(X,R) of continuous functions from a compact Hausdorff topological space X to a Banach ring R R whose topology is discrete. We prove that the Berkovich spectrum of C ​ ( X , R ) \text{\rm C}(X,R) is homeomorphic to ζ ⁡ ( X ) × ℳ ⁡ ( R ) \zeta(X)\times\mathscr{M}(R) , where ζ ⁡ ( X ) \zeta(X) is the Banaschewski compactification of X X and ℳ ⁡ ( R ) \mathscr{M}(R) is the Berkovich spectrum of R R . We study how the topology of the spectrum of C ​ ( X , R ) \text{\rm C}(X,R) is related to the notion of homotopy Zariski open embedding used in derived geometry. We find that the topology of ζ ⁡ ( X ) \zeta(X) can be easily reconstructed from the homotopy Zariski topology associated to C ​ ( X , R ) \text{\rm C}(X,R) . We also prove some results about the existence of Schauder bases on C ​ ( X , R ) \text{\rm C}(X,R) and a generalisation of the Stone–Weierstrass Theorem, under suitable hypotheses on X X and R R .

Introduction

Background

The theory of commutative
\text{\rm C}^{*}
-algebras, i. e. algebras of continuous functions from compact Hausdorff topological spaces
X
to
\mathbb{C}
, is one of the most important topics of functional analysis. The richness of this theory comes from the structure of
\mathbb{C}
as a complete valued field with respect to the standard absolute value. In the authors’ recent work
[
BM21
]
, it has been shown that the theory of
\text{\rm C}^{*}
-algebras has a nice description in terms of the derived analytic geometry introduced in
[
BK17
]
,
[
BB16
]
,
[
BBK19
]
and
[
BK20
]
. Therefore, it is natural to ask what happens when the continuous
\mathbb{C}
-valued functions on
X
are replaced by the Banach algebra
\text{\rm C}(X,R)
of continuous functions valued on other Banach rings. Already in
[
BM21
]
, the case when
R
is a complete non-Archimedean valued field has been considered and it has been checked that from the derived analytic geometry of the Banach algebra
\text{\rm C}(X,R)\cong\text{\rm C}(\zeta(X),R)
, one can reconstruct the topological space
\zeta(X)
, where
\zeta(X)
is the Banaschewski compactification of
X
.

Main results

In this work, we consider a case that is at the opposite extreme of the ones considered in
[
BM21
]
. We study the Banach modules
\text{\rm C}(X,M)
for a Banach module
M
whose topology induced by the norm is discrete. We call such an
M
a
Banach module isolated at
0
. In particular, we are interested in a Banach ring isolated at
0
. The main example of such a Banach ring is
\mathbb{Z}_{\infty}=(\mathbb{Z},{\mathchoice{\left|\cdot\right|}{|\cdot|}{|\cdot|}{|\cdot|}}_{\infty})
, i. e.
\mathbb{Z}
equipped with the Euclidean absolute value. Such rings have been already considered, for example in
[
Nöb68
]
, and more recently in Lecture 5 of
[
Sch19
]
, where they play a key role in the study of the notion of solid modules, but they have been usually considered just as algebras without considering the canonical Banach ring structure induced by the norm(s) on
\mathbb{Z}
. We will argue that this additional structure is a fundamental ingredient in the investigation of the algebras
\text{\rm C}(X,R)
and that these algebras should be considered to be objects whose study belongs both to geometry and to functional analysis as
\text{\rm C}^{*}
-algebras do. Moreover, the ring
R
can be more complicated than the complete valued fields considered in
[
BM21
]
, and this has a strong impact on the properties of the algebras
\text{\rm C}(X,R)
. Indeed, in the first five sections of this paper, we explain how to generalise the results of
[
BM21
]
to the case of continuous functions valued in a Banach ring isolated at
0
. The strategies of the proofs are the same as the ones in
[
BM21
]
, but almost all the lemmata have to be proved and almost all the basic computations have to be done in different ways. Whereas the last two sections prove some further properties of the Banach algebras
\text{\rm C}(X,R)
. This paper is the first part of a two-part work. In the second part, we will study the morphisms
\text{\rm C}(X,R)\to\text{\rm C}(X,S)
induced by a morphism of Banach ring
R\to S
(where
S
does not necessarily need to be isolated at
0
).

We notice that when the base ring
R
is non-Archimedean, all our results can be presented in two different contexts. One context is when only non-Archimedean Banach
R
-modules are considered, and the other is when all Banach modules are allowed. We denote such a choice by the choice of the base category
\mathscr{C}=\text{\rm Ab}^{\text{\rm A}}
, in the first case, and
\mathscr{C}=\text{\rm Ab}^{\text{\rm nA}}
, in the second case. We will explain the reasons for this notation in §
1
. Although most of the basic results of this paper are not influenced by this choice, the results in the last two sections are strongly influenced. More extreme is the situation of the results presented in the second part of this work, where we will be interested in understanding when a homotopy epimorphism
R\to S
of Banach rings induces a homotopy epimorphism
\text{\rm C}(X,R)\to\text{\rm C}(X,S)
. We will see that not only that the Archimedean projective tensor product rarely satisfies the property that the canonical morphism
\text{\rm C}(X,R)\widehat{\otimes}{}_{R}S\to\text{\rm C}(X,S)
is an isomorphism, but also that, unexpectedly, the most basic localisations of the Banach ring
\mathbb{Z}_{\infty}
are not homotopy epimorphisms. This is in stark contrast with the case of non-Archimedean Banach rings where one can prove that all the localisation used in analytic geometry are homotopy epimorphisms, even when they are not defined by a Koszul-regular sequence. See
[
BK20
]
for more information on this topic and in particular loc. cit. Proposition 5.8 where examples of such non-Koszul-regular homotopy epimorphisms are considered in relation to the sheafiness problem for Banach rings. So, in these situations, these two choices lead to very different outcomes.

4

Remark 6.12
.

Theorem

(i) just implies that

is bijective but does not imply that it is an isomorphism, as the open mapping theorem does not necessarily hold for Banach

-modules. Indeed, although it is an isometric isomorphism when
\mathscr{C}=\text{\rm Ab}^{\text{\rm nA}}
by Theorem
6.11
(ii), it is not necessarily an isomorphism when
\mathscr{C}=\text{\rm Ab}^{\text{\rm A}}
by Theorem
6.11
(iv). The condition on

in Theorem
6.11
(iii) is not equivalent to the condition that

is finitely generated as an

-module. Indeed, if

and

(cf. Example

(i) and (iii)), then

is finitely generated but does not satisfy the condition. It is also remarkable that the bijectivity is a special feature of the isolated setting and the local field setting (cf.
[
Mih14
]
Theorem 4.12). For example, when
\mathscr{C}=\text{\rm Ab}^{\text{\rm A}}
,

is the residue field of

,

, and
