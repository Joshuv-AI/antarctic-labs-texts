1.
Introduction

The number of partitions of
n
in which no parts are multiples of
k
is denoted by
b_{k}(n)
and referred to as
k
-regular partitions.

We agree that
b_{k}(0)=1
for convenience. Moreover, let
b_{k}(n)=0
if
n\not\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. The generating function for the
k
-regular partitions is as follows:

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{k}(n)q^{n}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1-q^{kn}}{1-q^{n}}.

In 1919, Ramanujan discovered three remarkable congruences for the unrestricted partition function
p(n)
, as shown below:

\displaystyle p(5n+4)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 5),

\displaystyle p(7n+5)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 7),

\displaystyle p(11n+6)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 11).

We refer to such congruences as Ramanujan-type congruences. Lovejoy
[
7
]
studied the distribution of
b_{2}(n)
and proved the existence of Ramanujan-type congruences for
b_{2}(n)
modulo every prime
m\geq 5
. In a previous work, the author
[
15
]
demonstrated similar results for
b_{3}(n)
and
b_{5}(n)
.

The primary result of this paper is as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

(1)

For every prime
m\geq 2
, there exist infinitely many Ramanujan-type congruences of
b_{4}(n)
modulo
m
.

(2)

For every prime
m\geq 5
, there exist infinitely many Ramanujan-type congruences of
b_{6}(n)
modulo
m
.

Specifically, we find two infinite explicit families of congruences for
b_{4}(n)
modulo
3
.

Theorem 1.2
.

If
l
is a prime with
l\equiv 13,17,19,23\ \left(\mathrm{mod}\ 24\right)
, then

b_{4}\left(3l(ln+j)+\frac{l^{2}-1}{8}\right)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3),

and

b_{4}\left(3l(ln+j)+\frac{9l^{2}-1}{8}\right)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3),

for every
n
and
1\leq j\leq l-1
.

Remark
.

If
l
is a prime with
l\equiv 13,17,19,23\ \left(\mathrm{mod}\ 24\right)

b_{4}\left(3l(ln+j)+\frac{17l^{2}-1}{8}\right)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)

may not always hold. For example, let

,

, and

, we have

.

We will now present the results that have been established for these regular partitions up to the present moment. Notably, a surprising finding is that

In the case of

2.
Preliminaries on modular forms

First, we will introduce the
U
operator. If
j
is a positive integer,

\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a(n)q^{n}\right)\ |\ U(j):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a(jn)q^{n}.

If
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a(n)q^{n}\in M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\chi)
represents a modular form and
m
is a prime,

\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a(n)q^{n}\right)\ |\ T(m):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(a(mn)+\chi(m)m^{k-1}a(n/m))q^{n},

where
a(n/m)=0
if
m\nmid n
. The
T(m)
operator corresponds to the standard Hecke operator. It’s worth recalling that Dedekind’s eta function is defined by

\eta(z)=q^{\frac{1}{24}}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{n}),

where
q=e^{2\pi iz}
.

If
m
is a prime, we denote by
M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\chi)_{m}
(respectively,
S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\chi)_{m}
) the
\mathbb{F}_{m}
-vector space obtained by reducing the
q
-expansions of modular forms (resp. cusp forms) in
M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\chi)
(resp.
S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\chi)
) with integer coefficients modulo
m
.

At times, for convenience, we will use the notation
a\equiv_{m}b
instead of
a\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ m)
.

The construction of modular forms requires the utilization of the following theorem
[
6
, Theorem 3]
:

Theorem 2.1

(B. Gordon, K. Hughes)
.

Let

f(z)=\prod_{\delta|N}\eta^{r_{\delta}}(\delta z)

be a
\eta
-quotient provided

\mathrm{(i)}

\sum_{\delta|N}\delta r_{\delta}\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 24);

\mathrm{(ii)}

\sum_{\delta|N}\frac{Nr_{\delta}}{\delta}\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 24);

\mathrm{(iii)}

k:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\delta|N}r_{\delta}\in\mathbb{Z},

then

f\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right)=\chi(d)(cz+d)^{k}f(z),

for each
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_{0}(N)
and
\chi
is a Dirichlet character
(\mathrm{mod}\ N)
defined by

\chi(n):=\left(\frac{(-1)^{k}\prod_{\delta|N}\delta^{r_{\delta}}}{n}\right),\ if\ n>0\ and\ (n,6)=1.

If
f(z)
is holomorphic (resp. vanishes) at all cusps of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, then
f(z)\in M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N)

,\chi)
(resp.
S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\chi)
), as
\eta(z)
never vanishes on
\mathcal{H}
. The following theorem (cf.
[
8
]
) provides a useful criterion for computing the orders of an
\eta
-quotient at all cusps of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
.

Theorem 2.2

(Y. Martin)
.

Let
c
,
d
, and
N
be positive integers with
d\mid N
and
(c,d)=1
. If
f(z)
is an
\eta
-quotient that satisfies the conditions of Theorem
2.1
, then the order of vanishing of
f(z)
at the cusp
c/d
is

\frac{N}{24}\sum_{\delta|N}\frac{r_{\delta}(d^{2},\delta^{2})}{\delta(d^{2},N)}.

3.
Ramanujan-type congruences

In this section, we will establish the proof of Theorem
1.1
using the theory of modular forms. However, it’s important to note that the generating function of the regular partition function is not a modular form. Nonetheless, for primes
m\geq 5
, it turns out that through the careful selection of a function
h_{m}(n)
, we have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{k}(h_{m}(n))q^{n},

which, in fact, represents the Fourier expansion of a modular form modulo
m
. In fact, we have

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
m\geq 3
be a prime, then

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{4}\left(\frac{mn-1}{8}\right)q^{n}\in M_{3m-3}(\Gamma_{0}(256))_{m}.

The case
m=2
will be discussed in Section
4
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
m\geq 7
be a prime, then

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{6}\left(\frac{mn-5}{24}\right)q^{n}\in S_{2m-2}(\Gamma_{0}(3456),\chi_{6})_{m},

where
\chi_{6}(n)=\left(\frac{6}{n}\right)
. In addition,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{6}\left(\frac{25n-5}{24}\right)q^{n}\in S_{48}(\Gamma_{0}(3456),\chi_{6})_{5}.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We begin with an
\eta
-quotient

f(m;z):=\frac{\eta(4z)}{\eta(z)}\frac{\eta^{a}(4mz)}{\eta^{a}(mz)}\eta^{6}(2mz),

where
m^{\prime}:=(m\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8)
and
a:=4-m^{\prime}
.

It is straightforward to verify that
f(m;z)\equiv_{m}\eta^{am+1}(4z)\eta^{6m}(2z)\eta^{-am-1}(z)
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
2.1
. Furthermore, by applying Theorem
2.2
, one can compute that it attains the minimal order of vanishing of
((8-m^{\prime})m+1)/8
at the cusp
\infty
,
(mm^{\prime}-1)/8
at the cusp
0
, and
m/2
at the cusp
1/2
.

The order is a half-integer due to the potential presence of an irregular cusp at
1/2
. Indeed, an irregular cusp of
\Gamma_{0}(4)
exclusively occurs at the cusp
1/2
with nontrivial nebentypus (see
[
5
, Chapter 3]
).

To sum up, we have

\frac{\eta^{am+1}(4z)}{\eta^{am+1}(z)}\eta^{6m}(2z)\in S_{3m}(\Gamma_{0}(4),\chi_{4}),\text{where}\ \chi_{4}(n)=\left(\frac{-1}{n}\right).

On the other hand,

f(m;z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{4}(n)q^{n+\frac{m(a+4)+1}{8}}\cdot\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q^{4mn})^{a}}{(1-q^{mn})^{a}}(1-q^{2mn})^{6}.

Thus,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\eta^{am+1}(4z)}{\eta^{am+1}(z)}\eta^{6m}(2z)\ |\ U(m)

\displaystyle\equiv_{m}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{4}(n)q^{n+\frac{m(a+4)+1}{8}}\ |\ U(m)\right)\cdot\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q^{4n})^{a}}{(1-q^{n})^{a}}(1-q^{2n})^{6}.

As for the right-hand side of (
3.1
),

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{4}(n)q^{n+\frac{m(a+4)+1}{8}}\ |\ U(m)={\sum_{n\geq 0}}^{*}b_{4}(n)q^{\frac{8n+m(a+4)+1}{8m}},

where
{\sum}^{*}
indicates taking integral power coefficients of
q
, i.e.,

8n+m(a+4)+1\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 8m).

Verifying the condition is straightforward and reveals its equivalence to
m\mid 8n+1
.

As for the left-hand side of (
3.1
), we have

\frac{\eta^{am+1}(4z)}{\eta^{am+1}(z)}\eta^{6m}(2z)\ |\ U(m)\equiv_{m}\frac{\eta^{am+1}(4z)}{\eta^{am+1}(z)}\eta^{6m}(2z)\ |\ T(m),

where
T(m)
denotes the usual Hecke operator acting on
S_{3m}(\Gamma_{0}(4),\chi_{4})
.

We now analyze the
\eta
-product
\eta^{4}(z)\eta^{2}(2z)\eta^{4}(4z)
. According to Theorem
2.1
and Theorem
2.2
, it qualifies as a cusp form of weight
5
and level
4
with nebentypus
\chi_{4}
. It possesses the minimal order of vanishing of
1/2
at the cusp
1/2
and
1
at the other cusps. Since
\eta(z)
never vanishes on
\mathcal{H}
, we can write

\frac{\eta^{am+1}(4z)}{\eta^{am+1}(z)}\eta^{6m}(2z)\ |\ T(m)=\eta^{4}(z)\eta^{2}(2z)\eta^{4}(4z)g(m;z),

where
g(m;z)\in M_{3m-5}(\Gamma_{0}(4))
.

To summarize, we have established

(3.2)

\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n\geq 0}{m|8n+1}}b_{4}(n)q^{\frac{8n+m(a+4)+1}{8m}}\equiv_{m}\eta^{4}(z)\eta^{2}(2z)\eta^{4}(4z)g(m;z)\cdot\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q^{n})^{a}}{(1-q^{2n})^{6}(1-q^{4n})^{a}}.

By substituting
q
with
q^{8}
and then multiplying both sides of (
3.2
) by
q^{-(a+4)}
, we obtain

\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n\geq 0}{m|8n+1}}b_{4}(n)q^{\frac{8n+1}{m}}\equiv_{m}\frac{\eta^{4+a}(8z)\eta^{4-a}(32z)}{\eta^{4}(16z)}g(m;8z),

which can be written as

(3.3)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{4}\left(\frac{mn-1}{8}\right)q^{n}\equiv_{m}\frac{\eta^{4+a}(8z)\eta^{4-a}(32z)}{\eta^{4}(16z)}g(m;8z).

Employing Theorem
2.1
and Theorem
2.2
again, one can confirm that the first term on the right-hand side of (

) lies within
M_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(256))
. In fact, it possesses the minimal order of vanishing of

at the cusps

for

;

for

; and

.

Therefore we obtain

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{4}\left(\frac{mn-1}{8}\right)q^{n}\in M_{3m-3}(\Gamma_{0}(256))_{m}.

∎

The set of primes

for every nonnegative integer

.

.

Let

be a prime. A positive density of the primes

possess the property that

b_{6}\left(\frac{mln-5}{24}\right)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ m)

. In addition, A positive density of the primes

.

b_{4}\left(\frac{mln-1}{8}\right)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ m)

holds for every integer
n
coprime to

. Moreover, the set of such primes
l
possesses a positive density.

∎

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

Let

According to Theorem

, the set of primes

has positive density. Here,

denotes the Hecke operator acting on

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}F(m;z)\ |\ T(l)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(b_{6}\left(\frac{mln-5}{24}\right)+\left(\frac{6}{l}\right)l^{2m-3}b_{6}\left(\frac{mn/l-5}{24}\right)\right)q^{n}.

Since

when

holds for every integer

with

. Moreover, the set of such primes

possesses a positive density.

The proof for
m=5
is similar.

∎

Considering that the number of choices for

is infinite, let us select

holds for every nonnegative integer

If

While we can derive results about the distribution of nonzero residues similar to the previous paper, it is important to note that this is not the main focus of our current work.

4.
Examples of Ramanujan-type congruences

Here, we introduce a theorem by Sturm
[
13
, Theorem 1]
, which offers a useful criterion for determining when modular forms with integer coefficients become congruent to zero modulo a prime through finite computation.

Theorem 4.1

(J. Sturm)
.

Suppose
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a(n)q^{n}\in M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\chi)_{m}
such that

a(n)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ m)

for all
n\leq\frac{kN}{12}\prod_{p|N}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)
. Then
a(n)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ m)
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

According to Theorem
4.1
, we discover that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{4}\left(\frac{mn-1}{8}\right)q^{n}\ |\ T(l)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ m)

is satisfied for the following combinations of
m
and
l
:

(1)

m=5
and
l=809,839,1249,1279,1319,1489,1811
;

(2)

m=7
and
l=1889,1901
.

A straightforward computation shows that

Proposition 4.2
.

For the given values of
m
and
l
, the congruence

b_{4}\left(ml(ln+j)+\frac{m^{2}l^{2}-1}{8}\right)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ m)

satisfied for each
n
and
1\leq j\leq l-1
.

In fact, we have a full characterization of
b_{4}(n)
modulo
2
. Since

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{4}\left(\frac{n-1}{8}\right)q^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{4}(n)q^{8n+1}=\frac{\eta(32z)}{\eta(8z)}\equiv_{2}\eta^{24}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\tau(n)q^{n},

where
\tau(n)
is the Ramanujan tau function and
\tau(n)
is odd if and only if
n
is an odd square (see for example
[
3
, Proof of Theorem 2.2.1]
). Thus
b_{4}(n)
is odd if and only if
8n+1
is an odd square, or equivalently,
b_{4}(n)
is odd if and only if
n
is a triangular number. (Full characterization of
b_{4}(n)
modulo
2
is already proved by Cherubini and Mercuri
[
4
, Theorem 1.3]
, but the argument here is simpler.) So we have

Proposition 4.3
.

For each odd prime
m
, the congruence

b_{4}(m^{2}n+j)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)

satisfied for each
n
, where
j
satisfies
m\,||\,8j+1
.

Now we are going to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We first observe that
l\equiv 13,17,19,23\ \left(\mathrm{mod}\ 24\right)
is equivalent to
\left(\frac{-6}{l}\right)=-1
.

By Theorem 3.1 of
[
2

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{4}(3n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}(q^{6};q^{6})_{\infty}^{4}}{(q;q)_{\infty}^{3}(q^{12};q^{12})^{2}_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\equiv_{3}\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})^{12}_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{\infty}^{3}(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}^{5}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})^{13}_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{\infty}^{5}(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}^{5}}\cdot\frac{(q;q)^{2}_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}.

By the fourth identity of Theorem 1.1 (2) of
[

Let
n_{0}=ln+(l^{2}-1)/24

(n,l)=1

5.
Open problems

In this paper, we establish the existence of infinitely many Ramanujan-type congruences modulo
m
for
b_{6}(n)
, where
m\geq 5
. Additionally, congruences modulo
3
have been explored (see
[
1
]
). However, the question arises: what occurs when
m=2
?

Problem
.

For
b_{6}(n)
, either discover a Ramanujan-type congruence modulo
2
or prove that no such congruence exists.

While Ramanujan-type congruences modulo nearly all primes
m
do exist, it is important to note that discovering them may require extensive computations. We encourage readers who are interested to explore and seek examples of congruences modulo different primes.
