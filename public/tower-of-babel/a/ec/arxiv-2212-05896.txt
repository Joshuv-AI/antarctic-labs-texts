A CLT for the LSS of large dimensional sample covariance matrices with diverging spikes

Abstract
In this paper, we establish the central limit theorem (CLT) for linear spectral statistics (LSSs) of a large-dimensional sample covariance matrix when the population covariance matrices are involved with diverging spikes. This constitutes a nontrivial extension of the Bai-Silverstein theorem (BST) (Ann Probab 32(1):553–605, 2004), a theorem that has strongly influenced the development of high-dimensional statistics, especially in the applications of random matrix theory to statistics. Recently, there has been a growing realization that the assumption of uniform boundedness of the population covariance matrices in the BST is not satisfied in some fields, such as economics, where the variances of principal components may diverge as the dimension tends to infinity. Therefore, in this paper, we aim to eliminate this obstacle to applications of the BST. Our new CLT accommodates spiked eigenvalues, which may either be bounded or tend to infinity. A distinguishing feature of our result is that the variance in the new CLT is related to both spiked eigenvalues and bulk eigenvalues, with dominance being determined by the divergence rate of the largest spiked eigenvalues. The new CLT for LSS is then applied to test the hypothesis that the population covariance matrix is the identity matrix or a generalized spiked model. The asymptotic distributions of the corrected likelihood ratio test statistic and the corrected Nagao’s trace test statistic are derived under the alternative hypothesis. Moreover, we present power comparisons between these two LSSs and Roy’s largest root test. In particular, we demonstrate that except for the case in which the number of spikes is equal to one, the LSSs could exhibit higher asymptotic power than Roy’s largest root test.

Introduction

We consider the general sample covariance matrix
{\bf B}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}{\bf T}_{p}{\bf X}_{n}{\bf X}_{n}^{\ast}{\bf T}_{p}^{\ast}
, where
{\bf X}_{n}
is a
p\times n
matrix with independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) standardized entries
\left\{x_{ij}\right\}_{1\leq i\leq p,1\leq j\leq n}
,
{\bf T}_{p}
is a
p\times p
deterministic matrix,
{\bf T}_{p}{\bf X}_{n}
is considered a random sample from the population with the population covariance matrix
{\bf T}_{p}{\bf T}_{p}^{\ast}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, and
∗
represents
the complex conjugate transpose. In the sequel, we simply write
{\bf B}\equiv{\bf B}_{n}
,
{\bf T}\equiv{\bf T}_{p}
and
{\bf X}\equiv{\bf X}_{n}
when there is no confusion.
Let
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{p}
be the eigenvalues of
{\bf B}
.
For a known test function
f
, we call
\sum_{j=1}^{p}f\left({\bf\lambda}_{j}\right)
a linear spectral statistic (LSS) of
{\bf B}
. Because most of the classical test statistics in multivariate statistical analysis are associated with the eigenvalues of sample covariance matrices, LSSs are remarkable tools in many statistical problems (see
Anderson 2003
;
Yao et al. 2015
for details).
Through extensive study of high-dimensional data,
it has been discovered that the distributions of LSSs significantly differ between low-dimensional and high-dimensional data.

For example, in the low-dimensional setting, Wilks’ theorem (see
Wilks 1938
) provides the
\chi^{2}
approximation for the likelihood ratio test (LRT) statistic, which is a kind of LSS. However, when
p
is large compared with the sample size
n
, the LRT statistic exhibits Gaussian fluctuations (see
Bai et al. 2009
;
Jiang and Yang 2013
).

More generally,
Bai and Silverstein 2004
established the central limit theorem (CLT) for the LSSs of a high-dimensional
{\bf B}
under Gaussian-like moments condition by employing random matrix theory (RMT). Here the term ‘Gaussian-like moments’ refers to the population second-order and fourth-order moments are the same as those of real or complex Gaussian population.
We refer to this CLT as the Bai–Silverstein theorem (BST) for brevity. Following the work of
Bai and Silverstein 2004
, many extensions have been developed under different settings.
Pan and Zhou 2008
generalized the BST by relaxing the Gaussian-like moments condition of
x_{ij}
, which at the price of adding a structural condition on
{\bf T}
.

Zheng 2012
,
Yang and Pan 2015
and
Bao et al. 2022
extended the BST to multivariate
F
matrices, canonical correlation matrices and block correlation matrices, respectively.
Pan 2014
presented the CLT for the LSS of noncentered sample covariance matrices, and
Zheng et al. 2015
studied the case of an unbiased sample covariance matrix when the population mean is unknown.
Chen and Pan 2015
focused on the ultrahigh dimensional case in which the dimension
p
is much larger than the sample size
n
.
Gao et al. 2017
and
Li et al. 2021
studied the CLTs for the LSSs of high-dimensional Spearman and Kendall’s rank correlation matrices, respectively. Without attempting to be comprehensive, we also refer readers to other extensions
(
Bai et al. 2007
;
Bai et al. 2015
;
Bai et al. 2019
;
Zheng et al. 2019
;
Banna et al. 2020
;
Najim and Yao 2016
;
Baik et al. 2018
;
Hu et al. 2019
;
Jiang and Bai 2021
)
.

Almost all the literature mentioned above have traditionally assumed that the spectral norms of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
are bounded in
n
.

This assumption limits the applications in data analysis because in many fields, such as economics and wireless communication networks, the leading eigenvalues may tend to infinity. We present two examples here.

•

Signal detection
(
Johnstone and Nadler 2017
): We consider a single signal model:

{\bm{x}}=\chi_{s}^{1/2}u{\bm{h}}+\sigma\bm{v},

where
{\bm{h}}
is an unknown
p
-dimensional unit vector,
u
is a random variable distributed as
N(0,1)
,
\chi_{s}
is the signal strength,
\sigma
is the noise level, and
\bm{v}
is
a random noise vector that is independent of
u
and follows a multivariate Gaussian distribution
N_{p}(0,\bm{\Sigma}_{v})
.
It is easy to check that the covariance matrix of
\bm{x}
is
\bm{\Sigma}_{x}=\sigma^{2}\bm{\Sigma}_{v}+\chi_{s}{\bm{h}}{\bm{h}}^{\top}
. When the noise level is low,
but the signal strength is large and sometimes tends to infinity, it is illogical to assume the boundedness of
\bm{\Sigma}_{x}
.

•

Factor model
(
Bai and Ng 2002
): Many economic analyses, such as arbitrage pricing theory and analyses of the rank of a demand system, align naturally within the framework of the factor model:

\displaystyle\mathop{\bm{x}_{t}}\limits_{\left(N\times 1\right)}=\mathop{{\bf\Lambda}}\limits_{\left(N\times r\right)}\mathop{\bm{f}_{t}}\limits_{\left(r\times 1\right)}+\mathop{\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}}\limits_{\left(N\times 1\right)}~~t=1,\dots,T.

where
\bm{x}_{t}
is the observed data,
N
represents the number of cross-sections,
T
is a large time dimension, and
\bm{f}_{t},{\bf\Lambda}
and
\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}
represent the common factors, the factor loadings and the idiosyncratic error term, respectively. To ensure the identification of the model, several conventional assumptions are needed, such as
\mathbb{E}\bm{f}_{t}=\bf 0
,
\mathbb{E}\left(\bm{f}_{t}\bm{f}_{t}^{\top}\right)={\bf I}_{r}
,
\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}
is independent of
\bm{f}_{t}
with
\mathbb{E}\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}=\bf 0
and
\mathbb{E}\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}>0
.
Then the covariance matrices of
\bm{x}_{t}
can be expressed as
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{x}={\bf\Lambda}{\bf\Lambda}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}
. A pervasive assumption is that the variances of the principal components
{\bf\Lambda}\bm{f}_{t}
can diverge as
N
increases to infinity (see Assumption B of
Bai and Ng 2002
). Therefore, the spectral norms of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{x}
are unbounded.

For these reasons, it is of practical value to obtain the asymptotic properties of the LSS when
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is unbounded. Therefore, in this paper,
we focus on a generalized CLT for the LSSs of a spiked covariance matrix with the following structure:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathbf{V}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bf D}_{1}&0\\
0&{\bf D}_{2}\end{array}\right)\mathbf{V}^{\ast},

where
\mathbf{V}
is a unitary matrix,
{\bf D}_{1}
is a diagonal matrix consisting of the descending unbounded eigenvalues, and
{\bf D}_{2}
is the diagonal matrix of the bounded eigenvalues.
As an application, the established CLT is employed to study the asymptotic behaviors of two special LSSs, i.e., the likelihood ratio (LR) statistic and Nagao’s trace (NT) statistic, under the hypothesis

\displaystyle H_{0}:\boldsymbol{\Sigma}={\bf I}_{p}\quad\text{vs.}\quad H_{1}:\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathbf{V}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bf D}_{1}&0\\
0&{\bf I}_{p-M}\end{array}\right)\mathbf{V}^{\ast},

where
M
is a constant.
We also derive the asymptotic power of Roy’s largest root test to detect the above hypothesis and make a comparison with these two LSSs.

The setting (
1
) is attributed to the famous spiked model
in which a few large eigenvalues of the population covariance matrix are assumed to be well separated from the remaining eigenvalues
(
Johnstone 2001
)
. The spiked model has served as the foundation for a rich theory of principal component analysis through the performance of extreme eigenvalues, as discussed in
Baik and Silverstein 2006
;
Paul 2007
;
Bai and Yao 2008
;
Nadler 2008
;
Jung and Marron 2009
;
Bai and Yao 2012
;
Onatski et al. 2014
;
Bloemendal et al. 2016
;
Wang and Yao 2017
;
Donoho et al. 2018
;
Perry et al. 2018
;
Johnstone and Paul 2018
;
Yang and Johnstone 2018
;
Yao et al. 2018
;
Dobriban 2020
;
Johnstone and Onatski 2020
;
Cai et al. 2020
;
Jiang and Bai 2021
.
There are also several works that have considered the asymptotic behaviors of various quantities as the spike strengths tend to infinity. Specifically,
Zhou and Marron 2015
focused on the consistency of the sample eigenvector, corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of the sample covariance matrix, under high dimension and low sample size settings, when
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is unbounded and the data set is Gaussian.
Wang and Fan 2017
derived the asymptotic distributions of the spiked eigenvalues and eigenvectors when
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is diagonal and unbounded, and the data set is sub-Gaussian.

Recently,
Li et al. 2020
,
Yin 2021
and
Zhang et al. 2022
investigated the trace of a large sample covariance matrix under the spiked model assumption.

To summarize, the contributions of this paper are as follows.

1.

We demonstrate a nontrivial extension of the BST to the situation in which the spectral norms of the population covariance matrices are allowed to diverge as
\min\{p,n\}\to\infty
. In particular, we show how the test function
f
and the divergence rate of the population spectral norm affect the new CLT.

2.

It was previously reported that Gaussian-like moments or diagonality of the population covariance matrix are necessary for the CLT of the LSS (e.g.,
Zheng et al. 2015
). Nevertheless, we prove that these restrictions can be completely removed by normalizing the LSS. More importantly, even if no limit exists on the variance of the LSS, the new CLT could still hold.

3.

The entire technical part of this paper is built on the decomposition of the LSS
\sum_{j=1}^{p}f\left(\lambda_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}f\left(\lambda_{j}\right)+\sum_{j=M+1}^{p}f\left(\lambda_{j}\right)
. Because the classical delta method cannot be applied to the unbounded part
\sum_{j=1}^{M}f\left(\lambda_{j}\right)
and the bounded part
\sum_{j=M+1}^{p}f\left(\lambda_{j}\right)
is not a strict LSS of a sample covariance matrix, the results of
Bai and Silverstein 2004
and
Jiang and Bai 2021
cannot be adopted directly. In this paper,
we leverage a ‘generalized delta method’ and employ skillful transformations to prove the CLTs for the unbounded and bounded parts, respectively. Moreover, we prove that the unbounded and bounded parts are asymptotically independent, which leads to the establishment of the new CLT.

4.

We verify that Roy’s largest root test is most powerful among the common tests when the alternative (
1
) has only one spiked eigenvalue, which has also been mentioned by
Olson 1974
;
Johnstone and Nadler 2017
. Furthermore,
we demonstrate that when the number of spikes is larger than one, the LSSs could exhibit higher asymptotic power than Roy’s largest root test when the divergence rates of spiked eigenvalues are higher than
\sqrt{n}
.

The remaining sections are organized as follows: Section
2
presents a detailed description of our notations and assumptions. The main results for the CLT for the LSSs of sample covariance matrices are stated in Section
3
. In Section
4
, we explore an application of our main results.
We also report the results of numerical studies in Section
5
.
Technical proofs are presented in Section
6
.

This paper is also accompanied by an online supplementary file that includes the following materials: (i) some postponed proofs for Theorems
3.1
–
4.5
; (ii) some additional simulation results, and (iii) some useful lemmas.

m_{2n}=\frac{1}{p-M}\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf S}_{22}-z{\bf I}_{p-M}\right)^{-1}
.

Note that

\sum_{j=1}^{p}f\left({\bf\lambda}_{j}\right)=p\int f\left(x\right)dF^{{\bf B}}(x).

Thus, for brevity, we define the normalized LSSs as

Y_{l}=\int f_{l}\left(x\right)dG_{n}\left(x\right)-{\sum_{k=1}^{K}d_{k}f_{l}\left(\phi_{n}\left(\alpha_{k}\right)\right)}-\frac{M}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f_{l}\left(z\right)\frac{\underline{m}_{2n0}^{\prime}(z)}{\underline{m}_{2n0}(z)}dz,\quad l=1,2,\dots,h,

where

G_{n}\left(x\right)=p[F^{{\bf B}}\left(x\right)-F^{c_{n},H_{n}}\left(x\right)].

3
Main results

Now, we are in a position to present our main theorems and their proofs are provided in Section
6
and the supplementary material. We first establish a CLT for an LSS
without any restrictions imposed on the Gaussian moments
or on the structures of the population covariance matrix by normalizing the LSS.

Theorem 3.1
.

Under Assumptions

1
–
4

\frac{Y_{1}-\mu_{1}}{\varsigma_{1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N\left(0,1\right),

where

\displaystyle\varsigma_{1}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\varpi_{nk1}^{2}s_{k}^{2}-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}f_{1}\left(z_{1}\right)f_{1}\left(z_{2}\right)\vartheta_{n}^{2}dz_{1}dz_{2},

(3.7)

\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
are nonoverlapping and closed contours in the complex plane enclosing the support of

.
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
are also enclosed in the analytic area of

Remark 3.1
.

Recall the definitions
s_{k}^{2}=\frac{\left(\alpha_{x}+1\right)d_{k}}{\theta_{k}}+\frac{\beta_{x}\nu_{k}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}\in J_{k}}\mathcal{U}_{j_{1}j_{1}j_{2}j_{2}}}{\theta_{k}^{2}}
and

\vartheta_{n}^{2}=\Theta_{0,n}(z_{1},z_{2})+\alpha_{x}\Theta_{1,n}(z_{1},z_{2})+\beta_{x}\Theta_{2,n}(z_{1},z_{2}).
Notably, the term
\Theta_{0,n}(z_{1},z_{2})
has a limitation under Assumptions

1
–
4
, which has already been discussed in
Bai and Silverstein 2004
. Additionally, if
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is complex, the convergence of
\Theta_{1,n}(z_{1},z_{2})
is not guaranteed.
The term
\Theta_{2,n}(z_{1},z_{2})
involves the quantities
\left[{\bf\Gamma}^{*}{\bf P}_{n}^{2}(z_{i}){\bf\Gamma}\right]_{ii}
, which depend not only on the eigenvalues of
{\bf D}_{2}
but also on their associated eigenvectors.
Furthermore, the term
s_{k}^{2}
indicates that the variance is influenced by the second and fourth moments of
x_{ij}
, spiked eigenvalues, and their associated eigenvectors. The limit of
\varpi_{nk1}^{2}
is allowed to be infinite.

Remark 3.2
.

After a closer look at the variance (
3.7
), we can also find that the first part of the formula (
3.7
) is the variance containing diverging spikes and the second part is the variance containing the bounded eigenvalues. When
\phi_{n}\left(\alpha_{1}\right)f^{\prime}_{1}\left(\phi_{n}\left(\alpha_{1}\right)\right)=o\left(\sqrt{n}\right),
the first term in formula (
3.7
) tends to 0, and the variance is mainly affected by the second part. When
\phi_{n}\left(\alpha_{1}\right)f^{\prime}_{1}\left(\phi_{n}\left(\alpha_{1}\right)\right)
is of order
\sqrt{n}
, the two parts of formula (
3.7
) are of the same order, and the variance is affected by both. When the order of
\phi_{n}\left(\alpha_{1}\right)f^{\prime}_{1}\left(\phi_{n}\left(\alpha_{1}\right)\right)
is higher than
\sqrt{n}
, the first part of formula (
3.7
) is much larger than the second part; therefore, the spiked part dominates the variance value.

As a minor price for the removal of the bounded spectrum condition, the new CLT described above applies only to a single LSS. To guarantee that the new CLT will apply to multiple normalized LSSs, structural
assumptions about the population covariance matrices are needed.

Assumption 5
.

{\bf T}
is real or the variables
x_{ij}
are complex satisfying
\alpha_{x}=0.

Assumption 6
.

{\bf T}^{*}{\bf T}
is diagonal or
\beta_{x}=0.

Remark 3.3
.

Assumptions

and
6
are used as a replacement for the Gaussian-like moments condition. It has been proved by

that these two structural assumptions regarding the population matrices are necessary for their results when the Gaussian-like moments condition in the BST does not hold.

The following theorem is a nontrivial extension of the BST:

Theorem 3.2
.

Under Assumptions

–
6
, the random vector

with variance

and covariance matrix

,

4
Application

In this section, we focus on a hypothesis test concerning whether the population covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is equal to the identity matrix or a spiked model, i.e.,

\displaystyle H_{0}:\boldsymbol{\Sigma}={\bf I}_{p}\quad\text{vs.}\quad H_{1}:\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathbf{V}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bf D}_{1}&0\\
0&{\bf I}_{p-M}\end{array}\right)\mathbf{V}^{\ast},

where
{\bf D}_{1}
is a diagonal matrix of the diverging spiked eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. There are several classical test statistics for this problem, but due to the limited length of this paper, we only consider the likelihood ratio (LR) test statistic
(
Wilks 1938
)
and the Nagao’s trace (NT) test statistic
(
Nagao 1973
)
in this section. Specifically, the LR and NT statistics can be formulated as

L=\operatorname{tr}{\bf B}-\log\left|{\bf B}\right|-p~~\mbox{and}~~W=\mathrm{tr}({\bf B}-{\bf I}_{p})^{2},

respectively. Under the null hypothesis, the asymptotic properties of the LR and NT statistics for high-dimensional settings have been investigated extensively in the literature; here, we refer to
Bai et al. 2009
;
Jiang and Yang 2013
;
Ledoit and Wolf 2002
;
Wang and Yao 2013
;
Onatski et al. 2013
for more details.
Thus, in this section, we mainly focus on the alternative hypothesis. However, to provide better comparisons, we also present the asymptotic distributions under the null hypothesis in the following theorems.

4.1
Asymptotic results for the LR and NT statistics

In this subsection, we present the asymptotic results for LR and NT test statistics for the testing problem (
4
).

Theorem 4.1
(CLT for the LR statistic).

Under Assumptions
1
–
4
with

•

(Under

•

(Under

\displaystyle 1-\frac{c_{nM}-1}{c_{nM}}\log\left(1-c_{nM}\right),~\breve{\mu}_{l}=-\frac{\log\left(1-c_{nM}\right)}{2}\alpha_{x}+\frac{c_{nM}}{2}\beta_{x}

Numerical studies

In this section, we report short numerical studies as an illustration of our results.
Our objective in the simulations is to examine the power analysis in subsection
4.2
.

We examine the following three different distributions of
x_{ij}:

Dt_{1}
:

\left\{x_{ij}\right\}
are i.i.d. samples from a standard Gaussian population.

Dt_{2}
:

\left\{x_{ij}\right\}
are i.i.d. samples from
Gamma(4,0.5)-2
.

Dt_{3}
:

\left\{x_{ij}\right\}
are i.i.d. samples from Uniform population distribution
U[-\sqrt{3},\sqrt{3}]
.

Note that in above settings,
\beta_{x}=0,\frac{3}{2},-\frac{6}{5},
respectively.

In the current numerical studies, the null hypothesis is defined as

H_{0}:\boldsymbol{\Sigma}={\bf I}_{p}.

For the alternative hypothesis, we adopt the following six population covariance matrix structures:

H_{1}
:

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}={\bf\Lambda}_{1}=diag(\alpha_{1},\underbrace{1,1,\dots,1}_{p-1})
.

H_{2}
:

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}={\bf\Lambda}_{2}=diag(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\underbrace{1,1,\dots,1}_{p-2}),\alpha_{2}=0.9\alpha_{1}
.

H_{3}
:

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}={\bf\Lambda}_{3}=diag(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{5},\underbrace{1,1,\dots,1}_{p-5}),\alpha_{2}=0.9\alpha_{1},\alpha_{3}=0.85\alpha_{1},\alpha_{4}=0.8\alpha_{1},\alpha_{5}=0.75\alpha_{1}.

H_{4}
:

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}={\bf U}_{0}{\bf\Lambda}_{1}{\bf U}_{0}^{*},

where
{\bf U}_{0}
is the left singular vectors of a
p\times p
random matrix with i.i.d.
N(0,1)

entries

H_{5}
:

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}={\bf U}_{0}{\bf\Lambda}_{2}{\bf U}_{0}^{*},
and
{\bf U}_{0}
is defined in
H_{4}.

H_{6}
:

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}={\bf U}_{0}{\bf\Lambda}_{3}{\bf U}_{0}^{*},
and
{\bf U}_{0}
is defined in
H_{4}.

Note that
in above settings,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is diagonal and

under

–
H_{3}

\xi
are constructed as follows:

0.01,

Technical proofs

In this section, we present some lemmas that are needed in the proofs of the main results. The truncation and renormalization are postponed to the end of this paper.

6.1
Some primary definitions and lemmas

In this section, we provide some useful results that are used later in the proofs of Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
. For the population covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}={\bf T}{\bf T}^{\ast}
, we consider the corresponding sample covariance matrix
{\bf B}={\bf T}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf T}^{\ast}
, where
{\bf S}_{x}=\frac{1}{n}{\bf X}{\bf X}^{\ast}
.
By singular value decomposition of
{\bf T}
(see (
2.5
)),

{\bf B}={\bf V}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{1}^{\ast}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf U}_{1}{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}},&{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{1}^{\ast}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf U}_{2}{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{2}^{\ast}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf U}_{1}{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}},&{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{2}^{\ast}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf U}_{2}{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right){\bf V}^{\ast}.

Note that

{\bf S}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{1}^{\ast}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf U}_{1}{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}},&{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{1}^{\ast}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf U}_{2}{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{2}^{\ast}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf U}_{1}{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}},&{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{2}^{\ast}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf U}_{2}{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right)\triangleq\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bf S}_{11},&{\bf S}_{12}\\
{\bf S}_{21},&{\bf S}_{22}\end{array}\right).

Moreover,
{\bf B}
and
{\bf S}
have the same eigenvalues.

Recall that
\underline{{\bf B}}=\frac{1}{n}{\bf X}^{\ast}{\bf T}^{\ast}{\bf T}{\bf X}
(the spectrum of which differs from that of
{\bf B}
by
\left|n-p\right|
zeros). Its LSD is
\underline{F}^{c,H}
,
\underline{F}^{c,H}\equiv\left(1-c\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\left[0,\infty\right)}+cF^{c,H}
, and its Stieltjes transform is
\underline{m}\left(z\right)
.
Let
\widetilde{{\bf\lambda}}_{j}
be the eigenvalues of
{\bf S}_{22}
so that the LSS of
{\bf S}_{22}
is
\sum_{j=1}^{p-M}f(\widetilde{{\bf\lambda}}_{j})
. Correspondingly, recall that
c_{nM}:=\frac{p-M}{n}
,
H_{2n}:=F^{{\bf D}_{2}}
and
m_{2n0}:=m_{2n0}(z)
is the
Stieltjes transform
of
F^{c_{nM},H_{2n}}
. First, in Lemma
6.1
we derive that the difference between the two centers is
0
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
4
,

\left(p-M\right)\int f\left(x\right)dF^{c_{nM},H_{2n}}=p\int f\left(x\right)dF^{c_{n},H_{n}}\left(x\right).

By the Cauchy integral formula,

p\int f(x)dF^{c_{n},H_{n}}=-\frac{p}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f(z)m_{1n0}dz=-\frac{n}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f(z)\underline{m}_{1n0}dz,

(p-M)\int f(x)dF^{c_{nM},H_{2n}}=-\frac{p-M}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f(z)m_{2n0}dz=-\frac{n}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f(z)\underline{m}_{2n0}dz,

where
\underline{m}_{1n0}
and
\underline{m}_{2n0}
are the Stieltjes transforms of
\underline{F}^{c_{n},H_{n}}
and
\underline{F}^{c_{nM},H_{2n}}
, respectively.
Then,

(p-M)\int f(x)dF^{c_{nM},H_{2n}}-p\int f(x)dF^{c_{n},H_{n}}=\frac{n}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f(z)\left(\underline{m}_{1n0}-\underline{m}_{2n0}\right)dz.

Next, we prove that
\underline{m}_{1n0}=\underline{m}_{2n0}
.

Note that

m_{1n0}
and
m_{2n0}
are the unique solutions to

\displaystyle z=-\frac{1}{\underline{m}_{1n0}}+c_{n}\int\frac{tdH_{n}\left(t\right)}{1+t\underline{m}_{1n0}}

(6.14)

\displaystyle z=-\frac{1}{\underline{m}_{2n0}}+c_{nM}\int\frac{tdH_{2n}\left(t\right)}{1+t\underline{m}_{2n0}},

(6.15)

respectively,
where
\underline{m}_{1n0}=-\frac{1-c_{n}}{z}+c_{n}m_{1n0}
and
\underline{m}_{2n0}=-\frac{1-c_{nM}}{z}+c_{nM}m_{2n0}
.
Since

H_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{p}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{0\leq t\right\}}+\sum_{i=M+1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\alpha_{i}\leq t\right\}}\right]=\frac{M}{p}+\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=M+1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\alpha_{i}\leq t\right\}}

and
H_{2n}(t)=\frac{1}{p-M}\sum_{i=M+1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\alpha_{i}\leq t\right\}}
,
(
6.14
) can be written as

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\underline{m}_{1n0}}+\frac{p}{n}\int\frac{td\left(\frac{M}{p}+\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=M+1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\alpha_{i}\leq t\right\}}\left(t\right)\right)}{1+t\underline{m}_{1n0}}=-\frac{1}{\underline{m}_{1n0}}+\frac{p}{n}\int\frac{td\left(\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=M+1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\alpha_{i}\leq t\right\}}\left(t\right)\right)}{1+t\underline{m}_{1n0}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\underline{m}_{1n0}}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=M+1}^{p}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{1+\alpha_{i}\underline{m}_{1n0}}.

(6.16)

Similarly, equation (
6.15
) can be written as

z=-\frac{1}{\underline{m}_{2n0}}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=M+1}^{p}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{1+\alpha_{i}\underline{m}_{2n0}}.

Thus, according to the fact that
m_{1n0}
and
m_{2n0}
are the unique solutions of (
6.16
) and (
6.1
), respectively, we have
m_{1n0}=m_{2n0}
, which completes the proof of this lemma.

∎

Note that for the bounded part of the LSS,
\sum_{j=M+1}^{p}f\left(\lambda_{j}\right)
, the BST cannot be used directly since it is not an LSS of a sample covariance matrix. In fact, it approximates the LSS of
{\bf S}_{22}
, that is
\sum_{j=1}^{p-M}f\left(\tilde{\lambda}_{j}\right)
, but they are not equal since the off-diagonal blocks of the sample covariance matrix are not null. The following lemma measures the difference between
\sum_{j=M+1}^{p}f\left(\lambda_{j}\right)
and
\sum_{j=1}^{p-M}f\left(\tilde{\lambda}_{j}\right)
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
4
,

\sum_{j=M+1}^{p}f\left(\lambda_{j}\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{p-M}f\left(\widetilde{{\bf\lambda}}_{j}\right)-\frac{M}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f\left(z\right)\frac{\underline{m}_{2n0}^{{}^{\prime}}(z)}{\underline{m}_{2n0}(z)}dz=o_{p}(1).

Note that

L_{1}:=\sum_{j=M+1}^{p}f\left(\lambda_{j}\right).

By the Cauchy integral formula, we have

L_{1}=-\frac{p}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f\left(z\right)m_{n}\left(z\right)dz,

where
m_{n}=\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf S}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}=\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf B}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}
. Analogously, we have

L_{2}:=\sum_{j=1}^{p-M}f\left(\widetilde{{\bf\lambda}}_{j}\right)=-\frac{p-M}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f\left(z\right)m_{2n}\left(z\right)dz,

where
m_{2n}=\frac{1}{p-M}\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf S}_{22}-z{\bf I}_{p-M}\right)^{-1}
. By applying the block matrix inversion formula to
m_{n}
, we can obtain

\displaystyle L_{1}-L_{2}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f\left(z\right)\left(T_{1}-T_{2}\right)dz,

(6.17)

where

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf S}_{11}-z{\bf I}_{M}-{\bf S}_{12}\left({\bf S}_{22}-z{\bf I}_{p-M}\right)^{-1}{\bf S}_{21}\right)^{-1},

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{tr}\left[\left({\bf S}_{11}-z{\bf I}_{M}-{\bf S}_{12}\left({\bf S}_{22}-z{\bf I}_{p-M}\right)^{-1}{\bf S}_{21}\right)^{-1}{\bf S}_{12}\left({\bf S}_{22}-z{\bf I}_{p-M}\right)^{-2}{\bf S}_{21}\right].

Note that for any matrix
{\bf Z}
,

\displaystyle{\bf Z}\left({\bf Z}^{\ast}{\bf Z}-\lambda{\bf I}\right)^{-1}{\bf Z}^{\ast}={\bf I}+\lambda\left({\bf Z}{\bf Z}^{\ast}-\lambda{\bf I}\right)^{-1},

which, together with the notation
{\bf\Upsilon}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{1}^{\ast}{\bf X}\left(\frac{1}{n}{\bf X}^{\ast}{\bf U}_{2}{\bf D}_{2}{\bf U}_{2}^{*}{\bf X}-z{\bf I}_{n}\right)^{-1}{\bf X}^{\ast}{\bf U}_{1}{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}
, implies that

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=-z^{-1}\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf I}_{M}+{\bf\Upsilon}_{n}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle T_{2}

6.2
Truncation and renormalization

In this subsection, we truncate and renormalize the random variables to ensure the existence of their higher-order moments.

Similar to
Jiang and Bai 2021
, we may select
\eta_{n}\rightarrow 0
such that
\eta_{n}^{-4}n^{2}P(|x_{ij}|\geq\eta_{n}\sqrt{n})\to 0

Let

\tilde{x}_{ij}=\frac{\hat{x}_{ij}-\mathbbm{E}\hat{x}_{ij}}{\hat{\sigma}_{n}}

\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}=\mathbbm{E}\left|\hat{x}_{ij}-\mathbbm{E}\hat{x}_{ij}\right|^{2}

Analogous to the discussion in
Li and Bai 2015

\eta_{n}\rightarrow 0
can be made arbitrarily slow, therefore, we may require it satisfying

.
Correspondingly, we define

\tilde{{\bf B}}=\frac{1}{n}{\bf T}\tilde{{\bf X}}\tilde{{\bf X}}^{\ast}{\bf T}^{\ast}

\hat{{\bf X}}=(\hat{x}_{ij})
and
\tilde{{\bf X}}=(\tilde{x}_{ij})

and

{\bf B}
replaced by
\hat{{\bf B}}
and
\tilde{{\bf B}}
, respectively.

\mathbf{X}
are replaced by the truncated and renormalized entries.

From Supplement B in
Jiang and Bai 2021

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}({\bf B}\neq\hat{{\bf B}})\leq\sum_{i,j}\mathrm{P}\left(\left|x_{ij}\right|\geq\eta_{n}\sqrt{n}\right)\leq np\mathrm{P}\left(\left|x_{11}\right|\geq\eta_{n}\sqrt{n}\right)\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{ as }n,p\rightarrow\infty.

It follows from the definition of LSS and Lemma
6.2

\displaystyle\int f_{l}(x)d\hat{G}_{n}-\int f_{l}(x)d\tilde{G}_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}(f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}(f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}}))+\sum_{i=M+1}^{p}(f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}}))

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{p-M}f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf S}}_{22}})-\sum_{i=M+1}^{p}f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}(f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}}))+\sum_{i=1}^{p-M}(f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf S}}_{22}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf S}}_{22}}))

\displaystyle+\frac{M}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f_{l}\left(z\right)\frac{\hat{\underline{m}}_{2n0}^{{}^{\prime}}(z)}{\hat{\underline{m}}_{2n0}(z)}dz-\frac{M}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f_{l}\left(z\right)\frac{\tilde{\underline{m}}_{2n0}^{{}^{\prime}}(z)}{\tilde{\underline{m}}_{2n0}(z)}dz.

Here
\hat{{\bf S}}_{22}
and
\tilde{{\bf S}}_{22}
denote the analogs of
{\bf S}_{22}
with the matrix
{\bf X}
replaced by
\hat{{\bf X}}
and
\tilde{{\bf X}}
, respectively.
\hat{\underline{m}}_{2n0}(z)
and
\tilde{\underline{m}}_{2n0}(z)
denote the analogs of
\underline{m}_{2n0}(z)
with the matrix
{\bf X}
replaced by
\hat{{\bf X}}
and
\tilde{{\bf X}}
, respectively.

Therefore,
\left|\hat{Y}_{l}-\tilde{Y}_{l}\right|=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{M}(f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}}))+\sum_{i=1}^{p-M}(f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf S}}_{22}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf S}}_{22}}))\right|\leq\\
\left|\sum_{i=1}^{M}(f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}}))\right|+\left|\sum_{i=1}^{p-M}(f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf S}}_{22}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf S}}_{22}}))\right|.

For
\left|\sum_{i=1}^{p-M}(f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf S}}_{22}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf S}}_{22}}))\right|,
we have

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{p-M}(f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf S}}_{22}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf S}}_{22}}))\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{p-M}\left|f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf S}}_{22}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf S}}_{22}})\right|\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{p-M}\left|\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf S}}_{22}}-\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf S}}_{22}}\right|.

Similar to Lemma 2.7 in
Bai 1999
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{p-M}\left|\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf S}}_{22}}-\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf S}}_{22}}\right|\leq\left(\frac{2}{n}\mathrm{tr}(\hat{{\bf S}}_{22}+\tilde{{\bf S}}_{22})\mathrm{tr}{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{2}^{*}(\hat{{\bf X}}-\tilde{{\bf X}})(\hat{{\bf X}}-\tilde{{\bf X}})^{*}{\bf U}_{2}{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2}{n}\mathrm{tr}(\hat{{\bf S}}_{22}+\tilde{{\bf S}}_{22})\mathrm{tr}(\hat{{\bf X}}-\tilde{{\bf X}})(\hat{{\bf X}}-\tilde{{\bf X}})^{*}{\bf U}_{2}{\bf D}_{2}{\bf U}_{2}^{*}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2C}{n}\mathrm{tr}(\hat{{\bf S}}_{22}+\tilde{{\bf S}}_{22})\mathrm{tr}(\hat{{\bf X}}-\tilde{{\bf X}})(\hat{{\bf X}}-\tilde{{\bf X}})^{*}\right)^{1/2},

where the last inequality is because
{\bf U}_{2}{\bf D}_{2}{\bf U}_{2}^{*}
is bounded. It is easy to prove
\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\mathrm{tr}(\hat{{\bf S}}_{22}+\tilde{{\bf S}}_{22})<C,
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathrm{tr}(\hat{{\bf X}}-\tilde{{\bf X}})(\hat{{\bf X}}-\tilde{{\bf X}})^{*}=\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}\left|(1-\frac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{n}})\hat{x}_{ij}+\frac{\mathbb{E}\hat{x}_{ij}}{\hat{\sigma}_{n}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\left(\mbox{var}((1-\frac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{n}})\hat{x}_{ij}+\frac{\mathbb{E}\hat{x}_{ij}}{\hat{\sigma}_{n}})+\left|\mathbb{E}\hat{x}_{ij}\right|^{2}\right).

(6.23)

Since
\mbox{var}\left((1-\frac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{n}})\hat{x}_{ij}+\frac{\mathbb{E}\hat{x}_{ij}}{\hat{\sigma}_{n}}\right)=(\hat{\sigma}_{n}-1)^{2}\leq\frac{(\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}-1)^{2}}{\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}+1}\leq 2\left(\mathbb{E}\left|x_{ij}\right|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\left|x_{ij}\right|\geq\eta_{n}\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)^{2},
and by the selection of
\eta_{n}
,

\mathbb{E}\left|x_{ij}\right|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\left|x_{ij}\right|\geq\eta_{n}\sqrt{n}\right\}}=o(\eta_{n}^{2}n^{-1}),

\mathbb{E}\hat{x}_{ij}=o(\eta_{n}n^{-3/2}),
then we can obtain (
6.23
) is o(1). Therefore,
\sum_{i=1}^{p-M}\left|\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf S}}_{22}}-\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf S}}_{22}}\right|
is
o_{p}(1)
.

For the first term
\left|\sum_{i=1}^{M}(f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}}))\right|
, from the arguments in Supplement B of
Jiang and Bai 2021
, we know that

\displaystyle\left|\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}}-\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}}\right|=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\rho_{i}}).

(6.24)

We recall that
\rho_{i}=\alpha_{k}
if
i\in J_{k}
. Then, for brevity, we denote
\beta_{i}=({\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}}-\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}}})/{\rho_{i}}
and
obtain that

\displaystyle f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}})=\int_{0}^{\beta_{i}\rho_{i}}f_{l}^{\prime}(t+\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}})dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\beta_{i}\rho_{i}f_{l}^{\prime}(\beta_{i}\rho_{i}s+\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}})ds=\beta_{i}\rho_{i}f_{l}^{\prime}(\rho_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{f_{l}^{\prime}(\rho_{i}(\beta_{i}s+\frac{\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}}}{\rho_{i}}))}{f_{l}^{\prime}(\rho_{i})}ds.

(6.25)

Since
\beta_{i}=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
,
\frac{\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}}}{\rho_{i}}
tends to 1,
then from Assumption
4
we obtain

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\dfrac{\left|f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}})\right|}{f_{l}^{\prime}(\rho_{i})\rho_{i}}=o_{p}(1).

(6.26)

Then we have
\frac{\left|\sum_{i=1}^{M}(f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}}))\right|}{\varsigma_{l}}\leq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left|f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}})\right|}{\varsigma_{l}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{\left|f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}})\right|}{f_{l}^{\prime}(\rho_{i})\rho_{i}/\sqrt{n}}=o_{p}(1).

Thus, it is concluded that the procedure of truncation does not affect the limiting distribution of LSS.

Therefore, in the following proofs, we can safely assume that
\left|x_{ij}\right|<\eta_{n}\sqrt{n}
.

Here,
a(c)=(1-\sqrt{c})^{2}
and
b(c)=(1+\sqrt{c})^{2}
. Since

\underline{m}\left(z\right)=-\frac{1-c}{z}+cm\left(z\right),

under
H_{1}
, we have

\underline{m}\left(z\right)=\frac{-(z+1-c)+\sqrt{(z-1-c)^{2}-4c}}{2z}.

As
z\rightarrow x\in

[a(c),b(c)]
, we obtain

\underline{m}\left(x\right)=\frac{-(x+1-c)+\sqrt{4c-(x-1-c)^{2}}i}{2x}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{a(c)}^{b(c)}f_{L}^{{}^{\prime}}\left(z\right)\Im\log\underline{m}(x+i\varepsilon)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{a(c)}^{b(c)}f_{L}^{{}^{\prime}}(x)\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{4c-(x-1-c)^{2}}}{-(x+1-c)}\right)dx

\displaystyle=\left[\left.\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{4c-(x-1-c)^{2}}}{-(x+1-c)}\right)f_{L}(x)\right|_{a(c)}^{b(c)}-\int_{a(c)}^{b(c)}f_{L}(x)d\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{4c-(x-1-c)^{2}}}{-(x+1-c)}\right)\right].

It is easy to verify that the first term is
0
, and we now focus on the second term,

\displaystyle\int_{a(c)}^{b(c)}f_{L}(x)d\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{4c-(x-1-c)^{2}}}{-(x+1-c)}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{a(c)}^{b(c)}\frac{\left(x-\log x-1\right)}{1+\frac{4c-(x-1-c)^{2}}{(x+1-c)^{2}}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{4c-(x-1-c)^{2}}+\frac{(x-1-c)(x+1-c)}{\sqrt{4c-(x-1-c)^{2}}}}{(x+1-c)^{2}}dx.

(8.35)

By substituting
x=1+c-2\sqrt{c}\cos(\theta)
, we obtain

\displaystyle(\ref{27})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(1+c-2\sqrt{c}\cos(\theta)-\log\left(1+c-2\sqrt{c}\cos(\theta)\right)-1\right)\frac{c-\sqrt{c}\cos(\theta)}{1+c-2\sqrt{c}\cos(\theta)}d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left[1-\frac{\log\left(1+c-2\sqrt{c}\cos(\theta)\right)+1}{1+c-2\sqrt{c}\cos(\theta)}\right]\left(c-\sqrt{c}\cos(\theta)\right)d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(c-\sqrt{c}\cos(\theta)\right)d\theta-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\log\left(1+c-2\sqrt{c}\cos(\theta)\right)}{1+c-2\sqrt{c}\cos(\theta)}\left(c-\sqrt{c}\cos(\theta)\right)d\theta-

(8.36)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{c-\sqrt{c}\cos(\theta)}{1+c-2\sqrt{c}\cos(\theta)}d\theta

It is easy to obtain that the first term of (
8.36
) is
\pi c
; then, we consider the second term. By substituting
\cos\theta=\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}
, we turn it into a contour integral on
\left|z\right|=1

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\log\left(1+c-2\sqrt{c}\cos(\theta)\right)}{1+c-2\sqrt{c}\cos(\theta)}\left(c-\sqrt{c}\cos\theta\right)d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\oint_{\left|z\right|=1}\log|1-\sqrt{c}z|^{2}\cdot\frac{c-\sqrt{c}\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}}{1+c-2\sqrt{c}\cdot\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}}\frac{dz}{iz}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4i}\oint_{\left|z\right|=1}\log|1-\sqrt{c}z|^{2}\cdot\frac{2cz-\sqrt{c}\left(z^{2}+1\right)}{(z-\sqrt{c})(-\sqrt{c}z+1)z}dz

When
c<1
,
0
and
\sqrt{c}
are poles, by using the residue theorem, the integral is
-\pi\log(1-c)
. The same argument also holds for the third term, and the integral is
0
after some calculation.

Therefore,

\frac{M}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f_{L}\left(z\right)\frac{\underline{m}^{\prime}(z)}{\underline{m}(z)}dz=-M(c+\log(1-c)),

and the result is still valid if
c
is replaces
c_{nM}
; therefore, formula (
7.29
) holds.

Now, we prove (
7.31
)
. Since
z=-\frac{1}{\underline{m}}+\frac{c}{1+\underline{m}}
, we have, for
c>1
,

\displaystyle\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f_{W}\left(z\right)\frac{\underline{m}^{\prime}(z)}{\underline{m}(z)}dz=\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}f_{W}\left(z\right)\frac{\underline{m}^{\prime}(z)}{\underline{m}(z)}dz+\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}f_{W}\left(z\right)\frac{\underline{m}^{\prime}(z)}{\underline{m}(z)}dz,

where
\mathcal{C}_{1}
is a contour that includes the interval
((1-\sqrt{c})^{2},(1+\sqrt{c})^{2})
, and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
is a contour that includes the origin. Using
\mathcal{C}_{\underline{m}}
to denote the contour of
\underline{m}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}f_{W}\left(z\right)\frac{\underline{m}^{\prime}(z)}{\underline{m}(z)}dz=\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{\underline{m}}}(-\frac{1}{\underline{m}}+\frac{c}{1+\underline{m}}-1)^{2}\frac{\underline{m}^{\prime}(z)}{\underline{m}(z)}\frac{dz}{d\underline{m}}d\underline{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{\underline{m}}}(-\frac{1+\underline{m}}{\underline{m}}+\frac{c}{1+\underline{m}})^{2}\frac{1}{\underline{m}}d\underline{m}=\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{\underline{m}}}(\frac{(1+\underline{m})^{2}}{\underline{m}^{3}}+\frac{c^{2}}{(1+\underline{m})^{2}\underline{m}}-\frac{2c}{\underline{m}^{2}})d\underline{m}

Since the
z
contour cannot enclose the origin, neither can the resulting
\underline{m}
contour. Thus, the only pole is
-1
, the residue is
-c^{2}
by residue theorem, and we obtain the integral as
-2\pi ic^{2}.

Then, we focus on the second integral
\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}f_{W}\left(z\right)\frac{\underline{m}^{\prime}(z)}{\underline{m}(z)}dz.
When
z=0,
we obtain
\underline{m}=\frac{1}{c-1}
; since
c>1,

\frac{1}{c-1}>0.
Both
\underline{m}=0
and
\underline{m}=-1
are not in the contour. Thus, the integrand
(\frac{(1+\underline{m})^{2}}{\underline{m}^{3}}+\frac{c^{2}}{(1+\underline{m})^{2}\underline{m}}-\frac{2c}{\underline{m}^{2}})
is analytic in the contour. The integral is
0
.
Therefore, when
c>1
,
\frac{M}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f_{W}\left(z\right)\frac{\underline{m}^{\prime}(z)}{\underline{m}(z)}dz=-Mc^{2}
. When
c<1,
, the contour integral
\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f_{W}\left(z\right)\frac{\underline{m}^{\prime}(z)}{\underline{m}(z)}dz
reduces to
\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}f_{W}\left(z\right)\frac{\underline{m}^{\prime}(z)}{\underline{m}(z)}dz
, and the result is also the same as above. When
c=1
, the result is still true by continuity in
c
. The results above are still valid if
c
is replaced by
c_{nM}.
Therefore, the proof of (
7.31
) is complete.
