Subfitness in distributive (semi)lattices
G. Bezhanishvili, J. Madden, M. A. Moshier, M. Tressl, J. Walters-Wayland

Abstract
. We investigate whether the set of subfit elements of a distributive semilattice is an ideal. This question was raised by the second author at the BLAST conference in 2022. We show that in general it has a negative solution, however if the semilattice is a lattice, then the solution is positive. This is somewhat unexpected since, as we show, a semilattice is subfit if and only if so is its distributive lattice envelope.

The property of subfitness was first studied by Wallman in

Based on this interpretation, the property was studied in a purely algebraic setting by Pierce in

It is reasonable to view subfitness as a property of semilattices, where we understand a semilattice to be a commutative, idempotent monoid
(A,\cdot,e)
. In practice, semilattices are often treated as posets, but there are two ways to do this. The operation

Or the operation

For example, in a bounded lattice

Our main theorem is stated and proved most naturally in the language of join-semilattices, because it is inspired by topology. On the other hand, our main example is naturally described in the language of meet-semilattices, because it is more natural to think about meet-semilattices of finite sets than about join-semilattices of co-finite sets.

2.
Subfit elements in distributive lattices are directed

For a distributive join-semilattice
A
, let
S
be the set of join-subfit elements of
A
. It is straightforward to see that
S
is a downset of
A
. In this paper we are concerned with the question of whether
S
is an ideal. In this section we show that this is indeed the case provided
A
is a lattice. Our proof below is constructive. In
Section

5
we provide a topological explanation of this result, which does use the Prime Ideal Theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

If
A
is a bounded distributive lattice, then the set
S
of join-subfit elements of
A
is an ideal of
A
.

Proof.

Let
a,b\in S
. To see that
a\vee b\in S
, it is sufficient to assume that
a\vee b=1
and prove that
A
is join-subfit.
Suppose
s,t\in A
with
t\nleq s
. We seek an element
z\in A
such that
s\vee z<1
and
t\vee z=1
.
If
a\wedge t\leq a\wedge s
and
b\wedge t\leq b\wedge s
, then by distributivity,
(a\vee b)\wedge t\leq(a\vee b)\wedge s
, so
t\leq s
, a contradiction. Therefore,
we may assume without loss of generality that
b\wedge t\not\leq b\wedge s
. Since
{\downarrow}b
is join-subfit, there is an element
y\leq b
such that

(b\wedge s)\vee y<b\mbox{ and }(b\wedge t)\vee y=b.

The latter, by distributivity, implies that
b\leq t\vee y
. Moreover, if
b\leq s\vee y
, then

(b\wedge s)\vee y=(b\vee y)\wedge(s\vee y)\geq b,

contradicting the former. Thus, we have

(1)

b\nleq s\vee y\;\text{ and }\;b\leq t\vee y.

If
s\vee y\vee a<1
, let
z=y\vee a
and we are done. On the other hand, if
s\vee y\vee a=1
, then

(2)

(b\wedge a)\vee(b\wedge s)\vee y=(b\wedge(a\vee s))\vee y=(b\vee y)\wedge(a\vee s\vee y)=b.

Hence, setting
w=(b\wedge s)\vee y
and using (
2
), we have:

(3)

(b\wedge s)\vee w=(b\wedge s)\vee y<b\text{ and }(a\wedge b)\vee w=(a\wedge b)\vee(b\wedge s)\vee y=b.

From the first part of (
3
),
b\nleq s\vee w
. From the second part of (
3
),
b\leq a\vee w
.
By (
1
),

(4)

t\vee w=t\vee(b\wedge s)\vee y=(t\vee b\vee y)\wedge(t\vee s\vee y)\geq b.

Therefore,
b\leq(a\vee w)\wedge(t\vee w)=(a\wedge t)\vee w
.
Thus,
(a\wedge t)\vee w\nleq s\vee w
, so
a\wedge t\nleq s\vee w
, and hence
a\wedge t\nleq a\wedge(s\vee w)
. By join-subfitness of
{\downarrow}a
, there is
x\leq a
such that
(a\wedge t)\vee x=a
and
(a\wedge(s\vee w))\vee x<a
, i.e.,
(a\wedge s)\vee(a\wedge w)\vee x<a
.
If
a\leq s\vee w\vee x
, then

a\leq(a\wedge s)\vee(a\wedge w)\vee(a\wedge x)=(a\wedge s)\vee(a\wedge w)\vee x,

a contradiction. So
a\not\leq s\vee w\vee x
, and hence

s\vee w\vee x<1
. Also,
b\leq t\vee w
by (
4
) and
a\leq t\vee x
(because
a=(a\wedge t)\vee x
), so
t\vee w\vee x\geq a\vee b=1
.
Let
z=w\vee x
, and we are done.
∎
