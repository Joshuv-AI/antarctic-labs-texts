Efficient and flexible causal mediation with time-varying mediators, treatments, and confounders
Iván Díaz, Nicholas Williams, Kara E. Rudolph

Abstract
Interventional effects have been proposed as a solution to the unidentifiability of natural (in)direct effects under mediator-outcome confounders affected by the exposure. Such confounders are an intrinsic characteristic of studies with time-varying exposures and mediators, yet the generalization of the interventional effect framework to the time-varying case has received little attention in the literature. We present an identification result for interventional effects in a general longitudinal data structure that allows flexibility in the specification of treatment-outcome, treatment-mediator, and mediator-outcome relationships. Identification is achieved under the standard no-unmeasured-confounders and positivity assumptions. We also present a theoretical and computational study of the properties of the identifying functional based on the efficient influence function (EIF). We use the EIF to propose a sequential regression estimation algorithm that yields doubly robust, n \sqrt{n} -consistent, asymptotically Gaussian, and efficient estimators under slow convergence rates for the regression algorithms used. This allows the use of flexible machine learning for regression while permitting uncertainty quantification through confidence intervals and p-values. A free and open source R package implementing our proposed estimators is made available on GitHub. We apply the proposed estimator to an application from a comparative effectiveness trial of two medications for opioid use disorder. In the application, we estimate the extent to which differences between the two treatments’ on subsequent risk of opioid use is mediated by craving symptoms.

Introduction

Mediation analyses have a long history in the statistical sciences
and have more recently been proposed in the causal inference literature
as a way of understanding the mechanisms through which effects operate

(
VanderWeele 2009
)
. For example, recent mediation
analyses have helped to uncover the types of immune response that
COVID-19 vaccines trigger in order to prevent disease

(
Gilbert et al. 2021
)
.

Multiple methods have been developed for mediation analysis in the
setting of a mediator measured at a single time point, using a
counterfactual framework.
Robins and Greenland 1992
and
Pearl 2001

defined and established conditions for identifiability for so-called

natural direct effect
and
natural indirect effects
,
which measure the difference in counterfactual outcomes under certain
simultaneous interventions on the treatment and mediator, and
decompose the total average treatment effect into direct and indirect
effects. Though the definition of the natural (in)direct effects is
scientifically interesting, their identification requires a so-called

cross-world
counterfactual independence assumption, which
generally precludes the presence of (measured or unmeasured)
intermediate confounders of the mediator-outcome relation affected by
treatment
(
Andrews and Didelez 2020
)
. This restriction limits the
applicability of natural direct and indirect effects in practice, as
intermediate confounders are expected to be present in many
applications, for example when the effect of an intervention operates
through adherence
(
Rudolph et al. 2021b
)
.

Several methods have been proposed to do away with the cross-world
independence assumption and/or the assumption of no intermediate
confounders. For example,

Robins and Richardson 2010
;
Tchetgen and Phiri 2014
;
Miles et al. 2015

present partial identification methods,
Robins and Richardson 2010

introduced so-called
separable effects
, and

Díaz and Hejazi 2020
;
Hejazi et al. 2020
propose the definition of the effects in terms
of stochastic interventions on the exposure. In this paper we focus on
the approach of
Petersen et al. 2006
;
van der Laan and Petersen 2008
;
VanderWeele et al. 2014
and
Vansteelandt and Daniel 2017
,
who propose to define mediation effects in terms of contrasts between
counterfactuals in hypothetical worlds in which the treatment is set
to some value deterministically, whereas the mediator is drawn
stochastically from its counterfactual distribution under
interventions on treatment. Efficient non-parametric estimators that
leverage machine learning to alleviate model misspecification bias
have been recently proposed for these parameters

(
Díaz et al. 2021a
)
. These effects have been called

interventional
effects
(
VanderWeele and Tchetgen 2017
)
, a name we adopt in this paper.

While the definition of interventional effects has allowed researchers
to make progress in settings with intermediate confounding, several
limitations remain, especially in the setting of variables measured at
multiple points in time. In a seminal paper in longitudinal mediation
analysis,
VanderWeele and Tchetgen 2017
present a non-parametric
identification formula for interventional effects in the case of
mediators and treatments measured longitudinally, assuming that the
time-dependent covariates are measured either before or after the
mediator, but not both. The authors propose estimation methods that
rely on the unlikely ability to correctly specify parametric models
for the distribution of the unobservable counterfactual outcomes.

Zheng and van der Laan 2017
propose similar interventional effects
where the mediator is drawn from its counterfactual distribution
conditional on all the past, and develop non-parametric efficient
estimators that rely on data-adaptive regression to alleviate model
misspecification bias. However, the “direct effect” defined in

Zheng and van der Laan 2017
does not capture the pathway from
treatment through intermediate confounder to outcome,
and is thus not a direct effect in the
sense that we are interested in this paper. In a similar vein,

Vansteelandt et al. 2019
develop methods for survival analysis
and treatment at a single time point. They define the indirect effect
as an effect that measures only paths where the treatment directly
affects the mediator (and not paths where there is an indirect effect
of the treatment on the mediator), and show that such effects are
identified even in the presence of longitudinal confounding of the
mediator-outcome relation affected by treatment.

Bind et al. 2016
propose effects for longitudinal treatments and
mediators, but does not allow for intermediate confounders and
requires the cross-world counterfactual
assumption.
Mittinty and Vansteelandt 2020
propose marginal
structural models for longitudinally measured mediators under
treatment at a single time point and no loss-to-follow-up, and propose
estimators that are based on parametric marginal structural models on the
counterfactual outcomes.

In this paper we develop a general longitudinal causal mediation approach that
fills several gaps from the above literature. The method we propose
satisfies the following: (i) the direct effect is defined
in terms of the effects operating through all the pathways that do not
include the mediator at any time point, (ii) allow for longitudinally
measured mediators and treatments, with confounders possibly measured
before and after treatment, (iii) allow for the use of data-adaptive
regression to alleviate concerns of model misspecification bias, and
(iv) allow the construction of efficient estimators and computation of
valid standard errors and confidence intervals, even under the use of
data-adaptive regression. A limitation of prior work remains: our proposed
methods can only handle categorical mediators, and the computational
complexity increases with the number of categories.

The remainder of
the paper is organized as follows. In §
2
we
introduce the parameters of interest as well as the identification
result, in §
3
we discuss efficiency theory for the
interventional mediation functional, presenting estimating equations
and the efficiency bound in the non-parametric model, in
§

we discuss the proposed estimator as well as its
asymptotic properties, and finally in §

we present
the results of an illustrative study on estimating the longitudinal
effect of initiating treatment for opioid use disorder (OUD) with extended-release naltrexone (XR-NTX) vs. buprenorphine-naloxone (BUP-NX) on risk of illicit opioid use during the fourth week of treatment
that operates through the mediator of craving symptoms, using
symptoms of depression
and withdrawal as time-varying covariates. We include weekly measures for each of the first 4 weeks of treatment.

. Specifically, our estimators will involve
finding von-Mises-type approximations for the parameters

\mathsf{Q}_{L,t}(\bar{m})
,
\mathsf{Q}_{Z,t}(\bar{m})
, and
\mathsf{Q}_{M,t}(\bar{m})
,
which can be intuitively understood as first order expansions with
second-order error remainder terms. Because the errors in the
expansion are second-order, this will mean that the resulting
estimator of
\hat{\theta}
will be consistent and asymptotically normal
at rate
n^{1/2}
as long as the second-order error terms converge to
zero at rate
n^{1/2}
. This convergence rate would be satisfied, for
example, if the all regression functions used for estimation converge
at rate
n^{1/4}
. We will elaborate on this discussion in
§
4
when we present the asymptotic normality theorems
for the proposed estimators.

3
Efficiency theory

Define the following random variables

\mathsf{G}_{A,t}(H_{A,t})^{\prime}=\frac{\mathds{1}\{A_{t}=a_{t}^{\prime}\}}{\mathsf{g}_{t}(a_{t}^{\prime}\mid H_{A,t})},\,\,\mathsf{G}_{A,t}^{\star}(H_{A,t})=\frac{\mathds{1}\{A_{t}=a_{t}^{\star}\}}{\mathsf{g}_{t}(a_{t}^{\star}\mid H_{A,t})},\,\,\,\mathsf{G}_{M,t}(H_{M,t},m_{t})=\frac{\mathds{1}\{M_{t}=m_{t}\}}{\mathsf{g}_{M,t}(M_{t}\mid H_{M,t})},

where, as before, we will sometimes omit the
dependence on
H_{A,t}
,
H_{M,t}
, and
m_{t}
. For
t=0,\ldots,\tau

and for
k=1,\ldots,\tau
, and for a nuisance parameter

\eta=\{\mathsf{G}_{A,t}^{\prime},\mathsf{G}_{A,t}^{\star},\mathsf{G}_{M,t},\mathsf{Q}_{Z,t},\mathsf{Q}_{M,t},\mathsf{Q}_{L,t}:t=1,\ldots,\tau\}
, define

\mathsf{K}_{l,u}^{\prime}=\prod_{r=l}^{u}\mathsf{G}_{A,r}^{\prime},\quad\mathsf{K}_{l,u}^{\star}=\prod_{r=l}^{u}\mathsf{G}_{A,r}^{\star},\quad\mathsf{H}_{l,u}=\prod_{r=l}^{u}\mathsf{G}_{M,r}

and

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{L,t}(\underaccent{\bar}{X}_{t},\underaccent{\bar}{m}_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=t}^{\tau}\mathsf{K}_{t+1,s}^{\prime}\mathsf{H}_{t,s}\left\{\mathsf{Q}_{Z,s+1}-\mathsf{Q}_{L,s}\right\}+\sum_{s=t+1}^{\tau}\mathsf{K}_{t+1,s}^{\prime}\mathsf{H}_{t,s-1}\left\{\mathsf{Q}_{L,s}-\mathsf{Q}_{Z,s}\right\}+\mathsf{Q}_{L,t}.

(5)

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{Z,t}(\underaccent{\bar}{X}_{t},\underaccent{\bar}{m}_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=t}^{\tau}\mathsf{K}_{t,s}^{\prime}\mathsf{H}_{t,s}\left\{\mathsf{Q}_{Z,s+1}-\mathsf{Q}_{L,s}\right\}+\sum_{s=t}^{\tau}\mathsf{K}_{t,s}^{\prime}\mathsf{H}_{t,s-1}\left\{\mathsf{Q}_{L,s}-\mathsf{Q}_{Z,s}\right\}+\mathsf{Q}_{Z,t}

(6)

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{M,t}(\underaccent{\bar}{X}_{t},\underaccent{\bar}{m}_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=t}^{\tau}\mathsf{K}_{t,s}^{\star}\left(\prod_{k=t}^{s-1}\mathds{1}(M_{k}=m_{k})\right)\left\{\mathds{1}(M_{s}=m_{s})\mathsf{Q}_{M,s+1}-\mathsf{Q}_{M,s}\right\}+\mathsf{Q}_{M,t}.

(7)

Whenever necessary, we make explicit the
dependence of these functions on
\eta
using notation such as

\mathsf{D}_{Z,t}(\eta)
or
\mathsf{D}_{Z,t}(\underaccent{\bar}{X}_{t},\underaccent{\bar}{m}_{t};\eta)
.

Lemma 1

(von-Mises-type approximation for
\mathsf{Q}_{Z,t}
,

\mathsf{Q}_{L,t}
, and
\mathsf{Q}_{M,t}
)
.

Let
\tilde{\eta}
denote an arbitrary value of
\eta
. For second
order terms
\mathsf{R}_{L,t}(\eta,\tilde{\eta})
,

\mathsf{R}_{Z,t}(\eta,\tilde{\eta})
, and
\mathsf{R}_{M,t}(\eta,\tilde{\eta})

we have the following first order expansions:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{L,t}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}[\mathsf{D}_{Z,t+1}(\tilde{\eta})\mid M_{t}=m_{t},H_{M,t}]+\mathsf{R}_{L,t}(\eta,\tilde{\eta})

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{Z,t}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}[\mathsf{D}_{L,t}(\tilde{\eta})\mid A_{t}=a_{t}^{\prime},H_{A,t}]+\mathsf{R}_{Z,t}(\eta,\tilde{\eta}),

where we let
\mathsf{D}_{Z,\tau+1}(\tilde{\eta})=Y
. For
H_{A,t}
such that

\bar{M}_{t-1}=\bar{m}_{t-1}
, we also have

\mathsf{Q}_{M,t}=\mathsf{E}[\mathds{1}\{M_{t}=m_{t}\}\mathsf{D}_{M,t+1}(\tilde{\eta})\mid A_{t}=a_{t}^{\star},H_{A,t}]+\mathsf{R}_{M,t}(\eta,\tilde{\eta}),

where we let
\mathsf{D}_{M,\tau+1}(\tilde{\eta})=1
.

The terms
\mathsf{R}_{L,t}(\eta,\tilde{\eta})
,

\mathsf{R}_{Z,t}(\eta,\tilde{\eta})
, and
\mathsf{R}_{M,t}(\eta,\tilde{\eta})

are second-order error terms involving expectations of products of
errors such as

(\tilde{\mathsf{G}}_{M,s}-\mathsf{G}_{M,s})(\tilde{\mathsf{Q}}_{L,s}-\mathsf{Q}_{L,s})
,

(\tilde{\mathsf{G}}_{A,s}^{\prime}-\mathsf{G}_{A,s}^{\prime})(\tilde{\mathsf{Q}}_{Z,s}-\mathsf{Q}_{Z,s})
, and

(\tilde{\mathsf{G}}_{A,s}^{\star}-\mathsf{G}_{A,s}^{\star})(\tilde{\mathsf{Q}}_{M,s}-\mathsf{Q}_{M,s})
,
and their explicit form is given in the supplementary materials. This
lemma and specifically the second-order form of these remainder terms
has important implications in terms of estimation. Specifically, this
lemma says that for any value
\tilde{\eta}
, which could represent an
inconsistent estimator, regressing
\mathsf{D}_{Z,t+1}(\tilde{\eta})
on

H_{M,t}
among units with
M_{t}=m_{t}
yields a consistent estimator of

\mathsf{Q}_{L,t}
, as long as
\mathsf{R}_{L,t}(\eta,\tilde{\eta})
is
small. Because
\mathsf{R}_{L,t}(\eta,\tilde{\eta})
is a sum of products of
errors, it may be reasonable to assume that this term is small for
data-adaptive regression estimators of
\eta
. Specifically, in
§
4
, consistency and asymptotic normality of the
estimators will require that
\mathsf{R}_{L,t}(\eta,\hat{\eta})
converges
to zero in probability at rate
n^{-1/2}
or faster. The second-order
term structure of this remainder term means that this fast convergence
rate can be achieved under slower convergence rates for each of the
components of
\hat{\eta}
. For example, it will be achievable if all
the components of
\hat{\eta}
converge in probability to their correct
limits at rate
n^{-1/4}
. This kind of rate is achievable by several
data-adaptive regression methods, such
\ell_{1}
regularization

(
Bickel et al. 2009
)
, regression trees

(
Wager and Walther 2015
)
, neural networks
(
Chen and White 1999
)
,
or the highly adaptive lasso
(
Benkeser and van der Laan 2016
)
. Analogous
considerations apply to estimation of
\mathsf{Q}_{Z,t}
and
\mathsf{Q}_{M,t}
.

In addition, an application of the Delta method along with
Lemma
1
yields the following efficient influence function
(EIF) for estimation of
\theta
in the non-parametric model (see the
supplementary materials for a proof):

Theorem 2

(Efficient influence function for

\theta
)
.

The EIF for
\theta
in the non-parametric model is given by

\mathsf{S}(X,\eta)=\sum_{\bar{m}\in\bar{\mathcal{M}}}\left[\{\mathsf{D}_{Z,1}(X,\bar{m};\eta)-\varphi(\bar{m})\}\lambda(\bar{m})+\{\mathsf{D}_{M,1}(X,\bar{m};\eta)-\lambda(\bar{m})\}\varphi(\bar{m})\right].

This implies that the non-parametric efficiency bound for estimation
of
\theta
is
\var[\mathsf{S}(X,\eta)]
, and that an efficient estimator of

\theta
will satisfy

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}-\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{S}(X_{i},\eta)+o_{\mathsf{P}}(1),

licensing the construction of Wald-type confidence intervals and
hypothesis tests based on the central limit theorem. In the following
section we will describe an algorithm to construct such an estimator.

4
Efficient estimation using sequential doubly robust
regression

For any natural number
n
we denote

[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Furthermore, let
\mathcal{D}=\{X_{i}:i\in[n]\}

denote the observed dataset, and let

\mathcal{M}_{t}=\{m_{j}:j\in[J_{t}]\}
denote the set of unique values
that the mediator
M_{t}
takes in the sample
\mathcal{D}
at time point

t
, where
J_{t}\leq n
is the number of unique values.

Estimating

\mathsf{Q}_{L,0}(\bar{m})
for each
\bar{m}
would in principle require
fitting
\prod_{k=1}^{\tau}J_{k}
sets of
\tau
sequential regressions
as in formula (
3
), one set of sequential regressions for
each one of the possible values of
\bar{m}
. This can be
computationally prohibitive. For example, estimating

\mathsf{Q}_{L,0}(\bar{m})
in a study with
\tau=5
and a mediator taking on
three different values involves
3^{5}=243
sets of five sequential
regressions, each in a dataset of size
n
. To alleviate computational
complexity, we propose to fit regressions in pooled datasets
constructed sequentially from
t=\tau
to
t=1
, where at time point
t
, each
observation in the dataset at time point
t+1
is repeated
J_{t}

times. Specifically, consider the original dataset

\mathcal{D}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
. Regressions at time point
\tau
are
constructed in an augmented dataset defined as

\mathcal{D}_{\tau}^{+}=\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{M}_{\tau}=\{(X_{1},m_{1}),\ldots,(X_{1},m_{J_{\tau}}),\ldots,(X_{\tau},m_{1}),\ldots,(X_{\tau},m_{J_{\tau}})\},

where
\times
denotes Cartesian product. Regressions at time point

\tau-1
are constructed in a dataset defined as

\mathcal{D}_{\tau-1}^{+}=\mathcal{D}_{\tau}^{+}\times\mathcal{M}_{\tau-1}
, and
so on. Thus, an observation in dataset
{\cal D}_{t}^{+}
is a duple

(X_{i},\underaccent{\bar}{m}_{t})
.

Under this data pooling approach estimating
\mathsf{Q}_{L,0}
in a study with

\tau=5
and a mediator taking on three different values involves five
sequential regressions, each in datasets of size
3n
,
3^{2}n
,
3^{3}n
,

3^{4}n
,
3^{5}n
, respectively, where the predictor set decreases in
size as the dataset increases in size. The specific details of this
sequential regression algorithm using pooled datasets are described in
Algorithm
2
. An R package implementing the algorithm is
available at
https://github.com/nt-williams/lcm
. In brief, the
algorithm proceeds sequentially from
t=\tau,\ldots,1
performing
regression of pseudo-outcomes given by
\mathsf{D}_{Z,t+1}(\hat{\eta})
,

\mathsf{D}_{L,t}(\hat{\eta})
, and
\mathds{1}\{M_{t}=m_{t}\}\mathsf{D}_{M,t+1}(\eta)
, where
these regressions are cross-fitted and based on datasets

\mathcal{D}_{t}^{+}
that pool over values of
m_{t}
as defined above.

In addition to using the above pooled datasets to construct sequential
regressions, we will use cross-fitting, which helps to avoid imposing
entropy conditions on the initial estimators

(
Bickel 1982
;
Klaassen 1987
;
Zheng and van der Laan 2011
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
)
, while yielding estimators that are
CAN. This allows us to use flexible regression algorithms from the
statistical and machine learning literature, which may be better at
capturing the true functional form of the regression functions and
thus making the error terms
\mathsf{R}_{L,0}(\hat{\eta},\eta)
,

\mathsf{R}_{Z,0}(\hat{\eta},\eta)
, and
\mathsf{R}_{M,0}(\hat{\eta},\eta)

small. Let
{\cal P}_{1},\ldots,{\cal P}_{V}
denote a random partition
of the data set
\mathcal{D}
into
V
prediction sets of approximately
the same size. That is,
{\cal P}_{v}\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
;

\bigcup_{j=1}^{J}{\cal P}_{v}={\cal D}
; and

{\cal P}_{v}\cap{\cal P}_{v^{\prime}}=\emptyset
. In addition, for each
v
,
the associated training sample is given by

{\cal T}_{v}={\cal D}\setminus{\cal P}_{v}
. The cross-fitting
algorithm is described in Algorithm
1
.

Our proposed estimation algorithm satisfies sequential double
robustness in the sense of Lemma
2
below
(
Luedtke et al. 2017
;
Rotnitzky et al. 2017
;
Díaz et al. 2021b
)
. To illustrate sequential double robustness,
consider an alternative estimation strategy in which the parameters

\mathsf{Q}_{L,t}
,
\mathsf{Q}_{Z,t}
, and
\mathsf{Q}_{M,t}
are estimated directly (i.e.,
based on (
3
) and (
4
)) using flexible regression techniques,
and the estimators of
\varphi(\bar{m})
and
\lambda(\bar{m})
are
constructed by taking the empirical mean of

\mathsf{D}_{L,1}(X,\bar{m};\hat{\eta})
and
\mathsf{D}_{M,1}(X,\bar{m};\hat{\eta})
. Note,
however, that the functions
\mathsf{Q}_{L,t}
,
\mathsf{Q}_{Z,t}
, and
\mathsf{Q}_{M,t}
are
functions of the sequences
\{\mathsf{Q}_{L,t+1},\ldots,\mathsf{Q}_{L,\tau}\}
,

\{\mathsf{Q}_{Z,t+1},\ldots,\mathsf{Q}_{Z,\tau}\}
, and

\{\mathsf{Q}_{M,t+1},\ldots,\mathsf{Q}_{M,\tau}\}
. It therefore appears that
consistent estimation of these parameters at time
t
requires
consistent estimation of these future sequences.

Sequential doubly robust estimators decouple estimation of conditional
expectations at time
t
from consistent estimation of sequences of
estimators at time
t+1,\ldots,\tau

robustness. To introduce sequential double robustness, define the data
dependent parameters

\displaystyle\check{\mathsf{Q}}_{L,t}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}[\mathsf{D}_{Z,t+1}(\hat{\eta})\mid M_{t}=m_{t},H_{M,t}]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}[\mathds{1}\{M_{t}=m_{t}\}\mathsf{D}_{M,t+1}(\hat{\eta})\mid A_{t}=a_{t}^{\star},H_{A,t}],

where the expectation is with respect to the distribution of

i.e., the estimator

Illustrative application

We applied our proposed estimators to a longitudinal mediation
question from a comparative effectiveness trial of extended-release
naltrexone (XR-NTX) vs. buprenorphine-naloxone (BUP-NX) for the
treatment of opioid use disorder (OUD)
(
Lee et al. 2018
)
.
Specifically, we were interested in estimating the extent to which
differences in use of illicit opioids during the first month of
treatment between the two medications was due to mediation by
self-reported craving of opioids, among those completing the
detoxification requirement and initiating treatment. This involved
estimating the interventional direct effect of being treated with
XR-NTX vs. BUP-NX on risk of using illicit opioids during the first
four weeks of treatment, not operating through differences in craving
of opioids; and the interventional indirect effect of being treated
with XR-NTX vs. BUP-NX on risk of using illicit opioids during the
first four weeks of treatment that did operate through differences in
craving.

Patients report less opioid use when on XR-NTX vs. BUP-NX (as well as
methadone)
(
Greiner et al. 2021
;
Solli et al. 2018
)
, but
the reasons underlying this difference are not well understood. In
this study, patients were randomized to receive XR-NTX or BUP-NX. At time of randomization, a large number (over 30) baseline covariates, denoted
L_{1}
, (listed in the supplemental
materials) were measured. Although patient assignment to XR-NTX vs. BUP-NX was
randomized, we are estimating effects only among those who initiated
treatment. Treatment initiation is not randomized, and likely depends on
patient characteristics. Initiation is also more difficult for those assigned to XR-NTX, because it requires complete detoxification
(
Lee et al. 2018
)
. Our adjustment for an extensive
set of possibly confounding variables (
L_{1}
) helps address lack of
randomization in the exposure groups. We use
A_{1}=1
to denote initiation with XR-NTX and

A_{1}=0
to denote initiation with BUP-NX. The outcome of this study is opioid use as detected by weekly urine drug screen or Timeline Followback interview
(
Sobell and Sobell 1995
)
. We use
L_{t}
to denote
opioid use measured at week
t+1
for
t\in\{2,3,4\}
, which detects use in the several days prior (via urine drug screen) to week prior (via interview), and where
t
represents the number of weeks since randomization. We hypothesized that
if XR-NTX reduces craving more than BUP-NX, that this could provide a
partial explanation of the lower opioid use while on XR-NTX. We use

M_{t}
to denote craving during week
t+1
since randomization, for
t\in\{1,2,3\}
. There
may also be differences in depressive symptoms

(
Rudolph et al. 2021a
)
and withdrawal symptoms
(
SAMHSA 2021
)

between the two medications, so we incorporated measures of each

(
Hamilton 1960
;
Cooper et al. 2016
, Hamilton Depression Scale, Subjective Opioid Withdrawal Scale;
see)
, as time-varying
confounders. We use
Z_{t}
to denote these confounders measured during week
t+1
for
t\in\{1,2,3\}
.

In addition, patients could drop
out of treatment or otherwise be lost to follow-up starting in week 3 after randomization. Importantly, the outcomes
L_{t}
are always observed, as it is assumed that a patient who has missing opioid use data (most likely due to a missed visit) would have been positive for opioid use
(
Hser et al. 2016
;
Hser et al. 2017
;
Weiss et al. 2011
;
Weiss et al. 2015
)
. For
t\in\{2,3\}
, we use
A_{t}=1
to denote
that a patient’s
Z_{t}
and
M_{t}
have been measured, and we let
A_{t}=0
otherwise. Thus, our intervention variable is
given by a combination of treatment and censoring,

\bar{A}=\{A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}\}
. In summary, we can write our
observed data for this example as

O=(L_{1},A_{1},Z_{1},M_{1},L_{2},A_{2},Z_{2},M_{2},L_{3},A_{3},Z_{3},M_{3},L_{4}).

We estimated the interventional direct effect of initiating treatment
with XR-NTX vs. BUP-NX on illicit opioid use during week 4 of
treatment, not operating through craving, had those who dropped out
not dropped out. That is, for
\bar{a}^{\prime}=(1,1,1)
and

\bar{a}^{\star}=(0,1,1)
, we estimated

\mathsf{E}(Y(\bar{a}^{\prime},\bar{G}(\bar{a}^{\star}))-Y(\bar{a}^{\star},\bar{G}(\bar{a}^{\star}))
. We also estimated the interventional indirect effect of
initiating treatment with XR-NTX vs. BUP-NX on illicit opioid use
during week 4 of treatment operating through craving, had those who
dropped out not dropped out. That is, we estimated

\mathsf{E}(Y(\bar{a}^{\prime},\bar{G}(\bar{a}^{\prime})-Y(\bar{a}^{\prime},\bar{G}(\bar{a}^{\star}))
. We used an ensemble of machine learning algorithms in
fitting the nuisance parameters. The ensemble included an
intercept-only model, lasso, multiple additive regression splines, and
extreme gradient boosting. The weights in the ensemble were chosen
using Super Learning
(
van der Laan et al. 2007
)
. We used
cross-fitting with 5 folds.

For the interventional direct effect, we estimated that initiating
treatment with XR-NTX vs. BUP-NX, not operating through craving, would
reduce risk of using illicit opioids during week 4 of treatment by 8.8
percentage points (risk difference: -0.088, 95% CI: -0.129,
-0.048). For the interventional indirect effect, we estimated that
initiating treatment with XR-NTX vs. BUP-NX, operating through
craving, would not meaningfully decrease risk of using illicit opioids
during week 4 of treatment (risk difference: -0.004, 95% CI: -0.019,
0.011). Thus, we conclude that reductions in risk of illicit opioid use during treatment with XR-NTX vs. BUP-NX is not due to differences that operate
through the treatments’ effects on craving.

6
Discussion

Our approach generalizes interventional causal effects to allow for
high-dimensional time-dependent variables measured post- and
pre-treatment. We present an estimation algorithm that leverages
machine learning to alleviate misspecification bias while retaining
statistical properties such as
\sqrt{n}
-consistency, non-parametric
efficiency, and asymptotic normality.

While this approach allows great flexibility in the data structure and
estimation method, some limitations remain. We assume that the
mediator is categorical, and computational tractability of our
proposed estimator requires that it takes values on a small set. This
limitation seems fundamental and hard to overcome within an
interventional effect framework, as all estimators will require
estimation of the density of the counterfactual variable

\bar{M}(\bar{a})
. We know of no method that can do this
non-parametrically in the case of a continuous or high-dimensional
variable
\bar{M}
, although recent approaches on estimation of
counterfactual densities in single time-point settings are promising

(
Kennedy et al. 2021
)
.

In addition, interventional effects have some limitations. First, they
do not decompose the average treatment effect

E[Y(\bar{a}^{\prime})-Y(\bar{a}^{\star})]
. Second, the interventional indirect
effect can be non-zero, even when there is no indirect effect for any
individual in the population
(
Miles 2021
)
. Solving this
limitation would require a different framework for mediation analysis.
