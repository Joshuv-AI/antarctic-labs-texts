A Note on the Zeros of Jensen Polynomials Thanks: The first author was supported by the SNU Mathematical Sciences Division for Creative Human Resources Development.
Young-One Kim and Jungseob Lee

Abstract
A recent result of Griffin, Ono, Rolen and Zagier on Jensen polynomials related with the Riemann zeta function is improved.



Classification:
30C15, 30D15

This note is concerned with the zeros of entire functions. Let
f

be an entire function. We denote the zero set of
f
by
Z(f)
,
that is,
Z(f)=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:f(z)=0\}
; the order

\rho=\rho(f)
of
f
is defined by

\rho=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\log M(r;f)}{\log r},

where
M(r;f)=\max_{|z|=r}|f(z)|
; and
f
is said to be
real

if
f^{(n)}(0)\in\mathbb{R}
for all
n
.

Throughout this note
f
denotes a real entire function of order

\rho<2
, and in the case where
f
is even
f_{0}
denotes the
function such that
f(z)=f_{0}(z^{2})
for all
z
. In this case,
f_{0}

is a real entire function of order
\rho/2<1
,
Z(f_{0})=\{z^{2}:z\in Z(f)\}
, and we have

f_{0}^{(k)}(0)=\frac{k!}{(2k)!}f^{(2k)}(0)\qquad(k=0,1,2,\dots).

Since
f
is a real entire function, we have
f(z)=0
if and only if

f(\bar{z})=0
; and since
f
is of order less than
2
, it can be
represented in the form

f(z)=cz^{m}e^{bz}\prod_{j}\left(1-\frac{z}{a_{j}}\right)e^{z/a_{j}},

where
c,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
m
is a non-negative integer and
\sum|a_{j}|^{-2}<\infty
. As a consequence, there are real polynomials

P_{1},P_{2},\dots
such that
Z(P_{k})\subset Z(f)\cup\mathbb{R}

for all
k
, and
P_{k}\to f
uniformly on compact sets in the complex
plane. In particular, if
Z(f)\subset\mathbb{R}
then
Z(f^{(n)})\subset\mathbb{R}
for every
n
unless
f^{(n)}
is
identically equal to zero. The Riemann Xi-function is given by

\Xi(iz)=\frac{1}{2}\left(z^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right)\pi^{-\frac{z}{2}-\frac{1}{4}}\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right)\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+z\right).

It is an even real entire function of order
1
, its zero set is
contained in the infinite strip
\mathbb{S}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|\operatorname{Im}z|<1/2\}
, and the Riemann hypothesis is the
conjecture that
Z(\Xi)\subset\mathbb{R}
, which is equivalent to
that
Z(\Xi_{0})\subset[0,\infty)
.

A real polynomial is said to be
hyperbolic
if it has real
zeros only. The Jensen polynomials
J(f;1),J(f;2),\dots
of
f

are defined by

J(f;d)(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\binom{d}{k}f^{(k)}(0)z^{k}\qquad(d=1,2,\dots).

It is known
[
3
,
8
]
that
Z(f)\subset\mathbb{R}
if and only if
all the Jensen polynomials of
f
are hyperbolic. Let
d
be a
positive integer. If there is a non-negative integer
N
such that

J(f^{(n)};d)
is hyperbolic for every
n\geq N
, we denote the
smallest such integer by
N(f;d)
, otherwise we write

N(f;d)=\infty
. One can easily check that the Riemann hypothesis
holds if and only if
N(\Xi_{0};d)=0
for all
d
. Griffin et al.

[
2
]
showed that
N(\Xi_{0};d)<\infty
for all
d
by establishing
an asymptotic formula for the sequence
\langle J(\Xi_{0}^{(n)};d)\rangle_{n=1}^{\infty}
which relates (for large
n
) the zeros of

J(\Xi_{0}^{(n)};d)
with those of the
d
-th Hermite polynomial.
We improve the result by applying some general theorems on real
entire functions of order less than
2
with restricted zeros.
However it should be remarked that our proof gives no information
about the Jensen polynomials considered, except that they are
hyperbolic.

Theorem 1

Suppose that
f
is a transcendental real
entire function of order
\rho<2
and
Z(f)\subset\mathbb{S}
. Then
for every
c>\rho
we have
N(f;d)=O(d^{c/2})
as
d\to\infty
. If

f
is even, we also have
N(f_{0};d)=O(d^{c/2})
as
d\to\infty
for
every
c>\rho
.

Since
\Xi
is of order
1
, it follows that
N(\Xi;d)=O(d^{c})
and

N(\Xi_{0};d)=O(d^{c})
as
d\to\infty
for every
c>1/2
. Theorem 1
is a consequence of Theorems 2 and 3 below. Let
\tilde{\mathbb{S}}=\{z^{2}:z\in\mathbb{S}\}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:(\operatorname{Im}z)^{2}-4^{-1}<\operatorname{Re}z\}
.

Theorem 2

Suppose that
f
is a transcendental real
entire function of order
\rho<2
and
Z(f)\subset\mathbb{S}
. Then
for every
c>\rho
there is a positive integer
n_{1}
such that

Z(f^{(n)})\subset\left\{z\in\mathbb{S}:|\operatorname{Re}z|\geq n^{1/c}\right\}\cup\mathbb{R}\qquad(n\geq n_{1}).

[formula]

If
f
is even, then for every
c>\rho
there is a positive integer

n_{2}
such that

Z(f_{0}^{(n)})\subset\left\{z\in\tilde{\mathbb{S}}:\operatorname{Re}z\geq n^{2/c}\right\}\cup[0,\infty)\qquad(n\geq n_{2}).

Proof

The first part follows from Theorem 2 in
[
4
]
, and
the second part is a consequence of Theorem 1 in
[
5
]
(with

k=1/2
) applied to
g
given by
g(z)=f_{0}(z-4^{-1})
. ∎

For
\delta>0
we set
S(\delta)=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|\operatorname{Im}z|\leq\delta|z|\}
.

Theorem 3

Suppose that
P
and
Q
are real polynomials,

\delta>0
,
Z(P)\subset S(\delta)
,
Q
is hyperbolic and

\deg Q\leq\delta^{-2}
. Then the polynomial

P(D)Q=\sum_{k=0}^{\deg P}\frac{P^{(k)}(0)}{k!}Q^{(k)}

is hyperbolic.

Proof

See
[
6
]
. ∎

Corollary

Suppose that
P
is a real polynomial,

\delta>0
and
Z(P)\subset S(\delta)
. Then
J(P;d)
is
hyperbolic for
d\leq\delta^{-2}
.

Proof

If
Q(z)=z^{d}
, then
J(P;d)(z)=z^{d}(P(D)Q)(z^{-1})
.
∎

Proof of Theorem 1

Suppose that
c>\rho
. By Theorem 2, there
is a positive integer
n_{1}
such that (1) holds. Let
d
and
n
be
positive integers such that

n\geq\max\{n_{1},(d/4)^{c/2}\},

and put
\delta=2^{-1}n^{-1/c}
. Then
d\leq\delta^{-2}
, and from
(1) we can see that
|\operatorname{Im}z|\leq\delta|z|
for all
z\in Z(f^{(n)})
. Hence we have
Z(f^{(n)})\cup\mathbb{R}\subset S(\delta)
. Let
P_{1},P_{2},\dots
be real polynomials such
that
Z(P_{k})\subset Z(f^{(n)})\cup\mathbb{R}
for all
k
, and

P_{k}\to f^{(n)}
uniformly on compact sets in the complex plane.
Then
J(P_{k};d)\to J(f^{(n)};d)
uniformly on compact sets in the
complex plane, and the corollary to Theorem 3 implies that

J(P_{k};d)
is hyperbolic for every
k
. Therefore
J(f^{(n)};d)

is hyperbolic, and we conclude that

N(f;d)\leq\left\lceil\max\{n_{1},(d/4)^{c/2}\}\right\rceil\qquad(d=1,2,\dots).

This proves the first part of the theorem. The second part is
analogously proved. ∎

Remark

Since our argument deals with entire functions of
order less than
2
, it refines the proof of Theorem 3.6 in

[
1
]
.

Suppose that
T>0
and
Z(f)\subset\mathbb{S}(T)=\{z\in\mathbb{S}:|\operatorname{Re}z|\geq T\}\cup\mathbb{R}
. Then the same argument as
in the proof of Theorem 1 shows that
J(f;d)
is hyperbolic whenever

d\leq 1+4T^{2}
, because we have
\mathbb{S}(T)\subset S(\delta)
with

\delta=(1+4T^{2})^{-1/2}
. Since the condition
Z(f)\subset\mathbb{S}(T)
does not imply
Z(f^{\prime})\subset\mathbb{S}(T)
in general, it is
not clear whether the polynomials
J(f^{(n)};d)
,
n=1,2,\dots
,
are hyperbolic for
d\leq 1+4T^{2}
. If
f
is even and
T\geq 1/2
,
then it is the case.

Theorem 4

Suppose that
f
is an even transcendental real
entire function of order less than
2
,
T\geq 1/2
and
Z(f)\subset\mathbb{S}(T)
. Then

J(f^{(n)};d)
is
hyperbolic for
d\leq 1+4T^{2}
and for every
n
.

Proof

Let
\delta=(1+4T^{2})^{-1/2}
,

\tilde{\delta}=2\delta\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}\,
(
=T^{-1}(1+4^{-1}T^{-2})^{-1}
)
and
\tilde{S}=\{z\in S(\tilde{\delta}):\operatorname{Re}z\geq 0\}
.
Then
\mathbb{S}(T)\subset S(\delta)
, and
\tilde{S}
is convex. Since

T\geq 1/2
, we have
0<\delta\leq 2^{-1/2}
, and it follows that

z\in S(\delta)
if and only if
z^{2}\in\tilde{S}
.

We prove the theorem by showing that
Z(f^{(n)})\subset S(\delta)
for every
n
. We have
Z(f)\subset\mathbb{S}(T)\subset S(\delta)
, and this implies that
Z(f_{0})\subset\tilde{S}
.
Since
f_{0}
is of order less than
1
and
\tilde{S}
is convex, the
Gauss-Lucas theorem implies that
Z(f_{0}^{\prime})\subset\tilde{S}
.
Hence
Z(f^{\prime})\subset S(\delta)
, because
f^{\prime}(z)=2zf_{0}^{\prime}(z^{2})
. We
have

f^{\prime\prime}(z)=2\left(f_{0}^{\prime}(z^{2})+2z^{2}f_{0}^{\prime\prime}(z^{2})\right).

If we write
g(z)=z(f_{0}^{\prime}(z))^{2}
, then
g
is of order less than
1

and
Z(g)\subset\tilde{S}
. Again, it follows from the
Gauss-Lucas theorem that
Z(g^{\prime})\subset\tilde{S}
, that is, all
the roots of the equation

f_{0}^{\prime}(z)\left(f_{0}^{\prime}(z)+2zf_{0}^{\prime\prime}(z)\right)=0

lie in
\tilde{S}
. Therefore
Z(f^{\prime\prime})\subset S(\delta)
. Since

f^{\prime\prime}
is even, the result follows from an induction. ∎

Remark

The proof shows that
Z(f_{0}^{(n)})\subset\tilde{S}\subset S(\tilde{\delta})
for all
n
. Hence
J(f_{0}^{(n)};d)
is
hyperbolic for
d\leq T^{2}(1+4^{-1}T^{-2})^{2}
and for every
n
.

Currently, it is known
[
7
]
that
Z(\Xi)\subset\mathbb{S}(T)

for some
T\geq 3\cdot 10^{12}
. Thus
J(\Xi^{(n)};d)
is hyperbolic
for
d\leq 3.6\cdot 10^{25}
and for every
n
, and

J(\Xi_{0}^{(n)};d)
is hyperbolic for
d\leq 9\cdot 10^{24}
and for
every
n
.
