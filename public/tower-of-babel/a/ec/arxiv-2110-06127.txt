Causal Mediation Analysis: Selection with Asymptotically Valid Inference
Jeremiah Jones, Ashkan Ertefaie, Robert L. Strawderman

Abstract
Researchers are often interested in learning not only the effect of treatments on outcomes, but also the pathways through which these effects operate. A mediator is a variable that is affected by treatment and subsequently affects outcome. Existing methods for penalized mediation analyses may lead to ignoring important mediators and either assume that finite-dimensional linear models are sufficient to remove confounding bias, or perform no confounding control at all. In practice, these assumptions may not hold. We propose a method that considers the confounding functions as nuisance parameters to be estimated using data-adaptive methods. We then use a novel regularization method applied to this objective function to identify a set of important mediators. We derive the asymptotic properties of our estimator and establish the oracle property under certain assumptions. Asymptotic results are also presented in a local setting which contrast the proposal with the standard adaptive lasso. We also propose a perturbation bootstrap technique to provide asymptotically valid post-selection inference for the mediated effects of interest. The performance of these methods will be discussed and demonstrated through simulation studies.



1
Introduction

Researchers are often interested in learning about the mechanisms by which interventions or exposures affect outcomes. Mediation analysis seeks to characterize the effect of treatment through different pathways – the effects directly resulting from the treatment, and those which are
mediated
through other variables.
A mediator is a variable that is affected by the treatment and subsequently affects the outcome.
Due in part to contributions from the field of causal inference, much progress has been made in understanding sufficient assumptions for identifying various mediated effects, and statistical methods for estimating these quantities. These contributions have focused almost entirely on the case of a single mediator

(
Robins and
Greenland 1992
;
van der Laan and Petersen 2008
;
VanderWeele and
Vansteelandt 2009
;
VanderWeele and
Vansteelandt 2010
)
,
with some attention given to the case of multiple mediators.
VanderWeele and
Vansteelandt 2014

proposed a regression-based approach to assess the effect of an exposure on an outcome through several mediators considered jointly.

More recently, methodology has been developed for the study of mediation in high-dimensional settings.
However, as illustrated in our simulation studies, standard regularization methods which do not account for the mediator-exposure relationship may lead to ignoring important mediators. Some examples of these regularization-based selection techniques are the adaptive lasso
(
Zou 2006
)
and the minimax concave penalty
(
Zhang 2010
)
.
A proposal by
Zhang et al. 2016
used the minimax concave penalty along with pre-screening in high-dimensional epigenetic studies, basing the selection solely upon the outcome model.
More recent methods attempt to improve upon standard selection techniques by developing methods specific to the problem of mediator selection.

Zhao and Luo 2016
proposed a method to perform sparse estimation of mediation pathway coefficients using a modified lasso penalty that directly penalized the estimated contribution to a causal estimand.
Schaid and Sinnwell 2020
critiqued this approach for penalizing true mediators more heavily than non-mediators, and instead proposed a penalty to the full-data likelihood function based on the sparse group lasso.
Importantly, neither of these methods explicitly handle measured confounders, which can invalidate causal inferences in mediation analysis.
Further, the theoretical properties of these selection procedures have not yet, to the authors’ knowledge, been established.
Instead of a selection-based methodology,
Song et al. 2020
formulated a Bayesian prior that performs ridge-like shrinkage in high-dimensional settings.
Other alternatives to penalized estimators include dimensionality reduction-based methods. These require transformations of the mediators and hence may negatively impact interpretability of the results
(
Chén et al. 2017
;
Huang and Pan 2016
)
.
All the methods discussed above assume that either no confounding is present, or that a postulated finite-dimensional linear model for confounders is sufficient to control for the residual confounding in the mediator and outcome models.

In the high dimensional settings of some of this previous work, techniques are broadly proposed from the perspective of combatting the curse of dimensionality and allowing the estimation of mediation effects. On the other hand, there may be scientific interest in identifying and interpreting parsimonious pathways through which causal effects are mediated, even when the dimensionality of the problem is not severe. From this perspective, methods for performing mediator selection are valued not based on an improvement in estimation, but based on an improvement in scientific understanding or interpretability. In this paper, we propose a framework for mediator selection focused on this scientific goal.

Our proposed framework for mediation analysis incorporates mediator selection and nonparametric confounding control. Specifically, we propose an estimating procedure where the functional association form of the confounder-treatment, confounder-mediators and confounder-outcome associations are considered infinite-dimensional nuisance parameters which are estimated using data-adaptive techniques.
The parametrization we leverage de-couples the estimation of the focal parameters from confounding control.
This allows a wide variety of techniques such as machine learning tools to be employed as confounding control, as long as the corresponding nuisance parameters are consistently estimated with certain rates.
We show that our estimators are asymptotically linear under certain assumptions.
We demonstrate through simulation studies that the commonly-employed parametric linear confounding control suffers from severe confounding bias; we further show that such bias can be mitigated by the proposed method.
In addition, we develop regularization tailored to mediation, which can be used to identify the true mediators among a set of candidates. In
Section

4
we establish the variable selection consistency of this approach—to our knowledge, the first result of this kind in the domain.
A perturbation bootstrap approach for inference after selection is presented which extends the results of
Minnier et al. 2011
to this setting.
A closer study of asymptotic behavior follows in
Section

4.3
using a local asymptotic framework that better approximates the situation where some parameters are close to zero. Our proposed estimator is shown to maintain mediator selection consistency while a standard regularization technique, the adaptive lasso, is shown to fail under the same scenario when the penalty term only involves coefficients in the outcome model.
We conduct extensive simulation studies to highlight the importance of the proposal and study the small sample performance of proposed estimators. In particular, we show: (1) failing to properly adjust for confounders can lead to a biased estimate of the mediated effects; (2) using standard regularization methods (e.g., adaptive lasso) that focus solely on the outcome model may lead to ignoring important mediators, and; (3) the perturbation bootstrap leads to confidence intervals close to the nominal level when combined with our novel estimator.

Our method is applied to a data set in education. The STAR study was a randomized controlled trial that sought to identify if small class sizes were responsible for measurably improving student outcomes in Grades K-3. A seminal analysis by
Krueger 1999
has shown that small class sizes lead to demonstrable improvements in student outcomes.
A mediation analysis may answer the question: through which pathways does the causal effect of small class sizes travel to improve these test scores?
We report the results of our method on simultaneous selection of mediators and estimation of corresponding effects when applied to two mathematics standardized test outcomes in Grade 8: Computation, and Concepts & Applications.

Using the development so far, we are ready to formally state our goals. First, we wish to select the model
\mathcal{S}^{*}
using the full data in
\mathbf{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{O}_{n}
. Second, we wish to estimate the natural effects corresponding to
\mathcal{S}^{*}
by plugging parameter estimates into (
6
)-(
7
). Finally, we wish to provide inference for these causal estimands after selection.

The choice to focus on inference for
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is motivated from a policy perspective.
Inclusion of many spurious variables as mediators may hamper researchers’ understanding of the causal structure of certain treatment-outcome relationships. For example, when studying the effect of small class sizes in grades K-3 upon 8th grade maths test scores, it may be of interest to discover which particular scores transmit the effect. Identifying a small set of scores could be interesting, as it could assist in determining which specific parts of the curriculum benefit from small class sizes, and how these benefits evolve over time. This can have policy implications, as more targeted efforts to improve the identified mediating variables may allow more efficient use of resources while maximizing student outcomes
(
Ertefaie et al. 2018
)
. We therefore do not view
\mathcal{S}^{F}
as the mediation model of direct interest, as natural effects under this model would correspond to interventions on a potentially large set of variables. Instead,
\mathcal{S}^{F}
is a universe of possible mediation models which might be selected, with
\mathcal{S}^{*}
the most interesting and relevant choice in the sense of minimizing the number of mediators while containing all of the causal effect in
\mathrm{NIE}_{\mathcal{S}^{*}}.

3
Methodology

3.1
Nonparametric Confounding Control

Parametric models have many desirable and well-studied properties when the class of models considered is sufficiently large to characterize the true model. However in many applications, there may be little prior evidence to suggest the parametric form of confounding effects on the outcome,
\psi_{Y}(\cdot)
, and those effects on
\mathbf{M}
,
\bm{\psi}_{M}(\cdot)
. These effects represent a nuisance to the researcher, since the primary motivation in mediation analysis lies in characterizing the effect of treatment through direct and indirect pathways, yet control for them must be made in order to obtain unbiased estimates.
In the previous section, we detailed a re-expression of Model (
1
) in terms of conditional expectations.
Whereas the original functions
\psi_{Y}(\mathbf{X})
and
\bm{\psi}_{M}(\mathbf{X})
were only estimable in the context of the other covariates in each model, the conditional expectations are directly estimable on the basis of the subscripted variables and
\mathbf{X}
.
In other words, the conditional expectations are the same between
\mathcal{S}^{*}
and
\mathcal{S}^{F}
and need only be estimated once.
The first step in our framework is then to provide acceptable estimates for
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.

To avoid over-fitting, we use
K
-fold cross-fitting to estimate the nuisance parameters
(
Klaassen 1987
)
.
We first randomly split the original sample into
disjoint (hence independent) samples
(\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}})_{k=1}^{K}
such that the size of each sample is roughly
\left\lfloor n/K\right\rfloor
and
\mathbf{I}_{k}\subset\{1,\ldots,n\},k=1,\ldots,K
partition the indices
\{1,\ldots,n\}.

Analogously, define
\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}
as the set of sample indices that are not included in
\mathbf{I}_{k};
that is,
\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}\setminus\mathbf{I}_{k},k=1,\ldots,K
. Then, for each
k=1,2,\cdots,K
, estimate the nuisance functions
\mu_{Y0}(\cdot),

\mu_{D0}(\cdot),
and
\bm{\mu}_{M0}(\cdot)
using the data in
\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}};

we respectively denote these estimators

\hat{\mu}_{Y}(\cdot;\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}}),

\hat{\mu}_{D}(\cdot;\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}}),
and
\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{M}(\cdot;\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}}),

k=1,\ldots K.
. As a convenience, we denote these parameters evaluated at
\mathbf{X}_{i}
as
\hat{\mu}_{Yi}
,
\hat{\mu}_{Di}
, and
\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Mi}
, respectively, with
\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Zi}=(\hat{\mu}_{Di},~\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Mi}^{\top})^{\top}
.
Similar notation will be used for the true function values, e.g.
\bm{\mu}_{Z0i}=(\mu_{D0i},\bm{\mu}_{M0i}^{\top})^{\top}
with
\mu_{D0i}=\mu_{D0}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
, et cetera.

Instead of relying on any individual machine-learning or parametric method to control confounding, we combine multiple techniques into an ensemble-based predictor. The “stacked ensemble” approach was introduced by
Breiman 1996
and later generalized under the name SuperLearner
(
van der Laan et al. 2007
)
. This method defines a library of candidate estimators and uses cross-validation to estimate an optimal weighted combination of these predictors, which then defines the SuperLearner. Due to previous asymptotic characterizations of cross-validation procedures, this procedure adapts to the optimal model at a fast rate. In addition, the size of the grid can grow at a polynomial rate compared with the sample size without detrimental effects to its oracle performance

(
Dudoit and van der
Laan 2005
;
van der Vaart et al. 2006
)
. For these reasons, it is recommended that the library consist of a
large and diverse set of regression modeling procedures (e.g., data-adaptive, semiparametric, and parametric). We also recommend including different tuning parameter specifications of a data-adaptive method.

3.2
Mediator Selection and Estimation

Identification of
\mathcal{S}^{*}
requires finding those elements of
\mathbf{M}
which are associated with both the treatment and the outcome.
Standard regularized estimators that only consider one of two associations may perform poorly in finite samples. For example, penalizing the
\bm{\beta}
parameters in the outcome model may lead to ignoring mediators that are strongly associated with treatment but weakly associated with the outcome. Removing such variables may bias the estimated
\mathrm{NIE}_{\mathcal{S}^{*}}
due to the violation of one of the core assumptions in mediation analysis
(
VanderWeele and
Vansteelandt 2014
)
.

To overcome these limitations, we propose an extension of the adaptive lasso
(
Zou 2006
)
uniquely tailored to the mediation setting.
Following the representation (
8
) for
\mathrm{NIE}_{\mathcal{S}^{F}}
and using the expression for the full partial linear model (
2
), we define a class of objective functions

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{n}(\bm{\theta};\hat{\mathbf{w}},\lambda_{n}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\Big\{Y_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{Yi}-\big(\mathbf{Z}_{i}-{\hat{\bm{\mu}}}_{Zi}\big)^{\top}\bm{\theta}\Big\}^{2}}+\frac{\lambda_{n}}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{p+1}{\hat{\mathbf{w}}(\{j\})\big|\bm{\theta}(\{j\})\big|},

(9)

where
\hat{\mathbf{w}}
represents a vector of (possibly estimated) penalty weights. For example, the standard adaptive lasso weights would use
\hat{\mathbf{w}}(\{j\})=|\breve{\bm{\theta}}(\{j\})|^{-\kappa}
for a preliminary estimator
\breve{\bm{\theta}}
.
The penalized estimator minimizes the display above,
\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{L}=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{n}(\bm{\theta};\hat{\mathbf{w}},\lambda_{n})
,
which also contains the unpenalized estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}:=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{n}(\bm{\theta};\hat{\mathbf{w}},0)
as a special case.
In light of (
1
), we partition
\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{L}=(\hat{\gamma}^{L},\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{L})^{\top}
. Finally, we estimate
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
using least-squares:

\hat{\bm{\alpha}}=\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(D_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{Di})^{2}\bigg\}^{-1}\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(D_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{Di})(\mathbf{M}_{i}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Mi})\bigg\}.

(10)

Equation (
10

p
single-parameter least-squares models. As explained in
Remark

2.3
, selection merely subsets the full
\hat{\bm{\alpha}}
vector as used in (
7

In other words, the individual estimates
\hat{\bm{\alpha}}(\{j\})
for
j=1,\ldots,p
do not change through selection.
After obtaining a selected model
\hat{\mathcal{S}}
as well as estimators
\hat{\bm{\alpha}}
and
\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{L}
, one may plug in these quantities to create estimators
\widehat{\mathrm{NDE}}=\hat{\gamma}^{L}
and
\widehat{\mathrm{NIE}}=\hat{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{L}
. Depending on the penalty used, these estimates may or may not target
\mathrm{NDE}_{\mathcal{S}^{*}}
and
\mathrm{NIE}_{\mathcal{S}^{*}}
, respectively (c.f.
Section

4
).

To identify
\mathcal{S}^{*}
, we should not penalize
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
for including a nonzero treatment coefficient; we accomplish this by setting
\hat{\mathbf{w}}(\{1\})=0
. The remaining weights
\hat{\mathbf{w}}(\{1+j\})
for
j=1,\ldots,p
use pilot estimators
\breve{\bm{\alpha}}
and
\breve{\bm{\theta}}
, which we assume are
\sqrt{n}-
consistent for
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
and
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, respectively. As verified in Theorem
4.1
, a straightforward choice under the assumption
p<n
would be to define
\breve{\bm{\alpha}}
as given in (
10
) and use the unpenalized estimator
\breve{\bm{\theta}}\equiv\hat{\bm{\theta}}
.
Adaptations could be made to accommodate the
p>n
setting; see
Section

7
for discussion.

Our extension proceeds by choosing the adaptive penalty based on the contribution to the
\mathrm{NIE}_{\mathcal{S}^{F}}
. Assuming we have pilot estimators
\breve{\bm{\alpha}}
and
\breve{\bm{\beta}}
, let
\kappa>0
. We propose the novel product weight (PRD):

\hat{\mathbf{w}}(\{1+j\})=|\breve{\bm{\alpha}}(\{j\})\breve{\bm{\beta}}(\{j\})|^{-\kappa}.

(11)

This differs from the standard adaptive lasso (ADP) weight
|\breve{\bm{\beta}}(\{j\})|^{-\kappa}
by including information on the relationship between mediator and treatment.
The penalty function resulting from (
11
) directly penalizes candidate mediators that have little estimated contribution to the
\mathrm{NIE}_{\mathcal{S}^{F}}
. This offers an improvement upon the ADP weight by incorporating information from the complete mediation model, protecting against removal of mediators with small
|\bm{\beta}_{0}(\{j\})|
but large
|\bm{\alpha}_{0}(\{j\})|
. Consequently, the PRD weight may include mediators that the ADP does not.

3.3
Performing Inference Post-Selection

Our estimators rely on the performance of the adaptive lasso estimator for its simultaneous selection and nearly-unbiased estimation capabilities. Inferential techniques based on the adaptive lasso have been explored by others in the context of outcome-only models.
Zou 2006
suggested appealing to the oracle normal distribution with a variance estimate informed by the Local Quadratic Approximation (LQA) technique of
Fan and Li 2001
. More recent work has explored the use of certain bootstrap techniques with the adaptive lasso as an improvement upon the oracle normal approximation.

Minnier et al. 2011
established first-order correctness of a perturbation bootstrap distribution, and claimed an improvement over the LQA approach supported by simulation studies. Later,
Das et al. 2019
provided a second-order correction to the perturbation bootstrap distribution and claimed further improvement over the
Minnier et al. 2011
approach.

Despite investigations into the performance of these approaches in a single-outcome model, it is unclear how various methods perform in the system of equations defined by the mediation model. Traditionally, inference for the
\mathrm{NIE}_{\mathcal{S}^{*}}
using a plug-in estimator based on (
7
) is possible by appealing to the Delta Method. Confidence intervals and tests could be constructed by plugging in estimates of the asymptotic covariance matrix by appealing to an oracle property similar to LQA. Due to the simultaneous selection and estimation properties of our estimator, we expect such a method to perform undesirably in certain situations
(
Leeb and Pötscher 2008
;
Leeb and Pötscher 2006
)
.

Instead, we propose a generalization of the perturbation bootstrap approach of
Minnier et al. 2011
. Let
G_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
represent i.i.d. data-independent draws from a user-specified distribution satisfying
\mathbb{E}G_{1}=1
and
\mathbb{E}G_{1}^{2}<\infty
. These random variables represent observation-specific weights used to multiply the observation-specific contribution to the relevant loss functions.
Specifically, the perturbation bootstrap estimates are defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\alpha}}^{b}=

\displaystyle\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}(D_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{Di})^{2}\bigg\}^{-1}\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}(D_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{Di})(\mathbf{M}_{i}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Mi})\bigg\}

(12)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{Lb}:=

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bm{\theta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}\big\{Y_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{Yi}-\bm{\theta}^{\top}(\mathbf{Z}_{i}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Zi})\big\}^{2}+\lambda_{n}^{b}\sum_{j=1}^{p+1}\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{b}(\{j\})\big|\bm{\beta}(\{j\})\big|

where the weights
\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{b}
make use of perturbed pilot estimators
(\breve{\bm{\alpha}}^{b},\breve{\bm{\beta}}^{b})
and we once again partition
\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{Lb}=(\hat{\gamma}^{Lb},\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{Lb\top})^{\top}
. As in
Minnier et al. 2011
, we propose to use the distributions of
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\gamma}^{Lb}-\hat{\gamma})
and
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\bm{\alpha}}^{b}\cdot\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{Lb}-\hat{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{L})
to approximate those of

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\gamma}^{L}-\gamma_{0})
and
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{L}-\bm{\alpha}_{0}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{0})
, respectively. This is the basis for forming confidence intervals for the
\mathrm{NDE}_{\mathcal{S}^{*}}
and
\mathrm{NIE}_{\mathcal{S}^{*}}
.

4.3
Selection and Estimation in a Local Asymptotic Framework

Next, we present a novel local asymptotic theorem that examines selection and estimation performance in a slightly different setting. This departs from the usual oracle selection analysis by allowing the true data-generating process to change with sample size. This framework was previously used in
Knight and Fu 2000
to shed light on the differences between ridge regression and lasso regression when some coefficients are small, but nonzero.
This local asymptotic framework allows us to examine how the proposed PRD weights perform relative to the ADP weights on the most difficult-to-estimate targets—those which are small but nonzero. This local view should allow the asymptotic theory to better approximate the finite-sample case
(
Knight and Fu 2000
)
.

The main idea of this framework is the local data-generating process.
Suppose the parameters of mediation model (
1
) vary with
n
, so that the true values of
\bm{\alpha}
,
\bm{\beta}
, and
\gamma
are given by
\bm{\alpha}_{0n}=\bm{\alpha}_{0}+\mathbf{h}_{1n}
,
\bm{\beta}_{0n}=\bm{\beta}_{0}+\mathbf{h}_{2n}
, and
\gamma_{0n}=\gamma_{0}+\mathbf{h}_{3n}(\{1\})
, respectively, where the “directions”
\mathbf{h}_{3n}(\{1\})
and
\mathbf{h}_{kn}
for
\ell=1,2
converge to zero in the sense that

|\mathbf{h}_{\ell n}(\{j\})|\asymp r_{\ell nj}
for
\ell=1,2,3
. We allow subsets
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{\ell}\subset\mathcal{S}^{F}
for
\ell=1,2
to be defined such that
|\mathbf{h}_{\ell n}(\{j\})|=0
for
j\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{\ell}^{c}
and
r_{\ell nj}\rightarrow 0
for
j\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{\ell}
. The rate associated with the unpenalized coefficient is assumed to satisfy
r_{3n1}\rightarrow 0
.
Then for each
n
, we may define a true set of mediators
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{*}
as those candidates for which
\bm{\alpha}_{0n}(\{j\})\bm{\beta}_{0n}(\{j\})\neq 0
. The true
\mathrm{NDE}_{\mathcal{S}_{n}^{*}}
and
\mathrm{NIE}_{\mathcal{S}_{n}^{*}}
values as listed in (
6
)-(
7
) may also be extended to use these local parameters. In other words, the true set of mediators, the effect sizes in each mediated pathway, and the mediation effect targets are defined relative to the local data-generating process. This allows the approximation of small-contribution mediators by setting, e.g.
\bm{\beta}_{0}(\{j\})=0
for some
j=1,\ldots,p
. Relative to the local data-generating process, the true
\bm{\beta}_{0n}(\{j\})\asymp r_{2nj}
.

To simplify our theorem statement, we will make some further assumptions on the rates at which the directions of the local data-generating process converge to zero. These assumptions could be relaxed, although a straightforward comparison becomes complicated, especially when identifying appropriate rates for
\lambda_{n}
. We will assume that
r_{\ell nj}=n^{-c_{\ell j}}
for
\ell=1,2
,
j\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{\ell}
and
\ell=3,~j=1
. For
\ell=1,2
, let
c_{\ell j}=0
for
j\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{\ell}^{c}
. We assume
c_{\ell j}\geq 0
and
0<c_{1j}+c_{2j}\leq 1/2
for all
j\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}\cup\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}
, whereas
0<c_{31}\leq 1/2
.
The oracle property for this local process is then generalized as

\displaystyle Pr\big\{\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{L}(\{j\})\neq 0\text{ for any }j\in\mathcal{S}^{c}\big\}\rightarrow 0~\hskip 5.0pt\text{and}\hskip 5.0pt~\sqrt{n}\big\{\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{L}(\mathcal{S})-\bm{\theta}_{0n\mathcal{S}}\big\}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathcal{J}_{1\mathcal{S}}).

(15)

Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{*}=\{j:\bm{\alpha}_{0n}(\{j\})\bm{\beta}_{0n}(\{j\})\neq 0\}
represent the set of true mediators relative to the local data-generating process. Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\dagger}=\{j:\bm{\alpha}_{0n}(\{j\})\neq 0\text{ or }\bm{\beta}_{0n}(\{j\})\neq 0\}

and
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\ddagger}=\{j:\bm{\beta}_{0}(\{j\})\neq 0\}\cup\{j:\bm{\beta}_{0}(\{j\})=0,c_{2j}<1/2\}
. Notice that
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\dagger}\supseteq\mathcal{S}_{n}^{*}
, but it is not the case that

\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\ddagger}\supseteq\mathcal{S}_{n}^{*}
.
That is, any procedure which selects
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\dagger}
conservatively selects the true set of mediators, while any procedure which selects
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\ddagger}
selects purely based on the outcome model and is sensitive to the rate at which
\bm{\beta}_{0n}(\{j\})
converges to zero. For relatively small coefficients satisfying
\bm{\beta}_{0n}(\{j\})\asymp n^{-1/2}
,
j\notin\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\ddagger}
. We will verify below that the PRD weights may target
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\dagger}
, while
the ADP weights target
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\ddagger}
, meaning that true mediators may be removed by using the ADP weights.

Theorem 4.5
.

Adopt the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
. Suppose Model (
1
) holds with parameters described by the local data-generating process above.

(i)

Suppose the PRD weights (
11
) are chosen. If
n^{(\kappa-1)/2}\lambda_{n}\rightarrow 0
and
n^{(2\kappa-1)/2}\lambda_{n}\rightarrow\infty
, then (
15
) holds with
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\dagger}
.

(ii)

Suppose the ADP weights are chosen. If
n^{(\kappa-1-\delta)/2}\lambda_{n}\rightarrow 0
and
n^{(\kappa-1)/2}\lambda_{n}\rightarrow\infty
for every
0<\delta<\kappa
, then (
15
) holds with
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\ddagger}
.

Under the conditions of this theorem, the proposed product weights (
11
) select a conservative set of mediators. Variables included in the set include all variables with at most one of
\bm{\alpha}_{0n}(\{j\}),\bm{\beta}_{0n}(\{j\})
equal to zero. However, the stated conditions for the ADP weights only allow identification of
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\ddagger}
. Variables with coefficients
\bm{\beta}_{0n}(\{j\})
that are too small will tend to be removed from the selected set, even if
\bm{\alpha}_{0n}(\{j\})\bm{\beta}_{0n}(\{j\})
is on the same order as other variables.

It may appear unintuitive that the standard adaptive lasso cannot distinguish between coefficients roughly of size
n^{-1/2}
, while the PRD weights can. The crucial difference between the PRD and ADP weights in this theorem is the size of
n^{(\kappa-1)/2}\lambda_{n}
.
For the ADP weights, the requirement
n^{(\kappa-1)/2}\lambda_{n}\rightarrow\infty
to de-select the noise variables replicates the results of
Zou 2006
. This comes with the side-effect of also estimating
\bm{\beta}_{0n}(\{j\})\asymp n^{-1/2}
as exactly zero.
In other words, this makes it possible to de-select true mediators based on how small
\bm{\beta}_{0n}(\{j\})
is, regardless of the magnitude of
\bm{\alpha}_{0n}(\{j\})\bm{\beta}_{0n}(\{j\})
.
The strength of the PRD weights is that the crucial requirement for removing completely irrelevant variables
\bm{\alpha}_{0n}(\{j\})=\bm{\beta}_{0n}(\{j\})=0
is
n^{(2\kappa-1)/2}\lambda_{n}\rightarrow\infty
, as verified in Theorem
4.2
,
which we may allow without impacting performance on the coefficients with
\bm{\alpha}_{0n}(\{j\})\bm{\beta}_{0n}(\{j\})\asymp n^{-1/2}
.
The price paid for this flexibility is conservativeness: this latter rate requirement is reported in Theorem
4.2
as ensuring in the fixed-parameter case that non-mediators with only one coefficient nonzero are selected.

5
Simulation Studies

We demonstrate the performance of these methods through extensive simulation studies. We draw attention to the quality of our confounding control under various true-model scenarios and modeling techniques, and the accuracy of our estimated causal effects in comparison with the traditional adaptive lasso. Since selection may improve interpretability, we also compare this established method with our proposed weights on the basis of selection performance. Our summarized findings are that the product weights match the performance of the standard method in the most favorable scenarios while providing better selection performance in the least favorable settings.

6
Data Application

We illustrate our method using data from the ‘Student/Teacher Achievement Ratio’ (STAR) study
Word et al. 1990
. In this study, 11,600 students were randomized to one of three class size configurations: small class size of 13-17, regular size of 22-25, and regular size with a teachers aide. Individual students maintained the randomized class size from kindergarten to grade 3. The STAR study concluded that small class sizes tended to improve student performance on standardized tests.

One interesting question that may be studied is whether small class sizes affect the developmental trajectory of a student. Small class sizes may improve later test scores via improvements in earlier skills. Identifying the pathways through which the causal effect travels may improve the understanding of how childhood development relates to standardized test scores. From an educational policy perspective, such an analysis may also help to identify particular skills to be targeted for intervention when the goal is the improvement of standardized test scores.

A previous analysis of the STAR study found no significant difference with the addition of a teacher’s aide at regular class sizes
Krueger 1999
. Therefore, we group these two configurations together, yielding a binary “treatment” of small class size compared to “control” of regular class size as pursued by
Ertefaie et al. 2018
. We study the effect of class size on student performance on grade 8 standardized maths tests via several candidate mediators. We performed this analysis on two separate outcome measures:
Comprehensive Tests of Basic Skills (CTSB) standardized test scores in Math Computation, and Concepts & Applications. Mediators were chosen to include 60 measures of academic performance and engagement measured from grades K-3. Confounding variables were gender, race, free/reduced lunch status, and school urbanicity status.

As recommended in
Section

3.1
, the SuperLearner library contained a variety of methods: a linear model (
SL.lm
), grand mean (
SL.mean
), Generalized Additive Model (
SL.gam
), Lasso regression (
SL.glmnet
), and Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines (
SL.earth
) with default hyperparameters. Two specifications of a
SL.randomForest
model
(
Liaw et al. 2002
)
were included with minimum node sizes of 5 and 9, 1000 trees, and 3 variables randomly selected. The
SL.xgboost
model
(
Chen and Guestrin 2016
)
was included with a maximum depth of 3 and 100 trees. All other hyperparameters were left at default values. SuperLearner was used to estimate
\mu_{Y0}
and

We used the proposed estimators, derived from equations (

) and (

). We compared the PRD and ADP estimators to the model using all 60 candidates (FULL), equivalently setting
\lambda_{n}=0
in (

Statistically significant direct effects were not found across any estimation method, inference technique, or outcome. For the Math Computation outcome, the proposed selection method found a significant natural direct effect using the proposed bootstrap methodology, while the ADP-selected model was not found to be significant. Notably, the naive Delta method confidence intervals did not cover zero for both post-selection estimators, while they did cover zero for the semiparametric full model (FULL). The proposed bootstrap applied to the ADP estimator resulted in a much larger confidence interval compared to the naive Delta method. In contrast, the proposed intervals applied to the PRD estimator resulted in a slight enlargement of the intervals. The PRD method selected a model nearly half the size of that of the ADP (20

Discussion

In this paper, we developed causal mediation analysis in a selection setting. We defined the sparse natural effect estimands, which were motivated from both policy implementation and researcher interpretability perspectives. To target these estimands, we proposed a method for simultaneously selecting mediators among a candidate set, estimating the natural effects, and performing inference upon the selected set. Theoretical properties were established for this method in both a classical asymptotic framework, as well as in a local asymptotic framework. These theoretical properties were verified in extensive simulation studies.

In
Remark

2.2
, we acknowledged a possible interventional-effect interpretation of our resulting estimators, which would be valid even after removing
2.1(v)
from
2.3
and
2.1
.
However,
2.1(iv)
still requires none of the variables indexed by
\mathcal{S}^{*c}
to confound the mediator-outcome relationship. These issues are fundamental to all mediation analysis, as encoded in the causal assumptions. In terms of the observed data, this implies that certain causal or correlation patterns between
\mathbf{M}(\mathcal{S}^{*})
,
\mathbf{M}(\mathcal{S}^{*c})
, and
Y
are disallowed, conditional on
\mathbf{X},D
. See
Remark

E.4
in
Appendix

E
for discussion and an example of this issue.

The results presented in this paper raise additional interesting questions that merit future study. For example,
our asymptotic framework assumed a fixed-dimensional set of mediators, as well as a binary treatment or exposure. Future work should examine establishing similar results, while allowing
p\rightarrow\infty
. For these large-
p
settings, we might instead relax the requirement that the pilot estimators be
\sqrt{n}-
consistent in favor of a relaxed
\sqrt{n/\log p}-
consistency result, which may be achieved by L1- or L2-penalized estimators
(
Zou and Zhang 2009
;
Negahban et al. 2012
, c.f. )
. The rates on
\lambda_{n}
might be adjusted to account for this reduced rate.
This framework brings several practical and theoretical challenges, as the dimension of
\bm{\mu}_{Z0}
increases with
p
.
It is unclear how this setting may impact bootstrap-based inference.

Theorem
4.5
presented a local asymptotic analysis to approximate the finite-sample situation of small coefficients, but did so by making assumptions on the rates at which the true parameters were perturbed.
Improvements upon these results could be made by making additional assumptions on the rates
r_{1nj},r_{2nj}
and more finely-tuning the choice of
\lambda_{n}
based on these assumptions. One implication of the existing requirement that
c_{1j}+c_{2j}\leq 1/2
is that none of the
\mathrm{NIE}_{\mathcal{S}^{F}}
contributions are too small to be of smaller order than sampling error. In the absence of refinements, the presented rates for
\lambda_{n}
are in some sense the most favorable to both weighting methods, in that they allow the inclusion of as many true mediators as possible. They also do not require that
\lambda_{n}
be chosen in a way that makes use of the underlying size of the coefficients.

Our proposed perturbation bootstrap leverages a first-order multiplier bootstrap distribution using a percentile approach. Studentization often can improve the quality of bootstrap approximation
(
Hall 1992
)
, although, in the case of the
\mathrm{NIE}_{\mathcal{S}^{F}}
, such a technique requires a variance estimate of the sum-product estimator
\hat{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\hat{\bm{\beta}}
. Such a variance estimate might be furnished by a Delta Method approximation with a Heteroskedastic-Consistent covariance estimate, although the resulting performance is unclear. A recent proposal by
Das et al. 2019
used an Edgeworth-expansion approach to arrive at a bias-correction to the
Minnier et al. 2011
approach. A higher-order study of our bootstrap approach with the Edgeworth-expansion may provide a bias correction, although it is unclear how this study might accommodate the nuisance parameter estimation via Robinson’s transformation. Simulation studies did not show an improvement when applying the Das bootstrap to our setting. Developing a higher-order-correct bootstrap deserves further study.

Useful Lemmas and a Theorem

Throughout these proofs, we will make use of the following lemmas, which are similar to those in
Ertefaie et al. 2021
with the addition of allowing random multipliers.

Lemma C.1
.

Suppose
\mathbf{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{O}_{n}
are i.i.d. random vectors, and
(R_{i},\mathbf{X}_{i})
are
\mathbf{O}_{i}
-measurable random variables. Use the cross-fitting notation defined in
Section

3.1
so that
\cup_{k=1,\ldots,K}\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}}=(\mathbf{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{O}_{n})
.
Recall the notation of
\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}}
for
(\mathbf{O}_{i}
for
i\notin\mathbf{I}_{k})
.
Let
\mathbb{E}(R_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{i})=\mathbb{E}(R_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{i},\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})=0
and
0<\mathrm{Var}(R_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{i})=\mathrm{Var}(R_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{i},\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})\leq C_{1}<\infty
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
. Then

\bigg|n^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{k}}R_{i}h(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})\bigg|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{P_{0},2,k}),

(16)

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{P,2,k}
for
k=1,\ldots,K
is defined in (
13
).
In particular, if
\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{P_{0},2,k}=O_{p}((n-n_{k})^{-a})=O_{p}(n^{-a})
for
k=1,\ldots,K
, then

\bigg|n^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{k}}R_{i}h(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})\bigg|=O_{p}(n^{-(1+a)/2}).

(17)

Finally, if we let
R_{i}=G_{i}H_{i}
, where
G_{1},\ldots,G_{n}
are i.i.d. random variables with constant variance
\mathbb{E}G_{1}^{2}<\infty
, which are independent of
(H_{i},\mathbf{X}_{i})
, and the random variables
H_{i}
otherwise satisfy the conditions on
R_{i}
above, then (
16
) and (
17
) hold.

Proof.

Let

S_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{k}}R_{i}h(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})

and using iterated expectations,

\mathbb{E}(S_{n}|\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})=\frac{n_{k}}{n}\mathbb{E}\Big\{h(\mathbf{X}_{1};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})\mathbb{E}\big(R_{1}\big|\mathbf{X}_{1},\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}}\big)\Big|\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}}\Big\}=0.

Then we can similarly calculate the conditional variance as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(S_{n}|\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(S_{n}^{2}\big|\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{-2}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{k}}\mathbb{E}\Big\{h(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})^{2}\mathrm{Var}(R_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{i})\big|\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}}\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{1}n^{-1}\mathbb{E}\Big\{n^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{k}}h(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})^{2}\Big|\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}}\Big\}

where the second line follows from
R_{i}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}R_{j}
and hence
\mathbb{E}(R_{i}R_{j}|\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{X}_{j},\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})=0
for
i\neq j\in\mathbf{I}_{k}
. The final statement is true by assumption.
By applying Chebyshev’s Inequality, using the definition of
\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{P_{0},2,k}
, and noting
n_{k}/n\rightarrow K^{-1}<\infty
, we have

\displaystyle Pr(|S_{n}|>\sqrt{n}\varepsilon~|~\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-1}C_{1}n_{k}n^{-1}\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{P_{0},2,k}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{p}(\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{P_{0},2,k}^{2})

which establishes (
16
).
Equation

17
follows directly.

The final statement of the Lemma follows by noting that if
R_{i}=G_{i}H_{i}
with
G_{i}
independent of the remaining random variables, then we can verify that the hypotheses of the Lemma hold with respect to
R_{i}
. These are the zero-mean assumption
\mathbb{E}(R_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{i})=\mathbb{E}(R_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{i},\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})=0
and the finite-variance assumption
\mathrm{Var}(R_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{i})=\mathrm{Var}(R_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{i},\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})<C_{2}
. The zero-mean assumption follows from

0=\mathbb{E}(R_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{i})=\mathbb{E}G_{i}\mathbb{E}(H_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{i})=\mathbb{E}G_{i}\mathbb{E}(H_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{i},\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})=\mathbb{E}(R_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{i},\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})

where the string of equalities hold due to independence of
G_{i}
from the remaining variables. Finally, if

\mathrm{Var}(H_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{i})=\mathrm{Var}(H_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{i},\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})<C_{1}

then the finite variance assumption holds with
C_{2}=C_{1}\mathbb{E}G_{1}^{2}
.

Lemma C.2
.

Assume the notation and setup in the statement of
C.1
. Additionally, let
h_{1},h_{2}
be measurable functions of
\mathbf{X}
and
\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}}
. Also, for fixed
\gamma\in\{0,1\}
, let
V(\gamma)=(1-\gamma)+\gamma\mathbb{E}G_{1}^{2}
. Then

\bigg|n^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{k}}G_{i}^{\gamma}h_{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})h_{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})\bigg|=O_{p}(\left\lVert h_{1}\right\rVert_{P_{0},2,k}\left\lVert h_{2}\right\rVert_{P_{0},2,k}).

In particular, if
\left\lVert h_{1}\right\rVert_{P_{0},2,k}=o_{p}((n-n_{k})^{-a_{1}/2})
and
\left\lVert h_{2}\right\rVert_{P_{0},2,k}=o_{p}((n-n_{k})^{-a_{2}/2})
, then

\bigg|n^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{k}}G_{i}^{\gamma}h_{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})h_{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})\bigg|=o_{p}((n-n_{k})^{-(a_{1}+a_{2})/2})=o_{p}(n^{-(a_{1}+a_{2})/2}).

Proof.

This is a rather straightforward application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Letting
S_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{k}}G_{i}^{\gamma}h_{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})h_{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})
, apply the triangle inequality and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to
|S_{n}|
to obtain

\displaystyle|S_{n}|\leq

\displaystyle\bigg\{n^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{k}}G_{i}^{2\gamma}h_{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})^{2}\bigg\}^{1/2}\bigg\{n^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{k}}h_{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})^{2}\bigg\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n_{k}/n)\bigg\{n_{k}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{k}}G_{i}^{2\gamma}h_{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})^{2}\bigg\}^{1/2}\bigg\{n_{k}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{k}}h_{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})^{2}\bigg\}^{1/2}

Now take expectations of both sides and apply Jensen’s Inequality to the concave function
x\mapsto x^{1/2}
to obtain the following sequence of inequalities:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(|S_{n}|~\big|~\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}}\right)\leq

\displaystyle(n_{k}/n)\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\bigg\{n_{k}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{k}}G_{i}^{2\gamma}h_{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})^{2}\bigg\}^{1/2}\bigg\{n_{k}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{k}}h_{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})^{2}\bigg\}^{1/2}~\Bigg|~\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(n_{k}/n)\Bigg[n_{k}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{k}}\mathbb{E}\Big\{G_{i}^{2\gamma}h_{1}(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})^{2}~\Big|~\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}}\Big\}\Bigg]^{1/2}\Bigg[n_{k}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{k}}\mathbb{E}\Big\{h_{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i};\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}})^{2}~\Big|~\bm{D}_{\mathbf{I}_{k}^{c}}\Big\}\Bigg]^{1/2}.

The independence between
G_{i}
and
\mathbf{O}_{i}

Let
