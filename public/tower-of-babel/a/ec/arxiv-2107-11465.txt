Efficient approximation of branching random walk Gibbs measures
Fu-Hsuan Ho, Pascal Maillard

Abstract
Disordered systems such as spin glasses have been used extensively as models for high-dimensional random landscapes and studied from the perspective of optimization algorithms. In a recent paper by L. Addario-Berry and the second author, the continuous random energy model (CREM) was proposed as a simple toy model to study the efficiency of such algorithms. The following question was raised in that paper: what is the threshold β G \beta_{G} , at which sampling (approximately) from the Gibbs measure at inverse temperature β \beta becomes algorithmically hard? This paper is a first step towards answering this question. We consider the branching random walk, a time-homogeneous version of the continuous random energy model. We show that a simple greedy search on a renormalized tree yields a linear-time algorithm which approximately samples from the Gibbs measure, for every β < β c \beta<\beta_{c} , the (static) critical point. More precisely, we show that for every ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , there exists such an algorithm such that the specific relative entropy between the law sampled by the algorithm and the Gibbs measure of inverse temperature β \beta is less than ε \varepsilon with high probability. In the supercritical regime β > β c \beta>\beta_{c} , we provide the following hardness result. Under a mild regularity condition, for every δ > 0 \delta>0 , there exists z > 0 z>0 such that the running time of any given algorithm approximating the Gibbs measure stochastically dominates a geometric random variable with parameter e − z ​ N e^{-z\sqrt{N}} on an event with probability at least 1 − δ 1-\delta . Keywords: branching random walk ; Gibbs measure ; Kullback–Leibler divergence. MSC2020 subject classifications: 68Q17, 82D30, 60K35, 60J80.

Introduction

We consider the following family of branching random walks. An initial particle is located at the origin. It gives birth to
d
child particles,
d\geq 2
, scattering on the real line, and each of the child particles produces
d
child particles again, and so on. The displacement of each particle is independent of the past of the process and of the displacements of its siblings. The genealogy of the particles can be represented by a
d
-ary tree
\mathbb{T}_{N}
, identifying particles with the vertices of the tree. We denote by
X_{v}
the location of a particle
v\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
.

Addario-Berry and Maillard
[
1
]
considered algorithms that, for a given
x>0
, find a leaf
v
of
\mathbb{T}_{N}
such that
X_{v}\geq xN
in the framework of the continuous random energy model (CREM). This is a binary time-inhomogeneous branching random walk with Gaussian displacements. More precisely, the CREM is a Gaussian process whose covariance function is given by

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{v}X_{w}\right]=A\left(\frac{\absolutevalue{v\wedge w}}{N}\right),\quad\forall v,w\in\mathbb{T}_{n}

where
A:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
is an increasing function with
A(0)=0
and
A(1)=1
and
\absolutevalue{v\wedge w}
is the depth of the most recent common ancestor of
v
and
w
.
The authors proved the existence of a threshold
x_{*}
such that the following holds: a) for every
x<x_{*}
, there exists a polynomial-time algorithm that can accomplish the task with high probability, b) for every
x>x_{*}
, every such algorithm has a running time which is at least exponential in
N
with high probability. The authors also raised the question of the complexity of sampling a
typical
vertex of value roughly
xN
, which can be interpreted as sampling a vertex according to a Gibbs measure with a certain parameter
\beta
depending on
x
.

The present work attacks this problem in the simpler setting of the (homogeneous) branching random walk, corresponding to the case
A(x)=x
of the CREM in the special case of Gaussian displacements. The Gibbs measure is a probability measure on the leaves
v
of
\mathbb{T}_{N}
with weight proportional to
e^{\beta X_{v}}
, where
\beta>0
is a given parameter called the
inverse temperature
. We show that there exists a threshold
\beta_{c}>0
such that the following holds: a) in the subcritical regime
\beta<\beta_{c}
, there exists a linear-time algorithm such that with high probability, the specific relative entropy between the law sampled by the algorithm and the Gibbs measure of inverse temperature
\beta
is arbitrarily small, b) in the supercritical regime
\beta>\beta_{c}
, under a mild regularity condition, we show that with high probability, the running time of any given algorithm approximating the Gibbs measure in this sense is at least stretched exponential in
N
.

Notations and main results

Let
\mathbb{T}

is a prefix of

We denote

to be the subtree of

containing vertices of depth less or equal to

Let

be a

\mathbf{X}=(X_{v})_{v\in\mathbb{T}}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}X_{\varnothing}=0,&\\[6.0pt]
X_{v}=\sum_{\varnothing<w\leq v}Y_{w},&\absolutevalue{v}\geq 1.\end{cases}

The process

is called the

.

It is well-known that

has the branching property: let

be its natural filtration. For any

with
\absolutevalue{v}=n

\mathbf{X}^{v}=(X^{v}_{w})_{\absolutevalue{w}\geq 0}=(X_{vw}-X_{v})_{\absolutevalue{w}\geq 0}.

Then

\mathbf{X}
and independent of
\mathscr{F}_{n}
.

Gibbs measures.

Define the following function

\displaystyle\varphi(\beta)=\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}e^{\beta Y_{i}}\right]\right)\in(-\infty,\infty],\quad\beta\in\mathbb{R},

and set
\mathcal{D}(\varphi)=\{\beta\in\mathbb{R}:\varphi(\beta)<\infty\}
.
It is well known that
\varphi
is convex and that it is smooth on
\mathcal{D}(\varphi)^{\circ}
, the interior of
\mathcal{D}(\varphi)
. Throughout the article, we assume
the following.

Assumption 1.1
.

0\in\mathcal{D}(\varphi)^{\circ}
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define the following (normalized) partition functions

\displaystyle W_{\beta,n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\absolutevalue{v}=n}e^{\beta X_{v}-\varphi(\beta)n},\quad n\geq 0,

\displaystyle W^{u}_{\beta,n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\absolutevalue{w}=n}e^{\beta X_{w}^{u}-\varphi(\beta)n},\quad u\in\mathbb{T}\text{ and }n\geq 0.

Note that for every
m\leq n
,

\displaystyle W_{\beta,n}=\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=m}e^{\beta X_{u}-\varphi(\beta)m}\cdot W^{u}_{\beta,n-m}.

(1.1)

For
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we now define the Gibbs measure of parameter
\beta
on
\mathbb{T}_{n}
to be

\displaystyle\mu_{\beta,n}(u)=e^{\beta X_{u}-\varphi(\beta)m}\cdot\frac{W^{u}_{\beta,n-m}}{W_{\beta,n}},\quad\absolutevalue{u}=m\leq n.

(1.2)

Note that
\mu_{\beta,n}
is usually defined on
\partial\mathbb{T}_{n}
only, but it will be helpful to define it on the whole tree
\mathbb{T}_{n}
. By (
1.1
), for every
m\leq n
, the restriction of
\mu_{\beta,n}
to
\partial\mathbb{T}_{m}
is a probability measure. Similarly, we can define

\displaystyle\mu_{\beta,n}^{v}(u)=e^{\beta X^{v}_{u}-\varphi(\beta)m}\cdot\frac{W^{vu}_{\beta,n-m}}{W^{v}_{\beta,n}},\quad v\in\mathbb{T}\text{ and }\absolutevalue{u}=m\leq n.

(1.3)

The free energy of the branching random walk has been calculated by Derrida and Spohn
[
16
]
(and can also be deduced from Biggins
[
10
]
)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\beta n}\log\sum_{\absolutevalue{v}=n}e^{\beta X_{v}}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\beta}\varphi(\beta)&\text{if $\beta\in(0,\beta_{c})$}\\
\frac{1}{\beta_{c}}\varphi(\beta_{c})&\text{if $\beta\geq\beta_{c}$},\end{cases}

where the limit is meant to be in probability and where the critical inverse temperature
\beta_{c}
is defined by

\beta_{c}=\sup\{\beta\in\mathcal{D}(\varphi)^{\circ}\mid\beta\varphi^{\prime}(\beta)<\varphi(\beta)\}\in(0,\infty].

We will mostly be interested in the phase

. In this phase, we recall the following result.

Fact 1.2

(
[
21
,
11
,
24
]
)
.

If
\beta<\beta_{c}
, then the martingale
(W_{\beta,n})_{n\geq 0}
is uniformly integrable. In fact, there exists a (strictly) positive random variable
W_{\beta,\infty}
with
\mathbb{E}[W_{\beta,\infty}]=1
and such that
W_{\beta,n}\to W_{\beta,\infty}
almost surely and in

We define an algorithmic model similar to the ones in
[

1
]
.

. Here,

\tilde{\mathscr{F}}_{k}=\sigma\left(v(1),\ldots,v(k);\,X_{v(1)},\ldots,X_{v(k)};\,U_{1},\ldots,U_{k+1}\right)

and such that

Often, we will consider a family of algorithms indexed by

We say that the algorithm is a

such that

Approximation and threshold.

Given two probability measures
P
and
Q
defined on a discrete space
\Omega
, the entropy of
Q
and the Kullback–Leibler divergence from
Q
to
P
are respectively defined by

\displaystyle H(Q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}Q(\omega)\cdot\log\left(\frac{1}{Q(\omega)}\right)

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}(P\;||\;Q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}P(\omega)\cdot\log\left(\frac{P(\omega)}{Q(\omega)}\right).

(1.6)

Note that by Jensen’s inequality, the entropy and the Kullback–Leibler divergence are non-negative. In what follows, we will often take
P
and/or
Q
to be a Gibbs measure
\mu_{\beta,N}
for some
N
. In that case, we set
\Omega=\partial\mathbb{T}_{n}
, where
n
is the largest number such that both
P
and
Q
are defined on
\partial\mathbb{T}_{n}
.

The following lemma is folklore. For completeness, we provide a proof in Section
6
.

Lemma 1.4
.

1.

If
\beta\in[0,\beta_{c})
, then
H(\mu_{\beta,N})/N
converges in probability to a positive constant as
N\to\infty
.

2.

If
\beta>\beta_{c}
and if
\beta_{c}\in\mathcal{D}(\varphi)^{\circ}
, then
H(\mu_{\beta,N})=O(1)
in probability, as
N\to\infty
. In other words, the sequence of random variables
(H(\mu_{\beta,N}))_{N\geq 1}
is tight.

We are interested in the
algorithmically efficient approximation
of the Gibbs measure
\mu_{\beta,N}
. The notion of approximation we will use is the following.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
\beta\geq 0
. We say that a sequence of random probability measures
(\tilde{\mu}_{\beta,N})_{N\geq 1}

approximates
the Gibbs measure
\mu_{\beta,N}
if

\frac{\mathbf{d}(\tilde{\mu}_{\beta,N}\;||\;\mu_{\beta,N})}{H(\mu_{\beta,N})}\to 0\;\;\text{in probability as $N\to\infty$}.

(1.7)

Remark 1.6
.

More generally, assume that we are given two sequences of probability measures
(P_{N})_{N\geq 1}
and
(Q_{N})_{N\geq 1}
satisfying
H(Q_{N})/N\to C\in(0,\infty)
and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{d}(P_{N}\;||\;Q_{N})\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as}\quad N\rightarrow\infty.

(1.8)

This has been called
measure equivalence
or
equivalence in the sense of specific relative entropy
in the physics literature
[
31
]
.
Mathematically, Equation (
1.8
) implies the following: if
(A_{N})_{N\geq 1}
is a sequence of sets such that
Q_{N}(A_{N})
convergences to
0
exponentially fast as
N\rightarrow\infty
, then we also have
P_{N}(A_{N})\to 0
. Indeed, this is an easy consequence of Birgé’s inequality (see e.g. Theorem 4.20 in
[
13
]
).

Main results.

We now state the main theorem of this paper.

Theorem 1.7

(Approximation bounds)
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
M\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket
, and
\beta\in[0,\beta_{c})
. Then for all
p\geq 1
, there exists a constant
C_{1}(p)>0
such that

\displaystyle\norm{\kld{\mu_{\beta,M,N}}{\mu_{\beta,N}}}_{p}\leq C_{1}(p)\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{N}{M}\right\rfloor.

(1.9)

Moreover, for all
p\geq 1
, there exists a constant
C_{1}(p)>0
such that

\displaystyle\norm{\kld{\mu_{\beta,M,N}}{\mu_{\beta,N}}-\EX{\kld{\mu_{\beta,M,N}}{\mu_{\beta,N}}}}_{p}\leq C_{1}(p).

(1.10)

By Theorem
1.7
, we can derive the following corollary which states the existence of an algorithm that approximates the Gibbs measure
\mu_{\beta,N}
efficiently.

Corollary 1.8

(Complexity upper bound)
.

If
\beta\in[0,\beta_{c})
, then
there exists a polynomial-time algorithm such that for every
p>1
, denoting by
\tilde{\mu}_{\beta,N}
the law of its output,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\norm{\kld{\tilde{\mu}_{\beta,N}}{\mu_{\beta,N}}}_{p}\rightarrow 0

(1.11)

as
N\rightarrow\infty
. In particular,
\tilde{\mu}_{\beta,N}
approximates the Gibbs measure
\mu_{\beta,N}
in the sense of Definition
1.5
.

Proof.

Let
M=M(N)
be a sequence that goes to infinity as
N\rightarrow\infty
, and set
\tilde{\mu}_{\beta,N}=\mu_{\beta,M,N}
. Equation (
1.11
) then follows from (
1.9
) in Theorem
1.7
. Assuming moreover that
M=O(\log N)
, Remark
1.3
implies that
\tilde{\mu}_{\beta,N}
can be computed by a polynomial-time algorithm. The second statement follows from the first part of Lemma
1.4
.
∎

Finally, we also provide a hardness result, assuming a mild regularity condition.

Theorem 1.9

(Complexity lower bound)
.

Assume
\beta_{c}\in\mathcal{D}(\varphi)^{\circ}
(in particular,

).
Let
\beta>\beta_{c}

Related work

An early study on searching algorithms on the branching random walk can be found in Karp and Pearl
[
22
]
. They considered the binary branching random walk with Bernoulli increments
\text{Ber}(p)
, and they showed that for
p>1/2
and
p=1/2
, they gave an algorithm that can find an exact maximal vertex in linear and quadratic expected time, respectively. While for
p<1/2
,
it is possible to find an approximate maximal vertex in linear time with high probability using a depth-first search on a renormalized tree. Aldous
[
5
]
gave a different algorithm and, among other things, extended the result of Karp and Pearl
[
22
]
to general increment distributions. A hardness result was obtained by Pemantle
[
28
]
. Among other things, he showed for the binary branching random walk with Bernoulli increments with mean
p<1/2
that any search algorithm which finds a vertex within a
(1-\varepsilon)
factor of the maximum with high probability needs at least
N\times\exp(\Theta(\varepsilon^{-1/2}))
with high probability.

1

To be precise, this explicit bound relied on a conjecture on branching random walk killed at a linear space-time barrier (Conjecture 1 in
[
28
]
), which was subsequently proven to be true
[
9
,
19
]
.

As mentioned above, Addario-Berry and Maillard
[
1
]
considered this optimization problem for the continuous random energy model (CREM), which is a binary time-inhomogeneous branching random walk with Gaussian displacements, proving the existence of an threshold
x_{*}
such that the following holds: a) for every
x<x_{*}
, there exists a polynomial-time algorithm that finds a vertex with
X_{v}\geq xN
with high probability, b) for every
x>x_{*}
, every such algorithm has a running time which is at least exponential in
N
with high probability.

The CREM, introduced by Bovier and Kurkova
[
14
]
based on previous work by Derrida and Spohn
[
16
]
, is a toy model of a disordered system in statistical physics, i.e. a model where the Hamiltonian – the function that assigns energies to the states of the system – is itself random. These systems have recently seen a lot of interest in the mathematical literature with regards to efficient algorithms for finding low-energy states. A key quantity of importance in these models is the so-called
overlap
between two states, a measure of their correlation. In the case of the CREM, it is equal to the depth of the most recent common ancestor of two vertices, divided by
N
. Then, for a given
\beta>0
, the
overlap distribution
is the limiting law (as
N\to\infty
) of the overlap of two vertices sampled independently according to the Gibbs measure with inverse temperature
\beta
. A picture that has emerged is that the existence of a gap in the support of the overlap distribution, the so-called “overlap gap property”, is an obstruction to the existence of efficient algorithms finding approximate minimizers of the Hamiltonian. This has been rigorously proven for a certain class of algorithms by Gamarnik and Jagannath
[
18
]
in the case of the Ising
p
-spin model, with
p\geq 4
. On the other hand, Montanari
[
26
]
showed that for
p=2
, the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model, there exists a quadratic time algorithm that can find near optimal state with high probability, assuming a widely believed conjecture that this model does not exhibit an overlap gap. A similar result for spherical spin glass models (for which the overlap distribution is explicitly known) has been obtained by Subag
[
30
]
.

The question of efficient
sampling
of the Gibbs measure of a disordered system seems to have been considered mostly under the angle of Glauber or Langevin dynamics. See e.g.
[
7
,
20
]
for the spherical spin glass model. We restrict ourselves here to the case of the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model. For this model, it has been recently obtained that fast mixing occurs for
\beta<1/4

[
8
,
17
]
. However, very recently, El Alaoui, Montanari and Sellke
[
3
]
have provided another algorithm which yields fast mixing for
\beta<1/2
, and they conjecture that this in fact holds for all
\beta<1
. They also provide a hardness result for
\beta>1
for a certain class of algorithms. Their algorithm for the
\beta<1/2
phase belongs to the class of
approximate message passing
algorithms, which is also the case for Montanari’s algorithm for the optimization problem
[
26
]
. This illustrates the fact that Glauber or Langevin dynamics may in general not be optimal sampling algorithms, and that algorithms which exploit the underlying tree structure of the model may be efficient in a wider range of the parameters. For a discussion of this question in the context of statistical inference problems, see e.g.
[
6
]
. Altogether, this motivates the study of tree-based models as a toy problem, such as the one from the present article.

2
Decomposition of the Kullback–Leibler divergence

The main goal of this section is to prove Theorem
2.2
. Before proving the theorem, we need the following lemma, which states that the weight of
u_{1}u_{2}
with respect to the Gibbs measure
\mu_{\beta,m}
can be decomposed into the product of the weights of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
with respect to another two Gibbs measures.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any
\absolutevalue{u_1}=m_{1}
and
\absolutevalue{u_2}=m_{2}
, we have the decomposition

\displaystyle\mu_{\beta,m}(u_{1}u_{2})=\mu_{\beta,m}(u_{1})\cdot\mu_{\beta,m-m_{1}}^{u_{1}}(u_{2})

for all
m\geq m_{1}+m_{2}
.

Proof.

By computation, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\beta,m}(u_{1}u_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\beta X_{u_{1}u_{2}}-\varphi(\beta)m}\cdot W_{\beta,m-m_{1}-m_{2}}^{u_{1}u_{2}}}{W_{\beta,m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\beta X_{u_{1}}-\varphi(\beta)m_{1}}\cdot W_{\beta,m-m_{1}}^{u_{1}}}{W_{\beta,m}}\cdot\frac{e^{\beta X_{u_{2}}^{u_{1}}-\varphi(\beta)(m-m_{1})}\cdot W_{\beta,m-m_{1}-m_{2}}^{u_{1}u_{2}}}{W_{\beta,m-m_{1}}^{u_{1}}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{\beta,m}(u_{1})\cdot\mu_{\beta,m-m_{1}}^{u_{1}}(u_{2}),

where the last equality is by (
1.2
) and (
1.3
).
∎

Now we can decompose the Kullback–Leibler divergence as follows.

Theorem 2.2
.

For any two
M
and
N
integers such that
M\leq N
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}(\mu_{\beta,M,N}\;||\;\mu_{\beta,N})=\sum_{K=0}^{\lfloor\frac{N}{M}\rfloor-1}\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)\cdot\mathbf{d}(\mu_{\beta,M}^{u}\;||\;\mu_{\beta,N-KM}^{u}).

(2.1)

Proof.

Denote
N^{\prime}=\lfloor\frac{N}{M}\rfloor\cdot M
for simplicity.
By (
1.4
) and Lemma
2.1
, for all
\absolutevalue{u_1}=N^{\prime}
and
\absolutevalue{u_2}=N-N^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\mu_{\beta,M,N}(u_{1}u_{2})}{\mu_{\beta,N}(u_{1}u_{2})}\right)=\log\left(\frac{\mu_{\beta,M,N^{\prime}}(u_{1})\cdot\mu_{\beta,N-N^{\prime}}^{u_{1}}(u_{2})}{\mu_{\beta,N}(u_{1})\cdot\mu_{\beta,N-N^{\prime}}^{u_{1}}(u_{2})}\right)=\log\left(\frac{\mu_{\beta,M,N^{\prime}}(u_{1})}{\mu_{\beta,M,N}(u_{1})}\right).

(2.2)

Thus, the Kullback–Leibler divergence can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}(\mu_{\beta,M,N}\;||\;\mu_{\beta,N})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=N}\mu_{\beta,M,N}(u)\cdot\log\left(\frac{\mu_{\beta,M,N}(u)}{\mu_{\beta,N}(u)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\absolutevalue{u_1}=N^{\prime}}\sum_{\absolutevalue{u_2}=N-N^{\prime}}\mu_{\beta,M,N}(u_{1}u_{2})\cdot\log\left(\frac{\mu_{\beta,M,N}(u_{1}u_{2})}{\mu_{\beta,N}(u_{1}u_{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\absolutevalue{u_1}=N^{\prime}}\sum_{\absolutevalue{u_2}=N-N^{\prime}}\mu_{\beta,M,N^{\prime}}(u_{1})\cdot\mu_{\beta,N-N^{\prime}}^{u_{1}}(u_{2})\cdot\log\left(\frac{\mu_{\beta,M,N^{\prime}}(u_{1})}{\mu_{\beta,N}(u_{1})}\right)

(by (
1.4
) and (
2.2
))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\absolutevalue{u_1}=N^{\prime}}\mu_{\beta,M,N^{\prime}}(u_{1})\cdot\log\left(\frac{\mu_{\beta,M,N^{\prime}}(u_{1})}{\mu_{\beta,N}(u_{1})}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{d}(\mu_{\beta,M,N^{\prime}}\;||\;\mu_{\beta,N}).

(2.3)

Next, by (
1.4
) and Lemma
2.1
, for all
\absolutevalue{u_1}=KM
,
\absolutevalue{u_2}=M
and
0\leq K\leq\lfloor\frac{N}{M}\rfloor-1
, we have

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\mu_{\beta,M,(K+1)M}(u_{1}u_{2})}{\mu_{\beta,N}(u_{1}u_{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u_{1})\cdot\mu_{\beta,M}^{u_{1}}(u_{2})}{\mu_{\beta,N}(u_{1})\cdot\mu_{\beta,N-KM}^{u_{1}}(u_{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u_{1})}{\mu_{\beta,N}(u_{1})}\right)+\log\left(\frac{\mu_{\beta,M}^{u_{1}}(u_{2})}{\mu_{\beta,N-KM}^{u_{1}}(u_{2})}\right).

(2.4)

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}(\mu_{\beta,M,(K+1)M}\;||\;\mu_{\beta,N})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=(K+1)M}\mu_{\beta,M,(K+1)M}(u)\cdot\log\left(\frac{\mu_{\beta,M,(K+1)M}(u)}{\mu_{\beta,N}(u)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\absolutevalue{u_1}=KM}\sum_{\absolutevalue{u_2}=M}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\mu_{\beta,M}^{u_{1}}(u_{2})\cdot\left[\log\left(\frac{\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u_{1})}{\mu_{\beta,N}(u_{1})}\right)+\log\left(\frac{\mu_{\beta,M}^{u_{1}}(u_{2})}{\mu_{\beta,N-KM}^{u_{1}}(u_{2})}\right)\right]

(by (
1.4
) and (
2.4
))

\displaystyle=\mathbf{d}(\mu_{\beta,M,KM}\;||\;\mu_{\beta,N})+\sum_{\absolutevalue{u_1}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u_{1})\cdot\mathbf{d}(\mu_{\beta,M}^{u_{1}}\;||\;\mu_{\beta,N-KM}^{u_{1}}).

(2.5)

Finally, by (
2.3
), (
2.5
) and the fact that
\mathbf{d}(\mu_{\beta,M,0}\;||\;\mu_{\beta,N})=0
, we derive (
2.1
).
∎

Next, we show that the Kullback–Leibler
divergence between two Gibbs measures can be written in terms of the logarithms of the partition functions.

Proposition 2.3
.

For any two
M
and
N
integers such that
M\leq N
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}(\mu_{\beta,M}\;||\;\mu_{\beta,N})=\log W_{\beta,N}-\log W_{\beta,M}-\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u)\cdot\log W^{u}_{\beta,N-M}.

(2.6)

Proof.

For any
\absolutevalue{u}=M
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{\beta,M}(u)}{\mu_{\beta,N}(u)}=\frac{e^{\beta X_{u}-\varphi(\beta)M}\cdot\frac{1}{W_{\beta,M}}}{e^{\beta X_{u}-\varphi(\beta)M}\cdot\frac{W_{\beta,N-M}^{u}}{W_{\beta,N}}}=\frac{W_{\beta,N}}{W_{\beta,M}\cdot W^{u}_{\beta,N-M}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}(\mu_{\beta,M}\;||\;\mu_{\beta,N})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u)\cdot\log\left(\frac{\mu_{\beta,M}(u)}{\mu_{\beta,N}(u)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u)\cdot\left(\log W_{\beta,N}-\log W_{\beta,M}-\log W_{\beta,N-M}^{u}\right)

\displaystyle=\log W_{\beta,N}-\log W_{\beta,M}-\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u)\cdot\log W^{u}_{\beta,N-M}.

This completes the proof.
∎

3
Some
L^{p}
bounds

We first show that whenever
\beta\in[0,\beta_{c})
,
(\log W_{\beta,n})_{n\geq 0}
is bounded in
L^{p}
for all
p>1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\beta\in[0,\beta_{c})
. Then
(\log W_{\beta,n})_{n\geq 0}
is a supermartingale such that

\sup_{n\geq 0}\norm{\log W_{\beta,n}}_{p}<\infty,\quad p\geq 1.

Proof.

The supermartingale property of
(\log W_{\beta,n})_{n\geq 0}
follows from the fact that
(W_{\beta,n})_{n\geq 0}
is a martingale and
x\mapsto\log x
is a concave function.

Now let
p\geq 1
. Since
\beta<\beta_{c}
, by Fact
1.2
, we derive that

\sup_{n\geq 0}\norm{W_{\beta,n}}_{1}<\infty

Furthermore, using Assumption
1.1
and Liu
[
23
, Theorem 2.4]
, we have for some
s>0
,

\sup_{n\geq 0}\|W_{\beta,n}^{-s}\|_{1}<\infty.

Then by the fact that

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\log x}^{p}\leq C\left(\absolutevalue{x}+\absolutevalue{x}^{-s}\right)

for some constant
C>0
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq 0}\norm{\log W_{\beta,n}}_{p}^{p}\leq C\cdot\sup_{n\geq 0}\left(\norm{W_{\beta,n}}_{1}+\|W_{\beta,n}^{-s}\|_{1}\right)<\infty.

This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma
3.1
implies the following proposition about the boundedness of the Kullback–Leibler divergence between two Gibbs measures.

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
p>1
, for any two integers
M
and
N
such that
M\leq N
, there exists a constant
C(p)>0
such that

\norm{\kld{\mu_{\beta,M}}{\mu_{\beta,N}}}_{p}\leq C(p).

Proof.

By Minkowski’s inequality and Proposition
2.3
, we have

\displaystyle\norm{\kld{\mu_{\beta,M}}{\mu_{\beta,N}}}_{p}\leq\norm{\log W_{\beta,N}}_{p}+\norm{\log W_{\beta,M}}_{p}+\norm{\sum_{\abs{u}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u)\cdot\log W^u_{\beta,N-M}}_{p}.

Since the first and the second term above are bounded by Lemma
3.1
, it suffices to prove that the third term above is bounded. By the branching property and Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{\sum_{\abs{u}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u)\cdot\log W^u_{\beta,M}}^{p}\,\middle|\,\mathscr{F}_{M}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{\log W_{\beta,N-M}}^{p}\right].

(3.1)

Therefore, by (
3.1
),

\displaystyle\norm{\sum_{\abs{u}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u)\cdot\log W^u_{\beta,N-M}}_{p}\leq\norm{\log W_{\beta,N-M}}_{p}.

(3.2)

Finally, we conclude by (
3.2
) and Lemma
3.1
that

\displaystyle\norm{\kld{\mu_{\beta,M}}{\mu_{\beta,N}}}_{p}\leq 3\cdot\sup_{n\geq 0}\norm{\log W_{\beta,n}}_{p}<\infty,

and the proof is completed.
∎

4
Proof of Theorem
1.7

In this section, we prove (
1.9
) and (
1.10
) of Theorem
1.7
. The proof of (
1.9
) relies essentially on the decomposition theorem of the Kullback–Leibler divergence (Theorem
2.2
) and Proposition
3.2
. The proof of (
1.10
) needs more precise moment estimates.

4.1
Proof of (
1.9
)

Let
p\geq 1
. By Theorem
2.2
and Minkowski’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\norm{\kld{\mu_{\beta,M,N}}{\mu_{\beta,N}}}_{p}

\displaystyle=\norm{\sum_{K=0}^{\floor{\frac{N}{M}}-1} \sum_{\abs{u}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)\cdot\kld{\mu_{\beta,M}^u}{\mu_{\beta,N-KM}^u}}_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K=0}^{\lfloor\frac{N}{M}\rfloor-1}\norm{ \sum_{\abs{u}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)\cdot\kld{\mu_{\beta,M}^u}{\mu_{\beta,N-KM}^u}}_{p}.

(4.1)

Let
K\leq\lfloor N/M\rfloor-1
. Applying Jensen’s inequality to
\mu_{\beta,M,KM}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{\sum_{\abs{u}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)\cdot\kld{\mu_{\beta,M}^u}{\mu_{\beta,N-KM}^u}}^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)\cdot\absolutevalue{\kld{\mu_{\beta,M}^u}{\mu_{\beta,N-KM}^u}}^{p}\right].

(4.2)

Then by the law of iterated expectation and the branching property, (
4.2
) is equal to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{\kld{\mu_{\beta,M}^u}{\mu_{\beta,N-KM}^u}}^{p}\,\middle|\,\mathscr{F}_{KM}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{\kld{\mu_{\beta,M}}{\mu_{\beta,N-KM}}}^{p}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{\kld{\mu_{\beta,M}}{\mu_{\beta,N-KM}}}^{p}\right].

(4.3)

Combining (
4.1
), (
4.3
) and Proposition
3.2
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\norm{\kld{\mu_{\beta,M,N}}{\mu_{\beta,N}}}_{p}\leq\left\lfloor\frac{N}{M}\right\rfloor\cdot C(p).

4.2
Proof of (
1.10
)

In this section, we prove (
1.10
) which gives a tighter control on the Kullback–Leibler divergence between
\mu_{\beta,M,N}
and the Gibbs measure
\mu_{\beta,N}
. We start with the following simple lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\beta\in[0,\beta_{c})
. For all
M\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
r\in(0,1)
independent of
M
such that,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u)^{2}\right]\leq r

(4.4)

Moreover, for all
K\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)^{2}\right]\leq r^{K}.

(4.5)

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

For
x\in(0,1)
,
x^{2}<x
. Thus, by the fact that
\mu_{\beta,M}(u)\in(0,1)
for all
\absolutevalue{u}=M
, we derive that

\displaystyle\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u)^{2}<\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u)=1,

for every
M\in\mathbb{N}
. This shows that (
4.4
) holds for every fixed
M\in\mathbb{N}
and with
r<1
possibly depending on
M
. Uniformity in
M
follows as soon as we show that

\limsup_{M\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u)^{2}\right]<1.

To this end, recall that
W_{\beta,M}\to W_{\beta,\infty}
almost surely as
M\to\infty
and that
\mathbb{E}[W_{\beta,\infty}]=1
. Hence, there exist
a<1
and
M_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\forall M\geq M_{0}:\mathbb{P}(W_{\beta,M}<1/2)\leq a.

(4.6)

Now fix
p\in(1,2]
such that
\varphi(p\beta)<p\varphi(\beta)
, which exists because
\beta<\beta_{c}
. Then decompose:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u)^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u)^{p}\right)\boldsymbol{1}_{W_{\beta,M}<1/2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u)^{p}\right)\boldsymbol{1}_{W_{\beta,M}\geq 1/2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(W_{\beta,M}<1/2)+2^{p}e^{(\varphi(p\beta)-p\varphi(\beta))M},

using the definition of
\mu_{\beta,M}(u)
for the last inequality. This shows that

\limsup_{M\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u)^{2}\right]\leq a,

which concludes the proof of (
4.4
).

We now show (
4.5
) by induction. The case
K=1
follows directly from (
1.4
) and (
4.4
).
For
K>1
, by (
1.4
) and branching property, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathscr{F}_{(K-1)M}\right]

(4.7)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u_1}=(K-1)M}\sum_{\absolutevalue{u_2}=M}\mu_{\beta,M,(K-1)M}(u_{1})^{2}\cdot\mu_{\beta,M}^{u_{1}}(u_{2})^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathscr{F}_{(K-1)M}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\absolutevalue{u_1}=(K-1)M}\mu_{\beta,M,(K-1)M}(u_{1})^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u_2}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u_{2})^{2}\right].

(4.8)

Taking expectations, we derive that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=(K-1)M}\mu_{\beta,M,(K-1)M}(u)^{2}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=M}\mu_{\beta,M}(u)^{2}\right].

(4.9)

The equation (
4.5
) then follows from the induction hypothesis.
∎

We now proceed with the proof of (
1.10
). Without loss of generality, using the fact that
\|\cdot\|_{p}\leq\|\cdot\|_{2}
for every
p\in[1,2]
, we assume that
p\geq 2
. By Theorem
2.2
and Minkowski’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\norm{\kld{\mu_{\beta,M,N}}{\mu_{\beta,N}}-\EX{\kld{\mu_{\beta,M,N}}{\mu_{\beta,N}}}}_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K=0}^{\lfloor\frac{N}{M}\rfloor-1}\norm{\sum_{\abs{u}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)\cdot\left(\kld{\mu_{\beta,M}^u}{\mu_{\beta,N-KM}^u}-\EX{\kld{\mu_{\beta,M}^u}{\mu_{\beta,N-KM}^u}}\right)}_{p}.

(4.10)

We now introduce some notation. For all
0\leq K\leq\lfloor\frac{N}{M}\rfloor-1
, denote

\displaystyle d_{K}^{u}=\mathbf{d}(\mu_{\beta,M}^{u}\;||\;\mu_{\beta,N-KM}^{u})\quad\text{and}\quad d_{K}=\mathbf{d}(\mu_{\beta,M}\;||\;\mu_{\beta,N-KM})

and

\displaystyle Z_{u}=\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)\cdot\left(d_{K}^{u}-\mathbb{E}\left[d_{K}^{u}\right]\right).

We claim that for all
p\geq 1
, the sequence

\displaystyle a_{K}=\norm{\sum_{\abs{u}=KM}Z_u}_{p}

(4.11)

is summable, with a bound independent of
M
. This will imply that the right-hand side of (
4.10
) is bounded by the same quantity, which completes proof. To prove this, first observe that
(Z_{u})_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}
is a sequence of iid random variables having zero mean and finite
p
-th moments, for any
p\geq 1
, with respect to
\mathbb{E}\left[\,\cdot\,\middle|\,\mathscr{F}_{KM}\right]
, by Proposition
3.2
and the branching property. Denote by
C_{1},C_{2},\ldots
some constants possibly depending on
p
(and
\beta
and the law of
\mathbf{Y}
). By Rosenthal’s inequality
[
29
, Theorem 3]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{\sum_{\abs{u}=KM} Z_u}^{p}\,\middle|\,\mathscr{F}_{KM}\right]\leq C_{1}\cdot\left\{\left(\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{Z_u}^{p}\,\middle|\,\mathscr{F}_{KM}\right]\right)+\left(\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{Z_u}^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathscr{F}_{KM}\right]\right)^{p/2}\right\}

(4.12)

By the branching property and Proposition
3.2
,
the first term of (

\displaystyle\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{Z_u}^{p}\,\middle|\,\mathscr{F}_{KM}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)^{p}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{d_K-\EX{d_K}}^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)^{p}\cdot C_{2}

(4.13)

using that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{Z_u}^{p}\,\middle|\,\mathscr{F}_{KM}\right]\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)^{2}\right]\cdot C_{2}\leq r^{K}\cdot C_{2},

with

.

We now estimate the second term of (

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{Z_u}^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathscr{F}_{KM}\right]\right)^{p/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{d_K-\EX{d_K}}^{2}\right]\right)^{p/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)^{2}\right)^{p/2}\cdot C_{3}

(4.15)

The inequality (

and
x^{p/2}\leq x
, for all

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{Z_u}^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathscr{F}_{KM}\right]\right)^{p/2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\absolutevalue{u}=KM}\mu_{\beta,M,KM}(u)^{2}\right]\cdot C_{3}\leq r^{K}\cdot C_{3}.

(4.16)

Combining (

5
Proof of hardness result (Theorem
1.9
)

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.9
. To do this, we recall two results of asymptotic behaviors of the maximal particle of a branching random walk.

Under the assumption of the theorem, it is known in Corollary (3.4) of
[
10
]
that

\frac{\max_{|u|=N}X_{u}}{N}\to m\coloneqq\varphi^{\prime}(\beta_{c}),\quad\text{a.s. as $N\to\infty$}.

(5.1)

Furthermore, we have the following tail estimate, which easily follows from a union bound together with Chernoff’s bound (see e.g. the proof of Theorem 2 in
[
32
]
): there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\forall x\geq 0:\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{|u|=N}X_{u}\geq mN+x\right)\leq e^{-cx}.

(5.2)

The key to Theorem
1.9
is the following observation:

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\beta>\beta_{c}
and assume
\beta_{c}\in\mathcal{D}(\varphi)^{\circ}
. Let
u
be a particle sampled according to the Gibbs measure
\mu_{\beta,N}
and let
w
be its ancestor at generation
\lfloor N/2\rfloor
. Then there exists a positive random variable
Z
with continuous distribution function such that

\frac{X_{u}-X_{w}-mN/2}{\sqrt{N}}\to Z,\quad\text{in law as $N\to\infty$}.

Proof.

This is a consequence of a result by Chen, Madaule and Mallein
[
15
]
. These authors show the following fact: if
u
is sampled according to the Gibbs measure
\mu_{\beta,N}
, and
X_{u}(t)
denotes the position of its ancestor at generation
\lfloor tN\rfloor
, and if we define

Z^{N}_{t}\coloneqq\frac{mtN-X_{u}(t)}{\sqrt{N}},\quad t\in[0,1],

then
(Z^{N}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
converges in law (w.r.t. Skorokhod’s topology) to a multiple of a Brownian excursion as
N\to\infty
. Note that the assumptions in their article are implied by our hypothesis that
\beta_{c}\in\mathcal{D}(\varphi)^{\circ}
and the fact that in our branching random walk, the number of offspring of a particle is deterministic. Now, we also have that
X_{u}-mN=O(\log N)
in probability (see e.g.
[
2
]
), so that

\frac{X_{u}-mN}{\sqrt{N}}\xrightarrow{\text{law}}0,\quad\text{as $N\to\infty$}.

Together, both results imply the lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.9
.

Assume that we are given an algorithm
(v(k))_{k\geq 1}
that samples a vertex according to a random probability measure
\tilde{\mu}_{\beta,N}
approximating the Gibbs measure
\mu_{\beta,N}
. By Lemma
1.4
, it follows that
\mathbf{d}(\tilde{\mu}_{\beta,N}\;||\;\mu_{\beta,N})\to 0
in probability as
N\to\infty
. Hence, by Pinsker’s inequality (see e.g. Theorem 4.19 in
[
13
]
), the total variation distance between
\tilde{\mu}_{\beta,N}
and
\mu_{\beta,N}
goes to 0 as well in probability, as
N\to\infty
. It follows that Lemma
5.1
holds as well for
u
sampled according to
\tilde{\mu}_{\beta,N}
.

Let
\delta>0
. For
z>0
, call a vertex
w\in\partial\mathbb{T}_{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}

z
-exceptional
if it has a descendant
u\in\partial\mathbb{T}_{N}
such that
X_{u}-X_{w}-mN/2>z\sqrt{N}
. By the preceding paragraph, there exists
z>0
such that for large enough
N
, the algorithm finds a vertex
u
whose ancestor
w
at generation
\lfloor N/2\rfloor
is
z
-exceptional with probability at least
1-\delta
. Hence, it is enough to show that any algorithm which solves the simpler problem of finding a
z
-exceptional vertex at generation
\lfloor N/2\rfloor
has a running time at least
e^{z^{\prime}\sqrt{N}}
with probability
1-\delta
, for some
z^{\prime}>0
. We will now show that the statement of the theorem holds even for this simpler problem. For this, we use an argument similar to the one in Section 3 of
[
1
]
. We first present the argument in an informal way.

Denote by
E_{w}
the event that a given vertex
w\in\partial\mathbb{T}_{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}
is
z
-exceptional. Note that this event only depends on the displacements of the descendants of
w
. Hence, the events
(E_{w})_{w\in\partial\mathbb{T}_{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}}
are independent by the branching property. Furthermore, by (
5.2
), for each
w\in\partial\mathbb{T}_{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}
, we have
\mathbb{P}(E_{w})\leq e^{-cz\sqrt{N/2}}
for some
c>0
. Finally, in order to determine whether a vertex
w
is
z
-exceptional, the algorithm has to explore at least one vertex in the subtree of the vertex
w
. Hence, the running time of the algorithm is bounded from below by the number of vertices
w
that have to be probed in order to find a
z
-exceptional vertex. But this quantity follows the geometric distribution with success probability
\mathbb{P}(E_{w})\leq e^{cz\sqrt{N/2}}
. Altogether, for any
z^{\prime}<cz/\sqrt{2}
and for
N
sufficiently large, this shows that the running time
\tau
of any algorithm solving the simpler problem is at least
e^{z^{\prime}\sqrt{N}}
with probability
1-\delta
. The statement readily follows.

We now make this argument formal. Recall that, by definition, an algorithm is a stochastic process
(v(n))_{n\geq 0}
previsible with respect to the filtration
\tilde{\mathscr{F}}
, defined by

\tilde{\mathscr{F}}_{k}=\sigma\left(v(1),\ldots,v(k);\,X_{v(1)},\ldots,X_{v(k)};\,U_{1},\ldots,U_{k+1}\right)

where
(U_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is a sequence of iid uniform random variables on
[0,1]
, independent of the branching random walk
\mathbf{X}
. We now define a larger filtration
\mathscr{G}
. For this, define for any
v\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
the following set of vertices:

\mathcal{V}_{v}=\begin{cases}w\in\mathbb{T}_{N}:|v\wedge w|\geq\lfloor N/2\rfloor,&\text{if $|v|\geq\lfloor N/2\rfloor$}\\
v,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that
v\in\mathcal{V}_{v}
for every
v\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
.
We then set

\mathscr{G}_{k}=\sigma\left(v(1),\ldots,v(k);\,(X_{w})_{w\in\mathcal{V}_{v(1)}},\ldots,(X_{w})_{w\in\mathcal{V}_{v(k)}};\,U_{1},\ldots,U_{k+1}\right).

Note that
\tilde{\mathscr{F}}_{k}\subset\mathscr{G}_{k}
for all
k\geq 0
— heuristically,
\mathscr{G}_{k}
adds to
\tilde{\mathscr{F}}_{k}
the information about the values in the branching random walk of all vertices contained in
\mathcal{V}_{v(i)}
,
i=1,\ldots,k
. Note that trivially, the stochastic process
v(n)_{n\geq 0}
is still previsible with respect to this larger filtration
\mathscr{G}
.

Now say that
\mathcal{V}_{v}
is
z
-exceptional if
|v|\geq\lfloor N/2\rfloor
and the ancestor of
v
at generation
\lfloor N/2\rfloor
is
z
-exceptional in the sense defined above — note that this definition does not depend on the choice of
v
. Define

\tau^{\prime}=\inf\{k\geq 0:\mathcal{V}_{v(k)}\text{ is $z$-exceptional}\},

and note that
\tau^{\prime}
is a stopping time with respect to the filtration
\mathscr{G}
.
Now, by the equality of events

\{\tau^{\prime}=k\}=\{\text{$\mathcal{V}_{v(k)}$ is $z$-exceptional}\}\cap\{\tau^{\prime}>k-1\},

and since
\{\tau^{\prime}>k-1\}\in\mathscr{G}_{k-1}
and
v(k)
is
\mathscr{G}_{k-1}
-measurable, we have

\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}=k\,|\,\mathscr{G}_{k-1})=\sum_{v\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\mathbb{P}(\text{$\mathcal{V}_{v}$ is $z$-exceptional}\,|\,\mathscr{G}_{k-1})\boldsymbol{1}_{(v(k)=v,\ \tau^{\prime}>k-1)}.

Now, for any
v\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
, if
|v|<\lfloor N/2\rfloor
, or if
v\in\bigcup_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathcal{V}_{v(i)}
, the above probability is zero, because none of
\mathcal{V}_{v(i)}
,
i=0,\ldots,k-1
are
z
-exceptional on the event
\{\tau^{\prime}>k-1\}
. On the other hand, if
v\not\in\bigcup_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathcal{V}_{v(i)}
, then, by the branching property, the above conditional probability is equal to the unconditioned probability that
\mathcal{V}_{v}
is
z
-exceptional, which is bounded by
e^{-cz\sqrt{N/2}}
by (
5.2
). Hence, we get in total that

\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}=k|\,\tau^{\prime}>k-1)\leq e^{-cz\sqrt{N/2}},

and
\tau^{\prime}
is dominated from below by a geometric random variable with success probability
e^{-cz\sqrt{N/2}}
. The proof now continues as above.
∎

Proof of Lemma
1.4

We first consider the case
\beta\in[0,\beta_{c})
. Define

D_{\beta,N}=\derivative{\beta}W_{\beta,N}=\sum_{|u|=n}(X_{u}-\varphi^{\prime}(\beta)N)e^{\beta X_{u}-\varphi(\beta)N}.

We express the entropy by

\displaystyle H(\mu_{\beta,N})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{W_{\beta,N}}\sum_{|u|=N}(\varphi(\beta)N-\beta X_{u})e^{\beta X_{u}-\varphi(\beta)N}+\log W_{\beta,N}

\displaystyle=(\varphi(\beta)-\beta\varphi^{\prime}(\beta))N-\beta\frac{D_{\beta,N}}{W_{\beta,N}}+\log W_{\beta,N}.

By the assumption on
\beta
, we have
\varphi(\beta)-\beta\varphi^{\prime}(\beta)>0
. Furthermore,
W_{\beta,N}
converges almost surely to a positive random variable as
N\to\infty
by Fact
1.2
and
D_{\beta,N}
converges almost surely as well as
N\to\infty
, see
[
12
]
. The first statement follows.

Now let
\beta>\beta_{c}
and assume
\beta_{c}\in\mathcal{D}(\varphi)^{\circ}
. Define

\widetilde{W}_{\beta,N}=\sum_{|u|=N}e^{\beta(X_{u}-\varphi^{\prime}(\beta_{c})N-\frac{3}{2\beta_{c}}\log N)}.

We now write

H(\mu_{\beta,N})=\frac{1}{\widetilde{W}_{\beta,N}}\sum_{|u|=N}\left(-\beta\left(X_{u}-\varphi^{\prime}(\beta_{c})N-\frac{3}{2\beta_{c}}\log N\right)\right)e^{\beta(X_{u}-\varphi^{\prime}(\beta_{c})N-\frac{3}{2\beta_{c}}\log N)}+\log\widetilde{W}_{\beta,N}.

Fix
\beta^{\prime}\in(\beta_{c},\beta)
. Then there exists
C>0
, such that
xe^{-\beta x}\leq Ce^{-\beta^{\prime}x}
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We have

H(\mu_{\beta,N})\leq C\frac{\widetilde{W}_{\beta^{\prime},N}}{\widetilde{W}_{\beta,N}}+\log\widetilde{W}_{\beta,N}.

Now,
\widetilde{W}_{\beta,N}
and
\widetilde{W}_{\beta^{\prime},N}
converge almost surely as
N\to\infty
to positive random variables, see
[
25
]
. The second statement follows.
