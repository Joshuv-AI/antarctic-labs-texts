A New Inexact Gradient Descent Method with Applications to Nonsmooth Convex Optimization
Pham Duy Khanh, Boris S. Mordukhovich, Dat Ba Tran

Abstract




Dedicated to the memory of Andreas Griewank,

an outstanding mathematician and a wonderful human being

Abstract
. The paper proposes and develops a novel inexact gradient method (IGD) for minimizing
{\cal C}^{1}
-smooth functions with Lipschitzian gradients, i.e., for problems of
{\cal C}^{1,1}
optimization. We show that the sequence of gradients generated by IGD converges to zero. The convergence of iterates to stationary points is guaranteed under the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KL) property of the objective function with convergence rates depending on the KL exponent. The newly developed IGD is applied to designing two novel gradient-based methods of nonsmooth convex optimization such as the inexact proximal point methods (GIPPM) and the inexact augmented Lagrangian method (GIALM) for convex programs with linear equality constraints. These two methods inherit global convergence properties from IGD and are confirmed by numerical experiments to have practical advantages over some well-known algorithms of nonsmooth convex optimization.

Key words
: inexact gradient methods, inexact proximal point methods, inexact augmented Lagrangian methods,
{\cal C}^{1,1}
optimization, nonsmooth convex optimization

Introduction

The paper addresses numerical optimization, the area to which the contribution of Andreas Griewank is difficult to overstate. In particular, his book with Andrea Walther
[
25
]
is the classical collection of constructive methods of algorithmic differentiation in nonlinear optimization being a strong inspiration for a great many of applied mathematicians and practitioners.

This paper is devoted to developing new algorithms to solve various classes of optimization problems. Let us start with the following basic problem of
{\cal C}^{1,1}
optimization
:

\displaystyle{\rm minimize}\quad f(x)\quad\text{ subject to }\;x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},

(1.1)

where
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
is a continuously differentiable (
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth) objective function whose gradient
\nabla f
is globally Lipschitzian on
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
with some constant
L>0
. A very natural and classical approach to solve optimization problems of type (
1.1
) is by using the
gradient descent method
described as follows: given a starting point
x^{1}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, construct the iterative procedure

\displaystyle x^{k+1}:=x^{k}-\dfrac{1}{L}\nabla f(x^{k})\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

(1.2)

Largely due to its simplicity, the gradient descent method is broadly used to solve various optimization problems; see, e.g.,
[
7
,
13
,
17
,
40
,
41
]
. However, errors in gradient calculations may appear deterministically in various contexts. In derivative-free optimization problems where only the function values are accessible, the exact gradients are not available but only their approximations via finite differences and simplex gradients are
[
2
,
5
,
16
]
. In addition, many optimization methods for minimizing nonsmooth functions
[
18
,
37
,
43
,
45
,
46
,
48
,
49
]
can be treated via the gradient descent method applied to their regularized functions. Errors in gradient calculations for regularized functions, which appear in practical situations, require developing gradient descent methods with
inexact gradient information
.

For these reasons, gradient methods taking errors into account have been developed over the years. Among the major ones, we list the following. Gilmore and Kelley
[
24
]
proposed the implicit filtering algorithm for minimizing a noisy smooth function under box constraints, which uses finite difference approximations of the exact gradient. Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis
[
11
]
justified the convergence of gradients in gradient methods with errors smaller than the multiplication of the stepsize by an expression depending on the exact gradient. The convergence for incremental and stochastic gradient methods was also established in
[
11
]
. Nesterov proposed in
[
38
]
and further developed in
[
39
]
and his paper with Devolder and Glineur

[
18
]
gradient methods with inexact oracle for convex functions. Generalizations of the inexact oracle are discussed in
[
12
,
19
]
for nonconvex functions. Additionally, accelerated gradient methods considering various types of errors are analyzed in
[
51
]
. Optimization methods with stochastic errors are also studied in
[
22
,
22
]
. Quite recently, the authors of the present paper
[
31
]
proposed a general framework of inexact reduced gradient (IRG) methods for minimizing nonconvex
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth function with or without the Lipschitz continuity of the gradient under the condition that the distance between the exact and the approximate gradient is less than a determined error at each iteration. To the best of our knowledge,
[
31
]
is the first paper addressing the global convergence and convergence rates of inexact gradient descent methods with deterministic error for general classes of nonconvex functions.

The present paper designs and justifies the novel
inexact gradient descent
(IGD) method to solve problem (
1.1
) by using some IRG ideas from
[
31
]
. The iterative procedure of IGD is (
1.2
) with the exact gradient replaced by one of its approximations. The main differences between IGD and its predecessor IRG are that the construction of IGD in Algorithm
1
use directly an approximate gradient as a descent direction instead of introducing another reduced gradient direction as in
[
31
, Algorithm 1]
. The convergence analysis of the new IGD method does not involve the notion of null iterations which is essential in the results of
[
31
, Propositions 4.6, 4.7]
and the construction of non-null iteration as in
[
31
, (5.23)]
. The IGD method also achieves the following fundamental convergence properties:

•

Convergence to zero of the gradient sequence.

•

Convergence to some stationary point of the iterative sequence under the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KL) property of the objective function.

•

Convergence rates depending on the KL exponent of the objective function for the sequences of iterates, gradients, and function values.

It should be mentioned that the results in
[
18
,
38
,
39
]
address only convex optimization problems. Although the implicit filtering method in
[
24
]
deals with nonconvex problems, it does not achieve any of the fundamental convergence properties above due to the presence of noise. The gradient methods with errors in
[
11
]
establish only the convergence to zero of the gradient sequence. Moreover, the rates of convergence for the gradient sequence and function value sequence are not provided in
[
31
]
.

The IGD method developed in this paper for
{\cal C}^{1,1}
optimization problems of type (
1.1
) is applied then to problems of
nonsmooth optimization
to design and justify the following gradient-based methods:

•

The
gradient-based inexact proximal point method
(GIPPM) for minimizing general nonsmooth convex functions.

•

The
gradient-based inexact augmented Lagrangian method
(GIALM) for minimizing nonsmooth convex functions under linear equality constraints.

The two methods listed above inherit the convergence properties from IGD with establishing in this way the stationarity of accumulation points and the global convergence under the KL property of the objective function with convergence rates depending on the KL exponent. Regarding application aspects, the conducted numerical experiments show that the GIALM method with the new handling of errors exhibit a better performance than the classical inexact augmented Lagrangian method by Rockafellar
[
46
]
in practical problems of image processing as well as in random generated examples.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
presents some preliminaries and discussions. The construction and convergence analysis of the main IGD method are given in Section
3
. The next Section
4
, which is split into two subsections, proposes and justifies the new GIPPM and GIALM methods of nonsmooth convex optimization with their convergence analysis. The numerical experiments and comparisons between the newly developed inexact gradient-based methods with the existing algorithms are given in the final Section
5
.

2
Preliminaries and Discussions

First we recall some notions and notation frequently used in the paper. All our considerations are given in the space
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
with the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|
. We use the matrix norm defined by

\left\|A\right\|:=\max\big\{\left\|Ax\right\|\;\big|\;\left\|x\right\|=1\big\}\;\text{ for any }\;m\times n\text{ matrix }A.

Along with the Euclidean norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|
, in Section
5
we also use the
\ell_{1}
norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1}
denoted by

\displaystyle\left\|x\right\|_{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}|x_{k}|\;\text{ for all }\;x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

As always,
{\rm I\!N}:=\{1,2,\ldots\}
signifies the collections of natural numbers. The symbol
x^{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle J}}{{\to}}\bar{x}
means that
x^{k}\to\bar{x}
as
k\to\infty
with
k\in J\subset{\rm I\!N}
. Recall that
\bar{x}
is a
stationary point
of a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth function
f\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
if
\nabla f(\bar{x})=0
. A
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth function
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
is said to have a Lipschitz continuous gradient with the uniform constant
L>0
on
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
if

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\right\|\leq L\left\|x-y\right\|\;\text{ for all }\;x,y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

It follows from
[
28
, Lemma A.11]
that the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
with constant
L>0
implies that

\displaystyle f(y)\leq f(x)+\left\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\right\rangle+\dfrac{L}{2}\left\|y-x\right\|^{2}\;\text{ for all }\;x,y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(2.1)

The converse implication may not hold in general as discussed in
[
31
, Section 2]
.

The following
Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz property
is taken from Absil et al.
[
1
, Theorem 3.4]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
be a differentiable function. We say that
f
satisfies the
KL property
at
\bar{x}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
if there exist a number
\eta>0
, a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
, and a nondecreasing function
\psi:(0,\eta)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
such that the function
1/\psi
is integrable over
(0,\eta)
and we have

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|\geq\psi\big(f(x)-f(\bar{x})\big)\;\mbox{ for all }\;x\in U\;\mbox{ with }\;f(\bar{x})<f(x)<f(\bar{x})+\eta.

(2.2)

Remark 2.2
.

It has been realized that the KL property is satisfied in broad settings. In particular, it holds at every
nonstationary point
of
f
; see
[
3
, Lemma 2.1 and Remark 3.2(b)]
. Furthermore, it is proved at the seminal paper by Łojasiewicz
[
35
]
that any analytic function
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
satisfies the KL property at every point
\bar{x}
with
\psi(t)\penalty\ =\penalty\ Mt^{q}
for some
q\in[0,1)
. As demonstrated in
[
31
, Section 2]
, the KL property formulated in Attouch et al.
[
3
]
is stronger than the one in Definition
2.1
. Typical smooth functions that satisfy the KL property from
[
3
]
, and hence the one from Definition
2.1
, are smooth
semialgebraic
functions and also those from the more general class of functions known as
definable in o-minimal structures
; see
[
3
,
4
,
32
]
. The following preservation properties for general semialgebraic functions can be found, e.g., in
[
3
]
:

•

Finite sums and products of semialgebraic functions are semialgebraic.

•

Functions of the marginal type type

{\rm I\!R}^{n}\ni x\mapsto f(x)=\inf_{y\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}}g(x,y),

where
g
is a semialgebraic function of two variables, are semialgebraic.

Next we present based on
[
1
]
some descent-type conditions ensuring the global convergence of iterates for smooth functions that satisfy the KL property.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth function, and let the following conditions hold along a sequence of iterates
\left\{x^{k}\right\}\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}
for the function
f
:

(H1)

(primary descent condition)
. There is
\sigma>0
such that we have

\displaystyle f(x^{k})-f(x^{k+1})\geq\sigma\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\cdot\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|

for sufficiently large
k\in{\rm I\!N}
.

(H2)

(complementary descent condition)
. The following implication holds:

\displaystyle\big[f(x^{k+1})=f(x^{k})\big]\Longrightarrow[x^{k+1}=x^{k}]

for sufficiently large
k\in{\rm I\!N}
.

If
\bar{x}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
and
f
satisfies the KL property at
\bar{x}
, then
x^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}
as
k\to\infty
.

When the sequence under consideration is generated by a linesearch method and satisfies some stronger conditions than (H1) and (H2) in
Proposition
2.3
, its convergence rates are established in
[
31
]
under the KL property with
\psi(t)=Mt^{q}
as given below.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth function. Assume that the sequences
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
and
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
satisfy the iterative update
x^{k+1}=x^{k}+\tau d^{k}
with
x^{k+1}\neq x^{k}
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
, and that

\displaystyle f(x^{k})-f(x^{k+1})\geq\alpha\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}\;\text{ and }\;\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\beta\left\|d^{k}\right\|

(2.3)

for sufficiently large
k\in{\rm I\!N}
with some constants
\tau,\alpha,\beta>0
. Suppose in addition that
\bar{x}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
and that
f
satisfies the KL property at
\bar{x}
with
\psi(t)=Mt^{q}
for some
M>0
and
q\in(0,1)
. Then the following convergence rates are guaranteed:

(i)

For
q\in(0,1/2]
, the sequence
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
converges linearly to
\bar{x}
as
k\to\infty
.

(ii)

For
q\in(1/2,1)
, we have
\left\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\right\|=\mathcal{O}(k^{-\frac{1-q}{2q-1}})
as
k\to\infty

3
Inexact Gradient Descent Method

In this section, which is split into two subsections, we design and provide a convergence analysis of our main
inexact gradient method
(IGD) to solve
{\cal C}^{1,1}
optimization problems of type (
1.1
).

3.1
Algorithm Formulation and Motivating Examples

Here is the proposed IGD algorithm to solve (
1.1
) by using inexact gradients.

Algorithm 1

(IGD)
.

Step 0

(initialization)
.

Select an initial point
x^{1}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, initial error
\varepsilon_{1}
, error reduction factor
\theta\in(0,1)
, and error scaling factor
\mu>1.
Set
k:=1
.

Step 1

(inexact gradient)
.

Find
g^{k}
and the smallest natural number
i_{k}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}\text{ and }\left\|g^{k}\right\|>\mu\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}.

(3.1)

Step 2

(iteration and error update)
.

Set
x^{k+1}:=x^{k}-\frac{1}{L}g^{k}
and
\varepsilon_{k+1}:=\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}.
Increase
k
by
1
and go back to Step
1
.

Let us discuss some important features of Algorithm
1
and present an illustrating figure.

Remark 3.1
.

We have the following observations:

1.

The existence of
g^{k}
and
i_{k}
in Step
1: The procedure of finding
g^{k}
and
i_{k}
that satisfy Step
1
can be given as follows. Set
i_{k}=0
and find some
g^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}.

(3.2)

While
\left\|g^{k}\right\|\leq\mu\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}
, increase
i_{k}
by
1
and recalculate
g^{k}
under (
3.2
). When
\nabla f(x^{k})\neq 0
, the existence of
g^{k}
and
i_{k}
in Step 1 is guaranteed. Indeed, otherwise we get a sequence
\left\{g^{k}_{i}\right\}
with

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}_{i}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\theta^{i}\varepsilon_{k}\;\text{ and }\;\left\|g^{k}_{i}\right\|\leq\mu\theta^{i}\varepsilon_{k}\;\text{ for all }\;i\in{\rm I\!N}.

Since
\theta\in(0,1)
, this implies that
\nabla f(x^{k})=0
, a contradiction. In fact, we can bound the number
i_{k}
by considering the two cases as follows.

Case 1:

\varepsilon_{k}<\frac{1}{\mu+1}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|
. In this case, we show that
i_{k}=0.

To proceed, find some
g^{k}
such that
\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\varepsilon_{k}
. Combining this with
\varepsilon_{k}<\frac{1}{\mu+1}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|
and with the triangle inequality yields

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}\right\|

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|-\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|-\varepsilon_{k}

\displaystyle>\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|-\frac{1}{\mu+1}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{\mu+1}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|>\mu\varepsilon_{k}.

This means that
g^{k}
satisfies the desired condition with
i_{k}=0.

Case 2:

\varepsilon_{k}\geq\frac{1}{\mu+1}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|.
In this case, we show that
i_{k}\leq\log_{\theta}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{k}(\mu+1)}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\right)+1
.

To proceed, assume while arguing by contradiction that
i_{k}>\log_{\theta}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{k}(\mu+1)}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\right)+1
. Define
j:=i_{k}-1
and get that
j>0.
We show that for any
g^{k}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
such that
\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\theta^{j}\varepsilon_{k}
, the estimate
\left\|g^{k}\right\|>\theta^{j}\varepsilon_{k}
holds, which violates the construction of
i_{k}
. It follows from
j>\log_{\theta}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{k}(\mu+1)}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\right)
and
\theta\in(0,1)
that

\displaystyle\theta^{j}\varepsilon_{k}<\frac{1}{\mu+1}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|.

Now take any
g^{k}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
such that
\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\theta^{j}\varepsilon_{k}
and deduce that

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\theta^{j}\varepsilon_{k}<\frac{1}{\mu+1}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|.

Applying the triangle inequality gives us

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}\right\|

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|-\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|

\displaystyle>\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|-\frac{1}{\mu+1}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{\mu+1}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|>\mu\theta^{j}\varepsilon_{k}.

Therefore,
i_{k}\leq\log_{\theta}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{k}(\mu+1)}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\right)+1
.

2.

Geometric illustration of (
3.1
)
: It follows from Step 1 and Step 2 of Algorithm
1
that

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\varepsilon_{k+1}\;\text{ and }\;\left\|g^{k}\right\|>\mu\varepsilon_{k+1}\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

(3.3)

Since
\mu>1
, the two conditions in (
3.3
) make the angle between
\nabla f(x^{k})
and
g^{k}
smaller than
90^{0}
, which ensures that
-g^{k}
is a descent direction of
f
at
x^{k}
. As illustrated in Figure
1
, the angle between these vectors can be chosen arbitrarily small by increasing
\mu
.

:
Geometric illustration of (
3.1
)

Note that the problem of finding
g^{k}
satisfying the first condition in (
3.1
) can be considered more generally as follows. Given any point
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
and any error value
\varepsilon>0
, find an approximation
\mathcal{G}
of the gradient
\nabla f(x)
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq\varepsilon.

(3.4)

Let us present some examples where the approximate condition (
3.4
) appears in practical situations.

Example 1

(Gradient approximation methods)
.

In many problems of
derivative-free optimization

[
5
,
16
]
, we only have access to function values, which can be used to derive approximate gradients satisfying (
3.4
) by employing gradient approximation methods.

1.

Forward finite difference
(
FFD
)
: For a fixed number
\delta>0
, the FFD formula gives us the approximation of the gradient
\nabla f(x)
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}:=\dfrac{1}{\delta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f(x+\delta e_{i})-f(x)\big)e_{i},

(3.5)

where
e_{i}
is the
i^{\rm th}
vector in the standard basis of
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
. The following error bound is standard and can be found, e.g., in
[
42
, Section 7.2]
and
[
9
, Theorem 2.1]
:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq\dfrac{L\sqrt{n}\delta}{2}.

(3.6)

Therefore, the choice of
\delta\in\left(0,\frac{2\varepsilon}{L\sqrt{n}}\right)
guarantees the error bound (
3.4
).

2.

Centered finite difference
(
CFD
)
: For a given number
\delta>0
, the CFD formula gives us the approximation
\nabla f(x)
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}:=\dfrac{1}{\delta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(f\big(x+\frac{\delta}{2}e_{i}\big)-f\big(x-\dfrac{\delta}{2}e_{i}\big)\Big)e_{i}.

(3.7)

Assume that
f
is twice continuously differentiable (
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth) having the Lipschitz continuous Hessian with constant
M>0
. It follows from
[
9
, Theorem 2.2]
that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq\dfrac{\sqrt{n}M\delta^{2}}{24}.

(3.8)

Therefore, the choice of
\delta\in\left(0,\sqrt{\frac{24\varepsilon}{M\sqrt{n}}}\right)
guarantees the error bound required in (
3.4
).

When
f
is smoothed from another (possibly
nonsmooth
) function, the exact information about the function value and its gradient is usually not available. However, some specific smoothing techniques allow us to find an approximation of the gradient that satisfies condition (
3.4
).

Example 2

(Moreau envelopes)
.

Let
g:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
be a proper, lower semicontinuous (l.s.c.), convex function, and let
f:=e_{\lambda}g
be its Moreau envelope with proximal parameter
\lambda>0
; see Section
4
for more details. By (
4.2
), the problem of calculating
\nabla f(x)
approximately is equivalent to the approximate calculation of
\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x)
approximately. The latter problem is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm minimize}_{y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}}\quad\varphi_{\lambda}(y):=g(y)+\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|y-x\right\|^{2}.

(3.9)

Let
\bar{y}
be such that
\varphi_{\lambda}(\bar{y})-\inf_{y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}}\varphi_{\lambda}(y)\leq\dfrac{\lambda\varepsilon^{2}}{2}
, and set
\mathcal{G}:=\lambda^{-1}(x-\bar{y})
. Since
\varphi_{\lambda}
is strongly convex with positive constant
1/\lambda
, we deduce from (
4.2
) that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}-\nabla f(x)\right\|=\lambda^{-1}\left\|\bar{y}-\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{-1}\sqrt{2\lambda\left(\varphi_{\lambda}(\bar{y})-\inf_{y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}}\varphi_{\lambda}(y)\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{-1}\cdot\sqrt{2\lambda\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\varepsilon^{2}}=\varepsilon.

Therefore,
\mathcal{G}
is an approximation of
\nabla f(x)
with the error bound
\varepsilon
.

3.2
Convergence Analysis

In this subsection, we establish the convergence properties of Algorithm
1
including the convergence of the sequence of gradients (to zero), the sequence of iterates, and the sequence of function values together with their convergence rates under the KL property of the objective function. Let us begin by connecting the conditions in (
3.3
) with the following two conditions on the gradient estimates:

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\nu_{1}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N},

(3.10)

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\nu_{2}\left\|g^{k}\right\|\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N},

(3.11)

with some
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in[0,1).
Condition (
3.10
) was first introduced and studied by Polyak
[
44
, Section 4.2.3]

\bar{x}
of

•

For

\left\{x^{k}\right\}

•

For

, we have the convergence rate estimates

\left\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\right\|=\mathcal{O}\left(k^{-\frac{1-q}{2q-1}}\right),

(3.12)

f(x^{k})-f(\bar{x})=\mathcal{O}\left(k^{-\frac{2-2q}{2q-1}}\right),

(3.13)

\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|=\mathcal{O}\left(k^{-\frac{1-q}{2q-1}}\right).

(3.14)

[
Proof.]
Since
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous with constant

\displaystyle f(x^{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq f(x^{k})+\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\rangle+\dfrac{L}{2}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}

4
Gradient-Based Inexact Methods in Nonsmooth Convex Optimization

In this section, we employ the inexact gradient method for
{\cal C}^{1,1}
optimization developed in Section
3
to design and justify two new gradient-based
inexact methods
to solve problems of
nonsmooth convex optimization
. The reductions of such nonsmooth problems to
{\cal C}^{1,1}
optimization is conducted by using
smoothing procedures
via Moreau envelopes and proximal mappings.

The methods we develop in this way are the following:

1.

The
gradient-based inexact proximal point method
(GIPPM) to minimize l.s.c. convex functions.

2.

The
gradient-based inexact augmented Lagrangian method
(GIALM) to solve nonsmooth convex programs with equality linear constraints.

Each of these two methods is considered in the corresponding subsection below. First we recall the appropriate constructions of convex analysis used in what follows; see
[
6
,
36
,
47
]
for more details. Given a proper (i.e., with
\mbox{\rm dom}\,g:=\{x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;|\;g(x)<\infty\}\neq\emptyset
), l.s.c., convex function
g\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}:=(-\infty,\infty]
, the
subdifferential
of
g
at
x\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,g
is defined by

\partial g(x):=\big\{v\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;\big|\;\left\langle v,y-x\right\rangle\leq g(y)-g(x)\;\text{ for all }\;y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\big\}.

For any proximal parameter
\lambda>0
, the
Moreau envelope

e_{\lambda}g\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
and the
proximal mapping

\textit{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\rm I\!R}^{n}
are defined, respectively, by

\begin{array}[]{ll}e_{\lambda}g(x):=\inf_{y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}}\left\{g(y)+\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|y-x\right\|^{2}\right\},\\
\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x):=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}}\left\{g(y)+\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|y-x\right\|^{2}\right\}.\end{array}

(4.1)

The following result taken from
[
6
, Proposition 12.28, 12.29]
and
[
47
, Theorem 2.26, Proposition 12.19]
presents remarkable properties of Moreau envelopes and proximal mappings for convex functions that allow us to pass from convex nonsmooth optimization problems with extended-valued objectives (i.e., incorporating constraints) to problems of
{\cal C}^{1,1}
optimization.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
g\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
be a proper, l.s.c., convex function, and let
\lambda>0
. Then the Moreau envelope
e_{\lambda}g
is convex,
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth, and has the Lipschitz continuous gradient on
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
with constant
1/\lambda
that satisfies the relationship

\nabla e_{\lambda}g(x)=\lambda^{-1}\Big(x-\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x)\Big)\;\mbox{ for all }\;x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(4.2)

Furthermore, the proximal mapping
\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}
is Lipschitz continuous on
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
with constant
1
, and every stationary point of
e_{\lambda}g
is a minimizer of
g
.

4.1
Gradient-Based Inexact Proximal Point Method

We consider here the class of optimization problems given in the form

\displaystyle{\rm minimize}\quad g(x)\;\text{ subject to }\;x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},

(4.3)

where
g:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
is a proper, l.s.c., and convex function. Although problems (
1.1
) and (
4.3
) are written in the similar format, there is a huge difference between the
{\cal C}^{1,1}
objective in (
1.1
) and the extended-real-valued objective in
(
4.3
). Besides different differential properties of the objective functions in (
1.1
) and (
4.3
), observe that (
1.1
) is a problem of unconstrained optimization, while (
4.3
) incorporates constraints coming from
x\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,g
when the function
g
is extended-real-valued.

The following gradient-based inexact proximal point method/algorithm GIPPM is now proposed to solve the general class of nonsmooth convex optimization problems (
4.3
).

Algorithm 2

(GIPPM)
.

Step 0

(initialization)
.

Choose a proximal parameter
\lambda>0
, initial point
x^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, initial error
\varepsilon_{1}>0
, error reduction factor
\theta\in(0,1)
, and scaling factor
\mu>2
. Set
k:=1
.

Step 1

(inexact proximal mapping)
.

Find
p^{k}
and the smallest natural number
i_{k}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|p^{k}-\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x^{k})\right\|\leq\lambda\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}\quad\text{ and }\quad\left\|x^{k}-p^{k}\right\|>\lambda\mu\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}.

(4.4)

Step 2

(error and iteration update)
.

Set
x^{k+1}:=p^{k}
,
\varepsilon_{k+1}:=\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}
. Increase
k
by
1
and go to Step
1
.

Remark 4.2
.

Similarly to the discussions in Remark
3.1
, the procedure of finding
p^{k}
and
i_{k}
that satisfy (
4.4
) can be given as follows. Set
i_{k}=0.
Find some
p^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|p^{k}-\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x^{k})\right\|\leq\lambda\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}.

While
\left\|x^{k}-p^{k}\right\|\leq\lambda\mu\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}
, increase
i_{k}
by
1
and recalculate
p^{k}
under the condition above. Then the existence of
p^{k}
and
i_{k}
satisfying (
4.4
) is guaranteed when
0\notin\partial g(x^{k})
. Indeed, otherwise for each
k\in{\rm I\!N}
we get a sequence
\left\{p^{k}_{i}\right\}
as
i\in{\rm I\!N}
with

\displaystyle\left\|p^{k}_{i}-\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x^{k})\right\|\leq\lambda\theta^{i}\varepsilon_{k}\text{ and }\left\|x^{k}-p^{k}_{i}\right\|\leq\lambda\mu\theta^{i}\varepsilon_{k}\text{ for all }i\in{\rm I\!N}.

Since
\theta\in(0,1),
the latter inequalities mean that
x^{k}=\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x^{k})
, which is equivalent to

\displaystyle x^{k}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}}\left\{g(x^{k})+\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|x-x^{k}\right\|^{2}\right\}.

By using the subdifferential Fermat rule, we get
0\in\partial g(x^{k}),
a contradiction.

Now we are ready to establish convergence properties of Algorithm
2

Theorem 4.3

\left\{x^{k}\right\}

2

\mu>2

k\to\infty

1.

g

0

k\to\infty

2.

If
e_{\lambda}g
satisfies the KL property at an accumulation point
\bar{x}
of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
, then
x^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}
as
k\to\infty
.

3.

If
e_{\lambda}g
satisfies the KL property at an accumulation point
\bar{x}
of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
with
\psi(t)=Mt^{q}

\bullet

q\in(0,1/2]

\bar{x}
as
k\to\infty

\bullet

q\in(1/2,1)

\left\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\right\|=\mathcal{O}\big(k^{-\frac{1-q}{2q-1}}\big)

[
Proof.]
Define

. By Lemma

, we have that

is Lipschitzian with constant

\displaystyle\nabla f(x)=\lambda^{-1}\big(x-\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x)\big)\;\mbox{ for all }\;x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

Defining

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|g^{k}-\nabla e_{\lambda}g(x^{k})\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\lambda^{-1}(x^{k}-p^{k})-\lambda^{-1}(x^{k}-{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x^{k}))\right\|

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}\right\|=\lambda^{-1}\left\|x^{k}-p^{k}\right\|>\mu\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k},

respectively.
Furthermore, it follows from Step

of Algorithm

\displaystyle x^{k+1}=p^{k}=x^{k}-\lambda g^{k}=x^{k}-\dfrac{1}{L}g^{k},

Therefore,

is the iterative sequence generated by Algorithm

Next we discuss some features of Algorithm
2
and its relationships with other developments.

Remark 4.4
.

Observe the following:

1.

By using the properties of semialgebraic functions listed in Remark
2.2
and the Moreau envelope construction in (

), we can verify that the assumption on the KL property of

Applying Theorem

and using the arguments similar to Theorem

allow us to get the convergence properties for Algorithm

if (

\displaystyle\left\|p^{k}-\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x^{k})\right\|\leq\nu_{2}\left\|x^{k}-p^{k}\right\|\;\text{ with }\;\nu_{2}<\frac{1}{2},

\displaystyle\left\|p^{k}-\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x^{k})\right\|\leq\nu_{1}\left\|x^{k}-\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x^{k})\right\|\text{ with }\nu_{1}<\frac{1}{3}.

3.

Let us highlight important improvements of our GIPPM over the two versions of the classical inexact proximal point method (IPPM) of Rockafellar
[
45
]
applied to (
3.9
) as follows:

\displaystyle x^{k+1}=p^{k},\;\left\|p^{k}-\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x^{k})\right\|\leq\delta_{k},\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\delta_{k}<\infty,

(A)

\displaystyle x^{k+1}=p^{k},\;\left\|p^{k}-\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x^{k})\right\|\leq\delta_{k}\left\|x^{k}-p^{k}\right\|,\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\delta_{k}<\infty.

(B)

To this end, we mentioned first that in deriving convergence results, our approach in Algorithm
2
is by employing the IGD method applied to the Moreau envelope
e_{\lambda}g
, while the one by Rockafellar in (
A
) and (

) is using the properties related to the maximal monotonicity of the subgradient mapping

A
) of IPPM, our GIPPM can achieve a linear convergence rate while the convergence rate of (
A
) is not established in
[
45
]
. In practice, the sequence of errors
\left\{\delta_{k}\right\}
in (
A
) is usually chosen as
\delta_{k}:=ck^{-p}
with
p>1
and
c>0
. If
\delta_{k}
is too small, the procedure of finding approximate proximal points

takes a lot of time while if
\delta_{k}
is too large,

becomes unreliable. Note that in each iteration, the GIPPM algorithm verifies whether the approximate proximal point is acceptable and then decreases the error if necessary.

Regarding the version (
B
) of IPPM, it follows from the choice of
\delta_{k}
that
\delta_{k}\rightarrow 0
as

\nu_{2}<\frac{1}{2}

Therefore, the convergence properties of the algorithm with the procedure (
B

\nu_{2}
is a constant while
\delta_{k}
is decreasing to

p^{k}
under the conditions in (

takes significantly less time than the procedure (
B
) of IPPM while maintaining impressive convergence results achieved in Theorem
4.3
.

4.2
Gradient-Based Inexact Augmented Lagrangian Method

In this subsection, we propose the gradient-based inexact augmented Lagrangian method/algorithm GIALM to solve equality-constrained convex programs given in the form:

\begin{split}&{\rm minimize}\quad h(x)\\
&{\rm subject\ to}\quad Ax=b,\end{split}

(P)

where
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is a proper, l.s.c., convex function, and where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},\;b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Assume in what follows that the feasible set
\left\{x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;|\;Ax=b\right\}
of (
P
) is nonempty. Given a parameter
\lambda>0
, for each pair
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, consider the
Lagrange function
and the
augmented Lagrangian
defined as in
[
27
]
, respectively, by

\displaystyle\ell(x,y):=h(x)+\left\langle y,Ax-b\right\rangle\;\text{ and }\;\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x,y):

\displaystyle=\ell(x,y)+\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\left\|Ax-b\right\|^{2}.

(4.5)

The
dual Lagrange function

d\colon{\rm I\!R}^{m}\rightarrow[-\infty,\infty)
is given by
d(y):=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ell(x,y)
, and the corresponding
dual problem
is formulated as

\begin{split}&{\rm maximize}\quad d(y)\\
&{\rm subject\ to}\quad y\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}.\end{split}

(D)

It follows from
[
20
, Lemma 9]
that the function
g:=-d
is l.s.c. and convex. Therefore, when
g
is proper, its proximal mapping is well-defined and can be represented via the dual function
d
as

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(y)=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{w\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}}\left\{g(w)+\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|w-y\right\|^{2}\right\}=\mathop{\rm argmax}_{w\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}}\left\{d(w)-\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|w-y\right\|^{2}\right\}.

(4.6)

Now we present the construction of the gradient-based inexact augmented Lagrangian method.

Algorithm 3

(GIALM)
.

Step 0
.

Choose a starting point
y^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, proximal parameter
\lambda>0
, initial error
\varepsilon_{1}>0
, error reduction factor
\theta\in(0,1)
, and scaling factor
\mu>2
. Set
k:=1.

Step 1
.

Find some

x^{k+1}
and the smallest natural number
i_{k}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x^{k+1},y^{k})-\inf_{x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x,y^{k})\leq\dfrac{\lambda\theta^{2i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}}{2}\;\text{ and }\;\left\|b-Ax^{k+1}\right\|>\mu\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}.

(4.7)

Step 2
.

Set
y^{k+1}:=y^{k}+\lambda(Ax^{k+1}-b)
and
\varepsilon_{k+1}:=\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}
. Increase
k
by
1
and go back to Step 1.

As a part of our convergence analysis for GIALM, we derive the following two propositions of their independent interest. The first proposition provides a useful representation of the augmented Lagrangian function different from (
4.5
).

Proposition 4.5
.

For any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\lambda>0
, we have the representation

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(z,\eta)=\sup_{w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}}\left\{\ell(z,w)-\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|w-\eta\right\|^{2}\right\}.

[
Proof.]
Taking into account the construction of
\ell
in (
4.5
), we see that

\displaystyle w\mapsto\ell(z,w)-\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|w-\eta\right\|^{2}=h(z)+\left\langle w,Az-b\right\rangle-\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|w-\eta\right\|^{2}

is a quadratic concave function, and thus it attains the global maximum when
Az-b=\frac{1}{\lambda}(w-\eta)
, i.e., at
w=\lambda(Az-b)+\eta
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\sup_{w}\left\{\ell(z,w)-\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|w-\eta\right\|^{2}\right\}=\

\displaystyle h(z)+\big\langle\lambda(Az-b)+\eta,Az-b\big\rangle-\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|\lambda(Az-b)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle h(z)+\left\langle\eta,Az-b\right\rangle+\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\left\|Az-b\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(z,\eta),

which therefore verifies the claim of the proposition.

Next we obtain a relationship between the augmented Lagrangian (
4.5
) and the proximal mapping (
4.6
). The result of this type was stated in
[
46
, Proposition 6]
but only for the iterative sequence of the inexact augmented Lagrangian method for convex programs with inequality constraints. Using a similar technique, a general result for problem (
P
) without considering a specific iterative sequence is presented in the next proposition.

Proposition 4.6
.

Suppose that the function
g
defined above is proper. Then we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|y+\lambda(Ax-b)-\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(y)\right\|^{2}\leq\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x,y)-\inf_{z\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(z,y)

for all pairs
(x,y)\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\times{\rm I\!R}^{m}
.

[
Proof.]
Fix any
(x,y)\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\times{\rm I\!R}^{m}
and denote
p=y+\lambda(Ax-b)
. Take an arbitrary
\eta\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}
and define the function
\vartheta\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\times{\rm I\!R}^{m}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
by

\displaystyle\vartheta(z,w):=\ell(z,w)-\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|w-\eta\right\|^{2}=h(z)+\left\langle w,Az-b\right\rangle-\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|w-\eta\right\|^{2}.

It is clear that the function
\vartheta
is l.s.c. convex in
z
, and continuous concave in
w
with
\vartheta(0,w)\rightarrow-\infty
as
\left\|w\right\|\rightarrow\infty
. Applying Proposition
4.5
and then
[
50
, Theorem 2.7]
to
\vartheta(z,w)
with taking
[
50
, Remark 2.2]
into account, we get the equalities

\displaystyle\inf_{z}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(z,\eta)

\displaystyle=\inf_{z}\sup_{w}\left\{\ell(z,w)-\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|w-\eta\right\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{w}\inf_{z}\left\{\ell(z,w)-\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|w-\eta\right\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{w}\left\{d(w)-\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|w-\eta\right\|^{2}\right\}.

(4.8)

Combining this with the construction of
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x,y)
in (
4.5
) gives us

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x,y)+\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\left\langle p-y,\eta-y\right\rangle

\displaystyle=h(x)+\left\langle y,Ax-b\right\rangle+\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\left\|Ax-b\right\|^{2}+\left\langle Ax-b,\eta-y\right\rangle

\displaystyle=h(x)+\left\langle\eta,Ax-b\right\rangle+\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\left\|Ax-b\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x,\eta)\geq\inf_{z}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(z,\eta)

\displaystyle=\sup_{w}\left\{d(w)-\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|w-\eta\right\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq d\big(\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(y)\big)-\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(y)-\eta\right\|^{2}.

(4.9)

Employing (
4.2
) again with
\eta:=y
and taking into account the construction of the proximal mapping in (
4.6
), we arrive at the equalities

\displaystyle\inf_{z}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(z,y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{w}\left\{d(w)-\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|w-y\right\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=d\big(\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(y))\big)-\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(y)-y\right\|^{2}.

The latter expression combined with (
4.2
) tells us that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x,y)-\inf_{z}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(z,y)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left(\left\|\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(y)-y\right\|^{2}-\left\|\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(y)-\eta\right\|^{2}-2\left\langle p-y,\eta-y\right\rangle\right).

Choosing finally
\eta:=\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(y)+y-p
brings us to

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x,y)-\inf_{z}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(z,y)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left(\left\|\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(y)-y\right\|^{2}-\left\|p-y\right\|^{2}-2\left\langle p-y,\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(y)-p\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|p-\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(y)\right\|^{2},

which therefore completes the proof of the proposition.

Remark 4.7
.

The procedure of finding
x^{k+1}
and
i_{k}
in Step 1 of Algorithm
3
can be performed similarly to Algorithm
1
and Algorithm
2
in the following way. Set
i_{k}=0
and find some
x^{k+1}
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x^{k+1},y^{k})-\inf_{x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x,y^{k})\leq\dfrac{\lambda\theta^{2i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}}{2}.

(4.10)

While
\left\|b-Ax^{k+1}\right\|\leq\mu\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k},
increase
i_{k}
by
1
and recalculate
x^{k+1}
under condition (
4.10
). This procedure terminates finitely when the function
g
defined above is proper, and when
y^{k}
is not a solution to (
D
). Indeed, if (
4.10
) does not stop, for each
k\in{\rm I\!N}
we get
\left\{x^{k+1}_{i}\right\}
,
i\in{\rm I\!N}
, such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x^{k+1}_{i},y^{k})-\inf_{x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x,y^{k})\leq\dfrac{\lambda\theta^{2i}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}}{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\left\|b-Ax^{k+1}_{i}\right\|\leq\mu\theta^{i}\varepsilon\;\text{ for all }\;i\in{\rm I\!N}.

(4.11)

Combining the first inequality in (
4.11
) with Proposition
4.6
gives us

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|y^{k}+\lambda(Ax^{k+1}_{i}-b)-\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(y^{k})\right\|^{2}\leq\dfrac{\lambda\theta^{2i}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}}{2}.

Since
\theta\in(0,1)
, letting
i\rightarrow\infty
with taking into account the second inequality in (
4.11
), we get that
y^{k}=\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(y^{k})
, which implies by the subdifferential Fermat rule that

. Therefore,
y^{k}
is a solution to the dual problem (
D
) due to its convexity. This is a contradiction.

Now we are ready to establish the convergence results of our GIALM algorithm.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let the sequences
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
,
\left\{y^{k}\right\}
are generated by Algorithm
3
. Assume that
\sup_{y\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}}d(y)>-\infty
and
y^{k}
is not a solution to (
D
) whenever
k\in{\rm I\!N}
. Then either
\left\|y^{k}\right\|\rightarrow\infty
as
k\to\infty
, or we have:

1.

Every accumulation point of
\left\{y^{k}\right\}
is a solution to (
D
). If in addition the sequence
\left\{y^{k}\right\}
is bounded, then every accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is a solution to (
P
).

2.

If
e_{\lambda}g
satisfies the KL property at an accumulation point
\bar{y}
of
\left\{y^{k}\right\}
, then
y^{k}\rightarrow\bar{y}
as
k\to\infty
.

3.

If
e_{\lambda}g
satisfies the KL property at an accumulation point
\bar{y}
of
\left\{y^{k}\right\}
with
\psi(t)=Mt^{q}
for some
M>0
and
q\in(0,1)
, then we get the convergence rates:

\bullet
If
q\in(0,1/2]
, then the sequence
\left\{y^{k}\right\}
converges linearly to
\bar{y}
as
k\to\infty
.

\bullet
If
q\in(1/2,1)
, then
\|y^{k}-\bar{y}\|={\cal O}\big(k^{-\frac{1-q}{2q-1}}\big)
as
k\to\infty
.

[
Proof.]
(i) Since
\sup_{y\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}}d(y)>-\infty
, the function
g=-d
in (
D
) is proper. It follows from Proposition
4.6
and the first inequality in (
4.7
) that

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|y^{k}+\lambda(Ax^{k+1}-b)-\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(y^{k})\right\|^{2}\leq\dfrac{\lambda\theta^{2i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}}{2}.

Defining
p^{k}:=y^{k}+\lambda(Ax^{k+1}-b)
, the latter can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle\left\|p^{k}-\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(y^{k})\right\|\leq\lambda\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

Moreover, the second inequality in (
4.7
) also tells us that

\displaystyle\left\|y^{k}-p^{k}\right\|>\lambda\mu\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

Therefore,
\left\{y^{k}\right\}
is the iterative sequence generated by Algorithm
2
to find minimizers of
g
. Then Theorem
4.3
(i) tells us that every accumulation point of
\left\{y^{k}\right\}
is a minimizer of
g
, i.e., it is a solution to (
D
). Furthermore, we have
\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
and
\left\|y^{k}-p^{k}\right\|\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.

Assume next that
\left\{y^{k}\right\}
is bounded and show that every accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is a solution to (
P
). Since
\left\|y^{k}-p^{k}\right\|\rightarrow 0
, we deduce from the construction of
p^{k}
that

\displaystyle Ax^{k}-b\rightarrow 0\;\text{ as }\;k\rightarrow\infty.

(4.12)

Picking any feasible solution
x^{*}
to (
P
) tells us that
Ax^{*}=b
. It follows from the first inequality in (
4.7
) and Step 2 of Algorithm
3
that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x^{k+1},y^{k})\leq\inf_{x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x,y^{k})+\dfrac{\lambda\varepsilon_{k+1}^{2}}{2}\leq\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x^{*},y^{k})+\dfrac{\lambda\varepsilon_{k+1}^{2}}{2}.

Combining this with the construction of
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
in (
4.5
) and the feasibility of
x^{*}
gives us

\displaystyle h(x^{k+1})+\left\langle y^{k},Ax^{k+1}-b\right\rangle+\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\left\|Ax^{k+1}-b\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq h(x^{*})+\left\langle y^{k},Ax^{*}-b\right\rangle+\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\left\|Ax^{*}-b\right\|^{2}+\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\varepsilon_{k+1}^{2}

\displaystyle=h(x^{*})+\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\varepsilon_{k+1}^{2}\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

(4.13)

Taking now any accumulation point
\bar{x}
of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
, we find some infinite subset
J\subset{\rm I\!N}
such that
x^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}\bar{x}
. Then it follows from (
4.12
) that
A\bar{x}=b
, which verifies the feasibility of
\bar{x}
. Invoking (
4.12
) again together with the boundedness of
\left\{y^{k}\right\}
gives us the convergence

\displaystyle\left\langle y^{k-1},Ax^{k}-b\right\rangle+\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\left\|Ax^{k}-b\right\|^{2}\rightarrow 0\;\text{ as }\;k\rightarrow\infty.

(4.14)

Combining the latter with the lower semicontinuity of
h
, the estimate (
4.2
), and the condition
\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
tells us that

\displaystyle h(\bar{x})

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{k\overset{J}{\rightarrow}\infty}h(x^{k})

\displaystyle=\liminf_{k\overset{J}{\rightarrow}\infty}\left\{h(x^{k})+\left\langle y^{k-1},Ax^{k}-b\right\rangle+\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\left\|Ax^{k}-b\right\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{k\overset{J}{\rightarrow}\infty}\left\{h(x^{*})+\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}\right\}=h(x^{*}).

Since
x^{*}
is any feasible solution to (
P
), we conclude that
\bar{x}
is an optimal solution to (
P
).

Finally, assertions (ii) and (iii) follow from Theorem
4.3
(ii), (iii), respectively.

5
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we compare numerical aspects of the newly designed GIALM and the classical IALM in solving the basic Lasso problem given by

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm minimize}\quad\dfrac{1}{2}\left\|Ax-b\right\|^{2}+\gamma\left\|x\right\|_{1}\;\text{ subject to }\;x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},

(5.1)

where
A
is an
m\times n
matrix,
b
is a vector in
{\rm I\!R}^{m}
, and
\gamma>0
. By omitting constants and defining
p(x):=\gamma\left\|x\right\|_{1}
and
c:=A^{*}b
, we observe that Lasso problem (
5.1
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mbox{maximize }\;-\dfrac{1}{2}\left\|Ax\right\|^{2}+\left\langle c,x\right\rangle-p(x),\quad x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}

(5.2)

This is the dual problem of the following primal convex program with linear equality constraints:

\displaystyle{\rm minimize}\quad\dfrac{1}{2}\left\|y\right\|^{2}+p^{*}(z)

(5.3)

\displaystyle{\rm subject\ to}\quad-A^{*}y-z=-c,

where the indicator function
p^{*}(\cdot)=\delta_{\gamma\mathbb{B}^{\infty}}(\cdot)
is the convex conjugate of
p
with
\mathbb{B}^{\infty}:=\left\{z\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;|\;\max|z_{i}|\leq\gamma\right\}
. Indeed, the corresponding Lagrange function and the dual function of (
5.3
) are given, respectively, by

\displaystyle\ell(y,z,x)=\dfrac{1}{2}\left\|y\right\|^{2}+p^{*}(z)+\left\langle x,c-A^{*}y-z\right\rangle,

\displaystyle d(x)

\displaystyle=\inf_{y,z}\ell(y,z,x)=\inf_{y}\left\{\dfrac{1}{2}\left\|y\right\|^{2}-\left\langle y,Ax\right\rangle\right\}+\inf_{z}\big\{p^{*}(z)-\left\langle x,z\right\rangle\big\}+\left\langle c,x\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2}\left\|Ax\right\|^{2}+\left\langle c,x\right\rangle-p^{**}(x).

Since
p
is convex and continuous, we know from basic convex analysis that
p^{**}=p
, and thus
d
is exactly the objective function in (
5.2
). Since (
5.3
) has the form of (
P
), we can apply GIALM to solve (
5.3
), which brings us to the following algorithm.

Algorithm 4

(GIALM for solving (
5.3
))
.

Step 0
.

Choose a starting point
x^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, proximal parameter
\lambda>0,
initial error
\varepsilon_{1}>0
, error reduction factor
\theta\in(0,1)
, and scaling factor
\mu>2
. Set
k:=1
.

Step 1
.

Find some

(y^{k+1},z^{k+1})
and the smallest natural number
i_{k}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(y^{k+1},z^{k+1},x^{k})-\inf_{y,z}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(y,z,x^{k})\leq\dfrac{\lambda\theta^{2i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}}{2}\quad\text{ and }\left\|A^{*}y^{k+1}+z^{k+1}-c\right\|>\mu\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}.

(5.4)

Step 2
.

Set
x^{k+1}:=x^{k}-\lambda(A^{*}y^{k+1}+z^{k+1}-c)
and
\varepsilon_{k+1}:=\theta^{i_{k}}\varepsilon_{k}
. Increase
k
by
1
and go to Step 1.

The augmented Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(y,z,x)
in (
5.4
) is written now as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(y,z,x)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left\|y\right\|^{2}+p^{*}(z)-\left\langle x,A^{*}y+z-c\right\rangle+\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\left\|A^{*}y+z-c\right\|^{2}.

Given
i_{k}\in{\rm I\!N}
, we employ the technique in
[
34
]
to find
(y^{k+1},z^{k+1})
that satisfy the first inequality in (

). Define the mapping
\Psi_{k}:{\rm I\!R}^{m}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}^{n}
and the function
\psi_{k}:{\rm I\!R}^{m}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
by

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}(y):=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{z}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(y,z,x^{k})\;\text{ and }\;\psi_{k}(y):=\inf_{z}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(y,z,x^{k}).

(5.5)

It follows from
[
34

that

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}(y)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\rm Prox}_{p^{*}/\lambda}\left(\dfrac{x^{k}}{\lambda}-A^{*}y+c\right),

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(y)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left\|y\right\|^{2}+\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda p}(x^{k}-\lambda(A^{*}y-c))\right\|^{2}-\dfrac{\left\|x^{k}\right\|^{2}}{2\lambda}.

In addition,
\psi_{k}
is strongly convex with constant
1
and
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth with the Lipschitzian gradient

\displaystyle\nabla\psi_{k}(y)=y+A\mbox{\rm Prox}_{\lambda p}(x^{k}-\lambda(A^{*}y+c))\;\text{ for all }\;y\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}.

Note that there are explicit formulas for calculating

and

[

and
[

To proceed further, deduce from the definitions of

and

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(y)=\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(y,\Psi_{k}(y),x^{k})\;\text{ and }\;\inf_{y,z}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(y,z,x^{k})=\inf_{y}\psi_{k}(y).

which implies that the first inequality in (

at each iteration:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(y^{k+1},z^{k+1},x^{k})-\inf_{y,z}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(y,z,x^{k})\leq\delta_{k}^{2}\;\text{ and }\;x^{k+1}=x^{k}-\lambda(A^{*}y^{k+1}+z^{k+1}-c),

(5.8)

where the sequence
\left\{\delta_{k}\right\}
is positive and summable. Using arguments similar to the above tells us that for each
k\in{\rm I\!N}
the pair
(y^{k+1},z^{k+1})
satisfying the inequality in (
5.8
) can be obtained from

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\psi_{k}(y^{k+1})\right\|\leq\omega_{k}:=\sqrt{2}\delta_{k}\;\text{ and }\;z^{k+1}=\Psi_{k}(y^{k+1}),

where
\psi_{k}
and
\Psi_{k}
are given in (
5.5
). In our implementation, we choose
\omega_{k}:=k^{-q}
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
with some
q>1
to ensure the summability of
\left\{\delta_{k}\right\}
. Consider also the two specific choices of
q=2
and
q=3
, which correspond to the versions IALM-2 and IALM-1.5, respectively.

Our two numerical experiments presented below are conducted by using a computer with the parameters: 10th Gen Intel(R), Core(TM), i5-10400 (6-Core 12M Cache, 2.9GHz to 4.3GHz), 16GB RAM memory, and the code written in MATLAB R2022b.
