Quasiparticle scattering in a superconductor near a nematic critical point: resonance mode and multiple attractive channels.
Dimitri Pimenov, Alex Kamenev, Andrey V. Chubukov

Abstract
We analyze the scattering rate for 2D fermions interacting via soft nematic fluctuations. The ground state is an s s -wave superconductor, but other pairing channels are almost equally attractive. This strongly alters the scattering rate: At energies beyond the pairing gap Δ \Delta , it is renormalized by contributions from all pairing channels. At energies of order Δ \Delta , it is determined by the competition between scattering into a gapped continuum and dispersing nematic resonance. The outcome is a “peak-peak-dip-hump” spectrum, similar, but not identical, to the “peak-dip-hump” structure in the cuprates.

Conclusion
.—
We analyzed the propagator of soft nematic fluctuations and
fermionic scattering rate
\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}(\omega)]
of fermions in an

s-
wave SC state near a nematic QCP. We argued that

\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}(\omega)]

is qualitatively different from that
in an ordinary
s
-wave SC because
near a QCP, the pairing interaction in the
s
-wave channel is only slightly larger than in pairing channels with other angular momenta. We showed that the spectrum of nematic fluctuations in a SC consists of a gapped continuum and a strongly dispersing resonance mode. We found three different regimes of the scattering rate, due to the interaction with a resonance: a jump at the threshold, a decrease towards a shallow minimum, and a slow subsequent increase.
A fourth regime is realized at
\omega\geq 3\Delta
, when the scattering rate predominantly comes from the interaction with the continuum.
At higher frequencies,
\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}(\omega)]
gradually transforms into the normal state expression. This leads to a “peak-peak-dip-hump” structure of the fermionic spectral function. This structure transforms into the more familiar peak-dip-hump
lineshape as the system comes closer to a nematic QCP and the coupling constant
\lambda
increases.

Superconductivity at the onset of a nematic order at
T=0
develops in several systems studied in recent years, most notably in FeSe
1-x
S
x
around
x=0.2
. We call for ARPES measurements of the fermionic spectral function to verify our results.

We first evaluate

We compute the momentum integrals first – in this way we omit a constant term

We find

\displaystyle\Pi(q)=2\rho\!\int\frac{d\theta_{{\boldsymbol{p}}{\boldsymbol{q}}}}{2\pi}f_{\boldsymbol{q}}({\boldsymbol{p}})^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{\omega}_{m}

\displaystyle\frac{(A_{+}+A_{-})\times(A_{+}A_{-}+(\Omega_{m})^{2}/4-(\tilde{\omega}_{m})^{2}-\Delta^{2})}{A_{+}A_{-}\left(\left(A_{+}+A_{-}\right)^{2}+(v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|\cos(\theta_{{\boldsymbol{p}}{\boldsymbol{q}}}))^{2}\right)}=

\displaystyle R=A_{-}+A_{+}+\frac{(A_{-}+A_{+})\times(v_{F}{\boldsymbol{q}})^{2}}{2(A_{+}+A_{-})^{2}+(v_{F}{\boldsymbol{q}})^{2}}\ ,

The last term

f_{\boldsymbol{q}}({\boldsymbol{p}})=\cos(2\theta_{{\boldsymbol{p}}{\boldsymbol{q}}})

v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|\ll\Omega_{m},\Delta

In the first case, we can pull out

In the normal state where

III
Nematic fluctuation spectrum
\mathcal{B}

With
\Pi
at hand, we can determine the nematic fluctuation spectrum as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\Omega,{\boldsymbol{q}})=-\text{Im}\left[-\frac{1}{M^{2}+|{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}+g\Pi^{R}(\Omega,{\boldsymbol{q}})}\right]\ .

(25)

Inserting expressions (
16
), (
17
), we obtain the pole-like resonance part of the spectrum as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\text{res}}(\Omega,{\boldsymbol{q}})=a_{\boldsymbol{q}}\times\delta(\Omega-\Omega_{\text{res}}({\boldsymbol{q}}))

(26)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\text{res}}({\boldsymbol{q}})\simeq\begin{cases}\Delta\times\sqrt{6}\kappa\left(1+\frac{|{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}}{M^{2}}\right)&\ v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|\ll\Delta\\
\sqrt{\Delta v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|}\times\sqrt{\frac{8}{\pi}}\kappa\left(1+\frac{|{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}}{M^{2}}\right)&\ v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|\gg\Delta\end{cases}

(27)

\displaystyle a_{\boldsymbol{q}}\simeq\begin{cases}\pi\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\times\frac{\Delta}{2\kappa g\rho}&\ v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|\ll\Delta\\
\sqrt{2\pi}\frac{\sqrt{\Delta v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|}}{2\kappa g\rho}&\ v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|\gg\Delta\ .\end{cases}

(28)

Here, we have introduced

\displaystyle\kappa=\sqrt{\frac{M^{2}}{2g\rho}}=\sqrt{\frac{{\omega_{\text{FL}}}}{v_{F}M}}=\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{4\lambda}}\ll 1\ ,

(29)

and also assumed
|{\boldsymbol{q}}|\ll M
. Note that the weight of the resonance peak increases with
v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|
.
The above expressions are valid as long as
\Omega_{\text{res}}<2\Delta
, which constraints the maximal allowed
|{\boldsymbol{q}}|
-values to
v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|<\Delta/\kappa^{2}
. For
\Omega>2\Delta
, the continuum part
\mathcal{B}_{\text{cont}}
sets in. While we do not discuss the form of the continuum in detail, notice the following trends that are reflected in Fig.
3
of the main text:

(i) As long as the resonance is well-separated from the continuum, always
\Pi^{R}(2\Delta+0^{+})\gg M^{2}
, thus
\mathcal{B}_{\text{cont}}
does not jump at
\Omega=2\Delta
, but sets in continuously. However, as the resonance approaches the continuum,
\Pi^{R}(2\Delta+0^{+})>M^{2}
only due to the logarithmic enhancement of
\text{Re}[\Pi^{R}]
. Eventually, for
v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|\gg\Delta/\kappa^{2}
,
M^{2}
becomes the dominant scale, which means that
\mathcal{B}_{\text{cont}}
jumps at
\Omega=2\Delta
. At these large momenta, we can approximate

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\text{cont}}(\Omega,{\boldsymbol{q}})\simeq\frac{2\pi g\rho}{M^{4}}\times\frac{\Delta}{v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|}\times\theta(\Omega-2\Delta)\ .

(30)

(ii) For small momenta, the maximum value of the continuum
\mathcal{B}_{\text{cont}}\simeq 1/M^{2}
is expected at frequencies
\Omega-2\Delta=O(\Delta)
when
\Pi^{R}\simeq M^{2}
. The weight of the continuum can be estimated as
\Delta/M^{2}
, which is larger than the weight of the resonance by about a factor of
\kappa
. As
|{\boldsymbol{q}}|
is increased, the weight goes down, as is also seen from Eq. (
30

\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}(\omega,{\boldsymbol{k}})]
for

We need to compute
\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}(\omega)]\equiv\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}(\omega,{\boldsymbol{k}})]
, with
{\boldsymbol{k}}
on the Fermi surface.
From the spectral representation, one can derive the following general formula

\displaystyle\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}(\omega)]=

(31)

\displaystyle g\!\int\!\frac{d{\boldsymbol{q}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}f^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}/2)\int_{0}^{\omega}\frac{d\Omega}{\pi}\mathcal{A}_{0}(\omega-\Omega,{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}})\mathcal{B}(\Omega,{\boldsymbol{q}})\ .

We focus on
\omega>0
.
\mathcal{A}_{0}
is the “bare” spectral function,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0}(\omega-\Omega,{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}})=-\text{Im}\left[G^{R}(\omega-\Omega,{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}})\right]=

\displaystyle\pi u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}^{2}\delta\left(\omega-\Omega-E_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}\right),

(32)

\displaystyle E_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}=\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+\xi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}^{2}},\ u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{\xi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}}{E_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}}\right).

In Eq. (
31

f^{2}_{\boldsymbol{q}}({\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}/2)\simeq f^{2}_{\boldsymbol{q}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\simeq(-1)^{2}=1
, since the integral will be dominated by angles such that
{\boldsymbol{k}}\perp{\boldsymbol{q}}
.

We start with the contribution of the nematic resonance, inserting
\mathcal{B}_{\text{res}}(\Omega,{\boldsymbol{q}})
into Eq. (
31

\displaystyle\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}_{\text{res}}(\omega)]=

(33)

\displaystyle g\!\int\!\frac{d{\boldsymbol{q}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}^{2}a_{\boldsymbol{q}}\delta(\omega-\Omega_{\text{res}}({\boldsymbol{q}})-E_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}})\ .

The resonance contribution is only important for
\omega<3\Delta
, which implies that
\xi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}
is at most of order
\Delta
or smaller. To get the correct order of magnitude, we can therefore approximate
u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}^{2}\simeq 1/2
and expand
E_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}
in
\xi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}=v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|\cos(\phi)
, where
\phi=\measuredangle({\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{q}})
.
\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}_{\text{res}}]
starts at the threshold
\omega=\omega_{\text{th}}
, where

\displaystyle\omega_{\text{th}}=\Delta+\Omega_{\text{res}}(0)=\Delta(1+\sqrt{6}\kappa)\ .

(34)

Close to
\omega_{\text{th}}
, we need the low-momentum branch of
\mathcal{B}_{\text{res}}
, cf. Eq. (
26
). We get

\displaystyle\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}_{\text{res}}(\omega)]=\frac{\sqrt{6}}{16}\frac{\Delta}{\kappa\rho}\int_{0}^{\Delta/v_{F}}d|{\boldsymbol{q}}||{\boldsymbol{q}}|\int\frac{d\phi}{2\pi}

(35)

\displaystyle\delta(\omega-\omega_{\text{th}}-(v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|\cos(\phi))^{2}/2\Delta-\sqrt{6}\kappa\Delta|{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}/2M^{2})\ .

We have cut off the
{\boldsymbol{q}}
-integral at
\Delta/v_{F}
, which marks the upper boundary of the low-momentum branch of
\mathcal{B}_{\text{res}}
. The trivial
|{\boldsymbol{q}}|
-integral yields

\displaystyle\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}_{\text{res}}(\omega)]=

(36)

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{6}}{16}\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\kappa\rho v_{F}^{2}}\times\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\phi}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\cos^{2}(\phi)+\sqrt{6}\kappa\Delta^{2}/(v_{F}M)^{2}}\times

\displaystyle\theta\!\left(\Delta-\frac{\omega-\omega_{\text{th}}}{\cos^{2}(\phi)+\sqrt{6}\kappa\Delta^{2}/(v_{F}M)^{2}}\right)\times\theta(\omega-\omega_{\text{th}})\ .

First consider the limit
\omega\searrow\omega_{\text{th}}
, then the first step function in Eq. (
36
) equals one. The integral comes from angles close to
\pi/2,3\pi/2
: the constant
\sqrt{6}\Delta^{2}/(v_{F}M)^{2}
is extremely small, since
\Delta={\omega_{\text{FL}}}\exp(-1/\lambda)
is exponentially suppressed compared to
v_{F}M={\omega_{\text{FL}}}/\kappa^{2}
. In this limit, we find

\displaystyle\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}_{\text{res}}(\omega_{\text{th}}+0^{+})]=

(37)

\displaystyle\frac{6^{3/4}}{16}\frac{\Delta M}{\kappa^{3/2}\rho v_{F}}=\left(\frac{27}{32}\right)^{\!1/4}\times(\kappa^{1/2}\lambda)\times\Delta\ .

Moving away from the threshold, for
\omega-\omega_{\text{th}}\gtrsim\Delta\sqrt{6}\kappa\Delta^{2}/(v_{F}M)^{2}
, the step-function becomes important: it restricts
\cos^{2}(\phi)\gtrsim(\omega-\omega_{\text{th}})/\Delta
. Since the upper cutoff
\Delta
enters explicitly, we no longer have control over numerical prefactors and suppress them from now on. We obtain

\displaystyle\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}_{\text{res},1}(\nu)]\sim\kappa^{3}\frac{\lambda\Delta^{2}}{{\omega_{\text{FL}}}}\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{(\omega-\omega_{\text{th}})}}\ ,

(38)

where the index
1
means that this is the low-
{\boldsymbol{q}}
contribution. Now we compare this to the large-
{\boldsymbol{q}}
resonance contribution of Eq. (
26
). It reads

\displaystyle\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}_{\text{res},2}(\omega)]\sim

(39)

\displaystyle\int d|{\boldsymbol{q}}|d\phi|{\boldsymbol{q}}|\frac{\sqrt{\Delta v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|}}{\kappa\rho}\delta(\omega-\kappa\sqrt{\Delta v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|}-E_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}})\ .

To find the low-energy result, we require that
v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|\gg\Delta
, but still
v_{F}|{\boldsymbol{q}}|\cos(\phi)\ll\Delta
, s.t. we can expand
E_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}
as before. Rescaling momenta,

, and linearizing the angular integral around
\pi/2
(variable

\displaystyle\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}_{\text{res},2}(\omega)]\sim

(40)

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\kappa\rho v_{F}^{2}}\int dx\int dy\ x^{3/2}\times\delta\left(\frac{\omega-\Delta}{\Delta}-\kappa\sqrt{x}-x^{2}y^{2}\right).

The lower bound on the

\displaystyle|y|=\frac{\sqrt{\frac{\omega-\Delta}{\Delta}-\kappa\sqrt{x}}}{x}\ .

To have

. Then, to have

. The

\displaystyle\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}_{\text{res},2}(\omega)]\sim

(42)

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\kappa\rho v_{F}^{2}}\int_{1}^{x_{\text{max}}}dx\frac{\sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{\frac{\omega-\Delta}{\Delta}-\kappa\sqrt{x}}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\kappa^{3/2}\rho v_{F}^{2}}\int_{1}^{x_{\text{max}}}dx\frac{\sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{\sqrt{x_{\text{max}}}-\sqrt{x}}}\ .

This contribution becomes sizeable compared to

if

(but still
x_{\text{max}}<1/\kappa^{2}
, otherwise
\omega-\Delta(1+\kappa)>\Delta
). We can then approximate

, and the integral yields

\displaystyle\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}_{\text{res},2}(\omega)]\sim\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\kappa^{3/2}\rho v_{F}^{2}}(x_{\text{max}})^{5/4}

(43)

\displaystyle\sim\kappa^{3}\frac{\lambda\Delta^{2}}{{\omega_{\text{FL}}}}\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{\omega-\omega_{\text{th}}}}\times\kappa^{-3}\left(\frac{\omega-\omega_{\text{th}}}{\Delta}\right)^{3}\ .

Comparing to Eq. (

Thus, the continuum contribution becomes larger than the resonance part (

\displaystyle\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}(\omega)]

\displaystyle\sim\begin{cases}\kappa^{2}\epsilon\frac{\Delta^{2}}{{\omega_{\text{FL}}}},&\Delta<\omega<\omega_{\text{th}}\\

V
Comparison to the SDW case

There are two qualitative differences between the self-energies due to excitation of the nematic resonance (Ising nematic case) and spin-resonances (SDW case): (i) in the SDW case, the superconducting ground state has
d
-wave order, and gapless states exist at the nodes along the Brillouin zone diagonal. Therefore,
\text{Im}[\Sigma^{R}(\omega)]
generically starts at
\omega=0
. However, the nodal states cannot couple efficiently to the spin resonance due to a momentum mismatch (see, e.g., Ref.
Eschrig 2006
), and therefore their contribution to the self-energy is small. (ii) the spin resonance is peaked at a large momentum
Q=(\pi,\pi)\sim k_{F}
and not at
Q\simeq 0
. As a result, the quadratic part of the corresponding polarization function scales as
Abanov et al. 2001

\displaystyle\Pi^{R}_{\text{SDW}}(\Omega)\sim\rho\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\Delta E_{F}}\ ,

(46)

while in the nematic case
\Pi^{R}\sim\rho\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}
. Thus,
\Pi^{R}
is generically much larger in the nematic case. In the SDW case, one then finds that the spin resonance is located at

\displaystyle\Omega_{\text{res}}^{\text{SDW}}\sim\sqrt{\Delta\omega_{\text{SF}}}\ ,

(47)

where
\omega_{\text{SF}}
is the analog of
{\omega_{\text{FL}}}
. For Eq. (
47
) to hold, one needs
\omega_{\text{SF}}<\Delta
. This shows that a generic spin resonance is only possible
at strong coupling

\lambda\gtrsim 1
, otherwise
\Delta\sim\omega_{\text{SF}}\exp(-1/\lambda)\ll\omega_{\text{SF}}
. Without the contribution from the nodal states, the SDW self-energy then starts at
\Delta+\Omega_{\text{res}}^{\text{SDW}}
. At larger energies, it becomes smaller, similar to the nematic case. However, with a calculation analogous to Eq. (
35
) one can show that this reduction is only by a factor

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{\frac{\Delta\Omega_{\text{res}}^{\text{SDW}}}{(v_{F}M)^{2}}}=O(1)\ \quad\text{for}\quad\lambda=O(1)\ .

(48)

The imaginary part of the self-energy is therefore step-like and not a sharp peak; it looks similar to Fig.
7
. Thus, only the peak-dip-hump scenario can be realized in the SDW case, not the peak-peak-dip-hump scenario.

If the coupling
\lambda
is small in the SDW case, a spin resonance is in principle still possible (see
Abanov et al. 2001
and references within). This weak-coupling resonance occurs due to the logarithmic divergence of the polarization function at
\Omega=2\Delta
, and appears at
\Omega_{\text{res}}^{\text{SDW}}=2\Delta(1-Z_{s})
, where

\displaystyle Z_{s}=\exp(-\omega_{\text{SF}}/\Delta)

(49)

is also the weight of this resonance (similar to
a_{\boldsymbol{q}}
in Eq. (
26
)). But for
\lambda\ll 1
,
\Delta/\omega_{\text{SF}}
is exponentially small, thus
Z_{s}
is “superexponentially” small. An analogous small-weight resonance is indicated in Fig.
3
of the main text at the largest momenta.
