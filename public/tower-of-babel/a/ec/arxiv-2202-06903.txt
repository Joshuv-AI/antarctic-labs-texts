Quadratic Forms in Prime Variables with small off-diagonal ranks
Jakub Dobrowolski

Abstract
. The main goal of this note is to establish the limits of L. Zhao’s techniques for counting solutions to quadratic forms in prime variables. Zhao considered forms with rank at least 9, and showed that these equations have solutions in primes provided there are no local obstructions. We consider in detail the degenerate cases of off-diagonal rank 1 and 2, and improve the rank lower bounds to at least 6 and at least 8 respectively. These results complement a recent breakthrough of Green on the non-degenerate rank 8 case.



1.
Introduction

This entire paper concentrates on a problem of finding solutions to the equation

(1)

\textbf{x}^{T}A\textbf{x}=t

where
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is a symmetric matrix,
t\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x
is a vector with all entries being prime. This problem was first looked at by Hua
[
2
]
. He proved that in the case of
A
an identity matrix,
n=5
and
t\equiv 5
(mod 24) sufficiently large we can always find a solution to equation (1) in primes. Not long ago Jianya Liu
[
3
]
was able to handle the more general case. For
A
satisfying
\operatorname{rank}(A)\geq 10
and some mild conditions, he was able to prove that an asymptotic number of solutions to (1) in primes in a box
[0,X]^{n}
is at least
C_{A}X^{n-2}(\log X)^{-n}
where
C_{A}>0
is some constant.
Lilu Zhao
[
1
]
was able to prove that
\operatorname{rank}(A)\geq 9
suffices for all regular matrices
A
. We will be mostly using his techniques throughout this paper. Most recently Ben Green
[
4
]
was able to prove using techniques from representation theory that under some mild non-degeneracy conditions if
\operatorname{rank}(A)=8
and
A
has an off-diagonal submatrix of rank 4 then we can find
C_{A}X^{6}(\log X)^{-8}
solutions to (
1
) in a box
[0,X]^{8}
with
C_{A}>0
.

In this paper we will be working with

(2)

\mathcal{N}_{A,t}(X)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq x_{1},...,x_{n}\leq X\\
\textbf{x}^{T}A\textbf{x}=t\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Lambda(x_{i})

where
\Lambda(x)
is the von Mangoldt function and
\mathcal{N}_{A,t}
is calculating number of solutions to (
1
) in prime powers in
[0,X]^{n}
box. This weighting is rather standard and makes the sum estimates much simpler. Finally we will write
\operatorname{rank}_{\operatorname{off}}(A)=k
if the biggest rank of a submatrix of
A
which does not contain diagonal entries is exactly
k
. We can now state main theorem of this paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}(A,t)
be defined as in (
11
) and
A
be an indefinite matrix satisfying one of the following:

(i)
\operatorname{rank}(A)\geq 6
and
\operatorname{rank}_{\operatorname{off}}(A)=1
;

(ii)
\operatorname{rank}(A)\geq 8
and
\operatorname{rank}_{\operatorname{off}}(A)=2
.

Then:

(3)

\mathcal{N}_{A,t}(X)=\mathfrak{S}(A,t)X^{n-2}+O_{K}(X^{n-2}(\log X)^{-K})

where
K
is an arbitrarily large constant.

This result was probably known to Zhao but he didn’t explicitly write down the details. With this theorem we establish the limitations of his methods for off-diagonal ranks 1 and 2.

It’s also important to point out how Theorem 1.1 relates to the result of Ben Green
[
4
]
. For
A\in GL_{8}(\mathbb{Z})
,
0\leq r=\operatorname{rank}_{\operatorname{off}}(A)\leq 4
. The case
r=0
is a result due to Hua
[
2
]
and for
r=1,2
Theorem 1.1 applies. Under mild non-degeneracy conditions the result of Ben Green deals with case
r=4
. Thus there’s only case
r=3
left to improve the restriction on
A
to
\operatorname{rank}(A)\geq 8
.

2.
Notation

Notation in this paper is mostly standard. We write
e(z)
for
e^{2\pi iz}
and we will let
X
be a sufficiently large integer and
L=\log X
. When we write
\lvert\textbf{x}\rvert\leq X
or
\textbf{x}\leq X
we mean that coefficients
x_{i}
of
x
satisfy
\lvert x_{i}\rvert\leq X
or
x_{i}\leq X
respectively.

In this paper matrix
A
and matrices
A_{i}
will always be symmetric.
We will write
M_{n,k}(S)
for the set of
n
by
k
matrices with entries in
S
and
M_{n}(S)
for the set of
n
by
n
matrices with entries in
S
. Finally as usual we denote by
GL_{n}(S)
the set of
n
by
n
invertible matrices with entries in
S
.

For
\textbf{x}^{T}=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})
we will write
\Lambda(\textbf{x})=\Lambda(x_{1})\Lambda(x_{2})...\Lambda(x_{n})
.

Many times in this paper we will be summing over the number of integer solutions of certain linear and bilinear equations in some box. Instead of writing that as

(4)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}equation1\\
equation2\\
equation3\\
\end{subarray}}1

we will write it as

(5)

\Bigg\{\begin{subarray}{c}equation1\\
equation2\\
equation3\end{subarray}\Bigg\}

to put a focus on the equations themselves. It will be clear from context which unknowns are actually variables and all of them will be lying in the box
[CX,C^{\prime}X]
for
C,C^{\prime}
some real constants.

3.
General approach to the problem

As usual in these kinds of problems we introduce the corresponding exponential sum

(6)

S(\alpha)=\sum_{1\leq\textbf{x}\leq X}\Lambda(\textbf{x})e(\alpha\textbf{x}^{T}A\textbf{x})

and note that

(7)

\int_{0}^{1}S(\alpha)e(-\alpha t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\textbf{x}\leq X\\
\textbf{x}^{T}A\textbf{x}=t\end{subarray}}\Lambda(\textbf{x})=\mathcal{N}_{A,t}(X).

For
a,q
coprime we will let:

(8)

\mathcal{M}(a,q;Q)=\{\alpha:\lvert\alpha-\tfrac{a}{q}\rvert\leq\tfrac{Q}{qX^{2}}\}.

For fixed
Q\leq X/2
sets
\mathcal{M}(a,q;Q)
are disjoint for different
a
and
q
provided that
q\leq Q
. We can then write

(9)

\mathcal{M}(Q)=\bigcup_{q=1}^{Q}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=1\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}^{q}\mathcal{M}(a,q;Q)

We define major arcs to be
\mathfrak{M}=\mathcal{M}(P)
where
P=L^{K}
. In this paper
K
and
K^{\prime}
will denote arbitrarily large parameters independent of
X
, satisfying
K>K^{\prime}
, which may change from line to line.

Finally we will let
\mathfrak{m}=[X^{-1},1+X^{-1}]\setminus\mathfrak{M}
be the minor arcs.

Let’s define a few quantities:

(10)

C(q,a)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\mathbf{h}\leq q\\
(\mathbf{h},q)=1\end{subarray}}e\Big(\mathbf{h}^{T}A\mathbf{h}\frac{a}{q}\Big),

(11)

\mathfrak{S}(A,t)=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a\leq q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}C(q,a)e\Big(-\frac{at}{q}\Big).

In the literature
\mathfrak{S}(A,t)
is referred to as the singular series. In
[
1
]
(Lemma 3.6) Zhao proved that for invertible, indefinite
A
with
\operatorname{rank}(A)\geq 5
major arcs bring the expected main contribution in
\int_{0}^{1}S(\alpha)e(-\alpha t)\,d\alpha
. Namely that:

(12)

\int_{\mathfrak{M}}S(\alpha)e(-\alpha t)\,d\alpha=\mathfrak{S}(A,t)X^{n-2}+O(X^{n-2}L^{-K})

if equation (1) has positive real solutions and where
\mathfrak{S}(A,t)\gg 1
provided that
\textbf{x}^{T}A\textbf{x}=t
has local solutions in the set of
p
-adic units for all primes
p
.

So all that’s left to do is to estimate the integral over the minor arcs. Our goal is to show that for certain classes of matrices

(13)

\int_{\mathfrak{m}}S(\alpha)e(-\alpha t)\,d\alpha\ll X^{n-2}L^{-K}.

This, combined with the result of Zhao about major arcs, will give us the following:

(14)

\int_{[0,1]}S(\alpha)e(-\alpha t)\,d\alpha=\mathfrak{S}(A,t)X^{n-2}+O(X^{n-2}L^{-K})\gg X^{n-2}

under all the local conditions mentioned above and hence proving Theorem 1.1.

Let’s look at the trivial bound for the integral over the minor arcs first.

(15)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{\mathfrak{m}}S(\alpha)e(-\alpha t)\,d\alpha\right\rvert\leq\sup_{\alpha\in\mathfrak{m}}\displaystyle\left\lvert S(\alpha)\right\rvert\leq\sum_{\textbf{x}\leq X}\Lambda(\textbf{x})\leq L^{n}X^{n}.

Let’s note that we’re still an arbitrarily large power of
\log
and power of
X^{2}
off of our goal in (13).
So the goal for this paper is to save this much over the trivial bound above.

To get arbitrarily large logarithm savings we will use a standard result:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathfrak{m}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
d\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}
, then we have

(16)

\Big\lvert\sum_{x\leq X}\Lambda(x)e(\alpha dx^{2}+\beta x)\Big\rvert\ll_{d,K}XL^{-K}.

The proof of it can be found in
[
5
]
as Theorem 10.
We now state some lemmas which are the current tool set for saving power of
X^{2}
in the integral over the minor arcs. They can be found with proofs in
[
1
]
as Lemmas 5.6, 5.7, 5.8 respectively.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
C\in M_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
be a symmetric matrix and
H\in M_{n,k}(\mathbb{Q})
. For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we let:

(17)

\mathcal{F}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})=\sum_{\textbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\Lambda(\textbf{x})e(\alpha\textbf{x}^{T}C\textbf{x}+\textbf{x}^{T}H\bm{\beta})

for
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}
a finite subset. Let

(18)

\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\textbf{y}\in\mathcal{X}\\
\textbf{x}^{T}C\textbf{x}=\textbf{y}^{T}C\textbf{y}\\
\textbf{x}^{T}H=\textbf{y}^{T}H\end{subarray}}\Lambda(\textbf{x})\Lambda(\textbf{y})

Then

(19)

\int_{[0,1]^{k+1}}\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathcal{F}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})\right\rvert^{2}\,d\alpha\,d\bm{\beta}\ll\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F})

with the implied constant depending only on the matrices
C
and
H
.

For the proof of Lemma 3.2 we simply expand
\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathcal{F}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})\right\rvert^{2}
into a double sum and use orthogonal property of the exponential.

Lemma 3.3
.

With
C
and
H
as above one has

(20)

\Bigg\{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{x}^{T}C\textbf{x}=\textbf{y}^{T}C\textbf{y}\\
\textbf{x}^{T}H=\textbf{y}^{T}H\end{subarray}\Bigg\}\ll\Bigg\{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{x}^{T}C\textbf{y}=0\\
\textbf{x}^{T}H=0\end{subarray}\Bigg\}

The proof of this Lemma is just a clever change of coordinates
\mathbf{u}=\textbf{x}+\textbf{y}
and
\mathbf{v}=\textbf{x}-\textbf{y}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
C\in M_{n,k}(\mathbb{Q})
and
\operatorname{rank}(C)\geq 2
then

(21)

\Big\{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{x}^{T}C\textbf{y}=0\end{subarray}\Big\}\ll LX^{k+n-2}

with the implied constant depending only on
C
.

This is the key step to Zhao’s estimates. Let’s note that we can trivially bound
\big\{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{x}^{T}C\textbf{y}=0\end{subarray}\big\}
by
X^{k+n}
. So we’re getting
X^{2}
saving over the trivial estimate with this lemma.

Let’s briefly discuss the use of mentioned lemmas. In all of the problems in section 4 we will be dealing with integrals of the form:

(22)

\int_{[0,1]^{k+1}}\sum_{\textbf{x}\leq X}\Lambda(\textbf{x})e(\alpha\textbf{x}^{T}C\textbf{x}+\textbf{x}^{T}H\bm{\beta})\,d\alpha\,d\bm{\beta}

where
C\in M_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
satisfies
\operatorname{rank}(C)\geq 2
. We can apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to get it to the form in Lemma 3.2 with.
So we will be able to trivially estimate

(23)

\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F})\ll L^{2n}\Bigg\{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{x}^{T}C\textbf{x}=\textbf{y}^{T}C\textbf{y}\\
\textbf{x}^{T}H=\textbf{y}^{T}H\end{subarray}\Bigg\}

and apply Lemma 3.3. From this, after some change of coordinates we will apply Lemma 3.4. Notice what would happen if we tried to estimate the integral in lemma 3.2 in a more crude way.

(24)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{[0,1]^{k+1}}\sum_{\textbf{x}\leq X}\Lambda(\textbf{x})e(\alpha\textbf{x}^{T}C\textbf{x}+\textbf{x}^{T}H\bm{\beta})\,d\alpha\,d\bm{\beta}\right\rvert\leq\sum_{\textbf{x}\leq X}\Lambda(\textbf{x})\ll L^{n}X^{n}

On the other hand after applying Lemma 3.2-3.4 we get a big improvement. The

saving in this case is of order
L^{O(1)}X^{(n-k-2)/2}
over the trivial estimate. Since in most of the cases we’ll be working with
k\leq n
this will bring us closer to the desired
X^{2}
saving that we need in our problem.

4.
Optimising Zhao’s results

4.1.
Off-diagonal rank 1

Firstly, as in Zhao’s paper, we need to work out the form of the matrix
A
. As
\operatorname{rank_{\operatorname{off}}}(A)=1
, we know
A
is not diagonal. So suppose
a_{1,2}\neq 0
. Then we have:

Lemma 4.1
.

If
A
satisfies
\operatorname{rank_{\operatorname{off}}}(A)=1
then
A
can be written as

(25)

A=\begin{pmatrix}a&\bm{\xi}^{T}\\
\bm{\xi}&D+h\bm{\xi}\bm{\xi}^{T}\end{pmatrix}

where
D=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1}
,…,
d_{n-1}
) with
d_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
,
h\in\mathbb{Q}
,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\textbf{0}\neq\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}
.

Proof.

Let’s define
c_{i}=a_{1,i+1}
for
i=1,...,n-1
.
We begin by noting that as
c_{1}\neq 0
, we have
\bm{\xi}^{T}\vcentcolon=(c_{1},...,c_{n-1})\neq\textbf{0}
. Now let’s define for
3\leq j\leq n
,
\bm{\eta}_{j}=(a_{j,2},...,a_{j,j-1},a_{j,j+1},...,a_{j,n})^{T}
,
\bm{\xi}_{j}=(a_{1,2},...a_{1,j-1},a_{1,j+1},...,a_{1,n})^{T}
and
\bm{\eta}_{2}=(a_{2,3},a_{2,4},...,a_{2,n})^{T}
,
\bm{\xi}_{2}=(a_{1,3},a_{1,4},...,a_{1,n})^{T}
. We know that
\operatorname{rank_{\operatorname{off}}}(A)=1
so for each
j
,
\bm{\eta}_{j}
is a rational multiple of
\bm{\xi}_{j}
as both of those are vectors not on the diagonal.
Therefore there is exactly one
a_{j,j}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}
and
h_{j}\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
(a_{j,2},...,a_{j,j-1},a_{j,j}^{\prime},a_{j,j+1},...,a_{j,n})=h_{j}\bm{\xi}^{T}
.
Let’s write

(26)

A=\begin{pmatrix}a&\bm{\xi}^{T}\\
\bm{\xi}&A_{1}\end{pmatrix}

and let
\textbf{h}^{T}=(h_{2},...,h_{n})
. By comparing coefficients we note that
\bm{h}\bm{\xi}^{T}-A_{1}
must be a diagonal matrix, call it
D
. Finally
\bm{h}\bm{\xi}^{T}
is a symmetric matrix so for each
1\leq k,\,l\leq n-1
we have
c_{k}h_{l}=c_{l}h_{k}
. As
c_{1}\neq 0
we can write

(27)

h_{k}=c_{k}\frac{h_{1}}{c_{1}}.

So
\bm{h}=h\bm{\xi}
for
h=\tfrac{h_{1}}{c_{1}}\in\mathbb{Q}
and we get the desired form.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let A be given by the form above with
\operatorname{rank}(A)\geq 6
and let

c_{i}=a_{1,i+1}
. We can find pairwise distinct
b_{i}
with

1\leq b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4},b_{5}\leq n-1
such that
c_{b_{1}}d_{b_{2}}d_{b_{3}}d_{b_{4}}d_{b_{5}}\neq 0
.

Proof.

By
\operatorname{rank}(A)\geq 6
we have
\operatorname{rank}(D)\geq 4
. We also assumed w.l.o.g. that
c_{1}\neq 0
so let’s choose
b_{1}=1
. Suppose that the above doesn’t hold. Namely, for any quadruple

k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\geq 2
we have
d_{k_{1}}d_{k_{2}}d_{k_{3}}d_{k_{4}}=0
. This gives the upper bound
\operatorname{rank}(D)\leq 4
, so we only have the case of
\operatorname{rank}(D)=4
left to consider.

We may suppose that
d_{1}d_{2}d_{3}d_{4}\neq 0
and
d_{j}=0
for
j>4
. But then by looking at the rank of the whole matrix we conclude that
\operatorname{rank}(c_{5},c_{6},...,c_{n-1})\neq 0
. That means
c_{j}\neq 0
for some
j\geq 5
giving us a quintuple
1,2,3,4,j
.
∎

With the desired form we can move on to the estimates of
S(\alpha)
over the minor arcs.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
satisfy
\operatorname{rank_{\operatorname{off}}}(A)=1
with
n\geq 6
, then

\int_{\mathfrak{m}}\displaystyle\left\lvert S(\alpha)\right\rvert\,d\alpha\ll X^{n-2}L^{-K}

Proof.

We start by noting that we may use lemmas above to read information on the form of
A
. Then we can write
S(\alpha)
as below:

(28)

S(\alpha)=\sum_{x\leq X}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{n-1}\\
\textbf{y}\leq X\end{subarray}}\Lambda(x)\Lambda(\textbf{y})e(\alpha(ax^{2}+2x\bm{\xi}^{T}\textbf{y}+\textbf{y}^{T}D\textbf{y}+h\textbf{y}^{T}\bm{\xi}\bm{\xi}^{T}\textbf{y})).

For each
\textbf{y}\leq X
we will let
z=\bm{\xi}^{T}\textbf{y}
. We can use orthogonality property of the exponential function to write

(29)

\displaystyle S(\alpha)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{\lvert z\rvert\ll X}\sum_{x\leq X}\sum_{\textbf{y}\leq X}\Lambda(\textbf{y})\Lambda(x)e(\alpha(ax^{2}+2xz+\textbf{y}^{T}D\textbf{y}+hz^{2}))

\displaystyle\times e((\bm{\xi}\textbf{y}^{T}-z)\beta)\,d\beta

Let’s introduce new sums using Zhao’s notation.

(30)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})=\sum_{\lvert z\rvert\ll X}\sum_{x\leq X}\Lambda(x)e(\alpha(ax^{2}+2xz+hz^{2})-z\beta)

\displaystyle f_{j}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})=\sum_{y_{j}\leq X}\Lambda(y_{j})e(\alpha d_{j}y_{j}^{2}+y_{j}c_{j}\beta)

for
j=1,...,n-1
. By the above lemma we can find a quintuple
b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4},b_{5}
and suppose for notational simplicity that it’s
1,2,3,4,5
i.e.
c_{1}d_{2}d_{3}d_{4}d_{5}\neq 0
. We bound the integral using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

(31)

\int_{\mathfrak{m}}\displaystyle\left\lvert S(\alpha)\right\rvert\,d\alpha\leq(\mathcal{I}_{1}\mathcal{I}_{2})^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\prod_{i=5}^{n-1}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathfrak{m}\\
\beta\in[0,1]\end{subarray}}\Big\lvert\sum_{y_{i}\leq X}\Lambda(y_{i})e(\alpha d_{i}y_{i}^{2}+c_{i}y_{i}\beta)\Big\rvert

where

(32)

\mathcal{I}_{1}=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}(\alpha,\beta)f_{4}(\alpha,\beta)\Big\rvert^{2}\,d\beta\,d\alpha

and

(33)

\mathcal{I}_{2}=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\Big\lvert f_{1}(\alpha,\beta)f_{2}(\alpha,\beta)f_{3}(\alpha,\beta)\Big\rvert^{2}\,d\beta\,d\alpha.

As
d_{5}\neq 0
we can bound the product in (
31
) using Lemma 3.1 by

(34)

\prod_{i=5}^{n-1}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathfrak{m}\\
\beta\in[0,1]\end{subarray}}\Big\lvert\sum_{y_{i}\leq X}\Lambda(y_{i})e(\alpha d_{i}y_{i}^{2}+c_{i}y_{i}\beta)\Big\rvert\ll X^{n-5}L^{-K}

where for
i\geq 6
we used a trivial bound on the sum:
LX
.

Now we turn to the two integrals. Note that by Lemma 3.2

(35)

\mathcal{I}_{1}\ll L^{4}\Bigg\{\begin{subarray}{c}ax^{2}+2xz+hz^{2}+d_{4}y_{4}^{2}=ax^{\prime 2}+2x^{\prime}z^{\prime}+hz^{\prime 2}+d_{4}y_{4}^{\prime 2}\\
c_{4}y_{4}-z=c_{4}y_{4}^{\prime}-z^{\prime}\end{subarray}\Bigg\}.

We can therefore apply Lemma 3.3 to get

(36)

\mathcal{I}_{1}\ll L^{4}\Bigg\{\begin{subarray}{c}axx^{\prime}+xz^{\prime}+x^{\prime}z+hzz^{\prime}+d_{4}y_{4}y_{4}^{\prime}=0\\
c_{4}y_{4}-z=0\end{subarray}\Bigg\}\\

and if we plug in
z=c_{4}y_{4}
note that we get a bilinear equation in 5 variables.

(37)

\mathcal{I}_{1}\ll L^{4}\Bigg\{axx^{\prime}+xz^{\prime}+c_{4}y_{4}x^{\prime}+hc_{4}y_{4}z^{\prime}+d_{4}y_{4}y_{4}^{\prime}=0\Bigg\}

The corresponding matrix is of rank at least 2 as coefficients by
xz^{\prime}
and
y_{4}y_{4}^{\prime}
are nonzero. So by Lemma 3.4 we can bound the last sum by
L^{5}X^{3}
.
We do the same procedure for the second integral.

(38)

\mathcal{I}_{2}=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\Big\lvert f_{1}(\alpha,\beta)f_{2}(\alpha,\beta)f_{3}(\alpha,\beta)\Big\rvert^{2}\,d\beta\,d\alpha\ll L^{6}\Bigg\{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}y_{1}y_{1}^{\prime}+d_{2}y_{2}y_{2}^{\prime}+d_{3}y_{3}y_{3}^{\prime}=0\\
c_{1}y_{1}+c_{2}y_{2}+c_{3}y_{3}=0\end{subarray}\Bigg\}

where we already used Lemma 3.2 and 3.3. Now since
c_{1}\neq 0
we can divide by it and write
y_{1}
in terms of
y_{2}
and
y_{3}
. So we get:

(39)

\mathcal{I}_{2}\ll L^{6}\Bigg\{-d_{1}y_{1}^{\prime}(c_{2}y_{2}+c_{3}y_{3})/c_{1}+d_{2}y_{2}y_{2}^{\prime}+d_{3}y_{3}y_{3}^{\prime}=0\Bigg\}

a bilinear equation in 5 variables with rank of a corresponding matrix at least two as this time
d_{2}d_{3}\neq 0
. So we can apply Lemma 3.4 to get

(40)

\mathcal{I}_{2}\ll L^{7}X^{3}.

We combine the results to get that gives us

(41)

\int_{\mathfrak{m}}\displaystyle\left\lvert S(\alpha)\right\rvert\,d\alpha\ll L^{-K}X^{n-5}L^{5/2}X^{3/2}L^{5/2}X^{3/2}\ll L^{-K}X^{n-2}

as we wanted.
∎

4.2.
Off-diagonal rank 2

Suppose now without loss of generality that

\operatorname{rank}_{\operatorname{off}}(A)=\operatorname{rank}(B)=2
where

B=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1,3}&a_{1,4}\\
a_{2,3}&a_{2,4}\end{pmatrix}
.
We consider different cases depending on ranks of following matrices:

(42)

\displaystyle B_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1,3}&a_{1,5}&a_{1,6}&.&.&.&a_{1,n}\\
a_{2,3}&a_{2,5}&a_{2,6}&.&.&.&a_{2,n}\end{pmatrix}

(43)

\displaystyle B_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1,4}&a_{1,5}&a_{1,6}&.&.&.&a_{1,n}\\
a_{2,4}&a_{2,5}&a_{2,6}&.&.&.&a_{2,n}\end{pmatrix}.

\textbf{x}^{T}(\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2})+(z_{3}v_{3},-q)=0\end{subarray}\Bigg\}.

Note that as
(\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2})
is invertible we can again write
x
as a combination of
z_{3}
and
q
. After that we are left with bilinear equation in 6 variables with rank at least 2

(69)

\Bigg\{-2(z_{3}v_{3},-q)(\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2})^{-1}A_{1}\textbf{x}^{\prime}+2h\epsilon_{3}z_{3}s^{\prime}+2d_{3}z_{3}z_{3}^{\prime}+qs^{\prime}+\epsilon_{3}z_{3}\bm{\gamma}_{2}^{T}\textbf{x}^{\prime}=0\Bigg\}.

So we again apply Lemma 3.4 to get:

(70)

\left(\int_{[0,1]^{3}}\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathcal{F}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})f_{3}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})\right\rvert^{2}\,d\alpha\,d\bm{\beta}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\ll(L^{7}X^{4})^{1/2}=L^{7/2}X^{2}.

For the second integral we use Lemmas 3.2 and 3.3 to arrive at:

(71)

\int_{[0,1]^{3}}\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathcal{H}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})f_{2}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})\right\rvert^{2}\,d\alpha\,d\bm{\beta}\ll L^{4}\Bigg\{\begin{subarray}{c}2d_{2}z_{2}z_{2}^{\prime}+wy^{\prime}+w^{\prime}y=0\\
2z_{2}v_{2}+ya-w=0\\
\epsilon_{2}z_{2}=0\end{subarray}\Bigg\}.

As
\epsilon_{2}\neq 0
we must have
z_{2}=0
. Leaving us with:

(72)

L^{4}\Bigg\{\begin{subarray}{c}wy^{\prime}+w^{\prime}y=0\\
ya=w\end{subarray}\Bigg\}\ll L^{4}\Bigg\{y(ay^{\prime}+w^{\prime})=0\Bigg\}\ll L^{4}X^{2}.

Finally as
\operatorname{rank}(D)\geq 3
we can find
k\neq 2,3
such that
d_{k}\neq 0
. Therefore by Lemma 3.1 we get

(73)

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathfrak{m}\\
\bm{\beta}\in[0,1]^{2}\end{subarray}}\displaystyle\left\lvert\prod_{k\neq 2,3}f_{k}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})\right\rvert\ll L^{-K}X^{n-5}

After combining all three estimates we get the desired result.
∎

4.2.3.
Proof of Lemma 4.6

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\operatorname{rank}(B_{1})=\operatorname{rank}(B_{2})=2
. Then
A
is of the form:

A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&(\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2})C\\
C^{T}(\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2})^{T}&D+C^{T}HC\end{pmatrix}

where
(\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2})\in GL_{2}(\mathbb{Q})
,
C\in M_{2,n-2}(\mathbb{Z})
,
H\in M_{2}(\mathbb{Q})
and
D=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},...,d_{n-2})

Proof.

The proof goes along the same lines as before. Note that each vector
(a_{1,2+j},a_{2,2+j})^{T}
for
j=1,2,...,n-2
is a linear combination of
(a_{1,3},a_{2,3})^{T}
and
(a_{1,4},a_{2,4})^{T}
. Therefore we have

A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&(\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2})C\\
C^{T}(\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2})^{T}&A_{2}\end{pmatrix}

after rescaling we get
C\in M_{2,n-2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\operatorname{rank}((\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2}))=2
. For the final part let’s define for
3\leq j\leq n
and
1\leq i\leq 2
,
\bm{\eta}_{j}=(a_{j,3},...,a_{j,j-1},a_{j,j+1},...,a_{j,n})^{T}
and
\bm{\theta}_{i,j}=(a_{i,3},...,a_{i,j-1},a_{i,j+1},...,a_{i,n})^{T}
. Similarly as before we can say that for each
j
,
\bm{\eta}_{j}
is a linear combination of
\bm{\theta}_{1,j},\bm{\theta}_{2,j}
. For
j=3
it’s because
\operatorname{rank}(B_{2})=2
for
j=4
because
\operatorname{rank}(B_{1})=2
and for
j\geq 5
because
\operatorname{rank}(B)=2
. Finally for
i=1,2
we define
\bm{\theta}_{i}=(a_{i,3},...,a_{i,n})^{T}
and for
3\leq j\leq n
we observe that there is a unique
a_{j,j}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
\bm{\varphi}_{j}=(a_{j,3},...,a_{j,j-1},a_{j,j}^{\prime},a_{j,j+1},...,a_{j,n})^{T}
is a linear combination of
\bm{\theta}_{1}
and
\bm{\theta}_{2}
. This means that
A
has the form

A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&(\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2})C\\
C^{T}(\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2})^{T}&D+MC\end{pmatrix}

and as
MC
is symmetric it can be written in the form
C^{T}HC
for

H\in M_{2}(\mathbb{Q})
.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
A
be given by the form above. Let
C=(\bm{\xi}_{1},\bm{\xi}_{2},...,\bm{\xi}_{n-2})
. Then we can find pairwise distinct
b_{i}
with
1\leq b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4},b_{5}\leq n-2
such that
\operatorname{rank}((\bm{\xi}_{b_{1}},\bm{\xi}_{b_{2}}))=2
and
d_{b_{3}}d_{b_{4}}d_{b_{5}}\neq 0

Proof.

We know that as
\operatorname{rank}(B)=2
we have
\operatorname{rank}(\bm{\xi}_{1},\bm{\xi}_{2})=2
. So suppose the above statement doesn’t happen. Namely
\forall k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\geq 3
we have
d_{k_{1}}d_{k_{2}}d_{k_{3}}=0
. This gives the upper bound
\operatorname{rank}(D)\leq 4
. But we know from looking at form of
A
and the fact that
\operatorname{rank}(A)\geq 8
we have
\operatorname{rank}(D)\geq 4
.

So we only have case
\operatorname{rank}(D)=4
left. We may suppose that
d_{1}d_{2}d_{3}d_{4}\neq 0
and
d_{j}=0
for
j\geq 4
. But then by looking at the rank of whole matrix we conclude that
\operatorname{rank}(\bm{\xi}_{5},\bm{\xi}_{6},...,\bm{\xi}_{n-2})=2
. That then obviously gives us a desired quintuple.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.6
.

Using Lemma
4.9
we know the structure of our exponential sum.

(74)

S(\alpha)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{x}\leq X\\
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{y}\leq X\\
\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{n-2}\end{subarray}}\Lambda(\textbf{x})\Lambda(\textbf{y})e(\alpha(\textbf{x}^{T}A_{1}\textbf{x}+2\textbf{x}^{T}(\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2})C\textbf{y}+\textbf{y}^{T}D\textbf{y}+\textbf{y}^{T}C^{T}HC^{T}\textbf{y}))

For each
\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{n-2}
satisfying
\textbf{y}\leq X
we let
\textbf{z}=C\textbf{y}
and use orthogonality of exponential function to get

(75)

\displaystyle S(\alpha)={}

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lvert\textbf{z}\rvert\ll X\\
\textbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{x}\leq X\\
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{y}\leq X\\
\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{n-2}\end{subarray}}\Lambda(\textbf{x})\Lambda(\textbf{y})e(\alpha(\textbf{x}^{T}A_{1}\textbf{x}+2\textbf{x}^{T}(\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2})\textbf{z}+\textbf{y}^{T}D\textbf{y})

\displaystyle\times e(\alpha\textbf{z}^{T}H\textbf{z}+(\textbf{y}^{T}C^{T}-\textbf{z}^{T})\bm{\beta})\,d\bm{\beta}.

We introduce new sums

(76)

\mathcal{F}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lvert\textbf{z}\rvert\ll X\\
\textbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{x}\leq X\\
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\end{subarray}}\Lambda(\textbf{x})e(\alpha(\textbf{x}^{T}A_{1}\textbf{x}+2\textbf{x}^{T}(\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2})\textbf{z}+\textbf{z}^{T}H\textbf{z})-\textbf{z}^{T}\bm{\beta})

and for
i=1,...,n-2

(77)

f_{i}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{i}\leq X\\
y_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\end{subarray}}\Lambda(y_{i})e(\alpha d_{i}y_{i}^{2}+y_{i}\bm{\xi}_{i}^{T}\bm{\beta})

where
\bm{\xi}_{i}
is the
i
-th column vector of
C
.
We again use CS inequality to get that:

(78)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathfrak{m}}\displaystyle\left\lvert S(\alpha)\right\rvert\,d\alpha\leq

\displaystyle\Bigg(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathfrak{m}\\
\bm{\beta}\in[0,1]^{2}\end{subarray}}\big|\prod_{k\neq b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4}}f_{k}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})\big|\Bigg)\displaystyle\left(\int_{[0,1]^{3}}\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathcal{F}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})\right\rvert^{2}\,d\alpha\,d\bm{\beta}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\times\displaystyle\left(\int_{[0,1]^{3}}\displaystyle\left\lvert f_{b_{1}}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})f_{b_{2}}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})f_{b_{3}}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})f_{b_{4}}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})\right\rvert^{2}\,d\alpha\,d\bm{\beta}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}

where
b_{i}
are chosen according to the rules from the previous lemma.
The supremum can be bounded using Lemma 3.1 (as
d_{b_{5}}\neq 0
) by

(79)

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathfrak{m}\\
\bm{\beta}\in[0,1]^{2}\end{subarray}}\displaystyle\left\lvert\prod_{k\neq b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4}}f_{k}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})\right\rvert\ll X^{n-6}L^{-K}.

First integral can be bounded using Lemma 3.2 and 3.3 by

(80)

\ll L^{4}\Bigg\{\begin{subarray}{c}2\textbf{x}^{T}A_{1}\textbf{x}^{\prime}+2\textbf{x}^{T}(\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2})\textbf{z}^{\prime}+2\textbf{z}^{T}(\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2})^{T}\textbf{x}^{\prime}+2\textbf{z}^{T}H\textbf{z}^{\prime}=0\\
z_{1}=0\\
z_{2}=0\end{subarray}\Bigg\}.

After plugging in
z_{1}=z_{2}=0
we are left with

(81)

\ll L^{4}\Bigg\{2\textbf{x}^{T}A_{1}\textbf{x}^{\prime}+2\textbf{x}^{T}(\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2})\textbf{z}^{\prime}=0\Bigg\}\ll L^{5}X^{4}

where we again used Lemma 3.4 as we’re working with 6 variables and
(\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2})
is invertible. If we look at the second integral and use Lemma 3.2 and 3.3 we obtain the bound:

(82)

\ll L^{8}\Bigg\{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{b_{1}}y_{b_{1}}y_{b_{1}}^{\prime}+d_{b_{2}}y_{b_{2}}y_{b_{2}}^{\prime}+d_{b_{3}}y_{b_{3}}y_{b_{3}}^{\prime}+d_{b_{4}}y_{b_{4}}y_{b_{4}}^{\prime}=0\\
y_{b_{1}}\bm{\xi}_{b_{1}}+y_{b_{2}}\bm{\xi}_{b_{2}}+y_{b_{3}}\bm{\xi}_{b_{3}}+y_{b_{4}}\bm{\xi}_{b_{4}}=0\end{subarray}\Bigg\}.

Because
\operatorname{rank}((\bm{\xi}_{b_{1}},\bm{\xi}_{b_{2}}))=2
we can write
y_{b_{1}}
and
y_{b_{2}}
in terms of
y_{b_{3}}
and
y_{b_{4}}
and plug in into the first equation. We are left with bilinear equation in 6 variables and as
d_{b_{3}}d_{b_{4}}\neq 0
it has rank at least 2. So after applying Lemma 3.4 we get

(83)

\left(\int_{[0,1]^{3}}\displaystyle\left\lvert f_{b_{1}}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})f_{b_{2}}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})f_{b_{3}}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})f_{b_{4}}(\alpha,\bm{\beta})\right\rvert^{2}\,d\alpha\,d\bm{\beta}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\ll(L^{9}X^{4})^{1/2}=L^{9/2}X^{2}

Once we combine all three estimated we get the desired result and finishes the whole discussion on off diagonal rank 2 matrices.
∎
