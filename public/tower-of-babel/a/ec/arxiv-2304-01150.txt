Algebraic and Geometric Models for Space Networking
William Bernardoni, Robert Cardona, Jacob Cleveland, Justin Curry, Robert Green, Brian Heller, Alan Hylton, Tung Lam, Robert Kassouf-Short

Abstract
In this paper we introduce some new algebraic and geometric perspectives on networked space communications. Our main contribution is a novel definition of a time-varying graph (TVG), defined in terms of a matrix with values in subsets of the real line 𝒫 ⁡ ( ℝ ) \displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}) . We leverage semi-ring properties of 𝒫 ⁡ ( ℝ ) \displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}) to model multi-hop communication in a TVG using matrix multiplication and a truncated Kleene star. This leads to novel statistics on the communication capacity of TVGs called lifetime curves, which we generate for large samples of randomly chosen STARLINK satellites, whose connectivity is modeled over day-long simulations. Determining when a large subsample of STARLINK is temporally strongly connected is further analyzed using novel metrics introduced here that are inspired by topological data analysis (TDA). To better model networking scenarios between the Earth and Mars, we introduce various semi-rings capable of modeling propagation delay as well as protocols common to Delay Tolerant Networking (DTN), such as store-and-forward. Finally, we illustrate the applicability of zigzag persistence for featurizing different space networks and demonstrate the efficacy of K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classification for distinguishing Earth-Mars and Earth-Moon satellite systems using time-varying topology alone.

Introduction and Outline

As humanity embarks on its next steps in space exploration, with international and commercial actors providing a huge influx of new space assets, the need to automate and scale space communications has become increasingly pressing.
Current communication in space—between, say, a rover on Mars and a particular building on Earth—is handled by teams of engineers manually scheduling which point-to-point links are to be used at what times to provide end-to-end transmission of data.
Once a message reaches a ground station on Earth traditional terrestrial networking theory, such as TCP/IP, takes over, which relies heavily on low latency and a largely static network architecture.
However, neither of these requirements hold in space.
Special relativity dictates that all communication is constrained by the speed of light, which means the fastest possible one-way communication between Earth and Mars is constrained to 3 and 22 light-minutes, depending on their relative positions in orbit.
Networking topology can also evolve rapidly, as illustrated by satellites in low-Earth orbit (LEO), whose orbits are typically 90 minutes and a random pair of satellites may have a line-of-sight connection for only a few minutes.
In all these scenarios occlusions typically force a break in point-to-point communication, which is handled by either local storage or the use of an alternate route that circumvents the occlusion.

Motivated by these problems, we introduce here a novel set of tools for modeling time-varying networks that appear in actual space networking situations.
Our main contribution is a clear definition of a time-varying graph (TVG), which we view as a matrix whose entries are populated by subsets of time.
We exploit the fact that subsets of time, viewed as elements of
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
, have a natural notion of addition and multiplication given by union and intersection, thereby making
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
into a semi-ring; see

With this observation in hand, we model end-to-end communication capacity more accurately by considering the Kleene star (

\displaystyle k

By considering the average measure of each entry

Prior Work in TDA on Time-Varying Graphs

Zigzag persistence
[
CSM09
,
CDS10
]
, which is a common tool from TDA, has been used to study time-evolving topology in dynamic networks for several years.
At a high-level, zigzag persistence reveals the formation, disappearance and duration of topological features of a space
\displaystyle X
that is parameterized by
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
, i.e., by using a function
\displaystyle f:X\to\mathbb{R}

To understand how topology at one time
\displaystyle t_{0}
is related to the topology at another time
\displaystyle t_{1}

\displaystyle f^{-1}(t_{0})\hookrightarrow f^{-1}[t_{0},t_{1}]\hookleftarrow f^{-1}(t_{1})

and takes homology, which is what gives zigzag persistence its name.
In order to analyze temporal topological features via zigzag persistence, a collection of snapshots of a temporal network are considered together with their zigzag unions (or intersections) to form a sequence of simplicial complexes.
Network snapshots can be obtained via a sliding window or temporal partitioning construction
[
Loz18
]
.
This approach is used in
[
Mye+23a
]
to analyze the Great Britain transportation network, for example.
A similar approach, which is used in
[
Mye+23
]
to analyze the social networks and cyber data of various communities, considers situations where the network snapshots are hypergraphs.
In the context of space communication, the study
[
Hyl+22
]
uses zigzag persistence on simulated space networks to identify and extract subnetwork structures in order to reduce the complexity of the network.
A more theoretical approach to dynamic graphs, carried out by Kim and Mémoli
[
KM17
]
, uses the Möbius inversion perspective on persistence to define a novel summary of time-evolving clusters called the
persistence clustergram

Prior Work on Algebraic Path Problems

To our knowledge, the semi-ring models of time-varying graphs advanced here have not been considered before.
In particular, we believe that the matrix TVG semi-ring, the propagation delay semi-ring, and the universal contact semi-ring are all new, with no clear presence in the literature.
We say this with much trepidation as the semi-ring perspective on networks has at least a 50 year history, with Carré’s pioneering work
[
Car71
]
as one of the first major papers.
This perspective has endured, with textbook length treatments in
[
Car79
,
Hei+06
,
GM08
]
and
[
BT10
]
.

In all these works, one wants to view any network characterization or optimization problem as a matrix with entries in a carefully chosen semi-ring.
Solutions to these characterization/optimization problems are usually calculated via the transitive closure of that matrix, i.e., the Kleene star.
For instance, and this is expanded on in more detail in
Section

2.4
, the shortest path problem on a network can be formulated use the tropical semi-ring
\displaystyle([0,\infty],\min,+)
.
One compares the costs along all paths using the
\displaystyle\min
(addition) operation, while each path cost is the result of aggregating arc weights along the path using the
\displaystyle+
(multiplication) operation.
The Kleene star for this matrix then consists of the lengths of pairwise shortest paths between nodes in networks, thus solving the all-pairs shortest-path problem with remarkable algebraic efficiency
[
Moh02
]
.
As Carré observed in
[
Car71
]
, these matrix-theoretic methods provably generalize direct methods of solving routing problems such as Bellman-Ford’s and Floyd–Warshal’s algorithms.
These observations were continued by
[
Min76
]
, who showed that generalized path algebras can be used to obtain the well-known pathfinding algorithms by Moore, Ford, and Dijkstra; see
[
Min98
,
Min98a
]
for more on this.

Surprisingly, our application of semi-rings to the internet-at-large is not new, as the works
[
GS05
,
Sob03
,
Sob01
,
Sob05
,
GG08
,
DG13
]
study internet routing protocols from this perspective.
However each of these works are concerned with so-called “closed networks,” where the nodes and edges are fixed from the outset.
More recent work, coming out of the Applied Category Theory (ACT) community, has developed a more flexible theory for networks that allows for node discovery and for routing between other, unknown networks; see the works of Jade Master
[
Mas20
,
Mas22
,
Mas21
]
, whose thesis
[
Mas21
]
made major headway in the theory of these so-called “open networks.” We anticipate that this work will be important in future work on developing generalized, composable algorithms for routing in space.

Algebraic Models for Time-Varying Graphs (TVGs)

Time-varying graphs (TVGs) have been studied extensively by many different groups of people, but there is no single agreed-upon definition of what a TVG should be.
One model of a TVG is that a time-varying graph is simply a graph sequence
\displaystyle G_{0},\ldots,G_{n}
, but this perspective obscures relationships across time.
Another model for a TVG (or temporal graph)
[
BM22
]
, which addresses this criticism, argues that a TVG is a graph
\displaystyle G=(V,E)
equipped with a function
\displaystyle\tau:E\to 2^{\mathbb{N}}
that specifies which indices in a graph sequence an edge lives.

Figure 1:
A screenshot from the Sattelite Orbital Analysis Program (SOAP) illustrates lines of sight between ground stations and satellites around Earth and Mars.

Both of these perspectives are inappropriate for the purposes of space networking, where we are primarily interested in when two assets (ground stations, rovers, satellites, etc.) have a clear line of sight for communication; see
Figure

1
.
Due to orbital mechanics, each line of sight in a space network starts at a
sunrise
time and ends with a
sunset
time, which together marks the boundary of a single interval of connectivity.
Since the metric properties of these intervals—
when
and
for how long?
—are crucial for determining when and how much data can be routed across our network, we introduce a framework for talking about lifetimes of connections. It will be useful to organize the collection of connection lifetimes into a poset structure organized by inclusion:

Definition 2.1

(Poset of Lifetimes)
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R})
be the
poset of lifetimes
.
A non-trivial element
\displaystyle a\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R})
is
a finite union of disjoint closed intervals

a=[x_{0},y_{0}]\cup\cdots\cup[x_{n},y_{n}],

where
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
and
\displaystyle y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, with
\displaystyle x_{n}\leq y_{n}\leq x_{n+1}
for all
\displaystyle n
.
The partial order on lifetimes is given by inclusion of subsets, i.e.,
\displaystyle a\preceq b
if
\displaystyle a\subseteq b
.

Sunrise and sunset times for line of sight communication in
Figure

1
define intervals of connectivity, which is what our definition of a TVG emphasizes.

Our definition of a time-varying graph is a graph that is equipped with an order-reversing map to the poset of lifetimes
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R})
.
More precisely, we have the following definition.

Definition 2.2

(TVG)
.

Let
\displaystyle G
be a graph, i.e., a set of edges
\displaystyle E
and a set of vertices
\displaystyle V
with an incidence relation
\displaystyle v<e
that indicates when a vertex
\displaystyle v
belongs to an edge
\displaystyle e
.
A
time-varying graph (TVG)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=(G,\ell_{M})
is a graph
\displaystyle G
along with an order-reversing
lifetime function

\ell_{M}:(G,<)^{op}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}),\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\text{if}\quad v<e\quad\Rightarrow\quad\ell_{M}(e)\subseteq\ell_{M}(v).

The assumption that
\displaystyle\ell_{M}(e)\subseteq\ell_{M}(v)
is the
containment axiom
, as it requires that a vertex be alive whenever an edge is alive.

Remark 2.3
.

We remark that any adjective for graphs descends to a modifier on time-varying graphs.
For example,
Definition

2.2
generalizes to hypergraphs as it only requires the notion of incidence (or containment) of vertices into hyperedges; see
[
Mye+23
]
, which uses temporal attribution for hypergraph applications.
Additionally, if
\displaystyle G
is a directed multigraph, then we can define
\displaystyle v<e
iff
\displaystyle v
is the head or tail of the edge
\displaystyle e
, but directedness of an edge
\displaystyle e
that goes from
\displaystyle i
to
\displaystyle j
.
Finally, recall that a (directed) graph is
simple
if there exists at most one (directed) edge between any two vertices.

For simplicity, we will work with an alternative matrix formulation for TVGs, which can be viewed as the primary definition for this paper. Note that in this approach, the poset is specialized to a total order.

Definition 2.4

(Lifetime Matrices and General Matrix TVGs)
.

Every simple directed TVG
\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}=(G,\ell_{M})}
that is equipped with a total order on its vertex set
\displaystyle V
has a representative
matrix of lifetimes
:

M:V\times V\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R})\qquad(i,j)\mapsto M(i,j)=\ell_{M}(i,j)\subseteq\mathbb{R}.

The collection of all lifetime matrices, written
\displaystyle\Mat_{n}(\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}))
, includes every possible matrix with entries in
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R})
, without any containment axiom.
We call a matrix whose entries are arbitrary subsets of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,
\displaystyle M\in\Mat_{n}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}))
, a
matrix TVG
.

Definition

2.4
should remind the reader of the adjacency matrix in graph theory, which for a simple graph has
\displaystyle 0
s along the diagonal and
\displaystyle 1
s whenever an edge from
\displaystyle i
to
\displaystyle j
exists.
One important difference is that we typically assume that a lifetime matrix
\displaystyle M
has
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
s along the diagonal, which, by analogy would say that every vertex has a self-loop sitting over it. This is a reasonable interpretation in the setting of message passing across a time-evolving graph, but has some drawbacks when trying to emulate (or generalize) more traditional constructions in graph theory.
To allow ourselves to work with both conventions, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 2.5

(Adjacency Matrix of a TVG)
.

The
adjacency matrix
of a simple directed TVG
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=(G,\ell_{M})
, written
\displaystyle A
, is identical to the associated matrix TVG
\displaystyle M
except that
\displaystyle A_{ii}=\varnothing
.

2.1
Semi-Ring and Matrix Perspectives on Graphs and TVGs

One of the important reasons for working with matrices is that certain operations—such as addition and multiplication—are available on matrices, which are not obvious for graphs.
In this section we recall the interpretation of simple directed graphs as a matrix of Booleans and how the higher powers of the matrix of Booleans models walks of corresponding length.
This then clears the way for defining and interpreting these matrix operations for TVGs.
At the heart of both of these perspectives is the observation that addition and multiplication are very general operations and are formally unified in the language of semi-rings, which are rings with negatives removed.

Definition 2.6
.

A
semi-ring

\displaystyle(S,\oplus,\otimes,\n,\e)
consists of a set
\displaystyle S
with an addition operation
\displaystyle\oplus
and a multipliciation operation
\displaystyle\otimes
along with neutral elements
\displaystyle\n
(the “0” element) and
\displaystyle\e
(the “1” element) for these operations. These operations need to further satisfy the following four collections of identities:

1.

\displaystyle(S,\oplus,\n)
is a commutative monoid (see
Definition

A.1
) with identity element
\displaystyle\n
. This means:

•

\displaystyle(a\oplus b)\oplus c=a\oplus(b\oplus c)

•

\displaystyle a\oplus b=b\oplus a

•

\displaystyle\n\oplus a=a=a\oplus\n

2.

\displaystyle(S,\otimes)
is a (not necessarily commutative) monoid with identity element
\displaystyle\e
. This means

•

\displaystyle(a\otimes b)\otimes c=a\otimes(b\otimes c)

•

\displaystyle\e\otimes a=a=a\otimes\e

3.

Multiplication distributes over addition on both the left and right:

•

\displaystyle a\otimes(b\oplus c)=(a\otimes b)\oplus(a\otimes c)

•

\displaystyle(a\oplus b)\otimes c=(a\otimes c)\oplus(b\otimes c)

4.

Multiplication by
\displaystyle\n
(the “
\displaystyle 0
” element) annihilates
\displaystyle S
:

•

\displaystyle\n\otimes a=\n=a\otimes\n

Remark 2.7
.

Since semi-rings are rings with negatives removed, some authors prefer to call semi-rings
rigs
—because they are “rings” with the “n” (as in “negatives”) removed.

The prototypical example of a semi-ring is the set of natural numbers
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
with normal addition and multiplication.
We will work primarily with the following semi-rings, which are pertinent to the study of TVGs.

Definition 2.8

(Boolean Semi-Ring)
.

Consider the set
\displaystyle\Bool=\{\bot,\top\}
with
\displaystyle\n=\bot=0
corresponding to
FALSE
and
\displaystyle\e=\top=1
corresponding to
TRUE
. If we define
\displaystyle\oplus=\vee
to be the logical
OR
operation and
\displaystyle\otimes=\wedge
to be the logical
AND
operation, then
\displaystyle(\Bool,\vee,\wedge,\bot,\top)
defines the
Boolean semi-ring
.

Definition 2.9

(Path Semi-Ring)
.

Suppose
\displaystyle G=(V,E)
is a simple directed graph.
Let
\displaystyle\Path(G)
be the set of formal combinations of paths (or
walks
) in
\displaystyle G
of arbitrary length, i.e.,

\Path(G)=\{\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}\mid\gamma_{i}=[v_{i_{0}},\ldots,v_{i_{n}}],\quad\text{where}\quad\forall i,j\,(v_{i_{j}},v_{i_{j+1}})\in E\}.

If
\displaystyle a=\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}
and
\displaystyle b=\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}
, then
\displaystyle a+b
is the Boolean sum (i.e., set-theoretic union) of the paths in
\displaystyle a
with the paths in
\displaystyle b
.
The additive neutral element
\displaystyle\n
is the empty collection of paths
\displaystyle\varnothing
.
For multiplication, we first define concatenation of paths
\displaystyle\gamma_{i}\ast\gamma_{j}
to be the extension of the path
\displaystyle\gamma_{i}
by
\displaystyle\gamma_{j}
if the last vertex in
\displaystyle\gamma_{i}
is the first vertex in
\displaystyle\gamma_{j}
, otherwise it is
\displaystyle\varnothing
; notice this multiplication is not commutative.
Extending linearly allows us to define

a\ast b=(\sum_{i}\gamma_{i})\ast(\sum_{j}\gamma_{j})=\sum_{ij}\gamma_{i}\ast\gamma_{j},\quad\text{where}\quad\e=1=\sum_{v_{i}\in V}[v_{i}]

is a multiplicative neutral element.

Definition 2.10

(Lifetime and Powerset Semi-Ring)
.

The poset of lifetimes
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R})
(
Definition

2.1
) is a commutative semi-ring. This is actually a sub-semi-ring of the bigger semi-ring consisting of the powerset of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
, written
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
.
Addition and multiplication are defined as

•

\displaystyle a+b:=a\cup b
, with neutral element
\displaystyle\n=\varnothing
being the empty set, and

•

\displaystyle a\cdot b:=a\cap b
, with neutral element
\displaystyle\e=\mathbb{R}
being the whole set
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
.

More generally, the powerset
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(X)

\displaystyle X

Remark 2.11

(Idempotency and Partial Orders)

Semi-rings such as

(Function Semi-Ring)

Remark 2.13

(Boolean Functions and Subsets)

2.3
Non-Convergence of the Kleene Star: Semi-Rings for Propagation Delay

In all of the previous sections, we have focused on TVGs coming from space networking scenarios, such as STARLINK, where the speed of light is negligible.
In what follows we are going to investigate satellite systems with assets around the Earth’s moon (Luna) and Mars.
In both of these settings, line-of-sight communication may require significant time due to propagation delay, even though messages are transmitted at the speed of light.
As a reminder, one-way light travel to the Moon takes about 1.2 seconds; this represents a round trip time (RTT) that begins to preclude standard or traditional feedback mechanisms for reliability in communications though reactivity is still possible.
Messages sent to Mars can take anywhere between 3 and 22 minutes, depending on the relative location of Earth and Mars in their respective orbits. When the RTT is measured in minutes, reliability in communications tends to be based on being proactive rather than reactive.
All of this necessitates a new semi-ring capable of modelling propagation delay.

In this section we introduce the Propagation Delay semi-ring, which is a combination of the Lifetime semi-ring of
Definition

2.10
with a delay parameter.
For this semi-ring

2.16

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}

Moreover, the classical Kleene star convergence result of Carré (Theorem

2.25

This is illustrated experimentally using a 17-node simulation of an Earth-Mars-Moon system, where a ping originating at one node “echoes” ad infinitum throughout the network.
This lack of convergence of the Kleene star may be interpreted as an algebraic characterization of the difficulty of deep space routing and communication.

Review of Prior Semi-Rings for Graph Optimization Problems

In this section we review some prior work on semi-rings for solving the all-pairs shortest-path problem for a weighted graph and the shortest path problem with time-inhomogeneous edges, with and without capacity constraints.

2.4.1
Tropical/Min-Plus Semi-Ring

As mentioned in
Example

2.30
, the propagation delay semi-ring uses a “max-plus” semi-ring in the second coordinate.
This semi-ring is dual to a more popular semi-ring for graph optimization problems.

Definition 2.31

(Tropical Semi-Ring)
.

Consider the set
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
. If we define
\displaystyle a\oplus b=\min\{a,b\}
and
\displaystyle a\odot b=a+b
, then the set
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}
with
\displaystyle\n=\infty
and
\displaystyle\e=0
defines the
tropical
or
min-plus semi-ring
.

Remark 2.32

(All Pairs Shortest Path Problem)
.

If
\displaystyle G
is a simple directed graph with lengths asigned to each edge, then viewing this graph as weighted in
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}
and calculating the Kleene star of this weighted adjacency matrix solves the
all pairs shortest path problem
, i.e.,
\displaystyle A^{*}_{ij}
will have the length of the shortest path from
\displaystyle i
to
\displaystyle j
in this entry.
We note that if a cycle has negative weights, then the hypotheses of
Theorem

2.25
fail to hold, as one can create an arbitrarily short (negative) length path by traversing this cycle repeatedly.

2.4.2
Tropical Endomorphism Semi-Ring for Time-Varying Networks

As
Remark

2.32
demonstrates, the shortest path problem can be solved by computing the Kleene star of the adjacency matrix weighted in the tropical semi-ring
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}
.
A model which generalizes this perspective, but also allows time-varying edge lengths uses a certain sub-semi-ring of
\displaystyle\End(\mathbb{T})
, which we now define.

Definition 2.33

(Non-Decreasing Endomorphisms)
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{W}
be the set of all endomorphisms
\displaystyle{w:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}}
such that
\displaystyle w
is non-decreasing and
\displaystyle\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}{w(t)}=\infty
. We define the operation
\displaystyle\oplus
and
\displaystyle\otimes
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{W}
as follows: for all
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{T}
,

•

\displaystyle(w\oplus v)(t)=\min\{w(t),v(t)\}
, and

•

\displaystyle(w\otimes v)(t)=w(v(t))
.

Remark 2.34
.

The intuition behind
Definition

2.33
is that a function
\displaystyle w_{ij}\in\mathbb{W}
dictates when a message transmitted at time
\displaystyle t
from node
\displaystyle i
will arrive at node
\displaystyle j
—this is the time
\displaystyle w_{ij}(t)
.
Considering the Kleene star of such a matrix will also solve the earliest time of arrival for a traffic network, where the density of traffic can vary over time.
The non-decreasing condition in
Definition

2.33
is also clear in the traffic example, as departing later in the day will never result in an earlier time of arrival.

Remark 2.35

(Connections to Delay Tolerant Networking)
.

[
BT10
, pg.33]
observes that
Definition

2.33
can also be used to model opportunistic networks and delay tolerant networking, which is the current paradigm for space networking.
Indeed, one can embed our lifetime semi-ring into this model as well, but will sacrifice the ease of use and the connections to topological data analysis described in
Section

3
.

Finally, we remark that capacity constraints can be added to
Definition

2.33
without issue.

Remark 2.36

(Capacity Constrained Time-Variate Routing)
.

Using a similar approach to what was described above, we can construct an endomorphism-based semi-ring that captures capacity-constrained delivery on time-varying networks.
Specifically, given a capacity
\displaystyle C>0
, for each
\displaystyle w\in\mathbb{W}
, there exists an
\displaystyle W\in\mathbb{W}
, such that:

W(t)=\min\big\{s:\int\limits_{t}^{s}{w(x)dx}=C\big\}.

One can then compose these elements just as in
Definition

2.33
.

2.5
The Universal Contact Semi-Ring (UCS) for Time-Variate Routing

We finish our discussion of semi-rings by introducing a novel semi-ring
called the
Universal Contact Semi-Ring (UCS)
that allows us to model a large class of networking problems.
Most significant for our application of semi-rings to space networking is the observation that the UCS allows us to model the ”store and forward” protocol
[
Tor+07
]
, which is the main paradigm for delay tolerant networking (DTN) and cannot be captured via the other semi-rings described above.
After providing the necessary definitions, we work through a synthetic example of how this semi-ring describes the store and forward networking protocol.

Definition 2.37
.

The
Universal Contact Semi-Ring (UCS)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
consists of a collection of maps from
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
to the powerset of
\displaystyle\,\mathbb{R}
,

\mathcal{C}=\{f:\mathbb{R}\to 2^{\mathbb{R}}\},

together with addition and multiplication operations defined as

f\oplus g(t)=f(t)\cup g(t)\quad\text{ and }\quad f\odot g(t)=\bigcup_{x\in f(t)}g(x+t)+x,

respectively. Here the formal sum
\displaystyle A+x
denotes the Minkowski sum, i.e

A+x=\{a+x,a\in A\}.

The additive identity of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is the constant function

\displaystyle\displaystyle 0_{\mathcal{C}}:\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\to 2^{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mapsto\emptyset,

while the multiplicative identity is the constant function

\displaystyle\displaystyle 1_{\mathcal{C}}:\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\to 2^{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mapsto\{0\}.

Although the Universal Contact semi-ring is idempotent, this semi-ring is poorly-behaved due to its lack of commutative and integral properties, as well as a total order.
However, we believe the UCS is suitable for modeling in real world application:
In the context of networking, a map
\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{C}
represents the possible delivery delays at a given time: if
\displaystyle x\in f(t)
, then a message can be transported along the edge to arrive at time
\displaystyle t+x
.
This allows the UCS to encapsulate contact windows, time forwarding, changing communication times, as well as storage—limited or unlimited—at a node.

Moreover, UCS contains most of the semi-rings that naturally arise in routing problems, described above, as subquotients, i.e., quotients of sub-semi-rings of UCS. The proof of the next result is presented under
Proposition

A.6
in the appendix.

Proposition 2.38
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
denote the Universal Contact Semi-Ring (
Definition

2.37
).
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
contains

(a).

an injective image of the boolean semi-ring
\displaystyle S
;

(b).

a sub-semi-ring which is isomorphic to the TVG semi-ring;

(c).

a sub-semi-ring which surjects onto the tropical semi-ring
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}
;

(d).

a sub-semi-ring which surjects onto the propagation delay semi-ring ; and

(e).

a sub-semi-ring which surjects onto the function endomorphism semi-ring
\displaystyle\mathbb{W}
.

3
Geometric and Topological Models for Time-Varying Graphs

In this section we take up the question of when two time-varying graphs (TVGs) are “close” in a precise sense.
This is an important question for a variety of reasons, but one immediate application of such a notion would be a calculation of whether an
\displaystyle n
-node STARLINK sub-system is actually close to a strongly connected system, as the lifetime curves in
Figure

5
suggest.
Lifetime curves are merely a summary statistic and lack the discriminatory power of a true metric, but
Figure

15
supports the intuition that (probabilistically) more than 40 nodes is sufficient for constructing a sub-TVG that is strongly connected, with
\displaystyle n=100
providing a higher probability for a guarantee on constant connectivity.

More broadly, the TVG model fits nicely within a growing topological data analysis (TDA) paradigm, where topological changes can be quantified precisely.
This is a fundamental improvement over traditional parametric graph models such as those used in
[
JS22
]
, where the removal of an edge causes a discontinuity in the coordinatization process; see
Figure

.

3.1
Distances on Time-Varying Graphs

In this section we begin the study of distances on TVGs (
Definition

2.2
).
We use distances rather than metrics because certain TVGs may be infinitely far away and also two TVGs may have distance zero, even if they are not exactly the same.
We briefly recall this more flexible notion of a metric.

Definition 3.1

(Distance)
.

A
distance
on a set
\displaystyle X

\displaystyle d:X\times X\to[0,\infty]
where for all
\displaystyle x,y\in X

1.

\displaystyle d(x,y)=d(y,x)

2.

\displaystyle d(x,y)\geq 0
and
\displaystyle d(x,x)=0

Fundamental to our distances on TVGs is the Hausdorff distance.

Definition 3.2
.

Given
\displaystyle A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle d_{H}
between
\displaystyle A

\displaystyle B

d_{H}(A,B)=\inf\{\varepsilon\geq 0|\;A\subseteq B^{\varepsilon}\text{ and }B\subseteq A^{\varepsilon}\},

\displaystyle A^{\varepsilon}=\bigcup\limits_{a\in A}{\text{Ball}_{\varepsilon}(a)}

\displaystyle B^{\varepsilon}=\bigcup\limits_{b\in B}{\text{Ball}_{\varepsilon}(b)}

Here
\displaystyle{\text{Ball}_{\varepsilon}(a)}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid|x-a|\leq\varepsilon\}

3.1.2
Distances with Unknown Node Correspondence

Both Definitions
Definition

3.3
and
Definition

3.5
are only useful when the number of nodes in two TVGs are the same and there is a fixed node correspondence. This is because the vertices need to be ordered to determine a matrix representation and then distances are computed entry-by-entry for these.
In order to consider the distance between two general TVGs—where node correspondence is unclear—we need to introduce a relaxed Hausdorff distance, which has provable connections with an interleaving-type distance that is popular in TDA.

Definition 3.7
.

The
symmetrized Hausdorff distance
between two matrix TVGs
\displaystyle M,N\in\Mat_{n}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}))
is

d_{\Sigma H}{(M,N)}=\inf\limits_{\sigma\in\Pi(n)}{d_{H}(M,\sigma(N))},

where
\displaystyle\Pi(n)
is the set of all permutations of
\displaystyle\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and
\displaystyle\sigma(N)
is the matrix TVG whose
\displaystyle(i,j)
-th entry is
\displaystyle N_{\sigma(i),\sigma(j)}
.

Corollary 3.8
.

For any pair of matrix TVGs
\displaystyle M,N\in\Mat_{n}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}))
, one has

d_{\Sigma H}{(M,N)}\leq d_{H}(M,N).

Remark 3.9
.

Since
\displaystyle d_{\Sigma H}
requires considering
\displaystyle d_{H}
over all
\displaystyle n!
possible permutations,
\displaystyle d_{\Sigma H}
tends to be prohibitively expensive to compute, which is why we will work with topological summaries of TVGs such as zigzag barcodes instead.

3.1.3
Connections with the Interleaving Distance via Cosheaves

Similar to the snapshot construction of
Definition

2.15
, one can summarize a TVG over any interval
\displaystyle I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.
Moreover, to better understand how a TVG behaves over a given interval
\displaystyle I
and the relationship of this summary to another summary
\displaystyle J
, where
\displaystyle I\subseteq J
, we introduce a functorial summary of a TVG that has the added benefit of being a cosheaf.
The finer details of cosheaf theory are not needed for this paper and a basic understanding of category theory, as reviewed in
Appendix

B
, is sufficient.
However, further reading on category theory is always encouraged and
[
Rie17
]
in particular is an excellent resource.

Definition 3.10

(Summary Graphs)
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=(G,\ell_{M})
be a time-varying graph in the sense of
Definition

2.2
.
To each interval
\displaystyle I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
we have a
summary subgraph over
\displaystyle I
:

\mathcal{E}_{M}(I):=\{e\in G\mid\ell_{M}(e)\cap I\neq\varnothing\}.

This is the subgraph of
\displaystyle G
where an edge is included iff it is alive at some point in the interval
\displaystyle I
.
The
underlying graph
of a time-varying graph
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

is the summary graph over
\displaystyle I=\mathbb{R}
.

We now promote the summary graph construction to a functor with two possible codomains.

Definition 3.11

(The Category of Subgraphs and Graph Monomorphisms)
.

Fix
\displaystyle G=(V,E)
a simple directed graph.

•

The collection of
subgraphs of
\displaystyle G
, written
\displaystyle\mathbf{Sub}(G)
, has for objects pairs
\displaystyle G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
where
\displaystyle V^{\prime}\subseteq V
and
\displaystyle E^{\prime}\subseteq E
. There is a unique morphism from
\displaystyle G^{\prime}\to G^{\prime\prime}
iff
\displaystyle V^{\prime}\subseteq V^{\prime\prime}
and
\displaystyle E^{\prime}\subseteq E^{\prime\prime}
, thus making
\displaystyle\mathbf{Sub}(G)
into a poset.

•

The collection of
graph monomorphisms to
\displaystyle G
, written
\displaystyle\mathbf{Mon}(G)
, consists of simple directed graphs
\displaystyle G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
along with an injective graph morphism
\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}:G^{\prime}\to G
. There is a morphism
\displaystyle\psi:(G^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})\to(G^{\prime\prime},\varphi^{\prime\prime})
if
\displaystyle\psi
is a graph morphism satisfying
\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime\prime}\circ\psi=\varphi^{\prime}
.

•

There is a functor
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\iota}:\mathbf{Sub}(G)\to\mathbf{Mon}(G)
that takes each subgraph to its corresponding inclusion map.

Remark 3.12
.

The difference between
\displaystyle\mathbf{Sub}(G)
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{Mon}(G)
is subtle, but crucial.
To see the difference, suppose
\displaystyle G
is a directed 3-cycle with vertex set
\displaystyle V=\{x,y,z\}
and edge set
\displaystyle E=\{[xy],[yz],[zx]\}
.
In
\displaystyle\mathbf{Sub}(G)
there is only one object with 3 vertices and 3 edges, namely,
\displaystyle G
itself.
However, in
\displaystyle\mathbf{Mon}(G)
there are three objects with 3 vertices and 3 edges, where the injective graph morphisms range over all three cyclic permutations of the 3-cycle
\displaystyle G
.

(Summary Cosheaf)

\displaystyle I

3.2
Topological Summaries of TVGs via Barcodes for Machine Learning

In this section we show how techniques from topological data analysis (TDA) can be used to simplify the study of TVGs, by converting them into two zigzag persistence barcodes: one for degree-0, which summarizes how components in the snapshots of
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
evolve over time, and one for degree-1, which summarizes how cycles in the snapshots evolve.
Two important conclusions of this section are

1.

The degree-0 and degree-1 zigzag barcodes are
stable features
of TVGs, and

2.

These features can be used to distinguish Earth-Mars vs. Earth-Moon space networking scenarios in a K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifier,
using the network topology alone.

These zigzag barcodes are based on the homology of a graph, along with maps that are induced on homology from graph morphisms.

Definition 3.17

(Homology)
.

Fix a field
\displaystyle\Bbbk
for the remainder of the paper.
To every graph
\displaystyle G
, one has two vector spaces associated:
\displaystyle H_{0}(G)
—the vector space generated by the connected components of
\displaystyle G
—and
\displaystyle H_{1}(G)
—the vector space generated by cycles in
\displaystyle G
.
Additionally, associated to any graph morphism
\displaystyle G^{\prime}\to G
there are induced linear maps
\displaystyle H_{k}(G^{\prime})\to H_{k}(G)
on these vector spaces.
In otherwords, we have the
homology functors
for
\displaystyle k=0
and
\displaystyle k=1
:

H_{k}:\mathbf{Mon}(G)\to\mathbf{Vec}.

Definition 3.18

(Homology Modules for TVGs)
.

Recall the subgraph categories of
Definition

3.11

Remark 3.20

(Zigzag Persistence Review)
.

Zigzag persistence is well studied in the TDA community with computational foundations established in
[
CSM09
,
DH23
,
DH21
,
DH22
,
Mor21
]
among others.
We review two theoretical perspectives on this construction.

The first proceeds directly by recognizing that associated to each TVG
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=(G,\ell_{M})
is a finite set of closed intervals for each edge
\displaystyle v,e\in G
.
Taking the union of the endpoints of these intervals across all
\displaystyle v,e
specifies a finite set of “critical values”
\displaystyle\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}

where an edge or vertex can appear, from this we can express any TVG as a zigzag diagram of sub-graphs of the complete graph

G_{-1}\hookrightarrow G_{0}\hookleftarrow G_{1}\hookrightarrow G_{2}\hookleftarrow\cdots\hookrightarrow G_{2n}\hookleftarrow G_{2n+1},

where even-indexed subgraphs
\displaystyle G_{2i}
indicate the snapshot of
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
at each critical value
\displaystyle\tau_{i}
and odd-indexed subgraphs
\displaystyle G_{2i\pm 1}
correspond to snapshots of
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
at times
\displaystyle\tau_{i}\pm\epsilon
for sufficiently small
\displaystyle\epsilon
.
Taking homology of each of these subgraphs and graph inclusions produces a representation of a type
\displaystyle A_{2n+3}
alternating quiver, which by Gabriel’s theorem
[
Gab72
]
has a canonical multiset of intervals associated to it.

Alternatively, one can follow the work of
[
KM17
]
, which shows that our TVGs are equivalently viewed as dynamic graphs.
Following
[
KM17
, Def. 2.17]
one sees that restricting
\displaystyle H_{k}\mathcal{E}_{M}(\mathcal{G})
to one-point intervals
\displaystyle[t,t]
produces a map
\displaystyle h_{k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbf{Vec}
that is “cosheaf-inducing.”
As shown in
[
BL18
,
Bje21
]
constructible cosheaves are block-decomposable and restricting these blocks to the diagonal
\displaystyle y=x
produces the zigzag barcode described above.

As previously mentioned, the zigzag barcode is a stable feature of a TVG.
The next proposition explains what is meant by “stable.”

Proposition 3.21
.

Suppose
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=(G,\ell_{M})
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=(H,\ell_{N})
are two TVGs with a fixed isomorphism between the underlying graphs
\displaystyle\Phi:G\cong H
, as in
Theorem

3.16
.
The bottleneck distance on the barcodes of
\displaystyle H_{k}\mathcal{E}_{M}(\mathcal{G})
and
\displaystyle H_{k}\mathcal{E}_{N}(\mathcal{H})
are bounded above as follows:

d_{B}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})\leq d_{I}\left(\boldsymbol{\iota}\circ\mathcal{E}_{M}(\mathcal{G}),\boldsymbol{\iota}\circ\mathcal{E}_{N}(\mathcal{H})\right)\leq d_{I}\left(\mathcal{E}_{M}(\mathcal{G}),\mathcal{E}_{N}(\mathcal{H})\right).

Proof.

The notation
\displaystyle H_{k}\mathcal{E}_{M}
actually refers to the composition of functors
\displaystyle H_{k}\circ\boldsymbol{\iota}\circ\mathcal{E}_{M}
.

[
BS12
, Proposition 3.6]
proves very generally that compositions of functors define Lipschitz-1 maps between categories that are equipped with interleaving distances.
Consequently

\displaystyle\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{I}}^{\mathbf{Vec}}\big(H_{k}\circ\boldsymbol{\iota}\circ\mathcal{E}_{M}(\mathcal{G}),H_{k}\circ\boldsymbol{\iota}\circ\mathcal{E}_{N}(\mathcal{H})\big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\phantom{=========}\leq{}d_{\mathsf{I}}^{\mathbf{Mon}(G)}\big(\boldsymbol{\iota}\circ\mathcal{E}_{M}(\mathcal{G}),\boldsymbol{\iota}\circ\mathcal{E}_{N}(\mathcal{H})\big)

which proves the stated result.

Future Directions

This paper is one installment in an on-going effort to integrate ideas from mathematics into the design of better engineered systems capable of supporting fast, reliable, and autonomous routing in a solar system-wide internet.
We conclude with a list of ongoing and future work that we hope will draw greater attention to the rich source of research problems that space networking presents.

1.

Faster Algorithms:
Current protocols for routing over a temporal and disruption tolerant network involves the construction of a contact graph and solving Dijkstra’s algorithm over this graph; this is called Contact Graph Routing (CGR). Recent efforts
[
Moy+23
]
show that this can be sped up substantially by using a contact multigraph, as it reduces the number of vertices that Dijkstra’s algorithm needs to search over. This mult-graph can be viewed as a display space associated to the cosheaf that defines a TVG. Do the perspectives introduced in this paper provide further computational benefits? For example, is there a routing algorithm that operates directly on the matrix TVG, viewed as a data structure?
The current goal for space simulation work is the ability to simulate routing over a network with
\displaystyle 10^{5}
nodes, with our current capabilities hovering around
\displaystyle 10^{3}
.

2.

Exploiting Periodicity:
From an algebraic perspective, the difficulty of routing in a system with propagation delay (such as in Earth-to-Mars communication) is the lack of convergence of the Kleene star. However, this comes from using
\displaystyle\End(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}))
as a semi-ring. In truly periodic systems, such as those governed by celestial mechanics, it may make more sense to view lifetimes as subsets of the circle
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{1}
. If we consider the communication matrix
\displaystyle A
with entries in
\displaystyle\End(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{1}))
, there may be conditions when non-trivial propagation delay may still yield a convergent Kleene star, such as in when a shift is a rational multiple of the circumference.

3.

Sub-Netting in TVGs:
Our KNN classification experiments indicate that TVGs, even when reduced to their zigzag barcodes, can represent distinct types of space networking scenarios. This leads us to believe that the metrics introduced in this paper can be used to identify motifs or other sub-structures in TVGs that can be used to automatically identify domains for routing. This is important as terrestrial internet relies heavily on fixed routing tables, which is not possible in a time-varying setting. Is it possible to identify algorithms or methods for automatically sub-netting a TVG? If so, this would help make routing protocols adapted to the type of (sub) TVG at hand, which could then be composed to efficiently route across disparate parts of a future space internet.

4.

More Realistic Models:
The approach to satellite network modelling taken in this paper is based on considering all possible contacts based on line-of-sight. In practice, many (or most) nodes might only be able to hold one link at a time; choosing one contact means not choosing others. Moreover setting up a link, tearing down a link, and pointing antennas all take time. This means that a realistic network is really based on a subset of all possible contacts. Methods to choose such a subset, particularly in a resilient and robust way, remain unknown. The machinery introduced in this paper could be used to create “best practices” and frameworks for network management.

Definitions and Technical Results on Semi-Rings

Definition A.1
.

A
monoid
is a set
\displaystyle(S,\circ,\e)
equiped with a binary operation
\displaystyle\circ:S\times S\to S
, that is associative, i.e.,
\displaystyle a\circ(b\circ c)=(a\circ b)\circ c
, and where
\displaystyle\e
acts as a neutral element on both the left and the right, i.e.,
\displaystyle a\circ\e=a=\e\circ a
.
Such a monoid is
commutative
if
\displaystyle a\circ b=b\circ a
for all pairs of elements
\displaystyle a,b\in S
.

Definition A.2
.

An
additive homomorphism
from a semi-ring
\displaystyle(S,\oplus,\otimes,\n,\e)
to
\displaystyle(T,+,\times,0,1)
is a map
\displaystyle h:S\to T
such that

•

\displaystyle h(\n)=0
, and

•

\displaystyle h(a\oplus b)=h(a)+h(b)
.

A
homomorphism
of semi-rings is an additive homomorphism that also satisfies the properties

•

\displaystyle h(\e)=1
, and

•

\displaystyle h(a\otimes b)=h(a)\times h(b)
.

An additive endomorphism is an additive homomorphism of
\displaystyle S
to itself.
Similarly, an endomorphism is a homomorphism from
\displaystyle S
to itself.

On
[
BT10
, pg. 14]
we have the following elementary observation:

Lemma A.3
.

If
\displaystyle S
is a semi-ring, then
\displaystyle H:=\End(S)
, the set of additive endomorphisms of
\displaystyle S
, is a semi-ring as well. The two operations are

•

\displaystyle(h_{1}+h_{2})(a):=h_{1}(a)+h_{2}(a)

•

\displaystyle(h_{1}\odot h_{2})(a):=h_{2}(h_{1}(a))

Notice that, in particular, we did not need to assume that endomorphisms preserve products to make
\displaystyle\End(S)
into a semi-ring. Also notice that even if
\displaystyle S
is a commutative semi-ring, i.e.,
\displaystyle\otimes
is commutative, then
\displaystyle\End(S)
need not be commutative.

Below is the proof of Theorem
2.16
, which we restate for convenience.

Theorem A.4
.

The semi-ring of matrix TVGs
\displaystyle\,\Mat_{n}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}))
is isomorphic to the semi-ring of functions from
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
to
\displaystyle\Mat_{n}(\Bool)
.
This isomorphism is witnessed by the homomorphism

\Psi:\Mat_{n}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}))\to\Fun(\mathbb{R},\Mat_{n}(\Bool)),\quad\text{where}\quad\Psi(M)(t)=\mathcal{S}_{t}(M)

is the snapshot of
\displaystyle M
at time
\displaystyle t

Proof.

\displaystyle\Psi
preseves neutral elements. The identity TVG matrix
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}I
, with diagonal entries
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
, is sent to the function
\displaystyle 1
that assigns to each
\displaystyle t
the identity Boolean matrix
\displaystyle\top I
. The TVG matrix of empty sets
\displaystyle\varnothing I
is sent to the function
\displaystyle 0
with constant value
\displaystyle\bot I
.

To establish clear notation, we will write TVG matrices with capital letters like
\displaystyle M
and
\displaystyle N
and use
\displaystyle M+N
and
\displaystyle MN
for matrix addition and multiplication.
To emphasize that the entries of these matrices are subsets, we use union and intersection to emphasize the operations occuring in
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
, i.e.,

(M+N)_{ij}=M_{ij}\cup N_{ij}\quad\text{and}\quad(MN)_{ij}=\bigcup_{k}M_{ik}\cap N_{kj}.

In the semi-ring
\displaystyle\Fun(\mathbb{R},\Mat_{n}(\Bool))
we will use lower case letters like
\displaystyle m
and
\displaystyle n
to stand for functions.
We write
\displaystyle m(t)
for the matrix of Booleans at time
\displaystyle t
, whose truth value in entry
\displaystyle(i,j)

\displaystyle m(t)(i,j)
.
Pointwise addition of functions is written

\displaystyle``+^{\prime\prime}

Multiplication in
\displaystyle\Fun(\mathbb{R},\Mat_{n}(\Bool))

\displaystyle(m\otimes n)(t)=m(t)n(t)

\displaystyle(m\otimes n)(t)(i,j)

\displaystyle\vee_{k}m(t)(i,k)\wedge n(t)(k,j)

\displaystyle\Psi(M)=m

\displaystyle\displaystyle t\in(MN)_{ij}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\displaystyle t\in\bigcup_{k}M_{ik}\cap N_{kj}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\displaystyle\top=\bigvee_{k}m(t)(i,k)\wedge n(t)(k,j)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\displaystyle\top=(m(t)n(t))(i,j)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\displaystyle\top=(m\otimes n)(t)(i,j).

Proposition A.5

(Convergence at the Temporal Diameter)
.

Let
\displaystyle A
be the adjacency matrix for a simple TVG
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=(G,\ell_{M})

The
\displaystyle(i,j)^{th}

\displaystyle k
-cumulant
\displaystyle C_{k}(A)

\displaystyle i
to
\displaystyle j

We set
\displaystyle d_{ii}=0
, by convention.
We define the

of a TVG
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
to be

\diam(\mathcal{G})=\max_{ij}d_{ij}.

Consequently, the Kleene star
\displaystyle A^{*}
convergences for
\displaystyle r\geq\diam(\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle C_{k}(A)
stabilizes at some value of
\displaystyle k

\displaystyle M^{k}=(I+A)^{k}
stabilizes at
\displaystyle k
.
Moreover, by Theorem

, we can faithfully recover the values of
\displaystyle M^{k}
by considering the associated Boolean function
\displaystyle m^{k}:V\times V\times\mathbb{R}\to\Bool
, when is
\displaystyle\Psi(M^{k})=\Psi(M)^{k}
.
This is the logical
OR
(union) of
\displaystyle a^{p}
, which is
\displaystyle\Psi(A^{p})=\Psi(A)^{p}
, across all
\displaystyle p=1,\dots,k
along with the identity relation, thus it suffices to characterize what nodes are related at time
\displaystyle t
using
\displaystyle a^{p}(t):V\times V\to\Bool
.
For
\displaystyle p=1

\displaystyle a(i,j,t)=1

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle t
.
By direct inspection of matrix multiplication, one can see that
\displaystyle a^{2}(i,j,t)=1

\displaystyle a^{p}(i,j,t)=1

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle i

.

(e).

Similar to the tropical semi-ring case, consider the collection of maps
\displaystyle\left\{\varphi\right\}
in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
that satisfy

•

\displaystyle\varphi(t)
is a finite subset of
\displaystyle[0,\infty)
for each
\displaystyle t
,

•

If
\displaystyle t_{1}\leq t_{2}
and
\displaystyle\varphi(t_{1}),\varphi(t_{2})
are not empty, then
\displaystyle\min(\varphi(t_{2}))+t_{2}\geq\min(\varphi(t_{1}))+t_{1}
, and

•

If
\displaystyle t_{1}\leq t_{2}
and
\displaystyle\varphi(t_{1})=\emptyset
then
\displaystyle\varphi(t_{2})=\emptyset
.

This collection is a sub-semi-ring of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
, and surjects onto
\displaystyle\mathbb{W}
via the semi-ring homomorphism:

\varphi\mapsto\left(t\mapsto\begin{cases}\min(\varphi(t))+t&\varphi(t)\neq\emptyset\\
\infty&\varphi(t)=\emptyset\end{cases}\right).

∎

Appendix B
Basic Category Theory

A
category

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
consists of a collection of
objects
,
\displaystyle\text{Ob},\mathcal{C}
and a collection of
morphisms
,
\displaystyle\hom(x,y)
, for every
\displaystyle x,y
in
\displaystyle\text{Ob}\mathcal{C}
, such that

•

if
\displaystyle f
in
\displaystyle\hom(x,y)
and g
\displaystyle\hom(y,z)
, then
\displaystyle g\circ f
in
\displaystyle\text{hom}(x,z)
,

•

for each
\displaystyle x
in
\displaystyle\text{Ob}\mathcal{C}
, there exists a morphism
\displaystyle\mathsf{id}_{x}
in
\displaystyle\text{hom}(x,x)
,

•

for
\displaystyle f
in
\displaystyle\hom(x,y)
,
\displaystyle f\circ\mathsf{id}_{x}=\mathsf{id}_{x}\circ f
, and

•

composition is associative:
\displaystyle(f\circ g)\circ h=f\circ(g\circ h)
.

A
functor

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}
between categories
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}
consists of

•

an object
\displaystyle F(x)\in\mathcal{D}
for each object
\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{C}
,

•

a morphism
\displaystyle F(f)\in\hom(F(x),F(y))
for each morphism
\displaystyle f\in\hom(x,y)
, such that

–

\displaystyle F
respects the composition rule in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}
:

F(f\circ g)=F(f)\circ F(g),

–

for each object
\displaystyle x
in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
,
\displaystyle F(\mathsf{id}_{x})=\mathsf{id}_{F(x)}
.

Appendix C
Barcodes and Distances on These

A
barcode

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
is a multiset of intervals
\displaystyle\{I\}
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
.
A matching
\displaystyle\sigma
between barcodes
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is defined as a partial bijection between

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{B}
,and
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{C}
:

\sigma:\mathcal{B^{\prime}}\to\mathcal{C^{\prime}}.

Given a
\displaystyle p\in[1,\infty]
we assign a
matching
\displaystyle p
-cost
of
\displaystyle\sigma
as follows

\cost{\sigma,p}=\left\{\begin{aligned} \left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in\mathcal{B},\\
\sigma(I)=J\end{subarray}}{\left\lVert I-J\right\rVert_{p}^{p}+}\sum\limits_{I\in\Delta}{\left\lVert I-\Mid(I)\right\rVert}_{p}^{p}\right)^{1/p},&\text{ when }1\leq p<\infty\\
\max\left\{\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in\mathcal{B},\\
\sigma(I)=J\end{subarray}}{\left\lVert I-J\right\rVert_{\infty},\max\limits_{I\in\Delta}{\left\lVert I-\Mid(I)\right\rVert}_{\infty}}\right\},&\text{ when }p=\infty,\end{aligned}\right\},

where
\displaystyle\left\lVert I-J\right\rVert_{p}
is the
\displaystyle\ell^{p}
-norm between intervals
\displaystyle I=[a,b)
and
\displaystyle J=[c,d)
viewed as vectors
\displaystyle(a,b)
and
\displaystyle(c,d)
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\displaystyle\Mid(I):=\left[\tfrac{a+b}{2},\tfrac{a+b}{2}\right)
is the empty interval at the midpoint of
\displaystyle I
, and
\displaystyle\Delta
denotes all the intervals unmatched by
\displaystyle\sigma
in
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\sqcup\mathcal{C}
.
The
Wasserstein
\displaystyle p
-distance
between barcodes
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is then defined as the infimum of
\displaystyle p
-costs over all possible matchings, i.e.,

d^{p}_{W}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})=\inf\limits_{\sigma}{\cost{\sigma,p}}.

The distance
\displaystyle d^{\infty}_{W}
is called the
bottleneck distance
, denoted as
\displaystyle d_{B}
.

Appendix D
Proof of Isometry Theorems

We now prove Theorem
3.16
.

Theorem D.1

(Isometry Theorems)
.

Suppose
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=(G,\ell_{M})
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=(G,\ell_{N})
are two TVGs with the same underlying graph.
We can view
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{N}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\iota}\circ\mathcal{E}_{N}
as functors to
\displaystyle\mathbf{Sub}(G)
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{Mon}(G)
, respectively.
Any ordering of the nodes in
\displaystyle G
then determines two isometries

d_{H}(M,N)=d_{I}(\mathcal{E}_{M},\mathcal{E}_{N})\quad\text{and}\quad d_{\Sigma H}(M,N)=d_{I}(\boldsymbol{\iota}\circ\mathcal{E}_{M},\boldsymbol{\iota}\circ\mathcal{E}_{N}),

where
\displaystyle M
and
\displaystyle N
are the lifetime matrices for
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=(G,\ell_{M})
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=(H,\ell_{N})
, respectively.

Proof.

Suppose
\displaystyle G
has
\displaystyle n
vertices and fix a labeling for them
\displaystyle\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

We first show

\displaystyle d_{\Sigma H}(M,N)\leq d_{I}(\iota\cdot\mathcal{E}_{M},\iota\cdot\mathcal{E}_{N})
.
Suppose
\displaystyle(\varphi,\psi)
is a
\displaystyle\varepsilon
-interleaving, by taking colimits we can
see that
\displaystyle\varphi
corresponds to some permutation
\displaystyle\sigma\in\Pi_{n}
.
We want to show that for all
\displaystyle i,j
, we have

\displaystyle M_{ij}\subseteq N_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}^{\varepsilon}
and

\displaystyle N_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}\subseteq M_{ij}^{\varepsilon}
.
If
\displaystyle[ij]\in\mathcal{E}_{M}(I)
then
\displaystyle\varphi_{I}
guaranteees

\displaystyle[{\sigma(i)}{\sigma(j)}]\in\mathcal{E}_{N}(I^{\varepsilon})
.
In particular, there exists
\displaystyle t\in I
such that
\displaystyle t\in M_{ij}
.
Since
\displaystyle\varphi_{I}
guarantees that
\displaystyle[{\sigma(i)}{\sigma(j)}]
exists in
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{N}(I^{\varepsilon})
,
there exists
\displaystyle t^{\prime}\in N_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}
with

\displaystyle t^{\prime}\in(t-\varepsilon,t+\varepsilon)
.
This shows
\displaystyle t\in N_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}^{\varepsilon}
.
A symmetric argument shows the other inclusion.

We now show

\displaystyle d_{I}(\iota\cdot\mathcal{E}_{M},\iota\cdot\mathcal{E}_{N})\leq d_{\Sigma H}(M,N)
.
Let
\displaystyle\sigma
be a permutation in
\displaystyle\Pi_{n}
and consider

\displaystyle d_{H}(M,\sigma(N))=\varepsilon
, that is, for all
\displaystyle i,j
, we have

\displaystyle M_{ij}\subseteq N_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}^{\varepsilon}
and

\displaystyle N_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}\subseteq M_{ij}^{\varepsilon}
.
For
\displaystyle I\in\mathbf{Int}
, define
\displaystyle\varphi_{I}:\mathcal{E}_{M}(I)\to\mathcal{E}_{N}(I^{\varepsilon})

by
\displaystyle i\mapsto{\sigma(i)}
and
\displaystyle[ij]\mapsto[{\sigma(i)}{\sigma(j)}]
.
If
\displaystyle e=[ij]\in\mathcal{E}_{M}(I)
, then there exists
\displaystyle t\in I
such that

\displaystyle t\in M_{ij}
. But since
\displaystyle M_{ij}\subseteq N_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}^{\varepsilon}
,
then
\displaystyle t\in N_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}^{\varepsilon}
which means there exists

\displaystyle t^{\prime}\in N_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}
such that
\displaystyle|t-t^{\prime}|<\varepsilon
which
means that
\displaystyle[{\sigma(i)}{\sigma(j)}]\in\mathcal{E}_{N}(I^{\varepsilon})
, hence
\displaystyle\varphi_{I}
is
well-defined. A symmetric argument shows
\displaystyle\psi_{J}
is also well-defined.

The first isometry follows from the second.
∎
