KOLMOGOROV SPACES

Part II
Kolmogorov spaces

Chapter 1
Functorial analysis

In the analysis of KAM iteration schemes, one often deals with spaces of functions defined
over smaller and smaller subsets. In order to keep track of this

functional analysis over shrinking sets
, we need a convenient framework.
We set up a particular formalism of
relative Banach spaces
that is inspired
by the theory of vector bundles over a base
B
and their associated sheaves of sections
over subsets of
B
. It turns out that the estimates we need in KAM-theory aquire a
meaning in terms of the geometry of the base
B
.
The main notion is that of a
Kolmogorov space
, which is a certain system
of Banach spaces, parametrized by an ordered set. At a superficial level the
formalism bears some resemblance to the classical notion of a Banach scale,
but on closer look it has quite a different flavour.
Many notions valid for Banach spaces, such as operator calculus or fixed point theory,
extend, properly understood, to the category of Kolmogorov spaces,
whereas within a naïve formalism based on Banach scales or Fréchet spaces they do not.
We try a systematic treatment and pay particular attention to the various types of morphisms
that occur naturally and which appear to be a key feature of the theory.

1.1.
Relative sets

Let

1.2.
Relative vector spaces

Let us now fix a field
K
, which in our applications always will be

\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
and a vector space will always be a
K
-vector space.
If
B
is a set, and for each
b\in B

E_{b}
, we can form the
B

All fibres

The elements of

It is useful to have the geometrical picture of a fibre bundle in mind, but
clearly we are dealing here with an almost trivial caricature of it.

We will denote elements of

(b,v)

and

. This is in fact redundant, as a vector

’knows’ its

and avoids confusion.

The vector space operations may be used pointwise to define
the structure of a vector space on the set

\Gamma(A,E)

of all sections of

over
A
. Of course,

\Gamma(A,E)=\prod_{a\in A}E_{a}

is just the

of all vector spaces

.
If we define the

of section

, then one can identify the
direct sum

as the subspace of sections with finite support.

1.3.
Relative Banach spaces

Similarly, if the fibres of a map

p:E\longrightarrow B

have the structure
of a Banach space, we speak of a
Banach space over
B
.
These form the objects of an obvious category
\text{\bf Ban}_{B}
.
The set
{\rm Hom}_{B}(E,F)
of morphisms in the category are given by

continuous linear maps
over
B
: any
u\in{\rm Hom}_{B}(E,F)
sits in a
commutative diagram

\textstyle{E\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{u}
\textstyle{F\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{B}

and for each
b\in B
the restriction of
u
to
E_{b}
is a continuous
linear map
u_{b}:E_{b}\longrightarrow F_{b}
. The set
{\rm Hom}_{B}(E,F)
has a natural structure of
a vector space, where the operations are defined pointwise.
We also introduce for two Banach spaces
E
and
F
over
B
an

internal Hom
, which defined to be the Banach space
\mathcal{H}om
over
B
whose fibre over
b
is the Banach space
Hom(E_{b},F_{b})
of continuous (=bounded) linear
maps
E_{b}\longrightarrow F_{b}
, with the operator norm as norm. Again one has:

{\rm Hom}_{B}(E,F)=\Gamma(B,{{\mathcal{H}}}om(E,F)).

When
p:E\longrightarrow B
is a Banach space over
B
, each Banach space
E_{b}
is
equipped with a specific norm
|-|_{b}
and the precise choice of the norm
is an important part of the data.
Each vector
x=(b,v)\in E
has a norm

|x|=|v|_{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

and so there is a map

|-|:E\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\;\;x\mapsto|x|.

Together with the projection
p:E\longrightarrow B
to the base we obtain the
norm map

\nu:E\longrightarrow B\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\ x=(b,v)\mapsto(b,|x|)=(b,|v|_{b}).

It plays a role analogous to that of the norm for ordinary Banach spaces,
which is the special case where
B
is a point.

The
unit ball

B_{E}
is the
B
-set whose fibre at
b
is the unit
ball of
E_{b}
. Note that this is just the preimage of
B\times[0,1]
under the norm map
\nu
:

B_{E}:=\nu^{-1}(B\times[0,1]).

(The ball of radius
r
will be denoted by
rB_{E}=\nu^{-1}(B\times[0,r])
.)
If
s\in\Gamma(A,E)
is a section, then for each
a\in A
we can
consider the norm
|s(a)|
, hence we obtain a
norm function

|s|:=|-|\circ s:A\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\;\;a\mapsto|s(a)|

of the section.

Using the norm of sections, there are different ways to define a topology on
the section spaces
\Gamma(A,E)
. One of the simplest ones is that of
pointwise convergence in norm, but as we only want to use Banach spaces, we
in fact will not make much use of this topology.

1.4.
Bounded sections and bounded homomorphisms

If
E\longrightarrow B
is a Banach space over
B
, we can use the norm to define a
subset of
\Gamma(A,E)
, consisting of those sections, for which the norm function is

bounded
.

Definition 1.3
.

\Gamma^{b}(A,E):=\{s\in\Gamma(A,E)\;\;|\;\;\sup_{a\in A}|s(a)|<\infty\}.

For
s\in\Gamma^{b}(A,E)
we put

|s|_{A}:=\sup_{a\in A}|s(a)|.

Proposition 1.4
.

If
p:E\longrightarrow B
is a Banach space over
B
then
\Gamma^{b}(A,E)
with
|-|_{A}
is a Banach space.

Proof.

We write
|-|
for
|-|_{A}
. Clearly, one has:

\displaystyle|s_{1}+s_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq|s_{1}|+|s_{2}|,

\displaystyle|\lambda\cdot s|

\displaystyle=|\lambda||s|

and so
\Gamma^{b}(A,E)
is a normed vector space.
Now consider a Cauchy sequence of sections
s_{n}
in
\Gamma^{b}(A,E)
, so for any
\epsilon>0

we can find
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

|s_{n}-s_{m}|\leq\epsilon\;\;\;\textup{for}\;\;n,m\geq N.

As for all
a\in A
one has

|s_{n}(a)-s_{m}(a)|\leq|s_{n}-s_{m}|,

it follows immediately that for any
a\in A
the sequences
s_{n}(a)
are Cauchy sequences
in
E_{a}
and thus converge in
E_{a}
to an element
s(a)
which defines a section

s\in\Gamma(A,E).

As

||s_{n}|-|s_{m}||\leq|s_{n}-s_{m}|

the sequence of norms
(|s_{n}|)
is Cauchy sequence in
\mathbb{R}
and is therefore bounded. As a consequence,
the norm function
|s|
is also bounded and thus the limit
s
is again an element in
\Gamma^{b}(A,E)
.

As mentioned above, if
p:E\longrightarrow B
is a Banach space over
B

precise choice of the norms

is an important part of the
data. If

\lambda:B\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}

the norm on

by
a factor
\lambda(b)

different

Definition 1.5
.

If
E\longrightarrow B
is a Banach space over
B
and

\lambda:B\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}

If the scaling function

the spaces of bounded sections of

This phenomenon is a bit similar to the existence of different vector bundles
over the same base and with isomorphic fibres.

.

Consider again the vector space

We consider the norm given by the absolute value of the second coordinate

A section

.

If

1.5.
Vector spaces and Banach spaces over categories

In the above constructions the vector spaces
E_{b}
for different
b
’s were
completely independent of each other and the above constructions are
essentially empty.

In interesting situations the fibres
E_{b}
are connected
to each other and we have to take this into account. In the theory of vector
bundles one can introduce a
connection
on
E\longrightarrow B
to compare fibres
over different points, but here we shall proceed in a different way.
We will assume that the base
B
is a
small category
.
This means we are given a set
B
and furthermore, for any two
a,b\in B

there is a set
Mor(a,b)
of
morphisms
from
a
to
b
, for which the
usual axioms are fulfilled. So we can compose a morphism from
a
to
b

with one from
b
to
c
to obtain a morphism from
a
to
c
and this composition of morphisms is associative.

Definition 1.9
.

A vector space over a small category
B
is a
functor

e:B\longrightarrow\text{\bf Vect}

from the category
B
to the category of
Vect
vector spaces.

A Banach space over a small category
B
is a
functor

e:B\longrightarrow\text{\bf Ban}

from the category
B
to the category
Ban
of Banach spaces.

So, as before, for each
b\in B
, we are given a vector space
e(b)=:E_{b}
, but
for each morphism
\alpha\in Mor(a,b)
we are also given linear maps

e(\alpha):E_{a}\longrightarrow E_{b}

with the condition

e(\beta\circ\alpha)=e(\beta)\circ e(\alpha)

whenever it makes sense. These maps between fibres over different points
serve to ’connect’ these different fibers.

If we have just a set
B
, we can turn it into a trivial category by setting

Mor(a,b)=\emptyset
for
a\neq b
and
Mor(b,b)=\{Id_{b}\}
, and then
we recover the notions of vector spaces and Banach spaces over the set
B

as defined before.

.

More generally, a vector space over

Similarly, a

to the category

As the morphisms in the category

mappings are continuous.

There are several simple special notions related to ordered sets that we will need.

An ordered map

the order relations on

and

if

In other words, we are dealing with monotonous maps and these are exactly
the functors from

to

ordered sets using ordered maps.

If

is an ordered set, and
A\subset B
a

of

we can restrict the order relation of

to

into an

and

Downsets and Upsets:
In an ordered set

A

is a set with the property

a\in A,\;\;a\geq b\Longrightarrow b\in A

and a

a\in A,\;\;b\geq a\Longrightarrow b\in A

If

\mathbb{R}
, the downsets of a point
(a,b)

in

looks as follows:

Arbitrary intersections and arbitrary unions of downsets are downsets.
As a consequence, the intersection of all downsets containing any arbitrary set

is the smallest downset containing

, the
downset hull

A^{d}
of

. Of particular importance are the

, determined by a
single element:

B[\tau]:=\{b\in B:b\leq\tau\}=\{\tau\}^{d}.

Of course, mutatis mutandis, all the same for upsets. If

is
an ordered map and

the preimage

is a downset in

for the topology which has the downsets as open sets.

Directed sets and limit:
In many of our examples the ordered set

is a set

For a linear space

1.7.
Horizontal sections

If
E\longrightarrow B
is a vector space over a small category
B
, then over each
morphism
\alpha\in Mor(a,b)
in
B
there is an associated linear
map

e(\alpha):E_{a}\longrightarrow E_{b}

between the fibres. As these maps allow us to relate fibres over
different points, it can be seen as a set-theoretic type of connection.

Definition 1.15
.

A section
s\in\Gamma(B,E)
is called a horizontal section if it
is compatible with all these linear maps:

e(\alpha)(s(a))=s(b),\;\;\;\forall a,b\in B,\alpha\in Mor(a,b).

We denote by

\Gamma^{h}(B,E)\subset\Gamma(B,E)

the linear subspace of horizontal sections.

Example 1.16
.

Consider the
\mathbb{R}
-vector space defined by the projection

\mathbb{R}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},\ (b,x)\mapsto b

with restriction mappings

(b,x)\mapsto(a,x).

Horizontal sections

\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2},\ b\mapsto(b,f(b))

are defined by constant functions:
f(b)=c
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Example 1.17
.

Consider again the vector space

C^{\bullet}([0,1],\mathbb{R}),
over
the ordered set
(\mathbb{N},\geq)
. A section is a sequence of
functions
(f_{n})
, but horizontality now tells that

should
map to

via the inclusion

C^{n+1}([0,1],\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow C^{n}([0,1],\mathbb{R}).

This means that choosing a global horizontal section is the same as the choice of a

-function

. The function

is the same as

but considered as an element of

.

In the case of vector spaces

over an ordered set

has the following simple construction: for any element

of

Thus to an element

Proposition 1.18
.

If

is a Banach space over a base

, then the space of horizontal bounded
sections

\Gamma^{h}(B,E)\cap\Gamma^{b}(B,E)

is a Banach space.

Proof.

Clearly, the equations

e(\alpha)(s(a))=s(b),\;\;\;\forall a,b\in B,\alpha\in Mor(a,b)

defining horizontality determine a closed linear subspace of
\Gamma^{b}(B,E)
.
∎

Definition 1.19
.

The Banach space of horizontal bounded sections is

\Gamma^{\infty}(A,E):=\Gamma^{h}(A,E)\cap\Gamma^{b}(A,E).

Example 1.20
.

Consider again the vector space over the ordered set

defined by the

If

Example 1.21

For the Banach space

of

1.8.
The concept of Kolmogorov space

We now arrive at our first important concept:

Definition 1.22
.

A Kolmogorov space

3

3

In set-theoretical topology the term Kolmogorov space is used for topological
spaces for which the
T_{0}
-separation axiom holds. We will never use the notion in this sense, although the topology defined by downsets in an ordered set
provide natural examples such
T_{0}
spaces.
is a Banach space

over an ordered set

An

E\longrightarrow B

over an ordered subset

.

In practice, we will be mostly working with K1 and K2 spaces and in this
text K3-spaces will not play a role.

Kolmogorov spaces over the interval
]0,S]\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}
are of particular importance and will be called
S
-Kolmogorov spaces
.

So, in a Kolmogorov space
E
, the restriction maps

e_{st}:E_{t}\longrightarrow E_{s},\ t\geq s,

are continuous mappings of Banach spaces with operator norm
|e_{st}|\leq 1
, i.e. one
has an estimate

|e_{st}(x)|_{s}\leq|x|_{t}.

S
-Kolmogorov spaces have the following characteristic property:

Proposition 1.23
.

For an
S
-Komogorov space
E
and
\tau\in]0,S]
, there is a natural isometry of Banach spaces

E_{\tau}=\Gamma^{\infty}(]0,\tau],E).

Proof.

A vector
v\in E_{\tau}
determines a unique horizontal section
s:t\mapsto e_{t\tau}(v)\in E_{t}
.
As
|e_{t\tau}(v)|\leq|v|_{\tau}
, the norm function
|s(t)|
attains its maximum at
t=\tau
.
∎

So in an
S
-Kolmogorov space, there is is no difference between vectors and
horizontal sections defined over sub-intervals
]0,t]
: each vector
(t,v)

determines a unique horizontal section over
]0,t]
. This is no longer
true for
K2
-spaces. The downsets
B[b]
determined a single

are
very special subsets and usually we will have to consider horizontal sections
over more general subsets.

A Kolmogorov space

p:E\longrightarrow B

carries a
canonical topology
.
As mentioned before, the upsets define a topology on
B
with open sets:

U[b]:=\{b^{\prime}\in B\;|\;\;b^{\prime}\geq b\}

with
b\in B
.
Now let
E_{U[b]}=\bigsqcup_{b^{\prime}\geq b}E_{b^{\prime}}\longrightarrow U[b]
be the pull-back via the inclusion map

U[b]\hookrightarrow B.

The restriction maps of
E
determine a canonical map

\pi_{b}:E_{U[b]}\longrightarrow E_{b},\;\;(b^{\prime},v)\mapsto(b,e_{bb^{\prime}}(v)).

We obtain a topology on
E
by declaring the sets

\pi_{b}^{-1}(V),\;\;V\subset E_{b}\;\;\textup{open}

to form the basis. This is the
coarsest
topology for which the maps
\pi_{b}
are continuous.

With this canonical topology the structure map

p:E\longrightarrow B
and all horizontal sections
s\in\Gamma^{h}(B,E)
, seen as
maps
s:B\longrightarrow E
, are continuous.

It is readily seen that a sequence
(b_{n},x_{n})\subset E
converges to
(b,x)
in the canonical topology
iff
(b_{n})
converges to
b
for the topology of upsets and

|e_{bc_{n}}(x_{n})-x|\xrightarrow[n\longrightarrow+\infty]{}0.

This is the way in which we use the topological structure.

If
E
is a Kolmogorov space over
B
and
\tau\in B
, then one can obtain a Kolmogorov space by
truncation
: if

i_{\tau}:B[\tau]\longrightarrow B,\ B[\tau]=\{b\in B\;|\;\;b\leq\tau\}

is the inclusion, then
E[\tau]
is just the pull-back
of
E
via this map:

E[\tau]:=i_{\tau}^{*}E.

For many questions only the properties of the Kolmogorov space
E[\tau]
for arbitrary small
\tau
are important, so one is willing to replace
E
by
E[\tau]
if needed.

Kolmogorov spaces over a fixed ordered base
B
form a
full sub-category
{\text{\bf Kol}_{B}}
of the category
\text{\bf Ban}_{B}
of Banach spaces over
B
: a morphism
f:E\longrightarrow F
in
{\text{\bf Kol}_{B}}

over
B
that is just a fibrewise continuous linear map.

If we want to compare Kolmogorov spaces over different bases, one has to
use pull-back by ordered maps: if
p:A\longrightarrow B
is an ordered map, we obtain
a functor

p^{*}:{\text{\bf Kol}_{B}}\longrightarrow{\text{\bf Kol}_{A}},(E\longrightarrow B)\mapsto(p^{*}E\longrightarrow A).

In fact we are in the context of fibred categories and can consider
a
2
-category
Kol
fibres over the category of ordered sets,
with fibre over
B
the category
\text{\bf Kol}_{B}
, etc.
Here we will not develop this point of view further.

1.9.
Kolmogorofication

There are many situations where we obtain Banach spaces over an ordered set
B
where the Kolmogorov space property
does not hold. For instance, if
E\longrightarrow B
is a Kolmogorov space over
B
and

\lambda:B\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}

a rescaling function, then the rescaled Banach space
E(\lambda)
over
B
is
only guaranteed to be a Kolmogorov space if the rescaling is monotonously
increasing:

t\geq s\Leftrightarrow\lambda(t)\geq\lambda(s).

If
E
is a Banach space over an ordered set
B
and
U\subset B
a subset, we can form the Banach space

\Gamma^{\infty}(U,E)

of bounded horizontal sections over
U
. If
U\supset V
, then there is a natural restriction map

\Gamma^{\infty}(U,E)\longrightarrow\Gamma^{\infty}(V,E)

and as the norms on these spaces are defined as supremum over the corresponding sets, these restriction maps trivially satisfy

|s|_{U}\geq|s|_{V}.

Moreover for any
U
and
V
we have the following general sheaf-like gluing property:

Proposition 1.24
.

There is an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\Gamma^{\infty}(U\cup V),E\longrightarrow\Gamma^{\infty}(U,E)\bigoplus\Gamma^{\infty}(V,E)\longrightarrow\Gamma^{\infty}(U\cap V,E)

Proof.

If we have two bounded horizontal sections over two sets
U
and
V
that coincide on the intersection

U\cap V
, then they define a unique section over the union
U\cup V
, bounded by the maximum of the
norms of the two sections.
∎

The subsets of
B
are ordered by the inclusion and therefore
\Gamma^{\infty}(-,E)

defines a Kolmogorov space
\mathcal{E}
over the power set
{\mathcal{P}}(B)
of all subsets of
B
.

Definition 1.25
.

If
E\longrightarrow B
is a Banach space over an ordered set
B

the secondary Kolmogorov space is the Kolmogorov space

\mathcal{E}\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}(B)
with fibre over
U\subset B
equal to
\Gamma^{\infty}(U,E)
.

Recall that

B[b]:=\{b^{\prime}\in N\;|\;b^{\prime}\leq b\}

is the principal downset determined by
b\in B
. Kolmogorov spaces have the
folowing simple key property:

Proposition 1.26
.

Let
E\longrightarrow B
be a Kolmogorov space and
b\in B

then the restriction to the fibre over
b

\Gamma^{\infty}(B[b],E)\longrightarrow E_{b},\;\;s\longrightarrow s(b)

is an isometry.

Proof.

If
v\in E_{b}
is any vector, we can form the horizontal section over
B[b]
by setting

s(a):=e_{ab}(v)

As for the restriction maps one has
|e_{st}|\leq 1
, it follows that the supremum of the
norm is attained at
a=b
, so

|s|_{B[b]}=|s|_{b}=|s(b)|.

∎

This is the basis for the following ’miraculous’ formal procedure that changes any Banach space over
B
into a Kolmogorov space.

Definition 1.27
.

Let
E\longrightarrow B
be a Banach space over an ordered set
B
. The
Kolmogorification
EK\longrightarrow B
of a
E
is the pull-back via

\eta:B\longrightarrow{\mathcal{P}}(B),b\mapsto B[b]:=\{b^{\prime}\in N\;|\;b^{\prime}\leq b\}

of the secondary Kolmogorov space
\mathcal{E}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{P}}(B)
:

EK:=\eta^{*}(\mathcal{E}),

i.e. the fibre of EK at
b\in B
is defined as

EK_{b}:=\Gamma^{\infty}(B[b],E).

Proposition
1.26
states that if
E
itself is a Kolmogorov space, then
EK=E
. Let us now consider a few examples.

Example 1.28
.

Let us consider the vector space
E=\mathbb{R}^{2}
over the base
B=\mathbb{R}_{>0}
:

\mathbb{R}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0},(b,x)\mapsto b

with the norm

|x|_{b}=b^{-k}|x|.

For
k>0
, this is not a Kolmogorov space. There are no non-zero bounded
sections for this norm and the Kolmogorofication is the trivial vector space
EK=\{0\}.

Example 1.29
.

A slightly more interesting example is given by the vector space
E=C^{\bullet}([0,1],\mathbb{R})
over the ordered set
B=(\mathbb{N},\geq)
with the norm

|f|_{n}=n^{-k}\sup_{x\in[0,1],j\leq n}|f^{(j)}(x)|.

For
k>0
, this is again not a Kolmogorov space. The Kolmogorofication has fibres

EK_{n}=C^{n}([0,1],\mathbb{R})

with norms

\sup_{x\in[0,1],j\leq n}|j^{-k}f^{(j)}(x)|.

This example shows that starting from a norm which behaves badly,
we get a systematic way to construct the adapted norms.

The category
{\text{\bf Kol}_{B}}
of Kolmogorov spaces over
B

is a full subcategory of the category
{\text{\bf Ban}_{B}}
of Banach spaces
over
B
. The process of Kolmogorification provides us with a functor

{\text{\bf Ban}_{B}}\longrightarrow{\text{\bf Kol}_{B}},

restricting to the identity on the
{\text{\bf Kol}_{B}}
. Note the similarity to the process of sheafification of a presheaf.

For this reason we will sometimes use an alternative terminology:

Definition 1.30
.

A pre-Kolmogorov space over
B
is a
Banach space over an ordered set
B
.

Another important construction is that of the
direct image
in the category of Kolmogorov spaces. If
p:A\longrightarrow B
is an ordered map, and
U\subset B
is a downset,
then the preimage
p^{-1}(U)\subset A
is also a downset.
If
E
is a Banach space over
A
, then the direct image

is
a Kolmogorov space over
B
, defined by setting

p_{*}(E)_{b}:=\Gamma^{\infty}(p^{-1}(B[b]),E).

The fact that we have to reverse the order relation in
A
is of course
due to the contravariant nature of the
Hom
-functor in the first variable.
If
E
and
F
are Kolmogorov spaces over
]0,S]
, then the external
Hom-space
\mathcal{H}om(E,F)
is a Kolmogorov space over
]0,S]^{op}\times]0,S]
.
This is a natural source of
K2
-spaces.

The external Hom can be used to solve the above mentioned problem concerning
the categorical
{\rm Hom}_{B}(E,F)
.

Definition 1.34
.

For a partial ordered set
B
, we denote by

\overline{\Delta}:=\{(s,t)\in B^{op}\times B\;|\;\;s\leq t\}

the closed subdiagonal.

Proposition 1.35
.

Restriction to the diagonal defines an isomorphism

\Gamma^{h}(\overline{\Delta},\mathcal{H}om(E,F))\longrightarrow{\rm Hom}_{B}(E,F)

of vector spaces and an isometry of Banach spaces

\Gamma^{\infty}(\overline{\Delta},\mathcal{H}om(E,F))\longrightarrow{\rm Hom}_{B}^{b}(E,F)

Proof.

We have to construct an inverse for the restriction map.
If
u:E\longrightarrow F
is a Kolmogorov space morphism over
B
, then for each
t\in B

we have a map
u_{t}:E_{t}\longrightarrow F_{t}
, so we obtain a section of
\mathcal{H}om(E,F)

over the diagonal
\{(t,t)\}
. This section extends naturally to a horizontal
section over
\overline{\Delta}
. Indeed, the compatibility with the restriction
maps in
E
und
F
tells us

f_{st}u_{t}=u_{s}e_{st},\;\;\;\textup{for all}\;\;\;s\leq t.

This means that by setting

u_{st}:=f_{st}u_{t}=u_{s}e_{st}:E_{t}\longrightarrow F_{s}

we obtain an horizontal extension of this section over
\overline{\Delta}
.
If we start with an element of
{\rm Hom}^{b}_{B}(E,F)
, it represents
a bounded section of
\mathcal{H}om(E,F)
over the diagonal
\{(t,t)\}
,
which extends in exactly the same way to a bounded horizontal
section of
\mathcal{H}om
over
\overline{\Delta}
. The fact that we
obtain an isometry follows from the Kolmogorov property: for horzontal
sections over
\overline{\Delta}
the norm function takes its supremum on
the diagonal.
∎

We should always make a careful distinction between the following
objects (here
A,B
are ordered sets):

(1)

Hom(E,F)
is the ordinary Banach space of continuous linear maps between Banach spaces
E
and
F
provided with the operator norm.

(2)

{\rm Hom}_{B}(E,F)
is the categorical Hom between two Banach spaces
E,F\longrightarrow B
over an ordered set
B
. It is a vector space.

(3)

{\rm Hom}_{B}^{b}(E,F)
consists of those elements of
{\rm Hom}_{B}(E,F)
whose operator norm function is bounded on
B
. It is a Banach space.

(4)

\mathcal{H}om_{B}(E,F)
is a Banach space over the
set

B
, with fibre
Hom(E_{b},F_{b})
; we will not make much use of this object.

(5)

\mathcal{H}om(E,F)
is the most important and more general object. It is a Banach space over
A^{op}\times B
. In the case that
A=B
the (resp. bounded) horizontal sections of
\mathcal{H}om(E,F)
over the closed subdiagonal gives back
{\rm Hom}_{B}(E,F)
(resp.
{\rm Hom}_{B}^{b}(E,F))
.

Chapter 2
Kolmogorov spaces and sheaves

In the previous chapter we introduced the formal framework of Kolmogorov spaces
that will be used to handle the functional analytic parts of KAM-theory.
We have seen that it is easy to construct examples and in this and later
chapters we will be mainly interested in specific examples related to spaces
of holomorphic objects. Due to the identity theorem for holomorphic
functions, in these examples the restriction mappings
are injective on domains and over a directed set the Kolmogorov space
structure leads to a descending filtration on the limit vector space

{\displaystyle\lim_{\longrightarrow}E}
by Banach spaces
E_{s}
. However, it is always
important to keep also track of the norms involved.

2.1.
The sheaf of holomorphic functions

For an open set
U
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
or more generally of a fixed analytic space
X
, we denote by
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(U)
or simply
{\mathcal{O}}(U)
the set of all holomorphic functions on
U
. As obviously the pointwise sum and product of holomorphic functions is again holomorphic,
the set
{\mathcal{O}}(U)
has the natural structure of a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra.
For an inclusion
V\subset U
there are natural
restriction maps

{\mathcal{O}}(U)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}(V),f\mapsto f_{|V}
, making the assignment
U\mapsto{\mathcal{O}}(U)
into a presheaf on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
or equivalently we have a vector space
{\mathcal{O}}
over
the ordered set
{\mathcal{T}}
of open subsets. In fact, this presheaf is a sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
: if any
U
is covered by open sets
U_{i},i\in I
and
f^{(i)}\in{\mathcal{O}}(U_{i})
have the property that

f^{(i)}_{|U_{i}\cap U_{j}}=f^{(j)}_{|U_{i}\cap U_{j}}

then there exist a unique
f\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)
with

f_{|U_{i}}=f^{(i)}.

The space of sections
{\mathcal{O}}(K)
of
{\mathcal{O}}
over a compact set
K
can be identified with the space of holomorphic functions that are defined on an open subset
containing
K
, where two such functions are identified when they coincide on a
common open subset containing
K
. That is, we are dealing with
germs

of holomorphic functions along the set
K
.
In the special case where
K
consists of a single point, we have the stalk

{\mathcal{O}}_{p}
of
{\mathcal{O}}
at the point in question, which can, after choice of coordinates
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
, be identified with the algebra of convergent
power series

{\mathcal{O}}_{0}=\mathbb{C}\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\}.

For an open subset
U\subset X
, the vector spaces
{\mathcal{O}}(U)
has
a natural structure of a
Fréchet-space
defined by the family of
semi-norms:

|f|_{K}=\sup_{z\in K}|f(z)|,\;\;K\subset U\;\;\textup{compact}

But we will not really use this topology, as in the formalism
of Kolmogorov spaces we developed, we work only with Banach spaces.

2.2.
Kolmogorov spaces
{\mathcal{O}}^{b}
,
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}
,
{\mathcal{O}}^{h}

So the question arises which Banach spaces of holomorphic functions one can
naturally associate to (certain) open subsets
U
of an analytic space
X
.
We will briefly discuss three different natural choices.

First, one can consider the vector space

{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(U):=\{f\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)\;|\;f\;\;\textup{is bounded}\},

consisting of the
bounded holomorphic functions
on
U
.
We can make
{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(U)
into a normed vector space using the supremum norm:

|f|_{U}:=\sup_{p\in{U}}|f(p)|.

and in fact with this norm
{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(U)
is a Banach space: a Cauchy sequence

(f_{n})
for
|-|_{U}
converges pointwise to a bounded continuous function and
by the Cauchy integral theorem that function is also holomorphic.

There are obvious restriction mappings, making the
{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(U)
into a Banach
space

{\mathcal{O}}^{b}
over
\mathcal{T}
. If
U\supseteq V
, the restriction mapping

f_{VU}:{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(U)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(V)

has operator norm at most
1
, as the supremum
|f|_{V}
over the smaller set

V
clearly is
\leq|f|_{U}
. Hence

{\mathcal{O}}^{b}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{T}}

is in fact a Kolmogorov space.
Note however that
U\mapsto{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(U)
is no longer a sheaf. For two
open subsets
U,V
the kernel of the obvious map

{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(U)\oplus{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(V)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(U\cap V),\;\;\;(f,g)\mapsto f_{|U\cap V}-g_{|U\cap V}

is precisely
{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(U\cup V)
, so bounded holomorphic functions can be
glued, but the sheaf axiom for infinite unions does not hold in general.

If we restrict to the sub-directed system
\mathcal{T}_{p}
of open sets
containing a point
p
, we obtain as limit the vector space of germs of
holomorphic functions at
p
, which can in turn be identified with the
space of convergent power series.

Second, for any open
U\subset X
we can consider the vector space

{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U):=C(\overline{U})\cap\mathcal{O}(U).

consisting of holomorphic functions on
U
that have a
continuous

extension to the closure
\overline{U}
.
It is again a vector space over
\mathcal{T}
:

{\mathcal{O}}^{c}\longrightarrow\mathcal{T}

and in fact
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}
is a sheaf on
X
. But
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)
is in general
not a Banach space, but if
U
is relatively compact (i.e. if
\overline{U}

is compact), then
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)
equipped with the
\sup
-norm
is
a Banach
space. So if we restrict
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}
to the system
{\mathcal{T}}_{c}
of relatively compact
open sets we obtain a Kolomogorov space. We can also glue
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}
-functions:
the kernel of the map

{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)\oplus{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(V)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U\cap V),

is precisely
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U\cup V)
. As the arbitrary union of relative compact
open sets need not be relative compact, we again do not quite obtain a sheaf
in the ordinary sense of the word.

Clearly, for
U\in\mathcal{T}_{c}
there is a natural inclusion

{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(U)

compatible with restrictions. So these combine to a natural morphism
I
of
Kolmogorov spaces over
\mathcal{T}_{c}
:

\textstyle{{\mathcal{O}}^{c}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{I}
\textstyle{{\mathcal{O}}^{b}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathcal{T}_{c}}

In other words:
I\in{\rm Hom}_{\mathcal{T}_{c}}({\mathcal{O}}^{c},{\mathcal{O}}^{b})
.

Third, if on the analytic manifold
X
a measure
\mu
is given, e.g.
X=\mathbb{C}^{n}

with the Lebesgue measure, one also can consider the Banach space

{\mathcal{O}}_{X}^{(p)}\longrightarrow\mathcal{T}

with fibre

{\mathcal{O}}^{(p)}(U):=L^{p}(U,\mu)\cap{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(U)

of
p
-integrable holomorphic functions on
U
, with norm

|f|_{U}=(\int_{U}|f(z)|^{p}d\mu)^{1/p}.

If
V\subset U
, then there are natural restriction mappings

{\mathcal{O}}^{(p)}(U)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{(p)}(V)

of norm
\leq 1
, so we obtain Kolmogorov spaces.

One can consider
{\mathcal{O}}^{(p)}
as an
L^{p}
-variant of the structure sheaf.
Again, they are not sheaves in the usual sense, but again the kernel of
the map

{\mathcal{O}}^{(p)}(U)\oplus{\mathcal{O}}^{(p)}(V)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{(p)}(U\cap V)

is
{\mathcal{O}}^{(p)}(U\cup V)
.

In the special case
p=2
we obtain a Hilbert space, for which we will
choose the particular notation

{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(U):={\mathcal{O}}^{(2)}(U).

This norm comes from a Hermitean scalar product on
{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(U)

given by

\langle f,g\rangle_{U}:=\int_{U}f(z)\overline{g(z)}d\mu.

Such a space might, for obvious reasons, be called a
Kolmogorov-Hilbert

space.

2.3.
Restriction to polydiscs

In practice it is convenient to work with systems of vector spaces over
smaller ordered sets, like intervals in
\mathbb{R}
, with their natural
ordering. We will often use the function spaces introduced above
over systems of (open or closed)
polydiscs
.
For a
polyradius

\rho:=(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\ldots,\rho_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}

we write

D_{\rho}:=D_{\rho_{1}}\times D_{\rho_{2}}\times\ldots\times D_{\rho_{n}}:=\{(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\;|\;\;|x_{i}|<\rho_{i}\}

for the
open polydisc
of polyradius
\rho
. The open
polydisc is an open set over the orderered set
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
of radii.

We will consider frequently the family
D=(D_{s})
of polydiscs

D_{s}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:|z_{i}|<s\}=D_{s,s,\ldots,s}.

We will call it the
unit polydisc
; it is a set
over
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
:

D\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}.

Using the map of ordered sets

i:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\longrightarrow\mathcal{T}_{c},\;\;s\mapsto D_{s},

we can pullback the Kolmogorov spaces
{\mathcal{O}}^{b},{\mathcal{O}}^{c}
and
{\mathcal{O}}^{h}
to
obtain Kolmogorov spaces

{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D):=i^{*}{\mathcal{O}}^{b},\;\;{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D):=i^{*}{\mathcal{O}}^{c},\;\;{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(D):=i^{*}{\mathcal{O}}^{h}

over
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, with fibres

{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D)_{t}:={\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D_{t}).

{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)_{t}:={\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D_{t})=C(\overline{D_{t}})\cap\mathcal{O}(D_{t}).

{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(D)_{t}:={\mathcal{O}}^{h}(D_{t})=L^{2}(\overline{D_{t}})\cap\mathcal{O}(D_{t}).

2.4.
The order filtration on
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}

Let us take a closer look to the Kolmogorov space
E:={\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)
over

\mathbb{R}_{>0}
of holomorphic functions on the unit polydics, with continuous
extension to the boundary. The order
k
-part
E^{(k)}
was defined in

1.31
as the Kolmogorification of
E(\lambda_{k})
, the Banach
space over
\mathbb{R}
obtained by rescaling the norm with the function

\lambda_{k}:s\mapsto s^{-k}.

It consists of those
f\in E_{t}={\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D_{t})
for which

|f|_{t}^{(k)}:=\sup_{s\leq t,|z|\leq s}s^{-k}|f(z)|_{s}.

is finite. Clearly, if
k\leq 0
, then
E^{(k)}=E
and furthermore for

k\geq 0
and
f\in E^{(k)}_{t}
, we have the estimate

|f|_{s}\leq Rs^{k},\;\;\textup{for all}\;s\leq t,\;\;\;\textup{with}\;\;R=|f|_{t}.

That is, for any
z\in D_{s}
, with
s:=|z|
:

|f(z)|\leq R|z|^{k}.

So we see that for
k\in\mathbb{N}
the space
E^{(k)}_{t}
consist of
those functions whose derivatives at the origin vanish up to order

k-1
. When we write

f(z)=z^{k}f_{k}(z)

then by the maximum principle

s^{-k}|f(z)|_{s}=|f_{k}|_{s}

and therefore

|f|_{t}^{(k)}=|f_{k}|_{t}.

The direct limit functor sends
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)
to the space of germs of holomorphic functions at
0
, which can be identified with convergent analytic series
\mathbb{C}\{z\}
.
The direct limit of
E^{(k)}
is equal to to the
k
-th power
{\mathcal{M}}^{k}
of the maximal ideal

{\mathcal{M}}:=\{f\in\mathbb{C}\{z\}:f(0)=0\}.

So the filtration by Kolmogorov-spaces

E=E^{(0)}\supset E^{(1)}\supset E^{(2)}\supset E^{(3)}\supset\ldots

corresponds
exactly
to the
{\mathcal{M}}
-adic filtration via the direct limit functor:

\mathbb{C}\{z\}\supset{\mathcal{M}}\supset{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\supset{\mathcal{M}}^{3}\supset\ldots

This generalises, of course, to an arbitrary number of variables.

The Kolmogorov space norms
|-|^{(k)}
are ’adapted’ to the filtration: for instance
according to Proposition
1.32
, the unit ball
B\subset{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)
contains any ball of
({\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D))^{(1)}[\tau]
for
\tau
small enough.

2.5.
The Kolmogorov spaces
\ell^{2}(\lambda)

We will see that the Kolmogorov-Hilbert space
{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(D)
can be seen a
special case of the
sequence Kolmogorov spaces

\ell^{2}(\lambda)
,
which we now define. These simple spaces depend on an additional datum,
that we will call a
weight sequence
.

Definition 2.1
.

A weight sequence
\lambda=(\lambda_{n}),\ n\in\mathbb{N}
over
]0,S]
is a sequence
of positive increasing functions indexed by
\mathbb{N}
:

\lambda_{n}:I\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0},\;\;\;\lambda_{n}(t)\geq\lambda_{n}(s)\text{ for }t\geq s.

In applications we will also consider weight sequences that are parametrised by
an arbitrary countable index set rather than
\mathbb{N}
.

To describe the space
\ell^{p}(\lambda)
, we first consider the sequence space

\mathbb{C}^{\infty}

consisting of arbitrary sequences

x=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots),\;x_{i}\in\mathbb{C}.

The elements

e_{n}:=(0,0,\ldots,0,1,0,\ldots)\in\mathbb{C}^{\infty}

with
1
at spot
n
form the
standard basis elements
of
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}

and we can write

x=\sum_{n\geq 0}x_{n}e_{n}.

If
\lambda=(\lambda_{n})
is a weight sequence and
x\in\mathbb{C}^{\infty}
, we set

|x|_{s}:=\left(\sum_{n\geq 0}|x_{n}|^{p}\lambda_{n}^{p}(s)\right)^{1/p}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\},

where we suppressed the dependence of the norm on
p
from the notation.

Definition 2.2
.

The Banach space
\ell^{p}(\lambda)
over the interval
]0,S]

is defined by the Banach spaces

\ell^{p}(\lambda)_{s}:=\{x\in\mathbb{C}^{\infty}:\;|x|_{s}<+\infty\},\;\;\;s\in\mathbb{R},

with
|-|_{s}
as norm.

Proposition 2.3
.

\ell^{p}(\lambda)
is an
S
-Kolmogorov space.

Proof.

As
\lambda_{n}(t)\geq\lambda_{n}(s)
for all
t\geq s
, one clearly has

t\geq s\Longrightarrow|x|_{t}\geq|x_{s}|

and therefore we have inclusions

\ell^{p}(\lambda)_{t}\hookrightarrow\ell^{p}(\lambda)_{s}.

These inclusions have clearly norm
\leq 1
, so the Banach spaces
\ell^{p}(\lambda)_{t}
,
with these inclusions as restriction maps, form a Kolmogorov space on the interval
]0,S]
.
∎

As an example, for the constant weight system
\lambda_{n}=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we get a “constant” Kolmogorov
space:
\ell^{p}(\lambda)_{t}=\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})
for all
t
’s and the restriction maps are all equal to the identity.
More interesting are the spaces defined by the weight sequence

\lambda_{n}(s)=s^{n}.

In this case the restriction maps

\ell(\lambda)^{p}_{t}\longrightarrow\ell(\lambda)^{p}_{s}

are limits of finite rank mappings

\rho_{k}:\ell^{p}(\lambda)_{t}\longrightarrow\ell^{p}(\lambda)_{s},\ (x_{n})\mapsto(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{k},0,0\dots)

and are therefore compact operators.

For the particular weight sequence
\lambda_{n}=s^{n}
it is very simple to determine
the order filtration of the Kolmogorov spaces
\ell^{p}(\lambda)
.

Proposition 2.4
.

\ell^{p}(\lambda)^{(k)}
consists of sequences with vanishing first
k
-terms.

Proof.

Indeed if

|x|_{s}=\left(\sum_{n\geq k}|a_{n}|^{p}s^{pn}\right)^{1/p}=s^{k}\left(\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n+k}|^{p}s^{pn}\right)^{1/p}

thus
x\in E^{(k)}
. Morever if
a_{k}\neq 0
then

|x|_{s}\geq|a_{k}|s^{k}

and
x\notin E^{(k+\varepsilon)}
for any
\varepsilon>0
. So the condition is necessary and sufficient.
∎

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
D
be the open unit disc over
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
Then the map

{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(D)_{s}\longrightarrow\ell^{2}(\lambda)_{s},f\mapsto({\langle}f,z^{I}\rangle_{s})

defines an isomorphism between the Kolmogorov spaces

{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(D)
and
\ell^{2}(\lambda)
, where the weight sequence
\lambda

is given by

\lambda_{I}(s)=\sqrt{C(I)}s^{n+|I|}.

and

C(I):=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\pi}{i_{k}+1}.

Proof.

The monomials

z^{I}:=z_{1}^{i_{1}}z_{2}^{i_{2}}\ldots z_{n}^{i_{n}}

form an orthogonal Hilbert basis of
{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(D)
. One computes

{\langle}z^{I},z^{J}\rangle_{s}=\int_{D_{s}}z^{I}\bar{z}^{J}d\mu=C(I)s^{2n+|I|+|J|}\delta_{IJ}

where

C(I)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\pi}{i_{k}+1}.

In particular:

|z^{I}|_{s}=C(I)^{1/2}s^{n+|I|}.

∎

2.6.
Kolmogorov spaces and coherent sheaves

In the previous sections, we introduced the Kolmogorov space versions
{\mathcal{O}}^{b}
,
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}
and

{\mathcal{O}}^{h}
of the sheaf of functions
{\mathcal{O}}
. In applications one not only encounters
functions, but also vector fields, differential forms and further tensor fields.
These objects can be seen as sections of vector bundles and form locally free
sheaves. Given a free sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
there is no difficulty in defining its Kolmogorov
versions
{\mathcal{F}}^{a}
,
a\in\{b,c,h\}
, as for sufficiently small
U
one has

{\mathcal{F}}(U)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}{\mathcal{O}}(U)e_{i}

for some choice of generators
e_{i}
, and one can simply put

{\mathcal{F}}^{a}(U):=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}{\mathcal{O}}^{a}(U)e_{i},\;\;\textup{for}\;\;\;a\in\{b,c,h\}.

In this way one can speak for instance about Kolmogorov spaces of vector fields
\Theta_{X}^{c}
over a smooth complex manifold and more generally of arbitrary tensors. Of course, this depends on the choice of generators, but this dependence
is usually not important.

However, often one has to deal with other types of sheaves, for example ideal sheaves

{\mathcal{I}}\subset{\mathcal{O}}
, or functions defined on a submanifold like
{\mathcal{O}}/{\mathcal{I}}
and such sheaves
are usually not locally free. The question arises if it is possible to define Kolmogorov versions
{\mathcal{F}}^{a}
,
a\in\{b,c,h\}
for a general coherent sheaf.
Recall the sections of a coherent sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
over sufficiently small open subsets
U

can be be described by a
presentation
, i.e. an exact sequence of the form

{\mathcal{O}}(U)^{q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathcal{O}}(U)^{p}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}(U)\longrightarrow 0

where

A\in Mat(p\times q,{\mathcal{O}}(U))

is a matrix of functions, holomorphic on
U
, so
{\mathcal{F}}(U)=Coker(A)
.
Given such a presentation, one is tempted to use the same matrix
A

(maybe on a slightly smaller open set) to define
{\mathcal{F}}^{a}
-versions by setting

{\mathcal{F}}^{a}(U):=Coker({\mathcal{O}}^{a}(U)^{q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathcal{O}}^{a}(U)^{p})

for
a\in\{c,b,h\}
.

A complication arises from the fact that the map
A
may not have a closed
image, and as a result the cokernel will not have the structure of a Banach
space. It was
H. Cartan
who realised that in most situations one may
circumvent this difficulty. We will use the following terminology:

Definition 2.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be a coherent sheaf on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. An open subset
U\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is
called
{\mathcal{F}}
-open, if
{\mathcal{F}}^{a}(U)
is a
Banach space for
a\in\{b,c,h\}
.
(These are more commonly called privileged neighbourhood for the sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
.)

Theorem 2.7
.

For any coherent sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
there exists open subsets
\Omega\subset GL(n,\mathbb{C})
and

\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
containing
0
in its closure, such that
AD_{r}
is
{\mathcal{F}}
-open for any
A\in\Omega
,
r\in\Delta
.

Note that
{\mathcal{F}}
-open sets can be glued: the union of two
{\mathcal{F}}
-open sets is again an
{\mathcal{F}}
-open set. Therefore the theorem implies the existence of privileged neighbourhoods over any compact sets. A proof of the theorem is given in the appendix.

The category of coherent sheaves admits version of kernels and cokernels in the
Kolmogorov space setting:

Corollary 2.8
.

Consider a morphism of coherent sheaves on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

{\mathcal{F}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathcal{G}}.

For each point
p\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
there exists a fundamental system of
S
-open sets
U_{s}
,
s\in]0,S]
,
such that the kernels, images and Cokernels of the maps

{\mathcal{F}}^{a}(U)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathcal{G}}^{a}(U),\;\;\;a\in\{b,c,h\}

are
S
-Kolmogorov spaces.

3.1.
An example

A homomorphism

between Kolmogorov-spaces over

defines in
particular for each

In many geometrical situations one has to deal with mappings of a different

for

but rather on is working with maps

for certain
pairs

and

Let us give a nice, crazy example. Consider the set

over

defined by

D_{t}:=\{x+iy\in\mathbb{C}:x^{2}+ty^{2}<t^{2}.\}

For

induces a morphism

Due to the fact that the set

is a round disc only for

,
there are no obvious maps

{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)_{t}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)_{t}

that extend the map

one has for

To be specific, let us look at the rotation

over

The ellipse

contains the rotated ellipse

if
and only if

and

If these inequalities holds, the map

into

and we obtain induced maps

in the following region.

Note that these mappings

We will call such thing a

to

From this example it should already be clear that the appearance of such

3.2.
Partial morphisms

Definition 3.1
.

Let
E,F
be Banach spaces over ordered sets

.
A partial morphism from

to

over

is
a horizontal section of
\mathcal{H}om(E,F)
over

. We use the notation

to denote the vector space of all partial morphisms over
C
.
We will give it the topology of pointwise convergence, but this topology will play no big role here.

We will often write partial morphisms just as ordinary morphisms

u:E\longrightarrow F

meaning that for
(t,s)\in C\subset A\times B
there is a corresponding map

u_{st}:E_{t}\longrightarrow F_{s}.

We call
C
the
definition set
of the partial morphism and write

C={\mathcal{D}}(u).

Restriction and Extension

Obviously, a partial morphism can be restricted to a smaller set:
if
C\subset D
, there is a natural restriction
map

{\rm Hom}_{D}(E,F)\longrightarrow{\rm Hom}_{C}(E,F),\;\;u\mapsto u_{|C}.

Conversely, if

is a partial morphism with definition
set
C
, one may try to extend

to a larger set
D\supset C
.

For example, if we consider a
single map

as a partial
morphism defined at the point

, we may extend it to the downset

C^{d}:=\{(t,s)\in A^{op}\times B\;|\;t\leq a,\,s\leq b\}

using the restriction maps
e
resp
f
of
E
resp.
F
, by defining

u_{st}=f_{sb}u_{ba}e_{at}:E_{t}\longrightarrow F_{s}.

Recall that
t\leq a
in

means concretely that

in
A
.

Nevertheless given

and two points

,
the extension of

on the downset of

and the extension on the

need not to coincide on their intersection.
But this is guaranteed if

p,q\in C\implies\inf(p,q)=(c,b)\in C.

Definition 3.2
.

For a partial ordered set

the closed subdiagonal.
If

The restriction map

We have seen in

The above procedure then produces a partial morphism

where the definition set is the cone

3.4.
Composition of partial morphisms

How to compose such partial morphisms?
If we are given partial morphisms

given maps

So if we have pairs

can define the composition

This composition may depend on the choice of

So if

This motivates the following two definitions:

Definition 3.3

The convolution of

A_{1}\star A_{2}:=\{(r,t)\in B_{1}\times B_{3}\;|\;\;\exists s\in B_{2}\;\;\textup{such that}(t,r)\in A_{1},(r,s)\in A_{2}\}

The above given definition of composition then results in the following

Proposition 3.4
.

Let

be K1-spaces.
The composition

of two partial morphisms

is a well-defined element of

The map

{\rm Hom}_{A_{1}}(E_{1},E_{2})\times{\rm Hom}_{A_{2}}(E_{2},E_{3})\longrightarrow{\rm Hom}_{A_{1}\star A_{2}}(E_{1},E_{3})

Of course, it may very well happen that

Chapter 4
Local morphisms

In functional analysis a major role is played by the operators that
behave like differential operators, and which might be called

local operators.
This notion is rather vague in general, but
in the context of Kolmogorov spaces we will define a precise notion
of locality.

4.1.
The derivative

The first important property of differential operators is that they only give rise to
partial morphisms of Kolmogorov spaces. To explain this point, let us start with simple examples.

If we differentiate a
C^{k}
function, we get a
C^{k-1}
function. So
we consider

E:=C^{\bullet}([0,1],\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}

as a Kolmogorov space
with fibre
E_{k}:=C^{k}([0,1],\mathbb{R})
. The inclusions

C^{k+1}([0,1],\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow C^{k}([0,1],\mathbb{R})

give restriction mappings and therefore a Kolmogorov space structure over
(\mathbb{N},\geq)
.

The derivative gives a well-defined maps

E_{k+1}\longrightarrow E_{k},f\mapsto f^{\prime}

and thus we obtain a partial morphism

D\in Hom_{\Delta}(E,E)

with

\Delta=\{(k,n)\in\mathbb{N}^{op}\times\mathbb{N}\;\;|\;\;\;n<k\}

The situation becomes more interesting in the case of holomorphic functions.

The derivative gives a well-defined map on the algebra of convergent power series in one variable
z
:

\mathbb{C}\{z\}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\{z\};\;f\mapsto f^{\prime}.

The space
\mathbb{C}\{z\}
arises as limit of the Kolmogorov space

{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)\longrightarrow D

with fibre

{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)_{t}:=C(\overline{D_{t}})\cap{\mathcal{O}}(D_{t}),

where

D\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}

is the unit disk.

The derivative
f\mapsto f^{\prime}
does not define a map

{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)_{t}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)_{t},\ f\mapsto f^{\prime}

and therefore it is not a morphism of Kolmogorov spaces.
However for all
s<t
, there
are
well defined maps:

\varphi_{st}:{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)_{t}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)_{s},\ f\mapsto f^{\prime}

and these are compatible with the restriction maps. This can be interpreted
as saying that the derivative is a partial morphism
\varphi
defined over the

open sub-diagonal

\Delta:=\{(s,t)\in]0,S]\times]0,S]\;|\;s<t\}.

The fact that this section
\varphi
has no extension over the
closed sub-diagonal
\overline{\Delta}
is an expression of the
’unbounded nature’ of the derivative operator

\varphi\in{\rm Hom}_{\Delta}(E,E).

We claim that
\varphi
is not bounded,

\varphi\notin{\rm Hom}^{b}_{\Delta}(E,E).

To see this, one can look at the holomorphic function

f(z)=(1-z)\log(1-z)

on the open disc
D_{1}
of radius
1
. It is an element of
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D_{1})=E_{1}

and by restriction to smaller
t
be obtain a bounded horizontal section of

E
over
]0,1]
. Its derivative is

f^{\prime}(z)=1+\log(1-z)\xrightarrow[z\mapsto 1]{}-\infty

which means that
\varphi(f)
is an unbounded section of
E
over
]0,1[
.

4.2.
The Cauchy-Nagumo inequality

An unbounded operator like the derivative is of course more difficult
to handle, but we will now see it defines a partial morphism
with a first order
pole along the diagonal
. The following fundamental fact shows what we need:

Proposition 4.1

(
Cauchy-Nagumo inequalities)
.

Let

D\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}

be the unit disk in
\mathbb{C}
. Then the
k
-th derivative of
f\in{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D_{t})
satisfies the estimate:

|f^{(k)}|_{s}\leq\frac{k!}{(t-s)^{k}}|f|_{t}

for any
s<t
.

Proof.

The statement is an easy corollary of the Cauchy integral formula.
If
z\in D_{s}
and
f\in{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D_{t})
, then one has

f(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma_{z}}\frac{f(\xi)}{z-\xi}d\xi,

where
\gamma_{z}
is the circle of radius
t-s
centred at
z
. Differentiation
under the integral sign gives

f^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma_{z}}\frac{f(\xi)}{(z-\xi)^{2}}d\xi.

We parametrise
\gamma_{z}
by:

\theta\mapsto\xi(\theta):=z+(t-s)e^{2\pi i\theta}

and thus

d\xi=2\pi i(t-s)e^{2\pi i\theta}d\theta

so that

f^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{t-s}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{f(\xi(\theta))}{e^{2\pi i\theta}}d\theta.

and finally

|f^{\prime}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{t-s}\int_{0}^{1}|f(\xi(\theta))|d\theta\leq\frac{1}{t-s}|f|_{t},\ \forall z\in D_{s}.

In other words:

|f^{\prime}|_{s}\leq\frac{1}{t-s}|f|_{t}

for any
t>s
. For higher values of
k
the proof is similar.
∎

This result means that as we approach the boundary of the disc
D_{t}
, the value of
f^{\prime}
may increase, but not faster than the inverse of the distance to the boundary. In other words the derivative defines an almost complete morphism with a simple pole along the diagonal.
So if we rescale rescale the K2-space
\mathcal{H}om(E,E)
with the function

\lambda(s)=(t-s),

then due to the Cauchy-Nagumo inequalities,
the derivative morphism
\varphi
is a bounded horizontal section
of
\mathcal{H}om(E,E)(\lambda)
over
\Delta
:

\varphi\in\Gamma^{\infty}(\Delta,\mathcal{H}om(E,E)(\lambda)).

More generally, if we consider a differential operator of higher order
as an almost complete morphism of a Kolmogorov space, the above Cauchy-Nagumo
inequality may be used to detect the
order
of the differential operator
in terms of the internal structure.

If

The map which associates

f
is given by the
\mathbb{C}
-linear map

\varphi:\mathbb{C}\{z\}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\{z\},f\mapsto\frac{f-f(0)}{z}.

We would like to understand this map in terms of Kolmogorov spaces.

So, we consider the unit disc
D=(D_{s})
over
B:=\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and the associated
Kolmogorov space
E:={\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)
.
Clearly, for fixed
t
we can consider the analogous map

\varphi_{t}^{c}:E_{t}\longrightarrow E_{t},\;\;f\mapsto\frac{f-f(0)}{z}

So unlike the case of the derivative, it is a complete morphism: the division maps are compatible with restrictions and we obtain a global horizontal section

\varphi^{c}\in\Gamma^{h}(\overline{\Delta},\mathcal{H}om(E,E)))={\rm Hom}_{B}(E,E)

But this map is again unbounded, i.e.

\varphi^{c}\notin\Gamma^{\infty}(\overline{\Delta},\mathcal{H}om(E,E)))={\rm Hom}_{B}^{b}(E,E)

To see this, consider for instance sequence of linear polynomials

1+nz.

We consider these as a horizontal section of
E
:

\sigma_{n}:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\longrightarrow E,\ s\mapsto(s,1+nz).

Clearly one has

\varphi^{c}(\sigma_{n}(s))=n

and

|\sigma_{n}(s)|=\sup_{|z|\leq s}|1+nz|=1+ns.

Hence, for
s\longrightarrow 0
, we get

|\varphi^{c}(\sigma)|_{s}\geq\frac{|\varphi^{c}(\sigma(s))|}{|\sigma(s)|}=\frac{n}{1+ns}\sim n.

Thus the section
\varphi^{c}
is unbounded.

Now rescale the K2-space
\mathcal{H}om(E,E)
with the function

\lambda(s,t)=s.

Using the maximum principle, we get that

\left|\frac{f-f(0)}{z}\right|_{t}=t^{-1}|f-f(0)|_{t}

and therefore

\left|\frac{f-f(0)}{z}\right|_{t}\leq 2t^{-1}|f|_{t}.

The norm of this section is defined by

|u|^{(\lambda)}:=\sup_{f\in B_{t}}t|u(f)|_{t}\leq 2

where
B_{t}
denotes the unit ball in
E_{t}
.
This shows that after rescaling we get a bounded map.

4.4.
Local operators

We have seen the derivative defines a partial morphism with poles along
s=t
, whereas the division operator by
z

has pole along
s=0
. If we now compose both operation, we get an operator:

\varphi:\mathbb{C}\{z\}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\{z\},f\mapsto z^{-1}(f^{\prime}(z)-f^{\prime}(0))

which lifts to a partial morphism with poles along
s=t
and
s=0
, i.e.
the two sides of the cone
0\leq s\leq t
.

This means that in the Kolmogorov space

E={\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0},

the map
\varphi
defines an unbounded
horizontal section

\varphi\in{\rm Hom}_{\Delta}(E,E)=\Gamma^{h}(\Delta,\mathcal{H}om(E,E)).

The norm of this section goes to infinity along the sides
s=0
and
t=s
of
\Delta
. If we now rescale the K2-space

\mathcal{H}om(E,E)\longrightarrow B^{op}\times B

with the scaling function

\lambda(t,s)=s(t-s),

the section
\varphi
becomes bounded:

\varphi\in\Gamma^{\infty}(\Delta,\mathcal{H}om(E,E)(\lambda)).

Recall that any map

\varphi_{t,s}:E_{t}\longrightarrow F_{s},\ E=F={\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)

extends horizontally to the downset of
(t,s)
. So we
get morphisms

\varphi_{r,u}:E_{u}\longrightarrow E_{r},\;\;u\geq t,\;\;s\geq r.

This was the basis of our considerations on
the external Hom
1.33
.

The situation here is in some sense reversed, a map

\varphi_{t,s}:E_{t}\longrightarrow F_{s}

that arise from the derivative or division map
has an
opposite behaviour
: it extend to the
part of the
upset
of
(t,s)
below the diagonal. As a result, a single

\varphi_{t,s}
defines a horizontal section over the downset of the upset of
(s,t)
!

Let us turn this property into a concept. Given two
S
-Kolmogorov spaces

E\longrightarrow]0,S],\ F\longrightarrow]0,S],

we consider the space
\mathcal{H}om(E,F)(\lambda)
with rescaling function

\lambda(t,s)=s^{k}(t-s)^{l},

where
k,l
are arbitrary positive numbers.
For a point
(t,s)\in\Delta
we consider the Banach-space

{\mathcal{L}}^{k,l}(E,F)_{t,s}:=\Gamma^{\infty}(\Delta({t,s}),\mathcal{H}om(E,F)(\lambda)),

where
\Delta({t,s})
is the upset of the point
(t,s)
.

The norm of an element of
{\mathcal{L}}^{k,l}(E,F)_{t,s}
is given by

|u|:=\sup_{(t^{\prime},s^{\prime})\leq(t,s)}((s^{\prime})^{k}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})^{l}|u_{s^{\prime}t^{\prime}}|).

As the elements of
{\mathcal{L}}^{k,l}(E,F)_{t,s}

are sections over
\Delta(t,s)
, if
(t^{\prime},s^{\prime})\in\Delta(t,s)
then
\Delta(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})\subset\Delta(t,s)
, hence there is an obvious restriction mapping of norm
\leq 1
:

{\mathcal{L}}^{k,l}(E,F)_{t,s}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}^{k,l}(E,F)_{t^{\prime},s^{\prime}}.

Thus we obtain in this way a
K2
-space but with opposite order on the base

{\mathcal{L}}^{k,l}(E,F)\longrightarrow\Delta^{op},

whose fibre above
(t,s)
is
{\mathcal{L}}^{k,l}(E,F)_{t,s}
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
E,F
be
S
-Kolmogorov spaces. Elements of the
K2
-space

{\mathcal{L}}^{k,l}(E,F)\longrightarrow\Delta^{op}

will be called local operators.

Frequently, the sections are non-singular along
s=0
, therefore we also use
the notation

{\mathcal{L}}^{k}(E,F):={\mathcal{L}}^{0,k}(E,F)

Example 4.3
.

The
k^{th}
derivative

E:={\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D),\;\;f\mapsto f^{(k)}

is an element of
{\mathcal{L}}^{k}(E,E)
. More generally, any differential operator
P
of order
k
,
with holomorphic coefficients, defines an element of
{\mathcal{L}}^{k}(E,E)
.

Proposition 4.4
.

For
m\geq 0
there are inclusions

{\mathcal{L}}^{k,l}(E,F)\subset{\mathcal{L}}^{k+m,l+n}(E,F)

and if

denotes the inclusion map one has

|i(u)|\leq S^{m+n}|u|.

Proof.

Fix
s<t
and denote by
\|\cdot\|
the operator norm in the Banach space

L(E_{t},E_{s})=Hom(E_{t},F_{s})
.
We can write

\|u_{st}\|\leq\frac{|u|}{s^{k}(t-s)^{l}}=\frac{s^{m}(t-s)^{n}|u|}{s^{k+m}(t-s)^{l+n}}\leq\frac{S^{m+n}|u|}{s^{k+m}(t-s)^{l+n}}.

∎

So the order of the pole along the sides of

gives a natural increasing filtration on the space of local operators:

4.5.
Composition properties

As has been remarked in chapter
8
, the upset triangle is an idempotent:

\Delta(t,s)\star\Delta(t,s)=\Delta(t,s),

it is straightforward to compose almost complete morphisms:
if

are

-Kolmogorov spaces, we have a well
defined bilinear composition map

\displaystyle\circ:{\rm Hom}_{\Delta(t,s)}(E,F))\times{\rm Hom}_{\Delta(t,s)}(F,G))

\displaystyle\longrightarrow{\rm Hom}_{\Delta(t,s)}(E,G))

\displaystyle(u,v)

\displaystyle\mapsto v\circ u

The sub-spaces of
(k,l)
-local operators

{\mathcal{L}}^{k,l}(E,F)_{t,s}\subset Hom_{\Delta(t,s)}(E,F))

behave well under this composition:

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
E,F,G
be
S
-Kolmogorov spaces.
If
u\in{\mathcal{L}}^{k_{1},l_{1}}(E,F)
and
v\in{\mathcal{L}}^{k_{2},l_{2}}(F,G)
then

v\circ u\in{\mathcal{L}}^{k,l}(R,G)

with

k=k_{1}+k_{2},\ l=l_{1}+l_{2}
. Moreover, one has the norm estimate:

|v\circ u|\leq\frac{l^{l}}{l_{1}^{l_{1}}l_{2}^{l_{2}}}|v||u|.

Proof.

Denote by
\|-\|
the operator norm. For
s<s^{\prime}<t
we have

\|(v\circ u)_{st}\|\leq\|v_{ss^{\prime}}\|\,\|u_{s^{\prime}t}\|

From the definition of locality we have:

\displaystyle\|v_{ss^{\prime}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|v|}{s^{k_{2}}(s^{\prime}-s)^{l_{2}}},

\displaystyle\|u_{s^{\prime}t}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|u|}{(s^{\prime})^{k_{1}}(s^{\prime}-t)^{l_{1}}}<\frac{|u|}{s^{k_{1}}(s^{\prime}-t)^{l_{1}}}.

We take the point
s^{\prime}
such that

(s^{\prime}-s)=\frac{l_{2}}{l}(t-s),\ (t-s^{\prime})=\frac{l_{1}}{l}(t-s)

and find the estimate

\|v_{ss^{\prime}}\|\,\|u_{s^{\prime}t}\|\leq\frac{l^{l}}{l_{1}^{l_{1}}l_{2}^{l_{2}}}\frac{|v||u|}{s^{k}(t-s)^{l}}

thus

|v\circ u|\leq\frac{l^{l}}{l_{1}^{l_{1}}l_{2}^{l_{2}}}|v||u|

∎

We remark that this estimate is slightly better then the one obtained by taking
s^{\prime}=(s+t)/2
the midpoint,
from which one gets the simpler estimate
|v\circ u|\leq 2^{l}|v||u|
.

The form of the above estimate leads to the idea to
recalibrate
the norm on
{\mathcal{L}}^{k,l}(E,F)

and introduce

Definition 4.6
.

||u||:=\lambda_{k}|u|

where

\lambda_{k}:=\left(\frac{e}{k}\right)^{k}

This new norm now becomes
sub-multiplicative

Corollary 4.7
.

||v\circ u||\leq||v||\,||u||

For this to work one could replace

in the above definition of
\lambda_{k}

by any number

, but we will see later that

is a particular
convenient choice.

Corollary 4.8
.

If
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(E,E)
then

u_{1}\circ u_{2}\circ\ldots\circ u_{n}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{n}(E,E)

and we have the estimate:

||u_{1}\circ u_{2}\circ\ldots\circ u_{n}||\leq||u_{1}||||u_{2}||\ldots||u_{n}||,

or, in terms of the original norm:

|u_{1}\circ u_{2}\circ\ldots\circ u_{n}|\leq n^{n}|u_{1}||u_{2}|\ldots|u_{n}|.

Especially for

||u^{n}||\leq||u||^{n},\;\;\;|u^{n}|\leq n^{n}|u|^{n}.

This is of course also easily proven directly by subdividing the interval

in

In reading these formulas one should be aware that for reasons of simplicity
we always write

or

for the norms, but which norm this in fact is,

if

is 1-local, the norm in the expression

refers to the norm

4.6.
Partial differential operators and Huygens sets

The Cauchy-Nagumo estimate links the norm of functions on discs to the size of these discs.
In the proof we used the fact that the disc
D_{t}
of radius
t
is the union of the
disc
D_{s}
of radius
s<t
and discs of radius
t-s
centered at points
z\in D_{s}
.
This is reminiscent of
Huygens principle
in geometrical optics and in fact the
argument carries over to much greater generality.

Definition 4.9
.

An
S
-Huygens open set
U
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a collection of
strictly increasing relatively compact open sets

U_{s}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n},\ s\in]0,S]

such that for any

and any

the polydisc

is contained in
U_{t}
.

It is clear that there exist many
S
-Huygens sets. For example, beside polydiscs

,
the neighbourhoods of the real torus

U_{s}=\{z\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}:1-s<|z|<1+s\}\subset(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}

form a Huygens sets.

For any
S
-Huygens set

we have a corresponding
S
-Kolmogorov space
\mathcal{O}^{c}(U)
.
The Cauchy-Nagumo inequalities can be stated as follows:

Proposition 4.10
.

If
U
is an
S
-Huygens set then any differential operator

of order

defines
a

-local operator

That is, there exist a constant

4.7.
The Cartan-Weierstrass theorem

We have seen that the division operator with
z
is local. This is a particular case of a general result proved by Cartan.
First let us recall what division means in an arbitrary number of variables.

We decompose the space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
as the product
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{C}
with coordinates
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})
and
y
.

An analytic series
g\in\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}
of the form

g=y^{d}+a_{1}(x)y^{d-1}+\ldots+a_{d}(x)

with
a_{i}(0)=0

is called a
Weierstrass polynomial
of degree
d
.

The Weierstrass division theorem asserts that for any
f\in\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}
,
there exist unique
q,p\in\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}
such that

f=qg+p.

where
p
is a Weierstrass polynomial of degree less than
g
.

We will call the map

\delta_{g}:f\mapsto q

the
division operator
and

\rho_{g}:f\mapsto r

the
remainder operator
.
For instance, in the one variable case and
g:=y^{d}
, the division operator is

\delta_{g}:f\mapsto y^{-d}(f-\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}y^{k}).

Assume now that the Weierstrass polynomial
g

defines a holomorphic function on an open susbet
U\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
As such, the function
g
is a polynomial in
y
depending on
x
as parameters.
At
x=0
, it reduces to
y^{d}
and therefore has a zero of multiplicity
d
at the origin.

We consider open polydiscs
\Delta_{\rho}\subset U
and write the polyradius

\rho
as

\rho=(\rho^{\prime},r),\;\;\rho^{\prime}=(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\ldots\rho_{n-1})

so that

\Delta_{\rho}=\Delta_{\rho^{\prime}}\times\Delta_{r}.

The linear projection

\Delta_{\rho}\longrightarrow\Delta_{\rho^{\prime}}

fibres the polydisc
\Delta_{\rho}
by open discs. We say that the polydisc
\Delta_{\rho}\subset U
is
adapted

to
g
if the hypersurface
V(g)
does not intersect the boundary of the fibres

V(g)\cap\left(\Delta_{\rho^{\prime}}\times\partial\Delta_{r}\right)=\emptyset

The existence of such a fundamental system of
0
-neighbourhood is elementary: taking the modulus of the Weierstrass polynomial

(x,y)\mapsto|g(x,y)|

gives a continuous. For
x=0
, it does not vanish on the circles

x=0,\ |y|=r.

Thus taking
\rho^{\prime}
sufficiently guarantees that
V(g)
does not intersect
\Delta_{\rho^{\prime}}\times\partial\Delta_{r}
.
Moreover as the sum
N_{x}
of the roots of the polynomial
g(x,-)
counted with multiplicities is obtained
by integrating the logarithmitic derivative along
\partial\Delta_{r}
:

N_{x}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{-1}\pi}\int_{\partial\Delta_{r}}\frac{\partial_{y}g(x,y)}{g(x,y)}dy,

it is a continuous function which therefore remains constant equal to
d
as
x
varies inside
\Delta_{\rho^{\prime}}
.

We may now formulate the Cartan-Weierstrass theorem:

Theorem 4.11
.

Consider polydiscs
U_{t}:=\Delta_{\rho^{\prime}(t)}\times\Delta_{t}
adapted to a Weierstrass polynomial
g\in E:={\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)
and defining
a fundamental system of
0
-neighbourhoods.
The division operator
\delta_{g}\in{\rm Hom}(E,E)
induces a
(d,0)
-local operator i.e.
\delta_{g}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{d,0}(E,E)

and more precisely:

|\delta(g)|_{t}\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon(t)}|f|_{t}

where

\varepsilon(t):=\min\{|g(x,y)|:(x,y)\in\Delta_{\rho^{\prime}}\times\partial\Delta_{t}\}.

The second part is more precise since the equality

g(0,y)=y^{d}

and the fact that the polycylinders are adapted to
g

imply there existence of a constant
C>0
such that

\varepsilon(t)\geq Ct^{d}

Proof.

We assert that the function

q(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|\xi|=t}\frac{f(x,\xi)}{g(x,\xi)}\frac{d\xi}{(\xi-y)}

equals
\delta_{g}(f)
.

Define

p:=f-qg.

Using the Cauchy formula, we get that:

p=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|\xi|=t}\frac{f(x,\xi)}{g(x,\xi)}\frac{g(x,\xi)-g(x,y)}{\xi-y}d\xi

As
g
is a polynomial in
y
, the function

\frac{g(x,\xi)-g(x,y)}{\xi-y}

is a polynomial of degree less than
d
in the
y
-variable and therefore so is
p
. This proves
the assertion

q=\delta_{g}(f).

From the first integral formula, we deduce the estimate:

|q|_{t}\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\sup_{|y|=t}|f(x,y)|\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}|f|_{t}

as well as

|p|_{t}\leq\frac{|g|_{t}}{\varepsilon}|f|_{t}.

This proves the theorem.
∎

Cartan used the theorem in order to prove, the following fundamental result:

Theorem 4.12
.

Consider the ring
R={\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{C}^{d},0}

and a map of free
R
-modules:

A:R^{m}\longrightarrow R^{n}.

There exists a fundamental system
\Delta
of neighbourhoods of the origin, a number
k

and a map

B:R^{n}\longrightarrow R^{m}

such that
B\in{\mathcal{L}}^{k,0}(\left({\mathcal{O}}^{c}(\Delta)\right)^{n},\left({\mathcal{O}}^{c}(\Delta)\right)^{m})

satisfies

ABA=A.

A proof is given in the Appendix.

Just as derivations decrease the

Proposition 4.13

A

of Kolmogorov spaces maps the term

of the order filtration to

Bi-Lipschitz nature of locality:
As in the definition one uses
t-s
, it seems that the notion of locality depends critically on the linear structure of the interval

. It turns out that this is not really the case.

If
u:E\longrightarrow F
is a partial morphism between
S
-Kolmogorov spaces,
and
\phi:]0,T]\longrightarrow]0,S]
a order preserving map, we obtain a linear map

\phi^{*}u:\phi^{*}E\longrightarrow\phi^{*}F;\;(\phi^{*}u)_{t^{\prime}t}:=u_{\phi(t^{\prime}),\phi(t)}

Proposition 4.14
.

Assume that
\phi
has an inverse with Lipschitz constant

.
Then

u\in L^{k}(E,F)\implies\phi^{*}u\in L^{k}(\phi^{*}E,\phi^{*}F)

If

If

Chapter 5
Local equivalence

5.1.
Introduction

Associated to a relatively compact open subset
U\longrightarrow B
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
over
a base
B
, we considered various Kolmogorov spaces such as
{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(U)
,

{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(U)
, and
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)
.

In all these cases, differential operators define almost complete morphisms

P^{a}:{\mathcal{O}}^{a}(U)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{a}(U),\;\;\;f\mapsto Pf,\;\;\;a\in\{b,c,h\}.

Moreover, we proved that if
U
is a Huygens set, then the partial differential operator
P^{c}
is local. Now a natural question arises: are the corresponding
operators
P^{b}
and
P^{h}
also local?

To answer this question, one would like to compare these Kolmogorov spaces.
This will lead us to the notion of
local equivalence
.
If Kolmogorov spaces happen to be in correspondence via local maps, then
automatically any local operator for one Kolmogorov space will be local for
the other one. This means that in applications, in order to prove that a
certain map is local, we can choose the most adequate Kolmogorov space
structure. This flexibility is very useful, as we do not have to fix a norm
once and for all. We can play with different norms at the same time and
change structure sheaves if needed, which turns out to be a powerful tool.

5.2.
Local equivalence of structure sheaves
{\mathcal{O}}^{b}
and
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}

If
U\longrightarrow B
is a relatively compact open subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
over a base
B
,
then, as any continuous function over a compact set is bounded, there is
a natural complete morphism

I:{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(U).

Conversely, a function
f\in{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(U)_{t}
is by definition holomorphic in
U_{t}
. Therefore given any
s<t
, its restriction to
\overline{U}_{s}
is continuous and thus belongs to
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)_{s}
.
This shows that the natural maps

{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(U)_{t}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)_{s},\ f\mapsto f_{\mid U_{s}},\;\;\;s<t,

define an almost complete morphism

J\in{\rm Hom}_{\Delta}({\mathcal{O}}^{b}(U),{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)).

However there are no natural maps
{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(U)_{t}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)_{t}
and the
almost complete morphism
J
does not extend to a complete morphism over

\overline{\Delta}
.

The morphism
J
is nevertheless
0
-local since

|J(f)|_{s}=|f|_{s}\leq|f|_{t}

and it is pseudo-inverse to
I
:

I\circ J=\iota_{\Delta},\;\;\;J\circ I=\iota_{\Delta}.

5.3.
Local equivalence of structure sheaves
{\mathcal{O}}^{h}
and
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}

As any continuous function over a compact subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is
integrable, there is also a natural complete morphism

I:{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(U).

Conversely, a function
f\in{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(U)_{t}

is by definition holomorphic in
U_{t}
. Therefore given any

\overline{U}_{s}
is continuous and thus belongs to
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U_{s})
.
This shows that the natural maps

{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(U)_{t}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)_{s},\ f\mapsto f_{\mid U_{s}},\;\;\;s<t

define an almost complete morphism

J\in{\rm Hom}_{\Delta}({\mathcal{O}}^{h}(U),{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)).

which is pseudo-inverse to
I
:

I\circ J=\iota_{\Delta},\;\;\;J\circ I=\iota_{\Delta}.

Again there are no natural maps
{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(U)_{t}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)_{t}
and this
almost complete morphism
J
does not extend to a complete morphism over

\overline{\Delta}
. We will show that it is in fact local over Huygens sets.

Proposition 5.1
.

If
U\longrightarrow B=\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is a relatively compact Huygens subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, then the morphism
J
is
n
-local.

Proof.

Let
s<t
and consider
f\in{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(U)_{t}
. Then the Taylor expansion
of
f
at a point

reads:

f(z)=\sum_{J\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}a_{J}(z-w)^{J},\ a_{J}\in\mathbb{C},

where we use multi-index notation. As
U
is a Huygens set, the polydisc

D_{w}
centred at

with radius
\sigma=t-s
is contained in
U_{t}
. We then have

\int_{D_{w}}|f(z)|^{2}dV=\sum_{J\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}C(J)|a_{J}|^{2}\sigma^{2|J|+2n},\;\;C(I)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\pi}{j_{k}+1}.

So we obtain

C(0)|a_{0}|^{2}\sigma^{2n}\leq\int_{D_{w}}|f(z)|^{2}dV\leq\int_{U_{t}}|f(z)|^{2}dV=|f|_{t}^{2}.

This shows that

|f(w)|=|a_{0}|\leq\frac{c}{\sigma^{n}}\left(\int_{\Delta_{w}}|f(z)|^{2}dV\right)^{1/2}\leq\frac{c}{(t-s)^{n}}|f|_{t}

for any

.
Thus we see that

This proves the proposition.
∎

As a corollary we see that differential operators indeed are also local on

write the operator

as composition of local operators

.

The above results give rise to the following concept:

Definition 5.2
.

Two Kolmogorov spaces

So on Huygens sets
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(U)

are local equivalent Kolmogorov
spaces. This chain of ideas can be pushed much further. If
{\mathcal{F}}
is a coherent

\mathbb{C}^{n}

an

-Huygens set which is
{\mathcal{F}}
-open. There there are Kolmogorov spaces
{\mathcal{F}}^{c}(U)
and
{\mathcal{F}}^{h}(U)
.
There are similar local maps
I
and

which shows that these Kolmogorov spaces are local equivalent. Their construction is natural, compatible with restriction and morphism of coherent sheaves.
So one may say that
{\mathcal{O}}^{b}

5.4.
Local equivalence of
\ell^{p}(\lambda)
-spaces

We study the issue of local equivalence for Kolmogorov-sequence spaces introduced
above. The estimate

\sup_{i}|x_{i}|\leq\left(\sum_{i\geq 0}|x_{i}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}

shows that the injection

I:\ell^{p}(\lambda)\longrightarrow\ell^{\infty}(\lambda)

is an ordinary morphism of Kolmogorov spaces. On the other hand, for any
s<t
the inclusion

\ell^{\infty}(\lambda)_{t}\longrightarrow\ell^{p}(\lambda)_{s}

induces an almost complete morphism of Kolmogorov spaces.

Given two sequences of increasing positive functions
\lambda,\mu
define condition
(\Lambda_{p})
by:

\Lambda_{p}=\Lambda_{p}(\lambda,\mu):\ \ \exists C,\alpha>0,\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\mu_{i}(s)^{p}}{\lambda_{i}(t)^{p}}\leq\frac{C}{(t-s)^{\alpha}}.

Proposition 5.3
.

If the condition
(\Lambda_{p}(\lambda,\mu))
holds with constants
C
and
\alpha
,
then there is an almost complete morphism

J:\ell^{\infty}(\lambda)\longrightarrow\ell^{p}(\mu)

induced by the inclusions

\ell^{\infty}(\lambda)_{t}\subset\ell^{p}(\mu)_{s},\ t>s

which is
\alpha/p
-local with norm at most
C^{1/p}
.

Proof.

Take

x=(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{i},\dots)\in\ell^{\infty}(\lambda)_{t}

then

\displaystyle|J(x)|_{s}^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq 0}|x_{i}|^{p}\mu_{i}(s)^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq 0}|x_{i}|^{p}\lambda_{i}(t)^{p}\frac{\mu_{i}(s)^{p}}{\lambda_{i}(t)^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq|x|_{t}^{p}\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\mu_{i}(s)^{p}}{\lambda_{i}(t)^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{(t-s)^{\alpha}}|x|_{t}^{p}.

∎

As an example, consider the weight sequences
\lambda=\mu=(s^{i})
on
any interval
]0,S]
. These satisfies the condition
(\Lambda_{p}((s^{i}),(s^{i}))
.
Indeed:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\lambda_{i}(s)^{p}}{\lambda_{i}(t)^{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-(s/t)p}

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{p}}{t^{p}-s^{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{p}}{(t-s)(t^{p-1}+\dots+s^{p-1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{t}{(t-s)(1+\dots)}\leq\frac{S}{(t-s)}.

Therefore the
S
-Kolmogorov sequence space

is
local equivalent to
\ell^{\infty}((s^{i}))

5.5.
Local convolution operators

Using local equivalences, one may sometimes deduce locality for certain maps
and even sometimes prove locality simultaneously for different Kolmogorov
spaces. As an example relevant for KAM theory, we study the case of a

convolution operator
which is
caracteristic of this type of simultaneous proof.

Consider the unit polycylinder
D\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. As an analytic
function is determined by its power series expansion in one point, the maps

{\mathcal{O}}^{a}(D)_{t}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\{z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{C}[[z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n}]],\;\;a\in\{b,c,h\}

that associate to a function its power series expansion at
0
, is injective.
So we can think of the space
{\mathcal{O}}^{a}(D)_{t}
as a sub-space of the ring of formal
power series.

Recall that for two formal power series

f=\sum_{I\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}a_{I}z^{I},\;\;\;g=\sum_{I\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}b_{I}z^{I}\in\mathbb{C}[[z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n}]],

the
Hadamard-product
or
convolution

\star
is defined by taking the coefficientwise product:

f\star g=\sum_{I\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}a_{I}b_{I}z^{I}.

If the convolution with
f\in\mathbb{C}[[z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n}]]
preserves the sub-space
{\mathcal{O}}^{a}(D)
, it defines a
convolution operator

f\star:{\mathcal{O}}^{a}(D)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{a}(D),\ g\mapsto f\star g

Proposition 5.4
.

Consider a formal power series

f=\sum_{I\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}a_{I}z^{I}\in\mathbb{C}[[z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n}]].

If there exists an
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a_{I}=O(|I|^{k})

then the map

{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(D)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(D),\;\;\;\ g\mapsto f\star g

is local.

Proof.

Recall that the monomials
z^{I}
form an orthogonal basis in
{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(D_{s})
and

|z^{I}|_{s}^{2}=C(I)s^{2|I|+2n},\;\;\;C(I)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\pi}{|I_{k}|+1}

By Cauchy-Nagumo identities the differential operators

\nabla^{c}_{st}:{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)_{t}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)_{s},\;\;\;g\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}\partial_{z_{i}}g

define a local map. As
{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)
and
{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(D)
are local equivalent, the
corresponding operator

\nabla^{h}:{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(D)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(D),g\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}\partial_{z_{i}}g

is also local.

If
g=\sum_{I\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}b_{I}z^{I}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{h}(D_{s})
we find

|\left(\nabla^{h}\right)^{k}g|_{s}^{2}=\sum_{I\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}|I|^{k}|b_{I}|^{2}C(I)s^{2|I|+2}

with

|I|=\sum_{k=1}^{n}i_{k},\ I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{n}).

The assumption on the
a_{I}
’s shows that there exists a constant
K
such that:

|f\star|\leq K|\left(\nabla^{h}\right)^{k}|.

As
\nabla^{h}
is local this shows that
f\star
is also
{\mathcal{O}}^{h}
-local.

∎

Note that if
f\star
is
{\mathcal{O}}^{h}
-local, then by local equivalence, the
corresponding almost complete maps

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D),

\displaystyle\ g\mapsto f\star g

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{c}(D),

\displaystyle\ g\mapsto f\star g

are also local.

Chapter 6
Operator calculus in Kolmogorov spaces

Given a bounded linear endomorphism
u:E\longrightarrow E
of a Banach space and a
convergent power series
f\in\mathbb{C}\{z\}
, we may define

f(u):E\longrightarrow E,

provided that the norm of
u
is smaller than
R(f)
, the convergence radius
of the power series
f
. Moreover, if
f\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\{z\}
,
then one has the estimate

\|f(u)\|\leq f(\|u\|)

for the operator norms. We want to generalise these fundamental facts to the
more general context of Kolmogorov spaces.

6.1.
The problem

Most of the interesting linear operators are not bounded and the
theory of Banach space does not allow to take the exponentials of them.
As a simple example, let us look again at the derivative:

\frac{d}{dx}:f\mapsto f^{\prime}

As the exponential series is

e^{\frac{d}{dx}}=1+\frac{d}{dx}+\frac{1}{2!}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\frac{1}{3!}\frac{d^{3}}{dx^{3}}+\ldots

we see that it can not be defined in a sensible way on the Banach space

C^{k}([-1,1],\mathbb{R})
, as we have only
k
derivatives. It consists of unbounded operators and it is relatively unclear how to define the infinite sum of them.

In the Fréchet space
C^{\infty}([-1,1],\mathbb{R})
, the terms in the esponential
series are well-defined, but the situation is in a sense even worse.
The bounded subsets

B_{\varepsilon}=\{f:\sup_{j\leq i}\|f^{(j)}\|<\varepsilon_{i}\}

indexed by sequences
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{0},\dots,\varepsilon_{n},\dots)
form a fundamental system of bounded sets. (Here
\|\cdot\|
denotes the supremum norm over the interval
[-1,1]
). In particular, the derivative just shifts the sequence
\varepsilon
and is therefore a bounded map. But its exponential, which should be equal to the
transformation

f(x)\mapsto f(x+1)

is ill-defined: the image of
1/(2x-3)
would be
1/(2x-1)
which is of course not
C^{\infty}
since it is not even defined at
x=1/2
. So in some sense it is again unbounded, although all terms of the defining series are bounded
operators.

In fact in a Fréchet space, a simple map like

exp:C^{0}(]0,1],\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow C^{0}(]0,1],\mathbb{R}),f\mapsto e^{f}

is again pathological. The directional derivative at
f
of the function
exp
is the multiplication by
e^{f}
, it is therefore an isomorphism.
In the Banach space
C^{0}([0,1],\mathbb{R})
, one would conclude, by the
implicit function theorem, that the map is locally surjective. In the Fréchet space
C^{0}(]0,1],\mathbb{R})
,
the situation is very different: the image of
exp
does not contain any open subset!

This is due to fact that the topology on
C^{0}(]0,1],\mathbb{R})
is defined by the fundamental basis of
0
-neighbourhoods

V_{\varepsilon,\delta}=\{f:\sup_{x\in[\delta,1]}|f(x)|<\varepsilon\}.

In such a neighbourhood, we have no control
of the function in the interval
]0,\delta[
. In particular, it contains functions with negative
values inside
]0,\delta[
.

As the exponential
e^{f}
is a positive function,
this means that, although the derivative is an isomorphism, the image does not contain any open subset. We will now see that the situation for Kolmogorov spaces is much simpler. For instance, the exponential of a
1-local map is, in general, well-defined and this provides a direct generalisation of the flow of a vector field.

6.2.
The Borel map

As mentioned above, if
f
is a convergent power series and
r<R(f)
,
the radius of convergence of
f
, then for any Banach space
E
, there
is a map

rB_{E}\longrightarrow Hom(E,E),\ u\mapsto f(u),

where
rB_{E}\subset E
denotes the ball of radius
r
in
E
.

For Kolmogorov spaces there is similar map, which however is
richer from a geometrical viewpoint.

Recall that the
Borel transform
of a formal power series is defined by

B:\mathbb{C}[[z]]\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}[[z]],\ \sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}\mapsto\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{a_{n}}{n!}z^{n}.

If
f
is a convergent power series,
f\in\mathbb{C}\{z\}
, then
Bf
is not only convergent, but
defines even an entire function on the complex plane. For example for the series

f(z)=\frac{1}{1-z}=1+z+\ldots+z^{n}+\ldots

has

Bf(z)=e^{z}=1+\frac{1}{1}z+\frac{1}{2!}z^{2}+\ldots+\frac{1}{n!}z^{n}+\ldots

as its Borel transform. As the Borel transform improves convergence of a series drastically,
it is very popular in the theory of resummation of divergent series, but this issue is not of
importance here.

There is a simple result in the theory of Kolmogorov spaces that allows to define for any
1
-local operator
u\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(E,E)
a morphism
{\mathcal{B}}f(u)\in{\mathcal{H}}om(E,E)
as the limit of an infinite sequence.
Via this map the upset of a point is mapped to a definition domain
A(u)
and therefore to
u
is associated also a partial morphism

Bf(u)\in{\rm Hom}_{A(u)}(E,E).

Definition 6.1

The tetrahedron
1

g\in{\rm Hom}_{A}(E,E)
where

A:=\{(s,t)\in]0,\tau]^{2}\;|\;s<\prod_{i\geq 0}(1-\alpha_{i})t\}.

Furthermore, one has the estimate for the norm function

|g|<\frac{1}{1-\sigma\nu},

with
\nu:=t/(t-s).

Proof.

Given an estimate of the form

\|u_{i}\|_{t}\leq\alpha_{i}\,t

the definition set
A(u_{i})
contains the simplex

A_{i}:=\{(s,t)\in]0,\tau]\times]0,\tau]\;|\;s<(1-\alpha_{i})t\}

and on
A
one has

|e^{u_{i}}|\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{i}\nu}

with
\nu=t/(t-s)
.

Let us now consider the composition of two exponentials
e^{u_{i}}e^{u_{i+1}}
, assuming that

\|u_{i}\|_{t}\leq\alpha_{i}

As

\frac{1}{1-x}\times\frac{1}{1-y}<\frac{1}{1-(x+y)}

for
x,y\in]0,1[
, we get that the composition
e^{u_{i+1}}e^{u_{i}}
defines a morphism over

A_{i+1}\star A_{i}=\{(s,t)\in]0,\tau]\times]0,\tau]\;|\;s<(1-\alpha_{i})(1-\alpha_{i+1})t\}

and one has the estimate

|e^{u_{i+1}}e^{u_{i}}|\leq\frac{1}{1-(\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{i+1})\nu}

By a straighforward induction, we get the estimate

|e^{u_{n}}e^{u_{n-1}}\cdots e^{u_{0}}|\leq\frac{1}{1-(\sum_{i=0}^{n}\alpha_{i})\nu}

over the set

A_{n}\star\cdots\star A_{0}=\{(s,t)\in]0,\tau]^{2}\;|\;s<\prod_{i=0}^{n}(1-\alpha_{i})t\}\supset A.

∎

Note that the sequence
e^{-u_{0}}e^{-u_{1}}\cdots e^{-u_{n}}
converges to a pseudo inverse
h
of
g
:

hg=gh=\iota_{E}\in{\rm Hom}_{A\star A}(E,E).

6.6.
Formulation with sequences

We will later use the following variant of Theorem
6.4
on the composition of exponentials, now considered as a sequence of elements of

L^{1}(E,E)
.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
E
be an
S
-Kolmogorov space and
(t:=t_{0},t_{1},t_{2},\ldots)

decreasing sequence converging to
s>0
. For any sequence

(t_{n},u_{n})\in L^{1}(E,E)
such that

i)

\|u_{n}\|\leq t_{n}-t_{n+1}

ii)

\sigma:=\sum_{n\geq 0}||u_{n}||/(t_{n}-t_{n+1})<+\infty

the sequence

g_{0}=e^{u_{0}},\;\;g_{1}=e^{u_{1}}e^{u_{0}},

g_{n}:=e^{u_{n}}e^{u_{n-1}}\cdots e^{u_{0}}

converges to an element
g\in{\rm Hom}(E_{t_{0}},E_{s})
.
Furthermore, we have the estimate:

|g|<\frac{1}{1-\sigma/(t-s)}.

Proof.

We have seen that
e^{u_{i}}
exists as a section and thus defines elements
of the Banach space
Hom(E_{t_{i}},E_{t_{i+1}})
as long as
\|u_{i}\|\leq t_{i}-t_{i+1}
,
which holds by the first assumption. As a consequence
the compositions

e^{u_{0}},\;\;e^{u_{1}}e^{u_{0}},\;\;\ldots,e^{u_{n}}\ldots e^{u_{1}}e^{u_{0}},\ldots

are well defined. Furthermore, the Borel estimate gives

|e^{u_{i}}|\leq\frac{1}{1-\nu_{i}},\ \nu_{i}:=\|u_{i}\|/(t_{i}-t_{i+1}).

As

\frac{1}{1-x}\times\frac{1}{1-y}<\frac{1}{1-(x+y)}

for
x,y\in]0,1[
, we get for the composition
e^{u_{i+1}}e^{u_{i}}

|e^{u_{i+1}}e^{u_{i}}|\leq\frac{1}{1-(\nu_{i}+\nu_{i+1})}.

By a straighforward induction (and the fact that restrictions have norm

\leq 1
), we obtain the estimate

|g_{n}|\leq\frac{1}{1-(\sum_{i=0}^{n}\nu_{i})}.

Therefore

|g_{n+1}-g_{n}|\leq\frac{|e^{u_{n+1}}-{\rm Id\,}|}{1-(\sum_{i=0}^{n}\nu_{i})}

Using again the Borel estimate

|e^{u_{n+1}}-1|\leq\frac{\nu_{n+1}}{1-\nu_{n+1}}

we get

|g_{n+1}-g_{n}|\leq\frac{\nu_{n+1}}{1-(\sum_{i=0}^{n+1}\nu_{i})}

From this it follows that the sequence

converges in the Banach
space
Hom(E_{t},E_{s})
with operator norm.
∎

Chapter 7
The fixed point theorem

In Part 1, we saw that Kolmogorov’s invariant torus theorem is a result on
normal forms, which can be studied in the more general framework of group
actions in infinite dimensional spaces. One could try to develop implicit
function theorems to mimic the finite dimensional case. This approach however
leads to inextricable difficulties. For this reason we developed the
analysis of Kolmogorov spaces, which allows us to use a simpler method.

We study the Lie-iteration directly in appropriate Kolmogorov spaces and
develop a
fixed point theory
in Kolmogorov spaces. The resulting

fixed point theorems
are a little bit like to one for Banach spaces, but slightly more involved.

In the next chapter we will then apply the fixed point theorems directly to the
Lie-iteration, which leads the general normal form theorems. We first focus on fixed
point theory.

7.1.
The Banach fixed point theorem

The fixed point theorem for a complete metric space has been popular
since its formulation by Banach in
1922
. It belongs to the most basic
results in functional analysis and has many applications.

A
contraction with factor
C
in a complete metric
space
(X,d)
is map
f:X\longrightarrow X
with the property that

d(f(x),f(y))\leq Cd(x,y).

If
C<1
, then starting from any point
x_{0}\in X
, we obtain by iteration
a sequence

x_{1}=f(x_{0}),\;\;x_{2}=f(x_{1}),\ldots,x_{n+1}=f(x_{n})

that by the above inequality is seen to be a Cauchy sequence, whose limit
is the unique fixed point
x_{\infty}
.
Clearly,

d(x_{n},x_{\infty})\leq C^{n}d(x_{0},x_{\infty})

The simplicity of the proof of this fixed point theorem stems in part from the
fact the a priori infinite dimensional iteration

x_{n+1}=f(x_{n}),\;\;x_{n}\in X

is ’governed’ by the very simple one-dimensional dynamical system

y_{n+1}=Cy_{n},\ y_{n}=\|x_{n}\|\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}.

We will formulate a similar iteration scheme in the context of
relative Banach spaces. There are two important differences: first, we
will need the fixed point to be
critical
, in order to overcome
the ’detoriation of the estimate’, which in the applications we have in
mind are caused by the appearence of small denominators and the shrinking
of domains of definition and more generally by the presence of singularities.
Second, the iteration is no longer ’governed’ by
a one-dimensional dynamical system. Rather we have to deal with iterations
in two or three dimensions, which fortunately are almost as simple as the
above one-dimensional system.

7.2.
Convergence in relative Banach spaces

Although our main concern are Kolmogorov spaces, the maps we wish to iterate are
usually only mappings of relative Banach space mappings.
We have seen in
1.8
that a Kolmogorov space carries a canonical
topology. For a relative Banach space

there is no obvious sense in which a sequence of elements can be said to converge.
If the base
B
has a topological structure, we may nevertheless define convergence
to zero, by mimicking the definition from Kolmogorov spaces. So from now on and for
the rest of this chapter, we assume that the base

a topology.

Definition 7.1

A sequence of elements

7.4.
Critical fixed points

For relative Banach spaces there is a version of the contraction theorem
in the case of
quadratic critical points
. This was an essential
discovery of Kolmogorov: the quadratic nature of an iteration compensates
the presence of small denominators. In order to appreciate this point,
let us first consider the prototype of a linear map in a Banach space:

\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},\;\;\;x\mapsto Cx,

which is a contraction when the multiplicator
C<1
.
In a relative Banach space over a base
B
, the constant
C=C(b)

will depend on
b\in B
, which during the iteration is changing.
So it may happen that for some
b
the multiplicator
C(b)\geq 1

and we loose control over the convergence.
Typically, we are dealing with local maps over the open sub-diagonal

\Delta=\{t>s>0\}
of the form

and during the iteration
s
or
t-s
can become small. So that
the multiplicator will become arbitrary large, complicating the issue
of convergence. It is unclear how to develop fixed point theory for such maps.

However, if we follow Kolmogorov and iterate maps of the form

x\mapsto\frac{C}{s^{k}(t-s)^{l}}x^{2},

then the extreme rapidity of convergence of the iterates of the
quadratic map
x\mapsto x^{2}
can compensate for the polar behaviour of
the coefficient

\frac{C}{s^{k}(t-s)^{l}}.

In the iteration process we are about to describe, the points
t
and

will approach each other according to a geometrical sequence, hence the
polar blow-up is easily compensated by the quadraticity of the map.
Of course, the same works more generally for maps that behave like

There is an important subtlety to be observed here: during the iteration,
the parameters

converge to a point on the diagonal

which the map is not defined. The fixed point lies in the
closure

of the domain of definition of the map we iterate, so we are not dealing
with a fixed point in the traditional sense of the word.

Chapter 8
The normal form theorem

In this chapter we will give a prove of a general normal form theorem. It
is based on the Lie-iteration of chapter 5, but we now let the iteration
take place in a Kolmogorov space. Using the results of the previous chapters,
we will see that a single simple condition ensures convergence of the process.

8.1.
Anatomy of the Morse lemma

Before we formulate the general statements, we look in some detail at the
complex one-variable
Morse lemma
in the KAM context. It states

f_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{2}z^{2}+o(z^{2})

there exists an biholomorphic germ

\varphi:(\mathbb{C},0)\longrightarrow(\mathbb{C},0)

such that

f_{0}\circ\varphi(z)=\frac{1}{2}z^{2}.

The result is of course straightforward, since

f_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\left(z\sqrt{1+o(1)}\right)^{2}.

It is nevertheless instructive to see how this basic result
fits inside the general theory of normal forms.
We take the particular example

f_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{2}z^{2}+z^{3}.

and want to use the Lie-iteration to bring this function back to
its normal form

a(z):=\frac{1}{2}z^{2}.

Lie iteration on power series

We begin by studying the Lie-iteration on the space
R=\mathbb{C}[[z]]
of formal
power series and define the ingredients for the Lie-iteration.
We let

\mathfrak{g}:=Der(R,R)=R\partial_{z}

the
R
-module of derivations.

There is an infinitesimal action of
\mathfrak{g}
on
R
, given by

Der(R,R)\times R\longrightarrow R,\;\;(v,f)\mapsto v(f),

and the action on the normal form
a
is simply multiplication by
z
:

\rho:Der(R,R)\longrightarrow R,\ v(z)\partial_{z}\mapsto v(z)\partial_{z}a(z)=zv(z).

As the map
\rho
is not surjective, there does not exist a right inverse
to it, but the map

j:R\longrightarrow{\rm Der\,}(R,R),\ b(z)\mapsto\frac{1}{z}(b(z)-b(0))\partial_{z}

is a right inverse to
\rho
, when restricted to the maximal ideal

{\mathcal{M}}=\left\{\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{n}z^{n}:b_{0}=0\right\}.

This means that

\rho\circ j_{|{\mathcal{M}}}=Id_{|{\mathcal{M}}}.

As we will always apply the map
j
to series with
b(0)=0
, the last term
in the formula could be omitted.

The Lie iteration produces a sequence of power series

f_{0}:=f=a+b_{0},\;\;f_{1}=a+b_{1},\;\;f_{2}=a+b_{2},\ldots

where the remainders
b_{n}
determine elements
v_{n}\in Der(R,R)
via the
formula

v_{n}:=j(b_{n}),

which in turn determine the next term of the sequence

f_{1}=e^{-v_{0}}(f_{0}),\;\;f_{2}=e^{-v_{1}}(f_{1}),\;\;\;\ldots,f_{n+1}=e^{-v_{n}}(f_{n}),\ldots

Written in terms of the remainders, we get:

b_{n+1}=e^{-v_{n}}(a+b_{n})-a,\ a=\frac{z^{2}}{2}

and in terms of the derivations:

v_{n+1}=j(e^{-v_{n}}(a+v_{n}(a))-a).

So the Lie iteration produces a sequence

v_{0},\;\;v_{1},\;\;v_{2},\ldots,v_{n},\ldots

of elements in
Der(R,R)
by the recursion:

\begin{array}[]{| c|}\hline\cr\\
\qquad v_{n+1}=j(e^{-v_{n}}({\rm Id\,}+v_{n})(z^{2}/2)-z^{2}/2)\\
\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

At the first step, we get .

f_{0}=\frac{z^{2}}{2}+z^{3},\;\;b_{0}=z^{3},\;\;v_{0}=j(z^{3})=z^{2}\partial_{z}.

The first terms of the sequence are seen to be:

\displaystyle v_{0}

\displaystyle=z^{2}\partial_{z}

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(-3z^{3}+8z^{4}-15z^{5}+24z^{6}-35z^{7}+48z^{8}-63z^{9}+o(z^{9}))\partial_{z}

\displaystyle v_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(-18z^{5}+108z^{6}-493z^{7}+2100z^{8}-8579z^{9}+o(z^{9}))\partial_{z}

\displaystyle v_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(-243z^{9}+o(z^{9}))\partial_{z}

\displaystyle v_{4}

Homogeneous normal forms

The computations and argument we used for the Morse lemma can be done in

Definition 8.1

Theorem 8.3

The map
g

1)

2)

We apply this in the context of Kolmogorov spaces.
As before in

8.4.
A heuristic for optimising the parameter choice

The iteration scheme we used depends on the parameters

r,\lambda,\alpha
and the estimate for the final domain of
convergence depends crucially on these choices.

Let us return to the calculations on the Morse lemma. We saw that
with the choice

C=2t_{0}^{2},\;\;r=\frac{1}{2},\;\;\lambda=\frac{1}{4},\;\;\;\mu=\frac{1}{2},

we found convergence for the starting value

t_{0}<\frac{3}{256\,e}=0.004311087202\ldots

and thus we obtain
t_{\infty}=t_{0}/3<0.001437029067\ldots
as radius of
convergence for the coordinate transformation.
It is this value that should be compared to the ’true’ radius of
convergence of the series
\psi(z)
, inverse to
z\sqrt{1+2z}
, which is
computed to be

\frac{\sqrt{3}}{9}=0.1924500898\ldots,

which is about
134
times larger.

If we keep our choices for
C
and
r
, the best choices for
\lambda
and

\mu
are determined by finding the maximum of the rational function

F(\mu,\lambda):=\frac{(1+\lambda-\lambda/\mu)\lambda^{2}(1-\mu)^{2}}{2\mu}\cdot\frac{\mu-\lambda}{(1-\lambda)}

that computes

.
The level curves of

suggest there is a single maximum in the region

:

(
x=\lambda
,

A direct calculation shows that a
maximum is obtained for

\lambda=8\mu^{2}+2\mu-4=0.448612476...

where

\mu=0.6311094891...

is a root of the cubic equation
8\mu^{3}-4\mu^{2}-7\mu+4=0
, which
leads to a slightly larger value

t_{\infty}=0.001949102953\ldots

for the radius of convergence.

However, we can also play with the parameter

. In general we have for

which using
\mu
can be written as:

\displaystyle e\,t_{0}\leq

\displaystyle r(1-\mu)/\mu^{2}

\displaystyle e\,t_{0}\leq

\displaystyle 2(1-r)^{2}\rho(t_{0},s_{0})\lambda^{2}(1-\mu)^{2}/\mu.

With the choice
r=1/2
we have seen that the second inequalities is much
stronger then the first.

Heuristic.

As we try to maximise the minimum of the two above
quantities, the maximum is realised when both inequalities coincide.

This heuristic is supported by numerical calculations.

Equating the right hand sides leads to a condition for
r
, depending
on
\lambda
and
\mu
gives:

\frac{r}{(1-r)^{2}}=2\rho\lambda^{2}(1-\mu)\mu=:\nu(\lambda,\mu).

We solve
r
as function of
\mu
and
\lambda
and find:

r(\lambda,\mu)=\frac{1+2\mu-\sqrt{1+4\mu}}{2\mu}

The level sets of the function

r(\lambda,\mu)\frac{(1-\mu)}{\mu^{2}}\frac{(\mu-\lambda)}{(1-\lambda)}(=et_{\infty})

look very much like what we had before.

(
x=\lambda
,
y=\mu
.)

It attains it maximum value

e\,t_{\infty}=0.01883436563\ldots,\;\;\;t_{\infty}=0.006928775903\ldots

for the values

\lambda=0.4145716992\ldots,\;\;\;\;\mu=0.6054472202\dots

which turn out to be rather complicated algebraic numbers of degree
14
.
We remark that the true radius of convergence is bigger by a factor

Q:=0.00692877/0.192450=27.7754...

Finally, we can apply the same method to the function

\frac{1}{2}z^{2}+\beta z^{n},\;\;\;\beta>0,n\geq 3.

In this case we find

\|u_{0}\|\leq e\beta s_{0}^{n-1}

and the two inequalities reduce to

\begin{array}[]{lrcl}i)&e\beta t^{n-2}&\leq&\frac{r(1-\mu)}{\mu^{n-1}}\\
ii)&e\beta t^{n-2}&<&2(1-r)^{2}\frac{\rho\lambda^{2}(1-\mu)^{2}}{\mu^{n-2}}\\
\end{array}

If we equate these bound, we obtain exactly the same value for
r
:

r(\lambda,\mu)=\frac{1+2\nu-\sqrt{1+4\nu}}{2\nu},\;\;\nu:=2\rho\lambda^{2}\mu(1-\mu),

and hence the following expression for the critical value for
t_{\infty}
:

t_{\infty}:=\Big(\frac{r(\lambda,\mu)(1-\mu)}{e\beta\mu}\Big)^{1/(n-2)}\frac{\mu-\lambda}{\mu(1-\lambda)}

This we have to compare with the radius of convergence of the coordinate
transformation
inverse
to

z\mapsto z\sqrt{1+2\beta z^{n-2}},

which is computed to be

R(n,\beta):=\Big(\frac{1}{n\beta}\Big)^{1/(n-2)}\sqrt{1-\frac{2}{n}}.

The ratio between these quantities is

Q:=\frac{R(n,\beta)}{T_{\infty}(n,\lambda,\mu)}=\frac{\mu-\lambda}{\mu(1-\lambda)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2/n}}\Big(\frac{r(1-\mu)n}{e\mu}\Big)^{1/(n-2)}

For each values of
n
one has the possibility of choosing appropriate
values
\lambda,\mu
, to make this
Q
as small as possible.

Below we summarise
the result of some numerical computations:

\begin{array}[]{|c||c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr n&3&4&5&6&7&8&9&10&20&50\\
\hline\cr\lambda&0.414&0.367&0.338&0.319&0.301&0.287&0.279&0.259&0.211&0.154\\
\mu&0.605&0.635&0.655&0.676&0.687&0.699&0.713&0.719&0.769&0.847\\
Q&27.775&5.439&3.153&2.397&2.033&1.820&1.682&1.584&1.249&1.099\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

The optimal choices of
\lambda
,
\mu
and
Q
appear to depend
monotonically on
n
. As one sees from the data and the explicit formula,
for
n\longrightarrow\infty
the quantity
Q
tends to
1
.
So our method is asymptotically optimal, despite the fact that all estimates
we used were rather crude. However it is probably,
that more refined methods lead to slightly better estimates for the radius
of convergence.

Bibliographical notes on part II

The program of general normal form theory and group actions was considered in:

J. Moser
,
A rapidly convergent iteration method and non-linear partial differential equations II
, Ann. Scuola Norm Sup. Pisa, 20:3, 1966, 499-535.

The ideas of Moser led to the important works on implicit function theorems:

R.S Hamilton
,
The inverse function theorem of Nash and Moser
, Bull. Amer. Math. Soc., 7:1,1982, 65–222.

F. Sergeraert
,
Un théorème de fonctions implicites sur certains espaces de Fréchet et quelques applications
, Ann. Sci. École Norm. Sup., 1972, 5:4, p. 599–660.

E. Zehnder
,
Generalized implicit function theorems with applications to some small divisor problems I
, Communications Pure Applied Mathematics, 28, 1975, p. 91–140.

J. Féjoz discovered an elementary version of Zehnders first implicit function theorem. The result was published later in:

J. Féjoz
,
Introduction to KAM theory, with a view to celestial mechanics in Variational methods in imaging and geometric control
, Radon Series on Comput. and Applied Math. 18, de Gruyter, 2016.

However implicit function theorems require a global linear inverse (or quasi-inverse) which
are seldom available, for instance in the presence of resonances.

An abstract theorem on infinite group dimensional group actions avoiding implicit
function theorems was given in:

J. Féjoz et M. Garay
,
Un théorème sur les actions de groupes de dimension
infinie
, Comptes Rendus à l’Académie des Sciences
348
, 7-8, 427 - 430, 2010.

Besides the problem of global inverse, it is clear now that the theorems could not be
pushed further, because they presuppose locality for operators which are in general not local
(see for instance Assumption H1 in Zehnder’s paper). Therefore, applications of implicit
function theorems require to parametrise the infinite dimensional groups in a non-standard
way such as
{\rm Id\,}+v
for diffeomorphisms, generating functions for symplectomorphisms, etc.

This explains that direct methods using power series turned out to be more powerful
and sometimes simpler compared to the abstract approach, see for instance:

Rüssmann, H.
,
Invariant tori in non-degenerate nearly integrable Hamiltonian systems
, Regular and chaotic dynamics, 2001, 6:2, p. 119-204.

Abstract KAM theory, including the theory of Kolmogorov spaces, was founded –although not in definitive form– in:

M. Garay
,
An Abstract KAM Theorem
, Moscow Math. Journal, 14:4 p.745-772, 2014.

M. Garay
,
Degenerations of invariant Lagrangian manifolds
, Journal of Singularities, 8, p.50-67, 2014.

It is only recently that the authors of this book discovered the general aspects of functorial analysis, came across the general definition of Kolmogorov spaces and discovered finite dimensional reduction using the norm map.
