Closing lemma and KAM normal form
Jinxin Xue

Abstract
. In this paper, we develop an approach to the problem of closing lemma based on KAM normal form. The new approach differs from existing C 1 C^{1} perturbation approach and spectral approach, and can handle the high regularity, high dimensional cases and even Riemannian metric perturbations. Moreover, the proof is constructive and effective. We apply the method to the original nearly integrable setting of Poincaré and confirm several old and new conjectures with weak formulations. First, for Poincaré’s original setting of nearly integrable systems, we prove that for typical perturbations, periodic orbits are asymptotically dense as the size of perturbation tends to zero. Second, we prove that typical smooth perturbation of the geodesic flow on the flat torus has asymptotically dense periodic orbits, which partially solves an open problem since Pugh-Robinson’s C 1 C^{1} -closing lemma. Third, we prove that for typical Hamiltonian or contact perturbation of the geodesic flows of the ellipsoid has asymptotically dense orbit on the energy level, which enhances the recent researches on strong closing lemma, and also confirms partially a conjecture of Fish-Hofer in this setting. We also discuss the relation of our models to the recent researches on many-body localization in physics.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study the problem of closing lemma in the original setting of Poincaré. In his work on the
N
-body problem, Poincaré intensively searched for periodic orbits. He explained his reasons as follows (c.f. Section 36 of Chapter III of
[
P
]
):

Here is a fact that I could not prove rigorously, but which nevertheless seems very likely to me. Given equations of the form defined in Section 13 and an arbitrary solution of these equations, one can always find a periodic solution
(
with a period which, admitedly, may be very long
)
, such that the difference between the two solutions is arbitrarily small. In fact, what makes these solutions so precious to us, is that they are, so to say, the only opening through which we can try to enter a place which, hitherto, was deemed inaccessible.

Here the equations under consideration are the canonical equations of nearly integrable Hamiltonian of the form

(1)

H_{\varepsilon}(I,\theta)=h(I)+\varepsilon f(I,\theta),\quad(I,\theta)\in T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{N}

used to model the
N
-body problem. The last paragraph is the origin of the problem of closing lemma.

1.1.
The torus case and KAM theory

The modern formulation of the conjecture of closing lemma states that
a nonwandering point can be closed into a periodic point by an arbitrarily small perturbation of the system
, where a nonwandering point is a point which does not have a neighborhood disjoint from its images under iterates. This, combined with Poincaré recurrence theorem, would imply the denseness of periodic orbits. With this formulation, it was proved by Pugh
[
P1
]
that closing lemma holds for
C^{1}
generic diffeomorphisms, and later with Robinson
[
PR
]
for
C^{2}
generic Hamiltonian systems. We refer readers to an elegant review
[
P2
]
by Pugh for some historical remarks. However, this approach is only local, and it is known that it fails for function spaces with higher regularity.

The work of Pugh and Robinson left open the following problem (c.f. Section 10 of
[
PR
]
)

Problem:

Prove the closing lemma for Riemannian metric perturbation of geodesic flows.

The difficulty of the problem is that Riemannian metric perturbations cannot be local, since a perturbation on the base manifold will simultaneously perturb the tangent bundle.
In
[
Ri
]
, Rifford proved closing lemma of geodesic flows for
C^{1}
generic Riemannian metrics. Note that the regularity is lower than the
C^{2}
Hamiltonian perturbations of
[
PR
]
.

Our first result proves a version of
C^{r}
closing lemma for typical Riemannian metric perturbation of the flat torus
\mathbb{T}^{N}
for any
r
large. We start with a formulation of the typicality.

We consider Riemannian metric
ds^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}d\theta_{i}^{2}
on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{N}
and its perturbation
ds^{2}_{\varepsilon A}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}(\delta_{ij}+\varepsilon a_{ij}(\theta))d\theta_{i}d\theta_{j}.
Let us denote by
\mathrm{Sym}^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{N})
the space of
N\times N
symmetric matrices each of whose entries is in
C^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{N})
. For
A:=(a_{ij})\in\mathrm{Sym}^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{N})
, we introduce its norm as
\|A\|:=\sum_{i\leq j}\|a_{ij}\|_{C^{r}}
. We also denote by
\mathcal{S}^{r}
the unit sphere in
\mathrm{Sym}^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{N})
.

Definition 1.1
.

We say that a sequence of sets
\mathcal{R}_{n}\subset\mathcal{S}^{r}
is
asymptotically residual
, if there is a sequence
c_{n}\to 0+
and a sequence of first category set
\mathcal{N}_{n}\subset\mathcal{S}^{r}
such that we have
\mathcal{R}_{n}\cup B^{2}_{c_{n}}(\mathcal{N}_{n})=\mathcal{S}^{r}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
where
B^{2}_{c_{n}}(\mathcal{N}_{n})
means the union of balls of radius
c_{n}
in the
C^{2}
-norm centered on
\mathcal{N}_{n}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists
r_{0}>0
such that for all
r>r_{0},
the following holds.
There is a sequence of asymptotically residual set
\mathcal{R}_{n}\subset\mathcal{S}^{r}
and a sequence of
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
, such that for any
\delta>0
, there exists
n_{\delta}
such that for any
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{n_{\delta}}
, any
(a_{ij})\in\mathcal{R}_{n_{\delta}}
and any ball
B_{\delta}
of radius
\delta
in the unit tangent bundle, the Riemannian metric
ds^{2}_{\varepsilon A}
has a closed geodesic intersecting
B_{\delta}.

As we will see in the proof, we can make the dependence of
\varepsilon_{n_{\delta}}
on
\delta
explicit, for instance
\varepsilon_{n_{\delta}}=\delta^{10}
, thus the proof is effective.

We illustrate the idea of the asymptotically residual set using an example. It is known that Morse functions (functions all whose critical points are nondegenerate) are generic in
C^{r}(M)
,
r\geq 2
, where
M
is a compact manifold. For a sequence
\mathbf{c}=\{c_{n}\}
with
c_{n}\to 0+
, we introduce
\mathcal{N}_{n}
to be the set of functions that are not Morse, i.e. some critical points are degenerate and
\mathcal{R}_{n}
to be the set of
C^{r}
functions such that for all
f\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
, we have the absolute values of the eigenvalues of
\mathrm{Hess}f
at each critical point are bounded away from
0
by
c_{n}^{2}
. A function on the boundary of
\mathcal{R}_{n}
can be perturbed into one with degenerate critical point, by a perturbation of
C^{2}
-norm at most
c_{n}
supported in an
O(1)
-neighborhood of a critical point.

The method of the proof is the normal form theory in Hamiltonian dynamics. Though our method is perturbative, it differs drastically from the approach of closing a nonwandering point. Indeed, our approach is to show that certain periodic orbits in the unperturbed system persist in the perturbed one. Generalizing the proof of the last theorem, we get the following version of closing lemma in Poincaré’s original setting.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded and open subset and
\bar{U}
its closure. We consider
C^{r}(\bar{U}\times\mathbb{T}^{N})
the space
C^{r}
functions on
\bar{U}\times\mathbb{T}^{N}
. Denote by
\mathcal{S}^{r}(\bar{U}\times\mathbb{T}^{N})\subset C^{r}(\bar{U}\times\mathbb{T}^{N})
its unit sphere consisting of functions with
\|f\|_{C^{r}}=1
. Similarly, we define
C^{r}(\bar{U})
and
\mathcal{S}^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{N})
.

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists
r_{0}>0
such that for all
r>r_{0},
the following holds. There is a residual set
\mathcal{R}\subset C^{r}(\bar{U})
, such that for each
h\in\mathcal{R}
, there exists a sequence of asymptotically residual set
\mathcal{R}_{n}\subset\mathcal{S}^{r}(\bar{U}\times\mathbb{T}^{N})

(
or
\mathcal{S}^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{N}))
and a sequence of
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
, such that for any
\delta>0
, there exists
n_{\delta}
such that for any
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{n_{\delta}}
, any
f\in\mathcal{R}_{n_{\delta}}
and any ball
B_{\delta}
of radius
\delta
in
T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{N}
centered on
U\cap h^{-1}(1)
, the Hamiltonian system (
1
) has a periodic orbit intersecting
B_{\delta}.

Here the first residual set
\mathcal{R}
is chosen to guarantee that
h\in\mathcal{R}
has nondegenerate Hessian for almost every point in
h^{-1}(1)
, i.e. the classical Kolmorogov’s nondegeneracy is satisfied almost everywhere. It is standard to assume certain nondegeneracy conditions in both KAM theory (
[
K
,
A
,
M
]
) and Nekhoroshev Theorem (
[
N
,
L
]
). The degenerate cases are also studied in literature with in particular the prototypical unperturbed Hamiltonian
h(I)=\omega\cdot I
where
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, mostly with Diophantine condition on
\omega
. If
I
is small, the relevant problem is to study the stability of elliptic fixed point. It is known that there is a positive measure set of Lagrangian invariant tori accumulating to the elliptic fixed point in the analytic category
[
EFK2
]
and that the elliptic fixed point may be isolated in the smooth category
[
FS
]
. If
I
is not small, then the relevant problem is the stability of KAM tori. Similar results were obtained in
[
EFK1
]
. There is also work on effective long time stability of the elliptic fixed point
[
Ni
]
.

1.2.
The ellipsoid case and strong closing lemma

As Pugh’s
C^{1}
closing lemma has been well-known for a long time, it came as a big surprise when Irie proved the denseness of periodic orbits for
C^{\infty}
-generic Reeb flows (c.f.
[
I1
]
) on a 3-dimensional contact manifold and later for
C^{\infty}
-generic surface Hamiltomorphisms (c.f.
[
AI
]
). The approach of Irie is global. He uses the spectral information given by ECH. The idea turns out to be very fruitful. For instance, similar idea was used in the min-max theory of minimal surfaces solving a conjecture of Yau (c.f.
[
IMN
]
) and proving an equidistribution result (c.f.
[
MNS
]
). See also
[
CPZ
,
EH
]
etc.

Then it is natural to pursue this stream of ideas in the higher dimensional case. For instance, it is conjectured in Fish and Hofer that periodic orbits are dense, for instance, for the Reeb flow of
C^{\infty}
-generic contact hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{2N}
. We refer readers to Conjecture 1 of
[
FH
]
for precise statement, speculations and more references.

In a recent paper
[
I2
]
, Irie introduced a notion of
strong closing property
formlated as follows. Let
M
be a manifold of dimension
2N-1
and
\lambda
a contact form that is a one-form satisfying
\lambda\wedge(d\lambda)^{N-1}\neq 0
at each point of
M
. This defines a Reeb vector field
R_{\lambda}
via
\iota_{R_{\lambda}}d\lambda\equiv 0
and
\lambda(R_{\lambda})\equiv 1
. The
positive strong closing property
is defined as:
for all
\psi\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
nonconstant, there exists
t\in[0,1]
such that there is a closed Reeb orbit for the contact manifold
(M,(1+t\psi)\lambda)
that intersects the support of
\psi
.

Though the strong closing property is weaker than existing dense periodic orbits, to our understanding, it is strong in the sense that a periodic orbit is created by a perturbation
\psi
, no matter how small the support of
\psi
is.

Irie also conjectured that the strong closing property holds for
(\partial E_{a},\lambda)
where
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}dx_{i}-x_{i}dy_{i}
is the standard contact form and
E_{a}
is the ellipoid

E_{a}=\left\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}\ |\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\pi(x_{i}^{2}+y_{i}^{2})}{a_{i}}\leq 1\right\}

where
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})
satisfies
0<a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\ldots\leq a_{N}
.
The conjecture was proved by Chaidez-Datta-Prasad-Tanny
[
CDPT
]
using tools from contact homology and by Cineli and Seyfaddini
[
CS
]
using spectral invariants. The latter also considers strong closing property for some very chaotic models such as Anosov-Katok construction for pseudo rotations. Note that the Reeb flow of
(\partial E_{a},\lambda)
is the same as a Hamiltonian flow of the Hamiltonian

(2)

H(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\pi(x_{i}^{2}+y_{i}^{2})}{a_{i}}

on the energy level 1. A small perturbation of the contact form induces a perturbed Reeb flow that can be viewed as a Hamiltonian system slightly perturbing the Hamiltonian (
2
). It is standard to introduce a symplectic transformation
(x_{i},y_{i})=(\sqrt{I}_{i}\cos\theta_{i},\sqrt{I}_{i}\sin\theta_{i})
. Thus we model the problem as a nearly integrable system

(3)

H_{\varepsilon}(I,\theta)=\omega\cdot I+\varepsilon f(I,\theta),\quad(I,\theta)\in T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{N},

where
\omega=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{N})
and
\omega_{i}=\frac{\pi}{a_{i}},\ i=1,\ldots,N.

Note that this is exactly the prototypical degenerate case of the KAM theory and Nekhoroshev theorem mentioned above.
We have the following results concerning Irie’s conjecture. We state our results in the language of nearly integrable Hamiltonian dynamics, which have counterparts for Reeb flows by the correspondence in Appendix
B
.

Let
C_{c}^{r}(T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{N})
be the space of compactly supported
C^{r}
functions on
T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{N}
. We first perform a splitting
C_{c}^{r}(T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{N})=C_{c}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\oplus(C_{c}^{r}(T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{N})/C_{c}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))
such that each
f\in C_{c}^{r}(T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{N})
is split into
f=\langle f\rangle+[f]
where
\langle f\rangle(I)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{N}}f(I,\theta)d\theta\in C_{c}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
[f]=f-\langle f\rangle\in C_{c}^{r}(T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{N})/C_{c}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

Let
D\subset\Delta^{N-1}:=\{\omega\cdot I=1,\ I_{i}>0,\ i=1,2,\ldots,N\}
be an open set and
\bar{U}
its closure with
\bar{D}\subset\Delta^{N-1}
. Let
C^{r}_{c}(\bar{D})
be the set of compactly supported
C^{r}
functions whose support contains
\bar{D}
, and let
\Sigma^{r}
be either
C^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{N})/\mathbb{R}
or
C_{c}^{r}(T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{N})/C_{c}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Let
\mathcal{S}^{r}
be the unit sphere in
\Sigma^{r}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

There exists
r_{0}>0
such that for all
r>r_{0},
the following holds. There is a residual set
\mathcal{R}\subset C_{c}^{r}(\bar{D})
such that for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}
and each
\langle f\rangle\in\mathcal{R}
, there is a sequence of intervals
(a_{n},b_{n})\subset(0,1)
with

and asymptotically residual sets
\mathcal{R}_{n}\subset\mathcal{S}^{r}
, such that for each
\varepsilon_{n}\in(a_{n},b_{n})
and each

in (
3

distinct periodic orbits
\gamma_{\varepsilon_{n},i},\ i=1,2,\ldots,2^{N-1},
all intersecting the support of

on the energy level 1. Moreover, if
\omega
is nonresonant
(
i.e. there is no integer vector
\mathbf{k}\neq 0
such that
\mathbf{k}\cdot\omega=0)
, then for any sequence

.

Note that we do not require

to be nonnegative or nonpositive as in the definition of strong closing property.
To appreciate better the role played by the asymptotically residual condition, we recall the well-known example of Katok.
In
[
Ka
]
(see also
[

]
), Katok constructed a family of Finsler metrics

on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{N}
, which reduces to the standard metric when

(for

, then the system

admits

\{I_{i}=x_{i}^{2}+y_{i}^{2}=a_{i}/\pi,I_{j}=x_{j}=y_{j}=0,\ j\neq i\},\ \ i=1,2,\ldots,N,

and the periodic orbits given by the last theorem are nontrivial. Katok’s example is excluded by our asymptotically residual condition, since it has no interval of parameters admitting nontrivial periodic orbits, but is still included in the strong closing lemma of
[

,
CS
]
.

Furthermore, we can indeed get asymptotically denseness on the energy level, if we get rid of a zero measure set of
\omega.

Theorem 1.5
.

There exists
r_{0}>0
such that for all
r>r_{0},
the following holds.
There is a full measure set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
and a residual set
\mathcal{R}\subset C_{c}^{r}(\bar{D})
, such that for each

and each

and a sequence of asymptotically residual sets

.

Lying in the heart of the proof is a recent probabilistic number theoretic result on Diophantine approximation proved by Shapira-Weiss
[

, in addition to the sharp normal form developed in the proof of Theorem

.
The conclusion part of the last theorem gives more information than the strong closing property, and indeed, the proof is constructive and effective. We know quite clearly the locations of each periodic orbit and its period information.

1.3.
Speculations

Let us compare the following three approaches for the problem of closing lemma:

(a)

closing nonwandering points,

(b)

spectral approach,

(c)

normal form approach.

Each has strengthes and weaknesses. The approach (a) gives dense periodic orbits but is only
C^{2}
generic (for Hamiltonian). The approach (b) gives
C^{\infty}
closing lemma for Reeb flows, but only for 3D and surface Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms. The approach (c) works for any dimension and any high regularity, but requires asymptotic genericity and gives asymptotic denseness and only for nearly integrable systems.

Our asymptotically residual set and asymptotic denseness look a bit artificial and unsatisfactory, but it seems to us that they are intrinsic to our approach. Indeed, as we will see in the proofs of the above theorems, a nonperturbative subsystem emerges after application of the normal form. The problem of finding periodic orbit in the original system is turned into a problem of finding nondegenerate critical points in the nonperturbative subsystem. Thus we have gone beyond the setting of nearly integrable systems considered by Poincaré.

1.4.
Further motivations from physics

Hamiltonian systems (
1
) with
N=2
were studied intensively in both mathematics and physics literature. It has important applications in physics such as Frenkel-Kontorova model in solid physics. The interests in the
N>2
cases, in particular the system (
3
) modeling an interacting chain of driven linear rotors, arise in the recent researches as the classical model for the quantum many-body localization (c.f.
[
KGRG
,
RG
]
etc), which is a phenomenon of localization of eigenstates and breaking of ergodicity in interacting many-body system. The classical origin of the localization is attributed to the KAM behavior when
\omega
satisfies Diophantine condition and when there is a lack of KAM tori, delocalization may occur. Our work reveals the richness of dynamics, i.e. the nonintegrability, of the system (
3
), even when
\omega
is Diophantine. Thus, models related to (
3
) clearly deserve further investigations with interests beyond the existence of periodic orbits. We expect that there exist lots of KAM tori around each elliptic periodic orbit with Floquet multipliers Diophantine and wiskered tori for each hyperbolic-elliptic type periodic orbit whose elliptic part has Diophantine Floquet multipliers, and they should also play a role in the localization.

1.6.
Outline of the proof

The proof consists of a hard part and a soft part. The hard part is a KAM normal form as well as its associated framework for studying similar problems, and the soft part is the genericity argument as well as some results on Diophantine approximation. We present the normal form package in Section
2
, which is expected to have more applications. On the other hand, the soft part is usually nonstandard and requires special observations for different problems.

We first invoke the following classical Dirichlet’s simultaneous Diophantine approximation theorem.

Lemma 1.6

(Dirichlet)
.

For any
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and any
T(>1)\in\mathbb{R}
, there exist
q\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
, such that
q\leq T
and

|\omega-\mathbf{p}/q|\leq\frac{1}{|q|T^{1/N}}.

The lemma motivates the following definition.

Definition 1.7
.

An integer vector
(\mathbf{p},q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}\times\mathbb{N}
is called a
best approximant
to
\omega
if for any
q^{\prime}<q
and any
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N},
we have
\|q\omega-\mathbf{p}\|<\|q^{\prime}\omega-\mathbf{p}^{\prime}\|,
where
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm.

When
\omega
is proportional to an integer
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
, we shall take
\omega=\mathbf{p}/q
, with
q=(\|\mathbf{p}\|/\|\omega\|)
. In this case, we can choose
\varepsilon
independent of
\mathbf{p}/q
. Otherwise, we can find a sequence of best approximants
\mathbf{p}_{n}/q_{n}
by Dirichlet theorem such that
|\omega-\mathbf{p}_{n}/q_{n}|\leq\frac{1}{|q_{n}|^{1+\frac{1}{N}}}
and choose
\varepsilon_{n}
to be a number comparable to
\frac{1}{|q_{n}|^{1+\frac{1}{N}}}
whose precise values would be determined later. In the latter case, we pick
\mathbf{p}_{n}/q_{n}
and
\varepsilon_{n}
and suppress the subscript
n
for simplicity.

With these, we shall rewrite the Hamiltonian as

(4)

H(I,\theta)=\frac{\mathbf{p}}{q}\cdot I+\varepsilon g(I)+\varepsilon Pf(I,\theta)+\varepsilon Qf(I,\theta),

where
g(I)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(\omega-\frac{\mathbf{p}}{q})\cdot I+\langle f\rangle(I)
,
\langle f\rangle(I)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{N}}f(I,\theta)d\theta
, and
Pf
(respectively
Qf
) consists of all the Fourier modes with
\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}=0
(respectively
\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}\neq 0
),
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
. We have the freedom to choose an approximating rational vector
\mathbf{p}/q
and
\varepsilon
.

The next step is to obtain a KAM normal form that is to find a sympletic transformation to write the Hamiltonian into the form

H(I,\theta)=\frac{\mathbf{p}}{q}\cdot I+\varepsilon G(I)+\varepsilon\bar{F}(I,\theta)+\varepsilon\tilde{F}(I,\theta),

where
G(I)
is a small perturbation of
g
, the term
\bar{F}
is a small perturbation of
Pf
still consists of Fourier modes with
\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}=0,\mathbf{k}\neq 0
, and
\varepsilon F
is negligible compared with other terms. See Proposition
2.1
.

Note that the Hamiltonian
\frac{\mathbf{p}}{q}\cdot I
determines a linear flow on
\mathbb{T}^{N}\times\{I\}
with velocity
\frac{\mathbf{p}}{q}
, thus each orbit is periodic. If we take the terms
\varepsilon G(I)+\varepsilon\bar{F}(I,\theta)
into consideration, restrict to an energy level and pick a Poincaré section, a critical point of
\varepsilon G(I)+\varepsilon\bar{F}(I,\theta)
on which would give a periodic orbit of the system
\frac{\mathbf{p}}{q}\cdot I+\varepsilon G(I)+\varepsilon\bar{F}(I,\theta)
that is also a linear flow with velocity
\frac{\mathbf{p}}{q}
. If we further assume that the critical point is nondegenerate and the eigenvalues of the Hessian are bounded away from zero by a number much larger than
\varepsilon\|\tilde{F}\|_{C^{2}}
, then by implicit function theorem adding the perturbation
\varepsilon\tilde{F}(I,\theta)
does not spoil the periodic orbit. This is why we have the asymptotically residual condition.

We remark that since we turn the problem of finding periodic orbit into a problem of finding nondegenerate critical points, we expect that this is a place where the normal form theory can meet the Floer theory to yield potentially more results.

We have outlined why we have Theorem
1.4
. The periodic orbit obtained in this way is a small perturbation of the linear flow with velocity
\mathbf{p}/q
, thus in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, would be dense when projected to
\mathbb{T}^{N}
if
\omega
is non resonant. However, the
I
-component is almost a constant along the orbit. To find dense periodic orbits also in the
I
-component, we note that the
I
-component of the orbit described above is very close to the critical point of
g(I)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(\omega-\frac{\mathbf{p}}{q})\cdot I+\langle f\rangle(I)
, i.e. solution of the equation
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(\omega-\frac{\mathbf{p}}{q})+D\langle f\rangle(I)=0
. To realize a dense set of
I
as solutions to the last equation, we invoke the following recent result of Shapira-Weiss.

Let
\mathrm{disp}(\omega,\mathbf{p},q):=q^{1/N}(\mathbf{p}-q\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be the
displacement
.

Theorem 1.8

(Theorem 1.1 of
[
SW
]
)
.

There exists a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that for Lebesgue almost every
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the sequence
\mathrm{disp}(\omega,\mathbf{p}_{k},q_{k})
equidistributes on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with respect to
\mu
as
k\to\infty
, where
(\mathbf{p}_{k},q_{k})
is the sequence of best approximants. Moreover, the measure
\mu
has bounded support, absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue and
\mathrm{SO}(N)
-invariant.

By the
\mathrm{SO}(N)
-invariance, we see that the sequence
\frac{\mathrm{disp}(\omega,\mathbf{p}_{k},q_{k})}{\|\mathrm{disp}(\omega,\mathbf{p}_{k},q_{k})\|}
equidistributes on
\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
with respect to Haar, and in particular, it is dense.

With this, we immediately have the following Lemma.

Lemma 1.9
.

For any
I_{*}
where
D\langle f\rangle(I_{*})
is nonzero, there exist a subsequence
n_{k}\to\infty
and a sequence
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
such that
\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{k}}(\omega-\frac{\mathbf{p}_{n_{k}}}{q_{n_{k}}})\to-D\langle f\rangle(I_{*})
as
k\to\infty
.

We also need the nondegeneracy of
D^{2}g(I_{*})=D^{2}\langle f\rangle(I_{*})
, with which, we can find by the implicit function theorem a sequence of
I_{k}\to I_{*}
with
\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{k}}(\omega-\frac{\mathbf{p}_{n_{k}}}{q_{n_{k}}})+D\langle f\rangle(I_{k})=0.
We have the following result.

Proposition 1.10
.

For
C^{r},\ r\geq 3,
generic function
g:\ B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
, the set
\{\det D^{2}g(z)=0\}
is a finite union of submanifolds of codimension 1, where
B_{1}
is the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Putting all these ingredients together, we get the proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Theorem
1.2
and
1.3
turn out to be similar and much simpler since the unperturbed part of the Hamiltonian can provide the needed nondegeneracy.

2.
The general framework

In this section, we present a general framework that can be used to study similar problems.

(1)

KAM normal form
: The first key ingredient in the framework is the KAM normal form, which shows that after a symplectic transformation, the Hamiltonian depends on dynamics in the
\mathbf{p}
direction very weakly.

(2)

Linear symplectic transformation
: The second step is to separate the
\mathbf{p}
direction and the directions transverse to
\mathbf{p}
, for which purpose, we perform a linear symplectic transformation.

(3)

Energetic reduction and locating a periodic orbit
: The third step is to locate a critical point in the transverse direction, that would be a periodic orbit in the truncated system. For this step, it would be helpful to perform an energetic reduction by restricting to an energy level and reduce the system to a non autonomous one.

(4)

Lyapunov center theorem
: The last step is to introduce the perturbation by applying Lyapunov center theorem. For the last step, we shall use the genericity assumption.

2.1.
The KAM normal form

We next perform a decomposition
f=\langle f\rangle+Pf+Qf
, where supposing
f(I,\theta)=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}}f_{\mathbf{k}}(I)e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\theta}
, then we have
P
the projection to Fourier modes with
\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}=0,\ \mathbf{k}\neq 0
and
Qf=f-Pf-\langle f\rangle
, i.e.

(5)

\langle f\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{N}}f(I,\theta)d\theta=f_{\mathbf{0}},\quad Pf(I,\theta)=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}=0,\mathbf{k}\neq 0}f_{\mathbf{k}}(I)e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\theta},\quad Qf(I,\theta)=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}\neq 0}f_{\mathbf{k}}(I)e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\theta}.

With Fourier expansion, we define the
C^{r}
-norm as
\|f\|_{C^{r}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}(|\mathbf{k}|^{r}+1)|f_{\mathbf{k}}|_{C^{r}}
where
|f_{\mathbf{k}}|_{C^{r}}
is the usual
C^{r}
-norm of
f_{\mathbf{k}}
as a function of
I
.

For later use, we define

(6)

\mathbf{k}_{i}=(p_{i},0,\ldots,0,-p_{1},0,\ldots,0)/(g.c.d.(p_{1},p_{i})),

where
-p_{1}
appears in the
i
-th entry,
i=2,3,\ldots,N.
Then for each
\mathbf{k}
with
\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}=0
, we have
\mathbf{k}\in\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{k}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{k}_{N}\}
.

Proposition 2.1

(The KAM Normal form)
.

Let
H
be the Hamiltonian (
4
). Then there exists
r_{0}
sufficiently large such that for all
r>r_{0}
and
\|f\|_{C^{r}}\leq 1
, the following holds. Let
I_{\star}
be a point where
Dg(I_{\star})
is vanishing and
D^{2}g(I_{\star})
is nondegenerate, then there exists
\varepsilon_{0}
such that for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, there exist
I_{*}
satisfying
|I_{*}-I_{\star}|\leq C\varepsilon q
and a symplectic transformation
\phi
defined on
\ B_{\gamma^{1/3}}(I_{*})\times\mathbb{T}^{N}
and being
\varepsilon q
-close to identity in the
C^{2}
norm, such that we have

(7)

H\circ\phi(I,\theta)=\frac{\mathbf{p}}{q}\cdot I+\varepsilon G(I)+\varepsilon\bar{F}(I,\theta)+\varepsilon\tilde{F}(I,\theta),

where we have

(1)

G(I)=g(I)+o(\varepsilon q)
in
C^{2}
satisfying
DG(I_{*})=0
,

(2)

\bar{F}=Pf+O(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2N}}\gamma)
in
C^{2}
and

(3)

\|\tilde{F}\|_{C^{2}}\leq\gamma^{1+\beta}
,

where we define
\gamma:=\varepsilon^{(r-2)\frac{N}{N+1}}
and
\beta>0
is a constant depending only on
N
.

We postpone the proof of the normal form to Section
5
.

Remark 2.1
.

The normal form looks similar to the normal form used by Lochak in his proof of Nekhoroshev theorem
(
c.f.
[
L
]
)
. However, it is much more delicate and essentially different. Indeed, its proof involves the KAM scheme with superexponential decay of the remainder. The main reason is that
PF
and
Pf
consist of Fourier modes with
\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}=0
and
|\mathbf{p}|\sim q
, and such a resonance only occurs first when
|\mathbf{k}|
is comparable with
q
. Then by the decay of Fourier modes, we have
\|Pf\|_{C^{2}}\leq\frac{C}{q^{r-2}}\|Pf\|_{C^{r}}\sim\gamma
, which is much smaller than
\|Qf\|_{C^{2}}
which is of order 1. Thus in the proof, we not only need to make the remainder
\tilde{F}
small, but also control carefully such that
G
and
\bar{F}
are dominated by
g
and
Pf
respectively, rather than get spoiled by
Qf\circ\phi
.

2.2.
Linear symplectic transformation

To reveal the information of periodic orbit clearly, we perform the following linear symplectic transformation. The point of the transformation is to separate the direction of
\mathbf{p}
and that transverse to
\mathbf{p}
.

Let
M
be the matrix
\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\ \frac{1}{q}\mathbf{p}&\\
0_{(N-1)\times 1}&\mathrm{Id}_{N-1}\end{array}\right]
where
\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{p}
is the first row, then we have
M^{-T}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{q}{p_{1}}&0\\
-\frac{1}{p_{1}}\bar{\mathbf{p}}^{T}&\mathrm{Id}_{N-1}\end{array}\right]
where
\bar{\mathbf{p}}=(p_{2},\ldots,p_{N})\in\mathbb{Z}^{N-1}
. Note in particular that the
i
-th row is a multiple of
\mathbf{k}_{i},\ i\geq 2.
The norms of the matrices are both order 1 as
\varepsilon\to 0.
In the limit
\mathbf{p}/q\to\omega
, we have
M\to M_{\infty}
where the first row in
M
is replaced by
\omega
in
M_{\infty}.

With this, we introduce a non standard torus
\mathbb{T}_{M}^{N}:=\mathbb{R}^{N}/(M^{-T}\mathbb{Z}^{N})
and a symplectic transformation

\Phi_{M}:\ T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{N}\to T^{*}\mathbb{T}_{M}^{N},\quad(I,\theta)\mapsto(MI,M^{-T}\theta):=(K,\vartheta).

Both
\Phi_{M}
and
\Phi^{-1}_{M}
have uniformly bounded norms as
\mathbf{p}/q\to\omega.

Explicitly, we have

K_{1}=\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{p}\cdot I,\ K_{i}=I_{i},\quad\vartheta_{1}=\frac{q}{p_{1}}\theta_{1},\ \vartheta_{i}=-\frac{p_{i}}{p_{1}}\theta_{1}+\theta_{i},\quad\ i=2,3,\ldots,n.

We next introduce the abbreviation
K=(K_{1},\bar{K})
and
\vartheta=(\vartheta_{1},\bar{\vartheta})
, so
\bar{\vartheta}=-\frac{\bar{\mathbf{p}}}{p_{1}}\theta_{1}+\bar{\theta}
and
\bar{K}=\bar{I}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

After the linear symplectic transformation, the Hamiltonian (
3
) becomes

H\circ\Phi_{M}^{-1}(K,\vartheta)=K_{1}+\varepsilon\mathsf{G}(K)+\varepsilon\bar{\mathsf{F}}(K_{1},\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta})+\varepsilon\tilde{\mathsf{F}}(K_{1},\vartheta_{1},\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta}),

where
\mathsf{G}=G\circ M^{-1}
,
\bar{\mathsf{F}}=\bar{F}\circ\Phi_{M}^{-1}
and
\tilde{\mathsf{F}}=\tilde{F}\circ\Phi_{M}^{-1}
.

2.3.
Energetic reduction

We next perform a step of energetic reduction to get rid of the dependence on
K_{1}
in the perturbation. The procedure is to restrict to the energy level set
H=1
and solve for
K_{1}:=-\mathcal{K}(\xi,\eta,\vartheta_{1})
to obtain the following by the implicit function theorem

(8)

\mathcal{K}(\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta},\vartheta_{1})=-1+\varepsilon\mathcal{G}(\bar{K})+\varepsilon\bar{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta})+\varepsilon\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta},\vartheta_{1}),\quad\vartheta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}/(q\mathbb{Z}),

where
\bar{\mathcal{F}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
has similar
C^{r}
norm as
\bar{F}
and
\tilde{F}
, in particular, we have
\bar{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta})=\bar{F}(1,\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta})+O(\varepsilon\gamma)
. This is a standard procedure which turns the autonomous system
H
into a non autonomous system
\mathcal{K}
and treats
\vartheta_{1}
as the new time variable.

Lemma 2.3
.

The nonautonomous Hamiltonian system
\mathcal{K}
defines the same flow as the autonomous system
H
restricted to the energy level 1.

Proof.

From the Hamiltonian
H
, we get the equations of motion

\begin{cases}\frac{dK_{1}}{dt}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\vartheta_{1}},\\
\frac{d\vartheta_{1}}{dt}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial K_{1}}=1+\varepsilon\frac{\partial\tilde{F}}{\partial K_{1}},\\
\end{cases}\begin{cases}\frac{d\bar{K}}{dt}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\bar{\vartheta}},\\
\frac{d\bar{\vartheta}}{dt}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\bar{K}}.\\
\end{cases}

Since in the system
\mathcal{K}
, we use
\vartheta_{1}
as the new time, we divide the
\frac{d\bar{K}}{dt}
and
\frac{d\bar{\vartheta}}{dt}
equations by the
\frac{d\vartheta_{1}}{dt}
equation to get the following by implicit function theorem

\begin{cases}\frac{d\bar{K}}{d\vartheta_{1}}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\bar{\vartheta}}/\frac{\partial H}{\partial K_{1}}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{K}}{\partial\bar{\vartheta}},\\
\frac{d\bar{\vartheta}}{d\vartheta_{1}}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\bar{K}}/\frac{\partial H}{\partial K_{1}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{K}}{\partial\bar{K}},\\
\end{cases}

which has the form of the canonical equations. ∎

2.4.
Locating a periodic orbit in the truncated system

We next show that a critical point of
\mathcal{G}+\bar{\mathcal{F}}
gives rise to an orbit of the system
\mathcal{K}
, if we ignore the
\varepsilon\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
-perturbation.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
(\bar{K}_{*},\bar{\vartheta}_{*})
be a nondegenerate critical point of
\mathcal{G}+\bar{\mathcal{F}}
, i.e.
D(\mathcal{G}+\bar{\mathcal{F}})(\bar{K}_{*},\bar{\vartheta}_{*})=0
and
D^{2}(\mathcal{G}+\bar{\mathcal{F}})(\bar{K}_{*},\bar{\vartheta}_{*})
is nondegenerate. Then it is a nondegenerate fixed point of the truncated system
\frac{d}{d\vartheta_{1}}(\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta})=JD(\mathcal{G}+\bar{\mathcal{F}})(\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta})
.

2.5.
Rescaling

Without loss of generality, we assume the critical point of
\mathcal{G}+\bar{\mathcal{F}}
is
(\bar{K}_{*},\bar{\vartheta}_{*})=(0,0)
. We perform the Taylor expansion around it

\mathcal{G}(\bar{K})+\bar{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta})=\langle D^{2}(\mathcal{G}+\bar{\mathcal{F}})(\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta}),(\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta})\rangle+O(|\bar{K}|^{3}+\gamma(|\bar{K}\bar{\vartheta}^{2}|+|\bar{K}^{2}\bar{\vartheta}|+|\bar{\vartheta}^{3}|)).

Note that
\partial_{\bar{\vartheta}}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{F}}
is of order
\gamma
much smaller than
D^{2}\mathcal{G}
. We introduce a rescaling

(9)

\bar{K}\mapsto\bar{K}/\gamma^{1/2},\quad\bar{\vartheta}\mapsto\bar{\vartheta},\quad\mathcal{K}\mapsto\mathcal{K}/(\varepsilon\gamma),\quad\vartheta_{1}\mapsto\vartheta_{1}\gamma^{1/2}\varepsilon.

This transformation does not change the form of Hamiltonian equations.

Proposition 2.5
.

After this transformation (
9
), the Hamiltonian becomes

\mathcal{K}(\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta},\vartheta_{1})=\langle D^{2}\mathcal{G}|_{(0,0)}\bar{K},\bar{K}\rangle+\frac{1}{\gamma}\langle\partial_{\bar{\vartheta}}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{F}}|_{(0,0)}\bar{\vartheta},\bar{\vartheta}\rangle+O(\gamma^{1/2})+\frac{1}{\gamma}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(\gamma\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta},\varepsilon^{-1}\gamma^{-1/2}\vartheta_{1})

in the region
(\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta})\in B_{1}(0)
, where we have discarded a constant
1/(\varepsilon\gamma)
.

Proof.

The
\frac{1}{\gamma}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
term is straightforward. The term
\varepsilon\langle D^{2}\mathcal{G}\bar{K},\bar{K}\rangle
is mapped to
\varepsilon\gamma\langle D^{2}\mathcal{G}\bar{K},\bar{K}\rangle
under the transformation
\bar{K}\mapsto\bar{K}/\gamma^{1/2}
. The factor
\varepsilon\gamma
gets canceled after dividing the Hamiltonian by
\varepsilon\gamma
. Similarly, the term
\langle\partial_{\bar{\vartheta}}^{2}\bar{\mathcal{F}}\bar{\vartheta},\bar{\vartheta}\rangle
gets a factor
1/\gamma
after the whole procedure and the term
\partial_{\bar{\vartheta}}\partial_{\bar{K}}\bar{\mathcal{F}}\bar{K}\bar{\vartheta}
gets a factor
\gamma^{1/2}
. The latter is of order
\gamma^{1/2}
since
\|\bar{\mathcal{F}}\|_{C^{2}}\sim\gamma
. For the same reason, the term
O(|\bar{K}|^{3}+\gamma(|\bar{K}\bar{\vartheta}^{2}|+|\bar{K}^{2}\bar{\vartheta}|+|\bar{\vartheta}^{3}|))
is of order
\gamma^{1/2}
.
∎

2.6.
Lyapunov center theorem

We cite the following Lyapunov center theorem.

Theorem 2.6

(Proposition 9.1.1 of
[
MHO
]
)
.

If the nontrivial multipliers of a periodic orbit of a system are not 1, then the periodic orbit can be continued.

In our case, the theorem can be understood easily as follows. Suppose we have a map
\phi:\ B_{1}\to B_{1}
with a fixed point at 0 and
D\phi(0)
has no eigenvalue 1, where
B_{1}
is the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2N}
. Then a small
C^{1}
perturbation of
\phi
also admits a fixed point by implicit function theorem.

We next prove a general proposition guaranteeing the existence of periodic orbit when a normal form and nondegeneracy condition is given.

Proposition 2.7
.

Suppose the eigenvalues of the matrix
e^{J\Xi}
are bounded away from 1 by a number much larger than
\gamma^{\beta}
, where
\Xi=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}D^{2}\mathcal{G}&0\\
0&\gamma^{-1}\partial^{2}_{\bar{\vartheta}\bar{\vartheta}}\bar{\mathcal{F}}\end{array}\right]\Big|_{(0,0)}
, then the system in Proposition
2.5
admits a periodic orbit. This implies that the original system
H_{\varepsilon}=\omega\cdot I+\varepsilon f
, if
\omega
is non resonant, admits a periodic orbit that intersects any ball of radius greater than
2/q
, when projected to
\mathbb{T}^{N}.

Proof.

The Hamiltonian equation of the system
\mathcal{K}
in Proposition
2.5
is written as

\frac{d}{d\vartheta_{1}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{K}\\
\bar{\vartheta}\end{array}\right]=J\Xi\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{K}\\
\bar{\vartheta}\end{array}\right]+O(\gamma^{\beta}),

where the
O
is in
C^{1}
-norm and we do not take derivative with respect to
\vartheta_{1}
on the RHS.

Note that the
\vartheta_{1}
variable is defined on the circle
\mathbb{R}/(\varepsilon\gamma^{1/2}q\mathbb{Z})
, since
\vartheta_{1}
before the rescaling (
9
) is defined on
\mathbb{R}/(q\mathbb{Z})
. We take
T
to be
[(\varepsilon\gamma^{1/2}q)^{-1}](\varepsilon\gamma^{1/2}q)
that is a number close 1, by repeating on the circle
\mathbb{R}/(\varepsilon\gamma^{1/2}q\mathbb{Z})
for
[(\varepsilon\gamma^{1/2}q)^{-1}]
times.

To apply the Lyapunov center theorem, it is enough to show that the
O(\gamma^{\beta})
-perturbation gives a
C^{1}
-small perturbation to the time-
T
map of the Hamiltonian system. The differential of the time-
T
map is obtained by integrating over time
T
the variational equation

\frac{d}{d\tau}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta\bar{K}\\
\Delta\bar{\vartheta}\end{array}\right]=(JD^{2}\mathcal{K}(\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta},\vartheta_{1}))\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta\bar{K}\\
\Delta\bar{\vartheta}\end{array}\right],

which is given by differentiating the Hamiltonian equation
\frac{d}{d\tau}(\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta})=JD\mathcal{K}(\bar{K},\bar{\vartheta},\vartheta_{1})
. Note that in these equations there is no derivative with respective to
\vartheta_{1}
. By DuHamel principle, the solution to the variational equation is given by

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta\bar{K}\\
\Delta\bar{\vartheta}\end{array}\right](T)=e^{J\Xi T}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta\bar{K}\\
\Delta\bar{\vartheta}\end{array}\right](0)+\int_{0}^{T}e^{J\Xi(T-s)}(O(\gamma^{\beta}))\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta\bar{K}\\
\Delta\bar{\vartheta}\end{array}\right](s)ds,

thus the differential of the time-
T
map is a
\gamma^{\beta}
perturbation of
e^{J\Xi T}
.
We can then apply Lyapunov center theorem to conclude that the perturbed Hamiltonian system
\mathcal{K}
also admits a periodic orbit.
The periodic orbit passes through a sufficiently small neighborhood of the maximum point of
f
thus passes through supp
f
.

The periodic orbit is
O(\gamma^{\beta})
-close to the unperturbed one
\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta\bar{K}\\
\Delta\bar{\vartheta}\end{array}\right]=0
. Going back to the original system by undoing
\Phi_{M}
, we see that this periodic orbit is an
O(\gamma^{\beta})
perturbation of a periodic orbit with slope
\frac{\mathbf{p}}{q}
when projected to
\mathbb{T}^{n}
. Since we have
\gamma=q^{-(r-2)\beta}\ll\frac{1}{q}
and
\frac{\mathbf{p}_{n}}{q_{n}}\to\omega
, we get complete the proof of the statement.
∎

3.2.
Proof of Theorem

In this section, we give the proof of Theorem

We next give the proof of Proposition

Proof of Proposition

We invoke a transversality theorem of Abraham(see Theorem

of Appendix). Here we show how to set up the problem to fit into the framework of Theorem
A.1
. In the following, we shall use the notations of Theorem

.

Let us take

a compact subset, and

is the space of symmetric matrices of

consisting of singular symmetric matrices. We next claim that

Claim:
V
is a finite union of submanifolds in

of codimension 1.

Proof of the claim:

Indeed, since each matrix

admits a decomposition

diagonal and real. A matrix

. We next count the dimension. We have

\frac{N(N-1)}{2}=dim\mathrm{SO}(N)

We next consider the pseudo-representation

via

C^{1}
in

for

. The evaluation map

via

, which is given explicitly as

and

We next verify that the pseudo-representation

is
C^{r}
-pseudo-transverse to
V
on
K

and
\psi_{a}
to be identity. Then we have
D
is dense in

. We next show that

For all

of

in
D
, such that
the evaluation map

and transverse to

.

Indeed, without loss of generality, we assume

is degenerate and diagonal. If

4.1.
The toral geodesic flow case

In this section, we give the proof of Theorem
1.3

ds^{2}_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i,j}(\delta_{ij}+\varepsilon a_{ij}(\theta))d\theta_{i}d\theta_{j}.

We treat

H(\theta,y)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}(\delta_{ij}+\varepsilon b_{ij}(\theta))y_{i}y_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\varepsilon b_{ij}(\theta)y_{i}y_{j},

where the matrix
(\delta_{ij}+\varepsilon b_{ij}(\theta))

(\delta_{ij}+\varepsilon a_{ij}(\theta)).
Thus solving the equation

\sum_{j}(\delta_{ij}+\varepsilon b_{ij}(\theta))(\delta_{jk}+\varepsilon a_{jk}(\theta))=\delta_{ik},

we get

. In the following, we denote
B=(b_{ij})=-A+O(\varepsilon)
.

We next pick
y_{*}=\mathbf{p}/\|\mathbf{p}\|
where
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
, such points are dense on the unit sphere. For given
\varepsilon
, we consider rational points with
\|\mathbf{p}\|\leq\varepsilon^{-\nu}
for some small

.
With this, we replace
y
by

, where
I
is small, and expand

H(\theta,y)=\frac{1}{2}(\|y_{*}\|^{2}+2y_{*}\cdot I+\|I\|^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon(\langle B(\theta)y_{*},y_{*}\rangle+2\langle B(\theta)y_{*},I\rangle+\langle B(\theta)I,I\rangle).

The main difference from the ellipsoid case is that the quadratic term
\|I\|^{2}
does not carry a factor
\varepsilon
as
\varepsilon g
does in system (
4
). If we examine the proof of Proposition
2.1
, we shall see that the reminder
Qf
here does not decay fast enough. To solve this problem we introduce the following procedure called
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
-blowup.

4.1.1.
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
-blowup

We consider an
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
-neighborhood of
I=0
and rescale

I\mapsto\sqrt{\varepsilon}I,\quad\theta\mapsto\theta,\quad H\mapsto H/\sqrt{\varepsilon},\quad t\mapsto t\varepsilon.

This transformation blows up the
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
-neighborhood of

into a neighborhood of unit size. The Poincaré-Cartan form gets multiplied by
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, thus the form of Hamiltonian equations does not change.
The Hamiltonian after the blowup has the form

H(I,\theta)=y_{*}\cdot I+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\frac{1}{2}\|I\|^{2}+\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{2}\big(\langle B(\theta)y_{*},y_{*}\rangle+2\sqrt{\varepsilon}\langle B(\theta)y_{*},I\rangle+\varepsilon\langle B(\theta)I,I\rangle\big)

which is defined on
B_{1}(0)\times\mathbb{T}^{N}
and we have discarded the constant
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\|y_{*}\|^{2}
.
We next donote by

the term in the big parenthesis, whose leading term is

. We next decompose

and denote by

\langle f\rangle

. Thus the leading term in

\theta

, which is
I
-independent.

Thus we get the form

H=\frac{\mathbf{p}}{\|\mathbf{p}\|}\cdot I+\sqrt{\varepsilon}(g(I)+Pf+Qf).

This is in the form of equation (
4
), with the only difference being that
\varepsilon
is now replaced by
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
and

replaced by
\|\mathbf{p}\|
. Since we choose
\|\mathbf{p}\|\leq\varepsilon^{-\nu}

\varepsilon q
in Proposition
2.1

.

, where the proportion
1+g_{\varepsilon}
is determined by the equation
\iota_{R_{\varepsilon}}\lambda_{\varepsilon}=1
.

Since
d\lambda_{\varepsilon}
is an exact 2-form for each
\varepsilon
, then Moser’s trick gives a diffeomorphism
\psi_{\varepsilon}
satisfying
\psi_{\varepsilon}^{*}d\lambda_{\varepsilon}=d\lambda
. Indeed, the diffeomorphism is defined by the differential equation
\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\psi_{\varepsilon}=X_{\varepsilon}\circ\psi_{\varepsilon}
, where the vector field
X_{\varepsilon}
is solved from
\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\lambda_{\varepsilon}=-\iota_{X_{\varepsilon}}d\lambda_{\varepsilon}
by the nondegenderacy of
d\lambda_{\varepsilon}
. The equation
\psi_{\varepsilon}^{*}d\lambda_{\varepsilon}=d\lambda
is verified by

0=\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\psi_{\varepsilon}^{*}d\lambda_{\varepsilon}=\psi_{\varepsilon}^{*}(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}d\lambda_{\varepsilon}+d\iota_{X_{\varepsilon}}d\lambda_{\varepsilon}+\iota_{X_{\varepsilon}}dd\lambda_{\varepsilon}).

The diffeomorphism
\psi_{\varepsilon}
is identity outside a small neighborhood of
\partial E_{a}
since
h
is compactly supported. Denote
\Sigma=\psi_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\partial E_{a})
. Applying
\psi_{\varepsilon}^{*}
to the equation
\iota_{R_{\varepsilon}}d\lambda_{\varepsilon}=(1+g_{\varepsilon})dH
, we get

\iota_{D\psi_{\varepsilon}^{-1}R_{\varepsilon}}d\lambda=d\psi^{*}_{\varepsilon}((1+g_{\varepsilon})(H-1)).

Since
d\lambda
is the standard symplectic form on
\mathbb{R}^{2N}
, the equation implies that the Reeb flow
R_{\varepsilon}
on
\partial E_{a}
is conjugate to the Hamiltonian flow on
\Sigma
given by the Hamiltonian
\psi^{*}_{\varepsilon}((1+g_{\varepsilon})(H-1))
which is
O(\varepsilon)
close to
H-1
.

∎

On the other hand, the next proposition shows how to go from a Hamiltonian flow to a Reeb flow.

Proposition B.2
.

Suppose the Hamiltonian
H_{\varepsilon}
that is an
O(\varepsilon)
-perturbation of
H-1
, then there exists a function
f:\ \partial E_{a}\to\mathbb{R}
that is
O(\varepsilon)
close to 1, such that the Hamiltonian flow of
H_{\varepsilon}
on the zeroth energy level set is conjugate to the Reeb flow on the contact manifold
(\partial_{a}E,f^{2}\lambda).

Proof.

We consider the zeroth energy level set
\Sigma
of
H_{\varepsilon}
. By the implicit function theorem, it is known that
\Sigma
that is an
O(\varepsilon)
-perturbation of
\partial E_{a}
. So we write
\Sigma
as a radial graph over
\partial E_{a}
, i.e.
\Sigma=\{f(z)z\ |\ z\in\partial E_{a}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2N}\}
, which induces a diffeomorphism
\psi:\partial E_{a}\to\Sigma
via
z\mapsto f(z)z
, where
f
is
O(\varepsilon)
close to 1. Noting that
\lambda_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\omega_{0}(z,\cdot)
where
\omega_{0}
is the standard symplectic form on
\mathbb{R}^{2N}
, we have

\displaystyle\psi^{*}\lambda_{z}|_{\Sigma}(h)=\frac{1}{2}\psi^{*}\omega_{0}(f(z)z,(df(z)h)z+f(z)h)=f(z)^{2}\frac{1}{2}\omega_{0}(z,h)=f(z)^{2}\lambda_{z}|_{\partial E_{a}}(h).

∎
