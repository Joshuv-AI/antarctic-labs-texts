Entropy-Stable Model Reduction of One-Dimensional Hyperbolic Systems using Rational Quadratic Manifolds
R.B. Klein, B. Sanderse, P. Costa, R. Pecnik, R.A.W.M. Henkes

Abstract
In this work we propose a novel method to ensure important entropy inequalities are satisfied semi-discretely when constructing reduced order models (ROMs) on nonlinear reduced manifolds. We are in particular interested in ROMs of systems of nonlinear hyperbolic conservation laws. The so-called entropy stability property endows the semi-discrete ROMs with physically admissible behaviour. The method generalizes earlier results on entropy-stable ROMs constructed on linear spaces. The ROM works by evaluating the projected system on a well-chosen approximation of the state that ensures entropy stability. To ensure accuracy of the ROM after this approximation we locally enrich the tangent space of the reduced manifold with important quantities. Using numerical experiments on some well-known equations (the inviscid Burgers equation, shallow water equations and compressible Euler equations) we show the improved structure-preserving properties of our ROM compared to standard approaches and that our approximations have minimal impact on the accuracy of the ROM. We additionally generalize the recently proposed polynomial reduced manifolds to rational polynomial manifolds and show that this leads to an increase in accuracy for our experiments.

Preliminaries: Entropy inequality for conservation laws, entropy-stable FOM, linear ROM

2.1
Introducing the entropy inequality

We give a short introduction to the concept of entropy, some related concepts used in its analysis and its role in the theory of nonlinear conservation laws. We consider conservation laws in one spatial dimension that can be written as partial differential equations (PDE) of the form:

\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})}{\partial x}=0,

(2)

where
\Omega
is a spatial domain and
[0,T]
is a temporal domain with
T>0
. Furthermore
\boldsymbol{u}:\Omega\times(0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the solution function,
\boldsymbol{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the nonlinear flux function,
n\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of conserved quantities and
x\in\Omega
and
t\in[0,T]
are the spatial coordinate and time, respectively. To facilitate conservation statements and minimize the role of boundary conditions we will focus on periodic spatial domains
\Omega=\mathbb{T}([a,b])
with
b>a
and
b,a\in\mathbb{R}
in this research, here
\mathbb{T}
is the torus. The equations are complemented by a set of initial conditions
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(x)=\boldsymbol{u}(x,0)
. The conservation law (
2
) is referred to as hyperbolic when the Jacobian matrix
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{f}}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}
is diagonalizable with real eigenvalues for all physically relevant
\boldsymbol{u}

[
82
]
. In many cases, the solutions of physically relevant hyperbolic conservation laws also satisfy
additional
conservation laws of the form:

\frac{\partial s(\boldsymbol{u})}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})}{\partial x}=0,

(3)

where the function
s:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called the entropy function which is defined to be convex
2
2

2

A function
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is convex if its Hessian,
\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}^{2}}(\boldsymbol{u})
, is positive definite for all
\boldsymbol{u}
.
and
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called the entropy flux. In particular, such an additional conservation law exists if the compatibility relation:

\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{f}}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}^{T},

(4)

is satisfied. Here,
\boldsymbol{\eta}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n},\hbox{ }\boldsymbol{u}\mapsto\frac{\partial s}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{u})
is the gradient of the entropy function
s
with respect to
\boldsymbol{u}
. This mapping is injective due to the convexity of
s
and hence can be inverted. A pair
(s,\mathcal{F})
satisfying the compatibility relation (
4
) is called an entropy pair of (
2
).

It is well-known that solutions
\boldsymbol{u}
to (
2
) can develop discontinuities in finite time for smooth
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}

[
40
,
82
,
81
]
. When this occurs the solution
\boldsymbol{u}
is said to contain a shock or contact discontinuity depending on the behaviour of the discontinuity
[
82
]
. In this case both formulation (
2
) and the manipulations to obtain (
3
) are no longer valid. We must therefore consider (
2
) in a weak sense to retain a notion of solutions. This weak form of the conservation law is obtained by integrating against a space of smooth test functions
\boldsymbol{v}:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
with compact support i.e.
\boldsymbol{v}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega\times[0,\infty))
and transferring all derivatives to these test functions to obtain:

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial t}+f(\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial x}dxdt+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}(x,0)dx=0,

(5)

on periodic
\Omega
. Note that this expression is valid even for discontinuous
\boldsymbol{u}
and that any smooth
\boldsymbol{u}
satisfying the strong form (
2
) also satisfies this weak form (
5
)
[
34
,
40
]
. However, weakening the notion of a solution like this comes at the cost that it does not necessarily yield unique solutions (examples of such cases may be found in
[
40
,
81
,
34
,
82
]
). Out of all weak solutions, i.e. solutions to (
5
), those of physical interest are the ones
satisfying:

\frac{\partial s(\boldsymbol{u})}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})}{\partial x}\leq 0,

(6)

in the sense of distributions, with equality for smooth
\boldsymbol{u}
following (
3
) and inequality for solutions containing shocks. This inequality arises from considering limits of regularized conservation laws, more details can be found in
[
82
]
. For scalar conservation laws Kruzkov
[
74
]
established that weak solutions satisfying (
6
) are unique, but for systems uniqueness is not yet completely established
[
12
]
. We can define the total entropy functional as:

\mathcal{S}[\boldsymbol{u}]:=\int_{\Omega}s(\boldsymbol{u})dx.

(7)

Defining appropriate sequences of test functions and taking limits
[
83
,
34
]
, it can be shown that the estimate:

\frac{d\mathcal{S}[\boldsymbol{u}]}{dt}\leq 0,

(8)

follows from (
6
) on periodic
\Omega
. This inequality will be the main interest of this paper.

2.2
Entropy stable spatial discretization (FOM)

We will discretize the conservation law (
2
) with a finite volume method (FVM) based on flux-differencing

Definition 1

(Entropy-conservative numerical flux)
.

An entropy-conservative two-point numerical flux
\boldsymbol{f}^{*}_{h}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a numerical two-point flux satisfying:

1.

consistency:
\boldsymbol{f}^{*}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})
;

2.

symmetry:
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u}_{l},\boldsymbol{u}_{r})=\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u}_{r},\boldsymbol{u}_{l})
;

3.

entropy conservation:
(\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u}_{l})-\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u}_{r}))^{T}\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u}_{l},\boldsymbol{u}_{r})=\psi(\boldsymbol{u}_{l})-\psi(\boldsymbol{u}_{r})
.

The entropy-dissipative fluxes
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
are now constructed from entropy-conservative fluxes
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{*}
by adding (possibly solution dependent) entropy dissipation operators like:

\boldsymbol{f}_{i+1/2}:=\boldsymbol{f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i+1},\boldsymbol{u}_{i},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})=\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i+1},\boldsymbol{u}_{i})-\boldsymbol{D}_{i+1/2}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\Delta\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i+1/2}

(10)

with
\boldsymbol{D}_{i+1/2}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
so that
\boldsymbol{D}_{i+1/2}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})
is symmetric positive semi-definite (SPSD) for any
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}
(
\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
and
\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
are the convex sets of symmetric positive definite and symmetric positive semi-definite
n\times n
matrices, respectively). Here,
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}
is the numerical solution vector on the whole grid to be defined in what follows (this is required for higher order reconstructions like in e.g.
[
44
]
). Additionally, we have defined
\Delta\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i+1/2}:=\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i+1})-\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i})
. We will refrain from denoting explicitly the dependence on
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
in the third argument of
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}
and simply write
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i+1},\boldsymbol{u}_{i})
for (
10
).

) with flux (

where

9

\bar{\Delta}_{v}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&-1\\
-1&1&0&0\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&0&-1&1\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},\quad\bar{\Delta}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&1&0&0\\

\bar{\Delta}_{v}=-\bar{\Delta}_{i}^{T},

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{dS_{h}[\boldsymbol{u}_{h}]}{dt}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}^{T}\Omega_{h}\frac{d\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}^{T}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}^{T}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{D}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\Delta_{i}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}

\boldsymbol{D}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})
(and thus of
\Delta_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{D}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\Delta_{i}
) and the zero column sum of
\bar{\Delta}_{i}
. Clearly, in case no entropy dissipation is added in the numerical flux (
10
), equation (
15
) reduces to

\frac{dS_{h}[\boldsymbol{u}_{h}]}{dt}=0.

We note that the inequality (
15
) allows for formal
L^{p}
-stability statements
[
115
,
34
,
44
]
.

2.3
The entropy-stable linear ROM of Chan
[
27
]

The main aim of this work is to propose reduced order models (ROMs) that are a nonlinear generalization of the entropy stable ROM of Chan
[
27
]
. To highlight key conceptual differences between the ROM in
[
27
]
and ours, and to introduce the ROM methodology, we will briefly discuss the elements of Chan’s ROM leading to its entropy stability. Classical reduced order models including
[
27
]
make the assumption that the evolution of
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)
can be accurately approximated with elements from a linear space
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}
where
\text{dim}(\mathcal{V}):=r\ll N_{h}
so that
\mathcal{V}
can be referred to as low-dimensional
[
73
,
107
,
69
,
68
,
10
,
24
,
51
,
23
]
. Classically, the subspace
\mathcal{V}
is constructed using a truncated proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) based on snapshot data collected in a matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times n_{s}}
with
n_{s}\in\mathbb{N}
the number of snapshots
[
109
,
73
]
.
The construction of the ROM in
[
27
]
starts by defining the approximation
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\approx\boldsymbol{u}_{r}:=\Phi\boldsymbol{a}
with
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
being generalized coordinates in
\mathcal{V}
relative to the basis
\Phi
. Here, we assume
\Phi
is orthogonal in the
\Omega_{h}
-weighted inner product, i.e.
\phi_{i}^{T}\Omega_{h}\phi_{j}=\delta_{ij}
with
\phi_{i}
the
i
-th column of
\Phi
and
\delta_{ij}
the Kronecker delta function. Then, the approximation is substituted in (
13
) introducing a semi-discrete residual
which is set orthogonal to
\mathcal{V}
by solving the Galerkin projected system:

\frac{d\boldsymbol{a}}{dt}+\Phi^{T}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{f}_{r}^{*}(\boldsymbol{a})=\Phi^{T}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{D}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a})\Delta_{i}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a}),

(16)

with
\boldsymbol{f}_{r}^{*}(\boldsymbol{a}):=\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{*}(\Phi\boldsymbol{a})
,
\boldsymbol{D}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a}):=\boldsymbol{D}_{h}(\Phi\boldsymbol{a})
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a}):=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}(\Phi\boldsymbol{a})
. Equation (
16
) defines a (linear) POD-Galerkin ROM
[
73
,
107
,
69
,
68
,
10
]
.

Similar to the FOM case, see equation (
14
), we can evaluate the evolution of the ROM total entropy. The reduced total entropy functional is defined as:

S_{r}[\boldsymbol{a}]:=S_{h}[\Phi\boldsymbol{a}]=\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\bar{\Omega}_{h}\boldsymbol{s}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a}),

(17)

with
\boldsymbol{s}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a}):=\boldsymbol{s}_{h}(\Phi\boldsymbol{a})
. The evolution of the reduced total entropy is:

\frac{dS_{r}[\boldsymbol{a}]}{dt}=\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\bar{\Omega}_{h}\frac{d\boldsymbol{s}_{r}}{dt}=\sum_{i}\Delta x_{i}\boldsymbol{\eta}(\Phi_{i}\boldsymbol{a})^{T}\Phi_{i}\frac{d\boldsymbol{a}}{dt}=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}^{T}\Omega_{h}\Phi\frac{d\boldsymbol{a}}{dt},

with
\Phi_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
the rows of
\Phi
approximating values in cell
i
. The entropy evolution of (
16
) is:

\displaystyle\frac{dS_{r}[\boldsymbol{a}]}{dt}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}^{T}\Omega_{h}\Phi\frac{d\boldsymbol{a}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}^{T}\Omega_{h}\Phi\Phi^{T}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{f}_{r}^{*}(\boldsymbol{a})+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}^{T}\Omega_{h}\Phi\Phi^{T}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{D}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a})\Delta_{i}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}

\displaystyle=-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}^{T}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{f}_{r}^{*}(\boldsymbol{a})+\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}^{T}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{D}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a})\Delta_{i}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r},

where, since
\Phi\Phi^{T}\Omega_{h}
defines an
\Omega_{h}
-orthogonal projection operator
[
4
]
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}:=\Phi\Phi^{T}\Omega_{h}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}
are the so-called projected entropy variables. It is unclear whether this expression is bounded. To solve this, Chan
[
27
]
proposes a technique used earlier in DG finite element literature
[
95
,
26
,
28
]
. Namely, the discretization is not evaluated at
\Phi\boldsymbol{a}
but at the entropy projected state:

\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r}:=\boldsymbol{u}(\Phi\Phi^{T}\Omega_{h}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r})=\boldsymbol{u}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}),

where we have defined
\boldsymbol{u}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}\mapsto\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
for notational convenience. Recall that the mapping
\boldsymbol{u}
is indeed available since
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is injective. In this case we have:

\displaystyle\frac{dS_{r}[\boldsymbol{a}]}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}^{T}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}))+\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}^{T}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{D}_{h}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r})\Delta_{i}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}

\displaystyle=(\Delta_{i}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}))-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}^{T}\Delta_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{D}_{h}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r})\Delta_{i}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\bar{\Delta}_{i}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{r}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}^{T}\Delta_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{D}_{h}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r})\Delta_{i}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}

\displaystyle=0-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}^{T}\Delta_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{D}_{h}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r})\Delta_{i}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}

\displaystyle\leq 0,

(18)

so that we re-obtain an entropy estimate that mimics the FOM estimate (
15
). Here,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{r}
is the entropy flux potential vector evaluated at the entropy projected state. A key difference with the DG literature
[
95
,
26
,
28
]
is that in the ROM case the basis
\Phi
is only constructed to resolve solutions present in the snapshot matrix
X
, whereas in DG the trial basis is able to approximate a larger subspace of the relevant PDE function spaces. As a result, the DG trial basis can resolve the entropy variables well, but this may not be the case for the reduced basis
\Phi
. To address this issue,
[
27
]
builds the basis
\Phi
from a set of augmented snapshots given (with some abuse of notation) by:

\tilde{X}=[X,\boldsymbol{\eta}(X)],

so that the projection of the entropy variables on the basis
\Phi
is close to the identity. As we will explain in section
3.3
, for our proposed nonlinear manifold ROMs, such a construction is not sufficient, and a new tangent space enrichment technique will be proposed to ensure the accuracy of the entropy projection.

3
An entropy-stable nonlinear manifold Galerkin ROM

The solution manifolds of many hyperbolic conservation laws (
2
) have slow Kolmogorov
n
-width decay (
1
). Hence, approximations using linear subspaces as in
[
27
]
may require very large reduced space dimensions
r
before they become accurate. This comes at the cost of their efficiency. For this reason ROMs built on nonlinear spaces endowed (at least locally) with a manifold structure have become a topic of interest
[
105
,
106
,
79
,
111
,
18
,
19
,
9
,
8
,
121
,
31
,
72
,
37
,
47
,
66
]
. To address the shortcomings of the linear subspaces employed in
[
27
]
we will generalize this method to nonlinear reduced spaces, while keeping the entropy-stability property. We will be interested specifically in nonlinear subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}
endowed with some inner product, instead of any abstract space. Therefore we will not be very rigorous about our use of the word manifold, following predominantly the treatise of
[
79
]
and standard multivariable calculus interpretations. For a rigorous treatment we suggest consulting the recent preprint
[
19
]
. We will give a brief description of nonlinear manifold ROMs and then propose our generalization of
[
27
]
.

3.1
Manifold Galerkin model reduction

In constructing ROMs on nonlinear manifolds we make the assumption that for any
t\in[0,T]
there are points
\boldsymbol{u}_{r}(t)
on a low-dimensional submanifold
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}
that accurately approximate
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)
. Here, we denote
r:=\text{dim}(\mathcal{M})
and the low-dimensionality of
\mathcal{M}
implies that
r\ll N_{h}
. We will refer to the submanifold
\mathcal{M}
as the reduced manifold. Instead of the classical affine reduced space parameterization seen in the previous section we will use nonlinear manifold parameterizations given as:

\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)\approx\boldsymbol{u}_{r}(t):=\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a}(t))\in\mathcal{M},

(19)

where:

\boldsymbol{\varphi}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}},

(20)

is assumed to be a smooth nonlinear injective function - at least when restricted to some subset
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r}
of interest where the ROM will be well-defined. This means that
\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\mathbb{R}^{r})=\mathcal{M}
with a Jacobian
\boldsymbol{J}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times r},\hbox{ }\boldsymbol{a}\mapsto\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\varphi}}{\partial\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{a})
of full rank for any
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r}
, where
\boldsymbol{a}:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r}
are generalized coordinates on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
. The function
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
may be obtained in many ways: some examples are quadratic approximations
[
8
,
111
,
17
,
47
,
66
]
or neural networks
[
105
,
106
,
79
,
72
,
121
,
37
,
9
,
31
]
. We will propose a new method based on rational polynomials in section
4
. In this section, we develop an entropy-stable ROM which is agnostic of the choice for
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
.

To construct a ROM we substitute (
19
) into the FOM discretization (
13
) so that after applying the chain rule we find the residual:

\boldsymbol{r}\left(\frac{d\boldsymbol{a}}{dt},\boldsymbol{a}\right):=\boldsymbol{J}\frac{d\boldsymbol{a}}{dt}+\Omega_{h}^{-1}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{f}_{r}^{*}(\boldsymbol{a})-\Omega_{h}^{-1}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{D}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a})\Delta_{i}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a}),

where we define
\boldsymbol{f}_{r}^{*}(\boldsymbol{a}):=\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a}))
,
\boldsymbol{D}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a}):=\boldsymbol{D}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a}))
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a}):=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a}))
. The ROM is defined by minimizing this residual in the
\Omega_{h}
-norm for
\frac{d\boldsymbol{a}}{dt}
given some
\boldsymbol{a}
, this results in the ROM:

(\boldsymbol{J}^{T}\Omega_{h}\boldsymbol{J})\frac{d\boldsymbol{a}}{dt}+\boldsymbol{J}^{T}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{f}_{r}^{*}(\boldsymbol{a})=\boldsymbol{J}^{T}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{D}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a})\Delta_{i}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a}),

which is indeed well-defined for
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r}
since the Jacobian
\boldsymbol{J}
is assumed to be of full-rank on the subset
\mathcal{A}
, making the mass matrix
(\boldsymbol{J}^{T}\Omega_{h}\boldsymbol{J})
invertible
[
4
]
. In this nonlinear case the ROM is given by the coefficients of an orthogonal projection of the FOM on the tangent space of
\mathcal{M}
defined by
T_{u_{r}}\mathcal{M}:=\text{span}(\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{a}))
with
\boldsymbol{a}
such that
\boldsymbol{u}_{r}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a})
. This orthogonal projection is carried out using the
\Omega_{h}
-weighted Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse
\boldsymbol{J}^{\dagger}:=(\boldsymbol{J}^{T}\Omega_{h}\boldsymbol{J})^{-1}\boldsymbol{J}^{T}\Omega_{h}
. Constructing a ROM by projecting the FOM on the tangent space instead of the reduced manifold itself will result in key differences in our approach compared to the linear case outlined in
[
27
]
: in contrast to the linear case where
\boldsymbol{J}=\Phi
,
T_{u_{r}}\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}
are no longer the same space. We introduce
\boldsymbol{J}^{+}=\boldsymbol{J}^{\dagger}\Omega_{h}^{-1}=(\boldsymbol{J}^{T}\Omega_{h}\boldsymbol{J})^{-1}\boldsymbol{J}^{T}
and write the ROM in compact form:

\frac{d\boldsymbol{a}}{dt}+\boldsymbol{J}^{+}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{f}_{r}^{*}(\boldsymbol{a})=\boldsymbol{J}^{+}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{D}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a})\Delta_{i}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a}).

(21)

Remark 1
.

The choice of inner-product spaces for ROMs of hyperbolic systems has recently come into question
[
94
]
. Indeed when
n>1
the norm
||\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{\alpha}||_{\Omega_{h}}
for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
is dimensionally inconsistent in general. It is shown in
[
94
,
69
]
that dimensionally consistent inner products that are more appropriate in some sense can improve robustness of the ROMs. For our approach however it will be important that the ROM is calculated with the same inner product as used to calculate
\frac{dS_{h}}{dt}
. Therefore, we will only deal with nondimensionalized conservation laws. Alternatively, our results can also be applied at an equation-by-equation basis at the cost of potentially introducing a larger number of generalized coordinates.

Remark 2
.

A popular approach to construct nonlinear manifold ROMs is the least squares Petrov-Galerkin (LSPG) method
[
106
,
79
]
. Using this method a fully discrete residual is minimized. We have chosen not to use this method because we want to use the structure of our entropy stable FOM discretization in constructing entropy stable ROMs. The fully discrete residual minimization approach of LSPG makes it more difficult to apply this structure.

3.2
An entropy stable nonlinear manifold Galerkin ROM

The reduced total entropy functional of the nonlinear manifold ROM is now defined similarly to the linear case (equation (
17
)) as:

S_{r}[\boldsymbol{a}]:=S_{h}[\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a})]=\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\bar{\Omega}_{h}\boldsymbol{s}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a}),

with
\boldsymbol{s}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a}):=\boldsymbol{s}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a}))
. The reduced total entropy evolution is given by:

\frac{dS_{r}[\boldsymbol{a}]}{dt}=\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\bar{\Omega}_{h}\frac{d\boldsymbol{s}_{r}}{dt}=\sum_{i}\Delta x_{i}\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}(\boldsymbol{a}))^{T}\boldsymbol{J}_{i}\frac{d\boldsymbol{a}}{dt}=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}^{T}\Omega_{h}\boldsymbol{J}\frac{d\boldsymbol{a}}{dt},

(22)

where
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the ROM approximation of the conserved variables in the
i
-th cell and
\boldsymbol{J}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
is the Jacobian matrix of
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}
evaluated at
\boldsymbol{a}
. Using (
21
), the entropy evolution of the nonlinear manifold Galerkin ROM is:

\displaystyle\frac{dS_{r}[\boldsymbol{a}]}{dt}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}^{T}\Omega_{h}\boldsymbol{J}\frac{d\boldsymbol{a}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}^{T}\Omega_{h}\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{J}^{+}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{f}_{r}^{*}(\boldsymbol{a})+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}^{T}\Omega_{h}\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{J}^{+}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{D}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a})\Delta_{i}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a})

\displaystyle=-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}^{T}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{f}_{r}^{*}(\boldsymbol{a})+\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}^{T}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{D}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a})\Delta_{i}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}(\boldsymbol{a}),

(23)

where the projected entropy variables are defined as
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}=(\Omega_{h}\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{J}^{+})^{T}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}=\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{J}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}
, where
\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{J}^{\dagger}
is an
\Omega_{h}
-orthogonal projection on
T_{u_{r}}\mathcal{M}
. It follows from (
23
) that the reduced total entropy evolution satisfies an equation that is quite similar to the total entropy evolution of the FOM. However, instead of the actual entropy variables
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}
evaluated at the point
\boldsymbol{u}_{r}
on the reduced manifold
\mathcal{M}
, the inner product is taken with the projected entropy variables
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}
. We would like to use the entropy conservation condition at this point to show that the inner product in (
23
) is zero, but this does not hold because
\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u}_{r})\neq\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}
in general. To solve this, we can instead use the invertibility of the entropy variables to find for what state
\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}
we do have
\boldsymbol{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r})=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}
. If we evaluate our flux at this state instead we can invoke the entropy conservation condition of the numerical flux to complete the proof of entropy conservation (or stability). This is exactly what is done in the linear setting in
[
27
]
and leads to our main novelty.

We now present the main novelty of our work. We introduce a novel nonlinear manifold generalization of the linear entropy projection of
[
27
]
. It is given by:

\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r}=\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{J}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r})=\boldsymbol{u}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}),

(24)

where the entropy variables
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}
evaluated at the ROM state
\boldsymbol{u}_{r}
are projected onto the tangent space
T_{u_{r}}\mathcal{M}
instead of the reduced space itself. We note that projecting the entropy variables on the tangent space
T_{u_{r}}\mathcal{M}
is a rather natural operation as the entropy variables can be interpreted as the gradient vector field of the entropy functional
S_{h}
and thus as tangent vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}
. The vector
\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r}
is the entropy projected state of the ROM. Carrying out this entropy projection on the tangent space is necessary as the projected entropy variables
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}
appearing in the reduced total entropy evolution equation also are projected on the tangent space.

A potential issue of our proposed form (
24
) is that the difference between the projected entropy variables
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}
and the original entropy variables
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}
can be very large. Namely, in similar fashion to the naively constructed linear spaces spanned by
\Phi
described in
subsection 2.3
and
[
27
]
, for arbitrary
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
the entropy variables
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}
may not be well-resolved by the columns of
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{a})
and thus by the
\Omega_{h}
-orthogonal projection in (
24
). This will very likely cause problems with accuracy of the ROM and the mapping
\boldsymbol{u}
might not even be well-defined at
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}
. We will propose a novel method to assure the difference between
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}
remains small in the following section.

First, we continue constructing an entropy stable nonlinear manifold ROM and perform a Galerkin projection of the FOM at the entropy projected state
\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r}
. Doing so we obtain the following ROM:

\frac{d\boldsymbol{a}}{dt}+\boldsymbol{J}^{+}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{*}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r})=\boldsymbol{J}^{+}\Delta_{v}D_{h}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r})\Delta_{i}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r},

(25)

where we used
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}))
. Here, the Jacobian matrix is still evaluated at the reduced coordinate
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
such that
\boldsymbol{u}_{r}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a})
i.e. the non-projected state. It can be seen that the reduced total entropy evolution is bounded for this ROM since:

\displaystyle\frac{dS_{r}[\boldsymbol{a}]}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}^{T}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}))+\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}^{T}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{D}_{h}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r})\Delta_{i}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}

\displaystyle=(\Delta_{i}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}))-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}^{T}\Delta_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{D}_{h}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r})\Delta_{i}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\bar{\Delta}_{i}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{r}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}^{T}\Delta_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{D}_{h}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r})\Delta_{i}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}

\displaystyle=0-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}^{T}\Delta_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{D}_{h}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r})\Delta_{i}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}

\displaystyle\leq 0,

(26)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{r}=\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r})
is the entropy flux potential of the entropy projected state and we were allowed to invoke the entropy conservation condition of the numerical fluxes. Clearly, we have:

\frac{dS_{r}[\boldsymbol{a}]}{dt}=0,

when no entropy dissipation is present. Note that this approach exactly recovers the linear approach of
[
27
]
when
\boldsymbol{J}=\Phi
, making it a proper generalization. Thus, by changing the state at which the FOM is evaluated and projected to the entropy projected state
\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r}
, correct total entropy evolution estimates can be recovered. We added a visualization of the ROM construction with an entropy projection in
Figure 2
.

Figure 2
:
A visualization of the ROM construction. The entropy variables
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}
are projected on the tangent space
T_{u_{r}}\mathcal{M}
to obtain
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}
. A new state
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
(not necessarily on the reduced manifold) is found with entropy variables
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}
such that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}
. We set
\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r}=\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
and project
-\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{f}_{h}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r})
orthogonally on the tangent space to complete the ROM.

Remark 3
.

To make relation (
26
) hold in a fully-discrete setting for Galerkin ROMs, entropy stable time integration is necessary. This is not trivial as most methods to satisfy entropy inequalities exactly during time integration require convexity of the entropy for existence results
[
102
,
118
,
70
]
. Although this is the case for
S_{h}
,
S_{r}
is not necessarily convex in the generalized coordinates
\boldsymbol{a}
unless
\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a})
is affine i.e.
\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a})=\Phi\boldsymbol{a}+\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
for some constant
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}
and
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times r}
.
In this work, we focus on semi-discrete entropy stability and leave fully discrete entropy-stable ROMs as a suggestion for future work. In numerical experiments we use sufficiently small time steps to make entropy errors coming from the time integration negligible, for details see
subsection 5.2
.

Remark 4
.

The entropy conservative hyper-reduction method proposed in
[
27
]
does not generalize to nonlinear spaces as it relies on precomputation of compositions of linear operators. In the nonlinear case precomputation is not possible due to the changing tangent space. An entropy conservative hyper-reduction method suitable for nonlinear reduced spaces is also a suggestion for future work.

3.3
Tangent space enrichment

To arrive at the correct entropy estimate (
26
) we carried out the entropy projection (
24
). Though the evolution of the entropy then satisfies a correct estimate, it is not clear whether the ROM solution itself remains accurate. Particularly, the difference between the entropy projected state (
24
) and the original state (
19
) can be very large. To see this we consider the entropy projection error:

\varepsilon_{s}:=||\boldsymbol{u}_{r}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r}||_{\Omega_{h}}.

Assuming the mapping
\boldsymbol{u}
from entropy variables to conservative variables is sufficiently smooth, using the mean-value theorem we can bound this term as follows:

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{u}_{r}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r}||_{\Omega_{h}}

\displaystyle=||\boldsymbol{u}_{r}-\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{J}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r})||_{\Omega_{h}}

\displaystyle=||\boldsymbol{u}_{r}-\left[\boldsymbol{u}_{r}-\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})(I-\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{J}^{\dagger})\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}\right]||_{\Omega_{h}}\quad\theta_{i}\in[(\boldsymbol{u}_{r})_{i},(\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r})_{i}]\hbox{ }\forall i

\displaystyle\leq||\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})||_{\Omega_{h}}||(I-\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{J}^{\dagger})\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}||_{\Omega_{h}}

\displaystyle=||\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\boldsymbol{s}}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}^{2}}\right)^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})||_{\Omega_{h}}||(I-\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{J}^{\dagger})\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}||_{\Omega_{h}},

where we used the definition of the entropy variables in the last line and used the induced operator
\Omega_{h}
-norm for the Hessian matrix of the entropy function. It can be seen that there are two contributions to this bound. There is one based on the model, specifically on the Hessian of the entropy, and one given by the projection error of
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}
on the tangent space
T_{u_{r}}\mathcal{M}
. As long as the entropy is a convex function at the mean value
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, the contribution of the model-based term is bounded by a term involving the inverse of the smallest eigenvalue of the entropy Hessian. We have little influence over this term. Specifically, this term can be large when the entropy is close to being non-convex. We do have control over the projection error. The magnitude of this term is controlled by the choice of reduced space. For a general reduced space constructed to contain solution snapshots this term can be very large, since the columns of the Jacobian
\boldsymbol{J}
can be close to orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}
while the standard nonlinear manifold ROM (
21
) works fine. In the linear case, Chan
[
27
]
solved this problem by enriching the snapshot data to construct
\Phi
with snapshots of the entropy variables. This lowered the projection error contribution to the bound on
\varepsilon_{s}
since
\boldsymbol{J}=\Phi
in this case. However, for the general nonlinear case,
\boldsymbol{J}
is not the same as the reduced space itself and we can no longer construct our reduced space to also contain the entropy variables to keep the projection error low. Instead, we need a different approach and therefore we propose a novel method to which we refer as
tangent space enrichment
.

The key idea of tangent space enrichment is to construct an
r+1
-dimensional manifold
\hat{\mathcal{M}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}
from the original
r
-dimensional manifold
\mathcal{M}
by a ‘lifting’ operation. Consequently, we use this new manifold for the ROM instead. This lifting operation is defined so that the original manifold
\mathcal{M}
is a subset of the lifted manifold
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
, i.e.
\mathcal{M}\subset\hat{\mathcal{M}}
. Most importantly however, for all points
\boldsymbol{u}_{r}\in\mathcal{M}\subset\hat{\mathcal{M}}

the lifting operation is constructed such that

\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}\in T_{u_{r}}\hat{\mathcal{M}}
. This means that the entropy variable projection error is precisely zero at the points contained in the
old
manifold when projecting
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}
on the tangent space of the
new
manifold. This assures that the entropy projection is accurate for the points
\boldsymbol{u}_{r}\in\mathcal{M}\subset\hat{\mathcal{M}}
when using tangent space enrichment.

We motivate this approach over a more straightforward generalization of Chan’s snapshots enrichment
[
27
]
method by the following. Nonlinear reduced spaces are often constructed iteratively by minimizing some loss function. A nonlinear version of Chan’s enrichment method would require, for a given
\boldsymbol{a}
, fitting
\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a})
to a snapshot
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
whilst the Jacobian
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{a})
has a low entropy projection error
\varepsilon_{s}
. The construction of
\mathcal{M}
would therefore require including terms based on
\boldsymbol{J}
in the loss function. This can be very expensive and generally will not exactly embed the entropy variables in the tangent space at the appropriate points. As will be discussed, our method requires no extra effort in constructing
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
and contrary to the straightforward approach exactly enriches the tangent spaces with the correct entropy variables.

Our novel tangent space enrichment is defined by the following parameterization for
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{a},\alpha)=\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a})+\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a}))\alpha,

(27)

here
\boldsymbol{\varphi}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}
is the parameterization of the original manifold
\mathcal{M}
,
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
are the
r
reduced coordinates associated to the original parameterization
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is the
r+1
-th reduced coordinate associated to the lifting operation. As in remark
1
, here we see another reason for the importance of non-dimensionalization. Namely, on dimensional grounds the expression (
27
) does not make sense if
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}
are not suitably normalized.

The new parameterization can be interpreted as follows. Given any point
\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a})\in\mathcal{M}
we generate new points
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r}\in\hat{\mathcal{M}}
by lifting the new points from the point
\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a})
in the direction of
\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a}))
by a distance
||\hat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{a},\alpha)-\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a})||=||\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a}))||\cdot|\alpha|
. Note that at
\alpha=0
we do not lift the point at all, resulting in a point at
\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a})
; in other words, the original manifold
\mathcal{M}
is the set
\hat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\mathbb{R}^{r},\alpha=0)
. The lifting operation is visualized in
Figure 3
. The Jacobian matrix of the new parameterization (
27
), whose columns span the tangent space
T_{\hat{u}_{r}}\hat{\mathcal{M}}
of the lifted manifold
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
at the point
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r}\in\hat{\mathcal{M}}
, is given by:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{J}}(\boldsymbol{a},\alpha)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{a}}&\frac{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}}{\partial\alpha}\end{bmatrix}

(28)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\left(I+\alpha\hbox{ }\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a}))\right)\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{a})&\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a}))\end{bmatrix},

where
I\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times N_{h}}
is the identity matrix and
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a}))=\frac{\partial^{2}\boldsymbol{s}_{h}}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}^{2}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a}))\succeq 0
is a sparse SPSD
2n-1
-diagonal matrix containing components of the Hessian of the local entropy value with respect to the solution on each diagonal. Note that the derivative with respect to the
r+1
-th tangent space enrichment coordinate
\alpha
is exactly
\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a}))
. Furthermore, on the old manifold
\mathcal{M}
associated to
\alpha=0
the matrix
\frac{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{a}}
is equal the original Jacobian
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{a})
. At the points
\hat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{a},\alpha=0)=\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a})
we have thus exactly enriched the tangent space with the entropy variables
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}=\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a}))
at those points. This is the direct result of the lifting operation. This can be seen from the enriched parameterization (
27
). Lifting a point from
\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a})
by changing
\alpha
while keeping
\boldsymbol{a}
constant, moves a point in the direction tangent to
\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a}))
. As a consequence
\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a}))
appears as a tangent vector in the enriched Jacobian (
28
).

is constructed by lifting points from

along lines in the direction of

Given the tangent space enrichment, the ROM is constructed in a similar fashion as in the previous section, but using the enriched lifted manifold
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
. The
r+1
-th tangent space enrichment coordinate
\alpha
is simply treated as an additional reduced coordinate. The ROM thus takes the form:

\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{a}\\
\alpha\end{bmatrix}+\hat{\boldsymbol{J}}^{+}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{*}(\hat{\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{r})=\hat{\boldsymbol{J}}^{+}\Delta_{v}\boldsymbol{D}_{h}(\hat{\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{r})\Delta_{i}\hat{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}_{r},

where:

\hat{\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{r}=\boldsymbol{u}(\hat{\boldsymbol{J}}\hat{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\dagger}\hat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{r}),

and
\hat{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}_{r}=\hat{\boldsymbol{J}}\hat{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\eta}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{a},\alpha))
. Since the proof of entropy stability for our ROM is independent of the manifold parameterization, this ROM is still entropy stable.

4
Rational polynomial manifolds

4.1
Background

In this article we are interested in systems that can exhibit strong spatial gradients that are moving in time. Many existing data compression methods for reduced manifold construction are not well suited for these types of systems. Linear data compression methods like proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) and general reduced basis methods fail because the moving gradients imply that the data is often of very high rank. This indicates that the data is not well represented in low-dimensional linear subspaces, which is the fundamental assumption of linear approaches. Nonlinear data compression methods offer a potential solution to this problem by instead compressing the highly nonlinear data on nonlinear reduced manifolds. In model reduction different parameterizations have become popular, in particular the decoder part of autoencoder neural networks
[
105
,
106
,
79
,
72
,
121
,
37
,
9
,
31
]
and multivariate quadratic polynomials
[
8
,
111
,
17
,
47
,
66
]
. However, in the vicinity of strong gradients these nonlinear methods can suffer from oscillations
[
47
]
. These oscillations may be difficult or impossible to remove. This is a problem for Galerkin projection-based ROMs which can be sensitive to errors in the solution compression
[
23
]
. To accurately assess the performance of our novel entropy stable manifold Galerkin ROM there is thus a need for nonlinear data compression methods that are more capable of dealing with large and moving spatial gradients, particularly without significant oscillations. Recently, neural networks with discontinuous activation functions have been proposed
[
36
]
, however training these networks can be cumbersome. Furthermore, registration based approaches
[
116
,
89
]
have been very effective, but have not yet been applied in the context of nonlinear manifold ROMs similar to ours. In this research we will propose a novel reduced manifold parameterization method based on rational polynomials, that is far less oscillatory around strong spatial gradients than the previously mentioned methods (neural networks and quadratic approaches), but is still equally interpretable as the recently proposed quadratic manifolds.

4.2
Pole-free rational quadratic manifolds

We now give a description of rational polynomial manifolds. A rational polynomial manifold is the element-wise ratio of two polynomial manifolds:

\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a})=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{p_{\text{num}}}\boldsymbol{H}^{i}:\boldsymbol{a}^{\otimes i}+\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{ref}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{p_{\text{den}}}\boldsymbol{G}^{i}:\boldsymbol{a}^{\otimes i}+\boldsymbol{1}},

(29)

here,
\boldsymbol{H}^{i},\boldsymbol{G}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times r\times...\times r}
are
(i+1)^{\text{th}}
-order tensors with the first axis of size
N_{h}
and
i
axes of length
r
,
\boldsymbol{a}^{\otimes i}
is the
i
-fold outer product such that for example
(\boldsymbol{a}^{\otimes 3})_{ijk}=a_{i}a_{j}a_{k}
,
\boldsymbol{H}^{i}:\boldsymbol{a}^{\otimes i}
denotes summation of the components of
\boldsymbol{a}^{\otimes i}
and the components of slices along the first axis of
\boldsymbol{H}^{i}
, again as example
\left(\boldsymbol{H}^{3}:\boldsymbol{a}^{\otimes 3}\right)_{i}=\sum_{j,k,l=0}^{r-1}\left(\boldsymbol{H}^{3}\right)_{ijkl}a_{j}a_{k}a_{l}
. Furthermore, we have
\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{ref}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}
and we consider division of two vectors element-wise. The constant vector in the denominator has been set to one without loss of generality. The expression (
29
) generalizes polynomial manifolds
[
47
,
8
]
, in the sense that those are recovered by setting
p_{\text{den}}=0
. This shows that rational polynomial manifold encapsulate a larger class of functions than polynomial manifolds. By introducing a polynomial in the denominator and allowing it to rapidly and smoothly approach zero for small changes in
\boldsymbol{a}
we can obtain very fast and smooth increases in the function value of
\varphi
without oscillations. This gives us the opportunity to model steep gradients in the snapshot data which may be present in the form of advected shocks. Since higher-order tensors can become quite expensive to deal with, we restrict our attention to rational quadratic manifolds by setting
p_{\text{num}}=p_{\text{den}}=2
.

To compromise between efficiency and expressiveness we will take
p_{\text{num}}=p_{\text{den}}=2
. The
i
-th component of the vector-valued function output
\varphi_{i}(\boldsymbol{a})
can then be written as:

\varphi_{i}(\boldsymbol{a})=\frac{\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{H}_{i}^{2}\boldsymbol{a}+\boldsymbol{H}_{i}^{1}\boldsymbol{a}+(\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{ref}})_{i}}{\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{G}_{i}^{2}\boldsymbol{a}+\boldsymbol{G}_{i}^{1}\boldsymbol{a}+1},

(30)

where
\boldsymbol{H}_{i}^{2},\boldsymbol{G}_{i}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
are the
i
-th slices along the first axes of
\boldsymbol{H}^{2}
and
\boldsymbol{G}^{2}
, respectively and
\boldsymbol{H}_{i}^{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{i}^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times r}
are the
i
-th rows of
\boldsymbol{H}^{1}
and
\boldsymbol{G}^{1}
, respectively. Since the matrices are only used in quadratic forms, we can, without loss of generality, assume
\boldsymbol{H}_{i}^{2},\boldsymbol{G}_{i}^{2}
to be symmetric. An obvious concern with expressions of this form is the occurrence of spurious poles, i.e. unwanted division by zero. We avoid this issue for the case
p_{\text{num}}=p_{\text{den}}=2
and all
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
by constraining the quadratic form in the denominator to be positive semi-definite and setting the linear term to zero:

\boldsymbol{G}_{i}^{2}\succeq 0,\quad\boldsymbol{G}_{i}^{1}=0,\quad\forall i\in\{0,...,N_{h}\}.

It is easily seen that, in this case, the denominator is never less than 1. Consequently, spurious poles cannot occur for any real
\boldsymbol{a}
. Removing the linear term has not resulted in a significant loss in accuracy in our numerical experiments. The full
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
is then given as:

\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a})=\frac{\boldsymbol{H}^{2}:[\boldsymbol{a}\otimes\boldsymbol{a}]+\boldsymbol{H}^{1}\boldsymbol{a}+\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{ref}}}{\boldsymbol{G}:[\boldsymbol{a}\otimes\boldsymbol{a}]+\boldsymbol{1}},\quad\boldsymbol{G}_{i}\succeq 0\hbox{ }\forall i,

(31)

since there is no linear term in the denominator we write
\boldsymbol{G}
instead of
\boldsymbol{G}^{2}
. To construct manifold Galerkin ROMs we will require the Jacobian matrix of this expression - see equations (
21
) and (
28
). The Jacobian matrix is given by the following:

\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\varphi}}{\partial\boldsymbol{a}}=\frac{2\boldsymbol{H}^{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}+\boldsymbol{H}^{1}}{\left(\boldsymbol{G}:[\boldsymbol{a}\otimes\boldsymbol{a}]+\boldsymbol{1}\right)\otimes\boldsymbol{1}}-\left[\frac{\boldsymbol{H}^{2}:[\boldsymbol{a}\otimes\boldsymbol{a}]+\boldsymbol{H}^{1}\boldsymbol{a}+\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{ref}}}{\left(\boldsymbol{G}:[\boldsymbol{a}\otimes\boldsymbol{a}]+\boldsymbol{1}\right)^{2}}\otimes\boldsymbol{1}\right]\circ(2\boldsymbol{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}),

where the operation
2\boldsymbol{H}^{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times r}
indicates slice-wise matrix multiplication, i.e. for the
i
-th row it holds that
(2\boldsymbol{H}^{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{a})_{i}=2\boldsymbol{H}_{i}^{2}\boldsymbol{a}
, due to symmetry of
\boldsymbol{H}_{i}^{2},\boldsymbol{G}_{i}
the order of axes is not relevant, division of matrices is understood element-wise and
\circ
is the Hadamard matrix multiplication operator.

4.3
Manifold construction

We will determine the coefficient tensors in (
31
) from data. Like for quadratic manifolds
[
8
,
47
]
, it holds for rational quadratic manifolds that the coefficients in two different slices of the coefficient tensors are independent, as can be seen in (
30
). Consequently, the coefficients can be determined purely from the parametric and temporal behaviour of the data in the specific cell and solution variable associated to the
i
-th component
\varphi_{i}(\boldsymbol{a})
of
\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{a})
. The task of fitting a rational manifold thus reduces to fitting an expression (
30
) to data for each cell and solution variable.

In the spirit of quadratic manifolds we will compress the snapshot data defined by:

X=[\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t^{0}),\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t^{1}),...,\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t^{n_{s}-1})]\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times n_{s}},

where
n_{s}\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of snapshots, as the coefficients of their projection on the first
r
left singular vectors of
X
given in
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times r}
. Following this we define
A:=(\Phi^{T}X)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{s}\times r}
. We aim to find the coefficients
\boldsymbol{G}_{i},\boldsymbol{H}_{i}^{2},\boldsymbol{H}_{i}^{1},(\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{ref}})_{i}
such that for all
j=0,...,n_{s}-1
we have
\varphi_{i}(\boldsymbol{a}_{j})\approx X_{ij}
, where
\boldsymbol{a}_{j}
is the
j
-th row of
A
written as a column vector. Defining the
i
-th row of
X
as
\boldsymbol{y}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{s}}
, the optimization procedure for the coefficients
\boldsymbol{G}_{i},\boldsymbol{H}_{i}^{2},\boldsymbol{H}_{i}^{1},(\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{ref}})_{i}
is formulated as:

\boldsymbol{G}_{i},\boldsymbol{H}_{i}^{2},\boldsymbol{H}_{i}^{1},(\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{ref}})_{i}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{G}\in\mathbb{S}^{r}_{+},\boldsymbol{H}\in\mathbb{S}^{r},\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{r},u\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\left|\boldsymbol{y}^{i}-\frac{(A\boldsymbol{H}\circ A)\boldsymbol{1}+A\boldsymbol{h}+u}{(A\boldsymbol{G}\circ A)\boldsymbol{1}+1}\right|\right|^{2},

(32)

though the sets
\mathbb{S}^{r}_{+}
and
\mathbb{S}^{r}
are convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
the objective function is non-convex due to the division operation.

A popular approach is to linearize this nonlinear optimization problem
[
59
,
3
]
by multiplying with the denominator. This results in a convex semi-definite program which can be solved very efficiently with e.g. interior point methods. However, the optimum value of this linearized problem is generally not the same as the nonlinear problem (
32
). Iterative approaches that attempt to somehow refine the solution of the linearized problem and that can potentially be supplemented with our semi-definite constraint exist
[
59
,
3
,
53
]
. However, convergence to (local) minima of the nonlinear problem is generally not guaranteed, nor is convergence in general
[
112
,
60
]
. For this reason, we will stick to the fully nonlinear and expensive optimization problem (
32
). Nonetheless, we believe that the linearized approach holds significant promise and that it will be crucial for future work in order to scale the approach to larger meshes and datasets. Finally, we will implement the semi-definite constraint in this fully nonlinear setting by optimizing for the Cholesky decomposition of
\boldsymbol{G}_{i}=L_{i}L_{i}^{T}

[
4
]
:

L_{i},\boldsymbol{H}_{i}^{2},\boldsymbol{H}_{i}^{1},(\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{ref}})_{i}=\argmin_{L\in\mathbb{L}^{r},\boldsymbol{H}\in\mathbb{S}^{r},\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{r},u\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\left|\boldsymbol{y}^{i}-\frac{(A\boldsymbol{H}\circ A)\boldsymbol{1}+A\boldsymbol{h}+u}{(AL\circ AL)\boldsymbol{1}+1}\right|\right|^{2},

(33)

where
\mathbb{L}^{r}\subset\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
is the vector subspace of lower triangular
r\times r
matrices. As initial guess we can either use
L_{i-1},\boldsymbol{H}_{i-1}^{2},\boldsymbol{H}_{i-1}^{1},(\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{ref}})_{i-1}
if available and corresponding to the same solution variable, or otherwise simply vectors or tensors consisting of “ones". We will carry out the fitting procedure using the JAXFit package
[
58
]
for GPU-accelerated nonlinear least squares solutions.

5.2
Shallow water equations

This experiment will mainly focus on the entropy conservation and stability aspect of our proposed ROM on rational manifolds. We show that our novel entropy stable ROM satisfies the reduced total entropy estimate (
26
). To this end we will carry out experiments with the shallow water equations. We use the shallow water equations due to their nontrivial entropy function, which will be defined later, as compared to the Burgers equation. We will perform one experiment where discontinuities appear in the solution and one where the solution remains smooth during the time interval of interest. In the smooth case we can run the FOM and ROMs without entropy dissipation operators. As a result we can analyse the entropy conservation properties of the ROM. In the discontinuous case we will analyse the behaviour of the reduced entropy as compared to the FOM entropy.

In the following we briefly introduce the shallow water equations and the entropy stable numerical scheme used to obtain the FOM. The shallow water equations are given by:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{bmatrix}h\\
hu\end{bmatrix}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\begin{bmatrix}hu\\
hu^{2}+\frac{1}{2}gh^{2}\end{bmatrix}=0,

(35)

with conserved variables
h:\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
hu:\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
representing the local water column height and the momentum per unit mass, respectively. We collect the conserved variables in a vector
\boldsymbol{u}:=\left[h,hu\right]^{T}
. The constant
g\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the positive real gravitational acceleration, we assume the equations are normalized such that
g=3
, which gave challenging test cases for our spatial domain size and initial conditions. As continuous and discrete total entropy we take the common choice
[
43
]
:

\mathcal{S}[\boldsymbol{u}]=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{u_{2}^{2}}{u_{1}}+gu_{1}^{2}\right)dx,

leading to:

S_{h}[\boldsymbol{u}_{h}]=\sum_{i}\Delta x_{i}\frac{h_{i}u_{i}^{2}+gh_{i}^{2}}{2},

from which the reduced total entropy follows. This choice leads to the following entropy variables:

\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u})=\begin{bmatrix}gu_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{u_{2}}{u_{1}}\right)^{2}\\
\frac{u_{2}}{u_{1}}\end{bmatrix},

where
u_{1}=h
and
u_{2}=hu
, and inverse function:

\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{\eta})=\frac{2\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}^{2}}{2g}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\eta_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

An entropy conservative flux is given by
[
43
,
44
]
:

\boldsymbol{f}_{i+1/2}=\begin{bmatrix}\overline{h}_{i+1/2}\overline{u}_{i+1/2}\\
\overline{h}_{i+1/2}\cdot\overline{u}_{i+1/2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}g\overline{h^{2}}_{i+1/2}\end{bmatrix},

where
\overline{a}_{i+1/2}=\frac{1}{2}(a_{i+1}+a_{i})
indicates taking the average of neighbouring volume based quantities. As entropy dissipation operator we take the diffusion operators of Roe type (see
[
44
]
) with the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the flux Jacobian evaluated at the arithmetic average of neighbouring values. We obtain a second accurate entropy dissipation operator using the entropy stable total variation diminishing (TVD) reconstruction based on the minmod limiter (see
[
44
]
).

We will discretize (
35
) for both experiments on a domain
\Omega=\mathbb{T}([-L,L])
with
L=1
using a numerical grid consisting of 300 cells. Discretization in time will be done using the RK4 method with a time step of size
\Delta t=0.0005

and the smooth experiment until

The smooth case will consist of a quiescent water level with a small perturbation, so that the initial condition is given by:

The reduced space dimension is taken at
r=15
for both experiments.

5.3
Compressible Euler equations

The focus of this experiment is on the effect of the entropy projection and tangent space enrichment on the accuracy of the ROM. We will be interested in particular in the benefit of tangent space enrichment in the reconstruction accuracy of the entropy projection. In addition, we will analyse the error that can be incurred with respect to the FOM by the introduction of an entropy projection step in the ROM as we propose. A good case to study for this experiment are the compressible Euler equations. Due to their nontrivial entropy functional and corresponding entropy variables it is not expected that without extra measures, like TSE, the entropy projection will be accurate.

A short introduction to the compressible Euler equations now follows. The compressible Euler equations are given by:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\rho u\\
E\end{bmatrix}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\begin{bmatrix}\rho u\\
\rho u^{2}+p\\
(E+p)u\end{bmatrix}=0,

(36)

where
\rho:\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the density,
\rho u:\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the momentum and
E:\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the total energy. We gather these conserved variables in a vector
\boldsymbol{u}=\left[\rho,\rho u,E\right]^{T}
. Furthermore, we assume the equations are suitably normalized so that they are dimensionless. The pressure
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is related to the conserved variables through an equation of state, representing the thermodynamics at hand:

p(\boldsymbol{u})=\left(u_{3}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{u_{2}^{2}}{u_{1}}\right)(\gamma-1),

(37)

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the specific heat ratio, which we take at the standard choice
\gamma=1.4
. The thermodynamic quantities, i.e. pressure, density and total energy are necessarily nonnegative. Throughout the experiments we will assume our FOM and ROMs respect this condition, assuring this condition mathematically may be the subject of future work. The entropy functional of interest will be taken as:

\mathcal{S}[\boldsymbol{u}]=\int_{\Omega}\frac{-u_{1}\sigma}{\gamma-1}dx,

where
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the specific entropy defined as a function of the conserved variables like:

\sigma(\boldsymbol{u})=\ln\left(\frac{p}{u_{1}^{\gamma}}\right),

where
p
is evaluated using (
37
). Different entropies are also possible, see for instance
[
54
]
. In turn, this gives rise to the discrete total entropy functional:

S_{h}[\boldsymbol{u}_{h}]=\sum_{i}\Delta x_{i}\frac{-\rho_{i}\sigma_{i}}{\gamma-1}.

The associated entropy variables are given by:

\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u})=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\gamma-\sigma}{\gamma-1}-\frac{u_{2}^{2}}{2u_{1}p}\\
u_{2}/p\\
-u_{1}/p\end{bmatrix},

and consequently the inverse of the entropy variables is:

\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{\eta})=\exp\left(\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}-\left[\eta_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\eta_{2}^{2}}{\eta_{3}}\right]\right)\begin{bmatrix}(-\eta_{3})^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}\\
-\eta_{2}(-\eta_{3})^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}}\\
\left(\frac{1}{2}\eta_{2}^{2}-\frac{\eta_{3}}{\gamma-1}\right)\cdot(-\eta_{3})^{\frac{2\gamma-1}{1-\gamma}}\end{bmatrix}.

Considering
-\eta_{3}=\rho/p
is generally exponentiated to some noninteger power we see the importance of positivity of the thermodynamic variables. As in
[
44
]
, we will use the entropy conserving numerical flux by Ismail and Roe
[
65
]
for which we define the following variables:

\boldsymbol{z}=\begin{bmatrix}z^{1}\\
z^{2}\\
z^{3}\end{bmatrix}=\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{p}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
u\\
p\end{bmatrix},

finally an entropy conservative flux is given by
\boldsymbol{f}_{i+1/2}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{F}_{i+1/2}^{1}&\boldsymbol{F}_{i+1/2}^{2}&\boldsymbol{F}_{i+1/2}^{3}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}_{i+1/2}^{1}

\displaystyle=\overline{z^{2}}_{i+1/2}\cdot(z^{3})_{i+1/2}^{\ln},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}_{i+1/2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\overline{z^{3}}_{i+1/2}}{\overline{z^{1}}_{i+1/2}}+\frac{\overline{z^{2}}_{i+1/2}}{\overline{z^{1}}_{i+1/2}}\boldsymbol{F}_{i+1/2}^{1},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}_{i+1/2}^{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\overline{z^{2}}_{i+1/2}}{\overline{z^{1}}_{i+1/2}}\left(\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}\frac{(z^{3})_{i+1/2}^{\ln}}{(z^{1})_{i+1/2}^{\ln}}+\boldsymbol{F}_{i+1/2}^{2}\right),

where
a^{\ln}
denotes the logarithmic mean, which is defined as:

a_{i+1/2}^{\ln}:=\frac{a_{i+1}-a_{i}}{\ln{a_{i+1}}-\ln{a_{i}}}.

Computation of the logarithmic mean is generally not numerically stable when
a_{i+1}\approx a_{i}
, but a popular algorithm which we will use to deal with this is also given in
[
65
]
. There is an abundance of alternative entropy conservative numerical fluxes that can be used
[
101
,
29
,
57
,
117
,
75
,
120
]
some of which also conserve kinetic energy in the sense of Jameson
[
67
]
. As an entropy dissipation operator we take the Roe-type diffusion operator
[
44
]
where the eigenvalues and vectors of the flux Jacobian are evaluated at the arithmetic average of the neighbouring conserved variables. As with the shallow water equations, we obtain a second accurate entropy dissipation operator using the entropy stable total variation diminishing (TVD) reconstruction based on the minmod limiter
[
44
]

6
Conclusion

In this article we have proposed a method to construct nonlinear manifold Galerkin reduced order models (ROMs) in such a way that the important total entropy functional of the ROM approximation is appropriately conserved or dissipated. This is a crucial concept in obtaining stable and physically admissible ROM solutions. In particular, we have focused on systems of one-dimensional nonlinear conservation laws. Correct semi-discrete entropy estimates upon orthogonal projection were obtained for these systems by evaluating the projected system not at the current state, but at its entropy projection. This was proposed earlier for linear ROMs and extended in this work to nonlinear manifold ROMs.

The entropy projection of the state is obtained by transforming conservative variables to entropy variables, consequently projecting these on the tangent space of the reduced manifold and finally transforming back to conserved variables. To assure accuracy, it is important that the entropy projection is as close as possible to the identity mapping. This is generally not the case for general nonlinear reduced spaces and hence we have proposed the method of tangent space enrichment (TSE). With TSE the manifold is lifted along an additional dimension parameterized by a new coordinate. This coordinate direction is constructed to linearly extend in the direction of the local entropy variables, so that the tangent space spans the entropy variable at least approximately given the absolute value of the TSE coordinate. Accordingly, the entropy projection error will stay small.

We have tested our proposed framework on several nonlinear conservation laws from fluid dynamics. We verified that the entropy estimates are satisfied (semi-discretely): the projection of entropy-conserving flux differences produces no total entropy and the projection of entropy dissipative terms dissipates total entropy. This is in contrast to the generic manifold Galerkin framework which leads to production of entropy in our numerical experiments, which is physically incorrect. We have also shown that the introduction of the artificial TSE coordinate is vital for the accuracy of the entropy projection and leads to minimal decreases in accuracy.

We have also for the first time generalized the recently proposed quadratic manifolds to rational quadratic manifolds. We have suggested a framework to find the coefficients of the rational quadratic manifolds based on a nonlinear curve fitting approach. We have also formulated the rational quadratic polynomials such that they do no not have real poles. This was achieved through semi-definite constraints, avoiding division by zero for any point in the reduced space. Numerical experiments on the inviscid Burgers equation have shown the increased performance of these rational quadratic manifold parameterizations as compared to existing quadratic manifold parameterizations and linear methods.

In future work, two challenges need to be tackled to make the approach computationally efficient: (i) we need a faster way to fit the rational quadratic manifolds and (ii) we need an entropy-stable hyperreduction approach.
