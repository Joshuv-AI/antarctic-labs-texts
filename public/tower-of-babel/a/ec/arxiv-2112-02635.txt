Maximal functions and multiplier theorem for Fourier orthogonal series Thanks: The author is partially supported by Simons Foundation Grant #849676.
Yuan Xu

Abstract
. Under the assumption that orthogonal polynomials of several variables admit an addition formula, we can define a convolution structure and use it to study the Fourier orthogonal expansions on a homogeneous space. We define a maximal function via the convolution structure induced by the addition formula and use it to establish a Marcinkiewicz multiplier theorem. For the homogeneous space defined by a family of weight functions on conic domains, we show that the maximal function is bounded by the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function so that the multiplier theorem holds on conic domains.

1.
Introduction

Fourier orthogonal expansions on a domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
have been studied in depth for several regular
domains in high dimensions in recent years. Let
\Omega
be either a hypersurface or a regular domain
with open interior in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and let
{\mathsf{w}}
be a weight function on
\Omega
. We consider the orthogonal
structure with respect to the inner product

(1.1)

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{\mathsf{w}}=\int_{\Omega}f(x)g(x){\mathsf{w}}(w)\mathrm{d}x,

well-defined for the space
\Pi^{d}(\Omega)
of polynomials polynomials restricted on
\Omega
.
Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}
be the subspace of
\Pi^{d}(\Omega)
that consists of orthogonal polynomials of degree

n
with respect to the inner product. With respect to an orthogonal basis
\{P_{k}^{n}:1\leq k\leq\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}\}

of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}
, the Fourier orthogonal expansion of
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
is defined by

(1.2)

f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};f),\qquad\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};f)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}}\frac{{\langle}f,P_{k}^{n}{\rangle}_{\mathsf{w}}}{{\langle}P_{k}^{n},P_{k}^{n}{\rangle}_{\mathsf{w}}}P_{k}^{n}.

The orthogonal projection operator
\operatorname{proj}_{n}:L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})\mapsto{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}
can be written as

(1.3)

\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};f,x)=\int_{\Omega}f(y)P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x,y){\mathsf{w}}(y)\mathrm{d}y,

where
P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot,\cdot)
is the reproducing kernel of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}
and it is given by

(1.4)

P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x,y):=\sum_{j=1}^{\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}}\frac{P_{k}^{n}(x)P_{k}^{n}(y)}{{\langle}P_{k}^{n},P_{k}^{n}{\rangle}_{\mathsf{w}}}.

This kernel is uniquely determined and is independent of the choice of orthogonal bases. To develop an
analysis of orthogonal expansions beyond the standard
L^{2}
theory, we need further information on the
orthogonal structure that can hold only for special
{\mathsf{w}}
and
\Omega
. The first domain on which a
systematic study is carried out is the spherical harmonic expansion on the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
; see, for example,

[
1
,
2
,
4
,
11
,
13
]
and references in
[
4
,
13
]
. The approach developed on the sphere
is extended to study the Fourier-orthogonal expansions on several other regular domains, including the unit
sphere with product type weight functions, the unit ball and the regular simplex with classical weight functions;
see, for example,
[
4
,
5
]
and references there as well as
[
7
,
8
,
12
,
10
]
. More recently,
they are studied for Jacobi type weight functions on conic domains in
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
.

A common trait that makes the study on these regular domains possible is the
addition formula
for the
orthogonal polynomials, which gives a closed-form formula for the reproducing kernels. The simplest case
is the spherical harmonic expansions on the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
, where the reproducing kernel satisfies

(1.5)

P_{n}(x,y)=\frac{n+{\lambda}}{{\lambda}}C_{n}^{\lambda}(x,y),\qquad{\lambda}=\frac{d-2}{2},\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}},

in which
C_{n}^{\lambda}
is the Gegenbauer polynomial of degree
n
. This identity is the classical addition formula
for spherical harmonics, since it gives a closed-form formula for the sum in (
1.4
). It is also
the reason why we retain the name addition formula for closed-formula for other orthogonal polynomials. The
remarkable formula (
1.5
) allows us to relate much of the
study on the unit sphere to the Fourier-Gegenbauer series in one variable (cf.
[
2
,
4
,
13
]

and references there). Analysis on other regular domains also relies on their addition formulas, which are
however more complex and given by integrals of the Gegenbauer or the Jacobi polynomials. Recently,
motivated by newly discovered addition formulas for the conic domains in
[
16
,
17
]
, we have shown
in
[
18
]
that a substantial portion of analysis holds in a general setting, under a mere assumption that an
addition formula for the reproducing kernel exists. The main result of the present paper is to show that this
paradigm applies to the maximal functions and multiplier operators.

We will assume that the orthogonal structure of
L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
admits an addition formula given
as an integral of a Jacobi polynomial (see Definition
2.1
below). It leads to a pseudo
convolution structure and a maximal function that is suitable for studying the Fourier orthogonal expansions.
Under a technical assumption, we can then prove a Marcinkiewicz multiplier theorem, following the original
approach in
[
2
]
for the unit sphere closely. We then show that the general setting applies to the conic surface

{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}=\left\{(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}:\|x\|\leq t,\,x\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d},\,0\leq t\leq 1\right\}

in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
with the weight function
t^{-1}(1-t)^{\gamma}
,
{\gamma}\geq 0
, and the cone bounded by
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}

and the hyperplane
t=1
with the weight function
(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}(1-t)^{\gamma}
,
\mu,{\gamma}\geq 0
. For these
conic domains, we shall show that the maximal function defined via the pseudo convolution is bounded
by the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function. The latter is sufficient for proving the multiplier theorem on the
conic domains.

The paper is organized as follows. The theory for the general setting is developed in the next section.
The conic surface is treated in Section 3 and the cone in Section 4. Throughout this paper, we shall use

c,c_{1},c_{2}
to denote positive constants that depend only on fixed parameters, and their values may
change from line to line. Furthermore, we write
A\sim B
if
c_{1}A\leq B\leq c_{2}A
.

2.
Homogenous space admitting an addition formula

In this section, we consider analysis on a homogeneous space that admits an addition formula. The
first subsection is preliminary, where we give basic definitions that will be used throughout the rest
of the paper. The addition formula and the convolution structure are defined and discussed in the
second subsection. The Poisson integrals and the Cesàro means are discussed in subsections three
and four, respectively. The latter contains an assertion that will be needed for the proof of the multiplier
theorem in subsection five. Finally, a maximal function is defined and discussed in subsection six.

2.1.
Preliminary

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with a metric
{\mathsf{d}}
and let
{\mathsf{w}}
be a nonnegative weight function
on
\Omega
. The space
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}})
is called a homogeneous space if all open balls

B(x,r)=\{y\in\Omega:{\mathsf{d}}(x,y)<r\}
are measurable and
{\mathsf{w}}
is a doubling weight. The latter means
that
{\mathsf{w}}(B(x,2r))\leq c\,{\mathsf{w}}(B(x,r))
for all
x\in\Omega
and
r>0
, where
c
is dependent of
x
and
r
,
and

{\mathsf{w}}(E):=\int_{E}{\mathsf{w}}(x)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(x),\qquad E\subset\Omega,

where
{\mathsf{m}}
is the Lebesgue measure on
\Omega
. For our purpose, the domain
\Omega
is either a
quadratic surface, such as the unit sphere or a conic surface, or a domain bounded by such a surface and,
when needed, hyperplanes. Moreover, our weight function will also be specified.

Let
\Pi(\Omega)
denote the space of polynomials restricted to
\Omega
and let
\Pi_{n}(\Omega)
denote
its subspace of polynomials of degree at most
n
. If the interior of
\Omega
is open, then
\Pi(\Omega)

contains all polynomials of degree
n
in
d
variables and we write
\Pi_{n}=\Pi_{n}(\Omega)
. If
\Omega

is an algebraic surface, such as the sphere
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
, then
\Pi_{n}(\Omega)
contains all polynomials restricted
to the surface.

Let
{\mathsf{w}}
be a doubling weight on
\Omega
normalized so that
\int_{\Omega}{\mathsf{w}}(x)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}=1
. We consider
orthogonal polynomials with respect to the inner product
{\langle}\cdot,\cdot{\rangle}_{\mathsf{w}}
defined in (
1.1
).
A polynomials
p_{n}\in\Pi_{n}(\Omega)
is an orthogonal polynomial if
{\langle}p_{n},q{\rangle}_{\mathsf{w}}=0
for all

q\in\Pi_{n-1}(\Omega)
.
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
be the space of orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
. If
\Omega
is a quadratic
surface, such as the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
, then

\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})=\binom{n+d-2}{n}+\binom{n+d-3}{n-1},\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots,

where we assume
\binom{n}{k}=0
if
k<0
. If
\Omega
is a solid domain, such as the unit ball
{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
,
then

\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})=\binom{n+d-1}{n},\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots,

The Fourier orthogonal expansion of
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
is defined as in (
1.2
) and the
reproducing kernel
P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot,\cdot)
of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
is given by (
1.3
) and
(
1.4
).

We need to consider specific domain
\Omega
and weight function
{\mathsf{w}}
. Our quintessential example is the
homogeneous space on the unit sphere with respect to the surface measure, for which orthogonal polynomials
are spherical harmonics. As an extension of the addition formula of spherical harmonics given in
(
1.5
), we shall assume that that the homogeneous space
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}})
admits a
closed formula given as an integral of the Jacobi polynomial
P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
. The precise definition is
given in the next subsection. First, we recall the definition of the Jacobi polynomials. These are orthogonal
polynomials associated with the weight function

w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(t)=(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta},\qquad{\alpha},{\beta}>-1.

The polynomial
P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
is of degree
n
and normalized so that
P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(1)=\binom{n+{\alpha}}{n}
.
With

(2.6)

c^{\prime}_{{\alpha},{\beta}}=\frac{1}{2^{{\alpha}+{\beta}+1}}c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}:=\frac{\Gamma({\alpha}+{\beta}+2)}{\Gamma({\alpha}+1)\Gamma({\beta}+1)}.

the orthogonality of the Jacobi polynomials is given by

c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}^{\prime}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)P_{m}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(t)\mathrm{d}t=h_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}\delta_{n,m},

where
h_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
is the square of the
L^{2}
norm that satisfies

h_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}=\frac{({\alpha}+1)_{n}({\beta}+1)_{n}({\alpha}+{\beta}+n+1)}{n!({\alpha}+{\beta}+2)_{n}({\alpha}+{\beta}+2n+1)}.

For convenience, we introduce the following notation

(2.7)

Z_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t):=\frac{P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(1)P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)}{h_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}}.

The Gegenbauer polynomials
C_{n}^{\lambda}
are orthogonal with respect to the weight function

w_{{\lambda}}(t)=(1-t^{2})^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad{\lambda}>-\frac{1}{2},

which is a special case of the Jacobi polynomials since
w_{\lambda}(t)=w_{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}}(t)
, but it is normalized
so that
C_{n}^{\lambda}(1)=\frac{(2{\lambda})_{n}}{n!}
; more precisely
[
14
, (4.7.1)]
,

C_{n}^{\lambda}(t)=\frac{(2{\lambda})_{n}}{({\lambda}+\frac{1}{2})_{n}}P_{n}^{({\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2})}(t).

The orthogonality of the Gegenbauer polynomials is given explicitly by

c_{{\lambda}}\int_{-1}^{1}C_{n}^{{\lambda}}(t)C_{m}^{{\lambda}}(t)w_{\lambda}(t)\mathrm{d}t=h_{n}^{{\lambda}}\delta_{n,m},\qquad h_{n}^{\lambda}=\frac{{\lambda}}{n+{\lambda}}C_{n}^{\lambda}(1),

where
c_{\lambda}=c_{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}}
. In particular, it follows readily that

Z_{n}^{\lambda}(t):=\frac{n+{\lambda}}{{\lambda}}C_{n}^{\lambda}(t)=Z_{n}^{({\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2})}(t).

2.2.
Addition formula and convolution structure

We work in the setting that
\Omega
is a compact domain and the weight function
{\mathsf{w}}
on
\Omega
admits
an addition formula for the the reproducing kernel
P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot,\cdot)
. The addition formula and the convolution
structure it leads to are defined in
[
18
]
, which we review in this subsection. The following definition appears
in
[
18
, Definition 3.2]
, in which
Z_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
is defined in (
2.7
).

Definition 2.1
.

Let
{\mathsf{w}}
be a weight function on
\Omega
. The reproducing kernel
P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot,\cdot)

is said to satisfy an addition formula if, for some
{\alpha}\geq{\beta}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
,

(2.8)

P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x,y)=\int_{[-1,1]^{m}}Z_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}\big(\xi(x,y;u)\big)\mathrm{d}\tau(u),

where
m
is a positive integer;
\xi(x,y;u)
is a function of
u\in[-1,1]^{m}
, symmetric
in
x
and
y
, and
\xi(x,y;u)\in[-1,1]
; moreover,
\mathrm{d}\tau
is a probability measure on

[-1,1]^{m}
, which can degenerate to have a finite support.

The classical addition formula for spherical harmonics is the degenerate case. The addition formula for
product type weight functions on the unit sphere and the Gegenbauer weight function on the unit ball is
given in terms of
Z_{n}^{\lambda}

[
5
,
15
]
, while those on the simplex and on the conic domains are given
in terms of
Z_{n}^{({\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2})}

[
16
,
17
]
.

For
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we denote by
\|f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}
the
L^{p}
norm of
L^{p}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
for

1\leq p<\infty
and the uniform norm of
C(\Omega)
for
p=\infty
. We also identify
L^{p}([-1,1],w_{{\alpha},{\beta}})

with
C([-1,1])
when
p=\infty
.

Making use of the one-dimensional structure premised by the addition formula, a convolution operator is
defined in
[
18
, Definition 3.3]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Assume
{\mathsf{w}}
admits the addition formula. For
f\in L^{1}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
and

g\in L^{1}([-1,1],w_{{\alpha},{\beta}})
, we define the convolution of
f
and
g
by

(f\ast_{\mathsf{w}}g)(x):=\int_{\Omega}f(y)T^{({\alpha},{\beta})}g(x,y){\mathsf{w}}(y)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(y),

where the operator
g\mapsto T^{({\alpha},{\beta})}g
is defined by

\displaystyle T^{({\alpha},{\beta})}g(x,y):=\int_{[-1,1]^{m}}g\big(\xi(x,y;u)\big)\mathrm{d}\tau(u).

The definition of this convolution structure is motivated by the expression of the reproducing kernel specified
by the addition formula, so that we can write

(2.9)

\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};f,x)=f*_{\mathsf{w}}Z_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}\quad\hbox{and}\quad P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x,y)=T^{({\alpha},{\beta})}\left(Z_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}\right).

The operator
T^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
is also defined with a more generic orthogonal polynomial in place of

P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
in
[
17
]
, where it is shown to be bounded
[
17
, Lemma 6.3]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
g\in L^{p}([-1,1],w_{{\alpha},{\beta}})
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, and
x\in\Omega
,

\left\|T^{({\alpha},{\beta})}g(x,\cdot)\right\|_{{\mathsf{w}},p}\leq\|g\|_{L^{p}([-1,1],w_{{\alpha},{\beta}})}.

The convolution operator
f\ast_{\mathsf{w}}g
satisfies the usual Young’s inequality.
Let
p,q,r\geq 1
and
p^{-1}=r^{-1}+q^{-1}-1
. For
f\in L^{q}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
and

g\in L^{r}([-1,1];w_{{\alpha},{\beta}})
,

(2.10)

\|f\ast_{\mathsf{w}}g\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})}\leq\|f\|_{L^{q}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})}\|g\|_{L^{r}([-1,1];{\mathsf{w}})}.

There is another way to define the convolution structure that uses a sort of translation operator defined
as in
[
18
, Definition 3.7]
.

Definition 2.4
.

Assume the addition formula holds for the weight function
{\mathsf{w}}
. For
0\leq{\theta}\leq\pi
, the translation
operator
S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}
is defined by

(2.11)

\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}f)=\frac{P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(\cos{\theta})}{P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(1)}\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};f),\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots.

Since
|P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(\cos{\theta})|\leq|P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(1)|
, the operator
S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}f
is well defined for

f\in L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
and, by density, for all
f\in L^{1}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
. The following proposition
is established in
[
18
, Proposition 3.8]
.

Proposition 2.5
.

The operator
S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}
satisfies the following properties:

(i)

For
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
and
g\in L^{1}([-1,1],w_{{\alpha},{\beta}})
,

(f*_{\mathsf{w}}g)(x)=c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}\int_{0}^{\pi}S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}f(x)g(\cos{\theta})w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(\cos{\theta})\sin{\theta}\mathrm{d}{\theta}.

(ii)

S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}f
preserves positivity; that is,
S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}f\geq 0
if
f\geq 0
.

(iii)

For
f\in L^{p}({\mathsf{w}};\Omega)
, if
1\leq p\leq\infty
, or
f\in C(\Omega)
if
p=\infty
,

\|S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq\|f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\lim_{{\theta}\to 0}\|S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}f-f\|_{{\mathsf{w}},p}=0.

These results, under the existence assumption of the addition formula, allow us to deduce many results
of the Fourier orthogonal series on
\Omega
from the corresponding results of the Fourier-Jacobi series.
To illustrate the result, we consider the Poisson sum and the Cesàro means below.

2.3.
Poisson integral

Let
q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t,s)
denote the Poisson kernel of the Jacobi polynomials,

q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t,s)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(s)}{h_{n}^{({\alpha},b)}}r^{n},\quad 0\leq r<1.

For
f\in L^{1}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
and
0\leq r<1
, the Poisson integral of
f
is defined by

(2.12)

\displaystyle Q_{r}({\mathsf{w}};f,x)\,

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};f,x)r^{n}

\displaystyle=f*_{\mathsf{w}}q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}\quad\hbox{with}\quad q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t):=q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t,1).

Lemma 2.6
.

For
{\alpha},{\beta}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
, the kernel
q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
satisfies

(2.13)

q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)=c_{\beta}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1-r}{\left(1-2\sqrt{r}\sqrt{\frac{1+t}{2}}u+r\right)^{{\alpha}+{\beta}+2}}(1-u^{2})^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}u,

where
c_{\beta}=c_{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2},{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}
and the identity holds for
{\beta}\to-\frac{1}{2}
under the limit

(2.14)

\lim_{{\beta}\to-\frac{1}{2}}c_{\beta}\int_{-1}^{1}g(u)(1-u^{2})^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}u=\frac{g(1)+g(-1)}{2}.

In particular,
q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)\geq 0
for
t\in[-1,1]
.

Proof.

By (
2.9
), the function
q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
is given by

q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Z_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)r^{n}.

The polynomial
P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(\cos 2{\theta})
can be identified with the generalized Gegenbauer polynomial
of degree
2n
that are orthogonal with respect to
|x|^{{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}}(1-x^{2})^{\beta}
on
[-1,1]
, and the latter
one can be written as an integral of the Gegenbauer polynomial of degree
2n
. In particular, by

[
5
, Theorem 1.5.6]
, the following identity holds,

Z_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(\cos 2{\theta})=c_{{\beta}}\int_{-1}^{1}Z_{2n}^{{\alpha}+{\beta}+1}(u\cos{\theta})(1-u^{2})^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}u,

where
c_{\beta}=c_{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2},{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}
, and the formula holds under the limit (
2.14
) for
{\beta}=-\frac{1}{2}
,

(2.15)

Z_{n}^{({\alpha},-\frac{1}{2})}(\cos 2{\theta})=Z_{2n}^{{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}}(\cos{\theta}).

The right-hand side of the last integral is zero if we replace
2n
by
2n+1
, as can be seen by a change of variable

u\mapsto-u
and using
C_{n}^{\lambda}(t)=(-1)^{\lambda}C_{n}^{\lambda}(t)
. Hence, using the
identity

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Z_{n}^{\lambda}(u)r^{n}=\frac{1-r^{2}}{(1-2ru+r^{2})^{{\lambda}+1}},\qquad 0\leq r<1,

we deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Z_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(\cos 2{\theta})r^{n}\,

\displaystyle=c_{{\beta}}\int_{-1}^{1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Z_{n}^{{\alpha}+{\beta}+1}(u\cos{\theta})(\sqrt{r})^{n}(1-u^{2})^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=c_{{\beta}}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1-r}{(1-2\sqrt{r}u\cos{\theta}+r)^{{\alpha}+{\beta}+2}}(1-u^{2})^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}u.

Setting
t=\cos(2{\theta})=2\cos^{2}{\theta}-1
proves the result for
{\beta}>-\frac{1}{2}
.
∎

The basic properties of the Poisson integral is given in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.7
.

Assume the addition formula holds for the weight function
{\mathsf{w}}
. Then

(1)

Q_{r}({\mathsf{w}};f_{0})=f_{0}
for
f_{0}(x)=1
;

(2)

Q_{r}({\mathsf{w}})
are positive:
Q_{r}({\mathsf{w}};f)\geq 0
if
f\geq 0
;

(3)

Q_{r}({\mathsf{w}})
are symmetric:
Q_{r}({\mathsf{w}})
is self-adjoint on
L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
;

(4)

Q_{r}({\mathsf{w}})
are contractions on
L^{p}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
:

\|Q_{r}({\mathsf{w}};f)\|_{p,\omega}\leq\|f\|_{p,\Omega}
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
;

(5)

If
f\in L^{p}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
,
1\leq p<\infty
, or
f\in C(\Omega)
if
p=\infty
, then

\lim_{r\to 1-}\|Q_{r}({\mathsf{w}};f)-f\|_{{\mathsf{w}},p}=0.

Proof.

The first three properties follow immediately from (
2.12
) and the properties of
q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
.
The fourth one is a consequence of the inequality (
2.10
) with
r=1
.

We now prove the fifth one. Since
c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}^{\prime}\int_{-1}^{1}q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(t)\mathrm{d}t=1
, we have

Q_{r}({\mathsf{w}};f,x)-f(x)=c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\left[S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}f(x)-f(x)\right]q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(\cos{\theta})w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(\cos{\theta})\sin{\theta}\mathrm{d}{\theta}.

By Proposition
2.5
, for every
{\varepsilon}>0
, there is a
\delta>0
such that

\|S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}f-f\|_{\omega,p}<{\varepsilon}
whenever
{\theta}<\delta
. Hence, by the Minkowski inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\|Q_{r}({\mathsf{w}};f)f-f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\,

\displaystyle\leq c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\|S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}f-f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(\cos{\theta})w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(\cos{\theta})\sin{\theta}\mathrm{d}{\theta}

\displaystyle\leq{\varepsilon}+2\|f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}\int_{\delta}^{\pi}q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(\cos{\theta})w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(\cos{\theta})\sin{\theta}\mathrm{d}{\theta}.

For
0\leq{\theta}\leq\pi
,
\cos\tfrac{{\theta}}{2}\geq 0
. Using the inequality

(2.16)

\displaystyle 1-2\sqrt{r}\cos\tfrac{{\theta}}{2}u+r\,

\displaystyle=(1-\sqrt{r})^{2}+2\sqrt{r}(1-\cos\tfrac{{\theta}}{2})+2\sqrt{r}\cos\tfrac{{\theta}}{2}(1-u)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\sqrt{r})^{2}+4\sqrt{r}\sin^{2}\tfrac{{\theta}}{4},

it follows readily that, for
{\theta}\geq\delta
,

\displaystyle q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(\cos{\theta})\,

\displaystyle=c_{\beta}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1-r}{\left(1-2\sqrt{r}\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2}u+r\right)^{{\alpha}+{\beta}+2}}(1-u^{2})^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1-r}{\left((1-\sqrt{r})^{2}+4\sqrt{r}\sin^{2}\tfrac{\delta}{4}\right)^{{\alpha}+{\beta}+2}}

so that
q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(\cos{\theta})\to 0
as
r\to 1-
for
\delta\leq{\theta}\leq\pi
. Putting these together completes the proof.
∎

The Poisson operator will be needed in the Subsection 2.5.

2.4.
Cesàro means and the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function

For
\delta>0
, the Cesàro
(C,\delta)
means of the Fourier orthogonal series with respect to
{\mathsf{w}}
on

\Omega
are defined by

\displaystyle S_{n}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}};f,x):=\frac{1}{\binom{n+\delta}{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n-k+\delta}{n-k}\operatorname{proj}_{k}({\mathsf{w}};f,x).

Let
k_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta}),\delta}(t,s)
be the kernel of the
(C,\delta)
mean of the Fourier-Jacobi series. Then

k_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta}),\delta}(t):=k_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta}),\delta}(t,1)=\frac{1}{\binom{n+\delta}{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n-k+\delta}{n-k}Z_{k}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t).

Using (
2.9
), we can write
S_{n}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}};f)
as the convolution

(2.17)

S_{n}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}};f)=f*_{\mathsf{w}}k_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta}),\delta}.

Our method allows us to derive the properties of
S_{n}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}};f)
from those of
k_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta}),\delta}
.
In particular, the following theorem is stated in
[
18
, Theorem 3.6]
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
{\mathsf{w}}
be a weight function that admits the addition formula. The Cesàro
(C,\delta)
means
of the Fourier orthogonal series with respect to
{\mathsf{w}}
satisfy

(1)

If
\delta\geq{\alpha}+{\beta}+2
, then
S_{n}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}};f)
is a nonnegative operator;

(2)

If
\delta>\max\{{\alpha},{\beta}\}+\frac{1}{2}
, then for
n=0,1,2,\ldots
,

\left\|S_{n}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}};f)\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq\|f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}},\qquad 1\leq p\leq\infty.

For the unit sphere, the Cesàro means of the Fourier expansions in spherical harmonics plays an
essential role for the proof of a multiplier theorem, as shown in
[
2
]
. Moreover, the approach also
applies to
h
-spherical harmonics series on the unit sphere, which are orthogonal polynomials with
respect to a family of product weight functions on the sphere. It turns out that the same approach can
also be used to derive an multiplier theorem when
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
admits an addition formula,
provided that the Cesàro means satisfies an additional property.

To state this property, we defined the maximal Cesàro
(C,\delta)
operator: for
\delta\geq 0
,

S_{*}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}};f,x):=\sup_{n\geq 0}\left|S_{n}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}};f,x)\right|,\qquad x\in\Omega.

We also need the Hardy-Littlerwood maximal function on the homogeneous space
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}})
,
which is defined by

M_{\mathsf{w}}f(x):=\sup_{r>0}\frac{1}{{\mathsf{w}}(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}|f(y)|{\mathsf{w}}(y)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(y),\quad x\in\Omega.

The property states that the maximal
(C,\delta)
operator is bounded by the maximal operator, which we
state as an assertion.

Assertion 1
.
Assume
{\mathsf{w}}
admits the addition formula (
2.8
). Let
\delta_{0}=\max\{{\alpha},{\beta}\}+\frac{1}{2}
.
For
\delta>\delta_{0}
and
f\in L^{1}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
,

(2.18)

S_{*}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}};f,x)\leq cM_{\mathsf{w}}f(x),\qquad x\in\Omega,

where
M_{\mathsf{w}}f
is the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function.

The maximal function
M_{\mathsf{w}}
requires local information for
f
, specified by the metric
{\mathsf{d}}
on
\Omega
.
This is the first time that we encounter the metric, since our development so far is entirely based on the
orthogonal structure specified by the addition formula (
2.8
). In order to establish the inequality
(
2.18
), we shall need further information on the orthogonal structure, so that the convolution
structure
f*_{\mathsf{w}}g
can be localized. Merely the existence of an addition formula (
2.8
) is not
sufficient for the inequality (
2.18
). The Assertion 1 is known to be true for the unit sphere, the
unit ball and the simplex with classical weight functions. We shall prove that it holds on conic domains as well
in the next section.

The Hardy-Littlewood inequality satisfies the Fefferman-Stein inequality
[
6
]
. A proof is given
in
[
13
, pp. 51-55]
.

Theorem 2.9
.

If
1<p,q<\infty
and
\{f_{j}\}
is a sequence of functions on
\Omega
, then

\left\|\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left|M_{\mathsf{w}}f\right|^{q}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq c_{p,q}\left\|\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left|f_{k}\right|^{q}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}.

As a consequence of the Assertion 1, we immediately obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 2.10
.

Assume Assertion 1 holds and
\delta>\delta_{0}
. Then, for any sequence
\{n_{k}\}
of positive integers
and
f_{k}\in L^{p}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
,
1<p<\infty
,

\left\|\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left|S_{n_{k}}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}},f_{k})\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq c\left\|\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left|f_{k}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}.

Both the Fefferman-Stein theorem and the corollary will be needed for proving the multiplier theorem,
as we shall describe in the next subsection.

2.5.
Multiplier theorem

We now state the Marcinkiewicz multiplier theorem for the Fourier orthogonal series, for which we need
the difference operator. Let
\{a_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
be a sequence of real numbers. The difference operators

\triangle^{n}
,
n=0,1,2,\ldots
, are defined recursively by

\triangle^{0}a_{k}=a_{k},\quad\triangle^{1}a_{k}=a_{k}-a_{k+1},\quad\triangle^{n+1}a_{k}=\triangle^{n}(\triangle a_{k}),\quad n=1,2,\ldots.

Theorem 2.11
.

Assume that
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
admits the addition formula (
2.8
) and that the Assertion 1 holds.
Let
\{\mu_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
be a bounded sequence of real numbers such that

\sup_{j\geq 0}2^{j(k-1)}\sum_{\ell=2^{j}+1}^{2^{j+1}}\left|\triangle^{k}\mu_{\ell}\right|\leq M<\infty

for some positive integer
k\geq\lfloor\delta_{0}+1\rfloor
. Then
\{\mu_{j}\}
defines an
L^{p}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
,

1<p<\infty
, multiplier; that is

\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mu_{k}\operatorname{proj}_{k}({\mathsf{w}};f)\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq c\|f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}},\quad 1<p<\infty,

where
c
is independent of
\mu_{j}
and
f
.

For the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
with
{\mathsf{w}}(x)=1
, such a multiplier theorem is established in
[
2
]
. The proof
in
[
2
]
is based on the Littlewood-Paley theory on the sphere but and it is quite involved and original.
The approach also applies, with appropriate twist, on the unit sphere with product type weight functions
and, as a consequence, on the unit ball and the simplex with classical weight function
[
3
]
. Moreover,
the proof can be extended to our general setting, under Assertion 1, almost straightforwardly. We provide
an outline of the proof below to show that this is indeed the case. Throughout the rest of the section, we
assume that
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
admits the addition formula (
2.8
) and the Assertion 1 holds.

The proof of the multiplier theorem relies on several auxiliary
g
-functions. The first one is defined in terms
of the Poisson integral
Q_{r}({\mathsf{w}};f)
,

g(f):=\left(\int_{0}^{1}(1-r)\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}Q_{r}({\mathsf{w}};f)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}r\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and the function
x\mapsto g(f)(x)
, defined on
\Omega
, is bounded in
L^{p}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
.

Proposition 2.12
.

If
1<p<\infty
and
f\in L^{p}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
, then

(2.19)

c_{p}^{-1}\|f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq\|g(f)\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq c_{p}\|f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}},

where the condition
\int_{\Omega}f(x){\mathsf{w}}(x)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(x)=0
is required in the left-hand inequality.

Proof.

Let
T^{t}
be the operator defined by
T^{t}f=Q_{e^{-t}}({\mathsf{w}};f)
. By (
2.12
), it is easy
to see that
T^{t}
satisfies
T^{t_{1}+t_{2}}=T^{t_{1}}T^{t_{2}}
. By the first four properties of
Theorem
2.7
,
\{T^{t}\}
is a symmetric semigroup of operators. Hence, by

[
13
, Theorem 10, p.111]
, the Littlewood-Paley
g
-function

\tilde{g}(f):=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}T^{t}f\right|^{2}t\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}Q_{r}({\mathsf{w}};f)\right|^{2}r|\log r|\mathrm{d}r\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

satisfies
c_{p}^{-1}\|f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq\|\tilde{g}(f)\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq c_{p}\|f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}
. Since
r|\log r|\leq c(1-r)
on
[0,1]
,
we obtain
\tilde{g}(f)(x)\leq g(f)(x)
, which implies immediately the left-hand inequality in (
2.19
).
To prove the right-hand inequality, we split the integral of
g(f)
into two parts, so that
g(f)\leq g_{1}(f)+g_{2}(f)
,
where
g_{1}(f)
has the integral over
[0,\frac{1}{2}]
and
g_{2}(f)
has the integral over
[\frac{1}{2},1]
. For
g_{1}(f)
, we use

\|\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};f)\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq Z_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(1)\|f\|_{{\mathsf{w}},p}\leq cn^{{\alpha}+1}\|f\|_{{\mathsf{w}},p}
, which follows from
(
2.9
) and (
2.10
), to obtain

\|g_{1}(f)|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq\sup_{0<r\leq\frac{1}{2}}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}Q_{r}({\mathsf{w}};f)\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n2^{-n+1}\|\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};f)\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq c\|f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}.

For
g_{2}(f)
, we observe
r|\log r|\sim 1-r
on
[\frac{1}{2},1]
, so that
g_{2}(f)(x)\leq\tilde{g}(f)(x)
and, consequently,
the boundedness of
g_{2}
follows from that of
\tilde{g}(f)
.
∎

The above proof is a straightforward extension of the version for the unit sphere in
[
2
]
; see
[
4
, Thm. 3.2.1]

since the weight and the domain does not play an essential role in the proof. This will be the case for the most part
in the proof of Theorem
2.11
below and we shall be brief.

To simplify the notation, we shall use
Q_{r}f=Q_{r}({\mathsf{w}};f)
and
S_{n}^{\delta}f=S_{n}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}};f)
for the Poisson
integral and the
(C,\delta)
means in the rest of this subsection. Using the orthogonality, it is easy to verify that

Q_{r}S_{n}^{\delta}=S_{n}^{\delta}Q_{r}
for
n\geq 0
. We will need several identities on these means that hold in fact
for the the Poisson sum and the
(C,\delta)
means of any given sequence. We call such identities algebraic and
take them as granted. All these identities can be found in
[
2
,
4
]
. The first such identity is

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}Q_{r}f=-(1-r)^{\delta}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(n+\delta+1)\binom{n+\delta}{n}\left(S_{n}^{\delta+1}f-S_{n}^{\delta}f\right)r^{n-1};

see, for example,
[
4
, (3.2.4) and (3.2.5)]
. This identity implies that
g(f)
is related to another
g
-function
defined in terms of the
(C,\delta)
means. For
\delta\geq 0
, define

g_{\delta}(f):=\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left|S_{n}^{\delta+1}({\mathsf{w}};f)-S_{n}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}};f)\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Then the aforementioned identity implies that
g(f)\leq cg_{\delta}(f)
and, consequently, the proposition below.

Proposition 2.13
.

If
f\in L^{p}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
satisfying
\int_{\Omega}f(x){\mathsf{w}}(x)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(x)=0
, then

(2.20)

\|f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq c\|g_{\delta}(f)\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}},\qquad 1<p<\infty.

The inverse inequality of (
2.13
) also holds and it can be established for a perturbation of
g_{\delta}
.
Let
\{\nu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of positive numbers satisfying

(2.21)

A:=\sup_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\nu_{k}<\infty.

The perturbed
g_{\delta}
function is denoted by
g_{\delta}^{*}
and defined by

g_{\delta}^{*}(f):=\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\nu_{n}}{n}\left|S_{n}^{\delta+1}({\mathsf{w}};f)-S_{n}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}};f)\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Evidently,
g_{\delta}^{*}(f)=g_{\delta}(f)
when
\nu_{k}=1
for all
k
. We need the boundedness of
g_{\delta}^{*}
.

Proposition 2.14
.

Assume that Assertion 1 holds for all
\{n_{k}\}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
f_{k}\in L^{p}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
. Then

(2.22)

\|g_{\delta}^{*}(f)\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq c_{p}\|f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}},\qquad 1<p<\infty,

where
c_{p}
is a constant independent of
f
and
\{\nu_{k}\}
.

The proof of this proposition is long. We trace its steps below and indicate where Assertion 1 is needed.
Several algebraic identities that involve
Q_{r}f
and
S_{n}^{\delta}f
will be need. The first one is
(
[
2
]
and
[
4
, (3.2.11)]
)

S_{n}^{\delta}(Q_{r}f)=Q_{r}(S_{n}^{\delta}f)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}b_{j,n}^{\delta}(r)S_{j}^{\delta}f,\qquad\hbox{where}\quad\sum_{j=0}^{n}|b_{j,n}^{\delta}(r)|\leq c_{\delta},

and
b_{j,n}^{\delta}(r)
is a polynomial in
r
that can be given explicitly, from which the estimate of the sum
follows. By Assertion 1 and Corollary
2.10
, this identity allows us to conclude

(2.23)

\left\|\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|S_{k}^{\delta}Q_{r_{k}}f_{k}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq c_{p}\left\|\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|f_{k}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}},

where
r_{k}\in(0,1)
. Let
I_{k}
be a subinterval of
[r_{k},1)
and let
|I_{k}|
be the length of
I_{k}
.
Define
\{r_{k,i}:0\leq i\leq 2^{n}\}
by
r_{k,i}-r_{k,i-1}=2^{-n}|I_{k}|
. Since the Poisson sum satsfies

Q_{r_{k}}f=Q_{r_{k}/r_{k,i}}Q_{r_{k,i}}f
, we can write

\left|S_{k}^{\delta}Q_{r_{k}}f_{k}\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}\left|S_{k}^{\delta}Q_{r_{k}/r_{k,i}}(Q_{r_{k,i}}f)\right|^{2}.

Using this identity first and then applying (
2.23
), we conclude that

\left\|\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|S_{k}^{\delta}Q_{r_{k}}f_{k}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq c_{p}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}\left|Q_{r_{k,i}}f\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}.

The inner sum in the right-hand side is a Riemann sum of the integral
\frac{1}{|I_{k}|}\int_{I_{k}}|Q_{r_{k}}f|^{2}\mathrm{d}r
.
Hence, passing to the limit
n\to\infty
, we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 2.15
.

Assume that
1<p<\infty
and Assertion 1 holds. If
r_{j}\in(0,1)
and
I_{j}
is a subinterval of
[r_{j},1)

for
j=1,2\ldots
, then the inequality

(2.24)

\left\|\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|S_{k}^{\delta}Q_{r_{k}}f_{k}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq c_{p}\left\|\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|I_{k}|}\int_{I_{k}}\left|Q_{r}f_{k}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}r\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}.

Let
\eta\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
be a cut-off function such that
\eta(t)=1
for
|t|\leq 1
and
\eta(t)=0
for

|t|\geq 2
. For
n=1,2,\ldots
, define

L_{n}f=\sum_{k=0}^{2N}\eta\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\operatorname{proj}_{k}f\quad\hbox{and}\quad D_{n}f=\sum_{k=0}^{2N}\eta\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)k\operatorname{proj}_{k}f.

Then
L_{n}f
is a resampling of
f
and
D_{n}
is a resampling of
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}Q_{r}f
when
r=1
. The
Assertion 1 is needed to prove the following lemma.

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
\delta_{0}
be the index in Assertion 1. Then

(2.25)

\left|Q_{r}(D_{N}f)\right|\leq c\,M_{\mathsf{w}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}Q_{r}f\right).

Proof.

The operator
L_{n}f
is highly localized. A standard procedure of summation by parts
\lfloor\delta_{0}+1\rfloor

times yields

\sup_{N\geq 0}\left|L_{N}f(x)\right|\leq c\sup_{N\geq 0}\left|S_{N}^{\lfloor\delta_{0}+1\rfloor}f(x)\right|\leq c\,M_{\mathsf{w}}f(x)

where the last step follows by Assertion 1. The identity
Q_{r}(D_{N}f)=rL_{N}(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}Q_{r}f)
can be easily
verified, from which (
2.25
) follows readily.
∎

The next algebraic identity allows us to replace
f
by
Q_{r}f
in the proof. It is given by (cf.
[
4
, (3.2.16)]
),

(2.26)

\displaystyle S_{n}^{\delta+1}f-S_{n}^{\delta}f=\,

\displaystyle r^{-n}\left[S_{n}^{\delta+1}(Q_{r}f)-S_{n}^{\delta}(Q_{r}f)\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{j+\delta+1}{n+\delta+1}a_{j,n}^{\delta}(r)\left[S_{j}^{\delta+1}(Q_{r}f)-S_{j}^{\delta}(Q_{r}f)\right],

where the coefficients
a_{j,n}^{\delta}(r)
satisfy
\displaystyle{\max_{0\leq j\leq n-1}|a_{j,n}^{\delta}(r)|\leq c(1-r)}

with
c
independent of
n
; see
[
4
, Lemma 3.2.5]
. Let
\nu_{k}
be given by (
2.21
).
Considering
1+A^{-1}\nu_{j}
instead of
\nu_{j}
, if necessary, we can
assume that
n\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nu_{j}\leq 2n
. Let
\mu_{1}=1
and
\mu_{n}=1+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\nu_{j}
for
n>1
.
Then
r_{n}:=1-\frac{1}{\mu_{n}}
satisfies
1-\frac{1}{n}\leq r_{n}\leq 1-\frac{1}{2n-1}
. In particular, for
r=r_{n}
,

|a_{j,n}^{\delta}(r_{n})|\leq c(1-r_{n})\leq cn^{-1}
. Hence, by (
2.26
),

\left|S_{n}^{\delta+1}f-S_{n}^{\delta}f\right|\leq c\left|S_{n}^{\delta+1}f_{n}-S_{n}^{\delta}f_{n}\right|+cn^{-2}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}j\left|S_{j}^{\delta+1}f_{n}-S_{j}^{\delta}f_{n}\right|.

We need a further algebraic identity that states
[
4
, (3.2.19)]
, for
0\leq j\leq N
,

(2.27)

S_{j}^{\delta+1}(Q_{r}f)-S_{j}^{\delta}(Q_{r}f)=\frac{-1}{j+\delta+1}rS_{j}^{\delta}\big(Q_{r}(D_{N}f)\big).

Using this identity and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, it follows that

\displaystyle\left|S_{n}^{\delta+1}f-S_{n}^{\delta}f\right|^{2}\leq cn^{-2}\left|S_{n}^{\delta}Q_{r_{n}}(D_{N}f)\right|^{2}+cn^{-3}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\left|S_{j}^{\delta}Q_{r_{n}}(D_{N}f)\right|^{2}.

Consequently, applying (
2.24
), we obtain

\left\|\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\nu_{n}}{n}\left|S_{n}^{\delta+1}f-S_{n}^{\delta}f\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq c\left\|\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\nu_{n}}{n^{3}}\frac{1}{|I_{n}|}\int_{r_{n}}^{r_{n+1}}\left|Q_{r}(D_{N}f)\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}},

where
|I_{n}|=r_{n+1}-r_{n}
. In particular,
\nu_{n}/|I_{n}|=\mu_{n}\mu_{n+1}\sim n^{2}
and
n^{-1}<1-r
for

r_{n}<r<r_{n+1}
. Hence, applying (
2.25
), the right-hand side of the displayed inequality is further
bounded by

\displaystyle c\left\|\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{n}\int_{r_{n}}^{r_{n+1}}\left|M_{\mathsf{w}}\left(\frac{{\mathsf{d}}}{{\mathsf{d}}r}Q_{r}f\right)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}r\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq c\left\|\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\int_{r_{n}}^{r_{n+1}}(1-r)\left|\frac{{\mathsf{d}}}{{\mathsf{d}}r}Q_{r}f\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}r\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq c\|g(f)\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}},

where the second step follows from applying the Fefferman-Stein inequality to the Riemann sum of the integral
over
[r_{n},r_{n+1}]
. Taking the limit
N\to\infty
proves the inequality (
2.22
) and
Proposition
2.14
. In particular, this shows that the proof for the unit sphere carries over to the
general setting when
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
admits an addition formula and that the Assertion 1 holds.

The proof of the multiplier theorem, Theorem
2.11
, follows form Proposition
2.13
and
Proposition
2.14
without further complication. Indeed, let
F=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mu_{n}\operatorname{proj}_{n}f
;
then
\|F\|\leq g_{\delta}(F)
by (
2.20
), so that it is sufficient to prove

g_{\delta}(F)\leq g_{\delta}^{*}(f)=\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\nu_{n}}{n}\left|S_{n}^{\delta+1}({\mathsf{w}};f)-S_{n}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}};f)\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

for some sequence
\{\nu_{n}\}
of positive numbers satisfying (
2.21
). The proof of this inequality
is entirely algebraic. Hence, Theorem
2.11
follows as in the case of the unit sphere; see

[
2
]
and
[
4
, Section 3.3]
.

2.6.
Maximal functions

The Assertion 1 states that the maximal
(C,\delta)
operator is bounded by the Hardy-Littlewood maximal
function
M_{\mathsf{w}}
. We discuss another maximal function defined via the convolution operator
f*_{\mathsf{w}}g
in
Definition
2.2
, which is useful for studying operators defined via convolution. Let
\chi_{E}

denote the characteristic function of the set
E
.

Definition 2.17
.

Assume
{\mathsf{w}}
admits the addition formula (
2.8
). For
f\in L^{1}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
and
0\leq{\theta}\leq\pi
, define

(2.28)

{\mathscr{M}}_{\mathsf{w}}f(x):=\sup_{0\leq{\theta}\leq\pi}\frac{(f*_{\mathsf{w}}\chi_{[\cos{\theta},1]})(x)}{{\int_{0}^{\theta}w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(\cos\phi)\sin\phi\,\mathrm{d}\phi}}.

Let
S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}
be the generalized translation operator defined in (
2.11
). By Proposition
2.5
,
we can also write

{\mathscr{M}}_{\mathsf{w}}f(x)=\sup_{0\leq{\theta}\leq\pi}\frac{\int_{0}^{\theta}S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}f(x)w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(\cos\phi)\sin\phi\,\mathrm{d}\phi}{\int_{0}^{\theta}w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(\cos\phi)\sin\phi\,\mathrm{d}\phi}.

For the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
, let
c(x,{\theta})=\{y:{\langle}x,y{\rangle}=\cos{\theta}\}
be the spherical cap and let
\mathrm{d}\sigma
denote the
Lebesgue measure on the sphere. Then the maximal function on
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
satisfies
[
4
, Lemma 3.2]

Mf(x)=\sup_{0\leq{\theta}\leq\pi}\frac{1}{c(x,{\theta})}\int_{c(x,{\theta})}|f(y)|\mathrm{d}\sigma(y),

which is exactly the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function
Mf
on the unit sphere. This, however, does not hold
in our general setting.

While the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function relies on the geometric information of the domain, as seen in
the metric, the definition of the
{\mathscr{M}}_{\mathsf{w}}
is purely analytical. The latter, however, is natural for studying
operators defined by the convolution
*_{\mathsf{w}}
on
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
.

Theorem 2.18
.

Let
g\in L^{1}([-1,1],w_{{\alpha},{\beta}})
with
{\alpha}\geq{\beta}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
. Assume
k({\theta})=g(\cos{\theta})
is a continuous, nonnegative,
and decreasing function on
[0,\pi]
. Then for
f\in L^{1}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
,

|f\ast_{\mathsf{w}}g(x)|\leq c{\mathscr{M}}_{\mathsf{w}}f(x),\qquad x\in\Omega,

where
c=\int_{0}^{\pi}k(\phi){\mathsf{w}}(\cos\phi)\sin\phi\mathrm{d}\phi
.

Proof.

The proof follows from the standard procedure of integration by parts. For fixed
x
, define

F_{x}({\theta})=\int_{0}^{\theta}S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}f(x,t){\mathsf{w}}(\cos\phi)\sin\phi\mathrm{d}\phi
. Then

\displaystyle f\ast_{\mathsf{w}}g(x)\,

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}g(\cos{\theta})S_{\theta}^{\lambda}f(x)w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(\cos{\theta})\sin{\theta}\mathrm{d}{\theta}

\displaystyle=\left[F_{x}(\pi)k(\pi)-\int_{0}^{\pi}k^{\prime}({\theta})F_{x}({\theta})w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(\cos{\theta})\sin{\theta}\mathrm{d}{\theta}\right].

Since
k({\theta})
is nonnegative and
k^{\prime}(t)\leq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|f\ast_{\mathsf{w}}g(x)\right|\,

\displaystyle\leq{\mathscr{M}}_{\mathsf{w}}f(x)\left[k(\pi)\int_{0}^{\pi}k(\phi)w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(\cos\phi)\sin\phi\mathrm{d}\phi k(\pi)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\left.\int_{0}^{\pi}k^{\prime}({\theta})\int_{0}^{\theta}w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(\cos\phi)\sin\phi\mathrm{d}\phi\mathrm{d}{\theta}\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathscr{M}}_{\mathsf{w}}f(x)\int_{0}^{\pi}k(\phi)w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(\cos\phi)\sin\phi\mathrm{d}\phi,

where the last step follows from another integration by parts.
∎

As an example, let us consider the Poisson integral operator.

Theorem 2.19
.

Assume
{\mathsf{w}}
admits the addition formula (
2.8
) and Assertion 1 holds. Then for
f\in L^{1}(\Omega;{\mathsf{w}})

and every
x\in\Omega
,

\sup_{0<r<1}{\mathsf{Q}}_{r}({\mathsf{w}};f,x)\leq c{\mathscr{M}}_{\mathsf{w}}f(x).

Proof.

By the inequality (
2.16
), the kernel
q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
satisfies

q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(\cos{\theta})\leq\frac{1-r}{\big((1-\sqrt{r})^{2}+{\theta}^{2}\big)^{{\alpha}+{\beta}+1}}=:k({\theta})

It is easy to verify that this
k({\theta})
satisfies the assumption of Theorem
2.18
, so that

\left|{\mathsf{Q}}_{r}({\mathsf{w}};f,x)\right|=\left|f*_{\mathsf{w}}q_{r}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(x)\right|\leq c{\mathscr{M}}_{\mathsf{w}}(f;x),

where
c
is a constant independent of
r
.
∎

For the Cesàro
(C,\delta)
operator, we obtain the following somewhat weaker result.

Theorem 2.20
.

Assume
{\mathsf{w}}
admits the addition formula (
2.8
) and Assertion 1 holds. If
\delta\geq{\alpha}+{\beta}+2

and
f\in L^{1}(\Omega;{\mathsf{w}})
, then for every
x\in\Omega
,

(2.29)

S_{*}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}}_{;}f,x)\leq c{\mathscr{M}}_{\mathsf{w}}f(x).

Proof.

By (
2.17
), we need an estimate of
k_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta}),\delta}(u,1)
. For
\delta\geq{\alpha}+{\beta}+2
, the
estimate given in
[
14
, (9.4.4) and (9.41.14)]
states

\displaystyle\left|k_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta}),\delta}(\cos{\theta},1)\right|\leq cn^{-1}({\theta}+n^{-1})^{-(2{\alpha}+3)}=:k_{n}({\theta}).

Clearly
k_{n}({\theta})
satisfies the conditions of (
2.18
) and that
\int_{0}^{\pi}k_{n}({\theta})w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(\cos{\theta})\sin{\theta}\mathrm{d}{\theta}

is bounded by a constant independent of
n
can be easily verified. Hence,

\left|S_{n}^{\delta}\left({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};f,(x,t)\right)\right|\leq c{\mathscr{M}}_{\gamma}f(x,t)

for all
n\geq 0
. Taking supreme over
n
completes the proof.
∎

This result is weaker in the case of the unit sphere and the unit ball, for which the condition
\delta\geq{\alpha}+{\beta}+2

can be lowered to
{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}
. This, however, requires more explicit formulation of the addition formula in
(
2.8
). The stronger version holds for the conic domains, as we shall show in the following two sections.

Since the maximal function
{\mathscr{M}}_{\mathsf{w}}f
is not the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function, it is not immediately
clear if it satisfy the usual property of the maximal function. In many cases, however, the two maximal
functions are closely related. For the conic domains, we shall show that
{\mathscr{M}}_{\mathsf{w}}f
is bounded by
the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function.

3.
Fourier orthogonal series on the conic surface

In this section we work with the conic surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
defined, for
d\geq 2
, by

{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}=\{(x,t):\|x\|=t,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,0\leq t\leq 1\}.

The addition formula on this domain is established only recently in
[
16
]
. We discuss the orthogonal
structure in the first subsection, study the maximal function in the second section, and state the multiplier
theorem in the third subsection.

3.1.
Analysis on the conic surface

For
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
, let
{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}
be the weight function defined by

{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}(t)=t^{-1}(1-t)^{\gamma},\qquad 0<t<1.

We consider the homogeneous space
({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}},{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}})
, where

{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}}
is the distance function on
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
, defined by

{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s)):=\arccos\left(\sqrt{\frac{{\langle}x,y{\rangle}+ts}{2}}+\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}\right)

for
(x,t)
and
(y,s)
on
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
. For
\Omega={\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
, the ball becomes the conic cap.
For
r>0
and
(x,t)
on
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
, we denote the conic cap centered at
(x,t)
with radius
r
by

{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),r):=\left\{(y,s)\in{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}:{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}}\big((x,t),(y,s)\big)\leq r\right\}.

Then
{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}
is a doubling measure with respect to
{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}}
since it satisfies
[
18
, (4.4)]

(3.1)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}\big({\mathsf{c}}((x,t),r)\big):=\,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{b}}_{\gamma}\int_{{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),r)}{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}(s)\mathrm{d}\sigma(y,s)

\displaystyle\sim\,

\displaystyle r^{d}(t+r^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(1-t+r^{2})^{{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}},

where
{\mathsf{b}}_{\gamma}
is the normalized constant defined by
{\mathsf{b}}_{\gamma}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}}{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}(t)\mathrm{d}\sigma(x,t)=1
.

The orthogonality on
L^{2}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}})
is defined in terms of the inner product

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{{\mathsf{w}}}={\mathsf{b}}_{{\gamma}}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}}f(x,t)g(x,t){\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}(t)\mathrm{d}\sigma(x,t),

where
\mathrm{d}\sigma
denote the surface measure on
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
, which is well-defined for all polynomials
restricted on the conic surface. The orthogonal structure is studied in
[
16
]
for a family of more general
weight functions
t^{\beta}(1-t)^{\gamma}
with
{\beta}>-d
and
{\gamma}>-1
. We consider
{\beta}=-1
and
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}

because the addition formula for
{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}
has a simpler form.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}})
be the space of orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
. The reproducing
kernel
{\mathsf{P}}_{n}({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};\cdot,\cdot)
of this space satisfies an addition formula given below.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
. Then, for
(x,t),(y,s)\in{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{n}\big({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};(x,t),(y,s)\big)=b_{{\gamma},d}\int_{[-1,1]^{2}}

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{({\gamma}+d-\frac{3}{2},-\frac{1}{2})}\left(2\zeta(x,t,y,s;v)^{2}-1\right)

\displaystyle\times(1-v_{1}^{2})^{\frac{d-4}{2}}(1-v_{2}^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}v,

where
b_{{\gamma},d}
is a constant so that
{\mathsf{P}}_{0}\big({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};(x,t),(y,s)\big)=1
and

(3.3)

\zeta(x,t,y,s;v)=v_{1}\sqrt{\tfrac{st+{\langle}x,y{\rangle}}{2}}+v_{2}\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s};

moreover, the identity holds under the limit (
2.14
) when
{\gamma}=-\frac{1}{2}
and/or
d=2
.

The identity (
3.2
) is stated in
[
16
]
in terms of
Z_{2n}^{{\gamma}+d-1}
, which we have rewritten using (
2.15
) following
[
18
]
. It gives the addition formula (
2.8
) with
{\alpha}={\gamma}+d-\frac{3}{2}
and

{\beta}=-\frac{1}{2}
. Following the Definition
2.2
, the convolution of
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}})

and
g\in L^{1}([-1,1],w_{{\gamma}+d-\frac{3}{2},-\frac{1}{2}})
, denoted by
f*_{\gamma}g
is defined by

f*_{\gamma}g(x,t):={\mathsf{b}}_{\gamma}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}}f(y,s){\mathsf{T}}^{\gamma}g\big((x,t),(y,s)\big){\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}(s)\mathrm{d}\sigma,

where the operator
g\mapsto{\mathsf{T}}^{\gamma}g
is defined by

{\mathsf{T}}^{\gamma}g\big((x,t),(y,s)\big):=b_{{\gamma},d}\int_{[-1,1]^{2}}g\left(2\zeta(x,t,y,s;v)^{2}-1\right)(1-v_{1}^{2})^{\frac{d-4}{2}}(1-v_{2}^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}v.

We can also define the operator
g\mapsto S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}g
as in (
2.11
) and use it to show that the
convolution
f*_{\gamma}g
satisfies Proposition
2.5
, but this will not be needed below. The maximal
function
{\mathscr{M}}_{\mathsf{w}}
defined in (
2.28
), denote by
{\mathscr{M}}_{\gamma}
for
{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}
, is given by

{\mathscr{M}}_{\gamma}f(x,t):=\sup_{0\leq{\theta}\leq\pi}\frac{f*_{\gamma}\chi_{[0,{\theta}]}(x,t)}{2^{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\theta}\big(\sin\frac{\phi}{2}\big)^{2{\lambda}}\mathrm{d}\phi}\quad\hbox{with}\quad{\lambda}={\gamma}+d-1,

where we have used the elementary identity

\int_{0}^{\theta}(1-\cos\phi)^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}}(1+\cos\phi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sin\phi\,\mathrm{d}\phi=2^{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\theta}\left(\sin\frac{\phi}{2}\right)^{2{\lambda}}\mathrm{d}\phi.

The Poisson integral
{\mathsf{Q}}_{r}({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};f)
, defined by (
2.12
), is the convolution of
f
with

q_{r}^{({\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2})}
, the latter is given by, as the limit of (
2.13
),

q_{r}^{({\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2})}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg[\frac{1-r}{\big(1-2\sqrt{r}\sqrt{\frac{1+u}{2}}+r\big)^{{\gamma}+\mathrm{d}}}+\frac{1-r}{\big(1+2\sqrt{r}\sqrt{\frac{1+u}{2}}+r\big)^{{\gamma}+\mathrm{d}}}\Bigg].

We define the maximal Poisson integral operator on the conic domain by

{\mathsf{Q}}_{*}({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};f,(x,t))=\sup_{0<r<1}{\mathsf{Q}}_{n}({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};|f|,(x,t)).

The following theorem shows that the maximal function is equivalent to the maximal Poisson integral operator
on the conic surface.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
. If
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1};{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}})
, then
for every
(x,t)\in{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
,

c_{1}{\mathscr{M}}_{\gamma}f(x,t)\leq{\mathsf{Q}}_{*}({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};f,(x,t))\leq c_{2}{\mathscr{M}}_{\gamma}f(x,t).

Proof.

Let
{\lambda}={\gamma}+d-1
. The right-hand side inequality is established in Theorem
2.19
. For
the left-hand inequality, we observe that, for
1-\sqrt{r}\sim{\theta}
and
0\leq\phi\leq{\theta}
,

\displaystyle q_{r}^{({\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2})}(\cos\phi)\,

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{1-r}{(1-2\sqrt{r}\cos\frac{\phi}{2}+r)^{{\lambda}+1}}\geq\frac{1-r}{((1-\sqrt{r})^{2}+\sqrt{r}\phi^{2})^{{\lambda}+1}}

\displaystyle\geq c\frac{1-r}{((1-\sqrt{r})^{2}+\sqrt{r}{\theta}^{2})^{{\lambda}+1}}\geq c\,{\theta}^{-2{\lambda}-1},

which implies, in particular, that

\chi_{[\cos{\theta},1]}(\cos\phi)\leq{\theta}^{2{\lambda}+1}q_{r}^{({\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2})}(\cos\phi),\qquad 0\leq\phi\leq\pi.

Consequently, since
\int_{0}^{\theta}(\sin\frac{\phi}{2})^{2{\lambda}}\mathrm{d}\phi\sim{\theta}^{2{\lambda}+1}
, for
0\leq{\theta}\leq\pi
and
r\sim(1-c{\theta})^{2}

we obtain

\frac{|f|\ast_{\gamma}\chi_{[\cos{\theta},1]}(x,t)}{{\theta}^{2{\lambda}+1}}\leq c|f|\ast_{\gamma}q_{r}^{({\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2})}(x,t)\leq c\,{\mathsf{Q}}_{*}({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};f,(x,t)).

Taking supreme over
{\theta}
proves
c_{1}{\mathscr{M}}_{\gamma}f(x,t)\leq{\mathsf{Q}}_{*}({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};f,(x,t))
.
∎

3.2.
Boundedness of the Maximal function

The Hardy-Littlewood maximal function for
({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}},{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}})
is defined by

M_{\gamma}f(x,t):=\sup_{0\leq{\theta}\leq\pi}\frac{1}{{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}\big({\mathsf{c}}((x,t),{\theta})\big)}\int_{{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),{\theta})}|f(y,s)|{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}(s)\mathrm{d}\sigma(y,s),

where
{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),{\theta})
is the conic cap. The main result of the subsection shows that the maximal function

{\mathscr{M}}_{\gamma}f
is bounded by
M_{\gamma}f
. More precisely, our main result in this subsection is the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
. For
f\in L^{1}\left({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}\right)
,

{\mathscr{M}}_{\gamma}f(x,t)\leq c\,M_{\gamma}f(x,t),\qquad(x,t)\in{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}.

Proof.

Rewriting the integral over
{{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),{\theta})}
in
M_{\gamma}f
as an integral over
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
,

\int_{{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),{\theta})}|f(y,s)|{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}(s)\mathrm{d}\sigma(y,s)=\int_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}}|f(y,s)|\chi_{{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),{\theta})}(y,x){\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}(s)\mathrm{d}\sigma(y,s),

where
\chi_{{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),{\theta})}
is the characteristic function of
{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),{\theta})
, we see that it is sufficient to
prove the inequality

\left|{\mathsf{T}}^{\gamma}\chi_{[\cos{\theta},1]}\big((x,t),(y,s)\big)\right|\leq c\frac{1}{{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}\big({\mathsf{c}}((x,t),\frac{{\theta}}{2})\big)}\int_{0}^{\theta}\left(\sin\frac{\phi}{2}\right)^{2{\lambda}}\mathrm{d}\phi\chi_{{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),\frac{{\theta}}{2})}(y,s).

Using
\int_{0}^{\theta}(\sin\frac{\phi}{2})^{2{\lambda}}\mathrm{d}\phi\sim{\theta}^{2{\lambda}+1}
and the relation (
3.1
), the above
inequality is equivalent to the following inequality, for
(x,t),(y,s)\in{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
,

(3.4)

\left|{\mathsf{T}}^{\gamma}\chi_{[\cos{\theta},1]}\big((x,t),(y,s)\big)\right|\leq c\frac{{\theta}^{2{\gamma}+d-1}}{(t+{\theta}^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(1-t+{\theta}^{2})^{{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}}}\chi_{{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),\frac{{\theta}}{2})}(y,s).

We prove this inequality for the case
d>2
and
{\gamma}>-\frac{1}{2}
. The degenerated cases of
d=2
and/or

{\gamma}=-\frac{1}{2}
can be handled similarly and are easier. By the definition of
{\mathsf{T}}^{\gamma}
, our main task is to establish
the inequality

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{LH}:=\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}

\displaystyle\left(|\zeta(x,t,y,s;v)|\right)(1-v_{1}^{2})^{\frac{d-4}{2}}(1-v_{2}^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq c\frac{{\theta}^{2{\gamma}+d-1}}{(t+{\theta}^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(1-t+{\theta}^{2})^{{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}}}

for
(y,s)\in{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),\tfrac{{\theta}}{2})
.
Indeed, since
2\zeta(x,t,y,s;v)^{2}-1\leq\cos{\theta}
is equivalent to
|\zeta(x,t,y,s;v)|\leq\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2}
, we obtain

\chi_{[\cos{\theta},1]}\left(2\zeta(x,t,y,s;v)^{2}-1\right)=\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}\left(|\zeta(x,t,y,s;v)|\right).

Moreover, set
\mathbf{1}=(1,1)
, then
\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s))=\zeta(x,t,y,s;\mathbf{1})\geq 0
, which
implies

\chi_{{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),\frac{{\theta}}{2})}=\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}\left(\zeta(x,t,y,s;\mathbf{1})\right).

Now, if
\zeta(x,t,y,s;\mathbf{1})\leq\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2}
, then
\left|\zeta(x,t,y,s;v)\right|\leq\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2}

for
-1\leq v_{1},v_{2}\leq 1
. Hence, if
\chi_{{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),\frac{{\theta}}{2})}(y,s)=0
then
\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}(|\zeta(x,t,y,s;v|))=0

and, consequently,
{\mathsf{T}}^{\gamma}\chi_{[\cos{\theta},1]}\big((x,t),(y,s)\big)=0
, which justifies the appearance of

\chi_{{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),\frac{{\theta}}{2})}(y,s)
in the right-hand side of (
3.4
), so that it suffices to prove the inequality
(
3.5
). Now, let
\eta(t,s;v)=v_{1}\sqrt{ts}+v_{2}\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}
. Then, using
|{\langle}x,y{\rangle}|\leq ts
for

(x,t),(y,s)\in{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
, we see that
|\zeta(x,t,y,s;v)|
is bounded by both
\eta(t,s;(|v_{1}|,1))
and

\eta(t,s;(1,|v_{2}|))
. The argument that justifies the appearance of
\chi_{{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),\frac{{\theta}}{2})}(y,s)
in (
3.4
)
also shows

\displaystyle\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}\left(|\zeta(x,t,y,s;v)|\right)\leq\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}\left(\eta(t,s;(|v_{1}|,1))\right)\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}\left(\eta(t,s;(1,|v_{2}|))\right),

which implies immediately that

\displaystyle\mathrm{LH}\,

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-1}^{1}\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}\left(|v_{1}|\sqrt{ts}+\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}\right)(1-v_{1}^{2})^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\mathrm{d}v_{1}

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\times\int_{-1}^{1}\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}\left(\sqrt{ts}+|v_{2}|\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}\right)(1-v_{2}^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}v_{2}

\displaystyle=I_{1}\times I_{2}.

For
t=\cos^{2}\frac{{\theta}}{2}
and
s=\cos^{2}\frac{\phi}{2}
in
[0,1]
, the function

{\mathsf{d}}_{[0,1]}(t,s):=\arccos\left(ts+\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}|{\theta}-\phi|

defines a distance on the interval
[0,1]
. From
[
18
, (4.2)]
, we deduce

{\mathsf{d}}_{[0,1]}(t,s)\leq{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s))\quad\hbox{for}\quad(x,t),(y,s)\in{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}.

Hence, if
(y,s)\in{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),\tfrac{{\theta}}{2})
, then
{\mathsf{d}}_{[0,1]}(t,s)\leq\tfrac{{\theta}}{2}
. Thus, the proof of (
3.5
)
is reduced to establish the inequalities

I_{1}\leq c\frac{{\theta}^{d-2}}{(t+{\theta}^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad I_{2}\leq c\frac{{\theta}^{2{\gamma}+1}}{(1-t+{\theta}^{2})^{{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}}}

for
t,s
satisfying
{\mathsf{d}}_{[0,1]}(t,s)\leq\tfrac{{\theta}}{2}
.

We consider
I_{1}
first. If
0\leq\sqrt{t}\leq 2{\theta}
, then
t+{\theta}^{2}\sim{\theta}^{2}
and the desired bound for
I_{1}
holds trivially.
Hence, we only need to consider the case
\sqrt{t}\geq 2{\theta}
. Using symmetry and changing variable

v_{1}\mapsto u=v_{1}\sqrt{ts}+\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}
, the integral
I_{1}
is bounded by

\displaystyle I_{1}\leq 2^{{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}\left(v\sqrt{ts}+\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}\right)(1-v)^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=2^{{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}}^{\sqrt{ts}+\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}}\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}(u)\left(1-\frac{u-\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}}{\sqrt{ts}}\right)^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\sqrt{ts}}

\displaystyle\leq c\frac{1}{(\sqrt{ts})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\int_{\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2}}^{\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{[0,1]}(t,s)}\big(\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{[0,1]}(t,s)-u\big)^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\leq c\frac{1}{(\sqrt{ts})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\big(\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{[0,1]}(t,s)-\cos{\theta}\big)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq c\frac{1}{(\sqrt{ts})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}|{\mathsf{d}}_{[0,1]}(t,s)-{\theta}|\cdot|{\mathsf{d}}_{[0,1]}(t,s)+{\theta}|

\displaystyle\leq c\frac{{\theta}^{d-2}}{(\sqrt{ts})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}

for
{\mathsf{d}}_{[0,1]}(t,s)\leq\frac{{\theta}}{2}
. Since
\sqrt{t}\geq 2{\theta}
, it follows
\sqrt{t}\geq(\sqrt{t}+2{\theta})/2
. Moreover,
using
t=\cos^{2}\frac{{\theta}}{2}
and
s=\cos^{2}\frac{\phi}{2}
, it is easy to see that
|\sqrt{t}-\sqrt{s}|\leq{\mathsf{d}}_{[0,1]}(t,s)
,
so that
\sqrt{s}\geq\sqrt{t}-\frac{{\theta}}{2}\geq(\sqrt{t}+{\theta})/2
if
(y,s)\in{\mathsf{c}}((x,t),\frac{{\theta}}{2})
. Thus,
\sqrt{ts}\geq\frac{1}{4}(t+{\theta}^{2})
,
from which the desired upper bound for
I_{1}
follows. The proof for
I_{2}
follows similarly, using

|\sqrt{1-t}-\sqrt{1-s}|\leq{\mathsf{d}}_{[0,1]}(t,s)
. This completes the proof.
∎

Since
{\mathscr{M}}_{\gamma}f
is dominated by the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function, it follows immediately that it
possesses the usual properties of maximal functions.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
\mathrm{d}\geq 2
and
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
. For
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d},{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}})
,
1<p<\infty
,

\|{\mathscr{M}}_{\gamma}f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}}\leq c_{p}\|f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}},\qquad 1<p<\infty,

and, for
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d},{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}})
and
{\alpha}>0
,

{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}\left\{(x,t)\in{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}:{\mathscr{M}}_{\gamma}f(x,t)>{\alpha}\right\}\leq c\frac{\|f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}}}{\alpha}.

3.3.
Multiplier theorem

The maximal
(C,\delta)
operator with respect to
{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}
is denoted by, in terms of the
(C,\delta)

operators with respect to
{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}
,

S_{*}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};f,(x,t))=\sup_{n\geq 0}\left|S_{n}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};f,(x,t))\right|,\quad(x,t)\in{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}.

Because of Theorem
3.3
, the Assertion 1 holds for
S_{*}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};f)
if
\delta\geq{\lambda}+1=d+{\gamma}

by (
2.29
). The restriction on
\delta
, however, can be relaxed for the conic surface.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
. If
\delta>{\gamma}+d-1
and
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}})
, then for
every
(x,t)\in{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
,

S_{*}^{\delta}({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};f,(x,t))\leq c\,{\mathscr{M}}_{\gamma}f(x,t).

Proof.

Let
{\lambda}={\gamma}+d-1
. Since the case
\delta\geq{\lambda}+1
is already established, we only need to consider
{\lambda}<\delta<{\lambda}+1
.
By the estimate of
k_{n}^{\delta}(w_{{\alpha},{\beta}};u,1)
in
[
14
, (9.4.4) and (9.41.14)]
,

\displaystyle\left|k_{n}^{\delta}(w_{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}};\cos{\theta},1)\right|\,

\displaystyle\leq cn^{{\lambda}-\delta}\left[(1-\cos{\theta}+n^{-2})^{-(\delta+{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2})/2}+1\right]

\displaystyle\leq cn^{{\lambda}-\delta}\left[(n^{-1}+{\theta})^{-(\delta+{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2})}+1\right]=:k_{n}({\theta}).

It is easy to see that
k({\theta})
satisfy the conditions of Theorem
2.18
. Moreover,

\int_{0}^{\pi}k_{n}(\phi)\left(\sin\frac{\phi}{2}\right)^{2{\lambda}}\mathrm{d}\phi\leq cn^{{\lambda}-\delta}\int_{0}^{\pi}\left[(n^{-1}+\phi)^{-(\delta+{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2})}+1\right]\phi^{2{\lambda}}\mathrm{d}{\theta}

is bounded by a constant depending only on
{\gamma}
and
d
, as can be easily seen by considering

0\leq\phi\leq n^{-1}
and
\phi\geq n^{-1}
separately. Consequently, we obtain

\left|S_{n}^{\delta}\left({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};f,(x,t)\right)\right|\leq c{\mathscr{M}}_{\gamma}f(x,t)

for all
n\geq 0
. Taking supreme over
n
completes the proof.
∎

This shows, by Theorem
3.3
, that the Assertion 1 holds for
\delta>{\lambda}
. In particular, by
Theorem
2.11
, the following multiplier theorem holds on the conic surface.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
. Let
\{\mu_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
be a bounded sequence of real numbers such that

\sup_{j\geq 0}2^{j(k-1)}\sum_{\ell=2^{j}+1}^{2^{j+1}}\left|\triangle^{k}\mu_{\ell}\right|\leq M<\infty

for
k\geq\lfloor d+{\gamma}\rfloor
. Then
\{\mu_{j}\}
defines an
L^{p}({\mathbb{V}}_{0},{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}})
multiplier for
1<p<\infty
; that is

\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mu_{k}\operatorname{proj}_{k}({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};f)\right\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}}\leq c\|f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}},\quad 1<p<\infty,

where
c
is independent of
\mu_{j}
and
f
.

4.
Fourier orthogonal series on the solid conic

In this section we work with the cone
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
defined, for
d\geq 1
, by

{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}=\{(x,t):\|x\|\leq t,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,0\leq t\leq 1\},

which is the domain bounded by the conic surface
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
and the hyperplane
t=1
. The development
on this domain is similar to that on the conic surface but has another layer of complication. The structure of this
section is parallel to that of the previous one.

4.1.
Analysis on the conic

For
\mu\geq 0
and
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
, let
W_{{\gamma},\mu}
be the weight function defined by

W_{{\gamma},\mu}(x,t)=(1-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}(1-t)^{\gamma},\qquad(x,t)\in{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}.

We consider the homogeneous space
({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},W_{{\gamma},\mu},{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}})
, where

{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}}
is the distance function on
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
, defined by

{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}}((x,t),(y,s)):=\arccos\left(\sqrt{\frac{{\langle}x,y{\rangle}+\sqrt{t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}}\sqrt{s^{2}-\|y\|^{2}}+ts}{2}}+\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}\right)

for
(x,t),(y,s)\in{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
. For
(x,t)\in{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
and
r>0
, the ball centered at
(x,t)
with radius
r
is
denoted by

{\mathbf{c}}((x,t),r):=\left\{(y,s)\in{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}:{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}}\big((x,t),(y,s)\big)\leq r\right\}.

Then
W_{\mu,{\gamma}}
is a doubling weight with respect to
{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}}
since it satisfies
[
18
, (5.5)]

(4.1)

\displaystyle W_{{\gamma},\mu}\big({\mathbf{c}}((x,t),r)\big):=\,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{b}}_{{\gamma},\mu}\int_{{\mathbf{c}}((x,t),r)}W_{{\gamma},\mu}(y,s)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\sim\,

\displaystyle r^{d+1}(t+r^{2})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}(1-t+r^{2})^{{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}}(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}+r^{2})^{\mu},

where
{\mathbf{b}}_{\mu,{\gamma}}
is the normalized constant defined by
{\mathbf{b}}_{\mu,{\gamma}}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}W_{{\gamma},\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t=1
.

The orthogonality on
L^{2}({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},W_{{\gamma},\mu})
is defined in terms of the inner product

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{W}={\mathbf{b}}_{\mu,{\gamma}}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}f(x,t)g(x,t)W_{{\gamma},\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

The orthogonal structure is studied in
[
16
]
for a family of more general weight functions

t^{\beta}(1-t)^{\gamma}(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}
with
{\beta}>-d
and
{\gamma}>-1
and
\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
. We consider
{\beta}=0
,

{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
and
\mu\geq 0
because the addition formula for
W_{{\gamma},\mu}
is more manageable.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},W_{{\gamma},\mu})
be the space of orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
. The reproducing
kernel
{\mathbf{P}}_{n}(W_{{\gamma},\mu};\cdot,\cdot)
of this space satisfies a closed form formula given below.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
\mu\geq 0
and
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
. Then

(4.2)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}_{n}\big(W_{{\gamma},\mu};(x,t),(y,s)\big)=\,

\displaystyle c_{\mu,{\gamma},d}\int_{[-1,1]^{3}}Z_{n}^{({\gamma}+d+2\mu-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2})}\left(2\xi(x,t,y,s;u,v)^{2}-1\right)

\displaystyle\times(1-u^{2})^{\mu-1}(1-v_{1}^{2})^{\mu+\frac{d-3}{2}}(1-v_{2}^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v,

where
c_{\mu,{\gamma},d}
is a constant, so that
{\mathbf{P}}_{0}=1
and

\xi(x,t,y,s;u,v)\in[-1,1]
is defined by

(4.3)

\displaystyle\xi(x,t,y,s;u,v)=

\displaystyle\,v_{1}\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\left(ts+{\langle}x,y{\rangle}+\sqrt{t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}}\sqrt{s^{2}-\|y\|^{2}}\,u\right)}

\displaystyle+v_{2}\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}.

When
\mu=0
and
d=1
or
\mu=0
and/or
{\gamma}=-\frac{1}{2}
, the identity (
4.2
) holds under the limit (
2.14
).

The identity (
4.2
) is stated in
[
16
]
in terms of
Z_{2n}^{{\lambda}}
, which we have rewritten
using (
2.15
). This gives the addition formula (
2.8
) with
{\alpha}={\gamma}+d+2\mu-\frac{1}{2}
and

{\beta}=-\frac{1}{2}
. Following Definition
2.2
, the convolution of
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},W_{{\gamma},\mu})

and
g\in L^{1}([-1,1],w_{{\gamma}+d+2\mu-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}})
, denoted by
f*_{{\gamma},\mu}g
, is defined by

f*_{{\gamma},\mu}g(x,t):={\mathbf{b}}_{\mu,{\gamma}}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}f(y,s){\mathbf{T}}^{{\gamma},\mu}g\big((x,t),(y,s)\big)W_{{\gamma},\mu}(y,s)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s,

where the operator
g\mapsto{\mathbf{T}}^{{\gamma},\mu}g
is defined by

\displaystyle{\mathbf{T}}^{{\gamma},\mu}g\big((x,t),(y,s)\big):=\,

\displaystyle c_{\mu,{\gamma},d}\int_{[-1,1]^{3}}g\left(2\xi(x,t,y,s;u,v)^{2}-1\right)

\displaystyle\times(1-u^{2})^{\mu-1}(1-v_{1}^{2})^{{\alpha}-1}(1-v_{2}^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v.

The maximal function
{\mathscr{M}}_{\mathsf{w}}
defined in (
2.28
), denote by
{\mathscrbf{M}}_{\mu,{\gamma}}
for
W_{{\gamma},\mu}
, is given by

{\mathscrbf{M}}_{{\gamma},\mu}f(x,t):=\sup_{0\leq{\theta}\leq\pi}\frac{f*_{{\gamma},\mu}\chi_{[0,{\theta}]}(x,t)}{2^{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\theta}\big(\sin\frac{\phi}{2}\big)^{2{\lambda}}\mathrm{d}\phi}\quad\hbox{with}\quad{\lambda}={\gamma}+d+2\mu.

Let
{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}(W_{{\gamma},\mu};f)
denote the Poisson integral operator on
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
, defined as in (
2.12
)
with
{\mathsf{w}}=W_{{\gamma},\mu}
. As in the case of conic surface, the above maximal function is equivalent to the maximal
Poisson integral operator

{\mathbf{Q}}_{*}(W_{{\gamma},\mu};f,(x,t))=\sup_{0<r<1}{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}(W_{{\gamma},\mu};|f|,(x,t)).

Indeed, the Poisson operator is again the convolution of
f
with
q_{r}^{({\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2})}
, where
{\lambda}={\gamma}+d+2\mu
,
and the proof of Theorem
3.2
requires only an estimate on
q_{r}^{({\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2})}
; hence, the proof of
the theorem below follows exactly as in the case of the conic surface.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
and
\mu\geq 0
. If
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1};W_{{\gamma},\mu})
, then
for every
(x,t)\in{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
,

c_{1}{\mathscrbf{M}}_{{\gamma},\mu}f(x,t)\leq{\mathbf{Q}}_{*}(W_{{\gamma},\mu};f,(x,t))\leq c_{2}{\mathscrbf{M}}_{{\gamma},\mu}f(x,t).

4.2.
Boundedness of the Maximal function

The Hardy-Littlewood maximal function for
({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},W_{{\gamma},\mu},{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}})
is defined by

{\mathbf{M}}_{{\gamma},\mu}f(x,t):=\sup_{0\leq{\theta}\leq\pi}\frac{1}{W_{{\gamma},\mu}\big({\mathbf{c}}((x,t),{\theta})\big)}\int_{{\mathbf{c}}((x,t),{\theta})}|f(y,s)|W_{{\gamma},\mu}(y,s)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s,

where
{\mathbf{c}}((x,t),{\theta})
is the ball on the cone. We show that the maximal function
{\mathscrbf{M}}_{{\gamma},\mu}f
is bounded by

{\mathbf{M}}_{{\gamma},\mu}f
, just like in the case of conic surface.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
and
\mu\geq 0
. For
f\in L^{1}\left({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},W_{{\gamma},\mu}\right)
,

{\mathscrbf{M}}_{{\gamma},\mu}f(x,t)\leq c\,{\mathbf{M}}_{{\gamma},\mu}f(x,t),\qquad(x,t)\in{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}.

Proof.

The proof is parallel to that of Theorem
3.3
. Let
{\lambda}={\gamma}+d+2\mu
. Following the argument
of the conic surface, the proof is reduced to show

(4.4)

\left|{\mathbf{T}}^{{\gamma},\mu}\chi_{[\cos{\theta},1]}\big((x,t),(y,s)\big)\right|\leq c\frac{{\theta}^{2{\lambda}-d}\chi_{{\mathbf{c}}((x,t),\frac{{\theta}}{2})}(y,s)}{(t+{\theta}^{2})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}(1-t+{\theta}^{2})^{{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}}(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}+{\theta}^{2})^{\mu}}.

We again prove only the non-degenerated case with
d>1
,
{\gamma}>-\frac{1}{2}
and
\mu>0
. By the definition of

{\mathbf{T}}^{{\gamma},\mu}
and
\chi_{{\mathbf{c}}((x,t),\frac{{\theta}}{2})}(y,s)=\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}\left(\xi(x,t,y,s;1,\mathbf{1})\right)
, using
the argument in the proof of Theorem
3.3
, we see that the main task for
proving (
4.4
) is to establish the inequality

\displaystyle\mathrm{LH}:=\int_{[-1,1]^{3}}\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}

\displaystyle\left(|\xi(x,t,y,s;u,v)|\right)(1-u^{2})^{\mu-1}(1-v_{1}^{2})^{\mu+\frac{d-3}{2}}(1-v_{2}^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq c\frac{{\theta}^{2{\lambda}-d}}{(t+{\theta}^{2})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}(1-t+{\theta}^{2})^{{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}}(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}+{\theta}^{2})^{\mu}}

for
(y,s)\in{\mathbf{c}}((x,t),\tfrac{{\theta}}{2})
. Using
|{\langle}x,y{\rangle}+\sqrt{t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}}\sqrt{s^{2}-\|y\|^{2}}|\leq ts
, it is easy
to see that

|\xi(x,t,y,s;u,v)|
is bounded by both
\eta(t,s;(|v_{1}|,1))
and
\eta(t,s;(1,|v_{2}|))
, where

\eta(t,s;v)=v_{1}\sqrt{ts}+v_{2}\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}
. Moroever, by
|v_{1}|\leq 1
and
|v_{2}|\leq 1
,

|\xi(x,t,y,s;u,v)|
is also bounded by
\xi(x,t,y,s;u,\mathbf{1})
. Consequently, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{LH}\,

\displaystyle\leq c\int_{-1}^{1}\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}\left(|v_{1}|\sqrt{ts}+\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}\right)(1-v_{1}^{2})^{\mu+\frac{d-3}{2}}\mathrm{d}v_{1}

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\times\int_{-1}^{1}\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}\left(\sqrt{ts}+|v_{2}|\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}\right)(1-v_{2}^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}v_{2}

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\times\int_{-1}^{1}\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}\left(\xi(x,t,y,s;u,\mathbf{1})\right)(1-u^{2})^{\mu-1}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}.

The first two terms,
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
, can be estimated exactly as in Theorem
3.3
, using

\big|\sqrt{t}-\sqrt{s}\big|\leq{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}}((x,t),(y,s))\quad\hbox{and}\quad\big|\sqrt{1-t}-\sqrt{1-s}\big|\leq{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}}((x,t),(y,s))

given in
[
18
, Lemma 5.4]
. Thus, it remains to prove

I_{3}\leq c\frac{{\theta}^{2\mu}}{\left(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}+{\theta}^{2}\right)^{\mu}},\qquad(y,s)\in{\mathbf{c}}((x,t),\tfrac{{\theta}}{2}).

This inequality holds trivially if
t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}\leq 4{\theta}^{2}
. We assume
t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}\geq 4{\theta}^{2}
below, so that

t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}\geq(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}+{\theta})/2
. Moroever, by
[
18
, Lemma 5.4]
,

|\sqrt{t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}}-\sqrt{s^{2}-\|y\|^{2}}|\leq{\mathsf{d}}_{\mathbb{V}}((x,t),(y,s)),

it follows that, for
(y,s)\in{\mathbf{c}}((x,t),\tfrac{{\theta}}{2})
,

s^{2}-\|y\|^{2}\geq\left(\sqrt{t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}}-{\theta}/2\right)^{2}\geq(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}+{\theta})/4.

Let
\phi(t,s)=\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}
. Since
\xi(x,t,y,s;1,\mathbf{1})=\arccos{\mathsf{d}}_{\mathbb{V}}((x,t),(y,s))
, we can
make a change of variables
u\mapsto v=\xi(x,t,y,s;u,\mathbf{1})
in the integral of
I_{3}
to obtain

\displaystyle I_{3}\,

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-1}^{1}\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}\left(\xi(x,t,y,s;u,\mathbf{1})\right)(1-u)^{\mu-1}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\int_{\xi(x,t,y,s;-1,\mathbf{1})}^{\xi(x,t,y,s;1,\mathbf{1})}\chi_{[\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2},1]}(v)\left(1-\frac{2(v-\phi(t,s))^{2}-(ts+{\langle}x,y{\rangle})}{\sqrt{t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}}\sqrt{s^{2}-\|y\|^{2}}}\right)^{\mu-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\frac{4(v-\phi(t,s))}{\sqrt{t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}}\sqrt{s^{2}-\|y\|^{2}}}\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\leq c\int_{\cos\frac{{\theta}}{2}}^{\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}}((x,t),(y,s))}\left(\big(\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}}((x,t),(y,s))-\phi(t,s)\big)^{2}-(v-\phi(t,s))^{2}\right)^{\mu-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\frac{v-\phi(t,s)}{\left(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}+{\theta}^{2}\right)^{\mu}}\mathrm{d}v.

The last integral can be integrated out, so that

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle\leq c\frac{1}{\left(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}+{\theta}^{2}\right)^{\mu}}\left(\big(\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}}((x,t),(y,s))-\phi(t,s)\big)^{2}-\big(\cos\tfrac{{\theta}}{2}-\phi(t,s)\big)^{2}\right)^{\mu}

\displaystyle\leq c\frac{1}{\left(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}+{\theta}^{2}\right)^{\mu}}\left(\cos{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}}((x,t),(y,s))-\cos\tfrac{{\theta}}{2}\right)^{\mu}

\displaystyle\leq c\frac{{\theta}^{2\mu}}{\left(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}+{\theta}^{2}\right)^{\mu}}.

This establishes the desired estimate for
I_{3}
and completes the proof.
∎

As in the case of conic surface, the
{\mathscrbf{M}}_{{\gamma},\mu}
inherits the properties of the Hardy-Littlewood
maximal function
{\mathbf{M}}_{{\gamma},\mu}
.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
\mathrm{d}\geq 1
,
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
and
\mu\geq 0
. For
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{V}}^{d},W_{{\gamma},\mu})
,
1<p<\infty
,

\|{\mathscrbf{M}}_{{\gamma},\mu}f\|_{p,W_{{\gamma},\mu}}\leq c_{p}\|f\|_{p,W_{{\gamma},\mu}},\qquad 1<p<\infty,

and, for
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{V}}^{d},W_{{\gamma},\mu})
and
{\alpha}>0
,

W_{{\gamma},\mu}\left\{(x,t)\in{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}:{\mathscrbf{M}}_{{\gamma},\mu}f(x,t)>{\alpha}\right\}\leq c\frac{\|f\|_{p,W_{{\gamma},\mu}}}{\alpha}.

4.3.
Multiplier theorem

The Cesàro
(C,\delta)
operator with respect to
W_{{\gamma},\mu}
and its maximal
(C,\delta)
operator
are denoted by

S_{*}^{\delta}(W_{{\gamma},\mu};f,(x,t))=\sup_{n\geq 0}\left|S_{n}^{\delta}(W_{{\gamma},\mu};f,(x,t))\right|,\quad(x,t)\in{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}.

We show that the Assertion 1 holds with
\delta_{0}={\gamma}+d+2\mu
, which is sharper than the one follows from
(
2.29
) and Theorem
4.3
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
and
\mu\geq 0
. If
\delta>{\gamma}+d+2\mu
and
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},W_{{\gamma},\mu})
, then
for every
(x,t)\in{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
,

S_{*}^{\delta}(W_{{\gamma},\mu};f,(x,t))\leq c\,{\mathscrbf{M}}_{{\gamma},\mu}f(x,t).

The proof of this theorem follows exactly as in that of Theorem
3.5
. Consequently, by
Theorem
2.11
, we obtain the following multiplier theorem on the cone.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
and
\mu\geq 0
. Let
\{\mu_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
be a bounded sequence of real
numbers such that

\sup_{j\geq 0}2^{j(k-1)}\sum_{\ell=2^{j}+1}^{2^{j+1}}\left|\triangle^{k}\mu_{\ell}\right|\leq M<\infty

for
k\geq\lfloor d+{\gamma}+2\mu\rfloor
. Then
\{\mu_{j}\}
defines an
L^{p}({\mathbb{V}},W_{{\gamma},\mu})
multiplier for

1<p<\infty
; that is

\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mu_{k}\operatorname{proj}_{k}(W_{{\gamma},\mu};f)\right\|_{p,W_{{\gamma},\mu}}\leq c\|f\|_{p,W_{{\gamma},\mu}},\quad 1<p<\infty,

where
c
is independent of
\mu_{j}
and
f
.
