Abstract.
We consider the Keplerian arcs around a fixed Newtonian center joining two prescribed distinct positions in a prescribed flight time. We prove that, putting aside the “opposition case” where infinitely many planes of motion are possible, there are at most two such arcs of each “type”. There is a bilinear quantity that we call
b
which is in all the cases a good parameter for the Keplerian arcs joining two distinct positions. The flight time satisfies a “variational” differential equation in
b
, and is a convex function of
b
.

1. Introduction

Gauss, 1809
wrote in his Theoria motus:

“Hence, inversely, it is apparent that two radius vectors given in magnitude and position, together with the time in which the heavenly body describes the intermediate space, determine the whole orbit. But this problem, to be considered among the most important in the theory of the motions of the heavenly bodies, is not so easily solved, since the expression of the time in terms of the elements is transcendental, and, moreover, very complicated.”

What Gauss calls “this problem” is called today Lambert’s problem:
given a center of attraction, an initial position and a final position, find an arc of Keplerian orbit joining the two positions in a given time
\tau
. Gauss gave two numerical methods to solve this problem, and modern authors have published dozens of efficient methods. Strangely enough, despite the prestigious mathematical descent of the problem, the basic question of the number of the solutions remained open. This number is correctly guessed for example in
Gooding, 1990
. We only know one publication addressing the question of the proof, by
Simó, 1973
, and giving a proof in the main cases. But a further convexity argument is needed to get this number in the multirevolution cases, requiring long computations which are only sketched by Simó. We have already presented some simple proofs in
Albouy and Ureña, 2020
. We can now complete this preliminary work. The unsolved questions about the number of the solutions completing a prescribed number of half-turns are now solved by observing a linear differential equation of the first order. We get stronger results by introducing two new parameters for the family of arcs with given ends. The flight time
\tau
is a convex function of each of them. The first parameter, the inverse of the angular momentum
C
, becomes singular when the line from the initial to the final positions includes the attracting center. We will explain why this singularity is a necessary consequence of a good property: the energy is a rational function of
C
or
1/C
. The second parameter
b
does not have this singularity: it is a good parameter in all the cases. But the expression of the energy contains square roots.

To solve Lambert’s problem
is to compute the Keplerian arcs corresponding to a given triangle
{\rm O}{\rm A}{\rm B}
and a positive flight time. Lambert’s problem is a root finding problem, where the question is to find the values of
C
or
b
which correspond to the given value of
\tau
. Our convexity results have an important practical consequence: Newton’s method for finding the roots is robust.

Hénon, 1968
found numerous cases where several Keplerian arcs start from a point of spacetime and arrive at another point of spacetime. In his study, one of the arcs is described by the Earth on its nearly circular orbit. He proposed a second arc as a possible trajectory for an interplanetary spacecraft, which would return to the Earth without spending energy.

Beyond the practical applications, a surprising aspect of our Theorems is their uniformity. Putting aside some obvious exceptions, the number of arcs does not depend on the initial and the final positions. It is the same whatever the prescribed number of completed turns. The arcs change continuously but never disappear when the prescribed flight time is increased.

2. Statement of the main results

2.1. Definitions.
A
Keplerian arc
is a solution of the Kepler problem restricted to a finite interval of time. The length of this interval is called the
flight time
. The center of attraction is denoted by
{\rm O}
. The arc starts at a point
{\rm A}
and ends at a point
{\rm B}
. For a nonflat triangle
{\rm O}{\rm A}{\rm B}
the
type

k
of a Keplerian arc is the number of completed half-turns around
{\rm O}
. An arc of type
k\geq 2
is called
multirevolution
. The arc then belongs to an elliptic solution of the Kepler problem. If for example
k=2
or
k=3
, the arc starts at
{\rm A}
, passes through
{\rm B}
and
{\rm A}
, and ends at
{\rm B}
. If
k=0
or
k=1
, the arc is called
simple
and the Keplerian solution may be elliptic, parabolic or hyperbolic.

2.2. Theorem.
Given any three points
{\rm O}
,
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}
forming a nonflat triangle, and any flight time
\tau>0
, there is exactly one arc of type 0 and exactly one arc of type 1.
Given any integer number
k\geq 2
, there is a positive real number
{\cal T}_{k}
such that the number of arcs of type
k
is zero if
\tau<{\cal T}_{k}
, one if
\tau={\cal T}_{k}
, two if
\tau>{\cal T}_{k}
.

Proofs.
See §6 for a proof and §8 for a second proof.

Euler’s formula and related formulas.
Let
r_{\rm A}=\|{\rm O}{\rm A}\|
,
r_{\rm B}=\|{\rm O}{\rm B}\|
,
c=\|{\rm A}{\rm B}\|
,
s=r_{\rm A}+r_{\rm B}
. At the unique parabolic arc of type 0 and at the unique parabolic arc of type 1 the flight times are respectively

{\cal T}_{0}=\frac{(s+c)^{3/2}-(s-c)^{3/2}}{6},\quad{\cal T}_{1}=\frac{(s+c)^{3/2}+(s-c)^{3/2}}{6}.

(1)

Let
\Delta/2
be the oriented area of the triangle
{\rm O}{\rm A}{\rm B}
. The respective angular momenta of these parabolic arcs are

{\cal C}_{0}=\frac{2\Delta}{\sqrt{s^{2}-c^{2}}(\sqrt{s+c}-\sqrt{s-c})},\qquad{\cal C}_{1}=-\frac{2\Delta}{\sqrt{s^{2}-c^{2}}(\sqrt{s+c}+\sqrt{s-c})}.

(2)

Here Heron’s formula for the area of the triangle

4\Delta^{2}=(s^{2}-c^{2})(c^{2}-(r_{\rm A}-r_{\rm B})^{2})
gives a simplification and the value
{\cal C}_{0}^{-2}={\cal C}_{1}^{-2}=(r_{\rm A}^{-1}+r_{\rm B}^{-1})/2
in the case
s=c
.

Remark.

Euler, 1743
obtained the first formula (
1
) as the result of a simplification in the end of a computation. As it depends only on
c
and
s
,
{\cal T}_{0}
is the same for all the class of triangles with same
s
and
c
, which includes a flat triangle where
{\rm B}
is between
{\rm O}
and
{\rm A}
. For such a triangle, the motion is rectilinear and formula (
1
) is easy to understand. In 1761, Lambert proved that the flight time, the energy,
s
and
c
are functionally dependent. Euler immediately expressed to Lambert his admiration. Many proofs were subsequently proposed, as well as sign discussions (see
Albouy, 2019
).

2.3. Proposition.
For a nonflat triangle
{\rm O}{\rm A}{\rm B}
, the above special flight times satisfy
0<{\cal T}_{0}<{\cal T}_{1}<{\cal T}_{2}<{\cal T}_{3}<\cdots
. Moreover, for any
k\geq 0
,
{\cal T}_{k+2}-{\cal T}_{k}>(\pi/4)(s+c)^{3/2}
.

Proof.
For a given elliptic orbit we have
\tau_{k}+P=\tau_{k+2}
where
\tau_{l}
is the time to go from
{\rm A}
to
{\rm B}
along the arc of type
l
, and
P=2\pi a^{3/2}
is the Keplerian period, where
a=-(2H)^{-1}
is the semi-major axis. We write
\inf\tau_{k}+\inf P<\inf\tau_{k+2}
where the infimum is on all the elliptic orbits passing through
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}
. The inequality is strict since the variational differential equation (
9
) excludes
\tau^{\prime}=H^{\prime}=0
. This gives the last inequality of the Proposition since according to
Gauss, 1809
, §106, the ellipse of minimal period has its second focus on the chord
{\rm A}{\rm B}
, and since
{\cal T}_{k}=\inf\tau_{k}
on the elliptic orbits, in both cases
k\leq 1
and
k\geq 2
. The inequality
{\cal T}_{2k}<{\cal T}_{2k+1}
is easily obtained by Lambert’s reduction (see 8.1) to the rectilinear case
0<x_{\rm B}<x_{\rm A}
. Here, starting with the same initial velocity
v_{\rm A}
, the arc of type
2k+1
takes more time than the arc of type
2k
, since when the latter reaches
{\rm B}
, the first still have to bounce and to reach
{\rm B}
again. The inequality
{\cal T}_{2k+1}<{\cal T}_{2k+2}
is obtained by a similar argument. QED

2.4. Corollary.
In the planar Kepler problem, for any endpoints
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}
,
{\rm A}\neq{\rm B}
, for any flight time
\tau>0
, the total number of the Keplerian arcs is finite. It is two when
\tau
is small enough. It increases with
\tau
and tends to infinity as
\tau\to+\infty
.

2.5. Proposition.
Consider the parabolic arc of type 0 and flight time
{\cal T}_{0}
, the parabolic arc of type 1 and flight time
{\cal T}_{1}
and the unique arc of type
k\geq 2
and flight time
{\cal T}_{k}
introduced in Theorem 2.2. Let
{\cal C}_{k}
,
k=0,1,2,\dots
be their respective angular momenta. Then

{\cal T}_{k}=\frac{4(s\Gamma_{k}^{2}+2c^{2})\Delta}{3(4c^{2}-\Gamma_{k}^{4}){\cal C}_{k}},\quad\hbox{where}\quad\Gamma_{k}=\frac{2\Delta}{\sqrt{s^{2}-c^{2}}{\cal C}_{k}}.

(3)

Proof.
We can check directly this formula in the cases
k=0
and
k=1
from (
1
) and (
2
). Note that the result of the computation is not so easy to predict. The formula is indeed a surprising consequence of the variational differential equation (
9
). This formula becomes
3\tau H^{\prime}=2
in both cases
k\leq 1
and
k\geq 2
. It is enough to rationalize this formula by using the angular momentum
C
as an intermediate variable:
H^{\prime}=({\rm d}H/{\rm d}C)({\rm d}C/{\rm d}b)
. QED

2.6. Remark.
We will extend Theorem 2.2 to the excluded cases where
{\rm O}
,
{\rm A}
,
{\rm B}
are collinear. In a first case, which we call the
opposition case
,
{\rm O}
is strictly between
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}
. In a second case, which we call the
rectilinear case
,
{\rm B}
is strictly between
{\rm O}
and
{\rm A}
. The statement may be adapted in order to include each of these cases. However, the adaptation is different in each case. In the opposition case, the arcs of types
2k
and
2k+1
are isometric. Indeed, any Keplerian arc may be rotated in space around the axis
{\rm A}{\rm O}{\rm B}
, giving infinitely many arcs. We may still have a statement where even and odd types are replaced by clockwise and anticlockwise types, and where the motions are all restricted to a given plane.
In the rectilinear case, the orbits are rectilinear, the clockwise and anticlockwise planar orbits merge together. The motion may experience collisions, after which it is classically extended by a bouncing which preserves the energy. The number
k
of half-turns should be adapted and defined as the number of completed periods plus the number of collisions with
{\rm O}
. In the more particular cases
{\rm O}={\rm B}
or
{\rm A}={\rm B}
, the questions are simpler, but the statements are different. We restrict the statement of the Theorem to the main case in order to avoid a too long statement. However, the main novelty of this work is the introduction of a variable
b
which parametrizes the Keplerian arcs in all the cases with
{\rm A}\neq{\rm B}
. Our method of proof is carefully selected in order to apply to all these cases. This may be useful, since the collinearity of
{\rm O}
,
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}
creates technical problems when the question is to follow a Keplerian arc changing while
{\rm A}
or
{\rm B}
are continuously changing (see
Russell, 2022
).

3. Brief description of the arcs from
{\rm A}
to
{\rm B}

We always consider that after a collision with
{\rm O}
the Keplerian arcs are continued in the classical way. The velocity tends to infinity when going to collision. The body “bounces” and goes back on the same rectilinear path with opposite velocity. The energy is unchanged at the collision. See e.g.
Albouy, 2019
.

3.1. Definition.
Given two points
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}
,
{\rm A}\neq{\rm O}
,
{\rm B}\neq{\rm O}
, we say that the pair of vectors
(p_{\rm A},p_{\rm B})
is a
pair of compatible terminal velocities
or in brief a
terminal pair
if the Keplerian orbit around
{\rm O}
which passes at
{\rm A}
with velocity
p_{\rm A}
passes at
{\rm B}
with velocity
p_{\rm B}
.

3.2. Remark.
If there is an arc starting from
{\rm A}
with velocity
p_{\rm A}
and
later
arriving at
{\rm B}
with velocity
p_{\rm B}
, then
(p_{\rm A},p_{\rm B})
is a terminal pair. As well, if there is an arc starting from
(q_{\rm B},p_{\rm B})
and later arriving at
(q_{\rm A},p_{\rm A})
, then
(p_{\rm A},p_{\rm B})
is a terminal pair. Both sorts of arc coexist in the case of an elliptic orbit. Terminal pairs may be continuously changed from a situation where there is a unique hyperbolic arc which is from
{\rm A}
to
{\rm B}
to a situation where there is a unique hyperbolic arc which is from
{\rm B}
to
{\rm A}
. These arcs are the two hyperbolic continuations of two complementary elliptic arcs.

The classical first integrals of the planar Kepler problem, where
q=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
p=\dot{q}=(v,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, are the angular momentum
C
, the eccentricity vector
(\alpha,\beta)
and the energy
H
:

C=xw-yv,\quad\alpha=\frac{x}{r}-Cw,\quad\beta=\frac{y}{r}+Cv,\quad H=\frac{v^{2}+w^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{r}.

By combining the first three equalities we find the equation of the orbit
r=\alpha x+\beta y+C^{2}
. Note that if the orbit is hyperbolic only the relevant branch of the hyperbola is represented by this equation.

3.3. Proposition.
The two vectors
p_{\rm A}
and
p_{\rm B}
form a terminal pair if and only if the angular momentum, the eccentricity vector and the energy have the same value at
(q_{\rm A},p_{\rm A})
as at
(q_{\rm B},p_{\rm B})
.

Proof.
In the nonrectilinear case we assume a common value at
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}
of
(C,\alpha,\beta)
. It determines the equation of a Keplerian orbit on which at least an arc may be found. So the pair of velocities is a terminal pair. In the rectilinear case, the energy is the only relevant first integral. Starting on the
{\rm O}x
axis at
(x_{\rm A},v_{\rm A})
, the body passes at
x_{\rm B}
with the velocity
v_{\rm B}
or
-v_{\rm B}
due to the energy conservation. If it is
-v_{\rm B}
at a passage, then it is
v_{\rm B}
at another passage. Meanwhile occurs a bouncing at
{\rm O}
or a culmination, which changes the sign of the velocity. QED

3.4. Proposition.
We assume that the triangle
{\rm O}{\rm A}{\rm B}
is not flat.
The map which associates to a terminal pair its angular momentum is one to one. Consequently for any angular momentum
C\in\;]-\infty,0[\;\cup\;]0,+\infty[
, there is a unique terminal pair
(p_{\rm A},p_{\rm B})
with angular momentum
C
. This pair is a linear combination of
C
and
C^{-1}
with coefficients depending on
{\rm O}{\rm A}{\rm B}
.

Proof.
When fixing a nonzero
C
, Proposition 3.3 gives a linear system of 4 equations in the 4 variables
(v_{\rm A},w_{\rm A},v_{\rm B},w_{\rm B})
:

{\displaystyle x_{\rm A}w_{\rm A}-y_{\rm A}v_{\rm A}=C,\quad-w_{\rm A}+w_{\rm B}}

{\displaystyle=-C^{-1}r_{\rm A}^{-1}x_{\rm A}+C^{-1}r_{\rm B}^{-1}x_{\rm B},}

{\displaystyle x_{\rm B}w_{\rm B}-y_{\rm B}v_{\rm B}=C,\qquad v_{\rm A}-v_{\rm B}}

{\displaystyle=-C^{-1}r_{\rm A}^{-1}y_{\rm A}+C^{-1}r_{\rm B}^{-1}y_{\rm B}.}

(4)

The energy integral is omitted, since the energies are equal if these 4 equations are satisfied. The determinant
x_{\rm A}y_{\rm B}-y_{\rm A}x_{\rm B}=\Delta
is nonzero due to the nonflatness hypothesis. QED

Formula for the terminal velocities.
Let us solve the linear system (
4
). The right-hand side is sum of a term in
C
and a term in
C^{-1}
. Thus the solution is sum of a term in
C
and a term in
C^{-1}
. The term in
C
satisfies
v_{\rm A}=v_{\rm B}
and
w_{\rm A}=w_{\rm B}
. Then
v_{\rm A}=(x_{\rm B}-x_{\rm A})C/\Delta
,
w_{\rm A}=(y_{\rm B}-y_{\rm A})C/\Delta
.
The term in
C^{-1}
satisfies
(v_{\rm A},w_{\rm A})=\lambda_{\rm A}(x_{\rm A},y_{\rm A})
,
(v_{\rm B},w_{\rm B})=\lambda_{\rm B}(x_{\rm B},y_{\rm B})
for some
(\lambda_{\rm A},\lambda_{\rm B})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then

C\lambda_{\rm A}\Delta=r_{\rm B}-\frac{x_{\rm A}x_{\rm B}+y_{\rm A}y_{\rm B}}{r_{\rm A}}=\frac{2r_{\rm A}r_{\rm B}+c^{2}-r_{\rm A}^{2}-r_{\rm B}^{2}}{2r_{\rm A}},

C\lambda_{\rm B}\Delta=\frac{x_{\rm A}x_{\rm B}+y_{\rm A}y_{\rm B}}{r_{\rm B}}-r_{\rm A}=\frac{-c^{2}+r_{\rm A}^{2}+r_{\rm B}^{2}-2r_{\rm A}r_{\rm B}}{2r_{\rm B}}.

Heron’s formula is

4\Delta^{2}=(s^{2}-c^{2})(c^{2}-(r_{\rm A}-r_{\rm B})^{2})
so

\frac{C}{\Delta}\lambda_{\rm A}=\frac{2}{r_{\rm A}(s^{2}-c^{2})},\qquad\frac{C}{\Delta}\lambda_{\rm B}=-\frac{2}{r_{\rm B}(s^{2}-c^{2})}.

The solution is

\pmatrix{v_{\rm A}\cr w_{\rm A}\cr v_{\rm B}\cr w_{\rm B}}=\frac{C}{\Delta}\pmatrix{x_{\rm B}-x_{\rm A}\cr y_{\rm B}-y_{\rm A}\cr x_{\rm B}-x_{\rm A}\cr y_{\rm B}-y_{\rm A}}+\frac{2\Delta}{(s^{2}-c^{2})C}\pmatrix{r_{\rm A}^{-1}x_{\rm A}\cr r_{\rm A}^{-1}y_{\rm A}\cr-r_{\rm B}^{-1}x_{\rm B}\cr-r_{\rm B}^{-1}y_{\rm B}}.

(5)

Similar formulas with other expressions of the two coefficients were given by
Jacobi, 1837
,
Jacobi, 1838
, p. 92, p. 96,
Godal, 1961
or
Battin, 1987
. Terminal velocities are described in Godal as drawing a hyperbola when
C
varies. The formula may be interpreted as interpolating the case
C=0^{+}
, where the arc is in the limit the segment
{\rm B}{\rm O}
then the segment
{\rm O}{\rm A}
, and the case
C=\infty
, where the arc is the segment
{\rm A}{\rm B}
.

3.5. Corollary.
Assume
\Delta>0
. The real numbers
{\cal C}_{0}
and
{\cal C}_{1}
given by (
2
) satisfy
0<-{\cal C}_{1}<{\cal C}_{0}
. The Keplerian arcs of type
k
have an angular momentum
C\in D_{k}
where
D_{0}=]-{\cal C}_{1},+\infty[
,
D_{1}=]-{\cal C}_{0},0[
and for any
m>1
,
D_{2m}=]-{\cal C}_{1},{\cal C}_{0}[
,
D_{2m+1}=]-{\cal C}_{0},{\cal C}_{1}[
. For any
C\in D_{k}
there is a unique arc of type
k
and of angular momentum
C
. Its terminal velocities are given by (
5
).

This Proposition only admits the arcs with
t_{\rm A}<t_{\rm B}
. An arc with
t_{\rm A}>t_{\rm B}
is obtained from an arc with
t_{\rm A}<t_{\rm B}
by changing the signs of the terminal velocities and consequently the sign of
C
. It is natural to consider that for example there are Keplerian arcs with
C\in\;]0,-{\cal C}_{1}]
. They are of type 1 but have a negative flight time.

Proposition 3.4 defines two branches of terminal pairs, one with
C>0
, the other with
C<0
. When
C>0
is close to zero, the orbit is hyperbolic, the arc has type 1 and negative flight time. Let us increase
C
.
At
C=|{\cal C}_{1}|
, the orbit is parabolic. Then, the orbit is elliptic and all the types coexist. At
C={\cal C}_{0}
, the orbit is parabolic, the arc has type 0 and positive flight time. Finally, for
C>{\cal C}_{0}
, the orbit is hyperbolic of type 0.

3.6. The energy.
Combining
2H=v_{\rm A}^{2}+w_{\rm A}^{2}-2/r_{\rm A}
with (
5
) gives

2H=\frac{C^{2}c^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}+\frac{4(x_{\rm A}x_{\rm B}+y_{\rm A}y_{\rm B}-r_{\rm A}^{2})}{r_{\rm A}(s^{2}-c^{2})}-\frac{2}{r_{\rm A}}+\frac{4\Delta^{2}}{(s^{2}-c^{2})^{2}C^{2}}

or

2H=\frac{C^{2}c^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}-\frac{4s}{s^{2}-c^{2}}+\frac{4\Delta^{2}}{(s^{2}-c^{2})^{2}C^{2}}.

(6)

This fraction factorizes if we use as variables the same square roots
\xi=\sqrt{s+c}
,
\eta=\sqrt{s-c}
as in Euler’s formula (the square root is also related to the
\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C},z\mapsto z^{2}
regularizing map, where
\mathbb{C}={\rm O}xy
). We get

2H\xi^{2}\eta^{2}\Gamma^{2}=(\eta-\xi-\Gamma)(\eta-\xi+\Gamma)(\eta+\xi-\Gamma)(\eta+\xi+\Gamma),\quad\hbox{where }\Gamma=\frac{2\Delta}{\xi\eta C}.

(7)

The roots correspond to
C=\pm{\cal C}_{0}
and
C=\pm{\cal C}_{1}
, where
{\cal C}_{0}
and
{\cal C}_{1}
are given by formula (
2
).

The description in Proposition 3.4 has a defect: flat triangles
{\rm O}{\rm A}{\rm B}
are excluded. Such exclusion should happen due to the following monodromy obstruction. Suppose there is a coordinate determining, as
C
does for the hyperbolic orbits, a unique Keplerian arc for each triangle, but which would also be defined for a flat triangle. Then, fixing this coordinate and following this arc while
{\rm O}
is turning around
{\rm B}
,
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}
being fixed, the arc would be rectilinear of type 0 at the beginning, but rectilinear of type 1 after one turn. This is a contradiction.

A natural idea while seeing formula (
5
) is to use
C/\Delta
as a coordinate instead of
C
. This gives an extension to the rectilinear case, but not to the opposition case, where the denominator
s^{2}-c^{2}
is zero, and where the terminal velocities are not in the line
{\rm B}{\rm O}{\rm A}
. Formula (
7
) suggests to consider the coordinate
\Gamma
. Here the monodromy obstruction shows that
\sqrt{s-c}
changes sign when
{\rm O}
crosses the segment
{\rm A}{\rm B}
.

4. A new coordinate for the families of arcs

Consider instead of
C
or
C/\Delta
or
\Gamma
the quantity
b=\langle q_{\rm B},p_{\rm B}\rangle-\langle q_{\rm A},p_{\rm A}\rangle
, which in the planar case satisfies

b=x_{\rm B}v_{\rm B}+y_{\rm B}w_{\rm B}-x_{\rm A}v_{\rm A}-y_{\rm A}w_{\rm A}=\frac{c^{2}C}{\Delta}-\frac{2s\Delta}{(s^{2}-c^{2})C}.

(8)

The right-hand side, deduced from formula (
5
), shows that
b
is an increasing function of
C/\Delta
in both intervals
]-\infty,0[
and
]0,+\infty[
. In both intervals
b
varies from
-\infty
to
+\infty
. So there are two values of
C
for each value of
b
(see Figure 1). Let us see what happens in the cases where the triangle
{\rm O}{\rm A}{\rm B}
is flat.

4.1. Lemma (rectilinear case).
If
{\rm O}
,
{\rm B}
,
{\rm A}
are on the
{\rm O}x
axis,
0<x_{\rm B}<x_{\rm A}
, then the motion is rectilinear. The terminal pairs
(v_{\rm A},v_{\rm B})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
are only constrained by the energy equation
v_{\rm A}^{2}-2x_{\rm A}^{-1}=v_{\rm B}^{2}-2x_{\rm B}^{-1}
. They form the two branches of an equilateral hyperbola, one with
v_{\rm B}<0
, the other with
v_{\rm B}>0
. Each value of the coordinate
b\in\mathbb{R}
corresponds to one terminal pair on one branch and one terminal pair on the other branch.

Proof.
The line
b=x_{\rm B}v_{\rm B}-x_{\rm A}v_{\rm A}
in the plane
(v_{\rm A},v_{\rm B})
cuts the equilateral hyperbola in two points, one on each branch. QED

4.2. Lemma (opposition case).
If
{\rm B}
,
{\rm O}
,
{\rm A}
are on the
{\rm O}x
-axis,
x_{\rm B}<0<x_{\rm A}
, then all the terminal pairs satisfy
\|q_{\rm A}\wedge p_{\rm A}\|={\cal C}_{0}
where
2{\cal C}_{0}^{-2}=r_{\rm A}^{-1}+r_{\rm B}^{-1}
. The plane of motion is arbitrary. In the plane
{\rm O}xy
where
y_{\rm A}=y_{\rm B}=0
we have
v_{\rm A}=v_{\rm B}
and this unconstrained real number together with the choice
C=\pm{\cal C}_{0}
parametrizes all the terminal pairs. Each value of the coordinate

b=x_{\rm B}v_{\rm B}-x_{\rm A}v_{\rm A}=-(r_{\rm A}+r_{\rm B})v_{\rm A}

4.4. Families of arcs.

{\rm A}

Simplest case.
If

the motion is rectilinear. Instead of a pair of compatible terminal velocities there is a terminal velocity

Rectilinear case.

Opposition case.

4.7. Proposition (algebraic Lambert theorem).
Let

4.8. Proposition.
Fix a branch of terminal velocities and consider
H:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
b\mapsto H(b)
. Then
H^{\prime\prime}(b)>0
. The energy
H
tends to
+\infty
when
b\to-\infty
or
b\to+\infty
. It has a unique minimum, for which
H
is negative.

Proof.
According to Proposition 4.7, it is enough to conclude in the rectilinear case, where
0<x_{\rm B}<x_{\rm A}
,
H=v_{\rm A}^{2}/2+1/x_{\rm A}
and
b=x_{\rm B}v_{\rm B}-x_{\rm A}v_{\rm A}
. We have
{\rm d}H/{\rm d}v_{\rm A}=v_{\rm A}
and
{\rm d}b/{\rm d}v_{\rm A}=x_{\rm B}v_{\rm A}/v_{\rm B}-x_{\rm A}
since
v_{\rm A}^{2}/2+1/x_{\rm A}=v_{\rm B}^{2}/2+1/x_{\rm B}
. Then

H^{\prime}=\frac{v_{\rm A}v_{\rm B}}{x_{\rm B}v_{\rm A}-x_{\rm A}v_{\rm B}}=\Bigl(\frac{x_{\rm B}}{v_{\rm B}}-\frac{x_{\rm A}}{v_{\rm A}}\Bigr)^{-1},

H^{\prime\prime}=\frac{x_{\rm B}v_{\rm A}^{3}-x_{\rm A}v_{\rm B}^{3}}{(x_{\rm B}v_{\rm A}-x_{\rm A}v_{\rm B})^{3}}=\Bigl(\frac{x_{\rm B}}{v_{\rm B}}-\frac{x_{\rm A}}{v_{\rm A}}\Bigr)^{-3}\Bigl(\frac{x_{\rm B}}{v_{\rm B}^{3}}-\frac{x_{\rm A}}{v_{\rm A}^{3}}\Bigr)>0.

The inequality is deduced from
|v_{\rm B}|>|v_{\rm A}|
, which also implies that the denominator is nonzero. Now if
b\to\pm\infty
, then
v_{\rm A}\to\pm\infty
or
v_{\rm B}\to\pm\infty
, and consequently
H\to+\infty
. Finally, we know that
H
has two zeros, so the minimum value is negative. QED

5. The variational differential equation

We chose the coordinate
b
after the following variational considerations.
Let
q
be the position and
q\mapsto U
a force function. In the Kepler problem
U=1/r
. The equation of motion is
\ddot{q}=\nabla U
. We set
p=\dot{q}
. The energy
H=\|p\|^{2}/2-U
is conserved along the solutions.
We call
(t_{\rm A},q_{\rm A},p_{\rm A})
the time, the position and velocity vectors at the initial point
{\rm A}
,
(t_{\rm B},q_{\rm B},p_{\rm B})
the same quantities at the final point
{\rm B}
. There are several remarkable linear combinations with constant coefficients of the
Maupertuis action
and the
Levi-Civita time
, i.e.,

\int_{t_{\rm A}}^{t_{\rm B}}\|p\|^{2}{\rm d}t\quad\hbox{and}\quad\int_{t_{\rm A}}^{t_{\rm B}}U{\rm d}t.

In particular, the integral of the Lagrangian function or
Hamilton action
from
{\rm A}
to
{\rm B}
is

S=\int_{t_{\rm A}}^{t_{\rm B}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\|p\|^{2}+U\Bigr){\rm d}t.

Hamilton’s principle of stationary action gives the laws of dynamics through the variation of
S
on paths with fixed ends
(t_{\rm A},q_{\rm A})
and
(t_{\rm B},q_{\rm B})
. A classical formula in
Hamilton, 1834
gives the variation of
S
during a change of orbit, as a function of the changes of ends
(\delta t_{\rm A},\delta q_{\rm A})
and
(\delta t_{\rm B},\delta q_{\rm B})
:

\delta S=\langle\delta q_{\rm B},\dot{q}_{\rm B}\rangle-\langle\delta q_{\rm A},\dot{q}_{\rm A}\rangle-H(\delta t_{\rm B}-\delta t_{\rm A}).

A report on the early proofs may be found in
Albouy, 2019
. As well known, Hamilton’s ideas were suggested by and extend to other fundamental physical theories. Another combination is

\int_{t_{\rm A}}^{t_{\rm B}}({1\over 2}\|p\|^{2}-U){\rm d}t=H(t_{\rm B}-t_{\rm A}).

The function
J=\langle q,\dot{q}\rangle
satisfies
\dot{J}=\|\dot{q}\|^{2}+\langle q,\ddot{q}\rangle=\|p\|^{2}+\kappa U
if we assume that
U
is positively homogeneous of degree
\kappa
, which implies
\langle\nabla U,q\rangle=\kappa U
. This linear combination

\int_{t_{\rm A}}^{t_{\rm B}}(\|p\|^{2}+\kappa U){\rm d}t=J_{\rm B}-J_{\rm A}=b

is “exact” in the sense that it is a periodic function of
{\rm B}
when
{\rm B}
is moving along a periodic orbit. All these remarkable integrals satisfy remarkable identities. Notably, the Kepler equation

(-2H)^{3/2}t=u-e\sin u,

appears as such an identity. Here
e
is the eccentricity,
u
the eccentric anomaly. Indeed, differentiating with respect to
t
gives
(-2H)^{3/2}=(1-e\cos u)\dot{u}=-2Hr\dot{u}
. So
(-2H)^{-1/2}u=\int{\rm d}t/r
is the Levi-Civita time. The Kepler equation, when multiplied by
(-2H)^{-1/2}
, is simply

-2\int_{t_{\rm A}}^{t_{\rm B}}({1\over 2}\|p\|^{2}-U){\rm d}t=\int_{t_{\rm A}}^{t_{\rm B}}U{\rm d}t-\int_{t_{\rm A}}^{t_{\rm B}}(\|p\|^{2}-U){\rm d}t,

with
t_{\rm A}
fixed at the pericenter.
It is difficult to deduce Theorem 2.2 from the Kepler equation and the usual Keplerian recipe. In contrast, another identity between the above integrals, which includes
S
instead of the Levi-Civita time, easily gives the proof. We denote by
\tau=t_{\rm B}-t_{\rm A}
the signed flight time. Here is the combination.

5.1. Lemma.
If the force function
U
is positively homogeneous of degree
\kappa
, then
(2+\kappa)S=2b+(-2+\kappa)H\tau
. If
\kappa=-1
as in the case of the Newtonian force function then
S=2b-3H\tau
.

5.2. Lemma.
For any natural integer
k
, consider
b
as parametrizing the arcs of type
k
from
{\rm A}
to
{\rm B}
. The flight time
\tau
satisfies the “variational” differential equation

2H\tau^{\prime}+3\tau H^{\prime}=2,

(9)

where
H
and
\tau
are expressed as functions of
b
and where
′
marks the derivative with respect to
b
.

Proof.
The variation of
S
with fixed ends is
\delta S=-H\delta\tau
. The variation of the previous identity is
2H\delta\tau+3\tau\delta H=2\delta b
. It is enough to take
b
as the parameter for the variation. QED

5.3. Remark.
The period
P
of the Keplerian orbits passing through
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}
, expressed as a function of
b
, satisfies
2HP^{\prime}+3PH^{\prime}=0
. If
\tau
is the flight time on an arc of type
k
,
\tau+P
is the flight time on an arc of type
k+2
with the same terminal pair. Both flight times satisfy the same differential equation.
The differential equation suggests the following renumbering of the types of arcs. An arc of type
2l
should have index
l
. An arc of type
2l-1
should have index
-l
. The flight time
\tau_{0}
on an arc of type 0, or index 0, is a solution of the differential equation. Then the
signed
flight time on an arc of index
l
with the same terminal pair is
\tau_{0}+lP
and it satisfies the same differential equation.

6. Proof of Theorem 2.2 with the variational differential equation.

Theorem 2.2 is an easy corollary of this Proposition.

6.1. Proposition.
Consider the Keplerian arcs from
{\rm A}
to
{\rm B}
,
{\rm A}\neq{\rm B}
, of a given type as a one parameter family, the parameter being the quantity
b=\langle q_{\rm B},p_{\rm B}\rangle-\langle q_{\rm A},p_{\rm A}\rangle
. The positive flight time
\tau
on this arc, considered as a function of
b
, does not have a local maximum. If the type corresponds to simple arcs,
\tau(b)
does not have a critical point.

Proof.
The derivative with respect to
b
of the variational differential equation
2H\tau^{\prime}+3\tau H^{\prime}=2
is

2H\tau^{\prime\prime}+5H^{\prime}\tau^{\prime}+3H^{\prime\prime}\tau=0.

Proposition 4.8 concludes that
H^{\prime\prime}(b)>0
everywhere. At a critical point
b_{c}
we have
\tau^{\prime}(b_{c})=0
and consequently
H(b_{c})\tau^{\prime\prime}(b_{c})<0
. Every critical point in the interval where
H<0
is a local minimum. In the multirevolution case, we have
H<0
and the proof is finished.
In the case of a family of simple arcs, any critical point
b_{c}
in the interval with
H>0
should be a local maximum. But at a bound
b_{p}
of this interval
H(b_{p})=0
and consequently
H^{\prime}(b_{p})\tau^{\prime}(b_{p})<0
. According to Corollary 4.6 this interval is
]-\infty,b_{p}[
or
]b_{p},+\infty[
. In the first case,
H^{\prime}(b_{p})<0
and
\tau^{\prime}(b_{p})>0
. There should be a local minimum in the interval
]b_{c},b_{p}[
. This is a contradiction. In the second case,
H^{\prime}(b_{p})>0

and there is the same contradiction. To further conclude that there are no critical points in the case of simple arcs, we apply the same argument to the interval with
H<0
. Between a bound
b_{p}
of this interval and a local minimum
b_{c}
, there would be a local maximum, which is impossible. QED

6.2. Remark.
It may be considered as inelegant to specify
b
as the parameter and then state a conclusion which essentially does not depend on the parameter. However, we should consider that parameters are often proposed which are not monotone functions of
b
, such as the energy or the eccentricity.
Such a parameter could in principle produce, at an arc where the monotonicity is lost, a local maximum. About the angular momentum, it is monotone but constant if
{\rm O}
,
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}
are on a line. In brief, the specification of
b
makes the statement shorter.

7. Convexity of the flight time. Particular results.

7.1. Proposition (arcs finishing at collision).
Let
\eta(x_{I},v_{I})\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
be the flight time up to the Keplerian collision of a body dropped without angular momentum from
x_{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
with a velocity
v_{I}\in\mathbb{R}
. The successive derivatives of
\eta
with respect to
v_{I}
are all positive.

Proof.
We parametrize the free fall by the velocity
v
, which satisfies Newton’s law
\dot{v}=-x^{-2}
. We have

\eta(x_{I},v_{I})=\int_{-\infty}^{v_{I}}x^{2}{\rm d}v,\quad\hbox{with}\quad x=\frac{2}{v^{2}-2H},\quad H=\frac{v_{I}^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{x_{I}}.

(10)

We change the variable
v
to
u=v_{I}-v
. Then

\eta(x_{I},v_{I})=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hat{x}^{2}{\rm d}u,\quad\hbox{with}\quad\hat{x}=\frac{2}{(v_{I}-u)^{2}-2H}.

This relation gives
1/\hat{x}=-uv_{I}+u^{2}/2+1/x_{I}
, and if
u
is fixed,
{{\rm d}\hat{x}}/{{\rm d}v_{I}}=\hat{x}^{2}u
. Denoting by
\eta^{\prime}
the partial derivative with respect to
v_{I}
, we get

\eta^{\prime}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}2x^{3}u{\rm d}u>0,\qquad\eta^{\prime\prime}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}6x^{4}u^{2}{\rm d}u>0,\qquad\hbox{etc. QED}

7.2. Differential equation.
As a limit case of the variational differential equation (
9
), we have
2H\eta^{\prime}+3v_{I}\eta=-2x_{I}
. We can also obtain this equation by differentiation and integration by parts from (
10
). In the case
H=0
, the differential equation becomes the identity
3v_{I}\eta=-2x_{I}
.

Remark.
If the motion continues by bouncing a given number of times, still finishing at collision, the flight time is still a convex function of
v_{I}
, but is no longer increasing. It decreases from infinity, reaches a minimum and increases again to infinity, while
v_{I}
varies between the escape velocity and its opposite. At the minimum the differential equation gives again the relation
3v_{I}\eta=-2x_{I}
.

Proposition 7.1 will now be extended to arcs from
{\rm A}
to
{\rm B}
where
{\rm B}
is not assumed to coincide with
{\rm O}
. The result depends on which coordinate for the arcs replaces
v_{\rm A}
.

7.3. Proposition (arcs of type 0).
In the plane
{\rm O}xy
, the Keplerian arcs of type 0 around
{\rm O}
whose ends
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}
are distinct, symmetric with respect to the vertical axis
{\rm O}y
and placed at an ordinate
y_{\rm A}>0
, are parametrized by the inverse of the angular momentum
\upsilon=1/C
varying in the interval
]0,(r_{\rm A}-y_{\rm A})^{-1/2}[
. The flight time
\tau_{0}=t_{\rm B}-t_{\rm A}
is a function of
\upsilon
whose successive derivatives are all positive. We have:
\tau_{0}\to 0
when
\upsilon\to 0
and
\tau_{0}\to+\infty
when
\upsilon\to(r_{\rm A}-y_{\rm A})^{-1/2}
.

7.4. Corollary.
In the Euclidean plane or space, consider three points
{\rm O}
,
{\rm A}
,
{\rm B}
forming a nonflat triangle with
r_{\rm A}=r_{\rm B}
.
There is a unique Keplerian arc of type 0 around
{\rm O}
going from
{\rm A}
to
{\rm B}
in a given positive flight time. This arc is in the plane
{\rm O}{\rm A}{\rm B}
and is symmetric with respect to the perpendicular bisector of
{\rm A}{\rm B}
.

Figure 2. The family of orbits in the case
r_{\rm A}=r_{\rm B}
.

Proof.
It is easily proved that the arc is symmetric with respect to the
{\rm O}y
axis (see
Albouy and Ureña, 2020
). It thus belongs to a conic section with polar equation

r=\frac{C^{2}}{1-\beta\sin\theta},

with
C>0
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. The absolute value
|\beta|
is the eccentricity,
C
is the angular momentum,
C^{2}
is the semi-parameter. The conic section passes through
{\rm A}
, of polar coordinates
(r_{\rm A},\theta_{\rm A})
, with
\theta_{\rm A}\in\;]0,\pi/2[
, and through
{\rm B}
, of polar coordinates
(r_{\rm B},\theta_{\rm B})=(r_{\rm A},\pi-\theta_{\rm A})
. This is expressed by the single condition

r_{\rm A}=\frac{C^{2}}{1-\beta\sin\theta_{\rm A}},\quad\hbox{which is}\quad r_{\rm A}-C^{2}=\beta y_{\rm A}

with
\beta\in\;]-\infty,r_{\rm A}/y_{\rm A}[
or equivalently with
C^{2}\in\;]0,+\infty[
. The new form of the polar equation is

r=\frac{C^{2}}{1-y_{\rm A}^{-1}(r_{\rm A}-C^{2})\sin\theta}=\frac{y_{\rm A}}{\sin\theta-(r_{\rm A}\sin\theta-y_{\rm A})C^{-2}}.

At the ends of the interval, the arc is a limit of hyperbolas. When
\beta\to-\infty
, it is a segment going from
{\rm A}
to
{\rm B}
. When
\beta\to\beta_{0}
, it is a pair of segments, from
{\rm A}
to
{\rm O}
and then from
{\rm O}
to
{\rm B}
(see Figure 2). The arc of type 0 is the upper arc, which exists if and only if
\beta\in\;]-\infty,1[
. This is
C^{2}\in\;]r_{\rm A}-y_{\rm A},+\infty[
. We express the flight time along the symmetric arc of type 0 by using the expression
C=r^{2}\dot{\theta}
of the angular momentum:

\tau_{0}=\int_{t_{\rm A}}^{t_{\rm B}}{\rm d}t=\int_{\theta_{\rm A}}^{\theta_{\rm B}}\frac{r^{2}}{C}{\rm d}\theta=y_{\rm A}^{2}C^{-1}\int_{\theta_{\rm A}}^{\theta_{\rm B}}\bigl(\sin\theta-(r_{\rm A}\sin\theta-y_{\rm A})C^{-2}\bigr)^{-2}{\rm d}\theta.

It is natural to use the variable
\upsilon=C^{-1}\in\;]0,(r_{\rm A}-y_{\rm A})^{-1/2}[
.
We differentiate under the integration symbol. Note that the function

{r^{2}\over y_{\rm A}^{2}C}=\frac{\upsilon}{\bigl(\sin\theta-(r_{\rm A}\sin\theta-y_{\rm A})\upsilon^{2}\bigr)^{2}}=\frac{\upsilon}{(h-g\upsilon^{2})^{2}}

with
h=\sin\theta>0
and
g=r_{\rm A}\sin\theta-y_{\rm A}>0
is already the derivative in
\upsilon
of

{r\over 2y_{\rm A}g}=\frac{1}{2g(h-g\upsilon^{2})}.

The function
(h-g\upsilon^{2})^{-1}
with
h>0
and
g>0
has all its successive derivatives in
\upsilon
positive in the domain
\upsilon\in\;]0,\sqrt{h/g}[
. Consequently
{\rm d}^{n}(\tau_{0}(\upsilon))/{\rm d}\upsilon^{n}>0
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. QED

The arcs of type 1 in the rectilinear case.
We use the notation
\eta
for the flight time to collision as in Proposition 7.1 and furthermore set
\eta_{\rm A}(v)=\eta(x_{\rm A},v)
,
\eta_{\rm B}(v)=\eta(x_{\rm B},v)
. The type 1 flight time is

\tau_{1}=\eta_{\rm A}(v_{\rm A})+\eta_{\rm B}(-v_{\rm B})
since after bouncing we arrive at
{\rm B}
with a
v_{\rm B}>0
, while
\eta
was counted in Proposition 7.1 from
{\rm B}
to
{\rm O}
, starting with the opposite velocity.
We compute the first and second derivatives of
\tau_{1}

with respect to
b=x_{\rm B}v_{\rm B}-x_{\rm A}v_{\rm A}
considered as the coordinate on the branch
v_{\rm B}>0
of terminal pairs
(v_{\rm A},v_{\rm B})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfying
v_{\rm A}^{2}-2x_{\rm A}^{-1}=v_{\rm B}^{2}-2x_{\rm B}^{-1}
. The positions
x_{\rm A}
and
x_{\rm B}
are fixed and satisfy
0<x_{\rm B}<x_{\rm A}
. We have

\tau_{1}^{\prime}=\eta_{\rm A}^{\prime}(v_{\rm A})v_{\rm A}^{\prime}-\eta_{\rm B}^{\prime}(-v_{\rm B})v_{\rm B}^{\prime},\qquad\tau_{1}^{\prime\prime}=\eta_{\rm A}^{\prime\prime}(v_{\rm A})v_{\rm A}^{\prime 2}+\eta_{\rm B}^{\prime\prime}(-v_{\rm B})v_{\rm B}^{\prime 2}+R,

R=\eta_{\rm A}^{\prime}(v_{\rm A})v_{\rm A}^{\prime\prime}-\eta_{\rm B}^{\prime}(-v_{\rm B})v_{\rm B}^{\prime\prime}.

According to Proposition 7.1, the first two terms of
\tau_{1}^{\prime\prime}
are always positive. We will prove that
R>0
. As
0=b^{\prime\prime}=x_{\rm B}v_{\rm B}^{\prime\prime}-x_{\rm A}v_{\rm A}^{\prime\prime}
, there is a function
\lambda
such that
v_{\rm A}^{\prime\prime}=\lambda x_{\rm A}^{-1}
,
v_{\rm B}^{\prime\prime}=\lambda x_{\rm B}^{-1}
. Let us compute
\lambda
.
The energy relation is
v_{\rm A}v_{\rm A}^{\prime}=v_{\rm B}v_{\rm B}^{\prime}
.
We have
{\rm d}(x_{\rm B}v_{\rm B}-x_{\rm A}v_{\rm A})/{\rm d}v_{\rm A}=(x_{\rm B}v_{\rm A}-x_{\rm A}v_{\rm B})/v_{\rm B}
and

v_{\rm A}^{\prime}=\frac{v_{\rm B}}{x_{\rm B}v_{\rm A}-x_{\rm A}v_{\rm B}},\qquad v_{\rm A}^{\prime\prime}=v_{\rm A}^{\prime}\frac{x_{\rm B}(v_{\rm A}^{2}-v_{\rm B}^{2})}{v_{\rm B}(x_{\rm B}v_{\rm A}-x_{\rm A}v_{\rm B})^{2}}=\frac{x_{\rm B}(v_{\rm A}^{2}-v_{\rm B}^{2})}{(x_{\rm B}v_{\rm A}-x_{\rm A}v_{\rm B})^{3}}.

\lambda=\frac{x_{\rm A}x_{\rm B}(v_{\rm A}^{2}-v_{\rm B}^{2})}{(x_{\rm B}v_{\rm A}-x_{\rm A}v_{\rm B})^{3}}=\frac{x_{\rm B}-x_{\rm A}}{(x_{\rm B}v_{\rm A}-x_{\rm A}v_{\rm B})^{3}},\qquad R=\lambda\Bigl(\frac{\eta_{\rm A}^{\prime}(v_{\rm A})}{x_{\rm A}}-\frac{\eta_{\rm B}^{\prime}(-v_{\rm B})}{x_{\rm B}}\Bigr).

We see that
\lambda
has the sign of
v_{\rm B}
, since
-x_{\rm A}v_{\rm B}
dominates in the denominator and the numerator is negative. So
\lambda
is positive and we are left to prove that the parenthesis is positive.

7.5. Lemma.
Let a particle be ejected from the Keplerian collision with a center
{\rm O}
, at time
t=0
. Call
v\in\mathbb{R}
its velocity,
x>0
its position,
H=v^{2}/2-1/x
its energy. Then
tv/x\to 2/3
as
t\to 0
. On all the motion in positive time,
tv/x
is decreasing up to
-\infty
if
H<0
, constant if
H=0
, increasing up to
+\infty
if
H>0
.

Proof.
In the case
H=0
,
x=gt^{2/3}
,
v=2gt^{-1/3}/3
with
g=\root 3 \of{9/2}
. This gives
tv/x=2/3
, and also gives the limiting value for
H\neq 0
. If
H>0
, the Keplerian recipe is
a=-(2H)^{-1}
,
x=a(1-\cosh u)
,
-|a|^{-3/2}t=u-\sinh u
,
{\rm d}u/{\rm d}t=(\cosh u-1)^{-1}|a|^{-3/2}
,
v=|a|^{-1/2}\sinh u(\cosh u-1)^{-1}
. Thus

\frac{tv}{x}=\frac{\sinh^{2}u}{(\cosh u-1)^{2}}-u\frac{\sinh u}{(\cosh u-1)^{2}},

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}u}\frac{tv}{x}=-\frac{3\sinh u}{(\cosh u-1)^{2}}+u\frac{2+\cosh u}{(\cosh u-1)^{2}}>0\quad\hbox{for all }u>0

since the Taylor expansion of the numerator
u^{5}(1/24-1/40)+u^{7}(1/720-1/1680)+\cdots
has no negative terms. If
H<0
,
x=a(1-\cos u)
,
a^{-3/2}t=u-\sin u
,
{\rm d}u/{\rm d}t=(1-\cos u)^{-1}a^{-3/2}
,
v=a^{-1/2}\sin u(1-\cos u)^{-1}
thus

\frac{tv}{x}=-\frac{\sin^{2}u}{(1-\cos u)^{2}}+u\frac{\sin u}{(1-\cos u)^{2}},

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}u}\frac{tv}{x}=\frac{3\sin u}{(1-\cos u)^{2}}-u\frac{2+\cos u}{(1-\cos u)^{2}}<0\quad\hbox{for all }u\in\;]0,2\pi[

since
3\sin u/(2+\cos u)-u
is zero
at
u=0
and its derivative
-(1-\cos u)^{2}/(2+\cos u)^{2}
is negative. QED

7.6. Lemma.
Call
\eta^{\prime}(x,v)
the partial derivative with respect to
v
, with fixed
x
, of the flight time
\eta(x,v)
needed to reach the collision by starting from
x
with velocity
v
. Let us choose an arbitrary energy
H
. Let
x(v)=(v^{2}/2-H)^{-1}
be the value of
x
corresponding to this choice. Then
\eta^{\prime}(x(v),v)/x(v)
is increasing with
v
.

Proof.
According to Proposition 7.1,
\eta^{\prime}>0
. According to the differential equation
2H\eta^{\prime}+3v\eta=-2x
,
\eta^{\prime}/x=(2H)^{-1}(-2-3v\eta/x)
. If
H\neq 0
, the question is reduced to the monotonicity of the function
v\eta(x(v),v)/x(v)
. But
\eta(x,v)
is the function
t(x,-v)
of Lemma 7.5, where the velocity
-v
is decreasing all along the orbit, and where
H
is constant all along the orbit. So
v\eta/x
is increasing with
v
if
H<0
, decreasing when
v
is increasing if
H>0
.
Consequently
\eta^{\prime}/x
is increasing with
v
in both cases. If
H=0
, the differential equation gives
v\eta/x=-2/3
, which we already know from Proposition 7.1. Differentiating it, we get
2H\eta^{\prime\prime}+5v\eta^{\prime}+3\eta=0
, which gives
5\eta^{\prime}/x=-3\eta/(xv)=2/v^{2}
if
H=0
, which is also increasing with
v
since
v<0
.
QED

7.7. Proposition (arcs of type 1).
In the rectilinear case,
0<x_{\rm B}<x_{\rm A}
, the second derivative
\tau_{1}^{\prime\prime}
with respect to
b
of the flight time
\tau_{1}>0
is positive. The first derivative
\tau_{1}^{\prime}
is negative.

Proof.
Since
v_{\rm B}>0
and
v_{\rm B}^{2}-v_{\rm A}^{2}>0
, we have
v_{\rm A}>-v_{\rm B}
and according to Lemma 7.6, the above quantity
R
is positive. This proves
\tau_{1}^{\prime\prime}>0
. Now as
v_{\rm A}\to-\infty
,
\tau_{1}\to 0
,
v_{\rm B}\to+\infty
and
b\to+\infty
. As we proved that
\tau_{1}^{\prime}
decreases,
\tau_{1}^{\prime}<0
. QED

8. Convexity of
\tau
. General results and second proof of Theorem 2.2.

8.1. Lambert’s theorem and L-congruence.
According to Lambert’s theorem (see
Lambert, 1761
;
Gooding, 1990
;
Albouy, 2019
), by moving
{\rm O}
on an ellipse with foci
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}
, we change continuously all the Keplerian arcs of type
k
going from
{\rm A}
to
{\rm B}
in a given flight time
\tau_{k}
, without changing either their number or the integer
k
or the energy of each arc.
Gooding, 1990
calls L-congruents the arcs obtained from each other by following such a continuous change (see Figure 3).

Figure 3. Four Keplerian arcs of type 0 with same
c
,
r_{\rm A}+r_{\rm B}
and
H
.

The L-congruence furthermore leaves invariant the quotient
C/\Delta
, as shown by formula (
6
). Formula (
8
) shows that
b=\langle q_{\rm B},p_{\rm B}\rangle-\langle q_{\rm A},p_{\rm A}\rangle
is also invariant. We gave in 4.7 an “algebraic Lambert theorem” which indeed states the L-invariance of
b
. The variational differential equation is L-invariant, which indicates an alternative proof of the L-invariance of
\tau
, which is Lambert’s theorem.

The L-congruence reduces Lambert’s problem to the case with
r_{\rm A}=r_{\rm B}
. As well, it reduces it to the case where
{\rm O}
,
{\rm B}
,
{\rm A}
are on a line in this order. So, Proposition 7.3 extends to the nonsymmetric cases. The opposition case should nevertheless be excluded. In the same way, Proposition 7.7 extends to the nonrectilinear cases, including the opposition cases which are L-congruent to the case of Proposition 7.1.

8.2. Convex composition of functions.
If the (smooth) real function
\varphi
satisfies
\varphi^{\prime}>0
and
\varphi^{\prime\prime}>0
, if the real function
f
satisfies
f^{\prime}>0
and
f^{\prime\prime}\geq 0
, then the composed function
g:y\mapsto f(\varphi(y))
satisfies
g^{\prime}>0
and
g^{\prime\prime}>0
. This is proved by the formulas
g^{\prime}=\varphi^{\prime}f^{\prime}
and
g^{\prime\prime}=\varphi^{\prime\prime}f^{\prime}+(\varphi^{\prime})^{2}f^{\prime\prime}
. The same formulas prove the convexity of
g
in other cases.

Here, we restrict the discussion to the coordinates
\Delta/C
and
b
, which are related by (
8
). For other coordinates and references see
Albouy and Ureña, 2020
.

Proposition 7.3 proves that the flight time
\tau_{0}>0
satisfies
\tau_{0}^{\prime}>0
and
\tau_{0}^{\prime\prime}>0
. Here
′
is the derivative with respect to the coordinate
\Delta/C
. Here
C>0
and
\Delta>0
since
x_{\rm A}=-x_{\rm B}>0
and
y_{\rm A}=y_{\rm B}>0
. Let us pass to the coordinate
b
.

The change of variable
\Delta/C\mapsto b
given by (
8
) satisfies
b^{\prime}<0
. If
C/\Delta<0
,
b^{\prime\prime}<0
. If
C/\Delta>0
,
b^{\prime\prime}>0
. We are in this last case. We need the reciprocal change:
\varphi:b\mapsto\Delta/C
. We have
\varphi^{\prime}<0
,
\varphi^{\prime\prime}>0
.
Consequently the function
b\mapsto\tau_{0}
has a negative first derivative and a positive second derivative.

Proposition 7.7 proves that the flight time
\tau_{1}>0
satisfies
\tau_{1}^{\prime}<0
and
\tau_{1}^{\prime\prime}>0
. Here
′
is the derivative with respect to the coordinate
b
. Here
v_{\rm B}>0
which is consistent with
\Delta/C<0
. Let us pass to the coordinate
\Delta/C
.
Here
\varphi(\Delta/C)=b
,
\varphi^{\prime}<0
,
\varphi^{\prime\prime}<0
. Then
C/\Delta\mapsto\tau_{1}
has a positive first derivative and a positive second derivative.

8.3. Theorem.
Consider a nonflat triangle
{\rm O}{\rm A}{\rm B}
. Consider the Keplerian arcs from
{\rm A}
to
{\rm B}
of any given type as a one parameter family, the parameter being
1/C
, inverse of the angular momentum. The positive flight time
\tau
on this arc, considered as a function of
1/C
, has a positive second derivative.

Proof.
About
\tau_{0}
, we extend Proposition 7.3 to general arcs by L-congruence. About
\tau_{1}
, we extend Proposition 7.7 by L-congruence and pass to the variable
1/C
by convex composition. So both
\tau_{0}
and
\tau_{1}
have a positive second derivative in
1/C
. We obtain a positive second derivative of the Keplerian period by adding
\tau_{0}(-1/C)
and
\tau_{1}(1/C)
. By similar additions, we include the multirevolution flight times. QED

This Theorem extends to the rectilinear case by changing the coordinate
1/C
into
v_{\rm A}-v_{\rm B}
, as shown by computing
v_{\rm A}-v_{\rm B}
with formula (
5
). Note that
(v_{\rm A}+v_{\rm B})(v_{\rm A}-v_{\rm B})=2/x_{\rm A}-2/x_{\rm B}
since the energy in
{\rm A}
is the energy in
{\rm B}
. The quantities
v_{\rm A}
,
H
,
b
are rational functions of
v_{\rm A}-v_{\rm B}
. But Theorem 8.3 cannot be extended to the opposition case. This is proved by the monodromy obstruction explained at the end of §3. In the next Theorem, all the cases are included.

8.4. Theorem.
Consider the Keplerian arcs from
{\rm A}
to
{\rm B}
,
{\rm A}\neq{\rm B}
, of any given type as a one parameter family, the parameter being the quantity
b=\langle q_{\rm B},p_{\rm B}\rangle-\langle q_{\rm A},p_{\rm A}\rangle
. The positive flight time
\tau
on this arc, considered as a function of
b
, has a positive second derivative.

Proof.
About
\tau_{0}
, we extend Proposition 7.3 to general arcs by L-congruence. The rectilinear case is included by changing
\Delta/C
into
v_{\rm A}-v_{\rm B}
. We then pass to the variable
b
by convex composition. The opposition case is included by extending Proposition 7.1 by L-congruence. About
\tau_{1}
, we extend Proposition 7.7 by L-congruence. We conclude as in the previous Theorem.
QED

Theorem 8.4 gives the convexity of
\tau
, while Proposition 6.1 only proves that
\tau
does not have a local maximum. Our main Theorem 2.2 was deduced from Proposition 6.1. It is
a fortiori
deduced from Theorem 8.4.
