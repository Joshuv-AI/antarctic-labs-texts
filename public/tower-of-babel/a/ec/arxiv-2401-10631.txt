Enumerating Finite Common Meadows
João Dias, Bruno Dinis

Abstract
Common meadows are commutative and associative algebraic structures with two operations (addition and multiplication) with additive and multiplicative identities and for which inverses are total. The inverse of zero is an error term 𝐚 \mathbf{a} which is absorbent for addition. We study the problem of enumerating all finite common meadows of order n n (that is, common meadows with n n elements). This problem turns out to be deeply connected with both the number of finite rings of order n n and with the number of a certain kind of partition of positive integers.



1
Introduction

Meadows are algebraic structures with two operations (addition and multiplication), introduced by Bergstra and Tucker in
[
1
]
. Perhaps the most interesting feature of these algebraic structures, which was one of the main motivations to introduce and study them, is that they allow to divide by zero, i.e. both addition and multiplication are operations for which the inverses are total. In particular, common meadows (introduced by Bergstra and Ponse in
[
4
]
), which can be decomposed as disjoint unions of rings
[
14
]
, enable to invert zero by introducing a term
\mathbf{a}
, such that
0^{-1}=\mathbf{a}
.

For the most part, meadows have been studied as abstract data types given by equational axiomatizations
[
3
,
5
,
6
,
2
]
. These allow to obtain simple term rewriting systems which are easier to automate in formal reasoning
[
2
,
7
]
. However, more recently, connections with nonstandard analysis
[
12
]
and the study of common meadows from a purely algebraic point of view
[
14
]
, opened new lines of research in the field.

The present paper also views common meadows from an algebraic point of view but it focuses on finite such structures. We study the problem of enumerating all finite common meadows of
order

n
(that is, common meadows with
n
elements). This problem is, of course, related with the similar problem of enumerating other finite algebraic structures, such as semigroups
[
18
,
17
,
15
,
16
]
, groups
[
11
]
and rings
[
10
]
.

For finite involutive meadows, i.e. such that
0^{-1}=0
, it is possible to obtain a unique representation of minimal finite meadows in terms of finite prime fields
[
8
]
, which solves the problem of enumerating finite involutive meadows.
For common meadows the problem becomes more interesting because no such result exists. Moreover, the number of meadows of both even and odd order grows exponentially, but at different rates (see Figures
4
and
5
). Of course, since meadows need to have at least 3 distinct elements, it only makes sense to start enumerating for orders
n\geq 3
. As it turns out, the problem of enumerating finite common meadows is deeply connected with both the number of finite rings of order
n\geq 3
and with partitions of positive integers of a certain kind (which we shall call
admissible partitions
).

After recalling some preliminary notions and results in Section
2
we start, in Section
3
, by showing that there are common meadows of any given order
n\geq 3
. We also give a lower bound on the number of meadows of a given order. Our main result states that a partition of a given positive integer is admissible if and only if it is possible to construct a common meadow associated with that partition, i.e. each number in the partition is the order of one of the rings that constitute the meadow. This result is enough to establish that, for the orders
3,4,6
and
8
, there is exactly one meadow. The result also allows to explicitly construct all the meadows of a given order by reducing the problem to the problem of finding the admissible partitions and the rings of that order. In Section
4
we illustrate this for orders
5
and
7
. These examples are somewhat typical. So much so that we present an algorithm in GAP 4, developed by the first author, which allows to obtain all the common meadows of a given order, provided that it is known both the number of finite rings and the number of lattices of lower orders. In Section
5
we describe the algorithm and present some of its output. Some final remarks are left for Section
6
.

We recall the notion of common meadow, introduced in

Definition 2.1
.

A
common meadow
is a structure
(M,+,-,\cdot)
satisfying the following equations

1.

(x+y)+z=x+(y+z)

2.

x+y=y+x

3.

x+0=x

4.

x+(-x)=0\cdot x

5.

(x\cdot y)\cdot z=x\cdot(y\cdot z)

6.

x\cdot y=y\cdot x

7.

1\cdot x=x

8.

x\cdot(y+z)=x\cdot y+x\cdot z

9.

-(-x)=x

10.

x\cdot x^{-1}=1+0\cdot x^{-1}

11.

(x\cdot y)^{-1}=x^{-1}\cdot y^{-1}

12.

(1+0\cdot x)^{-1}=1+0\cdot x

13.

0^{-1}=\mathbf{a}

14.

x+\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{a}

Definition 2.2
.

A
directed lattice
of rings
\Gamma
over a countable lattice
L
consists on a family of commutative rings
\Gamma_{i}
indexed by
i\in L
, such that
\Gamma_{i}
is a unital commutative ring for all
i\in L\setminus\min(L)

f_{j,i}:\Gamma_{i}\rightarrow\Gamma_{j}
whenever

f_{j,k}\circ f_{i,j}=f_{i,k}
for all
i>j>k

Theorem 2.3
.

Given a directed lattice of rings
\Gamma
over the lattice
L
there is an associated meadow
M=\bigsqcup_{i\in L}\Gamma_{i}
. Also, given a meadow

there is a directed lattice of rings over the lattice

5
An algorithm to enumerate meadows

The first author developed a code in GAP 4
[
19
]
that allows to count the number of meadows of a given order and construct them. The code is available at
[
13
]
.
With this code, one is able to obtain all finite meadows from order
3
up to order
16
. Hence the code gives a computational solution to the problem that this paper addresses, for these orders. The fact that we are restricted to these orders has to do with two limitations of the GAP 4 database. The first limitation is that GAP 4 only has stored the rings of order
15
and lower, so the fact that the code presented relies on this list prevents us from constructing, for example, all the meadows of order
17
that are related with the admissible partition
16+1
. A second limitation has to do with the fact that GAP 4 has stored all the finite semigroups, but only up to order
8

[
17
]
. In particular, the idempotent commutative semigroups which are equivalent to lattices, are stored but again only up to order
8
. This means that, in this way, one is not able to construct meadows associated with admissible partitions of the form
a_{0}+\cdots+a_{s}+1
with
s+2>8
. We would like to point out, however, that these are not limitations of the code but limitations on the database of GAP 4 which can be solved by adding to the database of the software the list of rings of order greater than
15
and lattices of order greater than
8
.

6
Final remarks

The lower bounds given by Propositions
3.4
and
3.5
, and illustrated in Figure
6
, show that for both odd and even order, the number of meadows have an exponential growth albeit at different rates. However, our bounds are very far from being optimal, as can be seen in Figure
4
. There are indeed some obvious ways to improve them. For example, the bound for meadows of odd order was established by observing that, one has the obvious partition

2n+1=\underbrace{2+\cdots+2}_{n\text{ times}}+1.

Now, of course one can always group some
2
’s into a
4
, say the first two, thus obtaining new admissible partitions which give rise to meadows. A similar but more involved technique also allows to refine the lower bounds for meadows of even order. However, so far we do not know if it is possible to obtain
optimal
lower bounds. The same question can be posed for upper bounds.

The ”saw” shape of the graph given in Figure
5
suggests that the number of meadows of order
2k+1
is always greater than the number of meadows of order
2k+2
, for
k>2
. For the moment, a proof of such result escapes us.
