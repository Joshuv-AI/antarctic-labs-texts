PMGDA: A Preference-based Multiple Gradient Descent Algorithm
Xiaoyuan Zhang, Xi Lin, Qingfu Zhang

Abstract
It is desirable in many multi-objective machine learning applications, such as multi-task learning with conflicting objectives and multi-objective reinforcement learning, to find a Pareto solution that can match a given preference of a decision maker. These problems are often large-scale with available gradient information but cannot be handled very well by the existing algorithms. To tackle this critical issue, this paper proposes a novel predict-and-correct framework for locating a Pareto solution that fits the preference of a decision maker. In the proposed framework, a constraint function is introduced in the search progress to align the solution with a user-specific preference, which can be optimized simultaneously with multiple objective functions. Experimental results show that our proposed method can efficiently find a particular Pareto solution under the demand of a decision maker for standard multiobjective benchmark, multi-task learning, and multi-objective reinforcement learning problems with more than thousands of decision variables. II-B

Basic MOO Definitions

A MOO problem can be expressed as the following formulation:

\min_{{\bm{\theta}}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}})=(L_{1}({\bm{\theta}}),L_{2}({\bm{\theta}}),\ldots,L_{m}({\bm{\theta}})),

(1)

where
{\bm{\theta}}
is a
n

is its decision space. Very often, no single solution can optimize all the objectives at the same time. Instead, there exists a set of solutions, called Pareto solutions, which can represent different optimal trade-offs among the objectives. The following MOO definitions are used in this paper. We have the following definition:

Definition 1

(Dominance
[
23
]
(Chap. 2.2))

Given two candidate solutions

{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(a)})\preceq_{\text{strict}}{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(b)})

Definition 2

(Pareto Optimality

]
(Chap. 2.2))

A solution is Pareto optimal if no other solution belongs to
\Theta

\mathcal{T}=f\circ\mathtt{PS}

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}\nabla L_{i}+\nu\nabla h=0,\quad\text{with}\quad\mu_{i}\geq 0.

(2)

(a)

Exact Pareto solution.

(b)

Pareto solutions with a region of interest (ROI).

A solution is Pareto stationary means that, this solution can not be locally improved, which is a necessary condition for Pareto solution. Since a Pareto set could contain more than one single solution, and the possible infinite Pareto solutions are not comparable (e.g., non-dominated) with each other, therefore, it is necessary to make a further decision among the Pareto solutions

(3)

II-C

The Multiple Gradient Descent Algorithm (MGDA)

In this section, we briefly review MGDA
[
19
]
, which serves as a fundamental tool of our method. MGDA extends the single objective steepest gradient descent algorithm for solving MOO problems. It updates the parameters as follows:
{\bm{\theta}}_{k+1}={\bm{\theta}}_{k}+\eta{\bm{d}}
, where
\eta
is the learning rate and
{\bm{d}}
is the MGDA search direction. The key idea of MGDA is to find a valid gradient direction to decrease all objectives
L_{i}({\bm{\theta}})
at the same time.
One way for computing
{\bm{d}}
is as follows
[
20
]
:

\displaystyle({\bm{d}},\alpha^{*})=

\displaystyle\argmin_{({\bm{v}},\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}}\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|^{2},

(4)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\nabla{L_{i}}^{\top}{\bm{v}}\leq\alpha,\quad i\in[m].

We would like to mention a less explored way
[
19
]
that also simultaneously reduces all objectives, which serves as a foundation of the proposed PMGDA method. In this way, to find a valid direction
{\bm{d}}
, we solve the following problem:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle({\bm{d}},\alpha^{*})=\argmin_{({\bm{v}},\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}}\alpha,

(5)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\;\left\{\begin{aligned} &\nabla{L_{i}}^{\top}{\bm{v}}\leq\alpha,\quad i\in[m],\\
&\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 1.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

If the optimal
\alpha^{*}<0
, then
{\bm{d}}
is a direction that can reduce all the objectives, and each objective
L_{i}
can be decreased by about
\eta\alpha^{*}
. When
\alpha^{*}=0
, no direction can optimize all the objectives simultaneously, and thus
{\bm{\theta}}_{k}
is Pareto stationary
[
19
]
.

III-A

Preference Functions

III-A
1
The exact preference function

There are several functions available to align a Pareto objective vector
{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}})
with a preference vector
{\bm{\lambda}}
. One commonly used function is the Euclidean distance from a Pareto objective to this preference vector
[
26
]
.

h({\bm{\theta}})=\left\|{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}})-\frac{{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}})\cdot{\bm{\lambda}}}{\left\|{\bm{\lambda}}\right\|}{\bm{\lambda}}\right\|

(6)

This function indicates a solution is exact if and only if
h({\bm{\theta}})
is equal to zero.

III-A
2
The Region of Interest (ROI) preference function

ROI is a specific region defined by the decision maker. In addition to the regular ROI discussed in
Section

II-B
, we also consider the ROI as a spherical shape. The preference function for the ROI constraint is defined as follows:

h({\bm{\theta}})=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}})\in\texttt{ROI}\\
\text{dist}({\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}),\texttt{ROI}),&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(7)

Here,
\text{dist}({\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}),\texttt{ROI})
represents the Euclidean distance to the
ROI
set.
h({\bm{\theta}})=0
means that this solution satisfies the ROI constraint.

III-B

The Predict-and-Correct Framework

The objective of the preference-based MOO (MOO) problem is to identify a Pareto solution that fulfills the preference constraint
h({\bm{\theta}})=0
. Drawing inspiration from Gebken et al.’s work
[
27
]
, we propose a predict-and-correct algorithmic framework. In this framework, let
{\bm{\theta}}_{k}
represent the solution at iteration
k
, with the subsequent solution
{\bm{\theta}}_{k+1}
determined as follows:

•

When
h({\bm{\theta}}_{k})=0
, we do the prediction step, which reduces all objective values
L_{i}
without hurting the preference constraint
h({\bm{\theta}}_{k})
:

\begin{split}({\bm{d}},\alpha^{*})=&\argmin_{({\bm{v}},\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}}\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|^{2},\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&\;\begin{cases}\nabla{L_{i}}^{\top}{\bm{v}}\leq\alpha,\quad i\in[m],\\
\nabla h^{\top}{\bm{v}}=0.\end{cases}\end{split}

(8)

•

Otherwise, we do the correction step. The major requirement is to reduce
h({\bm{\theta}}_{k})
while the objective values
L_{i}
should not be deteriorated too much. Specifically, we compute the direction
\bm{d}
as follows:

\begin{split}&({\bm{d}},\alpha^{*})=\argmin_{({\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\alpha\in\mathbb{R})}\alpha\\
&\text{s.t.}\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} &\nabla L_{i}^{\top}{\bm{v}}\leq\alpha,\quad i\in[m]\\
&\nabla h^{\top}{\bm{v}}\leq-\sigma\left\|\nabla h\right\|\cdot\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|,\\
&0<\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|\leq 1.\\
\end{aligned}\right.\end{split}

(9)

where
0<\sigma<1
is a preset control parameter. To prevent
{\bm{v}}
from becoming a zero vector, we need to constrain
\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|>0
.

•

Finally, update the decision variable

{\bm{\theta}}_{k+1}={\bm{\theta}}_{k}+\eta{\bm{d}}.

(10)

We would like to offer some remarks regarding the conceptual algorithm we just introduced. During the prediction phase, there are two critical constraints to consider. The first one ensures that each objective, denoted as
L_{i}
, can be reduced by a minimum of
\alpha
. The second constraint is designed to guide the search within the tangent space, ensuring that the resulting
h({\bm{\theta}}_{k})
value remains minimal. It’s important to note that the Problem (
8
) is essentially a variation of Problem (
4
) and (
5
) with an additional quality constraint
\nabla h^{\top}{\bm{v}}=0
. Therefore, the techniques used for analyzing and solving these equations can also be effectively applied to Problem (
8
).

In the correction phase, the first constraint outlined in Problem (
9
) aligns with the well-established Armijo rule (Armijo, 1966)
[
28
]
. This constraint is designed to ensure a sufficient reduction in the constraint function
h({\bm{\theta}})
. Such a reduction is pivotal for steering
h({\bm{\theta}})
towards a stationary solution, a concept validated by Bertsekas in 2016
[
29
]
. The second constraint regulates the increment of each
L_{i}
during the search process. The third constraint is critical in establishing the search boundary, ensuring that the vector
{\bm{v}}
remains non-zero. When the correction step is exactly a retraction map as defined in
[
30
]
onto the manifold
h({\bm{\theta}})=0
at
{\bm{\theta}}_{k}
, our method is a special case of the constrained multiple gradient method introduced in
[
27
]
and converges to a Pareto stationary solution satisfying the exact constraint.

Both Problem (
8
) and (
9
) have
n+1
decision variables, where
n
represents the number of parameters in a neural network. Given that neural networks typically have a large number of parameters,
n
is consequently large. Additionally, Problem (
9
) involves nonlinear constraints. To tackle these challenges, in the following sections, we will develop algorithms specifically designed to efficiently manage the large parameter count,
n
, in both the prediction and projection steps of the solution process.

Our proposed algorithm differs from Gebken et al.
[
27
]
, which is primarily conceptual and not directly suited for large-scale neural networks. Their assumption of an available projection operator (
{\bm{\theta}}\leftarrow\text{proj}({\bm{\theta}}-\eta{\bm{d}})
) is unrealistic in the context of neural networks. We have significantly modified this correction step in Problem (
9
) to enhance learning and investigated the prediction algorithm in its dual form. Consequently, our correction and prediction steps involve only
O(m)
decision variables, compared to
O(n)
in Gebken et al.
[
27
]
’s original work, where
m
is the number of objectives and
n
is the number of decision variables. Given that
n\gg m
, our approach is the first practical method for finding preference-based Pareto solutions in neural networks.

To solve problem (
Equation

(11)

The resulting optimal direction

, where
\mu_{i}^{*}
and

are solutions to problem of Equation (

III-D

An Efficient Correction Algorithm

The original correction problem (Equation (
9
)) has a large number of decision variables
(n)
.
n
is particularly large for a neural network problem. Additionally, the last two constraints are nonlinear, which makes the problem challenging to optimize.
To make the problem tractable, we assume the search direction
{\bm{d}}
is a linear combination of gradients of the objective functions and the constraint function

. Thus we can add the following condition to Problem (

{\bm{d}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}\widehat{\nabla L_{i}}-\mu_{m+1}\widehat{\nabla h}.

(12)

Here, the notation

\hat{{\bm{x}}}=\frac{{\bm{x}}}{\|{\bm{x}}\|}

Without loss of generality, we can assume that

Lemma 1
.

When

is not Pareto stationary,
0<\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|\leq 1
.

Proof.

We first prove that

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}\mu_{i}v_{i}\right\|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}\left\|\mu_{i}v_{i}\right\|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}|\mu_{i}|=1,

when
v_{i}
is a unit vector and
\mu\in\Delta_{m+1}
.
We then show that when
{\bm{\theta}}_{k}
is not Pareto stationary, there does not exist constants
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m+1}
such that
{\bm{v}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}\widehat{\nabla L_{i}}+\mu_{m+1}\widehat{\nabla h}=\bm{0}
, i.e.,
\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|>0
.

\diamondsuit

Lemma
1
implies that the last constraint in Problem (

\displaystyle\nabla h^{\top}{\bm{v}}\leq-\sigma\left\|\nabla h\right\|\cdot\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|.

Again, by Lemma

\nabla h^{\top}{\bm{v}}\leq-\sigma\left\|\nabla h\right\|.

III-E

Reuse of Gradients

For simple problems, obtaining the gradient of
\nabla h
directly is cheap. However, for large-scale problems, such as neural networks, computing gradients becomes expensive. Note that
h({\bm{\theta}})
is a function that only involves objectives
L_{i}
’s. Once all the gradients
\nabla L_{i}
’s are obtained, the gradient of the constraint function
\nabla h
can be estimated using the chain rule:

\nabla h=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial L_{i}}\cdot\nabla L_{i}\right),

(17)

where the first term
\frac{\partial h}{\partial L_{i}}
are just scalars.

For the specific case when
m=2
(two-objective problem) and
h(\cdot)
is defined by Equation (
6
), the partial derivatives
\frac{\partial h}{\partial L_{i}}
can be calculated using the following closed-form:

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial h}{\partial L_{1}}=\left(-\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\|{\bm{\lambda}}\|},\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\|{\bm{\lambda}}\|}\right),&\text{if }\frac{L_{1}({\bm{\theta}})}{\lambda_{1}}<\frac{L_{2}({\bm{\theta}})}{\lambda_{2}},\\
\frac{\partial h}{\partial L_{2}}=\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\|{\bm{\lambda}}\|},-\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\|{\bm{\lambda}}\|}\right),&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(18)

where
{\bm{\lambda}}
is the
m

For other preference functions
h({\bm{\theta}})
which may not have an analytical gradient expression, it is possible to perform a lightweight backward propagation to obtain the values of
\frac{\partial h}{\partial L_{i}}
.

In this section, we demonstrate the adaptability of our proposed framework to MORL tasks, which are very practical real-world problems. In MORL, each state-action pair

(20)

where
\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{\lambda}})

{\bm{\theta}}

can be estimated through interaction with the environment. We illustrate this using the most direct policy gradient method
[
33

\nabla L_{i}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}}(a_{t}|s_{t})(\bm{G}_{i}(s_{t})-{\bm{b}}_{i}(s_{t}))\right],

(21)

where
\bm{G}_{i}(s_{t})=\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{\top}\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}r_{t^{\prime},i}

t

T

{\bm{b}}_{i}(s_{t})

\nabla L_{i}\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}}(a_{t}|s_{t})(\bm{G}_{i}(s_{t})-{\bm{v}}_{\bm{\phi}_{i}}(s_{t})).

Our PMGDA method not only achieves faster convergence than the modified Tchebycheff (mTche) method in three-objective synthetic problems (refer to
Section

) but also surpasses mTche in terms of final performance. In practice, minimax optimization can be unstable and hard for
neural networks. As illustrated in
Figure

, the majority of PMGDA solutions are dominant over those of mTche. This superiority is primarily due to mTche’s bi-layer optimization structure, which is a max-min problem. In this setup, rapid switching of the arg min index among Pareto objectives occurs frequently after a solution has became ‘exact’, leading to a zig-zag convergence trajectory. Consequently, the ’effective optimization quantities’ for both objectives are reduced. Given that optimizing a single objective in RL is already challenging, this oscillation further detracts from mTche’s performance.

Conversely, the PMGDA method, particularly in its correction step, adopts a more flexible approach. It only necessitates that the update angle aligns with the negative gradient of the preference function while satisfying the Armijo condition. This approach makes the objectives are optimized as effectively as possible (see Equation
9
). This softer, more adaptable method of handling constraints has proven to yield significantly better final performances, especially in challenging tasks.

V
Conclusions, Limitations, and Future Works

This paper addresses a fundamental problem in MOO: satisfying user-specific preference functions. Unlike previous approaches such as EPO
[
14
]
, COSMOS
[
4
]
, and PMTL
[
8
]
, which rely on specific rules to identify Pareto solutions under preference constraints, our proposed framework is versatile enough to incorporate general preference functions. In the well-known task of finding Pareto solutions that precisely align with a preference vector, our PMGDA method surpasses previous methods like EPO and COSMOS in precision, and outperforms mTche in terms of convergence speed. Additionally, our framework is capable of undertaking new challenges, such as identifying Pareto solutions that meet region of interest constraints—a task rarely addressed in gradient-based MOOs (MOOs).

However, the proposed method is not without limitations. As a gradient-based approach, it cannot guarantee globally optimal solutions. Future work will explore integrating gradient-based methods with evolutionary approaches to circumvent local optima and enhance solution quality.
