Representations of Coxeter groups and homology of Coxeter graphs
Hongsheng Hu

Abstract
We classify a class of complex representations of an arbitrary Coxeter group via characters of the integral homology of certain graphs. Such representations can be viewed as a generalization of the geometric representation and correspond to the second-highest 2-sided cell in the sense of Kazhdan-Lusztig. We also give a description of the cell representation provided by this 2-sided cell, and find out all its simple quotients for simply laced Coxeter system with no more than one circuit in the Coxeter graph.

§1  Introduction

Let
W=\langle S\mid s^{2}=(st)^{m_{st}}=e,\forall s\neq t\in S\rangle
be a Coxeter group of finite rank.
Weyl groups and affine Weyl groups are important examples, playing crucial roles in Lie theory and representation theory.
The representations of (affine) Weyl groups are well understood, while for general Coxeter groups they are still to be explored.

In the remarkable paper
[
5
]
, Kazhdan and Lusztig introduced the notion of 2-sided cells for arbitrary Coxeter system
(W,S)
.
Each 2-sided cell gives a representation of
W
, called a cell representation.
When Lusztig’s
\boldsymbol{a}
-function has a uniform bound on
W
, it is well known that any irreducible representation
V
corresponds to a unique 2-sided cell
\mathcal{C}
in the sense that
V
is a quotient of the cell representation provided by
\mathcal{C}
.
We recollect these notions briefly in §
3
.
Incidentally, we obtain a simple proof of the existence of the lowest 2-sided cell with the boundedness assumption on the function
\boldsymbol{a}
.

On the other hand, elements with
\boldsymbol{a}
-function value
1
in an irreducible Coxeter system (i.e. a Coxeter system with connected Coxeter graph) form a 2-sided cell, denoted by
\mathcal{C}_{1}
.
One of the results in this article is a necessary and sufficient condition for a nontrivial irreducible representation
V
to correspond to
\mathcal{C}_{1}
:

\begin{split}&\text{there is no $v\in V\setminus\{0\}$, such that $r\cdot v=t\cdot v=-v$}\\
&\text{for some $r\neq t\in S$ with $m_{rt}<\infty$.}\end{split}

(A1)

See
3.12
for details.
In particular, the geometric representation
V_{\text{geom}}
satisfies (
A1
).

There is an interesting class of representations “between”
V_{\text{geom}}
and the ones satisfying (
A1
), namely, the representations
V
satisfying the following:

\begin{split}&\text{$V$ has a basis $\{\alpha_{s}\mid s\in S\}$, and for any $s\in S$ there is a subspace}\\
&\text{$H_{s}$ of codimension $1$ such that $s|_{H_{s}}=\operatorname{Id}_{H_{s}}$, and $s\cdot\alpha_{s}=-\alpha_{s}$.}\end{split}

(IR)

Let’s refer to such representations as
IR-representations
.
The main aim of §
4
is to present a classification of IR-representations assuming
m_{st}<\infty
for any pair
s,t\in S
.
It turns out that IR-representations can be classified by some sets of natural numbers and one dimensional representations of the first homology groups of certain graphs.

In §
5
, we investigate the reducibility of IR-representations, which is encoded in a matrix defined by (
5.5
).
Such a criterion is similar to that of the geometric representation.
As an application, we determine all semisimple R-representations, which are defined to be a slightly larger class than IR-representations.
In §
8
, we will see that if
(W,S)
is simply laced (i.e.
m_{st}=2
or
3
,
\forall s,t\in S
) and there is no more than one circuit in its Coxeter graph, all irreducible representations of
W
corresponding to
\mathcal{C}_{1}
are R-representations, thus we have found all irreducible quotients of the cell representation provided by
\mathcal{C}_{1}
for such Coxeter groups.

Further we present some odds and ends in §
6
.
Proposition
6.1
tells us when an IR-representation has a
W
-invariant bilinear (or sesquilinear) form, and
6.8
discusses the dual of an IR-representation.

The story is similar when we are in the general setting.
In §
7
, we drop the assumption
m_{st}<\infty
and present the classification of IR-representations.

In a sequel paper
[
3
]
, we use similar methods to construct some infinite dimensional irreducible representations of a large class of Coxeter groups, suggesting that the representation theory of general Coxeter groups is quite different from finite and affine counterparts.

Graphs and their homology

By definition, a
(undirected) graph

G=(S,E)
consists of a set
S
of vertices and a set
E
of edges, elements in
E
being of the form
\{s,t\}
(unordered), where
s,t\in S
.

For our purpose, we only consider finite graphs without loops and multiple edges, i.e. there are no edges of the form
\{s,s\}
, each pair
\{s,t\}
occurs at most once in
E
, and
S
is a finite set.
Such a graph can be viewed as a finite simplicial complex in a natural way: vertices as
0
-simplices, edges as
1
-simplices.
A
connected component
of the graph
G
is defined to be that of this simplicial complex.

The chain complex (with coefficient
\mathbb{Z}
) of
G
looks like:

0\to\operatorname{C}_{1}(G)\xrightarrow{\partial}\operatorname{C}_{0}(G)\to 0.

The homology group
\operatorname{H}_{1}(G)=\ker\partial
is a finitely generated free abelian group since
\operatorname{C}_{1}(G)
is such a group.

Call
(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n})
a
path
in
G
if
\{s_{i}\}_{i}\subseteq S
and
\{s_{i},s_{i+1}\}\in E,\forall i
.
If
s_{1}=s_{n}
, say such a path is a
closed path
.
We don’t distinguish the closed paths
(s_{1},\dots,s_{n-1},s_{1})
and
(s_{2},\dots,s_{n-1},s_{1},s_{2})
.
Further, if
s_{1},\dots,s_{n-1}
are distinct in this closed path, call it a
circuit
.

For a connected graph
G
, a
spanning tree
is defined to be a subgraph
T=(S,E_{0})
with the same vertex set
S
and an edge set
E_{0}\subseteq E
, such that
T
is connected, and there are no circuits in
T
.
For general
G
, choosing a spanning tree for each component of
G
, their union is called a
spanning forest
of
G
.
Spanning forests always exist, but are not unique in general.

Fix a spanning forest
G_{0}=(S,E_{0})
of
G
.
For any edge
\mathfrak{e}\in E\setminus E_{0}
, fixing an orientation of
\mathfrak{e}
, there is a unique circuit in
(S,E_{0}\cup\{\mathfrak{e}\})
compatible with the orientation of
\mathfrak{e}
, denoted by
c_{\mathfrak{e}}
, representing an element in
\operatorname{H}_{1}(G)
.

Lemma 2.12
.

\operatorname{H}_{1}(G)=\bigoplus_{\mathfrak{e}\in E\setminus E_{0}}\mathbb{Z}c_{\mathfrak{e}}
.

In a Coxeter graph
G
, the number
m_{st}
is regarded as a label on the edge
\{s,t\}
, rather than a multiplicity.
Denote again by
\operatorname{H}_{1}(G)
the first integral homology of
G
forgetting labels on its edges.

This section explains partial motivations for considering IR-representations.

is irreducible.

(1)

There is a unique up to scalar

\operatorname{B}
on

.

(2)

Reducibility of IR-representations

Let

be an IR-representation of
(W,S)
defined by datum
(k_{rt},a_{r}^{t})_{r,t}
, and
\widetilde{G}
be the Coxeter graph attached to the numbers
(k_{rt})_{r,t}
, with corresponding Coxeter group

, see §
4

If
S=\sqcup_{i}S_{i}
is the decomposition of
\widetilde{G}
into connected components, let

and

denote the subgroup of

The following are obvious.

Lemma 5.2
.

(1)

\widetilde{W}=\prod_{i}\widetilde{W}_{i}
.

(2)

V=\bigoplus_{i}V_{i}
where

is a sub-representation of

.

(3)

Each

is an IR-representation of the Coxeter system
(W_{i},S_{i})
, defined by the datum
(k_{rt},a_{r}^{t})_{r,t\in S_{i},r\neq t}
, and factors through

.

(4)

For
j\neq i
,
W_{j}
acts trivially on

.

Thus, we can focus our study on each direct summand.

Proposition 5.3
.

Suppose
\widetilde{G}
is connected, and
U\subsetneq V
is a sub-representation.

(1)

If
U\neq 0
, the action of
W
on
U
is trivial.

(2)

If
V
is reducible, then
V
has a maximal sub-representation
V_{0}
with trivial
W
-action, and the quotient
V/V_{0}
is irreducible.

V
is indecomposable.

Proof:.

Take
v\in U
, suppose
s\cdot v\neq v
for some
s\in S
, then
v-s\cdot v\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\alpha_{s}
by the proof of
4.2
, thus
\alpha_{s}\in U
.
If
r\in S
is adjacent to

in
\widetilde{G}
, then
\alpha_{r}\in U
since
\langle\alpha_{s},\alpha_{r}\rangle
forms an irreducible representation of
\langle s,r\rangle
.
Inductively, we obtain

which is a contradiction.
(2) follows from (1).

\Box

Label elements in
S
to be
S=\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n}\}
, and use notations
\alpha_{i},k_{ij},a_{i}^{j},
etc as in the proof of
4.7
.
Write
v=\sum_{j}x_{j}\alpha_{j}
,
x_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
, then

\displaystyle s_{i}\cdot v

\displaystyle=-x_{i}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}x_{j}\Bigl(\alpha_{j}+2\frac{a_{i}^{j}}{a_{j}^{i}}\cos\frac{k_{ij}\uppi}{m_{ij}}\alpha_{i}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(-x_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}2x_{j}\frac{a_{i}^{j}}{a_{j}^{i}}\cos\frac{k_{ij}\uppi}{m_{ij}}\Bigr)\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}x_{j}\alpha_{j}.

If

is fixed by all

, then

x_{i}-\sum_{j\neq i}x_{j}\frac{a_{i}^{j}}{a_{j}^{i}}\cos\frac{k_{ij}\uppi}{m_{ij}}=0,\hskip 11.74988pt\forall i=1,\dots,n.

(5.4)

V
is reducible if and only if there exists nonzero vector
v\in V
fixed by each
s_{i}
, if and only if the set of equations (
5.4
) has a nonzero solution for variables
\{x_{i}\}_{i}
, if and only if the following

matrix is singular:

A:=\begin{pmatrix}1&-\frac{a_{1}^{2}}{a_{2}^{1}}\cos\frac{k_{12}\uppi}{m_{12}}&\dots\\
-\frac{a_{2}^{1}}{a_{1}^{2}}\cos\frac{k_{12}\uppi}{m_{12}}&1&\\
\vdots&&\ddots\end{pmatrix}

(5.5)

(All diagonal elements are

the element at

-position is

The co-rank of

.
To conclude:

Proposition 5.6
.

Suppose
\widetilde{G}
is connected.

(1)

V
is indecomposable.

is irreducible if and only if the matrix

defined in (

(2)

If

is reducible, and

is the maximal sub-representation, then the quotient

In general,

Remark 5.7
.

Fixing
(k_{rt})_{r,t}
such that

is connected and adapt the choice in

R-representations

In the condition (
IR
), we require that those “reflection vectors”
\{\alpha_{s}\}_{s}
are linearly independent.
Now let’s remove this restriction, but we still want to stay in the (
A1
) assumption, so we define
R-representations
to be representations
V
of
W
satisfying the following condition:

\begin{split}&\text{$V$ is spanned by nonzero vectors $\{\alpha_{s}\mid s\in S\}$, and $\forall s\in S$, $\exists$ a subspace}\\
&\text{$H_{s}$ of codimension $1$ such that $s|_{H_{s}}=\operatorname{Id}_{H_{s}}$, and $s\cdot\alpha_{s}=-\alpha_{s}$.}\\
&\text{Moreover, $\forall r\neq t\in S$ with $m_{rt}<\infty$, $\alpha_{r}$ and $\alpha_{t}$ are linearly independent.}\end{split}

(R)

Note that at present we require
m_{rt}<\infty
for any
r,t
(see the beginning of §
4
), so each pair
\alpha_{r},\alpha_{t}
are not proportional.

By definition, R-representations form a larger class than IR-representations.
But in fact they don’t produce too much more.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
V
be an R-representation.

(1)

There is a unique (up to isomorphism) IR-representation
V^{\prime}
such that
V
is a quotient of
V^{\prime}
, say,
\pi:V^{\prime}\twoheadrightarrow V
, and
W
acts on
\ker\pi
trivially.

(2)

Isomorphism classes of semisimple R-representations one-to-one correspond to isomorphism classes of IR-representations, thus to the set of data
\{((k_{rt})_{r,t},\chi)\}
in
4.10
.
In particular, simple R-representations correspond to those data such that
\widetilde{G}
is connected.

Proof:.

For such
V
,
V=\sum_{s\in S}\mathbb{C}\alpha_{s}
, and
\forall s\in S
,
V=H_{s}\oplus\mathbb{C}\alpha_{s}
.
By assumption (
4.1
), for any

is two dimensional, and the same argument in
4.2
still works.
Thus, we can extract the datum
(k_{rt},a_{r}^{t})_{r,t}
from

as in §
4
.
Let

be the surjective linear map defined by

.

is a quotient of

.

,

.
Restrict to the dihedral subgroup

Next, consider

Let

be the decomposition of

into components, then

§6  Additional properties

W
-invariant bilinear form

First we determine when an IR-representation admits a
W
-invariant bilinear form.
By
5.2
, we may focus on a direct summand of
V
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
V
be an IR-representation defined by datum
(k_{rt},a_{r}^{t})_{r,t}
, and
\chi
be the corresponding character of
\operatorname{H}_{1}(\widetilde{G})
. Assume
\widetilde{G}
is connected.

(1)

V
has a nonzero
W
-invariant bilinear form if and only if
\chi(\operatorname{H}_{1}(\widetilde{G}))\subseteq\{\pm 1\}
.

(2)

V
has a nonzero
W
-invariant sesquilinear form if and only if
\chi(\operatorname{H}_{1}(\widetilde{G}))\subseteq S^{1}
.

Proof:.

Suppose there is a nonzero
W
-invariant bilinear form
\operatorname{B}
on
V
.
For any path
(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n})
in
\widetilde{G}
, suppose
\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1})=x
(here
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{s_{1}}
, similarly for other notations), then by
2.7
and the construction of
V
, it holds
\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{2})=x(a_{1}^{2}/a_{2}^{1})^{2}
.
Do this recursively along this circuit, finally we get

\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{n},\alpha_{n})=x\left(\frac{a_{1}^{2}a_{2}^{3}\cdots a_{n-1}^{n}}{a_{2}^{1}a_{3}^{2}\cdots a_{n}^{n-1}}\right)^{2}.

(6.2)

If
x=0
, then
\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{s})=0
for any
s\in S
since
\widetilde{G}
is connected.
As a result, if
s,t\in S
such that
\widetilde{m}_{st}\geq 3
, then
\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{t})=\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{t},\alpha_{s})=0
by
2.7
.
If
\widetilde{m}_{st}=2
, then
\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{t})=\operatorname{B}(s\cdot\alpha_{s},s\cdot\alpha_{t})=-\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{t})
yields
\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{t})=0
.
Therefore
\operatorname{B}=0
which is a contradiction. Thus
x\neq 0
.
Suppose now
s_{1}=s_{n}
, then
\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{n},\alpha_{n})=x
, (
6.2
) implies the image of the closed path under
\chi
lies in
\{\pm 1\}
.

Conversely, suppose
\chi(\operatorname{H}_{1}(\widetilde{G}))\subseteq\{\pm 1\}
.
Choose and fix a vertex
s_{1}
in
\widetilde{G}
, define
\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}):=1
.
For another vertex
s
, choose a path
(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n}=s)
, in view of
2.7
, define

\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{s}):=\left(\frac{a_{1}^{2}a_{2}^{3}\cdots a_{n-1}^{n}}{a_{2}^{1}a_{3}^{2}\cdots a_{n}^{n-1}}\right)^{2}.

For
t\neq s
, define

\displaystyle\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{t},\alpha_{s})=\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{t}):=-\frac{a_{s}^{t}}{a_{t}^{s}}\cos\frac{k_{st}\uppi}{m_{st}}\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{s})

\displaystyle\bigl(\text{also equals to }-\frac{a_{t}^{s}}{a_{s}^{t}}\cos\frac{k_{st}\uppi}{m_{st}}\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{t},\alpha_{t})\bigr).

\operatorname{B}
is well-defined, i.e.
\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{s})
is independent of the choice of the path.
This is because, choosing another path
(s_{1},s_{p},s_{p-1},\dots,s_{n+1},s_{n}=s)
,
p\geq n
, the two paths form a circuit
(s_{1},\dots,s_{n},s_{n+1},\dots,s_{p},s_{1})
, and our assumption says

\pm 1=\frac{a_{1}^{2}\cdots a_{n-1}^{n}a_{n}^{n+1}\cdots a_{p-1}^{p}a_{p}^{1}}{a_{2}^{1}\cdots a_{n}^{n-1}a_{n+1}^{n}\cdots a_{p}^{p-1}a_{1}^{p}},\text{ thus }\left(\frac{a_{1}^{2}a_{2}^{3}\cdots a_{n-1}^{n}}{a_{2}^{1}a_{3}^{2}\cdots a_{n}^{n-1}}\right)^{2}=\left(\frac{a_{1}^{p}a_{p}^{p-1}\cdots a_{n+1}^{n}}{a_{p}^{1}a_{p-1}^{p}\cdots a_{n}^{n+1}}\right)^{2}.

Now we need to check
\operatorname{B}
is
W
-invariant. It suffices to check
\operatorname{B}(s\cdot\alpha_{r},s\cdot\alpha_{t})=\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{r},\alpha_{t})
for
s,r,t\in S
.
If
s=r=t
, this is obvious.
If
s=r\neq t
, or
s=t\neq r
, or
s\neq r=t
, everything happens in a dihedral world, the equation holds by
2.7
.
If
s,r,t
are distinct,

\displaystyle\operatorname{B}(s\cdot\alpha_{r},s\cdot\alpha_{t})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{B}\Bigl(\alpha_{r}+2\frac{a_{s}^{r}}{a_{r}^{s}}\cos\frac{k_{sr}\uppi}{m_{sr}}\alpha_{s},\alpha_{t}+2\frac{a_{s}^{t}}{a_{t}^{s}}\cos\frac{k_{st}\uppi}{m_{st}}\alpha_{s}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{r},\alpha_{t})+2\frac{a_{s}^{t}}{a_{t}^{s}}\cos\frac{k_{st}\uppi}{m_{st}}\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{r},\alpha_{s})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}+2\frac{a_{s}^{r}}{a_{r}^{s}}\cos\frac{k_{sr}\uppi}{m_{sr}}\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{t})+4\frac{a_{s}^{t}a_{s}^{r}}{a_{t}^{s}a_{r}^{s}}\cos\frac{k_{st}\uppi}{m_{st}}\cos\frac{k_{sr}\uppi}{m_{sr}}\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{s})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{B}(\alpha_{r},\alpha_{t}).

The proof of (2) is similar to the argument above.

Compared with

Suppose
