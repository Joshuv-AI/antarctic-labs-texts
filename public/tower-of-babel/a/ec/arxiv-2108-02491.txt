Quantum Charging Advantage Cannot Be Extensive Without Global Operations
Ju-Yeon Gyhm, Dominik Šafránek, Dario Rosa

Abstract
Quantum batteries are devices made from quantum states, which store and release energy in a fast and efficient manner, thus offering numerous possibilities in future technological applications. They offer a significant charging speedup when compared to classical batteries, due to the possibility of using entangling charging operations. We show that the maximal speedup that can be achieved is extensive in the number of cells, thus offering at most quadratic scaling in the charging power over the classically achievable linear scaling. To reach such a scaling, a global charging protocol, charging all the cells collectively, needs to be employed. This concludes the quest on the limits of charging power of quantum batteries and adds to other results in which quantum methods are known to provide at most quadratic scaling over their classical counterparts.

All of these achievements require an efficient way of storing and using energy, as well as fast charging and discharging. The necessity of charging and discharging goes well beyond the quantum world. Examples are electric vehicles where the charging time is one of the main bottlenecks in preventing the widespread use of such technology, or future fusion power plants, in which a large amount of energy needs to be pumped in a short amount of time and discharged in an instant to start the reaction. It has been shown

The source of this quantum speedup lies in the use of entangling operations, in which the cells are charged collectively as a whole. Those operations, where the number of cells that are being entangled together collectively scales with the system size (i.e., creating multi-partite entanglement), are called

In principle, this bound allows for a super-extensive scaling of the quantum advantage, meaning that the advantage can scale more than linearly with the number

This prompted a race toward finding the best possible scaling — the authors of

In this letter, we prove this conjecture, showing that a quantum battery provides

, we show that all-to-all interactions, and more generally, the participation number, does not provide any scaling advantage.

Setup.—

We consider quantum batteries made out of a time independent initial Hamiltonian,
\hat{H}
, having discrete spectrum. At time
t=0
a possibly time-dependent driving Hamiltonian,
\hat{V}(t)
, is turned on and the initial state
{\hat{\rho}}_{0}
is evolved according to the quench

\tfrac{d{\hat{\rho}}_{t}}{dt}=-i[\hat{V}(t),{\hat{\rho}}_{t}].

(2)

The energy stored in the battery, measured by the initial Hamiltonian, changes from
E(0)=\mathrm{tr}(\hat{H}{\hat{\rho}}_{0})
to
E(t)=\mathrm{tr}(\hat{H}{\hat{\rho}}_{t})
during time evolution.
Charging the battery means reaching large values of
E(t)-E(0)
.

An important figure of merit is the instantaneous charging power of the battery.
It is defined as the instantaneous change in the energy stored per unit of time:

P(t)=\mathrm{tr}\big(\hat{H}\,\tfrac{d{\hat{\rho}}_{t}}{dt}\big).

(3)

where we used that
\hat{H}
is time-independent.

Generally, the instantaneous power is bounded in terms of the commutator between
\hat{H}
and the driving term

|P(t)|\leq\left\|[\hat{H},\hat{V}(t)]\right\|\leq 2\left\|\hat{H}\right\|\left\|\hat{V}(t)\right\|

(4)

through the operator norm
1
1

1

It is proved by inserting Eq. (
2
) to Eq. (
3
), which gives
P(t)=\mathrm{tr}({\hat{\rho}}[\hat{H},\hat{V}])=\sum_{k}\rho_{k}\langle\psi_{k}|[\hat{H},\hat{V}]|\psi_{k}\rangle\leq\left\|[\hat{H},\hat{V}]\right\|\leq 2\left\|\hat{H}\right\|\left\|\hat{V}\right\|
, using the spectral decomposition
{\hat{\rho}}=\sum_{k}\rho_{k}|\psi_{k}\rangle\langle\psi_{k}|
and
\sum_{k}\rho_{k}=1
. See, for example, Ref.
Julià-Farré et al. 2020
.

However, the driving is often limited in realistic situations. For example, in lattice systems, the interaction couples only nearby sites and therefore
\hat{V}
transfers energy only between not-too-distant energy levels of the initial Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
.
Taking the spectral decomposition of the initial Hamiltonian to be
\hat{H}=\sum_{j}E_{j}|E_{j}\rangle\langle E_{j}|
, where we assume the energy levels
E_{j}
being ordered, we express the driving Hamiltonian as
\hat{V}=\sum_{j,m=1}^{N}V_{jm}|E_{j}\rangle\langle E_{m}|
. The limiting property is formalized as follows: we define
\Delta E
as the minimum value such that for all
j
and
m
,

\mathrm{when}\quad|E_{j}-E_{m}|>\Delta E,\quad\mathrm{then}\quad V_{jm}=0.

(5)

Thus, it is natural to look for a more precise bound than eq. (
4
), taking this common property into account.

Main result.—
In the conditions of eq. (
5
), we now show that a more stringent bound can be derived.

Theorem 1
.

For driving that couples energy levels with at most
\Delta E
energy difference, as expressed by eq. (
5
), the instantaneous charging power is bounded as

|P(t)|\leq\Delta E\left\|\hat{V}(t)-v_{\min}(t)\right\|/2,

(6)

where
v_{\min}(t)
is the smallest eigenvalue of
\hat{V}(t)
, and
\left\|~\right\|
denotes the operator norm.

Hence, the operator norm of the initial Hamiltonian, central in inequality (
4
),
is not
the relevant figure of merit. Instead, the crucial quantity is the maximal value of energy (as measured by
\hat{H}
) that can be transferred by
\hat{V}
in a single time step. While non-trivial to prove, this result is very intuitive.
The charging power is the amount of the energy stored in the battery in a single time step. Thus, this change in energy must be bounded by the maximum amount of energy that the driving term can transfer to the system during that time.

The fact that the bound is not given by the operator norm of
\hat{H}
has a far reaching consequence. As outlined in the Introduction, it has been a matter of active research which combination of initial and driving Hamiltonians can reach a charging power scaling with
\left\|\hat{H}\right\|
. Theorem
1
shows that to reach this scaling one needs to consider driving terms having non vanishing matrix elements between the ground state and the highly excited states of
\hat{H}
.
This latter property defines the so-called
global
charging operations which we discuss more extensively later. Another point of this bound is that it applies to any Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
, even with interacting cells.

Sketch of the proof.—

The full proof of the theorem is technically involved. Here we sketch the main idea, while we refer the reader to the Supplementary material for details.

We express the commutator between
\hat{H}
and
\hat{V}
as an integral of commutators which are more easily and directly bounded. In particular, we define certain operator functions
\hat{h}(e)
and
\hat{v}(e)
, depending on a continuous parameter
e
and satisfying

\left[\hat{H},\hat{V}\right]=\int_{e=0}^{\Delta E}[\hat{h}(e),\hat{v}(e)]\,de,

(7)

as well as the following properties

\left\|\hat{h}(e)\right\|=\frac{1}{2},\quad\quad\left\|\hat{v}(e)\right\|=\left\|\hat{V}\right\|.

(8)

We apply triangle inequality to eq. (
7
) to derive bound
\left\|\left[\hat{H},\hat{V}\right]\right\|\leq\Delta E\left\|\hat{V}(t)\right\|
.
Since any number
\lambda
commutes with
\hat{H}
, and
\hat{V}^{\prime}_{\lambda}=\hat{V}-\lambda
also satisfies eq. (
5
), we can make this bound tighter by minimizing over
\lambda
,

\left\|\left[\hat{H},\hat{V}\right]\right\|\!=\!\inf_{\lambda}\left\|\left[\hat{H},\hat{V}^{\prime}_{\lambda}\right]\right\|\!\leq\!\inf_{\lambda}\Delta E\!\left\|\hat{V}\!-\!\lambda\right\|\!=\!\frac{\Delta E}{2}\!\left\|\hat{V}\!\!-\!v_{\min}\right\|.

(9)

The theorem then follows from eq. (
4
).

Corollary 1
.

For initial and driving Hamiltonians (

) and (

), the instantaneous charging power is bounded as,

|P(t)|\leq k\left\|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}-E_{\mathrm{s}\min}\right\|\left\|\hat{V}(t)-v_{\min}(t)\right\|/2.

(13)

where

is the single cell ground state energy.

The result is proven by showing that the maximum energy jump
\Delta E

.
As shown in the Supplementary Material, the corollary then follows directly from Theorem

.

The consequences of the bound in eq. (
66
) are remarkable. In particular, it rules out the possibility of having extensive quantum charging advantage without global charging operations.

To show that, we need to discuss each of these terms separately: as explained above,
k
is the number of cells being coupled together by the driving, and thus
k\leq L
.
\|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}-E_{\mathrm{s}\min}\|
is a number that depends on particulars of a single cell but does not scale with
L
. The last term,
\left\|\hat{V}(t)-v_{\min}(t)\right\|
, which we call
potential
(in analogy with electric circuits), can be in principle made arbitrarily large. Physically, this would correspond to investing a very large/infinite energy into the driving. With larger driving energy, the charging is faster. Thus, to compute the quantum charging advantage, we need to compare the parallel and quantum scaling on an equal footing, by assuming that the energy scale that is invested into the driving is the same in both cases.
We do that by fixing the potential of the quantum driving to be at most equal to the potential of the parallel driving,
\|\hat{V}-v_{\min}\|\leq\|\hat{V}^{\parallel}-v_{\min}^{\parallel}\|
. This is the constraint C0, introduced and argued for in Ref.
Campaioli et al. 2017
.

Parallel charging is given by
k=1
in driving Hamiltonian (
63
), while the initial state is assumed to be a product state
{\hat{\rho}}={\hat{\rho}}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\otimes L}
. Thus, in the parallel charging scenario the driving affects each cell independently. From this, we easily calculate that both the potential
\|\hat{V}^{\parallel}-v_{\min}^{\parallel}\|=L\|\hat{V}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\parallel}-v_{{\mathrm{s}}\min}^{\parallel}\|
and the charging power
P^{\parallel}=LP_{\mathrm{s}}^{\parallel}
scale linearly with
L
.
{\hat{\rho}}_{\mathrm{s}}
,
\|\hat{V}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\parallel}-v_{{\mathrm{s}}\min}^{\parallel}\|
, and
P_{\mathrm{s}}^{\parallel}
denote the state, potential, and charging power of a single cell, respectively.

Combining eq. (
66
), constraint C0, and the results for parallel charging, we bound the quantum advantage as

|\Gamma|=\frac{\left|P\right|}{\left|P^{\parallel}\right|}\leq\frac{k\|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}-E_{\mathrm{s}\min}\|\|\hat{V}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\parallel}-v_{{\mathrm{s}}\min}^{\parallel}\|L}{2|P_{\mathrm{s}}^{\parallel}|L}=\gamma k,

(14)

where
\gamma
is
L
and
k
-independent. Thus, the quantum advantage scales with the maximum number
k
of cells that are coupled together by
\hat{V}
. If this number
does not
scale with the lattice size,
L
, then the quantum advantage cannot scale with
L
, as extensively foreshadowed in the Introduction. The extensive scaling is possible only for global interactions,
k\propto L
. By showing that the only source of the quantum advantage comes from the global entangling operations, we showed that the advantage comes from genuine quantum effects. This addresses the discussion of the role of quantum-ness posed in relation to the bound on charging power found in
Julià-Farré et al. 2020
.

Finally, we ask what is the maximal scaling of power with
L
that a quantum charging protocol can achieve. Clearly, using Eq. (
66
), the maximum charging power is given by the product of
k
, and by whatever scaling can be constructed from
\|\hat{V}-v_{\min}\|
(for now leaving constraint
C0
behind). It is possible to artificially construct some driving Hamiltonians that scale super-extensively, i.e., with higher powers of
L

Le et al. 2018
;
Ferraro et al. 2018
. However, such models are unphysical
Julià-Farré et al. 2020
;
Rossini et al. 2020
, because they would lead to a free energy that is super-extensive in the thermodynamic limit. Therefore, for any physical model, considering extensive energy
\|\hat{V}-v_{\min}\|\sim L
, the maximal charging power scales at most quadratically
P\sim L^{2}
, for global operations
k\propto L
. (Compare with the linear scaling of parallel charging.)

As an illustrative example, we study charging of a quantum battery by means of a driving Hamiltonian obtained via a simple generalization of the celebrated SY Hamiltonian
Sachdev and Ye 1993
,
i.e.
a random,
2
-local,
all-to-all
Hamiltonian

\hat{V}=C\sum_{i<j}^{L}\sum_{\alpha=x,\,y,\,z}J_{ij}^{\alpha}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{\alpha}\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{\alpha},

(15)

where the coupling constants
J_{ij}^{\alpha}
are randomly extracted from a normal distribution and the normalization factor
C
is chosen such that
\left\|\hat{V}-v_{\mathrm{min}}\right\|=2
, to ensure a fair comparison between different realizations (instances). (
C\propto L^{-3/2}
, which for the SY Hamiltonian follows from the replica formalism
Bray and Moore 1980
;
Sachdev and Ye 1993
. We numerically confirm this scaling in the Supplementary material.) The results are shown in Fig.
3
(a). We clearly see that the power is bounded by the degree of
k
-locality and
not
by the participation number. As a result, we do not find any extensive charging advantage for this model as expected.
Interestingly, we observe that both the maximum power as well as the maximum value of the commutator norm

\left\|[\hat{H},\,\hat{V}]\right\|

slightly decreases with the system size,
L
.
This is a finite size effect which sensitively reduces by further increasing the system size.
We present an analysis of this phenomenon in the Supplemental Material.

Does global charging always lead to an extensive quantum advantage?—

The presence of a global charging term in
\hat{V}
does not guarantee an extensive charging advantage.

As an example, consider a battery composed of
L
qubits having initial Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}h\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{l}
and charged via the following driving

\hat{V}=\frac{V}{\lfloor L/2\rfloor+1}\big(\sum_{l=\textrm{odd}}\hat{\sigma}_{l}^{x}\otimes\hat{\sigma}_{l+1}^{x}+\bigotimes^{L}_{l=1}\hat{\sigma}_{l}^{x}\big),

(16)

with
V
being a constant. From Theorem
1
, we obtain

P\leq 2Lh\left\|\hat{V}\right\|=2LhV
(using
\|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}-E_{\mathrm{s}\min}\|=2h
),
due to the second term representing a global operation, which couples all of the sites at the same time. Presence of this global charging term suggests possibility of an extensive charging advantage.

However, in this case an extensive quantum advantage is not reached. This is because the nearest-neighbor terms
\sum_{l=\textrm{odd}}\hat{\sigma}_{l}^{x}\otimes\hat{\sigma}_{l+1}^{x}
, which provide
non-extensive
advantage, dominate the interaction. They contribute
V/(1+1/\lfloor L/2\rfloor)
while the global term contributes only
V/(\lfloor L/2\rfloor+1)
to the total norm
\left\|\hat{V}\right\|
. This sub-extensive scaling is confirmed by the following, alternative, inequality, which is derived in the Supplementary material,

|P(t)|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{L}k\left\|\hat{V}_{k}\right\|\left\|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}-E_{\mathrm{s}\min}\right\|,

(17)

where
\hat{V}_{k}
is the
k
-local part of
\hat{V}
. From inequality (
17
) we obtain

P\leq 4\|\hat{V}_{2}\|h+2L\|\hat{V}_{L}\|h=(\frac{4}{1+1/\lfloor L/2\rfloor}+\frac{2L}{\lfloor L/2\rfloor+1})Vh\approx 8Vh
for the present example, which indeed confirms that the power does not display an extensive advantage. As a further confirmation, we explicitly computed the maximum charging power for this driving Hamiltonian (
16
), reported in Fig.
3
(b). We clearly see that the charging power stays well-below the threshold given by eq. (
17
).

Discussion and conclusions.—

We found a bound on the maximum charging power which can be achieved by charging a quantum battery via an external quench protocol.

This bound shows that the maximum charging power is not dependent on the operator norm of the battery Hamiltonian, by which the amount of charged energy is measured. Instead, it is governed by the maximum energy difference,
\Delta E
, between two eigenstates of the battery Hamiltonian for which the driving Hamiltonian has a non-vanishing matrix element. In other words, the charging power is limited by the amount of energy that the driving Hamiltonian can add into the battery in a single step, a result which
a posteriori
seems very natural. This bound can be applied to a general quantum battery, described by any Hamiltonian, even those of interacting quantum cells.

When applied to quantum batteries made of
L
identical cells, this bound provides a limit on how fast they can be charged as compared to classical batteries. The maximum speed by which a quantum battery can be charged depends only on the number
k
of cells interacting together in a single term. It
does not
depend on the participation number, which is the number of independent terms in the driving Hamiltonian in which a single cell appears. For example, pair-wise interactions can provide a quantum speedup by at most a factor of two, even in the case of all-to-all couplings, where every cell is connected to every other cell. For a speedup of a factor of
k
, one needs to consider k-cell interactions, while the maximal speedup of
L
is achieved for
L
-particle interactions. While charging power of classical batteries scales linearly with the number of cells (
\propto L
), quantum batteries provide at most quadratic scaling in charging power (
\propto L^{2}
). This quadratic scaling cannot be reached without global operations. However, the mere presence of global charging operations does not always guarantee an extensive charging advantage, as we demonstrated on an explicit example.

This work adds to other results, in which quantum systems provide at most quadratic improvement over the known classical method, like the Heisenberg limit in sensitivity scaling in quantum metrology over the classically achievable shot-noise limit
Giovannetti et al. 2004
;
Giovannetti et al. 2011
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2012
, and Grover’s search algorithm
Grover 1996
, which is known to be asymptotically optimal
Bennett et al. 1997
.

The bound specifies, for a given battery Hamiltonian and for a given driving, the maximum instantaneous charging power achievable in that particular setup.
It does not give any information about the quantum state for which such a power can be achieved.
This constitutes an interesting question for future research.

Lemma 2
.

Operator norms of
\hat{v}(e)
and
\hat{V}
are equal for every
e
, expressed mathematically as

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{v}(e)\right\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{V}\right\|.

(38)

Proof.

We prove lemma
2
by showing that
\hat{v}(e)
and
\hat{V}
have same spectra. Then the Lemma follows directly from the definition of the operator norm.
Operator norm is defined as

\left\|\hat{A}\right\|=\sup_{|\psi\rangle}\frac{\left\|\hat{A}|\psi\rangle\right\|}{\left\||\psi\rangle\right\|}

(39)

where the supremum goes over all vectors
|\psi\rangle
. In the case of bounded Hermitian operators, this is equivalent to the supremum of absolutes value of eigenvalues of the operator
\hat{A}
,

\left\|\hat{A}\right\|=\sup_{\lambda}|\lambda|,

(40)

which means that the same spectrum directly imply the same operator norm between different operators.

We express each eigenvector
|{\alpha}\rangle
of the driving Hamiltonian
\hat{V}
in terms of eigenvectors of the initial Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
as

\displaystyle|{\alpha}\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\beta_{j}\lvert E_{j}\rangle}.

(41)

By definition of an eigenstate, the following equation holds:

\displaystyle{\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\beta_{j}\lvert E_{j}\rangle}={\alpha}|{\alpha}\rangle=\hat{V}|{\alpha}\rangle=\sum_{j,m=0}^{N}{V_{jm}\beta_{m}\lvert E_{j}\rangle}.

(42)

Therefore, the fact that
|\alpha\rangle
is an eigenstate of driving Hamiltonian
\hat{V}
is equivalent to that for all
j
,

{\alpha}\beta_{j}=\sum_{m=1}^{N}{V_{jm}\beta_{m}}.

(43)

Further, for each eigenvalue of the driving Hamiltonian
\alpha
, and for any arbitrary but fixed value of
e
, we define the state
|{\gamma}(e)\rangle
(will we omit writing the dependence on
\alpha
for clarity), which we will prove to be an eigenvector of operator
\hat{v}(e)
that corresponds to the same eigenvalue (but now of operator
\hat{v}(e)
)
\alpha
. Thus, by doing this we will show that
\hat{V}
and
\hat{v}(e)
have the same spectrum. We define state
|{\gamma}(e)\rangle
as

|{\gamma}(e)\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\gamma}_{j}(e)|E_{j}\rangle.

(44)

Coefficients
{\gamma}_{j}(e)
are defined by a recurrence relation

\begin{split}&{\gamma}_{1}(e)=\beta_{1}\\
&{\gamma}_{j+1}(e)=\begin{cases}\ {\gamma}_{j}(e)\frac{\beta_{j+1}}{\beta_{j}},\quad&\mathrm{for}\ h_{j+1}(e)\geq h_{j}(e),\\
-{\gamma}_{j}(e)\frac{\beta_{j+1}}{\beta_{j}},\quad&\mathrm{for}\ h_{j+1}(e)<h_{j}(e).\end{cases}\end{split}

(45)

Since
V_{jm}
and
v_{jm}(e)
are zero when
|E_{j}-E_{m}|>\Delta E
(as follows from eqs. (
18
) and (
21
)), we just need to consider pairs of
j,m
that satisfy
|E_{j}-E_{m}|\leq\Delta E
.

First, we assume
j>m
. We will show that there is at most one index
k
,
j>k\geq m
, for which
h_{k+1}(e)
and
h_{k}(e)
are different. This statement is relatively clear from Fig.
2
: for
|E_{j}-E_{m}|\leq\Delta E
and a fixed
e
, the horizontal line drawn at height of
e
can cross the red region into blue (or blue into red) at most once. To prove this statement mathematically (which duplicates the visual proof), for a contradiction we assume that there are two distinct indexes
k
and
k^{\prime}
such that
j>k^{\prime}>k\geq m
, where the sign changes, i.e., for which
h_{k+1}(e)\neq h_{k}(e)
and
h_{k^{\prime}+1}(e)\neq h_{k^{\prime}}(e)
. By definition of
h_{k}(e)
(eq. (
20
)),

E_{k^{\prime}+1}-E_{k}>\Delta E.

(46)

However, from

and from the fact that we assumed our energy levels to be ordered, we have

E_{j}-E_{m}\geq E_{k^{\prime}+1}-E_{k}

(47)

which according to eq. (
46
) means that
E_{j}-E_{m}>\Delta E
, which is in contradiction with our assumption
|E_{j}-E_{m}|\leq\Delta E
.

So we just showed that for every combination
j>m
there exists at most one index
k
,
j>k\geq m
, such that
h(e)
changes sign at this index, i.e.,
h_{k}(e)\neq h_{k+1}(e)
. Using this knowledge, we compute a ratio that will be useful later in showing that
|\gamma(e)\rangle
is an eigenstate of
\hat{v}(e)
.
In the case when
k
exists, we obtain ratio

\begin{split}&\frac{{\gamma}_{m}(e)}{{\gamma}_{j}(e)}=\prod_{s=m}^{j-1}\frac{{\gamma}_{s}(e)}{{\gamma}_{s+1}(e)}\\
&=\begin{cases}\;\;\;\prod_{s=m}^{j-1}\frac{\beta{s}}{\beta{s+1}}=\;\frac{\beta{m}}{\beta{j}}&\quad\mathrm{for}\;h_{k+1}(e)-h_{k}(e)>0\\
-\prod_{s=m}^{j-1}\frac{\beta{s}}{\beta{s+1}}=-\frac{\beta{m}}{\beta{j}}&\quad\mathrm{for}\;h_{k+1}(e)-h_{k}(e)<0\end{cases}\end{split}

(48)

by the definition of
{\gamma}
(eq. (
45
)).

Because
k
is the only point where value

changes, it means that
h_{j}(e)=h_{k+1}(e)
and
h_{m}(e)=h_{k}(e)
, which means we can rewrite the above equation as

\begin{split}&\frac{{\gamma}_{m}(e)}{{\gamma}_{j}(e)}=\begin{cases}\ \frac{\beta{m}}{\beta{j}}&\quad\mathrm{for}\;h_{j}(e)-h_{m}(e)>0\\
-\frac{\beta{m}}{\beta{j}}&\quad\mathrm{for}\;h_{j}(e)-h_{m}(e)<0.\end{cases}\end{split}

(49)

If such

h_{m}(e)=h_{m+1}(e)=\cdots=h_{j}(e)
, in which case

\begin{split}&\frac{{\gamma}_{m}(e)}{{\gamma}_{j}(e)}=\begin{cases}\ \frac{\beta{m}}{\beta{j}}&\quad\mathrm{for}\;h_{j}(e)-h_{m}(e)\geq 0\\
-\frac{\beta{m}}{\beta{j}}&\quad\mathrm{for}\;h_{j}(e)-h_{m}(e)<0.\end{cases}\end{split}

(50)

The case of

\frac{{\gamma}_{m}(e)}{{\gamma}_{j}(e)}=\begin{cases}\ \frac{\beta_{m}}{\beta_{j}}\quad\,\,\mathrm{for}\ (h_{j}(e)-h_{m}(e))(j-m)\geq 0\\
-\frac{\beta_{m}}{\beta_{j}}\quad\mathrm{for}\ (h_{j}(e)-h_{m}(e))(j-m)<0.\end{cases}

(51)

Combining eq. (

\sum_{m=1}^{N}{v_{jm}(e)\frac{{\gamma}_{m}(e)}{{\gamma}_{j}(e)}}=\sum_{m=1}^{N}{V_{jm}\frac{\beta_{m}}{\beta_{j}}}=\alpha.

(52)

Using this property, we have

\begin{split}\hat{v}(e)|{\gamma}(e)\rangle&=\sum_{j,m=1}^{N}v_{jm}(e){\gamma}_{m}(e)|E_{j}\rangle\\
&={\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{{\gamma}_{j}(e)\lvert E_{j}\rangle}={\alpha}|{\gamma}(e)\rangle.\end{split}

(53)

This directly implies that

is an eigenstate of
\hat{v}(e)
associated with eigenvalue

. Therefore,
\hat{V}
and
\hat{v}(e)
have the same spectrum. By Eq. (

),
\left\|\hat{V}\right\|=\left\|\hat{v}(e)\right\|
, which concludes the proof.

Theorem 1
.

(Extended version of Theorem 1. shown in the main text) For driving that couples energy levels with at most
\Delta E
energy difference, as expressed by eq. (
18
), the following series of inequalities holds.

\left\|[\hat{H},\hat{V}]\right\|\leq\Delta E\inf_{\hat{D}}\left\|\hat{V}\!-\!\hat{D}\right\|\leq\Delta E\left\|\hat{V}-v_{\min}\right\|/2\leq\Delta E\left\|\hat{V}\right\|,

(54)

where
\hat{D}
are matrices diagonal in the eigenbasis of the initial Hamiltonian
{\hat{H}}
.

Proof.

By definition (
20
) the eigenvalues of
\hat{h}(e)
are either
\frac{1}{2}
or
-\frac{1}{2}
. Hence

\left\|\hat{h}(e)\right\|=\frac{1}{2}

(55)

holds for all
e
’s.
Using lemma
1
,
\left\|[\hat{H},\hat{V}]\right\|
can be rewritten as

\left\|[\hat{H},\hat{V}]\right\|\\
=\left\|\int_{e=0}^{\Delta E}{[\hat{h}(e),\hat{v}(e)]de}\right\|.

(56)

We apply the triangle inequality of operator norm two times (once for the first and once for the second inequality) and obtain

\begin{split}&\left\|\int_{e=0}^{\Delta E}{[\hat{h}(e),\hat{v}(e)]de}\right\|\\
&\leq\int_{e=0}^{\Delta E}{\left\|[\hat{h}(e),\hat{v}(e)]\right\|de}\leq\int_{e=0}^{\Delta E}{2\left\|\hat{h}(e)\right\|\left\|\hat{v}(e)\right\|de}.\end{split}

(57)

By lemma
2
and eq. (
55
), the right hand side of this equation equals

\int_{e=0}^{\Delta E}2\left\|\hat{h}(e)\right\|\left\|\hat{v}(e)\right\|de=\int_{e=0}^{\Delta E}{\left\|\hat{V}\right\|de}=\Delta E\left\|\hat{V}\right\|,

(58)

Since any diagonal matrix
\hat{D}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}|E_{j}\rangle\langle E_{j}|
, commutes with
\hat{H}
, we can make this bound tighter by minimizing over
\hat{D}
. The key is to realize that if we define
\hat{V}^{\prime}=\hat{V}-\hat{D}
, where
\hat{V}
satisfies eq. (
18
), then also
\hat{V}^{\prime}=\sum_{j,m=1}^{N}(V_{jm}-\delta_{jm}\lambda_{j})|E_{j}\rangle\langle E_{m}|
satisfies the same equation, with the same
\Delta E
. Thus, equation (
58
) holds also for operator
\hat{V}^{\prime}
, and we have

\left\|\left[\hat{H},\hat{V}\right]\right\|=\left\|\left[\hat{H},\hat{V}-\hat{D}\right]\right\|\leq\Delta E\left\|\hat{V}-\hat{D}\right\|,

(59)

which holds for any
\hat{D}
which is diagonal in the energy basis.

We can take the infimum over all such diagonal matrices, which gives

\left\|\left[\hat{H},\hat{V}\right]\right\|=\inf_{\hat{D}}\left\|\left[\hat{H},\hat{V}-\hat{D}\right]\right\|\leq\Delta E\inf_{\hat{D}}\left\|\hat{V}-\hat{D}\right\|

(60)

This represents the tightest bound obtained by the present method.

To prove the theorem as it is written in the main text, we restrict ourselves to the case of
\hat{D}=\lambda\hat{I}
. In this case we can evaluate the infimum and obtain explicitly

\begin{split}&\left\|\left[\hat{H},\hat{V}\right]\right\|\leq\Delta E\inf_{\hat{D}}\left\|\hat{V}\!-\!\hat{D}\right\|\\
&\leq\Delta E\inf_{\lambda}\left\|\hat{V}\!-\!\lambda\hat{I}\right\|=\Delta E\!\left\|\hat{V}\!-\!v_{\min}\right\|/2,\end{split}

(61)

which proves the theorem.

∎

We consider a battery composed of

\hat{H}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\hat{H}^{(l)},

(62)

We charge this battery by turning on the driving Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle\hat{V}(t)=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{i}}\in K(L,k)}{\hat{V}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}}(t),

(63)

where, by definition, each term in the summation couples together at most
k
cells. Expressed mathematically,

\displaystyle K(L,k)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{n=1}^{k}C(L,n),

(64)

\displaystyle C(L,n)

\displaystyle=\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})|i_{1}<\cdots<i_{n}\text{\ and\ }i_{j}\in\{1,\dots,L\}\},

where
C(L,n)
is a set of all combinations of
n
sites,
and
V_{\boldsymbol{i}}
acts as an identity on the site which does not appear in the index, i.e., for any local matrix
\hat{M}^{(l)}=\hat{I}\otimes\cdots\otimes\hat{I}\otimes\hat{M}\otimes\hat{I}\otimes\cdots\otimes\hat{I}
, where
\hat{M}
is at the
l
-th place, if
l\not\in{\boldsymbol{i}}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})
, then

[\hat{M}^{(l)},\hat{V}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)]=0.

(65)

Corollary 1
.

For identical cells, which have initial Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\hat{H}^{(l)}
, where
\hat{H}^{(l)}=\hat{I}\otimes\cdots\otimes\hat{I}\otimes\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}\otimes\hat{I}\otimes\cdots\otimes\hat{I}
and the driving Hamiltonian is of form (
63
), the following inequality holds:

|P(t)|\leq k\left\|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}-E^{(\mathrm{s})}_{\min}\right\|\left\|\hat{V}(t)-v_{\min}(t)\right\|/2,

(66)

where
E^{(\mathrm{s})}_{\min}
is the single cell ground state energy. (In the main text, we denoted
E_{\mathrm{s}\min}\equiv E^{(\mathrm{s})}_{\min}
for better readability.)

Proof.

The single site Hamiltonian has spectral decomposition as
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{s}}}E_{j}^{(\mathrm{s})}|E_{j}^{(\mathrm{s})}\rangle\langle E_{j}^{(\mathrm{s})}|
, where
E_{j}^{(\mathrm{s})}
is ordered by increasing energy, and
|E_{j}^{(\mathrm{s})}\rangle
is the single site energy eigenstate. The basis of the initial Hamiltonian is rewritten as

\begin{split}&\hat{H}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{j}}=(1,\cdots,1)}^{(N_{\mathrm{s}},\cdots,N_{\mathrm{s}})}E_{{\boldsymbol{j}}}^{(\mathrm{s})}|E_{{\boldsymbol{j}}}^{(\mathrm{s})}\rangle\langle E_{{\boldsymbol{j}}}^{(\mathrm{s})}|\end{split}

(67)

where
{\boldsymbol{j}}=(j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,j_{L})
,
E_{{\boldsymbol{j}}}^{(\mathrm{s})}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}E_{j_{l}}^{(\mathrm{s})}
, and
|E_{{\boldsymbol{j}}}^{(\mathrm{s})}\rangle=|E_{j_{1}}^{(\mathrm{s})}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|E_{j_{L}}^{(\mathrm{s})}\rangle
.

An element of the driving Hamiltonian given by eq. (
63
),
\hat{V}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
, is decomposed in the basis of the initial Hamiltonian as

\hat{V}_{\boldsymbol{i}}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{j}},{\boldsymbol{m}}=(1,\cdots,1)}^{(N_{\mathrm{s}},\cdots,N_{\mathrm{s}})}V_{{\boldsymbol{i}},{\boldsymbol{j}}{\boldsymbol{m}}}|E_{{\boldsymbol{j}}}^{(\mathrm{s})}\rangle\langle E_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}^{(\mathrm{s})}|.

(68)

To understand this complicated expression, we illustrate the use of indexes as follows:
{\boldsymbol{i}}
denotes positions of all the sites which interact through
\hat{V}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
(for example
{\boldsymbol{i}}=(1,2)
means that only the first and the second site interact), while
{\boldsymbol{j}}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{L})
and
{\boldsymbol{m}}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{L})
denote energy eigenstates of each site (for example, for
L=4
,
{\boldsymbol{m}}=(1,2,1,4)
corresponds to tensor product of local energy eigenstates
|E_{(1,2,1,4)}^{(\mathrm{s})}\rangle=|E_{1}^{(\mathrm{s})}\rangle|E_{2}^{(\mathrm{s})}\rangle|E_{1}^{(\mathrm{s})}\rangle|E_{4}^{(\mathrm{s})}\rangle
). Then by definition (using additionally
{\boldsymbol{j}}=(1,3,2,1)
),

V_{(1,2),(1,3,2,1)(1,2,1,4)}=\langle E_{(1,3,2,1)}^{(\mathrm{s})}|\hat{V}_{(1,2)}|E_{(1,2,1,4)}^{(\mathrm{s})}\rangle

(69)

Many of the elements of
V_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}
are zero. To show that, let us assume that
l\notin{\boldsymbol{i}}
. Then choosing
\hat{M}=|E_{j_{l}}\rangle\langle E_{j_{l}}|
, from eq. (
65
) we obtain

0=\langle E_{\boldsymbol{j}}|[\hat{M}^{(l)},\hat{V}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)]|E_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle=\langle E_{\boldsymbol{j}}|\hat{V}_{\boldsymbol{i}}|E_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle(1-\delta_{j_{l}m_{l}})

(70)

Thus, if
j_{l}\neq m_{l}
, then

V_{{\boldsymbol{i}},{\boldsymbol{j}}{\boldsymbol{m}}}\equiv\langle E_{\boldsymbol{j}}|\hat{V}_{\boldsymbol{i}}|E_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle=0.

(71)

To summarize, for any
l\notin{\boldsymbol{i}}
, if
j_{l}\neq m_{l}
, then
V_{{\boldsymbol{i}},{\boldsymbol{j}}{\boldsymbol{m}}}=0
. In other words, every element
V_{{\boldsymbol{i}},{\boldsymbol{j}}{\boldsymbol{m}}}
that is non-zero must have the same indexes
m_{l}=j_{l}
for every
l\not\in{\boldsymbol{i}}
.

For example, element (
69
) is zero, because
3\notin{\boldsymbol{i}}=(1,2)
, and
2=j_{3}\neq m_{3}=1
. Elements that are allowed to be non-zero are, for example,
V_{(1,2),(1,3,1,1)(1,2,1,1)}
,
V_{(1,2),(2,4,2,1)(1,2,2,1)}
, or
V_{(1,2),(3,3,3,3)(3,2,3,3)}
.

In order to prove the Corollary, we must show that
for all
{\boldsymbol{j}}
and
{\boldsymbol{m}}
such that
|E_{{\boldsymbol{j}}}-E_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}|>k\left\|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}-E^{(\mathrm{s})}_{\min}\right\|
(where
E_{j}
and
E_{m}
are eigen energies of the full Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\sum_{l}\hat{H}^{(l)}
) implies

V_{{\boldsymbol{j}}{\boldsymbol{m}}}=0.

(72)

If we manage to show that, because
\Delta E
is the minimum of all such numbers (see eq. (
18
)), it must be that
k\left\|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}-E^{(\mathrm{s})}_{\min}\right\|\geq\Delta E
. Thus, the Corollary will follow from Theorem
1
.

Because
\left\|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}-E^{(\mathrm{s})}_{\min}\right\|
is the difference between the largest and the smallest eigenvalue of
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}
, it takes more than
k
sites to achieve energy difference
|E_{{\boldsymbol{j}}}-E_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}|
bigger than
k\left\|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}-E^{(\mathrm{s})}_{\min}\right\|
, meaning that at least
k+1
indices (elements) in
{\boldsymbol{j}}
and
{\boldsymbol{m}}
must differ. To prove that mathematically, for a contradiction we assume that there are
k^{\prime}\leq k
cells in which
{\boldsymbol{j}}
and
{\boldsymbol{m}}
are different, while
|E_{{\boldsymbol{j}}}-E_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}|>k\left\|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}-E^{(\mathrm{s})}_{\min}\right\|
. We have

\begin{split}|E_{\boldsymbol{j}}-E_{\boldsymbol{m}}|&=|\sum_{l=1}^{L}E_{j_{l}}^{(\mathrm{s})}-E_{m_{l}}^{(\mathrm{s})}|=|\sum_{l=1}^{k^{\prime}}E_{j_{\pi(l)}}^{(\mathrm{s})}-E_{m_{\pi(l)}}^{(\mathrm{s})}|\\
&\leq\sum_{l=1}^{k^{\prime}}|E_{j_{\pi(l)}}^{(\mathrm{s})}-E_{m_{\pi(l)}}^{(\mathrm{s})}|\leq k^{\prime}\left\|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}-E^{(\mathrm{s})}_{\min}\right\|,\end{split}

(73)

where
\pi(l)
is a relabeling of indices to include only those where
j_{l}
and
m_{l}
are different. This is in contradiction with our assumption. Thus, if
|E_{{\boldsymbol{j}}}-E_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}|>k\left\|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}-E^{(\mathrm{s})}_{\min}\right\|
, then vectors
{\boldsymbol{j}}
and
{\boldsymbol{m}}
have at least
k+1
different elements.

However, since vector
{\boldsymbol{i}}
in eq. (
71
) has at most
k
elements according to the assumption of the Corollary, but there are at least
k+1
elements of
{\boldsymbol{j}}
and
{\boldsymbol{m}}
that differ, according to that equation it must be that

V_{{\boldsymbol{i}},{\boldsymbol{j}}{\boldsymbol{m}}}=0.

(74)

This holds for every
{\boldsymbol{i}}
that make up
\hat{V}
, thus also

V_{{\boldsymbol{j}}{\boldsymbol{m}}}=0,

(75)

which, by explanation below eq. (
72
), proves the Corollary.

∎

Finally, we prove eq. (17) in the main text.

Corollary 2
.

Let us consider identical cells, which have initial Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\hat{H}^{(l)}
, where
\hat{H}^{(l)}=\hat{I}\otimes\cdots\otimes\hat{I}\otimes\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}\otimes\hat{I}\otimes\cdots\otimes\hat{I}
. Where we rewrite driving Hamiltonian
\hat{V}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{i}}\in K(L,k)}{\hat{V}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}}
as

\hat{V}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{i}}\in C(L,k)}{\hat{V}_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\hat{V}_{k}.

(76)

The following inequality holds:

|P(t)|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{L}k\left\|\hat{V}_{k}-v_{k,\min}\right\|\left\|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}-E^{(\mathrm{s})}_{\min}\right\|/2.

(77)

Proof.

By triangular inequality, the charging power is bounded as

|P(t)|\leq\left\|[\hat{H},\hat{V}]\right\|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|[\hat{H},\hat{V}_{k}]\right\|.

(78)

Using Corollary
1
, we obtain

\begin{split}&|P(t)|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|[\hat{H},\hat{V}_{k}]\right\|\\
&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{L}k\left\|\hat{V}_{k}-v_{k,\min}\right\|\left\|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}-E^{(\mathrm{s})}_{\min}\right\|/2.\end{split}

(79)

III
Random all-to-all Hamiltonian

In this section, we estimate the normalization factor in the SY-like Hamiltonian used as an example in the main text, and we provide an explanation of why in Fig 1. (b) the maximum decreases slightly with growing size of the system
L
.

Let us consider a battery made of a random,
2
-local,
all-to-all
driving Hamiltonian given by

\hat{V}=C\,\hat{V}_{0},

(80)

where

\hat{V}_{0}=\sum_{i<j}^{L}\sum_{\alpha=x,\,y,\,z}J_{ij}^{\alpha}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{\alpha}\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{\alpha}.

(81)

the couplings
J_{ij}^{\alpha}
are randomly extracted from a normal distribution and
C=\frac{2}{\left\|\hat{V}_{0}-v_{0,\min}\right\|}
is a normalization factor which ensures that
\left\|\hat{V}-v_{\min}\right\|=2
. We consider the initial Hamiltonian to be
\hat{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}h\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}
.

Since
C
depends on
J_{ij}^{\alpha}
, which are randomly extracted from a normal distribution, we see that
C
as well changes randomly in each simulation.
Our goal is to estimate the scaling of the quantity
\left\|\hat{V}_{0}-v_{0,\min}\right\|
with the system size,
L
.

To this end, we start by recalling that, for large
L
, the eigenvalues of
\hat{V}_{0}
,
v_{0,i}
, follow a Gaussian distribution.
In particular, they satisfy

\langle\sum_{i=1}^{2^{L}}{v_{0,i}}\rangle_{J}=\langle\mathrm{Tr}({\hat{V}_{0}})\rangle_{J}=0,

(82)

as well as

\begin{split}&\sigma^{2}2^{L}=:\langle\sum_{i=1}^{2^{L}}{v_{0,i}}^{2}\rangle_{J}=\langle\mathrm{Tr}({\hat{V}_{0}}^{2})\rangle_{J}\\
&=\langle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sum_{i<j}^{L}\sum_{\alpha=x,\,y,\,z}\sum_{i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}}^{L}\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}=x,\,y,\,z}J_{ij}^{\alpha}J_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{\alpha}\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{\alpha}\hat{\sigma}_{i^{\prime}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\hat{\sigma}_{j^{\prime}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\right)\rangle_{J},\end{split}

(83)

where
\langle\rangle_{J}
denotes ensemble averaging over
J_{ij}^{\alpha}
. Since trace of
\sigma^{\alpha}
is
0
, trace of elements in (
83
) are
0
, except in the case
i=i^{\prime}
,
j=j^{\prime}
and
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}
.
Using that the variance of
J_{ij}^{\alpha}
is
1
, we obtain

\sigma^{2}2^{L}=\langle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sum_{i<j}^{L}\sum_{\alpha=x,\,y,\,z}(J_{ij}^{\alpha})^{2}\mathds{1}\right)\rangle_{J}=\frac{3L(L-1)}{2}2^{L}.

(84)

Hence, the eigenvalues of
\hat{V}_{0}
,
v_{0,i}
, follow a Gaussian distribution having zero mean and variance given by
\sigma^{2}=\frac{3L(L-1)}{2}
.

As last step, we need to estimate the expectation value for the maximum among
N
numbers randomly extracted from a Gaussian distribution having vanishing mean value and variance equal to
\sigma
.
It can be shown,
Kamath 2015
, that such a maximum value goes like
\sigma\sqrt{2\ln{N}}
for large
N
.
As a result, using that, in our case,
N=2^{L}
the mean value of
\frac{\left\|\hat{V}_{0}-v_{0,\min}\right\|}{2}
averaged over
J_{ij}^{\alpha}
takes the form

\begin{split}&\langle\frac{\left\|\hat{V}_{0}-v_{0,\min}\right\|}{2}\rangle_{J}=\langle\frac{v_{0,\max}-v_{0,\min}}{2}\rangle_{J}=\langle v_{0,\max}\rangle_{J}\\
&=\sigma\sqrt{2\ln(2^{L})}=\sqrt{\frac{3L(L-1)}{2}2L\ln 2}=\sqrt{3L^{2}(L-1)\ln 2}\end{split}

(85)

Motivated by this result, in Fig.

16
and we have fitted the resulting values against an Ansatz of the form
\alpha\sqrt{L^{2}(L-1)}
, with
\alpha
being a fitting parameter.
As it is evident from the figure the agreement is excellent. Thus, from the analytical estimate, Eq. (
85
), tested in Fig.
3
, we conclude that the normalization factor scales as
C\propto\sqrt{L^{2}(L-1)}
.

Now we will explain why in Fig 1. (b) the maximum decreases slightly with growing size of the system
L
. Also the quantity
\left\|[\hat{H},\hat{V}]\right\|
is determined in terms
J_{ij}^{\alpha}
; therefore, it also changes in each realization (instance) of the simulation.
Fig.
4

\left\|[\hat{H},\hat{V}]\right\|
as a function of
L
. As
L

L

L

\left\|[\hat{H},\hat{V}]\right\|
reduces with
L

L
. Since
\left\|[\hat{H},\hat{V}]\right\|
is also an upper bound on maximum charging power
P_{\max}
, we expect a similar behavior also for this quantity.
