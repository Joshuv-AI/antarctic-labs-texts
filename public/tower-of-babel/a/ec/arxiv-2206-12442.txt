Abstract
. We introduce and study families of finite index subgroups of the modular group that generalize the congruence subgroups. Such groups, termed ϕ \phi -congruence subgroups, are obtained by reducing homomorphisms ϕ \phi from the modular group into a linear algebraic group modulo integers. In particular, we examine two families of examples, arising on the one hand from a map into a quasi-unipotent group, and on the other hand from maps into symplectic groups of degree four. In the quasi-unipotent case we also provide a detailed discussion of the corresponding modular forms, using the fact that the tower of curves in this case contains the tower of isogenies over the elliptic curve y 2 = x 3 − 1728 y^{2}=x^{3}-1728 defined by the commutator subgroup of the modular group.



1.
Introduction

The modular group
\Gamma=\SL_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
and its finite index subgroups play a fundamental role throughout mathematics and physics. The most well-known of these subgroups are the congruence subgroups, which have the property that membership in the group can be tested against a finite list of congruence conditions. Equivalently, a subgroup is congruence if it contains one of the principal congruence subgroups

\Gamma(N)=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mid\gamma\equiv 1\pmod{N}\}.

The quotients of the complex upper-half plane by these congruence subgroups define the modular curves, which classify elliptic curves decorated with additional structures related to torsion points on the curves. In this paper we study a generalization of this notion of congruence subgroup to families of noncongruence subgroups.

Most subgroups of
\Gamma
of finite index are not congruence subgroups. In recent years, it has been shown that they too define natural moduli spaces
[
9
]
, and their arithmetic has been shown to impinge on problems of classical interest. For example, Chen has shown
[
8
]
that the arithmetic of certain noncongruence moduli spaces is related to the description of Markoff triples
[
4
]
. Now that the unbounded denominators conjecture on noncongruence modular forms has been resolved
[
6
]
, one of the most interesting arithmetic open problems in this area, aside from the Grothendieck-Teichmuller conjecture
[
26
]
, is to determine the Eisenstein ideals
[
12
]
associated to noncongruence subgroups, which encode the unbounded denominators phenomenon. Determining the Eisenstein ideals arising from
\SL_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
-Teichmuller level structures would shed light on the work of
[
8
]
.

Future exploration of phenomena such as this could be aided by having at hand a healthy supply of interesting spaces and groups to study. Thinking along these lines, in this paper we generalize the congruence subgroups in a natural way. The notion depends on a group homomorphism

\phi\colon\Gamma\to G(\overline{\mathbf{Q}})

where
G
is some algebraic group defined over a number field. Given such data, there exists a number field
K/\mathbf{Q}
such that
\phi
takes values in
G(\mathcal{O}_{K}[1/M])
for some integer
M\geq 1
, where
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is the ring of integers in
K
. Therefore it makes sense to reduce this homomorphism
\phi
modulo all but finitely many prime ideals of
K
. The corresponding
principal
\phi
-congruence subgroups
of level
N
for
\gcd(M,N)=1
are defined as

\Gamma(\phi,N)=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mid\phi(\gamma)\equiv 1\pmod{N}\}.

A subgroup of
\Gamma
is said to be
\phi
-congruence
if it contains some principal
\phi
-congruence subgroup. These are all finite index subgroups of the modular group, and when
\phi
is the inclusion of
\SL_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
into
\SL_{2}(\mathbf{Q})
we recover the usual congruence subgroups. When
\phi
has Zariski dense image, these groups are related to the Malcev completion of
\Gamma
(see
[
16
]
for a nice discussion), where
\Gamma
is interpreted as the fundamental group of the moduli stack of elliptic curves.

Below we study in Section
2
basic properties of these
\phi
-congruence subgroups and, in particular, introduce some tools that allow us under suitable hypotheses to lift surjective maps
\Gamma\to G(\mathbf{F}_{p})
to continuous surjections
\widehat{\Gamma}\to G(\mathbf{Z}_{p})
, where
\widehat{\Gamma}
denotes the profinite completion of
\Gamma
and
\mathbf{Z}_{p}
denotes the
p
-adic integers.

In Section
3
we study a particular family of
\phi
-congruence subgroups arising from an upper-triangular but not decomposable representation of
\Gamma
of rank
2
. The modular curves that arise as
\phi
-congruence modular curves are precisely the curves that live beneath the abelian covers of the elliptic curve
y^{2}=x^{3}-1728
. These curves have quite a long history, going back at least to investigations of Poincaré, though there remain some open problems (for example, describing the modular curves and Eisenstein constants for the genus zero groups
G_{p}
introduced in Subsection
3.4
). We describe how to compute equations for the curves defined by
\phi
-congruence subgroups that lie between the commutator
\Gamma^{\prime}
and the double-commutator
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
, as well as give a detailed discussion of the corresponding modular forms and the Eisenstein constants. This material uses classical results on division polynomials associated to elliptic curves.

Finally, in Section
4
we use the moduli space of rank
4
irreducible representations of
\Gamma
described by Tuba-Wenzl
[
29
]
to identify some
\phi
-congruence subgroups related to
\Sp_{4}
. We were led to consider this case in view of the probabilistic results of
[
22
]
which show that it is rarer to find surjective maps from
\Gamma
onto
\Sp_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
than for other linear algebraic groups. We compute genus and dimension formulas for the corresponding modular curves and modular forms, though we say nothing about finding equations for these curves, which are very high degree covers of the moduli space of elliptic curves, nor do we say anything about the corresponding Eisenstein constants.

By combining our symplectic results with the general theory worked out in Section
2
, we are able to establish the following result:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
x\in\mathbf{Q}^{\times}\setminus(\mathbf{Q}^{\times})^{2}
, and let
\phi\colon\Gamma\to\Sp_{4}(\mathbf{Q})
be defined by the matrices

\displaystyle\phi\left(\begin{matrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right)=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}x&3x^{-1}&3x&x^{-1}\\
0&x^{-1}&2x&x^{-1}\\
0&0&x&x^{-1}\\
0&0&0&x^{-1}\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\phi\left(\begin{matrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{matrix}\right)=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}0&0&0&-x^{-1}\\
0&0&x&0\\
0&-x^{-1}&0&0\\
x&0&0&0\end{matrix}\right).

Let
p>7
be a prime such that
x
is a primitive root mod
p
, and let
\widehat{\Gamma}
denote the profinite completion of
\Gamma
. Then
\phi
gives rise to a continuous surjection

\widehat{\phi}\colon\widehat{\Gamma}\to\Sp_{4}(\mathbf{Z}_{p}).

Equivalently, the map
\phi
induces isomorphisms
\Gamma/\Gamma(\phi,p^{n})\cong\Sp_{4}(\mathbf{Z}/p^{n}\mathbf{Z})
for all
n\geq 0
.

See Section
4
for more technical details on this result, and for proof of the main statements in Theorem
1.1
. In particular, we warn the reader that the homomorphism
\phi
of Theorem
1.1
keeps a nonstandard symplectic form invariant, though the corresponding symplectic group is equivalent to that defined by more standard conventions. Briefly, the proof strategy of Theorem
1.1
has two key steps: we first establish the theorem mod
p
by showing that the image of
\phi
is not contained in any of the maximal subgroups of
\Sp_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
, using the explicit enumeration and description of these subgroups as detailed in
[
19
]
. Next we use the material from Section
2.2
to give a Hensel lifting type argument to deduce surjectivity mod prime powers from the prime case. The difficulty of executing this strategy more generally rests on the complicated nature of the maximal subgroup structure of classes of finite groups of Lie type, cf.
[
1
]
. To give the flavor of the difficulties that one encounters, even when considering symplectic groups of degree four over general finite fields, one must consider stabilizers of much more complicated symplectic spreads
[
2
]
than those that arise here over
\mathbf{F}_{p}
. For other groups the possibilities are presumably even more foreboding.

There remain many interesting open questions about
\phi
-congruence subgroups, aside from constructing nice examples. For example, how does the absolute Galois group
\Gal(\overline{\mathbf{Q}}/\mathbf{Q})
act on the corresponding curves and associated dessins d’enfants? If the prime
p
is fixed in Theorem
1.1
but
x
is varied, does this describe a single orbit under the action of the Galois group? In a slightly different direction, under what conditions do these families give rise to expander graphs as in the case of modular curves
[
23
]
? We hope to return to some of these questions in future work.

2.
Families of subgroups

2.1.
Definitions

Let
\Gamma=\SL_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
, let
G
be a finite group, and suppose given a homomorphism

\phi\colon\Gamma\to G.

The kernel of
\phi
is necessarily of finite index, and we are interested in two basic questions:

(a)

When is
\phi
surjective?

(b)

When is
\ker\phi
noncongruence?

Since most subgroups of finite index in
\Gamma
are noncongruence, the answer to question (b) is “almost always”, and in practice it can be tested easily using Wohlfahrt’s criterion. For example, one has the following well-known result whose proof demonstrates this principle:

Lemma 2.1
.

If
n\geq 5
and
\phi\colon\Gamma\to S_{n}
is surjective, then
\ker\phi
is noncongruence.

Proof.

Assume that
\Gamma(N)\subseteq\ker\phi
for some
N\geq 2
. This yields an isomorphism

\frac{\prod_{p^{r}\mid\mid N}\SL_{2}(\mathbf{Z}/p^{r}\mathbf{Z})}{K}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}S_{n}

where
K
is the image of
\ker\phi
in the quotient
\Gamma/\Gamma(N)\cong\prod_{p^{r}\mid\mid N}\SL_{2}(\mathbf{Z}/p^{r}\mathbf{Z})
. Since
S_{n}
has a unique nontrivial normal subgroup, we must have that
N=p^{r}
is a prime power. Let
H
be the kernel of the reduction map
\SL_{2}(\mathbf{Z}/p^{r}\mathbf{Z})\to\SL_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
and let
\phi\colon\SL_{2}(\mathbf{Z}/p^{r}\mathbf{Z})\to S_{n}
denote the surjective homomorphism obtained from our congruence hypothesis. Then
\phi(H)
is a normal subgroup of
S_{n}
, and so must be
\{1\}
,
A_{n}
or
S_{n}
. But
H
is a
p
-group, and so the only possibility is
\phi(H)=\{1\}
. Hence
H\subseteq K
and we obtain a surjection
\SL_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})\to S_{n}
, which is absurd, since
\PSL_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
and
A_{n}
belong to members of different families of finite simple groups.
∎

The answer to question (a) is more delicate as, for example, the maximal subgroup structure of
G
can be quite complicated, cf.
[
1
]
. Nevertheless, the results of papers such as
[
22
]
suggest again that question (a) should frequently have a positive answer. Our aim in this paper is to describe some methods for using global homomorphisms
\phi
into linear algebraic groups to identify and compute with families of finite-index subgroups of the modular group. We begin with some notation and basic properties.

Let
G
be a linear algebraic group defined over a number field and let
\phi\colon\Gamma\to G(\overline{\mathbf{Q}})
denote a homomorphism. Since
\Gamma
is finitely generated, there exists a number field
K/\mathbf{Q}
with ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
such that
\phi
takes values in
G(\mathcal{O}_{K}[1/M])
for some integer
M\geq 1
. That is, we have a map

\phi\colon\Gamma\to G(\mathcal{O}_{K}[1/M]).

It makes sense to reduce
\phi
mod integers
N
coprime to
M
, and the kernels of such maps are our analogues of principal congruence subgroups:

Definition 2.2
.

For
\phi
as above, we define the corresponding
principal
\phi
-congruence subgroups
of level
N\geq 1
for
\gcd(M,N)=1
as

\Gamma(\phi,N)=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mid\phi(\gamma)\equiv 1\pmod{N}\}.

Definition 2.3
.

More generally, a subgroup
\Lambda\subseteq\Gamma
is said to be a
\phi
-congruence subgroup
if there exists
N\geq 1
with
\Gamma(\phi,N)\subseteq\Lambda
.

Remark 2.4
.

Since isomorphism of representations corresponds to matrix conjugation, it is clear that the
\phi
-congruence subgroups of
\Gamma
only depend on
\phi
up to isomorphism.

Remark 2.5
.

Notice that
\Gamma(\phi,1)=\Gamma
for all representations
\phi
. Therefore one does not have, for example,
\Gamma(\phi,1)\cap\Gamma(N)=\Gamma(\phi,N)
.

Remark 2.6
.

Suppose that
\Lambda\subseteq\Gamma
is of finite index, and let
\rho
be a representation of
\Lambda
defined over
K
. One can define
\rho
-congruence subgroups
\Lambda(\rho,N)
analogously to above. However, by considering the block description of matrices defining
\Ind_{\Lambda}^{\Gamma}\rho
, one sees that

\Gamma(\Ind_{\Lambda}^{\Gamma}\rho,N)\subseteq\Lambda(\rho,N).

Therefore every
\rho
-congruence subgroup of
\Lambda
is an
\Ind_{\Lambda}^{\Gamma}\rho
-congruence subgroup of
\Gamma
and so, from this perspective, it suffices to consider
\phi
-congruence subgroups of the full modular group
\Gamma
as defined above.

Lemma 2.7
.

The following properties hold:

(1)

Each
\Gamma(\phi,N)
is a normal subgroup of finite index in
\Gamma
, and so each
\phi
-congruence subgroup of
\Gamma
is of finite index in
\Gamma
.

(2)

If
N_{1}\mid N_{2}
then
\Gamma(\phi,N_{2})\subseteq\Gamma(\phi,N_{1})
.

(3)

One has
\Gamma(\phi,N_{1})\cap\Gamma(\phi,N_{2})=\Gamma(\phi,\lcm(N_{1},N_{2}))
and

\bigcap_{\gcd(M,N)=1}\Gamma(\phi,N)=\{1\}.

(4)

Let
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
denote two representations of
\Gamma
defined over
K
. Then

\Gamma(\phi_{1},N)\cap\Gamma(\phi_{2},N)\subseteq\Gamma(\phi_{1}\otimes\phi_{2},N).

Proof.

Properties (2) and (3) follow directly from the definition, and the normality condition in (1) is clear since
\Gamma(\phi,N)
is the kernel of the composed homomorphism

\Gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi}}{{\longrightarrow}}G(\mathcal{O}_{K}[1/M])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\red_{N}}}{{\longrightarrow}}G(\mathcal{O}_{K}[1/M]/N\mathcal{O}_{K}[1/M]).

The ring
\mathcal{O}_{K}[1/M]/N\mathcal{O}_{K}[1/M]
is Artinian, and so
\red_{N}\circ\phi
takes values in a finite group. Therefore its kernel
\Gamma(\phi,N)
is finite index with

[\Gamma\colon\Gamma(\phi,N)]\leq\left\lvert G(\mathcal{O}_{K}[1/M]/N\mathcal{O}_{K}[1/M])\right\rvert.

Finally, (4) follows easily by considering the Kronecker products of matrices.
∎

Recall that the character group of
\Gamma
is cyclic of order
12
, generated by
\chi
, where
\chi((\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}))=e^{2\pi i/12}
. This representation is the character of the square of the Dedekind
\eta
-function.

Lemma 2.8
.

With
\chi
as above,

\Gamma(\chi^{r},N)=\begin{cases}\Gamma&N=1,\\
\ker\chi^{2r}&N=2,\\
\ker\chi^{r}&N>2.\end{cases}

Proof.

By definition

\Gamma(\chi^{r},N)=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mid\chi(\gamma)^{r}\equiv 1\pmod{N}\}.

Now,
\chi(\gamma)^{r}
is a twelfth root of unity. When
N=1
there is nothing to show. If
N=2
then
\gamma\in\Gamma(\chi^{r},2)
if and only if
\chi(\gamma)^{r}=\pm 1
, equivalently, if and only if
\chi(\gamma)^{2r}=1
. When
N>2
then
\gamma\in\Gamma(\chi^{r},N)
if and only if
\chi(\gamma)^{r}=1
, since
1
is the only twelfth root of unity congruent to
1
mod
N
in this case. This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

Since
\chi^{r}=\chi^{\otimes r}
, the preceding Lemma illustrates that the inclusion in part (4) of Lemma

Definition 2.10
.

The
\phi
-completion
of
\Gamma
is the inverse limit

\Gammahat_{\phi}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\varprojlim_{N}\frac{\Gamma}{\Gamma(\phi,N)}.

Suppose that
G
satisfies strong approximation. For each prime

, let

\widehat{\Gamma}\longrightarrow\Gammahat_{\phi}\longrightarrow\prod_{\gcd(\mathfrak{p},M)=1}G(\mathcal{O}_{K,\mathfrak{p}}),

where

. In the next subsection we introduce some basic tools, likely known to experts, that will be used in Section

Remark 2.11
.

All modular curves obtained from the
\phi
-congruence groups
\Gamma(\phi,M)
have moduli interpretations in the sense of
[
9
]
. One expects the finite groups underlying the Teichmuller level structures of these moduli interpretations to be closely related to the image of

2.2.
Lifting surjectivity mod prime powers

When considering families as above, it is natural to ask when the image of a map

\phi_{N}:\SL_{2}(\mathbf{Z})\rightarrow G(\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z})

is surjective. It is automatic, by the Chinese remainder theorem, that it suffices to check that
\phi_{p^{r}}
is surjective for each
p^{r}||N
.
In this section we show that typically, it would suffice to check each
\phi_{p}
where
p|N
.

For the remainder of this section we fix a reductive group
G
defined over
\mathcal{O}
,
the ring of integers of some number field, and a prime
\mathfrak{p}|p
of
\mathcal{O}
which is unramified.
We shall denote by
q=\left\lvert\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{p}\right\rvert
and
\mathbf{F}_{q}=\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{p}
.
We shall also fix a subgroup
L\subset G(\mathcal{O})
.

We will denote by
G_{r}=G(\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{p}^{r})
and for
s<r
by
G_{r,s}
the kernel of the map
G_{r}\rightarrow G_{s}
.
Similarly we shall denote by
L_{r}
the image of
L
in
G_{r}
and
L_{r,s}
the kernel of the map
L_{r}\rightarrow G_{s}
.

Lemma 2.12
.

For all but finitely many
p
we have for each
r\geq 1
that
G_{r+1,r}
is isomorphic to the Lie algebra,
\Lie(G_{\mathbf{F}_{q}})
, over
\mathbf{F}_{q}
and
the conjugation action of
G_{r+1}
on the normal subgroup
G_{r+1,r}
induces an action of
G_{1}
on
G_{r+1,r}
given by
the usual adjoint action on
\Lie(G_{\mathbf{F}_{q}})
.

Moreover, for almost all
p
, the map
G_{r+2,r}\rightarrow G_{r+2,r}
given by
x\mapsto x^{p}
induces an isomorphism of groups

G_{r+1,r}\rightarrow G_{r+2,r+1}
.

Consequently, if for
r_{0}\geq 1
we have
L_{r_{0}+1,r_{0}}=G_{r_{0}+1,r_{0}}
then for all
r>r_{0}
we have
L_{r,r_{0}}=G_{r,r_{0}}
.

Proof.

We recall that
\Lie(G)
can be identified with the kernel of the map

G(\mathcal{O}[\epsilon]/\epsilon^{2})\rightarrow G(\mathcal{O}[\epsilon]/\epsilon).

So with
G
embedded in
\GL_{n}
we may identify this with the
n
by
n
matrices
X
for which

1+\epsilon X\in G(\mathcal{O}[\epsilon]/\epsilon^{2}).

Similarly, we have that
G_{r+1,r}
is precisely the
n
by
n
integer matrices
X
for which

1+p^{r}X\in G(\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{p}^{r+1}).

The map
\mathcal{O}[\epsilon]/\epsilon^{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{p}^{r+1}
given by
\epsilon\mapsto p^{r}
then induces the map
\Lie(G)\otimes\mathbf{F}_{q}\hookrightarrow G_{r+1,r}
,
which is clearly equivariant for the action of conjugation by
G(\mathbf{F}_{q})
.

One can readily verify by case analysis that for almost all
p
the map is bijective for all semi-simple groups. In particular, in the applications in this paper we only require this for
\Sp_{4}
, and so this concludes the proof in that case.

Alternatively, for the general case, notice that for almost all
p
we have that the implicit function theorem allows us to lift elements of
G_{r+1,r}
to
G_{r+2,r}
, so that the map
G_{r+2,r}\rightarrow G_{r+2,r}
given by
x\mapsto x^{p}
, so:

(1+p^{r}X)^{p}=1+p^{r+1}X\pmod{p^{r+2}}

induces an injection
G_{r+1,r}\hookrightarrow G_{r+2,r+1}
which is seen to be a group homomorphism.
Whenever the implicit function theorem applies we have that
|G_{r+2,r+1}|=q^{{\rm dim}(G)}=|{\rm Lie}(G)\otimes\mathbf{F}_{q}|
.
If follows that the injection

\Lie(G)\otimes\mathbf{F}_{q}\rightarrow G_{r+1,r}\rightarrow G_{r+2,r+1}

is indeed an isomorphism, as are the maps
G_{r+1,r}\rightarrow G_{r+2,r+1}
.

It follows that if
L_{r_{0}+1,r_{0}}=G_{r_{0}+1,r_{0}}
then
L_{r+1,r}=G_{r+1,r}
for all
r\geq r_{0}
so that the groups
L_{r,r_{0}}
and
G_{r,r_{0}}
. have the same size, hence are isomorphic.
∎

Lemma 2.13
.

If the adjoint action of
G(\mathbf{F})
on the Lie algebra,
\Lie(G_{\mathbf{F}_{q}})
, is irreducible and
L_{2,1}\neq\{0\}
, then
L_{2,1}=G_{2,1}
and hence
L_{r,1}=G_{r,1}
for all
r>1
.

Proof.

This is a simple application of Burnside’s irreducibility criterion
[
17
, Thm. 3.10]
.
∎

Remark 2.14
.

Note that the adjoint action would typically be irreducible for simple groups, but not for instance for
\GL_{n}
, where the adjoint action has a summand being the trivial representation.

Lemma 2.15
.

Suppose
\ell
is the maximum nilpotency degree of an element of
\Lie(G)
.
If
p>2\ell
and
L_{1}
contains a nilpotent element of
G_{1}
, then
L_{2,1}\neq\{0\}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may suppose that the element
g\in L
is an element whose image in
L_{1}
is nilpotent.
Then
g
has the form
1+X
, will satisfy
g^{p}=1\pmod{p}

3.
A unipotent family

3.1.
Generalities on the commutator subgroup

Let
\Gamma=\SL_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
, and
K=\mathbf{Q}(\zeta)
where
\zeta
is a primitive twelfth root of unity, so that
\zeta^{4}-\zeta^{2}+1=0
. If
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
then
\mathbf{F}_{p^{2}}
contains all of the roots of
x^{4}-x^{2}+1
, so at times below we will restrict to such primes. On the other hand, we will sometimes want the image of
\phi_{p}
to not be contained in
\SL_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
, thus we’ll insist that
p\equiv 5\pmod{12}
. This ensures that
x^{4}-x^{2}+1
factors into two irreducible quadratics over
\mathbf{F}_{p}
.

Begin by defining a global representation
\phi\colon\Gamma\to\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
by setting:

\displaystyle\phi(T)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\zeta&0\\
0&\zeta^{-1}\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\phi(S)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}-\zeta^{3}&1\\
0&\zeta^{3}\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\phi(R)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}1-\zeta^{2}&\zeta-\zeta^{3}\\
0&\zeta^{2}\end{matrix}\right).

This representation is not irreducible, but it is also not reducible into a direct sum of two one-dimensional subrepresentations. Notice that
T^{12}\in\ker\phi
, so that this representation is certainly not injective!

Lemma 3.1
.

One has

\phi(\Gamma)=\left\{\left(\begin{matrix}u&uv\\
0&u^{-1}\end{matrix}\right)\mid u^{12}=1,~v\in\mathbf{Z}\zeta\oplus\mathbf{Z}\zeta^{3}\right\}.

Proof.

First observe the following identities:

\displaystyle\phi(T^{2}ST)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}1&\zeta\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\phi(T^{3}S)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}1&\zeta^{3}\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right).

This, combined with the description of
\phi(T)
, shows that the set on the right of the equality is contained inside
\phi(\Gamma)
. The reverse inclusion is obvious: notice that set on the right is a subgroup, containing
\phi(T)
and
\phi(S)
, which generate
\phi(\Gamma)
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
p\equiv 5\pmod{12}
. Then
\phi_{p}(\Gamma)
is the following subgroup of
\SL_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p^{2}})
of order
12p^{2}
,

\phi_{p}(\Gamma)=\left\{\left(\begin{matrix}u&v\\
0&u^{-1}\end{matrix}\right)\mid u\in\mathbf{F}_{p^{2}},~u^{12}=1,~v\in\mathbf{F}_{p^{2}}\right\}.

Proof.

This follows by reducing Lemma
3.1
mod
p
.
∎

Let
\Gamma^{\prime}=[\Gamma,\Gamma]
denote the commutator subgroup. For details on the group
\Gamma^{\prime}
used below without proof, the reader can consult
[
25
]
. If
U_{p}
is the unipotent radical in
\phi_{p}(\Gamma)
, then
\phi_{p}(\Gamma)/U_{p}\cong\mathbf{Z}/12\mathbf{Z}
whenever
p\equiv 5\pmod{12}
, and so
\phi
induces an isomorphism
\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}\cong\phi_{p}(\Gamma)/U_{p}
. It follows that if
p\equiv 5\pmod{12}
then
\phi_{p}^{-1}(U_{p})=\Gamma^{\prime}
.

It is known that
\Gamma^{\prime}
is free nonabelian of rank
2
generated by
A=[S,R]
and
B=[S,R^{2}]
, with the identity
[A,B^{-1}]=-T^{6}
. The group
\Gamma^{\prime}
defines a genus one curve via its action on the upper half plane. For each prime
p\equiv 5\pmod{12}
, the group
\ker\phi_{p}
is a normal subgroup of
\Gamma^{\prime}
with abelian quotient

\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\ker\phi_{p}}\cong\mathbf{F}_{p^{2}}.

Now consider
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}=[\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime}]
. Notice that
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\ker\phi_{p}\subseteq\Gamma^{\prime}
for all primes
p
, and thus in particular

\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\bigcap_{p\equiv 5\pmod{12}}\ker\phi_{p}\subseteq\Gamma^{\prime}.

If
U
denotes the unipotent matrices in
\phi(\Gamma)
, then likewise
\phi(\Gamma^{\prime})=U
. Since
U
is abelian, we have
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\ker\phi
.

Lemma 3.3
.

We have

\ker\phi=\bigcap_{p\equiv 5\pmod{12}}\ker\phi_{p}=\Gamma^{\prime\prime}.

Proof.

Since
\Gamma^{\prime}
is freely generated by
A
and
B
, which are of infinite order, it follows that

\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\Gamma^{\prime\prime}}\cong\mathbf{Z}^{2}.

If
p\equiv 5\pmod{12}
, then the intermediate identity

\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\ker\phi_{p}}\cong\mathbf{F}_{p^{2}}\cong(\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z})^{2}

is the reduction mod

map. It follows that
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}=\bigcap_{p\equiv 5\pmod{12}}\ker\phi_{p}
. Since we have seen that
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\ker\phi\subseteq\ker\phi_{p}
for all

, the lemma follows.
∎

Summarizing all of this, we have shown that if

L=\left\{\left(\begin{matrix}u&uv\\
0&u^{-1}\end{matrix}\right)\in\GL_{2}(\mathbf{C})\mid u^{12}=1,~v\in\mathbf{Z}\zeta\oplus\mathbf{Z}\zeta^{3}\right\},

then there is an exact sequence

(1)

1\to\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\to\Gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi}}{{\to}}L\to 1.

The next lemma illustrates that the notion of
\phi
-congruence subgroups is natural and useful.

Lemma 3.4
.

The discrete group

satisfies the congruence subgroup property. Therefore, the finite index subgroups

containing

are exactly the
\phi

Let

be a finite index subgroup, and let

, which is finite index in

since an intersection of finite index subgroups is of finite index. Now

for some

also contains

, so that it is a congruence subgroup. By the exact sequence (

\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
and

.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\chi
be a one-dimensional representation of
\Gamma^{\prime}
such that

Since

The following argument is adapted from an unpublished result of Geoff Mason, which may have been known earlier to experts.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
G\subseteq\Gamma
be normal, and assume that
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\subseteq G\subseteq\Gamma^{\prime}
. Then either
G=\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
or
G
is a
\phi
-congruence subgroup of
\Gamma
.

Proof.

One checks that

\displaystyle SAS^{-1}

\displaystyle=A,

\displaystyle SBS^{-1}

\displaystyle=B^{-1},

\displaystyle RAR^{-1}

\displaystyle=A^{-1}B,

\displaystyle RBR^{-1}

\displaystyle=A^{-1}.

In particular, the induced conjugation action of

and

on

is fixed-point free. We have

. In the first case

, while in the latter we have

of finite index in

, hence it is
\phi
-congruence by Lemma

. To rule out the case

would be given by multiplication by

acts trivially on

∎

3.2.

3.2.1.

To simplify discussions with modular forms in this section, we now let
\Gamma=\PSL_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
, which we maintain for the remainder of the section. Note that
\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}
is cyclic of order
6
. Recall the following facts about the modular curve

(1)

There are no elliptic points.

(2)

There is a unique cusp, which has width
6
.

(3)

The curve
X(\Gamma^{\prime})
has genus
1
.

(4)

The ring of modular forms is
M(\Gamma^{\prime})=\mathbf{C}[\eta^{4},E_{4},E_{6}]
.

For explanation of the terminology above, see
[
11
, Chapter 2]
.

Letting
x=E_{4}/\eta^{8}
and

we thus have that

so that the modular curve
X(\Gamma^{\prime})
is naturally the genus
1

Taking the cusp to be the distinguished point at infinity, it is seen to be an elliptic curve with complex multiplication by
\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]
.

3.2.2.
Étale Covers

If
\rho:Y\rightarrow X(\Gamma^{\prime})
is any isogeny of elliptic curves, then as the map
\rho
is étale, we must have
Y\cong X(G)
, for some finite-index subgroup
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\subset G\subset\Gamma^{\prime}
. Moreover,
Y
has no elliptic points and the cusps, which are the kernel of the isogeny, have width
6
. From the Weierstrass uniformization we see that such isogenies are naturally in bijection with lattices in
\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]
.

Conversely, if
G
is any finite index subgroup of
\Gamma^{\prime}
with
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\subset G\subset\Gamma^{\prime}

has no elliptic points and, because the image of
\Delta(6)^{-}
inside

is contained in

, all cusps have width
6
.
It follows both that
X(G)
is genus
1
, and the cover
X(G)\rightarrow X(\Gamma^{\prime})
is étale. In fact, it is simply an isogeny of elliptic curves.

We thus have a natural bijection between finite index lattices in
\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]
and finite index subgroups satisfying
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\subset G\subset\Gamma^{\prime}
.
The simplest part of this correspondence is

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\prime}(N)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\langle A^{N},B^{N},\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle N\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-3}].

We see that the index of

in

is

.

Remark 3.17
.

The preceding lower bounds on valuations in the two lemmas above are not tight in most cases.

Lemma 3.18
.

The polynomials

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{N}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12^{N^{2}-1}}\psi_{N}^{2}(12X),

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{N}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12^{N^{2}}}\phi_{N}(12X),

have integer coefficients. Moreover, if for
r\geq 1
we have
2^{r}\mid\mid N
, then
\frac{1}{2^{2r}}\hat{\psi}_{N}(X)^{2}
is integral and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{2r}}\hat{\psi}_{N}(X)^{2}

\displaystyle=X^{N^{2}-4}(X^{3}-1)\pmod{2},

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{N}(X)

\displaystyle=X^{N^{2}}\pmod{2}.

If for
r\geq 1
we have
3^{r}\mid\mid N
, then
\frac{1}{3^{2r}}\hat{\psi}_{N}(X)^{2}
is integral and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3^{2r}}\hat{\psi}_{N}(X)^{2}

\displaystyle=X^{2}(X-1)^{N^{2}-3}\pmod{3},

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{N}(X)

\displaystyle=(X-1)^{N^{2}}\pmod{3}.

Proof.

Performing the substitutions
x=12\hat{x}
and
y=24\sqrt{3}\hat{y}
yields the Weierstrass equation
y^{2}=x^{3}-1
. As this curve is isomorphic to our curve, and the division polynomials are uniquely determined up to scaling, we can compare constant terms to conclude that

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{N}(\hat{x})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12^{N^{2}-1}}\psi_{N}(12\hat{x})^{2},

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{N}(\hat{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12^{N^{2}}}\phi_{N}(12\hat{x}).

Next, we note that

\psi_{N}(X)^{2}=\psi_{p^{r}}(X)^{2M^{2}}\psi_{M}\left(\frac{\phi_{p^{r}}(X)}{\psi_{p^{r}}(X)^{2}}\right)^{2}

implies that in either case the divisibility claim we are trying to establish, as well as the claim about reduction for
\hat{\psi}_{N}
, follow from the analogous claims for
\hat{\psi}_{p^{r}}
and
\hat{\phi}_{p^{r}}
.

Similarly, using

\phi_{N}(X)=\psi_{p^{r}}(X)^{2M^{2}}\phi_{M}\left(\frac{\phi_{p^{r}}(X)}{\psi_{p^{r}}(X)^{2}}\right),

we see that the claims about the reduction reduce to those for the case of
\phi_{p^{r}}
, and using the divisibility claim about
\psi_{p^{r}}
.

To initiate a proof by induction, we note the following identities:

\displaystyle\hat{\psi_{2}}(x)^{2}

\displaystyle=4(x^{3}-1),

\displaystyle\hat{\phi_{2}}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{4}+2^{3}x,

\displaystyle\hat{\psi_{3}}(x)^{2}

\displaystyle=9(x^{8}-2^{3}x^{5}+2^{4}x^{2}),

\displaystyle\hat{\phi_{3}}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{9}+2^{5}\cdot 3x^{6}+2^{4}\cdot 3x^{3}-2^{6}.

The lemma thus holds in these cases.

For the inductive step, consider the recursive identity

\hat{\psi}_{p^{r}}(x)^{2}=\hat{\psi}_{p}(x)^{2p^{2r-2}}\hat{\psi}_{p^{r-1}}\left(\frac{\hat{\phi}_{p}(x)}{\hat{\psi}_{p}(x)^{2}}\right)^{2}

and divide both sides by
p^{2r}
. The claim concerning
\hat{\psi}
follows by induction.

Next, in considering the recursion

\phi_{p^{r}}(x)=\psi_{p^{r-1}}(x)^{2p^{2}}\phi_{p}\left(\frac{\phi_{p^{r-1}}(x)}{\psi_{p^{r-1}}(x)^{2}}\right),

we see that the claim concerning
\hat{\phi}
follows by induction. This concludes the proof of the Lemma.
∎

Proposition 3.19
.

Suppose
p>3
is an odd prime. For any
p\mid N
the
q
-series for
\tilde{x}
has unbounded denominators at

.

Proof.

Beginning as in Proposition

, we now set

\displaystyle\breve{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{N},

\displaystyle\breve{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{x}}{N^{2}}=\frac{q^{2}}{N^{2}}\tilde{x}=\breve{q}^{2}\tilde{x}

and note
\breve{x}=1\pmod{\breve{q}}
.
Next set

\displaystyle\breve{M}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2N^{2}-2}}\hat{M}(N^{2}X)

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{2N^{2}-2}}{N^{2N^{2}-2}}M\left(\tfrac{N^{2}}{q^{2}}X\right)

\displaystyle=\breve{q}^{2N^{2}-2}M\left(\frac{1}{\breve{q}^{2}}X\right)

\displaystyle=\breve{q}^{2N^{2}-2}\psi_{N}\left(\frac{1}{\breve{q}^{2}}X\right)^{2}E_{4}-N^{2}\breve{q}^{2N^{2}}\phi_{N}\left(\frac{1}{\breve{q}^{2}}X\right)\widehat{\eta^{8}}

Observe that
\breve{M}(X)\in\mathbf{Z}[\![\breve{q}]\!][X]
. Note also that, as a series in
\breve{q}

\displaystyle E_{4}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\eta^{8}}=1\pmod{N},

\displaystyle E_{4}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\eta^{8}}=\breve{x}=1\pmod{\breve{q}}.

Consequently:

\displaystyle\breve{M}(X)

\displaystyle=N^{2}X^{N^{2}-1}-N^{2}X^{N^{2}}\pmod{\breve{q}},

\displaystyle\breve{M}(X)

\displaystyle=\breve{q}^{2N^{2}-2}\psi_{N}\left(\frac{1}{\breve{q}^{2}}X\right)^{2}\pmod{\breve{N}}.

We note that if
\breve{x}
has unbounded denominators at
p
, then we are done, since then

must also have unbounded denominators at

We next consider the case that

However, since

3.3.1.

Suppose that the group

Remark 3.24
.

Proposition 3.25
.

Let

. Let
\chi
be any character of

and write

which has divisor

\sum_{[N]R=T}R-\sum_{[N]R=0}R.

.

For

is a basis of

and has the correct divisor.
∎

Proposition 3.26
.

Let

. Then every weight

.

Now we consider

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=1\\

\displaystyle p-\left(1+\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)\right)+\left(\frac{-3xy}{p}\right)\sum_{c=1}^{p}\left(\frac{y^{2}+c^{2}}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p-1-\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)-\left(\frac{-3xy}{p}\right)

\chi(T^{n})=\sum_{d\mid n}dc_{d}
. Performing Mobius inversion yields

c_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(n/d)\chi(T^{d}).

The following result is well-known, but we include the proof for completeness.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
c
denote the number of cusps, and let
N
denote the order of the mod
p
reduction of
\rho(T)
. Then

c=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{d\mid N}\varphi(N/d)\chi(T^{d}),

where
\varphi
denotes Euler’s totient function.

Proof.

Since
N
is the least common multiple of the cycle lengths of
\rho(T)
acting on
X
, and these cycle lengths are the widths of the cusps, we have
c=\sum_{n\mid N}c_{n}
and therefore

\displaystyle c=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\mid N}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(n/d)\chi(T^{d})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{d\mid N}\sum_{n\mid(N/d)}\frac{1}{dn}\mu(n)\chi(T^{d})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{d\mid N}\frac{1}{d}\chi(T^{d})\sum_{n\mid(N/d)}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{d\mid N}\varphi(N/d)\chi(T^{d})

∎

Thus, to compute the number of cusps, and therefore also the genus, it remains to understand the order of

mod

, as well as the character

. We start this by imposing constraints on

,

and

to be surjective, as follows.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose that

(1)

p>7
;

(2)

to a generator of
\mathbf{F}_{p}^{\times}
;

(3)

Then
\rho_{p}(T)
has order
p(p-1)
, and
\langle\rho_{p}(T),\rho_{p}(S)\rangle=\Sp_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{p})

Proof.

Let us begin by working over the function field
\mathbf{Q}(x)
, and write

,

\displaystyle t=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x&3x^{-1}&3x&x^{-1}\\

0&0&0&x^{-1}\end{smallmatrix}\right),

\displaystyle\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0&0&-x^{-1}\\

0&-x^{-1}&0&0\\

Let

and observe that

\det(P)=\left(\frac{3}{16}\right)\cdot x^{-4}\cdot(x-1)^{6}\cdot(x+1)^{6}\cdot(x^{2}-x+1)^{-2}\cdot(x^{2}+x+1)^{-2}\cdot(x^{2}+1)^{2}.

Therefore, since

and

\mathbf{F}_{p}^{\times}
, it follows that
\det(P)
is invertible mod
p
. Set

and observe that

\displaystyle t_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\frac{1}{x}&1&0&0\\
0&\frac{1}{x}&0&0\\
0&0&x&1\\
0&0&0&x\end{smallmatrix}\right).

This shows that the reduction of
t_{1}
mod

has order
p(p-1)
, and hence the same holds for its conjugate

.

Now, since
-1=\rho(S^{2})
, it will suffice to show that the image of our subgroup in
\PSp_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
is the entire group. Let
G\subseteq\PSp_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
be this image.

The maximal subgroups of
\PSp_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
were worked out by Mitchell – see Theorem 2.8 of
[
19
]
for a description. In the notation of that Theorem, we can ignore cases (a) and (b). For the remaining cases, notice that
\langle t\rangle\cap\langle sts^{-1}\rangle=\{\pm 1\}
. Therefore, inside of
\PSp_{4}
, both

and

generate subgroups of size

with trivial intersection. It follows that

has order satisfying
\left\lvert G\right\rvert\geq p^{2}(p-1)^{2}/4
. This is enough to eliminate the cases (i) and (j) just by order considerations alone.

Next, we consider cases (g) and (h), where the maximal subgroups have order
p(p-1)^{2}(p+1)
, and, respectively,
p(p-1)(p+1)^{2}
, and the
p
-Sylow subgroups have order

. We eliminate the possibility that

is a subgroup of one of these two maximal subgroups in two steps: first, we show that in each case there are at most
(p+1)

p
-Sylow subgroups, and second, we find
(p+2)
conjugate subgroups of order

inside

, and thus reach a contradiction.

For the first step, let

p
-Sylow subgroup; we may assume

. Then the number of

-Sylow subgroups

, and equals the index of the normalizer

inside the group. Note that
\langle t\rangle
, which has order

, centralizes

, and therefore normalizes it, so

divides the order

.

In case (g), the size of the maximal subgroup is

, so

is a divisor of

. Therefore,

or

, the group is isomorphic to a quotient of

-Sylow subgroup is the unipotent part

is a divisor of

or

.

would be a

0&0&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)

does not centralize

must have size

, and we have

is upper triangular, while

is lower triangular. So far, we have
(p+1)
distinct subgroups of

of order

.

Finally, for

, consider the matrices of the form
C_{d,k}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=t^{d(p-1)}st^{k(p-1)}s^{-1}t^{-d(p-1)}

, and each matrix in any

given above, we can explicitly check that the

-entry of this matrix is

. On the other hand, the

-entry of

is

. These matrices are therefore never equal, since

is a generator modulo

, and

. For the same reason,
sC_{1,1}s^{-1}
does not belong to

, since

is upper triangular. Therefore,
\langle sC_{1,1}s^{-1}\rangle
gives the
(p+2)
-nd distinct subgroup of order

inside

, and we have eliminated cases (g) and (h).

It remains to eliminate cases (c) through (f), and this can be done by a direct computation, since

is finitely generated by

and

. For (c), this amounts to verifying that
t
and
s
do not simultaneously stabilize a line and a hypersurface, which is easy to check. Therefore, all that remains is to treat cases (d), (e) and (f).

Case (d) concerns the stabilizer of a self-polar line in
\mathbf{P}^{3}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
, which corresponds to a two-dimensional Lagrangian subspace of
\mathbf{F}_{p}^{4}
. This is the case of the (conjugates of) the Siegel parabolic. It will thus suffice to verify that

and

do not act with simultaneous fixed points on

, with notation as in Section
4.2
. When

, already

acts without fixed points by Lemma

, so we may suppose that

. The fixed points of

and

are among the

and among the

. Those

fixed by

all have

and

, but
B(a,0)
is fixed by
R
if only if

and

. This leads to
3a^{2}-3a+1=0
, and coupled with

, one deduces that

has order

. This contradicts the fact that

is a primitive root in
\mathbf{F}_{p}
for

, thus no

is simultaneously fixed by
R
and
S
.

Next we verify that no

is simultaneously fixed by both

and
S
, where again we may assume that

. In order to be fixed by

, we must have

, and then we must have that

satisfies the equations:

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=-\frac{a}{3a^{2}+bc},

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=-\frac{cx^{2}}{3a^{2}+bc},

\displaystyle cx^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{b}{3a^{2}+bc}.

The first equation implies that either
a=0
or
3a^{2}+bc=-1
. If

then

and the last two equations simplify to

,

. Since

, there are four solutions to this to consider:

, there the signs need not match. It is easily checked that none of these four subspaces are stabilized by

.

It thus remains to treat the case when

and

, in which case we have

. This leads to considering

and

where

. It is easily seen that

is not fixed by

, while for the final cases we observe that

\rho(R)A(a,cx^{2},c)=A(a+c-1,-x^{2}(6a+3c-cx^{2}-2),x^{-2}(c-2))

Hence, to be fixed, we deduce that

and

. This contradicts the fact that

is primitive mod

, and so again we see that no

is simultaneously fixed by
R
and

. This verifies that

is not contained in any conjugate of a Siegel parabolic and eliminates case (d) from consideration.

Next we treat the case of (e), which is the stabilizer of a pair of skew polar lines in
\mathbf{P}^{3}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
. Such a pair corresponds to a pair of Lagrangian subspaces with trivial intersection. If
R
fixes such a pair then, since it is of order

, it fixes each Lagrangian subspace in the pair individually. Since we have already seen that

and

do not simultaneously fix a Lagrangian plane, to eliminate case (e), it suffices to show that

does not exchange any pair of Lagrangian planes with trivial intersection that are each fixed by

. Again, to ensure that

has fixed points, we may suppose that

.

In our analysis of the Siegel case (d), we saw that

only fixes subspaces

with

. Thus, we only need to consider pairs
\{A(a,b,c),A(d,e,f)\}
where

,

and

3a^{2}+bc+3d^{2}+ef-6ad-ce-bf\neq 0,

where this final condition ensures that

. Now, the conditions
\rho(S)A(a,b,c)=A(d,e,f)
,
\rho(R)A(a,b,c)=A(a,b,c)
, and

(along with the various nonvanishing conditions above) are algebraic equations, and one can show that they have no simultaneous solutions in a straightforward but tedious manner using the aid of a computer. Therefore, case (e) also does not arise.

It remains to treat case (f), which is the case of a regular spread. By
[
13
]
, this corresponds to the semidirect product
\PSL_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p^{2}}).\langle\sigma\rangle

is an order two automorphism. We will examine the orders of elements in this group; since

, we can safely work with
\SL_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p^{2}})
. By
[

, the elements in

with order divisible by

or

. However, the order of

is

. Since

is large enough,

is not contained in any subgroup of this group, and we have eliminated the last case. Therefore, the only possibility that remains is that
\langle\rho_{p}(T),\rho_{p}(S)\rangle=\Sp_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{p})

∎

p-1<d<\frac{p(p-1)}{2}\end{subarray}}(p+3)\varphi\left(\frac{p(p-1)}{d}\right)+\sum_{d\in\{\frac{p(p-1)}{2},\,p(p-1)\}}(p^{3}+p^{2}+p+1)\varphi\left(\frac{p(p-1)}{d}\right).

Rearranging the terms, using
\varphi(p)=\varphi(2p)=p-1
for odd

\sum_{d|n}\varphi(n/d)=n
, we get

\displaystyle p(p-1)c

\displaystyle=3\sum_{d|p(p-1)}\varphi\left(\frac{p(p-1)}{d}\right)+2(2p-2)(p-1)+p\sum_{d|(p-1)}\varphi\left(\frac{p-1}{d}\right)+2(p^{3}+p^{2}-2)

\displaystyle=3p(p-1)+4(p-1)^{2}+p(p-1)+2(p^{3}+p^{2}-2)

\displaystyle=p(p-1)(2p+12),

and the result follows.
∎

We can say more about the cusps in the following corollary.

Corollary 4.7
.

There are
3
cusps of width
1
,
4
cusps of width
\frac{p-1}{2}
, one cusp of width
p
, and
2p+4
cusps of width
\frac{p(p-1)}{2}
.

Proof.

We use the values
\chi(T^{d})
from the proof of Proposition
4.6
. First, we have
c_{1}=\chi(T)=3
.

Next, consider a divisor
d\mid\frac{p(p-1)}{2}
such that
1<d<\frac{p-1}{2}
. Then

c_{d}=\frac{1}{d}\left(\chi(T^{d})-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f|d\\
1\leq f<d\end{subarray}}fc_{f}\right)=\frac{1}{d}\left(3-3-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f|d\\
1<f<d\end{subarray}}fc_{f}\right)\geq 0,

therefore
c_{d}=0
.
This implies that

\displaystyle c_{\frac{p-1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{p-1}\left(\chi(T^{\frac{p-1}{2}})-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\mid\frac{p-1}{2}\\
1\leq f<\frac{p-1}{2}\end{subarray}}fc_{f}\right)=\frac{2}{p-1}\left(2p-2-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\mid\frac{p-1}{2}\\
1<f<\frac{p-1}{2}\end{subarray}}fc_{f}\right)=4.

Next, we have

c_{p}=\frac{1}{p}\left(\chi(T^{p})-c_{1}\right)=1.

On the other hand, suppose
d=p\cdot d^{\prime}
with
d^{\prime}
a divisor of
\frac{p-1}{2}
such that
1<d^{\prime}<\frac{p-1}{2}
. Then

c_{d}=\frac{1}{d}\left(\chi(T^{d})-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f|d\\
1\leq f<d\end{subarray}}fc_{f}\right)=\frac{1}{d}\left(p+3-3-p-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f|d\\
f\neq 1,p,d\end{subarray}}fc_{f}\right)\geq 0,

so again

.

The only other divisor left is
\frac{p(p-1)}{2}
itself, and

c_{\frac{p(p-1)}{2}}=2p+12-1-3-4=2p+4.

∎

Before we can calculate the genus of the curve corresponding to our noncongruence group, we need one last result on the number of elliptic points of order

is independent of

Proof.

We use the character

defined earlier. In particular,
\varepsilon_{3}=\chi(R)

Note that the minimal polynomial (in variable

in
\Sp_{4}(V)

\gamma^{2}\neq\pm 1
in
\mathbf{F}_{p}

\gamma
of
\mathbf{F}_{p}
or
\mathbf{F}_{p^{2}}
, a straightforward computation shows that the only conjugacy classes in
[

D_{21},D_{22},D_{23},D_{24}

.

Note that the minimal polynomials of classes
C_{1}(i)
,
C_{1}^{\prime}(i)
,
C_{3}(i)
and
C_{3}^{\prime}(i)
have the general form

u^{3}-\frac{\gamma^{2i}\pm\gamma^{i}+1}{\gamma}u^{2}\pm\frac{\gamma^{2i}\pm\gamma^{i}+1}{\gamma}\mp 1,

where

\mathbf{F}_{p^{2}}

\mathbf{F}_{p}

Finally, let us conclude this discussion by confirming that the groups considered above are indeed noncongruence.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
p
,
x
and
y
satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
4.3
. Let
H_{p}\subseteq\Gamma
denote the inverse image under
\phi_{p}
of a point stabilizer for the action of
\Sp_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
on the symplectic Grassmanian
X(\mathbf{F}_{p})
. Then
H_{p}
is a noncongruence subgroup.

Proof.

We have seen that since
\phi_{p}(T)
has order
p(p-1)
, due to our hypotheses, the Wohlfahrt level is
p(p-1)
. Thus, if
H_{p}
is congruence, we must have
\Gamma(p(p-1))\subseteq H_{p}
. Since
\ker\phi_{p}
is the intersection of the conjugates of
H_{p}
, this would imply that
\Gamma(p(p-1))\subseteq\ker\phi_{p}
. But this is a contradiction as one sees by considering the indices of
\Gamma(p(p-1))
and
\ker\phi_{p}
, which grow on the order of
p^{6}
and
p^{10}
, respectively.
∎

Remark 4.12
.

At this time we do not know how these groups behave as one fixes the prime
p
but varies the primitive root
x

acting via outer automorphisms on the finite-index subgroups of
