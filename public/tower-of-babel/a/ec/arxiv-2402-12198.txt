Zero shot VLMs for hate meme detection: Are we there yet?
Naquee Rizwan, Paramananda Bhaskar, Mithun Das, Swadhin Satyaprakash Majhi

Abstract
Multimedia content on social media is rapidly evolving, with memes gaining prominence as a distinctive form. Unfortunately, some malicious users exploit memes to target individuals or vulnerable communities, making it imperative to identify and address such instances of hateful memes. Extensive research has been conducted to address this issue by developing hate meme detection models. However, a notable limitation of traditional machine/deep learning models is the requirement for labeled datasets for accurate classification. Recently, the research community has witnessed the emergence of several visual language models that have exhibited outstanding performance across various tasks. In this study, we aim to investigate the efficacy of these visual language models in handling intricate tasks such as hate meme detection. We use various prompt settings to focus on zero-shot classification of hateful/harmful memes. Through our analysis, we observe that large VLMs are still vulnerable in zero-shot hate meme detection. Warning: Contains potentially offensive memes.



1
Introduction

Several
large
visual language models (VLMs) have recently become available to the public. These models exhibit impressive performance across various tasks, including sentiment analysis
Kheiri and Karimi 2023
, visual question answering
Lan et al. 2023
, and so on. However, the existing literature falls short in addressing how VLMs perform precisely in the context of hate meme detection
Van and Wu 2023
and particularly in a zero-shot setting. The urgency for such systems stem from the exponential growth in multi-modal content on social media platforms with malevolent individuals severely exploiting memes as a tool to target various communities and propagate hate
Gomez et al. 2020
. While manual moderation is impossible, traditional machine learning models can also be not extensively trained for automatic moderation due to the severe lack of labeled hate meme datasets that are diverse in terms of language, target groups and social setting. This gap in research underscores the need to explore and evaluate the effectiveness of zero-shot VLMs for identifying and mitigating the spread of harmful content in memes. Note that the zero-shot setting is important here as creating annotated hateful meme datasets has various obstacles as outlined above.

In this paper, for the first time, we employ various types of prompts and input instructions to assess the ‘power’ of well-known VLMs, including
IDEFICS

Laurençon et al. 2023
,
LLaVA-1.5

Liu et al. 2023
, and
InstructBLIP

Dai et al. 2023
in detecting hateful memes in a fully zero-shot setting. We evaluate the outputs of these models for four well-known datasets covering hateful, misogynistic, and harmful memes. One of the central contributions of this paper is the proposal of a wide array of prompt variations that we use to query these models to understand their strengths and vulnerabilities. In total we investigate as many as
48
prompts (8 prompt variations across 6 model setups) for a given dataset. These prompts can be broadly categorized into the following types based on the input and output patterns: input variants can comprise (a) vanilla input, (b) input along with the definition of what is hateful/harmful, (c) input along with OCR text (d) input along with definition and OCR text; output variants can be (a) vanilla output or (b) output along with an explanation. We observe that prompt variants that are most successful in eliciting correct responses vary across models and datasets; nevertheless, in many of the cases OCR text alone or OCR text with definition works well. Overall, we conclusively establish that VLMs are still quite vulnerable in zero-shot hateful meme classification even across multiple prompting variants. As a following objective, we collect all the misclassifed hate memes and cluster them to induce a
typology of cases
where the models are most vulnerable and guardrails need to implemented for enhanced robustness.

Models

We ran our experiments on a total of
six
different model setups. All models are open source large VLMs. Due to resource constraints, we ran most of our experiments using
quantization

Liu et al. 2021
.
We have used two most commonly used quantization cases which are
float32 -> float16
and
float32 -> int8
.

IDEFICS
:
IDEFICS

Laurençon et al. 2023
which closely follows the architecture of Flamingo, is trained on open source datasets like
OBELICS
and
LAION
. It combines two frozen uni-modal backbones which are, LLaMA as the language model and OpenClip as the vision encoder.
We used instruction fine-tuned IDEFICS-9B model with the checkpoint
2
2

2

https://huggingface.co/HuggingFaceM4/idefics-9b-instruct
for our experiments. We ran these models in 4-bit and 8-bit quantized experimental setups.

LLaVA-1.5
:
LLaVA-1.5

Liu et al. 2023
is an enhanced version of LLaVA. It combines LLaMA / Vicuna as the language model and CLIP as the vision encoder. Compared to LLaVA, it has enhanced capabilities due to the addition of an MLP vision-language connector and integration of academic task-oriented data which further enhances its performance and effectiveness. We have used the LLaVA-1.5-7B model with checkpoint
liuhaotian/llava-v1.5-7b
and the LLaVA-1.5-13B model with checkpoint
llava-hf/llava-1.5-13b-hf
. The 7B model was used without quantization, while 13B model was used with 16 bit quantization.

InstructBLIP
:
InstructBLIP

Dai et al. 2023
is an instruction fine-tuned model that uses the same architecture as BLIP-2 with a small but significant difference. It uses frozen Flan-T5 / Vicuna as the language model and a visual transformer as the image encoder. Extending BLIP-2,
InstructBLIP
proposes an instruction-aware Q-Former module. As additional inputs, the model takes instruction text tokens which interacts with the query embeddings via the self-attention layer of the Q-Former.
We have used two different
InstructBLIP
models with Vicuna-7B and Flan-T5-xl as backbone language models. The checkpoints of these models are
Salesforce/instructblip-vicuna-7b
and
Salesforce/instructblip-flan-t5 -xl
respectively. We ran these models in 8-bit quantized experimental setup.

5
Prompts

This section presents the array of prompt variants employed in our work. A concise summary of representative examples for the prompt variants is provided in Appendix
A
, while detailed information for each variant is discussed in the subsections below.

Input patterns
: We run our experiments on four different input patterns, which are as follows.

Vanilla input
: Following
Roy et al. 2023
, we use a prompt template to instruct the model to classify the given meme into a label from a predefined
list_of_labels
. However, in our scenario, the
list_of_labels
is only restricted to binary labels. In addition, we supply two
example_outputs
(one label per line for positive and negative samples) to assist the models in generating appropriate answers. In our case, ‘positive’ denotes content deemed hateful, misogynistic, or harmful based on the dataset passed to the model.

(+)
Definition input
: In the vanilla prompts, we assumed that VLMs are to some extent aware of the labels for classifying the input image. In this setup, we take a step further and add the
definition
of the labels as an additional context to the VLMs. Our intuition was similar to
Roy et al. 2023
, i.e., the
definition
can help the VLMs understand the classification tasks better. These definitions are picked from the corresponding datasets and one line of
definition
was added for all
list_of_labels
which in our case is limited to positive and negative samples only. We provide these definitions of these labels for each dataset in the Appendix
B
.

(+)
OCR input
: In a meme, multi-modality, i.e., embedded text and image play very crucial role in the classification task, similar to the works
Pramanick et al. 2021a
;
Das and Mukherjee 2023
. We therefore add
ocr_extracted_text
in the vanilla prompt. Our intuition was that the models would further be better in understanding the contexts with this addition and would be more successful in classifying the input image meme as per the
list_of_labels
. We provide the
ocr_extracted_text
enclosed within three back-ticks for the model to easily distinguish it from other texts in the prompt.

(+)
Definition & OCR input
: Here, we combine both
definition
and
ocr_extracted_text
with vanilla prompt and pass it as input prompt for our experiment. We use all intuitions discussed above in previous prompt variants and assume that this prompt would provide the models with deeper contexts for the classification task. Moreover, in this setup the order of the prompt text is the
definition
followed by the
ocr_extracted_text
.

Output patterns
: We run our experiment on two different output patterns which are noted below.

Vanilla output
: In this case, we prompt the model to generate as output only the correct class label from the
list_of_labels
corresponding to different datasets as mentioned in Table
1
.

(+)
Explanation output
: Adding to the above case of vanilla output, we prompt the model to further explain the raionale (within 30 words) based on which it made a prediction.

Thus we run a total of
eight
prompts for each dataset and for each model setup by running four different input patterns
\times
two different output patterns. In the
Results
section, we show the performance of the different models across the datasets for
eight
possible
\langle
input, output
\rangle
prompt pattern combinations.

Experimental setup

For all the models, we use a batch size of 1. We manually tune the temperature values and set them to 1.0 for the
IDEFICS
and
LLaVA-1.5
13B models, 0.7 for
LLaVA-1.5
7B, and 0.8 for the
InstructBLIP
model. The temperature parameter controls how random the generated output would be. However, with lower temperatures, we observed inferior performance of these models. As noted earlier, we experiment with eight different prompts on four datasets, studying them across six model setups. In short, we run
48
prompts per dataset and
192
prompts across all four datasets. All the models are coded in Python using the PyTorch library. We utilize 2xT4 GPUs from Kaggle, providing a total of 15GB memory on each GPU with a usage limit of 30hrs/week. We present the detailed results in the following section.

Error analysis

In this section, we consider the
four
best (model setup, prompt variant) combinations, one for each dataset and identify the cases of misclassification. We cluster the corresponding memes and identify leading topics in those clusters to build a typology of the error cases. This typology can directly feed back into the VLMs for better implementation of guardrails.

Cases of misclassification
: In the case of FHM dataset, we observe that misclassification into the hateful class (107) was more compared to misclassification into the not-hateful class (86). Opposite to this, for the MAMI we observe heavy misclassification into not-misogynistic memes (220) compared to the reverse case (118). These big differences in the nature of misclassifications in FHM and MAMI datasets despite the same model setup can be attributed to the prompt variants that lead to the best results (OCR + definition in case of MAMI as opposed to definition in case of FHM).

However, for HARM-C (70 misclassified as harmful and 56 misclassified as not-harmful) and HARM-P (54 misclassified as harmful and 76 misclassified as not-harmful) the differences are not as high as compared to previously discussed datasets having same model setups. This can be since both the datasets performed their best with OCR as input which seems to minimize the gap of misclassification trends between them.

X

Typology induction
: In order to make further sense from the obtained clusters we run the multi-modal BERTopic model within each cluster separately. We consider only those clusters that have a size larger than 15 for topic induction so that the results are meaningful. In Table
8
we present the results of this approach that neatly organizes the misclassified memes into a typology. Some of the key observations are as follows.

FHM
: If the image or text contains ‘Trump’, Arabic words, ‘Hitler’, unclear black and white images, Jews or hands around a dog, the model is biased towards forcefully classifying the meme as hateful. On the other hand, if in memes, people are portrayed decently, the model gives more importance to image over embedded text and misclassifies the memes as not-hateful. For instance, we see in certain topic clusters, women are being addressed as sandwich makers but still the model misclassifies them as not-hateful.

MAMI
: If the memes are in black and white shades or people are portrayed decently, the model gives less attention to the embedded text and misclassifies it as not-misogynistic. Most of the images in these cases have women in it. However, there are some outliers also visible in these image clusters and neither from text nor from image it is possible to infer why the model misclassified the meme as not-misogynistic. In misclassification to misogynistic memes, it is observed that these memes mostly do not contain face/body parts of women. If some hampered face of women are also present, the model misclassifies it to be misogynistic.

HARM-C
: In this dataset, we observe from image clusters that presence of Trump in the meme biases the model towards classifying the meme as harmful. When focusing on the text clusters, we can infer that the presence of text like ‘Biden’ makes the model biased towards predicting not-harmful. Similarly, presence of topics like mask and wear biases the model towards harmful prediction.

HARM-P
: In this dataset, it is clearly visible that presence of Joe Biden and Barack Obama either in the image cluster or text cluster tends the model to get biased toward being not-harmful. However, no common pattern is observed as to why the model misclassifies certain memes as harmful.

Also, all models across all datasets seem to be confused for memes which have different images stacked on one another. The models seem to consider different image splits to be occurring in the same environment and therefore gets confused in their prediction.

9
Conclusion

We present a comprehensive study of popular VLMs for hateful meme detection, considering eight different prompt variations. For this study, we utilize four datasets covering various hate dimensions. We observe that model performance varies based on datasets and prompts used. Furthermore, results are not consistent across all model setups. Our in-depth analysis reveals that models sometimes classify a meme as a joke or humorous, thereby not categorizing it as hateful or harmful. We believe our detailed observations can aid in improving the performance of models for detecting hate memes.

10
Limitations

Our work has a few limitations. First, we conducted our experiments on English meme datasets and did not assess the model’s capability for multilingual hate meme detection. Second, although we experimented with various prompt settings to identify misclassification patterns, these prompt variants are not exhaustive, and numerous other variants could be explored. Despite this, we are confident that our range of prompts can unveil the actual performance of VLMs in hate meme detection as they cover various broad meta-aspects. Third, we did not use hate meme datasets tailored explicitly for this task by finetuning the VLMs. In future, we plan to address these limitations.

11
Ethical statement

Our analysis refrains from attempting to trace users involved in disseminating hate, and we do not intend to harm any individuals or target communities. All experiments were thoroughly conducted using datasets crafted from prior research. Our primary focus was to assess the efficacy of large VLMs in hate meme detection, aiming to pinpoint potential areas for future enhancement.

Appendix B
Definitions

B.1
FHM dataset

•

hateful
: A direct or indirect attack on people based on characteristics, including ethnicity, race, nationality, immigration status, religion, caste, sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, and disability or disease. Attack is defined as violent or dehumanizing (comparing people to non-human things, e.g., animals) speech, statements of inferiority, and calls for exclusion or segregation. Mocking hate crime is also considered hateful.

•

not-hateful
: A meme which is not hateful and follows social norms.

B.2
MAMI dataset

•

misogynistic
: A meme is misogynous if it conceptually describes an offensive, sexist or hateful scene (weak or strong, implicitly or explicitly) having as target a woman or a group of women. Misogyny can be expressed in the form of shaming, stereotype, objectification and/or violence.

•

not-misogynistic
: A meme that does not express any form of hate against women.

B.3
HARM-C and HARM-P datasets

•

harmful
: Multi-modal units consisting of an image and a piece of text embedded that has the potential to cause harm to an individual, an organization, a community, or the society more generally. Here, harm includes mental abuse, defamation, psycho-physiological injury, proprietary damage, emotional disturbance, and compensated public image.

•

not-harmful
: Multi-modal units consisting of an image and a piece of text embedded which does not cause any harm to an individual, an organization, a community, or the society more generally.
