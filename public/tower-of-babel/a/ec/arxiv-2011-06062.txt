Roosevelt 50 - B-1050 Bruxelles, Belgium Email: mhallin@ulb.ac.be (b) Research Center for Statistics, University of Geneva Boulevard du Pont d’Arve 40 - CH-1211 Geneva, Switzerland Email: davide.lavecchia@unige.ch (c) Department of Mathematics and Statistics, Lancaster University LA1 4YF Lancaster, UK Email: h.liu11@lancaster.ac.uk

Abstract
We develop a class of tests for semiparametric vector autoregressive (VAR) models with unspecified innovation densities, based on the recent measure-transportation-based concepts of multivariate center-outward ranks and signs . We show that these concepts, combined with Le Cam’s asymptotic theory of statistical experiments, yield novel testing procedures, which (a) are valid under a broad class of innovation densities (possibly non-elliptical, skewed, and/or with infinite moments), (b) are optimal (locally asymptotically maximin or most stringent) at selected ones, and (c) are robust against additive outliers. In order to do so, we establish a Hájek asymptotic representation result, of independent interest, for a general class of center-outward rank-based serial statistics. As an illustration, we consider the problems of testing the absence of serial correlation in multiple-output and possibly non-linear regression (an extension of the classical Durbin-Watson problem) and the sequential identification of the order p p of a vector autoregressive (VAR( p p )) model. A Monte Carlo comparative study of our tests and their routinely-applied Gaussian competitors demonstrates the benefits (in terms of size, power, and robustness) of our methodology; these benefits are particularly significant in the presence of asymmetric and leptokurtic innovation densities. A real data application concludes the paper.



Keywords:

Multivariate ranks, Distribution-freeness, Hájek representation, Local asymptotic normality, Durbin-Watson, VAR order identification.

Introduction

Despite the overwhelming empirical evidence, in most real-life datasets, of non-Gaussian and non-symmetric (non-elliptical) distributions, multivariate time series analysis remains very deeply marked by explicit or implicit Gaussian assumptions: correlogram-based and spectral methods, pseudo-Gaussian tests, Gaussian quasi-likelihoods, and their possible robustifications, are ubiquitous in methodological developments as well as in daily practice.

(hence, without any loss of generality, in the covariates). Hallin and Paindaveine moreover assume that the innovation density
f
is elliptical and consider test procedures based on Mahalanobis ranks and signs. This very restrictive assumption of elliptical symmetry is precisely what we are dropping here as we only assume that
f\in{\cal F}_{d}^{*}
.

7.1
A multiple-output rank-based Durbin-Watson test

7.1.1
Testing for serial correlation in (nonlinear) multiple-output regression

A classical problem in single-output linear regression is the so-called Durbin-Watson problem of testing the null hypothesis under which
{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}_{t}
, in the single-output linear version of (
7.1
) with unspecified
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, is second-order white noise against the alternative of VAR(1) dependence: see
Durbin and Watson, 1950
;
Durbin and Watson, 1951
. Here, we extend that problem to nonlinear multiple-output regression and propose a rank-based solution. For the sake of simplicity, we limit ourselves to VAR(1) alternatives (writing
A
instead of
{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}_{1}
), but extensions to higher-order VAR(
p
) are straightforward.

It is easy to see that, under Assumption
1
and appropriate conditions on
g
and the asymptotic behavior of the covariates, model (
7.1
) is LAN with block-diagonal information matrix (a nonserial
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
-block and a serial one related with
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}=\text{vec}{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}
). LAN then implies that the unknown
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
safely can be replaced by any root-
n
consistent (and, in principle, locally discrete) estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(n)}
without having any local impact on the
\theta
-part of the central sequence (see
Hallin and Paindaveine, 2004b
for the case of a linear
g
; the only difference is that the
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
part of the central sequence here has an additional
g^{\prime}
factor which does not affect that fact that its covariance with the
\theta
part vanishes). As a consequence, one can construct a test as of
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
were known, then safely replace
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
with
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(n)}
in the resulting test statistic.

The traditional implementation of Durbin-Watson tests involves least-squares estimators of
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
. We rather suggest robust root-
n
consistent M-estimators
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(n)}
such as the bounded-influence M-estimator for dynamic location models proposed in
Mancini et al., 2005
.

7.2
VAR order identification

Turning to VAR order identification, let us consider the bivariate VAR(1) model with autoregression matrix coefficient
\text{vec}({\mbox{\boldmath$A$}})=(0.30,-0.06,0.12,0.24)^{\prime}
, the same innovation densities and the same contamination schemes as in Section

, and sample size
n=800
. The sequential method of Sections
5.2
and
6.2
was applied to

Each step consists in testing (at 5% nominal level) a VAR(
p
) against a VAR(
p+1

p
) model; throughout, this estimation is performed via the center-outward R-estimator based on vdW scores as proposed by
Hallin et al., 2019
, where it is shown to be root-
n

The numbers of under-, correct, and over-identification for the Gaussian, (corrected) vdW, (corrected) Spearman, and (corrected) sign tests are reported in Table
2
.

All tests are rejecting the hypothesis of white noise, irrespective of the underlying innovation density or the presence of outliers: no under-identifications, thus. Also, the rank-based procedures yield uniformly lower over-identification rates than the Gaussian one. Under the
t_{3}
distribution, the Gaussian procedure yields an almost 10% over-identification rate, indicating that its performance is badly affected by heavy tails. The rank-based procedures, on the other hand, do much better under heavy tails, skewness, and additive outliers.

8
Conclusion

This paper introduces rank-based tests for VAR models with unspecified innovation density. Based on the residual center-outward ranks and signs recently proposed by
Chernozhukov et al., 2017
and
(
Hallin et al., 2020a
, where they are shown to be essentially maximal ancillary)
, they constitute the testing counterpart of the R-estimators proposed in
Hallin et al., 2019
. When testing a VAR with specified parameter
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}
, they are fully distribution-free; when
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}
remains unspecified under the null, they are
strongly
asymptotically distribution-free in the sense of being asymptotically equal to a fully-distribution-free test. When based on appropriate score functions, they achieve parametric efficiency at selected reference densities. Monte Carlo experiments and an empirical example demonstrate the excellent performance of the proposed tests, which significantly outperform the traditional pseudo-Gaussian methods under skew, heavy-tailed, and contaminated innovations.

{\rm E}\left\|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1;{\rm a}}\right\|^{2}={\rm E}\left(\left\|{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}_{1}({\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}_{\pm,2})\right\|^{2}\right){\rm E}\left(\left\|{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}_{2}({\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}_{\pm,1})\right\|^{2}\right)<\infty.

Hence,
{\rm E}\left\|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1;{\rm a}}^{(n)}\right\|^{2}-{\rm E}\left\|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1;{\rm a}}\right\|^{2}=o(1)
. It follows (see, e.g., part
(iv)
of Theorem 5.7 in Chapter 3 of
Shorack, 2000
) that

{\rm E}\left\|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1;{\rm a}}^{(n)}-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}\right\|^{2}=o(1).

Turning to the second part of (
3.5
), put

\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}(\omega)=\text{vec}({\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}_{1}({\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}_{\pm,2}(\omega)){\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}_{2}^{\prime}({\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}_{\pm,1}(\omega))),\ \mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1;{\rm a}}^{(n)}(\omega)=\text{vec}({\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{\rm a}({\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,2}(\omega),{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,1}(\omega)))

for any point
\omega
in the sample space
\Omega
.
Since
\|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1;{\rm a}}^{(n)}-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}\|
converges to zero a.s., it follows from the Egorov theorem (see, e.g., part
(ii)
of Exercise 5.8 in
Shorack, 2000
) that, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a subset
{\mathcal{A}}\subset\Omega
such that

{\rm P}({\mathcal{A}})>1-\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\underset{\omega\in\mathcal{A}}{\sup}\left\|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1;{\rm a}}^{(n)}(\omega)-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}(\omega)\right\|=o(1).

(B1)

Denoting by
{\mathcal{A}}^{c}
the complement of
{\mathcal{A}}
in
\Omega
, we have

\displaystyle{\rm E}\left\|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1;{\rm e}}^{(n)}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle={\rm E}\left\|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}({\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}+{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{c}})-{\rm E}[\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}({\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}+{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{c}})|{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,2},{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,1}]\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3{\rm E}\left\|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}-{\rm E}[\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}|{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,2},{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,1}]\right\|^{2}+3{\rm E}\left\|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{c}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+3{\rm E}\|{\rm E}[\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{c}}|{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,2},{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,1}]\|^{2}

\displaystyle=:3(I_{1}^{(n)}+I_{2}^{(n)}+I_{3}^{(n)}).

By Jensen’s inequality,

I_{3}^{(n)}\leq{\rm E}\left({\rm E}[\|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{c}}\|^{2}|{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,2},{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,1}]\right)={\rm E}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{c}}\|^{2}=I_{2}^{(n)}

where, due to the square-integrability of
{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}_{1}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}_{2}
and the independence between
{\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}}_{1}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}}_{2}
,
I_{2}^{(n)}
is arbitrarily small as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.

It remains to prove that
I_{1}^{(n)}=o(1)
. Denoting by
\mathfrak{G}^{(n)}
the regular grid the empirical center-outward distribution function is mapping to, define, for any
\mathfrak{g}_{1},\mathfrak{g}_{2}\in\mathfrak{G}^{(n)}
,

{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}:=\{\omega:{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,1}(\omega)=\mathfrak{g}_{1},{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,2}(\omega)=\mathfrak{g}_{2}\}.

We have

\displaystyle I_{1}^{(n)}

\displaystyle={\rm E}\,\Big\|\sum_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\in\mathfrak{G}^{(n)}}\sum_{\mathfrak{g}_{2}\in\mathfrak{G}^{(n)}}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}-{\rm E}[\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}|{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,2},{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,1}]\right)\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle={\rm E}\sum_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\in\mathfrak{G}^{(n)}}\sum_{\mathfrak{g}_{2}\in\mathfrak{G}^{(n)}}\left\|{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}-{\rm E}[\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}|{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,2},{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,1}]\right)\right\|^{2},

where the second equality follows from the fact that
{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{h}_{1}\mathfrak{h}_{2}}^{(n)}}={\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{(\mathfrak{g}_{1}=\mathfrak{h}_{1},\mathfrak{g}_{2}=\mathfrak{h}_{2})}.
Note that

{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}}{\rm E}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}|{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,2},{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{(n)}_{\pm,1}\right]=\frac{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}}}{{\rm P}({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)})}\int_{\eta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}}\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}(\eta){\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\eta){\rm d}{\rm P}(\eta).

Therefore,

\displaystyle I_{1}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\in\mathfrak{G}^{(n)}}\sum_{\mathfrak{g}_{2}\in\mathfrak{G}^{(n)}}\int_{\Omega}\Big\|{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}}(\omega)\int_{\eta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}(\omega){\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\omega)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}(\eta){\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\eta)\right]\frac{{\rm d}{\rm P}(\eta)}{{\rm P}({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)})}\Big\|^{2}{\rm d}{\rm P}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\in\mathfrak{G}^{(n)}}\sum_{\mathfrak{g}_{2}\in\mathfrak{G}^{(n)}}\int_{\Omega}\Big\|{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}}(\omega)\int_{\eta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}}\left[\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}(\omega)-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1;{\rm a}}^{(n)}(\omega)\right){\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\omega)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1;{\rm a}}^{(n)}(\eta)-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}(\eta)\right){\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\eta)\right]\frac{{\rm d}{\rm P}(\eta)}{{\rm P}({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)})}\Big\|^{2}{\rm d}{\rm P}(\omega)

since, on
{\mathcal{A}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}
,

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1;{\rm a}}^{(n)}(\omega){\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\omega){\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}}(\omega)=\text{vec}({\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}_{1}(\mathfrak{g}_{2}){\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}_{2}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{g}_{1}))=\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1;{\rm a}}^{(n)}(\eta){\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\eta){\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}}(\eta).

In view of (
B1
), for any
\tilde{\varepsilon}>0
, there exists
n({\tilde{\varepsilon}})
such that
\|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1;{\rm a}}(\omega)-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}^{(n)}(\omega)\|^{2}<\tilde{\varepsilon}
for all
n>n({\tilde{\varepsilon}})
and all
\omega\in\mathcal{A}
. Then, using Jensen’s inequality again,

\displaystyle I_{1}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\in\mathfrak{G}^{(n)}}\sum_{\mathfrak{g}_{2}\in\mathfrak{G}^{(n)}}\int_{\Omega}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}}(\omega)\int_{\eta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}}\left[2\|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}(\omega)-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1;{\rm a}}^{(n)}(\omega)\|^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\omega)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+2\|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1;{\rm a}}^{(n)}(\eta)-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{2,1}(\eta)\|^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\eta)\right]\frac{{\rm d}{\rm P}(\eta)}{{\rm P}({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)})}{\rm d}{\rm P}(\omega)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\in\mathfrak{G}^{(n)}}\sum_{\mathfrak{g}_{2}\in\mathfrak{G}^{(n)}}\int_{\Omega}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}}(\omega)4\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}\frac{{\rm P}({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)})}{{\rm P}({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)})}{\rm d}{\rm P}(\omega)=4\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}{\rm E}\sum_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\in\mathfrak{G}^{(n)}}\sum_{\mathfrak{g}_{2}\in\mathfrak{G}^{(n)}}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{2}}^{(n)}}=4\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}.

The result follows. ∎

Appendix E: Computational aspects

In this section, we briefly discuss some computational aspects related to the implementation of our test statistics

\utilde{S}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}_{1}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}_{2}}^{(n)}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})
and
\widehat{\utilde{W}}^{(n)}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}_{1}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}_{2}}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(n)})

and we propose algorithms for ease of implementation.

(i)
The asymptotic distribution of our test statistic requires
that both
n_{R}
and
n_{S}
tend to infinity. In practice, we factorize
n
into
n_{R}n_{S}+n_{0}
in such a way that both
n_{R}
and
n_{S}
are large. Typically,
n_{R}
is of order
n^{1/d}
and
n_{S}
is of order
n^{(d-1)/d}
, whilst
0\leq n_{0}<\min(n_{S},n_{R})
has to be small as possible—its value, however, is entirely determined by the values of
n_{R}
and
n_{S}
.
Generating “regular grids” of
n_{S}
points over the unit sphere
\mathcal{S}_{d-1}
as described in Section
2.3
is easy for
d=2
, where perfect regularity can be achieved by dividing the unit circle into
n_{S}
arcs of equal length
2\pi/n_{S}
. For
d\geq 3
,
“perfect regularity” is no longer possible.
A random array of
n_{S}
independent and uniformly distributed unit vectors does satisfy (almost surely) the requirement for weak convergence to
{\mathrm{U}}_{d}
, representing the uniform distribution over the ball
\mathbb{S}_{d}
. More regular deterministic arrays (with faster convergence) can be constructed, though, such as the
low-discrepancy sequences
(see, e.g.,
Niederreiter, 1992
,
Judd, 1998
, or
Dick and Pillichshammer, 2014
) considered in numerical integration and the design of computer experiments; we suggest the use of the function
UnitSphere
in R package
mvmesh
.

(ii)
The empirical center-outward distribution function
{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}_{{\mspace{-1mu}{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}}}^{(n)}
is obtained as the solution of an optimal coupling problem. Many efficient algorithms have been proposed in the measure transportation literature (see, e.g.,
Peyré and Cuturi, 2019
). We followed
Hallin et al., 2020a
, using a Hungarian algorithm (see the
clue
R package). For a detailed account of the Hungarian algorithm and the complexity of different implementations, see, e.g., Chapter 4 in
Burkhard et al., 2009
). Faster algorithms are available, though, as Bertsekas’ auction algorithm or its variant, the forward/reverse auction algorithm, (Chapter 4 in
Bertsekas, 1991
), implemented in the R package
transport
.

(iii)
The computation of the test statistic
\widehat{\utilde{W}}^{(n)}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}_{1}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}_{2}}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(n)})
involves two basic ingredients: a root-
n
consistent estimator
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(n)}
of
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}
and a consistent estimator of the matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$\Upsilon$}_{{{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}}_{1},{{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}}_{2},f}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})
. For
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(n)}
, a great number of candidates are available, e.g., the center-outward R-estimator of
Hallin et al., 2019
, the reweighted multivariate least trimmed squares estimator of
Croux, 2008
, and the QMLE (provided that fourth-order moments finite). Turning to the estimation of
\mbox{\boldmath$\Upsilon$}_{{{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}}_{1},{{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}}_{2},f}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})
, the issue is that this matrix depends on the unknown actual density
f
.
A simple consistent estimator is obtained by letting
{\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}}={\bf e}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,p_{0}d^{2}
in (
4.8
) where
{\bf e}_{i}
denotes the
i
th vector of the canonical basis in the parameter space
{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{1}d^{2}}
: the difference
\utilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}}^{(n)}_{{{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}}_{1},{{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}}_{2}}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(n)}+n^{-1/2}{\bf e}_{i})-\utilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}}^{(n)}_{{{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}}_{1},{{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}}_{2}}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(n)})

then provides a consistent estimator of the
i
th column of
-\mbox{\boldmath$\Upsilon$}_{{{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}}_{1},{{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}}_{2},f}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})
. See
Hallin et al, 2006
or
Cassart et al, 2010
for more sophisticated estimation methods.
