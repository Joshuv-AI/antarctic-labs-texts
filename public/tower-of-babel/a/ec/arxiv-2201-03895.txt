Virtual motives for synthetic geometries, A. Definition and properties of K 0 ​ ( 𝒬 ℓ ) K_{0}(\mathcal{Q}_{\ell})
Koen Thas

Abstract
. In this note, we introduce the first basics on Grothendieck rings for incidence geometries as a new motivic way and tool to study synthetic geometry. In this first instance, we concentrate on generalized quadrangles and related geometries. Many questions, new properties and insights arise along the way.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
PPC

We start this section with an infamous conjecture in combinatorics, which has resisted many attacks, but is still standing strong:

Conjecture 1.1

(Prime Power Conjecture (ppc))
.

The order of a finite projective plane is a prime power.

To our understanding, there is no clear essential reason
why
this conjecture should be true; all the known examples of finite projective planes—which come in many infinite classes—satisfy the conjecture, but to our best of knowledge there has never been discovered a common property for all projective planes of the same finite order that
points to the direction of the ppc.

The ppc has proved to be an extremely important motivation—sometimes in an implicit guise—to develop a very deep theory underlying axiomatic projective planes. The first breakthrough in the quest for understanding the order of finite planes was famously published by Bruck and Ryser in 1949
[
5
]
:
if there is a finite projective plane of order
N
, and

N\equiv 1\mod{4}
or
N\equiv 2\mod{4}
, then
N
is the sum of two squares.
(The proof is short but ingenious: the authors express the assumption in terms of incidence matrices, and then pass to Hasse-Minkowski theory of rational equivalence of quadratic forms.) Despite this marvelous result, this is the
only known result on the order of a finite plane
whose statement does not impose further geometric or group theoretical restrictions on the plane. This was 70 years ago!

1.2.
Coordinatization

One promising general approach to analyzing planes, with possible applications to the prime power conjecture, certainly came with the coordinatization method devised by Hall in

[
10
]
. Defining an algebraic structure in which the binary operations are governed by incidence relations in a given plane, opened up a startling new vista to algebraic methods.
The importance of this approach is very clearly outlined in chapters V–VI–VII–VIII–IX of Hughes and Piper’s seminal book
[
15
]
.
But the prime power conjecture did not come out of the many papers on the algebraic side of coordinatization, although many sub-results were obtained which tried to characterize planes by using the underlying ternary rings. Wedderburn’s famous result that states that finite division rings are always finite fields
[
50
]
(and hence have a prime power number of elements), and later Artin-Zorn’s generalization to finite alternative division rings
[
51
]
, are the model results which inspired much of the algebraic theory in the finite case.

which violate Bruck-Ryser’s condition

by Lam, Thiel and Swiercz, after a 3000 hour computer search on a CRAY-1A super computer (and using theoretical work of many other authors, such as John Thompson’s work related to combinatorial projective curves).

Still, coordinatization is perhaps the only known method which handles all projective planes “at the same time.” The only other results, conjectures and applications known today which give a prime power result, all rely on hypothesized
local or global group actions on projective planes
. Also, the fact that the André-Bruck-Bose approach to translation planes

gave—besides a clear prime power result—a representation method for planes in projective spaces (which also works in the infinite case), gave the theory an unexpected boost beyond the initial goals. Note the author’s recent breakthrough on the André-Bruck-Bose theorem for other generalized polygons

. In André-Bruck-Bose theory one associates a vector space over a division ring

is the prime (including

Other hypothesized group actions often led to classical planes
\mathbb{P}^{2}(k)
, and hence lost any grip on general planes. Still, under the assumption that a finite projective plane admits a free transitive automorphism group, it is a famous conjecture that the ppc is true, even when the group is assumed to be cyclic!

We mention that for generalized quadrangles—the protagonists of the present paper— there is also a well-developed theory of coordinatization introduced by Hanssens and Van Maldeghem

Much more about prime power conjectures of point-line incidence geometries can be found in the author’s paper

1.3.
The present paper: “cohomology without vector spaces”

One of our main goals of the present paper is to add a new chapter to the algebraic theory of incidence geometries, and in this paper (which is the first instance in a series) we will almost solely concentrate on generalizations of generalized quadrangles. (The projective planes are next.) We will associate a commutative ring to a category
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
which is generated by all (known and unkown) generalized quadrangles with
\ell+1
points incident with a line. We hope that this formalism introduces a new way to study (in this case) generalized quadrangles from the viewpoint of cohomology (more precisely: virtual motives) without having the appropriate commutative rings at our disposal to build a Zariski topology.
Our construction is directly inspired by the
Grothendieck ring of
k
-varieties
with
k
a field, and the latter contains cohomological/motivic information of the category of
k
-varieties. Let us first define this ring.

1.3.1.
Grothendieck rings and Zariski topology

Let
k
be a given field.
The
Grothendieck ring of varieties over
k
, denoted as
K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
(where
\mathcal{V}_{k}
is the category of
k
-varieties), is formally freely generated as an additive abelian group by the isomorphism classes
[X]
of varieties
{X}
over
k
(call this group
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
), and endowed with two types of relations:

(1)

\Big[A\Big]\ =\ \Big[A\setminus B\Big]\ +\ \Big[B\Big]

for any closed subset
B
of
A
in the Zariski topology, and
A
any
k
-variety, and with a product structure given by

(2)

\Big[U\Big]\cdot\Big[V\Big]\ :=\ \Big[U\times_{{\text{ Spec}}(k)}V\Big].

The ring
K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
captures certain aspects of motives, and hence of various (Weil-)cohomological theories associated to
\mathcal{V}_{k}
—we refer to section
2
for a short discussion. For being able to define the ring
K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
, we need several ingredients which come with
\mathcal{V}_{k}
:

•

the Zariski topology of the elements of
\mathcal{V}_{k}
;

•

a good notion of “isomorphism” for the elements in
\mathcal{V}_{k}
;

•

a product

\otimes:\ \Big(A,B\Big)\ \mapsto\ A\otimes B

which maps couples of
k
-varieties to
k
-varieties.

Furthermore, as we will see, we also need to dispose of a good theory of lines. In order to have a Zariski topology at hand, the starting point is the category of commutative rings, and the main ingredients are the notions of maximal ideal, prime ideal and ideal to begin with (for a given commutative ring
A
, say). Once the affine scheme
{\text{ Spec}}(A)
is defined,
the maximal ideals correspond to closed points of
{\text{ Spec}}(A)
, the prime ideals—which define the points of the topology—correspond to irreducible subvarieties in
{\text{ Spec}}(A)
, and the general ideals give rise to the “general subvarieties.” The Zariski topology is a natural topology which involves all varieties which lie on
{\text{ Spec}}(A)
. In our case, our main geometrical objects are generalizations of generalized quadrangles, and in general, we do not have commutative rings associated to these geometries in a natural way.

1.3.2.
Geometrical approach

Let
k
be a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Irreducible
k
-quadrics in low dimensions (
n=3,4,5
) verify the axioms for generalized quadrangles; quadrics, together with low-dimensional Hermitian
k
-varieties (in which case
k=\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
), represent—up to point-line duality—the so-called finite “classical examples” of these geometries. They are all fully embedded in the ambient projective space, have highly symmetrical automorphism groups (of Lie type), and because of that, enjoy highly symmetrical combinatorial properties. Even more so, the points and lines of these classical generalized quadrangles are the
k
-rational points and lines of an associated projective
k
-variety, and so all these examples come with a well-defined Zariski toplogy (and so define classes in the ring
K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
). (Quadrics are birational to projective spaces, and that is the key fact for their classes to be inside
\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{L}]
; they come with virtual mixed Tate motives. We will consider them in some detail in Appendix
A
.)

On the other hand, there is a deeply
developed theory of
synthetic generalized quadrangles
, and many infinite classes of non-classical generalized quadrangles are known; those are not embedded in projective space at all (by a theorem of Buekenhout and Lefèvre
[
6
]
), usually have rather rigid automorphism groups, etc. Still, in these examples, one can see obvious subsets which should be closed sets in any reasonably defined Zariski topology.
In fact, what is very clear throughout the synthetic theory of generalized quadrangles is the fundamental role those subgeometries play in characterizations and classification results, which exactly are combinatorial versions of what would be irreducible sub
k
-varieties in the classical quadrangles.

Simple example.
In any of the generalized quadrangles which are embedded in a finite projective space, the set of points
a^{\perp}
collinear with an arbitrary point
a
is the intersection of the quadrangle with a hyperplane. So this point set is the set of rational points of some projective variety. It makes much sense to see sets
a^{\perp}
in abstract generalized quadrangles in this way, and hence to see them as synthetic irreducible closed subvarieties (prime ideals) in a Zariski topology.

This “translation method” is our starting point for founding the first definitions.

Obviously the topology we obtain will lack a lot of information in comparison to the scheme-theoretic picture (where for example a
k
-scheme with
k
a field covers the geometry over field extensions of
k
as well via Galois descent). In some classes of examples (where field extensions make sense), a much richer Zariski topology can be established, and we describe such a class in detail. In such cases, we do have a Galois descent at our disposal.

1.3.3.
Theory of lines

In the theory of Grothendieck rings of
k
-varieties the class
[\mathbb{L}]
of the affine
k
-line plays an exceptional role. Many foundational questions involve this class, and the subring
\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{L}]
describes virtual mixed Tate motives. Arguably the most striking identity in
K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
is

[\mathbb{P}^{m}(k)]\ =\ \mathbb{L}^{m}\ +\ \mathbb{L}^{m-1}\ +\ \ldots\ +\ [\mathrm{point}]

which arises inductively from the identity

[\mathbb{P}^{m}(k)]\ =\ [\mathbb{P}^{m-1}(k)]\ +\ [\mathbb{A}^{m}(k)].

In our theory, its natural avatar will come from the theory of geometrical hyperplanes in generalized quadrangles and more general geometries. On the other hand, we won’t dispose of one line anymore in
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
(the category we are using in this paper): as we will point out, due to the lack of natural structure of lines in the incidence-geometrical context (in stark contrast with algebraic geometry), we will refine the concept of line (and other degenerate geometries) in order to make the theory more capable. It would be very interesting to understand the subring of
K_{0}(\mathcal{Q}_{\ell})
generated by the isomorphism classes of all lines in
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
. We refer to
[
42
]
for a detailed discussion about synthetic projective lines, inspired by this paper.

Récoltes et Semailles

2.1.
The ring
K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})

Let
k
be a given field.
The
Grothendieck ring of varieties over
k
, denoted as
K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
(where
\mathcal{V}_{k}
is the category of
k
-varieties), is formally freely generated as an additive abelian group by the isomorphism classes
[X]
of varieties
{X}
over
k
(call this group
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
), and endowed with two types of relations:

(3)

\Big[A\Big]\ =\ \Big[A\setminus B\Big]\ +\ \Big[B\Big]\ \ \mbox{{\bf(``scissor relations'')}}

for any closed subset
B
of
A
, and
A
any
k
-variety, and with a product structure given by

(4)

\Big[U\Big]\cdot\Big[V\Big]\ :=\ \Big[U\times_{{\text{ Spec}}(k)}V\Big].

Note that the set of all elements
[A]-[A\setminus B]-[B]
(A a
k
-variety,
B
closed in
A
), forms an “ideal” in
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
.

We denote by
\mathbb{L}=[\mathbb{A}^{1}(k)]
the class of the affine line over
k
. The neutral element for addition is given by
[\emptyset]=:0
, and the neutral element for multiplication is
[{\text{ Spec}}(k)]=:1
.

The specific form of the scissor relations stems from motivic aspects, as we will indicate below.

Suppose
k
is contained in
\mathbb{C}
, and let
X
be a
k
-variety. Define

\chi^{c}(X):=\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}b_{k}^{c}(X),

with
b_{k}^{c}(X):=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{C}}H^{k}_{c}\Big(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{C}\Big)
(where we consider singular cohomology with compact support). Then for
Y
closed in
X
, we have that

\chi^{c}(X)=\chi^{c}(X\setminus Y)+\chi^{c}(Y).

Also, Gillet and Soulé proved in
[
9
]
that there is a homomorphism from
K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
to the
K_{0}
of the
category of “pure motives” if
k
has characteristic
0
.

2.2.
The motivic formalism

Grothendieck’s theory of
motives
predicts a universal cohomology
theory
h
for “good” cohomology theories of (say) varieties over fields.

The functor
h
must satisfy:

(M
1
)

the Künneth formula

(5)

h(V\times W)=h(V)\otimes h(W);

(M
2
)

the property that disjoint unions are translated into direct sums;

(M
3
)

certain additional axioms to obtain the Lefschetz formula.

Let
k
be a field.
The universal cohomology theory
h
should take values in a category of motives
{\text{ Mot}}(k)
which should look like the category of finite-dimensional
\mathbb{Q}
-vector spaces—more precisely:

(M
{}_{1}^{\prime}
)

homomorphism groups should “be”
\mathbb{Q}
-vector spaces;

(M
{}_{2}^{\prime}
)

{\text{ Mot}}(k)
should be an abelian category (or even better a “tannakian” category, cf.
[
8
]
).

Every Weil (= “good”) cohomology theory
\mathbf{H}
with coefficients in some field
\wp
(such as the étale
\ell
-adic one) should fit (uniquely) into the following diagram

(6)

\mathbf{H}\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{V}_{k}}\overset{h}{\longrightarrow}{\text{ Mot}}(k)\xrightarrow{\omega_{\mathbf{H}}\,}\{\text{graded }\wp\text{-vector spaces}\},

where we denote the category of nonsingular projective varieties over
k
by
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}_{k}}
.
Here,
\omega_{\mathbf{H}}
is a functor which comes with the cohomology theory
\mathbf{H}
, such that

(7)

\omega_{\mathbf{H}}\bigl(h(X)\bigr)=\mathbf{H}^{*}(X)=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2\mathrm{dim}(X)}H^{i}(X).

The functor
\omega_{\mathbf{H}}
gives rise to decompositions of the
h(X)
in “pure pieces” which yield a lot of extra information about
X
. In case of a projective space
\mathbb{P}^{n}(k)
over any base field
k
, it can be proven that

h(\mathbb{P}^{n}(k))\ =\ \mathbb{L}^{n}\ +\ \mathbb{L}^{n-1}\ +\ \cdots\ +\ 1,

where
\mathbb{L}:=h(\mathbb{A}^{1}(k))
is the so-called
Lefschetz motive
.
This is the star example of what Serre calls “meccano des motifs”
[
28
]
.

If we now turn back to the ring
K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
(
k
a field) for a moment, we see that
due to the scissor relations, we also have identities such as

\Big[\mathbb{P}^{n}(k)\Big]\ =\Big[\mathbb{P}^{n-1}(k)\Big]\ +\ \Big[\mathbb{A}^{n}(k)\Big],

given us the typical motivic composition of the kind discussed above:

\displaystyle\Big[\mathbb{P}^{n}(k)\Big]\

\displaystyle=\ \Big[\mathbb{A}^{n}(k)\Big]\ +\Big[\mathbb{A}^{n-1}(k)\Big]\ +\ \cdots\ +\ 1

(8)

\displaystyle=\ \mathbb{L}^{n}\ +\ \mathbb{L}^{n-1}\ +\ \cdots\ +\ 1.

Many identities of this type in
K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
correspond to analogous motivic decompositions in
{\text{ Mot}}(k)
. It is one of its many charms.

2.2.1.
Additive invariants

Let
R
be a commutative ring.
An
additive invariant

\chi\colon{\mathcal{V}_{k}}\to R
is a map with the following properties (where
X,Y
are objects in
\mathcal{V}_{k}
):

\left\lgroup\begin{tabular}[]{p{0.9\textwidth}}\begin{description}\description@item@[\rm\textsc{Isomorphism Invariance:}]$\chi(X)=\chi(Y)$ if $X\cong Y$;
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\description@item@[\rm\textsc{Multiplicativity:}]$\chi(X\times Y)=\chi(X)\chi(Y)$;
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\description@item@[\rm\textsc{Inclusion-Exclusion:}]$\chi(X)=\chi(Y)+\chi(X\setminus Y)$ for $Y$ closed in $X$.
\end{description}\end{tabular}\right.

A standard example is the
topological Euler characteristic
. It is clear that giving an additive invariant
\chi
is the same as giving a ring morphism

(9)

\chi\colon K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})\longrightarrow R.

Let
\mathbb{L}
be the class
[\mathbb{A}^{1}(k)]
in
K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
—
it is called the virtual
Lefschetz motive
.
The subring
\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{L}]\subset K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
is the subring of virtual
mixed Tate motives
. Let us say that a
k
-variety
X
has a
mixed Tate motive
if
[X]\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{L}]
.

2.3.
Mixed Tate motives and counting polynomials

If
X
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-variety, and
X
corresponds to a mixed Tate motive (over
k
), it has class

(10)

[X\times_{{\text{ Spec}}(\mathbb{Z})}{\text{ Spec}}(k)]\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{L}]\subset K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k}).

Up to a finite number of primes (where bad reduction phenomena could occur), it follows that

(11)

N_{p^{m}}(X):=\#\bigl(X\times_{{\text{ Spec}}(\mathbb{Z})}{\text{ Spec}}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}})\bigr)

(with
p
a prime and
m
a nonzero integer) is a polynomial in
p^{m}
, since the counting function
N_{p^{m}}(\cdot)
is also an additive
invariant and since
N_{p^{m}}\bigl(\mathbb{A}_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}})\bigr)=p^{m}
. We refer to
[
2
]
for more details.

2.4.
Naive motives versus “real” motives

Although
K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
is often considered as a naive model for working with “real” motives, it is still the case that
K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
can see certain properties which real motives cannot. A very interesting discussion can be found in the thread
[
20
]
. Under the cut-and-paste conjecture, two
k
-varieties are equivalent in the Grothendieck ring if and only if they can both be decomposed into the same set of locally closed pieces. There are actually many pairs of
k
-varieties with the same motive but which can not be cut-and-pasted into each other.
Example:

\mathbb{P}^{2}(k)
and a fake projective plane over
k
. So the Grothendieck ring
does
remember quite some information about the varieties. In particular, after work of Larsen and Lunts
[
19
]
, one can show that two smooth projective
\mathbb{C}
-varieties with the same class in the Grothendieck ring
K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}})
have isomorphic fundamental groups, which is a highly nonabelian invariant! So the category of motives loses some information which is contained in the Grothendieck ring — in some sense the “nonabelian information”
[
20
]
.

Poonen was the first to show (in
[
23
]
) that if
k
is a field of characteristic
0
, then
K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
is not a domain (by finding elliptic curves
E
and
E^{\prime}
over
k
for which
[E]\neq[E^{\prime}]
but nevertheless
([E]+[E^{\prime}])([E]-[E^{\prime}])=0
). Many other related results have been found since
[
23
]
—see e.g.
[
17
]
.

On the other hand,
K_{0}({\mathcal{V}_{k}})
does not remember nontrivial extensions, but Voevodsky’s category of motives does. For example, let
\mathcal{C}
be a smooth projective curve of positive genus, and let
x
and
y
be two rational points of
\mathcal{C}
. Then in Voevodsky’s version,
h^{1}(\mathcal{C}\setminus\{x,y\})
depends nontrivially on the class of
[x]-[y]
in the Jacobian of
\mathcal{C}
. But the expression
[\mathcal{C}]-[x]-[y]
in
K_{0}({\mathcal{V}_{k}})
does not depend on the choice of
x
and
y
.
In our approach to “synthetic Grothendieck rings” the latter property is in general
not
true (and such classes will depend on the choice).

3.
Quadrangles and subquadrangles

A
generalized quadrangle
of
order
(s,t)
is a point-line incidence structure
\Gamma=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathbf{I})
, where
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{L}

Axiom (3).

Generalized quadrangles, or “GQs” for short, were introduced in 1959 by Tits in a seminal paper

For that reason, but also for their uncanny ability to be used and applied in Incidence Geometry and far beyond, for the deep construction, classification and characterization theory of classical and nonclassical examples (think for instance of Tits and Weis’ famous classification of Moufang polygons

3.1.
Duality

If we replace the notions “point” and “line” in a point-line geometry which satisfies (3), then we obtain again a point-line geometry
which satisfies (3).

3.2.
Subquadrangles

Let

Examples

We refer to

4.
Symmetry, SPGQ theory and TGQs

The theory of
span-symmetric generalized quadrangles
and
translation generalized quadrangles
will be used in some of our guiding examples in the set-up
of the isomorphism theory we will formulate for local geometries. In this section, we will give a short overview with detailed references to
the most useful properties and results.

4.1.
Symmetry

Let
\Gamma
be a thick generalized quadrangle of order
(s,t)
. We define
automorphisms
of
\Gamma
in the usual
manner, and denote by
\mathrm{Aut}(\Gamma)
its automorphism group.

Let
U
be a line in
\Gamma
. A
symmetry
with axis
U
is an automorphism which fixes each line in
U^{\perp}
. Let
V\sim U
be
any line in
U^{\perp}\setminus\{U\}
. If the group
S(U)
of all symmetries with axis
U
acts transitively on the points of
V\setminus\{U\cap V\}
, we say that
U
is an
axis of symmetry
. The following two properties can then be shown:

(1)

the definition is independent of the choice of
V
;

(2)

the action of
S(U)
on
V\setminus\{U\cap V\}
is sharply transitively.

It is also straightforward to prove that
s\leq t
if
\Gamma
has an axis of symmetry.

4.2.
Grids and regularity

Abstractly, we will sometimes call a thin quadrangle with parameters
(m,1)
a
grid
. We say that a pair
(U,V)
of nonconcurrent lines in a thick generalized quadrangle
\Gamma
of order
(s,t)
is
regular
if they are contained in a (necessarily unique) grid
\Gamma^{\prime}
of order
(s,1)
. (If the parameter
s
would be infinite, we furthermore demand that the lines of the grid are “full,” meaning that each point in
\Gamma
which is incident with
U
and/or
V
is also a point of
\Gamma^{\prime}
.)
If
(U,V)
is regular for all noncurrent line-pairs
(U,V)
with
U
fixed, then
U
is by definition a
regular line
. It is easy to show that an axis of symmetry is a regular line.

4.3.
SPGQs

If a thick generalized quadrangle
\Gamma
has nonconcurrent axes of symmetry
A
and
B
, we call it
span-symmetric
, and
one can show that each line in
\{A,B\}^{\perp\perp}
is an axis of symmetry. The set
\{A,B\}^{\perp\perp}
is called the
base-span
.
A span-symmetric generalized quadrangle is also abbreviated as “SPGQ.”

If
\Gamma
is thick, finite and with parameters
(s,s)
, then it has been shown independently by Kantor
[
16
]
and the author
[
35
]
, that

s
is a prime power, and
\Gamma\cong\mathcal{Q}(4,s)
(cf. section
4.5.1
below for the definition of the latter quadrangle). Interestingly enough, this implies that
\mathrm{Aut}(\Gamma)
acts transitively on all lines, starting from a local group-theoretical condition.

The theory of SPGQs of order
(s,t)
with
s\neq t
is more challenging, and we refer to the papers
[
34
,
36
]
and the monograph
[
37
]
for the details. Let us just mention — and only for finite quadrangles — that:

(a)

one can prove that
t=s^{2}
with
s
a prime power;

(b)

(a) follows from the fact that an SPGQ of order
(s,t)
with
s\neq t
has
s+1
different subGQs, all isomorphic to
\mathcal{Q}(4,s)
(cf. section
4.5.1
below) and mutually intersecting in the grid with parameters
(s,1)
that contains the base-span;

(c)

contrary to the case
(s,s)
, there are nonclassical examples of SPGQs, even with many different base-spans. We will meet one such class of examples in section
4.5.2
.

4.4.
TGQs

Let
\Gamma
be a thick generalized quadrangle. If every line incident with some point
u
is an axis of symmetry, then
the automorphism group
T
generated by the symmetries about all axes of symmetry on
u
, is abelian and acts sharply transitively on the points opposite
u

[
22
, §8.3]
. Vice versa, if
\Gamma
would have an abelian automorphism group which fixes all lines incident with some point
u
and which happens to act sharply transitively on the points opposite
u
, then each line on
u
is an axis of symmetry. (This is an easy exercise).

This invariantly carries over to thin quadrangles of order
(s,1)
, and also to thick infinite quadrangles.

If
\Gamma
,
u
and
T
are as above, we call
(\Gamma,u,T)
(or
\Gamma
, if
u
and
T
are clearly defined), a
translation generalized quadrangle
(TGQ) with
translation point
u
and
translation group

. There is a pretty intricate literature on TGQs, and we welcome the reader to the monograph
[
32
]
for much more.

4.5.
Some finite examples

4.5.1.
Orthogonal quadrangles

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field. Below, we work with homogeneous coordinates
(x_{0}:x_{1}:\cdots:x_{m})
in a projective

Let

with parameters

Let

Let

Each generalized quadrangle isomorphic to one of the quadrangles defined above is called

(a)

(b)

by (a), each point is a translation point, so a finite orthogonal quadrangle is a TGQ relative to any given point.

5.
The Zariski topology

In order to define the Grothendieck ring, we need to assign a Zariski topology to each generalized quadrangle (since they will be representants of
the generators). The obvious obstruction is that we do not have defining equations, nor coordinate rings at our disposal, so we want to circumvent this lack of underlying
algebra. Comparing to the classical Zariski topology of a variety over an algebraically closed field
k
, there would be a straightforward way to define the points of the topology:
in the classical Zariski topology, they are the
k
-rational points, so if
\Gamma
is a generalized quadrangle, its points could be defined as the points of the topology. If
k
is
a finite field, the classical Zariski topology is a discrete topology, since all rational points are closed points, and since finite sets of closed points are closed. The same thing happens if
\Gamma
is finite: all points of
\Gamma
should be compared to rational, so closed points, and we obtain a discrete topology. In the infinite case, the situation is different (as the rest of this section will show).

We will introduce a different, richer, topology, which we model on the modern notion of Zariski topology. In the latter, points correspond to prime ideals, and in their turn those correspond to irreducible subvarieties. So the first thing we do is define irreducible subgeometries of generalized quadrangles. We do not see how to introduce analogons to closed points which are not rational, although we will indicate some situations in which one perfectly can: in some sense the topology will behave
as if the quadrangle will be defined over an algebraically closed field.
Also, as we will see, for classical quadrangles such as
\mathcal{Q}(4,q)
, which consist of the rational points and lines of

varieties
, the synthetically defined Zariski topology will be coarser than the real Zariski topology.

After that, the closed sets will be introduced.

Abstractly — that is, in case of generalized quadrangles which are not defined through classes — the finite case has much less structure than the Zariski topology of projective varieties over finite fields: the finite set of rational points of such a variety does not tell much about the variety, and nor does it about isomorphisms between varieties. Therefore, we need to be cautious when introducing isomorphisms, and on that level we will greatly deviate from the viewpoint of algebraic geometry.

5.1.
Closed subsets (1)

In this section we list a number of types of subsets which we will regard as (corresponding to) closed sets. So they will form a base, or at least
part of a base, for the closed set “abstract Zariski topology.” We first list the base elements which should be trivially included. The first candidates are thin, and need closer attention on the level of isomorphisms.

“Point and line sets”

Here, we consider finite sets. We need to decide what the role of collinearities and concurrencies is in this context.

“Perps”

We consider full lines in “perp geometries,” but will allow non-ideal perps.

“Subquadrangles”

An obvious candidate. Here, we will again only consider full examples.

“Geometrical hyperplanes”

A very important class of candidates, which contains some of the extremal examples of previous items. (We refer to section
5.4
for a precise definition.)
In
K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
, we have the typical motivic identity

(12)

\Big[\mathbb{P}^{n}(k)\Big]\ =\ \Big[\mathbb{A}^{n}(k)\Big]\ +\ \Big[\mathbb{P}^{n-1}(k)\Big]

at our disposal. So if
\mathcal{Q}
would be a geometrical hyperplane of a generalized quadrangle
\Gamma
, then a similar identity would be

(13)

[\Gamma]\ =\ [\mathbb{A}]\ +\ [\mathcal{Q}],

where
\mathbb{A}
is the affine quadrangle which arises by deleting
\mathcal{Q}
. So we definitely want to see each geometric hyperplane of a generalized quadrangle as a closed set. All such identities will remain true in
K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
. (See also Appendix
A
.)

5.2.
Points

We first obtain a result about decompositions of generalized quadrangles with order. The theorem applies to thick quadrangles and thin quadrangles with order, both
in the finite and infinite case.
We remark that a highly relevant paper is Van Maldeghem’s
[
48
]
, in which he studies finite generalized quadrangles which are unions of a number of full subquadrangles.

We start with a lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is a generalized quadrangle of order
(s,t)
with

, and let
\mathcal{S}
be a subgeometry of
\Gamma
.
Let axiom (3) be satisfied. If
\mathcal{S}
has one full line

, and at least one other line

not meeting

, then either
\mathcal{S}
is a full thick subquadrangle,
or
\mathcal{S}
is a grid with at least one full regulus.

Proof
. By axiom (3),

is also a full line, and also by (3), all lines in
\{U,V\}^{\perp}
are lines of
\mathcal{S}
.
Suppose

is a line which is not contained in
\{U,V\}^{\perp}\cup\{U,V\}^{\perp\perp}
. Then

cannot be concurrent with
both

and

. It follows easily that
\mathcal{S}
is a full thick subGQ of
\Gamma
.
\blacksquare

Theorem 5.2

(Decomposition Theorem)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a GQ of order
(s,t)
. Then
\Gamma
cannot be the union of two different proper subquadrangles (where the definition of subquadrangle is taken as in
§
3
), unless we are in the following cases.

\left\lgroup\begin{tabular}[]{p{0.9\textwidth}}\begin{itemize}\itemize@item@[\rm(i)]$\Gamma$ is a GQ of order $(s,1)$, and $\Gamma_{1}$ and $\Gamma_{2}$ are $(s+1)\times r_{i}$-grids ($i=1,2$) which share one regulus.
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@[\rm(ii)]$\Gamma$ is a GQ of order $(s,1)$, and $\Gamma_{2}$ is a GQ of order $(s,0)$.
Then $\Gamma_{1}$ is an $(s+1)\times s$-grid.
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@[\rm(iii)]$\Gamma$ is a GQ of order $(s,1)$, and $\Gamma_{2}$ is the perp geometry of one point.
Then $\Gamma_{1}$ is an $s\times s$-grid.
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@[\rm(iv)]$\Gamma$ is a GQ of order $(1,t)$, and $\Gamma_{1}$ and $\Gamma_{2}$ are dual $r_{i}\times(t+1)$-grids ($i=1,2$) which share one dual regulus.
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@[\rm(v)]$\Gamma$ is a GQ of order $(1,t)$, and $\Gamma_{2}$ is a GQ of order $(0,t)$. Then $\Gamma_{1}$ is a dual $(t+1)\times t$-grid.
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@[\rm(vi)]$\Gamma$ is a GQ of order $(1,t)$, and $\Gamma_{2}$ is a dual grid with parameters $(1,t-1)$, and $\Gamma_{1}$ is the perp geometry of one line.
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@[\rm(vii)]$s=2$ and $t=2$. In this case, $\Gamma\cong\mathcal{Q}(4,2)$, and $\Gamma_{2}$ is a grid with parameters $(2,1)$. A simple combinatorial exercise yields that
$\Gamma_{1}$ is a dual grid with parameters $(1,2)$.
\end{itemize}\end{tabular}\right.

Proof
. Let
\Gamma
be a generalized quadrangle of order
(s,t)
, and suppose by way of contradiction that
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}
. By this, we mean that each point and each line of
\Gamma
is either in
\Gamma_{1}
or
\Gamma_{2}
.

First note the following. If
\Gamma_{2}
would not have lines, then all lines of
\Gamma
are lines of
\Gamma_{1}
. Then any point of
\Gamma_{2}
is also a point of
\Gamma_{1}
, since it is incident with two intersecting lines of
\Gamma_{1}
. So
\Gamma_{2}\leq\Gamma_{1}
, contradiction. So both
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
contain
points and lines; in particular, neither one can be an ovoid, or merely a set of points.

Below, we will distinguish between the case where at least one of
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
has only full lines (or only ideal points), and the case where none of

\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
have. From the viewpoint of this paper, this is a natural distinction.

First let
\Gamma_{2}
have only full lines.

For now, we suppose that
\Gamma
is thick
.

Suppose first that
\Gamma_{2}
does not have an order. Then since
\Gamma_{2}
only has full lines, it must be the geometry of a partial perp. Say it is a subgeometry
of

with
x
a point of
\Gamma_{2}
, and note that we allow
\Gamma_{2}
to have order
(s,0)
. Then for any

y
a point of
\Gamma_{2}
, we have that

is incident with at least two lines of
\Gamma_{1}
. So

is a point of
\Gamma_{1}
. Whence each line of
\Gamma_{2}
is also a line of
\Gamma_{1}
, and it easily follows that
\Gamma_{2}\leq\Gamma_{1}
, contradiction.

Next, let
\Gamma_{2}
have an order; then each point of
\Gamma_{2}
is incident with at least one line of
\Gamma_{1}\setminus\Gamma_{2}
.
If each such point is incident with at least two such lines, then each point of
\Gamma_{2}
is also a point of
\Gamma_{1}
, so that
\Gamma_{2}\leq\Gamma_{1}
, contradiction.
So suppose now each point of
\Gamma_{2}
is incident with precisely one line of
\Gamma_{1}\setminus\Gamma_{2}
. Since
\Gamma_{1}
does not contain
\Gamma_{2}
,
there is at least one line of
\Gamma_{1}\setminus\Gamma_{2}
which meets
\Gamma_{2}
in one point which is not a point of
\Gamma_{1}
. Now let

be a point in
\Gamma_{1}\setminus\Gamma_{2}
. Project

onto all lines of
\Gamma_{2}
; since each line of
\Gamma_{2}
is full, we obtain a set of points in
\Gamma_{2}
, which we denote by

, and
which meets line of
\Gamma_{2}
in precisely one point. Now let

be a point in
\Gamma_{2}
which is not in
\mathcal{O}_{y}
; Then

is, up to one point (which is the one point of
\{x,y\}^{\perp}
which is not a point of
\Gamma_{2}
), contained in
\mathcal{O}_{y}
. So

contains an ovoid of
\Gamma_{2}
minus one point.

Now suppose
\Gamma_{2}
is a thick subGQ. In that case, let

be the point of
\mathcal{O}_{y}
which is not in

. Let

be a line of
\Gamma_{2}
incident with

\Gamma_{2}
must meet

, it follows that if we project all the points of

on

.
Let
\Gamma_{2}
be thin; then
\Gamma_{2}
is a either one full line (together with the incident points), or it is a dual grid with parameters

.

By inspection of the possibilities (and checking in each case whether an ovoid can occur of the above form), the following cases can occur:

(a)

is a line with two points, and
\Gamma_{1}

(b)

, and

(c)

is a dual grid with parameters

.

In scheme theory, a projective line over an arbitrary field

6.2.1.
Idea 1 (explained for GQs)

There must be thick subGQs

6.2.4.
Example

Let

be a GQ, and suppose that

is a line which is not regular. If

The example of this section show the wide difference between the notion of isomorphism in IDEA 1 and IDEA 2. As stated before: changing the basic

In the next subsection, we explain a third idea, which is a geometric counterpart of IDEA 2, while generalizing IDEA 1 in an intrinsic way.

6.2.5.
Idea 3 (explained for GQs)

Let

Now let

Let

X\not\cong X^{\prime}

Same definition as in the previous item.

6.4.
Isomorphisms between spans/perps and lines

If we allow dualities as isomorphisms on the level of simple examples, then in some circumstances stars
can be isomorphic to lines, and perp geometries to dual perp geometries, etc. In this paper, we do not consider
dualities as isomorphisms, but that depends entirely on the taste of the reader (or of the problem which has to be handled).

On the other hand, allowing dualities as isomorphisms, one would have to deal with rather unnatural decompositions in the Grothendieck
ring which is to be constructed! And we would end up with a bad notion of Krull dimension (see section
11
for examples of dual generalized quadrangles with
different dimensions).

6.5.
Intrinsic automorphisms

In each of the cases considered above, we have a quick look at the
automorphism groups
of the objects in the various settings. We use the notation
used in the previous paragraphs.

6.5.1.
Partial ovoids

If
(\mathcal{O},X)
is a partial ovoid, then
\mathrm{Aut}(X)_{\mathcal{O}}
is the automorphism group of
(\mathcal{O},X)
. Note that we do not
mod out a possible kernel here — the kernel of the action of
\mathrm{Aut}(X)_{\mathcal{O}}
on

contains information about the embedding

\mathcal{O}\hookrightarrow X
.

6.5.2.
Lines, IDEA 2

If
(U,X)
is a line, then
\mathrm{Aut}(X)_{U}\Big/\mbox{kernel}
is the automorphism group of
(U,X)
in the setting of IDEA 2. The remark in §
6.5.1
about the kernel
does not hold here, due to the fact that we have required less information about the embedding in the defining data of a line.

If
X=U
, then
\mathrm{Aut}(U)_{U}
is isomorphic to the symmetric group on the points incident with
U
.

6.5.3.
Lines, IDEA 1

For all
Y\leq X
,
U
in
Y
as a full subgeometry, an automorphism of
Y
fixing
U
would define an automorphism of
U
. One must decide when
two such automorphisms
\alpha\in\mathrm{Aut}(Y)_{U}
and
\beta\in\mathrm{Aut}(Y^{\prime})_{U}
, where

and

is fully contained in
Y^{\prime}
, define the same
automorphism of

. One possible natural approach would be to say that they define the same such automorphism if
\alpha
and
\beta

agree on (some subset of)
Y\cap Y^{\prime}
.

So let
\mathcal{Y}
be the set of all geometries
Y
as above (supposed sufficiently linear), and for

, write that
Y\preceq Y^{\prime}
if

Y^{\prime}
is a subgeometry of

, and if we can restrict each element of
\mathrm{Aut}(Y)_{U}
to

. The latter property is needed to make

\Big(\mathcal{Y},\preceq\Big)
into a directed system through the restriction of automorphisms. Obviously, in general this system does not behave well: if we consider elements
\widetilde{Y}
and

\widetilde{Y^{\prime}}
in
\mathcal{Y}
, there is no obvious upper bound for both geometries in this setting. So we relax the definition to the following: we say that
\alpha
and
\beta
(notation of the previous paragraph)
are equal if they agree on

(as

). It follows that

(40)

\varinjlim_{\Big(\mathcal{Y},\preceq\Big)}\mathrm{Aut}(Y)_{U}\ \cong\ \mathrm{Aut}(U),

where the latter is the naive automorphism group of

(without seeing the embedding

).

In the case that

, we obtain the same automorphism group (and the same action, of course) as in the previous subsection.

So let

7.1.
Trace isomorphism

Say that
\mathbf{T}
and
\mathbf{T}^{\prime}
(both subgeometries of the aforementioned type (a), (b) or (c)) are
trace isomorphic
if their
trace geometries

\texttt{Tr}(\mathbf{T},X)
and
\texttt{Tr}(\mathbf{T}^{\prime},X)

are isomorphic as point-line incidence geometries. We distinguish two sub-types of such isomorphisms: on the one hand,
p
-trace isomorphisms
which
keep track of the lines of type (i) (so which map lines of type (i) to lines of type (i)), and general trace isomorphisms, which do not necessarily have this property.

Note that
p
-trace isomorphisms preserve the type of the geometry. For general trace isomorphisms, the question needs a more detailed analysis. In any case,
a geometry of type (b) contains a number of concurrent lines which meet all other lines, and if it is not a projective plane, they are they only lines with this property.
A geometry of type (c) contains two parallel classes of lines which meet all other lines, so such a geometry can never be isomorphic to a geometry of type (b).
It is less clear to me how these geometries compare to geometries of type (a), certainly in the infinite case. In the finite case, we could give a simple number theoretic argument to exclude possible trace isomorphisms. If a geometry of type (a) (living in a GQ of finite order
(s,t)
) would be isomorphic to a geometry of type (b) or (c) (living in a GQ of finite order
(a,b)
), then obviously
t+1
(the line size in type (a)), must equal
a+1
(since in type (b) or (c), there are lines of size
a+1
). In case of geometries of type (b), we also need that
a=b
for such an isomorphism to exist. Comparing the number of points of the geometry in case of type (b), we see that
s^{2}+1=(s+1)s+1
, which is false. In case of type (c), we have that
s^{2}+1=(s+1)^{2}
, which is also false.

The following question is obviously important.

Question 7.1
.

Are all trace isomorphisms
p
-trace isomorphisms?

I claim that the answer is negative. I will describe a general class of counter examples for geometries of type (b); suppose the base

Question 7.5
.

\alpha:\ X^{\prime}\ \hookrightarrow\ X^{\prime}

be a full embedding of

in itself. Then obviously

Consider a quadric in an infinite-dimensional real vector space

, with quadratic form

This yields a generalized quadrangle

More generally, say that

(both subgeometries of the aforementioned type) are

Note that it is not essential to identify the lines of (i) if one keeps track of the embedding

10.1.

and

are birationally equivalent.

Now let
X
be a generalized quadrangle of order
(u,u)
, and let
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
be full grids in
X
. We suppose that

(\Gamma,X)\cong(\Gamma^{\prime},X^{\prime})
. The scissor relations imply that
[X\setminus\Gamma]=[X\setminus\Gamma^{\prime}]
. As
X\setminus\Gamma
and

X\setminus\Gamma^{\prime}
are affine quadrangles, the property that they would be isomorphic (by an isomorphism
\alpha
), implies that
there is an isomorphism

(68)

\overline{\alpha}:X\mapsto X

which maps
\Gamma
to
\Gamma^{\prime}
, and which induces
\alpha
. So this would mean that
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
are isomorphic in a stronger sense
than the one we want to use.

11.
Krull dimension

For a generalized quadrangle
\Gamma
, or any other of the connected variety-like elements in
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
, we define the
Krull dimension
as
the supremum of the lengths
d
of maximal chains

(69)

\mathfrak{p}_{0}\subset\mathfrak{p}_{1}\subset\cdots\subset\mathfrak{p}_{d}=\Gamma,

where each
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
is a (nonempty) point in the Zariski topology, and inclusions are with respect to the inclusion of incidence geometries.

11.1.
Example

Let
\Gamma\cong\mathcal{Q}(5,q)
,
q
a prime power. Then a maximal chain is of the form

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{0}=\mbox{point}

\displaystyle\subset\mathfrak{p}_{1}=\mbox{full line}\subset\mathfrak{p}_{2}=\mbox{two distinct intersecting full lines}

\displaystyle\subset\mathfrak{p}_{3}=\mbox{full grid}\subset\mathfrak{p}_{4}=\mbox{$\mathcal{Q}(4,q)$-subGQ}\subset\mathfrak{p}_{5}=\Gamma.

So
\mathcal{Q}(5,q)
and
\mathcal{Q}(4,q)
have dimension
5
and
4
. It is also clear that any grid has dimension
3
.

11.2.
Example

Let
\Gamma\cong\mathcal{H}(3,q^{2})
,
q
a prime power. Then a maximal chain is of the form

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{0}=\mbox{point}

\displaystyle\subset\mathfrak{p}_{1}=\mbox{full line}\subset\mathfrak{p}_{2}=\mbox{full ideal perp geometry}\subset\mathfrak{p}_{3}=\Gamma.

So the dimension of
\mathcal{H}(3,q^{2})
is
3
, while on the other hand
\mathcal{H}(3,q^{2})
is isomorphic to the point-line dual of
\mathcal{Q}(5,q)
.

11.3.
Example

Let
q
be a prime power and consider
\Gamma\cong\mathbf{W}(q)
. If
q
is even, we know that
\mathbf{W}(q)\cong\mathcal{Q}(4,q)
, so its dimension is
4
.
On the other hand, if
q
is odd, a maximal chain is of the form

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{0}=\mbox{point}

\displaystyle\subset\mathfrak{p}_{1}=\mbox{full line}\subset\mathfrak{p}_{2}=\mbox{full ideal perp geometry}\subset\mathfrak{p}_{3}=\Gamma,

and so its dimension is
3
!

Theorem 11.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a thick generalized quadrangle. Then its dimension is at least
3
.

Proof
. We always have chains of the form

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{0}=\mbox{point}

\displaystyle\subset\mathfrak{p}_{1}=\mbox{full line}\subset\mathfrak{p}_{2}=\mbox{full ideal perp geometry}\subset\mathfrak{p}_{3}=\Gamma.

\blacksquare

Needless to say, there are many degenerate examples in lower dimensions.

11.4.
Affine discussion

In the case of affine examples, we can also use the affine list in section
5.4
to introduce a notion of Krull dimension.

Example

We construct an affine quadrangle
\underline{\Gamma}
be taking away a

-subGQ

\displaystyle\subset\mathfrak{p}_{1}=\mbox{full line in $\underline{\Gamma}$}\subset\mathfrak{p}_{2}=\mbox{two distinct intersecting full lines in $\underline{\Gamma}$}

\displaystyle\subset\mathfrak{p}_{3}=\mbox{full grid in $\underline{\Gamma}$}\subset\mathfrak{p}_{4}=\underline{\Gamma}^{\prime}\subset\mathfrak{p}_{5}=\underline{\Gamma}.

Here,

11.5.
Larger dimensions

We now describe an example of a thick generalized quadrangle of non-finite dimension. The details are contained in
[

. In that latter paper, a generalized quadrangle is described of order

Theorem 11.2

.

. By

, the longest possible chains we can theoretically have in a thick finite GQ

12.
Infinite
\ell
, and Lefschetz motives

Suppose
\Gamma
is an abstract grid with
\ell+1
points per line. Let
\ell
be finite. Define a geometry
P(N)
to have two intersecting lines, with
N
points per lines (
N
a positive integer).
Then we have the following identity in
K_{0}(\mathcal{Q}_{\ell})
:

(70)

\Big[\Gamma=(\ell+1)\times(\ell+1)-\mbox{grid}\Big]\ =\ \Big[P(\ell+1)\Big]\ +\ \Big[(\ell\times\ell)-\mbox{grid}\Big]

so that inductively we get that

(71)

\Big[\Gamma=(\ell+1)\times(\ell+1)-\mbox{grid}\Big]\ =\ \Big[P(\ell+1)\Big]\ +\ \Big[P(\ell)\Big]\ +\ \ldots\ +\ \Big[P(1)\Big].

Now let
\ell
be not finite, and let
m
be a positive integer. Then

(72)

\Big[\Gamma=(\ell+1)\times(\ell+1)-\mbox{grid}\Big]\ =\ \Big[P(\ell+1)\Big]\ +\ \Big[P(\ell)\Big]\ +\ \ldots\ +\ \Big[P(\ell+2-m)\Big]\ +\ \Big[(\ell+1-m)\times(\ell+1-m)-\mbox{grid}\Big].

If we argue that
P(\ell+1)\cong P(\ell)\cong\cdots\cong P(\ell+1-m)
, and likewise that
(\ell+a)\times(\ell+a)
-grids and
(\ell-b)\times(\ell-b)
-grids are isomorphic for finite
a
and
b
, then we
obtain

(73)

0=m\Big[P(\ell+1)\Big],

so that either we have zero divisors, or

\Big[P(\ell+1)\Big]=0
, or
m=0
for any positive integer
m
. In the latter case, we would wind up in a set-up for working in characteristic
1
, but that is not what we want,
obviously.

The problem lies deeper: for suppose that
\mathcal{U}
is a hyperbolic quadric in
\mathbb{P}^{3}(k)
, with
k
an infinite field. If we remove the closed set
P(|k|+1)
from
\mathcal{U}
in

K_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{k})
(with “fully embedded lines”), then
\mathcal{U}\setminus P(|k|+1)
is not isomorphic to
\mathcal{U}
as a projective variety, and the reason essentially is that if we consider
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
and
remove (example given) a point, we obtain
{\text{ Spec}}(k[X])
and not again a projective line over
k
. In general, if we remove a finite number of points from a projective line
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
, the obtained object is not isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
. If we were to remove an
infinite
number of points, the situation is very different of course — see the examples at the end of section
12.1
.

If we go back to
K_{0}(\mathcal{Q}_{\ell})
, we see that we need to mend this problem in one way or the other (for finite
\ell
no problem occurs). Essentially four “questions” arise:

(F1)

we want to see a difference between lines which come from generalized quadrangles in
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
, and lines which are pieces of such lines;

(F2)

the dual problem of (F1);

(F3)

we want to see a difference between grids which are full grids in generalized quadrangles in
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
, and grids which are pieces of such grids, and
abstract grids which are products of lines (be it of full lines, or pieces of lines);

(F4)

formulation of (F3) for higher products.

Since
K_{0}(\mathcal{Q}_{\ell})
is generated by isomorphism classes of elements of
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
, each line is “of geometric origin”: it is a piece of a full line
in a generalized quadrangle. This is not the case for grids: either they are of “geometric origin,” or they are a product of lines, but then the latter are of geometric origin. For that matter we introduce
parents
: if, for example, a grid is of geometric origin, then we know it comes with some embedding in a thick generalized quadrangle
\Gamma
; we call
\Gamma
a
parent
of the grid. Objects in
K_{0}(\mathcal{Q}_{\ell})
can obviously come with many parents.

In any case, these simple facts allow us to distinguish between the various types of lines and grids. And that is exactly how we do it. For example, we agree that
a fully embedded grid
\Gamma
in a thick generalized quadrangle
X
with
\ell+1
points per lines
can never be isomorphic to an abstract grid

\Gamma_{ab}
with
\ell+1
points per line, even
if they would happen to be isomorphic in the theory of IDEA 2:

(74)

\Big(\mathrm{Aut}(X)_{\Gamma},\Gamma\Big)\ \cong\ \Big((\mathrm{Sym}(\ell+1)\times\mathrm{Sym}(\ell+1))\rtimes C_{2},\Gamma_{ab}\Big).

More details can be found in section
12.2
.

12.1.
Geometric grids in geometric grids

The following result considers a grid which is fully embedded in a generalized quadrangle, and a subgrid which is fully embedded in a subquadrangle. In some circumstances,
such subgrids cannot exist, and such results are of obvious interest for the discussion in this section.

Theorem 12.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a full grid in a generalized quadrangle
X
of order
(\ell,\omega)
; then for finite
m
, the grid
\widehat{\Gamma}
which arises by removing
m
lines (and their points) from each
of the reguli, can not be a full grid in an ideal subquadrangle of
X
.

Proof
. Suppose by way of contradiction that
\widehat{X}
is a subGQ of
X
which contains
\widehat{\Gamma}
as a full grid. Let
U
be a line which was removed to
construct
\widehat{\Gamma}
. By projecting each point of
\widehat{X}
on
U
, we obtain a set of lines
S_{U}
in
\widehat{X}
which forms a spread in the latter quadrangle.
Obviously, the lines of
S_{U}
are precisely the
lines of
\widehat{\Gamma}
which are contained in one of its reguli. But that means that the line of the opposite regulus meet all the lines of
S_{U}
, which is an obvious
contradiction.
\blacksquare

Note that the statement remains true if we allow
m
to be not finite.

Obviously the ideal property is crucial in the proof of the latter theorem. In the next theorem, we will not ask idealness. We will also generalize the numeric assumption.

Theorem 12.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a full grid in a generalized quadrangle
X
of order
(\ell,\omega)
, and consider a cardinal number
m
for which
\ell+1-m>m
; then the grid
\widehat{\Gamma}
which arises by removing
m
lines (and their points) from each of the reguli, can not be a full grid in a subquadrangle of
X
.

Proof
. Let
\widehat{X}
be a hypothetical subquadrangle of
X
which contains
\widehat{\Gamma}
as a full grid.
Suppose that
U
is a line which is not contained in
\Gamma
, and let
S_{U}
be the set of points which are incident with
U
but not contained in
\widehat{X}
. Let
V
be a line of
\Gamma\setminus\widehat{\Gamma}
, and suppose that
U\neq V
. Let
\mathcal{U}
be the set of lines of
\Gamma^{\prime}
which meet
V
. By projecting each line of
\mathcal{U}
on
U
, we immediately see that the intersection points of such lines with
V
, must be collinear with points in
S_{U}
. As the set of these intersection points has size
|\mathcal{U}|=\ell+1-m
, and as
\ell+1-m>m=|S_{U}|
, we conclude that triangles appear. This ends the proof of the statement.

\blacksquare

If we remove the condition
\ell+1-m>m
, the statement of the last theorem is no longer true. For example, consider a thick orthogonal quadrangle
\mathcal{Q}
defined in a projective space

\mathbb{P}^{n}(k)
, with
k
a field, by a quadratic form. By field reduction (to a field
f
of the same size as
k
), we can obtain a thick subquadrangle which is also orthogonal, say
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}
. As
each line in an orthogonal quadrangle is regular, any full grid
\widehat{\Gamma}
in
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}
lies in a full grid
\Gamma
of
\mathcal{Q}
, and with the notation of Theorem
12.2
, we have
that
\ell=|k|
, and
\ell+1-m=|f|+1
, so that
m=|k|=|f|
. There are many such examples.

Note that we can take
f
isomorphic to
k
(for example, let
k:=\mathbb{Q}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
with the variables
x_{i}
indexed by positive integers, and let
f:=\mathbb{Q}(x_{2},x_{3},\ldots)
). In this case,
we have that

(75)

\Gamma\cong\Gamma^{\prime},

so even if
\Gamma^{\prime}
is a piece of
\Gamma
, it can still be isomorphic to
\Gamma
(and the same can be said for the projective
k
-lines which are the lines of
\Gamma
).

12.2.
Further additive laws

Motivated by the discussion in the previous section, we impose the following extra rules on isomorphisms between degenerate elements of
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
, in case
\ell
is not finite. In the finite case,
the rules are trivial.

L1

A line
U
minus a finite nonzero number of points is not isomorphic to
U
itself.

L2

A grid
\Gamma
with a finite nonzero number of full lines removed is not isomorphic to
\Gamma
itself.

L3

General form
: an element

itself.

12.3.
Calculus of the empty line

Suppose
L
is a full (“proper”) line in some element
X
in
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
, and define
L^{e}:=L\setminus L^{*}
. As
L^{*}
is a full subgeometry of
L
(which is considered as a point set without further structure), we have

(76)

\Big[L\Big]\ :=\ \Big[L^{e}\Big]\ +\ \Big[L^{*}\Big].\\

Let
\mathcal{S}
be any other object in
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
which contains points. Then the collinearity graph of
L^{e}
is empty, so the product
L^{e}\otimes S
seems to be ill defined.
On the other hand, we have that

(77)

\Big[L^{e}\otimes S\Big]\ =\ \Big[(L\setminus L^{*})\otimes S\Big]\ =\ \Big[L\otimes S\Big]\ -\ \Big[L^{*}\otimes S\Big].\\

If
S^{*}
is the point set of
S
, and if we assume for the sake of convenience that
S^{*}
is finite, then obviously

(78)

\Big[L\otimes S\Big]\ -\ \Big[L^{*}\otimes S\Big]\ =\ \Big[(L\otimes S)\setminus(L^{*}\otimes S)\Big]\ =\ \Big[\coprod_{s\in S^{*}}L^{e}\Big]\ =\ |S^{*}|\cdot\Big[L^{e}\Big].\\

By Remark
12.3
below,
L^{e}\cong N^{e}
for all lines
L,N
in the setting of IDEA 2. In fact, even through a unique isomorphism. So

(79)

\Big[N^{e}\otimes S\Big]\ =\ |S^{*}|\cdot\Big[N^{e}\Big]\ =\ |S^{*}|\cdot\Big[L^{e}\Big]\ =\ \Big[L^{e}\otimes S\Big],

hence this class is independent of the empty line chosen. In any case, when constructing
L^{e}\otimes S
,
L^{e}
takes away (“resolves”) the points of
S
, while
duplicating itself
|S^{*}|
times.

Now suppose that
S=M\setminus M^{*}
, another empty line (and coming from the full proper line
M
in some element
Y\in\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
). Then

(80)

\Big[L^{e}\otimes S\Big]\ =\ \Big[(L\setminus L^{*})\otimes(M\setminus M^{*})\Big]\ =\ \Big[L\otimes M\Big]\ -\ \Big[L^{*}\otimes M\Big]\ -\ \Big[L\otimes M^{*}\Big]\ +\ \Big[L^{*}\otimes M^{*}\Big]\ =\ \Big[\mbox{\Large$\varnothing$}\Big].\\

Combining the laws (
78
) and (
80
) in more general expressions, one indeed also has a well-defined product in
K_{0}(\mathcal{Q}_{\ell})
if empty lines are involved.

It is very important to remark that in some expressions of type
L\setminus A
in
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
, when base extension (from
\ell
to
\nabla
) is defined for
L
and
A
such as in the examples which we will later
describe in section
13
, it is possible that while in
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
we have an empty line, still
(L\otimes_{\ell}\nabla)\setminus(A\otimes_{\ell}\nabla)
is not empty — as if an empty line is defined over “the empty field.” (One can for instance
easily adapt the examples in section
5.9
for this purpose.) This is yet another motivation for defining empty lines.

Remark 12.3
.

Note that
\mathrm{Aut}(L^{e})
is isomorphic to
\{\mathrm{id}\}\cong\texttt{S}_{0}
(so

Theorem 13.1

Suppose
\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}}
is a scheme defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}}
. Let
\cup X_{j}
be an open cover of
\mathcal{X}
such that
the

s are spectra
{\text{ Spec}}(A_{j})
of
\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}}
-algebras. Let
A
be any such
\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}}
-algebra. Let
\mathcal{X}_{\ell}:=\mathcal{X}\otimes_{{\text{ Spec}}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}})}{\text{ Spec}}(\ell)
and

A_{\ell}:=A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}}}\ell
. Then for each prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
of
A
,
we have that
\mathrm{Aut}(\ell/\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}})
acts transitively on the set of prime ideals of
\mathcal{X}_{\ell}
over
\mathfrak{p}
.

Proof.
First of all, note that for any
j\in T
, we have that
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{j}}/\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}})
acts transitively on the set of prime ideals of
A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}}}\mathbb{F}_{p^{j}}

over
\mathfrak{p}
, since
\mathbb{F}_{p^{j}}/\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}}
is a finite Galois extension. So, if
T
is a finite set,
\ell
is also finite, and the statement is true. So we may suppose that

T
is not finite.
Also, let
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}})
; then
\varphi
acts on the
prime ideals of
A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}}}\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}
. If
n\preceq m
in
T
, and
\varphi
sends
\mathfrak{p^{\prime}}
to
\varphi(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime})
, where
\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}
is a prime
ideal of
A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}}}\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}
, then it sends
\mathfrak{p}\cap(A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}}}\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}})
to
\varphi(\mathfrak{p})\cap(A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}}}\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}})
(action of
\pi_{nm}(\varphi)
).

Now let
\mathfrak{p}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{p}_{2}
be prime ideals in
A_{\ell}
over
\mathfrak{p}
. For each
r\in T
, we have that
\mathfrak{p}_{1}\cap(A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}}}\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}})
and

\mathfrak{p}_{2}\cap(A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}}}\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}})

\mathfrak{p}
, so there is an element
\varphi_{r}
sending the former prime ideal to the latter.
Now consider an element

(86)

\overline{\varphi}:=\Big(\widetilde{\varphi_{r}}\Big)_{r\in T}\in\mathrm{Aut}(\ell/\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}})=\varprojlim_{T}\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{u}}/\mathbb{F}_{p^{i}})

, making

it follows that

sends

13.4.
Base extension: an example

In this section we explain an example of base extension of nonclassical GQs over fields containing some given field
\mathbb{F}_{2^{r}}
, which allows us to pass to an infinite subfield
\ell
of an algebraic closure of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{i}}
. We then use the action of a suitable Galois group on the points of the topology of the quadrangle over
\ell
, so as to perform a
kind of Galois descent, to define new points and closed sets of the initial finite GQs.

We first need some terminology.

13.5.
Ovals and quadrangles

An
oval
of an axiomatic projective plane
\mathbb{P}
(of order at least
2
), is a set of points
\mathcal{O}
such that each line meets
\mathcal{O}
in at most two distinct points, and such that each
point
x
of
\mathcal{O}
is incident with precisely one line which does not meet
\mathcal{O}
in any other point (such a line is called a
tangent line
of
\mathcal{O}
at that point). If
\mathbb{P}
is finite of order
n
,
the second defining property can be re-phrased as:
|\mathcal{O}|=n+1
. Still in the case where
n
is finite, and assuming that
n
is odd furthermore,
the latter quantity is the maximal number of points
for a point set of
\mathbb{P}
in which no three distinct points are incident with the same line. If
n
is even, any set
\mathcal{O}
of size
n+1
with this property can be extended to a set
of size
n+2
still having this property; this is because one can show that the
n+1
tangents of
\mathcal{O}
meet in one and the same point of the plane, which we call the
nucleus
of
\mathcal{O}
.
A set of
n+2
distinct points
\mathcal{H}
in
\mathbb{P}
, still assumed of finite even order
n
, such that any line meets
\mathcal{H}
in
0
or
2
distinct points, is called a
hyperoval
.
In the general case, a
hyperoval

\mathcal{H}
is a set of points in an axiomatic projective plane of order
n\geq 2
, such that any line which is incident with one point of
\mathcal{H}
, is
incident with precisely one other point of
\mathcal{H}
.

Now let
\mathcal{H}
be a hyperoval of a projective plane
\mathbb{P}^{2}(k)
over a field
k
(in general, this does not force
k
to have characteristic
2
). Embed

\mathbb{P}^{2}(k)
as a hyperplane in
\mathbb{P}^{3}(k)
. Let
\eta
be one distinguished point of
\mathcal{H}
(so that it is the nucleus of the oval
\mathcal{H}\setminus\{\eta\}
).
Now we define the following point-line incidence structure,
\Gamma(\mathcal{H}\setminus\{\eta\},\eta)=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathbf{I})
.

The elements of
\mathcal{P}
are:

(1)

the points of
\mathbb{P}^{3}(k)\setminus\mathbb{P}^{2}(k)
;

(2)

the planes in
\mathbb{P}^{3}(k)
which meet
\mathbb{P}^{2}(k)
in a line containing
\eta
(and hence an other point of
\mathcal{H}
);

(3)

a symbol
(\infty)
.

Lines come in two guises:

(a)

lines of
\mathbb{P}^{3}(k)
that meet
\mathbb{P}^{2}(k)
in a point of
\mathcal{H}\setminus\{\eta\}
;

(b)

the points of
\mathcal{H}\setminus\{\eta\}
.

Incidence works as follows: the symbol
(\infty)
is incident with all lines of type (b); points of type (1) are incident with the lines of type (a) in which they
are contained as subspaces; finally, points of type (2) are incident with the one line of type (b) they contain as subspaces, and with all the lines of type (b)
which they contain as subspaces.

The one proves that
\Gamma(\mathcal{H}\setminus\{\eta\},\eta)
is a GQ of order
(|k|,|k|)
.

13.5.1.
Direct limit of ovals

The starting point for our construction is the following result of Segre.

Theorem 13.2

(Segre)
.

Let
i
and
h
be positive integers such that
\mathrm{gcd}(i,h)=1
, and let
q=2^{h}
. Then the set of points in
\mathbb{P}^{2}(q)
given
by homogeneous coordinates

(88)

\{(1:t:t^{2^{i}})\ |\ t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}\ \cup\ \{(0:0:1)\}

is an oval
\mathcal{O}(i,h)
with nucleus
\eta=(0:1:0)
.

If
\mathrm{gcd}(i,h)\neq 1
, then the set
\{(1:t:t^{2^{i}})\ |\ t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}\cup\{(0:0:1),(0:1:0)\}
does not form a hyperoval; see Hirschfeld
[
13
, §8.4, Corollary 3]
.

Each of the ovals constructed from Theorem
13.2
has as nucleus
\eta=(0:1:0)
(in planes represented over different fields, that is to say).

Now define a direct system of fields, as follows. First of all, fix one positive integer
i>1
(for the sake of convenience, this could be some prime). Now let
\mathcal{S}(i)

be the set of all positive integers
r
such that
\mathrm{gcd}(r,i)=1
. We define a directed set
(\mathcal{S}(i),\preceq)
as follows:
a\preceq b
if
a
divides
b

(which happens if and only if
\mathbb{F}_{2^{a}}
is a subfield of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{b}}
). We now consider the family
\{\mathcal{O}(i,h)\ |\ h\in\mathcal{S}(i)\}
. If
u\preceq v
,
then define the natural embeddings

(89)

\begin{cases}&\iota^{\mathcal{O}}_{uv}:=\mathcal{O}(i,u)\ \hookrightarrow\ \mathcal{O}(i,v),\\
&\iota^{\mathbb{F}}_{uv}:=\mathbb{F}_{2^{u}}\ \hookrightarrow\ \mathbb{F}_{2^{v}},\\
&\iota^{\mathbb{P}}_{uv}:=\mathbb{P}^{2}(2^{u})\ \hookrightarrow\ \mathbb{P}^{2}(2^{v}).\end{cases}

Obviously
\Big(\mathcal{O}(i,u),\iota_{uv}^{\mathcal{O}}\Big)
is a direct system over
\mathcal{S}(i)
, as well as
\Big(\mathbb{F}_{2^{u}},\iota_{uv}^{\mathbb{F}}\Big)
and
\Big(\mathbb{P}^{2}(2^{u}),\iota_{uv}^{\mathbb{P}}\Big)
.

Theorem 13.3
.

We have that
\varinjlim\mathcal{O}(i,u)
is an oval of
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\ell)
, with nucleus
(0:1:0)
; here,
\ell=\varinjlim\mathbb{F}_{2^{u}}
and
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\ell)=\varinjlim\mathbb{P}^{2}(2^{u})
.

Proof.
We only need to verify that
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}:=\varinjlim\mathcal{O}(i,u)
is an oval in
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\ell)
with nucleus
(0:1:0)
; the rest is obvious.
Note that in
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\ell)

(90)

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\ =\ \bigcup_{r\in\mathcal{S}(i)}\mathcal{O}(i,r),

where the homogeneous coordinates are chosen with respect to the same base in each of the planes
\mathbb{P}^{2}(2^{u})
.

Now consider any point
x
of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
, and suppose
L\mathbf{I}x
is a line of
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\ell)
which does not contain
(0:1:0)
, nor any other point of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
. Note that

(0:1:0)
is contained in any of the planes
\mathbb{P}^{2}(2^{h})
.
Let
y\neq x
be any other point incident with
L
. By eq. (
90
), there is some
m\in\mathcal{S}(i)
such that
(0:1:0)
,
x
and
y
are points of
\mathbb{P}^{2}(2^{m})
.
So
L
is also a line of
\mathbb{P}^{2}(2^{m})
. Since
\mathcal{O}(i,m)
is an oval with nucleus
(0:1:0)
, we conclude that
L
contains at least one point of
\mathcal{O}(i,m)\cup\{(0:1:0)\}
besides

x
, contradiction. We conclude that any line which meets
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
in at least one point, contains at least one other point of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\cup\{(0:1:0)\}
.
By a similar argument, it follows that any line which meets
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
in at least one point, contains precisely two distinct points of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\cup\{(0:1:0)\}
.
The statement is proved.

\blacksquare

13.5.2.
The quadrangles

Passing to the corresponding GQs, we obtain a direct system of GQs
\Gamma(\mathcal{O}(i,u),\eta)
over the directed set
\mathcal{S}(i)
, and

(91)

\varinjlim\Gamma(\mathcal{O}(i,u),\eta)=\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},\eta).

Note that
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},\eta)=\cup_{u\in\mathcal{S}(i)}\Gamma(\mathcal{O}(i,u),\eta)
.

Although
\ell
is not algebraically closed, it comes as close to being to algebraically closed as the property that the sets
\mathcal{O}(i,r)
should all be ovals — and
the sets of
\Gamma(\mathcal{O}(i,h),\eta)
be elements of
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbb{F}_{2^{h}}}
— allows. So we view
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},\eta)
as a synthetic analogon
of a base extension to an algebraically closed field of a given scheme over a finite field.

13.5.3.
Galois action and enriching

Now define
\underline{{\text{ Spec}}}(\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},\eta))
as before. In the context of the example under review, we can “enrich” the Zariski topology of the finite quadrangles

\Gamma(\mathcal{O}(i,h),\eta)
via the one of
\underline{{\text{ Spec}}}(\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},\eta))
, as follows.

CLOSED POINTS

First of all, consider the closed points in
\underline{{\text{ Spec}}}(\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},\eta))
. Following our definition of Zariski topology, we have three types of closed points
in
\underline{{\text{ Spec}}}(\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},\eta))=:\overline{\Gamma}
: the symbol
(\infty)
, the planes which meet
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
in a tangent line (“planar points”), and
the points of
\mathbb{P}^{3}(\ell)\setminus\mathbb{P}^{3}(\ell)
(“affine points”). Recall that in our context
\ell
plays the
role of algebraic closure
of the finite fields in consideration.

Consider
\Gamma(\mathcal{O}(i,h),\eta)
. Let
T:=\{n\cdot h\ |\ n\in\mathbb{Z},n>0\}\cap\mathcal{S}(i)
, and note that

(92)

\varinjlim_{T}\mathbb{F}_{2^{u}}=\varinjlim_{\mathcal{S}(i)}\mathbb{F}_{2^{u}}=\ell.

Descent on affine points.
The closed points of affine type of
\Gamma(\mathcal{O}(i,h),\eta)
are given by
\mathrm{Aut}(\ell/\mathbb{F}_{2^{h}})
-orbits in the affine point set of
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},\eta)
(so in the set

\mathbb{P}^{3}(\ell)\setminus\mathbb{P}^{2}(\ell)
).

Note that
\mathbb{P}^{3}(2^{h})\setminus\mathbb{P}^{2}(2^{h})
is a subset (if its elements are seen as singletons): they are the fixed points of the Frobenius automorphism

(93)

F:\ \mathbf{x}\mapsto\mathbf{x}^{2^{h}},

where
\mathbf{x}
is a point of
\mathbb{P}^{3}(\ell)\setminus\mathbb{P}^{2}(\ell)
expressed with homogeneous coordinates, and
F
acts on these coordinates.

Descent on planar points
. Points of this type are
\mathrm{Aut}(\ell/\mathbb{F}_{2^{h}})
-orbits of planar points of
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},\eta)
; after introducing homogeneous coordinates, elements
of
\mathrm{Aut}(\ell/\mathbb{F}_{2^{h}})
act on the points of the corresponding planes as in the case of affine points, and the planar points of
\Gamma(\mathcal{O}(i,h),\eta)
are singled
out by the Frobenius automorphism in a similar way.

The symbol
(\infty)
. We do not define a nontrivial descent on this point. The reason is obvious: it should be considered as a rational point of
\Gamma(\mathcal{O}(i,h),\eta)
, and
so constitutes a trivial orbit under
\mathrm{Aut}(\ell/\mathbb{F}_{2^{h}})
.

OTHER PRIME IDEALS

Other prime ideals in the new setting are defined in a similar way. Consider for instance a full subgrid in
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},\eta)
; theoretically, such a
grid could be of several different types, but the generic type is the following: consider a plane
\delta
in
\mathbb{P}^{3}(\ell)
which meets
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
(precisely) in two different points
u
and
v
;
then
\delta
defines such a grid. The lines of the grid are the lines
u
and
v
of the quadrangle, and the lines of
\delta
which meet
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
precisely in
u
or
in
v
(so they are not lines of
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\ell)
); the points are the affine points in
\delta
, and the planes of
\mathbb{P}^{3}(\ell)
which are different from
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\ell)
which
contain either
u\eta
or
v\eta
.

Consider
\mathcal{O}(i,h)
for some fixed
i
and
h
. Then note that with the notation of above, we could have that two elements, resp. one element, resp. no elements in
\{u,v\}
are contained in
\mathcal{O}(i,h)
(for instance,
\delta
could have an empty intersection with
\mathcal{O}(i,h)
). So different types of grids arise. On each such grid we can then consider the action of
\mathrm{Gal}(\ell/\mathbb{F}_{2^{h}})
(keeping Theorem
13.1
in the back of our mind).
Similar remarks can be made about, e. g., lines arising from enriching (see also the next section).

NEW CLOSED SETS

13.5.4.
Lines

If we look at generic lines (those not incident with
(\infty)
), then we look at all such lines of
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},\eta)
— call the set of all such lines
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
— and consider the action of
\mathrm{Aut}(\ell/\mathbb{F}_{p^{h}})
on
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
. (Note that
\mathrm{Aut}(\ell/\mathbb{F}_{p^{h}})
also acts on the points of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
— see the next section.)
Then the
\mathrm{Aut}(\ell/\mathbb{F}_{p^{h}})
-orbits in
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
define the new lines at level
\mathbb{F}_{p^{h}}
. We can single out the lines of
\Gamma(\mathcal{O}(i,h),\eta)
which are not incident with
(\infty)
as those which are fixed by each element of
\mathrm{Aut}(\ell/\mathbb{F}_{p^{h}})
; similarly we can single out the lines over field extensions of
\mathbb{F}_{p^{h}}
. We can work in the same way for other prototypes of closed sets, making distinction between “rational sets” and “orbit sets.”

One question which remains to be considered is the following variation:

Question 13.4
.

Let
\mathbf{I}
be an ideal geometry
(
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
)
; how does
C(\mathbf{I})
look like in the extended framework?

In case of schemes, ideals (varieties) are not defined by their rational points: an ideal corresponds to a set of equations which remains the same after base extension. In our case, an analogue to an ideal such as
(X^{2}+1)
cannot be given (if we imagine to be working over
\mathbb{R}
), as we only work with “points coming from Incidence Geometry” (here: rational points). Instead, we should be
given
the ideals
(X+i)
and
(X-i)
plus the action of
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
to define
(X^{2}+1)
as an orbit. And once an ideal geometry is given (over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
), matters are more complicated. We propose the following variation:

PROCEDURE
. The geometry
\mathbf{I}
is given over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
(as a point-line subgeometry of
\Gamma(\mathcal{O}(i,h),\eta)
). Over
\ell
, we need to consider all point-line subgeometries
\widetilde{\mathbf{I}}
of
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},\eta)
which only contain elements of
\mathbf{I}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and which are on the other hand in some sense
contained in
\mathbf{I}
once
\mathbf{I}
is considered over
\ell
.

(noting that
\mathbf{I}
is also a point-line subgeometry of
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},\eta)
). We first define
\overline{\mathbf{I}}=\mathbf{I}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\ell
, the base extension of
\mathbf{I}
to

\ell
. We impose the following rules to obtain
\overline{\mathbf{I}}
.

C1

Lines
. If
U
is a line in
\mathbf{I}
, it is full over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
by definition. Over
\ell
,
U\otimes_{\ell}\ell
also becomes a full line in
\overline{\mathbf{I}}
.

C2

Ideal perps
. As in the case of full lines, ideal perps in
\mathbf{I}
remain ideal perps over
\ell
in
\overline{\mathbf{I}}
.

C2.B

Non-ideal perps
. Suppose
\mathcal{G}
is a non-ideal perp in
\mathbf{I}
with base point
x
.
Let
V
be the set of all full (thin or thick) subGQs in
\mathbf{I}
(with an order) containing
x
. Then
\mathcal{G}\otimes\ell
is the union of all perp extensions
performed in all
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\in V
in which
\mathcal{G}\cap\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is ideal (following (C2) and (C3) below), so that we obtain

\bigcup_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\in V,\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{G}\ \mbox{ideal in}\ \mathcal{S}^{\prime}}\Big(\mathcal{G}\cap\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\Big)\otimes\ell.

The set of lines
W\mathbf{I}x
, where
W
is a line in
\mathcal{G}
which is not contained in an element
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\in V
for which
\mathcal{G}\cap\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is ideal in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, remains unchanged, and is extended following (C1).

C3

Subquadrangles
. Generalizing (C1) and (C2), each full subquadrangle
Y
(both thick and thin) which is contained in
\mathbf{I}
(over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
), becomes a full subquadrangle
of
\overline{\mathbf{I}}
over
\ell
, under the following agreement:
Y\otimes_{\ell}\ell
is the generalized quadrangle generated by the set of full lines over
\ell
of
Y
. (So by definition,
\overline{\mathbf{I}}
contains
Y\otimes_{\ell}\ell
.)

By (C3), full grids become full grids.

Now consider again any ideal geometry
\mathbf{I}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Then
\overline{\mathbf{I}}
defines a closed set
C(\overline{\mathbf{I}})
(over
\ell
), and using descent we obtain a closed set
\underline{C(\overline{\mathbf{I}})}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Note that the action of
\texttt{Gal}(\ell/\mathbb{F}_{q})
is well defined, since it acts on
\widetilde{\Gamma}
(so images of ideal geometries under elements of
\texttt{Gal}(\ell/\mathbb{F}_{q})
are again subgeometries of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
). In short, we have the following diagram:

(94)

\mathbf{I}\ \ \xrightarrow{\otimes_{\ell}}\ \ \overline{\mathbf{I}}\ \ \xrightarrow{\text{in}\ \underline{{\text{ Spec}}}(\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},\eta))}\ \ C(\overline{\mathbf{I}})\ \ \xrightarrow{\text{descent by}\ \texttt{Gal}(\ell/\mathbb{F}_{q})}\ \ C(\mathbf{I})=\underline{C(\overline{\mathbf{I}})}.

13.5.5.
Comparison to schemes

Fix a field
k=\mathbb{F}_{2^{h}}
, and let
i
be a positive integer such that
\mathrm{gcd}(i,h)=1
. Then observe that
\mathcal{O}(i,h)\cup\{(0:0:1)\}
is the set of
k
-rational points of
the algebraic curve
\mathcal{C}
defined by the equation

(95)

Y^{2^{i}}=ZX^{2^{i}-1}.

Let

be, as before, the limit

{\text{ Proj}}\Big(\ell[X,Y,Z]/(Y^{2^{i}}-ZX^{2^{j}-1})\Big)

13.5.6.
General picture for GQs coming from ovals

The example given in this section easily generalizes to other GQs coming from ovals. We leave the details to the reader.

13.5.7.
General recipe for enriching the Zariski topology

Of course one has to be cautious with this enriching procedure, since it
depends on the class in which the GQ is considered
.

Let
h
be a positive odd integer, and let
q=2^{h}
. Then the set of points in
\mathbb{P}^{2}(q)
given

13.6.
Variation

Consider a nonclassical Kantor-Knuth generalized quadrangle
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{K}(q,n,\gamma)
(
\gamma\neq\mathrm{id}
). As we have seen, it is the union of
\mathcal{Q}(4,q)
-subGQs (in that each point and line is in at least one such subGQ).

Question 13.5
.

Can we endow each
\mathcal{Q}(4,q)
with the classical Zariski topology
(
where we see
\mathcal{Q}(4,q)
as a projective variety
)
, such that the subspace topology induced on each intersection of
\mathcal{Q}(4,q)
-subGQs is the same
(
and hence yielding a topology on
\mathcal{S}
)
?

Note that for arbitrary
\mathcal{Q}(4,q)
-subGQs
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
contained in the same subGQ-orbit
\Omega_{i}
, and which intersect in a grid
\Gamma
, there is no automorphism of
\mathcal{S}
which maps
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
to
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
and which leaves each point and line of
\Gamma
invariant (we explore this property further in this paragraph). (We do not claim that the existence of such automorphisms is necessary for obtaining the exact same topology on
\Gamma
whether we start from
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
or
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
, but if it would exist, the property would come for free in this specific case.) In fact, since we have the different subGQ-orbits
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, there is even no
\alpha\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{S})_{\Gamma}
sending
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
to
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
when

\mathcal{Q}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
are picked in respectively
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
. So we could only look at
\Omega_{1}
for that matter. Here is a more
detailed analysis: in
\Omega_{1}
only two different
\mathcal{Q}(4,q)
-subGQs contain
\Gamma
— say
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
. The (nontrivial) involution
\sigma_{1}
which fixes
\mathcal{S}_{1}
elementwise stabilizes
\mathcal{S}_{2}
, and the same property holds for the involution
\sigma_{2}
which fixes
\mathcal{S}_{2}
elementwise. The group
\epsilon:=\langle\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\rangle
has size
4
and is the full group of automorphisms of
\mathcal{S}
which fixes
\Gamma
elementwise. It also fixes each of
\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2}
.
For any
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\in\Omega_{2}
, there is only one element in
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
(which also lives in
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{S})_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}
) which fixes
\Gamma
pointwise, so since no element in
\Omega_{2}
is fixed elementwise by a nontrivial automorphism of
\mathcal{S}
, it follows that
|{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}^{\epsilon}|=2
. So even in the orbits
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, our idea cannot work.

Now suppose again that
\mathcal{Q}_{1}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{2}=\Gamma
is a grid. Let
S_{1}=x^{\perp}\cap\Gamma
with
x
a point in
\mathcal{Q}_{1}\setminus\Gamma
; is there a point
y\in\mathcal{Q}_{2}\setminus\Gamma
such that

(98)

y^{\perp}\cap\Gamma\ =\ S_{1}?

From this perspective, it seems even more pessimistic: there are precisely
q+1

\mathcal{Q}(4,q)
-subGQs
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
which intersect two-by-two in
\Gamma
, and one can show that inside each
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, we have that for each point
z\not\in\Gamma
there is exactly one second point
z^{\prime}\not\in\Gamma
such that

(99)

z^{\perp}\cap\Gamma\ =\ {z^{\prime}}^{\perp}\cap\Gamma.

But by
[
22
, section 1.2.4]
the maximum number of points of
\mathcal{S}
in
S_{1}^{\perp}
is
q+1
. So there is no way that in general, closed sets such as
x^{\perp}\cap\Gamma
(in
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
) can be obtained in the same way through
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
. We have met this situation several times in the course of this paper: the local geometry induced by
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
on
\Gamma
and the local geometry induced by
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
can in principle be different. In this specific classical case, we know that there is an isomorphism

\gamma:\mathcal{Q}_{1}\mapsto\mathcal{Q}_{2}

for which
\Gamma^{\gamma}=\Gamma
(
\gamma
is not assumed to be in
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{S})
, of course), so we also know the induced geometries are isomorphic. If, more generally,
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
would merely be assumed to be isomorphic nonclassical quadrangles meeting in the common full subgrid
\Gamma
, with both
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
a subquadrangle of some larger quadrangle, then there is no need at all that the induced geometries on
\Gamma
by projection from external points in
\mathcal{Q}_{i}\setminus\Gamma
as above, be isomorphic.

Especially in the infinite formulation of this discussion, it might be easy enough to construct such examples.
