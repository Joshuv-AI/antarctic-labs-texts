Preferential attachment and vertex arrival times
Benjamin Bloem-Reddylabel=e1]benjamin.bloem-reddy@stats.ox.ac.uk, Peter Orbanzlabel=e2]porbanz@stat.columbia.edu

Abstract
We study preferential attachment mechanisms in random graphs that are parameterized by (i) a constant bias affecting the degree-biased distribution on the vertex set and (ii) the distribution of times at which new vertices are created by the model. The class of random graphs so defined admits a representation theorem reminiscent of residual allocation, or “stick-breaking” schemes. We characterize how the vertex arrival times affect the asymptotic degree distribution, and relate the latter to neutral-to-the-left processes. Our random graphs generate edges “one end at a time”, which sets up a one-to-one correspondence between random graphs and random partitions of natural numbers; via this map, our representation induces a result on (not necessarily exchangeable) random partitions that generalizes a theorem of Griffiths and Spanó. A number of examples clarify how the class intersects with several known random graph models.

The term
preferential attachment
describes generative mechanisms for random graph models
that select the terminal vertices of a new edge with probability biased by the vertex degrees.
These models come in many shapes and guises

[
4

often motivated by their ability to generate (and hence explain) power law distributions.
Degree-biased selection is a form of size bias
[

and this interplay between size-biasing and power laws is not confined to random graph models, but
also encountered in random partitions, which are used in population genetics,
machine learning, and other fields
[

,
16
,
10
, e.g.]
.
In partition models, power laws arise as heavy-tailed distributions of block sizes.
Size-biased sampling as such, however, need not result in a power laws:
The most basic form of
size-biased sampling from a countable number of categories is a type of Pólya urn with
an unbounded number of colors, or, equivalently, the one-parameter Chinese
restaurant process
[
36
]
. It does not generate a power law.
To obtain power laws, plain size-biased sampling can be modified in two ways:

(i)

By biasing the size-biased probability of each category downward.

(ii)

By forcing new categories to arrive at a faster rate than that induced by plain size-biased sampling.

An example is the two-parameter Chinese restaurant process with parameter
(\alpha,\theta)
,
which modifies the Chinese restaurant process with a single parameter
\theta
—a model that corresponds
to plain size-biased sampling—by effectively (i) damping the size bias by a constant offset
\alpha
, and (ii) increasing the rate at which new categories arrive.
An example of (ii) is the Barabási–Albert random graph model, in which vertices
arrive at fixed, constant time intervals; if these times were instead determined at random by size-biased sampling, intervals would grow over time.

The premise of this work is to study preferential attachment mechanisms in random graph models
by explicitly controlling these two effects:

(i)

The attachment probability, proportional to the degree
\deg_{k}
of each vertex
k
, is biased
by a constant offset as
{\deg_{k}-\,\alpha}
.

(ii)

Vertex arrival times are taken into account, by explicitly conditioning the generation process on a (random or non-random) sequence of given times.

The result is a class of random graphs parametrized by the offset
\alpha
and a sequence
t
of vertex

(\alpha,t)
-graph can be generalized by randomizing the arrival times,
i.e. to an
(\alpha,T)
-graph for a random sequence

Preferential attachment models that constantly bias the attachment probability

[

24

{\alpha\in(-\infty,1)}

The effects (i) and (ii) are not independent, and in models with a suitable exchangeability

In this sense, (ii) can provide more control over the model than (i).

3
Degree asymptotics

Let
G
be an
(\alpha,t)
-graph, and
G_{n}
the subgraph given by its first

edges. The

of

is the vector
\mathbf{D}(n)=(\deg_{k}(n))_{k\geq 1}
, where vertices are ordered by appearance. Denote by
m_{d}(n)
the number of vertices in
G_{n}
with degree
d
. The
empirical degree distribution

\displaystyle(p_{d}(n))_{d\geq 1}:=|\mathbf{V}(g_{n})|^{-1}(m_{d}(n))_{d\geq 1}

is the probability that a vertex sampled uniformly at random from

has degree

power laws and related properties.

3.1
Linear and sub-linear regimes

As will become clear in the next section, the scaling behavior of
(\alpha,t)
-graphs is the result of products of the form

\displaystyle W_{j,k}=\prod_{i=j+1}^{k}(1-\Psi_{i})\quad\text{as}\quad k\to\infty\;,

(16)

where
(\Psi_{j})_{j>1}
are as in (
5
). In particular, two regimes of distinct limiting behavior emerge. To which of the two regimes an
(\alpha,t)
-graph belongs is determined by whether or not
W_{j,k}
converges to a non-zero value as
k\to\infty
.

We consider
(\alpha,t)
-graphs that satisfy the following assumption:

\displaystyle|\mathbf{V}(G_{n})|/n^{\sigma}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{\small a.s.}}\mu_{\sigma}^{-\sigma}\quad\text{ for some }\quad 0<\sigma\leq 1\quad\text{ and }\quad 0<\mu_{\sigma}<\infty\;.

(17)

Slower vertex arrival rates (e.g., logarithmic) result in graphs that are almost surely dense (see
Section

3.4
), and as such exhibit less interesting structural properties. For example,
in order to generate power law distributions in
(\alpha,t)
-graphs, the asymptotic arrival rate must be super-logarithmic, which follows from work on exchangeable random partitions and can be read from
[
36
, Chapter 3]
.

For a growing graph sequence satisfying the assumption (
17
), consider the limiting average degree,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\bar{d}_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{2n}{\mu^{-\sigma}_{\sigma}n^{\sigma}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{2}{\mu^{-\sigma}_{\sigma}}n^{1-\sigma}\;.

The average degree is almost surely bounded if
\sigma=1
, which we call the
linear
regime; for
\sigma\in(0,1)
, the
sub-linear
regime, it diverges. This is a consequence of
Proposition

5
below: For a graph
G_{n}
on
k(n)
vertices, the probability that the end of edge
n+1
is attached to vertex
j
is equal to
\Psi_{j}W_{j,k(n)}
, which results in vertex
j
participating in a constant proportion of edges if and only if
W_{j,k(n)}
is bounded away from zero as
n
grows large.

5
.

For fixed
\alpha\in(-\infty,1)
and
t\in\mathbb{T}
such that (
17
) is satisfied for some
\sigma\in(0,1]
, let
W_{j,k}
be as in (
16
). Then for each
j\geq 1
,
W_{j,k}
converges almost surely as

to some random variable

, which is non-zero if and only if
\sigma<1
.

5
.

For slower vertex arrivals (e.g.logarithmic) or when the limiting number of vertices is finite,
W_{j,k(n)}
also converges to a non-zero value.

3.2
Limiting joint distributions of degree sequences for given arrival times

The previous section suggests that the limit of the scaled degrees should depend on the random variables
(\Psi_{j})_{j>1}
. Indeed, for any
(\alpha,t)
-graph

\displaystyle\bigl(n^{-1}\deg_{j}(n)\bigr)_{1\leq j\leq r}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{\small a.s.}}\bigl(\xi_{j}\bigr)_{1\leq j\leq r}\quad\text{where}\quad\xi_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny d}}}{{=}}\Psi_{j}\prod_{i=j+1}^{\infty}(1-\Psi_{i})\;,

(18)

and

are as in (

Griffiths and Spanò 2007
showed that relative degrees with such a limit uniquely characterize exchangeable Gibbs partitions among all exchangeable partitions; if the random partition

is exchangeable, that result applies to

(see

). For a general
{(\alpha,t)}
-graph,

need not be exchangeable, and indeed there are examples for which

converges to zero, in which case

does, as well.
In such cases, one may ask more generally whether a finite, non-zero limit

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{\infty}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-1/\gamma}\mathbf{D}(n)\;,

exists for an appropriate scaling exponent
\gamma
.
Theorem

6
establishes that this is true for
(\alpha,t)
-graphs.

6
.

Let
G
be an
(\alpha,t)
-graph for some
\alpha\in(-\infty,1)
and

. Then (

) holds. If

is such that (

) holds with

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{t_{j}}{j}=\mu\in(1,\infty)\;.

Then for every

, there exists a positive, finite constant

, and positive random variables

\displaystyle M_{r}(t)n^{-r/\gamma}\deg_{1}(n)\dotsm\deg_{r}(n)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{\small a.s.}}\zeta_{1}\dotsm\zeta_{r}\quad\text{where}\quad\gamma=\frac{\mu-\alpha}{\mu-1}\;.

The mixed moments also converge: For any

\displaystyle M_{\bar{p}}(t)\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-\bar{p}/\gamma}\deg_{1}(n)^{p_{1}}\dotsm\deg_{r}(n)^{p_{r}}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\zeta_{1}^{p_{1}}\dotsm\zeta_{r}^{p_{r}}\bigr]\;.

(19)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\bigl(\zeta_{j}\bigr)_{1\leq j\leq r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny d}}}{{=}}\bigl(\Psi_{j}\prod_{i=j+1}^{r}(1-\Psi_{i})\bigr)_{1\leq j\leq r}\;,

where
\Psi_{1}=1
and
(\Psi_{j})_{j>1}
are as in (

In the sub-linear regime, (

[
23
]
for exchangeable Gibbs partitions (though the proof uses different methods). In the linear regime, the mixed moments of the scaled degrees also converge to those of products of independent beta random variables.
However, the result does
not
completely describe the joint distributions, due to the presence of
the unknown scaling terms

. These terms depend on the moments

and on

, and express the randomness remaining, for large

, in

Section

4
provides stronger results in several cases for which these terms are

3.3
Neutrality

It was noted in
Section

2
that the map

from graphs to partitions translates results on graphs into results on partitions. Conversely, one can transfer properties from partitions to graphs. A sequence

of random variables is

\displaystyle X_{1},\frac{X_{2}}{X_{1}+X_{2}},\ldots,\frac{X_{j}}{\sum_{i=1}^{j}X_{i}},\ldots

\Pi
is an exchangeable partition,

show that the limiting relative block sizes of

is an exchangeable Gibbs partition. If so, the random graph

has a limiting degree sequence

that is NTL. Due to the representation in
Theorem

2

beyond the exchangeable case:

7
.

The limiting degree sequence

of an

3.4
Sparsity and power law degrees

Suppose
G_{n}
is the subgraph of an
(\alpha,T)
-graph
G
, given by the first
n
edges. Since
G_{n}
is finite,
one can sample a vertex uniformly at random from its vertex set
and report its degree
D_{n}
. One can then ask whether the sequence of random degrees
D_{n}
converges in distribution
to some limiting variable
D
. We show in this section that that is indeed the case for
(\alpha,T)
-graphs, under some regularity conditions. We also show how the degree distribution is related to the sparsity, or, equivalently, the edge density, of
(G_{n})
.

The sequence
{(G_{n})}
is defined to be
\varepsilon
-
dense
if

\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim\sup}\frac{n}{|\mathbf{V}(G_{n})|^{\varepsilon}}=c_{\varepsilon}>0\quad\text{for some}\quad\varepsilon\geq 1\;.

(21)

If
\varepsilon<2
, the graph sequence is typically called
sparse
; when
\varepsilon\geq 2
, the sequence is
dense
. Note that
{\varepsilon>2}
is only possible for multigraphs.
The level of sparsity follows from
\sigma
: Graph models in the linear regime
correspond to
\varepsilon=1

[
9
,
5
,
2
]
; graph models in the sub-linear regime with
\sigma>\frac{1}{2}
have appeared in the literature
[
12
,
41
,
14
,
11
]
, with
1<\varepsilon<2
. See
Section

4
for examples.

For functions
a
and
b
, we use the notation

\displaystyle a(n)\overset{\scriptscriptstyle{n\uparrow\infty}}{\mathrel{\scalebox{1.25}[1]{$\sim$}}}b(n)\quad:\Leftrightarrow\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}a(n)/b(n)\to 1\;.

The sequence
(G_{n})
has
power law degree distribution
with exponent
\eta>1
if

\displaystyle p_{d}(n)=\frac{m_{d}(n)}{|\mathbf{V}(G_{n})|}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}p_{d}\overset{\scriptscriptstyle{d\uparrow\infty}}{\mathrel{\scalebox{1.25}[1]{$\sim$}}}L(d)d^{-\eta}\quad\text{for all large $d$}\;,

(22)

for some slowly varying function
L(d)
, that is,
{\lim_{x\to\infty}L(rx)/L(x)=1}
for all
{r>0}

[
6
,
20
]
.

In the sub-linear regime, the degree distribution follows from results due to Pitman and Hansen
[
36
, Lemma 3.11]
, see also
[
22
]
, on the limiting block sizes of exchangeable random partitions (see
Section

4.2
for more details). In particular, if (
17
) is satisfied by an
(\alpha,t)
-graph

for
\sigma=\alpha\in(0,1)

\Pi
and a positive, finite random variable
S
such that
{|\mathbf{V}(\Phi(\Pi_{2n}))|/n^{\alpha}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{\small a.s.}}S}
,

and

. The limiting degree distribution is

p^{\alpha}_{d}\quad=\quad\alpha\frac{\Gamma(d-\alpha)}{\Gamma(d+1)\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\quad\overset{\scriptscriptstyle{d\uparrow\infty}}{\mathrel{\scalebox{1.25}[1]{$\sim$}}}\quad\frac{\alpha}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}d^{-(1+\alpha)}\;.

(23)

In the linear regime,
\sigma=1
, with limiting mean interarrival time
\mu_{1}
. We show (see
Section

A.4
) that the resulting limiting degree distribution is a generalization of the classical Yule–Simon distribution (which corresponds to

19

p^{\gamma}_{d}\quad=\quad\gamma\frac{\Gamma(d-\alpha)\Gamma(1-\alpha+\gamma)}{\Gamma(d+1-\alpha+\gamma)\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\quad\overset{\scriptscriptstyle{d\uparrow\infty}}{\mathrel{\scalebox{1.25}[1]{$\sim$}}}\quad\gamma\frac{\Gamma(1-\alpha+\gamma)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}d^{-(1+\gamma)}\;,

(24)

where
{\gamma:=\frac{\mu_{1}-\alpha}{\mu_{1}-1}}
, as in (
19

The tail behavior of the two distributions (
23

24
) partition the range of possible values of the power law exponent, as summarized by the following theorem.

8
.

Let
G
be a random
(\alpha,T)
-graph for some
\alpha\in(-\infty,1)
and
T\in\mathbb{T}
. If

\displaystyle T_{j}j^{-1/\sigma}\xrightarrow[j\to\infty]{\text{\small a.s.}}\mu_{\sigma}\quad\text{ for some }\quad\sigma\in(0,1]\quad\text{ and }\quad 1<\mu_{\sigma}<\infty\;,

then

\varepsilon

, assume that

,
{\text{Var}(\Delta_{j})<\infty}

Then the degree distribution converges asymptotically,

\displaystyle\frac{m_{d}(n)}{|\mathbf{V}(G_{n})|}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{\small p}}\begin{cases}p^{\alpha}_{d}&\text{ if }\sigma=\alpha\in(0,1)\\

which for large

\displaystyle\eta=\begin{cases}1+\alpha&\in(1,2)\quad\;\;\text{ if }\sigma=\alpha\in(0,1)\\

The distributions (

This representation can be refined further: The proof of

3.5
A note on almost surely connected graphs

Suppose one requires each graph in the evolving sequence
(G_{n})
drawn from an
(\alpha,T)
-graph to be
almost surely connected. That holds if and only if
{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{\tiny\rm 2}}}

A simple way to generate

\displaystyle T_{2}=2\Delta_{2},\quad T_{k}=T_{k-1}+2\Delta_{k}\quad\text{for}\quad k>2\;.

does not affect the degree asymptotics.
In the linear regime, the change has noticeable affect. For example, suppose the variables
\Delta_{k}
above are drawn
i.i.d. from some probability distribution on
\mathbb{N}_{+}
with mean

.
Then by

8
, the limiting degree distribution has power law exponent
\eta_{\text{\tiny\rm 2}}=1+\frac{2\mu-\alpha}{2\mu-1}
. For

, the upper limit of

4.1
Barabási–Albert trees and graphs with constant interarrival time

The basic

generates a random graph as follows: With parameter
{d\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}

At each step, select

in (

d

The Barabási–Albert model can be expressed in terms of a sequence
(L_{1},L_{2},\ldots)
with given arrival times
as follows:
Start, say, with a graph consisting
of a single vertex and
d
self-loops. Thus,
{t_{k}=1}
and
{L_{1}=\ldots=L_{2d}=1}
. Each new
vertex requires
2d
stubs, so
{t_{k+1}=t_{k}+2d}
. At time
{t_{k+1}-1=(2d)k}
,
just before the
{(k+1)}
-st vertex arrives, the graph

{G_{kd}=(L_{n})_{n\leq 2kd}}
has
k
vertices and

edges.
For
{t_{k}\leq n<t_{k+1}}
, we then set

L_{n}=k\quad\text{ if }n\text{ odd }\qquad\text{ and }\qquad L_{n}\sim P_{0}({\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,};G_{(k-1)d})\quad\text{ if }n\text{ even.}

The single vertex with self-loops is chosen as a seed graph here only to keep notation
reasonable. More generally, any graph with
k
vertices and
n
edges can be encoded
in the variables
{L_{1},\ldots,L_{2n}}
and the first
k
arrival times.

When

, the result is a tree (with a loop at the root). When

, the above sampling scheme does not produce an
(\alpha,t)
-graph.
However, the following modified sampling scheme produces an

-graph with

for all

and for

The modified sampling scheme differs from the basic preferential attachment model in that it updates the degrees after each step, allows loops, and does not require that each vertex begin with

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(n-k_{n}\alpha)\Gamma(k_{n}\bar{\alpha}-\bar{m}+\bar{p})}{\Gamma(n-k_{n}\alpha+\bar{p})\Gamma(k_{n}\bar{\alpha}-\bar{m})}\prod_{i=1}^{\bar{m}}\frac{\Gamma(k_{n}-i/\bar{\alpha})\Gamma(r+1+(\bar{p}-i)/\bar{\alpha})}{\Gamma(k_{n}+(\bar{p}-i)/\bar{\alpha})\Gamma(r+1-i/\bar{\alpha})}\dotsm

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\frac{\Gamma(r\bar{\alpha})}{\Gamma(r\bar{\alpha}+\bar{p})}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\frac{\Gamma(D_{j,n}-\alpha+p_{j})}{\Gamma(D_{j,n}-\alpha)}

\displaystyle=X_{n}(p,t)\frac{\Gamma(r\bar{\alpha})}{\Gamma(r\bar{\alpha}+\bar{p})}\prod_{i=1}^{\bar{m}}\frac{\Gamma(r+1+\frac{\bar{p}-i}{\bar{\alpha}})}{\Gamma(r+1-\frac{i}{\bar{\alpha}})}\;,

where

\displaystyle X_{n}(p,t):=\biggl(\frac{n}{2m}\biggr)^{-\bar{p}\frac{\bar{m}}{\bar{\alpha}}}\prod_{j=1}^{r}D_{j,n}^{p_{j}}(1+O(n^{-1}))\;.

Therefore, algebraic manipulations of
Z_{n}(p,t)
show that for large
n
:

\displaystyle Z_{n}(p,t)=X_{n}(p,t)\mathbb{E}[\prod_{i=1}^{\bar{m}}\mathcal{G}_{r+1-\frac{i}{\bar{\alpha}}}^{\bar{p}/\bar{\alpha}}]/\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}_{r\bar{\alpha}}^{\bar{p}}]

(52)

\displaystyle Z_{n}(p,t)=X_{n}(p,t)\mathbb{E}[\prod_{i=1}^{\bar{m}}\mathcal{G}_{1-\frac{i}{\bar{\alpha}}}^{\bar{p}/\bar{\alpha}}]/\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}_{r\bar{\alpha}}^{\bar{p}}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\mathcal{B}^{\bar{p}}_{j\bar{\alpha}-\bar{m},\bar{m}}]

(53)

\displaystyle Z_{n}(p,t)=X_{n}(p,t)\mathbb{E}[\prod_{i=1}^{\bar{m}}\mathcal{G}_{1-\frac{i}{\bar{\alpha}}}^{\bar{p}/\bar{\alpha}}]/\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}_{r\bar{\alpha}-\bar{m}}^{\bar{p}}\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}\mathcal{B}^{\bar{p}}_{j\bar{\alpha}-\bar{m},\bar{m}}]

(54)

\displaystyle Z_{n}(p,t)=X_{n}(p,t)\mathbb{E}[\prod_{i=1}^{\bar{m}}\mathcal{G}_{1-\frac{i}{\bar{\alpha}}}^{\bar{p}/\bar{\alpha}}]\mathbb{E}[\prod_{j=2}^{r}\mathcal{B}^{\bar{p}}_{(j-1)\bar{\alpha},1-\alpha}]/\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}_{1-\alpha}^{\bar{p}}]\;.

(55)

On the other hand,
Lemma

20
establishes that
Z_{n}(p,t)
converges almost surely to the product of random variables
\xi_{1}^{p_{1}}\dotsm\xi_{r}^{p_{r}}
. Now, letting
p\setminus p_{j}
denote
p
with the
j
th element set to zero,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}^{p_{1}}\dotsm\xi_{r}^{p_{r}}]=\mathbb{E}[\lim_{n\to\infty}Z_{n}(p,t)]=\mathbb{E}[Z_{t_{r}}(p,t)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Z_{t_{r}-1}(p\setminus p_{r},t)]\frac{\Gamma(1-\alpha+\bar{p})\Gamma(r\bar{\alpha}-\bar{m})\Gamma((r-1)\bar{\alpha}+\bar{p}_{r-1})}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)\Gamma(r\bar{\alpha}-\bar{m}+\bar{p}_{r})\Gamma((r-1)\bar{\alpha})}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=2}^{r}\frac{\Gamma(1-\alpha+p_{j})\Gamma(j\bar{\alpha}-\bar{m})\Gamma((j-1)\bar{\alpha}+\bar{p}_{j-1})}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)\Gamma((j-1)\bar{\alpha})\Gamma(j\bar{\alpha}-\bar{m}+\bar{p}_{j})}=\mathbb{E}[\prod_{j=2}^{r}\Psi_{j}^{p_{j}}(1-\Psi_{j})^{\bar{p}_{j-1}}]\;.

By equating (
52
)–(
55
) to
\mathbb{E}[Z_{t_{r}}(p,t)]
, the results follow.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

15
.

Define
T_{1:r}:=(T_{1},T_{2},\dotsc,T_{r})
and

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{\alpha,\theta}(p,T_{1:r})

\displaystyle:=\frac{\Gamma(n+\theta)}{\Gamma(n+\theta+\bar{p})}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\frac{\Gamma(D_{j,n}-\alpha+p_{j})}{\Gamma(D_{j,n}-\alpha)}

(56)

\displaystyle=n^{-\bar{p}}\prod_{j=1}^{r}D_{j,n}^{p_{j}}(1+O(n^{-1}))\;.

(57)

The fact that
{\mathbb{E}[Z_{n+1}^{\alpha,\theta}(p,T_{1:r})\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}]=Z_{n}^{\alpha,\theta}(p,T_{1:r})}
shows that
Z_{n}^{\alpha,\theta}(p,T_{1:r})
is a nonnegative martingale for
n\geq T_{r}
.
Z_{n}^{\alpha,\theta}(p,T_{1:r})
can be bounded in
L_{2}
following an argument similar to that given in the proof of
Lemma

20
, and hence it converges almost surely to
\xi_{1}^{p_{1}}\dotsm\xi_{r}^{p_{r}}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-\bar{p}}\prod_{j=1}^{r}D_{j,n}^{p_{j}}\mid T_{1:r}]=\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}^{p_{1}}\dotsm\xi_{r}^{p_{r}}\mid T_{1:r}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(1-\alpha+p_{r})\Gamma(T_{r}+\theta)\Gamma(T_{r}-1-(r-1)\alpha+\bar{p}_{r-1})}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)\Gamma(T_{r}+\theta+p_{1})\Gamma(T_{r}-1-(r-1)\alpha)}\dots

\displaystyle\quad\times\prod_{j=2}^{r-1}\frac{\Gamma(1-\alpha+p_{j})\Gamma(T_{j}-j\alpha)\Gamma(T_{j}-1-(j-1)\alpha+\bar{p}_{j-1})}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)\Gamma(T_{j}-j\alpha+\bar{p}_{j})\Gamma(T_{j}-1-(j-1)\alpha)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{B}_{T_{r}-r\alpha,\theta+r\alpha}^{\bar{p}}\cdot\prod_{j=2}^{r}\Psi_{j}^{p_{j}}(1-\Psi_{j})^{\bar{p}_{j-1}}]\;,

from which the result follows.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

16
.

For an exchangeable Gibbs partition with
\alpha\in(0,1)
and a sequence of coefficients
v_{n,k}
, one may construct a martingale similar to that in (
56
). It is then straightforward to show that the scaled degrees converge almost surely to random variables
(\xi_{j})_{1\leq j\leq r}
, conditionally on
T_{1},\dotsc,T_{r}
. Conditioned on
T_{j}
,
\xi_{j}
has marginal moments

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\xi_{j}^{p}\mid T_{j}]=\frac{\Gamma(1-\alpha+p)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\bigl(1+p\frac{V_{T_{j}+1,j}}{V_{T_{j},j}}\bigr)^{-1}\;.

Kerov 2006
showed that the only exchangeable Gibbs partitions with coefficients that can be represented as a ratio
V_{n,k}=v_{k}/c_{n}
are those of the
\text{\rm CRP}(\alpha,\theta)
, in which case the product in the previous equation becomes independent of
j
.
See also
[
23
, Lemma 4.1]
, which also implies the result.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

18
.

Define for
p>-1
,

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{\beta}(p,T_{1:r})

\displaystyle:=\frac{\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(n+\bar{p}(1-\beta))}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\frac{\Gamma(D_{j,n}+p_{j})}{\Gamma(D_{j,n})}

\displaystyle=n^{-\bar{p}(1-\beta)}\prod_{j=1}^{r}D_{j,n}^{p_{j}}(1+O(n^{-1}))\;,

which is by construction a nonnegative martingale, bounded in
L_{2}
, for
n\geq T_{r}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-\bar{p}(1-\beta)}\prod_{j=1}^{r}D_{j,n}^{p_{j}}]=\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}^{p_{1}}\dotsm\xi_{r}^{p_{r}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(T_{r}+\bar{p})}{\Gamma(T_{r}+\bar{p}(1-\beta))}\prod_{j=2}^{r}\frac{\Gamma(1+p_{j})\Gamma(T_{j})\Gamma(T_{j}-1+\bar{p}_{j-1})}{\Gamma(T_{j}-1)\Gamma(T_{j}+\bar{p}_{j})}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{M}_{1-\beta,T_{r}-1}^{-\bar{p}(1-\beta)}]\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{B}^{\bar{p}}_{T_{r},(T_{r}-1)\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}}]\mathbb{E}[\prod_{j=2}^{r}\Psi_{j}^{p_{j}}(1-\Psi_{j})^{\bar{p}_{j-1}}]\;,

from which the result follows. The marginal identities result from altering the above martingale to contain only
D_{j,n}
, which is also a martingale. Similar treatment of the moments, along with the beta-gamma algebra and the identity (1.3) in
James 2015
yields the rest of the identities.
∎
