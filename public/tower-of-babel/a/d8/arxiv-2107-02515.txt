Correlation decay and markovianity in open systems
Marco Merkli

Abstract
A finite quantum system S {\rm S} is coupled to a thermal, bosonic reservoir R {\rm R} . Initial SR {\rm S}{\rm R} states are possibly correlated, obtained by applying a quantum operation taken from a large class, to the uncoupled equilibrium state. We show that the full system-reservoir dynamics is given by a markovian term plus a correlation term, plus a remainder small in the coupling constant λ \lambda uniformly for all times t ≥ 0 t\geq 0 . The correlation term decays polynomially in time, at a speed independent of λ \lambda . After this, the markovian term becomes dominant, where the system evolves according to the completely positive, trace-preserving semigroup generated by the Davies generator, while the reservoir stays stationary in equilibrium. This shows that (a) after initial SR {\rm S}{\rm R} correlations decay, the SR {\rm S}{\rm R} dynamics enters a regime where both the Born and Markov approximations are valid, and (b) the reduced system dynamics is markovian for all times, even for correlated SR {\rm S}{\rm R} initial states.



1
Introduction

Open quantum systems are most commonly described by a tensor product Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm R}
(system and reservoir) and an interacting Hamiltonian

H_{\lambda}=H_{\rm S}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm R}+\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm S}\otimes H_{\rm R}+\lambda V

where
H_{\rm S}
and
H_{\rm R}
are the system and reservoir Hamiltonians,
\lambda
is a coupling constant, which we consider to be small in a suitable sense, and
V
is an interaction operator. In the literature, coupled
{\rm S}{\rm R}
systems are sometimes studied when the parts
{\rm S}
and
{\rm R}
are of comparable size. In the current work, we focus on the situation
\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}=N<\infty
and
\dim\mathcal{H}_{\rm R}=\infty
, and a reservoir Hamiltonian
H_{\rm R}
with continuous spectrum. In this sense, the reservoir
{\rm R}
is much larger than the system
{\rm S}
in our considerations. The dynamics of an initial state (density matrix)
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}
is given by the Liouville–von Neumann (or Schrödinger) equation

\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}^{t}=e^{-{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}\,e^{{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}

and the reduced system density matrix is obtained by taking the partial trace over the reservoir,

\rho_{{\rm S}}^{t}={\rm tr}_{\rm R}\,\big\{e^{-{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}\,e^{{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}\big\}.

Uncorrelated initial states.
In textbooks and research articles, most often the assumption is made that the initial state is uncorrelated, that is, of the product form
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}=\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{\rm R}
.
Then one can define the
dynamical map
acting on system density matrices,

{\mathcal{V}}_{t}:\rho_{\rm S}\mapsto\rho_{\rm S}^{t}.

\mathcal{V}_{t}
is the flow or propagator of the system dynamics and of course, it depends on the initial reservoir state
\omega_{\rm R}
. For example, if
\omega_{\rm R}=\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
is the reservoir equilibrium state at a given temperature
T=1/\beta
, then of course the system dynamics, and hence
\mathcal{V}_{t}
, will depend on
T
. Due to the interaction of
{\rm S}
and
{\rm R}
, mediated by the operator
V
, the state
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}^{t}
is generically correlated (not of product form) for
t>0
. This results in the violation of the group property,

\mathcal{V}_{t+t^{\prime}}\neq\mathcal{V}_{t}\mathcal{V}_{t^{\prime}}.

If the reservoir is not much influenced by the coupling to the system and initially in a stationary state under its own dynamics, then one might intuitively expect that
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}^{t}\approx\rho_{\rm S}^{t}\otimes\omega_{\rm R}
. This is called the
Born approximation
. If on top, one assumes that the reservoir loses its memory quickly, then one may expect the Markovian property to hold approximately,

\mathcal{V}_{t+t^{\prime}}\approx\mathcal{V}_{t}\mathcal{V}_{t^{\prime}}\quad\mbox{or}\quad\mathcal{V}_{t}\approx e^{t{\mathcal{L}}},

where the generator
{\mathcal{L}}=\mathcal{L}(\lambda)
depends implicitly on the initial reservoir state
\omega_{\rm R}
and
\mathcal{L}(0)=-{\rm i}[H_{\rm S},\cdot\,]
generates the uncoupled system dynamics. It has been known since the seventies (see the founding papers
[
10
,
11
,
42
]
) that in some generality, the Markovian approximation is valid in the weak coupling, or van Hove limit. This means that

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\sup_{0\leq\lambda^{2}t<a}\|\mathcal{V}_{t}-e^{t{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}(\lambda)}\|=0\qquad\mbox{for any $a>0$},

(1.1)

where the superoperator

{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}(\lambda)={\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}+\lambda^{2}{\mathcal{K}},\qquad{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}=-{\rm i}[H_{\rm S},\cdot\,]

(1.2)

is called the
Davies generator
. It generates the CPTP semigroup
e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\rm S}(\lambda)}

[
9
,
17
,
30
]
(completely positive trace preserving). The operator
\mathcal{K}
, a sum of a Hamiltonian plus a dissipative term, describes the influence of the bath to second order in perturbation
[
1
,
7
,
37
]
. From (
1.1
) we have an alternative expression for
\mathcal{K}
,

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\ \mathcal{V}_{\tau/\lambda^{2}}\,\circ\,e^{-\tau{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}/\lambda^{2}}=e^{\tau\mathcal{K}},\qquad\forall\tau\geq 0.

(1.3)

The shortcoming of (
1.1
) is that it guarantees the accuracy of the Markovian approximation only for bounded values of
\lambda^{2}t
. Said differently, (
1.1
) shows that the Markovian approximation is guaranteed to hold for
t\rightarrow\infty
only if at the same time, one takes
\lambda\rightarrow 0
in a way such that
\lambda^{2}t
stays finite. This deficiency of (
1.1
) was overcome in
[
25
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
, where it is proven, under suitable assumptions, that

\sup_{t\geq 0}\big\|\mathcal{V}_{t}-e^{t({\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}+\lambda^{2}\mathcal{K})}\big\|\leq C|\lambda|^{1/4},

(1.4)

provided
|\lambda|\leq\lambda_{0}
for some
\lambda_{0}>0
. The Markovian approximation is guaranteed to be accurate to
O(|\lambda|^{1/4})

uniformly in time

t\geq 0
, for all small, fixed
\lambda
. The error bound
|\lambda|^{1/4}
is derived for the least amount of regularity of the interaction operator necessary for the proof – under a more stringent (analyticity) assumption, it can be improved to
|\lambda|^{2}
(see
[
25
]
). In the recent work
[
8
]
it is shown that the validity of the Markovian approximation for arbitrarily large
finite
times does not imply the validity for all times. There the authors consider the zero temperature, dissipative Janes-Cummings model in the singular coupling limit. Given any time
t_{0}>0
, they explicitly design SR interactions (form factors
g(k)
) in such a way that the system (qubit) dynamics is
exactly Markovian
up to
t_{0}
, but for
t>t_{0}
the dynamics becomes non-Markovian. Even though this example uses the singular coupling limit (
g(k)
not square integrable), the result indicates that the passage from (
1.1
) to (
1.4
) is not a mere technicality. In an approach different from the above, a polymer expansion for initially factorized system-reservoir states was established in
[
13
]
.

Correlated initial states.
The assumption of initially uncorrelated SR states may not be physically realistic. It requires to bring together, at time
t=0
, a system and a reservoir which at earlier times did not interact (or which by some measurement or dynamical fluke would happen to be in a product state). One should then ask about the dynamics of correlated initial states, that is, ones which are not of product form, see
[
18
,
34
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
and the references in there. For a given correlated initial state
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}
, the reduced system dynamics
\rho_{\rm S}^{t}
is still well defined as above. However, one cannot define the dynamical map
\mathcal{V}_{t}
any longer, as different
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}
can have the same marginal
\rho_{\rm S}
(say, due to different
{\rm S}{\rm R}
correlations). Those different initial
{\rm S}{\rm R}
states evolve to states at time
t>0
which have different system marginals, which means that the initial marginal cannot be mapped consistently to the marginal at a later time. In our investigation, we
start
with a correlated initial state
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}
and describe its well defined reduced system dynamics
t\mapsto\rho^{t}_{\rm S}
. The opposite approach is also studied in the literature, starting with an initial system state
\rho_{\rm S}
, then mapping it to a
{\rm S}{\rm R}
state by an
assignment map

\rho_{\rm S}\mapsto\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}
. The initial correlation is then encoded in the assignment map, whose structure can be analyzed
[
2
,
15
,
16
,
36
,
41
]
.

Outline of main results.
We consider a class of open systems for which the Markov approximation (
1.4
) for uncorrelated initial states,
\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
, was proven in
[
32
]
. Here,
\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
is the thermal equilibrium state of the reservoir at temperature
T=1/\beta>0
.

1

1

This is not a necessary feature for our approach to work, one may also take non-equilibrium stationary states, as we will explain elsewhere.
Our main result, Theorem
2.2
, gives an expansion of the full
{\rm S}{\rm R}
dynamics with correlated initial states
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}
. It can be expressed as follows – see after Theorem
2.2
for more in-depth and precise statements. We show that

\rho^{t}_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}=\big(e^{t{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}(\lambda)}{\rm tr}_{\rm R}\{\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}\}\big)\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}+\chi(t,\lambda)+O(|\lambda|^{1/4}),

(1.5)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\rm S}(\lambda)
is the Davies generator (
1.2
) and
\chi(t,\lambda)
describes the dynamics of the
{\rm S}{\rm R}
correlations. The relation(
1.5
) is valid for all
|\lambda|\leq\lambda_{0}
, some
\lambda_{0}>0
, with a remainder independent of time
t\geq 0
. It is to be interpreted in a weak sense, that is, when applied to operators from a
{\rm S}{\rm R}
observable algebra
\mathcal{O}
, (
2.20
). The initial states are taken from a class of states obtained by applying (very general) quantum operations on the uncoupled
{\rm S}{\rm R}
equilibrium state
\rho_{{\rm S},\beta}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
(see (
2.25
)). We show the following results:

–

For
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}=\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
(with
\rho_{\rm S}
any system density matrix), we have
\chi(t,\lambda)=0
. This shows that
the Born approximation holds
and recovers the Markovianity result (
1.4
).

–

\chi(t,\lambda)
also vanishes when applied to observables
A_{\rm S}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm R}
of
{\rm S}
alone, for any initial
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}
. This means that the initial correlations only influence the dynamics of observables which involve the reservoir. In particular, this shows that
the Markovian approximation is also valid for correlated initial states
, and for all times
t\geq 0
.

–

In the presence of initial correlations, the Born approximation (
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}^{t}\approx\rho_{\rm S}^{t}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
) is obviously not correct for small times. However, we show that
\chi(t,\lambda)\sim 1/(1+t^{3})
, which implies that initial correlations decay, and after that, the
{\rm S}{\rm R}
state enters a regime where the Born approximation is valid, even for correlated initial states. This is a statement about the
full

{\rm S}{\rm R}
state dynamics. In contrast, as explained above, the
reduced
system state is approximated by the Markovian semigroup for all, even small, times.

Model and main results

2.1
Model

We consider an
N
-level system interacting with a reservoir modeled by a continuous family (field) of quantum harmonic oscillators, and we will largely follow the notation of
[
31
]
. The system and reservoir Hamiltonians are given by

H_{\rm S}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}E_{j}|\phi_{j}\rangle\langle\phi_{j}|\mbox{\quad and\quad}H_{\rm R}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\omega(k)a^{*}(k)a(k)d^{3}k

(2.1)

respectively, acting on the Hilbert space

{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}={\mathbb{C}}^{N}\otimes{\mathcal{F}}.

(2.2)

We often simply write
H_{\rm S}
for
H_{\rm S}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}
and so on. The
E_{j}
are the system energies and
\omega(k)\geq 0
is the frequency of the mode
k
(
\hbar=1
), which we take for definiteness to follow the dispersion
e.g.
of photons,

\omega(k)=|k|.

(2.3)

The energy eigenstates
\phi_{j}
form an orthonormal basis of the system Hilbert space
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
and the (momentum representation) creation and annihilation operators satisfy the canonical commutation relations
[a(k),a(\ell)]=0=[a^{*}(k),a^{*}(\ell)]
and
[a(k),a^{*}(\ell)]=\delta(k-\ell)
(Dirac delta
k,\ell\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
). The reservoir Hilbert space is the (Bosonic) Fock space

{\mathcal{F}}=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\,L_{\rm sym}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3n},d^{3n}k),

(2.4)

built over the single-particle space
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3},d^{3}k)
, the square integrable scalar valued functions. The full, interacting Hamiltonian is given by

H_{\lambda}=H_{\rm S}+H_{\rm R}+\lambda G\otimes\varphi(g),

(2.5)

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is the coupling constant,
G^{*}=G
is any hermitian system matrix and

\varphi(g)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big[a^{*}(g)+a(g)\big],\quad a^{*}(g)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}g(k)a^{*}(k)d^{3}k,\quad a(g)=[a^{*}(g)]^{*}

(2.6)

are the field, creation and annihilation operators, respectively.
The function
g(k)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3},d^{3}k)
in the interaction (
2.5
) is called the form factor – generally, when we consider
a^{*}(f)
,
a(f)
we also call
f
test functions.

The above is a paradigmatic model for open quantum systems. For
N=2
it is called the spin-Boson model. As presented here, it is the continuous mode (or thermodynamic, infinite-volume) limit of a system described by a discrete set of oscillators with Hamiltonian

H_{\rm R}^{\prime}=\sum_{k}\omega_{k}a^{*}_{k}a_{k}

(2.7)

and field operator in the interaction given by
\varphi^{\prime}(g)=\sum_{k}g_{k}a^{*}_{k}+{\rm h.c.}
In the theoretical physics literature, the limit of continuous values of
k
is often performed at the end of calculations of various expressions, such as transition probabilities. In this limit of a continuum of reservoir modes one can analyze irreversible dynamical effects (like time decay). In the present work, we start off directly with the reservoir having a continuum of frequencies, (
2.1
).

In contrast to the common assumption of initially uncorrelated system-reservoir states of the factorized form
\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\rho_{\rm R}
, we are concerned here with initial states obtained by correlating the system with the reservoir. The system Gibbs equilibrium density matrix at temperature

(2.8)

as a vector state

in the enlarged Hilbert space

as follows. For any

{\rm tr}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}\big(\rho_{{\rm S},\beta}A_{\rm S}\big)=\langle\Omega_{\rm S},\pi_{\rm S}(A_{\rm S})\Omega_{\rm S}\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{N}},

(2.9)

where
\pi(A_{\rm S})=A_{\rm S}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}

is a normalized vector (explicitly given in (
3.7
)). This purification representation is sometimes very helpful. For the continuous mode reservoir the situation is a bit more cumbersome. Its equilibrium state cannot be expressed as a density matrix acting on
\mathcal{F}
, because

is not trace class (

L^{2}_{0}=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3},d^{3}k)\cap L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3},|k|^{-1}d^{3}k).

(2.10)

For

we have

33

\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}(a(f))=\omega_{\rm{\rm R},\beta}(a^{*}(f))=0,\quad\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}\big(a^{*}(f)a(g)\big)=\langle g,(e^{\beta|k|}-1)^{-1}f\rangle,

where

It is very convenient to use a purification of

where

(2.22)

The

{\mathfrak{C}}={\rm Span}\big\{K_{1}\cdots K_{n}\ :\ n\in{\mathbb{N}},\ K_{j}\in{\mathcal{X}}\cup{\mathcal{P}}\big\}.

2.3
Initial states

We use the notation
\rho(A)={\rm tr}(\rho A)
for a density matrix
\rho
and an observable
A
. Let
K_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{C}
be Kraus operators satisfying

\rho_{{\rm S},\beta}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}\Big(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\nu}K^{*}_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}\Big)=1,

(2.24)

where
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We take initial states of the form

\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}(A)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\nu}\rho_{{\rm S},\beta}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}\big(K^{*}_{\alpha}AK_{\alpha}\big),\qquad A\in\mathcal{O}.

(2.25)

The summand of (
2.25
) is a short hand notation
for
\langle\Omega_{{\rm S}{\rm R},\beta,0},\pi(K^{*}_{\alpha})\pi(A)\pi(K_{\alpha})\Omega_{{\rm S}{\rm R},\beta,0}\rangle_{{\cal H}_{\rm GNS}}
. One may extend the results to
\nu=\infty
, see the remarks after Theorem
2.2
. The condition (
2.24
) guarantees that
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}
is properly normalized,
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}(\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul})=1
. The initial reduced system state is defined by the relation

\rho_{\rm S}(A_{\rm S})=\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}\big(A_{\rm S}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm R}\big),\quad\forall A_{\rm S}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{C}}^{N}).

(2.26)

The full
{\rm S}{\rm R}
state at time
t
is given by

\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}(e^{{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}Ae^{-{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\nu}\rho_{{\rm S},\beta}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}\big(K^{*}_{\alpha}\,e^{{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}Ae^{-{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}K_{\alpha}\big),\qquad A\in\mathcal{O}.

(2.27)

Technically, it is defined in a standard way as follows (see Section
3.2
). Use the Dyson series to define the dynamics
e^{{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}\cdot e^{-{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}
starting from the uncoupled dynamics
e^{{\rm i}tH_{0}}\cdot e^{-{\rm i}tH_{0}}
. Then apply the representation map
\pi
(
2.17
) to the Dyson series. One shows that the resulting series converges, and moreover, that the limit equals
e^{{\rm i}tL_{\lambda}}\pi(\cdot)e^{-{\rm i}tL_{\lambda}}
, where
L_{\lambda}
is a self-adjoint operator on
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm GNS}}
, called the
Liouville operator
. The summand of (
2.27
) is defined as

\rho_{{\rm S},\beta}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}\big(K^{*}_{\alpha}\,e^{{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}\cdot e^{-{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}K_{\alpha}\big)=\langle\Omega_{{\rm S}{\rm R},\beta,0},\pi(K_{\alpha}^{*})e^{{\rm i}tL_{\lambda}}\pi(\cdot)e^{-{\rm i}tL_{\lambda}}\pi(K_{\alpha})\Omega_{{\rm S}{\rm R},\beta,0}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm GNS}}}.

(2.28)

We prove the following result in Section
3.4
:

Proposition 2.1

The functional
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}(e^{{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}\cdot e^{-{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}})
given by (
2.27
) is a well defined state (positive, linear, normalized functional) on
\mathcal{O}
, for any choice of the
K_{j}\in{\mathfrak{C}}
.

One can show that
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}
is also defined (finite) on observables
O
which are products of polynomials
B\otimes a^{\sharp}(f_{1})\cdots a^{\sharp}(f_{n})
and operators
e^{{\rm i}\sum_{r=1}^{R}B_{r}\otimes a^{\sharp}(f_{r})}
with test functions
f_{j}\in L^{2}_{\rm obs}
. This is a larger class than
{\mathcal{O}}
. However, it will be more difficult to give a proof of our main result, Theorem
2.2
, for unbounded observables. In the proof, we use the time uniform estimate
\|e^{{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}Ae^{-{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}\|=\|A\|
, valid for
bounded
observables
A
. For unbounded
A
(say a product of creation and annihilation operators) one has to find time uniform bounds of
e^{{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}Ae^{-{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}
in a weak sense (on suitable functionals). This requires a more complicated analysis which we do not address it here.

The collection of initial states does not depend the state
\rho_{{\rm S},\beta}
in the definition (
2.25
). Indeed, suppose we had put an arbitrary system density matrix
\sigma_{\rm S}
in the place of
\rho_{{\rm S},\beta}
in (
2.25
). Then, since
\sigma_{\rm S}=K\rho_{{\rm S},\beta}K^{*}
for
K=\sqrt{\sigma_{\rm S}}\,(\rho_{{\rm S},\beta})^{-1/2}
, we obtain the same class of initial states because changing the system reference state simply amounts to a change in the Kraus operators. The same is not true for the role of
\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
in (
2.25
). If we replace there
\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
by
\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}(K^{*}\cdot K)
for some reservoir operator
K\in{\mathfrak{C}}
(bounded or unbounded), then we still obtain the same class of initial states. The set of such states
\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}(K^{*}\cdot K)
are called
normal states
w.r.t.
\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
, they form the
folium
of
\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
. However, not all states of the reservoir are of this form; for instance,
\omega_{{\rm R},\beta^{\prime}}
is not normal w.r.t.
\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
unless
\beta=\beta^{\prime}
. Normal states differ from each other only ‘quasilocally’. We refer to
[
6
,
20
]
for a more precise discussion of this point.

Example.
An admissible initial state is for instance

\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}=\Lambda(\rho_{{\rm S},\beta}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta})=\frac{1}{Z}\,e^{{\mathcal{E}}}\big(\rho_{{\rm S},\beta}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}\big)\,e^{{\mathcal{E}}^{*}},

where
Z
is a normalization factor and
{\mathcal{E}}=\sum_{j}B_{j}\otimes a^{*}(f_{j})+\sum_{k}D_{k}\otimes a(f_{k})
is an arbitrary expression linear in the creation and annihilation operators. This is a state in which
{\rm S}
and
{\rm R}
are entangled,
c.f.

[
40
]
.

2.4
The main result

Assumptions.

(A1)

Smoothness of the form factor.

We assume that the form factor
g(k)
in the interaction (
2.5
) is four times continuously differentiable in the raidal variable
|k|>0
and that
|g(k)|\leq C|k|^{-q}
for some
q>3/2
, for large enough
|k|
. Moreover, the infra-red behaviour of
g
is characterized as follows: There is a
\kappa_{0}>0
such that for
|k|\leq\kappa_{0}
, we have
g(k)=|k|^{p}h(k)
where either
p>3
and
h
is a four times continuously differentiable function, or
p=-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{3}{2},\tfrac{5}{2}
and
h(k)=h_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}
is constant or
h(k)=h(|k|^{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}
is real, radial and four times continuously differentiable.

(A2)

(a)

Fermi Golden Rule Condition
: The reservoir spectral density is defined as

J(\omega)=\tfrac{1}{2}\pi\omega^{2}\int_{S^{2}}|g(\omega,\Sigma)|^{2}d\Sigma,\qquad\omega\geq 0,

(2.29)

where
g(k)
is the form factor in the interaction (
2.5
) and the integral is over the (polar and azimuthal) angles. We assume that

\langle\phi_{m},G\phi_{n}\rangle J(|E_{m}-E_{n}|)\neq 0\qquad\mbox{for all system energies $E_{m}\neq E_{n}$},

(2.30)

where
\langle\phi_{m},G\phi_{n}\rangle
are the matrix elements of the coupling operator
G
(
2.5
) in the system energy basis.

(b)

Simplicity of resonance energies. We assume that the so-called
level shift operators

\Lambda_{e}
,
e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{0}=\{E_{m}-E_{n}\ :\ m,n=1,\ldots,N\}
, have simple eigenvalues. The
\Lambda_{e}
are explicit matrices describing the second order (
\lambda^{2}
) corrections to the energy differences
e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
, given in Section
3.6
.

The assumption (A1) on the form factor is more restrictive than
g\in L^{2}_{\rm obs}
(
c.f.
point (a) at the beginning of Section
2.2
) in that it requires a more stringent infrared behaviour (values of
p
) and the existence of one more derivative, relative to functions in
L^{2}_{\rm obs}
. The condition (
2.30
) ensures that the interaction does not suppress second order (
\lambda^{2}
) transition processes in S due to the coupling with R. The reservoir spectral density (
2.29
) governs these transitions since the coupling (
2.5
) is linear in the field operator. This linear form of the interaction is not necessary for our method to work – other interactions will lead to explicit conditions of effective coupling but are not expressed in terms of
J(\omega)
. The assumptions (A1) together with (A2a) guarantee that the coupled system-reservoir complex has a unique stationary state, the coupled equilibrium state, for small nonzero
\lambda
, see
[
28
]
. (A2b) is a simplifying assumption that can be quite easily removed by a slightly more cumbersome analysis.

Here is our main result.

Theorem 2.2
(SR dynamics for correlated initial states)

There is a constant
\lambda_{0}>0
such that if
|\lambda|<\lambda_{0}
, then the following holds.
Let
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}
be an initial system-reservoir state of the form (
2.25
) and let
\rho_{\rm S}
be its reduction to
{\rm S}
(
2.26
). Then for all
t\geq 0
,
A\in\mathcal{O}
,

\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}\big(e^{{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}Ae^{-{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}\big)=\big(e^{t{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}(\lambda)}\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}\big)(A)+\chi(\lambda,t,A)+R(\lambda,t,A),

(2.31)

where
{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}(\lambda)
is the Davies generator (
1.2
) and the remainder
R(\lambda,t,A)
satisfies

|R(\lambda,t,A)|\leq C(A)|\lambda|^{1/4}.

(2.32)

The dispersive term satisfies

\displaystyle\chi(\lambda,t,A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\qquad\mbox{if\ \ $\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}=\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}$}

(2.33)

\displaystyle\chi(\lambda,t,A_{\rm S}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm R})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(2.34)

\displaystyle|\chi(\lambda,t,A)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C(A)}{1+t^{3}},

(2.35)

and moreover,

|\chi(\lambda,t,A)-\big(\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}-\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}\big)(e^{{\rm i}tH_{0}}Ae^{-{\rm i}tH_{0}})|\leq C(A)\,|\lambda|.

(2.36)

The constants
C(A)
depend on
A
as well as the initial state
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}
(but are independent of
t,\lambda
). For
A=A_{\rm S}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm R}
we have
C(A_{\rm S}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm R})\leq c\|A_{\rm S}\|
with
c
independent of
A_{\rm S}
.

2.5
Implications

Theorem
2.2
has the following consequences.

(1)

Validity of the Born approximation for initially factorized states

For initial states
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}=\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
, where
\rho_{\rm S}
is any system density matrix, and for all
t\geq 0
,
A\in\mathcal{O}
, we have

\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}\big(e^{{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}Ae^{-{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}\big)=\big(e^{t{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}(\lambda)}\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}\big)(A)+O(|\lambda|^{1/4}).

(2.37)

This shows that if the system and reservoir are initially in a product state with the reservoir in equilibrium, then the total state stays (up to
O(|\lambda|^{1/4})
) of product form for all times, and the reservoir stays in its equilibrium state.

(2)

Validity of the Markov approximation for initially correlated states

Taking in (
2.31
) observables of the form
A=A_{\rm S}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm R}
shows the validity of the Markovian approximation, (
1.4
),
even for initially correlated
{\rm S}{\rm R}
states
. Namely, denoting by
\rho_{\rm S}^{t}
the reduction of the full state
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}=\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}(e^{{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}\cdot e^{-{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}})
at time
t
, and
\rho_{\rm S}=\rho_{\rm S}^{t=0}
, we have

\sup_{t\geq 0}\big\|\rho_{\rm S}^{t}-e^{t{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}(\lambda)}\rho_{\rm S}\big\|\leq C|\lambda|^{1/4},

(2.38)

for a constant
C
independent of
\lambda
, but generally depending on the initial state
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}
. (As
\rho_{\rm S}^{t}
is a finite-dimensional density matrix, it is not necessary to specify which norm we take in (
2.38
). We may take the trace norm.)

(3)

Decay of correlations and emergence of the Born approximation regime

The term
\chi
in (
2.31
) describes the evolution of the correlation between the system and reservoir. According to (
2.35
) it decays as
t^{-3}
. At
t=0
, (
2.31
) gives
\chi(\lambda,t=0,A)=\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}(A)-{\rm tr}_{\rm R}\{\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}\}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}+O(|\lambda|^{1/4})
, so if the initial state
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}
differs from
{\rm tr}_{\rm R}\{\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}\}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
then for small times, the correlation term
\chi(\lambda,t,A)
can dominate the Markovian term
(e^{t{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}(\lambda)}\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta})(A)
in (
2.31
). Whether, and by how much, this happens depends on the observable
A
– for example, as explained in the previous point, for
A=A_{\rm S}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm R}
we have
\chi(\lambda,t,A)=0
. To see which one, the Markovian or the correlation contribution, is dominant, we use the spectral decomposition
[
30
]

e^{t{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}(\lambda)}=\sum_{j}e^{{\rm i}t(e_{j}+\lambda^{2}a_{j})}P_{j},

(2.39)

where the
P_{j}
are spectral projections, the
e_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
are the Bohr energies of the system (differences of eigenvalues of
H_{\rm S}
) and
a_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
,
{\rm Im}a_{j}\geq 0
are the complex energy corrections (resonances) induced by the interaction with the reservoir. The Markovian dynamics (
2.39
) exhibits exponential time decay
\sim e^{-\lambda^{2}\gamma t}
in all directions
P_{j}
but one, which represents the projection onto the system equilibrium state at temperature
\beta
(and for which
e_{j}=a_{j}=0
). The decay (approach to equilibrium) is exponential at a rate
\propto\lambda^{2}
, which is very slow for small coupling
\lambda
. In contrast, the polynomial decay of the correlation term,
t^{-3}
, happens at a rate which is independent of
\lambda
, meaning that the constant in (
2.35
) does not depend on
\lambda
. (Indeed, that constant encodes the fact that the reservoir dynamics alone is dispersive away from the projection onto the reservoir equilibrium state.) It follows that for small coupling, the correlation term
\chi
can be dominant for small times, but as time increases, the Markovian term becomes dominant. Then a long time later (
\lambda
small), the exponentially decaying functions lie below the power decay function, and the correlation term is leading once again, even though by that time, both those terms are smaller than the remainder
O(|\lambda|^{4})
.

If the interacting
{\rm S}{\rm R}
complex is left to its own devices (no external influence) for sufficiently long, then according to (
2.31
), and since
\,{\rm e}^{t\mathcal{L}(\lambda)}\rho_{\rm S}
converges to
\rho_{{\rm S},\beta}\propto e^{-\beta H_{\rm S}}
, the
{\rm S}{\rm R}
state is approximately equal to the time-independent (uncoupled equilibrium) state
\rho_{{\rm S},\beta}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
. One may see this as an
a posteriori
justification for considering product initial states in certain circumstances.

Further remarks.

(a)

The power of the decay in (
2.35
) depends on the smoothness of the form factor
g
, (
2.5
), which in turn determines the decay speed of the reservoir correlation function
[
31
,
32
]
. We get higher powers
\sim t^{-n}
assuming that
g
is
(n+1)
times differentiable and that the test functions in
L^{2}_{\rm obs}
are
n
times differentiable. Under a certain analyticity condition on
g
(see
[
30
]
) and on the test functions in
L^{2}_{\rm obs}
, one can show that the time decay in (
2.35
) is exponential, with a
\lambda
-independent decay rate.

(b)

We cannot ascertain in general that the constants
C(A)
in Theorem
2.2
are bounded in the values of
t>0
for time-dependent observables of the form
A=e^{{\rm i}tH_{0}}A^{\prime}e^{-{\rm i}tH_{0}}
. This is so since the constants will depend on Sobolev norms of test functions appearing in the observables, that is, on their smoothness (the
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3},d^{3}k)
norm of their derivatives w.r.t.
|k|
). But
e^{{\rm i}tH_{\rm R}}W(f)e^{-{\rm i}tH_{\rm R}}=W(e^{{\rm i}|k|t}f)
has a test function
e^{{\rm i}|k|t}f(k)
that is rough (large
|k|
-derivative) for large
t
. Nevertheless, if
A=A_{\rm S}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm R}
, then
C(e^{{\rm i}tH_{\rm S}}A_{\rm S}e^{-{\rm i}H_{\rm S}t}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm R})\leq c\|A_{\rm S}\|
is bounded uniformly in time.

(c)

In the weak coupling regime one takes simultaneously
\lambda\rightarrow 0
and
t\rightarrow\infty
, keeping
\tau=\lambda^{2}t\in\mathbb{R}
fixed. By (
2.32
), (
2.35
) and the previous discussion point,

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}R\Big(\lambda,\tfrac{\tau}{\lambda^{2}},(e^{-{\rm i}\frac{\tau}{\lambda^{2}}H_{\rm S}}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm R})A(e^{{\rm i}\frac{\tau}{\lambda^{2}}H_{\rm S}}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm R})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}(\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}-\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta})\Big(e^{{\rm i}\frac{\tau}{\lambda^{2}}H_{0}}(e^{-{\rm i}\frac{\tau}{\lambda^{2}}H_{\rm S}}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm R})A(e^{{\rm i}\frac{\tau}{\lambda^{2}}H_{\rm S}}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm R})e^{-{\rm i}\frac{\tau}{\lambda^{2}}H_{0}}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

Thus (
2.31
) gives for all
\tau>0
,
A\in\mathcal{O}

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\ \rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}\Big(e^{{\rm i}\frac{\tau}{\lambda^{2}}H_{\lambda}}(e^{-{\rm i}\frac{\tau}{\lambda^{2}}H_{\rm S}}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm R})A(e^{{\rm i}\frac{\tau}{\lambda^{2}}H_{\rm S}}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm R})e^{-{\rm i}\frac{\tau}{\lambda^{2}}H_{\lambda}}\Big)=\big(e^{\tau\mathcal{K}}\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}\big)(A).

This shows that in the weak coupling scaling limit, the total system-reservoir state (in the interaction picture when the system dynamics is removed) is of the product form
(e^{\tau\mathcal{K}}\rho_{\rm S})\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}
for all
\tau>0
. This fact was already observed in
[
39
]
.

(d)

If
A
belongs to the norm closure of
{\mathcal{O}}
, then take an arbitrary
\epsilon>0
and
A_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{O}
such that
\|A-A_{\epsilon}\|<\epsilon
. The quantity
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}(e^{{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}Ae^{-{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}})
is still well defined and we have
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}(e^{{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}Ae^{-{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}})-\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}(e^{{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}A_{\epsilon}e^{-{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}})=O(\epsilon)
, uniformly in
t
. Theorem (
2.2
) then gives the expansion (
2.31
) for
\rho_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}(e^{{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}}A_{\epsilon}e^{-{\rm i}tH_{\lambda}})
, with constants
C(A_{\epsilon})
depending on
\epsilon
. In the main term,
\big(e^{t{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}(\lambda)}\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R},\beta}\big)(A_{\epsilon})+\chi(\lambda,t,A_{\epsilon})
, we can replace
A_{\epsilon}
again by
A
, making an error of
O(\epsilon)
, uniformly in
\lambda
and
t
(see also (
3.49
)). This shows that (
2.31
) remains valid for any
A
in the norm closure of
\mathcal{O}
, modulo adding an arbitrarily small term
O(\epsilon)
and allowing the constants
C(A)
to depend also on
\epsilon
. One may use a similar argument to extend the result of Theorem
2.2
to
\nu=\infty
in (
2.25
).

[

]
. To our knowledge, this is the only approach able to handle perturbation theory in small

valid for all times
t\geq 0
. On the physics side, our work is close to

]
, where the authors show that the master equation is valid for correlated initial states in the weak coupling limit (see the Remark (c) above). Their technique is based on the Nakajima-Zwanzig projection method and does not extend beyond the weak coupling scaling regime. In some aspects, the setup in
[
39
,
40
]
is more general (allowing to treat NESS for example – an extension of our methods to include this is planned) and in some aspects it is less general (their Kraus operators have to be bounded for the main result in
[
39
]
– even though unbounded ones are used in applications given in
[
40
]
).

One of our main conclusions is that initial correlations do not invalidate the Markovian approximation. This means that the distinguishability (trace distance) of reduced system states cannot increase during the evolution (by more than
O(|\lambda|^{1/4})
). An important feature responsible for this is that the reservoir dynamics is dispersive, meaning that the evolution converges to the stationary (equilibrium) state. This is built into the model by taking the infinite volume limit (continuous reservoir mode frequencies) and by the continuity of the spectrum of
H_{\rm R}
(see also
[
31
,
32
,
30
]
). In contrast, if
{\rm R}
is
finite
, an
M
-level system with discrete energy levels, then different effects appear. Those are studied in a variety of papers. It is shown in
[
26
,
14
]
that for finite-dimensional
{\rm S}
and
{\rm R}
, the backflow of information
{\rm R}\rightarrow{\rm S}
can lead to an
increase
in the distinguishability if the
{\rm S}{\rm R}
state is initially correlated, an effect which cannot happen for initial product states. The increase is a measure for non-Markovianity of the dynamics. In this situation, initial correlations lead to non-Markovianity of the system dynamics. A related question is whether and how the system dynamics can be represented by completely positive, Kraus representation maps. In
[
21
]
, the authors consider finite-dimensional
{\rm S}
and
{\rm R}
and show that a Kraus representation is valid for all times and for general initial correlations if and only if the joint dynamics is local unitary (no
{\rm S}{\rm R}
coupling). It is shown in
[
35
]
that the dynamics of an open
N
-level system can be described by at most
N^{2}
completely positive trace preserving maps. The result is built on a decomposition of the (correlated) initial state using a so-called bath-positive decomposition.

In
[
3
]
, a new correlation picture approach is used to show that the reduced system dynamics satisfies a Lindblad-like master equation, in which the jump operators depend on the initial
{\rm S}{\rm R}
correlation, yielding a nonlinear evolution equation. The philosophy there is to build a weak correlation, rather than a weak coupling, perturbation theory. The corresponding perturbation series does not converge uniformly in time, but allows to analyze a strongly interacting
{\rm S}{\rm R}
dynamics for finite times.
