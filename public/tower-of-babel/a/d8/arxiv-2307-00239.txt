Frederik Broucke, Sebastian Weishäupl

Abstract
. In a recent paper, Gonek, Graham, and Lee introduced a notion of the Lindelöf hypothesis (LH) for general sequences which coincides with the usual Lindelöf hypothesis for the Riemann zeta function in the case of the sequence of positive integers. They made two conjectures: that LH should hold for every admissible sequence of positive integers, and that LH should hold for the “generic” admissible sequence of positive real numbers. In this paper, we give counterexamples to the first conjecture, and show that the second conjecture can be either true or false, depending on the meaning of “generic”: we construct probabilistic processes producing sequences satisfying LH with probability 1, and we construct Baire topological spaces of sequences for which the subspace of sequences satisfying LH is meagre. We also extend the main result of Gonek, Graham, and Lee, stating that the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to LH for the sequence of prime numbers, to the context of Beurling generalized number systems.

1.
Introduction

The
Lindelöf hypothesis
is a conjecture about the growth of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
. It says that for each
\varepsilon>0
,

\zeta(1/2+\mathrm{i}\tau)\ll_{\varepsilon}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tau\right\rvert}^{\varepsilon},\quad\text{ as }\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tau\right\rvert}\to\infty.

Using Perron inversion and, conversely, the approximation of
\zeta(s)
by partial sums of
\sum_{n}n^{-s}
, one can show (see e.g. the appendix of
[
6
]
or the Appendix below) that the Lindelöf hypothesis is equivalent to the following statement: for every
\varepsilon>0
and
B>0
,

\sum_{n\leq x}n^{-\mathrm{i}t}=\frac{x^{1-\mathrm{i}t}}{1-\mathrm{i}t}+O_{\varepsilon,B}(x^{1/2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{\varepsilon}),

for
1\leq x\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{B}
.
Based on this formulation, Gonek, Graham, and Lee introduced in the article
[
6
]
a notion of the Lindelöf hypothesis for general “admissible” sequences
\mathcal{N}
. They call an increasing sequence of positive reals
\mathcal{N}=(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
with counting function
N(x)=\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}1

admissible
if
N(x)
can be written as
N(x)=M(x)+E(x)
, where

(1)

M(x)
is differentiable,

(2)

x^{\alpha_{1}}\ll M(x)\ll x^{\alpha_{2}}
for some
0<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}
, and

(3)

E(x)\ll_{A}M(x)/(\log x)^{A}
for every positive constant
A
.

The Lindelöf hypothesis is true for an admissible sequence
\mathcal{N}
with associated function
M(x)
, or
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},M(x))
for short, if for every
\varepsilon>0
and
B>0
,

\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}=\int_{n_{1}}^{x}u^{-\mathrm{i}t}M^{\prime}(u)\dif u+O_{\varepsilon,B}\bigl(M(x)^{1/2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{\varepsilon}\bigr),

for
n_{1}\leq x\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{B}
. With this notation, the classical Lindelöf hypothesis is equivalent to the statement
\mathrm{LH}(\mathbb{N}_{>0},x)
.

Note that we employ the notation of Banks
[
1
]
and explicitly mention the function
M(x)
, as the validity of
\mathrm{LH}
also depends on this function. (For example, the statements
\mathrm{LH}(\mathbb{N}_{>0},x)
and
\mathrm{LH}(\mathbb{N}_{>0},x+x^{3/4})
are mutually exclusive.)

The main result of
[
6
]
is

Theorem 1.1

(Gonek, Graham, Lee)
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
denote the sequence of prime numbers. Then
\mathrm{LH}(\mathbb{P},\Li(x))
is equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis.

Here we used
\Li(x)
to denote the logarithmic integral, which we define as
\Li(x)=\int_{1}^{x}\frac{1-u^{-1}}{\log u}\dif u
.
Interestingly, RH is also equivalent to LH for certain thin sets of primes. For example, in
[
1
]
Banks showed that there are arbitrarily small values of
\varepsilon>0
and subsequences
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}
of the primes with counting function
\sim\varepsilon\Li(x)
such that the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon\Li(x))
.

In
[
6
]
, the following two conjectures are made.

Conjecture 1

(Gonek, Graham, Lee)
.

\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N})
is true for every admissible sequence of positive integers
\mathcal{N}
.

Conjecture 2

(Gonek, Graham, Lee)
.

\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N})
is true for the “generic” admissible sequence of positive real numbers
\mathcal{N}
.

The aim of this paper is twofold. First, we resolve the above conjectures. Note that the conjectures are not completely unambiguous, as no function
M(x)
is mentioned in their statements, and a precise meaning of “generic” is not specified. However, we feel that the results we obtained provide compelling answers to what the authors of
[
6
]
likely had in mind when formulating their conjectures.

Concretely, we provide the following counterexample to Conjecture
1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exist integer sequences
\mathcal{N}=(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
given by
n_{j}=3j+\delta_{j}
, where
\delta_{j}=0,1
or
2
, with counting function
N(x)=x/3+O(1)
, and for which
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},x/3)
is false.

Actually, one can even obtain integer sequences
\mathcal{N}
of density
1
failing
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},x)
, see Theorem
2.1
below.

Regarding Conjecture
2
, we adopt two viewpoints commonly used when addressing questions concerning “generic” or “typical” behavior: a probabilistic (or measure-theoretical) viewpoint and a topological viewpoint.

For suitable functions
M(x)
, we construct natural probabilistic processes (natural measure spaces) which produce sequences
\mathcal{N}
satisfying
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},M(x))
with probability
1
(for which the subspace of sequences
\mathcal{N}
satisfying
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},M(x))
has full measure); see Theorems
2.3
and
2.6
. On the other hand, we also construct natural topological spaces of sequences for which the subspace of sequences
\mathcal{N}
satisfying
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},M(x))
is actually negligible in the sense of Baire category; see Theorem
2.7
. Both viewpoints can also be combined and applied to a single locally compact Hausdorff space equipped with a (Radon) probability measure.
Hence we demonstrate that Conjecture
2
can have different answers depending on the specific meaning of the word “generic”.

The second aim is to extend Theorem
1.1
to Beurling generalized number systems. Here we just state the main theorem, and refer to the beginning of Section
3
for the necessary background and notation regarding such number systems.

Given a Beurling number system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
, we denote its integer-counting function by
N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)
, its prime-counting function by
\pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)
and its Chebyshev function by
\psi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)
. We say that the Riemann hypothesis holds for the system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
if

\psi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=x+O_{\varepsilon}(x^{1/2+\varepsilon}),\quad\text{for every }\varepsilon>0.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
be a system of Beurling generalized primes and integers, for which
N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=Ax+O(x^{1/2})
for some
A>0
. Then
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{P},\Li(x))
is equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis for
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
.

We note that the exponent
1/2
in the error term for
N_{\mathcal{P}}
is sharp, see Proposition
3.3
below.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we answer the conjectures of Gonek, Graham, and Lee. Next, in Section
3
, we prove Theorem
1.3
, comment on the necessity of the condition
N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=Ax+O(x^{1/2})
, and show the existence of very “nice” Beurling systems, in the sense that the Riemann hypothesis,
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},Ax)
, and
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{P},\Li(x))
all hold. In Section
4
we make some remarks on the definition of
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},M(x))
. In particular, we show that under mild conditions on the sequence
\mathcal{N}
, one can always find a smooth function
M(x)
such that
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},M(x))
holds (see Proposition
4.1
). Finally, in the appendix, we sketch the equivalence of
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},Ax)
and “
|\tau|^{\varepsilon}
-bounds” for the associated Dirichlet series of
\mathcal{N}
, for a natural class of sequences
\mathcal{N}
.

Regarding notation, we will typically write complex numbers as
s=\sigma+\mathrm{i}\tau
and reserve the letter
t
for the parameter occurring in the definition of
\mathrm{LH}
. When considering a sequence
\mathcal{N}
, we will always use
N(x)
to denote its counting function. Sometimes we will also speak about
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},M(x))
for sequences
\mathcal{N}
which are not necessarily non-decreasing.

2.
The Lindelöf hypothesis for general sequences

2.1.
Integer sequences failing LH

In
[
6
, p. 2866]
an example due to Montgomery is given of a sequence
\mathcal{N}
satisfying
N(x)=x+O(1)
and for which
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},x)
is false. The idea is to consider sequences
(t_{k})_{k\geq 1}
and
(x_{k})_{k\geq 1}
and “cook up” the elements
n_{j}
of
\mathcal{N}
with
x_{k}/2<n_{j}\leq x_{k}
in such a way that all the
n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t_{k}}
point in the same direction, making the exponential sum
\sum_{n_{j}\leq x_{k}}n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t_{k}}
large (in fact even
\gg x_{k}
).

A similar method can be employed to construct
integer
sequences for which
\mathrm{LH}
fails.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We inductively construct an integer sequence
\mathcal{N}=(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
, where

0<\alpha<\pi/6
,
\beta=(1+\alpha/4\pi)^{-1}
and suppose that
n_{j}
has been selected for
j\leq\beta K
, where
K
is a large integer. We will now choose
n_{j}
for
\beta K<j\leq K
. Set

S_{J,t}=\sum_{j\leq J}n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}.

Let
t=2\pi K
and suppose first that
\operatorname{Re}S_{\beta K,t}\geq 0
. We have

(2.1)

n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}=(3j+\delta_{j})^{-\mathrm{i}t}=(3j)^{-\mathrm{i}t}\exp\Big\{-\mathrm{i}t\log\Big(1+\frac{\delta_{j}}{3j}\Big)\Big\}.

Here

Hence for sufficiently large

(2.2)

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\log\Big(1+\frac{\delta_{j}}{3j}\Big)-\frac{t\delta_{j}}{3j}\right\rvert}\leq\frac{\alpha}{2},\quad\text{for }j\geq\beta K.

If now
\beta K<j\leq K

(2.3)

\frac{2\pi\delta_{j}}{3}\leq\frac{t\delta_{j}}{3j}=\frac{2\pi K\delta_{j}}{3j}\leq\beta^{-1}\frac{2\pi\delta_{j}}{3}=\Big(1+\frac{\alpha}{4\pi}\Big)\frac{2\pi\delta_{j}}{3}\leq\frac{2\pi\delta_{j}}{3}+\frac{\alpha}{2}

Combining (

) and (

) yields

(2.4)

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\log\Big(1+\frac{\delta_{j}}{3j}\Big)-\frac{2\pi\delta_{j}}{3}\right\rvert}\leq\alpha,\quad\text{for }\beta K<j\leq K.

Now it’s geometrically easy to see that in view of (

) and (

) we can choose

It follows that

. In the case

we choose
\delta_{j}
for
\beta K<j\leq K
such that
\operatorname{Re}(n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t})\leq-c<0
, so

follows analogously. In this way we can select the sequence
(\delta_{j})_{j\geq 1}
inductively such that for infinitely many integers

Remark 2.2
.

For an increasing integer sequence
\mathcal{N}
satisfying
N(x)=x+O_{\epsilon}(x^{1/2+\epsilon})
for every
\epsilon>0
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
n\in\mathcal{N}\end{subarray}}n^{-\mathrm{i}t}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}\end{subarray}}n^{-\mathrm{i}t}+O_{\varepsilon,B}(x^{1/2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{\varepsilon}),

if
1\leq x\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{B}
(take
\epsilon=\varepsilon/B
),
so that
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},x)
is equivalent to
\mathrm{LH}(\mathbb{N}_{>0},x)
.

One could argue that introducing this “conspiracy” among the

Proof.

Let
(N_{j})_{j\geq 1}

\dif M\big|_{(x_{j-1},x_{j}]}

The sum

is far away from this value using an inequality of Hoeffding

7

a_{j}\leq Y_{j}\leq b_{j}
and with sum

In our case, applying Hoeffding’s inequality with
v_{J,t}=C\sqrt{J}\bigl(\sqrt{\log(x_{J}+1)}+\sqrt{\log(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}+1)}\bigr)

P\Bigl(\operatorname{Re}\big(S_{J,t}-E(S_{J,t})\big)\geq v_{J,k}\Bigr)\leq\exp\Bigl\{-\frac{C^{2}}{2}\bigl(\log(x_{J}+1)+\log(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}+1)\bigr)\Bigr\}.

Applying the same principle to the random variables

,
\pm\operatorname{Im}(X_{j,t})

P\Bigl(\,\mathinner{\!\Bigl\lvert S_{J,t}-E(S_{J,t})\Bigr\rvert}\geq\sqrt{2}v_{J,k}\Bigr)\leq\frac{4}{(x_{J}+1)^{C^{2}/2}(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}+1)^{C^{2}/2}},

Let now
A_{J,k}
denote the event
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S_{J,k}-E(S_{J,k})\right\rvert}\geq\sqrt{2}v_{J,k}
. By assumption

, so that

\sum_{J=1}^{\infty}x_{J}^{-C^{2}/2}=\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}u^{-C^{2}/2}\dif\,\lfloor M(u)\rfloor=\frac{C^{2}}{2}\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}\lfloor M(u)\rfloor u^{-C^{2}/2-1}\dif u<\infty,

provided that

. Hence, fixing some

we get that

\sum_{J=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}P(A_{J,k})\leq\sum_{J=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{4}{(x_{J}+1)^{C^{2}/2}(k+1)^{C^{2}/2}}<\infty.

By the Borel–Cantelli lemma, one has that with probability 1 only finitely many of the events
A_{J,k}
occur.

To complete the proof we show that a sequence
\mathcal{N}=(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
contained in only finitely many sets

satisfies (
2.6
). Clearly this holds if
x=x_{J}
and

. Set

. Then we have for

(2.7)

S(x,k)=S_{J-1,k}+O(1)=\int_{x_{0}}^{x}u^{-\mathrm{i}k}\dif M(u)+O\Bigl(\sqrt{M(x)}\bigl(\sqrt{\log(x+1)}+\sqrt{\log(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert k\right\rvert}+1)}\bigr)\Bigr).

Finally, if
t\in(k-1,k)
,

\displaystyle S(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{0}}^{x^{+}}u^{-\mathrm{i}(t-k)}\dif S(u,k)=S(x,k)x^{-\mathrm{i}(t-k)}+\mathrm{i}(t-k)\int_{x_{0}}^{x}S(u,k)u^{-\mathrm{i}(t-k)-1}\dif u

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{0}}^{x}u^{-\mathrm{i}k}\dif M(u)x^{-\mathrm{i}(t-k)}+\mathrm{i}(t-k)\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\biggl(\int_{x_{0}}^{u}y^{-\mathrm{i}k}\dif M(y)\biggr)u^{-\mathrm{i}(t-k)-1}\dif u

\displaystyle\quad+O\Biggl(\sqrt{M(x)}\bigl(\sqrt{\log(x+1)}+\sqrt{\log(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}+1)}\bigr)\Biggr)

\displaystyle\quad+O\Biggl(\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\frac{\sqrt{M(u)}}{u}\bigl(\sqrt{\log(u+1)}+\sqrt{\log(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}+1)}\bigr)\dif u\Biggr)

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{0}}^{x}u^{-\mathrm{i}t}\dif M(u)+O\Bigl(\sqrt{M(x)}\bigl(\sqrt{\log(x+1)}+\sqrt{\log(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}+1)}\bigr)\log(x+1)\Bigr),

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

The last
\log

The ideas of Theorem

Another probabilistic result in favor of Conjecture

Theorem 2.6
.

Let

and

By rescaling we can assume that

Now we want to see that

\displaystyle f(x)

2.3.
Topological viewpoint

Next we will consider topological spaces of sequences for which the subspace of sequences satisfying LH is
small
. For this we will use the notion of Baire category. We recall that if
X
is a locally compact Hausdorff space or a complete metric space, then it is a Baire space: the countable union of nowhere dense sets has empty interior. Such countable unions of nowhere dense sets are called
meagre
sets or
sets of first category
, and in Baire spaces they can be considered topologically small or negligible. We refer to
[
10
]
for more background on Baire category theory, as well as its relation to measure theory.

Given two increasing sequences
(a_{j})_{j\geq 1}
and
(b_{j})_{j\geq 1}
with
a_{j}<b_{j}
, we set

X=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}[a_{j},b_{j}]=\bigl\{\mathcal{N}=(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}a_{j}\leq n_{j}\leq b_{j}\bigr\}.

We do not require that
b_{j}\leq a_{j+1}
, so the sequences in
X
need not be non-decreasing. To fix ideas, we suppose that

(2.10)

\displaystyle a_{j}\sim b_{j}\sim j

(2.11)

\displaystyle b_{j}-a_{j}\gg 1

Assumption (
2.10
) implies that for any
(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}\in X
, its counting function satisfies
N(x)=\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}1\sim x
.

We equip
X
with two topologies. First we consider
\tau_{\Pi}
, the product topology or the topology of pointwise convergence. Clearly
(X,\tau_{\Pi})
is a compact Hausdorff space. Second, we consider the normalized uniform metric
d
, defined as

d(\mathcal{N}_{1},\mathcal{N}_{2})=\sup_{j\geq 1}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert n_{1,j}-n_{2,j}\right\rvert}}{b_{j}-a_{j}},\quad\text{ where }\mathcal{N}_{i}=(n_{i,j})_{j\geq 1},\quad i=1,2.

It is straightforward to verify that
(X,d)
is a complete metric space. We denote the associated topology by
\tau_{d}
. Clearly this topology is finer that the product topology: every
\Pi
-open (resp.
\Pi
-closed) set is also
d
-open (resp.
d
-closed). The nowhere dense sets are not comparable however: there are
\Pi
-nowhere dense sets which are not
d
-nowhere dense, and vice-versa.

It turns out that not only LH is a “rare” occurrence in the space
X
, but in fact any asymptotic behavior of the exponential sums
\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}
is non-typical.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
F(x,t)
be a function which is right continuous in
x
. We consider the subspace
Y(F)
of
X
of those sequences whose exponential sums behave like
F
:

Y(F)=\biggl\{\mathcal{N}\in X\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\exists x_{0}>1,\exists 0<c_{1}<1<c_{2},\exists\eta\in[0,1)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\\
\forall x,t\text{ with }x\geq x_{0}\text{ and }x^{c_{1}}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}\leq x^{c_{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathinner{\!\biggl\lvert\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}-F(x,t)\biggr\rvert}\leq\eta x\biggr\}.

Then
Y(F)
is both a
\Pi
-meagre and a
d
-meagre subset of
X
.

Proof.

Note that

Y(F)=\bigcup_{M>1}\bigcup_{k>1}\bigcup_{l>1}Y(F;M,k,l),

where

Y(F;M,k,l)=\\
\biggl\{\mathcal{N}\in X\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\forall x,t\text{ with }x\geq M\text{ and }x^{1-1/k}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}\leq x^{1+1/k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathinner{\!\biggl\lvert\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}-F(x,t)\biggr\rvert}\leq(1-1/l)x\biggr\}.

It suffices to show that each
Y(F;M,k,l)
is both
\Pi
-nowhere dense and
d
-nowhere dense. We show something slightly stronger, namely that
Y(F;M,k,l)
is
\Pi
-closed and has empty
d
-interior. Let
\mathcal{N}=(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be an element of the
\Pi
-closure of
Y(F;M,k,l)
, and fix some
x
and
t
with
x\geq M
and
x^{1-1/k}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}\leq x^{1+1/k}
. Let
(\mathcal{N}_{m})_{m\geq 1}
be a sequence in
Y(F;M,k,l)
converging pointwise to
\mathcal{N}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
be sufficiently small such that
n_{j}>x\implies n_{j}\geq x+2\varepsilon
. Then there is some
m(\varepsilon)
such that

m\geq m(\varepsilon)\implies\bigl(n_{j}\leq x\iff n_{m,j}\leq x+\varepsilon\bigr).

Hence

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\biggl\lvert\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}-F(x,t)\biggr\rvert}

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathinner{\!\biggl\lvert\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}n_{m,j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}-F(x,t)\biggr\rvert}

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathinner{\!\biggl\lvert\sum_{n_{m,j}\leq x+\varepsilon}n_{m,j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}-F(x,t)\biggr\rvert}\leq(1-1/l)x+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert F(x+\varepsilon,t)-F(x,t)\right\rvert}.

As
F(x,t)
is right-continuous in
x
and
\varepsilon
can be taken arbitrarily small,
\mathcal{N}\in Y(F;M,k,l)
. This shows that
Y(F;M,k,l)
is
\Pi
-closed.

Let now
\mathcal{N}\in Y(F;M,k,l)
and
\varepsilon>0
. We show that
B(\mathcal{N},\varepsilon)=\{\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\in X\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}d(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{N}^{\prime})<\varepsilon\}
is not contained in
Y(F;M,k,l)
. For
x
and
t
with
t=x^{1+1/k}
, we have
t\geq 4\pi x/(\varepsilon(b_{j}-a_{j}))
for all
j
if
x
is sufficiently large, in view of (
2.11
). For each
j\geq j_{0}
with
n_{j}\leq x
, pick
n_{j}^{\prime}\in[a_{j},b_{j}]
with
\mathinner{\lvert n_{j}-n_{j}^{\prime}\rvert}/(b_{j}-a_{j})<\varepsilon
and such that
\arg\bigl((n_{j}^{\prime})^{-\mathrm{i}t}\bigr)=-\arg F(x,t)
. This is possible, because

(n_{j}^{\prime})^{-\mathrm{i}t}=n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}\exp\Bigl(-\mathrm{i}t\log\frac{n_{j}^{\prime}}{n_{j}}\Bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathinner{\!\Bigl\lvert\log\Bigl(\frac{n_{j}\pm\varepsilon(b_{j}-a_{j})}{n_{j}}\Bigr)\Bigr\rvert}>\frac{\varepsilon(b_{j}-a_{j})}{2n_{j}}\geq\frac{2\pi}{t},

if
j_{0}
is sufficiently large so that
(b_{j}-a_{j})/a_{j}\leq 1
say.
For
j<j_{0}
or
j
with
n_{j}>x
, we set
n_{j}^{\prime}=n_{j}
. Then
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}=(n_{j}^{\prime})_{j\geq 1}\in B(\mathcal{N},\varepsilon)
and
N_{\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}(x)\sim N_{\mathcal{N}}(x)\sim x
, but

\mathinner{\!\biggl\lvert\sum_{n_{j}^{\prime}\leq x}(n_{j}^{\prime})^{-\mathrm{i}t}-F(x,t)\biggr\rvert}\geq\mathinner{\!\biggl\lvert\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}(n_{j}^{\prime})^{-\mathrm{i}t}-F(x,t)\biggr\rvert}+o(x)>\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{l}\Bigr)x,

provided that
x
is sufficiently large. Hence
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\not\in Y(F;M,k,l)
. As
\mathcal{N}
and
\varepsilon
were arbitrary, we have shown that
Y(F;M,k,l)
has empty
d
-interior.
∎

If we now suppose that
b_{j}-a_{j}\ll_{A}j/(\log j)^{A}
for all
A>0
, then uniformly for
\mathcal{N}\in X

N_{\mathcal{N}}(x)-x\ll_{A}x/(\log x)^{A}
for all
A>0
. If we omit the requirement that
\mathcal{N}
should be increasing, then each
\mathcal{N}\in X
is admissible with
M(x)=x
. However, the set of sequences
\mathcal{N}
satisfying
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},x)
is clearly contained in
Y(F)
, where
F(x,t)=x^{1-\mathrm{i}t}/(1-\mathrm{i}t)
. In fact, for any fixed
B>1
and
\varepsilon<1/2
, the set of sequences satisfying

\forall x,t\text{ with }a_{1}\leq x\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{B}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathinner{\!\biggl\lvert\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}-\frac{x^{1-\mathrm{i}t}}{1-\mathrm{i}t}\biggr\rvert}\ll x^{1/2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{\varepsilon}

is already contained in
Y(F)
. So in the topological sense, the “generic” sequence
\mathcal{N}
in
X
does not satisfy
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},x)
.

Of course, many variations of the above construction of topological spaces of sequences are possible. The main philosophy is that any prescribed asymptotic behavior of the exponential sums
\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}
is an unstable property: one can always find small perturbations of the sequence
(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
which completely destroy this asymptotic behavior.

Suppose now that there is a probability measure
P_{j}
defined on each interval
[a_{j},b_{j}]
. The Kolmogorov extension theorem then yields a (
\tau_{\Pi}
-)Radon probability measure
P
on
X
which extends finite products of the
P_{j}
: for every
J
and Borel set
A\subseteq\prod_{j=1}^{J}[a_{j},b_{j}]
,

P\biggl(A\times\prod_{j=J+1}^{\infty}[a_{j},b_{j}]\biggr)=P_{1}\times\dotsb\times P_{J}(A)=\int_{A}\dif P_{1}(u_{1})\dotsm\dif P_{J}(u_{J}).

The random variables
N_{J}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}\mapsto n_{J}
are then independent and have distribution
P_{J}
.
Under suitable conditions, this construction gives rise to a counting function
M(x)
for which
P
-almost all sequences
\mathcal{N}\in X
satisfy
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},M(x))
.

For example, let
a_{j}=j
,
b_{j}=j+1
,
M(x)
a right-continuous non-decreasing function such that
M(j+1)=j
and
M(x)<j
if
x<j+1
. If we set
P_{j}=\dif M\big|_{(j,j+1]}
, then the probability measure
P
is an explicit manifestation of the probability measure which is implicit in the statement of Theorem
2.3
. (The event
\{(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n_{j}=a_{j}\text{ for some }j\}
has probability zero.) Hence Theorem
2.3
yields a probability measure on
X
with respect to which almost all sequences
\mathcal{N}\in X
satisfy
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},M(x))
, but Theorem
2.7
with
F(x,t)=\int_{1}^{x}u^{-\mathrm{i}t}\dif M(u)
shows that in the topological sense, the generic sequence
\mathcal{N}\in X
does not satisfy
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},M(x))
.

Similar observations can be made when we set for example
a_{j}=j-Kj^{1/2}
,
b_{j}=j+Kj^{1/2}
for some
K>0
and compare the probabilistic Theorem
2.6
with the Baire-categoric Theorem
2.7
with
F(x,t)=x^{1-\mathrm{i}t}/(1-\mathrm{i}t)
.

3.
The Lindelöf hypothesis for sequences of Beurling primes

A Beurling generalized number system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
consists of a sequence of generalized primes
\mathcal{P}=(p_{j})_{j\geq 1}
, where

p_{j}\in\mathbb{R},\quad 1<p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\dotso,\quad p_{j}\to\infty,

and the corresponding sequence of generalized integers
\mathcal{N}=(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
, which consists of
1=n_{1}
and all possible (finite) products of generalized primes, ordered in a non-decreasing fashion. Both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{N}
are allowed to have repeated values. For example, if
p_{1}=p_{2}=2
,
p_{3}>2
, then the integer
4
occurs
3
times in
\mathcal{N}
, corresponding to
p_{1}^{2}
,
p_{1}p_{2}
, and
p_{2}^{2}
. We denote the counting functions of the sequences by
\pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=\sum_{p_{j}\leq x}1
and
N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}1
respectively, and omit the subscript
\mathcal{P}
if there is no risk of confusion. We also consider the Chebyshev function, defined as
\psi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=\sum_{p_{j}^{\nu}\leq x}\log p_{j}
.

Such generalized number systems were introduced by Beurling
[
2
]
, who wanted to investigate the “stability” of theorems from multiplicative number theory. For example, he showed that the prime number theorem (PNT)
\pi(x)\sim x/\log x
follows from the estimate
N(x)=Ax+O(x/\log^{\gamma}x)
for some
A>0
, provided that
\gamma>3/2
. On the other hand, the Riemann hypothesis need not hold in this general setting, as Diamond, Montgomery, and Vorhauer
[
4
]
(see also
[
13
]
) established the existence of generalized number systems satisfying
N(x)=Ax+O_{\varepsilon}(x^{1/2+\varepsilon})
for every
\varepsilon>0
, but for which the classical de la Vallée-Poussin estimate is best possible:
\pi(x)=\Li(x)+\Omega\bigl(x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x})\bigr)
for some
c>0
.

Given a generalized number system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
, we associate with it the Beurling zeta function
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)\coloneqq\sum_{n_{j}}n_{j}^{-s}
. The multiplicative structure of the Beurling integers is encoded in the Euler product

\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\prod_{p_{j}}\frac{1}{1-p_{j}^{-s}},

from which it follows that

-\frac{\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)}=\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}\dif\psi_{\mathcal{P}}(x).

For a general introduction to the theory of Beurling number systems, we refer to
[
5
]
.

In
[
6
]
, Gonek, Graham, and Lee showed Theorem
1.1
, stating that the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to
\mathrm{LH}(\mathbb{P},\Li(x))
. Here we extend this result to the context of Beurling generalized numbers. We consider number systems
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
for which
N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=Ax+O(x^{\theta})
for some
A>0
and
\theta\in(0,1)
. In particular they satisfy the PNT.
We recall that we use
s=\sigma+\mathrm{i}\tau
to denote a complex variable, while
t
will denote the variable occurring in the exponent in the exponential sums related to
\mathrm{LH}
.
We first define for
x\geq 1
and real
t

\displaystyle\pi(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p_{j}\leq x}p_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t},

\displaystyle r(x,t)

\displaystyle=\pi(x,t)-\int_{p_{1}}^{x}\frac{u^{-\mathrm{i}t}}{\log u}\dif u;

\displaystyle\psi(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}\Lambda(n_{j})n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t},

\displaystyle R(x,t)

\displaystyle=\psi(x,t)-\frac{x^{1-\mathrm{i}t}}{1-\mathrm{i}t}.

For
0<x<1
we set
r(x,t)=R(x,t)=0
.
Then
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{P},\Li(x))
is equivalent to the statement that for every
\varepsilon>0
and
B>0
,

(3.1)

R(x,t)\ll_{\varepsilon,B}x^{1/2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{\varepsilon},\quad\text{for }1\leq x\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{B}.

Indeed, writing

\psi(x,t)=\int_{1}^{x}\log u\dif\pi(u,t)+O(x^{1/2}),\quad\pi(x,t)=\int_{1}^{x}\frac{\dif\psi(u,t)}{\log u}+O\Bigl(\frac{x^{1/2}}{\log x}\Bigr),

integrating by parts, and using that
\log x\ll_{\varepsilon,B}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{\varepsilon}
and the fact that
\pi(x)\ll x/\log x
, it is clear that
r(x,t)\ll\Li(x)^{1/2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{\varepsilon}
is equivalent to
R(x,t)\ll x^{1/2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{\varepsilon}
.

Before giving the proof of Theorem
1.3
, we need a couple of preparatory lemmas. First we state a well-known effective Perron inversion formula.

Let
(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be a non-decreasing unbounded sequence with
n_{1}\geq 1
and
(a_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be an arbitrary complex sequence. Write
A(x)=\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}a_{j}
, and suppose
F(s)=\sum_{j\geq 1}a_{j}n_{j}^{-s}
converges absolutely for
\sigma>\sigma_{a}
. Then for
\kappa>\max(0,\sigma_{a})
,
T\geq 1
, and
x\geq 1
,

(3.2)

A(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\kappa-\mathrm{i}T}^{\kappa+\mathrm{i}T}F(s)x^{s}\frac{\dif s}{s}+O\biggl(x^{\kappa}\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\mathinner{\lvert a_{j}\rvert}}{n_{j}^{\kappa}\bigl(1+T\mathinner{\lvert\log(x/n_{j})\rvert}\bigr)}\biggr).

See e.g.
[
12
, Theorem II.2.3]
for the case
n_{j}=j
(the proof of the general case is identical).

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose
N(x)=Ax+O(x^{1/2})
for some
A>0
. Then
\zeta(s)
has analytic continuation to the half-plane
\operatorname{Re}s>1/2
, except for a simple pole at
s=1
with residue
A
. Furthermore
\zeta(s)
satisfies the following bounds (where
s=\sigma+\mathrm{i}\tau
):

\displaystyle\zeta(s)

\displaystyle\ll\Bigl(\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tau\right\rvert}+1}{\sigma-1/2}\Bigr)^{2-2\sigma},

\displaystyle\text{for }\frac{1}{2}<\sigma<\frac{3}{4},

\displaystyle\zeta(s)

\displaystyle\ll\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tau\right\rvert}^{2-2\sigma}\log\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tau\right\rvert}+1,

\displaystyle\text{for }\sigma\geq\frac{3}{4},\quad\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tau\right\rvert}\geq 2.

Proof.

We write
N(x)=Ax+E(x)
with
E(x)\ll x^{1/2}
. Then we have for
\sigma>1
and
x>1

\displaystyle\zeta(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}n_{j}^{-s}+\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\dif N(u)}{u^{s}}=\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}n_{j}^{-s}+A\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\dif u}{u^{s}}+\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\dif E(u)}{u^{s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}n_{j}^{-s}+A\frac{x^{1-s}}{s-1}-\frac{E(x)}{x^{s}}+s\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{E(u)}{u^{s+1}}\dif u.

As
E(x)\ll x^{1/2}
this has, for fixed
x
, analytic continuation to
\sigma>1/2
, except for a simple pole at
s=1
with residue
A
. Consequently, for
\sigma>1/2
we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\zeta(s)-\frac{A}{s-1}\right\rvert}\ll\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}n_{j}^{-\sigma}+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\frac{x^{1-s}-1}{1-s}\right\rvert}+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s\right\rvert}\frac{x^{1/2-\sigma}}{\sigma-1/2}.

Using again
N(x)=Ax+O(x^{1/2})
we obtain for
\sigma>1/2

\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}n_{j}^{-\sigma}=A\frac{x^{1-\sigma}-1}{1-\sigma}+O\Bigl(\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1/2}\Bigr),

where we take the first term of the right hand side to be
A\log x
if
\sigma=1
.

If
1/2<\sigma<3/4
we take
x=\bigl(\max\{1,|\tau|\}/(1/2-\sigma)\bigr)^{2}
, yielding

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\zeta(s)-\frac{A}{s-1}\right\rvert}\ll\Bigl(\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tau\right\rvert}+1}{\sigma-1/2}\Bigr)^{2-2\sigma}.

If
\sigma\geq 3/4
and
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tau\right\rvert}\geq 2
we take
x=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tau\right\rvert}^{2}\log\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tau\right\rvert}
, yielding

\zeta(s)\ll\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tau\right\rvert}^{2-2\sigma}\log\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tau\right\rvert}+1.

∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
N(x)=Ax+O(x^{1/2})
for some
A>0
, and that
\zeta(s)
has no zeros in
\sigma>1/2
. Then
-\zeta^{\prime}(s)/\zeta(s)
has meromorphic continuation to
\sigma>1/2
, with a simple pole at
s=1
with residue
1
. For each
\delta>0
, we have

-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)}\ll\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\Bigl(\log(|\tau|+2)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigr),\quad\text{for }\sigma\geq\frac{1}{2}+\delta\text{ and }\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s-1\right\rvert}\geq\frac{1}{4}.

Proof.

As
\zeta(s)
has no zeros for
\sigma>1/2
,
\log(\zeta(s)\frac{s-1}{s})
defines an analytic function in this half-plane. From Lemma
3.1
it easily follows that

(3.3)

\log\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\zeta(s)\frac{s-1}{s}\right\rvert}\leq\log(|\tau|+2)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}+O(1),\quad\text{for }\sigma\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{4}.

Let
z=2+\mathrm{i}\tau
. Applying the Borel–Carathéodory lemma to the two circles with centre
z
and radii
R=3/2-\delta/4
and
r=3/2-\delta/2
yields

\max_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s-z\right\rvert}=r}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\log\Bigl(\zeta(s)\frac{s-1}{s}\Bigr)\right\rvert}\leq\frac{2r}{R-r}\max_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s-z\right\rvert}=R}\log\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\zeta(s)\frac{s-1}{s}\right\rvert}+\frac{R+r}{R-r}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\log\Bigl(\zeta(z)\frac{z-1}{z}\Bigr)\right\rvert}.

Using (
3.3
) it follows that

\log\Bigl(\zeta(s)\frac{s-1}{s}\Bigr)\ll\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigl(\log(|\tau|+2)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigr),\quad\text{for }\sigma\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}.

By Cauchy’s formula we obtain

\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint\frac{\log(\zeta(z)\frac{z-1}{z})}{(z-s)^{2}}\dif z-\frac{1}{s-1}+\frac{1}{s}\ll\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\Bigl(\log(|\tau|+2)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigr),

for
\sigma\geq 1/2+\delta
and
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert s-1\right\rvert}\geq 1/4
say, and where the integral is over a circle with center
s
and radius
\delta/2
.
∎

Recall that we have said that the Riemann hypothesis holds for
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
if
\psi(x)=x+O_{\varepsilon}(x^{1/2+\varepsilon})
for all
\varepsilon>0
. Using the above lemma and Perron inversion, it now easily follows that
N(x)=Ax+O(x^{1/2})
and the non-vanishing of
\zeta(s)
for
\operatorname{Re}s>1/2
imply RH (indeed, one can even get
\psi(x)=x+O(x^{1/2}(\log x)^{4})
). Without assuming
N(x)=Ax+O(x^{1/2})
, merely the analyticity of
-\zeta^{\prime}(s)/\zeta(s)
for
\operatorname{Re}s>1/2
does not imply RH, as
-\zeta^{\prime}(s)/\zeta(s)
may have extreme growth.

On the other hand, RH implies of course that
\zeta(s)
has analytic continuation to
\operatorname{Re}s>1/2
with the exception of a simple pole at
s=1
, and that it does not vanish there.

We are now ready to give the proof of Theorem
1.3
. Suppose first that the Riemann hypothesis holds.
Let
t
be real with
|t|\geq 1
and let
m
be a positive integer with
m>\mathrm{e}^{4}
. Since
N(x)=Ax+O(x^{1/2})
by assumption there are
N(m+1)-N(m)=O(m^{1/2})
Beurling integers in the interval
(m,m+1]
. Hence we can choose
x\in(m,m+1]
such that
|x-n_{j}|\gg x^{-1/2}
for all
n_{j}
. Furthermore let
\kappa=1+1/\log x
and
T\geq|t|+2
. Then applying the effective Perron inversion formula (
3.2
) with
a_{j}=\Lambda(n_{j})n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}
yields

\displaystyle\psi(x,t)=\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}\Lambda(n_{j})n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\kappa-\mathrm{i}T}^{\kappa+\mathrm{i}T}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s+\mathrm{i}t)}{\zeta(s+\mathrm{i}t)}x^{s}\frac{\dif s}{s}

\displaystyle+O\Big(-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(\kappa)}{\zeta(\kappa)}\frac{x}{T}\Big)+O\Big(\frac{x\log x}{T}\sum_{x/2<n_{j}\leq 2x}\frac{1}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x-n_{j}\right\rvert}}\Big).

Using
-\zeta^{\prime}(\sigma)/\zeta(\sigma)\ll 1/(\sigma-1)
for
\sigma\to 1^{+}
and
|x-n_{j}|\gg x^{-1/2}
we obtain

(3.4)

\psi(x,t)=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\kappa-\mathrm{i}T}^{\kappa+\mathrm{i}T}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s+\mathrm{i}t)}{\zeta(s+\mathrm{i}t)}x^{s}\frac{\dif s}{s}+O\Big(\frac{x^{5/2}\log x}{T}\Big).

In order to evaluate the integral on the right, we integrate along the boundary of the rectangle with vertices
\kappa-\mathrm{i}T,\kappa+\mathrm{i}T,d+\mathrm{i}T,d-\mathrm{i}T
, where
d=1/2+1/\log x<3/4
by the assumption on
m
.
The function
\zeta^{\prime}(s)/\zeta(s)
is meromorphic in the right half-plane
\sigma>1/2
with a simple pole at
s=1
of residue
-1
. Since the Riemann hypothesis holds by assumption, there are in fact no further poles in this right half-plane. Hence

\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\kappa-\mathrm{i}T}^{\kappa+\mathrm{i}T}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s+\mathrm{i}t)}{\zeta(s+\mathrm{i}t)}x^{s}\frac{\dif s}{s}=-\frac{x^{1-\mathrm{i}t}}{1-\mathrm{i}t}+\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\Big(\int_{\kappa-\mathrm{i}T}^{d-\mathrm{i}T}+\int_{d-\mathrm{i}T}^{d+\mathrm{i}T}+\int_{d+\mathrm{i}T}^{\kappa+\mathrm{i}T}\Big)\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s+\mathrm{i}t)}{\zeta(s+\mathrm{i}t)}x^{s}\frac{\dif s}{s}.

Applying Lemma
3.2
gives

\displaystyle\int_{d\pm\mathrm{i}T}^{\kappa\pm\mathrm{i}T}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s+\mathrm{i}t)}{\zeta(s+\mathrm{i}t)}x^{s}\frac{\dif s}{s}

\displaystyle\ll(\log T)(\log x)^{3}\int_{d}^{\kappa}\frac{x^{\sigma}}{|\sigma+\mathrm{i}T|}\dif\sigma\ll\frac{x(\log T)(\log x)^{2}}{T},

\displaystyle\int_{d-iT}^{d+iT}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s+\mathrm{i}t)}{\zeta(s+\mathrm{i}t)}x^{s}\frac{\dif s}{s}

\displaystyle\ll x^{d}(\log T)(\log x)^{3}\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{\dif\tau}{d+\mathrm{i}\tau}\ll x^{1/2}(\log T)^{2}(\log x)^{3},

where we have used
T\geq|t|+2
.
Collecting all estimates yields

\psi(x,t)=\frac{x^{1-\mathrm{i}t}}{1-\mathrm{i}t}+O\Big(\frac{x^{5/2}\log x}{T}+x^{1/2}(\log T)^{2}(\log x)^{3}\Big).

Let now
B>0
be arbitrary and suppose
\mathrm{e}^{4}+1\leq x\leq|t|^{B}
. We then choose
T=\max(|t|^{2B},|t|+2)\geq x^{2}
. Hence it follows that

(3.5)

\psi(x,t)=\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}\Lambda(n_{j})n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}=\frac{x^{1-\mathrm{i}t}}{1-\mathrm{i}t}+O_{\varepsilon,B}(x^{1/2}|t|^{\varepsilon}).

In the beginning of the proof we restricted
x
to be a specific number in the interval
(m,m+1]
. Now if we replace
x
by an arbitary
\tilde{x}\in(m,m+1]
, the left-hand side of (
3.5
) changes by at most
O(x^{1/2+\varepsilon})=O(x^{1/2}|t|^{\varepsilon})
, while the main term of the right-hand side changes by at most
O(1)
. Hence (
3.5
) holds for all
\mathrm{e}^{4}+1\leq x\leq|t|^{B}
and also trivially for
1\leq x<\mathrm{e}^{4}+1
. This proves
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{P},\Li(x))
.

Suppose now that
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{P},\Li(x))
holds. One might think that one can show that
\zeta(s)
does not have zeros in the half-plane
\operatorname{Re}s>1/2
based on some kind of explicit formula

R(x,t)=-\sum_{\rho}\frac{x^{\rho-\mathrm{i}t}}{\rho-\mathrm{i}t}+\text{error},

where the sum is over zeros
\rho=\beta+\mathrm{i}\gamma
of
\zeta(s)
, limited to certain suitable ranges for
\beta
and
\gamma
. (Actually, such an explicit formula for
\psi(x)
, or equivalently,
R(x,0)
, was recently shown in the Beurling setting by Révész
[
11
, Theorem 5.1]
2
2

2

A term
x/t_{k}
is missing in the error term in the statement of
[
11
, Theorem 5.1]
.) A zero
\rho=\beta+\mathrm{i}\gamma
contributes to a term of size
x^{\beta}
in the sum (if the term
\mathrm{i}t
is sufficiently close to
\rho
say). Hence the existence of a zero
\rho_{0}=\beta_{0}+\mathrm{i}\gamma_{0}
with
\beta_{0}>1/2
would contradict
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{P},\Li(x))
. The problem is that it is very difficult to establish that there is no too severe cancellation in the sum caused by other zeros, even already in the case of the Riemann zeta function and with
t=0
(see e.g.
[
8
]
).

To show that
\zeta(s)
has no zeros in
\operatorname{Re}s>1/2
we instead follow a more subtle approach of Gonek, Graham, and Lee, based on a method by Pintz (see
[
9
]
). The idea is to consider a suitable average of
R
which suppresses the influence of all but one zero (namely the zero
\rho_{0}
with
\beta_{0}>1/2
).

We start by setting

H_{t}(s)\coloneqq\int_{1^{-}}^{\infty}x^{-s}\dif R(x,t)=-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s+\mathrm{i}t)}{\zeta(s+\mathrm{i}t)}-\frac{s}{(1-\mathrm{i}t)(s+\mathrm{i}t-1)}.

for
t\in\mathbb{R}
. This function has meromorphic continuation to
\sigma>1/2
, with poles at the points
s=\rho-\mathrm{i}t
for each zero
\rho=\beta+\mathrm{i}\gamma
of
\zeta(s)
with
\beta>1/2
. Suppose now that
\rho_{0}=\beta_{0}+\mathrm{i}\gamma_{0}
with
\beta_{0}>1/2
is such a zero, and let
m
be its multiplicity. We then consider

h_{t}(s)\coloneqq\frac{\zeta(s+\mathrm{i}t)(s+\mathrm{i}t-1)}{(s+\mathrm{i}t-\rho_{0})^{m}(s+\mathrm{i}t)^{3}},

which defines a function analytic in
\sigma>1/2
. Denote its inverse Mellin transform by
W_{t}(y)
, that is

W_{t}(y)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{2-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{2+\mathrm{i}\infty}h_{t}(s)y^{s}\frac{\dif s}{s},\quad y>0.

Since
\zeta(s)
is bounded on the half-plane
\sigma\geq 2
, the integral converges absolutely and locally uniformly in
y
, so that
W_{t}(y)
is a continuous function. It is furthermore supported on
[1,\infty)
, as can be seen by shifting the contour to the right when
0<y\leq 1
.
The function
W_{t}
is the weight by which we shall average
R
. Indeed, we set

I_{t}(x)\coloneqq\int_{1^{-}}^{x}\dif R(\cdot,t)\ast\dif W_{t}=\int_{1^{-}}^{x}R\Bigl(\frac{x}{y},t\Bigr)\dif W_{t}(y)=\int_{1^{-}}^{x}W_{t}\Bigl(\frac{x}{y}\Bigr)\dif R(y,t).

Here,
\ast
denotes the multiplicative convolution of measures, a notion extending Dirichlet convolution (see e.g.
[
5
, Chapter 2]
). Now
W_{t}(y)
is differentiable, with

(3.6)

W_{t}^{\prime}(y)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{2-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{2+\mathrm{i}\infty}h_{t}(s)y^{s-1}\dif s.

Hence we have

(3.7)

I_{t}(x)=\int_{1}^{x}R\Bigl(\frac{x}{y},t\Bigr)W_{t}^{\prime}(y)\dif y.

First we bound
I_{t}(x)
by employing the bounds provided by
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{P},\Li(x))
and by bounding
W_{t}^{\prime}(y)
. Let
x
be sufficiently large so that
1/\log x<\frac{\beta_{0}-1/2}{2}
. In the Perron integral (
3.6
) for
W_{t}^{\prime}
, we shift the contour of integration to the line
\sigma=1/2+1/\log x
. The convexity bound for
\zeta(s)
provided by Lemma
3.1
yields

\zeta\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\log x}+\mathrm{i}\tau\Bigr)\ll(|\tau|+1)\log x,

hence we get

W_{t}^{\prime}(y)\ll y^{-1/2+\frac{1}{\log x}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\log x}{|1/2+1/\log x+\mathrm{i}\tau-\rho_{0}|^{m}(|\tau|+1)}\dif\tau\ll_{\rho_{0}}y^{-1/2+\frac{1}{\log x}}\log x.

Substituting this bound and the Lindelöf hypothesis for
\mathcal{P}
in the form (
3.1
) into (
3.7
) yields

(3.8)

I_{t}(x)\ll_{\varepsilon,B,\rho_{0}}\int_{1}^{x}(x/y)^{1/2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{\varepsilon}y^{-1/2}\log x\dif y=x^{1/2}(\log x)^{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{\varepsilon},

provided that
1\leq x\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{B}
and
1/\log x<\frac{\beta_{0}-1/2}{2}
.

On the other hand,

I_{t}(x)=\int_{1^{-}}^{\infty}W_{t}\Bigl(\frac{x}{y}\Bigr)\dif R(y,t)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{2-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{2+\mathrm{i}\infty}h_{t}(s)H_{t}(s)x^{s}\frac{\dif s}{s},

4.
Remarks on the definition of
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},M(x))

As touched upon in the Introduction, the definition of
\mathrm{LH}
is not intrinsic to the considered sequence, but also depends on the function
M(x)
. For example, if
\mathcal{N}
is a non-decreasing sequence with
N(x)=x+O_{A}(x/\log^{A}x)
for every

, then it is admissible both with

and
M_{2}(x)=x+x^{3/4}
. However,
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},M_{1}(x))
and
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},M_{2}(x))
are mutually exclusive. Moreover, if
\mathcal{N}
does not have too large clusters, one can always artificially construct

such that
\mathrm{LH}(\mathcal{N},M(x))
holds.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{N}=(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be a non-decreasing sequence with

, and suppose that

for some

. Then there is a non-decreasing smooth function

such that

and such that for every
\varepsilon>0

x
,
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}\geq 1

\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}n_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}=\int_{n_{1}}^{x}u^{-\mathrm{i}t}M^{\prime}(u)\dif u+O_{\varepsilon}(x^{1/2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert t\right\rvert}^{\varepsilon}).

Proof.

The idea is to define
M
such that
M^{\prime}
approximates
\sum_{j}\delta_{n_{j}}(x)
, where
\delta_{y}(x)
denotes the Dirac delta supported at
y
. Let
\varphi
be a smooth non-negative function with
\supp\varphi\subseteq[-1,1]
and
\int\varphi=1
. For
\lambda>0
we set

and

\displaystyle\psi(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 1}\varphi_{\lambda_{j}}(x-n_{j}),\quad\lambda_{j}=\mathrm{e}^{Cn_{j}};

\displaystyle M(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\psi(u)\dif u;

where

. By the “clustering condition”

, one sees that

. Let now
\varepsilon>0
and

. If

, then

We conclude that
