Volume bound for the canonical lift complement of a random geodesic
Tommaso Cremaschi, Yannick Krifka, Dídac Martínez-Granado, Franco Vargas Pallete

Abstract
. Given a filling primitive geodesic curve in a closed hyperbolic surface one obtains a hyperbolic three-manifold as the complement of the curve’s canonical lift to the projective tangent bundle. In this paper we give the first known lower bound for the volume of these manifolds in terms of the length of generic curves. We show that estimating the volume from below can be reduced to a counting problem in the unit tangent bundle and solve it by applying an exponential multiple mixing result for the geodesic flow.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Volumes of lift complements of curves

In the following we consider a hyperbolic surface
X\cong S_{g,n}
of genus
g
with
n
punctures.

Associated to
X
is the
3
-manifold
\operatorname{PT}(X)
, the projectivised tangent bundle. For any finite collection of smooth essential closed curves
\mathcal{G}
on
X
, there is a
canonical
lift
\widehat{}\mathcal{G}
in
\operatorname{PT}(X)
realized by the set of tangent lines to
\mathcal{G}
.

Drilling
\widehat{}\mathcal{G}
from
\operatorname{PT}(X)
produces a
3
-manifold
M_{\widehat{}\mathcal{G}}=\operatorname{PT}(X)\setminus\widehat{}\mathcal{G}
and when
M_{\widehat{}\mathcal{G}}
is hyperbolic, by Mostow Rigidity
[
9
]
, any invariant of
M_{\widehat{}\mathcal{G}}
naturally becomes a mapping class group invariant of
\mathcal{G}
. When
\mathcal{G}
is filling, its components are primitive, and
\mathcal{G}
is in minimal position, Foulon and Hasselblatt
[
21
]
first observed that
M_{\widehat{}\mathcal{G}}
admits a complete hyperbolic metric of finite-volume, in particular
\text{Vol}(M_{\widehat{}\mathcal{G}})
is such an invariant. One should think of
\widehat{}\mathcal{G}
as a weak version of a link diagram where “over” and “under” crossings are encoded by the tangent directions to
\mathcal{G}
. The most general such hyperbolicity result appears in
[
16
]
. The authors show that if one takes a primitive filling system
\mathcal{G}
in minimal position over a surface
X
and then drills a transverse lift
\overline{\mathcal{G}}
in a Seifert-fibered manifold
M
then, the resulting manifold is hyperbolic. Transverse lifts of such systems will be called
topological lifts
. Canonical lifts are examples of topological lifts.

In the rest of the paper we will use
\widehat{}\mathcal{G}
to denote canonical lifts of
\mathcal{G}\subseteq S
in
\operatorname{PT}(S)
and
\overline{\mathcal{G}}
to denote topological lifts of
\mathcal{G}\subseteq X
in a Seifert-fibered manifold
M
.

Several upper and lower bounds for
\text{Vol}(M_{\overline{\mathcal{G}}})
in terms of invariants of
\mathcal{G}
have been studied in recent literature, see
[
10
,
12
,
30
,
31
,
16
,
17
]
.

Going back to the special sub-class that arises by considering
M
to be
\operatorname{PT}(X)
, or the unit tangent bundle, and using the bundle projection map
\pi\colon\operatorname{PT}(X)\rightarrow X
to lift a filling geodesic
\gamma
to its canonical lift
\widehat{\gamma}
. In the case that the surface
X
is the modular surface
\operatorname{PT}(X)
can be identified with the Trefoil complement in
\mathbb S^{3}
and Ghys
[
23
]
showed that all Lorenz knots and links arise as canonical lifts of geodesics on the modular surface.
Moreover, the setup of canonical lifts has been extensively studied in
[
31
,
10
,
12
,
30
]
and others.

In
[
16
]
the authors gave an upper bound which is linear in terms of the self-intersection number of
\mathcal{G}
, a fact reminiscent of classical results in knot theory. In
[
11
]
, it is shown that for every hyperbolic structure
X
on
S
, there is a constant
C_{X}
such that
\text{Vol}(M_{\widehat{}\mathcal{G}})\leq C_{X}\ell_{X}(\mathcal{G})
, where
\ell_{X}(\mathcal{G})
denotes the length of the geodesic representative. Observe that
\operatorname{Vol}(M_{\widehat{}\mathcal{G}})
is independent of the choice of
X
and, in fact, it is mapping class group invariant (see
[
30
, 2.1]
). Using this one can easily construct a sequence of filling closed curves whose volume
\operatorname{Vol}(M_{\widehat{}\mathcal{G}})
stays bounded while its hyperbolic length goes to infinity. See
[
30
,
31
]
for many other interesting examples.

There are key differences between the volumes corresponding to canonical lifts or to topological lifts and also between simple filling systems and closed filling geodesics. In
[
16
, Corollary 1.6]
the authors construct examples in which the volume of a topological lift, which is not canonical, is asymptotic to the self-intersection number
\iota(\gamma_{n},\gamma_{n})
of the filling curves. Fixing a hyperbolic structure
X
on
S
, the self-intersection number is bounded above by
\ell_{X}(\gamma_{n})^{2}
by a result of Basmajian
[
4
]
, which is in contrast with the general length upper bound for volumes of canonical lifts of
[
11
]
.

The best known lower bound appears in
[
30
,
16
]
, where the bound is given in terms of the number of essential homotopy classes of arcs of
\mathcal{G}
after cutting
X
open along any multi-curve
\mathfrak m
and taking the maximum over such
\mathfrak m
. While this lower bound is shown to be sharp for some families of
non-simple
closed curves on the modular surface
[
30
]
, it is always at most
6(3g+n)(3g-3+n)
whenever
\mathcal{G}
is composed entirely of
simple
closed curves. This is addressed in
[
17
]
.

In
[
17
]
the authors study the setting in which one considers a filling collection of simple closed curves in minimal position instead of a primitive filling curve in minimal position. This is interesting because the only known lower bound is completely ineffective in this case (see Remark
1.2
). In
[
17
, Theorem A]
the authors relate the volume of complement of the canonical lift of a pair of filling geodesics to pants distance in the pants graph of the surface.

1.2.
Upper bounds in terms of length

We now describe in more details the length upper bound and refer to some numerical evidence. The length upper bound of
[
11
, Theorem 1.1]
is:

Theorem
.

Let
X
be a hyperbolic surface. Then, there exists
C_{X}
such that for any filling primitive geodesic
\gamma\subseteq X
:

\text{Vol}(M_{\widehat{\gamma}})\leq C_{X}\ell_{X}(\gamma)

and the result also works for multi-curves. The proof goes by showing the equivalent result in the case of the modular surface
Y=\mathbb H^{2}/SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Then, one shows that by taking branched coverings
Z
of
Y
and curves
\gamma\subseteq Y
one can obtain all filling primitive systems on any
S_{g,n}
. Then, the constant
C_{X}
comes from considering the optimal quasi-conformal map from
X
to
Z
, which is in general a non-trivial problem.

Remark 1.1
.

There are large families of filling primitive geodesics for which the volume of the complements are uniformly bounded but whose length go to infinity. The easiest such example can be obtained by taking the mapping class group orbit of a fixed curve
\gamma
. However, there are also more interesting examples in which the curves
\gamma_{n}
are not in the same mapping class group orbit, see
[
30
,
31
]
. Some of these examples can be thought of as taking a filling curve
\gamma
intersecting another curve
\alpha
once and concatenating
\gamma
with powers of
\alpha
. These are called
twist families
and will always give rise to bounded volumes families.

A sequence of random geodesics is, informally, a sequence of geodesics that gets more and more equidistributed with respect to the volume of
\operatorname{UT}(X)
and converges, up to scaling, to the Liouville measure of
\operatorname{UT}(X)
. See Subsection
2.4
for precise definitions.

A sequence of random geodesics in the modular surface has been considered by Duke in
[
19
]
. This model is constructed via number theoretic techniques. In this paper we will construct another family of random geodesics using geometry and dynamics.
It would be interesting to obtain a lower bound for the volume of Duke’s random sequence.
In
[
12
]
the authors compute the volumes for finitely many terms of Duke’s random sequence and then give numerical evidence of the linear volume growth as a function of geodesic length (see Figure
1.1
).

Lambert function
is the principal branch of the inverse of
f(w)=w\log w
which is asymptotic to
\log(x)-\log\log(x)+o(1)
.

in the modular surface (see Figure

1.3.
Lower bound

No geometric lower bounds are currently known and we only have combinatorial ones. Using work of Agol, Storm and Thurston
[
1
]
, Rodriguez-Migueles showed in
[
30
]
that:

Theorem

(Combinatorial Lower Bound)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be an essential surface decomposition of
S
and let
\gamma
be a filling primitive curve in minimal position with respect to
\partial\mathcal{P}
and itself. Then:

\frac{v_{3}}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{Q}\left(\#{\left\{\gamma\text{-arcs in }Q\right\}}-3\right)\leq\text{Vol}(M_{\widehat{\gamma}}),

where
v_{3}
is the volume of a regular ideal tetrahedra and we sum over all components of the pants decomposition
\mathcal{P}
. For a pair of pants
Q
, the
\gamma
-arcs in
Q
are the connected components of
\gamma\cap Q
(see Figure
1.3
). However, when writing “
\#{\left\{\gamma\text{-arcs in }Q\right\}}
” we mean the number of
homotopy classes
of
\gamma
-arcs in
Q
with endpoints gliding on the boundary.

For example if
Q
is a pair of pants and
\gamma
is a filling geodesic there are at most
6
simple
\gamma
-arcs in
\gamma\cap Q
up to homotopy.

\gamma
-arcs configurations (up to homotopy) consisting of simple (undirected)

Remark 1.2
.

For a simple multi-curve
\gamma
, the Combinatorial Lower Bound Theorem is ineffective since the number of
\gamma
-arcs is upper bounded by a constant independent of
\gamma
(see Figure
1.3
).

In
[
16
]
the authors show that the above lower bound also works in the setting of Seifert-fibered spaces.

1.4.
Statement of the theorem

From now on, we will assume we are working with a surface without punctures,
S=S_{g,0}
, unless otherwise stated.
Because of the length upper bound it would be nice to have a lower bound also depending on length, possibly in a linear fashion. The possible linearity of a lower-bound is hinted by the experimental data of Figure
1.1
, consisting of a finite number of terms of a sequence of random geodesics that converge to the Liouville measure (see discussion on Duke’s model in page 3).

However, as mentioned in Remark
1.1
, one can construct sequences of geodesics
\gamma_{n}
such that
\ell_{X}(\gamma_{n})\rightarrow\infty
but the volume stays bounded. Similarly, there are examples of
\gamma_{n}^{\prime}s
where the growth is bounded above by a sub-linear function (see
[
31
]
). This motivates to ask what the generic behavior of the volume is for long geodesics.

To this end let us recall the notion of asymptotic density for collections of geodesics. Given a hyperbolic surface
X
we denote its set of closed geodesics by
\mathcal{G}
. For a collection of closed geodesics
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
we denote by

\mathcal{A}(R)
all geodesics in
\mathcal{A}
of length less than
R>0
. We say that
\mathcal{A}
has
asymptotic density
\rho\in[0,1]
if

\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{\#\mathcal{A}(R)}{\#\mathcal{G}(R)}=\rho.

Intuitively, the density
\rho
describes approximately the likelihood of drawing a geodesic in the subset
\mathcal{A}
by sampling uniformly at random every closed geodesic of length at most
R
for very large
R>0
. This definition has its origins in number theory where one is interested in the asymptotic density of infinite subsets of the natural numbers.

Our main result is the following.

Theorem
7.2
.

Let
X
be a closed hyperbolic surface glued without twists from isometric pairs of pants. Further, denote by
0<\delta<1
the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set of any one of the pairs of pants.

Then for every
\eta>1(>\delta>0)
and every
F(\ell)\in O(\ell^{\delta/2\eta})
the collection of primitive, filling geodesics
\gamma
satisfying the volume estimate

F(\ell_{X}(\gamma))\leq\operatorname{Vol}(M_{\gamma})

has asymptotic density
1
.

Remark 1.3
.

Since we will only deal with canonical lifts, we simplify our notation and denote the manifold obtained by drilling the canonical lift of a closed geodesic
\gamma
from
\operatorname{PT}(X)
by
M_{\gamma}
.

1.5.
Short outline

After reviewing some background material in Section
2
and giving a more detailed sketch of proof for Theorem
7.2
in Section
3
we start in Section
4
by constructing random sequences of closed geodesics as follows.

Let us fix
\varepsilon>0
small enough and denote for every
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
its set of
\varepsilon
-return times by
R_{\varepsilon}(v)
, i.e. those
t>0
such that
g_{t}(v)
is
\varepsilon
-close to
v
.
For every
t\in R_{\varepsilon}(v)
we can close-up the geodesic segment
\gamma_{v}[0,t]
of length
t
starting at
v
by an
\varepsilon
-short arc to obtain a broken-geodesic curve
\gamma_{v}(t)
. We will be interested in its geodesic representative which we shall denote by
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
. Note that a similar model has been considered by Bonahon
[
8
, Page 151]
(see also
[
13
, Theorem 3]
,
[
34
, Claim 2.3]
).

The relevance of this construction is that by Lemma
4.8
a collection of closed geodesics
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
has asymptotic density one if for
\mu
-almost every
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
and all sufficiently long
\varepsilon
-return times
t\geq t(v)
the constructed closed geodesic
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
is in
\mathcal{A}
.
This observation will allow us to use dynamical properties of the geodesic flow more directly.

First, we show in Lemma
4.4
that as
t\in R_{\varepsilon}(v)
tends to infinity,
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
converges to the Liouville current, so that
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
will be filling for all sufficiently large
t
(Lemma
4.5
).

Next, we want to use the Combinatorial Lower Bound Theorem to find a lower bound for
\Vol(M_{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)})
. To this end we show in Proposition
5.4
that the number (of homotopy classes) of
\gamma_{v}[0,t]
-arcs is roughly the number (of homotopy classes) of
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
-arcs up to a uniform error.

In Section
6
we use dynamical techniques involving higher order exponential mixing of the geodesic flow to estimate the number of
\gamma_{v}[0,t]
-arcs (see Theorem
6.5
).

Finally, we prove the Main Theorem
7.2
in Section
7
. Using Theorem
6.5
we first prove Theorem
7.1
in Section
7
, which shows that for
\mu
-almost every
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
the constructed geodesic curves
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
satisfy the asserted volume estimate for sufficiently large times
t
. Theorem
7.2
then follows from Lemma
4.8
.

2.
Background

2.1.
Notation

We will use the following notational conventions:

•

A\cong B
denotes that the two topological spaces
A,B
are homeomorphic;

•

\alpha\simeq\beta
denotes that
\alpha,\beta
are homotopic maps or spaces, generally curves or arcs.

For two functions
f,g\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
we will use the following notations, where
\mathbb{T}
stands for
\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{R}
:

•

f(t)\sim g(t)
means that the two functions
f,g
are asymptotic, i.e.

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{f(t)}{g(t)}=1;

•

f(t)\ll g(t)
means that there is a constant
C>0
and
T_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
such that
f(t)\leq Cg(t)
for all
t\geq T_{0}
;

•

f(t)\asymp g(t)
means that there are constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
and
T_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
such that

C_{1}f(t)\leq g(t)\leq C_{2}f(t)

for all
t\geq T_{0}
.

2.2.
Dynamics

We recall some concepts of dynamics that will play a key role in this paper.
Standard references are
[
28
, Page 151]
,
[
35
]
.

Let
X
be a topological space equipped with a (Borel) probability measure
\mu
.
Suppose that
g_{t}\colon X\to X
is a continuous
flow
, for
t\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e. a family of continuous maps so that
g_{0}
is the identity and

g_{s+t}=g_{s}\circ g_{t}
for all
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
.
We say
\mu
is
g
-invariant if for any Borel set
A\subseteq X
,
\mu(A)=\mu(g_{t}(A))
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
There is an analogous picture for discrete time dynamical systems, where instead of a flow, we have a continuous map
T\colon X\to X
(which need not be invertible) and
\mu
is said to be
T
-invariant if
\mu(B)=\mu(T^{-1}(B))
for every Borel set
B\subseteq X
.
One can go from a continuous to a discrete setting by taking
T=g_{1}
, the
time-one flow map
, and we will be doing so throughout the paper.
We will focus on two properties that a continuous or discrete dynamical system can have: ergodicity and mixing.

2.2.1.
Ergodicity

The property of ergodicity will be used to construct our geometric random model.
A flow
g_{t}\colon X\to X
(resp. transformation
T\colon X\to X
) is
ergodic
with respect to
\mu
if for every subset
A\subseteq X
which is
g
-invariant
g_{t}(A)=A
(resp.
T
-invariant
T^{-1}(A)=A
), then either
\mu(A)=0
or
\mu(X\backslash A)=0
.

If
P_{x}
is a property depending on a point
x\in X
, we say that
P
holds for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
if it holds for a subset
B\subseteq X
so that
\mu(B)=1
.

Ergodic dynamical systems satisfy the following two properties that we will use.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
35
, Theorem 1.7]
)
.

Let
X
be a compact metric space,
T\colon X\to X
a continuous transformation and
\mu
a probability measure so that
\mu(U)>0
for all open set
U\subseteq X
.
Suppose that
\mu
is
T
-invariant and
T
is ergodic with respect to
\mu
.
Then for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
the orbit of
x
, i.e.
\{T^{n}(x):x\in X\}
, is dense.

Theorem 2.2

(Birkhoff’s Ergodic Theorem;
[
35
, Theorem 1.14]
)
.

Let
X
be a topological space equipped with a probability measure
\mu
. For
g_{t}\colon X\rightarrow X
an ergodic flow with respect to
\mu
, and
f
any measurable function, we have that for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
:

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}f(g_{t}(x))dt=\intop\nolimits_{X}fd\mu

For a discrete system, the flow is replaced by a transformation
T
, the integral by a sum, and the limit in the previous theorem takes the following form

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{N-1}f(T^{n}(x))=\intop\nolimits_{X}fd\mu

for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
.

One also says that the orbits
\{g_{t}(x)\,|\,t\in[0,T]\}
resp.
\{x,T(x),\ldots,T^{N}(x)\}
equidistribute as
T\to\infty
resp.
N\to\infty
.

2.2.2.
Mixing

We will need a stronger property than ergodicity to have enough control on our model.
We say
\mu
is
mixing
for the flow
g_{t}\colon X\to X
if for all
f,g\in L^{2}(X,\mu)
, the
correlation function

\rho(t)\coloneqq\intop\nolimits_{X}(f\circ g_{t})gd\mu-\intop\nolimits_{X}fd\mu\intop\nolimits_{X}gd\mu,

satisfies
\rho(t)\to 0
as
t\to+\infty
.
For discrete systems, the correlation is written as

\rho(n)\coloneqq\intop\nolimits_{X}(f\circ T^{n})gd\mu-\intop\nolimits_{X}fd\mu\intop\nolimits_{X}gd\mu,

and mixing means
\rho(n)\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
.

We will be interested in extending this correlation function to more than two functions, as well as in quantifying its decay. This will lead to the notion of exponential
k
-mixing, see Appendix
B
.

Another equivalent way to phrase mixing is that, for all Borel sets
A,B\subseteq X
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(T^{-n}(A)\cap B)=\mu(A)\mu(B)

It follows that mixing implies ergodicity.

Intuitively, mixing means that for any two Borel sets
A,B\subseteq X
the events
x\in B
and
T^{n}(x)\in A
become more and more independent as
n\to\infty
.

In this paper, we will let
g_{t}
be the geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle
\operatorname{UT}(X)
, and
\mu

will be the normalized Liouville probability measure.
T
will be the time-one map of the geodesic flow.
The geodesic flow is mixing with respect to
\mu
(see
[
3
, Theorem 1]
), and is by the above discussion, also ergodic.

2.2.3.
Anosov closing

It is known that the geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle
Y=\operatorname{UT}(X)
of a closed hyperbolic surface
X
is
hyperbolic/Anosov
, i.e. there is a smooth
g_{t}
invariant splitting
TY=E^{s}\oplus E^{c}\oplus E^{u}
and constants
C\geq 1
,
\alpha>0
such that

•

E^{c}_{x}
is spanned by the direction of the flow
\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}g_{t}(x)
,

•

\|g_{t}(v)\|_{g_{t}(x)}\leq Ce^{-\alpha t}\|v\|_{x}
for all
v\in E^{s}_{x}
, i.e.
g_{t}
is exponentially contracting along
E^{s}
,

•

\|g_{t}(w)\|_{g_{t}(x)}\geq C^{-1}e^{\alpha t}\|w\|_{x}
for all
w\in E^{u}_{x}
, i.e.
g_{t}
is exponentially expanding along
E^{u}
,

for all
x\in Y
,
t\geq 0
. The vectors in
E^{s}
(resp.
E^{u}
) are called the
stable
(resp.
unstable
) directions.

In our concrete situation we can describe this splitting in the following way. Let
\Gamma\leq G=\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
be a torsion-free cocompact lattice such that
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
. The action of
G
on
\operatorname{UT}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
is free and transitive, and thus one may identify
\operatorname{UT}(\mathbb{H}^{2})\cong G
. This descends to an identification
\Gamma\backslash\operatorname{UT}(\mathbb{H}^{2})\cong\operatorname{UT}(X)\cong\Gamma\backslash G
. Using the right-action of
G
on
\Gamma\backslash G
we can identify the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
with the tangent space
T_{x}(\Gamma\backslash G)
for every
x\in\Gamma\backslash G
:

\mathfrak{g}\coloneqq\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to T_{x}(\Gamma\backslash G),\quad X\mapsto\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}x\cdot\exp(t\cdot X).

Recall that a basis of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is given by the matrices

H\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}&0\\
0&-\frac{1}{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad X_{+}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad X_{-}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

This basis amounts to an Anosov splitting of
T(\Gamma\backslash G)
for the geodesic flow
g_{t}
via the identification
T_{x}(\Gamma\backslash G)\cong\mathfrak{g}
:

E^{c}_{x}\cong\mathbb{R}\cdot H,\quad E^{s}_{x}\cong\mathbb{R}\cdot X_{+},\quad E^{u}_{x}\cong\mathbb{R}\cdot X_{-}.

Indeed,
g_{t}\colon\Gamma\backslash G\to\Gamma\backslash G
is given by right-multiplication
g_{t}(x)=xa_{t}
with the diagonal matrix

a_{t}\coloneqq\exp(t\cdot H)=\begin{pmatrix}e^{t/2}&0\\
0&e^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix},

and

\operatorname{Ad}_{a_{t}}(X_{\pm})=a_{t}X_{\pm}a_{-t}=e^{\pm t}X_{\pm}.

Anosov flows were subject to extensive research in the past which led to a detailed understanding of their dynamical properties; we refer to
[
22
]
for a detailed exposition. One of these properties is the
Anosov Closing Lemma
which intuitively says that near every “almost-periodic” orbit of an Anosov flow there is a truly periodic orbit. We will only need the following special version:

Lemma 2.3

(Anosov Closing Lemma)
.

Let
X
be a closed hyperbolic surface and let
\rho>0
. There are positive numbers
0<\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\rho)\leq\rho
and
0<T=T(\rho)
such that the following holds:

For every
t\geq T
,
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
with
d(g_{t}(v),v)<\varepsilon
there exist
t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
and
v^{\prime}\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
such that

|t-t^{\prime}|\leq\rho,\quad d(v,v^{\prime})\leq\rho,\quad g_{t^{\prime}}(v^{\prime})=v^{\prime}.

Moreover, the geodesics
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}
with respective starting vectors
\dot{\gamma}(0)=v,\dot{\gamma}^{\prime}(0)=v^{\prime}
stay close to each other:

(2.1)

\displaystyle d(\gamma(s\cdot t),\gamma^{\prime}(s\cdot t^{\prime}))\leq 2\rho

for every
s\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

This follows from
[
20
, 4.5.15 Proposition]
and the convexity of the distance function in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.
∎

2.2.4.
Asymptotic density of collections of closed geodesics

Given a hyperbolic surface
X
we will denote its set of closed geodesics by
\mathcal{G}
. Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
be a collection of closed geodesics. For every
R>0
we will denote by

\mathcal{A}(R)\coloneqq\{\gamma\in\mathcal{A}\,|\,\ell_{X}(\gamma)\leq R\}

all closed geodesics in
\mathcal{A}
of length less than
R
. We say that
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
has
asymptotic density
\rho\in[0,1]
if

\frac{\#\mathcal{A}(R)}{\#\mathcal{G}(R)}\to\rho\quad(R\to+\infty).

Remark 2.4
.

By work of Huber
[
26
]
(and Delsarte and Selberg) it is known that

\#\mathcal{G}(R)\sim\frac{e^{R}}{R}

as
R\to+\infty
; (see also Margulis
[
29
]
for compact negatively curved manifolds).

2.3.
Topology

In the following sections we recall some facts and definitions about the topology of surfaces and 3-manifolds. For references, see
[
25
,
24
,
27
]
.

Definition 2.5
.

A
knot
in
M
will be any embedding of
\mathbb S^{1}
into a 3-manifold
M
.

Definition 2.6
.

Given a curve
\gamma\subseteq S
and the projective tangent bundle
\operatorname{PT}(S)
we define the
canonical lift
to be the knot
\widehat{\gamma}\subseteq\operatorname{PT}(S)
obtained by taking the lift of
\gamma
given by the tangential line field.

We say that a curve is in
minimal position
if the number of self-intersections is minimal in its homotopy class.

We recall the combinatorial lower-bound Theorem:

Theorem 2.7

(
[
30
, Theorem 1.5]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be an essential surface decomposition of
S
and let
\gamma
be a filling primitive curve in minimal position with respect to
\partial\mathcal{P}
and itself. Then:

\frac{v_{3}}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{Q}\left(\#{\left\{\gamma\text{-arcs in }Q\right\}}-3\right)\leq\text{Vol}(M_{\widehat{\gamma}}),

where
v_{3}
is the volume of a regular ideal tetrahedra and we sum over all components of the pants decomposition
\mathcal{P}
.

For a pair of pants
Q
, the
\gamma
-arcs in
Q
are the connected components of
\gamma\cap Q
(see Figure
1.3
). However, when writing “
\#{\left\{\gamma\text{-arcs in }Q\right\}}
” we mean the number of
homotopy classes
of
\gamma
-arcs in
Q
with endpoints gliding on the boundary.

Remark 2.8
.

It is important to note that these
\gamma
-arcs are
undirected
. Moreover, even though our main results involve curve complements in the projective tangent bundle, in order to avoid talking about orientations, our proofs will involve the unit tangent bundle. This is because we want to take advantage of the geodesic flow which is naturally defined in
\operatorname{UT}(X)
, a double cover of
\operatorname{PT}(X)
.

2.4.
Geometry

A
geodesic current
is a positive finite
Radon measure
\mu
on

\operatorname{UT}(X)
which is invariant under the geodesic flow, in the sense
that
(g_{t})_{*}(\mu)=\mu
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where the subscript
*
denotes the push-forward of measures.

A closed geodesic
\gamma
can be seen as a geodesic current. Consider the canonical
lift
\widehat{\gamma}
of
\gamma
to
\operatorname{UT}(X)
; this is a periodic orbit
of
g_{t}
. By abuse of notation, let
\gamma
also denote the length-normalized
\delta
-function on
this orbit. That is, for an open set
U
we set
\gamma(U)
to
be the total length of
\widehat{\gamma}\cap U
with respect to the
Riemannian metric
\operatorname{UT}(X)
.
The geometric intersection number between closed geodesics extends continuously to a bilinear form
i(\cdot,\cdot)
on geodesic currents
[
7
, Proposition 4.5]
.
The
Liouville current

\mathcal{L}_{X}
associated to the hyperbolic metric
X
is the Liouville volume of
\operatorname{UT}(X)
normalized so that
i(\mathcal{L}_{X},\gamma)=\ell_{X}(\gamma)
for any closed geodesic
\gamma
.

A
sequence of random geodesics
is a sequence
(\gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of closed geodesics so that, after normalization, the geodesic currents corresponding to
\gamma_{n}
converge in the weak
∗
-topology to the Liouville current, i.e.

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{4\pi^{2}|\chi(S)|}{\ell(\gamma_{n})}\intop\nolimits_{\operatorname{UT}(X)}fd\gamma_{n}\to\intop\nolimits_{\operatorname{UT}(X)}fd\mathcal{L}_{X}

for any continuous and compactly supported function
f\in C_{c}(\operatorname{UT}(X))
.

Definition 2.9

(Unit tangent bundle decomposition)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a geodesic pants decomposition of
X\cong S_{g,k}
. Pick a pant
P^{j}\in\mathcal{P}
and let
\{o_{i}^{j}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be the collection of orthogeodesics in
P^{j}
. For
\mu
-almost every vector
v\in\operatorname{UT}(P^{j})
there are minimal real numbers
a,b>0
such that the geodesic arc
\gamma\colon[-a,b]\to P^{j}
with
\dot{\gamma}(0)=v
intersects
\partial P^{j}
in
\gamma(-a)
and
\gamma(b)
. We define
U_{i}^{j}\subseteq\operatorname{UT}(P^{j})
to be the set of all directions
v
such that the corresponding directed arc
\gamma\colon[-a,b]\to P^{j}
is freely homotopic to
o_{i}^{j}
in
P^{j}
with gliding endpoints
\gamma(-a),\gamma(b)\in\partial P^{j}
. This amounts to the following decomposition up to measure zero (see
[
14
, Section 7]
)

\operatorname{UT}(X)=\bigcupop\displaylimits_{j}\operatorname{UT}(P^{j})=\bigcupop\displaylimits_{i,j}U_{i}^{j},

where the second equality is up to a
\mu
-measure zero set.

Remark 2.10
.

This definition is relevant in view of the combinatorial lower-bound from Theorem
2.7
. Indeed, for a geodesic curve
\gamma
the number of (homotopy classes of)
\gamma
-arcs is half the number of different sets
\{U_{i}^{j}\}_{i,j}
that
\dot{\gamma}(t),t\in[0,\ell(\gamma)],
visits. Here, the factor
\tfrac{1}{2}
is due to the fact that
\gamma
-arcs are undirected; see Remark
2.8
.

2.5.
Counting arcs

Let
P
be a hyperbolic pair of pants with totally geodesic boundary components. Given two (possibly the same) boundary components
C_{-},C_{+}\subseteq\partial P
one may consider arcs starting on
C_{-}
and ending on
C_{+}
. In each relative homotopy class of such an arc, where we allow each endpoint to glide on the respective boundary component, there is a unique geodesic arc. It meets
C_{-}
and
C_{+}
perpendicularly and is called an
orthogeodesic arc
.

One may now ask how many orthogeodesics running from
C_{-}
to
C_{+}
of length
\leq\ell
there are. Let us denote by
N_{C_{-},C_{+}}(\ell)
the number of such orthogeodesic arcs.
In
[
33
]
Parkkonen and Paulin consider the same counting problem in the more general setting of pinched negatively curved manifolds and properly immersed closed locally convex subsets
C_{-},C_{+}
. Applying their result
[
33
, Theorem 1]
to our situation we obtain the following corollary:

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
0<\delta<1
denote the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set of
P
. Then there is a constant
C_{0}>0
such that asymptotically

N_{C_{-},C_{+}}(\ell)\sim C_{0}\cdot e^{\delta\ell}

as
\ell\to\infty
.

We want to point out that similar counting results in varying generality were obtained before by different authors; see
[
32
]
and the references therein.

2.6.
Sobolev Norms

Denote
G\coloneqq\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
and let
\Gamma\leq G
be a cocompact lattice.

Let
d_{G}\colon G\times G\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a left-invariant metric on
G
.
This metric descends to a metric on the quotient
d_{\Gamma\backslash G}\colon\Gamma\backslash G\times\Gamma\backslash G\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
We may assume that
d_{G}
is induced by a left-invariant Riemannian metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
G
.
In particular, we can equip
G
with such a metric via the identification
G\cong\operatorname{UT}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
.
We denote by
\nu
the corresponding (bi-)invariant Haar measure on
G
.

The left-action of
\Gamma
on
G
amounts to a quotient map
\pi\colon G\to\Gamma\backslash G,g\mapsto\Gamma g
.
Because the action
\Gamma\ \rotatebox[origin]{-180.0}{$\circlearrowright$}\ G
is isometric the metric
d_{G}
descends to a metric
d_{\Gamma\backslash G}\colon\Gamma\backslash G\times\Gamma\backslash G\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, and we obtain a Riemannian metric on the quotient, which we shall denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
as well, such that
\pi\colon G\to\Gamma\backslash G
is a Riemannian covering map.
The corresponding volume form amounts to a right-invariant quotient measure
\mu
on
\Gamma\backslash G
.
After possibly rescaling we may assume that
\mu
is a probability measure. In this way
\mu
coincides with the normalized Liouville measure on
\operatorname{UT}(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2})
via the usual identification
\Gamma\backslash G\cong\operatorname{UT}(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2})
.

Note that the left-action

\displaystyle G\times\Gamma\backslash G

\displaystyle\to G,

\displaystyle(g,\Gamma h)

\displaystyle\mapsto g\cdot\Gamma h\coloneqq\Gamma hg^{-1},

is probability measure preserving by right-invariance of the quotient probability measure
\mu
.
Thus the regular representation
\lambda\colon G\to\mathcal{U}(L^{2}(\Gamma\backslash G))
is unitary, where we denote

(\lambda_{g}f)(\Gamma h)=f(g^{-1}\cdot\Gamma h)=f(\Gamma hg)

for every
g\in G
,
f\in L^{2}(\Gamma\backslash G,\mu)
.

More generally, whenever there is a smooth
G
-action
G\ \rotatebox[origin]{-180.0}{$\circlearrowright$}\ M
on a smooth manifold
M
(e.g.
M=G
or
M=\Gamma\backslash G
), there is an induced action of the universal envelopping algebra
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
on the space of smooth functions with compact support
C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
. This action is given via differentiation of the left regular representation
\lambda\colon G\to C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
as follows

(X\cdot\varphi)(x)\coloneqq\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}(\lambda_{\exp(tX)}\varphi)(x)=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\varphi(\exp(-tX)\cdot x)

for all
X\in\mathfrak{g}
,
x\in M
,
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
.

We interpret the Riemannian metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
as an inner product on the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
(of left-invariant vector fields), and pick an orthonormal basis
E_{1},E_{2},E_{3}
.
Given a multi-index
\alpha=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\in\{1,2,3\}^{d}
of degree
\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert\coloneqq d
we may define

E_{\alpha}\cdot\varphi\coloneqq E_{i_{1}}E_{i_{2}}\cdots E_{i_{d}}\cdot\varphi

for every
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
.
We use the convention
\alpha=\emptyset
iff
\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert=0
, and define

E_{\emptyset}\cdot\varphi=\varphi.

This allows us to define the
(degree
d
) Sobolev norm

\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}_{d}(\varphi)\coloneqq\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert\leq d}\|E_{\alpha}\cdot\varphi\|_{2}

for all
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Gamma\backslash G)
. The
(degree
d
) Sobolev space
H^{d}(\Gamma\backslash G)
is by definition the completion of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Gamma\backslash G)
with respect to
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
.

The following version of the Sobolev Embedding Theorem applies.

Theorem 2.12

(
[
2
, Theorem 2.10]
)
.

If
(d-r)/3>1/2
then
H^{d}(\Gamma\backslash G)\subseteq C^{r}(\Gamma\backslash G)
and the identity operator is continuous. Here
r\geq 0
is an integer and
C^{r}(\Gamma\backslash G)
is the space of
C^{r}
-functions with norm
\|\varphi\|_{C^{r}}\coloneqq\max_{0\leq\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert\leq r}\|E_{\alpha}\cdot\varphi\|_{\infty}
,
\varphi\in C^{r}(\Gamma\backslash G)
.

Corollary 2.13
.

In particular, if the degree
d=3
and
r=1
then there is a
Sobolev constant

K_{\text{Sob}}>0
such that

\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{C^{1}}\leq K_{\text{Sob}}\cdot\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(\varphi)

for all
\varphi\in H^{3}(\Gamma\backslash G)
, where we dropped the degree
d=3
in
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(\varphi)=\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}_{3}(\varphi)
.

Recall that
L^{1}(G)
is a Banach algebra when we define multiplication by convolution:

\displaystyle(f_{1}*f_{2})(g)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\intop\nolimits_{G}f_{1}(h)\cdot f_{2}(h^{-1}g)\,d\nu(h)

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{G}f_{1}(gh)\cdot f_{2}(h^{-1})\,d\nu(h)\qquad\forall g\in G\quad\forall f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{1}(G)

There is a Banach algebra action of
L^{1}(G)
on
L^{2}(\Gamma\backslash G)
given by convolution

\displaystyle(\psi*f)(\Gamma g)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\intop\nolimits_{G}\psi(h)\cdot(\lambda_{h^{-1}}f)(\Gamma g)\,d\nu(h)

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{G}\psi(h)\cdot f(\Gamma gh)\,d\nu(h),

for all
\Gamma g\in\Gamma\backslash G,\psi\in L^{1}(G),f\in L^{2}(\Gamma\backslash G)
. An application of Fubini shows that

\|\psi*f\|_{2}\leq\|\psi\|_{1}\cdot\|f\|_{2}.

Moreover, we have the following lemma familiar from the situation in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, let
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(G)
, and let
f\in L^{2}(\Gamma\backslash G)
.

Then:

(1)

\psi*f
is smooth;

(2)

E_{\alpha}\cdot(\psi*f)=(E_{\alpha}\cdot\psi)*f
for all multi-indices
\alpha
;

(3)

\operatorname{supp}(\psi*f)\subseteq N_{\varepsilon}(\operatorname{supp}(f))
, if
\operatorname{supp}(\psi)\subseteq B_{\varepsilon}(e)
.

Proof.

(1)

This will follow from (2).

(2)

By induction on
\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert
it is enough to show this for
\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert=1
. Let
E\coloneqq E_{i}
be a basis vector. We compute:

\displaystyle(E\cdot(\psi*f))(\Gamma g)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\intop\nolimits_{G}\psi(h)\cdot f(\Gamma g\exp(tE)h)\,d\nu(h)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\intop\nolimits_{G}\psi(\exp(-tE)h)\cdot f(\Gamma gh)\,d\nu(h)

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{G}(E\cdot\psi)(h)\cdot f(\Gamma gh)\,d\nu(h)

\displaystyle=((E\cdot\psi)*f)(\Gamma g)

(3)

Recall that

(\psi*f)(\Gamma g)=\intop\nolimits_{G}\psi(h)\cdot f(\Gamma gh)\,d\nu(h).

Thus, if
(\psi*f)(\Gamma g)\neq 0
then there is
h\in\operatorname{supp}(\psi)\subseteq B_{\varepsilon}(e)
such that
\Gamma gh=h^{-1}\cdot\Gamma g\in\operatorname{supp}(f)
. Hence,

d_{\Gamma\backslash G}(\Gamma gh,\Gamma g)\leq d_{G}(gh,g)=d_{G}(h,e)<\varepsilon.

This shows that
\Gamma g\in N_{\varepsilon}(\operatorname{supp}(f))
.

∎

3.
Outline of the Main Theorem

We now outline the construction and proof of the main result. We fix a closed hyperbolic surface
X
, with injectivity radius
\rho
and with an isometric geodesic pants decomposition
\mathcal{P}
. We use Lemma
4.8
to show that the set of geodesics obtained by the construction we now describe has asymptotic density one (in the sense of Subsection
2.2.4
). Let
\gamma_{v}[0,t]
denote the ray starting at
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
, parameterised by arc length, and flowing for time
t
so
that
d(g_{t}(v),v)<\varepsilon
. We close up
\gamma_{v}[0,t]
by connecting its endpoints via a short arc to obtain the broken geodesic path
\gamma_{v}(t)
. Let

\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
be the corresponding geodesic representative and let
\widehat{\gamma}^{p}_{v}(t)
be the associated primitive sub-curve
of
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
. We then break the proof of the main Theorem
7.2
into the proof of the corresponding statement for the model of geodesic obtained by the geodesic flow which is Theorem
7.1
:

Theorem
7.1
.

Let
\eta>1(>\delta>0)
and let
F(t)\in O(t^{\delta/2\eta})
. Then for
\mu
-almost every
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
there is
T^{\prime}=T^{\prime}(v)
such that
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
is filling and

F(\ell_{X}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)))\leq\Vol(M_{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}^{p}(t)})

for every
t\in R_{\varepsilon}(v)\cap[T^{\prime}(v),+\infty)
. Moreover, as long as
F
is increasing we obtain that
F(\ell_{X}(\widehat{\gamma}^{p}_{v}(t)))\leq\Vol(M_{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}^{p}(t)})
.

Sketch of proof.

(1)

Given a pair of pants decomposition
\mathcal{P}
of
S
, recall the decomposition of
\operatorname{UT}(X)
from Definition
2.9
.
We consider the orthogeodesics
o^{j}_{i}
contained in some pair of pants
P^{j}
and define the set
U^{j}_{i}
of all unit tangent vectors
v\in\operatorname{UT}(P^{j})

whose associated geodesic is in the free homotopy class of
o^{j}_{i}
(see Subsection
6.2
).

Section
6.3
estimates the number of regions
U^{j}_{i}
visited by a ray
g_{[0,t]}(v)
of length
t
. We show that for every
\eta>1
and any (sub-linear) function

F(t)\in O\left(t^{\delta/2\eta}\right)

this number grows for
\mu
-almost every
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
at least like
F(t)

for
t
large enough, where
0<\delta<1
is the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set of any of the constituting (isometric) pairs of pants in
X
.

(2)

From our construction of the curves
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
,
t\in R_{\varepsilon}(v)
, by closing up
\gamma_{v}[0,t]
in section
4
we get that

t-\rho\leq\ell(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t))\leq t+\rho.

Moreover, we have that the primitive geodesic representative
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}^{p}(t)
satisfies

\ell(\widehat{\gamma}^{p}_{v}(t))\leq\ell(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)).

(3)

Section
5
shows that:

\#\{\gamma_{v}[0,t]\mbox{-arcs}\}-6\leq\#\{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)\mbox{-arcs}\}=\#\{\widehat{\gamma}^{p}_{v}(t)\mbox{-arcs}\}.

(4)

By (3) and Section
6.3
, we obtain that asymptotically

\displaystyle\frac{v_{3}}{2}F(t)-3v_{3}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{v_{3}}{2}\#\{\gamma_{v}[0,t]\mbox{-arcs}\}-3v_{3}\leq\frac{v_{3}}{2}\#\{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)\mbox{-arcs}\}

\displaystyle=\frac{v_{3}}{2}\#\{\widehat{\gamma}^{p}_{v}(t)\mbox{-arcs}\}\leq\Vol(M_{{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}^{p}(t)}})+C(g),

where
C(g)
is some constant only depending on the topological complexity of
X
.

(5)

Because
t-\rho\leq\ell_{X}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v})\leq t+\rho
and
F(t)\in O\left(t^{\delta/2\eta}\right)
there exists a
B>0
such that:

\frac{v_{3}}{2}F(t+\rho)-B\leq\frac{v_{3}}{2}F(t)-3v_{3},

Thus, for large
t
we have:

\frac{v_{3}}{2}F(\ell_{X}({\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)}))-B\leq\frac{v_{3}}{2}F(t+\rho)-B\leq\frac{v_{3}}{2}F(t)-3v_{3}\leq\Vol(M_{\widehat{\gamma}^{p}_{v}(t)})+C(g).

(6)

Putting all the steps together, we obtain that

O\left(\ell_{X}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t))^{\delta/2\eta}\right)\leq\Vol(M_{\widehat{\gamma}^{p}_{v}(t)})

with
0<\delta<1,
and
\eta>1
.

∎

Then the proof of the main Theorem
7.2
follows from Theorem
7.1
, Lemma
4.8
and the fact that primitive geodesics have density one:

Theorem
7.2
.

Let
\eta>1(>\delta>0)
and let
F(t)\in O(t^{\delta/2\eta})
.

Then the collection of filling, primitive closed geodesics satisfying

F(\ell_{X}(\gamma))\leq\Vol(M_{\gamma})

has asymptotic density one.

4.
Random sequences of closed geodesics and asymptotic density

In this section we will discuss a natural way of constructing sequences of closed geodesics. Similar constructions have already been considered in
[
8
]
,
[
13
]
and
[
34
]
. Finally, we will see how this is related to the notion of asymptotic density.

4.1.
Closing-up along geodesics

We fix
0<\rho<\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{inj}(X)
, and choose
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\rho)>0
and
T=T(\rho)>0
as in the Anosov Closing Lemma
2.3
. Further, let
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
and denote by
R_{\varepsilon}(v)=\{t>0\,|\,d(g_{t}(v),v)<\varepsilon\}
its
\varepsilon
-return times as before. Therefore, for every
\varepsilon
-return time
t\in R_{\varepsilon}(v)\cap[T,+\infty)
after
T=T(\rho)>0
we find a periodic vector
v^{\prime}\in B_{\rho}(v)
with period
t^{\prime}\in[t-\rho,t+\rho]
. Moreover, the geodesics
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}
with respective starting vectors
\dot{\gamma}(0)=v,\dot{\gamma}^{\prime}(0)=v^{\prime}
stay close to each other:

d(\gamma(s\cdot t),\gamma^{\prime}(s\cdot t^{\prime}))\leq 2\rho<\operatorname{inj}(X)

for every
s\in[0,1]
. Note that
R_{\varepsilon}(v)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is unbounded for
\mu
-almost every vector
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
by ergodicity of the geodesic flow.

Remark 4.1
.

We will need, in Section
5
, that
\rho
is smaller than the minimal distance
o
between any two pants curves in
\mathcal{P}
. However, this is easily achieved by shrinking the cuff length
c
in the isometric pants decomposition. Indeed,
0<\rho<\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{inj}(X)\to 0
and
o\to+\infty
as
c\to 0
.

The closed geodesic
\gamma^{\prime}
can be characterized as follows. We form a broken geodesic curve
\gamma_{v}(t)
by concatenating the geodesic segment
\gamma|_{[0,t]}
with the short geodesic arc
\alpha
connecting the endpoint
\gamma(t)
to the starting point
\gamma(0)
. Observe that
\ell(\alpha)\leq\rho
because
d(v,v^{\prime})\leq\rho<\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{inj}(X)
. By construction
\gamma_{v}(t)
is freely homotopic to
\gamma^{\prime}
. Therefore,
\gamma^{\prime}
is the unique closed geodesic in the free homotopy class of
\gamma_{v}(t)
, i.e. one obtains
\gamma^{\prime}
by pulling tight
\gamma_{v}(t)
.

Definition 4.2
.

We will use the notation
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)\coloneqq\gamma^{\prime}
. Moreover, we will denote by
\widehat{\gamma}^{p}_{v}(t)
its primitive subcurve. With an abuse of notation we will use
M_{\widehat{\gamma}^{p}_{v}(t)}
to denote
\operatorname{PT}(X)
minus the canonical lift of
\widehat{\gamma}^{p}_{v}(t)
.

In this way we obtain for
\mu
-almost every unit tangent vector
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
an infinite collection of geodesics
\{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)\}_{t\in R_{\varepsilon}(v)\cap[T,+\infty)}
. We will study the asymptotic properties of these closed-up geodesics as
t
tends to infinity in the following.

Remark 4.3
.

Because periodic orbits of the geodesic flow are dense (see
[
18
, Theorem 3.3]
) we have that, in general, the curve
\gamma_{v}(t)
will not be primitive. This is because we can find vectors
w
arbitrarily close to a periodic orbit
\beta
, i.e. they track the period orbit for a long time
t
. Thus, if
\beta
is filling we can obtain arbitrarily long curves
\widehat{\gamma}_{w}(t)
that are homotopic to arbitrarily high powers of
\beta
.

Let us first show that
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
converges to a filling geodesic current:

Lemma 4.4
.

For
\mu
-almost every
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
the closed geodesics
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
satisfy

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)}{\ell_{X}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t))}=\mathcal{L}_{X}

as geodesic currents, where
t\in R_{\varepsilon}(v)\cap[T,+\infty)
and
\mathcal{L}_{X}
denotes the Liouville current associated to the hyperbolic structure
X
(see definitions in Subsection
2.4
)

Proof.

By the Birkhoff Ergodic Theorem
2.2
it suffices to prove that for every continuous function
f\colon\operatorname{UT}(X)\to\mathbb{R}
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{\ell_{X}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t))}\intop\nolimits_{\operatorname{UT}(X)}fd\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}f(g_{s}(v))ds.

Take a transversal
D
to the leaf of the geodesic foliation the current
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)/\ell_{X}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t))
is supported on, intersecting at points
x_{1}(t),\cdots,x_{k}(t)
.
Let
y_{1}(t),\cdots,y_{k}(t)
be the intersection points of the leaf corresponding to
g_{s}(v)
for
s\in(-\infty,\infty)
.
We claim that for
t
long enough,
y_{i}(t)
and
x_{i}(t)
are close.
One way to see this is to look at the endpoints at infinity in the bands model, and note that the endpoints of the closed broken geodesic
\gamma_{v}(t)
and the corresponding closed geodesic
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
are the same, and are in a neighborhood of the boundary at infinity of the endpoints of
\gamma_{v}(t)
of radius
\varepsilon
that goes to
0
as
t
goes to infinity.
∎

As a result we obtain that for large enough
t\in R_{\varepsilon}(v)\cap[T,+\infty)
the geodesics
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
is filling:

Lemma 4.5
.

For
\mu
-almost every
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
there is
t^{*}\coloneqq t^{*}(v)\geq T(\rho)>0
such that the curve
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
is filling for every
t\in R_{\varepsilon}(v)\cap[t^{*},+\infty)
.

Proof.

For a geodesic current
\mu
, let
\sys\coloneqq\inf\{i(\mu,c):c\mbox{ closed curve }\}
be the systole of
\mu
.
By Lemma
4.4
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)/\ell(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t))\to\mathcal{L}_{X}
. Since
\mathcal{L}_{X}
is filling, by
[
6
, Theorem 1.3]
), we have
\sys(\mathcal{L}_{X})>0
. The result then follows since
\sys\colon\mathcal{GC}(S)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a continuous function on currents (
[
6
, Corollary 1.5]
).
∎

Definition 4.6

(Filling time)
.

We call the above time
t^{*}(v)
the
filling time
of
v
.

We conclude the section by observing that, by ergodicity, it follows that by closing-up along almost every geodesic the volume of the corresponding complements goes to infinity.

Lemma 4.7
.

For
\mu
-almost every
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
we have that
\text{Vol}(M_{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)})\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

Consider the decomposition of
\operatorname{UT}(X)
into the subsets
U_{i}^{j}\subseteq\operatorname{UT}(X)
of vectors corresponding to the homotopy classes of all orthogeodesics from Definition
2.9
. Therefore, for a closed geodesic
\gamma
the number of different homotopy classes of (undirected)
\gamma
-arcs is half the number of different sets
\{U_{i}^{j}\}_{i,j}
, that
\dot{\gamma}(t),t\in[0,\ell(\gamma)],
visits.

By Birkhoff’s Ergodic Theorem
2.2
,

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\chi_{U^{j}_{i}}(g_{t}(v))dt=\intop\nolimits_{\operatorname{UT}(X)}\chi_{U^{j}_{i}}(w)d\mu(w)=\mu(U^{j}_{i})>0

for
\mu
-almost every
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
and all
i,j
. Consequently, the geodesic ray
\gamma_{v}[0,T]
in
\mu
-almost every direction
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
will eventually intersect every subset
U^{j}_{i}
such that
\gamma_{v}[0,T]
will eventually have a subarc in every homotopy class
o^{j}_{i}
.

Later, in section
5
, we will see that the number of homotopy classes of subarcs of
\gamma_{v}[0,T]
is roughly the number of homotopy classes of subarcs of
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(T)
; see Proposition
5.4
. By the previous argument this number goes to infinity as

tends to infinity. It follows from Theorem
2.7
that

\Vol(M_{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(T)})\to+\infty\qquad(T\to+\infty).

∎

4.2.
Relation to asymptotic density

Let us see now how the previous construction of closing-up along a geodesic is related to the notion of asymptotic density:

Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
be a collection of closed geodesics. Consider the set
\Omega\subseteq\operatorname{UT}(X)
of starting vectors whose geodesic segments eventually close-up to a geodesic in
\mathcal{A}
:

{}_{\mathcal{A}}\coloneqq\{v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)\,|\,\exists T=T(v)>0\forall t\in R_{\varepsilon}(v)\cap[T,+\infty):\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)\in\mathcal{A}\}.

Lemma 4.8
.

A collection
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
has asymptotic density one if the set
A
has full measure.

Proof.

For any subset
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
we will use the following notation

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[T,T^{\prime})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\gamma\in\mathcal{B}\,|\,T\leq\ell(\gamma)<T^{\prime}\},

\displaystyle{}_{\mathcal{B}}[T,T^{\prime})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)\,|\,\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)\in\mathcal{B}\quad\forall t\in R_{\varepsilon}(v)\cap[T,T^{\prime})\}

for all
0<T<T^{\prime}
.

For every closed geodesic
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
we consider the set
N(\gamma)
of vectors
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
such that there is
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
with
d(v,\dot{\gamma}(t_{0}))<\varepsilon/2
and
d(g_{t}(v),\dot{\gamma}(t+t_{0}))<\varepsilon/2
for all
t\in[0,\ell(\gamma)]
. In particular, for every
v\in N(\gamma)
we have that
t\coloneqq\ell(\gamma)\in R_{\varepsilon}(v)
and the broken geodesic curve
\gamma_{v}(t)
is freely homotopic to
\gamma
, whence
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)=\gamma
.

If
v\in N(\gamma_{1})\cap N(\gamma_{2})
and
|\ell(\gamma_{1})-\ell(\gamma_{2})|<\varepsilon<\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{inj}(X)
then
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}
, for any
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{G}
. Indeed, both broken geodesic curves
\gamma_{v}(\ell(\gamma_{1}))
and
\gamma_{v}(\ell(\gamma_{2}))
are in the same free homotopy class. Because there is only one closed geodesic in any free homotopy class we obtain

\gamma_{1}=\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(\ell(\gamma_{1}))=\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(\ell(\gamma_{2}))=\gamma_{2}.

Moreover, a direct computation using the flowbox coordinates and the Anosov properties of the flow yields that there is a universal constant
C>0
such that

\mu(N(\gamma))\geq C\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\ell(\gamma)\cdot e^{-\ell(\gamma)}.

We have that

{}_{\mathcal{A}}=\bigcupop\displaylimits_{T>0}{}_{\mathcal{A}}[T,+\infty)

and
{}_{\mathcal{A}}[T,\infty)\subseteq{}_{\mathcal{A}}[T^{\prime},\infty)
for all
0<T\leq T^{\prime}
. Thus,

\mu({}_{\mathcal{A}}[T,\infty))\to\mu({}_{\mathcal{A}})=1\quad(T\to\infty).

Suppose
w\in N(\gamma)
for
\gamma\in\mathcal{A}^{c}[k\cdot\varepsilon,(k+1)\cdot\varepsilon)
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\ell(\gamma)\in R_{\varepsilon}(w)\cap[k\cdot\varepsilon,(k+1)\varepsilon)
and
\gamma=\widehat{\gamma}_{w}(\ell(\gamma))\notin\mathcal{A}
. Thus,
w\notin{}_{\mathcal{A}}[k\cdot\varepsilon,+\infty)
. Hence,

\bigsqcupop\displaylimits_{\gamma\in\mathcal{A}^{c}[k\cdot\varepsilon,(k+1)\cdot\varepsilon)}N(\gamma)\subseteq{}_{\mathcal{A}}[k\cdot\varepsilon,+\infty)^{c},

and

\sumop\displaylimits_{\gamma\in\mathcal{A}^{c}[k\cdot\varepsilon,(k+1)\cdot\varepsilon)}\mu(N(\gamma))\leq\mu({}_{\mathcal{A}}[k\cdot\varepsilon,+\infty)^{c})\eqqcolon\alpha_{k}\to 0\quad(k\to\infty).

Note that
\alpha_{k}\geq\alpha_{k+1}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
0\leq\alpha_{k}\leq 1
.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{\gamma\in\mathcal{A}^{c}[k\cdot\varepsilon,(k+1)\cdot\varepsilon)}\mu(N(\gamma))

\displaystyle\geq\sumop\displaylimits_{\gamma\in\mathcal{A}^{c}[k\cdot\varepsilon,(k+1)\cdot\varepsilon)}C\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\ell(\gamma)e^{-\ell(\gamma)}

\displaystyle\geq C\cdot(k+1)\varepsilon\cdot e^{-(k+1)\varepsilon}\cdot\#\mathcal{A}^{c}[k\cdot\varepsilon,(k+1)\cdot\varepsilon).

Thus,

(4.1)

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{A}^{c}[k\cdot\varepsilon,(k+1)\cdot\varepsilon)\leq\frac{e^{(k+1)\varepsilon}}{C\cdot(k+1)\cdot\varepsilon}\cdot\alpha_{k}.

We need to show that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\#\mathcal{A}(R)}{\#\mathcal{G}(R)}=\frac{\#\mathcal{A}[0,R)}{\#\mathcal{G}[0,R)}=1-\frac{\#\mathcal{A}^{c}[0,R)}{\#\mathcal{G}[0,R)}\to 1\quad(R\to\infty).

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n\varepsilon\leq R<(n+1)\varepsilon
. By (
4.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{A}^{c}[0,R)

\displaystyle\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{n}\#\mathcal{A}^{c}[k\varepsilon,(k+1)\varepsilon)\leq\frac{1}{C}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{k}}{(k+1)\varepsilon}e^{(k+1)\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{C}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor}\frac{\alpha_{k}}{(k+1)\varepsilon}e^{(k+1)\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{C}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor+1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{k}}{(k+1)\varepsilon}e^{(k+1)\varepsilon}.

We can estimate the first summand as follows

\frac{1}{C}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor}\frac{\alpha_{k}}{(k+1)\varepsilon}e^{(k+1)\varepsilon}\leq\frac{1}{C\varepsilon}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor}e^{(k+1)\varepsilon}\ll e^{\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor\varepsilon}\ll e^{R/2}\in o\left(\frac{e^{R}}{R}\right).

Regarding the second we get

\frac{1}{C}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor+1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{k}}{(k+1)\varepsilon}e^{(k+1)\varepsilon}\ll\frac{\alpha_{\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor}}{(\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor+2)\varepsilon}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor+1}^{n}e^{(k+1)\varepsilon}\ll\alpha_{\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor}\frac{e^{R}}{R}\in o\left(\frac{e^{R}}{R}\right),

because
\alpha_{\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor}\to 0
as
R\to\infty
.

By a result of Huber’s
[
26
]
it is known that

\#\mathcal{G}[0,R)\sim\frac{e^{R}}{R}

as
R\to\infty
; see Remark
2.4
. Therefore, the previous two estimates imply

\frac{\#\mathcal{A}^{c}[0,R)}{\#\mathcal{G}[0,R)}\to 0\quad(R\to\infty)

proving (
4.2
). This concludes the proof.
∎

Let

be a geodesic pants decomposition of

It will be useful to have a characterization of these homotopy types in terms of the universal covering

\widetilde{\gamma}^{\prime}
at the beginning such that the resulting arcs both start on
\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}=\widetilde{\beta}_{1}
. Iterating this procedure
r
-times until there are no more intersections with
\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{P})
for
\widetilde{\gamma}
or
\widetilde{\gamma}^{\prime}
, yields that
\widetilde{\alpha}_{i}=\widetilde{\beta}_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,r
. If
r
was not equal to both
l
and
m
, one of the two arcs would intersect another curve in
\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{P})
whence it would leave the component
\widetilde{Q}=\widetilde{Q}^{\prime}
and the two endpoints
q
and
q^{\prime}
would not be in the same connected component; a contradiction.

Now, suppose that
\widetilde{P}\neq\widetilde{P}^{\prime}
. It follows from (
5.1
) that
d(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{p}^{\prime})<\rho
. Since
\rho
is less than the minimal distance
o
between any two pants curves in
\mathcal{P}
,
\widetilde{P}
and
\widetilde{P}^{\prime}
are adjacent convex domains separated by some
\widetilde{\eta}\in\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{P})
. We claim that either
\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}=\widetilde{\eta}
or
\widetilde{\beta}_{1}=\widetilde{\eta}
holds: Note that
\gamma^{\prime}
is filling, so that it intersects every pants curve at least once and, in particular,
m\geq 3
.

Therefore, if both
\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}=\widetilde{\eta}
and
\widetilde{\beta}_{1}=\widetilde{\eta}
holds, then the endpoint
\widetilde{q}
is contained in the half-space
H
bordering on
\eta
that does not contain
\widetilde{p}
and
\widetilde{q}^{\prime}
is contained in the half-space
H^{\prime}
bordering on
\widetilde{\alpha}_{2}
which is disjoint from
H
. Thus,
d(\widetilde{q},\widetilde{q}^{\prime})\geq o>\rho
; a contradiction.

Likewise, if both
\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}\neq\widetilde{\eta}
and
\widetilde{\beta}_{1}\neq\widetilde{\eta}
then the endpoints
\widetilde{q}
and
\widetilde{q}^{\prime}
are contained in disjoint half-spaces
H
and
H^{\prime}
bordering on
\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}
and
\widetilde{\beta}_{1}
, respectively. Thus,
d(\widetilde{o},\widetilde{o}^{\prime})\geq o>\rho
; a contradiction.

Because either
\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}=\widetilde{\eta}
or
\widetilde{\beta}_{1}=\widetilde{\eta}
, we may either shorten
\widetilde{\gamma}
or
\widetilde{\gamma}^{\prime}
and delete
\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}
or
\widetilde{\beta}_{1}
from
\widetilde{\alpha}
or
\widetilde{\beta}
, respectively, to obtain two arcs starting in the same convex domain
\widetilde{P}=\widetilde{P}^{\prime}
. Similarly, we may shorten either
\widetilde{\gamma}
or
\widetilde{\gamma}^{\prime}
at the end to obtain arcs that end in the same domain
\widetilde{Q}=\widetilde{Q}^{\prime}
. This reduces the situation to the previous case. This proves that
\widetilde{\alpha}=\widetilde{\beta}
up to deletion at the beginning or the end.
∎

6.
Lower Bound on the number of arcs

In this section we derive our main dynamic estimate which yields a lower bound on the number or arcs appearing in a random geodesic.

6.1.
Notation

We denote by
\mu
the normalized Liouville (probability) measure on
\operatorname{UT}(X)
, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}_{X}/\mathcal{L}_{X}(\operatorname{UT}(X))
.
Furthermore, we denote by
T\colon\operatorname{UT}(X)\to\operatorname{UT}(X)
the time-one map of the geodesic flow (see Subsection
2.2
).
Also, we will use the notation

\mu(f)=\intop\nolimits_{\operatorname{UT}(X)}f\,d\mu

for
f\in L^{1}(\operatorname{UT}(X),\mu)
.
By
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(\varphi)
we will denote the Sobolev norm of a smooth function
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\operatorname{UT}(X))
as defined in section
2.6
.

6.2.
Smooth approximations of orthogeodesic sets

Recall that
X
is a hyperbolic surface obtained from gluing
\left\lvert\chi(X)\right\rvert
-many isometric pairs of pants along their cuffs without twists.
In each pair of pants there are infinitely many geodesic arcs that connect any two boundary components.
We consider free homotopy classes of these arcs where the endpoints are allowed to glide freely along their respective boundary component.
In each of these free homotopy classes there is a unique orthogeodesic arc that meets both boundaries perpendicularly.
For such an orthogeodesic arc
o\subseteq X
we denote its length by
\ell(o)
.
Now, we enumerate all orthogeodesic arcs in all pairs of pants
\{o_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
by increasing length, i.e.
\ell(o_{i})\leq\ell(o_{j})
if
i\leq j
.
The length of the
i
-th orthogeodesic arc
o_{i}
will be denoted by
\ell_{i}=\ell(o_{i})
, and for every orthogeodesic arc
o_{i}
we denote by
P_{i}
the pair of pants that contains it.
Moreover, we consider the set
B_{i}
of all unit tangent vectors
v\in\operatorname{UT}(P_{i})
whose associated geodesic
\gamma_{v}\cap P_{i}
is in the free homotopy class of
o_{i}
. In this way the sets
\{B_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are a certain enumeration of the sets
\{U_{k}^{l}\}_{k,l}
in section
2.4
.

Given such an orthogeodesic arc
o_{i}
we may lift it to an orthogeodesic arc
\widetilde{o}_{i}
in the universal covering
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, such that
\widetilde{o}_{i}
is a subarc of the imaginary axis in the upper half-plane model.
Furthermore, we may assume that the lifts of its boundary components are half-circles
C_{0}(\ell_{i})
and
C_{1}(\ell_{i})
centered at
0
of radius
e^{-\ell_{i}/2}
and
e^{\ell_{i}/2}
, respectively.
Then the universal covering
\pi\colon\mathbb{H}^{2}\to X
induces a bijection between the set
B_{i}
and the set
B(\ell_{i})
of all unit tangent vectors
v\in\operatorname{UT}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
, whose base point lies between
C_{0}(\ell_{i})
and
C_{1}(\ell_{i})
and whose induced geodesic
\gamma_{v}\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{2}
intersect
C_{0}(\ell_{i})
and
C_{1}(\ell_{i})
.

Figure 6.1.
This figure shows the set
B(\ell_{i})
of all unit tangent vectors
v\in\operatorname{UT}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
, whose base point lies between
C_{0}(\ell_{i})
and
C_{1}(\ell_{i})
and whose induced geodesic
\gamma_{v}\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{2}
intersect
C_{0}(\ell_{i})

There is a formula for the (normalized) volume of
B(\ell)
:

Lemma 6.1
.

(1)

The measure of
B(\ell)
is given by

\mu(B(\ell))=\frac{1}{2\pi\left\lvert\chi(X)\right\rvert}\cdot\left(Li_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)}\right)\log\left\lvert 1-\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)}\right\rvert\right).

(2)

Asymptotically, this yields

\mu(B(\ell))\sim\frac{1}{\pi\left\lvert\chi(X)\right\rvert}\cdot\ell e^{-\ell}

as
\ell\to\infty
.

Proof.

(1)

This follows from Bridgeman’s computations in
[
14
, Section 9]
.

(2)

Observe that

\displaystyle\mu(B(\ell))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\left\lvert\chi(X)\right\rvert}\cdot\left(Li_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)}\right)\log\left\lvert 1-\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)}\right\rvert\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\left\lvert\chi(X)\right\rvert}\cdot\left(Li_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)}\right)-\log\left(\cosh\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)\right)\log\left(\frac{\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)}{\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\left\lvert\chi(X)\right\rvert}\cdot\left(Li_{2}\left({\cosh^{-2}\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)}\right)+2\log\left(\cosh\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)\right)\log\left(\frac{\cosh\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)}{\sinh\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)}\right)\right)

Recall that

\log(\cosh(x))\sim x

as
x\to\infty
. Moreover,

\log\left(\frac{\cosh\left(x\right)}{\sinh\left(x\right)}\right)=\log\left(1+e^{-2x}\right)-\log\left(1-e^{-2x}\right)\sim 2e^{-2x}

as
x\to\infty
.
Thus, with
x=\frac{\ell}{2}
, we have that

(6.1)

\displaystyle 2\log\left(\cosh\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)\right)\log\left(\frac{\cosh\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)}{\sinh\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)}\right)\sim 2\frac{\ell}{2}\cdot 2e^{-\ell}=2\ell e^{-\ell}

as
\ell\to\infty
.

On the other hand

Li_{2}(z)=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{k^{2}}=z+O(z^{2}),

and

\cosh^{-2}\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)\sim 4e^{-\ell}.

Thus,

(6.2)

\displaystyle Li_{2}\left({\cosh^{-2}\left(\frac{\ell}{2}\right)}\right)\sim 4e^{-\ell}.

Using (
6.1
) and (
6.2
) we get that

\mu(B(\ell))\sim\frac{1}{2\pi\left\lvert\chi(X)\right\rvert}\cdot\left(2\ell e^{-\ell}+4e^{-\ell}\right)\sim\frac{1}{\pi\left\lvert\chi(X)\right\rvert}\cdot\ell e^{-\ell}

as
\ell\to\infty
.

∎

Remark 6.2
.

It follows from Lemma
6.1

(1)
that

\ell_{i}\leq\ell_{i+1}
implies
\mu(B(\ell_{i}))\geq\mu(B(\ell_{i+1}))
. In particular, the sequence
\{\mu(B(\ell_{i}))\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is decreasing.

Using Corollary
2.11
we obtain the following asymptotic for the length of the
i
-th orthogeodesic arc.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
0<\delta<1
denote the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set for (any-) one of the constituent pairs of pants of
X
. Then

\ell_{i}\sim\frac{1}{\delta}\cdot\log(i).

Consequently, there are sequences
(\theta_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}},(\theta^{\prime}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to
1
as
i\to\infty
, such that

\mu(B(\ell_{i}))=\frac{1}{\pi\left\lvert\chi(X)\right\rvert\delta}\log(i)i^{-\theta_{i}/\delta}\theta^{\prime}_{i}.

Proof.

Let us fix two boundary curves
C_{-},C_{+}
of one of the constituent pairs of pants
P
and denote by
N_{C_{-},C_{+}}(\ell)
the number of orthogeodesic arcs in
P
of length
\leq\ell
connecting the two given boundary curves.
By Corollary
2.11
there is a constant
C_{0}>0
such that

(6.3)

\displaystyle N_{C_{-},C_{+}}(\ell)\sim C_{0}\cdot e^{\delta\ell}

as
\ell\to\infty
, where
\delta>0
is the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set of
P
.

Let us now denote by
N(\ell)
the
total
number of
all
orthogeodesic arcs in any constituent pair of pants of length
\leq\ell
. It follows from (
6.3
) that there is a constant
C>0
such that

N(\ell)\sim C\cdot e^{\delta\ell}.

In particular, there are constants
0<C_{1}<C_{2}
such that

C_{1}\cdot e^{\delta\ell}\leq N(\ell)\leq C_{2}\cdot e^{\delta\ell}

for all
\ell>0
.

Because we have enumerated the orthogeodesics
\{o_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
by increasing length
\{\ell_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, there is a constant
D>0
such that

N(\ell_{i})-D\leq i\leq N(\ell_{i})+D

for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus,

i\leq N(\ell_{i})+D\leq C_{2}e^{\delta\ell_{i}}+D,

equivalently

\frac{1}{\delta}\log(i-D)-\frac{1}{\delta}\log(C_{2})\leq\ell_{i},

and similarly

\ell_{i}\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\log(i+D)-\frac{1}{\delta}\log(C_{1}).

Therefore,

\ell_{i}\sim\frac{1}{\delta}\log(i)

as
i\to\infty
.
∎

Moreover, we can approximate each of the sets
B(\ell)
by a smooth bump function
\varphi_{\ell}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\operatorname{UT}(\mathbb{H}^{2}))
. In the rest of this section we will explicitly construct for every
\ell>0
a smooth function
\varphi_{\ell}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\operatorname{UT}(\mathbb{H}^{2}))
, that satisfies
0\leq\varphi_{\ell}\leq 1
and
\operatorname{supp}\varphi_{\ell}\subseteq B(\ell)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
For any subset
A\subseteq\operatorname{UT}(X)
we denote by
N_{\varepsilon}(A)
the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
A
, i.e.

N_{\varepsilon}(A)\coloneqq\{x\in\operatorname{UT}(X)\,|\,d(x,A)<\varepsilon\},

where
d(x,A)\coloneqq\inf\{d(x,y)\,|\,y\in A\}
denotes the distance from
x
to the set
A
. We can define the
\varepsilon
-interior of
A
as

\mathop{int}\nolimits_{\varepsilon}(A):=A\setminus N_{\varepsilon}(A^{c}),

i.e. all points in
A
that have distance more than
\varepsilon
from its boundary.

Let
\{\psi_{\varepsilon}\}_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}\subseteq C_{c}^{\infty}(G)
be the family of smooth approximate convolutional identities as constructed in Appendix
A
. In particular, they satisfy

(1)

\psi_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
, and

(2)

\intop\nolimits_{G}\psi_{\varepsilon}\,d\nu=1
, and

(3)

\operatorname{supp}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\subseteq B_{\varepsilon}(e)
,

for all
\varepsilon>0
. In addition, for every
d>0
there is a constant
K=K(d)>0
such that

\left\lVert E_{\alpha}\cdot\psi_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{1}\leq K\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d}

for all
\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert\leq d
, where
E_{\alpha}
denotes the multi-index derivative by left-invariant vector fields (see section
2.6
and Lemma

).

For every
\ell>0
and
0<\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\ell)<\varepsilon_{0}
, that may depend on
\ell
, we set

\varphi_{\ell}\coloneqq\psi_{\varepsilon/2}*\mathbf{1}_{\mathop{int}\nolimits_{\varepsilon/2}(B(\ell))}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\operatorname{UT}(X)).

These smooth approximations satisfy the following lemma.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\ell)>0
. Then there are constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}>0
, such that

(1)

0\leq\varphi_{\ell}\leq 1;

(2)

\operatorname{supp}\varphi_{\ell}\subseteq B(\ell);

(3)

\|\mathbf{1}_{B(\ell)}-\varphi_{\ell}\|_{1}\leq C_{1}\varepsilon(\ell);

(4)

\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(\varphi_{\ell})\leq C_{2}\cdot\mu(B(\ell))\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon(\ell)}\right)^{d}
, where
d
denotes the degree of the Sobolev norm;

(5)

\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(\overline{\varphi}_{\ell})\leq C_{3}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon(\ell)}\right)^{d}
, where we denote
\overline{\varphi}_{\ell}\coloneqq 1-\varphi_{\ell}
.

Proof.

(1)

Follows immediately from the construction.

(2)

By definition
\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{1}_{\mathop{int}\nolimits_{\varepsilon/2}(B(\ell))})=\overline{\mathop{int}\nolimits_{\varepsilon/2}(B(\ell))}
. Thus every point in
\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{1}_{\mathop{int}\nolimits_{\varepsilon/2}(B(\ell))})
has distance at least
\varepsilon/2
from
\partial B(\ell)
. Because
\operatorname{supp}(\psi_{\varepsilon/2})\subseteq B_{\varepsilon/2}(\mathop{id}\nolimits)
and
d_{\operatorname{UT}(X)}(gv,w)\leq d_{G}(g,\mathop{id}\nolimits)
for all
v,w\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
,
g\in G
, it follows by the definition of convolution that
\operatorname{supp}(\varphi_{\ell})\subseteq B(\ell)
.

(3)

By construction
\varphi_{\ell}
differs from
\mathbf{1}_{B(\ell)}
only on the
inner
\varepsilon
-tube neighborhood of
B(\ell)

T_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq B(\ell)\setminus\mathop{int}\nolimits_{\varepsilon}(B(\ell))\subseteq B(\ell).

Because both functions take values in
[0,1]
, one obtains the trivial bound

\|\mathbf{1}_{B(\ell)}-\varphi_{\ell}\|_{1}\leq\mu(T_{\varepsilon}).

It can be shown that both the principal curvature of the smooth boundary components of
\partial B(\ell)
and its area
\Area(\partial B(\ell))
are uniformly bounded. Thus, there exists
C_{1}>0
independent of
\ell
and
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
such that

\mu(T_{\varepsilon})\leq C_{1}\varepsilon(\ell)

by the Tube Lemma
C.1
.

It is well-known that geodesic trajectories in
\operatorname{UT}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
are given unit vector fields of constant slope along geodesic lines in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Note that the unit vectors on
B(\ell)
have basepoint in a given ideal quadrilateral, such that forward and backwards trajectories intersect two designated opposite sides of the ideal quadrilateral, which we call
opposing sides
.

Figure 6.2.
This figure shows a vector
v
is in a side face of

. The basepoint of

The boundary of
\partial B(\ell)
can be described as the unions of 6 faces: 4 which we call
side faces
and 2 that we call

, which we will now describe.
Side faces are composed by unit vectors whose basepoints are in an ideal triangle formed by 3 out of the 4 ideal vertices associated to
B(\ell)
(see vector
v
in Figure
6.2
with basepoint in the shaded ideal triangle therein), and their geodesic trajectories end at the ideal vertex that does not pair to form an opposing side in the triangle. Hence, side faces are totally geodesic along the geodesic lines ending at the ideal vertex. In the orthogonal direction we have horocycles tangent at the ideal vertex and the unit vectors are normal to those horocycles. Since
\operatorname{UT}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
is a Riemannian submersion, these horocyclic trajectories have curvature equal to
1
. Because of the symmetries of side face, these horocyclic trajectories are lines of curvature, which tells us the the principal curvatures of a side face are
0
(the totally geodesic direction) and

(the horocyclic direction).

On the other hand, opposing faces are composed of vectors whose basepoints are on one of the opposing sides of the ideal quadrilateral, such that their geodesic trajectory intersects the other opposing side (see vector

in Figure

\operatorname{UT}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
), it follows that opposing faces are totally geodesic in
\operatorname{UT}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
, because these faces are open sets of totally geodesic submanifolds.

(4)

Recall that

\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(\varphi_{\ell})=\sumop\displaylimits_{\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert=0}^{d}\left\lVert E_{\alpha}\cdot\varphi_{\ell}\right\rVert_{2}.

By Lemma

we get

\displaystyle\left\lVert E_{\alpha}\cdot\varphi_{\ell}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert E_{\alpha}\cdot(\psi_{\varepsilon/2}*\mathbf{1}_{\mathop{int}\nolimits_{\varepsilon/2}(B(\ell))})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert(E_{\alpha}\cdot\psi_{\varepsilon/2})*\mathbf{1}_{\mathop{int}\nolimits_{\varepsilon/2}(B(\ell))}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert E_{\alpha}\cdot\psi_{\varepsilon/2}\right\rVert_{1}\cdot\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{\mathop{int}\nolimits_{\varepsilon/2}(B(\ell))}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq K\cdot\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon(\ell)}\right)^{d}\cdot\mu(B(\ell)).

Hence,

\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(\varphi_{\ell})=\sumop\displaylimits_{\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert=0}^{d}\left\lVert E_{\alpha}\cdot\varphi_{\ell}\right\rVert_{2}\leq C_{2}\cdot\mu(B(\ell))\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon(\ell)}\right)^{d}

for
C_{2}=dK2^{d}>0
.

(5)

Observe that

\overline{\varphi}_{\ell}=1-\overline{\varphi}_{\ell}=1-\psi_{\varepsilon/2}*\mathbf{1}_{\mathop{int}\nolimits_{\varepsilon/2}(B(\ell))}=\psi_{\varepsilon/2}*(1-\mathbf{1}_{\mathop{int}\nolimits_{\varepsilon/2}(B(\ell))}).

As before we get

\displaystyle\left\lVert E_{\alpha}\cdot\overline{\varphi}_{\ell}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert E_{\alpha}\cdot\psi_{\varepsilon/2}\right\rVert_{1}\cdot\left\lVert 1-\mathbf{1}_{\mathop{int}\nolimits_{\varepsilon/2}(B(\ell))}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq K\cdot\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon(\ell)}\right)^{d}.

Hence,

\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(\overline{\varphi}_{\ell})=\sumop\displaylimits_{\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert=0}^{d}\left\lVert E_{\alpha}\cdot\overline{\varphi}_{\ell}\right\rVert_{2}\leq C_{3}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon(\ell)}\right)^{d}

for some
C_{3}>0
.

∎

6.3.
Counting the number of visited sets

The orthogeodesic sets
\{B_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
defined in section
6.2
partition the unit tangent bundle
\operatorname{UT}(X)
(up to a null set). For a given unit tangent vector
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
let
C_{N}(v)
denote the number of
different
sets in this partition that the orbit
v,Tv,\ldots,T^{N-1}v
has visited, i.e.

C_{N}(v)\coloneqq\#\{i\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,\exists 0\leq n\leq N-1:T^{n}v\in B_{i}\}.

It follows immediately from the definition that

A_{\mathcal{P}}(\gamma_{v}[0,N])\geq C_{N}(v);

see also Remark
2.10
.
Therefore, we would like to find a certain growth rate
F(N)
for the number of visited sets such that for
\mu
-almost every
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
we have

C_{N}(v)\geq F(N)

for
N
large enough.
This is established by the following theorem.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
\eta>1
and let
F\colon\mathbb{N}\to(0,+\infty)
be a function satisfying

F(N)\in O(N^{\delta/2\eta}).

Then, for
\mu
-almost every
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
there is an
N_{0}=N_{0}(v)\in\mathbb{N}
such that

C_{N}(v)\geq F(N)

for all
N\geq N_{0}
.

Proof.

We need to prove that the set

\Omega\coloneqq\left\{v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)\,\bigg|\,\exists N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}\,\forall N\geq N_{0}:C_{N}(v)\geq F(N)\right\}

has full measure.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and denote by
{}_{N}\subseteq\operatorname{UT}(X)
the set of all vectors
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
such that

C_{N}(v)\geq F(N).

With this notation we have

\Omega=\liminf_{N\to\infty}\,{}_{N}=\bigcupop\displaylimits_{N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcapop\displaylimits_{N\geq N_{0}}{}_{N}.

In order to show that
\Omega
has full measure we need to see that its complement

{}^{c}=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\,{}^{c}_{N}=\bigcapop\displaylimits_{N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcupop\displaylimits_{N\geq N_{0}}{}^{c}_{N}

has measure zero. By the Borel–Cantelli Lemma this will follow if the sequence
(\mu({}^{c}_{N}))_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable, i.e.

(6.4)

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{N=1}^{\infty}\mu({}^{c}_{N})<+\infty.

Before we proceed with verifying (
6.4
) we make some general observations first: if the orbit
v,Tv,\ldots,T^{N-1}v
has visited less than
m
sets, i.e.
C_{N}(v)<m
, then
v
has not visited all of the first
m
sets
B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
in particular. Thus,

\mu(C_{N}<m)\leq\mu\left(\bigcupop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}\bigcapop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{N-1}T^{-k}(B^{c}_{i})\right)\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}\mu\left(\bigcapop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{N-1}T^{-k}(B^{c}_{i})\right).

Notice that

\mu\left(\bigcapop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{N-1}T^{-k}(B^{c}_{i})\right)=\intop\nolimits_{\operatorname{UT}(X)}\prodop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{N-1}\left(\mathbf{1}_{B^{c}_{i}}\circ T^{k}\right)\,d\mu.

We want to apply our exponential mixing theorem
B.3
to the right-hand-side.
For this we will approximate the sets
B_{i}
by smooth functions
\varphi_{i}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\operatorname{UT}(X))
as described in section
6.2
.
For each
i\in\mathbb{N}
we let
\varepsilon_{i}>0
be a positive number that we will specify later.
Given
\varepsilon(\ell_{i})=\varepsilon_{i}
we obtain smooth functions
\varphi_{\ell_{i}}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\operatorname{UT}(\mathbb{H}^{2}))
that approximate the sets
B(\ell_{i})\subseteq\operatorname{UT}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
.
Via the isometry

these pull-back to smooth approximations of the set
B_{i}

.
Finally, we set
\overline{\varphi}_{i}\coloneqq 1-\varphi_{i}\geq\mathbf{1}_{B^{c}_{i}}
.

Thus, we get

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\operatorname{UT}(X)}\prodop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{N-1}\left(\mathbf{1}_{B^{c}_{i}}\circ T^{k}\right)\,d\mu

\displaystyle\leq\intop\nolimits_{\operatorname{UT}(X)}\prodop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{N-1}\left(\overline{\varphi}_{i}\circ T^{k}\right)\,d\mu

In order to obtain an error term that tends to zero from Theorem

\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which we will also specify later, and set

l\coloneqq\left\lfloor\frac{N-1}{n-1}\right\rfloor.

By Theorem
B.3
we obtain

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\operatorname{UT}(X)}\prodop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{N-1}\left(\overline{\varphi}_{i}\circ T^{k}\right)\,d\mu

\displaystyle\leq\intop\nolimits_{\operatorname{UT}(X)}\prodop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\overline{\varphi}_{i}\circ T^{k\cdot l}\right)\,d\mu

\displaystyle\leq\mu(\overline{\varphi}_{i})^{n}+L\cdot(n-1)\cdot D^{n}\cdot\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(\overline{\varphi}_{i})^{n}\cdot e^{-\beta\cdot l}

for all
l\geq K_{0}
, where
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}
denotes the degree
d=3
Sobolev norm and the constants
\alpha,\beta>0
are as in Theorem
B.3
.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mu(C_{N}<m)

\displaystyle\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}\mu\left(\bigcapop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{N-1}T^{-k}(B^{c}_{i})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mu(\overline{\varphi}_{i})^{n}+L\cdot(n-1)\cdot D^{n}\cdot\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(\overline{\varphi}_{i})^{n}\cdot e^{-\beta\cdot l}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}\mu(\overline{\varphi}_{i})^{n}}_{\eqqcolon A_{1}(N)}+\underbrace{L\cdot(n-1)\cdot D^{n}\cdot e^{-\beta\cdot l}\cdot\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(\overline{\varphi}_{i})^{n}}_{\eqqcolon A_{2}(N)}.

Thus, we are left with proving that both sequences
\{A_{1}(N)\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{A_{2}(N)\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
are summable for
m=m(N)\coloneqq\left\lceil F(N)\right\rceil=O(N^{\delta/2\eta})
and suitable choices of
\varepsilon_{i}
and
n=n(N)
. To this end, we set

(6.5)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\mu(B_{i})}{C_{1}(2+\log(i))},

\displaystyle\forall i\in\mathbb{N},

and choose
n(N)\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(6.6)

\displaystyle n(N)\sim N^{1/2\eta^{\prime}}

for some fixed

.

Moreover, note that we may assume without loss of generality that

(6.7)

\displaystyle m(N)\asymp N^{\delta/2\eta},

i.e. there are positive constants

and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
A\cdot N^{\delta/2\eta}\leq m(N)\leq B\cdot N^{\delta/2\eta}
for all
N\geq N_{0}
. Indeed, for every function
m_{1}(N)\in O(N^{\delta/2\eta})
there is a function
m_{2}(N)\asymp N^{\delta/2\eta}
such that
m_{1}(N)\leq m_{2}(N)
for sufficiently large

. In this case,

\mu(C_{N}<m_{1}(N))\leq\mu(C_{N}<m_{2}(N)),

whence it suffices to prove that
(\mu(C_{N}<m(N)))_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable whenever
m(N)\asymp N^{\delta/2\eta}
. In particular, we may assume that
m(N)\to+\infty
as
N\to+\infty
.

Regarding
A_{1}(N)
.
Observe that

(6.8)

\displaystyle\mu(\overline{\varphi}_{i})\leq 1-\left(\mu(B_{i})-C_{1}\varepsilon_{i}\right)\leq 1-\tfrac{1}{2}\mu(B_{i})

for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
by (
6.5
).
Because the sets
\{B_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are enumerated in such a way that the sequence
(\mu(B_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is monotonically decreasing, we obtain that

A_{1}(N)\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}\mu(\overline{\varphi}_{i})^{n}\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}\left(1-\tfrac{1}{2}\mu(B_{i})\right)^{n}\leq m\left(1-\tfrac{1}{2}\mu(B_{m})\right)^{n}.

By Lemma
6.3
there are sequences
(\theta_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}},(\theta^{\prime}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to
1
as
i\to\infty
, such that

(6.9)

\displaystyle\mu(B_{i})=\mu(B(\ell_{i}))=\frac{1}{\pi\left\lvert\chi(X)\right\rvert\delta}\log(i)i^{-\theta_{i}/\delta}\theta^{\prime}_{i}

for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

Regarding the logarithm we get

\displaystyle\log(A_{1}(N))

\displaystyle\leq\log(m)+n\log\left(1-\tfrac{1}{2}\mu(B_{m})\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\diamond)}}{{\leq}}\log(m)-\frac{n}{2}\mu(B_{m})

\displaystyle=\log(m)-\frac{\theta^{\prime}_{m}}{2\pi\left\lvert\chi(X)\right\rvert\delta}n\log(m)m^{-\theta_{m}/\delta}

\displaystyle=-\log(m)\frac{n}{m^{\theta_{m}/\delta}}\left(\frac{\theta^{\prime}_{m}}{2\pi\left\lvert\chi(X)\right\rvert\delta}-\frac{m^{\theta_{m}/\delta}}{n}\right){\qquad(\star)}

where we used the fact that

for all

in

and plugged-in (

).

From

(6.10)

\displaystyle\log(m(N))\sim\frac{\delta}{2\eta}\log(N).

Furthermore,
n=n(N)\sim N^{1/2\eta^{\prime}}
,
\eta^{\prime}<\eta
,

as

and

as

, such that

\displaystyle\frac{m^{\theta_{m}/\delta}}{n}\ll\frac{N^{\theta_{m}/2\eta}}{N^{1/2\eta^{\prime}}}=N^{-\tfrac{1}{2\eta^{\prime}}+\tfrac{\theta_{m}}{2\eta}}\to 0

as
N\to+\infty
.

Using both (
6.10
) and (
6.11
) in
(\star)
we obtain that there is a positive constant

such that

\log(A_{1}(N))\leq-C^{\prime}\frac{n}{m^{\theta_{m}/\delta}}\log(N)

for sufficiently large
N
. Again by (

) we have that, as

,
\frac{n}{m^{\theta_{m}/\delta}}\to+\infty
whence

for sufficiently large

. Equivalently,

A_{1}(N)\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}

for sufficiently large

, which implies that the sequence
(A_{1}(N))_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable.

Regarding
A_{2}(N)
.
Let us consider the logarithm of

:

\displaystyle\log(A_{2}(N))

\displaystyle=\log(L)+\log(n-1)+\log(D)n-\beta l+\log\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(\overline{\varphi}_{i})^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=-\beta l\cdot\left(1-\frac{\log(D)}{\beta}\cdot\frac{n}{l}-\frac{\log(n-1)}{\beta l}-\frac{\log(L)}{\beta l}\right)+\log\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(\overline{\varphi}_{i})^{n}\right)

Note that

whence

\frac{n}{l}\sim\frac{N^{1/2\eta^{\prime}}}{N^{1-1/2\eta^{\prime}}}=N^{1/\eta^{\prime}-1}\to 0

and

\frac{\log(n-1)}{\beta l}\ll\frac{n}{l}\to 0,\quad\frac{\log(L)}{\beta l}\to 0

as

. Therefore,

(6.12)

\displaystyle-\beta l\cdot\left(1-\frac{\log(D)}{\beta}\cdot\frac{n}{l}-\frac{\log(n-1)}{\beta l}-\frac{\log(L)}{\beta l}\right)\sim-\beta\cdot N^{1-1/2\eta^{\prime}}.

By Lemma
6.4
there is
C_{3}>0
such that

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(\overline{\varphi}_{i})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(\overline{\varphi}_{\ell_{i}})\leq C_{3}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{i}}\right)^{d}

for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

Recall that, we set

\varepsilon_{i}=\frac{\mu(B_{i})}{C_{1}(2+\log(i))}

and that the sets

are enumerated in such a way that their measures

are decreasing in

\varepsilon_{i}
is decreasing in

\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(\overline{\varphi}_{i})^{n}\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}C_{3}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}^{-dn}\leq mC_{3}^{n}\varepsilon_{m}^{-dn}.

From (
6.9
) it follows that
\varepsilon_{m}\asymp m^{-\theta_{m}/\delta}.
Thus, there is a positive constant
C_{4}>0
such that

mC_{3}^{n}\varepsilon_{m}^{-dn}\leq mC_{4}^{n}m^{\theta_{m}nd/\delta}.

Regarding the logarithm this yields

(6.13)

\displaystyle\log\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(\overline{\varphi}_{i})^{n}\right)\leq\log(m)+\log(C_{4})n+\theta_{m}\frac{d}{\delta}n\log(m)\ll n\log(m)\ll N^{1/2\eta^{\prime}}\cdot\log(N).

Combining the estimates (
6.12
) and (

) there is a positive constant

such that

\displaystyle\log(A_{2}(N))

\displaystyle\leq C_{5}\cdot\left(-N^{1-1/2\eta^{\prime}}+N^{1/2\eta^{\prime}}\cdot\log(N)\right)

\displaystyle=-C_{5}\cdot N^{1-1/2\eta^{\prime}}\cdot\underbrace{\left(1-\frac{\log(N)}{N^{1-1/\eta^{\prime}}}\right)}_{\to 1}

Hence, there is a positive constant

and
\gamma\coloneqq 1-\tfrac{1}{2\eta^{\prime}}>0

for sufficiently large

\left(\exp\left(-C_{6}\cdot N^{\gamma}\right)\right)_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

is summable, too.

All in all, this shows that the sequence
(\mu({}^{c}_{N}))_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable, and we conclude by the Borel–Cantelli Lemma.
∎

7.
Proof of the Main Theorem

We now have all the ingredients needed to prove our main volume estimate for volumes of canonical lifts of generic filling geodesics.

We choose a hyperbolic structure
X
on our surface
S_{g}
so that all pair of pants
P\in\mathcal{P}
are isometric and are glued without twists and all the conditions of Section
4
are satisfied. Moreover, we let
0<\delta<1
denote the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set of
P
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\eta>1(>\delta>0)
and let
F(t)\in O(t^{\delta/2\eta})
. Then for
\mu
-almost every
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
there is
T^{\prime}=T^{\prime}(v)
such that
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
is filling and

F(\ell_{X}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)))\leq\Vol(M_{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}^{p}(t)})

for every
t\in R_{\varepsilon}(v)\cap[T^{\prime}(v),+\infty)
. Moreover, as long as
F
is increasing we obtain that
F(\ell_{X}(\widehat{\gamma}^{p}_{v}(t)))\leq\Vol(M_{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}^{p}(t)})
.

Proof.

Let
v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)
and
t\in R_{\varepsilon}(v)\cap[t^{*}(v),+\infty)
. We consider the filling closed geodesic
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}=\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
and its primitive subcurve
\widehat{\gamma}^{p}_{v}=\widehat{\gamma}^{p}_{v}(t)
. Then,
M_{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}^{p}}
is hyperbolic and we now want to estimate its volume. We know that by
[
30
]
we have;

\frac{v_{3}}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\left(\#\left\{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}^{p}\text{-arcs in }P\right\}-3\right)\leq\Vol(M_{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}^{p}}).

Since the arcs are not counted with multiplicity we have that

\frac{1}{2}A_{\mathcal{P}}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}^{p})=\sumop\displaylimits_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\#\left\{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}^{p}\text{-arcs in }P\right\}=\sumop\displaylimits_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\#\left\{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}\text{-arcs in }P\right\}=\frac{1}{2}A_{\mathcal{P}}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}),

where the factor
\tfrac{1}{2}
is due to the fact the number of arcs in the projective bundle is at least half the number of arcs in the unit tangent bundle; see Remark
5.3
.

By Proposition
5.4
we may compare the number
A_{\mathcal{P}}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v})
of homotopy classes of arcs for
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}
to the number
A_{\mathcal{P}}(\gamma_{v}[0,t])
of homotopy classes of arcs in the geodesic ray
\gamma_{v}[0,t]
of length
t
starting from
v
:

A_{\mathcal{P}}(\gamma_{v}[0,t])-6\leq A_{\mathcal{P}}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}).

With the notation of section
6.3
we may further estimate

C_{\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor}(v)\leq A_{\mathcal{P}}(\gamma_{v}[0,t]).

Thus, there are constants
A,B>0
(that are functions of
\chi(X)
and
v_{3}
) such that

A\cdot C_{\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor}(v)-B\leq\Vol(M_{\widehat{\gamma}^{p}_{v}}).

It follows from the Anosov Closing Lemma
2.3
that the length/period of
\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)
is close to
t

\left\lvert\ell_{X}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t))-t\right\rvert\leq\rho,

whence

\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor=\ell_{X}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t))+\Delta

for some
\Delta\in[-\rho-1,\rho+1]
.

Applying Theorem
6.5
we obtain that for any function
G(N)\in O(N^{\delta/2\eta})
there is a subset of full measure
\Omega\subseteq\operatorname{UT}(X)
satisfying that for every
v\in\Omega
there is
N_{0}=N_{0}(v)\in\mathbb{N}
such that

C_{N}(v)\geq G(N)

for every
N\geq N_{0}(v)
.

Putting everything together we obtain

(7.1)

\Vol(M_{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}^{p}})\geq A\cdot G(\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor)-B=A\cdot G(\ell_{X}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t))+\Delta)-B

for every
v\in\Omega
,
t\in R_{\varepsilon}(v)\cap[\max\{N_{0}(v),t^{*}(v)\},+\infty)
and some
\Delta\in[-\rho-1,\rho+1]
.

Note that for any
F(t)\in O(t^{\delta/2\eta})
the function

G(N)\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{A}\sup_{\Delta}F(N+\Delta)+\tfrac{B}{A},

where the supremum is taken over
\Delta\in[-\rho-1,\rho+1]
,
is in
O(N^{\delta/2\eta})
, too. With this choice of
G
it follows from (
7.1
) that

\Vol(M_{\widehat{\gamma}_{v}^{p}})\geq A\cdot G(\ell_{X}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t))+\Delta)-B\geq F(\ell_{X}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)))

for every
v\in\Omega
,
t\in R_{\varepsilon}(v)\cap[T^{\prime}(v),+\infty)
, where we set
T^{\prime}(v)\coloneqq\max\{N_{0}(v),t^{*}(v)\}
.

The case for
F
increasing follows from the fact that
\ell_{X}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t))\geq\ell_{X}(\widehat{\gamma}_{v}^{p}(t))
. This concludes the proof.∎

In combination with Lemma
4.8
this gives us:

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
\eta>1(>\delta>0)
and let
F(t)\in O(t^{\delta/2\eta})
.

Then the collection of filling, primitive closed geodesics satisfying

F(\ell_{X}(\gamma))\leq\Vol(M_{\gamma})

has asymptotic density one.

Proof.

A direct application of Lemma
4.8
yields that the collection

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\coloneqq\{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}\,|\,\gamma\text{ is filling and }F(\ell_{X}(\gamma))\leq\Vol(M_{\gamma^{p}})\}

has asymptotic density one since

{}_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}=\{v\in\operatorname{UT}(X)\,|\,\exists T>0\forall t\in R_{\varepsilon}(v)\cap[T,\infty):\widehat{\gamma}_{v}(t)\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\}

has full measure.

Finally, observe that the collection of primitive geodesics
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
has asymptotic density one. Indeed, it is known that

\#\mathcal{G}(R)=\#\{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}\,|\,\ell_{X}(\gamma)\leq R\}\sim\frac{e^{R}}{R}

as
R\to+\infty
; see Remark
2.4
.
Any non-primitive geodesic traverses some other geodesic at least twice whence

\#\mathcal{G}(R)-\#\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)\leq\#\mathcal{G}(R/2)\sim\frac{e^{R/2}}{R/2},

and

\frac{\#\mathcal{G}(R)-\#\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R)}{\#\mathcal{G}(R)}\sim 2\frac{e^{R/2}}{e^{R}}\to 0

as
R\to+\infty
.

Therefore, the intersection
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
has asymptotic density one, too.
∎

Appendix A
Explicit construction of approximate convolutional identities

We call
\{\psi_{\varepsilon}\}_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}\subseteq L^{1}(G)
a
family of approximate convolutional identities
if

(1)

\psi_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
, and

(2)

\intop\nolimits_{G}\psi_{\varepsilon}\,d\nu=1
, and

(3)

\operatorname{supp}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\subseteq B_{\varepsilon}(e)
,

for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
.

In the rest of this section we will construct an explicit family of approximate convolutional identities
\{\psi_{\varepsilon}\}_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}\subseteq C_{c}^{\infty}(G)
, and estimate the
L^{1}
-norms of their derivatives (see Lemma
A.1
).

There is
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
\exp\colon B_{\varepsilon_{0}}(0)\subseteq\mathfrak{g}\to G
is a diffeomorphism on its image. Let us denote by
\log\colon\exp(B_{\varepsilon_{0}}(0))\subseteq G\to B_{\varepsilon_{0}}(0)\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
its inverse. We may choose a smooth bump function
\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon_{0}}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})
, such that
\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon_{0}}\geq 0
and
\emptyset\neq\operatorname{supp}(\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon_{0}})\subseteq B_{\varepsilon_{0}}(0)
. We set

\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(g)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(\log(g)),&\text{if }g\in\exp(B_{\varepsilon_{0}}(0))\\
0,&\text{else;}\end{cases}

for every
g\in G
.
Further, we set

M_{\varepsilon_{0}}\coloneqq\intop\nolimits_{G}\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon_{0}}\,d\nu>0,

and define

\psi_{\varepsilon_{0}}\coloneqq\frac{\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon_{0}}}{M_{\varepsilon_{0}}}.

Then
\psi_{\varepsilon_{0}}
satisfies (1) and (2).

Regarding (3) observe that for every
X\in B_{\varepsilon_{0}}(0)
the curve
c(t)\coloneqq\exp(tX),0\leq t\leq 1
connects
e
and
\exp(X)\in\exp(B_{\varepsilon_{0}}(0))
. Therefore,

d_{G}(\exp(X),e)\leq\ell(c)=\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\|\dot{c}(t)\|\,dt=\|X\|<\varepsilon_{0},

and
\exp(B_{\varepsilon_{0}}(0))\subseteq B_{\varepsilon_{0}}(e)
. Thus
\psi_{\varepsilon_{0}}
satisfies (3), too.

We will continue to define the other members of the family
\{\psi_{\varepsilon}\}_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}
by rescaling
\psi_{\varepsilon_{0}}
. Let
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
. We define

\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}(X)\coloneqq\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon_{0}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon}X\right)

for all
X\in\mathfrak{g}
. By definition,
\operatorname{supp}(\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon})\subseteq B_{\varepsilon}(0)
.

As before, we set

\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}(g)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}(\log(g)),&\text{if }g\in\exp(B_{\varepsilon}(0))\\
0,&\text{else;}\end{cases}

for every
g\in G
,

M_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\intop\nolimits_{G}\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}\,d\nu>0,

and define

\psi_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\frac{\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}}{M_{\varepsilon}}.

Then
\psi_{\varepsilon}
satisfies (1), (2) and (3) as before.

The following
L^{1}
-estimates is used in section
6.2
.

Lemma A.1
.

For every
d>0
there is a constant
K=K(d)>0
such that

\left\lVert E_{\alpha}\cdot\psi_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{1}\leq K\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d}

holds for every
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
and every multi-index
\alpha
with
\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert\leq d
.

Proof.

Recall that

\left\lVert E_{\alpha}\cdot\psi_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{1}=M_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\left\lVert E_{\alpha}\cdot\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{1}.

Let us first estimate
M_{\varepsilon}=\intop\nolimits_{G}\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}\,d\nu
, and see that
M_{\varepsilon}\asymp\varepsilon^{3}
.

We compute

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{G}\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}\,d\nu=\intop\nolimits_{\exp(B_{\varepsilon}(0))}\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}(\log(g))\,d\nu(g)

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{B_{\varepsilon}(0)}\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}(X)\,d(\log_{*}\nu)(X)

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{B_{\varepsilon}(0)}\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}(X)\cdot\frac{d(\log_{*}\nu)}{d\lambda}(X)\,d\lambda(X),

where we denote by
\lambda
the Lebesgue measure on
\mathfrak{g}
and
\frac{d(\log_{*}\nu)}{d\lambda}
denotes the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\log_{*}\nu
with respect to
\lambda
. This is well-defined since the measure
\log_{*}(\nu)
is clearly of Lebesgue class. Moreover, because it is the push-forward of the Haar measure
\nu
via the diffeomorphism
\log\colon\exp(B_{\varepsilon_{0}}(0))\to B_{\varepsilon_{0}}(0)
, the Radon-Nikodym derivative is smooth. Consequently there are uniform upper and lower bounds

0<\inf_{X}\frac{d(\log_{*}\nu)}{d\lambda}(X),\qquad\sup_{X}\frac{d(\log_{*}\nu)}{d\lambda}(X)<+\infty,

where the infimum and the supremum are taken over the compact set
\operatorname{supp}(\psi_{\varepsilon_{0}})\subseteq B_{\varepsilon_{0}}(0)
.

Hence,

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\asymp\intop\nolimits_{B_{\varepsilon}(0)}\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}(X)\,d\lambda(X)=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_{0}}\right)^{3}\cdot\intop\nolimits_{B_{\varepsilon}(0)}\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon_{0}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon}X\right)\cdot\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{3}\,d\lambda(X)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_{0}}\right)^{3}\cdot\intop\nolimits_{B_{\varepsilon_{0}}(0)}\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon_{0}}\,d\lambda\asymp\varepsilon^{3},

as asserted.

We turn to
\left\lVert E_{\alpha}\cdot\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{1}
. For a single
E_{i}
we have

(E_{i}\cdot\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon})(g)=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}(\exp(-tE_{i})g)

for every
g\in G
. Thus, if we define by
\widetilde{E}_{i}
the
right-invariant
vector field given by
\widetilde{E}_{i}(e)=-E_{i}
, then we can rewrite

E_{\alpha}\cdot\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}=\widetilde{E}_{\alpha}\cdot\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}

where the right hand side is understood as the usual derivation with respect to vector fields.

On
\operatorname{supp}(\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon})\subseteq\exp(B_{\varepsilon}(0))
we obtain

\widetilde{E}_{\alpha}\cdot\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}=\widetilde{E}_{\alpha}\cdot(\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}\circ\log)=\left(\log_{*}(\widetilde{E})_{\alpha}\cdot\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}\right)\circ\log.

Here we denote by
\log_{*}(\widetilde{E})_{i}\in\mathfrak{X}(B_{\varepsilon}(0))
the push-forward of the vector field
\widetilde{E}_{i}
along the map
\log\colon\exp(B_{\varepsilon}(0))\to B_{\varepsilon}(0)\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
.

We may express
\log_{*}(\widetilde{E})_{i}
with respect to the standard vector fields
\partial_{1},\ldots,\partial_{3}
of
\mathfrak{g}\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
In this way, we obtain polynomials
P_{\alpha}=\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert}c^{\beta}_{\alpha}\cdot\partial_{\beta}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{X}(B_{\varepsilon}(0)))
such that

\log_{*}(\widetilde{E})_{\alpha}=P_{\alpha},

where
c^{\beta}_{\alpha}\colon B_{\varepsilon}(0)\to\mathbb{R}
are smooth coefficient functions. If we apply these to
\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\log_{*}(\widetilde{E})_{\alpha}\cdot\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}\right)(X)

\displaystyle=\left(P_{\alpha}\cdot\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}\right)(X)=\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert}c^{\beta}_{\alpha}(X)\cdot\partial_{\beta}\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}(X)

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert}c^{\beta}_{\alpha}(X)\cdot\partial_{\beta}\left(\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon_{0}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon}X\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert}c^{\beta}_{\alpha}(X)\cdot\left(\partial_{\beta}\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon_{0}}\right)\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon}X\right)

for every
X\in B_{\varepsilon}(0)
.

With these preliminary computations we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert E_{\alpha}\cdot\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{G}\left\lvert E_{\alpha}\cdot\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}\right\rvert\,d\nu

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{G}\left\lvert\left(\log_{*}(\widetilde{E})_{\alpha}\cdot\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}\right)\circ\log\right\rvert\,d\nu

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{B_{\varepsilon}(0)}\left\lvert\left(\log_{*}(\widetilde{E})_{\alpha}\cdot\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}\right)(X)\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert\frac{d\log_{*}(\nu)}{d\lambda}(X)\right\rvert\,d\lambda(X)

\displaystyle\ll\intop\nolimits_{B_{\varepsilon}(0)}\left\lvert\left(\log_{*}(\widetilde{E})_{\alpha}\cdot\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon}\right)(X)\right\rvert\,d\lambda(X)

\displaystyle\ll\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert}\left\lvert\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right\rvert^{\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert}\intop\nolimits_{B_{\varepsilon}(0)}\left\lvert c^{\beta}_{\alpha}(X)\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert\left(\partial_{\beta}\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon_{0}}\right)\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon}X\right)\right\rvert\,d\lambda(X)

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert}\left\lvert\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right\rvert^{\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert-3}\underbrace{\intop\nolimits_{B_{\varepsilon_{0}}(0)}\left\lvert c^{\beta}_{\alpha}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_{0}}X\right)\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert\left(\partial_{\beta}\widetilde{\psi}_{\varepsilon_{0}}\right)(X)\right\rvert\,d\lambda(X)}_{\text{uniformly bounded}}

\displaystyle\ll\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert-3}.

In conjunction with
M_{\varepsilon}\asymp\varepsilon^{3}
we get that

\left\lVert E_{\alpha}\cdot\psi_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{1}=M_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\left\lVert E_{\alpha}\cdot\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{1}\ll\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert}\leq\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d}\ll\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d}.

∎

Appendix B
Exponential higher-order mixing

Let
Y\coloneqq\operatorname{UT}(X)\cong\Gamma\backslash G
denote the unit tangent bundle of a closed hyperbolic surface
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb H^{2}
with
G=\PSL_{2}\mathbb{R}
and
\Gamma
a torsion-free cocompact subgroup. We set

a_{t}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}e^{t/2}&0\\
0&e^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad u_{s}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

for all
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
.

The geodesic flow on
Y
through
x=\Gamma g\in\Gamma\backslash G
is given by

xa_{t}\coloneqq\Gamma ga_{t}\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R},

and the horocycle flow is given by

xu_{s}\coloneqq\Gamma gu_{s}\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{R}.

Moreover, we denote by
T\colon Y\to Y
the time-one map of the geodesic flow, i.e.
T(x)\coloneqq xa_{1}
for all
x\in Y
. Moreover, we will abbreviate integration with respect to the unique invariant probability measure (the normalized Liouville measure) on
Y
by
dx
or
dy
.

The following formula will be useful:

a_{t}u_{s}a_{-t}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{t/2}&0\\
0&e^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e^{-t/2}&0\\
0&e^{t/2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&e^{t}s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=u_{e^{t}s}

for all
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Moreover, we will use the following result due to Burger
[
15
]
:

Theorem B.1

(
[
15
, Theorem 2 (C)]
)
.

Let
\lambda_{1}<0
be the first non-zero eigenvalue of the Laplacian of
X
and let
0<\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}
satisfy
\alpha(\alpha-1)\geq\lambda_{1}
. Then, we have for all
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
and
T\geq 1

\sup_{x\in Y}\left\lvert\frac{1}{2T}\intop\nolimits_{-T}^{T}f(xu_{t})dt-\intop\nolimits_{Y}f(y)dy\right\rvert\leq c\frac{T^{-\alpha}-T^{\alpha-1}}{1-2\alpha}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f),

where
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f)=\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}_{3}(f)
denotes the degree
d=3
Sobolev norm; see section
2.6
.

Corollary B.2
.

In particular, we obtain that there exists
C>0
,
\alpha>0
such that for all
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
, for all
T\geq 1/2
, and for all
x\in Y

\left\lvert\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}f(xu_{t})dt-\intop\nolimits_{Y}f(y)dy\right\rvert\leq CT^{-\alpha}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f).

We will now deduce the following exponential
k
-mixing result. Note that similar results have been deduced in more generality before; see
[
5
]
. However, for our application it is important that the given constants depend neither on the functions nor on the
number of functions
.

Theorem B.3

(Exponential
k
-mixing)
.

Let
\alpha
be as in Theorem
B.1
. There are constants
D,L\geq 1
and
K_{0}>0
, such that

\left\lvert\intop\nolimits_{Y}\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\left(f_{j}\circ T^{-j\cdot k}\right)\,dx-\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\left(\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{j}\,dx\right)\right\rvert\leq L\cdot(n-1)\cdot D^{n}\cdot e^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}k}\cdot\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{j})

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, for all
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\operatorname{UT}(X))
and all integers
k\geq K_{0}
. Here
\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f)=\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}_{3}(f)
denotes the degree
d=3
Sobolev norm as before.

Clearly, the above result for the geodesic flow translates into the following equivalent exponential
k
-mixing result for the time-reversed geodesic flow.

Corollary B.4
.

With the same constants as above we have that

\left\lvert\intop\nolimits_{Y}\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\left(f_{j}\circ T^{j\cdot k}\right)\,dx-\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\left(\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{j}\,dx\right)\right\rvert\leq L\cdot(n-1)\cdot D^{n}\cdot e^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}k}\cdot\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{j})

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, for all
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\operatorname{UT}(X))
and all integers
k\geq K_{0}
.

Proof of Corollary
B.4
.

Indeed, the measure
\mu
is invariant under
T^{-n\cdot k}\colon Y\to Y
. Thus,

\displaystyle\quad\left\lvert\intop\nolimits_{Y}\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\left(f_{j}\circ T^{j\cdot l}\right)\,dx-\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\left(\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{j}\,dx\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\intop\nolimits_{Y}\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\left(f_{j}\circ T^{-(n-j)\cdot k}\right)\,dx-\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\left(\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{j}\,dx\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq L\cdot(n-1)\cdot D^{n}\cdot e^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}k}\cdot\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{j})

after reversing the indices
f_{j}\leftrightarrow f_{n-j}
.
∎

We thank Einsiedler for suggesting the strategy for the following proof of Theorem
B.3
.

Proof of Theorem
B.3
.

We will prove the theorem by induction on the number of functions
n
. The induction base
n=2
is the classical result that the geodesic flow is exponentially mixing. However, we include a proof here, since it illustrates the idea for the induction step.

Let
f_{1},f_{2}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
T\geq 1/2
. We will choose
T
appropriately later on. In a first step we split the error into two terms
\Delta,\Delta^{\prime}
that we will then bound separately.

\displaystyle\quad\left\lvert\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)f_{2}(T^{-k}(x))dx-\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)dx\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{2}(x)dx\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)f_{2}(xa_{-k})dx-\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)dx\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{2}(x)dx\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(xu_{s})f_{2}(xu_{s}a_{-k})dxds-\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)dx\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{2}(x)dx\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\Delta+\Delta^{\prime},

where

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\lvert\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(xu_{s})f_{2}(xu_{s}a_{-k})dxds-\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)f_{2}(xu_{s}a_{-k})dx\right\rvert,

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\lvert\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)\left(\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}f_{2}(xu_{s}a_{-k})ds\right)dx-\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)\left(\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{2}(y)dy\right)dx\right\rvert.

Let us first bound
\Delta
. We denote by

U\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\exp(u_{t})\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R}).

By the Sobolev Embedding Theorem
2.12
there is a constant
D_{0}>0
such that

(B.1)

\displaystyle\left\lvert f(xu_{t})-f(x)\right\rvert=\left\lvert(\lambda_{\exp(tU)}f)(x)-f(x)\right\rvert\leq\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}\left\lvert(U\cdot f)(xu_{s})\right\rvert\,ds\leq t\cdot\|U\cdot f\|_{\infty}\leq D_{0}\cdot\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f)\cdot t

for all
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Gamma\backslash G)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
x\in\Gamma\backslash G
.

Thus, we may estimate

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\intop\nolimits_{Y}\underbrace{\left\lvert f_{1}(xu_{s})-f_{1}(x)\right\rvert}_{\leq D_{0}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{1})T}\underbrace{\left\lvert f_{2}(xu_{s}a_{-k})\right\rvert}_{\leq\left\lVert f_{2}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq K_{\text{Sob}}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{2})}dxds\leq D_{0}K_{\text{Sob}}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{1})\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{2})T.

Using Corollary
B.2
we may estimate
\Delta^{\prime}
as follows.

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)\left(\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}f_{2}(xa_{-k}a_{k}u_{s}a_{-k})ds\right)dx-\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)\left(\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{2}(y)dy\right)dx\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\intop\nolimits_{Y}\underbrace{\left\lvert f_{1}(x)\right\rvert}_{\leq\left\lVert f_{1}\right\rVert_{\infty}}\bigg|\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}f_{2}(xa_{k}u_{e^{k}s})ds}_{=\frac{1}{e^{k}T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{e^{k}T}f_{2}(xa_{k}u_{t})dt}-\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{2}(y)dy\bigg|dx

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\diamond)}}{{\leq}}K_{\text{Sob}}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{1})\cdot C\cdot\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{2})(e^{k}T)^{-\alpha}\leq CK_{\text{Sob}}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{1})\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{2})T^{-\alpha}e^{-\alpha k},

where we assumed in
(\diamond)
that
e^{k}T\geq 1/2
.

Altogether

\Delta+\Delta^{\prime}\leq D^{2}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{1})\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{2})\underbrace{(T+T^{-\alpha}e^{-\alpha k})}_{\eqqcolon F_{2}(T)}

for
D\coloneqq\max(1,C,K_{\text{Sob}},D_{0})
.

The function
F_{2}
attains its global minimum at its unique critical point

T_{2}\coloneqq\alpha^{1/(1+\alpha)}e^{k/(1+\alpha)},

with

F_{2}(T_{2})=\underbrace{\left(\alpha^{1/(1+\alpha)}+\alpha^{-\alpha/(1+\alpha)}\right)}_{\leq L}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}k},

where we set
L\coloneqq D_{1}^{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\left(\alpha^{1/(1+\alpha)}+\alpha^{-\alpha/(1+\alpha)}\right)
with
D_{1}\coloneqq 1/(1-e^{-1})>1
. (The relevance of these choices will become apparent in the induction step.)

Observe that

e^{k}T_{2}=\alpha^{1/(1+\alpha)}e^{k/(1+\alpha)}\geq\frac{1}{2}\quad\iff\quad k\geq-\log(\alpha)-(1+\alpha)\log(2).

In particular,
e^{k}T_{2}\geq 1/2
holds if

k\geq K_{0}\coloneqq\log(D_{1})-\log(\alpha)-(1+\alpha)\log(2).

All in all,

\Delta+\Delta^{\prime}\leq LD^{2}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{1})\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{2})e^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}k}

for all
k\geq K_{0}
.
This concludes the proof of the induction base.

We now proceed with the induction step, going from
n-1
to
n
functions. Let
f_{1},\dotsc,f_{n}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
,
T\geq 1/2
, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. As for the induction base we will determine
T
later on. This time we will split the error in three terms
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\Delta_{3}
that we will then bound separately.

\displaystyle\quad\left\lvert\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)\cdot\ldots\cdot f_{n}(xa_{-k(n-1)})dx-\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{j}(x)dx\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(xu_{s})\cdot\ldots\cdot f_{n}(xu_{s}a_{-k(n-1)})dxds-\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{j}(x)dx\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta_{3},

where we set

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigg|\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(xu_{s})\cdot\ldots\cdot f_{n}(xu_{s}a_{-k(n-1)})dxds

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)\cdot\ldots\cdot f_{n-1}(xa_{-k(n-2)})\cdot f_{n}(xu_{s}a_{-k(n-1)})dxds\bigg|,

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigg|\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)\cdot\ldots\cdot f_{n-1}(xa_{-k(n-2)})\cdot f_{n}(xu_{s}a_{-k(n-1)})dxds

\displaystyle\qquad-\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)\cdot\ldots\cdot f_{n-1}(xa_{-k(n-2)})\cdot\left(\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{n}(y)dy\right)dx\bigg|,

\displaystyle\Delta_{3}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigg|\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)\cdot\ldots\cdot f_{n-1}(xa_{-k(n-2)})\cdot\left(\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{n}(y)dy\right)dx

\displaystyle\qquad-\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)dx\cdot\ldots\cdot\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{n}(x)dx\bigg|.

Let us start with
\Delta_{3}
. By the induction hypothesis we have that

\displaystyle\Delta_{3}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)\cdot\ldots\cdot f_{n-1}(xa_{-k(n-2)})dx-\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{1}(x)dx\cdot\ldots\cdot\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{n-1}(x)dx\right\rvert\cdot\intop\nolimits_{Y}\left\lvert f_{n}(x)\right\rvert dx

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert f_{n}\right\rVert_{\infty}L(n-2)D^{n-2}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}k}\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n-1}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{j})

\displaystyle\leq L(n-2)D^{n-1}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}k}\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{j}).

Next, we consider
\Delta_{2}
. By Corollary
B.2
we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\intop\nolimits_{Y}\left\lvert f_{1}(x)\right\rvert\cdot\ldots\cdot\left\lvert f_{n-1}(xa_{-k(n-2)})\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}f_{n}(xa_{-k(n-1)}u_{e^{(n-1)k}s})ds-\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{n}(y)dy\right\rvert dx

\displaystyle\leq K_{\text{Sob}}^{n-1}\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n-1}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{j})\cdot\intop\nolimits_{Y}\left\lvert\frac{1}{e^{(n-1)k}T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{e^{(n-1)k}T}f_{n}(xa_{-k(n-1)}u_{s})ds-\intop\nolimits_{Y}f_{n}(y)dy\right\rvert dx

\displaystyle\leq CK_{\text{Sob}}^{n-1}e^{-\alpha k(n-1)}T^{-\alpha}\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{j})

\displaystyle\leq D^{n}e^{-\alpha k(n-1)}T^{-\alpha}\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{j}),

where we assumed that
e^{(n-1)k}T\geq 1/2
.

Finally, we turn to
\Delta_{1}
. Using a telescope sum argument one can show that

(B.2)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n-1}x_{j}-\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n-1}y_{j}\right\rvert\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n-1}\left\lvert x_{i}-y_{i}\right\rvert\prodop\displaylimits_{j\neq i}\max\left(|x_{j}|,|y_{j}|\right)

for all
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}
. We will use (
B.2
) with

x_{i}\coloneqq f_{i}(xu_{s}a_{-k(i-1)})=f_{i}(xa_{-k(i-1)}u_{e^{k(i-1)}s}),\qquad y_{i}\coloneqq f_{i}(xa_{-k(i-1)}),

for all
i=1,\ldots,n-1
. Notice that in this case
\max\left(|x_{j}|,|y_{j}|\right)\leq\left\lVert f_{j}\right\rVert_{\infty}
for all
j=1,\ldots,n-1
. Moreover,

\left\lvert x_{i}-y_{i}\right\rvert=\left\lvert f_{i}(xa_{-k(i-1)}u_{e^{k(i-1)}s})-f_{i}(xa_{-k(i-1)})\right\rvert\leq D_{0}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{i})e^{k(i-1)}T

for all
0\leq s\leq T
,
i=1,\ldots,n-1
by (
B.1
).

With these observations we may estimate
\Delta_{1}
as follows.

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\intop\nolimits_{Y}\left\lvert f_{1}(xu_{s})\cdot\ldots\cdot f_{n-1}(xa_{-k(n-2)}u_{e^{k(n-2)}s})-f_{1}(x)\cdot\ldots\cdot f_{n-1}(xa_{-k(n-2)})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\cdot\left\lvert f_{n}(xu_{s}a_{-k(n-1)})\right\rvert dxds

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert f_{n}\right\rVert_{\infty}\frac{1}{T}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{T}\intop\nolimits_{Y}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n-1}D_{0}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{i})e^{k(i-1)}T\prodop\displaylimits_{j\neq i}\left\lVert f_{j}\right\rVert_{\infty}dxds

\displaystyle\leq D^{n}\frac{e^{k(n-1)}-1}{e^{k}-1}T\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{j})

\displaystyle\leq D_{1}D^{n}e^{k(n-2)}T\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{j}),

where we used that
D_{1}=1/(1-e^{-1})\geq 1/(1-e^{-k})
.

Combining these estimates we get

\displaystyle\quad\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta_{3}

\displaystyle\leq D_{1}D^{n}e^{k(n-2)}T\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{j})+D^{n}e^{-\alpha k(n-1)}T^{-\alpha}\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{j})+L(n-2)\underbrace{D^{n-1}}_{\leq D^{n}}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}k}\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{j})

\displaystyle=D^{n}\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{j})\cdot\underbrace{\left(D_{1}Te^{k(n-2)}+e^{-\alpha(n-1)k}T^{-\alpha}+L(n-2)e^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}k}\right)}_{\eqqcolon F_{n}(T)}.

The function
F_{n}
attains its global minimum at its unique critical point

T_{n}\coloneqq\alpha^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}D_{1}^{-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}\exp\left(-k(n-1)+\frac{k}{1+\alpha}\right)

with

F_{n}(T_{n})=\left(\underbrace{D_{1}^{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\left(\alpha^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}+\alpha^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\right)}_{\leq L}+L(n-2)\right)e^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}k}\leq L(n-1)e^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}k}.

Also, notice that

T_{n}e^{(n-1)k}=\alpha^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}D_{1}^{-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}\exp\left(\frac{k}{1+\alpha}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\quad\iff\quad k\geq\log(D_{1})-\log(\alpha)-(1+\alpha)\log(2)=K_{0}.

All in all,

\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta_{3}\leq L(n-1)D^{n}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}k}\prodop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathcal{S}}(f_{j})

for all
k\geq K_{0}
. This concludes the proof.

∎

Appendix C
Tube Lemma

Let
M^{n+1}
be an oriented Riemannian manifold with bounded sectional curvature
\left\lvert K\right\rvert\leq b
. Let
S^{n}\subseteq M
be a codimension
1
orientable hypersurface with normal field
N\colon S\to TS^{\perp}\subseteq TM
. We denote by
\omega
the volume form on M, and by
\overline{\omega}
the induced volume form on
S
.

At every point
x\in S
we can find an orthonormal basis
\mathcal{B}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}\subseteq T_{x}S
of its tangent space. We consider the symmetric matrix

\overline{N}(x)=\left(\langle\nabla_{v_{i}}N,v_{j}\rangle\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}.

Its eigenvalues
\kappa_{1}(x),\ldots,\kappa_{n}(x)
are called the principal curvatures at
x
of
S
in
M
. Note that in this case
\|\overline{N}(x)\|_{2}=\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\left\lvert\kappa_{i}(x)\right\rvert
, and this quantity is independent of the basis
\mathcal{B}
.

We can parametrize the
\varepsilon
-tubular neighborhood about
S
via the map

\varphi\colon(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times S\to M,(t,x)\mapsto\operatorname{Exp}_{x}(t\cdot N(x)).

There is a smooth function
\rho\in C^{\infty}((-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times S)
such that

\varphi^{*}\omega=\rho\cdot dt\wedge\overline{\omega}.

Lemma C.1

(Tube Lemma)
.

Let
\widehat{b}\coloneqq\max(1,b)
.
There is a constant
C>0
such that

\left\lvert\rho(t,x)\right\rvert\leq C\cdot(1+\left\lVert\overline{N}(x)\right\rVert_{2})^{n}\cdot e^{\widehat{b}nt}

for all
(t,x)\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times S
.

In particular, if there is a uniform bound
\kappa>0
on the principal curvatures, i.e.

\left\lVert\overline{N}(x)\right\rVert_{2}=\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\left\lvert\kappa_{i}(x)\right\rvert\leq\kappa

for every
x\in S
, then

\|\rho\|_{\infty}\leq C\cdot(1+\kappa)^{n}\cdot e^{\widehat{b}n\varepsilon},

such that

\operatorname{Vol}_{M}(\varphi(B))\leq C\cdot(1+\kappa)^{n}\cdot e^{\widehat{b}n\varepsilon}\cdot\operatorname{Vol}_{(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times S}(B)

for every Borel set
B\subseteq(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times S
with respect to the volume form
dt\wedge\overline{\omega}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in S
be a point and denote by
c(t)\coloneqq\operatorname{Exp}_{x}(t\cdot N(x))
the normal geodesic at
x
. Let
v_{0}\coloneqq\dot{c}(0),v_{1},\dotsc,v_{n}\in T_{x}S\subseteq T_{x}M
be a positively oriented orthonormal basis and let
E_{i}(t)
be the parallel vector field along
c(t)
such that:
E_{i}(0)=v_{i}
. In particular,
E_{0}(t)=\dot{c}(t)
. Further, let
(t,x)\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times S
. Then,
\partial_{t}\in T_{t}(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\subseteq T_{(t,x)}((-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times S)=T_{t}(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\oplus T_{x}S
,
v_{i}\in T_{x}S\subseteq T_{(t,x)}((-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times S)
, and
(\partial_{t},v_{1},\dotsc,v_{n})
is a positively oriented orthonormal basis for
T_{(t,x)}((-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times S)
. Finally, choose
\gamma_{i}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow S
such that
\dot{\gamma}_{i}(0)=v_{i}
.

Then

\displaystyle\left\lvert\rho(t,x)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\rho(t,x)(dt\wedge\overline{\omega})(\partial_{t},v_{1},\dotsc,v_{n})=

\displaystyle=(\varphi^{*}\omega)(\partial_{t},v_{1},\dotsc,v_{n})=

\displaystyle=\omega(d\varphi(\partial_{t}),d\varphi(v_{1}),\dotsc,d\varphi(v_{n})).

Note that

d\varphi(\partial_{t})=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\operatorname{Exp}_{x}(t\cdot N(x))=\dot{c}(t)=E_{0}(t),\qquad d\varphi(v_{i})=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\bigg|_{s=0}\operatorname{Exp}_{\gamma_{i}(s)}(t\cdot N(\gamma_{i}(s)))\eqqcolon J_{i}(t).

As a geodesic variation
J_{i}(t)
is a Jacobi field, such that it satisfies the Jacobi equation

J^{\prime\prime}_{i}=-R(\dot{c},J_{i})\dot{c}=-R(E_{0},J_{i})E_{0}

with the initial conditions

\displaystyle J_{i}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\bigg|_{s=0}\operatorname{Exp}_{\gamma_{i}(s)}(0\cdot N(\gamma_{i}(s)))=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\bigg|_{s=0}\gamma_{i}(s)=v_{i}=E_{i}(0),\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle J_{i}^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{D}{\partial t}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\bigg|_{t,s=0}\operatorname{Exp}_{\gamma_{i}(s)}(t\cdot N(\gamma_{i}(s)))

\displaystyle=\frac{D}{\partial s}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_{t,s=0}\operatorname{Exp}_{\gamma_{i}(s)}(t\cdot N(\gamma_{i}(s)))=\frac{D}{\partial s}\bigg|_{s=0}N(\gamma_{i}(s))=\nabla_{v_{i}}N.

In parallel coordinates
J_{i}(t)=\sumop\displaylimits_{j}\alpha_{i}^{j}(t)\cdot E_{j}(t)
for some function
\alpha_{i}^{j}(t)
. Therefore,
J^{\prime\prime}_{i}=\sumop\displaylimits_{j}\ddot{\alpha}_{i}^{j}E_{j}
. Then,

\displaystyle-\langle R(E_{0},J_{i})E_{0},E_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle R(E_{0},\sumop\displaylimits_{k}\alpha_{i}^{k}E_{k})E_{0},E_{j}\rangle=

\displaystyle=-\sumop\displaylimits_{k}\alpha_{i}^{k}\langle R(E_{0},E_{k})E_{0},E_{j}\rangle

and we let
R_{kj}\coloneqq\langle R(E_{0},E_{k})E_{0},E_{j}\rangle
. By curvature symmetries we get
R_{kj}=R_{jk}
. Using matrix notation
A_{ij}\coloneqq\alpha_{i}^{j}
we have:

•

A^{\prime\prime}=-AR
;

•

A(0)=\mathop{id}\nolimits
;

•

A^{\prime}(0)=\left(\langle\nabla_{v_{i}}N,v_{j}\rangle\right)_{ij}=\overline{N}(x)
.

We reduce this to a first order system as follows:

(A^{\prime}\;\;A^{\prime\prime})=(A\;\;A^{\prime})\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0&-R\\
\mathop{id}\nolimits&0\end{pmatrix}.

It follows from ODE theory that

(C.1)

\displaystyle\left\lVert(A(t)\;\;A^{\prime}(t))\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert(A(0)\;\;A^{\prime}(0))\right\rVert_{2}\cdot\exp\left(t\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}0&-R(t)\\
\mathop{id}\nolimits&0\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{2}\right).

Notice that

\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}0&-R\\
\mathop{id}\nolimits&0\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{2}=\max(\left\lVert\mathop{id}\nolimits\right\rVert_{2},\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{2})=\max(1,\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{2}).

Since
R^{T}=R
we can diagonalise
R_{jk}=\langle R(E_{0},E_{j})E_{0},E_{k}\rangle
by
D=QRQ^{T}
with
Q
orthogonal and
D=\diag(\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{n})
. Set
F_{i}\coloneqq\sumop\displaylimits_{j}Q_{i}^{j}E_{j}
. Then,

\displaystyle\langle R(E_{0},F_{i})E_{0},F_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{k,\ell}Q_{i}^{k}\langle R(E_{0},E_{k})E_{0},E_{\ell}\rangle Q^{\ell}_{j}=

\displaystyle=\left(QRQ^{T}\right)_{ij}=\delta_{ij}\lambda_{i},

which implies that

\lambda_{i}=\langle R(E_{0},F_{i})E_{0},F_{i}\rangle=K(\langle E_{0},F_{i}\rangle)

for
K
the sectional curvature.

Therefore, we get that
\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert D\right\rVert_{2}=\max_{i=1,\dotsc,n}\left\lvert K(\langle E_{0},F_{i}\rangle)\right\rvert\leq b
by our curvature bound
\left\lvert K\right\rvert\leq b
, and consequently

\max(1,\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{2})\leq\max(1,b)=\widehat{b}.

From (
C.1
) we obtain

\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert(A(t)\;\;A^{\prime}(t))\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert(I\;\;\overline{N}(x))\right\rVert_{2}e^{\widehat{b}t}\leq(1+\left\lVert\overline{N}(x)\right\rVert_{2})e^{\widehat{b}t}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\rho(t,x)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\omega(d\varphi(\partial_{t}),d\varphi(v_{1}),\dotsc,d\varphi(v_{n}))

\displaystyle=\omega(E_{0},J_{1},\dotsc,J_{n})

\displaystyle=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&\langle J_{1},E_{0}\rangle&\dotsc&\langle J_{n},E_{0}\rangle\\
0&\langle J_{1},E_{1}\rangle&\dotsc&\langle J_{n},E_{1}\rangle\\
\vbox{\kern 6.0pt\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}}&\vbox{\kern 6.0pt\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}}&\vbox{\kern 6.0pt\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}}&\vbox{\kern 6.0pt\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}}\\
0&\langle J_{1},E_{n}\rangle&\dotsc&\langle J_{n},E_{n}\rangle\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\det(\langle J_{i},E_{j}\rangle_{1\leq i,j\leq n})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\det(A_{ij})}_{\text{polynomial of degree}\dim S}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}^{\dim S}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\left\lVert\overline{N}(x)\right\rVert_{2})^{n}\cdot e^{n\widehat{b}t},

for some constant
C>0
.
∎
