Optimal Sensor Precision for Multi-Rate Sensing for Bounded Estimation Error
Niladri Das, Raktim Bhattacharya

Abstract
We address the problem of determining optimal sensor precisions for estimating the states of linear time-varying discrete-time stochastic dynamical systems, with guaranteed bounds on the estimation errors. This is performed in the Kalman filtering framework, where the sensor precisions are treated as variables. They are determined by solving a constrained convex optimization problem, which guarantees the specified upper bound on the posterior error variance. Optimal sensor precisions are determined by minimizing the l 1 l_{1} norm, which promotes sparseness in the solution and indirectly addresses the sensor selection problem. The theory is applied to realistic flight mechanics and astrodynamics problems to highlight its engineering value. These examples demonstrate the application of the presented theory to a) determine redundant sensing architectures for linear time invariant systems, b) accurately estimate states with low-cost sensors, and c) optimally schedule sensors for linear time-varying systems.



I
Introduction

Kalman filtering provides the state estimate with minimum error covariance for a given sensor precision. Sensor precision is defined as the inverse of sensor noise. In this paper, we look at the inverse problem.
Given a dictionary of sensors, what is the most noise in these sensors such that the error covariance is below a given error bound. This problem is of significant engineering value since it allows system designers to determine the optimal accuracy of sensing components that satisfy a given system-level error budget. Since cost of sensors are proportional to the accuracy, the problem has economical implications as well. In this paper we present convex optimization formulations to solve the optimal sensing precision problem in a Kalman filtering framework, for linear discrete-time time-varying systems with multi-rate sensing.

The proposed framework also impacts other important problems in sensing, such as sensor scheduling and selection. Existing algorithms for sensor scheduling

While this paper focuses on determining the optimal sensor precisions, the presented framework can also be applied to schedule and select sensors. Starting with a dictionary of sensors with unknown precisions, minimizing the
l_{1}
norm of the sensor precisions will promote sparseness in the solution. Sensors with zero precisions can be eliminated from the system, and sensors with non zero precisions will guarantee the required estimation accuracy. Therefore, it is possible to simultaneously determine the optimal sensor precisions and prune out unnecessary sensors using the proposed framework. This is appealing because the problem can be solved in a convex optimization framework in a general setting. This is in contrast with the NP-hard formulations and heuristic methods to solve them.

The problem of determining optimal sensor precision was first introduced in

We focus on determining the optimal sensor precision for linear time-varying discrete-time stochastic systems described by the model of the form:

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}_{k}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{k}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{C}_{k}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\boldsymbol{n}_{k},

(1b)

\boldsymbol{C}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y_{k}}\times\boldsymbol{n}_{x}}

\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
is the

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{n}_{k}\boldsymbol{n}_{l}^{T}]=\boldsymbol{R}_{k}\delta_{kl}

In Kalman filtering, the propagation equations for the mean and the error covariance are given by
[
18
]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}^{-}_{k}=\boldsymbol{A}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{+}_{k-1}

\displaystyle\text{ and }\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-}=\boldsymbol{A}_{k}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{+}_{k-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{k}^{T}+\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{T},

and update equation for mean is
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{+}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{-}_{k}+\boldsymbol{K}_{k}(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}-\boldsymbol{C}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{-}_{k})
, and for variance is

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{+}_{k}=(\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{x}}-\boldsymbol{K}_{k}\boldsymbol{C}_{k})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-}(\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{x}}-\boldsymbol{K}_{k}\boldsymbol{C}_{k})^{T}+\boldsymbol{K}_{k}\boldsymbol{R}_{k}\boldsymbol{K}_{k}^{T},

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{K}_{k}
is the Kalman gain determined by minimizing
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{+}_{k}\right)
for a given
\boldsymbol{R}_{k}
.

In this paper, we treat
\boldsymbol{R}_{k}
as a
variable
, and for a given error bound
\gamma_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
(positive real number) we determine the
maximum

\boldsymbol{R}_{k}
such that
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{+}_{k}\right)\leq\gamma_{d}
. Assuming
\boldsymbol{R}_{k}
to be diagonal, i.e.
\boldsymbol{R}_{k}:=\mathbf{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{k}\right)
, where
\boldsymbol{r}_{k}:=\begin{bmatrix}r_{1}&r_{2}&\cdots&r_{n_{y_{k}}}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
, with

. As shown later, it is convenient to formulate the problem in terms of sensor precisions, defined by
\boldsymbol{S}_{k}
, which is the inverse of sensor noise
\boldsymbol{R}_{k}
, i.e.
\boldsymbol{S}_{k}:=\boldsymbol{R}_{k}^{-1}
, resulting in
s_{i}:=1/r_{i}
. Defining
\boldsymbol{s}:=\begin{bmatrix}s_{1}&s_{2}&\cdots&s_{n_{y}}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
, we can determine the least precise sensors, i.e. minimize
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{S}_{k}\right)
, for which

\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{+}_{k}\right)\leq\gamma_{d}
is guaranteed. Since more precise sensors are more expensive, satisfying required accuracy with least precise sensors has favorable economic implications.

Minimization of
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{S}_{k}\right)
also has sparseness implications as
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{S}_{k}\right)
, for
\boldsymbol{S}_{k}\geq 0
, is equivalent to the
\|\boldsymbol{s}\|_{1}
. Since it is well-known that the
l_{1}
norm is sparseness promoting
[
19
]
, minimizing
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{S}_{k}\right)
will result in a sparse solution that satisfies
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{+}_{k}\right)\leq\gamma_{d}
, if a sparse solution exists for the problem. Consequently, sensors with zero precisions would not contribute to achieving
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{+}_{k}\right)\leq\gamma_{d}
, and thus can be removed from the system. It should also me mentioned that for a given system parameters, there exists a range of
\gamma_{d}
, for which the minimization problem is infeasible.

With this background, we next present the formulation to determine the optimal precision for multi-rate information fusion in the Kalman filtering framework.

III-C

Optimal Sensor Precision for a Single Measurement Update

Here we present a convex optimization framework for determining the nosiest sensors, for which the estimation errors are below a given upper bound after
one measurement update
. That is, maximize
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{k}\right)
or minimize
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{k}\right)
where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{k}:=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{k}^{-1}
, for which
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{(k+1)m}^{+}\right)\leq\gamma_{d}
, given
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{km}^{+}
. This is achieved by solving the following optimization problem.

Theorem 1
.

Optimal sensor precision
\boldsymbol{s}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y_{k,m}}}\geq 0
, which satisfies
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{(k+1)m}^{+}\right)\leq\gamma_{d}
, is given by the solution of the following optimization problem,

\left.\begin{aligned} &\min_{\boldsymbol{s}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k},\boldsymbol{F}}{\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{k}\right)},\text{ subject to }\\
&\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{F}&\boldsymbol{M}_{12}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{P}^{-}_{k}}&\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\\
(\ast)^{T}&\boldsymbol{I}_{mn_{x}}&\boldsymbol{0}_{mn_{x}\times n_{y_{k,m}}}\\
(\ast)^{T}&(\ast)^{T}&\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{k}\end{bmatrix}\geq 0\\
&0\leq\boldsymbol{s}_{k}\leq\boldsymbol{s}_{k}^{\text{max}},\end{aligned}\right\}

(20)

where
\boldsymbol{M}_{12}:=\boldsymbol{M}_{m}(\boldsymbol{I}_{mn_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{k})
,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{F}
are the design variables with
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{F}\right)\leq\gamma_{d}
,
\boldsymbol{M}_{n}
and system parameter
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
is defined in (
18
) and (
10
). The variable
\boldsymbol{W}
is a diagonal matrix, which is user defined and serves as a normalizing weight on
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{k}:=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{k}^{-1}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{k}
is defined in (
12
).

Proof.

We can write the posterior error variance as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}^{+}_{k}=(\boldsymbol{I}_{mn_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{k})\boldsymbol{P}^{-}_{k}(\boldsymbol{I}_{mn_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{k})^{T}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}^{T}.

(21)

The optimal
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}
is determined by minimizing
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{P}^{+}_{k}\right)
and is given by (
17
). However, in this formulation, we leave
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn_{x}\times n_{y_{k,m}}}
as a variable, and write
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{m}\boldsymbol{P}^{+}_{k}\boldsymbol{M}_{m}^{T}\right)\leq\gamma_{d}
equivalently as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}-\boldsymbol{M}_{12}\boldsymbol{P}^{-}_{k}\boldsymbol{M}_{12}^{T}-\boldsymbol{M}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{M}^{T}_{m}\geq 0,

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{F}\right)\leq\gamma_{d},

where
\boldsymbol{M}_{12}:=\boldsymbol{M}_{m}(\boldsymbol{I}_{mn_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{S}^{n_{x}}_{+}
. Representing
\sqrt{\boldsymbol{P}^{-}_{k}}
as the principal matrix square-root of
\boldsymbol{P}^{-}_{k}
, substituting
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{k}:=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{k}^{-1}
which is defined in (
12
) , and using Schur complement we get the following LMI
[
21
]
,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{F}&\boldsymbol{M}_{12}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{P}^{-}_{k}}&\boldsymbol{M}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\\
(\ast)^{T}&\boldsymbol{I}_{mn_{x}}&\boldsymbol{0}_{mn_{x}\times n_{y_{k,m}}}\\
(\ast)^{T}&(\ast)^{T}&\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{k}\end{bmatrix}\geq 0.

Combining the inequalities and minimizing
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{k}\right)
we get (
20
).
∎

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}:=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{z}_{km}\\

(29a)

(29b)

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{M}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{k}&\boldsymbol{M}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{k}\\
\boldsymbol{0}_{qn_{w}\times n_{x}}&\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}\end{bmatrix},

(30a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}_{n_{x}\times qn_{w}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}\end{bmatrix},

(30b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{k}&(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{k})\end{bmatrix}.

IV-B

Steady-state Variance

Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{P}^{-}_{k}
be the prior mean and variance of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{k}
at time
k
. This defines the prior random variable
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{k}^{-}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-}_{k},\boldsymbol{P}^{-}_{k})
, where
\mathcal{N}(\cdot,\cdot)
defines a Gaussian distribution.

In Kalman filtering we assume the posterior is a linear function of the prior and the measurement, i.e.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{+}_{k}:=(\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m})\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\boldsymbol{Y}_{k},

(34)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{x}\times n_{y_{k,m}}}
is the unknown gain.

The coefficient
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}
is determined by minimizing the posterior variance. However, in this formulation, we leave it as a free variable along with
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{m}
. Both these variables will be jointly determined in a single optimization problem, presented in theorem
2
.

Using (
34
), the posterior variance is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}^{+}_{k}=(\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m})\boldsymbol{P}^{-}_{k}(\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m})^{T}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{T}_{k}.

(35)

Using (
29a
), the prior mean and variance of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{k}
at time
k+1
is given by

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}\bar{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{+}_{k},

(36a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}^{-}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}\boldsymbol{P}^{+}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{T}_{m}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}^{T},

(36b)

which defines the random variable
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{k+1}^{-}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{-}_{k+1},\boldsymbol{P}^{-}_{k+1})
.

Replacing
\boldsymbol{P}^{-}_{k}
from (
35
) in (
36b
), we get the propagation equation for the prior variance

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}^{-}_{k+1}=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}(\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m})\boldsymbol{P}^{-}_{k}(\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m})^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{T}_{m}

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{T}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{T}_{m}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}^{T}.

(37)

Steady-state variance
\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}
is determined by solving

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}(\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m})\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m})^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{T}_{m}

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{T}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{T}_{m}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}^{T},

(38)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}
is the steady-state gain. The steady-state variance of
\boldsymbol{x}_{(k+1)m}
is then given by
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\infty}:=\boldsymbol{M}_{x}\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{M}_{x}^{T}
.

Remark
2
.

Equations (
37
) becomes the Riccati difference equation (RDE) if
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}
is determined by minimizing the posterior variance. Consequently, it transforms (
38
) to the algebraic Riccati equation (ARE). That is, for
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{k}
given by (
17
),
(
37
) transforms to

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1}=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k}-\boldsymbol{P}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m}^{T}[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m}\boldsymbol{P}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m}^{T}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{m}]^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m}\boldsymbol{P}_{k})\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}^{T}

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{T}_{m},

(39)

and
(
38
) transforms to

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}(\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}-\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m}^{T}[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m}\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m}^{T}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{m}]^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m}\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty})

\displaystyle\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}^{T}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{T}_{m},

(40)

Equation (

IV-C

Optimal Sensor Precision for Bounded Steady-State Estimation Error

Let
\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}
be the desired steady-state error, and let us assume that it is the solution of (
40
) for some
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}^{d}_{m}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}(\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}-\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m}^{T}\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m}\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m}^{T}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}^{d}_{m}\right]^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m}\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty})

\displaystyle\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}^{T}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{T}_{m}.

Therefore, for any
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{m}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}^{d}_{m}\leq 0

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}\geq

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}(\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}-\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m}^{T}[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m}\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m}^{T}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{m}]^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m}\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty})

\displaystyle\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}^{T}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{T}_{m},

(41)

which makes the solution of the RDE monotonic.

According to Lemma 2 in
[
25
]
, if for some
k
the solution of the RDE in (
39
) is monotonic, i.e.
\boldsymbol{P}_{k}\geq\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1}
, then
\boldsymbol{P}_{k+i}\geq\boldsymbol{P}_{k+i+1}
for all
i\geq 1
. Therefore, (
41
), guarantees that the evolution of
\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}
is monotonic, and
\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}
is guaranteed to satisfy
\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}\geq\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}
. The gap in the inequality can be minimized by maximizing
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{m}\right)
.

The optimization problem that determines the optimal sensor precision to guarantee
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{x}\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}^{d}\boldsymbol{M}_{x}^{T}\right)\leq\gamma_{d}
, for a given
\gamma_{d}
is presented as the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Optimal sensor precision
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y_{k,m}}}\geq 0
, which satisfies
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{x}\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}^{d}\boldsymbol{M}_{x}^{T}\right)\leq\gamma_{d}
is given by the solution of the following optimization problem,

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{s}_{m},\boldsymbol{Z},\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}}{\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{m}\right)}\text{ subject to }

(42a)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{M}_{11}&\boldsymbol{M}_{x}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}(\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m})&\boldsymbol{M}_{x}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\\
(\ast)^{T}&\boldsymbol{Z}&\boldsymbol{0}_{N_{x}\times n_{y_{k,m}}}\\
(\ast)^{T}&(\ast)^{T}&\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{m}\end{bmatrix}\geq 0,

(42b)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}2\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}&\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}&\boldsymbol{Z}\\
\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}&\frac{1}{\delta}\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}&\boldsymbol{0}_{N_{x}\times N_{x}}\\
\boldsymbol{Z}&\boldsymbol{0}_{N_{x}\times N_{x}}&\delta\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}\end{bmatrix}\geq 0,

(42c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{x}\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}^{d}\boldsymbol{M}_{x}^{T}\right)\leq\gamma_{d},

(42d)

\displaystyle 0\leq\boldsymbol{s}_{m}\leq\boldsymbol{s}_{\text{max}},

(42e)

\boldsymbol{M}_{11}:=\boldsymbol{M}_{x}\left(\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}^{T}\right)\boldsymbol{M}^{T}_{x}
,
\boldsymbol{M}_{x}
defined in (
32
),
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{m}
are system parameters,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{m}:=\mathbf{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{s}_{m}\right)
,
n_{s}:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}
,
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{N_{x}}
is a design variable, and
p_{j}
is the dimension of the j
{}^{\text{th}}
sensor. Variables
\gamma_{d}>0
and
\delta>0
are user specified. The variable
\boldsymbol{W}
is a diagonal matrix, which is also user defined, and serves as a normalizing weight on
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{m}
.

Proof.

From (
37
), monotonicity of
\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}^{d}
is guaranteed if

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}\geq

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}(\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m})\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m})^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{T}_{m}

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{T}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{T}_{m}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}^{T}.

Introducing a new variable
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{N_{x}}
, and the relaxation

\boldsymbol{Z}^{-1}\geq\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty},

the condition for monotonicity can then be written as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}\geq

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}(\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m})\boldsymbol{Z}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m})^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{T}_{m}

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{T}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{T}_{m}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}^{T}.

However, we want to enforce monotonicity of
\boldsymbol{M}_{x}\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{M}_{x}^{T}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}_{x}\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{M}^{T}_{x}\geq

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}_{x}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}(\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}-\boldsymbol{M}_{x}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m})\boldsymbol{Z}^{-1}

\displaystyle\times(\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}-\boldsymbol{M}_{x}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m})^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{T}_{m}\boldsymbol{M}^{T}_{x}

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{M}_{x}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{T}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{T}_{m}\boldsymbol{M}^{T}_{x}

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{M}_{x}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}^{T}\boldsymbol{M}^{T}_{x}.

Using Schur complement
[
26
]
, and substituting
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{m}:=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{m}^{-1}
, we get

\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{M}_{11}&\boldsymbol{M}_{x}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}(\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}_{m})&\boldsymbol{M}_{x}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\\
(\ast)^{T}&\boldsymbol{Z}&\boldsymbol{0}_{N_{x}\times n_{y_{k,m}}}\\
(\ast)^{T}&(\ast)^{T}&\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{m}\end{bmatrix}\geq 0,

where
\boldsymbol{M}_{11}:=\boldsymbol{M}_{x}\left(\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}_{m}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{m}^{T}\right)\boldsymbol{M}^{T}_{x}
.

The relaxation
\boldsymbol{Z}^{-1}\geq\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}
can be written as
\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{Z}\leq\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}
, which is non convex. However, we know that

\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{Z}+\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}\leq\delta\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}+\frac{1}{\delta}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{Z},

for a given
\delta
following from a special case of Young’s relation
[
26
]
. Therefore,

\delta\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}+\frac{1}{\delta}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{Z}\leq 2\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}},

(43)

guarantees
\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{Z}\leq\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}
. The inequality in (
43
), can be written as the following linear matrix inequality

\begin{bmatrix}2\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}&\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}&\boldsymbol{Z}\\
\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}&\frac{1}{\delta}\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}&\boldsymbol{0}_{N_{x}\times N_{x}}\\
\boldsymbol{Z}&\boldsymbol{0}_{N_{x}\times N_{x}}&\delta\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{x}}\end{bmatrix}\geq 0.

The optimal precision is given by minimizing
\mathbf{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{m}\right)
.
∎

.

Remark
5
.

The optimal
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}^{\ast}_{m}
can be conservative. The actual steady-state variance, denoted by
\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}
, can be much smaller than
\boldsymbol{P}^{d}_{\infty}

Precision in laser-ranging sensors is determined by the energy of the laser beam and the reflectivity of the object being sensed. A lower value of precision implies lower energy requirements for sensing the object, which results in optimal sensor designs. Modern satellites have reflectors, which allows precise sensing with low powered lasers. Some space objects (e.g., asteroids, etc.) have poor reflectivity, which fundamentally limits how accurately it can be sensed. This limit can be incorporated using the variable

VI
Summary & Conclusion

This paper presented convex optimization problem-formulations to determine optimal sensor precisions that guarantee a specified estimation accuracy. The formulation is presented in a general multi-rate sensing framework, with linear time-varying discrete-time system dynamics. Optimality is achieved by minimizing sensor precisions, subject to the upper bound on the estimation error, as defined in the discrete-time Kalman filtering framework. Since the minimization of precisions is done with respect to the
l_{1}
norm, the proposed optimization framework can also be used to determine sparse sensing architectures. This will be valuable in the design of large-scale sensor networks. We have shown the proposed theory’s engineering value by applying it to realistic flight mechanics and astrodynamics problems.
