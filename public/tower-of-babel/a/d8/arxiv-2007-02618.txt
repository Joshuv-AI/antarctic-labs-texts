Nonconcavity of the Spectral Radius in Levinger’s Theorem
Lee Altenberg Information and Computer Sciences, University of Hawai‘i at Mānoa altenber@hawaii.edu and Joel E. Cohen Laboratory of Populations, Rockefeller University & Department of Statistics, Columbia University, New York Department of Statistics, University of Chicago cohen@rockefeller.edu

Abstract
. Let 𝐀 ∈ ℝ n × n {\bf A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n} be a nonnegative irreducible square matrix and let r ⁡ ( 𝐀 ) r({\bf A}) be its spectral radius and Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue. Levinger asserted and several have proven that r ⁡ ( t ) := r ⁡ ( ( 1 − t ) ​ 𝐀 + t ​ 𝐀 ⊤ ) r(t):=r((1{-}t){\bf A}+t{\bf A}^{\!\top}) increases over t ∈ [ 0 , 1 / 2 ] t\in[0,1/2] and decreases over t ∈ [ 1 / 2 , 1 ] t\in[1/2,1] . It has further been stated that r ⁡ ( t ) r(t) is concave over t ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) t\in(0,1) . Here we show that the latter claim is false in general through a number of counterexamples, but prove it is true for 𝐀 ∈ ℝ 2 × 2 {\bf A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2} , weighted shift matrices (but not cyclic weighted shift matrices), tridiagonal Toeplitz matrices, and the 3-parameter Toeplitz matrices from Fiedler, but not Toeplitz matrices in general. A general characterization of the range of t t , or the class of matrices, for which the spectral radius is concave in Levinger’s homotopy remains an open problem.

Introduction

The variation of the spectrum of a linear operator as a function of variation in the operator has been extensively studied, but even in basic situations like a linear homotopy

(1{-}t){\bf A}+t{\bf A}^{\!\top}

We refer to

2.1.
A Simple Example

Let

\displaystyle{\bf A}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
0&0&0\\

and
t\in[4/5,1]

r({\bf B}(t))
is not concave in the neighborhood of

(and

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[r({\bf B}(1/5-\epsilon)+r({\bf B}(1/5+\epsilon)]>r({\bf B}(1/5))=2/5.

By the continuity of the eigenvalues in the matrix elements

irreducible and yet preserve inequality (

.

Figure 1.

The basic principle behind this counterexample is that the maximum of two concave functions need not be concave. Here
{\bf B}(t)
is the direct sum of two block matrices. The eigenvalues of the direct sum are the union of the eigenvalues of the blocks, which are different functions of

. One block has a constant spectral radius and the other block has a strictly concave spectral radius. The spectral radius of
{\bf B}(t)
is their maximum.

Another example of this principle is constructed by taking the direct sum of two
2\times 2
blocks, each of which is a Levinger homotopy of the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}
,
but for values of

at opposite ends of the unit interval, one
2\times 2
block,
{\bf A}_{1}
, with
t_{1}=511/512
and the other
2\times 2
block,
{\bf A}_{2}
, with
t_{2}=1/8
. We take a weighted combination of the two blocks with weight

{\bf A}(h)=(1-h){\bf A}_{1}\oplus h{\bf A}_{2}
, to get:

(3)

\displaystyle{\bf A}(h)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&(1{-}h)\frac{511}{512}&0&0\\
(1{-}h)\frac{1}{512}&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&h\frac{1}{8}\\
0&0&h\frac{7}{8}&0\end{pmatrix}.

The eigenvalues of

The principle here may be codified as follows.

Proposition 1
.

Let
{\bf A}={\bf A}_{1}\oplus{\bf A}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, where
{\bf A}_{1}
and
{\bf A}_{2}
are irreducible nonnegative square matrices.
Then

r(t)\,{:=}\,r((1-t){\bf A}+t{\bf A}^{\!\top})
is not concave in
t\in(0,1)
if there exists
t^{*}\in(0,1)
such that

(1)

r((1-t^{*}){\bf A}_{1}+t^{*}{\bf A}_{1}^{\!\top})=r((1-t^{*}){\bf A}_{2}+t^{*}{\bf A}_{2}^{\!\top})
,

and

(2)

\left.\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}r((1-t){\bf A}_{1}+t{\bf A}_{1}^{\!\top})\right|_{t=t^{*}}\neq\left.\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}r((1-t){\bf A}_{2}+t{\bf A}_{2}^{\!\top})\right|_{t=t^{*}}
.

Proof.

Let
r^{*}\,{:=}\,r(t^{*})=r((1-t^{*}){\bf A}_{1}+t^{*}{\bf A}_{1}^{\!\top})=r((1-t^{*}){\bf A}_{2}+t^{*}{\bf A}_{2}^{\!\top})
. Since the spectral radius of a nonnegative irreducible matrix is a simple eigenvalue by Perron-Frobenius theory, it is analytic in the matrix elements
[
14
, Fact 1.2]
. Thus for each of
{\bf A}_{1}
and
{\bf A}_{2}
, Levinger’s function is analytic in
t
, and therefore has equal left and right derivatives around
t^{*}
. So we can set
s_{1}={\rm d}r((1-t){\bf A}_{1}+t{\bf A}_{1}^{\!\top})/{\rm d}t|_{t=t^{*}}
and
s_{2}={\rm d}r((1{-}t){\bf A}_{2}+t{\bf A}_{2}^{\!\top})/{\rm d}t|_{t=t^{*}}
. Then

\displaystyle r((1{-}t^{*}{-}\epsilon){\bf A}_{1}+(t^{*}{+}\epsilon){\bf A}_{1}^{\!\top})

\displaystyle=r^{*}+\epsilon s_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}),

\displaystyle r((1{-}t^{*}{-}\epsilon){\bf A}_{2}+(t^{*}{+}\epsilon){\bf A}_{2}^{\!\top})

\displaystyle=r^{*}+\epsilon s_{2}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}).

For a small neighborhood around
t^{*}
,

\displaystyle r(t^{*}{+}\epsilon)

\displaystyle=r((1{-}t^{*}{-}\epsilon){\bf A}+(t^{*}{+}\epsilon){\bf A}^{\!\top})

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\,r((1{-}t^{*}{-}\epsilon){\bf A}_{1}+(t^{*}{+}\epsilon){\bf A}_{1}^{\!\top}),r((1{-}t^{*}{-}\epsilon){\bf A}_{2}+(t^{*}{+}\epsilon){\bf A}_{2}^{\!\top})\,\right\}

\displaystyle=r^{*}+\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\epsilon\min(s_{1},s_{2})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}),&\qquad\epsilon<0,\\
\epsilon\max(s_{1},s_{2})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}),&\qquad\epsilon>0.\end{array}\right.

A necessary condition for concavity is

\frac{1}{2}(r(t^{*}{+}\epsilon)+r(t^{*}{-}\epsilon))\leq r(t^{*}).

However, for small enough
\epsilon>0
, letting
\delta=\max(s_{1},s_{2})-\min(s_{1},s_{2})>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{r(t^{*}{+}\epsilon)+r(t^{*}{-}\epsilon)}{2}

\displaystyle=r^{*}+\epsilon\frac{\max(s_{1},s_{2})-\min(s_{1},s_{2})}{2}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})

\displaystyle=r^{*}+\epsilon\delta/{2}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})>r^{*}.

The condition for concavity is thus violated.
∎

2.3.
Weighted Circuit Matrices

Another class of matrices where Levinger’s function can be nonconcave is the weighted circuit matrix. A weighted circuit matrix is an
n\times n
matrix in which there are
k\in[1,n]
distinct integers
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
such that all elements are zero except weights
c_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,k
, at matrix positions
(i_{1},i_{2}),(i_{2},i_{3}),\ldots,(i_{k-1},i_{k}),(i_{k},i_{1})
, which form a circuit. We refer to a
positive weighted circuit matrix
when the weights are all positive numbers.

When focusing on the spectral radius of a positive weighted circuit matrix, we may without loss of generality consider its non-zero principal submatrix, whose canonical permutation of the indices gives a
positive cyclic weighted shift matrix
,
{\bf A}
, with elements

\displaystyle A_{ij}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle c_{i}>0,&\qquad j=i\text{ mod }n+1,\quad i\in\left\{\,1,\ldots,n\,\right\},\\
0,&\qquad\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Equation (
2.3
) defines a cyclic
downshift
matrix, while an
upshift
matrix results from replacing
j=i\text{ mod }n+1
with
i=j\text{ mod }n+1
, which is equivalent for our purposes. Cyclic weighted shift matrices have the form

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&c_{1}&0&0\\
0&0&c_{2}&0\\
0&0&0&c_{3}\\
c_{4}&0&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}.

If one of the weights
c_{i}
is set to
0
, the matrix becomes a positive non-cyclic weighted shift matrix. In Section
3.4
, we show that Levinger’s function of a positive non-cyclic weighted shift matrix is strictly concave. Cyclicity from a single additional positive element
c_{i}>0
allows nonconcavity.

Here we provide an example of nonconcavity using a cyclic shift matrix with
reversible weights
, which have been the subject of recent attention
[
4
]
. Figure
4
shows Levinger’s function for a
16\times 16
cyclic weighted shift matrix with two-pivot reversible weights

(7)

\displaystyle c_{j}

\displaystyle=16+\sin\left(2\pi\frac{j}{16}\right),

\displaystyle\quad j=1,\ldots,16.

Levinger’s function is convex for most of the interval
t\in[0,1]
, and is concave only in the small interval around
t=1/2
.

Figure 4.

16\times 16
two-pivot reversible cyclic weighted shift matrix with weights
c_{j}=16+\sin(2\pi j/16)
, (

3.
Matrices with Concave Levinger’s Function

Here we show that several special classes of nonnegative matrices have concave Levinger’s functions:
2\times 2
matrices, non-cyclic weighted shift matrices, tridiagonal Toeplitz matrices, and Fiedler’s 3-parameter Toeplitz matrices.

3.1.
2\times 2
Matrices

Theorem 2
.

Let
{\bf A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
be nonnegative and irreducible. Then the spectral radius and Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue
r(t):=r((1{-}t){\bf A}+t{\bf A}^{\!\top})
is concave over
t\in(0,1)
, strictly when
{\bf A}
has different left and right Perron-Frobenius eigenvectors.

Proof.

Let
a,b,c,d\in(0,\infty),t\in(0,1)
,
and assume
b\neq c
to assure that
{\bf A}\neq{\bf A}^{\!\top}
and the left and right Perron-Frobenius eigenvectors are not colinear. Let

{\bf A}:=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix},\quad{\bf B}(t):=(1{-}t){\bf A}+t{\bf A}^{\!\top}.

The Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue of
{\bf B}(t)
is obtained by using the quadratic formula to solve the characteristic equation. After some simplification,

r(t):=r({\bf B}(t))=\frac{a+d+\sqrt{(a-d)^{2}+4t(1{-}t)(b-c)^{2}+4bc}}{2}.

The first derivative with respect to
t
is

r^{\prime}(t)=\frac{{\left(1{-}2\,t\right)}\,{{\left(b-c\right)}}^{2}}{\sqrt{(a-d)^{2}+4t(1{-}t)(b-c)^{2}+4bc}}.

The denominator above is positive for all
t\in(0,1)
because of the assumption that
b\neq c
.
The second derivative is, again after some simplification,

(8)

\displaystyle r^{\prime\prime}(t)=-\frac{2\,(b-c)^{2}\,\left((a-d)^{2}+(b+c)^{2}\right)}{{{\left((a-d)^{2}+4t(1{-}t)(b-c)^{2}+4bc\right)}}^{3/2}}<0.

The numerator in the fraction above is positive because
b\neq c
, and the minus sign in front of the fraction guarantees strict concavity for all
t\in(0,1)
.
∎

3.2.
Tridiagonal Toeplitz Matrices

Theorem 3

(Tridiagonal Toeplitz Matrices)
.

Let
{\bf A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
n\geq 2
, be a tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix with diagonal elements
b\geq 0
, subdiagonal elements
a\geq 0
, and superdiagonal elements
c\geq 0
, with
\max(a,\ c)>0
. Then for
t\in(0,1)
,
r((1{-}t){\bf A}+t{\bf A}^{\!\top})
is concave in
t
, increasing on
t\in(0,1/2)
, and decreasing on
t\in(1/2,1)
, all strictly when
a\neq c
.

Proof.

The eigenvalues of a tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix
{\bf A}
with
a,c\neq 0
are
[
6
, 22-5.18]

[
3
, Theorem 2.4]

(9)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}({\bf A})

\displaystyle=b+2\sqrt{ac}\ \cos\left(\frac{k\pi}{n{+}1}\right).

The matrix
(1{-}t){\bf A}+t{\bf A}^{\!\top}
has subdiagonal values
(1{-}t)a+tc
and superdiagonal values
ta+(1{-}t)c
. Since at least one of
a,c
is strictly positive,
(1{-}t)a+tc>0
and
ta+(1{-}t)c>0
for
t\in(0,1)
. Therefore (
9
) is applicable.

Writing
\lambda_{k}(t)\,{:=}\,\lambda_{k}((1{-}t){\bf A}+t{\bf A}^{\!\top})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=b+2\sqrt{((1{-}t)a+tc)(ta+(1{-}t)c)}\ \cos\left(\frac{k\pi}{n{+}1}\right).

It is readily verified that the first derivatives are

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\lambda_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\frac{k\pi}{n{+}1}\right)\frac{(a-c)^{2}(1{-}2t)}{\sqrt{((1{-}t)a+tc)(ta+(1{-}t)c)}},

and the second derivatives are

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}t^{2}}\lambda_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=-\cos\left(\frac{k\pi}{n{+}1}\right)\frac{(a^{2}-c^{2})^{2}}{2\big[((1{-}t)a+tc)(ta+(1{-}t)c)\big]^{3/2}}.

Since
(1{-}t)a+tc>0
and
ta+(1{-}t)c>0
for
t\in(0,1)
, the denominators are positive. When
a=c
both derivatives are identically zero. When
a\neq c
, the factors not dependent on
k
are strictly positive for all
t\in(0,1)
except for
t=1/2
where the first derivative of all the eigenvalues vanishes.

Because the second derivatives have no sign changes on
t\in(0,1)
, and since
[(1{-}t)a+tc][ta+(1{-}t)c]>0
, there are no inflection points. Therefore each eigenvalue is either convex in
t
or concave in
t
, depending on the sign of
\cos(k\pi/(n+1))
. The maximal eigenvalue is

\displaystyle r(t)=\lambda_{1}(t)=b+2\sqrt{((1{-}t)a+tc)(ta+(1{-}t)c)}\ \cos(\pi/(n{+}1)).

From its first derivative, since
\cos(\pi/(n{+}1))>0
,
r(t)
is increasing on
t\in(0,1/2)
and decreasing on
t\in(1/2,1)
, strictly when
a\neq c
. Since its second derivative is negative,
r(t)
is concave in
t
on
t\in(0,1)
, strictly when
a\neq c
.
∎

Theorem 4

(Fiedler’s 3-Parameter Toeplitz Matrices)
.

Consider a Toeplitz matrix
{\bf A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,
n\geq 3

with diagonal values
(v,0,\ldots,0,v,w,u,0,\ldots,0,u)

Let

{\bf A}

\displaystyle\lambda_{j+1}({\bf A})

\displaystyle=w+\omega^{j}u^{(1{-}1/n)}v^{1/n}+\omega^{n-j}u^{1/n}v^{(1{-}1/n)},\qquad j=0,1,\ldots,n{-}1.

We apply Theorem
4

Let
{\bf A}

Proof.

For

from Theorem

Let
{\bf B}(t)=(1{-}t){\bf A}+t{\bf A}^{\!\top}
. Then
{\bf B}(t)
is again a Toeplitz matrix of the form (

3.4.
Weighted Shift Matrices

An
n\times n
weighted shift matrix,
{\bf A}
, has the form

\displaystyle A_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle c_{i},&\qquad j=i+1,\quad i\in\left\{\,1,\ldots,n-1\,\right\},\\
0,&\qquad\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

where
c_{i}
are the weights. It is obtained from a cyclic shift matrix be setting any one of the weights to
0
and appropriately permuting the indices. Unless we explicitly use “cyclic”, we mean
non-cyclic shift matrix
when we write “shift matrix”.

We will show that Levinger’s function for positive weighted shift matrices is strictly concave. First we develop some lemmas.

Lemma 6
.

Let
{\bf c}\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
be a vector of complex numbers and
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
,
\alpha\neq 0
. Then the roots of a polynomial
p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}x^{k}\alpha^{n-k}c_{k}
are
r_{j}=\alpha f_{j}({\bf c})
, where
f_{j}:\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
.

Proof.

We factor and apply the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra to obtain

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}x^{k}\alpha^{n-k}c_{k}=\alpha^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\frac{x}{\alpha}\right)^{k}c_{k}=\alpha^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{x}{\alpha}-f_{j}({\bf c})\right).

Hence the roots of
p(x)
are
\left\{\,\alpha f_{j}({\bf c})\ |\ j=1,\ldots,n\,\right\}
.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}\backslash 0
,
{\bf A}(\alpha,\beta)=[A_{ij}]
be a hollow tridiagonal matrix, where
A_{ij}>0
for
j=i+1
and
j=i-1
,
A_{ij}=0
otherwise, and

\displaystyle A_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\alpha\,c_{ij},&\qquad j=i+1,\quad i\in\left\{\,1,\ldots,n-1\,\right\},\\
\beta\,c_{ij},&\qquad j=i-1,\quad i\in\left\{\,2,\ldots,n\,\right\},\end{array}\right.

so
{\bf A}(\alpha,\beta)
has the form

\displaystyle{\bf A}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&\alpha\,c_{12}&0&\cdots&0&0&0\\
\beta\,c_{21}&0&\alpha\,c_{23}&\cdots&0&0&0\\
0&\beta\,c_{32}&0&\ddots&0&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ddots&0&\alpha\,c_{n{-}2,n{-}1}&0\\
0&0&0&\cdots&\beta\,c_{n{-}1,n{-}2}&0&\alpha\,c_{n{-}1,n}\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0&\beta\,c_{n,n{-}1}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Let
{\bf c}\in\mathbb{C}^{2(n-1)}
represent the vector of
c_{ij}

Then the eigenvalues of
{\bf A}
are of the form
\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\>f_{h}({\bf c})
,

Proof.

The characteristic polynomial of
{\bf A}
is

\displaystyle p_{\bf A}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\det(\lambda{\bf I}-{\bf A})

\displaystyle=\begin{vmatrix}\lambda&-\alpha\,c_{12}&0&\cdots&0&0&0\\
-\beta\,c_{21}&\lambda&-\alpha\,c_{23}&\cdots&0&0&0\\
0&-\beta\,c_{32}&\lambda&\ddots&0&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ddots&\lambda&-\alpha\,c_{n{-}2,n{-}1}&0\\
0&0&0&\cdots&-\beta\,c_{n{-}1,n{-}2}&\lambda&-\alpha\,c_{n{-}1,n}\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0&-\beta\,c_{n,n{-}1}&\lambda\end{vmatrix}.

The characteristic polynomial has the recurrence relation

(11)

\displaystyle p_{{\bf A}_{k}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\lambda\ p_{{\bf A}_{k-1}}(\lambda)-\alpha\,\beta\,c_{k,k{-}1}\ c_{k{-}1,k}\ p_{{\bf A}_{k-2}}(\lambda),

\displaystyle\quad k\in\left\{\,3,\ldots,n\,\right\},

with initial conditions

(12)

\displaystyle p_{{\bf A}_{2}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}-\alpha\,\beta\ c_{12}\ c_{21},\quad\text{and}

(13)

\displaystyle p_{{\bf A}_{1}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\lambda,

where
{\bf A}_{k}
is the principal submatrix of
{\bf A}
over indices

We show by induction that for all

(14)

\displaystyle p_{{\bf A}_{k}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\lambda^{j}(\alpha\beta)^{(k-j)/2}\,g_{jk}({\bf c})=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\lambda^{j}\sqrt{\alpha\beta}^{\,(k-j)}g_{jk}({\bf c}),

where each

,
k\in\left\{\,2,\ldots,n\,\right\}
,

, is a function of constants

From (

p({\bf A}_{2})(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}-\alpha\,\beta\,c_{12}\,c_{21}.

For

\displaystyle p({\bf A}_{3})(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\lambda\,p_{{\bf A}_{2}}(\lambda)-\alpha\beta\,c_{32}\,c_{23}\,p_{{\bf A}_{1}}(\lambda)=\lambda(\lambda^{2}-\alpha\beta\,c_{12}\,c_{21})-\alpha\beta\,c_{32}\,c_{23}\,\lambda

\displaystyle=\lambda^{3}-\lambda\sqrt{\alpha\beta}^{\,2}(c_{12}\,c_{21}+c_{32}\,c_{23}),

which satisfies (

For the inductive step, we need to show that if (

\displaystyle p_{{\bf A}_{k}}(\lambda)=\lambda\,p_{{\bf A}_{k-1}}(\lambda)-\alpha\beta\,c_{k,k{-}1}\,c_{k{-}1,k}\ p_{{\bf A}_{k-2}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\lambda\sum_{j=0}^{k{-}1}\lambda^{j}\sqrt{\alpha\beta}^{\,(k{-}1{-}j)}g_{j,k{-}1}({\bf c})-\alpha\beta\,c_{k,k{-}1}\,c_{k{-}1,k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}\lambda^{j}\sqrt{\alpha\beta}^{\,(k-2-j)}g_{j,k-2}({\bf c})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda^{j}\sqrt{\alpha\beta}^{\,(k{-}j)}g_{j-1,k{-}1}({\bf c}){-}\sum_{j=0}^{k{-}2}\lambda^{j}\sqrt{\alpha\beta}^{\,(k-j)}c_{k,k{-}1}\,c_{k{-}1,k}\ g_{j,k-2}({\bf c}),

Then Lemma

Theorem 8

(Weighted Shift Matrices)
.

Levinger’s function is strictly concave for nonnegative weighted shift matrices with at least one positive weight.

Proof.

Let the positive weighted shift matrix

be defined as

\displaystyle A_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle c_{i}\geq 0,&\qquad j=i+1,\quad i\in\left\{\,1,\ldots,n-1\,\right\},\\

By Lemma

4.
Matrices with Constant Levinger’s Function

Bapat
[
1
]
and Fiedler
[
5
]
identified matrices with colinear left and right Perron vectors as having constant Levinger’s function. Here we make explicit a property implied by this constraint that appears not to have been described. We use the centered representation of Levinger’s homotopy. The
symmetric
part of a square matrix
{\bf A}
is

(15)

\displaystyle{\bf S}({\bf A})\,{:=}\,({\bf A}+{\bf A}^{\!\top})/2.

The
skew symmetric
part of
{\bf A}
is

(16)

\displaystyle{\bf K}({\bf A})\,{:=}\,({\bf A}-{\bf A}^{\!\top})/2.

Then
{\bf A}={\bf S}({\bf A})+{\bf K}({\bf A})
. Levinger’s homotopy in this centered representation is now, suppressing the
{\bf A}
argument,

\displaystyle{\bf C}(p)

\displaystyle\,{:=}\,{\bf S}+p\,{\bf K},\qquad p\in[-1,1],

and Levinger’s function is

\displaystyle c(p)

\displaystyle\,{:=}\,r((p+1)/2)=r({\bf S}+p\,{\bf K}).

The range of
p
in this centered representation may be extended beyond
[-1,1]
, while maintaining
{\bf C}(p)\geq{{\bf 0}}
, to the interval
p\in[-\alpha,\alpha]
where

\displaystyle\alpha=\min_{i,j}\frac{A_{ij}+A_{ji}}{|A_{ji}-A_{ij}|}\geq 1.

Theorem 11
.

Let
{\bf A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be irreducible and nonnegative. Then
r((1{-}t){\bf A}+t{\bf A}^{\!\top})
is constant in
t\in[0,1]
if and only if the Perron vector of
{\bf A}+{\bf A}^{\!\top}
is in the null space of
{\bf A}-{\bf A}^{\!\top}
.

Proof.

Bapat 1987
and
Fiedler 1995
proved that
r((1{-}t){\bf A}+t{\bf A}^{\!\top})
is constant in
t\in[0,1]
if and only if the left and right Perron vectors of
{\bf A}
are colinear. Suppose the left and right Perron vectors of
{\bf A}
are colinear. Without loss of generality, they can be normalized to sum to
1
in which case they are identical. Let the left and right Perron vectors of
{\bf A}
be
{\bf x}
.
Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}({\bf A}+{\bf A}^{\!\top}){\bf x}

\displaystyle=r({\bf A})\ {\bf x},

and

\displaystyle({\bf A}-{\bf A}^{\!\top}){\bf x}

\displaystyle=r({\bf A})\ ({\bf x}-{\bf x})={{\bf 0}}.

Hence
{\bf x}
is the Perron vector of
{\bf A}+{\bf A}^{\!\top}
and
{\bf x}>{{\bf 0}}
is in the null space of
{\bf A}-{\bf A}^{\!\top}
.

For the converse, let the Perron vector of
{\bf A}+{\bf A}^{\!\top}
be
{\bf x}>{{\bf 0}}
, and let
{\bf x}
be in the null space of
{\bf A}-{\bf A}^{\!\top}
. Then

\displaystyle({\bf A}+{\bf A}^{\!\top}){\bf x}

\displaystyle=r({\bf A}{+}{\bf A}^{\!\top})\ {\bf x}\text{ and }({\bf A}-{\bf A}^{\!\top}){\bf x}={\bf A}{\bf x}-{\bf A}^{\!\top}{\bf x}={{\bf 0}},

which gives

\displaystyle{\bf A}{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}[({\bf A}+{\bf A}^{\!\top})+({\bf A}-{\bf A}^{\!\top})]{\bf x}=\frac{1}{2}r({\bf A}{+}{\bf A}^{\!\top})\ {\bf x}+{{\bf 0}}=\frac{r({\bf A}{+}{\bf A}^{\!\top})}{2}\ {\bf x}

and

\displaystyle{\bf A}^{\!\top}{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}[({\bf A}+{\bf A}^{\!\top})-({\bf A}-{\bf A}^{\!\top})]{\bf x}=\frac{1}{2}r({\bf A}{+}{\bf A}^{\!\top})\ {\bf x}-{{\bf 0}}=\frac{r({\bf A}{+}{\bf A}^{\!\top})}{2}\ {\bf x}

hence

is a Perron vector of
{\bf A}
and of

.

The following corollary pursues the observation made by an anonymous reviewer that a matrix

with colinear left and right Perron vectors is orthogonally similar to a direct sum

Corollary 13
.

Let
{\bf S}={\bf S}^{\!\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a nonnegative irreducible symmetric matrix, and
{\bf K}=-{\bf K}^{\!\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

\displaystyle\,{:=}\,{{\bf Q}}^{\!\top}{\bf S}{{\bf Q}}=\begin{pmatrix}r({\bf S})&0&\cdots&0\\

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

Then

has a constant Levinger’s function if and only if

(17)

\displaystyle{\bf K}_{1}

\displaystyle\,{:=}\,{{\bf Q}}^{\!\top}{\bf K}{{\bf Q}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&{{\bf 0}}^{\!\top}\\
{{\bf 0}}&{\bf K}_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\end{pmatrix}\oplus{\bf K}_{2},

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{\!\top}{\bf K}

\displaystyle={\bf x}^{\!\top}{{\bf Q}}{\bf K}_{1}{{\bf Q}}^{\!\top}=(1\>0\cdots 0){\bf K}_{1}{{\bf Q}}^{\!\top}={{\bf 0}}^{\!\top}.

Since

is orthogonal, it has null space

, so

∎

5.
Conclusions

We have shown that it is not in general true that the spectral radius along a line from a nonnegative square matrix
{\bf A}
to its transpose — Levinger’s function — is concave. Our counterexamples to concavity have a simple principle in the case of direct sums of block matrices, namely, that the maximum of two concave functions need not be concave. However, for the other examples we present — Toeplitz matrices, and positive circuit or cyclic weighted shift matrices — whatever principles underly the nonconcavity remain to be discerned. Also remaining to be discerned are the properties of matrix families — a few of which we have presented here — that guarantee concave Levinger functions. A general characterization of the range of
t
for which the spectral radius is concave in Levinger’s homotopy remains an open problem.
