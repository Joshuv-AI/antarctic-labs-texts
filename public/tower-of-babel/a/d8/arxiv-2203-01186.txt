Hybrid Model-based / Data-driven Graph Transform for Image Coding
Saghar Bagheri, Tam Thuc Do, Gene Cheung, Antonio Ortega

Abstract
Transform coding to sparsify signal representations remains crucial in an image compression pipeline. While the Karhunen-Loève transform (KLT) computed from an empirical covariance matrix 𝐂 ¯ \bar{{\mathbf{C}}} is theoretically optimal for a stationary process, in practice, collecting sufficient statistics from a non-stationary image to reliably estimate 𝐂 ¯ \bar{{\mathbf{C}}} can be difficult. In this paper, to encode an intra-prediction residual block, we pursue a hybrid model-based / data-driven approach: the first K K eigenvectors of a transform matrix are derived from a statistical model, e.g., the asymmetric discrete sine transform (ADST), for stability, while the remaining N − K N-K are computed from 𝐂 ¯ \bar{{\mathbf{C}}} for performance. The transform computation is posed as a graph learning problem, where we seek a graph Laplacian matrix minimizing a graphical lasso objective inside a convex cone sharing the first K K eigenvectors in a Hilbert space of real symmetric matrices. We efficiently solve the problem via augmented Lagrangian relaxation and proximal gradient (PG). Using WebP as a baseline image codec, experimental results show that our hybrid graph transform achieved better energy compaction than default discrete cosine transform (DCT) and better stability than KLT.

Introduction

Transform coding remains a fundamental component in a conventional image / video compression pipeline: an input pixel block
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is transformed to a sparse representation
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}={\mathbf{T}}{\mathbf{x}}
, where
{\mathbf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

[
1

While

(DCT)
[
2
]

Specifically,

[
3
]

\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}

This means that the computed KLT—composed of eigenvectors of
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
—can become unstable, leading to an unacceptably large variance of coding performance across different blocks.

To alleviate this problem, leveraging on our previous work on spectral graph learning

Preliminaries

2.1
GSP Basics

A graph
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},{\mathbf{W}})
is defined by a set of
N
nodes
{\mathcal{V}}=\{1,\ldots,N\}
, edges
{\mathcal{E}}=\{(i,j)\}
, and an
adjacency matrix

{\mathbf{W}}
.

W_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}
is the edge weight if
(i,j)\in{\mathcal{E}}
, and
W_{i,j}=0
otherwise.
Self-loops may exist, in which case
W_{i,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the weight of the self-loop for node
i
.

Degree matrix

{\mathbf{D}}
has diagonal entries
D_{i,i}=\sum_{j}W_{i,j},\forall i
.
A
combinatorial graph Laplacian matrix

{\mathbf{L}}
is defined as
{\mathbf{L}}\triangleq{\mathbf{D}}-{\mathbf{W}}
, which is provably
positive semi-definite
(PSD) for positive graphs
[
19
]
.
If self-loops exist, then the
generalized graph Laplacian matrix

{\mathcal{L}}
, defined as
{\mathcal{L}}\triangleq{\mathbf{D}}-{\mathbf{W}}+\text{diag}({\mathbf{W}})
, is often used.
Any real symmetric matrix can be interpreted as a generalized graph Laplacian matrix.

2.2
Hilbert Space Definitions

We first define a
vector space

{\mathcal{S}}
of real, symmetric matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
.
We next define an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
for two matrices
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathcal{S}}
as

\displaystyle\langle{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\rangle=\text{tr}({\mathbf{B}}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}})=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ij}.

(1)

Assuming Cauchy sequence convergence, the vector space endowed with an inner product is a
Hilbert Space

{\mathcal{H}}

[
6
]
.
We define a
subspace

{\mathcal{H}}^{+}\subset{\mathcal{H}}
that contains PSD matrices,
i.e.
,
{\mathcal{H}}^{+}=\{{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{H}}\,|\,{\mathbf{A}}\succeq 0\}
.
It can be easily proven that
{\mathcal{H}}^{+}
is a convex cone
[
20
]
.
Further, define
{\mathcal{H}}^{+}_{{\mathbf{u}}}\subset{\mathcal{H}}^{+}
as the subset of matrices that share the first
K
eigenvectors
\{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
.

{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}
can also be proven to be a convex cone
[
20
]
; this implies that projection to
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}
is a projection to a convex set.

2.3
Graphical Lasso

Given an empirical covariance matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
estimated from data, GLASSO formulates the following problem for inverse covariance (precision) matrix
{\mathbf{L}}
:

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{L}}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{+}}\text{Tr}({\mathbf{L}}\bar{{\mathbf{C}}})-\log\text{det}{\mathbf{L}}+\rho\|{\mathbf{L}}\|_{1}.

(2)

The first two terms in (
2
) can together be interpreted as the likelihood given observation
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
, while the last
\ell_{1}
-norm term promotes sparse reconstruction of
{\mathbf{L}}
.
As done in
[
21
]
, we interpret computed
{\mathbf{L}}
as a generalized graph Laplacian matrix, and thus (
2
) can be considered a graph learning formulation given input covariance
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
.

4
Learning Graph Transform

Given an empirical covariance matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
as previously discussed, we now compute a hybrid graph transform.
We first develop a
projection operator

\text{Proj}(\cdot)
to project
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}^{-1}
to convex cone
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}
, where
\{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are the first
K
orthonormal eigenvectors derived
a priori
from a statistical model like ADST
[
5
]
.
We seek the optimal transform via a modified GLASSO formulation, where we use our developed
\text{Proj}(\cdot)
in an iterative algorithm to compute a solution.

4.1
Eigen-Pair
(\lambda_{1},{\mathbf{u}}_{1})

Given covariance
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
, the operator
\text{Proj}(\cdot)
computes one eigen-pair at a time to compose Laplacian
{\mathbf{L}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}+\sum_{i=K+1}^{N}\lambda_{i}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{\top}
,
where
\{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
and
\{{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\}_{i=K+1}^{N}
are the model-based and data-driven orthonormal eigenvectors, respectively.
Operator
\text{Proj}(\cdot)
is a projection
[
6
]
since it is provably
idempotent
,
i.e.
,
\text{Proj}(\text{Proj}({\mathbf{A}}))=\text{Proj}({\mathbf{A}}),\forall{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{H}}

[
20
]
.

We first compute the
first
eigen-pair
(\lambda_{1},{\mathbf{u}}_{1})
for
{\mathbf{L}}
, or equivalently, the
last
eigen-pair
(\mu_{N}=1/\lambda_{1},{\mathbf{u}}_{1})
for
{\mathbf{C}}={\mathbf{L}}^{-1}
, where
{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
is a known model-based eigenvector.
Specifically, we project
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
onto 1D subspace spanned by rank-1 matrix
{\mathbf{u}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\top}
to maximally preserve
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
.
This results in
\mu_{N}
:

\displaystyle\mu_{N}=\langle\bar{{\mathbf{C}}},{\mathbf{u}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\top}\rangle.

(3)

We compute residual signal
{\mathbf{E}}_{N}
as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{N}=\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}-\langle\bar{{\mathbf{C}}},{\mathbf{u}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\top}\rangle{\mathbf{u}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\top}.

(4)

4.2
Eigen-Pair
(\lambda_{k},{\mathbf{u}}_{k}),k\in\{2,\ldots,K\}

For eigen-pair
(\lambda_{k},{\mathbf{u}}_{k})
of
{\mathbf{L}}
,
k\in\{2,\ldots,K\}
, where
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
is also known, we compute the
(N-k+1)
-th eigenvalue
\mu_{N-k+1}=1/\lambda_{k}
of
{\mathbf{C}}
as

\displaystyle\mu_{N-k+1}=\min\left(\langle{\mathbf{E}}_{N-k+2},{\mathbf{u}}_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}\rangle,\mu_{N-k+2}\right).

(5)

(
5
) is similar to (
3
), with the addition of the minimization to ensure the ordered eigenvalues
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are non-decreasing.

4.3
Eigen-Pair
(\lambda_{i},{\mathbf{v}}_{i}),i\in\{K+1,\ldots,N\}

For eigen-pair
(\lambda_{i},{\mathbf{v}}_{i})
of
{\mathbf{L}}
,
i\in\{K+1,\ldots N\}
, where unknown
{\mathbf{v}}_{i}
needs to be computed from data, we seek a unit-norm
{\mathbf{v}}_{i}
that maximizes the following inner product to maximally preserve residual signal
{\mathbf{E}}_{N-k+2}
:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{v}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{{\mathbf{v}}}~~\langle{\mathbf{E}}_{N-k+2},{\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\rangle,

\displaystyle\mbox{s.t.}~\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}=0,~~\forall k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}\\
{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}=0,~~\forall j\in\{K+1,\ldots,i-1\}\\
\|{\mathbf{v}}\|_{2}=1\end{array}\right..

The constraints require
{\mathbf{v}}_{i}
to be orthogonal to the first
K
known eigenvectors
\{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
and the previously computed
\{{\mathbf{v}}_{j}\}_{j=K+1}^{i-1}
.
Objective (
4.3
) is equivalent to
{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}{\mathbf{E}}_{N-k+2}{\mathbf{v}}
, which is quadratic and convex, given
{\mathbf{E}}_{N-k+2}
can be proven to be PSD
[
20
]
.
Thus, maximization in (
4.3
) is non-convex and NP-hard.

4.3.1
Fast Approximation

We perform a fast approximation for (
4.3
).
We first approximate
{\mathbf{E}}_{N-k+2}
with its rank-1 approximation
{\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}
, where
{\mathbf{e}}
is the last eigenvector
4
4

4

Extreme eigenvectors can be computed in roughly linear time using fast algorithms like LOBPCG
[
22
]
.
of
{\mathbf{E}}_{N-k+2}
.
We then formulate the following problem:

\displaystyle\max_{{\mathbf{v}}}{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}},~~\mbox{s.t.}~\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}=0,~~\forall k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}\\
{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}=0,~~\forall j\in\{K+1,\ldots,i-1\}\\
\|{\mathbf{v}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 1\end{array}\right..

The first two constraint are the same as (
4.3
).
The third constraint is a relaxation of
\|{\mathbf{v}}\|_{2}=1
in (
4.3
), so that the feasible solution space is a convex set.

Then, we rewrite the constrained problem (
4.3.1
) into the corresponding unconstrained version as follows.
We first define
{\mathbf{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times(N-i)}
as a matrix containing eigenvectors to-date:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Y}}=\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\cdots{\mathbf{u}}_{K}{\mathbf{v}}_{K+1}\cdots{\mathbf{v}}_{i-1}\right].

(12)

We next define the
augmented Lagrangian

[
8
]
of the constrained problem (
4.3
) as

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{v}}}-{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}+{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}+\gamma{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}+\Phi_{1}({\mathbf{v}})

(13)

where
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-i}
is the Lagrange multiplier vector, and
\gamma>0
is a weight parameter.

\Phi_{1}({\mathbf{v}})
is a convex function defined as

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}({\mathbf{v}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{if}~~\|{\mathbf{v}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 1\\
\infty&\mbox{o.w.}\end{array}\right..

Denote by
\Theta({\mathbf{v}})=-{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}+{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}+\gamma{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}
.
Thus, the objective in (
13
) is composed of two functions:
i)
\Theta({\mathbf{v}})
is convex and differentiable w.r.t.
{\mathbf{v}}
with gradient
\nabla\Theta({\mathbf{v}})=-{\mathbf{e}}+{\mathbf{Y}}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}+2\gamma{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}
, and
ii)
\Phi_{1}({\mathbf{v}})
is convex and non-differentiable.
One can thus optimize variable
{\mathbf{v}}
in (
13
) iteratively using
proximal gradient
(PG)
[
9
]
, where multiplier
\zeta_{k}^{t+1}
at iteration
t+1
is updated using
\zeta_{k}^{t}
and solution
{\mathbf{v}}^{t}
at iteration
t
as

\displaystyle\zeta_{k}^{t+1}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\zeta_{k}^{t}+\gamma{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}^{t}&\mbox{if}~k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}\\
\zeta_{k}^{t}+\gamma{\mathbf{v}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}^{t}&\mbox{if}~k\in\{K+1,\ldots,i-1\}\end{array}\right..

For initialization, we set the first solution to be
{\mathbf{v}}^{0}={\mathbf{e}}/\|{\mathbf{e}}\|^{2}_{2}
.

4.3.2
Compute Eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}

Given approximated eigenvector
{\mathbf{v}}_{i}
from data, we compute the corresponding eigenvalue
\mu_{N-i+1}=1/\lambda_{i}
as

\displaystyle\mu_{N-k+1}=\min\left(\langle{\mathbf{E}}_{N-k+2},{\mathbf{v}}_{i}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{\top}\rangle,\mu_{N-k+2}\right).

(18)

Residual is updated as
{\mathbf{E}}_{N-i+1}={\mathbf{E}}_{N-i+2}-\mu_{N-k+1}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{\top}
.

4.4
Modified GLASSO Formulation

The previous eigen-component computation constitutes a projection
{\mathbf{L}}=\text{Proj}(\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}^{-1})
.
We now formulate the following GLASSO-like optimization problem to estimate a graph Laplacian matrix
{\mathbf{L}}

[
23
]
:

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{L}}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{+}_{{\mathbf{u}}}}~~\text{Tr}({\mathbf{L}}\bar{{\mathbf{C}}})-\log\det{\mathbf{L}}+\rho\;\|{\mathbf{L}}\|_{1}

(19)

where
\rho>0
is a shrinkage parameter for the
l_{1}
-norm.
The only difference from GLASSO is that (
19
) has an additional constraint
{\mathbf{L}}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{+}_{{\mathbf{u}}}
.

We solve (
19
) iteratively using projection operator
\text{Proj}(\cdot)
and a variant of the
block Coordinate descent
(BCD) algorithm in
[
24
]
.
Specifically, we solve the
dual
of GLASSO as follows.
Note first that the
l_{1}
-norm in (
19
) can be written as

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{L}}\|_{1}=\max_{\|{\mathbf{U}}\|_{\infty}\leq 1}~~\text{Tr}({\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{U}})

(20)

where
\|{\mathbf{U}}\|_{\infty}
is the maximum absolute value element of matrix
{\mathbf{U}}
.
Hence, the dual problem of GLASSO that seeks an estimated covariance matrix
{\mathbf{C}}={\mathbf{L}}^{-1}
is

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{C}}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{+}_{{\mathbf{u}}}}~~-\log\det{\mathbf{C}},~~~\mbox{s.t.}~~\|{\mathbf{C}}-\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}\|_{\infty}\leq\rho

(21)

where
{\mathbf{C}}=\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}+{\mathbf{U}}
implies that the primal and dual variables are related via
{\mathbf{L}}={(\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}+{\mathbf{U}})}^{-1}

[
25
]
.
The unconstrained objective in (
21
) can be iteratively minimized by updating one row-column pair of
{\mathbf{C}}
in (
21
) in each iteration
[
25
]
.

Our algorithm to solve (
19
) is thus as follows.
We minimize the GLASSO terms in (
19
) by solving its dual (
21
)—iteratively updating one row-column pair of
{\mathbf{C}}
.
We then project
{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}
to
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}
using our projection operator.
We repeat these two steps till convergence.
Note that in implementation
both steps can be computed using covariance
{\mathbf{C}}
directly
, and thus inversion to graph Laplacian
{\mathbf{L}}={\mathbf{C}}^{-1}
is not necessary until convergence, when we output a solution.

5.1
Experimental Setup

We conducted image compression experiments using our modified WebP codec on standard test images from the SIPI Image Database
5
5

5

https://sipi.usc.edu/database/database.php?volume=misc
.
For the model-based first
K

frequencies of ADST
[
5
]
(the first
K

ADST is well known to perform well for coding of intra-prediction residuals.
We also followed the practice in

5
]

The local mean was calculated as the average of the decoded pixels in the

We compared our hybrid transform with fixed transform DCT and adaptive transform KLT, where the latter was composed of eigenvectors computed via the eigen-decomposition of the estimated empirical covariance matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
as discussed in Section
3
.
We selected parameter
K
as

and

to compare with KLT and DCT on different images.

We also conducted stability experiments where we varied the number of samples

used to estimate variance
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
, resulting in different estimation reliability.
The number of
4\times 4
sub-blocks used for DC4 mode intra-prediction was roughly
3600
per image, but varied depending on the image resolution.

To evaluate the performance of different transforms for different images, we first applied different transform coding schemes to each
4\times 4
sub-block chosen for DC4 intra-prediction by WebP.
For each set of transform coefficients
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, we normalized its energy to
1
and sorted them according to energy.
We plotted the average of cumulative energy percentage for all encoded
4\times 4
blocks for each image.
We also calculated standard deviation (SD) of the cumulative energy at each index and computed the average across the indices to measure the stability of a transform.

6
Conclusion

While fixed transforms like discrete cosine transform (DCT) cannot adapt to non-stationary local image statistics, the adaptive Karhunen-Loève transform (KLT) is effective only if the empirical covariance matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
estimated from data is reliable.
In this paper, we proposed a hybrid model-based / data-driven graph transform for image coding, where the first
K
eigenvectors are derived from a statistical model for stability, while the remaining
N-K
are computed from
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
for performance.
The hybrid graph transform is computed via a graph learning formulation, solved efficiently using augmented Lagrangian relaxation and proximal gradient (PG).
Experimental results show that our hybrid graph transform offers a good tradeoff between energy compaction and error variation.

While we have demonstrated the merits of a hybrid model-based / data-driven transform, the computation of the transform at both the encoder and decoder is expensive.
For future work, we will investigate reduction of computation complexity of such hybrid transform for practical image coding.
