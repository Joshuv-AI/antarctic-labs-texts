Andrés Beltrán-Pulido, Ilias Bilionis, Dionysios Aliprantis

Abstract
The objective of this paper is to investigate the ability of physics-informed neural networks to learn the magnetic field response as a function of design parameters in the context of a two-dimensional (2-D) magnetostatic problem. Our approach is as follows. First, we present a functional whose minimization is equivalent to solving parametric magnetostatic problems. Subsequently, we use a deep neural network (DNN) to represent the magnetic field as a function of space and parameters that describe geometric features and operating points. We train the DNN by minimizing the physics-informed functional using stochastic gradient descent. Lastly, we demonstrate our approach on a ten-dimensional EI-core electromagnet problem with parameterized geometry. We evaluate the accuracy of the DNN by comparing its predictions to those of finite element analysis.

I
Introduction

The design and analysis of high-performance electromechanical energy conversion devices, such as electric vehicle or aircraft motors, typically requires conducting parametric studies based on first principles within an optimization framework
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
Recently, numerical methods for uncertainty quantification and sensitivity analysis of electric machines have been proposed
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.
Such studies rely on exhaustive evaluation of the underlying physical models.
Electrical and magnetic equivalent circuit-based motor analysis approaches are computationally inexpensive
[
7
,
8
]
;
however, they often rely on
a priori
assumptions regarding the flux paths and other simplifications.
Instead, industry practitioners commonly employ finite element (FE) solvers
[
9
,
10
]
.
Nevertheless, FE solvers can be computationally demanding, especially when analyzing complex device geometries with nonlinear material characteristics.
High-dimensional parametric studies using FE solvers are practically infeasible.
Numerical solvers based on particle swarm optimization (PSO) have also been proposed
[
11
,
12
]
.
Therein, an energy functional is first formulated in terms of the unknown magnetic vector potentials corresponding to a domain discretization (similar to FE solvers).
Then, the unknown potentials are determined by minimizing the energy functional using PSO.
Although PSO-based methods are gradient-free, they present similar limitations to FE solvers as we move to high-dimensional parametric studies.

A common way of addressing the computational cost is to replace the FE solver with an inexpensive-to-evaluate surrogate.
This may be accomplished by performing regression between a finite number of well-selected inputs and the corresponding FE solver outputs.
Researchers have built surrogates with various different techniques, e.g., Gaussian process regression
[
13
]
, generalized polynomial chaos
[
14
]
, and neural networks
[
15
]
.
However, the number of simulations required to build an accurate surrogate model grows exponentially with an increasing number of input design parameters due to the curse of dimensionality
[
16
]
.

Intrusive methods, such as stochastic FE
[
17
]
, modify the FE solver to directly solve the parametric physical equations.
Stochastic FE uses traditional finite elements to discretize space, but makes the FE coefficients polynomial functions of the parameters.
Stochastic FE has excellent performance in low- to moderate-dimensional settings, but scales poorly with increasing parameter dimension.
The biggest drawback of stochastic FE is that a fixed spatial mesh is required.
Therefore, this approach cannot be easily applied to machine design problems with geometric parameters.

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) are also examples of an intrusive approach.
PINNs use a neural network to represent the parametric physical response
[
18
]
.
The PINN weights and biases are obtained by minimizing a
physics-informed
loss function.
Suitable loss functions can be constructed by integrating the squared residual of the physical differential equations or the energy of the system over space, time, and parameters
[
19
]
.
When combined with deep neural networks (DNNs)
[
20
]
, PINNs can learn high-dimensional functions
[
15
]
.
The scalability of DNNs to high dimensions makes the PINN framework very promising for the solution of demanding parametric problems.

The idea of using physical laws to solve differential equations using neural networks has been explored since the 90’s
[
21
]
, albeit in a non-parametric context.
Implementation challenges stalled progress in PINNs for years.
Nowadays, implementing PINNs and their variants with parametric inputs is feasible thanks to recent hardware, software, and algorithmic developments, including:
i) notable advancements in stochastic optimization
[
22
]
,
ii) computer hardware for parallel computing, i.e., graphics processing units (GPUs),
and iii) computer software, i.e., automatic differentiation (AD)-capable libraries
[
23
]
, such as PyTorch
[
24
]
, Tensorflow
[
25
]
, and PINN-specific libraries such as DeepXDE
[
26
]
and NVIDIA SimNet
TM

[
27
]
.

Consequently, there has been a surge of PINN applications in a variety of fields, especially for the non-parametric case.
Examples of such applications include computational fluid mechanics
[
28
]
, heat transfer
[
29
]
,
and solid mechanics
[
30
]
, just to name a few.
Parametric studies with PINNs are fewer.
In
[
19
]
, the authors proposed a PINN method that solves elliptic partial differential equations (PDEs) with thousands of parameters.
In
[
27
]
, the geometry of a heat sink was designed by solving fluid and heat equations.
In
[
31
]
, a physics-informed
DeepONet
was employed to solve infinite dimensional parametric PDEs.
Nevertheless, the application of PINNs on electromagnetic problems is scarce and limited to domains with simple geometries.
In
[
32
]
, the authors investigated inverse problems in nano-optics and electromagnetic metamaterials.
To the best of our knowledge, the present manuscript is the first application of PINNs to solve parametric (nonlinear) magnetostatic problems.

The objective of this paper is to investigate the ability of PINNs to learn the magnetic field response as a function of design parameters in the context of a two-dimensional (
2-D
) magnetostatic problem.
Our approach is as follows.
We formulate a variational principle for parametric magnetostatic problems.
We use a DNN to represent the magnetic vector potential (MVP) as a function of space, geometric features, and operating point parameters.
We train the parameters of the MVP approximator by minimizing the physics-informed loss function using a variant of stochastic gradient descent.
Here, we approximate the MVP as it is a primary vector field of interest.
Other fields (e.g., the
B
-field) and quantities of interest (e.g., the electromagnetic force) can be derived from the MVP.
Subsequently, we conduct a numerical study using a ten-dimensional parametric EI-core electromagnet problem.
The selected test system is simple enough to help us solve fundamental theoretical and technical issues in the proposed PINN framework for magnetostatic problems.
Nonetheless, it is still a representative case study with all essential elements of electromechanical energy conversion found in more complex systems (e.g., electric motors).
We demonstrate our approach on this parametric problem and evaluate the accuracy of the DNN-based model by comparing its predictions with finite element analysis.

In summary, the key contributions of this work are the following:
i) A variational principle formulation for parametric nonlinear magnetostatic problems;
ii) A PINN-based approach for solving parametric magnetostatic problems; and
iii) A parametric numerical study to assess the performance of a DNN architecture to represent the MVP.

We highlight four relevant features of the proposed PINN-based approach:
i) The PINN framework is an alternative numerical solver that returns a physics-informed inexpensive-to-evaluate model.
Hence, it could be useful for sampling extensively from the parametric magnetostatic response surface.
For instance, multiple forward model evaluations are required for design optimization or uncertainty quantification applications for electric machinery.
In these multi-evaluation contexts, direct implementation via a traditional numerical solver (e.g., FE) is computationally prohibitive.
The proposed PINN framework makes the implementation feasible.
ii) The PINN loss function is an unbiased Monte Carlo (MC) estimator of a physics-informed functional.
A distinctive feature of MC estimators is that the convergence of the integral estimate is independent of the number of dimensions
[
33
]
, which makes the MC estimator a practical tool for numerical integration in high dimensions.
iii) In contrast to conventional deep learning, the PINN framework does not require training data generation, which is the bottleneck in non-intrusive, regression-based surrogate models as we move to higher dimensions
[
15
]
.
Consequently, there is no need to build and evaluate a forward traditional solver (except possibly for validation proposes).
Instead, the PINN framework requires only the generation of collocation points, which is computationally inexpensive.
iv) FE methods approximate the MVP with piecewise (linear) functions that are weakly differentiable.
Thus, discretization errors are present in the
B
-field from an FE-based solution.
On the other hand, the PINN framework is a mesh-free approach that approximates the MVP with a continuous differentiable function.
We can then compute the
B
-field exactly using AD.

II
Methodology

II-A

Variational formulation of 2-D parametric magnetostatic problems

Electric machines are low-frequency devices; therefore, quasi-magnetostatic modeling assumptions apply.
Maxwell’s equations are thus simplified as follows.
The magnetic flux density
\mathbf{B}
is a vector field that satisfies
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
.
The magnetic field intensity
\mathbf{H}
is a vector field that is created by the action of free currents (Ampère’s law), i.e.,
\nabla\times\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{J}
, where
\mathbf{J}
denotes the free current density.
Here, we have ignored displacement currents.
Furthermore, a constitutive law that captures the relationship between the
B
- and
H
-fields within isotropic anhysteretic materials (without permanent magnetization) can be written as
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{B})=\nu(B^{2})\mathbf{B}
.
In the presence of magnetically nonlinear materials (e.g., steel), the reluctivity
\nu
is a scalar function of the squared Euclidean norm of the
B
-field,
B^{2}=\left|\mathbf{B}\right|^{2}
.
For magnetically linear materials (e.g., copper, air), the reluctivity is a constant.

Instead of solving for
\mathbf{B}
or
\mathbf{H}
directly, the magnetic vector potential (MVP) is used as an intermediate vector field of interest,
\mathbf{A}
.
The MVP is continuous across material interfaces, and it is defined through its relationship with the flux density, i.e.,
\mathbf{B}=\nabla\times\mathbf{A}
.
Here, the fields of interest (
\mathbf{B}
,
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{A}
) are almost everywhere differentiable functions of position.
At interfaces between materials, discontinuities are allowed for the
B
- and
H
-fields.

The analysis of electric machinery is commonly conducted in two dimensions with sufficient accuracy, assuming the device cross-section is constant along the third axis
[
9
,
10
]
.
Here, we assume that the geometry of the device cross-section, material properties, and the operating conditions are variable.
Let
\boldsymbol{\xi}
be the vector of parameters that we wish to vary, and
\Xi
be the set within which these parameters live.
Hence, let
\Xi\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}
, where
d_{\xi}
is the total number of parameters.
The spatial domain of interest
X(\boldsymbol{\xi})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
fully covers the cross-section of an electromagnetic device with geometry described by
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, and its outer boundary extends some distance away from the outer boundary of the device.
We assume that
X(\boldsymbol{\xi})
is bounded with a piecewise smooth boundary
\Gamma_{D}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
.
We use
\mathbf{x}
to denote points in
X(\boldsymbol{\xi})
.

Let
\hat{\mathbf{k}}
be the unit vector orthogonal to the plane of
X(\boldsymbol{\xi})
.
We assume that the change of any function value with respect to the
k
-direction
is zero (
\partial/\partial z=0
).
Then, the fields
\mathbf{B}
,
\mathbf{H}
, and
\mathbf{A}
are changing only on the
x
-
y
plane of the device cross-section.
In 2-D problems, free and bound currents flow only along
\hat{\mathbf{k}}
.
The free current is represented as the scalar field
J(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})
.
Consequently, the MVP only has a
k
-component, and is denoted as the scalar field
A(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})
.
For 2-D problems, based on these assumptions, one concludes that the
B
-field is perpendicular to the gradient of the MVP, and the magnitude of the
B
-field equals the magnitude of the gradient of the MVP, i.e.,
B=|\mathbf{B}|=|\nabla A(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})|
.

Let
X_{m}
be the subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that contains all devices, i.e.,
X_{m}=\cup_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\Xi}X(\boldsymbol{\xi})
.
Our goal is to approximate the MVP
A:X_{m}\times\Xi\mapsto\mathbb{R}
that solves the parametric nonlinear PDE

\nabla\cdot(\nu(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\xi},B^{2})\,\nabla A(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\xi}))=-J(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\xi}),

(1)

for
\mathbf{x}
in
X(\boldsymbol{\xi})
and in
\Xi
, with Dirichlet boundary conditions given by

A(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})=0,

(2)

for
\mathbf{x}
on
\Gamma_{D}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
and in
\Xi
.
The boundary condition (
2
) guarantees that magnetic flux does not escape the domain (typical when analyzing electromagnetic devices).
Here, the material interfaces are also functions of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.
From Maxwell’s equations, it can be shown that the 2-D MVP satisfies the nonlinear PDE (
1
).

We use concepts from calculus of variations to pose this parametric boundary value problem (BVP) as an optimization problem.
It can be shown that solving the weak form of the parametric BVP is equivalent to minimizing the functional

\mathcal{L}(A)\coloneqq\int_{\Xi}\int_{X(\boldsymbol{\xi})}\left[w(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\xi},A)-J(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})A(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})\right]\,d\mathbf{x}\,d\boldsymbol{\xi},

(3)

i.e., the integral of the negative coupling field coenergy of the electromagnetic device (per unit depth) over all design parameters.
The magnetic field energy density
w(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\xi},A)
is

w(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\xi},A)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\left|\nabla A(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})\right|^{2}}\nu(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\xi},b^{2})\,d(b^{2}).

(4)

It can be shown that if
A
is a critical point of (
3
) and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is in
\Xi
, then
A(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})
is a weak solution of the magnetostatic equations for almost all
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.
Besides, it can be also shown that such critical point is indeed a unique minimum of (
3
).

II-B

Nondimensionalization of 2-D parametric magnetostatic problems

We nondimensionalize the magnetostatic equations in order to alleviate the effects of sharp changes of material properties across interfaces between materials.
For example, the reluctivity of air is a few thousand times higher than that of steel.
This property causes numerical instabilities, and slows the convergence of the DNN.

We introduce the scaled fundamental quantities

\bar{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{\mathbf{x}}{x^{\ast}}\,,~\bar{A}=\frac{A}{A^{\ast}},~\bar{J}=\frac{J}{J^{\ast}},

(5)

where the
x^{\ast}
,
A^{\ast}
, and
J^{\ast}
are constants to be chosen.
Similarly, we map the space of parameters
\Xi
to the
d_{\xi}
-dimensional unit hypercube
\bar{\Xi}=[0,1]^{d_{\xi}}
(i.e., the normalized input parameter space).
Thus, we scale the vector of parameters as follows:

\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=[\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}_{\max}-\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}_{\min})]^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}_{\min}),

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}_{\min}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}_{\max}
are the lower and upper limits of the range of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, respectively.
Here,
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{u})
is the diagonal matrix with
\mathbf{u}
in the diagonal.

Based on the scaled fundamental quantities, the
B
-field magnitude is:

B=\left|\nabla A(\mathbf{x})\right|=(A^{\ast}/x^{\ast})\left|\bar{\nabla}\bar{A}\right|
, where
\bar{\nabla}
is the gradient with respect to
\bar{\mathbf{x}}
.
So, we define the scaled
B
-field magnitude as

\bar{B}\coloneqq\frac{B}{B^{\ast}}=\left|\bar{\nabla}\bar{A}\right|\quad\text{with}\quad B^{\ast}=\frac{A^{\ast}}{x^{\ast}}.

(7)

Let

\bar{\nu}(\bar{b}^{2})\coloneqq\frac{\nu\left((B^{*}\bar{b})^{2}\right)}{\nu^{\ast}}\quad\text{with}\quad\nu^{\ast}=\frac{(x^{\ast})^{2}J^{\ast}}{A^{\ast}},

(8)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=J^{\ast}A^{\ast}\det\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}}{\partial\bar{\mathbf{x}}}\det\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\xi}}{\partial\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}

(9)

\displaystyle=J^{\ast}A^{\ast}(x^{\ast})^{2}\det\left(\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}_{\max}-\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}_{\min})\right),

where the determinant of the Jacobian matrix of
\mathbf{u}
with respect to
\bar{\mathbf{u}}
is denoted by
\det\partial\mathbf{u}/\partial\bar{\mathbf{u}}
, and the dependency of
\bar{\nu}
on
\bar{\mathbf{x}}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
has been omitted for clarity.
Then, the scaled form of the functional (
3
) is

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{L}}(\bar{A})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{{\mathcal{L}}(A)}{{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}}

(10)

\displaystyle=\int_{\bar{\Xi}}\int_{\bar{X}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\left|\bar{\nabla}\bar{A}\right|^{2}}\bar{\nu}(\bar{b}^{2})\,d\bar{b}^{2}-\bar{J}\bar{A}\right\}\,d\bar{\mathbf{x}}\,d\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}.

Lastly, the boundary condition (
2
) is rewritten as

\bar{A}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=0\,\,\,\text{on}\,\,\,\bar{\Gamma}_{D}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}).

(11)

In these equations, the scaled spatial domain is denoted as
\bar{X}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
, and its boundary as
\bar{\Gamma}_{D}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
.

II-C

Physics-informed neural networks for 2-D parametric magnetostatic problems

In this section, we develop a physics-informed machine learning method for solving 2-D parametric magnetostatic problems.
The method trains a neural network that represents the scaled physical response as a function of the scaled spatial coordinates
\bar{\mathbf{x}}
and parameters
\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
(capturing geometry features, material properties, and operating point conditions).

The first step is to build a neural network approximator for the scaled MVP
\bar{A}
.
We choose

\hat{A}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}};\boldsymbol{\theta})=C(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})+D(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\,N(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}};\boldsymbol{\theta}).

(12)

This function takes the scaled space point
\bar{\mathbf{x}}
and parameters
\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
as input.
Note that
\hat{A}
represents the solution of the physical equations for all possible parameter values.
The neural network
N(\cdot,\cdot\,;\boldsymbol{\theta})
is parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
(i.e., weights and biases).
One chooses the function
C
so that it satisfies the Dirichlet boundary condition of (
11
).
Since we have zero boundary conditions, we trivially have that
C=0
.
Similarly, we construct the function
D
so that it is zero at the boundary and positive inside
\bar{X}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
for all
\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
in
\bar{\Xi}
; see (
40
) for the specific choice of this function in our numerical example.

The idea is to find
\boldsymbol{\theta}
by minimizing the loss function

\bar{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\coloneqq\bar{\mathcal{L}}(\hat{A}(\cdot,\cdot\,;\boldsymbol{\theta})).

(13)

Despite the fact that
\mathcal{L}(A)
has a unique solution, here
\bar{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
does not have a unique solution.
One can very easily show that the same function can be represented by different
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, e.g., by relabelling the internal neurons.
We postulate that
\bar{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
behaves well if one defines appropriate equivalence function classes and makes the network
N
sufficiently expressive.
However, we only offer numerical evidence that this is true.
A theoretical investigation of the error introduced by the neural network parameterization is beyond the scope of this work.

II-C
1
Encoding layer

The input to the neural network passes through a so-called encoding layer, denoted here by
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{E}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
(see Fig.
1
).
The purpose of this encoding layer is to address the spectral bias pathology
[
34
]
.
The spectral bias causes neural networks to learn first the low-frequency components.
Consequently,
high-frequency
details in the solution are the last ones the network captures; in some cases, the number of iterations needed to capture those
high-frequency
components becomes intractable.
Notice that the encoding layer receives both the spatial coordinates
\bar{\mathbf{x}}
and the parameters
\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
.

Fig. 1:
Schematic representation of the PINNs approach for solving 2-D parametric magnetostatic problems.
A DNN models the MVP.
The input to the DNN passes through an encoding layer
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{E}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
.
We use AD to compute the
B
-field.
The PINN loss function is an estimate of the integral of the negative coupling field coenergy over all parameters, and the optimization is conducted by ADAM to find the DNN free parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

The encoding layer maps the spatial input
\bar{\mathbf{x}}
into a
high-dimensional
space using a set of basis space functions.
Specifically, we introduce the encoding layer
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{E}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
that maps a
two-dimensional
input vector
\bar{\mathbf{x}}=(\bar{x},\bar{y})^{\top}
to a Fourier feature space as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{x}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=(1,\varphi_{x,1}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),\ldots,\varphi_{x,2m}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}))^{\top},

(14)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{y}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=(1,\varphi_{y,1}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),\ldots,\varphi_{y,2m}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}))^{\top},

(15)

with

\displaystyle\varphi_{x,2j-1}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\cos{\left(\frac{2j\pi\bar{x}}{\lambda_{x}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}\right)},

(16)

\displaystyle\varphi_{x,2j}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\sin{\left(\frac{2j\pi\bar{x}}{\lambda_{x}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}\right)},

(17)

\displaystyle\varphi_{y,2j-1}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\cos{\left(\frac{2j\pi\bar{y}}{\lambda_{y}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}\right)},

(18)

\displaystyle\varphi_{y,2j}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\sin{\left(\frac{2j\pi\bar{y}}{\lambda_{y}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}\right)},

(19)

for
j={1,\ldots,m}
, where the number of harmonics in each spatial direction is denoted by
m
, and the wavelengths of the periodic features are given by
\lambda_{x}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
and
\lambda_{y}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
.
Here, the wavelengths depend on the geometric parameters of the device under study.
See Section
III-B
for the specific form of these functions in our numerical example.
The feature space of interest is described by the
(2m+1)\times(2m+1)
matrix

\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{x}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{y}^{\top}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}).

The encoding layer is

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{E}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})).

(20)

Here, the operator
\text{vec}(\cdot)
denotes the vectorization of the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
, i.e., the column vector obtained by stacking the columns of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
vertically.

II-C
2
Modified residual neural network

In this section, we show the definition of the DNN architecture of choice for our numerical studies, i.e., modified residual neural networks (ModResNets) proposed by
[
35
]
.
We selected ModResNets based on an exhaustive study of various PINN architectures for non-parametric magnetostatic problems
[
36
]
.
The forward pass of a scalar value ModResNet with
L
hidden layers is defined recursively:

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{(k)}\left(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right)=\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\,\mathbf{h}^{(k)}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})+\mathbf{b}^{(k)},

(21)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{h}^{(k+1)}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=&\left[1-\sigma\left(\mathbf{z}^{(k)}\left(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right)\right)\right]\circ\mathbf{u}\left(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right)\\
&+\sigma\left(\mathbf{z}^{(k)}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right)\circ\mathbf{v}\left(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right),\end{aligned}

(22)

for
k=0,\ldots,L-1
, where
\circ
denotes
element-wise
vector multiplication.
Thus, all the hidden layers have the same width,
d_{k}=d
for
k=1,\ldots,L
.
The activation function
\sigma
is applied element-wise.
Here, we used

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\sigma\left(\mathbf{W}^{u}\,\mathbf{h}^{(0)}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})+\mathbf{b}^{u}\right),

(23)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\sigma\left(\mathbf{W}^{v}\,\mathbf{h}^{(0)}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})+\mathbf{b}^{v}\right).

(24)

The input layer of the network is

\mathbf{h}^{(0)}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{E}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})^{\top},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\top}\right)^{\top}.

(25)

The input dimension
d_{0}
depends on the number of harmonics
m
in the encoding layer.
The output layer of a ModResNet is

N\left(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}};\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)=\mathbf{W}^{(L)}\,\mathbf{h}^{(L)}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})+\mathbf{b}^{(L)}.

(26)

Because the MVP is a scalar, the output dimension is simply
d_{L+1}=1
.
The tunable parameters of a ModResNet are

\boldsymbol{\theta}=\left\{\mathbf{W}^{u},\mathbf{b}^{u},\mathbf{W}^{v},\mathbf{b}^{v},\left\{\left(\mathbf{W}^{(k)},\mathbf{b}^{(k)}\right)\right\}_{k=0}^{L}\right\}.

The
d_{k+1}\times d_{k}
matrices
\mathbf{W}^{(k)}
are weights, and
\mathbf{b}^{(k)}
are the corresponding biases.
In addition, we have the
d\times d_{0}
weight matrices
\mathbf{W}^{u},\mathbf{W}^{v}
, and the
d
-dimensional bias vectors
\mathbf{b}^{u}
,
\mathbf{b}^{v}
, that parameterize the transformation layers.

The ModResNet accounts for multiplicative interactions between different inputs.
The two transformation layers (
23
) and (
24
) embed the input variables into a
high-dimensional
feature space.

II-C
3
Activation function

We consider the sigmoid linear unit (SiLU) activation function:

\sigma(\mathbf{z})=\frac{\mathbf{z}}{1+\exp{(-\mathbf{z}})}.

(27)

We use the SiLU function because it is a smooth and differentiable activation function.
The SiLU activation function has shown better performance than other common choices of activation functions (e.g., rectified linear unit, hyperbolic tangent)
[
37
]
.

II-D

Training deep neural network approximators

In this section, we discuss the complete details of training the DNN that solves the parametric magnetostatics problem.
We start with recasting the original optimization problem as a stochastic optimization problem, which leads to a sampling estimate of the physics-informed loss function.
Subsequently, we present the stochastic gradient descent algorithm.

II-D
1
Loss function minimization as a stochastic optimization problem

Minimizing the loss function using a deterministic optimization algorithm is problematic because it often converges to local minima.
To address this issue, we recast the loss function minimization as a stochastic optimization problem.

To turn the original formulation (
13
) into a stochastic optimization problem, we start by defining

\ell\left(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}};\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)=\left|\bar{\Xi}\right|\left|\bar{X}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right|\left[\bar{w}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\bar{J}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\hat{A}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right],

(28)

with

\bar{w}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\left|\bar{\nabla}\hat{A}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right|^{2}}\bar{\nu}\left(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}},\bar{b}^{2}\right)\,d\bar{b}^{2},

(29)

where
\left|S\right|
is the hypervolume of a set
S
.
Now notice that the loss function can be expressed as

\bar{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{1}{N_{\xi}N_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\xi}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{x}}\ell\left(\bar{\mathbf{X}}^{(i,j)},\bar{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}^{(i)};\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right],

(30)

where
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}^{(i)}
are independent random vectors distributed uniformly in
\bar{\Xi}
, for each
i=1,\dots,N_{\xi}
,
\bar{\mathbf{X}}^{(i,j)}
are independent conditional on
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}^{(i)}
and distributed uniformly in
\bar{X}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}^{(i)})
, for
j=1,\dots,N_{x}
, and
\mathbf{E}\left[\cdot\right]
is the expectation operator.

To start the DNN training process, we initialize the weights of the previously described DNN using the Glorot initialization scheme
[
38
]
.
Glorot and Bengio suggest to initialize the values of the weight matrices of each layer using a zero mean normal distribution, the standard deviation of which depends on the size of the respective weight matrix.
Here, the bias vectors are always initialized as zero.

We use a variant of the standard stochastic gradient descent (SGD) algorithm, which has updates of the form

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k+1}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}-\eta_{k}\frac{1}{N_{\xi}N_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\xi}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{x}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ell\left(\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(i,j)}_{k},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(i)}_{k};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}\right),

(31)

where
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
denotes the gradient with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(i)}_{k}
and
\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(i,j)}_{k}
are independent samples of the random vectors
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}^{(i)}
and
\bar{\mathbf{X}}^{(i,j)}
, respectively.
This algorithm converges to a local minimum of
\bar{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
if the learning rate
\eta_{k}
satisfies the conditions of Robbins–Monro
[
39
]
.

In our numerical example, we opted for the Adaptive Moments (ADAM) optimization algorithm
[
22
]
.
This algorithm computes adaptive learning rates for each parameter using exponentially decaying averages of past gradients and past squared gradients, and converges faster than the vanilla SGD.
In ADAM, the averaging hyper-parameters denoted as
\beta_{1}
, and
\beta_{2}
are free hyper-parameters to be chosen.
We use
\beta_{1}=0.9
,
\beta_{2}=0.999
as suggested by
[
22
]
.

We use an exponential decay scheduler given by

\eta_{k+1}=\gamma\eta_{k}\,,

(32)

where the learning rate is updated at the end of the
k
-iteration.
The parameter
\gamma
,
0<\gamma<1
, is the multiplicative factor of learning rate decay.
See (
49
) for the specific value of
\gamma
.

The entire DNN training process is depicted in Algorithm
1
.
Fig.

shows a schematic representation of the proposed approach.

III-C

Implementation details

Our PINNs approach for solving 2-D parametric magnetostatic problems is implemented using PyTorch
[
24
]
.
We use AD
[
23
]
to obtain the required derivatives.
Our code is deployed on a GPU cluster.
The specifications of the hardware used to train the PINN can be found in
[
41
]
.

The MVP solution predicted by our ModResNet is compared with the solution obtained from a custom FE solver.
We discretize the spatial domain of the device (for a specific geometry) into
N_{\text{ele}}
triangular elements using Triangle
[
42
]
(a two-dimensional quality mesh generator and Delaunay triangulator).
The FE mesh uses first-order triangular elements.
Thus, the MVP is a linear function of node potentials in each element and the
B
-field is constant inside each element.
The maximum triangle area is 0.04 mm
2
.
Our in-house FE code has been validated extensively against results from the commercial software
ANSYS Electronics Desktop/Maxwell

[
43
]
.

III-D

Evaluation metrics

We use absolute and relative errors to assess the performance of the DNN-based model.
The FE analysis (for a specific geometry) of the EI-core is the ground truth.
We assess the prediction accuracy of the MVP, the
B
-field, and the electromagnetic force acting on the I-core.

We define the mean relative error in MVP as

e_{A}^{\text{rel}}=\int_{\Xi}{\frac{\lVert A_{\text{FE}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\xi})-A_{\text{PINN}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\xi})\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\lVert A_{\text{FE}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\xi})\rVert_{2}^{2}}}\,d\boldsymbol{\xi},

(42)

where
A_{\text{FE}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\xi})
is the FE MVP prediction,
A_{\text{PINN}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\xi})
is the PINN-based MVP prediction, and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2}
is the norm of the space of square-integrable functions from
X(\boldsymbol{\xi})
to
\mathbb{R}
,
L^{2}(X(\boldsymbol{\xi}))
.
We approximate the mean relative error via

\hat{e}_{A}^{\text{rel}}=\frac{1}{N_{\xi}^{e}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\xi}^{e}}\left\{\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N^{(i)}_{\text{node}}}\hat{e}_{A}^{(i,j)}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N^{(i)}_{\text{node}}}\left[A_{\text{FE}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{\text{node}}^{(i,j)},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)}\right)\right]^{2}}\right\},

(43)

with

\hat{e}_{A}^{(i,j)}=\left[A_{\text{FE}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{\text{node}}^{(i,j)},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)}\right)-A_{\text{PINN}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{\text{node}}^{(i,j)},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)}\right)\right]^{2},

(44)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)}
are
N_{\xi}^{e}
uniformly distributed samples from
\Xi
, and
\{(\mathbf{x}_{\text{node}}^{(i,j)})\}_{j=1}^{N^{(i)}_{\text{node}}}
are the FE nodes for geometry
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)}
.

To visually assess how well our solution compares to FE, we plot the spatial contours of the point-wise absolute error for a single parameter
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.
For the MVP:

e_{A}^{\text{abs}}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{node}},\boldsymbol{\xi})=|A_{\text{FE}}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{node}},\boldsymbol{\xi})-A_{\text{PINN}}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{node}},\boldsymbol{\xi})|.

(45)

For the
B
-field, the point-wise absolute error of the

-field magnitude is

e_{B}^{\text{abs}}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{mid}},\boldsymbol{\xi})=|B_{\text{FE}}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{mid}},\boldsymbol{\xi})-B_{\text{PINN}}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{mid}},\boldsymbol{\xi})|.

(46)

Here, we use the mid-points of the each element in the mesh of the FE model, i.e.,
\{(\mathbf{x}_{\text{mid}}^{(i,j)})\}_{j=1}^{N^{(i)}_{\text{ele}}}
, as the spatial positions of interest for geometry
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)}
, when evaluating
e_{B}^{\text{abs}}
.

The electromagnetic force acting on the I-core is a primary quantity of interest.
Due to symmetry, the force acting along the
x
-direction vanishes, i.e.,
F_{x}=0
.
We use the Maxwell stress tensor (MST) method to compute the force (per unit depth) along the
y
-direction,
F_{y}(\boldsymbol{\xi})

[
10
]
.
The MST integration path is composed of three linear segments, and surrounds the I-core at a distance of
g/2
(see Fig.
5
).
The force is calculated based on the normal and tangential components of the
B
-field along the integration path.
In this particular example, these happen to coincide with the Cartesian components
B_{x}
and
B_{y}
.
Accounting for the entire device, we have

F_{y}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\nu_{0}\delta_{a}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{a}}\left\{B^{2}_{y}\left(\mathbf{x}_{a}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{\xi}\right)-B^{2}_{x}\left(\mathbf{x}_{a}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{\xi}\right)\right\}\\
\mbox{}+2\nu_{0}\delta_{b}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{b}}\left\{B_{x}\left(\mathbf{x}_{b}^{(j)},\boldsymbol{\xi}\right)B_{y}\left(\mathbf{x}_{b}^{(j)},\boldsymbol{\xi}\right)\right\}\\
\mbox{}+\nu_{0}\delta_{c}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{c}}\left\{B^{2}_{x}\left(\mathbf{x}_{c}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\xi}\right)-B^{2}_{y}\left(\mathbf{x}_{c}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\xi}\right)\right\},

(47)

where
\{(\mathbf{x}_{a}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N_{a}}
are
N_{a}
equidistant spatial points on the integration path
a
(with a spacing
\delta_{a}
), and similarly for paths
b
and
c
.
Thus, the relative force error is

e_{F}^{\text{rel}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\frac{|F_{y,\text{FE}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-F_{y,\text{PINN}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})|}{|F_{y,\text{FE}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})|}.

(48)

To compute
F_{y,\text{FE}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
and
F_{y,\text{PINN}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
, we evaluate (
47
) using the
B
-field predicted by the FE and PINN models, respectively.

In the following numerical study, (
43
)–(
48
) are the metrics used to assess the performance of the DNN-based model.
We compare the FE and PINN responses in the actual physical space.
Therefore, we use the nondimensionalization constants to scale back all the DNN-based predictions.

IV
Conclusions

A main contribution of our work is the formulation of parametric magnetostatic problems as a variational problem.
The functional to be minimized is the integral of the (negative) coupling field coenergy over all design parameters.
Furthermore, we designed a PINN-based approach for solving parametric magnetostatic problems.
We represented the MVP with a DNN, and trained its parameters by minimizing the coenergy functional through a stochastic optimization approach.

Subsequently, we conducted a parametric study in which we learned the MVP as a function of the geometry and the operating condition of an EI-core electromagnet described by ten parameters.
The trained PINN had a relative MVP error between 0.3% and 2.2% with 95% probability.
The absolute errors in MVP and
B
-field were more pronounced inside the core.

This ten-dimensional parametric magnetostatic problem is challenging, albeit it is probably solvable in a more computationally efficient way by non-intrusive, regression-based approaches such as combining Gaussian process regression with principal component analysis
[
6
]
.
However, it is known that regression approaches do not scale well with increasing dimensions.
Therefore, we anticipate that the proposed approach holds promise scaling up to higher-dimensional parametric magnetostatic problems, such as for permanent magnet synchronous machines.
Such a study is, however, beyond the scope of this paper.

As we go to more complex geometries and larger spatial domains, we believe that adaptive selection of collocation points will be needed.
[
44
]
.
However, we still lack the theoretical guarantees that such adaptive schemes converge, and in particular for the parametric case.
Finally, there is a need for PINN-specific training algorithms
[
35
]
.
