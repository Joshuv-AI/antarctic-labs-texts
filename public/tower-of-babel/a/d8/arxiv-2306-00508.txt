On stability of solitons for 3D Maxwell–Lorentz


Abstract
We consider stability of solitons of 3D Maxwell–Lorentz system with extended charged spinning particle. The solitons are solutions which correspond to a particle moving with a constant velocity v ∈ ℝ 3 v\in\mathbb{R}^{3} with | v | < 1 |v|<1 and rotating with a constant angular velocity ω ∈ R 3 \omega\in R^{3} . Our main results are the orbital stability of moving solitons with ω = 0 \omega=0 and a linear orbital stability of rotating solitons with v = 0 v=0 . The Hamilton–Poisson structure of the Maxwell–Lorentz system is degenerate and admits the Casimir invariants. We construct the Lyapunov function as a linear combination of the Hamiltonian with a suitable Casimir invariant. The key point is a lower bound for this function. The proof of the bound in the case ω ≠ 0 \omega\neq 0 relies on angular momentum conservation and suitable spectral arguments including the Heinz–Löwner–Kato inequality and closed graph theorem.

Introduction

The paper addresses
the stability of solitons of 3D Maxwell–Lorentz system
with an extended charged spinning particle.
We choose the units such that the speed
of light is
c=1
.
The equations read as follows
(see
[
35
,
38
]
):

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\dot{E}(x,t)&=&{\rm curl{\hskip 1.42262pt}}B(x,t)-w(x,t)\rho(x-q(t)),\qquad{\rm div{\hskip 1.42262pt}}E(x,t)=\rho(x-q(t))\\
\dot{B}(x,t)&=&-\,{\rm curl{\hskip 1.42262pt}}E(x,t),\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\quad\quad\quad{\rm div{\hskip 1.42262pt}}B(x,t)=0\\
m\dot{q}(t)&=&p(t),\quad\dot{p}(t)=\langle E(x,t)+w(x,t){\rm\wedge}B(x,t),\rho(x-q(t))\rangle\\
I\dot{\omega}(t)&=&\langle(x-q(t)){\rm\wedge}\big[E(x,t)+w(x,t){\rm\wedge}B(x,t)\big],\rho(x-q(t))\rangle\end{array}\right|,

(1.1)

where

\rho(x-q(t))
is the charge distribution of the extended
particle centered at the point
q(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

m>0
is the mass of the particle and
I>0
is its moment of inertia,

\omega(t)
is the angular velocity
of the particle rotation and
w(x,t):=\dot{q}(t)+\omega(t){\rm\wedge}(x\!-\!q(t))
is the velocity field.
The brackets
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
here and below
denote the
integral over
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
or inner product in the Hilbert spaces

L^{2}:=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
with suitable
n\geq 1
.

This model
was introduced by Abraham in 1903–1905 (see
[
1
,
2
]
)
for the description of the classical extended electron coupled to its own Maxwell field.
The detailed account on the genesis and early investigations
of the system
by Dirac, Poincaré, Sommerfeld, and others
can be found in
[
38
, Chapter 3]
.

The first results on the corresponding long-time behavior
for the system (
1.1
)
were obtained in the 1990s.
This system
plays a crucial role in a rigorous analysis of radiation by moving particles; see

[
24
,
38
]
.
The mathematical analysis of this system
is useful in connection to the related problems of
nonrelativistic QED.
In particular, the similarity in the renormalization of mass
was pointed out
by Hiroshima and Spohn
[
10
]
.

We assume that the charge density

\rho(x)
is smooth, spherically-symmetric, and not identically zero:

\rho\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),\qquad\rho(x)=\rho_{1}(|x|);\qquad\rho(x)=0\,\,\,\,{\rm for}\,\,|x|\geq R_{\rho};\qquad\rho(x)\not\equiv 0.

(1.2)

One of the basic peculiarities of
the system (
1.1
) is its invariance under
the Euclidean group generated by
translations, rotations and reflections of the space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Moreover, the system
admits solitons
{\cal S}_{v,\omega}

discovered by Spohn
[
38
]
.
The solitons are solutions
moving with constant speed

v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

and rotating with constant angular velocity
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
The solitons are trajectories of
one-parametric subgroups of the
Euclidean
symmetry group.
As is pointed out in
[
38
]
,
for
\rho(x)\not\equiv 0
,
the solitons with finite energy exist only
for
(v,\omega)\in\Sigma
, where

\Sigma=\{(v,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}:|v|<1\,\,{\rm and}\,\,{\rm either}\,\,\omega\|v\,\,{\rm or}\,\,\,\omega\bot v\}.

(1.3)

In Appendix B,
we justify
the restrictions (
1.3
) on the parameters of solitons
and calculated effective moment of inertia of solitons (
6.9
).

Remark 1.1
.

In the case
\rho(x)\equiv 0
we have
\ddot{q}(t)=\dot{\omega}(t)=0
and
the Maxwell equations do not depend on the motion of the particle, so the energy of the Maxwell field is conserved, and so are the particle momentum
and angular momentum.
The solitons read as
(E(x,t),B(x,t),q(t),p(t),\omega(t))=(0,0,vt+q_{0},mv,\omega)
with arbitrary
q_{0},v,\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and they are obviously orbitally stable
by the conservation laws; see Definition
6.5
.

Our main results are i)
the orbital stability of solitons
{\cal S}_{v,0}
with
|v|<1

and ii) the
linear
orbital stability
of solitons
{\cal S}_{0,\omega}
with
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

in the class of solutions with zero total momentum.
In particular, the total momentum is zero for solutions
with “symmetric” initial data:

q(0)=p(0)=0,\qquad E(-x,0)\equiv-E(x,0),\qquad B(-x,0)\equiv B(x,0).

(1.4)

Our approach essentially relies on the Hamilton–Poisson structure of the
system (
1.1
) which was constructed in
[
25
]
.
The structure is degenerate, so the system admits the Casimir invariants,
and the methods of
[
9
]
are not applicable to the system.

The proof of the orbital stability of solitons
{\cal S}_{v,0}
relies on the reduction to the comoving frame and lower bound
for the Hamiltonian as the Lyapunov function.
The Hamilton functional of the reduced system depends on the parameter
P
which is the conserved total momentum. Thus, we restrict the Hamiltonian to the states with the fixed value of
P
.

To study the stability of solitons
{\cal S}_{0,\omega}
, we employ conservation of
angular momentum
J
. The angular momentum is a functional on the phase space
of the reduced system only for the total momentum
P=0
. So, we
consider the stability in the class of solutions with
P=0
.
In particular,
all solutions with initial states (
1.4
) have zero total momentum.

The
linear orbital stability
of the
solitons refers to
the stability of the

linearized reduced dynamics

in the tangent space to the manifold of states with constant angular momentum.
The manifold is not well-defined, but the tangent space is;
see Section
9
.
So, the nonlinear stability problem on the manifold is not correct,
as opposed to
the linearized problem on the tangent space.

To prove the linear orbital stability,
we
construct the Lyapunov function
L

for the linearized equations
as the sum of the
quadratic part of the
Hamiltonian with the Casimir functional.
Such a strategy is an
analog of the energy–Casimir method

[
11
,
34
]
; see Remark
10.4
.
We prove the conservation of
L
in the linearized dynamics
and
establish a suitable lower bound for
L
.
A lower bound provides a priori estimate
and existence of global solutions, and at the same time
the stability of the linearized dynamics.
The bound is proved
by suitable spectral arguments including the Kato–Löwner–Heinz inequality from the theory
of interpolation
[
19
]
and the closed graph theorem.
Further comments on our methods and open questions can be found in
Appendix

.

Let us comment on related work.
The mathematical theory of Maxwell–Lorentz system (
1.1
) started after
Nodvick’s paper
[

]
, where the Hamilton least action principle
and conservation laws
have been established

The coordinate-free proof of the conservation laws
was given by Kiessling
[
20
]
.
The coordinate-free proof of the Hamilton principle
was given in

[
16
]
using the technique of
[
4
,
36
]
which relies on the
Lie algebras and the Poincaré equation.
This technique was developed
in
[
17
]
, where the general theory of invariants was constructed
for the Poincaré equations on manifolds and applied to the system (
1.1
).

In
[
5
,
13
,
15
,
27
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
, the stability of solitons was studied
for the Maxwell–Lorentz system (
1.1
)
without spinning
(the first three lines with
w(t)\equiv\dot{q}(t)
).
The results
[
13
,
30
]

provide the first rigorous proof of the
radiation damping

in classical electrodynamics.
For the survey, see
[
38
]
and
[
23
,
24
]
.
The adiabatic effective dynamics of solitons for the 3D system was proved
in
[
33
]
.

For the Maxwell–Lorentz system with spinning particle
at rest
(
q(t)\equiv 0
),
the global convergence to rotating solitons
was established in
[
14
]
and
[
32
]
.
In
[
14
]
,
the convergence is proved
in the case of sufficiently small charge density
\rho
.
In
[
32
]
, Kunze extended the result to
the same system without the smallness condition.
This condition is replaced by the assumption that
the mass of the particle does not belong to a discrete set of resonances.

The stability of solitons for the 3D Maxwell–Lorentz system (
1.1
) with moving and spinning particle was not considered previously.

The Maxwell potentials and the well-posedness

As is well-known
[
18
]
, the second line of the system (
1.1

the following expressions of the Maxwell fields in terms of the potentials

A(x,t)=(A_{1}(x,t),A_{2}(x,t),A_{3}(x,t))

in the Coulomb gauge,

Substituting the expressions (

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\ddot{A}(x,t)&\!\!=&\!\!\Delta A(x,t)+j(x,t)\\

I\dot{\omega}(t)&\!\!=&\!\!\langle(x\!-\!q(t)){\rm\wedge}[-\dot{A}(x,t)\rho(x\!-\!q(t))+j(x,t){\rm\wedge}{\rm curl{\hskip 1.42262pt}}A(x,t)]\rangle\end{array}\right|.

In the last two equations, the terms with

\displaystyle\!\!=\!\!

4
The canonical transformation to the comoving frame

Denote the fields in the comoving frame

{\bf A}(y,t):=A(q(t)+y,t),\qquad{\bm{\Pi}}(y,t):=\Pi(q(t)+y,t).

(4.1)

We are going to change the variables in the system (
3.3
)
via the map

T:(A,\Pi,q,p,\pi)\mapsto({\bf A},{\bm{\Pi}},q,P,\pi),\qquad P=p-\langle{\bm{\Pi}},\nabla_{*}{\bf A}\rangle.

(4.2)

Here
P
is the conserved total momentum (
2.9
).
Hence, we can define

{\bf H}({\bf A},{\bm{\Pi}},q,P,\pi):=H(A,\Pi,q,p,\pi),\qquad p=P+\langle{\bm{\Pi}},\nabla_{*}{\bf A}\rangle.

(4.3)

Lemma 4.1
.

The system
(
3.3
)
in the new variables is equivalent to
a similar system with the Hamiltonian
{\bf H}
:

\dot{\bf Y}(t)={\cal J}({\bf Y}(t))D{\bf H}({\bf Y}(t)),\qquad{\bf Y}(t)=({\bf A}(t),{\bm{\Pi}}(t),q(t),P(t),\pi(t)).

(4.4)

Proof.

First, the map (
4.2
)
is canonical, i.e., it leaves the canonical form unchanged:

\langle{\bm{\Pi}},\dot{\bf A}\rangle+P\cdot\dot{q}+\pi\cdot\omega=\langle\Pi,\dot{A}\rangle+p\cdot\dot{q}+\pi\cdot\omega.

(4.5)

Indeed,

\dot{\bf A}(y,t)=\dot{A}(q(t)+y,t)+\dot{q}(t)\cdot\nabla A(q(t)+y,t)
by (
4.1
).
Hence, the identity (
4.5
) reduces to

\langle\Pi(q+y),\dot{q}\cdot\nabla A(q+y)\rangle+P\cdot\dot{q}=p\cdot\dot{q},

which holds by (
4.2
).
Second,
the identities (
4.5
) and (
4.3
) imply that the Lagrangians
corresponding to the Hamiltonians
{\bf H}
and
H
are related via the map
T
:

{\bf l}({\bf A},q,\dot{\bf A},\dot{q},\omega)\equiv l(A,q,\dot{A},\dot{q},\omega).

Therefore, the Lagrangian actions also coincide:

\int_{a}^{b}{\bf l}({\bf A},q,\dot{\bf A},\dot{q},\omega)dt=\int_{a}^{b}l(A,q,\dot{A},\dot{q},\omega)dt.

Hence, the solutions of the systems (
3.3
) and (
4.4
)
also are related by this transformation since they are critical trajectories of the action.
∎

The Kato–Löwner–Heinz inequality

In this section we prove
the lower bound (

).
We will prove a stronger bound than (

\displaystyle{\cal Q}_{\rho}({\bm{\xi}})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int[|\beta(y)|^{2}+|\nabla\alpha(y)|^{2}]dy+\frac{\nu}{2}[\gamma-\langle y{\rm\wedge}\alpha,\rho(y)\rangle]^{2}-\frac{\nu_{\rm eff}}{2}\gamma^{2}

\displaystyle{\varkappa}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\int[|\beta(y)|^{2}+|\nabla\alpha(y)|^{2}]dy+\gamma^{2}\Big],\qquad{\bm{\xi}}=(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in{\mathbb{T}}_{\omega}.

{\mathbb{Z}}

.

.

Consider the restrictions of the quadratic forms

Let conditions (

For

of

The calculation of

.

For any

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nu\langle\nabla\hat{\rho}(k){\rm\wedge}\gamma,(k^{2}-\lambda)^{-1}\nabla\hat{\rho}(k){\rm\wedge}\gamma\rangle(\nu_{\rm eff}+\lambda)+(\delta-\lambda)\gamma^{2}

)

which

) with

.

Proof of Proposition

.

The nonnegativity (

By (

Appendix B
Density of
C_{0}^{\infty}
in the space of divergence-free vector fields

Here we prove that
C_{0}^{\infty}
is dense in the Hilbert space
{\cal F}^{0}\!:=\!\{A\!\in\!L^{2}\!:{\rm div{\hskip 1.42262pt}}A(x)\!\equiv\!0\}
. For any
A\in{\cal F}^{0}
,
its Fourier transform satisfies
\hat{A}(k)\bot k
for
k\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Hence,

\hat{A}(k)=k{\rm\wedge}\hat{a}(k)
with
\hat{a}(k)=-\frac{k{\rm\wedge}\hat{A}(k)}{k^{2}}
. For
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
let us denote

\hat{B}_{m}(k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\hat{A}(k),&|k|>\frac{1}{m}\\
0,&|k|<\frac{1}{m}\end{array}\right|,\qquad\hat{b}_{m}(k)=-\frac{k{\rm\wedge}\hat{B}_{m}(k)}{k^{2}}.

Then

\hat{B}_{m}(k)=k{\rm\wedge}\hat{b}_{m}(k)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\hat{A}(k),\qquad m\to\infty.

(B.1)

Note that
(1+|k|)\hat{b}_{m}(k)\in L^{2}
.
Hence,

b_{m}\in H^{1}
, so there exist
b_{m,n}\in C_{0}^{\infty}
such that
b_{m,n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle H^{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}b_{m}
as
n\to\infty
for every
m
.
Therefore,

A_{m,n}:={\rm curl{\hskip 1.42262pt}}b_{m,n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\rm curl{\hskip 1.42262pt}}b_{m},\quad n\to\infty\qquad{\rm and}\quad{\rm curl{\hskip 1.42262pt}}b_{m}=B_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}A,\quad m\to\infty.

Hence,
there exists a subsequence
A_{m,n(m)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}A
as
m\to\infty
. It remains to note that

A_{m,n}\in C_{0}^{\infty}
and
{\rm div{\hskip 1.42262pt}}A_{m,n}(x)\equiv 0
.
