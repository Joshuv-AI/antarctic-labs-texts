Linear Representations and Frobenius Morphisms of Groupoids
Juan Jesús BARBARÁN SÁNCHEZ † and Laiachi EL KAOUTIT ‡

Abstract
Given a morphism of (small) groupoids with injective object map, we provide sufficient and necessary conditions under which the induction and co-induction functors between the categories of linear representations are naturally isomorphic. A morphism with this property is termed a Frobenius morphism of groupoids . As a consequence, an extension by a subgroupoid is Frobenius if and only if each fibre of the (left or right) pull-back biset has finitely many orbits. Our results extend and clarify the classical Frobenius reciprocity formulae in the theory of finite groups, and characterize Frobenius extension of algebras with enough orthogonal idempotents.

Introduction

In this section, we first explain the motivations behind this research and we give a general overview of the theory developed here. Secondly, we introduce the notations and conventions that are needed in order to give a detailed description of the main results obtained in this paper and to make this introduction self-contained as much as possible.

1.1
Motivation and overview

So far, we have been dealing with situations where only finitely many objects were available. In other words, Frobenius unital algebras and groups (or finite bundles of these ones) are objects mainly built from categories with finitely many objects. Up to our knowledge, the general case of infinitely many objects is still unexplored in the literature. As an illustration, the Frobenius formulae for locally compact topological groupoids are far from being understood, since these formulae are not even explicitly computed for the case of abstract groupoids.

Our motivation is to introduce the main ideas that underpin techniques from the theory of linear representation of groupoids in relation with their non-unital algebras, which however admits enough orthogonal idempotents (see the subsequent subsection for the definition). Thus, this paper intends to set up, in a very elementary way, the basic tools to establish Frobenius formulae in the context of abstract groupoids and to employ these formulae to characterize Frobenius extensions of groupoids on the one hand and Frobenius extensions of their associated path algebras on the other, hoping in this way to fill in the lack that is present in the literature about this subject.

1.2
General notions and notations

We fix some conventions that will be held all along this paper. If
\mathcal{C}
is a small category (the class of objects is actually a set) and
{\mathcal{D}}
is any other category, then the symbol

F
,

and
F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}\colon\mathcal{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}\to{\mathcal{D}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}

the set of all arrows from

to

. Assume now that we have a functor

. Then by

to the composition
F_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}(f)p\in{\mathcal{D}}(d,F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}(t(f)))
(here

and

stand for the source and the target of a given arrow

). In this way, for each object

we have a functor
{\mathcal{D}}(d,F(-))
from
\mathcal{C}
to the category of sets. Similarly, for each object

from the category

Let

be a fixed base field and

\upphi
is a faithful functor and
\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}\colon{\mathcal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}\to{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
is an injective map. Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

\upphi
is a Frobenius extension;

(b)

For any
x\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
, the left
{\mathcal{H}}
-set
\vartheta^{-1}\big(\{x\}\big)
has finitely many orbits;

(c)

For any
x\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
, the right
{\mathcal{H}}
-set
\varsigma^{-1}\big(\{x\}\big)
has finitely many orbits.

In particular, any inclusion of finite groupoids is a Frobenius extension.

If we consider, in both Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2
, groupoids with only one object, then we recover the classical result on Frobenius extensions of group algebras, see Corollary
5.7
below for more details.

2
Abstract groupoids: General definition, basic properties

and examples

This section contains all the material: definitions, properties and examples of abstract groupoids that will be used in the course of the following sections. This material was recollected form
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
and from the references quoted therein. All groupoids discussed below are abstract and small ones, in the sense that the class of arrows is actually a set, and they do not admit any topological or combinatorial structures.

2.1
Notations, basic notions and examples

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a groupoid and consider an object
x\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
.
The isotropy group of
{\mathcal{G}}
at
x
, is the group:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}:={\mathcal{G}}(x,x)=\big\{g\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\,|\,s(g)=t(g)=x\big\}.

(2.1)

Clearly, for any two objects
x,y\in G_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
, we have that each of the sets
{\mathcal{G}}(x,y)
is, by the groupoid multiplication, a right
{\mathcal{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}
-set and left
{\mathcal{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{y}}
-set. Notice here that the multiplication of the groupoid is by convention defined as the map
{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}{}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{{s}}}}{\times}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{{t}}}}{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\to{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
sending
(f,g)
to
f\circ g:=fg
, so that
\mathcal{G}^{y}\times\mathcal{G}(x,y)
and
\mathcal{G}(x,y)\times\mathcal{G}^{x}
are both subsets of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}{}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{{s}}}}{\times}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{{t}}}}{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
. In fact, each of the
{\mathcal{G}}(x,y)
’s is a
({\mathcal{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{y}},{\mathcal{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{x}})
-biset, see
[
4
]
for pertinent definitions.

The
(
left
)
star
of an object
x\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
is defined by
{{\rm Star}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{l}}(x):=t^{-1}\big(\{x\}\big)=\{g\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\,|\,t(g)=x\}
. The right star is defined using the source map, and both left and right stars are in bijection. Now, given an arrow
g\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
, we define the
conjugation operation
(or the
adjoint operator
) as the morphism of groups:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{ad}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{g}}\colon\ {\mathcal{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{s(g)}}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{t(g)}},\qquad\big(f\longmapsto gfg^{-1}\big).

(2.2)

Let
\upphi\colon{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{G}}
be a morphism of groupoids. Obviously
\upphi
induces homomorphisms of groups between the isotropy groups:
\upphi^{\scriptscriptstyle{y}}\colon{\mathcal{H}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{y}}\to{\mathcal{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\upphi_{0}(y)}}
, for every
y\in H_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
. The family of homomorphisms
\{\upphi^{\scriptscriptstyle{y}}\}_{y\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}}
is referred to as
the isotropy maps of
\upphi
. For a fixed object
x\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
, its fibre
\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}^{-1}(\{x\})
, if not empty, leads to the following “star” of homomorphisms of groups:

G^{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}
H^{\scriptscriptstyle{y}}
H^{\scriptscriptstyle{y}}
H^{\scriptscriptstyle{y}}
H^{\scriptscriptstyle{y}}
H^{\scriptscriptstyle{y}}
H^{\scriptscriptstyle{y}}
H^{\scriptscriptstyle{y}}
H^{\scriptscriptstyle{y}}

where
y
runs in the fibre
\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}^{-1}(\{x\})
.

Example 2.1

(trivial groupoid)
.

Let
X
be any set. Then the pair
(X,X)
can be considered as a groupoid with a trivial structure. Thus, the only arrows are the identities. This groupoid is known as
a trivial groupoid
.

Example 2.2

(action groupoid)
.

Any group
G
can be considered as a groupoid by taking
G_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}=G
and
G_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}=\{*\}
(a set with one element). Now, if
X
is a right
G
-set with action
\rho\colon X\times G\to X
, then one can define the so called
the action groupoid
:
G_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}=X\times G
and
G_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}=X
, the source and the target are
s=\rho
and
t={\rm pr}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
, the identity map sends
x\mapsto(x,e)=\iota_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}
, where
e
is the identity element of
G
. The multiplication is given by
(x,g)(x^{\prime},g^{\prime})=(x,gg^{\prime})
, whenever
xg=x^{\prime}
, and the inverse is defined by
(x,g)^{-1}=\big(xg,g^{-1}\big)
. Clearly the pair of maps
({\rm pr}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},*)\colon{\mathcal{G}}:=(G_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},G_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}})\to(G,\{*\})
defines a morphism of groupoids. For a given
x\in X
, the isotropy group
{\mathcal{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}
is clearly identified with the stabilizer
\emph{Stab}_{\scriptscriptstyle{G}}(x)=\{g\in G\,|\,gx=x\}
subgroup of
G
.

Given
\sigma\colon X\to Y
a morphism of right
G
-sets, then the pair of maps
(X\times G,X)\to(Y\times G,Y)
sending
\big((x,g),x^{\prime}\big)\mapsto\big((\sigma(x),g),\sigma(x^{\prime})\big)
defines a morphism of action groupoids.

Example 2.3

(equivalence relation groupoid)
.

We expound here several examples which, in fact, belong to the same class, that of equivalence relation groupoids.

(1)

One can associate to a given set
X
the so called
the groupoid of pairs
(called
fine groupoid
in
[
5
]
and
simplicial groupoid
in
[
14
]
), its set of arrows is defined by
G_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}=X\times X
and the set of objects by
G_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}=X
; the source and the target are
s={\rm pr}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
and
t={\rm pr}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
, the second and the first projections, and the map of identity arrows
\iota
is the diagonal map. The multiplication and the inverse maps are given by

\displaystyle(x,x^{\prime})(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})=(x,x^{\prime\prime}),\qquad\text{and}\qquad(x,x^{\prime})^{-1}=(x^{\prime},x).

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be any map and consider the trivial groupoid
(X,X)
as in Example
2.1
together with the groupoid of pairs
(Y\times Y,Y)
. Then, the pair of maps

(F_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}})\colon(X,X)\to(Y\times Y,Y)
, where
F_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\colon X\to Y\times Y
,
x\mapsto(f(x),f(x))
and
F_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}=f
, establishes a morphism of groupoids.

(2)

Let
\upnu\colon X\to Y
be a map. Consider the fibre product
X{}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\upnu}}}{\times}_{{\;\scriptscriptstyle{\upnu}}}X
as a set of arrows of the groupoid
\textstyle{X{}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\upnu}}}{\times}_{{\;\scriptscriptstyle{\upnu}}}X\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle{{\rm pr}_{2}}}
\scriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle{{\rm pr}_{1}}}
\textstyle{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces X,}
\scriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle{\iota}}
where as before
s={\rm pr}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
and
t={\rm pr}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
, and the map of identities arrows is
\iota
the diagonal map. The multiplication and the inverse are the obvious ones.

(3)

Assume that
{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq X\times X
is an equivalence relation on the set
X
. One can construct a groupoid

\textstyle{{\mathcal{R}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle{{\rm pr}_{2}}}
\scriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle{{\rm pr}_{1}}}
\textstyle{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces X,}
\scriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle{\iota}}
with structure maps as before. This is an important class of groupoids known as
the groupoid of equivalence relation
(or
equivalence relation groupoid
). Obviously
({\mathcal{R}},X)\hookrightarrow(X\times X,X)
is a morphism of groupoid, see for instance
[
6
, Example 1.4, p. 301]
.

Notice that in all these examples each of the isotropy groups is the trivial group.

Example 2.4

(induced groupoid)
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},G_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}})
be a groupoid and
\varsigma\colon X\to G_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
a map. Consider the following pair of sets:

\displaystyle G^{\scriptscriptstyle{\varsigma}}{}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}:=X{}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\varsigma}}}{\times}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{t}}}G_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\;{}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{s}}}{\times}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\varsigma}}}X=\big\{(x,g,x^{\prime})\in X\times G_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\times X\,|\,\varsigma(x)=t(g),\varsigma(x^{\prime})=s(g)\big\},\quad G^{\scriptscriptstyle{\varsigma}}{}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}:=X.

Then
{\mathcal{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\varsigma}}{}=(G^{\scriptscriptstyle{\varsigma}}{}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},G^{\scriptscriptstyle{\varsigma}}{}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}})
is a groupoid, with structure maps:
s={\rm pr}_{\scriptscriptstyle{3}}
,
t={\rm pr}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
,
\iota_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}=(\varsigma(x),\iota_{\scriptscriptstyle{\varsigma(x)}},\varsigma(x))
,
x\in X
. The multiplication is defined by
(x,g,y)(x^{\prime},g^{\prime},y^{\prime})=(x,gg^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, whenever
y=x^{\prime}
, and the inverse is given by
(x,g,y)^{-1}=\big(y,g^{-1},x\big)
. The groupoid
{\mathcal{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\varsigma}}
is known as
the induced groupoid of
{\mathcal{G}}
by the map
\varsigma
, (or
the
pull-back groupoid of
{\mathcal{G}}
along
\varsigma
, see
[
14
]
for dual notion). Clearly, there is a canonical morphism
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle{\varsigma}}:=({\rm pr}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},\varsigma)\colon{\mathcal{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\varsigma}}\to{\mathcal{G}}
of groupoids. A particular instance of an induced groupoid, is the one when
{\mathcal{G}}=G
is a groupoid with one object. Thus, for any group
G
, one can consider the Cartesian product
X\times G\times X
as a set of arrows of a groupoid with set of objects
X
.

Example 2.5

(frame groupoid)
.

Let
\pi\colon{\mathcal{Y}}\to X
be a surjective map, and write
{\mathcal{Y}}=\biguplus_{\scriptscriptstyle{x\in X}}{\mathcal{Y}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}
, where
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}:=\pi^{-1}(\{x\})
(the fibres of
\pi
at
x
). For any pair of elements
x,x^{\prime}\in X
, we set

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}(x,x^{\prime}):=\big\{f\colon{\mathcal{Y}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{x^{\prime}}}\,|\,f\text{ is a bijective map}\big\},

then the pair
({\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}):=\big(\biguplus_{\scriptscriptstyle{x,x^{\prime}\in X}}{\mathcal{G}}(x,x^{\prime}),X\big)
admits a structure of groupoid (possibly a trivial one), referred to as
the frame groupoid of
({\mathcal{Y}},\pi)
and denoted by
{\rm Iso}({\mathcal{Y}},\pi)
, see also
[
29
]
.

In a more general setting, one can similarly define the frame groupoid of a given family
\{{\mathcal{Y}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}\}_{x\in X}
of objects in a certain category, indexed by a set
X
. For instance, we could take each of the
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}
’s as an abelian group (resp.
\Bbbk
-vector space), in this case, the set of arrows
{\mathcal{G}}(x,x^{\prime})
should be the set of all abelian group isomorphisms (resp.
\Bbbk
-linear isomorphisms).

Example 2.6

(isotropy groupoid)
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a groupoid, then the disjoint union
{\biguplus}_{\scriptscriptstyle{x\in G_{0}}}{\mathcal{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}
of all its isotropy groups form the set of arrows of a subgroupoid of
{\mathcal{G}}
whose source is equal to its target, namely the projection
\varsigma\colon{\biguplus}_{\scriptscriptstyle{x\in G_{0}}}{\mathcal{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}\to G_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
. We denote this groupoid by
{\mathcal{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}
and refer to it as
the isotropy groupoid of
{\mathcal{G}}
. For instance, the isotropy groupoid of any equivalence relation groupoid is a trivial one as in Example
2.1
.

2.2
Groupoids actions and equivariant maps

The subsequent definition is, in fact, an abstract formulation of that given in
[
23
, Definition 1.6.1]
for Lie groupoids, and essentially the same definition based on the Sets-bundles notion given in
[
29
, Definition 1.11]
.

Definition 2.7
.

Given a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
and a map
\varsigma\colon X\to{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
. We say that
(X,\varsigma)
is a
right

{\mathcal{G}}
-
set
(with a
structure map

\varsigma
), if there is a map (
the action
)
\rho\colon X{}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\varsigma}}}{\times}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{{t}}}}{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\to X
sending
(x,g)\mapsto xg
, satisfying the following conditions:

1)

s(g)=\varsigma(xg)
, for any
x\in X
and
g\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
with
\varsigma(x)=t(g)
,

2)

x\iota_{\varsigma(x)}=x
, for every
x\in X
,

3)

(xg)h=x(gh)
, for every
x\in X
,
g,h\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
with
\varsigma(x)={t}(g)
and
t(h)=s(g)
.

A
left action
is analogously defined by interchanging the source with the target. In general, a set with a (right or left) groupoid action is called
a groupoid-set
.

Remark 2.8
.

If we think of group as a groupoid with a single object, then Definition
2.7
leads to the definition of the usual action of a group on a set (see
[
4
]
). From a categorical point of view, this action is nothing but a functor from the underlying category of such a groupoid to the core category of sets
5
5

5

The core category of a given category is the subcategory whose arrows are all the isomorphisms.
. Writing down this formulation for groupoids with several objects, will leads to the Definition
2.7
. Specifically, following
[
10
, Remark 2.6]
, for any groupoid
{\mathcal{G}}
, there is a (symmetric monoidal) equivalence between the category of right
{\mathcal{G}}
-sets and the category of functors from
{\mathcal{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm op}}
to the core category of sets. An analogue equivalence of categories holds true for left
{\mathcal{G}}
-sets. Following the same reasons that were explained in
[
10
, Remark 2.6, Section 5.3]
, in this paper we will work with Definition
2.7
instead of the aforementioned functorial approach.

Obviously, any groupoid
{\mathcal{G}}
acts over itself on both sides by using the regular action, i.e., the multiplication
{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}{}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{{s}}}}{\times}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{{t}}}}{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\to{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
. That is,
({\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},{s})
is a right
{\mathcal{G}}
-set and
({\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},{t})
is a left
{\mathcal{G}}
-set with this action. On the other hand, the pair
({\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}},{\rm id})
admits a structure of right
{\mathcal{G}}
-set, as well as a structure of a left
{\mathcal{G}}
-set. For instance, the right action is given by the map
{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}{}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{{\rm id}}}}{\times}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{{t}}}}{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\to{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
sending
(x,g)\mapsto x.g=s(g)
.

morphism of right
{\mathcal{G}}
-sets

A trivial example of right groupoid-set is a right group-set. Specifically, if we consider a group as a groupoid with only one object, then its category of group-sets coincides with its category of groupoid-sets. 2.3
Translation groupoids and the orbits sets

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a groupoid and
(X,\varsigma)
a right
{\mathcal{G}}
-set. Consider the pair of sets
\big(X{}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\varsigma}}}{\times}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{{t}}}}{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},X\big)
as a groupoid with structure maps
{s}=\rho
,
t={\rm pr}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
,
\iota_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}=(x,\iota_{\varsigma(x)})
. The multiplication and the inverse maps are defined by
(x,g)(x^{\prime},g^{\prime})=(x,gg^{\prime})
and
(x,g)^{-1}=\big(xg,g^{-1}\big)
. This groupoid is denoted by
X\rtimes{\mathcal{G}}
and it is known in the literature as the
right translation groupoid of
X
by
{\mathcal{G}}
(or
semi-direct product groupoid
, see for instance
[
26
, p. 163]
and
[
9
]
). Furthermore, there is a canonical morphism of groupoids
\upsigma\colon X\rtimes{\mathcal{G}}\to{\mathcal{G}}
, given by the pair of maps
\upsigma=({\rm pr}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},\varsigma)
. Clearly any
{\mathcal{G}}
-equivariant map
F\colon(X,\varsigma)\to(X^{\prime},\varsigma^{\prime})
, induces a morphism
\mathsf{F}\colon X\rtimes{\mathcal{G}}\to X^{\prime}\rtimes{\mathcal{G}}
of groupoids, whose arrows map is given by
X{}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\varsigma}}}{\times}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{{t}}}}{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\to X^{\prime}{}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\varsigma^{\prime}}}}{\times}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{{t}}}}{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
,
(x,g)\mapsto(F(x),g)
, and its objects map is
F\colon X\to X^{\prime}
.

Example 2.10
.

Any groupoid
{\mathcal{G}}
can be seen as a (right) translation groupoid of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
along
{\mathcal{G}}
itself. Thus, the right translation groupoid of the right
{\mathcal{G}}
-set
({\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}},id)
coincides (up to a canonical iso) with
{\mathcal{G}}
itself. Now, let
G
be a group and
X
a right
G
-set. Then the attached action groupoid, as described in Example
2.2
, is precisely the right translation groupoid of
X
along
G
, where
G
is considered as a groupoid with one object.

Next we recall the notion of the orbit set attached to a right groupoid-set. This notion is a generalization of the orbit set in the context of group-sets. Here we use the (right) translation groupoid to introduce this set.
First we recall the notion of the orbit set of a given groupoid.
The orbit set of a groupoid

{\mathcal{G}}
is the quotient set of
G_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
by the following equivalence relation: Two objects
x,x^{\prime}\in G_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
are said to be equivalent if and only if there is an arrow connecting them, that is, there is
g\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
such that
t(g)=x
and
s(g)=x^{\prime}
. Viewing
x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
as elements in the right (or left)
{\mathcal{G}}
-set
({\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}},{\rm id})
, then this means that
x
and
x^{\prime}
are equivalent if and only if, there exists
g\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
such that
x.g=x^{\prime}
. The quotient set of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
by this equivalence relation, is nothing but the set of all connected components of
{\mathcal{G}}
, which we denote by
\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}({\mathcal{G}}):={\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}/{\mathcal{G}}
.

Given a right
{\mathcal{G}}
-set
(X,\varsigma)
, the
orbit set

X/{\mathcal{G}}
of
(X,\varsigma)
is the orbit set of the (right) translation groupoid
X\rtimes{\mathcal{G}}
, that is,
X/{\mathcal{G}}=\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}(X\rtimes{\mathcal{G}})
. If
{\mathcal{G}}=(X\times G,X)
is an action groupoid as in Example
2.2
, then obviously the orbit set of this groupoid coincides with the classical set of orbits
X/G
, see also Example
2.10
. Of course, the orbit set of an equivalence relation groupoid
({\mathcal{R}},X)
, see Example
2.3
, is precisely the quotient set
X/{\mathcal{R}}
modulo the equivalence relation
{\mathcal{R}}
.

Remark 2.11
.

In this remark we exhibit the connection between a given groupoid and its attached equivalence relation groupoid. So, let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a groupoid and consider the pair of maps
\big((t,s),{\rm id}\big)\colon({\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}})\to({\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}\times{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}},{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}})
, where the first component sends

. The pair
\big((t,s),{\rm id}\big)
establishes a morphism of groupoids from
{\mathcal{G}}
to the groupoid of pairs
({\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}\times{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}},{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}})
. Now, denotes by
{\mathcal{R}}
the equivalence relation defined as above by the action of
{\mathcal{G}}
on
{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
, that is, for a given pair of objects
x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}

x\sim_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\mathcal{R}}}}x^{\prime}

g\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}

s(g)=x

({\mathcal{R}},{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}})

3
Linear representations of groupoid. Revisited

We provide in this section the construction and the basic properties of the induction, restriction and co-induction functors attached to a morphism of groupoids, and connect the categories of linear representations. The material presented here is probably well known to specialists, with the exception perhaps the result dealing with the characterization of linear representations of quotient groupoid that has its own interest. 4
Frobenius reciprocity formulae

In this section we prove the left and the right Frobenius reciprocity formulae. This mainly establishes, from one side an adjunction between the restriction and induction functors, and from another one an adjunction between the restriction and co-induction functors. Form a categorical point of view, this amounts to the notions of left and right Kan extensions. Here we follow an elementary and direct exposition, taking advantage of groupoids structure, without appealing to any heavy categorical notions.

5
Frobenius extensions in groupoids context

The main aim of this section is to characterize Frobenius morphism of groupoids, see Definition
5.1
below. This in fact is a kind of an universal definition which can be applied to any functor with left and right adjoints functors. Typical examples are Frobenius algebras over a given field (or commutative ring), where the forgetful functor form the category of modules to vector spaces has isomorphic left and right adjoint functors, namely, the tensor and the homs functors (see
[
17
]
). Our main result can be seen also as an approach to Frobenius extensions of algebras with enough orthogonal idempotents.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\upphi\colon{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{G}}
be a morphism of groupoids. We say that
\upphi
is a
Frobenius morphism
provided that the induction and the co-induction functors
\upphi^{*}
and
{}^{*}\upphi
are naturally isomorphic.

From now on, we will freely use the notations and the notions expounded in Section
1.2
. So let us consider a morphism
\upphi\colon{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{G}}
of groupoids, and denote by
\phi\colon A\to B
the associated morphism of
\Bbbk
-vector space defined in equation (
1.1
). If we assume that
\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}\colon{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}\to{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
is injective, then
\phi
becomes a morphism of rings with enough orthogonal idempotents. The following counterexample shows that
\phi
could not be multiplicative, without assuming
\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
injective:

Example 5.2
.

Let

be a non injective map, by choosing two distinct elements

whose images are equal
f(x)=f(x^{\prime})
. Take
{\mathcal{H}}:=(X,X)
to be a trivial groupoid as in Example
2.1
, and
{\mathcal{G}}=(Y\times Y,Y)
to be a groupoid of pairs as in Example
2.3
(1). It is by definition that, for any two objects

,
y^{\prime}
in
{\mathcal{G}}
, there is only one arrow from
y
to
y^{\prime}
, namely, the one defined by the pair

. We denote by

the image of this arrow in the ring

, so that we have
\boldsymbol{1}_{\scriptscriptstyle{(z,z)}}=1_{\scriptscriptstyle{z}}
, for any

. In this situation, the associated rings with enough orthogonal idempotents are the direct sums of the form
A=\Bbbk^{(X)}
and
B=\Bbbk^{(Y\times Y)}
, respectively. Consider the functor
\upphi\colon{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{G}}
whose arrows map is
\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\colon X\to Y\times Y
, which sends
\iota_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}\mapsto\iota_{\scriptscriptstyle{(f(x),f(x))}}
, and its objects map is given by
\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}=f
. Therefore, the
\Bbbk
-linear map

attached to

, for any

. Now coming back to the chosen elements

, we know that

\displaystyle\phi(1_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}.1_{\scriptscriptstyle{x^{\prime}}})=\phi(0)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\phi(1_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}).\phi(1_{\scriptscriptstyle{x^{\prime}}})=1_{\scriptscriptstyle{f(x)}}.1_{\scriptscriptstyle{f(x^{\prime})}}=1_{\scriptscriptstyle{f(x)}}\neq 0,

which show that
\phi(1_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}.1_{\scriptscriptstyle{x^{\prime}}})\neq\phi(1_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}).\phi(1_{\scriptscriptstyle{x^{\prime}}})

By scalar restriction,

Lemma 5.4

For any

The notion of a
finite groupoid
is vague, in the sense that there are several interrelated notions and all generalize that of a finite group. Next we adopt the following one: a groupoid

is said to be

, provided that

is a finite set as well as each of its isotropy groups
{\mathcal{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}

. This is the case when for instance
{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
is a finite set. Given a morphism of groupoids

, we shall implicitly use the notations of Examples
2.9

The following corollary characterizes Frobenius extension by subgroupoids, in terms of finiteness of the orbits of the fibres of the pull-back biset, which in particular applies to the case of

.

Let
\upphi\colon{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{G}}
be a morphism of groupoids such that
\upphi
is a faithful functor and
\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}\colon{\mathcal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}\to{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}

(

-set

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1,\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}(u)}}=\begin{cases}h1_{\scriptscriptstyle{\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}(u)}},&(v,p)=h.(u,\iota_{\scriptscriptstyle{\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}(u)}}),\\
0,&\text{ if }(v,p)\notin{{\rm Orb}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{H}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{l}}\big(u,\iota_{\scriptscriptstyle{\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}(u)}}\big).\end{cases}

Since
\upphi
is a faithful functor, this

is uniquely determined from the pair
(v,p)
. Furthermore, for any arrow
\alpha\in{\mathcal{H}}(u^{\prime},u)
, we have that
\mathfrak{p}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1,\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}(u^{\prime})}}(p\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}(\alpha))=\mathfrak{p}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1,\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}(u)}}(p)\alpha
, as we know that
{}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\upphi}}\mathscr{U}({\mathcal{G}}):={\mathcal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}{}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\upphi_{0}}}}{\times}_{{\scriptscriptstyle{t}}}{\mathcal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
is an
({\mathcal{H}},{\mathcal{G}})

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle{(u,v)}}\colon\ {\mathcal{G}}(\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}(u),\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}(v))\longrightarrow\Bbbk{\mathcal{H}}(u,v),\qquad\big(p\longmapsto\mathfrak{p}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1,\upphi_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}(u)}}(p)\big).

On the other hand, if we take an element

, for some

and

as en element in the homogeneous component

\Bbbk{\mathcal{G}}(x,\phi(u))

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
