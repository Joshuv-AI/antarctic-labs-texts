Small-scale dynamo with nonzero correlation time Sympy ( Meurer et al. 2017 ) .
Kishore Gopalakrishnan, Nishant K Singh

Abstract
The small-scale dynamo is typically studied by assuming that the correlation time of the velocity field is zero. Some authors have used a smooth renovating flow model to study how the properties of the dynamo are affected by the correlation time being nonzero. Here, we assume the velocity is an incompressible Gaussian random field (which need not be smooth), and derive the lowest-order corrections to the evolution equation for the two-point correlation of the magnetic field in Fourier space. Using this, we obtain the evolution equation for the longitudinal correlation function of the magnetic field ( M L M_{L} ) in nonhelical turbulence, valid for arbitrary Prandtl number. The non-resistive terms of this equation do not contain spatial derivatives of M L M_{L} of order greater than two. We further simplify this equation in the limit of high Prandtl number, and find that the growth rate of the magnetic energy is much smaller than previously reported. Nevertheless, the magnetic power spectrum still retains the Kazantsev form at high Prandtl number.



I
Introduction

Magnetic fields are ubiquitous in astrophysics, being found in stars, planets, galaxies, and even galaxy clusters
(for a review, see
Brandenburg &
Subramanian 2005a
, section 2)
.
Dynamo theory attempts to explain the generation and sustenance of such magnetic fields
(
Moffatt 1978
;
Krause & Rädler 1980
;
Brandenburg &
Subramanian 2005a
;
Shukurov & Subramanian 2022
)
.
Typically, magnetic fields ordered on the system scale are explained by appealing to mean-field dynamo theory, which suggests that fluid motions correlated at some scale can generate magnetic fields ordered on much larger scales (the ‘large-scale dynamo’ or LSD).
However, it is well known that in a turbulent fluid, intermittent magnetic fields can be generated which are typically ordered on scales comparable to or smaller than that of the velocity field (the ‘small-scale dynamo’ or SSD)
(
Kazantsev 1968
;
Molchanov et al. 1985
)
.
The SSD grows on a timescale comparable to the eddy turnover time; this is much smaller than the timescale for growth of the LSD and the typical ages of astrophysical objects.

Kulsrud & Anderson 1992
argue that the presence of SSD-generated magnetic fields invalidates the usual treatment of the LSD.
While their conclusion has since been challenged
(
Subramanian 1998
, e.g.)
, magnetic
fields generated by the SSD are still expected to affect the evolution of any object that contains a sufficiently turbulent plasma (i.e. where the magnetic Reynolds number,
\operatorname{Rm}
, is above some critical value).

In general, the Lorentz force turns the evolution of the magnetic field into a nonlinear problem, which is difficult to study analytically.
As a first step, one can study the kinematic limit, where the magnetic field is assumed to be so weak that the effect of the Lorentz force on the velocity field can be neglected.
The statistical properties of the velocity field can then be treated as given quantities, and we are interested in the statistical properties of the magnetic field.

Since the evolution equation for the moment of a particular order of the magnetic field involves mixed higher-order moments of the velocity and magnetic fields, one ends up with a hierarchy of coupled evolution equations for the moments.
One needs to make additional assumptions in order to truncate this hierarchy (closure).

The standard treatment
(
Kazantsev 1968
;
Vainshtein & Kichatinov 1986
;
Schekochihin et al. 2002
)
is to model the velocity field as a Gaussian random field, such that all its higher moments can be expressed in terms of the first two moments.

II
Derivation of the evolution equation in Fourier space

II.1
The induction equation

Using
\boldsymbol{h}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
to denote the magnetic field and
\boldsymbol{w}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
to denote the velocity field, the induction equation is

\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{h}}{\partial t}=\nabla\!\times\!\left(\boldsymbol{w}\times\boldsymbol{h}\right)+\eta\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{h}

(1)

where
\eta
is the magnetic diffusivity, and boldface denotes a vectorial quantity.

Using a tilde to denote the Fourier transform such that

\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{k},t)\equiv\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}f(\boldsymbol{x},t)

(2)

and defining

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{ijk}^{(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})}\equiv{}&-i\delta_{ij}p_{k}+i\delta_{ik}q_{j}\end{split}

(3)

we write the induction equation as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial\widetilde{h}_{i}^{(\boldsymbol{k},t)}}{\partial t}={}&-\eta k^{2}\widetilde{h}_{i}^{(\boldsymbol{k},t)}+\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{\delta}^{(\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{q})}\mathcal{A}_{ijk}^{(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})}\,\widetilde{w}_{j}^{(\boldsymbol{p},t)}\,\widetilde{h}_{k}^{(\boldsymbol{q},t)}\end{split}

(4)

where we have assumed the velocity field is incompressible.
Above, and in what follows, we use parenthesized superscripts to denote arguments.
Further, we use the following condensed notation for integrals:
\int_{t^{\prime},\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}}\dots\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{p}\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{q}\dots
.

We use
\left<\Box\right>
to denote the average of a quantity
\Box
.
We assume that the double-correlation of the velocity field is homogeneous and separable, i.e. that it can be written as

\left<\widetilde{w}_{i}^{(\boldsymbol{k},t)}\,\widetilde{w}_{j}^{(\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}},t^{\prime})}\right>=T_{ij}^{(\boldsymbol{k})}\operatorname{\delta}^{(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}})}\mathfrak{D}^{(t-t^{\prime})}\,,\quad 2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{D}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=1\,,\quad 2\int_{0}^{\infty}t\,\mathfrak{D}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\equiv\tau_{c}

(5)

where
\tau_{c}
is the correlation time of the velocity field, and
\mathfrak{D}(\tau)
is its temporal correlation function.

II.2
Evolution equation as a series in
\tau_{c}

Defining

\mathcal{B}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{k},t;\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}},t^{\prime})\equiv\widetilde{h}_{i}{(\boldsymbol{k},t)}\,\widetilde{h}_{j}{(\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}},t^{\prime})}

(6)

we use equation
4
to write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial\mathcal{B}_{ij}^{(\boldsymbol{k},t;\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}},t)}}{\partial t}={}&-\eta\left|\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}}\right|^{2}\mathcal{B}_{ij}^{(\boldsymbol{k},t;\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}},t)}+\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{\delta}^{(\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{q})}\mathcal{A}_{jrs}^{(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})}\,\widetilde{w}_{r}^{(\boldsymbol{p},t)}\,\mathcal{B}_{is}^{(\boldsymbol{k},t;\boldsymbol{q},t)}+\left[i\leftrightarrow j;\,\boldsymbol{k}\leftrightarrow\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}}\right]\end{split}

(7)

where we have used ‘
\left[i\leftrightarrow j;\,\boldsymbol{k}\leftrightarrow\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}}\right]
’ at the end of the RHS to denote that all the preceding terms should be repeated under the indicated simultaneous relabelling.

We would like to obtain an evolution equation for
\left<\mathcal{B}_{ij}\right>
.
Averaging equation
7
gives us

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial\left<\mathcal{B}_{ij}^{(\boldsymbol{k},t;\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}},t)}\right>}{\partial t}={}&-\eta\left|\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}}\right|^{2}\left<\mathcal{B}_{ij}^{(\boldsymbol{k},t;\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}},t)}\right>+\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{\delta}^{(\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{q})}\mathcal{A}_{jrs}^{(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})}\left<\widetilde{w}_{r}^{(\boldsymbol{p},t)}\,\mathcal{B}_{is}^{(\boldsymbol{k},t;\boldsymbol{q},t)}\right>+\left[i\leftrightarrow j;\,\boldsymbol{k}\leftrightarrow\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}}\right]\end{split}

(8)

The evolution equation for
\left<\mathcal{B}\right>
thus depends on correlations of the form
\left<\widetilde{w}\mathcal{B}\right>
.
Similarly, the evolution equation for
\left<\widetilde{w}_{i_{1}}\dots\widetilde{w}_{i_{n}}\mathcal{B}\right>
would depend on correlations of the form
\left<\widetilde{w}_{i_{1}}\dots\widetilde{w}_{i_{n}}\widetilde{w}_{i_{n+1}}\mathcal{B}\right>
, where we have used the shorthand
\widetilde{w}_{i_{\alpha}}\equiv\widetilde{w}_{i_{\alpha}}{(\boldsymbol{k}^{(\alpha)},t^{(\alpha)})}
.
Truncating this hierarchy requires additional assumptions (these constitute what is usually referred to as a
closure
).

If we assume
\widetilde{w}
is a Gaussian random field, and note that
\mathcal{B}
is a functional of this Gaussian random field (since

at a particular time can depend on
\widetilde{w}

A

Assuming

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial\left<\mathcal{B}_{ij}^{(\boldsymbol{k},t;\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}},t)}\right>}{\partial t}={}&-\eta\left|\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}}\right|^{2}\left<\mathcal{B}_{ij}^{(\boldsymbol{k},t;\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}},t)}\right>+\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{k}^{(1)},t^{(1)}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{\delta}^{(\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{q})}\mathcal{A}_{jrs}^{(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})}\left<\widetilde{w}_{r}^{(\boldsymbol{p},t)}\,\widetilde{w}_{i_{1}}\right>\left<\frac{\delta\mathcal{B}_{is}^{(\boldsymbol{k},t;\boldsymbol{q},t)}}{\delta\widetilde{w}_{i_{1}}}\right>+\left[i\leftrightarrow j;\,\boldsymbol{k}\leftrightarrow\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}}\right]\end{split}

Our task is now to find an expression for
\left<\delta\mathcal{B}/\delta\widetilde{w}\right>
.

To evaluate the
n
-th functional derivative of
\mathcal{B}
, we integrate equation
7

n

For notational convenience, we define

\displaystyle\begin{split}A_{ijk}^{(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}};\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q};t,t^{\prime})}\equiv{}&e^{-\eta\left(k^{2}+k^{\prime 2}\right)\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)}\operatorname{\text{$\Theta$}}^{(t-t^{\prime})}\operatorname{\delta}^{(\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{q})}\mathcal{A}_{ijk}^{(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})}\end{split}

(10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Upsilon_{s;ij;mn}^{(\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}};\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}};\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q};t,t^{\prime})}\equiv{}&\delta_{is}\operatorname{\delta}^{(\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}})}A_{jmn}^{(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}};\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q};t,t^{\prime})}+\delta_{js}\operatorname{\delta}^{(\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}})}A_{imn}^{(\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{k};\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q};t,t^{\prime})}\end{split}

(11)

Recalling that
\left<\boldsymbol{w}\right>=\boldsymbol{0}
, the
n
-th functional derivative is given by the following recursion relation:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left<\frac{\delta^{n}\mathcal{B}_{ij}^{(\boldsymbol{k},t;\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}},t)}}{\delta\widetilde{w}_{i_{1}}\dots\delta\widetilde{w}_{i_{n}}}\right>={}&\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}t^{\prime},\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}},\\

\boldsymbol{k}^{(n+1)}\end{subarray}}\Upsilon_{s;ij;mn}^{(\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}};\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}};\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q};t,t^{\prime})}\left<\widetilde{w}_{m}^{(\boldsymbol{p},t^{\prime})}\widetilde{w}_{i_{n+1}}\right>\left<\frac{\delta^{n+1}\mathcal{B}_{sn}^{(\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}},t^{\prime};\boldsymbol{q},t^{\prime})}}{\delta\widetilde{w}_{i_{1}}\dots\delta\widetilde{w}_{i_{n+1}}}\right>\\
&+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\int_{\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}}}\Upsilon_{s;ij;i_{\alpha}n}^{(\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}};\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k^{\prime}};\boldsymbol{k}^{(\alpha)},\boldsymbol{q};t,t^{(\alpha)})}\left<\frac{\delta^{n-1}\mathcal{B}_{sn}^{(\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}},t^{(\alpha)};\boldsymbol{q},t^{(\alpha)})}}{\delta\widetilde{w}_{i_{1}}\dots\delta\widetilde{w}_{i_{\alpha-1}}\delta\widetilde{w}_{i_{\alpha+1}}\dots\delta\widetilde{w}_{i_{n}}}\right>\end{split}

(12)

Note that equation
12
relates the functional derivative of a particular order to other functional derivatives of both higher and lower orders.
Repeated use of equation
12
to eliminate all the functional derivatives in equation

Let a particular term in this series contain
m

n

\left<\widetilde{w}\widetilde{w}\right>

\left<\mathcal{B}\right>
.
This term is
\mathcal{O}(\tau_{c}^{m-n})

The infinite series we obtain is thus a series in
\tau_{c}
.
Note that to obtain all the terms at a particular order in
\tau_{c}
in this series, one must also use the fact that

\int^{t}_{-\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,f^{(\tau)}\,\mathfrak{D}^{(t-\tau)}=f^{(t)}\int^{t}_{-\infty}\mathfrak{D}^{(t-\tau)}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\frac{\mathrm{d}f^{(t)}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int^{t}_{-\infty}\left(t-\tau\right)\mathfrak{D}^{(t-\tau)}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\mathcal{O}(\tau_{c}^{2})=\frac{1}{2}\,f^{(t)}+\frac{\tau_{c}}{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}f^{(t)}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\mathcal{O}(\tau_{c}^{2})

(13)

where, for brevity, we have used

to denote the equal-time second-order correlation of the magnetic field.

V
Conclusions

By assuming that the velocity field is an incompressible separable Gaussian random field, we have derived the Fourier-space evolution equation for the two-point correlation function of the magnetic field.
Using this equation and further assuming that the velocity field is nonhelical, we have derived the evolution equation for the longitudinal correlation function of the magnetic field (
M_{L}
) in configuration space, valid for arbitrary
\operatorname{Pr}_{\mathrm{m}}
and
\operatorname{Rm}
(equation
28
).
Unlike in previous work, setting
\eta=0
gives an evolution equation with at most two spatial derivatives of
M_{L}
.

By choosing an appropriate form for the longitudinal correlation function of the velocity field, we have studied the
\operatorname{Pr}_{\mathrm{m}}\gg 1
limit.
In agreement with previous work
(
Chandran 1997
;
Mason et al. 2011
;
Bhat & Subramanian 2014
;
Carteret et al. 2023
;
Schekochihin & Kulsrud 2001
)
, we have found that the growth rate of the magnetic field decreases when the correlation time is nonzero.
The growth rate is suppressed much more strongly than in the renovating flow model
(
Bhat & Subramanian 2014
;
Carteret et al. 2023
)
.
However, the corrections to the spectral slope of the magnetic field are still negligible when
\operatorname{Rm}\gg 1
.

While our equation
28
can also be used to study the limit
\operatorname{Pr}_{\mathrm{m}}\ll 1

(which has been studied in the white-noise case by, e.g.
Vincenzi 2002
;
Arponen & Horvai 2007
;
Schober et al. 2012
; and using a renovating model by
Kleeorin & Rogachevskii 2012
),
this seems to be more complicated than the case presented here, and will be described elsewhere.
Further,
it may be interesting to study how the effects of kinetic helicity on the small-scale dynamo
(
Malyshkin & Boldyrev 2007
;
Malyshkin & Boldyrev 2010
)
are affected by the correlation time being nonzero.

Appendix A
Furutsu-Novikov theorem

Given a functional
R[f]
of a function
f
, its functional derivative is defined as
21
21

21

Some authors
(
Novikov 1965
, e.g.)
denote the functional derivative by
\delta R/(\delta f\mathrm{d}s)
, in order to make its dimensions explicit.

\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}R[f+\epsilon\chi]}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon^{n}}\right|_{\epsilon=0}=\int\frac{\delta^{n}R}{\delta f(s_{1})\dots\delta f(s_{n})}\,\chi(s_{1})\dots\chi(s_{n})\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}s_{n}

(A1)

where
\epsilon
is a real number, and
\chi(t)
is an arbitrary test function.
If

is a zero-mean Gaussian random function,

satisfies the Furutsu-Novikov formula
(
Furutsu 1963
;
Novikov 1965
)
:

\left<f(s)R[f]\right>=\int\left<f(s)f(s_{1})\right>\left<\frac{\delta R[f]}{\delta f(s_{1})}\right>\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}

(A2)

Equation
A2
also holds if
s
is a collection of spatio-temporal variables and vector indices.
