Boltzmann-Gibbs Random Fields with Mesh-free Precision Operators Based on Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics
Dionissios T. Hristopulos

Abstract
Boltzmann-Gibbs random fields are defined in terms of the exponential expression exp ⁡ ( − ℋ ) \exp\left(-{\mathcal{H}}\right) , where ℋ {\mathcal{H}} is a suitably defined energy functional of the field states x ⁡ ( 𝐬 ) x(\mathbf{s}) . This paper presents a new Boltzmann-Gibbs model which features local interactions in the energy functional. The interactions are embodied in a spatial coupling function which uses smoothed kernel-function approximations of spatial derivatives inspired from the theory of smoothed particle hydrodynamics. A specific model for the interactions based on a second-degree polynomial of the Laplace operator is studied. An explicit, mesh-free expression of the spatial coupling function (precision function) is derived for the case of the squared exponential (Gaussian) smoothing kernel. This coupling function allows the model to seamlessly extend from discrete data vectors to continuum fields. Connections with Gaussian Markov random fields and the Matérn field with ν = 1 \nu=1 are established.

Introduction

The particle-wave duality is a central concept of quantum mechanics. It refers to the fact that physical entities can be described as either waves (continuum functions) or particles (quanta), and each viewpoint has advantages depending on the particular phenomenon studied. For example, in studies of light propagation it is more suitable to view light as an electromagnetic wave; in contrast, in the photoelectric effect, light is best modeled as discrete particles (photons). There is a similar duality in probability and statistics: Random fields are defined over continuum support spaces, extending throughout an entire spatial domain, like waves. On the other hand, data-driven applications focus on discretely sampled states, in which the measured values of the field bear resemblance to discrete particles. The field values at unmeasured points then need to be inferred (in terms of a marginal probability distribution conditioned on the data) in order to obtain a complete representation of the studied process in continuum space.

For Gaussian random fields defined in terms of the expectation and the covariance function it is straightforward to reconcile the continuum and discrete viewpoints: if the expectation and covariance functions are known, the joint probability density function is known for every countable set of points. In addition, the marginal distribution of the field states is known at every point in the domain of definition. Furthermore, the

Gaussian Measure for Boltzmann-Gibbs Random Fields

Boltzmann-Gibbs random fields are defined by means of exponential “joint-density” expressions, i.e.,
\rho[x(\mathbf{s})]=Z^{-1}\exp\left(-{\mathcal{H}}[x({\mathcal{D}})]\right)
, where

Z
is a normalization constant, known as the partition function and
x({\mathcal{D}})=\{x(\mathbf{s}),\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{D}\}
denotes a field configuration over the domain
\mathcal{D}
.
The calculation of
Z
is not straightforward, or even mathematically well-defined for infinite-dimensional configuration spaces (i.e., if the field states are continuous functions).
Nonetheless, this definition of Boltzmann-Gibbs random fields is used in statistical field theory
[
13
,
2
]
. It is possible to rigorously define Gaussian measures in such infinite-dimensional spaces as shown in
[
3
]
.

Remark 3.1

(About the partition function)
.

The value of
Z
is not necessary in order to calculate the relative probabilities of two different states,
x_{1}(\mathcal{D})
and
x_{2}(\mathcal{D})
, i.e.,
\rho[x_{1}(\mathcal{D})]/\rho[x_{2}(\mathcal{D})]=\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathcal{H}}[x_{1}(\mathcal{D})]+{\mathcal{H}}[x_{2}(\mathcal{D})]}
. Similarly, the
characteristic functional (CF)
can be defined without reference to
Z
. Therefore, it is possible to define a Gaussian measure in terms of the CF without reference to the partition function
[
14
]
.

This paper focuses on B-G random fields with an energy functional which is quadratic with respect to
x(\mathbf{s})
. The spatial dependence is introduced by means of a coupling function that involves a polynomial of the Laplace operator (see also Remark
3.4
).

Definition 3.2

(Inner product)
.

The inner product
\langle f(\mathbf{s}),g(\mathbf{s})\rangle
is defined as follows:

1.

Let
f(\mathbf{s}),g(\mathbf{s}):\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be square-integrable scalar functions, i.e.,

\langle f(\mathbf{s}),g(\mathbf{s})\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{D}}{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{s}\,f(\mathbf{s})\,g(\mathbf{s}).

(3.1a)

2.

Let
\mathbf{f}_{v}(\mathbf{s}),\mathbf{g}_{v}(\mathbf{s}):\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be square integrable vector functions with
d

\mathbf{f}_{v}(\mathbf{s})=\left(f_{v,1}(\mathbf{s}),\ldots,f_{v,d}(\mathbf{s})\right)^{\top}

\langle\mathbf{f}_{v}(\mathbf{s}),\mathbf{g}_{v}(\mathbf{s})\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{D}}{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{s}\,\mathbf{f}_{v}(\mathbf{s})\cdot\mathbf{g}_{v}(\mathbf{s}),

(3.1b)

where the dot product of two vector functions is defined by
\mathbf{f}_{v}(\mathbf{s})\cdot\mathbf{g}_{v}(\mathbf{s})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_{v,i}(\mathbf{s})\,g_{v,i}(\mathbf{s})

Theorem 3.3

(Gaussian measure based on Polynomial of Laplace operator)

Let

\triangle=\nabla^{2}

{\mathcal{Q}}({\bm{\theta}})=\theta_{0}-\theta_{1}\triangle+\theta_{2}\triangle^{2},

(3.2)

{\bm{\theta}}=(\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\theta_{2})^{\top}
is a vector of polynomial coefficients and
\triangle^{2}=(\nabla^{2})^{2}
is the Bi-Laplacian.
Let us assume that the polynomial coefficients satisfy one of the following conditions:

C1:

\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\theta_{2}>0

C2:

\theta_{0},\theta_{2}>0
,
\theta_{1}<0

\theta_{1}^{2}<4\theta_{0}\theta_{2}
.

Then, the following statements are true:

S1:

{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}
defines a covariance operator in the Sobolev space
W^{2,2}(\mathcal{D})

S2:

The covariance

defines a unique Gaussian measure with characteristic functional

S(f)=\mathrm{e}^{-\langle f,\,{\mathcal{C}}\,f\rangle/2},\;\mbox{where}\;f\in W^{2,2}(\mathcal{D}),\;f\mid_{\partial\mathcal{D}}=0,

(3.3)

and
\langle\cdot\,,\cdot\rangle\,:W^{2,2}(\mathcal{D})\times W^{2,2}(\mathcal{D})\to\mathbb{R}

Proof.

(S1) We consider functions

so that all the second-order partial derivatives are defined in the weak sense. We need to show that
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}
defines a

\langle f,{\mathcal{C}}\,g\rangle

(i) To prove

\langle f,{\mathcal{C}}\,f\rangle\geq 0
for all
f\in W^{2,2}(\mathcal{D})

The existence theorem for the eigenfunctions of the Laplace operator states that if
\mathcal{D}
is any open bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with boundary

, then there exists in the Sobolev space

an orthonormal basis

of

such that
-\nabla^{2}f_{n}=\lambda_{n}f_{n}
in

with Dirichlet boundary conditions, i.e.,

on
\partial\mathcal{D}

\lambda_{n}
form a non-decreasing sequence

with
\lambda_{n}>0
for all
n\geq 1
, and
\lambda_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty

are in the space of smooth functions,
f_{n}\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})
and
\langle f_{n}\,,f_{m}\rangle=\delta_{n,m}

[
15
, p. 355]
,
[
16
]
.

The eigenfunctions of the (negative) Laplace operator are also eigenfunctions of the biharmonic operator, i.e.,
\nabla^{4}f_{n}=(-\nabla^{2})\,(-\nabla^{2})f_{n}=-\nabla^{2}\left(\lambda_{n}f_{n}\right)=\lambda_{n}^{2}f_{n}
. Therefore, the eigenfunction basis of
\nabla^{2}
also plays the same role for the operators
{\mathcal{Q}}
and
{\mathcal{C}}

{\mathcal{Q}}f_{n}=\left(\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}\lambda_{n}+\theta_{2}\lambda_{n}^{2}\right)\,f_{n}\Rightarrow{\mathcal{C}}\,f_{n}=\frac{f_{n}}{p_{2}(\lambda_{n})}\,,\;\forall n=1,2,\ldots,

{\mathcal{Q}}

Let
f\in W^{2,2}(\mathcal{D})
. Then,

f

, where

\langle f,{\mathcal{C}}f\rangle=\sum_{n}\sum_{m}\frac{a_{n}a_{m}}{p_{2}(\lambda_{n})}\langle f_{m},f_{n}\rangle=\sum_{n}\frac{a_{n}^{2}}{p_{2}(\lambda_{n})}\,.

(3.4)

The operator
{\mathcal{C}}
is associated with a real-valued, symmetric bilinear functional which is defined in terms of the following

\langle g,{\mathcal{C}}f\rangle=\sum_{n}\frac{a_{n}b_{n}}{p_{2}(\lambda_{n})},\;\mbox{where}\;f,g\in W^{2,2}(\mathcal{D}),\;\mbox{and}\;g=\sum_{m}b_{m}f_{m}.

\langle(g_{1}+g_{2}),{\mathcal{C}}f\rangle=\langle g_{1},{\mathcal{C}}f\rangle+\langle g_{2},{\mathcal{C}}f\rangle

In light of the spectral expansion in Eq. (
3.4

(ii) The

\langle f\,,{\mathcal{C}}f\rangle

\langle f\,,{\mathcal{C}}f\rangle\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\langle f\,,f\rangle

For this inequality to hold it is sufficient that

\sum_{n}\frac{1}{p_{2}(\lambda_{n})}\leq\frac{1}{\delta a^{2}_{\max}},\,\mbox{where}\;a_{\max}=\max\,\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}.

One idea is to investigate the convergence of the spectral series
\sum_{n}p_{2}^{-1}(\lambda_{n})
by means of d’Alembert’s ratio test, i.e., by

\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\left\lvert\frac{p_{2}(\lambda_{n})}{p_{2}(\lambda_{n+1})}\right\rvert=\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\left(\frac{\lambda_{n}}{\lambda_{n+1}}\right)^{4}.

Instead, we will use the Maclaurin-Cauchy integral test which states that a series
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}u_{n}
, where

\int_{1}^{\infty}\phi(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x

is positive and continuous if either condition C1 (

If C1 holds,

is monotonically increasing and thus

is monotonically decreasing. Then,

\int_{1}^{\infty}\phi(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\int_{1}^{x^{\ast}}\phi(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x+\int_{x^{\ast}}^{\infty}\phi(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,

where

does not have singularities in this interval. If the improper integral from
{x^{\ast}}
to
\infty

\int_{1}^{\infty}\phi(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x

\int_{x^{\ast}}^{\infty}\phi(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\int_{\lambda^{\ast}}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}\lambda\,\rho(\lambda)\,\frac{1}{\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}\lambda^{2}+\theta_{2}\lambda^{4}},

where
\rho(\lambda)={\mathrm{d}}x(\lambda)/{\mathrm{d}}\lambda

should increase more slowly than
\lambda^{3}

According to

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\frac{n(\lambda)}{\lambda^{d/2}}=\frac{v_{d}\lvert\mathcal{D}\rvert}{(2\pi)^{d}},

where

\mathcal{D}

4
Boltzmann-Gibbs Random Fields with Kernel-Mediated Interactions

Kernel smoothing is used in SPH to approximate functions defined in continuum spaces in terms of discrete, irregularly placed “pseudo-particles” which are seamlessly connected by means of the kernel function. Below we use
kernel (window) functions
to formulate smoothed expressions for the configurations in
{\mathcal{H}}[\cdot]
. This implies a respective smoothing of the spatial coupling, leading to a mesh-free representation of spatial interactions. This section focuses on a finite-dimensional vector
\mathbf{x}
of observations. In this case it is unnecessary to restrict
\mathbf{s}
within a bounded domain
\mathcal{D}
, since the spatial integration over
\mathcal{D}
in the energy functional
{\mathcal{H}}[\cdot]
is replaced by a summation. The SPH representation introduces a smoothed field
x^{\ast}(\mathbf{s})
, where in this case
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Hence, inner products that involve the smoothed fields represent integrals over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Definition 4.1

(Smoothing Kernels)
.

A real-valued, non-negative function
K(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\prime};h):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+,0}
and
h>0
is a smoothing kernel function if it satisfies the following properties:

1.

Translation symmetry:
K(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\prime};h)=K(\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}^{\prime};h)
.

2.

Symmetry under interchange of positions:
K(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\prime};h)=K(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mathbf{s};h)
.

3.

Existence of all partial derivatives of order
q
(herein it is sufficient that
q=4
).

4.

Normalization condition:
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{s}\,K(\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}^{\prime};h)=1
.

5.

Maximum of Fourier transform at zero frequency:
\tilde{K}(\mathbf{k};h)\leq\tilde{K}(\mathbf{0};h)
for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\mathbf{0}
is the zero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

6.

Isotropic dependence, i.e.,
K(\mathbf{r};h)=K(r;h)
is a
radial function
3
3

3

For economy of notation we do not distinguish between the function
K(\cdot;h):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and the radial function
K(\cdot;h):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
which depends on
\mathbf{r}
exclusively through the Euclidean distance
r=\|{\mathbf{r}}\|
.

Remark 4.2

(Zero-frequency peak)
.

Property (5) assumes the existence of the Fourier transform
\tilde{K}(\mathbf{k};h)
. The latter exists because
K(\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}^{\prime};h)
is a non-negative function which is integrable due to property (4); hence, it is also absolutely integrable.

Remark 4.3

(Kernel Support)
.

For computational reasons, SPH typically uses kernel functions with compact support, i.e.,
K(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\prime};h)=0
if
\|\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\|\geq h
such as the B-spline kernel
[
9
]
. However, kernels with infinite support, such as the squared exponential (Gaussian) are also possible.

Remark 4.4

(Isotropy)
.

Condition (6) is invoked to simplify analytical calculations. It is also possible to consider kernel functions with anisotropic distance dependence (geometric anisotropy). In this case, isotropic dependence can be restored by means of suitable rescaling and rotation transformations, e.g.
[
20
,
21
]
.

The kernel-based representation used in SPH and herein has roots in some theoretical results which we review below.

Definition 4.5

C(r):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
where
C(r)=C_{d}(\|{\mathbf{r}}\|):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a radial function which admits at least second-order continuous partial derivatives with respect to
r=\|{\mathbf{r}}\|
, its Laplacian is given by
[
29
, p. 16]
,
[
19
, p. 446]

\displaystyle\triangle C(r)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r^{d-1}}\,\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}r}\left[r^{d-1}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}C(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r}\right]=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}C(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{2}}+\frac{(d-1)}{r}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}C(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r}.

(4.17)

Lemma 4.13

(Bi-Laplacian of Radial function)
.

Let
C(r):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
r=\|{\mathbf{r}}\|
be a radial function that admits at least fourth-order continuous derivatives with respect to
r
. The Bi-Laplacian of
C(r)
is given by

\displaystyle\triangle^{2}C(r)=

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{4}C(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{4}}+\frac{2(d-1)}{r}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{3}C(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{3}}+\left[\frac{(d-1)^{2}-2(d-1)}{r^{2}}\right]\,\left[\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}C(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{2}}-\frac{1}{r}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}C(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r}\right].

(4.18)

Proof.

It holds that
\triangle^{2}C(r)=\triangle[\triangle C(r)]
, where
\triangle C(r)
is given by Eq. (
4.17
). Let
\triangle C(r)=c_{1}(r)+c_{2}(r)
, where
c_{1}(r)={\mathrm{d}}^{2}C(r)/{\mathrm{d}}r^{2}
and
c_{2}(r)=\frac{d-1}{r}{\mathrm{d}}C(r)/{\mathrm{d}}r
. Then
\triangle^{2}C(r)=\triangle c_{1}(r)+\triangle c_{2}(r)
. We can calculate
\triangle c_{i}(r)
, where
i=1,2
by application of Eq. (
4.17
).
For
i=1
we obtain

\triangle c_{1}(r)=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}c_{1}(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{2}}+\frac{(d-1)}{r}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}c_{1}(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r}=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{4}C(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{4}}+\frac{(d-1)}{r}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{3}C(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{3}}.

(4.19)

Similarly, for
i=2
we obtain

\triangle c_{2}(r)=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{2}}\left[\left(\frac{d-1}{r}\right)\frac{{\mathrm{d}}C(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r}\right]+\frac{(d-1)}{r}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}r}\left[\left(\frac{d-1}{r}\right)\frac{{\mathrm{d}}C(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r}.\right]

Let us define
g_{1}(r)\doteq\frac{d-1}{r}
and
g_{2}(r)\doteq\frac{{\mathrm{d}}C(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r}
. Then,
\triangle c_{2}(r)
is given by

\triangle c_{2}(r)=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{2}}\left[g_{1}(r)g_{2}(r)\right]+\left(\frac{d-1}{r}\right)\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}r}\left[g_{1}(r)g_{2}(r)\right].

The first and second order derivatives of the product
g_{1}(r)g_{2}(r)
can be evaluated using differentiation by parts leading to

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}r}\left[g_{1}(r)g_{2}(r)\right]=g_{1}(r)\frac{{\mathrm{d}}g_{2}(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r}+g_{2}(r)\frac{{\mathrm{d}}g_{1}(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r},

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{2}}\left[g_{1}(r)g_{2}(r)\right]=g_{1}(r)\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}g_{2}(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{2}}+g_{2}(r)\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}g_{1}(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{2}}+2\frac{{\mathrm{d}}g_{1}(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}g_{2}(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r}.

Based on the above, we obtain the following equation for
\triangle c_{2}(r)

\displaystyle\triangle c_{2}(r)=

\displaystyle\frac{2(d-1)}{r^{3}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}C(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r}+\frac{(d-1)}{r}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{3}C(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{3}}-\frac{2(d-1)}{r^{2}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}C(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{(d-1)}{r}\left[\frac{(d-1)}{r}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}C(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{2}}-\frac{(d-1)}{r^{2}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}C(r)}{{\mathrm{d}}r}\right].

(4.20)

Equation (
4.18
) follows by adding the respective terms on each side of Eqs. (
4.19
)-(
4.1
).
∎

Definition 4.14

(Spatial interaction)
.

Let
\mathbf{x}
represent a vector of sample values measured at the positions in the set
{\mathbb{S}_{N}}
, distributed according to the Boltzmann-Gibbs joint density
\rho\propto\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathcal{H}}[\mathbf{x}]}
where
{\mathcal{H}}[\mathbf{x}]
is the energy functional of Eq. (
4.7
). (i) Two points
\mathbf{s}_{n},\mathbf{s}_{m}\in{\mathbb{S}_{N}}
are considered to interact in the strict sense if and only if
Q_{n,m}\neq 0
. (ii) If on the other hand
Q_{n,m}=0
, the values of the field at
\mathbf{s}_{n}
and
\mathbf{s}_{m}
are conditionally independent. (iii)
Two points are considered to interact in the weak sense if and only if

\lvert Q_{n,m}\rvert<\epsilon
, where
0<\epsilon\ll 1
is a small, user-defined threshold.

The strong definition of spatial interaction is useful if the smoothing kernel
K(\cdot,\cdot;h)
is compactly supported. Then, sparse precision matrices
\mathbf{Q}
can be obtained. For domains whose length along orthogonal directions is approximately uniform, sparsity requires that
h\ll L
where
L
is a characteristic domain size. If the domain length varies along orthogonal directions, sparsity requires that
h\ll\overline{L}
, where
\overline{L}
is an “average” domain size. The bandwidth
h
is a parameter to be determined from the data. Experience with kernel methods shows that sparse precision matrices are typically obtained if the data do not have long-range correlations
[
30
,
8
]
.
Infinitely extended smoothing kernels (e.g., exponential, squared exponential) lead to dense precision matrices
\mathbf{Q}
. The latter can be approximated by a sparse
\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}
if weak interactions (with respect to an arbitrary small threshold
\epsilon
) are truncated. Conditional independence can be understood in the SPH framework as follows: the interactions are defined by the latent field
x^{\ast}(\mathbf{s})
via the Laplacian and Bi-Laplacian operators. The latent fields are thus coupled locally via the differential operators (or their discretized approximations). At the same time, if the smoothing kernel is compactly supported, the latent fields couple only points in their vicinity. Thus, the interactions in the SPH representation are restricted only over a maximum range
r_{\max}
(determined by the bandwidth, the LAP2 operator, and the kernel function), while
Q_{n,m}
vanishes if
\lVert\mathbf{s}_{n}-\mathbf{s}_{m}\rVert>r_{\max}
.

Definition 4.15

(Spatial interaction smoothing function)
.

The function
K^{(2)}(r;h)
is defined as the inverse Fourier transform of the squared modulus of the kernel function
K(\mathbf{r};h)
, i.e.,
K^{(2)}(r;h)={\mathrm{IFT}}\left[\lVert\tilde{K}(\mathbf{k};h)\rVert^{2}\right]
, where
r=\|{\mathbf{r}}\|
.

As will be shown below, the function
K^{(2)}(r;h)
and its derivatives determine the dependence of interactions on the distance between points. Therefore
K^{(2)}(r;h)
is a main ingredient of the SPH-LAP2 precision function. Since the spatial interactions, implemented via Eq. (
4.16
) are well-defined for all pairs of points, the spatial coupling function based on
K^{(2)}(r;h)
bypasses the need to define a specific mesh (spatial grid).

Theorem 4.16

(SPH-LAP2 Precision Function)
.

Let the SPH-LAP2 precision function be defined by means of the
d
-dimensional inverse Fourier transform

Q^{\ast}(\mathbf{r};h)={\mathrm{IFT}}\left[\,\lVert\tilde{K}(\mathbf{k};h)\rVert^{2}\,\left(\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}\lVert\mathbf{k}\rVert^{2}+\theta_{2}\lVert\mathbf{k}\rVert^{4}\right)\,\right],

(4.21)

where the parameter vector
{\bm{\theta}}
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
3.3
, and
\tilde{K}(\mathbf{k})
is the FT of a radial kernel function
K(r)
(see Definition
4.1
). Then,
Q^{\ast}(\mathbf{r};{\bm{\theta}},h)
is given by

Q^{\ast}(r;h)=\theta_{0}\,K^{(2)}(r;h)-\theta_{1}\,\triangle K^{(2)}(r;h)+\theta_{2}\triangle^{2}K^{(2)}(r;h),

(4.22)

where
K^{(2)}(r;h)
is the spatial interaction smoothing function of Definition
4.15
. More specifically, it holds that

\displaystyle Q^{\ast}(r;h)=

\displaystyle\theta_{0}\,K^{(2)}(r;h)-\theta_{1}\,\left[\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}K^{(2)}(r;h)}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{2}}+\frac{(d-1)}{r}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}K^{(2)}(r;h)}{{\mathrm{d}}r}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\theta_{2}\left\{\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{4}K^{(2)}(r;h)}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{4}}+\frac{2(d-1)}{r}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{3}K^{(2)}(r;h)}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{3}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad\quad\quad+\left[\frac{(d-1)^{2}-2(d-1)}{r^{2}}\right]\,\left[\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}K^{(2)}(r;h)}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{2}}-\frac{1}{r}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}K^{(2)}(r;h)}{{\mathrm{d}}r}\right]\right\}\,.

(4.23)

Proof.

Since
K(r;h)
is a radial function, the FT
\tilde{K}(\mathbf{k};h)
depends purely on the Euclidean norm of
\mathbf{k}
. Hence, the inverse Fourier transform of
\lVert\tilde{K}(\mathbf{k};h)\rVert^{2}
is also a radial function which depends on the Euclidean norm
r=\|{\mathbf{r}}\|
of the lag vector
\mathbf{r}
.

Since the image of the Laplace operator in the Fourier domain is
{\mathrm{FT}}[\triangle]=-\lVert\mathbf{k}\rVert^{2}
, the inverse Fourier transforms of
\lVert\mathbf{k}\rVert^{2n}\,\lVert\tilde{K}(\mathbf{k};h)\rVert^{2}
,
n=1,2
, are given by

\displaystyle{\mathrm{IFT}}[\,\lVert\mathbf{k}\rVert^{2}\,\lVert\tilde{K}(\mathbf{k};h)\rVert^{2}\,]

\displaystyle=-\triangle K^{(2)}(r;h),

\displaystyle{\mathrm{IFT}}[\,\lVert\mathbf{k}\rVert^{4}\,\lVert\tilde{K}(\mathbf{k};h)\rVert^{2}]

\displaystyle=\triangle^{2}K^{(2)}(r;h)\,.

Equation (
4.22
) follows from the above and the IFT given by Eq. (
4.21
). Finally, Equation (
4.16
) follows from Eq. (
4.22
), the Laplacian, Eq. (
4.17
), and the Bi-Laplacian of a radial function, Eq. (
4.18
)
of Lemma
4.13
.
∎

4.2
The Case of Gaussian Smoothing Kernel

In this section we assume that the smoothing kernel of Definition
4.1
is given by the squared exponential (Gaussian) function with bandwidth
h
:

K_{{\mathrm{se}}}(\mathbf{r};h)=\frac{1}{\left(h\sqrt{\pi}\right)^{d}}\exp\left[-\lVert\mathbf{r}\rVert^{2}/h^{2}\right].

(4.24)

Lemma 4.17

(Gaussian spatial interaction smoothing function)
.

Let
K_{{\mathrm{se}}}(\mathbf{r};h)
, where
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, be the squared exponential kernel function with Fourier transform
\tilde{K}_{{\mathrm{se}}}(\mathbf{k}h)
; also let
r=\|{\mathbf{r}}\|
. The spatial interaction smoothing function
K^{(2)}(r;h)={\mathrm{IFT}}\left[\lVert\tilde{K}_{{\mathrm{se}}}(\mathbf{k}h)\rVert^{2}\right]
, is given by

K^{(2)}(r;h)=K_{{\mathrm{se}}}(\mathbf{r};h^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\left(h\,\sqrt{2\pi}\right)^{d}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lVert\mathbf{r}\rVert^{2}/2h^{2}},\;\mbox{where}\;h^{\prime}=\sqrt{2}h.

(4.25)

Proof.

The Fourier transform of the Gaussian function
\exp(-\|{\mathbf{r}}\|^{2}/h^{2})
is also a Gaussian given by
[
19
, p. 160]

\mbox{FT}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\|{\mathbf{r}}\|^{2}/h^{2}}\right]=\pi^{d/2}\,h^{d}\,\exp\left(-k^{2}h^{2}/4\right),\;\mbox{where}\;k=\lVert\mathbf{k}\rVert.

Hence, the Fourier transform of the smoothing kernel of Eq. (
4.24
) is given by the Gaussian function

\tilde{K}_{{\mathrm{se}}}(kh)=\exp\left(-k^{2}h^{2}/4\right).

(4.26)

Thus, the modulus squared of
\tilde{K}_{{\mathrm{se}}}(kh)
is also a Gaussian function given by

\lVert\tilde{K}_{{\mathrm{se}}}(kh)\rVert^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{-k^{2}h^{2}/2}=\tilde{K}_{{\mathrm{se}}}(kh^{\prime}),\;\mbox{where}\;h^{\prime}=\sqrt{2}h.

(4.27)

Based on Eq. (
4.27
), the inverse Fourier transform of
K^{(2)}(r;h)
is given by Eq. (
4.25
).
∎

Theorem 4.18

(SPH-LAP2 precision function based on Gaussian smoothing kernel)
.

Let us consider the SPH-LAP2 precision function
Q^{\ast}(\mathbf{r};{\bm{\theta}},h)
defined by means of Eq. (
4.21
) and equipped with the squared-exponential kernel. Then, the precision function in the spectral domain is given by

\tilde{Q}^{\ast}(k;{\bm{\theta}},h)=\mathrm{e}^{-k^{2}h^{2}/2}\,\left(\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}k^{2}+\theta_{2}k^{4}\right),\;\mbox{where}\;k=\lVert\mathbf{k}\rVert.

(4.28)

The precision function
Q^{\ast}(\mathbf{r};{\bm{\theta}},h)
is given by the following radial function

\displaystyle Q^{\ast}(r;{\bm{\theta}},h)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/2h^{2}}}{\left(h\,\sqrt{2\pi}\right)^{d}}\,

\displaystyle\left\{\theta_{0}\,-\,\frac{\theta_{1}}{h^{2}}\,\left(\frac{r^{2}}{h^{2}}-d\right)+\frac{\theta_{3}}{h^{4}}\left[\frac{r^{4}}{h^{4}}-2(d+2)\frac{r^{2}}{h^{2}}+d(d+2)\right]\right\}.

(4.29)

Proof.

The precision spectral function is obtained from the IFT of Eq. (
4.21
) and the Fourier transform (
4.27
) of the squared exponential kernel. All the factors involved are radial functions of
k=\lVert\mathbf{k}\rVert
. Hence, Eq. (
4.28
) is proved.

As is evident in Eq. (
4.28
),
\tilde{Q}^{\ast}(k;{\bm{\theta}},h)
decays exponentially with
k
and lacks singularities on the real line; this ensures that
\tilde{Q}^{\ast}(k;{\bm{\theta}},h)
is absolutely integrable so that its inverse Fourier transform exists. Furthermore, since the parameter vector
{\bm{\theta}}
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
3.3
, based on Bochner’s theorem the inverse Fourier transform of Eq. (
4.28
) is a positive definite function
Q^{\ast}(\mathbf{r};{\bm{\theta}},h)
.

Based on Eq. (
4.21
),
Q^{\ast}(\mathbf{r};{\bm{\theta}},h)
is obtained from the following superposition of inverse Fourier transforms

Q^{\ast}(\mathbf{r};{\bm{\theta}},h)=\theta_{0}\,{\mathrm{IFT}}[\tilde{K}_{{\mathrm{se}}}(kh^{\prime})]+\theta_{1}\,{\mathrm{IFT}}[k^{2}\,\tilde{K}_{{\mathrm{se}}}(kh^{\prime})]+\theta_{2}\,{\mathrm{IFT}}[k^{4}\,\tilde{K}_{{\mathrm{se}}}(kh^{\prime})].

Since the image of the Laplace operator in the Fourier domain is
-k^{2}
, the second and third inverse Fourier transforms in the above equation are evaluated as follows:

{\mathrm{IFT}}[\,k^{2}\,\tilde{K}_{{\mathrm{se}}}(kh^{\prime})\,]=-\triangle K_{{\mathrm{se}}}(\mathbf{r};h^{\prime}),\quad{\mathrm{IFT}}[\,k^{4}\,\tilde{K}_{{\mathrm{se}}}(kh^{\prime})]=\triangle^{2}K_{{\mathrm{se}}}(\mathbf{r};h^{\prime})\,.

Taking into account the definition of the Gaussian spatial interaction smoothing function in Eq. (
4.25
), the SPH-LAP2 precision function becomes

Q^{\ast}(\mathbf{r};{\bm{\theta}},h)=\left[\theta_{0}\,K^{(2)}(r;h)-\theta_{1}\,\triangle K^{(2)}(r;h)+\theta_{2}\triangle^{2}K^{(2)}(r;h)\right].

(4.30)

The Laplacian and Bi-Laplacian of the radial function
K^{(2)}(r;h)
involves up to fourth-order derivatives of the Gaussian function
\exp(-r^{2}/2h^{2})
, according to Eqs. (
4.17
) and (
4.18
).
These derivatives can be evaluated by means of the
Hermite polynomials

\mathrm{He}_{n}(x):\,\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
of order
n\in\{1,2,3,4\}
, which are defined as follows:

\mathrm{He}_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}\exp(x^{2}/2)\,\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{n}\exp(-x^{2}/2)}{{\mathrm{d}}x^{n}}.

(4.31)

In particular, the Hermite polynomials for
n=1,2,3,4
are given by

\mathrm{He}_{1}(x)=x,\;\mathrm{He}_{2}(x)=x^{2}-1,\;\mathrm{He}_{3}(x)=x^{3}-3x,\;\mathrm{He}_{4}(x)=x^{4}-6x^{2}+3\,.

(4.32)

According to Eq. (
4.31
), the
n
-th order derivative of the Gaussian
K_{{\mathrm{se}}}(r;h)=\exp(-r^{2}/2h^{2})
is given by means of the Hermite polynomials,
\mathrm{He}_{n}(x)
, as follows

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{n}\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/2h^{2}}}{{\mathrm{d}}r^{n}}=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{h^{n}}\,\mathrm{He}_{n}\left(\frac{r}{h}\right)\,\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/2h^{2}},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(4.33)

Then, based on Eq. (
4.17
) and Eq. (
4.33
), the Laplacian of
K^{(2)}(r;h)
is given by

\triangle K^{(2)}(r;h)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/2h^{2}}}{\left(h\,\sqrt{2\pi}\right)^{d}}\,\left[\frac{1}{h^{2}}\,\mathrm{He}_{2}\left(\frac{r}{h}\right)+\left(\frac{1-d}{rh}\right)\,\mathrm{He}_{1}\left(\frac{r}{h}\right)\right]\,.

(4.34)

Using the explicit expressions for
\mathrm{He}_{2}(\cdot)
and
\mathrm{He}_{1}(\cdot)
from Eq. (
4.31
),
\triangle K^{(2)}(r;h)
becomes

\displaystyle\triangle K^{(2)}(r;h)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/2h^{2}}}{\left(h\,\sqrt{2\pi}\right)^{d}}\,\left[\frac{1}{h^{2}}\,\left(\frac{r^{2}}{h^{2}}-1\right)+\frac{1-d}{h^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/2h^{2}}}{\left(h\,\sqrt{2\pi}\right)^{d}}\,\left[\frac{1}{h^{2}}\,\left(\frac{r^{2}}{h^{2}}-d\right)\right]\,.

(4.35)

Similarly, based on Eqs. (
4.18
) and (
4.33
) the Bi-Laplacian of
K_{{\mathrm{se}}}(r;h^{\prime})
is given by

\displaystyle\triangle^{2}K^{(2)}(r;h)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/2h^{2}}}{\left(h\,\sqrt{2\pi}\right)^{d}}\,

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{h^{4}}\mathrm{He}_{4}\left(\frac{r}{h}\right)+\frac{2(1-d)}{rh^{3}}\,\mathrm{He}_{3}\left(\frac{r}{h}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\frac{(d-1)^{2}-2(d-1)}{(rh)^{2}}\,\mathrm{He}_{2}\left(\frac{r}{h}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\frac{(d-1)^{2}-2(d-1)}{r^{3}h}\mathrm{He}_{1}\left(\frac{r}{h}\right)\right]\,.

(4.36)

Note that the right hand-side of Eq. (
4.2
) includes two singular terms
\mathcal{O}(r^{-2})
coming from the Hermite polynomials
\mathrm{He}_{2}(x)
and
\mathrm{He}_{1}(x)
, cf. Eqs. (
4.32
). However, the singular terms cancel each other. Thus, the sum of terms proportional to
\mathrm{He}_{2}(x)
and
\mathrm{He}_{1}(x)
becomes
h^{-4}\,[(d-1)^{2}-2(d-1)]
. The remaining terms
\sim\mathcal{O}(h^{-4})
are evaluated using the expressions for
\mathrm{He}_{4}(x)
and
\mathrm{He}_{3}(x)
from Eqs. (
4.32
).

Finally, based on Eq. (
4.2
) for the Laplacian of
K^{(2)}(r;h)
, Eq. (
4.2
) for the Bi-Laplacian of
K^{(2)}(r;h)
, and Eq. (
4.30
) for
Q^{\ast}(r;{\bm{\theta}},h)
, the expression Eq. (
4.29
) is obtained for the SPH-LAP2 precision function
equipped with the Gaussian kernel.
∎

Remark 4.19

(Non-stationarity)
.

The SPH-LAP2 random field based on the Gaussian smoothing kernel is not stationary for
h\neq 0
. To see this, consider that the Fourier transform,
\tilde{Q}^{\ast}(k;{\bm{\theta}},h)
, of the precision function is given by Eq. (
4.28
).
If the SPH-LAP2 random field were stationary,
the inverse of the spectral image of the precision operator, i.e.,
1/\tilde{Q}(k;{\bm{\theta}},h)
, would correspond to the field’s spectral density. However, as is evident from Eq. (
4.28
),
\tilde{Q}^{-1}(k;{\bm{\theta}},h)
is not integrable over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
due to the exponential increase of the Gaussian function with
k
; therefore, it is
not a valid spectral density according to Bochner’s theorem
[
12
]
.

Remark 4.20

(Spatial interaction at zero separation)
.

Based on Eq. (
4.29
) and the definition of the Hermite polynomials given in Theorem
4.18
it follows that the precision function at zero separation (lag) for
h>0
is given by

Q^{\ast}(0;{\bm{\theta}},h)=\frac{1}{\left(h\,\sqrt{2\pi}\right)^{d}}\,\left[\theta_{0}+\frac{\theta_{1}d}{h^{2}}+\frac{\theta_{2}(d^{2}+2d)}{h^{4}}\right].

It is straightforward to show that
Q^{\ast}(0;{\bm{\theta}},h)
is the global maximum of
Q^{\ast}(r;{\bm{\theta}},h)
. Then, the above equation implies that for a fixed parameter vector
{\bm{\theta}}
the value
Q^{\ast}(0;{\bm{\theta}},h)
is lowered as
h
increases, marking a weakening of the spatial coupling. On the other hand, since
{\bm{\theta}}
is a vector of free parameters, it is possible to choose
\theta_{0}\propto h^{d}
,
\theta_{1}\propto h^{d+2}
and
\theta_{2}\propto h^{d+4}
so that finite values of
Q^{\ast}(0;{\bm{\theta}},h)
are obtained even for
h\to 0
.

4.29
) for
d=2

r\to\infty
, either from the positive or the negative side. Negative values of the precision function for a given separation favor opposite signs of the field values at this distance, because they tend to lower the energy in Eq. (
4.7
).

5
The Continuum Case

Theorem
4.9
presents the SPH-LAP2 joint pdf based on a discrete set of observations at the points in the set
{\mathbb{S}_{N}}
. This can be extended to a continuum-space formulation by replacing the smoothed field of Eq. (
4.3
) with the continuum convolution integral

x^{\ast}(\mathbf{s})=\int_{\mathcal{D}}{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{s}_{1}\,K(\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}_{1};h)\,x(\mathbf{s}_{1}).

(5.1)

In contrast with Definition (
4.7
) (which is valid in the discrete case), in order to define the Gaussian measure in continuum space the points
\mathbf{s}
should belong to a bounded domain
\mathcal{D}
instead of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
—as shown in Theorem
3.3
.

Then, the energy functional of Eq. (
4.12
) is replaced by

{\mathcal{H}}[x({\mathcal{D}});{\bm{\theta}}]=\frac{1}{2}\,\int_{\mathcal{D}}{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{s}_{1}\,\int_{\mathcal{D}}{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{s}_{2}\,x(\mathbf{s}_{1})\,Q^{\ast}(\mathbf{s}_{1}-\mathbf{s}_{2})\,x(\mathbf{s}_{2}),

(5.2)

where the kernel
Q^{\ast}(\mathbf{s}_{1}-\mathbf{s}_{2};{\bm{\theta}},h)
corresponds to the SPH-LAP2 precision function of Eq. (
4.22
).
The integral formulation extends the precision function (
4.22
) to continuum, bounded domains
\mathcal{D}
.

Remark 5.1

(Zero-bandwidth limit)
.

At the limit
h\to 0
the FFT of the Gaussian kernel given by Eq. (
4.26
) satisfies

which is the inverse spectral density (i.e., the image of the precision operator in Fourier space) of the FGC Boltzmann-Gibbs random fields (aka, Spartan random fields)
[

,

]
.

Remark 5.2

(Impact of kernel bandwidth)
.

The field configurations shown in Fig.
2
become increasingly rougher as
h
increases. Intuitively this behavior can be understood as follows: Based on the Fourier transform of the precision function, Eq. (
4.28
), higher
h
implies that the spatial coupling induced by the precision operator is strongly attenuated at high frequencies
k
due to the factor
\exp(-k^{2}\,h^{2}/2)
. As a result, the spatial continuity imposed by the coupling weakens at short distances (large
k
). This leads to sawtooth-like oscillations of the empirical variogram which diminish with increasing lag (see Fig.
4
).

Remark 5.3

(Spatial patterns for
h=1.5
)
.

The field states for
h=1.5
(bottom row), exhibit a rather unusual behavior which is marked by alternating high and low values at neighboring sites and “islands” of checkerboard patterns which correspond to variations in the magnitude of the fluctuations. In addition, the impact of
\theta_{1}
on the spatial patterns is not visible. The main reason for this behavior is that values of
h>1
emphasize the exponential decline of the precision function and thus attenuate the coupling between neighboring sites. A different way to interpret this pattern is by realizing that
1/\tilde{Q}^{\ast}(k;{\bm{\theta}},h)
has a sharp increase for large
k
due to the factor
\exp(k^{2}h^{2}/2)
. This leads to large fluctuations of the field values at short distances. At low
k
such that
k\,h\ll 1
(i.e., for large length scales) the impact of the exponential term is
\approx 1
, and the polynomial term shapes the configuration of the “islands”.

6
Discussion

The formulation presented herein was motivated by previous works by the author on Boltzmann-Gibbs random fields
[
4
,
19
,
5
]
and the theory of smoothed particle hydrodynamics
[
9
,
10
]
. The present work combines a local precision operator constructed from the second-order polynomial of the Laplacian and smoothing kernel functions. The link between covariance functions and polynomials of the Laplace operator has also been investigated in
[
4
,
36
]
. This is based on earlier ideas on the connections between covariance functions, rational spectral densities, Markov random fields and stochastic partial differential equations, e.g.
[
27
,
11
,
32
,
19
]
. Higher-order polynomials of the Laplace operator can be used to derive more general, SPH-LAP
n
precision functions, where
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
n>2
. Such precision functions can model more complex spectral properties, including non-monotonic dependence on
k
which is relevant for wave phenomena. Another interesting direction of research is the investigation of non-Gaussian smoothing kernel functions. While the Gaussian kernel leads readily to explicit expressions, it also imposes a drastic cutoff of the interactions at wavenumbers exceeding
\approx 1/h
. Furthermore, the SPH-LAP2 function derived herein was based on uniform SPH weights
v_{n}
in Eq. (
4.13
). Richer, potentially non-stationary models can be achieved by allowing local variations of SPH weights. Connections with machine learning models can be explored in this framework.

In the process convolution approach proposed by Higdon
[
37
]
, observable random fields are defined via the convolution of a latent process—which is assumed to have a known covariance—with a kernel function. The covariance of the observed random field is then calculated by means of convolution integrals that involve the kernel function and the latent process covariance.
In the SPH approach, the latent field
x^{\ast}(\mathbf{s})
is defined by the weighted sum of the sample values given in Eq. (
4.3
), where the weights are provided by the kernel function. However, the covariance function of
x^{\ast}(\mathbf{s})
is not known. Instead, the values of
x^{\ast}(\mathbf{s})
are coupled to each other via differential operators, which enforce the spatial correlations. Hence, the SPH representation focuses by construction on the precision operator. Another difference between the Higdon and the SPH representations is that in the former the observable process is defined by the convolution of a kernel function with a latent field which is not directly observable, while the SPH convolution in Eq. (
4.3
), and its continuum analogue Eq. (
5.1
), define the latent field
x^{\ast}(\mathbf{s})
in terms of the observed field values. The Higdon latent field can comprise a set of independent, identically distributed random variables; then, the covariance of the observable process is determined purely from the kernel function. This is not possible in the SPH representation, where the latent field is determined by the kernel function and the sampled process which is spatially correlated.

The class of SPH-LAP2 random fields can find applications in spatial statistics, statistical physics, and machine learning. In spatial statistics and machine learning the benefit of the model derives from the explicit form of the precision function. This permits the computationally fast estimation of the single-point predictive distribution even for big datasets. The efficiency of the method is due to the fact that the inversion of the large
N\times N
Gram matrix, necessary for covariance-based methods, is avoided.

In particular, using standard expressions for GMRFs
[
26
,
19
]
, the predictive pdf at any location
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{D}
is given by the following normal density

\displaystyle\rho\left(x_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}\right)=

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}(m_{x_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}},\sigma^{2}_{x_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}})

(6.1a)

\displaystyle m_{x_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}}=

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{Q^{\ast}(\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}_{n};{\bm{\theta}},h)}{Q^{\ast}(0;{\bm{\theta}},h)}\,x_{n},

(6.1b)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{x_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Q^{\ast}(0;{\bm{\theta}},h)},

(6.1c)

where
m_{x_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}}
and
\sigma^{2}_{x_{s}\mid\mathbf{x}}
are, respectively, the conditional mean and variance given a vector of sample values
\mathbf{x}
at the set of sampling positions
{\mathbb{S}_{N}}
.

For applications in statistical physics, the model enhances the standard Gaussian field theory
[
13
,
38
]
by including the curvature term in the energy functional and introducing a gradual decline of the interactions with distance. The correlation function and the free energy of this model are topics that deserve further investigation.
Notice that the curvature term is irrelevant for long wavelengths (small
k
), which is the interesting limit in the study of phase transitions
[
13
]
. However, the curvature term is pertinent in Ginzburg-Landau theories of physical systems such as ternary amphiphilic mixtures
[
39
,
40
]
. In such systems one is interested in the geometry of random field configurations; therefore the spectral properties at intermediate wavelengths are also important. Finally, it would be interesting to investigate connections between SPH-LAP2 and magnetic models.

The SPH-LAP2 model is expressed in terms of the precision function given by Eq. (
4.22
). This leads to sparse precision matrices for compactly supported kernel functions. As mentioned in the above paragraph, the ensuing formulation has a direct connection with GMRFs. These properties (sparse precision matrix and GMRF connection) are shared by the popular stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) approach
[
32
]
, recently reviewed in
[
41
]
.
Note that the BG-LAP2 model defined in Section
3
can be derived from an SPDE with a suitable differential operator which involves the first and the second power of the half-Laplacian
[
19
]
. However, the realizations of BG-LAP2 do not admit pointwise derivatives (for
d>1
). In
d=1
the BG-LAP2 model represents a linear harmonic damped oscillator driven by white noise
[
19
, Chap. 9]
. On the other hand, random fields with Matérn covariance, obtained from the classic SPDE approach
[
32
]
, admit (in mean square sense) partial derivatives up to order
m=\lfloor\nu\rfloor
, where
\nu
is the smoothness parameter.
In the SPH-LAP2 formulation, the high-frequency interactions are cut off by the smoothing kernel [cf. (
4.21
)]. This leads to sharp transitions of the field for finite
h
(cf. Fig.
2
). A comparison between the SPDE and SPH approaches can be carried further with respect to non-stationarity, non-Gaussian models, parameter inference, and computational efficiency. However, this is beyond the scope of the current paper. To summarize, the BG-LAP2 model can be derived from an SPDE, which is in general different than the Matérn-related SPDE; the two models become equivalent only in
d=2
if
\nu=1
(Matérn) and
{\bm{\theta}}\propto(\xi^{-2},2,\xi^{2})^{\top}
(BG-LAP2). The SPH-LAP2 model is not at this point linked with an SPDE, but it provides (at least in the case of a squared-exponential smoothing kernel) an explicit precision function given by Eq. (
4.29
).

7
Conclusions

This paper presents a novel class of Gaussian Boltzmann-Gibbs random fields defined by means of a mesh-free spatial coupling (precision) function determined by Eq. (
4.21
). The latter is based on a smoothing kernel and a second-degree polynomial of the Laplace operator. The smoothing kernel is a key factor for the mesh-free representation which can be applied to any spatial configuration of sampling points. The SPH representation allows constructing Boltzmann-Gibbs energy functionals, such as Eq. (
4.6
), that involve differential operators, even for discretely sampled data. Thus, the SPH-LAP2 model bridges the discrete (“particle”) and continuum (“wave”) representations. It extends the concept of Gaussian Markov random fields to a mesh-free formulation that can be applied in both continuous domains and scattered data. An explicit expression, given by Eq. (
4.29
), is derived for the precision function using the Gaussian (squared exponential) smoothing kernel.
