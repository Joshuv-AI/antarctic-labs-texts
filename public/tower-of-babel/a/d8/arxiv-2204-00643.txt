Corrections to the Hamiltonian Induced by Finite-Strength Coupling to the Environment
Marcin Łobejko, Marek Winczewski, Gerardo Suárez, Robert Alicki, Michał Horodecki

Abstract
If a quantum system interacts with the environment, the Hamiltonian acquires a correction known as the Lamb-shift term. There are two other corrections to the Hamiltonian, related to the stationary state. Namely, the stationary state is to first approximation a Gibbs state with respect to original Hamiltonian. However, if we have finite coupling, the true stationary state will be different, and regarding it as a Gibbs state to some effective Hamiltonian, one can extract a correction, which is called “steady-state” correction. Alternatively, one can take a static point of view, and consider the reduced state of total equilibrium state, i.e., system plus bath Gibbs state. The extracted Hamiltonian correction is called the “mean-force” correction. This paper presents several analytical results on second-order corrections (in coupling strength) of the three types mentioned above. Instead of the steady state, we focus on a state annihilated by the Liouvillian of the master equation, labeling it as the “quasi-steady state”. Specifically, we derive a general formula for the mean-force correction as well as the quasi-steady state and Lamb-shift correction for a general class of master equations. Furthermore, specific formulas for corrections are obtained for the Davies, Bloch-Redfield, and cumulant equation (refined weak coupling). In particular, the cumulant equation serves as a case study of the Liouvillian, featuring a non-trivial fourth-order generator. This generator forms the basis for calculating the diagonal quasi-steady state correction. We consider spin-boson model as an example, and in addition to using our formulas for corrections, we consider mean-force correction from reaction-coordinate approach.



I
Introduction

As is well known, when a system interacts with its environment, it undergoes not only dissipative but also experiences the renormalization of Hamiltonian dynamics, leading to the so-called ’Lamb-shift’ corrected Hamiltonian
Alicki and Lendi 1987
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2006
. Furthermore, for finite coupling strength with a single heat bath, the equilibrium state of the open system’s dynamics will (assuming ergodicity
Alicki and Lendi 1987
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2006
) deviate from the Gibbs state of the non-interacting system Hamiltonian. Instead, it is widely believed, and in many cases confirmed, that the appropriate candidate for the steady state is the reduced state of the total equilibrium, encompassing both the system and the environment (see
Trushechkin et al. 2021
and references therein). Generally, this can be regarded as a Gibbs state with respect to some effective Hamiltonian, referred to as the ’mean-force’ Hamiltonian. The state is called the ’mean-force Gibbs’ state. Similarly, one can deduce a Hamiltonian from the true steady state (referred to as the ’steady-state Hamiltonian’), with the expectation that the two Hamiltonians - i.e. the mean-force and the steady state Hamiltonian - are equal (see, in this context,
Roux and Simonson 1999
;
Fleming and Cummings 2011
;
Thingna et al. 2012
;
Cresser and Anders 2021
;
Purkayastha et al. 2020
;
Trushechkin et al. 2021
;
Mori and Miyashita 2008
;
Subaş ı et al. 2012
).

As previously mentioned, we are dealing with three types of corrections: the Lamb-shift, mean-force, and steady-state correction. However, obtaining the steady-state correction is particularly challenging, so we opt for an alternative approach. Since this state is likely to be equal to the true steady state (for instance, this holds true for a time-independent Liouvillian), we refer to it as the “quasi-steady” state, along with its corresponding Hamiltonian correction.

II.1
Lamb-shift correction

We start with the
Lamb-shift correction

H^{(2)}_{\text{LS}}
, which is defined according to the Liouvillian of the master equation in the Schrödinger picture of the following general form:

\mathcal{L}_{t}[\rho]=i[\rho,H_{0}+\lambda^{2}H^{(2)}_{\text{LS}}(t)]\\
+\lambda^{2}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha\beta}K_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)\mathcal{D}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})[\rho]+O(\lambda^{4}),

(6)

where:

\mathcal{D}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})[\rho]=A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\rho A_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\omega)-\frac{1}{2}\{A_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime}),\rho\}.

(7)

K
is the so-called Kossakowski matrix and for a while we do not determine it: For particular choice of
K
and
\Upsilon^{(\text{LS})}
we will obtain a given master equation, such as the Bloch-Redfield or Davies one. Notice that the Liouvillian in Eq. (
6
) leads to a completely positive dynamics if the matrix
K_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)
is positive semi-definite. Later, we consider the long-time limits (assuming they exist), when
t\to\infty
, for which we use the abbreviations:

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{(\text{LS})}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\equiv\lim_{t\to\infty}\Upsilon^{(\text{LS})}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t),

(8)

\displaystyle K_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\equiv\lim_{t\to\infty}K_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t).

(9)

The Lamb-shift correction introduces a renormalization of the bare Hamiltonian
H_{0}
of the open system due to the finite-strength coupling with the environment. The correction affects the Hamiltonian part of the evolution.

II.2
Mean-force correction

Let us then introduce the mean-force Hamiltonian
H_{\text{mf}}
, defined according to the marginal Gibbs state of the global equilibrium, i.e.,

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{mf}}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{\text{mf}}}}{\Tr_{S}[e^{-\beta H_{\text{mf}}}]}=\frac{\Tr_{R}[e^{-\beta H}]}{\Tr_{SR}[e^{-\beta H}]}.

(10)

The mean-force Gibbs state refers to the local equilibrium of the open system corresponding to the global equilibrium of the full system (i.e., open system plus the environment). The definition solely relies on static equilibrium and hence is not related to the dynamics.

Concerning the mean-force Hamiltonian

10

\displaystyle\Tr_{SR}[e^{-\beta H}]=\Tr_{S}[e^{-\beta H_{\text{mf}}}]\Tr_{R}[e^{-\beta H_{R}}],

\displaystyle H_{\text{mf}}=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left[\frac{\Tr_{R}[e^{-\beta H}]}{\mathcal{Z}_{R}}\right],

\mathcal{Z}_{R}=\Tr_{R}[e^{-\beta H_{R}}]

\displaystyle\Tr(H_{\text{mf}})=0

The latter gauge will be convenient, when extracting mean-force correction Hamiltonian numerically from a state

II.3
Quasi-steady state correction

The last correction is defined with respect to the so-called quasi-steady Gibbs state:

\displaystyle\varrho\propto e^{-\beta H_{\text{st}}},

(15)

which is defined as the fixed point of the Liouvillian in the long-time limit:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{L}_{t}[\varrho]\equiv\mathcal{L}_{\infty}[\varrho]=0.

(16)

Contrary to static mean-force state,
\varrho
corresponds the dynamical equilibrium, defined according to particular Liouvillian in the long-time limit. In analogy to the mean-force and Lamb-shift, we are interested in the leading order correction defined by the expansion:

\displaystyle H_{\text{st}}=H_{0}+\lambda^{2}H_{\text{st}}^{(2)}+\dots.

(17)

Then, to derive a solution for the correction

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\infty}[\varrho]=\mathcal{L}_{\infty}^{(0)}[\varrho]+\lambda^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\infty}^{(2)}[\varrho]+\lambda^{4}\mathcal{L}_{\infty}^{(4)}[\varrho]+\dots,

(18)

\displaystyle\varrho=\varrho_{0}+\lambda^{2}\varrho_{2}+\lambda^{4}\varrho_{4}\dots,

(19)

\displaystyle\varrho_{0}=e^{-\beta H_{0}},

(20)

\displaystyle\varrho_{2}=-e^{-\beta H_{0}}\int_{0}^{\beta}dt\ e^{tH_{0}}H^{(2)}_{\text{st}}e^{-tH_{0}}.

(23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\infty}^{(0)}[\varrho_{4}]+\mathcal{L}_{\infty}^{(2)}[\varrho_{2}]+\mathcal{L}_{\infty}^{(4)}[\varrho_{0}]=0,

(24)

\displaystyle\dots

III
Models of open systems

In this section, we introduce three specific Liouvillians used in the modeling of quantum open systems: the Liouvillians of Davies ME, Bloch-Redfield ME, and the cumulant equation. The first two are interconnected through the so-called secular approximation, which ensures that Davies ME is completely positive. Later, we will specifically elucidate how this impacts the quasi-steady-state correction. On the other hand, the cumulant equation restores the positivity of the map by incorporating higher-order generators into the Liouvillian. This presents an interesting case study for addressing diagonal corrections that involve fourth-order generators.

III.0.1
Bloch-Redfield and Davies master equations

Let us start with the most known master equations, i.e., the Bloch-Redfield and the Davies master equations. In general, for the Bloch-Redfield ME we define a time-dependent Kossakowski matrix and Lamb-shift coefficient (see Eq. (
6
)):

\displaystyle K_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)=\Gamma^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega^{\prime})+\Gamma^{t}_{\beta\alpha}(\omega)^{*}\equiv\gamma^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime}),

(25)

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{(\text{LS})}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)=\frac{1}{2i}[\Gamma^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega^{\prime})-\Gamma_{\beta\alpha}^{t}(\omega)^{*}]\equiv\mathcal{S}^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime}),

(26)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}ds\ e^{i\omega s}\langle R_{\alpha}(s)R_{\beta}(0)\rangle_{\gamma_{R}}.

(27)

However, in this paper we are mostly interested in the long-time limit of the Liouvillian, based on which we define the quasi-steady state correction. For Bloch-Redfield ME we have:

\mathcal{L}_{\infty}^{R}=i[\cdot,H_{0}]\\
+\lambda^{2}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\Big(i\mathcal{S}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})[\cdot,A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})]\\
+\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\mathcal{D}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\Big).

(28)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{S}^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\quad\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})

(29)

It is well-known that the Bloch-Redfield equation, in general, does not preserve the positivity of the state since
\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
is not a positive semi-definite matrix. Commonly, this issue is solved by applying the so-called secular approximation, which leads to the Davies master equation in the GKSL form. In accordance, applying the secular approximation, we obtain the Kossakowski matrix for the Davies dynamics:

\displaystyle\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\xrightarrow{\text{sec. approx.}}\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\delta_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}},

(30)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\xrightarrow{\text{sec. approx.}}\mathcal{S}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\delta_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}},

(31)

where

\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\equiv\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}ds\ e^{i\omega s}\langle R_{\alpha}(s)R_{\beta}(0)\rangle_{\gamma_{R}},

(32)

is the Fourier transform of the auto-correlation function, and

\mathcal{S}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\equiv\mathcal{S}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega)=\mathcal{P}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\Omega~\frac{\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\Omega)}{\omega-\Omega},

(33)

where
\mathcal{P}
denotes the principal value integral. We also have the following relation:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\Gamma^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\equiv\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)+i\mathcal{S}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega).

(34)

Finally, the Davies generator is given by:

\mathcal{L}_{\infty}^{D}=i[\cdot,H_{0}]+\lambda^{2}\sum_{\omega}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\Big(i\mathcal{S}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)[\cdot,A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega)]\\
+\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\mathcal{D}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega)\Big).

(35)

However, we want to notice that to restore the positivity of the Bloch-Redfield master equation, it is enough to do the secular approximation only for the dissipative part. For this reason, we additionally consider a so-called
non-secular Davies
defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\infty}^{D,ns}=i[\cdot,H_{0}]\\
+\lambda^{2}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\Big(i\mathcal{S}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})[\cdot,A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})]\\
+\delta_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\mathcal{D}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega)\Big).

(36)

In the following, we will also use the representation of the (time-dependent) Redfield generator in the interaction picture given by the expression:

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{R}=\lambda^{2}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\Big(i\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})[\cdot,A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})]\\
+\tilde{\gamma}^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\mathcal{D}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\Big),

(37)

with the following definitions:

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})t}\gamma^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime}),

(38)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})t}\mathcal{S}^{t}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime}).

(39)

III.0.2
Cumulant equation (refined weak-coupling)

Let us now introduce the cumulant equation

Alicki 1989
;
Rivas 2017
;
Rivas 2019
. Unlike the previous models of open system, which are in the form of differential equations, the cumulant equation is introduced as the dynamical map (which does not involve the Markovian approximation):

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}(t)=e^{\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}}\tilde{\rho}(0),

(40)

where

\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}[\rho(0)]=\\
-\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\Tr_{R}\left(\left[H_{I}(t_{1}),\left[H_{I}(t_{2}),\rho_{S}(0)\otimes\rho_{R}\right]\right]\right)

(41)

is the generator of the map in the interaction picture, such that
\tilde{\rho}(t)=e^{iH_{0}t}\rho(t)e^{-iH_{0}t}
.

It has been showed that cumulant equation is an alternative way to describe non-Markovian dynamics in the weak-coupling regime. Its essential feature is the GKSL form of the
\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}
super-operator. In this way, the cumulant equation defines one parameter family of CPTP dynamical maps. This feature of the cumulant equation is its advantage over the Bloch-Redfield equation, for which the fundamental property of completely positive evolution is not satisfied.

Since the Lamb-shift and quasi-steady state corrections are defined according to the generator of the master equations, from the dynamical map (
40
) we derive the corresponding differential equation, namely

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\left(\frac{d}{dt}e^{\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}}\right)e^{-\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}}\right]\tilde{\rho}(t).

(42)

This defines the Liouvillian of the cumulant equation in the interaction picture:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d}{dt}e^{\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}}\right)e^{-\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}}

(43)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}[\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t},\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}]+\dots

(44)

Interestingly, we have revealed that the cumulant super-operator

\displaystyle\frac{d\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}}{dt}=\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{R}_{t}.

(45)

Finally, applying (
45

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{C}_{t}[\rho]=\mathcal{L}_{t}^{R}[\rho]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}ds\ e^{-iH_{0}t}\left(\left[\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s}^{R},\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{R}\right][e^{iH_{0}t}\rho e^{-iH_{0}t}]\right)e^{iH_{0}t}

\displaystyle+O(\lambda^{6}).

(46)

We see that up to the second-order the Liouvillian of the cumulant is equal to the Bloch-Redfield one. Nevertheless, cumulant equation provides non-trivial higher order generators that in principle leads to different predictions of quasi-steady state diagonal correction.

IV.1
Mean-force correction

We are ready to state our first main result regarding the mean-force correction.

Theorem 1
.

In the gauge (
11
),
the mean-force correction is given by

\displaystyle H_{\text{mf}}^{(2)}(t)=\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\Upsilon^{(\text{mf})}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime}),

(48)

with the coefficients:

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{(\text{mf})}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\Omega\ D_{\text{mf}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},\Omega)\ \gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\Omega),

(49)

\displaystyle D_{\text{mf}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega^{\prime}-\Omega}-\frac{(\omega-\omega^{\prime})(e^{\beta(\omega-\Omega)}-1)}{(\omega-\Omega)(\omega^{\prime}-\Omega)(e^{\beta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})}-1)},

(50)

or equivalently, in terms of the
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)
function (
33

\Upsilon_{\alpha\beta}^{(\text{mf})}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\frac{1}{e^{\beta\omega}-e^{\beta\omega^{\prime}}}(e^{\beta\omega}\mathcal{S}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega^{\prime})-e^{\beta\omega^{\prime}}\mathcal{S}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\\
+e^{\beta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})}\left(\mathcal{S}_{\beta\alpha}(-\omega^{\prime})-\mathcal{S}_{\beta\alpha}(-\omega)\right)).

(51)

Remark
.

What is interesting about the expression (
49
) is that despite of its form, it does not exhibit any poles, which is quite unusual for a Lamb-shift correction. Thus, the principal value integral is not needed in this case (cf. Eq. (
47
)).

Proof.

The sketch of the proof is as follows. To solve Eq. (
10
) we write the exponents from both sides of the equality in terms of the Dyson series, which formally can be expressed via the time-ordering operator
\mathcal{T}
as:

\displaystyle e^{-\beta H_{\text{mf}}}\equiv e^{-\beta(H_{0}+\delta H_{\text{mf}})}=e^{-\beta H_{0}}\mathcal{T}e^{-\int_{0}^{\beta}dt\ \delta\hat{H}_{\text{mf}}(t)},

(52)

\displaystyle e^{-\beta H}\equiv e^{-\beta(H_{0}+H_{R}+\lambda H_{I})}=e^{-\beta(H_{0}+H_{R})}\mathcal{T}e^{-\lambda\int_{0}^{\beta}dt\ \hat{H}_{I}(t)},

(53)

where we put
\delta{H}_{\text{mf}}=H_{\text{mf}}-H_{0}
and we define an imaginary-time-dependent operators
\hat{A}(t)=e^{t(H_{0}+H_{R})}Ae^{-t(H_{0}+H_{R})}
. Consequently, using gauge (
11
) the equality (
10
) can be rewritten in the form:

\Tr_{R}\left[\mathcal{T}\left(e^{-\int_{0}^{\beta}dt\ \delta\hat{H}_{\text{mf}}(t)}-e^{-\lambda\int_{0}^{\beta}dt\ \hat{H}_{I}(t)}\right)\gamma_{R}\right]=0.

(54)

where
\gamma_{R}
is the Gibbs state with respect to
H_{R}
.
Note that this is exact for arbitrary coupling strength
\lambda
. Then, considering the weak-coupling limit (
\lambda\ll 1
), we expand the above equality and obtain, within the second-order, the following condition for the mean-force correction:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\beta}dt\ \hat{H}_{\text{mf}}^{(2)}(t)=-\int_{0}^{\beta}dt\int_{0}^{t}ds\left\langle\hat{H}_{I}(t)\hat{H}_{I}(s)\right\rangle_{\gamma_{R}}

(55)

After substituting the representation of the mean-force Hamiltonian given by Eq. (
4
) and the interaction term (
2
) with the definition of jump operators (
5
), we are able to calculate the coefficients
\Upsilon^{(\text{mf})}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
(see the detailed proof in Appendix
B
).
∎

Theorem
1
is the first derivation in the literature of the second-order mean-force Hamiltonian for a general weak-coupling of the form (
1
) (this was independently done in
Timofeev and Trushechkin 2022
; the expression for the correction to mean-force
Gibbs state
has been given earlier in
Subaş ı et al. 2012
;
Mori and Miyashita 2008
;
Trushechkin et al. 2021
). One observes that the coefficients are symmetric in
\omega

\Upsilon^{(\text{mf})}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\Upsilon^{(\text{mf})}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega^{\prime},\omega)
, which together with
\mathcal{S}^{*}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)=\mathcal{S}_{\beta\alpha}(\omega)
, ensures the hermiticity of the Hamiltonian.

According to this expression, let us compare the dynamical Hamiltonian with the mean-force. Using Eq. (
34
), we write down the Lamb-shift correction in terms of
\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)
and
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)

+\frac{i}{4}(\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)-\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega^{\prime})),

(56)

whereas for the Davies equation the off-diagonal elements vanish (due to the secular approximation). It is seen that the mean-force correction is different than the Lamb-shift one; in particular, the Lamb-shift correction has non-zero anti-hermitian part (in indices
\alpha,\beta

Indeed,

\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)
and

are hermitian matrices, and therefore the second term of Lamb-shift correction is non-hermitian, while there is no such term in mean-force correction.

IV.2
Quasi-steady state correction

In this section, we propose the general formulas for quasi-steady state correction in terms of the coefficients
\Upsilon_{\alpha\beta}^{(\text{st})}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
:

\displaystyle H_{\text{st}}^{(2)}(t)=\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\Upsilon^{(\text{st})}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime}).

(57)

First we consider a problem of specifying the off-diagonal contribution to the Hamiltonian
H_{\text{st}}^{(2)}
(in terms of the coefficients
\Upsilon_{\alpha\beta}^{(\text{st})}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
for
\omega\neq\omega^{\prime}
), and later the diagonal one (given by
\Upsilon_{\alpha\beta}^{(\text{st})}(\omega,\omega)
).

We start with off-diagonal elements, because (as indicated in
Thingna et al. 2012
) the first nontrivial correction for diagonal states one can get only in the fourth order. So we start with simpler case of off-diagonal elements.

IV.2.1
Off-diagonal elements

To derive a solution for off-diagonal elements of the Hamiltonian
H_{\text{st}}^{(2)}
, one needs to specify the solution of the second-order equation (see Eq. (
23

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\infty}^{(0)}[\varrho_{2}]+\mathcal{L}_{\infty}^{(2)}[\varrho_{0}]=0.

(58)

To provide of such a solution we propose the following representation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(k)}_{\infty}[\rho_{l}]=e^{-\beta H_{0}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}g_{\alpha\beta}^{(kl)}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})A_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime}),

(59)

for
l+k=2

k=0,l=2
and
k=2,l=0
(see Appendix
C.2
). Our methodology is based on replacing the equation for operators (
58

Lemma 1
.

Eq. (
23
) can be rewritten as:

\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}(g_{\alpha\beta}^{(02)}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})+g_{\alpha\beta}^{(20)}(\omega,\omega^{\prime}))A_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})=0,

(60)

which, in particular, is satisfied if:

\displaystyle g_{\alpha\beta}^{(02)}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})+g_{\alpha\beta}^{(20)}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=0

(61)

for all
\omega,\omega^{\prime}
and
\alpha,\beta
.

Next, we specify the explicit formula for coefficients of the most common models of master equations.

Lemma 2
.

For the general form of the second-order Liouvillian (
6

\displaystyle g_{\alpha\beta}^{(02)}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-i(\omega+\omega^{\prime})\Upsilon^{(\text{st})}_{\alpha\beta}(-\omega,\omega^{\prime})\alpha(\omega+\omega^{\prime}),

(62)

\displaystyle g_{\alpha\beta}^{(20)}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=i\Upsilon_{\alpha\beta}^{(\text{LS})}(-\omega,\omega^{\prime})(1-e^{-\beta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})})

\displaystyle+e^{\beta\omega}K_{\beta\alpha}(-\omega^{\prime},-\omega)

(63)

where
\alpha(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\beta}dt\ e^{-t\omega}

Finally, combining together those two Lemmas, we propose the following Theorem:

Theorem 2
.

The second-order equation (
23
) is satisfied by the state
\varrho\propto e^{-\beta(H_{0}+\lambda^{2}H_{\text{st}}^{(2)}+\dots)}
if

\displaystyle(i)\ \ K_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega)=K_{\beta\alpha}(-\omega,-\omega)e^{\beta\omega},

(64)

\displaystyle(ii)\ \Upsilon_{\alpha\beta}^{(\text{st})}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\Upsilon_{\alpha\beta}^{(\text{LS})}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})+\frac{i}{e^{\beta\omega}-e^{\beta\omega^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\times\left(K_{\beta\alpha}(-\omega,-\omega^{\prime})e^{\beta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})}-\frac{1}{2}K_{\alpha\beta}(\omega^{\prime},\omega)(e^{\beta\omega}+e^{\beta\omega^{\prime}})\right)

(65)

for
\omega\neq\omega^{\prime}
.

The condition (
64

K_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
and Lamb-shift

C.2.1

Corollary 2.1
.

If

and

\omega\neq\omega^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{\alpha\beta}^{(\text{st})}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\Upsilon_{\alpha\beta}^{(\text{mf})}(\omega,\omega^{\prime}).

(66)

Remark
.

Note that this relation is gauge independent, as the gauge only affects diagonal corrections.

As it follows from Eq. (
28

If

VI
Conclusions

We have presented several results of independent significance. Firstly, we derived the general formula for the (second-order) mean-force Hamiltonian, along with the quasi-steady state Hamiltonian for the canonical class of Liouvillians (i.e., with the leading order expressed in GKLS form for a general Kossakowski matrix). Subsequently, we discussed the relationships among different corrections (mean-force, quasi-steady state, and Lamb-shift). Finally, we applied our findings to the most well-known descriptions of open systems, specifically examining the extent to which the Bloch-Redfield, Davies, and cumulant equations satisfy the condition of converging to equilibrium. We emphasize that the corrections to Hamiltonians derived in this paper hold greater significance than corrections to states because they can be independently applied to various renormalization procedures (cf.
Winczewski and Alicki 2021
).

Our findings align with prior observations
Trushechkin et al. 2021
;
Mori and Miyashita 2008
;
Fleming and Cummings 2011
, affirming that the leading-order term of the Liouvillian should adopt the Bloch-Redfield form to yield the correct off-diagonal quasi-steady state correction. The cumulant
equation (if written in the form of master equation) meets this condition but it goes further by providing non-trivial higher-order generators (by definition absent in the Bloch-Redfield equation). Our analysis demonstrates that this feature offers an accurate approximation of the diagonal elements of the correction, coinciding pretty well with those of the mean-force, in contrast to the Bloch-Redfield (or Davies) master equation.

This may appear contradictory to the findings in
Rivas 2017
, where it is asserted that the dynamics governed by the cumulant equation converges to the dynamics governed by the Davies equation in the long time limit, implying that the stationary state should be the Gibbs state of the bare Hamiltonian. However, our result specifically addresses the second-order correction, and therefore, mathematically, it does not contradict the aforementioned conclusion. It is noteworthy that by collecting of all orders in the cumulant Liouvillian, it ensures the positivity of the dynamics. This suggests a nuanced trade-off between positivity and thermalization towards the mean-force Gibbs state in the cumulant description. Truncating the Liouvillian to a specific order approximates the mean-force state but compromises positivity, and vice versa.

In this section we provide general formula for second order correction for mean-force Hamiltonian.

Theorem 3
.

The explicit form of second order correction for mean-force Hamiltonian is the following:

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{(\text{mf})}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\Omega\ D_{\mathrm{mf}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},\Omega)\ \gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\Omega),\ \ D_{\mathrm{mf}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},\Omega)=\frac{1}{\omega^{\prime}-\Omega}-\frac{(\omega-\omega^{\prime})(e^{\beta(\omega-\Omega)}-1)}{(\omega-\Omega)(\omega^{\prime}-\Omega)(e^{\beta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})}-1)}

(143)

The coefficients
\Upsilon_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})

can be also expressed in terms of the imaginary part of
\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)
(see Eq. (
124

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{(\text{mf})}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\frac{1}{e^{\beta\omega}-e^{\beta\omega^{\prime}}}\left(e^{\beta\omega}\mathcal{S}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega^{\prime})-e^{\beta\omega^{\prime}}\mathcal{S}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)+e^{\beta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})}\left(\mathcal{S}_{\beta\alpha}(-\omega^{\prime})-\mathcal{S}_{\beta\alpha}(-\omega)\right)\right).

(144)

Remark
.

From (

\Upsilon^{(\text{mf})}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\Upsilon^{(\text{mf})}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega^{\prime},\omega)

This can be also seen by writing

C.1
General method

We look for a solution of the equation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\varrho]=0,

(161)

where
\mathcal{L}
is the generator of the master equation and
\varrho
is its stationary state. We expand the generator and steady-state in the series, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\rho]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}[\rho]+\lambda^{2}\mathcal{L}_{2}[\rho]+\lambda^{4}\mathcal{L}_{4}[\rho]+\dots

(162)

\displaystyle\varrho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varrho_{0}+\lambda^{2}\varrho_{2}+\lambda^{4}\varrho_{4}+\dots

(163)

such that we have the following set of equations (for each order in
\lambda

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}[\varrho_{0}]=0

(164)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}[\varrho_{2}]+\mathcal{L}_{2}[\varrho_{0}]=0

(165)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}[\varrho_{4}]+\mathcal{L}_{2}[\varrho_{2}]+\mathcal{L}_{4}[\varrho_{0}]=0

(166)

\displaystyle\dots.

(167)

Hence we postulate the stationary state (in the Gibbs form):

\displaystyle\varrho=e^{-\beta(H_{0}+\lambda^{2}H^{(2)}_{\text{st}}+\lambda^{4}H^{(4)}_{\text{st}}}+\dots)=\varrho_{0}+\lambda^{2}\varrho_{2}+\lambda^{4}\varrho_{4}+\dots

(168)

such that

\displaystyle\varrho_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\beta H_{0}}

(169)

\displaystyle\varrho_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-e^{-\beta H_{0}}\int_{0}^{\beta}dt\ e^{tH_{0}}H^{(2)}_{\text{st}}e^{-tH_{0}}

(170)

\displaystyle\varrho_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-e^{-\beta H_{0}}\int_{0}^{\beta}dt\ e^{tH_{0}}H^{(4)}_{\text{st}}e^{-tH_{0}}+e^{-\beta H_{0}}\int_{0}^{\beta}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\ e^{t_{1}H_{0}}H^{(2)}_{\text{st}}e^{-t_{1}H_{0}}e^{t_{2}H_{0}}H^{(2)}_{\text{st}}e^{-t_{2}H_{0}}

(171)

In the following, we use the summation convention, i.e., the repeating indices are summed up.
We start with representation of the second-order correction in the basis of jump operators:

\displaystyle H^{(2)}_{\text{st}}=\Upsilon_{\alpha\beta}^{(\text{st})}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime}).

(172)

Note that contrary to mean force correction, the above form assumes that Bohr spectrum is nondegenerate. Indeed, then pairs of jump operators span linearly all the space of operators of the system.
In accordance, we have the following expression for
\varrho_{2}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\varrho_{2}=-e^{-\beta H_{0}}\Upsilon^{(\text{st})}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\int_{0}^{\beta}dt\ e^{tH_{0}}A_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})e^{-tH_{0}}=-\Upsilon^{(\text{st})}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\alpha(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)e^{-\beta H_{0}}A_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime}),

(173)

where we define:

\displaystyle\alpha(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\beta}dt\ e^{-t\omega}=\begin{cases}\frac{1-e^{-\beta\omega}}{\omega},&\omega\neq 0\\
\beta,&\omega=0\end{cases}

(174)

In general, we are going to transform the operator equations (
165
) and (
166
) into the algebraic ones. For this we define:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{k}[\varrho_{l}]=g^{(kl)}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})e^{-\beta H_{0}}A_{\alpha}(\omega_{1})A_{\beta}(\omega_{2})

(175)

for the second-order (such that
k+l=2
), and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{k}[\varrho_{l}]=g^{(kl)}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3},\omega_{4})e^{-\beta H_{0}}A_{\alpha}(\omega_{1})A_{\beta}(\omega_{2})A_{\gamma}(\omega_{3})A_{\delta}(\omega_{4})

(176)

for
k+l=4

\displaystyle\left(g^{(02)}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})+g^{(20)}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\right)e^{-\beta H_{0}}A_{\alpha}(\omega_{1})A_{\beta}(\omega_{2})=0

(177)

whereas for the fourth-order:

\displaystyle\left(g^{(04)}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3},\omega_{4})+g^{(22)}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3},\omega_{4})+g^{(40)}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3},\omega_{4})\right)e^{-\beta H_{0}}A_{\alpha}(\omega_{1})A_{\beta}(\omega_{2})A_{\gamma}(\omega_{3})A_{\delta}(\omega_{4})=0

\displaystyle[A_{\alpha}(\omega),H_{0}]=\omega A_{\alpha}(\omega)

(179)

from which we get:

\displaystyle A_{\alpha}(\omega)e^{-\beta H_{0}}=e^{-\beta\omega}e^{-\beta H_{0}}A_{\alpha}(\omega).

(180)

\displaystyle[A_{\alpha_{1}}(\omega_{1})A_{\alpha_{2}}(\omega_{2})\dots A_{\alpha_{2}}(\omega_{2}),H_{0}]=(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}+\dots+\omega_{n})A_{\alpha_{1}}(\omega_{1})A_{\alpha_{2}}(\omega_{2})\dots A_{\alpha_{2}}(\omega_{2}).

(270)

(277)

\displaystyle H_{I}(t_{1})\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}H_{I}(t_{2})+\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}H_{I}(t_{2})H_{I}(t_{1})\Big]

From here it can be seen that we have three kind of terms, namely
H_{I}^{2}\rho,H_{I}\rho H_{I},\rho H_{I}^{2}
. Let us consider each of those independently

\displaystyle H_{I}\rho H_{I}

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\big(\theta(t_{1}-t_{2})+\theta(t_{2}-t_{1})\big)\Tr_{R}\Big[H_{I}(t_{1})\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}H_{I}(t_{2})+H_{I}(t_{2})\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}H_{I}(t_{1})\Big]

(285)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\Tr_{R}\Big[H_{I}(t_{1})\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}H_{I}(t_{2})+H_{I}(t_{2})\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}H_{I}(t_{1})\Big]

(286)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\Tr_{R}\Big[H_{I}(t_{1})\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}H_{I}(t_{2})\Big]

(287)

where in the last step we used a change of variables on the second term, such that
t_{1}\leftrightarrow{}t_{2}
. Next, we consider the other two missing terms

\displaystyle H_{I}^{2}\rho

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\Big(\theta(t_{1}-t_{2})\Tr_{R}\Big[H_{I}(t_{1})H_{I}(t_{2})\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}\Big]+\theta(t_{2}-t_{1})\Tr_{R}\Big[H_{I}(t_{2})H_{I}(t_{1})\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}\Big]\Big)

(288)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\Big(\theta(t_{1}-t_{2})\Tr_{R}\Big[[H_{I}(t_{1}),H_{I}(t_{2})]\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}\Big]+\Tr_{R}\Big[H_{I}(t_{2})H_{I}(t_{1})\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}\Big]\Big)

(289)

\displaystyle\rho H_{I}^{2}

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\Big(\theta(t_{1}-t_{2})\Tr_{R}\Big[\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}H_{I}(t_{2})H_{I}(t_{1})\Big]+\theta(t_{2}-t_{1})\Tr_{R}\Big[H_{I}(t_{1})H_{I}(t_{2})\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}\Big]\Big)

(290)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\Big(\theta(t_{1}-t_{2})\Tr_{R}\Big[\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}[H_{I}(t_{2}),H_{I}(t_{1})]\Big]+\Tr_{R}\Big[\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}H_{I}(t_{1})H_{I}(t_{2})\Big]\Big)

(291)

In both cases the step taken from one line to the other was summing a zero so that the terms could be recast in that form, they were
{\pm\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\theta(t_{1}-t_{2})\Tr_{R}\Big[H_{I}(t_{2})H_{I}(t_{1})\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}\Big]}
in the first case and
{\pm\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\theta(t_{1}-t_{2})\Tr_{R}\Big[\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}H_{I}(t_{1})H_{I}(t_{2})\Big]}
in the second one. Regrouping all terms we have

\displaystyle\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\Bigg(\Tr_{R}\Big[H_{I}(t_{1})\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}H_{I}(t_{2})\Big]-\frac{1}{2}\big(\Tr_{R}\Big[\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}H_{I}(t_{1})H_{I}(t_{2})\Big]+\Tr_{R}\Big[H_{I}(t_{2})H_{I}(t_{1})\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}\Big]\Big)\Bigg)

(292)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\theta(t_{1}-t_{2})\Big(\Tr_{R}\Big[[H_{I}(t_{1}),H_{I}(t_{2})]\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}\Big]-\Tr_{R}\Big[\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}[H_{I}(t_{1}),H_{I}(t_{2})]\Big]\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\Bigg(\Tr_{R}\Big[H_{I}(t_{1})\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}H_{I}(t_{2})\Big]-\frac{1}{2}\big(\Tr_{R}\Big[\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}H_{I}(t_{1})H_{I}(t_{2})\Big]+\Tr_{R}\Big[H_{I}(t_{2})H_{I}(t_{1})\rho_{S}(0)\rho_{R}\Big]\Big)\Bigg)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle i\lambda^{2}[\Lambda(t),\rho_{S}(0)]

(293)

where:

\displaystyle\Lambda(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2i}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\theta(t_{1}-t_{2})\Tr_{R}\Big[[H_{I}(t_{1}),H_{I}(t_{2})]\rho_{R}\Big]

(294)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2i}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}sgn(t_{1}-t_{2})\Tr_{R}\Big[H_{I}(t_{1})H_{I}(t_{2})\rho_{R}\Big]

(295)

where we used
\theta(x)=\frac{1+sgn(x)}{2}
. Now, if we expand the interaction Hamiltonian in the interaction picture
{H_{I}=\sum_{w,k}e^{iwt}A_{k}(w)B_{k}=\sum_{w,k}e^{-iwt}A^{\dagger}_{k}(w)B_{k}}

\displaystyle\Lambda(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{w,w^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}sgn(t_{1}-t_{2})e^{i(wt_{1}-w^{\prime}t_{2})}A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(w)A_{\beta}(w^{\prime})\langle B_{\alpha}(t_{1})B_{\beta}(t_{2})\rangle_{R}

(296)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{w,w^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\Xi(w,w^{\prime},t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(w)A_{\beta}(w^{\prime})

So we obtain:

\displaystyle\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}[\rho_{S}(0)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\sum_{w,w^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\Xi(w,w^{\prime},t)[A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(w)A_{\beta}(w^{\prime}),\rho_{S}(0)]+\xi_{\alpha\beta}(w,w^{\prime},t)\Big(A_{\beta}(w^{\prime})\rho_{S}(0)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(w)-\frac{1}{2}\{A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(w)A_{\beta}(w^{\prime}),\rho_{S}(0)\}\Big),

where

\xi_{\alpha\beta}(w,w^{\prime},t)=\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}e^{i(wt_{1}-w^{\prime}t_{2})}\langle R_{\alpha}(t_{1})R_{\beta}(t_{2})\rangle.

(297)

We may rewrite this in terms of previously obtained quantities as:

\displaystyle\xi_{\alpha\beta}(w,w^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{t}d\omega e^{i(ws-w^{\prime}\omega)}\langle R_{\alpha}(s)R_{\beta}(w)\rangle

(298)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{s}^{t}d\omega e^{i(ws-w^{\prime}\omega)}\langle R_{\alpha}(s)R_{\beta}(w)\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{s}d\omega e^{i(ws-w^{\prime}\omega)}\langle R_{\alpha}(s)R_{\beta}(w)\rangle

(299)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}d\omega\int_{0}^{\omega}dse^{i(ws-w^{\prime}\omega)}\langle R_{\alpha}(s)R_{\beta}(w)\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{s}d\omega e^{i(ws-w^{\prime}\omega)}\langle R_{\alpha}(s)R_{\beta}(w)\rangle

(300)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{s}d\omega e^{i(w\omega-w^{\prime}s)}\langle R_{\alpha}(w)R_{\beta}(s)\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{s}d\omega e^{i(ws-w^{\prime}\omega)}\langle R_{\alpha}(s)R_{\beta}(w)\rangle

(301)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{s}d\omega e^{i(w\omega-w^{\prime}s)}\langle R_{\alpha}(w-s)R_{\beta}\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{s}d\omega e^{i(ws-w^{\prime}\omega)}\langle R_{\alpha}(s-w)R_{\beta}\rangle

(302)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{s}d\xi e^{i((w-w^{\prime})s-w\xi)}\langle R_{\alpha}(-\xi)R_{\beta}\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{s}d\xi e^{i((w-w^{\prime})s+\xi w^{\prime})}\langle R_{\alpha}(\xi)R_{\beta}\rangle

(303)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}dse^{i(w-w^{\prime})s}(\Gamma_{\beta\alpha}^{*}(w,s)+\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}(w^{\prime},s))

(304)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}dse^{i(w-w^{\prime})s}\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(w,w^{\prime},s)=\int_{0}^{t}ds\tilde{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}(w,w^{\prime},s)

(305)

Now, we can notice that the derivative of such coefficient corresponds to:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\xi_{\alpha\beta}(w,w^{\prime},t)=e^{i(w-w^{\prime})t}\gamma(w,w^{\prime},t)=\tilde{\gamma}(w,w^{\prime},t)

(306)

Furthermore from
Winczewski and Alicki 2021
we know that:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Xi_{\alpha\beta}(w,w^{\prime},t)=\frac{e^{i(w^{\prime}-w)t}}{2i}(\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}(w^{\prime},t)-\Gamma_{\beta\alpha}^{*}(w,t))=e^{i(w^{\prime}-w)t}S_{\alpha\beta}(w,w^{\prime},t)=\tilde{S}_{\alpha\beta}(w,w^{\prime},t)

(307)

One may then rewrite
\tilde{K}_{t}^{(2)}
as:

\displaystyle\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}[\rho]=\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}ds\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\left(i\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},s)[\rho,A_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})]+\tilde{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},s)\left(A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\rho A_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\omega)-\frac{1}{2}\{A_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime}),\rho\}\right)\right)

(308)

Appendix E
Comparison with
Lamb-shift Hamiltonian and the steady state - qubit case

In this section we consider the particular case of a qubit coupled to a bosonic bath given by

H=\frac{\omega_{0}}{2}\sigma_{z}+\sum_{k}\Omega_{k}a^{\dagger}_{k}a_{k}+S\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}(a_{k}+a_{k}^{\dagger})

(309)

where we take S to be a general interaction operator in the pauli basis:

S=x\sigma_{x}+y\sigma_{y}+z\sigma_{z}

(310)

This form of Hamiltonian with
y=0
has been studied previously in
Purkayastha et al. 2020
, where it was reported that such Hamiltonian have steady-state coherences. In this section, we see that the general framework presented here agrees with that result. Using equation (
142
) and this interaction, the second-order correction to the Hamiltonian takes the form:

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{cor}}^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix}z^{2}\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(0,0)+(x^{2}+y^{2})\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(\omega,\omega)&(x-iy)z(\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(0,-\omega)-\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(\omega,0))\\
(x+iy)z(\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(0,-\omega)-\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(\omega,0))&z^{2}\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(0,0)+(x^{2}+y^{2})\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(-\omega,-\omega)\end{bmatrix}

(311)

where
\mathrm{cor}
indicates the Lamb-shift (
LS
), steady-state (
st
) or mean-force (
mf
) correction. We can rewrite this correction as a linear combination of the Pauli Matrices such that:

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{cor}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A\mathbb{1}+B\sigma_{x}+C\sigma_{y}+D\sigma_{z}

(312)

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z^{2}\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(0,0)+\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{2}(\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(\omega,\omega)+\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(-\omega,-\omega))

(313)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle xz(\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(0,-\omega)-\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(\omega,0))

(314)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle yz(\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(0,-\omega)-\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(\omega,0))

(315)

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{{}_{\mathrm{cor}}2}(\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(\omega,\omega)-\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(-\omega,-\omega))

(316)

We can see how the different approaches differ qualitatively by looking at the structure of the different
\Upsilon_{\mathrm{cor}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
given by each approach. It is important to remark that any approach that performs the secular approximation will have both
B
and
C
equal to zero, meaning the correction will be diagonal and as such won’t be able to describe the off-diagonal elements of the steady states accordingly. While nonsecular approaches such as the Bloch-Redfield equation, will have non-diagonal corrections, leading to a more appropriate description of the off-diagonal elements of the correction as well as steady state coherences. Let us for a moment recall the structure of the Bloch-Redfield coefficients which are given by (
203
), simply substituting the appropriate frequencies for the qubit leads to:

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{\mathrm{LS}}(0,-\omega)-\Upsilon_{\mathrm{LS}}(\omega,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{S}(-\omega)-\mathcal{S}(\omega)}{2}+i\frac{\gamma(0)-(\gamma(\omega)+\gamma(-\omega))}{4}

(317)

and

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{\mathrm{LS}}(\omega,\omega)+\Upsilon_{\mathrm{LS}}(-\omega,-\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\omega)+\mathcal{S}(-\omega)

(318)

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{\mathrm{LS}}(\omega,\omega)-\Upsilon_{\mathrm{LS}}(-\omega,-\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\omega)-\mathcal{S}(-\omega)

(319)

Let us now compare this coefficient with the one obtained with the mean force approach. We will only be considering the off-diagonal of the correction:

\displaystyle K(\omega)=\Upsilon_{\mathrm{mf}}(0,-\omega)-\Upsilon_{\mathrm{mf}}(\omega,0)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\Omega\gamma(\Omega)C(\omega,\Omega),

(320)

where

\displaystyle C(\omega,\Omega)=\left(\frac{\omega^{2}(1-e^{-\beta\Omega})\coth[\frac{\beta\omega}{2}]+(1+e^{-\beta\Omega})\Omega\omega}{\Omega(\Omega^{2}-\omega^{2})}\right).

(321)

Additionally, our coefficients satisfy detailed balance conditions such that:

\displaystyle\gamma(-\Omega)=\gamma(\Omega)e^{-\beta\Omega},\ \ C(\omega,-\Omega)=C(\omega,\Omega)e^{\beta\Omega}

(322)

Using those we see that
\gamma(-\Omega)C(\omega,-\Omega)=\gamma(\Omega)C(\omega,\Omega)
and

\displaystyle K(\omega)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\Omega\ \gamma(\Omega)C(\omega,\Omega).

(323)

Let us now separate
\gamma(\Omega)
into its symmetric and anti-symmetric parts:

\displaystyle\gamma_{s}(\Omega)=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma(\Omega)+\gamma(-\Omega))=\frac{1}{2}(1+e^{-\beta\Omega})\gamma(\Omega)

(324)

\displaystyle\gamma_{a}(\Omega)=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma(\Omega)-\gamma(-\Omega))=\frac{1}{2}(1-e^{-\beta\Omega})\gamma(\Omega)

(325)

Then we may write:

\displaystyle K(\omega)=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\Omega\ \left(\frac{\omega^{2}\gamma_{a}(\Omega)\coth[\frac{\beta\omega}{2}]+\gamma_{s}(\Omega)\Omega\omega}{\Omega(\Omega^{2}-\omega^{2})}\right).

(326)

As mentioned before the system with
y=0
had been previously considered in
Purkayastha et al. 2020
. Let us now compare our results to those previously available in the literature. Their effective Hamiltonian is given by:

\displaystyle H_{S}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda^{2}f_{1}^{2}\Upsilon_{st}(0,0)-\frac{1}{2}(\omega-2\lambda^{2}f_{2}^{2}\Upsilon_{st}(\omega,\omega))&\lambda^{2}f_{1}f_{2}K(\omega)\\
\lambda^{2}f_{1}f_{2}K(\omega)&\lambda^{2}f_{1}^{2}\Upsilon_{st}(0,0)+\frac{1}{2}(\omega-2\lambda^{2}f_{2}^{2}\Upsilon_{st}(\omega,\omega))\end{bmatrix}

(327)

The couplings in this notation are
x=f_{2},z=f_{1}
and
y=0
. It is also putted
\omega^{\prime}=\omega-2\lambda^{2}f_{2}^{2}\Upsilon(\omega,\omega)
and
E_{0}=\lambda^{2}f_{1}^{2}\Upsilon(0,0)
such that:

\displaystyle H_{S}=\begin{bmatrix}E_{0}-\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{2}&\lambda^{2}f_{1}f_{2}K(\omega)\\
\lambda^{2}f_{1}f_{2}K(\omega)&E_{0}+\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{2}\end{bmatrix}

(328)

Then we may find that

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{x}\rangle=\frac{\Tr[\sigma_{x}e^{-\beta H_{S}}]}{\Tr[e^{-\beta H_{S}}]}=-x\frac{\tanh[\sqrt{x^{2}+z^{2}}\beta]}{\sqrt{x^{2}+z^{2}}}

(329)

where
x=\lambda^{2}f_{1}f_{2}K(\omega)
and
z=\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{2}
, and we expand it up to the second order of
\lambda
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{x}\rangle=-\frac{2x}{\omega^{\prime}}\tanh[\frac{\beta\omega}{2}]+O(\lambda^{3}).

(330)

In
Purkayastha et al. 2020
the authors also put
\omega^{\prime}=\omega
, such that

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{4\lambda^{2}f_{1}f_{2}}{\pi\omega}\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\Omega\ \left(\frac{\gamma_{s}(\Omega)\omega\tanh[\frac{\beta\omega}{2}]}{\Omega^{2}-\omega^{2}}+\frac{\omega^{2}\gamma_{a}(\Omega)}{\Omega(\Omega^{2}-\omega^{2})}\right)

(331)

Now let us compare
\gamma_{a,s}(\Omega)
with the correlation function for a bosonic bath:

\displaystyle f(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\Omega\ J(\Omega)\left(\coth[\frac{\beta\Omega}{2}]\cos(\Omega t)-i\sin(\Omega t)\right)

(333)

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\Omega\ e^{-i\Omega t}\gamma(\Omega)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\Omega\ \gamma(\Omega)\left(\cos(\Omega t)-i\sin(\Omega t)\right)

(334)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\Omega\ (\gamma_{s}(\Omega)+\gamma_{a}(\Omega))\left(\cos(\Omega t)-i\sin(\Omega t)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\Omega\ (\gamma_{s}(\Omega)-\gamma_{a}(\Omega))\left(\cos(\Omega t)+i\sin(\Omega t)\right)

(335)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\Omega\ \left(\gamma_{s}(\Omega)\cos(\Omega t)-i\gamma_{a}(\Omega)\sin(\Omega t)\right)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\Omega\ \gamma_{a}(\Omega)\left(\frac{\gamma_{s}(\Omega)}{\gamma_{a}(\Omega)}\cos(\Omega t)-i\sin(\Omega t)\right)

(336)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\Omega\ \gamma_{a}(\Omega)\left(\frac{1+e^{-\beta\Omega}}{1-e^{-\beta\Omega}}\cos(\Omega t)-i\sin(\Omega t)\right)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\Omega\gamma_{a}(\Omega)\left(\coth[\frac{\beta\Omega}{2}]\cos(\Omega t)-i\sin(\Omega t)\right)

(337)

According to this, we have the following relations:

\displaystyle\gamma_{a}(\Omega)=\pi J(\Omega)=\pi\omega_{a}(\Omega),\ \ \gamma_{s}(\Omega)=\pi J(\Omega)\coth[\frac{\beta\Omega}{2}]=\pi\omega_{s}(\Omega)

(338)

and the final result becomes:

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{4\lambda^{2}f_{1}f_{2}}{\omega}\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\Omega\ \left(\frac{\omega_{s}(\Omega)\omega\tanh[\frac{\beta\omega}{2}]}{\Omega^{2}-\omega^{2}}-\frac{\omega^{2}\omega_{a}(\Omega)}{\Omega(\Omega^{2}-\omega^{2})}\right).

(339)

On the other hand, in
Purkayastha et al. 2020
we have

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{x}\rangle=\frac{2\lambda^{2}f_{1}f_{2}}{\omega}[\Delta_{s}(\omega)\tanh[\frac{\beta\omega}{2}]+\Delta_{a}(\omega)-\Delta_{a}(0)]

(340)

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{s}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\Omega\ \omega_{s}(\Omega)\left(\frac{1}{\Omega+\omega}-\frac{1}{\Omega-\omega}\right)=-2\int_{0}^{\infty}d\Omega\frac{\omega_{s}(\Omega)\omega}{\Omega^{2}-\omega^{2}},

(341)

\displaystyle\Delta_{a}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\Omega\ \omega_{a}(\Omega)\left(\frac{1}{\Omega+\omega}+\frac{1}{\Omega-\omega}\right)=2\int_{0}^{\infty}d\Omega\frac{\omega_{a}(\Omega)\Omega}{\Omega^{2}-\omega^{2}},

(342)

\displaystyle\Delta_{a}(\omega)-\Delta_{a}(0)=2\int_{0}^{\infty}d\Omega\ \omega_{a}(\Omega)\frac{\Omega^{2}-(\Omega^{2}-\omega^{2})}{\Omega(\Omega^{2}-\omega^{2})}=2\int_{0}^{\infty}d\Omega\frac{\omega_{a}(\Omega)\omega^{2}}{\Omega(\Omega^{2}-\omega^{2})},

(343)

such that (
340
) is equal to (
339
).

Appendix F
Bloch-Redfield master equation (derivation)

We shall derive the Bloch-Redfield master equation in terms of
\tilde{\gamma}
(
113
) and
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
(
114
) coefficients starting from the von Neumann equation:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{R}_{t}[\tilde{\rho}(t)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}ds\ \Tr_{R}[H_{I}(t),[H_{I}(s),\tilde{\rho}(t)\otimes\gamma_{R}]],

(344)

which is derived according to the Born-Markov approximation. We expand commutators and put an explicit form of the interaction Hamiltonian (
99
):

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{R}_{t}[\tilde{\rho}(t)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}ds\ \Tr_{R}[H_{I}(t),[H_{I}(s),\tilde{\rho}(t)\otimes\gamma_{R}]]=\int_{0}^{t}ds\Tr_{R}\left([H_{I}(s)\tilde{\rho}(t)\otimes\gamma_{R},H_{I}(t)]-[\tilde{\rho}(t)\otimes\gamma_{R}\ H_{I}(s),H_{I}(t)]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}ds\Tr_{R}\big[H_{I}(s)\tilde{\rho}(t)\otimes\gamma_{R}H_{I}(t)-\tilde{\rho}(t)\otimes\gamma_{R}H_{I}(s)H_{I}(t)-H_{I}(t)H_{I}(s)\tilde{\rho}(t)\otimes\gamma_{R}+H_{I}(t)\tilde{\rho}(t)\otimes\gamma_{R}H_{I}(s)\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\int_{0}^{t}ds\big[A_{\alpha}(s)\tilde{\rho}(t)A_{\beta}(t)\langle R_{\beta}(t)R_{\alpha}(s)\rangle_{\gamma_{R}}+A_{\beta}(t)\tilde{\rho}(t)A_{\alpha}(s)\langle R_{\alpha}(s)R_{\beta}(t)\rangle_{\gamma_{R}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ -\tilde{\rho}(t)A_{\alpha}(s)A_{\beta}(t)\langle R_{\alpha}(s)R_{\beta}(t)\rangle_{\gamma_{R}}-A_{\beta}(t)A_{\alpha}(s)\tilde{\rho}(t)\langle R_{\beta}(t)R_{\alpha}(s)\rangle_{\gamma_{R}}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\int_{0}^{t}ds\left[\langle R_{\alpha}(t)R_{\beta}(s)\rangle_{\gamma_{R}}\left(A_{\beta}(s)\tilde{\rho}(t)A_{\alpha}(t)-A_{\alpha}(t)A_{\beta}(s)\tilde{\rho}(t)\right)\right]+\text{h.c.}

After introducing the jump operators (
101
), we get

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{R}_{t}[\tilde{\rho}(t)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\int_{0}^{t}ds\ e^{-i(\omega^{\prime}s+\omega t)}\langle R_{\alpha}(t)R_{\beta}(s)\rangle_{\gamma_{R}}\left(A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\tilde{\rho}(t)A_{\alpha}(\omega)-A_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\tilde{\rho}(t)\right)+\text{h.c.}

(345)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\int_{0}^{t}ds\ e^{i(\omega t-\omega^{\prime}s)}\langle R_{\alpha}(t)R_{\beta}(s)\rangle_{\gamma_{R}}\left(A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\tilde{\rho}(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)-A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\tilde{\rho}(t)\right)+\text{h.c.}

(346)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\ \tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)\left(A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\tilde{\rho}(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)-A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\tilde{\rho}(t)\right)+\text{h.c.}

(347)

where we put the definition:

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)\equiv\int_{0}^{t}ds\ e^{i(\omega t-\omega^{\prime}s)}\langle R_{\alpha}(t)R_{\beta}(s)\rangle_{\gamma_{R}}.

(348)

This can be further simplified to the form:

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)=e^{i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})t}\int_{0}^{t}ds\ e^{i\omega^{\prime}s}\langle R_{\alpha}(s)R_{\beta}(0)\rangle_{\gamma_{R}}\equiv e^{i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})t}\ \Gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega^{\prime},t)

(349)

where we changed the variables in the integrand
s\to t-s
and use the property
\langle R_{\alpha}(t)R_{\beta}(s)\rangle_{\gamma_{R}}=\langle R_{\alpha}(t-s)R_{\beta}(0)\rangle_{\gamma_{R}}
.
Next, we rewritten the hermitian conjugate part in the form:

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\tilde{\Gamma}^{*}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)\left(A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\tilde{\rho}(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)-A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\tilde{\rho}(t)\right)^{\dagger}

(350)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\tilde{\Gamma}^{*}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)\left(A_{\alpha}(\omega)\tilde{\rho}(t)A^{\dagger}_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})-\tilde{\rho}(t)A^{\dagger}_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})A_{\alpha}(\omega)\right)

(351)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\tilde{\Gamma}^{*}_{\beta\alpha}(\omega^{\prime},\omega,t)\left(A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\tilde{\rho}(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)-\tilde{\rho}(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\right)

(352)

Finally, we get

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{R}_{t}[\tilde{\rho}(t)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\ (\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)+\tilde{\Gamma}^{*}_{\beta\alpha}(\omega^{\prime},\omega,t))A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\tilde{\rho}(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)

(353)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\left(\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\tilde{\rho}(t)+\tilde{\Gamma}^{*}_{\beta\alpha}(\omega^{\prime},\omega,t)\tilde{\rho}(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\right)

(354)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\left(\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\tilde{\rho}(t)+\tilde{\Gamma}^{*}_{\beta\alpha}(\omega^{\prime},\omega,t)\tilde{\rho}(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\right)

(355)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\left(\tilde{\Gamma}^{*}_{\beta\alpha}(\omega^{\prime},\omega,t))A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\tilde{\rho}(t)+\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)\tilde{\rho}(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\right)

(356)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\left(\tilde{\Gamma}^{*}_{\beta\alpha}(\omega^{\prime},\omega,t))A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\tilde{\rho}(t)+\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)\tilde{\rho}(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\right)

(357)

where the last two lines sum up to zero. After rearranging terms and putting the definition (

) and (

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{R}_{t}[\tilde{\rho}(t)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\left[i\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)[\tilde{\rho}(t),A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})]+\tilde{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},t)\left(A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\tilde{\rho}(t)A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)-\frac{1}{2}\{A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime}),\tilde{\rho}(t)\}\right)\right].

(358)

Appendix G
The cumulant equation in the Schrödinger pircture

The cumulant equation is originally derived in the interaction picture. In order to transform the cumulant equation into the Schrödinger picture we start with a simple observation.

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}(t)=e^{i[H_{0},\cdot]t}{\rho}(t).

(359)

The super-operator in the r.h.s. of the equation above has its unique inverse, and
\tilde{\rho}(0)={\rho}(0)
, therefore:

\displaystyle\rho(t)=e^{-i[H_{0},\cdot]t}e^{\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}}\rho(0)=e^{K^{(2)}_{t}}\rho(0).

(360)

The r.h.s. of the equation above defines the Schrödinger picture cumulant eqaution super-operator
K^{(2)}_{t}
:

\displaystyle e^{K^{(2)}_{t}}=e^{-i[H_{0},\cdot]t}e^{\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}}.

(361)

The explicit form of
K^{(2)}_{t}
can be found with the aid of the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff (BCH) formula.

\displaystyle e^{X}e^{Y}=\exp{X+Y+\frac{1}{2} [X,Y]+\frac{1}{12}[X,[X,Y]]-\frac{1}{12}[Y,[X,Y]]+\cdots}.

(362)

We observe that in a generic case the super-operator
K^{(2)}_{t}
in not of the GKSL form. This follows from the presence of multi-commutator terms in the formula (
362
). These terms do not vanish, as
[K^{(2)}_{t},H_{0}]
is not central. Therefore,
e^{K^{(2)}_{t}}
is an example of a one-parameter family of CPTP dynamical maps that are not of the GKSL form.

Appendix H
The cumulant equation in the differential form

We start this Section with the following Lemma on the properties of the derivative of an exponential map.

Lemma 4
.

The derivative of the exponential map is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}e^{X(t)}=\left(\frac{e^{[X(t),\cdot]}-\mathbb{1}}{[X(t),\cdot]}\frac{dX(t)}{dt}\right)e^{X(t)}.

(363)

Proof.

The proof of the above relation is identical to the proof of Theorem 5 in reference
Hunacek 2008
up to small modifications.
∎

Using Lemma
4
we instantly obtain the cumulant equation in the differential form:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{\rho}(t)=\left(\frac{e^{[\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t},\cdot]}-\mathbb{1}}{[\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t},\cdot]}\frac{d\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}}{dt}\right)\tilde{\rho}(t)=\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{C}\tilde{\rho}(t).

(364)

This result can also be obtained with integration of equation (
359
). When truncated to the leading order, the above formula reproduces the Bloch-Redfield master equation since
\frac{d\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}}{dt}=\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{R}_{t}
.

Equation (
364
) can be readily transformed into the Schrödinger picture. This is done with iterative application of the
e^{\pm iH_{0}t}
operators to the jump operators
A_{i}(\omega)
inside
\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t}
super-operator.

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(-i[H_{0},\cdot]+e^{-i[H_{0},\cdot]t}\frac{e^{[\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t},\cdot]}-\mathbb{1}}{[\tilde{K}^{(2)}_{t},\cdot]}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{R}_{t}e^{i[H_{0},\cdot]t}\right)\rho(t)

(365)

\displaystyle=\left(-i[H_{0},\cdot]+\frac{e^{[\bar{K}^{(2)}_{t},\cdot]}-\mathbb{1}}{[\bar{K}^{(2)}_{t},\cdot]}\bar{\mathcal{L}}^{R}_{t}\right)\rho(t)=\mathcal{L}_{t}^{C}{\rho}(t),

(366)

where

\displaystyle\bar{K}^{(2)}_{t}[\rho]

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}ds\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha\beta}e^{i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})(s-t)}\left(i{\mathcal{S}}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},s)[\rho,A_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})]+\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime},s)\left(A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\rho A_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\omega)-\frac{1}{2}\{A_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\omega)A_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime}),\rho\}\right)\right),

(367)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{L}}^{R}_{t}={\mathcal{L}}^{R}_{t}+i[H_{0},\cdot].

(368)

Moreover, we observe that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}^{C}=\mathcal{L}^{R}_{t}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4}).

(369)

Unfortunately, the problem of the long-time limit of the above super-operator was not resolved yet. This situation makes determination of the higher-order corrections to the steady state of the cumulant equation even more involving.

Equation (
366
) can be compared with the differential form of the Schrödinger picture cumulant equation obtained with Lemma
4
and the super-operator in equation (
361
).

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\rho}(t)=\left(\frac{e^{[{K}^{(2)}_{t},\cdot]}-\mathbb{1}}{[{K}^{(2)}_{t},\cdot]}\frac{d{K}^{(2)}_{t}}{dt}\right){\rho}(t)={\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{C}{\rho}(t).

(370)

The above formula has only a formal meaning, as the
{K}^{(2)}_{t}
super-operator does not possess a closed form formula. We present it only for the curiosity of the reader.

Appendix I
Extracting the correction from a density matrix

To extract the second order correction from the reaction coordinate, we started by obtaining the steady state density matrix, which is given by a Gibbs state of the form:

\rho_{\lambda}=\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{\mathcal{Z}}.

(371)

By taking the logarithm, one obtains

\log(\mathcal{Z})+\log(\rho_{\lambda})=-\beta H.

(372)

We then expand
H

\displaystyle H=H_{0}+\lambda^{2}H_{2}+\lambda^{4}H_{4}+\dots

(373)

substituting in Eq. (
372
)

\displaystyle\log(\mathcal{Z})+\log(\rho_{\lambda})=-\beta(H_{0}+\lambda^{2}H_{2}+\lambda^{4}H_{4}+\dots)

(374)

We now impose our gauge
\Tr[H]=0
.
Then tracing out both sides we obtain

\displaystyle\log(\mathcal{Z})=-\frac{1}{d}\Tr[\log(\rho_{\lambda})]

(375)

By substituting back into Eq.(
374
) and rearranging terms one obtains

H_{2}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\Big[\frac{1}{\beta}\Big(\frac{1}{d}\Tr[\log(\rho_{\lambda})]-\log(\rho_{\lambda})\Big)-\Big(H_{0}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4})\Big)\Big]

(376)

Finally as
\lambda
approaches zero

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}H_{2}=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\Big[\frac{1}{\beta}\Big(\frac{1}{d}\Tr[\log(\rho_{\lambda})]-\log(\rho_{\lambda})\Big)-H_{0}\Big]

(377)
