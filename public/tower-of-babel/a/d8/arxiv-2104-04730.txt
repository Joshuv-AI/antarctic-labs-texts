1.
Foreword

Let
\mathscr{S}_{x}
be the set of squares centered at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The Lebesgue density theorem states that if
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is Lebesgue summable, then

(
*
)

f(x)=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathscr{S}_{x}\\
\rmdiam S\to 0^{+}\end{subarray}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{S}fd\mathscr{L}^{2},

for
\mathscr{L}^{2}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
This consequence of the Vitali covering theorem fails if
\mathscr{S}_{x}
is replaced with
\mathscr{R}_{x}
, the set of rectangles centered at
x
of arbitrary direction and eccentricity.
It fails even for some indicator function
f=\mathbbm{1}_{A}
.
Indeed, Nikodým
[
12
]
defined a set
A\subseteq[0,1]\times[0,1]
of full measure with the following property: For every
x\in A
, there exists a line
L(x)
such that
A\cap L(x)=\{x\}
.
In fact, Nikodým’s example shows that the Lebesgue density theorem fails if we replace
\mathscr{S}_{x}
with
\mathscr{R}_{x}^{L}
, the set of rectangles of arbitrary eccentricity, centered at
x
, and one side of which is parallel to
L(x)
.
Furthermore
[
4
, Chap. IV Theorem 3.5]
, replacing
A
by a nonnegligible subset of
A
, one may choose
L
to be continuous with respect to
x
.
Yet, if
L
is constant, then the Lebesgue density theorem with respect to
\mathscr{R}_{x}^{L}
holds, for
p
-summable functions
f
,
1<p\leqslant\infty
, by virtue of a theorem of Zygmund, though the corresponding version of the Vitali covering theorem fails, according to an example of H. Bohr
[
4
, Chap. IV Theorem 1.1]
.
This raises the question: What regularity condition of
x\mapsto L(x)
guarantees that the Lebesgue density theorem holds with respect to
\mathscr{R}_{x}^{L}
for, say, functions that are square summable?
The following version is a conjecture reportedly
[
11
]
attributed to Zygmund.

Assume that
x\mapsto L(x)
is a Lipschitzian field of lines, with
x\in L(x)
, and
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is square summable.
Is it true that

f(x)=\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{L(x)\cap\mathbf{B}(x,r)}fd\mathscr{H}^{1},

for
\mathscr{L}^{2}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
?
Here,
\mathscr{H}^{1}
is the 1-dimensional Hausdorff measure.

E.M. Stein raised the singular integral variant of this conjecture.
Both have received much attention from the harmonic analysis community.
To the author’s knowledge, most results recorded so far in the literature, via the maximal function approach, assume some extra regularity property of
L
– namely, that
L
be
C^{1}
together with a hypothesis on the variation of the derivative – see, for instance,
[
1
]
and
[
11
]
.

In this paper we offer a novel approach – a kind of change of variable based on an appropriate fibration and the coarea formula.
This allows for treating the case when
L
is Lipschitzian, without further restriction.
We obtain the following lower density bound for indicator functions.

Let
x\mapsto L(x)
be a Lipschitzian field of lines, with
x\in L(x)
, and let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be Lebesgue measurable.
Then,

\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathscr{H}^{1}(A\cap B(x,r)\cap L(x))}{2r}\geqslant\frac{1}{4},

for
\mathscr{L}^{2}
-almost every
x\in A
.

Incidentally, the following corollary – a nonparallel version of Fubini theorem – seems to be new as well.

The set
A
is Lebesgue negligible if and only if
\mathscr{H}^{1}(A\cap L(x))=0
, for
\mathscr{L}^{2}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Our results hold in any dimension and codimension.

2.
Sketch of proof

Let
A
be a subset of Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geqslant 2
, and let
\mathscr{L}^{n}
be the Lebesgue outer measure.
We start by considering the following weak question: Can one tell whether
A
is Lebesgue negligible from the knowledge only of its trace on each member of some given collection of “lower dimensional” subsets
\Gamma_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in I
.
One expects that if
A\cap\Gamma_{i}
is “negligible in the dimension of
\Gamma_{i}
”, for each
i\in I
, then
\mathscr{L}^{n}(A)=0
.
Of course, a necessary condition is that the sets
\Gamma_{i}
cover almost all of
A
, i.e.,
\mathscr{L}^{n}(A\setminus\cup_{i\in I}\Gamma_{i})=0
.
Consider, for instance,
n=2
,
I=\mathbb{R}
, and
\Gamma_{t}=\{t\}\times\mathbb{R}
, for
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the collection of all vertical lines in the plane.
It is not true in general that if
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
A\cap\Gamma_{t}
is a singleton, for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\mathscr{L}^{2}(A)=0
.
There exist, indeed, functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
whose graph
A
satisfies
\mathscr{L}^{2}(A)>0
– see, e.g.,
[
10
, Chapter 2 Theorem 4]
for an example due to Sierpiński.
In order to rule out such examples, we will henceforth assume that
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is Borel measurable.
In that case, the theorem of Fubini, together with the invariance of the Lebesgue measure under orthogonal transformations, imply the following.
Given an integer
1\leqslant m\leqslant n-1
, if
(\Gamma_{i})_{i\in I}
is the collection of all
m
-dimensional affine subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of some fixed direction, and if
\mathscr{H}^{m}(A\cap\Gamma_{i})=0
for all
i\in I
, then
\mathscr{L}^{n}(A)=0
.
Here,
\mathscr{H}^{m}
denotes the
m
-dimensional Hausdorff measure.
A special feature of this collection
(\Gamma_{i})_{i\in I}
is that it partitions
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, its members being the level sets
f^{-1}\{y\}
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
, of a “nice map”
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
, indeed, an orthogonal projection.
This is an occurrence of the following more general situation when
f
and its leaves
f^{-1}\{y\}
are allowed to be nonlinear.
The coarea formula due to Federer
[
8
]
asserts that if
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
is Lipschitzian and
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is Borel measurable, then

\int_{A}Jf(x)d\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-m}}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(A\cap f^{-1}\{y\}\right)d\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(y).

Thus, if the Jacobian coarea factor
Jf
is positive,
\mathscr{L}^{n}
-almost everywhere in
A
, then the collection
\left(f^{-1}\{y\}\right)_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n-m}}
is suitable for detecting whether or not
A
is Lebesgue null.
At
\mathscr{L}^{n}
-almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the map
f
is differentiable, by virtue of Rademacher’s theorem, and

Jf(x)=\sqrt{\left|\det\left(Df(x)\circ Df(x)^{*}\right)\right|}=\left\|\wedge_{n-m}Df(x)\right\|,

see
[
6
, Chapter 3 §4]
and
[
9
, 3.2.1 and 3.2.11]
.

In this paper, we focus on the case when
\Gamma_{i}
,
i\in I
, are affine subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, but not necessarily members of a partition of the ambient space.
Specifically, we assume that with each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is associated an
m
-dimensional affine subspace
\mathbf{W}(x)
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing
x
.
Given a Borel set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the question whether

(1)

\textit{If }\mathscr{H}^{m}(A\cap\mathbf{W}(x))=0\text{, for all }x\in A\textit{, then }\mathscr{L}^{n}(A)=0\,,

has a negative answer, in view of Nikodým’s set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
evoked in the previous section.
Indeed, corresponding to this set
A
, there exists a field of lines
x\mapsto\mathbf{W}(x)
such that
A\cap\mathbf{W}(x)=\{x\}
, for all
x\in A
.
In this context, a selection theorem due to von Neumann implies that (possibly considering a smaller, non Lebesgue null, Borel subset of
A
) the correspondence
x\mapsto\mathbf{W}(x)
can be chosen to be Borel measurable (see
3.19
) and, in turn, it can be chosen to be continuous, according to Lusin’s theorem.
Nonetheless, when
\mathbf{W}
is Lipschitzian, the situation improves, as illustrated in our theorem below;
\mathbb{G}(n,m)
is the Grassmannian manifold consisting of
m
-dimensional vector subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem
. —

Assume
\mathbf{W}_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{G}(n,m)
is Lipschitzian and
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is Borel measurable. The following are equivalent.

(A)

\mathscr{L}^{n}(A)=0
.

(B)

For
\mathscr{L}^{n}
almost every
x\in A
,
\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(A\cap(x+\mathbf{W}_{0}(x))\right)=0
.

(C)

For
\mathscr{L}^{n}
almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(A\cap(x+\mathbf{W}_{0}(x))\right)=0
.

As should be apparent from the discussion above, one difficulty stands with the fact that the affine
m
-planes
\mathbf{W}(x)=x+\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)
may not be disjointed.
Nevertheless, they locally are, in the following sense.
Given
x_{0}\in A
there exist a neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
and Lipschitzian maps
\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that
\mathbf{w}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m}(x)
is an orthonomal frame spanning
\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)
, for
x\in U
, and the map
\Phi:(V_{x_{0}}\cap U)\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}:(\xi,t)\mapsto\xi+\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i}(\xi)
is a lipeomorphism of a neighborhood of
x_{0}
onto its image – here,
V_{x_{0}}=x_{0}+V
and
V\in\mathbb{G}(n,n-m)
is close to
\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})^{\perp}
.
This, and applications of Fubini’s theorem, yield
(B)\Rightarrow(A)
in the theorem above (see
8.2
).

However, we aim at obtaining a quantative version of this result, that the change of variable just described does not seem to provide.
A natural route is to reduce the problem to applying the coarea formula, by spreading out the
\mathbf{W}(x)
’s in a disjointed way, in a higher dimensional space – i.e., adding a variable
u\in\mathbf{W}(x)
to the given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and considering
\mathbf{W}(x)
as a fiber above the base space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We thus define

\Sigma=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\cap\{(x,u):x\in E\text{ and }u\in\mathbf{W}(x)\},

where
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is Borel measurable.
This set is
(n+m)
-rectifiable, owing to the Lipschitz continuity of
\mathbf{W}
.
It is convenient to assume that
\mathscr{L}^{n}(E)<\infty
, so that

(2)

\phi_{E}(B)=\int_{E}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(B\cap\mathbf{W}(x)\right)d\mathscr{L}^{n}(x),

B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, is a locally finite Borel measure
3.16
.
Now,
\Sigma
was precisely set up so that, for each
x\in E
,

\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(\Sigma\cap\pi_{2}^{-1}(B)\cap\pi_{1}^{-1}\{x\}\right)=\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(B\cap\mathbf{W}(x)\right),

where
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
denote the projections of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
to the
x
and
u
variable, respectively.
Abbreviating
\Sigma_{B}=\Sigma\cap\pi_{2}^{-1}(B)
, the coarea formula yields

(3)

\phi_{E}(B)=\int_{\Sigma_{B}}J_{\Sigma}\pi_{1}d\mathscr{H}^{n+m}.

A simple calculation
5.4
shows that
J_{\Sigma}\pi_{2}>0
,
\mathscr{H}^{n+m}
-almost everywhere on
\Sigma_{B}
. Since also

(4)

\int_{\Sigma_{B}}J_{\Sigma}\pi_{2}d\mathscr{H}^{n+m}=\int_{B}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(\Sigma_{B}\cap\pi_{2}^{-1}\{u\}\right)d\mathscr{L}^{n}(u),

the implication
(A)\Rightarrow(C)
above should now be clear: Letting
B=A
and
E=\mathbf{B}(0,R)
, one infers from hypothesis (A) and (
4
) that
\mathscr{H}^{n+m}(\Sigma_{B})=0
, whence,
\phi_{\mathbf{B}(0,R)}(A)=0
, by (
3
), and, in turn, conclusion (C) ensues from (
2
).

In order to establish that
(B)\Rightarrow(A)
by using this new change of variable, we need to observe
5.2
that
J_{\Sigma}\pi_{1}>0
,
\mathscr{H}^{n+m}
-almost everywhere, and, ideally, to show that
\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(\Sigma_{B}\cap\pi_{2}^{-1}\{u\}\right)>0
, for almost every
u\in B
.
This last part involves some difficulty.
To understand this, we let
m=n-1
, in order to keep the notations short.
Now
u\in\mathbf{W}(x)
iff
u-x\in\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)
iff
\langle\mathbf{v}_{0}(x),x-u\rangle=0
, where
\mathbf{v}_{0}(x)\in\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)^{\perp}
is, say, a unit vector.
Abbreviating
g_{u}(x)=\langle\mathbf{v}_{0}(x),x-u\rangle
, we infer that

\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(\Sigma_{B}\cap\pi_{2}^{-1}\{u\}\right)=\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(E\cap g_{u}^{-1}\{0\}\right).

The problem remains that two of the nonlinear
m
sets
E\cap g_{u}^{-1}\{0\}
and
E\cap g_{u^{\prime}}^{-1}\{0\}
may intersect, thereby preventing another application of the coarea formula when looking out for their lower bound.
Yet, we already know that

\phi_{E}(B)=\int_{B}\mathscr{Z}_{E}\mathbf{W}d\mathscr{L}^{n},

where
\mathscr{Z}_{E}\mathbf{W}
is a Radon-Nikodým derivative (see
5.6
) and also that
(\mathscr{Z}_{E}\mathbf{W})(u)
is comparable to
\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(E\cap g_{u}^{-1}\{0\}\right)
(see
5.8
).
Adding an extra variable
y
to the fibered space
\Sigma
,
5.10
, we improve on this by showing that

(\mathscr{Z}_{E}\mathbf{W})(u)\geqslant\boldsymbol{\eta}(n,m)\liminf_{j}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{-j^{-1}}^{j^{-1}}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(E\cap g_{u}^{-1}\{y\}\right)d\mathscr{L}^{1}(y)=\boldsymbol{\eta}(n,m)(\mathscr{Y}_{E}\mathbf{W})(u),

where the last equality defines
\mathscr{Y}_{E}\mathbf{W}
, and
\boldsymbol{\eta}(n,m)>0
is a lower bound for the coarea Jacobian factor of the restriction to the fibered space of the projection
(x,u,y)\mapsto(x,y)
(see
5.12
and
5.16
).
We are reduced to showing that
\mathscr{Y}_{E}\mathbf{W}>0
, almost everywhere.
The reason why this holds is the following.
Fix a Borel set
Z\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r>0
.
Let
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)
be the cylindrical box consisting of those
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\left|P_{\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})}(x-x_{0})\right|\leqslant r
and
\left|P_{\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})^{\perp}}(x-x_{0})\right|\leqslant r
.
We want to find a lower bound for

\int_{Z\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)}\left(\mathscr{Y}_{Z\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)}\right)(u)d\mathscr{L}^{n}(u).

To this end, we fix
z\in\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})\cap\mathbf{B}(0,r)
and we let
V_{z}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\cap\{x_{0}+z+s\mathbf{v}_{0}(x_{0}):-r\leqslant s\leqslant r\}
denote the corresponding vertical line segment.
According to Fubini’s theorem we are reduced to estimating

\int_{V_{z}}\left(\mathscr{Y}_{Z\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)}\right)(u)d\mathscr{H}^{1}(u).

By virtue of Vitali’s covering theorem, we can find a disjointed family of line segments
I_{1},I_{2},\ldots
, covering almost all
V_{z}
, such that the above integral nearly equals

\sum_{k}\mathscr{H}^{1}(I_{k})\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{I_{k}}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(Z\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\cap g_{u_{k}}^{-1}\{y\}\right)d\mathscr{L}^{1}(y)\\
\cong\sum_{k}\int_{Z\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\cap g_{u_{k}}^{-1}(I_{k})}\left|\nabla g_{u_{k}}(x)\right|d\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)\cong\mathscr{L}^{n}\left(Z\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\right),

where the first near equality follows from the coarea formula, and the second one because
\left|\nabla g_{u_{k}}\right|\cong 1
at small scales (see
3.12
) and the “nonlinear horizontal stripes”
g_{u_{k}}^{-1}(I_{k})
are nearly pairwise disjoint.
Verification of these claims takes up sections 6 and 7.
Now, we reach a contradiction if
Z=\mathbb{R}^{n}\cap\{\mathscr{Y}_{E}\mathbf{W}=0\}
is assumed to have
\mathscr{L}^{n}(Z)>0
, and
x_{0}
is a point of density of
Z
.

Along this path, we have, in fact, achieved a quantitative version of these observations.
Indeed, assuming that
\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathscr{H}^{m}(A\cap\mathbf{B}(x,r)\cap\mathbf{W}(x))}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}(m)r^{m}}<\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}(n,m)}{2^{m}}
, for
\mathscr{L}^{n}
positively many
x\in A
, we start by choosing
E\subseteq A
with the same property, whose diameter is small enough for the various estimates above to hold, and we refer to Egoroff’s theorem to select
\varepsilon>0
so that the set

Z=E\cap\left\{\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathscr{H}^{m}(A\cap\mathbf{B}(x,r)\cap\mathbf{W}(x))}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}(m)r^{m}}<(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}(n,m)}{(2+\varepsilon)^{m}}\right\}

has positive
\mathscr{L}^{n}
measure.
Thus, for
0<\hat{\varepsilon}\ll\varepsilon
, there exists
x_{0}\in Z
and there exists
r>0
as small as we please so that

\left(1-\hat{\varepsilon}\right)\mathscr{L}^{m}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\right)\leqslant\mathscr{L}^{n}\left(Z\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\right)\lesssim\int_{Z\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)}\frac{\mathscr{Y}_{Z\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)}\mathbf{W}(u)}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}(m)r^{m}}d\mathscr{L}^{n}(u)\\
\lesssim\frac{1}{\boldsymbol{\eta}(n,m)}\int_{Z\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)}\frac{\mathscr{H}^{m}(Z\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\cap\mathbf{W}(x))}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}(m)r^{m}}d\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)\\
\leqslant\frac{1}{\boldsymbol{\eta}(n,m)}\int_{Z\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)}\frac{\mathscr{H}^{m}(Z\cap\mathbf{B}(x,2r+o(r))\cap\mathbf{W}(x))}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}(m)r^{m}}d\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)\\
\lesssim\frac{1}{\boldsymbol{\eta}(n,m)}\int_{Z\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)}(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}(n,m)}{(2+\varepsilon)^{m}}d\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)\leqslant(1-C\varepsilon)\mathscr{L}^{n}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\right),

where the symbol
\lesssim
means that we loose a multiplicative factor
\lambda>1
as close as we wish to 1, according to the scale
r
.
The above contradiction proves our main result
8.4
, namely,

Theorem
. —

Assume
\mathbf{W}_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{G}(n,m)
is Lipschitzian and
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is Borel measurable. Abbreviating
\mathbf{W}(x)=x+\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)
, it follows that

\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathscr{H}^{m}\big(A\cap\mathbf{B}(x,r)\cap\mathbf{W}(x)\big)}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}(m)r^{m}}\geqslant\frac{1}{2^{n}},

for
\mathscr{L}^{n}
-almost every
x\in A
.

3.
Preliminaries

3.1
. —

In this paper,
1\leqslant m\leqslant n-1
are integers.
The ambient space is
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The canonical inner product of
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted
\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle
and the corresponding Euclidean norm of
x
is
|x|
.
If
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we let
\mathscr{B}(S)
be the
\sigma
-algebra of Borel measurable subsets of
S
.

3.2
Hausdorff measure. —

We let
\mathscr{L}^{n}
be the Lebesgue outer measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}(n)=\mathscr{L}^{n}(\mathbf{B}(0,1))
.
For
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we abbreviate
\zeta^{m}(S)=\boldsymbol{\alpha}(m)2^{-m}(\rmdiam S)^{m}
.
Given
0<\delta\leqslant\infty
, we call
\delta
-cover of
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
a finite or countably infinite family
(S_{j})_{j\in J}
of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
A\subseteq\cup_{j\in J}S_{j}
and
\rmdiam S_{j}\leqslant\delta
, for every
j\in J
.
We define

\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\delta}(A)=\inf\left\{\sum_{j\in J}\zeta^{m}(S_{j}):(S_{j})_{j\in J}\text{ is a $\delta$-cover of $A$ }\right\}

and
\mathscr{H}^{m}(A)=\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\delta}(A)=\sup_{\delta>0}\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\delta}(A)
.
Thus,
\mathscr{H}^{m}
is the
m
-dimensional Hausdorff outer measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(1)

If
(K_{k})_{k}
is a sequence of nonempty compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
converging in Hausdorff distance to
K
, then
\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\infty}(K)\geqslant\limsup_{k}\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\infty}(K_{k})
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, choose an
\infty
-cover
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of
K
such that
\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\infty}(K)+\varepsilon\geqslant\sum_{j}\zeta^{m}(S_{j})
.
Since
\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\zeta^{m}(\mathbf{U}(S_{j},r))=\zeta^{m}(S_{j})
, for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we can choose an open set
U_{j}
containing
S_{j}
such that
\zeta^{m}(U_{j})\leqslant\varepsilon 2^{-j}+\zeta^{m}(S_{j})
.
Since
U=\cup_{j}U_{j}
is open, there exists a positive integer
k_{0}
such that
K_{k}\subseteq U
, whenever
k\geqslant k_{0}
.
Thus, in that case
(U_{j})_{j}
is an
\infty
-cover of
K_{k}
and, therefore,
\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\infty}(K_{k})\leqslant\sum_{j}\zeta^{m}(U_{j})\leqslant 2\varepsilon+\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\infty}(K)
.
Take the limit superior of the left hand side, as
k\to\infty
, and then let
\varepsilon\to 0
.

(2)

For all
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, one has
\mathscr{L}^{m}(A)=\mathscr{H}^{m}(A)=\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\infty}(A)
.

It suffices to note that
\mathscr{H}^{m}(A)\geqslant\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\infty}(A)\geqslant\mathscr{L}^{m}(A)\geqslant\mathscr{H}^{m}(A)
.
The first inequality is trivial; the second one follows from the isodiametric inequality
[
9
, 2.10.33]
; the last one is a consequence of the Vitali covering theorem
[
6
, Chapter 2 §2 Theorem 2]
.

(3)

If
W\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an
m
-dimensional affine subspace and
A\subseteq W
, then
\mathscr{H}^{m}(A)=\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\infty}(A)
.

Let
\mathfrak{H}^{m}
denote the
m
-dimensional Hausdorff outer measure in the metric space
W
.
In other words,

\mathfrak{H}^{m}_{\delta}(A)=\inf\left\{\sum_{j\in J}\zeta^{m}(S_{j}):(S_{j})_{j\in J}\text{ is a $\delta$- cover of $A$ and $S_{j}\subseteq W$, for all $j\in J$}\right\},

and
\mathfrak{H}^{m}(A)=\sup_{\delta>0}\mathfrak{H}^{m}_{\delta}(A)
.
It is elementary to observe that
\mathfrak{H}^{m}(A)=\mathscr{H}^{m}(A)
and that
\mathfrak{H}^{m}_{\infty}(A)=\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\infty}(A)
.
Now, if
f:W\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is an isometry, then
\mathscr{H}^{m}(A)=\mathfrak{H}^{m}(A)=\mathscr{H}^{m}(f(A))=\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\infty}(f(A))=\mathfrak{H}^{m}_{\infty}(A)=\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\infty}(A)
, where the third equality follows from claim (2) above.

3.3
Coarea formula. —

We recall two versions of the coarea formula.
First, if
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
\mathscr{L}^{n}
-measurable and
f:A\to\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
is Lipschitzian, then
\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\to[0,\infty]:y\mapsto\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(A\cap f^{-1}\{y\}\right)
is
\mathscr{L}^{n-m}
- measurable and

\int_{A}Jf(x)d\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-m}}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(A\cap f^{-1}\{y\}\right)d\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(y).

Here, the coarea Jacobian factor is well-defined
\mathscr{L}^{n}
-almost everywhere, according to Rademacher’s theorem, and equals

Jf(x)=\sqrt{\left|\det\left(Df(x)\circ Df(x)^{*}\right)\right|}=\left\|\wedge_{n-m}Df(x)\right\|;

see, for instance,
[
6
, Chapter 3 §4]
.

Secondly, if
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
is
\mathscr{H}^{n}
-measurable and countably
(\mathscr{H}^{n},n)
-rectifiable and if
f:A\to\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
is Lipschitzian, then
\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\to[0,\infty]:y\mapsto\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(A\cap f^{-1}\{y\}\right)
is
\mathscr{L}^{n-m}
-measurable and

\int_{A}J_{A}f(x)d\mathscr{H}^{n}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-m}}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(A\cap f^{-1}\{y\}\right)d\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(y).

In order to state a formula for the coarea Jacobian factor
J_{A}f(x)
of
f
relative to
A
, we consider a point
x\in A
, where
A
admits an approximate
n
-dimensional tangent space
T_{x}A
and
f
is differentiable at
x
along
A
.
Letting
L:T_{x}A\to\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
denote the derivative of
f
at
x
, we have

J_{A}f(x)=\sqrt{\left|\det\left(L\circ L^{*}\right)\right|}=\left\|\wedge_{n-m}L\right\|;

see, for instance,
[
9
, 3.2.22]
.

In both cases, it is useful to recall the following.
If
L:V\to V^{\prime}
is a linear map between two inner product spaces
V
and
V^{\prime}
, then

(5)

\|\wedge_{k}L\|=\sup\left\{\langle\wedge_{k}L,\xi\rangle:\xi\in\wedge_{k}V\text{ and }|\xi|=1\right\}.

On the one hand,
\|\wedge_{k}L\|\leqslant\|L\|^{k}

[
9
, 1.7.6]
and
\|L\|\leqslant\rmLip f
, with
L
as above.
On the other hand, if
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
are linearly independent vectors of
V
, then

(6)

\|\wedge_{k}L\|\geqslant\frac{|L(v_{1})\wedge\ldots\wedge L(v_{k})|}{|v_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{k}|}.

Finally, we observe that both coarea formulæ hold true when
f
is merely locally Lipschitzian, according to the monotone convergence theorem.

3.4
Grassmannian. —

We let
\mathbb{G}(n,m)
be the set whose members are the
m
-dimensional vector subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
With
W\in\mathbb{G}(n,m)
, we associate
P_{W}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the orthogonal projection on
W
.
We give
\mathbb{G}(n,m)
the structure of a compact metric space by letting
d(W_{1},W_{2})=\|P_{W_{1}}-P_{W_{2}}\|
, where
\|\cdot\|
is the operator norm.
If
W\in\mathbb{G}(n,m)
, then
W^{\perp}\in\mathbb{G}(n,n-m)
satisfies
P_{W}+P_{W^{\perp}}=\rmid_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, therefore,
\mathbb{G}(n,m)\to\mathbb{G}(n,n-m):W\mapsto W^{\perp}
is an isometry.
The bijective correspondence
\varphi:\mathbb{G}(n,m)\to\rmHom(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n}):W\mapsto P_{W}
identifies
\mathbb{G}(n,m)
with the submanifold
M_{n,m}=\rmHom(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\{L:L\circ L=L\,,\,L^{*}=L\text{, and }\rmtrace L=m\}
.
There exists an open neighborhood
V
of
M_{n,m}
in
\rmHom(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
and a Lipschitzian retraction
\rho:V\to M_{n,m}
, according, for instance, to
[
9
, 3.1.20]
.
Therefore, if
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbf{W}_{0}:S\to\mathbb{G}(n,m)
is Lipschitzian, then there exist an open neighborhood
U
of
S
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a Lipschitzian extension
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{0}:U\to\mathbb{G}(n,m)
of
\mathbf{W}_{0}
.
Indeed,
\varphi\circ\mathbf{W}_{0}
admits a Lipschitzian extension
Y:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\rmHom(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
– see, e.g.,
[
9
, 2.10.43]
– and it suffices to let
U=Y^{-1}(V)
and
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{0}=\rho\circ\left(Y|_{U}\right)
.

3.5
Orthonormal frames. —

We let
\mathbb{V}(n,m)
be the set of orthonormal
m
-frames in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{V}(n,m)=(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{m}\cap\{(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}):\text{ the family }w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}\text{ is orthonormal}\}
.
We will consider it as a metric space, with its structure inherited from
(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{m}
.

3.6
. —

Let
\mathscr{V}\subseteq\mathbb{G}(n,m)
be a nonempty closed set such that
\rmdiam\mathscr{V}<1
. There exists a Lipschitzian map
\Xi:\mathscr{V}\to\mathbb{V}(n,m)
such that
W=\rmspan\{\Xi_{1}(W),\ldots,\Xi_{m}(W)\}
, for every
W\in\mathscr{V}
.

Proof.

Pick arbitrarily
W_{0}\in\mathscr{V}
.
If
W\in\mathscr{V}
, then the map
W_{0}\to W:w\mapsto P_{W}(w)
is bijective: If
w\in W_{0}\setminus\{0\}
, then
\left|P_{W}(w)-w\right|=\left|P_{W}(w)-P_{W_{0}}(w)\right|<|w|
, thus,
P_{W}(w)\neq 0
.
Letting
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}
be an arbitrary basis of
W_{0}
, it follows that, for each
W\in\mathscr{V}
, the vectors
w_{i}(W)=P_{W}(w_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, constitute a basis of
W
.
Furthermore, the maps
w_{i}:\mathscr{V}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
are Lipschitzian:
\left|w_{i}(W)-w_{i}(W^{\prime})\right|=\left|P_{W}(w_{i})-P_{W^{\prime}}(w_{i})\right|\leqslant d(W,W^{\prime})|w_{i}|
.
We apply the Gram-Schmidt process:

\overline{w}_{1}(W)=w_{1}(W)\quad\text{ and }\quad\overline{w}_{i}(W)=w_{i}(W)-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\langle w_{i}(W),\overline{w}_{j}(W)\rangle\overline{w}_{j}(W)\,,\,\,i=2,\ldots,m,

so that
\overline{w}_{1}(W),\ldots,\overline{w}_{m}(W)
is readily an orthogonal basis of
W
depending upon
W
in a Lipschitzian way.
Since each
\left|\overline{w}_{i}\right|
is bounded away from zero on
\mathscr{V}
, the formula
\Xi_{i}(W)=\left|\overline{w}_{i}(W)\right|^{-1}\overline{w}_{i}(W)
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, defines
\Xi
with the required property.
∎

3.7
. —

There exists a Borel measurable map
\Xi:\mathbb{G}(n,m)\to\mathbb{V}(n,m)
with the property that
W=\rmspan\{\Xi_{1}(W),\ldots,\Xi_{m}(W)\}
, for every
W\in\mathbb{G}(n,m)
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{G}(n,m)
is compact, it can partitioned into finitely many Borel measurable sets
\mathscr{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{V}_{J}
, each having diameter bounded by
1/2
.
Define
\Xi
, piecewise, to coincide on
\mathscr{V}_{j}
with
\Xi_{j}
associated to
\rmClos\mathscr{V}_{j}
in
3.6
,
j=1,\ldots,J
.
∎

3.8
. —

Assume
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
x_{0}\in S
, and
\mathbf{W}_{0}:S\to\mathbb{G}(n,m)
is Lipschitzian. There exist an open neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and Lipschitzian maps
\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m},\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the following properties.

(1)

For every
x\in U
, the family
\mathbf{w}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m}(x),\mathbf{v}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}(x)
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(2)

For every
x\in S\cap U
, one has

\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)=\rmspan\{\mathbf{w}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m}(x)\}

and

\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)^{\perp}=\rmspan\{\mathbf{v}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}(x)\}.

Proof.

We let
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{0}:\widehat{U}\to\mathbb{G}(n,m)
be a Lipschitzian extension of
\mathbf{W}_{0}
, where
\widehat{U}
is an open neighborhood of
S
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(recall
3.4
).
Abbreviate
W_{0}:=\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})
.
Define
\mathscr{V}=\mathbb{G}(n,m)\cap\left\{W:d(W,W_{0})<1/4\right\}
and
U=\widehat{U}\cap\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{0}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})
.
Apply
3.6
to
\rmClos\mathscr{V}
and denote by
\Xi
the resulting Lipschitzian map
\mathscr{V}\to(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{m}
.
Next, define
\mathscr{V}^{\perp}=\mathbb{G}(n,n-m)\cap\left\{W^{\perp}:W\in\mathscr{V}\right\}
, apply
3.6
to
\rmClos\mathscr{V}^{\perp}
, and denote by
\Xi^{\perp}
the resulting Lipschitzian map
\mathscr{V}^{\perp}\to(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n-m}
.
Letting
\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)=\left(\Xi_{i}\circ\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{0}\right)(x)
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, and
\mathbf{v}_{i}(x)=\left(\Xi^{\perp}_{i}\circ\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{0}\right)(x)
,
i=1,\ldots,n-m
, completes the proof.
∎

3.9
. —

Assume
\mathbf{W}_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{G}(n,m)
is Borel measurable. There exist Borel measurable maps
\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m},\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the following properties.

(1)

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the family
\mathbf{w}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m}(x),\mathbf{v}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}(x)
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(2)

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one has

\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)=\rmspan\{\mathbf{w}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m}(x)\}

and

\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)^{\perp}=\rmspan\{\mathbf{v}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}(x)\}.

Choose
\Xi:\mathbb{G}(n,m)\to\mathbb{V}(n,m)
and
\Xi^{\perp}:\mathbb{G}(n,n-m)\to\mathbb{V}(n,n-m)

3.7

∎

3.10
Definition of
\mathbf{W}(x)
. —

The typical situation that arises in the remaining part of this paper is that we are given a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a Lipschitzian map
\mathbf{W}_{0}:S\to\mathbb{G}(n,m)
, and
x_{0}\in S
.
We will represent
\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)
and
\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\perp}(x)
in a neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
as in
3.8
.
We will then further reduce the size of
U
several times, in order that various conditions be met.
With no exception, we will denote as
\mathbf{W}(x)=x+\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)
the affine subspace containing
x
, of direction
\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)
, whenever
\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)
is defined.

3.11
Definition of
g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}
and lower bound of its coarea factor. —

Given an open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a Lipschitzian map
\mathbf{v}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define
g_{\mathbf{v},u}:U\to\mathbb{R}
by the formula

g_{\mathbf{v},u}(x)=\langle\mathbf{v}(x),x-u\rangle.

Clearly,
g_{\mathbf{v},u}
is locally Lipschitzian.
If
\mathbf{v}
is differentiable at
x\in U
, then so is
g_{\mathbf{v},u}
and, for every
h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one has

(7)

Dg_{\mathbf{v},u}(x)(h)=\langle\nabla g_{\mathbf{v},u}(x),h\rangle=\langle D\mathbf{v}(x)(h),x-u\rangle+\langle\mathbf{v}(x),h\rangle.

Next, we assume that we are given Lipschitzian maps
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We define
g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
by the formula

g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}(x)=\left(g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},u}(x),\ldots,g_{\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}(x)\right).

It is locally Lipschitzian as well.
The relevance of
g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}
stems from the following observation, assuming that
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}
are associated with
\mathbf{W}_{0}
and
\mathbf{W}
as in
3.8
and
3.10
:

(8)

\begin{split}u\in\mathbf{W}(x)&\iff u-x\in\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)\\
&\iff\langle\mathbf{v}_{i}(x),u-x\rangle=0\text{, for all }i=1,\ldots,n-m\\
&\iff g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}(x)=0\\
&\iff x\in g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}^{-1}\{0\}.\end{split}

In fact,
|g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}(x)|=\left|P_{\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)^{\perp}}(x-u)\right|
.

Abbreviate
g=g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}
.
If each
\mathbf{v}_{i}
is differentiable at
x\in U
, and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then

Dg(x)(h)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}Dg_{\mathbf{v}_{i},u}(x)(h)e_{i},

where, here and elsewhere,
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n-m}
denotes the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
.
Thus, if
\mathbf{v}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}(x)
constitute an orthonormal family in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then

Dg_{\mathbf{v}_{i},u}(x)(\mathbf{v}_{j}(x))=\delta_{i,j}+\varepsilon_{i,j}(x,u),

where

(9)

|\varepsilon_{i,j}(x,u)|=\left|\langle D\mathbf{v}_{i}(x)(\mathbf{v}_{j}(x)),x-u\rangle\right|\leqslant\left(\rmLip\mathbf{v}_{i}\right)|x-u|,

according to (
7
), and, in turn,

Dg(x)(\mathbf{v}_{j}(x))=\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}\left(\delta_{i,j}+\varepsilon_{i,j}(x,u)\right)e_{i}.

This allows for a lower bound of the coarea factor of
g
at
x
as follows.

\begin{split}\left\|\wedge_{n-m}Dg(x)\right\|&\geqslant\left|Dg(x)(\mathbf{v}_{1}(x))\wedge\ldots\wedge Dg(x)(\mathbf{v}_{n-m}(x))\right|\\
&=\left|\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}\left(\delta_{i,1}+\varepsilon_{i,1}(x,u)\right)e_{i}\right)\wedge\ldots\wedge\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}\left(\delta_{i,n-m}+\varepsilon_{i,n-m}(x,u)\right)e_{i}\right)\right|\\
&=\left|\det\left(\delta_{i,j}+\varepsilon_{i,j}(x,u)\right)_{i,j=1,\ldots,n-m}\right|.\end{split}

In view of (
9
), we obtain the next lemma.

3.12
. —

Given
\Lambda>0
and
0<\varepsilon<1
, there exists
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{3.12}(n,\Lambda,\varepsilon)>0
with the following property. Assume that

(1)

U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

(2)

\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
are Lipschitzian;

(3)

\mathbf{v}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}(x)
is an orthonormal family, for every
x\in U
.

If

(4)

\rmLip\mathbf{v}_{i}\leqslant\Lambda
, for each
i=1,\ldots,n-m
;

(5)

\rmdiam\left(U\cup\{u\}\right)\leqslant\boldsymbol{\delta}_{3.12}(n,\Lambda,\varepsilon)
;

then

Jg_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}(x)\geqslant 1-\varepsilon,

at
\mathscr{L}^{n}
-almost every
x\in U
.

3.13
Definition of
\pi_{u}
and its relation with
g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}
. —

With
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we associate

\pi_{u}:\mathbb{V}(n,n-m)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-m}:(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n-m},x)\mapsto\left(\langle\xi_{1},x-u\rangle,\ldots,\langle\xi_{n-m},x-u\rangle\right).

When
(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n-m})\in\mathbb{V}(n,n-m)
is fixed, we also abbreviate as
\pi_{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n-m},u}
the map
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
defined by
\pi_{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n-m},u}(x)=\pi_{u}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n-m},x)
.
It is then rather useful to observe that, in the context described in
3.8
and
3.10
, the following holds:

(10)

\pi_{\mathbf{v}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}(x),u}^{-1}\left\{g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}(x)\right\}=\mathbf{W}(x).

Indeed,

\begin{split}h\in\mathbf{W}(x)&\iff h-x\in\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)\\
&\iff\langle\mathbf{v}_{i}(x),h-x\rangle=0\text{, for all }i=1,\ldots,n-m\\
&\iff\langle\mathbf{v}_{i}(x),h-u\rangle=\langle\mathbf{v}_{i}(x),x-u\rangle\text{, for all }i=1,\ldots,n-m\\
&\iff\pi_{\mathbf{v}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}(x),u}(h)=g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}(x).\end{split}

In the sequel, we will sometimes abbreviate
\xi=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n-m})\in\mathbb{V}(n,n-m)
.
It also helps to notice that, for given
\xi\in\mathbb{V}(n,n-m)
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
, the set
\pi_{\xi,u}^{-1}\{y\}
is an
m
-dimensional affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

3.14
. —

If
B\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then

h_{B}:\mathbb{V}(n,n-m)\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\to[0,\infty]:(\xi,y)\mapsto\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(B\cap\pi_{\xi,u}^{-1}\{y\}\right)

is Borel measurable.

Proof.

We start by showing that, when
B
is compact,
h_{B}
is upper semicontinuous.
Thus, if
((\xi_{k},y_{k}))_{k}
is a sequence in
\mathbb{V}(n,n-m)\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
that converges to
(\xi,y)
, we ought to show that

(11)

\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\infty}(K)\geqslant\limsup_{k}\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\infty}(K_{k}),

where
K=B\cap\pi_{\xi,u}^{-1}\{y\}
and
K_{k}=B\cap\pi_{\xi_{k},u}^{-1}\{y_{k}\}
.
This is indeed equivalent to the same inequality with
\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\infty}
replaced by
\mathscr{H}^{m}
, according to
3.2
(3) and the last sentence of
3.13
.
Considering, if necessary, a subsequence of
(K_{k})_{k}
we may assume that none of the compact sets
K_{k}
is empty and that the limit superior in (
11
) is a limit.
Since the set of nonempty compact subsets of the compact set
B
, equipped with the Hausdorff metric, is compact, the sequence
(K_{k})_{k}
admits a subsequence (denoted the same way) converging to a compact set
L\subseteq B
.
Given
z\in L
, there are
z_{k}\in K_{k}
converging to
z
.
Thus,
\pi_{u}(\xi,z)=\lim_{k}\pi_{u}(\xi_{k},z_{k})=\lim_{k}y_{k}=y
.
In other words,
z\in K
.
Therefore,
\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\infty}(K)\geqslant\mathscr{H}^{m}_{\infty}(L)
, so that (
11
) follows from
3.2
(1).

Next, we fix
r>0
and we abbreviate
\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\{B:h_{B\cap\mathbf{B}(0,r)}\text{ is Borel measurable}\}
.
Thus, we have just shown that
\mathscr{A}
contains the collection
\mathscr{F}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of all closed subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Observe that if
(B_{j})_{j}
is an nondecreasing sequence in
\mathscr{A}
and
B=\cup_{j}B_{j}
, then
h_{B\cap\mathbf{B}(0,r)}=\lim_{j}h_{B_{j}\cap\mathbf{B}(0,r)}
pointwise, thus,
B\in\mathscr{A}
.
In particular,
\mathbb{R}^{n}\in\mathscr{A}
.
Furthermore, if
B,B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{A}
and
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
, then
h_{B\setminus B^{\prime}}=h_{B}-h_{B^{\prime}}
, because all measures involved are finite; indeed,
h_{B\cap\mathbf{B}(0,r)}(\xi,y)\leqslant\boldsymbol{\alpha}(m)r^{m}
, for all
(\xi,y)
.
Accordingly,
B\setminus B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{A}
.
This means that
\mathscr{A}
is a Dynkin class.
Since
\mathscr{F}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a
\pi
-system,
\mathscr{A}
contains the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathscr{F}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, i.e.,
\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

[
3
, Theorem 1.6.2]
.
Finally, if
B\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
h_{B}=\lim_{j}h_{B\cap\mathbf{B}(0,j)}
pointwise, whence
h_{B}
is Borel measurable.
∎

3.15
. —

Assume
B\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mathbf{W}_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{G}(n,m)
is Borel measurable. The following function is Borel measurable.

\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty]:x\mapsto\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(B\cap\mathbf{W}(x)\right).

Proof.

Let
h_{\mathbf{W},B}
denote this function.
Let
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be Borel measurable maps associated with
\mathbf{W}_{0}
in
3.9
.
Fix
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
arbitrarily.
Define

\Upsilon:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{V}(n,n-m)\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}:x\mapsto\left(\mathbf{v}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}(x),g_{\mathbf{v}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}(x),u}(x)\right)

and notice that

h_{\mathbf{W},B}=h_{B}\circ\Upsilon

(where
h_{B}
is the function associated with
B
and
u
in
3.14
), according to (
10
).
One notes that
\Upsilon
is Borel measurable; whence, the conclusion ensues from
3.14
.
∎

3.16
Definition of
\phi_{E,\mathbf{W}}
. —

Let
\mathbf{W}_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{G}(n,m)
be Borel measurable and
E\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be such that
\mathscr{L}^{n}(E)<\infty
.
For each
B\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we define

\phi_{E,\mathbf{W}}(B)=\int_{E}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(B\cap\mathbf{W}(x)\right)d\mathscr{L}^{n}(x).

This is well-defined, according to
3.15
.
It is easy to check that
\phi_{E,\mathbf{W}}
is a locally finite – hence,
\sigma
-finite – Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
; indeed,
\phi_{E,\mathbf{W}}(B)\leqslant\boldsymbol{\alpha}(m)(\rmdiam B)^{m}\mathscr{L}^{n}(E)
.

To close this section, we discuss the relevance of
\phi_{E,\mathbf{W}}
to the problem of existence of “nearly Nikodým sets”.

3.17
Definition of Nearly Nikodým set. —

Let
E\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We say that
B\in\mathscr{B}(E)
is
nearly
m
-Nikodým in
E
if

(1)

\mathscr{L}^{n}(B)>0
;

(2)

For
\mathscr{L}^{n}
-almost each
x\in E
, there is
W\in\mathbb{G}(n,m)
such that
\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(B\cap(x+W)\right)=0
.

In case
n=2
,
m=1
, and
E=[0,1]\times[0,1]
, the existence of such a
B
(with
\mathscr{L}^{2}(B)=1
) was established by Nikodým
[
12
]
, see also
[
4
, Chapter 8]
.
For arbitrary
n\geqslant 2
and
m=n-1
, the existence of such a
B
was established by Falconer
[
7
]
.
In fact, in both cases, these authors established the stronger condition that, for every
x\in B
,
\mathscr{H}^{m}(B\cap(x+W))=0
can be replaced by
B\cap(x+W)=\{x\}
.
Thus, in case
1\leqslant m<n-1
, letting
B
be a set exhibited by Falconer, if
x\in B
and
W\subseteq\mathbb{G}(n,n-1)
is such that
B\cap(x+W)=\{x\}
, then picking arbitrarily
V\in\mathbb{G}(n,m)
such that
V\subseteq W
, we see that
B\cap(x+V)=\{x\}
.
Whence,
B
is also nearly
m
-Nikodým in
B
.

Assuming that
\mathbf{W}_{0}:E\to\mathbb{G}(n,m)
is Borel measurable, we say that
B\in\mathscr{B}(E)
is
nearly
m
-Nikodým in
E
relative to
\mathbf{W}
if

(1)

\mathscr{L}^{n}(B)>0
;

(2)

For
\mathscr{L}^{n}
-almost each
x\in E
, one has
\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(B\cap\mathbf{W}(x)\right)=0
.

3.18
. —

Let
E\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
have finite
\mathscr{L}^{n}
measure and
\mathbf{W}_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{G}(n,m)
be Borel measurable. The following are equivalent.

(1)

\mathscr{L}^{n}|_{\mathscr{B}(E)}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\phi_{E,\mathbf{W}}|_{\mathscr{B}(E)}
.

(2)

There does not exist a nearly
m
-Nikodým set in
E
relative to
\mathbf{W}
.

Proof.

A set
B\in\mathscr{B}(E)
such that
\phi_{E,\mathbf{W}}(B)=0
and
\mathscr{L}^{n}(B)>0
is, by definition, a nearly
m
-Nikodým set relative to
\mathbf{W}
.
Condition (1) is equivalent to their nonexistence.
∎

3.19
. —

Assume that
E\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and that
B\in\mathscr{B}(E)
is nearly
m
-Nikodým. The following hold.

(1)

There exists
\mathbf{W}_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{G}(n,m)
Borel measurable such that
B
is nearly
m
- Nikodým in
E
relative to
\mathbf{W}
.

(2)

There exists
C\subseteq B
compact and
\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{G}(n,m)
continuous such that
C
is nearly
m
-Nikodým in
C
relative to
\overline{\mathbf{W}}
.

Proof.

Define a Borel measurable map
\boldsymbol{\xi}:\mathbb{G}(n,m)\to\mathbb{V}(n,n-m)
by
\boldsymbol{\xi}(W)=\Xi\left(W^{\perp}\right)
, where
\Xi:\mathbb{G}(n,n-m)\to\mathbb{V}(n,n-m)
is as in
3.7
.
Choose arbitrarily
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and define a Borel measurable map

\Upsilon:E\times\mathbb{G}(n,m)\to\mathbb{V}(n,n-m)\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\\
(x,W)\mapsto\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}(W),\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}(W),x-u\rangle,\ldots,\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n-m}(W),x-u\rangle\right).

Similarly to (
10
), observe that

x+W=\pi_{\boldsymbol{\xi}(W),u}^{-1}\left\{\left(\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}(W),x-u\rangle,\ldots,\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n-m}(W),x-u\rangle\right)\right\},

for every
(x,W)\in E\times\mathbb{G}(n,m)
.
We infer from
3.14
that

h_{B}\circ\Upsilon:E\times\mathbb{G}(n,m)\to[0,\infty]:(x,W)\mapsto\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(B\cap(x+W)\right)

is Borel measurable.
Thus, the set

\mathscr{E}=E\times\mathbb{G}(n,m)\cap\left\{(x,W):\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(B\cap(x+W)\right)=0\right\}

is Borel measurable as well.
The set
N=E\cap\{x:\mathscr{E}_{x}=\emptyset\}
is coanalytic and
\mathscr{L}^{n}(N)=0
, by assumption.
By virtue of von Neumann’s selection theorem
[
13
, 5.5.3]
, there exists a universally measurable map
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{0}:E\setminus N\to\mathbb{G}(n,m)
such that
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{0}(x)\in\mathscr{E}_{x}
, for every
x\in E\setminus N
, i.e.,
\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(B\cap\left(x+\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{0}(x)\right)\right)=0
.
We extend
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{0}
to be an arbitrary constant on
N\cup(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus E)
.
This makes
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{0}
an
\mathscr{L}^{n}
-measurable map defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Therefore, it is equal
\mathscr{L}^{n}
-almost everywhere to a Borel measurable map
\mathbf{W}_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{G}(n,m)
.
This proves (1).

In order to prove (2), we recall
3.4
, specifically, the retraction
\rho:V\to M_{n,m}
and the homeomorphic identification
\varphi:\mathbb{G}(n,m)\to M_{n,m}
.
Owing to the compactness of
M_{n,m}
, there are finitely many open balls
U_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,J
, whose closures are contained in
V
and whose union contains
M_{n,m}
.
Since
\mathscr{L}^{n}(B)>0
, there exists
j\in\{1,\ldots,J\}
such that
\mathscr{L}^{n}\left(B\cap E_{j}\right)>0
, where
E_{j}=\left(\varphi\circ\mathbf{W}_{0}\right)^{-1}(U_{j})
.
It follows from Lusin’s theorem
[
9
, 2.5.3]
that there exists a compact set
C\subseteq B\cap E_{j}
such that
\mathscr{L}^{n}(C)>0
and the restriction
\mathbf{W}_{0}|_{C}
is continuous.
The map
\varphi\circ\mathbf{W}_{0}|_{C}
takes its values in the closed ball
\rmClos U_{j}
, therefore, it admits a continuous extension
Y_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\rmClos U_{j}\subseteq V
.
Letting
\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{0}=\varphi^{-1}\circ\rho\circ Y_{0}
completes the proof.
∎

\mathbf{W}(x)=x+\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)
, for each

.

(6)

\Lambda>0
.

,

.

5.
Two fibrations

5.1
A fibered space associated with
E,B,\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m}
. —

We define

F:E\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:(x,t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})\mapsto\left(x,x+\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)\right)

as well as

\Sigma=F(E\times\mathbb{R}^{m})=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\cap\left\{(x,u):x\in E\text{ and }u\in\mathbf{W}(x)\right\}.

We will oftentimes abbreviate
(x,t)=(x,t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})
.
It is obvious that
F
is locally Lipschitzian and, therefore,
\Sigma
is countably
(n+m)
-rectifiable and
\mathscr{H}^{n+m}
-measurable.
We also consider the two canonical projections

\pi_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}:(x,u)\mapsto x\quad\text{ and }\quad\pi_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}:(x,u)\mapsto u,

as well as the set

\Sigma_{B}=\Sigma\cap\pi_{2}^{-1}(B)=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\cap\left\{(x,u):x\in E\text{ and }u\in B\cap\mathbf{W}(x)\right\},

which also is, clearly, countably
(n+m)
-rectifiable and
\mathscr{H}^{n+m}
-measurable.
With the prospect of applying the coarea formula to
\Sigma_{B}
and
\pi_{1}
, and to
\Sigma_{B}
and
\pi_{2}
, respectively, we observe that, for each fixed
x\in E
,

\Sigma_{B}\cap\pi_{1}^{-1}\{x\}=\{x\}\times\big(\mathbb{R}^{n}\cap\{u:u\in B\cap\mathbf{W}(x)\}\big),

so that

(12)

\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(\Sigma_{B}\cap\pi_{1}^{-1}\{x\}\right)=\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(B\cap\mathbf{W}(x)\right),

and that, for each fixed
u\in B
,

\begin{split}\Sigma_{B}\cap\pi_{2}^{-1}\{u\}&=\big(\mathbb{R}^{n}\cap\{x:x\in E\text{ and }u\in\mathbf{W}(x)\}\big)\times\{u\}\\
&=\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\cap\left\{x:x\in E\cap g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}^{-1}\{0\}\right\}\right)\times\{u\},\end{split}

according to (
8
), so that

(13)

\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(\Sigma_{B}\cap\pi_{2}^{-1}\{u\}\right)=\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(E\cap g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}^{-1}\{0\}\right),

whenever
u\in B
.
It now follows from the coarea formula that

(14)

\int_{\Sigma_{B}}J_{\Sigma}\pi_{1}d\mathscr{H}^{n+m}=\int_{E}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(B\cap\mathbf{W}(x)\right)d\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)=\phi_{E,\mathbf{W}}(B)

and

(15)

\int_{\Sigma_{B}}J_{\Sigma}\pi_{2}d\mathscr{H}^{n+m}=\int_{B}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(E\cap g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}^{-1}\{0\}\right)d\mathscr{L}^{n}(u).

For these formulæ to be useful, we need to establish bounds for the coarea Jacobian factors
J_{\Sigma}\pi_{1}
and
J_{\Sigma}\pi_{2}
.
In order to do so, we notice that if
\Sigma\ni(x,u)=F(x,t)
,
F
is differentiable at
(x,t)
, i.e., each
\mathbf{w}_{i}
is differentiable at
x
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, then the approximate tangent space
T_{(x,u)}\Sigma
exists and is generated by the following
n+m
vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\begin{split}\sum_{p=1}^{n}\langle w_{j},e_{p}\rangle\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{p}}(x,t)&=\left(w_{j},w_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}D\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)(w_{j})\right)\,,\,j=1,\ldots,n,\\
\frac{\partial F}{\partial t_{k}}(x,t)&=\left(0,\mathbf{w}_{k}(x)\right)\,,\,k=1,\ldots,m,\end{split}

where
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
is an arbitrary basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
As usual,
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
denotes the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

5.2
Coarea Jacobian factor of
\pi_{1}
. —

For
\mathscr{H}^{n+m}
-almost every
(x,u)\in\Sigma
, one has

\left(1+m^{2}\Lambda^{2}|x-u|^{2}\right)^{-\frac{m}{2}}\left(2+2m\Lambda|x-u|+m^{2}\Lambda^{2}|x-u|^{2}\right)^{-\frac{n-m}{2}}\leqslant J_{\Sigma}\pi_{1}(x,u)\leqslant 1.

Proof.

We recall
3.3
.
That the right hand inequality be valid follows from
\rmLip\pi_{1}=1
.
Regarding the left hand inequality, fix
(x,u)=F(x,t)
such that
F
is differentiable at
(x,t)
and let
L:T_{(x,u)}\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the restriction of
\pi_{1}
to
T_{(x,u)}\Sigma
.
Define
w_{j}=\mathbf{w}_{j}(x)
,
j=1,\ldots,m
, and
w_{j}=\mathbf{v}_{j-m}(x)
,
j=m+1,\ldots,n
.
Put

v_{j}=\sum_{p=1}^{n}\langle w_{j},e_{p}\rangle\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{p}}(x,t)-\frac{\partial F}{\partial t_{j}}(x,t)=\left(w_{j},\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}D\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)(w_{j})\right),

j=1,\ldots,m
, and

v_{j}=\sum_{p=1}^{n}\langle w_{j},e_{p}\rangle\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{p}}(x,t)=\left(w_{j},w_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}D\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)(w_{j})\right),

j=m+1,\ldots,n
.
Recall (
6
) that

J_{\Sigma}\pi_{1}(x,u)=\|\wedge_{n}L\|\geqslant\frac{1}{|v_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{n}|},

since
L(v_{j})=w_{j}
,
j=1\ldots,n
.
Now, notice that, for
j=1,\ldots,m
,

\left|v_{j}\right|^{2}=|w_{j}|^{2}+\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}D\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)(w_{j})\right|^{2}\leqslant 1+m^{2}\Lambda^{2}|t|^{2},

whereas, for
j=m+1,\ldots,n
,

\left|v_{j}\right|^{2}=|w_{j}|^{2}+\left|w_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}D\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)(w_{j})\right|^{2}\leqslant 2+2\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}D\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)(w_{j})\right|+\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}D\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)(w_{j})\right|^{2}\\
\leqslant 2+2m\Lambda|t|+m^{2}\Lambda^{2}|t|^{2}.

Since
u=x+\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)
, one also has

|u-x|^{2}=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)\right|^{2}=|t|^{2}.

Finally,

|v_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{n}|\leqslant\left(1+m^{2}\Lambda^{2}|x-u|^{2}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}\left(2+2m\Lambda|x-u|+m^{2}\Lambda^{2}|x-u|^{2}\right)^{\frac{n-m}{2}},

and the conclusion follows.
∎

5.3
. —

Let
1\leqslant q\leqslant n-1
be an integer and let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{q}
be an orthonormal family in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. There exists
\lambda\in\Lambda(n,q)
such that

\left|\det\left(\langle v_{k},e_{\lambda(j)}\rangle\right)_{j,k=1,\ldots,q}\right|\geqslant\begin{pmatrix}n\\
q\end{pmatrix}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,.

Here,
\Lambda(n,q)
denotes the set of increasing maps
\{1,\ldots,q\}\to\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Proof.

We define a linear map
L:\mathbb{R}^{q}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}:(s_{1},\ldots,s_{q})\mapsto\sum_{k=1}^{q}s_{k}v_{k}
and we observe that
L
is an isometry.
Therefore, its area Jacobian factor
JL=1
, by definition.
Now, also

(JL)^{2}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda(n,q)}\left|\det\left(\langle v_{k},e_{\lambda(j)}\rangle\right)_{j,k=1,\ldots,q}\right|^{2},

according to the Binet-Cauchy formula
[
6
, Chapter 3 §2 Theorem 4]
.
The conclusion easily follows.
∎

5.4
Coarea Jacobian factor of
\pi_{2}
. —

The following hold.

(1)

For
\mathscr{H}^{n+m}
-almost every
(x,u)\in\Sigma
, one has

\frac{\begin{pmatrix}n\\
n-m\end{pmatrix}^{-\frac{1}{2}}-m(n-m)\Lambda|x-u|\left(1+m\Lambda|x-u|\right)^{n-m-1}}{\left(2+2m\Lambda|x-u|+m^{2}\Lambda^{2}|x-u|^{2}\right)^{\frac{n-m}{2}}}\leqslant J_{\Sigma}\pi_{2}(x,u)\leqslant 1.

(2)

For
\mathscr{H}^{n+m}
-almost every
(x,u)\in\Sigma
, one has
J_{\Sigma}\pi_{2}(x,u)>0
.

Proof.

Clearly,
J_{\Sigma}\pi_{2}(x,u)\leqslant(\rmLip\pi_{2})^{n}\leqslant 1
.
Regarding the left hand inequality, fix
(x,u)=F(x,t)
such that
F
is differentiable at
(x,t)
and, this time, let
L:T_{(x,u)}\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the restriction of
\pi_{2}
to
T_{(x,u)}\Sigma
.
We will now define a family of
n
vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
belonging to
T_{(x,u)}\Sigma
.
We choose
v_{k}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial t_{k}}(x,t)=(0,\mathbf{w}_{k}(x))
, for
k=1,\ldots,m
.
For choosing the
n-m
remaining vectors, we proceed as follows.
We select
\lambda\in\Lambda(n,n-m)
as in
5.3
applied with
q=n-m
to
\mathbf{v}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}(x)
and we let
v_{m+j}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{\lambda(j)}}(x,t)
,
j=1,\ldots,n-m
.
Recalling (
6
), we have

J_{\Sigma}\pi_{1}(x,u)=\|\wedge_{n}L\|\geqslant\frac{|L(v_{1})\wedge\ldots\wedge L(v_{n})|}{|v_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{n}|}.

As in the proof of
5.2
, we find that

|v_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{n}|\leqslant\left(2+2m\Lambda|x-u|+m^{2}\Lambda^{2}|x-u|^{2}\right)^{\frac{n-m}{2}}

and it remains only to find a lower bound for
|L(v_{1})\wedge\ldots\wedge L(v_{n})|
.
The latter equals the absolute value of the determinant of the matrix of coefficients of
L(v_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, with respect to any orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We choose the basis
\mathbf{w}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m}(x),\mathbf{v}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}(x)
.
Thus,

(16)

\begin{split}|L(v_{1})\wedge\ldots\wedge L(v_{n})|&=\left|\det\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c | c}1&\cdots&0&*\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&1&*\\
\hline\cr 0&\cdots&0&\left\langle e_{\lambda(j)}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}\frac{\partial\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\partial x_{\lambda(j)}}(x),\mathbf{v}_{k}(x)\right\rangle\\
\end{array}\right)\right|\\
&=\left|\det\left(\left\langle e_{\lambda(j)}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}\frac{\partial\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\partial x_{\lambda(j)}}(x),\mathbf{v}_{k}(x)\right\rangle\right)_{j,k=1,\ldots,n-m}\right|.\end{split}

Abbreviate

h_{\lambda(j)}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}\frac{\partial\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\partial x_{\lambda(j)}}(x)

and observe that
\left|h_{\lambda(j)}\right|\leqslant m\Lambda|t|=m\Lambda|x-u|
,
j=1,\ldots,n-m
(recall the proof of
5.2
).
It remains only to remember that
\lambda
has been selected in order that

\left|\det\left(\left\langle e_{\lambda(j)},\mathbf{v}_{k}(x)\right\rangle\right)_{j,k=1,\ldots,n-m}\right|\geqslant\begin{pmatrix}n\\
n-m\end{pmatrix}^{-\frac{1}{2}}

and to infer from the multilinearity of the determinant that

\left|\det\left(\left\langle e_{\lambda(j)},\mathbf{v}_{k}(x)\right\rangle+\left\langle h_{\lambda(j)},\mathbf{v}_{k}(x)\right\rangle\right)_{j,k}-\det\left(\left\langle e_{\lambda(j)},\mathbf{v}_{k}(x)\right\rangle\right)_{j,k}\right|\\
\leqslant(n-m)\left(\max_{j=1,\ldots,n-m}\left|h_{\lambda(j)}\right|\right)\left(\max_{j=1,\ldots,n-m}\left|h_{\lambda(j)}\right|+\left|e_{\lambda(j)}\right|\right)^{n-m-1}\\
\leqslant(n-m)m\Lambda|x-u|\left(1+m\Lambda|x-u|\right)^{n-m-1}.

This completes the proof of conclusion (1).

Let
E_{0}
denote the subset of
E
consisting of those
x
such that each
\mathbf{w}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, is differentiable at
x
.
Thus,
E_{0}
is Borel measurable and so is

A=E_{0}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\cap\bigg\{(x,t):\\
\rmrank\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c|c}\mathbf{w}_{1}(x)&\cdots&\mathbf{w}_{m}(x)&e_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}\frac{\partial\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\partial x_{1}}(x)&\ldots&e_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}\frac{\partial\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\partial x_{n}}(x)\end{array}\right)<n\bigg\}.

If
(x,u)\in\Sigma\setminus F(A)
, then the restriction of
\pi_{2}
to
T_{(x,u)}\Sigma
is surjective and, therefore,
J_{\Sigma}\pi_{2}(x,u)>0
.
Thus, we ought to show that
\mathscr{H}^{n+m}(F(A))=0
.
Since
F
is Lipschitzian, it suffices to establish that
\mathscr{L}^{n+m}(A)=0
.
As
A
is Borel measurable, it is enough to prove that
\mathscr{L}^{m}(A_{x})=0
, for every
x\in E_{0}
, according to Fubini’s theorem.
Fix
x\in E_{0}
.
As in the proof of conclusion (1), choose
\lambda\in\Lambda(n,n-m)
associated with
\mathbf{v}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}(x)
, according to
5.3
.
Based on (
16
), we see that

A_{x}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}\cap\left\{t:\det\left(\left\langle e_{\lambda(j)}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}\frac{\partial\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\partial x_{\lambda(j)}}(x),\mathbf{v}_{k}(x)\right\rangle\right)_{j,k=1,\ldots,n-m}=0\right\}.

The set on the right is of the form
S_{x}=\mathbb{R}^{m}\cap\{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}):P_{x}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})=0\}
, for some polynomial
P_{x}\in\mathbb{R}[T_{1},\ldots,T_{m}]
, and
P_{x}(0,\ldots,0)=\det\left(\langle e_{\lambda(j)},\mathbf{v}_{k}(x)\rangle\right)_{j,k=1,\ldots,n-m}\neq 0
.
It follows that
\mathscr{L}^{m}(S_{x})=0
– see, e.g.,
[
9
, 2.6.5]
– and the proof of (2) is complete.
∎

5.6
Definition of
\mathscr{Z}_{E}\mathbf{W}
. —

Note that
\phi_{E,\mathbf{W}}
is a
\sigma
-finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see
3.16
) and it is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathscr{L}^{n}
(see
5.5
).
It then ensues from the Radon-Nikodým theorem that there exists a Borel measurable function

\mathscr{Z}_{E}\mathbf{W}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

\int_{E}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(B\cap\mathbf{W}(x)\right)d\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)=\phi_{E,\mathbf{W}}(B)=\int_{B}\mathscr{Z}_{E}\mathbf{W}(u)d\mathscr{L}^{n}(u).

\mathscr{L}^{n}

Each time we write

particular Borel measurable function satisfying the above equality, for every

.

5.7
Definition of
\mathscr{Y}_{E}^{0}\mathbf{W}
. —

We define
\mathscr{Y}^{0}_{E}\mathbf{W}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty]

\mathscr{Y}_{E}^{0}\mathbf{W}(u)=\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(E\cap g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}^{-1}\{0\}\right),

u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Letting
B=\mathbb{R}^{n}
in (
13
), one infers from
3.3
that
\mathscr{Y}^{0}_{E}\mathbf{W}
is
\mathscr{L}^{n}
-measurable.
Using the estimates we have established so far regarding coarea Jacobian factors, we now show that
\mathscr{Z}_{E}\mathbf{W}
and
\mathscr{Y}_{E}^{0}\mathbf{W}
are comparable, when the diameter of

is not too large.

5.8 Proposition
. —

Given
0<\varepsilon<1
, there exists
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{5.8}(n,\Lambda,\varepsilon)>0
with the following property. If
\rmdiam E\leqslant\boldsymbol{\delta}_{5.8}(n,\Lambda,\varepsilon)

(1-\varepsilon)2^{-\frac{n-m}{2}}\mathscr{Y}^{0}_{E}\mathbf{W}(u)\leqslant\mathscr{Z}_{E}\mathbf{W}(u)\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)2^{\frac{n-m}{2}}\begin{pmatrix}n\\
n-m\end{pmatrix}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathscr{Y}^{0}_{E}\mathbf{W}(u),

for
\mathscr{L}^{n}
-almost every
u\in E
.

Proof.

We readily infer from
5.2
and
5.4
(1) that there exists
\boldsymbol{\delta}(n,\Lambda,\varepsilon)>0
such that, for
\mathscr{H}^{n+m}
-almost all
(x,u)\in\Sigma
, if
|x-u|\leqslant\boldsymbol{\delta}(n,\Lambda,\varepsilon)
, then

(18)

\alpha:=(1-\varepsilon)2^{-\frac{n-m}{2}}\leqslant J_{\Sigma}\pi_{1}(x,u)

and

(19)

\beta:=(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}2^{-\frac{n-m}{2}}\begin{pmatrix}n\\
n-m\end{pmatrix}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leqslant J_{\Sigma}\pi_{2}(x,u),

where the above define
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Assume now that
\rmdiam E\leqslant\boldsymbol{\delta}(n,\Lambda,\varepsilon)
.
Given
B\in\mathscr{B}(E)
, we infer from (
14
),
5.2
,
5.4
(1), (
15
), and the above lower bounds, that

\phi_{E,\mathbf{W}}(B)=\int_{\Sigma_{B}}J_{\Sigma}\pi_{1}d\mathscr{H}^{n+m}\geqslant\alpha\mathscr{H}^{n+m}(\Sigma_{B})\geqslant\alpha\int_{\Sigma_{B}}J_{\Sigma}\pi_{2}d\mathscr{H}^{n+m}\\
=\alpha\int_{B}\mathscr{Y}^{0}_{E}\mathbf{W}d\mathscr{L}^{n}

and

\phi_{E,\mathbf{W}}(B)=\int_{\Sigma_{B}}J_{\Sigma}\pi_{1}d\mathscr{H}^{n+m}\leqslant\mathscr{H}^{n+m}(\Sigma_{B})\leqslant\beta^{-1}\int_{\Sigma_{B}}J_{\Sigma}\pi_{2}d\mathscr{H}^{n+m}\\
=\beta^{-1}\int_{B}\mathscr{Y}^{0}_{E}\mathbf{W}d\mathscr{L}^{n}.

Thus,

\int_{B}\alpha\mathscr{Y}^{0}_{E}\mathbf{W}d\mathscr{L}^{n}\leqslant\int_{B}\mathscr{Z}_{E}\mathbf{W}d\mathscr{L}^{n}\leqslant\int_{B}\beta^{-1}\mathscr{Y}^{0}_{E}\mathbf{W}d\mathscr{L}^{n},

for every
B\in\mathscr{B}(E)
.
The conclusion follows from the
\mathscr{L}^{n}
-measurability of both
\mathscr{Z}_{E}\mathbf{W}
and
\mathscr{Y}^{0}_{E}\mathbf{W}

∎

5.9
Rest stop. —

The above upper bound for
\mathscr{Z}_{E}\mathbf{W}
is already enough to bound it from above, in turn, by a constant times
(\rmdiam E)^{m}
– see
6.4
.
We next want to establish that
\mathscr{Z}_{E}\mathbf{W}>0
, almost everywhere in
E
.
Yet, in the definition (
17
) of
\mathscr{Y}^{0}_{E}\mathbf{W}(u)
,
u
does not appear as the covariable of a function whose level sets we are measuring, thereby preventing the use of the coarea formula in an attempt to estimate
\mathscr{Y}^{0}_{E}\mathbf{W}(u)
.
This naturally leads to adding a variable
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
to the fibered space
\Sigma
, a covariable for
g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}
.

5.10
A fibered space associated with
E,B,\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m},\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m}
. —

Let
r>0
.
Abbreviate
C_{r}=\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\cap\left\{y:|y|\leqslant r\right\}
, the Euclidean ball centered at the origin, of radius
r
, in
\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
.
We define

\hat{F}_{r}:E\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times C_{r}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}:(x,t,y)\mapsto\left(x,x+\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}y_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}(x),y\right)

and

\hat{\Sigma}_{r}=\hat{F}_{r}\left(E\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times C_{r}\right)=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times C_{r}\cap\left\{(x,u,y):x\in E\text{ and }u\in\mathbf{W}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}y_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}(x)\right\}.

We note that
\hat{F}_{r}
is locally Lipschitzian and
\hat{\Sigma}_{r}
is countably
2n
-rectifiable and
\mathscr{H}^{2n}
-measurable.
Similarly to
5.1
, we define

\hat{\Sigma}_{r,B}=\hat{\Sigma}_{r}\cap\pi_{2}^{-1}(B)

which, clearly, is also countably
2n
-rectifiable and
\mathscr{H}^{2n}
-measurable.
We aim to apply the coarea formula to
\hat{\Sigma}_{r,B}
and to the two projections

\pi_{1}\times\pi_{3}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}:(x,u,y)\mapsto(x,y)

and

\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}:(x,u,y)\mapsto(u,y).

To this end, we notice that

\hat{\Sigma}_{r,B}\cap\left(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{3}\right)^{-1}\{(x,y)\}\\
=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\cap\left\{(x,u,y):u\in B\cap\left(\mathbf{W}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}y_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}(x)\right)\right\}

and, thus,

\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(\hat{\Sigma}_{r,B}\cap\left(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{3}\right)^{-1}\{(x,y)\}\right)=\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(B\cap\left(\mathbf{W}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}y_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}(x)\right)\right),

for every
(x,y)\in E\times C_{r}
.
We further notice that

\begin{split}\hat{\Sigma}_{r,B}\cap(\pi_{2}&\times\pi_{3})^{-1}\{(u,y)\}\\
&=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\cap\left\{(x,u,y):x\in E\text{ and }u\in\mathbf{W}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}y_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}(x)\right\}\\
&=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\cap\left\{(x,u,y):x\in E\cap g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}^{-1}\{y\}\right\},\end{split}

because

\begin{split}u\in\mathbf{W}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}y_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}(x)&\iff u-x-\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}y_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}(x)\in\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)\\
&\iff\left\langle\mathbf{v}_{j}(x),u-x-\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}y_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}(x)\right\rangle=0\text{, for all }j=1,\ldots,n-m\\
&\iff g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}(x)=y\end{split}

and, therefore,

\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(\hat{\Sigma}_{r,B}\cap(\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3})^{-1}\{(u,y)\}\right)=\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(E\cap g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}^{-1}\{y\}\right),

whenever
u\in B
and
y\in C_{r}
.

It now follows from the coarea formula and Fubini’s theorem that

(20)

\int_{\hat{\Sigma}_{r,B}}J_{\hat{\Sigma}_{r}}(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{3})d\mathscr{H}^{2n}=\int_{E}d\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)\int_{C_{r}}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(B\cap\left(\mathbf{W}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}y_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}(x)\right)\right)d\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(y)

and

(21)

\int_{\hat{\Sigma}_{r,B}}J_{\hat{\Sigma}_{r}}(\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3})d\mathscr{H}^{2n}=\int_{B}d\mathscr{L}^{n}(u)\int_{C_{r}}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(E\cap g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}^{-1}\{y\}\right)d\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(y).

5.11
Coarea Jacobian factors of
\pi_{1}\times\pi_{3}
and
\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3}
. —

The following inequalities hold, for
\mathscr{H}^{2n}
-almost every
(x,u,y)\in\hat{\Sigma}_{r}
.

2^{-(n-m)}\left(1+2n\Lambda|u-x|+2n^{2}\Lambda^{2}|u-x|^{2}\right)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\leqslant J_{\hat{\Sigma}_{r}}(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{3})(x,u,y)

and

J_{\hat{\Sigma}_{r}}(\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3})(x,u,y)\leqslant 1.

Proof.

The second conclusion is obvious, since
\rmLip\pi_{2}\times\pi_{3}=1
.
Regarding the first conclusion, we reason similarly to the proof of
5.2
.
Fix
(x,u,y)=\hat{F}_{r}(x,t,y)
such that
\hat{F}_{r}
is differentiable at
(x,t,y)
and denote by
L
the restriction of
\pi_{1}\times\pi_{3}
to
T_{(x,u,y)}\hat{\Sigma}_{r}
.
This tangent space is generated by the following
2n
vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
:

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\hat{F}_{r}}{\partial x_{j}}(x,t,y)&=\left(e_{j},e_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}\frac{\partial\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)}{\partial x_{j}}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}y_{i}\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}(x),0\right),\,j=1,\ldots,n,\\
\frac{\partial\hat{F}_{r}}{\partial t_{k}}(x,t,y)&=\left(0,\mathbf{w}_{k}(x),0\right),\,k=1,\ldots,m,\\
\frac{\partial\hat{F}_{r}}{\partial y_{\ell}}(x,t,y)&=\left(0,\mathbf{v}_{\ell}(x),e_{\ell}\right),\,\ell=1,\ldots,n-m.\end{split}

The range of
\pi_{1}\times\pi_{3}
being
(2n-m)
-dimensional, we need to select
2n-m
vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{2n-m}
in
T_{(x,u,y)}\hat{\Sigma}_{r}
in view of obtaining a lower bound

(22)

J_{\hat{\Sigma}_{r}}(\pi_{1}\times\pi_{3})(x,u,y)=\|\wedge_{2n-m}L\|\geqslant\frac{|L(v_{1})\wedge\ldots\wedge L(v_{2n-m})|}{|v_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{2n-m}|}.

Our choice of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{2n-m}
is as follows.
As in the proof of
5.2
, we let
w_{j}=\mathbf{w}_{j}(x)
, for
j=1,\ldots,m
, and
w_{j}=\mathbf{v}_{j-m}(x)
, for
j=m+1,\ldots,n
.
For
j=1,\ldots,m
, we define

v_{j}=\left(\sum_{p=1}^{n}\langle w_{j},e_{p}\rangle\frac{\partial\hat{F}_{r}}{\partial x_{p}}(x,t,y)\right)-\frac{\partial\hat{F}_{r}}{\partial t_{j}}(x,t,y)\\
=\left(w_{j},\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}D\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)(w_{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}y_{i}D\mathbf{v}_{i}(x)(w_{j}),0\right),

for
j=m+1,\ldots,n
, we define

v_{j}=\sum_{p=1}^{m}\langle w_{j},e_{p}\rangle\frac{\partial\hat{F}_{r}}{\partial x_{p}}(x,t,y)=\left(w_{j},w_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}D\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)(w_{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}y_{i}D\mathbf{v}_{i}(x)(w_{j}),0\right),

and, for
j=n+1,\ldots,2n-m
, we define

v_{j}=\frac{\partial\hat{F}_{r}}{\partial y_{j-n}}(x,t,y)=(0,\mathbf{v}_{j-n}(x),e_{j-n}),

so that
L(v_{1}),\ldots,L(v_{2n-m})
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
and, therefore, the numerator in (
22
) equals 1.
In order to determine an upper bound for its denominator, we start by fixing
j=1,\ldots,n
, we abbreviate
a_{j}(x,t,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}D\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)(x)(w_{j})
and
b_{j}(x,t,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}y_{i}D\mathbf{v}_{i}(x)(w_{j})
, and we notice that
|a_{j}(x,t,y)|\leqslant m\Lambda|t|\leqslant n\Lambda|t|
,
|b_{j}(x,t,y)|\leqslant(n-m)\Lambda|y|\leqslant n\Lambda|y|
.
Furthermore, since
u-x=\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}y_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}(x)
, one has
|u-x|^{2}=|t|^{2}+|y|^{2}\geqslant\max\{|t|^{2},|y|^{2}\}
.
Therefore, if
j=1,\ldots,m
, then

\begin{split}|v_{j}|^{2}&=|w_{j}|^{2}+\left|a_{j}(x,t,y)+b_{j}(x,t,y)\right|^{2}\\
&\leqslant 1+\left|a_{j}(x,t,y)\right|^{2}+\left|b_{j}(x,t,y)\right|^{2}+2\left|a_{j}(x,t,y)\right|\left|b_{j}(x,t,y)\right|\\
&\leqslant 1+n^{2}\Lambda^{2}\left(|t|^{2}+|y|^{2}+2|t||y|\right)\\
&\leqslant 1+2n^{2}\Lambda^{2}|u-x|^{2},\end{split}

whereas, if
j=m+1,\ldots,n
, then

\begin{split}\left|v_{j}\right|^{2}&=|w_{j}|^{2}+|w_{j}+a_{j}(x,t,y)+b_{j}(x,t,y)|^{2}\\
&\leqslant 1+1+|a_{j}(x,t,y)|^{2}+|b_{j}(x,t,y)|^{2}\\
&\quad\quad\quad+2|a_{j}(x,t,y)|+2|b_{j}(x,t,y)|+2|a_{j}(x,t,y)||b_{j}(x,t,y)|\\
&\leqslant 2\left(1+n^{2}\Lambda^{2}|u-x|^{2}+2n\Lambda|u-x|\right),\end{split}

and, if
j=n+1,\ldots,2n-m
, then

\left|v_{j}\right|=\sqrt{2}.

We conclude that

|v_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{2n-m}|\leqslant 2^{n-m}\left(1+2n^{2}\Lambda^{2}|u-x|^{2}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}\left(1+n^{2}\Lambda^{2}|u-x|^{2}+2n\Lambda|u-x|\right)^{\frac{n-m}{2}}\\
\leqslant 2^{n-m}\left(1+2n^{2}\Lambda^{2}|u-x|^{2}+2n\Lambda|u-x|\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}

and the proof is complete.
∎

5.12
Definition of
\mathscr{Y}_{E}\mathbf{W}
. —

It follows from the coarea theorem that the function

\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\to[0,\infty]:(u,y)\to\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(E\cap g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}^{-1}\{y\}\right)

is
\mathscr{L}^{n}\otimes\mathscr{L}^{n-m}
-measurable (recall
5.10
applied with
B=\mathbb{R}^{n}
).
It now follows from Fubini’s theorem that, for each

, the function

\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty]:u\mapsto\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{C_{r}}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(E\cap g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}^{-1}\{y\}\right)d\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(y)

is
\mathscr{L}^{n}
-measurable.
In turn, the function

6.

\mathscr{Y}_{E}\mathbf{W}

6.1
Bow tie lemma. —

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
W\in\mathbb{G}(n,m)
, and
0<\tau<1
. Assume that

(\forall\,x\in S)(\forall\,0<\rho\leqslant\rmdiam S):S\cap\mathbf{B}(x,\rho)\subseteq\mathbf{B}(x+W,\tau\rho).

Then there exists
F:P_{W}(S)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
S=\rmim F
and
\rmLip F\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}}}
. In particular,

\mathscr{H}^{m}(S)\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}}}\right)^{m}\boldsymbol{\alpha}(m)(\rmdiam S)^{m}.

Proof.

Let
x,x^{\prime}\in S
and define
\rho=|x-x^{\prime}|\leqslant\rmdiam S
.
Thus,
x^{\prime}\in S\cap\mathbf{B}(x,\rho)
and, therefore,
\left|P_{W^{\perp}}(x-x^{\prime})\right|\leqslant\tau\rho=\tau|x-x^{\prime}|
.
Since
|x-x^{\prime}|^{2}=\left|P_{W}(x-x^{\prime})\right|^{2}+\left|P_{W^{\perp}}(x-x^{\prime})\right|^{2}
, we infer that

(1-\tau^{2})\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|^{2}\leqslant\left|P_{W}(x-x^{\prime})\right|^{2}.

Therefore,
P_{W}|_{S}
is injective; and the Lipschitzian bound on
F=\left(P_{W}|_{S}\right)^{-1}
clearly follows from the above inequality.
Regarding the second conclusion, we note that

\mathscr{H}^{m}(S)=\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(F(P_{W}(S))\right)\leqslant\left(\rmLip F\right)^{m}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(P_{W}(S)\right)

and
P_{W}(S)
is contained in a ball of radius
\rmdiam P_{W}(S)\leqslant\rmdiam S
.
∎

6.2
. —

Given

Proof.

We show that
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\ref{ub.1}}(n,\Lambda,\tau)=\frac{\tau}{2\Lambda\sqrt{n}}
will do.
Let

&=\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{II}.\end{split}

Next, note that

\left|\mathrm{II}-\delta_{ij}\right|=\left|\mathrm{II}-\langle\mathbf{v}_{j}(x_{0}),\mathbf{v}_{i}(x_{0})\rangle\right|=\left|\langle\mathbf{v}_{j}(\gamma_{z}(y))-\mathbf{v}_{j}(x_{0}),\mathbf{v}_{i}(x_{0})\rangle\right|\\
\leqslant\Lambda\left|\gamma_{z}(y)-x_{0}\right|\leqslant\Lambda 2\sqrt{2}r\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2(n-m)},

where the last inequality follows from hypothesis (1), upon choosing
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\ref{53}}
small enough, according to
n
,
\Lambda
, and
\varepsilon
.
Moreover,

\left|\mathrm{I}\right|\leqslant\Lambda|\gamma_{z}(y)-u|\leqslant\Lambda 2\sqrt{2}r\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2(n-m)}.

Therefore, if
(a_{ij})_{i,j=1,\ldots,n-m}
is the matrix representing
Df_{z,u}(y)
with respect to the canonical basis, we have shown that
|a_{ij}-\delta_{ij}|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{n-m}
, for all
i,j=1,\ldots,n-m
.
This completes the proof of
Claim #2
.

Claim #3.

C_{\varepsilon r}\subseteq f_{z,u}(C_{r})
.

We shall show that
|y-f_{z,u}(y)|\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)r
, for every
y\in\rmBdry C_{r}
, and the conclusion will become a consequence of the intermediate value theorem, in case
m=n-1
, or a standard application of homology theory, see, e.g.,
[
5
, 4.6.1]
, in case
m<n-1
.
If
m<n-1
, it is clearly enough to establish this inequality only for
\mathscr{H}^{n-m-1}
-almost every
y\in\rmBdry C_{r}
.
In that case, owing to the coarea theorem
[
9
, 3.2.22]
, we may choose such a
y
so that that
f_{z,u}
is differentiable
\mathscr{H}^{1}
-almost everywhere on the line segment
\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\cap\{sy:0\leqslant s\leqslant 1\}
.
Whether
m<n-1
, or
m=n-1
, it then follows from
Claim #2
that

\left|f_{z,u}(y)-f_{z,u}(0)-y\right|=\left|\int_{0}^{1}Df_{z,u}(sy)(y)d\mathscr{L}^{1}(s)-y\right|\\
\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}\left|Df_{z,u}(sy)(y)-y\right|d\mathscr{L}^{1}(s)\leqslant\varepsilon|y|=\varepsilon r.

Accordingly,

\left|f_{z,u}(y)-y\right|\leqslant\left|f_{z,u}(y)-f_{z,u}(0)-y\right|+\left|f_{z,u}(0)\right|\leqslant\varepsilon r+\left|f_{z,u}(0)\right|

and the claim will be established upon showing that
\left|f_{z,u}(0)\right|\leqslant(1-2\varepsilon)r
.
Note that
f_{z,u}(0)=g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}(x_{0}+z)
; we shall use hypothesis (3) to bound its norm from above.
Given
j=1,\ldots,n-m
, recall that
\langle\mathbf{v}_{j}(x_{0}),z\rangle=0
, thus,

\left|g_{\mathbf{v}_{j},u}(x_{0}+z)-g_{\mathbf{v}_{j},u}(x_{0})\right|=\left|\langle\mathbf{v}_{j}(x_{0}+z),x_{0}+z-u\rangle-\langle\mathbf{v}_{j}(x_{0}),x_{0}-u\rangle\right|\\
=\left|\langle\mathbf{v}_{j}(x_{0}+z),x_{0}+z-u\rangle-\langle\mathbf{v}_{j}(x_{0}),x_{0}+z-u\rangle\right|\leqslant\Lambda|z||x_{0}+z-u|\\
\leqslant\Lambda r2\sqrt{2}r\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon r}{\sqrt{n-m}},

where the last inequality holds, according to hypothesis (1), provided
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\ref{53}}
is chosen sufficiently small.
In turn,

\left|f_{z,u}(0)\right|\leqslant\left|g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}(x_{0}+z)-g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}(x_{0})\right|+\left|g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}(x_{0})\right|\\
\leqslant\varepsilon r+(1-3\varepsilon)r=(1-2\varepsilon)r,

by virtue of hypothesis (3).

Claim #4.

For every
z\in\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})\cap\mathbf{B}(0,r)
and every closed
C\subseteq C_{\varepsilon r}
, one has
\mathscr{H}^{n-m}(C)\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\mathscr{H}^{n-m}\left(g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}^{-1}(C)\cap V_{z}\right)
.

First, notice that

g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}^{-1}(C)\cap V_{z}=\gamma_{z}\bigg(\gamma_{z}^{-1}\left(g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}^{-1}(C)\cap V_{z}\right)\bigg)=\gamma_{z}\left(f_{z,u}^{-1}(C)\right)

and, therefore,

\mathscr{H}^{n-m}\left(g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}^{-1}(C)\cap V_{z}\right)=\mathscr{H}^{n-m}\left(f_{z,u}^{-1}(C)\right),

since
\gamma_{z}
is an isometry.
Now, since
C\subseteq C_{\varepsilon r}\subseteq f_{z,u}(C_{r})
, according to
Claim #3
, we have

C=f_{z,u}\left(f_{z,u}^{-1}(C)\right).

It therefore follows from
Claim #1
that

\begin{split}\mathscr{H}^{n-m}(C)&\leqslant\left(\rmLip f_{z,u}\right)^{n-m}\mathscr{H}^{n-m}\left(f_{z,u}^{-1}(C)\right)\\
&\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\mathscr{H}^{n-m}\left(g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}^{-1}(C)\cap V_{z}\right).\end{split}

We are now ready to finish the proof, by an application of Fubini’s theorem:

\begin{split}\mathscr{L}^{n}\bigg(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\cap&g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}^{-1}(C)\bigg)\\
&=\int_{\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})\cap\mathbf{B}(0,r)}d\mathscr{L}^{m}(z)\mathscr{H}^{n-m}\left(g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u}^{-1}(C)\cap V_{z}\right)\\
&\geqslant\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\alpha}(m)r^{m}\mathscr{H}^{n-m}(C).\end{split}

∎

7.5
Lower bound for
\mathscr{Y}_{E}\mathbf{W}
. —

Given
0<\varepsilon<1/3
, there exists
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{7.5}(n,\Lambda,\varepsilon)>0
with the following property. If

(1)

0<r<\boldsymbol{\delta}_{7.5}(n,\Lambda,\varepsilon)
;

(2)

\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\subseteq U
;

(3)

A\subseteq U

(4)

then

\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)}\mathscr{Y}_{A\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)}\mathbf{W}(u)d\mathscr{L}^{n}(u)\geqslant(1-\mathbf{c}_{7.5}(n)\varepsilon)\boldsymbol{\alpha}(m)r^{m}\mathscr{L}^{n}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\right),

.

Proof.

Similarly to the proof of
7.3
, we will first establish a lower bound for

on “vertical slices”

of the given polyball, followed, next, by an application of Fubini.
Given

and

be as in

, and we consider the set

\check{V}_{z}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\cap\left\{x_{0}+z+\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}y_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}(x_{0}):y\in C_{(1-3\varepsilon)r}\right\}

(notice

used in the proof of
7.3
) and the isometric parametrization

For part of the proof, we find it convenient to abbreviate
E=A\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)
.
We also put
\check{\mathscr{Y}}_{E}\mathbf{W}=\left(\mathscr{Y}_{E}\mathbf{W}\right)\circ\check{\gamma}_{z}
.

By definition of
\mathscr{Y}_{E}\mathbf{W}
, for each
\check{\gamma}_{z}(y)\in\check{V}_{z}

\mathscr{C}_{y}
of closed balls in
\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
with the following properties: For every
C\in\mathscr{C}_{y}
,
C
is a ball centered at 0,
C\subseteq C_{\varepsilon r}
,

\mathscr{Y}_{E}\mathbf{W}\left(\check{\gamma}_{z}(y)\right)+\varepsilon\geqslant\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{C}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(E\cap g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},\check{\gamma}_{z}(y)}^{-1}\{h\}\right)d\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(h),

and
\inf\{\rmdiam C:C\in\mathscr{C}_{y}\}=0
.
Furthermore,
\check{\mathscr{Y}}_{E}\mathbf{W}
being
\mathscr{L}^{n-m}
-summable, according to
6.3
, there exists
N\subseteq C_{(1-3\varepsilon)r}
such that
\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(N)=0
and every
y\not\in N
is a Lebesgue point of
\check{\mathscr{Y}}_{E}\mathbf{W}
.
For such a
y
, we may reduce
\mathscr{C}_{y}
, if necessary, keeping all the previously stated properties valid, while enforcing also that

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{y+C}\check{\mathscr{Y}}_{E}\mathbf{W}d\mathscr{L}^{n-m}+\varepsilon\geqslant\left(\check{\mathscr{Y}}_{E}\mathbf{W}\right)(y),

whenever
C\in\mathscr{C}_{y}
.
We infer that, for each
y\in C_{(1-3\varepsilon)r}\setminus N
and each
C\in\mathscr{C}_{y}
,

(26)

\int_{y+C}\check{\mathscr{Y}}_{E}\mathbf{W}d\mathscr{L}^{n-m}+2\varepsilon\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(y+C)\geqslant\int_{C}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(E\cap g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},\check{\gamma}_{z}(y)}^{-1}\{h\}\right)d\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(h).

It ensues from the Vitali covering theorem that there is a sequence
(y_{k})_{k}
in
C_{(1-3\varepsilon)r}\setminus N
, and
C_{k}\in\mathscr{C}_{y_{k}}
, such that the balls

,

are pairwise disjoint and
\mathscr{L}^{n-m}\left(C_{(1-3\varepsilon)r}\setminus\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}(y_{k}+C_{k})\right)=0
.
It therefore follows, from (
26
) and the fact that
\gamma_{z}
is an isometry, that

(27)

\int_{V_{z}}\mathscr{Y}_{E}\mathbf{W}d\mathscr{H}^{n-m}+2\varepsilon\mathscr{H}^{n-m}(V_{z})\geqslant\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{C_{k}}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(E\cap g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u_{k}}^{-1}\{y\}\right)d\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(y),

where we have abbreviated
u_{k}=\check{\gamma}_{z}(y_{k})
.
We also abbreviate
S_{k}=g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u_{k}}^{-1}(C_{k})
and we infer from the coarea formula that, for each
k=1,2,\ldots
,

(28)

\int_{C_{k}}\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(E\cap g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u_{k}}^{-1}\{y\}\right)d\mathscr{L}^{n-m}(y)=\int_{E\cap S_{k}}Jg_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u_{k}}d\mathscr{L}^{n}\\
\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\mathscr{L}^{n}\left(E\cap S_{k}\right),

where the last inequality follows from
3.12
, applied with
U=\rmInt\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)
, provided that
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\ref{54}}(n,\Lambda,\varepsilon)
is chosen smaller than
(2\sqrt{2})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\ref{jac.g}}(n,\Lambda,\varepsilon)
.
Letting
S=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}S_{k}
and recalling that
E=A\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)
, we infer, from (
27
) and (
28
), that

(29)

\int_{V_{z}}\mathscr{Y}_{E}\mathbf{W}d\mathscr{H}^{n-m}+2\varepsilon\mathscr{H}^{n-m}(V_{z})\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\mathscr{L}^{n}(E\cap S)\\
\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\big(\mathscr{L}^{n}(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\cap S)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\setminus A)\big).

Applying
7.3
to each
S_{k}
does not immediately yield a lower bound for
\mathscr{L}^{n}(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\cap S)
, because the
S_{k}
are not necessarily pairwise disjoint.
This is why we now introduce slightly smaller versions of these:

\check{C}_{k}=(1-\varepsilon)C_{k}\quad\text{ and }\quad\check{S}_{k}=g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u_{k}}^{-1}\left(\check{C}_{k}\right).

Claim.

The sets
\check{S}_{k}\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)
,
k=1,2,\ldots
, are pairwise disjoint.

Assume, if possible, that there are
j\neq k
and
x\in\check{S}_{j}\cap\check{S}_{k}\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)
.
Letting
\rho_{j}
and
\rho_{k}
denote, respectively, the radius of
C_{j}
, and of
C_{k}
, we notice that
\rho_{j}+\rho_{k}<|y_{j}-y_{k}|
, because
(y_{j}+C_{j})\cap(y_{k}+C_{k})=\emptyset
.
Since
\check{\gamma}_{z}
is an isometry, we have
|u_{j}-u_{k}|=\left|\check{\gamma}_{z}(y_{j})-\check{\gamma}_{z}(y_{k})\right|=|y_{j}-y_{k}|
and, therefore, also

(30)

\left|g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u_{j}}(x)-g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u_{k}}(x)\right|\leqslant\left|g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u_{j}}(x)\right|+\left|g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u_{k}}(x)\right|\\
\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{j}+(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{k}<(1-\varepsilon)\left|u_{j}-u_{k}\right|.

We now introduce the following vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
:

h_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}\langle\mathbf{v}_{i}(x_{0}),u_{j}\rangle e_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad h_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}\langle\mathbf{v}_{i}(x_{0}),u_{k}\rangle e_{i}

and we notice that

\left|h_{j}-h_{k}\right|=\left|P_{\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})^{\perp}}(u_{j}-u_{k})\right|=|u_{j}-u_{k}|,

where the second equality holds because
u_{j}-u_{k}\in\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})^{\perp}
, as clearly follows from the definition of
\check{\gamma}_{z}
.
Furthermore,

\bigg|\big(g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u_{j}}(x)-g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u_{k}}(x)\big)-\big(h_{k}-h_{j}\big)\bigg|=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}\left|\langle\mathbf{v}_{i}(x)-\mathbf{v}_{i}(x_{0}),u_{k}-u_{j}\rangle\right|^{2}}\\
\leqslant\sqrt{n-m}\Lambda\sqrt{2}r\left|u_{j}-u_{k}\right|\leqslant\varepsilon\left|u_{j}-u_{k}\right|,

since we may choose
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\ref{54}}(n,\Lambda,\varepsilon)
to be so small that the last inequality holds, in view of hypothesis (1).
Whence,

\left|g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u_{j}}(x)-g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u_{k}}(x)\right|\geqslant\left|h_{j}-h_{k}\right|-\varepsilon\left|u_{j}-u_{k}\right|=(1-\varepsilon)\left|u_{j}-u_{k}\right|,

contradicting (
30
).
The
Claim
is established.

Thus,

(31)

\begin{split}\mathscr{L}^{n}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\cap S\right)&=\mathscr{L}^{n}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\cap\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}S_{k}\right)\\
&\geqslant\mathscr{L}^{n}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\cap\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}\check{S}_{k}\right)\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathscr{L}^{n}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\cap\check{S}_{k}\right)\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathscr{L}^{n}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\cap g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u_{k}}^{-1}\left(\check{C}_{k}\right)\right)\\
&\geqslant\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\alpha}(m)r^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathscr{L}^{n-m}\left(\check{C}_{k}\right),\end{split}

where the last inequality follows from
7.3
.
We notice that, indeed,
7.3
applies, since
\check{C}_{k}\subseteq C_{k}\subseteq C_{\varepsilon r}
and
\left|g_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n-m},u_{k}}(x_{0})\right|=\left|P_{\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})^{\perp}}(u_{k}-x_{0})\right|=\left|y_{k}\right|\leqslant(1-3\varepsilon)r
.

Now,

(32)

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathscr{L}^{n-m}\left(\check{C}_{k}\right)=(1-\varepsilon)^{n-m}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathscr{L}^{n-m}\left(C_{k}\right)=(1-\varepsilon)^{n-m}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathscr{L}^{n-m}\left(y_{k}+C_{k}\right)\\

We infer, from (

) and (

), that

\mathscr{L}^{n}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\cap S\right)\geqslant\frac{(1-3\varepsilon)^{2(n-m)}}{1+\varepsilon}\mathscr{L}^{n}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\right).

It therefore ensues, from (

) and hypothesis (4), that

\int_{V_{z}}\mathscr{Y}_{E}\mathbf{W}d\mathscr{H}^{n-m}+2\varepsilon\mathscr{H}^{n-m}(V_{z})\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\left(\frac{(1-3\varepsilon)^{2(n-m)}}{1+\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\right)\mathscr{L}^{n}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\right).

Integrating over

, we infer from Fubini’s theorem that

\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)}\mathscr{Y}_{A\cap\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)}\mathbf{W}d\mathscr{L}^{n}=\int_{\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})\cap\mathbf{B}(0,r)}d\mathscr{L}^{n}(z)\int_{V_{z}}\mathscr{Y}_{E}\mathbf{W}d\mathscr{H}^{n-m}\\
\geqslant\left[(1-\varepsilon)\left(\frac{(1-3\varepsilon)^{2(n-m)}}{1+\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\right)-2\varepsilon\right]\boldsymbol{\alpha}(m)r^{m}\mathscr{L}^{n}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\right).

∎

8.1 Theorem
. —

Assume that
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbf{W}_{0}:S\to\mathbb{G}(n,m)
is Lipschitzian, and
A\subseteq S
is Borel measurable. The following are equivalent.

(1)

\mathscr{L}^{n}(A)=0
.

(2)

For
\mathscr{L}^{n}
almost every

\mathscr{H}^{m}(A\cap\mathbf{W}(x))=0

(3)

For
\mathscr{L}^{n}
almost every
x\in S

\mathscr{H}^{m}(A\cap\mathbf{W}(x))=0
.

Recall our convention that
\mathbf{W}(x)=x+\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)

Proof.

Since

\mathbf{W}_{0}
to the closure of

.
Furthermore, if the theorem holds for

(1)\Rightarrow(3)
. It follows from

that each

in

can be associated with a Lipschitzian orthonormal frame satisfying all the conditions of

Since

Letting

Thus, if

-almost every

(3)\Rightarrow(2)
is trivial.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)

8.2 Remark
. —

Alternatively, one can prove the principal implication
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
in two other ways.
One way – more involved – consists in applying our main result
8.4
below.
A second – simpler – way, along the following lines, avoids reference to the estimates we obtained for the functions
\mathscr{Y}_{E}\mathbf{W}
and
\mathscr{Z}_{E}\mathbf{W}
.
Consider
x\in A
,
\eta>0
, and
V\in\mathbb{G}(n,n-m)
such that
d\left(V^{\perp},\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)\right)<\eta
, and put
V_{x}=x+V
.
Define

\Phi:(U_{x}\cap V_{x})\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}:(\xi,t)\mapsto\xi+\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i}(\xi)\,.

\Phi
is locally Lipschitzian and one checks that, in fact,
\Phi
is a lipeomorphism between
B
and
\Phi(B)
, where
B=\mathbf{B}(x,\rho)
, for some
\rho>0
depending upon
\eta
and
\Lambda_{x}
, because its differential is close to the identity.
Referring to Fubini’s theorem, one then further checks that

\mathscr{L}^{n}(A^{\prime})=0\text{ if and only if }\mathscr{H}^{m}(A^{\prime}\cap\mathbf{W}(\xi))=0\text{, for $\mathscr{H}^{n-m}$-almost every }\xi\in V_{x}.

Finally, using Fubini again, with respect to the decomposition
\mathbb{R}^{n}=\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)\oplus\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)^{\perp}
, one shows that
\mathscr{H}^{m}(A\cap\mathbf{W}(\zeta))=0
, for
\mathscr{H}^{n-m}
-almost every
\zeta\in x^{\prime}+\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)^{\perp}
, where
x^{\prime}
is as close as we wish to
x
.
Applying the previous construction with
x^{\prime}
replacing
x
, using
V=\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)^{\perp}
, we find that
\mathscr{L}^{n}(A\cap\mathbf{B}(x,r))=0
, for some
r>0
depending on
\Lambda_{x}
.

Notwithstanding, it seems that the (simpler) change of variable described here is not enough to yield the (stronger) theorem below.

8.3
Polyballs. —

Recalling
7.2
, we notice that

\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)=\mathbb{R}^{n}\cap\left\{\nu_{x_{0}}(x-x_{0})\leqslant r\right\},

where
\nu_{x_{0}}
is a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by the formula

\nu_{x_{0}}(x)=\max\left\{\left|P_{\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})}(x)\right|,\left|P_{\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})^{\perp}}(x)\right|\right\},

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
It is readily observed that
\rmLip\nu_{x_{0}}\leqslant 1
.

(1)

One has
|\nabla\nu_{x_{0}}(x)|=1
, for
\mathscr{L}^{n}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Abbreviate
P=P_{\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})}
and
Q=P_{\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})^{\perp}}
and define
S=\mathbb{R}^{n}\cap\{x:|P(x)|=|Q(x)|\}
, so that
\mathscr{L}^{n}(S)=0
.
Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus S
and notice
\nu_{x_{0}}
is differentiable at
x
.
We henceforth assume that
|P(x)|<|Q(x)|
, whence,
\nu_{x_{0}}(x)=|Q(x)|
– the proof in the other case is similar.
Define
\varepsilon=|Q(x)|-|P(x)|>0
,
e=\frac{Q(x)}{|Q(x)|}
, and let
0<t<\varepsilon
.
Note that
|Q(x+te)|=|Q(x)|\left(1+\frac{t}{|Q(x)|}\right)>|Q(x)|
and
|P(x+te)|\leqslant|P(x)|+t<|Q(x)
.
Thus,
\nu_{x_{0}}(x+te)=|Q(x+te)|
and, in turn,
\nu_{x_{0}}(x+te)-\nu_{x_{0}}(x)=|Q(x+te)|-|Q(x)|=t
.
It follows that
\langle\nabla\nu_{x_{0}}(x),e\rangle=1
.
Since
e
is a unit vector and
\rmLip\nu_{x_{0}}\leqslant 1
, we conclude that
|\nabla\nu_{x_{0}}(x)|=1
.

(2)

Let
U
,
\mathbf{W}
and
\Lambda>0
be as in
4.1
. Assume
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\subseteq U
,
x\in\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)
, and
0\leqslant t\leqslant 1
is so that
\nu_{x_{0}}(x-x_{0})=tr
. It follows that

\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)\cap\mathbf{W}(x)\subseteq\mathbf{B}\left(x,r(1+t)+8m\Lambda r^{2}\right)\cap\mathbf{W}(x).

First notice that
|z|\leqslant\sqrt{2}\nu_{x_{0}}(z)
, for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, so that
|x-x_{0}|\leqslant\sqrt{2}\nu_{x_{0}}(x-x_{0})=\sqrt{2}tr
and, for every
x^{\prime}\in\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x_{0},r)
,
|x-x^{\prime}|\leqslant\sqrt{2}\left(\nu_{x_{0}}(x-x_{0})+\nu_{x_{0}}(x_{0}-x^{\prime})\right)\leqslant\sqrt{2}r(1+t)
.
Next notice that, for each
h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\left|\left(P_{\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})}-P_{\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)}\right)(h)\right|=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle\mathbf{w}_{i}(x_{0}),h\rangle\mathbf{w}_{i}(x_{0})-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle\mathbf{w}_{i}(x),h\rangle\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)\right|\\
\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\langle\mathbf{w}_{i}(x_{0}),h\rangle\right|\left|\mathbf{w}_{i}(x_{0})-\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)\right|+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\langle\mathbf{w}_{i}(x_{0})-\mathbf{w}_{i}(x),h\rangle\right|\left|\mathbf{w}_{i}(x)\right|\\
\leqslant 2m\Lambda\left|x-x_{0}\right||h|.

We now assume that
x^{\prime}\in\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{W}}(x,r)\cap\mathbf{W}(x)
, in particular,
x-x^{\prime}\in\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)
, whence,

\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|=\left|P_{\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)}(x-x^{\prime})\right|\leqslant\left|P_{\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})}(x-x^{\prime})\right|+\left|\left(P_{\mathbf{W}_{0}(x_{0})}-P_{\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)}\right)(x-x^{\prime})\right|\\
\leqslant\nu_{x_{0}}(x-x_{0})+\nu_{x_{0}}(x^{\prime}-x_{0})+2m\Lambda|x-x_{0}||x-x^{\prime}|\leqslant r(1+t)+4m\Lambda t(1+t)r^{2}.

8.4 Theorem
. —

Assume that

\mathbf{W}(x)=x+\mathbf{W}_{0}(x)

Proof.

\mathbf{W}_{0}

It follows from
3.15

is Borel measurable.
Thus, for each
j=1,2,\ldots
, the function
g_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty]

g_{j}(x)=\sup_{0<r<\frac{1}{j}}\frac{\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(A\cap\mathbf{B}(x,r)\cap\mathbf{W}(x)\right)}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}(m)r^{m}}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<r<\frac{1}{j}\\
r\,\text{rational}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathscr{H}^{m}\left(A\cap\mathbf{B}(x,r)\cap\mathbf{W}(x)\right)}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}(m)r^{m}}

\boldsymbol{\eta}(n,m)=2^{-(n-m)}

9
