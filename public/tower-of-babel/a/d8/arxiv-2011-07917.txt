Expected signature of stopped Brownian motion on d d -dimensional C 2 , α C^{2,\alpha} -domains has finite radius of convergence everywhere: 2 ≤ d ≤ 8 2\leq d\leq 8
Siran Li, Hao Ni

Abstract
. A fundamental question in rough path theory is whether the expected signature of a geometric rough path completely determines the law of signature. One sufficient condition is that the expected signature has infinite radius of convergence, which is satisfied by various stochastic processes on a fixed time interval, including the Brownian motion. In contrast, for the Brownian motion stopped upon the first exit time from a bounded domain Ω \Omega , it is only known that the radius of convergence for the expected signature on sufficiently regular Ω \Omega is strictly positive everywhere, and that the radius of convergence is finite at some point when Ω \Omega is the 2 2 -dimensional unit disc ( [ 1 ] ). In this paper, we prove that on any bounded C 2 , α C^{2,\alpha} -domain Ω ⊂ ℝ d \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} with 2 ≤ d ≤ 8 2\leq d\leq 8 , the expected signature of the stopped Brownian motion has finite radius of convergence everywhere. A key ingredient of our proof is the introduction of a “domain-averaging hyperbolic development” (see Definition 4.1 ), which allows us to symmetrize the PDE system for the hyperbolic development of expected signature by averaging over rotated domains.

1.
Introduction

The theory of rough paths is a generalisation of the classical control theory. It sets forth a framework that makes sense of solutions to differential equations driven by irregular signals; for example, the Brownian motion.

A fundamental concept in rough path theory is the
signature
of a path. Playing a similar role to that of the moment generating function of a random variable, the
expected signature
is of both theoretical and practical significance. For example, computation for the expected signature of the Brownian motion on
[0,1]
leads to the notion of
cubature on Wiener space
, which is a high order numerical method for high dimensional SDEs (stochastic differential equations) and semi-elliptic PDEs (partial differential equations); see
[
2
]
. Recently, in
[
3
]
a metric for laws of stochastic processes has been proposed based on the normalised expected signature. It can be used for nonparametric two-sample hypothesis tests for laws of stochastic processes, and potentially has much wider applications in machine learning.

One central question concerning the expected signature, known as the
moment problem
, asks if the expected signature uniquely determines the law of random signature. A sufficient condition for the affirmative answer has recently been identified for the moment problem by Chevyrev–Lyons (
[
4
]
, Proposition 6.1): the radius of convergence of expected signature is infinite.

The infiniteness of radius of convergence for the expected signature has been verified for several popular stochastic processes on a fixed time horizon,
e.g.
, fractional Brownian motions with Hurst parameter in
\left]\frac{1}{4},1\right]
(see
[
4
,
5
]
). It is nonetheless considerably challenging to check whether this is satisfied by processes
up to a random time
. Even for the simplest case of the Brownian motion up to the first exit time from a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(in brief, “
stopped Brownian motion
” in the sequel), it has remained open ever since it was proposed in
[
6
]
.

As a first step towards the aforementioned problem, it was established in
[
6
]
that, under suitable regularity assumptions for the domain (see Proposition
3.1
and Remark
3.2
), the expected signature of the stopped Brownian motion has a geometric upper bound for its decay rate. This is achieved by deriving a system of nested PDEs (
i.e.
, partial differential equations graded by a parameter
n\in\mathbb{N}
) satisfied by the expected signature and applying the standard boundary regularity theory for elliptic PDEs. Despite its insufficiency to resolve the moment problem, the geometric upper bound warrants the positivity of the radius of convergence.

Recently, Boedihardjo–Diehl–Mezzarobba–Ni
[
1
]
showed that the stopped Brownian motion on the
2
-dimensional unit disc has finite radius of convergence. The proof in
[
1
]
relies crucially on the technique of
hyperbolic development
, which plays an essential role in Hambly–Lyons’ proof of the unique determination of paths of bounded variation by the signature; see
[
7
]
. Indeed, by taking a morphism (which appears in
[
7
]
) from the tensor algebra space to
\mathfrak{gl}(3;\mathbb{R})
and exploiting the partial symmetries of the resulting matrix-valued PDE systems, a nested system of three ODEs has been obtained in
[
1
]
. These ODEs can be solved explicitly, from which one deduces the finiteness of radius of convergence. Nonetheless,
[
1
]
relies heavily on the rotational invariance of the domain, thus making it difficult to be extended to general domains. In addition, even for the case of the unit disc, it remains unknown if the stopped Brownian motion starting at a point apart from the centre has finite radius of convergence.

The main result of our paper gives a complete solution to the finiteness problem of the expected signature of stopped Brownian motions on bounded domains under mild regularity assumptions, for all dimensions up to
8
:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega
be bounded
C^{2,\alpha}
-domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
;
2\leq d\leq 8
. The expected signature
\Phi
of a Brownian motion stopped upon the first exit time from
\Omega
has finite radius of convergence at every point in
\Omega
.

The strategy of our proof is outlined as follows. First, by averaging the hyperbolic development of expected signature over the rotated images of the domain, we construct a quantity
\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(z)
, the “
domain-averaging development
”, that has the following features:

•

\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(z)
is rotationally invariant (with respect the domain rotations);

•

\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(z)
satisfies the same PDE as that for
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\Omega}
; and

•

\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(z)
inherits the finiteness of radius of convergence from the non-averaged hyperbolic development
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\Omega}
of the expected signature. Thus, the operation of domain averaging preserves the lower bound for radius of convergence of the hyperbolic development.

Working locally near
z\in\Omega
, we arrive at the same PDE for
\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(z)
as for its non-averaged analogue on a small ball around
z
. Loosely speaking, “half” of the boundary conditions will gone missing for the PDE for
\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(z)
; nevertheless, geometric properties of the hyperbolic development enable us to establish uniform lower bounds for the remaining component of
\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(z)
.

Definition 2.2
.

The canonical epimorphism

For

S^{\lfloor p\rfloor}(X)=\left(1,X^{1}_{J},\cdots X^{\lfloor p\rfloor}_{J}\right)

\displaystyle X^{n}_{s,t}=\underset{s\leq u_{1}\leq\cdots\leq u_{n}\leq t}{\int\cdots\int}\,{\rm d}X_{u_{1}}\otimes\,{\rm d}X_{u_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\,{\rm d}X_{u_{n}},

. The signature of

Definition 2.3

(
p
-variation distance)
.

Let

. For smooth rough paths
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}:J\to T^{(\lfloor p\rfloor)}(E)
, the

-variation metric between

\displaystyle d(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=\max_{k=1,\cdots\lfloor p\rfloor}\left(\sup_{\mathcal{D}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left|\mathbf{X}_{u_{i},u_{i+1}}^{k}-\mathbf{Y}_{u_{i},u_{i+1}}^{k}\right|^{\frac{p}{k}}\right)^{\frac{k}{p}}+\sup_{t\in J}\left|X^{1}_{t}-Y^{1}_{t}\right|,

with the supremum taken over all finite divisions

of

.

Definition 2.4

(Geometric rough path)
.

\mathbf{X}:J\rightarrow T^{(\lfloor p\rfloor)}(E)
is said to be a geometric

-rough path if there exists a sequence of smooth rough path
(\mathbf{X}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\mathbf{X}
is the limit of
\mathbf{X}^{n}

p
-variation metric.

-rough paths is denoted as

.

2.3.
Hyperbolic development

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a normed algebra. Any linear operator
M:E\to\mathscr{A}
can be extended to
T((E))

by naturality
. Indeed, one first defines
\hat{M}
on
E^{\otimes k}
for each
k
via

\displaystyle\hat{M}\left(e_{i_{1}}\otimes e_{i_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{k}}\right):=M(e_{i_{1}})M(e_{i_{2}})\cdots M(e_{i_{k}}),

and then extends to
T((E))
by linearity. The right-hand side of the above identity is understood as a product in the algebra
\mathscr{A}
. By an abuse of notations, we always write
\hat{M}\equiv M
.

For any
\lambda>0
, the action of
\lambda M
on the signature of a path
\gamma
of bounded
1
-variation is given by
M(\lambda\gamma)
. Similarly, for
\lambda>0
, the action of
\lambda M
on
\Phi_{\Omega}
is given by

(5)

\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega}(z):=(\lambda M)\Phi_{\Omega}(z)\quad\text{ for each }z\in\Omega.

This can be recast into

(6)

\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}M\left[\mathbf{Proj}_{n}(\Phi_{\Omega}(z))\right]

at least for small enough
|\lambda|
. See Lyons–Ni
[
12
]
, Theorem 3.6.

The hyperbolic development (
cf.
Boedihardjo–Diehl–Mezzarobba–Ni
[
1
]
; Hambly–Lyons
[
7
]
) refers to following particular choice of
M
, denoted as
H:T((E))\to\mathfrak{gl}(d+1;\mathbb{R})
—

\displaystyle H(c)=cI_{d+1}\qquad\text{ for }c\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle H(x,y):=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&z^{1}\\
0&0&\cdots&0&z^{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&0&z^{d}\\
z^{1}&z^{2}&\cdots&z^{d}&0\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{ for }z=\left(z^{1},\ldots,z^{d}\right)\in E,

and
H
is extended to
T((E))
by naturality as above.

Lyons–Xu
[
13
]
characterises the space of the hyperbolic development of bounded 1-variation
\mathbb{R}^{d}
path and provides an explicit expression for the hyperbolic development in terms of the signature. Specifically, for
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
of bounded

, it is shown that

is confined within the

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{d}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}:\,\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\left(x^{j}\right)^{2}-\left(x^{d+1}\right)^{2}=-1,\,x^{d+1}>0\right\}.

Denote the set of “squared words” by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2n}=\Big\{w=(i_{1},i_{1},i_{2},i_{2},\cdots,i_{n},i_{n}):\,i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{n}\in\{1,2,\cdots,d\}\Big\}

and, for
k\in\{1,2,\cdots,d\}
, put

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2n}^{(k)}=\Big\{w=(i_{1},i_{1},i_{2},i_{2},\cdots,i_{n},i_{n},k):\,i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{n}\in\{1,2,\cdots,d\}\Big\}.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\gamma:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a path of bounded
1
-variation, and set

h_{\lambda}(\gamma)\equiv\left(h_{\lambda}^{(1)}(\gamma),\cdots,h_{\lambda}^{(d+1)}(\gamma)\right)^{\top}:=H\left(S\left(\lambda\gamma\right)\right).

We may express

\displaystyle h_{\lambda}^{(k)}(\lambda\gamma)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{E}_{2n}^{(k)}}\lambda^{2n+1}\mathbf{Proj}_{w}(S(\gamma)),

\displaystyle h_{\lambda}^{(d+1)}(\lambda\gamma)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{E}_{2n}}\lambda^{2n}\mathbf{Proj}_{w}(S(\gamma)).

3.
PDE for the expected signature

Here we investigate the PDE (
1
) for the expected signature. Our results in this section are valid for
E=\mathbb{R}^{d}
for any
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
. Also note that our regularity assumption on the domain is weaker than that in Lyons–Ni
[

]

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset E
be a bounded
C^{2,\alpha}
-domain;
\alpha>0
. Then for each
n=2,3,4,\ldots
, the
n^{\text{th}}
term of
\Phi_{\Omega}
satisfies the PDE

(7)

\displaystyle\Delta\left(\mathbf{Proj}_{n}(\Phi_{\Omega}(z))\right)=-2\sum_{i=1}^{d}e_{i}\otimes\frac{\partial\mathbf{Proj}_{n-1}(\Phi_{\Omega}(z))}{\partial z^{i}}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}e_{i}\otimes e_{i}\right)\otimes\mathbf{Proj}_{n-2}(\Phi_{\Omega}(z))

with the initial conditions (
3
), (
4
) and the boundary condition

(8)

\Phi_{\Omega}={\bf 1}\qquad\text{ on }\partial\Omega.

The above PDE has a unique classical solution in the sense that

\mathbf{Proj}_{n}(\Phi_{\Omega})\in C^{\infty}\left(\Omega;E^{\otimes n}\right)\cap C^{2,\alpha}\left(\overline{\Omega};E^{\otimes n}\right)\qquad\text{ for each }n=0,1,2,\ldots.

In addition, the radius of convergence of
\Phi
is strictly positive.

Proof.

The proof is standard and shall only be sketched here. See
[
6
]
, §3 for details.

The boundary condition (
8
) follows from Eq. (
2
) and a continuity argument.

Given the initial conditions (
3
) and (
4
), one may apply the standard Schauder theory (
[
14
,
15
]
) inductively to show that Eq. (
7
) has a unique classical solution

\mathbf{Proj}_{n}(\Phi_{\Omega})\in C^{\infty}\left(\Omega;E^{\otimes n}\right)\cap C^{2,\alpha}\left(\overline{\Omega};E^{\otimes n}\right).

As a remark, although the Schauder theory is not applicable for systems of elliptic PDEs in general, we can decouple the PDE (
7
) into scalar equations, thus there is no danger of utilising the Schauder theory here. More precisely, let
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}
be the canonical basis for
E
; we can find scalarfields
f_{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\Phi_{n}(z)=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots i_{n}\in\{1,2,\ldots,d\}}\,f_{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}}(z)\,e_{i_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes e_{i_{n}}.

Thus Eq. (
7
) is equivalent to finitely many scalar Poisson equations of
f_{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}}
for each
n
.

In addition, Schauder estimates together with a simple induction yield the geometric bound:

(9)

\|\mathbf{Proj}_{n}(\Phi_{\Omega})\|_{C^{0}(\Omega)}\leq C^{n},

with
C
depending only on
\Omega
and
d
, which implies that the radius of convergence of
\Phi
is strictly positive. See
[
6
]
, Theorem 3.6 or the appendix in
[
1
]
. ∎

Remark 3.2
.

In
[
6
]
, §3, a variant of Proposition
3.1
is established for strong solutions in Sobolev spaces
W^{m,2}(\Omega)
for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the geometric bound (
9
) can be deduced from the Sobolev–Morrey embedding, provided that
m>\lfloor n/\penalty 2\rfloor+1
. But this requires, as in
[
6
]
, that the domain
\Omega
is
C^{m}
. Proposition
3.1
indicates that such regularity assumption can be relaxed to
C^{2,\alpha}
.

In fact, thanks to the Kellogg theorem
[
16
]
one may further relax the regularity of
\Omega
to
C^{2,{\rm Dini}}
, i.e.,
\Omega
admits
C^{2}
-local charts with parametrisation maps being merely Dini continuous. In this case we have the classical solution in
C^{\infty}\left(\Omega;E^{\otimes n}\right)\cap C^{2}\left(\overline{\Omega};E^{\otimes n}\right)
, while the geometric bound (
9
) remains valid.

Recall the multi-index notation:

\displaystyle\left|I\right|=i_{1}+\ldots+i_{d},\qquad D^{I}u=\frac{\partial^{i_{1}+\ldots i_{d}}u}{\partial z_{1}^{i_{1}}\partial z_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots\partial z_{d}^{i_{d}}},\qquad I=\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

As an immediate consequence of Proposition
3.1
and Remark
3.2
, we have

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset E
be a bounded domain of class
C^{2,\alpha}
(or even
C^{2,{\rm Dini}}
). Let
M\in{\rm Hom}\big(\Omega;T((E))\big)
be an algebra homomorphism with the projective norm on
T((E))
. Then there exists a constant
L>0
depending only on
\Omega
and the operator norm of
M
such that, for all
\lambda\in[0,L[
, the series
z\mapsto\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}M\left[\mathbf{Proj}_{n}\left(\Phi_{\Omega}(z)\right)\right]
defines a
C^{2}
-function on
\Omega
. In addition, for any multi-index
I
with
|I|\leq 2
, it holds that

D^{I}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}M\left[\mathbf{Proj}_{n}\left(\Phi_{\Omega}(z)\right)\right]\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}D^{I}\Big\{M\left[\mathbf{Proj}_{n}\left(\Phi_{\Omega}(z)\right)\right]\Big\}.

Theorem
2.9
shows that for a bounded domain, the expected signature of the stopped Brownian motion satisfies the characteristic PDE (
3.4
). Conversely, the classical solution (
C^{2}
in the interior and
C^{1}
up to the boundary) for the characteristic PDE must coincide with the expected signature of the stopped Brownian motion.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
E
. Suppose that
\xi:\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow T((E))
is a classical solution to the following PDE, in the regularity class
C^{2}\big(\Omega;T((E))\big)\cap C^{1}\big(\overline{\Omega};T((E))\big)
:

\begin{cases}\Delta\mathbf{Proj}_{n}(\xi)=-2\sum_{i=1}^{d}e_{i}\otimes\frac{\partial\mathbf{Proj}_{n-1}(\xi)}{\partial z^{i}}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}e_{i}\otimes e_{i}\otimes\mathbf{Proj}_{n-2}(\xi)\qquad\text{in }\Omega;\\
\xi={\bf 1}\qquad\text{ on }\partial\Omega;\\
\mathbf{Proj}_{0}(\xi)\equiv 1\text{ and }\mathbf{Proj}_{1}(\xi)\equiv 0\qquad\text{in }\Omega.\end{cases}

Then
\xi(z)
is the expected signature of Brownian motion starting at
z
up to the first exit time
\tau_{\Omega}
;
i.e.
,
\xi=\Phi_{\Omega}
on
\overline{\Omega}
.

Let us outline the main ideas. Following the martingale approach in
[
12
]
for the diffusion process up to a deterministic time, if we can construct a martingale
{N}_{t}
based on
\xi
such that:

(a)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{N}_{t}=S(B_{0,\tau_{\Omega}})
where the limit are taken in both the almost surely and
L^{1}
-sense (note that
S(B_{0,\tau_{\Omega}})
is proven to be
L^{1}
-integrable); and that

(b)

{N}_{0}=\xi(z)
,

then we have
\mathbb{E}^{z}\left[\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}{N}_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}^{z}[{N}_{0}]
and hence
\mathbb{E}^{z}[S(B_{0,\tau_{\Omega}})]=\xi(z)
by Doob’s martingale convergence theorem.

The next question is how to construct the martingale
{N}_{t}
. For this purpose, note that
\hat{N}_{t}:=\mathbb{E}^{z}[S(B_{0,\tau_{\Omega}})|\mathcal{F}_{t\wedge\tau_{\Omega}}]
is a martingale (by Tower’s property) and satisfies the above condition. Thus, in light of Chen’s identity,
\hat{N}_{t}
can be rewritten as
S(B_{0,t\wedge\tau_{\Omega}})\otimes\Phi(B_{t\wedge\tau_{\Omega}})
. This motivates us to consider
\hat{N}_{t}:=S(B_{0,t\wedge\tau_{\Omega}})\otimes\xi(B_{t\wedge\tau_{\Omega}})
. Once we prove that
\hat{N}_{t}
is a martingale, we may conclude that the PDE solution
\xi
coincides with the expected signature
\Phi_{\Omega}
.

Proof.

Since
\xi_{0}=\mathbf{Proj}_{0}(\Phi_{\Omega})\equiv 1
and
\xi_{1}=\mathbf{Proj}_{1}(\Phi_{\Omega})\equiv 0
, we only need to prove that for any
n\geq 2
, one has
\xi_{n}=\mathbf{Proj}_{n}(\Phi_{\Omega})
. To this end, set
N_{t}:=S(B_{0,t})\otimes\xi(B_{t})
. First of all, let us show that
N_{t}
is a martingale, or equivalently, that
\mathbf{Proj}_{n}(N_{t})
is a martingale for every
n\geq 2
.

Indeed, note that

(10)

\displaystyle{\rm d}\pi_{n}(N_{t})={\rm d}\mathbf{Proj}_{n}\Big(S(B_{0,t})\otimes\xi(B_{t})\Big)={\rm d}\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{n}\mathbf{Proj}_{i}\big(S(B_{0,t})\big)\otimes\xi_{n-i}(B_{t})\right\}

and, by the definition of the signature of Brownian motion, it holds that

\displaystyle{\rm d}\mathbf{Proj}_{n}(S(B_{0,t}))

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Proj}_{n-1}(S(B_{0,t}))\otimes\,{\rm d}B_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Proj}_{n-1}(S(B_{0,t}))\otimes\cdot\,{\rm d}B_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Proj}_{n-2}(S(B_{0,t}))\otimes\sum_{j=1}^{d}e_{j}\otimes e_{j}\,{\rm d}t,

where
\cdot{\rm d}B_{t}
is understood in the Itô sense and
n\geq 2
.

On the other hand, we infer from the Itô formulae that

\displaystyle{\rm d}\xi_{n}(B_{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{z_{i}}\xi_{n}(B_{t})\,\cdot{\rm d}B^{i}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\xi_{n}(B_{t})\,{\rm d}t.

Thus, the SDE of
\pi_{n}({N}_{t})
in Eq. (
10
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\rm d}\Big(\mathbf{Proj}_{i}\big(S(B_{0,t})\big)\otimes\xi_{n-i}(B_{t})\Big)

\displaystyle\quad={\rm d}\mathbf{Proj}_{i}\big(S(B_{0,t})\big)\otimes\xi_{n-i}(B_{t})+\mathbf{Proj}_{i}\big(S(B_{0,t})\big)\otimes{\rm d}\xi_{n-i}(B_{t})+{\rm d}\Big\langle\mathbf{Proj}_{i}\big(S(B_{0,t})\big),\xi_{n-i}(B_{t})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbf{1}_{i\geq 2}\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Proj}_{i-2}\big(S(B_{0,t})\big)\otimes\sum_{j=1}^{d}e_{j}\otimes e_{j}\,{\rm d}t\otimes\xi_{n-i}(B_{t})

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbf{Proj}_{i}\big(S(B_{t})\big)\otimes\frac{1}{2}\Delta\xi_{n-i}(B_{t})\,{\rm d}t+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{1}_{i\geq 1}\mathbf{Proj}_{i-1}\big(S(B_{t})\big)\otimes e_{j}\otimes\partial_{z_{j}}\xi_{n-i}(B_{t})+Q^{n,i}_{t}\cdot{\rm d}B_{t},

where
i\leq n
and

\displaystyle Q^{n,i}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Proj}_{i}(S(B_{0,t}))\otimes\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{z_{j}}\xi_{n-i}(B_{t})\,\cdot{\rm d}B_{t}+\mathbf{1}_{i\geq 1}\mathbf{Proj}_{i-1}(S(B_{0,t}))\otimes\cdot{\rm d}B_{t}\otimes\xi_{n-i}(B_{t}).

Summing the above equation over
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
, we get

\displaystyle{\rm d}\mathbf{Proj}_{n}(N_{t})=\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}\mathbf{Proj}_{i}\big(S(B_{t})\big)\otimes\mathcal{R}_{n-i-2}(B_{t})\,{\rm d}t+\sum_{i=0}^{n}Q^{n,i}_{t}\cdot{\rm d}B_{t},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{i}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}e_{j}\otimes e_{j}\otimes\xi_{i}(z)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}e_{j}\otimes\partial_{z_{j}}\xi_{i+1}(z)+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\xi_{i+2}(z).

By the fact that
\xi
is a solution to the given PDE, the drift term equals zero. Therefore,
N_{t}
is a local martingale. It then implies that
Y_{t}:=N_{t\wedge\tau_{\Omega}}
is a local martingale.

It remains to show that
Y_{t}
is a martingale. Indeed, for any
n
and
i\in\{0,1,\cdots,n\}
, and for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Q^{n,i}_{t\wedge\tau_{\Omega}}\right|^{2}\right]<\infty
. This is because for every
i\in\{0,1,2,\cdots\}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{z}\left[|\mathbf{Proj}_{i}(S(B_{0,t\wedge\tau_{\Omega}}))|^{2}\right]<\infty,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{z}\left[\Big|\xi_{i}(B_{t\wedge\tau_{\Omega}})\Big|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big|\partial_{z_{j}}\xi_{i}(B_{t\wedge\tau_{\Omega}})\Big|^{2}\right]<\infty.

Then
\mathbb{E}^{z}[|Y_{t}|]<\infty
by Hölder’s inequality, and hence
Y_{t}
is a martingale. Moreover,
\mathbb{E}^{z}[|Y_{t}|]
is uniformly bounded, thus
\mathbb{E}^{z}[Y_{0}]=\mathbb{E}^{z}[Y_{t}]
for all
t>0
. In addition,
Y_{\infty}=S(B_{0,\tau_{\Omega}})\in L^{1}
, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{z}[Y_{0}]=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}^{z}[Y_{t}]=\mathbb{E}^{z}\left[\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}Y_{t}\right].

We conclude by noting that
\mathbb{E}^{z}[Y_{0}]=\xi(z)
and
\mathbb{E}^{z}[\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}Y_{t}]=\mathbb{E}^{z}[S(B)_{0,\tau_{\Omega}}]
. ∎

4.
Symmetrization of PDEs and Hyperbolic development

To overcome the difficulty that general domains are not rotationally invariant, we integrate the development
\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega}
(see Eq. (
5
)) over all the rotated domains centred at
x\in\Omega
. The resulting object is referred to as the “domain-averaging development” of expected signature in the sequel.

In this section, we first explore general properties of domain-averaging developments for any
M\in{\rm Hom}\big(T((E)),\mathfrak{gl}(d+1;\mathbb{R})\big)
, and then specialise to the hyperbolic development
H=M
.

4.1.
Domain-averaging development of the expected signature

We start with defining several operations on domains. The translation of domain
\Omega
by
h
is
\Omega+h:=\{z+h:\,z\in\Omega\}
. For
R\in SO(d)
, write
\Omega_{R}:=R(\Omega)
, the domain obtained by rotating
\Omega
with respect to the centre
x=0
(see Figure
1
for a 2-dimensional illustration).

Fix an arbitrary
x\in\Omega
. As
\Omega
is open, there is
\epsilon\in]0,1[
such that
\overline{\mathbf{B}(x,\epsilon)}\subset\Omega
. We first note that the expected signature is invariant under translations, namely that
\Phi_{\Omega}(x):=\Phi_{\Omega+h}(x+h)
whenever
x\in\Omega
. Without loss of generality we may assume
x=0\in\Omega
, since otherwise one simply takes
\tilde{\Omega}=\Omega-x
.

Recall that

(11)

\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega}(z):=\lambda M(\Phi_{\Omega}(z))=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}M\mathbf{Proj}_{n}[\Phi_{\Omega}(z)]

for sufficiently small
\lambda
(
[
12
]
, Theorem 3.6). Any
z\in\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)
lies in
\bigcap_{S\in SO(d)}\Omega_{S}
, so
\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega_{R}}(z)
is well defined for each
R\in SO(d)
.

Figure 1.
Rotation of the 2-dimensional domain
\Omega
(whose boundary is the solid blue curve) by degree
\alpha=\pi/\penalty 4
with respect to the centre marked in orange. The boundary of the rotated domain
R_{\alpha}(\Omega)
is the dashed blue curve, where
R_{\alpha}\in SO(2)
is the rotation matrix by
\alpha
.

Definition 4.1

(Domain-averaging development of expected signature)
.

Let
\Omega\Subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a domain containing
\overline{\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)}
. Set

(12)

\overline{\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}(z)=\int_{SO(d)}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega_{R}}(z)\,{\rm d}\chi(R)\in\mathfrak{gl}(d+1;\mathbb{R})\qquad\text{ for each }z\in\overline{\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)},

where
\chi
is the normalised Haar measure on
SO(d)
, and integration of matrices in
\mathfrak{gl}(d+1;\mathbb{R})
is understood in the entry-wise sense. For ease of notations, we write
\overline{\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}:=\overline{\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}
.

Lemma 4.2

(Rotational “symmetry” of
\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega}
)
.

For any
z\in\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)\subset\Omega
and
R\in SO(d)
,

(13)

\displaystyle\left(R\oplus{\bf id}\right)\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega}(z)\left(R^{\top}\oplus{\bf id}\right)=\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega_{R}}(Rz).

Proof.

This extends the proof of Corollary 4 for the unit disc in
[
1
]
, where
{\bf id}
is the identity map on
\mathbb{R}^{1}
and
R^{\top}
the transpose of
R
. Since the Brownian motion
B^{Rz}

starting at
Rz
has the same distribution as the rotated Brownian motion
R(B^{z})
(with
B^{z}
starting from
z
), for any
z\in\bigcap_{R\in SO(d)}R(\Omega)
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{z}\left[S\left(RB\right)_{\tau_{\Omega}}\right]=\mathbb{E}^{Rz}\left[S(B)_{\tau_{R(\Omega)}}\right].

Applying
\lambda M
to both sides of the above equation, we have

\displaystyle\lambda M\left(\mathbb{E}^{z}\left[S\left(RB\right)_{\tau_{\Omega}}\right]\right)=\lambda M\left(\mathbb{E}^{R(z)}\left[S(B)_{\tau_{R(\Omega)}}\right]\right).

By Lemma 3 in
[
1
]
(which clearly holds in arbitrary dimension), it equals

\displaystyle\lambda M\left(S\left(RB\right)_{\tau_{\Omega}}\right)=\left(R\oplus{\bf id}\right)\left\{\lambda M\left[S\left(B\right)_{\tau_{\Omega_{R}}}\right]\right\}\left(R^{\top}\oplus{\bf id}\right).

As
R
is deterministic, it is furthermore equal to

\displaystyle\left(R\oplus{\bf id}\right)\Big\{(\lambda M)\mathbb{E}^{z}\left[S(B)_{\tau_{\Omega}}\right]\Big\}\left(R^{\top}\oplus{\bf id}\right)=(\lambda M)\mathbb{E}^{Rz}\left[S(B)_{\tau_{\Omega_{R}}}\right].

This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Write
r=|z|
for
z\in\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)\subset\Omega
. Then for any
R\in SO(d)
,

(14)

(R\oplus{\bf id})\overline{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(r)\left(R^{\top}\oplus{\bf id}\right)=\overline{\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(z).

Proof.

Let
z=R(r,\underbrace{0,\cdots,0}_{d-1\text{ zeros}})^{\top}\in\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)\subset\bigcap_{O\in SO(d)}\Omega_{O}\subset\Omega
. We deduce from Eq. (
13
) that

(R\oplus{\bf id})\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega_{S}}(r)\left(R^{\top}\oplus{\bf id}\right)=\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega_{R\circ S}}\left(R(r,0,\cdots,0)^{\top}\right)=\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega_{R\circ S}}(z)

for any
S\in SO(d)
. We can now conclude by integrating both sides of this equation against the Haar measure
\mu
on
SO(d)
.
∎

As in
[
1
]
let us consider
F_{\lambda,\Omega}:=M_{\lambda,\Omega}(z)\cdot[\underbrace{0,\cdots,0}_{d-1\text{ zeros}},1]^{\top}
, and similarly

(15)

\overline{\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(z):=\overline{\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(z)\cdot[\underbrace{0,\cdots,0}_{d-1\text{ zeros}},1]^{\top}.

The following can be deduced directly from Lemma
4.3
.

Lemma 4.4

(Separation of variables)
.

For any
z\in\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)\subset\Omega
and
R\in SO(d)
, it holds that

(16)

\overline{\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(z)=(R\oplus{\bf id})\overline{{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}\left((r,\underbrace{0,\cdots,0}_{d-1\text{ zeros}})^{\top}\right).

In addition,
\overline{\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\Omega}
are related in the following manner:

Lemma 4.5
.

For
\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)\subset\Omega
, let
\overline{C_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(r)=[\underbrace{0,\cdots,0}_{d-1\text{ zeros}},1]\overline{\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(r)
and
C_{\lambda}(z)=[\underbrace{0,\cdots,0}_{d-1\text{ zeros}},1]\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\Omega}(z)
. Then
C_{\lambda}(0)=\overline{C_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(0)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.3
we have, for each
R\in SO(d)
, that

\displaystyle(R\oplus{\bf id})\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega}(0)\left(R^{\top}\oplus{\bf id}\right)=\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega_{R}}(0).

The
(d+1,d+1)^{\text{th}}
entry of both sides of the above equation is
C_{\lambda,\Omega}(0)=C_{\lambda,\Omega_{R}}(0)
. So, integrating both sides over the angular variable gives us
C_{\lambda,\Omega}(0)=\overline{C_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(0).
∎

Lemma 4.6

(Preservation of finite radius of convergence)
.

For
\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)\subset\Omega
, if
\overline{\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(0)
has finite radius of convergence, then so does
\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega}(0)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.3
we have
(R\oplus{\bf id})\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega}(0)\left(R^{\top}\oplus{\bf id}\right)=\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega_{R}}(0)
, which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega}(0)=(R^{\top}\oplus{\bf id})\mathcal{M}_{\lambda,\Omega_{R}}(0)\left(R\oplus{\bf id}\right).

The assertion follows immediately from the definition of radius of convergence. ∎

4.2.
Symmetrization of PDEs

By Corollary
3.3
(compare also with
[
12
]
), the map
z\mapsto\Phi_{\Omega}(z)
is twice differentiable on
\Omega
. This leads to

Theorem 4.7

(PDE for
\overline{\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}
)
.

Assume
\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)\subset\Omega
. There exists a
\lambda^{*}>0
such that for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\lambda|<\lambda^{*}
,
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\Omega}
is twice continuously differentiable on
\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)
. Moreover, it satisfies

(17)

\Delta\overline{\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(z)=-2\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{d}Me_{i}\frac{\partial\overline{\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}}{\partial z_{i}}(z)-\lambda^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(Me_{i}\right)^{2}\right)\overline{\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(z)\qquad\text{for any $z\in\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)$}.

Proof.

Consider

(18)

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}=\sup\left\{|\lambda|:\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}\left|\left|\mathbf{Proj}_{n}(\Phi_{\Omega}(z))\right|\right|<\infty\text{ for all }z\in\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)\right\}.

As remarked above,
z\mapsto\overline{\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\epsilon}(z)}
is
C^{2}
on
\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)
. The definition of
\overline{\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}
and Eq. (
12
) give us

(19)

\overline{\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(z)=\int_{SO(d)}\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\Omega_{R}}(z)\,{\rm d}\chi(R).

We shall use the PDE for
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\Omega_{R}}
to derive that for
\overline{\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}
. Indeed, for any
R\in SO(d)
we have

\Delta\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\Omega_{R}}(z)=-2\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{d}Me_{i}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\Omega_{R}}}{\partial z_{i}}(z)-\lambda^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(Me_{i}\right)^{2}\right)\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\Omega_{R}}(z).

As
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\Omega_{R}}
is
C^{2}
, we can differentiate under integral signs to get

\displaystyle\int_{SO(d)}\Delta\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\Omega_{R}}(z)\,{\rm d}\chi(R)

(20)

\displaystyle\qquad=-2\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{d}Me_{i}\int_{SO(d)}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\Omega_{R}}}{\partial z_{i}}(z)\,{\rm d}\chi(R)-\lambda^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(Me_{i}\right)^{2}\right)\overline{\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\epsilon}(z)}.

∎

4.3.
Hyperbolic development

Recall that the hyperbolic development (Hambly–Lyons
[
7
]
; Boedihardjo–Diehl–Mezzarobba–Ni
[
1
]
) is the morphism
H:T((E))\to\mathfrak{gl}(d+1;\mathbb{R})
—

\displaystyle H(x,y):=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&z^{1}\\
0&0&\cdots&0&z^{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&0&z^{d}\\
z^{1}&z^{2}&\cdots&z^{d}&0\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{ for }z=(z^{1},\ldots,z^{d})\in E,

\displaystyle H(v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{n}):=H(v_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot H(v_{n})\quad\text{ for any $n\in\mathbb{N}$ and $v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in E$}.

Let
\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}
denote the last column of domain-averaging development of expected stopped Brownian motion
\overline{\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}
corresponding to the hyperbolic development
H
.

Theorem 4.8

(ODE system of
\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}
)
.

Write, in the case
d=2
,

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(r)=:\left[\bar{A}_{\lambda}(r),\bar{B}_{\lambda}(r),\bar{C}_{\lambda}(r)\right]^{\top}.

When
|\lambda|<\lambda^{\star}
in Eq. (
18
), the following ODE system holds:

(21)

\displaystyle r^{2}\bar{A}_{\lambda}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}+r\bar{A}_{\lambda}^{{}^{\prime}}(r)-\bar{A}_{\lambda}(r)+\lambda^{2}r^{2}\bar{A}_{\lambda}(r)+2\lambda r^{2}\bar{C}_{\lambda}^{{}^{\prime}}(r)=0,

(22)

\displaystyle r^{2}\bar{B}_{\lambda}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(r)+r\bar{B}_{\lambda}^{\prime}(r)+(\lambda^{2}r^{2}-1)\bar{B}_{\lambda}(r)=0,

(23)

\displaystyle\bar{C}^{{}^{\prime}}_{\lambda}(r)+r\bar{C}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}_{\lambda}(r)+2\lambda^{2}r\bar{C}_{\lambda}(r)+2\lambda r\bar{A}^{{}^{\prime}}_{\lambda}(r)+2\lambda\bar{A}_{\lambda}(r)=0.

To ensure that
\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}
is
C^{2}
at the origin
x=0
, one needs to impose the conditions

(24)

\bar{A}_{\lambda}(0)=0,\quad\bar{B}_{\lambda}(0)=0,\quad\bar{C}^{{}^{\prime}}_{\lambda}(0)=0.

Proof.

As in
[
1
]

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)\subset\Omega
and let
\lambda^{\ast}
be as in Eq. (

). For every
\lambda\in[0,\lambda^{*}[
and

\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(r):=\left[h_{\lambda}^{(1)}(r),\cdots,h_{\lambda}^{(d+1)}(r)\right]^{\top}
. Then
h^{(d+1)}_{\lambda}(r)\geq 1
for every

Proof.

As the hyperbolic development is a normed algebra morphism, for any
\lambda\in[0,\lambda^{*}[
with
\lambda^{*}
defined in Eq. (
17
),

\displaystyle||(\lambda H)(\Phi_{\Omega}(z))||

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{n\geq 0}\lambda^{n}H(\mathbf{Proj}_{n}(\Phi_{\Omega}(z))\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\leq 0}\lambda^{n}\|H\|^{n}\|\mathbf{Proj}(\Phi_{\Omega}(z))\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\leq 0}\lambda^{n}\|\mathbf{Proj}(\Phi_{\Omega}(z))\|<\infty,

where the norm of
H
is
1
.
By the dominated convergence theorem, we can interchange the expectation and
\lambda H
to obtain that

\displaystyle\lambda H(\Phi_{\Omega}(z))=\mathbb{E}^{z}\left[\lambda H\left(S(B)_{0,\tau_{\Omega}}\right)\right].

By Lemma
2.7
, the Stratonovich signature of the Brownian motion
\left(S(B)_{0,t}\right)_{t\in[0,T]}
is a geometric rough path for any finite
T>0
. That is, there exists a sequence of bounded 1-variation paths
\left(B^{m}_{[0,T]}\right)_{m=1}^{\infty}
whose signatures converge almost surely to
\left(S(B)_{0,t}\right)_{t\in[0,T]}
in the
p
-variation distance for any
p>2
. For instance, we can choose
B^{m}_{[0,T]}
as the dyadic piecewise linear approximation of Brownian motion with mesh size
2^{-m}
up to time
T
.

It then holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\lambda H\left(S\left(B^{m}_{[0,T]}\right)\right)\overset{a.s}{=}\lambda H\left(S\left(B_{[0,T]}\right)\right).

As
\mathbb{E}^{z}[\tau_{\Omega}]
is almost surely finite, we can send
T
and
m
to
+\infty
to get

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\lambda H\left(S\left(B^{m}_{\left[0,T\wedge\tau_{\Omega}\right]}\right)\right)\overset{a.s}{=}\lambda H\left(S\left(B_{\left[0,\tau_{\Omega}\right]}\right)\right).

Note that for any
T>0
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the boundness of
1
-variation of
B^{m}_{[0,T]}
in the almost sure sense ensures that
\lambda H\left(S\left(B^{m}_{[0,T]}\right)\right)
is finite almost surly.
Lyons–Xu
[
13
]
showed that any bounded 1-variation path
\gamma
satisfies
H\left(S(\gamma)\right)\cdot[0,\cdots,0,1]^{\top}\in\mathbb{H}_{d}
, with

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{d}:=\left\{\left(x^{(1)},\cdots,x^{(d)},x^{(d+1)}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}:\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(x^{(i)}\right)^{2}-\left(x^{(d+1)}\right)^{2}=-1,\,x^{(d+1)}>0\right\}.

That is, the last co-ordinate of the hyperbolic development of the signature of
B^{m}_{[0,T\wedge\tau_{\Omega}]}\in\mathbb{N}
is no less than
1
almost surely for all
m
and
T>0
. Thus, the expectation of the limit of
\left(\lambda H(S(B^{m})_{[0,T\wedge\tau_{\Omega}}])^{(d+1)}\right)_{m,T}
, as
m,T\nearrow\infty
, is greater than or equal to

.

Therefore, we can conclude the desired result:

\displaystyle h^{(d+1)}_{\lambda}(r)=\left(\mathbb{E}^{z}\left[\lambda H\left(S\left(B_{\left[0,\tau_{\Omega}\right]}\right)\right)\right]\left(0,\cdots,0,1\right)^{\top}\right)^{(d+1)}\geq 1.

∎

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)
,
\lambda^{\ast}
, and
\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon}}(r)
be the same as in Lemma

above, where

. Then

for every

and

.

Proof.

By
[
13
]
again,
H\left(S(\gamma)\right)\cdot[0,\cdots,0,1]^{\top}:=\left(H^{(1)},\cdots,H^{(d+1)}\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{H}_{d}
and

for any bounded 1-variation path

. In particular,

denote the dyadic approximation of Brownian motion as in the proof of Lemma

, which has bounded

Step 1.

Then, by the interior regularity Theorem 8.10 in

\displaystyle h^{(d+1)}(0)=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}r^{1-\frac{d}{2}}\frac{{\rm Im}\left\{\eta_{-}\beta_{-}\left[J_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\left(\frac{\lambda\beta_{+}r}{2}\right)\right]^{\dagger}J_{\frac{d}{2}}\left(\frac{\lambda\beta_{+}}{2}\right)\right\}}{\Theta(\lambda)}=:\frac{\mathcal{N}(\lambda)}{\Theta(\lambda)}.

Let

\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\Omega}(z)
be the hyperbolic development of the expected signature of a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion starting from

and stopped at the first exit time from
\Omega
(scaled by
\lambda
). We are concerned with the domain-averaging hyperbolic development. Without loss of generality we may assume

, as
\Phi_{\Omega}(z)=\Phi_{\Omega-x}(z-x)

Recall from Definition
4.1
the domain-averaging hyperbolic development (with integration understood entry-wise):

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}(z):=\int_{SO(d)}\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\Omega_{R}}(z)\,{\rm d}\chi(R).

Here
\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}(z)\in\mathfrak{gl}(d+1;\mathbb{R})
for each

, with

chosen so small that
\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)\subset\bigcap_{S\in SO(d)}\Omega_{S}
. Compare with Proposition
6.3
where

By Theorem

,
\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}

Theorem 6.9

(PDE for
\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}
)
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and
\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon)\subset\bigcap_{R\in SO(d)}\Omega_{R}
. There exists
\lambda^{*}>0
such that for every
\lambda\in[0,\lambda^{*}]
,

\Delta\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}(z)=-2\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{d}He_{i}\frac{\partial\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}}{\partial z^{i}}(z)-\lambda^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(He_{i}\right)^{2}\right)\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}(z)\qquad\text{ for each }z\in\mathbf{B}(0,\epsilon).

The domain-averaging hyperbolic development

on a general domain
\Omega
does not have the sparseness property as in Lemma
6.1
, in contrast to the case of the unit disc. To overcome this difficulty, we shall symmetrize
\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}(z)
with a reflected version of it. More precisely, consider

\displaystyle\Omega_{\ast}:=\{(x_{1},-x_{2},\cdots,-x_{d}):\,(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d})\in\Omega\}.

Assume as before
0\in\Omega
; thus, there is an
\epsilon>0
such that
\mathbf{D}_{\epsilon}
, the disc of radius
\epsilon
, lies in
\Omega
. Set

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}(z):=\frac{\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}(z)+\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega_{\ast}}}(z)}{2}\qquad\text{ for }z\in\mathbf{D}_{\epsilon}.

Lemma 6.10

(Sparseness of
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}
)
.

\widetilde{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}
evaluated at
{\bf r}=(r,0,\cdots,0)
for
r\in]0,\epsilon[
has all components being zero, except possibly for the first and the last ones.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Lemma
6.1
for the unit disc case. Let
(B_{t})_{t}
denote the standard
d
-dimensional Brownian path.
Consider the reflected path

(67)

\hat{B}_{t}:=\left(B^{(1)}_{t},-B^{(2)}_{t},\cdots,-B^{(d)}_{t}\right)^{\top},

which is still a Brownian motion. If
B_{t}
starts at
\mathbf{r}=(r,0,\cdots,0)^{\top}
, we may further require that
\hat{B}_{0}=\mathbf{r}
.
By the definition of
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}
, for
k\in\{2,\cdots,d\}
we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}^{(k)}(r,0,\cdots,0)

\displaystyle\quad:=\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}^{(k)}(r)+\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega_{\ast}}}^{(k)}(r)

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{{\bf S}^{d-1}}\left\{\mathbb{E}^{r}\left[\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{E}_{2n}^{k}}\mathbf{Proj}_{\omega}\left(S\left(B_{\tau_{\Omega_{\alpha}}}\right)\right)\right]+\mathbb{E}^{r}\left[\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{E}_{2n}^{k}}\mathbf{Proj}_{\omega}\left(S\left(\hat{B}_{\tau_{\Omega_{\ast,\alpha}}}\right)\right)\right]\right\}\,{\rm d}\mu(\alpha)

\displaystyle\quad=0.

The last line holds as
\mathbf{Proj}_{\omega}\left(S\left(B_{\tau_{\Omega_{\alpha}}}\right)\right)+\mathbf{Proj}_{\omega}\left(S\left(\hat{B}_{\tau_{\Omega_{\ast,\alpha}}}\right)\right)=0
almost surely for
\omega\in\mathcal{E}_{2n}^{k}
. ∎

As before, write
\eta_{\pm}:=d+3\pm\sqrt{d^{2}-10d+9}
,
\beta_{\pm}:=\sqrt{2d-6\pm 2\sqrt{d^{2}-10d+9}}
, and
r=|z|
. We have the following analogue of Theorem
6.5
.

Theorem 6.11
.

Let
\mathbf{D}_{\epsilon}\subset\Omega
and

. Denote the boundary condition for

Remark 6.12
.

For

Theorem
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded
C^{2,\alpha}
-domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for some
d\in\{2,\ldots,8\}
and
\alpha>0
. The expected signature
\Phi
of a Brownian motion stopped upon the first exit time from
\Omega
has finite radius of convergence everywhere on
\Omega
.

Proof.

Our arguments will essentially be an adaptation of the proof of Theorem
6.8
. We shall only indicate necessary modifications.

First of all, Step 1 in the proof of Theorem
1.1
(
d=2
case) in §
5
remains valid for
\Omega\Subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This is the only place that we need the

-regularity of the domain. We conclude that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{Proj}_{n}\Phi\right\|_{C^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C^{n}

for

depending only on the geometry of
\Omega
, the Hölder index
\alpha
, and the dimension
d
.

Consider now
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\epsilon,\Omega}}=\left(h^{(1)}_{\lambda},\cdots,h^{(d+1)}_{\lambda}\right)^{\top}
. We show that there exists
\lambda_{\star}>0
for which
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\lambda_{\star}}h^{(d+1)}_{\lambda}(0)=\infty
. Label as before the boundary conditions at
r=\epsilon
by

(70)

h^{(1)}_{\lambda}(\epsilon)=\mathfrak{p}\quad\text{ and }\quad h^{(d+1)}_{\lambda}(\epsilon)=\mathfrak{q},

where, as a precaution,
\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{q}
depend in general on
\lambda
and
\epsilon
.

By Theorem
6.11
and the ensuing remark, we have the closed-form expression

\displaystyle h^{(d+1)}_{\lambda}(r)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{r}{\epsilon}\right)^{1-\frac{d}{2}}\left\{\frac{\frac{1}{8d}\mathfrak{p}\cdot{\rm Im}\,\left[\eta_{+}\beta_{+}\eta_{-}\beta_{-}J_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\left(\frac{\lambda\beta_{-}\epsilon}{2}\right)J_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\left(\frac{\lambda\beta_{+}r}{2}\right)\right]+\mathfrak{q}\cdot{\rm Im}\,\left[\eta_{-}\beta_{-}J_{\frac{d}{2}}\left(\frac{\lambda\beta_{+}\epsilon}{2}\right)J_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\left(\frac{\lambda\beta_{-}r}{2}\right)\right]}{{\rm Im}\left[\eta_{-}\beta_{-}J_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\left(\frac{\lambda\beta_{-}\epsilon}{2}\right)J_{\frac{d}{2}}\left(\frac{\lambda\beta_{+}\epsilon}{2}\right)\right]}\right\}

for
d\in\{2,\dots,8\}
. Set
h^{(d+1)}_{\lambda}(0):=\lim_{r\searrow 0}h^{(d+1)}_{\lambda}(r)
; by properties of the Bessel function,

\displaystyle h^{(d+1)}_{\lambda}(0)=\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}(\lambda)}{\Theta_{\epsilon}(\lambda)}

with the numerator

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\underset{r\rightarrow 0}{\lim}\left(\frac{r}{\epsilon}\right)^{1-\frac{d}{2}}\Bigg\{\frac{\mathfrak{p}}{8d}\cdot{\rm Im}\,\left[\eta_{+}\beta_{+}\eta_{-}\beta_{-}J_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\left(\frac{\lambda\beta_{-}\epsilon}{2}\right)J_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\left(\frac{\lambda\beta_{+}r}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\mathfrak{q}\cdot{\rm Im}\,\left[\eta_{-}\beta_{-}J_{\frac{d}{2}}\left(\frac{\lambda\beta_{+}\epsilon}{2}\right)J_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\left(\frac{\lambda\beta_{-}r}{2}\right)\right]\Bigg\}

(71)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\mathfrak{p}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\cdot{\rm Im}\,\left[\frac{1}{8d}\eta_{+}\beta_{+}\eta_{-}\beta_{-}J_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\left(\frac{\lambda\beta_{-}\epsilon}{2}\right)\left(\frac{\lambda\beta_{+}\epsilon}{4}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\right]}_{\mathcal{N}^{(2)}_{\epsilon}(\lambda)}

4.9
and Corollary

and

Case 1:
\mathfrak{p}=0
.
Then we have

.
Since the 2-dimensional case has been treated separately, we assume from now on

. Once we establish the following

. Indeed, it holds that

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}(\lambda)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathfrak{q}\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}^{(1)}(\lambda)+\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}^{(2)}(\lambda)\right|

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{p}\left|\frac{\mathfrak{q}}{\mathfrak{p}}\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}^{(1)}(\lambda)+\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}^{(2)}(\lambda)\right|

\displaystyle\geq\mathfrak{p}\left(\left|\frac{\mathfrak{q}}{\mathfrak{p}}\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}^{(1)}(\lambda)\right|-\left|\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}^{(2)}(\lambda)\right|\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathfrak{p}\left(\left|\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}^{(1)}(\lambda)\right|-\left|\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}^{(2)}(\lambda)\right|\right)

\displaystyle>0.

The proof of
claim
(
72
) will be presented in the appendix. See Lemma
A.5
. ∎

6.3.
Concluding remarks

We have proved in this paper the finiteness of radius of convergence of the expected signature for stopped Brownian motions in dimensions
2\leq d\leq 8
. The restriction on the range of
d
mainly arises from the ODE system for the hyperbolic development
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,\Omega}
derived in Proposition
6.3
(see also Theorem
6.5
). This ODE has explicit solution involving key parameters
\eta_{\pm}:=d+3\pm\sqrt{d^{2}-10d+9}
and

In this appendix, we first collect a (crude) upper bound for the Bessel functions:

Lemma A.1
.

Consider Bessel functions of the first kind,
J_{\nu}(z)
, and their Taylor expansion:

\displaystyle{J}_{\nu}(z):=\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{\nu}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\frac{\left(\frac{z^{2}}{4}\right)^{k}}{k!\Gamma(k+\nu+1)}\qquad\text{ for $z\in\mathbb{C}$}.

Write
J_{\nu}(z)=\mathring{J}_{\nu}^{n}(z)+\mathscr{R}_{\nu}^{n}(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}+\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}
. The remainder term
\mathscr{R}_{\nu}^{n}(z)
can be estimated by

\displaystyle\left|\mathscr{R}_{\nu}^{n}(z)\right|\leq E(z,n,\nu)\qquad\text{for $|z|<2(n+1)$},

where

\displaystyle E(z,n,\nu):=\begin{cases}\left\{\frac{\left(\frac{|z|}{2}\right)^{2n+\nu}}{n!(n+\nu)!}\right\}\cdot\left\{\frac{1}{1-\frac{|z|^{2}}{4(n+1)^{2}}}\right\}&\text{ if $\nu$ is an integer},\\
\left\{\frac{2^{n}}{{n!(2n+2\nu+1)!!}}\left(\frac{|z|}{2}\right)^{2n+\nu}\right\}\cdot\left\{\frac{1}{1-\frac{|z|^{2}}{4(n+1)^{2}}}\right\}&\text{ if $\nu$ is a half-integer}.\end{cases}

Proof.

From the Taylor expansion for
J_{\nu}
we get

\displaystyle\left|\mathscr{R}_{\nu}^{n}(z)\right|\leq\left(\frac{|z|}{2}\right)^{\nu}\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{|z|}{2}\right)^{2k}}{k!\Gamma(k+\nu+1)}=\left(\frac{|z|}{2}\right)^{2n+\nu}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{|z|}{2}\right)^{2k}}{(n+k)!\Gamma(n+k+\nu+1)}.

Then, we deduce from the functional identity
\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)
that

\displaystyle\left|\mathscr{R}_{\nu}^{n}(z)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{|z|}{2}\right)^{2n+\nu}\frac{1}{n!\Gamma(n+\nu+1)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{|z|}{2}\right)^{2k}}{(n+1)^{2k}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{|z|}{2}\right)^{2n+\nu}\frac{1}{n!\Gamma(n+\nu+1)}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\frac{|z|^{2}}{4(n+1)^{2}}}\qquad\text{ for }|z|<2(n+1).

The proof is complete in view of the special values for
\Gamma(z)
at integers and half-integers. ∎

The following simple lemma is rather helpful when estimating errors in Taylor expansions:

Lemma A.2
.

Let
f:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a non-increasing function. Let
g:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be such that
\sup_{r\in[a,b]}|f(r)-g(r)|\leq C
for some constant
C
. Then
\sup_{r\in[a,b]}g(r)\leq f(a)+C
.

Proof.

By the monotonicity of
f
, for any
r\in[a,b]
,

\displaystyle g(r)=g(r)-f(r)+f(r)\leq g(r)-f(r)+f(a)\leq|g(r)-f(r)|+f(a).

Therefore, taking the supremum over
[a,b]
, we get

\displaystyle\sup_{r\in[a,b]}g(r)\leq\sup_{r\in[a,b]}|g(r)-f(r)|+f(a)\leq C+f(a).

∎

\displaystyle 0<{\rm Im}\left\{J_{0}\left(\mu\bar{\zeta}\right)\right\}<-{\rm Im}\left\{\bar{\alpha}J_{1}\left(\mu\bar{\zeta}\right)\right\}\quad\text{ for all }\mu\in[2.5,3].
