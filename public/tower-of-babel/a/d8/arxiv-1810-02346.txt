Geometry and Conservation Laws for a Class of Second-Order Parabolic Equations II: Conservation Laws
Benjamin B. MCMILLAN

Abstract
I consider the existence and structure of conservation laws for the general class of evolutionary scalar second-order differential equations with parabolic symbol. First I calculate the linearized characteristic cohomology for such equations. This provides an auxiliary differential equation satisfied by the conservation laws of a given parabolic equation. This is used to show that conservation laws for any evolutionary parabolic equation depend on at most second derivatives of solutions. As a corollary, it is shown that the only evolutionary parabolic equations with at least one non-trivial conservation law are of Monge–Ampère type.

Introduction

The celebrated contributions of Noether began the systematic study of conservation laws for non-linear partial differential equations. More recently, Vinogradov introduced cohomological tools to the calculation of conservation laws, with the
\mathcal{C}
-spectral sequence of
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
. Much work has been developed from the
\mathcal{C}
-spectral sequence by Vinogradov and subsequent authors, but particularly relevant here is the work of Bryant and Griffiths in
[
3
]
, where they translate the
\mathcal{C}
-spectral sequence to the setting of exterior differential systems. Their approach is particularly amenable to considerations of the coordinate invariant geometry of a given class of differential equation.

In a follow-up paper
[
4
]
, Bryant and Griffiths applied their technique to the class of non-linear second-order scalar parabolic equations for one unknown function of 2 variables. They defined the class of 7-dimensional exterior differential systems that are locally equivalent to such parabolic equations and showed that, in this case, conservation laws are in bijection with functions satisfying an auxiliary differential equation on the 7-manifold. Their 7-manifold is essentially the space of 2-jets of solutions, so an immediate consequence of their theorem is that any conservation law in this context depends on at most second derivatives of solutions. This should be contrasted with other classes of differential equation, of which there are many well known examples whose conservation laws depend on arbitrarily many derivatives of solutions – one famous example being the KdV equation. Bryant and Griffiths also showed that a 2-variable parabolic equation with at least one non-trivial conservation law is necessarily of Monge–Ampère type.

There are new phenomena for parabolic equations in three or more variables. For example, it is no longer the case that every parabolic equation can be put into evolutionary form. In her thesis
[
5
]
, Clelland studied the exterior differential systems corresponding to evolutionary parabolic equations in 3 variables. She showed that in this case too, conservation laws depend on at most second derivatives of solutions and only Monge–Ampère equations have non-trivial conservation laws.

In this paper, I extend these results to evolutionary parabolic equations in any number of variables. I show that for any evolutionary parabolic equation, conservation laws are functionals depending on at most second derivatives of solutions. As a corollary, if an evolutionary parabolic equation has at least one non-trivial conservation law, then it is necessarily Monge–Ampère (Theorem
8.2
).

The jump from 3 to all degrees of freedom relies on the author’s recent progress on the geometry of parabolic differential equations.
In
[
6
]
(henceforth, Part I), I introduce the general class of exterior differential systems that model second-order parabolic differential equations.
This puts the class of parabolic equations in a more geometric form, which I then study using Cartan’s method of equivalence, introducing several local invariants of interest.

The local invariants derived from the equivalence problem – and their geometric interpretation – are crucial to the calculations in this paper.
Indeed, the extended Goursat invariants introduced in Part I allow for a simplification of the structure equations of evolutionary parabolic type exterior differential systems.
This reduction makes the calculations done here significantly more tractable.
The Monge–Ampère invariants, also introduced in Part I, provide a geometric characterization of Monge–Ampère parabolic equations.
To wit, the existence of a non-trivial conservation law on an evolutionary parabolic equation allows one to show that a component of the Monge–Ampère invariants vanishes identically, which is enough to show that the equation is Monge–Ampère.

To be clear, this strategy is the same as was applied by the previous authors in dimensions 2 and 3.
The difficulty is that the complexity of the equivalence problem grows quickly; the number of local invariants is of order 4 in dimension.
This complexity can be mitigated in higher dimensions with a careful application of representation theory.
Indeed, the local invariants take values in various representations of
\SO(n)
, so that we may consider the invariants in families.
From this perspective, the Goursat and the Monge–Ampère invariants are treated the same in all dimensions, in much the same way as the Riemannian curvature tensor of Riemannian geometry is.

To expand on this point, in any Cartan equivalence problem there is an associated Lie group that controls the local invariants.
The Lie group
G
associated to the geometry of parabolic equations is somewhat complicated to write down, but it contains a copy of
\SO(n)
.
(Here
n+1
is the number of independent variables in the given parabolic equation.)
The local invariants of a parabolic equation are equivariant functions from a
G
-structure to a particular representation of
G
.
The local invariants can then be categorized according to which subrepresentations they take values in, and thus treated as unitary objects.
It is worth noting that in 2 and 3 dimensions the representation theory is obscured simply by low rank considerations, and there it is simpler to treat each scalar invariant separately.

The first several sections of this paper are more or less expository, devoted to recalling known results and tools, but with an emphasis on parabolic equations. In Section

Also of note are the geometric characterizations of evolutionary parabolic equations and of Monge–Ampère parabolic equations.

In Section

(M_{0},\I_{0})
. This is an infinite-dimensional replacement of
M_{0}
that has the same solutions as
M_{0}

To experts, there will be no surprises here, just a direct calculation depending on the symbol type of a parabolic system.

Sections
4

5
, and
6

[

Effort has been made throughout to present the material in such way that they may provide a useful guide for other examples.

In Section

M_{0}
. This function satisfies an auxiliary differential equation on the infinite prolongation, so that in principle, one needs to solve an infinite-dimensional PDE to find conservation laws.

In Section

Any conservation law for any evolutionary parabolic equation depends on at most second derivatives of solutions.

If an evolutionary parabolic equation has at least one non-trivial conservation law, then it is of Monge–Ampère type in the neighborhood where the conservation law does not vanish.

Now consider a parabolic system

Furthermore, any

has a unique lift to

are in bijection with those of

for each

Let

be the infinite prolongation of

, the inverse limit of the prolongation tower. More precisely, the manifold

is given by the inverse limit of underlying manifolds,

\displaystyle M=\varprojlim M_{0}^{(r)},

and the ideal
\I
is given by

\displaystyle\I=\bigcup_{r=0}^{\infty}\I^{(r)}.

The manifold

is infinite-dimensional, but this will not cause any technical difficulty in practice, because any conservation law factors through some finite level. In particular, we are concerned with conservation laws of
finite type
, which are represented by certain differential forms pulled back from a finite prolongation of
M_{0}
.
Accordingly, it will suffice to consider finite-type functions on
M
, those that can be expressed as the pullback of a function on
M_{0}^{(r)}
for some
r
.
The space of finite-type smooth functions is given by

\displaystyle C^{\infty}(M)=\bigcup_{r=0}^{\infty}C^{\infty}\big(M_{0}^{(r)}\big)

and the finite-type differential forms by

\displaystyle\Omega^{*}(M)=\bigcup_{r=0}^{\infty}\Omega^{*}\big(M_{0}^{(r)}\big).

It is worth noting that a function

is one which, when restricted to the
r
-jet graph of a solution

to the differential equation
F
, results in a functional of
x
, of

, and of the derivatives of

up to order

. For example, a point of
M_{0}
is the 2-jet of a solution, and a function on
M_{0}
is a functional depending on at most second derivatives of solutions.

Now for some linear algebra preliminaries, which will be used to describe the structure of
(M,\I)
. Fix a vector space
W
and subspace
W^{\prime}
of respective dimensions
n+1
and
n
, and basis

on

so that

\displaystyle W^{\prime}=\R\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}\subset W=\R\{e_{0},\dots,e_{n}\}.

Let

\displaystyle W^{\prime\prime}=W/W^{\prime}\cong\R\{e_{0}\}.

This decomposition of
W
induces one on the symmetric powers of
W
, so that

\displaystyle\Sym^{r}W\cong\bigoplus_{s+t=r}\Sym^{s}(W^{\prime})\otimes\Sym^{t}(W^{\prime\prime}).

For notation, elements will be indexed with regards to this splitting, so that
\Sym^{r}(W)
has basis given by the elements

\displaystyle e_{I,t}:=e_{i_{1}}{\circ}\cdots{\circ}e_{i_{s}}{\circ}(e_{0})^{t}

for
I=(i_{1}\dots i_{s})
as

ranges over symmetric multi-indices of length
|I|
up to

, and

.

It will be convenient to relabel the adapted coframing

,
\theta_{\varnothing}
,

,

of

so that it is consistent with the basis of

\displaystyle\theta_{\varnothing}\xmapsto{\hskip 8.19447pt}\theta_{\varnothing,0},\qquad\pi_{ij}\xmapsto{\hskip 8.19447pt}\pi_{ij,0},

\displaystyle\theta_{i}\xmapsto{\hskip 8.19447pt}\theta_{i,0},\qquad\ \,\pi_{i0}\xmapsto{\hskip 8.19447pt}\pi_{i,1},

\displaystyle\theta_{0}\xmapsto{\hskip 8.19447pt}\theta_{\varnothing,1},\qquad\pi_{00}\xmapsto{\hskip 8.19447pt}\pi_{\varnothing,2}.

The total symmetric product
\Sym_{\bullet}:=\Sym_{\bullet}(W)
of

is an algebra, with multiplication given by the action of

\displaystyle e_{i}e_{I,t}=e_{Ii,t},

\displaystyle e_{0}e_{I,t}=e_{Ii,t+1}.

Note that this is nothing but a free polynomial algebra with a special indeterminate picked out.
There is also an action of

on

\displaystyle e^{i}e_{I,t}:=\#(i,I)e_{I\backslash i,t},\qquad e^{0}e_{I,t+1}:=(t+1)e_{I,t},

where

These can be used to define the

operator on

, given by

\displaystyle\tr=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{i}e^{i}.

Now, a parabolic system
(M_{0},\I_{0})
is a linear Pfaffian system, and the general theory of such (see
[

, Chapter IV]
) determines the associated tableaux

\displaystyle K\cong\Sym^{2}_{0}(W^{\prime})\oplus(W^{\prime}\otimes W^{\prime\prime})\oplus\Sym^{2}(W^{\prime\prime})\subset W\otimes W,

where

is the traceless component of

.
The

\displaystyle K^{(r)}=(K\otimes\Sym^{r}W)\cap\big(W\otimes\Sym^{r+1}W\big),

which is readily computed to be

\displaystyle K^{(r)}\cong\bigoplus_{s=0}^{2+r}\Sym^{s}_{0}(W^{\prime})\otimes\Sym^{2+r-s}(W^{\prime\prime}).

Each

is naturally identified with the kernel of the spatial trace

\displaystyle\tr\colon\ \Sym^{r+2}(W)\xrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447pte^{i}e^{i}\hskip 8.19447pt}\Sym^{r}(W).

This linear algebra determines the structure equations in the following proposition, whose proof is standard.

Proposition 3.1

(principal structure equations)
.

Near any point of the infinite prolongation
(M,\I)
of a parabolic system, there is a spanning set of
1
-forms

\displaystyle\omega^{a},\qquad\theta_{I,t},

where
I
ranges over symmetric multi-indices and

over non-negative integers, so that

1.

\I=\{\theta_{I,t}\}
,

2.

\I^{(r)}=\{\theta_{I,t}\colon|I|+t\leq r+1\}
,

3.

for any
\theta_{I,t}
,
the principal structure equations

\displaystyle{\rm d}\theta_{I,t}\equiv-\theta_{Ii,t}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{i}-\theta_{I,t+1}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{0}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\theta_{J,s}\colon|J|+s\leq|I|+t\big)

(3.1)

hold,

4.

for each

is locally spanned by

and the 1-forms of

for each fixed

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{Iii,t}\equiv 0\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\theta_{J,s}\mbox{ for which }|J|+s<|I|+t+2\big).

Definition 3.2
.

Any choice of
\omega^{a}
,
\theta_{I,t}
as in Proposition

of

.

Remark 3.3
.

As in Remark
2.3
, a parabolic coframing only defines a coframing of

after composing with the appropriate quotient. Nonetheless, the bundle of all parabolic coframes can be used to define a

-structure, where

is the infinite group prolongation of

. Essentially by construction,

acts stably on the filtration of

by the ideals

.

The theorem has the following corollary.

Corollary 3.4
.

A function

factors through

if and only if

\displaystyle{\rm d}A\equiv 0\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\omega^{a},\I^{(r+1)}\big).

Proof.

The function

is finite type, so factors through

for sufficiently large

are connected,

factors through

if and only if

\displaystyle{\rm d}A\equiv 0\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\omega^{a},\I^{(r+1)}\big).

4
The characteristic cohomology

With
(M,\I)
the infinitely prolonged parabolic system described in the last section, let
\overline{\Omega}^{*}
be chain complex defined by the exact sequence

\displaystyle 0\xrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447pt}\I^{*}\xrightarrow{\hskip 7.97224pt}\Omega^{*}(M)\xrightarrow{\hskip 7.97224pt}\overline{\Omega}^{*}\xrightarrow{\hskip 7.97224pt}0.

Because
\I
is a differential ideal, the differential of
\Omega^{*}(M)
descends to a differential
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}
on the quotient
\overline{\Omega}^{*}
, which is therefore a graded commutative differential algebra.
This cdga is intimately connected with the behavior of solutions to
M
. The next definition follows Bryant and Griffiths
[
3
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

The
characteristic cohomology

{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0muH}^{q}
of an infinitely prolonged parabolic system
(M,\I)
is the cohomology of the complex
\overline{\Omega}^{*}
,

\displaystyle{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0muH}^{q}=H^{q}\big(\overline{\Omega}^{*},\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}\big).

Due to the symbol of
(M,\I)
, it follows from the results in
[
3
]
that
{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0muH}^{n}
and
{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0muH}^{n+1}
are the only nontrivial characteristic cohomology groups. Furthermore,

{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0muH}^{n}
is naturally identified as the space of conservation laws of
(M,\I)
:

Definition 4.2
.

The
space of conservation laws
for a parabolic system is given by the degree
n
characteristic cohomology,

\displaystyle\overline{\mathscr{C}}={}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0muH}^{n}(M).

The cohomology of the differential operator
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}
is difficult to compute directly. However, the characteristic cohomology fits into a spectral sequence. In turn, the first page of this spectral sequence can be computed using a second spectral sequence. This second spectral sequence is quite amenable to calculations, as the differential of it first page is linear over functions, allowing a pointwise computation determined entirely by the symbol of
(M,\I)
.

To define the first spectral sequence, consider the filtration of
\Omega^{*}(M)
defined recursively by

\displaystyle\pwf^{0}=\Omega^{*}(M),

\displaystyle\pwf^{p+1}=\I{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\pwf^{p}.

Each level of the filtration is graded, with graded components

\displaystyle\pwf^{p,q}=\pwf^{p}\cap\Omega^{p+q}(M).

Then the bi-graded associated graded spaces are given by

\displaystyle Gr^{p,q}:=\pwf^{p,q}/\pwf^{p+1,q-1}.

\I
is formally Frobenius, so, for each
p\geq 0
, the exterior derivative descends to a well defined operator
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}
. These complexes comprise page 0 of the filtration spectral sequence, so that

\displaystyle E^{p,q}_{0}=Gr^{p,q}.

It follows immediately from the definition that

\displaystyle E^{0,q}_{0}=\overline{\Omega}^{q},

and thus

\displaystyle E^{0,q}_{1}={}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0muH}^{q}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle E_{\infty}\Rightarrow H^{*}(M),

so for any contractible neighborhood in
M
the spectral sequence converges to the homology of a point. As we are concerned here with local conservation laws, assume henceforth that
M
is contractible, restricting attention to a neighborhood if necessary.

It follows from the two-line theorem of Vinogradov
[
1
]
, that for parabolic equations,

\displaystyle E^{p,q}_{1}=0\text{\quad for\quad}q<n,

so the
E_{1}
page is

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0muH}^{n+1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{E^{1,n+1}_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{E^{2,n+1}_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots,}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0muH}^{n}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{E^{1,n}_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{E^{2,n}_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots,}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}}

Note that as an immediate consequence, the bottom row is exact, so that
{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0muH}^{n}
is isomorphic to the kernel of
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}
in
E^{1,n}_{1}
. This motivates the following definition, from
[
3
]
.

Definition 4.3
.

The
space of differentiated conservation laws
is

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}=\ker\big(E^{1,n}_{1}\xrightarrow{\ \hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}\ }E^{2,n}_{1}\big).

The operator
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}
provides an isomorphism between
\overline{\mathscr{C}}
and
\mathscr{C}
, and the latter space may be computed in two steps: First one computes
E^{1,n}_{1}
, and then one computes the kernel of
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}
. It is the second spectral sequence that helps to compute
E_{1}^{1,n}
, which is explained in the next section.

5
The weight filtrations

Bryant and Griffiths introduced a second filtration on
E_{1}^{p,q}
when
p\geq 1
, the
principal weight filtration
, that linearizes the calculation of each
E^{p,q}_{1}
.

Definition 5.1
.

A
weight function
is a function
\wt\colon\Omega^{*}(M)\to\Z
satisfying the following properties:

1.

\wt(f)=0
for
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
.

2.

\wt(\alpha{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\beta)=\wt(\alpha)+\wt(\beta)
for
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega^{*}(M)
.

3.

\wt(\alpha+\beta)=\max(\wt(\alpha),\wt(\beta))
.

Example 5.2

(principal weight filtration)
.

Fix any parabolic coframing of
M
and consider the weight function
\pwt
on
\Omega^{*}(M)
uniquely specified by

\displaystyle\pwt(\omega^{a})=-1,

\displaystyle\pwt(\theta_{I,t})=|I|+t.

The weight function
\pwt
descends to
\Gr^{p,*}
for each
p>0
. As argued in
[
3
]
,
\pwt
on
\Gr^{p,*}
is independent of the choice of parabolic coframing. See also Remark
3.3
.

For each integer
k
and each
p\geq 1
, define

\displaystyle\pwf^{p}_{k}=\big\{\alpha\in\pwf^{p}\colon\pwt(\alpha)\leq k\big\}/\pwf^{p+1},

the subspace in
\Gr^{p,*}
of forms with principal weight less than
k
.
The grading by form degree descends to
\pwf^{p}_{k}
, with graded components

\displaystyle\pwf^{p,q}_{k}=\big\{\alpha\in\pwf^{p,q}\colon\pwt(\alpha)\leq k\big\}/\pwf^{p+1}.

The
principal weight filtration
on
\Gr^{p,*}
is defined by the sequence

\displaystyle\cdots\subset\pwf^{p}_{k}\subset\pwf^{p}_{k+1}\subset\cdots\subset\Gr^{p,*}.

It follows immediately from equations (
3.1
) that this filtration is stable under
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}
.

For each fixed
p>0
there is a spectral sequence
\overline{E}
associated to this filtration, with
\overline{E}_{0}
page given by

\displaystyle\overline{E}_{0}^{q,k}=\pwf^{p,q}_{k}/\pwf^{p,q}_{k-1}.

This spectral sequence converges to
E^{p,*}_{1}
.

In calculations, it will be convenient to abuse notation slightly, using
\pwf^{p}_{k}
again to denote the preimage of
\pwf^{p}_{k}
under the mapping
\pwf^{p}\to\Gr^{p,*}
, that is,

\displaystyle\pwf^{p}_{k}=\big\{\alpha\in\pwf^{p}\colon\pwt(\alpha)\leq k\big\}+\pwf^{p+1}

and

\displaystyle\pwf^{p,q}_{k}=\big\{\alpha\in\pwf^{p,q}\colon\pwt(\alpha)\leq k\big\}+\pwf^{p+1}.

Then for any element
[\Phi]\in\overline{E}_{0}^{q,k}
represented by a form
\Phi
, the choice of
\Phi
is uniquely defined modulo
\pwf^{p}_{k-1}
. For example, if
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}\Phi
is any representative of
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}[\Phi]\in\overline{E}_{0}^{q+1,k}
, then

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}\Phi\equiv{\rm d}\Phi\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{p}_{k-1}\big).

The distinct usages occur in different contexts, and should not cause confusion.

Any element of
\overline{E}_{0}^{q,k}
can be represented by a linear combination of
(p+q)
-forms

\displaystyle\theta_{I_{1},t_{1}}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\cdots{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\theta_{I_{p},t_{p}}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{a_{1}}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\cdots{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{a_{q}}

of principal weight exactly
k
– explicitly, those for which

\displaystyle|I_{1}|+t_{1}+\cdots+|I_{p}|+t_{p}-q=k.

The exterior derivative
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}
is
C^{\infty}(M)
-linear on
\overline{E}_{0}^{*,k}
, and treats the forms
\omega^{a}
as constants. Indeed, by
\R
-linearity of
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}
, it suffices to check that

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}(f\theta_{I_{1},t_{1}}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\cdots{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\theta_{I_{p},t_{p}}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{a_{1}}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\cdots{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{a_{q}})

\displaystyle\qquad{}\equiv f\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}(\theta_{I_{1},t_{1}}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\cdots{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\theta_{I_{p},t_{p}}){\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{a_{1}}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\cdots{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{a_{q}}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{p}_{k-1}\big),

but this follows from the observation that
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}
strictly decreases weight for functions and for each
\omega^{a}
.

Corollary
3.4
can be restated in terms of the principal weight filtration:

Corollary 5.3
.

A function
A\in C^{\infty}(M)
factors through
C^{\infty}\big(M_{0}^{(r)}\big)
if and only if

\displaystyle{\rm d}A\equiv 0\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\omega^{a},\pwf^{1}_{r+2}\big).

Example 5.4

(sub-principal weight filtration)
.

For parabolic systems, the principal weight filtration doesn’t see “lower order” information, such as the sub-principal symbol. So, it will also be necessary to introduce the
sub-principal weight filtration
, which sees further into the structure of
(M,\I)
. The proof of Theorem
8.1
relies heavily on the sub-principal weight filtration.

Unlike the previous case, the sub-principal weight filtration on
\Gr^{p,*}
will necessarily depend on a choice of parabolic coframing. However, it will momentarily be shown that there are more refined choices of parabolic coframing and that all such coframings define the same sub-principal weight filtration.

Relative to a parabolic coframing, the
sub-principal weight function
is the unique weight function
\wt
such that

\displaystyle\wt\big(\omega^{i}\big)=-1,

\displaystyle\wt\big(\omega^{0}\big)=-2,

\displaystyle\wt(\theta_{I,t})=|I|+2t.

For each integer
k
and each
p\geq 1
, define the subspace

\displaystyle\swf^{p}_{k}=\big\{\alpha\in\pwf^{p}\colon\wt(\alpha)\leq k\big\}/\pwf^{p+1}

of
\Gr^{p,*}
.
The
sub-principal weight filtration
on
\Gr^{p,*}
is defined by the sequence

\displaystyle\cdots\subset\swf^{p}_{k}\subset\swf^{p}_{k+1}\subset\cdots\subset\Gr^{p,*}.

As with the principal weight filtration, it will be useful to have the notation

\displaystyle\swf^{p}_{k}=\big\{\alpha\in\pwf^{p}\colon\wt(\alpha)\leq k\big\}+\pwf^{p+1}

and

\displaystyle\swf^{p,q}_{k}=\big\{\alpha\in\pwf^{p,q}\colon\wt(\alpha)\leq k\big\}+\pwf^{p+1}.

For a generic choice of parabolic coframing, the induced filtration is not automatically
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}
-stable. So make the following definition.

Definition 5.5
.

A parabolic coframing
\omega^{a}
,
\theta_{I,t}
is a
refined parabolic coframing
of

if it satisfies the refined structure equations

\displaystyle{\rm d}\theta_{I,t}\equiv-\theta_{Ii,t}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{i}-\theta_{I,t+1}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{0}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\swf^{1}_{|I|+2t-1}\big).

(5.1)

Before turning to the proof that refined parabolic coframings exist for evolutionary parabolic systems, I state several useful properties of such coframings. Only the proof of the second property relies on the parabolic system being evolutionary.

Proposition 5.6
.

Suppose given a refined parabolic coframing
\omega^{a}
,
\theta_{I,t}
on an evolutionary parabolic system and corresponding sub-principal weight filtration
\swf
.

1.

The ideal
\swf^{p}_{S}
is
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}

2.

3.

4.

Any other choice of refined parabolic coframing gives the same sub-principal weight filtration.

Proof.

1. It suffices to check this for elements in

The claim is clear from the structure equations (

\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}

2. This follows because

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}\omega^{0}\equiv 0\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\omega^{0},\pwf^{1}\big).

3. Equation (

\displaystyle\theta_{ii,0}=\theta_{\varnothing,1},

providing the base case for induction.
If

\displaystyle-\theta_{Ijii,t}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{j}-\theta_{Iii,t+1}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{0}\equiv-\theta_{Ij,t+1}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{j}-\theta_{I,t+2}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{0}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\swf^{1}_{|I|+2+2t-1}\big),

\displaystyle\theta_{Ijii,t}-\theta_{Ij,t+1}\equiv 0\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\swf^{1}_{|I|+2+2t}\big),

\displaystyle\theta_{Iii,t+1}-\theta_{I,t+2}\equiv 0\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\swf^{1}_{|I|+3+2t}\big).

4. Let
\tilde{\omega}^{a}

\tilde{\theta}_{I,t}
be a second choice of refined parabolic coframing, with associated sub-principal weight filtration

\displaystyle\pwf^{1,0}_{N}\cap\swf^{1,0}_{S}=\pwf^{1,0}_{N}\cap\tilde{\swf}^{1,0}_{S}.

So, suppose

\displaystyle\pwf^{1,0}_{N}\cap\swf^{1,0}_{S}\neq\pwf^{1,0}_{N}\cap\,\tilde{\swf}^{1,0}_{S}.

Then there are some
\tilde{\theta}_{Ii,t}\in\pwf^{1,0}_{N}\cap\,\tilde{\swf}^{1,0}_{S}
and functions

for which

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}_{Ii,t}\equiv\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|J|+s\leq N\\
|J|+2s>S\end{subarray}}A_{Ii,t}^{J,s}\theta_{J,s}\not\equiv 0\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{1,0}_{N}\cap\swf^{1,0}_{S}\big)

and thus for

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{\theta}_{I,t}\equiv-\tilde{\theta}_{Ii,t}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{i}\equiv\sum A_{Ii,t}^{J,s}\theta_{J,s}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{i}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\omega^{0},\pwf^{1,1}_{N-2}\cap\swf^{1,1}_{S-2}\big).

The right hand side is not in

is

-stable, so

is in

∎

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
(M,\I)
be the infinite prolongation of an evolutionary parabolic system. There exist refined parabolic coframings.

Proof.

It will suffice to inductively prove the following statement for each
N>0
: there is a parabolic coframing such that for each
\theta_{I,t}
with
\pwt(\theta_{I,t})\leq N
,

\displaystyle{\rm d}\theta_{I,t}\equiv-\theta_{Ii,t}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{i}-\theta_{I,t+1}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{0}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{1}_{|I|+t-1}\cap\swf^{1}_{|I|+2t-1}\big).

(5.2)

The base case
N=1
follows immediately from the pullback to
M
of the refined structure equations (
2.3
) for an evolutionary parabolic system.

Suppose the induction has been carried out up to
N-1
.
Then, for each sub-principal weight

, one has

\displaystyle{\rm d}\big(\pwf^{1,1}_{N-2}\cap\swf^{1,1}_{k}\big)\subset\pwf^{1,2}_{N-2}\cap\swf^{1,2}_{k}.

This follows because, modulo
\pwf^{2}
, any element of
\pwf^{1,1}_{N-2}\cap\swf^{1,1}_{k}
is a linear combination of terms
\theta_{I,t}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{a}
such that
\pwt(\theta_{I,t})\leq N-1
.

It follows from the principal structure equations of Proposition
3.1
and from

\displaystyle\pwf^{1,1}_{N-1}=\big\{\dots,\theta_{\varnothing,N}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{i}\big\}\subset\swf^{1,1}_{2N-1}

that

\displaystyle{\rm d}\theta_{0,N}

\displaystyle\equiv-\theta_{i,N}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{i}-\theta_{0,N+1}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{0}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{1}_{N-1}\big),

\displaystyle\equiv-\theta_{i,N}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{i}-\theta_{0,N+1}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{0}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{1}_{N-1}\cap\swf^{1}_{2N-1}\big)

which provides the base case for a second induction.

Suppose then that (

) holds for all
\theta_{J,s}
with

\displaystyle\pwt(\theta_{J,s})=N,\qquad\wt(\theta_{J,s})>N+S.

Consider

of weights

\displaystyle\pwt(\theta_{Ii,t})=N,\qquad\wt(\theta_{Ii,t})=N+S.

\displaystyle{\rm d}\theta_{I,t}\equiv-\theta_{Ii,t}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{i}-\theta_{I,t+1}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega^{0}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{1,1}_{N-2}\cap\swf^{1,1}_{N+S-2}\big)

6
Horizontal and vertical derivatives

The differentials
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}
and
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}
in the characteristic spectral sequence have natural geometric interpretations, which I describe here. They are essentially the same operators as defined in
[
3
]
.

By definition, the operator
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}
is the restriction of
{\rm d}
to the associated graded
\Gr^{p,*}
. More explicitly, given an equivalence class
[\alpha]\in\Gr^{p,*}
represented by
\alpha\in\pwf^{p}
, the horizontal derivative
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}[\alpha]
is represented by
{\rm d}\alpha\in\pwf^{p}
, so that

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}\alpha\equiv{\rm d}\alpha\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{p+1}\big).

For any element
[\varphi]\in\pwf^{0}/\pwf^{1}
, the operator
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}
is
horizontal
with respect to any solution manifold
\Sigma
:

\displaystyle(\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}\varphi)|_{\Sigma}={\rm d}(\varphi|_{\Sigma}).

In particular, a function
A\in C^{\infty}(M)
is a functional on solution manifolds
\Sigma
, from which we may define new functionals
D_{a}A
so that

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}A\equiv(D_{a}A)\omega^{a}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{1}\big).

Then from

\displaystyle(\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}A)|_{\Sigma}={\rm d}(A|_{\Sigma})

it follows that the operators
D_{a}
are locally the restriction to
M
of the total derivatives
D_{a}
defined on
J^{\infty}\big(\R^{n+1},\R\big)
.

Now consider the
vertical derivative

\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}
. Given a form
\alpha\in\pwf^{p}
such that
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}\alpha\equiv 0
, the derivative
{\rm d}\alpha
is an element of
\pwf^{p+1}
. As such, define the vertical derivative of
\alpha
by

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}\alpha\equiv{\rm d}\alpha\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{p+2}\big).

Then, the horizontal derivative of this vanishes,

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}\alpha\equiv\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}{\rm d}\alpha\equiv{\rm d}^{2}\alpha=0\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{p+2}\big).

This last observation will be useful, because it is often easier to compute
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}
than
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}
, as the operator
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}
aids one in filtering by weights.

It will be useful for calculations to extend the operator
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}
to all functions. This is accomplished in an invariant manner using the Cartan system
\mathcal{J}=\{\theta_{\varnothing,0},\theta_{a},\omega^{a}\}=\big\{\omega^{a},\pwf^{1,0}_{1}\big\}
defined in Remark
2.4
, so that

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}A\equiv{\rm d}A\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\mathcal{J}\big).

The following lemma shows how to compute the vertical derivative of the directional total derivatives of a function.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
M
be the infinite prolongation of an evolutionary parabolic system. For any function
A\in C^{\infty}(M)
with
w=\wt({\rm d}A)
, there are functions
A_{I,s}
, not all zero, for which

\displaystyle{\rm d}A\equiv(D_{a}A)\omega^{a}+\sum_{|I|+2s=w}A_{I,s}\theta_{I,s}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\swf^{1}_{w-1}\big).

Then

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}(D_{i}A)\equiv\sum A_{I,s}\theta_{Ii,s}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\mathcal{J},\pwf^{1}_{2},\swf^{1}_{w}\big),

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}(D_{0}A)\equiv\sum A_{I,s}\theta_{I,s+1}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\mathcal{J},\pwf^{1}_{2},\swf^{1}_{w+1}\big).

Furthermore, if
w\geq 4
, then

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}(D_{i}A)\equiv\sum A_{I,s}\theta_{Ii,s}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\mathcal{J},\swf^{1}_{w}\big),

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}(D_{0}A)\equiv\sum A_{I,s}\theta_{I,s+1}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\mathcal{J},\swf^{1}_{w+1}\big).

Proof.

From the structure equations for
\omega^{i}
and
\omega^{0}
,

\displaystyle{\rm d}\omega^{a}\equiv 0\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\Lambda^{2}\mathcal{J},\pwf^{1}_{1}\big).

Then

\displaystyle 0={\rm d}^{2}A\equiv{}

\displaystyle{\rm d}\bigg((D_{a}A)\omega^{a}+\sum_{|I|+2s=w}A_{I,s}\theta_{I,s}\bigg)\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\swf^{1}_{w-1}\big),

\displaystyle\equiv{}

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}(D_{a}A)\omega^{a}-\sum_{|I|+2s=w}A_{I,s}\big(\theta_{Ii,s}\omega^{i}+\theta_{I,s+1}\omega^{0}\big)\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\Lambda^{2}\mathcal{J},\pwf^{1}_{1},\swf^{1}_{w-1}\big),

so that the result follows from an application of Cartan’s lemma.
The last claim follows from the observation that
\pwf^{1,0}_{2}\subseteq\swf^{1,0}_{4}
.
∎

The previous lemma holds with principal weight replacing sub-principal weight. The proof is,
mutatis mutandis
, the same, so omitted.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
M
be the infinite prolongation of a parabolic system. For any function
A\in C^{\infty}(M)
, with
N=\pwt({\rm d}A)\geq 2
, there are functions
A_{I,s}
, not all zero, for which

\displaystyle{\rm d}A\equiv(D_{a}A)\omega^{a}+\sum_{|I|+s=N}A_{I,s}\theta_{I,s}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{1}_{N-1}\big).

Then

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}(D_{i}A)\equiv\sum A_{I,s}\theta_{Ii,s}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\mathcal{J},\pwf^{1}_{N}\big),

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}(D_{0}A)\equiv\sum A_{I,s}\theta_{I,s+1}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\mathcal{J},\pwf^{1}_{N}\big).

Corollary 6.3
.

If
f\in C^{\infty}\big(M_{0}^{(r)}\big)
, then
D_{a}f\in C^{\infty}\big(M_{0}^{(r+1)}\big)
.

Proof.

For a function
f\in C^{\infty}\big(M_{0}^{(r)}\big)
, one has by Corollary
5.3
,

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}f\equiv 0\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\mathcal{J},\pwf^{1}_{r+2}\big),

and thus by the previous lemma,

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}D_{a}f\equiv 0\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\mathcal{J},\pwf^{1}_{r+3}\big),

so that
D_{a}f\in C^{\infty}\big(M_{0}^{(r+1)}\big)
.
∎

8
Conservation laws of strongly parabolic systems

Finally, with the tools developed, it is not difficult to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
(M,\I)
be the infinite prolongation of an evolutionary parabolic system
(M_{0},\I_{0})
and

\Phi
a differentiated conservation law on
(M,\I)
. The defining function
A
of
\Phi
factors through
C^{\infty}\big(M_{0}\big)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
7.1
, a conservation law
\Phi
has a defining function
A\in C^{\infty}(M)
so that the
\I
-linear part of
\Phi
is given by

\displaystyle\Phi\equiv A\Upsilon-(D_{i}A)\theta_{\varnothing,0}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega_{(i)}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{2}\big).

Since
\Phi
is closed,
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}\Phi\equiv 0
and
\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}\Phi
is defined.
Calculating directly,

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}\Phi\equiv({\rm d}A){\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\Upsilon-{\rm d}(D_{i}A){\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\theta_{\varnothing,0}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega_{(i)}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{2}_{2-n},\swf^{2}_{2-n}\big).

(8.1)

Here it is worth noting that only
\I
-quadratic terms need be considered (the
\I
-linear terms cancel each other), and that the
\I
-quadratic terms of
{\rm d}\Upsilon
were computed in Remark
7.2
.

Now, using Corollary
5.3
, it suffices to demonstrate that

\displaystyle\pwt({\rm d}A)\leq 2.

This is done by first bounding the sub-principal weight of
{\rm d}A
.
To this end, let

\displaystyle w=\wt({\rm d}A),

and
N
the largest integer for which

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}A\not\equiv 0\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\mathcal{J},\pwf^{1}_{N-1},\swf^{1}_{w-1}\big).

Then, for

, there are functions

,
B_{J,S+1}
so that

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}A\equiv\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|I|+S=N\\
|I|+2S=w\end{subarray}}A_{I,S}\theta_{I,S}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|J|+S+1=N-1\\
|J|+2(S+1)=w\end{subarray}}B_{J,S+1}\theta_{J,S+1}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\mathcal{J},\pwf^{1}_{N-2},\swf^{1}_{w-1}\big).

(If
|I|=N-S=0,1
then the second sum is vacuous, so let the
B_{J,S+1}=0
in that case.)
From Lemma
6.1
, if

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}(D_{i}A)\equiv\sum A_{I,S}\theta_{Ii,S}+\sum B_{J,S+1}\theta_{Ji,S+1}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\mathcal{J},\pwf^{1}_{N-1},\swf^{1}_{w}\big).

Suppose that either

(in which case

is automatic) or

and

, and plug into equation (
8.1
),

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}\Phi\equiv{}

\displaystyle{}A_{I,S}\left(\theta_{I,S}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\theta_{i,0}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega_{(i)}-\theta_{I,S}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\theta_{\varnothing,0}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega_{(0)}-\theta_{Ii,S}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\theta_{\varnothing,0}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega_{(i)}\right)

\displaystyle+B_{J,S+1}\left(\theta_{J,S+1}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\theta_{i,0}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega_{(i)}-\theta_{J,S+1}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\theta_{\varnothing,0}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega_{(0)}-\theta_{Ji,S+1}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\theta_{\varnothing,0}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega_{(i)}\right)

\displaystyle 0\equiv\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}\Phi\equiv-2A_{I,S}\theta_{I,S+1}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\theta_{\varnothing,0}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega_{(\varnothing)}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{2}_{N-n-1},\swf^{2}_{w-n-1}\big),

With the theorem just proven, it is not difficult to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 8.2
.

Given an evolutionary parabolic system
M_{0}
with a non-trivial conservation law
\Phi
on its infinite prolongation
M
, in any neighborhood of
M_{0}
where the defining function of
\Phi
is not zero,
M_{0}
has a Monge–Ampère deprolongation.

Proof.

Suppose
M_{0}
has a non-trivial conservation law

\displaystyle\Phi\equiv A\Upsilon-(D_{i}A)\theta_{\varnothing,0}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega_{(i)}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{2}\big).

By Theorem
8.1
,
A
is a function on
M_{0}
. Consequently, in any neighborhood where
A
is non-zero, there are parabolic cofframings that reduce
A
to 1. For example, the change of coframing that fixes the
\omega^{a}
but transforms the other coframing terms as

\displaystyle\theta_{\varnothing,0}\xmapsto{\hskip 8.19447pt}(1/A)\theta_{\varnothing,0},\qquad\ \,\theta_{i,0}\xmapsto{\hskip 8.19447pt}(1/A)\theta_{i,0},\qquad\theta_{\varnothing,1}\xmapsto{\hskip 8.19447pt}(1/A)\theta_{\varnothing,1},

\displaystyle\pi_{ij,0}\xmapsto{\hskip 8.19447pt}(1/A)\pi_{ij,0},\qquad\ \,\pi_{i,1}\xmapsto{\hskip 8.19447pt}(1/A)\pi_{i,1},\qquad\pi_{\varnothing,2}\xmapsto{\hskip 8.19447pt}(1/A)\pi_{\varnothing,2}

preserves the evolutionary parabolic structure of
(M_{0},\I_{0})
, and after this reduction,

\displaystyle\Phi\equiv\Upsilon\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{2}\big).

By Remark
7.2
,

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}\Phi\equiv-2V_{i}^{jkl}(\theta_{kl,0}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\theta_{j,0}-\theta_{jkl,0}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\theta_{\varnothing,0}){\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega_{(i)}

\displaystyle\hphantom{$\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}$\Phi\equiv}{}-W^{I,t}(\theta_{I,t}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\theta_{i,0}-\theta_{Ii,t}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\theta_{\varnothing,0}){\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega_{(i)}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{2}_{2-n}\big).

Then, recalling that
V_{i}^{jkl}
has values in
\Sym_{0}^{2}(\Sym_{0}^{2}\R^{n})
,

\displaystyle 0=\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{h}\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}_{v}\Phi\equiv-2V_{i}^{jkl}\theta_{ijkl,0}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\theta_{\varnothing,0}{\mathchoice{\,{\scriptstyle\wedge}\,}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\wedge}}}\omega_{(\varnothing)}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod\kern 5.0pt}\pwf^{2}_{2-n}\big),

and the
\Sym_{0}^{4}\R^{n}
component of the Monge–Ampère invariant vanishes identically.
∎
