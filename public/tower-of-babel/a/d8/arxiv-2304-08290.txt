Backward martingale transport maps in pseudo-Euclidean spaces
Dmitry Kramkov, Mihai Sîrbu

Abstract
We consider an optimal transport problem with backward martingale constraint. The objective function is given by the scalar product of a pseudo-Euclidean space S S . We show that the supremum over maps equals the supremum over plans provided that the law ν \nu of the input random variable Y Y is atomless. An optimal map X X exists if ν \nu does not charge any c − c c-c surface (the graph of a difference of convex functions) with strictly positive normal vectors in the sense of the S S -space. The optimal map X X is unique if, in addition, ν \nu does not charge c − c c-c surfaces with positive and nearly isotropic normal vectors in the S S -space. In the latter case, the joint law of ( X , Y ) (X,Y) is the unique optimal backward martingale plan.



Keywords:

martingale optimal transport, pseudo-Euclidean space,
optimal maps.

60G42, 91B24, 91B52.

Introduction

Let
S
be an symmetric invertible
d\times d
matrix with

m\in\left\{{0,1,\dots d}\right\}
positive eigenvalues. We interpret the
bilinear form

S(x,y):=\left\langle x,Sy\right\rangle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}x^{i}S_{ij}y^{j},\quad x,y\in\real{d},

as the scalar product of a pseudo-Euclidean space, called later the

S
-space.

Let
Y
be a
d
-dimensional random variable with finite second
moments defined on a probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. We consider the Monge-type
optimal transport problem:

\text{maximize~}\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(S(X,Y)\right)\text{~over~}X\in\mathcal{X}(Y),

where
\mathcal{X}(Y)
is the family of backward martingale

maps
:

\mathcal{X}(Y):=\left\{{{X}\;|\;X\text{~is
$Y$-measurable and~}\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y\right\lvert{X}\right)=X}\right\}.

The Kantorovich-type relaxation of this problem is to maximize the
same objective over backward martingale
plans
. By possibly
enlarging the probability space, we can represent such plans as the
joint laws of
(X,Y)
for random variables
X
satisfying the
martingale constraint:
\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y\right\lvert{X}\right)=X
. The maps have the additional

Y
-measurability property:
X=f(Y)
for some Borel function
f
.

The plan problem is easier to study due to the convexity of the
optimization set. As shown in
[
9
]
for
d=2
and the

standard
matrix
S=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
, and in
[
7
]
for the general case, it
has the same value as its dual counterpart:

\text{minimize~}\mathbb{E}\left(\psi_{G}(Y)\right)\text{~over~}G\in\mathfrak{M}\left({S}\right),

and both problems have solutions. Here
\mathfrak{M}\left({S}\right)
is the family of all
maximal monotone sets in the
S
-space and
\psi_{G}
is the
Fitzpatrick function:

\psi_{G}(y):=\sup_{x\in G}\left({S(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}S(x,x)}\right),\quad y\in\real{d}.

A closed set
G\subset\real{d}
is called
S
-monotone or

S
-positive if

S(x-y,x-y)\geq 0,\quad x,y\in G.

The set
G
is called maximal
S
-monotone if it is not a strict
subset of another
S
-monotone set. The Fitzpatrick function
\psi_{G}

plays the role of the Kantorovich potential from the classical optimal
transport.

Any backward martingale map yields a plan, so the supremum over plans
is always greater or equal than the supremum over maps. Theorem

2.1
shows that if the law of
Y
is atomless, then these
supremums coincide. Its proof is based on a result of independent
interest, the
pointwise uniform
approximation of plans by maps
in Theorem
A.1
.

Let
G\in\mathfrak{M}\left({S}\right)
be
any
dual minimizer and

P_{G}(y):=\argmin_{x\in G}S(x-y,x-y),\quad y\in\real{d},

be the projection multi-function on
G
. Theorem
3.4
and
Lemma
3.5
show that if
P_{G}(Y)
takes values (almost surely)
in convex closed
S
-isotropic subsets of
G
, then

X:=\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y\right\lvert{P_{G}(Y)}\right)

is an optimal map and the law of
(X,Y)
is an optimal plan. Here a
set
A
is called
S
-isotropic if

S(x-y,x-y)=0,\quad x,y\in A.

By Theorem
3.8
,
P_{G}(Y)
indeed takes values in convex closed

S
-isotropic subsets of
G
provided that

(a)

The law of
Y
does not charge any
c-c
surface
(the graph of a difference of convex functions) having

strictly

S
-positive
S
-normal vectors
w
at every point:

S(w,w)>0
.

If
P_{G}(Y)
is single-valued, then Theorem
4.1
shows that

X:=P_{G}(Y)

is the unique optimal map and the law of
(X,Y)
is the unique optimal
plan. By Theorem
4.2
,
P_{G}(Y)
is indeed single-valued provided
that
(a)
and either
(b)
or
(c)
hold:

(b)

If
m=1
, (
m
is the number of positive
eigenvalues for
S
), then the law of
S(w,Y)=\left\langle w,Sy\right\rangle
is
atomless for every nonzero
S
-isotropic vector
w
:
\left\lvert w\right\rvert>0
and

S(w,w)=0
.

(c)

If
m>1
, then the law of
Y
does not charge any

c-c
surface having
S
-positive and “almost”
S
-isotropic

S
-normal vectors
w
at every point.

We point out that
(b)
is weaker than
(c)
stated for

m=1
and that
(a)
+
(b)
or
(a)
+
(c)

are the first known conditions for the uniqueness of optimal plans in
the case of general invertible matrix
S
.

For
d=2
and the standard matrix
S
, Theorems
3.8

and
4.2
improve the existence and uniqueness criteria
from
[
9
]
, where the covering in
(a)
is
accomplished with Lipschitz (not
c-c
) surfaces.

The original motivation for the backward martingale transport comes
from the Kyle’s equilibrium for insider trading introduced
in
[
10
]
. The paper
[
9
]
studies a version of
such equilibrium from
Rochet and Vila 1994
and shows its connection to
the map problem for
d=2
and the standard matrix
S
. The current
paper sets up the base for the study of a multi-asset version of the
Rochet and Vila’s equilibrium.

Under the conditions of Theorem
4.2
, an optimal set
G
becomes
a pseudo-Euclidean version of a
principal surface

from
Hastie and Stuetzle 1989
and
Hastie and Tibshirani 1993
:
G
passes through the
“middle” of the law of the input random variable
Y
in the sense
that
Y
is uniquely projected on
G
and its projection
X=P_{G}(Y)

has the martingale property:
X=\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y\right\lvert{X}\right)
. An Euclidean principal
surface is not unique even for the case of a standard Gaussian law
and, at least at this moment, there are no general conditions for its
existence. The pseudo-Euclidean
S
-structure is more tractable. It
naturally arises in some applications (like the study of equilibrium
above). Moreover, being
S
-monotone, the optimal set
G
is an

Euclidean-type
subset of the
S
-space. As a result,
G
has
qualitative properties of an Euclidean principal surface and can be
used as a substitute.

Notations

The scalar product and the norm in the Euclidean space
\real{d}
are
written as

\left\langle x,y\right\rangle:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}y_{i},\quad\left\lvert x\right\rvert:=\sqrt{\left\langle x,x\right\rangle},\quad x,y\in\real{d}.

For a Borel probability measure
\mu
on
\real{d}
, a

\mu
-integrable
m
-dimensional Borel function
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})
,
and an
n
-dimensional Borel function
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
, the
notation
\mu(f|g)
stands for the
m
-dimensional vector of
conditional expectations of
f_{i}
given
g
under
\mu
:

\mu(f|g)=(\mu(f_{1}|g_{1},\dots,g_{n}),\dots,\mu(f_{m}|g_{1},\dots,g_{n})).

In particular, we write
\mu(f)
for the vector of expected values:

\mu(f)=\int fd\mu=(\int f_{1}d\mu,\dots,\int f_{m}d\mu)=(\mu(f_{1}),\dots,\mu(f_{m})).

We write
\supp{\mu}
for the support of
\mu
, the smallest closed
set of full measure.

Similarly, if
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is a probability
space,
X
and
Y
are respectively,
m
- and
n
-dimensional random
variables, and
Y
is integrable, then

\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y\right\lvert{X}\right)=\left({\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y_{1}\right\lvert{X_{1},\dots,X_{m}}\right),\dots,\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y_{n}\right\lvert{X_{1},\dots,X_{m}}\right)}\right),

denotes the
n
-dimensional vector of conditional expectations of

Y_{i}
with respect to
X
. All relations between random variables are
understood in the
\mathbb{P}-a.s.
sense. In particular,
X
is

Y
-measurable if and only if
X=f(Y)
(
\mathbb{P}-a.s.
) for a Borel
function
f:\;\real{m}\rightarrow\real{d}
.

For a closed (equivalently, lower-semicontinuous) convex function

f:\;\real{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{+\infty}\right\}
, we denote by
\partial f
its
subdifferential and by
f^{*}
its convex conjugate:

\displaystyle\partial f(x)

\displaystyle=\left\{{{z\in\real{d}}\;|\;f(y)-f(x)\geq\left\langle z,y-x\right\rangle,\;y\in\real{d}}\right\},\quad x\in\real{d},

\displaystyle f^{*}(y)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{x\in\real{d}}\left({\left\langle y,x\right\rangle-f(x)}\right),\quad y\in\real{d}.

The domains of
f
and of
\partial f
are defined as

\displaystyle\dom f

\displaystyle:=\left\{{{x\in\real{d}}\;|\;f(x)<\infty}\right\},

\displaystyle\dom\partial f

\displaystyle:=\left\{{{x\in\dom f}\;|\;\partial f(x)\not=\emptyset}\right\}.

2
Equality of values of plan and map problems

We denote by
\mathcal{S}^{d}_{m}
the family of symmetric

d\times d
-matrices of full rank with
m\in\left\{{0,1,\dots,d}\right\}

positive eigenvalues. For
S\in\mathcal{S}^{d}_{m}
, the bilinear form

S(x,y):=\left\langle x,Sy\right\rangle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}x^{i}S_{ij}y^{j},\quad x,y\in\real{d},

defines the scalar product on a pseudo-Euclidean space
\real{d}_{m}

with dimension
d
and index
m
, which we call the
S
-space. The
quadratic form
S(x,x)
is called the
scalar square
; its value
may be negative.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and
Y

be a
d
-dimensional random variable with finite second moment:

Y\in{\mathcal{L}_{2}\left({\real{d}}\right)}
. Our goal is to

\text{maximize}\quad\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(S(X,Y)\right)\quad\text{over}\quad X\in\mathcal{X}(Y),

(1)

where

\mathcal{X}(Y):=\left\{{{X\in{\mathcal{L}_{2}\left({\real{d}}\right)}}\;|\;X\text{~is
$Y$-measurable and~}\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y\right\lvert{X}\right)=X}\right\}.

The Kantorovich-type relaxation of the optimal
map

problem (
1
) is the optimal
plan
problem:

\text{maximize}\quad\frac{1}{2}\int S(x,y)d\gamma\quad\text{over}\quad\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu),

(2)

where
\nu:=\Law(Y)
belongs to
\mathcal{P}_{2}\left({\real{d}}\right)
, the family of
Borel probability measures on
\real{d}
with finite second moments,
and

\Gamma(\nu):=\left\{{{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\left({\real{2d}}\right)}\;|\;\gamma(\real{d},dy)=\nu(dy)\text{~and~}\gamma(y|x)=x}\right\}.

Theorem 2.5 in
[
7
]
shows that an optimal plan
for (
2
) always exists. Clearly,
\Law(X,Y)\in\Gamma(\nu)

for every
X\in\mathcal{X}(Y)
. Thus,

\sup_{X\in\mathcal{X}(Y)}\mathbb{E}\left(S(X,Y)\right)\leq\max_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu)}\int S(x,y)d\gamma.

Notice that the inequality may be strict and an optimal map may not
exist, as Examples 5.2 and 5.3 in
[
9
]
show.

The following theorem is similar to that of
Pratelli 2007
obtained
for the classical unconstrained optimal transport problem. For
d=2

and the
standard

S=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{S}^{2}_{1}
, see Example
3.1
below, it has
been proved in
[
9
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{S}^{d}_{m}
,
Y\in{\mathcal{L}_{2}\left({\real{d}}\right)}
, and suppose that

\nu:=\Law(Y)
is atomless. Then the problems (
1
)
and (
2
) have the same values:

\sup_{X\in\mathcal{X}(Y)}\mathbb{E}\left(S(X,Y)\right)=\max_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu)}\int S(x,y)d\gamma.

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be an optimal plan for (
2
). By extending, if
necessary, the underlying probability space we can assume that

\gamma=\Law(X,Y)
for some random variable
X
. As

\gamma(y|x)=x
, we have that
X=\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y\right\lvert{X}\right)
.

Let
\epsilon>0
. Theorem
A.1
yields a
d
-dimensional random
variable
Z=Z(\epsilon)
such that

\Law(Z)=\Law(Y),\quad\left\lvert Z-Y\right\rvert\leq\epsilon,\quad X\text{~is $Z$-measurable}.

Since
X
is
Z
-measurable, the conditional expectation

V:=\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Z\right\lvert{X}\right)
is
Z
-measurable as well. Thus, there is a
Borel function
f:\;\real{d}\rightarrow\real{d}
such that
V=f(Z)
.
Since
Y
and
Z
have identical laws,

U:=f(Y)=\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y\right\lvert{U}\right).

For all
x,y,v,z
in
\real{d}
, we have that

\displaystyle\left\lvert S(x,y)-S(v,z)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert S(x-v,y)\right\rvert+\left\lvert S(v,z-y)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert S\rVert(\left\lvert y\right\rvert\left\lvert x-v\right\rvert+\left\lvert v\right\rvert\left\lvert z-y\right\rvert),

where
\lVert S\rVert:=\max_{\left\lvert x\right\rvert=1}\left\lvert Sx\right\rvert
, the norm of
S
.
Since
\left\lvert Z-Y\right\rvert\leq\epsilon
and

\displaystyle\left\lvert V-X\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Z-Y\right\lvert{X}\right)\right\rvert\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\left.\left\lvert Z-Y\right\rvert\right\lvert{X}\right)\leq\epsilon,

we have that

\displaystyle\left\lvert S(X,Y)-S(V,Z)\right\rvert\leq\epsilon\lVert S\rVert(\left\lvert Y\right\rvert+\left\lvert V\right\rvert).

As

\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lvert V\right\rvert\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\left(Z|X\right)\right\rvert\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lvert Z\right\rvert\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lvert Y\right\rvert\right)+\epsilon
and
\Law(U,Y)=\Law(V,Z)
, we obtain that

\int S(x,y)d\gamma=\mathbb{E}\left(S(X,Y)\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left(S(U,Y)\right)+\epsilon\lVert S\rVert(2\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lvert Y\right\rvert\right)+\epsilon).

The result follows, because
U\in\mathcal{X}(Y)
and
\epsilon
is
any positive number.
∎

Existence of optimal maps

Let
S\in\mathcal{S}^{d}_{m}
. A set
G\subset\real{d}
is called

S
-
monotone
or
S
-
positive
if

S(x-y,x-y)\geq 0,\quad x,y\in G.

An
S
-monotone set
G
is called
maximal
if it is not a strict
subset of an
S
-monotone set.

Example 3.1

(Standard form)
.

If

and

S(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left({x^{i}y^{m+i}+x^{m+i}y^{i}}\right),\quad x,y\in\real{2m},

then

and the
S
-monotonicity means the standard
monotonicity in
\real{2m}=\real{m}\times\real{m}
.

Example 3.2

(Canonical form)
.

If
S
is the
canonical
quadratic form in
\mathcal{S}^{d}_{m}
:

S(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}x^{i}y^{i}-\sum_{i=m+1}^{d}x^{i}y^{i},\quad x,y\in\real{d},

then a closed set
G
is
S
-monotone if and only if

G=\graph{f}:=\left\{{{(u,f(u))}\;|\;u\in D}\right\},

where
D
is a closed set in
\real{m}
and
f:\;D\rightarrow\real{d-m}

is a
1
-Lipschitz function:

\left\lvert f(u)-f(v)\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert u-v\right\rvert,\quad u,v\in D.

In view of the Kirszbraun Theorem,
[
4
, 2.10.43]
,
G
is
maximal
S
-monotone if and only if
D=\real{m}
.

It has been shown in
[
7
, Theorem 2.5]
, that a dual
problem to (
2
) is to

\text{minimize}\quad\mathbb{E}\left(\psi_{G}(Y)\right)=\int\psi_{G}(y)d\nu\quad\text{over}\quad G\in\mathfrak{M}\left({S}\right),

(3)

where
\mathfrak{M}\left({S}\right)
is the family of all maximal
S
-monotone sets and

\psi_{G}(y):=\sup_{x\in G}\left({S(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}S(x,x)}\right),\quad y\in\real{d},

is a
Fitzpatrick
function in the
S
-space. We refer the reader
to
[
7
, Appendix A]
for the basic facts about the
Fitzpatrick functions in the
S
-space. Theorem 2.5
in
[
7
]
shows that an optimal set for (
3
)
always exists and

\max_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu)}\frac{1}{2}\int S(x,y)d\gamma=\min_{G\in\mathfrak{M}\left({S}\right)}\int\psi_{G}(y)d\nu.

Let
G\in\mathfrak{M}\left({\real{d}}\right)
. We denote by
P_{G}
the projection on
G

in the
S
-space:

P_{G}(y):=\argmin_{x\in G}S(x-y,x-y),\quad y\in\real{d}.

We write
P_{G}^{-1}(A)
for the pre-image of a set

under

Theorem A.4 in
[
7
]
states that

\displaystyle P_{G}(y)

\displaystyle=\left\{{{x\in G}\;|\;Sx\in\partial\psi_{G}(y)}\right\},\quad y\in\real{d},

\displaystyle P_{G}^{-1}(x)

\displaystyle:=P_{G}^{-1}(\left\{{x}\right\})=\partial\psi_{G}^{*}(Sx),\quad x\in G,

\displaystyle\dom{P_{G}}

\displaystyle:=P_{G}^{-1}(\real{d})=\dom{\partial\psi_{G}}:=\left\{{{x\in\real{d}}\;|\;\partial\psi_{G}(x)\not=\emptyset}\right\},

where
\partial\psi_{G}

\psi_{G}
and its convex conjugate
\psi^{*}_{G}
. In

P_{G}^{-1}(x)
is a closed convex set for every

.

For

the largest closed convex subset

. Lemma 2.12 in

[

shows that such a set exists, and

in
[

]

Theorem 3.3
.

Let

\displaystyle\supp{\gamma}\subset F:=\left\{{{(x,y)}\;|\;x\in P_{G}(y),y\in\dom{P_{G}}}\right\}.

Observe now that if

, then

. In other words,

.

The last assertion of the theorem is straightforward.

Let

. Following

, p. 59]

. In

.

in
\real{d}
.

If

is a

-dimensional random variable, then

stands for the

-algebra of all the events

\left\{{Y\in A}\right\}
,

, or, equivalently, generated by the events

. In particular, a conditional

random variable.

A point
y\in\dom{P_{G}}
is called

, if
P_{G}(y)
is not
a singleton. We decompose the set of singular points of

as

\displaystyle\Sigma(P_{G})

\displaystyle:=\left\{{{x\in\dom{P_{G}}}\;|\;P_{G}(x)\text{~is not a
point}}\right\}=\Sigma_{0}(P_{G})\cup\Sigma_{1}(P_{G}),

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}(P_{G})

\displaystyle:=\left\{{{x\in\Sigma(P_{G})}\;|\;S(y_{1}-y_{2},y_{1}-y_{2})=0\text{~for \emph{all}~}y_{1},y_{2}\in P_{G}(x)}\right\},

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}(P_{G})

\displaystyle:=\left\{{{x\in\Sigma(P_{G})}\;|\;S(y_{1}-y_{2},y_{1}-y_{2})>0\text{~for \emph{some}~}y_{1},y_{2}\in P_{G}(x)}\right\}.

Clearly,

. Lemma

provides an equivalent description of

. Lemma

shows that the singular sets

belong to
\sigma(P_{G})
. In
particular, they are Borel sets in
\real{d}
.

We recall that an optimal set

for the dual

Theorem 3.4
.

Let

,

, and denote

\nu:=\Law(Y)
. Let

be an optimal set
for (

requires the knowledge of a dual

The proof of Theorem

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
G\in\mathfrak{M}\left({S}\right)
and
y\in\dom{P_{G}}
. Then
P_{G}(y)
is a convex
set if and only if

S(u-v,u-v)=0,\quad u,v\in P_{G}(y).

In other words, the singular set
\Sigma_{0}(P_{G})
admits the
equivalent description:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}(P_{G})=\left\{{{y\in\Sigma(P_{G})}\;|\;P_{G}(y)\text{~is a convex
set}}\right\}.

Proof.

Let
u,v\in P_{G}(y)
and denote
w=\frac{1}{2}(u+v)
. We have that

\displaystyle\psi_{G}(y)

\displaystyle=S(y,u)-\frac{1}{2}S(u,u)=S(y,v)-\frac{1}{2}S(v,v)

\displaystyle=S(y,w)-\frac{1}{2}S(w,w)-\frac{1}{4}\left({S(u,u)+S(v,v)-2S(w,w)}\right)

\displaystyle=S(y,w)-\frac{1}{2}S(w,w)-\frac{1}{8}S(u-v,u-v).

In particular,

\psi_{G}(y)=S(y,w)-\frac{1}{2}S(w,w)\iff S(u-v,u-v)=0.

If
P_{G}(y)
is convex, then
w\in P_{G}(y)
, which implies that

S(u-v,u-v)=0
.

Assume now that
S(u-v,u-v)=0
. Direct computations show that

S(x-w,x-w)=\frac{1}{2}\left({S(x-u,x-u)+S(x-v,x-v)}\right)\geq 0,\quad x\in G.

As
G\in\mathfrak{M}\left({S}\right)
, we obtain that
w\in G
, and, necessarily,

w\in P_{G}(y)
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{S}^{d}_{m}
and
G\in\mathfrak{M}\left({S}\right)
. Then

(a)

Every
A\in\sigma(P_{G})
is a Borel set in

\real{d}
.

(b)

For every
s\in\real{d}
the function

g_{s}(y):=\sup_{x\in P_{G}(y)}\left\langle s,x\right\rangle\in\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{-\infty}\right\}\cup\left\{{+\infty}\right\},\quad y\in\real{d},

is
\sigma(P_{G})
-measurable, where we used the usual convention
that
\sup
over an empty set is
-\infty
.

(c)

The singular sets
\Sigma_{0}(P_{G})
and

\Sigma_{1}(P_{G})
belong to
\sigma(P_{G})
.

Proof.

(a)
: We first show that the inverse image of a compact set

K
by
P_{G}
is closed. Since

P^{-1}_{G}(K)=\left\{{{y\in\real{d}}\;|\;P_{G}(y)\cap K\not=\emptyset}\right\}=P^{-1}_{G}(K\cap G),

we can assume that
K
is a subset of
G
. The Fitzpatrick-type
function

\displaystyle\psi_{K}(y):=\sup_{x\in K}\left({S(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}S(x,x)}\right),\quad y\in\real{d}.

is convex and finite, therefore continuous. In addition,

K\subset G
ensures that
\psi_{K}\leq\psi_{G}
. We observe that

P^{-1}_{G}(K)=\left\{{{y\in\real{d}}\;|\;\psi_{K}(y)=\psi_{G}(y)}\right\}=\left\{{{y\in\real{d}}\;|\;\psi_{G}(y)\leq\psi_{K}(y)}\right\}.

Since
\psi_{G}
is lower semi-continuous and
\psi_{K}
is
continuous,
P^{-1}_{G}(K)
is closed.

As every open set
U
is a countable union of compact sets
(K_{n})

and

P^{-1}_{G}\left({U}\right)=P^{-1}_{G}\left({\bigcup_{n}K_{n}}\right)=\bigcup_{n}P^{-1}_{G}\left({K_{n}}\right),

we deduce that
P^{-1}_{G}(U)
is a
F_{\sigma}
-set. In particular,

P^{-1}_{G}(U)
is a Borel set.

(b)
: Fix
s\in\real{d}
. For every
a\in\real{}
we have
that

\left\{{{y\in\real{d}}\;|\;g_{s}(y)>a}\right\}=P^{-1}_{G}\left({\left\{{{x\in\real{d}}\;|\;\left\langle s,x\right\rangle>a}\right\}}\right)\in\sigma(P_{G}).

The
\sigma(P_{G})
-measurability of
g_{s}
readily follows.

(c)
: Fix a sequence
x_{i}
,
i=1,2,\dots
dense in

\real{d}
. Denote by
r_{k}
,
k=1,2,\dots
an enumeration of all
positive rationals. Denote by
\alpha:=(i,j,k,l)
, and set

B^{\alpha}_{1}:=\left\{{{x\in\real{d}}\;|\;|x-x_{i}|<r_{k}}\right\},\quad B^{\alpha}_{2}:=\left\{{{x\in\real{d}}\;|\;|x-x_{j}|<r_{l}}\right\}.

Restrict the set of countable indexes
\alpha=(i,j,k,l)
to those
for which

B^{\alpha}_{1}\cap B^{\alpha}_{2}=\emptyset.

We further denote by
\beta
the indexes
\alpha
with the
additional property:

\inf_{u\in B^{\beta}_{1},v\in B^{\beta}_{2}}S(u-v,u-v)>0.

(4)

The conclusion follows from the definition of
\sigma(P_{G})
, once
we observe that

\displaystyle\Sigma(P_{G})

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\alpha}\left({P^{-1}_{G}(B^{\alpha}_{1})\cap P^{-1}_{G}(B^{\alpha}_{2})}\right),

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}(P_{G})

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\beta}\left({P^{-1}_{G}(B^{\beta}_{1})\cap P^{-1}_{G}(B^{\beta}_{2})}\right).

For the last relation, to show the inclusion
\subset
, we use the
joint continuity of
S(u-v,u-v)
to choose small enough rational

r_{k}
and
r_{l}
for (
4
) to hold.
∎

Let
x\in G
. We recall that
Q_{G}(x)
denotes the largest closed
convex subset of
P^{-1}_{G}(x)
whose relative interior contains
x
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{S}^{d}_{m}
,
G\in\mathfrak{M}\left({S}\right)
,
x\in G
, and

y\in Q_{G}(x)
. Then
P_{G}(x)\subset P_{G}(y)
. If, in addition,

P_{G}(y)
is convex, then
P_{G}(x)=P_{G}(y)
.

Proof.

Let
v\in Q_{G}(x)
and
z\in P_{G}(x)
. Then
x\in P_{G}(v)
and

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\psi_{G}(v)-\left({S(z,v)-\frac{1}{2}S(z,z)}\right)

\displaystyle=S(x,v)-\frac{1}{2}S(x,x)-S(z,v)+\frac{1}{2}S(z,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}S(x-z,x-z)+S(x-z,v-x)=S(x-z,v-x).

Choosing
v=x\pm\epsilon(y-x)
for some
\epsilon\in(0,1)
,
which is possible by the construction of
Q_{G}(x)
, we obtain that

S(x-z,y-x)=0.

Taking
v=y
, we deduce that

\psi_{G}(y)=S(z,y)-\frac{1}{2}S(z,z).

Hence,
z\in P_{G}(y)
, as required.

Assume now that
P_{G}(y)
is a closed convex set and take

z\in P_{G}(y)
. Lemma
3.5
shows that
S(x-z,x-z)=0
. Hence,

z\in P_{G}(x)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Let
\gamma
be an optimal plan for (
2
). By extending, if
necessary, the probability space, we can find a
d
-dimensional
random variable
Z
such that
\gamma=\Law(Z,Y)
. As

x=\gamma(y|x)
, we have that
Z=\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y\right\lvert{Z}\right)
. Moreover,

3.3
yields that

\displaystyle Z\in G,\quad Y\in Q_{G}(Z).

It goes without saying that all pointwise relations are understood
in the almost sure sense. Using the assumption

, Lemma
3.5
shows that
P_{G}(Y)

takes values in closed convex sets. Lemma
3.7
yields that

The events that generate
\sigma(P_{G}(Y))
and, respectively,

\sigma(P_{G}(Z))
, differ by null sets. Therefore, conditioning on
either of the two
\sigma
-algebras yields the same result. In
addition,
\sigma(P_{G}(Z))\subset\sigma(Z)
. By the tower property
of conditional expectations we then have

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y\right\lvert{P_{G}(Y)}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y\right\lvert{P_{G}(Z)}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\left.\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y\right\lvert{Z}\right)\right\lvert{P_{G}(Z)}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Z\right\lvert{P_{G}(Z)}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Z\right\lvert{P_{G}(Y)}\right).

Since
\sigma(P_{G}(Y))\subset\sigma(Y)
, we have that

X\in\mathcal{X}(Y)
. By Theorem
3.3
, the result holds if
and only if

. As

takes values in closed

X\in P_{G}(Y)\iff\left\langle s,X\right\rangle\leq g_{s}(Y):=\sup_{z\in P_{G}(Y)}\left\langle s,z\right\rangle,\quad s\in\real{d},

where in showing the implication
\impliedby
, we choose an
exceptional null set that works for a dense countable subset of

s\in\real{d}
.

Let
s\in\real{d}
. By Lemma
3.6
, the function
g_{s}
is

\sigma(P_{G})
-measurable and so, the random variable
g_{s}(Y)
is

\sigma(P_{G}(Y))
-measurable. As
Z\in P_{G}(Y)
, we have that

\left\langle s,Z\right\rangle\leq g_{s}(Y)
. It follows that

\left\langle s,X\right\rangle=\mathbb{E}\left(\left.\left\langle s,Z\right\rangle\right\lvert{P_{G}(Y)}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\left.g_{s}(Y)\right\lvert{P_{G}(Y)}\right)=g_{s}(Y).

Hence,

, as required.
∎

]

Appendix A
Uniform approximation by maps

We continue to identify random variables on a probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
if they differ only on a set of
measure zero and interpret relations between them in the

\mathbb{P}-a.s.
sense. In particular, if
X
and
Z
are random
variables taking values in Polish (complete separable metric) spaces

(\mathbb{S}_{1},\rho_{1})
and
(\mathbb{S}_{2},\rho_{2})
, respectively,
then
X
is
Z
-measurable if and only if
X=f(Z)
(
\mathbb{P}-a.s.
)
for a Borel function
f:\;\mathbb{S}_{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}_{1}
.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be random variables on

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
taking values in Polish spaces

(\mathbb{S}_{1},\rho_{1})
and
(\mathbb{S}_{2},\rho_{2})
,
respectively. If the law of
Y
is atomless, then for every

\epsilon>0
there is a random variable
Z=Z(\epsilon)
taking
values in
(\mathbb{S}_{2},\rho_{2})
such that
\Law(Z)=\Law(Y)
,

\rho_{2}\left({Y,Z}\right)\leq\epsilon
, and
X
is
Z
-measurable.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon>0
. We take a dense sequence
y_{n}\in\mathbb{S}_{2}
,

n=1,2,\dots
, and define

B_{n}:=\left\{{{z\in\mathbb{S}_{2}}\;|\;\rho_{2}(z,y_{n})\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}}\right\},\quad D_{n}:=B_{n}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{n-1}B_{i},\quad n\geq 1.

By keeping only the terms having strictly positive probability, we
obtain sets
D_{n}\subset\mathbb{S}_{2}
,
n=1,2,\dots
, that are

mutually disjoint
and such that

Y\in\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}D_{n},\quad\mathbb{P}\left({Y\in D_{n}}\right)>0,\quad\diam(D_{n}):=\sup_{x,y\in D_{n}}\rho_{2}(x,y)\leq\varepsilon.

For each
n\geq 1
we apply Lemma
A.2
using the probability
measure

\mathbb{P}_{n}(A):=\mathbb{P}(A|Y\in D_{n})=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left({A\cap\left\{{Y\in D_{n}}\right\}}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left({Y\in D_{n}}\right)},\quad A\in\mathcal{F},

instead of the original measure
\mathbb{P}
. As a result, we
obtain a random variable
Z_{n}
taking values in
D_{n}
and a Borel
function
f_{n}:\;\mathbb{S}_{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}_{1}
such that
Z_{n}
and
Y
have
the same law under
\mathbb{P}_{n}
and

\mathbb{P}_{n}(X=f_{n}(Z_{n}))=1.

For a random variable
Z
taking values in
\mathbb{S}_{2}
and a
Borel function
f:\;\mathbb{S}_{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}_{1}
such that

Z(\omega)=Z_{n}(\omega)\text{~if~}Y(\omega)\in D_{n},\quad f(y)=f_{n}(y)\text{~if~}y\in D_{n},\quad n\geq 1,

we have that

\Law(Y)=\Law(Z),\quad{X=f(Z)},\quad\rho_{2}\left({Y,Z}\right)\leq\epsilon,

as required.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
X
and
Y
be random variables taking values in Polish spaces

(\mathbb{S}_{1},\rho_{1})
and
(\mathbb{S}_{2},\rho_{2})
,
respectively. If the law of
Y
is atomless, then there is a random
variable
Z
taking values in
(\mathbb{S}_{2},\rho_{2})
such that

\Law(Z)=\Law(Y)
and
X
is
Z
-measurable.

Proof.

Replacing
X
with
(X,Y)
we can assume from the start that the law
of
X
is atomless. Using bijections

g:\;\mathbb{S}_{1}\rightarrow\real{}
and

h:\;\mathbb{S}_{2}\rightarrow\real{}
that are Borel measurable together
with their inverses, we can also replace
X
with
g(X)
and
Y

with
h(Y)
. For the existence of such bijections we refer
to
[
3
, Theorem 13.1.1]
. Thus, we assume that both
X

and
Y
are real valued and their laws are atomless.

We denote by
F_{X}
and
Q_{X}
the cumulative distribution and
quantile functions of
X
:

\displaystyle F_{X}(x)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}\left({X\leq x}\right),\quad x\in\real{},

\displaystyle Q_{X}(u)

\displaystyle:=\min\left\{{{x\in\real{}}\;|\;u\leq F_{X}(x)}\right\},\quad u\in(0,1).

As
F_{X}
is continuous, the random variable
U:=F_{X}(X)
has
uniform distribution on
(0,1)
and
X=Q_{X}(U)
. Similarly,

V:=F_{Y}(Y)
has uniform distribution on
(0,1)
and
Y=Q_{Y}(V)
,
where
F_{Y}
and
Q_{Y}
are the cumulative distribution and quantile
functions of
Y
. Setting

Z:=Q_{Y}(U)=Q_{Y}(F_{X}(X)),

we deduce that
Z
has the same law as
Y
, that
U=F_{Y}(Z)
, and

X=Q_{X}(F_{Y}(Z)))
.
∎

Remark A.3
.

Let
X
and
Y
be random variables on

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
taking values in
\real{d}
and
assume that the law of
Y
is atomless. The conditional law of
X

given
Y
describes the randomized transport from
Y
, the
“origin”, to
X
, the “target”. A classical question is to
approximate
(X,Y)
by
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
, where the transport from
Y^{\prime}
to

X^{\prime}
is deterministic, that is,
X^{\prime}
is
Y^{\prime}
-measurable.

Theorem
A.1
shows that the random variable
(X,Y)
can be

pointwise uniformly
approximated by the elements of the
family

\mathcal{C}_{1}(X,Y):=\left\{{{(X,Z)}\;|\;\Law(Z)=\Law(Y)\text{~and
$X$ is $Z$-measurable}}\right\},

where the
target
random variable
X
is kept frozen.

In a more traditional approach, see
[
11
]

and
[
1
]
among the others, the approximating family is
taken to be

\mathcal{C}_{2}(X,Y):=\left\{{{(Z,Y)}\;|\;\Law(Z)=\Law(X)\text{~and
$Z$ is $Y$-measurable}}\right\},

where the
origin
random variable
Y
remains unchanged. It
has been shown that the law of
(X,Y)
can be
weakly

approximated by the laws of the elements of
\mathcal{C}_{2}(X,Y)
.

In general,
(X,Y)
can not be pointwise approximated by the
elements of
\mathcal{C}_{2}(X,Y)
even in the sense of convergence in
probability. For instance, if
X
and
Y
are independent
identically distributed and
(Z,Y)\in\mathcal{C}_{2}(X,Y)
, then
Z

and
X
are also independent (
Z
is a function of
Y
) identically
distributed. It follows that

\mathbb{P}\left({\left\lvert Z-X\right\rvert\geq\epsilon}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left({\left\lvert Y-X\right\rvert\geq\epsilon}\right)\geq\epsilon,

for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
.
