DUST: A Framework for Data-Driven Density Steering
Joshua Pilipovsky JPnow, Panagiotis Tsiotras

Abstract
We consider the problem of data-driven stochastic optimal control of an unknown LTI dynamical system. Assuming the process noise is normally distributed, we pose the problem of steering the state’s mean and covariance to a target normal distribution, under noisy data collected from the underlying system, a problem commonly referred to as covariance steering (CS). A novel framework for Data-driven Uncertainty quantification and density STeering (DUST) is presented that simultaneously characterizes the noise affecting the measured data and designs an optimal affine-feedback controller to steer the density of the state to a prescribed terminal value. We use both indirect and direct data-driven design approaches based on the notions of persistency of excitation and subspace identification to exactly represent the mean and covariance dynamics of the state in terms of the data and noise realizations. Since both the mean and the covariance steering sub-problems are plagued with stochastic uncertainty arising from noisy data collection, we first estimate the noise realization from this dataset and subsequently compute tractable upper bounds on the estimation errors. The first and second moment steering problems are then solved to optimality using techniques from robust control and robust optimization. Lastly, we present an alternative control design approach based on the certainty equivalence principle and interpret the problem as one of CS under multiplicative uncertainty. We analyze the performance and efficacy of each of these data-driven approaches using a case study and compare them with their model-based counterparts.

Introduction

The pursuit of safe and reliable control under uncertainty stands as a fundamental challenge in control theory.
Traditional model-based techniques, such as the linear-quadratic regulator (LQR) or model-predictive control (MPC) have been extensively explored, and have been shown to be extremely effective at controlling dynamical systems when the model accurately represents the actual physical system.
When there are model inaccuracies due to errors during system identification, robust control techniques
[
25
]
have been used to combat these inaccuracies and ensure robust constraint satisfaction and optimality under worst-case conditions.
By the same token, exogenous disturbances affecting the state of a system have been treated in a multitude of ways; when the uncertainties are bounded, the problem
falls under the realm of robust control
[
38
]
; when the uncertainties are probabilistic, the problem is treated using techniques from stochastic control
[
46
]
.

Recently, there has been a paradigm shift from looking at control synthesis as an
indirect
design process of first estimating a model and subsequently solving an optimal control problem using the identified model, to a
direct
control design from raw data collected from the underlying physical system.
This methodological shift has been inspired by the early works on behavioral system theory by Willems et. al.
[
67
]
, which showed that one can completely characterize the trajectory space of an LTI system by solely using raw data, as long as this data is persistently exciting, a result known as the
Fundamental Lemma
(FL)
[
67
]
.
This data-driven formalism is attractive for a variety of reasons: firstly, it bypasses the technicalities and challenges of system identification methods which fail for complex models, and, instead, provides a
direct
end-to-end solution from data input to control output.
Secondly, it is, in general, a more optimal scheme for control design
[
44
]
.
More importantly, however, it provides a
non-parametric
dynamics model that is, by construction, adaptable to any linear system.
As such, many of the traditional model-based control techniques have been re-explored within this data-driven context, with notable foundational works including DeePC
[
19
,
8
]
and data-driven LQR
[
60
,
22
,
23
]
.

The main issue, however, with the use of Willems’ Fundamental Lemma in many real-world applications is the fact that it only holds exactly for
deterministic
LTI systems.
When the system is perturbed by bounded disturbances or is subject to stochastic dynamics or measurement noise,
these direct data-driven schemes degrade rather quickly
[
27
]
.
As a result, much of the recent work on this front has been tailored to exploring ways to robustify against noisy data.
Notable methods along this line of research include suitably regularizing the optimization problem
[
18
,
17
]
, adding slack variables to account for data infeasibility
[
19
,
8
]
, and low-rank approximations of the data using truncated singular value decomposition
[
34
,
45
]
.
In fact, certain regularizers have a one-to-one correspondence with the corresponding indirect design methods, such as certainty-equivalence and subspace predictive control
[
29
]
, providing a bridge between the two approaches
[
26
,
27
]
.
Extensions such as
\gamma
-DeePC
[
12
]
and generalized-DeePC
[
39
]
aim to bridge the gap between subspace-predictive control and DeePC, benefiting from the positive aspects of both methods to tackle noisy data.
In the context of direct state-feedback control design, techniques from robust control have been used to robustly stabilize all possible systems consistent with the data and perturbed by energy-bounded disturbances
[
10
,
11
]
.

The setting of stochastic predictive control (SPC), however, is far less studied, and the problem of designing optimal controllers for
data-driven
stochastic LTI systems under noisy data is much more challenging.
Existing works on data-driven stochastic control adopt rather restrictive assumptions, such as noise-free offline data
[
35
]
or exact polynomial chaos expansions of stochastic measurements
[
51
,
28
]
.
A notable step towards a generalized framework for SPC is
[
72
]
, where the authors explicitly quantify the statistical properties of error quantification from an indirect design based on noisy data, use a Kalman filter to estimate the initial condition, and reformulate the chance-constrained SPC problem as a semi-definite program (SDP).

In this work, we take a slightly different approach to the data-driven stochastic optimal control problem.
We pose the control problem as one of steering the probability density of the state, as opposed to just the (mean of the) state vector.
The canonical problem in this regard is to steer the state density of a linear system to some target Gaussian distribution subject to independent Gaussian disturbances and chance constraints.
This problem has been solved exactly in both discrete-time
[
50
,
5
]
and continuous-time
[
16
,
15
]
settings, with demonstrated success in many engineering applications, such as spacecraft rendezvous
[
52
]
, interplanetary trajectory optimization
[
58
]
, and autonomous driving
[
69
]
, to name a few.
In the Gaussian setting, and since the state remains normally distributed for the entire horizon, the problem boils down to steering the first two moments of the state, hence the approach is often referred to as covariance steering (CS).
Extensions of CS that take into account non-Gaussian disturbances
[
62
,
42
]
, multiplicative and parametric disturbances
[
37
,
6
]
, distributional uncertainty
[
54
,
56
]
, and extensions to nonlinear systems
[
65
,
57
]
have also been reported in the literature.

In this paper, we develop a general framework to steer the distribution of an
unknown
stochastic LTI system using raw data collected offline, instead of using a known system model.
We are interested in the so-called Data-Driven Density Steering (DD-DS) control problem and
we develop a generalized framework to solve this problem, henceforth referred to as
D
ata-driven
U
ncertainty quantification and density
ST
eering (DUST).
Since we will be dealing primarily with linear systems, we will, alternatively, investigate the Data-Driven Mean Steering (DD-MS) and Data-Driven Covariance Steering (DD-CS) problems.
By combining model-based CS theory with behavioral systems theory and statistical learning, we provide a robust framework to steer both the mean and covariance of the state distribution to a desired terminal distribution.
An illustrative flowchart of the proposed framework is shown in Figure
1
.

We first decompose the problem into one of data-driven mean steering (DD-MS) and one of data-driven covariance steering (DD-CS), as this framework allows for a separation principle for the individual moment trajectories, excluding the presence of constraints (Section
2
).
We then follow an indirect certainty-equivalence (CE) route to exactly parameterize the mean dynamics in terms of the unknown noise realizations in the data, resulting in an uncertain quadratic program (Section
3
).
For covariance control, we parameterize the feedback gains directly using the collected data and use established model-based CS theory to arrive at an uncertain SDP.

To handle the uncertainty during data collection, Section
4
develops novel noise estimation schemes to quantify the noise using techniques from maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) and neural networks.
We then provide connections with the corresponding indirect design techniques.
Using the known statistical properties from MLE, as well as quantitative notions of persistence of excitation, we construct high-confidence noise estimation error bounds in Section
5
.
These uncertainty bounds are then used in Section
6
to construct uncertainty sets to
tractably reformulate the uncertain MS and CS problems as robust control and robust optimization problems, respectively, which can be solved efficiently to optimality using standard off-the-shelf solvers.

Since the noise enters multiplicatively in the indirect design formulation, we alternatively formulate a parametric uncertainty DD-DS (PU-DD-DS) problem and use convex relaxations to tractably solve the original problem in Section
7
.
Lastly, to illustrate the proposed framework, in Section
8
we perform an in-depth study of the proposed control design methods, analyzing their efficacy and precision, and compare them with their model-based counterparts.
We conclude with a discussion of the proposed framework, and we offer several avenues for future extensions.

Contributions

This paper extends our previous work
[
53
,
55
]
, where we studied the limiting case of this problem, namely, the dynamics were assumed to be deterministic and the uncertainty resided solely in the boundary conditions.
In this case, Willems Fundamental Lemma holds exactly, and we can achieve an exact correspondence with model-based designs.
In addition,
[
55
]
studied the full data-driven density steering problem with noise quantification.
However, in that work, the uncertainty sets were unnecessarily conservative (see Section
5
).
In this paper, we remove this restrictive assumption.

In summary, the contributions of this work are as follows:

1.

We introduce a unified data-driven framework for steering the full state distribution of an unknown stochastic LTI system using offline data, by combining behavioral systems theory with model-based covariance steering ideas.

2.

We develop data-driven noise estimation and uncertainty quantification tools to find the most likely noise realization, along with its covariance, and provide finite-sample/asymptotic, high-confidence ambiguity sets for the corresponding estimation errors.

3.

We provide tractable (convex optimization) formulations for both the data-driven mean steering (DD-MS) and covariance steering (DD-CS) problems.
Each problem is solved so as
to guarantee terminal distribution constraint satisfaction with high probability.

4.

We present a parametric-uncertainty (PU-DD-DS) formulation, interpreting the certainty equivalent (CE) problem formulation in terms of controlling a system subject to multiplicative uncertainty, and provide a tractable convex formulation for its solution, showcasing an alternative approach to the data-driven density steering problem.

5.

We evaluate the performance and efficacy of the proposed data-driven designs against alternative CE-based approaches and against model-based counterparts.

Problem Formulation

2.1
Problem Statement

We consider the following linear, discrete-time stochastic time-invariant system

~\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}=A\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+B\boldsymbol{u}_{k}+D\boldsymbol{w}_{k},

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state,
\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the control input, and
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
are i.i.d. Gaussian disturbances, with time steps
k\in[\![N-1]\!]_{0}
, where
N
represents the horizon length.
Alternatively, we may re-write (
1
) as

~\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}=A\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+B\boldsymbol{u}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k},

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
are i.i.d. random vectors for all
k\in[\![T-1]\!]_{0}
, and where
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\triangleq DD^{\intercal}
is the covariance of the noise.
The constant system matrices
A,B,D
are assumed to be
unknown
.
The initial state
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
is a random
n
-dimensional vector drawn from the normal distribution

~\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i}),

(3)

where
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the initial state mean and
\Sigma_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}

is the initial state covariance.
It is assumed that the initial state is independent of the noise sequence, that is,
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\intercal}]=0
, for all
k\in[\![N-1]\!]_{0}
.

The objective is to steer the trajectories of (
1
) from the initial distribution (
3
) to the terminal distribution

~\boldsymbol{x}_{N}=\boldsymbol{x}_{f}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{f},\Sigma_{f}),

(4)

where
\mu_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\Sigma_{f}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
are the desired state mean and covariance at time step
N
, respectively.
The cost function to be minimized is

~J(\boldsymbol{u})\triangleq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-x_{k}^{r})^{\intercal}Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-x_{k}^{r})+\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{\intercal}R_{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\bigg],

(5)

where
\{x_{k}^{r}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
is a given reference trajectory, and
Q_{k}\succeq 0
and
R_{k}\succ 0
for all
k\in[\![N-1]\!]_{0}
.

Remark 1
.

We assume that the system (
1
) is controllable, that is, for any
x_{0},x_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and no noise (
w_{k}\equiv 0,~k\in[\![N-1]\!]_{0}
), there exists a sequence of control inputs
\{u_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
that steer the system from
x_{0}
to
x_{f}
.

It is assumed that we are given a
T
-long trajectory dataset
\mathbb{D}\triangleq\{x_{k}^{(d)},u_{k}^{(d)},x_{T}^{(d)}\}_{k=0}^{T-1}
for control design.
Although
it is also possible to synthesize the controllers using multiple episodic datasets
\mathbb{D}_{\ell}
(
\ell=1,\ldots,M
), for simplicity, in the present work we assume only a single dataset.
Borrowing from the work in
[
41
]
,
we assume affine state feedback policies of the form,

~\boldsymbol{u}_{k}=K_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\mu_{k})+v_{k},

(6)

parameterized by an open-loop control sequence
v=\{v_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
and a feedback gain sequence
K=\{K_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
,
where

\mu_{k}\triangleq{}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{x}_{k}]
is the mean state.

In summary, the data-driven distribution steering (DD-DS) problem is stated below.

Problem 2

(DD-DS)
.

Given the dataset
\mathbb{D}
, find
the feedforward
\{v_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
and feedback gain

\{K_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
sequences
that minimize the objective function (
5
), subject to the initial state (
3
) and terminal state (
4
) boundary conditions.

We note that under the control law (
6
), and with complete system knowledge, it is possible to re-write Problem
2
as a convex program
[
41
]
, which can be solved to optimality using off-the-shelf solvers
[
4
]
.
Next, we proceed to solve Problem
2
in the case where the system matrices are not known.

2.2
Problem Reformulation

Since the state distribution remains Gaussian at all time steps, we decompose the system dynamics (
1
) into the mean dynamics and covariance dynamics.
Plugging in the control law (
6
) into the dynamics (
1
) yields the decoupled dynamics

\displaystyle\mu_{k+1}

\displaystyle=A\mu_{k}+Bv_{k},

(7a)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(A+BK_{k})\Sigma_{k}(A+BK_{k})^{\intercal}+DD^{\intercal},

(7b)

where the state covariance
\Sigma_{k}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\mu_{k})(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\mu_{k})^{\intercal}]
.
Note that
v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
controls the state mean and

K_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
controls the state covariance.

Contrary to the approaches in
[
5
,
50
]
that formulate a convex program in the lifted space of state and control trajectories, in this work, and similarly to
[
41
]
, we treat the moments of the intermediate states
\{\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N}
over the steering horizon as decision variables subject to the dynamic constraints (
7
).

First, and similar to the decoupled dynamics (
7
), the cost function (
5
) can be decoupled in terms of the mean and covariance as follows

J=J_{\mu}(\mu_{k},v_{k})+J_{\Sigma}(\Sigma_{k},K_{k}),

(8)

where,

\displaystyle J_{\mu}

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\Big((\mu_{k}-x_{k}^{r})^{\intercal}Q_{k}(\mu_{k}-x_{k}^{r})+v_{k}^{\intercal}R_{k}v_{k}\Big),

(9a)

\displaystyle J_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\Big(\textrm{tr}(Q_{k}\Sigma_{k})+\textrm{tr}(R_{k}K_{k}\Sigma_{k}K_{k}^{\intercal})\Big).

(9b)

The two boundary conditions (
3
) and (
4
) are written as

\displaystyle\mu_{0}=\mu_{i},\quad\mu_{N}=\mu_{f},

(10a)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}=\Sigma_{i},\quad\Sigma_{N}=\Sigma_{f},

(10b)

where
\Sigma_{i},\Sigma_{f}\succ 0
.
Problem
2
is now recast as the following two sub-problems.

Problem 3

(DD-MS)
.

Given the (unknown) mean dynamics (
7a
), find the
optimal feed-forward control sequence
\{v_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
and the corresponding
optimal mean trajectory
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N}
that minimize the mean cost (
9a
) subject to the boundary conditions (
10a
).

Problem 4

(DD-CS)
.

Given the (unknown) covariance dynamics (
7b
), find the optimal
feedback gain sequence
\{K_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}

and the corresponding
covariance trajectory
\{\Sigma_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N}
that minimize the covariance cost (
9b
) subject to the boundary conditions (
10b
).

Remark 5
.

Under complete model knowledge, Problem
3
is a standard quadratic program with linear constraints that can be solved analytically given knowledge of the system matrices.
Problem
4
, on the other hand, is a non-linear and non-convex program due to the term
K_{k}\Sigma_{k}K_{k}^{\intercal}
arising both in the cost function (
9b
) and the covariance dynamics (
7b
).
However,
as it was shown in
[
41
]
this problem can be relaxed to a convex optimization problem by the introduction of additional slack variables.
Moreover, this relaxation is lossless.
For further details, we refer the interested reader to
[
41
]
.

3
Data-Driven Parameterization

In this section, we review the main concepts from behavioral systems theory
[
43
]
that will allow us to parametrize the decision variables in Problems
3
and
4
in terms of the collected input and output data streams.

First, recall the following definitions.

Definition 6
.

Given a signal
\{z_{k}\}
where
z_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{\sigma}
, we denote the Hankel matrix of depth
\ell
by

Z_{i,\ell,j}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}z_{i}&z_{i+1}&\ldots&z_{i+j-1}\\
z_{i+1}&z_{i+2}&\ldots&z_{i+j}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
z_{i+\ell-1}&z_{i+\ell}&\ldots&z_{i+\ell+j-2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{\sigma\ell\times j},

(11)

where
i\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\ell,j\in\mathbb{N}
.
For shorthand of notation, if
\ell=1
, we denote the Hankel matrix by

Z_{i,1,j}\equiv Z_{i,j}=[z_{i}\ z_{i+1}\ \ldots\ z_{i+j-1}].

(12)

Definition 7
.

The signal
\{z_{k}\}_{k=0}^{T-1}:[\![T-1]\!]_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\sigma T}

is
persistently exciting
of order
\ell
if the matrix
Z_{0,\ell,j}
with
j=T-\ell+1
has rank
\sigma\ell
.

Suppose we carry out an experiment of duration
T\in\mathbb{N}
, where we collect input and noisy state data
\{u_{k}^{(d)}\}_{k=0}^{T-1}
and
\{x_{k}^{(d)}\}_{k=0}^{T}
, respectively.
Let the corresponding Hankel matrices for the input sequence, state sequence, and shifted state sequence (with
\ell=1
) be

\displaystyle U_{0,T}

\displaystyle\triangleq[u_{0}^{(d)}\ u_{1}^{(d)}\ \ldots\ u_{T-1}^{(d)}],

(13a)

\displaystyle X_{0,T}

\displaystyle\triangleq[x_{0}^{(d)}\ x_{1}^{(d)}\ \ldots\ x_{T-1}^{(d)}],

(13b)

\displaystyle X_{1,T}

\displaystyle\triangleq[x_{1}^{(d)}\ x_{2}^{(d)}\ \ldots\ x_{T}^{(d)}].

(13c)

Using (
13
), the system dynamics (
2
) take the form

X_{1,T}=AX_{0,T}+BU_{0,T}+\Xi_{0,T},

(14)

where
\Xi_{0,T}\triangleq[\xi_{0}^{(d)},\ldots,\xi_{T-1}^{(d)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}
is the Hankel matrix of the (unknown) disturbances, and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}^{(d)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
.

Assuming that the data is
persistently exciting
(PE), the block Hankel matrix of the input and state data has full row rank

~\mathrm{rank}\begin{bmatrix}U_{0,T}\\
X_{0,T}\end{bmatrix}=n+m.

(15)

This PE assumption is crucial for direct data-driven control design, and is generally a mild assumption in practice, especially when noisy data is used
[
23
]
.
The condition in (
15
) implies that
any
arbitrary input-state sequence of (
1
) can be expressed as a linear combination of the collected input-state data.
Furthermore,
this idea can be used to parameterize any arbitrary feedback interconnection
[
60
]
.
In the following section, we parameterize the feedback gains in terms of the input-state data and reformulate the covariance steering problem as a semi-definite program (SDP).
First, we focus on the mean steering problem, by first identifying the system matrices using the collected input-state data.
Because of this extra system identification step, the data-driven mean steering problem is solved using an indirect approach.

3.1
Indirect Data-Driven Mean Steering (DD-MS)

Given the mean dynamics (
7a
) in terms of the open-loop control
v_{k}
, the PE condition (
15
) provides a system identification type of result using the following theorem.

Theorem 8
.

Suppose
\mathbb{D}=\{x_{k}^{(d)},u_{k}^{(d)},x_{T}^{(d)}\}_{k=0}^{T-1}
is a dataset collected from the underlying system (
1
) such that the rank condition (
15
) holds.
Then, system (
7a
) has the following equivalent representation

~\mu_{k+1}=(X_{1,T}-\Xi_{0,T})\begin{bmatrix}U_{0,T}\\
X_{0,T}\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger}\begin{bmatrix}v_{k}\\
\mu_{k}\end{bmatrix}.

(16)

PROOF.

See
[
22
]
for details.

Theorem
8
provides a data-based representation of a linear system.
Assuming exact knowledge of the noise realization
\Xi_{0,T}
, one may equivalently interpret equation (
16
)
as the solution to the least-squares problem

~\min_{B,A}\ \left\|X_{1,T}-\Xi_{0,T}-[B\ \ A]\begin{bmatrix}U_{0,T}\\
X_{0,T}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}},

(17)

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{F}}
is the Frobenius norm.
Data-driven indirect designs based on the certainty-equivalence (CE) principle compute an approximate system description by solving (
17
) assuming no noise (i.e.,
\Xi_{0,T}=0
).
Using Theorem
8
, we can express the mean steering problem as the following convex optimization problem

\min_{\mu_{k},v_{k}}J_{\mu}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\Big((\mu_{k}-x_{k}^{r})^{\intercal}Q_{k}(\mu_{k}-x_{k}^{r})+v_{k}^{\intercal}R_{k}v_{k}\Big),

(18a)

such that, for all
k\in[\![N-1]\!]_{0}
,

F_{v}(\Xi_{0,T})v_{k}+F_{\mu}(\Xi_{0,T})\mu_{k}-\mu_{k+1}=0,

(18b)

with the boundary conditions (
10a
), where
F_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
F_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
result from the partition of the matrix
F
as follows

~F\triangleq(X_{1,T}-\Xi_{0,T})\begin{bmatrix}U_{0,T}\\
X_{0,T}\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger}=\begin{bmatrix}F_{v}(\Xi_{0,T})&F_{\mu}(\Xi_{0,T})\end{bmatrix}.

(18c)

3.2
Direct Data-Driven Covariance Steering (DD-CS)

To solve the DD-CS problem, first notice that
from the rank condition (
15
), we can express the feedback gains as follows

~\begin{bmatrix}K_{k}\\
I_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}U_{0,T}\\
X_{0,T}\end{bmatrix}G_{k},

(19)

where
G_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times n}
are newly defined decision variables that provide the link between the feedback gains and the input-state data.
Furthermore, using this parameterization, we can re-write the covariance dynamics (
7b
) as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k+1}

\displaystyle=[B\ \ A]\begin{bmatrix}K_{k}\\
I_{n}\end{bmatrix}\Sigma_{k}\begin{bmatrix}K_{k}\\
I_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}[B\ \ A]^{\intercal}+DD^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=(X_{1,T}-\Xi_{0,T})G_{k}\Sigma_{k}G_{k}^{\intercal}(X_{1,T}-\Xi_{0,T})^{\intercal}+\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}.

(20)

Similarly, the covariance cost (
9b
) can be re-written as

~J_{\Sigma,k}=\mathrm{tr}(Q_{k}\Sigma_{k})+\mathrm{tr}(R_{k}U_{0,T}G_{k}\Sigma_{k}G_{k}^{\intercal}U_{0,T}^{\intercal}).

(21)

To remedy the nonlinearity
G_{k}\Sigma_{k}G_{k}^{\intercal}
in the covariance dynamics and the cost, define the new decision variables
S_{k}\triangleq G_{k}\Sigma_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times n}
, which yields

~\Sigma_{k+1}=(X_{1,T}-\Xi_{0,T})S_{k}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}S_{k}^{\intercal}(X_{1,T}-\Xi_{0,T})^{\intercal}+\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}},

(22)

and

~J_{\Sigma,k}=\mathrm{tr}(Q_{k}\Sigma_{k})+\mathrm{tr}(R_{k}U_{0,T}S_{k}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}S_{k}^{\intercal}U_{0,T}^{\intercal}).

(23)

This problem is still non-convex due to the nonlinear term
S_{k}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}S_{k}^{\intercal}
.
To this end, we define a new set of decision variables
Y_{k}\succeq S_{k}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}S_{k}^{\intercal}
and relax the objective function with these new variables.
Similarly, we relax the covariance propagation constraints to soft (inequality) constraints, which yields the relaxed optimization problem

\min_{\Sigma_{k},S_{k},Y_{k}}\bar{J}_{\Sigma}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left(\mathrm{tr}(Q_{k}\Sigma_{k})+\mathrm{tr}(R_{k}U_{0,T}Y_{k}U_{0,T}^{\intercal})\right),

(24a)

such that, for all
k\in[\![N-1]\!]_{0}
,

\displaystyle S_{k}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}S_{k}^{\intercal}-Y_{k}\preceq 0,

(24b)

\displaystyle(X_{1,T}-\Xi_{0,T})S_{k}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}S_{k}^{\intercal}(X_{1,T}-\Xi_{0,T})^{\intercal}

\displaystyle\hskip 99.58464pt+\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}-\Sigma_{k+1}\preceq 0,

(24c)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k}-X_{0,T}S_{k}=0,

(24d)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{N}-\Sigma_{f}=0.

(24e)

The equality constraint (
24d
) comes from the second block in (
19
) by multiplying
\Sigma_{k}
on the right.
The relaxed optimization problem (
24
) is convex, since the constraints (
24b
) and (
24c
) can be written using the Schur complement in terms of the linear matrix inequalities (LMI)

\displaystyle\hskip 17.07164pt\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{k}&S_{k}^{\intercal}\\
S_{k}&Y_{k}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,

(25a)

\displaystyle G_{\Sigma}^{k}

\displaystyle\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\hskip-8.53581pt\Sigma_{k+1}-\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}&(X_{1,T}-\Xi_{0,T})S_{k}\\
S_{k}^{\intercal}(X_{1,T}-\Xi_{0,T})^{\intercal}&\Sigma_{k}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0.

(25b)

The cost (
24a
) and the equality constraints (
24d
)-(
24e
), on the other hand, are linear in all the decision variables, and hence are trivially convex.

In Appendix
A
we show that
the optimal
\{\Sigma_{k}^{\star}\}
from the solution of (
24
) satisfy
P_{k}\preceq\Sigma_{k}^{\star}
with respect to the closed-loop covariance evolution under
K_{k}^{\star}
, where
P_{k}
denotes the true covariance at time step
k
.
Hence, the solution from the relaxed DD-CS problem (
24
) will satisfy the terminal covariance constraints
P_{N}\preceq\Sigma_{f}
.

It should be mentioned, however, that, in general, the relaxed convex program (
24
) is
not
lossless, contrary to the model-based case
[
41
]
.

4
Noise Estimation Algorithms

The optimization problems (
18
) and (
24
), as they stand, albeit convex, are still intractable because we know neither the disturbance covariance
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
nor the noise realization history
\Xi_{0,T}
.
To this end, in this section we propose a method to estimate the noise realization matrix
\Xi_{0,T}
and the disturbance matrix
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
using the collected data
\mathbb{D}
.
We propose two methods
to find these matrices: the first method trains a feed-forward neural network (NN) to estimate both the disturbance and noise realization matrices; the second method estimates
\Xi_{0,T}
and
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
using maximum likelihood estimation (MLE).
We illustrate these noise estimation techniques next.

4.1
ML Noise Estimation

To encode the stochastic linear system dynamics (
2
) as a constraint that we can use in the MLE scheme, we need to enforce
consistency
of the realization data.
To this end, any realization of the dynamics (
2
) must satisfy
(
14
).
For notational convenience, henceforth, we denote the augmented Hankel matrix in (
13
) as

\mathcal{S}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}U_{0,T}\\
X_{0,T}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+n)\times T},

(26)

from which we may re-write the dynamics realization (
14
) as

X_{1,T}=[B\ A]\mathcal{S}+\Xi_{0,T}.

(27)

Additionally, noting that the matrix pseudoinverse satisfies the property
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}
, equation (
27
) can be written, equivalently, as

X_{1,T}-\Xi_{0,T}=[B\ A]\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}\mathcal{S}.

(28)

Inserting the relation
X_{1,T}-\Xi_{0,T}=[B\ A]\mathcal{S}
into the right-hand side of (
28
) yields

~(X_{1,T}-\Xi_{0,T})(I_{T}-\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}\mathcal{S})=0.

(29)

Equation (
29
) is a
model-free
type of condition that must be satisfied for all noisy linear system data realizations, and hence is a consistency relation for any feasible set
\mathcal{S}
of data.

Given the constraint (
29
), the MLE problem then becomes

\displaystyle\max_{\Xi_{0,T},\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{ML}}(\Xi_{0,T},\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\log\rho_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\xi_{k})

(30a)

\displaystyle(X_{1,T}-\Xi_{0,T})(I_{T}-\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}\mathcal{S})=0,

(30b)

where
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\xi)
is the probability density function (PDF) of the random vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, given as

~\rho_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\xi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}(\det\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})^{-1/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\xi^{\intercal}\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{-1}\xi\right).

(31)

Remark 9
.

Since the dynamics (
1
) are uncertain, it follows that there may be multiple noise realizations
\Xi_{0,T}
that satisfy the linear dynamics constraints (
29
).
Consequently, the purpose of the constrained MLE problem (
30
) is to find the most likely
noise realization sequence
\hat{\Xi}_{0,T}
given that the noise is normally distributed according to (
31
).

The next theorem provides the optimal solution to the MLE problem (
30
).

Theorem 10
.

The solution to the MLE problem for the most probable noise realization
\Xi_{0,T}
and disturbance covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is given by

\displaystyle\Xi_{0,T}^{\star}

\displaystyle=X_{1,T}(I_{T}-\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}\mathcal{S}),

(32a)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}X_{1,T}(I_{T}-\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}\mathcal{S})X_{1,T}^{\intercal}.

(32b)

PROOF.

The proof is given in Appendix
B
.

Remark 11
.

The solution (
32
) of the MLE program in (
30
) is contingent on
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\succ 0
.
In fact, the optimal covariance estimate is simply the sample covariance of the dataset with respect to the estimated noise realizations, i.e.,
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\star}=\frac{1}{T}\Xi_{0,T}^{\star}(\Xi_{0,T}^{\star})^{\intercal}
.
If
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is singular, however, then
\log\det\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is undefined, hence the problem is infeasible.
As a result, other methods, such as NN estimation (Section
4.2
below), or regularization techniques (e.g., GLASSO
[
30
]
, distributionally-robust estimation
[
49
]
) should be used in these cases, instead.
It should also be noted that such degenerate cases arise when the number of disturbance channels is less than the number of states channels, i.e.,
D\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, with
d<n
.

4.2
NN Noise Estimation

An alternative way to estimate the realization noise given the data set
\mathbb{D}

is by training a feed-forward neural network.
To this end, let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n(T+1)+mT}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{nT}
denote the NN mapping, where the input is
x\triangleq[x_{0}^{(d)^{\intercal}},x_{1}^{(d)^{\intercal}},\ldots,x_{T}^{(d)^{\intercal}},u_{0}^{(d)^{\intercal}},\ldots,u_{T-1}^{(d)^{\intercal}}]^{\intercal}
, and the output is
y\triangleq[\xi_{0}^{(d)^{\intercal}},\ldots,\xi_{T-1}^{(d)^{\intercal}}]^{\intercal}=\mathrm{vec}(\Xi_{0,T})
.
Without loss of generality, we may consider a NN with ReLU activation functions.
A ReLU NN transforms, at each layer
k
, the input as

x_{k}=f_{k}(x_{k-1})=\max(W_{k}x_{k-1}+b_{k},0),

where
W_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{k}\times\ell_{k-1}}
is the weight and
b_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{k}}
is the bias.
Once an estimate,
\hat{\Xi}_{0,T}
, of the noise realization history is obtained, the noise covariance is computed simply as the sample covariance of the estimated data via (
32b
).
Of course, it is also possible to construct more elaborate networks to estimate both matrices of interest simultaneously.

Learning-based disturbance estimators (e.g., NN above) can achieve strong empirical performance on specific problem instances; however, in this work we choose to adopt an MLE-based approach because it yields closed-form, finite-sample characterizations of the estimation errors (see Appendix
F
), which we leverage to construct tractable convex uncertainty sets that certify terminal constraints with probability greater than
1-\delta
.
By contrast, the NN scheme is supervised and presumes access to an
oracle
that provides noise-realization labels.
Moreover, the learned mapping depends on the system dynamics and on the data-collection horizon
T
, typically necessitating re-training across regimes, and its performance is sensitive to the informativeness of the inputs used to excite the system.
Systematically quantifying this data-informativeness/identification-performance link for learned disturbance models remains an interesting topic for future work.

4.3
Indirect Design Estimation

We conclude this section by observing that an alternative way to extract disturbance information from noisy data is by examining the difference between the observed state and the state prediction from the dynamics model.
Referring back to (
2
), and assuming knowledge of the system matrices
A
and
B
, the disturbance would be given by

~\xi_{k}^{(d)}=x_{k+1}^{(d)}-Ax_{k}^{(d)}-Bu_{k}^{(d)},\quad k\in[\![T-1]\!]_{0}.

(33)

Given the collected data, and under the rank condition (
15
), an estimate
(\hat{B},\hat{A})
of the system matrices can be obtained as the unique solution to the (noiseless) least-squares problem

~[\hat{B}\ \hat{A}]=\mathrm{argmin}_{B,A}\ \|X_{1,T}-[B\ A]\mathcal{S}\|_{\rm F}=X_{1,T}\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}.

(34)

Concatenating (
33
) over the entire sampling horizon yields the equality
\Xi_{0,T}=X_{1,T}-[B\ A]\mathcal{S}
(see also (
27
)).
We may then estimate the noise sequence from the estimated model parameters in (
34
) as
\hat{\Xi}_{0,T}=X_{1,T}-[\hat{B}\ \hat{A}]\mathcal{S}
, or

~\hat{\Xi}_{0,T}=X_{1,T}(I_{T}-\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}\mathcal{S}),

(35)

from which we may compute the disturbance covariance as in (
32b
).
The procedure, then, is to first estimate the nominal model, then estimate the disturbance structure, and lastly solve the associated CS problem using these estimated model parameters.

Notice that the optimal noise realization under the assumption of a
noiseless
system, (
35
), is equivalent to that of the optimal ML noise realization, (
32a
).
Thus, the CE estimation solution (
35
) is equivalent to the MLE solution (
32a
) under a known disturbance structure, implying that there may be deeper parallels between indirect and direct design methods in the context of noisy data.
For an overview of this observation, please see
[
26
]
.

The estimated value of
\hat{\Xi}_{0,T}
(and, perhaps,
\hat{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
) will differ, in general, from its true value
{\Xi}_{0,T}
.
For control design,
we need to quantify the estimation error
\Delta\Xi_{0,T}\triangleq{\Xi}_{0,T}-\hat{\Xi}_{0,T}
.
In the next section, we therefore derive high-probability bounds on the estimation error
\Delta\Xi_{0,T}
which is used in Section
6
in a robust data-driven design by enforcing the running and terminal state constraints to hold within the given uncertainty sets, defined by these bounds, with high probability.

5
Uncertainty Set Synthesis

Given the noise estimation techniques outlined in Section
4
, we now present two methods to derive bounds for the noise realization estimation errors
to be used later in a robust control design.
As mentioned earlier, this is a necessary step to account for the model mismatch due to noisy data.
The first method is based on the so-called
Robust
Fundamental Lemma (RFL)
[
20
]
, which provides a stricter persistency of excitation condition, and guarantees bounded least-squares estimation errors for the indirect design.
The second method, based on the MLE noise estimation scheme, uses the statistical properties of the estimator to construct an upper bound on the noise estimation error with high confidence.

5.1
Robust Fundamental Lemma

As mentioned in Section
4.3
, an estimate of the system matrices

\{\hat{A},\hat{B}\}
using an input/output dataset
\mathbb{D}

can be obtained as the unique solution to the least-squares problem (
34
).
Now, recall from rom (
34
) that
[\hat{B}\ \hat{A}]=X_{1,T}\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}
.
The PE condition
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{S})=n+m
implies that
\mathcal{S}
is right invertible, and therefore it follows from (
27
) that
[B\ A]=(X_{1,T}-\Xi_{0,T})\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}
.
It then follows that
the model error can be upper-bounded as

~\|[\hat{B}\ \hat{A}]-[B\ A]\|=\left\|\Xi_{0,T}\begin{bmatrix}U_{0,T}\\
X_{0,T}\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger}\right\|\leq\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\Xi_{0,T})}{\sigma_{\min}(\mathcal{S})},

(36)

where
\sigma_{\min}(\mathcal{S})>0
.
Since we do not have any control over the noise realization
\Xi_{0,T}
, we instead focus on the data matrix
\mathcal{S}
defined in (
26
).

Definition 12

(
[
20
]
, Quantitative PE)
.

Let
T>0,z_{k}:[\![T-1]\!]_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\sigma},\alpha>0
, and let
\ell>0
, such that
T\geq\ell(\sigma+1)-1
.
The input sequence
\{z_{k}\}_{k=0}^{T-1}
is
\alpha
-persistency exciting
of order
\ell
if
\sigma_{\min}(Z_{0,\ell,T-\ell+1})\geq\alpha
.

Note that this is a direct generalization of the familiar PE condition in Definition
7
.
Indeed, any
\alpha
-PE input sequence of order
\ell
is also PE of order
\ell
.
Using this definition, the following theorem establishes sufficient conditions for the lower bound of the minimum singular value of
\mathcal{S}
.

Theorem 13

(
[
20
]
, Robust Fundamental Lemma)
.

Let
T,\delta>0
and assume that the pair
(A,B)
is controllable.
Define the square matrix

0_{m\times n}&0_{m}&0_{m\times mn}\end{bmatrix},

\mathcal{Z}=\{z=[\xi^{\intercal}\ \eta^{\intercal}\ 0_{nm}^{\intercal}]^{\intercal}\ |\ \xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\|z\|=1\}

Define, for
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, the matrix
\Theta_{z}\triangleq[z\ M^{\intercal}z\ \cdots\ (M^{\intercal})^{n}z]^{\intercal}

\kappa>0
such that
1
1

1

The existence of such a

(A,B)
. See
[
20

for all

\sigma_{\min}(\Theta_{z})\geq\kappa

Let
\{x_{k}^{(d)},u_{k}^{(d)},x_{T}^{(d)}\}_{k=0}^{T-1}

(37)

where

This, in turn, provides a tighter upper bound through (

[\Delta B\ \Delta A]\triangleq[B\ A]-[\hat{B}\ \hat{A}]
in the indirect design method of Section
6.1
for the robust solution of the DD-MS problem.
Alternatively, we can use this model error bound in the direct robust DD-CS design of Section

as follows.

First, re-write the realization dynamics (
14

~X_{1,T}-(\Delta\Xi_{0,T}+\hat{\Xi}_{0,T})=([\hat{B}\ \hat{A}]+[\Delta B\ \Delta A])\mathcal{S}.

(38)

Further, by taking
\hat{\Xi}_{0,T}
as the MLE solution in (

), and
[\hat{B}\ \hat{A}]=X_{1,T}\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}

(39)

\displaystyle\|\Delta\Xi_{0,T}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|[\Delta B\ \Delta A]\|\|\mathcal{S}\|

Thus, we obtain a bound of the form
\|\Delta\Xi_{0,T}\|\leq\rho(\delta)
, given the desired robustness level
\delta>0
.

Unfortunately, the estimation error bound (

) cannot be computed easily, due to the unknown noise realization
\Xi_{0,T}
and the constant

.
However, it may be possible to upper bound these quantities.
For example, using techniques from random matrix theory (RMT), it can be shown from the Sudakov-Fernique inequality

that

\mathbb{E}[\|\Xi_{0,T}\|]\leq\|\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{1/2}\|(\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{T}).

(41)

We will not pursue this avenue further in this work.
Instead, in the next sections we establish computable bounds for
\|\Delta\Xi_{0,T}\|
that can be derived from the problem data.

5.2
Moment-Based Ambiguity Sets

In light of the discussion following (
40
), we are interested in practical bounds we can implement to ensure robust satisfaction of the constraints for the DD-MS (e.g., (
10a
))
and DD-CS (e.g., (
24c
))
problems.
To do so, and equipped with the MLE noise realization estimate (
32a
), we will use the statistical properties of
\hat{\Xi}_{0,T}
to generate an ellipsoidal uncertainty set based on some degree of confidence
1-\delta\in[0.5,1)
.
In the context of the control design problem, this will imply that the resulting controller will steer the system to the desired final distribution
for all
uncertainty estimates
\Delta\Xi_{0,T}\in\mathbf{\Delta}
, in some compact set
\mathbf{\Delta}
, with a prescribed degree of confidence level
1-\delta
.

For simplicity, assume
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\succ 0
is known.
First, we re-write the MLE problem (
30
) in terms of the vectorized parameters to be estimated
\xi\triangleq\mathrm{vec}(\Xi_{0,T})=[\xi_{0}^{\intercal},\cdots,\xi_{T-1}^{\intercal}]^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{nT}
as the optimization problem below,
where for notational simplicity, we have dropped the superscript
(d)
from
\xi^{(d)}
:

\displaystyle\min_{\xi}~

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{ML}}(\xi\ |\ \mathbb{D})=\frac{1}{2}\xi^{\intercal}(I_{T}\otimes\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{-1})\xi

(42a)

\displaystyle C(\xi)\triangleq(\Gamma\otimes I_{n})\xi-\lambda=0,

(42b)

where
\Gamma\triangleq I_{T}-\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}\mathcal{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
, and
\lambda\triangleq\mathrm{vec}(X_{1,T}\Gamma)
.
It can be shown
[
48
]
that, as the number of samples grows, the unconstrained ML noise estimate
\hat{\xi}
is asymptotically efficient and converges to a normal distribution as
\sqrt{T}\,\Delta\xi\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{I}^{-1})
, where
\Delta\xi\triangleq\xi-\hat{\xi}
and where

\mathcal{I}=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\xi^{2}}\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{ML}}(\xi\ |\ \mathbb{D})\right]
is the
Fisher Information Matrix
(FIM), which is given by
\mathcal{I}=I_{T}\otimes\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{-1}
in the unconstrained case.

In our setting, the constraint Jacobian
J(\xi)\triangleq\partial C(\xi)/\partial\xi=\Gamma\otimes I_{n}
is generally
rank-deficient
because
\Gamma
is an orthogonal projector.
The constraint manifold is nevertheless smooth, and asymptotic efficiency holds on the tangent space if we enforce only an
independent
set of equality constraints.
Concretely,
let
V_{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times p}
have orthonormal columns spanning
\operatorname{range}(\Gamma)
(so that
p=\operatorname{rank}(\Gamma)=T-(n+m)
), and define the full row-rank Jacobian

J_{r}\triangleq(V_{\perp}^{\intercal}\otimes I_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{(pn)\times(nT)},

which satisfies
\operatorname{range}(J_{r})=\operatorname{range}(J)

and encodes the same constraint manifold.
Under standard regularity conditions, and with
J_{r}
used as the effective constraint Jacobian,
the constrained MLE is
\sqrt{T}
-consistent, asymptotically normal, and asymptotically efficient on the tangent space of the constraints
[
1
,
31
]
.

It follows from
[
63
]
that the asymptotic covariance of the constrained MLE error is the projection of
\mathcal{I}^{-1}
onto the tangent space.

Robust DD-DS

Equipped with the machinery to efficiently estimate and bound the uncertainty due to the noise, we are now in a position to tackle the uncertain convex programs in (

The analysis in this section will lead to robust versions of the DD-MS and DD-CS problems.
To this end, notice that the DD-MS problem essentially becomes a robust control problem with unstructured model uncertainty, albeit only with high probability guarantees.
Hence, in Section

We note that this procedure is similar to the

First, we address the robust DD-MS control design problem.

6.1

Solving the DD-MS program (

6.3
Solution of the Robust DD-CS Problem

In this section, we reformulate the uncertain CS program (

) so that it is amenable to a tractable convex matrix feasibility problem.
The original constraints in (
24c
), when reformulated as the LMI constraints
G_{k}^{\Sigma}\succeq 0
in (
25b
), may be robustly satisfied with high-probability, using the decomposition
\Xi_{0,T}=\hat{\Xi}_{0,T}+\Delta\Xi_{0,T}
along with the established estimation error bounds, as the semi-infinite uncertain LMIs

~\hat{G}_{k}^{\Sigma}+\Delta G_{k}^{\Sigma}(\Delta\Xi_{0,T})\succeq 0,\quad\forall\|\Delta\Xi_{0,T}\|\leq\rho(\delta),

(63)

where,

\hat{G}_{k}^{\Sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{k+1}-\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}&(X_{1,T}-\hat{\Xi}_{0,T})S_{k}\\
S_{k}^{\intercal}(X_{1,T}-\hat{\Xi}_{0,T})^{\intercal}&\Sigma_{k}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,

(64)

is the nominal covariance LMI, and

\Delta G_{k}^{\Sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{n}&-\Delta\Xi_{0,T}S_{k}\\
-S_{k}^{\intercal}\Delta\Xi_{0,T}^{\intercal}&0_{n}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0

(65)

is the perturbation to the covariance LMI.
Next, we rewrite the perturbation matrix (
65
) as

\Delta G_{k}^{\Sigma}=\Theta^{\intercal}(S_{k})\Delta\Xi_{0,T}^{\intercal}\Pi+\Pi^{\intercal}\Delta\Xi_{0,T}\Theta(S_{k}),

(66)

where
\Theta^{\intercal}(S_{k})\triangleq[0_{n,T};-S_{k}^{\intercal}]
and
\Pi^{\intercal}=[I_{n};0_{n}]
.
Finally, using
[
7
]
, we may equivalently
represent the uncertain LMI (
63
) as the following standard LMI

~\begin{bmatrix}\lambda I_{T}&\rho(\delta)\,\Theta(S_{k})\\
\rho(\delta)\,\Theta^{\intercal}(S_{k})&\hat{G}_{k}^{\Sigma}(\Sigma_{k},\Sigma_{k+1},S_{k})-\lambda\Pi^{\intercal}\Pi\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,

(67)

in terms of the decision variables
S_{k},\lambda,\Sigma_{k}
and
\Sigma_{k+1}
.

7
Parametric Uncertainty DD-CS

Rather than deriving bounds on
\Delta\Xi_{0,T}
and designing for the worst-case disturbance (Sections
5
and
6
), in this section we treat the parametric disturbances as stochastic ones obeying a known probability law
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}^{(d)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
.
For simplicity, in this section, we assume that
D
is known.
We pose a data-driven density steering problem with probabilistic parametric uncertainty (PU-DD-DS).
In other words, we treat the noise vectors
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}^{(d)}
as random variables with known probability distributions rather than attempting to estimate their specific realization.
This approach has the advantage of allowing us
to design a controller that is inherently robust to the entire distribution of possible noise realizations, rather than being robustly optimized for a single estimated noise instance.

7.1
Solution of the PU-DD-DS Problem

The dynamics of the actual system may be written as

\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}=(\hat{A}+\Delta A(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{0,T}))\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+(\hat{B}+\Delta B(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{0,T}))\boldsymbol{u}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k},

(68)

where
[\hat{B}\ \hat{A}]=X_{1,T}\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}
is the CE estimate from (
34

is the error from the CE design.

Partition the matrix
\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}=[\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2}]\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times(m+n)}
in (

) into
\mathcal{S}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times m}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times n}

\mathcal{S}_{1}

\mathcal{S}_{2}

\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k+1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k+1}^{\intercal}

\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}\triangleq\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\mu_{k}

~\Sigma_{{k+1}}=\hat{\Sigma}_{x_{k+1}}+\Delta\Sigma_{x_{k+1}}^{(1)}+\Delta\Sigma_{x_{k+1}}^{(2)},

where,

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\hat{\Sigma}_{x_{k+1}}

\displaystyle=\hat{A}\Sigma_{k}\hat{A}^{\intercal}+\hat{A}\Sigma_{x_{k},u_{k}}\hat{B}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt+\hat{B}\Sigma_{x_{k},u_{k}}^{\intercal}\hat{A}^{\intercal}+\hat{B}\Sigma_{u_{k}}\hat{B}^{\intercal}+\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}},

\displaystyle\Delta\Sigma_{x_{k+1}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\Big(\sigma_{i}^{\intercal}\Sigma_{k}\sigma_{i}+\sigma_{i}^{\intercal}\Sigma_{x_{k},u_{k}}\tau_{i}

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt+\tau_{i}^{\intercal}\Sigma_{x_{k},u_{k}}^{\intercal}\sigma_{i}+\tau_{i}^{\intercal}\Sigma_{u_{k}}\tau_{i}\Big)\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}},

(71b)

~\Sigma_{u_{k}}=K_{k}\Sigma_{k}K_{k}^{\intercal},\quad\Sigma_{x_{k},u_{k}}=\Sigma_{k}K_{k}^{\intercal}.

For the uncertain mean dynamics, we have the following expression

\mu_{k+1}=(\hat{A}+\Delta A(\Xi_{0,T}))\mu_{k}+(\hat{B}+\Delta B(\Xi_{0,T}))v_{k}.

9
Discussion and Open Problems

The numerical study in Section
8
highlights the principal strengths and trade-offs of the proposed DUST framework.
On the strengths side, the uncertainty quantification used in the robust formulations is both principled and tight: the empirical distribution of the joint noise estimation error norm
\|\Delta\Xi_{0,T}\|
is essentially invariant to the horizon
T
(once persistence of excitation fixes the subspace dimension), while the subspace-based bound from (
46
)
matches the empirical quantiles across confidence levels, in sharp contrast to the earlier, overly loose construction
[
55
]
whose conservatism worsens with
T
.
This is evidenced by the invariance of
\|\Delta\Xi_{0,T}\|
across
T
(Fig.
2
) and in Table
1
, where the “Tight” subspace decomposition nearly coincides with the true quantile, whereas the original “Loose” bound deteriorates markedly.
These uncertainty sets enable tractable robust mean and covariance steering optimization programs (e.g., SDPs) that are solved to global optimality with standard solvers delivering improved performance and constraint satisfaction compared with the certainty-equivalent and model-based baselines used in this study.

The main limitations of the proposed approach are two-fold: (i) modeling assumptions under which the proposed guarantees are defined, namely, linear time-invariant dynamics and Gaussian disturbances, and (ii) feasibility of the reformulated steering problems, namely, there is no guarantee that for the given uncertainty error bound
\rho
(in robust CS), or similarly for the computed model-mismatch error bound
\alpha
(in robust MS), the convex programs will have a solution.

We remark that, using the statistical properties of the noise for prediction and estimation is not unique to this work.
Indeed, in the context of DeePC, recent methods such as the signal matrix model
[
70
,
71
]
and Wasserstein estimation
[
40
]
have been proposed to find a relationship between past inputs/outputs and future outputs.
Additionally,
[
73
]
extended these estimators to generate confidence sets, similar to our work, which can be used in the control design.
Our work differs from these methods in the sense that we estimate the
noise realizations
(not just the statistics of the noise process) arising from the noisy data, from which we generate confidence sets for robust control.

The framework developed in this work assumes normally distributed additive noise as well as Gaussian boundary conditions for the state distribution.
As such, we envision the theory to serve as a baseline for future work incorporating increasing layers of complexity to the problem.
One natural extension is to incorporate probabilistic (or chance) constraints on the state and the input along the planning horizon, which typically represent physical limitations or safety considerations, such as thrust saturation or no-fly zones (NFZs).
For example, assuming that the allowable state constraint space is modeled through the polytope
\mathcal{X}\triangleq\{x:\max_{j\in[\![J]\!]}\alpha_{j}^{\intercal}x+\beta_{j}\leq 0\}
, these chance constraints, under Gaussian noise and linear dynamics, can be sufficiently enforced through the following inequalities
[
41
]

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}^{\intercal}\mu_{k}+\Phi^{-1}(1-\delta_{j,k})\sqrt{\alpha_{j}^{\intercal}\Sigma_{k}\alpha_{j}}+\beta_{j}\leq 0,

\displaystyle\hskip 112.38829pt\forall j\in[\![J]\!],\ \forall k\in[\![N]\!],

(78)

where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the standard normal CDF and
\delta_{j,k}>0
are the individual risk allocations.
Notice from (
78
) that the underlying unknown model
\{A,B,D\}
does not show up in the reformulated constraints.
Furthermore, since
\{\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k}\}
are decision variables in our formulation, enforcing these chance constraints will not require much modification compared to that of the model-based design.

It is often the case that we do not know the exact distributional form of the additive noise.
Most works (including the current one) assume, for simplicity, normally distributed disturbances.
This too may be a limiting assumption depending on the context.
There has been a surge in recent works on distributionally robust (DR) motion planning and trajectory optimization that is fueled by this very point
[
3
,
64
,
17
]
.
To this end, another natural extension of the DD-DS framework is to the class of problems where there is distributional uncertainty in the disturbances belonging, for example, to a Wasserstein ambiguity set
\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})
of radius
\varepsilon
centered around the nominal distribution
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
that may either be chosen as a Gaussian or is empirically estimated from data.
In this regard, one would not only steer the mean and covariance of the center distribution of the state (as we have done here), but, additionally, steer the radius of the ambiguity set through feedback control
[
54
]
.
The synthesis of DR optimization techniques with direct DD control methods stemming from notions of PE data is a fruitful avenue for future work.

On a more conceptual note, while we parameterize the feedback gains in (
19
) in terms of the collected data using the new decision variables
G_{k}
, this required the intermediate step of estimating the noise realization
\hat{\Xi}_{0,T}
(e.g., via MLE) which was essential to generate an uncertainty set
\{\|\Delta\Xi_{0,T}\|\leq\rho\}
for terminal constraint satisfaction with high probability.
We leave it as an open problem to design feedback controllers that satisfy the terminal constraints, which use
only
noisy input/state data without any intermediate noise estimation.

The PU-DD-DS design is attractive because it does not rely on the necessity for any type of noise estimation or error bounds, and directly uses the known statistics of
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}^{(d)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
for control design.
In this Bayesian viewpoint the parameters are treated as random variables rather than known, fixed quantities
[
33
]
.
However, the resulting mean trajectories are not robust to the noisy data, as we assumed a CE design.
Hence, a possible extension, as mentioned previously, is to robustify this parametric uncertainty design.

The robust fundamental lemma, outlined in Section
5.1
, is a great theoretical tool to bound estimation errors from CE indirect designs based on generalized notions of persistence of excitation.
However, it is not very practical because the parameters needed for these upper bounds are functions of the underlying (unknown) model.
An interesting question is how to formulate tractable upper bounds based on the RFL (for example, using RMT) for later use in robust DD-DS.

Willems’ fundamental lemma has inspired much of the work on direct data-driven control, including the current work.
However, the original WFL as stated, is only valid for deterministic dynamics.
The authors in
[
51
]
state a stochastic FL, however, this is limited to the context of polynomial chaos expansions of random variables.
We leave it for future investigation to derive a more general moment-based FL characterizing the space of the state mean and covariance trajectories of a stochastic LTI system under additive Gaussian noise.

10
Conclusion

We have presented a novel framework for data-driven stochastic optimal control for unknown linear systems with distributional boundary conditions, referred to as data-driven density steering (DD-DS).
The proposed framework provides a comprehensive approach to the design of optimal controllers that steer the state distribution of an uncertain linear system to a desired terminal Gaussian distribution, using only input-state data collected from the actual system.
By parameterizing the feedback gains directly in terms of the collected data, we have reformulated the data-driven distribution steering problem as an uncertain convex problem in terms of the unknown noise realizations.
Using techniques from behavioral systems theory and statistical learning, we were able to develop tight, tractable uncertainty sets for the
errors of these
estimated noise realizations, which were subsequently used to formulate and solve robust data-driven extensions for the mean steering (DD-MS) and covariance steering (DD-CS) of the state, which guarantee high-probability constraint satisfaction under bounded estimation errors.
Additionally, an alternative parametric uncertainty formulation (PU-DD-DS) was developed that treats model uncertainties probabilistically rather than deterministically.
Extensive numerical studies demonstrated the efficacy of the proposed methods compared to certainty-equivalence and model-based approaches.

\Delta\xi\in\mathrm{range}(\Sigma_{\Delta})
, where
\lambda_{\min}^{+}
denotes the minimum
non-zero
eigenvalue.
Hence,
\Delta\xi^{\intercal}\Sigma_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\Delta\xi\leq Q_{\boldsymbol{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}^{2}_{nT}}(1-\delta)
implies that
\lambda_{\min}^{+}(\Sigma_{\Delta}^{\dagger})\|\Delta\xi\|^{2}\leq Q_{\boldsymbol{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}^{2}_{nT}}(1-\delta)
.
Also, from the definition of the Frobenius norm, it follows that

\|\Delta\Xi_{0,T}\|_{\rm F}^{2}=\mathrm{tr}(\Delta\Xi_{0,T}^{\intercal}\Delta\Xi_{0,T})=\|\Delta\xi\|^{2}.

Thus, the uncertainty set (
91
) can be
over-approximated by the set

\mathbf{\Delta}_{\Xi}=\left\{\Delta\Xi_{0,T}:\|\Delta\Xi_{0,T}\|_{\rm F}\leq\sqrt{\frac{Q_{\boldsymbol{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}^{2}_{nT}}(1-\delta)}{\lambda_{\min}^{+}(\Sigma_{\Delta}^{\dagger})}}\right\},

in the sense that

\Delta\xi\in\mathbf{\Delta}_{\xi}
implies
\Delta\Xi_{0,T}\in\mathbf{\Delta}_{\Xi}
.
Since
\mathbf{\Delta}_{\xi}
is a
(1-\delta)
confidence set for the distribution
\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}
, it then follows that

\mathbb{P}(\Delta\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{0,T}\in\mathbf{\Delta}_{\Xi})\geq\mathbb{P}(\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{\Delta}_{\xi})\geq 1-\delta,

and hence
\mathbf{\Delta}_{\Xi}
is a
(1-\delta)
confidence set for the distribution of
\Delta\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{0,T}
.
Letting
\rho^{2}=Q_{\boldsymbol{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}^{2}_{nT}}(1-\delta)/\lambda_{\min}^{+}(\Sigma_{\Delta}^{\dagger})
, and noting that
\|\Delta\Xi_{0,T}\|\leq\|\Delta\Xi_{0,T}\|_{\rm F}
, we achieve the desired result.

\blacksquare

Appendix E
Proof of Corollary
16

First, we begin with a result on the minimum non-zero eigenvalue of singular matrices.

Lemma 28
.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{q}
be a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix of rank
0<r\leq q
.
For
\epsilon>0
, let
\Sigma^{\epsilon}\triangleq\Sigma+\epsilon I_{q}
.
Then,

\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\lambda_{\min}\big((\Sigma^{\epsilon})^{-1}\big)=\lambda_{\max}^{-1}(\Sigma)=\lambda_{\min}^{+}(\Sigma^{\dagger}).

(92)

PROOF.

Consider the spectral decomposition

~\Sigma=U\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{r}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}U^{\intercal},

(93)

where
\Lambda_{r}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})
, with
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{r}>0
in decreasing order.
From the decomposition (
93
), it follows that
\Sigma^{\epsilon}=U\Lambda_{r}^{\epsilon}U^{\intercal}
, where

\Lambda_{r}^{\epsilon}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,\ldots,\lambda_{r}+\epsilon,\underbrace{\epsilon,\ldots,\epsilon}_{q-r}).

Hence,

(\Sigma^{\epsilon})^{-1}=U\mathrm{diag}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon},\ldots,\frac{1}{\lambda_{r}+\epsilon},\underbrace{\frac{1}{\epsilon},\ldots,\frac{1}{\epsilon}}_{q-r}\right)U^{\intercal}.

Therefore,

~\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\lambda_{\min}\big((\Sigma^{\epsilon})^{-1}\big)=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma)}.

(94)

From (
93
) and the definition of the pseudoinverse, we have that

\Sigma^{\dagger}=U\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{r}^{-1}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}U^{\intercal}.

Hence, the non-zero eigenvalues of
\Sigma^{\dagger}
are precisely
\{1/\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
.
Therefore, the minimum (non-zero) eigenvalue of the pseudoinverse coincides with (
94
), which concludes the proof of the lemma.

\blacksquare

We can now proceed with the proof of Corollary
16
.
From Lemma
14
, the covariance of the noise estimation error from the MLE scheme is
\Sigma_{\Delta}=\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}\mathcal{S}\otimes\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
.
Let
\Sigma_{\Delta}^{\epsilon}\triangleq(\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}\mathcal{S}+\epsilon I_{T})\otimes\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, for some
\epsilon>0
.
From the properties of the Kronecker product
[
74
]
,
it follows that the eigenvalues of the matrix
(\Sigma_{\Delta}^{\epsilon})^{-1}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{spec}(\Sigma_{\Delta}^{\epsilon})^{-1}=\bigg\{\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}\mu_{j}},\lambda_{i}\in\mathrm{spec}(\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\mu_{j}\in\mathrm{spec}(S^{\dagger}S+\epsilon I_{T})\bigg\}.

It then follows that

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{\Delta}^{\epsilon})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\mu_{\max}(S^{\dagger}S+\epsilon I_{T})}

\displaystyle=\|\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{1/2}\|^{-2}(1+\epsilon)^{-1},

where we have used the fact that
\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\sigma_{\max}(\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\|\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{1/2}\|^{2}
, and that
\mathrm{spec}(\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}\mathcal{S}+\epsilon)=\{\epsilon,1+\epsilon\}
, since
\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}\mathcal{S}
is a projection matrix.
Taking the limit as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, we get

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{\Delta}^{\epsilon})^{-1}=\|\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{1/2}\|^{-2},

and the result follows immediately from Lemma
28
.

\blacksquare

Appendix F
Finite-Sample Uncertainty Set Construction

Using the MLE solution (
32a
), the estimation error becomes

~\Delta\Xi_{0,T}\triangleq\Xi_{0,T}-\hat{\Xi}_{0,T}=\Xi_{0,T}\mathcal{P},\quad\mathcal{P}\triangleq\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}\mathcal{S},

(95)

where
\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}\succeq 0
is the orthogonal projector onto the row-space of
\mathcal{S}
.
The matrix
\mathcal{P}
is symmetric, idempotent, and since the input/state data is persistently exciting (PE) it follows that
r=\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{P})=n+m
.
Since
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{0,T}=[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{T-1}]
, where each individual disturbance satisfies
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
, it follows that

\mathrm{vec}(\Delta\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{0,T})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{P}\otimes\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

(96)

which follows from
\mathrm{vec}(\Delta\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{0,T})=\mathrm{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{0,T}\mathcal{P})=(\mathcal{P}^{\intercal}\otimes I)\mathrm{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{0,T})
and
\mathrm{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{0,T})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{T}\otimes\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
.
Since
\mathcal{P}
is symmetric and idempotent, the covariance of
\mathrm{vec}(\Delta\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{0,T})

simplifies to
\mathcal{P}\otimes\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
.
This is exactly the covariance
obtained via the constrained-MLE Fisher information calculation in Lemma
14
.

To derive the non-asymptotic distribution for the norm of the estimation error,
note first that
we can equivalently express the disturbance matrix as
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{0,T}=\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{G}
, where
\boldsymbol{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}
is a standard Gaussian random
matrix
(each entry
\boldsymbol{G}_{ij}
is a standard normal random
variable
).
By decomposing the projection matrix as
\mathcal{P}=V_{r}V_{r}^{\intercal}
, where
V_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times r}
defines an orthonormal basis on
\mathbb{R}^{r}
, and using (
95
), yields the equivalent expression of the estimation error as

~\Delta\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{0,T}=\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{G}V_{r}V_{r}^{\intercal}.

(97)

Using (
97
) and the unitary invariance of the Frobenius norm, it follows that

~\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{0,T}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\|\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{G}_{r}\|_{\mathrm{F}},\quad\boldsymbol{G}_{r}\triangleq\boldsymbol{G}V_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}.

(98)

The above identity is finite-sample and depends
only
on
r
(the relevant subspace dimension) and
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
.
Importantly, it does not involve the data collection horizon
T
.

To translate the normed estimation error distribution in (
98
) to a high-probability uncertainty set, first notice that

\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{0,T}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{G}_{r}^{\intercal}\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\boldsymbol{G}_{r})\leq\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\|\boldsymbol{G}_{r}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

(99)

Lemma 29
.

Let
\boldsymbol{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}
have i.i.d. entries
\boldsymbol{G}_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
Let
V_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times r}
have orthonormal columns, i.e.,
V_{r}^{\intercal}V_{r}=I_{r}
.
Then, all entries of
\boldsymbol{G}_{r}\triangleq\boldsymbol{G}V_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

PROOF.

Write the
i
th row of
\boldsymbol{G}
as the vector
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
.
Because
\boldsymbol{G}
has i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
entries, the rows
\{\boldsymbol{g}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are independent and
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{T})
for each
i
.
Define
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}\triangleq V_{r}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{g}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
.
The mean and covariance of
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}]

\displaystyle=V_{r}^{\intercal}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{g}_{i}]=0,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}[\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}]

\displaystyle=V_{r}^{\intercal}\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{g}_{i})V_{r}=V_{r}^{\intercal}I_{T}V_{r}=I_{r}.

Hence,
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{r})
for each
i=1,\ldots,n
.
It follows that the entries of the matrix
\boldsymbol{G}_{r}=[\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}^{\intercal};\ldots;\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}^{\intercal}]
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

\blacksquare

Since
\boldsymbol{G}_{r}
is a standard Gaussian random matrix from Lemma
29
, it follows that
\|\boldsymbol{G}_{r}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
is the sum of
nr
independent

\boldsymbol{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}_{1}^{2}
random variables, hence
\|\boldsymbol{G}_{r}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\boldsymbol{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}_{nr}^{2}
.

For any random variables
\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}
such that
\boldsymbol{X}\leq\boldsymbol{Y}
a.s., their respective CDFs satisfy
F_{\boldsymbol{X}}(t)\geq F_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(t)
for all
t

[
61
]
.
Hence, for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the respective
\alpha
-quantiles obey
Q_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\alpha)\leq Q_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\alpha)
.
Applying this fact to the present setting with
\boldsymbol{X}=\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{0,T}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}=\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\boldsymbol{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}_{nr}^{2}
, we obtain

Q_{\boldsymbol{X}}(1-\delta)\leq\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})Q_{\boldsymbol{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}^{2}_{nr}}(1-\delta)\triangleq t^{\star}.

(100)

Finally, based on the definition of the quantile function, it follows that
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{Y}\leq t^{\star})=1-\delta
, and, from the fact that
\boldsymbol{X}\leq\boldsymbol{Y}
a.s., we have the event inclusion
\{\boldsymbol{Y}\leq t^{\star}\}\subseteq\{\boldsymbol{X}\leq t^{\star}\}
.
Putting these two facts together, it follows readily that

\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\leq t^{\star})\geq\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{Y}\leq t^{\star})=1-\delta,

and hence,

\mathbb{P}\big(\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{0,T}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})Q_{\boldsymbol{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}_{nr}^{2}}(1-\delta)\big)\geq 1-\delta.
