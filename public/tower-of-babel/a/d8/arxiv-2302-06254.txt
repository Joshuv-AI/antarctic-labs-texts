Localization measures of parity adapted U( D D )-spin coherent states applied to the phase space analysis of the D D -level Lipkin-Meshkov-Glick model
Alberto Mayorgas, Julio Guerrero, Manuel Calixto

Abstract
Abstract We study phase-space properties of critical, parity symmetric, N N -quDit systems undergoing a quantum phase transition (QPT) in the thermodynamic N → ∞ N\to\infty limit. The D = 3 D=3 level (qutrit) Lipkin-Meshkov-Glick (LMG) model is eventually examined as a particular example. For this purpose, we consider U ( D ) (D) -spin coherent states (DSCS), generalizing the standard D = 2 D=2 atomic coherent states, to define the coherent state representation Q ψ Q_{\psi} (Husimi function) of a symmetric N N -quDit state | ψ ⟩ |\psi\rangle in the phase space ℂ ​ P D − 1 \mathbb{C}P^{D-1} (complex projective manifold). DSCS are good variational aproximations to the ground state of a N N -quDit system, specially in the N → ∞ N\to\infty limit, where the discrete parity symmetry ℤ 2 D − 1 \mathbb{Z}_{2}^{D-1} is spontaneously broken. For finite N N , parity can be restored by projecting DSCS onto 2 D − 1 2^{D-1} different parity invariant subspaces, which define generalized “Schrödinger cat states” reproducing quite faithfully low-lying Hamiltonian eigenstates obtained by numerical diagonalization. Precursors of the QPT are then visualized for finite N N by plotting the Husimi function of these parity projected DSCS in phase space, together with their Husimi moments and Wehrl entropy, in the neighborhood of the critical points. These are good localization measures and markers of the QPT.



I
Introduction

Information theoretic and statistical measures together with phase space methods have proved to be useful in the description and characterization of quantum phase transitions (QPTs). For example, in the traditional Anderson metal-insulator transition
Anderson 1958
;
Aulbach et al. 2004
;
Evers and Mirlin 2008
, where Hamiltonian eigenfunctions underlie strong fluctuations.
Phase space methods are a fundamental tool in quantum optics
Schleich 2001
, providing connections between quantum mechanics (in the so-called Wigner/Weyl/Moyal scheme
E. and Schroeck 1996
) and classical statistical mechanics. This connection is often established through (quasi-classical,
minimum uncertainty) coherent states (CSs). The best known CSs are the canonical (harmonic oscillator) CSs introduced long time ago by Schrödinger
Schrödinger 1926
and later used by Glauber to study the radiation field
Glauber 1963
. Canonical CSs are linked to the Heisenberg-Weyl group (with the typical Lie algebra canonical commutation relations
[q,p]=\mathrm{i}\hbar
) and can be seen as a group action/displacement on the vacuum. Replacing the Heisenberg-Weyl group by the rotation group SU(2) (with angular momentum commutation relations
[J_{x},J_{y}]=\mathrm{i}\hbar J_{z}
and cyclic permutations), we get the so called spin-
j
, atomic or Bloch CSs
Zhang et al. 1990
;
Arecchi et al. 1972
. From this perspective,
generalizations to arbitrary (finite-dimensional) Lie groups
G
provide further families of CSs (we address the reader to the standard reference
Perelomov 1986
). In particular, this article is involved with the generalization from
\mathrm{U}(2)
to
\mathrm{U}(D)
, which is in the heart of the generalization from qubits (physically represented by two-level/component atom/particle quantum systems) to quDits (
D
-level quantum systems).

Canonical CSs provide complex analytic (Bargmann, phase space) representations of quantum states and operators in quantum mechanics
Vourdas 2006
. Among all phase-space quasi-probability distribution functions (playing a role similar to genuine probability distributions of statistical mechanics), the more popular are Wigner
W
, Husimi
Q
and Glauber-Sudarshan
P
(also called Berezin’s covariant and contravariant symbols, respectively) functions, usually associated with the symmetric, antinormal and normal ordering of position and momentum operators, respectively
Leonhardt 1997
;
Schleich 2001
;
Curtright et al. 2014
. Although Wigner function is perhaps more popular, Husimi function can be more easily extended to general phase spaces associated to coset spaces
X=G/H
of a symmetry Lie group
G
for an isotropy subgroup
H\subset G
. This will be our case, with
G=\mathrm{U}(D)
the unitary group of degree
D
, and phase space
X=U(D)/U(D-1)=\mathbb{C}P^{D-1}
the complex projective space generalizing the Bloch sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}=\mathbb{C}P^{1}
for
D=2
. This case is linked to the totally symmetric (bosonic) representation of
\mathrm{U}(D)
, to which we are going to restrict ourselves here (see
Calixto et al. 2021a
for other phase spaces like the flag manifold
\mathrm{U}(D)/U(1)^{D}
linked to more general fermion mixtures and Young tableaux).

Given a CS system
\{|z\rangle,z\in X\}
, the Husimi function of a density matrix
\rho
is the phase space
X
valued function
Q_{\rho}(z)=\langle z|\rho|z\rangle
. In an attempt to build bridges between classical and quantum entropies, and even though
Q_{\rho}(z)
is only a semiclassical quasi-probability distribution function, a semiclassical Shannon-like entropy was defined by Wehrl
Wehrl 1979
as
\mathcal{S}_{W}(\rho)=-\int Q_{\rho}(z)\log Q_{\rho}(z)d\mu_{X}(z)
, with
d\mu_{X}(z)
a
G
-invariant measure on the phase space
X
. Wehrl’s entropy measures the area occupied by the quantum state
\rho
in phase space; actually, moments
M_{\nu}
of
Q_{\rho}
(and their associated Rényi-Wehrl entropies
Mintert and Życzkowski 2004
;
Gnutzmann and Zyczkowski 2001
;
Giovannetti et al. 2004
), like the so called inverse participation ratio
M_{2}
, also measure the localization of
\rho
in phase space and are easier to compute.

For a critical quantum system described by a Hamiltonian
H(\lambda)
depending on a control parameter
\lambda
, abrupt changes in the Wehrl entropy of the ground state (as a function of
\lambda
) usually provide good indicators of the existence of a quantum phase transition (QPT) around a critical point
\lambda_{c}
, even for a finite number
N
of particles. Moreover, Wehrl entropy can be also used to identify the order of a QPT
Castaños et al. 2015
, as an alternative definition to the standard Ehrenfest classification based on discontinuities of the derivatives of the ground state energy density with respect to
\lambda
in the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
. Husimi function and its Wehrl entropy have already given a good phase space description of interesting quantum critical systems like Bose-Einstein condensates
Pérez-Campos et al. 2010
, the Dicke model of superradiance for two-level
Romera et al. 2012
;
del Real et al. 2013
and three-level
Castaños et al. 2018
atoms, the U(3) vibron model of molecular benders
Calixto et al. 2012
, the U(4) bilayer quantum Hall system
Calixto and Peón-Nieto 2018
, the U(2) (two-level) ubiquitous Lipkin-Meshkov-Glick (LMG) model
Romera et al. 2014
;
Romera et al. 2017
;
Castaños et al. 2006
, etc. Here we want to extend the scope of applicability of these phase space methods to symmetric multi-quDit systems (like
D
-level atom models) described by a
\mathrm{U}(D)
invariant LMG model. In addition to the obvious technical complication,
\mathrm{U}(D)
provides some novelties and a much richer structure
that is not possible to grasp starting from
\mathrm{U}(2)
. In particular, the standard discrete parity symmetry group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\{0,1\}
, which is spontaneously broken in the thermodynamic limit for second order QPTs of
D=2
level systems, now becomes
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{D-1}
and provides more case studies of Schrödinger cat states than the standard even and odd ones of the literature
Dodonov et al. 1974a
;
Dodonov et al. 1974b
;
Dodonov et al. 1995
;
Gerry and Grobe 1997
, in the sense of quantum superpositions of weakly overlapping quasiclassical (coherent) states, the most symmetric one mimicking the structure of the ground state in the highly interacting quantum phase (see later in Section
V
and
López-Peña et al. 2015
for previous studies on Dicke models of three-level atoms interacting with one-mode radiation field).

The organization of the article is as follows. In Sec.
II
we introduce the
D
-level LMG model and particularize it for the cases
D=2
(qubits) and
D=3
(qutrits). A brief discussion about the Fock basis and the discrete parity symmetry
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{D-1}
is also included. In Sec.
III
we define U(
D
)-spin coherent states
|\bm{z}\rangle
(DSCSs for brevity) labelled by points
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}P^{D-1}
in phase space; we also compute the DSCS matrix elements
\langle\bm{z}|S_{ij}|\bm{z}^{\prime}\rangle
of
\mathrm{U}(D)
-spin operators
S_{ij},i,j=1,\dots,D
, and we project DSCSs
|\bm{z}\rangle
into the
2^{D-1}
invariant subspaces
\mathbbm{c}
of the parity symmetry group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{D-1}
, introducing the notion of “
\mathbbm{c}
-parity
\mathrm{U}(D)
Schrödinger cat states”
|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}
(called
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT states, for short). Then, in Sec.
IV
, the traditional Husimi function
Q_{\psi}(z)=|\langle z|\psi\rangle|^{2}
of a quantum state
|\psi\rangle
in the standard phase space
\mathbb{C}\ni z
(for canonical, harmonic oscillator or Heisenberg-Weyl coherent states) is extended to the phase space
\mathbb{C}P^{D-1}\ni\bm{z}
using DSCSs
|\bm{z}\rangle
and a convenient Harr integration measure, which allows to define
\nu
-moments of the Husimi function and the Wehrl entropy as usefull localization measures in phase space. These measures are computed in the case of DSCS and
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT states, including their thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
. The Appendices
B
and
C
show in more detail some of the long calculations of this section. In Sec.
V
we focus on the
D=3
level LMG Hamiltonian for symmetric qutrits and the minimization of its energy surface in the limit
N\to\infty
using DSCSs as variational states. The degeneration of the ground state in the thermodynamic limit and the QPTs make their apparition here, but are not discussed in depth until the next two sections. In Sec.
VI
, the variational ground state obtained in the previous section is projected on parity
\mathbbm{c}
subspaces and the corresponding
\mathbbm{c}
-DCATs are compared to the low-lying Hamiltonian eigenstates of the LMG model obtained by numerical diagonalization for finite
N
. This procedure (projection after energy minimization) provides a fairly good variational aproximation to the ground state in terms of the completely even,
\mathbbm{c}=\mathbb{0}
, DCAT state, but not so precise for first excited states in terms of DCAT states of other parities
\mathbbm{c}
, for which we try a proper overlap maximization (fidelity) procedure.
In Sec.
VII
, the Husimi function and the localization measures of the Sec.
IV
are employed to visualize how the variational and the numerical eigenstates split into Gaussian-like wave packets throughout the three different quantum phases of the
D=3
level LMG model. The Inverse Participation Ratio (Husimi second moment) and the Wehrl entropy are also used to quantify the overlap of these packets, and hence the localization/spread of the low-lying Hamiltonian eigenstates in phase space is compared to that of DSCS and
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT variational states. Finally, in Sec.
VIII
we present the main conclusions of this work.

II
D
-level LMG model Hamiltonian and parity symmetry

The original (
D=2
levels/modes) LMG schematic shell model appeared in nuclear physics
Lipkin et al. 1965a
;
Lipkin et al. 1965b
;
Ring and Schuch 1980

to describe the quantum phase transition from spherical to deformed shapes in nuclei. Since then, it is an ubiquitous model that appears in a multitude of physical contexts.
For example, the Hamiltonian of an anisotropic XY
Ising model, with
\mu=1,\dots,N
lattice sites, in an external transverse magnetic field
\varepsilon
with infinite-range constant
interactions

H_{XY}=\varepsilon\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}\sigma_{z}^{(\mu)}+\sum_{\mu<\nu}\lambda_{x}\sigma_{x}^{(\mu)}\sigma_{x}^{(\nu)}+\sum_{\mu<\nu}\lambda_{y}\sigma_{y}^{(\mu)}\sigma_{y}^{(\nu)}\>,

(1)

[
\sigma_{x,y,z}^{(\mu)}
denote the Pauli matrices at site
\mu
] adopts the form of the two-level LMG schematic shell model Hamiltonian
Lipkin et al. 1965a
;
Lipkin et al. 1965b

H_{2}=\varepsilon J_{z}+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}(J_{+}^{2}+J_{-}^{2})+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}(J_{+}J_{-}+J_{-}J_{+})\>

(2)

when written in terms of the SU
(2)
angular momentum collective operators

\vec{J}=(J_{x},J_{y},J_{z})=\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}(\sigma_{x}^{(\mu)},\sigma_{y}^{(\mu)},\sigma_{z}^{(\mu)}),

(3)

and
J_{\pm}=(J_{x}\pm\mathrm{i}J_{y})/2
, as usual. We could also think of a model describing
a system of
N
interacting two-level identical atoms (symmetric “qubits”).
Long-range constant interactions make this Hamiltonian translation invariant, that is, it is
symmetric under permutation of lattice sites
\mu\leftrightarrow\nu
(or permutation of atoms/qubits). Therefore,
the Hamiltonian does not couple different angular momentum sectors
j=N/2,N/2-1,\dots,1/2
or
0
(for odd or even
N
, respectively) and it is a common practice
to restrict oneself to the largest (fully symmetric) sector
j=N/2
to which the ground state of the system belongs. This restriction
reduces the size of the Hamiltonian matrix to be diagonalized from
2^{N}
to
N+1=2j+1
and assumes that
D=2
-level atoms/qubits are indistinguishable.
For this case, it is convenient to use a Jordan-Schwinger realization of angular momentum operators in terms of bilinear products of bosonic creation
a^{\dagger}_{i}
and annihilation
a_{j}
operators as

S_{ij}=a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{j},\;i,j=0,\dots,D-1,

(4)

where we are already extending to arbitrary
D
-level atom systems with
\mathrm{U}(D)
symmetry. For example, for
D=2
we recover

J_{+}=S_{10},J_{-}=S_{01}
,
J_{z}=\frac{1}{2}(S_{11}-S_{00})
and the conserved total number
N
of particles
C_{1}=S_{00}+S_{11}
[the linear Casimir operator of
\mathrm{U}(2)
].
\mathrm{U}(D)
-spin operators
S_{ij}
fulfill the commutation relations

\left[S_{{ij}},S_{{kl}}\right]=\delta_{{jk}}S_{{il}}-\delta_{{il}}S_{{kj}}.

(5)

The LMG Hamiltonian
H_{2}
in (
2
) for
D=2
level systems is generalized to arbitrary
D
levels as

H_{D}=\sum_{i=0}^{D-1}\varepsilon_{i}(S_{i+1,i+1}-S_{ii})+\sum_{i\not=j=0}^{D-1}(\lambda_{1}S_{ij}^{2}+\lambda_{2}S_{ij}S_{ji}),

(6)

where
\varepsilon_{i}
now denotes the energy gap between levels
i
and
i+1
.
The
\lambda_{1}
interaction term annihilates pairs of particles in one level and creates pairs in other level, whereas the
\lambda_{2}

term scatters one particle from
i\to j
while another is scattered back from
j\to i
. The total number of particles
N=\sum_{i=0}^{D-1}S_{ii}
(the linear Casimir operator of
\mathrm{U}(D)
) is conserved. For the sake of simplicity, we shall consider
\lambda_{2}=0

and
\varepsilon_{i}=\varepsilon
(same energy spacing between levels). Since we are interested in the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
, we shall also renormalize one-body interactions
\varepsilon\to\epsilon/N
by the total number
N
of particles, and two-body interactions
\lambda_{2}\to-\lambda/[N(N-1)]
by the total number
N(N-1)
of pairs, so that the final Hamiltonian density for us becomes

H=\frac{\epsilon}{N}(S_{D-1,D-1}-S_{00})-\frac{\lambda}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i\not=j=0}^{D-1}S_{ij}^{2}.

(7)

We shall measure energy in
\epsilon>0
units, and discuss the
energy spectrum and the phase diagram in terms of the control parameter
\lambda
(see later in Section
V
). There are already some studies in the literature of this Hamiltonian for
D=3
level atoms and its chaotic behavior (see e.g.

Meredith et al. 1988
;
Lopez-Arias and Manfredi 1989
;
Gnutzmann and Kuś 1999
;
Gnutzmann et al. 1999
;
Wang et al. 1998
;
Leboeuf and Saraceno 1999
;
Calixto et al. 2021a
).

We shall consider indistinguishable atoms, so that the Hilbert space dimension reduces from
D^{N}
to
\tbinom{N+D-1}{D-1}
, the dimension of the fully symmetric irreducible representation of
\mathrm{U}(D)
(which
coincides with the total number of compositions of
N
into
D
non-negative integers when order does not matter). This restriction considerably reduces the computational complexity for large number of particles
N
(see
Calixto et al. 2021a
for the role played by other mixed permutation symmetry sectors in the
thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
).
Therefore, the Hilbert space is spanned by the Bose-Einstein-Fock basis states (
|\vec{0}\rangle
denotes the Fock vacuum)

|\vec{n}\rangle=|n_{0},\dots,n_{D-1}\rangle=\frac{(a_{0}^{\dagger})^{n_{0}}\dots(a_{D-1}^{\dagger})^{n_{D-1}}}{\sqrt{n_{0}!\dots n_{D-1}!}}|\vec{0}\rangle,

(8)

where
n_{i}
denotes the occupancy number of level
i
(the eigenvalue of
S_{ii}
), with the restriction
n_{0}+\dots+n_{D-1}=N
(the total number of atoms/quDits). In the low-interaction regime
\lambda\ll 1
, the ground state of (
7
)
is a Bose-Einstein condensate
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}(a_{0}^{\dagger})^{N}|\vec{0}\rangle
of
N
atoms in the
i=0
level, which we shall take as a reference level from now on.

These Fock states are the natural generalization of angular momentum
j=N/2
Dicke states
|j,m\rangle
with angular momentum third component
m=-j,\dots,j
; more explicitly

|j,m\rangle=|n_{0}=j+m,n_{1}=j-m\rangle,

(9)

so that
m=(n_{0}-n_{1})/2
(the eigenvalue of
J_{z}=\frac{1}{2}(S_{11}-S_{00})
) represents the population imbalance between levels
i=0
and
i=1
. The expansion of a general symmetric
N
-quDit state
\psi
in the Fock basis will be written as

|\psi\rangle=\sum_{\|\vec{n}\|_{1}=N}\,c_{\vec{n}}|\vec{n}\rangle,

(10)

where the sum is restricted to
\|\vec{n}\|_{1}=n_{0}+\dots+n_{D-1}=N
.
Collective
\mathrm{U}(D)
-spin operators (
4
) matrix elements in the Fock basis are easily computed as

\displaystyle\langle\vec{m}|S_{ii}|\vec{n}\rangle=n_{i}\delta_{\vec{m},\vec{n}}\,,

(11)

\displaystyle\langle\vec{m}|S_{ij}|\vec{n}\rangle=\sqrt{(n_{i}+1)n_{j}}\delta_{m_{i},n_{i}+1}\delta_{m_{j},n_{j}-1}\prod_{k\not=i\not=j}\delta_{m_{k},n_{k}}\,.

At this point, we would like to highlight the existence of an interesting parity symmetry. Indeed,
this symmetry of the Hamiltonian has to do with the fact that the interaction only scatters pairs of particles, thus conserving
the parity
\Pi_{j}=\exp(\mathrm{i}\pi S_{jj})
, even (+) or odd (
-
), of the population
S_{jj}
in each level
j=0,\dots,D-1
.
Note that
\Pi_{j}|\vec{n}\rangle=(-1)^{n_{j}}|\vec{n}\rangle
, and therefore we have the constraint

\Pi_{0}\dots\Pi_{D-1}|\vec{n}\rangle=(-1)^{N}|\vec{n}\rangle
which allows to write for example
\Pi_{0}=(-1)^{N}\Pi_{1}\dots\Pi_{D-1}
. Hence, this discrete parity symmetry corresponds to the finite group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{D-1}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D-1}}{{\dots}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\{0,1\}
the usual parity group (the cyclic group of order 2). Consequently, energy eigenstates have well defined parity under
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{D-1}
.
We will see later in Sec.
V
that low-lying Hamiltonian eigenstates with different parities collapse in the thermodynamic
N\to\infty
limit, giving rise to a degenerate ground state as a consequence of a spontaneous breakdown of the parity symmetry
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{D-1}
.

Let us denote by the binary string
\mathbbm{b}=[b_{1},\dots,b_{D-1}]\in\{0,1\}^{D-1}
one of the
2^{D-1}
elements of the parity group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{D-1}
. There are
2^{D-1}
parity invariant subspaces labeled by the inequivalent group characters
\mathbbm{c}=[c_{1},\dots,c_{D-1}]\in\{0,1\}^{D-1}
of the Pontryagin dual group
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{D-1}}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{D-1}
.
The projectors onto these invariant subspaces of definite parity
\mathbbm{c}
are given by

\Pi_{\mathbbm{c}}=2^{1-D}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}\in\{0,1\}^{D-1}}(-1)^{\mathbbm{c}\cdot\mathbbm{b}}\Pi^{\mathbbm{b}}\,,

(12)

with
\mathbbm{c}\cdot\mathbbm{b}=c_{1}b_{1}+\dots+c_{D-1}b_{D-1}
and

\Pi^{\mathbbm{b}}\equiv\Pi_{1}^{b_{1}}\dots\Pi_{D-1}^{b_{D-1}}.

(13)

Note that

\sum_{\mathbbm{c}\in\{0,1\}^{D-1}}\Pi_{\mathbbm{c}}=I\,,

(14)

the identity
I
in the representation space. For example, for
D=2
we have just
\Pi_{\mathbb{0}}=\Pi_{\mathrm{even}}
and
\Pi_{\mathbbm{1}}=\Pi_{\mathrm{odd}}
the standard projectors on even and odd parities, with

I=\Pi_{\mathrm{even}}+\Pi_{\mathrm{odd}}
. For general
D
, we sometimes shall single out the totally even
\mathbb{0}=[0,\dots,0]
and totally odd
\mathbbm{1}=[1,\dots,1]
parity representations.

III
\mathrm{U}(D)
-spin coherent states and adaptation to parity

III.1
\mathrm{U}(D)
-spin coherent states

\mathrm{U}(D)
-spin coherent states (DSCSs for brevity) are defined as a generalization of standard binominal (two-mode)
\mathrm{U}(2)
-spin coherent states to the multinomial (
D
-mode) case as

|\bm{z}\rangle^{(N)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\left(\frac{a_{0}^{\dagger}+z_{1}a_{1}^{\dagger}+\cdots+z_{D-1}a_{D-1}^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{1+|z_{1}|^{2}+\cdots+|z_{D-1}|^{2}}}\right)^{N}|\vec{0}\rangle,

(15)

so that they are labeled by
D-1
complex numbers
z_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
arranged in the column vector
\bm{z}=(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{D-1})^{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{D-1}
. Properly speaking,
this really corresponds to a certain patch of the complex projective manifold
\mathbb{C}P^{D-1}
,
which results when choosing
i=0
as a reference level; see e.g.
Calixto et al. 2021b
for more information about other choices and patches. DSCSs are also labeled by the total number of particles
N
[also labelling a specific symmetric representation of U(
D
)], which will be omitted as superscript in eq.(
15
) to simplify the notation, i.e.
|\bm{z}\rangle\equiv|\bm{z}\rangle^{(N)}
.

DSCSs
|\bm{z}\rangle
have the form of a Bose-Einstein condensate of
D
modes, generalizing the spin
\mathrm{U}(2)
(binomial) coherent states of two modes introduced by
Radcliffe 1971
and
Arecchi et al. 1972
long time ago.
If we take
i=0
as a reference energy level, then the state
|\bm{z}=\bm{0}\rangle
would be the ground state, whereas general
|\bm{z}\rangle
could be seen as coherent excitations. The coefficients
c_{\vec{n}}(\bm{z})
of the expansion (
10
)
of
|\psi\rangle=|\bm{z}\rangle
in the Fock basis are simply

c_{\vec{n}}(\bm{z})=\sqrt{\frac{N!}{\prod_{i=0}^{D-1}n_{i}!}}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{D-1}z_{i}^{n_{i}}}{(1+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{z})^{N/2}},

(16)

where
\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{z}=|z_{1}|^{2}+\dots+|z_{D-1}|^{2}
denotes the standard scalar product in
\mathbb{C}^{D-1}
.

In general, DSCSs are not orthogonal since the scalar product

\langle\bm{z}|\bm{z}^{\prime}\rangle=\frac{(1+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{z}^{\prime})^{N}}{(1+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{z})^{N/2}(1+\bm{z}^{\prime{\dagger}}\bm{z}^{\prime})^{N/2}}

(17)

is not necessarily zero. However, they are a overcomplete set of states closing a resolution of the identity

\displaystyle 1=

\displaystyle\,\int_{\mathbb{C}^{D-1}}|\bm{z}\rangle\langle\bm{z}|d\mu(\bm{z}),

(18)

\displaystyle d\mu(\bm{z})=

\displaystyle\,\frac{(D-1)!}{\pi^{D-1}}\binom{N+D-1}{N}\frac{d^{2}z_{1}\dots d^{2}z_{D-1}}{(1+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{z})^{D}}\,,

with
d^{2}z_{i}=d\Re(z_{i})d\Im(z_{i})
the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{C}
and
d\mu(\bm{z})
the Fubini-Study measure
Mintert and Życzkowski 2004
;
Bengtsson and Zyczkowski 2006
in the corresponding complex projective space.
This closure relation of DSCSs will be important when discussing phase space constructions.

III.2
Coherent state operator matrix elements

DSCS matrix elements of
D
-spin operators
S_{ij}
are easily computed from (
11
) and (
16
) and they are simply

\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|S_{ij}|\bm{z}\rangle=N\bar{z}^{\prime}_{i}z_{j}\frac{(1+\bm{z}^{\prime{\dagger}}\bm{z})^{N-1}}{(1+\bm{z}^{\prime{\dagger}}\bm{z}^{\prime})^{N/2}(1+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{z})^{N/2}},

(19)

where we understand
z_{0}=1=z^{\prime}_{0}
. From here, DSCS matrix elements of quadratic powers of
D
-spin operators can be concisely written as

\displaystyle\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|S_{ij}S_{kl}|\bm{z}\rangle=

\displaystyle\,\delta_{jk}\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|S_{il}|\bm{z}\rangle

(20)

\displaystyle+\frac{N-1}{N}\frac{\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|S_{ij}|\bm{z}\rangle\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|S_{kl}|\bm{z}\rangle}{\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}\rangle}\,.

Note that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\langle\bm{z}|S_{ij}S_{kl}|\bm{z}\rangle}{\langle\bm{z}|S_{ij}|\bm{z}\rangle\langle\bm{z}|S_{kl}|\bm{z}\rangle}=1,

(21)

which means that quantum fluctuations are negligible in the thermodynamic (classical) limit

. We shall use these ingredients when computing energy surfaces in Section

III.3
Parity adapted
\mathrm{U}(D)
-spin coherent states

DSCSs are sometimes called “quasi-classical” states. As we shall
see in Section

\Pi_{\mathbbm{c}}
in (

invariant subspaces of definite parity
\mathbbm{c}
. The effect of level

on DSCSs reduces to

\displaystyle\Pi_{i}|\bm{z}\rangle=|(z_{1},\dots,-z_{i},\dots,z_{D-1})\rangle.

(22)

That is,
\Pi_{i}
just changes the sign of
z_{i}
in
|\bm{z}\rangle
. Let us denote by

|\bm{z}\rangle^{\mathbbm{b}}=\Pi^{\mathbbm{b}}|\bm{z}\rangle=|((-1)^{b_{1}}z_{1},\dots,(-1)^{b_{D-1}}z_{D-1})\rangle\equiv|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle,

(23)

with

(24)

with
\Pi_{\mathbbm{c}}
in (
12
), the normalized projection of
|\bm{z}\rangle
onto the parity
\mathbbm{c}

(25)

We will write
|\bm{z}\rangle^{\mathbbm{b}}=|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle
indistinctly, with

with

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbbm{c}\in\{0,1\}^{D-1}}\chi_{\mathbbm{c}}(\mathbbm{b})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{D-1}\delta_{\mathbbm{c},\mathbb{0}}\,,

(27)

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathbbm{c}}(\mathbbm{b})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathbbm{b}}(\mathbbm{c})\,,

(28)

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathbbm{c}}(\mathbb{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1\,,

(29)

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathbbm{c}}(\mathbbm{b})\chi_{\mathbbm{c}^{\prime}}(\mathbbm{b})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathbbm{c}+\mathbbm{c}^{\prime}}(\mathbbm{b})\,.

(30)

The coefficients

\displaystyle c_{\vec{n}}(\bm{z})_{\mathbbm{c}}=

\displaystyle\,\frac{2^{1-D}}{\mathcal{N}(\bm{z})_{\mathbbm{c}}}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}\in\{0,1\}^{D-1}}(-1)^{(\mathbbm{c}+\mathbbm{n})\cdot\mathbbm{b}}c_{\vec{n}}(\bm{z})

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}(\bm{z})_{\mathbbm{c}}}c_{\vec{n}}(\bm{z})\delta_{\mathbbm{n},\mathbbm{c}}\,,

where

IV
Husimi function and localization measures in phase space

Coherent states provide phase space representations (also known as Bargmann/holomorphic representation) of wave functions in quantum physics.
Here we shall concentrate on the
Husimi or
Q
-function
Husimi 1940
of a pure state
|\psi\rangle
, defined as
Q_{\psi}(z)=|\langle z|\psi\rangle|^{2}
for a given overcomplete set of coherent states
|z\rangle
.
The most popular case
is in quantum optical systems, for which
|z\rangle
makes reference to a Glauber
Glauber 1963
or canonical (harmonic oscillator) coherent state associated to the Heisenberg-Weyl group.
This definition can be extended to other coherent state systems
like those associated to more general symmetry groups
Perelomov 1986
(see also
Sugita 2002
for some generalizations).
In our case, the Husimi function of the quantum state (
10
) is defined in terms of the DSCS coefficients (
16
) as

Q_{\psi}(\bm{z})=|\langle\bm{z}|\psi\rangle|^{2}=\left|\sum_{\|\vec{n}\|_{1}=N}\,\overline{c_{{\vec{n}}}(\bm{z})}c_{\vec{n}}(\psi)\right|^{2},

(40)

and it is normalized

\int_{\mathbb{C}^{D-1}}Q_{\psi}(\bm{z})d\mu(\bm{z})=1,

(41)

according to the measure (
18
). This definition is straightforwardly extended to non pure states defined by a density matrix
\rho
as
Q_{\rho}(\bm{z})=\langle\bm{z}|\rho|\bm{z}\rangle
(see e.g.,
Mintert and Życzkowski 2004
;
Sugita 2002
).

The Husimi function of a DSCS
|\bm{z}\rangle
is simply
Q_{|\bm{z}\rangle}(\bm{z}^{\prime})=|\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}\rangle|^{2}
, where the coherent state overlap
\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}\rangle
is given in (
17
). A more interesting example is the Husimi function of a
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT state
|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}
(
24
), which adopts the form

\displaystyle Q_{|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\,|\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}|^{2}=\frac{4^{1-D}}{\mathcal{N}(\bm{z})_{\mathbbm{c}}^{2}}\left|\sum_{\mathbbm{b}}(-1)^{\mathbbm{c}\cdot\mathbbm{b}}\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{1-D}\left|\sum_{\mathbbm{b}}(-1)^{\mathbbm{c}\cdot\mathbbm{b}}(1+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{z}^{\prime\mathbbm{b}})^{N}\right|^{2}}{(1+\bm{z}^{\prime{\dagger}}\bm{z}^{\prime})^{N}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}}(-1)^{\mathbbm{c}\cdot\mathbbm{b}}(1+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}})^{N}},

(42)

where we have used the coherent state overlap
\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle
in (
17
) and the normalization constant
\mathcal{N}(\bm{z})_{\mathbbm{c}}
in (
25
).
There are studies in the literature relating the distribution of zeros in phase space of the Husimi function of the ground state of a critical quantum system and the onset of quantum chaos
(see e.g.
Leboeuf and Voros 1990
;
Arranz et al. 2013
) and also studies on the critical behavior of excited states and its relation to order and chaos
Pérez-Fernández et al. 2011
. Note that, for
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT states
|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}
, the structure of zeros of their Husimi function (
IV
) depends on the parity
\mathbbm{c}
. Moreover, the case
D>2
is much richer and opens new possibilities since
Q
is multivariate and its zeros are not necessarily isolated points but form curves, surfaces, etc.

In order to visualize the QPT in the critical LMG model across the phase diagram, we shall use the
\nu
-th moments of the Husimi quasi-distribution function

M_{\nu}(\psi)=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{D-1}}[Q_{\psi}(\bm{z})]^{\nu}d\mu(\bm{z}),\quad\nu>1.

(43)

Among all Husimi moments, we shall single out
\nu=2
, which corresponds with the so called “Inverse Participation Ratio” (IPR)
Aulbach et al. 2004
;
Calixto and Peón-Nieto 2018
, that measures
the localization of (inverse area occupied by)
Q_{\psi}
in phase space and can be generalized to any probability density function
Wegner 1980
;
Kramer and MacKinnon 1993
.
The
\nu
-th moments of the Husimi function supposedly reach their maximum value when
\psi
itself is a coherent (highly localized) state, that is, when

\psi
only participates of a single coherent state. These conjecture has been proved in the cases of families of coherent states of compact semisimple Lie groups
Sugita 2002
, including the symmetric and antisymmetric representations of
SU(D)
as particular cases
Sugita 2003
. This affirmation is widely known as part of the Lieb conjecture, which is mentioned at the end of this subsection. For example, for the particular case of
|\psi\rangle=|\bm{z}=\bm{0}\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}(a_{0}^{\dagger})^{N}|\vec{0}\rangle
(a boson condensate of
N
atoms in their ground state
i=0
) and a generic number of levels
D
, a quite straightforward calculations gives

\displaystyle M_{\nu}(|\bm{0}\rangle)=

\displaystyle\,\frac{(N\nu)!}{N!}\frac{(N+D-1)!}{(N\nu+D-1)!}

(44)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{(N+D-1)_{D-1}}{(N\nu+D-1)_{D-1}}\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}1/\nu^{D-1},

where
(x)_{n}=x(x-1)\ldots(x-n+1)
denotes the descending factorial or Pochhammer symbol.
The last result (
44
) can be straightforwardly extended to any DSCS, that is

M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}\rangle)=M_{\nu}(|\bm{0}\rangle),\;\forall\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{D-1},

(45)

and, in fact, to any boson condensate of
N
atoms in any level
i=0,\dots,D-1
(see Appendix
B
for a proof).
This in particular means that all coherent states occupy the same area in phase space.
Indeed, any DSCS
|\bm{z}\rangle
can be obtained by translating/rotating
|\bm{z}=\bm{0}\rangle\to U(\bm{z})|\bm{0}\rangle
by a unitary
transformation
U(\bm{z})\in\mathrm{U}(D)
(that is,
|\bm{z}\rangle
can be seen as a “displaced ground state”), which means that
Q_{|\bm{z}\rangle}(\bm{z}^{\prime})=Q_{|\bm{0}\rangle}(\bm{z}^{\prime}*\bm{z}^{-1})
with
U(\bm{z}^{\prime}*\bm{z}^{-1})=U^{\dagger}(\bm{z})U(\bm{z}^{\prime})
the composition of two
\mathrm{U}(D)
transformations; the fact that the Fubini-Study measure
d\mu(\bm{z})
in (
18
) is
\mathrm{U}(D)
-invariant completes the proof. Therefore, the
\nu
-moments of the Husimi function of a DSCS
|\bm{z}\rangle
do not depend on the phase space points
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{D-1}
, but just on
\nu
, the number of particles/atoms
N
, and the number of atom levels
D
. The equations (
44
,
45
) agree with those of Refs.
Giovannetti et al. 2004
;
Calixto et al. 2012
in the particular cases of
D=2
and
D=3
respectively, and with
Sugita 2003
in the general
D
case.

The
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT states
|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}
in (
24

|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle
and therefore have a lower IPR value (i.e., they occupy a bigger area in phase space), usually a fraction of
M_{\nu}(|\bm{0}\rangle)
. More concretely, the
\nu
-moment of
Q_{|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}}
can be explicitly calculated as in the reference
Sugita 2003
,

M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}})=M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}\rangle)\sum_{|\vec{k}|=N\nu}|B_{\vec{k}}^{2}|\,,

(46)

with

\displaystyle B_{\vec{k}}=

\displaystyle\,\sqrt{\frac{(N!)^{\nu}}{(N\nu)!}}\sum_{|\vec{n}_{1}|=\ldots=|\vec{n}_{\nu}|=N}\binom{\vec{k}}{\vec{n}_{1},\vec{n}_{2},\ldots,\vec{n}_{\nu}}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\,\times c_{{\vec{n}}_{1}}(\bm{z})_{\mathbbm{c}}c_{{\vec{n}}_{2}}(\bm{z})_{\mathbbm{c}}\cdots c_{{\vec{n}}_{\nu}}(\bm{z})_{\mathbbm{c}}\,,

(47)

where
c_{{\vec{n}}_{i}}(\bm{z})_{\mathbbm{c}}
are the
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT coefficients in the Fock basis (
31
). The last sum is restricted to
{\vec{n}}_{1}+{\vec{n}}_{2}+\ldots+{\vec{n}}_{\nu}=\vec{k}
, and we are denoting

\binom{\vec{k}}{\vec{n}_{1},\vec{n}_{2},\ldots,\vec{n}_{\nu}}\equiv\frac{\vec{k}!}{\vec{n}_{1}!\cdots\vec{n}_{\nu}!}\,,

(48)

where all the vectors
{\vec{n}}_{1},{\vec{n}}_{2},\ldots,{\vec{n}}_{\nu}
correspond to different Fock vectors according to (

|{\vec{n}}_{i}\rangle=|n_{i,0},n_{i,1},\ldots,n_{i,D-1}\rangle
, so that we mean by

and by
|\vec{n}_{i}|\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}n_{i,j}\,
.

In the thermodynamic

for a proof)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}})=(2^{D-1})^{1-\nu}\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}\rangle)=\frac{(2^{D-1})^{1-\nu}}{\nu^{D-1}}\,,

(49)

which proves that
\mathbbm{c}
-DCATs have lower IPR value than DSCSs, since
(2^{D-1})^{1-\nu}<1
for all
\nu\geq 2
. Hence, DCATs are less localized (occupy a greater area) than DSCSs in phase space. In addition, the limit is independent of the DCAT parity
\mathbbm{c}
. To be more precise, the equation above is only valid when all the coordinates
z_{i}
are non-zero, i.e.
z_{i}\neq 0

\forall i=1,\dots,D-1
. Nevertheless, for a totally even
\mathbb{0}
-DCAT which has only

non-zero vector components in

that tend to 0, transforming the
\mathbb{0}
-DCAT into a reduced
\mathbb{0}_{K}
-DCAT with a parity symmetry described by
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\lim\limits_{\bm{z}_{L}\to\bm{0}_{L}}M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbb{0}})=

\displaystyle\,(2^{k})^{1-\nu}\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\lim\limits_{\bm{z}_{L}\to\bm{0}_{L}}M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}\rangle)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{(2^{k})^{1-\nu}}{\nu^{D-1}}\,,

(50)

where the notation is the same as in the eq.(
39

Note that the denominator

-DCAT, the expression above transforms into

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\lim\limits_{\bm{z}_{L}\to\bm{0}_{L}}M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}})=\frac{(2^{k+\|\mathbbm{c}_{L}\|_{0}})^{1-\nu}}{\nu^{D-1}}\,,

where

\bm{z}^{\prime}

-DCAT.

Instead of
M_{\nu}(\psi)
, it is sometimes preferred to express delocalization (as a measure of area in phase space) in terms of Rényi-Wehrl entropy, which is defined as
Mintert and Życzkowski 2004
;
Gnutzmann and Zyczkowski 2001
;
Giovannetti et al. 2004
as

\mathcal{S}_{W,\nu}(\psi)=\frac{1}{1-\nu}\ln[M_{\nu}(\psi)],\quad\nu\not=1.

(52)

Taking the limit
\nu\to 1
in the Rényi-Wehrl entropy (
52
), one obtains the Wehrl entropy
Wehrl 1978
given by

\mathcal{S}_{W}(\psi)=-\int_{\mathbb{C}^{D-1}}Q_{\psi}(\bm{z})\ln[Q_{\psi}(\bm{z})]d\mu(\bm{z}).

(53)

Since the definition of the Husimi function is related to a specific classical phase space (the
\mathbb{C}P^{D-1}
complex projective space defined by DSCSs in our case), the Wehrl entropy is also called (semi)classical entropy
Wehrl 1978
;
Lieb and Solovej 2014
. It is the Gibbs entropy continuous form of the Husimi probability function
Q_{\rho}
for the state described by a density matrix
\rho

Wehrl 1978
;
Mintert and Życzkowski 2004
.
This picture contrast with other common entropies such as the von Neumann entropy
\mathcal{S}_{N}=-\mathrm{tr}(\rho\ln\rho)
, which we have previously used to study entanglement (quantum nonlocality) in symmetric
multiquDit systems
Calixto et al. 2021b
,
and has no immediate relation to classical mechanics. The last one measures how much a state is mixed (non pure), rather than its localization in phase-space. According to the Lieb conjecture
Lieb 1978
, the minimum Wehrl entropy (
53
) is attained when
\psi
is a DSCS. It was proved for SU(2) spin-
j
CSs in
Lieb and Solovej 2014
;
Baecklund 2013

D
) spin CSs (DSCSs for us) in
Lieb and Solovej 2016

Sugita 2002
. The minimum Wehrl entropy value can be easily obtained from the Husimi
\nu
-moment of the highest-weight vector
|\bm{z}\rangle=|\bm{0}\rangle
in (

\mathcal{S}_{W}=\lim_{\nu\to 1}\mathcal{S}_{W,\nu}
in (
52

\min_{\psi}\mathcal{S}_{W}(\psi)=\mathcal{S}_{W}(|\bm{z}\rangle)=\mathcal{S}_{W}(|\bm{0}\rangle)

(54)

according to (
45

\nu\to 1
in (

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{W}(|\bm{0}\rangle)=

\displaystyle\,N\big(\psi^{(0)}(N+D)-\psi^{(0)}(N+1)\big)

(55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,N\sum_{k=1}^{D-1}\frac{1}{N+k}\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}D-1,

for a generic number
D
of levels, where
\psi^{(0)}(x)=\Gamma^{\prime}(x)/\Gamma(x)
is the digamma function.
There is a particular version of this result for a

;
Lieb 1973

\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT states, except in the thermodynamic limit when, in general,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{S}_{W}(|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}})=

\displaystyle\,\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{S}_{W}(|\bm{z}\rangle)+\log(2^{D-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,(D-1)(1+\log(2)),

(56)

being the same for all different parities
\mathbbm{c}
. As we already commented in the equation (

for the fully even

(57)

Therefore, the totally even parity adaptation of a DSCS entails a Wehrl entropy (area in phase space) excess of

(58)

in the thermodynamic limit.
This is a particular case of the result proposed by Mintert and Zyczkowski in

\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\lim\limits_{\bm{z}_{L}\to\bm{0}_{K}}\mathcal{S}_{W}(|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}})=(D-1)+(k+\|\mathbbm{c}_{L}\|_{0})\log(2)\,.

V
LMG model for three-level atoms and its quantum phase diagram

We particularize the Hamiltonian (
7
) for
D=3
(3-level atoms or qutrits). Therefore, our Hamiltonian density will be

H=\frac{\epsilon}{N}(S_{33}-S_{11})-\frac{\lambda}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i\not=j=1}^{3}S_{ij}^{2}.

(60)

We shall measure energy in
\epsilon
units and discuss the energy spectrum and the phase diagram in terms of the only control parameter
\lambda
.
In
Calixto et al. 2021a
we have proved that this model displays three different quantum phases for the completely symmetric unitary irreducible representation of U(3) labelled by the total number of particles
N
; Ref.
Calixto et al. 2021a
also studies other permutation symmetry sectors (fermionic mixtures from two-row Young diagrams) which will not be discussed here. Let us summarize the essential points. Coherent (semiclassical) states are in general good
variational states which faithfully reproduce the ground state energy of Hamiltonian models in the semiclassical/thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
. Therefore, we define
the energy surface associated to the Hamiltonian density
H
in (
60
) as the DSCS expectation value of the Hamiltonian density in the thermodynamic limit

\displaystyle E_{|\bm{z}\rangle}(\epsilon,\lambda)=

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle\bm{z}|H|\bm{z}\rangle

(61)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\hskip-2.84526pt\left(\hskip-1.42262pt\epsilon\tfrac{\langle\bm{z}|S_{33}|\bm{z}\rangle-\langle\bm{z}|S_{11}|\bm{z}\rangle}{N}-\lambda\tfrac{\sum_{i\not=j=1}^{3}\langle\bm{z}|S_{ij}|\bm{z}\rangle^{2}}{N(N-1)}\hskip-1.42262pt\right)\hskip-2.84526pt,

with
\langle\bm{z}|S_{ij}|\bm{z}\rangle
in (
19
). Note that we have used that there are no spin fluctuations in the thermodynamic limit (
21
).
Denoting
\bm{z}=(z_{1},z_{2})
the phase space coordinates for U(3)-spin coherent states (
15
), the energy surface has the explicit form

E_{|\bm{z}\rangle}(\epsilon,\lambda)=\epsilon\frac{|z_{2}|^{2}-1}{|z_{1}|+|z_{2}|^{2}+1}-\lambda\frac{z_{1}^{2}\left(\bar{z}_{2}^{2}+1\right)+z_{2}^{2}+\mathrm{c.c.}}{\left(|z_{1}|+|z_{2}|^{2}+1\right)^{2}}.

(62)

This energy surface is invariant under parity transformations
z_{1}\to-z_{1}
,
z_{2}\to-z_{2}
, a symmetry which is inherited from the discrete parity symmetry of the Hamiltonian
(
60
). In fact, the energy surface
E_{|\bm{z}\rangle}(\epsilon,\lambda)
coincides with all
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT Hamiltonian expectation values in the thermodynamic limit, that is

E_{|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}}(\epsilon,\lambda)=E_{|\bm{z}\rangle}(\epsilon,\lambda)\quad\forall\mathbbm{c}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{D-1}.

(63)

This can be seen by using the linear and quadratic
\mathrm{U}(D)
-spin operator expectation values in a
\mathbbm{c}
-parity DCAT defined in
Calixto et al. 2021b
, and realizing that

\lim_{N\to\infty}{}_{\mathbbm{c}}\langle\bm{z}|S_{ij}|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle\bm{z}|S_{ij}|\bm{z}\rangle,

(64)

which can also be extended to quadratic (two-body)
\mathrm{U}(D)
-spin operator expectation values because of the absence of quantum fluctuations in the thermodynamic limit (
21
).
This fact has important consequences in the spontaneous breakdown of the parity symmetry in the thermodynamic limit and the quantum phase transition, as we are going to see in the following.

The variational minimum (ground state) energy

E^{(0)}(\epsilon,\lambda)=\mathrm{min}_{z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}}E_{|(z_{1},z_{2})\rangle}(\epsilon,\lambda)

(65)

is attained at the critical (real) coherent state parameters

\displaystyle z_{1\pm}^{(0)}(\epsilon,\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pm\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0,&&0\leq\lambda\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2},\\
\sqrt{\frac{2\lambda-\epsilon}{2\lambda+\epsilon}},&&\frac{\epsilon}{2}\leq\lambda\leq\frac{3\epsilon}{2},\\
\sqrt{\frac{2\lambda}{2\lambda+3\epsilon}},&&\lambda\geq\frac{3\epsilon}{2},\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle z_{2\pm}^{(0)}(\epsilon,\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pm\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0,&&0\leq\lambda\leq\frac{3\epsilon}{2},\\
\sqrt{\frac{2\lambda-3\epsilon}{2\lambda+3\epsilon}},&&\lambda\geq\frac{3\epsilon}{2}.\end{array}\right.

Inserting (
V
) into (
62
) gives the ground state energy density in the thermodynamic limit

E^{(0)}(\epsilon,\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lllr}-\epsilon,&&0\leq\lambda\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2},&\mathrm{(I)}\\
-\frac{(2\lambda+\epsilon)^{2}}{8\lambda},&&\frac{\epsilon}{2}\leq\lambda\leq\frac{3\epsilon}{2},&\mathrm{(II)}\\
-\frac{4\lambda^{2}+3\epsilon^{2}}{6\lambda},&&\lambda\geq\frac{3\epsilon}{2}.&\mathrm{(III)}\end{array}\right.

(73)

Here we clearly distinguish three different phases: I, II and III, and two second-order QPTs (according to Ehrenfest’s classification) occurring at critical points
\lambda^{(0)}_{\mathrm{I}\leftrightarrow\mathrm{II}}=\epsilon/2
and

\lambda^{(0)}_{\mathrm{II}\leftrightarrow\mathrm{III}}=3\epsilon/2
, respectively, at which the second derivative of
E_{0}(\epsilon,\lambda)
is discontinuous. As we have already anticipated, the ground state is degenerated, since there are four different DSCSs
|z_{1\pm}^{(0)},z_{2\pm}^{(0)}\rangle
(or equivalently, four 3CAT states
|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}
with parities
\mathbbm{c}=[0,0],[1,0],[0,1],
and
[1,1]
) with the same energy (
73
) in the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
. This is a consequence of the spontaneous breakdown of the discrete parity symmetry
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}
of the Hamiltonian (
60
), as was already pointed out in
Calixto et al. 2021a
. For general
D
, the ground state degeneracy would go as
2^{k}
, with
k
the number of nonzero components of
\bm{z}^{(0)}
, with a maximum degeneracy of
2^{D-1}
(the number of elements of the parity group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{D-1}
).

For a finite number
N

|\bm{z}\rangle
still provide a fairly good approximation to the ground state when properly adapted to the (not yet broken) parity. There are two possible variational approaches for finite

1.

Project
|\bm{z}\rangle
onto parity
\mathbbm{c}=\mathbb{0}=[0,0]

\mathbb{0}
-3CAT state

Calixto et al. 2021b

Use one of the four critical coherent state parameters combinations

\displaystyle\,+\left.|z_{1-}^{(0)},z_{2+}^{(0)}\rangle+|z_{1-}^{(0)},z_{2-}^{(0)}\rangle\right]

VIII
Conclusions

The concept of Husimi function in the canonical phase space is extended to the complex projective space
\mathbb{C}P^{D-1}=\text{U}(D)/[\text{U}(1)\times\text{U}(D-1)]
using
\text{U}(D)
-spin coherent states (DSCSs for short) for symmetric multi-quDit systems. The
\nu
-moments of the Husimi function and some localization measures in phase space such as the Inverse Participation Ratio and the Wehrl entropy are accordingly extended with a proper integration (Harr) measure. We prove that the Lieb conjecture is fulfilled for the DSCSs in the eq.(
44
) and the Appendix
B
. The parity
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{D-1}\ni\mathbbm{c}
adaptations of DSCSs (called
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT states) turn out to be less localized than the DSCSs, which exhibit maximum localization (minimum area in phase space) according to Lieb’s conjecture. This becomes clear when we calculate the thermodynamic limit of the Husimi function
\nu
-th moments and Wehrl entropy for DSCSs and
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT states.

The previous study of the LMG U(3) ground state
Calixto et al. 2021a
is then extended to the first excited states, which turn out to be modeled by
\mathbbm{c}
-3CATs of different parities, as Figures
1
and
2
show. In particular, we compare the numerical eigenstates of the LMG U(3) model (for finite
N
) to different variational
\mathbbm{c}
-3CATs states via fidelity (
78
), where the variational states are evaluated at the critical points
\bm{z}^{(0)}=(z_{1\pm}^{(0)},z_{2\pm}^{(0)})
which minimize the LMG U(3) energy surface in the thermodynamic limit (
V
). The variational
\mathbbm{c}
-3CAT states turn out to be fairly faithful to the low-lying excited Hamiltonian eigenstates except in the vicinity of the critical points
\lambda^{(0)}_{\mathrm{I}\leftrightarrow\mathrm{II}}=\epsilon/2
and
\lambda^{(0)}_{\mathrm{II}\leftrightarrow\mathrm{III}}=3\epsilon/2
separating quantum phases I, II and III. We believe this is a consequence of the growth of quantum fluctuations at the critical points. However, this fidelity can be improved by maximizing the corresponding overlap in the complex projective phase space
\mathbb{C}P^{2}\ni\bm{z}
, as we display in the Figures
3
and
4
.

The fact that the minimization of the energy surface in the thermodynamic limit provides critical vectors
\bm{z}^{(0)}
with some zero components in certain phases, makes it necessary to revise the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{D-1}
-parity adaptation
|\bm{z}^{(0)}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}
of
|\bm{z}^{(0)}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}
and to resolve some “
0/0
” indeterminacies. In the case when
\bm{z}
has
l=D-1-k
null coordinates,
the corresponding
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT
|\bm{z}^{(0)}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}
reduces to
\mathbbm{c}_{K}
-DCATs with lower
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
-parity (
39
). This result permeates in the majority of magnitudes (Husimi function, its moments, etc.) calculated in this work.

The QPTs of the LMG U(3) model are visualized in the phase space
\mathbb{C}P^{2}\ni\bm{z}^{\prime}
across the phase diagram via the Husimi function
Q_{\mathbb{0}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})
of the variational ground state
|\bm{z}^{(0)}\rangle_{\mathbb{0}}
. We draw contour plots of the Husimi function in “position space”
(x_{1},x_{2})=\text{Re}(\bm{z}^{\prime})
and in “momentum space”
(p_{1},p_{2})=\text{Im}(\bm{z}^{\prime})
in Figure
5
). In position space, the variational GS Husimi function
Q_{\mathbb{0}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})
displays several humps depending on the number of non-zero coordinates of
\bm{z}^{(0)}(\lambda)
, which changes in the different quantum phases I, II and III. A similar reasoning is followed in Figure
6
with the Husimi function of the other variational
\mathbbm{c}
-3CAT states
|\bm{z}^{(0)}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}
mimicking low-lying Hamiltonian eigenstates with parity
\mathbbm{c}
. We propose a general expression (
81
) for the number of humps (in position phase) of the Husimi function of general
\mathbbm{c}
-3CATs
|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}
, depending on the number of zero components of
\bm{z}
and the parity
\mathbbm{c}
. This number also appears in the thermodynamic limit of the
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT Husimi moments (
51
) and in the rank of the
M
-particle reduced density matrix of a
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT
Guerrero et al. 2023
.

Finally, we also characterize the QPTs via localization measures in phase space, since the Husimi fuction
Q_{\mathbb{0}}
of the ground state of the LMG model suddenly suffers delocalization when passing through the quantum critical
\lambda^{(0)}
points, as shwon in Figures of Wehrl entropy
7
and IPR
8
of
Q_{\mathbb{0}}
as a function of the control parameter
\lambda
. More localization implies less Wehrl entropy (less area) and more IPR. This effect is more abrupt for the variational ground state than for the numerical one, and gets sharper and sharper when increasing
N
, approaching to the limits proposed in the Section
IV
and proved in the Appendix
C
. The same analysis is extended to the numerical excited states and variational
\mathbbm{c}
-3CATs in Figure

. Note that the reduced
\mathbb{0}_{i}
-DCAT is not a (D-1)CAT, as it is the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{D-2}
-parity adapted version of a DSCS with
z_{i}=0
, i.e.
|\bm{z}_{i}\rangle^{(N)}=\lim_{z_{i}\to 0}|\bm{z}\rangle^{(N)}
. The normalization constant
\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}_{i})_{\mathbb{0}_{i}}
is calculated as in (
25
) but using a reduced sum in
\mathbbm{b}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{D-2}
, and with the new coordinates
\bm{z}_{i}
,

\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}_{i})_{\mathbb{0}_{i}}^{2}=2^{2-D}\frac{\sum_{\mathbbm{b}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{D-2}}(1+\bm{z}_{i}^{\dagger}\bm{z}_{i}^{\mathbbm{b}_{i}})^{N}}{(1+\bm{z}_{i}^{\dagger}\bm{z}_{i})^{N}}.

(83)

The zero limit (
82
) can be used repeatedly for a set of
l=D-1-k
different coordinates
\bm{z}_{L}=\{z_{i_{1}},\ldots,z_{i_{l}}\}
, whose indexes are taken form the set
L=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{l}\}
, transforming the totally even
\mathbb{0}
-DCAT into a reduced
\mathbb{0}_{K}
-DCAT with a parity symmetry given by
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
,

\lim\limits_{z_{L}\to\bm{0}_{L}}|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbb{0}}^{(N)}=|(\bm{z}_{K},\bm{z}_{L}=\bm{0}_{L})\rangle_{\mathbb{0}_{K}}^{(N)}\,,

(84)

where
(\bm{z}_{K},\bm{z}_{L}=\bm{0}_{L})=\lim_{z_{L}\to\bm{0}_{L}}\bm{z}
has only
k
non-zero coordinates
\bm{z}_{K}=\{z_{j_{1}},\ldots,z_{j_{k}}\}
, whose associated parity components are
\mathbb{0}_{K}=[0,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(k)}}{{\ldots}},0]\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
. That is,
K=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}
is the set including all the
k
non-zero coordinates of
\bm{z}
.
\bm{0}_{L}
denotes the
l
coordinates
(0,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle l}}{{\ldots}},0)
. The existence and uniqueness of the multiple limit (
84
) can be derived using hyperspherical coordinates with the moduli of
|z_{i}|
. The norm of the reduced
\mathbb{0}_{K}
-DCAT in (
84
) is calculated using an equivalent expression of the eq.(
83
).

In the case where all coordinates
z_{i}
tend to 0 (
k=0
), the
\mathbb{0}
-DCAT collapses to a Fock state,

\lim\limits_{\bm{z}\to\bm{0}}|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbb{0}}^{(N)}=|{\scriptstyle n_{0}=N,\,n_{1}=0,\,\ldots,\,n_{D-1}=0}\rangle\,,

(85)

which is the highest weight vector of the
N
-particle symmetric irreducible representation of U
(D)
that we are considering. This highest weight vector deserves our attention because it is the ground state of the free (
\lambda=0
) LMG U(
D
) Hamiltonian (see in Section
V
for a detailed discussion). The limit (
85
) has previously been calculated in
Guerrero et al. 2023
for a general
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT, giving the so called
Fock-cat states
.

Appendix B
Analytical calculation of the
\nu
-moments of the Husimi function of a DSCS

Here we show in detail the calculations that lead to the expressions of the
\nu
-th moments of the DSCSs (
44
,
45
), the DCATs (
46
) and its thermodynamic limit (
44
,
49
,
IV
).

Firstly, the moments of the DSCSs are computed by previously using the highest-weight state
|\psi\rangle=|\bm{z}=\bm{0}\rangle=(a_{0}^{\dagger})^{N}/\sqrt{N!}|\vec{0}\rangle
(a boson condensate of
N
atoms in their lower level
i=0
)
according to the equation(
15
). Using the scalar product of the DSCSs (
17
), we calculate the Husimi function (
40
) of this state as

Q_{|\bm{0}\rangle}(z)=|\langle\bm{z}|\bm{0}\rangle|^{2}=\frac{1}{(1+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{z})^{N}}\,.

(86)

It is straightforward to perform the integration in the
\nu
-moments formula (
43
) for the Husimi function
Q_{|\bm{0}\rangle}(z)
and arbitrary
\nu
. The integral in
\mathbb{C}^{D-1}
is mapped to
(\mathbb{R}^{+}\times[0,2\pi])^{D-1}
using polar coordinates
z_{j}=\rho_{j}e^{i\theta_{j}}
,
d^{2}z_{j}=\rho_{j}d\rho_{j}d\theta_{j}
for all
j=1,\ldots,D-1
. Then, we integrate recursively for all
\rho_{j}
from
j=1
to
j=D-1
, and the equation (
44
) for
M_{\nu}(|\bm{0}\rangle)
is achieved. The extension (
45
) from
|\bm{z}=\bm{0}\rangle
to an arbitrary DSCS
|\bm{z}\rangle
is direct using the
\mathrm{U}(D)
invariance of the Fubini-Study measure
d\mu(\bm{z})
in
\mathbb{C}P^{D-1}
.

Appendix C
Thermodynamic limit of the
\nu
-moments of the Husimi function of a
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT

In Eq. (
46
) we have given the
\nu
-moments of the Husimi function
Q_{|\bm{z}_{\mathbbm{c}}\rangle}
of a
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT. This bulky expression acquires a simpler form (
IV
) in the thermodynamic limit. Let us prove it.

We shall initially give some auxiliary results and calculate their Husimi function. First of all, the scalar product of the DSCSs (
17
) has a Kronecker delta-like thermodynamic limit,

\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}\rangle=\begin{cases}1\quad\mathrm{if}\quad\bm{z}^{\prime}=\bm{z}\,,\\
0\quad\mathrm{if}\quad\bm{z}^{\prime}\neq\bm{z}\,,\end{cases}

(87)

which leads to

\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle\langle\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}}|\bm{z}^{\prime}\rangle=\begin{cases}1\quad\mathrm{if}\quad\bm{z}^{\prime}=\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\text{ and }\bm{z}^{\prime}=\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}},\\
0\quad\text{elsewhere}\,,\end{cases}

(88)

as
(1+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}})<(1+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{z})
for all
\mathbbm{b}\neq\mathbb{0}
and
\bm{z}
with non-zero components.
The non-null condition of the last equation implies that
\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}=\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}}
, what leads to
\mathbbm{b}=\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}
provided that
z_{i}\neq 0
for all
i=1,\ldots,D-1
. Therefore, we begin studying the case where
\bm{z}
does not have any null component.

The Husimi function of the
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT (
IV
) can also be written using the Husimi function (
40
) and the
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT (
24
) definitions,

\displaystyle Q_{|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\,|\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}|^{2}

(89)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\left(\frac{2^{1-D}}{\mathcal{N}(\bm{z})_{\mathbbm{c}}}\right)^{2}\sum_{\mathbbm{b},\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}}(-1)^{\mathbbm{c}\cdot(\mathbbm{b}+\mathbbm{b}^{\prime})}\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle\langle\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}}|\bm{z}^{\prime}\rangle\,.

Since the
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT normalization
\mathcal{N}(\bm{z})_{\mathbbm{c}}
is non-zero for all
\bm{z}
(without any null component) and
\mathbbm{c}
, we take the limit of the numerator and denominator of
Q_{|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}}(z^{\prime})
separately. The denominator is, according to the equation (
25
),

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{N}(\bm{z})_{\mathbbm{c}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\,\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}2^{1-D}\frac{\sum_{\mathbbm{b}}(-1)^{\mathbbm{c}\cdot\mathbbm{b}}(1+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}})^{N}}{(1+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{z})^{N}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,2^{1-D}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}}(-1)^{\mathbbm{c}\cdot\mathbbm{b}}\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\frac{(1+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}})^{N}}{(1+\bm{z}^{\dagger}\bm{z})^{N}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,2^{1-D}\,.

(90)

The numerator limit is performed using the equation (
88
) and its derived condition
\mathbbm{b}=\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\,\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\mathbbm{b},\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}}(-1)^{\mathbbm{c}\cdot(\mathbbm{b}+\mathbbm{b}^{\prime})}\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle\langle\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}}|\bm{z}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}}(-1)^{\mathbbm{c}\cdot(\mathbbm{b}+\mathbbm{b})}\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle\langle\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}|\bm{z}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}}Q_{|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle}(\bm{z}^{\prime})\,,

(91)

as
(-1)^{\mathbbm{c}\cdot(\mathbbm{b}+\mathbbm{b})}=1
. Therefore, the limit of the
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT Husimi function is

\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}Q_{|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})=2^{1-D}\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}}Q_{|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle}(\bm{z}^{\prime})\,.

(92)

The number of humps of
\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}Q_{|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})
in the phase space
\bm{z}^{\prime}
will be the number of terms in the sum
\sum_{\mathbbm{b}}
(right term in eq.(
92
)), that is
2^{D-1}
, as showed in the Figures
5
and
6
for
D=3
and
\lambda=2.5
.

The next step is calculate the limit of the
\nu
-th power of the Husimi function of the
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT,
[Q_{|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})]^{\nu}
for all
\nu\geq 2
. We split again the limit in numerator and denominator, where the last one is trivial using the same procedure as in (
C
), that is
\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{N}(\bm{z})_{\mathbbm{c}}^{2\nu}=(2^{1-D})^{\nu}
. The numerator limit is

\displaystyle\,\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\left(\sum_{\mathbbm{b},\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}}(-1)^{\mathbbm{c}\cdot(\mathbbm{b}+\mathbbm{b}^{\prime})}\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle\langle\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}}|\bm{z}^{\prime}\rangle\right)^{\nu}

(93)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbbm{b}_{\nu}}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}_{\nu}}(-1)^{\mathbbm{c}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}(\mathbbm{b}_{i}+\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}_{i})}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\times\prod_{i=1}^{\nu}\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}_{i}}\rangle\langle\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}_{i}}|\bm{z}^{\prime}\rangle\,,

which reduces, with the auxiliary equation (
88
), to

\displaystyle\,\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}}(-1)^{\mathbbm{c}\cdot 2\nu\mathbbm{b}}\prod_{i=1}^{\nu}\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle\langle\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}|\bm{z}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}}[Q_{|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle}(\bm{z}^{\prime})]^{\nu}\,.

(94)

So we have

\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}[Q_{|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})]^{\nu}=(2^{1-D})^{\nu}\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}}[Q_{|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle}(\bm{z}^{\prime})]^{\nu}\,.

(95)

Eventually, we can calculate the
\nu
-moments of the
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT Husimi function, that is

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}})=\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{D-1}}[Q_{|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})]^{\nu}d\mu(\bm{z}^{\prime})\,.

(96)

Employing the equation (
95
), and commuting the integral and the limit, the last expression turns into

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}})=

\displaystyle\,(2^{1-D})^{\nu}\int\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}}[Q_{|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle}(\bm{z}^{\prime})]^{\nu}d\mu(\bm{z}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,(2^{1-D})^{\nu}\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}}\int[Q_{|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle}(\bm{z}^{\prime})]^{\nu}d\mu(\bm{z}^{\prime})\,.

(97)

The new integral is equal to the moment
M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle)
of the DSCS
|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle
, which fulfills
M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}^{\mathbbm{b}}\rangle)=M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}\rangle)=M_{\nu}(|\bm{0}\rangle)
according to the equation (
45
) and the Fubini-Study measure invariance. In the end, the equation (
49
) of the moments of the
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT in the thermodynamic limit is reached,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbbm{c}})=

\displaystyle\,(2^{1-D})^{\nu}\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}}M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}\rangle)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,(2^{D-1})^{1-\nu}\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}\rangle)\,.

(98)

When there are only
k
non-zero components in
\bm{z}
, the even
\mathbb{0}
-DCAT (with
\mathbb{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{D-1}
) reduces to a
\mathbb{0}_{K}
-DCAT with a smaller parity symmetry
\mathbb{0}_{K}=[0,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(k)}}{{\ldots}},0]\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
(see the notation of the eq.(
84
)). Therefore, the equation (
89
) turns into

\displaystyle\,\lim\limits_{\bm{z}_{L}\to\bm{0}_{L}}Q_{|\bm{z}\rangle_{\mathbb{0}}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})=Q_{|\bm{z}_{K}\rangle_{\mathbb{0}_{K}}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})=|\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}_{K}\rangle_{\mathbb{0}_{K}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\left(\frac{2^{-k}}{\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}_{K})_{\mathbb{0}_{K}}}\right)^{2}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}_{K},\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}_{K}}(-1)^{\mathbb{0}_{K}\cdot(\mathbbm{b}_{K}+\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}_{K})}\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}_{K}^{\mathbbm{b}_{K}}\rangle\langle\bm{z}_{K}^{\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}_{K}}|\bm{z}^{\prime}\rangle\,,

(99)

where
\bm{z}_{K}=\lim_{\bm{z}_{L}\to\bm{0}_{L}}\bm{z}
(it would be more correct to write it as
(\bm{z}_{K},\bm{z}_{L}=\bm{0}_{L})
) and
\mathbb{0}_{K},\mathbbm{b}_{K},\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}_{K}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
. As previously done in the non-zero case (
C
), the reduced normalization constant of the denominator tends to
\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}_{K})_{\mathbb{0}_{K}}=2^{-k}
, where we have used a generalization of the expression (
83
). The equation (
88
) can be adapted to

\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}_{K}^{\mathbbm{b}_{K}}\rangle\langle\bm{z}_{K}^{\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}_{K}}|\bm{z}^{\prime}\rangle=\begin{cases}1\quad\mathrm{if}\>\>\bm{z}^{\prime}=\bm{z}_{K}^{\mathbbm{b}_{K}}\text{ and }\bm{z}^{\prime}=\bm{z}_{K}^{\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}_{K}},\\
0\quad\text{elsewhere}\,,\end{cases}

(100)

where the non-null value is achieved when
\bm{z}_{K}^{\mathbbm{b}_{K}}=\bm{z}_{K}^{\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}_{K}}
, which implies
\mathbbm{b}_{K}=\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}_{K}
. This is true because all the coordinates in
\bm{z}_{K}
(
(\bm{z}_{K},\bm{z}_{L}=\bm{0}_{L})
in fact) associated to
\mathbbm{b}_{K}
are non-zero by construction. Consequently, the numerator in (
C
) transforms into

\displaystyle\,\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}_{K},\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}_{K}}(-1)^{\mathbb{0}_{K}\cdot(\mathbbm{b}_{K}+\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}_{K})}\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}_{K}^{\mathbbm{b}_{K}}\rangle\langle\bm{z}_{K}^{\mathbbm{b}^{\prime}_{K}}|\bm{z}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}_{K}}\langle\bm{z}^{\prime}|\bm{z}_{K}^{\mathbbm{b}_{K}}\rangle\langle\bm{z}_{K}^{\mathbbm{b}_{K}}|\bm{z}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}_{K}}Q_{|\bm{z}_{K}^{\mathbbm{b}_{K}}\rangle}(\bm{z}^{\prime})\,,

(101)

using in the second line the property (
29
) of the parity group characters. The thermodynamic limit of the
\tilde{\mathbb{0}}
-DCAT Husimi function is finally

\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}Q_{|\bm{z}_{K}\rangle_{\mathbb{0}_{K}}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})=2^{-k}\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}_{K}}Q_{|\bm{z}_{K}^{\mathbbm{b}_{K}}\rangle}(\bm{z}^{\prime})\,.

(102)

From this moment on, it is straightforward to adapt the procedure followed at the beginning for the moments of the
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT to the
\mathbb{0}_{K}
-DCAT, arriving to the expression

\displaystyle\,\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}_{K}\rangle_{\mathbb{0}_{K}})

(103)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,(2^{-k})^{\nu}\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}_{K}}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{D-1}}[Q_{|\bm{z}_{K}^{\mathbbm{b}_{K}}\rangle}(\bm{z}^{\prime})]^{\nu}d\mu(\bm{z}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,(2^{-k})^{\nu}\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\mathbbm{b}_{K}}M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}_{K}^{\mathbbm{b}_{K}}\rangle)=(2^{k})^{(1-\nu)}\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}\rangle)\,,

since
M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}_{K}^{\mathbbm{b}_{K}}\rangle)=M_{\nu}(|\bm{z}\rangle)
(
45
), and using the parity characters property (
27
) for the reduced parity group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
. The last equation ends the calculations to prove the eq.(
IV
) for the
\mathbb{0}
-DCAT. The general case of zero coordinates in the
\mathbbm{c}
-DCAT (see eq.(
51
)) has been computed with a symbolic calculation software, so the analytical calculations are devoted to future research.
