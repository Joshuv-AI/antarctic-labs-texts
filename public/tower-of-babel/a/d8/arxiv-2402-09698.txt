Combining Evidence Across Filtrations
Yo Joong Choe, Aaditya Ramdas

Abstract
In sequential anytime-valid inference, any admissible procedure must be based on e-processes : generalizations of test martingales that quantify the accumulated evidence against a composite null hypothesis at any stopping time. This paper proposes a method for combining e-processes constructed in different filtrations but for the same null. Although e-processes in the same filtration can be combined effortlessly (by averaging), e-processes in different filtrations cannot because their validity in a coarser filtration does not translate to a finer filtration. This issue arises in sequential tests of randomness and independence, as well as in the evaluation of sequential forecasters. We establish that a class of functions called adjusters can lift arbitrary e-processes across filtrations. The result yields a generally applicable ‘‘adjust-then-combine’’ procedure, which we demonstrate on the problem of testing randomness in real-world financial data. Furthermore, we prove a characterization theorem for adjusters that formalizes a sense in which using adjusters is necessary. There are two major implications. First, if we have a powerful e-process in a coarsened filtration, then we readily have a powerful e-process in the original filtration. Second, when we coarsen the filtration to construct an e-process, there is a logarithmic cost to recovering validity in the original filtration. 1 1 1 Code to reproduce all empirical results is publicly available at https://github.com/yjchoe/CombiningEvidenceAcrossFiltrations .

Introduction

Given a null hypothesis over a sequence of observations, how do we combine evidence processes that are constructed in different filtrations?
This is a recurring question in problems such as randomness testing
(
Vovk, 2021b
;
Ramdas et al., 2022
)
, independence testing
(
Podkopaev et al., 2023
;
Henzi and Law, 2024
)
, and forecast evaluation
(
Henzi and Ziegel, 2022
;
Choe and Ramdas, 2023
)
, for which the evidence processes may be constructed in coarsened filtrations.
This paper addresses the problem of combining arbitrary evidence processes in such scenarios, specifically when the evidence is measured as an
e-process

(
Ramdas et al., 2020
)
.

E-processes (defined soon below) are the sequential generalization of
e-values

(
Wasserman et al., 2020
;
Vovk and Wang, 2021
;
Grünwald et al., 2024
)
and
betting scores

(
Shafer, 2021
)
.
An e-value
\tilde{E}
for a composite null hypothesis
\mathcal{P}
is a nonnegative random variable whose expectation is at most one under any null distribution, that is,
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\tilde{E}]\leq 1
for any
P\in\mathcal{P}
.
E-values generalize likelihood ratios to composite and nonparametric hypothesis tests, and
\tilde{P}=\min\{1/\tilde{E},1\}
is a p-value for
\mathcal{P}
by Markov’s inequality.
A key benefit of using e-values, as opposed to using p-values, is that we can seamlessly combine e-values across tests by averaging them, even when the e-values are arbitrarily dependent.
E-processes, which generalize the validity of an e-value to arbitrary stopping times (w.r.t. a filtration), can also enjoy the same benefit whenever we combine e-processes that have the same underlying filtration.

Despite much attention on the ease of combining e-values, recent work on
sequential
testing problems has shown that we face a rather subtle yet substantial challenge when combining e-processes that have
different
underlying filtrations.
Before jumping into an example, we first recap the necessary background on e-processes and sequential anytime-valid inference (SAVI).

1.1
Background: E-processes and sequential anytime-valid inference (SAVI)

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
be a measurable space, and let
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
\mathbb{G}=(\mathcal{G}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be two filtrations on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
, with
\mathcal{F}_{0}=\mathcal{G}_{0}=\{\emptyset,\Omega\}
.
In this paper, we focus on sequences of data and evidence measures in discrete time (
t=0,1,2,\dotsc
).
If
\mathcal{G}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{t}
for each
t
, then we say that
\mathbb{G}
is a
sub-filtration
or a
coarsening
of
\mathbb{F}
, denoted as
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
.
We also say that
\mathbb{G}
is
coarser
than
\mathbb{F}
, or that
\mathbb{F}
is
finer
than
\mathbb{G}
(
\mathbb{F}
contains more information than
\mathbb{G}
).
Given a filtration
\mathbb{G}
, a random variable
\tau
taking values in
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
is an
\mathbb{G}
-stopping time
if
\{\tau\leq t\}\in\mathcal{G}_{t}
for each
t
.

Next, we say that a sequence of random variables
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
process
in a filtration
\mathbb{F}
(or an
\mathbb{F}
-process
) if it is
\mathbb{F}
-
adapted
, that is,
X_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable for each
t
.
Given a distribution
P
, we say that a nonnegative
\mathbb{F}
-process
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
test (super)martingale

(
Shafer et al., 2011
)
for
P
in
\mathbb{F}
if
M_{0}=1
and
\mathbb{E}_{P}[M_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=M_{t-1}
(or
\mathbb{E}_{P}[M_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq M_{t-1}
for test supermartingales) for all
t\geq 1
.
Given a family of distributions
\mathcal{P}
—understood as a
composite
null, as opposed to a
point
null
P
, a nonnegative
\mathbb{F}
-process is a test (super)martingale for
\mathcal{P}
if it is a test (super)martingale for each
P\in\mathcal{P}
.
A test supermartingale possesses the
optional stopping
property: at any
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau
, it satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{P}[M_{\tau}]\leq 1
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
.

An
e-process

(
Ramdas et al., 2020
;
Ramdas et al., 2022
)
is a nonnegative adapted process that satisfies the optional stopping property under the null.
More formally, given a family of distributions
\mathcal{P}
, we say that a nonnegative
\mathbb{F}
-process is an e-process
(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{F}
if
\mathfrak{e}_{0}\leq 1
and,

\text{for any $\mathbb{F}$-stopping time $\tau$ and any $P\in\mathcal{P}$,}\quad\mathbb{E}_{P}[\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}]\leq 1.

(1)

In this sense, e-processes strictly generalize test supermartingales, and there exist composite nulls
\mathcal{P}
for which nontrivial e-processes exist while all test supermartingales (in
\mathbb{F}
) are trivial.
When
\mathcal{P}
is understood as a (composite) null hypothesis, an e-process for
\mathcal{P}
quantifies evidence
against

\mathcal{P}
, as it is not expected to be large at arbitrary stopping times
unless

\mathcal{P}
does not adequately describe the data.
Following
Ramdas et al., 2022
, we say that an e-process
\mathfrak{e}=(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
for a null hypothesis
\mathcal{P}
is
powerful
(or
consistent
) against a family of alternatives
\mathcal{Q}
, or
\mathcal{Q}
-powerful, if for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}\setminus\mathcal{P}
, we have
\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathfrak{e}_{t}=\infty
,
Q
-almost surely.

Note that the definition of an e-process explicitly depends on the filtration
\mathbb{F}
through the choice of stopping times
\tau
.
We refer to the notion of validity at arbitrary (
\mathbb{F}
-)stopping times, such as (
1
), as (
\mathbb{F}
-)
anytime-validity

(
Ramdas et al., 2020
)
or (
\mathbb{F}
-)
safety

(
Grünwald et al., 2024
)
.
It is known that any anytime-valid testing procedure must go through an e-process, suggesting e-processes are the central objects for SAVI
(
Ramdas et al., 2020
)
.

We provide a glossary of the key definitions and notations in Section
A
.

1.2
A motivating example from financial time series modeling

We shall now introduce a practical example motivating the problem of combining e-processes constructed in different filtrations.
Below, we say that a sequence of random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\dotsc
is
random

(
Vovk, 2021b
)
if they are independent and identically distributed (IID).

Example 1

(Sequentially testing the randomness of high-volatility days in financial time series)
.

Suppose that a portfolio manager wants to monitor whether the patterns of high-volatility days for stock returns are random.
This is because the manager wants to act more conservatively when there are abnormal patterns of high-volatility days, which may hurt the performance of their forecasting model in unexpected ways.
Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dotsc
be a sequence of binary random variables that indicate high-volatility days (
t=1,2,\dotsc
denotes each trading day).
The manager wants to test if this sequence is IID and raise an alarm if the process deviates from randomness:

\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{IID}}:X_{1},X_{2},\dotsc\;\text{are IID.}

(2)

Note that
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{IID}}
is a composite null hypothesis and that there are many ways in which the data can deviate from it (e.g., presence of changepoints or temporal dependence).

A priori
, the manager does not know exactly how the data may deviate from randomness.
Thus, they will want to leverage (at least) two different testing procedures that achieve power under different alternatives.
One such procedure is based on the
universal inference

(
Wasserman et al., 2020
;
Ramdas et al., 2022
, UI;)
e-process, denoted as
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{UI}}=(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{UI}})_{t\geq 0}
, which generalizes likelihood ratios to composite nulls and alternatives.
Formally, the UI e-process can be written as
\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{UI}}=\frac{\int_{\theta\in[0,1]^{2}}Q_{\theta}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{t})p(\theta)d\theta}{\sup_{\mu\in[0,1]}P_{\mu}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{t})}
, where
P_{\mu}
is an IID
\mathsf{Ber}(\mu)
null distribution;
Q_{\theta}
is a first-order Markovian alternative with transition parameters
\theta=(p_{0\to 1},p_{1\to 1})\in[0,1]^{2}
; and
p(\theta)
is a “prior” distribution over
[0,1]^{2}
.
Another is based on the
conformal test martingale

(
Vovk, 2021b
)
, denoted as
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{conf}}=(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{conf}})_{t\geq 0}
, where
\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{conf}}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}[1+\lambda(s_{i}-1/2)]
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and “conformal p-values”
s_{1},\dotsc,s_{t}\in[0,1]
.
Intuitively, these conformal p-values are computed from summary statistics of the data, and they are IID
\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
with mean
1/2
under the null but deviate from
1/2
under a changepoint alternative.
(Additional details about these e-processes are not central to our discussion, so we defer them to Section
B
.)

The manager now faces an intriguing challenge.
By design, the UI e-process is powerful against Markovian alternatives but powerless against changepoint alternatives; the opposite is true for the conformal test martingale.
Yet, unlike in the case of e-values, these two e-processes cannot be averaged straightforwardly because the conformal test martingale only has
restricted
anytime-validity, only within a sub-filtration of the data filtration.

More formally, consider the mean
\bar{m}=(\bar{m}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of the two e-processes
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{UI}}
and
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{conf}}
:

\bar{m}_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{UI}}+\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{conf}}\right],\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(3)

For each
fixed

t
,
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{m}_{t}]\leq 1
under any
P\in\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{IID}}
, so
\bar{m}
is a sequence of e-values.
However, is
\bar{m}
an e-
process
? To answer this question, we need to clarify which filtration we are working with.
The most natural candidate is the data filtration
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, where
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{t})
.
However, while the UI e-process
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{UI}}
is a well-defined e-process in
\mathbb{F}
, the conformal test martingale
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{conf}}
is not an e-process in
\mathbb{F}
.
In fact,
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{conf}}
is only an e-process in a sub-filtration
\mathbb{G}=(\mathcal{G}_{t})_{t\geq 0}\subsetneq\mathbb{F}
constructed by the conformal p-values—that is,
\mathcal{G}_{t}=\sigma(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{t})
.
In particular,
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{conf}}
is only valid at arbitrary
\mathbb{G}
-stopping times but
not
at arbitrary
\mathbb{F}
-stopping times.
Therefore,
\bar{m}
is a sequence of e-values that is not an e-process in
\mathbb{F}
.
In fact, even in the sub-filtration
\mathbb{G}
,
\bar{m}
is not an e-process because its first term,
\mathfrak{e}_{t}
, is not
\mathcal{G}_{t}
-measurable.

Empirically, we can demonstrate how
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{conf}}
does
not
satisfy the anytime-validity definition (
1
) at an
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time.
Suppose we repeatedly sample an IID data sequence from
\mathsf{Ber}(0.3)
, and consider as our stopping time
\tau^{\mathbb{F}}
the first time we observe five consecutive zeros:

\tau^{\mathbb{F}}=\inf\{t\geq 5:X_{t-4}=X_{t-3}=X_{t-2}=X_{t-1}=X_{t}=0\}.

(4)

While
\tau^{\mathbb{F}}
is a
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time, it is not a
\mathbb{G}
-stopping time because it depends directly on the individual data points and not just the conformal p-values
s_{1},\dotsc,s_{t}
. Over 100 repeated runs, we compute both the UI e-process and the conformal test martingale until they each reach
\tau^{\mathbb{F}}
.
Figure
1
shows that, while
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{UI}}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}}
remains small (
\leq 1
) across repeated runs,
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{conf}}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}}
often exceeds
1
.
Over
10,000
repeated runs, we find that the mean of
\mathfrak{e}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}}^{\mathsf{conf}}
is significantly larger than one (
1.33\pm 0.016
), while the mean of
\mathfrak{e}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}}^{\mathsf{UI}}
remains small (
0.25\pm 0.008
).
This example makes clear that an e-process in a coarser filtration (
\mathfrak{e}^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{G}
) need not be an e-process in a finer filtration (
\mathbb{F}
).

Figure 1
:
E-processes in a sub-filtration
\mathbb{G}\subsetneq\mathbb{F}
are not valid at
\mathbb{F}
-stopping times.

The plot shows 100 instances the UI e-process (solid blue) and the conformal test martingale (dashed orange) for testing randomness (
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{IID}}
), using data generated repeatedly from an IID
\mathsf{Ber}(0.3)
.
Each e-process is stopped at an
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau^{\mathbb{F}}
, the first time we observe five consecutive zeros in the data (

).
For the conformal test martingale, many stopped e-values are above
1
, even though the data comes from a null distribution; in contrast, the UI e-process remains small (
\leq 1
) at
\tau^{\mathbb{F}}
.
Over
10,000
repeated runs, the mean of stopped e-values for the conformal test martingale (vertical dashed orange) is
1.33\pm 0.016
, implying that
\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{e}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}}^{\mathsf{conf}}]>1
.

This phenomenon poses both a technical and practical challenge when combining sequentially obtained evidence across different filtrations.
On the technical side, this puts an emphasis on specifying an e-process’ underlying filtration, which is often “swept under the rug.”
On the practical side, the experimenter must be cautious about exactly what information is included in the stopping rule because accessing extra information can (either inadvertently or deliberately) invalidate the resulting test, particularly when that information “leaks” into the decision to stop the experiment.
This is impractical in a setup like Example
1
, as the experimenter then cannot look at the data itself.

Some recent works have noted the challenge of combining e-processes (or test martingales) across filtrations.
For randomness testing,
Vovk et al., 2022a
note that the mean of conformal test martingales need not be a test martingale, even when computed on the same data, as each one is constructed using differently randomized conformal p-values that yield different filtrations.
Besides randomness testing, there are at least two other practical examples in the literature, one in sequential forecast comparison
(
Henzi and Ziegel, 2022
;
Choe and Ramdas, 2023
)
and another in independence testing
(
Podkopaev et al., 2023
;
Henzi and Law, 2024
)
; we discuss these examples in Section
5
.
Yet another example is testing a scale-invariant Gaussian mean, for which different e-processes exist in different filtrations
(
Pérez-Ortiz et al., 2024
;
Wang and Ramdas, 2025a
)
.
We review this example in Section
C
.

Motivated by these examples, we study how to combine arbitrary e-processes across different filtrations (for the same null), such that their combination is an e-process in a finer filtration.
Central to our approach is the use of
adjusters

(
Shafer et al., 2011
;
Dawid et al., 2011a
;
Dawid et al., 2011b
;
Koolen and Vovk, 2014
;
Ramdas et al., 2022
)
, which are closely related to
p-to-e calibrators

(
Vovk, 1993
;
Sellke et al., 2001
;
Ramdas et al., 2020
;
Vovk and Wang, 2021
)
, that allow us to
lift
an e-process from a coarser filtration into a finer one.
This is somewhat surprising because these tools were originally devised for different purposes, namely to insure against the loss of evidence over time (for adjusters) and to convert p-values to e-values (for calibrators).

Even though we focused on practical motivation thus far, our main results have standalone implications in enhancing our theoretical understanding of both adjusters and e-processes.
First, we establish a novel characterization of adjusters that suggests adjusters are not only sufficient but also, in a formal sense,
necessary
for lifting arbitrary e-processes.
Second, our “
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting” result implies that, unlike in the case of test martingales, there always exists a powerful e-process for a testing problem in the data filtration whenever there exists one in a coarser filtration.

1.3
Related work on combining e-values (and p-values)

Many recent papers highlight how e-values, unlike p-values, can be combined seamlessly, even when they are arbitrarily dependent
(
Vovk and Wang, 2021
;
Wang and Ramdas, 2022
;
Grünwald et al., 2024
, e.g.,)
.
In particular,
Vovk and Wang, 2021
show that the arithmetic mean function, which straightforwardly yields a valid combined e-value, dominates any other symmetric “e-merging” function.
This is useful when combining two or more different e-values for a hypothesis
\mathcal{P}
and each e-value is constructed using the same data sample
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n}
.
In the sequential context, given that an e-process in a filtration
\mathbb{F}
is an e-value at any
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time, any two e-processes in the same filtration
\mathbb{F}
for a (shared) null hypothesis
\mathcal{P}
can also be combined seamlessly by averaging.
The main challenge we highlight in this work is when two e-processes are defined in two
different
filtrations, such that one (or both) e-processes must first be
lifted
into a common filtration before the averaging step.

Much of the literature also concerns cases where the e-values exhibit various dependence structures that can be exploited.
Generally, taking the product of e-values is a (weakly) dominating e-merging strategy
(
Vovk and Wang, 2021
, proposition 4.2)
when the e-values either are independent or form a supermartingale.
Taking the product is a useful strategy in sequential settings involving continuous monitoring
(
Howard et al., 2021
;
Waudby-Smith and Ramdas, 2024
)
and in meta-analysis
(
ter Schure and Grünwald, 2022
;
Grünwald et al., 2024
)
.
In this work, we focus on cases where the two e-processes are constructed using the same data sequence, so the product strategy does not apply.
Others study e-values for multiple hypotheses with different, including arbitrary, dependence structures
(
Xu et al., 2021
;
Wang and Ramdas, 2022
;
Ignatiadis et al., 2024
, e.g.,)
.
We do not generally consider such multiple testing scenarios.

Finally, there are various strategies for combining p-values
(
Vovk and Wang, 2020
, see, e.g.,)
.
Yet,
Vovk et al., 2022b
’s recent results suggest that any admissible combination function for p-values must go through e-values.
Thus, we primarily focus on e-processes, which are also the fundamental notion of evidence in SAVI
(
Ramdas et al., 2020
;
Ramdas et al., 2023
)
.
That said, we will additionally discuss the combination of e-processes with p-processes, which are the anytime-valid counterpart to p-values, as a preliminary step to our main result; this task is related to
Ignatiadis et al., 2024
’s study on combining (non-sequential) e-values and p-values for multiple testing.
As we shall see later, p-processes can be lifted “freely” across filtrations, and they appear in an intermediary step when combining e-processes across filtrations.
The underlying connection is made via p-to-e calibrators, which we review in Section
2.2
.

2
Calibrating and adjusting anytime-valid evidence

In this section, we review additional SAVI concepts as well as the notions of adjusters and calibrators. These will be central to our main results presented in subsequent sections.

2.1
SAVI part 2: P-processes, sequential tests, and Ville’s inequality

Continuing from our review of SAVI in Section
1.1

Given a family of distributions

\mathbb{F}

\mathbb{F}

\text{for any $\mathbb{F}$-stopping time $\tau$ and any $P\in\mathcal{P}$,}\quad P(\mathfrak{p}_{\tau}\leq\alpha)\leq\alpha.

(5)

\alpha

\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{F}

\{0,1\}

\text{for any $\mathbb{F}$-stopping time $\tau$ and any $P\in\mathcal{P}$,}\quad P(\phi_{\tau}=1)\leq\alpha.

(6)

As with e-processes, p-processes and sequential tests are (
\mathbb{F}

\mathbb{F}

Note that their definitions generalize their corresponding validity notions in fixed sample sizes (for p-values and tests).

2.2
Calibrating e-processes and p-processes

Combining evidence in the forms of an e-process and a p-process requires calibrating one to another.
Prior work by
Shafer et al., 2011
;
Vovk and Wang, 2021
suggests that any e-process can be calibrated into a p-process and vice versa.
Hereafter, we allow e-processes and calibrators to be defined on the extended nonnegative reals,
[0,\infty]
, and we refer the reader to
Wang and Ramdas, 2025b
for the arithmetic and measure-theoretic properties on
[0,\infty]
.

\mathfrak{e}
-to-
\mathfrak{p}
calibration.

First, any e-process
(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
can be calibrated into a p-process via either

\mathfrak{p}_{t}=\frac{1}{\mathfrak{e}_{t}}\wedge 1\quad\text{or}\quad\mathfrak{p}_{t}^{*}=\frac{1}{\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*}},

(8)

where
\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*}=\sup_{i\leq t}\mathfrak{e}_{i}
is the running maximum of the e-process and
\wedge
denotes the minimum operator.
Note that
\mathfrak{p}_{t}^{*}\leq\mathfrak{p}_{t}
, so taking the running maximum yields more powerful p-processes (though the running maximum process itself is
not
an e-process—we shall return to this soon).

\mathfrak{p}
-to-
\mathfrak{e}
calibration.

Converting a p-process into an e-process requires using a p-to-e calibrator
(
Vovk, 1993
;
Sellke et al., 2001
;
Shafer et al., 2011
;
Ramdas et al., 2020
;
Vovk and Wang, 2021
)
.
Formally, a decreasing function
\mathsf{C}:[0,1]\to[0,\infty]
is a
p-to-e calibrator
if

\int_{0}^{1}\mathsf{C}(\mathfrak{p})d\mathfrak{p}\leq 1,

(9)

and it is
admissible
(i.e., no other p-to-e calibrator strictly dominates it) if and only if it is right-continuous,
\mathsf{C}(0)=\infty
, and the condition (
9
) holds with equality
(
Vovk and Wang, 2021
, ppn. 2.1)
.
Since there is no benefit to using a non-admissible p-to-e calibrator, we restrict our attention to admissible ones hereafter, unless specified otherwise.
As its name suggests, a p-to-e calibrator
\mathsf{C}
converts any p-value
p
for some null
P
into an e-value
\mathsf{C}(p)
for
P
, and analogously, any p-process
(\mathfrak{p}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
for
\mathcal{P}
into an e-process
(\mathsf{C}(\mathfrak{p}_{t}))_{t\geq 0}
for
\mathcal{P}

(
Ramdas et al., 2020
, ppn. 14)
.

Examples of admissible p-to-e calibrators include the family of functions
\mathsf{C}_{\kappa}(\mathfrak{p})=\kappa\mathfrak{p}^{\kappa-1}
,
\kappa\in(0,1)
, and the hyperparameter-free function obtained by mixing over
\kappa
:
\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{mix}}(1)=1/2
and

\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{mix}}(\mathfrak{p})=\int_{0}^{1}\mathsf{C}_{\kappa}(\mathfrak{p})d\kappa=\frac{1-\mathfrak{p}+\mathfrak{p}\log(\mathfrak{p})}{\mathfrak{p}\log^{2}(\mathfrak{p})},\quad\forall\mathfrak{p}\in[0,1).

(10)

2.3
Adjusting the running maximum of e-processes

In general, the running maximum of a test supermartingale or an e-process is not a test supermartingale or an e-process; in fact, the expected running maximum will typically diverge to infinity under the null
(
Ramdas et al., 2022
)
.
For test supermartingales,
Dawid et al., 2011a
;
Dawid et al., 2011b
;
Shafer et al., 2011
develop a class of functions called
adjusters
, which convert the running maximum of a test supermartingale into an e-process (in the same filtration).
The papers use the terms
simple lookback adjusters
,
capital calibrators
, and
martingale calibrators
, respectively; here, we call them adjusters for brevity.
In the context of statistical inference, adjusters allow us to “insure against the loss of evidence” by trading off some amount of evidence.
This is useful when, e.g., the test supermartingale sharply decreases following a changepoint.

Formally, an
adjuster
is an increasing function
\mathsf{A}:[1,\infty]\to[0,\infty]
such that

\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e})}{\mathfrak{e}^{2}}d\mathfrak{e}\leq 1.

(11)

Furthermore,
\mathsf{A}
is
admissible
(i.e., no other adjuster dominates it on the entire domain of
[1,\infty]
) if and only if it is right-continuous,
\mathsf{A}(\infty)=\infty
, and the condition (
11
) holds with equality.

One known class of admissible adjusters is the family of functions
\mathsf{A}_{\kappa}(\mathfrak{e})=\kappa\mathfrak{e}^{1-\kappa}
, for
\kappa\in(0,1)

(
Shafer et al., 2011
)
.
Following the analogous strategy for p-to-e calibrators, we can derive a hyperparameter-free admissible adjuster by mixing over
\kappa
:
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{mix}}(1)=1/2
and

\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{mix}}(\mathfrak{e})=\int_{0}^{1}\mathsf{A}_{\kappa}(\mathfrak{e})d\kappa=\frac{\mathfrak{e}-1-\log(\mathfrak{e})}{\log^{2}(\mathfrak{e})},\quad\forall\mathfrak{e}>1.

(12)

Other examples include
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{KV}}(\mathfrak{e})=\frac{\mathfrak{e}^{2}\log 2}{(1+\mathfrak{e})\log^{2}(1+\mathfrak{e})}

(
Koolen and Vovk, 2014
)
and
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{sqrt}}(\mathfrak{e})=\sqrt{\mathfrak{e}}-1
.

In Figure
2
, we plot four admissible adjusters (in logarithmic scale):
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{mix}}
,
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{KV}}
,
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{sqrt}}
, and
\mathsf{A}_{\kappa}
for
\kappa=1/2
.
While all of these adjusters are admissible, it is apparent some grow substantially faster than others:
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{mix}}
and
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{KV}}
grow linearly in
\mathfrak{e}
, up to logarithmic factors, and thus they eventually dominate the other three variants, which only grow sublinearly in
\mathfrak{e}
.

\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{mix}}
and
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{KV}}
are close to each other, although
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{mix}}
dominates
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{KV}}
for
\mathfrak{e}\geq 4.40
.
Thus, we use the mixture adjuster
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{mix}}
as a default.
In Section
D.1
, we discuss another variant due to
Shafer et al., 2011
that grows faster than
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{mix}}
by a constant factor by completely zeroing out small values of
\mathfrak{e}
.

Note that, even when we use
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{mix}}
, we pay a logarithmic “price” relative to the original e-process.
Nevertheless, for any admissible adjuster
\mathsf{A}
, we must have
\lim_{\mathfrak{e}\to\infty}\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e})=\infty
, implying that the adjusted version of a powerful e-process is still powerful.

2.4
One-to-one correspondence between p-to-e calibrators and adjusters

Adjusters are closely related to p-to-e calibrators.
There exists a straightforward one-to-one correspondence between p-to-e calibrators and adjusters via

\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e})=\mathsf{C}\left(\frac{1}{\mathfrak{e}}\right),\quad\forall\mathfrak{e}\in[1,\infty]

(13)

(denoting
1/\infty=0
).
To see this, observe that for any p-to-e calibrator
\mathsf{C}:[0,1]\to[0,\infty]
, we have that
\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e})=\mathsf{C}(1/\mathfrak{e})
is right-continuous,
\mathsf{A}(\infty)=\mathsf{C}(0)=\infty
, and

\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e})}{\mathfrak{e}^{2}}d\mathfrak{e}=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathsf{C}(1/\mathfrak{e})}{\mathfrak{e}^{2}}d\mathfrak{e}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathsf{C}(\mathfrak{p})d\mathfrak{p}=1,

(14)

via the change-of-variables formula
\mathfrak{p}=1/\mathfrak{e}
.
Thus,
\mathsf{A}
defined according to (
13
) is an adjuster.
The analogous argument in the converse direction further shows that any adjuster
\mathsf{A}
can be mapped to a p-to-e calibrator via
\mathsf{C}(\mathfrak{p})=\mathsf{A}(1/\mathfrak{p}),\,\forall\mathfrak{p}
(denoting
1/0=\infty
).

3
Combining evidence across filtrations via lifting

In this section, we describe the two main results of this paper, referred to as
\mathfrak{p}
-lifting
and
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting
, that allow us to combine an e-process with a p- or e-process in a coarse filtration.

3.1
Combining with a p-process in a sub-filtration via
\mathfrak{p}
-lifting

We first establish that any p-process can be
lifted
2
2

2

This notion of lifting across filtrations is largely distinct from the notion of lifting in early studies of complex-valued, continuous-time martingales
(
Getoor and Sharpe, 1972
)
. See Section
E
for additional context.
into a finer filtration without calibration.
This somewhat surprising result is a direct consequence of the “equivalence lemma”
(
Ramdas et al., 2020
;
Howard et al., 2021
)
between time-uniform validity, random time validity, and anytime-validity of sequential tests.
Specifically, given a p-process
(\mathfrak{p}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
for a null hypothesis
\mathcal{P}
in a filtration
\mathbb{F}
, the lemma says that the following statements involving the probability of erroneously rejecting the null are equivalent, given any
\alpha\in(0,1)
and for each
P\in\mathcal{P}
:

1.

Time-uniform validity
:

P(\exists t\geq 1:\mathfrak{p}_{t}\leq\alpha)\leq\alpha
.

2.

Random time validity
:
For any random time
T
,
P(\mathfrak{p}_{T}\leq\alpha)\leq\alpha
.

3.

Anytime-validity
:
For any arbitrary
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau
,
P(\mathfrak{p}_{\tau}\leq\alpha)\leq\alpha
.

In our case, we can specifically use the equivalence between
2
and
3
to move from a sub-filtration to any finer filtration by avoiding the dependence on the filtration in the first place.
We summarize this result as the
lifting lemma
.
In the following, we say that a sequence of events
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is adapted to
\mathbb{G}
if
\xi_{t}\in\mathcal{G}_{t}
for
t\geq 0
, and we denote
\xi_{\infty}=\limsup_{t\to\infty}\xi_{t}=\cap_{t}\cup_{i\geq t}\xi_{i}
.

Lemma 1

(The lifting lemma)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
, and let
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a sequence of events adapted to
\mathbb{G}
.
Given a family of distributions
\mathcal{P}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, for any
P\in\mathcal{P}
, the following are equivalent:

1.

For any
\mathbb{G}
-stopping time
\tau
,
P(\xi_{\tau})\leq\alpha
.

2.

For any
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau
,
P(\xi_{\tau})\leq\alpha
.

We give a self-contained proof in Section
F.1
.
Only the lifting direction,
1

\Rightarrow

2
, is nontrivial: it states that, if any fixed upper bound on the probability of a
\mathbb{G}
-adapted event holds at all stopping times in
\mathbb{G}
, then the bound further holds at any stopping time in any finer filtration
\mathbb{F}\supseteq\mathbb{G}
.
Note that it is the coarser filtration
\mathbb{G}
that the sequence of events has to be adapted to—if
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
were adapted to
\mathbb{F}
but not
\mathbb{G}
, then the equivalence would no longer hold.
We also remark that the analogous claim involving upper bound statements about an
expectation
(e.g.,
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}]\leq 1
) is not true in general.
This necessitates the use of an adjuster when lifting e-processes (as opposed to p-processes), which we will discuss in the next subsection.

The lifting lemma directly implies the following result.

Theorem 1

(
\mathfrak{p}
-lifting)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
.
If
(\mathfrak{p}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a p-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{G}
, then
(\mathfrak{p}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a p-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{F}
.

Thus,
\mathfrak{p}
-lifting is “free”: any p-process can be lifted without calibration.
Theorem
1
follows by applying Lemma
1
to the sequence of
\mathbb{G}
-adapted events,
\xi_{t}=\{\mathfrak{p}_{t}\leq\alpha\}
for each
t
.

We note in passing that, by complete analogy to Theorem
1
, we can also “freely” lift sequential tests and even confidence sequences
(
Darling and Robbins, 1967
;
Howard et al., 2021
, CS;)
at arbitrary stopping times.
However, we do not focus on them in this work.

The p-lifting result (Theorem
1
) tells us how we can combine an e-process
\mathfrak{e}
in
\mathbb{F}
with a p-process
\mathfrak{p}
from a sub-filtration
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
.
We can first lift the p-process
\mathfrak{p}
into the
\mathbb{F}
; calibrate it into an e-process
\mathsf{C}(\mathfrak{p})
in
\mathbb{F}
via a p-to-e calibrator
\mathsf{C}
; and then combine it with the e-process
\mathfrak{e}
, using the fact that any set of e-processes in the same filtration can be combined into an e-process via averaging
(
Vovk and Wang, 2021
)
.
This is summarized in the following corollary.

Corollary 1

(Combining an e-process with a p-process in a sub-filtration)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
.
If
(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an e-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{F}
and
(\mathfrak{p}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a p-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{G}
, then for any p-to-e calibrator
\mathsf{C}
, the sequence of random variables
(\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}^{\mathsf{cal}})_{t\geq 0}
defined by

\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}^{\mathsf{cal}}=\gamma\mathfrak{e}_{t}+(1-\gamma)\mathsf{C}(\mathfrak{p}_{t}),\quad\forall t\geq 0,

(15)

for any fixed
\gamma\in[0,1]
, is an e-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{F}
.

Although
\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}^{\mathsf{cal}}
is an e-process for any fixed constant
\gamma\in[0,1]
, the default choice for
\gamma
would be to use the simple arithmetic mean (
\gamma=1/2
).
One exception is when there is a prior belief that one evidence weighs more than the other—in such a case,
\gamma
must be specified in advance.

3.2
Combining with an e-process in a sub-filtration via
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting

Next, we consider the problem of combining two e-processes, one residing in a sub-filtration of another’s filtration.
Recall Example
1
, where we saw that simply taking the average does not yield an e-process in the finer filtration.
As we remarked in Section
3.1
, we now know this is because e-processes, whose guarantee relies on an expectation, do not enjoy the same “free” lifting property as p-processes, whose guarantee relies on a probability.

How can we avoid this issue and lift the validity of an e-process in a coarser filtration to the finer filtration
\mathbb{F}
?
Surprisingly, we can do so directly by using an adjuster.
Leveraging the correspondence between adjusters and p-to-e calibrators, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 2

(
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting)
.

Let
\mathsf{A}
be an adjuster, and let
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
.
If
\mathfrak{e}=(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an e-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{G}
, then the sequence of random variables
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{adj}}=(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{adj}})_{t\geq 0}
, defined by
\mathfrak{e}_{0}^{\mathsf{adj}}=1
and

\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{adj}}=\mathsf{A}\left(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*}\right),\quad\forall t\geq 1,

(16)

is an e-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{F}
.
Here,
\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*}
denotes the running maximum,
\sup_{i\leq t}\mathfrak{e}_{i}
.

Theorem
2
establishes that, if
\mathfrak{e}
is anytime-valid in a sub-filtration
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
, then
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{adj}}
is anytime-valid in the finer filtration
\mathbb{F}
.
This implies that we can always lift the anytime-validity of an e-process to the finer filtration via adjusters.
The theorem also says an adjuster can lift an e-process to
any
finer filtration
\mathbb{F}
since the choice of
\mathbb{F}
was arbitrary.
In particular, if an e-process is constructed in a coarsening of the data filtration, then the adjusted process is anytime-valid in the data filtration, so it is valid
at any data-dependent sample sizes.

The adjusted process is even valid in any enlargement of the data filtration (
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\supseteq\mathbb{F}
), such that if the e-process we want to combine it with involves other (independent) random variables, say for external randomization, then the adjusted e-process is valid at stopping times involving them.

The proof of Theorem
2
, included in Section
F.2
, consists of three steps:

1.

\mathfrak{p}_{t}^{*}=(1/\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*})\wedge 1
is a p-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{G}
(Section
2.2
);

2.

By
\mathfrak{p}
-lifting (Theorem
1
),
\mathfrak{p}_{t}^{*}
is also a p-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{F}
; and

3.

Given an adjuster
\mathsf{A}
, there is a corresponding p-to-e calibrator
\mathsf{C}
(Section
2.4
) such that
\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*})=\mathsf{C}(\mathfrak{p}_{t}^{*})
, proving that
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}^{*})]\leq 1
for any
P\in\mathcal{P}
at any
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau
.

A useful side effect of using adjusters for
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting is that taking the running maximum of the e-process comes for free.
(In Section
5
, we empirically illustrate this side effect in a concrete example.)
Nevertheless, as alluded to in Section
2.3
, using an adjuster involves sacrificing a logarithmic amount of evidence, meaning that achieving
\mathbb{F}
-anytime-validity itself is not free.
In Section
6
, we formalize a sense in which this is a necessary cost; in Section
G.1
, we further provide an empirical study of the statistical power when using adjusters.

Analogous to
\mathfrak{p}
-lifting (Theorem
1
),
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting (Theorem
2
) yields a general recipe, dubbed the
adjust-then-combine
procedure, for combining arbitrary e-processes across filtrations.

Corollary 2

(Adjust-then-combine: Combining with an e-process in a sub-filtration)
.

Let
\mathsf{A}
be an adjuster and
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
.
If
\mathfrak{e}=(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an e-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{F}
and
\mathfrak{e}^{\prime}=(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
is an e-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{G}
, then the sequence of random variables
\bar{\mathfrak{e}}^{\mathsf{adj}}=(\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}^{\mathsf{adj}})_{t\geq 0}
, defined by
\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{0}^{\mathsf{adj}}=1
and

\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}^{\mathsf{adj}}=\gamma\mathfrak{e}_{t}+(1-\gamma)\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\prime*}),\quad\forall t\geq 1,

(17)

is an e-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{F}
, for any fixed
\gamma\in[0,1]
. Here,
\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\prime*}=\sup_{i\leq t}\mathfrak{e}_{i}^{\prime}
.

Corollary
2
establishes that we can first lift the e-process in the sub-filtration and then average the lifted e-process with the other e-process.
Note the asymmetry in (
17
): we leave the e-process
\mathfrak{e}
in
\mathbb{F}
as-is, while we take the running supremum of the e-process
\mathfrak{e}^{\prime}
and then apply the adjuster.
Despite the asymmetry, unless there are reasons
a priori
to prefer one e-process over the other, we recommend using
\gamma=1/2
as in Section
3.1
.

shows plots of the conformal test martingale
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{conf}}
(the same one used in Figure

) alongside its
\mathfrak{e}
-lifted version
\mathsf{A}((\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{conf}})^{*})
, where
\mathsf{A}
is the mixture adjuster (
12
).
The plots are drawn analogously to Figure

, showing 100 runs of the e-processes and their values at the
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau^{\mathbb{F}}
.
We use the same
\tau^{\mathbb{F}}
as before (the first time seeing five consecutive zeros in the data sequence), and we use the same data sequence as before, sampled from an IID
\mathsf{Ber}(0.3)
.

As shown in the plots, unlike the conformal test martingale, its adjusted version is mostly no greater than 1.
Over
10,000
repeated runs, the mean of the stopped e-values for the adjusted e-process is
0.47\pm 0.003
, suggesting that the adjusted e-process meets the validity definition (
1
) at this
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time.
This gives evidence that the adjusted e-process can be combined with the UI e-process via averaging, such that the combined version is an e-process in
\mathbb{F}
.

Next, we demonstrate how the combined e-process via
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting can leverage the statistical power of UI and conformal e-processes, which are powerful against different alternative hypotheses.
In Figure
5
, we compare UI, conformal, and the combined e-processes on a simulated,
non-random
binary data sequence.
Following Corollary
2
, we construct the combined e-process as
\bar{\mathfrak{e}}^{\mathsf{adj}}=(\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}^{\mathsf{adj}})_{t\geq 0}
, where
\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{0}^{\mathsf{adj}}=1
and

\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}^{\mathsf{adj}}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{UI}}+\mathsf{A}((\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{conf}})^{*})\right],\quad\forall t\geq 1.

(18)

We use two alternatives for which the UI and conformal procedures are each known to be powerful: a Markovian alternative and a changepoint alternative, respectively.

First, we generate data from a first-order Markov chain with
p_{0\to 1}=0.5
and
p_{1\to 1}=0.4
, where
p_{j\to k}
denotes the transition probability from state
j
to state
k
, for
j,k\in\{0,1\}
.
Then, in Figure
5(a)
, we plot the three e-processes, each averaged over 1,000 repeated samples of length
T=2,500
.
The plot confirms that the UI e-process (dashed blue) grows large while the conformal test martingale (dotted orange) does not; more importantly, it shows that the combined e-process (solid green) closely tracks the fast-growing UI e-process.
This is expected as the combined e-process
\bar{\mathfrak{e}}^{\mathsf{adj}}
is effectively half of the exponentially growing UI e-process
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{UI}}
.

Next, we generate data from a drifting distribution that is typically an IID
\mathsf{Ber}(0.5)
(“usual state”), but after the first 500 steps it briefly switches to an IID
\mathsf{Ber}(0.2)
(“unusual state”) for 100 steps, before returning to the usual state for the remainder of the sequence.
The resulting data contains two changepoints over the sequence of length
T=2,500
.
As mentioned earlier, conformal test martingales are known to be powerful against changepoint alternatives, unlike the UI e-process.
Figure
5(b)
, which shows the three e-processes averaged over 1,000 repeated runs, confirms this: the conformal test martingale grows large upon entering the unusual state and then gradually decreases upon reentering the usual state.
In contrast, the UI e-process remains small throughout the sequence.
Because one of the two processes grows large (in the unusual state), analogous to the Markovian case, the combined e-process also grows large.

Unlike in Figure
5(a)
, however, in Figure
5(b)
we additionally observe two peculiarities stemming from the
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting procedure.
First, because the combined e-process utilizes the
adjusted
conformal test martingale, it grows less quickly (
\approx 10^{2}
at
t=500
) than the conformal test martingale (
\approx 10^{4}
).
In this example, the adjusted evidence still exceeds a typical threshold for a “strong” evidence
(
Shafer, 2021
)
.
(When using an e-process for a level-
\alpha
sequential test (
7
), setting
\alpha
to
0.1
,
0.05
, or
0.01
corresponds to rejecting the null when the e-value exceeds 10, 20, or 100, respectively.)
Second, as a byproduct of using an adjuster, the combined e-process retains the largest evidence from the conformal test martingale over time (the running maximum).
As the data returns to its usual state (past time 600), the conformal test martingale gradually decreases, while the combined e-process eventually exceeds the conformal test martingale.
Thus, while adjusting the e-process involves sacrificing some amount of evidence, it also comes with the useful byproduct of being able to insure against losing evidence over time.

G.1
, we include a simulated study on the statistical power of tests induced by the combined e-process (
18
) against that of the simple mean without adjustment (
3
).

4.2
Testing the randomness of high-volatility days in financial returns

To test the viability of the adjust-then-combine approach in real-world applications, we revisit and expand upon the motivating example from Section
1.2
involving financial time series data.
As briefly described earlier, modeling the volatility of returns is a key problem in financial modeling, and a good volatility model is instrumental in tasks such as portfolio management, risk management, and options pricing
(
Engle and Patton, 2007
)
.
When considering a volatility model, it is useful to accompany it with a monitoring procedure that can check this model’s viability on live data, which may change unpredictably over time.

The problem of sequentially testing whether high-volatility days are distributed at random, as described in Example
1
, is a concrete example of this monitoring task.
A “stylized fact” about financial returns is that they are heteroskedastic, and specifically, that high-volatility days are often clustered together
(
Engle, 1982
, e.g.,)
.
This makes it practically important to detect exactly when (and how)
volatility clustering
happens on live data.
The task can be viewed as a diagnostic test for whether a volatility model is needed in the first place: if high-volatility days are IID, then we would not expect a volatility model to provide added predictive power.

Let
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dotsc\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
denote the daily closing prices of a financial asset, where
t
indexes each trading day (say, starting January 1, 2019).
It is standard practice to model the daily
(log-)returns

R_{t}=\log(Y_{t}/Y_{t-1})
, for each day
t\geq 1
.
In such a model, a simple way to quantify the
volatility
of returns is by their absolute magnitude:
V_{t}=\left\lvert R_{t}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\log(Y_{t}/Y_{t-1})\right\rvert
.
Then, we can define an indicator for a
high-volatility
trading day using a pre-defined threshold
c
:

X_{t}=\mathds{1}\left(V_{t}\geq c\right)=\mathds{1}\left(\left\lvert R_{t}\right\rvert\geq c\right),\quad\forall t\geq 1.

(19)

In this experiment, we choose
c
as the 80% upper quantile of the historical returns for the same asset, from a 1-year period before the first date (say, during the year 2018).
In Figure
6
, we plot the volatility process
V_{t}
and the high-volatility days
X_{t}
(marked as red) for the S&P 500 index, which shows volatility clustering around March 2020 and during 2022.

Figure 6
:
Volatility (magnitude of daily log-returns)
V_{t}
(gray) and high-volatility days
X_{t}=\mathds{1}\left(V_{t}\geq c\right)
(marked in red) for the S&P 500 index, from January 1, 2019, to December 31, 2023. The threshold
c
is chosen as the 80% upper quantile of volatility during the year 2018.

Our goal is to sequentially test whether high-volatility days are distributed at random, that is, test the null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{IID}}
(
2
).
To test this null, we can combine a UI e-process and a conformal test martingale so that we can detect either Markovian dependence or changepoints.

Figure 7
:
E-processes (in log-scale) for the randomness of high-volatility days (
2
) in the returns of four financial assets, from 2019 to 2023.
The combined e-process,
\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}^{\mathsf{adj}}=\frac{1}{2}[\mathfrak{e}_{t}+\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{mix}}((\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{conf}})^{*})]
(solid green), is the mean of the UI e-process
\mathfrak{e}_{t}
(dashed blue) and the
adjusted
conformal test martingale
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{mix}}((\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{conf}})^{*})
(dotted orange).
The combined e-process grows large whenever either of its components “react” to different deviations from randomness.

Using data from various real-world financial assets, we compute the UI e-process
\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{UI}}
, the
adjusted
conformal test martingale
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{mix}}((\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{conf}})^{*})
, and their combined average e-process
\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}^{\mathsf{adj}}=\frac{1}{2}[\mathfrak{e}_{t}+\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{mix}}((\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{conf}})^{*})]
, for each
t=1,\dotsc,T
where
t
indexes all trading days from January 1, 2019 to December 31, 2023 (5 years total).
Figure
7
shows these e-processes for four financial assets: the S&P 500 index (top left), Amazon stock (top right), Nike stock (bottom left), and the US Dollar to Chinese Yuan (USD-CNY) exchange rate (bottom right).

These cases illustrate different ways in which the combined e-process, via
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting, leverages the statistical power of its components.
For S&P 500 and Nike, the UI e-process grows more quickly than the adjusted conformal test martingale around March 2020 (coinciding with the global Covid-19 outbreak), suggesting that the cluster of high-volatility days was more likely Markovian than IID during this time.
On the other hand, during the early-to-middle part of 2022 (coinciding with peak inflation rates in the U.S.), the UI e-process decreases, while the adjusted conformal test martingale grows large, suggesting the presence of a changepoint in volatility during this time.
Across both periods, only the combined e-process enjoys the growth of either e-process; if we were running these experiments live, we would have successfully detected both types of deviations from IID.
Amazon and USD-CNY present different cases, where the adjusted conformal test martingale grows either comparably to the UI e-process (Amazon in 2020) or more quickly (Amazon in 2022; USD-CNY in 2023).
The combined e-process, by construction, enjoys growth in either e-process (up to a constant multiplicative factor).

While we focused on a binarized notion of volatility for simplicity, we can apply an analogous procedure to the continuous-valued residuals from a volatility model, by replacing the UI e-process with
Saha and Ramdas, 2024
’s e-process (powerful against autoregressive alternatives) and using the continuous analog of
Vovk, 2021b
’s conformal test martingale.

5
Two more representative use cases

The
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting result is not specific to the problem of testing randomness; rather, it applies to any case where we want to combine arbitrary e-processes across different filtrations.
Here, we describe two different types of sequential testing problems for which
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting can be useful.

5.1
Combining e-processes for sequential independence testing

The first example comes from sequential (nonparametric) independence testing
(
Balsubramani and Ramdas, 2016
;
Podkopaev et al., 2023
;
Podkopaev and Ramdas, 2023
;
Shekhar and Ramdas, 2024
;
Henzi and Law, 2024
)
.
This represents a scenario where
both
e-processes are constructed in a coarsening of the data filtration. By a complete analogy to Corollary
2
, we can combine these e-processes by adjusting each and then averaging them.

Example 2

(Sequentially testing independence)
.

Suppose we have an IID stream of paired observations
(X_{t},Y_{t})\sim P_{XY}
in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
, for
t=1,2,\dotsc
.
For example, an economist may be interested in tracking the dependence between market volatility and inflation rates; a meteorologist may be interested in monitoring the relationship between temperature and precipitation levels.
Our goal is to sequentially test whether the joint distribution factorizes:

\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{indep}}:P_{XY}=P_{X}\times P_{Y}\quad\text{vs.}\quad\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\mathsf{indep}}:P_{XY}\neq P_{X}\times P_{Y}.

(20)

This is a challenging problem:
Henzi and Law, 2024
show that it is impossible to construct nontrivial test martingales in the data filtration
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\,\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{t})
.
(In fact,
Ramdas et al., 2022
show the analogous statement for randomness testing.)
For independence testing, the added challenge is that all known nontrivial e-processes, not just test martingales, are constructed by coarsening
\mathbb{F}
.
Nevertheless, we still want to leverage the statistical power of different e-processes, so that we can detect different deviations from independence.

Recent work has developed two different types of e-processes for the independence null.
First,
Podkopaev et al., 2023
construct an e-process
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{pair}}=(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{pair}})_{t\geq 0}
for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{indep}}
based on the idea of betting on
pairs
of incoming data points (“pairwise betting”):
\mathfrak{e}_{0}^{\mathsf{pair}}=1
and

\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{pair}}=\prod_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor t/2\right\rfloor}\left[1+\lambda_{i}f_{i}\left((X_{2i-1},Y_{2i-1}),(X_{2i},Y_{2i})\right)\right],\quad\forall t\geq 1,

(21)

where
\lambda_{i}\in[-1,1]
is (
\mathcal{F}_{2i-2}
)-measurable and
f_{i}\in[-1,1]
is a payoff function that takes a pair of data points as inputs.
The payoff is expected to be zero whenever
X_{2i}
and
Y_{2i}
, as well as
X_{2i-1}
and
Y_{2i-1}
, are independent.
Crucial to our discussion is that this e-process is adapted in a sub-filtration that “updates” once every even number of steps:
\mathbb{F}^{[2]}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
, where
\mathbb{F}^{[k]}=(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{[k]})_{t\geq 0}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{[k]}=\mathcal{F}_{\left\lfloor t/k\right\rfloor k}
.
With this e-process, one can only stop at even times and not at odd times.

On the other hand,
Henzi and Law, 2024
develop a test martingale
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{rank}}=(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{rank}})_{t\geq 0}
for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{indep}}
that is based on the sequential
ranks
of the data.
Naturally, this e-process is only valid in a sub-filtration
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
generated by the ranks.
In their experiments comparing the rank-based test martingale with the pairwise betting e-process, the authors remark that
“neither of the methods uniformly dominate each other.”

This motivates the use of a combined e-process.

Since the two e-processes reside in different sub-filtrations of
\mathbb{F}
, we would need to adjust both before averaging them: given any adjusters
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{A}^{\prime}
(say,
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{A}^{\prime}=\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{mix}}
),

\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{pair}})+\mathsf{A}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{rank}})\right],\quad\forall t,

(22)

is an e-process for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{indep}}
in the data filtration
\mathbb{F}
(i.e., at any data-dependent stopping times).

We note in closing that the recent work by
Saha and Ramdas, 2024
also utilize pairwise betting but for testing randomness. The procedure described in this subsection would provide a way to combine their e-process with the UI and conformal e-processes in Section
4
.

5.2
Evaluating and comparing
h
-step-ahead sequential forecasters

The next example represents a scenario where different e-processes, say
\mathfrak{e}^{[k]}
for each
k
, are constructed in different sub-filtrations of the data filtration, say
\mathbb{F}^{[k]}\subsetneq\mathbb{F}
.
In particular, we consider the problem of forecast evaluation, which is a classical task in econometrics, meteorology, and other areas
(
Diebold and Mariano, 1995
, see, e.g.,)
.
Recent works have found useful synergies between this classical problem and the modern tools of SAVI.
These works develop e-processes and sequential tests for testing whether one forecaster uniformly outperforms the other over time
(
Henzi and Ziegel, 2022
)
; testing whether a forecaster is calibrated
(
Arnold et al., 2023
)
; and continuously monitoring the mean score difference between two forecasters
(
Choe and Ramdas, 2023
)
.
It turns out that all three results implicitly utilize the
\mathfrak{p}
-lifting procedure; here, we build upon their results and describe how we can obtain e-processes directly via
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting.

We proceed with a concrete example based on the formulations of
Choe and Ramdas, 2023
and
Arnold et al., 2023
.
Let
h>1
be an integer lag, and suppose that at each time step
t=1,2,\dotsc
, two forecasters
\mathsf{p}
and
\mathsf{q}
make respective probability forecasts
p_{t},q_{t}\in[0,1]
for an eventual binary outcome
y_{t+h-1}\in\{0,1\}
, say, whether it will rain on the
(t+h-1)
th day.
The underlying distribution for the outcome sequence is not known.
Let
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the filtration with which both
p_{t}
and
q_{t}
are
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable—that is,
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
represents the information available to each forecaster before making their forecast on
y_{t+h-1}
.

Example 3

(Comparing
h
-step-ahead sequential forecasters)
.

Consider evaluating two
h
-step-ahead forecasters with the Brier score
\mathsf{s}(p,y)=(p-y)^{2}
.
For each
t\geq h
, define

\Delta_{t}^{[k]}=\frac{1}{\left\lvert I_{t}^{[k]}\right\rvert}\sum_{i\in I_{t}^{[k]}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathsf{s}(p_{i},y_{i+h-1})-\mathsf{s}(q_{i},y_{i+h-1})\left.\middle|\right.\mathcal{F}_{i-1}}\right],\quad\forall k\in\{1,\dotsc,h\},

(23)

where
I_{t}^{[k]}
is an index set that includes every
h
th time step starting at
k
up to
t-h+1
.
The parameter
\Delta_{t}^{[k]}
denotes the mean conditional expectation of score differences between the two forecasters up to time
t
at offset
k
.
For
h=2
,
\Delta_{t}^{[1]}
and
\Delta_{t}^{[2]}
represent the mean score difference on odd and even days, respectively.
We condition the scores of
p_{i}
and
q_{i}
on
\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
to adequately capture the information set available to the forecasters
at the time of their forecasting
.

Now, suppose we aim to sequentially test the null hypothesis that asserts, for each time offset
k\in\{1,\dotsc,h\}
, forecaster
\mathsf{p}
is no better than forecaster
\mathsf{q}
on average over time:

\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{pw}}:\Delta_{t}^{[k]}\leq 0,\quad\forall t\geq 1,\quad\forall k\in\{1,\dotsc,h\}.

(24)

For example, if
h=2
, then the null asserts that
\mathsf{p}
is no better than
\mathsf{q}
on odd
and
even days.
For each offset
k
,
Choe and Ramdas, 2023
construct an e-process
\mathfrak{e}^{[k]}=(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{[k]})_{t\geq 0}
for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{pw}}
in a sub-filtration
\mathbb{F}^{[k]}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
.
The sub-filtration
\mathbb{F}^{[k]}
updates its information every
h
steps, starting at time
k
(if
h=2
, then
\mathbb{F}^{[1]}=(\mathcal{F}_{0},\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{3},\mathcal{F}_{3},\mathcal{F}_{5},\mathcal{F}_{5},\dotsc)
).
For
h>1
, this filtration design due to
Arnold et al., 2023
is necessary because, at the time of forecasting
y_{t+h-1}
, we do not have access to the intermediary outcomes
y_{t},\dotsc,y_{t+h-2}
that can affect any
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time.

For each
k
, the e-process
\mathfrak{e}^{[k]}
captures evidence from the forecasts made for the offset
k
(e.g., all odd days).
Therefore, for a holistic evaluation, we would want to combine all
h
e-processes,
\mathfrak{e}^{[k]}
for
k\in\{1,\dotsc,h\}
, even though they each live in a different sub-filtration.

This strategy of constructing
h
e-processes for each offset
k
is common to the three aforementioned approaches to forecast evaluation.
With the
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting strategy at hand, we can now lift each
\mathfrak{e}^{[k]}
to
\mathbb{F}
and then average all of them, obtaining one combined e-process for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{pw}}
in
\mathbb{F}
:

\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}=\frac{1}{h}\sum_{k=1}^{h}\mathsf{A}\left(\sup_{i\leq t}\mathfrak{e}_{i}^{[k]}\right),\quad\forall t.

(25)

This e-process is expected to be bounded by 1 at any
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time, as long as forecaster
\mathsf{p}
outperforms
\mathsf{q}
on average across all offsets (that is, under
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{pw}}
), but it may grow large if
\mathsf{q}
outperforms
\mathsf{p}
on at least one offset (e.g., on Sundays).

The
h
-step-ahead forecast comparison problem presents a unique application of
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting, as there are ways to bypass the construction of a combined e-process if one only cares about constructing a sequential test.
In particular, as noted (implicitly) in prior work, we may directly utilize the “free”
\mathfrak{p}
-lifting procedure (Theorem
1
) given the particular problem setup.
To be clear, if our goal is to construct a combined e-process instead, then using an adjuster (
25
) remains the most straightforward and effective way.
We elaborate on these points and include an empirical comparison of the resulting approaches, in Section
G.2
.

6
Characterizing adjusters as
\mathfrak{e}
-lifters

In the early works of
Dawid et al., 2011a
;
Dawid et al., 2011b
;
Shafer et al., 2011
;
Koolen and Vovk, 2014
, an adjuster is defined as a function that turns the running maximum of a test supermartingale into a process that is upper-bounded by another test supermartingale.
These works show that their definition is equivalent to the analytic definition of adjusters (
11
).
We shall refer to their definition as
game-theoretic
because it views a test supermartingale as the wealth of a skeptic who bets against the null.
In this view, an adjuster is a function that turns the running maximum of a skeptic’s wealth into a valid bet (i.e., the skeptic is not expected to make money if the data comes from the null).
We include a game-theoretic interpretation of
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting in Section
H
.

Here, our main question is how this game-theoretic characterization of adjusters relates, if at all, to their ability to lift e-processes across filtrations.
The first point of connection is that the existence of an upper-bounding test supermartingale is a characterizing property of e-processes
(
Ramdas et al., 2020
, lemma 6)
.
Then, in our context, an adjuster can be understood as a function that maps the running maximum of a test supermartingale, for
P
in
\mathbb{G}
, into an e-process for
P
in
\mathbb{G}
(the same filtration).
Then, by generalizing this definition to a condition that only involves e-processes, we will ultimately show how the game-theoretic notion is equivalent to a function being an
\mathfrak{e}
-lifter
that can lift arbitrary e-processes across filtrations.

Characterizing adjusters as
\mathfrak{e}
-lifters gives us a (partial) converse to Theorem
2
.
As illustrated earlier, adjusting an e-process is not entirely free because we sacrifice some evidence to achieve validity in the finer filtration.
A natural question is then:

Is using an adjuster
necessary
to lift e-processes to finer filtrations?

The characterization establishes the extent to which the loss of evidence by an adjuster is necessary.
We will see that, if an increasing function takes in the running maximum of an arbitrary e-process and makes it anytime-valid in a finer filtration, then the function
must
be an adjuster.

6.1
Known characterization of adjusters for test supermartingales

We begin by reviewing the notion of “adjusters for test supermartingales.”
This appears in each of the aforementioned early works, although the work of
Dawid et al., 2011b
is the most relevant to our setup.
Their work (theorem 1) shows that an increasing function
\mathsf{A}:[1,\infty]\to[0,\infty]
is an adjuster
if and only if
, for any probability distribution
P
and for any test supermartingale
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
for
P
in some filtration
\mathbb{G}
, there exists another test supermartingale
(M_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
for
P
in
\mathbb{G}
that upper-bounds the adjusted running maximum process
(\mathsf{A}(M_{t}^{*}))_{t\geq 0}
, that is,

\mathsf{A}(M_{t}^{*})\leq M_{t}^{\prime},\quad\text{$P$-a.s.},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(26)

Dawid et al., 2011b
;
Shafer et al., 2011
use this characterization as the definition and prove its equivalence to our definition (
11
).
This alternative definition is now in terms of test supermartingales in some (arbitrary) probability and filtration, whereas definition (
11
) was purely analytic.
It also does not describe whether the analogous characterization for e-processes would be equivalent, or how it relates to the fact that adjusters can perform
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting across filtrations.

6.2
The main characterization theorem

We now introduce the main result of this section, synthesizing the two characterizations of adjusters with Theorem
2
.
The takeaway is that all notions of adjusters coincide,
including their characterization as
\mathfrak{e}
-lifters,
as long as
\mathsf{A}
is an increasing function applied to the running maximum of an e-process.

Theorem 3

(Equivalent characterizations of adjusters for e-processes)
.

Let
\mathsf{A}:[1,\infty]\to[0,\infty]
be an increasing function.
Then, the following statements are equivalent:

1.

\mathsf{A}
is an adjuster in the sense of (
11
), that is, it satisfies
\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e})}{\mathfrak{e}^{2}}d\mathfrak{e}\leq 1
.

2.

\mathsf{A}
is an adjuster for test supermartingales in the sense of (
26
).

3.

\mathsf{A}
is an
adjuster for e-processes
. That is, for any
\mathcal{P}
, and for any e-process
(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
for
\mathcal{P}
in some filtration
\mathbb{G}
, there exists an e-process
(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{G}
such that

\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*})\leq\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\prime},\quad\text{$P$-a.s.},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(27)

4.

For any
\mathcal{P}
, and for any e-process
(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
for
\mathcal{P}
in some filtration
\mathbb{G}
, the sequence
(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{adj}})_{t\geq 0}
, defined as
\mathfrak{e}_{0}^{\mathsf{adj}}=1
and
\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{adj}}=\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*})
for
t\geq 1
, is an e-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{G}
.

5.

(
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting characterization.)
For any
\mathcal{P}
, suppose that
(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an e-process for
\mathcal{P}
in some filtration
\mathbb{G}
.
Then, for any finer filtration
\mathbb{F}\supseteq\mathbb{G}
, the sequence
(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{adj}})_{t\geq 0}
, defined as
\mathfrak{e}_{0}^{\mathsf{adj}}=1
and
\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{adj}}=\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*})
for
t\geq 1
, is an e-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{F}
.

Furthermore,
\mathsf{A}
is admissible according to any one of these characterizations if and only if
\mathsf{A}
is right-continuous,
\mathsf{A}(\infty)=\infty
, and
\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e})}{\mathfrak{e}^{2}}d\mathfrak{e}=1
.

As discussed earlier, statements
1
and
2
are equivalent.
Further, the
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting theorem says that
1
implies
5
.
Notice also that
5
trivially implies
4
.
It then suffices to show two nontrivial statements, which we prove in Section
F.3
, that connect the rest of the dots:

•

4

\Rightarrow

27
: if a function maps the running maximum of an arbitrary e-process to an e-process (in the same filtration), then the function must be an adjuster for e-processes.

•

27

\Rightarrow

2
:
\mathsf{A}
is an adjuster for test supermartingales if it is also an adjuster for e-processes.

The proof of the latter statement leverages a technical lemma from
Ramdas et al., 2020
that connects test supermartingales and e-processes.
Given these two implications, we can establish that all five conditions are equivalent, via
1

\Rightarrow

5

\Rightarrow

4

\Rightarrow

27

\Rightarrow

2

\Leftrightarrow

1
.
The equivalence implies that admissibility according to any characterization must coincide with each other.

Theorem
3
establishes an extent to which the use of an adjuster is
necessary
for lifting evidence: the implication
5

\Rightarrow

1
says that, if
f
is some increasing function that maps the running maximum of any e-process in
\mathbb{G}
into an e-process in any
\mathbb{F}\supseteq\mathbb{G}
, for an arbitrary
\mathcal{P}
, then
f

must
be an adjuster.
Further, even if we found an increasing function
f
that maps, for any
\mathcal{P}
, the running maximum of any e-process in
\mathbb{G}
into an e-process in
some

\mathbb{F}\supseteq\mathbb{G}
, then we can still be assured that
f
is an adjuster because
f
satisfies condition
4
.
Put together, we may conclude that, when it comes to mapping the running maximum of arbitrary e-processes to a valid e-process in some finer filtration, using an adjuster is necessary.

For conditions
2
through
5
, it is not enough that they hold for specific choices of the null hypothesis
\mathcal{P}
(or
P
) or the e-process, for the function to be an adjuster.
Indeed, there can be some function
f
that can
\mathfrak{e}
-lift particular types of e-processes or for a particular null
\mathcal{P}
; such functions need not be adjusters and thus may not be
\mathfrak{e}
-lifters in other cases.

Finally, the implication
4

\Rightarrow

5
suggests that an adjustment is only needed once and not more than once.
This makes sense because the definition of an adjuster
1
also does not depend on the specific choice of the filtration
\mathbb{G}
.
It also explains why the same adjuster can be used to lift any e-process at arbitrary levels of coarseness to, say, the data filtration.

We close with the note that the characterization of adjusters and
\mathfrak{e}
-lifters in Theorem

is specific to (increasing) functions that apply to the running maximum of e-processes.
Thus, in theory, the theorem does not rule out the existence of an
\mathfrak{e}
-lifter that depends on, say,
\mathfrak{e}_{t}
but not
\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*}
, or other functions with entirely different forms.
In Section
D.2
, we explore a class of generalized adjusters
(
Dawid et al., 2011a
)
that take both
\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*}
and
\mathfrak{e}_{t}
as inputs, and we show how these are
not

\mathfrak{e}

Identifying alternate forms of

7
Discussion

We close by discussing both the theoretical and practical implications of our main results.

When do powerful e-processes exist for a composite testing problem?

The
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting result establishes the answer to a theoretical question that, to the best of our knowledge, was not previously known in its generality.
To motivate the question, we recall that when it comes to test martingales (
not
e-processes), there are testing problems for which there exist no powerful test martingales in the data filtration while there do exist powerful test martingales in a coarser filtration, such as
Vovk, 2021b
’s conformal test martingale for randomness and
Henzi and Law, 2024
’s test martingale for independence.
We may ask the analogous question for e-processes:

Are there testing problems for which there is no powerful e-process in the data filtration but there exist ones in a coarser filtration?

Theorem
2
implies that the answer is “no”: if we can find a powerful e-process in a sub-filtration, then we can always
\mathfrak{e}
-lift it into the data filtration using an adjuster.
Since any admissible adjuster
\mathsf{A}
satisfies
\lim_{\mathfrak{e}\to\infty}\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e})=\infty
by definition (
11
), we can be assured that the e-process lifted by
\mathsf{A}
is also powerful.
In general, adjusting a test martingale in a sub-filtration yields an e-process but
not
a test martingale in the data filtration, so this result does not contradict prior results on how there exist no powerful test martingales for randomness or independence.

A practical drawback of coarsening the filtration to design e-processes

One motivation for our study is that an e-process (or a test martingale) designed in a coarse filtration is not
truly
immune to data peeking, even though a proposed key benefit of using an anytime-valid method in the first place is the ability to freely peek at the data and deciding when to stop.
Yet, if we were to lift an e-process from a coarse filtration to achieve anytime-validity at the data level, we generally need to go through an adjuster (Theorem
3
), which may hurt the power when there is only small data or weak evidence.
In other words, depending on the use case, designing e-processes in a sub-filtration does not come without a cost, as it limits the flexibility of the experimenter’s actions during a sequential experiment.
Of course, some approaches like pairwise betting can avoid this issue in practice, and for certain composite nulls (e.g., independence), coarsening the filtration remains the only known way of sequentially testing them.

Randomized adjustments of e-processes

Since adjusters sacrifice some amount of evidence, we may consider leveraging external randomization to recover the loss in statistical power.
In Section
I
, we explore this idea in two ways; here, we briefly summarize our findings.
The first approach is to borrow ideas from
Ramdas and Manole, 2024
, who utilize an independent
\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
random variable
U
and use the threshold of
U/\alpha
for testing at a stopping time.
This “lift-then-randomize” approach is straightforwardly applicable to the adjusted e-process.
The second approach is to build upon the ideas of
Ignatiadis et al., 2024
, who develop a randomized e-to-p calibration method for e-values.
However, we find that the resulting “randomize-then-lift” approach does not generally yield anytime-valid procedures when applied to
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting.

When should we consider alternatives to adjusters?

Theorem
3
suggests that adjusters may be necessary for lifting arbitrary e-processes across filtrations.
Yet, there is room for exploring alternative ways in certain setups.
The
h
-step-ahead forecast comparison problem from Section
5.2
presents one such example: if we only care about constructing a sequential test,
and
if there is a way to naturally combine the evidence in the
\mathfrak{p}
-domain, then we can instead leverage
\mathfrak{p}
-lifting, and the resulting procedure may achieve good statistical power.

At a high level, we can imagine two possible directions for future research.
The first is to develop an
\mathfrak{e}
-lifter that is more powerful than an adjuster, either by not utilizing the running maximum or by exploiting specific structures of the problem.
The second is to avoid
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting by “un-lifting” to the coarser filtration.
Suppose we combine an e-process
\mathfrak{e}_{t}
in
\mathbb{F}
with another e-process
\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
.
Then, the sequence
\tilde{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{e}_{t}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t}]
,
\forall t
, is an e-process in
\mathbb{G}
, so it can be combined with
\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{G}
without an adjuster.
This process does not have validity at arbitrary
\mathbb{F}
-stopping times, but it can be converted into a p-process and then
\mathfrak{p}
-lifted for sequential testing.
The main challenge is that the conditional expectation would have to be computable in practice.

Appendix B
Details on e-processes for sequentially testing randomness

B.1
Problem setup

Given a stream of binary random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\dotsc
, we are interested in sequentially testing the randomness (IID-ness) of this sequence:

\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{IID}}:X_{1},X_{2},\dotsc\text{ are IID.}

(28)

We remark that any nontrivial e-process for the IID null must be a nontrivial e-process for sequentially testing whether the data forms an exchangeable sequence
(
Ramdas et al., 2022
)
.
Recall that a sequence of random variables
(X_{t})_{t\geq 1}
is
exchangeable
if, for any
t\geq 1
and any permutation
\sigma
over
\{1,\dotsc,t\}
,
(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{t})
has the same distribution as
(X_{\sigma(1)},\dotsc,X_{\sigma(t)})
.
Then, we may equivalently consider the following null:

\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{exch}}:X_{1},X_{2},\dotsc\text{ are exchangeable.}

(29)

In the below, let
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the data filtration such that
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{t})
.

B.2
The universal inference e-process

Ramdas et al., 2022
derive an e-process for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{exch}}
in the data filtration
\mathbb{F}
that is powerful against Markovian alternatives by leveraging universal inference
(
Wasserman et al., 2020
, UI;)
.
The high-level idea of UI is to compute a generalized likelihood ratio, whose denominator is the maximum likelihood under the null and the numerator is a mixture
(
Robbins, 1970
)
over a class of alternatives.
For the denominator, it suffices to use the maximum likelihood under the IID family
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{IID}}=\{\mathsf{Ber}(p)^{\infty}:p\in[0,1]\}
, whose convex hull is precisely
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{exch}}
due to
de Finetti, 1931
’s theorem, and then invoke
Ramdas et al., 2020
’s result that says any e-process for a family of distributions (e.g.,
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{IID}}
) is also an e-process for its convex hull (
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{exch}}
).
For the numerator,
Ramdas et al., 2022
choose a tractable mixture distribution over all first-order Markovian alternatives, mixing over their two parameters
p_{0\to 1}
and
p_{1\to 1}
, where
p_{j\to k}
denotes the transition probability from state
j
to state
k
for
j,k\in\{0,1\}
.

Put together, the UI e-process
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{UI}}=(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{UI}})_{t\geq 0}
for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{exch}}
in
\mathbb{F}
is given by the following closed-form expression:
\mathfrak{e}_{0}^{\mathsf{UI}}=1
, and for each
t\geq 1
,

\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{UI}}=\frac{\Gamma\left(n_{0\to 0}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(n_{1\to 0}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(n_{0\to 1}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(n_{1\to 1}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{4}\Gamma\left(n_{0\to 0}+n_{0\to 1}+1\right)\Gamma\left(n_{1\to 0}+n_{1\to 1}+1\right)}\bigg/\left(\left(\frac{n_{1}}{t}\right)^{n_{1}}\left(\frac{n_{0}}{t}\right)^{n_{0}}\right),

(30)

where given the observed data
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{t}
up to time
t
,
n_{j}
denotes the number of observations for the value
j\in\{0,1\}
and
n_{j\to k}
denotes the number of transitions from
j
to
k
for
j,k\in\{0,1\}
.

\Gamma
denotes the usual gamma function.

The UI e-process is the first known nontrivial e-process in the data filtration for a composite null, where there exist no nontrivial test supermartingale in the data filtration for that null.

B.3
Conformal test martingales and the “simple jumper” algorithm

Vovk et al., 2003
;
Vovk, 2021b
introduce a sequential testing procedure for exchangeability entirely based on conformal p-values.
At each time
t
, define the
nonconformity score

\alpha_{t}=\mathcal{A}(X_{t};X_{1},\dotsc,X_{t})\in\mathbb{R}
.
For general data types,
\mathcal{A}
can be any measure for the “strangeness” of
X_{t}
alongside the observed data
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{t}
; for our binary case, we follow the prior works and use the identity function, that is,
\alpha_{t}=X_{t}
for each
t\geq 1

(
Vovk, 2021a
)
.
Then, the
conformal p-value

s_{t}
at time
t
is defined using the nonconformity scores
\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{t}
as follows:

\displaystyle s_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\left[\#\left\{i\leq t:\alpha_{i}>\alpha_{t}\right\}+U_{t}\cdot\#\left\{i\leq t:\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{t}\right\}\right]

(31)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}U_{t}\cdot f_{1}(t)&\mbox{if }X_{t}=1;\\
f_{1}(t)+U_{t}\cdot f_{0}(t)&\mbox{if }X_{t}=0,\end{cases}

(32)

where
f_{k}(t)=t^{-1}\cdot\#\{i\leq t:X_{i}=k\}
denotes the empirical frequency of seeing
k\in\{0,1\}
up to time
t
, and
U_{t}\sim\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
is an IID Uniform random variable that is independent of
\mathbb{F}
.
Note that
s_{t}
is based on summary statistics of the data up to time
t
along with external randomization.
Equation (
31
) is a general definition for conformal p-values, while equation (
32
) represents a simplification assuming that the nonconformity score is the identity function and that the data is binary.
By
Vovk et al., 2003
;
Vovk et al., 2022a
, the conformal p-values
(s_{t})_{t\geq 1}
are IID with
\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
under the null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{IID}}
.
Despite its name, the conformal p-value is not a p-value in the traditional sense, and under changepoint alternatives, it deviates from
1/2
to either 0
or
1.

Next, define the filtration
\mathbb{G}=(\mathcal{G}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
consisting of
\sigma
-fields generated by the conformal p-values, that is,
\mathcal{G}_{t}=\sigma(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{t})
.
Then, in
\mathbb{G}
, we can construct test martingales (e-processes) for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathsf{IID}}
of the following form:
\mathfrak{e}_{0}(\lambda)=1
and

\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{conf}}(\lambda)=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\left[1+\lambda\left(s_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right],\quad\forall t\geq 1,

(33)

for any fixed
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
The scalar
\lambda
determines the sign and amount of the “bet” on the deviation of each
s_{i}
from its mean of
1/2
under the null hypothesis.
In Figure
1
, we used the value
\lambda=1
, corresponding to betting when
p_{i}
exceeds its expected mean under the null hypothesis.

In practice, since a weighted average of e-processes in
\mathbb{G}
is also an e-process in
\mathbb{G}
, we can take a mixture of
(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{conf}}(\lambda))_{t\geq 0}
defined using different values of
\lambda
.

Vovk et al., 2021
defines the “simple jumper” conformal test martingale as

\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{jump}}=w_{-1}\mathfrak{e}_{t}(-1)+w_{0}\mathfrak{e}_{t}(0)+w_{1}\mathfrak{e}_{t}(1),\quad\forall t\geq 0,

(34)

for some fixed weights
w_{-1},w_{0},w_{1}\geq 0
that sum to 1, or alternatively notice that (
33
) is also an e-process for any
\mathbb{G}
-predictable sequence
(\lambda_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and use clever choices of the hyperparameter
(
Fedorova et al., 2012
;
Waudby-Smith and Ramdas, 2024
, e.g.,)
.

The e-process we use in Example
1
corresponds to the simple jumper with weights
w_{1}=1
and
w_{0}=w_{-1}=0
.
The mean of stopped e-values (right plot of Figure
1
) also exceeds 1 when
w_{-1}=w_{1}=1/2
and the stopping time is the first time when
k
consecutive zeros
or
ones are observed, although the standard error over repeated runs is substantially larger.

Vovk, 2021a
’s original version, with
w_{-1}=w_{0}=w_{1}=1/3
, does not empirically violate the stopping time validity in the data filtration
\mathbb{F}
, despite not having
\mathbb{F}
-anytime-validity in theory.
For the simulations under the alternative hypothesis (Figures
5
and
9
), and for the real data experiments (Section
4.2
), we use the original version with
w_{-1}=w_{0}=w_{1}=1/3
.

We note that, in this paper, the specific choice of the simple jumper conformal test martingale is made solely for the clarity of exposition, and the naïve simple jumper algorithm is by no means the optimal version of the conformal test martingale.
There exist other variants of conformal test martingales, including ones powerful against other types of alternatives; see
Vovk et al., 2022a
.
Yet, as referenced in the main text, even when combining different instantiations of conformal test martingales, the same issue of not having validity at data-dependent stopping times would still arise.

B.4
Why we do not need to lift the UI e-process

Technically speaking, when we later combine the UI e-process and the conformal test martingale in Section
4
, we implicitly combine them in an enlargement of the data filtration
\mathbb{F}
that also includes the independent Uniform random variables,
(U_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, used for constructing the conformal test martingale (
31
).
We may denote this enlarged filtration as
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
, such that
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}_{t}\vee\sigma(U_{t})
for each
t\geq 1
; the conformal test martingale is adapted to
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}
but not
\mathbb{F}
.
Even though the UI e-process is originally defined in
\mathbb{F}
, we do not need to additionally
\mathfrak{e}
-lift the UI e-process to
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}
simply because it is also an e-process in
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}
in this particular case.
Since the UI e-process
(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is independent of
(U_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, stopping in an independent random variable(s) is effectively the same as stopping in a constant.
Thus, the UI e-process is still valid at arbitrary
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}
-stopping times, making the combined e-process (via Corollary
2
) anytime-valid in
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}
.

Appendix C
Testing a scale-invariant Gaussian mean

Here, we give another concrete example of an e-process defined in a coarser filtration
\mathbb{G}\subsetneq\mathbb{F}
that is not valid at a
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time.
This particular construction is due to
Hendriksen et al., 2021
;
Pérez-Ortiz et al., 2024
; we follow the exposition of the latter paper.
The e-process described below can be combined with another e-process that is valid in
\mathbb{F}
, such as
Wang and Ramdas, 2025a
’s e-process based on universal inference.

Example 4

(Sequentially testing a scale-invariant Gaussian mean;
Hendriksen et al., 2021
;
Pérez-Ortiz et al., 2024
)
.

Suppose that the data
X_{1},X_{2},\dotsc
is sequentially sampled from a Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
with unknown parameters,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
Consider the following composite null and alternative hypotheses:

\mathcal{H}_{0}:\frac{\mu}{\sigma}=\delta_{0}\quad\text{vs.}\quad\mathcal{H}_{1}:\frac{\mu}{\sigma}=\delta_{1},

(35)

for some
\delta_{0}\neq\delta_{1}
.
The parameter of interest
\delta:=\mu/\sigma
is scale-invariant, in the sense that it is invariant to the mapping
(\mu,\sigma)\mapsto(c\mu,c\sigma)
for any
c\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
The null and alternative hypotheses corresponding to (
35
) can be parametrized as
\Theta_{j}=\{(\delta_{j}\sigma,\sigma):\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
for
j=0,1
, respectively (making them composite hypotheses).

Let
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
denote the canonical data filtration, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{t})
, and let
\mathbb{G}=(\mathcal{G}_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
denote the following scale-invariant coarsening of
\mathbb{F}
:

\mathcal{G}_{t}=\sigma\left(\frac{X_{1}}{\left\lvert X_{1}\right\rvert},\dotsc,\frac{X_{t}}{\left\lvert X_{1}\right\rvert}\right),\quad\forall t\geq 1.

(36)

Intuitively,
\mathbb{G}
removes from
\mathbb{F}
the scale information about the initial data point
X_{1}
and retains only its sign.
Then, the following ratio of likelihood mixtures
(
Cox, 1952
;
Lai, 1976
)
,

\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{MaxInv}}=\frac{\int_{\sigma>0}\frac{1}{\sigma^{t}}\exp\left[-\frac{t}{2}\left\{\left(\frac{\hat{\mu}_{t}}{\sigma}-\delta_{1}\right)^{2}+\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right\}\right]d\sigma}{\int_{\sigma>0}\frac{1}{\sigma^{t}}\exp\left[-\frac{t}{2}\left\{\left(\frac{\hat{\mu}_{t}}{\sigma}-\delta_{0}\right)^{2}+\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right\}\right]d\sigma},

(37)

is an e-process for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
in
\mathbb{G}

(
Pérez-Ortiz et al., 2024
, proposition 1.3)
, where
\hat{\mu}_{t}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{t}^{2}
are the empirical mean and variance of
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{t}
.
(Here, we sidestep group-theoretic notions in the original paper that are not central to our discussion.)
Under the null, the expectation of
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{MaxInv}}
at any
\mathbb{G}
-stopping time
\tau^{\mathbb{G}}
is upper-bounded by 1.

However,
Pérez-Ortiz et al., 2024
also notes that
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{MaxInv}}
is
not
upper-bounded by 1 at
\mathbb{F}
-stopping times in expectation.
Define
\tau^{\mathbb{F}}=1+\mathds{1}\left(\left\lvert X_{1}\right\rvert\in[a,b]\right)
for some constants
0<a<b
, meaning that we stop immediately if the absolute value of
X_{1}
is outside a range, and if not we stop after observing the second data point.
While
\tau^{\mathbb{F}}
is clearly an
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time, it is not adapted in
\mathbb{G}
because it depends on the scale of
X_{1}
.
For the values
\delta_{0}=0
,
a\approx 0.44
,
b\approx 1.70
, and a suitable choice of mixture over
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, numerical calculations show that

\mathbb{E}_{{P}}\left[{\mathfrak{e}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}}^{\mathsf{MaxInv}}}\right]\approx 1.19>1,

(38)

where
P\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
is a null distribution that posits a standard normal distribution over the first two data points, i.e.,
X_{1},X_{2}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

For this problem, we can also consider combining different e-processes in an analogous manner to the case of testing randomness (Example
1
).
For instance,
Wang and Ramdas, 2025a
develop a different e-process for the same null hypothesis (assuming, for simplicity, that
\delta_{0}=0
).
This e-process is based on UI
(
Wasserman et al., 2020
)
, analogous to
Ramdas et al., 2022
’s UI e-process for testing randomness (
30
), and it is valid under the data filtration
\mathbb{F}
.
The e-process has the following closed-form expression:

\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{UI}}=\left(\overline{X_{t}^{2}}\right)^{t/2}\exp(1)^{t/2}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\frac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{t-1}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{X_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{i-1}}{\hat{\sigma}_{i-1}}\right)^{2}\right\},

(39)

where
\hat{\mu}_{t-1}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{t-1}^{2}
are the empirical mean and variance of
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{t-1}
, and
\overline{X_{t}^{2}}=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}X_{i}^{2}
.
This e-process is designed to achieve power against the composite alternative hypothesis consisting of any Gaussian with nonzero mean
\mu\neq 0
(and unknown variance
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
).

To combine the two e-processes
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{MaxInv}}
(
37
) and
\mathfrak{e}^{\mathsf{UI}}
(
39
), we can employ the adjust-then-combine strategy (Corollary
2
) to obtain an e-process in
\mathbb{F}
:

\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}^{\mathsf{adj}}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathsf{A}((\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{MaxInv}})^{*})+\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\mathsf{UI}}\right].

(40)

Appendix D
Other types of adjusters

D.1
“Zero” adjusters can be (even more) powerful
\mathfrak{e}
-lifters

In addition to the admissible adjusters described in Section
2.3
, there exists another known class of admissible adjusters, originally proposed by
Shafer et al., 2011
: for
\mathfrak{e}\in[1,\infty]
,

\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{zero},\kappa}(\mathfrak{e})=\begin{cases}\kappa(1+\kappa)^{\kappa}\frac{\mathfrak{e}}{\log^{1+\kappa}(\mathfrak{e})}&\mbox{if }\mathfrak{e}\geq\exp(1+\kappa);\\
0&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

(41)

where
\kappa>0
is a hyperparameter to be chosen
a priori
.
We refer to these adjusters as
“zero” adjusters
as they completely zero out smaller values of
\mathfrak{e}
.
(For example, if
\kappa=1
, then
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{zero},1}(\mathfrak{e})=0
for any
\mathfrak{e}<\exp(2)\approx 7.39
.)
Elementary calculus shows that
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{zero},\kappa}(\mathfrak{e})
is indeed an admissible adjuster, or that
\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{zero},\kappa}(\mathfrak{e})d\mathfrak{e}=1
, for any fixed
\kappa>0
.

Figure 8
:
A comparison of admissible adjusters including the “zero” adjuster
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{zero},1}
.

The main benefit of using zero adjusters is that their growth rate as
\mathfrak{e}\to\infty
is even faster, by a constant factor, than
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{mix}}
or
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{KV}}
in Section
2.3
.
All three types of adjusters grow linearly in
\mathfrak{e}
, up to logarithmic factors, but
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{zero},\kappa}
gains the extra constant factor of advantage by completely zeroing out smaller values of evidence.
In Figure
8
, we compare
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{zero},1}
with
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{mix}}
,
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{KV}}
,
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{sqrt}}
and
\mathsf{A}_{1/2}
, confirming that the zero adjusters grow faster than
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{mix}}
or
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{KV}}
by a constant factor.

As evident from Figure
8
, when using e-processes to construct sequential tests (i.e., binary decisions), zero adjusters can provide an additional constant factor of statistical power.
For this reason, we utilize the zero adjuster
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{zero},1}
for the power comparison plots in Section
G.1
.

On the other hand, when interpreting e-processes as sequentially accumulated (non-binary) evidence, zero adjusters are not as useful because they completely zero out smaller amounts of evidence.
It is also unnatural to interpret the zero-adjusted process as a wealth process because the adjusted process will usually start with a string of zeros.
For these reasons, we do not use zero adjusters in our simulated and real data experiments in Sections
4.1
and
4.2
.

D.2
Generalized adjusters based on spines are
not

\mathfrak{e}
-lifters

While Theorem
3
leaves open the possibility of other functional forms for adjusters, one generalization that does
not
work is the class of functions that take both the running maximum of a process and its current value.
These functions have the form
\mathsf{A}:[1,\infty]\times[0,\infty]\to[0,\infty]
.
Dawid et al., 2011a
derive a characterization theorem for such generalized adjusters via univariate functions that they call
spines
.
Based on spines, they give a family of admissible spine-based adjusters having the form

\mathsf{A}_{\kappa}(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*},\mathfrak{e}_{t})=\kappa(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*})^{1-\kappa}+(1-\kappa)(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*})^{-\kappa}\mathfrak{e}_{t},

(42)

for some
\kappa\in[0,1]
.
Here, admissibility is defined among all spine-based adjusters.

However, while the function
\mathsf{A}_{1}(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*},\mathfrak{e}_{t})=\mathfrak{e}_{t}
is an admissible spine-based adjuster, it is not an
\mathfrak{e}
-lifter since
(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
does not achieve validity in a finer filtration.
Empirically, when we repeat the experiment for Figure
1
using different spine-based adjusters (by varying the choice of
\kappa
), we find that the mean of
\mathbb{F}
-stopped e-values for the conformal test martingale still exceeds one.
For example, if
\kappa=1/2
, then
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\mathsf{A}_{1/2}(\mathfrak{e}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}}^{*},\mathfrak{e}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}})]\approx 1.09\pm 0.005
over 10,000 simulations.

Appendix E
A bibliographic note on terminology

The notion of “lifting” in this paper is largely distinct from that of
Getoor and Sharpe, 1972
, who used the term in the (unrelated) context of continuous-time, complex-valued martingales.
Their notion has since been called
Hypothèse

(
Brémaud and Yor, 1978
)
, extension
(
Dubins et al., 1996
)
, and immersion
(
Beghdadi-Sakrani and Emery, 2006
)
, among others, as it has been adapted to the (also unrelated) literature of Brownian innovations
(
Tsirelson, 1998
, e.g.,)
.
Our terminology only concerns the lifting of events and evidence processes across filtrations defined in discrete time, with a focus on the validity of inference procedures at stopping times.

Further, as noted in
Vovk, 2021a
, it is a coincidence that the subject of
Getoor and Sharpe, 1972
’s study was termed “conformal martingales” (unrelated to
Vovk, 2021a
;
Vovk, 2021b
’s “conformal test martingales” that we discuss in Example
1
).

F.3
Two characterization lemmas and the proof of Theorem
3

We first state and prove the two nontrivial statements in Theorem
3
as lemmas.
Each lemma gives a novel characterization of adjusters, so we state it as an “if and only if” statement and prove both directions.
In the proof of Theorem
3
, we use the “if” direction from each lemma.

The first lemma establishes that an increasing function is an adjuster for test supermartingales if and only if it is an adjuster for e-processes.

Lemma 2

(Equivalence between adjusters for test supermartingales and e-processes)
.

Let
\mathsf{A}:[1,\infty]\to[0,\infty]
be an increasing function.
Then,
\mathsf{A}
is an adjuster for test supermartingales, in the sense of (
26
), if and only if
\mathsf{A}
is an adjuster for e-processes, in the sense of (
27
).

Proof.

(
\Leftarrow
) Given any
P
, let
(M_{t}(P))_{t\geq 0}
be a test supermartingale for
P
in
\mathbb{G}
.
By supermartingale optional stopping
(e.g.,
Durrett, 2019
, theorem 4.8.4)
,
(M_{t}(P))_{t\geq 0}
is an e-process for
\{P\}
in
\mathbb{G}
.
Then, condition (
27
) implies that there exists an e-process
(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
for
\{P\}
in
\mathbb{G}
satisfying
\mathsf{A}(M_{t}(P)^{*})\leq\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\prime}
,
P
-a.s., for all
t\geq 0
.
By
Ramdas et al., 2020
, there exists a test supermartingale
(M_{t}^{\prime}(P))_{t\geq 0}
for
P
in
\mathbb{G}
that upper-bounds
(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
, implying

\mathsf{A}(M_{t}(P)^{*})\leq\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\prime}\leq M_{t}^{\prime}(P),\quad\text{$P$-a.s.},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(47)

Thus,
\mathsf{A}
is an adjuster for test supermartingales, in the sense of (
26
).

(
\Rightarrow
) Suppose that
\mathsf{A}
is an adjuster for test supermartingales, in the sense of (
26
).
For any
\mathcal{P}
, consider an arbitrary e-process
(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
for
\mathcal{P}
in some filtration
\mathbb{G}
.
By
Ramdas et al., 2020
, for each
P\in\mathcal{P}
, there is a test supermartingale
(M_{t}(P))_{t\geq 0}
for
P
in
\mathbb{G}
that upper-bounds the e-process, that is,

\mathfrak{e}_{t}\leq M_{t}(P),\quad\text{$P$-a.s.},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(48)

Now, because
\mathsf{A}
is an adjuster for test supermartingales, there exists a test supermartingale
(M_{t}^{\prime}(P))_{t\geq 0}
for
P
in
\mathbb{G}
such that

\mathsf{A}(M_{t}(P)^{*})\leq M_{t}^{\prime}(P),\quad\text{$P$-a.s.},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(49)

Because
\mathsf{A}
is an increasing function, we can combine the inequalities (
48
) and (
49
) into

\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*})\leq M_{t}^{\prime}(P),\quad\text{$P$-a.s.},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(50)

Finally, let
(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
be defined as
\mathfrak{e}_{0}^{\prime}=1
and
\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\prime}=\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*})
for each
t\geq 1
.
By
Ramdas et al., 2020
, equation (
50
) implies that
(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
is an e-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{G}

).
∎

The next lemma establishes another characterization that says a function is an adjuster for e-processes if and only if the adjusted running maximum of an e-process is an e-process.

Lemma 3

(Anytime-validity characterization of adjusters for e-processes)
.

An increasing function
\mathsf{A}:[1,\infty]\to[0,\infty]
is an adjuster for e-processes, in the sense of (

for

.

Proof.

(
\Leftarrow
) For any
\mathcal{P}
, let
(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be any e-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{G}

By the condition,

\mathbb{G}

.

G.2
An empirical comparison with existing approaches for the
h
-step-ahead forecast evaluation problem

Here, we elaborate on the
h
-step-ahead forecast comparison problem from Section
5.2
and provide additional empirical comparisons between the e-lifting approach and alternative methods (some of which do not yield e-processes).
Recall from the main text that the
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting theorem yields the
adjust-then-combine
approach (
25
) for constructing an e-process (restated here):

\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}=\frac{1}{h}\sum_{k=1}^{h}\mathsf{A}\left(\sup_{i\leq t}\mathfrak{e}_{i}^{[k]}\right),

where for each
k\in\{1,\dotsc,h\}
,
\mathfrak{e}^{[k]}
is an e-process in a sub-filtration
\mathbb{F}^{[k]}\subsetneq\mathbb{F}
that captures evidence from forecasts made on days at offset
k
.

Due to the unique setup of the problem—specifically, due to
Arnold et al., 2023
’s filtration construction—if we only need a sequential test then there exist alternative approaches that bypass the need to construct an e-process (and thus an adjuster).

Arnold et al., 2023
’s strategy is the following: given
h
e-processes
\mathfrak{e}^{[k]}
for
\mathcal{P}
in a sub-filtration
\mathbb{F}^{[k]}\subsetneq\mathbb{F}
, the unadjusted mean of the e-processes
\mathfrak{e}^{[k]}
, call it the “simple mean”
\bar{M}_{t}=\frac{1}{h}\sum_{k=1}^{h}\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{[k]}
(
not
an e-process), satisfies a
lagged
version of
\mathbb{F}
-anytime-validity in the form of

\text{for any $\mathbb{F}$-stopping time $\tau$ and for any $P\in\mathcal{P}$,}\quad\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\bar{M}_{\tau+h-1}\right]\leq 1.

(52)

For
h=1
, this coincides with the usual e-process definition, but for
h>1
it is a strictly weaker condition.
The lagged validity implies that, if we are willing to wait
h
extra steps (and throw away information during those
h
steps) before making a decision, then we can treat the unadjusted mean of the
h
e-processes as valid evidence.
In practice, this may or may not be a reasonable trade-off, depending on the application and the size of
h
.

To obtain a fully anytime-valid test, prior works also note that one can utilize the harmonic-mean p-merging function
(
Vovk and Wang, 2020
)
in the
\mathfrak{p}
-domain:

\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}_{t}=\frac{\exp(1)\log(h)}{\frac{1}{h}\sum_{k=1}^{h}\left(1/\mathfrak{p}_{t}^{[k]}\right)},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathfrak{p}_{t}^{[k]}=\frac{1}{\left(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{[k]}\right)^{*}}.

(53)

The validity of this combined p-value critically relies on the fact that
\mathfrak{p}
-lifting is free (Theorem
1
): each
\mathfrak{p}_{t}^{[k]}
is a p-process in a sub-filtration
\mathbb{F}^{[k]}
, and thus it is also a p-process in
\mathbb{F}
, allowing the combined version to be a p-process in
\mathbb{F}
as well.
Then, the resulting level-
\alpha
sequential test is based on the mean of (the running maximum of) the unadjusted e-processes,
rescaled by a factor of
(\exp(1)\log(h))^{-1}
:

\phi_{t}=\mathds{1}\left(\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}_{t}\leq\alpha\right)=\mathds{1}\left(\tilde{M}_{t}\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}\right),\quad\text{where}\quad\tilde{M}_{t}=\frac{1}{\exp(1)\log(h)}\left[\frac{1}{h}\sum_{k=1}^{h}\left(\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{[k]}\right)^{*}\right].

(54)

If our main goal is to construct an anytime-valid sequential test, then
\phi_{t}
suffices.

However, if our goal is to report an anytime-valid notion of evidence, then neither
\bar{M}_{t}
nor
\tilde{M}_{t}
is adequate as it is not an e-process.

We also remark that the rescaling factor decreases as
h
grows large.

To construct an e-process, the only other known way
(
Choe and Ramdas, 2023
, ppn. 5.3)
is to calibrate the harmonic-mean p-process in the p-to-e direction: use

\tilde{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}=\mathsf{C}\left(\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}_{t}\right),

(55)

for some p-to-e calibrator
\mathsf{C}
.
Yet, this approach is suboptimal.
At a high level, this is because the harmonic-mean p-merging function, like any other admissible p-merging function, implicitly transforms each p-value into an e-value using different p-to-e calibrators before averaging them
(
Gasparin et al., 2024
)
.
This implies that
Choe and Ramdas, 2023
’s approach (
55
) effectively applies p-to-e calibration twice.
In contrast, the adjust-then-combine approach (
25
) only goes through one p-to-e calibrator (by the use of an adjuster).

-step-ahead forecasters, on simulated data and forecasters with
h=3
.

\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}
and

are e-processes in
\mathbb{F}
.
*The mean of unadjusted e-process,

\tau+h-1

, where
\tilde{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}
is always dominated by the mean of adjusted evidence
\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}
by a (roughly) constant factor.

h

\tilde{M}_{t}
and
\bar{M}_{t}

This confirms our earlier claim that, for the purpose of (only) constructing a sequential test, the harmonic-mean p-process (
54
) can be more powerful than utilizing the mean of adjusted evidence.
On the other hand, for the purpose of reporting evidence in an anytime-valid manner, the e-process
\bar{\mathfrak{e}}_{t}
is appropriate.

Appendix H
Cooperative skepticism: A game-theoretic protocol for combining evidence

While we state and prove our main results in the language of measure-theoretic probability, we may also interpret them in the language of game-theoretic probability and statistics
(
Shafer and Vovk, 2005
;
Shafer and Vovk, 2019
;
Ramdas et al., 2023
)
.
In this view, a test martingale represents the wealth of a gambler who tries to discredit a forecaster by betting against their proposed forecasts (the null hypothesis) over a sequence of outcomes.
Specifically, in the
testing-by-betting
framework
(
Shafer, 2021
)
, the wealth of a gambler (“skeptic”) who plays against a point null
P
is a test (super)martingale for
P
, such that large wealth for the skeptic indicates evidence against
P
.
For a composite null
\mathcal{P}
, the gambler is evaluated by the
minimum
wealth of all skeptics, each of whom plays against each member of the null
P\in\mathcal{P}
.
An e-process for
\mathcal{P}
, a composite generalization of a test martingale, corresponds to this minimum wealth against each
P\in\mathcal{P}

(
Ramdas et al., 2022
)
.
Definition (
1
) then translates to saying that an e-process can only grow large if the data discredits every possible null scenario.

Following
Shafer and Vovk, 2019
, we can formalize the game each skeptic plays against a null forecaster
P=(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
(the distribution is over the entire data sequence).
At each round
t=1,2,\dotsc
, the forecaster proposes a probability distribution
P_{t}
on the eventual outcome
Y_{t}
, and then a skeptic chooses a bet
S_{t}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that determines the multiplicative increment on their wealth depending on the outcome.
The game requires that this bet is expected to be small under the forecaster’s proposal:

\mathbb{E}_{{P_{t}}}\left[{S_{t}(Y_{t})}\right]\leq 1.

(56)

Assuming the skeptic starts with an initial wealth of
\mathcal{K}_{0}=1
, the skeptic’s wealth at time
t
would be
\mathcal{K}_{t}=K_{t-1}\cdot S_{t}(Y_{t})
, forming a test martingale for
P
.
By the constraint (
56
), the wealth process
(\mathcal{K}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
can only grow large if the forecast
P
does not accurately describe the actual outcomes
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.

Based on this framework, we can introduce a game-theoretic protocol that mirrors the setup for Corollary
2
.
The setup corresponds to having two skeptics that are playing against a common forecaster (the shared null), and their wealth processes correspond to their respective e-processes.
In the general setup for this work, each skeptic operates with their own information set, which we will make explicit.
For the sake of exposition, we assume that the two e-processes are simply test supermartingales for a point null
P=(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
over a data sequence
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, but the framework can be generalized to e-processes by playing many parallel games for each
P\in\mathcal{P}
and taking the minimum wealth for each skeptic
(
Ramdas et al., 2022
)
.

Inspired by
Dawid et al., 2011b
’s
competitive skepticism
protocol, we refer to the following protocol as
cooperative skepticism
to highlight the shared effort among the two skeptics.

Protocol 1

(Cooperative skepticism)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{0}^{(1)}=\mathcal{K}_{0}^{(2)}=1
.
For rounds
t=1,2,\dotsc
:

1.

Forecaster announces a distribution
P_{t}
on
\mathcal{Y}
.

2.

Skeptics 1 and 2
individually
place their bets:

•

Skeptic 1 makes a
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable bet
S_{t}^{(1)}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
s.t.
\mathbb{E}_{P_{t}}[S_{t}^{(1)}(Y_{t})\left.\middle|\right.\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq 1
.

•

Skeptic 2 makes a
\mathcal{G}_{t-1}
-measurable bet
S_{t}^{(2)}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
s.t.
\mathbb{E}_{P_{t}}[S_{t}^{(2)}(Y_{t})\left.\middle|\right.\mathcal{G}_{t-1}]\leq 1
.

3.

Reality announces
y_{t}\in\mathcal{Y}
.

4.

Each skeptic’s wealth is updated as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{t}^{(1)}=\mathcal{K}_{t-1}^{(1)}\cdot S_{t}^{(1)}(y_{t})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{K}_{t}^{(1)}=\mathcal{K}_{t-1}^{(2)}\cdot S_{t}^{(2)}(y_{t}).

(57)

Note:

\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{t-1}
represent information sets (
\sigma
-fields) with which skeptics 1 and 2 respectively choose their bets for round
t
.
In general, they are distinct (but not necessarily disjoint).

In Protocol
1
, we (as the statisticians who stand outside of the game) may choose to bet against the forecaster by splitting our initial wealth and (say) use half of our money following skeptic 1’s strategy and the other half following skeptic 2’s strategy.
The protocol suggests that the skeptics’ wealth processes,
(\mathcal{K}_{t}^{(1)})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\mathcal{K}_{t}^{(2)})_{t\geq 0}
, are test supermartingales in the respective filtrations,
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
\mathbb{G}=(\mathcal{G}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.
Then, assuming that we operate with our own information set,
\mathbb{H}
, that includes both skeptic’s information sets in each round (
\mathbb{F},\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{H}
), Theorem
2
translates to saying that we can construct our own test martingale (wealth process) by
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting each skeptic’s wealth process and averaging them.
Put differently, in each round
t
, we agree to play against the forecaster and receive the wealth of
\frac{1}{2}[\mathsf{A}((\mathcal{K}_{t}^{(1)})^{*})+\mathsf{A}^{\prime}((\mathcal{K}_{t}^{(2)})^{*})]
for a choice of adjusters
\mathsf{A},\mathsf{A}^{\prime}
.
The interpretation generalizes to e-processes, where each skeptic is now evaluated by their minimum wealth over multiple games, one against each member (forecaster) of the null hypothesis.
Denoting the minimum wealth after round
t
as
\kappa_{t}^{(1)}
and
\kappa_{t}^{(2)}
for each skeptic, we would receive the analogous wealth of
\frac{1}{2}[\mathsf{A}((\kappa_{t}^{(1)})^{*})+\mathsf{A}^{\prime}((\kappa_{t}^{(2)})^{*})]
.

We close with the remark that, while Protocol
1
describes a game-theoretic interpretation of Theorem
2
and Corollary
2
, it is not itself a result in game-theoretic probability like
Dawid et al., 2011b
’s.

Dawid et al., 2011b
defines a perfect-information protocol, with which they formally prove the equivalence between (
11
) and (
26
) to characterize adjusters.
On the other hand, Protocol
1
is not a perfect-information game, and it remains unclear whether Theorem
2
can be proven solely in the game-theoretic framework (without measure-theoretic language).

Appendix I
Randomized adjustments of e-processes across filtrations

Statistical methods often leverage external randomization to improve their power, and generally, these methods can be readily incorporated into the
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting procedure.
For instance, the conformal test martingale in Example
1
uses randomization to break ties (see Section
B
for details);
Ramdas and Manole, 2024
discuss many other known examples.
In this section, we explore an external randomization method that may improve the
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting procedure itself.

The main motivation stems from two recent works that demonstrate how utilizing an independent random variable
U\sim\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
(“
U
-randomization”) can improve the statistical power.
First, in a non-sequential setup,
Ignatiadis et al., 2024
remark that if
e
is an e-value and
U
is independent of
e
, then
\tilde{p}=(U/e)\wedge 1
is a valid,
U
-randomized p-value.
In fact, the
U
-randomized e-to-p calibrator

e\mapsto(U/e)\wedge 1
dominates the e-to-p calibrator
e\mapsto(1/e)\wedge 1
of (
8
), which is the only admissible
deterministic
e-to-p calibrator
(
Vovk and Wang, 2021
, ppn. 2.2)
.
Second, in a sequential setup,
Ramdas and Manole, 2024
show that we can analogously incorporate
U
-randomization at a stopping time, increasing the statistical power of the stopped process.
(More generally, both of these results, as well as all main results in this section, still hold if
U
is “stochastically larger” than
\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
, or
P(U\leq u)\leq u
for all
u\in[0,1]
.
For the sake of exposition, we will let
U
denote an independent
\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
random variable in the below.)

In the following, we first show that a “lift-then-randomize” strategy, inspired by
Ramdas and Manole, 2024
’s
U
-randomization method, is naturally applicable to our setup: first apply the
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting procedure (Theorem
2
), and then apply
U
-randomization at an arbitrary data-dependent stopping time.
Next, we describe an analogous “randomize-then-lift” strategy, corresponding to
Ignatiadis et al., 2024
’s
U
-randomized e-to-p calibration method for the non-sequential setup, and show empirically that the strategy does
not
translate to a valid
\mathfrak{e}
-lifting procedure in general.

I.1
The lift-then-randomize procedure

Consider an e-process
\mathfrak{e}=(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
.
Given an adjuster
\mathsf{A}
, Theorem
2
says that, for any
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}^{*})]\leq 1
for any
P\in\mathcal{P}
.
Then, by the uniformly-randomized Markov’s inequality
(UMI;
Ramdas and Manole, 2024
, theorem 1.2)
, we have

P\left(\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}^{*})\geq\frac{U}{\alpha}\right)\leq\alpha,\quad\forall P\in\mathcal{P},\;\forall\alpha\in(0,1).

(58)

Here, each distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}
now additionally describes the external random variable
U
, which we assume to be independent of
\mathbb{F}
(and of any
\mathbb{G}
- or
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time).
In particular, this means that the stopping time
\tau
cannot depend on the external variable
U
.

This simple application of UMI gives us the two-step
lift-then-randomize
procedure:

\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}\quad\overset{\text{$\mathfrak{e}$-lifting}}{\underset{\text{(e-to-e)}}{\longrightarrow}}\quad\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}^{*})\quad\overset{\text{$U$-rand.}}{\underset{\text{(e-to-p)}}{\longrightarrow}}\quad\frac{U}{\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}^{*})}\wedge 1.

(59)

While
\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}
is only an e-value when
\tau
is a
\mathbb{G}
-stopping time,
\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}^{*})
is an e-value for any
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau
, and the last random variable,
(U/\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}^{*}))\wedge 1
, is a p-value for any
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau
.
We summarize this result as a proposition below.
Note that, since
U<1
almost surely, the
U
-randomization step strictly increases (a.s.) the power of the resulting sequential test.

Proposition 1

(Uniformly-randomized p-value from a lifted e-process)
.

If
\mathfrak{e}=(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an e-process for
\mathcal{P}
w.r.t
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
and
\mathsf{A}
is an adjuster, then

\text{for any $\mathbb{F}$-stopping time $\tau$,}\quad\tilde{p}_{\tau}^{\mathsf{ltr}}:=\frac{U}{\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}^{*})}\wedge 1\quad\text{is a p-value for $\mathcal{P}$}.

(60)

In other words, for any
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau
,
P(\tilde{p}_{\tau}^{\mathsf{ltr}}\leq\alpha)\leq\alpha
for any
P\in\mathcal{P}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Thus, we have an
\mathbb{F}
-anytime-valid level-
\alpha
sequential test for
\mathcal{P}
that leverages
U
-randomization: stop at a
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau
, sample an independent random variable
U\sim\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
, compute
\tilde{p}_{\tau}^{\mathsf{ltr}}
, and reject
\mathcal{P}
if
\tilde{p}_{\tau}^{\mathsf{ltr}}\leq\alpha
.
However, notice that
\tilde{p}_{\tau}^{\mathsf{ltr}}
is
not
a p-process in
\mathbb{F}
because it is not
\mathbb{F}
-adapted—it depends on the external random variable
U
.
More generally, when using
U
-randomization, we do not expect to obtain
\mathbb{F}
-processes because of
U
.

If we wanted to combine
\tilde{p}_{\tau}^{\mathsf{ltr}}
with a stopped e-process in
\mathbb{F}
, then we would need to apply an extra p-to-e calibration before being able to average the two.
Alternatively, if we wanted to combine the p-value with a stopped p-process in
\mathbb{F}
, then we can use an admissible p-merging function
(
Vovk et al., 2022b
)
to obtain a combined p-value.
Neither of these combination methods, however, will result in a p-process (or an e-process).

I.2
Lifting the randomized Ville’s inequality

Typically, a p-process with stopping time validity can further yield a time-uniform inference procedure that can be monitored continuously due to the equivalence lemma (Section
3.1
).
However, because the lift-then-randomize procedure of Proposition
1
only yields a stopped p-value and not a p-process, we cannot directly obtain an inference procedure that can be monitored continuously over time.
Fortunately, it follows straightforwardly from
Ramdas and Manole, 2024
’s randomized Ville’s inequality that, when it comes to deriving a level-
\alpha
sequential test, we can still monitor the original e-process
\mathfrak{e}
at the threshold
1/\alpha
before reaching a stopping time, at which we can use the
U
-randomized p-value.

Formally, the
randomized Ville’s inequality
states that, given an e-process
(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in
\mathbb{G}
, for any
P\in\mathcal{P}
and for any
\mathbb{G}
-stopping time
\tau
,

P\left(\exists t\leq\tau:\mathfrak{e}_{t}\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}\quad\text{OR}\quad\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}\geq\frac{U}{\alpha}\right)\leq\alpha,

(61)

where
U\sim\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
is independent of
\mathbb{G}
(and thus of
\mathfrak{e}
and
\tau
).
The inequality yields a level-
\alpha
sequential inference procedure for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{G}
: (a) collect data and monitor the e-process in
\mathbb{G}
; (b) if
\mathfrak{e}_{t}
ever crosses
1/\alpha
, stop and reject the null; (c) otherwise, stop at any
\mathbb{G}
-stopping time
\tau
, draw an independent random variable
U\sim\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
, and reject the null if
\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}\geq U/\alpha
.
Because the threshold
U/\alpha
is almost surely smaller than the level
1/\alpha
, it gives a better chance of rejecting the null under the alternative at a more lenient threshold than the usual
1/\alpha
.
Note that we may only apply randomization at the stopping time
\tau
, as opposed to at all times
t
; see
Ramdas and Manole, 2024
for further discussion.

Proposition
1
allows us to extend this procedure to continuously monitoring in the finer filtration
\mathbb{F}
: we can apply
U
-randomization to the lifted e-process at an
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time.
This gives us the
\mathfrak{e}
-lifted version of randomized Ville’s inequality, which we state below.

Corollary 3

(
\mathfrak{e}
-lifted randomized Ville’s inequality)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
, and
let
\mathfrak{e}=(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be an e-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{G}
.
Suppose that
U\sim\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
is independent of
\mathbb{F}
.
Then, given an adjuster
\mathsf{A}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the e-process
\mathfrak{e}
satisfies, for any
P\in\mathcal{P}
and any
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau
,

P\left(\exists t\leq\tau:\mathfrak{e}_{t}\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}\quad\text{OR}\quad\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}^{*})\geq\frac{U}{\alpha}\right)\leq\alpha.

(62)

The proof follows directly from Proposition
1
by choosing the
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time

\rho=\tau\wedge\eta,\quad\text{where}\quad\eta=\inf\{t\geq 1:\mathfrak{e}_{t}\geq 1/\alpha\},

(63)

given any
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau
.
We can equivalently say
\eta=\inf\{t\geq 1:\mathfrak{e}_{t}^{*}\geq 1/\alpha\}
since the time that a process first exceeds
1/\alpha
is also when its running maximum first exceeds
1/\alpha
.

Corollary
3
suggests the following level-
\alpha
sequential test in
\mathbb{F}
:

1.

Collect data and continuously monitor the e-process
\mathfrak{e}
in
\mathbb{F}
.

2.

If
\mathfrak{e}
ever crosses
1/\alpha
, stop and reject the null.
Otherwise, stop at any
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau
.

3.

Draw an independent random variable
U\sim\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
. Reject the null if
\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}^{*})\geq U/\alpha
.

The key difference from (
61
) is that the stopping time
\tau
can be chosen in the
finer
filtration
\mathbb{F}
, even though
\mathfrak{e}
is an e-process only in the coarser filtration
\mathbb{G}
.

The main takeaway here is that the randomized Ville’s inequality can be applied to e-processes in
\mathbb{G}
at any
\mathbb{F}
-stopping times in a specific way: we can track the e-process up until
\tau
but then use its adjusted version at the stopping time before applying
U
-randomization.
In practice, the utility of this monitoring procedure depends on whether the stopping condition
\mathsf{A}(\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}^{*})\geq U/\alpha
is more lenient than its deterministic counterpart
\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}\geq 1/\alpha
, which is valid at an
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time due to Ville’s inequality and
\mathfrak{p}
-lifting (Theorem
1
).

I.3
The randomize-then-lift procedure is
not

\mathbb{F}
-anytime-valid, generally

An alternative proposal to the above is to first apply
U
-randomization and then apply a
\mathfrak{p}
-lifting procedure.
If we could first obtain a
U
-randomized p-process, then we may freely lift it into any finer filtration via Theorem
1
, before applying a p-to-e calibrator to obtain an e-process.

First, we define the
randomize-then-lift
procedure as follows.
Given an e-process
\mathfrak{e}
in a sub-filtration
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
and any
\mathbb{G}
-stopping time
\tau
, we apply the
U
-randomized e-to-p calibrator, which maps
\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}
to a
\mathbb{G}
-stopped p-value of
\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tau}=(U/\mathfrak{e}_{\tau})\wedge 1
.
Then, we apply any p-to-e calibrator
\mathsf{C}
to obtain an
\mathbb{G}
-stopped e-value.
The following diagram summarizes this:

\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}\quad\overset{\text{randomized e-to-p}}{\underset{U\sim\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]}{\longrightarrow}}\quad\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tau}^{\mathsf{rtl}}:=\frac{U}{\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}}\wedge 1\quad\overset{\text{p-to-e}}{\longrightarrow}\quad\tilde{\mathfrak{e}}_{\tau}^{\mathsf{rtl}}:=\mathsf{C}\left(\frac{U}{\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}}\wedge 1\right).

(64)

While
\tilde{\mathfrak{e}}_{\tau}^{\mathsf{rtl}}
is a valid e-value for
\mathcal{P}
at any
\mathbb{G}
-stopping time
\tau
, it remains unclear whether its validity extends to
\mathbb{F}
-stopping times.
The challenge here is that, once
U
is included in the process, any event involving the resulting sequence is no longer adapted to the original filtration (
\mathbb{G}
).
This precludes the use of the lifting lemma (Lemma
1
), which requires a
\mathbb{G}
-adapted sequence of events.
As before, when to stop the experiment cannot depend on
U
.

We first derive an inflated type I error bound on the
U
-randomized p-value at
\mathbb{F}
-stopping times.
Ideally, what we would hope is that
\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}}^{\mathsf{rtl}}
is a p-value for
\mathcal{P}
for any
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau^{\mathbb{F}}
: for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, its type I error,
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P(\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}}^{\mathsf{rtl}}\leq\alpha)
, should be upper-bounded by
\alpha
.
However, the following lemma instead gives an upper bound that can be strictly greater than
\alpha
.

Lemma 4

(The
U
-lifting lemma for e-processes in a sub-filtration)
.

Let
\mathfrak{e}=(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be an e-process for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
, and let
U\sim\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
be independent of
\mathbb{F}
.
Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Then, for any
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau^{\mathbb{F}}
and any
P\in\mathcal{P}
,

P\left(\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}}^{\mathsf{rtl}}\leq\alpha\right)=P\left(\mathfrak{e}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}}\geq\frac{U}{\alpha}\right)\leq\alpha\left[\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathfrak{e}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}}}\right]\wedge\left(1+\log(1/\alpha)\right)\right].

(65)

The lemma says that the “pseudo-p-value” obtained via
U
-randomized lifting,
\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}}^{\mathsf{rtl}}
, has its type I error upper-bounded by the smaller of two terms.
The first is the upper bound obtained by the UMI,
\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathfrak{e}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}}}\right]
, and it exceeds the intended level
\alpha
whenever the e-process is not valid at the stopping time
\tau^{\mathbb{F}}
.
While this is only an upper bound, we empirically observe that the bound is achieved by the stopping time (
4
) for the conformal test martingale in Example
1
.
With
\alpha=0.05
, over 100K repeated samples of the data and
U
, we obtain a Monte Carlo estimate of
P(\mathfrak{e}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}}\geq U/\alpha)\approx 0.0657\pm 0.00078
. (For the UI e-process, which is
\mathbb{F}
-anytime-valid, the analogous estimate is
0.0116\pm 0.00034
.)
Whether this bound is tight depends on the setup, but the example illustrates that the randomize-then-lift procedure is not generally
\mathbb{F}
-anytime-valid.

The second bound is the constant
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha+\alpha\log(1/\alpha)
.
If
\alpha=0.05
, then
\tilde{\alpha}
is approximately
0.200
, which is substantially larger than
\alpha
.
To control the type I error at the level
\tilde{\alpha}=0.05
, we need to use a more stringent level of
\alpha\approx 0.0087
for
\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}}^{\mathsf{rtl}}
, but then this may offset gains in the statistical power when compared with the non-randomized
\mathfrak{p}
-lifting procedure (Theorem
1
).
The proof of this bound (shown below) only uses Markov’s inequality and the equivalence lemma
(
Ramdas et al., 2020
;
Howard et al., 2021
)
.
It remains to be seen whether it is possible to tighten this bound for arbitrary
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau^{\mathbb{F}}
, although the tightness of the first bound (for a specific
\tau^{\mathbb{F}}
) indicates that this bound will not be as small as
\alpha
in general.

We close the section with a proof of Lemma
4
.

Proof of Lemma
4
.

The first bound,
\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathfrak{e}_{\tau^{\mathbb{F}}}}\right]
, follows directly from the UMI
(
Ramdas and Manole, 2024
)
.
The second bound can be proved using Markov’s inequality and the equivalence lemma.
Compared to the proof of the lifting lemma (Lemma
1
), it involves extra steps to handle the external random variable
U
, which makes the event
\{\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}\geq U/\alpha\}
non-adapted to
\mathbb{G}
or
\mathbb{F}
.
Because
\{\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}\geq U/\alpha\}
is not adapted to
\mathbb{G}
or
\mathbb{F}
, the usual equivalence between random time validity and stopping time validity (in
\mathbb{G}
or
\mathbb{F}
) no longer holds.

Given that
\mathfrak{e}
is an e-process in
\mathbb{G}
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}]\leq 1
for any
\mathbb{G}
-stopping time
\tau
.
Then, Markov’s inequality implies that

P\left(\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha}\right)\leq\frac{\alpha}{\epsilon},\quad\forall P\in\mathcal{P},

(66)

for any fixed
\epsilon\in(\alpha,1)
.
(The bound is vacuous when
\epsilon\in[0,\alpha]
.)

By
Ramdas et al., 2020
, specifically the (iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii) direction, the statement then holds for all random times
T
(possibly infinite): for any fixed
\epsilon\in(\alpha,1)
,

P\left(\mathfrak{e}_{T}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha}\right)\leq\frac{\alpha}{\epsilon},\quad\forall P\in\mathcal{P}.

(67)

(The random time
T
may be, e.g., a stopping time in any filtration.)

Then, for any
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time
\tau
and any
P\in\mathcal{P}
, we have

\displaystyle P\left(\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}\geq\frac{U}{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}P\left(\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}\geq\frac{u}{\alpha}\right)du

(68)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{\alpha}{u}\wedge 1\right)du

(69)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\alpha}du+\int_{\alpha}^{1}\frac{\alpha}{u}du=\alpha+\alpha\log(1/\alpha).

(70)

Note that we used the independence of
U
from
\mathbb{F}
in (
68
).
The inequality (
69
) follows from (
67
).
If
U
were stochastically larger than
\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
, or
P(U\leq u)\leq u
for all
u\in[0,1]
, then the equality in (
68
) would be replaced with an inequality (
\leq
), and the rest would still follow.
∎
