Nonlinear steepest descent on a torus: a case study of the Landau-Lifshitz equation Thanks: ∗ Corresponding author
Harini Desiraju, Alexander Its, Andrei Prokhorov ∗

Abstract
. We obtain rigorous large time asymptotics for the Landau-Lifshitz equation in the soliton free case by extending the nonlinear steepest descent method to genus 1 surfaces. The methods presented in this paper pave the way to a rigorous analysis of other integrable equations on the torus and enable asymptotic analysis on different regimes of the Landau-Lifshitz equation.



1.
Introduction

In modern theory of integrable systems, the key conceptual role is played by the identification of an integrable nonlinear PDE or ODE as
a compatibility condition of a certain linear system-Lax pair. Usually, Lax pairs are realized
as matrix valued first order differential linear operators with respect to
the basic dynamical time-space variables whose
coefficients are rational functions of the additional complex
”spectral” parameter. For the vast majority of integrable PDEs, this spectral parameter varies on the
Riemann sphere. For this case, the apparatus for the analysis of integrable systems has been very well developed. The situation when the spectral parameter belongs to an algebraic curve of a higher genera possess very interesting and serious challenges.
We refer the reader to the 1981 work of I. Krichever and S. Novikov
[
KN81
]
and the 1987 work of N. Hitchin
[
Hit87
]
for a detailed exposition of the specific algebra-geometric features that the integrable systems whose Lax pairs are set on Riemann surfaces do possess.

The first example of an integrable system whose Lax pair “lives” on a Riemann surface
with nontrivial genus is the classical Landau-Lifshitz (LL) equation whose definition we will
soon recall. The associated Lax pair representation was found in the late 70s by
E. Sklyanin
[
Skl79
]
and, independently, by A. Borovik
[
BR81
]
and it is set on
a torus, i.e. on an elliptic (genus one) curve. Soon after this discovery, several
authors had extended to the Landau-Lifshitz equation such key ingredients of the standard inverse scattering technique
as dressing procedure, finite gap integration, and the Riemann-Hilbert representation.
We refer the reader to the survey paper
[
BBI14
]
for comprehensive descriptions
of the concrete results obtained in 70-80s (see also the end of this introduction) and for a detailed history of the subject. We want to highlight specifically the work of A. Mikhailov
[
Mik82
]
where the exact formulation of the
associated Riemann-Hilbert problem on the torus was presented and the
works of Y. Rodin
[
Rod89
,
Rod88
,
Rod84
]
, where the solvability of this Riemann-Hilbert problem
was thoroughly analyzed. In our work, we shall constantly use the results of
[
Mik82
,
Rod89
,
Rod88
,
Rod84
]
.

Since the above mentioned papers written in the 70-80s not much of the further progress in studying
integrable systems on the algebraic curves has been achieved owing to the non-trivial topological environment.
It is only in recent years that we have seen a rigorous generalization of some techniques
such as Fredholm determinants
[
DDG23
]
, Padé approximations
[
Ber21
]
, orthogonal polynomials
[
Ber22
,
DLR24
]
, and nonlinear waves
[
KT12
,
KT09
,
MT12
]

among others, to genus 1 surfaces. We should specifically mention M. Bertola’s paper
[
Ber22
]

where a key analytical apparatus of the theory integrable systems - the nonlinear steepest descent method
of Deift and Zhou, or, rather, its orthogonal polynomial version, has been extended to the genus one curves.
In our paper we are suggesting a similar extension of the PDE version of the method
(i.e., large time analysis of the solutions of Cauchy problems for integrable PDEs). We take the LL equation as a case study.
As has already been mentioned, the corresponding Lax pair is set on the algebraic curve of genus 1. We show how one can extend the nonlinear steepest descent method
on this nontrivial genus situation and obtain the large time asymptotic of the solution of the
Cauchy problem in the Schwartz class.

As in the genus zero case, the two versions (orthogonal polynomials and large time PDE analysis) of
the nonlinear steepest descent differ in several methodological aspects. Hence, in our analysis
we cannot directly use the results of
[
Ber22
]
. In particular, to accommodate a
rather unusual symmetry-normalization of the Riemann-Hilbert problem associated
with LL equation, we have to use, instead of the discussed in
[
Ber22
]
matrix Cauchy kernel,
a very special Gusman-Rodin Cauchy kernel (
[
GR62
]
) on the torus which creates certain extra difficulties in the analysis - see section
3.1
for more details. It should be noted that the Gusman-Rodin kernel coincides with the kernel considered in
[
Ber21
]
(see Remark
3.1
for details). This scalar kernel should be compared with the different matrix Cauchy kernel in
[

,
KT09
,
MT12
]
also used to construct solutions of the Riemann-Hilbert problem on the Riemann surface that
satisfy the symmetry conditions. We believe that the constructions of
paper
[
Ber22
]
will be relevant and indeed very useful for another important
question in the asymptotic theory of PDE which is dispersionless limits.

We should also mention that the Riemann-Hilbert problem on Riemann surfaces was
used to construct explicit solutions of model problems in terms of hyperelliptic functions for the necessities of Deift-Zhou nonlinear steepest descent method;
see
[

,

The large time behavior of the solution of the Cauchy problem for the LL equation in the framework of the Riemann-Hilbert method has already been considered in the paper
[
BI88
]
. In that paper, the asymptotics of the solution of Mikhailov’s

[

The nonlinear steepest descent technique we are developing in this paper not only provides a “short cut” to the rigorous derivation of the asymptotics of the particular problem associated with the particular system - Landau-Lifshitz equations.
We also believe that it can be used in the further analysis of the nontrivial genus integrable systems, which have recently attracted a lot of interest.

The Landau-Lifshitz (LL) equation was introduced by Landau and Lifshitz in 1935
[
LL08
]
to describe the time evolution of magnetism of ferromagnetic material or spin waves and since has become a fundamental tool in the magnetic recording industry
[
Wei12
]
. It can be interpreted as the continuum limit of the Heisenberg spin chain
[
QC82
]
. The LL equation in one space and one time dimension (1+1) is the most general model of magnetism that is integrable and has garnered much attention in the literature due to its applications in mathematics and physics. It can be viewed as the universal integrable equation due to the appearance of several integrable equations
in appropriate limits
[
FT07
,
Lai22
,
Lai20
]
, it has rich physical properties such as the existence of soliton solutions
[
Bob83
,
GMI19
]
and the relation to classical mechanics
[
Ves83
]
.
A rigorous analysis of its solutions is therefore consequential to a variety of fields.

The LL equation reads

(1.1)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}L(x,t)}{}=L(x,t)\times{\partial_{x}^{2}L(x,t)}+L(x,t)\times JL(x,t);

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{3}\left(L_{j}(x,t)\right)^{2}=1

with

\displaystyle L(x,t)=\left(L_{1}(x,t),L_{2}(x,t),L_{3}(x,t)\right),

\displaystyle J=\textrm{diag}\left(J_{1},J_{2},J_{3}\right),

\displaystyle J_{1}<J_{2}<J_{3}.

Here, the vector
L(x,t)
describes the magnetization of the material.
It is immediate to note that for
J_{1}=J_{2}=J_{3}
, the above equation describes a continuous Heisenberg spin chain,
whose Lax pair integrability was established in 1978 by L. Takhtajan
[
Tak77
]
, and in matrix form, it relates to the nonlinear Schrödinger equation
[
ZT79
]
.
The seminal papers by Sklyanin
[
Skl79
]
and Borovik
[
BR81
]
showed that the LL equation is associated to the following linear system on the torus (the Lax pair)

(1.2)

\displaystyle{\partial_{x}\Psi(\lambda,x,t)}=U(\lambda,x,t)\Psi(\lambda,x,t),

(1.3)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}\Psi(\lambda,x,t)}=V(\lambda,x,t)\Psi(\lambda,x,t),

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{2}=\left\{\lambda:|\mathrm{Re}(\lambda)|\leq 2K,|\mathrm{Im}(\lambda)|\leq 2K^{\prime}\right\}
shown in Figure
1
, and

are complete elliptic integrals of moduli
k

k^{\prime}=\sqrt{1-k^{2}}
respectively.

It is noteworthy that the linear system above takes values on the torus while the non-trivial topology is not reflected in the LL equation. The matrices

\displaystyle U(\lambda,x,t)

\displaystyle:=-\mathrm{i}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\sigma_{j}L_{j}(x,t)w_{j}(\lambda),

(1.5)

\displaystyle V(\lambda,x,t)

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,m,n=1\\
j\neq m\neq n\end{subarray}}^{3}\sigma_{j}L_{j}(x,t)w_{m}(\lambda)w_{n}(\lambda)+\mathrm{i}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\sigma_{j}P_{j}(x,t)w_{j}(\lambda),

(1.6)

\displaystyle P_{j}(x,t)

\displaystyle:=\left({\partial_{x}L}(x,t)\times L(x,t)\right)_{j}

are written with

The Pauli matrices are given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\

\displaystyle w_{1}(\lambda)=\rho\,\frac{1}{\mathrm{sn}(\lambda,k)},

\displaystyle w_{3}(\lambda)=\rho\,\frac{\mathrm{cn}(\lambda,k)}{\mathrm{sn}(\lambda,k)}.

\displaystyle w_{i}^{2}(\lambda)-w_{j}^{2}(\lambda)=-\frac{1}{4}(J_{i}-J_{j});\qquad i,j=1,2,3,

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}U}(\lambda,x,t)-{\partial_{x}V}(\lambda,x,t)+\left[U(\lambda,x,t),V(\lambda,x,t)\right]=0.

The Cauchy problem for (

using Jost solutions of the linear equation (

and the corresponding multi soliton solutions were studied by several authors, see
[

The asymptotic behaviour of soliton free solution of (

The basic idea of the approach of
[

is the construction of the asymptotic solution of the Riemann - Hilbert problem from
[

]
using the asymptotic solution of the direct monodromy problem for an auxiliary linear system posed on the same torus. The solution of this direct monodromy problem, in turn, uses certain prior information about the solution of the LL equation. This approach is a genus one version of the method first suggested in
[

]
for the case of genus zero of the mKdV equation
and, as already mentioned, is not fully rigorous. In this paper, as we have also mentioned above, we generalize the nonlinear steepest descent technique to the case of the torus and obtain rigorous large time asymptotics for the LL equation. We are now going to state the main results of this paper. For that, we need to introduce some additional notation.

3.1.

To study the solution of the Riemann-Hilbert problems on the torus, we will need the singular integral equation. The first step to writing the singular integral equation is constructing an appropriate Cauchy kernel. In general, the Cauchy kernel

\displaystyle C(\mu,\lambda):=\zeta(\mu-\lambda)-\zeta(\mu-\mathrm{i}K^{\prime})+\zeta(\lambda-K-\mathrm{i}K^{\prime})+\zeta(K),

The Cauchy kernel above has the following properties.

•

The periodicity properties of the function
\zeta(.)
(

)–(
B.4

(3.10)

\displaystyle C(\mu+4K,\lambda)=C(\mu,\lambda+4K)=C(\mu+4\mathrm{i}K^{\prime},\lambda)=C(\mu,\lambda+4\mathrm{i}K^{\prime})=C(\mu,\lambda).

It has poles at

•

It has zeros for

Proof of Proposition
2.2
.

For
\lambda\neq 0,2K,2\mathrm{i}K^{\prime},2K+2\mathrm{i}K^{\prime}
we can obtain the result just by iterating the integral equation.
We will focus our analysis on function
\upsilon_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,x)
first. We write the Neumann series expansion

(D.1)

\displaystyle\upsilon_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\upsilon_{-}^{(1)}\right)_{n}(\lambda,x)

where
\left(\upsilon_{-}^{(1)}\right)_{n}(\lambda,x)
are defined recursively

(D.2)

\displaystyle\left(\upsilon_{-}^{(1)}\right)_{n+1}(\lambda,x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\mathbb{1}-\sigma_{3})w_{3}(\lambda)(x-\tau)}\left(U(\lambda,\tau)+\mathrm{i}w_{3}(\lambda)\sigma_{3}\right)\left(\upsilon_{-}^{(1)}\right)_{n}(\lambda,\tau)d\tau,

\displaystyle\left(\upsilon_{-}^{(1)}\right)_{0}(\lambda,x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
0\end{array}\right).

Observe that for
\lambda\in\Omega_{-}
we have

(D.5)

\displaystyle\left|\left(\upsilon_{-}^{(1)}\right)_{0}(\lambda,x)\right|\leq 1,\quad\left|\left(\upsilon_{-}^{(1)}\right)_{1}(\lambda,x)\right|\leq\intop_{-\infty}^{x}\left|U(\lambda,\tau)+\mathrm{i}w_{3}(\lambda)\sigma_{3}\right|d\tau=\sigma_{-}(x),

(D.6)

\displaystyle\left|\left(\upsilon_{-}^{(1)}\right)_{2}(\lambda,x)\right|\leq\intop_{-\infty}^{x}\left|U(\lambda,\tau)+\mathrm{i}w_{3}(\lambda)\sigma_{3}\right|\sigma_{-}(\tau)d\tau=\frac{\sigma_{-}^{2}(x)}{2}.

Here we denoted the Hilbert-Schmidt matrix norm by
|M|
. Then we have the following estimates proven by induction

(D.7)

\displaystyle\left|\left(\upsilon_{-}^{(1)}\right)_{n}(\lambda,x)\right|\leq\frac{\sigma_{-}^{n}(x)}{n!}\leq\frac{\sigma_{-}^{n}(\infty)}{n!}.

That shows that the series (
D.1
) converges uniformly, function
\upsilon_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,x)
satisfies (
2.1
) and is analytic in
\Omega_{-}
for
\lambda\neq 0,2K,2\mathrm{i}K^{\prime},2K+2\mathrm{i}K^{\prime}
.

Next, we construct
\widehat{\upsilon}_{+}^{(2)}(\lambda,x)
using the Neumann series

(D.8)

\displaystyle\upsilon_{+}^{(2)}(\lambda,x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\upsilon_{+}^{(2)}\right)_{n}(\lambda,x)

with iterations given by

(D.9)

\displaystyle\left(\upsilon_{+}^{(2)}\right)_{n+1}(\lambda,x)=-\int^{\infty}_{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\mathbb{1}+\sigma_{3})w_{3}(\lambda)(x-\tau)}\left(U(\lambda,\tau)+\mathrm{i}w_{3}(\lambda)\sigma_{3}\right)\left(\upsilon_{-}^{(2)}\right)_{n}(\lambda,\tau)d\tau,

\displaystyle\left(\upsilon_{+}^{(2)}\right)_{0}(\lambda,x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
1\end{array}\right).

We can show similarly to above that the series (
D.8
) converges uniformly, function
\upsilon_{+}^{(2)}(\lambda,x)
satisfies (
2.1
) and is analytic in
\Omega_{-}
for
\lambda\neq 0,2K,2\mathrm{i}K^{\prime},2K+2\mathrm{i}K^{\prime}
. This procedure can be repeated for
\upsilon_{+}^{(1)}(\lambda,x)
and
\upsilon_{-}^{(2)}(\lambda,x)
.

Next, let’s show that these functions are bounded at
\lambda=0
. We follow the idea of
[
Rod84
]
to approximate solution at that point with the solution of Lax pair of NLS equation. The fact that isotropic degeneration of Landau-Lifshitz equation is equivalent to NLS equation was shown in
[
ZT79
]
. We introduce functions
\accentset{\circ}{\upsilon}_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,x)
,
\accentset{\circ}{\upsilon}_{+}^{(2)}(\lambda,x)
as the solutions of the integral equations

\displaystyle\accentset{\circ}{\upsilon}_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,x)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
0\end{array}\right)+\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{{\mathrm{i}(\mathbb{1}-\sigma_{3})(x-\tau)\rho}/{\lambda}}\left(\accentset{\circ}{U}(\lambda,\tau)+\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho\sigma_{3}}{\lambda}\right)\accentset{\circ}{\upsilon}_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,\tau)d\tau,

\displaystyle\accentset{\circ}{\upsilon}_{+}^{(2)}(\lambda,x)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
1\end{array}\right)-\int^{\infty}_{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\mathbb{1}+\sigma_{3})(x-\tau)\rho/\lambda}\left(\accentset{\circ}{U}(\lambda,\tau)+\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho\sigma_{3}}{\lambda}\right)\accentset{\circ}{\upsilon}_{+}^{(2)}(\lambda,\tau)d\tau,

where
\accentset{\circ}{U}(\lambda,x)=-\mathrm{i}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\dfrac{L_{j}(x)\rho\sigma_{j}}{\lambda}
. We form matrix
\accentset{\circ}{v}_{-}(\lambda,\tau)=(\accentset{\circ}{\upsilon}_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,\tau),\accentset{\circ}{\upsilon}_{+}^{(2)}(\lambda,\tau))
. It satisfies the differential equation

(D.16)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\accentset{\circ}{v}_{-}(\lambda,x)}{\partial x}=\accentset{\circ}{U}(\lambda,x)\accentset{\circ}{v}_{-}(\lambda,x)+\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho}{\lambda}\accentset{\circ}{v}_{-}(\lambda,x)\sigma_{3}.

In other words, we replaced the coefficient matrix
U(\lambda,x)
with the leading term of its asymptotics at
\lambda=0
. This is part of the Lax pair for the isotropic Landau-Lifshitz equation. To deal with function
\accentset{\circ}{v}_{-}(\lambda,x)
we consider
\accentset{\circ}{f}_{-}(\lambda,x)=\accentset{\circ}{v}_{-}(\lambda,x)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}x\rho}{\lambda}\sigma_{3}}
. It satisfies the differential equation

(D.17)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\accentset{\circ}{f}_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,x)}{\partial x}=\accentset{\circ}{U}(\lambda,x)\accentset{\circ}{f}_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,x)

We can notice
\accentset{\circ}{U}(\lambda,x)
has eigenvalues
\pm\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho}{\lambda}
. Let’s diagonalize it. Its matrix of eigenvectors has two forms

(D.18)

\displaystyle T_{1}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\left(L_{3}(x)+1\right)&-\dfrac{L_{1}(x)-\mathrm{i}L_{2}(x)}{L_{3}(x)+1}\\
\frac{1}{2}\left(L_{1}(x)+\mathrm{i}L_{2}(x)\right)&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\mbox{if }L_{3}(x)\neq-1,

(D.19)

\displaystyle T_{2}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}-\dfrac{L_{1}(x)-\mathrm{i}L_{2}(x)}{L_{3}(x)-1}&\frac{1}{2}({L_{3}(x)-1}{})\\
1&\frac{1}{2}(L_{1}(x)+\mathrm{i}L_{2}(x))\end{pmatrix},\quad\mbox{if }L_{3}(x)\neq 1.

We can notice that
T_{2}(x)=T_{1}(x)\left(\frac{2}{L_{1}+\mathrm{i}L_{2}}\right)^{\sigma_{3}}
. Since function
L_{3}(x)-1
is smooth, there is
\varepsilon>0
such that the sets
\{x:|L_{3}(x)-1|<\varepsilon\}
and
\{x:|L_{3}(x)+1|<\varepsilon\}
are disjoint. We would like to construct the eigenvectors using the partition of unity. We take nonvanishing infinitely smooth function
c(x)
such that

(D.20)

\displaystyle c(x)=1,\quad\mbox{if }|L_{3}(x)-1|<\varepsilon,\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle c(x)=\frac{2}{L_{1}(x)+\mathrm{i}L_{2}(x)},\quad\mbox{if }0<|L_{3}(x)+1|<\varepsilon.

and define

(D.21)

\displaystyle T(x)=T_{1}(x)c^{\sigma_{3}}(x)d^{\sigma_{3}}(x),

where function
d(x)
will be determined in a moment.
We can check the identity

(D.22)

\displaystyle\left(T(x)\right)^{-1}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{3}{L_{j}(x)\sigma_{j}}\right)T(x)=\sigma_{3}.

We introduce function
s(\lambda,x)=\left(T(x)\right)^{-1}\accentset{\circ}{f}_{-}(\lambda,x)
. It satisfies the differential equation

(D.23)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial s(\lambda,x)}{\partial x}=\left(-\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho}{\lambda}\sigma_{3}-T^{-1}\dfrac{dT}{dx}\right)s(\lambda,x)

It is already very similar to the Zakharov-Shabat system, which are part of the Lax pair of NLS equation. On the next step we would like to cancel the diagonal terms of
T^{-1}\dfrac{dT}{dx}
. For that we can choose function
d(x)
to satisfy the equation

(D.24)

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{\prime}(x)}{d(x)}=-\dfrac{c^{\prime}(x)}{c(x)}-\frac{L_{3}^{\prime}(x)}{2}-\dfrac{(L_{1}^{\prime}(x)+\mathrm{i}L_{2}^{\prime}(x))(L_{1}(x)-\mathrm{i}L_{2}(x))}{2(L_{3}(x)+1)}

We can notice that for
0<|L_{3}(x)+1|<\varepsilon
it takes form

(D.25)

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{\prime}(x)}{d(x)}=-\frac{L_{3}^{\prime}(x)}{2}-\dfrac{(L_{1}^{\prime}(x)+\mathrm{i}L_{2}^{\prime}(x))(L_{1}(x)-\mathrm{i}L_{2}(x))}{2(L_{3}(x)-1)}.

Since
\frac{d^{\prime}(x)}{d(x)}\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty
the function
d(x)
approaches to nonvanishing constants at
\pm\infty
, one of which we can choose.
As the result we have equation (
D.23
) becoming Zakharov-Shabat system with spectral parameter
\frac{1}{\lambda}
. More specifically the off-diagonal elements of
T^{-1}\dfrac{dT}{dx}
which correspond to potentials are given by

(D.26)

\displaystyle T^{-1}\frac{dT}{dx}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\dfrac{1}{c^{2}(x)d^{2}(x)}\left(\dfrac{L_{1}(x)-\mathrm{i}L_{2}(x)}{L_{3}(x)+1}\right)^{\prime}\\
(L_{3}(x)+1)^{2}\dfrac{c^{2}(x)d^{2}(x)}{4}\left(\dfrac{L_{1}(x)+\mathrm{i}L_{2}(x)}{L_{3}(x)+1}\right)^{\prime}&0\end{pmatrix}.

We can notice that the entries of the matrix above belong to the Schwartz class
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
. Using standard methods of theory of NLS equation we can show that columns of
s(\lambda,x)=(s^{(1)}(\lambda,x),s^{(2)}(\lambda,x))
admit the following integral representations, see
[
FT07
, Chapter 1, (5.10)]
.

(D.27)

\displaystyle s^{(1)}(\lambda,x)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho x}{\lambda}}\\
0\end{pmatrix}+\intop_{-\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho\tau}{\lambda}}\Gamma^{(1)}(x,\tau)d\tau,\quad\Gamma^{(1)}(x,\tau)\in L_{1}(-\infty,x)

(D.28)

\displaystyle s^{(2)}(\lambda,x)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho x}{\lambda}}\end{pmatrix}-\intop^{\infty}_{x}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho\tau}{\lambda}}\Gamma^{(2)}(x,\tau)d\tau,\quad\Gamma^{(2)}(x,\tau)\in L_{1}(x,\infty)

We can notice that
s(\lambda,x)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho x}{\lambda}\sigma_{3}}
is bounded for
\lambda\in\overline{\Omega}_{-}
. Therefore since
\accentset{\circ}{v}_{-}(\lambda,x)=T(x)s(\lambda,x)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho x}{\lambda}\sigma_{3}}
we see that
\accentset{\circ}{v}_{-}(\lambda,x)
is bounded near
\lambda=0
as well.

Introduce the ratio between the matrix function
\accentset{\circ}{v}_{-}(\lambda,x)
and original vector function
{\upsilon}_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,x)

(D.29)

\displaystyle{\eta}_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,x)=\left(\accentset{\circ}{v}_{-}(\lambda,x)\right)^{-1}{\upsilon}_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,x).

It satisfies the differential equation

(D.30)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\eta}_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,x)}{\partial x}=\left(\accentset{\circ}{v}_{-}(\lambda,x)\right)^{-1}\left(U(\lambda,x)-\accentset{\circ}{U}(\lambda,x)+\mathrm{i}w_{3}(\lambda)-\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho}{\lambda}\right)\accentset{\circ}{v}_{-}(\lambda,x){\eta}_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,x).

We see that the coefficient matrix does not have singularity at
\lambda=0
anymore. That implies that repeating the iteration procedure for the corresponding integral equation we can show that
{\eta}_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,x)
is bounded at zero. As result solution
{\upsilon}_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,x)
is bounded at zero as well, as desired. The proof for
\upsilon_{+}^{(2)}(\lambda,x)
,
\upsilon_{+}^{(1)}(\lambda,x)
,
\upsilon_{-}^{(2)}(\lambda,x)
is similar.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

Equation (
2.29
) and Proposition
2.2
imply that
{\sf a}(\lambda)
is analytic in
\Omega_{+}
, while the expression (
2.30
) and Proposition
2.2
say that
{\sf b}(\lambda)\in C^{\infty}(\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2})
.

To obtain the behavior of
{\sf b}(\lambda)
at zero, we look at the integral representation (
1
). We rewrite
\upsilon_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,\tau)
using functions constructed in the proof of Proposition
2.2
:

(D.37)

\displaystyle\upsilon_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,\tau)=T(\tau)s(\lambda,\tau)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho\tau}{\lambda}\sigma_{3}}\eta_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,\tau).

As the result we have

(D.38)

\displaystyle{\sf b}(\lambda)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}w_{3}(\lambda)\tau}\left[\left(U(\lambda,\tau)+\mathrm{i}w_{3}(\lambda)\sigma_{3}\right)T(\tau)s(\lambda,\tau)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho\tau}{\lambda}\sigma_{3}}\eta_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,\tau)\right]_{2}d\tau.

where columns of
s(\lambda,x)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho x}{\lambda}\sigma_{3}}
can be written as

(D.39)

\displaystyle s^{(1)}(\lambda,x)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho x}{\lambda}}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}+\intop_{-\infty}^{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho y}{\lambda}}\Gamma^{(1)}(x,x+y)dy,

(D.40)

\displaystyle s^{(2)}(\lambda,x)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho x}{\lambda}}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}-\intop^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho y}{\lambda}}\Gamma^{(2)}(x,x+y)dy.

Where functions
\Gamma^{(1)}(x,\tau)
,
\Gamma^{(2)}(x,\tau)
actually are infinitely smooth in both variables and decay faster than any power as
\tau\to\infty
. The behavior of
{\sf b}(\lambda)
for
\lambda\to 0
can be obtained using integration by parts. More precisely, denote

(D.41)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\lambda,\tau)=\left[\left(U(\lambda,\tau)+\mathrm{i}w_{3}(\lambda)\sigma_{3}\right)T(\tau)s(\lambda,\tau)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\rho\tau}{\lambda}\sigma_{3}}\eta_{-}^{(1)}(\lambda,\tau)\right]_{2}

We can see that

(D.42)

\displaystyle{\sf b}(\lambda)=\left.\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}w_{3}(\lambda)\tau}}{(-2\mathrm{i}w_{3}(\lambda))^{k+1}}\dfrac{d^{k}}{d\tau^{k}}\mathcal{F}(\lambda,\tau)\right|_{\tau=-\infty}^{\tau=\infty}

(D.43)

\displaystyle+\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(-2\mathrm{i}w_{3}(\lambda))^{n}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}w_{3}(\lambda)\tau}\dfrac{d^{n}}{d\tau^{n}}\mathcal{F}(\lambda,\tau)d\tau,\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

Since the function
\mathcal{F}(\lambda,\tau)
belongs to the Schwartz class
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
in variable
\tau
, the substitution at
\pm\infty
vanishes. In the same time derivatives with respect
\tau
applied to
\mathcal{F}(\lambda,\tau)
do not increase the power of singularity at
\lambda=0
. That provides as with decay of
{\sf b}(\lambda)
at
\lambda=0
faster than any power.
The derivatives with respect to
\lambda
preserve
\mathcal{F}(\lambda,\tau)
in the Schwartz class
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
with respect to the variable
\tau
, which allows us to repeat integration by parts argument and get decay of derivatives of
{\sf b}(\lambda)
as well.
∎
