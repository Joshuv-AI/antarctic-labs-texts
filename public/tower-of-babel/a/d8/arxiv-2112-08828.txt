Enveloping algebras of Krichever-Novikov algebras are not noetherian
Lucas Buzaglo

Abstract
. This work is part of the overarching question of whether it is possible for the universal enveloping algebra of an infinite-dimensional Lie algebra to be noetherian. The main result of this paper is that the universal enveloping algebra of any Krichever-Novikov algebra is not noetherian, extending a result of Sierra and Walton on the Witt (or classical Krichever-Novikov) algebra. As a subsidiary result, which may be of independent interest, we show that if 𝔥 \mathfrak{h} is a Lie subalgebra of 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} of finite codimension, then the noetherianity of U ⁡ ( 𝔥 ) U(\mathfrak{h}) is equivalent to the noetherianity of U ⁡ ( 𝔤 ) U(\mathfrak{g}) . The second part of the paper focuses on Lie subalgebras of W ≥ − 1 = Der ⁡ ( 𝕜 ⁡ [ t ] ) W_{\geq-1}=\Der(\Bbbk[t]) . In particular, we prove that certain subalgebras of W ≥ − 1 W_{\geq-1} (denoted by L ⁡ ( f ) L(f) , where f ∈ 𝕜 ⁡ [ t ] f\in\Bbbk[t] ) have non-noetherian universal enveloping algebras, and provide a sufficient condition for a subalgebra of W ≥ − 1 W_{\geq-1} to have a non-noetherian universal enveloping algebra. Furthermore, we make significant progress on a classification of subalgebras of W ≥ − 1 W_{\geq-1} by showing that any infinite-dimensional subalgebra must be contained in some L ⁡ ( f ) L(f) in a canonical way.

Introduction

Let
\Bbbk
be a field of characteristic
0
and let
\overline{\Bbbk}
be its algebraic closure. For brevity, we say that a
\Bbbk
-algebra is
noetherian
if it is left and right noetherian. Note that the universal enveloping algebra of a Lie algebra is left noetherian if and only if it is right noetherian. Therefore, when referring to universal enveloping algebras there is no need to distinguish between left and right noetherianity.

We are motivated by the long-standing question of whether it is possible for an infinite-dimensional Lie algebra to have a noetherian universal enveloping algebra. This question has been posed by many authors, but its earliest appearance was nearly fifty years ago, in Amayo and Stewart’s book on infinite-dimensional Lie algebras
[
AS74
, Question 2.7]
.

It is widely believed that the answer to this question is “no”
[
SW14
, Conjecture 0.1]
. The simplest example, an infinite-dimensional abelian Lie algebra, has a universal enveloping algebra isomorphic to a polynomial ring in infinitely many variables, which is clearly non-noetherian.

In
[
SW14
]
, Sierra and Walton proved that the universal enveloping algebra of the
Witt algebra

W=\Der(\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}])
is not noetherian, providing the first nontrivial example of a non-noetherian enveloping algebra. The question of whether
U(W)
is noetherian first appeared in print in
[
DS90
]
, over twenty years before being answered.

Sierra and Walton’s proof also allows us to conclude that the Lie algebra
W_{\geq-1}=\Der(\Bbbk[t])
has a non-noetherian universal enveloping algebra. An easy consequence of this result is that any infinite-dimensional
\mathbb{Z}
-graded simple Lie algebra of polynomial growth has a non-noetherian enveloping algebra
[
SW14
, Corollary 0.6]
. Beyond this, the noetherianity of enveloping algebras of infinite-dimensional Lie algebras remains mysterious. This paper focuses on questions of noetherianity for general Lie algebras of vector fields on affine curves.

We begin by proving the following result, which will be useful throughout the paper, and may be of independent interest.

Proposition 0.1

(Proposition
1.1
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
be Lie algebras such that
\mathfrak{h}
has finite codimension in
\mathfrak{g}
(i.e.
\dim_{\Bbbk}(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h})<\infty)
. Then
U(\mathfrak{h})
is noetherian if and only if
U(\mathfrak{g})
is noetherian.

The proof is achieved by constructing a positive filtration of
U(\mathfrak{g})
with the property that the associated graded ring is noetherian if
U(\mathfrak{h})
is noetherian, which is enough to conclude that
U(\mathfrak{g})
is noetherian.

In Section
2
, we consider
Krichever-Novikov algebras
. A Krichever-Novikov algebra is the Lie algebra of vector fields on an affine curve. Since the Lie algebras considered by Sierra and Walton are examples of Krichever-Novikov algebras, it is natural to ask whether universal enveloping algebras of Krichever-Novikov algebras are noetherian. We show that they are not, providing a second general class of infinite-dimensional Lie algebras with non-noetherian universal enveloping algebras.

Theorem 0.2

(Theorem
2.3
)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a Krichever-Novikov algebra. Then
U(\mathcal{L})
is not noetherian.

In order to prove Theorem
0.2
we first note that, by a result from
[
SW14
]
, it suffices to show that
\mathcal{L}
has a Lie subalgebra with a non-noetherian universal enveloping algebra. We begin by considering the case where
\mathcal{L}
is the Lie algebra of vector fields on a nonsingular curve. In this case, we construct an injective Lie algebra homomorphism from a finite-codimensional subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
to
\mathcal{L}
. By Proposition
0.1
, this subalgebra has a non-noetherian universal enveloping algebra, therefore proving that
U(\mathcal{L})
is not noetherian.

The injective Lie algebra homomorphism is constructed as follows: let
\pi\colon C\to\mathbb{A}^{1}
be a dominant morphism, where
C
is the nonsingular affine curve corresponding to the Krichever-Novikov algebra
\mathcal{L}
. We show that any vector field
v
on
\mathbb{A}^{1}
which vanishes on the branch point locus of
\pi
extends uniquely to a vector field
\widetilde{v}
on
C
. The map
v\mapsto\widetilde{v}
is the injective homomorphism we need.

If
\mathcal{L}
is the Lie algebra of vector fields on a singular curve
C
, we require a different argument. We consider the normalisation
\widetilde{C}
of
C
and let
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
be the Lie algebra of vector fields on
\widetilde{C}
. We construct a Lie subalgebra of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
which is also a subalgebra of
\mathcal{L}
and further prove that the enveloping algebra of this subalgebra is not noetherian. This is enough to conclude that
U(\mathcal{L})
is not noetherian.

At the end of Section
2
, we further show that some related Lie algebras known as
Krichever-Novikov type algebras
(see Definition
2.15
) also have non-noetherian enveloping algebras.

After this, in Section
3
, we turn our attention to arbitrary subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
and make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 0.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an infinite-dimensional Lie subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
. Then
U(\mathfrak{g})
is not noetherian.

We are not able to prove Conjecture
0.3
. However, we make significant progress in understanding the structure of infinite-dimensional subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
.

We first show that the above conjecture holds for graded subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
. Furthermore, if
\mathfrak{g}
is a Lie subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
of finite codimension, then
U(\mathfrak{g})
is not noetherian by Proposition
0.1
. We therefore focus on (ungraded) infinite-dimensional subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
of infinite codimension.

Veronese subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
(see Definition
3.2
) and their Lie subalgebras were the only known infinite-dimensional subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
of infinite codimension. However, we give a new class of such subalgebras which are not, in general, contained in any Veronese. We denote these subalgebras by
L(f)
, where
f\in\Bbbk[t]
. Given
f\in\Bbbk[t]
, the subalgebra
L(f)
is a
\Bbbk[f]
-submodule of
W_{\geq-1}
. We give two proofs that these subalgebras always have non-noetherian universal enveloping algebras. The first one is direct.

In Section
4
we make partial progress on a classification of subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
by attempting to prove the following conjecture.

Conjecture 0.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an infinite-dimensional subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
. Then there exists
f\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
L(f)
.

Subalgebras of finite codimension in
W_{\geq-1}
have already been classified in
[
PS21
]
; it is known that they satisfy Conjecture
0.4
. Therefore, it remains to prove the conjecture for subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
of infinite codimension.

Note that if Conjecture
0.4
holds, then so does Conjecture
0.3
. We tackle Conjecture
0.4
by introducing two closely related invariants of subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
. The first one is the
set of ratios
of
\mathfrak{g}
, defined as follows:

R(\mathfrak{g})=\left\{\frac{w}{u}\mid w\partial,u\partial\in\mathfrak{g},u\neq 0\right\}.

The second invariant is the
field of ratios

F(\mathfrak{g})=\Bbbk(R(\mathfrak{g}))
, the subfield of
\Bbbk(t)
generated by
R(\mathfrak{g})
.

We first consider the field of ratios, and prove the following result:

Theorem 0.5

(Theorem
4.3
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an infinite-dimensional Lie subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
. Then there exists some
f\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\{0\}
such that:

(1)

The field of ratios
F(\mathfrak{g})
is generated by
f
.

(2)

The subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
is contained in
L(f)
.

We further believe that
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in the subalgebra
L(f)
from Theorem
0.5
, but are not able to prove it.

The proof of Theorem
0.5
goes as follows: by Lüroth’s theorem there exists
f\in\Bbbk(t)
such that
F(\mathfrak{g})=\Bbbk(f)
. We first show that
f
can be chosen to be a polynomial, and then deduce that
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq L(f)
.

We then move on to the set of ratios
R(\mathfrak{g})
. In particular, we consider the situation where
R(\mathfrak{g})
is a field:

Theorem 0.6

(Theorem
4.4
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an infinite-dimensional Lie subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

There exists a nonzero
f\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
L(f)
.

(2)

The set
R(\mathfrak{g})
is a field.

(3)

There exists a nonzero
f\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
R(\mathfrak{g})=\Bbbk(f)
.

The proof of Theorem
0.6
follows by applying Theorem
0.5
and an analogous result to
[
PS21
, Proposition 3.19]
for subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
. As an immediate consequence of Theorem
0.6
, we get:

Corollary 0.7

(Corollary
4.6
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an infinite-dimensional Lie subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
. If
R(\mathfrak{g})
is a field then
U(\mathfrak{g})
is not noetherian.

If we do not assume that
R(\mathfrak{g})
is a field, then it is not clear is whether
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
L(f)
, or whether
U(\mathfrak{g})
can be noetherian in this case.

We finish the paper by finding a sufficient condition on a subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq W_{\geq-1}
, which we call
(*)
, for
U(\mathfrak{g})
to be non-noetherian. This condition is defined by analysing the image of
U(\mathfrak{g})
under a certain homomorphism

\Phi\colon U(W_{\geq-1})\to A_{1}(\Bbbk(y))[t^{-1}].

Here,
A_{1}(\Bbbk(y))=\Bbbk(y)[t,\partial]
denotes the first Weyl algebra over
\Bbbk(y)
. All known infinite-dimensional subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
satisfy
(*)
; in particular, we show that the subalgebras
L(f)
satisfy
(*)
, providing a second proof of the non-noetherianity of
U(L(f))
. We believe that condition
(*)
will be useful in future work on Conjecture
0.3
.

1.
Lie subalgebras of finite codimension

If
\mathfrak{h}
is a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
and
U(\mathfrak{g})
is noetherian, then so is
U(\mathfrak{h})

[
SW14
, Lemma 1.7]
, which is a consequence of the faithful flatness of
U(\mathfrak{g})
over
U(\mathfrak{h})
. It is much less clear when the converse implication holds. The main purpose of this section is to prove that when
\mathfrak{h}
has finite codimension in
\mathfrak{g}
, then the noetherianity of
U(\mathfrak{h})
is equivalent to the noetherianity of
U(\mathfrak{g})
. We will use this result extensively in later sections.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
be Lie algebras such that
\mathfrak{h}
has finite codimension in
\mathfrak{g}
. Then
U(\mathfrak{h})
is noetherian if and only if
U(\mathfrak{g})
is noetherian.

The key technique in the proof is to construct a filtration of
U(\mathfrak{g})
whose associated graded ring is noetherian if
U(\mathfrak{h})
is noetherian
1
1

1

The idea of this proof is due to Pavel Etingof, in personal correspondence with Susan Sierra and Chelsea Walton. We thank Prof. Etingof for allowing us to include it.
. This filtration will be constructed with the help of the following useful ideal of
\mathfrak{h}
.

Lemma 1.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
be Lie algebras such that
\mathfrak{h}
has finite codimension in
\mathfrak{g}
. Then

\mathfrak{k}=\{k\in\mathfrak{h}\mid[k,x]\in\mathfrak{h}\ \text{for all}\ x\in\mathfrak{g}\}

is an ideal of
\mathfrak{h}
of finite codimension.

Proof.

Consider the linear map

\displaystyle\varphi\colon\mathfrak{h}

\displaystyle\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h})

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle\mapsto\varphi_{h}

induced by the adjoint action of
\mathfrak{h}
on
\mathfrak{g}
. In other words,

\varphi_{h}(x+\mathfrak{h})=[h,x]+\mathfrak{h}

for all
x\in\mathfrak{g}
. We claim that
\varphi
is a well-defined Lie algebra homomorphism. Consider the adjoint map

\displaystyle\ad\colon\mathfrak{h}

\displaystyle\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle\mapsto\ad_{h}.

Then
\varphi_{h}(x+\mathfrak{h})=\ad_{h}(x)+\mathfrak{h}
for all
h\in\mathfrak{h}
. As
\ad_{h}
preserves
\mathfrak{h}
for
h\in\mathfrak{h}
, it follows that
\varphi
is well-defined, as claimed.

Note that
\mathfrak{k}=\ker(\varphi)
, so
\mathfrak{k}
is an ideal of
\mathfrak{h}
. Since
\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h})
is finite-dimensional, it follows that
\mathfrak{h}/\mathfrak{k}\cong\varphi(\mathfrak{h})\subseteq\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h})
is finite-dimensional.
∎

We now prove Proposition
1.1
.

Proof of Proposition
1.1
.

By
[
SW14
, Lemma 1.7]
, it suffices to prove that if
U(\mathfrak{h})
is noetherian so is
U(\mathfrak{g})
.

Suppose
U(\mathfrak{h})
is noetherian. Let
\mathfrak{k}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}
be as in Lemma
1.2
and let
x_{1}+\mathfrak{k},\ldots,x_{n}+\mathfrak{k}
be a basis for
\mathfrak{h}/\mathfrak{k}
, where
x_{i}\in\mathfrak{h}
. Similarly, let
y_{1}+\mathfrak{h},\ldots,y_{m}+\mathfrak{h}
be a basis for
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}
, where
y_{i}\in\mathfrak{g}
. Define a filtration
\mathcal{F}=(F_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
on
U(\mathfrak{g})
as follows:

F_{i}=\spn\{wx_{u_{1}}\ldots x_{u_{r}}y_{v_{1}}\ldots y_{v_{s}}\mid w\in U(\mathfrak{k}),r+2s\leq i\}.

We claim that
F_{i}F_{j}\subseteq F_{i+j}
, so that
\mathcal{F}
is indeed a filtration. First, we let
k\in\mathfrak{k}
and consider
x_{\ell}k
for some
\ell
:

x_{\ell}k=kx_{\ell}+[x_{\ell},k]

in
U(\mathfrak{g})
. Note that
[x_{\ell},k]\in\mathfrak{k}
since
\mathfrak{k}
is an ideal of
\mathfrak{h}
. Therefore,
x_{\ell}k\in F_{1}
. Inductively, we see that
x_{\ell}w\in F_{1}
for all
w\in U(\mathfrak{k})
.

Now consider
y_{\ell}k
:

y_{\ell}k=ky_{\ell}+[y_{\ell},k]

in
U(\mathfrak{g})
. Since
[y_{\ell},k]\in\mathfrak{h}\subseteq F_{2}
and
ky_{\ell}\in F_{2}
, it follows that
y_{\ell}k\in F_{2}
. Inductively, we see that
y_{\ell}w\in F_{2}
for all
w\in U(\mathfrak{k})
.

Finally, consider
y_{\ell}x_{\ell^{\prime}}
for some
\ell,\ell^{\prime}
:

y_{\ell}x_{\ell^{\prime}}=x_{\ell^{\prime}}y_{\ell}+[y_{\ell},x_{\ell^{\prime}}]

in
U(\mathfrak{g})
. It follows similarly to before that
y_{\ell}x_{\ell^{\prime}}\in F_{3}
. By induction,
y_{\ell}x_{u_{1}}\ldots x_{u_{r}}\in F_{r+2}
.

Let
z\in F_{j}
. Combining the above, we see that
x_{\ell}z\in F_{j+1}
and
y_{\ell}z\in F_{j+2}
for all
\ell
. Now, consider
z^{\prime}\in F_{i}
of the form

z^{\prime}=wx_{u_{1}}\ldots x_{u_{r}}y_{v_{1}}\ldots y_{v_{s}},\quad r+2s\leq i.

Since
x_{u_{r}}z\in F_{j+1}
and
y_{v_{s}}z\in F_{j+2}
, it now follows by an easy induction that
z^{\prime}z\in F_{i+j}
. Therefore,
F_{i}F_{j}\subseteq F_{i+j}
, as claimed.

Note that
F_{0}=U(\mathfrak{k})
,
x_{i}\in F_{1}\setminus F_{0}
and
y_{i}\in F_{2}\setminus F_{1}
, so that
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq F_{1}
and
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq F_{2}
. Consider the associated graded ring

\gr_{\mathcal{F}}U(\mathfrak{g})=\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{N}}F_{i}/F_{i-1}.

Let
\overline{x_{i}}=x_{i}+F_{0}\in\gr_{\mathcal{F}}U(\mathfrak{g})
and
\overline{y_{i}}=y_{i}+F_{1}\in\gr_{\mathcal{F}}U(\mathfrak{g})
. By definition of the filtration, it follows that
U(\mathfrak{k})\subseteq\gr_{\mathcal{F}}U(\mathfrak{g})
, and
\gr_{\mathcal{F}}U(\mathfrak{g})
is generated by
U(\mathfrak{k})
and the
\overline{x_{i}}
and
\overline{y_{j}}
as a
\Bbbk
-algebra. We have

\overline{x_{i}}\ \overline{x_{j}}-\overline{x_{j}}\ \overline{x_{i}}=[x_{i},x_{j}]+F_{1}.

But
[x_{i},x_{j}]\in\mathfrak{h}\subseteq F_{1}
, so
[x_{i},x_{j}]+F_{1}=0
and therefore
\overline{x_{i}}
and
\overline{x_{j}}
commute in
\gr_{\mathcal{F}}U(\mathfrak{g})
for all
i,j
. Similarly, we have

\overline{y_{i}}\ \overline{y_{j}}-\overline{y_{j}}\ \overline{y_{i}}=[y_{i},y_{j}]+F_{3}=0,

\overline{x_{i}}\ \overline{y_{j}}-\overline{y_{j}}\ \overline{x_{i}}=[x_{i},y_{j}]+F_{2}=0,

since
[y_{i},y_{j}]\in\mathfrak{g}\subseteq F_{2}\subseteq F_{3}
and
[x_{i},y_{j}]\in\mathfrak{g}\subseteq F_{2}
.

Now let
k\in\mathfrak{k}\setminus\{0\}\subseteq F_{0}
. We have

k\overline{x_{i}}-\overline{x_{i}}k=[k,x_{i}]+F_{0}=0,

k\overline{y_{i}}-\overline{y_{i}}k=[k,y_{i}]+F_{1}=0,

since
[k,x_{i}]\in\mathfrak{k}\subseteq F_{0}
by Lemma
1.2
and
[k,y_{i}]\in\mathfrak{h}\subseteq F_{1}
by definition of
\mathfrak{k}
.

Hence, the elements
\overline{x_{i}}
and
\overline{y_{j}}
are central in
\gr_{\mathcal{F}}U(\mathfrak{g})
, so we see that

\gr_{\mathcal{F}}U(\mathfrak{g})=U(\mathfrak{k})[\overline{x_{1}},\ldots,\overline{x_{n}},\overline{y_{1}},\ldots,\overline{y_{m}}].

Since
U(\mathfrak{h})
is noetherian, it follows by
[
SW14
, Lemma 1.7]
that
U(\mathfrak{k})
is noetherian. By Hilbert’s basis theorem,
\gr_{\mathcal{F}}U(\mathfrak{g})
is noetherian. Therefore,
U(\mathfrak{g})
is noetherian since it has a positive filtration whose associated graded ring is noetherian.
∎

2.
Krichever-Novikov algebras

In this section, we focus on universal enveloping algebras of Krichever-Novikov algebras, which we define below. In order to define these, we first need to recall the notion of a derivation of an associative algebra.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
A
be a
\Bbbk
-algebra and let
C
be an affine curve.

(1)

A
\Bbbk
-derivation
of
A
is a linear map
v\colon A\to A
which satisfies the Leibniz rule

v(ab)=av(b)+v(a)b

for all
a,b\in A
. We denote the Lie algebra of
\Bbbk
-derivations of
A
by
\Der(A)
.

(2)

A
vector field on
C
is a derivation of the coordinate ring
\Bbbk[C]
.

(3)

The
Krichever-Novikov algebra on
C
, denoted
\mathcal{L}(C)
, is the Lie algebra of vector fields on
C
. In other words,
\mathcal{L}(C)=\Der(\Bbbk[C])
with Lie bracket given by the commutator of derivations.

Remark 2.2
.

The standard definition of a Krichever-Novikov algebra assumes that the underlying curve is nonsingular (see, for example,
[
Sch14
]
). However, in this paper we also consider Lie algebras of vector fields on singular curves and also refer to them as Krichever-Novikov algebras.

The goal of this section is to prove that universal enveloping algebras of Krichever-Novikov algebras are not noetherian, providing a new class of infinite-dimensional Lie algebras with non-noetherian enveloping algebras.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
C
be an affine curve. Then
U(\mathcal{L}(C))
is not noetherian.

We establish notation for the Krichever-Novikov algebra on the affine line, which will be of central importance throughout the paper.

Notation 2.4
.

Let
W_{\geq-1}=\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{A}^{1})=\Der(\Bbbk[t])
. Then

W_{\geq-1}=\Bbbk[t]\partial,

where
\partial
denotes differentiation by
t
. The Lie bracket in
W_{\geq-1}
is given by

[f\partial,g\partial]=(fg^{\prime}-f^{\prime}g)\partial.

For
f\in\Bbbk[t]
, we let
W_{\geq-1}(f)=\Bbbk[t]f\partial
. It is clear that
W_{\geq-1}(f)
is a Lie subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
. We write
W_{\geq n}=W_{\geq-1}(t^{n+1})
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Remark 2.5
.

The notation
W_{\geq-1}
comes from the fact that
W_{\geq-1}
is a Lie subalgebra of the
Witt algebra

W=\Der(\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}])=\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}]\partial
. Letting
e_{n}=t^{n+1}\partial\in W
, we see that
\{e_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is a basis for
W
and

[e_{n},e_{m}]=(m-n)e_{n+m}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
W_{\geq-1}
is the Lie subalgebra of
W
spanned by
\{e_{n}\mid n\geq-1\}
, and
W_{\geq n}
is spanned by
\{e_{m}\mid m\geq n\}
. The
positive Witt algebra

W_{+}=W_{\geq 1}
is another noteworthy subalgebra of
W
.

The proof of Theorem
2.3
will go as follows: if
C
is nonsingular, we will show that there is an injective Lie algebra homomorphism

W_{\geq-1}(f)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}(C),

for some
f\in\Bbbk[t]
. We also show that
U(W_{\geq-1}(f))
is not noetherian, which is enough to conclude that
U(\mathcal{L}(C))
is not noetherian.

If
C
is singular, we will consider the normalisation
\widetilde{C}
of
C
, and construct a Lie subalgebra
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
of both
\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{C})
and
\mathcal{L}(C)
which has finite codimension in both. The non-noetherianity of
U(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})
will follow from the non-noetherianity of
U(\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{C}))
by Proposition
1.1
, allowing us to conclude that

is not noetherian.

The following result is the main theorem of
[

]
.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
SW14
, Theorem 0.5]
)
.

The universal enveloping algebra of the positive Witt algebra

is not noetherian.

It follows from Proposition
1.1
and Sierra and Walton’s work that

and its subalgebras of the form

have non-noetherian universal enveloping algebras.

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
f\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\{0\}
. Then
U(W_{\geq-1}(f))
is not noetherian.

Proof.

Since
W_{+}\subseteq W_{\geq-1}
, it follows that

is not noetherian by

, Lemma 1.7]
and Theorem

. Note that

In order to construct the homomorphism
W_{\geq-1}(f)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}(C)
for the proof of Theorem
2.3
, some technical results about derivations are required. We will need to consider dominant morphisms between affine curves, and localisations of coordinate rings. We recall these notions below.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
X
and
Y
be affine curves. A morphism
\pi\colon X\to Y
is
dominant
if
\pi(X)
is dense in
Y
(in the Zariski topology).

Remark 2.9
.

Let
X,Y
be affine curves, and let
A=\Bbbk[X],B=\Bbbk[Y]
. If
\pi\colon X\to Y
is a dominant morphism, then the homomorphism

\displaystyle\pi^{*}\colon B

\displaystyle\to A

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto f\circ\pi

is injective, so we make the identification
B=\pi^{*}(B)\subseteq A
.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
\pi\colon X\to Y
be a dominant morphism of nonsingular affine curves. Let
A=\Bbbk[X]
and
B=\Bbbk[Y]\subseteq A
. Let
K=Q(A)
and
L=Q(B)\subseteq K
be the fields of fractions of
A
and
B
, respectively. Let
P\in X
and
Q=\pi(P)
, and let

A_{P}=\{\frac{f}{g}\in K\mid f,g\in A,g(P)\neq 0\},

B_{Q}=\{\frac{f}{g}\in L\mid f,g\in B,g(Q)\neq 0\}\subseteq A_{P}.

The rings
A_{P}
and
B_{Q}
are discrete valuation rings, so let
s
and
t
be uniformising parameters of
A_{P}
and
B_{Q}
, respectively. Let
\nu_{P}
be the discrete valuation on
K
associated to
A_{P}
(i.e.
\nu_{P}(as^{k})=k
for
a\in A_{P}^{*}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
). The
ramification index

e_{P}
of
\pi
at the point
P
is defined as

e_{P}=\nu_{P}(t)\geq 1

where
t
is viewed as an element of
A_{P}
. If
e_{P}>1
, we say that
\pi
is
ramified
at
P
and that
Q
is a
branch point
of
\pi
. If
e_{P}=1
, we say that
\pi
is
unramified
at
P
.

The following proposition shows that the branch point locus of a dominant morphism is a finite set, a fact that we will use implicitly from now on.

Proposition 2.11

(
[
Har77
, Proposition IV.2.2(a)]
)
.

Let
\pi\colon X\to Y
be a dominant morphism of nonsingular affine curves. Then
\pi
is ramified at only finitely many points.

The next result is a technical lemma about restricting derivations to the local ring
A_{P}
.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
X
be a nonsingular affine curve, let
A=\Bbbk[X]
, and let
K=Q(A)
. Let
P\in X
, and let
s\in A_{P}
be a uniformising parameter. Then a derivation
v\in\Der(K)
restricts to a derivation of
A_{P}
if and only if
v(s)\in A_{P}
.

Proof.

Suppose
v\in\Der(K)
such that
v(s)\in A_{P}
. Let
\mathfrak{m}=(s)
be the maximal ideal of
A_{P}
, and consider the completion

\widehat{A_{P}}=\varprojlim_{k\in\mathbb{N}}A_{P}/\mathfrak{m}^{k}\cong\Bbbk[[s]].

Then
v
extends uniquely to a derivation
\hat{v}
of
Q(\widehat{A_{P}})\cong\Bbbk((s))
. We have

\hat{v}(s)=v(s)\in A_{P}\subseteq\widehat{A_{P}}.

It follows that
\hat{v}(\widehat{A_{P}})\subseteq\widehat{A_{P}}
, since
\widehat{A_{P}}\cong\Bbbk[[s]]
and
\hat{v}
is
\Bbbk
-linear. Hence, we have

v(A_{P})=\hat{v}(A_{P})=\hat{v}(\widehat{A_{P}}\cap K)\subseteq\widehat{A_{P}}\cap K=A_{P},

and therefore
v
restricts to a derivation of
A_{P}
.
∎

Definition 2.13
.

Let
X
be an affine curve. We say that a vector field
v\in\mathcal{L}(X)

vanishes at

x\in X
if
v(f)(x)=0
for all
f\in\Bbbk[X]
. If
S
is a finite subset of
X
, we denote the Lie subalgebra of
\mathcal{L}(X)
consisting of vector fields which vanish on
S
by
\mathcal{L}_{S}(X)
.

The following proposition shows that if we have a dominant morphism
\pi\colon X\to Y
of nonsingular curves, a vector field on
Y
induces a vector field on
X
provided it vanishes on the branch point locus of
\pi
. This gives an injective homomorphism from a subalgebra of
\mathcal{L}(Y)
to
\mathcal{L}(X)
, which will be one of the main ingredients in the proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
X
and
Y
be nonsingular affine curves, and let
\pi\colon X\to Y
be a dominant morphism. Let
S\subseteq Y
be the branch point locus of
\pi
. Then any vector field
v\in\mathcal{L}_{S}(Y)
extends uniquely to a vector field
\widetilde{v}\in\mathcal{L}(X)
and the map

\displaystyle\varphi\colon\mathcal{L}_{S}(Y)

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{L}(X)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\mapsto\widetilde{v}

defines an injective Lie algebra homomorphism.

Proof.

Let
A=\Bbbk[X]
and
B=\Bbbk[Y]\subseteq A
. Let
v\in\mathcal{L}(Y)=\Der(B)
be a vector field which vanishes on
S
. Since
\pi
is dominant, we identify
B
with
\pi^{*}(B)\subseteq A
. Let
K=Q(A)
and
L=Q(B)\subseteq K
be the fields of fractions of
A
and
B
, respectively. Then
v
extends uniquely to some
v_{L}\in\Der(L)
. Since
\pi
is dominant, it follows by
[
Har77
, Proposition II.6.8]
that
\dim_{L}K<\infty
, so
v_{L}
extends uniquely to some
v_{K}\in\Der(K)
. Let
\widetilde{v}\colon A\to K
be the restriction of
v_{K}
to
A
. We claim that
\widetilde{v}(A)\subseteq A
, so that
\widetilde{v}\in\Der(A)=\mathcal{L}(X)
.

For
P\in X
, let
A_{P}=\{\frac{f}{g}\in K\mid f,g\in A,g(P)\neq 0\}
, and let
s_{P}\in A_{P}
be a uniformising parameter of
A_{P}
. By Lemma
2.12
,
v_{K}(A_{P})\subseteq A_{P}
if and only if
v_{K}(s_{P})\in A_{P}
. Since

A=\bigcap_{P\in X}A_{P},

it then follows that that
\widetilde{v}(A)\subseteq A
if and only if
v_{K}(s_{P})\in A_{P}
for all
P\in X
. Hence, it suffices to show that
v_{K}(s_{P})\in A_{P}
for an arbitrary
P\in X
.

Fix
P\in X
and let
Q=\pi(P)\in Y
. Let
B_{Q}=\{\frac{f}{g}\in L\mid f,g\in B,g(Q)\neq 0\}
, and let
t\in B_{Q}
be a uniformising parameter of
B_{Q}
. For simplicity of notation, let
s=s_{P}
. Since
t\in B_{Q}\subseteq A_{P}
is a uniformising parameter of
B_{Q}
, it follows that
t=as^{e}
for some
a\in A_{P}^{*}
, where
e=e_{P}
is the ramification index of
\pi
at
P
. Furthermore,
v(t)\in B_{Q}
so
v(t)=bt^{f}
for some
b\in B_{Q}^{*}
and some
f\geq 0
. Note that
f
is the order of vanishing of
v(t)
at the point
Q
, so in particular
f\geq 1
if
v
vanishes at
Q
.

As in the proof of Lemma
2.12
, consider the completion
\widehat{A_{P}}\cong\Bbbk[[s]]
and the unique extension of
v
to a derivation
\hat{v}
of
Q(\widehat{A_{P}})\cong\Bbbk((s))
. Similarly, the completion
\widehat{B_{Q}}\subseteq\widehat{A_{P}}
is isomorphic to
\Bbbk[[t]]
. Now, we have

(2.1)

t=s^{e}\sum_{i\geq 0}\alpha_{i}s^{i}\in\widehat{A_{P}}

(2.2)

v(t)=t^{f}\sum_{i\geq 0}\beta_{i}t^{i}\in\widehat{B_{Q}}

for some
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in\Bbbk
with
\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}\neq 0
. From (
2.1
), we get

(2.3)

v(t)=\hat{v}(s^{e}\sum_{i\geq 0}\alpha_{i}s^{i})=v_{K}(s)s^{e-1}\sum_{i\geq 0}\gamma_{i}s^{i},

where
\gamma_{i}=(e+i)\alpha_{i}\in\Bbbk
. Since
\gamma_{0}=e\alpha_{0}\neq 0
, the element
H=\sum_{i\geq 0}\gamma_{i}s^{i}\in\widehat{A_{P}}
is invertible in
\widehat{A_{P}}\cong\Bbbk[[s]]
. Let
F=H^{-1}\in\widehat{A_{P}}
.

We can also combine (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) to get

(2.4)

v(t)=t^{f}\sum_{i\geq 0}\beta_{i}t^{i}=s^{ef}\sum_{i\geq 0}\mu_{i}s^{i},

for some
\mu_{k}\in\Bbbk
.

Let
G=\sum_{i\geq 0}\mu_{i}s^{i}\in\widehat{A_{P}}
. Equating (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), we get
v_{K}(s)s^{e-1}H=s^{ef}G
, and therefore

v_{K}(s)=s^{e(f-1)+1}FG.

If
e=1
, then
e(f-1)+1\geq 0
since
f\geq 0
. If
e\geq 2
, then the point
P
is ramified, so
v
vanishes at
Q
by assumption. Therefore,
f\geq 1
and
e(f-1)+1\geq 1
. In either case, we see that
e(f-1)+1\geq 0
. Hence,
v_{K}(s)=s^{e(f-1)+1}FG\in\widehat{A_{P}}
.

But we also know that
v_{K}(s)\in K
, so that

v_{K}(s)\in\widehat{A_{P}}\cap K=A_{P}.

We conclude that
\widetilde{v}\in\Der(A)
, as claimed.

It follows that the map
\varphi\colon\mathcal{L}_{S}(Y)\to\mathcal{L}(X)
defined in the statement of the proposition is a Lie algebra homomorphism by uniqueness of the extension: if
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{S}(Y)
, then the vector field

[\widetilde{v_{1}},\widetilde{v_{2}}]=\widetilde{v_{1}}\circ\widetilde{v_{2}}-\widetilde{v_{2}}\circ\widetilde{v_{1}}\in\mathcal{L}(X)

restricts to

{\left.\kern-1.2pt[\widetilde{v_{1}},\widetilde{v_{2}}]\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{B}}=v_{1}\circ v_{2}-v_{2}\circ v_{1}=[v_{1},v_{2}]\in\mathcal{L}_{S}(Y).

By uniqueness of the extension, we conclude that

[\varphi(v_{1}),\varphi(v_{2})]=[\widetilde{v_{1}},\widetilde{v_{2}}]=\varphi([v_{1},v_{2}]).

Furthermore,
\varphi
is clearly injective since
\widetilde{v}
is an extension of
v
.
∎

We now have all the necessary tools to prove Theorem
2.3
.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

First we consider the case where
C
is nonsingular. There is an injective homomorphism
\Bbbk[t]\hookrightarrow\Bbbk[C]
, which induces a dominant morphism
\pi\colon C\to\mathbb{A}^{1}
. Let
S=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
be the branch point locus of
\pi
, and let
f=(t-x_{1})\ldots(t-x_{n})\in\Bbbk[t]
. Consider

\mathcal{L}_{S}(\mathbb{A}^{1})=W_{\geq-1}(f),

and let

\displaystyle\varphi\colon\mathcal{L}_{S}(\mathbb{A}^{1})

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}(C)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\mapsto\widetilde{v}

be the injective Lie algebra homomorphism from Proposition
2.14
. By Corollary
2.7
, we know that
U(\mathcal{L}_{S}(\mathbb{A}^{1}))=U(W_{\geq-1}(f))
is not noetherian, and therefore
U(\mathcal{L}(C))
is not noetherian.

Now suppose
C
is singular, and let
\widetilde{C}
be its normalisation. Set the following notation:

\displaystyle A=\Bbbk[C],\quad

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}=\Bbbk[\widetilde{C}],

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(C),\quad

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}=\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{C}).

We will view
A
as a subring of
\widetilde{A}
. Let

I=\Ann_{A}(\widetilde{A}/A)=\{a\in A\mid a\widetilde{A}\subseteq A\}.

Then
I
is an ideal of both
A
and
\widetilde{A}
. Letting
v\in\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
and
a\in I
, we have that

av(A)\subseteq A.

It follows that
av
is a derivation of
A
, that is
av\in\mathcal{L}
. Therefore,
I\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
.

We can check that

[a_{1}v_{1},a_{2}v_{2}]=a_{1}a_{2}[v_{1},v_{2}]+a_{1}v_{1}(a_{2})v_{2}-a_{2}v_{2}(a_{1})v_{1}\in I\widetilde{\mathcal{L}},

where
a_{1},a_{2}\in I
and
v_{1},v_{2}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
. Hence,
I\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is a Lie subalgebra of both
\mathcal{L}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
.

Since
\mathcal{L}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
are rank 1 torsion-free modules over
A
and
\widetilde{A}
, respectively, it follows that
I\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
has finite codimension in both
\mathcal{L}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
. We have already shown that
U(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}})
is not noetherian, so it follows that
U(I\widetilde{\mathcal{L}})
is not noetherian by Proposition
1.1
. Therefore,
U(\mathcal{L})
is not noetherian.
∎

We finish this section by briefly mentioning some other related Lie algebras.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
C
be an affine curve and let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra. We define the following Lie algebras:

(1)

The
Lie algebra of differential operators of degree
\leq 1
on
C
is defined as the semidirect sum
\mathcal{D}^{1}(C)=\Bbbk[C]\rtimes\mathcal{L}(C)
. In other words,
\mathcal{D}^{1}(C)=\Bbbk[C]\oplus\mathcal{L}(C)
as a vector space, with Lie bracket given by

[(f_{1},v_{1}),(f_{2},v_{2})]=(v_{1}(f_{2})-v_{2}(f_{1}),[v_{1},v_{2}]),

where
f_{1},f_{2}\in\Bbbk[C]
and
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{L}(C)
.

(2)

The
current algebra

\overline{\mathfrak{g}}(C)=\mathfrak{g}\otimes_{\Bbbk}\Bbbk[C]
has Lie bracket

[x\otimes f,y\otimes g]=[x,y]\otimes fg,

where
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
and
f,g\in\Bbbk[C]
.

These Lie algebras, as well as
\mathcal{L}(C)
, are called
Krichever-Novikov type algebras
(cf.
[
Sch14
, Definition 2.21]
).

An easy consequence of Theorem
2.3
is that the enveloping algebra of a Krichever-Novikov type algebra is not noetherian.

Corollary 2.16
.

Krichever-Novikov type algebras have non-noetherian universal enveloping algebras.

Proof.

Let
C
be an affine curve and let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra. Consider
\overline{\mathfrak{g}}(C)
and
\mathcal{D}^{1}(C)
.

To see that
U(\overline{\mathfrak{g}}(C))
is not noetherian, we simply note that
\overline{\mathfrak{g}}(C)
has an infinite-dimensional abelian Lie subalgebra, which clearly has a non-noetherian enveloping algebra. For example, we can take the subalgebra spanned by
\{x\otimes f^{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
, where
x\in\mathfrak{g}\setminus\{0\}
and
f\in\Bbbk[C]\setminus\{0\}
.

The non-noetherianity of
U(\mathcal{D}^{1}(C))
follows directly from Theorem
2.3
, since
\mathcal{D}^{1}(C)
contains
\mathcal{L}(C)
as a Lie subalgebra.
∎

3.
Subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
of infinite codimension

We would like to prove that any infinite-dimensional subalgebra of a Krichever-Novikov algebra has a non-noetherian universal enveloping algebra. In order to achieve this, we first focus on subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
, with the eventual aim of extending the results to arbitrary Krichever-Novikov algebras using similar methods to those in Section
2
.

A special case of Conjecture 0.1 of
[
SW14
]
is:

Conjecture 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an infinite-dimensional Lie subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
. Then
U(\mathfrak{g})
is not noetherian.

The main difficulty in proving this conjecture is that we do not know what a general subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
looks like. Therefore, we first focus on graded subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
and show that Conjecture
3.1
holds for these.

3.1.
Veronese and graded subalgebras

Since many of the details are straightforward, we omit some of the proofs in this subsection.

Note that
W_{\geq-1}
is a graded Lie algebra. Under the standard grading of
W_{\geq-1}
, the element
e_{n}
is homogeneous of degree
n
. However, this grading is not unique: fix
x\in\Bbbk^{*}
, and define
e_{n}(x)=(t-x)^{n+1}\partial
for all
n\geq-1
. It is easy to see that

[e_{n}(x),e_{m}(x)]=(m-n)e_{n+m}(x).

Hence, we could also have chosen a different grading of
W_{\geq-1}
, where
e_{n}(x)
is a homogeneous element of degree
n
. We call this grading the
grading of
W_{\geq-1}
based at
x
. Note that
e_{n}
is no longer homogeneous under this grading. We can therefore consider Veronese subalgebras with respect to the different choices of grading of
W_{\geq-1}
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer and let
x\in\Bbbk
. The
d
-Veronese subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
based at
x
, denoted
\Ver_{d}(x)
, is the Lie subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
with basis

\{e_{0}(x),e_{d}(x),e_{2d}(x),\ldots\}.

For brevity, when
x=0
we simply write
\Ver_{d}
instead of
\Ver_{d}(0)
.

Remark 3.3
.

Veronese subalgebras are the simplest infinite-dimensional subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
of infinite codimension. They are therefore the first examples of subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
for which Proposition
1.1
does not apply.

It is rather straightforward to prove that these subalgebras have non-noetherian universal enveloping algebras; we merely have to notice that they are isomorphic to
W_{\geq 0}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer and let
x\in\Bbbk
. Then
U(\Ver_{d}(x))
is not noetherian.

Proof.

Let
f_{n}=\frac{1}{d}e_{nd}(x)\in\Ver_{d}(x)
. Then
\{f_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a basis for
\Ver_{d}(x)
, and

[f_{n},f_{m}]=\frac{1}{d^{2}}[e_{nd}(x),e_{md}(x)]=\frac{1}{d^{2}}(md-nd)e_{(n+m)d}(x)=(m-n)f_{n+m}.

Therefore,
\Ver_{d}(x)\cong W_{\geq 0}
, where the isomorphism maps
f_{n}\mapsto e_{n}
. By Corollary
2.7
,
U(W_{\geq 0})
is not noetherian, so
U(\Ver_{d}(x))
is not noetherian.
∎

Our next goal is to prove that general infinite-dimensional graded subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
have non-noetherian universal enveloping algebras. For simplicity, we only consider the standard grading of
W_{\geq-1}
, but the same results will hold for the different choices of grading. This is because the map

\displaystyle W_{\geq-1}

\displaystyle\to W_{\geq-1}^{(x)}

\displaystyle e_{n}

\displaystyle\mapsto e_{n}(x)

is a graded isomorphism of Lie algebras, where
W_{\geq-1}^{(x)}
is
W_{\geq-1}
with grading based at
x
.

Graded subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
are some of the easiest to understand since they have a basis which is a subset of
\{e_{n}\mid n\geq-1\}
. We will mostly be concerned with leading terms of elements of
W_{\geq-1}
, so we establish the following notation.

Notation 3.5
.

For
\lambda\in\Bbbk
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, when we write
a=\lambda e_{n}+\ldots
, we mean that there exist
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n+1}\in\Bbbk
such that

a=\lambda e_{n}+\alpha_{1}e_{n-1}+\ldots+\alpha_{n}e_{0}+\alpha_{n+1}e_{-1}.

For non-homogeneous
a\in W_{\geq-1}
, we write
\deg(a)=n
to mean
a=\lambda e_{n}+\ldots
for some
\lambda\in\Bbbk^{*}
.

Remark 3.6
.

Let
a,b\in W_{\geq-1}
with
\deg(a)\neq\deg(b)
. Then
\deg([a,b])=\deg(a)+\deg(b)
.

The following result gives a way of generating elements of high degree in a subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq W_{\geq-1}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
, let
a,b\in\mathfrak{g}
and let
n=\deg(a)
,
m=\deg(b)
. Suppose
n,m\geq 1
and
n\neq m
, and let
d=\gcd(n,m)
. Then there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
\ell\geq k
, there is an element
c_{\ell}\in\mathfrak{g}
with
\deg(c_{\ell})=\ell d
.

Proof.

For all
i,j\geq 1
, by taking Lie brackets of
a
and
b
we see that there is an element
h_{ij}\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
\deg(h_{ij})=in+jm
. For example, we can set
h_{ij}=\ad_{b}^{j-1}(\ad_{a}^{i}(b))
.

By the Euclidean algorithm, there exist
u,v\in\mathbb{N}
such that

un-vm=d.

Let
r=\frac{n}{d}
, let
s=n+((r-1)v+1)m
, and let
k=\frac{s}{d}
. Setting
c_{k}=h_{1,(r-1)v+1}\in\mathfrak{g}
, we see that
\deg(c_{k})=s=kd
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k\leq\ell<k+r
, and let
q=\ell-k
. We have

\displaystyle\ell d

\displaystyle=(k+q)d=s+qd=(n+((r-1)v+1)m)+q(un-vm)

\displaystyle=(qu+1)n+((r-q-1)v+1)m

with
qu+1\geq 1
and
(r-q-1)v+1\geq 1
, since
0\leq q<r
. Setting

c_{\ell}=h_{qu+1,(r-q-1)v+1}\in\mathfrak{g},

we see that
\deg(c_{\ell})=\ell d
.

Now consider
\ell\geq k+r
. There exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k\leq\ell-Nr<k+r
. We have already constructed
c_{\ell-Nr}\in\mathfrak{g}
. Define
c_{\ell}=\ad_{a}^{N}(c_{\ell-Nr})\in\mathfrak{g}
. Then

\deg(c_{\ell})=(\ell-Nr)d+Nn=\ell d-Nrd+Nn=\ell d,

since
rd=n
, so we are done.
∎

When applied to graded subalgebras, Lemma
3.7
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an infinite-dimensional graded Lie subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
. Then either
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
W_{\geq-1}
or
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
\Ver_{d}
for some
d\geq 2
. ∎

Combining the results above, it follows that infinite-dimensional graded subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
have non-noetherian enveloping algebras.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an infinite-dimensional graded Lie subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
. Then
U(\mathfrak{g})
is not noetherian. ∎

3.2.
The subalgebras
L(f,g)

We turn our attention to subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
which are not necessarily graded. If
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq\Ver_{d}(x)
for some
x\in\Bbbk
, where
d\geq 2
is maximal, then consider the isomorphism

\varphi\colon\Ver_{d}(x)\xrightarrow{\sim}W_{\geq 0}

from Lemma
3.4
. By maximality of
d
, it follows that
\varphi(\mathfrak{g})
is not contained in any Veronese subalgebra.

Veronese subalgebras, of course, have infinite codimension in
W_{\geq-1}
. It is not immediately obvious that there are any infinite-dimensional subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
of infinite codimension which do not have finite codimension in a Veronese subalgebra; the goal of this subsection is to provide examples of such. By the previous paragraph, without loss of generality we seek for an infinite-dimensional subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
of infinite codimension which is not contained in a Veronese subalgebra.

The following result gives a useful method to determine whether some element of
W_{\geq-1}
is contained in a Veronese subalgebra.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
a=e_{n}+\alpha e_{n-1}+\ldots\in W_{\geq-1}
. If
a\in\Ver_{d}(x)
for some
d\geq 2
and some
x\in\Bbbk
, then
\alpha=-(n+1)x
. In particular, either
a\in\Ver_{d}(\frac{-\alpha}{n+1})
for some
d\geq 2
or
a
is not contained in any Veronese subalgebra.

Proof.

Suppose
a\in\Ver_{d}(x)
for some
d\geq 2
and some
x\in\Bbbk
. Then

a=e_{n}(x)+\beta e_{n-d}(x)+\ldots,

for some
\beta\in\Bbbk
. Therefore, we have

a=(t-x)^{n+1}\partial+\beta(t-x)^{n-d+1}\partial+\ldots=e_{n}-(n+1)xe_{n-1}+\ldots\quad(\text{since }d\geq 2)

and hence we see that
\alpha=-(n+1)x
.
∎

We now define a new family of subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
. Members of this family will provide examples of infinite-dimensional subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
of infinite codimension which are not contained in any Veronese subalgebra.

Notation 3.11
.

For
f,g\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\{0\}
, we let
L(f,g)
be the subspace of
W_{\geq-1}
spanned by
\{f^{n}g\partial\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. In other words,
L(f,g)=\Bbbk[f]g\partial
.

Note that if
\deg(f)>1
, then
L(f,g)
has infinite codimension in
W_{\geq-1}
. The next lemma characterises when
L(f,g)
is a Lie algebra.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
f,g\in\Bbbk[t]
. Then
L(f,g)
is a Lie subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
if and only if
f^{\prime}g\in\Bbbk[f]
(i.e.
f^{\prime}g=h(f)
for some
h\in\Bbbk[t]
). Furthermore, if
f^{\prime}g=h(f)
, then
L(f,g)\cong W_{\geq-1}(h)
.

Proof.

Note that

[f^{n}g\partial,f^{m}g\partial]=(m-n)f^{n+m-1}f^{\prime}g^{2}\partial.

It is now clear that
L(f,g)=\Bbbk[f]g\partial
is closed under the Lie bracket of
W_{\geq-1}
if and only if
f^{\prime}g\in\Bbbk[f]
.

For the final sentence, suppose
f^{\prime}g=h(f)
. We can easily check that the map

\displaystyle L(f,g)

\displaystyle\to W_{\geq-1}(h)

\displaystyle f^{n}g\partial

\displaystyle\mapsto t^{n}h\partial

is an isomorphism of Lie algebras.
∎

The following proposition shows that
f
can be arbitrary, and that for a fixed
f\in\Bbbk[t]
there is a maximal Lie algebra of the form
L(f,g)
.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
f\in\Bbbk[t]
be a non-constant polynomial. Then there exists a (unique up to scalar) polynomial
g_{f}\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\{0\}
such that

(1)

f^{\prime}g_{f}\in\Bbbk[f]
,

(2)

If
h\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
f^{\prime}h\in\Bbbk[f]
then
\deg(h)\geq\deg(g_{f})
.

Furthermore, if
f^{\prime}h\in\Bbbk[f]
then
h\in\Bbbk[f]g_{f}
and
L(f,h)
has finite codimension in
L(f,g_{f})
. We write
L(f)=L(f,g_{f})
.

Proof.

Let

I(f)=\{P\in\Bbbk[t]\mid f^{\prime}\text{ divides }P(f)\}.

It is immediate that
I(f)
is an ideal of
\Bbbk[t]
. We claim that
I(f)\neq 0
. Let
Q\in\Bbbk[t]
be a polynomial that vanishes at
\{f(\alpha)\mid\alpha\in\overline{\Bbbk},f^{\prime}(\alpha)=0\}
. Then
Q(f)
vanishes at all the roots of
f^{\prime}
. Therefore, if we take
n\in\mathbb{N}
large enough, we have that
P=Q^{n}\in I(f)\setminus\{0\}
.

The ideal
I(f)
is nonzero and principal, so there exists some
P_{f}\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\{0\}
such that

I(f)=(P_{f}).

Let
g_{f}=P_{f}(f)/f^{\prime}
.

Suppose we have
h\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
f^{\prime}h\in\Bbbk[f]
. Write

f^{\prime}h=P(f),

where
P\in\Bbbk[t]
. Then
P\in I(f)=(P_{f})
, so
P_{f}
divides
P
. Therefore, there exists some
Q\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
P=P_{f}Q
. It follows that

h=P(f)/f^{\prime}=P_{f}(f)Q(f)/f^{\prime}=Q(f)g_{f}.

Hence,

L(f,h)=\Bbbk[f]h\partial=\Bbbk[f]Q(f)g_{f}\partial\subseteq\Bbbk[f]g_{f}\partial=L(f).

It is easy to see that
\dim L(f,h)/L(f)=\deg Q<\infty
.
∎

Remark 3.14
.

Let
f,g,h\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
f^{\prime}g=h(f)
. We have

\deg(f)-1+\deg(g)=\deg(h)\deg(f),

and therefore

\deg(g)=(\deg(h)-1)\deg(f)+1.

Consider
p(f)g\partial\in L(f,g)
, where
p\in\Bbbk[t]
. Then

\displaystyle\deg(p(f)g\partial)

\displaystyle=\deg(p(f)g)-1=\deg(p)\deg(f)+\deg(g)-1

\displaystyle=(\deg(p)+\deg(h)-1)\deg(f).

Therefore, the degrees of elements of
L(f,g)
are multiples of
\deg(f)
.

Since we already know that
U(W_{\geq-1}(h))
is not noetherian, the following result follows immediately from Lemma
3.12
.

Proposition 3.15
.

U(L(f,g))
is not noetherian for any
f,g\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\{0\}
such that
f^{\prime}g\in\Bbbk[f]
and
\deg(f)\geq 1
. ∎

The example below shows that we can construct subalgebras of infinite codimension in
W_{\geq-1}
which are not contained in any Veronese subalgebra.

Example 3.16
.

If we let
f=t^{3}+3t
then we claim that
g_{f}=(t^{2}+1)(t^{2}+4)
. Indeed,
f^{\prime}g_{f}=3(f^{2}+4)\in\Bbbk[f]
, and it is easy to see that
f^{\prime}h\not\in\Bbbk[f]
for all
h\in\Bbbk[t]
of degree 1, so
g_{f}
has minimal degree.

Note that
L(f)
is an infinite-dimensional subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
of infinite codimension. Furthermore, we can easily use Lemma
3.10
to show that
L(f)
is not contained in any Veronese subalgebra.

4.
Working toward a classification of subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}

In this section, we attempt a classification of infinite-dimensional subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
. Subalgebras of finite codimension in
W_{\geq-1}
have already been classified in
[
PS21
]
: if
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
W_{\geq-1}
then there exist
f\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\{0\},n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

W_{\geq-1}(f^{n})\subseteq\mathfrak{g}\subseteq W_{\geq-1}(f).

Therefore, it remains to classify subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
of infinite codimension. We believe that the subalgebras
L(f,g)
from Subsection
3.2
are infinite-codimensional analogues of the subalgebras
W_{\geq-1}(f)
, in the following sense:

Conjecture 4.1
.

If
\mathfrak{g}
is an infinite-dimensional subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
then there exist
f,g\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
f^{\prime}g\in\Bbbk[f]
and

L(f,g)\subseteq\mathfrak{g}\subseteq L(f).

In particular,
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
L(f)
.

Note that if Conjecture
4.1
holds, then so does Conjecture
3.1
.

In fact, in order to prove Conjecture
4.1
, it suffices to prove that if
\mathfrak{g}
is an infinite-dimensional subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
then there exists
f\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
L(f)
. This can be seen by noting that if
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
L(f)
then
\mathfrak{g}
is isomorphic to a subalgebra of finite codimension in
W_{\geq-1}
, by Lemma
3.12
. Thus, we can apply Petukhov and Sierra’s result to deduce that
\mathfrak{g}
contains a subalgebra isomorphic to
W_{\geq-1}(h)
, for some
h\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\{0\}
. This subalgebra is of the form
L(f,g)
, for some
g\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\{0\}
.

In this section, we show that the subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
must be contained in some
L(f)
in a canonical way, but we have not been able to show that
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
L(f)
. In fact, we do not know of any infinite-dimensional subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
which does not have finite codimension in some
L(f)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
and suppose
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
L(f)
for some
f\in\Bbbk[t]
. If
\mathfrak{g}=L(f)
then by considering ratios
\frac{w}{u}
, where
w\partial,u\partial\in\mathfrak{g}
, we can generate any element of
\Bbbk(f)
. This suggests that looking at such ratios should give us a way of extracting the polynomial
f
from the subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
. To this end, we introduce the following invariants of subalgebras of
W_{\geq-1}
:

Definition 4.2
.

If
\mathfrak{g}
is a subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
then we define the
set of ratios

R(\mathfrak{g})
of
\mathfrak{g}
by

R(\mathfrak{g})=\left\{\frac{w}{u}\in\Bbbk(t)\mid w\partial,u\partial\in\mathfrak{g},u\neq 0\right\}.

We define the
field of ratios

F(\mathfrak{g})
of
\mathfrak{g}
as the subfield of
\Bbbk(t)
generated by
R(\mathfrak{g})
.

We start by considering the field of ratios: the following result shows that
F(\mathfrak{g})=\Bbbk(f)
for some
f\in\Bbbk[t]
and, furthermore,
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq L(f)
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an infinite-dimensional Lie subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
and let
K\subseteq\Bbbk(t)
be a field containing
R(\mathfrak{g})
. Then there exists some
f\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\{0\}
such that:

(1)

The field
K
is generated by
f
.

(2)

The subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
is contained in
L(f)
.

We leave the proof of Theorem
4.3
for later, and instead focus on the set of ratios
R(\mathfrak{g})
. Certainly,
R(\mathfrak{g})
is closed under scalar multiplication and taking multiplicative inverses, but it is not clear if it satisfies any other properties. As suggested by Theorem
4.3
, it will be of particular interest to consider situations where
R(\mathfrak{g})
is a field. In fact, we have:

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an infinite-dimensional Lie subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

There exists a nonzero
f\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
L(f)
.

(2)

The set
R(\mathfrak{g})
is a field.

(3)

There exists a nonzero
f\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
R(\mathfrak{g})=\Bbbk(f)
.

Proof.

The implication
(3)\Rightarrow(2)
is clear. We will show that
(1)\Rightarrow(3)
and
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
.

(1)\Rightarrow(3)
: Suppose there exists
f\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
L(f)
. Then
R(\mathfrak{g})\subseteq\Bbbk(f)
. We will show that
R(\mathfrak{g})=\Bbbk(f)
.

Let
g=g_{f}
in the notation of Proposition
3.13
, so
L(f)=L(f,g)
, and let
h\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
f^{\prime}g=h(f)
. Consider the isomorphism

\displaystyle\varphi\colon L(f)

\displaystyle\to W_{\geq-1}(h)

\displaystyle p(f)g\partial

\displaystyle\mapsto ph\partial

from Lemma
3.12
, where
p\in\Bbbk[t]
. Let
\mathfrak{k}=\varphi(\mathfrak{g})
. Since
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
L(f)
and
W_{\geq-1}(h)
has finite codimension in
W_{\geq-1}
, it follows that
\mathfrak{k}
has finite codimension in
W_{\geq-1}
. By an analogous result to
[
PS21
, Proposition 3.19]
(which considers subalgebras of finite codimension in
W=\Der(\Bbbk[t,t^{-1}])
), there exists
q\in\Bbbk[t]
such that

W_{\geq-1}(qh)\subseteq\mathfrak{k}\subseteq W_{\geq-1}(h).

Note that
\varphi^{-1}(W_{\geq-1}(qh))=L(f,q(f)g)
. Hence,

L(f,q(f)g)\subseteq\mathfrak{g}.

It is immediate that
R(\mathfrak{g})=\Bbbk(f)
.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)
: Now suppose
R(\mathfrak{g})
is a field, so that
R(\mathfrak{g})=F(\mathfrak{g})
. By Theorem
4.3
there exists
f\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\{0\}
such that
R(\mathfrak{g})=\Bbbk(f)
and
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq L(f)
.

We must show that
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
L(f)
. We have
f\in R(\mathfrak{g})
, so there exist
w\partial,u\partial\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
f=\frac{w}{u}
. Therefore,
\deg(w)-\deg(u)=\deg(f)
. Since
w\partial,u\partial\in\mathfrak{g}\subseteq L(f)
, we also know that
\deg(f)
divides
\deg(w\partial)
and
\deg(u\partial)
, by Remark
3.14
. Hence,
\gcd(\deg(w\partial),\deg(u\partial))=\deg(f)
. But then it follows by Lemma
3.7
that
\mathfrak{g}
contains elements of degree
k\deg(f)
for all
k\gg 0
. Since all elements of
L(f)
have degree
k\deg(f)
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we conclude that
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
L(f)
, as required.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an infinite-dimensional Lie subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
and let
f\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
F(\mathfrak{g})=\Bbbk(f)
(which exists by Theorem
4.3
). What was shown in the final part of the proof of Theorem
4.4
is the following surprising statement: if there exist
w\partial,u\partial\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
\frac{w}{u}=f
then
R(\mathfrak{g})
is a field.

As an immediate consequence of Proposition
1.1
, Proposition
3.15
, and Theorem
4.4
, we get:

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an infinite-dimensional Lie subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
. If
R(\mathfrak{g})
is a field then
U(\mathfrak{g})
is not noetherian. ∎

If we do not assume that
R(\mathfrak{g})
is a field, we still know that
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq L(f)
for some
f\in\Bbbk[t]
, by Theorem
4.3
. What is not clear is whether
\mathfrak{g}
has finite codimension in
L(f)
, or whether
U(\mathfrak{g})
can be noetherian in this case.

The rest of the subsection is devoted to proving Theorem
4.3
. Before we can prove it, we require some technical results about rational functions.

Notation 4.7
.

Let
f\in\Bbbk(t)
and
g\in\Bbbk[t]
. We write
f\#g
if there exists
h\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
g=fh
.

The following result shows that the relation
f\#g
is nothing new, but it still gives a concise way of encoding the relationship between
f
and
g
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
f=\frac{a}{b}\in\Bbbk(t)
, where
a,b\in\Bbbk[t]
are coprime, and let
g\in\Bbbk[t]
. Then
f\#g
if and only if
a
divides
g
. ∎

In order to prove Theorem
4.3
, we will want to consider polynomials in
f
, where
f
is a rational function in
t
. The following proposition shows that if we have two coprime polynomials
p,q\in\Bbbk[t]
, then
p(f)
and
q(f)
are still coprime after clearing the denominators.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
f=\frac{a}{b}\in\Bbbk(t)
, where
a,b\in\Bbbk[t]
are coprime, and let
p,q\in\Bbbk[t]
be coprime. Let
n=\max\{\deg(p),\deg(q)\}
. Then

b^{n}p(f),b^{n}q(f)\in\Bbbk[t]

are coprime polynomials. In particular, let
m=\deg(p)
. Then
b^{m}p(f)
and
b^{m}
are coprime.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\deg(p)\geq\deg(q)
, so
n=\deg(p)\geq 1
. Let
\widehat{p}=b^{n}p(f)
and
\widehat{q}=b^{n}q(f)
. Letting
m=\deg(q)
, we have

p=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(t-\lambda_{i}),\quad q=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(t-\mu_{i}),

for some
\lambda_{i},\mu_{i}\in\overline{\Bbbk}
, and therefore

\widehat{p}=b^{n}p(f)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(a-\lambda_{i}b),\quad\widehat{q}=b^{n}q(f)=b^{n-m}\prod_{i=1}^{m}(a-\mu_{i}b)\in\Bbbk[t].

We must show that
\widehat{p}
and
\widehat{q}
are coprime.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that
\gamma\in\overline{\Bbbk}
is a root of both
\widehat{p}
and
\widehat{q}
.

Case 1:

\gamma
is a root of
b
.

Since
\gamma
is a root of
\widehat{p}
,

a(\gamma)-\lambda_{i}b(\gamma)=0

for some
i
. But since
b(\gamma)=0
, it follows that
a(\gamma)=0
, which contradicts
a
and
b
being coprime.

Case 2:

\gamma
is not a root of
b
.

As in Case 1, we must have

(4.1)

a(\gamma)-\lambda_{i}b(\gamma)=0

for some
i
. Furthermore, since
\gamma
is a root of
\widehat{q}
and is not a root of
b
,

(4.2)

a(\gamma)-\mu_{j}b(\gamma)=0

for some
j
. Subtracting (
4.1
) from (
4.2
),

(\lambda_{i}-\mu_{j})b(\gamma)=0.

But
b(\gamma)\neq 0
, so
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{j}
, which contradicts
p
and
q
being coprime.

Therefore,
\widehat{p}=b^{n}p(f)
and
\widehat{q}=b^{n}q(f)
are coprime polynomials, as required.
∎

The next result now follows immediately from Lemma
4.8
and Proposition
4.9
.

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
f=\frac{a}{b}\in\Bbbk(t)
, where
a,b\in\Bbbk[t]
are coprime, and let
p,g\in\Bbbk[t]
. Then
p(f)\#g
if and only if
b^{n}p(f)\in\Bbbk[t]
divides
g
, where
n=\deg(p)
.

Proof.

Letting
\widehat{p}=b^{n}p(f)\in\Bbbk[t]
, we can write

p(f)=\frac{\widehat{p}}{b^{n}}.

We know that
\widehat{p}
and
b^{n}
are coprime by Proposition
4.9
. By Lemma
4.8
,
p(f)\#g
if and only if
\widehat{p}
divides
g
.
∎

As an easy consequence of Proposition
4.9
and Corollary
4.10
, we get:

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
f\in\Bbbk(t)
, and let
p,q\in\Bbbk[t]
be coprime polynomials. Let
w,u\in\Bbbk[t]
such that

\frac{w}{u}=\frac{p(f)}{q(f)}.

Then
p(f)\#w
and
q(f)\#u
.

Proof.

Write
f=\frac{a}{b}
, where
a,b\in\Bbbk[t]
are coprime. Let
n=\max\{\deg(p),\deg(q)\}
, and let
\widehat{p}=b^{n}p(f),\widehat{q}=b^{n}q(f)\in\Bbbk[t]
. By Proposition
4.9
,
\widehat{p}
and
\widehat{q}
are coprime. Furthermore,
w\widehat{q}=u\widehat{p}
. Hence,
\widehat{p}
divides
w
and
\widehat{q}
divides
u
.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
n=\deg(p)
. Thus, by Corollary
4.10
,
p(f)\#w
. Therefore, there exists
g\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
w=p(f)g
, so that

up(f)=wq(f)=p(f)q(f)g.

Thus
u=q(f)g
, so
q(f)\#u
.
∎

For convenience, we introduce some new notation:

Notation 4.12
.

For
f\in\Bbbk(t)
, we write

\Bbbk[f]_{\#}=\{g\in\Bbbk[t]\mid p(f)\#g\text{ for some }p\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\Bbbk\}.

We now show that, provided
f\in\Bbbk(t)
has a pole at infinity, any polynomial
h\in\Bbbk[t]
can be written as
h=p(f)g
, where
p\in\Bbbk[t]
,
g\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\Bbbk[f]_{\#}
.

Proposition 4.13
.

Let
f\in\Bbbk(t)
such that
f
has a pole at infinity, and let
h\in\Bbbk[t]
. Then we can write
h=p(f)g
, where
p\in\Bbbk[t],g\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\Bbbk[f]_{\#}
.

Proof.

If
h\not\in\Bbbk[f]_{\#}
, then we are done, as we can take
p=1,g=h
.

If
h\in\Bbbk[f]_{\#}
, then let
p_{1}\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\Bbbk
such that
p_{1}(f)\#h
. We can write

h=p_{1}(f)h_{1},

where
h_{1}\in\Bbbk[t]
. Write
f=\frac{a}{b}
, where
a,b\in\Bbbk[t]
are coprime. By hypothesis,
\deg(a)>\deg(b)
. Let
n_{1}=\deg(p_{1})
and let
\widehat{p}_{1}=b^{n_{1}}p_{1}(f)\in\Bbbk[t]
. By Proposition
4.9
,
\widehat{p}_{1}
and
b^{n_{1}}
are coprime. Furthermore,

b^{n_{1}}h=\widehat{p}_{1}h_{1},

so
\widehat{p}_{1}
divides
h
. Write
h=\widehat{p}_{1}\widehat{h}_{1}
, where
\widehat{h}_{1}\in\Bbbk[t]
. We have

\displaystyle\deg(h)

\displaystyle=\deg(\widehat{p}_{1})+\deg(\widehat{h}_{1}),

\displaystyle\deg(\widehat{p}_{1})

\displaystyle=n_{1}\deg(a),

\displaystyle\deg(h_{1})

\displaystyle=n_{1}\deg(b)+\deg(\widehat{h}_{1}).

Combining the above,

\deg(h_{1})=\deg(h)-n_{1}(\deg(a)-\deg(b))<\deg(h),

since
\deg(a)>\deg(b)
.

Therefore, if
h\in\Bbbk[f]_{\#}
then we can write
h=p_{1}(f)h_{1}
, where
p_{1},h_{1}\in\Bbbk[t]
and
\deg(h_{1})<\deg(h)
. Inducting on
\deg(h)
, we can write
h_{1}=p_{2}(f)g
for
p_{2}\in\Bbbk[t],g\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\Bbbk[f]_{\#}
. Thus, we can take
p=p_{1}p_{2}
and we are done.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
4.3
.

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

We have

\Bbbk\subsetneqq K\subseteq\Bbbk(t),

so by Lüroth’s theorem there exists some
f\in\Bbbk(t)
such that
K=\Bbbk(f)
. Write
f=\frac{a}{b}\in\Bbbk(t)
, where
a,b\in\Bbbk[t]
are coprime. By applying a Möbius transformation to
f
if necessary, we may assume that
\deg(a)>\deg(b)
.

Let
w\partial\in\mathfrak{g}\setminus\{0\}
. Using Proposition
4.13
, write
w=p(f)g
, where
p\in\Bbbk[t],g\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\Bbbk[f]_{\#}
. We claim that every element of
\mathfrak{g}
can be written as
q(f)g\partial
for some
q\in\Bbbk[t]
.

Let
u\partial\in\mathfrak{g}\setminus\{0\}
. Then
\frac{w}{u}=\frac{p(f)g}{u}\in\Bbbk(f)
, so

\frac{g}{u}=\frac{w/u}{p(f)}\in\Bbbk(f).

Write

\frac{g}{u}=\frac{r(f)}{q(f)},

where
q,r\in\Bbbk[t]
are coprime. By Corollary
4.11
,
r(f)\#g
. But we know that
g\not\in\Bbbk[f]_{\#}
, so
r
must be constant. Without loss of generality, we can take
r=1
, so that

u=q(f)g\in\Bbbk[f]g.

Since
u\partial\in\mathfrak{g}
was arbitrary, we conclude that
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq\Bbbk[f]g\partial
.

We now show that
f
must be a polynomial. Since
\mathfrak{g}
is infinite-dimensional, there exists
u\partial\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
u=q(f)g
, where
q\in\Bbbk[t]
is such that
n=\deg(q)>\deg(g)
. By Proposition
4.9
, the polynomials
\widehat{q}=b^{n}q(f)
and
b^{n}
are coprime. We have

b^{n}u=\widehat{q}g,

and therefore
b^{n}
divides
g
. But
n>\deg(g)
, so
b
must be constant, and thus
f=\frac{a}{b}\in\Bbbk[t]
, as claimed. Hence,
K=\Bbbk(f)
, where
f\in\Bbbk[t]
, and
\Bbbk[f]g\partial=L(f,g)
.

We now have
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq L(f,g)
. To conclude the proof, we must show that
f^{\prime}g\in\Bbbk[f]
, so that
L(f,g)
is a Lie algebra by Lemma
3.12
, and
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq L(f)
by Proposition
3.13
. Let
p(f)g\partial,q(f)g\partial\in\mathfrak{g}\setminus\{0\}
with
p\neq q
. Then

[p(f)g\partial,q(f)g\partial]=r(f)f^{\prime}g^{2}\partial\in\mathfrak{g}\setminus\{0\},

where
r=pq^{\prime}-p^{\prime}q\in\Bbbk[t]
. Since
F(\mathfrak{g})=\Bbbk(f)
, it follows that
\frac{r(f)f^{\prime}g^{2}}{p(f)g}\in\Bbbk(f)
, so
f^{\prime}g\in\Bbbk(f)
.
Therefore,

f^{\prime}g\in\Bbbk(f)\cap\Bbbk[t]=\Bbbk[f],

as required.
∎

5.
A sufficient condition for the non-noetherianity of the enveloping algebra of a subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}

In this section, we adapt the methods of
[
SŠ17
]
to give an alternative proof that
U(L(f,g))
is not noetherian. The advantage of this method is that it is more likely to generalise to give a proof of Conjecture
3.1
. In particular, we give a sufficient condition, which we call condition
(*)
, for a subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq W_{\geq-1}
to have a non-noetherian enveloping algebra.

Notation 5.1
.

Let
\sigma\in\Aut(\Bbbk[x,y])
such that
f^{\sigma}(x,y)=f(x+1,y)
. Define
T=\Bbbk[x,y][t^{\pm 1};\sigma]
, i.e. as a vector space

T=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Bbbk[x,y]t^{n},

with
tf(x,y)=f^{\sigma}(x,y)t=f(x+1,y)t
. The algebra
T
is graded with
\deg(t)=1
and
\deg(x)=\deg(y)=0
. For non-homogeneous elements
u\in T
, we write
\deg(u)
for the highest power of
t
which appears in the expansion of
u
.

Remark 5.2
.

The algebra
T
is isomorphic to
A_{1}(\Bbbk(y))[t^{-1}]
, where
A_{1}(\Bbbk(y))=\Bbbk(y)[t,\partial]
is the first Weyl algebra over
\Bbbk(y)
, with commutation relation

\partial t-t\partial=1.

Under this isomorphism, the element
x\in T
corresponds to
-t\partial\in A_{1}(\Bbbk(y))[t^{-1}]
.

We define a homomorphism from
W_{\geq-1}
to
T
, first introduced by Sierra and Špenko. Although they used much more general machinery to construct this map, it is straightforward to directly check that it is a homomorphism. This will allow us to work over the more accessible algebra
T
by proving that the image of
U(L(f,g))
in
T
is not noetherian.

Lemma 5.3
.

The linear map

\displaystyle\varphi\colon W_{\geq-1}

\displaystyle\to T

\displaystyle e_{n}

\displaystyle\mapsto-(x+ny)t^{n}

induces a graded
\Bbbk
-algebra homomorphism
\Phi\colon U(W_{\geq-1})\rightarrow T
. This homomorphism can also be written as

\Phi(f\partial)=-(xft^{-1}+y(f^{\prime}-ft^{-1}))

for
f\in\Bbbk[t]
.

Proof.

In order to prove that
\Phi
is a
\Bbbk
-algebra homomorphism, it is enough to check that
\varphi
is a Lie algebra homomorphism, i.e. that
\varphi([e_{n},e_{m}])=\varphi(e_{n})\varphi(e_{m})-\varphi(e_{m})\varphi(e_{n})
. We leave this computation to the reader.

It suffices to check the final sentence for
f=t^{n+1}
, so that
f\partial=e_{n}
. Note that

f^{\prime}-ft^{-1}=(n+1)t^{n}-t^{n}=nt^{n},

so the formula holds.
∎

Equipped with the homomorphism
\Phi
, we can now define condition
(*)
.

Definition 5.4
.

We say that a Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq W_{\geq-1}

satisfies condition
(*)
if there exists some
q\in U(\mathfrak{g})
with
\Phi(q)\in\Bbbk[y,t,t^{-1}]\setminus\Bbbk[y]
.

Remark 5.5
.

The requirement that
\Phi(q)\not\in\Bbbk[y]
guarantees that
\Phi(q)
is not central in
T
.

The definition of condition
(*)
is motivated by Sierra and Špenko’s proof that the enveloping algebra of the Witt algebra is not noetherian (see
[
SŠ17
, Section 4]
). In their paper, they define the elements

p_{n}=\frac{1}{2}(e_{n}e_{3n}+e_{3n}e_{n})-e_{2n}^{2}\in U(W),

and then show that

\Phi(p_{n})=n^{2}y(1-y)t^{4n}\in\Bbbk[y,t,t^{-1}]\setminus\Bbbk[y]

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. They use this to show that the two-sided ideal
\Phi(U(W))\Phi(p_{n})\Phi(U(W))
is not finitely generated as a left or right ideal of
\Phi(U(W))
.

Our goal is to generalise Sierra and Špenko’s proof by showing that if a subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq W_{\geq-1}
satisfies
(*)
, then
\Phi(U(\mathfrak{g}))
is not noetherian. We will do this by showing that the two-sided ideal
\Phi(U(\mathfrak{g}))\Phi(q)\Phi(U(\mathfrak{g}))
is not finitely generated as a left ideal of
\Phi(U(\mathfrak{g}))
.

For the rest of this section, we let
\mathfrak{g}
be an infinite-dimensional subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
which satisfies
(*)
. Although for our purposes it suffices to consider
\Phi(q)\in y(1-y)\Bbbk[t]
, it is not much more difficult to work in the more general setting where
\Phi(q)\in\Bbbk[y,t,t^{-1}]\setminus\Bbbk[y]
.

In fact, we claim that without loss of generality, we can choose
q\in U(\mathfrak{g})
so that
\Phi(q)\in t\Bbbk[y,t]\setminus\{0\}
. Having
\Phi(q)
in this form will significantly simplify the computations below. To prove that
q\in U(\mathfrak{g})
can be chosen in this way, we write

\Phi(q)=\sum_{i=-m}^{n}f_{i}(y)t^{i},

where
f_{i}(y)\in\Bbbk[y]
, and let
a\in\mathfrak{g}
such that

a=\sum_{i=m+1}^{d}\alpha_{i}e_{i},

where
\alpha_{i}\in\Bbbk
. In other words,
a
is some element of
\mathfrak{g}
with no terms of degree less than
m+1
. Letting
q^{\prime}=aq-qa\in U(\mathfrak{g})
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi(q^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=m+1}^{d}\sum_{j=-m}^{n}\alpha_{i}f_{j}(y)\Phi(e_{i})t^{j}-\sum_{i=m+1}^{d}\sum_{j=-m}^{n}\alpha_{i}f_{j}(y)t^{j}\Phi(e_{i})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=m+1}^{d}\sum_{j=-m}^{n}\alpha_{i}f_{j}(y)(x+iy)t^{i+j}+\sum_{i=m+1}^{d}\sum_{j=-m}^{n}\alpha_{i}f_{j}(y)t^{j}(x+iy)t^{i}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=m+1}^{d}\sum_{j=-m}^{n}\alpha_{i}f_{j}(y)(x+iy)t^{i+j}+\sum_{i=m+1}^{d}\sum_{j=-m}^{n}\alpha_{i}f_{j}(y)(x+iy+j)t^{i+j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=m+1}^{d}\sum_{j=-m}^{n}j\alpha_{i}f_{j}(y)t^{i+j}\in t\Bbbk[y,t].

We see that
\Phi(q^{\prime})\in t\Bbbk[y,t]\setminus\{0\}
. Therefore, we assume that
\Phi(q)
is of this form.

Notation 5.6
.

Write

\Phi(q)=f(y)\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(y)t^{i},

where
f(y),g_{i}(y)\in\Bbbk[y]
are such that the
g_{i}(y)
share no common factors. Let
1\leq k\leq n
be maximal such that
g_{k}(y)
is not a multiple of
y
.

The next lemma shows that we can generate elements similar to
\Phi(q)
of arbitrarily high degree by simply commuting
q
with elements of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
a\in\mathfrak{g}
and let
m=\deg(a)
. Then
\Phi(aq-qa)\in\Bbbk[y,t]
and
\deg(\Phi(aq-qa))=n+m
. Furthermore, writing

\Phi(aq-qa)=f(y)\sum_{i=0}^{n+m}h_{i}(y)t^{i},

we have that
y
does not divide
h_{m+k}(y)
.

Proof.

Write
a=\sum_{i=-1}^{m}\alpha_{i}e_{i}
, where
\alpha_{i}\in\Bbbk
and
\alpha_{m}\neq 0
. Similarly to the above computation, we have

\Phi(aq-qa)=f(y)\sum_{i=-1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}j\alpha_{i}g_{j}(y)t^{i+j}\in\Bbbk[y,t].

We see that
\deg(\Phi(aq-qa))=n+m
and that

h_{\ell}(y)=\sum_{i+j=\ell}j\alpha_{i}g_{j}(y),

for all
\ell
. Letting
N=\min(n,m+k+1)
, we have

h_{m+k}(y)=k\alpha_{m}g_{k}(y)+(k+1)\alpha_{m-1}g_{k+1}(y)+\ldots+N\alpha_{m+k-N}g_{N}(y).

By maximality of
k
, it follows that
y
divides
g_{k+1}(y),\ldots,g_{N}(y)
, but does not divide
g_{k}(y)
. Hence,
y
does not divide
h_{m+k}(y)
, since
k\alpha_{m}\neq 0
.
∎

We now show that
B=\Phi(U(\mathfrak{g}))
is not left noetherian, which suffices to prove that
U(\mathfrak{g})
is not noetherian.

Proposition 5.8
.

The associative algebra
B
is not left noetherian.

Proof.

We will show that
I=B\Phi(q)B
is not finitely generated as a left ideal of
B
. Note that
I\subseteq f(y)T
.

Assume, for a contradiction, that
I
is finitely generated as a left ideal of
B
. Let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell}\in I
be generators of
{}_{B}I
. Since
\mathfrak{g}
is infinite-dimensional, there is some
a=e_{m}+\ldots\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
m+k>\deg(c_{i})
for all
1\leq i\leq\ell
. Write

s=\Phi(aq-qa)=f(y)\sum_{i=0}^{n+m}h_{i}(y)t^{i}\in I=Bc_{1}+\ldots+Bc_{\ell}

as in Lemma
5.7
. Then there exist
b_{1},\ldots,b_{\ell}\in B
such that

s=b_{1}c_{1}+\ldots+b_{\ell}c_{\ell}.

Since
m+k>\deg(c_{i})
for all
i
, it follows that if
b_{j}c_{j}
has a nonzero contribution to the degree
m+k
term of
b_{1}c_{1}+\ldots+b_{\ell}c_{\ell}
, then we must have
\deg(b_{j})\geq 1
. Write
c_{i}=f(y)\sum_{j}F_{ij}(x,y)t^{j}
, where
F_{ij}(x,y)\in\Bbbk[x,y]
. Note that

\displaystyle\Phi(e_{\ell})c_{i}

\displaystyle=-(x+\ell y)t^{\ell}f(y)\sum_{j}F_{ij}(x,y)t^{j}

\displaystyle=-f(y)\sum_{j}(x+\ell y)F_{ij}(x+\ell,y)t^{j+\ell}\in f(y)(x,y)T,

where
(x,y)
is the ideal of
\Bbbk[x,y]
generated by
x
and
y
. We therefore see that the degree
m+k
term of
b_{1}c_{1}+\ldots+b_{\ell}c_{\ell}
is contained in
f(y)(x,y)t^{m+k}
. However, Lemma
5.7
says that
h_{m+k}(y)\not\in(x,y)
, a contradiction. Therefore,
B
is not left noetherian.
∎

The following result now follows immediately.

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an infinite-dimensional subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
which satisfies
(*)
. Then
U(\mathfrak{g})
is not noetherian. ∎

As stated in Definition
5.4
, condition
(*)
does not seem like an easy condition to check. The following proposition provides a method to construct elements
q\in U(W_{\geq-1})
such that
\Phi(q)\in\Bbbk[y,t,t^{-1}]
, providing a simple way to check if a subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
satisfies
(*)
.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2},g_{1},g_{2}\in\Bbbk[t]
such that
f_{1}f_{2}=g_{1}g_{2}
, and let
a_{i}=f_{i}\partial
,
b_{i}=g_{i}\partial\in W_{\geq-1}
. Let

q=(a_{1}a_{2}+a_{2}a_{1})-(b_{1}b_{2}+b_{2}b_{1})\in U(W_{\geq-1}).

Then
\Phi(q)\in y(1-y)\Bbbk[t]
. Furthermore, if
\{\deg(f_{1}),\deg(f_{2})\}\neq\{\deg(g_{1}),\deg(g_{2})\}
, then
\Phi(q)\neq 0
and
\deg(\Phi(q))=\deg(f_{1})+\deg(f_{2})-2
.

Proof.

Using the formula
\Phi(f\partial)=-(xft^{-1}+y(f^{\prime}-ft^{-1}))
from Lemma
5.3
and the commutation relation
fx=xf+f^{\prime}t
, we get

\displaystyle\Phi(a_{1}a_{2}+a_{2}a_{1})=2

\displaystyle x^{2}f_{1}f_{2}t^{-2}+x(f_{1}^{\prime}f_{2}t^{-1}-2f_{1}f_{2}t^{-2}+f_{1}f_{2}^{\prime}t^{-1})

\displaystyle+2xy(f_{1}^{\prime}f_{2}t^{-1}-2f_{1}f_{2}t^{-2}+f_{1}f_{2}^{\prime}t^{-1})

\displaystyle+y(f_{1}^{\prime\prime}f_{2}-f_{1}^{\prime}f_{2}t^{-1}+2f_{1}f_{2}t^{-2}-f_{1}f_{2}^{\prime}t^{-1}+f_{1}f_{2}^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle+2y^{2}(f_{1}^{\prime}-f_{1}t^{-1})(f_{2}^{\prime}-f_{2}t^{-1}),

and similarly for
\Phi(b_{1}b_{2}+b_{2}b_{1})
with
g_{i}
replacing
f_{i}
. Since
f_{1}f_{2}=g_{1}g_{2}
, we have
(f_{1}f_{2})^{\prime}=(g_{1}g_{2})^{\prime}
and
(f_{1}f_{2})^{\prime\prime}=(g_{1}g_{2})^{\prime\prime}
, and therefore

(5.1)

\displaystyle f_{1}^{\prime}f_{2}+f_{1}f_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=g_{1}^{\prime}g_{2}+g_{1}g_{2}^{\prime}

(5.2)

\displaystyle f_{1}^{\prime\prime}f_{2}+f_{1}f_{2}^{\prime\prime}+2f_{1}^{\prime}f_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=g_{1}^{\prime\prime}g_{2}+g_{1}g_{2}^{\prime\prime}+2g_{1}^{\prime}g_{2}^{\prime}

Hence, subtracting
\Phi(b_{1}b_{2}+b_{2}b_{1})
from
\Phi(a_{1}a_{2}+a_{2}a_{1})
and using (
5.1
), we get

\Phi(q)=y(f_{1}^{\prime\prime}f_{2}+f_{1}f_{2}^{\prime\prime}-g_{1}^{\prime\prime}g_{2}-g_{1}g_{2}^{\prime\prime})+2y^{2}((f_{1}^{\prime}-f_{1}t^{-1})(f_{2}^{\prime}-f_{2}t^{-1})-(g_{1}^{\prime}-g_{1}t^{-1})(g_{2}^{\prime}-g_{2}t^{-1})).

Furthermore,

\displaystyle(f_{1}^{\prime}-f_{1}t^{-1})(f_{2}^{\prime}-f_{2}t^{-1})

\displaystyle=f_{1}^{\prime}f_{2}^{\prime}-(f_{1}^{\prime}f_{2}+f_{1}f_{2}^{\prime})t^{-1}+f_{1}f_{2}t^{-2},

\displaystyle(g_{1}^{\prime}-g_{1}t^{-1})(g_{2}^{\prime}-g_{2}t^{-1})

\displaystyle=g_{1}^{\prime}g_{2}^{\prime}-(g_{1}^{\prime}g_{2}+g_{1}g_{2}^{\prime})t^{-1}+g_{1}g_{2}t^{-2},

and thus, using (
5.1
), we see that

(f_{1}^{\prime}-f_{1}t^{-1})(f_{2}^{\prime}-f_{2}t^{-1})-(g_{1}^{\prime}-g_{1}t^{-1})(g_{2}^{\prime}-g_{2}t^{-1})=f_{1}^{\prime}f_{2}^{\prime}-g_{1}^{\prime}g_{2}^{\prime}.

By (
5.2
), we also have

f_{1}^{\prime\prime}f_{2}+f_{1}f_{2}^{\prime\prime}-g_{1}^{\prime\prime}g_{2}-g_{1}g_{2}^{\prime\prime}=-2(f_{1}^{\prime}f_{2}^{\prime}-g_{1}^{\prime}g_{2}^{\prime}).

Combining all the above, we conclude

\Phi(q)=y(1-y)(f_{1}^{\prime\prime}f_{2}+f_{1}f_{2}^{\prime\prime}-g_{1}^{\prime\prime}g_{2}-g_{1}g_{2}^{\prime\prime})=-2y(1-y)(f_{1}^{\prime}f_{2}^{\prime}-g_{1}^{\prime}g_{2}^{\prime})\in y(1-y)\Bbbk[t].

For the final sentence, we let
n_{i}=\deg(f_{i}),m_{i}=\deg(g_{i})
, and assume that
\{n_{1},n_{2}\}\neq\{m_{1},m_{2}\}
. Let
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in\Bbbk^{*}
be the leading coefficients of
f_{i},g_{i}
, respectively. Let
\gamma=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}=\beta_{1}\beta_{2}
and
N=n_{1}+n_{2}=m_{1}+m_{2}
. The leading term of
f_{1}^{\prime}f_{2}^{\prime}-g_{1}^{\prime}g_{2}^{\prime}
is therefore

(n_{1}n_{2}-m_{1}m_{2})\gamma t^{N-2}\neq 0,

since
\{n_{1},n_{2}\}\neq\{m_{1},m_{2}\}
. Hence,
\Phi(q)\neq 0
and
\deg(\Phi(q))=N-2
.
∎

In light of Proposition
5.10
, we make the following definition:

Definition 5.11
.

We say that a subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq W_{\geq-1}

satisfies condition
(\dagger)
if there exist
f_{1},f_{2},g_{1},g_{2}\in\Bbbk[t]
with
f_{i}\partial,g_{i}\partial\in\mathfrak{g}
such that

\displaystyle f_{1}f_{2}

\displaystyle=g_{1}g_{2},

\displaystyle\{\deg(f_{1}),\deg(f_{2})\}

\displaystyle\neq\{\deg(g_{1}),\deg(g_{2})\}.

By Proposition
5.10
, if
\mathfrak{g}
satisfies
(\dagger)
then
\mathfrak{g}
satisfies
(*)
, and therefore
U(\mathfrak{g})
is not noetherian in this case. In particular, it is clear that
L(f,g)
satisfies condition
(\dagger)
for any
f,g\in\Bbbk[t]\setminus\{0\}
such that
f^{\prime}g\in\Bbbk[f]
and
\deg(f)\geq 1
, providing an alternative proof of Proposition
3.15
.

In fact, every known infinite-dimensional subalgebra of
W_{\geq-1}
satisfies
(\dagger)
(and therefore also satisfies
(*)
), but we have not been able to show that this will always be the case. This is the subject of ongoing research.
