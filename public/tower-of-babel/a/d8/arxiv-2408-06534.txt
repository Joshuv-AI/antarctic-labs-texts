Linearization of Monge–Ampère Equations and Statistical Applications
Alberto González-Sanz, Shunan Sheng

Abstract
Optimal transport has found numerous applications across data science, many of which require differentiating the optimal transport map with respect to the underlying probability densities in the Fréchet sense. In this work, we show that when the reference measure Q Q is sufficiently regular in space and the curve of target measures { P t } t ∈ I \{P_{t}\}_{t\in I} is both spatially regular and 𝒞 1 \mathcal{C}^{1} in time, then the associated curve of optimal transport maps { ∇ ϕ t } t ∈ I \{\nabla\phi_{t}\}_{t\in I} pushing Q Q toward P t P_{t} is itself a 𝒞 1 \mathcal{C}^{1} curve. Moreover, we identify its time derivative as the solution to the linearized Monge–Ampère equation , a second-order elliptic PDE with strictly oblique boundary conditions and a vanishing zero-order term. Our proof relies on applying the implicit function theorem to the Monge–Ampère equation with natural boundary conditions. As consequences, we establish regularity of the transport-based quantile regressor with respect to the covariates and derive a central limit theorem for smooth optimal transport maps.

More recent results in

Their analysis relies crucially on the periodic boundary conditions of the Monge–Ampère equation on the torus, and therefore does not extend to the setting of interest here, particularly when the supports

A related but distinct line of work concerns stability of optimal transport maps, with extensive literature on both qualitative and quantitative aspects.

[

Quantitative stability estimates can be found in

However, stability results establish only

In contrast, identifying the time derivative of the transport map is central to many of the aforementioned applications.

Overall, there are few results concerning the time regularity of optimal transport maps when the target measures
\{P_{t}\}_{t\in I}
have time-varying supports
\{\Omega_{t}\}_{t\in I}

Results and Methodology

In this work, we show that if the reference measure
Q
is sufficiently regular in space, and the curve of probability measures
\{P_{t}\}_{t\in I}
is sufficiently regular in space and evolves smoothly in time (in the sense of
Definition

3.1
), then the corresponding curve of optimal transport maps
\{\nabla\phi_{t}\}_{t\in I}
also evolves smoothly in time. Moreover, its time derivative solves the
linearized Monge–Ampère equation
, which is a second-order elliptic partial differential equation with strictly oblique boundary conditions and a null zero-order term (see
Theorem

3.2
).

Our approach follows the strategy of linearizing the Monge–Ampère equation (see
[
24
,
56
]
) and applying the implicit function theorem. To handle the time-varying supports, we make essential use of the tool introduced in
[
54
]
, which characterizes the evolution of the supports of the target measures via
convex defining functions
.

The proof of our main result relies on analyzing the following functional derived from the Monge–Ampère equation:

\Gamma(t,\phi)=\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma_{t}^{(1)}(\phi)\\[4.0pt]
\Gamma_{t}^{(2)}(\phi)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\log(\det(D^{2}\phi))-\log(q)+\log(p_{t}(\nabla\phi))\\[4.0pt]
h_{t}(\nabla\phi)\end{pmatrix},

where
h_{t}
is a uniformly convex defining function for
{\rm supp}(P_{t})
.
Analyzing the invertibility of its Fréchet derivative leads to the study of the following second-order elliptic PDE:

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\!\big([D^{2}\phi_{t}]^{-1}D^{2}\xi\big)+\frac{\langle\nabla p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}),\nabla\xi\rangle}{p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})}

\displaystyle=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla h_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}),\nabla\xi\rangle

\displaystyle=g\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.

In
Theorem

5.6
, we show that this PDE is solvable between the spaces

\mathcal{X}_{t}=\left\{\xi\in\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}):\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}p_{t}\,\xi(\nabla\phi_{t}^{*})=0\right\}

and

\mathcal{Y}_{t}=\left\{(f,g)\in\mathcal{C}^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\times\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega):\int_{\Omega}qf=\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}p_{t}\,g(\nabla\phi_{t}^{*})\right\}.

The proof then proceeds by applying the implicit function theorem to a re-centered version of
\Gamma
, since the range of
\Gamma
generally exceeds the space
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
.

Building on our theoretical results, we present two statistical applications.
The first concerns the transport-based regression problem (
Section

4.1
), where the conditional distribution
P_{Y\mid X=x}
plays the role of the time-varying target measure. We show that the associated transport-based quantile regressor varies smoothly with respect to the covariate
x
.
Our second application (
Section

4.2
) establishes a central limit theorem for the smoothed optimal transport map, whose limiting distribution is again characterized through the linearized Monge–Ampère equation.

3
Main Result

In this section, we state the main result and introduce its assumptions. We consider a curve
\{P_{t}\}_{t\in I}
of probability measures with both smooth (and uniformly convex) supports and densities.

As mentioned in the introduction, our main result accommodates time-dependent supports. Due to this dependency, the curve
\{p_{t}\}_{t\in I}
is not well-defined in
\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}(\Omega^{\prime})
as in the case
P_{t}={\rm Unif}[0,1+t]
for
t\in(0,1)
. To address this issue, we will start assume that
\{p_{t}\}_{t\in I}
has a
\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}
extension. Later, in
Lemma

3.4
, we will provide sufficient conditions for this extension.

To begin with, we define our notion of a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
curve of probability measures in
\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega^{\prime}})
for a given set
\Omega^{\prime}
. The definition below captures two aspects of the curve of probability measures: changes in support and changes in the density functions.

Definition 3.1
.

A curve
\{P_{t}\}_{t\in I}
of probability measures over a domain
\Omega^{\prime}
is said to be
\mathcal{C}^{1}
in
\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega^{\prime}})
if the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

There exists
\kappa\in(0,+\infty)
and a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
curve of convex functions
\{h_{t}\}_{t\in I}
in
\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega^{\prime}})
such that

\Omega_{t}:={\rm int}(\mathrm{supp}(P_{t}))=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:h_{t}(y)<0\right\},

with
\|\nabla h_{t}\|=1
on
\partial\Omega_{t}
,

\bigcup_{t\in I}\Omega_{t}+\frac{1}{\kappa}\mathbb{B}:=\left\{y:{\rm dist}\left(y,\bigcup_{t\in I}\Omega_{t}\right)<\frac{1}{\kappa}\right\}\subset\Omega^{\prime}
,
and

\frac{1}{\kappa}{\rm I}_{d}\leq D^{2}h_{t}\leq\kappa{\rm I}_{d}\quad\text{in }\Omega^{\prime}.

(2)

There exists a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
curve
\{\log(p_{t})\}_{t\in I}
in
\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega^{\prime}})
such that
P_{t}=p_{t}{\bf 1}_{\Omega_{t}}
.

The function

is commonly known as the

of

. It is noteworthy that

represents an open, bounded, and uniformly

, one can construct the function

to be smooth and uniformly convex, approximating

. For example, if
\Omega_{t}=\mathbb{B}(0,1)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|=1\}

(see, e.g.,
[
24
, pp 40]
).

Theorem 3.2
.

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial_{t}p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})}{p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})}\quad\rm in\,\,\Omega,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla h_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}),\nabla\xi\rangle

(ii)

Lemma 3.4
.

Assume that

Then the curve

Proof.

We want to extend
\log p
from
{\rm int}(\mathcal{D}):={\rm int}\left(\{(t,x):\,t\in I,\ x\in\Omega_{t}\}\right)
to
I\times\Omega^{\prime}
using

is open and connected, we may assume without loss of generality that

. Then, for every
(t_{0},x_{0})\in\partial\mathcal{D}

and a
\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}

\Psi:B(t_{0},x_{0})\to D:=\Psi(B(t_{0},x_{0}))\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
such that
\Psi(B(t_{0},x_{0})\cap\mathcal{D})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}_{+}
,
\Psi(B(t_{0},x_{0})\cap\partial\mathcal{D})\subset\partial\mathbb{R}^{d+1}_{+}
, and
\Psi\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}(B(t_{0},x_{0})),\Psi^{-1}\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}(D)

\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\ni(t,x_{1},\dots,x_{d-1},x_{d})\mapsto(t,x_{1},\dots,x_{d-1},-h_{t}(x))\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}.

Since

is a convex defining function of
\Omega_{t}

\Psi
is
\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}
. We now show that
\Psi
is one-to-one in a small neighbourhood of
(t_{0},x_{0})

\{x\in\Omega:h_{t}(x)<0\}
is open and

is uniformly convex, one may choose
\delta

\|\nabla h_{t}(x)\|\neq 0
for all
(t,x)\in B:=\mathbb{B}((t_{0},x_{0}),\delta)

(t,x)=(t,x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\neq(s,y)=(s,y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\in B

\Psi(t,x)=\Psi(s,y)

\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}

Therefore, the fundamental theorem of calculus yields that

0=\tilde{h}(y_{d})-\tilde{h}(x_{d})=(y_{d}-x_{d})\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{h}^{\prime}(x_{d}+t(y_{d}-x_{d}))\,dt\neq 0,

which leads to a contradiction.
∎

\displaystyle{\rm tr}([D^{2}\phi_{t}]^{-1}D^{2}\xi)+\frac{\langle\nabla p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}),\nabla\xi\rangle}{p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial_{t}p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})}{p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})}\quad\rm in\,\,\Omega\,,

\displaystyle\langle\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\nabla\phi_{t}),\nabla\xi\rangle

\displaystyle=0\quad\rm on\,\,\partial\Omega\,,

where
\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}
denotes the unit outer normal vector-field to
\partial\Omega^{\prime}
.

Corollary

3.5
follows immediately from
Theorem

3.2
once we show that the family
\{\log(p_{t})\}_{t\in I}\subset\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}(\overline{\Omega^{\prime}})
can be extended to a larger set
\Omega^{\prime}_{\kappa}:=\Omega^{\prime}+\frac{1}{\kappa}\mathbb{B}
for some
\kappa
depending on the uniform convexity of
\Omega^{\prime}
(see

3.6
below). This extension is crucial for computing the Fréchet derivative of the target functional (see
Lemma

5.5
). In contrast with
Lemma

3.4
, the fact that the support does not depend on
t
eliminates the main challenge to our analysis. As a consequence, we no longer need to impose any extra regularity on the curve
t\mapsto\log(p_{t})
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Under the assumptions of
Corollary

3.5
, the curve
\{P_{t}\}_{t\in I}
, with
P_{t}=p_{t}{\bf 1}_{\Omega^{\prime}}
, is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
in
\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}(\overline{\Omega^{\prime}})
(in the sense of
Definition

3.1
).

Proof.

Fix
t\in I
. Since
\Omega^{\prime}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2,\gamma}
, for each
x\in\partial\Omega^{\prime}
, there exists a ball
B(x)=\mathbb{B}(x,\epsilon_{x})
and a
\mathcal{C}^{2,\gamma}
diffeomorphism
\Psi=\Psi_{x}:B(x)\to D_{x}:=\Psi_{x}(B(x))\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
that straightens the boundary (see (
2
)). Since
\partial\Omega^{\prime}\subset\bigcup_{x\in\partial\Omega^{\prime}}\mathbb{B}\left(x,\frac{\epsilon_{x}}{2}\right)
and
\partial\Omega^{\prime}
is compact, there exists a finite covering
\partial\Omega^{\prime}\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{B}\left(x_{i},\frac{\epsilon_{x_{i}}}{2}\right)
, with
x_{i}\in\partial\Omega^{\prime}
. Let
\kappa>0
be such that
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\Omega_{\kappa}^{\prime}\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{B}\left(x_{i},\frac{\epsilon_{x_{i}}}{2}\right)\cup\Omega^{\prime}
.

Denote by
u_{t}:=\log p_{t}
for ease of notation. Set
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and

D_{+}^{(i)}:=\Psi(\Omega^{\prime}\cap B(x_{i}))=D_{x_{i}}\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}.

Setting
\tilde{u}_{t}^{(i)}(y):=u\circ\Psi^{-1}_{x_{i}}(y)
for every

, the extension of
\tilde{u}_{t}
to
D_{x_{i}}
is given by

\tilde{w}_{t}^{(i)}(y^{\prime},y_{d}):=-3\tilde{u}_{t}^{(i)}(y^{\prime},-y_{d})+4\tilde{u}_{t}^{(i)}(y^{\prime},-y_{d}/2),\quad y_{d}<0,

so that

(see
[

) and

w_{t}^{(i)}:=\begin{cases}\tilde{w}_{t}^{(i)}\circ\Psi_{x_{i}}&{\rm if}\ x\notin\Omega^{\prime}\\
u_{t}&{\rm if}\ x\in\Omega^{\prime}\\
\end{cases}

is a
\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}

in

\{\eta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a locally finite partition of unity subordinate to the covering
\{B(x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Then we have

w_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}w_{t}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}(\overline{\Omega_{\kappa}^{\prime}})

and

in
\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}

for all

. Since

is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
in

(below) shows that for each

4.2
Central Limit Theorem for Smooth Optimal Transport Map

The smooth optimal transport problem is introduced as a way to alleviate the curse of dimensionality in estimating the Wasserstein distance
[
29
,
30
,
28
,
48
,
31
]
.
Let
\Omega
be a
\mathcal{C}^{2,1}
uniformly convex domain.
Let
K:\overline{\Omega}\times\overline{\Omega}\to(0,\infty)
be a
\mathcal{C}^{2,1}
kernel satisfying

\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\,{\rm d}y=1
and
\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\,{\rm d}x=1
for all
(x,y)\in\Omega\times\Omega
.
Let the reference measure
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
satisfy
\log(q)\in\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
for some
0<\alpha<1
.
The smooth (or convoluted) optimal transport problem between
P\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
and
Q
is defined as

\min_{\pi\in\Pi(P_{K},Q)}\int\|x-y\|^{2}\,d\pi(x,y),

(4)

where
P_{K}
admits the smoothed density
\int K(\cdot,x)\,dP(x)
.

Suppose we observe i.i.d. samples
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
from
P
and define the empirical measure

\widehat{P}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
.
Let
T=\nabla\phi
be the optimal transport map from
Q
to
P_{K}
, and let
\widehat{T}_{n}
denote the optimal transport map from
Q
to
\widehat{P}_{n,K}
.
In this section, we establish a central limit theorem for the smooth optimal transport maps
\widehat{T}_{n}
(see
Proposition

4.4
).

As expected, the result follows from the functional delta method.
To apply it, one needs the Hadamard differentiability of the mapping
\Phi
that sends a density
p
to the optimal transport map
\nabla\phi_{q\to p}
pushing
q
forward to
p
.
This differentiability follows from our general result
Corollary

3.5
, together with the lemma below.
We remark that this lemma provides a general principle showing that smoothness along every
\mathcal{C}^{1}
curve implies Hadamard differentiability, which may be of independent interest to the reader.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}})
,
(\mathcal{Y},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Y}})
be normed spaces. Assume that
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{X}
is open. Let
\Phi:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a functional. Suppose that for any
\mathcal{C}^{1}
curve
\eta:[0,1]\mapsto\mathcal{K}
the function
\Phi\circ\eta:[0,1]\to\mathcal{Y}
is also
\mathcal{C}^{1}
. Then
\Phi
is Hadamard differentiable at any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{K}
. That is, for any
t_{k}\to 0^{+}
and
h_{k}\to h\in\mathcal{X}
with
\{h_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}\subset\mathcal{X}
, it follows that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\Phi(x_{0}+t_{k}h_{k})-\Phi(x_{0})}{t_{k}}=D\Phi(x_{0})(h)=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\Phi(x_{0}+ht).

Proof.

For any
h\in\mathcal{X}
, the curve
x_{t}=x_{0}+th
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, for
t
small enough, then there exists a mapping
D\Phi(x_{0}):\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
such that

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\left\|\frac{\Phi(x_{t})-\Phi(x_{0})}{t}-D\Phi(x_{0})(h)\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}}=0\,.

That is,
\Phi
is Gateaux differentiable at
x_{0}
. Note that
\Phi
is positive homogeneous as for
\lambda>0
,

D\Phi(x)(\lambda h)=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Phi(x_{0}+t\lambda h)-\Phi(x_{0})}{t}=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Phi(x_{0}+sh)-\Phi(x_{0})}{s/\lambda}=\lambda D\Phi(x)(h)\,.

We proceed by contradiction. Suppose that

\lim_{k}\left\|\frac{\Phi(x_{0}+t_{k}h_{k})-\Phi(x_{0})}{t_{k}}-D\Phi(x_{0})(h)\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}}=\alpha\in(0,\infty]\,.

for some sequence
t_{k}\to 0^{+}
and
h_{k}\to h\in\mathcal{X}
with the convention that
\alpha=\infty
if the sequence
\left\{\|(\Phi(x_{0}+t_{k}h_{k})-\Phi(x_{0}))/t_{k}\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\right\}_{k\geq 1}
diverges. The above condition shows that

\lim_{k}\left\|\frac{\Phi(x_{0}+t_{k}h_{k})-\Phi(x_{0}+t_{k}h)}{t_{k}}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}}>0\,.

(5)

Define
\eta_{k}:s\mapsto x_{0}+t_{k}(h+s(h_{k}-h))\in\mathcal{K}
for
k
large enough, then
\Phi\circ\eta_{k}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
in
\mathcal{Y}
. Hence, the mean-value theorem (see e.g.,
[
41
, Theorem 4.2.]
) and the positive homogeneity of
D\Phi
give that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\Phi(x_{0}+t_{k}h_{k})-\Phi(x_{0}+t_{k}h)}{t_{k}}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\|D\Phi(\eta_{k}(s))\left(h_{k}-h\right)\|_{\mathcal{Y}}

(6)

\displaystyle=\|h_{k}-h\|_{\mathcal{X}}\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\left\|D\Phi(\eta_{k}(s))\left(\frac{h_{k}-h}{\|h_{k}-h\|_{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\,.

Write
v_{k}=\frac{h_{k}-h}{\|h_{k}-h\|_{\mathcal{X}}}
. If
\{\|D\Phi(\eta_{k}(s))(v_{k})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}:k\in\mathbb{N},\,s\in[0,1]\}
is bounded, then (
6
) yields that

\left\|\frac{\Phi(x_{0}+t_{k}h_{k})-\Phi(x_{0}+t_{k}h)}{t_{k}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}\lesssim\|h_{k}-h\|_{\mathcal{X}}\to 0,

contradicting (
5
).
Therefore, as
h_{k}\to h
and
t_{k}\to 0^{+}
, after taking subsequences, we can choose a sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathcal{X}
such that
\|x_{n}-x_{0}\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq 2^{-n}
and
\|D\Phi(x_{n})(v_{n})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\geq 2^{n^{2}}
. Applying
[
40
, Corollary 2.10]
shows that there exist a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
curve
\eta:[-1,1]\to\mathcal{X}
and two sequences of positive numbers
\{s_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset(0,\infty)
and
\{s_{n}^{\prime}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset(0,\infty)
with
s_{n},s_{n}^{\prime}\to 0
and such that
\eta(s_{n}+t)=x_{n}+t\frac{v_{n}}{2^{n}}
for
t\in(-s_{n}^{\prime},s_{n}^{\prime})
. Since
\Phi\circ\eta
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
by assumption, we derive that

\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\Phi(\eta(s_{n}+t))=\frac{1}{2^{n}}D\Phi(x_{n})(v_{n}).

(7)

Moreover,
\left\|\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\Phi(\eta(s_{n}+t))\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}}
is finite for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. On the other hand, (
7
) and the fact that
\|D\Phi(x_{n})(v_{n})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\geq 2^{n^{2}}
imply

\left\|\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\Phi(\eta(s_{n}+t))\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left\|D\Phi(x_{n})(v_{n})\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}}>2^{n^{2}-n}\to\infty,

which leads to a contradiction.
∎

As a consequence, we arrive at the following result.

Corollary 4.3
.

The functional
\Phi:\mathcal{C}^{2}(\overline{\Omega})\to\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, mapping a density

to its transport map

from

to
p
, is Hadamard differentiable at
P_{K}
tangentially to

\mathcal{X}=\left\{\text{$f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\overline{\Omega})$ with $\int_{\Omega}f=0$}\right\}.

Proof.

Since
P_{K}
is uniformly bounded away from zero, there exists an open neighborhood
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{X}
of zero such that
P+f
is a probability density. The function
\mathcal{U}\ni f\mapsto\Phi(P_{K}+f)
is differentiable along curves in the sense of

4.2
. Hence, applying

3.5
concludes the result.
∎

.

It follows that

5
Proof of
Theorem

3.2

In this section, we provide the proof of Theorem
3.2
. We recall that for each
t
, the function
\phi_{t}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is strictly convex and solves the boundary problem (
1
). Following
[
54
]
, we rewrite (
1
) in a form that allows for a linearization:

\displaystyle\det(D^{2}\phi_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})}\quad{\rm in}\,\,\Omega,

(10)

\displaystyle h_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})

\displaystyle=0\quad{\rm in}\,\,\partial\Omega.

As indicated previously, the strategy is to differentiate (
10
) and apply the implicit function theorem
[
17
, Theorem 15.1]
over the functional

\displaystyle\Gamma:I\times\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}_{>0}(\bar{\Omega})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{C}^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\times\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)

(11)

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}t\\
\phi\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\Gamma_{t}^{(1)}(\phi)\\
\Gamma_{t}^{(2)}(\phi)\end{array}\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log(\det(D^{2}\phi))-\log(q)+\log(p_{t}(\nabla\phi))\\
h_{t}(\nabla\phi)\end{array}\right),

where for
\lambda\geq 0
,

\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}_{>\lambda}(\bar{\Omega}):=\{f\in\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}):D^{2}f>\lambda I\}.

We observe that the terms
\log(p_{t}(\nabla\phi))
and
h_{t}(\nabla\phi)
appear in the definition of
\Gamma
. Therefore, we must ensure that the composition of Hölder continuous functions with the same exponents remains Hölder continuous with exponent unchanged. Unfortunately, such a claim is untrue in general. For example, if
f,g\in\mathcal{C}^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, then
f\circ g\in\mathcal{C}^{0,\alpha^{2}}(\overline{\Omega})
rather than being
\mathcal{C}^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
. However, the claim is valid when either
f
or
g
has higher regularity as in the following lemma, which is an immediate result from
[
15
, Theorem 4.3]
.

Lemma 5.1

(Compositions of Hölder Functions)
.

For any
\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
and integers
l\geq k\geq 0
with
l\geq 1
, there exists a constant
C=C(k,l,\alpha,\beta,\Omega,\Omega^{\prime})>0
such that for all
f\in\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}(\Omega^{\prime})
and
g\in\mathcal{C}^{l+1,\beta}(\overline{\Omega})
satisfying
\nabla g(\overline{\Omega})\subset\Omega^{\prime}
, we have
f\circ\nabla g\in\mathcal{C}^{k,\min(\alpha,\beta)}(\overline{\Omega})
and

\|f\circ\nabla g\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k,\min(\alpha,\beta)}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\bigl(1+\|g\|_{\mathcal{C}^{l+1,\beta}(\overline{\Omega})}^{k+\alpha}\bigr)\|f\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}(\Omega^{\prime})}.

As a direct consequence of Lemma
5.1
, we establish that
\Gamma
is well-defined. We conclude this subsection by presenting a set of properties related to Hölder spaces, which will be instrumental in the subsequent subsection for proving that the Fréchet derivative of
\Gamma
with respect to
\phi
is well-defined. The following lemma, adapted from
[
15
, Proposition 6.1,6.2]
, states the stability of Hölder functions under composition, and justifies the condition in
Theorem

3.2
, where we assume that
p_{t}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1,\gamma}
rather than merely
\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Under the same setting as in
Lemma

5.1
, the map

\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}(\Omega^{\prime})\ni f\mapsto f\circ\nabla g\in\mathcal{C}^{k,\min{(\alpha,\beta)}}(\overline{\Omega})

is linear and continuous. In addition, if
\alpha>\beta
, then the map

\mathcal{C}^{l+1,\beta}(\overline{\Omega})\ni g\mapsto f\circ\nabla g\in\mathcal{C}^{k,\beta}(\overline{\Omega})

is also continuous. More precisely, there exists
\delta,\rho,M>0
such that if
\left\lVert g^{\prime}-g\right\rVert_{\mathcal{C}^{l+1,\beta}(\overline{\Omega})}<\delta
, one has that

\|f\circ\nabla g-f\circ\nabla g^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k,\beta}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq M\|f\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}(\Omega^{\prime})}\|g-g^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{l+1,\beta}(\overline{\Omega})}^{\rho}.

The next result is standard and can be found in
[
27
, Section 4.1]
.

Lemma 5.3

(Product of Hölder Functions)
.

For any
\alpha,\beta\in[0,1],l\geq k\geq 0
, there exists a constant
C=C(k,l,\alpha,\beta,{\rm diam}(\Omega))>0
such that for all
f\in\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}),g\in\mathcal{C}^{l,\beta}(\overline{\Omega})
,

\left\lVert fg\right\rVert_{\mathcal{C}^{l,\min(\alpha,\beta)}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathcal{C}^{l,\beta}(\overline{\Omega})}.

As a consequence of
Lemma

5.1
and
Lemma

5.3
, we immediately arrive at the following result as the function
1/g
may be viewed as the composition of
x\mapsto 1/x
and
g
, and
x\mapsto 1/x
is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
on
[\lambda,\infty)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
and
g\geq c_{0}
for some constant
c_{0}>0
, then
f/g\in\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
and there exists
C=C(k,\alpha,c_{0},{\rm diam}(\Omega))
such that

\left\lVert f/g\right\rVert_{\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}(1+\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}^{k+\alpha}).

Lemma 5.5
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\rm tr}([D^{2}\phi_{t}]^{-1}D^{2}\xi)+\frac{\langle\nabla p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}),\nabla\xi\rangle}{p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})}\\

The first claim is straightforward by (

5.2
Linearized Monge–Ampère Equation

In this subsection, we start by showing that the linearized Monge–Ampère operator
D_{\phi}\Gamma_{t}(\phi_{t})
is a second-order elliptic differential operator (see
Lemma

5.8
) with strictly oblique boundary conditions (see
Lemma

5.9
). As a consequence, it is invertible (see
Theorem

5.6
) as a mapping from
\mathcal{X}_{t}
to
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
, where

\mathcal{X}_{t}=\left\{\xi\in\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}):\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}p_{t}\xi(\nabla\phi_{t}^{*})=0\right\},

and

\mathcal{Y}_{t}:=\left\{(f,g)\in\mathcal{C}^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\times\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega):\int_{\Omega}qf=\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}p_{t}g(\nabla\phi_{t}^{*})\right\}.

Here, we recall that
p_{t}g(\nabla\phi_{t}^{*})
is the function
x\mapsto p_{t}(x)g(\nabla\phi_{t}^{*}(x))
.
Here, we equip
\mathcal{X}_{t}
with
\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}
norm and
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
with the product norm on
\mathcal{C}^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\times\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)
, under which
\mathcal{X}_{t},\mathcal{Y}_{t}
are Banach spaces. We note that
\phi_{t}\in\mathcal{X}_{t}
, and, as shown in
Theorem

5.6
,
D_{t}\Gamma(\phi_{t})\xi\in\mathcal{Y}_{t}
for every
\xi\in\mathcal{X}_{t}
.
To invert it, we must obtain the existence and uniqueness of solutions in
\mathcal{X}_{t}
to the linearized Monge–Ampère equation:

\displaystyle{\rm tr}([D^{2}\phi_{t}]^{-1}D^{2}\xi)+\frac{\langle\nabla p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}),\nabla\xi\rangle}{p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})}

\displaystyle=f\quad\rm in\,\,\Omega,

(14)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla h_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}),\nabla\xi\rangle

\displaystyle=g\quad\rm on\,\,\partial\Omega,

for every
(f,g)\in\mathcal{Y}_{t}
.Due to the absence of a zero-order term, the standard Schauder theory for elliptic equations with oblique derivative boundary conditions
[
27
, Section 6.7]
does not apply directly. Motivated by the Fredholm-type argument used in
[
47
, Theorem 3.1]
to establish solvability of the Neumann problem, and by the strategy of
[
22
]
for solving the second boundary-value problem for the Monge–Ampère equation in the presence of a
non-vanishing
zero-order term when
d=2
, we obtain the following theorem. It establishes solvability of the linearized Monge–Ampère equation and is of independent interest for second-order elliptic equations with strictly oblique boundary conditions and a
vanishing
zero-order term.

Theorem 5.6

(Solvability of the Linearized Monge–Ampère Equation)
.

Under the setting of Theorem
3.2
. Let
f\in\mathcal{C}^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}),g\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}(\partial{\Omega})
.
Then the linearized Monge–Ampère equation (
14
) admits a unique solution
\xi\in\mathcal{X}_{t}
if and only if

\int_{\Omega}qf=\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}p_{t}g(\nabla\phi_{t}^{*}).

(CC)

As a consequence,
D_{\phi}\Gamma_{t}(\phi_{t}):\mathcal{X}_{t}\to\mathcal{Y}_{t}
is bounded and invertible.

Remark 5.7
.

The condition presented in (
CC
) is commonly known as the
compatibility condition
in the literature related to the Neumann-type boundary condition.

We now proceed with the proof of
Theorem

5.6
. We start by proving that (
14
) is a second-order elliptic partial differential equation with strictly oblique boundary conditions. The following lemma controls
[D^{2}\phi_{t}]^{-1}
from below and above yielding the uniform ellipticity of
D_{\phi}\Gamma_{t}(\phi_{t})
. The proof is omitted as it is presented in
[
24
, Remark 1.1]
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let the setting of Theorem
3.2
hold, for each
t\in I
, there exists
\beta_{t}>0
such that

\beta_{t}^{-1}{\rm I}_{d}\leq[D^{2}\phi_{t}]^{-1}\leq\beta_{t}{\rm I}_{d}\quad\rm in\,\,\overline{\Omega}.

The following result shows that
D_{\phi}\Gamma_{t}(\phi_{t})
admits a strictly oblique boundary condition. Its proof follows among the lines of
[
54
, Section 2]
.

Lemma 5.9

(Strict Obliqueness)
.

Under the setting of Theorem
3.2
, for each
t\in I
, there exist
\rho_{t}>0
such that

\left\langle\nabla h_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}(x)),\nu(x)\right\rangle\geq\rho_{t}\quad\text{for all}\ x\in\partial\Omega,

where
\nu
denotes the unit outer normal vector-field to
\partial\Omega
.

Proof.

For brevity, we may drop any sub-indices related to
t
. Define
H(x):=\nabla h(\nabla\phi(x))
for
x\in\overline{\Omega}
and
\chi(x):=\left\langle H(x),\nu(x)\right\rangle
for
x\in\partial\Omega
. Since
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
and
h\in\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega^{\prime}})
,
\chi
is continuous on
\partial\Omega
and admits minimum at some point
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
. Let
\{{e}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Up to a translation and rotation, we may assume
x_{0}={0}
,
{e}_{1},\dots,{e}_{d-1}
are tangential to
\partial\Omega
at
{0}
, and
\nu({0})=e_{d}
.
Since
h(\nabla\phi)<0
in
\Omega
and equals zero on
\partial\Omega
, we have

D_{j}H({0})=\left\langle DH({0}),e_{j}\right\rangle=\left\langle D^{2}\phi({0})\nabla h(\nabla\phi({0})),e_{j}\right\rangle=D_{jk}\phi D_{k}h=0\quad{\rm on}\,\,\partial\Omega,

(15)

for
j=1,\dots,d-1,

and

D_{d}H({0})=\left\langle DH({0}),e_{d}\right\rangle=\left\langle D^{2}\phi({0})\nabla h(\nabla\phi({0})),e_{d}\right\rangle=D_{dk}\phi D_{k}h\geq 0\quad\rm on\,\,\partial\Omega,

(16)

where
D_{dk}\phi:=\partial_{e_{d}}\partial_{{e}_{k}}\phi({0})
,
D_{k}h:=\partial_{{e}_{k}}h(D\phi({0}))
, and we are summing over repeated indices. For ease of the notations, the functions are assumed to be evaluated at
{0}
unless explicitly specified in the rest of this proof. In particular, combining (
15
) and (
16
) implies that

D^{2}\phi\nabla h=DH,

(17)

and

DH=D_{d}H\,e_{d}=(0,\dots,D_{d}H)

(18)

where
\nabla h=(D_{1}h,\dots,D_{d}h)^{\intercal}
.
Since
\phi
is strictly convex,
D^{2}\phi
is invertible on
\partial\Omega
, and thus

D_{d}h=e_{d}^{\intercal}[D^{2}\phi]^{-1}DH.

(19)

Taking the square of both sides of (
19
), we obtain that

\displaystyle(D_{d}h)^{2}

\displaystyle=(e_{d}^{\intercal}[D^{2}\phi]^{-1}DH)(e_{d}^{\intercal}[D^{2}\phi]^{-1}DH)

\displaystyle\eqref{eq: nablaHDnHe}\implies

\displaystyle=(e_{d}^{\intercal}[D^{2}\phi]^{-1}(D_{d}H\,e_{d}))(e_{d}^{\intercal}[D^{2}\phi]^{-1}(D_{d}H\,e_{d}))

\displaystyle=e_{d}^{\intercal}[D^{2}\phi]^{-1}e_{d}(e_{d}D_{d}H)^{\intercal}[D^{2}\phi]^{-1}(D_{d}He_{d})

\displaystyle\eqref{eq: nablaHDnHe}\implies

\displaystyle=e_{d}^{\intercal}[D^{2}\phi]^{-1}e_{d}(DH)^{\intercal}[D^{2}\phi]^{-1}DH

\displaystyle\eqref{eq: combined}\implies

\displaystyle=e_{d}^{\intercal}[D^{2}\phi]^{-1}e_{d}(\nabla h)^{\intercal}D^{2}\phi[D^{2}\phi]^{-1}D^{2}\phi\nabla h

\displaystyle=e_{d}^{\intercal}[D^{2}\phi]^{-1}e_{d}(\nabla h)^{\intercal}D^{2}\phi\nabla h.

Hence,

\chi({0})=\left\langle\nabla h(\nabla\phi({0})),\nu({0})\right\rangle=D_{d}h=\sqrt{e_{d}^{\intercal}[D^{2}\phi]^{-1}e_{d}(\nabla h)^{\intercal}D^{2}\phi\nabla h}.

By applying
Lemma

5.8
, we derive the bound

\chi({0})\geq\beta_{t}^{-2}\sqrt{e_{d}^{\intercal}e_{d}\|\nabla h\|^{2}},

which implies the existence of a constant
\rho_{t}>0
such that
\chi({0})\geq\rho_{t}
as
\|\nabla h\|=1
by assumption. Since
\inf_{x\in\partial\Omega}\chi(x)=\chi({0})
, the claim follows.
∎

So far, we have shown that the linearized Monge–Ampère operator
D_{\phi}\Gamma_{t}(\phi_{t})
is a second-order elliptic differential operator with strictly oblique boundary condition. Moreover, as shown in the following lemma, we may prove that, if a solution exists, it is unique up to a constant shift.

Lemma 5.10
.

Under the setting of
Theorem

3.2
, let
\xi\in\mathcal{X}_{t}
be a solution to

\displaystyle{\rm tr}([D^{2}\phi_{t}]^{-1}D^{2}\xi)+\frac{\langle\nabla p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}),\nabla\xi\rangle}{p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})}

\displaystyle=0\quad\rm in\,\,\Omega,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla h_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}),\nabla\xi\rangle

\displaystyle=0\quad\rm on\,\,\partial\Omega.

Then
\xi=0
.

Proof.

Assume that the contrary holds. By strong maximum principle
[
27
, Theorem 3.5]
,
\xi
attains its maximum at some
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
. Let
\nu
be the outer
normal unit vector field at
\partial\Omega
. As
\Omega
is uniformly convex, there exists a ball
B\subset\Omega
such that
x_{0}\in\Omega
.
Lemma

5.9
implies that
\left\langle\nabla h_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}),\nu\right\rangle>0
on
\partial\Omega
(hence
\left\langle\nabla h_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}(x_{0})),\nu(x_{0})\right\rangle>0
), so that Hopf’s Lemma
[
34
, Theorem 2.5]
yields that
\left\langle\nabla h_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}(x_{0})),\nabla\xi(x_{0})\right\rangle>0,

which contradicts the boundary condition.
∎

Now we are ready to proceed with the proof of Theorem
5.6
. As mentioned earlier, the uniqueness result presented in Lemma
5.10
motivates an application of the Fredholm alternative. It remains to identify the necessary conditions for the solvability of the linearized Monge–Ampère equation for a general
f\in\mathcal{C}^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
and
g\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}(\partial{\Omega})
.

Proof of Theorem
5.6
.

We prove necessity and sufficiency separately.

Necessity:

Suppose
\xi\in\mathcal{X}_{t}
solves (
14
), then by (
1
)

\int_{\Omega}qf=\int_{\Omega}\left(p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})\mathrm{tr}([D^{2}\phi_{t}]^{-1}D^{2}\xi)+\left\langle\nabla p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}),\nabla\xi\right\rangle\right)\det(D^{2}\phi_{t})\,.

(20)

Using a change of variable from
x\mapsto\nabla\phi_{t}(x)
and noting that
[D^{2}\phi_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}^{*})]^{-1}=D^{2}\phi_{t}^{*}
, (
20
) becomes

\int_{\Omega_{t}}\mathrm{tr}(D^{2}\phi_{t}^{*}D^{2}\xi(\nabla\phi_{t}^{*}))p_{t}+\left\langle\nabla p_{t},\nabla\xi(\nabla\phi_{t}^{*})\right\rangle=\int_{\Omega_{t}}\,\mathrm{div}(p_{t}\nabla\xi(\nabla\phi_{t}^{*})).

(21)

By the divergence theorem
[
23
, Appendix C.1]
, (
21
) can be written as

\int_{\Omega_{t}}\,\mathrm{div}(p_{t}\nabla\xi(\nabla\phi_{t}^{*}))=\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}\left\langle p_{t}\nabla\xi(\nabla\phi_{t}^{*}),\nabla h_{t}\right\rangle=\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}p_{t}g(\nabla\phi^{*}_{t}).

Sufficiency:
By
Lemma

5.8
,
Lemma

5.9
, and
[
27
, Theorem 6.31]
, the operator

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}:\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{C}^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\times\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\begin{array}[]{c}L\\
B\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\rm tr}([D^{2}\phi_{t}]^{-1}D^{2}\xi)+\frac{\langle\nabla p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}),\nabla\xi\rangle}{p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})}\\
\langle\nabla h_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}),\nabla\xi\rangle+\xi\end{array}\right)

is bounded with bounded inverse
\mathbb{M}^{-1}
. The necessary condition above implies that if
\xi\in\mathcal{X}_{t}
, then

\int_{\Omega}qL(\xi)=\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}p_{t}[B(\xi)-\xi](\nabla\phi_{t}^{*})=\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}p_{t}B(\xi)(\nabla\phi_{t}^{*}),

so that
\mathbb{M}(\xi)\in\mathcal{Y}_{t}
. As a consequence,
\mathbb{M}|_{\mathcal{X}_{t}}:\mathcal{X}_{t}\to\mathcal{Y}_{t}
is a bijection. Call
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{Y}_{t}\to\mathcal{X}_{t}
its (bounded) inverse and consider the equation

\xi-\mathcal{L}(0,\xi)=\mathcal{L}(f,g).

(22)

Here, the boundedness of
\mathcal{L}
follows from the inverse mapping theorem
[
5
, Corollary 2.7]
. We see that
\xi\in\mathcal{X}_{t}
solves (
14
) if and only if
\xi
solves (
22
). Note that
\mathcal{L}(0,\xi)
is a well-defined element of
\mathcal{X}_{t}
as
(0,\xi)\in\mathcal{Y}_{t}
.

Now we apply the Fredholm alternative
[
27
, Theorem 5.3]
to conclude the solvability of (
22
). Define
T:\mathcal{X}_{t}\ni g\mapsto\mathcal{L}[0,g]\in\mathcal{X}_{t}
. Since
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{Y}_{t}\to\mathcal{X}_{t}
is bounded and the map

{\bf i}:\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\cap\mathcal{X}_{t}\ni g\mapsto g\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)\cap\left\{\xi\in\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega):\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}p_{t}\xi(\nabla\phi_{t}^{*})=0\right\}

is compact,
the identity

T(g)=\mathcal{L}(0,{\bf i}(g))
yields the compactness of
T
. Since (
22
) is equivalent to

\xi-T\xi=\mathcal{L}(f,g),

(23)

the Fredholm alternative implies that (
23
) admits a unique solution
\xi\in\mathcal{X}_{t}
if and only if the homogeneous equation

\xi-T\xi=0

(24)

has only a trivial solution. As (
24
) is equivalent to (
14
), according to
Lemma

5.10
,
\xi=0
is the unique solution of (
14
) in
\mathcal{X}_{t}
. The result follows.
∎

5.3
The Implicit Function Theorem

Theorem

5.6
guarantees the invertibility of
D_{\phi}\Gamma_{t}(\phi_{t})
between the spaces
\mathcal{X}_{t}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
. However, the range of
\Gamma
may be beyond
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
. Therefore, we modify the functional as follows to satisfy the compatibility conditions (
CC
). Set
t\in I
and consider

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}^{(t)}:I\times(\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}_{>0}(\bar{\Omega})\cap\mathcal{X}_{t})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{Y}_{t}

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}s\\
\phi\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\Gamma_{s}^{(1)}(\phi)-\int_{\Omega}q\Gamma_{s}^{(1)}(\phi)+\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}p_{t}[\Gamma_{s}^{(2)}(\phi)](\nabla\phi_{t}^{*})\\
\Gamma_{s}^{(2)}(\phi)\end{array}\right).

The following result shows that for each
t\in I
, the implicit function theorem (see e.g.,
[
27
, Theorem 17.6]
or
[
17
, Theorem 15.1 and Corollary 15.1]
) can be applied over
\tilde{\Gamma}^{(t)}
.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
t\in I
be as above. Under the assumptions of
Theorem

3.2
, the following holds:

(1)

\tilde{\Gamma}^{(t)}_{s}(\phi_{s})=0
if and only if
\Gamma_{s}(\phi_{s})=0
.

(2)

There exist an open set
I^{\prime}\times\mathcal{U}_{t}\subset I\times(\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}_{>0}(\bar{\Omega})\cap\mathcal{X}_{t})
with
(t,\phi_{t})\in I\times\mathcal{U}_{t}
such that
\tilde{\Gamma}^{(t)}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
in
I^{\prime}\times\mathcal{U}_{t}
.

(3)

The Fréchet derivative of
\tilde{\Gamma}^{(t)}
w.r.t.
\phi\in\mathcal{X}_{t}
at
(t,\phi_{t})\in I\times(\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}_{>0}(\bar{\Omega})\cap\mathcal{X}_{t})
, namely
D_{\phi}\tilde{\Gamma}_{t}(\phi_{t})
, satisfies
D_{\phi}\tilde{\Gamma}_{t}(\phi_{t})h=D_{\phi}\Gamma_{t}(\phi_{t})h
for all
h\in\mathcal{X}_{t}
.

(4)

The bounded linear operator
D_{\phi}\tilde{\Gamma}_{t}(\phi_{t}):\mathcal{X}_{t}\to\mathcal{Y}_{t}
is invertible.

Proof.

The second point is a direct consequence of
Lemma

5.5
and the fourth point holds due to the third point and
Theorem

5.6
.

Proof of (1).
We prove that if
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}_{>0}(\bar{\Omega})
solves

\det(D^{2}\phi)=C\frac{q}{p_{s}(\nabla\phi)}\quad{\rm in}\,\,\Omega,\ h_{s}(\nabla\phi)=0\quad{\rm in}\,\,\partial\Omega,

for some
C\in\mathbb{R}
, then
C=1
. Note that
h_{s}(\nabla\phi)=0
in
\partial\Omega
implies
\nabla\phi(\Omega)=\Omega_{s}
, then

C=\int_{\Omega}\det(D^{2}\phi)p_{s}(\nabla\phi)=\int_{\Omega_{s}}p_{s}=1

and the claim follows.

Proof of (3).
From the linearity of integration, one have for
\xi\in\mathcal{X}_{t}

D_{\phi}\tilde{\Gamma}^{(t)}_{t}(\phi_{t})(\xi)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}D_{\phi}\Gamma_{t}^{(1)}(\phi_{t})(\xi)-\int_{\Omega}qD_{\phi}\Gamma_{t}^{(1)}(\phi_{t})(\xi)+\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}p_{t}[D_{\phi}\Gamma_{t}^{(2)}(\phi_{t})(\xi)](\nabla\phi_{t}^{*})\\
D_{\phi}\Gamma_{t}^{(2)}(\phi_{t})(\xi)\end{array}\right).

In view of
D_{\phi}\Gamma_{t}(\phi_{t})(\mathcal{X}_{t})=\mathcal{Y}_{t}
by
Theorem

5.6
,

\int_{\Omega}qD_{\phi}\Gamma_{t}^{(1)}(\phi_{t})(\xi)=\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}p_{t}[D_{\phi}\Gamma_{t}^{(2)}(\phi_{t})(\xi)](\nabla\phi_{t}^{*}),

then the result follows.
∎

The implicit function theorem in Banach spaces (see, e.g.,
[
17
, Theorem 15.1]
) implies that
I\ni t\mapsto\phi_{t}\in\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, meaning that
\{\phi_{t}\}_{t\in I}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
curve.
Moreover, it holds that

[D_{\phi}\tilde{\Gamma}_{t}^{(t)}(\phi_{t})](\partial_{t}\phi_{t})=-D_{t}\tilde{\Gamma}_{t}^{(t)}(\phi_{t}),

so that
\partial_{t}\phi_{t}
is the unique solution of

\displaystyle{\rm tr}([D^{2}\phi_{t}]^{-1}D^{2}\xi)+\frac{\langle\nabla p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}),\nabla\xi\rangle}{p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial_{t}p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})}{p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})}-\int_{\Omega}q\frac{\partial_{t}p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})}{p_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})}+\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}p_{t}{\partial_{t}h_{t}}\quad\rm in\,\,\Omega,

(29)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla h_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t}),\nabla\xi\rangle

\displaystyle=-\partial_{t}h_{t}(\nabla\phi_{t})\quad\rm on\,\,\partial\Omega.

We focus now on simplifying the right-hand side of (
29
). To do so, we prove

\int_{\Omega_{t}}q\partial p_{t}=\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}p_{t}{\partial_{t}h_{t}},

(30)

which follows as a consequence of the following Reynolds transport theorem (see e.g.,
[
23
, Appendix C.4]
) for implicitly defined surfaces.

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
\{h_{t}\}_{t\in I}
,
\{\Omega_{t}\}_{t\in I}
and
\Omega
be as in
Theorem

3.2
. Then for every
\mathcal{C}^{1}
curve
\{f_{t}\}_{t\in I}
on
\mathcal{C}(\Omega^{\prime})
it holds that

\partial_{t}\int_{\Omega_{t}}f_{t}=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\partial_{t}f_{t}-\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}f_{t}\partial_{t}h_{t}.

Proof.

Set
t\in I
and
define the curve
T_{s}=(\nabla h_{t+s})^{-1}\nabla h_{t}
. By assumption and by the inverse function theorem,
\{T_{s}\}_{s\in I}\subset\mathcal{C}(\Omega^{\prime})
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
,
h_{t+s}(T_{s})=0
in
\partial\Omega_{t}
, and
T_{s}(\Omega_{t})=\Omega_{s}
. Applying the second-order Taylor development on
h_{t+s}
provides the existence of a curve
\{\omega_{s}\}_{s\in I}
on
\mathcal{C}(\partial\Omega_{t})
such that
o(\|\omega_{s}\|_{\mathcal{C}(\partial\Omega_{t})})=o(s)
and

h_{t}=0=h_{t+s}(T_{s})=h_{t+s}+\langle\nabla h_{t+s},T_{s}-T_{0}\rangle+\omega_{s}\quad\text{on}\ \partial\Omega_{t}.

As a consequence, we get the relation

\partial_{t}h_{t}=-\langle\left.\partial_{s}\right|_{s=0}T_{s},\nabla h_{t}\rangle\quad\text{on}\ \partial\Omega_{t}.

(31)

Since
\nabla h_{t}
is the unit outer-normal vector field to
\partial\Omega_{t}
, Reynolds transport theorem implies that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int_{\Omega_{t}}f_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\partial_{t}f_{t}+\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}f_{t}\left\langle\left.\partial_{s}\right|_{s=0}T_{s},\nabla h_{t}\right\rangle

and the result follows by (
31
).
∎
