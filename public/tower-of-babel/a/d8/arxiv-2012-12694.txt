Orthogonal symmetric matrices and joins of graphs
Rupert H. Levene, Polona Oblak, Helena Šmigoc

Abstract
. We introduce a notion of compatibility for multiplicity matrices. This gives rise to a necessary condition for the join of two (possibly disconnected) graphs G G and H H to be the pattern of an orthogonal symmetric matrix, or equivalently, for the minimum number of distinct eigenvalues q q of G ∨ H G\vee H to be equal to two. Under additional hypotheses, we show that this necessary condition is also sufficient. As an application, we prove that q ⁡ ( G ∨ H ) q(G\vee H) is either two or three when G G and H H are unions of complete graphs, and we characterise when each case occurs.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background and related work

Orthogonal matrices, ubiquitous in matrix theory and central in applications, have been widely studied. However, some basic questions on their combinatorial structure remain open
[
20
,
8
]
. In this paper we advance our understanding of the zero-nonzero patterns of symmetric orthogonal matrices, drawing motivation from the Inverse Eigenvalue Problem for Graphs, a more general problem on the interplay between spectral and structural properties of a matrix.

Let
G
be a simple graph with vertex set
V(G)=\{1,\dots,n\}
and edge set
E(G)
, and consider
S(G)
, the set of all real symmetric
n\times n
matrices
A=(a_{ij})
such that, for
i\neq j
,
a_{ij}\neq 0
if and only if
\{i,j\}\in E(G)
, with no restriction on the diagonal entries of
A
. The
Inverse Eigenvalue Problem for Graphs (IEP-G)
seeks to characterise all possible sets of eigenvalues of matrices in
S(G)
. The IEP-G is unsolved for all except a few families of graphs, and it has motivated the study of several related parameters. One widely studied parameter is the
minimum number of distinct eigenvalues of a graph
,
namely
q(G)=\min\{q(A)\colon A\in S(G)\}
,
where
q(A)
denotes the number of distinct eigenvalues of a square matrix
A
. For a graph
G
the set
S(G)
contains an orthogonal symmetric matrix (or
G
is
is realisable by
an orthogonal symmetric matrix) if and only if
q(G)=2
.

The study of the minimum number of distinct eigenvalues of a graph was initiated by Leal-Duarte and Johnson in
[
14
]
, where they proved that if
T
is a tree, then
q(T)
cannot be smaller than
\diam(T)+1
; this bound was later improved for infinitely many trees (see e.g.,
[
9
]
).
Determining
q
for many graphs with cycles seems to be a difficult problem. It is well known that
q(G)=n
if and only if
G
is a path
[
10
]
, and graphs with
q(G)=n-1
were characterised in
[
5
]
using strong spectral properties. At the other extreme, while it is clear that
q(G)=1
if and only if
E(G)=\emptyset
, there is no known characterisation of graphs with
q(G)=2
, i.e., the graphs that are realisable by an orthogonal symmetric matrix.

Several families of graphs are known to have a realisation with an orthogonal symmetric matrix (see, e.g.,
[
6
,
3
,
13
,
16
]
), and several necessary conditions were determined by Adm et. al.
[
3
,
2
]
. The same authors also proved that
q(G\vee G)=2
for any connected graph
G
. Later, this result was generalized by Monfared and Shader
[
18
]
, who proved that
q(G\vee H)=2
for any connected graphs
G
and
H
with the same number of vertices. Recently, joins of disconnected graphs were investigated in
[
1
,
2
]
, where particular attention was given to joins of unions of complete graphs.

1.2.
Overview

Results in this paper contribute to the IEP-G, to the study of combinatorial structure of orthogonal matrices, and also shed light on certain completion problems for orthogonal matrices.

The main framework is developed in Section
2
, where we present a necessary condition for the join of two (possibly disconnected) graphs,
G\vee H
, to have
q(G\vee H)=2
, as well as a closely related sufficient condition. Section
3
contains remarks, extensions of previous results and initial examples derived from results in Section
2
. Finally, in Section
4
we apply our results to determine
q(G\vee H)
whenever
G
and
H
are unions of complete graphs. As a preview of our results, we state the main theorem of Section
4
below.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G=G_{1}\cup\dots\cup G_{k}
and
H=H_{1}\cup\dots\cup H_{\ell}
where
G_{1},\dots,G_{k}
and
H_{1},\dots,H_{\ell}
are connected graphs and
k\leq\ell
. By
\iso(G)
we denote the set of isolated vertices of
G
. If at least one of the following three conditions holds, then
G\vee H
is not realisable by an orthogonal symmetric matrix (i.e.,
q(G\vee H)\neq 2
).

(a)

|G|<\ell
;

(b)

\iso(G)\neq\emptyset
and
k+\ell>|G|+|\iso(G)|
;

(c)

\iso(G)=\emptyset
and
\iso(H)\neq\emptyset
, and
k<\ell<2k
and
|H|<2k
and
|G|<k+\ell
.

Moreover, if the connected components of
G
and
H
are all complete graphs, then

q(G\vee H)=\begin{cases}2&\text{if none of conditions (a), (b), (c) is satisfied,}\\
3&\text{if at least one of (a), (b), (c) is satisfied.}\end{cases}

.

2.
Compatible multiplicity matrices

Below we introduce the notion of compatible multiplicity matrices, which will give a necessary condition for
q(G\vee H)=2
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a connected graph,
n:=|G|
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
. We call a vector
\mathbf{v}=\left(\begin{matrix}v_{1}&v_{2}&\ldots&v_{r}\end{matrix}\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r}

a multiplicity vector
for
G
if
\mathbf{v}
is an ordered multiplicity list that can be realised by a matrix in
S(G)
. In other words,
\sum_{i=1}^{r}v_{i}=n
and there is an eigenvalue list
\Lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
with
\lambda_{i}<\lambda_{i+1}
for
i\in[r-1]
, and an orthogonal matrix
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, so that
UD_{\Lambda,\mathbf{v}}U^{\top}\in S(G)
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
G
be a graph and
r,k\in\mathbb{N}
. We say that a matrix
V\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times k}
is a
multiplicity matrix
for
G
if
G
has
k
connected components
G_{1},\dots,G_{k}
, and for
1\leq i\leq k
, the
i
th column of
V
is a multiplicity vector for
G_{i}
.

We note for future reference that
|G|={\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}V{\bf 1}_{k}
whenever
V
is an
r\times k
multiplicity matrix for a graph
G
.

Definition 2.3
.

For a matrix
X
with at least
3
rows, we write
\widetilde{X}
for the matrix obtained by deleting the first row and the last row of
X
. Let
r,k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
with
r\geq 3
. Two matrices
V\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times k}
and
W\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times\ell}
are said to be
compatible

if
\widetilde{V}{\bf 1}_{k}=\widetilde{W}{\bf 1}_{\ell}
and
\widetilde{V}^{\top}\widetilde{W}>0
. We say that two graphs
G,H

have compatible multiplicity matrices
if there exist compatible matrices
V,W
where
V
is a multiplicity matrix for
G
and
W
is a multiplicity matrix for
H
.

In Theorem
2.5
we will show that compatibility of multiplicity matrices is a necessary condition for the join of two graphs to have
q=2
. For this, we use the following lemma on the Sylvester equation (see, e.g.,
[
12
]
), whose simple proof we omit.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
satisfy the equation
AB-BC=0
. If
\mathbf{v}
is an eigenvector of
C
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda
, then
B\mathbf{v}
is either equal to zero, or is an eigenvector of
A
corresponding to the same eigenvalue,
\lambda
. In particular, if
B
has trivial kernel, then
\sigma(C)\subseteq\sigma(A)
and hence
m\leq n
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
G
and
H
be two graphs. If
q(G\vee H)=2
, then
G
and
H
have compatible multiplicity matrices.

Proof.

Decompose
G
and
H
into their connected components as
G=\bigcup_{i\in[k]}G_{i}
and
H=\bigcup_{j\in[\ell]}H_{j}
. Since
q(G\vee H)=2
, there is an orthogonal symmetric matrix

X:=\left(\begin{matrix}A&B\\
B^{\top}&-C\end{matrix}\right)\in S(G\vee H)

where
A:=\bigoplus_{i\in[k]}A_{i}
with
A_{i}\in S(G_{i})
,
C:=\bigoplus_{j\in[\ell]}C_{j}
with
C_{j}\in S(H_{j})
, and
B
is a nowhere-zero matrix. Since
X
is orthogonal and symmetric, we have
X^{2}=I
.

Let us have a closer look at eigenvalues of
A
and
C
. First we note that if
Y\in\{A_{1},\dots,A_{k},C_{1},\dots,C_{\ell}\}
, then
Y
is a principal submatrix of the orthogonal symmetric matrix
X
, so every eigenvalue of
Y
is in the closed interval
[-1,1]
. Moreover, at least one eigenvalue of
Y
is in the open interval
(-1,1)
; for otherwise,
Y
is orthogonal and from
X^{2}=I
it follows easily that some row or column of
B
must be zero, contrary to hypothesis.

Therefore
A\oplus C
has at least one eigenvalue in the interval
(-1,1)
. Write the distinct eigenvalues of
A\oplus C
in
(-1,1)
as
\lambda_{2}<\dots<\lambda_{r-1}
, where
r\in\mathbb{N}
with
r\geq 3
, and define
\lambda_{1}=-1
and
\lambda_{r}=1
. Let
V
be the multiplicity matrix for
G
realised by
A
, i.e.,
V=(v_{si})_{s,i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times k}
where
v_{si}:=\mlt(\lambda_{s},A_{i})
for
s\in[r]
and
i\in[k]
.
Similarly, let
W=(w_{sj})_{s,j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times\ell}
be the multiplicity matrix for
H
realised by
C
, with
w_{sj}:=\mlt(\lambda_{s},C_{j})
. We proceed to show that
V
and
W
are compatible.

Let
U_{A}:=\bigoplus_{i\in[k]}U_{A_{i}}
and
U_{C}:=\bigoplus_{j\in[\ell]}U_{C_{j}}
where
U_{A_{i}}
and
U_{C_{j}}
are orthogonal matrices which diagonalise
A_{i}
and
C_{j}
, respectively.
Consider the orthogonal symmetric matrix
X^{\prime}:=(U_{A}\oplus U_{C})^{\top}X(U_{A}\oplus U_{C})
. Since
X^{\prime}
is orthogonal, each of its rows and columns is a unit vector. In particular, any row or column of
X^{\prime}
with
\pm 1
on the diagonal has every other entry equal to zero. Deleting all such rows and columns, we obtain an orthogonal symmetric matrix
X^{\prime\prime}
of the form

X^{\prime\prime}:=\left(\begin{matrix}D_{G}&F\\
F^{\top}&-D_{H}\end{matrix}\right)
,
where
D_{G}
and
D_{H}
are diagonal. Note that the diagonal entries of
D_{G}
and
D_{H}
are precisely the numbers
\lambda_{s}
for
s=2,\dots,r-1
, where
\mlt(\lambda_{s},D_{G})=(\widetilde{V}{\bf 1}_{k})_{s-1}
, and
\mlt(\lambda_{s},D_{H})=(\widetilde{W}{\bf 1}_{\ell})_{s-1}
. Since
-1<\lambda_{s}<1
for
s=2,\ldots,r-1
, the matrix
F^{\top}F=I-D_{H}^{2}
is invertible, and hence
F
has trivial kernel. The identity
(X^{\prime\prime})^{2}=I
also yields
D_{G}F-FD_{H}=0
, so by Lemma
2.4
we have
\sigma(D_{H})\subseteq\sigma(D_{G})
. By symmetry,
F^{\top}
also has trivial kernel, allowing us to conclude
\sigma(D_{H})=\sigma(D_{G})
. Hence,
\widetilde{V}{\bf 1}_{k}=\widetilde{W}{\bf 1}_{\ell}
.

It only remains to show that
\widetilde{V}^{\top}\widetilde{W}>0
. Let
D_{G_{i}}
be the restriction of
D_{G}
to the vertices in
G_{i}
which survived the earlier deletion, and define
D_{H_{j}}
similarly. By our earlier observations, these are all non-empty matrices.
Let
F=\left(F_{ij}\right)
be the block partition of
F
compatible with
\bigoplus_{i\in[k]}D_{G_{i}}
and
\bigoplus_{j\in[\ell]}D_{H_{j}}
. From
(X^{\prime\prime})^{2}=I
, we conclude that
D_{G_{i}}F_{ij}-F_{ij}D_{H_{j}}=0
. If
\sigma(D_{G_{i}})\cap\sigma(D_{H_{j}})=\emptyset
, then Lemma
2.4
shows that the Sylvester equation
D_{G_{i}}Y-YD_{H_{j}}=0
has only the trivial solution
Y=0
, so
F_{ij}=0
. Tracing back, this implies that the
(i,j)
th block of
B
is also zero, which is a contradiction. Therefore
D_{G_{i}}
and
D_{H_{j}}
have a common eigenvalue
\lambda_{s}
for at least one
s\in\{2,\ldots,r-1\}
, for all pairs
i
and
j
. By construction,
A_{i}
and
C_{j}
have the same property, so

(\widetilde{V}^{\top}\widetilde{W})_{ij}=\sum_{s=2}^{r-1}\mlt(\lambda_{s},A_{i})\mlt(\lambda_{s},C_{j})>0
.

Remark 2.11
.

Clearly, for a given graph
G
, any generically realisable multiplicity vector is sane, and any sane multiplicity vector is spectrally arbitrary. In fact, in some situations those concepts agree, but as we will see in Section
3
, there are cases where they are different.

Sane multiplicity vectors were considered in the literature before, although not under that name. For example, it is well known that if
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r}
and
n:={\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\geq 2
, then
\mathbf{v}
is spectrally arbitrary for

if it has at least two positive coordinates, and that any such
\mathbf{v}
is sane for
K_{n}
(see, e.g.,
[
19
, Theorem 4.5]
). Furthermore, any multiplicity vector
\mathbf{v}\in\{0,1\}^{r}
(i.e., with all its elements equal to
0
or
1
) is sane for every connected graph
G
of order
{\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{v}
, by
[
17
, Theorem 4.2]
.

In fact,
K_{n}
turns out to be generically realisable, as we will see in Proposition
4.1
below. In
[
15
]
, we will prove that

is generically realisable as well.

To prove Theorem
2.14
we will need the following technical lemma. For
q\in\mathbb{N}
, we write

for the set of

, considered as an algebraic subvariety of

. For

, we write

SO(\mathbf{p}):=SO(p_{1})\times\dots\times SO(p_{r})
.
We will use the elementary fact from algebraic geometry that the algebraic variety

is irreducible for any

(see, e.g.,
[
11
]
). Since

is a product of such varieties, it is also an irreducible algebraic variety.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{r})^{\top}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
and for
s\in[r]
, let
A_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{s}\times p_{s}}
with
\rk A_{s}=1
. If
\sum_{s=1}^{r}\tr(A_{s}X_{s})=0
for all
(X_{1},\dots,X_{r})\in SO(\mathbf{p})
, then
r\geq 2
and
p_{s}=1
for each
s\in[r]
.

Proof.

For
s\in[r]
, write
A_{s}=a_{s}\mathbf{v}_{s}{\bf w}_{s}^{\top}
where
\mathbf{v}_{s},{\bf w}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{s}}
are unit vectors and
a_{s}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Suppose for a contradiction that
p_{s}>1
for some
s\in[r]
. Then
SO(p_{s})
acts transitively on the unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{p_{s}}
, so there exist
X_{s},X_{s}^{\prime}\in SO(p_{s})
with
X_{s}\mathbf{v}_{s}={\bf w}_{s}=-X_{s}^{\prime}\mathbf{v}_{s}
. For
t\in[r]\setminus\{s\}
, choose any
X_{t}\in SO(p_{t})
and set
X_{t}^{\prime}=X_{t}
. We have
\sum_{t=1}^{r}\tr(A_{t}X_{t})=0=\sum_{t=1}^{r}(A_{t}X_{t}^{\prime})
, and cancelling common terms gives
\tr(A_{s}X_{s})=\tr(A_{s}X_{s}^{\prime})
. This implies that
a_{s}=0
, a contradiction. We conclude that
p_{s}=1
for each
s\in[r]
. In particular,
SO(p_{1})=\{1\}
, hence
\tr(A_{1}1)=\pm a_{1}\neq 0
, so
r\neq 1
.
∎

In the next result we identify a particular situation that Lemma
2.12
will eventually be applied to.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
k,m,n\in\mathbb{N}
. For
s\in[k]
, let
p_{s}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\emptyset\neq\mathcal{R}_{s},\mathcal{C}_{s}\subseteq[p_{s}]
where
m^{\prime}:=\sum_{s\in[k]}|\mathcal{R}_{s}|\leq m
and
n^{\prime}:=\sum_{s\in[k]}|\mathcal{C}_{s}|\leq n
.
Let
F:\times_{s=1}^{k}\mathbb{R}^{p_{s}\times p_{s}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
be given by

F(X_{1},\dots,X_{k}):=\left(\begin{matrix}\bigoplus_{s\in[k]}X_{s}[\mathcal{R}_{s},\mathcal{C}_{s}]&0_{m^{\prime}\times(n-n^{\prime})}\\
0_{(m-m^{\prime})\times n^{\prime}}&0_{(m-m^{\prime})\times(n-n^{\prime})}\end{matrix}\right).

Fix
a\in[m]
,
b\in[n]
and invertible nowhere-zero matrices
S\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
T\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
. Then there exist matrices
A_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{s}\times p_{s}}
with
\rk A_{s}=1
for
s\in[k]
so that

\left(S^{\top}F(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})T\right)_{ab}=\sum_{s\in[k]}\tr(A_{s}X_{s})

for any
X_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{1}},\dots,X_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{k}\times p_{k}}
.

Proof.

Let us write
F(\mathcal{X})=F(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})
.
Define
\mathcal{E}_{s}:=(\sum_{1\leq t<s}|\mathcal{R}_{t}|)+[|\mathcal{R}_{s}|]\subseteq[m]
and
\mathcal{F}_{s}:=(\sum_{1\leq t<s}|\mathcal{C}_{t}|)+[|\mathcal{C}_{s}|]\subseteq[n]
, so that for
s\in[k]
we have

X_{s}[\mathcal{R}_{s},\mathcal{C}_{s}]=F(\mathcal{X})[\mathcal{E}_{s},\mathcal{F}_{s}]
. Let
\chi_{\mathcal{E}_{s}\times\mathcal{F}_{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
denote the matrix with ones on
\mathcal{E}_{s}\times\mathcal{F}_{s}
and zeros everywhere else, and let
\circ
denote the Hadamard product. The block structure in the definition of
F(\mathcal{X})
implies that

F(\mathcal{X})=F(\mathcal{X})\circ\sum_{s\in[k]}\chi_{\mathcal{E}_{s}\times\mathcal{F}_{s}}
.
Observe that the matrix
Y:=(T{\bf e}_{a})(S{\bf e}_{b})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is rank one and nowhere-zero, so any submatrix of
Y
shares these properties. Define
A_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{s}\times p_{s}}
by setting
A_{s}[\mathcal{C}_{s},\mathcal{R}_{s}]:=Y[\mathcal{F}_{s},\mathcal{E}_{s}]
and defining all other entries to be zero (i.e.,
(A_{s})_{\alpha,\beta}=0
if
(\alpha,\beta)\not\in\mathcal{C}_{s}\times\mathcal{R}_{s}
). Then
\rk A_{s}=1
, and we have

\tr(A_{s}X_{s})=\tr(A_{s}[\mathcal{C}_{s},\mathcal{R}_{s}]X_{s}[\mathcal{R}_{s},\mathcal{C}_{s}])=\tr(Y[\mathcal{F}_{s},\mathcal{E}_{s}]F(\mathcal{X})[\mathcal{E}_{s},\mathcal{F}_{s}])=\tr(Y(F(\mathcal{X})\circ\chi_{\mathcal{E}_{s}\times\mathcal{F}_{s}}))
,
so

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in[k]}\tr(A_{s}X_{s})

\displaystyle=\tr\left(Y\Big(F(\mathcal{X})\circ\sum_{s\in[k]}\chi_{\mathcal{E}_{s}\times\mathcal{F}_{s}}\Big)\right)=\tr(YF(\mathcal{X}))

\displaystyle=\tr(T{\bf e}_{a}(S{\bf e}_{b})^{\top}F(\mathcal{X}))={\bf e}_{b}^{\top}S^{\top}F(\mathcal{X})T{\bf e}_{a}=(S^{\top}F(\mathcal{X})T)_{ab}.\qed

Theorem 2.14
.

Suppose that
G
and
H
have compatible sane multiplicity matrices
V=(v_{si})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times k}
and
W=(w_{sj})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times\ell}
. Let
\mathbf{p}=(p_{2},\dots,p_{r-1})^{\top}:=\widetilde{V}{\bf 1}_{k}=\widetilde{W}{\bf 1}_{\ell}
.
Then
q(G\vee H)\leq 2
if at least one of the following conditions is satisfied:

(a)

Whenever
(\widetilde{V}^{\top}\widetilde{W})_{ij}\geq 2
, there exists
s\in\{2,\ldots,r-1\}
with
v_{si}w_{sj}\neq 0
and
p_{s}\geq 2
.

(b)

V
is a generically realisable multiplicity matrix for
G
.

(c)

W
is a generically realisable multiplicity matrix for
H
.

Proof.

Let
G_{1},\dots,G_{k}
and
H_{1},\dots,H_{\ell}
be the connected components of
G
and

H
, and let
\Lambda=(-1,\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{r-1},1)
where
-1<\lambda_{2}<\ldots<\lambda_{r-1}<1
.
Write
\mathbf{v}_{i}
for
i
th column of
V
and let
D_{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{|G|\times|G|}
be the diagonal matrix
D_{G}=\bigoplus_{i\in[k]}D_{G_{i}}
, where

D_{G_{i}}=D_{\Lambda,\mathbf{v}_{i}}
.
Similarly, let
{\bf w}_{j}
be the
j
th column of
W
and let
D_{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{|H|\times|H|}
be the diagonal matrix
D_{H}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{\ell}D_{H_{j}}
, where

D_{H_{j}}=D_{\Lambda,{\bf w}_{j}}
.
Note that for
s\in\{2,\ldots,r-1\}
, we have
p_{s}=\mlt(\lambda_{s},D_{G})=\mlt(\lambda_{s},D_{H})
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{r})\in SO(\mathbf{p})
. The freedom in the choice of
\mathcal{Z}
will be needed at a later stage. For each
s\in\{2,\ldots,r-1\}
, let
U_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{2p_{s}\times 2p_{s}}
be the orthogonal symmetric matrix

U_{s}:=\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda_{s}I_{p_{s}}&(1-\lambda_{s}^{2})^{1/2}Z_{s}\\
(1-\lambda_{s}^{2})^{1/2}Z_{s}^{\top}&-\lambda_{s}I_{p_{s}}\end{matrix}\right).

Note that
U_{s}
depends on our choice of
\mathcal{Z}
.
Set
n_{1}:={\bf e}_{1}^{\top}V{\bf 1}_{k}+{\bf e}_{r}^{\top}W{\bf 1}_{\ell}
to be the sum of the entries in the first row of
V
and the last row of
W
, and
n_{r}:={\bf e}_{r}^{\top}V{\bf 1}_{k}+{\bf e}_{1}^{\top}W{\bf 1}_{\ell}
to be the sum of the entries in the first row of
W
and the last row of
V
. Consider the orthogonal matrix

U_{0}:=\left(\bigoplus_{s=2}^{r-1}U_{s}\right)\oplus\left(-I_{n_{1}}\right)\oplus I_{n_{r}}
.
By construction, the diagonal of
U_{0}
is a permutation of the diagonal of
D_{G}\oplus(-D_{H})
. It follows that
U_{0}
is permutation similar to an orthogonal matrix

U=\left(\begin{matrix}D_{G}&B(\mathcal{Z})\\
B(\mathcal{Z})^{\top}&-D_{H}\end{matrix}\right)

under a permutation which maps the top left block of each
U_{s}
to a submatrix of
D_{G}
, and the bottom right block to a submatrix of
-D_{H}
, where
B(\mathcal{Z})
is a
|G|\times|H|
matrix which depends on
\mathcal{Z}
. We partition
B(\mathcal{Z})
as a
k\times\ell
block matrix
B(\mathcal{Z})=(B_{ij}(\mathcal{Z}))_{i\in[k],j\in[\ell]}
with block-partition compatible with those of
D_{G}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}D_{G_{i}}
and
D_{H}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{\ell}D_{H_{j}}
, so that each
B_{ij}(\mathcal{Z})
is an
|G_{i}|\times|H_{j}|
matrix. For
(i,j)\in[k]\times[\ell]
, let
Q(i,j)=\{s\colon 2\leq s\leq r-1,v_{si}w_{sj}\neq 0\}
. Since
V
and
W
are compatible,
(\widetilde{V}^{\top}\widetilde{W})_{ij}\neq 0
and so
Q(i,j)\neq\emptyset
for every
i,j
.
For
2\leq s\leq r-1
, note that
\lambda_{s}
is a diagonal value of both
D_{G_{i}}
and
D_{H_{j}}
if and only if
s\in Q(i,j)
. By construction, the rows and columns of
B_{ij}(\mathcal{Z})
may be permuted to obtain the
|G_{i}|\times|H_{j}|
matrix

(1)

B_{ij}^{\prime}(\mathcal{Z}):=\left(\begin{matrix}\bigoplus_{s\in Q(i,j)}(1-\lambda_{s}^{2})^{1/2}Z_{s}[\mathcal{R}_{si},\mathcal{C}_{sj}]&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}\right)

where for each
s\in Q(i,j)
both
\{\mathcal{R}_{si}\colon i\in[k]\}
and
\{\mathcal{C}_{sj}\colon j\in[\ell]\}
are partitions of
[p_{s}]
, with
|\mathcal{R}_{si}|=\mlt(\lambda_{s},D_{G_{i}})=v_{si}
and
|\mathcal{C}_{sj}|=\mlt(-\lambda_{s},-D_{H_{j}})=w_{sj}
, and where the zeros in the matrix
B_{ij}^{\prime}(\mathcal{Z})
above represent the (possibly empty) zero matrices which pad the direct sum out to
|G_{i}|
rows and
|H_{j}|
columns.

Since
V
and
W
are sane multiplicity matrices for
G
and
H
, respectively, there exist nowhere-zero orthogonal matrices
S_{i},T_{j}
for
i\in[k]
,
j\in[\ell]
, such that
S_{i}D_{G_{i}}S_{i}^{\top}\in S(G_{i})
and

T_{j}D_{H_{j}}T_{j}^{\top}\in S(H_{j})
. Let
\mathcal{S}:=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})
and
\mathcal{T}:=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{\ell})
, and note that
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
can typically be chosen in several different ways. The matrix
R:=(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}S_{i})\oplus(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{\ell}T_{j})
is orthogonal and thus

RUR^{\top}=\left(\begin{matrix}A_{G}&C(\mathcal{Z})\\
C(\mathcal{Z})^{\top}&A_{H}\end{matrix}\right)
is an orthogonal symmetric matrix with
A_{G}\in S(G)
and
A_{H}\in S(H)
. Hence, provided the matrix
C(\mathcal{Z})=(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}S_{i})B(\mathcal{Z})(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{\ell}T_{j})^{\top}
has no zero elements we will have
RUR^{\top}\in S(G\vee H)
and so
q(G\vee H)\leq 2
, as required. To analyse when this happens, we will now refer back to the freedom we have in choosing
\mathcal{Z}
,
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
.

Note that the matrix
C(\mathcal{Z})
inherits the block-partition of
B(\mathcal{Z})
, i.e.,
C(\mathcal{Z})
is a
k\times\ell
block matrix with
|G_{i}|\times|H_{j}|
blocks
C_{ij}(\mathcal{Z})=S_{i}B_{ij}(\mathcal{Z})T_{j}^{\top}
. We will first fix
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
arbitrarily and show that hypothesis
(a)
of the theorem guarantees that
C(\mathcal{Z})
is nowhere-zero for some appropriate choice of
\mathcal{Z}
.

For
i\in[k]
,
j\in[\ell]
,
a\in[|G_{i}|]
and
b\in[|H_{j}|]
, consider the the linear functionals

L_{ab}(i,j):SO(\mathbf{p})\to\mathbb{R}
given by

L_{ab}(i,j)(\mathcal{Z}):=(C_{ij}(\mathcal{Z}))_{ab}
.
If
q(G\vee H)>2
, then
C(\mathcal{Z})
has at least one entry equal to zero for any choice of
\mathcal{Z}\in SO(\mathbf{p})
, so
SO(\mathbf{p})\subseteq\bigcup_{i,j,a,b}L_{ab}(i,j)^{-1}(0)
.
Since
SO(\mathbf{p})
is an irreducible algebraic variety, there exist
i_{0},j_{0},a_{0},b_{0}
so that for
L_{0}:=L_{a_{0},b_{0}}(i_{0},j_{0})
we have
SO(\mathbf{p})\subseteq L_{a_{0},b_{0}}(i_{0},j_{0})^{-1}(0)
, i.e.,
L_{0}(\mathcal{Z})=0
for
\mathcal{Z}\in SO(\mathbf{p})
.
On the other hand,
L_{0}(\mathcal{Z})=(S_{i_{0}}\Pi_{1}B_{i_{0},j_{0}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{Z})\Pi_{2}T_{j_{0}}^{\top})_{a_{0}b_{0}}
where
\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2}
are permutation matrices with
\Pi_{1}B_{i_{0},j_{0}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{Z})\Pi_{2}=B_{i_{0},j_{0}}(\mathcal{Z})
. By equation (
1
) and Lemma
2.13
, there exist matrices
A_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{s}\times p_{s}}
with
\rk A_{s}=1
for
s\in Q(i_{0},j_{0})
so that
0=L_{0}(\mathcal{Z})=\sum_{s\in Q(i_{0},j_{0})}\tr(A_{s}Z_{s})
for every
\mathcal{Z}\in SO(\mathbf{p})
. By Lemma
2.12
, we have
(\widetilde{V}^{\top}\widetilde{W})_{i_{0}j_{0}}\geq|Q(i_{0},j_{0})|\geq 2
, and
p_{s}=1
for every
s\in Q(i_{0},j_{0})
. On the other hand, for
s\in\{2,\dots,r-1\}\setminus Q(i_{0},j_{0})
, we have
(\widetilde{V}^{\top}\widetilde{W})_{i_{0}j_{0}}=0
.
This is inconsistent with hypothesis
(a)
. Hence, when
(a)
holds, we can find a suitable
\mathcal{Z}
for which the orthogonal matrix
RUR^{\top}
lies in
S(G\vee H)
, so
q(G\vee H)=2
in this case.

Now suppose that hypothesis
(b)
holds. Fix any invertible nowhere-zero
|G_{i}|\times|G_{i}|
matrices
\widetilde{S}_{i}
for
i\in[k]
, and
consider the linear functionals

\widetilde{L}_{ab}(i,j):SO(\mathbf{p})\to\mathbb{R}
given by
\widetilde{L}_{ab}(i,j)(\mathcal{Z}):=(\widetilde{S}_{i}B_{ij}(\mathcal{Z})T_{j}^{\top})_{ab}
.
Suppose that for any
\mathcal{Z}\in SO(\mathbf{p})
, there exist
i,j,b
so that the
b
th column of
B_{ij}(\mathcal{Z})T_{j}^{\top}
is zero. Then
SO(\mathbf{p})\subseteq\bigcup_{i,j,b}\bigcap_{a}\widetilde{L}_{ab}(i,j)^{-1}(0)
,
and by the irreducibility of the algebraic variety
SO(\mathbf{p})
there exist some fixed
i_{0},j_{0},b_{0}
so that
SO(\mathbf{p})\subseteq\bigcap_{a}\widetilde{L}_{ab_{0}}(i_{0},j_{0})^{-1}(0)
. In other words, for every
\mathcal{Z}\in SO(\mathbf{p})
, the
b_{0}
th column of
\widetilde{S}_{i_{0}}B_{i_{0}j_{0}}(\mathcal{Z})T_{j_{0}}^{\top}
is zero, so by the invertibility of
\widetilde{S}_{i_{0}}
, the
b_{0}
th column of
B_{i_{0}j_{0}}(\mathcal{Z})T_{j_{0}}^{\top}
is zero. Now (taking
a=1
) observe that
\widetilde{L}:=\widetilde{L}_{1b_{0}}(i_{0},j_{0})
vanishes at every
\mathcal{Z}\in SO(\mathbf{p})
. Since
\widetilde{S}_{i_{0}}
is nowhere-zero, Lemma
2.13
implies that
\widetilde{L}
may be written in the form
\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{Z})=\sum_{s\in Q}\tr(A_{s}Z_{s})
, where
\emptyset\neq Q:=Q(i_{0},j_{0})
and
\rk A_{s}=1
for each
s\in Q
. By Lemma
2.12
, for each
s\in Q
we have
p_{s}=1
. So for every
\mathcal{Z}\in SO(\mathbf{p})
and
s\in Q
, we have
Z_{s}\in SO(1)
, i.e.,
Z_{s}=1
. This implies that
B_{i_{0}j_{0}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{Z})=\left(\begin{matrix}D&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}\right)
where
D=\diag((1-\lambda_{s}^{2})^{1/2}:s\in Q)
is an invertible diagonal matrix and the
0
s represent (possibly empty) zero matrices. (In particular,
B^{\prime}_{i_{0}j_{0}}(\mathcal{Z})
is independent of
\mathcal{Z}
.) This implies that the kernel of
B^{\prime}_{i_{0}j_{0}}(\mathcal{Z})
does not contain a nowhere-zero vector, and the same is therefore true of the permutation equivalent matrix
B_{i_{0}j_{0}}(\mathcal{Z})
. Since
T_{j_{0}}
is nowhere-zero, it follows that the
b_{0}
th column of
B_{i_{0}j_{0}}(\mathcal{Z})T_{j_{0}}^{\top}
is not zero, a contradiction.

Therefore it is possible to choose
\mathcal{Z}\in SO(\mathbf{p})
so that
B_{ij}(\mathcal{Z})T_{j}^{\top}
has no column equal to zero for all pairs
i
and
j
. Since
V
is generically realisable for
G
, we can find a
k
-tuple
\mathcal{S}=(S_{1},\dots,S_{k})
of nowhere-zero orthogonal matrices such that for every
i\in[k]
, we have
S_{i}D_{G_{i}}S_{i}^{\top}\in S(G_{i})
and for every
j\in[\ell]
, the matrix
C_{ij}(\mathcal{Z})=S_{i}B_{ij}(\mathcal{Z})T_{j}^{\top}
is nowhere-zero. Hence,
q(G\vee H)\leq 2
in this case. The argument for hypothesis
(c)
is of course symmetric. ∎

Theorems
2.5
and
2.14
yield:

Corollary 2.15
.

If
G
is generically realisable and
H
is sane,
then
q(G\vee H)=2
if and only if
G
and

have a pair of compatible multiplicity matrices.

3.
Remarks, applications, examples

Our principal application of Theorem
2.14
in this paper is to determine the minimum number of distinct eigenvalues of the join of two graphs, each of which is a union of complete graphs. Before turning to that in Section
4
, we give some other consequences and related discussion.

3.1.
Construction of orthogonal symmetric matrices

As is apparent from the proof of Theorem
2.14
, several things would have to align for two graphs
G
and
H
with compatible multiplicity matrices to have
q(G\vee H)>2
, and this won’t happen in any sufficiently generic situation. In other words, once two graphs have compatible multiplicity matrices, they will typically have
q(G\vee H)
equal to
2
. This potentially gives us a wealth of examples, including situations in which we cannot verify the technical hypotheses of Theorem
2.14
.

In Pseudo-algorithm
3.1
, we concisely summarise the construction used repeatedly in the previous section. This is illustrated by the example involving cycles below.

Suppose
G
and
H
are graphs with
k
and
\ell
components, respectively, and

A_{G}\in S(G)
and
A_{H}\in S(H)
have compatible multiplicity matrices
V\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times k}
and
W\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times\ell}
, with respect to
\Lambda=(-1,\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{r-1},1)
where
\lambda_{i}\in(-1,1)
for
i=2,\dots,r-1
.

Step 1.

Choose orthogonal matrices
S,T
with
S^{\top}A_{G}S=D_{\Lambda,V{\bf 1}_{k}}
and
T^{\top}A_{H}T=D_{\Lambda,W{\bf 1}_{\ell}}
.
Choose
Z_{i}\in O(p_{i})
for
i=2,\ldots,r-1
, where
p_{i}={\bf e}_{i}^{\top}V{\bf 1}_{k}={\bf e}_{i}^{\top}W{\bf 1}_{\ell}
.

Step 2.

Let
C:=S\left(0_{{\bf e}_{1}^{\top}V{\bf 1}_{k}\times{\bf e}_{1}^{\top}W{\bf 1}_{\ell}}\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{s=2}^{r-1}(1-\lambda_{s}^{2})^{1/2}Z_{s}\right)\oplus 0_{{\bf e}_{r}^{\top}V{\bf 1}_{k}\times{\bf e}_{r}^{\top}W{\bf 1}_{\ell}}\right)T^{\top}
.

Step 3.

If
C
is nowhere-zero, return
X:=\left(\begin{matrix}A_{G}&C\\
C^{\top}&-A_{H}\end{matrix}\right)
.
Otherwise, go to Step 1, making a different choice of orthogonal matrices
S
,
T
and
Z_{i}
, if possible.

Of course, we have no guarantee that this procedure will terminate; this is why the technical hypotheses of Theorem
2.14
were imposed.

3.2.
Some families of graphs realisable by an orthogonal symmetric matrix

In
[

the authors proved that

if
G
and
H
are connected graphs with
|G|=|H|
. We can use Theorem
2.14
(a)
to extend their result to more than one connected component. (In fact, the statement below is also valid for
k=1

[
15
]
.)

Theorem 3.2
.

If
G
and
H
are graphs each having
k\geq 2
connected components, and there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
so that the order of every one of these connected components is either
n
,
n+1
or
n+2
,
then
q(G\vee H)=2
.

Proof.

Let
G_{1},\dots,G_{k}
and
H_{1},\dots,H_{k}
be the connected components of
G
and
H
, respectively. Consider the matrices

V:=\left(\begin{matrix}{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\\
E\\
{\mathbf{v}^{\prime}}^{\top}\end{matrix}\right)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{(n+2)\times k}\text{ and }W:=\left(\begin{matrix}{{\bf w}}^{\top}\\
E\\
{{\bf w}^{\prime}}^{\top}\end{matrix}\right)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{(n+2)\times k},

where
E:={\bf 1}_{n}{\bf 1}_{k}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
is the matrix full of ones and
\mathbf{v}=(v_{i})_{i\in[k]}
,
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}=(v^{\prime}_{i})_{i\in[k]}
,
{\bf w}=(w_{i})_{i\in[k]}
and
{\bf w}^{\prime}=(w^{\prime}_{i})_{i\in[k]}
are all
0
-
1
vectors in
\{0,1\}^{k}
, such that
v_{i}+v^{\prime}_{i}=|G_{i}|-n
and
w_{i}+w^{\prime}_{i}=|H_{i}|-n
for
i\in[k]
.
Note that
V
and
W
are sane multiplicity matrices for
G
and
H
, respectively, by Remark
2.11
. Moreover,
V
and
W
are compatible since
\widetilde{V}=\widetilde{W}
and
\widetilde{V}^{\top}\widetilde{W}=n{\bf 1}_{k}{\bf 1}_{k}^{\top}>0
.
Since
k\geq 2
, Theorem
2.14
(a)
implies that
q(G\vee H)=2
.
∎

It is easy to see that a complete graph is generically realisable (see Proposition
4.1
below for details). This fact together with Theorem
2.14
(c)
allows us to extend

[

, Lemmas 3.13 and 3.14]
, where the authors proved that if

.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
G
is a connected graph of order
m\in\{\ell,\ell+1,\ell+2\}
and

,
then
q(G\vee\bigcup_{j\in[\ell]}K_{n_{j}})=2
.

Proof.

Let us define

V:=\left(\begin{matrix}\varepsilon\\
{\bf 1}_{\ell}\\
\varepsilon^{\prime}\end{matrix}\right)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\ell+2}\text{ and }W:=\left(\begin{matrix}{\mathbf{n}^{\top}}\\
I_{\ell}\\
{{\bf 0}_{\ell}}^{\top}\end{matrix}\right)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{(\ell+2)\times\ell},

where
\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}\in\{0,1\}
and
{\mathbf{n}}=\left(\begin{matrix}n_{1}-1&\ldots&n_{\ell}-1\end{matrix}\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\ell}
. By Remark

,

is a sane multiplicity matrix for

,

∎

3.3.
Generic realisability and sanity

In the remainder of this section, we explore the relationship between the sane and the generically realisable multiplicity vectors. First, we show that provided its minimum multiplicity is greater than one, any sane multiplicity vector is automatically generically realisable.

Proposition 3.4
.

If
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r}
is a sane multiplicity vector for
G
with no entry equal to
1
, then
\mathbf{v}
is also generically realisable for
G
.

Proof.

Generic realisability for
G
is not changed by inserting or deleting zeros from
\mathbf{v}=\left(\begin{matrix}v_{1}&v_{2}&\ldots&v_{r}\end{matrix}\right)^{\top}
, so we may assume that
v_{i}\geq 2
for all
i\in[r]
.
Let
\Lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
with
\lambda_{i}<\lambda_{i+1}
for
i\in[r-1]
. Since
\mathbf{v}
is sane for
G
, there is a nowhere-zero orthogonal matrix
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
where
n=|G|
with
UD_{\Lambda,\mathbf{v}}U^{\top}\in S(G)
.
Note that for
\mathcal{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{r})\in SO(\mathbf{v})
, we have
(\oplus_{i=1}^{r}X_{i})D_{\Lambda,\mathbf{v}}(\oplus_{i=1}^{r}X_{i}^{\top})=D_{\Lambda,\mathbf{v}}
, hence
U
can be replaced by
U(\oplus_{i=1}^{r}X_{i})
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
be finite. For every
{\bf y}\in\mathcal{Y}
, and
j\in[n]
, we define linear functionals:

\displaystyle L(j,{\bf y}):SO(\mathbf{v})\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle L(j,{\bf y})(\mathcal{X}):=(U(\oplus_{i=1}^{r}X_{i}){\bf y})_{j}.

If
\mathbf{v}
is not generically realisable, then there exists
U
as above, and some finite set of nonzero vectors
\mathcal{Y}
so that:

SO(\mathbf{v})\subseteq\bigcup_{j\in[n],{\bf y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(j,{\bf y})^{-1}(0).

Irreducibility of the algebraic variety
SO(\mathbf{v})
guarantees the existence of a fixed
j_{0}\in[n]
and
{\bf y}_{0}\in\mathcal{Y}
so that
SO(\mathbf{v})\subseteq L(j_{0},{\bf y}_{0})^{-1}(0)
. For
\mathcal{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{r})\in SO(\mathbf{v})
, we can write:

L(j_{0},{\bf y}_{0})(\mathcal{X})=\tr\left(({\bf y}_{0}{\bf e}_{j_{0}}^{\top}U)(\oplus_{i=1}^{r}X_{i})\right).

Let
R_{i}:=[0_{v_{i}\times(v_{1}+\dots+v_{i}-1)}\;I_{v_{i}}\;0_{v_{i}\times(v_{i+1}+\dots+v_{r})}]
,
{\bf x}_{i}:=R_{i}{\bf y}_{0}
,
{\bf u}_{i}:=R_{i}U^{\top}{\bf e}_{j_{0}}
, and
A_{i}:={\bf x}_{i}{\bf u}_{i}^{\top}
. Since
U
is nowhere-zero, we have
{\bf u}_{i}\neq 0
for every
i\in[r]
, and
{\bf y}_{0}\neq 0
, so the set
Q=\{i\in[r]\colon{\bf x}_{i}\neq 0\}
is non-empty, and
L(j_{0},{\bf y}_{0})(\mathcal{X})=\sum_{i\in Q}\tr(A_{i}X_{i})
with
\rk A_{i}=1
for all
i\in Q
. Since
v_{i}\geq 2
for all
i
, this contradicts Lemma
2.12
.
∎

Our next task is to find an example of a sane multiplicity vector which is not generically realisable. First, a simple lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
{\mathcal{V}}
is a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which contains a nowhere-zero vector,
then there is a nowhere-zero orthonormal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}
.

Proof.

The case
\dim{\mathcal{V}}=1
is trivial. Assume inductively that
r=\dim{\mathcal{V}}\geq 2
and
{\bf b}_{1},\dots,{\bf b}_{r}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}
for which
{\bf b}_{1},\dots,{\bf b}_{r-1}
are nowhere-zero. For
0<t<1
, replace
{\bf b}_{r-1}
with
{\bf b}_{r-1}^{\prime}=\sqrt{1-t^{2}}{\bf b}_{r-1}+t{\bf b}_{r}
and replace
{\bf b}_{r}
with
{\bf b}_{r}^{\prime}=t{\bf b}_{r-1}+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}{\bf b}_{r}
. Since
{\bf b}_{r-1}
is nowhere-zero, for sufficiently small
t>0
the vectors
{\bf b}_{r-1}^{\prime}
and
{\bf b}_{r}^{\prime}
are nowhere-zero.
∎

Example 3.6
.

We claim that
\mathbf{v}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&m&1\end{matrix}\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
,
m\geq 3
, is sane, but not generically realisable for
K_{2}\vee mK_{1}
, showing that the two concepts are indeed different.

In fact,
\mathbf{v}
is not generically realisable for
K_{2}\vee mK_{1}
with respect to any list
\Lambda
of three distinct eigenvalues. To see this, it suffices by translation and scaling to take
\Lambda=(-1,0,\lambda)
where
\lambda>0
. So, let
X=\left(\begin{matrix}A&B\\
B^{\top}&D\end{matrix}\right)\in S(K_{2}\vee mK_{1})

be a rank two matrix with simple eigenvalues
-1
and
\lambda
, where
A\in S(K_{2})
and
D
is diagonal. Since
\rank X=2
and
m>2
, at least one diagonal entry of
D
is zero. Further,
B
is nowhere-zero and
\rank X=2
, and this in turn implies that
D=0
. Considering the spectral decomposition
X=\lambda{\bf x}{\bf x}^{\top}-{\bf w}{\bf w}^{\top}
where
{\bf x},{\bf w}
are orthogonal unit eigenvectors with eigenvalues
\lambda,-1
, we write

{\bf x}=\left(\begin{matrix}\sqrt{1-s^{2}}{\bf x}_{1}\\
s{\bf x}_{2}\end{matrix}\right)\text{ and }{\bf w}=\left(\begin{matrix}\sqrt{1-t^{2}}{\bf w}_{1}\\
t{\bf w}_{2}\end{matrix}\right)

where
0\leq s,t\leq 1
and
{\bf x}_{i},{\bf w}_{i}
are unit vectors with
{\bf x}_{1},{\bf w}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
{\bf x}_{2},{\bf w}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Now
D=\lambda s^{2}{\bf x}_{2}{\bf x}_{2}^{\top}-t^{2}{\bf w}_{2}{\bf w}_{2}^{\top}=0
, so
t=\sqrt{\lambda}s\neq 0
,
s\leq\lambda^{-1/2}
and
{\bf w}_{2}=c{\bf x}_{2}
for some
c\in\{-1,1\}
, so we have

{\bf w}=\left(\begin{matrix}\sqrt{1-\lambda s^{2}}{\bf w}_{1}\\
c\sqrt{\lambda}s{\bf x}_{2}\end{matrix}\right).

To see that
\mathbf{v}
is not generically realisable for
K_{2}\vee mK_{1}
, consider the set
\mathcal{Y}:=\{{\bf y}_{-1},{\bf y}_{1}\}
, where
{\bf y}_{k}:={\bf e}_{1}+k\sqrt{\lambda}{\bf e}_{m+2}
for
k\in\{-1,1\}
. Any orthogonal matrix
U
with
U^{\top}XU=D_{\{-1,0,\lambda\},\mathbf{v}}
has first and last columns equal to
a{\bf w}
and
b{\bf x}
, respectively, for some
a,b\in\{-1,1\}
. Removing the first two entries of the vector
U{\bf y}_{k}=a{\bf w}+bk\sqrt{\lambda}{\bf x}
leaves
(ac+bk)\sqrt{\lambda}s{\bf x}_{2}=\mathbf{0}_{m}
for
k=-abc
, so
U{\bf y}_{-abc}
is not nowhere-zero. This proves that
\mathbf{v}
is not generically realisable for
K_{2}\vee mK_{1}
.

To prove that
\mathbf{v}
is sane for
K_{2}\vee mK_{1}
, it suffices for each
\lambda>0
to construct a matrix in
S(K_{2}\vee mK_{1})
with a nowhere-zero orthonormal eigenbasis, and eigenvalues
\lambda
and
-1
with multiplicity
1
, and
0
with multiplicity
m
. To deal with the
\lambda\neq 1
case, consider the unit vectors
{\bf x}_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}{\bf 1}_{2}
and
{\bf x}_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}{\bf 1}_{m}
, and the orthonormal vectors

{\bf x}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\lambda}}\left(\begin{matrix}\sqrt{\lambda}{\bf x}_{1}\\
{\bf x}_{2}\end{matrix}\right),\quad{\bf w}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\lambda}}\left(\begin{matrix}-{\bf x}_{1}\\
\sqrt{\lambda}{\bf x}_{2}\end{matrix}\right).

The rank two matrix
X:=\lambda{\bf x}{\bf x}^{\top}-{\bf w}{\bf w}^{\top}
is then given by

X=\left(\begin{matrix}(\lambda-1){\bf x}_{1}{\bf x}_{1}^{\top}&\sqrt{\lambda}{\bf x}_{1}{\bf x}_{2}^{\top}\\
\sqrt{\lambda}{\bf x}_{2}{\bf x}_{1}^{\top}&0_{m}\end{matrix}\right)\in S(K_{2}\vee mK_{1}).

It is easy to find a nowhere-zero vector orthogonal to both
{\bf x}
and
{\bf w}
, so by Lemma
3.5
, the kernel of
X
has a nowhere-zero orthonormal basis. Appending
{\bf w}
and
{\bf x}
gives a nowhere-zero orthonormal eigenbasis for
X
, as required.

The final case, when
\lambda=1
, can be resolved by the matrix
X:=\left(\begin{matrix}A&B\\
B^{\top}&0_{m}\end{matrix}\right)
where
A=\tfrac{1}{20}\left(\begin{matrix}4&3\\
3&-4\end{matrix}\right)
and
B=\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4\sqrt{m}}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf 1}_{m}^{\top}\\
2\cdot{\bf 1}_{m}^{\top}\end{matrix}\right)
.
We can check that
X
has rank
2
, nonzero eigenvalues
1
and
-1
, and a nowhere-zero orthonormal eigenbasis, by straightforward calculation.

4.
Joins of unions of complete graphs

Proposition
4.1
together with the solution to the IEP-G for complete graphs, emphasises that the spectra of matrices in
S(K_{n})
are the least constrained. We exploit this fact to determine
q
of joins of unions of complete graphs, and derive some conditions for
q\neq 2
in a more general setting.

Proposition 4.1
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the complete graph
K_{n}
is generically realisable.

Proof.

This is trivial for
n=1
. Let
n\geq 2
and recall (Remark
2.11
) that any multiplicity list with sum
n
and at least two nonzero entries is spectrally arbitrary for
K_{n}
. Hence, if
D
is any
n\times n
diagonal matrix with
D\neq aI_{n}
,
a\in\mathbb{R}
, then there is an orthogonal matrix
U
so that
A:=UDU^{\top}\in S(K_{n})
. For any finite set
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
we can find an orthogonal
V
arbitrarily close to
I_{n}
so that
VU{\bf y}
is nowhere-zero for every
{\bf y}\in\mathcal{Y}
. Since
A\in S(K_{n})
, for
V
sufficiently close to
I_{n}
, we also have
VAV^{\top}\in S(K_{n})
. Hence, the multiplicity list of
D
is generically realisable for
K_{n}
.
∎

We now introduce some notation. Assume throughout this section that
k,\ell\geq 1
are positive integers. Let
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
denote a
k
-tuple of natural numbers.
We abbreviate a tuple such as
(m_{1},\dots,m_{s},a,\dots,a)
, with
a
appearing
t
times, as
(m_{1},\dots,m_{s},a^{t})
.
We define
K_{\mathbf{m}}:=\bigcup_{i\in[k]}K_{m_{i}}
,
and
|\mathbf{m}|:=\sum_{i\in[k]}m_{i}=|K_{\mathbf{m}}|
. Clearly,
k\leq|\mathbf{m}|
. Recall that
\iso(K_{\mathbf{m}})
denotes the set of isolated vertices of the graph
K_{\mathbf{m}}
, and let
\iota(\mathbf{m})=|\{i\in[k]\colon m_{i}=1\}|=|\iso(K_{\mathbf{m}})|
.
Note that

|\mathbf{m}|+\iota(\mathbf{m})\geq 2k
.
In particular, if
\iota(\mathbf{m})=0
, then
|\mathbf{m}|\geq 2k
.

Next, we wish to prove that the number of distinct eigenvalues of a join of two unions of complete graphs can only be equal to
2
or to
3
. To see this, we will use the following simple lemma, which follows from
[
4
, Theorem 3]
or
[
16
, Lemma 2.9]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
G
be a graph with at least one edge, and let
v\in V(G)
. Let
H
be the graph obtained from
G
by replacing
v
with a complete graph
K_{n}
and adding edges from every neighbour of
v
in
G
to every vertex of
K_{n}
. Then
q(H)\leq q(G)
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
\mathbf{m},\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
,
\mathbf{n},\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
, where
\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\geq\mathbf{n}
and
\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\geq\mathbf{m}
.
If
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
, then
q(K_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}})=2
.

Proof.

The graph
K_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}
may be obtained from
K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}}
by choosing a set of
k+\ell
vertices (one from each component of
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and one from each component of
K_{\mathbf{n}}
) and applying the procedure described in Lemma
4.2
successively to each one.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

If
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
and
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
, then

q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=\begin{cases}2&\text{if $K_{\mathbf{m}}$ and $K_{\mathbf{n}}$ have compatible multiplicity matrices,}\\
3&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Since
K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}}
has at least one edge,
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})\geq 2
. Moreover,
q(kK_{1}\vee\ell K_{1})=q(K_{k,l})\in\{2,3\}
, by
[
3
, Corollary 6.5]
. Applying Lemma
4.2
a total of
k+\ell
times, we obtain

2\leq q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})\leq q(kK_{1}\vee\ell K_{1})\leq 3
,
so
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})\in\{2,3\}
.

By Proposition
4.1
,
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
are generically realisable (and, hence, they are also sane). By Corollary
2.15
,
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
if and only if
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
have compatible multiplicity matrices.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

Corollary
4.3
can also be deduced easily from Corollary
4.4
by considering multiplicity matrices.
Given
\mathbf{m},\mathbf{m}^{\prime},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{n}^{\prime}
as in Corollary
4.3
, suppose

V\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times k}
and
W\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times\ell}
are compatible multiplicity matrices for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
, respectively. Then
V^{\prime}:=V+{\bf e}_{1}(\mathbf{m}^{\prime}-\mathbf{m})^{\top}
and
W^{\prime}:=W+{\bf e}_{1}(\mathbf{n}^{\prime}-\mathbf{n})^{\top}
are multiplicity matrices for
K_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}
, respectively, and
\widetilde{V}=\widetilde{V^{\prime}}
and
\widetilde{W}=\widetilde{W^{\prime}}
, so
V^{\prime}
and
W^{\prime}
are compatible. Hence, if
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
, then
q(K_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}})=2
.

The following easy observations about multiplicity matrices are worth recording at this point.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
k,r\in\mathbb{N}
with
r\geq 3
.

(a)

For
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
, a matrix
V\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times k}
is a multiplicity matrix for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
if and only if, for every
i\in[k]
, we have
{\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}V{\bf e}_{i}=m_{i}
, and if
m_{i}\geq 2
, then
V{\bf e}_{i}
has at least two nonzero entries.

(b)

If
V
is a multiplicity matrix for a graph
G=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}G_{i}
, where each
G_{i}
is connected, then
V
is a multiplicity matrix for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
where
\mathbf{m}:=(|G_{1}|,\dots,|G_{k}|)
.

(c)

If
V
and
W
are compatible matrices, then
\widetilde{V}
and
\widetilde{W}
have at least one nonzero entry in each column.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
G=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}G_{i}
,
H=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell}H_{j}
,
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})
and
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell})
, where
G_{i}
and
H_{j}
are connected graphs with
|G_{i}|=m_{i}
and
|H_{j}|=n_{j}
. If
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=3
, then
q(G\vee H)\geq 3
.

Proof.

Since
G\vee H
has at least one edge,
q(G\vee H)\geq 2
. If
q(G\vee H)=2
, then by Theorem
2.5
, there is a pair
V,W
of compatible multiplicity matrices for
G
and
H
. By Proposition
4.6
, these are multiplicity matrices for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
, respectively. Hence, by Corollary
4.4
,
we have
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
, a contradiction.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

In contrast with complete graphs, paths
P_{n}
are realisable by matrices
A\in S(P_{n})
with the most constrained spectrum. We will explore this in our follow up paper
[
15
]
, where it will be shown that
q(\bigcup_{i\in[k]}P_{m_{i}}\vee\bigcup_{j\in[\ell]}P_{n_{j}})=2
implies
q(G\vee H)=2
,
where
G
and
H
are as in Corollary
4.7
.

The following straightforward lemma is used several times in our arguments below. The proof is left to the reader.

Lemma 4.9
.

If
s,t\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
, then there exists
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
with
|\mathbf{b}|=t
and
\mathbf{b}\leq\mathbf{c}
if and only if
s\leq t\leq|\mathbf{c}|
.

Now that we have established the underlying framework, we are left with the combinatorial question of deciding under what conditions on
\mathbf{m}
and
\mathbf{n}
compatible multiplicity matrices for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
exist. Our first result in this direction gives a sufficient condition.

Proposition 4.10
.

For
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
,
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
, the following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

k+\ell\leq\min\{|\mathbf{m}|+\iota(\mathbf{m}),|\mathbf{n}|+\iota(\mathbf{n})\}
.

(b)

K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
have a compatible pair of multiplicity matrices in
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3\times k}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3\times\ell}
, respectively;

Moreover, if either
(a)
or
(b)
is valid, then
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
.

Proof.

Suppose
(b)
holds and
V\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3\times k}
,
W\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3\times\ell}
are compatible multiplicity matrices for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
,
K_{\mathbf{n}}
, respectively. Let
p:=\widetilde{V}{\bf 1}_{k}=\widetilde{W}{\bf 1}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\widetilde{V}^{\top}\widetilde{W}>0
, every entry of the row vectors
\widetilde{V}
and
\widetilde{W}
is at least
1
, so
p\geq\max\{k,\ell\}
. Every column of
V
corresponds to a connected component
K_{m_{i}}
of
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and is of the form
(a_{i},b_{i},c_{i})^{\top}
where
b_{i}\geq 1
and
a_{i}+b_{i}+c_{i}=m_{i}
. If
m_{i}=1
for some
i
, then the corresponding column is necesarily equal to
(0,1,0)^{\top}
. For columns with
m_{i}>1
we have
a_{i}+c_{i}\geq 1
by Proposition
4.6
, so
1\leq b_{i}\leq m_{i}-1
. Summing up, we obtain

p=\sum_{i\in[k]}b_{i}\leq\iota(\mathbf{m})+\sum_{i:m_{i}>1}(m_{i}-1)=|\mathbf{m}|+\iota(\mathbf{m})-k
,
and similarly
p\leq|\mathbf{n}|+\iota(\mathbf{n})-\ell
. Since
\max\{k,\ell\}\leq p
, we get
(a)
.

Now suppose the inequality
(a)
holds, and without loss of generality assume
k\leq\ell
.
Then
k\leq\ell\leq|\mathbf{m}|-k+\iota(\mathbf{m})=\sum_{i\in[k]}\max\{1,m_{i}-1\}
, and there exist
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
with
t_{i}\leq\max\{1,m_{i}-1\}
for
i\in[k]
and
\sum_{i\in[k]}t_{i}=\ell
, by Lemma
4.9
.
Now

V:=\left(\begin{matrix}m_{1}-t_{1}&\dots&m_{k}-t_{k}\\
t_{1}&\dots&t_{k}\\
0&\dots&0\end{matrix}\right)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3\times k}\text{ and }W:=\left(\begin{matrix}n_{1}-1&\dots&n_{\ell}-1\\
1&\dots&1\\
0&\dots&0\end{matrix}\right)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3\times\ell}

are compatible multiplicity matrices for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
, respectively. Hence
(b)
holds.

The final claim is immediate from Corollary
4.4
.
∎

The next result follows from
[
3
, Theorem 4.4]
. We present an alternative proof using our methods.

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
,
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
. If
|\mathbf{m}|<\ell
, then

q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=3
.

Proof.

Otherwise, by Corollary
4.4
, there exist compatible multiplicity matrices
V\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times k}
and
W\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times\ell}
for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
, respectively. Then
\widetilde{V}^{\top}\widetilde{W}>0
, which implies that
\widetilde{W}
has no zero columns, so
{\bf 1}_{r-2}^{\top}\widetilde{W}\geq{\bf 1}_{\ell}^{\top}
. Since
\widetilde{V}{\bf 1}_{k}=\widetilde{W}{\bf 1}_{\ell}
, it follows that

\ell={\bf 1}_{\ell}^{\top}{\bf 1}_{\ell}\leq{\bf 1}_{r-2}^{\top}\widetilde{W}{\bf 1}_{\ell}={\bf 1}_{r-2}^{\top}\widetilde{V}{\bf 1}_{k}\leq{\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}V{\bf 1}_{k}=|K_{\mathbf{m}}|=|\mathbf{m}|
,
a contradiction.
∎

Note that there is a gap between the sufficient conditions for
q=2
given in Proposition
4.10
and the necessary conditions for
q=2
that follow from Proposition
4.11
. It turns out that isolated vertices play an important role in the complete solution, and we consider different cases that can occur below.

4.1.
No isolated vertices

First we examine the case when at least one of
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
has no isolated vertices. In particular, when neither one of these graphs has an isolated vertex we will see in Proposition
4.14
that the sufficient condition in Proposition
4.11
for
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})
to be 3 is also necessary.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
k\leq\ell
,
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
and
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
with
\iota(\mathbf{m})=0
. If
|\mathbf{n}|=2k
, then
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
.

Proof.

By permuting the entries of
\mathbf{n}
if necessary, we may assume that
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{t},1^{\iota(\mathbf{n})})
, where
t:=\ell-\iota(\mathbf{n})
and
n_{i}\geq 2
for
i\in[t]
. Write
\iota(\mathbf{n})=2a+b
for
b\in\{0,1\}
. From
2k=|\mathbf{n}|=\iota(\mathbf{n})+\sum_{i\in[t]}n_{i}
, we get

k=a+\frac{1}{2}(b+\sum_{i\in[t]}n_{i})\geq a+\frac{1}{2}b+t
, and hence
k\geq a+b+t
.
Denote
\mathbf{R}:=(r_{1},r_{2})=(k-t-a-b,k-t-a)
,
\mathbf{C}:=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{t})=(n_{1}-2,\ldots,n_{t}-2)
, and note that
r_{1}+r_{2}=\sum_{j\in[t]}c_{j}
. Hence, by
[
7
, Theorem 2.1.2]
, there exists a matrix
Y=[y_{ij}]\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2\times t}
with row sums equal to
\mathbf{R}
and column sums equal to
\mathbf{C}
. Since
\iota(\mathbf{m})=0
, we have
m_{i}\geq 2
for
i\in[k]
. Define compatible matrices
V\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{4\times k}
and
W\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{4\times\ell}
as follows:

V:=\left(\begin{matrix}m_{1}-2&\dots&m_{k}-2\\
1&\dots&1\\
1&\dots&1\\
0&\dots&0\end{matrix}\right),\quad W:=\left(\begin{matrix}0&\dots&0&0&0\\
y_{11}+1&\dots&y_{1t}+1&{\bf 1}_{a+b}^{\top}&0\\
y_{21}+1&\dots&y_{2t}+1&0&{\bf 1}_{a}^{\top}\\
0&\dots&0&0&0\end{matrix}\right).

By Corollary
4.6
,
V
and
W
are multiplicity matrices for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
, respectively, so
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
by Corollary
4.4
.
∎

Proposition 4.13
.

Let
k\leq\ell
,
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
and
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
with
\iota(\mathbf{m})=0
.
If
2k\leq\ell\leq|\mathbf{m}|
or
\ell\leq 2k\leq|\mathbf{n}|
, then
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
2k\leq\ell\leq|\mathbf{m}|
. Then
k\leq\ell-k\leq|\mathbf{m}|-k
, and since
\iota(\mathbf{m})=0
, we have
m_{i}\geq 2
for every
i\in[k]
. By Lemma
4.9
, there exist
r_{i}\in[m_{i}-1]
so that
\ell-k=\sum_{i\in[k]}r_{i}
. The matrices

{\small\left(\begin{matrix}m_{1}-r_{1}-1&\dots&m_{k}-r_{k}-1\\
1&\dots&1\\
r_{1}&\dots&r_{k}\\
0&\dots&0\end{matrix}\right)}\text{\,and\,}{\small\left(\begin{matrix}n_{1}-1&\dots&n_{k}-1&n_{k+1}-1&\dots&n_{\ell}-1\\
1&\dots&1&0&\dots&0\\
0&\dots&0&1&\dots&1\\
0&\dots&0&0&\dots&0\end{matrix}\right)}

are compatible multiplicity matrices for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
, respectively, hence
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
by Corollary
4.4
.

Suppose instead that
\ell\leq 2k\leq|\mathbf{n}|
. By Lemma
4.9
, there is a vector
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
with
\mathbf{p}\leq\mathbf{n}
and
|\mathbf{p}|=2k
.
By Lemma
4.12
we have
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{p}})=2

and it follows by Corollary
4.3
that
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
.
∎

Assuming
\iota(\mathbf{m})=\iota(\mathbf{n})=0
, we get the first complete resolution of
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})
.

Proposition 4.14
.

Let
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
,
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
. If
\iota(\mathbf{m})=\iota(\mathbf{n})=0
, then

q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=\begin{cases}2&\text{if $\max\{k,\ell\}\leq\min\{|\mathbf{m}|,|\mathbf{n}|\}$},\\
3&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

By symmetry we may assume without loss of generality that
k\leq\ell
. Under this assumption, we must prove that
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
if and only if
\ell\leq|\mathbf{m}|
.
If
|\mathbf{m}|<\ell
, then
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=3
by Proposition
4.11
, and
if
2k\leq\ell\leq|\mathbf{m}|
, then
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
by Proposition
4.13
.

The only remaining case is when
\ell\leq|\mathbf{m}|
and

k\leq\ell<2k
. Then
1\leq 2k-\ell\leq k\leq\ell
and
\iota(\mathbf{m})=\iota(\mathbf{n})=0
implies that
m_{i},n_{j}\geq 2
for all
i\in[k]
and
j\in[\ell]
. We define matrices
V\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{4\times k}
and
W\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{4\times\ell}
as follows:

V:=\left(\begin{matrix}m_{1}-2&\dots&m_{k}-2\\
1&\dots&1\\
1&\dots&1\\
0&\dots&0\end{matrix}\right)

and

W:=\small\left(\begin{matrix}n_{1}-2&\dots&n_{2k-\ell}-2&n_{2k-\ell+1}-1&\dots&n_{k}-1&n_{k+1}-1&\dots&n_{\ell}-1\\
1&\dots&1&1&\dots&1&0&\dots&0\\
1&\dots&1&0&\dots&0&1&\dots&1\\
0&\dots&0&0&\dots&0&0&\dots&0\end{matrix}\right).

Since
V,W
are compatible multiplicity matrices for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
, respectively, we conclude that
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
by Corollary
4.4
.
∎

4.2.
Unions of complete graphs with isolated vertices

When both
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
have isolated vertices, we have the following characterisation.

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
,
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
. If
\iota(\mathbf{m})>0
and
\iota(\mathbf{n})>0
, then the following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
;

(b)

K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
have a compatible pair of multiplicity matrices;

(c)

K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
have a compatible pair of multiplicity matrices in
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3\times k}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3\times\ell}
, respectively;

(d)

k+\ell\leq\min\{|\mathbf{m}|+\iota(\mathbf{m}),|\mathbf{n}|+\iota(\mathbf{n})\}
.

Proof.

(a)
and
(b)
are equivalent by Corollary
4.4
.
(c)
and
(d)
are equivalent by Proposition
4.10
and plainly imply
(a)
and
(b)
.

Suppose now
(b)
holds, and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k}
and
W\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times\ell}
are compatible multiplicity matrices for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
, respectively, for some
r\geq 3
. Since
\iota(\mathbf{m})>0
and
\iota(\mathbf{n})>0
, the condition
\widetilde{V}^{\top}\widetilde{W}>0
implies that for some
i\in[r-2]
,
i
th rows of both
\widetilde{V}
and
\widetilde{W}
are nowhere-zero. Define
V^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3\times k}
and
W^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3\times\ell}
as follows:

V^{\prime}:=\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}V-{\bf e}_{i}^{\top}\widetilde{V}\\
{\bf e}_{i}^{\top}\widetilde{V}\vphantom{\displaystyle\int}\\
\mathbf{0}_{k}^{\top}\end{matrix}\right),\quad W^{\prime}:=\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}W-{\bf e}_{i}^{\top}\widetilde{W}\\
{\bf e}_{i}^{\top}\widetilde{W}\vphantom{\displaystyle\int}\\
\mathbf{0}_{\ell}^{\top}\end{matrix}\right).

Note that
V^{\prime}
has the same column sums as
V
, and if the
j
th column of
V
has more than one non-zero entry, then the
j
th column of
V^{\prime}
has (precisely) two non-zero entries, so by Proposition
4.6
,
\widetilde{V^{\prime}}
is a multiplicity matrix for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
; similarly,
W^{\prime}
is a multiplicity matrix for
K_{\mathbf{n}}
. Since
\widetilde{V^{\prime}}={\bf e}_{i}^{\top}\widetilde{V}
and
\widetilde{W^{\prime}}={\bf e}_{i}^{\top}\widetilde{W}
, it follows that
V^{\prime}
and
W^{\prime}
are compatible multiplicity matrices for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
, so
(b)
implies
(c)
.
∎

Suppose now
k\leq\ell
and
\iota(\mathbf{n})=0
. Under this assumption we have
|\mathbf{n}|\geq 2\ell\geq k+\ell
. Hence the sufficient condition
(a)
in Proposition
4.10
for
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
is equivalent to
k+\ell\leq|\mathbf{m}|+\iota(\mathbf{m})
. We will show that this condition is also necessary for
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
in the case
\iota(\mathbf{m})>0
.

Lemma 4.16
.

If
r\geq 3
and
V\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times k}
is a multiplicity matrix for a graph
G
with
k
connected components and
\mathbf{m}={\bf 1}_{r}^{\top}V
is the vector of connected component sizes of
G
, then for any
s\in[r-2]
, we have
{\bf e}_{s}^{\top}\widetilde{V}{\bf 1}_{k}\leq\iota(\mathbf{m})+|\mathbf{m}|-k
.

Proof.

We have

{\bf e}_{s}^{\top}\widetilde{V}{\bf 1}_{k}=\sum_{i:m_{i}=1}{\bf e}_{s}^{\top}\widetilde{V}{\bf e}_{i}+\sum_{i:m_{i}>1}{\bf e}_{s}^{\top}\widetilde{V}{\bf e}_{i}\leq\iota(\mathbf{m})+\sum_{i:m_{i}>1}(m_{i}-1)=\iota(\mathbf{m})+|\mathbf{m}|-k.\qed

Proposition 4.17
.

Let
k\leq\ell
,
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
and
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
. If
\iota(\mathbf{m})>0
and
\iota(\mathbf{n})=0
, then

q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=\begin{cases}2&\text{if $k+\ell\leq|\mathbf{m}|+\iota(\mathbf{m})$},\\
3&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Since
k\leq\ell
and
\iota(\mathbf{n})=0
, we have
|\mathbf{n}|\geq 2\ell\geq k+\ell
and thus the sufficiency of the condition
k+\ell\leq|\mathbf{m}|+\iota(\mathbf{m})
follows by Proposition
4.10
.

Conversely, if
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
, then by Corollary
4.4
there exist compatible multiplicity matrices
V\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times k}
and
W\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times\ell}
for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
, respectively. Since
\iota(\mathbf{m})>0
, some column of
\widetilde{V}
is equal to
{\bf e}_{s}
, where
s\in[r-2]
. Since
\widetilde{V}^{\top}\widetilde{W}>0
, this implies that
{\bf e}_{s}^{\top}\widetilde{W}>0
, so
{\bf e}_{s}^{\top}\widetilde{W}\geq{\bf 1}_{\ell}^{\top}
. By Lemma
4.16
,

\ell={\bf 1}_{\ell}^{\top}{\bf 1}_{\ell}\leq{\bf e}_{s}^{\top}\widetilde{W}{\bf 1}_{\ell}={\bf e}_{s}^{\top}\widetilde{V}{\bf 1}_{k}\leq\iota(\mathbf{m})+|\mathbf{m}|-k,

so
k+\ell\leq\iota(\mathbf{m})+|\mathbf{m}|
.
∎

Proposition 4.18
.

Let
k\leq\ell
,
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
and
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
. If
\iota(\mathbf{m})=0
and
\iota(\mathbf{n})>0
,
then

q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=\begin{cases}2&\text{if $2k\leq\ell\leq|\mathbf{m}|$, or $\ell\leq 2k\leq|\mathbf{n}|$, or $k+\ell\leq|\mathbf{m}|$},\\
3&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

If
2k\leq\ell\leq|\mathbf{m}|
or
\ell\leq 2k\leq|\mathbf{n}|
or
k+\ell\leq|\mathbf{m}|
, then
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
by Propositions
4.10
and
4.13
.
If
\ell>|\mathbf{m}|
, then
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=3
by Proposition
4.11
.

If none of the above relations holds, then in particular, we have
|\mathbf{n}|<2k
and
|\mathbf{m}|<k+\ell
.
If
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
, then by Corollary
4.4
there exist
r\geq 3
and compatible multiplicity matrices
V\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times k}
and
W\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times\ell}
for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
, respectively. Since
\iota(\mathbf{n})>0
, we can assume without loss of generality that
n_{\ell}=1
and that
\widetilde{W}{\bf e}_{\ell}={\bf e}_{1}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r-2}
. The condition
\widetilde{V}^{\top}\widetilde{W}>0
now implies that
{\bf e}_{1}^{\top}\widetilde{V}\geq{\bf 1}_{k}^{\top}
.
Lemma
4.16
together with
\iota(\mathbf{m})=0
gives

{\bf e}_{1}^{\top}\widetilde{W}{\bf 1}_{\ell}={\bf e}_{1}^{\top}\widetilde{V}{\bf 1}_{k}\leq|\mathbf{m}|-k<\ell
.
If
{\bf e}_{1}^{\top}\widetilde{W}\geq{\bf 1}_{\ell}^{\top}
, then we obtain an immediate contradiction. So the first row of
\widetilde{W}
contains at least one zero entry. Hence, each of the
k
columns of
\widetilde{V}
necessarily has at least one nonzero entry in a row other than row
1
, in addition to the nonzero entry in row
1
, to satisfy
\widetilde{V}^{\top}\widetilde{W}>0
.
So
2k\leq{\bf 1}_{r-2}^{\top}\widetilde{V}{\bf 1}_{k}={\bf 1}_{r-2}^{\top}\widetilde{W}{\bf 1}_{\ell}\leq{\bf 1}_{r}W{\bf 1}_{\ell}=|\mathbf{n}|
, a contradiction. This proves
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=3
.
∎

We remark that by the constructions in Subsections
4.1
and
4.2
, whenever
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
, there exist compatible multiplicity matrices for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
with at most four rows.

4.3.
Complete result

We summarise the different cases that we have considered in a theorem that completely resolves
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})
, and follows immediately from Propositions
4.14
,
4.15
,
4.17
and
4.18
.

Theorem 4.19
.

Let
k\leq\ell
,
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
and
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
. We have
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
if and only if one of the following is true:

(a)

\iota(\mathbf{m})=0
,
\iota(\mathbf{n})=0
and
\ell\leq|\mathbf{m}|
,

(b)

\iota(\mathbf{m})>0
and
k+\ell\leq|\mathbf{m}|+\iota(\mathbf{m})
, or

(c)

\iota(\mathbf{m})=0
,
\iota(\mathbf{n})>0
and either
k+\ell\leq|\mathbf{m}|
, or
2k\leq\ell\leq|\mathbf{m}|
, or
\ell\leq 2k\leq|\mathbf{n}|
.

Otherwise,
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=3
.

Theorem
1.1
is a straightforward consequence of Theorem
4.19

and Corollary
4.7
.
Note that Theorem
4.19
allows us to observe properties of
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})
. For example,
for any two fixed numbers of connected components
k
and
\ell
, we can always choose
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
and
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
, with
|\mathbf{m}|
and
|\mathbf{n}|
sufficiently large to achieve
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
.

4.4.
Multiplicities

Let
X
be an
n\times n
orthogonal symmetric matrix, and let
i_{+}(X)
and
i_{-}(X)
denote the multiplicities of
1
and
-1
as eigenvalues of
X
, respectively. The study of
i_{+}(X)
can be motivated by the fact that the map
X\mapsto P(X)=\frac{1}{2}(I+X)
is a bijection between the
n\times n
orthogonal symmetric matrices and the orthogonal projections onto subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\rank P(X)=i_{+}(X)
. Moreover, for any graph
G
, we have
X\in S(G)
if and only if
P(X)\in S(G)
.

Let
G
and
H
be graphs such that
q(G\vee H)=2
. Let
X\in S(G\vee H)
be an orthogonal matrix with the corresponding compatible multiplicity matrices
V,W
guaranteed by Theorem
2.5
. Let
\mu={\bf 1}_{r-2}^{\top}\widetilde{V}{\bf 1}_{k}={\bf 1}_{r-2}^{\top}\widetilde{W}{\bf 1}_{\ell}
.
By the proof of Theorem
2.5
, we have
i_{+}(X)\geq\mu
and
n-i_{+}(X)=i_{-}(X)\geq\mu
, implying
\mu\leq i_{+}(X)\leq n-\mu
.

To consider the special case
G=K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
H=K_{\mathbf{n}}
, let
\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})
denote the minimum value of
{\bf 1}_{r-2}^{\top}\widetilde{V}{\bf 1}_{k}={\bf 1}_{r-2}^{\top}\widetilde{W}{\bf 1}_{\ell}
over all compatible multiplicity matrices
V
and
W
for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
. By the previous paragraph, we have
\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})\leq i_{+}(X)\leq|\mathbf{m}|+|\mathbf{n}|-\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})
for any orthogonal
X\in S(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})
. Moreover, if
V_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times k}
and
W_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times\ell}
are compatible multiplicity matrices for
K_{\mathbf{m}}
and
K_{\mathbf{n}}
, then so are any matrices
V\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times k}
and
W\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r\times\ell}
satisfying
\widetilde{V}=\widetilde{V_{0}}
,
\widetilde{W}=\widetilde{W_{0}}
,

({\bf e}_{1}+{\bf e}_{r})^{\top}V=({\bf e}_{1}+{\bf e}_{r})^{\top}V_{0}
and
({\bf e}_{1}+{\bf e}_{r})^{\top}W=({\bf e}_{1}+{\bf e}_{r})^{\top}W_{0}
. By the construction of Theorem
2.14
, this implies that for
every
integer
i
with
\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})\leq i\leq|\mathbf{m}|+|\mathbf{n}|-\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})
, we have
i=i_{+}(X)
for some orthogonal matrix
X\in S(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})
.

Let
k\leq\ell
,
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
and
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
, then:

(2)

\ell\leq\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})\leq\max\{\ell,2k\}.

The first inequality is clear, and the second one follows by examining the constructions of multiplicity matrices in the proof of Proposition
4.10
, and in the proofs in Subsections
4.1
and
4.2
.

Lemma 4.20
.

Let
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\leq\ell
,
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
,
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
. If
k+\ell>|\mathbf{m}|+\iota(\mathbf{m})
, then
\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})\geq 2k
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})<2k
and there exist
V,W
as above with

{\bf 1}_{r-2}^{\top}\widetilde{V}{\bf 1}_{k}<2k
. Some column of
\widetilde{V}
then has sum less than
2
, so that column has exactly one non-zero entry, say in row
s
. Since
\widetilde{V}^{\top}\widetilde{W}>0
, this implies that row
s
of
\widetilde{W}
is nowhere-zero. By Lemma
4.16
and the compatibility of
V
and
W
, we have
|\mathbf{m}|+\iota(\mathbf{m})-k\geq{\bf e}_{s}^{\top}\widetilde{V}{\bf 1}_{k}={\bf e}_{s}^{\top}\widetilde{W}{\bf 1}_{\ell}\geq\ell
, a contradiction. So
\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})\geq 2k
.
∎

In the next theorem we show that
\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})
always reaches one of the two upper bounds given in (
2
).

Theorem 4.21
.

Let
k\leq\ell
,
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
and
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
. If
q(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})=2
, then

\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})=\begin{cases}2k&\text{if }|\mathbf{m}|+\iota(\mathbf{m})-k<\ell<2k,\\
\ell&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Consequently, there exists a matrix
A\in S(K_{\mathbf{m}}\vee K_{\mathbf{n}})
with
q(A)=2
whose eigenvalues have multiplicities
\{t,|\mathbf{m}|+|\mathbf{n}|-t\}
if and only if
\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})\leq t\leq|\mathbf{m}|+|\mathbf{n}|-\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})
.

Proof.

If
|\mathbf{m}|+\iota(\mathbf{m})-k<\ell<2k
, then we have
2k\leq\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})\leq\max\{\ell,2k\}=2k
by Lemma
4.20
and (
2
), so
\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})=2k
.
On the other hand, if
\ell\geq 2k
, then
\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})=\ell
by (
2
), and if
|\mathbf{m}|+\iota(\mathbf{m})-k\geq\ell
, then the matrix
W
constructed in the the proof of Proposition
4.10
shows that
\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})\leq\ell
, so
\mu(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})=\ell
by (
2
).
The final claim regarding multiplicities follows immediately from the discussion before Lemma
4.20
.
∎

Note that both cases in Theorem
4.21
occur, for example when
k=2
and
\ell=3
we have
\mu((K_{3}\cup K_{2})\vee(K_{2}\cup 2K_{1}))=\ell
and
\mu(2K_{2}\vee(K_{2}\cup 2K_{1}))=2k
.

Example 4.22
.

For

Considering conditions
(a)
,

and

Throughout this section we were noting the effect that the number of connected components and isolated points have on

Example 4.23
.

Let

q(aK_{s}\vee bK_{1})=\begin{cases}2&\text{if $s=2\text{ and }b\in\{a,2a\},\text{ or }s\neq 2\text{ and }a\leq b\leq sa$},\\

(b)

\displaystyle a+b\leq as,\text{ or }2a\leq b\leq as,\text{ or }b=2a.

If
