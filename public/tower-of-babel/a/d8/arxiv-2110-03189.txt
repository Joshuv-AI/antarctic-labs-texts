Pointwise Bounds for Distribution Estimation under Communication Constraints
Wei-Ning Chen, Peter Kairouz, Ayfer Özgür

Abstract
We consider the problem of estimating a d d -dimensional discrete distribution from its samples observed under a b b -bit communication constraint. In contrast to most previous results that largely focus on the global minimax error, we study the local behavior of the estimation error and provide pointwise bounds that depend on the target distribution p p . In particular, we show that the ℓ 2 \ell_{2} error decays with O ⁡ ( max ⁡ ( ‖ p ‖ 1 / 2 n ​ 2 b , 1 n ) ) O\left(\max\left(\frac{\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{1/2}}{n2^{b}},\frac{1}{n}\right)\right) when n n is sufficiently large, hence it is governed by the half-norm of p p instead of the ambient dimension d d . For the achievability result, we propose a two-round sequentially interactive estimation scheme that achieves this error rate uniformly over all p p . This two-round scheme extends to ℓ q \ell_{q} loss with q ≥ 1 q\geq 1 , and hence gives pointwise upper bounds on ℓ q \ell_{q} error. We also develop a new local minimax lower bound with (almost) matching ℓ 2 \ell_{2} error, showing that any interactive scheme must admit a Ω ⁡ ( ‖ p ‖ ( 1 + δ ) / 2 n ​ 2 b ) \Omega\left(\frac{\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{{(1+\delta)}/{2}}}{n2^{b}}\right) ℓ 2 \ell_{2} error for any δ > 0 \delta>0 . Our upper and lower bounds together indicate that the 𝖧 1 / 2 ​ ( p ) ≜ log ⁡ ( ‖ p ‖ 1 / 2 ) \mathsf{H}_{1/2}(p)\triangleq\log(\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{{1}/{2}}) bits of communication is both sufficient and necessary to achieve the optimal (centralized) performance, where 𝖧 1 / 2 ​ ( p ) \mathsf{H}_{{1}/{2}}(p) is the Rényi entropy of order 2 2 . Therefore, under the ℓ 2 \ell_{2} loss, the correct measure of the local communication complexity at p p is its Rényi entropy.

Introduction

Learning a distribution from its samples has been a fundamental task in unsupervised learning dating back to the late nineteenth century
[
1
]
. This task, especially under distributed settings, has gained growing popularity in the recent years as data is increasingly generated “at the edge” by countless sensors, smartphones, and other devices. When data is distributed across multiple devices, communication cost and bandwidth often become a bottleneck hampering the training of high-accuracy machine learning models
[
2
]
. This is even more so for federated learning and analytics type settings
[
3
]
which rely on wireless mobile links for communication.

To resolve this issue, several communication-efficient distribution learning schemes have been recently proposed and studied in the literature (see Section
1.2
for a thorough discussion). On the positive side, the state-of-the-art schemes are known to be worst-case (minimax) optimal as they have been shown to achieve the information-theoretic lower bounds on the global minimax error
[

O(\frac{d}{n2^{b}})
under a
b

d

linearly
in

d
on the estimation accuracy. This is true even if we allow for interaction between clients
[

A recent work
[

has moved a step forward from the “global minimax regime” by restricting the target distribution

\ell_{2}

However, both of these schemes and their performance guarantees are limited to the

s
-sparse. Moreover, these schemes are essentially still worst-case schemes as they are designed to be minimax optimal, albeit over a smaller class of distributions, i.e.
s
-sparse.

In this paper, we argue that all these results can be overly pessimistic, as worst-case notions of complexity and schemes designed to optimize these worst-case notions can be too conservative. Instead, we seek a measure of local complexity that captures the hardness of estimating a specific instance
p
. Ideally, we want a scheme that adapts to the hardness of the problem instead of being tuned to the worst-case scenario; that is, a scheme achieving smaller error when
p
is “simpler.”

Our techniques
Our proposed two-round interactive scheme is based on a local refinement approach, where in the first round, a standard (global) minimax optimal estimation scheme is applied to localize
p
. In the second round, we use additional
\Theta(n)
samples (clients), together with the information obtained from the previous round, to locally refine the estimate. The localization-refinement procedure enables us to tune the encoders (in the second round) to the target distribution
p
and hence attain the optimal pointwise convergence rate uniformly.

On the other hand, our lower bound is based on the quantized Fisher information framework introduced in
[
12
]
. However, in order to obtain a local bound around
p
, we develop a new approach that first finds the best parametric sub-model containing
p
, and then upper bounds its Fisher information in a neighborhood around
p
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first impossibility result that allows to capture the local complexity of high dimensional estimation under information constraints and can be of independent interest, e.g. to derive pointwise lower bounds for other estimation models under general information constraints.

1.1
Notation and Setup

The general distributed statistical task we consider in this paper can be formulated as follows. Each one of the
n
clients has local data
X_{i}\sim p
, where
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}
and

\mathcal{P}_{d}\triangleq\left\{p\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\,\middle|\,\sum_{j}p_{j}=1\right\}

is the collection of all
d
-dimensional discrete distributions. The
i
-th client then sends a message
Y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
to the server, who upon receiving
Y^{n}
aims to estimate the unknown distribution
p
.

At client
i
, the message
Y_{i}
is generated via a
sequentially interactive
protocol; that is, samples are
communicated sequentially by broadcasting the communication to all nodes in the system including the server. Therefore, the encoding function
W_{i}
of the
i
-th client can depend on all previous messages
Y_{1},...,Y_{i-1}
. Formally, it can be written as a randomized mapping (possibly using shared randomness across participating clients and the server) of the form
W_{i}(\cdot|X_{i},Y^{i-1})
, and the
b
-bit communication constraint restricts
\left\lvert\mathcal{Y}\right\rvert\leq 2^{b}
. As a special case, when
W_{i}
depends only on
X_{i}
and is independent of the other messages
Y^{i-1}
for all
i
(i.e.
W_{i}(\cdot|X_{i},Y^{i-1})=W_{i}(\cdot|X_{i})
), we say the corresponding protocol is
non-interactive
.
Finally, we call the tuple
\left(W^{n},\hat{p}(Y^{n})\right)
an estimation scheme, where
\hat{p}\left(Y^{n}\right)
is an estimator of
p
. We use
\Pi_{\mathsf{seq}}
and
\Pi_{\mathsf{ind}}
to denote the collections of all sequentially interactive and non-interactive schemes respectively.

Our goal here is to design a scheme
\left(W^{n},\hat{p}\left(Y^{n}\right)\right)
to minimize the
\ell_{2}
(or
\ell_{1}
) estimation error:

r\left(\ell_{2},p,\left(W^{n},\hat{p}\right)\right)\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert p-\hat{p}\left(Y^{n}\right)\right\rVert^{2}_{2}]
,
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}
, as well as characterizing the best error achievable by any scheme in
\Pi_{\mathsf{seq}}
. We note that while our impossibility bounds hold for any scheme in
\Pi_{\mathsf{seq}}
, the particular scheme we propose uses only one round of interaction. For
\ell_{1}
error, we replace
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert^{2}_{2}
in the above expectations with
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1}
. In this work, we mainly focus on the regime
1\ll d\ll n
, aiming to characterize the statistical convergence rates when
n
is sufficiently large.

The first round of our two-round estimation scheme (the localization phase) is built upon the global minimax optimal scheme
[
4
]
, in which symbols are partitioned into

(2^{b}-1)
, and each of the first

(n/2)/(d/(2^{b}-1))
clients). The client reports its observation only if it is in its assigned subset by using
b

This first round allows us to obtain a coarse estimate
\hat{p}
with error
\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert p-\hat{p}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}]\asymp\sum_{j}p_{j}/n2^{b}=O(d/n2^{b})
. Then in the second round (the refinement phase), we locally refine the estimate by adaptively assigning more “resources” (i.e. samples), according to
\hat{p}
obtained from the first round, to the more difficult symbols (i.e.
j\in[d]
with larger error
\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{p}_{j}-p_{j})^{2}\right]

\ell_{2}
error, the number of samples assigned to symbol

\sqrt{p_{j}}
.

It turns out that the local refinement step can effectively mitigate the estimation error, enabling us to move from a worst-case bound to a pointwise bound.

Round 1 (localization):

In the first round, the first

clients collaboratively produce a coarse estimate

for the target distribution

Upon obtaining

To design the encoding functions of the remaining

5
Analysis of the
\ell_{2}
error (proof of Theorem
2.1
)

In this section, we analyze the estimation errors of the above two-round localization-refinement scheme and prove Theorem
2.1
. Before entering the main proof, we give the following lemma that controls the estimation error of between
\hat{p}
and
\pi
.

Lemma 5.1

Let
\left(W^{n},\hat{p}\right)
be the grouping scheme given in Algorithm
1
. Then
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{nd}
, it holds that

\left\lvert\sqrt{p_{j}}-\sqrt{\hat{p}_{j}}\right\rvert\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}}
and
\left\lvert\sqrt[3]{p_{j}}-\sqrt[3]{\hat{p}_{j}}\right\rvert\leq\sqrt[3]{\varepsilon_{n}},

where
\varepsilon_{n}\triangleq\frac{3d\log(nd)}{n2^{b}}
.

Now consider the scheme described in Section
4.1
. After the first round, we obtain a coarse estimate
\hat{p}=(\hat{p}_{1},...,\hat{p}_{j})
. Set

\mathcal{E}\triangleq\bigcap_{j\in[d]}\left\{\sqrt{\hat{p}_{j}}\in\left[\sqrt{p_{j}}-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}},\,\sqrt{p_{j}}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}}\right]\right\}
.
Then by Lemma
5.1
and taking the union bound over
j\in[d]
, we have
\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{E}\right\}\geq 1-\frac{1}{n}
. In order to distinguish the estimate obtained from the first round to the final estimator, we use
\check{p}_{j}
to denote the final estimator.

Now observe that the
\ell_{2}
estimation error can be decomposed into

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert p-\check{p}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{E}^{c}\right\}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert p-\check{p}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{E}^{c}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{E}\right\}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert p-\check{p}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{E}\right]\overset{\text{(a)}}{\leq}\frac{2}{n}+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert p-\check{p}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{E}\right]

where (a) holds since
\left\lVert p-\check{p}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 2
almost surely.
Hence it remains to bound
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert p-\check{p}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{E}\right]
.
Next, as described in Section
4.1
, we partition the second
n/2
clients into
d
overlapping groups
\mathcal{G}_{1},...,\mathcal{G}_{d}
according to (
2
).
The reason we choose
n_{j}
in this way is to ensure that 1) for symbols with larger
p_{j}
(which implies larger estimation error), we allocate them more samples; 2) every symbol is assigned with at least
\Theta(n/d)
samples.

Clients in
\mathcal{G}_{j}
then collaboratively estimate
p_{j}
. In particular, client
i
reports her observation if
X_{i}\in\mathcal{J}_{i}\triangleq\left\{j|i\in\mathcal{G}_{j}\right\}
, and the server computes

\check{p}_{j}(Y^{n}|\hat{p})\triangleq\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{i}=j\right\}}}{n_{j}}\sim\frac{1}{n_{j}}\mathsf{Binom}(n_{j},p_{j}).

Finally, the following lemma controls
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert p-\check{p}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{E}\right]
, completing the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Lemma 5.2

Let
\check{p}
be defined as above. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert p-\check{p}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{E}\right]\leq\frac{6}{n2^{b}}\left(\sum_{j\in[d]}\sqrt{p_{j}}\right)^{2}+\frac{10d^{2}\varepsilon_{n}}{n2^{b}}+\frac{1}{n}.

For the
\ell_{1}
error, we set
\pi_{j}(p)\triangleq\frac{\sqrt[3]{p}_{j}}{\sum_{k}\sqrt[3]{p_{k}}}
. Following the similar but slightly more sophisticated analysis, we obtain Theorem

6
The local minimax lower bound (proof of Theorem
2.4
)

Our proof is based on the framework introduced in
[
8
]
, where a global upper bound on the quantized Fisher information is given and used to derive the minimax lower bound on the
\ell_{2}
error. We extend their results to the local regime and develop a
local
upper bound on the quantized Fisher information around a neighborhood of
p
.

To obtain a local upper bound, we construct an
h
-dimensional parametric sub-model
\Theta^{h}_{p}
that contains
p
and is a subset of
\mathcal{P}_{d}
, where
h\in[d]
is a tuning parameter and will be determined later. By considering the sub-model
\Theta^{h}_{p}
, we can control its Fisher information around
p
with a function of
h
and
p
. Optimizing over
h\in[d]
yield an upper bound that depends on
\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\frac{1}{2}}
. Finally, the local upper bound on the quantized Fisher information can then be transformed to the local minimax lower bound on the
\ell_{2}
error via van Tree’s inequality
[
28
]
.

Before entering the main proof, we first introduce some notation that will be used through this section. Let
(p_{(1)},p_{(2)},...,p_{(d)})
be the sorted sequence of
p=(p_{1},p_{2},...,p_{d})
in the non-increasing order; that is,
p_{(i)}\geq p_{(j)}
for all
i>j
. Denote
\pi:[d]\rightarrow[d]
as the corresponding sorting function
5
5

5

With a slight abuse of notation, we overload
\pi
so that
\pi\left((p_{1},...,p_{d})\right)\triangleq(p_{\pi(1)},...,p_{\pi(d)})
, i.e.
p_{(i)}=p_{\pi(i)}
for all
i\in[d]
.

Constructing the sub-model
\Theta^{h}_{p}

We construct
\Theta^{h}_{p}
by “freezing” the
(d-h)
smallest coordinates of
p
and only letting the largest
(h-1)
coordinates to be free parameters. Mathematically, let

\Theta^{h}_{p}\triangleq\left\{\left(\theta_{2},\theta_{3},...,\theta_{h}\right)\middle|\pi^{-1}\left(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{h},p_{(h+1)},p_{(h+2)},...,p_{(d)}\right)\in\mathcal{P}_{d}\right\},

(3)

where
\theta_{1}=1-\sum_{i=2}^{h}\theta_{i}-\sum_{i=h+1}^{d}p_{i}
is fixed when
(\theta_{2},...,\theta_{h})
are determined. For instance, if
p=\left(\frac{1}{16},\frac{1}{8},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{16},\frac{1}{4}\right)
(so
d=5
) and
h=3
, then the corresponding sub-model is

\Theta^{h}_{p}\triangleq\left\{(\theta_{2},\theta_{3})\middle|\left(\frac{1}{16},\theta_{3},\theta_{1},\frac{1}{16},\theta_{2}\right)\in\mathcal{P}_{d}\right\}.

Bounding the quantized Fisher information

Now recall that under this model, the score function

S_{\theta}(x)\triangleq\left(S_{\theta_{2}}(x),...,S_{\theta_{h}}(x)\right)\triangleq\left(\frac{\partial\log p(x|\theta)}{\partial\theta_{2}},...,\frac{\partial\log p(x|\theta)}{\partial\theta_{h}}\right)

can be computed as

S_{\theta_{i}}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\theta_{i}},&\text{ if }x=\pi(i),\,2\leq i\leq h\\
-\frac{1}{\theta_{1}},&\text{ if }x=\pi(1)\\
0,&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

The next lemma shows that to bound the quantized Fisher information, it suffices to control the variance of the score function.

Lemma 6.1
(Theorem 1 in
[
8
]
)

Let
W
be any
b
-bit quantization scheme and
I_{W}(\theta)
is the Fisher information of
Y
at
\theta
where
Y\sim W(\cdot|X)
and
X\sim p_{\theta}
. Then for any
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{h}
,

\mathsf{Tr}\left(I_{W}(\theta)\right)\leq\min\left(I_{X}(\theta),2^{b}\max_{\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\mathsf{Var}\left(\langle u,S_{\theta}(X)\rangle\right)\right).

Therefore, for any unit vector
u=(u_{2},...,u_{h})
with
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{2}=1
, we control the variance as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}\left(\langle u,S_{\theta}(X)\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(a)}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{h}\theta_{i}\left(\sum_{j=2}^{h}u_{j}S_{\theta_{j}}(\pi(i))\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\theta_{1}\left(\sum_{j=2}^{h}u_{j}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\theta_{1}}\right)^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{h}\theta_{i}u_{i}^{2}\frac{1}{\theta_{i}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\sum_{j=2}^{h}u_{j}\right)^{2}}{\theta_{1}}+\sum\limits_{j=2}^{h}\frac{u_{i}^{2}}{\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{h}{\theta_{1}}+\frac{1}{\min_{j\in\left\{2,...,h\right\}}\theta_{j}},

(4)

where (a) holds since the score function has zero mean.
This allows us to upper bound

\theta(p)

\theta(p)

p
in the sub-model

\theta(p)=(\theta_{2}(p),...,\theta_{h}(p))\triangleq(p_{(2)},...,p_{(h)}).

In particular, for any

\min_{j\in\{2,...,h\}}\theta_{j}^{\prime}\geq\min_{j\in\left\{2,...,h\right\}}\theta_{j}(p)-\frac{p_{(h)}}{3}\geq\frac{2p_{(h)}}{3}.

Therefore (

Together with Lemma

\forall\theta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{B,h}(p),\,\,\mathsf{Tr}\left(I_{W}(\theta^{\prime})\right)\leq 2^{b}\left(6h+\frac{3}{2p_{(h)}}\right).

Bounding the
\ell_{2}

Applying
[
8

on

\sup_{\theta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{B,h}(p)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\hat{\theta}-\theta^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\right]\geq\frac{h^{2}}{n2^{b}\left(6h+\frac{3}{2p_{(h)}}\right)+\frac{h\pi^{2}}{B^{2}}}\geq\frac{h^{2}p_{(h)}}{10n2^{b}+\frac{10hp_{(h)}}{B^{2}}}\geq\frac{h^{2}p_{(h)}}{20n2^{b}},

Finally, we maximize

7
Conclusion and open problems

We have investigated distribution estimation under
b
-bit communication constraints and characterized the local complexity of a target distribution
p
. We show that under
\ell_{2}
loss, the half-norm of
p
dictates the convergence rate of the estimation error. In addition, to achieve the optimal (centralized) convergence rate,
\Theta(\mathsf{H}_{1/2}(p))
bits of communication is both necessary and sufficient.

Many interesting questions remain to be addressed, including investigating if the same lower bound can be achieved by non-interactive schemes, deriving the local complexity under general information constraints (such as
\varepsilon
-local differential privacy constraint), and extending these results to the distribution-free setting (i.e. the frequency estimation problem).

A.1

In terms of the convergence rate, Theorem

8

5
]
require

.

That being said, with additional prior knowledge on the target distribution such as sparse/near-sparse assumptions, we can easily improve our two-stage scheme by replacing the uniform grouping localization step with other sparse estimation schemes

A.2

We note that when the goal is to achieve the centralized rate with minimal communication (instead of achieving the best convergence rate for a fixed communication budget as we have assumed so far), the performance promised in the above corollary can be achieved without knowing

beforehand. We can do that by modifying our proposed scheme to include an initial round for estimating

\mathsf{H}_{1/2}(p)
within
1

is sufficiently large (e.g.

(i.e. the scheme described in Section

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c}\right\}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j\in[d]}\left\lvert p_{j}-\check{p}_{j}\right\rvert\middle|\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{1}\right\}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j\in[d]}\left\lvert p_{j}-\check{p}_{j}\right\rvert\middle|\mathcal{E}_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j\in[d]}\left\lvert p_{j}-\check{p}_{j}\right\rvert\middle|\mathcal{E}_{1}\right].

As in the
\ell_{2}

\check{p}_{j}\triangleq\frac{1}{n_{j}}\mathsf{Binom}(n_{j},p_{j}).

For the ease of analysis, we partition
[d]

\mathcal{J}^{+}\triangleq\left\{j\in[d]\middle|n_{j}=n\right\},\,\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}\triangleq\left\{j\in[d]\setminus\mathcal{J}^{+}\middle|p_{j}\geq\alpha\right\}\,\text{ and }\mathcal{J}^{c}\triangleq[d]\setminus\left(\mathcal{J}^{+}\cup\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}\right),

where

n_{j}

•

n_{j}=\frac{n}{2}

•

•

for

We can then compute the estimation error by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j\in[d]}\left\lvert p_{j}-\check{p}_{j}\right\rvert\middle|\mathcal{E}_{1}\right]\leq\sum_{j\in[d]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(p_{j}-\check{p}_{j}\right)^{2}\middle|\mathcal{E}_{1}\right]}\overset{\text{(a)}}{\leq}\sum_{j\in[d]}\sqrt{\frac{p_{j}}{n_{j}}}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(b)}}{\leq}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{+}}\sqrt{\frac{2p_{j}}{n}}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}}\sqrt{\frac{4p_{j}}{n2^{b}\hat{\pi}_{j}}}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{c}}\sqrt{\frac{4dp_{j}}{n2^{b}}}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(c)}}{\leq}\sqrt{\frac{2\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\frac{1}{2}}}{n}}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}}\sqrt{\frac{4p_{j}}{n2^{b}\hat{\pi}_{j}}}+\sqrt{\frac{4d^{3}\alpha}{n2^{b}}}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(d)}}{=}\sqrt{\frac{2\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\frac{1}{2}}}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{4}{n2^{b}}}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{j^{\prime}\in[d]}\sqrt[3]{\hat{p}_{j^{\prime}}}}\right)\left(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}}\sqrt{\frac{p_{j}}{\sqrt[3]{\hat{p}_{j}}}}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{4d^{3}\alpha}{n2^{b}}}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(d)}}{\leq}\sqrt{\frac{2\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\frac{1}{2}}}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{4}{n2^{b}}}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{j^{\prime}\in[d]}\left(\sqrt[3]{p_{j^{\prime}}}+\sqrt[3]{\varepsilon_{n}}\right)}\right)\left(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}}\sqrt{\frac{p_{j}}{\sqrt[3]{p_{j}}-\sqrt[3]{\varepsilon_{n}}}}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{4d^{3}\alpha}{n2^{b}}},

(6)

where (a) holds since conditioned on
\mathcal{E}_{1}
,
\check{p}\sim\frac{1}{n}\mathsf{Binom}(n_{j},p_{j})
, (b) holds by the definition of
n_{j}
, (c) is due to Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, (d) follows from the definition of
\hat{\pi}_{j}
and finally (d) is due to Lemma
5.1
. If we pick
\alpha=8\varepsilon_{n}
, then we can further bound (
C
) by

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\frac{1}{2}}}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{4}{n2^{b}}}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{j^{\prime}\in[d]}\left(\sqrt[3]{p_{j}}+\sqrt[3]{\varepsilon_{n}}\right)}\right)\left(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}}\sqrt{\frac{p_{j}}{\sqrt[3]{p_{j}}-\sqrt[3]{\varepsilon_{n}}}}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{4d^{3}\alpha}{n2^{b}}}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(a)}}{\leq}\sqrt{\frac{2\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\frac{1}{2}}}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{16}{n2^{b}}\left(\sum_{j\in[d]}\sqrt[3]{p_{j}}\right)^{3}}+\sqrt{\frac{16d\sqrt[3]{\varepsilon_{n}}}{n2^{b}}\left(\sum_{j\in[d]}\sqrt[3]{p_{j}}\right)^{2}}+\sqrt{\frac{32d^{3}\varepsilon_{n}}{n2^{b}}}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(b)}}{\leq}\sqrt{\frac{2\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\frac{1}{2}}}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{16}{n2^{b}}\left(\sum_{j\in[d]}\sqrt[3]{p_{j}}\right)^{3}}+\sqrt{\frac{48d^{3}\varepsilon_{n}}{n2^{b}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\frac{1}{2}}}{n}}+C_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\frac{1}{3}}}{n2^{b}}}+C_{3}\sqrt{\frac{d^{3}\varepsilon_{n}}{n2^{b}}},

where (a) holds since

and in (b) we use the following Young’s inequality:

.

Appendix E
A complete characterization for the local complexity of Zipf distributions

Below we characterize the local complexity of Zipf distributions for all regime
\lambda>0
. Interestingly, as
\lambda
decreases, we see different dependency on
d
.

Corollary E.1
(Truncated Zipf distributions with
\lambda\geq 2
)

Let
p\triangleq\mathsf{Zipf}_{\lambda,d}
be a truncated Zipf distribution with
\lambda\geq 2
. That is, for
d,k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathsf{Zipf}_{\lambda,d}(k)\triangleq\frac{\frac{1}{k^{\lambda}}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{k\leq d\right\}}}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{d}\frac{1}{(k^{\prime})^{\lambda}}}.

Then the skewness of
p
is characterized by

1.

\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{1/2}=\Theta\left(\left(\frac{1-d^{-\lambda/2+1}}{1-d^{-\lambda+1}}\right)^{2}\right)=\Theta(1)
, if
\lambda>2
.
In this case,
r\left(\ell_{2},p,\left(W^{n},\hat{p}\right)\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
.

2.

\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{1/2}=\Theta\left(\left(\frac{\log d}{1-d^{-\lambda+1}}\right)^{2}\right)=\Theta\left(\log^{2}d\right)
, if
\lambda=2
,
so
r\left(\ell_{2},p,\left(W^{n},\hat{p}\right)\right)=O\left(\frac{\log^{2}d}{n2^{b}}\vee\frac{1}{n}\right)
.

3.

\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{1/2}=\Theta\left(\left(\frac{d^{-\lambda/2+1}}{1-d^{-\lambda+1}}\right)^{2}\right)=\Theta\left(d^{2-\lambda}\right)
, if
1<\lambda<2
.
So
r\left(\ell_{2},p,\left(W^{n},\hat{p}\right)\right)=O\left(\frac{d^{2-\lambda}}{n2^{b}}\vee\frac{1}{n}\right)
.

4.

\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{1/2}=\Theta\left(\left(\frac{d^{-\lambda/2+1}}{\log d}\right)^{2}\right)=\Theta\left(\frac{d}{\log^{2}d}\right)
, if
\lambda=1
.
So
r\left(\ell_{2},p,\left(W^{n},\hat{p}\right)\right)=O\left(\frac{d}{n2^{b}\log^{2}d}\vee\frac{1}{n}\right)
.

5.

\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{1/2}=\Theta\left(\left(\frac{d^{-\lambda/2+1}}{d^{-\lambda+1}}\right)^{2}\right)=\Theta\left(d\right)
, if
\lambda<1
.
So
r\left(\ell_{2},p,\left(W^{n},\hat{p}\right)\right)=O\left(\frac{d}{n2^{b}}\vee\frac{1}{n}\right)
.

F.1
Proof of Lemma
5.1

Let
\left(W^{n},\hat{p}\right)
be the naive grouping scheme introduced by
[
4
]
. Then

\hat{p}_{j}\sim\frac{1}{n^{\prime}}\mathsf{Binom}(n^{\prime},p_{j})
, where
n^{\prime}\triangleq\frac{n}{2d}
. By the Chernoff bound,
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{nd}

\hat{p}_{j}\in\left[p_{j}-\sqrt{\frac{3d\log(nd)p_{j}}{n2^{b}}},p_{j}+\sqrt{\frac{3d\log(nd)p_{j}}{n2^{b}}}\right]=\left[p_{j}-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}p_{j}},\,p_{j}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}p_{j}}\right].

(7)

The first result follows from

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sqrt{p_{j}}-\sqrt{\hat{p}_{j}}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\frac{p_{j}-\hat{p}_{j}}{\sqrt{p_{j}}+\sqrt{\hat{p}^{+}_{j}}}\right\rvert\leq\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}p_{j}}}{\sqrt{p_{j}}}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}}

and taking union bound over
j\in[d]
. To prove the second result, for each
j\in[d]
, we consider two cases.

•

If
p_{j}\geq\varepsilon_{n}
, then it holds with probability at least
1-1/nd

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sqrt[3]{\hat{p}_{j}}-\sqrt[3]{p_{j}}\right\rvert=\frac{\left\lvert\hat{p}_{j}-p_{j}\right\rvert}{\hat{p}_{j}^{2/3}+\left(\hat{p}_{j}p_{j}\right)^{1/3}+p_{j}^{2/3}}\overset{\text{(a)}}{\leq}\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}p_{j}}}{p_{j}^{2/3}}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}}p_{j}^{-1/6}\overset{\text{(b)}}{\leq}\varepsilon_{n}^{1/3},

where (a) holds with probability at least
1-1/nd
due to (
7
), and (b) holds since by assumption
p_{j}\geq\varepsilon_{n}
.

•

If
p_{j}\leq\varepsilon_{n}
, then since
\hat{p}_{j}\geq 0
almost surely, it suffices to control

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\sqrt[3]{\hat{p}_{j}}>\sqrt[3]{p_{j}}+\sqrt[3]{\varepsilon_{n}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{{\hat{p}_{j}}>{p_{j}}+\varepsilon_{n}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\lvert\hat{p}_{j}-{p_{j}}\right\rvert>\varepsilon_{n}\right\}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(a)}}{\leq}\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\lvert\hat{p}_{j}-{p_{j}}\right\rvert>\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}p_{j}}\right\}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(b)}}{\leq}\frac{1}{nd},

where (a) holds since
p_{j}\leq\varepsilon_{n}
, and (b) holds by (
7
).

The proof is complete since for both cases we have
\left\lvert\sqrt[3]{\hat{p}_{j}}-\sqrt[3]{p_{j}}\right\rvert\leq\sqrt[3]{\varepsilon_{n}}
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{nd}
.

Proof of Lemma

We prove by contradiction.

1.

For the first inequality, assume by contradiction that
\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\frac{1}{2}}\geq\max_{h\in[d]}h^{2}p_{(h)}
does not hold. Then there must exists some

(8)

However, this implies

\displaystyle\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\frac{1}{2}}\overset{\text{(a)}}{=}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{j}}\right)^{2}\geq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{h}}\sqrt{p_{(j)}}\right)^{2}\overset{\text{(b)}}{\geq}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{h}}\sqrt{p_{(\tilde{h})}}\right)^{2}\overset{\text{(c)}}{>}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{j}}\right)^{2},

where (a) is by definition, (b) holds since
p_{(\tilde{h})}\leq p_{(j)}
for all
j\leq\tilde{h}
, and (c) is due to (
8
).
This yields contradiction.

2.

Secondly, assume by contradiction that for all
h
,
h^{2}p_{(h)}<\left(\frac{\delta}{1+\delta}\right)^{\frac{2}{1+\delta}}\left(\sum_{j\in[d]}p_{j}^{\frac{1+\delta}{2}}\right)^{\frac{2}{1+\delta}}
. This would imply

\displaystyle p_{(h)}^{\frac{1+\delta}{2}}<\left(\frac{\delta}{1+\delta}\right)\frac{\sum_{j\in[d]}p_{j}^{\frac{1+\delta}{2}}}{h^{1+\delta}}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{h}p_{(h)}^{\frac{1+\delta}{2}}=\sum_{j\in[d]}p_{j}^{\frac{1+\delta}{2}}<\left(\frac{\delta}{1+\delta}\right)\left(\sum_{j\in[d]}p_{j}^{\frac{1+\delta}{2}}\right)\sum_{h}\frac{1}{h^{1+\delta}}
