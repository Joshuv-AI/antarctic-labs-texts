Optimal and Near-Optimal Adaptive Vector Quantization
Ran Ben Basat, Yaniv Ben-Itzhak, Michael Mitzenmacher, Shay Vargaftik

Abstract
Quantization is a fundamental optimization for many machine-learning use cases, including compressing gradients, model weights and activations, and datasets. The most accurate form of quantization is adaptive , where the error is minimized with respect to a given input, rather than optimizing for the worst case. However, optimal adaptive quantization methods are considered infeasible in terms of both their runtime and memory requirements. We revisit the Adaptive Vector Quantization (AVQ) problem and present algorithms that find optimal solutions with asymptotically improved time and space complexity. We also present an even faster near-optimal algorithm for large inputs. Our experiments show our algorithms may open the door to using AVQ more extensively in a variety of machine learning applications.



1
Introduction

Quantization is a central building block for optimizing a large range of machine learning applications. It is often used for compressing gradients to reduce network requirements in distributed and federated learning (e.g.,
Suresh et al. 2017
;
Konečnỳ & Richtárik 2018
;
Caldas et al. 2018
;
Vargaftik et al. 2022
;
Dorfman et al. 2023
); for reducing memory footprint while accelerating the computation via post-training quantization (e.g.,
Frantar et al. 2023
;
Jeon et al. 2023
), quantization-aware training of models (e.g.,
Micikevicius et al. 2018
;
Shen et al. 2020
) and LLMs key-value (KV) cache
(
Sheng et al. 2023
)
; and for quantization of datasets for faster training and inference (e.g.,
Zhou et al. 2023
).

A fundamental quantization method is
stochastic quantization
,
where a sender quantizes an input vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
using a set
Q\subset\mathbb{R}
of
|Q|=s
quantization values in an unbiased manner
(
Ben Basat, Ran and Mitzenmacher, Michael and Vargaftik, Shay 2021
)
.
That is, each coordinate
x\in X
is quantized into a value
\widehat{x}\in Q
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{x}\right]=x
. The Adaptive Vector Quantization (AVQ) problem (e.g.,
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Fu et al. 2020
;
Faghri et al. 2020
) then lies in selecting the set
Q
to minimize the mean squared error given by

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\widehat{X}-X\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]=\sum_{x\in X}\mathrm{Var}{[}\widehat{x}]\,\,,

also known as the sum of variances.

Previous quantization works can largely be categorized into two classes. The first includes methods that are distribution-agnostic, i.e., design the quantization without optimizing it for the specific input. For example, Stochastic Uniform Quantization
(
Suresh et al. 2017
)
, QGSD
(
Alistarh et al. 2017
)
, and Natural Compression
(
Horvóth et al. 2022
)
determine the quantization values with respect to global properties such as the vector’s norm, or min and max values.

Other works, including
Suresh et al. 2017
;
Caldas et al. 2018
;
Safaryan et al. 2020
;
Vargaftik et al. 2021
;
Vargaftik et al. 2022
;
Basat et al. 2022
, apply a reversible transformation that converts
X
into a vector
X^{\prime}
with controlled distribution (e.g., with
\max(X^{\prime})-\min(X^{\prime})=\tilde{O}(\left\lVert X\right\rVert/\sqrt{d})
), thus allowing quantizing
X^{\prime}
in a way that optimizes the worse-case sum of variances. The receiver then applies the inverse transform on the quantization
of
X^{\prime}
to obtain an estimate of
X
.

The second class, which addresses the AVQ problem, uses the fact that in many cases the quantized inputs have a significant structure that can be leveraged to reduce the error. For example, DNN gradients (which are often compressed in distributed and federated learning applications to reduce bandwidth
(
Konečný et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2024
)
) were observed to follow log-normal-like
(
Chmiel et al. 2021
)
or normal-like
(
Banner et al. 2019
;
Ye et al. 2020
)
distributions, while sub-Weibull characterizes the distribution of deep activation layers
(
Vladimirova et al. 2018
)
.

Adaptive
methods therefore ask: how can we optimize the choice of
Q

for a given input
X
?

The answer to this question proves tricky, with the problem being non-convex even for
s{=}4
(two-bit quantization)
(
Faghri et al. 2020
)
. Thus, solutions like ZipML
(
Zhang et al. 2017
)
approach the challenge using a dynamic programming approach that allows one to optimize
Q
in polynomial time. However, this solution has a significant overhead and solving the problem optimally is often considered to be infeasible; for example,
(
Faghri et al. 2020
)
develop approximate solutions as they write,

“To find the optimal sequence of quantization values, a dynamic program is solved whose computational and memory cost is quadratic in the number of points … For this reason, ZipML is impractical for quantizing on the fly”.

As another evidence of the problem’s hardness,
Fu et al. 2020
solve the problem only for a given (Weibull) distribution, writing that

“The empirical distribution is usually non-differentiable, making the searching of
Q
infeasible”.

Nevertheless, there is a significant interest in advancing AVQ solutions towards wider adoption as even approximate adaptive solutions like ALQ
(
Faghri et al. 2020
)
were shown to be more precise than advanced distribution-agnostic methods such such Non-Uniform QSGD (NUQSGD)
(
Ramezani-Kebrya et al. 2021
)
. Moreover, AVQ methods can improve more complex schemes (e.g., including the aforementioned ones that utilize worst-case to average-case transformations) by replacing distribution-agnostic quantization with an adaptive one.

In this paper, we show that one can, in fact, solve the problem optimally and efficiently. To this end, we introduce QUantized Adaptive Vector EncodeR (QUIVER), an algorithm that features novel acceleration methods and leverages concaveness properties of the underlying problem to reduce the runtime complexity from
O(s\cdot d^{2})
to
O(s\cdot d)
and the space complexity from
O(d^{2})
to
O(s\cdot d)
.
We then further accelerate QUIVER by deriving a closed from solution for
s=3
, allowing it to place two quantization values at a time. Finally, we show that one can achieve a
1+o(1)
approximation in
O(d)+\widetilde{O}(s\cdot\sqrt{d})
time and space complexities, allowing QUIVER to quantize
large vectors on the fly.

We implement our algorithms in C++ and show that QUIVER can, e.g., compute the
optimal
quantization values for a vector with
1M
entries in
250ms
and compute a
1.005
-approximation for a
133M
-sized vector in under a millisecond on a commodity PC. We evaluate our solutions compared to the state of the art AVQ methods on a variety of distributions that are of interest, vector sizes, number of quantization values, and approximation ratios, and demonstrate a speed up of (up to four) orders of magnitude.

We note that there are many works that investigate different forms of compression, including non adaptive quantization, sparsification, low-rank decomposition, variable-length coding and more. Many of these are orthogonal to our work and can be used in conjunction with it. For example, one can use AVQ to quantize a sparsified vector or apply variable length encoding to further reduce the quantization size.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Notations and definitions

Given two quantization values
a,b
and a number
x\in[a,b]
, Stochastic Quantization (SQ) is a procedure that rounds
x
to
\widehat{x}
where
\widehat{x}\in\left\{a,b\right\}
. Specifically,
\widehat{x}
obtains the value
a
with probability
p_{a}=\frac{b-x}{b-a}
and the value
b
with probability
p_{b}=\frac{x-a}{b-a}
(i.e.,
p_{a}+p_{b}=1
). An important property of SQ is that the expected rounded value is
unbiased
, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{x}\right]=a\cdot p_{a}+b\cdot p_{b}=x.

The variance of stochastically quantizing
x
is then given by

\mathbb{E}\left[(x-\widehat{x})^{2}\right]=(x-a)^{2}\cdot p_{a}+(x-b)^{2}\cdot p_{b}=(b-x)(x-a).

Given a vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and an integer
s\in\mathbb{N}
, the Adaptive Vector Quantization (AVQ) problem
(
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Faghri et al. 2020
;
Fu et al. 2020
)
looks for a set of quantization values
Q
where
|Q|=s
and that minimizes the mean squared error (MSE) resulting in stochastically quantizing
X
to
\widehat{X}\in Q^{d}
. That is, each entry
x\in X
is stochastically quantized between
a_{x}=\max\left\{q\in Q\mid q\leq x\right\}
and
b_{x}=\min\left\{q\in Q\mid q\geq x\right\}
.

Formally, AVQ seeks to minimize the MSE, given by

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert X-\widehat{X}\right\rVert^{2}\right]=\sum_{x\in X}(b_{x}-x)(x-a_{x}),

where
\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{X}\right]=X
holds by construction.

2.2
Existing AVQ methods

Leveraging the properties that any optimal solution must include
\min X
and
\max X
and that there exists an optimal solution in which
Q\subseteq X

(
Zhang et al. 2017
)
, one can naively solve the problem in
O\left({d-2\choose s-2}\right)=d^{\Theta(s)}
time by going over all choices for the quantization values (as mentioned, the AVQ problem is non-convex for any
s>3
).
Instead, we present the following dynamic program (DP) that allows us to solve it optimally and efficiently for any
s
.
Given a
sorted
vector
X=\left\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\right\rangle
, we denote by
\mathit{MSE}[i,j]
the optimal MSE of quantizing the prefix vector
X_{j}=\left\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{j}\right\rangle
using
i
quantization values
that include

x_{j}
. Our goal is then to compute
\mathit{MSE}[s,d]
and a quantization values set
Q
that obtains it.
Accordingly, we express the dynamic program as follows.
To begin, we define
C[k,j]
as the sum of variances of all entries in the range
[x_{k},x_{j}]
, i.e.,

C[k,j]=\sum_{x\in[x_{k},x_{j}]}(x_{j}-x)(x-x_{k})\ \ .

Here and when clear from context, to simplify notation, we write
\sum_{x}
to denote
\sum_{x\in X}
.

For
i\in[2,s],j\in[i,d]
, we set
\mathit{MSE}[2,j]=C[1,j]\,\,\forall j
and use the recurrence

\mathit{MSE}[i,j]=\min\limits_{k\in\left\{i,\ldots,j\right\}}\ \mathit{MSE}[i-1,k]+C[k,j]~.

Here, the index
k
denotes the coordinate in
X
,
x_{k}
, of the rightmost quantization value to the left of
x_{j}
.
A naive solution for the above DP is first to compute the matrix
C
(which takes
O(d^{3})
time and
O(d^{2})
space) and then calculate
\mathit{MSE}[s,d]
in
O(s\cdot d^{2})
time and
O(s\cdot d)
space.

An improved solution that uses
O(s\cdot d^{2})
time and
O(d^{2})
space was proposed by
(
Zhang et al. 2017
)
, but it remains infeasible even for moderate (e.g.,
d=10^{5}
) dimensions.

3
Optimization Using Pre-processing

A vital ingredient in our solution is the usage of preprocessed arrays that allow us to efficiently compute
C[k,j]
in constant time, at the cost of only
O(d)
additional space.
We define the following arrays,
\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that store the cumulative sums of the vector and its squared entries:

\displaystyle\beta_{j}

\displaystyle={\sum_{x\in X_{j}}x}\quad\ \ ,\quad j\in\left\{1,\ldots,d\right\}\ ,

\displaystyle\gamma_{j}

\displaystyle={\sum_{x\in X_{j}}x^{2}}\quad,\quad j\in\left\{1,\ldots,d\right\}\ .

Denoting
\beta_{0}=\gamma_{0}=0
, both are computable in
O(d)
time as
\beta_{i}=\beta_{i-1}+x_{i}
and
\gamma_{i}=\gamma_{i-1}+x_{i}^{2}
.

We can then express
C[k,j]
as follows:

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle[k,j]=\sum_{x\in[x_{k},x_{j}]}(x_{j}-x)(x-x_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in(x_{k},x_{j}]}(x_{j}-x)(x-x_{k})

\displaystyle=x_{j}\cdot x_{k}\cdot\hskip-5.69054pt\sum_{x\in(x_{k},x_{j}]}1+(x_{j}-x_{k})\cdot\hskip-5.69054pt\sum_{x\in(x_{k},x_{j}]}x-\sum_{x\in(x_{k},x_{j}]}x^{2}

\displaystyle=x_{j}\cdot x_{k}\cdot(j-k)+(x_{j}-x_{k})\cdot(\beta_{j}-\beta_{k})-(\gamma_{j}-\gamma_{k}).

With this optimization, we can evaluate
C[k,j]
in constant time, yielding a solution that uses
O(s\cdot d)
memory instead of
O(d^{2})
. Next, we show how to improve the runtime.

4
An
O(s\cdot d\cdot\log d)
Time Algorithm Using Binary Search

We now state a property of the problem’s structure and later show how to leverage it to accelerate the computation.

Proposition 4.1
.

Assume
j_{1},j_{2}\in\left\{i,\ldots,d\right\}
and let

\displaystyle k_{1}

\displaystyle=\mbox{argmin}_{k}\ \ \mathit{MSE}[i-1,k]+C[k,j_{1}],

\displaystyle k_{2}

\displaystyle=\mbox{argmin}_{k}\ \ \mathit{MSE}[i-1,k]+C[k,j_{2}].

Then,
j_{1}\geq j_{2}
implies
x_{k_{1}}\geq x_{k_{2}}
.

Proof.

Assume to the contrary that
j_{1}\geq j_{2}
but
x_{k_{1}}<x_{k_{2}}
.
We now show that this implies that
k_{1}
is not optimal for
j_{1}
.

Recall that
\mathit{MSE}[i,j_{1}]
is the MSE of stochastically quantizing the elements in
X_{j_{1}}
using
i
quantization values. Let us break this quantity into (a) the MSE of quantizing the elements in
X_{j_{2}}
and (b) the MSE of quantizing the elements in
X_{j_{1}}\setminus X_{j_{2}}
. For (a), we have that switching to
k_{2}
must minimize the MSE by its definition. For (b), notice that using
k_{1}
, the MSE is
\sum_{x\in X_{j_{1}}\setminus X_{j_{2}}}(x_{j_{1}}-x)(x-x_{k_{1}})
; however, since
x_{k_{1}}<x_{k_{2}}\leq x_{j_{2}}\leq x
, we have that
(x_{j_{1}}-x)(x-x_{k_{1}})<(x_{j_{1}}-x)(x-x_{k_{2}})
.
That is, we have

\mathit{MSE}[i,j_{1}]=\mathit{MSE}[i-1,k_{1}]+C[k_{1},j_{1}]<\mathit{MSE}[i-1,k_{2}]+C[k_{2},j_{1}]
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Intuitively, this proposition suggests that if we know that the optimal value for
\mathit{MSE}[i,j]
was obtained using
k^{*}
, we can restrict the search range for
\mathit{MSE}[i,j^{\prime}]
to
k\in\left\{k^{*},\ldots,j^{\prime}\right\}
for all
j^{\prime}>j
and to
k\in\left\{i,\ldots,,k^{*}\right\}
for
j^{\prime}<j
.
That is, when filling
\mathit{MSE}[i,j]
, we do not need to consider the minimum over all
k\in\left\{i,\ldots,j\right\}
.

Using this proposition, our algorithm computes
\mathit{MSE}
more efficiently. Namely, we define the MSEi(
i,\ell,r,k_{\min},k_{\max}
) procedure, whose pseudo-code is given in
Algorithm

2
, to compute
\mathit{MSE}[i,j]
for
j=\ell,\ldots,r
, knowing that

k_{\min}\leq k^{*}\leq k_{\max}
,
where

k^{*}=\text{argmin}_{k\in\left\{i,\ldots,j\right\}}\quad\mathit{MSE}[i-1,k]+C[k,j].

The procedure first computes
k^{*}
for the middle of the range,
m=\left\lfloor(\ell+r)/2\right\rfloor
. It then recursively invokes MSEi(
i,\ell,m-1,k_{\min},k^{*}
) to calculate
\mathit{MSE}[i,j]
for the prefix
j=\ell,\ldots,m-1
and MSEi(
i,m+1,r,k^{*},k_{\max}
) for
j=m+1,\ldots,r
. For example, as the initial call is MSEi(
i,i,d,i,d
), it computes
\mathit{MSE}[i,\left\lfloor(i+d)/2\right\rfloor]
, where
k^{*}\in\left\{i,\ldots,\left\lfloor(i+d)/2\right\rfloor\right\}
, and recurses accordingly.

This gives the following time complexity bound recursion:

T(n,z)\leq\max_{k^{\prime}\in\left\{0,\ldots,z\right\}}T(n/2,k^{\prime})+T(n/2,z-k^{\prime})+O(z),

where we are interested in
T(d,d)
. Here,
T(n,z)
corresponds to a bound on the time it takes to compute MSEi
(i,\ell,r,k_{\min},k_{\max})
such that
n=r-\ell
and
z=k_{\max}-k_{\min}
.
The simple observation that
k^{\prime}+(n-k^{\prime})=n
implies that regardless of the choice of
k^{\prime}
, the amount of work at each level of the recursion for a given
i
is bounded by
d
while we have
O(\log d)
depth for a total runtime of
O(d\log d)
. Overall, this implies that the runtime of
Algorithm

2

O(s\cdot d\log d)

The above algorithm is simple and effective for moderately-sized inputs. To address quantizing vectors of larger dimensions, we further optimize the asymptotic runtime.

5
An
O(s\cdot d)
Time Algorithm via Concaveness

To derive a faster algorithm, we observe that
C
satisfies the quadrangle inequality, defined below:

Definition 5.1
.

A function
w{:}\left\{1,\ldots,d\right\}{\times}\left\{1,\ldots,d\right\}{\to}\mathbb{R}
satisfies the quadrangle inequality if for any
\texttt{a}{\leq}\texttt{b}{\leq}\texttt{c}{\leq}\texttt{d}

w(\texttt{a},\texttt{c})+w({\texttt{b}},\texttt{d})\leq w(\texttt{a},\texttt{d})+w({\texttt{b}},\texttt{c}).

Lemma 5.2
.

C
satisfies the quadrangle inequality.

Proof.

We first observe that for any
x\in[x_{\texttt{a}},x_{\texttt{b}}]:

\displaystyle(x_{\texttt{c}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{a}})

\displaystyle=(x_{\texttt{d}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{a}})+(x_{\texttt{c}}-x_{\texttt{d}})(x-x_{\texttt{a}})

\displaystyle\leq(x_{\texttt{d}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{a}}).

(1)

For any
x\in[x_{\texttt{c}},x_{\texttt{d}}]
, we similarly get:

\displaystyle(x_{\texttt{d}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{b}})

\displaystyle=(x_{\texttt{d}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{a}})+(x_{\texttt{d}}-x)(x_{\texttt{a}}-x_{\texttt{b}})

\displaystyle\leq(x_{\texttt{d}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{a}}).

(2)

Similarly, for
x\in[x_{\texttt{b}},x_{\texttt{c}}]
, we have that:

(x_{\texttt{c}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{a}})+(x_{\texttt{d}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{b}})\\
=(x_{\texttt{c}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{b}})+(x_{\texttt{d}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{a}})+(x_{\texttt{a}}-x_{\texttt{b}})(x_{\texttt{d}}-x_{\texttt{c}})\\
\leq(x_{\texttt{c}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{b}})+(x_{\texttt{d}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{a}}).

(3)

Therefore, we get:

C({\texttt{a}},{\texttt{c}})+C({\texttt{b}},{\texttt{d}})\\
=\sum_{x\in[x_{\texttt{a}},x_{\texttt{c}}]}(x_{\texttt{c}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{a}})+\sum_{x\in[x_{\texttt{b}},x_{\texttt{d}}]}(x_{\texttt{d}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{b}})\\
=\sum_{x\in[x_{\texttt{a}},x_{\texttt{b}}]}(x_{\texttt{c}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{a}})+\sum_{x\in[x_{\texttt{c}},x_{\texttt{d}}]}(x_{\texttt{d}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{b}})\\
+\sum_{x\in[x_{\texttt{b}},x_{\texttt{c}}]}(x_{\texttt{c}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{a}})+(x_{\texttt{d}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{b}})\\
\leq\sum_{x\in[x_{\texttt{a}},x_{\texttt{b}}]}(x_{\texttt{d}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{a}})+\sum_{x\in[x_{\texttt{c}},x_{\texttt{d}}]}(x_{\texttt{d}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{a}})\\
+\sum_{x\in[x_{\texttt{b}},x_{\texttt{c}}]}(x_{\texttt{c}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{b}})+(x_{\texttt{d}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{a}}).\\
=\sum_{x\in[x_{\texttt{a}},x_{\texttt{d}}]}(x_{\texttt{d}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{a}})+\sum_{x\in[x_{\texttt{b}},x_{\texttt{c}}]}(x_{\texttt{c}}-x)(x-x_{\texttt{b}})\\
=C({\texttt{a}},{\texttt{d}})+C({\texttt{b}},{\texttt{c}}).

Here, the inequality follows from equations (
1
)-(
3
).
∎

q
:

\displaystyle Q(q)=\sum_{x\in[x_{k},q]}(q-x)(x-x_{k})+\sum_{x\in(q,x_{j}]}(x_{j}-x)(x-q).

This function is differentiable in
[x_{k},x_{j}]\setminus X
, and we get:

\displaystyle\frac{dQ(q)}{dq}=\sum_{x\in[x_{k},q]}(x-x_{k})-\sum_{x\in(q,x_{j}]}(x_{j}-x).

Notice that the derivative is monotonically non-decreasing and for any
\ell\in\left\{k,k+1,\ldots,j-1\right\}
is fixed (independent of
q
) at any interval
(x_{\ell},x_{\ell+1})
. This means that
Q(q)
is minimized at
u=\inf_{q}(\frac{dQ(q)}{dq}>0)
, where
u\in X
.

Denote by
b^{*}_{k,j}\in\left\{k,\ldots,j\right\}
the value such that
x_{b^{*}_{k,j}}=u
. Notice that while
\frac{dQ(u)}{dq}
may not be defined, we have that
\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{dQ(u+h)}{dq}>0
is well-defined.
We thus require

\sum_{i=k+1}^{b^{*}_{k,j}}(x_{i}-x_{k})-\sum_{i=b^{*}_{k,j}+1}^{j}(x_{j}-x_{i})>0.

With some simplifications, this is equivalent to:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=k+1}^{j}x_{i}-(b^{*}_{k,j}-k)x_{k}-(j-b^{*}_{k,j})x_{j}>0,

yielding

\displaystyle b^{*}_{k,j}>\frac{jx_{j}-kx_{k}-\sum_{i=k+1}^{j}x_{i}}{x_{j}-x_{k}}.

As
b^{*}_{k,j}
is an integer, we get a formula for
C_{2}[k,j]
that can be computed in constant time using:

b^{*}_{k,j}{=}\!\left\lceil\frac{jx_{j}-kx_{k}-\sum_{i=k+1}^{j}x_{i}}{x_{j}-x_{k}}\right\rceil\!{=}\!\left\lceil\frac{jx_{j}-kx_{k}-(\beta_{j}-\beta_{k})}{x_{j}-x_{k}}\right\rceil\!\!.

That is, for any
1\leq k\leq j\leq d
we have that

C_{2}[k,j]=C[k,b^{*}_{k,j}]+C[b^{*}_{k,j},j]

is the sum of the variances in quantizing the points in
[x_{k},x_{j}]
using the quantization values
\left\{x_{k},x_{b^{*}_{k,j}},x_{j}\right\}
.

We can then use this method to halve the required number of invocations of Concave-1D by always using it to pick the
second-next
quantization value and computing the optimal quantization value in between directly. Our accelerated dynamic program is then given by:

\mathit{MSE}[i,j]=\begin{cases}\min\limits_{k\in[i,j]}\ \ \mathit{MSE}[i-2,k]+C_{2}[k,j]&\mbox{$i>3$}\\
C_{2}[1,j]&\mbox{$i=3$}\\
C[1,j]&\mbox{$i=2$}\end{cases}\ \ .

To prove the correctness of this approach when used with Concave-1D, we provide the following lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

C_{2}
satisfies the quadrangle inequality.

Proof.

The lemma claims that, for any
\texttt{a}\leq\texttt{b}\leq\texttt{c}\leq\texttt{d}
:

C_{2}[\texttt{a},\texttt{c}]+C_{2}[\texttt{b},\texttt{d}]\leq C_{2}[\texttt{a},\texttt{d}]+C_{2}[\texttt{b},\texttt{c}].

Recall that for any
a\leq c\in\left\{1,\ldots,d\right\}
, we denote

\displaystyle b^{*}_{a,c}=\argmin\limits_{b\in\left\{a,\ldots,c\right\}}C[a,b]+C[b,c].

We prove the lemma by a case analysis:

•

Case
b^{*}_{\texttt{b},\texttt{c}}\leq b^{*}_{\texttt{a},\texttt{d}}
.
In this case, we have that:

C_{2}(\texttt{a},\texttt{c})+C_{2}(\texttt{b},\texttt{d})\\
=C(\texttt{a},b^{*}_{\texttt{a},\texttt{c}})+C(b^{*}_{\texttt{a},\texttt{c}},\texttt{c})+C(\texttt{b},b^{*}_{\texttt{b},\texttt{d}})+C(b^{*}_{\texttt{b},\texttt{d}},\texttt{d})\\
\underset{(i)}{\leq}C(\texttt{a},b^{*}_{\texttt{b},\texttt{c}})+C(b^{*}_{\texttt{b},\texttt{c}},\texttt{c})+C(\texttt{b},b^{*}_{\texttt{a},\texttt{d}})+C(b^{*}_{\texttt{a},\texttt{d}},\texttt{d})\\
\underset{(ii)}{\leq}C(\texttt{b},b^{*}_{\texttt{b},\texttt{c}})+C(b^{*}_{\texttt{b},\texttt{c}},\texttt{c})+C(\texttt{a},b^{*}_{\texttt{a},\texttt{d}})+C(b^{*}_{\texttt{a},\texttt{d}},\texttt{d})\\

Here, the Inequality

Case
b^{*}_{\texttt{b},\texttt{c}}>b^{*}_{\texttt{a},\texttt{d}}

In this case, we have that:

6
Faster Approximate Solutions

All the above techniques find the optimal quantization values. We now show how the usage of
histograms
gives a controllable tradeoff between accuracy and speed. Intuitively, by replacing the vector with a histogram (which takes
O(d)
time to compute) of size
M+1
, we can reduce QUIVER’s computation time to
O(d+s\cdot M)
and get a good solution when
M
is not too small. In this section, we assume without loss of generality that
X
has more than
s
distinct values, as otherwise there is a trivial optimal solution with no error.

To that end, consider the discrete set

S=\left\{x_{1}+\ell\cdot\frac{x_{d}-x_{1}}{M}\mid\ell\in\left\{0,\ldots,M\right\}\right\}~.

We first stochastically quantize each coordinate
x_{i}
to a point in
S
. Namely, denoting
\ell_{i}=\left\lceil M\cdot\frac{x_{i}-x_{1}}{x_{d}-x_{1}}\right\rceil
, we round
x_{i}
to
x_{1}+\ell_{i}\cdot\frac{x_{d}-x_{1}}{M}
with probability

\frac{x_{i}-\left(x_{1}+\ell_{i}\cdot\frac{x_{d}-x_{1}}{M}\right)}{\frac{x_{d}-x_{1}}{M}}
and round it to
x_{1}+(\ell_{i}-1)\cdot\frac{x_{d}-x_{1}}{M}
otherwise.

We then compute the frequency vector of the rounded vector,
W\in\left\{0,\ldots,d\right\}^{M+1}
, i.e.,
W
is the vector that for each
\ell\in\left\{0,\ldots,M\right\}
counts how many coordinates were rounded to the value
x_{1}+\ell\cdot\frac{x_{d}-x_{1}}{M}
.

As we show, our algorithms can be adapted to work with
weighted vectors
, thus allowing solving the problem given
W
as input in time
O(s\cdot M)
. As the computation of
W
takes
O(d)
time, the overall runtime is then
O(d+s\cdot M)
.

We note that the introduction of the histogram introduces an error as we compute the optimal quantization value set on
W
instead of
X
. However, we show that the error is controllable, and by selecting the appropriate
M
value, the error does not increase significantly. Specifically, for any
M=\omega(\sqrt{d})
, the resulting solution has a sum of variances (for quantizing
X
) of
\texttt{opt}\cdot(1+o(1))+o(\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}^{2})
, where
opt
is the MSE of the optimal solution for
X
.
We note that for a constant
s
, the worst-case sum of variances is
\Omega(\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}^{2})
, and this is generally unavoidable except for very structured inputs. Accordingly, the additive
o(\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}^{2})
is generally insignificant.
To summarize, this allows us to compute a
1+o(1)
approximation in time
O(d)+\widetilde{O}(s\cdot\sqrt{d})
, e.g., by selecting
M=\sqrt{d}\cdot\log d
. In most practical settings, the
s
values that are of interest satisfy
s=O(\sqrt{d}/\log d)
, and thus our algorithm would have time and space complexities that are linear in
d
, which is optimal.

Analyzing the resulting error

We first observe that
x_{d}-x_{1}\leq\sqrt{2}\cdot\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}
, and thus the distance between every two consecutive points in
S
is at most
\frac{\sqrt{2}\cdot\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}}{M}
.

Each coordinate in
X
is stochastically quantized to a point in
S
; let us denote by
\widetilde{X}
the resulting vector. For a given coordinate, this process has a variance of at most
\frac{\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{2M^{2}}
(which is tight if a coordinate is exactly mid-range between points in
S
). As a result, the sum of variances of this process (without quantizing yet) is therefore no larger than
\frac{d\cdot\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{2M^{2}}
.

Our algorithm gives the optimal quantization values for
W
, and thus for
\widetilde{X}
. This means that quantizing
X
using the quantization values for
\widetilde{X}
is at least as accurate as first rounding it and then quantizing it.
We then use the following lemma, which we restate for completeness.

Lemma 6.1

(
Vargaftik et al. 2022
)
.

Consider two unbiased compression techniques
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
(i.e.,
\forall X:E[\mathcal{A}(X)]=E[\mathcal{B}(X)]=X
) with independent randomness. Then:

\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert\mathcal{A}(X)-X]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\right)\leq A\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert\mathcal{B}(X)-X]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\right)\leq B,

imply:

\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{A}(X))-X]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\right)\leq A+AB+B.

In our case,
A=\frac{d}{2M^{2}}
, and
B=\texttt{opt}/\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
is the sum of variances for optimal quantization for
\widetilde{X}
, normalized by the squared norm of
X
. Therefore, we get that the sum of variances is at most:

\frac{d\cdot\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{2M^{2}}+\texttt{opt}\cdot\left(1+\frac{d}{2M^{2}}\right).

By selecting
M=\omega(\sqrt{d})
, e.g.,
M{=}\sqrt{d}\cdot\log d
, we get a sum of variances of
\texttt{opt}\cdot(1+o(1))+o(\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}^{2})
.
The space and time complexities are then
O(d+s\cdot M)=O(d){+}\widetilde{O}(s{\cdot}\sqrt{d})
, i.e., linear for values of
s
that are of interest.

8
Discussion

We show that computing the optimal (or near-optimal) quantization values can be done on the fly, even for large input vectors, using our algorithms that asymptotically improve the runtime and space complexity of the state of the art. Similarly to previous works (e.g.,
Zhang et al. 2017
), we assume that the input vector is sorted. If it is not, one can sort the vector, bringing the exact solution complexity to
O(d\log d+s\cdot d)
. In practice, this sorting can be done on the GPU and is rarely the bottleneck; indeed, in
Appendix

C
we measure the time it takes to sort the vector on a T4 GPU, and also to quantize the vector after an AVQ outputs the optimal quantization values.

For example, the sorting and quantization time for a 1
M
-sized vector sums up to only 4ms where the runtime of Accelerated QUIVER is 250ms.

Finally, we note that the histogram solutions do not require the vector to be sorted and the time complexity remains
O(d+s\cdot M)
even for non-sorted inputs, potentially making them even more appealing compared to the exact ones. Moreover, the histogram calculation is GPU-friendly, and by offloading it to GPU we get that the time complexity of the CPU implementation can reduce to
O(s\cdot M)
, i.e., sublinear in the input vector size.

Appendix A
Generalizing Our Algorithms to Weighted Inputs

We now generalize our algorithms for processing sorted weighted inputs

Y=\left\langle(y_{1},w_{1}),\ldots,(y_{n},w_{n})\right\rangle
(where each entry has value
y_{\ell}
and weight
w_{\ell}
and
y_{1}\leq y_{2}\leq\ldots,y_{n}
).

Most of the algorithmic parts, including the Binary Search (
Algorithm

2
) and QUIVER (
Algorithm

3
) only require a revised method for computing
C
in constant time, which is achieved through the modified pre-processing procedure below. The accelerated method (
Algorithm

4
) needs some
C_{2}
to be computable in constant time, which is possible for the histogram use case, as we later show.

Pre-processing.

To allow constant time computation of
C
for weighted inputs we need another auxiliary array. Namely, we define the following:

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}={\sum_{(y,w)\in Y_{j}}w}\qquad\ \ \ ,\quad j\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}\ ,

\displaystyle\beta_{j}={\sum_{(y,w)\in Y_{j}}w\cdot y}\quad\ \ ,\quad j\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}\ ,

\displaystyle\gamma_{j}={\sum_{(y,w)\in Y_{j}}w\cdot y^{2}}\quad,\quad j\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}\ .

Then, we can then write:

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle[k,j]=\sum_{y_{\ell}\in[y_{k},y_{j}]}w\cdot(y_{j}-y_{\ell})(y_{\ell}-y_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{y_{\ell}\in(y_{k},y_{j}]}w\cdot(y_{j}-y_{\ell})(y_{\ell}-y_{k})

\displaystyle=y_{j}\cdot y_{k}\cdot\hskip-2.84526pt\sum_{y_{\ell}\in(y_{k},y_{j}]}w_{\ell}+(y_{j}-y_{k})\cdot\hskip-2.84526pt\sum_{y_{\ell}\in(y_{k},y_{j}]}w_{\ell}\cdot y_{\ell}

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt-\sum_{y_{\ell}\in(y_{k},y_{j}]}w_{\ell}\cdot y_{\ell}^{2}

\displaystyle=y_{j}\cdot y_{k}\cdot(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{k})+(y_{j}-y_{k})\cdot(\beta_{j}-\beta_{k})-(\gamma_{j}-\gamma_{k}).

Optimal quantization value.

We repeat the analysis for the weighted case.
Let us define the sum of weighted variances in the range
[y_{k},y_{j}]
as a function of
q
, given quantization values at
\left\{y_{k},q,y_{j}\right\}
:

\displaystyle Q(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y_{\ell}\in[y_{k},q]}w_{\ell}\cdot(q-y_{\ell})(y_{\ell}-y_{k})

\displaystyle+\sum_{y_{\ell}\in(q,y_{j}]}w_{\ell}\cdot(y_{j}-y_{\ell})(y_{\ell}-q).

This function is differentiable in
[y_{k},y_{j}]\setminus Y
, and we get:

\displaystyle\frac{dQ(q)}{dq}=\sum_{y_{\ell}\in[y_{k},q]}w_{\ell}\cdot(y_{\ell}-y_{k})-\sum_{y_{\ell}\in(q,y_{j}]}w_{\ell}\cdot(y_{j}-y_{\ell}).

Similarly,
Q(q)
is minimized at
u=\inf_{q}(\frac{dQ(q)}{dq}>0)
, where
u\in Y
.
Denote by
b^{*}_{k,j}
, where
b^{*}_{k,j}\in\left\{k,\ldots,j\right\}
the value such that
y_{b^{*}_{k,j}}=u
.
Therefore, we require

\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{b^{*}_{k,j}}w_{\ell}\cdot(y_{\ell}-y_{k})-\sum_{\ell={b^{*}_{k,j}}+1}^{j}w_{\ell}\cdot(y_{j}-y_{\ell})>0.

With some simplifications, this is equivalent to:

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{j}w_{\ell}\cdot y_{\ell}-y_{k}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{b^{*}_{k,j}}w_{\ell}-y_{j}\sum_{\ell={b^{*}_{k,j}}+1}^{j}w_{\ell}>0

\displaystyle(\beta_{j}-\beta_{k})-y_{k}(\alpha_{b^{*}_{k,j}}-\alpha_{k})-y_{j}(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{b^{*}_{k,j}})>0

\displaystyle\alpha_{b^{*}_{k,j}}>\frac{y_{j}\alpha_{j}-y_{k}\alpha_{k}+(\beta_{j}-\beta_{k})}{y_{j}+y_{k}}.

For our histogram use-case (i.e.,
n=M+1
), all the weights are integers and they sum up to
d
.
We thus find that:

\alpha_{b^{*}_{k,j}}=\left\lceil\frac{y_{j}\alpha_{j}-y_{k}\alpha_{k}+(\beta_{j}-\beta_{k})}{y_{j}+y_{k}}\right\rceil,

which gives

{b^{*}_{k,j}}=\alpha^{-1}\left(\left\lceil\frac{y_{j}\alpha_{j}-y_{k}\alpha_{k}+(\beta_{j}-\beta_{k})}{y_{j}+y_{k}}\right\rceil\right).

We can therefore compute
{b^{*}_{k,j}}
in constant time by storing the inverse mapping
\alpha^{-1}:\left\{1,\ldots,d\right\}\to\left\{1,\ldots,M+1\right\}
explicitly using
O(d)
space.

We also compare against ALQ
(
Faghri et al. 2020
)
, which fits the parameters of a truncated normal distribution to approximate the distribution of the input vector after normalizing it by its norm.
It then uses an iterative solution to approximate the optimal quantization values of the fitted distribution up to the desired precision. As suggested by the authors, we use ten iterations, which were shown to converge to the optimal quantization levels for the resulting (truncated normal) distribution.
