Titanium abundances in late-type stars
J. W. E. Mallinson, K. Lind, A. M. Amarsi, K. Youakim

Abstract
Context. Rapidly growing datasets from stellar spectroscopic surveys are providing unprecedented opportunities to analyse the chemical evolution history of our Galaxy. However, spectral analysis requires accurate modelling of synthetic stellar spectra for late-type stars, for which the assumption of local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE) has been shown to be insufficient in many cases. Errors associated with LTE can be particularly large for Ti I , which is susceptible to over-ionisation, particularly in metal-poor stars. Aims. The aims of this work are to study and quantify the 1D non-LTE effects on titanium abundances across the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram for a large sample of stars. Methods. A large grid of departure coefficients, β ν \beta_{\nu} , were computed on standard MARCS model atmospheres. The grid extends from 3000 ​ K 3000\,\mathrm{K} to 8000 ​ K 8000\,\mathrm{K} in T eff T_{\mathrm{eff}} , − 0.5 ​ dex -0.5\,\mathrm{dex} to + 5.5 ​ dex +5.5\,\mathrm{dex} in log ⁡ g \log{g} , and − 5.0 -5.0 to + 1.0 +1.0 in [ Fe / H ] \mathrm{[Fe/H]} , with non-LTE effects in this grid reaching up to 0.4 dex. This was used to compute abundance corrections that were subsequently applied to the LTE abundances of over 70 000 stars selected from the GALAH survey in addition to a smaller sample of literature Keck data for metal-poor dwarfs. Results. The non-LTE effects grow towards lower [ Fe / H ] \mathrm{[Fe/H]} , lower log ⁡ g \log{g} , and higher T eff T_{\mathrm{eff}} , with a minimum and maximum Δ ​ A ​ ( Ti ) Ti ​ I \Delta\,\mathrm{A(Ti)_{Ti\,I}} of 0.02 0.02 and 0.19 0.19 in the GALAH sample. For metal-poor giants, the non-LTE modelling reduces the average ionisation imbalance ( Δ I − II \Delta_{\textsc{I}-\textsc{II}}\, ) from − 0.11 ​ dex -0.11\,\mathrm{dex} to − 0.01 ​ dex -0.01\,\mathrm{dex} at [Fe/H] = − 1.7 -1.7 , and the enhancement in titanium abundances from Ti I lines results in a [ Ti / Fe ] \mathrm{[Ti/Fe]} versus [ Fe / H ] \mathrm{[Fe/H]} trend that more closely resembles the behaviour of Ti II at low metallicities. At higher metallicities, the results are limited by the precision of the GALAH DR3 LTE abundances and the effects are within the errors. For the most metal-poor dwarfs from the Keck sample, the average ionisation imbalance increases from − 0.1 -0.1 dex to + 0.2 +0.2 dex, a shortcoming that is consistent with previous 1D non-LTE studies and which we speculate could be related to 3D effects. Conclusions. Non-LTE effects on titanium abundances are significant. Neglecting them may alter our understanding of Galactic chemical evolution. We have made our grid of departure coefficients publicly available, with the caveat that the Ti abundances of metal-poor dwarfs need further study in 3D non-LTE.

Data from increasingly expansive stellar spectroscopic surveys have facilitated the advancement of our understanding of galactic chemical evolution (GCE) on a large scale
(
Jofré et al. 2019
)
. The imminent influx of new data from surveys such as WEAVE
(
Dalton et al. 2018
;
Jin et al. 2023
)
and 4MOST
(
de Jong et al. 2019
)
, which will gather of the order of
\sim 10^{7}
high-resolution spectra, will set the stage for unprecedented investigations of the stellar content in our Galaxy.

However, the impact and reliability of such studies are contingent on the accuracy of the applied spectral analysis methods and the underlying modelling of the spectral absorption lines.
Local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE) is a common assumption in the modelling of spectral lines in late-type stars, including our Sun. In the regime of LTE, collisional transitions dominate over radiative ones, and as such the populations of excited and ionised states can be described by the Saha and Boltzmann distributions
(
Mihalas & Athay 1973
;
Hubeny & Mihalas 2015
)
. This simplification offers the advantage of fast computation times, which is especially important when analysing large datasets with many spectra. However, this convenience comes at the cost of ignoring potentially important physics.

In the case of titanium, previous 1D LTE work has demonstrated that abundances depend heavily on the diagnostic species: neutral or singly ionised. These so-called ionisation imbalances can be particularly severe in metal-poor stars
(
Gratton & Sneden 1991
)
. For example, 1D LTE analyses of the metal-poor giant HD122563 have an ionisation imbalance of
\Delta_{\textsc{I}-\textsc{II}}\,
=-0.4
to
-0.45\,\mathrm{dex}

(
Sitnova et al. 2020
;
Mallinson et al. 2022
)
.

Non-LTE analyses have been shown to affect and often reduce this ionisation imbalance in many cases

(
Bergemann 2011
;
Sitnova et al. 2016
;
Sitnova et al. 2020
;
Mallinson et al. 2022
)
, with the exception of metal-poor dwarfs.
Departures from LTE can be taken into account by solving for the statistical equilibrium
(
Rutten 2003
, e.g.)
. This allows external radiation to impact the local medium, which is vital for the accurate modelling of Ti
I
; it is the minority species in late-type stars, and with an ionisation energy of
6.8\,\mathrm{eV,}
it has a number of continua in the blue, making it sensitive to over-ionisation. These effects are enhanced in metal-poor stars due to the lack of free electrons from metals such that there are fewer electron collisions to bring Ti
I
to LTE, and because there is less UV opacity and consequently a larger over-ionising flux. These effects on Ti
I
do not strongly impact the Ti
II
lines, however, given that that Ti
II
is typically the majority species in late-type stars; Ti
II
also has a higher ionisation energy of
13.6\,\mathrm{eV,}
which prohibits it from experiencing analogous over-ionisation effects.

Inelastic hydrogen collisions are typically a source of large uncertainty in non-LTE calculations, especially for metal-poor stars
(
Barklem 2016
)
.
Past studies, such as
Bergemann 2011
and
Sitnova et al. 2016
, have carried out non-LTE calculations for titanium using the Drawin recipe for hydrogen collisions
(
Steenbock & Holweger 1984
;
Lambert 1993
)
, while more recent studies
(
Sitnova et al. 2020
;
Mallinson et al. 2022
, e.g.)
have improved upon this by employing the asymptotic models of
Belyaev et al. 2017
and
Belyaev & Voronov 2018
.
Mallinson et al. 2022
also implemented a more extensive model atom and more recent data
from
Grumer & Barklem 2020
based on the asymptotic model of
Barklem 2016
, combined with the recipe from
Kaulakys 1991
as suggested in
Amarsi et al. 2018
;
Amarsi et al. 2019
.

The calculations in
Sitnova et al. 2020
and
Mallinson et al. 2022
show improvements in the ionisation balance in metal-poor giants, in the latter case reducing the ionisation imbalance of HD122563 from
\Delta_{\textsc{I}-\textsc{II}}\,
=-0.45\,\mathrm{dex}
in LTE to
\Delta_{\textsc{I}-\textsc{II}}\,
=-0.16\,\mathrm{dex}
in non-LTE. However, for the standard dwarf and subgiant stars considered in
Mallinson et al. 2022
, departures from LTE appeared to make the ionisation imbalance more severe. Nevertheless, that small-scale study of a few standard stars was not sufficient to conclusively assess the impact of non-LTE modelling across a broad range of parameter space, especially given that 1D hydrostatic model atmospheres were employed in combination with non-spectroscopic stellar parameters.

In this work we aim to more thoroughly study and quantify the 1D non-LTE effects on titanium abundances via the analysis of a large dataset.
Following
Amarsi et al. 2020
, we constructed a grid of departure coefficients
3
3

3

Our grid of departure coefficients is publicly available at
https://zenodo.org/record/10753497
.
(
Sect.
2
) and used it to correct line-by-line LTE titanium abundances for
70\,347
stars (
Sect.
3.1
) selected from the GALAH survey
(
Martell et al. 2017
;
Buder et al. 2021
)
and 13 metal-poor stars observed with Keck
(
Sneden et al. 2023
)
.
We used the results to assess the impact of our non-LTE modelling on titanium ionisation imbalances as well as on our understanding of its Galactic evolution (
Sect.
3
). We present our overall conclusions in
Sect.
4
.

2
Method

2.1
Model atom

The model atom used in this work is based on that constructed in
Mallinson et al. 2022
, and the full details are presented therein. The model
contains
459
Ti
I
levels,
127
Ti
II
levels, and the ground state of Ti
III
(
587
levels in total). All fine structure was collapsed. All Ti
I
and Ti
II
lines were radiatively coupled to the target ground states of the next ionisation stage.
In this work the model contains
5\,026
bound-bound radiative transitions, and the wavelength resolution of these lines was reduced such that the atom contained
63
thousand frequency points overall, compared to the
157
thousand in
Mallinson et al. 2022
; this had no noticeable impact on final abundances. Inelastic collisions with free electrons were based on
van Regemorter 1962
and
Cox 2000
, and inelastic collisions with neutral hydrogen were based on
Grumer & Barklem 2020
and
Kaulakys 1991
for Ti
I
, and on
Sitnova et al. 2020
for Ti
II
.

2.2
Grid of departure coefficients with
Balder

The departure coefficient,
\beta_{i}
, for a level
i
is the ratio of populations between LTE and non-LTE:

\beta_{i}=\frac{n_{i}}{n_{i}^{*}}.

(1)

These departure coefficients for titanium were calculated in the same way as discussed in
Amarsi et al. 2022

for iron, using the MPI-parallelised non-LTE radiative transfer code
Balder
, which is a modified version of
Multi3D

(
Leenaarts & Carlsson 2009
)
. The equation of state and background bound-bound, bound-free, and free-free opacities were calculated with the
Blue
package
(
Amarsi et al. 2016
)
.
Details of the included species are provided in
Zhou et al. 2023
. In particular, Thomson scattering from electrons, and Rayleigh scattering on the red wing of Ly-
\alpha
, were included, while the opacity from background lines was treated in pure absorption.

As described in
Amarsi et al. 2020

and
Amarsi et al. 2022
,
the calculations were carried out for
3\,756

MARCS
model atmospheres
(
Gustafsson et al. 2008
)
spanning

3\,000\,\mathrm{K}
to
8\ 000\,\mathrm{K}
in
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
,

-0.5\,\mathrm{dex}
to
5.5\,\mathrm{dex}
in
\log{g}
, and

-5.0\,\mathrm{dex}
to
+1.0\,\mathrm{dex}
in
\mathrm{[Fe/H]}
.
Plane-parallel models with microturbulence
\xi_{\mathrm{t}}=1.0\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}

were used for
\log{g}\geq 4.0
,
and spherical models with solar mass and
\xi_{\mathrm{t}}=2.0\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}

were used otherwise for consistency with
Amarsi et al. 2021
.
For each of these grid nodes,
the calculations with
Balder
were carried out for
different choices of titanium abundance, with

\mathrm{[Ti/Fe]}\in[-2.0,-1.0,-0.5,0.0,+0.5,+1.0,+2.0]
. The microturbulence used in
Balder
was fixed to that used for the construction
of the model atmosphere, namely
\xi_{\mathrm{t}}=1.0\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}

for
\log{g}\geq 4.0
, and
\xi_{\mathrm{t}}=2.0\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
otherwise.

It should be noted that the model atom
adopts level configurations and spectroscopic terms based on those from
Kurucz 2016
. Following
Amarsi et al. 2020
, the departure coefficients were mapped from these levels onto those given in NIST
(
Ralchenko & Kramida 2020
)
.

The departure coefficients from
Sect.
2.2
were constructed in such a way as to be compatible with the spectrum synthesis codes
SME
and
PySME

(
Wehrhahn et al. 2023
)
. For each Ti line,
PySME
searches for its upper and lower level in the grid of departure coefficients, and uses them to correct the LTE level populations before synthesising the line with the correct non-LTE line opacity and line source function
(
Piskunov & Valenti 2017
)
.

As in
Lind et al. 2022
,
PySME
was used to generate abundance corrections using the departure coefficients for more values of
\mathrm{[Ti/Fe]}
and
\xi_{\mathrm{t}}
. Namely, synthetic spectra were calculated for
\mathrm{[Ti/Fe]}
between
-2.0\,\mathrm{dex}
and
+2.0\,\mathrm{dex}
in steps of
0.25\,\mathrm{dex}
, and
\xi_{\mathrm{t}}=1\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
and

. These were performed on a subset of the
MARCS
model atmospheres;
Fig.
1
shows these grid nodes, as well as the selected GALAH stars (described in
Sect.
2.4
). For a given titanium line, equivalent widths were calculated in LTE and non-LTE and linearly interpolated to generate a grid of abundance corrections shown in
Fig.
12
. Finally, these abundance corrections were linearly interpolated onto the line-by-line LTE GALAH abundances, as functions of
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
,
\log{g}
,
\mathrm{[Fe/H]}
,
\xi_{\mathrm{t}}
, and abundance A
(\mathrm{Ti})_{\text{line, \,LTE}}
. The star selection is described in
Sect.
2.4
and the line selection is described in
Sect.
2.5
, below.

2.4
GALAH DR3 star selection

This work builds upon the few benchmark stars analysed in
Mallinson et al. 2022
and examines a subsample of
70\,347
stars, all of which have some measurement of titanium abundance from
either Ti
I
or Ti
II
(or both), out of the
588\,571
stars in GALAH DR3
(
Buder et al. 2021
)
.
These data contain
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
,
\log{g}
, and
\mathrm{[Fe/H]}
(both the initial atmospheric and final estimate of abundance, with the latter being used in this work),
\xi_{\mathrm{t}}
, and line-by-line LTE titanium abundances,
\mathrm{A\left(Ti\right)}_{\text{line,\,LTE}}
. The way in which these parameters were determined are described in
Buder et al. 2021
. To give a short summary, these parameters were determined by normalising the spectra based on some initial guess, and optimising the stellar parameters. Forming an initial
\log{g}
by the mass-luminosity relation,
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
,
\mathrm{[Fe/H]}
,
v_{\mathrm{broad}}
, and
v_{\mathrm{rad}}
are optimised with double-sided partial derivatives and
\chi^{2}
, updating
\log{g}
and
v_{\mathrm{mic}}
with each change, repeating this until the change in
\chi^{2}
was below 0.001 for each iteration. [Fe/H] was finally redetermined in non-LTE via analysing Fe lines directly (and this value of [Fe/H] is adopted for the present study).

The selected stars are shown in
Fig.
1
.
The selection was made by limiting to those stars with the most reliable parameters and highest signal-to-noise ratio. This was done in several steps. First, only the stars with quality flags of 0 for
\mathrm{[Fe/H]}
and
\mathrm{[Ti/Fe]}
(‘flag_fe_h’, ‘flag_ti_fe’, and ‘flag_ti2_fe’) and the stellar parameter flag ‘flag_sp’ were selected. Secondly, as recommended in the best practices for using the data from the GALAH consortium
4
4

4

https://www.galah-survey.org
, the average signal-to-noise ratio per pixel for the third charged couple device was limited to
30
or above (
\text{snr\_c3\_iraf}>30
) for very metal-poor stars (
\mathrm{[Fe/H]}<-1.5
), in order to keep enough low metallicity stars in the sample for a robust analysis, and average signal-to-noise ratio over all lines of
\mathrm{S/N}>70
for the stars at higher metallicities. Finally, only stars with at least two lines with a reduced equivalent width
\mathrm{W_{red,\lambda}}=\mathrm{log(W}_{\lambda}/\lambda)
in LTE between
-6.0<\mathrm{W_{red,\,\lambda}}<-4.9
were kept.

2.5
Line selection

As the GALAH reanalysis of absolute abundances,
\mathrm{A\left(Ti\right)}{}
, was done on a line-by-line basis, the line-by-line scatter can be used as another diagnostic of the non-LTE modelling.
The line selection strongly influences the final results, and care was taken to try to select the best available lines out of those considered by GALAH.

Ti
I
lines were initially limited to those that are reported as unblended in
Heiter et al. 2020
in the Sun and Arcturus, although the 6599.11Å line was also removed due to a lack of titanium abundance data in the available stars.

In the case of Ti
II
, no lines were reported as unblended, so lines were instead restricted to those that were reported as uncertain.
Of the remaining available lines, 4865.6
\AA
can be strongly affected by H
\beta
, causing blending and continuum normalisation, primarily in dwarfs. However, to keep the line list identical for all stars, it was removed entirely. To increase the line list to more than two lines, the least visibly blended line in the Sun and Arcturus that was not examined in
Heiter et al. 2020
, at 4719.51
\AA
, was added.

The relative abundances
\mathrm{[Ti/H]}
and
\mathrm{[Ti/Fe]}
were determined on a line-by-line basis using the GALAH abundances, with non-LTE solar corrections applied to their values when in non-LTE. Average values of
\mathrm{[Ti/H]}
were calculated for different species,
\mathrm{[Ti/H]}_{\mathrm{{Ti\textsc{I}}}}
and
\mathrm{[Ti/H]}_{\mathrm{{Ti\textsc{II}}}}
,
via a mean of the available lines for a given star weighted by their error. Weak lines and strong lines were cut using the reduced equivalent
width criterion of
-6.0<\mathrm{W_{red,\,\lambda}}<-4.9
. The upper limit prevents the use of saturated lines, which would have both given an under-abundance and increase the sensitivity to microturbulence error. The lower limit reduces the impact of noise fluctuation.
The un-normalised weights were given by
w_{\text{line}}=1/\sigma_{\text{line}}^{2}
, using the uncertainties provided by GALAH.
Finally, where illustrated in
Sect.
3.1
below, the absolute abundances use the non-LTE line-averaged solar correction:
0.052\,\mathrm{dex}
for Ti
I
and
-0.002\,\mathrm{dex}
for Ti
II
.

3
Results and discussion

3.1
Non-LTE effects across the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram

Figures
2
–
5
show the line-averaged non-LTE corrections of Ti
I
and
Ti
II
as a function of stellar parameters.
The non-LTE effects on both species grow in severity towards lower metallicities, lower surface gravities, and higher effective temperatures, qualitatively similar to that found for iron in
Lind et al. 2012
.

For Ti
I
, the abundance corrections rise to
+0.18\,\mathrm{dex}
on average for giants at
\mathrm{[Fe/H]}=-2.0
(
Fig.
5
).
The analysis of benchmark stars in
Mallinson et al. 2022
suggests that the dwarfs should show a similar trend with
\mathrm{[Fe/H]}
as the giants, namely increasing non-LTE abundance corrections with decreasing metallicity. However, the dwarfs in this sample only extend down to
\mathrm{[Fe/H]}=-1.0
,
reflecting the low number of very metal-poor dwarfs in the dataset of
Buder et al. 2021

\mathrm{[Fe/H]}=-1.0

the abundance corrections reach

The abundance corrections for Ti

This is because of the majority status of Ti

The corrections are typically

However, at the lowest metallicities around
\mathrm{[Fe/H]}=-2.0

consistent with
Mallinson et al. 2022

(
Sitnova et al. 2020

.

Additionally, the impact of titanium abundance itself on the non-LTE corrections for Ti

Mallinson et al. 2022

-1.00
¡ [Ti/Fe] ¡
0.75
the correction changes by only 0.05 dex for the Sun, and 0.09 dex for HD122563. Due to the large difference in abundance, this is not a significant change and within the error of this paper.

3.2
Ionisation imbalances

3.2.1
GALAH stars

The difference in the predictions based on Ti
I
and Ti
II
is a long-standing problem in
studies of titanium abundances, with Ti
I
predicting lower abundances than Ti
II
.
Neglected non-LTE effects are a contributing factor to this problem
(
Bergemann 2011
)
.
For both dwarfs and giants, the abundance corrections become larger towards lower metallicities for Ti
I
,
while remaining rather close to zero for Ti
II
.

Consequently, the ionisation imbalance is reduced in non-LTE in giants from the GALAH sample at low metallicities, as shown in
Fig.
7
.
For giants in LTE, the ionisation imbalance reaches on average
\Delta_{\textsc{I}-\textsc{II}}\,
=-0.11,\mathrm{dex}
at
\mathrm{[Fe/H]}\approx-1.7
.
In non-LTE, this is reduced to
\Delta_{\textsc{I}-\textsc{II}}\,
=-0.01
, demonstrating a non-LTE impact on the imbalance of
0.10
dex.

For dwarfs in the GALAH sample, departures from LTE do not significantly alter the ionisation imbalance on average, with an imbalance of
\Delta_{\textsc{I}-\textsc{II}}\,
=-0.000
for LTE and
\Delta_{\textsc{I}-\textsc{II}}\,
=0.001
in non-LTE, at
\mathrm{[Fe/H]}\approx-0.9
.
It should be noted that this results in a solar abundance correction of
0.05\,\mathrm{dex}
for Ti
I

and
-0.002\,\mathrm{dex}
for Ti
II
(see
Table
1
and
Sect.
2.5
);
the square bracket notation thus hides an ionisation imbalance of around
0.05\,\mathrm{dex}
.

3.2.2
Keck stars

Due to the lack of low metallicity dwarfs available in the GALAH data sample, an additional set of stars were taken from
Sneden et al. 2023
, consisting of 13 dwarfs of the original 37 due to line selection and saturation; the majority of which have a metallicity between
-3.0<\mathrm{\mathrm{[Fe/H]}}<-2.0,
as shown in
Fig.
8
. The spectra of these stars were obtained with Keck I/HIRES spectrograph
(
Vogt et al. 1994
)
, and originally used in
Boesgaard et al. 2011
. The lines available for these stars do not overlap with those in the GALAH data, and the majority instead are below
4000\AA
. The inclusion of these lines dramatically reduces the abundance of both species of titanium.

In HD84937,
Lawler et al. 2013
found a strong reduction in Ti
I
abundances of around
0.15-0.2
dex when moving to the near-UV range of
\lambda=3100-3700\AA
.
Wood et al. 2013
finds similarly for the high excitation energy lines. The overabundance of UV lines in the Ti
II
line list may result in the heavily positive LTE ionisation imbalance
\Delta_{\textsc{I}-\textsc{II}}\,
seen in
Sneden et al. 2023
that is in disagreement with the expectations from previous work that focused on the longer optical wavelength lines
(
Bergemann 2011
;
Sitnova et al. 2020
;
Mallinson et al. 2022
)
. The cause of this may be related to the Balmer continuum opacity
(
Wood et al. 2013
)
. In
Sneden et al. 2016
, the removal of these lines raises the Ti
II
-based abundance and results in a smaller imbalance, similar to that found for HD84937 in
Mallinson et al. 2022
.

By including only unsaturated and reasonably strong lines (
-6.0<\mathrm{W_{red,\,\lambda}}<-4.8
) above this limit (i.e.
>3700\AA,
as shown in Table
2
), only 13 of the original 34 stars in
Sneden et al. 2023
can be analysed. However, this gives the ionisation imbalance for dwarfs at these metallicities a closer agreement with
Mallinson et al. 2022
and remains close to 0 in LTE, worsening in non-LTE and prompting 3D analysis to further investigate.

3.2.3
3D impact

The inclusion of 3D effects is a potential solution to the ionisation imbalance of Ti. To estimate its impact in LTE, we employed four snapshots of two 3D
STAGGER
radiation-hydrodynamic models
(
Magic et al. 2013
)
. The models pertain to metal-poor dwarfs with
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
= 6000K,
\log{g}
= 4.5, [Fe/H] =
-3.0
and [Fe/H] =
-2.0
, respectively. [Ti/Fe] was set to 0.4, and we adopted
\xi=1
km s
-1
in the corresponding 1D calculations. The spectral lines listed in Table 2 were computed both in 1D and 3D under LTE conditions using the
SCATE

(
Hayek et al. 2011
)
code. The mean 3D effects (i.e. 3D minus 1D abundance differences) were found to be
-0.41
dex and 0.01 dex for Ti
I
and Ti
II
, respectively, in the [Fe/H] =
-3
model, and
-0.36
dex and
-0.05
dex, respectively, in the [Fe/H] =
-2
model.

For giants characterised by
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
= 4000K,
\log{g}
= 2.0, and [Fe/H] =
-2.0
, a considerably lesser impact of
-0.04
and +0.06 is found for Ti
I
and Ti
II
, respectively. These findings align with prior research
(
Dobrovolskas et al. 2013
)
that reports a large abundance change in 3D for resonant lines only at around
-0.45
dex at [Fe/H] =
-3
, but that steeply decreases to -0.05 dex in abundance change as the excitation energy increases to 2eV.

These results underscore the significant influence of 3D effects on Ti
I
in the inverse direction of non-LTE, offering a potential resolution to the dwarf metallicity discrepancy at lower metallicities. However, the interaction between 3D and non-LTE is not necessarily additive
(
Lagae et al. 2023
)
and a full 3D non-LTE investigation would be needed to make strong conclusions.

3.4
Departures from LTE for different stellar parameters

The impact that stellar parameters have on the magnitude of the non-LTE effect is shown in
Fig.
12
, computed using the
MARCS
atmospheres at specific grid points with
(
PySME), and plotted as the average non-LTE impact on A(Ti), averaged over the chosen GALAH Ti
I
lines.
As is found in previous work, a decrease in metallicity increases the magnitude of
\Delta
A(Ti)
{}_{\mathrm{Ti\,I}}
in most cases, particularly between
-3<\mathrm{[Fe/H]}<0
.
Similarly, a decreasing
\log{g}
results in a larger non-LTE effect as collisional rates — scaling with the number density of both titanium atoms and the ionising impact species — reduce faster than radiative rates, which scales only with the number density of titanium.
As seen in
Fig.

until around

Sitnova et al. 2016
, the increasing temperature begins to reduce the non-LTE effect up to our limit of 7000K. The exact temperature at which this decline occurs varies with
\log{g}
and
\mathrm{[Fe/H]}
. Of the available grid points, the most extreme non-LTE effects range from -0.004 (at
T_{\mathrm{eff}}=4000
K,
\log{g}=2.5
, [Fe/H]
=0.5
) to 0.442 dex(at
T_{\mathrm{eff}}=5000K
,
\log{g}=1.0
, [Fe/H]
=-4.0
).

3.5
The Galactic evolution of titanium

Precision measurements of chemical abundances are crucial to accurately interpreting and constraining theories of GCE. While titanium is formally considered an iron peak element, it often behaves as an alpha element in observations
(
Zhao et al. 2016
;
Kobayashi et al. 2020
)
Thus, it is useful in disentangling Galactic components and tracing back their evolution histories. For example, [Ti/Fe] can be used as a chemical tracer to separate the chemical thin and thick discs, stellar halo and accreted stellar populations
(
Bensby et al. 2005
;
Bensby et al. 2014
;
Mashonkina et al. 2019
, e.g.)
. In addition, the Galactic Anticenter Stellar Structure, or ‘Monoceros Ring’, has been shown to exhibit abundance patterns in [Ti/Fe] and [Y/Fe] that are similar to those of Sagittarius stars and distinct from Milky Way stars, allowing for constraints to be placed on the origin of this structure
(
Chou et al. 2010
)
.

Interestingly, some recent GCE models show that [Ti/Fe] is under-predicted by
\sim 0.4
dex over a wide metallicity range when compared to observational data
(
Kobayashi et al. 2006
;
Romano et al. 2010
;
Kobayashi et al. 2020
)
, even in non-LTE
(
Mashonkina et al. 2019
)
.
It has been shown that [Ti/Fe] levels can be increased by including yields from asymmetric 2D models of high-entropy, jet-like ejecta from hypernovae
(
Maeda & Nomoto 2003
;
Tominaga 2009
)
in these GCE models
(
Sneden et al. 2016
;
Kobayashi et al. 2020
, see model K15, as presented in)
, but high precision measurements of [Ti/Fe] are needed to constrain the parameters of these ejecting supernovae. Notably, the non-LTE corrections presented in this work have shown that average giant [Ti/Fe] abundances measured with Ti
I
become larger by
\sim 0.1
dex as compared to 1D-LTE analyses, thereby exacerbating the discrepancy between models and observations, particularly at low metallicities of [Fe/H] ¡
-0.7
.

In a study designed to investigate the impact of modelling assumptions on GCE yields,
Côté et al. 2017
emphasise the significant influence of the choice of stellar yields on the trends in chemical evolution. With different stellar models within their set of stellar yields, they substantially reduce the titanium underprediction of
Côté et al. 2016
and
Kobayashi et al. 2020
and successfully replicate an approximately +0.25 enhancement in [Ti/Fe] at low metallicities, within the framework of a one-zone model, and consistent with observations in the Sculptor dwarf galaxy.
However, they point out that these abundance trends can be realised through various combinations of input parameters, making it challenging to distinguish among several degenerate models, a caveat that was also described in previous work
(
Romano & Starkenburg 2013
)
.

Clearly, the abundance trends from these models are highly dependent on the choice of input parameters and the adopted stellar yields. This implies a fundamental limitation in one-zone GCE models, which cannot be overcome by simply increasing the precision of observed abundance data.

Therefore, while the non-LTE corrections reduce the discrepancy between abundances measured using Ti
I
and Ti
II
for giants at low metallicities, they are not sufficient to offer further insight needed to constrain GCE models. They nevertheless emphasise the need for improved precision in theoretical yields for these models.

4
Conclusion

This study is the first to investigate the impact of 1D non-LTE effects on titanium abundances across a large sample of stars, building upon previous work that had primarily examined a small number of benchmark stars. It was expected that larger non-LTE effects would be found for Ti I, in metal-poor stars, and in giants: all three of these predictions are confirmed in this investigation. This resulted in a reduced ionisation imbalance for the metal-poor giants, a slight reduction in the disagreement between dwarf and giant titanium abundances at lower metallicities, and a shift in the [Ti/Fe] trend of Ti
I
to more closely match that of Ti
II
at low metallicities. This takes observations further away from predictions by some GCE models of titanium
(
Kobayashi et al. 2020
, and references therein)
. Overall, this suggests that the inclusion of the departure coefficient grid and correcting for non-LTE effects results in more accurate Ti
I
abundances for giants at lower metallicities, although this is dependent on the line selection.

Due to the small sample of metal-poor dwarfs and the relatively low accuracy of the GALAH sample, the limitations of this analysis are apparent. In
Mallinson et al. 2022
, metal-poor dwarfs were seen to have their imbalance worsened by the inclusion of non-LTE, a conclusion that is reinforced by our analysis of the
Sneden et al. 2023
sample in this work. An investigation of 3D non-LTE effects for Ti in metal-poor stars may shed further light on the problem. Additionally, the low number of lines and their quality — particularly for Ti
II
— further limit the reliability of these results.

Finally, there were larger departures from LTE towards higher effective temperatures and lower surface gravities, as well as lower metallicities. However, at lower surface gravities of
\log{g}<3.5
, increasing the temperature beyond
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx 5500
K reduced the departures from LTE. Likewise, a lower metallicity can occasionally result in lower non-LTE effects in all lines analysed, primarily at low temperatures and high surface gravity. In summary, we find the non-LTE corrections reach up to
+0.41
dex at
T_{\mathrm{eff}}=5000
K,
\log{g}=1.0
, [Fe/H]
=-4.0
.
