Using ChatGPT for Data Science Analyses
Ozan Evkaya, Miguel de Carvalho

Abstract
As a result of recent advancements in Generative AI, the field of Data Science is prone to various changes. The way practitioners construct their Data Science workflows is now irreversibly shaped by recent advancements, particularly by tools like OpenAI’s Data Analysis plugin. While it offers powerful support as a quantitative co-pilot, its limitations demand careful consideration in empirical analyses. This paper assesses the potential of ChatGPT for Data Science analyses, illustrating its capabilities for data exploration and visualization, as well as for commonly used supervised and unsupervised modeling tasks. While we focus here on how the Data Analysis plugin can serve as a co-pilot for the Data Science workflow, its broader potential for automation is implicit throughout.

1
Introduction

Machines have been key to the production of statistics since at least the 19th century. In a
paper read before the Royal Statistical Society in 1894, Herman Hollerith introduced his invention,
the ‘Tabulating Machine’. Prior to the use of this machine, it took about 10 years to process the
collected data from the 1880 US Census to arrive at a final report. Quoting
Hollerith (1894)
:

“
While engaged in work in the tenth census, that of 1880, […] I found, for example, that while we had collected the information regarding the conjugal condition of our 50 000 000 inhabitants, we were unable to compile this information even in its simplest form, so that, until the census of 1890, we never even knew the proportion of our population that was single, married, and widowed.
”

The ‘Tabulating Machine’ was able to process data recorded on punch cards, and thanks to it the 1890 census was completed in only 18 months and within a much smaller budget. Although much has changed since Hollerith’s invention, there was no turning back: The use of machines for handling and modeling data continued throughout the 20th century and has been increasing at a
steep rate in the 21st century with the rise of AI.

This note introduces the latest contribution to that space: OpenAI’s Data Analysis (DA) extension for ChatGPT. We provide an outlook of its performance across various tasks; the walkthrough covers initiating the software and assessing its capabilities in exploring, visualizing, and extracting insights from data. ChatGPT’s DA provides a comprehensive Python-based toolbox, supports
memory-intensive operations, and leverages the strengths of LLMs (Large Language Models)
Mollick (2023)
. Also, it incorporates a Python interpreter into the ChatGPT environment for on-the-fly
code execution, and to ensure maximum security all computing occurs within a firewall-protected
sandbox.

The growing interest in automating Data Science is well documented (e.g.,
De Bie et al. (2022)
,
and references therein), and tools like the DA plugin offer strong potential to streamline workflows.
Below, we illustrate how it can act as a co-pilot for Data Science tasks. Although this is not our
main focus, its broader potential for automation is implicit and worth keeping in mind.

Although LLMs remain prone to hallucinations, biases, and inaccuracies, they nonetheless have
the potential to become central tools in the statistical software landscape; indeed, the significant
promise of LLMs in Data Science has been highlighted recently by
Tu et al. (2023)
. In particular,
DA enables even non-programmers to run code and obtain elaborate analyses. Despite significant
progress, we argue that it is key to emphasize that no AI-powered statistical software should operate
without human critique and oversight, nor should it be considered a complete substitute for the
skills of a professional data analyst.

Both supervised and unsupervised learning tasks will be covered in this review; for technical
details on the methods covered here see, for instance,
Murphy (2022)
;
Murphy (2023)
. A publicly available
laptop
price dataset from Kaggle
Kushwaha (2023)
is used for exploratory data analysis
and visualizations, while the publicly available
duke_forest
dataset from the
openintro
R package
Çetinkaya-Rundel et al. (2023)
is employed for the modeling examples. While leading the conversation with DA, we primarily focused on simple prompts and on following the suggestions provided
by DA.

We start with exploratory analyses and visualizations in Section 2. The next sections are dedicated to supervised and unsupervised learning tasks. Lastly, the paper ends with some closing
remarks. All statistical analyses and comments below in bluish white boxes, figures, and code were
generated by ChatGPT’s DA. Throughout, we will use a star rating system to score statements
and analyses produced by DA. In addition to this rating system, we briefly highlight the impact of
prompting and repeated prompting over some tasks.

Closing Remarks

We have presented a hands-on review that examines the strengths and limitations of one of the
latest additions to the statistical software toolbox: OpenAI’s DA plugin.

The history of statistical software is extensive and multifaceted, with various aspects detailed in

Chambers (2017)
. Despite its current limitations, GPT has the potential to become a key player in
this landscape and to augment traditional statistical tools, providing researchers and practitioners
with unprecedented analytical capabilities.

Overall, the DA plugin can be useful for exploratory purposes. Yet, the modeling component
requires a detailed understanding and the application of more specific prompts along with domain
expertise. Without these, the generated outputs may be misleading. This is especially true for
novice learners, for whom the use of such a tool without meticulous supervision could inadvertently
hinder their learning process.

This note highlights the potential of the DA plugin to work as a co-pilot in Data Science workflows. While not the central focus here, the illustrations above demonstrate its potential to streamline and partially automate key steps in the process. At this stage, tools such as the DA plugin are
better suited to assistance-style automation rather than full automation. For certain Data Science
tasks, system-level prompting with targeted instructions may help address some of the issues noted
above. Yet, for more complex tasks, a multi-faceted and hybrid deployment of these tools is likely
required within an AI-augmented Data Science workflow.

In a well-known quote, world-renowned statistician Brian Ripley once claimed:

“Let’s not kid ourselves: the most widely used piece of software for statistics is Excel.”

Ripley (2002)
.

Since then, modern statistical computing tools—such as Julia, Python, and R—have been taking
over, but we might be reaching another paradigm shift. The interface between LLMs and statistical
models has been effectively established by ChatGPT’s DA plugin. Yet, as illustrated above, users
should critically assess its recommendations and outputs. Equipping the Data Science workflow with
appropriate computational components is essential to unlock new opportunities in automation. In
this regard, integrating multi-agent frameworks and leveraging established systems such as AutoML
may play a pioneering role in shaping the future of Data Science. Crucially, such efforts must involve
not only the use of LLMs but also sustained expert oversight

Our focus has been on illustrating the DA toolkit, but a full evaluation would require analyses

Appendix A: Further Literature

Some recent papers focus on how generative AI is being applied to the various Data Science tasks—including the impact on traditional workflows (from data wrangling and exploratory analysis to modeling and decision-making), as well as the challenges regarding accuracy, interpretability, and ethical use. Some relevant papers include:

(i) DataSciBench:

Zhang et al. [2025]
provides a benchmark to evaluate LLM capabilities in Data Science, DataSciBench. Their framework comprises three key procedures: prompt definition and collection, response integration and validation, and LLM evaluation; they rely on six Data Science tasks, including: (i) Data Cleaning & Preprocessing, (ii) Data Exploration & Statistical Understanding, (iii) Visualization, (iv) Predictive Modelling, (v) Statistical Modelling, (vi) Data Mining & Pattern Recognition and (vii) Interpretability & Report Generation.

(ii) Text2Analysis:

He et al. [2023]

proposed a Text2Analysis benchmark for tabular data analysis to conduct advanced analysis tasks that go beyond the SQL-compatible operations. They introduce five annotation methods to improve data quality, and worked on four Data Science tasks, including: (i) Rudimentary Operations (such as group by, aggregation, filter, and sort), (ii) Basic Insights incorporates value-based ranking, group attribution, time-series trends, monotonicity, outlier detection, and unimodality, (iii) Forecasting and (iv) Chart Generation.

(iii) Infiagent-dabench:

Hu et al. [2024]

introduced an InfiAgent-DABench benchmark for Data Science tasks, which requires agents to interact with an execution environment to end-to-end solve complex Data Science tasks. They focused on: (i) Comprehensive Data Preprocessing (12.0%), (ii) Summary Statistics (24.0%), (iii) Feature Engineering (13.3%), (iv) Correlation Analysis (19.2%), (v) Machine Learning (5.1%), (vi) Distribution Analysis (17.1%) and (vii) Outlier Detection (9.3%).

(iv) DA-Code:
The DA-Code benchmark is designed by
Huang et al. [2023]
to assess LLMs on challenging, real, and diverse Data Science tasks. This study evaluated nine LLMs within an agent framework, considering tasks grouped under the following categories: (i) Data Wrangling, (ii) Machine Learning, (iii) Data Manipulation, (iv) Data Insights, (v) Visualization and (vi) Statistical Analysis.

(v) StatQA:

Zhu et al. [2024]
introduced StatQA, a benchmark designed for statistical analysis tasks. StatQA comprises 11,623 examples tailored to evaluate LLMs’ proficiency in statistical tasks and their applicability assessment capabilities. Overall, five typical categories of statistical tasks are created, ranging over (i) Correlation Analysis, (ii) Contingency Table Test, (iii) Distribution Compliance Test, (iv) Variance Test and (v) Descriptive Statistics.

Selected Comments on Repeated Prompting

Our experiments highlight the inherent randomness of LLMs such as ChatGPT’s DA plug-in, showing that zero-shot prompts can yield variable results across tasks, even when repeated. While some tasks, like data visualization, produce outputs that are often unreliable or difficult to interpret, performance generally declines as task complexity increases, particularly when multiple steps are required. These findings emphasize the need for human oversight in automated data analysis, since certain parts of the workflow may require user confirmation or expert judgment, especially for interpretative tasks and visualizations, where uncontrolled automation risks misleading outcomes.
