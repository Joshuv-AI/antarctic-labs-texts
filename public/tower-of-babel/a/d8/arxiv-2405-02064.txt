Abstract
. For bounded domains Ω \Omega with Lipschitz boundary Γ \Gamma , we investigate boundary value problems for elliptic operators with variable coefficients of fourth order subject to Wentzell (or dynamic) boundary conditions. Using form methods, we begin by showing general results for an even wider class of operators of type 𝒜 = ( B ∗ ​ B 0 − 𝒩 𝔟 ​ B γ ) , {\mathcal{A}}=\begin{pmatrix}B^{*}B&0\\ -{\mathscr{N}}_{\mathfrak{b}}B&\gamma\end{pmatrix}, where B B is associated to a quadratic form 𝔟 {\mathfrak{b}} and 𝒩 b {\mathscr{N}}_{b} an abstractly defined co-normal Neumann trace. Even in this general setting, we prove generation of an analytic semigroup on the product space ℋ := L 2 ​ ( Ω ) × L 2 ​ ( Γ ) {\mathcal{H}}:=L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Gamma) . Using recent results concerning weak co-normal traces, we apply our abstract theory to the elliptic fourth-order case and are able to fully characterize the domain in terms of Sobolev regularity for operators in divergence form B = − div Q ∇ B=-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla with Q ∈ C 1 , 1 ​ ( Ω ¯ , ℝ d × d ) Q\in C^{1,1}(\overline{\Omega},{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}) , also obtaining Hölder-regularity of solutions. Finally, we also discuss asymptotic behavior and (eventual) positivity.

1.
Introduction

Wentzell, or dynamic boundary conditions, appear in a multitude of physical applications and pose a mathematically challenging problem. Given a bounded domain
\Omega
with boundary
\Gamma
, they model the interchange of free energy of a physical system between
\Omega
and
\Gamma
. The main issue with modeling this interchange is that the energy flux is represented by an integral over the domain, which cannot “see” the boundary as it is a set of Lebesgue measure zero. This is usually resolved by considering functions in a product space, e.g.,
{\mathcal{H}}:=L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Gamma)
, and a related operator
{\mathcal{A}}
for which the action in the interior of the domain and on the boundary is decoupled (cf.
[
AE96
,
AMPR03
,
Eng03
]
). The connection between interior and boundary is then encoded by a coupling condition in the definition of the domain of
{\mathcal{A}}
.

A detailed discussion of a physical interpretation of these boundary conditions in comparison to classical ones can be found in
[
Gol06
]
for the two most famous PDE, the heat and the wave equation. Other instances where these boundary conditions occur are the Stefan problem with surface tension (see
[
EPS03
, Section 1]
), and climate models including coupling between the deep ocean and the surface (see
[
DT08
, Section 2]
) where they incorporate the external energy transported into the ocean by the sun. Furthermore, they are used in the Cahn–Hilliard equation describing spinodal decomposition of binary polymer mixtures (see
[
RZ03
, Section 1]
) in order to model effects close to the boundary, e.g., that one of the agents is more attracted to the boundary than the other, which might lead to further separation effects. Contrary to the first examples whose leading part is given by (variations of) the Laplacian, the Cahn–Hilliard equation is based on the Bi-Laplacian, an operator of order 4 which fits into the setting of the present work.

We are going to consider a general class of operators. As prototype and main application, we study the following system of fourth order:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+B(\alpha B)u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\textnormal{ in }(0,\infty)\times\Omega,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}B(\alpha B)u-\beta\partial^{Q}_{\nu}(\alpha B)u-\beta\delta\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}(\alpha B)u-\gamma\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\textnormal{ on }(0,\infty)\times\Gamma,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\partial^{Q}_{\nu}u+\delta\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\textnormal{ on }(0,\infty)\times\Gamma,

(1.4)

\displaystyle u|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=u_{0}

\displaystyle\textnormal{ in }\Omega.

Here
B
is given by
B=-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla
, where
Q\in C^{1,1}(\overline{\Omega},{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d})
is uniformly positive definite and
\Omega
is a Lipschitz domain. Let
\partial_{\nu}^{Q}
denote its corresponding co-normal derivative given by
\left\langle\nu,Q\nabla u\right\rangle
(cf. (
3.2
) below), and assume
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta
to be bounded, real-valued functions. The precise smoothness assumption of the coefficients will be specified later on (cf. Hypotheses
2.9
and
3.1
).
In (
1.1
)–(
1.4
), it is implicitly assumed that the initial value
u_{0}
is sufficiently smooth to have a trace on the boundary and that this trace is used as an initial condition for
u
on the boundary.

Note that, as Equation (
1.1
) is of fourth order with respect to
x\in\Omega
, we have to impose two boundary conditions. Here, we have chosen the Robin boundary condition (
1.3
) in addition to the Wentzell boundary condition (
1.2
).

The main mathematical challenge in tackling Wentzell boundary conditions lies in the fact that the elliptic operator that governs the equation in the interior itself appears in the boundary condition, and the standard condition
B(\alpha B)u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is not sufficient to guarantee existence of the trace. In order to decouple this system and circumvent this issue, we rewrite the Wentzell boundary condition (
1.2
) as a dynamic boundary condition using
B(\alpha B)u=-\partial_{t}u
from (
1.1
). Then we rename
u
to
u_{1}
and replace the time derivative
\partial_{t}u_{1}
in the boundary condition by the time derivative
\partial_{t}u_{2}
of an independent function
u_{2}
that lives on the boundary. Even though
u_{2}
is formally independent of
u_{1}
, we think of
u_{2}
as the trace of
u_{1}
; this condition will be incorporated into the domain of our operator
D({\mathcal{A}})
, later. We thus obtain the following decoupled version of (
1.1
)–(
1.4
):

(1.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{1}+B(\alpha B)u_{1}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\textnormal{ in }(0,\infty)\times\Omega,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{2}+\beta\partial^{Q}_{\nu}(\alpha B)u_{1}+\beta\delta\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}(\alpha B)u_{1}+\gamma u_{2}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\textnormal{ on }(0,\infty)\times\Gamma,

(1.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}^{Q}u_{1}+\delta u_{2}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\textnormal{ on }(0,\infty)\times\Gamma,

(1.8)

\displaystyle u_{1}|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=u_{1,0}

\displaystyle\textnormal{ in }\Omega,

(1.9)

\displaystyle u_{2}|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=u_{2,0}

\displaystyle\textnormal{ on }\Gamma.

Note that, as
u_{2}
is independent of
u_{1}
, we have to impose an additional initial condition for
u_{2}
. If, however, the initial value
u_{0}
in (
1.4
) is smooth enough, we can put
u_{1,0}=u_{0}
and
u_{2,0}=u_{0}|_{\Gamma}
.
However, this coupling condition for the initial value is not mandatory. The Hilbert space theory established below will allow to prescribe non-continuous initial data, even the extreme case of
u_{1,0}=0
and
u_{2,0}\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
arbitrary is allowed. This is especially useful to model situations where in the beginning the entire energy only lives on the boundary and slowly dissipates into the interior of the domain over time.
Rewritten as a Cauchy problem, for
\mathcal{u}=(u_{1},u_{2})\in{\mathcal{H}}
we obtain

(1.10)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{u}+{\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{u}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\textnormal{ for }\mathcal{u}(t,\cdot)\textnormal{ in }{\mathcal{H}},

(1.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{u}|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{u}_{0}

\displaystyle\textnormal{ for }\mathcal{u}_{0}\in{\mathcal{H}},

where
{\mathcal{A}}
is given by

(1.12)

\begin{pmatrix}B(\alpha B)&0\\
\beta(\partial_{\nu}^{Q}(\alpha B)+\delta\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}(\alpha B))&\gamma\end{pmatrix}

on a suitable domain
D({\mathcal{A}})
that incorporates (
1.7
) and the coupling condition
u_{2}=\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u_{1}
. In order to construct a solution for (
1.5
)–(
1.9
), the main idea is to obtain an analytic semigroup (generated by
-{\mathcal{A}}
), whose smoothing effects will allow us to recover the original system with Wentzell boundary conditions.
Using this decoupling idea and form methods to tackle Wentzell boundary conditions, has proven to be a suitable approach for the second-order case, e.g., the Laplace operator subject to Wentzell boundary conditions. A series of papers starting in 2003 has shown generation results concerning an analytic semigroup for the decoupled system on
L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Gamma)
, using the classical Beurling–Deny criteria
[
AMPR03
]
. These results were then extended to the
L^{p}
-scale, and later also to general second-order elliptic operators on Lipschitz domains, where also Hölder continuity of the solution was deduced, see
[
Nit11
]
and
[
War13
]
. Under additional smoothness assumptions also spaces of continuous functions were considered in
[
AMPR03
]
; see also
[
EF05
]
and
[
BE19
]
where generation of an analytic semigroup was shown in an abstract perturbation framework. For higher order elliptic operators the extension procedure to the
L^{p}
-scale does not work, because the Beurling–Deny criteria are in general not fulfilled (see also Proposition
4.14
). Less results are available and they typically rely on being in a smooth setting. For fourth-order equations with sufficiently smooth coefficients in
C^{4}
-domains, it was shown in
[
FGGR08
, Theorem 2.1]
that the related operator in the product space is essentially self-adjoint. For the Cahn–Hilliard equation, classical well-posedness was shown in
[
RZ03
, Theorem 5.1]
in the
L^{2}
-setting, and in
[
PRZ06
, Theorem 2.1]
in the
L^{p}
-setting. Again the domain and the coefficients were assumed to be (sufficiently) smooth, and the methods do not carry over to Lipschitz domains.

In
[
DKP21
]
, the Lipschitz-case was solved for the Bi-Laplacian using weak Green’s formulae and the theory of quasi-boundary triples
[
BHdS20
, Chapter 8]
. In the present paper, however, we choose a more abstract approach which deals with a larger class of systems and does not depend on the theory of boundary triples. It contains the results of
[
DKP21
]
as a special case, also giving a simpler proof employing recent developments on co-normal derivatives in Lipschitz domains for operators with variable coefficients
[
BGM22
]
.

To that end, in Section
2.1
, we begin by addressing very general forms
{\mathfrak{b}}
whose associated operators will take the role of
B
in (
1.12
). To proceed, we start by considering the Neumann case
\delta=0
and define an abstract Neumann trace
{\mathscr{N}}_{b}
that fits into the form approach and is connected to Green’s second formula. Afterwards, in Section
2.2
, we investigate the quadratic form
\mathfrak{a}
on the product space
{\mathcal{H}}=L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Gamma)
to which the operator
\mathcal{A}
is associated. Based on the analysis of that form, we can show that the operator
\mathcal{A}
is self-adjoint and
-{\mathcal{A}}
is the generator of a strongly continuous and analytic semigroup
(\mathcal{T}(t))_{t\geq 0}
(Theorem
2.12
). This will also show that the operator
\mathcal{A}
indeed governs a generalized version of (
1.5
)–(
1.9
) with
\delta=0
, given by (
2.4
)–(
2.8
). We will explain that we can also obtain a solution of the Wentzell system in the original formulation generalizing (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) with initial condition (
1.4
). If
u_{2,0}
is not the trace of
u_{1,0}
, there are some subtleties concerning the initial values, see Remark
2.14
.
In Section
3
, we return to the main application where
B
is a second-order elliptic operator in divergence form, identifying the associated operator
B
and its minimal and maximal realization, as well as the normal trace
{\mathscr{N}}_{b}
(Section
3.2
). In Section
3.3
, we finally collect our results for the fourth-order system, which culminate in Corollary
3.20
where we precisely identify the operator
\mathcal{A}
and its domain in terms of Sobolev regularity. After extending our results to the Robin case
\delta>0
, we obtain that the operator
\mathcal{A}
indeed governs the system precisely as formulated in (
1.5
)–(
1.9
).

In Section
4
, we briefly discuss higher regularity for smoother domains and coefficients (Section
4.1
) before undertaking further investigations of the operator
{\mathcal{A}}
in the original setting. One of the main results of this section is Theorem
4.7
, which states that for every element
(u_{1},u_{2})
of
D(\mathcal{A}^{\infty})
the function
u_{1}
is Hölder continuous and
u_{2}
is the trace of
u_{1}
. As the semigroup
{\mathcal{T}}
is analytic, it follows that for positive time the solution of (
1.5
)–(
1.9
) is Hölder continuous and satisfies the Wentzell boundary condition in a pointwise sense. Moreover, this result implies regularity of the eigenfunctions of the operator
{\mathcal{A}}
and is used later on.
In Section
4.3
, we show that the operator
\mathcal{A}
has compact resolvent and thus a decomposition into a basis consisting of eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{A}
. This allows us to describe the semigroup in terms of the eigenfunctions and to characterize the asymptotic behavior of the semigroup. In particular, we study its positivity properties: It turns out that the generated semigroup is neither positive nor
L^{\infty}
-contractive (Proposition
4.14
) as the operator does not satisfy the Beurling–Deny criteria.
However, as shown for the for the semigroup generated by the Bi-Laplacian in
[
DKP21
]
, in the case
\gamma=\delta=0
our semigroup is again eventually positive in the sense of
[
DGK16a
]
and
[
DG18
]

(Theorem
4.17
). We close the article by showing that the same abstract approach can be used to obtain abstract results for higher-order operators, e.g.,
(-\Delta)^{4k}
.

2.
The abstract setting

Aim of this section is to establish a solution theory for Wentzell boundary conditions for higher-order operators which can be represented by nested forms, i.e. two quadratic forms where the operator associated to the first one is used to construct the second. We fix the following setting:

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a domain with Lipschitz boundary
\Gamma
.
We denote the inner products in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and
L^{2}(\Gamma)
by

\langle f,g\rangle_{\Omega}\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}f\overline{g}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\quad\mbox{and}\quad\langle f,g\rangle_{\Gamma}\coloneqq\int_{\Gamma}f\overline{g}\,{\mathrm{d}}S,

respectively, and write
\|\cdot\|_{\Omega}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\Gamma}
for the induced norms.
We denote the standard Sobolev spaces by
H^{s}(\Omega)
for
s\geq 0
.
By slight abuse of notation, we will also write

\langle\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle_{\Omega}\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{j}u\overline{\partial_{j}v}\,{\mathrm{d}}x

whenever
u,v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. For fractional orders we may either use complex interpolation, or, equivalently, restriction. For negative orders we employ duality.
Additionally for an elliptic operator of second-order
B
, we introduce the space
H^{s}_{B}(\Omega)
for the space of functions
u\in H^{s}(\Omega)
such that
Bu
belongs to
L^{2}(\Omega)
(cf. Definition
3.12
). We endow
H^{s}_{B}(\Omega)
with the canonical norm

\|u\|_{H^{s}_{B}(\Omega)}^{2}\coloneqq\|u\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|Bu\|_{\Omega}^{2}\quad(u\in H^{s}_{B}(\Omega)).

The Dirichlet-trace on
C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
, defined by
u\mapsto u_{|\Gamma}
, and its extension to any Sobolev space
H^{s}(\Omega)
for
s>\frac{1}{2}
is denoted by
\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}
.

Taking a brief look back to the Bi-Laplacian case (cf.
[
DKP21
]
), where the form
{\mathfrak{a}}(u,v)=\left\langle\Delta_{N}u,\Delta_{N}v\right\rangle
is considered on the domain

\{\mathcal{u}=(u_{1},u_{2})\in{\mathcal{H}}\penalty\ |\penalty\ u_{1}\in D(\Delta_{N}),u_{2}=\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u_{1}\},

we recall that its associated operator is given by

{\mathcal{A}}=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta^{2}&0\\
-\partial_{\nu}\Delta&0\end{pmatrix}.

In order to tackle the general system, in the form
{\mathfrak{a}}
, we are going to replace the Neumann Laplacian
\Delta_{N}
by a more general operator
B_{N}
. To that end, we introduce a second form that somehow operates on a “lower level”. More precisely,
\Delta_{N}
is naturally associated to the form
{\mathfrak{b}}(u,v)=\left\langle\nabla u,\nabla v\right\rangle_{\Omega}
on
L^{2}(\Omega)
with form domain
D({\mathfrak{b}})=H^{1}(\Omega),
so we may generalize this form. In order to distinguish between
{\mathfrak{a}}
and
{\mathfrak{b}}
terminologically, we will call
{\mathfrak{a}}
the primary form and
{\mathfrak{b}}
the subsidiary form.

At first, we will establish our theory for quite general subsidiary quadratic forms
{\mathfrak{b}}
whose associated operators are not necessarily differential operators in divergence form or even of second order.

2.1.
Abstract realizations of the lower-order operator

Recall, that if a form
{\mathfrak{b}}:D({\mathfrak{b}})\times D({\mathfrak{b}})\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}\penalty\ (D({\mathfrak{b}})\subset H)
is densely-defined, semi-bounded by
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, closed, and continuous in the sense of
[
Ouh05
, Chapter 1]
, its associated operator
A
satisfies that
\lambda-A
generates an analytic contraction semigroup on
{\mathcal{H}}
. We call such forms
generating
.
Note that, in this terminology,
{\mathfrak{b}}
is semi-bounded by
\lambda
if the shifted form
{\mathfrak{b}}_{\lambda}(u,v)={\mathfrak{b}}(u,v)+\lambda\left\langle u,v\right\rangle_{H}
is accretive. Furthermore,
A
will be self-adjoint if
{\mathfrak{a}}
is also symmetric.

Definition 2.1
.

Consider the Hilbert space
H=L^{2}(\Omega)
. We call a form
{\mathfrak{b}}:D({\mathfrak{b}})\times D({\mathfrak{b}})\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}

admissible
, if it is a generating, symmetric form on
H
such that for some
\rho\in(0,1)

(2.1)

C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)\subseteq(D({\mathfrak{b}}),\|\cdot\|_{\mathfrak{b}})\subseteq H^{\frac{1}{2}+\rho}(\Omega)

holds, where the latter embedding is continuous and dense.

Remark 2.2
.

The continuous embedding into the space
H^{1/2+\rho}(\Omega)
is assumed to ensure existence of the Dirichlet trace. The space
H^{1/2+\rho}(\Omega)
can be replaced by any space on which the Dirichlet trace exists and is bounded, and its range embeds densely into
L^{2}(\Gamma)
, as for example
H^{1/2}_{\Delta}
or some variant of it, if such an embedding of the form domain is known. However, in this abstract setting, we want to avoid spaces depending on specific operators.

Next, we introduce two operators, connected to the subsidiary form
{\mathfrak{b}}
. We think of them as realizations of a certain “general” operator
B
subject to Neumann or Dirichlet boundary conditions. While this is indeed true in the setting of elliptic differential operators on domains (cf.
B_{\max}
in Definition
3.12
), we point out that in the abstract setting considered here, it is unclear which manner to define such an operator would be the most sensible. Therefore, in this section the operator
B_{0}^{*}
will be used as a suitable substitute for the formally undefined operator
B
, which only appears terminologically in the following definition.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
{\mathfrak{b}}:D({\mathfrak{b}})\times D({\mathfrak{b}})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
be an admissible form.

(1)

Denote by
\lambda_{\mathfrak{b}}
the maximal semi-bound
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
{\rm Re\,}{\mathfrak{b}}(u,u)\geq\lambda\|u\|_{\Omega}^{2},
i.e. we have
\|u\|_{\mathfrak{b}}^{2}={\rm Re\,}{\mathfrak{b}}(u,u)+(1-\lambda_{\mathfrak{b}})\|u\|_{\Omega}^{2}
for
u\in D({\mathfrak{b}})
.

(2)

The operator
B_{N}
associated to
{\mathfrak{b}}
on
L^{2}(\Omega)
is called the
Neumann realization of
B
.

(3)

The
Dirichlet realization of
B
is the associated operator to
{\mathfrak{b}}_{D}
, the restriction of
{\mathfrak{b}}
to
\{u\in D({\mathfrak{b}})\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u=0\}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

If
{\mathfrak{b}}
is an admissible form, then
{\mathfrak{b}}_{D}
is generating and symmetric, so
B_{D}
is well defined and self-adjoint. Furthermore, we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle D(B_{N})\cap D(B_{D})=\{u\in D(B_{N})\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u=0\}=D(B_{N})\cap H_{0}^{1/2+\rho}(\Omega)

and

B_{N}u=B_{D}u\textrm{\penalty\ for\penalty\ }u\in D(B_{D})\cap D(B_{N}).

Proof.

The restricted form
{\mathfrak{b}}_{D}
is clearly symmetric, and densely defined as the test functions still lie in
D({\mathfrak{b}}_{D})
. By definition, we have
\|\cdot\|_{\mathfrak{b}}=\|\cdot\|_{{\mathfrak{b}}_{D}}
on
D({\mathfrak{b}}_{D})
, from which continuity and semi-boundedness follow. In order to show that (
D({\mathfrak{b}}_{D}),\|.\|_{{\mathfrak{b}}_{D}}
) is complete as well, take a Cauchy-sequence
u_{n}
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathfrak{b}}_{D}}=\|\cdot\|_{\mathfrak{b}}
. As
{\mathfrak{b}}
is a closed form,
u_{n}
converges to some
u\in D({\mathfrak{b}})
, and by continuous embedding also in
H^{1/2+\rho}(\Omega)
. By continuity of the Dirichlet trace, the traces converge as well, whence
\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u=0
and
u\in D({\mathfrak{b}}_{D})
as desired. Hence it is also generating.

We verify the second part in (
2.2
) first: In Lipschitz domains we have the identity
\{u\in H^{s}(\Omega)\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u=0\}=H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
for all
s\in(1/2,3/2)
(cf.
[
BGM22
, Equation (3.7)]
). So we directly obtain
D(B_{N})\cap H_{0}^{1/2+\rho}(\Omega)=\{u\in D(B_{N})\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u=0\}
due to
D(B_{N})\subset D({\mathfrak{b}})\subset H^{1/2+\rho}(\Omega)
.

For the remaining identity assume
u\in D(B_{N})
with
\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u=0
. Hence there is an
f_{N}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that, for all
v\in D({\mathfrak{b}})
,
\left\langle f_{N},v\right\rangle_{\Omega}={\mathfrak{b}}(u,v)
holds. But now
u\in D({\mathfrak{b}}_{D})
and in particular for all
v\in D({\mathfrak{b}}_{D})\subset D({\mathfrak{b}})
, we have

\left\langle f_{N},v\right\rangle_{\Omega}={\mathfrak{b}}(u,v)={\mathfrak{b}}_{D}(u,v),

which shows
u\in D(B_{D})
and
B_{D}u=f_{N}=B_{N}u.
The converse is trivial. ∎

Remark 2.5
.

(1)

Note that
b_{D}
itself is not admissible, as
D({\mathfrak{b}}_{D})
cannot be embedded continuously into
H^{1/2+\rho}(\Omega)
due to the continuity of the trace, which is the reason why pure Dirichlet-Wentzell boundary conditions (without Neumann term) can not be handled via this method.

(2)

In the following, we are going to assume that
D(B_{D})\cap D(B_{N})
is dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, which is useful to define realizations of
B
that are in a sense minimal (or maximal) and still densely defined. We will see below, in Proposition
2.11
, that this is a very natural assumption for considering the primary form
{\mathfrak{a}}
, as it will ensure that it will be densely defined as well. The simplest way to ensure this density will be to demand that
D(B_{D})\cap D(B_{N})
contains the test functions. However, this excludes the case of operators of the form
-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla
for
Q
only in
L^{\infty}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d})
. As we assume more regularity on
Q
in this article, anyway, this will be no restriction for us, though.

Next, we introduce two notions of generalized weak Neumann traces, the first of which is connected to a generalization of Green’s first formula, while the second is closer related to the abstract notion of the associated operator and Green’s second formula.

Definition 2.6
.

Assume that
D(B_{D})\cap D(B_{N})
is dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Define
B_{0}={B_{N}|}_{D(B_{D})\cap D(B_{N})}.

Let

N^{{\mathfrak{b}}}:D(N^{{\mathfrak{b}}})\subseteq L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Gamma)
be the linear operator defined by

\displaystyle D(N^{{\mathfrak{b}}})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\{u\in D({\mathfrak{b}})\cap D(B_{0}^{*})\penalty\ |\penalty\

\displaystyle\hphantom{spacesp}\exists g\in L^{2}(\Gamma)\,\forall v\in D({\mathfrak{b}}):\left\langle B_{0}^{*}u,v\right\rangle_{\Omega}-{\mathfrak{b}}(u,v)=\left\langle g,\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}v\right\rangle_{\Gamma}\}

and
N^{\mathfrak{b}}u\coloneqq g.

Let furthermore
{\mathscr{N}}^{\mathfrak{b}}:D({\mathscr{N}})\subseteq L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Gamma)
be the linear operator defined by

\displaystyle D({\mathscr{N}}^{{\mathfrak{b}}})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\{u\in D(B_{0}^{*})\penalty\ |\penalty\

\displaystyle\hphantom{spacesp}\exists g\in L^{2}(\Gamma)\forall v\in D(B_{N}):\left\langle B_{0}^{*}u,v\right\rangle_{\Omega}-\left\langle u,B_{N}v\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\left\langle g,\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}v\right\rangle_{\Gamma}\}

and

\mathcal{u}(t,\cdot)
are in
D({\mathcal{A}}^{2})
and thus we have

\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}B_{0}^{*}(\alpha B_{N})u=\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}({\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{u})_{1}=({\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{u})_{2}=-\beta{\mathscr{N}}^{{\mathfrak{b}}}(\alpha B_{N})u+\gamma\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u,

which shows (
2.11
). In fact, the analyticity even yields
\mathcal{u}(t,\cdot)\in D({\mathcal{A}}^{\infty})
for
t>0
.

Remark 2.14
.

We point out that the system (
2.10
)–(
2.13
) has to be interpreted in such a way that
u_{0}
is sufficiently smooth to have a trace, say
u_{0}\in H^{1/2+\rho}(\Omega)
; in this setting, the solutions of (
2.10
)–(
2.13
) are in a one-to-one correspondence with the solutions of (
2.4
)–(
2.8
) with
u_{1,0}=u_{0}|_{\Omega}
and
u_{2,0}=u_{0}|_{\Gamma}
. In our semigroup approach, however,
u_{2,0}
can be chosen independently of
u_{1,0}
and, by the above, all of these solutions are (distinct!) solutions of (
2.10
)–(
2.13
). In a way, choosing
u_{2,0}
different from
\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u_{1,0}
corresponds precisely to having some free energy on the boundary, which was a main motivation to consider Wentzell boundary conditions in the first place.

3.
Application to strongly elliptic operators in divergence form

In this section, we will specify the operator
B
to be a strongly elliptic second-order operator in divergence form and return to the investigation of the system (
1.1
)–(
1.4
). We consider
\delta=0
at first and deal with the Robin case at the end of Section

We begin by settling the precise regularity assumptions on the matrix
Q
and recalling some facts concerning different realizations of co-normal traces.

3.1.
Co-normal traces

Hypothesis 3.1
.

Assume
Q\in C^{1,1}(\overline{\Omega},{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d})
to be symmetric and uniformly positive definite, which means there is some open superset
\widetilde{\Omega}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
containing
\overline{\Omega}
such that
Q\in C^{1,1}(\widetilde{\Omega},{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d})
is symmetric and satisfies for some
\kappa_{Q}>0

(3.1)

\left\langle Q(x)\xi,\xi\right\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}\geq\kappa_{Q}|\xi|^{2}\penalty\ \penalty\ (x\in\widetilde{\Omega},\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}).

Remark 3.2
.

The regularity
Q\in C^{1,1}(\bar{\Omega},{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d})
is not necessary for all the subsequent steps, part of the theory can be done using only
W^{1,\infty}
-regularity. However
C^{1,1}
is the regularity from
[
BGM22
, Chapter 11]
, and thus used when we establish higher regularity and a precise identification of the occurring traces and the domain of our operator. For a finer distinction in regularity, we refer to
[
Plo24
, Hypothesis 2.5 and Section 3.1]
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a Lipschitz domain with outward normal
\nu
. We consider the following notions of strong traces:

(1)

For a real-valued matrix
Q\in W^{1,\infty}(\bar{\Omega}),{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
, we denote the
co-normal Neumann trace
of a function
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
by

\tau^{Q}_{N}u\coloneqq\nu\cdot\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}Q\nabla u
, where we read the operator
\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}
component-wise.

(2)

For any function
\delta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we will call
\tau^{Q}_{\delta}=\tau^{Q}_{N}+\delta\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}
the
(co-normal) Robin trace
.

It is known, that the Dirichlet trace extends by continuity to a bounded linear surjective operator

\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}\colon H^{s}(\Omega)\to H^{s-1/2}(\Gamma)\text{\penalty\ for\penalty\ all\penalty\ }s\in\left(\dfrac{1}{2},\dfrac{3}{2}\right)

(cf.
[
GM08
, Equation (2.7)]
). In fact, this operator is even a retraction, i.e. there exists a continuous right-inverse.
Even for smooth domains, however, the continuity of
\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}\colon H^{s}(\Omega)\to H^{s-1/2}(\Gamma)
does not hold for the endpoint case
s=\frac{1}{2}
, see
[
LM72
, Theorem 1.9.5]
. However, one can include the cases
s=\frac{1}{2}
and
s=\frac{3}{2}
by replacing
H^{s}(\Omega)
by
H^{s}_{\Delta}(\Omega)
. In particular, it was shown in
[
GM08
, Lemma 2.3]
that the smooth trace extends to a retraction
\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}\colon H^{3/2}_{\Delta}(\Omega)\to H^{1}(\Gamma)
.

Next we consider the weak definition of the (co-normal) Neumann trace.

(3.2)

\displaystyle D(\partial_{\nu}^{Q})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\big\{u\in H^{1}_{\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla}(\Omega)\,|\,\mbox{there exists a }g\in L^{2}(\Gamma)\mbox{ such that}

\displaystyle\quad\langle\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u,v\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle Q\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle_{\Omega}=\langle g,\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}v\rangle_{\Gamma}\text{ for all }v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\big\},

where we set
\partial_{\nu}^{Q}u=g
. Naturally, one wants to know whether
\partial^{Q}_{\nu}
coincides with an extension of
\tau_{N}^{Q}
. For
Q=\mathop{\textrm{{id}}}
one has
\partial_{\nu}=\tau_{N}\colon H^{3/2}_{\Delta}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Gamma)
, see
[
GM08
, Lemma 2.4]
. For
Q\neq\mathop{\textrm{{id}}}
the properties of such a possible extension were much less clear for some time. In the recent preprint
[
BGM22
]
, those issues were resolved. We recall their central result for our case (
[
BGM22
, Corollary 11.28]
) adapted to the notation we are going to use.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a Lipschitz domain. Let
{\mathscr{B}}
be a formal second-order differential operator acting on elements in
L^{2}(\Omega)
in a distributional sense via

{\mathscr{B}}u=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\partial_{i}q_{ij}(x)\partial_{j}u,

where the matrix
Q=(q_{ij})
is given as in Hypothesis
3.1
. Let
B
denote its
L^{2}(\Omega)
-realization (cf. Definition
3.12
). Then, the co-normal Neumann trace defined by
u\mapsto\nu\cdot\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}(Q\nabla u)
for smooth functions extends uniquely to

(3.3)

\gamma^{s}_{Q}:H^{s}_{B}(\Omega)\rightarrow H^{s-3/2}(\Gamma)

for all
s\in[\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}]
, forming a compatible family in
s
.
Furthermore, for all
s\in[\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}]
, we have the following:

(1)

The generalized Neumann traces in (
3.3
) are surjective. In fact, there are bounded linear operators

(3.4)

\Upsilon^{s}_{Q}:H^{s-3/2}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{s}_{B}(\Omega),

which are also compatible with each other for different

, and right inverses to the Neumann trace, meaning for all
\psi\in H^{s-3/2}(\Gamma)
we have
\gamma_{Q}^{s}(\Upsilon_{Q}^{s}\psi)=\psi
.

(2)

For any

and

the following Green’s formula holds:

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}h,\gamma_{Q}^{s}f\right\rangle_{H^{3/2-s}(\Gamma)\times(H^{3/2-s}(\Gamma))^{\prime}}

\displaystyle-\left\langle\gamma_{Q}^{s}h,\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}f\right\rangle_{H^{s-1/2}(\Gamma)\times(H^{1/2-s}(\Gamma))^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{space}=\left\langle h,Bf\right\rangle_{\Omega}-\left\langle Bh,f\right\rangle_{\Omega}.

(3)

\ker(\gamma_{Q}^{s})\subseteq H^{3/2}(\Omega)
,
\ker(\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}})\subseteq H^{3/2}(\Omega)
, and for any
u\in H^{1/2}_{B}(\Omega)
with either
\gamma^{s}_{Q}u=0
or
\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u=0
, there is some
C>0
such that

\|u\|^{2}_{H^{3/2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|u\|_{\Omega}^{2}+\|Bu\|_{\Omega}^{2}.

Proof.

This is
[
BGM22
, Corollary 11.25 and 11.28]
.
∎

We are going to verify the compatibility to the weak formulation
\partial_{\nu}^{Q}
in Theorem
3.14
, later.

3.2.
On the second-order operator

With the trace results from the last section, we are going to be able to identify the operator
{\mathcal{A}}
for the case
B=-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla
, and to fully describe its domain in precise terms of Sobolev regularity. The underlying subsidiary form is given as follows.

Definition 3.5
.

Assume Hypothesis
3.1
. Set
D({\mathfrak{b}})\coloneqq H^{1}(\Omega)
, and let
{\mathfrak{b}}:D({\mathfrak{b}})\times D({\mathfrak{b}})\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
be given by

(3.5)

{\mathfrak{b}}(u,v)=\left\langle Q\nabla u,\nabla v\right\rangle_{\Omega}

for
u,v\in D({\mathfrak{b}})

{\mathfrak{b}}_{D}(u,v)\coloneqq{\mathfrak{b}}(u,v)
for

.

Lemma 3.6
.

The subsidiary form
{\mathfrak{b}}
defined as above is admissible in the sense of Definition
2.1
. Furthermore, we have
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)\subseteq D(B_{N})\cap D(B_{D})
. Hence, we are in the situation of Definition
2.6
and Theorem
2.8
.

Proof.

We choose
\rho=1/2
in Equation (

) and have
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)\subseteq D({\mathfrak{b}})=H^{1}(\Omega)
, whence the form
{\mathfrak{b}}
is also densely defined. It is accretive, as
Q
is uniformly positive definite. As
Q
is also bounded, we have

\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}}\leq{\mathfrak{b}}(u,u)+\|u\|_{\Omega}^{2}\leq C\|u\|_{H^{1}}.

So
{\mathfrak{b}}
is closed and continuous, and therefore
{\mathfrak{b}}
is generating. It is symmetric, as
Q
is symmetric and real-valued.
Hence the Neumann realization
B_{N}
, as the associated operator to
{\mathfrak{b}}
, and the Dirichlet realization, as the associated operator to
{\mathfrak{b}}_{D}={\mathfrak{b}}|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}
, are well defined and we have
D(B_{N})\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)=D(B_{D})\cap D(B_{N})
(cf. Proposition
2.4
).
As
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)\subseteq H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
we only need to show that
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)\subseteq D(B_{N}).
So let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, which implies that
Q\nabla\varphi\in(H^{1}(\Omega)^{d})
as
C^{1,1}(\overline{\Omega},{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d})\subset W^{1,\infty}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d})
. Now, we may use the following version of Green’s formula taken from
[
BGM22
, Corollary 4.5]
, which holds for their case of
\varepsilon=1/2
, as
\Delta
maps from
H^{1}(\Omega)
to
H^{-1}(\Omega)
.
For all

we have that

\left\langle Q\nabla\varphi,\nabla v\right\rangle_{\Omega}+\left\langle\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla\varphi,v\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\left\langle\nu\cdot\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}Q\nabla\varphi,\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}v\right\rangle_{\Gamma}=0

as
\nabla\varphi=0
close to the boundary, which shows

{\mathfrak{b}}(\varphi,v)=\left\langle-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla\varphi,v\right\rangle_{\Omega}

for all

and thus
\varphi\in D(B_{N})
and
B_{N}\varphi=-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla\varphi.

∎

So we may define
B_{0}
,
B_{0}^{*}
,
N^{\mathfrak{b}}
, and
{\mathscr{N}}^{\mathfrak{b}}
as stated in Theorem
2.8
. Furthermore, the next Lemma shows that we are in the situation where
N^{\mathfrak{b}}={\mathscr{N}}^{\mathfrak{b}}
holds.

Lemma 3.7
.

The operator
N^{\mathfrak{b}}
is surjective.

Proof.

The surjectivity of
N^{\mathfrak{b}}
is a special case of
[
Nit11
, Lemma 3.8]
. There it is shown that for any
g\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
there is a
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and a
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that for all
v\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
we have

{\mathfrak{b}}(u,v)+\left\langle\lambda u,v\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\left\langle g,\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}v\right\rangle_{\Gamma}
. Approximation in
H^{1}(\Omega)
yields this result for all
v\in H^{1}(\Omega).

Hence
u\in D(B_{0}^{*})
as for
v\in D(B_{0})
we have
-\left\langle\lambda u,v\right\rangle_{\Omega}={\mathfrak{b}}(u,v)=\left\langle u,B_{N}v\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\left\langle u,B_{0}v\right\rangle_{\Omega}
and

so

and
-N^{\mathfrak{b}}u=g
.
∎

Next we show that the operator
B_{0}^{*}
is in some sense the maximal
L^{2}
-realization of
-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla
, and
-N^{{\mathfrak{b}}}
coincides with the usual co-normal derivative
\partial_{\nu}
, which extends
\nu\cdot\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}Q\nabla.
So we begin by verifying that both operators act as desired on smooth functions.

Proposition 3.8
.

If
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
, we have
B_{0}^{*}\varphi=-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla\varphi
and
N^{{\mathfrak{b}}}\varphi=-\nu\cdot\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}Q\nabla\varphi
.

Proof.

For any function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
, so as seen above
Q\nabla\varphi
is an element of
(H^{1}(\Omega))^{d}.
So again by
[
BGM22
, Corollary 4.5]
, for all
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, we have

\left\langle Q\nabla\varphi,\nabla v\right\rangle_{\Omega}+\left\langle\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla\varphi,v\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\left\langle\nu\cdot\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}Q\nabla\varphi,\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}v\right\rangle_{\Gamma}.

This means, for all functions
v\in D(B_{0})\subseteq D(B_{N})
, that

\left\langle-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\varphi,v\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\left\langle Q\nabla\varphi,\nabla v\right\rangle_{\Omega}={\mathfrak{b}}(\varphi,v)=\left\langle\varphi,B_{0}v\right\rangle_{\Omega},

which shows
\varphi\in D(B_{0}^{*})
and
B_{0}^{*}\varphi=-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla\varphi.
Furthermore, we even have
\varphi\in D(N^{{\mathfrak{b}}})
and
N^{{\mathfrak{b}}}\varphi=-\nu\cdot\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}Q\nabla\varphi.

∎

We point out that
\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla
does not map test functions onto test functions. Hence, there is no distributional realization of that operator and the largest space we can work on is
H^{-2}(\Omega)
, the dual of
H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
, whence we use the
L^{2}(\Omega)
-realization of that version. To that end, we establish an elliptic regularity result on
{\mathbb{R}}^{dm}
on the level of test functions.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
Q
satisfy Hypothesis
3.1
. Then there is a symmetric, uniformly positive definite extension
\widehat{Q}\in\mathop{\textrm{{BUC}}}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d})
of
Q
. Furthermore, for all
s\in[0,2]
, there is a
\lambda_{0}>1
such that for any
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
there exists
C_{\lambda}>0
for which

\|\varphi\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq C_{\lambda}\|(\lambda-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}\widehat{Q}\nabla)\varphi\|_{H^{s-2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}

holds for all
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

We first construct the extension. As
Q
is uniformly positive definite in some open superset
\widetilde{\Omega}
which contains
\overline{\Omega}
, there is a
C^{\infty}
-domain
\Omega^{\prime}
with
\overline{\Omega}\subseteq\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\widetilde{\Omega},
which can be constructed by approximation with mollified functions in the supremum norm. As
\Omega^{\prime}
is smooth with smooth boundary
\Gamma^{\prime}
, there is a small tubular neighborhood

\Gamma^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\rm dist}(x,\Gamma^{\prime})<\varepsilon\}

of
\Gamma^{\prime}
, which can be parameterized by the normal vector, i.e. there is a smooth bijective map

\gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times\Gamma^{\prime}\rightarrow\Gamma^{\prime}_{\varepsilon};(h,x^{\prime})\mapsto\gamma(h,x^{\prime}):=x^{\prime}+h\cdot\partial_{\nu}(x^{\prime}).

Choosing
\varepsilon>0
small enough, it is possible to guarantee
\Gamma^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\in\widetilde{\Omega}\setminus\overline{\Omega},
so
Q
is defined on
\overline{\Omega^{\prime}\cup\Gamma^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}}
.
For the extension, let
\psi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},[0,1])
be a strictly decreasing function satisfying
\psi=1
on
(-\infty,\frac{-\varepsilon}{2})
and
\psi=0
on
(\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\infty)
. Then, we obtain
\varphi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
by setting
\varphi=1
on
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\Gamma^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}
,
\varphi=0
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus(\Gamma^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\cup\Omega^{\prime})
, and
\varphi(x)\coloneqq\psi(h)
for
x=\gamma(h,x^{\prime})\in\Gamma^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}
. Hence, we can define
\widehat{Q}(x)=(1-\varphi(x))Q(x^{*})+\varphi(x)Q(x)
for an arbitrary but fixed
x^{*}\in\Omega
, and the new matrix
\widehat{Q}\in\mathop{\textrm{{BUC}}}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d})
still satisfies (
3.1
) as the set of uniform positive definite matrices is convex.

For the ellipticity estimate, we use parabolic theory from
[
DPRS23
]
. The constructed extension
\widehat{Q}
satisfies their assumption
(S2)
(its entries are
\mathop{\textrm{{BUC}}}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
-functions which are constant for large
|x|
), and a simple calculation shows that due to the uniform positive definiteness, the resulting operator
\lambda-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla
is also parameter-elliptic. Hence the assumptions of
[
DPRS23
, Lemma 3.14]
are satisfied for
\sigma=0
and
r=\lceil|s-1|\rceil=1
and, we obtain the estimate in the
\lambda
-dependent spaces by
[
DPRS23
, Lemma 3.14]
. As the constant is allowed to depend on
\lambda
, this finishes the proof as we switch to
\lambda
-independent spaces.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

A thorough comparison of regularities will show that a strict application of

, Lemma 3.14]
would need

Now we can show that the minimal realization of

Let

{(-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla)|}_{H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)}={B_{\min}}={B_{0}|{{}_{H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)}}}.

(i)

. ∎

Now we can characterize

Proposition 3.13

We have

In particular, this shows that

Proof.

Let

This, equivalently, means

B_{N}
, and therefore symmetric.

Theorem 3.14
.

(1)

For all

(2)

Proof.

(i)

In the case

. The cases

to the closed subspace

. By definition, however,

\widehat{Q}
in

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}\widehat{Q}\nabla e^{0}h_{2},\varphi\right\rangle_{H^{-2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times H^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}

,

and

\displaystyle\Lambda(u)

\displaystyle=\left\langle h_{1},F\right\rangle_{H^{-s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}+\left\langle e^{0}h_{2},e^{0}B_{\max}u\right\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}

(ii)

Let

(iii)

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}
be a closed sector in the complex plane with vertex at the origin. Using the standard convention
D:=-i\nabla
for parabolic boundary value problems, let
A
and
B=(B_{1},...,B_{m})
be formally given by

A(x,D):=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq 2m}a_{\alpha}(x)D^{\alpha}\text{\penalty\ \penalty\ and\penalty\ \penalty\ }B_{j}(x,D):=\sum_{|\beta|\leq m_{j}}b_{\beta}(x)\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}D^{\beta}\penalty\ \penalty\ (j=1,\ldots,m),

where
m_{j}<2m

(i)

We define the

of

and

as

a_{0}(x,\xi)\coloneqq\sum_{|\alpha|=2m}a_{\alpha}(x)\xi^{\alpha}\text{\penalty\ \penalty\ and\penalty\ \penalty\ }b_{0,j}(x,\xi)\coloneqq\sum_{|\beta|=m_{j}}b_{j\beta}(x)\xi^{\beta}\penalty\ \penalty\ (j=1,\ldots,m),

respectively.

(ii)

We call the family
\lambda-A(x,D)

parameter-elliptic in
\Lambda
if the principal symbol
a_{0}(x,\xi)

satisfies

(4.4)

|\lambda-a_{0}(x,\xi)|\geq C\big(|\lambda|+|\xi|^{2m}\big)\quad(x\in\overline{\Omega},\,\lambda\in\Lambda,\,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,(\xi,\lambda)\not=0)

for some constant
C>0
.

(iii)

The boundary value problem
\binom{\lambda+A}{B}
is called
parameter-elliptic in
\Lambda

if
\lambda-A(x,D)
is parameter-elliptic in
\Lambda
, and the following
Shapiro–Lopatinskii condition
holds:

Let
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
be an arbitrary point of the boundary; rewrite the boundary value problem
(\lambda-a_{0}(x_{0},D),

b_{0,1}(x_{0},D),\dots,

b_{0,m}(x_{0},D))
in the coordinate system associated with
x_{0}
obtained from the original one by a rotation after which the positive

-axis has the direction of the interior normal to
\partial\Omega
at
x_{0}
. Then, for all
\xi^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
and
\lambda\in\Lambda
with
(\xi^{\prime},\lambda)\not=0
, the trivial solution
w=0
is the only stable solution of the ordinary differential equation on the half-line

\displaystyle(\lambda-a_{0}(x_{0},\xi^{\prime},D_{d}))w(x_{d})

\displaystyle=0\quad(x_{d}\in(0,\infty)),

\displaystyle b_{0,j}(x_{0},\xi^{\prime},D_{d})w(0)

\displaystyle=0\quad(j=1,\dots,m).

Lemma 4.2
.

The system
\begin{pmatrix}{\lambda+B\alpha B}\\
{-\beta\partial_{\nu}^{Q}(\alpha B)}\\
{\partial_{\nu}^{Q}+\delta\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}}\end{pmatrix}
is parameter-elliptic in
\overline{\Sigma_{\theta}}
for
\theta\in(0,\pi).

Proof.

The parameter-ellipticity for the family
\lambda+B\alpha B
is simple as we have

\displaystyle a_{0}(x,\xi)\coloneqq{\mathop{\text{{symb}}}}_{0}[B\alpha B](x,\xi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}(\xi_{j}q_{jk}(x)\xi_{k})\alpha(x)\sum_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}(\xi_{j^{\prime}}q_{j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}(x)\xi_{k^{\prime}})>0

for all
|\xi|\neq 0
as the matrix
Q=(q_{jk})_{jk}
is symmetric and uniformly positive definite. Note for the first line that all the terms where the derivative hits the coefficient are of lower order. Hence, we can exploit homogeneity and boundedness of the domain, which shows parameter-ellipticity in any closed sector that does not contain the negative real line.

A similar calculation shows

\displaystyle a^{\prime}(x,\xi)\coloneqq{\mathop{\text{{symb}}}}_{0}[B](x,\xi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\xi_{j^{\prime}}q_{j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}(x)\xi_{k^{\prime}},

\displaystyle b_{0,1}(x,\xi)\coloneqq{\mathop{\text{{symb}}}}_{0}[-\beta\partial_{\nu}^{Q}(\alpha B)](x,\xi)

\displaystyle=i\beta(x)\sum_{k}q_{dk}\xi_{k}\alpha(x)a^{\prime}(x,\xi),

\displaystyle b_{0,2}(x,\xi)\coloneqq{\mathop{\text{{symb}}}}_{0}[\partial_{\nu}^{Q}](x,\xi)

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{k}q_{dk}(x)\xi_{k}.

In order to verify the Shapiro–Lopatinskii condition, we need to show that the ODE below only has the trivial solution. For this, the operators are locally transformed into the half-space at every fixed point
x_{0}\in\Gamma
. It is a known fact that this coordinate transformation leaves the coefficients of the main symbol invariant (cf.
[
Wlo87
, Satz 10.3]
). Furthermore, we would like to note that, as we only consider a fixed
x_{0}
, the coefficients commute with all derivatives and we can simply pass from divergence to non-divergence form, which shows we may investigate the system (
4.5
) below. We write
\Xi=\binom{\xi^{\prime}}{-i\partial_{d}},
where
\xi=(\xi^{\prime},\xi_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as usual, and interpret the first components as multiplication, so
\Xi w=(\xi_{1}w,\ldots,\xi_{d-1}w,-i\partial_{d}w)
. Then, for
x_{d}\in(0,\infty)
, and
\lambda\in\overline{\Sigma_{\theta}}
,
\xi^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
satisfying
(\lambda,\xi^{\prime})\neq 0
, we assume

(4.5)

\displaystyle(\lambda+a_{0}(x_{0},\Xi))w(x_{d})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle b_{0,1}(x_{0},\Xi)w(0)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle b_{0,2}(x_{0},\Xi)w(0)

\displaystyle=0.

Note that integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\left\langle\sum_{j}\Xi_{j}u,v\right\rangle_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}

\displaystyle=\left\langle u,\sum_{j}\Xi_{j}v\right\rangle_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}-iu_{d}(0)\cdot\overline{v_{d}(0)}.

Multiplying the first line with
w
in
L^{2}((0,\infty))
and using integration by parts and (
4.5
), we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=|\lambda|\|w\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2}+\sum_{j,k}\left\langle\Xi_{j}q_{jk}(x_{0})\Xi_{k}\alpha(x_{0})a^{\prime}(x_{0},\Xi)w,w\right\rangle_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}

\displaystyle=|\lambda|\|w\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2}+\sum_{j.k}\left\langle q_{jk}(x_{0})\Xi_{k}\alpha(x_{0})a^{\prime}(x_{0},\Xi)w,\Xi_{j}w\right\rangle_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ }-\beta^{-1}(x_{0})b_{0,1}(x_{0},\Xi)w(0)\cdot\overline{w(0)}

\displaystyle=|\lambda|\|w\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2}+\sum_{j,k}\left\langle\Xi_{k}\alpha(x_{0})a^{\prime}(x_{0},\Xi)w,q_{jk}(x_{0})\Xi_{j}w\right\rangle_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}

\displaystyle=|\lambda|\|w\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2}+\left\langle\alpha(x_{0})a^{\prime}(x_{0},\Xi)w,\sum_{k,j}\Xi_{j}q_{kj}(x_{0})\Xi_{k}w\right\rangle_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ }-\alpha(x_{0})a^{\prime}(x_{0},\Xi)w(0)\cdot\overline{b_{0,2}(x_{0},\Xi)w(0)}

\displaystyle=|\lambda|\|w\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2}+\|\sqrt{\alpha(x_{0})}a^{\prime}(x_{0},\Xi)w\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2}.

Note that we used that the matrix
Q=(q_{jk})
is symmetric and real-valued and that
\alpha>0,\beta>0
.
If
\lambda\neq 0
, this implies that
w=0
as desired, since
\overline{\Sigma_{\theta}}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(-\infty,0).

If
\lambda=0
, and thus by assumption
\xi^{\prime}\neq 0
, we have

a^{\prime}(x_{0},\Xi)w=0.

Multiplying with
w
in
L^{2}(0,\infty)
once more, we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{{j^{\prime}},{k^{\prime}}}\left\langle\Xi_{j^{\prime}}q_{{j^{\prime}}{k^{\prime}}}(x_{0})\Xi_{k^{\prime}}w,w\right\rangle_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{j^{\prime}},{k^{\prime}}}\left\langle q_{{j^{\prime}}{k^{\prime}}}(x_{0})\Xi_{k^{\prime}}w,\Xi_{j^{\prime}}w\right\rangle_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}+b_{0,2}(x_{0},\Xi)w(0)\cdot\overline{w(0)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{j^{\prime}},{k^{\prime}}}\left\langle q_{{j^{\prime}}{k^{\prime}}}(x_{0})\Xi_{k^{\prime}}w,\Xi_{j^{\prime}}w\right\rangle_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\langle Q(\Xi w)(x_{d}),(\Xi w)(x_{d})\right\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}\mathrm{d}x_{d}\geq\kappa_{Q}\int_{0}^{\infty}|(\Xi w)(x_{d})|^{2}\mathrm{d}x_{d}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{Q}(|\xi^{\prime}|^{2}\|w\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2}+\|\partial_{d}w\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2}).

In the last step we used that
Q
is uniformly positive definite (cf. (
3.1
)). As
\xi^{\prime}\neq 0
, we also have

in this case. Hence altogether the system is parameter-elliptic in every sector smaller than
\pi
.
∎

This shows

-regularity of any solution:

Proof.

By the above all assumptions of

are satisfied, hence we obtain

∎

4.2.
Hölder regularity

We show next that on Lipschitz domains and with coefficients as in Hypotheses
2.9
and
3.1
the solution
\mathcal{u}(t,\cdot)=(u_{1}(t,\cdot),u_{2}(t,\cdot))
of (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) satisfies that
u_{1}(t,\cdot)
is Hölder continuous for every
t>0
. This implies that
\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u_{1}(t,\cdot)=u_{2}(t,\cdot)
also holds in a classical sense.

Recall that
{\mathcal{T}}(t)
maps
{\mathcal{H}}
into
D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}^{\infty})
for any
t>0
as it is analytic. It is not to be expected, however, that
\mathcal{u}(t,\cdot)\in D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}^{\infty})
implies
u_{1}(t,\cdot)\in H^{3/2+\varepsilon}(\Omega)
for any
\varepsilon>0
, as such a gain in differentiability does not even necessarily hold for the much simpler Neumann Laplacian due to possible non-convex corners (cf.
[
Kon67
]
). So Hölder-continuity cannot be derived by Sobolev embedding directly in high dimensions. However, we can use a bootstrapping idea on the integrability.

To that end we use the regular spaces
L^{p}(\Omega)
and
L^{p}(\Gamma)
where the coefficient
\beta
is not included, and write
\|\cdot\|_{\Omega,p}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\Gamma,p}
for the occurring norms, respectively. In the case
p=2
, the index is dropped. Note that the
L^{p}
-spaces are nested as our domain
\Omega
is bounded. Furthermore, recall that
C^{0,\vartheta}(\Omega)
refers to the space of
\vartheta
-Hölder continuous functions on
\Omega
, and note that every function
u\in C^{0,\vartheta}(\Omega)
can be extended uniquely
to a (Hölder) continuous function on
\overline{\Omega}
.

As a preparation, we establish some further results concerning weak solutions of the inhomogeneous Neumann problem

(4.6)

\displaystyle(\lambda-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla)u

\displaystyle=\tilde{f}

\displaystyle\textnormal{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\partial^{Q}_{\nu}u

\displaystyle=\tilde{g}

\displaystyle\textnormal{ on }\Gamma.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\tilde{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
\tilde{g}\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
.

(1)

For
\lambda>0
, (
4.6
) has a unique weak solution, by which we mean a function

such that

(4.7)

for all

Furthermore,

, and we have the estimate

(2)

Let
\tilde{f}\in L^{d/2+\varepsilon}(\Omega)

\tilde{g}\in L^{d-1+\varepsilon}(\Gamma)

If

-term on the right-hand side.

(3)

Let
\tilde{f}\in L^{p}(\Omega)
,
\tilde{g}\in L^{p}(\Gamma)
for some

\lambda>0

of (

and

\|u\|_{\Omega,\varphi(p)}+\|\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u\|_{\Gamma,\varphi(p)}\leq C_{0}\Big(\|\tilde{f}\|_{\Omega,p}+\|\tilde{g}\|_{\Gamma,p}\Big)

where

\varphi(p)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{d-2}{d-p}\,p&\text{ if }p\in(2,d),\\
\infty&\text{ if }p\in[d,\infty).\end{cases}

Proof.

(i)

We construct a solution candidate by collecting properties of a weak solution. At first we observe that for any such weak solution

we have, given any

,

Hence

and
\tilde{f}=(\lambda+B_{0}^{*})u
. By Theorem
3.14
(ii), we also have

by , so the first line of (

, where

is seen as

which shows

and
-N^{{\mathfrak{b}}}u=\tilde{g}
. However, Theorem
3.14
(iii) yields

and

from Lemma

\displaystyle=\tilde{f}+\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla v-\lambda v

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\gamma_{Q}^{3/2}(u-v)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma.

.

Hence, a suitable solution candidate is given by

\displaystyle\|\tilde{u}-v\|^{2}_{H^{3/2}_{B}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|\tilde{u}-v\|^{2}_{\Omega}+\|B_{N}(\tilde{u}-v)\|_{\Omega})=C\|\tilde{u}-v\|^{2}_{B_{N}}

\displaystyle=C\|(\lambda+B_{N})^{-1}(\tilde{f}+\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla v-\lambda v)\|^{2}_{B_{N}}\leq C\big(\|\tilde{f}\|_{\Omega}^{2}+\|v\|_{H^{3/2}_{B}(\Omega)}^{2}\big)

(where the constant
C
is generic) and thus by the above and the continuity of

\|\tilde{u}\|^{2}_{H^{3/2}_{B}(\Omega)}\leq C\big(\|\tilde{f}\|^{2}_{\Omega}+\|v\|^{2}_{H^{3/2}_{B}(\Omega)}\big)\leq C(\|\tilde{f}\|^{2}_{\Omega}+\|\tilde{g}\|^{2}_{\Gamma}).

Now, naturally,
\tilde{u}
solves (
4.6
) in a strong sense by construction. By definition of
-N^{\mathfrak{b}}=\partial_{\nu}^{Q}
it also satisfies (
4.7
) and is in particular a weak solution with all the desired properties.

The uniqueness is straightforward: If we had two weak solutions
u,w\in H^{1}(\Omega)
satisfying (
4.7
) for all
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, we would have
{\mathfrak{b}}^{\lambda}(u-w,v)=0
for all
v\in H^{1}(\Omega).
Now
\lambda+B_{N}
is the associated operator to
{\mathfrak{b}}_{\lambda}
, whence
u-w
in
D(B_{N})
and
(\lambda+B_{N})(u-w)=0.
Again, by
(-\infty,0)\subseteq\rho(B_{N})
, we have
u-w=0
.

(ii)

This is
[
Nit10
, Theorem 3.1.6]
applied to
A(x,u,p)=Q\cdot p
,
a(x,u,p)=\lambda u
. Note that their Assumption 2.9.1 is satisfied, and we are in the situation of
[
Nit10
, Remark 3.1.7]
. If
\lambda>0
, we can estimate

\|u\|_{\Omega}\leq\|u\|_{H^{3/2}_{B}(\Omega)}\leq C(\|\tilde{f}\|^{2}_{\Omega}+\|\tilde{g}\|^{2}_{\Omega})\leq C(\|\tilde{f}\|^{2}_{\frac{d}{2}+\varepsilon,\Omega}+\|\tilde{g}\|^{2}_{d-1+\varepsilon,\Omega}).

(iii)

Let
\lambda>0
. By (i) and (ii) the unique solution satisfies the two estimates

(4.11)

\|u\|_{\Omega,2}+\|\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u\|_{\Gamma,2}\leq C\|u\|_{H^{3/2}_{B}(\Omega)}\leq C\big(\|\tilde{f}\|_{\Omega,2}+\|\tilde{g}\|_{\Gamma,2}\Big),

as well as

(4.12)

\|u\|_{\Omega,\infty}+\|\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u\|_{\Gamma,\infty}\leq\|u\|_{C^{0,\vartheta}(\Omega)}\leq C\big(\|\tilde{f}\|_{\Omega,d}+\|\tilde{g}\|_{\Gamma,d}\big).

More precisely, the solution operator
R_{\lambda}
that maps
(\tilde{f},\tilde{g})
to
(u,\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u)
is well defined and continuous from
X_{0}\coloneqq L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Gamma)
to
Y_{0}\coloneqq L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)

as well as from
X_{1}\coloneqq L^{d}(\Omega)\times L^{d}(\Gamma)
to
Y_{1}\coloneqq L^{\infty}(\Omega)\times L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
By complex interpolation, we obtain that
R_{\lambda}
is also continuous from
[X_{0},X_{1}]_{\theta}
to
[Y_{0},Y_{1}]_{\theta}
for all

\theta\in(0,1)
. To identify the interpolation spaces, recall from
[

, Theorem 1.18.1]
that
complex interpolation of tuples of

-spaces yields the tuple of interpolated spaces in the sense of

[L^{p_{0}}(\Omega)\times L^{q_{0}}(\Gamma),L^{p_{1}}(\Omega)\times L^{q_{1}}(\Gamma)]_{\theta}=[L^{p_{0}}(\Omega),L^{p_{1}}(\Omega)]_{\theta}\times[L^{q_{0}}(\Gamma)\times L^{q_{1}}(\Gamma)]_{\theta}

for all
p_{0},p_{1},q_{0},q_{1}\in[1,\infty]
.
Moreover, we have the equality
[L^{p_{0}}(\Omega),L^{p_{1}}(\Omega)]_{\theta}=L^{p}(\Omega)
(and a similar equality for
\Gamma
) for
\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1-\theta}{p_{0}}+\frac{\theta}{p_{1}}
in the sense of equivalent norms, see
[
Tri95
, Theorem 1.18.6/2]
. From this, we obtain for all

\theta\in(0,1)
the
continuity of
R_{\lambda}:X_{\theta}\to Y_{\theta}
where

X_{\theta}\coloneqq L^{p}(\Omega)\times L^{p}(\Gamma)

and
Y_{\theta}\coloneqq L^{\varphi(p)}(\Omega)\times L^{\varphi(p)}(\Gamma)
with
p
and
\varphi(p)
being
defined by
\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1-\theta}{2}+\frac{\theta}{d}
and

\frac{1}{\varphi(p)}=\frac{1-\theta}{2}
. For
p\in(2,d)
, the first equation yields

\theta=\frac{d(p-2)}{(d-2)p}
, and the second equation gives

\varphi(p)=\frac{2}{1-\theta}=\frac{d-2}{d-p}\;p.

This proves the assertion for
p\in(2,d)
. For
p\geq d
the statement follows
directly from (
4.12
).∎

However, the estimate we actually would like to make use of would be of type (
4.9
) for solutions of the inhomogeneous
Robin
problem with
\lambda=0
, i.e.

(4.13)

\displaystyle-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\partial^{Q}_{\nu}u+\delta\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma,

because in order to obtain higher regularity for the Wentzell problem we decouple it into two underlying Robin problems of precisely that form.
Though Hölder continuity for the Robin case is also established in
[
Nit10
, Example 4.2.7]
, this is not helpful in our situation, since an explicit estimate of type (
4.8
) is not given there due to the complexity of the bootstrapping argument, and we cannot deduce (
4.9
), as before.
To avoid this obstacle, we rewrite the Robin problem into a Neumann problem, to which we apply Proposition
4.4
. The price we pay is that the solution
u
appears on the right-hand side and we have to assume a priori that its integrability is as high as the data’s.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
d\geq 2,\,p\in(2,\infty)
. Then, there is a constant
C_{0}>0
such that whenever
u\in H^{3/2}_{B}(\Omega)
is a weak solution of (
4.13
) with
f,u\in L^{p}(\Omega)
, as well as
g,\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u\in L^{p}(\Gamma)
, we have
(u,\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u)\in L^{\varphi(p)}(\Omega)\times L^{\varphi(p)}(\Gamma)
and

\|u\|_{\Omega,\varphi(p)}+\|\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u\|_{\Gamma,\varphi(p)}\leq C_{0}\Big(\|u\|_{\Omega,p}+\|f\|_{\Omega,p}+\|g\|_{\Gamma,p}+\|\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u\|_{\Gamma,p}\Big)

where

(4.14)

\varphi(p)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{d-2}{d-p}\,p&\textnormal{ if }p\in(2,d),\\
\infty&\textnormal{ if }p\in[d,\infty).\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
be a weak solution of (
4.13
). Then, given any
v\in D(B_{0})\subseteq D(B_{N})
,

\left\langle u,B_{0}v\right\rangle_{\Omega}={\mathfrak{b}}(u,v)={\mathfrak{b}}_{\delta}(u,v)=\left\langle f,v\right\rangle_{\Gamma}.

Hence,
u\in D(B_{0}^{*})
,
f=B_{0}^{*}u=(B_{0,\delta})^{*}u
by Theorem
3.18
(ii). Once more, we have
B_{\max}u=B_{0}^{*}u=f
by Theorem
3.14
(ii), so the first line of (
4.13
) holds in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, where
-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla
is seen as
L^{2}(\Omega)
-realization of an object in
H^{-2}(\Omega)
. Furthermore, for all
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
we obtain

\left\langle-(B_{0,\delta})^{*}u,v\right\rangle_{\Omega}+{\mathfrak{b}}_{\delta}(u,v)=\left\langle g,\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}v\right\rangle_{\Gamma}

for all
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, which shows
u\in D(N^{{\mathfrak{b}}_{\delta}})
and (by Theorem
3.18
)
-N^{{\mathfrak{b}}_{\delta}}u=\partial_{\nu}^{Q}u+\delta\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u=g
. Therefore,
u
also solves (
4.6
) with
\lambda=1
,
\tilde{f}=f+u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, and
\tilde{g}=g-\delta\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
, whence it must coincide with this problem’s unique solution. Hence, Proposition
4.4
is applicable and as, due to the extra assumption,
(u,\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u)
is also an element of
L^{p}(\Omega)\times L^{p}(\Gamma)
, so is
(\tilde{f},\tilde{g})
. Then, by Proposition
4.4
we have

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\Omega,\varphi(p)}+\|\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u\|_{\Gamma,\varphi(p)}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\|\tilde{f}\|_{\Omega,p}+\|\tilde{g}\|_{\Gamma,p}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}\Big(\|u\|_{\Omega,p}+\|f\|_{\Omega,p}+\|g\|_{\Gamma,p}+\|\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u\|_{\Gamma,p}\Big)

as desired.
∎

We obtain the following corollary about the integrability of elements of
D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})
, where
\varphi(r)
is defined as in (
4.14
).

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
r>2
.
If
\mathcal{u}\in D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})\cap(L^{r}(\Omega)\times L^{r}(\Gamma))
,
{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}\mathcal{u}\in L^{r}(\Omega)\times L^{r}(\Gamma)
and
(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1},\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1})\in L^{r}(\Omega)\times L^{r}(\Gamma)
, then

\mathcal{u}\in L^{\varphi(r)}(\Omega)\times L^{\varphi(r)}(\Gamma)\textrm{\penalty\ and\penalty\ }(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1},\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1})\in L^{\varphi(r)}(\Omega)\times L^{\varphi(r)}(\Gamma).

Proof.

By Theorem
3.18
, we have for
\mathcal{u}\in D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})

\displaystyle({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}\mathcal{u})_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla\alpha(\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1}),

\displaystyle({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}\mathcal{u})_{2}

\displaystyle=-\beta(\partial_{\nu}^{Q}+\delta\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}})(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1})+\gamma u_{2}.

Thus, if
\mathcal{u}
satisfies the assumption of this corollary, then
w=-\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1}

solves the inhomogeneous Robin problem

\displaystyle-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla w

\displaystyle=({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}\mathcal{u})_{1}\in L^{r}(\Omega)

\displaystyle(\partial_{\nu}^{Q}+\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}\delta)w

\displaystyle=\beta^{-1}({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}\mathcal{u})_{2}-\beta^{-1}\gamma u_{2}\in L^{r}(\Gamma).

As
(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1},\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1})\in L^{r}(\Omega)\times L^{r}(\Gamma)
by assumption, so is
(w,\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}w)
. Hence, by Lemma
4.5
,
(w,\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}w)\in L^{\varphi(r)}(\Omega)\times L^{\varphi(r)}(\Gamma)
, which also implies
\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1}\in L^{\varphi(r)}(\Omega)
as the functions
\alpha,\alpha^{-1}
are bounded.
Naturally
L^{\varphi(r)}(\Omega)\subseteq L^{r}(\Omega)
.
Since
\mathcal{u}\in D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})\cap(L^{r}(\Omega)\times L^{r}(\Gamma))
, we also know that
(\partial_{\nu}^{Q}+\delta\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}})u_{1}=0
and
(u,\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u)=(u_{1},u_{2})\in L^{r}(\Omega)\times L^{r}(\Gamma)
, whence
u_{1}
solves the homogeneous Robin problem

\displaystyle-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1}

\displaystyle=-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1}\in L^{r}(\Omega),

\displaystyle(\partial_{\nu}^{Q}+\delta\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}})u_{1}

\displaystyle=0\in L^{r}(\Gamma).

Applying Lemma
4.5
once more yields
u_{1}\in L^{\varphi(r)}(\Gamma)
and
u_{2}=\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u_{1}\in L^{\varphi(r)}(\Gamma)
, as claimed.
∎

We can now prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{u}\in D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}^{\infty})
. Then
u_{1}\in C^{0,\vartheta}(\Omega)
for some
\vartheta\in(0,1)
. In particular, this shows Hölder continuity of
u_{1}(t,\cdot)
for
t>0
due to the analyticity of the semigroup
{\mathcal{T}}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{u}\in D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})
. Then
u_{1},\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1}\in H^{3/2}_{\Delta}(\Omega)\subseteq H^{1}(\Omega)
. Furthermore,
\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u_{1}
,
\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1})\in H^{1}(\Gamma)
. By Sobolev embedding (see
[
AF03
, Theorem 4.12]
), we obtain
H^{1}\subseteq L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}
. Hence, for
d\leq 4
, we have
H^{1}\subseteq L^{d},
which shows

D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})\subseteq\{\mathcal{u}\in L^{d}(\Omega)\times L^{d}(\Gamma)\penalty\ |\penalty\ \big(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1},\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1})\big)\in L^{d}(\Omega)\times L^{d}(\Gamma)\}.

If
d\geq 5
, we have
u_{1},\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1}\in L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}(\Omega)
and
\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u_{1},\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1})\in L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}(\Gamma).

This shows

D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})\subseteq\{\mathcal{u}\in L^{r_{1}}(\Omega)\times L^{r_{1}}(\Gamma)\penalty\ |\penalty\ \big(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1},\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1})\big)\in L^{r_{1}}(\Omega)\times L^{r_{1}}(\Gamma)\}

for
r_{1}=\frac{2d}{d-2}.

Inductively, we obtain

D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}^{k})\subseteq\{\mathcal{u}\in L^{r_{k}}(\Omega)\times L^{r_{k}}(\Gamma)\penalty\ |\penalty\ \big(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1},\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1})\big)\in L^{r_{k}}(\Omega)\times L^{r_{k}}(\Gamma)\},

where
r_{k}=\varphi(r_{k-1})=\varphi^{k-1}(\frac{2d}{d-2})
.
Indeed, assume this statement is true for some
k
and consider
\mathcal{u}\in D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}^{k+1})
. Then
\mathcal{u}\in D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}^{k})\subseteq D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})

and
{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}\mathcal{u}\in D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}^{k})
. By induction hypothesis,
\mathcal{u},{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}\mathcal{u}\in L^{r_{k}}(\Omega)\times L^{r_{k}}(\Gamma)
, and
\big(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1},\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1})\big)\in L^{r_{k}}(\Omega)\times L^{r_{k}}(\Gamma)
. Hence, Corollary
4.6
yields
\mathcal{u}\in L^{\varphi(r_{k})}(\Omega)\times L^{\varphi(r_{k})}(\Gamma)=L^{r_{k+1}}(\Omega)\times L^{r_{k+1}}(\Gamma)
as well as

\big(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1},\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1})\big)\in L^{\varphi(r_{k})}(\Omega)\times L^{\varphi(r_{k})}(\Gamma)=L^{r_{k+1}}(\Omega)\times L^{r_{k+1}}(\Gamma).

From the structure of the map
\varphi
it is clear that
(r_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{{\mathbb{N}}}}
is an increasing sequence that tends to
\infty
.
Hence for all
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have found a
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}^{k_{0}})\subseteq\{\mathcal{u}\in L^{d}(\Omega)\times L^{d}(\Gamma)\penalty\ |\penalty\ \big(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1},\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}(\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1})\big)\in L^{d}(\Omega)\times L^{d}(\Gamma)\}.

For any such
\mathcal{u}\in D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}^{k_{0}})
,
we have
-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1}=-\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1}\eqqcolon\tilde{f}\in L^{d}(\Omega)
as well as
\partial_{\nu}^{Q}u_{1}=-\delta\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u_{1}\eqqcolon\tilde{g}\in L^{d}(\Gamma)
due to

,
\delta\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
. Thus

is a weak solution of (
4.6
) for

, and Proposition
4.4
(ii) implies

as claimed.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

The proof of Theorem
4.7
actually yields a number
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, depending only on the dimension
d
, such that
\mathcal{u}\in D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}^{k_{0}})
implies
u_{1}\in C^{0,\vartheta}(\Omega)
. The number
k_{0}
we calculated there is not sharp, but the embedding certainly does not hold for dimensions that are too large.

For example, for the case of Neumann boundary conditions, i.e.
\delta=0
, we can verify
D({\mathcal{A}})\subseteq C^{0,\vartheta}(\Omega)
for
d\leq 6
, simply as
\alpha\mathop{\textrm{{div}}}Q\nabla u_{1}\in H^{3/2}(\Omega)\subseteq L^{\frac{2d}{d-3}}(\Omega)\subseteq L^{d/2+\varepsilon}(\Omega)
from which the assertions follows from Proposition
4.4
(ii) for
\lambda=0
(cf.
[
AF03
, Theorem 7.34]
).

But, at least for constant
Q
and
\alpha
it is quite simple to construct functions in
D({\mathcal{A}})
which are not Hölder continuous for
d\geq 8
. As Sobolev embeddings are sharp, we know that for
d\geq 8
the Sobolev space
H^{4}(\Omega)
is not contained in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
Now, let
\Omega^{\prime}
be a smooth domain contained in
\Omega
.
Let
v
be a function that lives in
H^{4}(\Omega^{\prime})
but not in
L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})
. As
\Omega^{\prime}
is smooth there exists an extension of
v
(denoted by
v
again) to
H^{4}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, which still cannot be in
L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Now let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\varphi=1
on
\Omega^{\prime}
. Then the function
u=\varphi\cdot v\in H^{4}(\Omega)\setminus L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Moreover, it satisfies the Neumann boundary condition
\partial_{\nu}u=0
as it is compactly supported on
\Omega
. Hence
(u,\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u)\in D({\mathcal{A}})
(as all the other regularity conditions are implied by
H^{4}
-regularity because
Q
and
\alpha
are constant). However,
(u,\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u)\not\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\times L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
, so
\mathcal{u}
cannot be Hölder continuous.

Lemma 4.9

Lemma 4.11
.

(1)

If
\gamma=0
almost everywhere, then we have
\ker({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})\subseteq{\rm span}({\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega}},{\mathbbm{1}_{\Gamma}})
and
\int_{\Gamma}\delta|\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u_{1}|^{2}\textrm{d}S=0
.

(2)

If
\gamma=0
,
\delta=0
almost everywhere, then
\lambda_{1}=0
and
\ker({\mathcal{A}})={\rm span}({\mathbbm{1}}_{\Omega},{\mathbbm{1}}_{\Gamma}).

(3)

If
\gamma,\delta\geq 0
and either
\gamma>0
or
\delta>0
on a set of positive surface measure, then

\lambda_{1}>0
and we have
\ker({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})=\{0\}
.

(4)

If
\int_{\Gamma}\gamma\,{\mathrm{d}}S<0
, then
\lambda_{1}<0
.

Proof.

In cases (i)–(iii), we have
\gamma,\delta\geq 0
. Hence,
{\mathfrak{a}}_{\delta}
is accretive, so we have
\lambda_{1}\geq 0
. Thus, whether
\lambda_{1}=0
or
\lambda_{1}>0
depends only on
\ker({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})
.

(i) Suppose
\gamma=0
almost everywhere, then
\mathcal{u}\in\ker({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})
implies
u_{1}\in\ker(B_{N,\delta})
. More precisely, let
\mathcal{u}\in\mathrm{ker}({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})\subseteq D({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})\subseteq D({\mathfrak{a}}_{\delta})
. Then

0=\left\langle{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}\mathcal{u},\mathcal{u}\right\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}={\mathfrak{a}}_{\delta}(\mathcal{u},\mathcal{u})=\int_{\Omega}\alpha|B_{N,\delta}u_{1}|^{2}\mathrm{d}x.

It follows that
\alpha|B_{N,\delta}u_{1}|^{2}=0
and hence, since
\alpha(x)\geq\eta
,

. This means

0=\left\langle Q\nabla u_{1},\nabla u_{1}\right\rangle_{\Omega}+\left\langle\delta u_{1},u_{1}\right\rangle_{\Gamma}=\|\sqrt{Q}\nabla u_{1}\|_{\Omega}^{2}+\|\sqrt{\delta}\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u_{1}\|^{2}

and shows
\nabla u_{1}=0
, whence
u_{1}
is constant (and, therefore, also
u_{2}=\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u_{1}
). Moreover,
\int_{\Gamma}\delta|\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u_{1}|^{2}\mathrm{d}S=0

(ii) If
\delta=0

and the constant functions satisfy the Neumann boundary condition, which shows
\lambda_{1}=0.

(iii) If
\gamma=0
,
\delta>0
on a set of positive surface measure
\Gamma_{0}
, due to (i),
u_{1}
is still constant. But now, we also find a set of positive measure
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\Gamma
where
\delta>\varepsilon
. However, if we had
u=c\neq 0
by (i), this would yield

0=\int_{\Gamma}\delta|\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u_{1}|^{2}\mathrm{d}S\geq\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}\delta|\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u_{1}|^{2}\mathrm{d}S\geq\varepsilon c^{2}S(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})>0.

Analogously, if
\gamma>0
on a set of positive measure and
\mathcal{u}\in\ker({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})
, we calculate

\displaystyle 0=\left\langle{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}\mathcal{u},\mathcal{u}\right\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}={\mathfrak{a}}(\mathcal{u},\mathcal{u})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\alpha|\Delta u_{1}|^{2}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\beta^{-1}\gamma|u_{2}|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}S

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}\beta^{-1}\gamma c^{2}\,\mathrm{d}S\geq\|\beta\|^{-1}_{\infty}\varepsilon c^{2}S(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})>0,

another contradiction.

(iv) Plugging

In this short excursion, we point out that the abstract theory established in Chapter

is not necessarily restricted to problems of order 4, though identification of the abstract operators and regularity characterizations can be more difficult.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain,
H=L^{2}(\Omega)
, and let
\Delta_{N}
denote the Neumann Laplacian. For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
consider the form

{\mathfrak{b}}_{k}=\left\langle\Delta^{k}u,\Delta^{k}v\right\rangle_{\Omega}
on
D({\mathfrak{b}}_{k})=D(\Delta_{N}^{k})
. Then
{\mathfrak{b}}_{k}
is admissible and
B_{N}=\Delta_{N}^{2k},
whence also
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)\subset D(B_{N})\cap D(B_{D})
is satisfied, and we are in the situation of Theorem
2.12
and Remark
2.13
.

Proof.

The form
{\mathfrak{b}}_{k}
is symmetric and accretive by default. We have
\|u\|^{2}_{{\mathfrak{b}}_{k}}=\|u\|^{2}_{\Omega}+\|\Delta^{k}u\|_{\Omega}^{2}.
As
\Delta_{N}^{k}
is a closed operator.
D(\Delta_{N}^{k})
is a Hilbert space with respect to the graph norm and hence the form is closed. The continuity follows from Cauchy–Schwarz’s inequality. Thus
{\mathfrak{b}}_{k}
is generating and its associated operator
B_{N}
is self-adjoint.
We have
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)\subseteq D(\Delta_{N}^{k})\subseteq D(\Delta_{N})\subseteq H^{1}(\Omega)
and may choose
\rho=1/2
in (
2.1
). For the last embedding we calculate

\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=\left\langle\Delta u,u\right\rangle_{\Omega}\leq C(\|u\|_{\Omega}^{2}+\|\Delta u\|_{\Omega}^{2}).

That
D(\Delta^{k}_{N})
is continuously embedded in
D(\Delta^{N})
follows by the closed graph theorem applied to the identity id:
D(\Delta^{N}_{k})\rightarrow D(\Delta_{N})
.
D(\Delta_{N}^{k})
is also dense in
H^{1}(\Omega)
due to
[
Ouh05
, Proof of Lemma 1.25]
and the analyticity of the semigroup. We begin by showing that
B_{N}
coincides with
\Delta^{2k}
distributionally.
Let
u\in D(B_{N})
and
v\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)\subset D(\Delta_{N}^{2k})
, then
\left\langle f,\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle\Delta_{N}^{k}u,\Delta_{N}^{k}\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle(\Delta_{N}^{k})^{*}u,\Delta_{N}^{k}\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle u,\Delta^{2k}\varphi\right\rangle
as
\Delta^{k}\varphi\in D(\Delta_{N}^{k}).
Hence
f=\Delta^{2k}u
as
\Delta_{N}
and thus
\Delta_{N}^{k}
is self-adjoint. So
D(B_{N})\subset\{u\in D(\Delta_{N}^{k})\penalty\ |\penalty\ \Delta_{N}^{2k}u\in L^{2}(\Omega)\}.
On the other hand
D(\Delta_{N}^{2k})\subset D(B_{N})
as for the same reason for
u\in D(\Delta_{N}^{2k})
the relation
\left\langle\Delta_{N}^{2k}u,v\right\rangle=\left\langle\Delta_{N}^{k}u,\Delta_{N}^{k}v\right\rangle
holds for all
v\in D(\Delta_{N}^{k})
. So
\Delta^{2k}_{N}\subset B_{N}=B_{N}^{*}\subset(\Delta_{N}^{2k})^{*}=\Delta_{N}^{2k}.

∎

Now we obtain
D(B_{0})=D(B_{D})\cap D(B_{N})=D(B_{N})\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
by Proposition
2.4
. Thus
D(B_{0})=\{u\in D(\Delta_{N}^{2k})\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}u=0\}.

Now for
u\in D(B_{0}^{*})
, there is a
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that for all
v\in D(B_{0})
we have
\left\langle u,B_{0}v\right\rangle=\left\langle u,\Delta^{2k}v\right\rangle=\left\langle f,v\right\rangle_{\Omega}
, so

is a restriction of the maximal

-realization of the distribution
\Delta^{2k}

Hence (taking

) we can find a solution of the system

(5.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\Delta^{4k}u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\textnormal{ in }(0,\infty)\times\Omega,

\displaystyle\mathop{\textrm{{tr}}}\Delta^{4k}u+{\mathscr{N}}^{{\mathfrak{b}}}(\Delta^{2k})u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\textnormal{ on }(0,\infty)\times\Gamma,

\displaystyle{\mathscr{N}}^{{\mathfrak{b}}}u

\displaystyle u|_{t=0}
