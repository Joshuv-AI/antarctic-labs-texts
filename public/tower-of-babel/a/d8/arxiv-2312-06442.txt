1.
Introduction

The Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz inequality is a classical result in probability theory that establishes the speed of convergence of the empirical distribution function of a random variable to its true counterpart.
More accurately, let
X
be a real-valued random variable and set
F(t)=\mathbb{P}(X\leq t)
to be its distribution function.
Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
be independent copies of
X
, and denote the
empirical distribution function
by

F_{m}(t)=\mathbb{P}_{m}(X\leq t)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}1_{(-\infty,t]}(X_{i}).

The celebrated
Glivenko-Cantelli
theorem
[
11
,
17
,
30
]
implies that

\|F_{m}-F\|_{L_{\infty}}=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|F_{m}(t)-F(t)|\to 0

almost surly as
m
tends to infinity.
The
Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz
(DKW) inequality
[
13
]
extends the Glivenko-Cantelli theorem by specifying the correct speed of convergence.
The following formulation of the DKW inequality is from
[
24
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
\Delta>0
, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-2\Delta m)
,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\|F_{m}-F\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\sqrt{\Delta}.

The estimate (
1.1
) is optimal:
roughly put, if
X
is a ‘non-trivial’ random variable
1
1

1

For instance, if there is no
t\in\mathbb{R}
for which
\mathbb{P}(X=t)>0.99
.
, there are absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
that satisfy for every
\Delta\leq\frac{1}{2}
and
m\geq 4
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|F_{m}-F\|_{L_{\infty}}\geq c_{1}\sqrt{\Delta}\right)\geq c_{2}\exp(-c_{3}\Delta m).

Note that as absolute constants,
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
are strictly positive and do not depend on the random variable
X
(as long as
X
is ‘non-trivial’ in a reasonable sense).
For more information on this lower bound, see, e.g.,
[
8
]
.

While Theorem
1.1
is a characterisation of the speed of convergence of
\|F_{m}-F\|_{L_{\infty}}
in the case of a single random variable, modern applications often require dealing with a collection of random variables
simultaneously
.
Understanding the uniform behaviour of
\|F_{m}-F\|_{L_{\infty}}
is the goal of this article.

To formulate the question at hand, let
(\mathcal{X},\mu)
be a probability space and let
X
be distributed as
\mu
.
Set
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
to be independent copies of
X
, and let
\mathcal{H}
be a class of real valued functions defined on
\mathcal{X}
.
For
h\in\mathcal{H}
, let
F_{h}(t)=\mathbb{P}(h(X)\leq t)
be the distribution function of
h(X)
and put

F_{m,h}(t)=\mathbb{P}_{m}(h(X)\leq t)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}1_{(-\infty,t]}(h(X_{i}))

to be the corresponding empirical distribution function.

Question 1.2
.

Under what conditions on
X
and for what values of
\Delta
does a version of the DKW inequality hold uniformly in
\mathcal{H}
: that with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c\Delta m)
,

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\|F_{m,h}-F_{h}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\sqrt{\Delta}?

As a starting point, consider the case where

\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
X
is an isotropic
2
2

2

That is,
X
is centred and its covariance is the identity.
random vector. Let
A\subset S^{d-1}
and set

\mathcal{H}=\{\left\langle\cdot,x\right\rangle:x\in A\}.

Thus,
\mathcal{H}
is the class of
liner functionals
associated with
A
.
Let
F_{x}(t)=F_{\left\langle\cdot,x\right\rangle}(t)=\mathbb{P}(\left\langle X,x\right\rangle\leq t)
and set
F_{m,x}(t)=\mathbb{P}_{m}(\left\langle X,x\right\rangle\leq t)
.

1.1.
The current state of the art

Let us begin by describing (known) restrictions on
\Delta
.
The trivial case,
|A|=1
is fully understood thanks to the optimality of Theorem
1.1
.
In that case, a nontrivial estimate is possible only when
\Delta\gtrsim\frac{1}{m}
.

The other extreme case,
A=S^{d-1}
, was explored in
[
6
]

using Talagrand’s concentration inequality and standard methods from VC-theory.
The following formulation is an immediate outcome of
[
6
, Corollary 2.8]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

There are absolute constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that the following holds.
If
m\geq d
and

(1.2)

\Delta\geq c_{1}\frac{d}{m},

then with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{2}\Delta m)
,

(1.3)

\sup_{x\in S^{d-1}}\left\|F_{m,x}-F_{x}\right\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\sqrt{\Delta}.

Theorem
1.3
is clearly optimal for
\Delta
that satisfies (
1.2
), simply because (
1.3
) is the best one can expect even for a single random variable.
Moreover, one can show (e.g. using Proposition
6.1
below) that the restriction on
\Delta
in (
1.2
) is
optimal
: for example, if
X
is the standard gaussian random vector then

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{x\in S^{d-1}}\left\|F_{m,x}-F_{x}\right\|_{L_{\infty}}\geq c\sqrt{\frac{d}{m}}\right)\geq 0.9.

Thus, Question
1.2
is fully understood in the two extreme cases:
|A|=1
and
A=S^{d-1}
.
At the same time, the situation for an
arbitrary
set
A\subset S^{d-1}
is far more subtle.
Indeed, while Theorem
1.3
still applies, the restriction that
\Delta\gtrsim\frac{d}{m}
(and
m\gtrsim d
) is independent of
A
and therefore cannot be optimal.
One would expect a ‘continuous shift’ from
\Delta\sim\frac{d}{m}
for the entire sphere to
\Delta\sim\frac{1}{m}
for a singleton—that takes into account some appropriate notion of
complexity
of the set
A
.

There are various notions of complexity that one typically considers (e.g. the gaussian mean-width, see
[
5
,
29
,
31
]
), but the correct notation of complexity here turns out to be rather surprising:
Talagrand’s
\gamma_{1}
-functional
.

Definition 1.4
.

An
admissible sequence
of
A
is a collection of subsets
(A_{s})_{s\geq 0}
that satisfy
|A_{0}|=1
and
|A_{s}|\leq 2^{2^{s}}
for
s\geq 1
.
For
x\in A
let
\pi_{s}x\in A_{s}
be the nearest element to
x
with respect to the Euclidean distance. For
\alpha=1,2
define
Talagrand’s
\gamma_{\alpha}
-functional
by

\gamma_{\alpha}(A)=\inf\sup_{x\in A}\sum_{s\geq 0}2^{s/\alpha}\|x-\pi_{s}x\|_{2},

with the infimum taken over all admissible sequences of
A
.

We will assume without loss of generality that
A
is a symmetric set, that is,
A=-A
; in particular, it is standard to verify that
\gamma_{\alpha}(A)\geq 1
.

The
\gamma_{2}
-functional is a natural complexity parameter of
A
, seeing that is equivalent to the set’s gaussian mean-width.
Indeed, if
G
is the standard gaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then by Talagrand’s majorizing measures theorem
[
28
,
29
]
, for any
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

c\,\gamma_{2}(A)\leq\mathbb{E}\sup_{x\in A}\left\langle G,x\right\rangle\leq c^{\prime}\,\gamma_{2}(A),

for absolute constants
c
and
c^{\prime}
.

An indication why
\gamma_{2}(A)
is likely to be the wrong choice in the context of Question
1.2
is the fact that with high probability,

\displaystyle(\ast)=\sup_{x\in A}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle G_{i},x\right\rangle-\mathbb{E}\left\langle G,x\right\rangle\right|\sim\frac{\gamma_{2}(A)}{\sqrt{m}}.

Using tail integration it is evident that

(\ast)=\sup_{x\in A}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(F_{m,x}(t)-F_{x}(t)\big)\,dt\right|,

which can be significantly smaller than
\sup_{x\in A}\|F_{m,x}-F_{x}\|_{L_{\infty}}
.

At the same time, there is no apparent reason why
\gamma_{1}(A)
determines
\sup_{x\in A}\|F_{m,x}-F_{x}\|_{L_{\infty}}
.

Remark 1.5
.

It worth noting that there are several situations where
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}^{2}
are equivalent.
For instance,
\gamma_{1}(S^{d-1})\sim\gamma_{2}^{2}(S^{d-1})\sim d
; and if
A
is the set of
s
-sparse vectors in
S^{d-1}
then
\gamma_{1}(A)\sim\gamma_{2}^{2}(A)\sim s\log(\frac{ed}{s})
. However, in general there can be a substantial difference between the two:
for example, if
A
is a well-chosen spherical cap,
\gamma_{1}(A)\sim\sqrt{d}
and
\gamma_{2}^{2}(A)\sim 1
.

The following answer to Question
1.2
when the random vector
X
is the standard gaussian was recently established in
[
7
]
—it is an immediate outcome of Theorem 1.7 there.

Theorem 1.6

(
A\subset S^{d-1}
,
X
=gaussian)
.

There are absolute constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
for which the following holds.
Let
X=G
be the standard gaussian vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If
A\subset S^{d-1}
is a symmetric set,
m\geq\gamma_{1}(A)
, and

(1.4)

\Delta\geq c_{1}\frac{\gamma_{1}(A)}{m}\log^{3}\left(\frac{em}{\gamma_{1}(A)}\right),

then with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{2}\Delta m)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in A}\left\|F_{m,x}-F_{x}\right\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\sqrt{\Delta}.

Just as in Theorem
1.3
, the probability estimate is clearly optimal.
The only question is whether the restriction on
\Delta
in (
1.4
) can be improved, and it turns out that it cannot (up to, perhaps, logarithmic factors):
There is an absolute constant
c
and for every
A\subset S^{d-1}
and any
m
large enough, with probability at least
0.9
,

(1.5)

\sup_{x\in A}\|F_{m,x}-F_{x}\|_{L_{\infty}}\geq\frac{c}{\sqrt{\log(d)}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{1}(A)}{m}}.

More details on (
1.5
) can be found in Section
6.1
.

1.2.
General random vectors

Although Theorem
1.6
is (almost) a complete answer to Question
1.2
when
X
is the standard gaussian random vector, it does not reveal what happens for more general isotropic random vectors (nor what happens when considering an arbitrary class of functions for that matter).
When trying to extend Theorem
1.6
one immediately encounters a surprise:
The proof of Theorem
1.6
is based on a chaining argument, and it stands to reason that its assertion should remain true under a suitable tail-assumption on the random vector
X
(e.g. if
X
is subgaussian
3
3

3

An isotropic random vector
X
is subgaussian with constant
L
if for every
x\in S^{d-1}
and
t\geq 0
,
\mathbb{P}(|\left\langle X,x\right\rangle|\geq Lt)\leq 2\exp(-t^{2})
.
).
However, as we show in what follows, a strong tail assumption is not enough.
In fact, it turns out that:

For a uniform DKW inequality to hold, the marginal distributions of
X
must

\cdot

exhibit a fast tail decay
and

\cdot

satisfy a suitable small ball condition.

The obvious choice of random vectors that will be used as a benchmark in our analysis are isotropic with iid coordinates.
As it happens, the necessity of the two conditions (the fast tail decay and the small ball condition) are already exhibited on such random vectors.

Let
w
be a symmetric random variable with variance 1, and consider the random vector
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose coordinates are independent copies of
w
.
In particular,
X
is isotropic.
We also assume that
w
is ‘non-trivial’, e.g. in the sense that
\mathbb{P}(|w|\geq 0.1)\geq 0.1
.

Lemma 1.7
.

Suppose that
w
has an atom of size
\eta>0
(i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(w=t_{0})=\eta
for some
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
).
Then for every
m\geq\frac{c_{1}}{\eta^{2}}
there is
d\in\mathbb{N}
and a set
A\subset S^{d-1}
satisfying
\gamma_{1}(A)\leq 10
and

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{x\in A}\|F_{m,x}-F_{x}\|_{L_{\infty}}\geq c_{2}\cdot\eta\right)\geq 0.9.

If Theorem
1.6
were true for the random vector
X=(w_{1},\dots,w_{d})
, then it would follow that with high (polynomial) probability,

\sup_{x\in A}\left\|F_{m,x}-F_{x}\right\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq c\sqrt{\frac{\log^{3}(m)}{m}},

which is far from the true behaviour as seen in (
1.6
).
As an example, let
w
be the symmetric
\{-1,1\}
-valued random variable, and thus
X
is the standard Bernoulli random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
, and it follows from Lemma
1.7
that the Bernoulli random vector does not satisfy any reasonable uniform version of the DKW inequality.

The obvious conclusion is that once one moves from the gaussian to the subgaussian realm, the best possible bound on

\sup_{x\in A}\left\|F_{m,x}-F_{x}\right\|_{L_{\infty}}

is far weaker than in the gaussian case—unless one imposes more assumptions on
X
—starting with not having significant atoms.

Remark 1.8
.

(a)

As it happens, the situation is even worse than what is indicated by Lemma
1.7
:
The set
A
from the lemma can be chosen to have an Euclidean diameter that is arbitrarily small.
Hence, a satisfactory estimate on
\sup_{x\in A}\left\|F_{m,x}-F_{x}\right\|_{L_{\infty}}
that is based on
any
reasonable complexity parameter of
A
cannot follow from a tail assumption.

(b)

It is possible to show that Lemma
1.7
remains valid even if
w
has a density, but that density is unbounded in a suitable sense (roughly put, e.g. if there is an interval with length proportional to
\frac{\eta}{m}
on which the density is of order
m
).

Following Lemma
1.7
, a uniform DKW inequality cannot be true if
w
has significant atoms or a density that is unbounded in a suitable sense—and that cannot be remedied by a subgaussian tail behaviour.
The next result shows that some (relatively strong) tail assumption is also needed: without a
subexponential tail decay
4
4

4

An isotropic random vector
X
has a subexponential tail decay with constant
L
if for every
x\in S^{d-1}
and
t\geq 0
,
\mathbb{P}(|\left\langle X,x\right\rangle|\geq Lt)\leq 2\exp(-t)
.
a uniform DKW inequality cannot be true.

Lemma 1.9
.

Let
X
be an isotropic random vector with independent coordinates distributed according to a symmetric random variable
w
.
If
w
does not have a subexponential tail decay, then for every
m\geq 20
there is
d\in\mathbb{N}
and a set
A\subset S^{d-1}
satisfying
\gamma_{1}(A)\leq 10
and

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{x\in A}\|F_{m,x}-F_{x}\|_{L_{\infty}}\geq\frac{1}{10}\right)\geq 0.9.

The proofs of Lemma
1.7
and Lemma
1.9
are presented in Section
4
.

With these obstructions in mind, let us explore Question
1.2
for random vectors that satisfy the minimal conditions that are a ‘must’:

Assumption 1.10
.

Let
X
be an isotropic random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
There are constants
L,D\geq 1
for which:

(a)

For every
x\in S^{d-1}
and
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}(|\left\langle X,x\right\rangle|\geq Lt)\leq 2\exp(-t).

(b)

For every
x\in S^{d-1}
,
\left\langle X,x\right\rangle
has a density
f_{x}
satisfying that
\|f_{x}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq D
.

The main result of this article (formulated in the context of a class of linear functionals in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) is that those necessary conditions are actually sufficient for a uniform DKW inequality:

Theorem 1.11
.

For every
L,D\geq 1
there are constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
that depend only on
L
and
D
for which the following holds.
If
X
satisfies Assumption
1.10
,
A\subset S^{d-1}
is symmetric,
m\geq\gamma_{1}(A)
, and

\Delta\geq c_{1}\frac{\gamma_{1}(A)}{m}\log^{3}\left(\frac{em}{\gamma_{1}(A)}\right),

then with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{2}\Delta m)
,

\sup_{x\in A}\left\|F_{m,x}-F_{x}\right\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\sqrt{\Delta}.

Observe that the gaussian random vector is a ‘legal candidate’ in Theorem
1.11
: it satisfies Assumption
1.10
for absolute constants
L
and
D
.
As a result, by the lower bounds in the gaussian case, the probability estimate is optimal and the restriction on
\Delta
in Theorem
1.11
cannot be improved beyond logarithmic factors.

An important example of a family of random vectors that satisfy Assumption
1.10
is the one endowed by
log-concave
measures.
Indeed, it follows from Borell’s lemma (see, e.g.,
[
5
, Lemma 3.5.10]
) that if
X
is isotropic and log-concave, linear functionals
\left\langle X,x\right\rangle
exhibit a subexponential tail decay. Also, the densities of
\left\langle X,x\right\rangle
are uniformly bounded by an absolute constant
[
9
, Proposition 2.1]
.
Therefore, we have the following.

Corollary 1.12
.

There are absolute constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
for which the following holds.
Let
X
be an isotropic, log-concave random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
If
A\subset S^{d-1}
is a symmetric set,
m\geq\gamma_{1}(A)
and

\Delta\geq c_{1}\frac{\gamma_{1}(A)}{m}\log^{3}\left(\frac{em}{\gamma_{1}(A)}\right),

then with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{2}\Delta m)
,

\sup_{x\in A}\left\|F_{m,x}-F_{x}\right\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\sqrt{\Delta}.

The proof of Theorem
1.11
is presented in Section
3
, and the proofs of Lemma
1.7
and Lemma
1.9
can be found in Section
4
.
In Section
5
we present an application of the uniform DKW inequality to a collection of estimation problems.
These problems have been studied extensively in recent years, and their objective is estimating
\mathbb{E}\varphi(\left\langle X,x\right\rangle)
(for various choices of

) uniformly in

, using only the random sample
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
as data. Finally, Section

2.
Preliminary estimates

In this section we prove several basic estimates that play a crucial role in the proof of Theorem
3.2
.

The first observation is that if
X
and
Y
are random variables,
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
r>0
, then

\displaystyle\left\{X\leq t\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{X\leq t\ \wedge\ |X-Y|<r\right\}\cup\left\{X\leq t\ \wedge\ |X-Y|\geq r\right\}

(2.1)

\displaystyle\subset\left\{Y\leq t+r\right\}\cup\left\{|X-Y|\geq r\right\}.

In particular, using (
2
) for
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}_{m}
respectively,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\leq t)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(Y\leq t+r)+\mathbb{P}(|X-Y|\geq r)\text{ and}

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{m}(X\leq t)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{m}(Y\leq t+r)+\mathbb{P}_{m}(|X-Y|\geq r).

In fact, (
2.2
) is two-sided. Indeed,

\displaystyle\left\{Y\leq t+r\right\}=

\displaystyle\left\{Y\leq t-r\right\}\cup\left\{Y\in(t-r,t+r]\right\}

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\left\{X\leq t\right\}\cup\left\{|X-Y|\geq r\right\}\cup\left\{Y\in(t-r,t+r]\right\}

and therefore

(2.4)

\mathbb{P}(Y\leq t+r)\leq\mathbb{P}(X\leq t)+\mathbb{P}(|X-Y|\geq r)+\mathbb{P}(Y\in(t-r,t+r]).

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be random variables.
For any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
r>0
, we have that

\left|F_{X}(t)-F_{Y}(t)\right|\leq\mathbb{P}(|X-Y|\geq r)+\mathbb{P}(Y\in(t-r,t+r]).

In particular, it follows from Corollary
2.1
that if
Y
has a density
f_{Y}
that satisfies
\|f_{Y}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq D
, then

\left|F_{X}(t)-F_{Y}(t)\right|\leq\mathbb{P}(|X-Y|\geq r)+2Dr.

Next, let us turn to a simple ‘stability’ estimate: if
Y
is a random variable and
\|F_{m,Y}-F_{Y}\|_{L_{\infty}}
is small on some event, then for a random variable
X
that is ‘close to
Y
’ in an appropriate sense,
\|F_{m,X}-F_{X}\|_{L_{\infty}}
is also small on the same event. More accurately:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
X
and
Y
be random variables.
For any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
r>0
, we have that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|F_{m,X}-F_{X}\|_{L_{\infty}}&\leq\|F_{m,Y}-F_{Y}\|_{L_{\infty}}+[\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{P}_{m}](|X-Y|\geq r)\\
&\qquad+\mathbb{P}(Y\in(t+r,t+r]).\end{split}

Proof.

Fix
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
r>0
.
By (
2.3
),

\displaystyle F_{m,X}(t)

\displaystyle\leq F_{m,Y}(t+r)+\mathbb{P}_{m}(|X-Y|\geq r)

\displaystyle\leq F_{Y}(t+r)+\|F_{m,Y}-F_{Y}\|_{L_{\infty}}+\mathbb{P}_{m}(|X-Y|\geq r),

and by (
2.4
),

F_{Y}(t+r)\leq F_{X}(t)+\mathbb{P}(|X-Y|\geq r)+\mathbb{P}(Y\in(t-r,t+r]).

In a similar manner, by (
2.2
),

\displaystyle F_{X}(t)

\displaystyle\leq F_{Y}(t+r)+\mathbb{P}(|X-Y|\geq r)

\displaystyle\leq F_{Y}(t-r)+\mathbb{P}(Y\in(t-r,t+r])+\mathbb{P}(|X-Y|\geq r),

and by (
2.3
) (with the roles of
X
and
Y
reversed),

\displaystyle F_{Y}(t-r)

\displaystyle\leq F_{m,Y}(t-r)+\|F_{m,Y}-F_{Y}\|_{L_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq F_{m,X}(t)+\mathbb{P}_{m}(|X-Y|\geq r)+\|F_{m,Y}-F_{Y}\|_{L_{\infty}}.\qed

For the rest of this section, we assume that the random variables
X
and
Y
are absolutely continuous.
For simplicity, we also assume that their distribution functions are invertible; the necessary modifications when that is not the case are minimal.
In particular, setting
u=F_{X}(t)
,

\|F_{m,X}-F_{X}\|_{L_{\infty}}=\sup_{u\in(0,1)}\left|F_{m,X}(F^{-1}_{X}(u))-u\right|.

Next, let us turn to a continuity argument of a different flavour: that if
|F_{m,X}(F^{-1}_{X}(u))-u|
is small on a ‘grid’ of
(0,1)
, then
\|F_{m,X}-F_{X}\|_{\infty}
is small as well.
To that end, for
\delta\in(0,1/4)
, set

U_{\delta}=\left\{\delta\ell:1\leq\ell\leq\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\right\}.

Lemma 2.3
.

For every
\delta\in(0,1/4)
,

(2.6)

\|F_{m,X}-F_{X}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\delta+\max_{u\in U_{\delta}}\left|F_{m,X}(F^{-1}_{X}(u))-u\right|.

Proof.

Let
t\in\mathbb{R}
, consider
u=F_{X}(t)
, and set

\beta=\max_{w\in U_{\delta}}\left|F_{m,X}(F^{-1}_{X}(w))-w\right|.

We will only prove one direction of (
2.6
), namely that
F_{m,X}(t)-F_{X}(t)\leq\delta+\beta
.
The other direction follows the same path and is omitted.

Case 1:

u\in[0,\delta)
.
It follows from the monotonicity of
F_{X}
that
t\leq F_{X}^{-1}(\delta)
, and by the monotonicity of
F_{m,X}
and the definition of
\beta
,

F_{m,X}(t)\leq F_{m,X}(F^{-1}_{X}(\delta))\leq\delta+\beta\leq F_{X}(t)+\delta+\beta.

Case 2:

u\in[\delta,1/2]
.
Denote by
w
the smallest element in
U_{\delta}
that satisfies
w\geq u
; in particular
|u-w|\leq\delta
.
By the monotonicity of
F_{X}
,
F_{X}^{-1}(u)\leq F_{X}^{-1}(w)
, and by the monotonicity of
F_{m,X}
,

F_{m,X}(t)=F_{m,X}(F^{-1}_{X}(u))\leq F_{m,X}(F^{-1}_{X}(w)).

Moreover,

F_{m,X}(F^{-1}_{X}(w))\leq w+\beta\leq u+\delta+\beta=F_{X}(t)+\delta+\beta,

and therefore
F_{m,X}(t)-F_{X}(t)\leq\delta+\beta
, as claimed.

Case 3:

u\in(1/2,1]
.
The proof follows an identical path to the case
u\in[0,1/2]
, and is omitted.
∎

The final ingredients we need are estimates on the symmetric differences of ‘level sets’ of functions that have the same measure.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
X
and
Y
be absolutely continuous random variables and assume that their densities satisfy that
\|f_{X}\|_{L_{\infty}},\|f_{Y}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq D
.
Then for every
u\in(0,1)
and
r>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{X\leq F^{-1}_{X}(u)\right\}\ \bigtriangleup\ \left\{Y\leq F_{Y}^{-1}(u)\right\}\right)\leq 4\bigl(\mathbb{P}(|X-Y|\geq r)+rD\bigr).

The proof of Lemma
2.4
requires some preparations.
Observe that by the union bound,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{X\leq F^{-1}_{X}(u)\right\}\ \bigtriangleup\ \left\{Y\leq F_{Y}^{-1}(u)\right\}\right)\leq 2\min\{u,1-u\};

hence, we may assume that
\delta=\mathbb{P}(|X-Y|\geq r)<\min\{u,1-u\}
—otherwise the lemma is trivially true.

The key to the proof of Lemma
2.4
is the fact that if
\delta<\min\{u,1-u\}
then

(2.7)

F_{X}^{-1}(u)\leq F_{Y}^{-1}\left(u+\delta\right)+r.

Indeed, following (
2.2
) once again, we have that
F_{Y}(t)\leq F_{X}(t+r)+\delta
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
r>0
.
Setting
t=F^{-1}_{X}(u)-r
,

F_{Y}(F_{X}^{-1}(u)-r)\leq u+\delta,

and (
2.7
) follows.

Proof of Lemma
2.4
.

We only consider the case
u\leq 1/2
.
If
\delta=\mathbb{P}(|X-Y|\geq r)\geq u
then the claim is trivially true; thus, assume that
\delta<u
.

Clearly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X\leq F^{-1}_{X}(u)\,,\,Y>F_{Y}^{-1}(u)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(X\leq F^{-1}_{X}(u)\,,\,Y\geq F_{Y}^{-1}(u+\delta)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{P}\left(X\leq F^{-1}_{X}(u)\,,\,Y\in(F^{-1}_{Y}(u),F^{-1}_{Y}(u+\delta))\right)

\displaystyle=(1)+(2).

Firstly, note that

(2)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(Y\in(F^{-1}_{Y}(u)\,,\,F^{-1}_{Y}(u+\delta))\right)=(u+\delta)-u=\delta.

Secondly, by (
2.7
),
F_{X}^{-1}(u)\leq F_{Y}^{-1}(u+\delta)+r
.
Therefore, if
t=F_{X}^{-1}(u)
,

(1)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(X\leq t\,,\,Y\geq t-r\right).

Also,

\displaystyle\left\{X\leq t\,,\,Y\geq t-r\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{X\leq t\,,\,Y\geq t-r\,,\,|X-Y|<r\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\cup\left\{X\leq t\,,\,Y\geq t-r\,,\,|X-Y|\geq r\right\}

\displaystyle\subset\left\{X\in[t-2r,t]\right\}\cup\left\{|X-Y|\geq r\right\},

and therefore

\displaystyle(1)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(X\leq t\,,\,Y\geq t-r\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(X\in[t-2r,t]\right)+\mathbb{P}(|X-Y|\geq r)

\displaystyle\leq 2rD+\delta.

An identical argument can be used to estimate
\mathbb{P}(X>F^{-1}_{X}(u)\,,\,Y\leq F_{Y}^{-1}(u))
, completing the proof.
∎

3.
Proofs of the main results

The main results of this article are actually true in a more general setting then merely for a class of linear functionals.
In what follows,
X
is a random variable taking values in a measurable space
\mathcal{X}
and
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
are independent copies of
X
.
Let

\mathcal{H}\subset S(L_{2}(X))=\left\{h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\text{ measurable and }\mathbb{E}h^{2}(X)=1\right\},

and set
\|h\|_{L_{2}}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}h^{2}(X)}
.
We ignore (well understood) measurability issues that may be easily dealt with by assuming that
\mathcal{H}
is regular in an appropriate sense.

For
h\in\mathcal{H}
, let

F_{h}(t)=\mathbb{P}(h(X)\leq t)\quad\text{and}\quad F_{m,h}(t)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}1_{(-\infty,t]}(h(X_{i}))

be the real and empirical distribution function of
h
.

An
admissible sequence
of
\mathcal{H}
is a collection of subsets
(\mathcal{H}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
that satisfy
|\mathcal{H}_{0}|=1
and
|\mathcal{H}_{s}|\leq 2^{2^{s}}
for
s\geq 1
.
For
h\in\mathcal{H}
let
\pi_{s}h\in\mathcal{H}_{s}
be the nearest element to
h
with respect to the
L_{2}
distance. Talagrand’s
\gamma_{1}
-functional is defined by

\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{H})=\inf\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{s\geq 0}2^{s}\|h-\pi_{s}h\|_{L_{2}},

with the infimum taken over all admissible sequences of
\mathcal{H}
.
We assume that
\mathcal{H}
is symmetric class of functions, that is,
\mathcal{H}=-\mathcal{H}
; in particular,
\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{H})\geq 1
.

Just as in the linear case, the ‘trivial obstacles’—namely, the existence of atoms and a relatively slow tail decay—are removed by assuming the following:

Assumption 3.1
.

There are constants
L
and
D
for which the following holds:

(a)

For every
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}\cup\{0\}
and
t>0
,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|h(X)-h^{\prime}(X)|\geq tL\|h-h^{\prime}\|_{L_{2}}\right)\leq 2\exp(-t).

(b)

For every
h\in\mathcal{H}
, the density
f_{h}
of
h(X)
exists and satisfies
\|f_{h}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq D
.

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of the following generalization of Theorem
1.11
.

Theorem 3.2
.

For every
L,D\geq 1
there are constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
that depend only on
L
and
D
, and for which the following holds.
If Assumption
3.1
is satisfied,
m\geq\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{H})
and

\Delta\geq c_{1}\frac{\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{H})}{m}\log^{3}\left(\frac{em}{\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{H})}\right),

then with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{2}\Delta m)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\|F_{m,h}-F_{h}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq c_{3}\sqrt{\Delta}.

Remark 3.3
.

Note that Theorem
3.2
immediately implies Theorem
1.11
.

The proof of Theorem
3.2
relies on a chaining argument. For a comprehensive study of chaining methods and their applications, we refer to Talagrand’s treasured book
[
29
]
.

We may and do assume that
\Delta\leq\frac{1}{10}
and
m\geq 10
. To ease notation, we also assume that for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
the distribution function
F_{h}
is invertible; the modifications needed in the general case are straightforward.

Let
c_{0}=c_{0}(L,D)
be a (small) constant that is specified in what follows, and set
\Delta
to satisfy that

(3.2)

\displaystyle c_{0}\frac{\Delta m}{\log^{3}(e/\Delta)}\geq\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{H}).

Consider
(\mathcal{H}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
for which

(3.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{s\geq 0}2^{s}\|h-\pi_{s}h\|_{L_{2}}\leq 2\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{H}),

and set
s_{0}
and
s_{1}
to be the smallest integers that satisfy

2^{s_{0}}\geq\Delta m\quad\text{and}\quad 2^{s_{1}}\geq\sqrt{\Delta}m;

in particular
2^{s_{0}}\leq 2\Delta m
and
2^{s_{1}}\leq 2\sqrt{\Delta}m
.

The proof of Theorem
3.2
consists of three steps that are outlined here.
The complete arguments are presented in Sections
3.1
–
3.3
.

Invoking Lemma
2.2
(with
r=\sqrt{\Delta}
),

\displaystyle\left\|F_{m,h}-F_{h}\right\|_{L_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\|F_{m,\pi_{s_{1}}h}-F_{\pi_{s_{1}}h}\|_{L_{\infty}}+2D\sqrt{\Delta}

\displaystyle\qquad+[\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{P}_{m}]\left(|h(X)-\pi_{s_{1}}h(X)|\geq\sqrt{\Delta}\right)

\displaystyle=(1)+(2)+(3)

and one has to show that on a high probability event and uniformly in
h\in\mathcal{H}
,
(1)
and
(3)
are at most of order
\sqrt{\Delta}
.

To that end, by the choice of
s_{1}
and the restriction on
\Delta
in (
3.2
),

\|h-\pi_{s_{1}}h\|_{L_{2}}\leq\frac{2\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{H})}{2^{s_{1}}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{\Delta}}{L\log(e/\Delta)};

therefore, by the subexponential tail decay,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|h(X)-\pi_{s_{1}}h(X)|\geq\sqrt{\Delta}\right)\leq\sqrt{\Delta}.

An ‘empirical version’ of (
3.4
) is also true, and its proof is presented in Section
3.2
.

The combination of these two facts implies that
(3)\lesssim\sqrt{\Delta}
.

At the heart of the proof for Theorem
3.2
is the estimate on
(1)
: that with high probability,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\|F_{m,\pi_{s_{1}}h}-F_{\pi_{s_{1}}h}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq c(L,D)\sqrt{\Delta}.

The proof of (
3.5
) is based on an unorthodox chaining argument, though its starting point is rather obvious: seeing ‘how far’ the DKW inequality for a single random variable takes us.

Lemma 3.4
.

There is an absolute constant
c_{1}
such that with probability at least
1-2\exp(-\Delta m)
, for every
h\in\mathcal{H}

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|F_{m,\pi_{s_{0}}h}-F_{\pi_{s_{0}}h}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq c_{1}\sqrt{\Delta}.

The proof of Lemma
3.4
is obvious: by Theorem
1.1
, (
3.6
) holds for every fixed
h\in\mathcal{H}
with probability at least
1-2\exp(-3\Delta m)
. Since

|\{\pi_{s_{0}}h:h\in\mathcal{H}\}|\leq 2^{2^{s_{0}}}\leq\exp(2\Delta m),

the claim follows from the union bound.

3.1.
Going further down the chain

With
\{\pi_{s_{0}}h:h\in\mathcal{H}\}
being the starting points of each chain, the next step is to go further down the chains — up to
\pi_{s_{1}}h
.

Lemma 3.5
.

There is an absolute constant
c_{1}
and a constant
c_{2}
depending only on
L
and
D
such that with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{1}\Delta m)
, for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\|F_{m,\pi_{s_{1}}h}-F_{\pi_{s_{1}}h}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq c_{2}\sqrt{\Delta}.

Rather than controlling the
L_{\infty}
-norm directly, it follows from Lemma
2.3
that it is enough to bound the difference on a grid.
Indeed, setting

U_{\Delta}=\left\{\ell\Delta:1\leq\ell\leq\frac{1}{\Delta}-1\right\},

the wanted estimate follows once we show that with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{1}\Delta m)
, for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
and
u\in U_{\Delta}
,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\left|F_{m,\pi_{s_{1}}h}(F_{\pi_{s_{1}}h}^{-1}(u))-u\right|\leq c_{2}(L,D)\sqrt{\Delta}.

To establish (
3.8
), note that

\displaystyle\left|F_{m,\pi_{s_{1}}h}(F_{\pi_{s_{1}}h}^{-1}(u))-u\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|F_{m,\pi_{s_{1}}h}(F_{\pi_{s_{1}}h}^{-1}(u))-F_{m,\pi_{s_{0}}h}(F_{\pi_{s_{0}}h}^{-1}(u))\right|

\displaystyle\qquad+\left|F_{m,\pi_{s_{0}}h}(F_{\pi_{s_{0}}h}^{-1}(u))-u\right|

\displaystyle=(1)+(2).

By Lemma
3.4
, with high probability and uniformly in
h\in\mathcal{H}
,

(2)\leq\sup_{u\in(0,1)}\left|F_{m,\pi_{s_{0}}h}(F_{\pi_{s_{0}}h}^{-1}(u))-u\right|=\|F_{m,\pi_{s_{0}}h}-F_{\pi_{s_{0}}h}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq c_{2}\sqrt{\Delta}.

To control
(1)
, we first show that a well-chosen auxiliary functional dominates that term, and then show that this functional can be controlled using
\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{H})
.

From here on, denote the
symmetric difference
between level sets of
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
at probability value
u\in(0,1)
by

\displaystyle{\rm S}^{u}[h,h^{\prime}]=\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{h(X)\leq F^{-1}_{h}(u)\right\}\ \bigtriangleup\ \left\{h^{\prime}(X)\leq F_{h^{\prime}}^{-1}(u)\right\}\right).

Remark 3.6
.

In the setup explored in
[
7
]
we only consider classes of linear functionals, i.e., with each
h
of the form
h=\left\langle\cdot,x\right\rangle
. Moreover,
X=G
is the standard gaussian vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the analogue of
{\rm S}^{u}[h,h^{\prime}]
is

{\rm S}_{t}[h,h^{\prime}]=\mathbb{P}(\{h(X)\leq t\}\ \bigtriangleup\ \{h^{\prime}(X)\leq t\}),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

It follows from the rotation invariance of the standard gaussian random vector that
\mathbb{P}(\{h(X)\leq t\})=\mathbb{P}(\{h^{\prime}(X)\leq t\})
.
That feature is crucial for the argument used in
[
7
]
but is not true here—which is the reason for using
{\rm S}^{u}[h,h^{\prime}]
instead of
{\rm S}_{t}[h,h^{\prime}]
.

Lemma 3.7
.

There are absolute constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
for which, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{1}\Delta m)
, for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
and

,

\displaystyle\left|F_{m,\pi_{s_{1}}h}(F_{\pi_{s_{1}}h}^{-1}(u))-F_{m,\pi_{s_{0}}h}(F_{\pi_{s_{0}}h}^{-1}(u))\right|\leq c_{2}\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{s_{1}-1}\left(\frac{2^{s}}{m}+\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{m}\mathbb{P}\left({\rm S}^{u}[\pi_{s+1}h,\pi_{s}h]\right)}\right).

The following (simple) observation is at the heart of the proof of Lemma
3.7
.

Lemma 3.8
.

There is an absolute constant
c
such that for every
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
,
u\in(0,1)
, and
t\geq 0
, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-t)
,

\displaystyle\left|F_{m,h}\left(F_{h}^{-1}(u)\right)-F_{m,h^{\prime}}\left(F_{h^{\prime}}^{-1}(u)\right)\right|\leq c\left(\frac{t}{m}+\sqrt{\frac{t}{m}\mathbb{P}\left({\rm S}^{u}[h,h^{\prime}]\right)}\right).

Proof.

For
1\leq i\leq m
, set

Z_{i}=1_{(-\infty,F_{h}^{-1}(u)]}(h(X_{i}))-1_{(-\infty,F_{h^{\prime}}^{-1}(u)]}(h^{\prime}(X_{i}))

and let
Z
be distributed as
Z_{1}
.
Using this notation,

\displaystyle F_{m,h}\left(F_{h}^{-1}(u)\right)-F_{m,h^{\prime}}\left(F_{h^{\prime}}^{-1}(u)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{i},

and clearly
\|Z\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq 2
,
\mathbb{E}Z=u-u=0
, and
\mathbb{E}Z^{2}=\mathbb{P}({\rm S}^{u}[h,h^{\prime}])
.
The claim follows immediately from Bernstein’s inequality (see, e.g.,
[
10
, Theorem 2.10]
).
∎

Proof of Lemma

.

Let us show that for every

, with probability at least

,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left|F_{m,\pi_{s_{1}}h}(F_{\pi_{s_{1}}h}^{-1}(u))-F_{m,\pi_{s_{0}}h}(F_{\pi_{s_{0}}h}^{-1}(u))\right|\leq c_{2}\sqrt{\Delta}.

Clearly, once (
3.9

|U_{\Delta}|\leq\frac{1}{\Delta}
and by the restriction on
\Delta
in (

) we may assume that

.

To prove (
3.9
), fix
u\in U_{\Delta}
and observe that for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
,

\displaystyle F_{m,\pi_{s_{1}}h}(F_{\pi_{s_{1}}h}^{-1}(u))-F_{m,\pi_{s_{0}}h}(F_{\pi_{s_{0}}h}^{-1}(u))

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{s_{1}-1}\left(F_{m,\pi_{s+1}h}(F_{\pi_{s+1}h}^{-1}(u))-F_{m,\pi_{s}h}(F_{\pi_{s}h}^{-1}(u))\right).

3.8
implies that with probability at least

,

\displaystyle F_{m,\pi_{s+1}h}(F_{\pi_{s+1}h}^{-1}(u))-F_{m,\pi_{s}h}(F_{\pi_{s}h}^{-1}(u))\leq c_{2}\left(\frac{2^{s}}{m}+\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{m}\mathbb{P}({\rm S}^{u}[\pi_{s+1}h,\pi_{s}h])}\right).

And since

that with probability at least

\displaystyle\left|F_{m,\pi_{s_{1}}h}(F_{\pi_{s_{1}}h}^{-1}(u))-F_{m,\pi_{s_{0}}h}(F_{\pi_{s_{0}}h}^{-1}(u))\right|\leq c_{2}\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{s_{1}-1}\left(\frac{2^{s}}{m}+\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{m}\mathbb{P}({\rm S}^{u}[\pi_{s+1}h,\pi_{s}h])}\right).\quad\qed

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

Invoking Lemma
2.3
and Lemma

, it suffices to show that for every
u\in U_{\Delta}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\ast)_{u}&=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{s_{1}-1}\left(\frac{2^{s}}{m}+\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{m}\mathbb{P}({\rm S}^{u}[\pi_{s+1}h,\pi_{s}h])}\right)\\
&\leq c\left(\sqrt{\Delta}+\log^{3/2}\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{H})}{m}}\right),\end{split}

where

is a constant that depends only on
L
and

.
Indeed, if (
3.11
) is true, then the claim follows from the restriction on
\Delta
in (
3.2
).

To estimate

, observe that

\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{s_{1}-1}\frac{2^{s}}{m}\leq\frac{2^{s_{1}}}{m}\leq 2\sqrt{\Delta},

and all that is left is to control the second term in the definition of

.

To that end, set

\delta_{s}(h)=\|\pi_{s+1}h(X)-\pi_{s}h(X)\|_{L_{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad r_{s}(h)=L\delta_{s}(h)\log\left(\frac{e}{\delta_{s}(h)}\right).

By the subexponential tail decay,

\mathbb{P}(|\pi_{s+1}h(X)-\pi_{s}h(X)|\geq r_{s}(h))\leq 2\exp\left(-\log\left(\frac{e}{\delta_{s}(h)}\right)\right)\leq\delta_{s}(h)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({\rm S}_{u}[\pi_{s+1}h,\pi_{s}h])

\displaystyle\leq 4\big(\mathbb{P}(|\pi_{s+1}h(X)-\pi_{s}h(X)|\geq r_{s}(h))+r_{s}(h)D\big)

\displaystyle\leq(4+DL)\delta_{s}(h)\log\left(\frac{e}{\delta_{s}(h)}\right).

Next, since
2^{s_{0}}\geq\Delta m
and
2^{s_{1}}\leq 2\sqrt{\Delta}m
, it follows that

s_{1}-s_{0}\leq\log_{2}(2\sqrt{\Delta}m)-\log_{2}(\Delta m)\leq\log\left(\frac{4}{\Delta}\right);

hence,

\displaystyle(\ast)_{u}

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}(L,D)\left(\sqrt{\Delta}+\log\left(\frac{4}{\Delta}\right)\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\max_{s_{0}\leq s<s_{1}}\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{m}\delta_{s}(h)\log\left(\frac{e}{\delta_{s}(h)}\right)}\right).

A straightforward computation (distinguishing between the cases
\delta_{s}(h)\leq\Delta
and
\delta_{s}(h)>\Delta
) shows that for a constant
c_{2}
that depends only on
L
and
D
,

\displaystyle(\ast)_{u}

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\left(\sqrt{\Delta}+\log^{3/2}\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right)\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\max_{s_{0}\leq s<s_{1}}\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{m}\delta_{s}(h)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\left(\sqrt{\Delta}+\log^{3/2}\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{H})}{m}}\right),

where the second inequality holds because
(\mathcal{H}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
is an almost optimal admissible sequence for
\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{H})
.
∎

3.2.
Controlling
[\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{P}_{m}](|\pi_{s_{1}}h(X)-h(X)|\geq\sqrt{\Delta})

With Lemma
2.2
in mind, the next component in the proof of Theorem
3.2
is that with high probability,

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}[\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{P}_{m}]\left(|h(X)-\pi_{s_{1}}h(X)|\geq\sqrt{\Delta}\right)\leq c\sqrt{\Delta}.

The estimate on the
\mathbb{P}
-probability is straightforward:
Because
(\mathcal{H}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
is an almost optimal admissible sequence for
\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{H})
and by the restriction on
\Delta
,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\|h-\pi_{s_{1}}h\|_{L_{2}}\leq 2\frac{\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{H})}{2^{s_{1}}}\leq 2c_{0}\frac{\sqrt{\Delta}}{\log^{3}(e/\Delta)}

where
c_{0}=c_{0}(L,D)
is a constant that may be chosen as small as needed.
Thus, invoking the subexponential tail decay,

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|h(X)-\pi_{s_{1}}h(X)|\geq\sqrt{\Delta}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\log^{3}\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right)\right)\leq 2\sqrt{\Delta},

as required.

Obtaining a similar estimate for the empirical measure
\mathbb{P}_{m}
is more subtle.

Lemma 3.9
.

There is an absolute constant
c_{1}
satisfying that with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{1}\Delta m)
, for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
,

(3.14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{m}\left(|h(X)-\pi_{s_{1}}h(X)|\geq\sqrt{\Delta}\right)\leq 2\sqrt{\Delta}.

Note that (
3.14
) is equivalent to

(3.15)

\displaystyle\left|\left\{i:|h(X_{i})-\pi_{s_{1}}h(X_{i})|\geq\sqrt{\Delta}\right\}\right|\leq 2\sqrt{\Delta}m,

a fact that will be used in the proof of Lemma
3.9
:
Denote by
(a^{\ast}_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
the monotone non-increasing rearrangement of
(|a_{i}|)_{i=1}^{m}
.
Setting
a=(h(X_{i})-\pi_{s_{1}}h(X_{i}))_{i=1}^{m}
, (
3.15
) holds if
a^{\ast}_{2\sqrt{\Delta}m}<\sqrt{\Delta}
; in particular it suffices that
a^{\ast}_{2^{s_{1}}}<\sqrt{\Delta}
.
Clearly

a_{k}^{\ast}\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{\ast}

for every
1\leq k\leq m
, and Lemma
3.9
follows once we show that
with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{1}\Delta m)
,

(3.16)

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{1}{2^{s_{1}}}\sum_{i=1}^{2^{s_{1}}}\left(h(X_{i})-\pi_{s_{1}}h(X_{i})\right)^{\ast}<\sqrt{\Delta}.

The proof of (
3.16
) is based on a (rather standard) chaining argument.
For
1\leq k\leq m
, set

\mathcal{S}_{k}=\{b\in\{-1,0,1\}^{m}:|\{i:b_{i}\neq 0\}|=k\}

and note that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{\ast}=\max_{b\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}a_{i}
.
The next observation is an immediate consequence of the
\psi_{1}
-version of Bernstein’s inequality and its proof is presented for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 3.10
.

There exists a constant
c_{1}
that depends only on
L
such that the following holds.
For every
1\leq k\leq m
,
b\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
,
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
and
t\geq 0
, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-t)
,

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}\left(h(X_{i})-h^{\prime}(X_{i})\right)\right|\leq c_{1}\left(\sqrt{tk}+t\right)\|h-h^{\prime}\|_{L_{2}}.

Proof.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a rearrangement of
b
that is supported on the first
k
coordinates.
Set
Z_{i}=h(X_{i})-h^{\prime}(X_{i})
, and clearly
\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}Z_{i}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}Z_{i}
have the same distribution.
The proof follows from a version of Bernstein’s inequality, see, e.g.
[
2
, Lemma 3.6]
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.9
.

Recall that
2^{s_{1}}\sim\sqrt{\Delta}m
.
Therefore,

|\mathcal{S}_{2^{s_{1}}}|=2^{2^{s_{1}}}\binom{m}{2^{s_{1}}}\leq\exp\left(c_{1}\sqrt{\Delta}m\log\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right)\right),

and by the union bound it suffices to show that for every
fixed

b\in\mathcal{S}_{2^{s_{1}}}
, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-2c_{1}\sqrt{\Delta}m\log(\frac{e}{\Delta}))
,

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{1}{2^{s_{1}}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}\left(h(X_{i})-\pi_{s_{1}}h(X_{i})\right)<\sqrt{\Delta}.

To that end, fix
b\in\mathcal{S}_{2^{s_{1}}}
and let
\eta=\eta(c_{1})\geq 1
be an absolute constant to be specified in what follows.
For
s\geq s_{1}
, Lemma
3.10
(with the choices of
k=2^{s}
and
t=\eta 2^{s}\log(\frac{e}{\Delta})
) shows that for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-\eta 2^{s}\log(\frac{e}{\Delta}))
,

(3.17)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}(\pi_{s+1}h(X_{i})-\pi_{s}h(X_{i}))\right|\leq c_{2}(L)\eta 2^{s}\log\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right)\|\pi_{s+1}h-\pi_{s}h\|_{L_{2}}.

Recalling that
|\{(\pi_{s+1}h,\pi_{s}h):h\in\mathcal{H}\}|\leq\exp\left(2^{s+2}\right)
,
it follows from the union bound over
s\geq r
and pairs
(\pi_{s+1}h,\pi_{s}h)
that (
3.17
) holds uniformly
with probability at least

1-\sum_{s\geq s_{1}}2\exp\left(-\eta 2^{s}\log\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right)\right)\geq 1-2\exp\left(-2c_{1}\sqrt{\Delta}m\log\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right)\right);

the last inequality holds for a suitable choice of
\eta=\eta(c_{1})
.
On that event,

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{1}{2^{s_{1}}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}\left(h(X_{i})-\pi_{s_{1}}h(X_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{1}{2^{s_{1}}}\sum_{s\geq s_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}(\pi_{s+1}h(X_{i})-\pi_{s}h(X_{i}))

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}(L)\eta\log\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right)\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{1}{2^{s_{1}}}\sum_{s\geq s_{1}}2^{s}\|\pi_{s+1}h-\pi_{s}h\|_{L_{2}}=(\ast).

Finally,
(\mathcal{H}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
is an almost optimal admissible sequence, and by the restriction on
\Delta
in (
3.2
), we have that
(\ast)<\sqrt{\Delta}
.
∎

3.3.
Putting everything together—proof of Theorem
3.2

Let
\Omega(\mathbb{X})
be the event in which the assertions of Lemma
3.5
and Lemma
3.9
hold; in particular

\mathbb{P}(\Omega(\mathbb{X}))\geq 1-2\exp(-c_{1}\Delta m)
.
By the estimate in (
3.13
), it follows that for every realization
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in\Omega(\mathbb{X})
and
h\in\mathcal{H}
,

\|F_{m,\pi_{s_{1}}h}-F_{\pi_{s_{1}}h}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq c_{2}(L,D)\sqrt{\Delta},

and

[\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{P}_{m}]\left(|\pi_{s_{1}}h(X)-h(X)|\geq\sqrt{\Delta}\right)\leq 4\sqrt{\Delta}.

Therefore, an application of Lemma
2.2
(with
r=\sqrt{\Delta}
) shows that

\displaystyle\|F_{m,h}-F_{h}\|_{L_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\|F_{m,\pi_{s_{1}}h}-F_{\pi_{s_{1}}h}\|_{L_{\infty}}+[\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{P}_{m}]\left(|h(X)-\pi_{s_{1}}h(X)|\geq\sqrt{\Delta}\right)+2D\sqrt{\Delta}

\displaystyle\leq(c_{2}(L,D)+4+2D)\sqrt{\Delta},

as claimed.
∎

4.
Proof of Lemma
1.7
and Lemma
1.9

Recall that
w
is a symmetric random variable with variance 1 and that
X
has independent coordinates distributed according to
w
.
We begin with the proof of Lemma
1.9
, which is the simpler of the two.

4.1.
Proof of Lemma
1.9

Let
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{10})
, set
d\in\mathbb{N}
to be specified in what follows, and put

(4.1)

\displaystyle A=\left\{\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}\cdot e_{1}+\delta\cdot e_{k}:2\leq k\leq d\right\}\subset S^{d-1}.

Note that
\gamma_{1}(A)\leq c_{1}\delta\log(d)
.
Indeed, this follows by considering the admissible sequence
(A_{s})_{s\geq 0}
defined by
A_{s}=\{\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}e_{1}+\delta e_{2}\}
when
2^{2^{s}}<d
and
A_{s}=A
otherwise, and noting that
{\rm diam}(A,\|\cdot\|_{2})\leq 2\delta
.

Clearly,
\left\langle X,x\right\rangle
is a symmetric random variable with variance 1, and by Markov’s inequality,

\sup_{x\in A}F_{x}(-2)\leq\frac{1}{8}.

Next, let us show that with probability at least
0.9
, there exists
x\in A
which satisfies that
F_{m,x}(-2)\geq\frac{4}{10}
; in particular

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{x\in A}|F_{m,x}(-2)-F_{x}(-2)|\geq\frac{1}{10}\right)\geq 0.9,

as claimed.

To that end, set

I=\{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}:\left\langle X_{i},e_{1}\right\rangle\leq 0\}

and denote by
\Omega_{1}(\mathbb{X})
the event in which
|I|\geq\frac{4}{10}m
.
Since
\mathbb{P}(\left\langle X,e_{1}\right\rangle\leq 0)=\frac{1}{2}
, by Markov’s inequality
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{1}(\mathbb{X}))\geq 0.99
when
m\geq 20
.
Consider

\Omega_{2}(\mathbb{X})=\left\{\text{there is }2\leq k\leq d\text{ such that}\left\langle X_{i},e_{k}\right\rangle\leq-\frac{2}{\delta}\text{ for every }1\leq i\leq m\right\}

and set

\beta=\mathbb{P}\left(w\leq-\frac{2}{\delta}\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{2}(\mathbb{X}))=1-(1-\beta^{m})^{d-1}

\displaystyle=1-\exp\big((d-1)\log(1-\beta^{m})\big)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\exp\left(-(d-1)\beta^{m})\right),

where we used that
\log(1-\lambda)\leq-\lambda
for
\lambda\in(0,1)
.
Setting
d=c_{2}\beta^{-m}
, it follows that
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{2}(\mathbb{X}))\geq 0.99
.

Clearly, for every realization
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in\Omega_{1}(\mathbb{X})\cap\Omega_{2}(\mathbb{X})
there exists
x\in A
for which

\left\langle X_{i},x\right\rangle=\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}\left\langle X_{i},e_{1}\right\rangle+\delta\left\langle X_{i},e_{k}\right\rangle\leq-2\quad\text{for every }i\in I,

and thus
F_{m,x}(-2)\geq\frac{|I|}{m}\geq\frac{4}{10}
.

It remains to show that there is some
\delta<\frac{1}{10}
for which
\gamma_{1}(A)\leq 10
.
Since
d=c_{2}\beta^{-m}
, we have that
\gamma_{1}(A)\leq c_{3}\delta m\log(\frac{1}{\beta})
; therefore
\gamma_{1}(A)\leq 10
if

\displaystyle\beta=\mathbb{P}\left(w\leq-\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\geq\exp\left(-\frac{10}{c_{3}\delta m}\right).

Finally,
w
does not have a subexponential tail decay, and in particular, for every
L\geq 1
there exists
t>20
that satisfies
\mathbb{P}(w\leq-t)\geq\exp(-t/L)
.
The claim follows by setting
\delta=\frac{2}{t}
for
t
corresponding to
L=\frac{c_{3}m}{5}
.
∎

4.2.
Proof of Lemma
1.7

The proof is based on a similar construction to the one used in the proof of Lemma
1.9
.

Consider
t_{0}
that satisfies
\mathbb{P}(w=t_{0})=\eta
and set
H(\cdot)=\mathbb{P}(w\leq\cdot)
.
Since
w
is symmetric, we may assume without loss of generality that
t_{0}\geq 0
.

Let
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{10})
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
to be specified in what follows and again set

A=\left\{\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}\cdot e_{1}+\delta\cdot e_{k}:2\leq k\leq d\right\}.

Moreover, put
\alpha=\frac{1}{10}
and let
\beta=\mathbb{P}(w\geq\alpha)
.
Recalling that
w
is ‘nontrivial’ we have that
\beta\geq\frac{1}{20}
.

Let us show that there is some
\delta_{0}>0
(that may depend on
H
) satisfying that for every
\delta\leq\delta_{0}
,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in A}F_{x}(t_{0})\leq H(t_{0})-\frac{\beta}{2}\eta.

Indeed, set
x(\delta)=\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}e_{1}+\delta e_{2}\in A
, and since
X
has iid coordinates,
F_{y}=F_{x(\delta)}
for every
y\in A
.
Moreover, if
w^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
w
, then

\displaystyle F_{x(\delta)}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}w^{\prime}+\delta w\leq t_{0}\right)=\mathbb{E}H\left(\frac{t_{0}-\delta w}{\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(1_{w\leq 0}+1_{w>0})H\left(\frac{t_{0}-\delta w}{\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}}\right).

Observe that if
w\leq 0
then
\frac{t_{0}-\delta w}{\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}}
decreases to
t_{0}
as
\delta\to 0^{+}
, and if
w>0
then
\frac{t_{0}-\delta w}{\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}}
increases to
t_{0}
as
\delta\to 0^{+}
.
By the right-continuity of
H
and the dominated convergence theorem,

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}F_{x(\delta)}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(w\leq 0)H(t_{0})+\mathbb{P}(w>0)H(t_{0}-)

where
H(t_{0}-)=\lim_{s\uparrow t_{0}}H(s)=H(t_{0})-\eta
.
Clearly
\mathbb{P}(w>0)\geq\mathbb{P}(w\geq\alpha)\geq\beta
and (
4.2
) follows.

Next, set

I=\{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}:\left\langle X_{i},e_{1}\right\rangle\leq t_{0}\},

put
\varepsilon=\frac{\beta\eta}{4H(t_{0})}
, and let
\Omega_{1}(\mathbb{X})
be the event in which
|I|\geq(1-\varepsilon)H(t_{0})m
.
By Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{1}(\mathbb{X})^{c})\leq\mathbb{P}\big(||I|-mH(t_{0})|\geq\varepsilon H(t_{0})m\big)\leq\frac{mH(t_{0})(1-H(t_{0}))}{\varepsilon^{2}H(t_{0})^{2}m^{2}}\leq 0.01,

where the last inequality holds if
m\geq\frac{c_{1}(\beta)}{\eta^{2}}
.
Setting

\Omega_{2}(\mathbb{X})=\{\text{there is }2\leq k\leq d\text{ such that }\left\langle X_{i},e_{k}\right\rangle\leq-\alpha\text{ for every }1\leq i\leq m\},

the same arguments used in the proof of Lemma
1.9
show that if
d=c_{2}\beta^{-m}
then

\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{2}(\mathbb{X}))=1-(1-\beta^{m})^{d-1}\geq 0.99.

For a realization
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in\Omega_{1}(\mathbb{X})\cap\Omega_{2}(\mathbb{X})
there is
x\in A
satisfying that for every
i\in I
,

\displaystyle\left\langle X_{i},x\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}\left\langle X_{i},e_{1}\right\rangle+\delta\left\langle X_{i},e_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}t_{0}-\alpha\delta=\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}t_{0}-\frac{\delta}{10}\leq t_{0},

where the last inequality holds whenever
\delta\leq c_{3}(t_{0})
if
t_{0}>0
, and for any
\delta>0
if
t_{0}=0
.
In particular,
F_{m,x}(t_{0})\geq\frac{|I|}{m}
, and by the choice of
\varepsilon
and (
4.2
),

F_{m,x}(t_{0})-F_{x}(t_{0})\geq\left(1-\frac{\beta\eta}{4H(t_{0})}\right)H(t_{0})-\left(H(t_{0})-\frac{\beta}{2}\eta\right)\geq\frac{\beta}{4}\eta.

To complete the proof, recall that
\gamma_{1}(A)\leq c_{4}\delta\log(d)
and that
d=c_{2}\beta^{-m}
.
Therefore,
\gamma_{1}(A)\leq c_{5}\delta m\log(\frac{1}{\beta})
, and for
\delta\leq\min\{\delta_{0},c_{3}(t_{0})\}
small enough we have that
\gamma_{1}(A)\leq 10
.
∎

5.
Application: Uniform estimation of monotone functions

A problem one encounters frequently in high dimensional statistics is estimating the means
\mathbb{E}\varphi(\left\langle X,x\right\rangle)

for a given function
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, and uniformly for every
x\in A
—
using only a sample
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
consisting of independent copies of
X
.

For example,
\varphi(t)=t^{2}
corresponds to
covariance estimation
, where an intuitive (yet ultimately suboptimal) approach is to use the empirical mean
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle X_{i},x\right\rangle^{2}
as an estimator.
With that choice the resulting error is

\sup_{x\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle X_{i},x\right\rangle^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\langle X,x\right\rangle^{2}\right|=\sup_{x\in S^{d-1}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}t^{2}\,dF_{m,x}(t)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}t^{2}\,dF(t)\right|.

Another example is estimating
\mathbb{E}|\left\langle X,x\right\rangle|^{p}
for every
x\in A
and
p>2
(see, e.g.,
[
18
]
) which naturally leads to the choice
\varphi(t)=|t|^{p}
.
Just as in the case
p=2
, the empirical
p
-mean happens to be a suboptimal procedure.
In both cases, the suboptimality is caused by
outliers
: atypically large values of
|\left\langle X_{i},x\right\rangle|
that occur with nontrivial probability for some
x\in S^{d-1}
and some
1\leq i\leq m
.

As it happens, a
trimmed
p
-mean
estimator can be shown to overcome the effect of outliers
[
25
]
, and exhibits optimal statistical performance.
To formulate that result, for
w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
denote by
w^{\sharp}
its monotone non-decreasing rearrangement and for
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{10})
set

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(w)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=\delta m}^{(1-\delta)m}w^{\sharp}_{i}.

Let
L,p\geq 2
, and
q>2p
. Set
\varphi(t)=|t|^{p}
, let
X
be an isotropic random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and assume that
\sup_{x\in S^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}|\left\langle X,x\right\rangle|^{q}\leq L^{q}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

There are constants
c_{1},\ldots,c_{4}
that depend only on
L,p,q
for which the following holds.
If
m\geq d
and

\Delta\in\left[c_{1}\frac{d}{m}\log\left(\frac{em}{d}\right)\,,\,1\right],

then with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{2}\Delta m)
,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in S^{d-1}}\left|\mathcal{E}_{c_{3}\Delta}\Big(\big(\varphi(\left\langle X_{i},x\right\rangle)\big)_{i=1}^{m}\Big)-\mathbb{E}\varphi(\left\langle X,x\right\rangle)\right|\leq c_{4}\sqrt{\Delta}.

Theorem
5.1
was extended in
[
6
]
: (
5.1
) actually holds for any
\varphi
in the class

\mathcal{M}_{p,\beta}=\left\{\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}:\varphi\text{ is monotone and }\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\varphi(t)|}{1+|t|^{p}}\leq\beta\right\}.

There are two issues with the estimator from Theorem
5.1
.
Firstly, it deals with
S^{d-1}
and not with arbitrary subsets of the sphere (thus forcing that
m\geq d
and
\Delta\gtrsim\frac{d}{m}
).
Secondly, the trimmed mean estimator is highly nonlinear.

Here, we resolve both issues by constructing a linear estimator that is (almost) optimal for an arbitrary set
A\subset S^{d-1}
.
More accurately, an
estimator
here is a measurable function
\widehat{\nu}\colon(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{m}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
that assigns to a sample
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Denote by
\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\nu}}
the expectation with respect to the measure
\widehat{\nu}
, and in particular

\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\nu}}\,\varphi(\left\langle X,x\right\rangle)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(\left\langle w,x\right\rangle)\,\widehat{\nu}(dw).

Theorem 5.2
.

For every
L,D,p,\beta\geq 1
there are constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
that depend only on
L,D,p,\beta
for which the following holds.
If
X
satisfies Assumption
1.10
,
A\subset S^{d-1}
is a symmetric set,
m\geq\gamma_{1}(A)
and

\Delta\in\left[c_{1}\frac{\gamma_{1}(A)}{m}\log^{3}\left(\frac{em}{\gamma_{1}(A)}\right)\,,\,1\right],

then there exists an estimator
\widehat{\nu}
, for which with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{2}\Delta m)
,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in A}\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}_{p,\beta}}\big|\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\nu}}\,\varphi(\left\langle X,x\right\rangle)-\mathbb{E}\varphi(\left\langle X,x\right\rangle)\big|\leq c_{3}\sqrt{\Delta}\log^{p}\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right).

Note that the estimator
\widehat{\nu}
depends on the parameters
L,D,p,\beta
and the wanted accuracy level
\Delta
, but not on any other feature of
X
or of
A
.

The proof of Theorem
5.2
requires some preparations. We may assume that
\sqrt{\Delta}\leq\frac{1}{10}
, and
let
\Omega(\mathbb{X})
be the event in which the assertion of Theorem
1.11
holds, that is,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in A}\|F_{m,x}-F_{x}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\sqrt{\Delta}.

Hence,
\mathbb{P}(\Omega(\mathbb{X}))\geq 1-2\exp(-c_{0}\Delta m)
for a constant
c_{0}=c_{0}(L,D)
.

Fix a realization
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in\Omega(\mathbb{X})
.
Clearly, (
5.3
) implies that for every
x\in A
and
u\in(\sqrt{\Delta},1-\sqrt{\Delta})
,

(5.4)

\displaystyle F_{m,x}^{-1}(u)\in\left[F_{x}^{-1}(u-\sqrt{\Delta}),F_{x}^{-1}(u+\sqrt{\Delta})\right].

Moreover, by the subexponential tail decay of
\left\langle X,x\right\rangle
, it follows that for every
u\in(0,1)
,

(5.5)

\displaystyle|F_{x}^{-1}(u)|\leq L\log\left(\frac{e}{\min\{u,1-u\}}\right).

Lemma 5.3
.

For every
L,p,\beta\geq 1
there is a constant
c
that depends only on
L,p,\beta
for which the following holds.
For every
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}_{p,\beta}
,
x\in A
, and
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in\Omega(\mathbb{X})
,

\left|\int_{\sqrt{\Delta}}^{1-\sqrt{\Delta}}\varphi(F_{m,x}^{-1}(u))\,du-\mathbb{E}\varphi(\left\langle X,x\right\rangle)\right|\leq c\sqrt{\Delta}\log^{p}\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right).

Proof.

Fix
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}_{p,\beta}
and assume without loss of generality that
\varphi
is non-decreasing.
Define
I^{+}_{x}
and
I_{x}^{-}
by

I^{\pm}_{x}=\int_{\sqrt{\Delta}}^{1-\sqrt{\Delta}}\varphi\left(F_{x}^{-1}(u\pm\sqrt{\Delta})\right)\,du.

Invoking (
5.4
) and since
\varphi
is non-decreasing,

\int_{\sqrt{\Delta}}^{1-\sqrt{\Delta}}\varphi(F_{m,x}^{-1}(u))\,du\in\left[I_{x}^{-}\,,\,I_{x}^{+}\right].

A change of variables
u\mapsto u\pm\sqrt{\Delta}
shows that

\displaystyle\left|I^{\pm}_{x}-\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(F_{x}^{-1}(u))\,du\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{[0,1]\setminus[2\sqrt{\Delta},1-2\sqrt{\Delta}]}|\varphi(F_{x}^{-1}(u))|\,du

\displaystyle\leq c(L,p,\beta)\sqrt{\Delta}\log^{p}\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right),

where the last inequality follows from
the tail behaviour (
5.5
) and the growth assumption that
|\varphi(\cdot)|\leq\beta(1+|\cdot|^{p})
.
Finally, by tail-integration,
\mathbb{E}\varphi(\left\langle X,x\right\rangle)=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(F_{x}^{-1}(u))\,du
, from which the claim follows.
∎

The proof of Theorem
5.2
uses a simple (and clever) idea from
[
1
]
.

Proof of Theorem
5.2
.

Let
c=c(L,p,\beta)
be the constant appearing in Lemma
5.3
and set
\delta=c\sqrt{\Delta}\log^{p}\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right)
.
For every
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}_{p,\beta}
and
x\in A
, put

\mathcal{P}_{\varphi,x}=\left\{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\left|\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\,\varphi(\left\langle X,x\right\rangle)-\int_{\sqrt{\Delta}}^{1-\sqrt{\Delta}}\varphi(F_{m,x}^{-1}(u))\,du\right|\leq\delta\right\}

(where, if
\varphi(\left\langle X,x\right\rangle)
is not integrable with respect to
\nu
we set
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\varphi(\left\langle X,x\right\rangle)=\infty
).

By Lemma
5.3
, for every realization
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in\Omega(\mathbb{X})
, the set
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi,x}
contains
\mu
.
In particular, on that event

\bigcap\left\{\mathcal{P}_{\varphi,x}:\varphi\in\mathcal{M}_{p,\beta},\,x\in A\right\}\neq\emptyset,

and set
\widehat{\nu}
to be any element in that intersection.
Hence, for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}_{p,\beta}
and
x\in A
, it follows from the definition of
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi,x}
and Lemma
5.3
that on
\Omega(\mathbb{X})
,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\nu}}\,\varphi(\left\langle X,x\right\rangle)-\mathbb{E}\varphi(\left\langle X,x\right\rangle)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\nu}}\,\varphi(\left\langle X,x\right\rangle)-\int_{\sqrt{\Delta}}^{1-\sqrt{\Delta}}\varphi(F_{m,x}^{-1}(u))\,du\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left|\int_{\sqrt{\Delta}}^{1-\sqrt{\Delta}}\varphi(F_{m,x}^{-1}(u))\,du-\mathbb{E}\varphi(\left\langle X,x\right\rangle)\right|\leq 2\delta,

as claimed.
∎

6.
Concluding remarks

We end this article with a few remarks that may help putting our main results in some context.

6.1.
A Sudakov-type bound

Theorem
1.6
and Theorem
1.11
have a restriction on the choice of
\Delta
: that

\Delta\gtrsim\frac{\gamma_{1}(A)}{m}\log^{3}\left(\frac{em}{\gamma_{1}(A)}\right).

At a first glance this restriction appears strange.
As we explain here, it is (essentially) optimal and the argument is based on a Sudakov-type lower bound.
Before formulating that lower bound, let us outline the standard relations between the
\gamma_{1}
-functional and
covering numbers
.

Let
\mathcal{N}(A,\delta B_{2}^{d})
be the smallest number of (open) Euclidean balls of radius
\delta
needed to cover
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
It is standard to verify that for any
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(6.1)

\displaystyle c_{1}\sup_{\delta\geq 0}\left(\delta\cdot\log\mathcal{N}\left(A,\delta B_{2}^{d}\right)\right)\leq\gamma_{1}(A)\leq c_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log\mathcal{N}\left(A,\delta B_{2}^{d}\right)\,d\delta

for absolute constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
.
Indeed, the lower bound holds because for an (almost) optimal admissible sequence and every
s
,

\sup_{x\in A}2^{s}\|x-\pi_{s}x\|_{2}\leq 2\gamma_{1}(A);

the upper bound follows by using suitable
\delta
-covers of
A
as the sets
A_{s}
.

When
A\subset S^{d-1}
, the inequalities in (
6.1
) are sharp up to a logarithmic factor in the dimension
d
; in particular

(6.2)

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(A)\leq c\log(ed)\cdot\sup_{\delta\geq 0}\left(\delta\cdot\log\mathcal{N}\left(A,\delta B_{2}^{d}\right)\right),

see e.g.,
[
29
, Section 2.5]
.

Proposition 6.1

(
[
7
, Proposition 1.9]
)
.

There are absolute constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that the following holds.
Let
X=G
be the standard gaussian random vector, let
A\subset S^{d-1}
be symmetric and put
\delta>0
.
If

m\geq c_{1}\max\left\{\frac{1}{\delta^{2}},\frac{\log\mathcal{N}(A,\delta B_{2}^{d})}{\delta}\right\},

then with probability at least
0.9

(6.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in A}\|F_{m,x}-F_{x}\|_{L_{\infty}}\geq c_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\delta\cdot\log\mathcal{N}\left(A,\delta B_{2}^{d}\right)}{m}}.

Following (
6.1
) and (
6.2
), the right hand side in (
6.3
) almost coincides with
\sqrt{\gamma_{1}(A)/m}
.
In particular, a straightforward consequence of Proposition
6.1
is that asymptotically,
\gamma_{1}
is the correct notion of complexity (up to logarithmic factors):

\displaystyle\liminf_{m\to\infty}\sqrt{m}\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{x\in A}\|F_{m,x}-F_{x}\|_{L_{\infty}}\right)\geq c_{1}\sup_{\delta\geq 0}\sqrt{\delta\log\mathcal{N}(A,\delta B_{2}^{d})}\geq c_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{1}(A)}{\log(d)}}.

Remark 6.2
.

(a)

The proof of Proposition
6.1
extends (without any modifications) to the more general setting considered in Section
3
.
Indeed, the key feature of the gaussian random vector used in the proof is a certain small ball assumption.
In the general case, if
\mathcal{H}
is an arbitrary class of functions, that assumption corresponds to

(6.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\{h(X)\leq 0\}\,\bigtriangleup\,\{h^{\prime}(X)\leq 0\}\right)\geq\kappa\|h-h^{\prime}\|_{L_{2}},

and in that case the proof is identical to that of Proposition
6.1
, with the constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
depending on the small ball constant
\kappa
.

(b)

Note that if
\mathcal{H}=\{\left\langle\cdot,x\right\rangle:x\in A\}
for
A\subset S^{d-1}
and
X
is rotation invariant, then (
6.4
) holds for an absolute constant
\kappa
.

6.2.
A variance dependent DKW inequality

One may rightly ask if choosing the
L_{\infty}
-norm to measure the distance between
F_{m,x}
and
F_{x}
is the best option.
On the one hand, both the estimate

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{x\in A}\|F_{m,x}-F_{x}\|_{L_{\infty}}\geq\sqrt{\Delta}\right)\leq 2\exp(-c\Delta m)

and the restriction
\Delta\gtrsim\frac{\gamma_{1}(A)}{m}\log^{3}(\frac{em}{\gamma_{1}(A)})
are optimal (at least up to logarithmic factors).
On the other hand, setting
\sigma_{x}^{2}(t)=F_{x}(t)(1-F_{x}(t))
one has that
\mathrm{\mathbb{V}ar}(F_{m,x}(t))=\frac{1}{m}\sigma_{x}^{2}(t)
;
therefore it is reasonable to expect that the error
|F_{m,x}(t)-F_{x}(t)|
can be significantly smaller than
\sqrt{\Delta}
when
F_{x}(t)
is far from
1/2
.
And indeed, as was shown in
[
7
, Theorem 1.7]
, a variant of the uniform DKW inequality with an improved
variance dependent
error is true when
X
is the standard gaussian random vector:

Theorem 6.3
.

There are absolute constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
for which the following holds.
Let
X=G
, the standard gaussian vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If
A\subset S^{d-1}
is a symmetric set,
m\geq\gamma_{1}(A)
and

\Delta\geq c_{1}\frac{\gamma_{1}(A)}{m}\log^{3}\left(\frac{em}{\gamma_{1}(A)}\right),

then with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{2}\Delta m)
, for every
x\in A
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

(6.5)

\displaystyle|F_{m,x}(t)-F_{x}(t)|\leq\Delta+\sigma_{x}(t)\sqrt{\Delta}.

The estimate in Theorem
6.3
is optimal, and we refer to
[
7
,
8
]
for more information on the problem and the proof.

It is natural to ask whether Theorem
6.3
extends beyond the gaussian framework.
The proof from
[
7
]
is ‘very gaussian’: it relies heavily on several special features of the gaussian density
f_{x}
, in particular that in addition to being bounded, each
f_{x}
satisfies that

\displaystyle f_{x}(t)\sim\sigma_{x}^{2}(t)\log\left(\frac{e}{\sigma_{x}^{2}(t)}\right).

As it happens, an upper bound on the density that ‘scales well’ with the variance is
crucial
to a variance dependent version of the DKW inequality. As the next example shows, without that feature even ‘nice’ random vectors (subgaussian, log-concave, with a density that is bounded by 1) do not satisfy (
6.5
).

Lemma 6.4
.

There are constants
c_{1},\dots,c_{5}
and for every
m\geq c_{1}
there is
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
A\subset S^{d-1}
with
\gamma_{1}(A)\leq c_{2}
for which the following holds.
Let
X
be uniformly distributed in
[-\sqrt{3},\sqrt{3}]^{d}
.
Then there is
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
that satisfies

\sigma_{x}^{2}(t_{0})\in[\frac{c_{3}}{\sqrt{m}},\frac{c_{4}}{\sqrt{m}}]
for every
x\in A

and, with probability at least
0.9
,

\sup_{x\in A}\left|F_{m,x}(t_{0})-F_{x}(t_{0})\right|\geq\frac{c_{5}}{\sqrt{m}}.

If the assertion of Theorem
6.3
for the random vector in Lemma
6.4
were true, then with high probability (and ignoring logarithmic factors)

\sup_{x\in A}|F_{m,x}(t_{0})-F_{x}(t_{0})|\lesssim\frac{\sigma_{x}(t_{0})}{\sqrt{m}}\sim\frac{1}{m^{3/4}};

that is far from the true behaviour for
X
.

The proof of Lemma
6.4
follows the arguments used in the proofs of Lemmas
1.7
and
1.9
but requires certain modifications.

Proof of Lemma
6.4
.

Let
Y=X/\sqrt{3}
and note that
Y
is uniformly distributed in
[-1,1]^{d}
.
Set
\delta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}
and
d=\exp(c_{0}\sqrt{m})
for a suitable constant
c_{0}
that is specified in what follows, and put

A=\left\{\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}\cdot e_{1}+\delta\cdot e_{k}:k=2,\dots,d\right\}\subset S^{d-1};

thus
\gamma_{1}(A)\leq c_{1}\delta\log(d)\leq c_{1}c_{0}
.

Denote by
I
the set of indices corresponding to the smallest
\frac{1}{3}\delta m
coordinates of
(\left\langle Y_{i},e_{1}\right\rangle)_{i=1}^{m}
and let

\Omega_{1}(\mathbb{Y})=\left\{\left\langle Y_{i},e_{1}\right\rangle\leq-1+\frac{7}{10}\delta\text{ for every }i\in I\right\}.

Since

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\langle Y,e_{1}\right\rangle\leq-1+\frac{7}{10}\delta\right)=\frac{7}{20}\delta>\frac{1}{3}\delta,

it follows from Markov’s inequality that
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{1}(\mathbb{Y}))\geq 0.99
when
m\geq\frac{c_{1}}{\delta}=c_{1}\sqrt{m}
.

Set

\Omega_{2}(\mathbb{Y})=\left\{\text{there is }2\leq k\leq d\text{ such that }\left\langle Y_{i},e_{k}\right\rangle\leq-\frac{8}{10}\text{ for every }i\in I\right\}.

Clearly
\mathbb{P}(\left\langle Y,e_{k}\right\rangle\leq-\frac{8}{10})=\frac{1}{10}
, and using the independence of the coordinates of
Y
,

\mathbb{P}\big(\Omega_{2}(\mathbb{Y})\,\big|\,\Omega_{1}(\mathbb{Y})\big)=1-\left(1-10^{-|I|}\right)^{d-1}\geq 1-\exp\left(-(d-1)10^{-|I|}\right)\geq 0.99.

Indeed, the first inequality holds because
\log(1-\lambda)\geq-\lambda
for
\lambda\in(0,1)
, and the second one follows if
d\geq c_{2}10^{|I|}
. Since
|I|=\frac{1}{3}\sqrt{m}
and
d=\exp(c_{0}\sqrt{m})
for a constant
c_{0}
that we are free to choose, the latter is satisfied.

Fix a realization
(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in\Omega_{1}(\mathbb{Y})\cap\Omega_{2}(\mathbb{Y})
.
Then there exists
x=\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}e_{1}+\delta e_{k}\in A
and at least
\frac{1}{3}\delta m
indices
i
for which

\displaystyle\left\langle Y_{i},x\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}\left\langle Y_{i},e_{1}\right\rangle+\delta\left\langle Y_{i},e_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}\cdot\left(-1+\frac{7}{10}\delta\right)-\frac{8}{10}\delta=(\ast).

Moreover, if
\delta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\leq c_{3}
then
(\ast)\leq-1
and in particular

\mathbb{P}_{m}(\left\langle Y,x\right\rangle\leq-1)\geq\frac{1}{3}\delta.

On the other hand, it is straightforward to show that for every
x\in A
,

\mathbb{P}(\left\langle Y,x\right\rangle\leq-1)\in\left[c_{4}\delta,\frac{1}{4}\delta\right].

Setting
t_{0}=-\sqrt{3}
it follows that
F_{m,x}(t_{0})-F_{x}(t_{0})\geq\frac{1}{12}\delta
and that
F_{z}(t_{0})\in[c_{4}\delta,\frac{\delta}{4}]
for every
z\in A
, as claimed.
∎

6.3.
Uniform estimates on Wasserstein distances

As was shown in Theorem
5.2
, the uniform DKW inequality can be used to construct an estimator
\widehat{\nu}
of the underlying distribution
\mu
of
X
that is ‘close’ in the sense of integration relative to a (large) family of test functions.
An alternative and more standard approach of comparing similarity of measures is via the Wasserstein distance.

Definition 6.5
.

For two probability measures
\alpha
and
\beta
on
\mathbb{R}
and
p\geq 1
, the
p
-order
Wasserstein distance
is

\mathcal{W}_{p}(\alpha,\beta)=\left(\inf\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}|s-t|^{p}\,\pi(ds,dt)\right)^{1/p},

with the infimum taken over all couplings
\pi
, i.e. over all probability measures whose first marginal is
\alpha
and the second marginal is
\beta
.

We refer to
[
14
,
32
]
for more information on the
\mathcal{W}_{p}
distance and optimal transport.

An estimate on
\sup_{x\in A}\mathcal{W}_{2}(F_{m,x},F_{x})
for general
A\subset S^{d-1}
was recently established in
[
7
]
for the standard gaussian vector.
There are other estimates on
\sup_{x\in A}\mathcal{W}_{p}(F_{m,x},F_{x})
for more general random vectors but only for
A=S^{d-1}
— in which case the distance is the so-called
max-sliced Wasserstein distance

[
12
,
21
]
, see

[
6
,
23
,
26
,
27
]

and the references therein.

The methods developed in the previous sections can be used for estimating
\sup_{x\in A}\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{m,x},F_{x})
for an arbitrary
A\subset S^{d-1}
and relatively general random vectors:

Theorem 6.6
.

For every
L,D\geq 1
there are constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
that depend only on
L
and
D
for which the following holds.
If
X
satisfies Assumption
1.10
,
A\subset S^{d-1}
is a symmetric set,
m\geq\gamma_{1}(A)
and

\Delta\in\left[c_{1}\frac{\gamma_{1}(A)}{m}\log^{3}\left(\frac{em}{\gamma_{1}(A)}\right)\,,\,1\right],

then with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{2}\Delta m)
,

\sup_{x\in A}\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(F_{m,x},F_{x}\right)\leq c_{3}\sqrt{\Delta}\log\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right).

Sketch of proof.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{1}
be the set of all real
1
-Lipschitz functions
\varphi
that satisfy
\varphi(0)=0
.
By the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality (see, e.g.,
[
14
, Proposition 2.6.6]
),

(6.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(F_{m,x},F_{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{1}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(t)\,dF_{m,x}(t)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(t)\,dF_{x}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{1}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(F_{m,x}^{-1}(u))\,du-\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(F_{x}^{-1}(u))\,du\right).

Every
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
can be written as the difference of two increasing functions with sublinear growth, and it follows from Lemma
5.3
that with probability at least
1-2\exp(c_{1}\Delta m)
, for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
,

\left|\int_{\sqrt{\Delta}}^{1-\sqrt{\Delta}}\varphi(F_{m,x}^{-1}(u))\,du-\int_{\sqrt{\Delta}}^{1-\sqrt{\Delta}}\varphi(F_{x}^{-1}(u))\right|\leq c_{2}(L,D)\sqrt{\Delta}\log\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right).

All that remains is to control the ‘tails’, that is, estimate the integrals in the intervals
[0,\sqrt{\Delta}]
and
[1-\sqrt{\Delta},1]
.

Set
U=[0,1]\setminus[\sqrt{\Delta},1-\sqrt{\Delta}]
and recall that by the tail behaviour of
F_{x}
(see (
5.5
)),

\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{1}}\int_{U}\varphi(F_{x}^{-1}(u))\,du\leq\int_{U}|F_{x}^{-1}(u)|\,du\leq c_{3}(L)\sqrt{\Delta}\log\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right).

Moreover, using the notation of Section
3.2
,

\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{1}}\int_{U}\varphi(F_{m,x}^{-1}(u))\,du\leq\int_{U}|F_{m,x}^{-1}(u)|\,du\sim\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{2\sqrt{\Delta}m}\left\langle X_{i},x\right\rangle^{\ast}=\Psi(x).

A standard chaining argument similar to the one used in Section
3.2
shows that with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{4}\Delta m)
,

\sup_{x\in A}\Psi(x)\leq c_{5}(L)\sqrt{\Delta}\log\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right),

and the proof is completed by combining these observations.
∎

Remark 6.7
.

By applying the uniform DKW inequality it is possible to extend the scope of Theorem
6.6
and analyse higher order Wasserstein distances
\mathcal{W}_{p}
as well (rather than just
\mathcal{W}_{1}
).
However, the proof is more involved because a representation as in (
6.6
) is no longer true when
p>1
.
We defer those results to future work.
