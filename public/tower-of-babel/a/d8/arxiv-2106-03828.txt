A q q -deformation of an algebra of Klyachko and Macdonald’s reduced word formula
Philippe Nadeau, Vasu Tewari

Abstract
. There is a striking similarity between Macdonald’s reduced word formula and the image of the Schubert class in the cohomology ring of the permutahedral variety Perm n \mathrm{Perm}_{n} as computed by Klyachko. Toward understanding this better, we undertake an in-depth study of a q q -deformation of the 𝕊 n \mathbb{S}_{n} -invariant part of the rational cohomology ring of Perm n \mathrm{Perm}_{n} , which we call the q q -Klyachko algebra. We uncover intimate links between expansions in the basis of squarefree monomials in this algebra and various notions in algebraic combinatorics, thereby connecting seemingly unrelated results by finding a common ground to study them. Our main results are as follows. ∘ \circ A q q -analog of divided symmetrization ( q q -DS) using Yang-Baxter elements in the Hecke algebra. It is a linear form that picks up coefficients in the squarefree basis. ∘ \circ A relation between q q -DS and the ideal of quasisymmetric polynomials involving work of Aval–Bergeron–Bergeron. ∘ \circ A family of polynomials in q q with nonnegative integral coefficients that specialize to Postnikov’s mixed Eulerian numbers when q = 1 q=1 . We refer to these new polynomials as remixed Eulerian numbers. For q > 0 q>0 , their normalized versions occur as probabilities in the internal diffusion limited aggregation (IDLA) stochastic process. ∘ \circ A lift of Macdonald’s reduced word identity in the q q -Klyachko algebra. ∘ \circ The Schubert expansion of the Chow class of the standard split Deligne–Lusztig variety in type A A , when q q is a prime power.



1.
Introduction

The permutahedral variety in type
A
has garnered a lot of attention recently, especially since the study of its cohomology ring plays a key role in the dramatic recent resolutions of various questions concerning log-concavity of various polynomials arising in combinatorics; see for instance
[
5
,
22
]
.

Let
\mathrm{Flag}({n})
denote the (type
A
) complete flag variety.
Denote by
\mathrm{Perm}_{n}\subset\mathrm{Flag}({n})
the closure of a torus orbit of a general point in
\mathrm{Flag}({n})
. Equivalently,
\mathrm{Perm}_{n}
can be described as the smooth projective toric variety described by the braid fan. It is the
permutahedral variety of type
A
.
The inclusion
\mathrm{Perm}_{n}\subset\mathrm{Flag}({n})
induces a pullback
i^{*}:H^{*}(\mathrm{Flag}({n}),\mathbb{Q})\to H^{*}(\mathrm{Perm}_{n},\mathbb{Q})
between cohomology rings. In fact the image of
i^{*}
coincides with the algebra of invariants
H^{*}(\mathrm{Perm}_{n},\mathbb{Q})^{\mathbb{S}_{n}}
.

It is this
\mathbb{S}_{n}
-invariant subring of the cohomology ring, which is also isomorphic to the cohomology ring of the Peterson variety
[
1
]
, that was first studied by Klyachko
[
24
]
.
In particular he obtained a presentation for this ring of invariants, and this presentation has subsequently been rederived in
[
16
,
21
]
. This ring of invariants has dimension
2^{r}
where
r=n-1
as shown by Klyachko
[
24
]
and later by Stembridge
[
42
, §3]
.
The dimension hints at the existence of a nice combinatorial linear basis for this space, and indeed, Klyachko constructs a basis indexed by subsets of
\{1,\dots,r\}
. We note that Klyachko’s results work in all Lie types and we have emphasized the type
A
picture here.

Recall that the cohomology ring
H^{*}(\mathrm{Flag}({n}),\mathbb{Q})
has a basis given by Schubert classes
\sigma_{w}
as
w
ranges over elements in
\mathbb{S}_{n}
, which brings us to our point of departure.
It is natural to inquire about the image of
\sigma_{w}
under
i^{*}
.
Klyachko expresses
i^{*}(\sigma_{w})
as a sum of monomials indexed by reduced words of
w
; see
[
34
, §8]
where we reproduce his proof
1

with mild modifications.
Particularly remarkable is the fact that Klyachko’s expression bears an unmistakable resemblance to Macdonald’s reduced word formula
[
30
]
, which we now recall. Let
\mathrm{Red}(w)
denote the set of reduced words for a permutation
w\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
. Macdonald
[
30
]
established that for
w
with length
\ell
we have

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{w}(1,\dots,1)=\frac{1}{\ell!}\sum_{a_{1}\dots a_{\ell}\in\mathrm{Red}(w)}a_{1}\cdots a_{\ell},

where
\mathfrak{S}_{w}
denotes the Schubert polynomial indexed by
w
. These polynomials are certain distinguished representatives of Schubert classes and were introduced by Lascoux and Schützenberger.
Note that the left-hand side counts the number of reduced pipe dreams for
w
.
We note here that Macdonald’s reduced word identity has already been given several proofs
[
6
,
17
,
20
]
, each shedding new light.
More importantly for us, a
q
-analogue (conjectured by Macdonald) was first proved by Fomin and Stanley
[
15
]
by working in the NilCoxeter algebra; see also
[
31
]
.

To make matters more intriguing, we note that Klyachko did not have access to the combinatorial formula for Schubert polynomials established by Billey-Jockusch-Stanley
[
7
]
and Fomin-Stanley
[
15
]
as it came later. Furthermore, from the perspective of Klyachko’s work, there is
a priori
nothing that suggests that a particular representative of a Schubert class may be more important than others, whereas Macdonald’s identity clearly deals with a specific representative.

This article stems from our desire to explain this resemblance between Klyachko’s result and Macdonald’s reduced word identity, as this appears to have been overlooked in literature as far as we can tell.
With the benefit of hindsight, we see that Klyachko’s result foreshadows Macdonald’s result.
In fact, given that Macdonald’s result permits a
q
-analogue, we are able to generalize Klyachko’s result by working a parameter
q
into the setup.

Let
\mathbf{k}
be a field of characteristic zero, and
q\in\mathbf{k}
not equal to a nontrivial root of unity.
We introduce an algebra
\mathcal{K}
, called the
Klyachko algebra
henceforth, by considering a natural
q
-deformation of Klyachko’s presentation
[
24
]
.
More precisely
\mathcal{K}
is the commutative algebra over
\mathbf{k}
on the generators
\{u_{i}\;|\;i\in\mathbb{Z}\}
subject to the following relations for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
:

(1.2)

\displaystyle(q+1)u_{i}^{2}=qu_{i}u_{i-1}+u_{i}u_{i+1}.

The algebra that results on setting
u_{i}=0
for
i\geq n
and
i<0
, will be called
\mathcal{K}_{n}
. In this case, when we additionally set
q=1
, the relations in (
1.2
) descend to Klyachko’s presentation of
H^{*}(\mathrm{Perm}_{n},\mathbb{Q})^{\mathbb{S}_{n}}

[
24
]
.
We will be particularly interested in the ‘positive half’
\mathcal{K}_{+}
of
\mathcal{K}
obtained by setting
u_{i}=0
for all
i\leq 0
.

1.1.
Discussion of main results

The first half of the article focuses on the interplay between an algebraic operation that we call
q
-divided symmetrization, and the coefficients when considering expansions in the distinguished basis
\{u_{I}\}
of
\mathcal{K}
comprising squarefree monomials indexed by finite subsets
I
of
\mathbb{Z}
.
The algebras
\mathcal{K}_{+}
and
\mathcal{K}_{n}
inherit bases of squarefree monomials by omitting terms involving
u_{i}
where
i\notin\mathbb{Z}_{+}
or
i\notin\{1,\dots,n-1\}
respectively.
The
q
-divided symmetrization operator is a linear form that takes a polynomial
f\in\mathbf{k}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
of degree
n-1
as input and output
\big\langle{f}\big\rangle_{n}^{q}\in\mathbf{k}
; see §
4
for the precise definition.
When
q=1
,
q
-divided symmetrization specializes to Postnikov’s divided symmetrization
[
37
, §3]
, and our lift naturally involves substituting Hecke operators
T_{i}
acting on rational functions in
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
as opposed to ordinary transpositions
s_{i}
in Postnikov’s symmetrization.

Fix
r\geq 1
. Our first main result is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
f
be a polynomial in
\mathbf{k}[\mathbf{x}_{r+1}]
with
\deg(f)=r
.
Consider the expansion of
f(u_{1},u_{2}-u_{1},\dots,u_{r}-u_{r-1},-u_{r})
in the squarefree basis of
\mathcal{K}_{r+1}
, and let
\mathrm{Top}_{r+1}(f)
be the top coefficient in this expansion, namely that of
u_{1}\cdots u_{r}
. Then

\big\langle{f}\big\rangle^{q}_{r+1}=(r)_{q}!\times\mathrm{Top}_{r+1}(f).

We are in fact able to extend Theorem
1.1
to a statement involving coefficients of arbitrary squarefree monomials occuring in expansions in the full Klyachko algebra
\mathcal{K}
; see Theorem
4.15
.
We proceed to describe our results borne out of this interplay between the Klyachko algebra and
q
-divided symmetrization, with a particular focus on combinatorially pertinent families of polynomials.

Our second main result concerns a family of polynomials that we call remixed Eulerian numbers.
While our original definition describes them as
q
-divided symmetrizations of special polynomials
y_{c}
, the link to the Klyachko algebra in Theorem
1.1
permits an alternative characterization that we present next.
To state it we need some notation.
For a fixed positive integer
r
, let
\mathcal{W}_{r}^{r}
be the set of sequences of nonnegative integers
(c_{1},\dots,c_{r})
whose parts sum to
r
.
We let
(r)_{q}!
denote the usual
q
-factorial of
r
.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists a
unique
family of polynomials
\{A_{c}(q)\}_{c\in\mathcal{W}_{r}^{r}}
with
A_{(1^{r})}(q)=(r)_{q}!
that satisfies the following relations: for any
i=1,\ldots,r
such that
c_{i}\geq 2
,

(q+1)A_{(c_{1},\dots,c_{r})}(q)=qA_{(c_{1},\dots,c_{i-1}+1,{c_{i}}-1,\dots,c_{r})}(q)+A_{(c_{1},\dots,c_{i}-1,c_{i+1}+1,\dots,c_{r})}(q).

If
i=1

(
resp.
i=r
)
, then the first
(
resp. second
)
term on the right hand side is to be omitted.

It follows easily from
[
34
,
36
]
that
A_{c}(1)=A_{c}
where the latter are the mixed Eulerian numbers introduced by Postnikov
[
37
]
. Furthermore, for
c\in\mathcal{W}_{r}^{r}
, the following properties hold:

(1)

A_{c}(q)\in\mathbb{N}[q]
.

(2)

A_{c}(q)
is the probability to end in the configuration
(1^{r})
starting from the configuration
c
in the process described in §
5
.

(3)

Given
c\in\mathcal{W}_{r}^{r}
, define
\widetilde{c}\coloneqq(c,0)
. Let
\mathsf{Cyc}(c)
be the set of all
c^{\prime}
such that
(c^{\prime},0)
is a cyclic rotation of
\widetilde{c}
. We have the following cyclic sum rule:

\sum_{c^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Cyc}(c)}A_{c^{\prime}}(q)=(r)_{q}!.

We postpone further study of these polynomials to a separate article
[
35
]
, and proceed to make several remarks.
It is worth noting that the nonnegativity of the coefficients of
A_{c}(q)
does not immediately follow from the unique characterization above. One needs to derive a better recursion, which in turn implies a nontrivial divisibility property; see §§
5.2
.
If one considers
A_{c}(q)
where the
c
are drawn from
\mathsf{Cyc}((r,0^{r-1}))
, then one obtains a familiar family of polynomials: they are the MacMahon-Carlitz
q
-Eulerians, i.e. the
q
tracks the major index of permutations on
r
letters with a fixed number of descents.
Note furthermore that there are
\mathrm{Cat}_{r}\coloneqq\frac{1}{r+1}\binom{2r}{r}
cyclic classes determined by compositions in
\mathcal{W}_{r}^{r}
, and we get a Mahonian distribution for each class.

Let
\operatorname{QSym}
denote the ring of quasisymmetric functions
in
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
. We may truncate quasisymmetric functions to obtain quasisymmetric polynomials by declaring
x_{i}=0
for
i>m
for some choice of positive integer
m
.
We denote by
\mathrm{QSym}_{m}^{+}
the ideal in
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}]
generated by positive degree quasisymmetric polynomials.
Of particular interest to is the quotient
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}]/\mathrm{QSym}_{m}^{+}
, studied in the work of Aval–Bergeron–Bergeron
[
4
]
.
These authors established that this quotient has dimension
\mathrm{Cat}_{m}
and possesses a monomial basis (henceforth the ABB basis) naturally indexed by lattice paths subject to certain constraints.
Since we care particularly about degree
m-1
polynomials in the context of
q
-divided symmetrization, we note here that in the top degree of
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}]/\mathrm{QSym}_{m}^{+}
, the ABB monomial basis is identified with Dyck paths from
(0,0)
to
(m-1,m-1)
, as the exponent vectors of these monomials give ‘prototypical’ Catalan objects.

Principal specializations of truncations of quasisymmetric functions are important in combinatorics especially in the context of the study of
P
-partitions.
Our third main result demonstrates that
q
-divided symmetrization, computations in
\mathcal{K}
, and the ABB basis fit nicely in this context.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
f
be a quasisymmetric function of degree
r>0
.

(1)

We have

(1.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 1}f(1,q,\dots,q^{j-1})t^{j}=\frac{\sum_{m}\big\langle{f(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})}\big\rangle_{r+1}^{q}t^{m}}{(t;q)_{r+1}}.

(2)

The expression
f(u_{1},u_{2}-u_{1},\dots,u_{r}-u_{r-1},-u_{r})
vanishes in
\mathcal{K}_{r+1}
.

(3)

\big\langle{\cdot}\big\rangle_{r+1}^{q}
vanishes on the space of degree
r
polynomials in
\mathrm{QSym}_{r+1}^{+}
.

If
f=g+h
where
h\in\mathrm{QSym}_{r+1}^{+}
and

is expressed in the basis of ABB monomials, then

\big\langle{f}\big\rangle_{r+1}^{q}=g(1,q,q^{2},\dots,q^{r}).

In particular for
\mathbf{x}^{c}
an ABB monomial of degree
r
we have

\big\langle{\mathbf{x}^{c}}\big\rangle_{r+1}^{q}=q^{\mathrm{coarea}(\mathsf{Dyck}(c))}.

Here
\mathsf{Dyck}(c)
is the Dyck path corresponding to
c
, and coarea refers to the well-known
complementary area
statistic.

Let
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}^{+}
denote the ideal in
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
generated by positive degree symmetric polynomials in
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. The quotient
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/\mathrm{Sym}_{n}^{+}
is the well-known coinvariant algebra, which is known to be isomorphic to
H^{*}(\mathrm{Flag}({n}),\mathbb{Q})
by Borel’s isomorphism under which the Schubert classes can be regarded as Schubert polynomials.
Clearly
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}^{+}\subset\mathrm{QSym}_{n}^{+}
.
By Theorem
1.3
we have that
\big\langle{\cdot}\big\rangle_{n}^{q}
vanishes on degree
n-1
polynomials in
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}^{+}
.

It is thus natural to inquire if anything substantial can be said about the
q
-divided symmetrization of Schubert polynomials of the appropriate degree. It turns out that the
\big\langle{\mathfrak{S}_{w}}\big\rangle_{n}^{q}
for
w\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
of length
n-1
possess a geometric meaning in the setting of Deligne–Lusztig varieties, thanks to the work of D. Kim
[
23
]
.
Given the link between
q
-divided symmetrization and
\mathcal{K}
in Theorem
1.1
, we are thus led to consider expansions for Schubert polynomials in
\mathcal{K}
.
Fortuitously, our initial motivation to unify Klyachko’s identity and Macdonald’s identity aligns with this new endeavour.

This brings us to our fourth main result.
We will need some notions attached to reduced words.
Suppose
w\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
has length
\ell
.
Let
\mathbf{a}=a_{1}\cdots a_{\ell}\in\mathrm{Red}(w)
.
We define
\mathrm{comaj}(\mathbf{a})
to be the sum of all
i\in\{1,\dots,\ell-1\}
such that
a_{i}<a_{i+1}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

(
q-Klyachko-Macdonald identity
)

Let
w\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
of length
\ell
.
The following equality holds in
\mathcal{K}_{+}
.

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{w}(u_{1},u_{2}-u_{1},\dots)=\frac{1}{(\ell)_{q}!}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathrm{Red}(w)}q^{\mathrm{comaj}(\mathbf{a})}u_{a_{1}}u_{a_{2}}\cdots u_{a_{\ell}}.

Theorem
1.4
is a generalization of Klyachko’s result for
q=1
in the quotient
\mathcal{K}_{n}
. The reader is invited to compare our proof in §
7
with Klyachko’s proof presented in
[
34
, §8]
. It should become apparent that the latter proof does not modify so as to incorporate the
q
. Instead we adapt the argument in
[
15
]
to
\mathcal{K}_{+}\otimes NC_{n}
and employ a ‘Yang-Baxter’ relation that works particularly well with the relations in
\mathcal{K}_{+}
.
Here
NC_{n}
refers to the (type
A
) NilCoxeter algebra.

We define the
content
of
\mathbf{a}
, denoted by
\mathrm{cont}(\mathbf{a})
to be the sequence of nonnegative integers whose
i
th entry counts the number of
i
s in
\mathbf{a}
for all
i\geq 1
. The following two equalities are immediate consequences of Theorem
1.4
.

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{w}(1,q,q^{2},\dots)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\ell)_{q}!}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathrm{Red}(w)}q^{\mathrm{comaj}(\mathbf{a})}(a_{1})_{q}(a_{2})_{q}\cdots(a_{\ell})_{q},

\displaystyle\big\langle{\mathfrak{S}_{w}}\big\rangle_{\ell+1}^{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\ell)_{q}!}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathrm{Red}(w)}q^{\mathrm{comaj}(\mathbf{a})}A_{\mathrm{cont}({\bf a})}(q),

The first one is the
q
-Macdonald reduced word identity
[
15
]
and is obtained by specialization, while the second formula is a consequence of Theorem
1.1
.

We turn our attention to
a_{w}(q)\coloneqq\big\langle{\mathfrak{S}_{w}}\big\rangle_{\ell+1}^{q}
in this latter formula, from which it is not obvious that
a_{w}(q)\in\mathbb{N}[q]
. That this is indeed true follows from work of Kim
[
23
]
, which has the feature that whilst it implies the nonnegativity and integrality of the coefficients of
a_{w}(q)
, the resulting explicit expression unfortunately involves generalized Littlewood-Richardson coefficients.
These are in general hard to compute, and finding a combinatorial rule to describe them is a wide open problem.
We approach understanding
a_{w}(q)
from our explicit formula above and uncover some curious properties of these polynomials, extending previous work of the authors
[
34
]
in the case
q=1
. We briefly describe some developments next, referring the reader to §§
7.2
, §§
7.3
, and §§
7.4

We obtain a
cyclic sum rule
which generalizes
[
34
, Theorem 1.5]
nicely. Informally our rule says that
\mathbb{S}^{\prime}_{n}
can be decomposed into
\mathrm{Cat}_{n-1}
cyclic classes, in such a way that the sum of the
a_{w}(q)
as
w
ranges over a fixed class tracks the distribution of
\mathrm{comaj}
over the set of reduced words for any choice of representative from that class.

This summatory rule suggests that an approach to describing the
a_{w}(q)
combinatorially might involve decomposing the set of reduced words of
{\bf a}
in some hitherto undetermined manner.
Nevertheless, in §§
7.4
, we are able to deal with special cases, namely,
w
being a Łukasiewicz permutation or a Grassmannian permutation.

Additionally, for the class of permutations we call quasiindecomposable permutations, we show that the corresponding
a_{w}(q)
‘encode’ the generating function tracking principal specializations of certain Schubert polynomials. Specifically, for
v
an indecomposable permutation in
\mathbb{S}_{p+1}
of length
\ell
, we show in Theorem
7.9
that

(1.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 0}t^{j}\;\mathfrak{S}_{1^{j}\times v}(1,q,q^{2},\dots)=\frac{{\displaystyle{\sum_{m=0}^{\ell-p}a_{1^{m}\times v\times 1^{\ell-p-m}}(q)t^{m}}}}{(t;q)_{\ell+1}}.

See §§
7.3
for details on undefined notation.
A remark of independent interest is that we do not know an alternative proof for the fact that the rational function expressing the left-hand side of (
1.4
) has a numerator which is a polynomial in
\mathbb{N}[q,t]
.

This brings us to our final stop.
The reader may have observed that two of our highlighted results — equation
1.3
and equation (
1.4
)— are equalities that involve the specialization
x_{i}\mapsto q^{i-1}
on one side and
q
-divided symmetrization of either Schubert polynomials or quasisymmetric polynomials on the other side.
What do these two families have common?
Motivated by this we introduce a property of polynomials that we refer to as the interval property. In fact this property is shared by numerous expansions in this work.
Broadly speaking, we say that a polynomial possesses the said property if its image in
\mathcal{K}
(or
\mathcal{K}_{+}
) expands in the squarefree basis of
\mathcal{K}
in terms of monomials
u_{I}
where
I
is a finite interval in
\mathbb{Z}
.
As we demonstrate in §
8
, this has interesting consequences for principal specializations of the sort considered here.

2.1.

-vectors and compositions

We call a sequence
c=(c_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{Z}}
an
\mathbb{N}
-vector
if its
support
is finite. Recall that the support of
c
, denoted by
\mathrm{Supp}(c)
, is the set of indices
i\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
c_{i}>0
. Let
\mathcal{W}
be the set of
\mathbb{N}
-vectors.
For
c\in\mathcal{W}
, define its
weight

|c|
to equal
\sum_{i}c_{i}
, and let
\mathcal{W}^{k}
be the set of
\mathbb{N}
-vectors with
|c|=k
.
We also define
e(c)=|c|-|\mathrm{Supp}(c)|
. Note that
e(c)
is nonnegative, and is equal to
0
precisely when
c_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for all
i
. Such vectors are naturally identified with finite subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
via their support.

We will also refer to finite sequences
(c_{1},\dots,c_{N})
of nonnegative integers as
\mathbb{N}
-vectors, or simply vectors. They are naturally embedded in
\mathcal{W}
by setting
c_{i}=0
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus[N]
. We write
\mathcal{W}_{N}
for these finite sequences, and
\mathcal{W}_{N}^{k}
for those with
|c|=k
. Let
\mathrm{rev}(c)
denote the reverse of the vector
c
: if
c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{N})
, then
\mathrm{rev}(c)
is by definition
(c_{N},\dots,c_{1})
.
Given an
\mathbb{N}
-vector
c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})
, we define
N(c)=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}(i-1)c_{i}
.

A
composition

\alpha
of
r
is an
\mathbb{N}
-vector
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N})
with positive entries such that
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\dots+\alpha_{N}=r
. This is denoted
\alpha\vDash r
. Let
\mathrm{Set}(\alpha)
denote the subset of
[r-1]
associated to
\alpha
under the folklore one-to-one correspondence
\alpha\mapsto\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\dots+\alpha_{N-1}\}
.

2.2.
Symmetric and quasisymmetric polynomials/functions

Throughout, let
\mathbf{x}=\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
,
\mathbf{x}_{+}=\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}
and
\mathbf{x}_{n}=\{x_{i}\;|\;i\in[n]\}
for
n
a positive integer.

A polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Q}[\mathbf{x}_{n}]
is called
quasisymmetric
if for any strong composition
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})
the coefficient of
x_{i_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}^{\alpha_{k}}
equals that of
x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}
whenever
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n
.
We denote the ring of quasisymmetric polynomials in
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
by
\operatorname{QSym}_{n}
. A linear basis for the degree
d
homogeneous component of
\operatorname{QSym}_{n}
is given by the
fundamental quasisymmetric polynomials

F_{\alpha}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
indexed by compositions
\alpha\vDash d
. To define this recall the correspondence sending
\alpha\vDash d
to
\mathrm{Set}(\alpha)\subseteq[d-1]
from the last section.
We have

(2.5)

F_{\alpha}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i_{1}\leq\cdots\leq i_{d}\leq n\\
i_{j}<i_{j+1}\text{ if }j\in\mathrm{Set}(\alpha)\end{subarray}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{d}}.

We note here that
F_{\alpha}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=0
if
\ell(\alpha)>n
.

Let
\operatorname{QSym}
denote the ring of quasisymmetric functions.
Elements of
\operatorname{QSym}
can be defined as bounded-degree formal power series
f\in\mathbb{Q}[\![{\mathbf{x}_{+}}]\!]
such that for any strong composition
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})
the coefficient of
x_{i_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}^{\alpha_{k}}
equals that of
x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}
for positive integers
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}
. The fundamental quasisymmetric functions
F_{\alpha}
for
\alpha
a strong composition are then naturally defined by removing the condition
i_{d}\leq n
in the sum (
2.5
).

2.3.
Reduced words of permutations, and Schubert polynomials

We denote the set of permutation on
n
letters by
\mathbb{S}_{n}
. An
inversion
in
w\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
is an ordered pair
(i,j)
such that
i<j
and
w_{i}>w_{j}
. The number of inversions will be denoted by
\mathrm{inv}(w)
. This quantity is also otherwise known as the
length
of
w
and denoted by
\ell(w)
.
Of particular importance is the subset
\mathbb{S}^{\prime}_{n}
of
\mathbb{S}_{n}
comprising permutations of length
n-1
.
A
descent
in
w\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
is an index
i\in[n-1]
such that
w_{i}>w_{i+1}
. The set of descents in
w
will be denoted by
\mathrm{Des}(w)
. The sum of entries in
\mathrm{Des}(w)
is referred to as the
major index
of
w
and denoted by
\mathrm{maj}(w)
.

A
reduced expression
of a permutation
w
is any minimal length factorization
w=s_{i_{1}}\dots s_{i_{\ell(w)}}
where each
s_{i_{j}}
is a simple transposition.
We refer to the word
i_{1}\dots i_{\ell(w)}
as a
reduced word
.
The set of reduced words for
w
is denoted by
\mathrm{Red}(w)
.
Given
{\bf a}\in\mathrm{Red}(w)
, its
content

\mathrm{cont}({\bf a})
is the
\mathbb{N}
-vector whose
j
th entry counts occurrences of
j
in
{\bf a}
for all
j
. We define the statistic
\mathrm{comaj}({\bf a})
to be the sum of all
j
such that
a_{j}<a_{j+1}
. For instance given the reduced word
{\bf a}=7534232
for
w=16423587\in\mathbb{S}_{8}
, we have
\mathrm{cont}({\bf a})=(0,2,2,1,1,0,1)
and
\mathrm{comaj}({\bf a})=3+5=8
.

2.4.
Schubert polynomials

Let
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}\subseteq\mathrm{QSym}_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}[\mathbf{x}_{n}]
be the subring of symmetric polynomials in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, and
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}^{+}
be the ideal of
\mathbb{Q}[\mathbf{x}_{n}]
generated by elements
f\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
such that
f(0)=0
.
The quotient ring
R_{n}=\mathbb{Q}[\mathbf{x}_{n}]/\mathrm{Sym}_{n}^{+}
is the
coinvariant ring
.

Let
\partial_{i}
be the divided difference operator on
\mathbb{Q}[\mathbf{x}_{n}]
, given by

(2.6)

\partial_{i}(f)=\frac{f-s_{i}\cdot f}{x_{i}-x_{i+1}}.

Define the
Schubert polynomials
for
w\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
as follows:
\mathfrak{S}_{w_{o}}=x_{1}^{n-1}x_{2}^{n-2}\cdots x_{n-1}
, while if
i
is a descent of
w
, let
\mathfrak{S}_{ws_{i}}=\partial_{i}\mathfrak{S}_{w}
. Here
w_{o}
is the longest word in
\mathbb{S}_{n}
.
For
w\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
, the Schubert polynomial
\mathfrak{S}_{w}
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
\ell(w)
in
\mathbb{Q}[\mathbf{x}_{n}]
.
In fact Schubert polynomials are well defined for
w\in\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
. Moreover, when
w\in\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
runs through all permutations whose largest descent is at most
n
, the Schubert polynomials
\mathfrak{S}_{w}
form a basis for
\mathbb{Q}[\mathbf{x}_{n}]
.

Let
\mathbf{k}

.
We assume that

.
In particular

. This condition ensures that

for

, so that we can safely divide by
q
-integers and
q
-factorials.

We will mostly be interested in the ‘generic’ case where
q
is an indeterminate and
\mathbf{k}=\mathbb{Q}(q)
or
\mathbb{C}(q)
say. However important applications occur when:

\circ

\mathbf{k}=\mathbb{R}
and
q>0
, cf. §
5
.

\circ

q
is a prime power, cf. §
7
;

The case

is the starting point of this work, since certain results presented here will give new proofs of certain results of the authors in
[
33
,
34
]
.

3.1.
The algebra
\mathcal{K}

The central object of our study is the following
\mathbf{k}
-algebra depending on the parameter
q
.

Definition 3.1

(Klyachko algebra
\mathcal{K}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}^{q}
be the commutative algebra over
\mathbf{k}
on the generators
\{u_{i}\;|\;i\in\mathbb{Z}\}
subject to the following relations for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
:

(3.1)

\displaystyle(q+1)u_{i}^{2}=qu_{i}u_{i-1}+u_{i}u_{i+1}.

It is a
quadratic algebra
, i.e. its defining relations have degree two. Let
\mathsf{z}=(z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be indeterminates; we will also need
\mathsf{z}_{+}=(z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
and
\mathsf{z}_{r}=(z_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,r}
for
r>0
. We let

\pi:\mathbf{k}[\mathsf{z}]\to\mathcal{K},z_{i}\mapsto u_{i}

be the defining projection.
If we let
{\sf IK}
be the ideal of
\mathbf{k}[\mathsf{z}]
generated by the elements
(q+1)z_{i}^{2}-qz_{i}z_{i-1}-z_{i}z_{i+1}
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
\mathcal{K}
is by definition isomorphic to
\mathbf{k}[\mathsf{z}]/{\sf IK}
.

Shift and homothecies.
Consider the
shift

\tau:i\mapsto i+1
on
\mathbb{Z}
. This induces an automorphism on
\mathbf{k}[\mathsf{z}]
via
z_{i}\mapsto z_{i+1}
, which we continue to denote by
\tau
. The relations of
\mathcal{K}
are invariant under
\tau
, so that
u_{i}\mapsto u_{i+1}
defines an automorphism of
\mathcal{K}
still denoted by
\tau
and named shift.

We also note that for any
\lambda\in\mathbf{k}^{*}
,
u_{i}\mapsto\lambda u_{i}
extend to an automorphism of
\mathcal{K}
since the relations (
3.1
) are homogeneous.

Remark 3.2
.

There is a more homogeneous version of the relations of
\mathcal{K}
, namely:

(q_{1}+q_{2})u_{i}^{2}=q_{1}u_{i}u_{i-1}+q_{2}u_{i}u_{i+1}.

for some
q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathbf{k}
. If
q_{2}\neq 0
this becomes the relation for
\mathcal{K}^{q}
with
q=q_{1}/q_{2}
so there is no loss of generality in keeping just one parameter, as we opted to do in our definition.

The homogeneous version has the advantage of exhibiting more symmetry. In particular the relation is invariant under
u_{j}\mapsto u_{-j}
and
q_{1}\leftrightarrow q_{2}
, which shows that
\mathcal{K}^{q}\simeq\mathcal{K}^{1/q}
for nonzero
q
.

Quotients.
Given any subset
I
of
\mathbb{Z}
, we let
\mathcal{K}_{I}
be the algebra generated by
u_{i}\in I
subject to the relations (
3.1
) with
u_{i}=0
for
i\notin I
. That is,
\mathcal{K}_{I}
is the quotient of
\mathcal{K}
by the ideal generated by the
u_{i}
for
i\notin I
. The most important quotients are for
I
semi-infinite and for
I
an interval:

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{+}\coloneqq\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
and
\mathcal{K}_{n}\coloneqq\mathcal{K}_{\{1,\ldots,n-1\}}
.

We define
\pi_{+}:\mathbf{k}[\mathsf{z}_{+}]\to\mathcal{K}_{+}
to be the natural projection sending
z_{i}
to
u_{i}
, whose kernel
{\sf IK}_{+}
is generated by the elements
(q+1)z_{i}^{2}-qz_{i}z_{i-1}-z_{i}z_{i+1}
for
i>1
together with
(q+1)z_{1}^{2}-z_{1}z_{2}
.
Analogously, define
\pi_{n}:\mathbf{k}[\mathsf{z}_{n-1}]\to\mathcal{K}_{n}
with kernel
{\sf IK}_{n}
generated by the elements
(q+1)z_{1}^{2}-z_{1}z_{2}
,
(q+1)z_{n-1}^{2}-qz_{n-1}z_{n-2}
and
(q+1)z_{i}^{2}-qz_{i}z_{i-1}-z_{i}z_{i+1}
for
1<i<n-1
.

Note that we have kept the notation
u_{i}
for the generators
u_{i}
in different algebras
\mathcal{K}_{I}
, in order to avoid too much notation. When a computation holds in a quotient but not in the full algebra
\mathcal{K}
, we will always make sure to specify it clearly.

Remark 3.4
.

The presentation of
\mathcal{K}_{n}
for
q=1
coincides with
H^{*}(\mathrm{Perm}_{n},\mathbb{Q})^{\mathbb{S}_{n}}
as proved in
[
24
,
25
]
and used by the authors in
[
34
]
.

3.2.
Basic identities.

Definition 3.5
.

For any finite subset
I\subset\mathbb{Z}
, define
u_{I}\coloneqq\prod_{i\in I}u_{i}\in\mathcal{K}
.

Given the defining relations of
\mathcal{K}
, it is natural to expect that these squarefree monomials form a linear basis of
\mathcal{K}
, which is the content of Proposition
3.9
. Toward establishing this we prove some basic identities that hold in
\mathcal{K}
. The next lemma lays the groundwork for many of the computations in this article.

Lemma 3.6
.

Consider integers
a\leq b
and
i\in[a,b]
. In
\mathcal{K}
, we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle(b-a+2)_{q}u_{i}u_{[a,b]}

\displaystyle=q^{i-a+1}(b-i+1)_{q}u_{[a-1,b]}+(i-a+1)_{q}u_{[a,b+1]}

Moreover, (
3.2
) is a consequence of the relations (
3.1
) for
i=a,\ldots,b
.

The second claim shows a
local
property of the rewriting, which is useful in order to compare computation in various quotients
\mathcal{K}_{I}
.

Proof.

Let
L=u_{[a-1,b]}
and
R=u_{[a,b+1]}
, whih are the two elements of
\mathcal{K}
occurring on the right hand side of (
3.2
).
Then the relations (
3.1
) gives that the elements
f_{i}\coloneqq u_{i}u_{\{a,\dots,b\}}
satisfy

\displaystyle(1+q)f_{i}

\displaystyle=qf_{i-1}+f_{i+1}\text{ for }a<i<b;

\displaystyle(1+q)f_{a}

\displaystyle=qL+f_{a+1};

\displaystyle(1+q)f_{b}

\displaystyle=qf_{b-1}+R.

Let
n=b-a+1
. Let
F
be the
n\times 1
vector with entries
f_{i}
;
M
the
n\times n
tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix with diagonal
1+q
, superdiagonal
-1
and subdiagonal
-q
; and
C
the
n\times 1
vector with
C_{1}=qL,C_{n}=R
and all other entries zero. Then the above system of equations can be succinctly written
MF=C
. For
a=1,b=4
we get

\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1+q&-1&0&0\\
-q&1+q&-1&0\\
0&-q&1+q&-1\\
0&0&-q&1+q\end{pmatrix}}_{M}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}u_{[1,4]}\\
u_{2}u_{[1,4]}\\
u_{3}u_{[1,4]}\\
u_{4}u_{[1,4]}\end{pmatrix}}_{F}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}qu_{[0,4]}\\
0\\
0\\
u_{[1,5]}\end{pmatrix}}_{C}

The determinant of
M
can be computed to be
(b-a+2)_{q}
, which is nonzero by our assumption on
q
. The inverse
M^{-1}
is easily checked to be given by

\displaystyle(M^{-1})_{ij}=\frac{1}{(b-a+2)_{q}}\times\begin{cases}q^{j-i}(n+1-i)_{q}(j)_{q}\text{ for }i\geq j;\\
(n+1-j)_{q}(i)_{q}\text{ for }i<j.\end{cases}

Then the rows of the identity
F=M^{-1}C
give the relations (
3.2
). The second claim in Lemma
3.6
follows immediately from the previous proof.
∎

We note a corollary which follows from elementary computations:

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
i\in\mathbb{Z}
and assume the interval
[a,b]
contains
i-1
or
i
; that is
[a,b]\cap\{i-1,i\}\neq\emptyset
. Then we have

(3.3)

(b-a+2)_{q}(u_{i}-u_{i-1})u_{[a,b]}=q^{i-a}\left(u_{[a,b+1]}-u_{[a-1,b]}\right).

Now we give an important relation allowing us to expand any element as a linear combination of the elements
u_{I}
.

Let
c
be an
\mathbb{N}
-vector and let
J=\mathrm{Supp}(c)
.
Assume there exists an index
i
such that
c_{i}\geq 2
, and fix such an
i
. Let
I=[a,b]
be the
maximal
interval in
J
containing
i
, so that
a-1,b+1\notin J
and thus

c_{a-1}=c_{b+1}=0
.
Let
L_{i}(c)
(resp.
R_{i}(c)
) be the
\mathbb{N}
-vector
c^{\prime}
defined by
c^{\prime}_{k}=c_{k}
if
k\neq a-1,i
(resp.
k\neq i,b+1
),
c^{\prime}_{i}=c_{i}-1
, and
c^{\prime}_{a-1}=1
(resp.
c^{\prime}_{b+1}=1
). In words, these are obtained from

by decrementing

by

and incrementing

(resp.
c_{b+1}
) by
1
.

For an
\mathbb{N}
-vector

, let

u^{c}=\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{i}^{c_{i}}.

Corollary 3.8
.

With
c,i,a,b
as above and assuming

, we have in
\mathcal{K}

(3.4)

(b-a+2)_{q}u^{c}=q^{i-a+1}(b-i+1)_{q}u^{L_{i}(c)}+(i-a+1)_{q}u^{R_{i}(c)}.

Proof.

By definition the generators
u_{j}
for

occur in

, and since

). Since

∎

3.3.
The squarefree basis

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
J\subset\mathbb{Z}
. The
u_{I}
for
I
finite subset of
J
form a
\mathbf{k}
-basis of the algebra
\mathcal{K}_{J}
.

We denote the basis of squarefree monomials for
\mathcal{K}_{I}
by
\mathcal{B}_{I}
. In the important cases
\mathcal{K}
,
\mathcal{K}_{+}
, and
\mathcal{K}_{n}
, we denote the corresponding bases by
\mathcal{B}
,
\mathcal{B}_{+}
, and
\mathcal{B}_{n}
respectively.

Proof.

From (
3.4
), by induction on
e(c)=|c|-|\mathrm{Supp}(c)|
, one can reduce any monomial in
u_{i}
to a linear combination of squarefree monomials. Indeed
e(L_{i}(c))=e(R_{i}(c))=e(c)-1
when
c_{i}\geq 2
, and recall that
e(c)=0
precisely when
u^{c}
is squarefree. It follows immediately that the
u_{I}
span
\mathcal{K}
; by passing to the quotient, the same holds for any
\mathcal{K}_{J}
.

We now want to show linear independence of these elements. First, it is enough to prove this for the algebras
\mathcal{K}_{n}
for
n\geq 1
. Indeed any relation between squarefree monomials in an algebra
\mathcal{K}_{J}
must hold in an algebra
\mathcal{K}_{J^{\prime}}
for
J^{\prime}
a finite interval: this is a consequence of the second part of Lemma
3.6
. By the shift invariance of the relations, we can assume
J^{\prime}=[1,n-1]
for a certain
n
.

We will use standard notions of commutative algebra in the proof, for which we refer to
[
40
, §I.5]
for instance.
Recall that
\mathcal{K}_{n}
is defined as the quotient of
\mathbf{k}[\mathsf{z}_{n-1}]
by
{\sf IK}_{n}
, an ideal generated by
n-1
quadratic polynomials. Now we have just shown that squarefree monomials span
\mathcal{K}_{n}
, which is therefore finite-dimensional over
\mathbf{k}
. It follows that the generators of
{\sf IK}_{n}
form a
homogeneous system of parameters
for
\mathbf{k}[\mathsf{z}_{n-1}]
, as a module over itself.

Since
\mathbf{k}[\mathsf{z}_{n-1}]
is Cohen-Macaulay, these generators form necessarily a
regular sequence
. Hence we get the following identity between Hilbert series
2
2

2

Recall that the Hilbert series
\mathrm{Hilb}(\oplus_{i\geq 0}V_{i},t)
of a graded vector space with finite-dimensional homogeneous components is the generating series
\sum_{i\geq 0}(\dim)V_{i}t^{i}
.
:

\mathrm{Hilb}(\mathbf{k}[\mathsf{z}_{n-1}],t)=\frac{1}{(1-t^{2})^{n-1}}\mathrm{Hilb}(\mathcal{K}_{n},t).

Since
\mathrm{Hilb}(\mathbf{k}[\mathsf{z}_{n-1}],t)=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{n-1}}
, we obtain
\mathrm{Hilb}(\mathcal{K}_{n},t)=(1+t)^{n-1}
. So
\mathcal{K}_{n}
has
\mathbf{k}
-dimension
2^{n-1}
, which is exactly the number of squarefree monomials it contains. Since we have shown that these span
\mathcal{K}_{n}
, they necessarily form a basis.
∎

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
P\in\mathbf{k}[\mathsf{z}_{+}]
, and suppose that there exists
N>0
such that
P(u_{1},u_{2},\dots)=0
in
\mathcal{K}_{n}
for all
n\geq N
. Then
P(u_{1},u_{2},\dots)=0
in
\mathcal{K}_{+}
.

Proof.

P(u_{1},u_{2},\dots)
vanishes in
\mathcal{K}_{+}
if all its coefficients on the basis
u_{I},I\subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}
are zero. Now let
d
be the degree of
P
and
a
be the maximal index of an indeterminate
z_{i}
occurring in
P
. It follows from the second part of Lemma
3.6
that, in order to reduce
P
in the basis
u_{I},I\subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, one only needs relations involving generators
u_{i}
with
i<a+d
. These relations occur in any
\mathcal{K}_{n}
for
n
large enough, and so by hypothesis we have
P(u_{1},u_{2},\dots)=0
in
\mathcal{K}_{+}
. ∎

As we will see in §
5
, coefficients of general monomials expressed in the basis
\mathcal{B}
have a probabilistic interpretation due to Diaconis and Fulton
[
11
]
. In this seminal work, the algebras
\mathcal{K}_{I}
actually occur as special cases of a wide class of algebras, and Proposition

is proved in greater generality. We included our own proof as to be self contained; our arguments are also of a different nature.

3.4.
Specializations

The following specialization will be instrumental in this work to go from computations in
\mathcal{K}
to numerical formulas.

Proposition 3.11
.

The assignment

u_{i}\mapsto\begin{cases}(i)_{q}&\text{ for }i>0;\\
0&\text{ for }i\leq 0,\end{cases}

Proof.

One has

∎

The specialization obviously factors through

, and we keep the same notation

\displaystyle\operatorname{sp}:\mathcal{K}_{+}

\displaystyle\to\mathbf{k}

\displaystyle u_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto(i)_{q}

Now consider an interval

\operatorname{sp}\left(\frac{u_{[a+1,b]}}{(b-a)_{q}!}\right)=\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{b}{a}_{q}.

This evaluation will allow us to go from identities in

to numerical formulas.

Other specializations

It is natural to consider specializations, i.e. characters of
\mathcal{K}
. Can we characterize the algebra morphisms

? Given the presentation (

), the problem is equivalent to characterizing all sequences

in
\mathbf{k}
that satisfy

for all

u_{i}\mapsto a_{i}
.

\circ

A first family of specializations is given by

that do not vanish.

\circ

A second family of specializations, depending on two integer parameters

and two parameters

, can given by

\begin{cases}a_{i}=0\text{ for }i\in[m,M];\\

It is then an easy exercise to show that every specialization belongs to one of the two families.

It follows immediately that the specializations of

5.
Remixed Eulerian numbers

In this section we give certain properties of positivity of the numbers
A_{c}^{q}
. First we show that when
q
is a positive real number, these give certain probabilities in a certain diffusion process on
\mathbb{Z}
. We then show that as polynomials in

,

has nonnegative integer coefficients.

A much more in-depth study of these polynomials is given by the authors in
[
35
]
.

5.1.
A probabilistic process after Diaconis-Fulton

Consider the integer line
\mathbb{Z}
as a set of
sites
.
A
configuration
is an
\mathbb{N}
-vector
c=(c_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
with
\sum_{i}c_{i}<\infty
as before, which we now visualize as a finite set of particles, where there are
c_{i}
particles stacked at site
i
. The configuration
c=(\dots,0,3,0,c_{0}=1,1,2,0,2,0,\dots)
is represented below.
Stable
configurations are those for which
c_{i}\leq 1
for all
i
, and are naturally identified with finite subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
via their support.

Given
jump probabilities

q_{L},q_{R}\geq 0
satisfying
q_{L}+q_{R}=1
, we consider the following process with state space the set of configurations: Suppose we are in a configuration
c
. If
c
is stable, the process stops. Otherwise, pick any
i
such that
c_{i}>1
and move the top particle at site
i
to the top of site
i-1
with probability
q_{L}
, and to the top of site
i-1
(resp.
i+1
)(resp.
q_{R}
). The resulting configurations are
L_{i}(c)
and
R_{i}(c)
respectively, as seen already in this work.

Let us assume here that we perform such a jump every unit of time while the process hasn’t stabilized. To specify the process completely, one needs to give a rule (deterministic or probabilistic) on how to pick the site
i
in case there are several sites with more than one particle. It turns out that for our purposes it will not matter, as we now explain.

Note first that whichever rule is chosen, it is easy to show that the process will end up in a stable configuration with probability
1
. We can then define
\mathbb{P}_{c}(I)
to be the probability that the process, according to the chosen rules and starting a configuration
c
, ends up in the stable configuration given by the finite subset
I
of
\mathbb{Z}
. Diaconis and Fulton
[
11
]
showed a strong
abelian property
of this model, namely that
\mathbb{P}_{c}(I)
is independent of the chosen rules for picking the jumping particle
.

Assume
q_{R}>0
and let
q\coloneqq q_{L}/q_{R}
, so that
q_{L}=\frac{q}{1+q}
and
q_{R}=\frac{1}{1+q}
. We have then the following result:

Proposition 5.1
.

Fix an
\mathbb{N}
-vector
c
. Then we have the following expansion in
\mathcal{K}
in the basis
\mathcal{B}
:

u^{c}=\sum_{\text{finite }I\subset\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{P}_{c}(I)u_{I}.

In particular, if
c\in\mathcal{W}_{r}^{r}
, then

\mathbb{P}_{c}([r])=\frac{A^{q}_{c}}{(r)_{q}!}.

In this probabilistic interpretation, we recognize Lemma
3.6
as the
gambler’s ruin
. Namely, the coefficients on the right hand side compute the left and right exit probabilities of an asymmetric random walker on the integer segment
[a,b]
, starting from
i
.

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

If
c
is already stable, there is nothing to show as
u^{c}=u_{\mathrm{Supp}(c)}\in\mathcal{B}
, and
\mathbb{P}_{c}(J)=\delta_{J,\mathrm{Supp}(c)}
for any finite
J\subset\mathbb{Z}
.
Otherwise suppose
c_{i}>2
for some
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. We are allowed to pick
i
to hop because of the abelian property, and then obtain immediately the following transition equation

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{c}(I)=q_{L}\mathbb{P}_{L_{i}(c)}(I)+q_{R}\mathbb{P}_{R_{i}(c)}(I).

Now the we see that the coefficient
A^{I}_{c}
of
u_{I}
in the
\mathcal{B}
-expansion of
u^{c}
satisfies the same relation. For a fixed
I
, these relations characterize
A^{I}_{c}
following the proof for remixed Eulerian numbers in the case

, and the proof is complete.
∎

The obvious parallel between the transition moves in this particle jumping process and the relations defining
\mathcal{K}
eliminates the element of surprise from our preceding result. Nevertheless, on viewing the result in isolation, it is intriguing that this simple probabilistic process computes
q
-analogues of a discrete-geometric notion — mixed Eulerian numbers in this case. Our next remark frames this particle process in the appropriate context which is the work of Diaconis-Fulton
[
11
]
.

Remark 5.2
.

The above process is an instance of Diaconis-Fulton’s
growth game

[
11
]
. For the case where finitely many particles are dropped on the origin beginning with the origin being the only inhabited site, one obtains
q
-analogues of classical Eulerian numbers (suitably normalized), and this case is also discussed in
[
32
]
.

A property of remixed Eulerian numbers that will come handy in the sequel is a cyclic sum rule satisfied by remixed Eulerian numbers. In the case
q=1
, this was established by Postnikov
[
37
]
by a geometric argument. It was later reproven by Petrov
[
36
]
by a probabilistic argument which we essentially follow here.

To state the rule, we need some notation.
For
c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{r})\in\mathcal{W}_{r}^{r}
, set
\tilde{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{r},0)
. Write
c\sim c^{\prime}
if
\tilde{c}^{\prime}
can be obtained as a cyclic rotation of
\tilde{c}
. This is an equivalence relation on
\mathcal{W}_{r}^{r}
, and we define

\mathsf{Cyc}(c)
to be the equivalence class of
c
.
For
c=(2,0,1)
,
\mathsf{Cyc}(c)
contains
c
and
c^{\prime}=(1,0,2)
since
\tilde{c}=(2,0,1,0)\sim(1,0,2,0)=\tilde{c}^{\prime}.

Proposition 5.3
.

For
c\in\mathcal{W}_{r}^{r}
, we have

\sum_{c^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Cyc}(c)}A_{c^{\prime}}(q)=(r)_{q}!.

Proof.

As this is a polynomial relation, it is enough to prove it when
q
is a nonnegative real. Consider the same dynamic as above, but on a circular set of sites labeled
1
through
r+1
clockwise. Fix
c\in\mathcal{W}_{r}^{r}
and start the process with the configuration of
c_{i}
particles at site
i
for
1\leq i\leq r
, and
0
at site

. The process stabilizes with probability one and has the abelian property, as it is still an instance of the processes considered in
[
11
]
.

In a stable configuration, exactly one of the
r+1
sites will be unoccupied. By rotational symmetry, the probability
P_{j}
of ending at the stable configuration supported on

is equal to the probability of reaching the stable configuration supported on
[r]
but starting from the configuration
c

is

if

. If not, the rotated configuration has support in

and so corresponds naturally to an element

in

. The probability

in this case is given by

introduced above, since the process behaves exactly as on

.

The preceding argument together with Proposition

gives

\displaystyle\sum_{c^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Cyc}(c)}\frac{A_{c^{\prime}}(q)}{(r)_{q}!}=1,

which is what we set out to prove.
∎

Continuing our example above, we get the following relation which can be checked from the data in Example

A particularly interesting case of Proposition

is an interval. Indeed, the cyclic rotations do not matter and the sum in question only runs over linear shifts of

5.2.
Positivity of coefficients

In the rest of this section we consider

What is not obvious from its definition, or even from the relations (

For any

Proof.

Suppose first that

Now consider

.
Now note that (

\displaystyle(m_{j}+1)_{q}A_{c}(q)=q^{i-a+1}(b-i+1)_{q}A_{L_{i}(c)}(q)+(i-a+1)_{q}A_{R_{i}(c)}(q),

are defined as in the proof of Theorem

By dividing both sides of this equation by

Here

and

If

If

(5.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{m\geq 0}t^{m}\prod_{i=1}^{k}(m+i)_{q}^{c_{i}}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{r-k}A_{0^{i}c0^{r-k-i}}(q)t^{i}}{(t;q)_{r+1}}.

Proof.

Fix
m\geq 0
. Using the shift invariance of the relations defining
\mathcal{K}
, we can expand
u_{m+1}^{c_{1}}\dots u_{m+k}^{c_{k}}=\tau^{m}(u_{1}^{c_{1}}\cdots u_{k}^{c_{k}})
in the basis
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathcal{K}
by shifting indices on the right-hand side in equation (
4.19
).
We thus obtain

(5.6)

\displaystyle(r)_{q}!u_{m+1}^{c_{1}}\dots u_{m+k}^{c_{k}}=\sum_{j=0}^{r-k}A_{0^{j}c0^{r-j-k}}(q)u_{[1,r]+(m-j)}.

Via the specialization
\operatorname{sp}:u_{i}\mapsto(i)_{q}
for
i>0
, see
3.11
, we obtain the polynomial identity

(m+1)_{q}^{c_{1}}\dots(m+k)_{q}^{c_{k}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\min(m,r-k)}A_{0^{j}c0^{r-k-j}}(q)\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{r+m-j}{m-j}_{q}.

By Cauchy’s
q
-binomial theorem (
2.4
), the right-hand side is the coefficient of
t^{m}
in
\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{r-k}A_{0^{i}c0^{r-k-i}}(q)t^{i}}{(t;q)_{n}}
, which completes the proof.
∎

We briefly touch upon various instances of connected remixed Eulerian numbers in disguise in literature. As elaborated in
[
19
, §3]
, given positive integers
p_{1}
through
p_{m}
, MacMahon considered the
\mathrm{maj}
-
\mathrm{des}
distribution over the set of permutations
M_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})}
of the multiset
\{1^{p_{1}},\dots,m^{p_{m}}\}
. Suppose
r=p_{1}+\cdots+p_{m}
. MacMahon showed
[
19
, Eq. (3.3)]
that

(5.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}t^{n}\prod_{1\leq i\leq m}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{n+p_i}{p_i}_{q}=\frac{\sum_{\pi\in M_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})}}t^{\mathrm{des}(\pi)}q^{\mathrm{maj}(\pi)}}{(t;q)_{r+1}}.

By setting
c_{i}\coloneqq|\{j\;|\;p_{j}\geq i\}|
and
k\coloneqq\max\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\}
, one can rewrite this as

(5.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}t^{n}\prod_{1\leq i\leq k}(n+i)_{q}^{c_{i}}=\frac{\prod_{1\leq i\leq m}(p_{i})_{q}!\left(\sum_{\pi\in M_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})}}t^{\mathrm{des}(\pi)}q^{\mathrm{maj}(\pi)}\right)}{(t;q)_{r+1}}.

Note that we must have
c_{1}\geq c_{2}\geq\cdots\geq c_{k}>0
. Also given any such partition
(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
, one can always associate a corresponding
(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})
. Thus we see that for partitions
c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
, the polynomial
A_{0^{i}c0^{r-k-i}}(q)
tracks the distribution of major index over
\pi\in M_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})}
where
\mathrm{des}(\pi)=i
, up to scaling by
\prod_{1\leq i\leq m}(p_{i})_{q}!
. The case where
p_{i}=1
for all
1\leq i\leq m
is particularly well known.
In this case
c=(r)
and one obtains

(5.9)

\displaystyle A_{(0^{i},r,0^{r-k-i})}(q)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{r}\\
\mathrm{des}(\pi)=i\end{subarray}}q^{\mathrm{maj}(\pi)},

and the right-hand side gives the
q
-Eulerian number of Carlitz
[
8
]
.

More generally, the connected remixed Eulerian numbers appear in work of Garsia-Remmel
[
18
]
as
q
-hit polynomials
.
Indeed, Equations I.11 and I.12 in loc. cit. together imply that the
A_{0^{i}c0^{r-k-i}}(q)
are
q
-hit polynomials (up to a power of
q
) of certain Ferrers boards determined by
c
. Informally, this is how the correspondence goes. Consider any Dyck path from
(0,0)
to
(r,r)
such that its
area sequence
has
c_{i}
instances of
i
for
i\in[r]
. Then it must be the case that
\mathrm{Supp}(c)
is an interval in
[r]
. Such a Dyck path in turn determines a Ferrers diagram
\lambda(c)
, and computing the
q
-hit polynomial as a weighted sum over all nonattacking placements in the
r\times r
board where
i
rooks land inside
\lambda(c)
gives
A_{0^{i}c0^{r-k-i}}(q)
.
We address this connection in more detail in
[
35
]
.

6.
Specializations of quasisymmetric polynomials

We now aim to give the expansion of
\rho_{+}(P)=P(u_{1},u_{2}-u_{1},u_{3}-u_{2},\dots)
in the basis
\mathcal{B}_{+}
for any quasisymmetric polynomial
P(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
. Amongst other things, this will shed a new light on the main results of the authors in
[
33
]
for
q=1
. It helps to consider the case of monomials first.

6.1.
Expansion of monomials in
u_{i}-u_{i-1}

Consider the expansion of
\rho(\mathbf{x}^{c})=\prod_{i}(u_{i}-u_{i-1})^{c_{i}}
for
c
any
\mathbb{N}
-vector:

(6.1)

\displaystyle\prod_{i}(u_{i}-u_{i-1})^{c_{i}}=\sum_{I}\psi^{c}_{I}u_{I}.

We use Theorem
4.15
to find a formula for the coefficients
\psi^{c}_{I}
. Assume
|I|=|c|
, otherwise
\psi^{c}_{I}=0
for degree reasons.
Let
I_{1}\sqcup I_{2}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup I_{k}
be the maximal interval decomposition of
I
, with
I_{j}=[a_{j},b_{j}]
. We then introduce
\widetilde{I}\coloneqq\widetilde{I}_{1}\sqcup\widetilde{I}_{2}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup\widetilde{I}_{k}

where
\widetilde{I}_{j}=[a_{j},b_{j}+1]
for all
j
.

Arguing as in the proof of Theorem
4.15
, we have that
\psi^{c}_{I}=0
unless
\mathrm{Supp}(c)\subseteq\widetilde{I}
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
c,I
satisfy
\mathrm{Supp}(c)\subseteq\widetilde{I}
and
{\sum_{i\in\widetilde{I}_{j}}c_{i}=|I_{j}|}
for
j=1,\dots,k
. Then

(6.2)

\displaystyle\psi^{c}_{I}=\prod_{1\leq j\leq k}\frac{1}{(|I_{j}|)_{q}!}\big\langle{x_{a_{j}}^{c_{a_{j}}}x_{a_{j}+1}^{c_{a_{j}+1}}\cdots x_{b_{j}}^{c_{b_{j}}}x_{b_{j}+1}^{c_{b_{j}+1}}}\big\rangle_{\{a_{j},\dots,b_{j}+1\}}^{q}.

Note that all terms in the product can be calculated by Proposition
4.3
. If
\sum_{i\in\widetilde{I}_{j}}c_{i}=|I_{j}|
fails for a certain
j
, then
\psi^{c}_{I}=0
.

We now restrict to the case where
\mathrm{Supp}(c)
is an interval
. The reason for this will become clear soon. Without loss of generality we may assume that
\mathrm{Supp}(c)=[k]
for some positive integer
k
, and suppose
|c|=r
.

By the analysis above, the only nonzero coefficients
\psi^{c}_{I}
occur when
\widetilde{I}
is an interval containing
[k]
. A direct analysis reveals the following two scenarios are the only possible ones:

(1)

\widetilde{I}=\widetilde{I}_{1}
is an interval of length
r+1
containing
[k]
. Write
I=[1,r]-a
for some
0\leq a\leq r-k+1
. Then we immediately get from (
6.2
) that

(6.3)

\displaystyle(r)_{q}!\psi_{I}^{c}=\big\langle{\mathbf{x}^{c}}\big\rangle_{[1,1+r]-a}^{q}.

(2)

\widetilde{I}=\widetilde{I}_{1}\sqcup\widetilde{I}_{2}
with
\widetilde{I}_{1}=[i-L_{i},i]
and
\widetilde{I}_{2}=[i+1,i+1+R_{i}]
for a certain
i\in[1,k-1]
, where
L_{i}=\sum_{j\leq i}c_{j}
and
R_{i}=\sum_{j>i}c_{j}
. Now (
6.2
)
says

(6.4)

\displaystyle(r)_{q}!\psi_{I}^{c}=\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{r+1}{L_i+1}_{q}\big\langle{x_{1}^{c_{1}}\cdots x_{i}^{c_{i}}}\big\rangle_{[i-L_{i},i]}^{q}\big\langle{x_{i+1}^{c_{i+1}}\cdots x_{k}^{c_{k}}}\big\rangle_{[i+1,i+1+R_{i}]}^{q}

which can be easily evaluated.

6.2.
qDS of fundamental quasisymmetric polynomials

In this section we will determine
\big\langle{F_{\alpha}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})}\big\rangle_{n}^{q}
for
\alpha
a strong composition of
r
and
m\leq n
. The case
q=1
was one of the main results of
[
33
]
; see Remark
6.4
below.

Recall that
N(\alpha)=\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell(\alpha)}(i-1)\alpha_{i}
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\alpha\vDash r
, and let
m\geq\ell(\alpha)
.
We have that in
\mathcal{K}_{+}
,

(6.5)

\rho_{+}(F_{\alpha}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}))=\frac{q^{N(\alpha)}}{(r)_{q}!}u_{[1,r]+m-\ell(\alpha)}.

In words, the expansion of
F_{\alpha}(u_{1},u_{2}-u_{1},\dots,u_{m}-u_{1})
in the basis
\mathcal{B}_{+}
of
\mathcal{K}_{+}
consists of the single term
\frac{q^{N(\alpha)}}{(r)_{q}!}u_{[1,r]+m-\ell(\alpha)}
. Let us point out that the complete expansion in
\mathcal{K}
has many more terms in general. It can be described explicitly, but we will not need it here.

Proof.

Let
k\coloneqq\ell(\alpha)
. We will prove the claim by induction on
m-k
.

If
m=k
, then
F_{\alpha}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
is the monomial
x^{\alpha}
.
As
\alpha
is a strong composition,
\mathrm{Supp}(\alpha)
is connected and so the expansion of
\rho(x^{\alpha})=(u_{1}-u_{0})^{\alpha_{1}}\dots(u_{r}-u_{r-1})^{\alpha_{k}}
in the
\mathcal{B}
basis is given by (
6.3
) and (
6.4
).
To get the expansion for
\rho_{+}(x^{\alpha})
, we retain the basis elements in
\mathcal{B}_{+}
. Only
u_{[r]}
survives, corresponding to the case
a=0
in (
6.3
), so that
it comes with the coefficient
\psi_{[r]}^{\alpha}=\big\langle{\mathbf{x}^{c}}\big\rangle_{r+1}^{q}/{(r)_{q}!}
. Now
\big\langle{\mathbf{x}^{c}}\big\rangle_{r+1}^{q}
equals
q^{N(\alpha)}
by Proposition
4.3
as wanted. The claim is true for
m=k
.

So we turn our attention to the case
m>k
.
Observe the following well-known recursion:

\displaystyle F_{\alpha}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}F_{(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1})+x_{m}F_{(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k-1},\alpha_{k}-1)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})&\alpha_{k}>1,\\
F_{(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1})+x_{m}F_{(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k-1})}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1})&\alpha_{k}=1.\end{array}\right.

We induct on
m+|\alpha|
.
Let
\alpha^{\prime}
denote the composition
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}-1)
when
\alpha_{k}>1
and
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k-1})
if
\alpha_{k}=1
. In either case, applying
\rho_{+}
to the recursion above, and using the inductive hypothesis gives

(6.6)

\displaystyle\rho_{+}((r)_{q}!F_{\alpha}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}))=q^{N(\alpha)}u_{[1,r]+m-k-1}+q^{N(\alpha^{\prime})}(r)_{q}x_{m}u_{[1,r-1]+m-k},

which by Corollary
3.7
becomes

(6.7)

\displaystyle\rho_{+}((r)_{q}!F_{\alpha}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}))=q^{N(\alpha)}u_{[1,r]+m-k-1}+q^{N(\alpha^{\prime})}q^{k-1}\left(u_{[1,r]+m-k}-u_{[1,r]+m-k-1}\right).

The inductive step is completed by noting that
q^{k-1}q^{N(\alpha^{\prime})}=q^{N(\alpha)}
.
∎

We obtain the following consequence generalizing
[
33
, Theorem 1.1]
which is the case
q=1
.

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
\alpha\vDash n-1
, and let
\ell(\alpha)\leq m\leq n
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\big\langle{F_{\alpha}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})}\big\rangle_{n}^{q}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}q^{N(\alpha)}&\text{if }m=\ell(\alpha);\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

In particular, for
f
any quasisymmetric function of degree
n-1
, we have

(6.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 1}f(1,q,\dots,q^{j-1})t^{j}=\frac{\sum_{m}t^{m}\big\langle{f(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})}\big\rangle_{n}^{q}}{(t;q)_{n}}.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.11
, we know that
\big\langle{F_{\alpha}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})}\big\rangle_{n}^{q}
equals
(r)_{q}!
times the coefficient of
u_{[r]}
in
\rho_{+}(F_{\alpha}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}))
. The first claim of Corollary
6.3
then follows immediately from Theorem
6.2
. Now let
f=F_{\alpha}
and pick
j\geq\ell(\alpha)
. The specialization
\operatorname{sp}:i\mapsto(i)_{q}
in Theorem
6.2
gives immediately

(6.9)

\displaystyle F_{\alpha}(1,q,\dots,q^{j-1})=q^{N(\alpha)}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{j-\ell(\alpha)+r}{r}_{q}.

Multiplying by
t^{j}
and summing over all
j
, and then appealing to (
2.3
), we get

(6.10)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 1}F_{\alpha}(1,q,\dots,q^{j-1})t^{j}=\frac{t^{\ell(\alpha)}q^{N(\alpha)}}{(t;q)_{n}}.

This last formula is well-known in the theory of quasisymmetric functions and
(P,\omega)
-partitions, cf.
[
19
, Eq. (2.1)]
of which it is a special case.
The right-hand side is clearly
\frac{t^{\ell(\alpha)}\big\langle{F_{\alpha}(x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell(\alpha)})}\big\rangle_{n}^{q}}{(t;q)_{n}}
. Linearity implies the claim for general
f
.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

The reader should compare our proof of Corollary
6.3
with the proof in the case
q=1

[
33
, Proposition 4.1]
. The latter relies upon the (slightly involved) exact computation of the divided symmetrization of monomial quasisymmetric polynomials.
It turns out that the qDS of monomial quasisymmetric polynomials is not as nice or easy.
We do not currently know of ways to arrive at Corollary
6.3
outside of the one that we employ — identify (and subsequently leverage) the link between qDS and coefficient extraction in the Klyachko algebra.

Remark 6.5
.

For a large class of quasisymmetric functions arising ‘naturally’ as quasisymmetric functions of posets in the theory of P-partitions, our previous result allows us to assign a combinatorial meaning to their qDS generalizing that in
[
33
, §4.3]
.

6.3.
The work of Aval–Bergeron–Bergeron

Recall that
\mathrm{QSym}_{n}^{+}\subset\mathbf{k}[\mathbf{x}_{n}]
is the ideal generated by quasisymmetric polynomials without constant term.
We claim that
\big\langle{f}\big\rangle_{n}^{q}
is equal to
0
if
f\in\mathrm{QSym}_{n}^{+}
has degree
n-1
.
In fact we have the following stronger statement.

Corollary 6.6
.

For any polynomial
f
in
\mathrm{QSym}_{n}^{+}
, we have

\rho_{n}(f)=0.

In particular, if
f
has degree
n-1
then
\big\langle{f}\big\rangle_{n}^{q}=0
.

Proof.

Since
\rho_{n}
is an algebra morphism, it suffices to verify this for a quasisymmetric polynomial
f
, and even for
f=F_{\alpha}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
for some nonempty strong composition
\alpha
. Setting
m=n
in the statement of Theorem
6.2
immediately implies the first claim. The second claim then follows immediately from Theorem
4.11
.
∎

Since
\rho_{n}
vanishes on
\mathrm{QSym}_{n}^{+}
, one is naturally lead to consider the quotient
\mathbb{Q}[\mathbf{x}_{n}]/\mathrm{QSym}_{n}^{+}
.
A monomial basis for this quotient was computed in Aval–Bergeron–Bergeron
[
4
, Theorem 4.1]
and fortunately it fits nicely in our context.

Consider the set

\mathcal{A}_{n}=\{(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\;|\;\sum_{i\leq j\leq n}c_{j}\leq n-i\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq n\}.

Let
L_{n}=\mathrm{Vect}\left(\mathbf{x}^{c}\;|\;{c}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}\right)
be the linear span of the corresponding monomials.
For instance, if
n=3
, then
\mathcal{A}_{3}=\{(0,0,0),(1,0,0),(0,1,0),(1,1,0),(2,0,0)\}
and therefore
\{1,x_{1},x_{2},x_{1}x_{2},x_{1}^{2}\}
is a basis for
L_{3}
.
The results in
[
4
]
imply the vector space decomposition

(6.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}[\mathbf{x}_{n}]=L_{n}\oplus\mathrm{QSym}_{n}^{+}.

Theorem 6.7
.

If
f\in\mathbf{k}[\mathbf{x}_{n}]
, homogeneous of degree
n-1
, is written
f=g+h
with
g\in L_{n}
and
h\in\mathrm{QSym}_{n}^{+}
, then

\big\langle{f}\big\rangle_{n}^{q}=g(1,q,q^{2},\dots,q^{n-1}).

Proof.

Let
g
and
h
be as in the statement of the theorem.
We have that
\big\langle{h}\big\rangle_{n}^{q}=0
by Corollary
6.6
. So we consider
\big\langle{g}\big\rangle_{n}^{q}
. By linearity it suffices to consider
\big\langle{\mathbf{x}^{c}}\big\rangle_{n}^{q}
for
c\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
satisfying
|c|=n-1
. We use Proposition
4.3
.
Note that
\sum_{1\leq j\leq i}c_{i}\geq i
for
i\in[n]
, which means that the Łukasiewicz path
P_{c}
drops below the
x
-axis only in its final step, and thus the descent set
S_{c}
is empty. It follows that

(6.12)

\displaystyle\big\langle{\mathbf{x}^{c}}\big\rangle_{n}^{q}=q^{N(c)}=q^{\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}(i-1)c_{i}}.

The claim now follows.
∎

In words, qDS on the degree
n-1
component of
\mathbf{k}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
may be obtained by first projecting to the first factor in (
6.11
) and then computing the principal specialization (sending
x_{i}
to
q^{i-1}
) of the resulting polynomial.

Example 6.8
.

From Example
4.4
, we know
\big\langle{x_{1}x_{3}}\big\rangle_{3}^{q}=-(1+q)
. We can arrive at the same result differently by noting that

x_{1}x_{3}=x_{1}(x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3})-(x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}).

Since
x_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}^{2}\in L_{3}
and
x_{1}(x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3})\in\mathrm{QSym}_{3}^{+}
Theorem
6.7
tells us that
\big\langle{x_{1}x_{3}}\big\rangle_{3}^{q}=g(1,q,q^{2})
where
g=-x_{1}x_{2}-x_{1}^{2}
.

We close this subsection by recording a corollary that naturally leads us to looking at the coinvariant algebra closely.

Corollary 6.9
.

The map
\rho
induces a morphism from the coinvariant algebra
\mathbf{k}[\mathbf{x}_{n}]/\mathrm{Sym}_{n}^{+}
to
\mathcal{K}_{n}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}^{+}\subset\mathrm{QSym}_{n}^{+}
, and
\rho_{n}
vanishes on
\mathrm{QSym}_{n}^{+}
by Corollary
6.6
, the claim follows.
∎

This morphism for
q=1
coincides with the pullback homomorphism
\iota^{*}
between the cohomology ring of the flag variety and the
\mathbb{S}_{n}
-invariant part of the cohomology ring of the permutahedral variety, as detailed in the introduction. A geometric interpretation for generic
q
, or any other value of
q
, is not known to the authors.

7.
Klyachko–Macdonald identity and Chow class of a Deligne–Lusztig variety

We are now ready to return to our primary motivation mentioned in the introduction, which was to understand the relationship between the two following formulas for
w\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
of length
\ell
, and
\mathfrak{S}_{w}
is the corresponding Schubert polynomial (cf. §§
2.4
).

(7.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{w}(u_{1},u_{2}-u_{1},\dots,)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathrm{Red}(w)}u_{a_{1}}u_{a_{2}}\cdots u_{a_{\ell}}\quad\textup{ in }\mathcal{K}^{1}_{n}\simeq H^{*}(\mathrm{Perm}_{n},\mathbb{Q})^{\mathbb{S}_{n}}.

(7.2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{w}(1,\dots,1))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathrm{Red}(w)}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{\ell}.

The first identity is Klyachko’s identity (for type
A
) first stated in
[
24
]
and proved in
[
25
]
; since the latter is in Russian, the authors included a slightly simplified version of the proof in
[
34
, §8]
. The second identity is Macdonald’s reduced word identity
[
30
]
.

Before we deal with the
q
-version and prove Theorem
1.4
which encompasses both identities, let us explain that it is possible to deduce (
7.2
) from (
7.1
). It is not as straightforward as specializing
u_{i}\mapsto 1
in (
7.1
), since the identity holds in
\mathcal{K}_{n}^{1}
and not in
\mathcal{K}^{1}
or
\mathcal{K}_{+}^{1}
, cf. §
3.4
. In fact both sides in (
7.1
) vanish for
\ell\geq n
since the top graded dimension of
\mathcal{K}_{n}^{1}
is
n-1
.
It should not come as a surprise since Klyachko’s identity actually holds for any representative of the Schubert classes. Now Schubert polynomials have the important stability property under the inclusions
\mathbb{S}_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n+1}
, and are thus defined for
w\in\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
. So pick
w\in\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
, and let
n_{0}
be such that
w\in\mathbb{S}_{n_{0}}
. Then (
7.1
) is valid for all
n\geq n_{0}
, and thus holds in
\mathcal{K}^{1}_{+}
. The specialization
u_{i}\mapsto 1
is now possible and thus we obtain (
7.2
).

The methodology of proof of
(
7.1
)
cannot be simply extended to the
q
-case: it is based on the theory of root systems, and it is not clear how the parameter
q
fits into this story. Also the inductive nature of this proof does not mix well with the
\mathrm{comaj}
statistic. We will instead prove Theorem
1.4
using the Fomin-Stanley approach to the
q
-Macdonald identity. The subsequent remainder of this section involves applications to certain coefficients
a_{w}(q)
related to a particular Deligne–Lusztig variety.

7.1.
A
q
-Klyachko–Macdonald identity

We define the
NilCoxeter algebra

NC_{n}
as the free associative algebra over
\mathbb{C}
generated by
v_{1},\dots,v_{n-1}
subject to
v_{i}^{2}=0
,
v_{i}v_{j}=v_{j}v_{i}
for
|i-j|\geq 2
and
v_{i}v_{i+1}v_{i}=v_{i+1}v_{i}v_{i+1}
for
1\leq i\leq n-2
. Given these relations, it follows that
NC_{n}
has a linear basis comprising of elements
v_{w}
indexed by

. The NilCoxeter algebra plays a crucial role in Fomin and Stanley’s proof of the

In

Proposition 7.11

For

The proof is similar to that presented in

Remark 7.12

Two classes of permutations in

A permutation is dominant if it is

A Coxeter element in

through

A permutation is said to be

Note that together with the position of the first descent, the shape allows us to reconstruct a unique Grassmannian permutation interpreted as an element of

Given a partition

denote the set of standard Young tableaux of shape

Proposition 7.13
.

Let

Then we have

a_{w}(q)=\sum_{T\in\mathrm{SYT}(\lambda(w),m-1)}q^{\mathrm{maj}(T)}.

We follow the approach in
[

, Proposition]
using our results from §

Set
\lambda\coloneqq\lambda(w)
.
We infer that

(7.16)

\displaystyle a_{w}(q)=\sum_{T\in\mathrm{SYT}(\lambda,m-1)}q^{(m-1)(n-1)-\mathrm{maj}(T)}.

Recall now that Schützenberger’s evacuation map applied to

\mathrm{maj}(T)+\mathrm{maj}(\mathrm{evac}(T))=(m-1)(n-1)
