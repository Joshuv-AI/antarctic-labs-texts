1.
Introduction

One of the fundamental differences between orthogonal polynomials in one variable and several variables
lies in the multiplicity of orthogonal bases. Let
L^{2}(\Omega,W)
be a weighted
L^{2}
space on the domain

\Omega
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. For
n=0,1,2,\ldots
, let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}
denote the space of orthogonal polynomials of
degree
n
in
L^{2}(\Omega,W)
, which consists of polynomials of degree
n
that are orthogonal to all
polynomials of lower degrees for the inner product of
L^{2}(\Omega,W)
. For
d=1
, the space

{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}
is one dimension, so the orthogonal polynomial of degree
n
is unique up to a constant
multiple. For
d>1
, the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}
has the dimension
\binom{n+d-1}{n}
and it has infinitely many
distinct bases. Different bases are often needed for different occasions or applications, and each basis may
catch a specific perspective of the orthogonal structure. One could choose an orthonormal basis and there
are infinitely many of those. There are also outstanding bases that may not be mutually orthogonal.

Among the plethora of choices, we single out two that are not orthogonal but are biorthogonal with each other.
The first basis consists of monomial polynomials

V_{\mathbf{k}}(x)=x^{{\mathbf{k}}}+R_{\mathbf{k}}(x),\qquad{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d},\quad R_{\mathbf{k}}\in\Pi_{n-1}^{d},

where
\Pi_{n}^{d}
denotes the space of polynomials of degree
n
in
d
variables, which is uniquely
determined by the requirement that
\{V_{{\mathbf{k}}}:|{\mathbf{k}}|=n\}
is a basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}
. This basis
is not necessarily an orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}
. One of the important features of this basis is that

V_{\mathbf{k}}
has the least
L^{2}
norm among all polynomials of the form
x^{\mathbf{k}}-P
,
P\in\Pi_{n-1}^{d}
;
more precisely,

(1.1)

\inf_{P\in\Pi_{n-1}^{d}}\left\|x^{\mathbf{k}}-P\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,W)}=\left\|V_{\mathbf{k}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,W)}.

The second basis consists of polynomials
U_{\mathbf{k}}
,
|{\mathbf{k}}|=n
, defined by the Rodrigues type formulas that
involve repeated differentiation, which can only be defined for special weight functions and domains. The
study of such bases was initiated for classical orthogonal polynomials on the unit disk and the unit ball
{\mathbb{B}}^{d}

by Hermite and his contemporaries (see, for example,
[
3
]
), where the orthogonality is defined for the weight
function
(1-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}
,
\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
, on
{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
. For the unit ball, these two families of polynomials
can be given explicitly, satisfy elegant generating functions, and turn out to be mutually
biorthogonal
[
3
,
7
,
8
]
(see Section 2.2 below).

In the present paper, we study orthogonal polynomials on the conic domain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}=\{(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}:\,\|x\|\leq t,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,t\geq 0\},

on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
and the orthogonality is defined with respect to the weight function

W(x,t)=w(t)(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\mu>-\tfrac{1}{2},

where
w
is a weight function defined on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}=[0,\infty)
and the cone could be regarded as finite if
w

is supported on the finite interval, say,
[0,1]
. Orthogonal polynomials on the cone are studied only recently.
The two most important cases are the Laguerre polynomials on the cone, with
w(t)=t^{\beta}{\mathrm{e}}^{-t}
, and the
Jacobi polynomials on the cone, with
w(t)=t^{\beta}(1-t)^{\gamma}
. In these cases, an orthonormal basis is defined
in
[
15
]
and used to show that orthogonal polynomials in
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d+1}
are eigenfunctions of a second-order
differential operator and they satisfy an addition formula. The latter gives a closed-form formula for the
reproducing kernel of the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d+1}
, which provides essential tools for an extensive study on
approximation theory and computational harmonic analysis over the cone
[
16
,
17
]
. The study of the
orthogonal structure over the cone is still
in its beginning, the present paper aims to explore the monomial basis and the basis defined by the Rodrigues formulas.

Our main work lies in understanding the monomial basis. In the variable
(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
, such a basis
takes of the form

{\mathsf{V}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}(x,t)=t^{n-|{\mathbf{k}}|}x^{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathsf{P}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}\quad\hbox{with}\quad{\mathsf{P}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}\in\Pi_{n-1}^{d+1}.

Given the nature of the weight function, one may expect that the polynomial
{\mathsf{V}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
can be constructed
simply in terms of the monomial polynomials
V_{\mathbf{k}}
on the unit ball and orthogonal polynomial of one
variable in the
t
variable. The problem, however, turns out to be more subtle, since
V_{\mathbf{k}}
can only appear in the form of
t^{|{\mathbf{k}}|}V_{{\mathbf{k}}}(\frac{x}{t})
, which however is a homogeneous polynomial, far from a
monomial. Even the case
d=1
, or
{\mathbb{V}}^{2}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, turns out to be non-trivial. One of our main results provides
an explicit formula for the polynomials
{\mathsf{V}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
and its
L^{2}
norm. For the Laguerre and the Jacobi cases,
we also define the second family of polynomials, denoted by
{\mathsf{U}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
, via the Rodrigues type formulas.
The polynomials
{\mathsf{U}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
for
|{\mathbf{k}}|=n
is also a basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d+1}
and they satisfy a generating
function. The two families,
\{{\mathsf{V}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}\}
and
\{{\mathsf{U}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}\}
, are, however, only partially biorthogonal, in
contrast to the case of the unit ball.

The paper is organized as follows. The next section is preliminary, where we recall the results of classical
orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball and develop orthogonal polynomials on the cone. The monomial
polynomials on the cone, called the
V
-family, are defined and studied in the third section, where we first
consider the case
d=1
to illustrate the idea. The second family of polynomials, the
U
-family, is defined
via Rodrigues formulas in the fourth section and shown to be partially biorthogonal to the
V
-family and, in

d=1
case, a basis that is biorthogonal to the
V
-family is explicitly constructed in terms of the
U
-family.
Finally, we discuss generating functions for these polynomials in the fifth section.

2.
Preliminary

In the first subsection, we discuss orthogonal polynomials, especially monomial orthogonal polynomials, in
general. In the second section, we illustrate the general setup by recalling results from classical orthogonal
polynomials on the unit ball, which will also be needed in our study. In the last subsection, we lay down
the basics for orthogonal polynomials on the cone.

Throughout this paper, we use multi-index notation: for
{\mathbf{k}}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
and

{\mathbf{a}}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we shall write, for example,

{\mathbf{k}}!=k_{1}!\cdots k_{d}!,\qquad({\mathbf{a}})_{\mathbf{k}}=(a_{1})_{k_{1}}\cdots(a_{d})_{k_{d}},\qquad\sum_{{\mathbf{j}}\leq{\mathbf{k}}}A_{\mathbf{j}}=\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{k_{1}}\cdots\sum_{j_{d}=0}^{k_{d}}A_{{\mathbf{j}}},

where
(a)_{k}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+k-1)
denotes the Pochhammer symbol.

2.1.
Monomial orthogonal polynomials

Let
{\mathsf{w}}
be a weight function on a domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Let
{\langle}\cdot,\cdot{\rangle}_{{\mathsf{w}}}
be the inner product defined by

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{{\mathsf{w}}}=\int_{\Omega}f(x)g(x){\mathsf{w}}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

Let
\Pi_{n}^{d}
denote the space of polynomials of degree at most
n
in
d
variables. A polynomial
P
of degree
n

is an orthogonal polynomial with respect to the inner product if

{\langle}P,Q{\rangle}_{{\mathsf{w}}}=0,\qquad\forall Q\in\Pi_{n-1}^{d}.

Assume that
{\mathsf{w}}
is regular so that orthogonal polynomials with respect to the inner product are well-defined.
Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
be the space of orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
in
d
variables with respect
to the inner product. It is known that

r_{n}^{d}:=\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})=\binom{n+d-1}{n},\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.

For
d>1
, the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
contains infinitely many
bases. Moreover, since orthogonality is defined as orthogonal to all polynomials of lower degrees, elements of a
basis may not be mutually orthogonal. A basis
\{P_{j}^{n}:1\leq j\leq r_{n}^{d}\}
of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})

is called an orthogonal basis if
{\langle}P_{j}^{n},P_{k}^{n}{\rangle}_{\mathsf{w}}=0
whenever
j\neq k
and it is called an orthonormal
basis if, in addition,
{\langle}P_{j}^{n},P_{j}^{n}{\rangle}_{\mathsf{w}}=1
for all
j
. It is often convenient to consider an orthonormal basis
for many applications, such as dealing with the Fourier orthogonal series. Notice, however, that if

{\mathbb{P}}_{n}=\{P_{j}^{n}:1\leq j\leq r_{n}^{d}\}
is an orthonormal basis, then so is
Q{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
if
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
is regarded as
a column vector and
Q
is an orthogonal matrix of size
\binom{n+d-1}{n}
. Hence, there are infinitely
many distinct orthonormal bases. Our goal in this paper is to consider the monomial basis

\{V_{\mathbf{k}}:|{\mathbf{k}}|=n,{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}\}
, where
V_{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
is characterized by

V_{\mathbf{k}}(x)=x^{\mathbf{k}}+P_{\mathbf{k}},\qquad P_{\mathbf{k}}\in\Pi_{n-1},\quad n=|{\mathbf{k}}|;

in other words, the polynomial
V_{\mathbf{k}}
of degree
n
contains a single monomial,
x^{\mathbf{k}}
, of degree
n
.
The monomial orthogonal polynomial
V_{\mathbf{k}}
is the error of the least square approximation to the monomial
x^{\mathbf{k}}

from
\Pi_{|{\mathbf{k}}|-1}^{d}
; more precisely, it satisfies (
1.1
) since
V_{\mathbf{k}}=x^{\mathbf{k}}+P_{{\mathbf{k}}}
is
orthogonal to all polynomials in
\Pi_{n-1}^{d}
. Moreover, in the case of several families of classical orthogonal polynomials,
such as those on the unit ball or the standard simplex, the monomial orthogonal polynomials possess fine
structures and are biorthogonal to another family of orthogonal polynomials defined by the Rodrigues type
formulas. We illustrate this in the next subsection.

2.2.
Orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball

For
\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
, the classical weight function on the unit ball
{\mathbb{B}}^{d}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\|x\|\leq 1\}
of
{\mathbb{R}}
is

\displaystyle{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu}(x)=(1-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}},\quad\mu>-\tfrac{1}{2},\quad x\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d},

the normalization constant
b_{\mu}^{\mathbb{B}}
, so that
b_{\mu}^{\mathbb{B}}{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu}(x)
has the unit integral, is given by

b_{\mu}^{\mathbb{B}}=\frac{1}{\int_{{\mathbb{B}}^{d}}{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu}(x)\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{\Gamma(\mu+\frac{d+1}{2})}{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}\Gamma(\mu+\frac{1}{2})}.

When
d=1
, the ball is
[-1,1]
and the associated orthogonal polynomials are the classical Gegenbauer
polynomials
C_{n}^{\mu}
, which satisfy the orthogonal relation

(2.1)

c_{\mu}\int_{-1}^{1}C_{n}^{\mu}(t)C_{m}^{\mu}(t)(1-t^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}t=h_{n}^{\mu}\delta_{n,m},\qquad h_{n}^{\mu}=\frac{\mu(2\mu)_{n}}{(n+\mu)n!},

where
c_{\mu}=\frac{\Gamma(\mu+1)}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\mu+\frac{1}{2})}
is the same as
b_{\mu}^{{\mathbb{B}}}
with
d=1

and
\mu\neq 0
. If
\mu=0
, the polynomial
C_{n}^{\mu}
becomes the Chebyshev polynomial
T_{n}
under the limit

\lim_{\mu\to 0}\frac{1}{\mu}C_{n}^{\mu}(x)=\frac{2}{n}T_{n}(x),\qquad n\geq 1.

For
d>1
, the orthogonal polynomials with respect to
{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu}
on
{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
are well studied; see
[
7
, Section 5.2]
. Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu})
be the space of OPs for this weight function. There are
many distinct bases for this space and two orthonormal bases can be given explicitly in either Cartesian or polar
coordinates. We are mostly interested in the monomial basis and the basis defined by the Rodrigues formula. While
the former has been discussed before, the latter is an extension of the Rodrigues formula for classical orthogonal
polynomials in one variable to several variables; see, for instance,

[
3
,
4
,
7
,
8
,
10
,
14
]
, for some examples.
For the unit ball, both bases can be traced back to the work of Hermite
[
3
]
. Following the classical presentation (cf.
[
8
, Chapter 12]
and
[
7
, Chapter 5]
), we denote the elements of these
two bases by
V_{\mathbf{k}}
and
U_{\mathbf{k}}
with
{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
, respectively.

We consider the
U
-basis given by the Rodrigues formula first. For each
{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
, the polynomials

U_{\mathbf{k}}
is defined by

(2.2)

U_{{\mathbf{k}}}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{|{\mathbf{k}}|}(2\mu)_{|{\mathbf{k}}|}}{2^{|{\mathbf{k}}|}(\mu+\frac{1}{2})_{|{\mathbf{k}}|}{\mathbf{k}}!}\frac{1}{(1-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}}\frac{\partial^{|{\mathbf{k}}|}}{\partial_{1}^{{\mathbf{k}}_{1}}\cdots\partial_{d}^{{\mathbf{k}}_{d}}}(1-\|x\|^{2})^{|{\mathbf{k}}|+\mu-\frac{1}{2}}.

Then
\{U_{\mathbf{k}}:|{\mathbf{k}}|=n\}
is a basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu})
. These polynomials also satisfy a
generating function given by

(2.3)

\frac{1}{\left((1-{\langle}{\mathbf{a}},x{\rangle})^{2}+\|{\mathbf{a}}\|^{2}(1-\|x\|^{2})\right)^{\mu}}=\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}}U_{\mathbf{k}}(x){\mathbf{a}}^{\mathbf{k}},\quad{\mathbf{a}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d}.

The
V
-basis is the monomial basis, For each
{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
, the polynomial
V_{\mathbf{k}}
is defined by

(2.4)

V_{{\mathbf{k}}}(x)=x^{\mathbf{k}}F_{B}\left(-\frac{{\mathbf{k}}}{2},\frac{\mathbf{1}-{\mathbf{k}}}{2};-|{\mathbf{k}}|-\mu-\frac{d-3}{2};\frac{1}{x_{1}^{2}},\ldots,\frac{1}{x_{d}^{2}}\right),

where
\mathbf{1}=(1,\ldots,1)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
and
F_{B}
is the Lauricella hypergeometric series of
d
variables
defined by

\displaystyle F_{B}({\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}};c;x)=\sum_{{\mathbf{m}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}}\frac{({\mathbf{a}})_{\mathbf{m}}({\mathbf{b}})_{\mathbf{m}}}{(c)_{|{\mathbf{m}}|}{\mathbf{m}}!}x^{\mathbf{m}},\qquad\max_{1\leq j\leq d}|x_{j}|<1.

It is easy to see that
V_{\mathbf{k}}(x)=x^{\mathbf{k}}+Q_{\mathbf{k}}
with
Q_{\mathbf{k}}\in\Pi_{n-1}^{d}
, so that
V_{\mathbf{k}}
is monomial for

{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
. Moreover,
\{V_{\mathbf{k}}:|{\mathbf{k}}|=n\}
is a basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{|{\mathbf{k}}|}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu})
. These polynomials
satisfy a generating function given by

(2.5)

\frac{1}{\left(1-2{\langle}{\mathbf{a}},x{\rangle}+\|{\mathbf{a}}\|^{2}\right)^{\mu+\frac{d-1}{2}}}=\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}}\frac{2^{|{\mathbf{k}}|}}{{\mathbf{k}}!}\left(\mu+\frac{d-1}{2}\right)_{|{\mathbf{k}}|}V_{\mathbf{k}}(x){\mathbf{a}}^{\mathbf{k}},\quad{\mathbf{a}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d}.

As we mentioned before, the polynomial
V_{\mathbf{k}}
has the least
L^{2}
norm among all polynomials of the form

x^{\mathbf{k}}-P(x)
,
P\in\Pi_{n-1}^{d}
. Its norm is computed in
[
13
, Corollary 4.8]
, which shows in particular
that, for
{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
and
|{\mathbf{k}}|=n
,

(2.6)

\inf_{P\in\Pi_{n-1}^{d}}\left\|x^{\mathbf{k}}-P\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu})}^{2}=\frac{{\lambda}{\mathbf{k}}!}{2^{n-1}({\lambda})_{n}}\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{i=1}^{d}P_{k_{i}}(t)t^{n+2{\lambda}-1}\mathrm{d}t,

where
{\lambda}=\mu+\frac{d-1}{2}>0
and
P_{n}
is the Legendre polynomial of degree
n
, which implies,
in particular, that the integral on the right-hand side is positive, a fact that is not obvious. For
d=2
,
a different expression for this quantity is given in
[
5
]
. For small
n
, the least square of some
symmetric monomials are given in
[
2
]
, which can be deduced from the norm of
V_{{\mathbf{k}}}
, see
[
13
]
.
Orthogonal polynomials are also used in deriving the exact constant in the Bernstein inequality on the ball in the recent work of
[
9
]
.

Neither
U
-basis nor
V
-basis are orthogonal bases for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu})
. They are, however, biorthogonal
with respect to each other. More precisely, they satisfy

b_{\mu}^{\mathbb{B}}\int_{{\mathbb{B}}^{d}}U_{\mathbf{k}}(x)V_{\mathbf{j}}(x){\mathsf{w}}_{\mu}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\frac{(2\mu)_{|{\mathbf{k}}|}}{2^{|{\mathbf{k}}|}(\mu+\frac{d+1}{2})_{|{\mathbf{k}}|}}\delta_{{\mathbf{j}},{\mathbf{k}}}.

We will need the explicit expression of
V_{\mathbf{k}}(x)
in monomials, which is derived by rewriting (
2.4
)
in the following form

(2.7)

V_{{\mathbf{k}}}(x)=\sum_{2{\mathbf{j}}\leq{\mathbf{k}}}b_{{\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{j}}}^{\mu}x^{{\mathbf{k}}-2{\mathbf{j}}},\quad\hbox{where}\quad b_{{\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{j}}}^{\mu}:=\frac{(\frac{-{\mathbf{k}}}{2})_{{\mathbf{j}}}(\frac{-{\mathbf{k}}+1}{2})_{{\mathbf{j}}}}{(-|{\mathbf{k}}|-\lambda+1)_{|{\mathbf{j}}|}{\mathbf{j}}!},

and we shall denote, throughout the rest of the paper,

{\lambda}=\mu+\frac{d-1}{2}.

The relation (
2.7
) can be reversed, as seen in
[
13
, Corollary 6.2]
, as

(2.8)

x^{{\mathbf{k}}}=\sum_{2{\mathbf{j}}\leq{\mathbf{k}}}c_{{\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{j}}}^{\mu}V_{{\mathbf{k}}-2{\mathbf{j}}}(x),

where the coefficients are given by

(2.9)

c_{{\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{j}}}^{\mu}=\frac{(-1)^{|{\mathbf{j}}|}(-\frac{{\mathbf{k}}}{2})_{{\mathbf{j}}}(\frac{-{\mathbf{k}}+\mathbf{1}}{2})_{{\mathbf{j}}}}{(-|{\mathbf{k}}|-\lambda+1)_{2|{\mathbf{j}}|}\,{\mathbf{j}}!}\left(2(-|{\mathbf{k}}|-\lambda+1)_{|{\mathbf{j}}|}-(-|{\mathbf{k}}|-\lambda)_{|{\mathbf{j}}|}\right).

Putting the two identities together, we obtain

\displaystyle x^{{\mathbf{k}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{2{\mathbf{i}}\leq{\mathbf{k}}}c_{{\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{i}}}^{\mu}V_{{\mathbf{k}}-2{\mathbf{i}}}(x)=\sum_{2{\mathbf{i}}\leq{\mathbf{k}}}c_{{\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{i}}}^{\mu}\sum_{2{\mathbf{j}}\leq{\mathbf{k}}-2{\mathbf{i}}}b_{{\mathbf{k}}-2{\mathbf{i}},{\mathbf{j}}}^{\mu}x^{{\mathbf{k}}-2{\mathbf{i}}-2{\mathbf{j}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{2{\mathbf{i}}\leq{\mathbf{k}}}c_{{\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{i}}}^{\mu}\sum_{2{\mathbf{j}}\leq{\mathbf{k}}}b_{{\mathbf{k}}-2{\mathbf{i}},{\mathbf{j}}-{\mathbf{i}}}^{\mu}x^{{\mathbf{k}}-2{\mathbf{j}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{2{\mathbf{j}}\leq{\mathbf{k}}}x^{{\mathbf{k}}-2{\mathbf{j}}}\sum_{{\mathbf{i}}\leq{\mathbf{j}}}c_{{\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{i}}}^{\mu}b_{{\mathbf{k}}-2{\mathbf{i}},{\mathbf{j}}-{\mathbf{i}}}^{\mu}

Consequently, it follows readily that

(2.10)

\sum_{{\mathbf{i}}\leq{\mathbf{j}}}b_{{\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{i}}}^{\mu}c_{{\mathbf{k}}-2{\mathbf{i}},{\mathbf{j}}-{\mathbf{i}}}^{\mu}=\delta_{{\mathbf{j}},0},\quad j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

This identity will be used in the sequel.

2.3.
Orthogonal polynomials on the cone

Let
w
be a weight function on an interval in
{\mathbb{R}}
. We can assume that the interval is either
[0,1]
or

{\mathbb{R}}_{+}=[0,\infty)
without losing generality. On the cone
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
, we define

(2.11)

W_{\mu}(x,t)=w(t)(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}},\quad\mu>-\tfrac{1}{2},

and define the inner product

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{\mu}:=b_{\mu}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}f(x,t)g(x,t)W_{\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t,

where
b_{\mu}
is a normalization constant so that
{\langle}1,1{\rangle}_{\mu}=1
and

b_{\mu}=b_{\mu}^{\mathbb{B}}\times b_{\mu}^{w}\quad\hbox{with}\quad b_{\mu}^{w}=\frac{1}{\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{d+2\mu-1}w(t)\mathrm{d}t}.

This can be easily verified by using the separation of variables

\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}f(x,t)W_{\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{B}}^{d}}f(ty,t)(1-\|y\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}y\,t^{d+2\mu-1}w(t)\mathrm{d}t.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},W_{\mu})
be the space of orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
in
d+1
variables
with respect to the inner product
{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{\mu}
. A basis of this space can be given in terms of orthogonal
polynomials on the unit ball and a family of orthogonal polynomials in one variable.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
{\mathbb{P}}_{m}=\{P_{\mathbf{k}}:|{\mathbf{k}}|=m\}
be a basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{m}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu})
for
m\leq n
and let
q_{n-m}^{\alpha}

be an orthogonal polynomial in one variable with respect to the weight function
t^{\alpha}w(t)
on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Define

denoted by letters in the sans serif font, such as
{\mathsf{Q}}_{{\mathbf{k}}}
,
{\mathsf{S}}_{{\mathbf{k}}}
, and
{\mathsf{V}}_{{\mathbf{k}}}
.

(2.12)

{\mathsf{Q}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}(x,t)=q_{n-m}^{{\alpha}_{m}}(t)t^{m}P_{\mathbf{k}}\!\left(\frac{x}{t}\right),\qquad|{\mathbf{k}}|=m,\quad 0\leq m\leq n,

where
{\alpha}_{m}=d+2m+2\mu-1
. Then
{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}=\{{\mathsf{Q}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}:|{\mathbf{k}}|=m,\,0\leq m\leq n\}
is a basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},W_{\mu})
.
In particular, if
{\mathbb{P}}_{m}
is an orthogonal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{m}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu})
for
0\leq m\leq n
, then
{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}

is an orthogonal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},W_{\mu})
.

Proof.

This can be easily seen from

\displaystyle{\langle}{\mathsf{Q}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n},{\mathsf{Q}}_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime},n^{\prime}}{\rangle}_{\mu}

\displaystyle\phantom{olaola}=b_{\mu}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{d+m+m^{\prime}+2\mu-1}q_{n-m}^{{\alpha}_{m}}(t)q_{n^{\prime}-m^{\prime}}^{{\alpha}_{m^{\prime}}}(t)\int_{{\mathbb{B}}^{d}}P_{\mathbf{k}}^{m}(y)P_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}\!(y){\mathsf{w}}_{\mu}(y)\mathrm{d}yw(t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\phantom{olaola}=b_{\mu}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!q_{n-m}^{{\alpha}_{m}}(t)q_{n^{\prime}-m}^{{\alpha}_{m}}(t)t^{{\alpha}_{m}}w(t)\mathrm{d}t\!\int_{{\mathbb{B}}^{d}}\!P_{\mathbf{k}}^{m}(y)P_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}^{m}(y){\mathsf{w}}_{\mu}(y)\mathrm{d}y\,\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\phantom{olaola}={\|q_{n-m}^{{\alpha}_{m}}\|^{2}}b_{\mu}^{\mathbb{B}}\int_{{\mathbb{B}}^{d}}P_{\mathbf{k}}^{m}(y)P_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}^{m}(y){\mathsf{w}}_{\mu}(y)\mathrm{d}y\,\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}\delta_{n,n^{\prime}},

where we have used the orthogonality of
P_{\mathbf{k}}^{m}
. Moreover, if
{\mathbb{P}}_{m}
is an orthogonal basis,
then the last integral can be replaced by a constant multiple of
\delta_{{\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}
so that the basis
{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}

is an orthogonal basis on the cone.
∎

What we are interested in is the monomial basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},W_{\mu})
. Monomials of degree
n

in
(x,t)
variables are
t^{n-|{\mathbf{k}}|}x^{\mathbf{k}}
for
{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
and
0\leq|{\mathbf{k}}|\leq n
. We assume that the
monomial orthogonal polynomial
{\mathsf{V}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
is of the form

(2.13)

{\mathsf{V}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}(x,t)=t^{n-|{\mathbf{k}}|}x^{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathsf{R}}_{{\mathbf{k}}}(x,t),\qquad{\mathsf{R}}_{\mathbf{k}}\in\Pi_{n-1}^{d}.

Given the Proposition
2.1
, it is tempting to consider orthogonal polynomials

(2.14)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{S}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}(x,t):=q_{n-m}^{{\alpha}_{m}}(t)t^{m}V_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right),\quad|{\mathbf{k}}|=m,\,\,{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d},\,\,0\leq m\leq n,

where
V_{\mathbf{k}}
denotes the monomial orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball, defined in (
2.4
),
and
q_{n-m}^{{\alpha}_{m}}
is the monic orthogonal polynomial in the
t
variable. However, although both

q_{n-m}^{{\alpha}_{m}}
and
V_{\mathbf{k}}
are monomial polynomials, the polynomials
{\mathsf{S}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
are monomial only when

{\mathbf{k}}=0
since the polynomial
t^{|{\mathbf{k}}|}V_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
m

in the
(x,t)
variables when
|{\mathbf{k}}|>0
, hence not monomial. The polynomials
{\mathsf{S}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
nevertheless play
an important role in our construction of the monomial basis in the next section.

For our study, the most interesting cases are when
w
is a classical weight function, for which
q_{n-m}^{{\alpha}_{m}}

can be given explicitly in classical orthogonal polynomials. This comes down to two families of classical weight
functions, which lead to the Laguerre polynomials on the cone and the Jacobi polynomials on the cone studied
in
[
15
]
. We review these two families of polynomials below.

2.3.1.
Laguerre polynomials on the cone

In this case, the cone is unbounded,

{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}=\left\{(x,t):\|x\|\leq t,\,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,\,0\leq t<\infty\right\},

and the weight function
w(t)=t^{\beta}{\mathrm{e}}^{-t}
, so that
W_{\mu}
in (
2.11
) becomes

W_{{\beta},\mu}(x,t)=(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}t^{\beta}{\mathrm{e}}^{-t},\quad\mu>-\tfrac{1}{2},\quad{\beta}>-d,

and its normalization constant becomes
b_{{\beta},\mu}^{L}
given by

b_{{\beta},\mu}^{L}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2\mu+{\beta}+d)}b_{\mu}^{\mathbb{B}}.

Recall that the Laguerre polynomial
L_{n}^{\alpha}
is defined by, for
{\alpha}>-1
,

L_{n}^{\alpha}(t)=\frac{({\alpha}+1)_{n}}{n!}{}_{1}F_{1}(-n;{\alpha}+1;t)=\frac{({\alpha}+1)_{n}}{n!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-n)_{k}}{({\alpha}+1)_{k}k!}t^{k},

and it satisfies the orthogonal relation

\frac{1}{\Gamma({\alpha}+1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}L_{n}^{\alpha}(t)L_{m}^{\alpha}(t)t^{\alpha}{\mathrm{e}}^{-t}\mathrm{d}t=\frac{({\alpha}+1)_{n}}{n!}\delta_{m,n}.

The orthogonal polynomials
{\mathsf{Q}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
in (
2.12
) on the cone are now given in terms of the
Laguerre polynomials, which we denote by
{\mathsf{L}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
and they are

{\mathsf{L}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}(x,t)=L_{n-m}^{2m+2\mu+{\beta}+d-1}(t)t^{m}P_{{\mathbf{k}}}^{m}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right),\quad|{\mathbf{k}}|=m,\,\,0\leq m\leq n.

2.3.2.
Jacobi polynomials on the cone

In this case, the cone is bounded,

{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}=\left\{(x,t):\|x\|\leq t,\,\,x\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d},\,\,0\leq t\leq 1\right\},

and the weight function
w(t)=t^{\beta}(1-t)^{\gamma}
with
{\beta}>-d
and
{\gamma}>-1
. Now
W_{\mu}
in (
2.11
) becomes

W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}(x,t)=(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}t^{\beta}(1-t)^{\gamma},\quad\mu>-\tfrac{1}{2},\,\,{\gamma}>-1,\,\,{\beta}>-d,

and its normalization constant becomes
b_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{J}
given by

(2.15)

b_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{J}=c_{2\mu+{\beta}+d-1,{\gamma}}b_{\mu}^{\mathbb{B}},\quad\hbox{with}\quad c_{{\alpha},{\gamma}}=\frac{\Gamma({\alpha}+{\gamma}+2)}{\Gamma({\alpha}+1)\Gamma({\gamma}+1)}.

Recall that the Jacobi polynomial
P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
is given by, for
{\alpha},{\beta}>-1
,

P_{n}^{{\alpha},{\beta}}(t)=\frac{({\alpha}+1)_{n}}{n!}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}-n,n+{\alpha}+{\beta}+1\\
{\alpha}+1\end{matrix};\frac{1-t}{2}\right),

in terms of the hypergeometric function and it satisfies the orthogonal relation

\frac{c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}}{2^{{\alpha}+{\beta}+1}}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)P_{m}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}\mathrm{d}t=h_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}\delta_{n,m},

where the norm square is given by

h_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}=\frac{({\alpha}+1)_{n}({\beta}+1)_{n}({\alpha}+{\beta}+n+1)}{n!({\alpha}+{\beta}+2)_{n}({\alpha}+{\beta}+2n+1)}.

The orthogonal polynomials
{\mathsf{Q}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
in (
2.12
) on the cone are given in terms of the Jacobi
polynomials, which we denote by
{\mathsf{J}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
and they are

{\mathsf{J}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}(x,t)=P_{n-m}^{(2m+2\mu+{\beta}+d-1,{\gamma})}(1-2t)t^{m}P_{{\mathbf{k}}}^{m}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right),\quad|{\mathbf{k}}|=m,\,\,0\leq m\leq n.

3.
Monomial orthogonal polynomials on the cone

This section aims to construct monomial orthogonal polynomials on the cone. To illustrate our construction,
we consider the case
d=1
in the first subsection and present our construction for
d>1
in the second
subsection for the setting of
W_{\mu}
with a generic weight
w
. We specialize the result
to the Laguerre and the Jacobi polynomials on the cone in the third subsection.

3.1.
Monomial orthogonal polynomials for
d=1

Our construction is based on the orthogonal polynomials
{\mathsf{S}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
defined in (
2.14
). To
illustrate our approach, we consider the case
d=1
first, for which

W_{\mu}(x,t)=w(t)(t^{2}-x^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}},\quad|x|\leq t,\quad\mu>-\tfrac{1}{2},

and we assume the polynomial
{\mathsf{S}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
takes the form

\displaystyle{\mathsf{S}}_{k,n}(x,t):=q_{n-k}^{2k+2\mu}(t)t^{k}C_{k}^{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right),\quad 0\leq k\leq n,

where
q_{n-k}^{2k+2\mu}(t)=t^{n-k}+\cdots
is a monic polynomial of degree
n-k
and it is orthogonal
with respect to
t^{2k+2\mu}w(t)
on
{\mathbb{R}}
, and we retain
C_{k}^{\mu}
instead of using the monic Gegenbauer
polynomial for convenience.

3.1.1.
The case of a generic weight
w

Here we construct monomial orthogonal polynomials for
W_{\mu}
with a generic function
w
.
The polynomial
{\mathsf{S}}_{0,n}(x,t)=q_{n}^{2\mu}(t)=t^{n}+\cdots
is a monomial orthogonal polynomial of degree
n

in the
(x,t)
variable. For
k>0
,
{\mathsf{S}}_{k,n}
is not monomial since the polynomial
t^{k}C_{k}^{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)

is a homogeneous polynomials of degree
k
in the
(x,t)
variables. For

k>0
, we use the expansion of the Gegenbauer polynomials given by
[
6
, (18.5.10)]

\displaystyle C_{n}^{\mu}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}b_{n,i}^{\mu}x^{n-2i}\quad\hbox{with}\quad b_{n,i}^{\mu}=(-1)^{i}2^{n-2i}\frac{(\mu)_{n-i}}{i!(n-2i)!},

which can also be reversed to give
[
6
, (18.18.17)]

\displaystyle x^{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}c_{k,j}^{\mu}C_{k-2j}^{\mu}(x)\quad\hbox{with}\quad c_{k,j}^{\mu}=\frac{k!}{2^{k}}\frac{k-2j+\mu}{(\mu)_{k-j+1}j!}.

The above formulas hold for
\mu\neq 0
and
\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
. If
\mu=0
, then
C_{n}^{\mu}
becomes the
Chebyshev polynomial
T_{n}
, for which the above relations become

\displaystyle x^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}c_{k,j}^{0}T_{k-2j}(x)\quad\hbox{with}\quad c_{k,j}^{0}=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\binom{k}{j}\begin{cases}2&k\neq 2j,\\
1&k=2j,\end{cases}

\displaystyle T_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}b_{n,i}^{0}x^{n-2i}\quad\hbox{with}\quad b_{n,i}^{0}=(-1)^{i}2^{n-2i-1}n\frac{(n-i-1)!}{i!(n-2i)!},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Theorem 3.1
.

For
0\leq k\leq n
, define

\displaystyle{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}(x,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}c_{k,j}^{\mu}{\mathsf{S}}_{k-2j,n}(x,t).

Then
{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}(x,t)=t^{n-k}x^{k}+\cdots
is a monomial polynomial of degree
n
and

\{{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}:0\leq k\leq n\}
is the monomial basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}^{2},W_{\mu})
.

Proof.

Since
q_{n-k+2j}^{2(k-2j)+2\mu}(t)-t^{n-k+2j}=R_{n-k+2j-1}(t)
, which is a polynomial of degree

n-k+2j-1
, it follows from the definition of
c_{k,j}
that

\displaystyle{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}(x,t)\,

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}c_{k,j}^{\mu}q_{n-k+2j}^{2(k-2j)+2\mu}(t)t^{k-2j}C_{k-2j}^{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)

\displaystyle=t^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}c_{k,j}^{\mu}C_{k-2j}^{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)+\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}c_{k,j}^{\mu}R_{n-k+2j-1}(t)t^{k-2j}C_{k-2j}^{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)

\displaystyle=t^{n-k}x^{k}+\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}c_{k,j}^{\mu}R_{n-k+2j-1}(t)t^{k-2j}C_{k-2j}^{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right).

Since the second term is evidently of degree at most
n-1
,
{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}
is monomial, and the set

\{{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}:0\leq k\leq n\}
is clearly independent. Moreover, as a linear combination of
{\mathsf{S}}_{j,n}
,

{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}
is an element of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}^{2},W_{\mu})
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

For
0\leq k\leq n
, the norm of
{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}
is given by

\displaystyle b_{\mu}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{2}}\left|{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}(x,t)\right|^{2}W_{\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}[c_{k,i}^{\mu}]^{2}h_{k-2i}^{\mu}\left\|q_{n-k+2i}^{2(k-2i)+2\mu}\right\|^{2},

where
h_{n-2i}^{\mu}
is given by (
2.1
) and it can be rewritten as

h_{k-2i}^{\mu}=\frac{(2\mu)_{k}\mu}{k!(k-2i+\mu)}\frac{(-\frac{k}{2})_{i}(\frac{-k+1}{2})_{i}}{(\frac{-k+1}{2}-\mu)_{i}(\frac{-k+2}{2}-\mu)_{i}},

and
\|q_{n-j}^{2j+2\mu}\|
is defined by

\left\|q_{n-j}^{2j+2\mu}\right\|^{2}=b_{\mu}^{w}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|q_{n-j}^{2j+2\mu}(t)\right|^{2}t^{2j+2\mu}w(t)\mathrm{d}t.

Proof.

For
d=1
, the polynomials
{\mathsf{S}}_{k,n}
are mutually orthogonal and, more precisely,

\displaystyle b_{\mu}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{2}}{\mathsf{S}}_{k,n}(x,t){\mathsf{S}}_{j,n}(x,t)W_{\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\phantom{ola}=\,b_{\mu}^{w}\int_{0}^{\infty}q_{n-k}^{2k+2\mu}(t)q_{n-j}^{2k+2\mu}(t)w(t)t^{k+j+2\mu}\mathrm{d}t\,c_{\mu}\int_{-1}^{1}C_{k}^{\mu}(x)C_{j}^{\mu}(x)(1-x^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\phantom{ola}=\,\left\|q_{n-k}^{2k+2\mu}\right\|^{2}h_{k}^{\mu}\delta_{k,j},

where
h_{k}^{\mu}
is given in (
2.1
). Hence, the stated result follows from

\displaystyle b_{\mu}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{2}}\left|{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}(x,t)\right|^{2}W_{\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\,

\displaystyle=b_{\mu}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{2}}\bigg|\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}c_{k,i}^{\mu}S_{k-2i,n}(x,t)\bigg|^{2}W_{\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}[c_{k,i}^{\mu}]^{2}b_{\mu}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{2}}\left|S_{k-2i,n}(x,t)\right|^{2}W_{\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

Finally, we use
(a)_{k-2i}={(a)_{k}}/{(1-a-k)_{2i}}
and
(x)_{2j}=2^{2j}(\frac{x}{2})_{j}(\frac{x+1}{2})_{j}
to rewrite
h_{k-2i}^{\mu}
into the given form.
∎

3.1.2.
The Laguerre polynomials on the cone

In this case, the weight function is
w(t)=t^{\beta}e^{-t}
and the polynomial
q_{n-k}^{2k+2\mu}
is given
by the monic Laguerre polynomial, denoted by
\widehat{L}_{n-k}^{\alpha}
; more precisely,

q_{n-k}^{2k+2\mu}(t)=\widehat{L}_{n-k}^{2k+2\mu+{\beta}}(t)=(-1)^{n-k}(n-k)!L_{n-k}^{2k+2\mu+{\beta}}(t),

so that the monomial polynomial
{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}^{\mathsf{L}}
is given by

{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}^{\mathsf{L}}(x,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}c_{k,j}^{\mu}{\mathsf{S}}_{k-2j,n}(x,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}c_{k,j}^{\mu}\widehat{L}_{n-k+2j}^{2(k-2j)+2\mu+{\beta}}(t)t^{k}C_{k}^{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right).

It is now easy to verify that

\displaystyle\left\|q_{n-k}^{2k+2\mu}\right\|^{2}\,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2\mu+{\beta}+1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\widehat{L}_{n-k}^{2k+2\mu+{\beta}}(t)\right|^{2}t^{2k+2\mu+{\beta}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-t}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=(n-k)!(2\mu+{\beta}+1)_{n+k}.

Hence, the norm of the monomial polynomial given in Proposition
3.2
can be specified. Thus,
we obtain

(3.1)

\displaystyle b_{{\beta},\mu}^{L}

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{2}}\left|{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}^{\mathsf{L}}(x,t)\right|^{2}W_{{\beta},\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}[c_{k,i}^{\mu}]^{2}h_{k-2i}^{\mu}(n-k+2i)!(2\mu+{\beta}+1)_{n+k-2i}.

The sum can be written as a sum of two hypergeometric functions
{}_{6}F_{5}
evaluated at
1
, which however
do not have a closed-form formula. It remains to be seen if the error can be expressed by a formula similar to
that of (
2.6
) on the cone. By (
1.1
), the right-hand side of the identity gives the explicit
formula for the error of the least square approximation to the monomial
t^{n-|{\mathbf{k}}|}x^{\mathbf{k}}
by lower degree
polynomials. Let

E_{n}(f)_{{\beta},\mu}=\inf_{P\in\Pi_{n-1}^{d+1}}\|f-{\mathsf{P}}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{V}}^{2},W_{{\beta},\mu})},

where the norm is defined by normalized weight function. Using (
3.1
), we can compute this
quantity when
f
is a monomial. Below we list the first few for small
k
:

\displaystyle\left[E_{n}(t^{n})_{{\beta},\mu}\right]^{2}\,

\displaystyle=n!({\beta}+2\mu+1)_{n};

\displaystyle\left[E_{n}(t^{n-1}x)_{{\beta},\mu}\right]^{2}\,

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-1)!({\beta}+2\mu+1)_{n+1}}{2(\mu+1)};

\displaystyle\left[E_{n}(t^{n-2}x^{2})_{{\beta},\mu}\right]^{2}\,

\displaystyle=\frac{n!({\beta}+2\mu+1)_{n}}{4(\mu+1)^{2}}+\frac{(2\mu+1)(n-2)!({\beta}+2\mu+1)_{n+2}}{4(\mu+1)^{2}(\mu+2)};

\displaystyle\left[E_{n}(t^{n-3}x^{3})_{{\beta},\mu}\right]^{2}\,

\displaystyle=\frac{9(n-1)!({\beta}+2\mu+1)_{n+1}}{8(\mu+1)(\mu+2)^{2}}+\frac{3(2\mu+1)(n-3)!({\beta}+2\mu+1)_{n+3}}{8(\mu+1)(\mu+2)^{2}(\mu+3)}.

It should be noted that, for
d=1
, a change of variables
(t,x)\mapsto(u,v)
given by

(3.2)

u=\frac{t+x}{2}\quad\hbox{and}\quad v=\frac{t-x}{2}

maps
{\mathbb{V}}^{2}
to
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{2}
and the weight function
t^{\beta}e^{-t}
to

W_{\beta}^{L}=(u+v)^{\beta}{\mathrm{e}}^{-u-v},\qquad(u,v)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{2}.

In particular, when
{\beta}=0
, the orthogonal polynomials become the product of classical Laguerre polynomials.
For
{\beta}>0
, orthogonal polynomials with respect to
W_{\beta}^{L}
are studied in
[
10
]
. We notice, however, that
the monomial polynomial
{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}
is no longer monomial under the mapping since

{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}(x,t)={\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}(u-v,u+v)=(u+v)^{n-k}(u-v)^{k}+\cdots,

so that our result remains new even when
{\beta}=0
.

3.1.3.
The Jacobi polynomials on the cone

Here the weight function is
w(t)=t^{\beta}(1-t)^{\gamma}
and the polynomial
q_{n-k}^{2k+2\mu}
is
given in terms of the monic Jacobi polynomial, denoted by
\widehat{P}_{n-k}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
; more
precisely,

\displaystyle q_{n-k}^{2k+2\mu}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(-2)^{n-k}}\widehat{P}_{n-k}^{(2k+2\mu+{\beta},{\gamma})}(1-2t)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{n-k}(n-k)!}{(n+k+2\mu+{\beta}+{\gamma}+1)_{n-k}}P_{n-k}^{(2k+2\mu+{\beta},{\gamma})}(1-2t),

so that the monomial polynomial
{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}^{\mathsf{J}}
is given by

\displaystyle{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}^{\mathsf{J}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}c_{k,j}^{\mu}{\mathsf{S}}_{k-2j,n}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}\frac{c_{k,j}^{\mu}}{(-2)^{n-k+2j}}\widehat{P}_{n-k+2j}^{(2(k-2j)+2\mu+{\beta},{\gamma})}(1-2t)t^{k}C_{k}^{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right).

In this case, it is easy to verify that

\displaystyle\left\|q_{n-k}^{2k+2\mu}\right\|^{2}\,

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{2\mu+{\beta},{\gamma}}}{(-2)^{2n-2k}}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{P}_{n-k}^{(2k+2\mu+{\beta},{\gamma})}(1-2t)\right|^{2}t^{2k+2\mu+{\beta}}(1-t)^{\gamma}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-k)!(2\mu+\beta+1)_{n+k}({\gamma}+1)_{n-k}}{(2\mu+{\beta}+{\gamma}+2)_{2n}(n+k+2\mu+{\beta}+{\gamma}+1)_{n-k}}.

Hence, the norm of the monomial polynomial is given by the sum

(3.3)

\displaystyle b_{\beta,\gamma,\mu}^{J}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{2}}\left|{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}^{\mathsf{J}}(x,t)\right|^{2}W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}[c_{k,i}^{\mu}]^{2}h_{k-2i}^{\mu}\frac{(n-k+2i)!(2\mu+{\beta}+1)_{n+k-2i}({\gamma}+1)_{n-k+2i}}{(2\mu+{\beta}+{\gamma}+2)_{2n}(n+k-2i+2\mu+{\beta}+{\gamma}+1)_{n-k+2i}}.

The sum can be written as a sum of three hypergeometric functions
{}_{8}F_{7}
evaluated at
1
but no
closed-form formula. It remains to be seen if it can be expressed by a formula similar to that of (
2.6
).
Let

E_{n}(f)_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}=\inf_{P\in\Pi_{n-1}^{d+1}}\|f-{\mathsf{P}}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{V}}^{2},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu})},

where the norm is defined by normalized weight function. We can use (
3.3
) to compute this quantity
for
f
is a monomial. Below are the first few cases for
f(x,y)=t^{n-|{\mathbf{k}}|}x^{\mathbf{k}}
, in which
{\alpha}={\beta}+{\gamma}+2\mu+1
,

\displaystyle\left[E_{n}(t^{n})_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}\right]^{2}\,

\displaystyle=\frac{n!({\gamma}+1)_{n}({\alpha})_{n}({\beta}+2\mu+1)_{n}}{n!({\alpha})_{2n}({\alpha}+1)_{2n}};

\displaystyle\left[E_{n}(t^{n-1}x)_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}\right]^{2}\,

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-1)!({\gamma}+1)_{n-1}({\alpha}+1)_{n}({\beta}+2\mu+1)_{n+1}}{2(\mu+1)({\alpha}+1)_{2n}({\alpha}+1)_{2n-1}};

\displaystyle\left[E_{n}(t^{n-2}x^{2})_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}\right]^{2}\,

\displaystyle=\frac{n!({\gamma}+1)_{n}({\alpha})_{n}({\alpha}+1)_{n}({\beta}+2\mu+1)_{n}}{4(\mu+1)^{2}({\alpha})_{2n}({\alpha}+1)_{2n}}

\displaystyle+\frac{(2\mu+1)(n-2)!({\gamma}+1)_{n-2}({\alpha}+1)_{n}({\alpha}+2)_{n}({\beta}+2\mu+1)_{n+2}}{4(\mu+1)^{2}(\mu+2)({\alpha}+1)_{2n}({\alpha}+2)_{2n-2}}.

In particular,

is the

norm of

We note that, for

4.

The Laguerre and Jacobi polynomials can be defined by the Rodrigues formulas, which we use to define

4.1.

Recall that on the infinite cone

Definition 4.1
.

n

which becomes, after integration by parts
k

\displaystyle I=(-1)^{k}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t^{2k+2\mu+d-1}}(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{k+\mu-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\partial^{n-k}}{\partial t^{n-k}}\left({\mathrm{e}}^{-t}t^{n+k+2\mu+\beta+d-1}\right)Q(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t,

it follows

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{B}}^{d}}(1-\|y\|^{2})^{k+\mu-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\partial^{n-k}}{\partial t^{n-k}}\left({\mathrm{e}}^{-t}t^{n+k+2\mu+{\beta}+d-1}\right)Q(ty,t)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t.

Since
Q
is a polynomial of total degree
n-k-1
in the
(x,t)
variables,
Q(ty,t)
is a polynomial of
degree at most
n-k-1
in the
t
variable. In particular,
\frac{\partial^{n-k}}{\partial t^{n-k}}Q=0
.
Hence, applying integration by parts
n-k
times with respect to
t
, we conclude that
I=0
. This
completes the proof.
∎

Unlike orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball, the two bases of
\{{\mathsf{U}}^{\mathsf{L}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}\}
and
\{{\mathsf{V}}^{\mathsf{L}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}\}

are not mutually biorthogonal. They satisfy the following partial biorthogonality.

Proposition 4.3
.

The polynomials
{\mathsf{U}}^{\mathsf{L}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}(x,t)
and
{\mathsf{V}}^{\mathsf{L}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}(x,t)
are partial biorthogonal in the sense
that, for
{\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{j}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
with
k=|{\mathbf{k}}|\leq n
and
j=|{\mathbf{j}}|\leq n
, the relation

\displaystyle b_{{\beta},\mu}^{L}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}

\displaystyle{\mathsf{U}}^{\mathsf{L}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}(x,t){\mathsf{V}}^{\mathsf{L}}_{{\mathbf{j}},n}(x,t)W_{{\beta},\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k!(n-k)!(-1)^{n}(2\mu+\beta+d)_{n+k}\frac{(\mu+\frac{1}{2})_{k}}{(\mu+\frac{d+1}{2})_{k}}\delta_{{\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{j}}},

holds if
k\geq j
or
j>k
and
{\mathbf{j}}-{\mathbf{k}}
has an odd component.

Proof.

Since
{\mathsf{U}}^{{\mathsf{L}}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
is an orthogonal polynomial of degree
n
and
{\mathsf{V}}^{\mathsf{L}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
is monomial,
we obtain immediately that

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathsf{U}}^{{\mathsf{L}}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n},{\mathsf{V}}^{{\mathsf{L}}}_{{\mathbf{j}},n}\right\rangle\,

\displaystyle=b_{{\beta},\mu}^{L}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}{\mathsf{U}}^{{\mathsf{L}}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}(x,t){\mathsf{V}}^{{\mathsf{L}}}_{{\mathbf{j}},n}(x,t)W_{\beta,\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=b_{{\beta},\mu}^{L}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}{\mathsf{U}}^{{\mathsf{L}}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}(x,t)t^{n-|{\mathbf{j}}|}x^{\mathbf{j}}W_{\beta,\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

Integrating by parts
{\mathbf{k}}
times with respect to
x
as in the previous theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathsf{U}}^{{\mathsf{L}}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n},{\mathsf{V}}^{{\mathsf{L}}}_{{\mathbf{j}},n}\right\rangle=\,

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}b_{{\beta},\mu}^{L}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}\frac{t^{n-k}}{t^{2k+2\mu+d-1}}(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{k+\mu-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\partial^{n-k}}{\partial t^{n-k}}\left({\mathrm{e}}^{-t}t^{n+k+2\mu+\beta+d-1}\right)\frac{\partial^{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial x^{\mathbf{k}}}x^{{\mathbf{j}}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t,

which is zero if
k>j
. If, however,
j>k
and there is an
i
such that
j_{i}-k_{i}
is odd, then

\frac{\partial^{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial x^{\mathbf{k}}}x^{{\mathbf{j}}}=\frac{{\mathbf{j}}!}{({\mathbf{j}}-{\mathbf{k}})!}x^{{\mathbf{j}}-{\mathbf{k}}}
has a component
x_{i}^{j_{i}-k_{i}}
of odd power, so that the last integral is zero if we make a change of variable
x_{i}\mapsto-x_{i}
.
Finally, for
{\mathbf{j}}={\mathbf{k}}
,
we use substitution
x=ty
and integrate by parts with respect to
t
, as in the previous proof, to obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathsf{U}}^{{\mathsf{L}}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n},{\mathsf{V}}^{{\mathsf{L}}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}\right\rangle=\,

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}{\mathbf{k}}!b_{\mu}^{\mathbb{B}}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}(1-\|y\|^{2})^{k+\mu-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\times\,\frac{1}{\Gamma(2\mu+{\beta}+d)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{n-k}\frac{\partial^{n-k}}{\partial t^{n-k}}\left({\mathrm{e}}^{-t}t^{n+k+2\mu+\beta+d-1}\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}{\mathbf{k}}!\frac{b_{\mu}^{\mathbb{B}}}{b_{\mu+k}^{\mathbb{B}}}\frac{(n-k)!}{\Gamma(2\mu+{\beta}+d)}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-t}t^{n+k+2\mu+\beta+d-1}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}{\mathbf{k}}!(n-k)!(2\mu+{\beta}+d)_{n+k}\frac{(\mu+\frac{1}{2})_{k}}{(\mu+\frac{d+1}{2})_{k}},

which completes the proof.
∎

We can find a basis in terms of
{\mathsf{U}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{L}}
that is biorthogonal with respect to the basis of
{\mathsf{V}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{L}}
. This is illustrated in the following for the case
d=1
.

Proposition 4.4
.

For
d=1
, define the polynomials
\{{\mathsf{Y}}_{n-k,n}^{\mathsf{L}}:0\leq k\leq n\}
recursively as follows: for

k=0,1
,
{\mathsf{Y}}_{n-k,n}^{\mathsf{L}}={\mathsf{U}}_{n-k,n}^{\mathsf{L}}
, and for
k=2,3,\ldots,n
,

(4.1)

{\mathsf{Y}}_{n-k,n}^{\mathsf{L}}(x,t)={\mathsf{U}}_{n-k,n}^{{\mathsf{L}}}-\sum_{1\leq i\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor}c_{i,k}{\mathsf{Y}}_{n-k+2i,n}^{\mathsf{L}}

recursively, where the coefficients are

c_{i,k}=\frac{k!\left(\mu+i+n-k+1\right)_{i}}{2^{2i}\left(k-2i\right)!i!\left(\mu+n-k+1/2\right)_{2i}\left(2\mu+\beta+2n-k+1\right)_{2i}}.

Then
\{{\mathsf{Y}}_{n-k,n}^{\mathsf{L}}:0\leq k\leq n\}
is a basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}^{2},W_{{\beta},\mu})
and it is mutually
biorthogonal to the basis
\{{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}^{\mathsf{L}}:0\leq k\leq n\}
.

Proof.

We define
{\mathsf{Y}}_{n-k,n}^{\mathsf{L}}
as in (
4.1
) and determine the coefficients
c_{i,k}
by requiring

I_{n-k,n-j}:={\langle}{\mathsf{Y}}^{{\mathsf{L}}}_{n-k,n},{\mathsf{V}}^{{\mathsf{L}}}_{n-j,n}{\rangle}=h_{n-k}\delta_{k,j},

where, by (
4.1
) and the required biorthogonality,

h_{n-k}={\langle}{\mathsf{U}}^{{\mathsf{L}}}_{n-k,n},{\mathsf{V}}^{{\mathsf{L}}}_{n-k,n}{\rangle}.

The case
k=0
and
1
holds trivially by Proposition
4.3
. Assume that
{\mathsf{Y}}_{n-k+2i,n}^{\mathsf{L}}

has been determined for
i=1,\ldots,\lfloor k/2\rfloor
for
2\leq k\leq n
. We now define
{\mathsf{Y}}_{n-k,n}^{\mathsf{L}}

as given in (
4.1
). Then

I_{n-k,n-j}={\langle}{\mathsf{U}}_{n-k,n}^{\mathsf{L}},{\mathsf{V}}_{n-j,n}^{\mathsf{L}}{\rangle}-h_{n-j}\sum_{1\leq l\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor}c_{l,k}\delta_{n-k+2l,n-j},

which is zero if
j>k
or
k
and
j
have different parity when
j<k
. Thus, we only need to consider

j=k-2i
, for which
I_{n-k,n-j}=0
becomes

0={\langle}{\mathsf{U}}_{n-k,n}^{\mathsf{L}},{\mathsf{V}}_{n-k+2i,n}^{\mathsf{L}}{\rangle}-c_{i,k}h_{n-k+2i},\quad 1\leq i\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor,

which gives immediately

c_{i,k}={\langle}{\mathsf{U}}_{n-k,n}^{\mathsf{L}},{\mathsf{V}}_{n-k+2i,n}^{\mathsf{L}}{\rangle}/h_{n-k+2i}.

The value of
h_{n-k+2i}
is already given in Proposition
4.3
. Furthermore, following the
proof there, we also obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathsf{U}}_{k,n}^{\mathsf{L}},{\mathsf{V}}_{k+2i,n}^{\mathsf{L}}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\,b_{{\beta},\mu}^{L}(-1)^{k}\frac{(k+2i)!}{(2i)!}\int_{-1}^{1}y^{2i}(1-y^{2})^{k+\mu-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{n-k}\frac{\partial^{n-k}}{\partial t^{n-k}}\left(t^{n+k+{\beta}+2\mu}e^{-t}\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}\frac{(k+2i)!(n-k)!(2\mu+{\beta}+1)_{n+k}(\mu+\frac{1}{2})_{k}(\frac{1}{2})_{i}}{(2i)!(\mu+1)_{k+i}},

where the last step follows from integrating by parts
n-k
times in the
t
variable and evaluating the
two integrals of one variable. Using this identity with
k
replaced by
n-k
, we obtain the formula of
the coefficient
c_{i,k}
after rewriting the Pochhammer symbols.
∎

4.2.
Jacobi polynomials on the cone

Recall that on the finite cone
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
, the Jacobi weight function is defined by

W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}(x,t)=(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}t^{\beta}(1-t)^{\gamma},\qquad\mu>-\frac{1}{2},\quad{\beta}>-d,\quad{\gamma}>-1.

We define a family of polynomials
{\mathsf{U}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{L}}
by the Rodrigues type formula.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
,
{\beta}>-d
and
{\gamma}>-1
. For
{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
and
|{\mathbf{k}}|=k
,
0\leq k\leq n
, define

{\mathsf{U}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{J}}(x,t)=\frac{1}{W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}(x,t)}\frac{1}{t^{2k+2\mu+d-1}}\frac{\partial^{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial x^{\mathbf{k}}}\left(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}\right)^{k+\mu-\frac{1}{2}}\\
\times\frac{\partial^{n-k}}{\partial t^{n-k}}\left[t^{n+k+{\beta}+2\mu+d-1}(1-t)^{{\gamma}+n-k}\right].

Theorem 4.6
.

The family of polynomials
\{{\mathsf{U}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{J}}:|{\mathbf{k}}|\leq n\}
is a basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu})
.

Proof.

The definition of
{\mathsf{U}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{J}}
can be rewritten as

{\mathsf{U}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{J}}(x,t)=\frac{1}{(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}}\frac{\partial^{{\mathbf{k}}}}{\partial x^{{\mathbf{k}}}}(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{k+\mu-\frac{1}{2}}\\
\times\frac{1}{t^{2k+{\beta}+2\mu+d-1}(1-t)^{\gamma}}\frac{\partial^{n-k}}{\partial t^{n-k}}\left[t^{n+k+{\beta}+2\mu+d-1}(1-t)^{{\gamma}+n-k}\right],

from which it follows immediately that
{\mathsf{U}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{J}}
is indeed a polynomial of degree
n
. We can prove that
it is an orthogonal polynomial similar to the case of the Laguerre polynomials. Let
P
be a polynomial of degree
n-1

in
(x,t)
variable. Integrating by parts
k
times with respect to
x
gives

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}{\mathsf{U}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{J}}(x,t)

\displaystyle P(x,t)W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}\frac{1}{t^{2k+2\mu+d-1}}(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{k+\mu-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\partial^{n-k}}{\partial t^{n-k}}\left[(1-t)^{\gamma+n-k}t^{n+k+2\mu+\beta+d-1}\right]Q(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t,

which becomes zero upon integrating by parts
n-k
times with respect to
t
.
∎

Theorem 4.7
.

The polynomials
{\mathsf{U}}^{\mathsf{J}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}(x,t)
and
{\mathsf{V}}^{\mathsf{J}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}(x,t)
are partially biorthogonal in the
sense that, for
{\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{j}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
and
k=|{\mathbf{k}}|\leq n
and
j=|{\mathbf{j}}|\leq n
,

(4.2)

\displaystyle b_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{J}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}

\displaystyle{\mathsf{U}}^{\mathsf{J}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}(x,t){\mathsf{V}}^{\mathsf{J}}_{{\mathbf{j}},n}(x,t)W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k!(n-k)!(-1)^{n}\frac{(\mu+\frac{1}{2})_{k}(2\mu+{\beta}+d)_{n+k}({\gamma}+1)_{n-k}}{(\mu+\frac{d+1}{2})_{k}(2\mu+{\beta}+{\gamma}+d+1)_{2n}}\delta_{{\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{j}}}

holds if
k\geq j
or
j>k
and
{\mathbf{j}}-{\mathbf{k}}
has an odd component.

Proof.

Since
{\mathsf{U}}^{{\mathsf{J}}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
is orthogonal to polynomials of lower degree and
{\mathsf{V}}^{\mathsf{J}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
is monomial, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathsf{U}}^{{\mathsf{J}}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n},{\mathsf{V}}^{{\mathsf{J}}}_{{\mathbf{j}},n}\right\rangle\,

\displaystyle=b_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{J}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}{\mathsf{U}}^{{\mathsf{J}}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}(x,t){\mathsf{V}}^{{\mathsf{J}}}_{{\mathbf{j}},n}W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=b_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{J}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}{\mathsf{U}}^{{\mathsf{J}}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}(x,t)t^{n-|{\mathbf{j}}|}x^{\mathbf{j}}W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

Integrating by parts
{\mathbf{k}}
times with respect to
x
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathsf{U}}^{{\mathsf{J}}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n},{\mathsf{V}}^{{\mathsf{J}}}_{{\mathbf{j}},n}\right\rangle=\,

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}b_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}^{J}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}\frac{t^{n-k}}{t^{2k+2\mu+d-1}}(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{k+\mu-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\partial^{n-k}}{\partial t^{n-k}}\left[(1-t)^{{\gamma}+n-k}t^{n+k+2\mu+\beta+d-1}\right]\frac{\partial^{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial x^{\mathbf{k}}}x^{{\mathbf{j}}}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t,

from which the partially biorthogonal follows as in the Laguerre case.
For
{\mathbf{j}}={\mathbf{k}}
, we use substitution
x=ty

and integrate by parts with respect to
t
to obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathsf{U}}^{{\mathsf{J}}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n},{\mathsf{V}}^{{\mathsf{J}}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}\right\rangle=\,

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}{\mathbf{k}}!b_{\mu}^{\mathbb{B}}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}(1-\|y\|^{2})^{k+\mu-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\times\,c_{2\mu+{\beta}+d-1,{\gamma}}\int_{0}^{1}t^{n-k}\frac{\partial^{n-k}}{\partial t^{n-k}}\left[(1-t)^{{\gamma}+n-k}t^{n+k+2\mu+\beta+d-1}\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}{\mathbf{k}}!\frac{b_{\mu}^{\mathbb{B}}}{b_{\mu+k}^{\mathbb{B}}}(n-k)!c_{2\mu+{\beta}+d-1,{\gamma}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)^{{\gamma}+n-k}t^{n+k+2\mu+\beta+d-1}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}{\mathbf{k}}!(n-k)!\frac{(\mu+\frac{1}{2})_{k}}{(\mu+\frac{d+1}{2})_{k}}\frac{c_{2\mu+{\beta}+d-1,{\gamma}}}{c_{n+k+2\mu+{\beta}+d-1,n-k+{\gamma}}},

where
c_{{\alpha},{\gamma}}
is defined in (
2.15
), from which (
4.2
) follows.
∎

We can also define a basis in terms of
{\mathsf{U}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{J}}
so that it is biorthogonal with respect to the basis of

{\mathsf{V}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{J}}
. We state the result for the case
d=1
as in the Laguerre case.

Proposition 4.8
.

For
d=1
, define the polynomials
\{{\mathsf{Y}}_{n-k,n}^{\mathsf{J}}:0\leq k\leq n\}
recursively as follows: for

k=0,1
,
{\mathsf{Y}}_{n-k,n}^{\mathsf{J}}={\mathsf{U}}_{n-k,n}^{\mathsf{J}}
, and for
k=2,3,\ldots,n
,

(4.3)

{\mathsf{Y}}_{n-k,n}^{\mathsf{J}}(x,t)={\mathsf{U}}_{n-k,n}^{{\mathsf{J}}}-\sum_{1\leq i\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor}d_{i,k}{\mathsf{Y}}_{n-k+2i,n}^{\mathsf{J}}

recursively, where the coefficients are given by

d_{i,k}=\frac{k!(\mu+i+n-k+1)_{i}(\gamma+k-2i+1)_{2i}}{2^{2i}(k-2i)!i!(\mu+n-k+1/2)_{2i}(2\mu+\beta+2n-k+1)_{2i}}.

Then
\{{\mathsf{Y}}_{n-k,n}^{\mathsf{J}}:0\leq k\leq n\}
is a basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}^{2},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu})
and it is mutually
biorthogonal to the basis
\{{\mathsf{V}}_{k,n}^{\mathsf{J}}:0\leq k\leq n\}
.

The proof is parallel to the Jacobi case, we shall omit the details.

5.
Generating function for
U
-family and
V
-family

Like the case of the unit ball, the
U
-family of orthogonal polynomials on the cone satisfies a
generating function. For the
V
-family, however, we do not have a generating function unless we
use the relation in Proposition
3.5
to relate
{\mathsf{V}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
to
{\mathsf{S}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
first, since then
we can obtain a generating function for
{\mathsf{V}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
in terms of a generating function for
{\mathsf{S}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
.
We shall give the generating function for
{\mathsf{V}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}
in this section as well. In both cases, the
generating functions are given for the Laguerre polynomials and the Jacobi polynomials on the cone.

5.1.
Laguerre polynomials on the cone

The polynomials
U_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{L}}
are indexed by
({\mathbf{k}},n)
with
{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
,
|{\mathbf{k}}|\leq n
and

n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Its generating function is given below.

Proposition 5.1
.

For
{\mathbf{b}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
,
r>0
and
r<1
, the generating function for
{\mathsf{U}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{L}}
is given by

(5.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\sum_{|{\mathbf{k}}|\leq n}\frac{(-1)^{k}(2\mu)_{|{\mathbf{k}}|}}{2^{|{\mathbf{k}}|}(\mu+\frac{1}{2})_{|{\mathbf{k}}|}|{\mathbf{k}}|!(n-|{\mathbf{k}}|)!}{\mathsf{U}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{L}}(x,t){\mathbf{b}}^{\mathbf{k}}r^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-r)^{2\mu}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\frac{tr}{1-r}}}{(1-r)^{{\beta}+d}\big([(1-r)^{2}-r{\langle}{\mathbf{b}},x{\rangle}]^{2}+r^{2}\|{\mathbf{b}}\|^{2}(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})\big)^{\mu}}.

Proof.

Changing variable
x=ty
, we can write

\frac{1}{(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}}\frac{\partial^{{\mathbf{k}}}}{\partial x^{\mathbf{k}}}(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{k+\mu-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{t^{k}}{(1-\|y\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}}\frac{\partial^{{\mathbf{k}}}}{\partial y^{\mathbf{k}}}(1-\|y\|^{2})^{k+\mu-\frac{1}{2}}.

Consequently, using the Rodrigues formulas for
U_{\mathbf{k}}
in (
2.2
) and the Laguerre polynomial,
we see that the polynomial
{\mathsf{U}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{L}}
can be written as

{\mathsf{U}}_{k,n}^{\mathsf{L}}(x,t)=\frac{2^{|{\mathbf{k}}|}(\mu+\frac{1}{2})_{|{\mathbf{k}}|}!{\mathbf{k}}|!(n-|{\mathbf{k}}|)!}{(-1)^{k}(2\mu)_{|{\mathbf{k}}|}}t^{k}U_{{\mathbf{k}}}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)L_{n-k}^{2k+{\alpha}}(t),

where
{\alpha}=2\mu+{\beta}+d-1
. Hence, using the generating formula for the Laguerre polynomials first, we see that the
left-hand side of (
5.1
) is equal to

\displaystyle\mathrm{LHS}\,

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{|{\mathbf{k}}|=k}t^{k}U_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)L_{n-k}^{2k+{\alpha}}(t){\mathbf{b}}^{\mathbf{k}}r^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{|{\mathbf{k}}|=k}t^{k}U_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right){\mathbf{b}}^{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}L_{n-k}^{2k+{\alpha}}(t)r^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{|{\mathbf{k}}|=k}t^{k}U_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right){\mathbf{b}}^{\mathbf{k}}r^{k}\frac{1}{(1-r)^{2k+{\alpha}+1}}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{-rt}{1-r}}.

Now, applying the generating formula for
U_{\mathbf{k}}
in (
2.3
) with
{\mathbf{a}}=tr|{\mathbf{b}}|/(1-r)^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{LHS}=\frac{1}{\big((1-\frac{r}{(1-r)^{2}}{\langle}{\mathbf{b}},x{\rangle})^{2}+r^{2}\frac{\|{\mathbf{b}}\|^{2}}{(1-r)^{4}}(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})\big)^{\mu}}\frac{1}{(1-r)^{{\alpha}+1}}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{-rt}{1-r}},

which becomes (
5.1
) after rearranging the terms.
∎

We now give a generating function for
{\mathsf{S}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{L}}
given by

\displaystyle{\mathsf{S}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{L}}(x,t):=\widehat{L}_{n-k}^{2k+2\mu+{\beta}+d-1}(t)t^{k}V_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right),\quad|{\mathbf{k}}|=k,\,\,{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d},\,\,0\leq k\leq n.

Using the generating function (
2.5
) in place of (
2.3
), the generating function
can be derived exactly as in the proof for (
5.1
). The result is the following.

Proposition 5.2
.

For
{\mathbf{b}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
,
r>0
and
r<1
, the generating function for
{\mathsf{S}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{L}}
is given by

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\sum_{|{\mathbf{k}}\leq n}\frac{(-1)^{n-|{\mathbf{k}}|}2^{|{\mathbf{k}}|}(\mu+\frac{d-1}{2})_{|{\mathbf{k}}|}}{(n-|{\mathbf{k}}|)!{\mathbf{k}}!}{\mathsf{S}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{L}}(x,t){\mathbf{b}}^{\mathbf{k}}r^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-r)^{2\mu+d}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\frac{tr}{1-r}}}{(1-r)^{{\beta}+2}\big((1-r)^{4}-2r(1-r)^{2}{\langle}{\mathbf{b}},x{\rangle}+t^{2}r^{2}\|{\mathbf{b}}\|^{2}\big)^{\mu+\frac{d-1}{2}}}.

5.2.
Jacobi polynomials on the cone

Similarly to the Laguerre case, the generating function for the
U
-family of the Jacobi polynomials on the cone
is given as follows.

Proposition 5.3
.

For
{\mathbf{b}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
,
r>0
and
r<1
, the generating function for
{\mathsf{U}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{J}}
is given by

(5.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\sum_{|{\mathbf{k}}|\leq n}\frac{(-1)^{k}(2\mu)_{|{\mathbf{k}}|}}{2^{|{\mathbf{k}}|}(\mu+\frac{1}{2})_{|{\mathbf{k}}|}|{\mathbf{k}}|!(n-|{\mathbf{k}}|)!}{\mathsf{U}}_{{\mathbf{k}},n}^{\mathsf{J}}(x,t){\mathbf{b}}^{\mathbf{k}}r^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{2\mu+{\beta}+{\gamma}+d-1}R^{-1}(1+r+R)^{-{\gamma}}(1-r+R)^{2\mu-{\beta}-d+1}}{\big([(1-r+R)^{2}-4r{\langle}{\mathbf{b}},x{\rangle}]^{2}+16r^{2}\|{\mathbf{b}}\|^{2}(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})\big)^{\mu}},

where

R=\left[(1-r)^{2}+4rt\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Proof.

Changing variable
x=ty
, we can rewrite the polynomial

{\mathsf{U}}_{k,n}^{\mathsf{J}}(x,t)=\frac{2^{|{\mathbf{k}}|}(\mu+\frac{1}{2})_{|{\mathbf{k}}|}!{\mathbf{k}}|!(n-|{\mathbf{k}}|)!}{(-1)^{k}(2\mu)_{|{\mathbf{k}}|}}t^{k}U_{{\mathbf{k}}}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)P_{n-k}^{(2k+{\alpha},{\gamma})}(1-2t),

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{|{\mathbf{k}}|=k}t^{k}U_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)P_{n-k}^{(2k+{\alpha},{\gamma})}(1-2t){\mathbf{b}}^{\mathbf{k}}r^{n}

and simplify the result to complete the proof.
∎

Proposition 5.4
