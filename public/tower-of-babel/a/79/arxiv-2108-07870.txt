Nonlocal and nonadiabatic Pauli potential for time-dependent orbital-free density functional theory
Kaili Jiang (姜凯立), Xuecheng Shao (邵学成), Michele Pavanello

Abstract
Time-dependent orbital-free density functional theory (TD-OFDFT) is an efficient ab-initio method for calculating the electronic dynamics of large systems. In comparison to standard TD-DFT, it computes only a single electronic state regardless of system size, but it requires an additional time-dependent Pauli potential term. We propose a nonadiabatic and nonlocal Pauli potential whose main ingredients are the time-dependent particle and current densities. Our calculations of the optical spectra of metallic and semiconductor clusters indicate that nonlocal and nonadiabatic TD-OFDFT performs accurately for metallic systems and semiquantitatively for semiconductors. This work opens the door to wide applicability of TD-OFDFT for nonequilibrium electron and electron-nuclear dynamics of materials.



I
Introduction

The ab-initio simulation of nano-scale quantum systems when their electrons are out of equilibrium is a challenging task
Schirmer and Dreuw 2007
;
Holas et al. 2008
;
Maitra et al. 2008
;
Schirmer and Dreuw 2008
;
Vignale 2008
, not only for time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) but for almost all other electronic structure methods. One of the challenges is the computational cost involved in the simulations, which compounds on top of the already challenging computation of the ground state. In DFT and TD-DFT, the computational expense typically scales cubically with system size, and for wavefunction-based methods, the cost is even higher
Pereira Gomes and Jacob 2012
. The ab-initio methods currently available, are thus not suited to address systems relevant to most experiments (where typical sizes of nanoclusters are in the range of 1 to 1000 nm
Ringe 2020
;
Baletto 2019
;
Andres-Arroyo et al. 2016
). Clearly, alternative methods are needed. Several research groups are actively working to tackle this issue developing such methods as the semi-empirical time-dependent density functional tight biding (TD-DFTB)
Rüger et al. 2016
. In this work, we further the time-dependent orbital-free density functional theory (TD-OFDFT), an
ab initio
method with potentially far reduced computational cost compared to TD-DFT and even TD-DFTB.

TD-OFDFT tackles the scaling problem by only considering one active orbital. In contrast, TD-DFT uses a number of orbitals equal to the number of electrons in the system. Thus, TD-OFDFT can achieve a linear computational scaling with the number of electrons so long as approximate functionals are used. In addition to the time-dependent exchange-correlation (XC) potential that also needs to be approximated in TD-DFT
Fuks et al. 2018
, TD-OFDFT requires to approximate the time-dependent Pauli potential
Jiang and Pavanello 2021
;
White et al. 2018
;
Ding et al. 2018
;
Schild and Gross 2017
. Thus, the main challenge is to find a good approximation to this potential.

Early adoptions of TD-OFDFT utilize adiabatic and local density approximations, i.e., the adiabatic Thomas-Fermi (TF)
Thomas 1926
;
Fermi 1927
potential as the time-dependent Pauli potential, sometimes referred to as time-dependent Thomas Fermi
Pi et al. 1986
;
Domps et al. 1998
;
Rusek et al. 2000
, or hydrodynamic DFT
Banerjee and Harbola 2000
;
Michta et al. 2015
;
Moldabekov et al. 2018
;
Yan 2015
. Such a simple approximation has been successful for systems such as Na clusters
Domps et al. 1998
, but only qualitatively captures the spectra for other types of systems
Banerjee and Harbola 2000
;
Palade and Baran 2015
;
Shao et al. 2021
. As we will show in this work, TD-OFDFT can only become semiquantitative for a wide class of systems if the approximate time-dependent Pauli potential adopted in the simulations is nonlocal and nonadiabatic.

To account for nonlocality, several nonlocal kinetic energy density functionals (KEDFs) have been developed for ground state OFDFT
Huang and Carter 2010
;
Mi et al. 2018
;
Mi and Pavanello 2019a
. These can be employed in TD-OFDFT by applying the so-called adiabatic approximation (i.e., the potentials are given by the ground state functional evaluated at the time-dependent electron density). To account for nonadiabaticity (i.e., going beyond the adiabatic approximation), potentials need to become at least dependent on the time-dependent electron density as well as the current density
Vignale and Kohn 1996
;
Vignale et al. 1997
;
Fuks et al. 2018
, as done recently by White
et al.

White et al. 2018
. Along these lines of research, in this work we derive an improved nonadiabatic correction to the time-dependent Pauli potential from a frequency-dependent Pauli kernel derived from the response of the uniform electron gas (UEG) which we recently proposed
Jiang and Pavanello 2021
.

Another challenge for TD-OFDFT has been the general lack of software that implements it. We recently filled this gap by developing DFTpy
Shao et al. 2021
(dftpy.rutgers.edu), an object-oriented Python software for OFDFT as well as real-time and linear-response (Casida
Casida 1995
) TD-OFDFT. DFTpy is fully parallelized using mpi4py
Dalcín et al. 2008
, and scales well with system size as we showed in Ref.
26
.

This work is structured as follows. In Section
II
we briefly introduce the formalism of TD-OFDFT, derive the proposed nonadiabatic correction to the time-dependent Pauli potential from the frequency-dependent Pauli kernel, and discuss the implementation of the potential in DFTpy. In Section
IV
we present the optical spectra derived from real-time TD-OFDFT runs carried out in a linear-response regime (weak perturbation). As examples, we chose several metallic (Na, Mg, Ag) and semiconductor clusters (Si, GaAs).

II
Formalism

II.1
Time-dependent Schrödinger-like equation for TD-OFDFT

For more details on TD-OFDFT, we refer the readers to Refs.
12
;
13
;
34
. Here we provide a brief introduction to the formalism.
In TD-OFDFT, one-to-one invertible maps are established between the real system of interacting electrons, the fictitious system of noninteracting electrons (a.k.a. the KS system), and a fictitious system of noninteracting bosons. The bosonic system yields the following density-wavefunction relationship

n({\mathbf{r}},t)=N|\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}({\mathbf{r}},t)|^{2},

(1)

where the subscription
\mathrm{B}
stands for the bosonic system.

In a typical setup for real-time propagations, the system starts with its ground state density
n_{0}({\mathbf{r}})
and at
t=0
the system is perturbed with a kick with strength
k

Yabana et al. 2006
. A time-dependent Schrödinger-like equation is employed to propagate the system for
t>t_{0}
,

\left[-\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2}+v_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}[n]({\mathbf{r}},t)\right]\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}({{\mathbf{r}},t})=i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}({{\mathbf{r}},t}),

(2)

with the initial condition

\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}({\mathbf{r}},t_{0})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sqrt{n_{0}({\mathbf{r}})}e^{ikz},

(3)

where the exponential term effectively donates momentum
k
to the electronic system.
The time-dependent effective potential is given by

v_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}[n]({\mathbf{r}},t)=v_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}[n]({\mathbf{r}},t)+v_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm S}[n]({\mathbf{r}},t),

(4)

where
v_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}[n]({\mathbf{r}},t)
is the time-dependent Pauli potential which is the difference between the bosonic noninteracting kinetic potential and the fermionic one. Thus, it is said that the Pauli potential introduces the effects of the Pauli exclusion prnciple in the boson electronic structure
Eq. (
2
)
. In practice, the Pauli energy is defined as the subtraction of the von Weizsäcker
Weizsäcker 1935
(vW,
T_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm vW}[n]
) functional from the exact noninteracting kinetic energy functional. Therefore, kinetic energy functionals developed for ground-state OFDFT can be used for approximating the Pauli potential by simply taking the functional derivative of the functional removed of the von Weizsäcker kinetic energy.

The corresponding adiabatic approximation for the Pauli potential is given by,

v_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}^{\rm ad}({\mathbf{r}},t)=\left.\frac{\delta T_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}[n_{0}]}{\delta n_{0}}\right|_{n_{0}({\mathbf{r}})\rightarrow n({\mathbf{r}},t)},

(5)

where
T_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}[n_{0}]
is the Pauli energy,
T_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm S}[n_{0}]-T_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm vW}[n_{0}]
. However, to achieve accurate results in TD-OFDFT, the nonadiabatic contribution to the potential should not be neglected. In this case, the total Pauli potential can be represented as the adiabatic portion plus a nonadiabatic correction

v_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}({\mathbf{r}},t)=v_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}^{\rm ad}({\mathbf{r}},t)+v_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}^{\rm nad}({\mathbf{r}},t).

(6)

The nonadiabatic contribution to the Pauli potential has much stronger effects on the electron dynamics compared to the nonadiabatic contribution to the exchange-correlation (XC) potential
Fuks et al. 2018
. The latter, needs to bridge the dynamics of
N
noninteracting electrons with the one of
N
interacting electrons. Thus, states described by so-called double excitations and beyond need to be accounted for by the nonadiabatic XC term. In contrast, the nonadiabatic contribution to the Pauli potential needs to bridge the dynamics of
N
noninteracting bosonic electrons to the one of
N
noninteracting fermionic electrons, therefore it needs to create all those excitations that are missing when exciting a single effective electron in comparison to exciting
N
fermionic electrons. Thus, the nonadiabaticity in the Pauli potential is very strong and typically accounts for qualitative aspects of the electronic response, as we reported recently
Jiang and Pavanello 2021
in a study about the first excited state of selected systems. Thus, we expect the addition of an approximate nonadiabatic Pauli potential contribution to the TD-OFDFT effective time-dependent potential will significantly improve the description of the electronic structure of the systems considered in comparison to adiabatic TD-OFDFT.

II.2
Approximating the nonadiabatic Pauli potential

In this section, we will derive a nonadiabatic correction to the Pauli potential from a nonadiabatic Pauli kernel developed by the authors
Jiang and Pavanello 2021
. In our previous work, we derived a Pauli kernel relying on the Dyson equation connecting the bosonic response,
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}
, with the Kohn-Sham response,
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm S}
,

\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}^{-1}-\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm S}^{-1}=f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}.

(7)

All the above quantities have a dependence on time as
t-t^{\prime}
and two space variables,
{\mathbf{r}}
and
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
. By substituting in
Eq. (
7
)
the frequency dependent Lindhard function for
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm S}
and the response of a uniform boson gas for
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}
, one obtains an expression for
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}
which can be expanded in terms of unitless variables,
\bar{\eta}=\frac{q}{2k_{F}}
,
\bar{\omega}=\frac{\omega}{qk_{F}}
,
\bar{\gamma}=\frac{\eta}{qk_{F}}
, where
q
is the conjugate variable to
|{\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}|
,
\omega
is the frequency of the external time-dependent perturbation and
\eta
is the broadening parameter introduced to implement causality
Ullrich 2011
.

Retaining terms up to the first order of
\omega
,

f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}^{\rm nad}({\mathbf{r}},q,\omega)=\frac{i\pi^{3}}{12}\left(\frac{6}{k_{F}^{2}({\mathbf{r}})q}+\frac{q}{k_{F}^{4}({\mathbf{r}})}\right)\omega,

(8)

where the local density approximation for the Fermi wavevector is introduced,
k_{F}({\mathbf{r}})=(3\pi^{2}n({\mathbf{r}}))^{1/3}
.

We now use the nonadiabatic Pauli kernel to approximate the nonadiabatic correction to the time-dependent Pauli potential adopting the same technique as described in Ref.
13
.
Considering the continuity equation

\frac{\partial n({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)}{\partial t}=-\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{j}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t),

(9)

and expanding the density at
t^{\prime}
around time
t
up to the first order, we obtain

\displaystyle\delta n({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\approx\delta n({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)+\frac{\partial n({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)}{\partial t}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\delta n({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)-\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{j}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)(t-t^{\prime}).

(10)

Using the definition of the nonadiabatic Pauli kernel

f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}^{\rm nad}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})=\frac{\delta v_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}^{\rm nad}({\mathbf{r}},t)}{\delta n({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})},

(11)

we can write the variation of the Pauli potential as

\delta v_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}^{\rm nad}({\mathbf{r}},t)=\int{\rm d}{{\mathbf{r}}}^{\prime}\,\int{\rm d}(t-t^{\prime})\delta n({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}^{\rm nad}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime}).

(12)

Plugging
Eq. (
9
)
into
Eq. (
12
)
we get

\displaystyle v_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}^{\rm nad}({\mathbf{r}},t)

\displaystyle=-\int{\rm d}{{\mathbf{r}}}^{\prime}\,\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{j}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)\int{\rm d}(t-t^{\prime})(t-t^{\prime})f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}^{\rm nad}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\int{\rm d}{{\mathbf{r}}}^{\prime}\,\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{j}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)\frac{i\partial f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}^{\rm nad}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},\omega)}{\partial\omega}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot{\mathbf{j}}({\mathbf{q}},t)\frac{i\partial f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}^{\rm nad}({\mathbf{q}},\omega)}{\partial\omega}\right\},

(13)

where
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\cdot\}
represents inverse Fourier transform in the space domain.

We immediately clarify that
Eq. (
II.2
)
is approximate. Not only because the time-dependent density is expanded only to first order, but, most importantly, because the recovered quantity is formally not

v_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}^{\rm nad}

but its first-order variation
\delta v_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}^{\rm nad}
. Formally, a procedure of functional integration should be employed which, unlike the static case
van Leeuwen and Baerends 1995
;
Gaiduk et al. 2009
;
Mi et al. 2018
, is not straightforward in the time domain.

Finally, we plug
Eq. (
8
)
into
Eq. (
II.2
)
and note that the derivative of the adiabatic contribution of
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}
with respect to
\omega
vanishes, we obtain

v_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm P}^{\rm nad}({\mathbf{r}},t)=\frac{\pi^{3}}{12}\left(\frac{6}{k_{F}^{2}({\mathbf{r}})}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot{\mathbf{j}}({\mathbf{q}},t)\frac{1}{q}\right\}+\frac{1}{k_{F}^{4}({\mathbf{r}})}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot{\mathbf{j}}({\mathbf{q}},t)q\right\}\right).

(14)

We note that
Eq. (
14
)
corrects the potential proposed by White
et al

White et al. 2018
with the addition of a second term. As our calculations will demonstrate, the second term of
Eq. (
14
)
is key to achieving accurate results and thus should not be neglected.

II.3
Implementation of the nonadiabatic potential

We numerically solve
Eq. (
2
)
in the usual way by a propagation method implemented in our software package DFTpy. We choose the Crank-Nicolson propagator for this task, with a predictor-corrector up to any desired order. We refer the readers to Ref.
26
for the detailes of the implementation. In short, the propagator is to solve the following equation

\left(1+i\frac{{\rm d}t}{2}\hat{H}\right)\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}(t+{\rm d}t)=\left(1-i\frac{{\rm d}t}{2}\hat{H}\right)\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}(t).

(15)

Typically, the predictor-corrector only checks the density to be converged
Castro et al. 2004
;
Gómez Pueyo et al. 2018
. However, to implement a current-dependent potential such as the one in
Eq. (
14
)
, the predictor-corrector needs to be modified to include the current density, i.e.,
|j_{\rm{corr}}-j_{\rm{pred}}|<\epsilon
. Unfortunately, this often results in a large number of predictor-corrector loops, increasing computation time and causing numerical instabilities. To resolve this issue, we implemented the nonadiabatic potential as a correction with a separate Taylor-like propagation.

To achieve this goal, we first write the Hamiltonian as

\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}^{0}(t)+\hat{H}^{\prime}(t),

(16)

where
\hat{H}^{\prime}
includes the nonadiabatic potential in
Eq. (
14
)
and
\hat{H}^{0}
includes everything else. The solution of the time-dependent Schrödinger-like equation
Eq. (
2
)
can be expressed in terms of a time evolution operator
Gómez Pueyo et al. 2018

\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}(t+{\rm d}t)\approx\exp\left[-i{\hat{H}}(t+\frac{{\rm d}t}{2}){\rm d}t\right]\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}(t),

(17)

where the approximation implies a small
{\rm d}t
.

In the first step, we use the Crank-Nicolson propagator with a predictor-corrector for only
\hat{H}^{0}(t)
. We essentially obtain

\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}^{0}(t+{\rm d}t)\approx\exp\left[-i{\hat{H}}^{0}(t+\frac{{\rm d}t}{2}){\rm d}t\right]\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}(t).

(18)

Plug
Eq. (
18
)
into
Eq. (
17
)
, we obtain

\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}(t+{\rm d}t)\approx\exp\left[-i{\hat{H}}^{\prime}(t+\frac{{\rm d}t}{2}){\rm d}t\right]\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}^{0}(t+{\rm d}t).

(19)

Note here we used the approximation
\exp\left[-i{\hat{H}}(t+\frac{{\rm d}t}{2}){\rm d}t\right]\approx\exp\left[-i{\hat{H}}^{0}(t+\frac{{\rm d}t}{2}){\rm d}t\right]\exp\left[-i{\hat{H}}^{\prime}(t+\frac{{\rm d}t}{2}){\rm d}t\right]
. Even though
\hat{H}^{\prime}
does not commute with
\hat{H}^{0}
, the cross terms are in the second and higher order of
{\rm d}t
. In the case of small
{\rm d}t
, the cross terms are negligible.

In the case of small
\hat{H}^{\prime}(t)
and small
{\rm d}t
, we can make the assumption that
{\hat{H}}^{\prime}(t+\frac{{\rm d}t}{2})\approx{\hat{H}}^{\prime}(t)
and Taylor expand
Eq. (
19
)
up to the first order

\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}(t+{\rm d}t)=\left[1-i{\hat{H}}^{\prime}(t){\rm d}t\right]\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm B}^{0}(t+{\rm d}t).

(20)

Therefore, as the second step, we use the first-order Taylor propagator to propagate the nonadiabatic correction. We tested this method for a Mg
8
cluster and achieved nearly identical result compared to propagating the whole Hamiltonian with the Crank-Nicolson propagator.

We note that when using
Eq. (
14
)
for clusters, the
k_{F}^{-4}({\mathbf{r}})
dependence of the second term can cause numerical instabilities in low density regions. In such case, a mask function such as
1-1/\{1+[n({\mathbf{r}})/n{\scriptscriptstyle\rm cutoff}]^{2}\}
can be applied to the second term to fix the numerical instabilities.

III
Computational Details

The TD-OFDFT calculations are performed with DFTPy
Shao et al. 2021
and the benchmark TD-DFT calculations are performed with embedded Quantum Espresso (eQE)
Genova et al. 2017
;
Mi et al. 2021
and its real-time TD-DFT implementation
Krishtal et al. 2015
. All calculations employ the same adiabatic Perdew-Zunger LDA
Perdew and Zunger 1981
as the XC functional and OEPP
Mi et al. 2016
pseudopotentials. The kinetic energy cutoff for the TD-DFT wavefunctions and the TD-OFDFT density are chosen to converge the ground state energy within 1 meV/atom. The oscillator strength is calculated using
\sigma(\omega)=-\omega\text{Im}\bigg[\delta\tilde{\mu}(\omega)\bigg]
, where
\delta\tilde{\mu}(\omega)
is the Fourier transform of the dipole moment change calculated at every time step of the propagation. The time step chosen is consistent with the plane wave cutoff employed and is
{\rm d}t=0.1
a.u. in all simulations, except for Ag, Si and GaAs simulations with the LMGP functional, where the time step is
{\rm d}t=0.01
a.u.

IV
Results and Discussion

We first present results for clusters of metallic systems (Mg, Na, Ag) and in a second step we discuss results for clusters of semiconducting systems (Si, GaAs).

IV.1
Benchmarking metallic systems

Previously
Shao et al. 2021

Our results showed that TFW is only in semiqualitative agreement with the TD-DFT benchmark. We remark that we take TD-DFT as benchmark here, because it formally has an exact Pauli potential. Figure

Because OFDFT does not have a notion of single-particle orbitals or bands, it is not surprising that several details in the optical spectra related to interband trasitions are missing in TD-OFDFT. Formally, these would be recovered by the nonadiabatic Pauli potential. However, in this work, we have developed a nonadiabatic potential which was derived from the uniform electron gas. Thus, it would simply be too much to ask from the uniform electron gas to also be able to reproduce interband transitions in GaAs. Overall, despite the mentioned flaws, the agreement between the TD-OFDFT and the experiment is better than semiquantitative.

V
Conclusion

We proposed and implemented a nonadiabatic (current-dependent) time-dependent Pauli potential for time-dependent orbital-free DFT (TD-OFDFT) simulations of electron dynamics in materials. The nonadiabatic part of the Pauli potential is responsible for major qualitative features in the optical spectrum because it bridges the dynamics of
N
noninteracting bosons with the one of
N
noninteracting electrons. The proposed potential derives from the frequency dependent dielectric function of the uniform electron gas.

We test our method on clusters of metallic elements (Na, Mg, Ag) and semiconductors (Si, GaAs). In most cases, we find our TD-OFDFT to be close to bechmark TD-DFT spectra. TD-DFT is chosen as benchmark because it relies on an exact description of the
N
-electron fermionic system (i.e., exact time-dependent Pauli potential). The study of Na clusters revealed that previous investigations bare of the nonadiabatic local Pauli potential enjoyed strong error cancellation between the absence of nonadiabaticity in the potential and errors in the dynamics from the adiabatic part of the potential (which we address employing the LMGP nonlocal kinetic energy functional). As expected, the method struggles in the quantitative description of features in the spectra attributed to interband excitations. However, even for such difficult cases as GaAs nanoparticles, the method captures semiquantitatively the optical spectra and overall spectral envelopes.

This work sets the stage for using time-dependent orbital-free methods for simulating the dynamics of complex systems for chemistry, energy applications and beyond.
