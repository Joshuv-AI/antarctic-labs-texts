Counting Restricted Dyck Paths Through Random Walks
EJ Infeld

Abstract
We show connection between Dyck paths with peaks of bounded height and random walks. The correspondence between a certain class of random walks and such Dyck paths allows us to develop a probabilistic perspective on Chebyshev polynomials. Keywords: Dyck paths, random walks

Introduction

A
Dyck path
of order
k
is a lattice path with end points
(0,0)
and
(2k,0)
, that consists of two kinds of steps: a step up,
(1,1)
and a step down
(1,-1)
. A
peak
of a Dyck path is the node formed at the joint of an up step and a down step that follows it.

Figure 1:
A Dyck path of order 6 with peaks of height 1, 3, 2, and 2 respectively.

The number of possible Dyck paths of order
k
is described by the
k
th Catalan number,
C_{k}=\frac{1}{k+1}{{2k}\choose k}
. The number
A(n,k)
of possible Dyck paths of order
k
that have peak height bounded by
n
is known to be described by the following generating function:
[
1
]

\displaystyle\underset{k\geq 0}{\sum}A(n,k)x^{k}=\frac{1}{\Large 1+z-\frac{z}{1+z-\frac{z}{\frac{\ddots}{1+z-\frac{z}{1+z-C(z)}}}}}\normalsize,

(1)

where the continued fraction has
n+1
layers and
C(z)
is the Catalan generating function. Note that the bound on the peak height is equivalent to the condition that in no initial segment the number of steps to the right minus the number of steps up exceeds
n
.

We will present a new and surprising way to count bounded Dyck paths through a property of random walks. In section 2 we discuss the random walk setup and probabilistic results that serve as the starting point. Section 3 is the overview of a calculation that takes us from the realm of probabilities to a suitable generating function. Section 4 presents how the resulting formula outputs the correct functions for some example values of
n
. We present our conclusions in section 5.

\displaystyle\Pi_{m,p}L_{(}p)=p+(\Pi_{m,p}-p)(L_{m-1}(p)+L_{m}(p)).

3
Calculation

We will now focus on obtaining a suitable form for
\Pi_{n+2,p}/p\times L_{n+2}(p)
as a function of
x=p(1-p)
. We will use the following notation:

•

g_{i}(p)=(1-p)^{i}-p^{i}
,

•

f_{i}(p)=\Pi_{i,p}/p=\frac{(1-p)^{i-1}-p^{i-1}}{(1-p)^{i}-p^{i}}=\frac{g_{i-1}(p)}{g_{i}(p)}
,

•

h_{i}(p)=\frac{g_{i}(p)}{g_{1}(p)}=\frac{g_{i}(p)}{1-2p}
,

It is worth noting that:

\displaystyle g_{i}(p)=g_{i}(p)(1-p+p)=g_{i+1}(p)+xg_{i-1}(p),

(9)

and that equation (
2
) is equivalent to:

\displaystyle L_{m}(p)=\Pi_{m,p}/p+L_{m-1}(p)(\Pi_{m,p}/p-1)

(10)

\displaystyle=f_{m}(p)+L_{m-1}(p)(f_{m}(p)-1).

(11)

By iterating the above relation we can discover that:

L_{m}(p)=f_{m}(p)+f_{m-1}(p)(f_{m}(p)-1)+

+f_{m-2}(p)(f_{m-1}(p)-1)(f_{m-2}(p)-1)+\dots+L_{2}(p)\underset{j=3}{\overset{m}{\prod}}(f_{j}(p)-1),

where
L_{2}(p)=1
. We can express this in a more transparent form as:

L_{m}(p)=\underset{i=2}{\overset{m}{\sum}}f_{i}(p)\underset{j=i+1}{\overset{m}{\prod}}(f_{j}(p)-1),

since
f_{2}(p)=L_{2}(p)=1.
Finally, since we have established that:

\displaystyle\underset{k=o}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}(p(1-p))^{k}(2k+1)A(m-2,k)=f_{m}(p)L_{m}(p),

(12)

we would like to express
f_{m}(p)L_{m}(p)
as a formal power series of
p(1-p)=x
.

\displaystyle f_{m}(p)L_{m}(p)=\frac{g_{m-1}(p)}{g_{m}(p)}\left[\underset{i=2}{\overset{m}{\sum}}\frac{g_{i-1}(p)}{g_{i}(p)}\underset{j=i+1}{\overset{m}{\prod}}(\frac{g_{j-1}(p)}{g_{j}(p)}-1)\right]

(13)

\displaystyle=\frac{g_{m-1}(p)}{g_{m}(p)}\left[\underset{i=2}{\overset{m}{\sum}}\frac{g_{i-1}(p)}{g_{i}(p)}\underset{j=i+1}{\overset{m}{\prod}}x\frac{g_{j-2}(p)}{g_{j}(p)}\right],

(14)

since
xg_{j-2}(p)=g_{j-1}(p)-g_{j}(p)
. This can be simplified to:

\displaystyle f_{m}(p)L_{m}(p)=\frac{1}{(g_{m}(p))^{2}}\underset{i=2}{\overset{m}{\sum}}x^{m-i}(g_{i-1}(p))^{2}=\frac{1}{(g_{m}(p))^{2}}\underset{i=1}{\overset{m-1}{\sum}}x^{m-i-1}(g_{i}(p))^{2}.

(15)

The rest of the calculation is as follows:

\displaystyle g_{i}(p)^{2}=(1-p)^{2i}+p^{2i}-x^{i}=x^{i}((\frac{1-p}{p})^{i})+(\frac{p}{1-p})^{i}-2),

(16)

\displaystyle\underset{i=1}{\overset{m-1}{\sum}}x^{m-i-1}(g_{i}(p))^{2}=\underset{i=1}{\overset{m-1}{\sum}}x^{m-1}\left[(\frac{1-p}{p})^{i}+(\frac{p}{1-p})^{i}-2\right]

(17)

\displaystyle=x^{m-1}\left[\frac{1-p}{p}\frac{1-(\frac{1-p}{p})^{m}-1}{1-\frac{1-p}{p}}+\frac{p}{1-p}\frac{1-(\frac{p}{1-p})^{m}-1}{1-\frac{p}{1-p}}-2(m-1)\right]

(18)

\displaystyle=x^{m-1}\left[\frac{1-p-(1-p)(\frac{1-p}{p})^{m}-1}{2p-1}+\frac{p-p(\frac{p}{1-p})^{m}-1}{1-2p}-2(m-1)\right]

(19)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{m-1}(1-2m)g_{1}(p)+(1-p)^{2m-1}-p^{2m-1}}{g_{1}(p)}=\frac{x^{m-1}(1-2m)g_{1}(p)+g_{2m-1}(p)}{g_{1}(p)}.

(20)

We can therefore conclude that:

\displaystyle f_{m}(p)L_{m}(p)=\frac{x^{m-1}(1-2m)g_{1}(p)+g_{2m-1}(p)}{g_{m}(p)^{2}g_{1}(p)}

(21)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{m-1}(1-2m)/g_{1}(p)^{2}+(g_{2m-1}(p)/g_{1}(p))/g_{1}(p)^{2}}{(g_{m}(p)/g_{1}(p))^{2}}

(22)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{m-1}(1-2m)+h_{2m-1}(p)}{g_{1}(p)^{2}h_{m}(p)^{2}}.

(23)

Finally, note that
g_{1}(p)=(1-2p)^{2}=1-4p+4p^{2}=1-4x
, and that since
h_{1}(p)=h_{2}(p)=1
, and
h_{m}(p)=h_{m-1}(p)-xh_{m-2}(p)
for
m\geq 3

H(x)=h(p)

•

H_{1}(x)=H_{2}(x)=1

•

For
m\geq 3
,
H_{m}(x)=H_{m-1}(x)-xH_{m-2}(x),

and:

\displaystyle f_{m}(p)L_{m}(p)=\frac{x^{m-1}(1-2m)+H_{2m-1}(x)}{(1-4x)H_{m}(x)^{2}}.

(24)

Therefore, for
A(n,k)
the number of Dyck paths of order
k
and height no more than

\displaystyle\underset{k\geq 0}{\sum}A(n,k)(2k+1)x^{k}=\frac{x^{n+1}(-2n-3)+H_{2n+1}(x)}{(1-4x)H_{n+2}(x)^{2}}.

(25)

This result is equivalent to the continued fraction form that is known, but the probability viewpoint is novel. The factor of
2k+1
could be removed by integrating both sides, but the formula on the right would lose much of its simplicity. We prioritize efficient use of computational resources and so propose that the factor should be left as is.

4
Examples

Let us check this expression for some small numbers. Note that
f_{2}(p)L_{2}(p)=0+1=1
, and in fact the only Dyck path of height 0 is the empty path.

4.1
Paths of height 1

For any positive length, there is only one path of height 1, and so we are hoping to obtain:

\displaystyle f_{3}(p)L_{3}(p)=\underset{k\geq 0}{\sum}(2k+1)x^{k}.

(26)

We do it as follows. From

\displaystyle f_{3}(p)L_{3}(p)=\frac{x^{2}(-5)+H_{5}(x)}{(1-4x)H_{3}(x)^{2}}

(27)

we find that
H_{3}(x)^{2}=(1-x)^{2}
, and
H_{5}(x)=1-3x+x^{2}
.

Since
H_{5}(x)-5x^{2}=1-3x-4x^{2}=(1-4x)(1+x)
, we conclude that:

\displaystyle f_{3}(p)L_{3}(p)=\frac{1+x}{(1-x)^{2}}=\underset{k\geq 0}{\sum}(2k+1)x^{k}.

(28)

4.2
Paths of height at most 2

Similarly, we can check this formula for Dyck paths of heights no more than 2 as follows:

\displaystyle f_{4}(p)L_{4}(p)=\frac{x^{2}(-7)+H_{7}(x)}{(1-4x)H_{4}(x)^{2}}=\frac{1-5x+6x^{2}-8x^{3}}{(1-4x)(1-2x)^{2}}=\frac{1-x+2x^{2}}{(1-2x)^{2}}

(29)

\displaystyle=(1-x+2x^{2})\underset{k\geq 0}{\sum}(k+1)(2x)^{k}=1+\underset{k\geq 1}{\sum}(2k+1)2^{k-1}x^{k}.

(30)

To see that this is the generating function we are looking for, consider such path of some order
k\geq 1
. This path can touch ’ground’ after any even number of steps, and necessarily touches ground after 0 and after
2k
steps. There are
k-1
spots where it could touch ground or not. And in fact for any subset of these, there is a unique Dyck path of length
2k
of height at most 2. Therefore there are
2^{k-1}
such paths of order
k
.

Conclusions

We found a generating function that describes the number of Dyck paths of order
k

n

25
). An iterative method to count these already existed, but we believe that the random walk connection is of interest.
