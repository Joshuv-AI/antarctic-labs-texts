Abstract
We study the limit in low intensity of Poisson–Voronoi tessellations in hyperbolic spaces ℍ d \mathbb{H}_{d} for d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 . In contrast to the Euclidean setting, a limiting nontrivial ideal tessellation 𝒱 d \mathcal{V}_{d} appears as the intensity tends to 0 0 . The tessellation 𝒱 d \mathcal{V}_{d} is a natural, isometry-invariant decomposition of ℍ d \mathbb{H}_{d} into countably many unbounded polytopes, each with a unique end. We study its basic properties, in particular, the geometric features of its cells.

Introduction

Voronoi diagrams go back to Descartes. Their uses span the sciences, social sciences, and engineering.
Poisson–Voronoi tessellations are ubiquitous objects in stochastic geometry
[
Møl94
, Chapter 4]
. They have been used to model real-world networks (see
[
BB+10
, Section 19.3.2]
for an introduction and
[
BBK05
]
for a concrete application) and have also been studied for their purely theoretical properties and their intrinsic beauty.
The recent works
[
Bhu19
,
BCP22
]
independently study properties of Poisson–Voronoi tessellations in hyperbolic space in the limit when the intensity of their nuclei tends to 0. Both these works also mention without proof the existence of a limiting tessellation. Here, we prove existence of this limit and study its fundamental properties. The existence of a nontrivial limit is surprising when one’s intuition is grounded in Euclidean space.

Ideal Poisson–Voronoi tessellations.

Let
(E,d_{E},\mathbf{o},\mu)
be an abstract, locally compact metric space equipped with an origin point,
\mathbf{o}
, and an infinite Radon measure,
\mu
, such that the spheres centered at
\mathbf{o}
have measure
0
. For
\lambda>0
, we consider a Poisson cloud of points
\mathbf{X}^{(\lambda)}=(X^{{(\lambda)}}_{1},X^{{(\lambda)}}_{2},\ldots)
with intensity
\lambda\cdot\mu
(the points being ranked by their increasing distances to the origin of
E
). This point process enables us to define the
Voronoi diagram

\mathrm{Vor}(\mathbf{X}^{(\lambda)})\coloneqq(C_{1},C_{2},\ldots)

relative to
\mathbf{X}^{(\lambda)}
, which is a tiling of
E
where the tile (or cell)
C_{i}
is made of the points of
E
that are closer (in the weak sense) to
X_{i}^{(\lambda)}
than to any other
X_{j}^{(\lambda)}
. When the underlying space
E
is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with Lebesgue measure (hence having polynomial volume growth), the diagrams
\mathrm{Vor}(\mathbf{X}^{(\lambda)})
degenerate towards the trivial tiling
(E)
when the intensity
\lambda
tends to
0
. However, if the underlying space has exponential growth, for example on
d
-regular metric trees with
d\geq 3
equipped with the length measure, or on
d
-dimensional hyperbolic spaces
d\geq 2
equipped with their volume measure, then
\mathrm{Vor}(\mathbf{X}^{(\lambda)})
may converge in distribution as
\lambda\to 0
to a nontrivial random tiling, which we name the ideal Poisson–Voronoi tessellation. We refer to Section
2
, and in particular
2.3
for details of the convergence and to
Section

3.1
for background on hyperbolic spaces.

Ideal Poisson–Voronoi tessellations on hyperbolic spaces
\mathbb{H}_{d}
.

In particular, our general convergence result,
2.3
, applies when
(E,d_{E},\mathbf{o},\mu)
is the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}_{d}
equipped with its volume measure and yields:

Theorem 1.1

(
Convergence of low-intensity tessellations
)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}_{d}^{{(\lambda)}}
be a Poisson point process with intensity
\lambda>0
on the
d
-dimensional hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}_{d}
with
d\geq 2
. Then we have the convergence in law

\mathrm{Vor}\big(\mathbf{X}_{d}^{{(\lambda)}}\big)\Rightarrow{}\mathcal{V}_{d}.

The limiting tessellation
\mathcal{V}_{d}
is called the
ideal Poisson–Voronoi tessellation
of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
; see
Fig.

1.1
.

Figure 1.1
:
From
[
BCP22
]

\mathcal{V}_{2}
, the

By
tessellation
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\mathbb{H}_{d}
, we mean a locally finite collection of nonempty, closed sets (called “tiles” or “cells”) that are (geodesically) convex, have nonempty and pairwise disjoint interiors, and that cover the space. The cells are then (possibly unbounded) polytopes, i.e., each cell is the convex hull of a locally finite set of points (its vertices)—equivalently, each cell is the intersection of a locally finite set of closed half-spaces (see, e.g.,
[
SW08
, Lemma 10.1.1]
for tessellations in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; the same follows for tessellations in
\mathbb{H}_{d}
by using the Beltrami–Klein model, where every complete, totally geodesic submanifold of dimension
k
is the intersection of the Euclidean unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{d}
with an affine Euclidean plane of dimension
k

[
Rat19
, Section 6.3]
). The faces of a cell are the intersections of the cells with one or more of its supporting (geodesic) hyperplanes. In our case, the intersection of two cells will always be a face of each cell, or empty; such a tessellation is called
face-to-face
. Furthermore, in our case, a
k
-face will always be the intersection of
d-k+1
cells, but no fewer and no more; such a tessellation is called
normal
.

The limiting tessellation
\mathcal{V}_{d}
can be constructed from a Poisson process of points on the boundary of the
d
-dimensional hyperbolic space,
\partial\mathbb{H}_{d}
, cross the real numbers, the second coordinates of which we call
delays
. Equivalently, this product is the space of horospheres in
\mathbb{H}_{d}
. In coordinates, we provide the following explicit descriptions of
\mathcal{V}_{d}
as a multiplicatively weighted Voronoi diagram, where
\mathrm{Leb}
denotes Lebesgue measure on the appropriate spaces,
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}\coloneqq\partial\mathbb{B}_{d}
, and
|\,\cdot\,|
denotes the Euclidean norm:

Theorem 1.2

(
Coordinate description of
\mathcal{V}_{d}
)
.

For the unit ball model of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
, let
N
be a Poisson point process with intensity
\mathrm{Unif}\otimes\mathrm{Leb}
on
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, while for the upper half-space model of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
, let
N
be a Poisson point process with intensity
\mathrm{Leb}\otimes\mathrm{Leb}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
. In both models, the ideal Poisson–Voronoi tessellation
\mathcal{V}_{d}
consists of the cells
\bigl\{C(\theta,r)\,;\;(\theta,r)\in N\bigr\}
defined by

C(\theta,r)\coloneqq\bigl\{z\,;\;r^{1/2(d-1)}|z-\theta|\leq s^{1/2(d-1)}|z-\psi|\mbox{ for all }(\psi,s)\in N\bigr\}.

(1.1)

This enables us to study the stochastic properties of
\mathcal{V}_{d}
directly. In particular, we prove that
\mathcal{V}_{d}
is a natural cell decomposition of
{\mathbb{H}_{d}}
in the following sense:

Theorem 1.3

(
Tiles have one end
)
.

Almost surely,

•

\mathcal{V}_{d}
is a tessellation of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
;

•

each tile of
\mathcal{V}_{d}
is unbounded with an infinite number of bounded faces and with a unique limit point on the ideal boundary of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
, which we refer to as its
end
;

•

\mathcal{V}_{d}
is face-to-face and normal; and

•

the law of
\mathcal{V}_{d}
is invariant under every isometry of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
.

In particular, in dimension
2
, the union
\partial\mathcal{V}_{2}
of the boundaries of the tiles is a random embedding in
\mathbb{H}_{2}
of the 3-regular tree with geodesic edges whose law is invariant under isometries of the hyperbolic plane.

The last bullet point of Theorem
1.3
follows from the Möbius invariance of the hyperbolic measure, hence of the law of the Poisson cloud
\mathbf{X}_{d}^{{(\lambda)}}
for each
\lambda>0
. The third point is also classical in stochastic geometry and is in particular a well-known fact for standard Poisson–Voronoi tessellations in Euclidean space; see
[
SW08
, Theorems 10.2.1 and 10.2.3]
.

2
Abstract convergence results

In this section, we give an abstract point of view on the convergence of Voronoi diagrams based on the concept of the Gromov boundary of a metric space
(E,d)
. The main idea is that building a Voronoi diagram requires us to compare only
differences of distances
, rather than actual distances. In particular, the forthcoming Theorem
2.3
is purely deterministic and could be applied to many different spaces.

2.1
Gromov boundary and ideal Voronoi diagrams

Let
(E,d)
be a locally and boundedly compact metric space. The set
C(E)
of real-valued continuous functions on
E
is endowed with the topology of uniform convergence on every compact subset of
E
. We define an equivalence relation on
C(E)
by declaring two functions equivalent if they differ by an additive constant; the associated quotient space endowed with the quotient topology is denoted by
C(E)/\mathbb{R}
. Following
[
Gro81
]
, one can embed the original space
E
in
C(E)/\mathbb{R}
using the injection

i\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\begin{array}[]{ccccc}E&\longrightarrow&{C}(E)&\longrightarrow&{C}(E)/\mathbb{R}\\
x&\longmapsto&d_{x}\coloneqq d(x,\cdot)&\longmapsto&\overline{d_{x}}\,.\end{array}

The
Gromov compactification
of
E
is then the closure of
i(E)
in
C(E)/\mathbb{R}
. The
Gromov boundary
or
horoboundary

\partial E
of
E
is composed of the points in the closure of
i(E)
in
C(E)/\mathbb{R}
that are not already in
i(E)
. The points in
\partial E
are called
horofunctions
; see
[
Gro81
]
. They are obtained as limits of shifted distance functions
d(x_{n},\cdot)-d(x_{n},\mathbf{o})
for some sequence of points
x_{n}\to\infty
.
Let us denote a point on the Gromov boundary by
\theta
and fix the associated representative function
\mathrm{d}_{\theta}(\cdot)
so that
\mathrm{d}_{\theta}(\mathbf{o})=0
for all
\theta\in\partial E
. In particular, if
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\partial E
and
x\in E
, one can make sense of the “difference of distances”

\mathrm{d}_{\theta}(x)-\mathrm{d}_{\theta^{\prime}}(x)\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.1)

We will then enhance the Gromov boundary by the addition of a second coordinate, real numbers which we call the
delays
, yielding the
extended ideal boundary
,
\widehat{\partial E}\coloneqq\partial E\times\mathbb{R}
. Extending the preceding display, if
x\in E
and
(\theta,\delta),(\theta^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\in\widehat{\partial E}
are two extended ideal points, we say that
x
is
closer
to
(\theta,\delta)
than to
(\theta^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})
if

\mathrm{d}_{\theta}(x)-\mathrm{d}_{\theta^{\prime}}(x)\leq\delta^{\prime}-\delta.

Letting

\mathrm{d}\bigl(x,(\theta,\delta)\bigr)\coloneqq\mathrm{d}_{\theta}(x)+\delta,

which we call the
linear separation
of
x
from
(\theta,\delta)
and which is real valued, the condition becomes
\mathrm{d}\bigl(x,(\theta,\delta)\bigr)\leq\mathrm{d}\bigl(x,(\theta^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\bigr)
.

The level sets of a horofunction
\mathrm{d}_{\theta}
are called
horospheres
at
\theta
. A horosphere is thus a limit of spheres in the sense that
\mathrm{d}_{\theta}(x)=\delta
iff there are spheres containing
x
centered at points
x_{n}\to\theta
with radii
d(x_{n},\mathbf{o})+\delta_{n}
and
\delta_{n}\to\delta
. The horosphere
\mathrm{d}_{\theta}^{-1}\bigl[\{\delta\}\bigr]
is also the zero set of
\mathrm{d}\bigl(\cdot,(\theta,-\delta)\bigr)
. If
\phi
is an isometry of
(E,d)
, then
\phi
acts naturally on
\partial E
mapping one horosphere to another in the following way, as shown from the definition: for all
x\in E
,

\mathrm{d}_{\phi(\theta)}\bigl(\phi(x)\bigr)=\mathrm{d}_{\theta}(x)-\mathrm{d}_{\theta}\bigl(\phi^{-1}(\mathbf{o})\bigr).

(2.2)

Hence, if we write

\phi(\theta,\delta)\coloneqq\bigl(\phi(\theta),\delta+\mathrm{d}_{\theta}(\phi^{-1}(\mathbf{o}))\bigr),

(2.3)

then we obtain an action of
\phi
on the space of horospheres,
\widehat{\partial E}
, that preserves linear separation:

\forall x\in E\kern 5.0pt\forall(\theta,\delta)\in\widehat{\partial E}\quad\mathrm{d}\bigl(\phi(x),\phi(\theta,\delta)\bigr)=\mathrm{d}\bigl(x,(\theta,\delta)\bigr).

(2.4)

(Ideal) Voronoi diagrams.

Let us first recall the basic definition of Voronoi diagrams. Fix a sequence of distinct points
(x_{i}\,;\;i\geq 1)\in E
, called the
nuclei
. We will always suppose that the nuclei
x_{i}
are ranked by their increasing distances to the origin of
E
and that
d(\mathbf{o},x_{i})\to\infty
. The
Voronoi diagram

\mathrm{Vor}(x_{i}\,;\;i\geq 1)=(C_{i}\,;\;i\geq 1)
is defined via its
tiles

C_{i}
associated to
x_{i}
via

C_{i}\coloneqq\left\{w\in E\,;\;\forall j\geq 1\ d(w,x_{i})\leq d(w,x_{j})\right\}.

We also define the “open” version of the tiles denoted by
C_{i}^{\circ}
when the weak inequality is replaced by a strict one. Notice that the
C_{i}^{\circ}
are disjoint; since we imposed that
d(\mathbf{o},x_{j})\to\infty
as
j\to\infty
, it follows that
C_{i}^{\circ}
are indeed open sets.

The framework above enables us to define diagrams using nuclei
that are not points of
E
but extended ideal points of
\widehat{\partial E}
. More precisely, if
\boldsymbol{\theta}\coloneqq(\theta_{i}\,;\;i\geq 1)
is a sequence of boundary points on
\partial E
and
\boldsymbol{\delta}\coloneqq(\delta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\,;\;i\geq 1)
is an increasing sequence of reals tending to
\infty
, we define the (ideal) Voronoi diagram associated to
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\delta})
as follows:

Definition 2.1

(
Ideal Voronoi diagrams
)
.

The Voronoi diagram
\mathrm{Vor}\bigl((\theta_{i},\delta_{i})\,;\;{i\geq 1}\bigr)
is given by its tiles
(C_{i}\,;\;i\geq 1)
, where

C_{i}\coloneqq\bigl\{x\in{E}\,;\;\forall j\neq i\kern 5.0pt\mathrm{d}\bigl(x,(\theta_{i},\delta_{i})\bigr)\leq\mathrm{d}\bigl(x,(\theta_{j},\delta_{j})\bigr)\bigr\},

and similarly with
C_{i}^{\circ}
defined via a strict inequality.

We say that the diagram is
nondegenerate
if
\overline{C_{i}^{\circ}}=C_{i}
for every
i\geq 1
. Notice that since we impose that
\delta_{i}\to\infty
, every compact
K\subset E
intersects only a finite number of tiles of the diagram, and the tiles
C_{i}^{\circ}
are again open. Also, the diagram is invariant under a shift of all delays
(\delta_{i}\,;\;i\geq 0)
because it depends only on the differences of linear separations.

2.2
Convergence of diagrams

As we will see, the concept of Gromov boundary and ideal diagrams is well suited for studying
convergence
of standard Voronoi diagrams.
We will use the
Fell topology
on the collection of closed subsets of
E
, which is generated by the sets
\{F\,;\;F\cap C=\varnothing\}
for compact
C\subset E
and the sets
\{F\,;\;F\cap G\neq\varnothing\}
for open
G\subseteq E
. This topology makes the collection of closed subsets compact, while if the empty set is omitted, then the collection becomes locally compact (see
[
SW08
, Section 12.2]
).

Definition 2.2

(
Convergence of diagrams
)
.

We will say that the sequence of diagrams
\bigl((C_{i}^{(k)}\,;\;i\geq 1)\bigr)_{k\geq 1}
converges to
(C_{i}\,;\;i\geq 1)
as
k\to\infty
if for each
i\geq 1
, the closed subsets
C_{i}^{(k)}
converge to the closed subset
C_{i}
in the Fell topology.

The following lemma, roughly speaking, entails the continuity of the Voronoi-diagram mapping with respect to the convergence of nuclei towards the extended Gromov boundary,
\widehat{\partial E}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose that for each
k
, we have a sequence of points
(x_{i}^{(k)}\,;\;i\geq 1)
ranked by increasing distances to
\mathbf{o}
on a space
(E,d)
that together satisfy the following three conditions:

(i)

(Convergence to the boundary)
For all
i\geq 1
, we have convergence in the Gromov sense

x_{i}^{(k)}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}\theta_{i}\in\partial E.

(ii)

(Convergence of proto-delays)
For all
i\geq 1
, we have

d(x_{i}^{(k)},\mathbf{o})-d(x_{1}^{(k)},\mathbf{o})\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}\delta_{i}.

Furthermore,
\delta_{i}\to\infty
as
i\to\infty
.

(iii)

(Nondegeneracy)

\mathrm{Vor}\bigl((\theta_{i},\delta_{i})\,;\;{i\geq 1}\bigr)
is nondegenerate.

Then the Voronoi diagrams
\mathrm{Vor}(x_{i}^{(k)}\,;\;\allowbreak i\geq 1)
converge as
k\to\infty
to the ideal Voronoi diagram
\mathrm{Vor}\bigl((\theta_{i},\delta_{i})\,;\;{i\geq 1}\bigr)
.

Remark 2.4

(Degenerate cases)
.

To see that the third condition in the lemma is needed, suppose that
E=[0,\infty)
, so that
\partial E
consists of a single point,
\theta
. Then
x_{i}^{(k)}\to\theta
as soon as
d(x_{i}^{(k)},\mathbf{o})\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
. If
d(x_{1}^{(k)},\mathbf{o})<d(x_{2}^{(k)},\mathbf{o})
for each
k
and the limiting delays satisfy
0=\delta_{1}=\delta_{2}<\delta_{3}\leq\cdots
, then the tiles
C_{i}
of the limiting degenerate ideal diagram

\mathrm{Vor}\bigl((\theta,\delta_{i})\,;\;i\geq 1\bigr)
are
C_{1}=C_{2}=E
and
C_{i}=\varnothing
for
i\geq 3
, yet
C_{2}^{(k)}\to\varnothing
as
k\to\infty
.

Proof.

Denote by
C_{i}^{(k)}
(resp.,
C_{i}^{\circ,(k)}
) the closed (resp., open) tiles of
\mathrm{Vor}(x_{i}^{(k)}\,;\;i\geq 1)
and by
C_{i}
(resp.,
C_{i}^{\circ}
) those of
\mathrm{Vor}\bigl((\theta_{i},\delta_{i})\,;\;{i\geq 1}\bigr)
. Since the space of closed subsets of
E
endowed with the Fell topology is itself compact, we can suppose up to passing to a subsequence that we have

C_{i}^{(k)}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}\mathfrak{C}_{i}

in the Fell topology for some closed subset
\mathfrak{C}_{i}
, and our goal is now to show that
\mathfrak{C}_{i}=C_{i}
.
We define the proto-horofunctions

\mathrm{d}_{x_{i}^{(k)}}(z)\coloneqq d(x_{i}^{(k)},z)-d(x_{i}^{(k)},\mathbf{o}),

as well as the proto-delays
\delta_{i}^{(k)}\coloneqq d(x_{i}^{(k)},\mathbf{o})-d(x_{1}^{(k)},\mathbf{o})
. A given point
z\in E
thus belongs to
C_{i}^{(k)}
iff for all
j\neq i
, we have the inequality

d(x_{i}^{(k)},z)-d(x_{j}^{(k)},z)=\mathrm{d}_{x_{i}^{(k)}}(z)-\mathrm{d}_{x_{j}^{(k)}}(z)+\delta_{i}^{(k)}-\delta_{j}^{(k)}\leq 0,

(2.5)

and similarly for
C_{i}^{\circ,(k)}
with a strict inequality.
By our first two assumptions, the function of
z
in the last display converges uniformly on compact sets towards

\mathrm{d}_{\theta_{i}}(\cdot)-\mathrm{d}_{\theta_{j}}(\cdot)+\delta_{i}-\delta_{j}.

It follows immediately that we have
\mathfrak{C}_{i}=\lim_{k\to\infty}C_{i}^{(k)}\subseteq C_{i}
: indeed, if
z\in\lim_{k\to\infty}C_{i}^{{(k)}}
, then there are
z_{i}^{(k)}\in C_{i}^{(k)}
satisfying (
2.5
) with
z_{i}^{(k)}\to z
, so that passing (
2.5
) to the limit, we deduce that
z\in C_{i}
. For the other inclusion, we show that
C_{i}^{\circ}\subset\mathfrak{C}_{i}
and use the non-degeneracy
\overline{C_{i}^{\circ}}=C_{i}
to conclude. If
z\in C_{i}^{\circ}
, then for every fixed
j\neq i
, (
2.5
) holds with strict inequality for all large
k
, whence
z\in C_{i}^{(k)}
for all large
k
. This gives
z\in\mathfrak{C}_{i}
, as desired.
∎

3
Ideal Poisson–Voronoi tessellations on hyperbolic spaces

As mentioned in the introduction, the limit in low intensity of Poisson–Voronoi tessellations in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is trivial. This comes from the fact that, although the Gromov boundary of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is nontrivial (it is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}
), the polynomial growth of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
imposes that the difference of distances to
\mathbf{o}
(the delays) of the first two closest points in a PPP with intensity
\lambda
tends to
\infty
in probability as
\lambda\to 0
. Indeed, it is easy to convince oneself that superpolynomial growth is required to get tight delays as
\lambda\to 0
. A natural choice of such a space is the
d
-dimensional hyperbolic space,
\mathbb{H}_{d}
.

3.1
Background on hyperbolic spaces

For the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}_{d}
with dimension
d\geq 2
, we will use the model of the open unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{d}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,;\;|x|<1\}
equipped with the metric
2|dx|/\bigl(1-|x|^{2}\bigr)
, which leads to the

\mbox{distance}\quad(x,y)\mapsto 2\arcsinh{\frac{|x-y|}{\sqrt{(1-|x|^{2})(1-|y|^{2})}}}\quad\mbox{and measure}\quad\Bigl(\frac{2}{1-|x|^{2}}\Bigr)^{d}\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{B}_{d}}\cdot\mathrm{Leb},

and the model of the upper half-space
\mathbb{U}_{d}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
equipped with the metric
|dx|/x_{d}
, where
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d})
, which leads to the

\mbox{distance}\quad(x,y)\mapsto 2\arcsinh{\frac{|x-y|}{2\sqrt{x_{d}\,y_{d}}}}\quad\mbox{and measure}\quad\frac{1}{x_{d}^{d}}\mathbf{1}_{x_{d}>0}\cdot\mathrm{Leb},

where
\mathrm{Leb}
denotes the usual Lebesgue measure; see
[
Sto16
, pp. 9, 11, 13, 14]
.
We will write respectively
\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}
and
\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}
for the hyperbolic distance and measure. The origin of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
will be denoted by
\mathbf{o}
, meaning the center of the ball or
(0,0,\ldots,1)
in
\mathbb{U}_{d}
.

The isometries of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
form the group
\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}
of Möbius transformations of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
.
An isometric mapping from
\mathbb{B}_{d}
onto
\mathbb{U}_{d}
is given by the generalized Cayley transform, which is the diffeomorphism
\kappa\colon\mathbb{B}_{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{U}_{d}
defined by

\displaystyle\kappa(z)

\displaystyle=x\coloneqq\frac{1}{z_{1}^{2}+\dots+z_{d-1}^{2}+(z_{d}-1)^{2}}\big(2z_{1},\dots,2z_{d-1},1-|z|^{2}\big),

\displaystyle\kappa^{-1}(x)

\displaystyle=z\coloneqq\frac{1}{x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{d-1}^{2}+(x_{d}+1)^{2}}\big(2x_{1},\dots,2x_{d-1},|x|^{2}-1\big)

for
z\in\mathbb{B}_{d}
and
x\in\mathbb{U}_{d}

[
Lee18
, p. 66]
. If
\mathrm{rf}
denotes reflection in the plane
x_{d}=0
, then the maps
z\mapsto\kappa\bigl(\mathrm{rf}(z)\bigr)
and
x\mapsto\mathrm{rf}\bigl(\kappa^{-1}(x)\bigr)
are both restrictions of inversion in the sphere of radius
\sqrt{2}
centered at
(0,0,\dots,-1)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
; see
[
Sto16
, Exercise 2.4.6(a)]
.
The maps
\kappa
and
\kappa^{-1}
extend continuously to the boundaries where
|z|=1
and
x_{d}=0
or
x=\infty
, yielding
the stereographic projection from the north pole onto the plane containing the equator,
\mathrm{Ste}\colon\mathbb{S}_{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, and its inverse.
The image of the uniform measure on
\mathbb{S}_{d}
by
\mathrm{Ste}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
is given by:

Lemma 3.1
.

The image of the uniform measure on
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}
by the stereographic projection
\mathrm{Ste}\colon\mathbb{S}_{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
is the stereographic law given by

\frac{1}{c_{d}\bigl(1+|x|^{2}\bigr)^{d-1}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{{c_{d}}\bigl(1+\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}x_{i}^{2}\bigr)^{d-1}}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\,\mathrm{d}x_{d-1},

where
c_{d}
is as in (
1.2
).

Proof.

This can be proved by a tedious but elementary calculation or by the explicit expression of the metric tensor on the sphere
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}
in stereographic coordinates in
[
Lee18
, Equation 3.7]
.
A third proof uses the hyperbolic Poisson kernels, since
\mathrm{Ste}
is merely the extension to the boundaries of the isometry
\kappa
; for such a proof, see
[
Sto16
, Exercise 5.7.15]
.
∎

3.2
The ideal tessellation
\mathcal{V}_{d}

Below we prove a slightly stronger version of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 3.2
.

The Poisson–Voronoi tessellation of
(X_{i}^{(\lambda)}\,;\;i\geq 1)
converges in distribution as
\lambda\downarrow 0
towards the nondegenerate ideal Voronoi diagram
\mathrm{Vor}(\boldsymbol{\Theta},\mathbf{D})
where
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=(\Theta_{1},\ldots)
are i.i.d. uniform angles over
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}=\partial\mathbb{B}_{d}
and
\mathbf{D}=(D_{i}\,;\;i\geq 1)
is such that
(\frac{c_{d}}{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{(d-1)D_{i}})_{i\geq 1}
is a homogeneous Poisson point process (PPP) on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
of unit intensity. The process
\mathbf{D}
is independent of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
.

We call
\mathrm{Vor}(\boldsymbol{\Theta},\mathbf{D})
the
ideal Poisson–Voronoi tessellation
of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
and denote it by
\mathcal{V}_{d}
; we still need to prove that it is a tessellation, which we will do in
Section

3.4.2
when we prove
1.3
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{X}^{(\lambda)}=(X_{i}^{(\lambda)}\,\,;\;\,i\geq 1)
be a PPP of intensity
1
1

1

The normalization of the intensity as
\lambda^{d-1}
is here to ensure that the closest point to
\mathbf{o}
in
\mathbf{X}^{(\lambda)}
is roughly at distance
\log(1/\lambda)
as
\lambda\to 0
for every
d\geq 2
.

\lambda^{d-1}\cdot\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}
on the hyperbolic space
{\mathbb{H}_{d}}
, where as usual the points are ranked by increasing hyperbolic distance to the origin,
\mathbf{o}
. In particular, almost surely
\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}(\mathbf{o},X_{i}^{(\lambda)})
is strictly increasing in
i\geq 1
. Our goal is to prove convergence of the corresponding Voronoi tessellations towards a limiting ideal diagram as
\lambda\to 0
. To apply Theorem
2.3
, one needs to check convergence of points towards the ideal boundary and convergence of proto-delays. Both turn out to be very easy:

Convergence of proto-delays.

By the mapping theorem for Poisson processes
[
Kle20
, Theorem 24.16]
, it is simple to see that as
\lambda\to 0
, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}(\mathbf{o},X_{1}^{(\lambda)})}{|\!\log\lambda|}\xrightarrow[\lambda\downarrow 0]{(\mathbb{P})}1,

and so it is natural to introduce the shifted distances, i.e., the
proto-delays
, as

D_{i}^{\left(\lambda\right)}\coloneqq\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}(\mathbf{o},X_{i}^{(\lambda)})-\log(1/\lambda),\quad i\geq 1.

(3.1)

The mapping theorem for Poisson processes readily entails that,
as
\lambda\downarrow 0
, the set of increasing proto-delays
(D_{i}^{(\lambda)})_{i\geq 1}
converges in law to the increasing points
(D_{i})_{i\geq 1}
of a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}
with intensity measure

c_{d}\,\mathrm{e}^{(d-1)s}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Equivalently, the set
(\frac{c_{d}}{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{(d-1)D_{i}})_{i\geq 1}
is a rate-1 homogeneous Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
These statements can be proved via the following simple calculation. Recall that the volume growth function for hyperbolic space is given by

f_{d}({u})\coloneqq\mathrm{Vol}_{{\mathbb{H}_{d}}}\big(B_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}(\mathbf{o},{u})\big)=\Omega_{d}\int_{0}^{{u}}\left(\sinh{\rho}\right)^{d-1}\;\mathrm{d}\rho,

where
\Omega_{d}\coloneqq 2\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}
is the Euclidean volume of
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}
. A straightforward calculation shows that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\lambda^{d-1}\left(f_{d}\Bigl(x+\log{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\Bigr)-f_{d}\Bigl(y+\log{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\Bigr)\right)=\frac{2^{1-d}}{d-1}\Omega_{d}\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{(d-1)x}-\mathrm{e}^{(d-1)y}\bigr),

whence both claims follow from the mapping theorem for Poisson processes.

Convergence to ideal points.

Let us return to the Poisson point process of nuclei
(X_{i}^{(\lambda)}\,;\;i\geq 1)
and adopt the ball model
\mathbb{B}_{d}
for hyperbolic space. By rotational symmetry, it is clear that conditionally on the distance process
\bigl(\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}(\mathbf{o},X_{i}^{(\lambda)})\,;\;i\geq 1\bigr)
, or equivalently conditionally on the proto-delays, the angles
(\Theta_{i}^{(\lambda)}\,;\;i\geq 1)
of the points
X_{i}^{(\lambda)}
in the ball model
\mathbb{B}_{d}
are i.i.d. uniform over
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}
. It is well known that the Gromov boundary of
\mathbb{B}_{d}
is
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}
, or equivalently that a divergent sequence of points
x_{i}\in\mathbb{B}_{d}
with angles
\theta_{i}
converges to
\theta\in\partial\mathbb{B}_{d}=\mathbb{S}_{d-1}
if and only if
x_{i}\to\infty
and
\theta_{i}\to\theta
(see

[
BH13
, Example 8.11, page 265]
).

We can now conclude the proof of the theorem. By the Skorokhod embedding theorem, we can couple, on the same probability space, all the Poisson point processes
(X_{i}^{(\lambda)}\,;\;i\geq 1)
for
\lambda>0
in such a way that
for every
i\geq 1
, the proto-delays and the angles converge almost surely:
D_{i}^{(\lambda)}\to D_{i}
and
\Theta_{i}^{(\lambda)}\to\Theta_{i}
. In particular,
D_{i}\to\infty
almost surely and the convergence of angles implies the convergence towards the Gromov boundary. Using the fact that all
\Theta_{i}
and
D_{i}
have continuous distributions, it is an easy matter to check that the limiting ideal diagrams are a.s. nondegenerate. We can then apply
2.3
to get the desired convergence. ∎

3.3
Computing the separations

Having identified the limiting tessellation, we now aim to look more closely into its properties. The final piece needed to prove Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
is a concrete way of computing distances from extended ideal points. For this, we will see that it is more practical to first perform a change of variable and consider the exponentials of the delays.

Recall the definition of an extended ideal point
(\theta,\delta)\in\widehat{\partial\mathbb{H}_{d}}=\partial\mathbb{H}_{d}\times\mathbb{R}
where
\theta
is a boundary point and
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
is a delay. It will be convenient in the rest of the manuscript to consider the following equivalent description after taking the exponentials of the delays: Introduce
\widetilde{\partial\mathbb{H}_{d}}\coloneqq\partial\mathbb{H}_{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, which we call the
corona
, and consider the image of the extended ideal points

(\theta,\delta)\in\widehat{\partial\mathbb{H}_{d}}\mapsto\bigl(\theta,\frac{c_{d}}{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{(d-1)\delta}\bigr)\in\widetilde{\partial\mathbb{H}_{d}},

where
c_{d}
is as in (
1.2
). We call the first coordinate of a point in the corona its
angle
and the second coordinate its
radius
. We will also use the letters
r,r_{i},R,R_{i}
for radii and the letter “
\delta
” or “
D
” for delays.
It follows from the first part of the proof of
Theorem

3.2
that
(\theta_{i},\frac{c_{d}}{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{(d-1)D_{i}})_{i\geq 1}
is a Poisson point process in the corona with intensity

\mu_{d}\coloneqq\mathrm{Unif}\otimes\mathrm{Leb};

(3.2)

the points of this PPP will be called

. In these new coordinates where
r=\frac{c_{d}}{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{(d-1)\delta}
, we introduce the
(exponential) separation
between
z\in\mathbb{H}_{d}
and a point
(\theta,r)
in the corona in terms of the linear separation between
z
and an ideal point
(\theta,\delta)
as follows:

\mathbf{d}\bigl(z,(\theta,r)\bigr)\coloneqq\frac{c_{d}}{d-1}\exp\bigl\{(d-1)\mathrm{d}\bigl(z,(\theta,\delta)\bigr)\bigr\}.

(3.3)

This monotone, strictly increasing transformation preserves the inequality in
2.1
and hence the associated ideal Voronoi tessellation.
See
Figure

3.1
for an example of the Poisson point process on the corona and its associated Voronoi and Delaunay tessellations.
In the latter tessellation, one associates to every Voronoi vertex

the ideal simplex formed from the

angles of the ideal nuclei whose separation from

is smallest. The resulting collection of ideal simplices forms the Delaunay tessellation; complete details are given in
Section

3.3.1
Separations to the ideal nuclei via the Poisson kernel

Remember that the ideal boundary of

is identified to the

\mathbb{S}_{d-1}

that gives the density of the harmonic measure on

\displaystyle(z_{d})^{d-1}&\mbox{if }\theta=\infty.\end{cases}\\

These kernels occur naturally when computing separations from ideal nuclei:

Lemma 3.3

(
Separation in coordinates
)
.

The separation from

to

Remark 3.5

. Shown is the portion over a

4
Face intensities

Since
\mathcal{V}_{d}
is invariant under isometries of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
, we can define the intensities of various geometric quantities such as
k
-faces. Using existing results in stochastic geometry, many of those quantities can be explicitly computed for the ideal Poisson–Voronoi tessellations. Let us first recall the classical construction of the Palm distribution for general stationary point processes.

4.1
Palm measures and typical points

4.1.1
Euclidean space

Recall that a point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be seen as an (atomic) measure on the Borel sets
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and that the set
\mathrm{Mes}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be endowed with the smallest
\sigma
-algebra
\Sigma
that renders evaluation functionals on Borel sets measurable. Given a random point process
\mathbf{X}
(hence a probability measure on
\mathrm{Mes}(\mathbb{R}^{d}
)), one can define a (possibly infinite) measure
\mu
on the space
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathrm{Mes}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
endowed with the product sigma-field
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\Sigma
by the formula
\mu(B)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{1}_{B}(x,\mathbf{X}-x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}(x)
for
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\Sigma
. The intuitive meaning of
\mu
is that it gives the joint “distribution” of a point of the process together with how the other points look relative to that point. When the law of
\mathbf{X}
is invariant under translation,
A\mapsto\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{X}(A)\bigr]
is a translation-invariant map on Borel subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whence is a multiple of Lebesgue measure; that constant multiplier is called the
intensity

\mathrm{I}_{\mathbf{X}}
of
\mathbf{X}
. Similarly in this case,
\mu
is invariant under translations in the first coordinate, that is,
\mu(B_{1}\times B_{2})=\mu\bigl((B_{1}-x)\times B_{2}\bigr)
for Borel
B_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, measurable
B_{2}\in\Sigma
, and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Hence, if
\mathrm{I}_{\mathbf{X}}
is positive and finite, then
\mu=\mathrm{I}_{\mathbf{X}}\cdot\mathrm{Leb}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{o}}
for a unique probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{o}}
on measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
SW08
, Theorem 3.3.1]
. For example,
\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{o}}(B_{2})=\mathrm{I}_{\mathbf{X}}^{-1}\cdot\mathbb{E}\int_{x\in B_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{B_{2}}(\mathbf{X}-x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}(x)
for every
B_{1}
with
\mathrm{Leb}(B_{1})=1
. The measure
\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{o}}
is called the
Palm distribution
of
\mathbf{X}
.
When
\mathbf{X}
is a point process,
\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{o}}
has one of its points at
\mathbf{o}
a.s.,
\mathbf{o}
then being referred to as a
typical point
of
\mathbf{X}
.
When
\mathbf{X}
is a PPP, Slivnyak’s theorem
[
SW08
, Theorem 3.3.5]
shows that the
\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{o}}
-law of
\mathbf{X}
equals the
\mathbb{P}
-law of
\mathbf{X}+\delta_{\mathbf{o}}
.
Let
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbf{o}}
denote integration with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{o}}
.
The definition of
\mu
tells us that to integrate a nonnegative, measurable function
f
with respect to
\mu
, we merely replace the indicator
\mathbf{1}_{B}
by
f
, which is the content of the
refined Campbell theorem
:
\mathrm{I}_{\mathbf{X}}\cdot\mathbb{E}^{\mathbf{o}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,\mathbf{X})\,\mathrm{d}x=\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,\mathbf{X}-x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}(x)

[
SW08
, Theorem 3.3.3]
.

4.1.2
Hyperbolic space

Similar definitions hold for a random measure on a group whose law is invariant under translations. In the case of a random measure
\mathbf{X}
on a homogeneous space like
\mathbb{H}_{d}
, a slightly more complicated definition of Palm measure is needed
[
Las10
]
. Assume that the law of
\mathbf{X}
is invariant under
\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}
. Then
A\mapsto\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{X}(A)\bigr]
is an isometry-invariant map on Borel subsets of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
, whence is a multiple of
\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}
; that multiplier is called the
intensity

\mathrm{I}_{\mathbf{X}}
of
\mathbf{X}
. Let
\kappa
be the probability Haar measure on the isotropy (stabilizer) subgroup
\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}^{\mathbf{o}}
of
\mathbf{o}
.
Choose maps
x\mapsto\phi_{x}
from
\mathbb{H}_{d}\to\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}
such that
\phi_{x}(\mathbf{o})=x
. Write
\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}^{x}\coloneqq\{\phi\in\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}\,;\;\phi(\mathbf{o})=x\}
and
\kappa_{x}\coloneqq\kappa\circ\phi_{x}^{-1}
; the measure
\kappa_{x}
does not depend on the choice of
\phi_{x}
. Define
\mu(B)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}\int_{\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}^{x}}\mathbf{1}_{B}(x,\mathbf{X}\circ\phi)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa_{x}(\phi)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}(x)
for
B
in the product
\sigma
-field generated by Borel sets in
\mathbb{H}_{d}
and the evaluation functionals on Borel sets. If
\mathrm{I}_{\mathbf{X}}
is positive and finite, then
\mu=\mathrm{I}_{\mathbf{X}}\cdot\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{o}}
for a unique probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{o}}
on measures on
\mathbb{H}_{d}
.
The measure
\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{o}}
is called the
Palm distribution
of
\mathbf{X}
.
When
\mathbf{X}
is a point process,
\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{o}}
has one of its points at
\mathbf{o}
a.s.,
\mathbf{o}
then being referred to as a
typical point
of
\mathbf{X}
.
The refined Campbell theorem says that
\mathrm{I}_{\mathbf{X}}\cdot\mathbb{E}^{\mathbf{o}}\int_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}f(x,\mathbf{X})\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}(x)=\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}\int_{\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}^{x}}f(x,\mathbf{X}\circ\phi)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa_{x}(\phi)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}(x)
for nonnegative, measurable functions,
f
.
When
\mathbf{X}
is a PPP, we again have a Slivnyak-type theorem: the
\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{o}}
-law of
\mathbf{X}
equals the
\mathbb{P}
-law of
\mathbf{X}+\delta_{\mathbf{o}}
. This is proved as follows.
Let
\mathbf{X}
be a PPP with intensity
\lambda
. By Mecke’s theorem
[
SW08
, Theorem 3.2.5]
, we have that for all nonnegative, measurable
g
,

\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}g(x,\mathbf{X})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}(x)=\lambda\int_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(x,\mathbf{X}+\delta_{x})\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}(x).

(4.1)

Choose
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{H}_{d}
with
\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}(B_{1})=1
and a Borel set
B_{2}
of measures on
\mathbb{H}_{d}
. Consider

g(x,\mathbf{X})\coloneqq\int_{\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}^{x}}\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(x)\mathbf{1}_{B_{2}}(\mathbf{X}\circ\phi)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa_{x}(\phi).

By the refined Campbell theorem,

\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}g(x,\mathbf{X})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}(x)=\lambda\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbf{o}}\int_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(x)\mathbf{1}_{B_{2}}(\mathbf{X})\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}(x)=\lambda\,\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{o}}[\mathbf{X}\in B_{2}].

(4.2)

On the other hand, because
(\mathbf{X}+\delta_{x})\circ\phi=\mathbf{X}\circ\phi+\delta_{\mathbf{o}}
for all
\phi\in\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}^{x}
,

\displaystyle\lambda\int_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(x,\mathbf{X}+\delta_{x})\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}\int_{\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}^{x}}\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(x)\,\mathbb{E}\mathbf{1}_{B_{2}}(\mathbf{X}\circ\phi+\delta_{\mathbf{o}})\,\mathrm{d}\kappa_{x}(\phi)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}\int_{\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}^{x}}\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(x)\,\mathbb{E}\mathbf{1}_{B_{2}}(\mathbf{X}+\delta_{\mathbf{o}})\,\mathrm{d}\kappa_{x}(\phi)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}(x),

\displaystyle=\lambda\,\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{X}+\delta_{\mathbf{o}}\in B_{2}],

(4.3)

where, in the second step, we used the
\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}
-invariance of the
\mathbb{P}
-law of
\mathbf{X}
. Comparing (
4.1
), (
4.2
), and (
4.1.2
) proves our claim.

4.1.3
Marked random measures

We will be especially interested in the Palm distribution for marked
random measures, with marks being polytopes. Let
E
be either
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\mathbb{H}_{d}
. Although we can use any measurable space for the space of marks
[
Las10
, Remark 3.9]
, we will use
the metrizable space
M
, which is the set of closed subsets of
E
with the Fell topology.
A
marked random measure

\mathbf{X}
on
E
with marks in
M
is a random measure on
E\times M
that projects to a random measure on
E
.
We say that the law of
\mathbf{X}
is invariant if it is invariant by the group of translations or isometries in the first coordinate, that is, if
\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{X}\in A\times B]
does not change under the group action on
A
for each fixed
B
, where
A\subseteq E
and
B\subseteq M
are Borel. In this case, the preceding paragraphs with virtually no modification give the Palm distribution of
\mathbf{X}
, which, when the projection of
\mathbf{X}
to
E
is a point process, is concentrated on points of the form
(\mathbf{o},m)
, and also give the refined Campbell theorem. In this point-process case, we also call the (random) mark
m
of
\mathbf{o}
a
typical mark
.

4.1.4
Tessellations

Let us apply the preceding paragraphs to our setting. Recall that we denote by
\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}
the Poisson–Voronoi tessellation on
\mathbb{H}_{d}
with positive intensity
\lambda>0
and by
\mathcal{V}_{d}
our limiting ideal Poisson–Voronoi tessellation. For fixed
0\leq k\leq d-1
, to each
k
-dimensional face
\mathfrak{s}
of
\mathcal{V}_{d}
or of
\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}
(we speak
of vertices when
k=0
and edges when
k=1
), we associate a point
\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{s})\in\mathfrak{s}
, called the
center
, in an isometry-equivariant, measurable way (i.e.,
\mathrm{Cen}\bigl(\phi(\mathfrak{s})\bigr)=\phi\bigl(\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{s})\bigr)
for every isometry,
\phi
). It will be convenient to assume that
\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{s})
lies in the relative interior of
\mathfrak{s}
, which implies that
\mathrm{Cen}
is injective on the set of
k
-faces. The centroid
[
Rat19
, p. 222]
is such a center. We will also have use for the
measure centroid
, defined like the centroid using the hyperboloid model
[
Rat19
, p. 59]
, but integrating with respect to
\nu_{\mathfrak{s}}
rather than with respect to
\sum_{x\in\mathfrak{s}\cap\mathcal{V}_{d,0}}\delta_{x}
, where
\nu_{\mathfrak{s}}
is the
k
-dimensional hyperbolic measure on
\mathfrak{s}
.
Thus, the centers of the
k
-faces of a Poisson–Voronoi tessellation allow us to define the invariant marked random measure corresponding to
\mathcal{V}_{d,k}
that was described in
Section

4.1.3
as

\mathbf{X}_{d,k}\colon A\mapsto\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{s}\in k\textrm{-faces}\end{subarray}}\int_{\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}^{\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{s})}}\mathbf{1}_{A}\bigl(\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{s}),\phi^{-1}(\mathfrak{s})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa_{\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{s})}

(4.4)

for Borel
A\subseteq E\times M
, where
\kappa_{x}
is as above
[
Her21
, Section 5.4]
.
A typical mark is now called a
typical
k
-face
. This applies to
k=d
as well when
\lambda>0
, since the cells have finite volume, making it possible to define a center; in this case, we refer to a
typical cell
. When
k=0
, we speak of a typical vertex; in this case, the origin may be thought of as conditioned to be a Voronoi vertex.

Similar considerations apply for the faces of the (possibly ideal) Delaunay tessellations
\mathcal{D}_{d}^{(\lambda)}
or
\mathcal{D}_{d}
and for the Delaunay simplices themselves. Here, it may be necessary to use
(generalized) centers
of the
k
-simplices, where we no longer require that the generalized center of a
k
-simplex lie in that simplex or we use extra randomness. In our case, we may use the randomness of the PPP of (ideal) nuclei. In fact, for the generalized center of a Delaunay
k
-simplex, we will use the center of the corresponding
(d-k)
-face of the Voronoi tessellation. Here,
1\leq k\leq d
, although in the case of positive-intensity nuclei, rather than ideal nuclei, we may allow
k=0
. The only centers we will use for the ideal Delaunay tessellation are the generalized centers of the ideal Delaunay simplices, which are, then, the corresponding Voronoi vertices.

4.2
Intensities and ergodic properties

Let
\mathcal{V}_{d,k}
denote the collection of
k
-faces of cells of
\mathcal{V}_{d}
; in particular,
\mathcal{V}_{d,d}=\mathcal{V}_{d}
. Consider the random measures

\mu_{d,k}\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{s}\in{\mathcal{V}_{d,k}}\end{subarray}}\delta_{\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{s})}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\tilde{\mu}_{d,k}\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{s}\in{\mathcal{V}_{d,k}}\end{subarray}}\nu_{\mathfrak{s}}.

(4.5)

Of course,
\mu_{d,0}=\tilde{\mu}_{d,0}
.
Since
\mathcal{V}_{d}
is isometry invariant in law (Lemma
3.10
), so are
\mu_{d,k}
and
\tilde{\mu}_{d,k}
, whence the mean measures (their expectations) are multiples of the hyperbolic measure on
\mathbb{H}_{d}
. We thus set

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mu_{d,k}]=\mathrm{I}_{d,k}\cdot\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mu}_{d,k}]=\tilde{\mathrm{I}}_{d,k}\cdot\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}\;.

(4.6)

We call the constant
\mathrm{I}_{d,k}\in[0,\infty]
(resp.,
\tilde{\mathrm{I}}_{d,k}
), the
counting
k
-face intensity

(resp.,
measure
k
-face intensity
) of
\mathcal{V}_{d}
. The latter intensity is called by
[
SW08
, p. 141]
the
k
-volume
density
or
specific
k
-volume
.

The counting intensities do not depend on the choice of center function. To see this, let
\mathrm{Cen}
and
\mathrm{Cen}^{\prime}
be two center functions. Denote by
\mu_{d,k}
and
\mu^{\prime}_{d,k}
the corresponding random measures as in (
4.5
).
Define the measure

\widehat{\mu}_{d,k}\coloneqq\sum_{\mathfrak{s}\in\mathcal{V}_{d,k}}\delta_{\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{s})}\otimes\delta_{\mathrm{Cen}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{s})}

on
\mathbb{H}_{d}\times\mathbb{H}_{d}
. For Borel
A\subseteq\mathbb{H}_{d}
, we have

\widehat{\mu}_{d,k}(A\times\mathbb{H}_{d})=\mu_{d,k}(A)\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\mu}_{d,k}(\mathbb{H}_{d}\times A)=\mu^{\prime}_{d,k}(A).

The measure
\widehat{\mu}_{d,k}
is equivariant under the diagonal action of
\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}
and the law of
\mathcal{V}_{d,k}
is invariant under the action of
\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}
, whence the
mean measure
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mu}_{d,k}]
is invariant under the diagonal action of
\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}
.
By unimodularity of
\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}
, the mass-transport principle
[
LP16
, Theorem 8.47]
yields

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\widehat{\mu}_{d,k}(A\times\mathbb{H}_{d})\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\widehat{\mu}_{d,k}(\mathbb{H}_{d}\times A)\bigr].

This gives our claim.

These intensities can naturally be defined for the Poisson–Voronoi tessellation
\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}
with positive intensity
\lambda>0
, which we denote by

\mathrm{I}_{d,k}^{(\lambda)}
and
\tilde{\mathrm{I}}^{(\lambda)}_{d,k}
for
0\leq k\leq d
.
By choosing the center of a cell to be its nucleus, we obtain
\mathrm{I}_{d,d}^{(\lambda)}=\lambda
. The definition gives
\tilde{\mathrm{I}}_{d,d}^{(\lambda)}=1
. Similar intensities can be defined for the (ideal) Poisson–Delaunay tessellation.

As one would expect, the intensities for
\lambda>0
converge to the corresponding intensities of the ideal tessellation as
\lambda\to 0
:

Lemma 4.1

(
Convergence of
k
-face intensities
)
.

For
0\leq k\leq d-1
, we have

\mathrm{I}_{d,k}^{(\lambda)}\xrightarrow[\lambda\to 0]{}\mathrm{I}_{d,k}\in(0,\infty)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\tilde{\mathrm{I}}_{d,k}^{(\lambda)}\xrightarrow[\lambda\to 0]{}\tilde{\mathrm{I}}_{d,k}\in(0,\infty).

Proof.

Let
B_{u}=B_{u}(\mathbf{o})
be the ball of hyperbolic radius
u\geq 0
around the origin in
\mathbb{H}_{d}
, so that

\mathrm{I}_{d,k}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mu_{d,k}(B_{u})]}{\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}(B_{u})}

(4.7)

for
u>0
, with a similar formula for positive intensities
\lambda>0
. As the center of a face, choose its measure centroid, which is continuous with respect to the Fell topology when restricted to compact, nonempty polytopes. Since
\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}\to\mathcal{V}_{d}
by
Theorem

1.1
, we have, with obvious notation, that
\mu_{d,k}^{(\lambda)}(B_{u})\to\mu_{d,k}(B_{u})
in distribution as
\lambda\to 0
. By (
4.7
), it thus suffices to prove that
(\mu_{d,k}^{(\lambda)}(B_{u})\,;\;0<\lambda<1)
is uniformly integrable to deduce convergence of the expectations from the convergence in law. Define
N^{(\lambda)}_{d,k}(B_{u})
to be the number of
k
-faces of
\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}
that intersect
B_{u}
. Adapting
3.8
for positive intensity
\lambda>0
, we obtain again a (uniform in
\lambda\in(0,1)
) stretched exponential bound on the tail probabilities of
N^{(\lambda)}_{d,k}(B_{u})
, which yields the desired uniform integrability because
\mu^{(\lambda)}_{d,k}(B_{u})\leq N^{(\lambda)}_{d,k}(B_{u})
.

Since
\tilde{\mu}^{(\lambda)}_{d,k}(B_{u})\leq\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}_{k}}(B^{(k)}_{u})\cdot N^{(\lambda)}_{d,k}(B_{u})
, where
B^{(k)}_{u}
is a ball of radius
u
in
\mathbb{H}_{k}
, a similar proof holds for the convergence of
\tilde{\mathrm{I}}_{d,k}^{(\lambda)}
.
∎

These face intensities are directly related to aspects of typical faces and cells as shown in the following two propositions.

Proposition 4.2

(
Intensities and typical volumes
)
.

For
0\leq k\leq d-1
, the quotient
\tilde{\mathrm{I}}_{d,k}/\mathrm{I}_{d,k}
equals the expected
k
-volume of the typical
k
-face of
\mathcal{V}_{d}
.
For
\lambda>0
and
0\leq k\leq d
, the quotient
\tilde{\mathrm{I}}_{d,k}^{(\lambda)}/\mathrm{I}_{d,k}^{(\lambda)}
equals the expected
k
-volume of the typical
k
-face of
\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}
.

Proof.

Define the measure

\widehat{\mu}_{d,k}\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{s}\in{\mathcal{V}_{d,k}}\end{subarray}}\delta_{\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{s})}\otimes\nu_{\mathfrak{s}}

on
\mathbb{H}_{d}\times\mathbb{H}_{d}
. Write
\|\nu_{\mathfrak{s}}\|
for the total
k
-volume of
\mathfrak{s}
. For Borel
A\subseteq\mathbb{H}_{d}
, we have

\widehat{\mu}_{d,k}(\mathbb{H}_{d}\times A)=\tilde{\mu}_{d,k}(A)\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\mu}_{d,k}(A\times\mathbb{H}_{d})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{s}\in{\mathcal{V}_{d,k}},\\
{\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{s})\in A}\end{subarray}}\|\nu_{\mathfrak{s}}\|.

The mean measure
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mu}_{d,k}]
is invariant under the diagonal action of
\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}
, whence
the mass-transport principle yields

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\widehat{\mu}_{d,k}(\mathbb{H}_{d}\times A)\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\widehat{\mu}_{d,k}(A\times\mathbb{H}_{d})\bigr].

(4.8)

The left-hand side equals
\tilde{\mathrm{I}}_{d,k}\cdot\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}(A)
, while the right-hand side equals
\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{s}\in{\mathcal{V}_{d,k}},\\
\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{s})\in A\end{subarray}}\|\nu_{\mathfrak{s}}\|\Bigr]
. Let
\mathbf{X}_{d,k}
denote the marked random measure corresponding to
\mathcal{V}_{d,k}
defined in
Eq.

4.4
. Using the function

f\bigl(\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{s}),\mathbf{X}_{d,k}\bigr)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathbf{1}_{A}\bigl(\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{s})\bigr)\|\nu_{\mathfrak{t}}\|&\text{if }\mathfrak{t}\text{ is the unique $k$-face with }\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{t})=\mathbf{o}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

in the refined Campbell theorem, we obtain that
\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{s}\in{\mathcal{V}_{d,k}},\\
\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{s})\in A\end{subarray}}\|\nu_{\mathfrak{s}}\|\Bigr]
equals the expected
k
-volume of the typical
k
-face times
\mathrm{I}_{d,k}\cdot\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}(A)
. Hence (
4.8
) gives our claim.

A similar proof gives the result for positive
\lambda
.
∎

Proposition 4.3

(
Typical face vectors and volumes
)
.

For
\lambda>0
and
0\leq k\leq d-1
, the mean number (resp.,
k
-volume) of
k
-faces of the typical cell of
\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}
is
(d+1-k)\mathrm{I}_{d,k}^{(\lambda)}/\lambda
(resp.,
(d+1-k)\tilde{\mathrm{I}}_{d,k}^{(\lambda)}/\lambda
).

Proof.

Let
f_{k}(\mathfrak{t})
be the number of
k
-faces of a cell,
\mathfrak{t}
. Define the measure

\widehat{\mu}_{d,k}^{(\lambda)}\coloneqq\sum_{\mathfrak{s}\in\mathcal{V}_{d,k}^{(\lambda)},\ \mathfrak{t}\in\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{s}\subset\mathfrak{t}}\cdot\delta_{\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{s})}\otimes\delta_{\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{t})}

on
\mathbb{H}_{d}\times\mathbb{H}_{d}
. For Borel
A\subseteq\mathbb{H}_{d}
, we have

\widehat{\mu}_{d,k}^{(\lambda)}(A\times\mathbb{H}_{d})=(d+1-k)\mu_{d,k}^{(\lambda)}{(A)}\text{ a.s.}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\mu}_{d,k}^{(\lambda)}(\mathbb{H}_{d}\times A)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{t}\in\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}{,}\\
{\mathrm{Cen}(\mathfrak{t})\in A}\end{subarray}}f_{k}(\mathfrak{t}).

The mean measure
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mu}_{d,k}^{(\lambda)}]

\frac{1}{c_{d}}\frac{1}{\bigl(1+\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}x_{i}^{2}\bigr)^{d-1}}\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{d-1}\otimes\mathrm{d}t\,\mathbf{1}_{t>0}

in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Now conditionally on
R_{1}=s
, we apply the Poisson mapping theorem to compute the intensity of the point process of the centers and radii
by the change of variable
\rho\coloneqq\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}}\bigl(\frac{s}{s+t}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2(d-1)}}
, which gives, for a test function
u
,

\begin{split}\frac{1}{c_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}u\Bigl(x,\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}}&\Bigl(\frac{s}{s+t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2(d-1)}}\Bigr)\frac{1}{\left(1+|x|^{2}\right)^{d-1}}\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\frac{d-1}{c_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}u\left(x,\rho\right)\frac{2s}{\rho^{2d-1}}\mathbf{1}_{1+|x|^{2}\geq\rho^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}\rho.\end{split}

Recalling that
R_{1}
is an
{\rm Exp}(1)
random variable and noting that
\frac{d-1}{c_{d}}
times an
{\rm Exp}(1)
random variable is an
{\rm Exp}(\frac{c_{d}}{d-1})
random variable concludes the proof.
∎

5.2
The hole probability

In this section, we use the preceding construction of the zero cell of
\mathcal{V}_{d}
to compute the probability for
\mathcal{C}_{d}
to contain a ball centered at the origin: this provides the law of the distance of
\mathbf{o}
to
\partial\mathcal{C}_{d}
.

Proposition 5.1

(
Hole probability
)
.

The hole probability, i.e., the probability that the ball
B_{u}(\mathbf{o})
centered at
\mathbf{o}
with hyperbolic radius
u
is contained in
\mathcal{C}_{d}
, is given by, respectively,

(i)

conditional on
R_{1}=s
,

\mathbb{P}\bigl[B_{u}(\mathbf{o})\subset\mathcal{C}_{d}\bigm|R_{1}=s\bigr]={\rm exp}\left(-sI_{d}(u)\right);

(ii)

averaging on the value of
R_{1}
,

\mathbb{P}[B_{u}(\mathbf{o})\subset\mathcal{C}_{d}]=\frac{1}{1+I_{d}(u)}\,.

Here,
I_{d}(u)
as defined below in (
5.1
) satisfies

1+I_{d}(u)=\frac{2^{d-1}\Gamma(d/2)}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma\bigl(\frac{d-1}{2}\bigr)}(\cosh u)^{1-d}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{d-2}\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{\frac{d-3}{2}}}{\left(1-t\tanh u\right)^{2d-2}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

In particular,

\mathbb{P}\bigl[B_{u}(\mathbf{o})\subset\mathcal{C}_{d}\bigm|R_{1}=s\bigr]\leq\mathrm{e}^{-s(d-1)u}
and
\mathbb{P}[B_{u}(\mathbf{o})\subset\mathcal{C}_{d}]\leq\mathrm{e}^{-(d-1)u}
; that is, the distance from
\mathbf{o}
to the boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{d}
is stochastically dominated by an
\mathrm{Exp}\bigl(s(d-1)\bigr)
random variable when conditioned on
R_{1}=s
and is stochastically dominated by an
\mathrm{Exp}(d-1)
random variable unconditionally. Also,

\mathbb{P}[B_{u}(\mathbf{o})\subset\mathcal{C}_{d}]\geq\mathrm{e}^{-2(d-1)u}
, that is, the distance from
\mathbf{o}
to the boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{d}
stochastically dominates an
\mathrm{Exp}\bigl(2(d-1)\bigr)
random variable.

Proof.

First, we condition on
R_{1}=s
, so that the intensity measure of the Poisson point process
(x,\rho)
is given by
2s\frac{d-1}{c_{d}}\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\rho^{2d-1}}\mathbf{1}_{\rho\leq\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}}}
. Second, we parametrize
B_{u}(\mathbf{o})
in the upper half-space model
\mathbb{U}_{d}
: here,
B_{u}(\mathbf{o})
is represented by a Euclidean ball
B(C_{\mathbf{o}},R_{\mathbf{o}})
of Euclidean center
C_{\mathbf{o}}=\left(0,\ldots,0,\cosh{u}\right)
and Euclidean radius
R_{\mathbf{o}}=\sinh{u}
. Hence the event
B_{u}(\mathbf{o})\subset\mathcal{C}_{d}
corresponds to the event that this point process has no point in the region
\bigl\{(x,\rho)\in\Pi\,;\;\left(\rho+R_{\mathbf{o}}\right)^{2}\geq|x|^{2}+|C_{\mathbf{o}}|^{2}\bigr\}
. Therefore, by
1.4
, the conditional hole probability is

\mathbb{P}[B_{u}(\mathbf{o})\subset\mathcal{C}_{d}\mid R_{1}=s]=\text{exp}\left\{-s\,I_{d}(u)\right\},

where

\begin{split}I_{d}(u)&\coloneqq\frac{2(d-1)}{c_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\mathrm{d}x\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\rho^{2d-1}}\mathbf{1}_{(\rho+R_{\mathbf{o}})^{2}\geq|x|^{2}+C_{\mathbf{o}}^{2}}\times\mathbf{1}_{\rho^{2}\leq 1+|x|^{2}}\\
&=\frac{1}{c_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\mathrm{d}x\frac{1}{\bigl(\sqrt{|C_{\mathbf{o}}|^{2}+|x|^{2}}-R_{\mathbf{o}}\bigr)^{2d-2}}-\frac{1}{c_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\mathrm{d}x\frac{1}{\left(1+|x|^{2}\right)^{d-1}}\;.\\
\end{split}

(5.1)

The equality arises from the fact that the region
\bigl\{(x,\rho)\in\Pi\,;\;\left(\rho+R_{\mathbf{o}}\right)^{2}\geq|x|^{2}+|C_{\mathbf{o}}|^{2}\bigr\}
contains the set
\bigl\{(x,\rho)\,;\;\rho^{2}\geq 1+|x|^{2}\bigr\}
, which is where the hemisphere corresponding to
(x,\rho)
contains
\mathbf{o}
.
The second integral is equal to 1 by
3.1
. For the first integral, change to polar coordinates, then to
\sinh\eta\coloneqq|x|/|C_{\mathbf{o}}|
, and finally to
t\coloneqq 1/\cosh\eta
. This gives (i), which immediately implies (ii).

The last unconditional inequalities are equivalent to
\mathrm{e}^{(d-1)u}\leq 1+I_{d}(u)\leq\mathrm{e}^{2(d-1)u}
for all
u\geq 0
. (For the conditional inequality, we also use that
\mathrm{e}^{(d-1)u}-1\geq(d-1)u
.)
These follow from elementary calculations:
Because
1=\mathrm{e}^{-2u}+2(\sinh u)\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{-2u}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-2u}+2(\sinh u)\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{-2u}+|x|^{2}}
, it follows that
|C_{\mathbf{o}}|^{2}+|x|^{2}\leq\bigl(\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{-2u}+|x|^{2}}+R_{\mathbf{o}}\bigr)^{2}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle 1+I_{d}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\bigl(\sqrt{|C_{\mathbf{o}}|^{2}+|x|^{2}}-R_{\mathbf{o}}\bigr)^{2d-2}}\geq\frac{1}{c_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\left(\mathrm{e}^{-2u}+|x|^{2}\right)^{d-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{(d-1)u}\,\mathrm{d}y}{\left(1+|y|^{2}\right)^{d-1}}=\mathrm{e}^{(d-1)u}\,,

where we used the change of variables
y\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{u}x
.
Also,
(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2u})(1+|x|^{2})\geq(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2u})\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}}=2\mathrm{e}^{-u}(\sinh u)\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}}
, whence
|C_{\mathbf{o}}|^{2}+|x|^{2}\geq\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{-u}\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}}+R_{\mathbf{o}}\bigr)^{2}
. Hence,

1+I_{d}(u)=\frac{1}{c_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\bigl(\sqrt{|C_{\mathbf{o}}|^{2}+|x|^{2}}-R_{\mathbf{o}}\bigr)^{2d-2}}\leq\frac{1}{c_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2(d-1)u}\,\mathrm{d}x}{\left(1+|x|^{2}\right)^{d-1}}=\mathrm{e}^{2(d-1)u}\;.\qed

Remark 5.2
.

For
d=2

I_{2}(u)=\frac{1}{\pi}\bigl(4\left(\arctan{\mathrm{e}^{u}}\right)\cosh^{2}{u}+2\sinh{u}\bigr)-1
, which gives, for the integrated hole probability,

\mathbb{P}[B_{u}(\mathbf{o})\subset\mathcal{C}_{2}]=\frac{\pi}{4\left(\arctan{\mathrm{e}^{u}}\right)\cosh^{2}{u}+2\sinh{u}}\,,

(5.2)

]

.
It is easily seen, using the change of variables

In dimension

whose value at

Proposition 5.3

(
Measure face intensities
\tilde{\mathrm{I}}_{d,d-1}
)
.

For all
d\geq 2
, we have

\tilde{\mathrm{I}}_{d,d-1}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})\Gamma(d)}{\Gamma(\frac{d-1}{2})\Gamma(d-\frac{1}{2})}.

The first few values are
2/\pi
,
4/3
,
32/(5\pi)
, and
96/35
.
For large
d
, it is asymptotically
d/\sqrt{2}-\frac{9}{8\sqrt{2}}+O(\frac{1}{d})
.

Proof.

Fix
d\geq 2
, and for
\lambda,\varepsilon>0
, consider the probability that the origin is within distance less than
\varepsilon
from the boundary
\partial\mathcal{V}^{(\lambda)}_{d}\coloneqq\bigcup\mathcal{V}^{(\lambda)}_{d,d-1}
of the Poisson–Voronoi tessellation
\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}
with intensity
\lambda
on
\mathbb{H}_{d}
:

h(\varepsilon,d,\lambda)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\bigl[B_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{o})\cap\partial{\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}}\neq\varnothing\bigr].

By isometry invariance,
h(\varepsilon,d,\lambda)
can be interpreted as the mean volume (per unit volume of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
) of the region within distance
\varepsilon
from
\partial{\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}}
. We then have the limit

\frac{h(\varepsilon,d,\lambda)}{2\varepsilon}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}\tilde{\mathrm{I}}_{d,d-1}^{{(\lambda)}}\xrightarrow[\lambda\to 0]{\mathrm{Lem.\ \ref{lem:convintensities}}}\tilde{\mathrm{I}}_{d,d-1}

(5.3)

and the one in which we interchange the order of limits, which can be evaluated explicitly thanks to our results,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\frac{h(\varepsilon,d,\lambda)}{2\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\underset{\mathrm{Thm.}\ \ref{thm:decomposition}}{=}

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}\bigl[B_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{o})\cap\partial\mathcal{V}_{d}\neq\varnothing\bigr]}{2\varepsilon}

\displaystyle{=}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\bigl[B_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{o})\subset\mathcal{C}_{d}\bigr]\right|_{\varepsilon=0}

\displaystyle\underset{\mathrm{Prop.}\ \ref{prop.holeprob}}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})\Gamma(d)}{\Gamma(\frac{d-1}{2})\Gamma(d-\frac{1}{2})}.

We claim that exchanging the order of limits gives the same result, whence the above display combined with (
5.3
) computes
\tilde{\mathrm{I}}_{d,d-1}
.

To establish our claim, suppose that we have a sequence
\mathbf{C}^{(j)}=\bigl((C_{i}^{(j)}\,;\;i\geq 1)\bigr)_{j\geq 1}
of normal Voronoi tessellations that converges to a normal, ideal Voronoi tessellation
\mathbf{C}=(C_{i}\,;\;i\geq 1)
as
j\to\infty
. Fix
1\leq k\leq d-1
. Denote the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of the
k
-skeleton of a tessellation
\mathbf{C}
by
\mathbf{C}(\varepsilon)
. Suppose that
x
is an interior point of a
k
-face of a cell,
C_{i}
. Then
x
is a limit of interior points of
k
-faces of
C_{i}^{(j)}
but not of any other
k
-faces, because faces are convex and the tessellations are normal. This implies that
\varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{Vol}\bigl(\mathbf{C}^{(j)}(\varepsilon)\cap B_{1}(\mathbf{o})\bigr)
converges to
\varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{Vol}\bigl(\mathbf{C}(\varepsilon)\cap B_{1}(\mathbf{o})\bigr)
as
j\to\infty
uniformly in
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, whence
\lim_{j\to\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{Vol}\bigl(\mathbf{C}^{(j)}(\varepsilon)\cap B_{1}(\mathbf{o})\bigr)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{j\to\infty}\varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{Vol}\bigl(\mathbf{C}^{(j)}(\varepsilon)\cap B_{1}(\mathbf{o})\bigr)
. In particular, this holds a.s. for
{\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}}
and
\mathcal{V}_{d}
if we take a coupling that converges a.s. as
\lambda\to 0
. As in the proof of Lemma
4.1
, uniform integrability shows that

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{Vol}\bigl({\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}}(\varepsilon)\cap B_{1}(\mathbf{o})\bigr)\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{Vol}\bigl({\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}}(\varepsilon)\cap B_{1}(\mathbf{o})\bigr)\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{Vol}\bigl({\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}}(\varepsilon)\cap B_{1}(\mathbf{o})\bigr)\bigr]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{Vol}\bigl({\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}}(\varepsilon)\cap B_{1}(\mathbf{o})\bigr)\bigr].

Choose
k=d-1
. Since
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{Vol}\bigl({\mathcal{V}_{d}^{(\lambda)}}(\varepsilon)\cap B_{1}(\mathbf{o})\bigr)\bigr]=h(\varepsilon,d,\lambda)\mathrm{Vol}\bigl(B_{1}(\mathbf{o})\bigr)
, dividing by
\mathrm{Vol}\bigl(B_{1}(\mathbf{o})\bigr)
gives our claim.
∎

It would be interesting to compute the hole probabilities for lower-dimensional faces, in particular for vertices, but the calculations seem considerably more intricate. As in the preceding proposition, this would provide one way to compute
\tilde{\mathrm{I}}_{d,k}
for other
k
.

5.3
Asymptotic properties of the zero cell: the typical cell

In this section, we give the basic properties of the underlying stationary model defining the law of
\mathcal{C}_{d}
far away from the origin. It is obtained from the original deposition model of Theorem
1.4
by removing the indicator function in the intensity (
1.3
), which ensured that no ball would contain the origin.

More precisely, following the proof of Theorem
1.4
in
Section

5.1
, conditioning on the value of the smallest radius
R_{1}=s
, we denote by
\mathcal{T}_{d}=\mathcal{T}_{d}(s)
the random closed subset obtained by removing the balls whose centers and radii
(x,\rho)
are distributed according to a Poisson point process with intensity

2\frac{d-1}{c_{d}}{s}\,\mathrm{d}x\otimes\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\rho^{2d-1}}.

(5.4)

In their natural coupling, the sets
\mathcal{T}_{d}{(R_{1})}
and
\mathcal{C}_{d}
are created by the same PPP of balls except that
\mathcal{T}_{d}(R_{1})
uses
\mathrm{Pois}(R_{1})
more balls given
R_{1}
,
as we saw in evaluating the last integral of (
5.1
).
Hence
\mathcal{T}_{d}
uses
\mathrm{Geom}(1/2)-1
more balls than
\mathcal{C}_{d}
, so that
\mathcal{T}_{d}=\mathcal{C}_{d}
with probability 1/2. (The fact that an
\mathrm{Exp}(1)
mixture of Poisson random variables is
\mathrm{Geom}(1/2)-1
can be seen by classifying the events of a Poisson process of rate 2 into two equally likely types, then counting those of type 1 that appear before the first event of type 2.)
In particular, the asymptotic properties of
\mathcal{C}_{d}
and of
\mathcal{T}_{d}
are the same a.s. Clearly, the law of
\mathcal{T}_{d}
is
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
-invariant and ergodic. Generally, we are interested only in geometric properties of
\mathcal{T}_{d}
that are
\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}
-invariant. For example, given
t>0
, the homothety
(x,\rho)\mapsto(tx,t\rho)
of
\mathbb{U}_{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
is an isometry that preserves
\infty
. It corresponds to changing
s
in (
5.4
) to
t^{d-1}s
. Thus,
\mathcal{T}_{d}(s)
for all
s>0
can be coupled to be isometric to each other. Hence, in studying
\mathcal{T}_{d}(s)
, the value of
s
will mainly be important for the natural coupling of
\mathcal{C}_{d}
with
\mathcal{T}_{d}
.

By virtue of
3.4
, another way to describe
\mathcal{T}_{d}(s)
is that it is the ideal Voronoi cell of the ideal nucleus
(\infty,s)
in
\mathrm{Vor}\bigl(\mathbf{X}\cup\{(\infty,s)\}\bigr)
, where
\mathbf{X}
is a PPP with intensity
\mu_{d}
on
\widetilde{\partial\mathbb{H}_{d}}
. Because
\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}
acts transitively on
\widetilde{\partial\mathbb{H}_{d}}
preserving
\mu_{d}
(
3.10
), we have that
\mathcal{T}_{d}(s)
has the same law as the cell
C(Y)
of
Y
in
\mathrm{Vor}\bigl(\mathbf{X}\cup\{Y\}\bigr)
for any fixed
Y\in\widetilde{\partial\mathbb{H}_{d}}
, provided that we restrict to the
\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}
-invariant
\sigma
-field.

Consider now the typical cell
\mathcal{T}_{d}^{(\lambda)}
in
\mathrm{Vor}(\mathbf{X}^{(\lambda)})
for
\lambda>0
. As before, we use the nucleus of a cell for its center, so that the typical cell is the cell of a typical nucleus in
\mathbf{X}^{(\lambda)}
. By Slivnyak’s theorem, as explained in
Section

4.1.2
, it follows that
\mathcal{T}_{d}^{(\lambda)}
is the cell of
\mathbf{o}
in
\mathrm{Vor}\bigl(\mathbf{X}^{(\lambda)}\cup\{\mathbf{o}\}\bigr)
. Again, this has the same law on the
\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}
-invariant
\sigma
-field as the cell
C(z)
of
z
in
\mathrm{Vor}\bigl(\mathbf{X}^{(\lambda)}\cup\{z\}\bigr)
for any
z\in\mathbb{H}_{d}
. Consider temporarily the ball model of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
and fix any
z\neq\mathbf{o}
. Let us use the dilations
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{(\lambda)}
of
Remark

3.5
together with the corresponding dilations
z^{(\lambda)}
, and let
\lambda\to 0
. The cell
C(z^{(\lambda)})
in
\mathrm{Vor}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{(\lambda)}\cup\{z^{(\lambda)}\})
will tend to the cell of
Y\coloneqq\lim_{\lambda\to 0}z^{(\lambda)}
in
\mathrm{Vor}\bigl((\Theta,\mathbf{R})\cup\{Y\}\bigr)
in the Fell topology on
\mathbb{H}_{d}
, whose law is thus that of
\mathcal{T}_{d}
. Therefore, it is reasonable to call
\mathcal{T}_{d}
the
typical (ideal) cell
.

There is another reason for that nomenclature.
Namely, we have constructed our IPVT based on a PPP on the corona,
\widetilde{\partial\mathbb{H}_{d}}
. We regard this as a marked PPP on
\widetilde{\partial\mathbb{H}_{d}}
with the mark of an ideal nucleus being its cell in
\mathbb{H}_{d}
. In this way, we may define a typical cell of
\mathcal{V}_{d}
as the cell of a typical ideal nucleus. However, the action of
\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}
on
\widetilde{\partial\mathbb{H}_{d}}
does not yield compact isotropy groups. Instead, we may use an alternative notion of Palm distribution based on disintegrations
[
Kal17
, Chapter 6]
. For a PPP
\mathbf{X}
on a standard Borel space
E
, the Palm distribution at
x\in E
is
\mathbf{X}+\delta_{x}
according to Mecke’s theorem
[
Kal17
, Lemma 6.15]
. In our case, if
\mathbf{X}
is a PPP with intensity
\mu_{d}
on
\widetilde{\partial\mathbb{H}_{d}}
and
Y\in\widetilde{\partial\mathbb{H}_{d}}
, then the typical cell corresponding to
Y
is the cell of
Y
in
\mathrm{Vor}\bigl(\mathbf{X}\cup\{Y\}\bigr)
. Transitivity allows us to call any fixed
Y
typical.

5.3.1
Height and angle of the boundary

The typical cell
\mathcal{T}_{d}
can be seen as the epigraph (see Figure
1.3
for the graph) of a stationary random function
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{{d-1}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
whose law is computed below. Specifically, for
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, let us denote by
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(x_{0})
the height of
\partial\mathcal{T}_{d}
at
x_{0}
and by
\Psi(x_{0})
the angle the hypersurface
\partial\mathcal{T}_{d}
makes with the vertical direction at that point (in other words, the complement of the angle between the vertical direction and the direction orthogonal to the hypersurface). It should be clear that this angle is defined for almost all
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{{d-1}}
, and since the law of
\bigl(\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(x_{0}),\Psi(x_{0})\bigr)
is independent of
x_{0}
, we denote it for short by
(\mathcal{H},\Theta)
.

Proposition 5.4

(
Height and angle of the boundary
)
.

For
d\geq 2
, the law of
(\mathcal{H},\Theta)
satisfies

(i)

conditional on
R_{1}=s
,

\Bigl(\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{d-1}},\sin^{2}(\Theta)\Bigr)\sim{\rm{Exp}}\left(s\right)\otimes\,{\rm Beta}\Bigl(\frac{d+1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\Bigr);

(ii)

averaging on the value of
R_{1}
,

\bigl(\mathcal{H}^{d-1},\sin^{2}(\Theta)\bigr)\sim\frac{1-U}{U}\otimes\,{\rm Beta}\Bigl(\frac{d+1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\Bigr),

where
U
is a uniform random variable over
[0,1]
.

Proof.

(i)
Without loss of generality, we consider the variables
(\mathcal{H},\Theta)=\bigl(\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbf{0}),\Psi(\mathbf{0})\bigr)
.
Condition on
R_{1}=s
. In a preliminary step, we study the first marginal of this pair, namely, the law of
\mathcal{H}
. Recall that the intensity measure of the Poisson point process
\Pi_{d}
is given by
(x,\rho)\mapsto 2s\frac{d-1}{c_{d}}\mathrm{d}x\otimes\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\rho^{2d-1}}
. The event
\mathcal{H}\leq h
corresponds to the event where this point process has no point inside the region
\bigl\{(x,\rho)\,;\;\rho^{2}>|x|^{2}+h^{2}\bigr\}
. Hence, changing variables to
y\coloneqq x/h
and

, the Voronoi partition on
V(B)
induced by
\mathbf{X}^{(\lambda)}
equals the Voronoi partition on
V(B)
induced by
\mathbf{Y}^{(\lambda)}
. When
\lambda
and thus
p
are small, this proviso holds with high probability. Hence, if the limit exists of low-intensity Bernoulli–Voronoi tessellations on
\mathbb{T}_{k}
, then so does the limit of the restriction to
V(\mathbb{T}_{k})
of the Poisson–Voronoi tessellations.

[
Bhu19
]
proved the existence of the limit of low-intensity Bernoulli–Voronoi tessellations on regular trees by explicitly calculating the probabilities of all elementary cylinder events, showing that they are polynomials in
p
(with rational coefficients). For example,
[
Bhu19
, Lemma 2.5]
shows that the degree of the root equals
j\in[1,k]
with limiting probability

\frac{1}{(k-2)(j-1)+1}\cdot\frac{1}{\prod_{i=j}^{k-1}(1+\frac{1}{i(k-2)})}.

It is unclear whether there are any Cayley graphs other than trees where there is a unique low-intensity limit of Bernoulli–Voronoi tessellations, except when the limit is trivial; see
[
Bhu19
]
.

The following proposition is the analogue of the corresponding result,
3.9
, for
\mathbb{H}_{d}
.

Proposition 6.3

(
One end on trees
)
.

For each
\xi\in[1,k-1)
, a.s. no cell in the IPVT
\mathcal{I}_{\xi}
on
\mathbb{T}_{k}
contains a biinfinite geodesic.

Proof.

Let the delays be
(\mathbb{D}_{i}\,;\;i\geq 1)
in increasing order corresponding to the ideal boundary points
{\Theta}_{i}
. By
6.1
, we have
\mathbb{P}\bigl[\mathbb{D}_{1}<-n\bigr]=O\bigl((k-1)^{-n}\bigr)
as
n\to\infty
and
\mathbb{P}\bigl[\mathbb{D}_{1}\geq n\bigr]\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\xi(k-1)^{n-1}}
. For any vertex
x
, let
\mathbb{D}_{i}^{x}\coloneqq\mathrm{d}_{{\Theta}_{i}}(x)+\mathbb{D}_{i}
be the separation of
({\Theta}_{i},\mathbb{D}_{i})
from
x
. Then
\min_{i}\mathbb{D}_{i}^{x}
has the same distribution as
\mathbb{D}_{1}
by automorphism invariance. Let
S^{\prime}_{2n}
be the set of vertices
x
such that the geodesic from
x
to
{\Theta}_{1}
contains the geodesic from
\mathbf{o}
to
{\Theta}_{1}
as well as precisely
2n
additional edges. For
x\in S^{\prime}_{2n}
, we have
\mathbb{D}_{1}^{x}=\mathbb{D}_{1}+2n
. Also, any such
x
belongs to the cell of
\mathbf{o}
iff
\mathbb{D}_{1}^{x}=\min_{i}\mathbb{D}_{i}^{x}
. Let
A_{n}
be the event that some
x\in S^{\prime}_{2n}
belongs to the cell of
\mathbf{o}
. Let
S_{2n}
be the sphere of radius
2n
about
\mathbf{o}
, which has cardinality
k(k-1)^{2n-1}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl[\mathbb{D}_{1}<-n\bigr]+\mathbb{P}\bigl[\exists x\in S^{\prime}_{2n}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{D}_{1}\geq-n,\;\mathbb{D}_{1}^{x}<\mathbb{D}_{i}^{x}\text{ for all }i>1\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl[\mathbb{D}_{1}<-n\bigr]+\mathbb{P}\bigl[\exists x\in S^{\prime}_{2n}\kern 5.0pt\min_{i}\mathbb{D}_{i}^{x}\geq n\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl[\mathbb{D}_{1}<-n\bigr]+\mathbb{P}\bigl[\exists x\in S_{2n}\kern 5.0pt\min_{i}\mathbb{D}_{i}^{x}\geq n\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq O\bigl((k-1)^{-n}\bigr)+k(k-1)^{2n-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\xi(k-1)^{n-1}}\to 0\quad\mbox{as }n\to\infty,

where the last inequality follows from the second sentence of the proof.

It follows that
\mathbb{P}\bigl(\bigcap_{n}A_{n}\bigr)=0
, in other words,
\mathbf{o}
does not belong to a biinfinite geodesic in the cell of
\mathbf{o}
a.s. Since
\mathcal{I}_{\xi}
is invariant under all automorphisms of
\mathbb{T}_{k}
, the same holds for every vertex in place of
\mathbf{o}
, whence all cells have only one end a.s.
∎

We use this result to prove our assertion that
\mathcal{I}_{\xi}
is different for different
\xi\in[1,k-1)
:

Proposition 6.4
.

There is a measurable function
f
on tessellations of
\mathbb{T}_{k}
such that for each
\xi\in[1,k-1)
, we have
f(\mathcal{I}_{\xi})=\xi
a.s.

Proof.

Let
V
be a tessellation. Define
f
to be 0 if some cell of
V
does not have a unique end. If each cell has a unique end, then let
\theta_{1}
be the end of the cell of
\mathbf{o}
. Set
g(\theta_{1})\coloneqq 0
. There is a unique extension of
g
to the set
E
of ends of all cells such that when two cells with ends
\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}
share a boundary point
x
, we have
g(\theta)+\mathrm{d}_{\theta}(x)=g(\theta^{\prime})+\mathrm{d}_{\theta^{\prime}}(x)
. When
V
is the tessellation corresponding to ideal nuclei
({\Theta}_{i},\mathbb{D}_{i})
, we have that
g({\Theta}_{i})=\mathbb{D}_{i}-\mathbb{D}_{1}
a.s. Define
G(t)\coloneqq|\{\theta\in E\,;\;g(\theta)\leq t\}|
for
t\geq 0
. Finally, let

f(V)\coloneqq\limsup_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{G(t+s)}{(k-1)^{t+s}}\,\mathrm{d}s\Bigm/\int_{0}^{1}\frac{s+1/(k-2)}{(k-1)^{s}}\,\mathrm{d}s.

In order to show that
f(\mathcal{I}_{\xi})=\xi
a.s., consider the Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}
described in
6.1
, and denote by
N(t)
the number of its points at most
t
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Now
\lim_{t\to\infty}N(t)/\mathbb{E}\bigl[N(t)\bigr]=1
a.s. with

\mathbb{E}\bigl[N(t)\bigr]=\xi\Bigl(\sum_{m<\lfloor t\rfloor}(k-1)^{m}+(k-1)^{\lfloor t\rfloor}\bigl(t-\lfloor t\rfloor\bigr)\Bigr)=\xi\Bigl((k-1)^{\lfloor t\rfloor}\bigl(t-\lfloor t\rfloor+\frac{1}{k-2}\bigr)\Bigr),

whence

\frac{N(t)}{(k-1)^{t}}\sim\xi\frac{t-\lfloor t\rfloor+1/(k-2)}{(k-1)^{t-\lfloor t\rfloor}}\mbox{ a.s.\ as }t\to\infty.

At every sample where this holds, we obtain

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{N(t+s)}{(k-1)^{t+s}}\,\mathrm{d}s=\xi\int_{0}^{1}\frac{s+1/(k-2)}{(k-1)^{s}}\,\mathrm{d}s.

On the other hand,
G(t)=N(t+\mathbb{D}_{1})
when
V=\mathcal{I}_{\xi}
, which proves that
f(\mathcal{I}_{\xi})=\xi
a.s.
∎

Similarly to the last part of the proof of
Theorem

3.11
, one can extend this argument to prove that for each
\xi\in[1,k-1)
, there is an isomorphism between the Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}
described in
6.1
and the IPVT on
\mathbb{T}_{k}
that is equivariant with respect to all automorphisms of
\mathbb{T}_{k}
.

Conjecture 7.5

Regarding

Question 7.7

(
Variance of the volume of the typical cell
)
.

Let

Question 7.8

(
Indistinguishability
)
.

All cells of
\mathcal{V}_{d}
share the same asymptotics, which are those of the typical cell. Are they, in fact, indistinguishable? This would mean that for every measurable set
A
of pairs
(C,V)
, where
C
is a cell of a tessellation
V
of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
, if
A
is invariant under the diagonal action of
\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}
, then a.s., for all cells
C\in\mathcal{V}_{d}
, we have
(C,\mathcal{V}_{d})\in A
or, for all cells
C\in\mathcal{V}_{d}
, we have
(C,\mathcal{V}_{d})\notin A
. Since the IPVT is
\textnormal{{M\"{o}b}}_{d}
-ergodic, this is equivalent to
\mathbb{P}\bigl[(\mathcal{C}_{d},\mathcal{V}_{d})\in A\bigr]\in\{0,1\}
. Because of
Theorem

3.11
, indistinguishability is equivalent to the nonexistence of a proper factor of the PPP
\mathbf{X}
on the corona, in other words, the nonexistence of an equivariant map
f
on discrete subsets of
\widetilde{\partial\mathbb{H}_{d}}
such that
\varnothing\neq f(\mathbf{X})\subsetneq\mathbf{X}
a.s. The concept of indistinguishability is important in percolation theory
[
LS99
]
and in measured group theory
[
GL09
]
.

Update: Sam Mellick
[
Mel24
]
has proved indistinguishability after seeing our question.

4.4

for Hausdorff dimension in trees). What is that constant?
