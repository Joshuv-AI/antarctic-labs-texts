Random geometric graphs

Let us briefly consider the history of Hamilton cycles in random geometric graphs.
The sharp threshold for connectivity in

Díaz, Mitsche and Pérez-Giménez

See the works of

In this paper, we consider graphs perturbed by a random geometric graph.

1.3.
Powers of Hamilton cycles

For any integer
k\geq 1
, the
k
-th power of a graph
G
is obtained by adding an edge between any two vertices whose distance in
G
is at most
k
.
Powers of Hamilton cycles have received much attention in the literature.

Komlós, Sárközy and Szemerédi 1996
,
Komlós, Sárközy and Szemerédi 1998
proved that, for
n
sufficiently large, any
n
-vertex graph with minimum degree at least
kn/(k+1)
contains the
k
-th power of a Hamilton cycle.
The threshold for the containment of the
k
-th power (
k\geq 2
) of a Hamilton cycle in
G(n,p)
is
n^{-1/k}
: this was determined by
Kühn and Osthus 2012
for
k\geq 3
(applying an earlier result of
Riordan 2000
) and, very recently, by
Kahn, Narayanan and Park 2021
for
k=2
.

Dudek, Reiher, Ruciński and Schacht 2020
proved that, if
\delta(H_{n})\geq kn/(k+1)
, then
H_{n}\cup G(n,C/n)
a.a.s. contains a
(k+1)
-th power of a Hamilton cycle,
before
Nenadov and Trujić 2021
strengthened this result, forcing the
(2k+1)
-th power of a Hamilton cycle in the same situation.

Antoniuk, Dudek, Reiher, Ruciński and Schacht 2021
, and more recently
Antoniuk, Dudek and Ruciński 2022
, expanded in several ways on the result in
[
24
]
.
Very recently,
Böttcher, Parczyk, Sgueglia and Skokan 2022
completely solved the problem for
H_{n}\cup G(n,p)
containing the square of a Hamilton cycle, giving the
G(n,p)
-perturbed threshold when
H_{n}
has minimum degree
\alpha n
for
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
.
Interestingly, this perturbed threshold exhibits a ‘jumping’ behaviour in terms of
\alpha
, presenting an infinite number of ‘jumps’ (such jumps had previously been observed in the
G(n,p)
-perturbed thresholds for the containment of clique
[
31
]
and cycle
[
16
]
factors in
H_{n}\cup G(n,p)
, but only finitely many).

In contrast with the behaviour in binomial random graphs, where the threshold is different for each
k\geq 1
, the threshold for the containment of the
k
-th power of a Hamilton cycle in random geometric graphs is
(\log n/n)^{1/d}
independently of the value of
k
.
Indeed, simply observe that, if
G^{d}(n,r)
contains a Hamilton cycle
\mathcal{C}
, then, by the triangle inequality,
G^{d}(n,kr)
must contain the
k
-th power of
\mathcal{C}
.
This simple argument cannot be used to extend
Theorem

1.1
, however, as any Hamilton cycle in this setting will use edges of
H_{n}
, which may have arbitrary lengths.

We extend
Theorem

1.1
in this paper, proving the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any integers
d,k\geq 1
and
\alpha\in(0,k/(k+1))
,
n^{-1/d}
is a
G^{d}(n,r)
-perturbed threshold for the containment of the
k
-th power of a Hamilton cycle.

Observe that the
G^{d}(n,r)
-perturbed threshold in
Theorem

1.2
is the same as that in
Theorem

1.1
.
Indeed, this accords with the behaviour of
G^{d}(n,r)
.
Note also that this improves upon the threshold in the purely random setting by a logarithmic factor.
The behaviour of the threshold is very different when compared with the same problem in graphs perturbed by
G(n,p)
: indeed, the
G^{d}(n,r)
-perturbed threshold is the same for all values of
k
, and there are no jumps in the behaviour of the threshold as a function of
\alpha
.

In order to prove
Theorem

1.2
, we will establish the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

For any integers
d,k\geq 1
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant
C
such that the following holds.
Let
H_{n}
be an
n
-vertex graph with minimum degree at least
\alpha n
and let
r\geq(C/n)^{1/d}
.
Then, a.a.s.
H_{n}\cup G^{d}(n,r)
contains the
k
-th power of a Hamilton cycle.

This provides the upper bound for the threshold.
We will provide a construction for the lower bound in
Section

4
, after proving
Theorem

1.3
.

Our proof of
Theorem

1.3
builds on ideas which have been successful in proving results about Hamiltonicity in random geometric graphs
[
25
,
10
,
45
,
26
,
9
,
28
]
.
We partition the hypercube
[0,1]^{d}
into smaller cubes, called
cells
, of side comparable with
r
, and analyse the number of vertices that fall into each of these cells.
The side is chosen so that most cells will contain “many” vertices, which provides high local connectivity.
On the other hand, a global structure using the cells is used to obtain global connectivity.
In our case, the random geometric graph we are working with is below the threshold for connectivity, so the desired global structure cannot be obtained purely in
G^{d}(n,r)
; to achieve the desired global connectivity we will use some of the edges of
H_{n}
, adapting an absorption idea introduced in
[
26
]
.

1.4.
Applications

Our main result has a series of corollaries that extend several lines of research in randomly perturbed graphs into graphs perturbed by a random geometric graph.

The first of these lines deals with

A different direction is that of universality.

For any integer

In more generality, we may consider the concept of bandwidth.

For any integers

By extension, the same is true of

2.1.
Notation

For any

For parameters

2.2.

Let

), and let

For some positive

such that

and

In order to bound the deviations of certain random variables, we consider the following consequence of Azuma’s inequality (see, e.g.,

Lemma 2.1
.

Let

be independent random variables taking values in a set

Let

Then, for any

The following simple observation will come in useful for proving the lower bounds of the thresholds.

Proposition 4.1

Let

If

o(n)

Proof.

For any two distinct indices

, where the implied constant depends only on

\mathbb{E}[|E(G^{d}(n,r))|]=\left(\kern-1.00006pt\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{2}\kern-1.00006pt\right)\cdot\Theta(r^{d})=o(n)
.
