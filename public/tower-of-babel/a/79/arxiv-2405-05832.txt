Introduction

In this article, we deal with the multiplicity of weak solutions to the singular elliptic problems given by

{}\begin{split}-\Delta_{p}u+(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}u&=\frac{\lambda}{u^{\gamma}}+u^{r}\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u&>0\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u&=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\Omega,\end{split}

(1.1)

where
\displaystyle\gamma\in(0,1)
,
\displaystyle n>p>1
,
\displaystyle s\in(0,1)
,
\displaystyle\lambda>0
,
\displaystyle p<r+1<p^{*}
,
\displaystyle\Omega
is bounded domain in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\displaystyle C^{1}
boundary;
and

{}\begin{split}-\Delta u+(-\Delta)^{s}u&=\frac{\lambda}{u^{\gamma}}+u^{r}\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u&>0\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u&=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\Omega,\end{split}

(1.2)

for
\displaystyle\gamma\geq 1
. Of course, in the latter case, we need strict convexity and smooth boundary of
\displaystyle\Omega
along with
\displaystyle 0<s<1/2
.
To this aim, we start with a brief background of the problems available in the literature.

Singular elliptic problems have been extensively studied for the past few decades, starting with the pioneering work of Crandall-Rabinowitz-Tartar
[
20
]
, who showed that the unperturbed case of (
1.2
) (and for local operator), under Dirichlet boundary conditions given by

{}\begin{array}[]{c}-\Delta u=\frac{f}{u^{\gamma}}\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u>0\;\text{ in }\Omega,\quad u=0\;\text{ in }\partial\Omega,\end{array}

(1.3)

admits a unique solution
\displaystyle u\in C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C(\overline{\Omega})
for any
\displaystyle\gamma>0
along with the fact that the solution behaves like a distance function near the boundary provided
\displaystyle f
is Hölder Continuous. Interestingly enough, Lazer-Mckenna
[
37
]
showed that the unique solution obtained by
[
20
]
is indeed in
\displaystyle W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
if and only if
\displaystyle 0<\gamma<3
. They also showed that the solution belongs to
\displaystyle C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
provided
\displaystyle 0<\gamma<1
. This was followed for the perturbed singular case by the work of Haitao
[
35
]
, who studied the problem

{}\begin{array}[]{c}-\Delta u=\frac{\lambda}{u^{\gamma}}+u^{r}\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u>0\;\text{ in }\Omega,\quad u=0\;\text{ in }\partial\Omega,\end{array}

(1.4)

and showed the existence of
\displaystyle\Lambda>0
such that there exists at least two solutions
\displaystyle u,v\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
to problem (
1.4
) for
\displaystyle\lambda<\Lambda
, no solution for
\displaystyle\lambda>\Lambda
and at least one solution for
\displaystyle\lambda=\Lambda
provided
\displaystyle 0<\gamma<1<r\leq 2^{*}-1
. The generalization of these results for
\displaystyle p
-Laplacian was given by Giacomoni et al.
[
31
]
, who showed the existence of at least two solutions for
\displaystyle 0<\gamma<1
and
\displaystyle p-1<r\leq p^{*}-1
. In the above-mentioned works on the perturbed problems, the solution so obtained satisfied the boundary condition in the trace sense, and the restriction
\displaystyle 0<\gamma<1
is due to the use of variational methods which require the associated functional to be well-defined on
\displaystyle W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
.

Boccardo-Orsina
[
16
]
in a beautiful paper showed the followings regarding solutions of (
1.3
)

\begin{cases}u\in W_{0}^{1,\frac{nm(1+\gamma)}{n-m(1-\gamma)}}(\Omega)&\text{ if }0<\gamma<1\text{ and }f\in L^{m}(\Omega)\text{ with }m\in\left[1,\left(2^{*}/(1-\gamma)\right)^{\prime}\right),\\
u\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)&\text{ if }0<\gamma<1\text{ and }f\in L^{m}(\Omega)\text{ with }m=\left(2^{*}/(1-\gamma)\right)^{\prime},\\
u\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)&\text{ if }\gamma=1\text{ and }f\in L^{1}(\Omega),\\
u^{\frac{1+\gamma}{2}}\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)&\text{ if }\gamma>1\text{ and }f\in L^{1}(\Omega).\end{cases}

The boundary condition is now understood as such that
\displaystyle u^{\frac{1+\gamma}{2}}
belongs to
\displaystyle W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. This has been generalized by Canino et al.
[
19
]
for the
\displaystyle p
-Laplacian where existence of a solution
\displaystyle u\in W_{\operatorname{loc}}^{1,p}(\Omega)
was shown for
\displaystyle\gamma>0
and
\displaystyle f\in L^{1}(\Omega)
such that
\displaystyle u^{\frac{p-1+\delta}{p}}\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
. The perturbed problem (
1.4
) was studied by Arcoya-Boccardo
[
5
]
for
\displaystyle 0<\gamma<1
using the variational method. Again for
\displaystyle\gamma\geq 1
, Arcoya-Mérida
[
6
]
obtained the existence of at least two solutions in
\displaystyle W_{\operatorname{loc}}^{1,2}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Moreover, any solution
\displaystyle u
so obtained satisfies
\displaystyle u^{\frac{1+\gamma}{2}}\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. This was generalized for the quasilinear case by Bal-Garain
[
9
]
for any
\displaystyle\frac{2n+2}{n+2}<p<n
.

For the nonlocal case of
Eq.

1.3
, we refer
[
10

, where the authors obtained, among other results, existence and summability of weak solutions. For multiplicity results in nonlocal perturbed singular case, with

and obtained plenty of results regarding existence and other properties of solutions. In

1.1

[

In this article, we will extensively use the regularity results, maximum principles and other properties obtained in

Goal of this paper:

We intend to deduce the existence of two different weak solutions to
Eq.

1.1
, with
\displaystyle 1<p<\infty
and for
\displaystyle 0<\gamma<1
. For
\displaystyle\gamma\geq 1
, we continue to show the existence of at least two weak solutions of the semilinear case of
Eq.

1.1
given by
Eq.

1.2
.

Difficulties:
To deal with the singular term, we follow the classical approach of obtaining solutions to a sequence of approximated problems given by

{}\begin{array}[]{c}-\Delta_{p}u+(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}u=\frac{\lambda}{(u+\frac{1}{k})^{\gamma}}+u^{r}\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u>0\text{ in }\Omega,\quad u=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\Omega,\end{array}

(1.5)

where
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}
. Note that this problem is non-singular for any
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}
. We start by showing the existence of two different solutions to
Eq.

1.5
for each
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}
. For
Eq.

1.1
, we will use the variational technique (as
\displaystyle\gamma<1
allows us so) and show that the functional corresponding to
Eq.

1.5
satisfies the mountain pass geometry. We will obtain a solution as the critical point of the functional which is different from the minimizer. This, along with a uniform apriori bound in
\displaystyle W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
, will give our multiplicity result (of course, one needs to show the almost everywhere convergence of the gradients of weak solutions (of
Eq.

1.5
) to the gradient of their limit, to pass
\displaystyle k\to\infty
in
Eq.

1.5
).

For
Eq.

1.2
, we obtain a uniform apriori estimate for the
\displaystyle L^{\infty}
bound of the solutions of
Eq.

1.5
(with
\displaystyle p=2
) independent of
\displaystyle k
and then use Leray-Schauder degree. We conclude by passing to the limit to obtain two distinct solutions to our main problem. One of this study’s main challenges is finding the uniform a priori estimates independent of
\displaystyle k
. For the Laplacian operator, one can use Kelvin transformation (see
[
6
]
) which fails for the mixed local-nonlocal case. We overcome this difficulty by considering strict convexity of our domain. Once we have a uniform neighbourhood of the boundary, the blow-up analysis of Gidas-Spruck
[
34
]
goes through. Here we consider the fractional Laplace operator as a lower order term and approach by taking limit.

Another difficulty arises in constructing an appropriate subsolution to the approximated problem for the unperturbed case, which is handled by taking the first eigenfunction of Laplacian and using its regularity. It is important to mention that we also prove existence result regarding
Eq.

1.2
in
Section

4
, which represents the boundary data of
\displaystyle u
. A result regarding nonexistence of solutions to
Eq.

1.2
for large
\displaystyle\lambda
is also obtained.

2.2
Function Spaces

In this section, we present
definitions and properties of some function spaces that will be useful for our work. We recall that for
\displaystyle E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the Lebesgue space

\displaystyle L^{p}(E),1\leq p<\infty
, is defined to be the space of
\displaystyle p
-integrable functions
\displaystyle u:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with the finite norm

\|u\|_{L^{p}(E)}=\left(\int_{E}|u(x)|^{p}dx\right)^{1/p}.

By
\displaystyle L_{\operatorname{loc}}^{p}(E)
we denote the space of locally
\displaystyle p
-integrable functions, which means,
\displaystyle u\in L_{\operatorname{loc}}^{p}(E)
if and only if
\displaystyle u\in L^{p}(F)
for every
\displaystyle F\subset\subset E
. In the case
\displaystyle 0<p<1
, we denote by
\displaystyle L^{p}(E)
a set of measurable functions such that
\displaystyle\int_{E}|u(x)|^{p}dx<\infty
.

Definition 2.1
.

The Sobolev space
\displaystyle W^{1,p}(\Omega)
, for
\displaystyle 1\leq p<\infty
, is defined as the Banach space of locally integrable weakly differentiable functions
\displaystyle u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
equipped with the following norm

\|u\|_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}=\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

The space
\displaystyle W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
is defined as the closure of the space
\displaystyle{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, in the norm of the Sobolev space
\displaystyle W^{1,p}(\Omega)
, where
\displaystyle{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
is the set of all smooth functions whose supports are compactly contained in
\displaystyle\Omega
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\displaystyle 0<s<1
and
\displaystyle\Omega
be an open connected subset of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\displaystyle C^{1}
boundary. The fractional Sobolev space
\displaystyle W^{s,q}(\Omega)
for any
\displaystyle 1\leq q<+\infty
is defined by

W^{s,q}(\Omega)=\left\{u\in L^{q}(\Omega):\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|^{\frac{n}{q}+s}}\in L^{q}(\Omega\times\Omega)\right\},

and it is endowed with the norm

{}\|u\|_{W^{s,q}(\Omega)}=\left(\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{q}dx+\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{q}}{|x-y|^{n+sq}}dxdy\right)^{1/q}.

(2.1)

It can be treated as an intermediate space between
\displaystyle W^{1,q}(\Omega)
and
\displaystyle L^{q}(\Omega)
. For
\displaystyle 0<s\leq s^{\prime}<1
,
\displaystyle W^{s^{\prime},q}(\Omega)
is continuously embedded in
\displaystyle W^{s,q}(\Omega)
, see [
25
, Proposition 2.1]. The fractional Sobolev space with zero boundary values is defined by

W_{0}^{s,q}(\Omega)=\left\{u\in W^{s,q}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):u=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\Omega\right\}.

However
\displaystyle W_{0}^{s,q}(\Omega)
can be treated as the closure of
\displaystyle{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
in
\displaystyle W^{s,q}(\Omega)
with respect to the fractional Sobolev norm defined in
Eq.

2.1
. Both
\displaystyle W^{s,q}(\Omega)
and
\displaystyle W_{0}^{s,q}(\Omega)
are reflexive Banach spaces, for
\displaystyle q>1
, for details we refer to the readers [
25
, Section 2]. The spaces
\displaystyle W^{1,p}(\Omega)
and
\displaystyle W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
are also reflexive for
\displaystyle p>1
.

The following result asserts that the classical Sobolev space is continuously embedded in the fractional Sobolev space; see [
25
, Proposition 2.2]. The idea applies an extension property of
\displaystyle\Omega
so that we can extend functions from
\displaystyle W^{1,q}(\Omega)
to
\displaystyle W^{1,q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and that the extension operator is bounded.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\displaystyle\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\displaystyle C^{0,1}
boundary. Then
\displaystyle\exists\,C=C(\Omega,n,s)>0
such that

\|u\|_{W^{s,q}(\Omega)}\leq C\|u\|_{W^{1,q}(\Omega)},

for every
\displaystyle u\in W^{1,q}(\Omega)
.

For the fractional Sobolev spaces with zero boundary value, the next embedding result follows from [
17
, Lemma 2.1]. The fundamental difference of it compared to
Lemma

2.3
is that the result holds for any bounded domain (without any smoothness condition on the boundary), since for the Sobolev spaces with zero boundary value, we always have a zero extension to the complement.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\displaystyle\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\displaystyle 0<s<1
. Then
\displaystyle\exists\,C=C(n,s,\Omega)>0
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{q}}{|x-y|^{n+sq}}dxdy\leq C\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{q}dx,

for every
\displaystyle u\in W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)
. Here, we consider the zero extension of
\displaystyle u
to the complement of
\displaystyle\Omega
.

We now proceed with the basic Poincaré inequality, which can be found in [
26
, Chapter 5, Section 5.8.1].

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with
\displaystyle{C}^{1}
boundary and
\displaystyle q\geq 1
. Then there exists a positive constant
\displaystyle C>0
depending only on
\displaystyle n
and
\displaystyle\Omega
, such that

\int_{\Omega}|u|^{q}dx\leq C\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{q}dx,\qquad\forall u\in W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega).

Specifically if we take
\displaystyle\Omega=B_{\bar{r}}
, then we will get for all
\displaystyle u\in W^{1,q}(B_{\bar{r}})
,

\fint_{B_{\bar{r}}}\left|u-(u)_{B_{\bar{r}}}\right|^{q}dx\leq c{\bar{r}}^{q}\fint_{B_{\bar{r}}}|\nabla u|^{q}dx,

where
\displaystyle c
is a constant depending only on
\displaystyle n
, and
\displaystyle(u)_{B_{\bar{r}}}
denotes the average of
\displaystyle u
in
\displaystyle B_{\bar{r}}
, and
\displaystyle B_{\bar{r}}
denotes a ball of radius
\displaystyle{\bar{r}}
centered at
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Using
Lemma

2.4
, and the above Poincaré inequality, we observe that the following norm on the space
\displaystyle W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)
defined by

\|u\|_{W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)}=\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{q}dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{q}}{|x-y|^{n+sq}}dxdy\right)^{1/q},

is equivalent to the norm

\|u\|_{W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)}=\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{q}dx\right)^{1/q}.

The following is a version of fractional Poincaré.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with
\displaystyle{C}^{1}
boundary,
\displaystyle s\in(0,1)
and
\displaystyle q\geq 1
. If
\displaystyle u\in W^{s,q}_{0}(\Omega)
, then

\int_{\Omega}|u|^{q}dx\leq c\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{q}}{|x-y|^{n+sq}}dxdy,

holds with
\displaystyle c\equiv c(n,s,\Omega)
.

In view of
Lemma

2.6
, we observe that the Banach space
\displaystyle W_{0}^{s,q}(\Omega)
can be endowed with the norm

\|u\|_{W_{0}^{s,q}(\Omega)}=\left(\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{q}}{|x-y|^{n+sq}}dxdy\right)^{1/q},

which is equivalent to that of
\displaystyle\|u\|_{W^{s,q}(\Omega)}
.
Now, we define the local spaces as

W_{\operatorname{loc}}^{1,q}(\Omega)=\left\{u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}:u\in L^{q}(K),\int_{K}|\nabla u|^{q}dx<\infty,\text{ for every }K\subset\subset\Omega\right\},

and

W_{\operatorname{loc}}^{s,q}(\Omega)=\left\{u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}:u\in L^{q}(K),\int_{K}\int_{K}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{q}}{|x-y|^{n+sq}}dxdy<\infty,\text{ for every }K\subset\subset\Omega\right\}.

Now for
\displaystyle n>p
, we define the critical Sobolev exponent as
\displaystyle p^{*}=\frac{np}{n-p}
, then we get the following embedding result for any bounded open subset
\displaystyle\Omega
of class
\displaystyle{C}^{1}
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
, see for details [
26
, Chapter 5].

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\displaystyle n>p
. Then,
\displaystyle\exists\,C\equiv C(n,\Omega)>0
, such that for all
\displaystyle u\in{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)

\|u\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\Omega)}^{p}\leq C\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p}dx.

Moreover the inclusion map

W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{r}(\Omega)

is continuous for
\displaystyle 1\leq r\leq p^{*}
and the above embedding is compact except for
\displaystyle r=p^{*}
.

Similarly, for
\displaystyle n>ps
, we define the fractional Sobolev critical exponent as
\displaystyle p_{s}^{*}=\frac{np}{n-ps}
. The following result is a fractional version of the Sobolev inequality (
Theorem

2.7
) which also implies a continuous embedding of
\displaystyle W_{0}^{s,p}(\Omega)
in the critical Lebesgue space
\displaystyle L^{p_{s}^{*}}(\Omega)
. One can see the proof in
[
25
]
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
\displaystyle 0<s<1
be such that
\displaystyle n>ps
. Then, there exists a constant
\displaystyle S(n,s)
depending only on
\displaystyle n
and
\displaystyle s
, such that for all
\displaystyle u\in{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)

\|u\|_{L^{p_{s}^{*}}(\Omega)}\leq S(n,s)\left(\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{n+sp}}dxdy\right)^{1/p}.

We now recall the following algebraic inequality that can be found in [
2
, Lemma 2.22].

Lemma 2.9
.

i)

Let
\displaystyle\alpha>0
. For every
\displaystyle x,y\geq 0
one has

(x-y)(x^{\alpha}-y^{\alpha})\geq\frac{4\alpha}{(\alpha+1)^{2}}\left(x^{\frac{\alpha+1}{2}}-y^{\frac{\alpha+1}{2}}\right)^{2}.

ii)

Let
\displaystyle 0<\alpha\leq 1
. For every
\displaystyle x,y\geq 0
with
\displaystyle x\neq y
one has

\frac{x-y}{x^{\alpha}-y^{\alpha}}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}(x^{1-\alpha}+y^{1-\alpha}).

iii)

Let
\displaystyle\alpha\geq 1
. Then there exists a constant
\displaystyle C_{\alpha}
depending only on
\displaystyle\alpha
such that

|x+y|^{\alpha-1}|x-y|\leq C_{\alpha}\left|x^{\alpha}-y^{\alpha}\right|.

Next, we state the algebraic inequality from [

, Lemma 2.1].

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\displaystyle 1<p<\infty
. Then for any
\displaystyle a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a constant
\displaystyle C\equiv C(p)>0
, such that

\langle|a|^{p-2}a-|b|^{p-2}b\ ,a-b\rangle\geq C\frac{|a-b|^{2}}{(|a|+|b|)^{2-p}}.

2.3
Weak Solutions and Main Results

We are interested in multiplicity of weak solutions to the problem

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\inf_{\|v\|=R}I_{\lambda,\epsilon}(v)&\geq&\inf_{\|v\|=R}I_{0,\epsilon}(v)-\lambda\sup_{\|v\|=R}\left(\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\int_{\Omega}|v|^{1-\gamma}dx\right)\geq 2\rho-\lambda\sup_{\|v\|=R}\left(\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\int_{\Omega}|v|^{1-\gamma}dx\right)\geq\rho,\end{array}

if
\displaystyle\lambda\in(0,\Lambda)
. Finally, it is easy to observe that
\displaystyle I_{0,\epsilon}\left(te_{1}\right)\rightarrow-\infty
as
\displaystyle t\rightarrow+\infty
, and hence we can choose
\displaystyle T>R
such that
\displaystyle I_{0,\epsilon}\left(Te_{1}\right)<-1
. Hence

I_{\lambda,\epsilon}(Te_{1})\leq I_{0,\epsilon}(Te_{1})<-1,

which completes the proof.
∎

Testing

Lemma 2.22

{}\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w_{\epsilon}|^{p}dx+\frac{1}{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|w_{\epsilon}(x)-w_{\epsilon}(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{n+sp}}dxdy-\lambda\int_{\Omega}\frac{\left(w_{\epsilon}+\epsilon\right)^{1-\gamma}-\epsilon^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}dx-\frac{1}{r+1}\int_{\Omega}w_{\epsilon}^{r+1}dx\leq A.

\displaystyle\phi=-\frac{w_{\epsilon}}{r+1}

\displaystyle w_{\epsilon}

2.4

{}-\frac{1}{r+1}\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w_{\epsilon}|^{p}dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|w_{\epsilon}(x)-w_{\epsilon}(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{n+sp}}dxdy\right)+\frac{\lambda}{r+1}\int_{\Omega}\frac{w_{\epsilon}}{\left(w_{\epsilon}+\epsilon\right)^{\gamma}}dx+\frac{1}{r+1}\int_{\Omega}w_{\epsilon}^{r+1}dx=0.

(2.24)

Adding

2.23

2.24

\begin{array}[]{l}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{r+1}\right)\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w_{\epsilon}|^{p}dx+\frac{1}{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|w_{\epsilon}(x)-w_{\epsilon}(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{n+sp}}dxdy\right)\\

2.7

Remark 2.23
.

In view of

Lemma 2.24
.

Suppose
\displaystyle u_{0}
be as in
Remark

2.23
. Further assume that for each
\displaystyle\omega\subset\subset\Omega
, there exists a constant
\displaystyle c>0
, depending on
\displaystyle\omega
such that for all
\displaystyle\varepsilon
,
\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}\geq c(\omega)
in
\displaystyle\omega
. Then up to a subsequence,
\displaystyle\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow\nabla u
pointwise almost everywhere in
\displaystyle\Omega
.

Proof.

Let us take a compact
\displaystyle K\subset\Omega
and consider a function
\displaystyle\phi_{K}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\displaystyle\operatorname{supp}\phi_{K}=\omega
,
\displaystyle 0\leq\phi_{K}\leq 1
in
\displaystyle\Omega
and
\displaystyle\phi_{K}\equiv 1
in
\displaystyle K
. Now for
\displaystyle\mu>0
, we define the truncated function
\displaystyle T_{\mu}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

T_{\mu}(t)=\begin{cases}t,&\text{ if }|t|\leq\mu,\\
\mu\frac{t}{|t|},&\text{ if }|t|>\mu.\end{cases}

Now choose
\displaystyle\psi_{\epsilon}=\phi_{K}T_{\mu}\left(\left(u_{\epsilon}-u_{0}\right)\right)\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
as a test function in
Eq.

2.4
, to get

I+J=R+S,

where

\begin{array}[]{c}I=\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u_{\epsilon}\right|^{p-2}\nabla u_{\epsilon}\cdot\nabla\psi_{\epsilon}\,dx,\quad J=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u_{\epsilon}(x)-u_{\epsilon}(y)|^{p-2}(u_{\epsilon}(x)-u_{\epsilon}(y))(\psi_{\epsilon}(x)-\psi_{\epsilon}(y))}{|x-y|^{n+ps}}dxdy\\
R=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\lambda\psi_{\epsilon}}{\left(u_{\epsilon}+\epsilon\right)^{\gamma}}dx\quad\text{ and }\quad S=\int_{\Omega}(u_{\epsilon})^{r}\psi_{\epsilon}\,dx.\end{array}

As
\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}
is uniformly bounded in
\displaystyle W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
, we estimate
\displaystyle S
by using Hölder and Sobolev inequality as

S\leq\mu\int_{\Omega}(u_{\epsilon})^{r}dx\leq\mu\left(\int_{\Omega}u_{\epsilon}\,dx\right)^{r}\leq C\mu(\|u_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)})^{r}\leq C\mu(\|u_{\epsilon}\|_{W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)})^{r}\leq C\mu,

for
\displaystyle p-1<r<1
(the case when
\displaystyle 1<p<2
). Now for
\displaystyle 1\leq r<p^{*}-1<p^{*}
, one gets

S\leq\mu\int_{\Omega}(u_{\epsilon})^{r}dx\leq C\mu\left(\int_{\Omega}(u_{\epsilon})^{p^{*}}dx\right)^{r/p^{*}}\leq C\mu(\|u_{\epsilon}\|_{W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)})^{r}\leq C\mu.

Denoting by
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\eta(x,y)=|\eta(x)-\eta(y)|^{p-2}(\eta(x)-\eta(y))
and
\displaystyle d\nu=\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n+ps}}dxdy
, we can write
\displaystyle J
as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}J&=&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{A}u_{\epsilon}(x,y)(\psi_{\epsilon}(x)-\psi_{\epsilon}(y))d\nu\\
&=&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi_{K}(x)(\mathcal{A}u_{\epsilon}(x,y)-\mathcal{A}u_{0}(x,y))(T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x))-T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y)))d\nu\\
&&+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y))\mathcal{A}u_{\epsilon}(x,y)(\phi_{K}(x)-\phi_{K}(y))d\nu\\
&&+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y))\mathcal{A}u_{0}(x,y)(\phi_{K}(y)-\phi_{K}(x))d\nu\\
&&+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{A}u_{0}(x,y)(\phi_{K}(x)T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x))-\phi_{K}(y)T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y)))d\nu\\
&&:=J_{1}+J_{2}+J_{3}+J_{4}.\end{array}

We show

J_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi_{K}(x)(\mathcal{A}u_{\epsilon}(x,y)-\mathcal{A}u_{0}(x,y))(T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x))-T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y)))d\nu\geq 0.

To this end, it is enough to prove that the integrand is nonnegative. We observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}=\cup_{i=1}^{4}S_{i},

where

\begin{array}[]{c}S_{1}=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:|(u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x)|\leq\mu,|(u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y)|\leq\mu\right\},\\
S_{2}=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:|(u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x)|\leq\mu<|(u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y)|\right\},\\
S_{3}=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:|(u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y)|\leq\mu<|(u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x)|\right\}\end{array}

and

S_{4}=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:|(u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x)|>\mu,|(u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y)|>\mu\right\}.

Case
\displaystyle 1
.
If
\displaystyle x,y\in S_{1}
, then,

\displaystyle T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x))=(u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x)
and
\displaystyle T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y))=(u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y).

Therefore by
Lemma

2.10
, it easily follows that
\displaystyle J_{1}\geq 0
.

Case
\displaystyle 2
.
Let
\displaystyle x,y\in S_{2}
. Then,
\displaystyle|(u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x)|\leq\mu<|(u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y)|
and we consider four cases.

Firstly when
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)\geq u_{\epsilon}(x)
and
\displaystyle u_{0}(y)\geq u_{\epsilon}(y)
, we have
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)-u_{\epsilon}(x)\leq\mu<u_{0}(y)-u_{\epsilon}(y)
. Therefore,

T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x))-T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y))=(u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x)+\mu\geq 0.

Moreover, in this case,
\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}(x)-u_{\epsilon}(y)>u_{0}(x)-u_{0}(y)
. Hence by monotonicity of
\displaystyle t\to|t|^{p-2}t
it holds

\mathcal{A}u_{\epsilon}(x,y)-\mathcal{A}u_{0}(x,y)\geq 0

and hence we get
\displaystyle J_{1}\geq 0
.

Second case occurs when
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)<u_{\epsilon}(x)
and
\displaystyle u_{0}(y)\geq u_{\epsilon}(y)
, and in this case,
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)-u_{\epsilon}(x)<u_{\epsilon}(x)-u_{0}(x)\leq\mu<u_{0}(y)-u_{\epsilon}(y)
and

T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x))-T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y))=(u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x)+\mu>0,

and further,
\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}(x)-u_{\epsilon}(y)>u_{0}(x)-u_{0}(y)
implies
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}u_{\epsilon}(x,y)-\mathcal{A}u_{0}(x,y)\geq 0
giving
\displaystyle J_{1}\geq 0
.

For the third case
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)\geq u_{\epsilon}(x)
and
\displaystyle u_{0}(y)<u_{\epsilon}(y)
, one has
\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}(x)-u_{0}(x)<u_{0}(x)-u_{\epsilon}(x)\leq\mu<u_{\epsilon}(y)-u_{0}(y)
.
Then

T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x))-T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y))=(u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x)-\mu\leq 0,

and
\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}(x)-u_{\epsilon}(y)<u_{0}(x)-u_{0}(y)
implies
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}u_{\epsilon}(x,y)-\mathcal{A}u_{0}(x,y)\leq 0
will give
\displaystyle J_{1}\geq 0
.

Finally when
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)<u_{\epsilon}(x)
and
\displaystyle u_{0}(y)<u_{\epsilon}(y)
, we have
\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}(x)-u_{0}(x)\leq\mu<u_{\epsilon}(y)-u_{0}(y)
. Therefore

T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x))-T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y))=(u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x)-\mu\leq 0,

and
\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}(x)-u_{\epsilon}(y)\leq u_{0}(x)-u_{0}(y)
implies
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}u_{\epsilon}(x,y)-\mathcal{A}u_{0}(x,y)\leq 0
. Hence we get
\displaystyle J_{1}\geq 0
.

Due to symmetry, the case for
\displaystyle x,y\in S_{3}
will follow similarly like Case
\displaystyle 2
.

Case 3.
Let
\displaystyle x,y\in S_{4}
. Then a case-by-case inspection clearly guarantees that

T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x))-T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y))=0,

if
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)<u_{\epsilon}(x)
,
\displaystyle u_{0}(y)<u_{\epsilon}(y)
or if
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)\geq u_{\epsilon}(x)
,
\displaystyle u_{0}(y)\geq u_{\epsilon}(y)
.

Further
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)\geq u_{\epsilon}(x)
,
\displaystyle u_{0}(y)<u_{\epsilon}(y)
gives
\displaystyle T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x))-T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y))=-2\mu<0
; and
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)<u_{\epsilon}(x)
,
\displaystyle u_{0}(y)\geq u_{\epsilon}(y)
gives
\displaystyle T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(x))-T_{\mu}((u_{\epsilon}-u_{0})(y))=2\mu>0
. In each case, one can easily check the sign of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}u_{\epsilon}(x,y)-\mathcal{A}u_{0}(x,y)
and find that
\displaystyle J_{1}\geq 0
.

Combining all the three cases, we conclude

\displaystyle J_{1}\geq 0.

The rest of the proof follows from [
30
, Theorem A.1].
∎

\displaystyle w_{0}\neq v_{0}
are weak solutions to

Lemma 2.25
.

\displaystyle u_{0}\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)

satisfying

\begin{array}[]{c}-\Delta_{p}\zeta+(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}\zeta=B\text{ in }\Omega,\\

such that for every

in
\displaystyle\omega
.

(2.25)

2.25

\leq 0.\end{array}

Following the same arguments as in the proof of [

Then using

2.10
, one gets
\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}\geq\zeta
in
\displaystyle\Omega
. Hence there exists

\displaystyle\epsilon

Remark 4.7

\displaystyle C^{m}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})

\displaystyle m

Lemma 4.12

Step
\displaystyle 1

{}\left(C\phi_{1}+\frac{1}{k^{(\gamma+1)/2}}\right)^{2/(\gamma+1)}-\frac{1}{k}\leq w_{k,\lambda_{0}}\leq z_{k}\leq M,

(5.4)

where
\displaystyle M
is a positive constant independent of
\displaystyle k
. Further, by
Eq.

4.12
, for every
\displaystyle\omega\subset\subset\Omega
there exists
\displaystyle c(\omega)
(recall that
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}
is fixed) such that

\displaystyle z_{k}(x)\geq c(\omega)>0,\text{ for every }x\in\omega,\text{ for every }k\in\mathbb{N}.

One can now follow
Theorem

2.14
and show
\displaystyle z_{k}
is bounded in
\displaystyle W_{\operatorname{loc}}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, and the pointwise (a.e.) limit
\displaystyle u
(of
\displaystyle\{u_{k}\}
) and
\displaystyle v
(of
\displaystyle\{v_{k}\}
) are solutions to
Eq.

2.3
. As
\displaystyle\left\|u_{k}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\delta
and
\displaystyle\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{\infty}\geq\delta+\epsilon>\delta
we deduce that
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle v
are different.

Step
\displaystyle 3
:

Fixing
\displaystyle\alpha>\frac{\gamma+1}{4}
and
\displaystyle\theta=2\alpha-1>\frac{\gamma-1}{2}
, we take
\displaystyle\phi=\left(z_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta}-\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta}
as a test function in
Eq.

4.7
{}_{\lambda_{0}}
to obtain

{}\begin{array}[]{l}\int_{\Omega}\nabla z_{k}\cdot\nabla\left(z_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta}dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(z_{k}(x)-z_{k}(y))\left((z_{k}(x)+\frac{1}{k})^{\theta}-(z_{k}(y)+\frac{1}{k})^{\theta}\right)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy\\
=\lambda_{0}\int_{\Omega}\frac{(z_{k}+\frac{1}{k})^{\theta}-(\frac{1}{k})^{\theta}}{(z_{k}+\frac{1}{k})^{\gamma}}dx+\int_{\Omega}\left(\left(z_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta}-\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta}\right)z_{k}^{r}\,dx.\end{array}

(5.5)

Now using item
\displaystyle(i)
of
Lemma

2.9
, we get

{}\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{4\theta}{(\theta+1)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\left(\left((z_{k}(x)+\frac{1}{k})^{\frac{\theta+1}{2}}-(\frac{1}{k})^{\theta}\right)-\left((z_{k}(y)+\frac{1}{k})^{\frac{\theta+1}{2}})-(\frac{1}{k})^{\theta}\right)\right)^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy\\
\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(z_{k}(x)-z_{k}(y))\left((z_{k}(x)+\frac{1}{k})^{\theta}-(z_{k}(y)+\frac{1}{k})^{\theta}\right)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy.\end{array}

(5.6)

By
Eq.

5.6
, the nonlocal integral of
Eq.

5.5
is nonengative. Therefore we get (also note
Lemma

2.4
)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\frac{4\theta}{(\theta+1)^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\left(\left(z_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\alpha}-\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\alpha}\right)\right|^{2}&=&\theta\int_{\Omega}\left(z_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta-1}\left|\nabla z_{k}\right|^{2}dx\\
&\leq&\lambda_{0}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\left(z_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta}-\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta}}{\left(z_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\gamma}}dx+\int_{\Omega}\left(\left(z_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta}-\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta}\right)z_{k}^{r}\,dx\\
&\leq&\lambda_{0}\int_{\Omega}\left(z_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta-\gamma}dx+\int_{\Omega}\left(z_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta}z_{k}^{r}\,dx\\
&\leq&\lambda_{0}\int_{\Omega}\left(z_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta-\gamma}dx+\int_{\Omega}\left(z_{k}+1\right)^{\theta}z_{k}^{r}\,dx.\end{array}

By
Eq.

5.4
, this implies
\displaystyle\left\{\left(z_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\alpha}-\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\alpha}\right\}
is bounded in
\displaystyle W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
for
\displaystyle\theta\geq\gamma
. While for
\displaystyle\theta<\gamma
, we observe

\left(z_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta-\gamma}\leq\left(C_{0}\phi_{1}+\frac{1}{k^{(\gamma+1)/2}}\right)^{\frac{2(\theta-\gamma)}{1+\gamma}}\leq\left(C_{0}\phi_{1}\right)^{\frac{2(\theta-\gamma)}{1+\gamma}}.

Since
\displaystyle\theta>\frac{\gamma-1}{2}
, hence
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\phi_{1}^{\frac{2(\theta-\gamma)}{1+\gamma}}dx<+\infty
(see
[
39
]
). Therefore
\displaystyle\left\{\left(z_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\alpha}-\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\alpha}\right\}
is bounded in
\displaystyle W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. Consequently, a subsequence of
\displaystyle\left\{\left(z_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\alpha}-\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\alpha}\right\}
is weakly convergent in
\displaystyle W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. Finally by pointwise convergence of
\displaystyle\{z_{k}\}
, we have the weak limit is
\displaystyle u^{\alpha}
or
\displaystyle v^{\alpha}
and hence
\displaystyle u^{\alpha},v^{\alpha}\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega).

Remark 5.1
.

As mentioned before, for
\displaystyle\gamma<3
, the above theorem shows that the solutions are in
\displaystyle W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and, in particular, belong to
\displaystyle W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)
for every
\displaystyle q<2
. Indeed if
\displaystyle\gamma\geq 3
, one can get that the solutions of
Eq.

2.3
still belong to
\displaystyle W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)
for some
\displaystyle q<2
, we refer [
6
, Remark 1] for this.

A related problem

In spirit of [
6
, Theorem 3], we mention that the same result can be obtained for mixed operators. We briefly outline the result and the proof. Consider the problem

{}\begin{array}[]{c}-\Delta u+(-\Delta)^{s}u=\frac{f}{u^{\gamma}}\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u>0\text{ in }\Omega,\quad\quad\quad u=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\Omega;\end{array}

(5.7)

where
\displaystyle f\in L^{m}(\Omega),m>1
. By a solution of
Eq.

5.7
, we mean a function
\displaystyle u\in W^{1,2}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\Omega)
such that
\displaystyle u>c_{\omega}>0
(where
\displaystyle c_{\omega}
is a constant), in
\displaystyle\omega
for every
\displaystyle\omega\subset\subset\Omega
, and satisfies

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\phi\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(\phi(x)-\phi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy=\int_{\Omega}\frac{f\phi}{u^{\gamma}}dx\quad\forall\phi\in C_{c}^{1}(\Omega),

The following result slightly improves the case
\displaystyle\gamma>1
, see [
30
, Theorem 2.15] for
\displaystyle p=2
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open, bounded with
\displaystyle C^{1}
boundary and
\displaystyle\exists\,f_{0}>0
, a constant such that
\displaystyle f\geq f_{0}>0
a.e. in
\displaystyle\Omega
.
If
\displaystyle 1<\gamma<\frac{3m-1}{m+1}
, then
\displaystyle\exists\,u\in W_{\operatorname{loc}}^{1,2}(\Omega)
satisfying
Eq.

5.7
with
\displaystyle u^{\alpha}\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega),\forall\alpha\in\left(\frac{(m+1)(\gamma+1)}{4m},\frac{\gamma+1}{2}\right]
.

Remark 5.3
.

Clearly if
\displaystyle 1<\gamma<\frac{3m-1}{m+1}
, we have
\displaystyle\frac{(m+1)(\gamma+1)}{4m}<1<\frac{\gamma+1}{2}
and
\displaystyle\alpha=1
can be chosen in the previous theorem to obtain
\displaystyle u\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. Therefore the hypothesis on
\displaystyle f
allows us to obtain a solution
\displaystyle u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
for all
\displaystyle m>1
and
\displaystyle 1<\gamma<\frac{3m-1}{m+1}
, earlier which was restricted for
\displaystyle\gamma\leq 1
only.

Proof.

We outline the idea of the proof only. Let
\displaystyle u_{k}
be the unique positive weak solution to the approximating problem (see [
30
, Lemma 3.2])

{}\begin{array}[]{c}-\Delta u_{k}+(-\Delta)^{s}u_{k}=\frac{f_{k}}{(u_{k}+\frac{1}{k})^{\gamma}}\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u_{k}>0\text{ in }\Omega,\quad u_{k}=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\Omega;\end{array}

(5.8)

where
\displaystyle f_{k}(x)=\min\{f(x),k\}
. As in Step 2 of the proof of
Theorem

2.15
, one can get that the function
\displaystyle z_{k}(x)=\left(C_{0}\phi_{1}(x)+\frac{1}{k^{\frac{(\gamma+1)}{2}}}\right)^{\frac{2}{(\gamma+1)}}-\frac{1}{k},
is a subsolution to
Eq.

5.8
(as
\displaystyle f\geq f_{0}
a.e. in
\displaystyle\Omega
) for sufficiently small
\displaystyle C_{0}\equiv C_{0}(f_{0},\gamma)
.

Thus by
Remark

4.7
,

{}u_{k}\geq\left(C_{0}\phi_{1}+\frac{1}{k^{(\gamma+1)/2}}\right)^{2/(\gamma+1)}-\frac{1}{k}.

(5.9)

Fixing
\displaystyle\alpha>\frac{(m+1)(\gamma+1)}{4m}
and
\displaystyle\theta=2\alpha-1>\frac{(m+1)\gamma+1-m}{2m}
, one can take
\displaystyle\phi=\left(u_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta}-\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta}
as a test function in
Eq.

5.8
to obtain similarly like
Theorem

2.15
,

\theta\int_{\Omega}\left(u_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta-1}\left|\nabla u_{k}\right|^{2}dx\leq\int_{\Omega}f_{k}\frac{\left(u_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta}-\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta}}{\left(u_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\gamma}}dx,

and hence, by Hölder inequality,

\frac{4\theta}{(\theta+1)^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\left(\left(u_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\alpha}-\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\alpha}\right)\right|^{2}dx\leq\int_{\Omega}f_{k}\left(u_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\theta-\gamma}dx\leq\|f\|_{L^{m}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\left(u_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{m^{\prime}(\theta-\gamma)}\right)^{1/m^{\prime}}dx.

As
\displaystyle\alpha\leq\frac{\gamma+1}{2}
, so
\displaystyle\theta-\gamma\leq 0
. Further
\displaystyle\theta>\frac{(m+1)\gamma+1-m}{2m}
and
\displaystyle\Omega
satisfies interior sphere condition, so by
Eq.

5.9
,

\left(z_{k}(x)+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{m^{\prime}(\theta-\gamma)}\leq\left(C_{0}\phi_{1}+\frac{1}{k^{(\gamma+1)/2}}\right)^{\frac{2m^{\prime}(\theta-\gamma)}{1+\gamma}}\leq\left(C_{0}\phi_{1}\right)^{\frac{2m^{\prime}(\theta-\gamma)}{1+\gamma}}\in L^{1}(\Omega).

The rest of the proof follows similarly like
Theorem

2.15
.∎
