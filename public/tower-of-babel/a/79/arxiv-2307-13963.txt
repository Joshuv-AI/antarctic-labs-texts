On The Cost Function associated with Legendrian Knots Thanks: Subjclass[2020] : 57K10, 57K14, 57K33
Dheeraj Kulkarni, Tanushree Shah, Monika Yadav

Abstract
. In this article, we introduce a non-negative integer-valued function that measures the obstruction for converting topological isotopy between two Legendrian knots into a Legendrian isotopy. We refer to this function as the Cost function. We show that the Cost function induces a metric on the set of topologically isotopic Legendrian knots. Hence, the set of topologically isotopic Legendrian knots can be seen as a graph with path-metric given by the Cost function. Legendrian simple knot types are shown to be characterized using the Cost function. We also get a quantitative version of Fuchs-Tabachnikov’s Theorem that says any two Legendrian knots in ( 𝕊 3 , ξ s ​ t ​ d ) (\mathbb{S}^{3},\xi_{std}) in the same topological knot type become Legendrian isotopic after sufficiently many stabilizations [ 8 ] . We compute the Cost function for Legendrian simple knots (for example torus knots) and we note the behavior of Cost function for twist knots and cables of torus knots (some of which are Legendrian non-simple). We also construct examples of Legendrian representatives of 2-bridge knots and compute the Cost between them. Further, we investigate the behavior of the Cost function under the connect sum operation. We conclude with some questions about the Cost function, its relation with the standard contact structure, and the topological knot type.



1.
Introduction

A
Legendrian
knot in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the standard contact structure
\xi_{st}=\text{ker}(dz-ydx)
is a smooth
embedding of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that its image is tangent to the contact plane at each point. Two
Legendrian knots are called
Legendrian
isotopic if there is a smooth 1-parameter family of Legendrian knots starting with one and ending with the other.
The classification of Legendrian knots up to Legendrian isotopy is not completely understood. It is easy to see that given a topological embedding of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, denoted by
K
, it can be approximated by a Legendrian knot in an arbitrarily small
C^{0}
-neighborhood of
K
.
Hence, there are infinitely many Legendrian knots approximating
K
. All of them are topologically isotopic to
K
. We refer to a Legendrian knot that approximates
K
as a
Legendrian representative
of the knot type of
K
.

The properties of the contact structure
\xi_{st}
reflect deeply in the classification problem of Legendrian representatives of a given knot. Sometimes, the classical invariants given by the Thurston-Bennequin number (
tb
) and the rotation number (
rot
) completely classify Legendrian representatives up to Legendrian isotopy.
On the other hand, there are knot types where classical invariants are not sufficient to tell the Legendrian representatives apart (see
[
2
]
,
[
1
]
). Deep mathematical ideas based on counting J-holomorphic curves and packaging the information into differential graded algebras (DGAs) were developed by Eliashberg-Chekanov (
[
4
]
) and Ng (
[
10
]
) to classify Legendrian representatives. Additionally, Legendrian knots are classified by invariants such as
\rho
-rulings. For exposition on
\rho
-rulings refer to
[
5
]
.

In contrast, there have not been many attempts to understand the
space
of all Legendrian representatives of a given knot type. Whether the set of all Legendrian representatives of a given knot type admits a meaningful and natural structure is the question in focus. Mountain ranges formed by Legendrian representatives based on
tb
and
rot
have been useful in a limited manner (
[
5
]
). The limitation of mountain ranges arises from the fact that
tb
and
rot
are not sufficient for many families of knot types.

In this article, we introduce a function that assigns a non-negative integer to a pair of Legendrian representatives of a given knot type. Roughly speaking, this function measures the obstruction to converting a topological isotopy between two Legendrian representatives into a Legendrian isotopy. The output of this function, an integer, tells us the minimum cost we have to pay to upgrade a topological isotopy into a Legendrian isotopy. Hence we refer to it as the ‘Cost function’. We show that the Cost function gives a metric (see Section
6
) on the set of Legendrian representatives of a given knot type turning it into a metric space. Therefore, we get a graph of Legendrian representatives where path metric is given by the Cost function.

We investigate the properties of the Cost function. We give bounds on the Cost function in terms of
tb

and
rot
(Lemma
3.22
). We obtain a characterization of Legendrian simple knot types in terms of an explicit formula for the Cost function. We give a quantitative version of Fuchs-Tabachnikov’s Theorem (
[
8
]
). We study the effect of Legendrian connect-sum operation on the Cost function (
[
3
]
). We give examples of Legendrian twist knots and torus knots together with values of Cost function.
Further, we compute the Cost function of certain new families of 2-bridge knots which are distinguished using
\rho
-rulings (see
4.3
).
Finally, we raise a few questions for further understanding of the behavior of the Cost function from theoretical as well as computational viewpoints.

3.
Cost Function

Fuchs-Tabachnikov
[
8
]
proved that any two Legendrian knots, that are topologically isotopic, can be stabilized sufficiently many times to obtain
Legendrian isotopic knots. In the light of this theorem, we define the Cost function which measures the obstruction for the two Legendrian knots in the same topological type from becoming Legendrian isotopic.
Our situation reduces to having two front diagrams that are topologically isotopic. Assume that we have front diagrams
F_{0}
and
F_{1}
that represent topologically isotopic knots. Then there is a sequence of Reidemeister moves and
planar isotopies converting the knot diagram
F_{0}
to
F_{1}
.

Our goal is to find out how one can upgrade a topological isotopy to a Legendrian isotopy.
Hence the goal is twofold: convert Reidemeister moves into Legendrian Reidemeister moves and convert planar isotopies into planar isotopies that lift to Legendrian isotopies (note that not all planar isotopies lift to Legendrian isotopies, for example, a rotation may not lift to a Legendrian isotopy). Hence it is necessary to consider planar isotopies.
We recall that a planar isotopy can be converted to a Legendrian isotopy if and only if there are no vertical tangencies that appear during the planar isotopy. Planar isotopies can be divided into – rotations, perturbation of arcs without introducing new self intersection, scaling, and translations. Among these, scaling
and translations can be lifted to a Legendrian isotopy. However, the rotations (local or global) may introduce vertical tangencies. Further, a
global
rotation of knot diagram can be
seen as a composition of finitely many locally supported rotations (see Remark
3.4
). Thus, it is
sufficient
to consider local rotations for our purpose.
Therefore we have the following definition.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that front projections
F_{0}
and
F_{1}
differ by an R-move (local rotation) in a disc
Q
and they are identical outside
Q
if there is a
Reidemeister move (local rotation) supported within the disc
Q
such that after application of it on
F_{0}
as a topological knot diagram,
F_{0}
changes to topological knot diagram
F_{1}
.

Please note that in the above definition, a knot diagram refers to a regular projection and a front projection refers to a generic front projection where there is no vertical tangency and only one type of crossing allowed. Also note that during this process we add cusps which are then counted in the cost function.

In the following statement, we show that a topological isotopy between two Legendrian knots can be converted to a Legendrian isotopy by the process of stabilizations. It can be articulated in the language of R-moves and LR-moves.

First, we will show that any R-move between two front projections can be converted into an LR-move by stabilizing the fronts.

.

Lemma 3.20
.

Let

be Legendrian knots of the same topological knot type. Then

\text{Cost}(K,\tilde{K})\geq\max\{\lvert tb(K)-tb(\tilde{K})\rvert,\lvert rot(K)-rot(\tilde{K})\rvert\}.

Let

is a non-destabilizable Legendrian knot then it admits a unique prime decomposition up to possible permutations.

Proof.

Let
K=K_{1}\#K_{2}\cdots\#K_{n}
be a prime decomposition unique up to stabilization and possible permutations. If none of the
K_{i}
s destabilizes, then the prime decomposition is unique up to permutation using Remark
2.4
. If possible, let
K_{i}
be obtained by stabilizing
\bar{K_{i}}
, that is
K_{i}=(S^{+})^{p}(S^{-})^{m}(\bar{K}_{i})
for some positive integer
p
and
m
. Then

\displaystyle K=

\displaystyle K_{1}\#\cdots(S^{+})^{p}(S^{-})^{m}(\bar{K}_{i})\cdots\#K_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(S^{+})^{p}(S^{-})^{m}(K_{1}\#\cdots\bar{K}_{i}\cdots\#K_{n}).

Thus we arrive at a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let

be a prime decomposition. If every Legendrian knot in
\mathcal{L}(K)
destabilizes to a unique Legendrian knot
\bar{K}
then for each

, there exists a unique Legendrian representative
\bar{K_{i}}

\mathcal{L}(K_{i})
destabilizes to
\bar{K_{i}}
.

Proof.

Let
\bar{K}_{i}
be a maximal
tb
Legendrian knot in
\mathcal{L}(K_{i})\ \forall\ 1\leq i\leq n.
If possible, let
\hat{K_{i}}\in\mathcal{L}(K_{i})
be another maximal
tb
Legendrian knot. Then using Remark
2.3
,
\bar{K_{1}}\#\cdots\bar{K_{i}}\#\cdots\bar{K_{n}}
and
\bar{K_{1}}\#\cdots\hat{K_{i}}\#\cdots\bar{K_{n}}
are Legendrian knots in
\mathcal{L}(K)
, with maximal

. Since
\bar{K}

\bar{K_{1}}\#\cdots\bar{K_{i}}\#\cdots\bar{K_{n}}
and

tb
representative of
K_{i}

\forall\ 1\leq i\leq n

If possible, let

∎

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
K_{1},L_{1},K_{2}
and
L_{2}
be Legendrian knots of same topological knot type, then

\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})\leq\min\{\text{Cost}(K_{1},L_{1})+\text{Cost}(K_{2},L_{2}),\text{Cost}(K_{1},L_{2})+\text{Cost}(K_{2},L_{1})\}.

Proof.

Let
\text{Cost}(K_{i},L_{j})=p_{ij}+n_{ij}+q_{ij}+m_{ij}
with

(S^{+})^{p_{ij}}(S^{-})^{n_{ij}}(K_{i})=(S^{+})^{q_{ij}}(S^{-})^{m_{ij}}(L_{j}),\>i,j=1,2.

Then,

\displaystyle(S^{+})^{p_{11}+p_{22}}(S^{-})^{n_{11}+n_{22}}(K_{1}\#K_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{p_{11}}(S^{-})^{n_{11}}(K_{1})\#(S^{+})^{p_{22}}(S^{-})^{n_{22}}(K_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{q_{11}+q_{22}}(S^{-})^{m_{11}+m_{22}}(L_{1}\#L_{2}).

Thus,

\displaystyle\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{2}p_{ii}+n_{ii}+q_{ii}+m_{ii}

(5.10)

\displaystyle=\text{Cost}(K_{1},L_{1})+\text{Cost}(K_{2},L_{2}).

Now,

\displaystyle(S^{+})^{p_{12}+p_{21}}(S^{-})^{n_{12}+n_{21}}(K_{1}\#K_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{p_{12}}(S^{-})^{n_{12}}(K_{1})\#(S^{+})^{p_{21}}(S^{-})^{n_{21}}(K_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{q_{12}}(S^{-})^{m_{12}}(L_{2})\#(S^{+})^{q_{21}}(S^{-})^{m_{21}}(L_{1})

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{q_{12}+q_{21}}(S^{-})^{m_{21}+m_{21}}(L_{2}\#L_{1}).

Thus,

(5.11)

\displaystyle\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\text{Cost}(K_{1},L_{2})+\text{Cost}(K_{2},L_{1}).

Using equations
5.10
and
5.11
, we have

\displaystyle\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})\leq\min\{\text{Cost}(K_{1},L_{1})+\text{Cost}(K_{2},L_{2}),\text{Cost}(K_{1},L_{2})+\text{Cost}(K_{2},L_{1})\}.

∎

Lemma 5.13
.

Let
K_{1},L_{1}
and
K_{2},L_{2}
be Legendrian knots of two different topological knot types such that
K_{1},K_{2}
destabilizes to
L_{1},L_{2}
, respectively. Then

.

As

, respectively, there exist non-negative integers
p,n,q
and
m
such that the following holds

•

and

•

\text{Cost}(K_{1},L_{1})=p+n
and
\text{Cost}(K_{2},L_{2})=q+m
by Remark
3.21

Then the lemma follows from Remark

and the observation

Lemma 5.14
.

Let
K_{1},L_{1}
and
K_{2},L_{2}
be Legendrian knots of two different topological knot types such that
K_{1}=(S^{+})^{p}(S^{-})^{n}(L_{1})
and
L_{2}=(S^{+})^{q}(S^{-})^{m}(K_{2})
. Then

\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})=|p-q|+|n-m|.

Proof.

We will prove this Lemma in cases.

Case I:

p\geq q,n\geq m
.

We prove
K_{1}\#K_{2}
destabilizes to
L_{1}\#L_{2}
as follows:

\displaystyle K_{1}\#K_{2}

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{p}(S^{-})^{n}(L_{1}\#K_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{p-q}(S^{-})^{n-m}(L_{1}\#(S^{+})^{q}(S^{-})^{m}(K_{2}))

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{p-q}(S^{-})^{n-m}(L_{1}\#L_{2}).

Using Remark
3.21
, we have

\displaystyle\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})

\displaystyle=p-q+n-m

\displaystyle=|\text{Cost}(K_{1},L_{1})-\text{Cost}(K_{2},L_{2})|.

Case II:

p\leq q,n\leq m
.

Using the similar technique as used in Case I, we prove that
K_{1}\#K_{2}
destabilizes to
L_{1}\#L_{2}
.

\displaystyle L_{1}\#L_{2}

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{q}(S^{-})^{m}(L_{1}\#K_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{q-p}(S^{-})^{m-n}((S^{+})^{p}(S^{-})^{n}(L_{1})\#K_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{q-p}(S^{-})^{m-n}(K_{1}\#K_{2}).

Therefore, by Remark
3.21

\displaystyle\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})

\displaystyle=q-p+m-n

\displaystyle=|\text{Cost}(K_{1},L_{1})-\text{Cost}(K_{2},L_{2})|.

Case III:

p\geq q,n\leq m
.

Since
rot(K_{1})=rot(L_{1})-n+p
, and
,rot(L_{2})=rot(K_{2})-m+q
, using Lemma
3.20
, we get

\displaystyle\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})

\displaystyle\geq|rot(K_{1}\#K_{2})-rot(L_{1}\#L_{2})|

\displaystyle=p-q+m-n.

The reverse inequality follows from the computations done below.

\displaystyle(S^{-})^{m-n}(K_{1}\#K_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{-})^{m-n}((S^{+})^{p}(S^{-})^{n}(L_{1})\#K_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{p}(S^{-})^{m}(L_{1}\#K_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{p-q}(L_{1}\#(S^{+})^{q}(S^{-})^{m}(K_{2}))

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{p-q}(L_{1}\#L_{2}).

Case IV:

p\leq q,m\leq n
.

Using Lemma
3.20
, we have

\displaystyle\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})

\displaystyle\geq|rot(K_{1}\#K_{2})-rot(L_{1}\#L_{2})|

\displaystyle=|p-q+m-n|=q-p+n-m.

The reverse inequality follows from the following computations:

\displaystyle(S^{+})^{q-p}(K_{1}\#K_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{q-p}((S^{+})^{p}(S^{-})^{n}(L_{1})\#K_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{q}(S^{-})^{n}(L_{1}\#K_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{-})^{n-m}(L_{1}\#(S^{+})^{q}(S^{-})^{m}(K_{2}))

\displaystyle=(S^{-})^{n-m}(L_{1}\#L_{2}).

Hence, we conclude from the above cases that

\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})=|p-q|+|n-m|.

∎

Lemma 5.15
.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{1}
and
\mathcal{K}_{2}
be different topological knot types which are Legendrian simple. Let
K_{1},L_{1}\in\mathcal{K}_{1}
and
K_{2},L_{2}\in\mathcal{K}_{2}
be Legendrian knots with maximal
tb
, then

(i) If
rot(K_{i})\leq rot(L_{i}),i=1,2
, then

\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})=\text{Cost}(K_{1},L_{1})+\text{Cost}(K_{2},L_{2}).

(ii) If
rot(K_{1})\leq rot(L_{1})
and
rot(K_{2})\geq rot(L_{2})
, then

\displaystyle|\text{Cost}(K_{1},L_{1})-\text{Cost}(K_{2},L_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\max\{Cost(K_{1},L_{1}),Cost(K_{2},L_{2})\}.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.22
, we have
(S^{+})^{r_{i}}(K_{i})=(S^{-})^{r_{i}}(L_{i})
, where

2r_{i}=|rot(K_{i})-rot(L_{i})|=\text{Cost}(K_{i},L_{i}),\>i=1,2.

(i)
rot(K_{i})\leq rot(L_{i}),i=1,2
.

Using Lemma
5.12
, we have

\displaystyle\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\text{Cost}(K_{1},L_{1})+\text{Cost}(K_{2},L_{2})

\displaystyle=2(r_{1}+r_{2}).

The reverse inequality can be derived using Lemma
3.20
in the following way.

\displaystyle\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})

\displaystyle\geq|rot(K_{1}\#K_{2})-rot(L_{1}\#L_{2})|

\displaystyle=(rot(L_{1})-rot(K_{1}))+(rot(L_{2})-rot(K_{2}))

\displaystyle=2(r_{1}+r_{2}).

Hence,
\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})=\text{Cost}(K_{1},L_{1})+\text{Cost}(K_{2},L_{2})
.

(ii)
rot(K_{1})\leq rot(L_{1})
and
rot(K_{2})\geq rot(L_{2})
.

Observe that,

\displaystyle rot(K_{1}\#K_{2})

\displaystyle=rot(K_{1})+rot(K_{2})

\displaystyle=rot(K_{1})+rot(L_{2})+2r_{2},\>\text{ and }

\displaystyle rot(L_{1}\#L_{2})

\displaystyle=rot(L_{1})+rot(L_{2})

\displaystyle=rot(K_{1})+rot(L_{2})+2r_{1}.

Then,
|rot(K_{1}\#K_{2})-rot(L_{1}\#L_{2})|=2r
, where
r=|r_{1}-r_{2}|
. Using Lemma
3.20
, we have

\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})\geq 2r=|\text{Cost}(K_{1},L_{1})-\text{Cost}(K_{2},L_{2})|.

Now, we consider two cases to prove

\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})\leq\max\{\text{Cost}(K_{1},L_{1}),\text{Cost}(K_{2},L_{2})\}.

First consider
r_{2}\leq r_{1}
. Then

\displaystyle(S^{+})^{r_{1}}(K_{1}\#K_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{-})^{r_{1}}(L_{1}\#K_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{-})^{r}(L_{1})\#(S^{-})^{r_{2}}(K_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{-})^{r}(L_{1})\#(S^{+})^{r_{2}}(L_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{-})^{r}(S^{+})^{r_{2}}(L_{1}\#L_{2}).

Thus,
\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})\leq r+r_{1}+r_{2}=2r_{1}.

Now consider the case with
r_{2}\geq r_{1}
. Then

\displaystyle(S^{-})^{r_{2}}(K_{1}\#K_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{r_{2}}(K_{1}\#L_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{+})^{r_{1}}(K_{1})\#(S^{+})^{r}(L_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{-})^{r_{1}}(L_{1})\#(S^{+})^{r}(L_{2})

\displaystyle=(S^{-})^{r_{1}}(S^{+})^{r}(L_{1}\#L_{2}).

Thus,
\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})\leq r+r_{1}+r_{2}=2r_{2}.
Hence,

\text{Cost}(K_{1}\#K_{2},L_{1}\#L_{2})\leq\max\{\text{Cost}(K_{1},L_{1}),\text{Cost}(K_{2},L_{2})\}.

∎

6.
Conclusion

For a topological knot type
\mathcal{K}
, let
\mathcal{L(K)}/\sim
denote the set of equivalence classes of Legendrian representatives of
\mathcal{K}
up to Legendrian isotopy. The Cost function gives a metric on the set
\mathcal{L(K)}/\sim
in the following way. The map

d_{C}:(\mathcal{L(K)}/\sim)\times(\mathcal{L(K)}/\sim)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{+}\cup\{0\}

defined as
d_{C}([K],[\tilde{K}])=\text{Cost}(K,\tilde{K})
for all
[K],[\tilde{K}]\in\mathcal{L(K)}/\sim
is a metric. The map
d_{C}
satisfies the first two properties of a metric (see Theorem
3.14
and Remark
3.10
). For the triangle
inequality, consider
[K_{1}],[K_{2}],[K_{3}]\in\mathcal{L(K)}/\sim
. Let
F_{i}
be a front projection of
K_{i},\>i=1,2,3
. Let
SF_{1}\in\mathcal{SF}(F_{1},F_{2})
and
SF_{2}\in\mathcal{SF}(F_{2},F_{3})
be such that
\text{Cost}(K_{1},K_{2})=\text{Cost}(F_{1},F_{2},SF_{1})
and
\text{Cost}(K_{2},K_{3})=\text{Cost}(F_{2},F_{3},SF_{2})
. Therefore the sequence
(SF_{1},SF_{2})\in\mathcal{SF}(F_{1},F_{3})
. Thus

\displaystyle\text{Cost}(K_{1},K_{3})

\displaystyle\leq\text{Cost}(F_{1},F_{3},(SF_{1},SF_{2}))

\displaystyle=\text{Cost}(F_{1},F_{2},SF_{1})+\text{Cost}(F_{2},F_{3},SF_{2})

\displaystyle=\text{Cost}(K_{1},K_{2})+\text{Cost}(K_{2},K_{3}).

Hence,
d_{C}
is a metric.

Using the Cost function we define a graph invariant of the topological knot. We associate a graph
G_{\mathcal{K}}
to a topological knot type
\mathcal{K}
in the following way. Fix the set of vertices for
G_{\mathcal{K}}
to be
\mathcal{L(K)}/\sim
. Two vertices
[K],[\tilde{K}]\in\mathcal{L(K)}/\sim
are joined by an edge if and only if
\text{Cost}(K,\tilde{K})=1
. We refer to this graph as the graph of
Legendrian representatives
of
{\mathcal{K}}
. The Cost function graph for unknot is shown in Figure
22
.
