On fits to correlated and auto-correlated data
Rainer Sommer

Abstract
Observables in particle physics and specifically in lattice QCD calculations are often extracted from fits. Standard χ 2 \chi^{2} tests require a reliable determination of the covariance matrix and its inverse from correlated and auto-correlated data, a challenging task often leading to close-to-singular estimates. These motivate modifications of the definition of χ 2 \chi^{2} such as uncorrelated fits. We show how the goodness-of-fit measured by their p-value can still be estimated robustly for a broad class of such fits.



1
Introduction

In particle physics, physical information is often
extracted from fits to correlated data. When their covariance matrix is known well,
the minimization of the so-called correlated

\chi^{2}

is the ideal method to obtain the best estimate of
the fit parameters from the statistical point of view.
In many cases model functions are
guided by theoretical principles, but in practice
one has to examine their validity case by case,
and often specific model functions or data points are discarded
based on a measure of their goodness-of-fit.
For
N_{\mathrm{x}}
data points normally distributed,
and for correlated fits to
model functions depending on
N_{\mathrm{A}}
parameters,
the expectation
value of
\chi^{2}
(at the minimum)
equals the number of degrees
of freedom,
N_{\mathrm{x}}-N_{\mathrm{A}}
.
When there are many more fitted observables than fit parameters,
a reduced
\chi^{2}
close to 1 (defined by the ratio
of the observed
\chi^{2}
over
(N_{\mathrm{x}}-N_{\mathrm{A}})
),
provides a first good indication that a given model
describes well the data.
In addition, the goodness-of-fit is
judged by computing the probability
Q
, often called p-value, of observing a
\chi^{2}

larger or equal than the observed
\chi^{2}
, which we call
\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{obs}}
.
The p-value is easily obtained in closed analytic form
due to the exact cancellation
between the covariance matrix,
C
, of the underlying data and
C^{-1}

used in the definition of the correlated
\chi^{2}
. It depends only on
\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{obs}}
and on

N_{\mathrm{x}}-N_{\mathrm{A}}
.

However, data sets with limited statistics often lead to close-to-singular

C^{-1}
.
Moreover, for data generated from Monte-Carlo (MC)
methods, specifically from Markov chains, additional complications
arise from the presence of auto-correlations, namely correlations along
the Monte-Carlo history (or “time”).
Hence for all cases where reliable estimations of the
inverse of the covariance matrix are not possible,
and correlated fits can not be performed,
the standard
\chi^{2}
-test is not applicable.
In this work we point out a robust solution
to the problem.

Our object of study is primarily lattice QCD, where Monte-Carlo
methods based on Markov chains are an essential ingredient
to obtain non-perturbative predictions.
Field configurations are generated by successive repetitions
of a predefined update scheme, and as a consequence,
expectation values of correlation functions, obtained from
averages over measurements on these field
configurations, are both correlated and auto-correlated.
The
\Gamma
-method is the preferable choice to estimate
the statistical errors of primary and derived observables
[
1
]

obtained from a Markov process,
but it is difficult to turn it into a practical method for
general matrix elements of the covariance matrix.

N_{\mathrm{x}}

\tau_{\mathrm{exp}}

Then the window size of Ref.

\tau_{\mathrm{exp}}
and fixed independently of
i,j
for
the estimate of

given in the reference. Also jackknife

Because of these issues and the general difficulty in estimating
covariances for large number of data points

[

]
,
ad hoc modifications of

used, like so-called SVD cuts
(see e.g.

) or uncorrelated fits.
We will show how the
expected value of
\chi^{2}
and the goodness-of-fit can
be estimated robustly for all these cases. In fact, we emphasize
that this can also be done when the weights of the data points
are independent of the covariance matrix, which may be a good
strategy when fits are used to perform extrapolations away from the region where data
exists.

Our text is organized as follows:
in Section 2 we present the derivation
of the formulae for the expectation value of
\chi^{2}

and the correponding p-value;
in Section 3 we describe how to handle auto-correlations, while
in Section 4 we present a numerical
test in a toy model, before concluding.

2
Method

We want to test whether data
Y_{j}
at kinematic coordinates
x_{j}

are described by model functions
\Phi(x,a)
,
depending on the parameters
a^{\alpha}
,

Y_{j}=\Phi(x_{j},A)=\phi_{j}(A)\,,

(2.1)

with
A^{\alpha}
the exact values of
the model parameters: the so-called null-hypothesis.
In our convention Greek indices run from
1
to
N_{\mathrm{A}}
,
Roman ones run from
1
to
N_{\mathrm{x}}
, repeated indices are summed over, and we use the natural scalar product
(r,s)=r_{i}s_{i}
and norm
||r||=(r,r)^{1/2}
.
We also often use the matrix-vector
notation to suppress Roman indices.
Given data
\bar{y}_{j}
with normal distribution,

P_{C}(\bar{y})=(2\pi)^{-N_{\mathrm{x}}/2}(\det C)^{-1/2}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}(\delta\bar{y},C^{-1}\delta\bar{y})\Big)\,,\quad\,\quad\delta\bar{y}=\bar{y}-Y\,,

(2.2)

with covariance matrix
C
,
we want to find robust estimates for the fit parameters
and have a measure for the likelihood that our model
is compatible with the data. Expectation values
are defined in the usual manner. In particular we have

\displaystyle\langle\delta\bar{y}_{i}\delta\bar{y}_{j}\rangle\equiv\int\mathrm{d}\bar{y}\ P_{C}(\bar{y})\ \delta\bar{y}_{i}\delta\bar{y}_{j}=C_{ij}\,.

(2.3)

One possibility when such a normal distribution is approximately realized is when

\bar{y}_{j}
are averages over Monte Carlo data of length
N

and
N
is sufficiently large. In this case,
all elements of
C
are of order
1/N
, and
\langle\bar{y}_{i}\rangle\to Y_{i}
in the
limit
N\to\infty
.

The natural measure for the distance of the model to the data is

\chi^{2}(a)=||W(\bar{y}-\phi(a))||^{2}\,,

(2.4)

in terms of a symmetric and positive
N_{\mathrm{x}}\times N_{\mathrm{x}}
weight matrix
W_{ij}
.
A reasonable requirement is that
\chi^{2}
remains finite
as
N\to\infty
, which means that
W
should be of
\mathrm{O}(N^{1/2})
:
common choices for
W^{2}
are the inverse covariance matrix
C^{-1}

or the inverse of its diagonal projection

W_{ij}=\delta_{ij}/\sqrt{C_{ii}}
(no summation over
i
).
The latter definition of
\chi^{2}
leads to so-called uncorrelated fits. However, one can also consider weight matrices
W
which
scale weaker with
N
, e.g.
\mathrm{O}(N^{0})
. We will remark on that later.

As usual, from the minimization of the
\chi^{2}
function
we obtain
the best estimate of the parameters

\chi^{2}(\bar{a})=\underset{a}{\mathrm{Min}}\,\chi^{2}(a)\,,\quad\bar{a}=\bar{a}(\bar{y})\,.

(2.5)

Here we assume that the position
\bar{a}(\bar{y})
of the minimum of
\chi^{2}
is unique.
Our goal is to derive an expression for the expectation value

\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}\bar{y}\ P_{C}(\bar{y})\ \chi^{2}(\bar{a}(\bar{y}))\,,

(2.6)

predicted by the normal distribution of the data and under the assumption that Eq. (
2.1
) holds.

The condition for
\bar{a}
,

(W\phi^{\alpha}(\bar{a}),W(\bar{y}-\phi(\bar{a}))=0\,,\quad\text{with }\phi^{\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{m}}(a)\equiv\frac{\partial^{m}\phi(a)}{\partial a^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial a^{\alpha_{m}}}\,,

(2.7)

can be rewritten as

\mathcal{P}\,W\,(\bar{y}-\phi(\bar{a}))=0\,,

(2.8)

with
\mathcal{P}
the projector onto the space spanned by
\left\{W\,\phi^{\alpha}\right\}
, namely

\mathcal{P}W\phi^{\alpha}=W\phi^{\alpha}\,,\;\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}^{2}\,,\;\mathrm{tr}\mathcal{P}=N_{\mathrm{A}}\,,

(2.9)

and we have

\chi^{2}(\bar{a})=||(1-\mathcal{P})W\,(\bar{y}-\phi(\bar{a}))||^{2}\,.

(2.10)

2.1
Expectation value of
\chi^{2}

We expand
\phi
around
a=\bar{a}
,

\phi(A)=\phi(\bar{a})-\phi^{\alpha}(\bar{a})\delta\bar{a}^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\phi^{\alpha\beta}(\bar{a})\delta\bar{a}^{\alpha}\delta\bar{a}^{\beta}+\mathrm{O}(\delta\bar{a}^{3})\,,\quad\delta\bar{a}^{\alpha}\equiv\bar{a}^{\alpha}-A^{\alpha}\,,

(2.11)

and obtain, using
(1-\mathcal{P})W\phi^{\alpha}(\bar{a})=0
,

\chi^{2}(\bar{a})=||(1-\mathcal{P})W\,\delta\bar{y}||^{2}+(W\delta\bar{y},(1-\mathcal{P})W\phi^{\alpha\beta})\delta\bar{a}^{\alpha}\delta\bar{a}^{\beta}+\mathrm{O}(N^{-1})\,.

(2.12)

Taking
\delta\bar{a}=\mathrm{O}(N^{-1/2})=\delta\bar{y}
, the expected value of
\chi^{2}

is then

\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle=\mathrm{tr}[C_{\mathrm{w}}(1-\mathcal{P})]+\mathrm{O}(N^{-1})\,,\quad C_{\mathrm{w}}=WCW\,.

(2.13)

We stress the fact that in the result above even an approximate knowledge
(up to statistical errors) of the covariance matrix is sufficient.
The corrections to eq. (
2.13
) are seen to be
\mathrm{O}(N^{-1})
in the following way.
Expanding
\delta\bar{a}(\delta\bar{y})
in terms of
\delta\bar{y}
(and therefore in terms of
N^{-1/2}
),
we have
\langle(W\delta\bar{y},(1-\mathcal{P})W\phi^{\alpha\beta})\delta\bar{a}^{\alpha}\delta\bar{a}^{\beta}\rangle=g_{ijk}\langle\delta\bar{y}_{i}\delta\bar{y}_{j}\delta\bar{y}_{k}\rangle+\mathrm{O}(N^{-1})

where
g=\mathrm{O}(N)
due to the two factors of
W
.
In the assumed symmetric (normal) distribution
\langle\delta\bar{y}_{i}\delta\bar{y}_{j}\delta\bar{y}_{k}\rangle
vanishes and skewed corrections to it are down by a factor
N^{-1/2}

leading overall to
\mathrm{O}(N^{-1})
.
However, in practice the estimate of
C_{\mathrm{w}}
from the data
themselves introduces order
N^{-1/2}
uncertainties, as well as the
projector
\mathcal{P}
which is a function of
\bar{a}^{\alpha}
.
The essential point is that
\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle
can
easily be computed also with auto-correlated data, as we will show in the next section.

For the special choice of an “uncorrelated fit”, i.e.

W_{ij}=\delta_{ij}/\sqrt{C_{ii}}
, we have
[C_{\mathrm{w}}]_{ii}=1
and

\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle=N_{\mathrm{x}}-\mathrm{tr}[C_{\mathrm{w}}\mathcal{P}]\,.

(2.14)

Similarly, for a “correlated fit” with
W^{2}=C^{-1}
,

[C_{\mathrm{w}}]_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
,
we reproduce the standard result
\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle=N_{\mathrm{x}}-N_{\mathrm{A}}
.

Above we used
W=\mathrm{O}(N^{1/2})
. However, one could also consider a more general
functional dependence of
W
on
C
, for instance by taking

W
independent of
C
2
2

2

Consider for example a linear extrapolation of

\Phi(x,a)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+\mathrm{O}(x^{2})
, to
x=0
, i.e. we want to determine
a_{0}
. When the data set is not
rich enough to include higher order terms in
\phi
, it can be
a good strategy to choose a weight
W_{ii}=1/x_{i}^{n}
, e.g. with
n=2
.
In this case
W
is
\mathrm{O}(N^{0})
.
.
This changes nothing in the above,
since
\delta\bar{a}
remains
\mathrm{O}(N^{-1/2})
.
In Appendix
C
we discuss several other
generalizations of the fit functions assumed above.

At this point,
it is natural to define the reduced-
\chi^{2}
for fits with arbitrary
W
as

\chi^{2}/\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle
with
eq. (
2.13
) for
\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle
.
Although a reduced-
\chi^{2}
of order 1 is
already a first indication of whether a given model fits well the data,
the real discriminator for the goodness of a fit is the p-value.

2.2
Quality-of-fit

The probability for having a value
of
\chi^{2}(\bar{a})
which is larger or equal than
a given value
\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{obs}}
is

Q=\int\mathrm{d}\bar{y}\,P_{C}(\bar{y})\,\theta\big(\chi^{2}(\bar{a}(\bar{y}))-\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{obs}}\big)\,.

(2.15)

It is useful to decide whether the model can be statistically rejected
(the null hypothesis).
In practice
\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{obs}}
stands for the value of

\chi^{2}(\bar{a})
that one has in the particular fit
to the existing data and
Q
gives the probability of finding a fit worse than the one that one found, i.e. with a larger value of
\chi^{2}(\bar{a})
.
If
Q
is too low,
then one has
significant reasons to doubt that the model
describes the data, and usually dismisses the fit
and modifies the model or the fit ranges. The probability
Q
is often called quality-of-fit or p-value.

Using the variables
z=C^{-1/2}\delta\bar{y}

it is given by

Q(\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{obs}},\nu)=\int\mathrm{d}z\,(2\pi)^{-N_{\mathrm{x}}/2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}||z||^{2}}\;\theta\big((z,\nu z)-\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{obs}}\big)\,,

(2.16)

in terms of the positive, symmetric matrix

\nu=C^{1/2}W(1-\mathcal{P})WC^{1/2}\,.

(2.17)

The latter takes over the role of the number of degrees of freedom
in correlated fits, where
CW=1
and
\mathrm{tr}[\nu]=N_{\mathrm{x}}-N_{\mathrm{A}}
.
Denoting the strictly positive eigenvalues of
\nu
by

\lambda_{i}(\nu)\,,i=1\ldots N_{\nu}\leq N_{\mathrm{x}}-N_{\mathrm{A}}
,
we have

Q(\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{obs}},\nu)=\int\theta\big(\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\nu}}\lambda_{j}(\nu)z_{j}^{2}-\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{obs}}\big)\;\prod_{i=1}^{N_{\nu}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z_{i}}{(2\pi)^{1/2}}\;e^{-\frac{1}{2}z_{i}^{2}}\,\,.

(2.18)

In this form,
Q

is easily evaluated by a MC over
z_{i}
drawn from a Gaussian distribution. A sample of order thousand random numbers is sufficient for a precision
of
Q
of order 0.01, and our proposal is to
use this as estimate for the quality-of-fit.

The difficult part is not the statistical error in
the MC estimate of eq. (
2.18
) but the fact that

\nu
has to be estimated from the data
\bar{y}
and thus the eigenvalues

\lambda_{i}(\nu)
are uncertain themselves. This leads to the expectation that the statistical error
\Delta Q
of
Q
is order
N^{-1/2}
.
In contrast to the case of a correlated
\chi^{2}

fit no enhancement by small eigenvalues (of the estimated
\nu
) will occur in
\Delta Q
.
Instead the contribution of modes with small eigenvalues are suppressed. In sect.
4
we will demonstrate
in a toy model that
\Delta Q
is small and can be neglected in practice.

In general one should decide whether a fit
is to be discarded based on the quality-of-fit. For example,
if
Q(\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{obs}},\nu)=0.05
there is only a 5% chance that
one finds such a
\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{obs}}
or worse but the fit-function describes the data on average. One will then
dismiss fits with
Q(\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{obs}},\nu)\leq 0.05

or with a different threshold.
When one performs a correlated fit and has a large number of data, more precisely when

N_{\mathrm{x}}-N_{\mathrm{A}}=\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle\gg 1
, then

Q(\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{obs}},\nu)

is close to a step function
1-\theta(\chi_{\mathrm{obs}}^{2}/\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle)
. But this is not the case in general.
To understand this behavior, we examine the region where

Q
makes the transition from
Q\approx 1
to
Q\approx 0
. Its width can be
estimated by the variance
of
\chi^{2}(\bar{a})
. Using eqs. (
2.12
,
2.13
) we obtain

\langle[\chi^{2}(\bar{a})-\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle]^{2}\rangle=\langle(\chi^{2}(\bar{a}))^{2}\rangle-\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle^{2}=2\mathrm{tr}(\nu^{2})+\mathrm{O}(N^{-1/2})\,,

(2.19)

valid for arbitrary weights
W
.
For a correlated fit with
WCW=1
, the width is suppressed by
1/(N_{\mathrm{x}}-N_{\mathrm{A}})

relative to
\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle^{2}
.
For a large number of data points,
a step-function behavior is approached. However, in general this is
not the case. Assume for example that the eigenvalues of
\nu
are
geometrically distributed, namely
\lambda_{j}=\lambda_{0}r^{j}
with
r<1
. Then we have

\frac{\langle[\chi^{2}(\bar{a})-\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle]^{2}\rangle}{2\,\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle^{2}}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\nu}}(\lambda_{j}(\nu))^{2}}{(\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\nu}}\lambda_{j}(\nu))^{2}}=\frac{1-r}{1+r}+\mathrm{O}(r^{N_{\nu}+1})\,.

(2.20)

In general the width is therefore not suppressed at all and it is necessary to examine the p-value.
For an illustration the reader may look ahead at

Q
-functions in our toy model in Fig.
3
.

3
Auto-correlated Monte Carlo data

In the above formulae the covariance matrix is assumed to be known,
but in practice we have to replace it
by an estimate from the Monte Carlo data, often in presence
of non-negligible auto-correlations, which we discuss next.

We consider explicitly the case where MC data from Markov chains with different update
schemes or different equilibrium distributions enter the fit.
The specialization to multiple MC chains with the
same update scheme, often called replica, is straightforward.
On a single MC chain labelled by
{\cal E}
we assume to have estimators

p_{i}^{{\cal E}}(t)
at MC time
t
of several quantities labelled by
i
, such that

\bar{p}_{i}^{\cal E}=\frac{1}{N_{\cal E}}\sum_{t=1}^{N_{\cal E}}p_{i}^{\cal E}(t)

(3.21)

converges to
P_{i}
for
N_{\cal E}\to\infty
. In general, the
Y
-variables in our fits are functions

Y_{j}=\eta_{j}(P)

(3.22)

of primary observables
P
, estimated by
\bar{y}_{j}=\eta_{j}(\bar{p})
.
Note that if different chains yield estimators for
the same observable
P_{i}
, we just replace

P_{i}
by
the (weighted) average over the different chains. This average is then considered as part of the function
\eta_{j}
.

3.1
\Gamma
-method

The preferred method for dealing with auto-correlations
in the error estimate of
\bar{p}_{i}^{\cal E}
is the so-called
\Gamma
-method
[
5
,
1
]
.
Different error estimators are discussed in Appendix
D
.
We refer the interested reader to Ref.
[
6
]

for a proposal to estimate
\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle
and p-value
based on bootstrap resampling,
from data which are blocked in MC time with a block-length
large compared to the exponential auto-correlation time
(see also Refs.
[
7
,
8
]
for practical applications).

The
\Gamma
-method inherits its name from the matrix valued auto-correlation function

\Gamma^{\cal E}_{ij}(t)=\langle\Delta p^{\cal E}_{i}(t+t_{0})\,\Delta p^{\cal E}_{j}(t_{0})\rangle_{\cal E}\,,\quad\Delta p^{\cal E}_{i}(t)=p^{\cal E}_{i}(t)-P_{i}\,.

(3.23)

Here the average
\langle.\rangle_{\cal E}
may be defined
as the
t_{0}
-average of an infinitely long chain.
Linearizing the error propagation and
applying the chain rule as in
[
1
]

leads to the same simple form above (
\Delta p\to\Delta y
) also for the auto-correlation
function of derived quantities, with
the linearized fluctuations given by

\Delta y_{i}^{{\cal E}}(t)=\sum_{k}\frac{\partial\eta_{i}}{\partial p_{k}}\bigg|_{p=P}\Delta p^{\cal E}_{k}(t)\,.

(3.24)

Since
the covariance matrix of the variables
\bar{y}_{i}^{\cal E}
reads

C_{ij}^{\cal E}=\frac{1}{N_{\cal E}}\sum_{t=-\infty}^{\infty}\Gamma_{ij}^{\cal E}(t)\,,

(3.25)

the expected
\chi^{2}
,
eq. (
2.13
), becomes

\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle=\sum_{\cal E}\frac{1}{N_{\cal E}}\sum_{t=-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{tr}\big[\Gamma^{\cal E}(t)\,W(1-\mathcal{P})W\big]\,,

(3.26)

in presence of auto-correlations.
With finite length MC data
the above formulae need to be supplemented
by summation windows in
t
, over which
the MC estimate of
\Gamma_{ij}^{\cal E}(t)
,
denoted by
\Gamma_{ij}^{{\cal E},\mathrm{MC}}(t)
,
should be summed
[
5
,
1
,
9
]
.
Hence a good approximation to eq. (
3.26
) is found in the form

\displaystyle\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{f}\pm\Delta E_{f}\,,\quad E_{f}=\sum_{\cal E}E_{f}^{\cal E}(w_{\cal E})\,,

(3.27)

\displaystyle E_{f}^{\cal E}(w_{\cal E})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\cal E}}\sum_{t=-w_{\cal E}}^{w_{\cal E}}\mathrm{tr}[\Gamma^{{\cal E},\mathrm{MC}}(t)\,W(1-\mathcal{P})W]\,,

(3.28)

or, assuming
W^{-2}=\mathrm{diag}(C)
(uncorrelated fit),

\displaystyle\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{x}}-E_{s}\pm\Delta E_{s}\,,\quad E_{s}=\sum_{\cal E}E_{s}^{\cal E}(w_{\cal E})\,,

(3.29)

\displaystyle E_{s}^{\cal E}(w_{\cal E})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\cal E}}\sum_{t=-w_{\cal E}}^{w_{\cal E}}\mathrm{tr}[\Gamma^{{\cal E},\mathrm{MC}}(t)\,W\mathcal{P}W]\,.

(3.30)

In both cases the standard automatic windowing procedure of
Ref.
[
1
]
or the one of Ref.
[
9
]

may be applied simply replacing
\Gamma_{F}(t)
in Ref.
[
1
]

by
\mathrm{tr}[\Gamma(t)\,W\mathcal{P}W]
or

\mathrm{tr}[\Gamma(t)\,W(1-\mathcal{P})W]
,
while nothing changes in the discussions in Refs.
[
1
,
9
]
.
Note that the knowledge of the full covariance matrix is not required.
Its presence is captured by the traces in eq. (
3.28
) and eq. (
3.30
).
We may use the same automatic determination of the windows ensemble by ensemble and
possibly the addition of a tail
[
9
]
due to large auto-correlations
and will have balanced statistical and systematic errors
(from the truncation of the
t
-sum) of size

\Delta E_{f,s}^{2}\approx 2\sum_{\cal E}\frac{2w_{\cal E}+1}{N_{\cal E}}\,[E_{f,s}^{\cal E}(w_{\cal E})]^{2}\,.

(3.31)

Assuming that the required
w_{\cal E}
are not much different,
for strong correlations (not auto-correlations)
eq. (
3.28
) will be more precise, while in
the opposite case eq. (
3.30
) will be better.
Obviously one may just evaluate
both and take the one with the smaller error.

The covariance matrix may be estimated
from
\Gamma_{ij}^{{\cal E},\mathrm{MC}}

by truncating the
t
-sum in eq. (
3.25
)
to the summation windows
w_{\cal E}
obtained from
the automatic procedure for
E_{f,s}^{\cal E}
.
The p-value is
then obtained from eq. (
2.18
). In Sect.
4
we demonstrate in a toy model that this is a valid approach, namely
the p-value is stable against increasing the window size. Note that only modes with

\lambda(\nu)>0
enter eq. (
2.18
).

Two remarks on the error estimates, eq. (
3.31
) are in order. These are of the type error-of-the-error.
As is common
[
5
,
1
,
9
]
, we use the Madras-Sokal approximation, which consists of neglecting the connected parts of a dynamical four-point function. We are not aware of
a general argument on the quality of this approximation, but F. Virotta found that it
works quite well in a simple interacting field theory
[
10
]
.
Secondly eq. (
3.31
) neglects the statistical error of
\mathcal{P}
which
is a function of
\bar{a}
and of the same order in
N^{-1}
as
\Delta E_{f,s}
. In Appendix
A
we provide the analytic formula for
its evaluation and in all our experiments we find it to be subdominant compared to
the terms in eq. (
3.31
).

Note that when the data can be partitioned in sufficiently many and large blocks,
i.e. when autocorrelations are not an issue, one may consider the bootstrap
approach to get an estimate of the error of the p-value.

4
Numerical illustration

4.1
Toy model

As an illustration, we consider the free one-component
scalar theory with standard action,

S=a^{d}\sum_{x}\,\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\phi(x)\partial_{\mu}\phi(x)+{m^{2}\over 2}\phi^{2}(x)\bigg]

(4.32)

on a
T\times L^{d}
torus
(periodic boundary conditions) discretized on a hyper-cubic
lattice with spacing
a
and standard discrete forward derivative
\partial_{\mu}
.
Our observables are the often used time-momentum correlation
functions

G_{\phi\phi}(t,\mathbf{p})=a^{d}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\langle\phi(t,\mathbf{x})\phi(0)\rangle\,,\quad G_{\phi^{2}\phi^{2}}(t)=a^{d}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\langle\phi^{2}(t,\mathbf{x})\phi^{2}(0)\rangle\,.

(4.33)

In absence of interactions, the Green function
G_{\phi\phi}

on a torus with finite time extent
T
is given by

G_{\phi\phi}(t,\mathbf{p})=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\Delta(t+nT,\mathbf{p})\,,

(4.34)

in terms of the infinite
T
propagator

\Delta(t,\mathbf{p})=\frac{e^{-|t|\,\omega(\mathbf{p})}}{2\sinh(a\omega(\mathbf{p}))/a}\,,

(4.35)

where
\omega(\mathbf{p})
satisfies the lattice dispersion relation

2[\cosh(a\omega(\mathbf{p}))-1]=a^{2}m^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{d}(1-\cos(ap_{i}))\,,

(4.36)

and
p_{i}=\frac{2\pi}{L}k_{i}\,,k_{i}=0,1,\ldots L/a-1
.
A further correlator is

G_{\phi^{2}\phi^{2}}(t)=\frac{2}{L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}}G_{\phi\phi}(t,\mathbf{p})^{2}+\frac{1}{L^{d}}\Big[\sum_{\mathbf{p}}G_{\phi\phi}(0,\mathbf{p})\Big]^{2}\,,

(4.37)

where the second term is a disconnected contribution
which dominates at long distances.

On a given MC configuration, one may estimate
G_{\phi\phi}
from

E_{\phi\phi}(t,\mathbf{p})=\tilde{\phi}(t,\mathbf{p})\,\tilde{\phi}(0,-\mathbf{p})\,,\quad\tilde{\phi}(t,\mathbf{p})=\frac{a^{d}}{L^{d/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{z}}e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{z}}\phi(z_{0},\mathbf{z})\,,\,

(4.38)

and for the case of negligible auto-correlations,
i.e. measurements separated by MC time much larger than
the exponential auto-correlation time,
the covariance matrix of
N
measurements of the estimators
E(t,\mathbf{p})

takes the simple form (
g(t)\equiv G_{\phi\phi}(t,\mathbf{p})
)

N\times\mathrm{Cov}(t,t^{\prime})=g(t)g(t^{\prime})+\delta_{\mathbf{p},0}\,g(t-t^{\prime})g(0)\,.

(4.39)

We note that off-diagonal matrix elements are exponentially suppressed
by
e^{-|t-t^{\prime}|m}
and there is the typical strong exponential
noise/signal enhancement at large
t\approx t^{\prime}
.
For the noisy
G_{\phi^{2}\phi^{2}}
correlator,
we employ an estimator with a maximal
volume average,

E_{\phi^{2}\phi^{2}}(t)=\frac{a^{d+1}}{TL^{d}}\sum_{z_{0}=0}^{T-a}\overline{\phi^{2}}(z_{0}+t)\,\overline{\phi^{2}}(z_{0})\,,\quad\overline{\phi^{2}}(z_{0})\equiv\sum_{\mathbf{z}}\phi^{2}(z_{0},\mathbf{z})\,,\,

(4.40)

4.4
Determination of the fit window

At this point we turn to the typical problem of finding the
minimal value of the lower end of the fit range,

for which the single exponential
ansatz describes well the data.
We want to compare the use of the uncorrelated fits
to correlated ones, but the covariance matrices estimated with the statistics of our test-runs
are (typically) not positive.
Therefore we generated a long MC chain with
40000 measurements solely to estimate
C
, and use
W=C^{-1/2}
in the definition of the correlated
\chi^{2}
. Of course, this is not a
viable procedure in general while uncorrelated fits are always possible.

uncorrelated fits tend to give

precision of the estimated best fit parameters.

5
Conclusions

Estimating the covariance matrix
C
from limited correlated and auto-correlated data,
in particular the lower part of its spectrum, often proves to be challenging. Here we investigated a method that bypasses
the need for the inverse
covariance matrix and is numerically robust
against fluctuations of its small eigenvalues.

By studying the expansion of the
\chi^{2}
function about its minimum, we have
obtained expressions to compute the expected value of
\chi^{2}

and the corresponding p-value, for arbitrary positive weight matrices
W
. The formulae contain the covariance matrix in such a way that
the upper part of the spectrum of
C
predominantly determines the expected
\chi^{2}
and the p-value. They therefore provide
robust
statistical tests for the fits.

We observe that it is important to use the p-value
to discriminate between good and bad fits, not
just the reduced
\chi^{2}
.
An investigation in a toy model illustrates how
the method works with moderate statistics and with auto-correlations present. Unfortunately,
the p-values of uncorrelated fits are somewhat less sharply discriminating than the ones of correlated fits. This is the price that one has
to pay for their far superior robustness against
statistical fluctuations. Thus, when the correlation of the data is well known (very long MC chains),
correlated fits are the preferred choice. Of course, one can also balance the pros and cons of
the two extreme choices by using intermediate weight matrices
W
together with the proper p-value.

Statistical error of
\mathcal{P}

Here we derive the explicit form of the projector and other useful

\mathcal{P}W\delta\bar{y}=W\phi^{\alpha}(\bar{a})\delta\bar{a}^{\alpha}+\mathrm{O}(N^{-1})\,.

(A.42)

By multiplying on the left by
\phi^{\beta\mathrm{T}}(\bar{a})W
, we obtain

\delta\bar{a}^{\alpha}=[H^{-1}]^{\alpha\beta}(W\phi^{\beta},W\delta\bar{y})\,,\quad H^{\alpha\beta}=(W\phi^{\alpha}(\bar{a}),W\phi^{\beta}(\bar{a}))\,,

(A.43)

which tells how to propagate the errors from the data to the fitted parameters.
More specifically the covariance matrix of
\bar{a}^{\alpha}

C^{\alpha\beta}=[H^{-1}]^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\,(W\phi^{\alpha^{\prime}},C_{\mathrm{w}}W\phi^{\beta^{\prime}})\,[H^{-1}]^{\beta^{\prime}\beta}\,.

(A.44)

Now combining together eq. (
A.43

A.42

\mathcal{P}=W\phi^{\alpha}(\bar{a})\,[H^{-1}]^{\alpha\beta}\,(\phi^{\beta})^{\mathrm{T}}(\bar{a})W

(A.45)

which satisfies all the properties outlined in the main text.
The partial derivative

\frac{\partial\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle}{\partial\bar{a}^{\alpha}}=2\mathrm{tr}\big[C_{\mathrm{w}}\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}(1-\mathcal{P})\big]\,,

(A.46)

combined with eq. (
A.44
) gives us the explicit form of the contribution
to the error of
\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle
that originates from the

\mathcal{P}

\bar{a}^{\alpha}

we have used

\mathcal{P}^{\alpha}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial\bar{a}^{\alpha}}=\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}(1-\mathcal{P})+(1-\mathcal{P})\mathcal{(}K^{\alpha})^{\mathrm{T}}\,,\quad\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}=W\phi^{\alpha^{\prime}}(\bar{a})\,[H^{-1}]^{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}\,(\phi^{\beta^{\prime}\alpha})^{\mathrm{T}}(\bar{a})W\,.

(A.47)

Appendix C
Generalizations

In the main text we have considered the
case of data described by a single fit function,
but more complicated cases can be easily
accommodated.

When different
sets of data
Y^{(1)},Y^{(2)}
(with different sizes
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
)
are described by different
models
\phi^{(1)},\phi^{(2)}
with a few common parameters,
it suffices to extend
N_{\mathrm{x}}=M_{1}+M_{2}
to incorporate all
points, such that

\begin{split}\bar{y}_{i}&=\{\bar{y}^{(1)}_{1},\dots,\bar{y}^{(1)}_{M_{1}},\bar{y}^{(2)}_{1},\dots,\bar{y}^{(2)}_{M_{2}}\}\,,\\
\phi_{i}(a)&=\{\phi^{(1)}_{1}(a),\dots,\phi^{(1)}_{M_{1}}(a),\phi^{(2)}_{1}(a),\dots,\phi^{(2)}_{M_{2}}(a)\}\,.\end{split}

(C.48)

If the two data sets are correlated, e.g. they originate
from the same MC ensembles, but estimating the full
N_{\mathrm{x}}\times N_{\mathrm{x}}

covariance matrix and its inverse turns out to be impractical, the method proposed in this
manuscript allows to arbitrarily regularize the covariance matrix
in the definition of
\chi^{2}
(e.g. just consider the diagonal blocks

M_{1}\times M_{1}
and
M_{2}\times M_{2}
) and still have a reliable
statistical interpretation of the associated fit.

Similarly, there are situations where
we might want to include the error of the kinematic
coordinates
\bar{x}_{i}
(for instance they might be
obtained from averages over MC chains which may also be correlated
with the data points
\bar{y}_{i}
). Such cases are easily incorporated
by considering the vectors

r_{i}=\{\bar{y}_{1}-\Phi(X_{1},A),\dots,\bar{y}_{N_{\mathrm{x}}}-\Phi(X_{N_{\mathrm{x}}},A),\bar{x}_{1}-X_{1},\dots\bar{x}_{N_{\mathrm{x}}}-X_{N_{\mathrm{x}}}\}

(C.49)

in the definition of
\chi^{2}=||Wr||^{2}
.
The matrix
W^{2}
may correspond to various regularizations of
the inverse of the extended covariance matrix

C=\Big\langle\,\begin{matrix}\delta\bar{y}\delta\bar{y}^{\mathrm{T}}&\delta\bar{y}\delta\bar{x}^{\mathrm{T}}\\
\delta\bar{x}\delta\bar{y}^{\mathrm{T}}&\delta\bar{x}\delta\bar{x}^{\mathrm{T}}\end{matrix}\,\Big\rangle\,,

(C.50)

and nothing changes in the derivation of
\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle

and in the discussion on the goodness-of-fit
for the model function
\Phi
.
In the equations above
X_{i}
represent the true values of
the kinematic coordinates, and
\delta\bar{x}_{i}=\bar{x}_{i}-X_{i}
.

Appendix D
Alternative estimators of the covariance matrix

In this Appendix we discuss the replacement of the
\Gamma
-method by
other error estimation techniques.

D.1
Jackknife and binning methods

When it is known that the (exponential) auto-correlation time
is small compared to the run length
N_{\cal E}
, the covariance matrix
may be estimated by Jackknife resampling
of binned data, with bin length significantly
larger than the auto-correlation time.
Assuming that
N_{\cal E}=B_{\cal E}N_{B}^{\cal E}
, blocked measurements
on a given ensemble are defined as

b_{i}^{\cal E}(k)=\frac{1}{B_{\cal E}}\sum_{t=1}^{B_{\cal E}}p_{i}^{\cal E}((k-1)B_{\cal E}+t)\,,\quad k=1,\dots,N_{B}^{\cal E}\,,

(D.51)

while complementary or jackknife bins are

c_{i}^{\cal E}(k)=\frac{N_{B}^{\cal E}\bar{p}_{i}^{\cal E}-b_{i}^{\cal E}(k)}{N_{B}^{\cal E}-1}\,.

(D.52)

An estimator of the covariance matrix is given by

C_{ij}=\sum_{{\cal E}}\frac{N_{B}^{\cal E}-1}{N_{B}^{\cal E}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{B}^{\cal E}}\Delta c^{\cal E}_{i}(k)\Delta c^{\cal E}_{j}(k)\,,\quad\Delta c_{i}^{\cal E}(k)=c_{i}^{\cal E}(k)-\bar{p}_{i}^{\cal E}\,,

(D.53)

and the case of derived observables
Y_{i}=\eta_{i}(P)

is obtained by replacing
c_{i}^{\cal E}(k)
with
\eta_{i}(c^{\cal E}(k))
in the equations above.
The generalization of our previous results to jackknife estimators is
straightforward: starting from the intermediate quantities

\displaystyle E_{f}^{\cal E}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\Delta c^{\cal E}(k)]^{\mathrm{T}}W(1-\mathcal{P})W\Delta c^{\cal E}(k)\,,

(D.54)

\displaystyle E_{s}^{\cal E}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\Delta c^{\cal E}(k)]^{\mathrm{T}}W\mathcal{P}W\Delta c^{\cal E}(k)\,,

(D.55)

the central value of
\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle
is obtained by
replacing
E_{f,s}
in eq. (
3.27
) and eq. (
3.29
) with

E_{f,s}=\sum_{\cal E}\frac{N_{B}^{\cal E}-1}{N_{B}^{\cal E}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{B}^{\cal E}}E_{f,s}^{\cal E}(k)\,.

(D.56)

Using the results of Ref.
[
1
]

one can estimate their errors according to

[\Delta E_{f,s}]^{2}\approx 2\sum_{\cal E}\frac{1}{N_{B}^{\cal E}}\big[E_{f,s}^{\cal E}\big]^{2}\,,

(D.57)

whereas the p-value is estimated from the covariance matrix
in eq. (
D.53
).

D.2
Priors and the Bayesian approach

Gaussian priors on the parameters, a special case of a Bayesian analysis,
are easily taken into account by extending the
r
vectors for
\chi^{2}=||Wr||^{2}
to

r_{i}=\{\bar{y}_{1}-\Phi(x_{1},a),\dots,\bar{y}_{N_{\mathrm{x}}}-\Phi(x_{N_{\mathrm{x}}},a),a_{1}-\widetilde{a}_{1},\dots,a_{N_{\mathrm{priors}}}-\widetilde{a}_{N_{\mathrm{priors}}}\}

(D.58)

and the covariance matrix to

C=\Big\langle\,\begin{matrix}\delta\bar{y}\delta\bar{y}^{\mathrm{T}}&0\\
0&\mathrm{diag}[1/\sigma_{\widetilde{a}_{1}}^{2},\dots,1/\sigma_{\widetilde{a}_{N_{\mathrm{priors}}}}^{2}]\end{matrix}\,\Big\rangle\,,

(D.59)

with
\widetilde{a}^{\alpha}
and
\sigma_{\widetilde{a}^{\alpha}}
representing the prior and its
width for the parameter
a^{\alpha}
.
One can easily verify that for correlated fits
\langle\chi^{2}(\bar{a})\rangle=N_{\mathrm{x}}+N_{\mathrm{priors}}-N_{\mathrm{A}}
.

D.3
Master fields

Recently M. Lüscher has derived formulae
[
15
]
for
statistical errors based on the invariance under translations of space-time, rather than of Monte-Carlo time. We write

\bar{y}_{i}=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{s}y_{i}(s)\,,\quad\lim_{V\to\infty}\bar{y}_{i}=Y_{i}\,,

(D.60)

with
s
labelling the sites for a given large-volume configuration,
called master field. Following Ref.
[
16
]
,
the
\Gamma
method is then defined from the counter-part of

\Gamma^{\mathrm{MC}}(t)
, which we may now call
\Gamma^{\mathrm{MF}}_{ij}(s)
,
and which is evaluated from the fluctuations

\delta\bar{y}_{i}(s)=y_{i}(s)-\bar{y}_{i}
. As described in Ref.
[
15
]
,
the variance of
\bar{y}_{i}
may be obtained by summing
\Gamma^{\mathrm{MF}}_{ii}(s)

up to distances
|s|<R
, thus introducing only an exponentially small bias
of order
e^{-2m_{\pi}R}
.
Our formulae then hold after
replacing
\Gamma^{\mathrm{MC}}_{ij}
with
\Gamma^{\mathrm{MF}}_{ij}

e.g. in eq. (
3.26
) and
after providing an appropriate windowing procedure.
