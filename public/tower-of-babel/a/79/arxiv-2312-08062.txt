Non-hydrodynamic response in QCD-like plasma
Weiyao Ke, Yi Yin

Abstract
Quark-gluon plasma’s (QGP) properties at non-hydrodynamic and non-perturbative regimes remain largely unexplored. Here, we examine the response functions describing how a QGP-like plasma responds to initial energy-momentum disturbance in both static and Bjorken-expanding plasma at non-hydrodynamic gradient using the Boltzmann equation in the relaxation-time approximation (RTA). We show that the resulting response functions are remarkably similar in both static and expanding backgrounds at non-hydrodynamic gradients. While non-hydrodynamic response can not be described by the conventional first-order and second-order theories, its behavior is reasonably captured by the extended version of hydrodynamics proposed by us Ke:2022tqf . The potential sensitivity of the Euclidean correlator to non-hydrodynamic response is also illustrated.

Introduction

When an in-homogeneous and time-dependent disturbance is created on the quark-gluon plasma (QGP),
its response reveals the rich structure of the medium.
The hydrodynamic theory should describe the medium’s response in long wavelength and low-frequency limits.
On the other hand,
one could apply the perturbative QCD method to study QGP’s behavior at an asymptotic large gradient.
Apparently, exploring the intermediate regime between those two
asymptotic limits is crucial to understanding how the properties of QCD matter evolve as scale changes. Yet, our knowledge about this non-hydrodynamic and possibly non-perturbative regime is very limited as illustrated in Fig.

.

Figure 1:

A cartoon illustrating our current understanding of Quark-Gluon Plasma (QGP) as a function of the characteristic momentum scale (
k

k
is small, QGP behaves like a near-perfect liquid. As
k

The properties of QGP at the intermediate scale are less known, but they can be explored by studying jet-medium interaction observables in heavy-ion collisions and by conducting the lattice QCD calculation of the Euclidean correlators, see further discussion in the text.

By comparing the behavior of conventional hydrodynamic theories extrapolated outside the hydrodynamic regime with QGP-like models,
we may monitor how some hydrodynamic behavior disappears, and possible new features emerge as characteristic gradients gradually increase.
Doing so can bring insight into the nature of the transition from QGP liquid to weakly-coupled parton gas.
In this work,
we will compare the non-hydrodynamic energy-momentum response obtained from the first-order and some variants of second-order hydrodynamics with that from a particular microscopic theory, which we consider the kinetic theory under relaxation time approximation (RTA).
Despite its simplicity, the RTA kinetic theory captures a transition from the hydrodynamic regime to a regime whose response is dominated by quasi-particle excitations and has been widely used as a model for a QCD-like system.
The study of the retarded correlator, which describes the medium’s response induced by the metric perturbation using the Boltzmann equation, can be found in Refs.
Hong:2010at
;
Romatschke:2017vte
;
Kurkela:2017xis
.
Our focus is on the response function that describes the induced energy-momentum due to the initial energy-momentum disturbance.

Another motivation of our work is to further investigate and elaborate on the notion of the extended hydrodynamic regime (EHR) proposed by us in Ref.
Ke:2022tqf
,
This scenario corresponds to the situation that a high-frequency sound exists that governs the medium’s response outside the hydrodynamic regime on the one hand, and its dispersion is not described by naively extending conventional hydrodynamic theories outside the hydrodynamic scale on the other hand.
In Ref.
Ke:2022tqf
, we demonstrate the presence of this scenario for strongly coupled
{\cal N}=4
super-symmetric Yang-Mills (SYM) theory and RTA kinetic theory.
We also show that the sound dispersion can be captured by a “deformed” version of MIS, namely MIS*, with a suitable choice of model parameters.
In this work, we show detailed analysis indicating that the same MIS* equations can describe not only dispersion relation but also response function outside the hydrodynamic regime.

Phenomenologically, the observables sensitive to the jet-medium interaction can help extract the medium’s properties at a non-hydrodynamic scale.
An energetic parton may be viewed as a point-like source that deposits energy and momentum density to the medium it transverses to. It would generate perturbation for a vast range of gradients, including those outside the hydrodynamic regime.
Recently, evidence has been suggesting that properly incorporating the medium’s response to those disturbances is essential in describing data (e.g., see Refs.
KunnawalkamElayavalli:2017hxo
;
He:2018xjv
;
Cao:2020wlm
;
Cao:2022odi
;
JETSCAPE:2020uew
).
To date, many studies are using linearized first-order and second-order hydrodynamics (or its variant) to account for the medium’s response (see, for example, Refs.
Casalderrey-Solana:2004fdk
;
Neufeld:2008fi
;
Casalderrey-Solana:2020rsj
).
We have analyzed the energy-momentum response for a Bjorken-expanding background.
This allows us to compare the response functions for the static and expanding background and discuss the importance of non-hydrodynamic response in the context of jet-medium interaction.

Theoretically, one may extract the medium’s properties from the Euclidean correlator, which can be studied from the first-principle lattice QCD calculations.
While the Euclidean correlators are notoriously insensitive to the infrared structure of the real-time response (see ref.
Moore:2010bu
for a review), it may be suitable for extracting response at a gradient of the order
\pi T
.
We present perhaps the first illustrative analysis that suggests the sensitivity of the Euclidean correlator to the non-hydrodynamic response.
Assuming the EHR scenario, we also explore the lattice’s potential to extract the medium’s properties in EHR.

2
Response in a static background

2.1
Response function and retarded correlator

We want to investigate the behavior of a thermal medium when it is in equilibrium and then undergoes an infinitesimally small perturbation.
For definiteness, we look at a set of observables such as energy, and charge densities and collectively denoted the corresponding operator by
\hat{O}^{I}
where
I,J
are the labels.
In response to the perturbation, the expectation value
O=\langle\hat{O}\rangle
will deviate from its equilibrium value
\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{{\rm eq}}
, i.e.
\delta O=O-\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{{\rm eq}}\neq 0
.

The present work primarily considers the response induced by the out-of-equilibrium deviation
\delta O^{I}_{{\rm in}}(\bm{x})\equiv\delta O^{I}(t=0,\bm{x})
at some initial time, say
t=0
.
To describe such a response, we define response function (R.F. )
{\cal G}
as

\displaystyle\delta O^{I}(t,\bm{x})=\int_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}{\cal G}^{I}_{\,J}(t,\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})\,\delta O^{J}_{{\rm in}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\,

(1)

where
\int_{\bm{x}}\equiv\int d^{3}\bm{x}
and the summation over the dummy index is understood.
We shall assume that
{\cal G}
satisfies the following initial condition:

\displaystyle{\cal G}^{I}_{\,J}(t=0,\bm{x})=\delta^{I}_{\,J}\,\delta^{3}(\bm{x})\,,

(2)

meaning we have assumed that when
I\neq J
, a non-zero
\delta O_{I}
will not cause a non-zero
\delta O_{J}
at the beginning.
The discussion below can be easily generalized when this assumption is relaxed.

In parallel,
the retarded correlator

\displaystyle G_{R}^{IJ}(t,t^{\prime};\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\equiv-i\langle\,\left[\hat{O}^{I}(t,\bm{x}),\hat{O}^{J}(t^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime})\right]\rangle\,\theta(t-t^{\prime})

(3)

has been widely considered in literature.
This correlator describes the response induced by the source
\psi_{I}(t,\bm{x})
that is conjugate to
\hat{O}^{I}
asuming that the perturbed Hamiltonian takes the form
\Delta\hat{H}(t)=-\int_{\bm{x}}\,\hat{O}^{J}(t,\bm{x})\psi_{J}(t,\bm{x})
.

We can establish the relation between R.F. and the retarded correlator.
The key observation is that
{\cal G}
is entirely determined by the medium’s properties and hence does not depend on how the initial deviation
\delta O_{{\rm in}}
is created.
We may generate
\delta O^{J}_{{\rm in}}
by turning on the source
\psi_{J}
at
t=-\infty
and switch it off at
t=0
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\psi_{J}(t,\bm{x})=\theta(-t)\,e^{-\epsilon t}\psi_{J,{\rm in}}(\bm{x})\,,

(4)

where an infinitesimal value
\epsilon
is introduced to ensure that the source vanishes at
t=-\infty
.
Using the linear response theory (e.g., ref
Petreczky:2005nh
), we then have, after taking spatial Fourier transform (assuming the translational invariance in space and time), that

\displaystyle\delta O^{I}(t,\bm{k})=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\,dt^{\prime}\,G^{IJ}_{R}(t-t^{\prime},\bm{k})\,\psi_{J}(t^{\prime},\bm{k})\,.

(5)

Particularly, evaluating eq. (
5
) at
t=0
with
\psi_{J}
specified by eq. (
4
) yields

\displaystyle\delta O^{I}_{{\rm in}}(\bm{k})=\left(\int^{0}_{-\infty}dt^{\prime}\,G_{R}^{IJ}(-t^{\prime},\bm{k})e^{\epsilon t^{\prime}}\right)\,\psi_{J,{\rm in}}(\bm{k})=\chi^{IJ}(\bm{k})\,\psi_{J,{\rm in}}(\bm{k})\,.

(6)

In other words, the induced
\delta O^{I}_{{\rm in}}
resulting from turning on a time-independent source
\psi_{J,{\rm in}}
from
t=-\infty
to
t=0
is determined by the static susceptibility.

\displaystyle\chi^{IJ}(\bm{k})=\int^{\infty}_{0}dt\,G^{IJ}_{R}(t,\bm{k})=G^{IJ}_{R}(\omega=0,\bm{k})\,.

(7)

Now, we take the time derivative of eq. (
5
) w.r.t time
t
.
After using
\partial_{t}G_{R}(t-t^{\prime})=-\partial_{t^{\prime}}G_{R}(t-t^{\prime})
and performing the integration over
t^{\prime}
by parts,
we obtain (see also eq. 2.7 of Ref.
Petreczky:2005nh
)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\delta O^{I}(t,\bm{k})=-G_{R}^{IJ}(t,\bm{k})\psi_{J,{\rm in}}(\bm{k})\,.

(8)

Assuming
\chi^{IJ}
is invertible, we can rewrite (
8
) as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\delta O^{I}(t,\bm{k})

\displaystyle=-G_{R}^{IJ^{\prime}}(t,\bm{k})\,(\chi^{-1}(\bm{k}))_{J^{\prime}J}\delta O^{J}_{0}(\bm{k})\,.

(9)

If we take the derivative of eq. (
1
) and compare it with eq. (
9
), we find the relation between the response function and retarded correlator that we are after:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\cal G}^{I}_{J}(t,\bm{k})=-G_{R}^{IJ^{\prime}}(t,\bm{k})\,(\chi^{-1}_{R}(\bm{k}))_{J^{\prime}J}\,.

(10)

Further performing the temporal Fourier transformation, we have

\displaystyle i\omega{\cal G}^{I}_{J}(\omega,\bm{k})+\delta^{I}_{J}=G_{R}^{IJ^{\prime}}(\omega,\bm{k})\,(\chi^{-1}(\bm{k}))_{J^{\prime}J}

(11)

where we have used the property of ”one-sided” Fourier transform

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{0}dt\,e^{i\omega t}\,\partial_{t}\,g(t)=-i\omega g(\omega,\bm{k})-g_{{\rm in}}\,.

(12)

for any function
g(t)
satisfying
g(t=0)=g_{{\rm in}}
and the initial condition for
{\cal G}
(
2
) in the spatial Fourier space :

\displaystyle{\cal G}^{I}_{J}(t=0,\bm{k})=\delta^{I}_{J}\,.

(13)

Eqs. (
10
) and (
11
) are the main results of this section.
They make it clear that one can determine the R.F. from the knowledge of the retarded correlator. In other words,
how the medium reacts to the external source also tells us how it would respond to the initial perturbation.
Moreover, the relation (
11
) reveals that the analytical structure of R.F. is the same as that of the retarded correlator. For instance, the pole of the correlator in the complex frequency plane should be the pole of R.F. at exactly the same location.

In the upcoming sections, we will discuss R.F. of the energy-momentum tensor (EMT):

\displaystyle\delta T^{\mu\nu}(t,\bm{k})={\cal G}^{\mu\nu}_{\,\,\,\,\,\alpha\beta}(t,\bm{k})\,\delta T^{\alpha\beta}_{{\rm in}}(\bm{k})\,.

(14)

Our focus will be on a charge-neutral and conformal fluid.
In this situation, there are only three independent components in
{\cal G}^{\mu\nu}_{\,\,\alpha\beta}
. Among them, one is associated with the behavior of shear stress. We will concentrate on the remaining two, which we choose to be

where without losing generality, we take the Fourier momentum
\bm{k}
along

2.2
Kinetic theory

The response function has to be computed from some microscopic theories to which we take the kinetic equation under relaxation time approximation (RTA)

\displaystyle p^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}f-\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\alpha\sigma}p^{\alpha}p^{\sigma}\frac{\partial f}{\partial p^{\lambda}}=-\frac{u\cdot p}{\tau_{R}}\,\left(f-f_{{\rm eq}}\right)\,.

(16)

Here,
f(t,\bm{x},\bm{p})
is the distribution of relativistic particles and
\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\alpha\beta}
is the metric connection.
The relaxation time
\tau_{R}
sets the timescale at which
f
approaches the local equilibrium

\displaystyle f_{{\rm eq}}\equiv e^{\beta p^{\mu}u_{\mu}}\,.

(17)

The local fluid velocity,
u^{\mu}
together with energy density
\epsilon
, are determined by the matching condition

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}=\int_{\bm{p}}\,\frac{1}{p}\,p^{\mu}\,p^{\nu}\,f\,,\qquad T^{\mu\nu}u_{\nu}=\epsilon\,u^{\mu}\,.

(18)

where
\int_{\bm{p}}\equiv\int d^{3}\bm{p}/(2\pi)^{3}
,
and for ultra-relativistic particle, we have
p=|\bm{p}|
.
With
\epsilon
given, the effective temperature
T=1/\beta
in eq. (
17
) is determined by assuming the equation of state for the relativistic Boltzmann gas, i.e.,

\epsilon(T)=N_{0}(3T^{4})/\pi^{2}
where
N_{0}
counts the quantum state and species of particles under consideration, although the results shown in the present paper do not depend on the value of
N_{0}
.
For the system to be conformal system, we require
\tau_{R}
scales with
\epsilon

\displaystyle\tau_{R}\sim(\epsilon)^{-1/4}\,.

(19)

We shall study R.F.  (
15
) describing the induced energy density and fluid velocity around the equilibrium background

\displaystyle\epsilon=\epsilon_{0}+\delta\epsilon\,,\qquad u^{\mu}=(1,{\bm{0}})+(0,\delta\bm{u})\,,

(20)

where we use subscript
0
to denote the background value of thermodynamic and fluid variables such as
\epsilon,u^{\mu}
.
Using

\displaystyle\delta f_{\textrm{eq}}=e^{-\beta_{0}p^{0}}\left(-p_{0}\delta\beta+\beta_{0}\,{\bm{p}}\cdot\delta{\bm{u}}\right)\,

(21)

we have

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}+\bm{v}\cdot{\bm{\partial}}+\tau^{-1}_{R}\right)\,\delta f=\beta p_{0}\,f_{0}\,\tau^{-1}_{R}\left[-\frac{\delta\beta}{\beta}+\delta\bm{u}\cdot\bm{v}\right]\,.

(22)

where we have introduced the short-handed notation
f_{0}\equiv e^{-\beta_{0}p^{0}}
for the background equilibrium distribution and
\bm{v}=\hat{p}
is the single particle velocity.
We shall solve eq. (
22
) under the initial condition that the distribution is in local equilibrium:

\displaystyle\delta\,f_{I}=\delta\,e^{\beta(p\cdot u)}=\beta_{0}p^{0}\,f_{0}\left(\frac{-\delta\beta_{I}}{\beta_{0}}+\delta\bm{u}_{I}\cdot\bm{v}\right)\,,

(23)

where
\delta\bm{u}_{I},\delta\beta_{I}
denotes the value of
\delta\bm{u},\delta\beta
at
t=0
.
The solution to eq. (
22
) in Fourier space then reads

\displaystyle\delta f(\bar{\omega},\bar{k};\bm{v})=\frac{i\,\beta_{0}p_{0}f_{0}\tau_{R}^{-1}}{(\omega-\bm{v}\cdot\bar{\bm{k}}+i\tau_{R}^{-1})}\,\left[\left(-\frac{\delta\beta(\bar{\omega},\bar{\bm{k}})}{\beta_{0}}+\delta\bm{u}(\bar{\omega},\bar{\bm{k}})\cdot\bm{v}\right)+\frac{\delta f_{I}(\bar{\bm{k}};\bm{v})}{\beta_{0}p_{0}f_{0}\tau_{R}^{-1}}\right]\,.

(24)

where
\bar{\omega}=\omega\tau_{R}
and
\bar{k}=k\tau_{R}
.
Plug in the Fourier transform of the initial condition, and further use the relation
\beta^{2}\delta\epsilon=-c_{V}\delta\beta
with
c_{V}=\partial\epsilon/\partial T
being the specific heat, we arrive at

\displaystyle\delta f(\bar{\omega},\bar{k})=\frac{i\,\beta_{0}p_{0}f_{0}}{(\bar{\omega}-\bm{v}\cdot\bar{\bm{k}}+i)}\,\left[\frac{\beta_{0}\delta\epsilon(\bar{\omega},\bar{\bm{k}})}{c_{V}}+\delta\bm{u}(\bar{\omega},\bar{\bm{k}})\cdot\bm{v}+\left(\frac{\beta_{0}\delta\epsilon_{I}(\bar{\bm{k}})}{c_{V}}+\delta\bm{u}_{I}(\bar{\bm{k}})\cdot\bm{v}\right)\,\tau_{R}\right]\,.

(25)

Note that the mass dimension of
\delta\bm{u}(\bar{\omega},\bar{k})
is
-4
while that of
\delta\bm{u}_{I}(\bar{k})
is
-3
.

We are now ready to determine the R.F. .
For the response in the longitudinal channel, we consider non-zero
\delta\epsilon,\delta u^{z}
where, as we mentioned earlier, we shall take
\bm{k}
along the
z
-direction.
We are interested in computing energy-energy response function
{\cal G}_{L}
(
15
) so we use the initial condition
(\delta\epsilon,\delta u^{z})=(\delta\epsilon_{{\rm in}},0)
at
t=0
in accordance with eq. (
2
))
Substituting eq. (
24
) into the linearized version of eq. (
18
),

\displaystyle\delta T^{\mu\nu}=\int_{\bm{p}}\frac{p^{\mu}p^{\nu}}{p}\,\delta f\,,

(26)

and use the relation

\displaystyle\delta\epsilon=\delta T^{00},\qquad\delta T^{0i}=w_{0}\delta u^{i}

(27)

where
w=\epsilon+P
is the enthalpy density,
we find

\displaystyle\,\delta\epsilon(\omega,\bm{k})=C_{0}(\omega,\bm{k})(\delta\epsilon(\omega,\bm{k})+\delta\epsilon_{{\rm in}}(\bm{k})\tau_{R})+\frac{1}{c^{2}_{s}}\,C_{1}(\omega,\bm{k})\,\delta T^{0z}(\omega,\bm{k})\,,

(28a)

\displaystyle\,\delta T^{0z}(\omega,\bm{k})=C_{1}(\omega,\bm{k})(\delta\epsilon(\omega,\bm{k})+\epsilon_{{\rm in}}(\bm{k})\tau_{R})+\frac{1}{c^{2}_{s}}\,C_{2}(\omega,\bm{k})\,\delta T^{0z}(\omega,\bm{k})\,.

(28b)

Here, we have used the expression for
c_{V}

\displaystyle c_{V}=\int_{\bm{p}}p\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial T}=\beta^{2}\int\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}\,p^{2}f_{0}\,

(29)

which leads to the following useful expression

\displaystyle\,\int\,\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}\,\beta_{0}p^{2}f_{0}(-\frac{\delta\beta}{\beta_{0}})\,(\ldots)=\beta^{-2}_{0}\,c_{V}(-\delta\beta)\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\,(\ldots)=\delta\epsilon\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\,(\ldots)

(30a)

\displaystyle\,\int\,\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}\,\beta_{0}p^{2}f_{0}\,\delta u^{i}\,(\ldots)=\frac{\delta T^{0i}}{w_{0}}\beta^{-2}_{0}\,c_{V}\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\,(\ldots)=\frac{\delta T^{0i}}{c^{2}_{s}}\,\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\,(\ldots)\,,

(30b)

Here,
d\Omega=d\phi\,d\cos\theta
denotes the integration over the solid angle in the phase space, and
(\ldots)
refer to any functions which only depend on
\hat{p}=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\cos\phi,\cos\theta)
.
The sound velocity is denoted by
c^{2}_{s}
and obeys the relation
c_{V}T/w=1/c^{2}_{s}
.
In eq. (
28
), functions
C_{n}
are defined by

\displaystyle C_{n}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\int^{1}_{-1}\,d\cos\theta\,\frac{i\cos^{n}\theta}{\bar{\omega}-\bar{k}\,\cos\theta+i}\,.

(31)

Solving eq. (
28
) for
\delta\epsilon,\delta\bm{u}
and using
c^{2}_{s}=1/3
yields
\delta\epsilon
induced by the initial energy disturbance

\displaystyle\,\delta\epsilon=\frac{-\left(-3C^{2}_{1}+3C_{0}C_{2}-C_{0}\right)\epsilon_{{\rm in}}(\bm{k})\tau_{R}}{-3C^{2}_{1}+3C_{0}C_{2}-C_{0}-3C_{2}+1}\,,

(32)

from which we read the response function

\displaystyle\,{\cal G}_{L}(\omega,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{-\left[6\bar{k}+\left(3(i+\bar{\omega})+i\bar{k}^{2}\right)L(\bar{\omega},\bar{k})\right]\,\tau_{R}}{2\bar{k}(\bar{k}^{2}+3i\bar{\omega})+i\left(\bar{k}^{2}+3\bar{\omega}(i+\bar{\omega})\,\right)L(\bar{\omega},\bar{k})}\,.

(33)

Here, for convenience, we have defined

\displaystyle L(\omega,k)=\ln\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}-\bar{k}+i}{\bar{\omega}+\bar{k}+i}\right)\,.

(34)

and used the relation among
C_{n}
s.

\displaystyle C_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{-i}{2\bar{k}}\,L(\bar{\omega},\bar{k})\,,\qquad C_{1}=\frac{-i}{\bar{k}}\,(1-(1-i\bar{\omega})C_{0})\,.

(35a)

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{k}}\,(i+\bar{\omega})\,C_{1}=\frac{(1-i\bar{\omega})}{\bar{k}^{2}}\,(1-(1-i\bar{\omega})C_{0})\,.

(35b)

Similarly, we find that in the transverse channel

\displaystyle\,{\cal G}_{T}(\omega,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{3i\left[2\bar{k}(\bar{\omega}+i)-\left(\bar{k}^{2}+(1-i\bar{\omega})^{2}\right)L(\bar{\omega},\bar{k})\right]\,\tau_{R}}{2\bar{k}(3+2\bar{k}^{2}-3i\bar{\omega})+3i\left(\bar{k}^{2}+(1-i\bar{\omega})^{2}\right)L(\bar{\omega},\bar{k})}\,.

(36)

The expression for the RTA retarded correlators has been obtained in Ref.
Romatschke:2015gic
, see also Ref.
Kurkela:2017xis
.
We copy the relevant expression in terms of
C_{0,1,2}
below (up to a contact term) in our notation:

\displaystyle\,G^{00,00}_{R}(\omega,k)=3w_{0}\,\frac{i\bar{k}C_{1}}{-3C^{2}_{1}+3C_{0}C_{2}-C_{0}-3C_{2}+1}\,,

(37)

\displaystyle\,G^{0x,0x}_{R}(\omega,k)=-3w_{0}\,\frac{i\bar{\omega}(C_{0}-C_{2})}{2-3(C_{0}-C_{2})}\,.

(38)

Using the expressions (
33
) and (
36
), one can verify the relation (
11
) between the response function and the retarded correlator.

2.3
Hydrodynamic response function

The primary goal of this paper is to investigate the response function’s behavior beyond the hydrodynamic regime.
To benchmark hydrodynamic behavior, we now calculate
{\cal G}_{L}
and
{\cal G}_{T}
within hydrodynamics and/or its cousin theory, Muller, Israel and Stewart (MIS) theory.
In the following section, we will compare these results with the RTA response.

We begin with the decomposition of EMT

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}=\epsilon u^{\mu}u^{\nu}+P\,\Delta^{\mu\nu}+\pi^{\mu\nu}

(39)

where
p
denotes the pressure, and the projector is given by

\displaystyle\Delta^{\mu\nu}=\eta^{\mu\nu}+u^{\mu}u^{\nu}\,,

(40)

and
\pi^{\mu\nu}
arise from the gradient correction to
T^{\mu\nu}
.
For a conformal fluid,
P=\epsilon/3
and
\pi^{\mu\nu}
is traceless.
One may expand
\pi^{\mu\nu}
in gradient in the long time and wavelength limit.
To the first-order gradient,

\pi^{\mu\nu}
takes the form

\displaystyle\pi^{\mu\nu}_{(1)}=-2\,\eta\,\sigma^{\mu\nu}

(41)

where
\eta
is the shear viscosity, and the shear stress is given by

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu\nu}=\nabla^{\langle\mu}\,u^{\nu\rangle}\,.

(42)

Here
\nabla^{\mu}
denotes the covariant derivative.
Angular brackets around a pair of Lorentz indices,
\,{}^{<\mu\nu>}
, mean that the indices are to be symmetrized, space-projected, and trace-subtracted, i.e.:

\displaystyle A^{<\mu\nu>}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{\mu\alpha}\Delta^{\mu\beta}\left(A_{\alpha\beta}+A_{\beta\alpha}\right)-\frac{1}{3}\Delta^{\mu\nu}\Delta^{\alpha\beta}\,A_{\alpha\beta}\,.

(43)

As usual,

One may include contributions from higher-order gradient terms

Various versions of higher-order theories have shared a feature that

For the theory developed by Muller, Israel and Stewart (MIS),

\displaystyle D\pi^{\mu\nu}=-\frac{1}{\tau_{\pi}}\left(\pi^{\mu\nu}-\pi^{\mu\nu}_{(1)}\right)\,,

We first consider the response function in the longitudinal channel obtained from linearizing hydrodynamic theories.
As we did earlier, we take

Therefore the relevant dynamical fields are

\displaystyle-i\omega\,\frac{\delta\epsilon}{w_{0}}

(46b)

\displaystyle-i\omega\frac{\pi^{zz}}{w_{0}}

3.1
Excitations

The behavior of the R.F. is determined by the properties of the medium’s excitations that can be recognized by studying its analytic structure (pole, branch-cut) in the complex
\omega
-plane.
In physical terms, poles indicate the collective modes and the branch-cuts are typically associated with quasi-particle excitations.
In the time domain,

\displaystyle{\cal G}(\Delta t,k)

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,{\cal G}(\omega,k)e^{-i\omega\Delta t}

(56)

we can close the contour of the integration over
\omega
in the lower half-plane to explicitly show the contributions of the pole and branch cut,

\displaystyle{\cal G}(\Delta t,k)

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{n}\mathcal{R}_{n}(k)e^{-i\omega_{n}\Delta t}+\sum_{m}\oint_{\rm\mathcal{C}_{m}}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}{\cal G}(\omega,k)e^{-i\omega\Delta t}\,,

(57)

where all the poles and branch cuts are labeled by
n
and
m
, respectively.
Here,
\mathcal{R}_{n}(k)
is the residue of the
{\cal G}(\omega,k)
at pole
\omega=\omega_{n}(k)
.
\mathcal{C}_{m}
schematically denote the contour around the branch-cut
m
.

Specifically, the RTA response function has poles given by the zeros of the denominator in eqs. (
33
),(
36

\displaystyle\,2\bar{k}\,(\bar{k}^{2}+3i\bar{\omega})+i\left(\bar{k}^{2}+3\bar{\omega}(i+\bar{\omega})L(\bar{\omega},\bar{k})\right)=0\,,

(58a)

\displaystyle\,2\bar{k}\,(3+2\bar{k}-3i\bar{\omega})+3i\left(\bar{k}^{2}+(1-i\bar{\omega})^{2}\right)L(\bar{\omega},\bar{k})=0\,,

(58b)

for the longitudinal and transverse channels, respectively.
In the longitudinal channel (
58a
),
the solutions present a pair of propagating modes (sound-like modes)
\omega_{L}(k)=\pm v(k)k-i\Gamma(k)
where
v
is the phase velocity and
\Gamma
is the attenuation rate.
In the transverse sector (
58b
),
the solution is purely imaginary
\omega_{T}=-i\Gamma_{T}(k)
.
In the regime where

is small,
RTA collective modes are usual hydrodynamic sound and shear modes (see also Fig.

Nevertheless, as first noticed in Ref.
Romatschke:2015gic

\bar{k}=\bar{k}_{O}

\bar{k}_{O}=4.52

We refer to those modes outside the hydrodynamic regime as high-frequency sound and shear modes for the clarity of presentation and for the reasons that will be revealed later in Sec.

3.1.1
Extended hydrodynamic regime: generalities

More generally, one may introduce the notion of
”Extended Hydrodynamic regime”
(EHR), as discussed recently in Ref.
Ke:2022tqf
.
EHR has two defining properties: 1) high-frequency sound and/or shear modes exist that are gapped from other non-hydrodynamic excitations; 2) the dispersion relation of high-frequency sound and shear mode can not be described by conventional hydrodynamic theory.
The RTA kinetic theory exemplifies the existence of EHR, as discussed in the preceding sections.

Importantly, EHR can appear in a wide range of microscopic theories.

First of all,
the authors of Ref.
Du:2023bwi
;
Ochsenfeld:2023wxz
demonstrate remarkable agreement among the retarded correlator in the sound channel for kinetic theory with different collision kernels once time and gradient are rescaled by the effective relaxation time
\eta/w
.
Those collision kernels include those obtained from RTA, scalar
\phi^{4}
,
SU(3)
Yang-Mills theory and QCD effective kinetic theory with
N_{f}=3
.
This study indicates that EHR, observed using the simplified RTA collision kernel, applies to more sophisticated and realistic collision kernels derived from quantum field theory.

Furthermore, high-frequency sound mode is not uncommon in strongly coupled gauge theories that can be calculated using holography duality.
For example, in the strongly coupled
{\cal N}=4
super-symmetric Yang-Mills theory in large
N_{c}
limit (SYM), which captures many features of QCD in strong coupling, the sound mode exists at any finite gradient and has a smaller than damping rate than other excitations (quasi-normal modes)
Amado:2008ji
;
Fuini:2016qsc
.
This similarity between kinetic theory and strongly coupled SYM theory is remarkable, given that the coupling strength is drastically different between the two.

Finally, various liquid metals can sustain sound modes extending from hydrodynamic regime to wavelengths comparable to inter-atomic distances, as reviewed in Refs.
RevModPhys.77.881
;
Trachenko_2015
.
All the results discussed above indicate the generality of the EHR scenario.

The construction of MIS
∗

We review MIS* theory, which we proposed in Ref.
Ke:2022tqf
.
The purpose of constructing MIS* is to provide a simple model that can describe the response in EHR.
Our approach is by ”trial and error” strategy: we explore the simplest extension of MIS and evaluate its effectiveness.
The resulting theory may be seen as a ”deformation” of MIS theory that we decompose the non-equilibrium corrections to the EMT,
\pi^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\pi^{\mu\nu}=\pi^{\mu\nu}_{1}+\pi^{\mu\nu}_{2}\,,

\displaystyle D\pi^{\mu\nu}_{a}=-\frac{1}{\tau_{a}}\left(\pi^{\mu\nu}_{a}+2\eta_{a}\,\sigma^{\mu\nu}\right)\,,\qquad a=1,2\,,

(61)

where
\eta_{1,2},\tau_{1,2}

Without losing generality, we shall assume that
\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}

\pi_{2}

\pi_{1}

In the long time limit,
\pi^{\mu\nu}
should approach to its value in the first-order hydrodynamics (

yielding the following constraint

\displaystyle\sum^{2}_{a=1}\eta_{a}=\eta\,.

(62)

We shall examine if MIS* can be used to describe EHR response in RTA kinetic theory.
Following a similar procedure as in Sec.

\displaystyle\nu(\omega)=\frac{4}{3}\sum^{2}_{a=1}\frac{\nu_{a}}{1-i\omega\tau_{a}}\,,\qquad\nu_{T}(\omega)=\frac{3}{4}\nu(\omega)\,.

where
\nu_{a}=\eta_{a}/w_{0}

To further simplify the model,

\displaystyle\nu(\omega)=\frac{4\nu_{0}}{3}\left(\frac{1-\delta}{1-i\omega\tau_{1}}+\delta\right)\,.

\displaystyle\delta=\frac{\eta_{2}}{\eta}\,,\qquad\gamma=\frac{\tau_{1}}{\tau_{\pi}}\,.

This limit means that even at non-hydrodynamic timescales like

3.4
Discussion on the extension of hydrodynamics

We developed the MIS* theory to describe how the medium responds in the extended hydrodynamic regime (EHR), where the high-frequency sound mode still has a longer lifetime than other excitations.
Our analysis above shows that this framework could describe high-frequency hydrodynamic mode in EHR (or at least part of it) with a minimum number of model parameters.
This is not the only possible formalism with which to describe EHR.
An alternative method is to include higher-order gradient terms in hydrodynamics, and the efficiency of doing so relies on the convergence of the gradient expansion, which has attracted many recent studies
Grozdanov:2019kge
;
Grozdanov:2019uhi
;
Heller:2020hnq
.
See also Ref.
Heller:2021yjh
for a different strategy in extending hydrodynamics.

In MIS*,
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
are introduced to describe sound dispersion outside the hydrodynamic regime.
While including non-hydrodynamic fields to extend hydrodynamic is not new and has been widely discussed in literature
Heller:2014wfa
;
Gavassino:2023odx
;
Gavassino:2023qwl
,
most of those studies are motivated by the expectation that the number of relevant modes increases at shorter time scales or non-hydrodynamic distances.
Moreover, the emergence of parametrically slow modes also necessitates including additional modes even in the hydrodynamic regime, and the result theory is referred to as ”Hydro+” in Ref.
Stephanov:2017ghc
.
One familiar situation is near a critical point; the relaxation time of the fluctuations of the order parameter field grows with increasing correlation length.
In all cases discussed above, the number of relevant modes is larger than that of hydrodynamic ones, which are conserved densities.
In contrast, the number of slow modes in EHR is the same as that of hydrodynamic ones in the sound channel.
The purpose of including non-hydrodynamic dynamic fields
\pi^{\mu\nu}_{1,2}
is not because they are additionally slow modes but because they bring about new parameters needed to characterize the dispersion of high-frequency sound modes.

5
5

5

In our view, to understand the need to introduce non-hydrodynamic d.o.fs.
\pi^{\mu\nu}_{1,2}
in MIS*, it might be useful to recall that some heavy particles are introduced to make neutrino light through the sea-saw mechanism in some beyond-standard model scenarios.

This distinguishes the MIS
∗
theory from early studies.

In this section, we will study the energy-momentum response around a Bjorken expanding conformal plasma and compare the results with those in the static background.

4.1
Response function for Bjorken background

We divide the stress-energy tensor (EMT) into a background and a perturbation part:

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}(\tau,\bm{x})=T^{\mu\nu}_{0}(\tau)+\delta T^{\mu\nu}(\tau,\bm{x})\,,

(66)

where we assume the background is under Bjorken expanding.
Accordingly, background EMT
T^{\mu\nu}_{0}(\tau)
only depends on the Bjorken proper time
\tau=\sqrt{t^{2}-z^{2}}
and takes form

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}_{0}={\rm diag}\left(\epsilon_{0},P_{0},P_{0},P_{0}\right)+{\rm diag}\left(0,1,1,-\frac{2}{\tau^{2}}\,\right)\,\pi_{0}\,.\,

(67)

Here, the first and second terms on the R.H.S. of eq. (
67
) represent the contribution from the ideal and non-equilibrium part of the constitutive relation, see eq. (
39
).
The background
\pi^{\mu\nu}_{0}
is diagonalized and is fully parameterized by
\pi_{0}=\pi^{xx}_{0}=\pi^{yy}_{0}
for a conformal system under consideration.

Following Ref.
Kurkela:2018vqr
, we define the response function describing the subsequent evolution of the initial perturbation
\delta T^{\mu\nu}
at
(\tau^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime}_{\perp})
(c.f. eq. (
1
)):

\displaystyle\delta T^{\mu\nu}(\tau,\bm{x}_{\perp})=\int d^{2}\bm{x}^{\prime}_{\perp}\,{\cal G}^{\mu\nu}_{\,\,\,\,\,\alpha\beta}(\bm{x}_{\perp}-\bm{x}^{\prime}_{\perp};\tau,\tau^{\prime})\,\delta T^{\alpha\beta}(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{\perp},\tau^{\prime})\,.

(68)

where we have used the fact that the response functions depend only on the different
\bm{r}=\bm{x}_{\perp}-\bm{x}^{\prime}_{\perp}
.
For the present purpose,
we only consider the perturbation that depends on the spatial vector lying entirely in the plane transverse to
z
-direction
\bm{x}_{\perp}
.
The non-zero independent components of
\delta T^{\mu\nu}
are

\displaystyle\delta\epsilon=\delta T^{\tau\tau}\,\qquad\delta g^{a}\equiv\delta T^{\tau a}\,,\qquad\delta T^{ab}\,,

(69)

where
a=x,y
and
\delta g^{a}
is nothing but transverse momentum density perturbation.
The value of
\delta T^{\eta\eta}
is related to
\delta\epsilon,\delta T^{ab}
by the conformality condition.
We are interested in the response of the plasma near equilibrium, so we shall implicitly assume that
\tau^{\prime}
is larger than the thermalization time of the plasma.
The response functions of a far-from-equilibrium Bjorken expanding plasma have been studied using effective QCD kinetic theory
Kurkela:2018vqr
;
Kurkela:2018wud
;
Du:2020zqg
and RTA kinetic theory
Kamata:2020mka
.

Following the discussion of Ref.
Kurkela:2018vqr
, one can show that there are five independent components of
{\cal G}^{\mu\nu}_{\,\,\,\,\,\alpha\beta}
that describe the energy-momentum response to the initial energy density
\delta\epsilon
and initial transverse momentum density
\delta g^{a}
disturbance (see also Ref.
Kamata:2020mka
):

\displaystyle\,{\cal G}^{\tau\tau}_{\,\,\,\,\,\tau\tau}(\bm{r})\equiv{\cal G}_{\epsilon\epsilon}(r)\,,

(70a)

\displaystyle\,{\cal G}^{\tau a}_{\,\,\,\,\,\tau\tau}(\bm{r})\equiv\hat{r}^{a}\,{\cal G}_{\epsilon L}(r)\qquad{\cal G}^{\tau\tau}_{\,\,\,\,\,\tau a}(\bm{r})\equiv\hat{r}^{a}\,{\cal G}_{L\epsilon}(r)\,,

(70b)

\displaystyle\,{\cal G}^{\tau a}_{\,\,\,\,\,\tau b}(\bm{r})=\hat{r}^{a}\hat{r}^{b}{\cal G}_{LL}(r)+\left(\delta^{ab}-\hat{r}^{a}\hat{r}^{b}\right){\cal G}_{TT}(r)\,.

(70c)

In the above expressions, we suppress the dependence on
\tau,\tau^{\prime}
in R.F. to highlight their dependence on
\bm{r}
.
The physical interpretation of those R.F. should be transparent.
For example,
{\cal G}_{\epsilon L}
describes energy density at
\tau
induced by the momentum density disturbance projected along direction
\hat{r}
at initial time
\tau^{\prime}
.
Likewise,

{\cal G}_{TT}
describes the transverse (with respect to
\hat{r}
) momentum density induced by the initial momentum density.

Following the common practice (see Refs
Kurkela:2018wud
;
Kamata:2020mka
;
Du:2020zqg
), we first compute
{\cal G}^{\mu\nu}_{\,\,\,\,\,\alpha\beta}
in Fourier space
\bm{k}=(k_{x},k_{y})
and obtain the real space R.F.

\displaystyle{\cal G}^{\mu\nu}_{\,\,\,\,\,\alpha\beta}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0};\tau,\tau_{0})=\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,{\cal G}^{\mu\nu}_{\,\,\,\,\,\alpha\beta}(\bm{k};\tau,\tau_{0})\,e^{i\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})}\,.

(71)

The relevant independent components of
G^{\mu\nu}_{\,\,\,\,\,\alpha\beta}(\bm{k};\tau,\tau_{0})
are
Kurkela:2018vqr

\displaystyle{\cal G}^{\tau\tau}_{\,\,\,\,\,\tau\tau}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{\epsilon\epsilon}(\bm{k})\,,

(72)

\displaystyle{\cal G}^{\tau\tau}_{\,\,\,\,\,\tau a}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\,\hat{\bm{k}}_{a}{\cal G}_{\epsilon L}(k)\,,\qquad{\cal G}^{\tau a}_{\,\,\,\,\,\tau\tau}(\bm{k})=i\,\hat{\bm{k}}_{a}\,{\cal G}_{L\epsilon}(k),

(73)

\displaystyle{\cal G}^{\tau a}_{\,\,\,\,\,\tau b}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{k}}^{a}\hat{\bm{k}}^{b}\,{\cal G}_{LL}(k)+(\delta^{ab}-\hat{\bm{k}}^{a}\hat{\bm{k}}^{b})\,{\cal G}_{TT}(k).

(74)

They determine the real-space response function through the relation:

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{\epsilon\epsilon}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{dkk}{2\pi}\,J_{0}(kr)\,{\cal G}_{\epsilon\epsilon}(k)\,,

(75a)

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{\epsilon L}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{dkk}{2\pi}\,J_{1}(kr){\cal G}_{\epsilon L}(k)\,,\qquad{\cal G}_{L\epsilon}(r)=\int\frac{dkk}{2\pi}\,J_{1}(kr){\cal G}_{L\epsilon}(k)\,,

(75b)

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{LL}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{dkk}{2\pi}\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(J_{0}(kr)-J_{2}(kr)\right)\,{\cal G}_{LL}(k)+\left(J_{0}(kr)+J_{2}(kr)\right){\cal G}_{TT}(k)\right]\,,

(75c)

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{TT}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{dkk}{2\pi}\frac{1}{2}\,\left[\left(J_{0}(kr)+J_{2}(kr)\right){\cal G}_{LL}(k)+\left(J_{0}(kr)-J_{2}(kr)\right){\cal G}_{TT}(k)\right]\,,

(75d)

where
J_{0,1,2}
are standard Bessel functions.

4.2
Kinetic theory calculation

In this section, we present the calculation of the response function for a Bjorken expanding plasma using RTA kinetic equation (
16
).
In Bjorken coordinate the kinetic equation (
16
) reads

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial\tau}+\frac{\mathbf{p}_{a}}{p}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{a}}-\frac{p^{\eta}}{\tau}\frac{\partial f}{\partial p^{\eta}}=-\frac{p_{\mu}u^{\nu}\,(f-f_{\rm eq})}{p\,\tau_{R}}

(76)

where the summation over the dummy variable
a
is understood.
As explained earlier, we consider massless particles with single-particle energy
p=|\bm{p}|
.

Since we are interested in energy-momentum disturbance, it is convenient to introduce the integrated distribution function (see also Refs.
Brewer:2019oha
;
Brewer:2022ifw
)

\displaystyle F(\hat{\bm{p}})=\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}\,p^{3}f\,,

(77)

from which one can obtain the components of EMT through the solid angle integration with the appropriate weight, e.g.,

\displaystyle\epsilon=\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\,F,\qquad g_{a}=\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{p_{a}}{p}\,F\,,\qquad T_{ab}=\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}(\frac{p_{a}p_{b}}{p})\,F\,.

(78)

By performing the integration over
dp
over the kinetic equation (
76
), we then derive the equation for
F
(see also Refs.
Brewer:2019oha
;
Kurkela:2019kip
;
Brewer:2022vkq
)

\displaystyle\left[\partial_{\tau}+\hat{p}_{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{a}}+\frac{1}{\tau}\left(4\cos^{2}\theta-\cos\theta(1-\cos^{2}\theta\right)\partial_{\cos\theta}\right]\,F=-\frac{1}{\tau_{R}}\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}\,p^{2}\,(p\cdot u)\,(f-f_{eq})\,.

(79)

We first consider background solution
F_{0}(\tau,\cos\theta)
with
u^{\mu}_{0}=(1,0,0,0)
(in Bjorken coordinate).
The equation (
79
) reduces to

\displaystyle\left[\partial_{\tau}+\frac{1}{\tau}\left(4\cos^{2}\theta-\cos\theta(1-\cos^{2}\theta\right)\partial_{\cos\theta}\right]F_{0}(\tau,\cos\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau_{R}}\left[\epsilon_{0}(\tau)-F_{0}(\tau,\cos\theta)\right]\,.

(80)

The R.H.S. of eq. (
80
) can be understood from the fact that in equilibrium,
F_{0}=\epsilon_{0}
and is independent of
\cos\theta
.

Next, we turn to the perturbation around the background solution
F=F_{0}+\delta F
.
Linearizing the L.H.S. of eq. (
79
) is straightforward.
For the R.H.S., we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\,\delta\left[\frac{p\cdot u}{\tau_{R}}\,(f-f_{\rm eq})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta f-\delta f_{\textrm{eq}}}{\tau_{R,0}}+\left(p\cdot\delta u-\frac{p^{0}\,\delta\tau_{R}}{\tau_{R}}\right)\,\frac{f_{0}-f_{\textrm{eq},0}}{\tau_{R,0}}\,.

(81)

Note
\delta\tau_{R}
term will be present because
\tau_{R}
depends on
\epsilon
(see eq. (
19
)).
For convenience, we shall count
f_{0}-f_{\rm eq,0}
of the order
{\cal O}(\delta_{0})
where
\delta_{0}
is assumed to be another small parameter as we are interested in the near equilibrium background.
In fact,
\pi_{0}/w_{0}\sim{\cal O}(\delta_{0})
in this counting scheme.
For a simple estimate of this ratio, we use the first-order hydrodynamic expectation
\pi_{0}/w_{0}=2\eta/(3\tau w)
.
Using
\eta/w=(1/5)\tau_{R}
in the RTA kinetic theory (
54
),
we find
\pi_{0}/w_{0}\leq 2/15
for
\tau>\tau_{R}
and is numerically small.
See also Fig.
5
where the numerical solutions to the background equation (
80
) is plotted and the smallness of
\pi_{0}/w_{0}
is verified.
To further simplify our analysis, we will ignore the terms of the order
{\cal O}(\delta_{0}\,\delta)
, i.e. the second term on the R.H.S of eq. (
81
).

To calculate the first term on the R.H.S of eq. (
81
), one can use an expression analogous to eq. (
21
) to expand
\delta f_{{\rm eq}}
.
On the other hand,

\displaystyle\delta T^{\tau a}=\delta g^{a}=w_{0}\delta u^{a}+\delta\pi^{\tau a}\,,

(82)

and
\pi^{\mu\nu}
is transverse to the fluid velocity

\displaystyle\delta u_{\mu}\pi^{\mu\nu}_{0}+u_{\mu}\delta\pi^{\mu\nu}_{0}=0\,,

(83)

so we find
\delta\pi^{\tau a}=\pi_{0}\delta u^{a}
and consequently

\displaystyle\delta u^{a}=\frac{g^{a}}{w_{0}(1-\frac{\pi_{0}}{w_{0}})}=\frac{g^{a}}{w_{0}}+{\cal O}(\delta\,\delta_{0})\,.

(84)

After integrating the linearized kinetic equation over
\int^{\infty}_{0}dp\,p^{2}
,
we obtain, in Fourier space, the equation for
\delta F
(see also Ref.
Brewer:2022vkq
)

\displaystyle\left[\partial_{\tau}+i\,k_{a}\,p_{a}+\frac{1}{\tau}\left(4\cos^{2}\theta-\cos\theta(1-\cos^{2}\theta\right)\partial_{\cos\theta}\right]\delta F

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau_{R}}\left(\delta\epsilon+\frac{1}{c^{2}_{s}}\delta g^{a}\cdot\hat{p}-\delta F\right)\,.

(85)

By solving eq. (
85
), we can obtain the response function through the definition in Sec.
4.1
.

4.3
Hydrodynamic theory calculation

As we did in the previous sections
2.3
,
we collectively call first-order hydrodynamics, MIS (or its variants), and MIS* ”hydrodynamic theories.”

We shall first derive E.o.M for background energy density
\epsilon_{0}
and linearized energy and momentum density
\delta\epsilon,\delta g^{a}
.
The resulting equations apply equally to all ”Hydrodynamic theories” under consideration.
In contrast, the description of
\pi^{\mu\nu}
differs among those theories and will be specified later.

We begin with the energy-momentum conservation equation

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}+\Gamma^{\mu}_{\mu\lambda}T^{\lambda\nu}+\Gamma^{\nu}_{\mu\lambda}T^{\mu\lambda}\,,

(86)

where the non-zero connection in Bjorken coordinates are
\Gamma^{\eta}_{\eta\eta}=\tau,\Gamma^{\eta}_{\eta\tau}=\Gamma^{\eta}_{\tau\eta}=1/\tau
.
Using the expression for the background EMT (
67
), we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\epsilon_{0}=-\frac{4}{3\tau}\epsilon_{0}+\frac{2\pi_{0}}{\tau}\ ,

(87)

Turning to the perturbation,
we have from (
86
) that

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\tau}+\frac{4}{3\tau}\right)\delta\epsilon+\partial_{a}\delta g^{a}-\tau\,\delta\pi^{\eta\eta}=0,

(88a)

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\tau}+\frac{1}{\tau}\right)\delta g^{a}+\frac{1}{3}\partial^{a}\delta\epsilon+\partial_{b}\delta\pi^{ba}=0,\,,

(88b)

where we have used eq. (
69
).

Now, we turn to discuss
\pi^{\mu\nu}_{0},\delta\pi^{\mu\nu}
for different ”hydrodynamic theories”.
As discussed earlier,
the background
\pi^{\mu\nu}_{0}
is fully determined by
\pi_{0}
.
To close eqs. (
88
),
only
\delta\pi^{ab}
are needed since
\delta\pi^{\eta\eta}
can be expressed in terms of
d\pi^{ab}
due to the conformality condition, i.e.,

\displaystyle\delta\pi^{\eta\eta}=-\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\sum_{a}\delta\pi^{aa}\,.

(89)

In the first-order hydrodynamics,
\pi^{\mu\nu}
is not dynamical and (
41
) yields

\displaystyle\pi_{0}=-2\eta_{0}\,\sigma^{\mu\nu}_{0}\,,\qquad\delta\pi^{\mu\nu}=-2\eta_{0}\delta\sigma^{\mu\nu}\,.

(90)

where for the background

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu\nu}_{0}=\frac{1}{3\tau}\textrm{diag}\{0,-1,-1,\frac{2}{\tau^{2}}\}\,.

(91)

Further using eq. (
84
), we arrived at the expression:

\displaystyle\delta\sigma^{ab}=\frac{1}{w_{0}}\,\partial^{\langle a}\,\delta g^{b\rangle}\,.

(92)

Following Ref.
Jaiswal:2019cju
,
we shall use a modern variant of MIS, i.e. the theory formulated by Denicol, Niemi, Molnar and Rischke (DNMR)
Denicol:2012cn
where the equations of motion for
\pi^{\mu\nu}
reads (see Ref.
Baier:2007ix
for examples of other variants of the second-order hydrodynamic theories ):

\displaystyle D\pi^{\mu\nu}=-\frac{\pi^{\mu\nu}+2\eta\sigma^{\mu\nu}}{\tau_{\pi}}-\frac{4}{3}\pi^{\mu\nu}\theta-\tilde{\lambda}_{1}\pi_{\alpha}^{\langle\mu}\sigma^{\nu\rangle\alpha}-\ldots\,.

(93)

Here,
\ldots
on the L.H.S of (
93
) denotes a term proportional to the vorticity, but we shall discard this contribution since the Bjorken flow is irrotational.
The second-order transport coefficient equals
\tilde{\lambda}_{1}=10/7
by matching to RTA kinetic theory
Jaiswal:2019cju
.

The DNMR equation for the background reads
Jaiswal:2019cju

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\pi_{0}=-\frac{1}{\tau_{\pi}}\,\left(\pi_{0}-\frac{2\eta}{3\tau}\right)-\left(\frac{4}{3}+\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{1}}{3}\right)\,\frac{\pi_{0}}{\tau}

(94)

and the linearized equation can be written as

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\delta\pi^{ab}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\delta\pi^{ab}+2\eta\delta\sigma^{ab}}{\tau_{\pi}}+\frac{4}{3\tau}\delta\pi^{ab}+{\cal O}(\delta_{0}\delta)\,.

(95)

Here and hereafter, we shall drop the terms of the order
{\cal O}(\delta_{0}\delta)
as we have already done in kinetic theory calculations.

Finally,
we discuss the MIS* theory introduced in Sec.
3.2
in the limit
\tau_{2}/\tau_{1}\to 0
such that

\pi^{\mu\mu}_{2}
(see eq. (
60
))
is given by

\displaystyle\pi^{\mu\nu}_{2}=-2\eta_{2}\sigma^{\mu\nu}\,.

(96)

For
\pi^{\mu\nu}_{1}
, instead of using (
61
), we consider the equation of the form (
93
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\pi^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi_{1}^{\mu\nu}+\pi_{2}^{\mu\nu},

(97)

\displaystyle D\pi^{\mu\nu}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\pi^{\mu\nu}+2\eta_{1}\sigma^{\mu\nu}}{\tau_{1}}-\frac{4}{3}\pi^{\mu\nu}_{1}\theta-\tilde{\lambda}_{1}\pi^{\langle\mu}_{\,\,\,\alpha}\sigma^{\nu\rangle\alpha}\,

(98)

so that when
\eta_{2}=0
, our theory reduces to eq. (
93
).
Consequently, the background and linearized equation for
\pi^{\mu\nu}_{1}
can be obtained by replacing
\pi_{0},\delta\pi^{ab},\eta
etc in eqs. (
94
), (
95
). with
(\pi_{1})_{0},\delta\pi^{ab}_{1},\eta_{1}
respectively.

in the first row of Fig.

) is the presence of a peak at a specific value of
v_{\textrm{eff}}=r/\Delta\tau
.
This value represents the effective velocity for the propagation of energy and momentum perturbation.
The real space results are given by summing the contribution from different
k
with an appropriate weight.
Therefore
v_{\textrm{eff}}
also tells us the average phase velocity of the sound.
As time passes,

v_{\textrm{eff}}
approaches the hydrodynamic limit
c_{s}=1/\sqrt{3}
at late times. However, even at
\Delta\tau=16\tau_{R}
,
v_{\textrm{eff}}
is still visually different from
c_{s}
.
Going back in time,
v_{\textrm{eff}}
is supersonic and becomes closer to
0.86
, the maximum value of the sound velocity in EHR (see Fig.
3
).
This indicates that unless
\Delta\tau
is very large,
the contribution from EHR is significant, and high-frequency sound mode plays a crucial role in early times.

Regarding the peak’s width, it measures the average sound attenuation rate, where a larger attenuation rate implies a broader peak.
We anticipate from Fig.

and now confirm here that first-order theory (MIS/DNMR) will underestimate (overestimate) the peak width.
In contrast, MIS* theory reasonably describes almost all the R.F. functions under consideration with a suitable model parameter

This confirms that MIS* can be utilized to describe the response in the EHR.

Finally, we note that

We have also checked that this peak is also in

This further makes us believe that describing non-hydrodynamic sound propagation is more important than that for the shear mode.

6
Non-hydrodynamic response from the Euclidean correlators

Figure 8:

The rescaled Euclidean energy-energy retarded correlator
G_{E}
computed using eqs. (
102
) and (
104
) at Euclidean time
\tau_{E}=1/(2T)
for four different values of
k
.
The filled squares show results obtained with the benchmark values of parameters in the parameterization of spectral density in the extended hydrodynamic regime,

In this section, we discuss the implications of the EHR scenario for extracting the real-time properties of QGP from Euclidean correlators, which are directly accessible from first-principle lattice QCD calculations.

We recall the relation between Euclidean correlator
G_{E}
and the spectral density
\rho
:

\displaystyle G_{E}(\tau_{E},k)=\int^{\infty}_{0}d\omega\rho(\omega,k)\,\frac{\cosh\omega\left(\tau_{E}-\frac{1}{2T}\right)}{\sinh\frac{\omega}{2T}}\,.

(102)

where
\tau_{E}
is the Euclidean time.
There have been extensive efforts in extracting the low-frequency behavior of spectral density from the lattice QCD calculation of
G_{E}
to determine transport coefficients such as shear viscosity non-perturbatively (see Ref.
Meyer:2011gj
for a review).
Nevertheless, since the characteristic frequency of the kernel that is convoluted with the spectral density in eq. (
102
) is of the order
\omega_{T}=\pi T
,
the resulting Euclidean correlators are notoriously insensitive to the features in hydrodynamic regimes where the typical gradient and frequency are expected to be smaller than
\omega_{T}
.

Here, we argue that the characteristic gradient/frequency in the conjectured EHR of QGP should be of the order
\omega_{T}
— a frequency domain to which the Euclidean correlator may still be sensitive.
To support our claim,
we consider the rescaled wave momentum
\tilde{k}=\frac{k}{(s/\eta)Tc^{2}_{s}}
.
While
\eta/s
can be different even by order of magnitude for different microscopic theories,
we hope that the dimensionless value of the characteristic
\tilde{k}
in EHR,
\tilde{k}_{{\rm EHR}}
, is of the same order in those theories.
In fact,
the study of Ref.
Du:2023bwi
indeed indicates that the retarded correlator expressed in terms of
\tilde{k}
is much less sensitive to the microscopic details.
For RTA kinetic theory, we can use eq. (
54
) and read from Fig.
3
that
\frac{9}{16}<\tilde{k}<\frac{9}{4}
may be considered as the domain of EHR.
In Ref.
Hong:2010at
, the spectral density of EMT has been calculated using the leading log Boltzmann equation for the QCD plasma.
In sound channel, the authors observe that while hydrodynamics ceases to be a good description for
\tilde{k}>0.7
,
the peak associated with high-frequency sound (in the view of the present paper) in the spectral density persists up to
\tilde{k}\sim 5.6
(see Fig. 2 of Ref.
Hong:2010at
).
On the other hand, for the SYM correlator obtained from the AdS/CFT method, the EHR exists for
\tilde{k}>1
.
Combining all the information above,
it is reasonable to assume that for QGP,
\tilde{k}_{{\rm EHR}}=1\text{--}2
, or

\displaystyle k_{{\rm EHR}}=(1\text{--}2)\,\left(\frac{\eta}{s}\right)^{-1}\,c^{2}_{s}\,T\,.

(103)

Taking
\eta/s=2\times(1/4\pi)
as a crude estimate for QGP gives
k_{{\rm EHR}}\sim\pi T
.

We now investigate the sensitivity of the Euclidean correlator
G_{E}
to the properties of EHR.
For definiteness, we consider the energy-energy Euclidean correlator so that spectral density is given by
\rho=-{\rm Im}G^{00,00}_{R}/\pi
.
Motivated by Refs.
Meyer:2008gt
;
Borsanyi:2018srz
,
we assume a very simple EHR plus tree-level ansatz for the spectral function

\displaystyle\rho(\omega,k)=\rho_{{\rm EHR}}(\omega,k)\theta(k-\omega)+\rho_{{\rm tree}}(\omega,k)\theta(\omega-k)\,,

(104)

Here,
\rho_{{\rm EHR}}
can be obtained by computing
G_{R}
from the MIS* response function through the relation (
10
).
We find

\displaystyle\rho_{{\rm EHR}}(\omega,k)=\frac{-1}{\pi}{\rm}{\rm Im}\,\left[\frac{wk^{2}}{\omega^{2}-c^{2}_{s}k^{2}+i\nu(\omega)\omega k^{2}}\right]\,,

(105)

where we shall use the expression of
\nu(\omega)
in MIS* (
64
), which we copy below for convenience

\displaystyle\nu(\omega)=\frac{4\nu_{0}}{3}\left(\frac{1-\delta}{1-i\omega\tau_{1}}+\delta\right)\,.

(106)

In the estimation below, we always use
w=\frac{3}{4}w_{SB}=\frac{1}{15}d_{A}\pi^{2}T^{4}
with
d_{A}=N^{2}_{c}-1
and
w_{SB}
denotes the enthalpy density of a gluon plasma in Stefan-Boltzmann limit.
The tree-level spectral function of a gluon plasma is taken from Ref.
Meyer:2008gt
:

\displaystyle\rho_{{\rm tree}}(\omega,k)\theta(\omega-k)=\frac{d_{A}k^{4}}{4(4\pi)^{2}}\,\int^{1}_{0}dz\,\frac{(1-z^{2})^{2}\sinh(\frac{\omega}{2T})}{\cosh(\frac{\omega}{2T}-\cosh{\frac{kz}{2T}})}\,,

(107)

We have calculated the rescaled Euclidean correlator
\tilde{G}_{E}\equiv G_{E}/(d_{A}T^{5})
from eq. (
104
) for
k=(1/2,1,2,4)\pi T
at
\tau_{E}=\frac{1}{2T}
.
The results will depend on parameters entering eq. (
106
), i.e.
\nu_{0}
(or
\eta/s
) and MIS* parameters
\tau_{1},\delta
.
We first perform the computation with the benchmark values
(\eta/s,\tau_{1},\delta)=(1/2\pi,4\eta/(sT),0.1)
.
Note that for SYM,
T\tau_{\pi}=2.61(\eta/s)
while a next leading order QCD calculation show
T\tau_{\pi}\approx 6.5(\eta/s)

Ghiglieri:2018dgf
.
So we pick up a somewhat arbitrary value in between for
\tau_{1}
assuming
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{\pi}
should be of the same order.
The value of
\delta
is inspired by the comparison between high-frequency sound dispersion in MIS* theory and that in RTA kinetic theory and SYM theory
Ke:2022tqf
.
Then we do the same but increase the value of one of the parameters by a factor of two to explore the sensitivity of
G_{E}
to those parameters.

The results shown in Fig.
8
are suggestive and encouraging.
First, it confirms that at small
k
, the Euclidean correlator is insensitive to the value of the transport coefficient and MIS* parameters.
For example, for
k=\frac{\pi T}{2}
which might be considered as a gradient in the hydrodynamic regime,
a factor of two difference in
\eta/s
only results in a few percent difference in
G_{E}
.
On the other hand,
as
k
increases, we observe sizable difference in
G_{E}
with varying MIS* parameters
\tau_{1},\delta
.
Furthermore, since the energy-energy response in EHR is determined by MIS* parameters together with
\eta/s
,
we also observe the sensitivity of
G_{E}
to the value of shear viscosity at a non-hydrodynamic gradient.
Those results suggest that by studying Euclidean correlators, we may be able to test the EHR scenario for QGP and could potentially extract both shear viscosity and parameters that characterize the medium’s response in EHR.

7
Summary and outlook

We have studied the response of a QGP-like plasma to initial energy-momentum disturbance at a gradient outside the hydrodynamic regime.
By comparing RTA kinetic theories results with those obtained from ”hydrodynamic theories,”
we demonstrate a naive extrapolation of the first-order and second-order (or MIS/DNMR theory) outside hydrodynamic regime that fails to describe the response.
Nevertheless, high-frequency sound modes might dominate the response at a non-hydrodynamic gradient.
Under this extended hydrodynamic regime scenario, the description of the response can still be simplified significantly.
Notably, we observe the significant improvement achieved by MIS* that we propose.

We have also compared the energy-momentum response function at static and Bjorken-expanding.
We learned that at a non-hydrodynamic gradient, the response as a function of proper time is not very sensitive to the bulk background profile.
This indicates that one may use the response functions obtained in the static background to estimate that in the expanding bulk profile, allowing for the simplification in the numerical studies.
We also learned that although hydrodynamics would eventually describe those response functions, the ”hydrodynamization time” of the response function would be numerically large.
This, in turn, suggests that jet-medium observables might be employed to extract the medium’s properties outside the hydrodynamic regime, and MIS* theory, or its extension, can be applied within this context.

We also hope the exploratory study presented in Sec.
6
would arouse interest in extracting the medium’s behavior at a non-hydrodynamic gradient from the lattice Euclidean correlator.
The latter should be sensitive to the response at the gradient of order
\pi T
.
This is precisely the key missing gap in our understanding of QGP’s behavior.
