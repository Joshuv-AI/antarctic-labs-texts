1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a finite group and
\operatorname{K}(G):=\{[a,b]\mid a,b\in G\}
. It is well known that the commutator subgroup
\gamma_{2}(G)
of
G
is generated by
\operatorname{K}(G)
. A natural question that has attracted the attention of many mathematicians over last many many decades is whether
\gamma_{2}(G)
is equal to
\operatorname{K}(G)
or not for groups
G
in a given class of groups. In 1899, G.A. Miller
[
19
]
proved that
\operatorname{K}(G)=\gamma_{2}(G)
for all alternating groups
G=A_{n}
,
n\geq 5
. This result was reinvented by N. Ito
[
12
]
and O. Ore
[
20
]
in 1951, and Ore conjectured that the same is true for all finite simple groups. With the efforts of several people over the years, the conjecture was proved in 2010
[
17
]
. It is also true that
\operatorname{K}(G)=\gamma_{2}(G)
for most of finite quasi-simple groups
[
18
]
. Furthermore, the commutator length of all finite quasi-simple groups is at most
2
. The commutator length of a finite group
G
is the smallest positive integer
m
such that every element of
\gamma_{2}(G)
can be written as a product of at most
m
commutators in
G
.

Interest in the above question for non-perfect groups also goes back to 1902 when W.B. Fite
[
6
]
proved that if a finite
p
-group
G
of nilpotency class
2
is such that
G/\operatorname{Z}(G)
is minimally generated by three elements, then
\operatorname{K}(G)=\gamma_{2}(G)
. He also presented examples of
2
-groups
G
of nilpotency class
2
such that
G/\operatorname{Z}(G)
is minimally generated by four elements and
\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)
. The smallest such
2
-group is of order
2^{8}
. There do exist such groups of order
p^{8}
for all odd primes
[
16
, Theorem A]
. In 1953, K. Honda
[
10
]
re-invented, in a slightly different form, the famous character sum formula of F.G. Frobenius for determining whether a given element of a finite group is a commutator or not. This result was used to prove that if, for a finite group
G
,
\gamma_{2}(G)
is generated by a commutator, then each generator of
\gamma_{2}(G)
is a commutator. D.M. Rodney
[
21
,
22
]
proved that if a nilpotent group
G
has cyclic commutator subgroup, or if
G
is finite and
\gamma_{2}(G)
is elementary abelian of order
p^{3}
for any prime integer
p
, then
\operatorname{K}(G)=\gamma_{2}(G)
. This result was further generalised by R. Guralnick
[
7
]
, who proved that if
G
is a finite group whose
\gamma_{2}(G)
is an abelian
p
-group minimally generated by at most
3
elements, where
p\geq 5
, then
\operatorname{K}(G)=\gamma_{2}(G)
.

Coming to
p
-groups, Fernández-Alcober and De las Heras
[
4
]
proved that
\operatorname{K}(G)=\gamma_{2}(G)
for finite
p
-group
G
such that
\gamma_{2}(G)
is generated by
2
elements. This result was further extended by De las Heras
[
9
]
for finite
p
-groups
G
,
p\geq 5
, such that
\gamma_{2}(G)
is generated by
3
elements. No more generalisation is possible in terms of the rank of the commutator subgroup of finite
p
-groups. Kappe and Morse
[
14
]
, constructed examples of group
G
of order
p^{6}
,
p
odd, such that
\gamma_{2}(G)
is elementary of order
p^{4}
, but
\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)
. In the same paper, they proved that
\operatorname{K}(G)=\gamma_{2}(G)
for all
p
-groups of order at most
p^{5}
and for all
2
-groups of order at most
2^{6}
. They also exhibited groups of order
2^{7}
for which
\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)
. Classification of finite
p
-groups
G
(upto isoclinism) with
\gamma_{2}(G)
of order
p^{4}
and exponent
p
such that
\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)
is provided in
[
16
]
by the present authors. As a result, a clear characterisation of groups
G
of order
p^{6}
such that
\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)
now exists. These are the groups which fall in the isoclinism class
\Phi(23)
. As classified in
[
13
]
, there are total
43
isoclinism classes of groups of order
p^{6}
,
p
odd prime. A detailed historical survey on the above mentioned question is available in
[
15
]
.

Apart from these results, to the best of our knowledge, nothing is known about finite
p
-groups
G
with
\operatorname{K}(G)=\gamma_{2}(G)
. And the theory developed so far seems revealing no patterns. So it is highly desirable to investigate more classes of finite
p
-groups. In this article we take up this investigation for groups of order
p^{7}
, and prove the following result.

Theorem A
.

Let
G
be a group of order
p^{7}
,
p\geq 5
. Then the following statements hold:

(1)
If
d(\gamma_{2}(G))\leq 3
or the nilpotency class of
G
is
6
, then
\operatorname{K}(G)=\gamma_{2}(G)
.

(2)
If
|\gamma_{2}(G)|=p^{4}
and
d(\gamma_{2}(G))=4
, then
\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)
if and only if the nilpotency class of
G
is either
3
or
4
and
|\operatorname{Z}(G)|=p^{3}
.

(3)
If the nilpotency class of
G
is
5
,
|\gamma_{2}(G)|=p^{5}
,
d(\gamma_{2}(G))=4
and
\exp(\gamma_{2}(G))=p^{2}
, then
\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)
.

(4)
If the nilpotency class of
G
is
4
,
|\gamma_{2}(G)|=p^{5}
,
d(\gamma_{2}(G))\geq 4
, then
\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)
if and only if there exists a subgroup
H\leq Z(G)
of order
p
such that
|Z(G/H)|=p^{2}
.

(5)
If the nilpotency class of
G
is
5
and
\gamma_{2}(G)
is elementary of order
p^{5}
, then
\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)
.

(6)
If the nilpotency class of
G
is
5
and
\gamma_{2}(G)
is non-abelian of order
p^{5}
and exponent
p
, then
\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)
if and only if
|\gamma_{5}(G)|=p
and
|\operatorname{Z}_{2}(G)|=p^{3}
.

Moreover, the commutator length of
G
is at most
2
.

While proving this theorem, we obtain many interesting structural results on groups of order
p^{7}
.
We use GAP
[
3
]
for handing the remaining cases of

, that is,

and

, and derive the following conclusion.

.

For a group
G
of order

,
\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)

is of nilpotency class

,
\gamma_{2}(G)
is a

-generated abelian subgroup of order

.

Let

be a group of order

. Then

if and only if one of the following holds:

(i)
The nilpotency class of

is
5
,
\gamma_{2}(G)
is a
3
-generated non-abelian subgroup,
|\gamma_{5}(G)|=3
and
|\operatorname{Z}_{2}(G)|=3^{3}
.

(ii)
The nilpotency class of
G
is
5
,
\gamma_{2}(G)
is a
3
-generated abelian subgroup of order
3^{5}
and exponent
3^{2}
and
|\operatorname{Z}(G)|\leq 3^{2}
.

(iii)
The nilpotency class of
G
is
4
,
\gamma_{2}(G)
is a
3
-generated abelian subgroup of order
3^{4}
and
|\operatorname{Z}(G)|=3^{3}
.

(iv)
The nilpotency class of

is

,
\gamma_{2}(G)
is a

-generated abelian subgroup of exponent

.

(v)
The nilpotency class of

is

,
\gamma_{2}(G)
is a
4
-generated elementary abelian subgroup and
|\operatorname{Z}(G)|=3^{3}
.

Moreover, the commutator length of
G
is at most
2
.

Let
1\to H\to E\to G\to 1
be a group extension. Notice that if
\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)
, then
\operatorname{K}(E)\neq\gamma_{2}(E)
(see Lemma
2.5
). But the converse is not true in general. Indeed, there do exist groups
E
such that
\operatorname{K}(E)\neq\gamma_{2}(E)
, but all proper quotient groups

of

. All groups of order

from

are such. It follows that the groups

of nilpotency class

occurring in Theorem A (2) are also such groups. But, as it follows from the results below, all other groups

of order

with

are central extensions of groups of order

(from the isoclinism class

) by a subgroup of order

:

\operatorname{K}(G)=\gamma_{2}(G)
if and only if
\operatorname{Z}(G/N)\cap\gamma_{2}(G/N)\subseteq\operatorname{K}(G/N)
for all normal subgroups

of

(including the trivial one)
.

2.
Prelamanaries

The following concept of isoclinism of groups is due to P. Hall
[
8
]
.
Let
X
be a group and
\bar{X}:=X/\operatorname{Z}(X)
. Then commutation in
X
gives a well defined map
a_{X}:\bar{X}\times\bar{X}\mapsto\gamma_{2}(X)
such that

a_{X}(x\operatorname{Z}(X),y\operatorname{Z}(X))=[x,y]
for
(x,y)\in X\times X
. Two groups
G
and
H
are called
isoclinic
if there exists an isomorphism
\phi
of the factor group

\bar{G}=G/\operatorname{Z}(G)
onto
\bar{H}=H/\operatorname{Z}(H)
, and an isomorphism
\theta
of the subgroup
\gamma_{2}(G)
onto
\gamma_{2}(H)
such that the following diagram is commutative

\begin{CD}\bar{G}\times\bar{G}@>{a_{G}}>{}>\gamma_{2}(G)\\
@V{\phi\times\phi}V{}V@V{}V{\theta}V\\
\bar{H}\times\bar{H}@>{a_{H}}>{}>\gamma_{2}(H).\end{CD}

The resulting pair
(\phi,\theta)
is called an
isoclinism
of
G
onto
H
. Notice that isoclinism is an equivalence relation among groups. The equivalences classes under isoclinism are called
isoclinism classes
. The following result follows from
[
8
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

In each isoclinism class of groups there exists a group
G
such that
\operatorname{Z}(G)\leq\gamma_{2}(G)
.

The following three results are from
[
16
, Lemma 2.2, Lemma 2.5, Lemma 4.1]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G
and
H
be two isoclinic finite
p
-groups. Then
\operatorname{K}(G)=\gamma_{2}(G)
if and only if
\operatorname{K}(H)=\gamma_{2}(H)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
G
be a finite group and
H\leq\gamma_{2}(G)\cap Z(G)
. If there exist
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
such that
\gamma_{2}(G)/H=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{n}[x_{i}H,G/H]
and
H\subseteq\bigcap\limits_{i=1}^{n}[x_{i},G]
, then
\gamma_{2}(G)=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{n}[x_{i},G]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G
be a group of order
p^{6}
,
p\geq 3
with
\gamma_{2}(G)
elementary abelian of order
p^{4}
and
|\operatorname{Z}(G)|=p^{2}
. Then
\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)
.

We now mention very elementary but extremely useful observations.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
G
be a finite group and
H
a normal subgroup of
G
. If
\operatorname{K}(G/H)\neq\gamma_{2}(G/H)
, then
\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)
.

Proof.

Let
\bar{G}=G/H
, and
\bar{x}\in\gamma_{2}(\bar{G})
be an element which is not in
\operatorname{K}(\bar{G})
. Then its pre-image
x\notin\operatorname{K}(G)
. Hence
\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)
.

\Box

∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
G
be a group and
H
a normal subgroup of
G
contained in
\operatorname{K}(G)
such that
\operatorname{K}(G/H)=\gamma_{2}(G/H)
. Then the commutator length of
G
is at most
2
.

The following basic identities will be used throughout, mostly, without any further reference

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
x,y,z
be elements of a group
G
and
n
be a positive integer. Then

(i)
[x,yz]=[x,z][x,y]^{z}
,

(ii)
[xy,z]=[x,z]^{y}[y,z]

(iii)

.

Let

be a

is

. Then
\operatorname{C}_{i}:=\operatorname{C}_{G}(\gamma_{i}(G)/\gamma_{i+2}(G))
are called

. It is clear that

]

Let

\operatorname{K}(G)=\gamma_{2}(G)
. More precisely,
\gamma_{2}(G)=[s,G]

We now mention some number theoretic results. For a prime integer
p
and
d\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
, the field of
p

(\frac{d}{p})
, is defined as

\left(\frac{d}{p}\right)=\begin{cases}0&\ \ \ \ \ \ d=0,\\
1&\ \ \ \ \ \text{ d is quadratic residue}\pmod{p},\\
-1&\ \ \ \ \ \ \text{d is non-quadratic residue}\pmod{p}.\end{cases}

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and
f(\lambda,\mu)=a\lambda^{2}+b\lambda\mu+c\mu^{2}
be a binary quadratic form over
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
. If
p
divides

, then
f=0
has a nontrivial solution in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
\mu
be a fixed element of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
. Then
f=a\lambda^{2}+b\lambda\mu+c\mu^{2}
will be a quadratic equation in
\lambda
, whose discriminant is
(b^{2}-4ac)\mu^{2}
is zero in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Thus
\lambda=-b\mu/2a
is a nontrivial solution of
f=0
.
\Box

∎

We say that a binary quadratic form
f(\lambda,\mu)=a\lambda^{2}+b\lambda\mu+c\mu^{2}
represents an integer

, if there exist some integers
\lambda_{0},\mu_{0}
such that
f(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0})=r
. The following theorem and its corollary are being proved in
[
23
, Chapter 3, Page 153]
.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
n
and
d
be given integers with
n\neq 0
. Then there exist a binary quadratic form of discriminant
d
that represents
n
if and only if the congruence
x^{2}\equiv d\pmod{4|n|}
has a solution.

Corollary 2.13
.

Let
d=0
or
1\pmod{4}
, and
p
be an odd prime. Then there exists a binary quadratic form of discriminant
d
that represents
p
if and only if
(\frac{d}{p})=1
.

Lemma 2.14
.

For given
m,n\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
, let
f(\lambda,\mu)=m\lambda^{2}+n\lambda\mu-\mu^{2}
and
g(\lambda,\mu)=\lambda^{2}+n\lambda\mu-m\mu^{2}
, be two quadratic forms, where
p
is an odd prime. Then
f(\lambda,\mu)=0
admits only a trivial solution in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
if and only if
g(\lambda,\mu)=0
does the same.

Proof.

Assume that
f(\lambda,\mu)=0
implies
\lambda=\mu=0
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Let
f
represent
p
; meaning there exist
\lambda_{0},\mu_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
f(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0})=p
. If both
\lambda_{0}
and
\mu_{0}
are congruent to zero modulo
p
, then
p^{2}
divides
p
(=
m\lambda_{0}^{2}+n\lambda_{0}\mu_{0}-\mu_{0}^{2}
), which is not possible. Hence, it follows that none of
\lambda_{0},\mu_{0}
is a multiple of
p
. We can then find
a_{0},b_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
such that
f(a_{0},b_{0})\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, where
\lambda_{0}\equiv a_{0}\pmod{p}
and
\mu_{0}\equiv b_{0}\pmod{p}
. This contradicts our assumption. Thus
f
does not represent
p
. By our assumption and Lemma
2.11
,
n^{2}+4m\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
, and since
n^{2}+4m\equiv 0,1\pmod{4}
, it follows from Corollary
2.13
that
\big(\frac{n^{2}+4m}{p}\big)=-1
. Hence
x^{2}\equiv n^{2}+4m\pmod{p}
does not have a solution. Now let
g(\lambda,\mu)=0
has a non-trivial solution. Thus
g
represents
ps
for some
s\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
, hence by Theorem
2.12
it follows that
x^{2}\equiv n^{2}+4m\pmod{4ps}
has a solution. Thus
x^{2}\equiv n^{2}+4m\pmod{p}
must have a solution, which is not possible. Hence
g(\lambda,\mu)=0
has only trivial solution. Converse also follows on the same line.

\Box

∎

3.
Groups of nilpotency class 5

In this section we deal with groups
G
of order
p^{7}
and nilpotency class
5
with
d(\gamma_{2}(G))\geq 4
. As we’ll see, for most of these groups
G
,
\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be a group of order
p^{7}
,
p\geq 5
, and nilpotency class
5
such that
\gamma_{2}(G)
is a
4
-generated subgroup of order
p^{5}
and exponent
p^{2}
. Then the following statements hold:

(i)
|\gamma_{5}(G)|=p
,

(ii)
|\operatorname{Z}(G/\gamma_{5}(G))|=p^{2}
and
|\operatorname{Z}_{2}(G)|=p^{3}
,

(iii)
\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)
,

(iv) Commutator length of
G
is
2
.

Proof.

Notice that
|\gamma_{5}(G)|\leq p^{2}
. For the first assertion, contrarily assume that the order of
\gamma_{5}(G)
is
p^{2}
. Thus
\gamma_{5}(G)=\operatorname{Z}(G)
and
\gamma_{i}(G)/\gamma_{i+1}(G)
is of order
p
for every
i\in\{2,3,4\}
. By the given hypothesis we can assume that
G
is generated by
2
elements, say
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
. Recall that
\beta=[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]
. If
\beta^{p}=1
, then,
[x,\beta,\beta]
being in
\gamma_{5}(G)\leq\operatorname{Z}(G)
and
p
being at least
5
, for any
x\in G
we have

1=[x,\beta^{p}]=[x,\beta]^{p}.

Since
\gamma_{3}(G)
is abelian, it now follows that the exponent of
\gamma_{3}(G)
is
p
; hence, being
[\gamma_{2}(G),\gamma_{3}(G)]
central, the exponent of
\gamma_{2}(G)
is also
p
, which contradicts the given hypothesis. Hence
\beta^{p}\neq 1
. Since
d(\gamma_{2}(G))=4
, it follows that
|\Phi(\gamma_{2}(G))|=p
, which further implies that
\Phi(\gamma_{2}(G))\leq\gamma_{5}(G)
. Using the fact that
p\geq 5

p
. Let

2

2.4
and Lemma
2.5

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

elementary abelian of order

Proof.

We can assume

\beta
,
\beta_{i}

\gamma_{2}(G)/\gamma_{3}(G)=\left<\beta\gamma_{3}(G)\right>

\gamma_{3}(G)/\gamma_{4}(G)=\left<\beta_{1}\gamma_{4}(G),\beta_{2}\gamma_{4}(G)\right>

\eta_{22}=\eta_{21}=1

; not possible. So let

, we get
\eta_{11}={\eta_{12}}^{-ji^{-1}}={\eta_{22}}^{j^{2}i^{-2}}
, which implies that the order
\gamma_{4}(G)
is at most
p
; not possible. The proof is now complete.
\Box

∎

First assume that

.

. If

[

. So assume that

. Thus

. Notice that, in this case, the nilpotency class of

is at least
4
. Obviously,

is either
2
or

. If

, then there exists a minimal generating set

such that

2.1
, there exists a

of order

such that

and

are isoclinic and
\operatorname{Z}(M)\leq\gamma_{2}(M)
. Also

. It now follows from
[

that
\operatorname{K}(M)\neq\gamma_{2}(M)
if and only if the nilpotency class of

is
4
and
\operatorname{Z}(M)
is of order

. This, using Lemma
2.2

\operatorname{K}(G)\neq\gamma_{2}(G)
if and only if the nilpotency class of

is
4
and
|\operatorname{Z}(G)|=p^{3}

We now assume that

. We can then choose a minimal generating set

for

such that

. By the given hypothesis

. Let

. Then the nilpotency class of

and

are equal,

. Notice that

and

is of order

. One can now easily conclude that

∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem A.

Proof of Theorem A.
If
d(\gamma_{2}(G))\leq 3
, then
\operatorname{K}(G)=\gamma_{2}(G)
by
[

. Also when the nilpotency class of

5.1
. The only case which remains is
