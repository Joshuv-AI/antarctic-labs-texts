Topology of augmented Bergman complexes
Elisabeth Bullock, Aidan Kelley, Victor Reiner, Kevin Ren, Gahl Shemy, Dawei Shen, Brian Sun, Amy Tao, Zhichun Joy Zhang

Abstract
. The augmented Bergman complex of a matroid is a simplicial complex introduced recently in work of Braden, Huh, Matherne, Proudfoot and Wang. It may be viewed as a hybrid of two well-studied pure shellable simplicial complexes associated to matroids: the independent set complex and Bergman complex. It is shown here that the augmented Bergman complex is also shellable, via two different families of shelling orders. Furthermore, comparing the description of its homotopy type induced from the two shellings re-interprets a known convolution formula counting bases of the matroid. The representation of the automorphism group of the matroid on the homology of the augmented Bergman complex turns out to have a surprisingly simple description. This last fact is generalized to closures beyond those coming from a matroid.

Introduction

Matroids

2.
Background

2.1.
Matroids

We begin with two axiomatizations of matroids;
see Oxley
[
11
, Chap. 1]
for other axioms.

Definition 2.1
.

(
Matroids defined by independent sets
)

A
matroid

M
on ground set
E
is a collection

\mathcal{I}(M)\subseteq 2^{E}
, called its

independent sets
, satisfying axioms:

I1.

\varnothing\in\mathcal{I}(M)
.

I2.

I\subseteq J
and
J\in\mathcal{I}(M)
implies
I\in\mathcal{I}(M)
.

I3.

If
I,J\in\mathcal{I}(M)
and
\#I<\#J
, then there exists
j\in J\setminus I
with

I\cup\{j\}\in\mathcal{I}(M)
.

From Axioms
I1, I2
, one sees that the collection
\mathcal{I}(M)
forms an abstract simplicial complex on vertex set
E
. Axiom
I3
shows that it
is pure of dimension
r-1
where
r=r(M)

is the cardinality of all maximal independent sets
B
, called the
bases

\mathcal{B}(M)
.

Equivalently, one can define a matroid via flats.

Definition 2.2
.

(
Matroids defined by flats
)

A
matroid

M
on ground set
E
is a collection

\mathcal{F}(M)\subseteq 2^{E}
, called its

flats
, satisfying axioms:

F1.

E\in\mathcal{F}(M)
.

F2.

F,G\in\mathcal{F}(M)
implies
F\cap G\in\mathcal{F}(M)
.

F3.

For every
F\in\mathcal{F}(M)
and

e\in E\setminus F
, there exists a unique
G\in\mathcal{F}(M)
containing
e
that
covers

F
in this sense: there does not exist
H\in\mathcal{F}(M)

with
F\subsetneq H\subsetneq G
.

Assume from here on that
E
is
finite
. Then
the flats
\mathcal{F}(M)
let one define the
matroid closure
operator

(4)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}2^{E}&\longrightarrow&2^{E}\\
A&\longrightarrow&\overline{A}\end{array}

where
\overline{A}
is the smallest flat containing
A
, namely

\overline{A}:=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}(M):\\
F\supseteq A\end{subarray}}F.

We will also wish to view
\mathcal{F}(M)
as a partially ordered set (
poset
) via inclusion, with unique bottom element
\overline{\varnothing}
and top element
E
. In fact,
\mathcal{F}(M)
is a
ranked lattice
, with
rank function
r:\mathcal{F}(M)\rightarrow\{0,1,2,\ldots,r\}
satisfying
r(\overline{\varnothing})=0
,
r(E)=r
, and

r(G)=r(F)+1
if there is no
flat
H
satisfying
F\subsetneq H\subsetneq G
.

Passing between the independent sets and flats of a matroid
M
is not hard. First, given any subset
A\subseteq E
, one can define its

rank function

r(A)
either using
\mathcal{I}(M)
or
\mathcal{F}(M)
as follows:

\displaystyle r(A)

\displaystyle=\max\{\#I:I\in\mathcal{I}(M),I\subseteq A\}

\displaystyle=r(\overline{A}).

Then one can recover either of
\mathcal{I}(M)
or
\mathcal{F}(M)
from the rank function
r
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(M)

\displaystyle:=\{I\subseteq E:r(I)=\#I\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(M)

\displaystyle:=\{F\subseteq E:r(F)<r(F\cup\{e\})\text{ for all }e\in E\setminus F\}

The following two matroid constructions will turn out to be useful in the sequel.

Definition 2.3
.

Given a matroid
M
on ground set
E
, and a subset
A\subseteq E
, one can define two new matroids, the
restriction

M|_{A}
, and the
contraction

M/A
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(M|_{A})

\displaystyle:=\{I\in\mathcal{I}(M):I\subseteq A\}=\mathcal{I}(M)\cap 2^{A},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(M/A)

\displaystyle:=\{F\setminus A:F\in\mathcal{F}(A),F\supseteq A\}.

In particular, for a flat
F
, one has a poset isomorphism between
\mathcal{F}(M/F)
and the poset interval

[F,E]:=\{G\in\mathcal{F}(M):F\subseteq G\subseteq E\}.

The isomorphism sends a flat
G
in the interval
[F,E]
of
\mathcal{F}(M)
to the flat
G\setminus F
in

\mathcal{F}(M/F)
.

2.2.
Order complexes

Several simplicial complexes that we will consider come from this construction.

Definition 2.4
.

Given any partially ordered set

]

.

Given a matroid

6.
Augmented Bergman complexes for other closures

One can characterize a matroid
M
on ground set
E
in terms of its
matroid closure operator

A\longmapsto\overline{A}

defined in (
4
). This is an instance of the following more general notion.

Definition 6.1
.

Given a set
E
, a map
2^{E}\overset{f}{\longrightarrow}2^{E}

is called a
closure operator
on
E
if it satisfies three axioms: for all subsets
A,B\subseteq E
,

C1.

A\subseteq f(A)

C2.

A\subseteq B
implies
f(A)\subseteq f(B)

C3.

f(f(A))=f(A)

Any closure operator on a finite set
E
has analogues of the complexes
\mathcal{I}(M),\underline{\Delta}_{M},\Delta_{M}
, introduced next.

Definition 6.2
.

Given a closure operator
f
on a finite set
E
, define a subset

I\subseteq E
to be
independent
if

f(I\setminus\{i\})\subsetneq f(I)\text{ for all }i\in I.

Let
\mathcal{I}(f)
denote the collection of all independent subsets
I\subseteq E
.
It is not hard to check that
\mathcal{I}(f)
always
satisfies axioms
{\sf I1,I2}
from Definition
2.1
,
so that it defines a simplicial complex,
also denoted
\mathcal{I}(f)
.

Definition 6.3
.

Given a closure operator
f
on a finite set
E
, define its

poset of closed sets

\mathcal{F}(f):=\{F\subseteq E:f(F)=F\}

partially ordered via inclusion.
It is not hard to check that
\mathcal{F}(f)
always
satisfies axioms
{\sf F1,F2}
from Definition
2.2
,
so that it becomes a lattice.
Define the
Bergman complex

\underline{\Delta}_{f}:=\Delta(\,\,\mathcal{F}(f)\setminus\{f(\varnothing),E\}\,\,)

to be the order complex of the proper part of this lattice
\mathcal{F}(f)
.

Definition 6.4
.

Given a closure operator
f
on a finite set
E
, define
its
augmented Bergman complex

\Delta_{f}
to
be the abstract simplicial complex on
vertex set

\{y_{i}:i\in E\}\sqcup\{x_{F}:F\in\mathcal{F}(f)\setminus\{E\}\}

whose simplices are the subsets

(9)

\{y_{i}\}_{i\in I}\cup\{x_{F_{1}},x_{F_{2}},\ldots,x_{F_{\ell}}\}

for which
I\in\mathcal{I}(f)

and the
F_{i}
are all closed sets in
\mathcal{F}(f)
, satisfying

I\subseteq F_{1}\subsetneq F_{2}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq F_{\ell}\quad(\subsetneq E).

As before with matroid closures,
\Delta_{f}
always
contains as subcomplexes both

•

\mathcal{I}(f)

as the simplices in
(
9
) with
\ell=0
, and

•

\operatorname{Cone}(\underline{\Delta}_{f})=\Delta(\,\mathcal{F}(F)\setminus\{E\}\,\,)
having cone vertex
x_{f(\varnothing)}
, as
the simplices in (
9
) with
\#I=0
.

However, in contrast to matroid closures, the complexes
\mathcal{I}(f),\underline{\Delta}_{f},\Delta_{f}
need not be pure, nor shellable.

Definition 6.6
.

For a closure
f
on a finite set
E
, define the set
\mathcal{B}(f)
of
bases

\mathcal{B}(f):=\{B\in\mathcal{I}(f):f(B)=E\}.

Definition 6.7
.

For a closure
f
on
E
, define its
automorphism group

\operatorname{Aut}(f):=\{\text{bijections }E\overset{g}{\longrightarrow}E:f(g(A))=g(f(A))\text{ for all }A\subseteq E\}

Note
\operatorname{Aut}(f)
stabilizes

\mathcal{B}(f)
, and acts on

\mathcal{I}(f),\underline{\Delta}_{f},\Delta_{f}

via simplicial automorphisms.

Theorem 6.1
.

For any closure operator on a finite set
E
,
the bases
\mathcal{B}(f)
index a collection
of homology facets for

\Delta_{f}
.
Hence
\Delta
is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of spheres

\bigvee_{B\in\mathcal{B}(f)}\mathbb{S}^{\#B-1}.

and the action of

\operatorname{Aut}(f)
on

\tilde{H}_{i}(\Delta,\mathbb{Z})
is the same
as on
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}\{[B]:B\in\mathcal{B}(f),\#B-1=i\}.
within
\tilde{C}_{i}(\Delta_{f},\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

In light of Lemma
5.2
, it suffices to show that

\Delta=\Delta_{f}
has its subcomplex
\Delta^{\prime}:=\Delta\setminus\mathcal{B}
contractible. Our proof strategy introduces another simplicial complex
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
, and shows it has these two properties:

(a)

\Delta^{\prime\prime}
is a simplicial subdivision of
\Delta^{\prime}
, and hence homeomorphic to it.

(b)

\Delta^{\prime\prime}
is homotopy equivalent to the subcomplex
\operatorname{Cone}(\underline{\Delta}_{f})=\Delta(\mathcal{F}\setminus\{E\})
inside
\Delta_{f}
.

Since cones are contractible, (b) would show
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
is contractible, and then (a) would show the same for
\Delta^{\prime}
.
We define
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
to be the simplicial complex on vertex
set

\{y_{I}:I\in\mathcal{I}(f)\setminus\mathcal{B}(f)\}\cup\{x_{F}:F\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\{E\}\}

whose simplices are subsets

\{y_{I_{1}},\cdots,y_{I_{k}}\}\cup\{x_{F_{1}},\ldots,x_{F_{\ell}}\}

with

I_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq I_{k}\subseteq F_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq F_{\ell}(\subsetneq E).

Proof of assertion (a).

One can view
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
as having been obtained from
\Delta^{\prime}
by performing

barycentric subdivision

[
10
, §2.15]

\sigma\mapsto\mathrm{Sd}(\sigma)
to every simplex within the subcomplex
\mathcal{I}(f)\setminus\mathcal{B}(f)
of
\Delta^{\prime}
; each simplex
\sigma=\{y_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is replaced by
the simplices
\{y_{I_{1}},\ldots,y_{I_{k}}\}

for which

I_{1}\subsetneq I_{2}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq I_{k}\subseteq I.

More generally, the typical simplex of
\Delta^{\prime}

as in (
9
)
is the
simplicial join

\sigma*\sigma^{\prime}

[
10
, §8.62]

of the simplex
\sigma=\{y_{i}\}_{i\in I}
above and
the simplex
\sigma^{\prime}=\{x_{F_{1}},\ldots,x_{F_{\ell}}\}
;
one replaces this with the simplicial join

\mathrm{Sd}(\sigma)*\sigma^{\prime}
in
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
.

Proof of assertion (b).

Attempt to define a simplicial map
\Delta^{\prime\prime}\overset{\pi}{\longrightarrow}\Delta(\mathcal{F}\setminus\{E\})
via this set map on vertices:

\begin{array}[]{rcll}x_{F}&\longmapsto&x_{F}&\text{ for }F\in\mathcal{F}(f)\setminus\{E\}\\
y_{I}&\longmapsto&x_{f(I)}&\text{ for }I\in\mathcal{I}(f)\setminus\mathcal{B}(f)\\
\end{array}

It is not hard to check that
\pi
indeed carries simplices to simplices, that is, it is a well-defined
simplicial map. One can also check that, for every element
F
in the poset
\mathcal{F}(f)\setminus\{E\}
, the inverse image under
\pi
of the
order complex
\Delta\mathcal{F}(f)_{\leq F}
of the principal order ideal
\mathcal{F}(f)_{\leq F}
is the star of the vertex
x_{F}
within
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
, and hence contractible. Thus by Quillen’s Fiber Lemma
[
1
, (10.5)(i)]
,
the map
\pi
induces a homotopy equivalence.
∎

Remark 6.8
.

Note that
\operatorname{Cone}(\underline{\Delta}_{M})=\Delta(\mathcal{F}(f)\setminus\{E\})
can be identified with
the subcomplex of
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
induced on the vertex subset
\{x_{F}:F\in\mathcal{F}(f)\setminus\{E\}\}
.
Since these vertices
x_{F}
are all pointwise fixed by
\pi
, the same is true for
this subcomplex
\operatorname{Cone}(\underline{\Delta}_{M})
, so that the map
\pi
is actually a homotopy inverse to the inclusion map
\operatorname{Cone}(\underline{\Delta}_{M})\hookrightarrow\Delta^{\prime\prime}
, showing that
\pi
is a
deformation retraction
.

In fact, if one removes the vertex
x_{f(\varnothing)}
from both
\Delta^{\prime},\Delta^{\prime\prime}
, one finds that

\Delta^{\prime\prime}\setminus\{x_{f(\varnothing)}\}
is a subdivision of
\Delta^{\prime}\setminus\{x_{f(\varnothing)}\}
,
and the simplicial map
\pi
also restricts
to a deformation retraction

(10)

\Delta^{\prime\prime}\setminus\{x_{f(\varnothing)}\}\overset{\pi}{\longrightarrow}\underline{\Delta}_{f}.
