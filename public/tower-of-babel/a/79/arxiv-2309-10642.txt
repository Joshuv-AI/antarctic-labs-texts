Abstract
. This paper tackles the critical issue of sample selection bias in cross-country comparisons using international assessments such as PISA (Program for International Student Assessment). While PISA is widely used to rank educational performance, its reliance on samples of students still in school at age 15 introduces survival bias, potentially distorting comparisons. To address this, I develop an econometric framework based on a quantile selection model. Under a stochastic dominance assumption, the selection-corrected quantile function is partially identified, with point identification achieved under parametric restrictions on the joint distribution of test scores and selection. Applying this method to PISA 2018 data, I demonstrate that correcting for selection bias leads to significant changes in country rankings based on mean performance. These findings underscore the importance of accounting for sample selection bias to ensure accurate and meaningful international educational comparisons. Keywords : Quantiles, Sample selection, International student achievement assessments, Pisa. JEL codes : C34, C83, I20



1.
Introduction

International comparisons of educational achievement, typically based on standardized assessment scores, have become a critical tool for evaluating and shaping educational policies worldwide. These comparisons allow policymakers to benchmark their national education systems against others, assess the effectiveness of reforms, and identify best practices. Rankings derived from such assessments influence funding decisions, curriculum changes, and broader educational strategies, with governments often using them to justify policy choices (see
Nagy 1996
,
Martin et al. 2000
,
McEwan and Marshall 2004
,
Cromley 2009
,
Tienken 2008
,
McGaw 2008
, and
Jakubowski and Pokropek 2015
). Among these assessments, the Program for International Student Assessment (PISA) is one of the most widely recognized and influential.

PISA, conducted triennially by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), evaluates education systems by assessing the knowledge and skills of 15-year-old students in three core domains: reading, mathematics, and science. The assessment is designed to measure how well students can apply their knowledge to real-world problems, rather than simply testing mastery of a prescribed curriculum. Participation in PISA is contingent on students being enrolled in school at age 15 and having completed at least six years of formal education. However, not all 15-year-olds in a given country are included in the assessment, as some students drop out before reaching the required age, while others are excluded due to logistical challenges, such as living in remote areas. Consequently, the PISA coverage rate the proportion of the entire 15-year-old population represented in the sample varies widely across countries.

This variation in coverage rates introduces a fundamental sample selection problem. Consider two countries, Country A and Country B, both participating in PISA. Country A includes a diverse range of students in its sample, encompassing various socio-economic backgrounds, urban and rural schools, and both general and vocational education tracks. Assume it has a coverage rate of 95%, meaning that nearly all 15-year-olds participate in PISA
Country B in contrast inadvertently excludes students from rural areas and vocational schools due to an outdated sampling frame and higher non-response rates in these groups. In addition, in country B, the high-school drop-out rate is high. Those parameters may lead to a low coverage rate (for example 70%) for country B, implying that
\displaystyle 30\%
of the eligible individuals are excluded.

When PISA results are published, Country B may appear to outperform Country A. However, this apparent superiority may be misleading. The exclusion of lower-performing student groups in Country B’s sample means the results don’t fully represent the nation’s overall student performance. In reality, if all student groups were appropriately represented, Country B’s average scores might have been lower than those of Country A. In other words, rather than comparing all students across countries, we are effectively comparing a nearly full distribution in Country A with a selective, potentially higher-achieving subset in Country B.

The problem of selection bias in PISA and other international assessments has been widely acknowledged (see
Rotberg 1995
,
Berliner 1993
,
Ferreira and Gignoux 2014
). Critics argue that rankings based on unadjusted PISA scores are unreliable and should not be used as definitive measures of educational quality. From an econometric perspective, addressing selection bias is essential, as participation in the assessment is often correlated with unobserved student characteristics—such as motivation, parental support, or prior academic performance—that also influence test scores. Without correcting for this bias, cross-country comparisons may yield distorted conclusions, leading to misguided policy decisions.

Addressing this selection problem presents a non-trivial econometric challenge due to two main obstacles. First, unlike in labor economics, where sample selection models often rely on auxiliary data sources containing information on both participants and non-participants (see
Heckman 1974
,
Arellano and Bonhomme 2017
,
Chernozhukov et al. 2023
), no such data exist for PISA. We do not observe the test scores of excluded students, nor do we have direct measures of their academic potential. Second, traditional methods for correcting sample selection, such as instrumental variable (IV) techniques, require a valid exclusion restriction—an exogenous variable that affects selection into the sample but does not directly influence test scores. In this context, no universally valid instrument is available, making nonparametric identification difficult.

To overcome these challenges, I propose a novel selection correction method based on a quantile selection model (see
Huber and Melly 2015
,
Arellano and Bonhomme 2017
). This approach builds on the insight that under a quantile selection mechanism, the observed quantile function of test scores can be systematically shifted to recover the distribution of the full population. Specifically, I show that selection-corrected quantiles can be obtained by adjusting the observed quantile levels according to a known selection threshold, as in
Arellano and Bonhomme 2017
. Under a mild stochastic dominance assumption, I establish nonparametric partial identification of the selection-corrected quantiles. Furthermore, I demonstrate that full point identification can be achieved under a parametric assumption on the joint distribution of test scores and selection probabilities.

Applying this methodology to PISA 2018 data reveals that correcting for selection bias has significant implications for international rankings. Countries with low coverage rates often experience downward adjustments in their estimated mean scores once selection bias is accounted for, while those with near-complete coverage remain largely unaffected. This finding underscores the importance of accounting for sample selection biases when interpreting PISA rankings, as failure to do so may result in misleading conclusions about educational performance.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 gives an overview and presents the importance of standardized tests, in particular PISA. Section 3 presents the formal econometric model and the identification results. Section 4 applies the method to PISA 2018 data and discusses the empirical findings. The conclusion is in section 5. Detailed proofs are provided in the appendix.

2.
Importance of standardized test scores

2.1.
Overview of test scores

International comparison tests compare student achievement data across countries. These tests can help inform educational policies and reforms. There exist mainly four international assessments which are : TIMSS, PIRLS, PASEC and PISA.

The Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), organized by the OECD, measures the skills of 15-year-old students in reading, mathematics, and science. Unlike traditional exams, PISA focuses on how well students can apply their knowledge to real-life situations, testing their ability to analyze, reason, and communicate effectively. Conducted every three years, PISA offers valuable insights into how prepared students are to tackle the challenges of today’s world. It also collects data from various educational stakeholders, helping to identify factors that influence performance gaps. The OECD collaborates with international education experts, policymakers, and psychometricians to develop the test. Around 80 countries/economies take part in PISA. Each country selects a nationally representative sample of 15-year-old students, regardless of grade level. Typically, 4,500 to 10,000 students from 150 to 250 schools are randomly selected per country. The sample includes public and private schools, ensuring diversity in socioeconomic backgrounds. Since 2015, PISA has been computer-based for most countries, though a few still use paper-based tests. The test lasts two hours and includes multiple-choice, short-response, and open-ended questions. The responses are analyzed using Item Response Theory (IRT) to compare scores across countries.

The Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS)
TIMSS, managed by the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA), assesses the mathematics and science knowledge of fourth and eighth graders. Administered every four years, TIMSS provides critical data on global trends in STEM education. Beyond measuring student achievement, it gathers information on curricula, teaching practices, and school environments, offering a comprehensive view of educational systems worldwide.

The Progress in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS)
evaluates the reading comprehension skills of fourth-grade students. Conducted every five years since 2001. PIRLS tracks trends in reading achievement and collects data on home and school factors that impact literacy development. The study aims to inform education policies and practices by identifying effective strategies to improve reading instruction and learning outcomes.

The Programme d’Analyse des Systèmes Éducatifs de la CONFEMEN (PASEC) is an initiative launched in 1991 by the Conference of Ministers of Education of French-Speaking Countries (CONFEMEN). Its primary goal is to evaluate and report on the performance of education systems in CONFEMEN member countries, which include nations in sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East, and, since 2011, Southeast Asia. PASEC assesses students in the early (2nd or 3rd year) and later (5th or 6th year) stages of primary education, focusing on reading and mathematics skills. These evaluations provide insights into student learning outcomes at key stages of their education.

The results of those international assessments have sparked discussions about education systems in many countries. By comparing their results with other nations, governments have had to implement reforms to improve their performance in these assessments.

2.2.
Impact of PISA on policy and reforms

This section highlights some of the changes in education policies across various countries in response to PISA outcomes.

The release of the first PISA report in 2001 revealed that German students performed significantly below the OECD average.
Knodel et al. 2013
noted that Germany’s low ranking dealt a major blow to national self-esteem, with media referring to it as ”a disaster in almost every way.” The failure was attributed to flawed educational policies. In response, Germany implemented several reforms. In 2002, a central institution was established to standardize and monitor education. The PISA influence led to changes in how school performance was assessed, aligning with international benchmarks. By 2012, Germany’s PISA scores had surpassed the OECD average, validating the effectiveness of these reforms (see
Ringarp 2016
,
Knodel et al. 2013
). Until the mid-2000s, Sweden’s education system was considered exemplary. However, declining PISA scores prompted authorities to study successful systems in Finland, South Korea, and Canada. A major reform in 2010 revised teacher certification standards, drawing heavily from the Finnish model (see
Ringarp 2016
). Canada’s PISA results consistently exceeded the OECD average, earning media praise. However, concerns arose regarding disparities in student performance across different provinces. PISA results were used to assess the effectiveness of government education investments and highlight workforce quality issues (see
Knighton et al. 2010
). Norway’s poor performance in PISA 2000 and 2003 was particularly alarming given the country’s significant education funding. The government used these results as a catalyst for reform. In 2006, Norwegian policymakers introduced curriculum changes emphasizing basic skills aligned with PISA objectives. Despite score improvements in 2009, PISA continued to serve as a tool for advocating ongoing reforms (see
Stray and Wood 2020
). New Zealand’s education reforms targeted disparities in PISA results based on ethnic backgrounds. Policymakers analyzed successful education systems in Finland, Singapore, and South Korea to inform their strategies. Shanghai leveraged PISA to assess student performance and learning capabilities through international comparisons. Politicians used high PISA scores to both criticize local educational inequalities and validate the superiority of the Shanghai education model (see
Suleyman 2020
).

As seen in these examples, PISA has significantly shaped education policies worldwide. It has driven debates on restructuring education systems and prompted major reforms in various countries.

Model and Identification

Consider the country being fixed, let
\displaystyle Y^{\ast}
denote the hypothetical score an individual would achieve if they had completed their education up to the age required for the assessment (e.g., age 15 for the PISA assessment). I refer to
\displaystyle Y^{\ast}
as the potential assessment score. Since
\displaystyle Y^{\ast}
is a continuous random variable, it can be represented using a Skorohod quantile representation. Let
\displaystyle U\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]
be the rank of
\displaystyle Y^{\ast}
, we have :

(3.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle Y^{\ast}=Q_{Y^{\ast}}(U)

Let
\displaystyle S
be a binary variable that takes the value
\displaystyle 1
if the individual meets the requirement to be included in the evaluation and
\displaystyle 0
otherwise. Note that if the probability of
\displaystyle S
is
\displaystyle 1
,
\displaystyle Y^{\ast}
is directly observable in the data for the entire target population. However, if this probability is strictly less than
\displaystyle 1
,
\displaystyle Y^{\ast}
is unobserved for the subpopulation with
\displaystyle S=0
. Let
\displaystyle Y
denote the observed assessment score available to the researcher. Importantly,

\displaystyle Y
and
\displaystyle Y^{\ast}
coincide only when
\displaystyle S=1
. While country rankings are typically based on the mean of
\displaystyle Y
, they should ideally be based on the mean of
\displaystyle Y^{\ast}
for more accurate comparison. We have the following :

(3.2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle Y^{\ast}=Y\textit{ if }S=1

And when
\displaystyle S=0
,
\displaystyle Y^{\ast}
is completely unknown. In this analysis, I focus on the entire distribution of
\displaystyle Y^{\ast}
as researchers may sometimes be interested in distributional characteristics beyond the mean. The objective is to identify the quantiles
\displaystyle Q_{Y^{\ast}}(u)
for all
\displaystyle u\in[0,1]
and, moreover,
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y^{\ast})
.

3.1.
Non-parametric partial identification

For a given rank
\displaystyle u\in[0,1]
, define
\displaystyle\tilde{u}\equiv\mathbb{P}(U\leq u|S=1)
as the selection-corrected rank. The first important result of this paper is the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

\forall u\in[0,1],\textit{ we have :}

(3.3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle Q_{Y^{\ast}}(u)=Q_{Y|S=1}(\tilde{u})

From the data, we can identify the quantile function
\displaystyle Q_{Y|S=1}(u),u\in[0,1]
. By this lemma, we can see that it is possible to recover the quantiles of
\displaystyle Y^{\ast}
by applying a suitable shift of the percentile levels in the quantile function of
\displaystyle Y|S=1
. Since we do not know
\displaystyle\tilde{u}
, lemma 1 is not enough to identify
\displaystyle Q_{Y^{\ast}}(u)
. However, it allows us to focus on the identification of the selection-corrected rank. Since we cannot observe anything on the excluded individuals, we make the following assumption concerning the coverage rate.

Assumption 1

(Identification of coverage rate)
.

\displaystyle p\equiv\mathbb{P}(S=1)
is identified.

Assumption 1 is trivially satisfied with PISA data since we always have access to the coverage rate: the proportion of individuals in the target population included in the assessment. In the absence of more restrictive assumptions and because of the data limitations, the exact value of the quantiles cannot be pinpointed, we therefore have a range of many possible values. That means that without more assumptions the quantiles are partially identified. We can build the most intuitive bounds using the Fréchet–Hoeffding inequality. In fact, we know that :
\displaystyle\tilde{u}=\mathbb{P}(U\leq u,S=1)/p
. Now if we apply Fréchet-bounds to the joint probability, and we obtain :

(3.4)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\max\{u+p-1,0\}}{p}\leq\tilde{u}\leq\frac{\min\{u,p\}}{p}

Using the monononicity of
\displaystyle Q_{Y|S=1}
, we finally obtain :

(3.5)

\displaystyle\displaystyle Q_{Y|S=1}\left(\frac{\max\{u+p-1,0\}}{p}\right)\leq Q_{Y^{\ast}}(u)\leq Q_{Y|S=1}\left(\frac{\min\{u,p\}}{p}\right)

But these bounds can be wide and uninformative. One can obtain better bounds by adding a reasonable structure to the unobservables.

Assumption 2

(Stochastic Dominance)
.

\forall\textit{ u}\in[0,1],\textit{ }Q_{Y^{\ast}|S=0}(u)\leq Q_{Y^{\ast}|S=1}(u)

The assumption states that the distribution of
\displaystyle Y^{\ast}|S=1
first-order stochastically dominates the distribution of
\displaystyle Y^{\ast}|S=0
. This typically implies that the values of
\displaystyle U
tend to be higher when
\displaystyle S=1
compared to when
\displaystyle S=0
.

This assumption conveys the idea that individuals who meet the requirement for the assessment
\displaystyle(S=1)
generally have higher latent abilities than those who do not
\displaystyle S=0
. With this assumption, we can derive the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(Partial Identification)
.

Under Assumptions 1 and 2, the following bounds are valid and sharp

\displaystyle\forall u\in[0,1]
,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\displaystyle Q_{Y|S=1}\left(\frac{\max\{u+p-1,0\}}{p}\right)\leq Q_{Y^{\ast}}(u)\leq Q_{Y|S=1}(u)

We also have :

(3.7)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}Q_{Y|S=1}\left(\frac{\max\{u+p-1,0\}}{p}\right)du\leq E(Y^{\ast})\leq E(Y|S=1)

This theorem establishes that, under the given model and assumptions, the latent quantile function for a given
\displaystyle u\in[0,1]
lies within an interval whose bounds are sharp, meaning they cannot be improved without additional assumptions. In other words, the lower and upper bounds represent the tightest possible range consistent with the data and the assumptions. This new interval is narrower than the one derived in Lemma 1, as its upper bound is smaller. This refinement is a direct consequence of introducing Assumption 2. Notably, when
\displaystyle p=1
, the bounds in Lemma 2 and Theorem 1 collapse to a single point, and the latent quantile coincides exactly with the observed quantile. This occurs because sample selection bias disappears when
\displaystyle p=1
.

The upper bound in the theorem corresponds precisely to the observed quantile function. This highlights that analyses or comparisons based on observed quantiles may suffer from positive bias, as they implicitly assume the highest possible values for the quantiles (and, consequently, for the mean). This upper bound is attained only when selection is random, that is, independent of the assessment outcomes. Thus, the official rankings rely on the implicit assumption that the mechanism excluding individuals from the test is random.

The lower bound, on the other hand, is achieved under the assumption that individuals meet the assessment requirements only if their rank
\displaystyle U
exceeds the proportion of those who do not satisfy the requirements,
\displaystyle(1-p)
. This leads to the selection structure
\displaystyle S=1\{U\geq 1-p\}
. In this scenario, the outcome
\displaystyle Y
is observed only for the top
\displaystyle p
proportion of individuals in the distribution of
\displaystyle U
. This corresponds to a situation where individuals with the lowest values of
\displaystyle U
(e.g., the least talented) are systematically excluded from the evaluation.

The theorem also gives the corresponding bounds for the mean, which are obtained by integration of the quantile functions.
When using nonparametric bounds, we typically assume minimal restrictions on the distribution of unobserved test scores.
In countries with low coverage rates, the lower and upper bounds can be extremely far apart, making it difficult to determine where the country ranks relative to others. If the bounds overlap significantly across countries, they do not provide useful ranking information. In contrast, a parametric approach allows for point estimates.

Therefore, for meaningful comparisons, it is essential to impose additional structure. While nonparametric bounds can serve as a robustness check, they are generally too wide and uninformative for direct use in policy decisions or international comparisons.

3.2.
Parametric point identification

It is possible to get point identification by adding more structure to the selection mechanism. Consider the following assumption :

Assumption 3

(Structure of S)
.

.

(3.8)

\displaystyle\displaystyle S=1\{U\geq V\},V\sim\beta(a,b)

The assumption introduces a structured selection mechanism based on a latent comparison between two unobserved variables,
\displaystyle U
and
\displaystyle V
. Here,
\displaystyle U
can be interpreted as the return in terms of skills to the economic decision of entering and remaining in the educational system while
\displaystyle V
can be seen as the cost associated with that decision. The assumption states that selection occurs when the return exceeds the cost, i.e., when
\displaystyle U\geq V
.

The choice of a Beta distribution for
\displaystyle V
is mainly motivated by its statistical flexibility. In fact, the Beta distribution is highly flexible, allowing for various shapes (e.g., increasing, decreasing, U-shaped densities) depending on its parameters. This flexibility helps capture differences in selection patterns, such as varying school enrollment behaviors across countries. Also, the support of
\displaystyle V
is
\displaystyle[0,1]
, which matches the support of
\displaystyle U
(assumed to be uniform on
\displaystyle[0,1]
), making the comparison between returns and costs meaningful. By using this assumption, we can characterize the distribution of costs (
\displaystyle V
) based on observed selection probabilities (
\displaystyle p
), providing insights into how selection mechanisms vary across different contexts. In fact, we have the following :

(3.9)

\displaystyle\displaystyle a=(1-p)c\quad,b=pc

\displaystyle c=\alpha+\beta
, the concentration parameter is not identified.
This lemma shows us that we can identify the parameters of the Beta distribution up to the concentration parameter. This is the case because the only information we have is
\displaystyle p
, which is not sufficient to fully identify
\displaystyle(a,b)
. One can also notice an interesting result:

(3.10)

\displaystyle\displaystyle E(V)=1-p

The average cost decreases as
\displaystyle p
increases, the distribution shifts leftward; more mass is concentrated near
\displaystyle 0
. This means individuals are more likely to face a lower cost when they are in a country with a higher
\displaystyle p
. Now, for point identification, we need to find of way of choosing the value of
\displaystyle c
. For that, I use some economic intuition.

Since
\displaystyle V
represents the cost faced by individuals, it is reasonable to expect its likelihood function (i.e., its probability density) to be monotonically decreasing. The intuition behind this assumption is that in any given country, a larger proportion of individuals face relatively low costs rather than high costs. As the cost
\displaystyle V
increases, the number of individuals facing such costs should naturally decline, leading to a density that decreases as
\displaystyle V
grows. To better capture this economic intuition, the distribution of
\displaystyle V
should satisfy the following properties: First, the density function of
\displaystyle V
should be highest at zero and continuously decrease as
\displaystyle V
increases. This reflects the fact that there are many individuals with low costs but fewer individuals with high costs. The distribution should be right-tailed, meaning that most values of
\displaystyle V
are clustered around low values, but the distribution should still allow for a longer right tail. This accounts for the presence of some individuals who do face high costs, even though they are a minority. The most frequent value (mode) of
\displaystyle V
should be zero, indicating that the lowest cost is the most commonly experienced among individuals. This aligns with the idea that in most countries, education policies, subsidies, or other social programs make schooling relatively affordable for a significant portion of the population. For the Beta density with parameters
\displaystyle\alpha=(1-p)c
and
\displaystyle\beta=pc
to satisfy these conditions, we must have:

a=1\quad,\quad b>1

These conditions together lead to :

c=\frac{1}{1-p}\quad,\quad b=\frac{p}{1-p}\quad,\quad p>0.5

The condition
\displaystyle p>0.5
is generally satisfied for all the countries in PISA.
\displaystyle a
being fixed, all the parameters of the beta distribution are identified. This gives us the possibility to point-identify the selection-corrected quantiles using the following formula :

Theorem 2

(Identification)
.

Under Assumptions 1 and 3,
\displaystyle Q_{Y^{\ast}}
is identified and we have :

(3.11)

\displaystyle\displaystyle Q_{Y^{\ast}}(u)=Q_{Y|S=1}\left(\frac{1}{p}\left(uF_{V,a,b}(u)-\int_{0}^{u}vdF_{V,a,b}(v)\right)\right)

(3.12)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y^{\ast})=\int_{0}^{1}Q_{Y|S=1}\left(\frac{1}{p}\left(uF_{V,a,b}(u)-\int_{0}^{u}vdF_{V,a,b}(v)\right)\right)du

This theorem gives us a practical and very simple way to correct the sample selection problem in the rankings. In the next section, we will see how this can actually make a difference.

5.
Conclusion

In this paper, I introduce a novel method to address sample selection bias in cross-country comparisons using data from international assessments such as PISA. The proposed correction leverages a quantile selection model, which, under varying assumptions, enables the partial and point identification of latent quantiles and, consequently, the latent means. The application of this method to the PISA 2018 data reveals that the observed means are subject to upward bias, necessitating adjustments to the rankings. The extent of this bias is closely tied to the proportion of excluded individuals from the target population, highlighting the importance of accounting for sample coverage in comparative analyses.

The findings underscore the need to revise rankings derived from international assessments, as the observed means do not accurately reflect the true performance of countries due to sample selection issues. By correcting for these biases, the method provides a more reliable basis for cross-country comparisons and informs more accurate interpretations of educational outcomes.
