Causal Effect Estimation from Observational and Interventional Data Through Matrix Weighted Linear Estimators
Klaus-Rudolf Kladny, Julius von Kügelgen, Bernhard Schölkopf, Michael Muehlebach

Abstract
We study causal effect estimation from a mixture of observational and interventional data in a confounded linear regression model with multivariate treatments. We show that the statistical efficiency in terms of expected squared error can be improved by combining estimators arising from both the observational and interventional setting. To this end, we derive methods based on matrix weighted linear estimators and prove that our methods are asymptotically unbiased in the infinite sample limit. This is an important improvement compared to the pooled estimator using the union of interventional and observational data, for which the bias only vanishes if the ratio of observational to interventional data tends to zero. Studies on synthetic data confirm our theoretical findings. In settings where confounding is substantial and the ratio of observational to interventional data is large, our estimators outperform a Stein-type estimator and various other baselines.

Introduction

Estimating the causal effect of a treatment variable on an outcome of interest is a fundamental scientific problem that is central to disciplines such as econometrics, epidemiology, and social science
(
Angrist and Pischke 2009
;
Morgan and Winship 2014
;
Imbens and Rubin 2015
;
Hernán and Robins 2020
)
.
A fundamental obstacle to this task is the possibility of hidden confounding: unobserved variables that influence both the treatment and the outcome may introduce additional
associations between them
(
Reichenbach 1956
)
.
As a result, estimators purely based on observational (passively collected) data can be biased and typically do not recover the true causal effect.

This contrasts experimental studies such as randomized controlled trials
(
Neyman 1923
;
Fisher 1936
, RCTs;)
, where the treatment assignment mechanism is modified through an external intervention, thus breaking potential influences of confounders on the treatment.
For this reason, RCTs have become the gold standard for causal effect estimation.
However, obtaining such interventional data is difficult in practice because the necessary experiments are often infeasible, unethical, or very costly to perform.

In contrast, observational data is usually cheap and abundant, motivating the study of causal inference from observational data
(
Rubin 1974
;
Pearl 2009
)
.
In fact, in certain situations causal effects can be identified and estimated from purely observational data, even under hidden confounding, e.g., in the presence of natural experiments
(
Angrist et al. 1996
, instrumental variables;)
or observed mediators
(
Pearl 1995
, front-door adjustment;)
.
However, this does not apply to the general case in which a treatment
\mathbf{X}
and an outcome
Y
are confounded by an unobserved variable
\mathbf{Z}
as shown in
fig.

.

In the present work, we study treatment effect estimation in this general setting under the assumption that we have access to both observational and interventional data. The latter can be viewed as sampled from the setting shown in
fig.

1(b)
, where the arrow
\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{X}
has been removed as a result of the intervention on
\mathbf{X}

(

, graph surgery;)
, and is thus unbiased for our task.
Due to small sample size, however, the estimator based only on interventional data may exhibit high variance.
Our main idea is therefore to use the (potentially large amounts of) observational data for variance reduction—at the cost of introducing some bias. This is achieved by forming a combined estimator, which is superior to the purely interventional one in terms of
mean squared error.

We make the key assumption that both the treatment
\mathbf{X}\to Y
and confounding
\mathbf{Z}\to\{\mathbf{X},Y\}
effects are linear, but allow for treatment
\mathbf{X}
and unobserved confounder
\mathbf{Z}
to be continuous and multi-variate.
We then consider a class of estimators of the causal effect parameter vector that combine the unbiased, but high-variance interventional estimator and the biased, but low-variance observational estimator through weight matrices—akin to a multi-variate convex combination.
We study the statistical properties of these estimators, establish theoretical optimality results, and investigate their empirical behavior through simulations.

In summary, we highlight the following contributions:

•

We introduce a new framework of weighing linear estimators using matrices and show that several existing approaches fall into this category (
section

4
).

•

We prove that, unlike pooling observational and interventional data (
proposition

4.1
), our matrix weighting approaches
achieve vanishing mean squared error in the interventional sample limit (
propositions

4.3
and
4.4
) if the ratio between observational and interventional data is non-vanishing.

•

We discuss two practical approaches for variance reduction in estimating optimal weight matrices (
section

4.4
;
proposition

4.5
), and demonstrate through simulations that our estimators outperform
baselines and existing methods
in situations where confounding is substantial (
section

5
).

Related Work

Causal reasoning, i.e., inferring a causal query such as a causal effect, can be split up into the tasks of (i) identification and (ii) estimation.
Step (i) operates at the population level and seeks to answer whether a causal question can—at least in principle—be answered given infinite data.
If the answer is positive and a valid estimand is provided, step (ii) then aims to construct a statistically efficient estimator.

A causal query is identified from a set of assumptions if it can be expressed in terms of the available distributions (e.g., a mixture of different observational and interventional distributions).
To this end,
Pearl 2009
’s do-calculus (
Pearl 1995
;
Pearl 2009
) provides an axiomatic set of rules for manipulating causal expressions based on graphical criteria.
The identification task has been studied extensively
(
Tian and Pearl 2002
;
Pearl and Bareinboim 2014
;
Bareinboim and Pearl 2016
)
and has by now been solved for many settings of interest: In these cases, the do-calculus—and its extensions
(
Correa and Bareinboim 2020
)
—are sound and complete in that they provide a valid estimand if and only if one exists
(
Huang and Valtorta 2006
;
Shpitser and Pearl 2006
;
Bareinboim and Pearl 2012
;
Lee et al. 2020
)
.

In our setting from
fig.

1
, the causal effect
\bm{\alpha}
is not identifiable from observational data, but is trivially identified by intervening on
\mathbf{X}
.
Yet, this leaves open the question of
how to estimate
\bm{\alpha}
from finite data in the best possible way
.
In contrast to the plethora of works on identification, there is much less
prior literature about statistical efficiency of causal parameter estimation, particularly for confounded settings.

.

A common source of inspiration for both prior work and our approach is that of shrinkage estimation.
In light of the bias-variance decomposition of the mean squared error
(e.g.,
Hastie et al. 2009
, p. 24)
, shrinkage can yield a strictly better (“dominating”) estimator by reducing variance, at the cost of introducing some bias.
These ideas were first introduced in frequentist statistics by
Stein 1956
;
James and Stein 1961
who showed that the maximum likelihood estimate of a multivariate mean is dominated by shrinking towards a fixed point such as the origin.
Similar ideas are also at the heart of empirical Bayes analysis
(
Robbins 1964
;
Efron and Morris 1973
;
Efron 2012
)
.
For estimating a parameter vector
\bm{\alpha}
in a linear model, as is the focus of the present work, a classical shrinkage method is ridge regression
(
Hoerl 1970
)
.

Instead of shrinking towards the origin, an intuitive idea for causal effect estimation
is to
shrink towards the observational estimator
. The hope is that the latter constitutes a better attractor
if the confounding bias is not too large—despite a slight increase in variance compared to shrinking toward a constant.
We refer to this approach as scalar estimator weighting.
Figure

2
shows a visual comparison to classical shrinkage estimation.
The most closely related work on estimator weighting is that of

Green and Strawderman 1991
;
Green et al. 2005
and
Rosenman et al. 2020
. The former two consider general biased and unbiased estimators. The latter propose weighting schemes for estimating vectors of multiple
binary
treatment effects. These works are strongly inspired by James-Stein shrinkage estimators
and minimize a generalized version of Stein’s unbiased risk estimate
(
Wasserman 2006
, p. 150)
.
Rosenman et al. 2020

show optimality among scalar weights with respect to minimizing the true risk as the dimensionality of the estimated treatment effects goes to infinity. However, these theoretical results rely on knowledge of the true covariance matrix of the interventional estimator (which is typically unknown in practice), and
the behavior of their estimators in the infinite sample limit is not analyzed.

Other work that focuses on combining observational and interventional data to estimate causal effects of
binary
treatments includes, e.g.,
Kallus et al. 2018
;
Cheng and Cai 2021
;
Ilse et al. 2021
;
Rosenman et al. 2022
;
Hatt et al. 2022
, see
Colnet et al. 2020
for a comprehensive survey.

Yang and Ding 2020
also study combining estimators of binary treatment effects. However, in their framework an estimator with less bias in addition to a second error-prone estimator is computed from a second observational “validation set”, in which all confounders are measured. Our framework, in contrast, does not require measurements of the confounders.

In the present work, we consider a
general
linear regression setting with continuous (rather than binary) multi-variate treatments.
To combine observational and interventional data, we introduce a new class of matrix (rather than scalar) weighted estimators, of which ridge regression and data pooling are special cases.
Instead of employing Stein’s unbiased risk estimate, we develop and analyze estimates for the theoretically optimal weight matrix, without making assumptions about the covariance structure of estimators.

Most approaches to causal estimation, including the present work, assume that the causal structure among variables is known and takes the general form of the directed acyclic graph in
fig.

1
.
For prior work on leveraging observational and interventional data for causal discovery, or structure learning, see, e.g.,
Wang et al. 2017
.

Setting & Preliminaries

Notation.

Upper case
Y
denotes a scalar random variable, lower-case
y
a scalar, bold lower-case
\mathbf{x}
a vector, and bold upper-case
\mathbf{X}
either a matrix or random vector.
The spectral norm of a matrix
\mathbf{X}
is denoted by
\left\lVert\mathbf{X}\right\rVert_{2}
.

Causal Model.

To formalize our problem setting,
we adopt the structural causal model framework of
Pearl 2009
. Specifically, we assume that the causal relationships between the
d
-dimensional confounder
\mathbf{Z}
, the
p
-dimensional treatment
\mathbf{X}
, and the scalar outcome
Y
are captured by the following linear Gaussian structural equation model (SEM):

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}

\displaystyle\;\leftarrow\;\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{Z}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{Z}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{Z}}},\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{Z}}})

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}

\displaystyle\;\leftarrow\;\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{X}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{X}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{X}}},\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{X}}})

(2)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\;\leftarrow\;\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}+\mathbf{X}^{\top}\bm{\alpha}+N_{Y},

\displaystyle N_{Y}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{N_{Y}},\sigma^{2}_{N_{Y}})

(3)

with
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
,
\bm{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
(\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{Z}},\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{X}},N_{Y})
mutually independent exogenous noise variables.
The SEM in
eq.

1
–(
3
) induces an observational distribution over
(\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X},Y)
which is referred to as
\mathbb{P}_{\text{obs}}
, see
fig.

1(a)
.

To model the interventional setting, we consider a soft intervention
(
Eberhardt and Scheines 2007
)
,
which randomizes the treatment
\mathbf{X}
by replacing the assignment in
eq.

2
with

\mathbf{X}\;\leftarrow\;\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathbf{X}},\qquad\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathbf{X}}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathbf{X}}},\qquad

(4)

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathbf{X}}
is mutually independent of
\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{Z}}
and
N_{Y}
. We note that
\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathbf{X}}
may be non-Gaussian. The modified interventional SEM consisting of
eqs.

1
,
4
and
3
induces a different, interventional distribution over
(\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X},Y)
, which we refer to as
\mathbb{P}_{\text{int}}
, see
fig.

1(b)
.

For ease of notation and for the remainder of this work, we assume without loss of generality that all noise variables are zero-mean. Details on how to extend our method to non zero-mean noise variables are provided in App.
D
.

Data.

We assume access to two separate datasets of observations of
(\mathbf{X},Y)
of size
n
and
m
, each sampled independently from the observational and interventional distributions (i.i.d.), respectively:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\;

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\;\mathbb{P}_{\text{obs}},\quad i=1,...,n,

\displaystyle(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\;

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\;\mathbb{P}_{\text{int}},\quad i=n+1,...,n+m,

where
\mathbb{P}_{\text{obs}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\text{int}}
denote the distributions of
(\mathbf{X},Y)
in the observational and interventional settings, respectively.
We note that the confounder
\mathbf{Z}
remains unobserved.
We concatenate the observational sample in a treatment matrix
\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
and outcome vector
\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{o}}=(y_{1},...,y_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and similarly with
\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}},\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{i}}
for the interventional sample.
Finally, we denote the pooled data by
\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{p}}=(\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}},\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}})\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+m)\times p}
and
\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{p}}=(\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{o}},\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{i}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
.

Goal.

Our objective is to obtain an accurate estimate of the parameter vector
\bm{\alpha}
, which characterizes the linear causal effect of
\mathbf{X}
on
Y
in
eq.

3
. Formally, it is given by

\displaystyle\bm{\alpha}=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbb{E}[Y|\mathrm{do}(\mathbf{X}\leftarrow\mathbf{x})],

where the
\mathrm{do}(\cdot)
operator denotes a manipulation of the treatment assignment akin to
eq.

4
, and the expectation is taken with respect to the corresponding conditional distribution.

Confounding Issues.

In the general case with non-zero
\mathbf{B}
and
\bm{\gamma}
, the observational setting is confounded, meaning

\mathbb{P}_{\text{obs}}(Y|\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x})\neq\mathbb{P}(Y|\mathrm{do}(\mathbf{X}\leftarrow\mathbf{x}))=\mathbb{P}_{\text{int}}(Y|\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}),

which complicates the use of observational data.
Specifically, for our assumed model
eq.

1
–(
3
) the conditional expectation of
Y
under
\mathbb{P}_{\text{obs}}
is given by
the following perturbed linear model
(
Ćevid et al. 2020
)
:

\mathbb{E}_{\text{obs}}[Y|\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}]=(\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\Delta})^{\top}\mathbf{x},

(5)

where
\bm{\Delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denotes the
confounding bias
, which
is given explicitly in terms of the model parameters as

\bm{\Delta}=(\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{X}}}+\mathbf{B}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{Z}}}\mathbf{B}^{\top})^{-1}\mathbf{B}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{Z}}}\bm{\gamma}.

(6)

It can be seen from
eq.

6
that the confounding bias
\bm{\Delta}
is zero if
\mathbf{B}

or
\bm{\gamma}
are zero (i.e.,
\mathbf{Z}
only affects either
\mathbf{X}
or
Y
).
Furthermore, we have that, in general,

\text{Var}_{\text{obs}}(Y|\mathbf{X})=\sigma^{2}_{Y|\mathbf{X}}\neq\sigma^{2}_{Y|\text{do}(\mathbf{X})}=\text{Var}_{\text{int}}(Y|\mathbf{X})\,.

(7)

Assessing Estimator Quality.

We rely on
mean squared error with respect to the true parameter
\bm{\alpha}
as a measure for comparing different estimators.

Definition 3.1

(MSE)
.

Let
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}
be any function of the pooled data
(\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{p}},\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{p}})
taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Then

\mathrm{MSE}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}-\bm{\alpha}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right],

where the expectation is taken over
\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{p}},\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{p}}
.

We note that the mean squared error can also be written as follows:

\mathrm{MSE}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}})=\left\lVert\mathbf{Bias}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}))\,,

(8)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{Bias}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}]-\bm{\alpha}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}])(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}])^{\top}]\,.

This decomposition highlights that biased estimators can dominate unbiased ones through variance reduction.

Pure Estimators.

We study estimators for
\bm{\alpha}
that are linear combinations of the following ordinary least squares estimators obtained on the two data sets individually.

Definition 3.2

(Pure Estimators)
.

For non-singular moment matrices
\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}}
and
\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}
,
the
pure estimators
based only on the observational/interventional sample are given by:

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}})^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{o}},

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}})^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{i}}.

Recall that
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}
is unbiased while
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}
has bias
\bm{\Delta}
. Their covariances conditionally on
\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}}
and
\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}})^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{Y|\mathbf{X}},

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}})^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{Y|\text{do}(\mathbf{X})}.

Unlike previous work (see
section

2
), we do not make assumptions about the covariance structure of either estimator.

Almost sure convergence.

To analyze the behavior of estimators in the infinite sample limit, we will employ the following characterization known as
almost sure convergence
.

Definition 3.3

(Almost Sure Convergence)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a random matrix with realizations in
\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
. We say a sequence of random matrices
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{m}
indexed by
m\in\mathbb{N}

converges almost surely
to
\mathbf{M}
, denoted
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{m}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\mathbf{M}
, if and only if

\lim_{m\to\infty}\text{P}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{m}=\mathbf{M}\right)=1,

where
P
denotes probability.

4
Matrix Weighted Linear Estimators

We now introduce our class of matrix weighted linear estimators, which combine the two pure estimators from
definition

3.2
using a weight matrix
\mathbf{W}

to obtain a new (better) estimator.

Definition 4.1

(
\mathbf{W}
-weighted Linear Estimator)
.

Let
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
(possibly random). The
\mathbf{W}
-weighted linear estimator
for
\bm{\alpha}
is given by

\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\mathbf{W}}\coloneqq\mathbf{W}\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}+(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{W})\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}.

We furthermore refer to
\mathbf{W}
as a weight matrix.

We will generally think of
n
as a function of
m
, where we sometimes even explicitly write
n(m)
. However, to simplify notation we index estimators by
m
only, omitting the dependence
n(m)
.

Note that the purely interventional estimator is a special case of a
\mathbf{W}
-weighted estimator with
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{I}_{p}
.
However, while unbiased, it may be subject to high variance if
m
is very small.
1
1

1

E.g., consider a one-dimensional setting with
x_{i}=1
if
i
is even
and
-1
otherwise. Then, for odd
m
,
\text{Var}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}|\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}})\propto(\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2})^{-1}=\frac{1}{m}
.

Hence, we generally prefer to employ the observational data as well and choose
\mathbf{W}\neq\mathbf{I}_{p}
.

4.1
Existing Methods as Special Cases

First, we show that several standard approaches can be viewed as special cases of matrix-weighted estimators.

Data Pooling.

A straightforward approach for combining both data sets
is to
compute an estimator on the pooled data.
The resulting least-squares estimator
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{p}}
is:

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{p}}:=

\displaystyle(\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{p}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{p}})^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{p}}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{p}}

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}}+\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}})^{-1}(\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{o}}+\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{i}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}^{m}_{\textsc{p}}\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}+(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{m}_{\textsc{p}})\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}},

where

\mathbf{W}^{m}_{\textsc{p}}\coloneqq(\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}}+\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}})^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}.

(11)

We see that

\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{p}}
indeed qualifies as a valid matrix weighted estimator in the sense of
definition

4.1
.

However, data pooling can lead to highly undesirable limiting behavior in cases where the amount of observational data
n(m)
does not vanish in the limit of infinite interventional data
m\rightarrow\infty
.
An example for this is given in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{n(m)}{m}=c
for some
c>0
and
\bm{\Delta}\neq\mathbf{0}
. Then, it holds that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathrm{MSE}\left(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{p}}\right)>0.

The proof of
proposition

4.1
is provided in App.
A.1
. We note, however, that this does not happen for a vanishing amount of observational data, that is
\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{n(m)}{m}=0
(see Prop. 4.2 in App.
A.2
.

Ridge Regression.

The ridge regression estimator on the interventional data, which shrinks
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}
towards the origin (see
section

2
and
fig.

2
),
is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\text{ridge}}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{i}}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}_{\text{ridge}}\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}+(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}_{\text{ridge}})\mathbf{0},

where

\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}_{\text{ridge}}\coloneqq(\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}.

(12)

Hence,
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\text{ridge}}
can also be seen as a special case of a matrix weighted estimator with no observational data and
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}=\mathbf{0}
.
Further, comparing
eqs.

11
and
12
suggest an interpretation of
ridge regression
as a
poor man’s
data pooling since access to observational data is replaced by a positive definite data matrix
\lambda\mathbf{I}_{p}
.
However,
\lambda
is a constant, and therefore
\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathrm{MSE}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\text{ridge}})=0
even in the setting of
proposition

4.1
, which contrasts data pooling.

4.2
Optimal Weighting Schemes

We now establish theoretically optimal weighting schemes that minimize the mean squared error of
\mathbf{W}
-weighted linear estimators
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\mathbf{W}}
for different classes of weight matrices
\mathbf{W}
by exploiting the specific structure of our problem setting (
section

3
).

Optimal Scalar Weight.

First, we consider the special case of scalar estimator weighting by considering weight matrices of the form
\mathbf{W}=w\mathbf{I}_{p}

with weight
w\in[0,1]
.
The optimal scalar weight is then derived as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\mathrm{MSE}\left(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{w\mathbf{I}_{p}}\right)\overset{!}{=}0

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:bias_variance}}}{{\iff}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\left(\left\lVert\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{w\mathbf{I}_{p}}-\bm{\alpha}]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{Cov}\left(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{w\mathbf{I}_{p}}\right)\right)\right)\overset{!}{=}0

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle w^{m}_{*}=\frac{\text{Tr}(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}))+\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\text{Tr}(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}))+\text{Tr}(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}))+\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}.

Optimal Diagonal Weight Matrix.

A more general case is to weigh each dimension individually by different scalars

w^{(k)}\in[0,1],\;k=1,...,p
, corresponding to a weight matrix of the form
\mathbf{W}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{w})
.
The optimal diagonal weighting
\text{diag}(\mathbf{w}^{m}_{*}
) is then given by

w_{*}^{m(k)}=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}^{(k,k)}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})+\Delta^{(k)\,2}}{\mathrm{Cov}^{(k,k)}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}})+\mathrm{Cov}^{(k,k)}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})+\Delta^{(k)\,2}}\,,

for
k=1,...,p
.
The derivation is analogous to that for the optimal scalar weight above, with the only difference being that we optimize over each dimension separately.

Optimal Weight Matrix.

Finally, we can also determine the optimum weighting as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{W}^{m}_{*}=&\left(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})+\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Delta}^{\top}\right)\\
&\left(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}})+\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})+\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Delta}^{\top}\right)^{-1}.\end{split}

(13)

A thorough derivation of the proposed weighting schemes can be found in App.
C
. In addition, we elaborate on how this weighting scheme handles sample imbalance in App.
E
.

Remark 4.2
.

If (i)
\bm{\Delta}=\mathbf{0}
and (ii)
\sigma^{2}_{Y|\mathbf{X}}=\sigma^{2}_{Y|\text{do}(\mathbf{X})}
, then
\mathbf{W}^{m}_{*}=\mathbf{W}^{m}_{\textsc{p}}
, i.e., data pooling corresponds to weighing with the optimal weight matrix under these two assumptions.

Remark

4.2
can be verified by simplifying
eq.

13
with assumptions (i) and (ii) and comparing to
eq.

11
. It agrees with our intuition: Ordinary least squares relies on the assumption that
\mathbb{E}[Y|\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}_{i}]=\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}
with equal variance, for all
i
. Thus, data pooling recovers the optimal estimator if these assumptions are true, i.e., the two conditional distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\text{obs}}(Y|X)
and
\mathbb{P}_{\text{int}}(Y|\mathrm{do}(X))
are identical. However, in general, they will not be identical and data pooling then amounts to model misspecification. This is likely to result in a non-vanishing mean squared error for
m\rightarrow\infty
as highlighted in
proposition

4.1
.

4.3
Practical Estimators

Unfortunately, the optimal weighting derived in
eq.

13
cannot be implemented directly, since the quantities

\bm{\Delta}
,
\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})
, and
\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}})
are unknown in practice.
To construct practical estimators informed by our theoretical insights, one option is thus to
rely on plug-in estimates of these unknown quantities.
For
\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}})
and
\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})
, we use the standard estimators

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{Cov}}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}})^{-1}\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{Y|\mathrm{do}(\mathbf{X})},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{Cov}}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}})^{-1}\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{Y|\mathbf{X}},

which replace the conditional variances in
eq.

9
by

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{Y|\mathrm{do}(\mathbf{X})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m-1}\left\lVert\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{i}}-\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{Y|\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-1}\left\lVert\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{o}}-\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}}\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

For
\bm{\Delta}
, one may consider using the unbiased estimator

\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}_{m}=\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}-\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}.

(14)

Substituting these into
eq.

13
then yields:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}_{*}=&\left(\widehat{\mathbf{Cov}}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})+\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}_{m}\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}_{m}^{\top}+\epsilon\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)\\
&\left(\widehat{\mathbf{Cov}}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}})+\widehat{\mathbf{Cov}}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})+\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}_{m}\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}_{m}^{\top}+\epsilon\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1}.\end{split}

(15)

The regularization with
\epsilon>0
ensures that the inverse remains stable even in the large sample limit where
\widehat{\mathbf{Cov}}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})
and
\widehat{\mathbf{Cov}}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}})
tend to zero. The reason for instability without such regularization is that
\mathbf{W}^{m}_{*}
is not uniquely defined in the infinite sample limit. With regularization, however, we can guarantee that
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}_{*}
converges to
\mathbf{I}_{p}
almost surely.

Proposition 4.3

(Weight Matrix Convergence)
.

Let
\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{n(m)}{m}=c
, for some constant
c>0
. Then,

\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}_{*}
from
eq.

15
converges almost surely to
\mathbf{I}_{p}
, i.e.,
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}_{*}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\mathbf{I}_{p}
.

The proof for
proposition

4.3
is included in App.
A.3
. We can show that this convergence
implies that the mean squared error vanishes asymptotically.

Theorem 4.4

(Zero Mean Squared Error in the Sample Limit)
.

Let
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}
be any sequence of random weight matrices such that
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\mathbf{I}_{p}
and
\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{n(m)}{m}=c
for some constant
c>0
.
Then,

\underset{m\rightarrow\infty}{\text{lim}}\;\mathrm{MSE}\left(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}}\right)=0,

where
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}}
denotes the matrix-weighted linear estimator with weight matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}
, as defined in
definition

4.1
.

The proof of Thm.
4.4
is included in App.
A.4
.

Theorem

4.4
has the following relevant implication:
we can incorporate an arbitrarily large amount of biased observational data and are still guaranteed that the bias (and also variance) of
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}_{*}}
will vanish in the infinite sample limit. Moreover,
this guarantee is independent of
\bm{\Delta}
and
|\sigma_{Y|\mathbf{X}}^{2}-\sigma_{Y|\mathrm{do}(\mathbf{X})}^{2}|
.

We also note that
Theorem

4.4
does not imply unbiasedness of
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}_{*}}
for any finite sample size.

Further, we note
that almost sure convergence of
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}
to
\mathbf{I}_{p}
may generally not
be the only option to achieve vanishing mean squared error.
For example, if
\bm{\Delta}=\mathbf{0}
such that
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}
is unbiased, we also obtain vanishing mean squared error for almost sure convergence of
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}
to
\mathbf{0}
.

4.4
Suitable Inductive Biases

Despite the desirable performance established in
theorem

4.4
, the
plug-in estimates from
section

4.3
will often not perform very well in finite sample settings. The main issue is the estimation of
\bm{\Delta}
, which has a large variance when done according to
eq.

14
.
To see this, we first note that

\text{Tr}(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}_{m}))=\text{Tr}(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}))+\text{Tr}(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})),

(16)

since the observational and interventional data are independent.
Now, if we only have a small interventional sample (as is typically the case),

\text{Tr}(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}))
and hence according to
eq.

16
also
\text{Tr}(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}_{m}))
will be large.

We therefore explore possible
inductive biases in the form of additional assumptions on the type of confounding that lead to reduced variance when estimating
\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}_{m}
. These inductive biases can be motivated from domain knowledge and validation techniques such as cross-validation
(
Schaffer 1993
)
. Specifically, the application itself may provide some prior knowledge about the nature of confounding, which can then be confirmed by a better validation score compared to
the other inductive biases/methods proposed here.

To this end, we observe that
eq.

14
can be written
as the solution of the following two-step ordinary least squares procedure:

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\;\mathrm{arg}\;\min_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\;\left\{\left\lVert\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{o}}\;-\;\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}}\bm{\alpha}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\;\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{i}}-\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}_{m}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\;\mathrm{arg}\;\min_{\bm{\Delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\;\left\{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}\;+\;\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(17)

Small
\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{2}
.

In some settings, we may be willing to assume that, despite the existence of unobserved confounders, the resulting confounding bias is rather weak, i.e., that its Euclidean norm
\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{2}
is small.
Since this is precisely the assumption underlying ridge regression,
we reformulate (
17
) using a regularizer
\lambda_{\ell^{2}}>0
as

\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}_{m}^{\ell^{2}}\;\leftarrow\;\mathrm{arg}\;\min_{\bm{\Delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\;\left\{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}\;+\;\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\;+\;\lambda_{\ell^{2}}\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right\},

for which a closed-form solution of the same computational complexity as least squares exists.
We refer to the weight matrix estimate obtained by using
\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}_{m}^{\ell^{2}}
in place of
\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}_{m}
in
eq.

15
as
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}_{\ell^{2}}
. By
proposition

4.5
, we still obtain the same limiting guarantees of
theorem

4.4
for
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}_{\ell^{2}}
, as long as
\lambda_{\ell^{2}}
is fixed (
\lambda_{\ell^{2}}
is independent of
m
,
\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{p}}
,
\mathbf{y}_{\textsc{p}}
).

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{n(m)}{m}=c
and
\lambda_{\ell^{2}}>0
be fixed. Then,

\underset{m\rightarrow\infty}{\text{lim}}\;\mathrm{MSE}\left(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}_{\ell^{2}}}\right)=0.

The proof for Proposition
4.5
is given in App.
A.5
.

Small
\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{0}
.

In other settings, we may have prior beliefs that only some treatment variables
X_{i}
are confounded, i.e., that the number of nonzero elements of
\bm{\Delta}
, denoted by
\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{0}
, is small.
If we are unaware of
which treatments are confounded, but

p
is small, we can simply fit all
2^{p}
possible models or use best subset selection
(
James et al. 2013
, p. 205)
. For larger
p
, a more efficient technique known as the LASSO employs
\ell^{1}
-regularization and has become a standard tool
(
Tibshirani 1996
)
. For the LASSO, approximate optimization techniques exist that have a computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}(p^{2}n)

(
Efron et al. 2004
)
, which is of the same order as ordinary least squares. In this case, we reformulate
(
17
) as

\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}_{m}^{\ell^{1}}\;\leftarrow\;\mathrm{arg}\;\min_{\bm{\Delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\;\left\{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}\;+\;\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\;+\;\lambda_{\ell^{1}}\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{1}\right\},

for some
\lambda_{\ell^{1}}>0
, and where
\left\lVert\;\cdot\;\right\rVert_{1}
denotes the
\ell^{1}
-norm. We refer to the weight matrix obtained by using
\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}_{m}^{\ell^{1}}
in place of
\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}_{m}
in
eq.

15
as
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}_{\ell^{1}}
.

Compared Methods.

We report the mean squared error attained by the theoretically optimal weight matrix
\mathbf{W}^{m}_{*}
from (

) as an oracle, as well as the plug-in estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}_{*}

), and the regularized regression-based
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}_{\ell^{2}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{m}_{\ell^{1}}
from

4.4
.
For the latter two, we choose the regularization hyperparameters
\lambda_{\ell^{2}}
and

by cross-validation on the interventional data.

\mathbf{W}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}=\mathbf{I}_{p}
) and data pooling according to
\mathbf{W}^{m}_{\textsc{p}}
from (
11
).
We also compare to the
Rosenman et al. 2020

\mathbf{W}=\widehat{w}^{m}_{\text{rm}}\mathbf{I}_{p}
with

\widehat{w}^{m}_{\text{rm}}\;:=\;\max\left\{1\;-\;\frac{\text{Tr}\left(\widehat{\textbf{Cov}}\left(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}\right)\right)}{\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}-\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}},\;0\right\}.

(

)
are not applicable,
because they require the relevant confounders to be observed, which is not the case in our setting.

General Setup.

In all experiments, we use
p=30
treatments, a one-dimensional (
d=1
) confounder
Z
, and unit/isotropic (co)variances:
\sigma_{N_{Y}}^{2}=\sigma_{N_{Z}}^{2}=1
,
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{X}}}=\mathbf{I}_{p}
. We sample
\tilde{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathbf{X}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{Cov}(\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{o}}))
,

\bm{\alpha}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},9\mathbf{I}_{p})
, and choose
\mathbf{b}
and
\gamma
depending on the settings described below.
Unless otherwise specified, we then draw
m=300
interventional and
n=600
observational examples from
\mathbb{P}_{\text{int}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\text{obs}}
, respectively, and compute estimates of
\bm{\alpha}
using the different weighting approaches. We repeat this procedure
1000
times and report the resulting mean and standard deviation of the mean squared error.

Different Types of Confounding.

In our main experiment, we investigate how estimators perform under different types of confounding encoded by (
2
) and (
3
), specifically by the parameters
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
(for a scalar confounder
Z
).
For
spread
confounding, we sample
\mathbf{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{p})
such that the confounder affects all treatment variables almost surely.
For
sparse
confounding, we sample
b^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
for
k=1,...,5
, and
b^{(k)}=0
otherwise, such that only the first five treatments are confounded.
In both cases, we investigate
\gamma\in\{1,5\}
which controls the strength of
Z\to\penalty\ Y
and thus the extent to which
\bm{\Delta}=0
is violated.

The results are presented in

.

. Data pooling works relatively well when

do not perform very well in this finite sample setting due to high variance, as explained in

.

6
Discussion

Connection to Transfer Learning.

Our setting bears resemblance to transfer and multi-task learning
(
Thrun 1995
;
Caruana 1997
)
, specifically to supervised domain adaptation, which aims to leverage knowledge from a source domain to improve a model in a target domain, for which typically much less data is available.
In our case, we aim to use the source model
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}
, learned by estimating
\mathbb{E}[Y|\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}]
in the observational setting, to improve our (high-variance) target model
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}
of
\mathbb{E}[Y|\text{do}(\mathbf{X}\leftarrow\mathbf{x})]
.
Transfer learning
can only work if the domains are sufficiently similar, resulting in numerous approaches leveraging different assumptions about shared components
(
Quiñonero-Candela et al. 2008
)
. These assumptions are often phrased in causal terms
(
Schölkopf et al. 2012
;
Zhang et al. 2013
;
Gong et al. 2016
;
Rojas-Carulla et al. 2018
)
.
Similarly, our observational (source) and interventional (target) domains share the same causal model and only differ in the treatment assignment mechanisms (
2
) and (
4
). Still, the bias in (
6
) can in theory be arbitrary large, and our methods from
section

4.4
implicitly rely on it being small or sparse.

Beyond Linear Regression.

Some of our derivations and theoretical results rely on the fact that the confounding bias in (
5
) is linear in
\mathbf{x}
.
For the class of
linear
SCMs (
1
)–(
3
), Gaussianity is necessary and sufficient
3
3

3

Note
\mathbb{E}[Y|\mathbf{X}]=\bm{\gamma}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}|\mathbf{X}]+\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}|\mathbf{X}]
is linear in
\mathbf{X}
only in the Gaussian case
(
Peters et al. 2017
, Thm. 4.2)
.
for this condition to hold, but it may also hold for more general classes of SCMs.
For binary treatments
\mathbf{X}\in\{0,1\}^{p}
, in particular, it is always possible to write the difference between the biased and unbiased average treatment effect estimates using a constant offset
\bm{\Delta}
akin to (
14
), irrespective of the confounding relationship.
4
4

4

Specifically, we have
\bm{\Delta}=\mathbb{E}[Y|\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{1}]-\mathbb{E}[Y|\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{0}]-\left(\mathbb{E}[Y|\text{do}(\mathbf{X}\leftarrow\mathbf{1})]-\mathbb{E}[Y|\text{do}(\mathbf{X}\leftarrow\mathbf{0})]\right)
.

Future work may thus investigate nonlinear extensions, e.g., by drawing inspiration from semi-parametrics
(
Robins and Rotnitzky 1995
)
, doubly robust estimation
(
Bang and Robins 2005
)
, and debiased machine learning
(
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
)
.

Incorporating Covariates.

Our current formulation does not
explicitly
account for
observed confounders, or pre-treatment covariates, which need to be adjusted for in the observational setting to avoid introducing further bias.
In principle, such covariates can simply be included in
\mathbf{X}
, as different treatment components
X_{i}
are allowed to be dependent.
However, this may result in high-dimensional treatments and thus render full randomization in (
4
) unrealistic.
Other covariates, while unproblematic with regard to bias, may help further reduce variance
(
Henckel et al. 2022
)
.
Extending our framework to incorporate different types of covariates is thus a worthwhile future direction.

7
Conclusion

In the present work, we have introduced a new class of matrix weighted linear estimators for learning causal effects of continuous treatments from finite observational and interventional data.
Here, our focus has been on optimizing statistical efficiency, which complements the vast causal inference literature on identification from heterogeneous data.
Our estimators are connected to classical ideas from shrinkage estimation applied to causal learning and provide a unifying account of data pooling and ridge regression, which emerge as special cases.
We show that our estimators are theoretically grounded and compare favorably to baselines and prior work in simulations.
While we restricted our analysis to linear models for now, we hope that the insights and methods developed here will also be useful for a broader class of causal models and transfer learning problems.

In general, we observe that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Bias}}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\mathbf{W}})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}\bm{\alpha}+(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W})(\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\Delta})-\bm{\alpha}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W})\bm{\Delta},

\displaystyle\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\mathbf{W}})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}})\mathbf{W}^{\top}+(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W})\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W})^{\top}.

C.1
Optimal Scalar Weight

Here, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\mathrm{MSE}\left(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{w\mathbf{I}_{p}}\right)

\displaystyle=\quad

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\Big|\Big|{\mathbf{Bias}}\left(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{w\mathbf{I}_{p}}\right)\Big|\Big|_{2}^{2}\;+\;\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{Cov}\left(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{w\mathbf{I}_{p}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\quad

\displaystyle-2(1-w)||\bm{\Delta}||_{2}^{2}+2w\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}})\right)-2(1-w)\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})\right)\overset{!}{=}0.

By rearranging, we get

w^{m}_{*}=\frac{\text{Tr}(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}))+\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\text{Tr}(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}))+\text{Tr}(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}))+\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}.

C.2
Optimal Diagonal Weight Matrix

Here, we see that the objective decouples into a sum over the individual dimensions

\mathrm{MSE}\left(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{w\mathbf{I}_{p}}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\left(1-w^{(k)}\right)^{2}\bm{\Delta}^{(k)\,2}\;+\;w^{(k)\,2}\mathbf{Cov}^{(k,k)}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}})+\left(1-w^{(k)}\right)^{2}\mathbf{Cov}^{(k,k)}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}).

Thus, we optimize for each dimension
k
separately and obtain

w_{*}^{m(k)}=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}^{(k,k)}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})+\Delta^{(k)\,2}}{\mathrm{Cov}^{(k,k)}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}})+\mathrm{Cov}^{(k,k)}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})+\Delta^{(k)\,2}}.

C.3
Optimal Weight Matrix

Using
\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{W}}\text{Tr}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}^{\top})=2\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}
, since
\mathbf{A}
is symmetric, we observe that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{W}}\mathrm{MSE}\left(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\mathbf{W}}\right)

\displaystyle=\quad

\displaystyle 2\mathbf{W}\left(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}})+\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})+\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Delta}^{\top}\right)-2\left(\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Delta}^{\top}+\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})\right)

\displaystyle\overset{!}{=}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}.

We see that this minimum is attained for

\left(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})+\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Delta}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}})+\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}})+\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Delta}^{\top}\right)^{-1}.

D
Non Zero-Mean Exogenous Variables

All results established here can readily be extended to settings, where any of the exogenous variables have non-zero mean, i.e.,
\bm{\mu}_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{X}}}
,
\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathbf{X}}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathbf{X}}]
,
\bm{\mu}_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{Z}}}
,
\mu_{N_{Y}}
(see (
1
)–(
3
)) may be non-zero. In order to extend the practical estimators introduced here, one needs to consider the following two pre-processing steps:

First, we center both treatment distributions separately, without scaling:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}\;\leftarrow\;\mathbf{x}_{i}-n^{-1}\sum_{j\in 1,...,n}\mathbf{x}_{j},\quad

\displaystyle\forall i\in 1,...,n,

(J)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}\;\leftarrow\;\mathbf{x}_{i}-m^{-1}\sum_{j\in n+1,...,n+m}\mathbf{x}_{j},\quad

\displaystyle\forall i\in n+1,...,n+m.

(K)

In this manner, both treatment variables become zero-mean.

Furthermore, we add a dummy dimension with value one to all treatment vectors:

\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime}_{i}\;\leftarrow\;(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i},\;1),\quad\forall i\in 1,...,n+m.

This naturally adds one more dimension also to
\bm{\alpha}
, which corresponds to the intercept term. We then use the constructed
\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime}_{i}
to compute the weight matrices proposed in this work.

Finally, we see that the intercept term must be identical for both distributions, interventional and observational:

\mathbb{E}[Y\;|\;\mathbf{X}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}]\;=\;\bm{\gamma}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}\;|\;\mathbf{X}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}]+\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\mu_{N_{Y}}.

We then have in the observational setting (data points
1,...,n
) that

\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}\;|\;\mathbf{X}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\bm{\gamma}^{\top}\bm{\mu}_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{Z}}}+\bm{\gamma}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{Z}}}\mathbf{B}^{\top}(\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{X}}}+\mathbf{B}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{Z}}}\mathbf{B}^{\top})^{-1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}^{\prime}])

\displaystyle=\bm{\gamma}^{\top}\bm{\mu}_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{Z}}}+\bm{\Delta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{\prime},

where
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}^{\prime}]=\mathbf{0}
due to (
J
).

For the interventional data, we have independence between
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{Z}
by definition and so we trivially get

\bm{\gamma}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}\;|\;\mathbf{X}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}]=\bm{\gamma}^{\top}\bm{\mu}_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{Z}}}

here. Thus, the intercept is
\bm{\gamma}^{\top}\bm{\mu}_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{Z}}}+\mu_{N_{Y}}
for both distributions and we fix
\hat{\Delta}^{(p+1)}=0
.

E
Sample Imbalance

We see that the ground truth covariance matrices of
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}}
and
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}
adapt to changes in the sample sizes, keeping the distributions of all variables fixed. For instance, we see that

\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}})=(\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}})^{-1}\sigma_{Y|\text{do}(X)}^{2}=m^{-1}(m^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}})^{-1}\sigma_{Y|\text{do}(X)}^{2}.

The term
(m^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\textsc{i}})^{-1}\sigma_{Y|\text{do}(X)}^{2}
is bounded in probability, for large enough
m
. Accordingly, this implies that
\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}})\xrightarrow{\text{P}}\mathbf{0}
. Thus, when keeping
n
fixed, we obtain
\mathbf{W}^{m}_{*}\xrightarrow{\text{P}}\mathbf{I}_{p}
, for
m\rightarrow\infty
.

On the other hand, if we keep
m
fixed and consider the limit
n\rightarrow\infty
instead, we observe that

\mathbf{W}^{m}_{*}\xrightarrow{\text{P}}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Delta}^{\top}(\mathbf{Cov}(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{m}_{\textsc{i}})+\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Delta}^{\top})^{-1}.

We note that we do not have
\mathbf{W}^{m}_{*}\xrightarrow{\text{P}}\mathbf{0}
here in general, because the bias in
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}_{\textsc{o}}
remains, independent of the sample size
n
.
