Robust Stutter Bisimulation for Abstraction and Controller Synthesis with Disturbance: Proofs wasp
Jonas Krook *cor, Robi Malik, Sahar Mohajerani, Martin Fabian

Abstract
This paper proposes a method to synthesise controllers for cyber-physical systems such that the controlled systems satisfy specifications given as linear temporal logic formulas. The focus is on systems with disturbance , where future states cannot be predicted exactly due to uncertainty in the environment. The approach used to solve this problem is to first construct a finite-state abstraction of the original system and then synthesise a controller for the abstract system. For this approach, the robust stutter bisimulation relation is introduced, which preserves the existence of controllers for any given linear temporal logic formula. States are related by the robust stutter bisimulation relation if the same target sets can be guaranteed to be reached or avoided under control of some controllers, thereby ensuring that disturbances have similar effect on paths that start in related states. This paper presents an algorithm to construct the corresponding robust stutter bisimulation quotient to solve the abstraction problem, and it is shown, by explicit construction, that there exists a controller enforcing a linear temporal logic formula for the original system if and only if a corresponding controller exists for the quotient system. Lastly, the result of the algorithm and the controller construction are demonstrated by application to an example of robot navigation.

Introduction

Cyber-physical systems
consist of physical systems and digital computers affecting and interacting with each other
[
Lee 2015
]
. This can be a continuous time dynamical system subject to control inputs and process disturbances, which is under control of a computer with a fixed sampling time. Cyber-physical systems are often safety critical, thus it is highly desirable to have correctness guarantees.
One way to achieve such guarantees is to use
formal synthesis
to construct the control logic automatically
[
Belta et al. 2017
]
.

Formal synthesis uses a model of the cyber-physical system and a
formal specification
to compute the allowed control actions such that the behaviour of the controlled system satisfies the specification. The specification is a formalisation of the requirements, and it can be expressed in, for instance,
Linear Temporal Logic
(
\mathrm{LTL}
,
Baier and Katoen 2008
).
\mathrm{LTL}
extends propositional logic with temporal operators with which requirements on future behaviour can be specified.

Standard algorithms
[
Ramadge 1989
,
Kloetzer and Belta 2008
,
Belta et al. 2017
]

for controller synthesis with
\mathrm{LTL}
specifications require finite transition systems, whereas many cyber-physical systems are described by continuous models with an infinite state space. Such models can be turned into transition systems by
discretisation
, but in general neither the state space nor the transition relation of the resulting
concrete
transition system are finite
[
Tabuada 2009
]
. One approach to reduce the size of the state space is by attempting to form a finite-state
abstraction
by grouping states into a finite
quotient
state space. One such abstraction method is
bisimulation

[
Milner 1989
]
. It preserves all
\mathrm{LTL}
properties
[
Baier and Katoen 2008
]
, and is guaranteed to produce finite quotient spaces for certain types of systems
[
Alur et al. 2000
]
.

If the bisimulation quotient of a system is infinite, a finite quotient space might be obtained by using a coarser abstraction. One approach to obtain coarser quotients is
approximate
bisimulation where bisimulation is relaxed to allow a bounded difference between the behaviours of the concrete and abstract system
[
Girard and Pappas 2007
]
. Coarser quotients also result from (approximate)
simulation

[
Tabuada 2006
,
Zamani et al. 2011
,
Belta et al. 2017
,
Reissig et al. 2016
]
, which relaxes bisimulation by retaining only some controlled behaviours of the concrete system to the abstract system.

Dual-simulation

[
Wagenmaker and Ozay 2016
]
produces a coarser abstraction than bisimulation by using overlapping subsets.
Another coarse abstraction is obtained by
divergent stutter bisimulation
which allows
stutter steps
within the abstract states
[
Baier and Katoen 2008
]
.
Mohajerani et al. 2021
show that divergent stutter bisimulation yields abstractions for which a controller can be synthesised if and only if a controller can be synthesised for the concrete system, given that the synthesis is performed with
\mathrm{LTL}
specifications without the next operator (
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
). Omitting this operator means that specifications cannot refer to specific time intervals, but this is not always necessary for formalising the requirements.
All the above work applies to
deterministic
transition systems and does not work for systems that are subject to process disturbances.

Many concrete systems are subject to disturbances that either stem from the system dynamics or fidelity loss in the discretisation. Abstraction methods for controller synthesis for non-deterministic transition systems have been considered before. In the work by
Liu and Ozay 2016
,
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
is used as the specification formalism, and the abstract transition systems are constructed from continuous-time dynamical systems subject to disturbances.
Nilsson et al. 2012
considers abstractions of discrete-time systems subject to disturbances where
N
-step reachability is the basis for abstraction. However, for these approaches, controllers that exist in the concrete system might not have a corresponding controller in the abstract system.
Another abstraction approach is (approximate)
alternating bisimulation

[
Alur et al. 1998
,
Pola and Tabuada 2009
]
which is an extension of (approximate) bisimulation applicable for synthesis with
\mathrm{LTL}
specifications on non-deterministic systems.

This paper addresses the problem of designing a controller for a given non-deterministic concrete transition system and an
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
specification through an abstraction method that guarantees that a controller can be synthesised for the concrete system if and only if a controller can be synthesised for the abstract system.
By only considering
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
specifications, it is possible to construct coarser abstractions compared to existing work.
The abstractions are based on the
robust stutter bisimulation
relation introduced in this paper, which
extends divergent stutter
bisimulation to non-deterministic systems, and relaxes alternating
bisimulation to
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
specifications. As such, this work can be seen as a
combination of the works by
Mohajerani et al. 2021
and

Pola and Tabuada 2009
. It is shown how an abstract transition system can be
constructed based on robust stutter bisimulation, and how this abstraction is used to synthesise
and implement a controller for the concrete system.

In the following, Section
2
introduces notations of
transitions systems and linear temporal logic.
Section
3
gives an overview of the proposed steps for
controller synthesis by abstraction, which are detailed in the
following sections
4
–
7
.
Afterwards, Section
8
applies to idea to an example,
and Section
9
adds concluding remarks.
Formal proofs of all results can be found in
the appendix.

Preliminaries

2.1
Transition systems

Transition systems describe how systems or processes evolve from one state
to the next.
The states are labelled with atomic propositions, which can be used by
formulas to refer to specific states or groups of states.

Definition 1
.

A
transition system
is a tuple
G=\langle S,\penalty\Sigma,\penalty\delta,\penalty S^{\circ},\penalty{\mathord{\mathrm{AP}}},\penalty L\rangle
where

•

S
is a set of
states
;

•

\Sigma
is a set of
transition labels
;

•

\delta\subseteq S\times\Sigma\times S
is a

transition relation
;

•

S^{\circ}\subseteq S
is a set of
initial states
;

•

\mathord{\mathrm{AP}}
is a set of
atomic propositions
;

•

L\colon S\to 2^{\mathord{\mathrm{AP}}}
is a
state labelling function
.

A system modelled by a difference equation

x(t+\Delta t)=g(x(t),u(t),w(t))

(1)

where
x\in\mathcal{X}
is the state,
u\in\mathcal{U}
is the control input, and
w\in\mathcal{W}
is the disturbance, can be represented as a transition system
G=\langle S,\penalty\Sigma,\penalty\delta,\penalty S^{\circ},\penalty{\mathord{\mathrm{AP}}},\penalty L\rangle
with state space
S=\mathcal{X}
and transition labels
\Sigma=\mathcal{U}

[
Tabuada 2009
]
. If for
u\in\mathcal{U}
, there exists
w\in\mathcal{W}
such that
s_{1}=g(s_{0},u,w)
, then there is transition
(s_{0},u,s_{1})\in\delta
.

Sequences of states of a transition system are used to represent traversals of the transition system.
S^{*}
and
S^{\omega}
denote the sets of all finite and infinite sequences of elements of
S
, respectively, and
S^{\infty}=S^{*}\cup\mkern 1.5muS^{\omega}
. The set of non-empty finite sequences is denoted by
S^{+}=S^{*}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
, where
\varepsilon
is the empty sequence. Two sequences
\rho\in S^{*}
and
\pi\in S^{\infty}
can be concatenated to form a new sequence
\rho\pi\in S^{\infty}
.
A finite sequence
\rho\in S^{*}
is a
prefix
of
\pi\in S^{\infty}
, written
\rho\sqsubseteq\pi
, if there exists a sequence
\pi^{\prime}\in S^{\infty}
such that
\rho\pi^{\prime}=\pi
, and
\rho
is a
proper prefix
of
\pi
, written
\rho\sqsubset\pi
, if
\rho\sqsubseteq\pi
and
\rho\neq\pi
.
To be a traversal of a transition system, a sequence of states must be allowed by the transition relation:

Definition 2
.

Let
G
be a transition system.
A finite sequence of states
\rho=s_{0}\cdots s_{n}\in S^{*}
is a
finite path fragment
of
G
if for all
0\leq i<n
there exists
\sigma_{i}\in\Sigma
such that
(s_{i},\sigma_{i},s_{i+1})\in\delta
. The set of all finite path fragments of
G
is denoted
\mathord{\mathrm{Frags}}^{*}(G)
.
An infinite sequence of states
\pi\in S^{\omega}
is an
infinite path fragment
of
G
if for all finite prefixes
\rho\sqsubset\pi
it holds that
\rho\in\mathord{\mathrm{Frags}}^{*}(G)
. The set of all infinite path fragments of
G
is denoted
\mathord{\mathrm{Frags}}^{\omega}(G)
.
The set of all path fragments of
G
is
\mathord{\mathrm{Frags}}^{\infty}(G)=\mathord{\mathrm{Frags}}^{*}(G)\cup\mathord{\mathrm{Frags}}^{\omega}(G)
.
A
path
of
G
is an infinite path fragment
\pi=s_{0}s_{1}\cdots\in\mathord{\mathrm{Frags}}^{\omega}(G)
with
s_{0}\in S^{\circ}
. The set of all paths of
G
is denoted
\mathord{\mathrm{Paths}^{\omega}}(G)
.

That is, path fragments can start in any state of the transition system, whereas paths start in an initial state.

An important property of transition systems is
deadlock freedom
, which ensures that progress is possible from every state.

Definition 3
.

A transition system
G=\langle S,\penalty\Sigma,\penalty\delta,\penalty S^{\circ},\penalty{\mathord{\mathrm{AP}}},\penalty L\rangle
is
deadlock-free
if for each
state
s\in S
there exists a label
\sigma\in\Sigma
and a state
t\in S
such that
(s,\sigma,t)\in\delta
.

In a deadlock-free system, every finite path fragment can be extended infinitely.
If a system is not deadlock-free, it contains deadlock states from where no
further transitions are possible.

2.2
Relations

Given a set
X
, a relation
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}\subseteq X\times X
is an
equivalence relation
on
X
if it is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. The
equivalence class
of
x\in X
is
[x]_{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}=\{\,x^{\prime}\in X\mid(x,x^{\prime})\in{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}\,\}
. The set of all equivalence classes modulo
\mathord{\mathcal{R}}
, the
quotient space
of
X
under
\mathord{\mathcal{R}}
, is
X/{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}=\{\,[x]_{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}\mid x\in X\,\}
.
Such partitioning into equivalence classes is one way to abstract the state set of a transition system, where in the abstract system each equivalence class is one state.
A relation
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{1}
is a
refinement
of a relation
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{2}
if
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{2}
. In this case,
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{1}
is said to be
finer
than
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{2}
, and
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{2}
is
coarser
than
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{1}
.

Definition 4
.

Let
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}\subseteq X\times X
be an equivalence relation. A set
T\subseteq X
is a
superblock
of
\mathord{\mathcal{R}}
, if for all
x_{1}\in T
and all
x_{2}\in X
such that
(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}
, it holds that
x_{2}\in T
. The set of all superblocks of
\mathord{\mathcal{R}}
is denoted
\mathord{\mathrm{SB}}({\mathord{\mathcal{R}}})
.

Superblocks are sets of elements that are closed under the equivalence
relation
\mathord{\mathcal{R}}
. Alternatively, they can be characterised as unions of zero
or more equivalence classes. Note that the empty set also is a superblock.

Example 1
.

Let
X=\{1,2,3,4,5\}
and

\displaystyle{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}=\{

\displaystyle(1,1),(1,2),(1,3),(2,1),(2,2),(2,3),(3,1),

\displaystyle(3,2),(3,3),(4,4),(4,5),(5,4),(5,5)\}\ .

{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}
is a relation on
X
since
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}\subseteq X\times X
, and it is an equivalence relation since it is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.
The equivalence classes are
[1]_{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}=[2]_{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}=[3]_{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}=\{1,2,3\}
, and
[4]_{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}=[5]_{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}=\{4,5\}
. The
set of equivalence classes modulo
\mathord{\mathcal{R}}
is
X/{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}=\{[1]_{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}},\penalty[2]_{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}},\penalty[3]_{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}},\penalty[4]_{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}},\penalty[5]_{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}\}=\{\{1,2,3\},\penalty\{4,5\}\}
. The set of superblocks is
\mathord{\mathrm{SB}}({\mathord{\mathcal{R}}})=\{\emptyset,\penalty\{1,2,3\},\penalty\{4,5\},\penalty\{1,2,3,4,5\}\}
.∎

2.3
Linear Temporal Logic

A formula of
Linear Temporal Logic without Next
(
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
) is a logical formula consisting of atomic propositions from a set
{\mathord{\mathrm{AP}}}
, the propositional logic operators, and the binary operator
\mathrel{\mathcal{U\!}}
.
Its syntax is defined by
\varphi=\top\mid p\mid\lnot\psi\mid\psi\land\theta\mid\psi\mathrel{\mathcal{U\!}}\theta
, where
p\in{\mathord{\mathrm{AP}}}
, and
\psi
and
\theta
are
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
formulas.

Definition 5
.

Let
G=\langle S,\penalty\Sigma,\penalty\delta,\penalty S^{\circ},\penalty{\mathord{\mathrm{AP}}},\penalty L\rangle
. Let
\psi
and
\theta
be
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
formulas, and let
p\in{\mathord{\mathrm{AP}}}
be an atomic proposition. Whether a path fragment
\pi=s_{0}s_{1}\cdots\in\mathord{\mathrm{Frags}}^{\infty}(G)
satisfies the
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
formula
\varphi
, written
\pi\models\varphi
, is defined inductively on the structure of
\varphi
:

•

\pi\models\top
always holds;

•

\pi\models p
iff
p\in L(s_{0})
;

•

\pi\models\lnot\psi
iff
\pi\models\psi
does not hold;

•

\pi\models\psi\land\theta
iff
\pi\models\psi
and
\pi\models\theta
;

•

\pi\models\psi\mathrel{\mathcal{U\!}}\theta
iff there is
m\geq 0
such that
s_{m}s_{m+1}\cdots\models\theta
and for all
0\leq i<m
it holds that
s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots\models\psi
.

An
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
formula
\varphi
holds at state
s
, written
\langle G,s\rangle\models\varphi
, if all infinite path fragments starting at
s

satisfy
\varphi
, i.e,
\pi\models\varphi
for all infinite path
fragments
\pi\in\mathord{\mathrm{Frags}}^{\omega}(G)
with
s\sqsubset\pi
.
The transition system
G

satisfies
the

\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
formula
\varphi
, written
G\models\varphi
, if
\langle G,s^{\circ}\rangle\models\varphi
for all initial states
s^{\circ}\in S^{\circ}
.

The other propositional logic operators (
\lor
,
\rightarrow
,

\leftrightarrow
) can be defined based on
\top
,
\land
, and
\lnot
.
Some additional temporal operators can be defined based on the existing
ones by

\Diamond\psi\equiv\top\mathrel{\mathcal{U\!}}\psi
,

\square\psi\equiv\lnot\Diamond\lnot\psi
, and

\psi\mathrel{\mathcal{W}}\theta\equiv\square\psi\lor(\psi\mathrel{\mathcal{U\!}}\theta)
. The operators
\mathrel{\mathcal{U\!}}
and
\mathrel{\mathcal{W}}
, read as
(strong) until
and
weak until
, are of particular importance in this paper. If
\psi\mathrel{\mathcal{U\!}}\theta
is satisfied on a path fragment, then
\theta
eventually holds for some state in the path fragment, and
\psi
holds in all states before that.
\psi\mathrel{\mathcal{W}}\theta
is similar to
\psi\mathrel{\mathcal{U\!}}\theta
, but it is also satisfied on path fragments where
\psi
holds in all states.

\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
can be generalised to be defined over state sets
X\subseteq S
instead of only atomic propositions. For a path fragment
\pi=s_{0}s_{1}\cdots\in\mathord{\mathrm{Frags}}^{\infty}(G)
, let
\pi\models X
iff
s_{0}\in X
. Such
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
formulas will in the following be called
generalised

\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
formulas.

Definition 6
.

Let
G=\langle S,\penalty\Sigma,\penalty\delta,\penalty S^{\circ},\penalty{\mathord{\mathrm{AP}}},\penalty L\rangle
be a transition system, and let
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}\subseteq S\times S
be an equivalence relation. A
stutter step formula
for
G
is a generalised
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
formula of the form
P\mathrel{\mathcal{V}}T
, where
P,T\subseteq S
with
P\cap T=\emptyset
and
\mathrel{\mathcal{V}}
is either
\mathrel{\mathcal{U\!}}
or
\mathrel{\mathcal{W}}
.
If
P,T\in\mathord{\mathrm{SB}}({\mathord{\mathcal{R}}})
, then the formula
P\mathrel{\mathcal{V}}T
is also called
an
\mathord{\mathcal{R}}
-superblock step formula
from
P
.

Stutter step formulas
P\mathrel{\mathcal{V}}T
describe the immediate future when a system is in some state set
P
. The set
T
contains states that can be entered next from
P
. A stutter step formula of the form
P\mathrel{\mathcal{U\!}}T
means that a path visiting
P
will eventually reach
T
, whereas
P\mathrel{\mathcal{W}}T
means that it is possible to stay in
P
indefinitely or enter
T
. Superblock step formulas require the source and target sets
P
and
T
to be superblocks of an equivalence relation. They describe elementary steps of an abstract system whose states are equivalence classes.
The interest in stutter step formulas is because they make it possible to abstract away so-called
stutter steps
, which are transitions within an equivalence class.
Whether a path fragment satisfies an
\mathord{\mathcal{R}}
-superblock step formula is independent of the number of stutter steps. If
\mathord{\mathcal{R}}
preserves state labels, then it can also be shown that the satisfaction of
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
formulas is independent of such repeated steps.

Given a transition system
G=\langle S,\penalty\Sigma,\penalty\delta,\penalty S^{\circ},\penalty{\mathord{\mathrm{AP}}},\penalty L\rangle
and a sequence
\pi=s_{0}s_{1}\cdots\in\mathord{\mathrm{Frags}}^{\infty}
, the state labelling function
L
is extended to
\pi
by
L(\pi)=L(s_{0})L(s_{1})\cdots\in(2^{{\mathord{\mathrm{AP}}}})^{\infty}
. Moreover, the
stutter free
sequence
\mathrm{sf}(\pi)\in S^{\infty}
is obtained from
\pi
by removing all elements
s_{i+1}
such that
s_{i+1}=s_{i}
.

Definition 7
.

Let
G
be a transition system. Two path fragments
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\mathord{\mathrm{Frags}}^{\infty}(G)
are
stutter equivalent
if
\mathrm{sf}(L(\pi_{1}))=\mathrm{sf}(L(\pi_{2}))
and if
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are either both finite or both infinite.

Theorem 1

(
Baier and Katoen 2008
)
.

Let
G
be a transition system,
\varphi
an
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
formula, and let
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\mathord{\mathrm{Frags}}^{\omega}(G)
be two stutter equivalent path fragments. Then
\pi_{1}\models\varphi
iff
\pi_{2}\models\varphi
.

2.4
Controllers

Transition systems can be controlled by restricting the subset of transitions that are allowed next. A controller does this by deciding, based on the history of visited states, the set of allowed transition labels.

Definition 8
.

Let
G=\langle S,\penalty\Sigma,\penalty\delta,\penalty S^{\circ},\penalty{\mathord{\mathrm{AP}}},\penalty L\rangle
be a transition
system. A
general controller
, or simply
controller
, for
G

is a function
C:S^{+}\to 2^{\Sigma}
.

When a controller is applied to a transition system
G
, the resultant behaviour is described by another transition system, called the

controlled
system. As the controller has access to all historic states of the original transition system
G
, the states of the controlled system are sequences of states of
G
.

Definition 9
.

Let
G=\langle S,\penalty\Sigma,\penalty\delta,\penalty S^{\circ},\penalty{\mathord{\mathrm{AP}}},\penalty L\rangle
be a transition system, and let
C\colon S^{+}\to 2^{\Sigma}
be a controller for
G
. The
controlled system
is
C/G=\langle S^{+},\penalty\Sigma,\penalty\delta_{C},\penalty S^{\circ},\penalty{\mathord{\mathrm{AP}}},\penalty L_{C}\rangle
, where

\delta_{C}=\{\,\vtop{$(s_{0}\cdots s_{n},\sigma,s_{0}\cdots s_{n}s_{n+1})\in S^{+}\times\Sigma\times S^{+}$ $\mid$ $(s_{n},\sigma,s_{n+1})\in\delta$ and $\sigma\in C(s_{0}\cdots s_{n})$\,\lx@text@rbrace~.}

and
L_{C}(s_{0}\cdots s_{n})=L(s_{n})
.

The initial states of the controlled system
C/G
are the initial states of the original system
G
, interpreted as sequences of length one. After observing a state sequence
s_{0}\cdots s_{n}
,
the state of
G
is
s_{n}
and the state of
C/G
is
s_{0}\cdots s_{n}
.
The transitions from this state in
C/G
are those possible in
G
from
s_{n}
and allowed by
C
from
s_{0}\cdots s_{n}
.

Definition 10
.

A controller
C
for a transition system
G
is
deadlock-free
if

C/G
is deadlock-free.

Definition 11
.

Let
G
be a transition system.
A controller
C

enforces
a (generalised)

\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
-formula
\psi
from state
s
of
G
if
\langle C/G,s\rangle\models\psi
, and
C
enforces
\psi
on
G
if
C/G\models\psi
.

In other words, an
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
formula is enforced from a state if every controlled path fragment starting from that state satisfies the formula. The state in question may or may not be an initial state, as controllers may also be defined for path fragments that do not start with an initial state. An
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
formula is enforced on a transition system if it is enforced from every initial state.

The path fragments of a controlled system
C/G
are given by
\mathord{\mathrm{Frags}}^{\infty}(C/G)
, which by definition are sequences of states of
C/G
and thus sequences of sequences of states of
G
. The following definition of
permitted
path fragments,
\mathord{\mathrm{Frags}}^{\infty}(C,G)
, is used to replace such by simple sequences of states, projecting a path fragment
(s_{0})\penalty(s_{0}s_{1})\cdots\penalty(s_{0}s_{1}\cdots s_{n})\cdots
of the controlled system
C/G
to the path fragment
s_{0}s_{1}\cdots s_{n}\cdots
of
G
.

Definition 12
.

Let
G
be a transition system, and
let
C\colon S^{+}\to 2^{\Sigma}
be a controller for
G
.
A finite path fragment
\rho=s_{0}s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}\in\mathord{\mathrm{Frags}}^{*}(G)

is
permitted
by
C
if for all
0\leq i<n
there exists
\sigma_{i}\in C(s_{0}\cdots s_{i})
such that
(s_{i},\sigma_{i},s_{i+1})\in\delta
.
The set of all finite path fragments in
G
permitted by
C
is denoted by

Robust stutter bisimulation

This section defines the
robust stutter bisimulation

which identifies two transition systems as equivalent if the same

\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}

criterion to identify solutions to the
3

ensuring that any transition system
\widetilde{G}
that is robust stutter

G

thus a solution to the
3

The goal is to classify two states of a transition system as robust stutter bisimilar when the same
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
formulas can be enforced from both.
Due to the absence of the “next” operator, this condition can be
simplified by considering only stutter step formulas, or more precisely
superblock step formulas, as will be clear later. Given an equivalence class
P

formula
P\mathrel{\mathcal{U\!}}T
or
P\mathrel{\mathcal{W}}T
expresses that the system can
transition to other equivalence classes with or without the guarantee that this transition occurs eventually. The condition can be further simplified to superblock step formulas that can be enforced by positional controllers because of Prop.
2
.
Controllers for arbitrary
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
formulas can be constructed by combining positional controllers enforcing stutter step formulas from different states.

9
Conclusions

This paper proposes a method to synthesise controllers that enforce requirements specified in
\mathrm{LTL}_{\setminus\circ}
for cyber-physical systems subject to disturbances. This is done by constructing a finite-state abstraction of the system and then synthesising a controller for the abstract system. The
robust stutter bisimulation
relation is shown to characterise the relevant abstraction accurately, and it is shown how this relation can be used to construct a concrete controller. Although the main driver of this work is the ability to handle process noise, robust stutter bisimulation is more general and can be used on any system that can be represented as transition systems.

For future usage an efficient implementation of Algorithm
1
is important. As given here, every superblock step formula is checked whether it is a splitter, though some implied formulas would not have to be checked. The number of checks may be further reduced by considering what other formulas did not lead to a split.

This paper considers disturbances added to the state transitions, but for some applications there is considerable measurement noise that results in unobservable transitions. Future work might investigate how such disturbances can be incorporated in the abstraction.

Appendix A
Fixpoint characterisation of weak and strong until

Robust stutter bisimulations are closely linked to the existence of
positional controllers for stutter step formulas according to
Def.
16

(ii)
.
Therefore it is important for algorithms to compute the set

of states from which a stutter step formula

can

Such characterisations can be derived as fixpoints of a function on the

Definition A.1
.

Let

\displaystyle\mathord{\mathrm{Post}}_{G}(s,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\{\,s^{\prime}\in S\mid(s,\sigma,s^{\prime})\in\delta\,\}\ ;

\displaystyle\mathord{\mathrm{Post}}_{G}(s)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\mathord{\mathrm{Post}}(s,\sigma)\ .

Baier and Katoen 2008
describe fixpoint characterisations of weak and strong

considered here. Given a transition system

with state set

and a

\displaystyle\mathord{\mathrm{gfp}}\Theta

\displaystyle=\emptyset

\displaystyle{\Theta\mathord{\uparrow}(\beta+1)}

\displaystyle=\Theta({\Theta\mathord{\uparrow}\beta})

Appendix D
Coarsest robust stutter bisimulation

This section proves that the relation
\mathord{\approx}
from
Def.
17
is the coarsest robust stutter bisimulation on a
transition system. They key argument in this proof is that the transitive
and reflexive closure of the union of robust stutter bisimulations is again
a robust stutter bisimulation.

The
transitive closure

({\mathord{\mathcal{R}}})^{+}
of a relation
\mathord{\mathcal{R}}
is the smallest
transitive relation such that
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}\subseteq({\mathord{\mathcal{R}}})^{+}
.
The
transitive and reflexive closure
of
\mathord{\mathcal{R}}
is
({\mathord{\mathcal{R}}})^{*}=({\mathord{\mathcal{R}}})^{+}\cup\mathcal{I}
, where
\mathcal{I}
is the identity relation.

Lemma D.1
.

Let
G
be a transition system, and let
\{\,{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{i}\mid i\in I\,\}
be a set of robust stutter bisimulations on
G
, where
I
is an arbitrary index set. Then
(\bigcup_{i\in I}{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{i})^{*}
is a robust stutter bisimulation on
G
.

As the union of symmetric relations is again symmetric, it is clear that
the reflexive and transitive closure of the union of equivalence
relations
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{i}
is again an equivalence relation.
It remains to be shown that
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}=(\bigcup_{i\in I}{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{i})^{*}
satisfies
conditions
(i)
and
(ii)
in
Def.
16
.

Let
(x,y)\in{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}
. This can be written as

x=x_{0}\mathrel{\mathcal{R}}_{i_{1}}x_{1}\mathrel{\mathcal{R}}_{i_{2}}\cdots\mathrel{\mathcal{R}}_{i_{n}}x_{n}=y

(33)

where
i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\in I
.
As each
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{i}
is a robust stutter bisimulation, it immediately follows that

L(x)=L(x_{0})=L(x_{1})=\cdots=L(x_{n})=L(y)
, showing
(i)
.
To show condition
(ii)
,
let
x=x_{0}\in\mathord{\overline{\mathcal{EC}}}_{G}(\psi)
for some
\mathord{\mathcal{R}}
-superblock step formula
\psi\equiv[x_{0}]_{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}\mathrel{\mathcal{V}}T
.
If
n=0
, it immediately holds that
y=x_{n}=x_{0}\in\mathord{\overline{\mathcal{EC}}}_{G}(\psi)
.
Otherwise notice that
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{i_{1}}\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in I}{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{i}\subseteq(\bigcup_{i\in I}{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{i})^{*}={\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}
.
Then
[x_{0}]_{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}
and
T
are superblocks of
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{i_{1}}
, i.e.,
\psi
is an
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{i_{1}}
-superblock step formula.
Since
{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{i_{1}}
is a robust stutter bisimulation and
(x_{0},x_{1})\in{\mathord{\mathcal{R}}}_{i_{1}}
, it follows by Lemma
C.2
that
x_{1}\in\mathord{\overline{\mathcal{EC}}}_{G}(\psi)
.
By induction, it follows that

.
∎

An alternative way to define the coarsest robust stutter bisimulation
\mathord{\approx}
is by considering
the union of all robust stutter bisimulations. This union is again a robust stutter bisimulation, so it must be the
coarsest such relation. This argument is made in the following proof.

Proposition D.2
.

Let

is a robust stutter bisimulation that

By Def.

, the relation
\mathord{\approx}
can be written as

{\mathord{\approx}}=\bigcup\;\{\,\vtop{{$\mathord{\mathcal{R}}$}\ $\mid$ {$\mathord{\mathcal{R}}$}\ is a robust stutter

(34)

Thus,
\mathord{\approx}
includes every robust stutter bisimulation on

. To prove that
\mathord{\approx}
is a

∎
