Abstract
. The Leray transform 𝑳 \bm{L} is studied on a family M γ M_{\gamma} of unbounded hypersurfaces in two complex dimensions. For a large class of measures, we obtain necessary and sufficient conditions for the L 2 L^{2} -boundedness of 𝑳 \bm{L} , along with an exact spectral description of 𝑳 ∗ ​ 𝑳 \bm{L}^{*}\bm{L} . This yields both the norm and high-frequency norm of 𝑳 \bm{L} , the latter affirming an unbounded analogue of an open conjecture relating the essential norm of 𝑳 \bm{L} to a projective invariant on a bounded hypersurface. 𝑳 \bm{L} is also shown to play a central role in bridging the function theoretic and projective geometric notions of duality. Our work leads to the construction of projectively invariant Hardy spaces on the M γ M_{\gamma} , along with the realization of their duals as invariant Hardy spaces on the dual hypersurfaces.



1.
Introduction

This article continues a series aimed at further developing the theory of the Leray transform from a projective dual point of view. Much of our focus here will be on the following family of real hypersurfaces. For
\gamma\geq 1
, define

(1.1)

M_{\gamma}:=\left\{(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\colon\text{Im}(\zeta_{2})=\left|\zeta_{1}\right|^{\gamma}\right\},

together with the unbounded domain lying on its
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex side

(1.2)

\Omega_{\gamma}:=\left\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\colon\text{Im}(z_{2})>\left|z_{1}\right|^{\gamma}\right\}.

The Leray transform is a higher-dimensional analogue of the Cauchy transform of a planar domain; it acts by taking in data given on a real hypersurface to construct holomorphic functions on the interior of the hypersurface. (See Section
2.1
for precise definitions.) As is typical in multi-dimensional complex analysis constuctions, convexity conditions play a crucial role. In particular, the Leray transform
\bm{L}_{{\mathcal{S}}}:=\bm{L}
is a well-defined integral operator on any smoothly bounded
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex hypersurface
{\mathcal{S}}
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. When
{\mathcal{S}}
is an unbounded hypersurface (such as
M_{\gamma}
), additional care must be taken. As in the case of the Cauchy transform, knowledge of both quantitative and qualitative information related to
\bm{L}
provides insight into holomorphic function spaces associated to
{\mathcal{S}}
.

A great deal of additional information can be obtained by viewing the Leray transform through the lens of projective duality. The projective dual of a hypersurface
{\mathcal{S}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, denoted
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
, is the set of complex hyperplanes tangent to
{\mathcal{S}}
. In
[
4
]
, the first author demonstrates that the efficiency of a natural pairing of two dual Hardy spaces associated respectively to
{\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
is measured by the
L^{2}
-norm of
\bm{L}_{\mathcal{S}}
. The Cauchy transform plays an analogous role in the pairing of interior and exterior Hardy spaces associated to a planar curve; see
[
32
]
. New aspects of this theory are set forth in Section
6
, where they are carefully illustrated in the setting of
M_{\gamma}
.

Each
M_{\gamma}
is homogeneous with respect to certain projective automorphisms (see Section
3.1
) and
\bm{L}
admits a transformation law with respect to such maps; see (
2.15
). It thus makes sense to pay special attention to
L^{2}
-spaces on
M_{\gamma}
which are built from measures satisfying desirable transformation laws. Our analysis begins by considering the measure
\sigma:=\alpha^{\gamma-1}d\alpha\wedge d\theta\wedge ds
, with
\alpha=|\zeta_{1}|,\theta=\arg(\zeta_{1}),s=\re(\zeta_{2})
. This is a constant multiple of the Leray-Levi measure corresponding to the most natural choice of defining function for
M_{\gamma}
; see Section
2.1
.

We now highlight several results established in this paper. Continuing the theme of
[
6
]
, the exact
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
-norm of the Leray transform is obtained:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\bm{L}
be the Leray transform of
M_{\gamma}
. Then
\bm{L}\colon L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)\to L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
is a bounded projection operator with norm

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)}=\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}}.

We go on to consider a more general class of measures: for
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
, let
\mu_{r}=\alpha^{r}d\alpha\wedge d\theta\wedge ds
. Then we have

Theorem 1.4
.

The Leray transform
\bm{L}
is a bounded projection from
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})\to L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
if and only if
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0}:=(-1,2\gamma-1)
.

Theorem
1.3
is proved in Section
4.3
, Theorem
1.4
in Section
5.1
. The analysis involved in proving these results relies heavily on the symmetries of
M_{\gamma}
. In particular, the
S^{1}
action in the
\zeta_{1}
variable of (
1.1
) yields a (partial) Fourier series decomposition of the space

L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})=\bigoplus_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r}).

(Here we use the “subspace” notion of direct sum as set forth, for example, on page 81 of
[
3
]
.)
The Leray transform decomposes similarly, but a holomorphic function on
M_{\gamma}
may have nonzero components in
L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
only if
k\geq 0
. In other words,

(1.5)

\bm{L}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}\bm{L}_{k},

where the
sub-Leray
operator
\bm{L}_{k}
is nonzero only when acting on
L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
. Very precise information on each
\bm{L}_{k}
is obtained, including both the sharp range of
r
for which boundedness in
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
holds and the exact operator norm when it does. Define the interval

(1.6)

{\mathcal{I}}_{k}=(-2k-1,(2k+2)(\gamma-1)+1),

along with the
\mu_{r}
-
symbol function

C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)=\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\big)\Gamma\big(2k+2-\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\big)}{\Gamma(k+1)^{2}}\left(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\right)^{2k+2}(\gamma-1)^{-\left(2k+2-\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\right)}.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
k\geq 0
be an integer and
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
\bm{L}_{k}
is bounded from
L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})\to L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
if and only if
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
. Furthermore when
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
,

\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})}=\sqrt{C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)}.

When
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
(the interval of boundedness from Theorem
1.4
), the Leray transform admits a bounded adjoint
\bm{L}^{(*,\mu_{r})}
in the
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
-inner product. For these
r
values, we obtain a complete spectral description of the self-adjoint
\bm{L}^{(*,\mu_{r})}\bm{L}
and
\bm{L}\bm{L}^{(*,\mu_{r})}
, and anti-self-adjoint
\bm{A}^{\mu_{r}}:=\bm{L}^{(*,\mu_{r})}-\bm{L}
in terms of the symbol function. In Section
5.3
it is shown that:

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
and let
\bm{T}
denote either
\bm{L}^{(*,\mu_{r})}\bm{L}
or
\bm{L}\bm{L}^{(*,\mu_{r})}
. Then
\bm{T}
admits an orthogonal basis of eigenfunctions and its spectrum is given by

\{0\}\cup\left\{C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k):k=0,1,2,\dots\right\}.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
. The operator
\bm{A}^{\mu_{r}}:=\bm{L}^{(*,\mu_{r})}-\bm{L}
admits an orthogonal basis of eigenfunctions and its spectrum is given by

\{0\}\cup\left\{\pm i\sqrt{C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)-1}:k=0,1,2,\dots\right\}.

Theorems
1.8
and
1.9
follow from work carried out in Section
5.3
.

We now emphasize a fascinating observation about the limiting behavior of the sub-Leray operators. Define the
(
\mu_{r}
)-high-frequency limit norm
by

(1.10)

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L_{HF}^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})}:=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})}.

This is definable for any operator admitting a Fourier series decomposition and it can be viewed as a generalized essential norm in the sense of Lefèvre
[
31
]
– in Section
2.3.1
we introduce the terminology
grade-essential-norm
.

As the value of
r
varies, the norm
\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})}
expectedly changes. But in Section
5.1
it is shown that all choices of
r
yield the same high frequency limit:

Theorem 1.11
.

For
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the
\mu_{r}
-high-frequency limit norm of
\bm{L}
is

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L_{HF}^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}}}.

An analogous result was observed in
[
9
]
on smoothly bounded, strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex Reinhardt hypersurfaces in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. We’ll see in Section
2.3
that this quantity is closely connected to a projective geometric invariant of
M_{\gamma}
. Note also that this quantity is the square-root of the
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
-norm in Theorem
1.3
; a similar observation was recently noted for the Leray transform on boundaries of
L^{p}
-balls
\{(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}:|\zeta_{1}|^{p}+|\zeta_{2}|^{p}=1\}
; see
[
34
]
.

Lanzani and Stein have written a series of recent articles on the Leray transform in various settings. One of the takeaways in
[
26
,
27
]
is that
\bm{L}
is well behaved (one can expect both
L^{2}
and
L^{p}
boundedness results) as long as the hypersurface in question is both
\bm{(1)}
strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex and
\bm{(2)}

C^{1,1}
-smooth. When either hypothesis is dropped, they are able to construct elementary counter-examples with no Leray

-boundedness (including

); see
[

30

. The positive Lanzani-Stein results don’t apply in our setting since
M_{\gamma}
(viewed in projective space) fails even to be

when it meets the line at infinity (except when
\gamma=2
); see Section
3.6
. But unlike their counter-examples, our results show that
\bm{L}
is
L^{2}
-bounded for a range of reasonable measures on
M_{\gamma}
.

Interest in
M_{\gamma}
also stems from other considerations. In
[
4
]
, Barrett defines a scalar invariant (denoted
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(\zeta)
in Section

below) associated to any strongly

-convex hypersurface. The

comprise one of the few families for which this invariant is constant. Another such family

(1.12)

{\mathcal{S}}_{\beta}=\{(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}:\text{Im}(\zeta_{2})=|\zeta_{1}|^{2}+\beta\re(\zeta_{1}^{2})\},\qquad 0\leq\beta<1,

was recently studied by Barrett and Edholm in
[

. Both

and

are intriguing models in connection with Conjecture
2.16
on the essential norm of
\bm{L}
, but this conjecture does not directly apply to either family since these hypersurfaces are unbounded (or more to the point, they fail to be
C^{1}
at infinity – except in the very special case of
M_{2}={\mathcal{S}}_{0}
).

Holomorphic function theory on
M_{\gamma}
has been previously studied for positive even integers
\gamma=2n
. (These are the only
\gamma
for which
M_{\gamma}
is
C^{\infty}
at the origin.) Greiner and Stein
[
22

found an explicit formula for the Szegő kernel on
L^{2}(M_{2n},dx_{1}\wedge dy_{1}\wedge dx_{2})
. Their work was later used by Diaz
[
18

to determine mapping properties of the Szegő projection in this setting. Note that
dx_{1}\wedge dy_{1}\wedge dx_{2}
is the Euclidean surface measure on the parameter space
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
; this measure agrees with
\mu_{1}
in our above notation. Since
r=1\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
for all
\gamma>1
, a special case of Theorem
1.4
implies that
\bm{L}
is a bounded, skew (unless
\gamma=2
) projection on the same
L^{2}
-spaces considered by Greiner, Stein and Diaz.

Recall that the Szegő projection is the orthogonal projection from a pre-specified
L^{2}
-space onto its associated Hardy space. The projection is represented by its Szegő kernel, a Hilbert space reproducing kernel. The existence of this projection is guaranteed, but such kernels can only be concretely written down in a small number of situations. The Leray transform on the other hand, is always given by an explicit integral formula and can often be shown to be a bounded, skew projection operator onto reasonable Hardy spaces. An intimate relationship between Szegő projections and “Cauchy-like” integral operators
\bm{C}
(the Leray transform is just one such example) was noticed by Kerzman and Stein in
[
24
,
25
]
. They observed that detailed information about the Szegő projection can be extracted from the operator
\bm{A}:=\bm{C}^{*}-\bm{C}
. See
[
10
]
for an expository treatment of these ideas in the complex plane and
[
13
,
12
,
9
,
28
]
for more recent developments.

There are important reasons to think of complex projective space (not affine space) as the ideal setting in which to study the Leray transform. Following the point of view taken in
[
1
]
, we maintain that
{\mathbb{C}}
-convexity most naturally belongs in
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
due to the duality seen between points and hyperplanes. Formula (
2.1b
) shows that
{\mathbb{C}}
-convexity is built into the definition of
\bm{L}
: the denominator of
\mathscr{L}_{\mathcal{S}}
vanishes on the supporting complex hyperplanes of
{\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathbb{C}}
-convexity is precisely the condition needed for this hyperplane to avoid the domain bounded by
{\mathcal{S}}
. The set of complex hyperplanes – and therefore the set of
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex hypersurfaces – is preserved under projective automorphism. In Section
2.2.3
, we define the
preferred measure

\widetilde{\mu}_{\mathcal{S}}
. It follows from work of Bolt
[
11

that if the Leray kernel is written in terms of
\widetilde{\mu}_{\mathcal{S}}
, then it admits a projective transformation law; cf. (
2.15
).

This material is consolidated into a satisfying circle of ideas in Section
6
, where
\bm{L}
is used to bridge the notions of function theoretic and projective geometric duality. We construct a projectively invariant Hardy space on each
M_{\gamma}
, then show that its dual space can be naturally identified with the pullback of the invariant Hardy space on the dual hypersurface. Unlike Bergman spaces which come naturally equipped with a transformation law, Hardy spaces only transform nicely if set up with respect to very specific measures. This is where our preferred measure comes in:
\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}=\widetilde{\mu}
is shown to be a constant multiple of some
\mu_{r}
,
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
. Therefore, Theorem
1.3
allows us to define the Hardy space

H^{2}(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}):=\bm{L}\big(L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu})\big).

By its construction, this space admits a projective transformation law. (One important order of business is the verification that functions in this space actually correspond to holomorphic functions on the domain
\Omega_{\gamma}
. This turns out to be the case, but the proof is postponed until the Appendix.)

In Section
6.1
we show that the projective dual of
M_{\gamma}
can be represented by
M_{\gamma^{*}}
(where
\gamma^{*}=\tfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}
is the Hölder conjugate) and construct a diffeomorphism
w:M_{\gamma}\to M_{\gamma^{*}}
. The pullback of this map is used to define the
preferred dual measure

w^{*}\big(\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma^{*}}}\big):=\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*}=\widetilde{\mu}^{*}
, along with the dual Hardy space

H_{\sf dual}^{2}\big(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}^{*}\big):=w^{*}\Big(H^{2}\big(M_{\gamma^{*}},\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma^{*}}}\big)\Big).

A third measure of interest facilitates a bilinear pairing of these two Hardy spaces. The
pairing measure

\nu
appears in
[
6
]
as part of a universal formulation of the Leray transform; see Theorem
6.18
below; also see
[
1
]
.
In the
M_{\gamma}
setting,
\nu
is a constant multiple of
\sigma
from Theorem
1.3
. A multiplicative constant can be specified to give a sharp Cauchy-Schwarz inequality relating
\widetilde{\mu}
,
\widetilde{\mu}^{*}
and
\nu
: for
f\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu})
and
g\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}^{*})
,

\bigg|\int_{M_{\gamma}}fg\,\nu\bigg|\leq\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}\left(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}\right)}\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}\left(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}^{*}\right)},

where equality is achieved for any such
f
(likewise for any such
g
). Consider now the map

\chi_{\gamma}:L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}^{*})\to\left(L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu})\right)^{*}

given by
\chi_{\gamma}(g):f\mapsto\int_{M_{\gamma}}fg\,\nu
, along with the companion map

\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}\colon H^{2}_{\sf dual}\big(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}^{*}\big)\to\left(H^{2}\big(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}\big)\right)^{*}

given by the restriction
\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}(g)=\chi_{\gamma}(g)\big|_{H^{2}(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu})}
.

In Section

it is shown that

induces a faithful representation of the dual space and that the efficiency of the Hardy space pairing is measured by the appropriate norm of
\bm{L}
:

.

The operator
\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}\colon H^{2}_{\sf dual}\big(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}^{*}\big)\to\left(H^{2}\big(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}\big)\right)^{*}
is invertible with norm

\left\|{\widehat{\chi}}_{\gamma}^{\,-1}\right\|=\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu})}.

2.
Background and preliminaries

2.1.
The Leray transform

A domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is said to be
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex if its intersection with any complex line is both connected and simply connected (when non-empty). It is said to be
{\mathbb{C}}
-linearly convex if the complement can be written as the union of complex hyperplanes. These two notions coincide when the boundary of
\Omega
is
C^{1}
(see Section 2.5 in
[
1
]
). A hypersurface
{\mathcal{S}}
bounding a domain
\Omega
is said to be
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex
(resp.
{\mathbb{C}}
-linearly convex
) if
\Omega
is
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex (resp.
{\mathbb{C}}
-linearly convex). A hypersurface
{\mathcal{S}}
that is locally projectively equivalent to a strongly convex hypersurface is said to be
strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex
. (Section 5.2 of
[
4
]
discusses equivalent characterizations of strong
{\mathbb{C}}
-convexity.)

Let
{\mathcal{S}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be a
{\mathbb{C}}
-linearly convex hypersurface with defining function
\rho
and
f
a function defined on
{\mathcal{S}}
. The Leray transform maps
f
to a holomorphic function on
\Omega
whenever the following integral makes sense (Theorem
A.1
in the Appendix gives conditions on
f
which guarantee the holomorphicity of
\bm{L}f
on
\Omega_{\gamma}
):

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\bm{L}_{{\mathcal{S}}}f(z)

\displaystyle:=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}}f(\zeta)\,\mathscr{L}_{{\mathcal{S}}}(z,\zeta),

(2.1b)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{{\mathcal{S}}}(z,\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{n}}\frac{\partial\rho(\zeta)\wedge(\overline{\partial}\partial\rho(\zeta))^{n-1}}{\left<\partial\rho(\zeta),(\zeta-z)\right>^{n}}.

Note that the
Leray kernel

\mathscr{L}_{{\mathcal{S}}}
is a form of bi-degree
(n,n-1)
. Here
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
denotes the natural
bilinear
pairing between
(1,0)
-forms and vectors. This definition is independent of the choice of
\rho
; see Chapter IV of
[
33
]
for more information.

Separate the Leray kernel (
2.1b
) into two pieces, each of which
does
depend on the choice of defining function:

(2.1c)

\displaystyle\ell_{\rho}(z,\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\left<\partial\rho(\zeta),(\zeta-z)\right>^{n}}

(2.1d)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rho}(\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{n}}\,\partial\rho(\zeta)\wedge(\overline{\partial}\partial\rho(\zeta))^{n-1}.

Refer to
\lambda_{\rho}
as
the Leray-Levi measure for the defining function
\rho
. We often suppress the subscript
\rho
when it is clear from context.

Now calculate the pieces of the Leray transform on
M_{\gamma}
. Using the defining function

(2.2)

\rho(z)=|z_{1}|^{\gamma}-\text{Im}{(z_{2})},

equations (
2.1c
) and (
2.1d
) yield

(2.3a)

\displaystyle\ell_{\rho}(z,\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\left(\gamma\overline{\zeta}_{1}|\zeta_{1}|^{\gamma-2}(\zeta_{1}-z_{1})+i(\zeta_{2}-z_{2})\right)^{2}},

(2.3b)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rho}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{32\pi^{2}i}|\zeta_{1}|^{\gamma-2}\,d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}.

In this paper, the Leray transform
\bm{L}_{M_{\gamma}}
will frequently be denoted by
\bm{L}
, or sometimes by
\bm{L}_{\gamma}
when it is important to keep track of the exponent.
Equations (
2.1a
) and (
2.1b
) give

(2.3c)

\displaystyle\bm{L}f(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{8\pi^{2}i}\int_{M_{\gamma}}f(\zeta)\frac{|\zeta_{1}|^{\gamma-2}d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}}{\left[\gamma\overline{\zeta}_{1}|\zeta_{1}|^{\gamma-2}(\zeta_{1}-z_{1})+i(\zeta_{2}-z_{2})\right]^{2}}.

2.2.
Projective invariants and transformation laws

Recall that, with respect to the standard affinization, automorphisms of
{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{P}^{2}
have the form

(2.4)

\widehat{X}\colon(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto\left(\frac{D+Ez_{1}+Fz_{2}}{A+Bz_{1}+Cz_{2}},\frac{G+Hz_{1}+Iz_{2}}{A+Bz_{1}+Cz_{2}}\right),

where
\widehat{X}
is induced by the invertible matrix

(2.5)

X:=\begin{pmatrix}A&B&C\\
D&E&F\\
G&H&I\end{pmatrix}.

In homogeneous coordinates, these are linear maps. Projective automorphisms map strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex hypersurfaces to strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex hypersurfaces; see Section 5.2 of
[
4
]
. See also
[
14
,
15
]
for a detailed treatment of Möbius geometry on hypersurfaces.

Direct computation reveals the Jacobian determinant of
\widehat{X}
is given by

\det\widehat{X}^{\prime}=\frac{\det X}{(A+Bz_{1}+Cz_{2})^{3}}.

A similar formula holds in dimension
n
with exponent
n+1
in the denominator. Below we will have occasion to refer to
(\det\widehat{X}^{\prime})^{n/(n+1)}:
it is clear that there are
n+1
distinct well-defined branches of this function. It is understood that the same branch is to be used within a single computation when this expression appears repeatedly.

2.2.1.
A projective scalar invariant

For a strongly pseudoconvex hypersurface
{\mathcal{S}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
with defining function
\rho
and a point
\zeta\in{\mathcal{S}}
, the scalar quantity

(2.6)

\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(\zeta):=\left|\frac{\det{\begin{pmatrix}0&\rho_{z_{1}}&\rho_{z_{2}}\\
\rho_{z_{1}}&\rho_{z_{1}z_{1}}&\rho_{z_{2}z_{1}}\\
\rho_{z_{2}}&\rho_{z_{1}z_{2}}&\rho_{z_{2}z_{2}}\end{pmatrix}}}{\det\begin{pmatrix}0&\rho_{z_{1}}&\rho_{z_{2}}\\
\rho_{\overline{z}_{1}}&\rho_{z_{1}\overline{z}_{1}}&\rho_{z_{2}\overline{z}_{1}}\\
\rho_{\overline{z}_{2}}&\rho_{z_{1}\overline{z}_{2}}&\rho_{z_{2}\overline{z}_{2}}\end{pmatrix}}(\zeta)\right|

(where subscripts denote derivatives) is directly invariant under projective automorphisms. This scalar is the absolute value of an invariant tensor
{\mathscr{B}}_{S}
introduced by the first author in Section 5.3 of
[
4
]
. A computation shows that for all
\zeta\in M_{\gamma}
,

(2.7)

\beta_{M_{\gamma}}(\zeta)=\frac{|\gamma-2|}{\gamma}.

2.2.2.
Fefferman surface measure

On page 259 of
[
20
]
, Fefferman introduced an invariant measure
\mu_{\mathcal{S}}^{\sf Fef}
for an arbitrary
smooth strongly pseudoconvex hypersurface
{\mathcal{S}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Viewing

\mu_{\mathcal{S}}^{\sf Fef}
as a positive

(2n-1)
-form, it is characterized by the equation

(2.8)

\mu_{\mathcal{S}}^{\sf Fef}\wedge d\rho=c_{n}\,M(\rho)^{1/(n+1)}\omega_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}},

where
\omega_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}
is the Euclidean volume
2n
-form,
\rho
is a defining
function for
{\mathcal{S}}
and

M(\rho)
denotes Fefferman’s complex Monge-Ampère operator defined by

(2.9)

M(\rho)=(-1)^{n}\det\begin{pmatrix}\rho&\rho_{z_{j}}\\
\rho_{\overline{z}_{k}}&\rho_{z_{j}{\overline{z}_{k}}}\end{pmatrix}.

(The subscripts denote differentiation and
c_{n}
is a dimensional constant. In Section
6.2
we specify an explicit value of this constant that is convenient for the purposes of this paper.)

Fefferman’s measure satisfies the transformation law

(2.10)

\Psi^{*}\left(\mu_{\Psi({\mathcal{S}})}^{\sf Fef}\right)=\left|\det\Psi^{\prime}\right|^{2n/(n+1)}\,\mu_{\mathcal{S}}^{\sf Fef}

for CR diffeomorphisms
\Psi
; see
[
23
]
.

If a Szegő projection operator for a compact strongly pseudoconvex
{\mathcal{S}}
is defined with respect to
\mu_{\mathcal{S}}^{\sf Fef}
and
\Psi
is a CR diffeomorphism with a well-defined branch of
\left(\det\Psi^{\prime}\right)^{n/(n+1)}
, then from (
2.10
) we find that the operator

f\mapsto\left(\det\Psi^{\prime}\right)^{n/(n+1)}\cdot\left(f\circ\Psi\right)

maps
L^{2}\big(\Psi({\mathcal{S}}),\mu_{\Psi({\mathcal{S}})}^{\sf Fef}\big)
isometrically to
L^{2}\left({\mathcal{S}},\mu_{\mathcal{S}}^{\sf Fef}\right)
, preserving the corresponding Hardy spaces. This leads to the transformation law demonstrated in
[
23
]
:

(2.11)

\bm{S}_{{\mathcal{S}}}\left(\left(\det\Psi^{\prime}\right)^{n/(n+1)}\left(f\circ\Psi\right)\right)=\left(\det\Psi^{\prime}\right)^{n/(n+1)}\left(\left(\bm{S}_{\Psi({\mathcal{S}})}f\right)\circ\Psi\right).

From (
2.2
), (
2.8
) and (
2.9
), the Fefferman measure on
M_{\gamma}
is seen to be given by

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mu_{M_{\gamma}}^{\sf Fef}

\displaystyle=c_{2}\frac{\gamma^{2/3}}{2^{7/3}i}|\zeta_{1}|^{\frac{\gamma-2}{3}}\,d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}.

2.2.3.
The preferred measure

For the general theory of the Leray transform it is useful to work with a modified version of Fefferman’s measure. In Section 8 of
[
4
]
, the first author defines a projective modification of
\mu_{\mathcal{S}}^{\sf Fef}
tailored to suit a natural pairing of Hardy spaces for which the Fefferman measure is not optimal; we examine this pairing in Section
6.4
.

Throughout this paper, we refer to this modification of Fefferman’s measure as the
preferred measure
and denote it by
\widetilde{\mu}_{\mathcal{S}}
. In two dimensions it is given by the formula

\widetilde{\mu}_{\mathcal{S}}(\zeta)=\dfrac{\mu_{\mathcal{S}}^{\sf Fef}(\zeta)}{\sqrt[3]{1-\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(\zeta)^{2}}},

where
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(\zeta)
is defined in (
2.6
). By (
2.7
), this invariant is constant on
M_{\gamma}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2/3}}{2^{2/3}{(\gamma-1)}^{1/3}}\,\mu_{M_{\gamma}}^{\sf Fef}

(2.13)

\displaystyle=c_{2}\frac{\gamma^{4/3}}{8(\gamma-1)^{1/3}i}\,|\zeta_{1}|^{\frac{\gamma-2}{3}}\,d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}.

Equation (
2.10
) and the projective invariance of
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(\zeta)
now show that

(2.14)

\Psi^{*}\left(\widetilde{\mu}_{\Psi({\mathcal{S}})}\right)=\left|\det\Psi^{\prime}\right|^{4/3}\,\widetilde{\mu}_{\mathcal{S}}

for any projective automorphism
\Psi
.

For such
\Psi
we can use (
2.14
) to deduce that a transformation law analogous to (
2.11
) holds for Szegő projections based on the preferred measure
\widetilde{\mu}_{\mathcal{S}}
. If
{\mathcal{S}}
is strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex and
\Psi
is a projective automorphism, then Bolt
[
11
]
has shown that the Leray transform satisfies the same transformation law. In two dimensions, this is

(2.15)

\bm{L}_{{\mathcal{S}}}\left(\left(\det\Psi^{\prime}\right)^{2/3}\left(f\circ\Psi\right)\right)=\left(\det\Psi^{\prime}\right)^{2/3}\left(\left(\bm{L}_{\Psi({\mathcal{S}})}f\right)\circ\Psi\right).

2.3.
Projective invariants and the essential norm conjecture

In
[
6
]
, the authors conjecture a quantitative relationship between the projective invariant
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(\zeta)
defined in (
2.6
) and the essential norm of the Leray transform on smoothly bounded, strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex hypersurfaces
{\mathcal{S}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. We restate a version of this conjecture here:

Conjecture 2.16
.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
be a smoothly bounded, strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex hypersurface and
\bm{L}
its Leray transform. There is a “reasonable” family
{\mathscr{F}}
of measures on
{\mathcal{S}}
such that if
\mu\in{\mathscr{F}}
, the essential
L^{2}({\mathcal{S}},\mu)
-norm is given by

(2.17)

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{{L^{2}_{\sf ess}({\mathcal{S}},\mu)}}=\sup_{\zeta\in{\mathcal{S}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{1-\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(\zeta)^{2}}}.

At this stage, the word “reasonable” is left intentionally vague, but there is good reason to expect such a relationship. This has been previously established (Theorem 3 in
[
9
]
) when
{\mathcal{S}}
is the boundary of a
C^{2}
-smooth, bounded, strongly convex
Reinhardt
domain and
\mu
is any continuous, positive multiple of surface measure on
{\mathcal{S}}
.

While Conjecture
2.16
only pertains to
bounded
hypersurfaces, the invariant
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(\zeta)
does not require
{\mathcal{S}}
to be bounded. It was noted in
[
4
]
that this invariant is constant for both
{\mathcal{S}}_{\beta}
(defined in (
1.12
)) and
M_{\gamma}
. It easily follows from (
2.6
) that

\beta_{{\mathcal{S}}_{\beta}}(\zeta)=\beta\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\beta_{M_{\gamma}}=\frac{|\gamma-2|}{\gamma},

and consequently, the quantity on the right hand side of (
2.17
) is equal to

(2.18)

\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{1-\beta^{2}}}\,\,\textrm{when}\,\,{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{S}}_{\beta}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}}}\,\,\textrm{when}\,\,{\mathcal{S}}=M_{\gamma}.

We raise the following question:

(\star)

Are the quantities in (
2.18
) connected to the Leray transform in a meaningful way?

In
[
6
]
, the authors gave an affirmative answer to
(\star)
on
{\mathcal{S}}_{\beta}
by proving that

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathcal{S}}_{\beta},\sigma)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{1-\beta^{2}}}=\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L_{\sf ess}^{2}({\mathcal{S}}_{\beta},\sigma)},

where
\sigma=dx_{1}\wedge dy_{1}\wedge dx_{2}
is (a constant multiple of) the Leray-Levi measure corresponding to the defining function
\rho(\zeta)=|\zeta_{1}|^{2}+\beta\re{(\zeta_{1}^{2})}-\text{Im}{(\zeta_{2})}
. The norm coincides with the essential norm because the former is attained on an infinite dimensional subspace of
L^{2}({\mathcal{S}}_{\beta},\sigma)
.

In the present paper,
(\star)
is answered affirmatively on
M_{\gamma}
, but the interpretation of this quantity is slightly different. Indeed, Theorem
1.3
and Remark
4.27
show that

(2.19)

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)}=\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}}=\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L_{\sf ess}^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)},

where now
\sigma=\alpha^{\gamma-1}d\alpha\wedge d\theta\wedge ds
is (a constant multiple of) the Leray-Levi measure corresponding to the defining function
\rho(\zeta)=|\zeta_{1}|^{\gamma}-\text{Im}{(\zeta_{2})}
. Notice that (
2.19
) is the
square
of the desired quantity in (
2.18
).
But this desired quantity does naturally arise (Theorem
1.11
) as the high-frequency limit norm of
\bm{L}
in the space
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
for
every
measure of the form
\mu_{r}=\alpha^{r}d\alpha\wedge d\theta\wedge ds
,
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
, (which clearly includes
\sigma
):

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}}}.

This suggests a connection between the geometric invariant
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}
on similar unbounded hypersurfaces (those admitting both
S^{1}
and
{\mathbb{R}}
actions) and the behavior of the Leray transform at high frequencies. This connection is further developed in the coming work of Edholm and Shelah
[
19
]
, where a much more general class of such hypersurfaces is shown to satisfy an analogue of Conjecture
2.16
. In the two dimensional Reinhardt setting, the high frequency limit norm coincides with the essential norm, but
M_{\gamma}
shows these two notions don’t necessarily agree on unbounded domains. The high frequency limit norm can, however, be conceptualized as a generalized essential norm as we now shall see.

2.3.1.
The grade-essential-norm

Given a Hilbert space
E
, recall that the norm-closure of the space of finite-rank operators is the two-sided ideal of compact operators, and that the essential norm of a bounded operator
\bm{T}\colon E\to E
is the distance to the ideal of compact operators; equivalently, the essential norm of
\bm{T}
is its norm in the quotient
Calkin algebra
. See
[
17
]
for more information.

Consider on the other hand a Hilbert space
E
admitting an orthogonal decomposition

E=\displaystyle{\bigoplus_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}E_{k}}.

(The decomposition is also known as a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-grading
of
E
.) Call a bounded operator

\bm{T}\colon E\to E

decomposable
if it may be written as

\bm{T}=\displaystyle{\bigoplus_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\bm{T}_{k}}

with each
\bm{T}_{k}\colon E_{k}\to E_{k}
.
(One way this could arise is from Fourier series decomposition based on an
S^{1}
action on
E
commuting with
\bm{T}
.) The decomposable operators form an algebra.

We will call a decomposable
\bm{T}

grade-compact
if it is the norm-limit of operators taking values in

\Span\left\{E_{-k},E_{-k+1},\dots,E_{k}\right\}
; equivalently, if
\|\bm{T}_{k}\|\to 0
as
|k|\to\infty
.

We define the
grade-essential-norm
of a decomposable
\bm{T}
to be the distance
(
\limsup\|\bm{T}_{k}\|
) from
\bm{T}
to the space of
grade-compact decomposable operators. This is consistent with Lefèvre’s notion of a
generalized essential norm

[
31
]
. It is clear that the grade-essential-norm coincides with the high-frequency limit norm defined in (
1.10
) above.

The grade-compact decomposable operators form a two-sided ideal in the algebra of decomposable operators; the grade-essential-norm induces a norm on the corresponding quotient algebra.

Remark 2.20
.

In Theorem
5.22
, we will have occasion to consider situations in which finitely many of the
\bm{T}_{k}
are unbounded; in such a setting we still refer to

\limsup\|\bm{T}_{k}\|
as the grade-essential norm.
\lozenge

We begin by gathering formulas for previously discussed measures on

\alpha=|\zeta_{1}|

The
Leray-Levi measure
associated to the defining function

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{32\pi^{2}i}|\zeta_{1}|^{\gamma-2}d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{16\pi^{2}}\,\alpha^{\gamma-1}\,ds\wedge d\alpha\wedge d\theta.

In (
6.17
), a family
\nu^{A}
of
pairing measures

A_{\gamma}

). For a fixed

, the pairing measure we are interested in is

(2.22)

\displaystyle\nu^{A_{\gamma}}=4\lambda_{\rho}=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\,\alpha^{\gamma-1}ds\wedge d\alpha\wedge d\theta.

The
preferred measure

\widetilde{\mu}_{\mathcal{S}}
defined in (
2.13
) will be used in Section

to construct a family of invariant Hardy spaces on

. It takes the form

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=c_{2}\frac{\gamma^{4/3}}{8(\gamma-1)^{1/3}i}\,|\zeta_{1}|^{\frac{\gamma-2}{3}}d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}

(2.23)

\displaystyle=c_{2}\frac{\gamma^{4/3}}{4(\gamma-1)^{1/3}}\,\alpha^{\frac{\gamma+1}{3}}ds\wedge d\alpha\wedge d\theta.

Also in Section
6
, we meet the counterpart to
\widetilde{\mu}_{{\mathcal{S}}}
, which we call the

. This is obtained by taking the preferred measure on the projective dual hypersurface and pulling it back to

. The representation of the dual hypersurface (and hence the measure) in affine space depends on an affinization of projective space, and therefore on a matrix. These measures are used to construct the duals of the Hardy spaces mentioned above. With respect to the aforementioned matrix
A_{\gamma}
, the preferred dual measure is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=c_{2}\frac{\gamma^{4/3}}{8(\gamma-1)i}|\zeta_{1}|^{\frac{5(\gamma-2)}{3}}\,d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}

(2.24)

\displaystyle=c_{2}\frac{\gamma^{4/3}}{4(\gamma-1)}\,\alpha^{\frac{5\gamma-7}{3}}ds\wedge d\alpha\wedge d\theta.

Notice that these measures take a very specific form when written in
(\alpha,\theta,s)
coordinates. For
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
, define the measure

(2.25)

\mu_{r}:=\alpha^{r}ds\wedge d\alpha\wedge d\theta=\frac{1}{2i}|\zeta_{1}|^{r-1}d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1},

together with the family of all constant positive multiples of

(2.26)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{F}}_{r}=\{c\mu_{r}:c>0\}.

Thus,

\lambda_{\rho},\nu^{A_{\gamma}}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{\gamma-1},\qquad\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{\frac{\gamma+1}{3}},\qquad\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{\frac{5\gamma-7}{3}}.

Finally, because the measure
\mu_{\gamma-1}
occurs so frequently throughout the paper, we give it its own symbol:

(2.27)

\displaystyle\sigma=\alpha^{\gamma-1}ds\wedge d\alpha\wedge d\theta.

We emphasize the trivial fact that though multiplying a measure by a positive constant will uniformly scale the norms of functions, norms of operators remain unchanged. Indeed, if
\mu
is a measure on
M_{\gamma}
and
\bm{T}
is a bounded operator on

, then for all constants

we have

(2.28)

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{T}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu)}=\left\|\bm{T}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},c\mu)}.

2.4.2.
Inner products, norms and adjoints

Let
\mu
be a measure on
M_{\gamma}
and
f,g\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu)
. We often write their
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu)
-inner product by

(2.29)

\displaystyle\Big<f,g\Big>_{\mu}

\displaystyle:=\int_{M_{\gamma}}f(\zeta)\overline{g(\zeta)}\,\mu(\zeta).

The
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu)
-norm is similarly defined:

(2.30)

\left\|f\right\|_{\mu}=\sqrt{\Big<f,f\Big>_{\mu}}.

Now let
\mu_{r}
be the measure in (
2.25
). Throughout this paper, we frequently encounter the following one-dimensional integral related to
\mu_{r}
. For
f,g\in L^{2}\big((0,\infty),\alpha^{r}d\alpha\big)
, define the one-dimensional inner product

(2.31)

\displaystyle\Big<f,g\Big>_{(\mu_{r},1)}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(\alpha)\overline{g(\alpha)}\,\alpha^{r}d\alpha.

Similarly, define the norm

(2.32)

\left\|f\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}=\sqrt{\Big<f,f\Big>_{(\mu_{r},1)}}.

If
\bm{T}:L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu)\to L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu)
is a bounded operator, it admits a bounded adjoint
\bm{T}^{(*,\mu)}
, satisfying

(2.33)

\Big<\bm{T}f,g\Big>_{\mu}=\Big<f,\bm{T}^{(*,\mu)}g\Big>_{\mu}.

It is trivial to see that

(2.34)

\bm{T}^{(*,\mu)}=\bm{T}^{(*,c\mu)}

for all constants
c>0
, a fact that is repeatedly used in Section
6
.

3.
Geometry and Analysis on
M_{\gamma}

M_{\gamma}
is both strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex and real analytic away from
\{\zeta_{1}=0\}
for each
\gamma>1
. But on this set, these properties simultaneously hold only in the special case of
\gamma=2
.
M_{\gamma}
is weakly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex here when
\gamma>2
, and it fails to be
C^{2}
smooth when
1<\gamma<2
. (It is only
C^{\gamma}
smooth). These two notions are highly intertwined; see
[
9
]
for a detailed study of this in the Reinhardt setting. The interplay between
{\mathbb{C}}
-convexity and smoothness is further illuminated from a projective dual point of view; see Section
6
. At infinity, the behavior of
M_{\gamma}
is even less regular; this is the subject of Section
3.6
.

3.1.
Projective automorphisms of
M_{\gamma}

Certain affine maps preserving
M_{\gamma}
are examined here. These play an important role in our analysis of the Leray transform. The three types of maps considered are

•

Rotations in the first coordinate:
r_{\theta}(z_{1},z_{2})=(e^{i\theta}z_{1},z_{2})
,
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
.

•

Real translations in the second coordinate:
t_{s}(z_{1},z_{2})=(z_{1},z_{2}+s)
,
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

•

Non-isotropic dilations:
\delta_{\alpha}(z_{1},z_{2})=(\alpha z_{1},\alpha^{\gamma}z_{2})
,
\alpha>0
.

It is easily verified that the following pairs of maps commute:

•

r_{\theta_{1}}\circ r_{\theta_{2}}=r_{\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}}=r_{\theta_{2}}\circ r_{\theta_{1}}

•

t_{s_{1}}\circ t_{s_{2}}=t_{s_{1}+s_{2}}=t_{s_{2}}\circ t_{s_{1}}

•

\delta_{\alpha_{1}}\circ\delta_{\alpha_{2}}=\delta_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}=\delta_{\alpha_{2}}\circ\delta_{\alpha_{1}}

•

r_{\theta}\circ t_{s}=t_{s}\circ r_{\theta}

•

r_{\theta}\circ\delta_{\alpha}=\delta_{\alpha}\circ r_{\theta}
.

(Note though, that
t_{s}\circ\delta_{\alpha}\neq\delta_{\alpha}\circ t_{s}
.) Because
r_{\theta}
commutes with both
t_{s}
and
\delta_{\alpha}
, this rotational symmetry is the starting point of our analysis.

Define the set

(3.1)

V=\{(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})\in M_{\gamma}:\zeta_{1}=0\}.

V
is a copy of
{\mathbb{R}}
sitting inside
M_{\gamma}
. Each
r_{\theta},t_{s},\delta_{\alpha}
fixes
V
; collectively, they act transitively on
M_{\gamma}\backslash V
. Indeed:

Theorem 3.2
.

Given any pair of points
z,\zeta\in M_{\gamma}\backslash V
, there is a unique map of the form
t_{s}\circ r_{\theta}\circ\delta_{\alpha}
sending
z\mapsto\zeta
.

Proof.

The numbers
\alpha
and
\theta
are determined by setting
\alpha e^{i\theta}=\zeta_{1}{z_{1}}^{-1}
. The second variable is appropriately adjusted by setting
s=\re{(\zeta_{2})}-\alpha^{\gamma}\re{(z_{2})}
.
∎

3.2.
Leray reparametrization

The symmetries
r_{\theta},t_{s},\delta_{\alpha}
lead to a reparametrization of
M_{\gamma}
. Write
z,\zeta\in M_{\gamma}
as

(3.3)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{z}e^{i\theta_{z}},s_{z}+i\alpha_{z}^{\gamma}),

(3.4)

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{\zeta}e^{i\theta_{\zeta}},s_{\zeta}+i\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma}),

where each
\alpha\geq 0
, each
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, and each
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Equation (
2.21
) says the Leray-Levi measure from (
2.3b
) takes the form

(3.5)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rho}=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{32\pi^{2}i}|\zeta_{1}|^{\gamma-2}d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{16\pi^{2}}\,\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}ds_{\zeta}\wedge d\alpha_{\zeta}\wedge d\theta_{\zeta}.

Now re-write the Leray transform. Expression (
2.3c
) becomes

\displaystyle\bm{L}f(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{M_{\gamma}}f(\zeta)\frac{\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}ds_{\zeta}\wedge d\alpha_{\zeta}\wedge d\theta_{\zeta}}{\left[\left((\gamma-1)\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma}+\alpha_{z}^{\gamma}+i(s_{\zeta}-s_{z})\right)-\left(\gamma\alpha_{z}\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}e^{i(\theta_{z}-\theta_{\zeta})}\right)\right]^{2}}

(3.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{M_{\gamma}}\,\frac{f(\zeta)}{\big[A-Be^{i(\theta_{z}-\theta_{\zeta})}\big]^{2}}\,\sigma(\zeta),

where
\sigma(\zeta)=\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}ds_{\zeta}\wedge d\alpha_{\zeta}\wedge d\theta_{\zeta}
and

(3.7)

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=(\gamma-1)\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma}+\alpha_{z}^{\gamma}+i(s_{\zeta}-s_{z}),\qquad B:=\gamma\alpha_{z}\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}.

3.3.
Series expansion

The
S^{1}
action on
M_{\gamma}
yields a decomposition of the
L^{2}
-space

(3.8)

\displaystyle L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)=\bigoplus_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}L_{k}^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma),

where functions in
L_{k}^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
have the form
f_{k}(s,\alpha)e^{ik\theta}
. Each
f(\zeta)=f(s_{\zeta},\alpha_{\zeta},\theta_{\zeta})\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
decomposes as a partial Fourier series

(3.9)

f(s_{\zeta},\alpha_{\zeta},\theta_{\zeta})=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{k}(s_{\zeta},\alpha_{\zeta})\,e^{ik\theta_{\zeta}},

and the following version of Parseval’s theorem holds

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)}

\displaystyle=\int_{M_{\gamma}}|f(s_{\zeta},\alpha_{\zeta},\theta_{\zeta})|^{2}\,\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}ds_{\zeta}\,d\alpha_{\zeta}\,d\theta_{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{j,k=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{j}(s_{\zeta},\alpha_{\zeta})\overline{f_{k}(s_{\zeta},\alpha_{\zeta})}e^{i(j-k)\theta_{\zeta}}\right)\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}ds_{\zeta}\,d\alpha_{\zeta}\,d\theta_{\zeta}

(3.10)

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f_{k}(s_{\zeta},\alpha_{\zeta})|^{2}\,\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}ds_{\zeta}\,d\alpha_{\zeta}.

Return now to the computation of the Leray transform. The rational function appearing in the integrand of (
3.6
) may be expanded as a series as a consequence of the following:

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
x,y\geq 0
and
\gamma>1
. Then

(3.12)

x^{\gamma}+(\gamma-1)y^{\gamma}\geq\gamma xy^{\gamma-1},

with equality if and only if
x=y
.

Proof.

Divide both sides of (
3.12
) by
y^{\gamma}
and set
u=\frac{x}{y}
. Now let
f(u)=u^{\gamma}-\gamma u+(\gamma-1)
. This function is convex and attains its global minimum of
0
at
u=1
.
∎

Consider the formula for
\bm{L}f
in (
3.6
). Lemma
3.11
shows
\left|\frac{B}{A}\right|<1
, unless both
s_{z}=s_{\zeta}
and
\alpha_{z}=\alpha_{\zeta}
. Away from this set of
\sigma
-measure
0
,

(3.13)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\big[A-Be^{i(\theta_{z}-\theta_{\zeta})}\big]^{2}}=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\,(k+1)\left(\frac{B}{A}\right)^{k}e^{ik(\theta_{z}-\theta_{\zeta})}.

Returning now to (
3.6
),

\displaystyle\bm{L}f(s_{z},\alpha_{z},\theta_{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{M_{\gamma}}\,\frac{f(\zeta)\sigma(\zeta)}{\big[A-Be^{i(\theta_{z}-\theta_{\zeta})}\big]^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\,\frac{\gamma^{2}(k+1)}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{M_{\gamma}}f(\zeta)\frac{B^{k}}{A^{k+2}}e^{-ik\theta_{\zeta}}\,\sigma(\zeta)e^{ik\theta_{z}}

(3.14)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Lambda_{k}f(s_{z},\alpha_{z})e^{ik\theta_{z}}.

Now replace
f
with its Fourier expansion (
3.9
)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{k}f(s_{z},\alpha_{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}(k+1)}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{M_{\gamma}}\left(\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{j}(s_{\zeta},\alpha_{\zeta})\,e^{ij\theta_{\zeta}}\right)\frac{B^{k}}{A^{k+2}}e^{-ik\theta_{\zeta}}\,\sigma(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}(k+1)}{4\pi^{2}}\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{M_{\gamma}}\frac{B^{k}}{A^{k+2}}f_{j}(s_{\zeta},\alpha_{\zeta})e^{i(j-k)\theta_{\zeta}}\,\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}ds_{\zeta}d\alpha_{\zeta}d\theta_{\zeta}

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}(k+1)}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{B^{k}}{A^{k+2}}f_{k}(s_{\zeta},\alpha_{\zeta})\,\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}ds_{\zeta}\,d\alpha_{\zeta}.\

This gives the series decomposition of the Leray transform:

Definition 3.16
.

For each nonnegative integer
k
, define the sub-Leray operator
\bm{L}_{k}
to be the restriction of
\bm{L}
to the subspace
L_{k}^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
.

Remark 3.17
.

We later consider the same decomposition in the space
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
.
\lozenge

The orthogonal decomposition (
3.8
) shows
\bm{L}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}\bm{L}_{k}
, and (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) give that

(3.18)

\displaystyle\bm{L}_{k}f(s_{z},\alpha_{z},\theta_{z})=\Lambda_{k}f(s_{z},\alpha_{z})e^{ik\theta_{z}}.

3.4.
Fourier transforms

We continue the analysis of
\bm{L}_{k}
by taking a closer look at
\Lambda_{k}f(s_{z},\alpha_{z})
. The goal is to use the Fourier transform and understand the unitarily equivalent operator
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}
.

Importing
A
and
B
from (
3.7
),

(
3.15
)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{k+2}(k+1)\alpha_{z}^{k}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha_{\zeta}^{(k+1)(\gamma-1)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{f_{k}(s_{\zeta},\alpha_{\zeta})}{[(\gamma-1)\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma}+\alpha_{z}^{\gamma}+i(s_{\zeta}-s_{z})]^{k+2}}ds_{\zeta}\,d\alpha_{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\frac{(i\gamma)^{k+2}(k+1)\alpha_{z}^{k}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha_{\zeta}^{(k+1)(\gamma-1)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{f_{k}(s_{\zeta},\alpha_{\zeta})}{[(s_{z}-s_{\zeta})+i\big((\gamma-1)\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma}+\alpha_{z}^{\gamma}\big)]^{k+2}}ds_{\zeta}\,d\alpha_{\zeta}.

This shows that

(3.19)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{k}f(s_{z},\alpha_{z})=\frac{(i\gamma)^{k+2}(k+1)\alpha_{z}^{k}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha_{\zeta}^{(k+1)(\gamma-1)}\Big(f_{k}(\,\cdot\,,\alpha_{\zeta})\,*\,G_{k}\Big)(s_{z})\,d\alpha_{\zeta},

where the function
G_{k}
is given by

(3.20)

\displaystyle G_{k}(s):=\frac{1}{(s+i((\gamma-1)\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma}+\alpha_{z}^{\gamma}))^{k+2}}.

To better understand the integral defining
\Lambda_{k}f
, consider the Fourier transform
{\mathcal{F}}
and its inverse
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}
: For
g\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\cap L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
,

(3.21)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}g(\xi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(s)e^{-2\pi is\xi}\,ds,\qquad{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}g(\xi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(s)e^{2\pi is\xi}\,ds.

Under this convention,
{\mathcal{F}}
and
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}
are well known to transform convolutions to products

(3.22)

{\mathcal{F}}(g*h)(\xi)={\mathcal{F}}{g}(\xi)\cdot{\mathcal{F}}{h}(\xi),\qquad{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}(g*h)(\xi)={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}{g}(\xi)\cdot{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}{h}(\xi).

These operators also extend to isometries of
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})

(3.23)

\displaystyle\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}=\left\|{\mathcal{F}}g\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}=\left\|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}g\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}.

Applying
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}
in the
s_{z}
variable to (
3.19
) yields

(3.24)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\Lambda_{k}f(\xi,\alpha_{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{(i\gamma)^{k+2}(k+1)\alpha_{z}^{k}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha_{\zeta}^{(k+1)(\gamma-1)}{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\Big(f_{k}(\,\cdot\,,\alpha_{\zeta})\,*\,G_{k}\Big)(\xi)\,d\alpha_{\zeta}.

By (
3.22
),

(3.25)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\Big(f_{k}(\,\cdot\,,\alpha_{\zeta})\,*\,G_{k}\Big)(\xi)={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}f_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})\cdot{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}G_{k}(\xi).

Now calculate
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}G_{k}(\xi)
. From (
3.20
), notice the term
C:=(\gamma-1)\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma}+\alpha_{z}^{\gamma}>0
, unless both
\alpha_{z}=\alpha_{\zeta}=0
.

Proposition 3.26
.

Let
C>0
. The inverse Fourier transform of
G_{k}(s)=\frac{1}{(s+iC)^{k+2}}
is

(3.27)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}G_{k}(\xi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{2\pi is\xi}}{(s+iC)^{k+2}}\,ds=\begin{cases}0,&\xi\geq 0\\
\\
-\frac{(2\pi i)^{k+2}}{(k+1)!}\xi^{k+1}e^{2\pi\xi C},&\xi<0.\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

Since
C>0
, the function
H_{k}(z):=\frac{e^{2\pi iz\xi}}{(z+iC)^{k+2}}
has a pole in the lower half plane. For any
R>0
, the integral

\int_{-R}^{R}\frac{e^{2\pi is\xi}}{(s+iC)^{k+2}}\,ds.

can be thought of as a piece of a contour integral around a semicircle with base on the
x
-axis.

When
\xi>0
, consider such a semicircle in the upper half plane traversed counterclockwise. It can be seen that the radial portion of the integral tends to
0
as
R\to 0
. On the other hand, this function is holomorphic inside this semicircle contour. Thus Cauchy’s theorem implies
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}G_{k}(\xi)=0
for
\xi>0
.

When
\xi<0
, consider a semicircle in the lower half plane traversed clockwise. For
R
sufficiently large, the contour encloses the pole of
H_{k}
. As above, the radial portion of this integral tends to
0
as
R\to 0
. Thus, Cauchy’s integral formula shows

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{-R}^{R}\frac{e^{2\pi is\xi}}{(s+iC)^{k+2}}\,ds

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\pi i}{(k+1)!}\frac{d^{k+1}}{ds^{k+1}}\Big(e^{2\pi is\xi}\Big)\Bigg|_{s=-iC}=-\frac{(2\pi i)^{k+2}}{(k+1)!}\xi^{k+1}e^{2\pi\xi C},

completing the proof.
∎

Combining (
3.24
) and (
3.25
) with Proposition
3.26
shows

(3.28)

{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\Lambda_{k}f(\xi,\alpha_{z})=\eta_{k}(\xi)\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}f_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})\,\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}d\alpha_{\zeta},

where

(3.29)

\displaystyle\eta_{k}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-2\pi\xi)^{k+1}\gamma^{k+2}}{k!}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\{\xi<0\}},

(3.30)

\displaystyle\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{z}^{k}e^{2\pi\xi\alpha_{z}^{\gamma}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\{\xi<0\}},

(3.31)

\displaystyle\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\zeta}^{k(\gamma-1)}e^{2\pi\xi(\gamma-1)\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\{\xi<0\}},

and
\mathds{1}_{\{\xi<0\}}
is the indicator function of the set
\{\xi<0\}
. These three functions occur frequently throughout the rest of the paper.

Recall the one dimensional inner product
\big<\cdot,\cdot\big>_{(\sigma,1)}
defined by formula (
2.31
):

\Big<f,g\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(\alpha)\overline{g(\alpha)}\,\alpha^{\gamma-1}d\alpha.

We summarize this in the following

Proposition 3.32
.

The operator
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}
is given by

(3.33)

{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}f(\xi,\alpha_{z},\theta_{z})=\eta_{k}(\xi)\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})\Big<f_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}\,e^{ik\theta_{z}},

where
f(\xi,\alpha_{z},\theta_{z})=\sum_{j}f_{j}(\xi,\alpha_{z})e^{ik\theta_{z}}\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
.

Proof.

By its definition,
\bm{L}_{k}
is only a nonzero operator on
L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
. Since
{\mathcal{F}}f=\sum_{j}{\mathcal{F}}f_{j}e^{ij\theta}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}({\mathcal{F}}f)(\xi,\alpha_{z},\theta_{z})

\displaystyle={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\Lambda_{k}{\mathcal{F}}f_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})\,e^{ik\theta_{z}}

\displaystyle=\eta_{k}(\xi)\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}d\alpha_{\zeta}\right)e^{ik\theta_{z}}

\displaystyle=\eta_{k}(\xi)\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})\Big<f_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}\,e^{ik\theta_{z}},

where the second equality follows from (
3.28
).
∎

3.5.
Norms of the sub-Leray operators

Let us first record a computational lemma that will be used several times throughout the paper.

Proposition 3.34
.

Fix
x>-1
,
y>0
,
\gamma\geq 1
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha^{x}e^{-y\alpha^{\gamma}}\,d\alpha=\tfrac{1}{\gamma}\,y^{-\frac{1+x}{\gamma}}\Gamma\big(\tfrac{1+x}{\gamma}\big).

Proof.

This follows from the substitution
t=y\alpha^{\gamma}
and the definition of the
\Gamma
-function.
∎

This immediately implies the following

Corollary 3.35
.

Let
\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot)
and
\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)
be as defined in (
3.30
) and (
3.31
). Then for
\xi<0
,

(3.36)

\displaystyle\left\|\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\gamma}\cdot(-4\pi\xi)^{-1-\frac{2k}{\gamma}}\Gamma\big(\tfrac{2k}{\gamma}+1\big),

(3.37)

\displaystyle\left\|\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\gamma}\cdot(-4\pi\xi(\gamma-1))^{\frac{2k}{\gamma}-2k-1}\Gamma\big(1+2k-\tfrac{2k}{\gamma}\big).

Proof.

Recall that
\tau_{k}
and
\kappa_{k}
are nonzero only for
\xi<0
. Now by choosing the appropriate values of
x
and
y
in Proposition
3.34
,

\displaystyle\left\|\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha_{z}^{2k+\gamma-1}e^{4\pi\xi\alpha_{z}^{\gamma}}d\alpha_{z}=\tfrac{1}{\gamma}\cdot(-4\pi\xi)^{-1-\frac{2k}{\gamma}}\Gamma\big(\tfrac{2k}{\gamma}+1\big),

\displaystyle\left\|\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha_{\zeta}^{(2k+1)(\gamma-1)}e^{4\pi\xi(\gamma-1)\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma}}d\alpha_{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\gamma}\cdot(-4\pi\xi(\gamma-1))^{\frac{2k}{\gamma}-2k-1}\Gamma\big(1+2k-\tfrac{2k}{\gamma}\big).

∎

Now consider a function
f(\xi,\alpha_{z},\theta_{z})=f_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})e^{i\theta_{z}}\in L_{k}^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
. Equation (
3.33
) and Cauchy-Schwarz show

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}f(\xi,\alpha_{z},\theta_{z})|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\eta_{k}(\xi)|^{2}|\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})|^{2}\left|\Big<f_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}\right|^{2}

(3.38)

\displaystyle\leq|\eta_{k}(\xi)|^{2}|\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})|^{2}\left\|f_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2}\left\|\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2},

with equality if and only if
f_{k}(\xi,\cdot)
is a multiple of
\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)
, i.e.,

(3.39)

\displaystyle f_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})=m_{k}(\xi)\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z}),

where, recalling the definition of
\kappa_{k}
in (
3.31
), the multiplier function
m_{k}
must satisfy

(3.40)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}|m_{k}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{\frac{2k}{\gamma}-2k-1}\,d\xi<\infty.

Estimate (
3.38
) implies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}f(\xi,\alpha_{z},\theta_{z})|^{2}\alpha_{z}^{\gamma-1}d\alpha_{z}

\displaystyle\leq|\eta_{k}(\xi)|^{2}\left\|\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2}\left\|f_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2}\left\|\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2}

\displaystyle:=C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)\left\|f_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2},

where the (
\xi
independent) quantity
C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)=|\eta_{k}(\xi)|^{2}\left\|\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2}\left\|\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2}
. We will call this the
\sigma
-
symbol function
. Combining equations (
3.29
), (
3.36
) and (
3.37
), we see

(3.41)

\displaystyle C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)=\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2k}{\gamma}+1\big)\Gamma\big(2k-\frac{2k}{\gamma}+1\big)}{\Gamma(k+1)^{2}}\left(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\right)^{2k+2}(\gamma-1)^{\frac{2k}{\gamma}-2k-1}.

Detailed analysis of the
\sigma
-symbol function is carried out in Section
4
.

Remark 3.42
.

It is remarkable that
C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)
is independent of
\xi
. The more general symbol function
C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)
calculated in Section
5.1
also shares this property.
\lozenge

We now compute the norm of the operator
\bm{L}_{k}
.

Theorem 3.43
.

The operator
\bm{L}_{k}:L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)\to L_{k}^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
is bounded with norm given by

\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)}=\sqrt{C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)}.

Proof.

First note that
\bm{L}_{k}
is unitarily equivalent to
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}
. Now

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}f\right\|_{\sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}f(\xi,\alpha_{z},\theta_{z})|^{2}\alpha_{z}^{\gamma-1}\,d\xi\,d\alpha_{z}d\theta_{z}

(3.44)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\|f_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2}\,d\xi\,d\theta_{z}

\displaystyle=C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}|f_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})|^{2}\alpha_{z}^{\gamma-1}d\alpha_{z}\,d\xi\,d\theta_{z}

\displaystyle=C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)\left\|f\right\|_{\sigma}^{2}.

Equality in (
3.44
) holds if and only if
f_{k}
is of the form given by (
3.39
) and (
3.40
).
∎

Theorem 3.45
.

The operator
\bm{L}_{k}:L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)\to L_{k}^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
is a projection.

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
. It must be shown that
\bm{L}_{k}\circ\bm{L}_{k}=\bm{L}_{k}
. Proceed by conjugating by the Fourier transform. Proposition
3.32
says

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}\circ\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}(f)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}\circ{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}(f)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}\Big(\eta_{k}(\xi)\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})\Big<f_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}\,e^{ik\theta_{z}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\eta_{k}(\xi)\Big<f_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}\Big(\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})e^{ik\theta_{z}}\Big)

(3.46)

\displaystyle=\eta_{k}(\xi)^{2}\Big<f_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha^{\prime}_{z})\Big<\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)},

where
\alpha_{z}^{\prime}
denotes the radial part of the
\zeta_{1}
variable after two applications of
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}
.

From the definitions of
\tau_{k}
and
\kappa_{k}
in (
3.30
) and (
3.31
),

\displaystyle\Big<\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha)\overline{\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha)}\alpha^{\gamma-1}\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{\{\xi<0\}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha^{\gamma(k+1)-1}e^{2\pi\xi\gamma\alpha^{\gamma}}d\alpha

(3.47)

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{\{\xi<0\}}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{(-2\pi\xi)^{k+1}\gamma^{k+2}}

(3.48)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\eta_{k}(\xi)},

where (
3.47
) follows from Proposition
3.34
. Returning to (
3.46
), we now see that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}\circ\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}(f)

\displaystyle=\eta_{k}(\xi)^{2}\Big<f_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha^{\prime}_{z})\Big<\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}

\displaystyle=\eta_{k}(\xi)\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha^{\prime}_{z})\Big<f_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}(f).

This shows that
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}\circ\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}
and thus
\bm{L}_{k}\circ\bm{L}_{k}=\bm{L}_{k}
.
∎

3.6.
M_{\gamma}
at infinity

To understand the behavior of
M_{\gamma}
at infinity, apply the projective automorphism
\Phi
mapping
(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto(\frac{z_{1}}{z_{2}},\frac{1}{z_{2}})
. This transformation swaps the line
\{z_{2}=0\}
with the line at
\infty
. Setting
\widetilde{M}_{\gamma}:=\Phi({M_{\gamma}})
, the transformed hypersurface can be represented as follows:

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\left\{(z_{1},z_{2}):-|z_{2}|^{\gamma-2}\,\text{Im}{(z_{2})}=|z_{1}|^{\gamma}\right\}

(3.49)

\displaystyle=\left\{(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}):-y_{2}(x_{2}^{2}+y_{2}^{2})^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}=(x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2})^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\right\}

(3.50)

\displaystyle=\left\{(r_{1}e^{i\theta_{1}},r_{2}e^{i\theta_{2}}):-r_{2}^{\gamma-1}\sin{\theta_{2}}=r_{1}^{\gamma}\right\}.

The behavior of
\widetilde{M}_{\gamma}
at
z_{2}=0
gives the behavior of
M_{\gamma}
at
\infty
. For any
\gamma>1
, (
3.50
) shows that sending
r_{2}\to 0
forces
r_{1}\to 0
. This implies that the closure of
M_{\gamma}
in
{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{P}^{2}
contains a single point at infinity with homogeneous coordinates
[0:0:1]
. We now consider the regularity here:

Theorem 3.51
.

Let
\gamma>1
. The closure of
M_{\gamma}
in
{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{P}^{2}
fails to be a
C^{1}
submanifold near
[0:0:1]
except in the case
\gamma=2
. However,
M_{\gamma}
is globally Lipschitz.

Proof.

The discussion above lets us transfer the problem to
\widetilde{M}_{\gamma}
. If
\widetilde{M}_{\gamma}
were a
C^{1}
manifold at the origin, then one of the four real variables in (
3.49
) would be expressable as a
C^{1}
function of the other three in a neighborhood of the origin. Now, given the constraint in (
3.49
) that
y_{2}\leq 0
, we see that the only possibility is for
y_{2}
to be a function of
x_{1},y_{1}
and
x_{2}
.

Starting from the equation
(x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2})^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}+y_{2}(x_{2}^{2}+y_{2}^{2})^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}=0
, implicit differentiation shows

(3.52)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial y_{2}}{\partial x_{2}}=\frac{(2-\gamma)x_{2}y_{2}}{x_{2}^{2}+(\gamma-1)y_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2-\gamma)\cos{\theta_{2}}\sin{\theta_{2}}}{1+(\gamma-2)\sin^{2}{\theta_{2}}}.

Now set, for instance,
\theta_{2}=\pi
and
\theta_{2}=\frac{5\pi}{4}
in (
3.52
) and see that different values are obtained,
except in the case that
\gamma=2
. (Any two distinct choices of
\theta_{2}\in[\pi,2\pi]
can be used.) This shows that
\frac{\partial y_{2}}{\partial x_{2}}
fails to be continuous at the origin, which in turn proves
M_{\gamma}
is not
C^{1}
at infinity.

Now see that
M_{\gamma}
is Lipschitz. It will again suffice to consider
\widetilde{M}_{\gamma}
in the form of (
3.49
) and show that the derivatives
\frac{\partial y_{2}}{\partial x_{1}}
,
\frac{\partial y_{2}}{\partial y_{1}}
, and
\frac{\partial y_{2}}{\partial x_{2}}
are
L^{\infty}
functions near the origin.

First, see that the quantity
|1+(\gamma-2)\sin^{2}{\theta}|\geq\min\{1,{\gamma-1}\}
for any choice of
\theta
. Then implicit differentiation shows

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial y_{2}}{\partial x_{1}}\right|=\left|\frac{-\gamma x_{1}(x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2})^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}(x_{2}^{2}+y_{2}^{2})^{2-\frac{\gamma}{2}}}{x_{2}^{2}+(\gamma-1)y_{2}^{2}}\right|\lesssim r_{1}^{\gamma-1}r_{2}^{2-\gamma}\lesssim r_{2}^{\frac{\gamma^{2}-2\gamma+1}{\gamma}+{(2-\gamma)}}=r_{2}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}},

which is clearly bounded near the origin. By symmetry,
\frac{\partial y_{2}}{\partial y_{1}}
satisfies an analogous bound. Similarly, it quickly follows from (
3.52
) that
\left|\frac{\partial y_{2}}{\partial x_{2}}\right|\lesssim 1
, finishing the proof.
∎

4.
Analysis of the
\sigma
-symbol function

4.1.
Properties of
C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)

Analysis of
C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)
for each
k
value yields precise information on
\bm{L}
. Recall that

(4.1)

\displaystyle C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2k}{\gamma}+1\big)\Gamma\big(2k-\frac{2k}{\gamma}+1\big)}{\Gamma(k+1)^{2}}\left(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\right)^{2k+2}(\gamma-1)^{\frac{2k}{\gamma}-2k-1}.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\gamma>1
and
k
be a non-negative integer. The symbol function
C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)
has the following properties:

(a)

C_{\sigma}(\gamma,0)=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4(\gamma-1)}
.

(b)

C_{\sigma}(2,k)=1
for all positive integers
k
.

(c)

C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)
is Hölder symmetric in
\gamma
, i.e.,
C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)=C_{\sigma}\big(\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1},k\big)
.

(d)

For each
\gamma\neq 2
,
C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)
strictly decreases as a function of
k
.

(e)

\lim_{k\to\infty}C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)=\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}}
.

Proof.

Parts
(a)
and
(b)
are immediate from the formula. We will also quickly verify
(c)
and
(e)
, leaving
(d)
for the next section.

For part
(c)
, compute

\displaystyle C_{\sigma}\big(\tfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1},k\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2k(\gamma-1)}{\gamma}+1\big)\Gamma\big(2k-\frac{2k(\gamma-1)}{\gamma}+1\big)}{\Gamma(k+1)^{2}}\big(\tfrac{\gamma}{2\gamma-2}\big)^{2k+2}\big(\tfrac{1}{\gamma-1}\big)^{\frac{2k(\gamma-1)}{\gamma}-2k-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\big(2k-\frac{2k}{\gamma}+1\big)\Gamma\big(\frac{2k}{\gamma}+1\big)}{\Gamma(k+1)^{2}}\big(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\big)^{2k+2}(\gamma-1)^{\frac{2k}{\gamma}-2k-1}

\displaystyle=C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k).

Part
(e)
follows from Stirling’s formula. Recall that
f
and
g
are said to be
asymptotically equivalent
when

(4.3)

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=1.

When (
4.3
) holds, we write

\displaystyle f(x)\sim g(x).

Stirling’s formula says
\Gamma(x+1)\sim{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\left({\tfrac{x}{e}}\right)^{x}
, which implies the following asymptotic equivalences:

(4.4)

\displaystyle\Gamma({k+1})^{2}

\displaystyle\sim 2\pi e^{-2k}k^{2k+1},

(4.5)

\displaystyle\Gamma\big(\tfrac{2k}{\gamma}+1\big)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{2\pi}\cdot e^{-\frac{2k}{\gamma}}\big(\tfrac{2k}{\gamma}\big)^{\frac{2k}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}},

(4.6)

\displaystyle\Gamma\big(2k-\tfrac{2k}{\gamma}+1\big)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{2\pi}\cdot e^{\frac{2k}{\gamma}-2k}\big(\tfrac{2k}{\gamma}\big)^{2k-\frac{2k}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}}(\gamma-1)^{2k-\frac{2k}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}}.

Combining (
4.4
), (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) shows

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2k}{\gamma}+1\big)\Gamma\big(2k-\frac{2k}{\gamma}+1\big)}{\Gamma(k+1)^{2}}\sim\big(\tfrac{2}{\gamma}\big)^{2k+1}(\gamma-1)^{2k-\frac{2k}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)

\displaystyle\sim\big(\tfrac{2}{\gamma}\big)^{2k+1}(\gamma-1)^{2k-\frac{2k}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}}\big(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\big)^{2k+2}(\gamma-1)^{\frac{2k}{\gamma}-2k-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}}.

This completes the proof of item
(e)
.
∎

4.2.
Proof of item
(d)
in Theorem
4.2

We will prove that the function
C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)
decreases in the integer variable
k
by showing that

(4.7)

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k+1)}{C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)}\leq 1,

with equality holding only in the case of
\gamma=2
. (That equality (
4.7
) holds when
\gamma=2
is item
(b)
in Theorem
4.2
.) Note the Hölder symmetry in the variable
\gamma
(item
(c)
in Theorem
4.2
) lets us reduce our investigation to
1<\gamma<2
. After cancellation, the above ratio may be written as

(4.8)

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k+1)}{C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2}{\gamma}(k+1)\big)\Gamma\big((2-\frac{2}{\gamma})(k+1)\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2k}{\gamma}+1\big)\Gamma\big((2-\frac{2}{\gamma})k+1\big)}(\gamma-1)^{\frac{2}{\gamma}-1}.

Now observe further symmetries that simplify the situation. Letting
x=\frac{2}{\gamma}
(so
1<x<2
) and taking the logarithm of (
4.8
) leads to the definition of the function

(4.9)

\displaystyle A(k,x):=\log{\frac{\Gamma\left((k+1)x\right)}{\Gamma\left(kx+1\right)}}+\log\frac{\Gamma\left((k+1)(2-x)\right)}{\Gamma\left(k(2-x)+1\right)}+(x-1)\log\left(\frac{2}{x}-1\right).

Establishing inequality (
4.7
) amounts to showing that
A(k,x)<0
for each integer
k\geq 0
and
1<x<2
.

Notice that
A(k,1)=0
, so the required negativity of
A(k,x)
will follow after it is shown that

(4.10)

\frac{\partial A}{\partial x}(k,x)<0,\qquad 1<x<2.

With this in mind, observe that

\displaystyle A(k,x)

\displaystyle=\log\Gamma((k+1)x)-\log\Gamma(kx+1)-(x-1)\log x

\displaystyle\qquad+\log\Gamma((k+1)(2-x))-\log\Gamma(k(2-x)+1)-((2-x)-1)\log(2-x)

\displaystyle:=B(k,x)+B(k,2-x),

where

(4.11)

\displaystyle B(k,x)=\log{\Gamma((k+1)x)}-\log{\Gamma((kx+1))}-(x-1)\log x.

Therefore,

(4.12)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial A}{\partial x}(k,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial B}{\partial x}(k,x)-\frac{\partial B}{\partial x}(k,2-x).

Note that for
1<x<2
, the inequality
0<2-x<x
holds. The validity of (
4.10
) will follow from a stronger claim: that for each fixed integer
k\geq 0
,
\frac{\partial B}{\partial x}(k,x)
is a decreasing function for all
x>0
. This of course is equivalent to showing

(4.13)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}B}{\partial x^{2}}(k,x)<0,

for integers
k\geq 0
and all
x>0
. The establishment of (
4.13
) will imply (
4.10
), which in turn will imply (
4.7
) and complete the proof. In order to proceed, we need to better understand the logarithmic derivative of the gamma function.

4.2.1.
The digamma function

Define the digamma function
\psi
to be the logarithmic derivative of
\Gamma(x)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\psi(x):=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(x)}{\Gamma\left(x\right)},

and its derivative, the trigamma function
\psi^{\prime}(x)
. Properties of digamma, trigamma and further polygamma functions (obtained by taking further derivatives) have been extensively studied in special function theory; see, for instance,
[
2
]
.

From (
4.11
), it is seen that

\frac{\partial B}{\partial x}(k,x)=(k+1)\psi((k+1)x)-k\psi(kx+1)-\log{x}+\frac{1}{x}-1,

and

(4.14)

\frac{\partial^{2}B}{\partial x^{2}}(k,x)=(k+1)^{2}\psi^{\prime}((k+1)x)-k^{2}\psi^{\prime}(kx+1)-\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{x^{2}}.

We make use of the well known formula for
\psi^{\prime}(x)
, valid for all
x
outside of the non-positive integers:

(4.15)

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(j+x-1)^{2}}.

Substituting (
4.15
) into (
4.14
),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}B}{\partial x^{2}}(k,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{(j+(k+1)x-1)^{2}}-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2}}{(j+kx)^{2}}-\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{x^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{(j+(k+1)x)^{2}}-\frac{k^{2}}{(j+kx)^{2}}\right]-\frac{1}{x}.

\displaystyle:=D(k+1,x)-D(k,x),

where

(4.16)

D(k,x)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2}}{(j+kx)^{2}}-\frac{k}{x}.

Thus to establish
\frac{\partial^{2}B}{\partial x^{2}}(k,x)<0
, it is sufficient to show that for any
x>0
,
D(k,x)
decreases as a function of
k\geq 0
. Treating
k
as a continuous variable and differentiating, we claim

(4.17)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial D}{\partial k}(k,x)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{2kj}{(kx+j)^{3}}-\frac{1}{x}<0.

Setting
a:=kx
, inequality (
4.17
) is a consequence of the following.

Proposition 4.18
.

For all
a>0

(4.19)

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{2aj}{(a+j)^{3}}<1.

Proof.

Observe the following estimate:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{2j}{(a+j)^{3}}

\displaystyle<\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{2j}{(a+j-1)(a+j)(a+j+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{j}{(a+j-1)(a+j)}-\frac{j}{(a+j)(a+j+1)}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{1}{a+j-1}-\frac{1}{a+j}\right]+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{j-1}{(a+j-1)(a+j)}-\frac{j}{(a+j)(a+j+1)}\right]

(4.20)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a}.

∎

Proposition
4.18
shows that (
4.17
) holds, implying (
4.13
). This gives (
4.7
), completing the proof of item
(d)
in Theorem
4.2
. ∎

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Following Section
3.4
, we denote the Fourier transform in the
s
variable along with its inverse by
{\mathcal{F}}
and
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}
, respectively. These operators respect the decomposition of
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
into the orthogonal sum of the spaces
L_{k}^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
. Since each
\bm{L}_{k}
is a projection operator (Theorem
3.45
), the full
\bm{L}
is as well. We now confirm that the full operator is bounded and calculate its norm.

Since
{\mathcal{F}}
and
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}
are isometries,

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{\sigma}=\left\|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}{\mathcal{F}}\right\|_{\sigma}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{\sigma}=\left\|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}\right\|_{\sigma}.

In what follows, let
f(s,\alpha,\theta)=\sum_{j}f_{j}(s,\alpha)e^{ij\theta}\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma).

Since
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}
(and thus
\bm{L}_{k}
) is only non-zero when acting on
L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
,

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}{\mathcal{F}}f\right\|_{\sigma}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}f\right\|^{2}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}(f_{k}e^{ik\theta})\right\|^{2}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}\right\|^{2}_{\sigma}\left\|f_{k}\right\|^{2}_{\sigma}

(4.22)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}C_{\sigma}(\gamma,k)\left\|f_{k}\right\|^{2}_{\sigma}

(4.23)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4\gamma-4}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|f_{k}\right\|^{2}_{\sigma}=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4\gamma-4}\left\|f\right\|^{2}_{\sigma},

where (
4.22
) follows from Theorem
3.43
and (
4.23
) follows from Theorem
4.2
.
This tells us that

(4.24)

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{\sigma}\leq\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}}.

To see that equality holds in (
4.24
), let
f
be of a multiple of
\kappa_{0}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle f(\xi,\alpha,\theta)

\displaystyle=f_{0}(\xi,\alpha)

(4.25)

\displaystyle:=m_{0}(\xi)e^{2\pi\xi(\gamma-1)\alpha^{\gamma}}\mathds{1}_{\{\xi<0\}}\in L^{2}_{0}(M_{\gamma},\sigma),

where, by (
3.40
), the multiplier function
m_{0}
satisfies

(4.26)

\int_{-\infty}^{0}|m_{0}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{-1}\,d\xi<\infty.

With this choice of
f=f_{0}
, equations (
3.38
) and (
3.39
) show that

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}{\mathcal{F}}f\right\|_{\sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{M_{\gamma}}|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{0}{\mathcal{F}}f(\xi,\alpha,\theta)|^{2}\,\alpha^{\gamma-1}d\xi\wedge d\alpha\wedge d\theta

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{0}{\mathcal{F}}f(\xi,\alpha,\theta)|^{2}\alpha^{\gamma-1}d\alpha\,d\xi

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\eta_{0}(\xi)|^{2}\left\|f_{0}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2}\left\|\kappa_{0}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}|\tau_{0}(\xi,\alpha)|^{2}\alpha^{\gamma-1}d\alpha\right)d\xi

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\eta_{0}(\xi)|^{2}\left\|\kappa_{0}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2}\left\|\tau_{0}(\xi,\alpha)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2}\left\|f_{0}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle=2\pi\,C_{\sigma}(\gamma,0)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\|f_{0}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\sigma,1)}^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4\gamma-4}\left\|f\right\|^{2}_{\sigma}.

This shows the upper bound in (
4.24
) is actually attained. ∎

5.
Modified measures and adjoints

We have seen that
\bm{L}:L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)\to L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
is bounded, where
\sigma
is a constant multiple of the Leray-Levi measure
\lambda_{\rho}
. More flexibility on measures will now be allowed. For
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
, recall the rotationally invariant measure

\mu_{r}:=\alpha^{r}ds\wedge d\alpha\wedge d\theta.

5.1.
Leray boundedness

Just as in (
3.8
),
S^{1}
-invariance leads to the decomposition

(5.1)

L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})=\bigoplus_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r}),

where the functions in
L_{k}^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
take the form
f_{k}(s,\alpha)e^{ik\theta}
. In the spirit of Definition
3.16
, the restriction of
\bm{L}
to each
L_{k}^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
yields the decomposition
\bm{L}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}\bm{L}_{k}
.

We once again work with the unitarily equivalent
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}
. From Section
3.4
, recall

(5.2)

\displaystyle\eta_{k}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-2\pi\xi)^{k+1}\gamma^{k+2}}{k!}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\{\xi<0\}},

(5.3)

\displaystyle\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{k}e^{2\pi\xi\alpha^{\gamma}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\{\xi<0\}},

(5.4)

\displaystyle\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{k(\gamma-1)}e^{2\pi\xi(\gamma-1)\alpha^{\gamma}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\{\xi<0\}},

where
\mathds{1}_{\{\xi<0\}}
is the indicator function of the set
\{\xi<0\}
.

Let
f(s,\alpha,\theta)=\sum_{j}f_{j}(s,\alpha)e^{ij\theta}\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
. The form of Proposition
3.32
remains valid, i.e.,

(5.5)

{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}f(\xi,\alpha,\theta)=\eta_{k}(\xi)\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha)\Big<f_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}e^{ik\theta},

as long as the inner product makes sense.
Recall that
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{(\sigma,1)}
is a special case of (with
r=\gamma-1
):

(5.6)

\left<g,h\right>_{(\mu_{r},1)}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}g(\alpha)\overline{h(\alpha)}\,\alpha^{r}d\alpha.

When working with
\mu_{r}
, we will frequently encounter its counterpart measure
\mu_{r^{\prime}}
, where
r
and
r^{\prime}
are related by

(5.7)

\frac{r+r^{\prime}}{2}=\gamma-1.

The following version of Cauchy-Schwarz is crucial to the investigation of the boundedness of
\bm{L}_{k}
in
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Fix
\gamma>1
and let
r
and
r^{\prime}
be real numbers related by
\frac{r+r^{\prime}}{2}=\gamma-1
. Then

\left|\left<g,h\right>_{(\sigma,1)}\right|\leq\left\|g\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}\left\|h\right\|_{(\mu_{r^{\prime}},1)},

where the inner product and norms are defined by (
5.6
). Equality holds if and only if there exists a constant
c
such that
g(\alpha)\alpha^{r/2}=c\cdot h(\alpha)\alpha^{r^{\prime}/2}
. When this happens,

(5.9)

\left\|g\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}=|c|\cdot\left\|h\right\|_{(\mu_{r^{\prime}},1)}.

Proof.

Start by setting
r=\gamma-1
in (
5.6
). Split the integrand into
g_{1}(\alpha)=g(\alpha)\alpha^{r/2}
and
h_{1}(\alpha)=h(\alpha)\alpha^{r^{\prime}/2}
and apply the usual Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
∎

To make use of Lemma
5.8
, let us generalize Corollary
3.35
. The definitions of
\tau_{k}
and
\kappa_{k}
in (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) ensure that we are only interested in negative
\xi
. Combining (
5.7
) and Proposition
3.34
shows that for
\xi<0
:

(5.10)

\displaystyle\left\|\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha^{2k+r}e^{4\pi\xi\alpha^{\gamma}}d\alpha=\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{\gamma}(-4\pi\xi)^{-\left(\frac{2k+r+1}{\gamma}\right)}\Gamma\big(\tfrac{2k+r+1}{\gamma}\big)&\text{ for }r>-2k-1\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

(5.11)

\displaystyle\left\|\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r^{\prime}},1)}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha^{(2k+2)(\gamma-1)-r}e^{4\pi\xi(\gamma-1)\alpha^{\gamma}}d\alpha

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{\gamma}(-4\pi\xi(\gamma-1))^{-\left(\frac{(2k+2)(\gamma-1)-r+1}{\gamma}\right)}\Gamma\left(\tfrac{(2k+2)(\gamma-1)-r+1}{\gamma}\right)&\text{ for }r<(2k+2)(\gamma-1)+1\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

These computations suggest the definition of the interval

(5.12)

{\mathcal{I}}_{k}=(-2k-1,(2k+2)(\gamma-1)+1).

We are now able to prove the general boundedness result stated in Theorem
1.7
.

Theorem 5.13
.

Let
k\geq 0
be an integer and
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
\bm{L}_{k}
is bounded from
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})\to L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
if and only if
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
. Furthermore, when
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
,

(5.14)

\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})}=\sqrt{C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)},

where the
\mu_{r}
-symbol function is

(5.15)

C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)=\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\big)\Gamma\big(2k+2-\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\big)}{\Gamma(k+1)^{2}}\left(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\right)^{2k+2}(\gamma-1)^{-\left(2k+2-\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\right)}.

Proof.

Consider three cases:
(a)

r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
,
(b)

r\leq-2k-1
and
(c)

r\geq(2k+2)(\gamma-1)+1
.

(a)
: Let
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
and, without loss of generality, assume
f=f_{k}(\xi,\alpha)e^{ik\theta}\in L_{k}^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
. From (
5.5
),

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}f\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})}

\displaystyle=\int_{M_{\gamma}}\left|\eta_{k}(\xi)\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha)\Big<f_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}\,e^{ik\theta}\right|^{2}\mu_{r}

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\eta_{k}(\xi)\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha)\Big<f_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}\right|^{2}\alpha^{r}\,d\alpha\,d\xi

(5.16)

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left|\eta_{k}(\xi)\right|^{2}\left|\Big<f_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}\right|^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha)\right|^{2}\alpha^{r}\,d\alpha\,d\xi

(5.17)

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left|\eta_{k}(\xi)\right|^{2}\left\|\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}^{2}\left\|\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r^{\prime}},1)}^{2}\left\|f_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}^{2}\,d\xi,

where (
5.17
) follows from Lemma
5.8
. The norms appearing in the last integral are finite since
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
. By combining (
5.2
), (
5.10
) and (
5.11
) it is easily verified that the quantity

\left|\eta_{k}(\xi)\right|^{2}\left\|\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}^{2}\left\|\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r^{\prime}},1)}^{2}

appearing in the integrand of (
5.17
) equals
C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)
. Note that this more general symbol function is once again independent of
\xi
. Returning now to (
5.17
),

(5.18)

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}f\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})}

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi\,C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left\|f_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}^{2}\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\,C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}|f_{k}(\xi,\alpha)|^{2}\alpha^{r}d\alpha\,d\xi\,d\theta

\displaystyle=C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})}^{2}.

Thus,
\sqrt{C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)}
is an upper bound on
\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})}
. We now show this to be sharp. Lemma
5.8
says that equality in (
5.17
) holds if and only if

(5.19)

f_{k}(\xi,\alpha)\alpha^{r/2}=m_{k}(\xi)\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha)\alpha^{r^{\prime}/2},

where
m_{k}(\xi)
must be chosen so that
f(\xi,\alpha,\theta)=f_{k}(\xi,\alpha)e^{ik\theta}\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
. This happens if and only if

(5.20)

\int_{-\infty}^{0}|m_{k}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{\left(\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}-2k-2\right)}d\xi<\infty.

These conditions generalize (
3.39
) and (
3.40
). When
f
takes this form equality in (
5.18
) must hold. This establishes (
5.14
) and concludes case
(a)
.

(b)
: Let
r\leq-2k-1
. Combining (
5.5
) and (
5.16
), we see that for any
f(\xi,\alpha,\theta)=f_{k}(\xi,\alpha)e^{ik\theta}\in L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
with
\langle f_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\rangle_{(\sigma,1)}\neq 0
,
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}f\notin L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
, since
\left\|\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}^{2}=\infty
by (
5.10
).

(c)
: Let
r\geq(2k+2)(\gamma-1)+1
. For
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
set

\kappa_{k}^{j}(\xi,\alpha):=\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha)\cdot\mathds{1}_{\{\frac{1}{j}<\alpha\}},

where
\mathds{1}_{\{\frac{1}{j}<\alpha\}}
is the indicator function for the set
\{(\xi,\alpha,\theta):\frac{1}{j}<\alpha\}
. Now define

f_{k}^{j}(\xi,\alpha):=\tfrac{\kappa_{k}^{j}(\xi,\alpha)\alpha^{\gamma-1-r}}{\left\|\kappa_{k}^{j}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r^{\prime}},1)}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\{-\frac{1}{2\pi}<\xi<0\}}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad f^{j}(\xi,\alpha,\theta):=f_{k}^{j}(\xi,\alpha)e^{ik\theta}.

It is easily shown that for any
j
,

\left\|f_{k}^{j}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}=\mathds{1}_{\{-\frac{1}{2\pi}<\xi<0\}}.

Therefore

(5.21)

\left\|f^{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})}=\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\|f_{k}^{j}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|^{2}_{(\mu_{r},1)}\,d\xi\,d\theta\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=1.

From (
5.5
),

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}f^{j}(\xi,\alpha,\theta)

\displaystyle=\eta_{k}(\xi)\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha)\Big<f^{j}_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}e^{ik\theta}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{\{-\frac{1}{2\pi}<\xi<0\}}\cdot\eta_{k}(\xi)\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha)\left\|\kappa_{k}^{j}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r^{\prime}},1)}e^{ik\theta},

and consequently

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}f^{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{-\frac{1}{2\pi}}^{0}|\eta_{k}(\xi)|^{2}\left\|\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|^{2}_{(\mu_{r},1)}\left\|\kappa_{k}^{j}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|^{2}_{(\mu_{r^{\prime}},1)}\,d\xi.

But the monotone convergence theorem says

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\left\|\kappa_{k}^{j}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|^{2}_{(\mu_{r^{\prime}},1)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\frac{1}{j}}^{\infty}|\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha)|^{2}\alpha^{r^{\prime}}\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\frac{1}{j}}^{\infty}\alpha^{(2k+2)(\gamma-1)-r}e^{4\pi\xi(\gamma-1)\alpha^{\gamma}}\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\infty

for any
-\frac{1}{2\pi}<\xi<0
. This means

\lim_{j\to\infty}\left\|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}f^{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})}=\infty,

which together with (
5.21
) shows that
\bm{L}_{k}
fails to be bounded. This completes
(c)
.
∎

Fix any
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Despite the limited boundedness range in Theorem
5.13
, the interval
{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
tends to
(-\infty,\infty)
as
k\to\infty
. This means that the tail of the sequence of operators
\{\bm{L}_{k}\}
is always bounded in the
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
norm. In fact, a much stronger result holds:

Theorem 5.22
.

Fix
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The high frequency limit norm of
\bm{L}
in
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
is given by the (
r
-independent) quantity

(5.23)

\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{L_{HF}^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}}}.

Proof.

For
k
large enough, Theorem
5.13
says

(5.24)

\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{\mu_{r}}^{2}=\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\big)\Gamma\big(2k+2-\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\big)}{\Gamma(k+1)^{2}}\left(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\right)^{2k+2}(\gamma-1)^{-\left(2k+2-\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\right)}.

First focus on the following quotient:

(5.25)

\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\big)\Gamma\big(2k+2-\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\big)}{\Gamma(k+1)^{2}}.

Sterling’s formula says

\displaystyle\Gamma({k+1})^{2}

\displaystyle\sim 2\pi e^{-2k}k^{2k+1},

\displaystyle\Gamma\big(\tfrac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\big)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{2\pi}\cdot e^{\left(1-\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\right)}\left(\tfrac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}-1\right)^{\left(\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}\right)},

\displaystyle\Gamma\big(2k+2-\tfrac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\big)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{2\pi}\cdot e^{\left(-2k-1+\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\right)}\left(2k+1-\tfrac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\right)^{\left(2k+\frac{3}{2}-\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\right)}.

Combining these asymptotic equivalences yields

(
5.25
)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{k^{2k+1}}\left(\tfrac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}-1\right)^{\left(\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}\right)}\left(2k+1-\tfrac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\right)^{\left(2k+\frac{3}{2}-\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2k+1}}\cdot\left(2+\tfrac{1+r-\gamma}{k}\right)^{\left(\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}\right)}\left(2\gamma-2+\tfrac{\gamma-1-r}{k}\right)^{\left(2k+\frac{3}{2}-\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\right)}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{\gamma^{2k+1}}\cdot 2^{\left(\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}\right)}\left(2\gamma-2\right)^{\left(2k+\frac{3}{2}-\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\right)}

(5.26)

\displaystyle=\left(\tfrac{2}{\gamma}\right)^{2k+1}\left(\gamma-1\right)^{\left(2k+\frac{3}{2}-\frac{2k+1+r}{\gamma}\right)}.

Now combine (
5.24
) and (
5.26
) to see that

(5.27)

\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{\mu_{r}}^{2}\sim\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\gamma-1}},

which completes the proof.
∎

We now establish the sharp interval of Leray boundedness as stated in Theorem 1.4.

Corollary 5.28
.

The Leray transform
\bm{L}
is bounded from
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})\to L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
if and only if
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0}=(-1,2\gamma-1)
.

Proof.

If
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
, then each
\bm{L}_{k}
is bounded on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
since
{\mathcal{I}}_{0}\subset{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
for all
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
. And since
\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{\mu_{r}}
is finite, we conclude that there is some
C>0
with
\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{\mu_{r}}\leq C
for all
k
. Let
f=\sum_{k}f_{k}e^{ik\theta}
. Then,

\left\|\bm{L}f\right\|_{\mu_{r}}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|\bm{L}_{k}f\right\|_{\mu_{r}}^{2}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\right\|_{\mu_{r}}^{2}\left\|f_{k}\right\|_{\mu_{r}}^{2}\leq C^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|f_{k}\right\|_{\mu_{r}}^{2}=C^{2}\left\|f\right\|_{\mu_{r}}^{2}.

On the other hand if
r\notin{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
, then
\bm{L}_{0}
is unbounded, implying the same for
\bm{L}
.
∎

Remark 5.29
.

Notice that
r_{0}=\gamma-1
(the
r
-value for the measure
\sigma
) lies exactly at the midpoint the interval
{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
. This says that if
\frac{r+r^{\prime}}{2}=\gamma-1
, then
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
if and only if
r^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
. In other words,
\bm{L}
is bounded on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
if and only if it is bounded on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r^{\prime}})
.
\lozenge

5.2.
Adjoints

Theorem
5.13
says
\bm{L}_{k}
is bounded on
L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
when
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
. It therefore admits a bounded adjoint
\bm{L}_{k}^{(*,\mu_{r})}
which satisfies

(5.30)

\displaystyle\Big<\bm{L}_{k}f,g\Big>_{\mu_{r}}=\Big<f,\bm{L}_{k}^{(*,\mu_{r})}g\Big>_{\mu_{r}}.

We wish to describe this adjoint more explicitly.

The following lemma justifies later applications of Fubini’s theorem.

Lemma 5.31
.

Choose
r
and
k
so that
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
, and take
\eta_{k},\tau_{k},\kappa_{k}
as in (
5.2
), (
5.3
), (
5.4
).

(a) Let
f_{k}(\xi,\alpha)e^{ik\theta},g_{k}(\xi,\alpha)e^{ik\theta}\in L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
. Then the integral

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big|\eta_{k}(\xi)\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})f_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})g_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})\Big|\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}\alpha_{z}^{r}\,d\alpha_{z}\,d\alpha_{\zeta}\,d\xi\,d\theta

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)}\left\|f_{k}\right\|_{\mu_{r}}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{\mu_{r}}.

(b) Let
\phi_{k}(\xi,\alpha)e^{ik\theta}\in L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
and
\psi_{k}(\xi,\alpha)e^{ik\theta}\in L^{2}_{k}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r^{\prime}})
. Then the integral

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big|\eta_{k}(\xi)\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})\phi_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})\psi_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})\Big|\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}\alpha_{z}^{\gamma-1}\,d\alpha_{z}\,d\alpha_{\zeta}\,d\xi\,d\theta

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)}\left\|\phi_{k}\right\|_{\mu_{r}}\left\|\psi_{k}\right\|_{\mu_{r^{\prime}}}.

Proof.

Recall that
\eta_{k},\tau_{k},\kappa_{k}
are everywhere non-negative and identically
0
for
\xi\geq 0
. Write

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{0}\eta_{k}(\xi)\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}|g_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})|\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})\alpha_{z}^{r}d\alpha_{z}\right]\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})|f_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})|\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}d\alpha_{\zeta}\right]d\xi

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{0}\eta_{k}(\xi)\left\|g_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}\left\|\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}\left\|f_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}\left\|\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r^{\prime}},1)}d\xi

\displaystyle=2\pi\sqrt{C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left\|g_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}\left\|f_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}d\xi

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)}\left\|f_{k}\right\|_{\mu_{r}}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{\mu_{r}}.

In the first inequality we used both the standard version of Cauchy-Schwarz and the variant from Lemma
5.8
. Also, recall that
\eta_{k}(\xi)\left\|\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}\left\|\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r^{\prime}},1)}
is equal to the (
\xi
-independent) term
\sqrt{C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)}
encountered in Theorem
5.13
.

Similarly,

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{0}\eta_{k}(\xi)\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}|\psi_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})|\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})\alpha_{z}^{\gamma-1}d\alpha_{z}\right]\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})|\phi_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})|\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}d\alpha_{\zeta}\right]d\xi

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{0}\eta_{k}(\xi)\left\|\psi_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r^{\prime}},1)}\left\|\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}\left\|\phi_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}\left\|\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r^{\prime}},1)}d\xi

\displaystyle=2\pi\sqrt{C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left\|\psi_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r^{\prime}},1)}\left\|\phi_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\right\|_{(\mu_{r},1)}d\xi

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{C_{\mu_{r}}(\gamma,k)}\left\|\phi_{k}\right\|_{\mu_{r}}\left\|\psi_{k}\right\|_{\mu_{r^{\prime}}},

completing the proof.
∎

We now compute a formula for
({\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}})^{(*,\mu_{r})}={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}^{(*,\mu_{r})}{\mathcal{F}}
when
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
:

Proposition 5.32
.

Choose
k
and
r
so that
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
. Then if
g(\xi,\alpha,\theta)=\sum_{j}g_{j}(\xi,\alpha)e^{ij\theta}\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r}),

(5.33)

{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}^{(*,\mu_{r})}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}g(\xi,\alpha,\theta)=\alpha^{\gamma-1-r}\eta_{k}(\xi)\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha)\Big<g_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\mu_{r},1)}e^{ik\theta},

where
\eta_{k},\tau_{k},\kappa_{k}
are the real valued functions given by (
5.2
), (
5.3
), (
5.4
), respectively.

Proof.

For
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
, the boundedness of
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}
shows there exists an adjoint operator
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}^{(*,\mu_{r})}{\mathcal{F}}
satisfying

(5.34)

\Big<{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}f,g\Big>_{\mu_{r}}=\Big<f,{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}^{(*,\mu_{r})}{\mathcal{F}}g\Big>_{\mu_{r}}

for all
f,g\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
. Without loss of generality, assume that
f(\xi,\alpha,\theta)=f_{k}(\xi,\alpha)e^{ik\theta}
.

Lemma
5.31
part (a) justifies the use of Fubini’s theorem in what follows:

\displaystyle\Big<{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}f,g\Big>_{\mu_{r}}

\displaystyle=\int_{M_{\gamma}}\eta_{k}(\xi)\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})\Big<f_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}\,e^{ik\theta}\overline{g_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})e^{ik\theta}}\,\mu_{r}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{M_{\gamma}}\eta_{k}(\xi)\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})\overline{g_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})}\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}d\alpha_{\zeta}\alpha_{z}^{r}d\xi\,d\alpha_{z}\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{M_{\gamma}}f_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}\eta_{k}(\xi)\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})\int_{0}^{\infty}\overline{g_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})}\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})\alpha_{z}^{r}d\alpha_{z}d\xi\,d\alpha_{\zeta}\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{M_{\gamma}}f_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})e^{ik\theta}\overline{\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1-r}\eta_{k}(\xi)\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})\Big<g_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\mu_{r},1)}e^{ik\theta}}\mu_{r}(\zeta)

(5.35)

\displaystyle:=\Big<f,{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}^{(*,\mu_{r})}{\mathcal{F}}g\Big>_{\mu_{r}},

completing the proof.
∎

We now obtain a formula for the adjoint of the full operator
\bm{L}
in the space
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\sigma)
.

Proposition 5.36
.

The adjoint of the Leray transform with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\sigma}
is given by the following integral:

(5.37)

\bm{L}^{(*,\sigma)}g(z)=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{8\pi^{2}i}\int_{M_{\gamma}}g(\zeta)\frac{|\zeta_{1}|^{\gamma-2}d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}}{\left[\gamma{z}_{1}|z_{1}|^{\gamma-2}(\overline{z}_{1}-\overline{\zeta}_{1})-i(\overline{z}_{2}-\overline{\zeta}_{2})\right]^{2}}.

Proof.

Define an integral operator
\bm{T}
, where
\bm{T}g(z)
is equal to the right hand side of (
5.37
) for all choices of
g
for which this integral converges. Referring back to (
2.3a
) and (
2.3b
), we see that
\bm{T}
can be expressed as

\bm{T}g(z)=\int_{M_{\gamma}}\overline{\ell_{\rho}(\zeta,z)}\lambda_{\rho}(\zeta).

Revisit Section
3.2
, but this time start with
\bm{T}
and carry out the reparametrization (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) together with all subsequent steps through Section
3.3
. In particular,
\bm{T}
admits an orthogonal decomposition into
\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}\bm{T}_{k}
.

Now for each
\bm{T}_{k}
(following the outline in Section
3.4
), conjugate by the Fourier transform to obtain the operator
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{T}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}
. This leads to a result in the spirit of Proposition
3.32
:

(5.38)

{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{T}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}g(\xi,\alpha,\theta)=\eta_{k}(\xi)\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha)\Big<g_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}e^{ik\theta}.

Since
\sigma=\mu_{\gamma-1}
, we see that (
5.38
) is exactly (
5.33
) when
r=\gamma-1
. This says that for each
k
,
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{T}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}^{(*,\sigma)}{\mathcal{F}}
, implying each
\bm{T}_{k}=\bm{L}_{k}^{(*,\sigma)}
and therefore
\bm{T}=\bm{L}^{(*,\sigma)}
.
∎

Lemma 5.39
.

Let
\frac{r+r^{\prime}}{2}=\gamma-1
. The map
\bm{R}_{r}:L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r^{\prime}})\to L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
given by

(5.40)

g(\xi,\alpha,\theta)\longmapsto\alpha^{\gamma-1-r}g(\xi,\alpha,\theta)

is an isometry.

Proof.

This is essentially a reformulation of the condition which tells when equality holds in Lemma
5.8
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{R}_{r}g\right\|_{\mu_{r}}^{2}=\int_{M_{\gamma}}|\alpha^{\gamma-1-r}g(\xi,\alpha,\theta)|^{2}\alpha^{r}\,d\xi\,d\alpha\,d\theta=\int_{M_{\gamma}}|g(\xi,\alpha,\theta)|^{2}\alpha^{r^{\prime}}\,d\xi\,d\alpha\,d\theta=\left\|g\right\|_{\mu_{r^{\prime}}}^{2}.

It is clear this map is bijective with inverse
\bm{R}_{r}^{-1}
given by
h(\xi,\alpha,\theta)\mapsto\alpha^{r+1-\gamma}h(\xi,\alpha,\theta)
.
∎

For
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
, we now give an explicit formula for the more general adjoint
\bm{L}^{(*,\mu_{r})}
:

Theorem 5.41
.

Let
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0}=(-1,2\gamma-1)
. The adjoint of the Leray transform in the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mu_{r}}
is found by computing the following integral

(5.42)

\bm{L}^{(*,\mu_{r})}g(z)=\frac{\gamma^{2}|z_{1}|^{\gamma-1-r}}{8\pi^{2}i}\int_{M_{\gamma}}g(\zeta)\frac{|\zeta_{1}|^{r-1}d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}}{\left[\gamma{z}_{1}|z_{1}|^{\gamma-2}(\overline{z}_{1}-\overline{\zeta}_{1})-i(\overline{z}_{2}-\overline{\zeta}_{2})\right]^{2}}.

Proof.

Let
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
,
f\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
and
g\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r^{\prime}})
. Remark
5.29
guarantees
r^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
, so
\bm{L}
is bounded in both of these function spaces.

The Fourier transform
{\mathcal{F}}
(acting in the variable
s=\re(\zeta_{2})
) is obiviously an isometry of both
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
and
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r^{\prime}})
. Now write
f={\mathcal{F}}(\phi),g={\mathcal{F}}(\psi)
for
\phi=\sum_{j}\phi_{j}e^{ij\theta}\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
,
\psi=\sum_{j}\psi_{k}e^{ij\theta}\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r^{\prime}})
. On one hand,

(5.43)

\langle\bm{L}f,g\rangle_{\sigma}=\big\langle\bm{L}{\mathcal{F}}(\phi),{\mathcal{F}}(\psi)\big\rangle_{\sigma}=\big\langle{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}{\mathcal{F}}(\phi),\psi\big\rangle_{\sigma}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\big\langle{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}(\phi),\psi\big\rangle_{\sigma},

where the summand can be re-written as

\displaystyle\big\langle{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}{\mathcal{F}}(\phi),\psi\big\rangle_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\int_{M_{\gamma}}\eta_{k}(\xi)\tau_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})\Big<\phi_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\kappa_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}\overline{\psi_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})}\,\alpha_{z}^{\gamma-1}d\xi\,d\alpha_{z}\,d\theta

(5.44)

\displaystyle=\int_{M_{\gamma}}\phi_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{z})\overline{\eta_{k}(\xi)\kappa_{k}(\xi,\alpha_{\zeta})\Big<\psi_{k}(\xi,\cdot),\tau_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\Big>_{(\sigma,1)}}\,\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma-1}d\xi\,d\alpha_{\zeta}\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\big\langle\phi,{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}^{(*,\sigma)}{\mathcal{F}}(\psi)\big\rangle_{\sigma}.

The rearrangement in (
5.44
) is justified by Lemma
5.31
part (b) together with the fact that
\eta_{k},\tau_{k}
and
\kappa_{k}
are real valued. Thus,

\displaystyle\eqref{E:mu_r-adoint-comp-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\big\langle\phi,{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{k}^{(*,\sigma)}{\mathcal{F}}(\psi)\big\rangle_{\sigma}=\big\langle\phi,{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\bm{L}^{(*,\sigma)}{\mathcal{F}}(\psi)\big\rangle_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\langle f,\bm{L}^{(*,\sigma)}g\rangle_{\sigma}

(5.45)

\displaystyle=\langle f,\bm{R}_{r}\bm{L}^{(*,\sigma)}g\rangle_{\mu_{r}}.

Notice that Lemma
5.39
guarantees that
\bm{R}_{r}\bm{L}^{(*,\sigma)}g\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
.

On the other hand,

(5.46)

\langle\bm{L}f,g\rangle_{\sigma}=\langle\bm{L}f,\bm{R}_{r}g\rangle_{\mu_{r}}=\langle f,\bm{L}^{(*,\mu_{r})}\bm{R}_{r}g\rangle_{\mu_{r}}.

Equating (
5.45
) and (
5.46
) shows
\bm{R}_{r}\bm{L}^{(*,\sigma)}g=\bm{L}^{(*,\mu_{r})}\bm{R}_{r}g
for all
g\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},r^{\prime})
. This is equivalent to saying that, as an operator on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
,

(5.47)

\bm{L}^{(*,\mu_{r})}=\bm{R}_{r}\bm{L}^{(*,\sigma)}\bm{R}_{r}^{-1}.

Writing this out as an integral equation yields (
5.42
).
∎

5.3.
Related operators

In the discussion below, we often simplify notation and write
\bm{L}^{(*,\mu_{r})}=\bm{L}^{*}
. This convention is also extended to other operators.

Proposition 5.48
.

\bm{L}:L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})\to L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
is a projection for
r\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0}=(-1,2\gamma-1)

Proof.

If

\bm{L}
is bounded on

. Formula (

and the same argument given in Theorem

shows that each

, and consequently the full operator
\bm{L}
, is a projection.
∎

Let

so that
\bm{L}:L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})\to L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
is bounded. Define a Hardy space in the following way:

(5.49)

H^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r}):=\bm{L}\left(L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})\right)=\ker\left({{\bm{L}}}-I\right).

It is verified in the Appendix that
H^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
consists entirely of boundary values of holomorphic functions. In what follows, write
H^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})=H
.

Let
\bm{K}\colon H^{\perp}\to H
be the restriction of
\bm{L}
to
H^{\perp}
; thus, with respect to the decomposition
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})=H\oplus H^{\perp}
,
\bm{L}
is given by the operator matrix

\begin{pmatrix}I&\bm{K}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Similarly,
\bm{L}^{*}
is given by

\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
\bm{K}^{*}&0\end{pmatrix},

\bm{L}^{*}\bm{L}
is given by

\begin{pmatrix}I&\bm{K}\\
\bm{K}^{*}&0\end{pmatrix},

\bm{L}\bm{L}^{*}
is given by

\begin{pmatrix}I+\bm{K}\bm{K}^{*}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

and
\bm{A}:=\bm{L}^{*}-\bm{L}
is given by

\begin{pmatrix}0&-\bm{K}\\
\bm{K}^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}.

These representations are standard operator theory facts (see, e.g., (2.1.6) and (2.1.34) in
[
35
]
) and it is easy to verify that

(5.50a)

\displaystyle\|\bm{A}\|

\displaystyle=\|\bm{K}\|=\|\bm{K}^{*}\|

(5.50b)

\displaystyle\|\bm{L}\|

\displaystyle=\|\bm{L}^{*}\|=\sqrt{1+\|\bm{K}\|^{2}}

(5.50c)

\displaystyle\|\bm{L}\bm{L}^{*}\|

\displaystyle=\|\bm{L}^{*}\bm{L}\|=\|\bm{L}\|^{2}=1+\|\bm{K}\|^{2}.

The operator
\bm{A}
stems from work of Kerzman and Stein
[
24
,
25
]
examining the relation between certain Cauchy-Fantappiè projections and the self-adjoint Szegő projection

{\bm{S}}
(corresponding to
\bm{K}=0
). See
[
13
,
12
,
5
,
16
,
10
]
for results on

in the complex plane and
[
9
]
for results on Reinhardt domains in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.

The formulas in (
5.50
) respect the decomposition
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}L_{k}^{2}(M_{\gamma},\mu_{r})
, i.e., identical statements hold with
\bm{L}
,
\bm{A}
and
\bm{K}
replaced by
\bm{L}_{k}
,

and

6.
Projective Duality

We now introduce the notion of
projective duality
. Define
{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{P}^{n*}
to be the set of hyperplanes in
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
. It is easily seen that this set can be isomorphically identified with
{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Indeed, each hyperplane in
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
corresponds to a unique point
\zeta=[\zeta_{0}:\dots:\zeta_{n}]\in{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{P}^{n}
:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}_{\zeta}=\{\zeta^{*}\in{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}:\zeta_{0}\zeta_{0}^{*}+\dots+\zeta_{n}\zeta_{n}^{*}=0\},

and it is clear that this identification is reflexive.

Given a domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
with smooth boundary
{\mathcal{S}}
, we define the dual
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}\subset{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n*}
to be the set of complex hyperplanes tangent to
{\mathcal{S}}
. See
[
1
]
for a detailed treatment of this topic; also see
[
7
,
8
]
for related recent developments. When
{\mathcal{S}}
is a smooth and strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex hypersurface,
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
is also smooth and strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex and
{\mathcal{S}}^{**}={\mathcal{S}}
. However, such regularity properties for
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
often fail under weaker hypotheses on
{\mathcal{S}}
; see Remark
6.16
.

From the denominator of
\mathscr{L}_{{\mathcal{S}}}
in (
2.1b
), we see that the complex tangent hyperplanes to a smooth hypersurface
{\mathcal{S}}
play a critical role in the Leray transform. Strong
{\mathbb{C}}
-convexity ensures that each supporting hyperplane intersects
\overline{\Omega}
at the point of tangency and nowhere else (see Section 2.5 in
[
1
]
). In
[
4
]
, the first author develops the connection between the Leray transforms
\bm{L}_{{\mathcal{S}}}
and
\bm{L}_{{\mathcal{S}}^{*}}
and shows that these operators respect a natural bilinear pairing of two “dual” Hardy spaces when
{\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
are smoothly bounded, strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex hypersurfaces. The Leray transform plays a role in higher dimensions analogous to the role played by the Cauchy transform of a planar domain in pairing two Hardy spaces of holomorphic boundary values associated to the interior and exterior of a planar curve, respectively; see
[
32
]
. We extend this work in Section
6.4
.

6.1.
The dual hypersurface of
M_{\gamma}

If
{\mathcal{S}}
is a smooth strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex hypersurface, we can choose an appropriate matrix
A\in GL(n+1,{\mathbb{C}})
to induce an affinization of
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n*}
and thereby represent
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
as a hypersurface in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. The details below were first introduced by the authors in Section 4 of
[
6
]
.

Define the map
\Phi_{A}:{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
by calculating the following matrix product:

(6.1)

\Phi_{A}((z_{1},\dots,z_{n}),(w_{1},\dots,w_{n}))=[1\,\,\,\,w_{1}\,\,\dots\,\,w_{n}]\,A\,[1\,\,\,\,z_{1}\,\,\dots\,\,z_{n}]^{T}.

The following proposition is stated for two dimensional
{\mathcal{S}}
, but it admits a straightforward generalization to higher dimensions.

Proposition 6.2
.

Given a smooth real hypersurface
{\mathcal{S}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
with defining function
\rho

and a matrix

(6.3)

A=\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}&a_{1}&a_{2}\\
b_{1}&m_{11}&m_{12}\\
b_{2}&m_{21}&m_{22}\end{pmatrix}\in GL(3,{\mathbb{C}})

the following conditions are equivalent:

(6.4a)

There are functions
w_{1}^{A}
and
w_{2}^{A}
on
{\mathcal{S}}
with the property that the complex tangent line to
{\mathcal{S}}
at
\zeta
is given by

\left\{(z_{1},z_{2})\colon\Phi_{A}\big((z_{1},z_{2}),(w_{1}^{A}(\zeta),w_{2}^{A}(\zeta))\big)=0\right\}.

(6.4b)

Same as
(
6.2
a
)
with the additional conditions that

w_{1}^{A}
and
w_{2}^{A}
are uniquely determined and smooth.

(6.4c)

The matrix
\begin{pmatrix}m_{11}\zeta_{1}+m_{12}\zeta_{2}+b_{1}&m_{21}\zeta_{1}+m_{22}\zeta_{2}+b_{2}\\
m_{11}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\zeta_{2}}-m_{12}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\zeta_{1}}&m_{21}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\zeta_{2}}-m_{22}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\zeta_{1}}\end{pmatrix}
is invertible for all
\zeta\in{\mathcal{S}}
.

Proof.

The complex tangent line to
{\mathcal{S}}
at
\zeta
is the unique (affine) line joining
\zeta
to the point
\zeta+\big(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\zeta_{2}},-\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\zeta_{1}}\big)
. This follows from the fact that the vector field
\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\zeta_{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta_{1}}-\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\zeta_{1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta_{2}}
is tangent to
{\mathcal{S}}
at
\zeta
. To try to represent the tangent line in the desired form we seek solutions
(w_{1}^{A},w_{2}^{A})
of the inhomogeneous linear system

\displaystyle(1,w_{1}^{A},w_{2}^{A})\,A\,(1,\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})^{T}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle(1,w_{1}^{A},w_{2}^{A})\,A\,\left(1,\zeta_{1}+\tfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial\zeta_{2}},\zeta_{2}-\tfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial\zeta_{1}}\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=0.

Uniqueness of the line implies that the system has at most one solution, so if there is any solution the associated homogeneous system is non-singular; conversely, non-singularity of the associated homogeneous system implies the existence of a unique smooth solution of the inhomogeneous system. A computation reveals that the matrix representing the associated homogeneous system is

\begin{pmatrix}m_{11}\zeta_{1}+m_{12}\zeta_{2}+b_{1}&m_{21}\zeta_{1}+m_{22}\zeta_{2}+b_{2}\\
m_{11}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\zeta_{2}}-m_{12}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\zeta_{1}}&m_{21}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\zeta_{2}}-m_{22}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\zeta_{1}}\end{pmatrix}.

∎

\displaystyle=\frac{i\rho_{\zeta_{1}}}{\gamma\rho_{\zeta_{2}}},\qquad w_{2}^{A_{\gamma}}(\zeta)=\frac{\zeta_{1}\rho_{\zeta_{1}}+\zeta_{2}\rho_{\zeta_{2}}}{(1-\gamma)\rho_{\zeta_{2}}}.

(6.11b)

6.2.
Distinguished measures on

In Section

6.2.1.
The pairing measure

Let

be as in (

) and
{\mathcal{S}}
an
A
-admissible, strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex hypersurface in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. Define the pairing measure (with respect to
A
) on
{\mathcal{S}}
by the following formula:

(6.17)

\nu^{A}:=\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{2}}\Bigg(\left(dw_{1,A},dw_{2,A}\right)\begin{pmatrix}m_{11}&m_{12}\\
m_{21}&m_{22}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{2}\\
-a_{1}\end{pmatrix}\\
+\begin{vmatrix}m_{11}&m_{12}\\
m_{21}&m_{22}\end{vmatrix}\left(w_{2,A}\,dw_{1,A}-w_{1,A}\,dw_{2,A}\right)\Bigg)\wedge d\zeta_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{2}.

The authors previously introduced this measure in
[
6
]
. (Related objects also appear in Section 7 of
[
4
]
and Section 3.2 of
[
1
]
.) This measure allows for the Leray transform to be written in terms of the dual variables. We restate Proposition 4.30 from
[
6
]
, which gives a universal formula for the Leray transform of any
A
-admissible
{\mathcal{S}}

Proposition 6.18
.

Let
A\in GL(3,{\mathbb{C}})
be as in (
6.3
) and
{\mathcal{S}}
an
A
-admissible, strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex hypersurface in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. Then the Leray integral from (
2.1a
) may be written as

(6.19)

\bm{L}_{{\mathcal{S}}}f(z)=\int_{\zeta\in{\mathcal{S}}}\frac{f(\zeta)}{\big[\Phi_{A}(z,w^{A}(\zeta))\big]^{2}}\nu^{A}(\zeta),

where
\Phi_{A}
is defined in (
6.1
).

In the special case of
{\mathcal{S}}=M_{\gamma}
,

we have

\displaystyle\nu^{A_{\gamma}}=-\frac{i\gamma}{4\pi^{2}}\,dw_{1}^{A_{\gamma}}\wedge d\zeta_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{2}

6.2.2.
The preferred measure and its dual

In Section
2.2.3
, we defined (up to a constant) the preferred measure
\widetilde{\mu}_{{\mathcal{S}}}
. This measure was introduced by the first author in
[
4
]
to define a pair of dual Hardy spaces with desirable projective transformation laws. Employing the Fefferman measure (
2.8
) to construct such spaces still leads to such transformation laws, but the preferred measure
\widetilde{\mu}_{\mathcal{S}}
dovetails with a pairing of these spaces in the spirit of (
6.19
) in a way that
\mu_{\mathcal{S}}^{\sf Fef}
does not.

One Hardy space is defined using
\widetilde{\mu}_{{\mathcal{S}}}
. To define the other Hardy space, introduce the
preferred dual measure
by pulling back the preferred measure on the dual
{\mathcal{S}}^{*,A}
via the map
w^{A}
:

(6.21)

\widetilde{\mu}_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{*,A}:=\left(w^{A}\right)^{*}\left(\widetilde{\mu}_{{\mathcal{S}}^{*,A}}\right).

When the hypersurface and matrix are clear from context, we may simply write
\widetilde{\mu}
and
\widetilde{\mu}^{*}
to denote the preferred measure and its dual.

The preferred measure on
M_{\gamma}
was already recorded in (
2.13
):

\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}=c_{2}\frac{\gamma^{4/3}}{8(\gamma-1)^{1/3}i}\,|\zeta_{1}|^{\frac{\gamma-2}{3}}\,d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}.

We will chose a convenient normalization constant
c_{2}
shortly. But first let us compute the dual measure. From (
6.11
) we obtain

d\overline{w}_{1}^{A_{\gamma}}\wedge dw_{1}^{A_{\gamma}}=(1-\gamma)|\zeta_{1}|^{2\gamma-4}\,d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1},

so from (
6.21
), we see

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{2}\cdot\left(\gamma^{*}\right)^{4/3}|w_{1}^{A_{\gamma}}|^{\frac{\gamma^{*}-2}{3}}}{8(\gamma^{*}-1)^{1/3}i}\,dw_{2}^{A_{\gamma}}\wedge d\overline{w}_{1}^{A_{\gamma}}\wedge dw_{1}^{A_{\gamma}}

(6.22)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{2}\cdot\gamma^{4/3}}{8(\gamma-1)i}|\zeta_{1}|^{\frac{5(\gamma-2)}{3}}\,d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}.

Note the Radon-Nikodym derivative

(6.23)

\frac{{\rm d}\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}}{{\rm d}\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}}=\frac{|\zeta_{1}|^{\frac{4}{3}(\gamma-2)}}{(\gamma-1)^{2/3}}.

Now define the geometric mean
\sqrt{\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}\,\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}}
of measures
\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}
and
\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}
by

{\rm d}\sqrt{\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}\,\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}}:=\sqrt{\frac{{\rm d}\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}}{{\rm d}\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}}}\>{\rm d}\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}.

Therefore

\sqrt{\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}\,\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}}=\dfrac{c_{2}\gamma^{4/3}|\zeta_{1}|^{\gamma-2}}{8(\gamma-1)^{2/3}i}\,d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}

and thus,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{M_{\gamma}}fg\,{\rm d}\nu^{A_{\gamma}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\int_{M_{\gamma}}|f|^{2}\,{\rm d}\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}}\sqrt{\int_{M_{\gamma}}|g|^{2}\,{\rm d}\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}}\frac{{\rm d}\nu^{A_{\gamma}}}{{\rm d}\sqrt{\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}\,\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\gamma(\gamma-1)\right)^{2/3}}{c_{2}\pi^{2}}\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}\left(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}\right)}\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}\left(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}^{*}\right)}.

(Here we identify the positive three-form
\nu^{A_{\gamma}}
with a measure so that
\int fg\,{\rm d}\nu^{A_{\gamma}}
and
\int fg\,\nu^{A_{\gamma}}
have the same meaning.)

In order to obtain a sharp Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

(6.24)

\left|\int_{M_{\gamma}}fg\,\nu^{A_{\gamma}}\right|\leq\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}\left(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}\right)}\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}\left(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}^{*}\right)},

we set

c_{2}=\frac{\left(\gamma(\gamma-1)\right)^{2/3}}{\pi^{2}}

so that

(6.25)

\sqrt{\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}\,\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}}=\dfrac{\gamma^{2}|\zeta_{1}|^{\gamma-2}}{8\pi^{2}i}\,d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}=\nu^{A_{\gamma}}.

For each
f
, there is some non-zero
g
such that equality holds in (
6.24
). (The same is true with the roles of
f
and
g
reversed.) Here, we’ve allowed
c_{2}
to depend not only on the dimension but also on the choice of matrix
A_{\gamma}
.

This choice of
c_{2}
will be fixed throughout the rest of the paper. We therefore settle on the convention that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}(\gamma-1)^{1/3}}{8\pi^{2}i}|\zeta_{1}|^{\frac{\gamma-2}{3}}\,d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}

(6.26)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}(\gamma-1)^{1/3}}{4\pi^{2}}\alpha^{\frac{\gamma+1}{3}}ds\wedge d\alpha\wedge d\theta

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{8\pi^{2}(\gamma-1)^{1/3}i}|\zeta_{1}|^{\frac{5\gamma-10}{3}}\,d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4\pi^{2}(\gamma-1)^{1/3}}\alpha^{\frac{5\gamma-7}{3}}ds\wedge d\alpha\wedge d\theta.

6.3.
Dual Hardy spaces and the dual Leray transform

Formulas (
6.2.1
), (
6.2.2
) and (
6.2.2
) show that each of the distinguished measures from the previous section are contained in some
{\mathscr{F}}_{r}
. In particular,

(6.28)

\nu^{A_{\gamma}}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{\gamma-1},\qquad\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{\frac{\gamma+1}{3}},\qquad\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{\frac{5\gamma-7}{3}}.

Notice that for any
\gamma>1
, the numbers
\gamma-1,\tfrac{\gamma+1}{3},\tfrac{5\gamma-7}{3}\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0}=(-1,2\gamma-1)
. Therefore, Corollary
5.28
implies that the Leray transform is a bounded operator on all three spaces
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\nu^{A_{\gamma}})
,
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}})
and
L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}})
.

In what follows,
\left(w^{A_{\gamma}}\right)^{*}
and
\left(w^{A_{\gamma}}\right)_{*}
refer to the pullback and pushforward of
w^{A_{\gamma}}
, respectively. In the spirit of (
5.49
), define the Hardy space

H^{2}(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}):=\bm{L}_{\gamma}\big(L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}})\big)=\ker({{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}}-I)

and the
dual Hardy space

(6.29)

H^{2}_{\sf dual}\big(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}\big):=\left(w^{A_{\gamma}}\right)^{*}\Big(H^{2}\big(M_{\gamma^{*}},\widetilde{\mu}_{{M_{\gamma^{*}}}}\big)\Big).

Also define the
dual Leray transform

(6.30)

\widetilde{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}:=\left(w^{A_{\gamma}}\right)^{*}\circ{\bm{L}}_{\gamma^{*}}\circ\left(w^{A_{\gamma}}\right)_{*}.

Proposition 6.31
.

The dual Leray transform
\widetilde{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}
is a bounded projection operator from
L^{2}\big(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}\big)
onto
H^{2}_{\sf dual}\big(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}\big)
.

Proof.

We occasionally write
\widetilde{\mu}^{*}
for
\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}
when context is clear. Letting
g\in L^{2}(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}^{*})
and writing the purely imaginary constant in (
6.2.2
) as
\frac{\gamma^{2}}{8\pi^{2}(\gamma-1)^{1/3}i}:=-Ci
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|g\right\|^{2}_{\widetilde{\mu}^{*}}

\displaystyle=-Ci\int_{M_{\gamma}}|g(\zeta)|^{2}|\zeta_{1}|^{\frac{5\gamma-10}{3}}\,d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}

(6.32)

\displaystyle=-Ci\int_{M_{\gamma^{*}}}|g\circ w^{-1}|^{2}|w_{1}|^{\frac{2-\gamma}{3\gamma-3}}\,dw_{2}\wedge d\overline{w}_{1}\wedge dw_{1}<\infty.

Since
-Ci\,|w_{1}|^{\frac{2-\gamma}{3\gamma-3}}\,dw_{2}\wedge d\overline{w}_{1}\wedge dw_{1}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\frac{2\gamma-1}{3\gamma-3}}
, we see
g\circ w^{-1}\in L^{2}\Big(M_{\gamma^{*}},\mu_{\frac{2\gamma-1}{3\gamma-3}}\Big)
and that each function in this space corresponds to a unique such
g
. Now considering that

\tfrac{2\gamma-1}{3\gamma-3}\in(-1,2\gamma^{*}-1)={\mathcal{I}}_{0}

(the interval of
r
values with Leray boundedness on
L^{2}(M_{\gamma^{*}},\mu_{r})
), Theorem
1.4
says that
\bm{L}_{\gamma^{*}}
is bounded on
L^{2}\Big(M_{\gamma^{*}},\mu_{\frac{2\gamma-1}{3\gamma-3}}\Big)
. Using
w^{A_{\gamma}}=w
to pull the computation back to
M_{\gamma}
, (since
\widetilde{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}g=w^{*}\circ\bm{L}_{\gamma^{*}}\circ w_{*}\circ g
) we conclude that
\left\|\widetilde{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}g\right\|_{\widetilde{\mu}^{*}}\lesssim\left\|g\right\|_{\widetilde{\mu}^{*}}
, where the implied constant is independent of the particular choice of
g
. We are also now able to invoke Proposition
5.48
to conclude that the dual Leray transform is a projection.

Since
\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma^{*}}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\frac{2\gamma-1}{3\gamma-3}}
,
H^{2}\big(M_{\gamma^{*}},\widetilde{\mu}_{{M_{\gamma^{*}}}}\big)
is the image of
L^{2}\Big(M_{\gamma^{*}},\mu_{\frac{2\gamma-1}{3\gamma-3}}\Big)
under
\bm{L}_{\gamma^{*}}
. Definition (
6.29
) now lets us conclude

\widetilde{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}\Big(L^{2}\big(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}\big)\Big)=H^{2}_{\sf dual}\big(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}_{M_{\gamma}}^{*,A_{\gamma}}\big).

∎

Remark 6.54

Proof.

Let
g\in\ker\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}
, i.e.,
\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}(g)
is the zero functional on

. It can be easily checked that

. This means that

\displaystyle 0=\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}(g)\left(\bm{L}_{\gamma}\widetilde{\bm{R}}\,\overline{g}\right)=\left\langle\!\left\langle\bm{L}_{\gamma}\widetilde{\bm{R}}\,\overline{g},g\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=\left\langle\!\left\langle\widetilde{\bm{R}}\,\overline{g},\widetilde{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}g\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=\left\langle\!\left\langle\widetilde{\bm{R}}\,\overline{g},g\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=\left\|g\right\|_{\widetilde{\mu}^{*}}^{2}.

Thus
g\equiv 0
and
\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}
is injective.

Let
T\in\left(H^{2}\big(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}\big)\right)^{*}
. There is some
h\in L^{2}\big(M_{\gamma},\widetilde{\mu}^{*}\big)
representing

and satisfying

Now for all

,

\displaystyle Tf

\displaystyle=\left\langle\!\left\langle f,h\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=\left\langle\!\left\langle\bm{L}_{\gamma}f,h\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=\left\langle\!\left\langle f,\widetilde{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}h\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}\left(\widetilde{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}h\right)(f)

In other words,
T=\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}\left(\widetilde{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}h\right)
with
\|\widetilde{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}h\|_{\widetilde{\mu}^{*}}\leq\|\widetilde{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}\|_{\widetilde{\mu}^{*}}\|h\|_{\widetilde{\mu}^{*}}=\|{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}\|_{\widetilde{\mu}}\|T\|
.
So
\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}
is surjective with
\|\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}^{\,-1}\|\leq\|{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}\|_{\widetilde{\mu}}
.

To prove the reverse estimate let

and pick

such that
\left\|h\right\|_{\widetilde{\mu}^{*}}=1
. Then

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}(\widetilde{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}h)(f)\right|=\left|\langle\!\langle f,\widetilde{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}h\rangle\!\rangle\right|=\left|\langle\!\langle\bm{L}_{\gamma}f,h\rangle\!\rangle\right|=\left|\langle\!\langle f,h\rangle\!\rangle\right|\leq\|f\|_{\widetilde{\mu}}

and so
\|\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}(\widetilde{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}h)\|\leq 1
for any such

\epsilon>0
and choose a specific such

\|\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}^{-1}\|\geq\|\widetilde{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}h\|_{\widetilde{\mu}^{*}}\geq\|{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}\|_{\widetilde{\mu}}-\epsilon
.
Since
\epsilon
was arbitrary we have
\|\widehat{\chi}_{\gamma}^{-1}\|\geq\|{\bm{L}}_{\gamma}\|_{\widetilde{\mu}}
, concluding the proof.
∎

Appendix A

We verify here that for certain values of

to holomorphic functions on the domain
\Omega_{\gamma}=\left\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\colon\text{Im}(z_{2})>\left|z_{1}\right|^{\gamma}\right\}.

The main result is the following:

Theorem A.1
.

Let
\bm{L}
denote the Leray transform.
\bm{L}

Notice that

\bm{L}
is bounded from

5.3

for each

A.1
confirms that these Hardy spaces can be viewed as consisting entirely of boundary values of holomorphic functions.

A.1.
Symmetries of the Leray kernel

Partition
\Omega_{\gamma}
into translated copies of
M_{\gamma}
by defining

(A.2)

M_{\gamma}^{\epsilon}=\left\{(z_{1},z_{2}):\text{Im}(z_{2})=|z_{1}|^{\gamma}+\epsilon\right\}.

It is clear that

Let

. Equations (
2.3a
), (
2.3b
) and (
2.3c
) provide the formula for the Leray transform:

(A.3)

where the kernel
\ell_{\rho}=\ell
and measure

\displaystyle\ell(z,\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\left(\gamma\overline{\zeta}_{1}|\zeta_{1}|^{\gamma-2}(\zeta_{1}-z_{1})+i(\zeta_{2}-z_{2})\right)^{2}},\qquad\sigma(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2i}|\zeta_{1}|^{\gamma-2}\,d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}.

The measure
\sigma
is special instance of

, given in rectangular coordinates for

by

\displaystyle\mu_{r}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2i}|\zeta_{1}|^{r-1}\,d\zeta_{2}\wedge d\overline{\zeta}_{1}\wedge d\zeta_{1}.

Recall the three types of automorphisms of

(A.4b)

\displaystyle\ell(r_{\theta}(z),r_{\theta}(\zeta))

(A.17)

\displaystyle=32\pi\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+1)^{2}\gamma^{2k}\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha_{\zeta}^{2k(\gamma-1)+r}\left\{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{ds_{\zeta}}{|(\gamma-1)\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma}+q+is_{\zeta}|^{2k+4}}\right\}\,d\alpha_{\zeta}.

We now import the first of two integral formulas which are established in Section
A.4
. Proposition
A.35
shows that for any
C>0
,

(A.18)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{|C+is|^{2k+4}}=\frac{\pi}{4^{k+1}C^{2k+3}}\frac{\Gamma(2k+3)}{\Gamma(k+2)^{2}}.

Substituting this into (
A.17
) with
C=(\gamma-1)\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma}+q
shows

(A.19)

\Theta_{r}(1,iq)=8\pi^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(2k+3)}{\Gamma(k+1)^{2}}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)^{2k}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha_{\zeta}^{2k(\gamma-1)+r}}{((\gamma-1)\alpha_{\zeta}^{\gamma}+q)^{2k+3}}d\alpha_{\zeta}.

For each fixed
k
, two conditions on
r
are required for the integral in (
A.19
) to converge:

(1)

For
\alpha_{\zeta}
near
0
, we need
2k(\gamma-1)+r>-1
.

(2)

As
\alpha_{\zeta}
tends to
\infty
, we need
\gamma(2k+3)-2k(\gamma-1)-r>1
.

These conditions suggest the definition of the following interval of
r
values

(A.20)

{\mathcal{J}}^{\prime}_{k}:=(-2k(\gamma-1)-1,3\gamma-1+2k).

Note that
{\mathcal{J}}^{\prime}_{0}\subset{\mathcal{J}}^{\prime}_{k}
for all
k
, so the convergence of the
k=0
integral implies the convergence of all other integrals in the sum. But if the
k=0
integral diverges, i.e.,
r\notin{\mathcal{J}}^{\prime}_{0}
, then
\Theta_{r}(1,iq)=\infty
. Now make use of the second integral formula established in Section
A.4
. Proposition
A.37
shows that, for
q>0
,
\gamma>1
,
r\in{\mathcal{J}}^{\prime}_{k}
and
k
a non-negative integer,

(A.21)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{2k(\gamma-1)+r}}{((\gamma-1)\alpha^{\gamma}+q)^{2k+3}}d\alpha=\frac{\Gamma\big(3+\frac{2k-1-r}{\gamma}\big)\Gamma\big(2k+\frac{1+r-2k}{\gamma}\big)}{\gamma(\gamma-1)^{\frac{1+r}{\gamma}}q^{3-\frac{1+r}{\gamma}}\Gamma\big(2k+3\big)}\left(q(\gamma-1)^{\gamma-1}\right)^{-\frac{2k}{\gamma}}.

Plugging this into (
A.19
) yields

(A.22)

\Theta_{r}(1,iq)=\frac{8\pi^{2}}{\gamma(\gamma-1)^{\frac{1+r}{\gamma}}}q^{\frac{1+r}{\gamma}-3}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\tfrac{\Gamma\left(3+\frac{2k-1-r}{\gamma}\right)\Gamma\left(2k+\frac{1+r-2k}{\gamma}\right)}{\Gamma\big(k+1\big)^{2}}\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4(\gamma-1)^{2-\frac{2}{\gamma}}q^{\frac{2}{\gamma}}}\right)^{k}.

We study the convergence of this sum by considering (for
r\in{\mathcal{J}}^{\prime}_{k}
) the related series

(A.23)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}x^{k},\qquad\textrm{where}\qquad a_{k}=\frac{\Gamma\big(3+\frac{2k-1-r}{\gamma}\big)\Gamma\big(2k+\frac{1+r-2k}{\gamma}\big)}{\Gamma\big(k+1\big)^{2}}.

Sterling’s formula says

\displaystyle\Gamma({k+1})^{2}

\displaystyle\sim 2\pi e^{-2k}k^{2k+1},

\displaystyle\Gamma\big(3+\tfrac{2k-1-r}{\gamma}\big)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{2\pi}\cdot e^{\frac{1+r-2k}{\gamma}-2}\left(2+\tfrac{2k-1-r}{\gamma}\right)^{\frac{5}{2}+\frac{2k-1-r}{\gamma}},

\displaystyle\Gamma\big(2k+\tfrac{1+r-2k}{\gamma}\big)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{2\pi}\cdot e^{1-2k-\frac{1+r-2k}{\gamma}}\left(2k-1+\tfrac{1+r-2k}{\gamma}\right)^{2k-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1+r-2k}{\gamma}}.

Combining these shows that

\displaystyle a_{k}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{ek^{2k+1}}\cdot\left(2+\tfrac{2k-1-r}{\gamma}\right)^{\frac{5}{2}+\frac{2k-1-r}{\gamma}}\left(2k-1+\tfrac{1+r-2k}{\gamma}\right)^{2k-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1+r-2k}{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{k}{e}\cdot\left(\tfrac{2}{\gamma}+\tfrac{2\gamma-1-r}{\gamma k}\right)^{\frac{5}{2}+\frac{2k-1-r}{\gamma}}\left(2-\tfrac{2}{\gamma}+\tfrac{1+r-\gamma}{\gamma k}\right)^{2k-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1+r-2k}{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\sim\tfrac{k}{e}\cdot\left(\tfrac{2}{\gamma}\right)^{\frac{5}{2}-\frac{1+r}{\gamma}}\left(\tfrac{2}{\gamma}+\tfrac{2\gamma-1-r}{\gamma k}\right)^{\frac{2k}{\gamma}}\left(2-\tfrac{2}{\gamma}\right)^{\frac{1+r}{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}\left(2-\tfrac{2}{\gamma}+\tfrac{1+r-\gamma}{\gamma k}\right)^{2k-\frac{2k}{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{k}{e}\cdot\left(\tfrac{2}{\gamma}\right)^{2}(\gamma-1)^{\frac{1+r}{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\tfrac{2}{\gamma}\right)^{\frac{2k}{\gamma}}\left(1+\left(\tfrac{2\gamma-1-r}{\gamma}\right)\tfrac{\gamma}{2k}\right)^{\frac{2k}{\gamma}}

(A.24)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\left(2-\tfrac{2}{\gamma}\right)^{2k-\frac{2k}{\gamma}}\left(1+\left(\tfrac{1+r-\gamma}{\gamma}\right)\left(2k-\tfrac{2k}{\gamma}\right)^{-1}\right)^{2k-\frac{2k}{\gamma}}.

Since
\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(1+\tfrac{c}{t}\right)^{t}=e^{c}
, we see from (
A.24
) that

\displaystyle a_{k}

\displaystyle\sim\tfrac{k}{e}\cdot\left(\tfrac{2}{\gamma}\right)^{2}(\gamma-1)^{\frac{1+r}{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\tfrac{2}{\gamma}\right)^{\frac{2k}{\gamma}}e^{\frac{2\gamma-1-r}{\gamma}}\left(2-\tfrac{2}{\gamma}\right)^{2k-\frac{2k}{\gamma}}e^{\frac{1+r-\gamma}{\gamma}}

(A.25)

\displaystyle=4\gamma^{-2}(\gamma-1)^{\frac{1+r}{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}k\left(4\gamma^{-2}(\gamma-1)^{2-\frac{2}{\gamma}}\right)^{k}.

It is easy to see that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\sqrt[k]{a_{k}}=4\gamma^{-2}(\gamma-1)^{2-\frac{2}{\gamma}}
, so the root test guarantees the convergence of
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}x^{k}
on the interval

(A.26)

0\leq x<\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4(\gamma-1)^{2-\frac{2}{\gamma}}}.

Also from (
A.25
), it is immediate that
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}x^{k}
diverges at the right endpoint of (
A.26
), since the sum
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}k
diverges.

These observations about
\sum a_{k}x^{k}
yield immediate analogues for
\Theta_{r}(1,iq)
. We now see that, for any
r\in{\mathcal{J}}^{\prime}_{0}
, formula (
A.22
) converges for
q>1
. Indeed,

\Theta_{r}(1,iq)=q^{\frac{1+r}{\gamma}-3}F_{r}(q),

where

(A.27)

F_{r}(q)=\frac{8\pi^{2}}{\gamma(\gamma-1)^{\frac{1+r}{\gamma}}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\big(3+\frac{2k-1-r}{\gamma}\big)\Gamma\big(2k+\frac{1+r-2k}{\gamma}\big)}{\Gamma\big(k+1\big)^{2}}\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4(\gamma-1)^{2-\frac{2}{\gamma}}q^{\frac{2}{\gamma}}}\right)^{k}.

It is now clear that
F_{r}
is positive, strictly decreasing and real-analytic on
(1,\infty)
. Furthermore,
\lim_{q\to 1^{+}}F_{r}(q)=\infty
. It is also clear that

(A.28)

\lim_{q\to\infty}F_{r}(q)=\frac{8\pi^{2}\Gamma\big(3-\frac{1+r}{\gamma}\big)\Gamma\big(\frac{1+r}{\gamma}\big)}{\gamma(\gamma-1)^{\frac{1+r}{\gamma}}}.

This concludes the proof of Proposition
A.13
.
∎

A.1

Let
\mathscr{B}
denote the set of all subsets

satisfying the following two properties:

\alpha_{K}:=\sup\{\alpha:(\alpha,\theta,s,\epsilon)\in K\}<\infty
.

(2)

\epsilon_{K}:=\inf\{\epsilon:(\alpha,\theta,s,\epsilon)\in K\}>0
.

Given a fixed
K\in{\mathscr{B}}
, observe that the infimum of
q(\alpha,\epsilon)
when restricted to

. Indeed, if we define

(A.32)

Fix some

. This determines a unique

value with

A.30

\Theta_{r^{\prime}}(z)=(\alpha^{\gamma}+\epsilon)^{\frac{r^{\prime}+1}{\gamma}-3}F_{r^{\prime}}(q(\alpha,\epsilon))\leq\epsilon_{K}^{\frac{r^{\prime}+1}{\gamma}-3}F_{r^{\prime}}(q_{K}).

A.13

If

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{16\pi^{2}}\sup_{z\in K}\left(\int_{M_{\gamma}}|\ell(z,\zeta)|^{2}\mu_{r^{\prime}}(\zeta)\right)\left(\int_{M_{\gamma}}|f_{j}(\zeta)-f(\zeta)|^{2}\mu_{r}(\zeta)\right)

A.4.
Two residue integral computations

Proposition A.35
.

For
C>0
and
k
a non-negative integer,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{|C+is|^{2k+4}}=\frac{\pi}{4^{k+1}C^{2k+3}}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(2k+3)}{\Gamma(k+2)^{2}}.

Proof.

Write

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{|C+is|^{2k+4}}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{(s^{2}+C^{2})^{k+2}}

Let
\mathcal{P}_{R}
denote a counter-clockwise oriented, closed half-circle contour with radius
R
lying in the upper-half plane with base lying on the interval
[-R,R]
. If we set
g(z)={(z^{2}+C^{2})^{-k-2}}
, the residue theorem says (provided
R>C
),

(A.36)

2\pi i\,\textrm{Res}(g,iC)=\int_{\mathcal{P}_{R}}g(z)\,dz.

We calculate

\displaystyle\textrm{Res}(g,iC)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(k+2)}\frac{d^{k+1}}{dz^{k+1}}\left[\frac{1}{(z+iC)^{k+2}}\right]\Bigg|_{z=iC}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i2^{2k+3}C^{2k+3}}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(2k+3)}{\Gamma(k+2)^{2}}.

Now send
R\to\infty
and note that the circular portion of the contour integral in (
A.36
) tends to
0
. This gives the result.
∎

Proposition A.37
.

Let
q>0
,
\gamma>1
,
r\in{\mathcal{J}}^{\prime}_{k}=(-2k(\gamma-1)-1,3\gamma-1+2k)
and
k
a non-negative integer. Then

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{2k(\gamma-1)+r}}{((\gamma-1)\alpha^{\gamma}+q)^{2k+3}}d\alpha=\frac{\Gamma\left(3+\frac{2k-1-r}{\gamma}\right)\Gamma\left(2k+\frac{1+r-2k}{\gamma}\right)}{\gamma(\gamma-1)^{\frac{1+r}{\gamma}}q^{3-\frac{1+r}{\gamma}}\Gamma\big(2k+3\big)}\left(q(\gamma-1)^{\gamma-1}\right)^{-\frac{2k}{\gamma}}.

Proof.

Making the change of variable
x=\alpha^{\gamma}
, see that

(A.38)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{2k(\gamma-1)+r}}{((\gamma-1)\alpha^{\gamma}+q)^{2k+3}}\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma(\gamma-1)^{2k+3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2k-1+\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}}}{(x+E)^{2k+3}}\,dx,

with
E=\frac{q}{\gamma-1}
. Now define the function

(A.39)

h(z)=\frac{z^{2k-1+\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}}}{(z+E)^{2k+3}},

which is multi-valued whenever the numerator exponent is a non-integer. Restrict attention to a single-valued meromorphic branch of
h
defined on
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash[0,\infty)
. Note that
h
has a pole of order
2k+3
at
z=-E
.

For
0<\delta<R
, define the closed, positively oriented contour
\mathcal{P}_{\delta,R}
by traversing the following paths in sequence:

I
.

A line segment on the positive
x
-axis moving
right
from
\delta
to
R
.

II
.

A circle of radius
R
centered at the origin, starting on the positive
x
-axis and traveling
counterclockwise
.

III
.

A line segment on the positive
x
-axis moving
left
from
R
to
\delta
.

IV
.

A circle of radius
\delta
centered at the origin, starting on the positive
x
-axis and traveling
clockwise
.

As long as
\delta<E<R
, the residue theorem says

\displaystyle 2\pi i\,\textrm{Res}(h,-E)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\delta,R}}h(z)\,dz

(A.40)

\displaystyle=\int_{I}h(z)\,dz+\int_{II}h(z)\,dz+\int_{III}h(z)\,dz+\int_{IV}h(z)\,dz.

Keeping in mind the multi-valued nature of
h
, combine the
I
and
III
integrals:

\displaystyle\int_{I}h(z)\,dz+\int_{III}h(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{R}h(x)\,dx+\int^{\delta}_{R}h(xe^{2\pi i})\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{R}\frac{x^{2k-1+\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}}}{(x+E)^{2k+3}}\,dx+\int^{\delta}_{R}\frac{x^{2k-1+\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}}e^{2\pi i(2k-1+\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma})}}{(xe^{2\pi i}+E)^{2k+3}}\,dx

(A.41)

\displaystyle=\left(1-e^{\frac{2\pi i(r+1-2k)}{\gamma}}\right)\int_{\delta}^{R}\frac{x^{2k-1+\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}}}{(x+E)^{2k+3}}\,dx.

Standard estimates show that since
r\in{\mathcal{J}}^{\prime}_{k}
,

(A.42)

\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{II}h(z)\,dz=0=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{IV}h(z)\,dz.

Combining (
A.42
) with (
A.40
) and (
A.41
) shows

(A.43)

2\pi i\,\textrm{Res}(h,-E)=\left(1-e^{\frac{2\pi i(r+1-2k)}{\gamma}}\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2k-1+\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}}}{(x+E)^{2k+3}}\,dx.

Now calculate this residue.

\displaystyle\textrm{Res}(h,-E)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2k+3)}\frac{d^{2k+2}}{dz^{2k+2}}\left(z^{2k-1+\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}}\right)\Bigg|_{z=-E}

(A.44)

\displaystyle=\frac{-\Gamma\big({2k+\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}\big)}}{\Gamma\big(2k+3\big)\Gamma\big(\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}-2\big)}E^{\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}-3}e^{\frac{\pi i(r+1-2k)}{\gamma}}.

Combining (
A.43
) and (
A.44
) shows that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2k(1-\frac{1}{\gamma})}}{(x+E)^{2k+3}}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{-2\pi i}{1-e^{\frac{2\pi i(r+1-2k)}{\gamma}}}\cdot\frac{\Gamma\big({2k+\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}\big)}}{\Gamma\big(2k+3\big)\Gamma\big(\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}-2\big)}E^{\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}-3}e^{\frac{\pi i(r+1-2k)}{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\pi\csc\left(\pi\big(\tfrac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}\big)\right)\frac{\Gamma\big({2k+\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}\big)}}{\Gamma\big(2k+3\big)\Gamma\big(\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}-2\big)}E^{\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}-3}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}\big)\Gamma\big(1-\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}\big)\Gamma\big(2k+\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}\big)}{\Gamma\big(2k+3\big)\Gamma\big(\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}-2\big)}E^{\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}-3}

\displaystyle=\big(\tfrac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}-1\big)\big(\tfrac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}-2\big)\frac{\Gamma\big(1-\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}\big)\Gamma\big(2k+\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}\big)}{\Gamma\big(2k+3\big)}E^{\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}-3}

(A.45)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\big(3+\frac{2k-r-1}{\gamma}\big)\Gamma\big(2k+\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}\big)}{\Gamma\big(2k+3\big)}E^{\frac{r+1-2k}{\gamma}-3}.

Combining (
A.38
) and (
A.45
) gives the result.
∎
