A degree-biased cutting process for random recursive trees
Laura Eslava, Sergio I. López, Marco L. Ortiz

Abstract
We investigate a degree-biased cutting process on random recursive trees, where each vertex is deleted with probability proportional to its degree. We establish the splitting property and derive the explicit distribution of the number of vertices deleted in each cut. This leads to a recursive formula for K n K_{n} , the number of cuts needed to erase a random recursive tree with n n vertices. Furthermore, we show that K n K_{n} is stochastically dominated by J n J_{n} , the number of jumps made by a related walk with a barrier. We prove that J n J_{n} converges in distribution to a random variable with a spectrally negative stable distribution. Finally, we examine connections between this cutting procedure and a coalescing process on the set of n n elements. Keywords: Random trees; cutting down process; random cuts; degree sequence. AMScode: 05C80, 60F05.

Introduction

An increasing tree on
[n]
is a rooted labeled tree, where labels along a path away from the root are in increasing order. We write
I_{n}
for the set of increasing trees of size
n
; namely, with
n
vertices. A random recursive tree on
[n]
, denoted
T_{n}
, is a tree chosen uniformly at random from
I_{n}
. A classical construction of
T_{n}
proceeds as follows: starting with vertex
1
as the root, for each
2\leq k\leq n
a vertex is chosen uniformly at random from
\{1,...,k-1\}
, and vertex
k
is attached to the chosen vertex.

The concept of cutting down random networks was introduced in
[
17
]
for random rooted labeled trees and soon afterward studied for the case of random recursive trees
[
18
]
. In this original procedure, the goal is to isolate the root by sequentially removing a randomly chosen edge and discarding the subtree that does not contain the root. The procedure ends when only the root remains, at which point we say that the
root has been isolated
or that the
tree has been erased
.

Note that a vertex-removal process is asymptotically equivalent to the edge-removal process since removing a uniformly chosen edge is equivalent to removing a vertex uniformly chosen from the current vertex set, excluding the root. On the other hand, a central property of cutting processes, known as the
splitting property
is that, conditionally on its size, the remaining tree after each cut is itself distributed as the original random tree. The probabilistic techniques used to analyze the limiting distribution of
X_{n}
, the number of random cuts required to erase a random recursive tree with
n
vertices are based on the splitting property and are extended, for example, to study the number of collisions in a beta-coalescent until coagulation
[
14
,
15
]
.

More specifically, Meir and Moon
[
18
]
exploit the splitting property to obtain the asymptotic limit of the expected value of
X_{n}
and Panholzer established the law of large numbers for
X_{n}
[
19
]
. While using different methods (singularity analysis of the generating functions and a probabilistic argument, reviewed in Section
2
), both Drmota et al, and Iksanov and Möhle deduce that the fluctuations of
X_{n}
, after an appropriate normalization and centering, converge in distribution to a random variable with a spectrally negative stable distribution
[
8
,
14
]
; see (
5
) and (
6
) in Section
2.1
for a detailed exposition of these results.

The splitting property determines which of the two models is deemed more suitable for analysis; for example, Galton-Watson trees are studied through vertex-removal processes due to the independent reproduction of their vertices; see for example
[
2
,
16
]
.
Baur
[
3
]
provides a comprehensive survey of cutting models and related processes for random recursive trees up to 2014. In 2019, Cai et al. introduced a cutting procedure aimed at network resilience. In their
k
-cut model, edges are randomly selected and removed only after receiving
k
cuts
[
5
,
6
,
21
]
.

The main objective of this paper is to introduce cutting procedures for random recursive trees that speed up the deletion of the tree, meaning that fewer cuts are needed to isolate the root. Eslava et. al.
[
10
]
introduced a targeted cutting process for random recursive trees.
Conditionally given the degree sequence of
T_{n}
, this is a deterministic procedure that significantly reduces the number of cuts required to isolate the root. The process begins by listing the vertices in decreasing order of their degrees and then removing vertices in this order. In the context of Galton-Watson trees, Dieuleveut studied a degree-biased vertex-removal process
[
7
]
. In this process, a vertex is selected with probability proportional to its degree, then all its descendants (including their subtrees) are removed while the selected vertex remains.

In this paper, we introduce the
degree-biased cutting procedure
, an iterative process of
degree-biased vertex-removals
where vertices are removed, with probability proportional to their degree,
until the root is isolated or the tree is instantly erased. In either case, we say that the
tree has been erased
(see Definitions
1.1
and
1.4
). We are concerned with the limiting distribution of
K_{n}
, the number of cuts required to erase a random recursive tree with
n
vertices.

This procedure resembles the model studied by Dieuleveut; however, the key difference is that in our model, the selected vertex is removed rather than its descendants so that the removal scheme preserves the splitting property (see Proposition
1.2
).
Although the splitting property is maintained in the degree-biased cutting process, applying the methods from
[
14
,
15
]
to degree-biased cutting processes presents challenges, see Remark
3.3
. In our case, it is only possible to approximate the number of vertices removed using a jump distribution
\zeta
, leading to an stochastic upper bound for
K_{n}
whose asymptotic growth is explicitly known. Our main result, Theorem
1.5
, together with Theorem
1.6
show that
K_{n}
grows faster than
X_{n}
by at least by a factor of
\ln n
.

An alternative approach to edge removal is the continuous-time process where the edges are deleted in the order determined by independent exponential clocks. This perspective draws a connection to coalescent processes, first observed in
[
13
]
and explored further for the degree-biased vertex-removal in Section
4
.

1.1
Properties of the degree-biased cutting process

In what follows, it is relevant to make a difference between random recursive trees
T_{n}
and their deterministic feasible values; thus, deterministic trees will be denoted with lowercase letters; e.g.,
t\in I_{n}
.
We consider edges in increasing trees as being directed towards the root. In particular, for an increasing tree
t
and vertex
v\in t
, we define the
degree
of
v
, denoted
\deg_{t}(v)
, as the number of edges directed towards
v
.
If
v_{1}v_{2}
is an edge of an increasing tree, we will implicitly assume that
v_{1}<v_{2}
. Whenever
v_{1}>1
, there is a unique outgoing edge from
v_{1}
to a vertex (its parent) which we denote
v_{0}
.

Let
t^{\prime}
be a tree with vertex set
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\}
, where
v_{1}<\dots<v_{m}
. We denote by
\Phi_{m}
the function that relabels each
v_{i}\in t^{\prime}
with label
i
. In particular, if
t\in I_{n}
and
t^{\prime}\subset t
then
\Phi_{m}(t^{\prime})\in I_{m}
. In what follows, we abuse notation and write
\Phi(\cdot)=\Phi_{m}(\cdot)
whenever the size of the tree is clear from the context. Finally, for a vertex
v\in t
, let
t^{(v)}
denote the subtree rooted at
v
; i.e.
t^{(v)}\subset t
contains
v
and all vertices
w
such that there is a directed path from
w
to
v
.
The following definition of degree-biased cutting has the property that, at each step, the probability of deleting vertex
v
is proportional to its degree.

Definition 1.1

(Degree-biased cut)
.

Let
t
be an increasing tree. Choose an edge
v_{1}v_{2}
uniformly at random and
delete
vertex
v_{1}
along with
t^{(v_{1})}
. More precisely, if
v_{1}>1
, then remove the edge
v_{0}v_{1}
and keep only the subtree containing the root of
t
; that is, keep
t^{root}:=t\setminus(t^{(v_{1})}\cup v_{0}v_{1})
. Otherwise,
v_{1}
is the root of
t
, we let
t^{root}:=\emptyset
and say that the cut
erases the tree instantly
.

A key property for our results is that the splitting property holds for the degree-biased cutting of random recursive trees.

Proposition 1.2

(Splitting property)
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
T_{n}
be a random recursive tree. Let
T_{n}^{root}
be the tree obtained after a degree-biased cut. For
\ell\in[n-2]
, conditionally given that
|T_{n}^{root}|=\ell
,
\Phi(T_{n}^{root})
has the distribution of a random recursive tree of size
\ell
.

Note that the cutting process removes at least both ends of the selected edge. Consequently, the size of each cut satisfies
2\leq|T_{n}^{(v_{1})}|\leq n
. Moreover, we obtain its distribution by exploiting the fact that
{\mathbf{E}}\left[\deg_{T_{n}}(1)\right]=H_{n-1}
, where
H_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}i^{-1}
is the
n
-th
harmonic number
.

Proposition 1.3

(Size of a cut)
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
T_{n}
be a random recursive tree. Let
T_{n}^{(v_{1})}
be the tree defined by a degree-biased cut of
T_{n}
; where
v_{1}v_{2}
is a uniformly chosen edge of
T_{n}
. The distribution of
|T_{n}^{(v_{1})}|
is given by

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}\left(|T_{n}^{(v_{1})}|=k\right)=\frac{nH_{k-1}}{(n-1)(k+1)k}{\mathbf{1}}_{[2\leq k<n]}+\frac{H_{n-1}}{n-1}{\mathbf{1}}_{[k=n]}.

(1)

We note in passing that the probability that the degree-biased cutting procedure ends in one step has a relatively large mass coming from an
instant deletion
of the tree; see Remark
3.3
.

Definition 1.4

(Degree-biased process)
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
T_{n}
be a random recursive tree. Sequentially perform degree-biased cuts until the resulting tree (or empty set) cannot be cut anymore; at which point we say that
T_{n}
was
erased
. More precisely, let
(G_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a sequence of trees such that
G_{0}=T_{n}
and, for
t\geq 1
, if
|G_{t-1}|\geq 2
, then
G_{t}=G_{t-1}^{root}
; otherwise,
G_{t}=G_{t-1}
. The number of cuts needed to erase
T_{n}
is defined by

\displaystyle K_{n}=\min\{t\geq 1:|G_{t}|\in\{0,1\}\};

the halting cases correspond to either instantly erasing the tree or isolating the root.

1.2
Main results

Let
\{\xi_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
be a sequence of independent copies of a random variable
\xi

taking positive integer values and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The
random walk with a barrier
at
n
,
\{R^{(n)}_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
is defined recursively as
R^{(n)}_{0}=0
and, for
i\geq 1
,

R^{(n)}_{i}:=R^{(n)}_{i-1}+\xi_{i}\mathbf{1}_{\{R_{i-1}^{(n)}+\xi_{i}<n\}}.

Throughout this section, consider
\xi=\zeta
with mass probability, for
k\geq 2
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta=k\right)=\frac{H_{k-1}}{k(k+1)}.

(2)

Let
J_{n}
denote the number of jumps of the process
\{R^{(n+1)}_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
when
\xi=\zeta
; that is,
J_{n}:=\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathbf{1}_{\{R^{(n+1)}_{i-1}+\xi_{i}\leq n\}}
.
Proposition
1.2
allows us to obtain a recursive formula for
K_{n}
and to compare
K_{n}
with
J_{n}
, akin to
[
14
, Lemma 1]
.
Our main result states that
J_{n}
may be interpreted as a stochastic overcount of
K_{n}
.

Theorem 1.5
.

J_{n}
stochastically dominates
K_{n}
.

Unfortunately, Proposition
1.3
implies that the degree-biased cutting places some extra mass of instant deletion at each step of the process, preventing
J_{n}
from being a good approximation of
K_{n}
; see Remark
3.3
. Nevertheless,
the next result gives us insight into the deletion time
K_{n}
.
Using results in
[
15
]
, which are summarized in Section
2
, we obtain the asymptotic behavior of both the mean and the limiting distribution of
J_{n}
under an appropriate normalization.

Theorem 1.6
.

As
n
goes to infinity,
{\mathbf{E}}\left[J_{n}\right]\sim 2n(\ln n)^{-2}
and, in probability,

\frac{J_{n}}{{\mathbf{E}}\left[J_{n}\right]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}1,

and

\frac{J_{n}}{n}(\ln{n})^{3}-2\ln{n}

converges in distribution to a random variable
4X
, where
X
is a random variable with characteristic function

{\mathbf{E}}\left[e^{itX}\right]=\exp\left(it\ln|t|-\frac{\pi}{2}|t|\right).

In conclusion, the degree-biased cutting process results in quicker deletion times compared to the random cutting approach, at least by a factor of
\ln n
in the first order of
X_{n}
; compare Theorem
1.6
with (
5
) and (
6
).
In contrast, the degree-biased procedure is significantly slower when compared to deleting vertices deterministically based on their ordered degrees, as in the targeted vertex-cutting process, where the number of required cuts has asymptotic growth at most
n^{1-\ln 2}

[
10
]
.

Further research into this topic includes obtaining tighter bounds for the deletion time of both the degree-biased cutting process and the targeted vertex-cutting. In addition, it would be interesting to develop a better understanding of the asymptotic properties of the size of the subtrees removed at each step of the degree-biased cutting procedure, as this is key to further understanding the properties of the related coalescent process.

The outline for the paper is the following.
In Section

we review the results for random walks with a barrier at
n

[

]
and place the known properties of

into such context.

In Section

we
establish Propositions
1.2
and

; namely, the splitting property and the distribution of the mass lost at each degree-biased cut of the process. To do so, we compute the joint probability of

and

Finally, in Section

2
Jumps for random walks with a barrier

Recall the process
\{R^{(n)}_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
, defined in Section
1.2
, it follows from the definition that the process
\{R^{(n)}_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
has non-decreasing paths and satisfies
R_{i}^{(n)}<n
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Let
M_{n}=|\{i\in\mathbb{N}|R^{(n)}_{i-1}\neq R^{(n)}_{i}\}|=\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathbf{1}_{\{R^{(n)}_{i-1}+\xi_{i}<n\}}
denote the number of jumps of the process
\{R^{(n)}_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
. Note that
M_{n}\leq n
since
\xi\geq 1
with probability one.

The asymptotic behavior of
M_{n}
has been studied when
\xi
is distributed as in (
4
) in
[
14
]
and for broader classes of
\xi
in
[
15
]
; in particular, for the case of an infinite-mean
\xi
. In the latter, the results are stated under the assumption of
\xi
having a probability atom at
1
; however, a straightforward adaptation allows us to present their results without such restriction.

Proposition 2.1

(Theorem 1.1 in
[
15
]
)
.

If
\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbf{P}}\left(\xi\geq j\right)\sim\ell(n)
for some function
\ell
slowly varying function at infinity then, as
n\to\infty
,
{\mathbf{E}}\left[M_{n}\right]\sim n/\ell(n)
and, in probability,

\displaystyle\frac{M_{n}}{{\mathbf{E}}\left[M_{n}\right]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}1.

Proposition 2.2

(Theorem 1.4 in
[
15
]
)
.

If
{\mathbf{P}}\left(\xi\geq n\right)\sim\frac{\ell(n)}{n}
for some function
\ell(n)
slowly varying at infinity and
{\mathbf{E}}\left[\xi\right]=\infty
, the normalization

\frac{M_{n}-b(n)}{a(n)}

converges in distribution to a random variable
X
with characteristic function

{\mathbf{E}}\left[e^{itX}\right]=\exp\left(it\ln|t|-\frac{\pi}{2}|t|\right)

whenever the following conditions hold for positive functions
a(x),b(x)
and
c(x)
:

i)

\lim_{x\to\infty}x{\mathbf{P}}\left(\xi\geq c(x)\right)=1
,

ii)

b(\phi(x))\sim\phi(b(x))\sim x
where

\phi(x):=x\int_{0}^{c(x)}{\mathbf{P}}\left(\xi>y\right)dy;

iii)

a(x)\sim x^{-1}b(x)c(b(x))
.

The proofs of Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
rely on a coupling of the process
R^{(n)}_{i}
with the classic random walk
S_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\xi_{j}
. Observe that the jumps of
S_{i}
correspond precisely to the independent variables
\{\xi_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
; while the jumps of

, being conditioned to not exceed the barrier at

, lose both the independence and identical distribution property.

The limiting distribution of
N_{n}:=\inf\{i\in\mathbb{N}:S_{i}\geq n\}
is well understood and it depends on the tails of
\xi
; moreover,
N_{n}
may be interpreted as the number of jumps of
S_{i}
before exceeding the barrier at
n
. The key idea in
[
14
,
15
]
is to prove that the difference
M_{n}-N_{n}
, under a natural coupling of
(R^{(n)}_{i},S_{i})
, is negligible with respect to the first order of
N_{n}
.

Other related works on the domain of attraction of
\alpha
-stable distributions are the following. Erickson studies the asymptotic behavior of the spent and residual times associated with a renewal process
[
9
]
. Geluk and Haan derive the theory of stable probability distributions and their domains of attraction via Fourier transforms
[
12
]
.
Berger surveys the case of random walks in the Cauchy domain of attraction
[
4
]

2.1
Uniform random cuts

Consider
T_{n}
a random recursive tree on
n
vertices and perform a random cut; then the number of vertices removed is given by
\xi^{(n)}
, with

{\mathbf{P}}\left(\xi^{(n)}=k\right)=\frac{n}{(n-1)k(k+1)},

(3)

for
k\in[n-1]
; see e.g.
[
18
]
. On the other hand, let
\xi
be defined, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, by

{\mathbf{P}}\left(\xi=k\right)=(k(k+1))^{-1}.

(4)

Then
{\mathbf{P}}\left(\xi\geq n\right)=n^{-1}
and the distribution of
\xi
conditionally given
\xi<n
, coincides with the distribution of
\xi^{(n)}
. It follows that
M_{n}
corresponds precisely to
X_{n}
, the number of uniform random cuts required to isolate the root of
T_{n}
.

The known properties of
X_{n}
may be recovered using Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
. Namely, as
n
goes to infinity,
{\mathbf{E}}\left[X_{n}\right]\sim n(\ln n)^{-1}
, and in probability,

\frac{X_{n}}{{\mathbf{E}}\left[X_{n}\right]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}1;

(5)

moreover,

\displaystyle\frac{(\ln n)^{2}}{n}X_{n}-\ln n-\ln(\ln n)

(6)

converges in distribution to a random variable with a spectrally negative stable distribution.

3
Analysis of the degree-biased cutting process

Consider a random recursive tree
T_{n}
,
n\geq 2
. In what follows, we let
v_{1}v_{2}
be a uniformly chosen edge in
T_{n}
so that, after performing a degree-biased cut to
T_{n}
, we obtain trees
T_{n}^{(v_{1})}
and
T_{n}^{root}
. The following proposition computes the joint distribution of their relabeled versions.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
T_{n}
be a random recursive tree of size
n
and
T_{n}^{root}
,
T_{n}^{(v_{1})}
be the resulting subtrees of a degree-biased cut. For
2\leq k<n
,
t^{\prime}\in I_{k}
and
t^{\prime\prime}\in I_{n-k}
we have that

{\mathbf{P}}\left(\Phi(T_{n}^{(v_{1})})=t^{\prime},\Phi(T_{n}^{root})=t^{\prime\prime}\right)=\dfrac{n\text{deg}_{t^{\prime}}(1)}{(n-1)(k+1)!(n-k-1)!};

(7)

while, for
k=n
and
t^{\prime}\in I_{n}
,

{\mathbf{P}}\left(\Phi(T_{n}^{(v_{1})})=t^{\prime}\right)=\frac{\text{deg}_{t^{\prime}}(1)}{(n-1)(n-1)!}.

(8)

Proof.

Note that, conditionally given
v_{1}v_{2}
, we have either
v_{1}=1
, so that
\Phi(T_{n}^{(v_{1})})=T_{n}
and
T_{n}^{root}=\emptyset
, or
v_{1}>1
which implies that
T_{n}=T_{n}^{root}\cup T_{n}^{(v_{1})}\cup\{v_{0}v_{1}\}
and
2\leq|T_{n}^{(v_{1})}|<n
.

For the first case, let
t,t^{\prime}\in I_{n}
, then

{\mathbf{P}}\left(T_{n}^{(v_{1})}=t^{\prime}|T_{n}=t\right)={\mathbf{P}}\left(v_{1}=1|T_{n}=t\right){\mathbf{1}}_{[t^{\prime}=t]}.

The probability on the right side, given that
t=t^{\prime}
, is precisely the ratio between the degree of the root of
t^{\prime}
and the number of edges. Therefore

{\mathbf{P}}\left(\Phi(T_{n}^{(v_{1})})=t^{\prime}\right)=\sum_{t\in I_{n}}{\mathbf{P}}\left(T_{n}^{(v_{1})}=t^{\prime}|T_{n}=t\right){\mathbf{P}}\left(T_{n}=t\right)=\frac{\text{deg}_{t^{\prime}}(1)}{n-1}{\mathbf{P}}\left(T_{n}=t^{\prime}\right).

Furthermore,
|I_{n}|=(n-1)!
, so that
{\mathbf{P}}\left(T_{n}=t^{\prime}\right)=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}
and (
8
) is obtained.

Let
2\leq k<n
,
t^{\prime}\in I_{k}
and
t^{\prime\prime}\in I_{n-k}
. Similarly, it suffices to verify that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}\left(\Phi(T_{n}^{(v_{1})})=t^{\prime},\Phi(T_{n}^{root})=t^{\prime\prime}|T_{n}=t\right)=\frac{\text{deg}_{t^{\prime}}(1)}{n-1}{\mathbf{1}}_{[t\in C_{t^{\prime}}]};

(9)

where
C_{t^{\prime}}
is the set of increasing trees
t
of size
n
such that there is a vertex set
\{v_{0},\dots,v_{k}\}
such that
i)

t^{(v_{1})}
has vertex set
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}
,
ii)

\Phi(t^{(v_{1})})=t^{\prime}
,
iii)

v_{0}v_{1}\in t
and
iv)

\Phi(t\setminus t^{\prime})=t^{\prime\prime}
(in particular, the vertex set of
t\setminus t^{\prime}
is
[n]\setminus\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}
).

The above conditions imply that
|C_{t^{\prime}}|=\binom{n}{k+1}
. To see this, let

L=\{v_{0},\dots,v_{k}\}\subset[n]
and
W=\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-k}\}=[n]\setminus\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
satisfy
v_{i}<v_{i+1}
and
w_{j}\leq w_{j+1}
for all
i,j
. Denote by
t^{\prime}_{L}
the tree obtained after relabelling each vertex
i\in t^{\prime}
with vertex
v_{i}
; similarly, let
t^{\prime\prime}_{W}
be the tree obtained after relabelling each vertex
j\in t^{\prime\prime}
with vertex
w_{j}
. It is clear that each
t\in C_{t^{\prime}}
has the form
t=t^{\prime}_{L}\cup t^{\prime\prime}_{W}\cup\{v_{0}v_{1}\}
for some set
L\subset[n]
of size
k+1
.

To verify (
9
) we observe that the probability vanishes whenever
t\notin C_{t^{\prime}}
. On the other hand, conditionally given that
t=t^{\prime}_{L}\cup t^{\prime\prime}_{W}\cup\{v_{0}v_{1}\}\in C_{t^{\prime}}
, the event
\{\Phi(T_{n}^{(v_{1})})=t^{\prime},\Phi(T_{n}^{root})=t^{\prime\prime}\}
is equivalent to choosing one of the
\text{deg}_{t^{\prime}}(1)
edges that are children of
v_{1}
in
t
, this probability is uniform over
t\in C_{t^{\prime}}
.
Finally, (

3.1
Harmonic-biased random walk with a barrier

As we mentioned before, when
\xi
has distribution as in (
4
), the process
(R^{(n)}_{i})_{i\geq 0}
corresponds precisely to the number of jumps of the classic cutting process of random recursive trees. In the case of the degree-biased cutting, conditionally given a tree of size
n
, the size of the cut
\zeta^{(n)}
follows the distribution

{\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta^{(n)}=k\right)=f_{n}(k)n/(n-1)
where

\displaystyle f_{n}(k):=\frac{H_{k-1}}{k(k+1)}{\mathbf{1}}_{[2\leq k<n]}+\frac{H_{k-1}}{k}{\mathbf{1}}_{[k=n]}.

(13)

Unfortunately, there is no distribution
\zeta
on positive integers such that, for
2\leq k\leq n
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta=k|\zeta\leq n\right)={\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta^{(n)}=k\right)

(14)

To see this, write
p_{k}:={\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta=k\right)
. Rearranging the terms of (
14
), we need

\displaystyle\frac{n}{n-1}\sum_{i=2}^{n}p_{i}=\frac{p_{k}}{f_{n}(k)}

(15)

to hold for all
2\leq k\leq n
. Since the left side does not depend on
k
and converges to one, we infer that it is necessary that
p_{k}=\frac{H_{k-1}}{k(k+1)}
for all
k\geq 2
. However, in such a case, (
15
) fails to hold when
k=n
and
n\geq 3
.

Remark 3.3
.

The probability that the degree-biased cutting procedure is erased in one step has a relatively large mass coming from an
instant deletion
of the tree (case
k=n
in (
13
)). Thus,
\zeta^{(n)}
has anomalous behaviour at its largest atom
n
, preventing an accurate approximation by an unbounded random variable
\zeta
.

In what follows we let
(R^{(n)}_{i})_{i\geq 0}
be the random walk with a barrier on
n
with jump distribution
\zeta
given by
{\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta=k\right)=\frac{H_{k-1}}{k(k+1)}
and let
J_{n}
be the number of jumps of the process
(R^{(n+1)}_{i})_{i\geq 0}
; see Section
1.2
. Then
\zeta
is our best jump distribution approximation for the number of vertices discarded after each degree-biased cut. The role of
\zeta
is analogous to
\xi
, defined (
3
), for the classic random cutting process of
T_{n}
.

Let
\tau=\inf\{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:R^{(n+1)}_{i}>0\}
. By the strong Markov property, the process
(R^{(n+1)}_{\tau+j})_{j\geq 0}
is independent of
(R^{(n+1)}_{i})_{0\leq i\leq\tau}
given
R^{(n+1)}_{\tau}
. In particular, conditionally given
R^{(n+1)}_{\tau}=k
,
1<k\leq n
,
(R^{(n+1)}_{\tau+j})_{j\geq 0}
has the same distribution as
(R^{(n-k+1)}_{j})_{j\geq 0}
.
Let
J_{0}=J_{1}=0
. Similarly to Proposition
3.2
, for
j\geq 2
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}\left(J_{n}=j\right)=\sum_{k=2}^{n-2}{\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta=k\,|\,\zeta\leq n\right){\mathbf{P}}\left(J_{n-k}=j-1\right).

(16)

We now prepare for the proofs of Theorems
1.6
and
1.5
. Using a telescopic argument, we have for
i\geq 0
,

\sum_{k=i+1}^{n}\frac{1}{k(k+1)}=\sum_{k=i+1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k+1}=\frac{1}{i+1}-\frac{1}{n+1}.

(17)

In turn, for
n\geq 2
,

{\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta\leq n\right)=\sum_{k=2}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\dfrac{1}{ik(k+1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\dfrac{1}{i}\sum_{k=i+1}^{n}\dfrac{1}{k(k+1)}=1-\frac{1}{n}-\frac{H_{n-1}}{n+1}.

(18)

This verifies, in passing, that
\zeta
is a random variable on
\mathbb{N}
. In addition, note that

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{n}-\frac{H_{n-1}}{n+1}\right)^{-1}=\frac{n(n+1)}{n^{2}-nH_{n-1}-1}\geq\frac{n+1}{n-H_{n-1}}\geq\frac{n}{n-1}.

Consequently, for
2\leq k<n
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta=k|\zeta\leq n\right)=\frac{H_{k-1}}{k(k+1)}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}-\frac{H_{n-1}}{n+1}\right)^{-1}\geq{\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta^{(n)}=k\right).

(19)

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

For
n\leq 3
,
K_{n}=J_{n}
almost surely.
For
n\geq 4
, it suffices to verify that

{\mathbf{P}}\left(J_{n}\geq j\right)\geq{\mathbf{P}}\left(K_{n}\geq j\right)
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Clearly
{\mathbf{P}}\left(J_{n}\geq 1\right)={\mathbf{P}}\left(K_{n}\geq 1\right)=1
, so henceforth we assume that
j\geq 2
.
From the expression in (
16
), by changing the order of the sums, we get

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}\left(J_{n}\geq j\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=j}^{\infty}\sum_{k=2}^{n-2}{\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta=k\,|\,\zeta\leq n\right){\mathbf{P}}\left(J_{n-k}=i-1\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{n-2}{\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta=k\,|\,\zeta\leq n\right){\mathbf{P}}\left(J_{n-k}\geq j-1\right);

(20)

analogously, using (
12
),

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}\left(K_{n}\geq j\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{n-2}{\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta^{(n)}=k\right){\mathbf{P}}\left(K_{n-k}\geq j-1\right).

(21)

The proof of Theorem
1.5
is complete by induction on
j\geq 2
and
n\geq 4
using (
19
)–(
21
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

From (
18
) we have that the tails of
\zeta
satisfy

{\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta\geq n\right)=1-{\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta\leq n-1\right)=\frac{1}{n-1}+\frac{H_{n-2}}{n}=\frac{\ln n}{n}(1+o(1)).

(22)

Thus,

\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta\geq j\right)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{H_{j}}{j}\right)(1+o(1))\sim\frac{(\ln n)^{2}}{n}.

Therefore, conditions of Proposition
2.1
are satisfied with
\ell(x)=(\ln n)^{2}/2
for
x>1
. Hence,
{\mathbf{E}}\left[J_{n}\right]\sim 2n(\ln n)^{-2}
and
J_{n}/{\mathbf{E}}\left[J_{n}\right]
converges in probability to one as

.

We now verify that the conditions of Proposition
2.2
are satisfied for

,
b(x)=2x(\ln x)^{-2}
and

.

First, substitution of
n=x\ln x
in (
22
) yields

x{\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta\geq x\ln x\right)=x{\mathbf{P}}\left(\zeta\geq\lceil x\ln x\rceil\right)=\frac{x(\ln x+\ln\ln x)}{x\ln x}(1+o(1))\to 1,\text{ as }x\to\infty,

where

is the ceiling function.

Again, by using (
22
) in the definition of
\phi(x)
and

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{n}\frac{\ln x}{x}dx=\frac{(\ln n)^{2}}{2},

(23)

we have
\phi(x)\sim\frac{x(\ln x)^{2}}{2}
since

\phi(x)=2x+x\int_{2}^{x\ln{x}}\frac{\ln y}{y}(1+o(1))dy=\frac{x(\ln(x\ln x))^{2}}{2}(1+o(1))\sim\frac{x(\ln x)^{2}}{2};

where we used that

Similarly, the fact that

is a slowly varying function implies that

. Therefore

whereas,

Proposition
2.1
then implies that

\displaystyle\frac{J_{n}-b(n)}{a(n)}=\frac{J_{n}}{4n}(\ln n)^{3}-\frac{\ln n}{2}

converges in distribution to a random variable

which recovers the statement in Theorem

∎

4
A related coalescent process

There is a natural coalescent process associated with the cutting procedure of a random tree. A coalescent process is a Markov process that takes its values in the set of partitions of
\mathbb{N}
and it is generally described by its restrictions to the set
[n]
, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The general procedure for obtaining an
n
-coalescent from a cutting procedure is the following. At any given step, we have a tree whose vertex set
\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{m}\}
is the blocks of a partition of
[n]
; moreover, the blocks are listed in increasing order of least elements and all paths starting from the root
v_{1}
are increasing. Select a random vertex
v
(which in the cutting process would be removed) and add the labels of the removed vertices into the label
v
; in other words, coalesce all the blocks of the subtree of
v
, inclusive, to form a tree on a new label-set. The
n
-coalescent is the process of the partition of
[n]
corresponding to the label set; starting from a tree with all singletons.

Goldschmidt and Martin first proposed the connection between cutting procedures and coalescent processes
[
13
]
. They identified that the coalescent starting from a random recursive tree
T_{n}
defines the Bolthausen Sznitman coalescent on
\mathbb{N}
. Conditionally given that there are
b
blocks in the partition, for
2\leq k\leq b\leq n
, the rate at which any given set of
k
labels coalesce is

\frac{(b-k)!(k-2)!}{(n-1)!}.

(24)

Procedures to obtain beta-coalescent use uniformly random binary trees and Galton-Watson trees with offspring distribution in the domain of attraction of a stable law of index
\alpha\in[1/2,1)

[
1
]
.

A similar process is studied in
[
20
]
for plane-oriented recursive trees (also identified as preferential attachment trees): Select a vertex
v_{1}
uniformly at random. If
v_{1}
is a leaf, do nothing. Otherwise, select one of its successors, say
v_{2}
, uniformly at random. Proceed as the process described above with the edge
v_{1}v_{2}
. In this case, the resulting coalescent process is related to an arcsine coalescent; however, the finite coalescent processes are not consistent, that is, these cannot be extended to a coalescent process on
\mathbb{N}
.

4.1
Degree-biased version

We define the degree-biased coalescent process as follows: start with
T_{n}
a random recursive tree on
[n]
and associate an independent exponential random variable with mean
1
to each edge. This random variable is the time when the edge is selected to perform the first step of the degree-biased cutting process.

If the edge
\ell_{i}\ell_{j}
is selected and
i>1
, then there exists
\ell_{h}
such that
\ell_{h}\ell_{i}
is an edge on
T_{n}
. The labels in the subtree
T^{(\ell_{i})}
are instantly added to the label of the vertex
\ell_{h}
. That is, the new set of labels replaces
\ell_{h}
with
\ell_{h}\cup\{s:s\in T_{n}^{(\ell_{i})}\}
.

The proof of the following proposition is based on Proposition
1.3
and the ideas in Lemma
3.1
.

Proposition 4.1
.

The first coalescing event corresponding to the degree-biased cutting of a random recursive tree of size
n
merges any given set of
k
labels at rate

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(n-k)!(k-2)!}{(n-1)!}H_{k-2}\quad\text{for $3\leq k<n$},

(25)

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}_{n,n}

\displaystyle=H_{n-1}+\dfrac{H_{n-2}}{n-1}.

(26)

Proof.

We start with the case
k=n
corresponding to having all singletons merged in one step; from the degree-biased cutting procedure, this is equivalent to having the process end in exactly one step. Namely, either the tree is instantly erased (an edge adjacent to the root is selected in the cutting process) or the root has exactly one vertex attached, vertex 2, and the selected edge
v_{1}v_{2}
has
v_{1}=2
. In other words, the probability that all
blocks coalescent corresponds to the probability that
|T_{n}^{(v_{1})}|\in\{n-1,n\}
. Since the next coalescent event is the minimum of
n-1
exponential random variables with rate 1, the total rate of instant coagulation is, by Proposition
1.3
,

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}_{n,n}=(n-1){\mathbf{P}}\left(|T_{n}^{(v_{1})}|\in\{n-1,n\}\right)=H_{n-1}+\frac{H_{n-2}}{n-1}.

For the case
3\leq k<n
, let
L=\{v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k-1}\}
be a set of
k
vertices in
T_{n}
such that
v_{0}<v_{1}<\cdots<v_{k-1}
. Then, the rate
\lambda_{L}
at which elements in
L
coalesce is given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{L}=(n-1){\mathbf{P}}\left(V(T_{n}^{(v_{1})})=L\right).

For each
t^{\prime}\in I_{k-1}
, let
C_{L,t^{\prime}}
be the set of increasing trees
t
of size
n
such that
i)

t^{(v_{1})}
has vertex set
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k-1}\}
,
ii)

\Phi(t^{(v_{1})})=t^{\prime}
and
iii)

v_{0}v_{1}\in t
. By a similar argument as that in the proof of Lemma
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}\left(V(T_{n}^{(v_{1})})=L\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t^{\prime}\in I_{k-1}}\sum_{t\in C_{L,t^{\prime}}}{\mathbf{P}}\left(T_{n}=t,\Phi(T_{n}^{(v_{1})})=t^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t^{\prime}\in I_{k-1}}\sum_{t\in C_{L,t^{\prime}}}\frac{\text{deg}_{t^{\prime}}(1)}{(n-1)(n-1)!};

using
|C_{L,t^{\prime}}|=|I_{n-k+1}|=(n-k)!
together with (
10
), we get

\displaystyle\lambda_{L}=\frac{(n-k)!}{(n-1)!}\sum_{t^{\prime}\in I_{k-1}}\text{deg}_{t^{\prime}}(1)=\frac{(n-k)!(k-2)!}{(n-1)!}H_{k-2}.

The rate
\lambda_{L}
depends only on the size of
L
; completing the proof of (
25
).
∎

As we mentioned before, these coalescents can not be extended to a
\Lambda
-coalescent process on
\mathbb{N}
since their rates do not satisfy the consistency equations
\lambda^{\prime}_{n,k}=\lambda^{\prime}_{n+1,k}+\lambda^{\prime}_{n+1,k+1}
. For example, the following expression does not vanish,

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}_{n,n}+\lambda^{\prime}_{n,n-1}-\lambda^{\prime}_{n-1,n-1}

\displaystyle=H_{n-1}-H_{n-2}+\frac{H_{n-2}}{n-1}-\frac{H_{n-3}}{n-2}+\frac{H_{n-3}}{(n-1)(n-2)};

after a careful rearrangement and cancellation of terms, it simplifies to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n-1}+\frac{H_{n-2}}{n-1}-\frac{H_{n-3}}{n-1}=\frac{1}{n-1}+\frac{1}{(n-1)(n-2)}=\frac{1}{n-2}>0.
