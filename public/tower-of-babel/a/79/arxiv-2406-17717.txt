Michael P. Landry, Yair N. Minsky, Samuel J. Taylor

Abstract
. Let φ \varphi be a transitive pseudo-Anosov flow on an oriented, compact 3 3 -manifold M M , possibly with toral boundary. We characterize the surfaces in M M that are (almost) transverse to φ \varphi . When φ \varphi has no perfect fits (e.g. φ \varphi is the suspension flow of a pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism), we prove that any Thurston-norm minimizing surface S S that pairs nonnegatively with the closed orbits of φ \varphi is almost transverse to φ \varphi , up to isotopy. This answers a question of Cooper–Long–Reid. Our main tool is a correspondence between surfaces that are almost transverse to φ \varphi and those that are relatively carried by any associated veering triangulation. The correspondence also allows us to investigate the uniqueness of almost transverse position, to extend Mosher’s Transverse Surface Theorem to the case with boundary, and more generally to characterize when relative homology classes represent Birkhoff surfaces.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview

Let
\varphi
be a transitive pseudo-Anosov flow on a compact, oriented
3
-manifold
M
, possibly with toral boundary. Important examples include the geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle of a closed hyperbolic surface and the suspension flow on the mapping torus of a pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism. A pseudo-Anosov flow is called
circular
if it is such a suspension flow, up to isotopy and reparametrization.

A basic problem
is to classify the surfaces in
M
that are transverse to a pseudo-Anosov flow, up to isotopy. Mosher’s Transverse Surface Theorem
[
Mos92a
,
Mos92b
]
from the early 1990s
answers the related question of which homology classes have representatives almost transverse to the flow
\varphi
. Here,
almost transverse
means that
a mild operation
must first be performed on the singular orbits of flow; see
Section

3
for details.
When
M
is closed, Mosher proves that if a surface
S
has nonnegative algebraic intersection with each closed orbit of
\varphi
, then
S
is homologous to a surface almost transverse to
\varphi
.

Although Mosher’s theorem is quite powerful, there are situations in which one needs to know whether a given surface
S
is transverse to
\varphi
up to isotopy rather than homology (see e.g. the discussion following
A
). Thirty years ago Cooper–Long–Reid asked, in the special case where
\varphi
is circular and
M
is closed:
which surfaces in
M
can be made transverse to
\varphi
up to isotopy
[
CLR94
, Sec. 4, Ex. 2]
?

Among our results, we give a complete answer to this question in the more general setting of transitive pseudo-Anosov flows on compact manifolds.
An obvious necessary condition for
S
to be almost transverse to
\varphi
up to isotopy is that
S
have nonnegative algebraic intersection with each closed orbit. A more subtle one is that
S
be
taut
, meaning it realizes the Thurston norm of its homology class and has no nullhomologous collection of components (
[
Mos92b
, Sec. 2]
and
Lemma

5.2
). When specialized to the setting of Cooper–Long–Reid’s question, our results say these two necessary conditions are in fact sufficient for almost transversality (
A
); we also describe exactly when almost transversality can be promoted to transversality (
E
).

Our characterization follows from a general treatment of almost transversality for surfaces with respect to an arbitrary transitive pseudo-Anosov flow
\varphi
, which can be informally summarized as follows. See the next section for the formal statements.

•

Characterization via veering triangulations:
Our primary technical result is that, up to isotopy, a surface
S
is almost transverse to
\varphi
if and only if it is relatively carried by any veering triangulation associated to
\varphi
(
E
). The point is that both the veering triangulation and the relatively carried condition allow us to study transversality in a way which is purely combinatorial. Moreover, the surface is honestly transverse to
\varphi
if and only if it has a specific type of carried position with respect to the veering triangulation.

•

Transverse surface theorems:
Using the correspondence above, we prove a general transverse surface theorem that states that after isotopy
S
is almost transverse if and only if it is taut, algebraically nonnegative on closed orbits, and can be isotoped to have no negative intersections with a specific finite collection of orbits (
B
). This last condition is vacuous for a large class of pseudo-Anosov flows (including circular flows), but we show by example that it is necessary in general (
Theorem

6.7
).

•

Uniqueness of transverse position:
We show that the position of an almost transverse surface is essentially unique, up to isotopy along the flow. This includes the nontrivial fact that there is a unique way to minimally modify the flow (in a certain combinatorial sense) to make the surface transverse (
D
).

•

Existence of Birkhoff surfaces:

Plenty of pseudo-Anosov flows do not have (almost) transverse surfaces (e.g. geodesic flow of a hyperbolic surface). However, all pseudo-Anosov flows admit
Birkhoff surfaces
. By extending Mosher’s transverse surface theorem to manifolds with toral boundary (
Theorem

6.1
) we give a general characterization for which relative homology classes represent (almost) Birkhoff surfaces for
\varphi
(
C
). This also extends Fried’s characterization of relative classes representing Birkhoff sections (i.e. Birkhoff surfaces meeting every closed orbit in bounded time).

As mentioned above, our results include the case of pseudo-Anosov flows on manifolds with (toral) boundary. See
Section

3
for a precise definition. There are two important reasons for this, beyond simply generalizing from the closed case. First, the application to Birkhoff surfaces stated above is fairly straightforward after proving the transverse surface theorem (
Theorem

6.1
) for manifolds with boundary. Second, such flows can be used to understand the structure of the Thurston norm on manifolds with boundary in a way that has only previously been studied in the closed case. See
Theorem

3.6
for details.

1.2.
Results on transverse surfaces

Throughout the introduction we work with a pseudo-Anosov flow
\varphi
on a compact, oriented
3
–manifold
M
. We allow
\partial M\neq\emptyset
in which case each component of
\partial M
is a torus. See
Section

3
for the definitions.

Our first result characterizes almost transverse surfaces for the class of pseudo-Anosov flows
without perfect fits
. Such flows were first studied by Fenley
[
Fen98
,
Fen99a
]
and include the class of circular (i.e. suspension) flows mentioned above. For our purposes here, it suffices to know that
\varphi
has no perfect fits if and only if there are no
anti-homotopic orbits
, i.e. closed orbits
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
with
\gamma_{1}
homotopic to
-\gamma_{2}
.
See
Section

3.5.1
, and particularly
Section

3.5.1
, for the precise definition and further discussion.

The following lemma says that everything “seen” by these flows at the level of homology is also seen at the level of isotopy:

Theorem A

(Strong transverse surface theorem)
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudo-Anosov flow with no perfect fits on
M
, possibly with boundary. Then a properly embedded, oriented surface
S
is almost transverse to
\varphi
, up to isotopy, if and only if
S
is taut and pairs nonnegatively with the closed orbits of
\varphi
.

See
Lemma

5.2
and
Theorem

6.2
. We remark that in general the classes in
H_{2}(M,\partial M)
that pair nonnegatively with the closed orbits of
\varphi
form a closed cone, which under the hypotheses of
A
, is exactly the cone over a closed face of the Thurston norm ball. This was proved by Mosher when
\partial M=\emptyset
and we give a proof in the case with boundary (
Theorem

3.6
). Hence,
A
implies that the surface
S
is almost transverse to
\varphi
up to isotopy if and only if
e_{\varphi}([S])=\chi(S)
, where
e_{\varphi}
is the Euler class of the flow, suitably defined, and
\chi
is Euler characteristic. See
Section

3.7
for additional details.

A
is a key ingredient in
[
LMT23a
, Theorem A]
where we prove that every atoroidal endperiodic map is isotopic to the first return map of a circular pseudo-Anosov flow to a leaf of a transverse depth one foliation.

To complement
A
, we show by explicit example in
Theorem

6.7
that the hypothesis of no perfect fits cannot be entirely dropped. However, we
can
characterize transverse surfaces for general transitive pseudo-Anosov flows using an auxiliary (noncanonical) collection of closed orbits. For the statement, a finite collection of closed orbits
\kappa

kills the perfect fits of
\varphi
if there are no anti-homotopic orbits in the punctured manifold
M\smallsetminus\kappa
; again see
Section

3.5.1
. The following is a restatement of
Theorem

6.3
.

Theorem B
.

Let
\varphi
be a transitive pseudo-Anosov flow on
M
, and let
\kappa
be any finite collection of closed regular orbits of
\varphi
that kills its perfect fits.
Then an oriented surface
S
in
M
is isotopic to a surface that is almost transverse to
\varphi
if and only if

(1)

S
is taut,

(2)

S
has nonnegative (homological) intersection with the closed orbits of
\varphi
,
and

(3)

the algebraic and geometric intersection numbers of
S
with
\kappa
are equal.

Given a taut surface
S
that has nonnegative algebraic intersection with each closed orbit of
\varphi
,
B
gives a simple criterion for whether
S
is almost transverse to
\varphi
up to isotopy: one need only check whether
S
can be isotoped so that all its intersection points with the oriented link
\kappa
, if any, are positive.

Our general technique, which handles pseudo-Anosov flows on manifolds with boundary, has applications to the more general situation of Birkhoff surfaces. A
Birkhoff surface
(sometimes called a partial section) of
\varphi
is an immersed surface
\Sigma
in
M
whose boundary components cover closed orbits of
\varphi
and
whose interior is embedded and transverse to
\varphi
. More generally, a Birkhoff surface of a dynamic blowup of
\varphi
will be called an
almost
Birkhoff surface of
\varphi
.

The next theorem generalizes Fried’s result on Birkhoff sections
[
Fri82
, Theorem N]
and seems new even for geodesic flow on hyperbolic surfaces. See
Theorem

6.4
for statement that includes conditions for when the ‘almost’ can be dropped.

Theorem C

(Representing relative classes with Birkhoff surfaces)
.

Let
\varphi
be a transitive pseudo-Anosov flow and let
\kappa
be any collection of closed orbits. Then a class
\eta\in H_{2}(M,\kappa)=H^{1}(M\smallsetminus\kappa)
is represented by an almost Birkhoff surface
\Sigma
in
M
with
\partial\Sigma\subset\kappa
if and only if it is nonnegative on closed orbits of
M\smallsetminus\kappa
.

Finally, we turn to the sense in which ‘almost transverse’ position is essentially unique.
For this, note that the statement

“
S
is almost transverse to the pseudo-Anosov flow
\varphi
up to isotopy”

contains two potential ambiguities. The first is that it does not specify a particular dynamic blowup to which
S
is transverse up to isotopy. The second is that it says nothing about the
way
in which
S
can be realized transversely to a given dynamic blowup
\varphi^{\sharp}
—can one interpolate between any two such positions by flowing along
\varphi^{\sharp}
, or are there multiple transverse positions such that any isotopy between them must move through non-transverse surfaces?

These ambiguities are resolved with the following result, a combination of
Theorem

8.1
and
Theorem

8.2
.

Theorem D
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudo-Anosov flow on
M
and let
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
be isotopic properly embedded surfaces which are minimally transverse to dynamic blowups
\varphi_{1}^{\sharp}
and
\varphi_{2}^{\sharp}
, respectively. Then
\varphi_{1}^{\sharp}
and
\varphi_{2}^{\sharp}
are combinatorially equivalent.

Moreover, if the surfaces are transverse to a single blowup
\varphi^{\sharp}
, then
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are isotopic along flowlines of
\varphi^{\sharp}
.

We note here that ‘combinatorial equivalence’ is a slightly weaker notion than orbit equivalence; see
Section

3.2
.
The proof of the theorem requires a detailed analysis of the flow space shadows of transverse surfaces that is carried out in
Section

7
.

1.3.
Veering triangulations as the main tool

As before, let
\varphi
be a transitive pseudo-Anosov flow on
M
and let
\kappa
be a finite collection of closed orbits that kills its perfect fits. Such a collection exists by work of Fried
[
Fri83
]
and Brunella
[
Bru95
]
; see also Tsang
[
Tsa24
, Proposition 2.7]
.
We let
\kappa_{s}
denote the union of
\kappa
and any singular orbits or boundary components of
M
.

Our primary technical tool is a canonical
veering triangulation
on the manifold
M\smallsetminus\kappa_{s}
which was essentially constructed by Agol and Gueritaud.
In our previous work (
[
LMT23b
]
), we show the
2
-skeleton of this triangulation is a branched surface properly embedded in
M\smallsetminus\kappa_{s}
that is positively transverse to
\varphi
. This is exactly what makes the veering triangulation useful in understanding transverse surfaces.

Given a properly embedded surface
S
in
M
, one can ask whether
S
can be put into a ‘relatively carried’ position with respect to the triangulation. In essence, this means
S
is carried by the branched surface away from
\kappa_{s}
and intersects a neighborhood of
\kappa_{s}

efficiently.
See
Section

1
for the precise definitions and details.

In
[
Lan22
, Introduction]
it was remarked that “being [relatively] carried by
\tau^{(2)}
is a combinatorial version of almost transversality.”
Our main result relating the veering triangulation to transverse surfaces makes this precise:

Theorem E

(Almost transverse if and only if relatively carried)
.

Let
\varphi
be a transitive pseudo-Anosov flow on
M
and let
\tau
be any dual veering triangulation. For any surface
S
in
M
,

S\text{ is almost transverse to }\varphi\quad\iff\quad S\text{ is relatively carried by }\tau.

Moreover,
S
is
honestly
transverse to
\varphi
if and only if it is relatively carried
\tau
without complementary annuli.

See
Theorem

5.1
and
Corollary

8.6
.

Basic conventions

In this paper all the 3-manifolds we consider are oriented, and all homology and cohomology groups have coefficients in
\mathbb{R}
. If
B
is a subset of a metric space
A
, we denote the completion of
A-B
in the induced path metric by
A\!\bbslash\!B
.

2.
Veering triangulations and relatively carried surfaces

We begin with some background on veering triangulations. Our goal is to establish
Theorem

2.12
, which itself extends the main theorem from
[
Lan22
]
.

2.1.
Index and train tracks

A
surface with cusps
is a surface
S
with a discrete collection of points in
\partial S
called
cusps
which are modeled on the point
(0,0)\in\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid y\geq\sqrt{|x|}\}
.
The
index
of a compact surface with cusps
S
is

\mathrm{index}(S)=2\chi(S)-\#\cusps(S).

Recall that a
train track
in a surface
S
is a 1-complex in
S
with an everywhere-defined tangent space. This means that the edges meeting a given vertex are “combed” so as to be tangent. The edges of a train track are called
branches
and the vertices are called
switches
. In this paper all the train tracks we consider will lie in the interior of surfaces.

Given a train track
t\subset S
, a
patch
of
t
is a component of
S\!\bbslash\!t
. A patch naturally has the structure of a surface with cusps, with the cusps arising from the switches of
t
. The index of these patches is additive, in the sense that
2\chi(S)=\mathrm{index}(S)=\sum\mathrm{index}(p)
where the sum is taken over all patches of
t
.

2.2.
Veering triangulations

An ideal tetrahedron is a tetrahedron minus its vertices, and an ideal triangulation of a (necessarily noncompact) 3-manifold
Z
is a cellular decomposition of
Z
into ideal tetrahedra.

A
veering tetrahedron

\Delta
is an ideal tetrahedron together with the following extra data:

•

Two faces are cooriented outward, and two are cooriented inward. These are called the top and bottom faces of
\Delta
, respectively.

•

The edge along which the top faces meet is labeled
\pi
, and so is the edge along which the bottom faces meet. These are called the top and bottom edges of
\Delta
, respectively.

•

The remaining edges, called equatorial edges, are labeled 0.

•

The equatorial edges are colored red or blue in an alternating fashion (see
Figure

1
). In some of the literature the red and blue edges are called right and left veering, respectively, and the property of being right or left veering is called the
veer
of an edge.

Up to oriented equivalence, there are two veering tetrahedra in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The “standard” veering tetrahedron is distinguished by the property that when viewed from above and projected to the plane as in
Figure

1
, the red edges have positive slope and the blue edges have negative slope.

Figure 1.
The standard veering tetrahedron. Each face is cooriented out of the page.

A
veering triangulation
of an oriented 3-manifold
Z
is an ideal triangulation of
Z
together with an assignment of veer to each edge and coorientation to each face, such that:

•

each ideal tetrahedron is equivalent to the
standard
veering tetrahedron via an orientation-preserving map, and

•

the sum of the 0 and
\pi
labels around each edge of the ideal triangulation is
2\pi
.

We will always think of the the 0 or
\pi
label of an edge of a veering tetrahedron as describing the dihedral angle for that edge. Face coorientations, together with the condition that edge labels sum to
2\pi
around each edge, endow the 2-skeleton with the structure of a cooriented “branched surface” as depicted in
Figure

2
. We now briefly discuss branched surfaces.

Figure 2.
A local picture of part of an edge of a veering triangulation, showing the 2-skeleton’s branched surface structure.

2.3.
Branched surfaces and carrying

A
branched surface
is the 2-dimensional analogue of a train track: it is a 2-complex with a continuously varying tangent plane at each point, locally modeled on the quotient of a finite stack of disks by identifying half disks of adjacent disks, requiring that the inclusion of each disk be smooth. If
B
is a branched surface, then the
branch locus

\brloc(B)
is the union of all nonmanifold points of
B
, and the
sectors
of
B
are the components of
B\!\bbslash\!\brloc(B)
.

Let
B
be a branched surface living in a 3-manifold. A
standard neighborhood
of
B
is a small tubular neighborhood of
B
foliated in a standard way by intervals (see
[
FO84
,
Oer86
]
). This foliation is called the
vertical foliation
of
N(B)
. We say that
B
is
cooriented
if the vertical foliation is oriented. If
\Lambda
is a 2-dimensional lamination embedded in
N(B)
transversely to the vertical foliation, we say
\Lambda
is
carried
by
B
(note
\Lambda
could simply be a compact surface). If
B
is cooriented and
\Lambda
is cooriented, when we say that
\Lambda
is
carried
by
B
we additionally assume that each leaf of the vertical foliation passes from the negative side to the positive side of
\Lambda
at each intersection point. We refer the reader to
[
FO84
,
Oer86
]
for more details on branched surfaces and carrying.

There is a homotopy equivalence
N(B)\twoheadrightarrow B
, called the
collapsing map
, that collapses the leaves of the vertical foliation. If
S
is a surface carried by
B
, the
collapse
of
S
is the image of
S
under the collapsing map.

Left: a truncated veering tetrahedron. Right: the tips of truncated tetrahedra divide the boundary of
M_{U}
into upward and downward ladders (green and blue, respectively) separated by ladderpole curves.

2.4.
Relative branched surfaces and relative veering triangulations

Let
M
be a compact oriented 3-manifold with toral boundary. A
tube system

U
for
M
is the union of a small closed tubular neighborhood of
\partial M
with a small closed tubular neighborhood of a link
L
in
M
. Here it is possible that
\partial M=\varnothing
or
L=\varnothing
. The solid torus components of
U
are called
solid tubes
and the components homeomorphic to
T^{2}\times I
are called
hollow tubes
.
A
veering triangulation of
M
relative to
U
is a veering triangulation of
Z=\intr(M)-L
such that

•

the 2-skeleton
\tau^{(2)}
of
\tau
intersects
\partial U
transversely, and

•

\tau^{(2)}\cap U
is diffeomorphic to
(\tau^{(2)}\cap\partial U)\times[0,\infty)
.

We remark that for any veering triangulation on
Z
, a tube system of
M
can be chosen to satisfy these conditions.
We also use the terminology “relative veering triangulation of
M
with tube system
U
.”

Let
\tau
be a relative veering triangulation of
M
with tube system
U
. We set the notation

\tau_{U}=\tau^{(2)}\cap M\!\bbslash\!U.

We see that
\tau_{U}
is a branched surface in
M_{U}:=M\!\bbslash\!U
. There is an induced cellular decomposition of
M_{U}:=M\!\bbslash\!U
whose cells are truncated veering tetrahedra, as shown on the left side of
Figure

3
.
Each tip of a truncated veering tetrahedron, corresponding to an ideal vertex, is combinatorially a triangle with cooriented sides. However, the veering structure endows each tip with the smooth structure of a disk with 2 cusps. The 1-skeleton of the tessellation of
\partial M
is a train track
\partial\tau_{U}\colon=\tau_{U}\cap\partial M_{U}
whose patches are the tips of truncated veering tetrahedra.

A tip is called
upward
if it has two sides cooriented outward, and
downward
otherwise. The definition of veering triangulations implies, with some work, that the union of all upward tips meeting
\partial M_{0}
is a collection of annuli called
upward ladders
, and symmetrically for downward tips and
downward ladders
. See the right side of
Figure

3
. The upward and downward ladders meet along a collection of curves called
ladderpole curves
. For a given component
T
of
\partial M_{U}
, the slope of the ladderpole curves on
T
is called the
ladderpole slope
for
T
. This structure was first observed in
[
FG13
, Section 2]
; see that paper for more details.

A properly embedded surface
S\subset M
is
relatively carried by
\tau
if
S\cap(M\!\bbslash\!U)
is carried by the branched surface
\tau_{U}
in the standard sense of branched surfaces, and each component of
S\cap U
is either a meridional disk in a solid tube or an annulus with at most one boundary component on
\partial M
.
There are two types of possible annuli in
S\cap U
; those that meet
\partial M
and those that do not. We call the latter type
ladderpole annuli
since both their boundary components have ladderpole slope in
\partial M_{U}
.

Let
N(\tau_{U})
be a standard neighborhood of
\tau_{U}
in
M\!\bbslash\!U
. The collapsing map
N(\tau_{U})\twoheadrightarrow\tau_{U}
extends over
U
by a map that is the identity away from a small neighborhood of
\partial M_{U}\subset M
. Hence we can speak of the collapse of a surface relatively carried by
\tau_{U}
—this will be the union of some sectors of
\tau_{U}
with some disks and annuli lying in
U
.

2.5.
Cusped tori

Let
D
be a compact disk with
n>0
cusps in its boundary, and let
f\colon D\to D
be an orientation-preserving diffeomorphism. The mapping torus
T
of
f
is called a
cusped solid torus
(see
Figure

4
), and the suspensions of the cusps of
D
under
f
are called the
cusp curves
of
T
. The
index
of
T
is defined to be the index of
D
, that is
2(1)-n
. We say that
n
is the
number of prongs
of
T
, denoted
\prongs(T)
. In particular
\mathrm{index}(T)=2-\prongs(T)
. Note that
\prongs(T)
is not necessarily the same as the number of cusp curves; in general the number of cusp curves is equal to
\prongs(T)
divided by the order of the permutation obtained by restricting
f
to the cusps of
D
.

Figure 4.
An example of a cusped solid torus. To obtain a cusped torus shell, we would drill out a neighborhood of the core circle.

A
cusped torus shell
is the object obtained by modifying the definition of a cusped solid torus by replacing the disk
D
with an annulus with one smooth boundary component and one with
n
cusps. The cusp curves of cusped torus shells are defined as for cusped solid tori. We do not define the index of a cusped torus shell because there is no canonical choice of meridian.

2.6.
Stable and unstable branched surfaces

Let
M
be a compact oriented 3-manifold, and let
\tau
be a veering triangulation of
M
relative to a tube system
U
.
There are two branched surfaces with generic branch locus associated to
\tau
. The
stable branched surface
, denoted
B^{s}
, intersects each veering tetrahedron as shown the top row of
Figure

5
. The
unstable branched surface
, denoted
B^{u}
, intersects each veering tetrahedron as shown in the bottom row of
Figure

5
. In this paper we are not concerned with the positions of
B^{s}
and
B^{u}
relative to each other—it is enough to know that they intersect each tetrahedron as described. We refer the reader to
[
LMT24
, Section 4.2]
for additional details.

Figure 5.

L
and
R
labels denote left and right veering edges, respectively. Note the veers of the bottom edges in the top row are irrelevant, as are the veers of the top edges in the bottom row.

The components of
M\!\bbslash\!B^{s}
and
M\!\bbslash\!B^{u}
are cusped solid tori and cusped torus shells, each of which contains a single solid or hollow tube, respectively. We will refer to these as the
cusped tori of
B^{s}
and
B^{u}
.

Let
U_{0}
be a component of
U
, and let
T_{0}^{s}
and
T_{0}^{u}
be the cusped tori of
B^{s}
and
B^{u}
containing
U_{0}
. Let
t_{0}
be the tessellation induced by the intersection of
\tau
with
\partial U_{0}
. Then as described in
[
LMT24
, Section 5.1.1]
, the cusp curves of
T_{0}^{s}
are in canonical bijection with the cores of upward ladders of
t_{0}
. Similarly the cusp curves of
T_{0}^{u}
are in canonical bijection with the cores of downward ladders. In particular
T_{0}^{s}
and
T_{0}^{u}
are diffeomorphic.

2.7.
Spheres, disks, tori, and annuli

We continue to assume
\tau
is a relative veering triangulation of
M
relative to
U
.

The existence of
\tau
restricts the types of properly embedded surfaces in
M
with nonnegative Euler characteristic that can exist.
As observed in
[
FSS19
]
, the branched surfaces
B^{s}
and
B^{u}
are laminar and therefore carry essential laminations; it follows that
M
is irreducible
[
Li02
,
GO89
]
. (For a given boundary component of
M
, the degeneracy slope of both laminations is equal to the ladderpole slope of the veering triangulation). The next lemma lets us analyze disks and tori.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
M
be a 3-manifold admitting a relative veering triangulation
\tau
. Let
S
be any
properly embedded surface in
M
. Then
S
can be isotoped rel
\partial M
so that
S\cap B^{s}
and
S\cap B^{u}
have no patches of positive index disjoint from
\partial S
.

Proof.

This is
[
Lan22
, Lemma 5.1]
in the additional generality of our setting, i.e.
S
may have boundary. The same proof (which uses irreducibility of
M
) applies.
∎

Now suppose that
D
is a properly embedded disk in
M
. Applying
Lemma

2.1
, we can isotope
D
to have no patches of positive index disjoint from
\partial D
. Euler characteristic forces
D\cap B^{s}=\varnothing
, so
D
lies in a cusped torus shell of
B^{s}
and is isotopic into
\partial M
.

Now we consider annuli and tori. We say that the relative veering triangulation
\tau
is
strict
if each cusped solid torus of
B^{s}
has negative index. By the discussion above this is equivalent to requiring the same of
B^{u}
, or requiring that the meridional curve for every solid tube has geometric intersection number
\geq 6
with the union of all ladderpole curves.

Suppose that
\tau
is strict, and that
A
is a properly embedded annulus in
M
. Note that no patch of
A\cap B^{s}
meeting
\partial A
can have positive index. After an application of
Lemma

2.1
, all patches of
A\cap B^{s}
disjoint from
\partial A
will have nonpositive index. This implies that all patches of
A\cap B^{s}
have index
0
. By strictness, none of the patches disjoint from
\partial A
is a meridional disk for a solid tube; hence
A
may be isotoped to lie outside of
U
away from a regular neighborhood of
\partial A
, unless
A
already lies entirely inside a component of
U
. Since
M-U
admits a complete hyperbolic metric by
[
HRST11
,
FG11
]
,
A
must be homotopic rel
\partial A
into
\partial M
.

Continuing to assume
\tau
is strict, suppose that
T
is a torus embedded in
M
. As with annuli, we can apply
Lemma

2.1
to arrange for
T\cap B^{s}
to have no patches of positive index. In this case, strictness implies that
T
can be isotoped to lie outside of
U
. As above,
M-U
admits a complete hyperbolic metric. Hence,
T
is either parallel to a component of
\partial M
or
T
is compressible in
M-U
, hence also in
M
.

We summarize the above discussion in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.2
.

If
\tau
is a relative veering triangulation of a 3-manifold
M
, then:

(a)

M
is irreducible and
\partial
-irreducible, and

(b)

if
\tau
is strict, then
M
is also atoroidal and anannular.

2.8.
The dual graph

Let
M
be a compact 3-manifold, and let
\tau
be a veering triangulation of
M
relative to a tube system
U
.

We construct a graph by placing a vertex in each tetrahedron of
\tau
and placing an edge between two vertices for each face identification. There is an obvious embedding of
\Gamma
into
M
such that each edge of
\Gamma
passes through one face of
\tau
, and we can orient each edge so that these intersections are compatible with the coorientation of the
\tau
-faces. This directed graph (together with its embedding into
M
) is called the
dual graph
, and denoted
\Gamma
. Since each vertex of
\Gamma
has two incoming and two outgoing edges,
\Gamma
is a 1-cycle representing a class in
H_{1}(M)
that (by a slight abuse) we also call
\Gamma
.

It is clear from
Figure

5
that the branch loci of both
B^{s}
and
B^{u}
can be canonically identified with
\Gamma
. We will always think of
\brloc(B^{s})
and
\brloc(B^{u})
as directed graphs using this identification with
\Gamma
. Note that this endows each cusp curve of each cusped torus of
B^{s}
and
B^{u}
with an orientation. It turns out that these orientations are coherent, in the sense that any two cusp curves of a cusped torus are isotopic preserving orientation.

If
T
is a cusped solid torus, there is a natural orientation of its core curve, defined by the property that any cusp curve is homotopic to a positive multiple of the core curve.

2.9.
Removable annuli and efficient carrying.

Let
\tau
be a veering triangulation of
M
relative to a tube system
U
, and let
S
be a surface in
M
relatively carried by
\tau
. This position is not (combinatorially) unique in general, and it will be important for us (e.g. in
Proposition

4.2
) to discuss a particular simplification that can eliminate components of
S\cap U
.

For this, suppose that
A
is an annulus component of
S\cap U
disjoint from
\partial M
, such that after collapsing
S
, both of its boundary components lying on
adjacent
ladderpoles of some ladder
\ell
. If this collapsing of

is homotopic in
U
to
\ell
fixing
\partial A
, then we say that

is a
removable annulus
.
If
S
is relatively carried by
\tau
without any removable annuli, then we say that
S
is
efficiently relatively carried by
\tau
.
The next lemma justifies this terminology by saying that “removable annuli can be removed”:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\tau
be a veering triangulation of a 3-manifold
M
relative to a tube system
U
. Let
S
be a surface relatively carried by
\tau
. Then
S
is isotopic to a surface efficiently relatively carried by
\tau
.

Proof.

Suppose
A
is a removable annulus, with both components of
\partial A
carried by the ladderpoles of a ladder
L
. If
A
is innermost, meaning that the component of
U\!\bbslash\!A
containing

is disjoint from

, then we may apply an
annulus move
as defined in
[
Lan22
, Sec. 4.5]
and shown in
Figure

6
(technically, this is the reverse of what that paper calls an annulus move) to eliminate

while preserving the fact that

is relatively carried by

. Applying this argument finitely many times to innermost such annuli, we are done.
∎

2.10.
Thurston’s norm on homology and its dual

We now briefly define the Thurston norm on
H_{2}(M,\partial M)
and mention some of its properties. See
[
Thu86
]
for more details and proofs.
Let
S
be an oriented surface that is properly embedded in
M
, and define

\chi_{-}(S)=-\chi\left(S-(\text{sphere, disk, torus, and annulus components})\right).

For an integral homology class
\alpha\in H_{2}(M,\partial M)
, define

x(\alpha)=\min_{[S]=\alpha}\{\chi_{-}(S)\},

where the minimum is taken over all oriented properly embedded surfaces representing
\alpha
.

Thurston showed that this function on the integral lattice of
H_{2}(M,\partial M)
extends to a continuous convex function
H_{2}(M,\partial M)\to\mathbb{R}
that is linear on rays through the origin, and vanishes precisely on the subspace spanned by the classes of properly embedded surfaces with nonnegative Euler characteristic. If there are no such surfaces representing nonzero homology classes, then
x
is a vector space norm on
H_{2}(M,\partial M)
; otherwise
x
is merely a pseudonorm. In a slight abuse of terminology,
x
is known as the
Thurston norm
regardless of whether it is a
bona fide
norm. We often think of
x
as a function on
H^{1}(M)
via Poincaré/Lefschetz duality.

The
dual Thurston norm

x^{*}\colon H^{2}(M,\partial M)\to\mathbb{R}\cup{\infty}
is defined by

x^{*}(u)=\sup\{u\mid x(u)\leq 1\}.

Regardless of whether
x
is a norm, the function
x^{*}
restricts to a norm on the subspace on which it takes finite values.

2.11.
The Euler class

Let
\gamma
be the homology class of the union of the oriented cores of the solid torus components of
U
. Define
e_{\tau}\in H^{2}(M,\partial M)
by the formula

2e_{\tau}=\langle 2\gamma-\Gamma,\cdot\rangle.

Here
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the intersection pairing between
H_{1}(M)
and
H_{2}(M,\partial M)
. We call
e_{\tau}
the
Euler class
of
\tau
.

We now explain that this generalizes previous definitions in the literature of “Euler classes” for veering triangulations.
If
U
has no solid torus components then the formula reduces to
2e_{\tau}=-\langle\Gamma,\cdot\rangle
, which is the formula for combinatorial Euler class given in
[
LMT24
]
, based on Lackenby’s definition for arbitrary taut ideal triangulations from
[
Lac00
]
.

If
T
is a cusped solid torus of
B^{s}
, let
\beta_{T}
be the union of cusp curves of
T
. Then
\beta_{T}
is homologous to the 1-chain
\prongs(T)\cdot\core(T)
, which can be seen by homotoping
\beta_{\tau}
onto
\core(T)
.
The Euler class defined in
[
Lan22
]
is an element of the first homology of a closed manifold defined as
\frac{1}{2}\sum\mathrm{index}(T)\cdot[\core(T)]
, where the sum is taken over all cusped solid tori of
B^{s}
(all cusped tori are solid here because the manifold is closed). We have

\displaystyle\sum\mathrm{index}(T)\cdot[\core(T)]

\displaystyle=\sum(2-\prongs(T))\cdot[\core(T)]

\displaystyle=\sum(2[\core(T)]-[\beta_{T}])

\displaystyle=2\gamma-\Gamma,

where in the last line we have used that
\Gamma
is homologous to the union of
\beta_{T}
over all tubes
T
. Therefore in the case when
B^{s}
has no cusped torus shells, our Euler class is Poincaré dual to the Euler class from
[
Lan22
]
. If cusped torus shells are present, we can still apply the above analysis to each cusped solid torus. This shows the following.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
c
be the union of all
cusp curves of cusped torus shells of
B^{s}
. Then

2e_{\tau}=\left\langle\left(\sum\mathrm{index}(T_{i})\cdot\core(T_{i})\right)-c,\cdot\right\rangle,

where the sum is over all cusped solid tori
T_{i}
of
B^{s}
.

The next two lemmas will be key tools for connecting veering triangulations to the Thurston norm. They are analogous to
[
Lan22
, Lemma 5.3, Lemma 5.4]
and the proofs are adaptations of the proofs of those lemmas to the more general setting here. Before we state and prove them, we make a few definitions and set some notation.

Let
S
be a surface properly embedded in
M
transverse to
B^{s}
such that each patch
p
of the train track
t^{s}:=S\cap B^{s}
is
\pi_{1}
-injective in its cusped torus, which we denote by
T(p)
.
If
p
is a patch such that
T(p)\!\bbslash\!p
is disconnected, or if
p\cap\partial M
is nonempty and has null pairing with the cusp curves or
T
, then we say that
p
is
superfluous
. Note that if
p
is superfluous, then
p
does not contribute to the algebraic intersection of
S
with
\Gamma
or
\gamma
, hence does not contribute to the pairing of
S
with
e_{\tau}
.

Let
NSD
and
NSA
denote the sets of nonsuperfluous disks and annuli, respectively. If
p
is a nonsuperfluous disk then
T(p)
is a cusped solid torus and
p
is a meridional disk of
T(p)
. Moreover
p
has pairing
+1
or
-1
with the core of
T(p)
; we denote this pairing by
\sign(p)
. For any patch
p
, we say a cusp of
p
is positive or negative according to whether the corresponding point of
S\cap\Gamma
is positive or negative, respectively.
Define
\mathrm{algcusps}(p)
to be the number of positive cusps of
p
minus the number of negative cusps of
p
.
Note that
\mathrm{algcusps}(p)
is always less than or equal to
\cusps(p)
, the number of cusps of
p
.

Having set all this notation,
Lemma

2.4
now gives

(2.5)

\displaystyle 2e_{\tau}([S])=\sum_{\text{NSD}}\sign(p)\cdot\mathrm{index}(T(p))+\sum_{\text{NSA}}(-\mathrm{algcusps}(p)).

Recall that a properly embedded surface
S
in
M
is
taut
if
x([S])=\chi_{-}(S)
and no collection of its components is nullhomologous.

Lemma 2.6
.

The dual Thurston norm of
e_{\tau}
is
\leq 1
.

As a consequence, if
Y
is a surface such that
\chi_{-}(Y)=-e_{\tau}([Y])
, then

(a)

x([Y])=-e_{\tau}([Y])
and

(b)

if no collection of components of
Y
is nullhomologous, then
Y
is taut.

Proof.

Let
S
be a taut surface in
M
.
We can isotope
S
so that each patch
\pi_{1}
-injects into its cusped torus, and so that, by
Lemma

2.1
combined with the fact that
S
is not a disk homotopic into
\partial M
, there are no patches of
S\cap B^{s}
with positive index.

We then have the following equalities and inequalities (justifications are below):

\displaystyle 2e_{\tau}([S])

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{NSD}}\sign(p)\cdot\mathrm{index}(T(p))+\sum_{\text{NSA}}(-\mathrm{algcusps}(p))

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\text{NSD}}\mathrm{index}(T(p))+\sum_{\text{NSA}}-\cusps(p)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\text{NSD}}\mathrm{index}(p)+\sum_{\text{NSA}}\mathrm{index}(p)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\text{all patches}}\mathrm{index}(p)

\displaystyle=2\chi(S)

The first line is a restatement of
Equation

2.5
.
The second uses that all cusped solid tori have index
\leq 0
.
The third line uses that
\mathrm{index}(p)\leq\mathrm{index}(T(p))
for
p\in\text{NSD}
and
\mathrm{index}(p)=-\cusps(p)
for
p\in\text{NSA}
.
For the fourth line we have used that all patches of
S\cap B^{s}
have nonpositive index. Hence
\chi(S)\leq e_{\tau}([S])
, so

-e_{\tau}([S])\leq-\chi(S)=x([S]).

This shows that
e_{\tau}
has dual Thurston norm
\leq 1
as claimed.

Now suppose that
Y
is as in the statement of the lemma. Then
x([Y])\leq\chi_{-}(Y)=-e_{\tau}([Y])\leq x([Y])
so all these quantities are equal. In particular (a) holds, and if no collection of components of
Y
is nullhomologous then
Y
is taut.
∎

The next lemma is a generalization of
[
Lan22
, Lemma 5.4]
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
S
be a surface properly embedded in
M
that is relatively carried by a relative veering triangulation
\tau
of
M
. Then
S
is taut and
x([S])=-e_{\tau}([S])
.

Proof.

By
[
LMT24
, Lemma 5.8]
the dual graph
\Gamma
is strongly connected, so every
\Gamma
-edge is part of a directed cycle. Hence each component of
S
pairs positively with some directed curve in
M
, so no collection of components is nulhomologous. In particular, using
Proposition

2.2
, we have
\chi_{-}(S)=-\chi(S)
.

Because
S
is relatively carried by
\tau
, each patch
p
of
S\cap B^{s}
is either

(a)

a meridional disk of a cusped solid torus,

(b)

a topological annulus with one boundary component on
\partial M
and another boundary component having a nonzero number of positive cusps, or

(c)

a topological annulus with no cusps in its boundary.

In case (a),
\mathrm{index}(p)=2-\cusps(p)
. In case (b),
\mathrm{index}(p)=-\cusps(p)
. In case (c),
\mathrm{index}(p)=0
. In each case
\mathrm{index}(p)
is precisely the contribution of
p
to
2e_{\tau}([S])
, so we have
\chi_{-}(S)=-e_{\tau}([S])
.
Lemma

2.6
finishes the proof.
∎

2.12.
Combinatorial transverse surface theorems

We define
\mathrm{cone}_{1}(\Gamma)
to be the smallest closed convex cone in
H_{1}(M)
containing the homology class of each directed cycle in the dual graph
\Gamma
. Its dual cone
\mathrm{cone}_{1}^{\vee}(\Gamma)
is the cone of all classes in
H^{1}(M)
that pair nonnegatively with each element of
\mathrm{cone}_{1}(\Gamma)
.

The following is the generalization of
[
Lan22
, Theorem 5.5]
to our setting.

Theorem 2.8

(Combinatorial TST)
.

Let
\tau
be a veering triangulation of
M
relative to a tube system
U
. Let
u
be an integral cohomology class in
H^{1}(M)
. Then
u\in\mathrm{cone}_{1}^{\vee}(\Gamma)
if and only if there exists a surface
S
, necessarily taut, such that
S
is relatively carried by
\tau
and
[S]
is Poincaré dual to
u
.

Proof.

The proof of
[
Lan22
, Theorem 5.5]
goes through almost without modification, but we describe it here in broad strokes.

If
u
is dual to a carried surface, then that surface is taut by
Lemma

2.7
and it is clear that
u\in\mathrm{cone}_{1}^{\vee}(\Gamma)
since the surface is positively transverse to
\Gamma
.

Conversely, let
u\in\mathrm{cone}_{1}^{\vee}(\Gamma)
, and let
u_{U}
be the pullback to
H^{1}(M_{U})
. We can use
u_{U}
to produce a surface
S_{U}
in
M_{U}
that is carried by
\tau_{U}
and dual to
u_{U}

[
LMT24
, Proposition 5.11]
. Let
M_{0}
be the union of
M_{U}
with the solid tubes. The surface
S_{U}
can be capped off by annuli and disks inside the solid tubes to produce a surface dual to
u_{0}
, the pullback of
u
to
M_{0}
. This is essentially because
u\in H^{1}(M)
(see
[
Lan18
, Lemma 3.3]
; that proof goes through here, guaranteeing that capping off is possible).

The only mild novelty in the setting of this theorem is the possibility that
M_{0}\neq M
; that is, our tube system may have hollow tubes. At a component of
\partial M_{U}
to which a hollow tube is glued, we simply extend
S_{U}
to
\partial M
by gluing in annuli using the
T^{2}\times I
product structure of the hollow tubes.
The result is a relatively carried surface
S
that is taut by
Lemma

2.7
. Since
S_{U}
is dual to
u_{U}
,
S
is dual to a class mapping to
u_{U}
under
H^{1}(M)\to H^{1}(M_{U})
. Since this map is injective,
S
is Poincaré dual to
u
.
∎

Corollary 2.9
.

If
\tau
is a relative veering triangulation in
M
, then the dual cone
\mathrm{cone}_{1}^{\vee}(\Gamma)
is contained in the cone in
H^{1}(M)
on which
-e_{\tau}
and
x
agree as maps
H^{1}(M)\to\mathbb{R}
.

Corollary 2.10
.

Let
S
be a taut surface with
[S]\in\mathrm{cone}_{1}^{\vee}(\Gamma)
. Then
x([S])=\chi_{-}(S)=-e_{\tau}([S])
.

We now aim to prove a version of the main result in
[
Lan22
]
when
\tau
is strict. We will use the following, which is a stronger version of
[
Lan22
, Theorem 8.1]
:

Lemma 2.11
.

Assume that
\tau
is a strict relative veering triangulation of
M
and
let
S
be an incompressible and boundary incompressible surface in
M
. Further suppose that
S
has the property that for any surface
S^{\prime}
isotopic to
S
that is transverse to
B^{u}
and
B^{s}
, either:

•

one of
S^{\prime}\cap B^{u}
or
S^{\prime}\cap B^{s}
has a patch of positive index, or

•

every negative switch of
S^{\prime}\cap B^{u}
and every negative switch of
S^{\prime}\cap B^{s}
belongs to a bigon.

Then
S
is isotopic to a surface relatively carried by
\tau
.

2.11
.

Lemma

2.11
is stated in greater generality than originally appeared in
[
Lan22
]
. There it was assumed that all tubes of
U
are hollow, so that
\tau
is automatically strict.
However, the proof given in
[
Lan22
]
goes through as written, modulo slight differences in terminology.
We note the additional fact that the individual “moves” comprising the isotopy in
Lemma

2.11
can be performed some distance away from
\partial M
, so the isotopy can be performed fixing
\partial S
pointwise.

Theorem 2.12

(Combinatorial Strong TST)
.

Let
\tau
be a strict relative veering triangulation of
M
. Let
S
be a taut surface in
M
. The following are equivalent:

(a)

[S]\in\mathrm{cone}_{1}^{\vee}(\Gamma)

(b)

x([S])=-e_{\tau}([S])

(c)

S
is relatively carried by
\tau
up to an isotopy, which can be chosen to fix
\partial S
.

Proof.

Condition (a) implies (b) by
Corollary

2.10
. Also (c) implies (a) because any carried surface has only positive intersections with
\Gamma
.

It remains to prove that (b) implies (c), so suppose that
x([S])=-e_{\tau}([S])
. In light of
Lemma

2.11
, our strategy will be to show that for any surface isotopic to
S
, one of the two conditions in
Lemma

2.11
holds. The proof is analogous to the proof of
[
LMT24
, Theorem 5.12]
, but with our slightly different definition of the Euler class. We present it for the sake of completeness.

By an isotopy, we can assume that
S
is transverse to both
B^{s}
and
B^{u}
and that each patch of
t^{s}=B^{s}\cap S
and
t^{u}=B^{u}\cap S
is
\pi_{1}
-injective in its cusped torus. We will show that if
t^{s}
has no patches of positive index, then every negative switch of
t^{s}
belongs to a bigon.

If
p
is a superfluous patch, note that
p
has
\mathrm{algcusps}(p)=0
and the number of intersections of
p
with
\gamma
counted with sign is 0.

By (b) and the fact that
S
is taut, we have
2\chi(S)=2e_{\tau}([S])
. By
Equation

2.5

we can write this as

\displaystyle 2\chi(S)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{NSD}}\sign(p)\cdot\mathrm{index}(T(p))+\sum_{\text{NSA}}(-\mathrm{algcusps}(p)).

If we let SP denote the set of superfluous patches, the index sum formula for
\chi(S)
gives

2\chi(S)=\sum_{\text{all patches}}\mathrm{index}(p)=\sum_{\text{SP}}\mathrm{index}(p)+\sum_{\text{NSD}}\mathrm{index}(p)+\sum_{\text{NSA}}\mathrm{index}(p).

Subtracting the above two expressions for
2\chi(S)
, we obtain

0=\sum_{\text{SP}}\mathrm{index}(p)+\sum_{\text{NSD}}(\mathrm{index}(p)-\sign(p)\cdot\mathrm{index}(T(p)))+\sum_{\text{NSA}}(\mathrm{index}(p)+\mathrm{algcusps}(p)).

We claim every term in each of the three sums above is nonpositive:

•

First sum: every term is nonpositive because we are assuming there are no patches of positive index.

•

Second sum: if
p
is a nonsuperfluous disk with
\sign(p)=-1
then clearly
\mathrm{index}(p)-\sign(p)\cdot\mathrm{index}(T(p))\leq 0
is a sum of two nonpositive numbers, and if
\sign(p)=1
then the inequality follows from the fact that a meridional disk of a cusped solid torus has index at most the index of the cusped solid torus.

•

Third sum: if
p
a nonsuperfluous annulus then
\chi(p)\leq 0
. Hence
\mathrm{index}(p)+\mathrm{algcusps}(p)\leq\mathrm{index}(p)+\cusps(p)=2\chi(p)\leq 0
, so every term in the second sum is nonpositive.

Therefore each term in the sums over SP, NSD, and NSA is equal to 0. This has the following implications:

•

each superfluous patch has index 0, so is either a bigon or an annulus with no cusps,

•

each nonsuperfluous disk patch

has
\mathrm{index}(p)=\sign(p)\cdot\mathrm{index}(T(p))
, so
\sign(p)=+1
and all cusps of

are positive.

•

each nonsuperfluous annulus patch
p
in a cusped torus shell has
\mathrm{index}(p)=-\mathrm{algcusps}(p)
, so
p
is equal to an annulus with no negative cusps,

We have shown that any negative switch of
t^{s}
must belong to a bigon. A symmetric argument shows the same for
t^{u}
. Hence (b) implies (c).
∎

Corollary 2.13

If

3.
Pseudo-Anosov flows and dynamic blowups

We refer to Mosher
[
Mos92a
]
, Fenley–Mosher
[
FM01
]
and Agol–Tsang
[
AT24
]
for thorough discussions of the definition of a
pseudo-Anosov flow on a closed 3-manifold. There are in fact two
definitions, a smooth and a topological one, and their equivalence for transitive flows has
recently been shown by Agol–Tsang
[
AT24
]
relying on the Anosov-case established by Shannon
[
Sha20
]
.
It will suffice for our purposes to record some of the main properties associated with (either
type of) pseudo-Anosov flow, which we will do below.

We will then define the notion of a
dynamic blowup

of such a flow, which is a
generalization of the definition given by Mosher
[
Mos90
]
. A special
case of this will yield a definition—basically the expected one—of pseudo-Anosov flows on manifolds with toral
boundary, and the general case will give us the definition of
almost
pseudo-Anosov
flows, in both the closed case and the case with boundary.

A
pseudo-Anosov flow

\varphi
on a closed 3-manifold
M
has in particular the
following properties:

(1)

The flow
\varphi
has a finite collection
\Pi_{s}

of closed orbits (called

singular orbits
), and a
\varphi
-invariant pair of
transverse singular
foliations
of codimension 1 whose leaves intersect along orbits of
\varphi
. The foliations are nonsingular and transverse in the complement of
\Pi_{s}
and have a standard form in a neighborhood of
\Pi_{s}
, as described below.

(2)

The two foliations are called the
stable
and the
unstable
foliations
of
\varphi
, denote
W^{s}
and
W^{u}
, respectively.
For any two points
x,y
in a single leaf of the stable foliation
W^{s}
, the
trajectories are asymptotic, meaning that for some orientation-preserving

s\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\lim_{t\to+\infty}d(\varphi_{t}(x),\varphi_{s(t)}(y))=0
. The
corresponding statement holds for the unstable foliation
W^{u}
with
t\to\infty
replaced by
t\to-\infty
.

We will typically work with
transitive
flows (i.e. possessing a dense
orbit). Mosher
[
Mos92a
, Proposition 2.7]
proved that all pseudo-Anosov
flows on closed atoroidal manifolds are transitive. Once we define pseudo-Anosov flows on manifolds with boundary, it will be easy to see that atoroidality implies transitivity in the broader setting, by choosing appropriate Dehn fillings and applying Mosher’s result in the closed case.

It remains to describe
the local dynamics around a periodic orbit
\gamma
in

\Pi_{s}
, as a suspension of a
surface homeomorphism.
In what follows, we err on the laborious side in order to prepare for the
description of the blowups.
Our description is equivalent to the
pseudo-hyperbolic orbits
described in e.g.
[
FM01
,
AT24
]
.

Let
U\subset V
be nested open 2-disks, and
f\colon U\to V
an orientation-preserving embedding, with the following
properties: There is a one-vertex tree
T
with an even number of
prongs
(at least 4) properly embedded
in
V
so that
f^{-1}(T)\subset T\cap U
and
f
fixes the vertex
p
and no other
points.
Each component
Q
of
V\smallsetminus T
is an
open quadrant
and its closure in
V
,

\overline{Q}
, is
a
closed quadrant.
The map
f
permutes the germs of quadrants in the sense that it takes
Q\cap U
into
some
Q^{\prime}
. Thus there is a least power
f^{k}
that takes germs of quadrants into themselves (that
is, there is some smaller neighborhood
U^{\prime}\subset U
such that
f^{k}(Q\cap U^{\prime})\subset Q
).

Finally, there is an identification of
\overline{Q}
with a neighborhood of the
origin in a closed quadrant of the Euclidean plane, which conjugates
f^{k}
to the map
(x,y)\mapsto(\lambda x,y/\lambda)
for some
\lambda>1
(on the image of
\overline{Q}\cap U
).

In particular note that along the interval
\partial Q\cap T
the map
f
fixes
the point
p
and translates the rest in the
same
direction as viewed from inside
Q
.
Hence, each prong at
p
is either
stable
(i.e. contracting) or
unstable
(i.e. expanding). Note that our convention is to draw the stable direction as vertical and the unstable direction as horizontal.

We suspend this picture by considering
V\times[0,1]
, with
U\times 1
identified with

f(U)\times 0
using
f
. Vertical flow is then defined, for bounded times, on a small neighborhood of the
closed orbit
p\times[0,1]/\sim
. This is the model for a pseudo-Anosov flow in a
neighborhood of a closed orbit
\gamma
; the orbit is
singular
(i.e.
\gamma\in\Pi_{s}
) if the number of stable (and unstable) prongs is at least
3
.
The suspended vertical lines of the quadrants are the intersections of leaves of the
stable foliation with the neighborhood, and the suspended horizontal lines are
intersections of leaves of the unstable foliation. Note that prongs thus alternate between
stable and unstable singular leaves.
Finally, we define the
index
of
\gamma
to be
2
minus the number of stable prongs.

From this description one sees that each singular periodic orbit is contained in a singular leaf of
the stable foliation, which has the structure of 3 or more annuli glued together along the
orbit (this is true at least locally and it is not hard to see that it is globally
true.) The orbit is the attractor of all flow lines in its stable leaf. The corresponding
statement is true for the unstable singular leaf.

3.0
Nearby homeomorphism.

Note that in the disk case, the map

3.2.
Global blowups

A
dynamic blowup
of a pseudo-Anosov flow
\varphi
on a closed 3-manifold
M
is a flow

\varphi^{\sharp}
on a compact 3-manifold
M^{\sharp}
, together with a map
\pi\colon M^{\sharp}\to M
with the
following properties:

(1)

\pi
semiconjugates
\varphi^{\sharp}
to
\varphi
.

(2)

There is a finite collection
\Pi
of closed orbits of
\varphi
such that, from

\pi^{-1}(M\smallsetminus\Pi)
to
M\smallsetminus\Pi
,
\pi
is a homeomorphism and a conjugacy of
the flows.

(3)

For each orbit
\gamma
in
\Pi
there is a neighborhood
W
whose preimage under

\pi
is a local blowup in the sense described above. In particular,
\pi^{-1}(W)
is either a
neighborhood of the suspension of a tree homeomorphism (the “disk case” of
Section

3.1
), or a
neighborhood of a toral boundary component (the “annulus case”).

We call the neighborhood
W
from item
(3)
above a
standard neighborhood
of
\gamma
.

A blowup
\varphi^{\sharp}
of a pseudo-Anosov flow is called an
almost pseudo-Anosov flow
.
We say that the
combinatorial type
of such a blowup is the blowup graph
T^{\sharp}

associated to each local blowup, together with its embedding in the disk or annulus
V^{\sharp}
,
up to proper isotopy.
Note that the combinatorial type is completely specified by saying
which pairs of quadrants of the original return map, in a neighborhood of each orbit, are to be
connected along the blowup graph. We do
not
claim that, given the combinatorial
type, the blowup flow
\varphi^{\sharp}
is unique up to orbit equivalence. Indeed there are
further choices in the construction (notably the choice of gluing map across the blowup
graph), but these will not matter to our discussion.

There are several important special cases of dynamic blowups. If all the local blowups are in the disk case, then

M^{\sharp}
is still a closed manifold and in fact, using the discussion in
Section

3.1
, there is a homotopy of
\pi
to a homeomorphism from
M^{\sharp}
to

,
supported on a small neighborhood of
\pi^{-1}(\Pi)
. This is exactly the case defined
and used by Mosher
[
Mos90
]
.

If a local blowup is in the annulus case then the subgraph
G
is a circle each of whose vertices has degree
3
in

(i.e. each singularity is

so we have blown up a closed orbit to a boundary component in the simplest possible
way. We call this a

(see

Singular orbits and blowups.

By definition only singular orbits of

3.4.
Stable/unstable foliations of almost pseudo-Anosov flows

Let
\varphi
be an almost pseudo-Anosov flow on a compact 3-manifold
M
, obtained by
dynamically blowing up a pseudo-Anosov flow
\varphi^{\flat}
on a closed 3-manifold
M^{\flat}
. The

stable foliation
of
\varphi
is a singular foliation whose leaves are the
preimages of leaves of the stable foliation of
\varphi^{\flat}

under the semiconjugacy
(M,\varphi)\to(M^{\flat},\varphi^{\flat})
.
It is not hard to see that this definition is independent of which blowdown

(M^{\flat},\varphi^{\flat})
we choose.
We similarly define the

unstable foliation
of
\varphi
. One sees from the local structure of a
blowup that the preimage of a leaf is is connected, so
there is a one-to-one correspondence between leaves of the stable and unstable foliations
of
\varphi^{\flat}
and
\varphi
. If
H
is a stable leaf of
\varphi^{\flat}
containing a singular orbit

\gamma
of
\varphi^{\flat}
which is blown up to obtain
\varphi
, then the stable leaf of

\varphi
corresponding to
H
will contain every blown annulus collapsing to

\gamma
. Note that the blown annuli simultaneously belong to both stable and unstable leaves
of the foliations of
\varphi
, and in particular the foliations are not transverse
even away from the periodic orbits.

We say two orbits
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
of a flow on
a manifold
X
with metric
d
are
asynchronously proximal
(in the forward
direction)
if there are sequences of
times
(t_{n}),(t_{n}^{\prime})
with
t_{n},t_{n}^{\prime}\to\infty
such that
d(\gamma(t_{n}),\gamma^{\prime}(t_{n}^{\prime}))\to 0
. Note that this is a weaker notion than forward asymptotic: asynchronously proximal orbits get arbitrarily close, while asymptotic orbits get arbitrarily close and
stay
arbitrarily close.

The definition of asynchronously proximal will be relevant to us when we lift an almost pseudo-Anosov flow on a compact 3-manifold
M
to its universal cover.

For the next lemma, a stable
half-leaf
is a complementary component of the closed orbits within a stable leaf of
\varphi
.
An unstable half-leaf is defined similarly. Note that a stable/unstable leaf can contain
k
half-leaves, where
k
is any integer
k\geq 1
. The following is a key feature of almost pseudo-Anosov flows.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\varphi
be an almost pseudo-Anosov flow on a compact 3-manifold
M
, and let
\widetilde{\varphi}
be its lift to the universal cover
\widetilde{M}
. If
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
lie in the same stable half-leaf
of
\widetilde{\varphi}
, then
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
are asynchronously proximal. If they lie in the same unstable half-leaf, then they are asynchronously proximal in the backward direction.

In the case where
M
is closed, this lemma is an observation of Fenley in
[
Fen09
, Proof of Theorem 4.1]
although he does not use the term asynchronously proximal. The key point there and in this more general setting is that a semiconjugacy collapsing blown annuli can be taken to be an isometry away from a neighborhood of the blown annuli.

3.5.
Flow spaces and perfect fits

For a flow
\varphi
on a manifold
M
, its
flow space

\mathcal{O}
is obtained by taking the quotient of the universal cover
\widetilde{M}
of
M
by the orbits of the lifted flow
\widetilde{\varphi}
. The quotient map
\Theta\colon\widetilde{M}\to\mathcal{O}
is equivariant with respect to the
\pi_{1}(M)

which we continue to call the

.

A

in
\mathcal{O}
is a topological arc obtained by lifting a blown annulus to
\widetilde{M}
and projecting to
\mathcal{O}
; it is contained in leaves of both foliations.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\varphi
be an almost pseudo-Anosov flow on a compact manifold
M
and let
\mathcal{O}
be its flow space. Then
\mathcal{O}
is a simply connected surface that is (singularly) foliated by the images of the stable/unstable foliations, which are transverse away from blown segments.
If
M
is closed,
\mathcal{O}
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Otherwise,
\mathcal{O}
is homeomorphic to a disk minus a closed subset of its boundary.

In the case where
\varphi
is pseudo-Anosov and

is closed,
Theorem

3.3
was proven by Fenley–Mosher
[

. They also establish the result when

is almost pseudo-Anosov (again when

is closed) assuming that
\varphi
is transverse to a taut foliation.

We begin with some setup with the goal of showing that the general result follows from the special case proven by Fenley–Mosher. By definition (see
Section

3.2
), there is a quotient map
\pi\colon M\to M^{\flat}
, that blows
\varphi
down to a pseudo-Anosov flow
\varphi^{\flat}
on a closed manifold
M^{\flat}
. Let
\mathcal{O}^{\flat}
be the flow space of
\varphi^{\flat}

is a bifoliated plane.

3.3
.

The blowdown

is not unique (see
Section

3.3
) but the following discussion is true for any such blowdown.

The map
\pi\colon M\to M^{\flat}
is
\pi_{1}

be the

Denote by

the quotient of

\pi^{\prime}\colon M^{\prime}\to\widetilde{M}^{\flat}
collapses blown annular complexes (including boundary components) to singular orbits,
\pi^{\prime}_{\mathcal{O}}\colon\mathcal{O}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{O}^{\flat}

.

By construction, the flow space

\pi_{*}

Proof of
Theorem

3.3
.

With the description above, it now suffices to show that
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
is a surface, possibly
with boundary; that is, locally two-dimensional, and Hausdorff. The argument in

[

Following Fenley–Mosher, to show that
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}

it suffices to show that every point of

has a transverse (half-)disk neighborhood

\mathcal{O}^{\prime}

To prove the fact, note that such a cycle will project to an

-cycle in

with

,

is uniformly bounded

This shows that

given above then implies

This implies that its universal cover

3.5.1.
Perfect fits and flow space relations

Fix an almost pseudo-Anosov flow
\varphi
on
M
with flow space
\mathcal{O}
. A
perfect fit rectangle
in
\mathcal{O}
is a proper embedding of the rectangle with missing corner
[0,1]^{2}\setminus\{(1,1)\}
into
\mathcal{O}
that maps the horizontal/vertical foliations to the unstable/stable foliations of
\mathcal{O}
. We note that any perfect fit rectangle contains a perfect fit subrectangle that is disjoint from the singularities and blown segments of
\mathcal{O}
.

For any collection of closed orbits
\kappa
, let
\widehat{\kappa}
be the
\pi_{1}
–invariant
discrete collection of points in
\mathcal{O}
obtained by lifting
\kappa
to the universal
cover
\widetilde{M}
and projecting to
\mathcal{O}
. Then we say that
\kappa

kills perfect
fits
if
\widehat{\kappa}
meets the interior of every perfect fit rectangle in
\mathcal{O}
.
Note that for the definition, it suffices to only ever consider regular orbits since
singular orbits correspond to points in
\mathcal{O}
that do not lie in the interior of any
rectangle. If
\mathcal{O}
has no perfect fit rectangles (i.e.
\kappa=\emptyset
kills
perfect fits) then we say that
\varphi

has no perfect fits
. The importance of this condition was first recognized and studied by Fenley; see
[
Fen98
,
Fen99a
]
.

Now fix a collection
\kappa
of closed
regular
orbits of
\varphi
, and let
\pi\colon M\to M^{\flat}
be any blowdown to a pseudo-Anosov flow
\varphi^{\flat}
on a closed
manifold
M^{\flat}
.
Since the orbits in
\kappa
are regular,
\kappa
is mapped
homeomorphically to its image in
M^{\flat}
.
We define
\kappa^{\flat}
to be this image together with any
regular orbits of
\varphi^{\flat}
that are the images of components of
\partial M
.
Using the induced flow space map
\pi_{\mathcal{O}}\colon\mathcal{O}\to\mathcal{O}^{\flat}
(discussed
before the proof of

Theorem

3.3
), it is clear that
\kappa
kills perfect fits for
\varphi
if and only
if
\kappa^{\flat}
kills perfect fits for
\varphi^{\flat}
.

Still with
\kappa
fixed, we set
\kappa_{s}
to be the union of
\kappa
with the set of all singular orbits and blown annular complexes of
\varphi
(including those containing the boundary), and we let
\widehat{\kappa}_{s}
be obtained by lifting to the universal cover and projecting to
\mathcal{O}
. The blowdown
\pi
sends
\kappa_{s}
to
\kappa^{\flat}_{s}
, where
\kappa^{\flat}_{s}
is the union of
\kappa^{\flat}
with all singular orbits of
\varphi^{\flat}
.
In particular, we may use the restriction of
\pi
to
identify
the manifolds
M\smallsetminus\kappa_{s}=M^{\flat}\smallsetminus\kappa^{\flat}_{s}
, which we call
the
fully-punctured manifold
associated to the pair
(\varphi,\kappa)
.
The flow space of the fully-punctured manifold (for the restriction of
\varphi
) is denoted
\mathring{\mathcal{P}}
, and the natural projections
\mathring{\mathcal{P}}\to\mathcal{O}\smallsetminus\widehat{\kappa}_{s}\to\mathcal{O}^{\flat}\smallsetminus\widehat{\kappa}^{\flat}_{s}
are covering spaces.

The associated branched cover
\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{O}^{\flat}
, infinitely branched over

\widehat{\kappa}^{\flat}_{s}\subset\mathcal{O}^{\flat}
,
is called the
completed flow space
of the fully-punctured manifold. The added branch points are also called completion points or singularities of
\mathcal{P}
. Finally, we observe that
\kappa
fills perfect fits of
\varphi
if and only if
\mathcal{P}
has no perfect fit rectangles.

3.3
.

From the discussion here, we see that
\kappa
kills perfect fits of
\varphi
if and only if the associated Fried blowup
has no perfect fits.

To conclude, we recall the fact that for every transitive pseudo-Anosov flow
\varphi
there exists a finite collection of regular orbits that kills its perfect fits. This follows from that fact, due to Fried
[
Fri83
]
and Brunella
[
Bru95
]
, that every such flow has a Fried blowup that blows down to pseudo-Anosov suspension flow. From the discussion above, it is then clear that the closed regular orbits of the Fried blowup kill the perfect fits of
\varphi
. In fact, Tsang has recently proven that for any transitive pseudo-Anosov flow on a closed
3
-manifold, there is a single regular orbit that kills its perfect fits
[
Tsa24
, Proposition 2.7]
. Just as before, this is easily extended to all transitive, almost pseudo-Anosov flows on compact manifolds using any blowdown to a pseudo-Anosov flow on closed manifold.

We conclude with a remark that ties the notion of perfect fits to that of anti-homotopic orbits from the introduction.

3.3
Anti-homotopic orbits.

Let
M
be a closed
3
-manifold with a pseudo-Anosov flow
\varphi
. If
\varphi
has perfect fits, then Fenley proved that
\varphi
has anti-homotopic orbits (
[
Fen16
, Theorem B]
). Conversely, if
\varphi
has anti-homotopic orbits
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, then after taking compatible lifts to the universal cover and projecting to the flow space
\mathcal{O}
, we obtain points

and

that are fixed by some

in the conjugacy class determined by

. Fenley shows that in this case

and
p_{2}
are connected by a

(
[
Fen99a
, Theorem 4.8]
), which in particular implies that
\varphi
has perfect fits.

3.7.
Flows represent faces

We have now developed enough combinatorics to easily prove Mosher’s beautiful theorem “Flows Represent Faces”
[
Mos92b
]
, which connects the Thurston norm to dynamics, in the broader setting of pseudo-Anosov flows on manifolds with boundary.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudo-Anosov flow with no perfect fits on a manifold
M
with its associated strict relative veering triangulation
\tau
, furnished by
Theorem

3.4
.
Let
\gamma
be an orbit of
\varphi
. We define the index of
\gamma
to be 2 minus its number of prongs; in particular periodic orbits in
\partial M
have index
-1
. Define
e_{\varphi}\in H^{2}(M,\partial M)
by

2e_{\varphi}=\sum_{i}\langle\mathrm{index}(\gamma_{i})[\gamma_{i}],\cdot\rangle,

where the sum is over all singular orbits in
\intr(M)
and all
stable
periodic orbits in
\partial M
(i.e. the boundaries of stable half-leaves meeting
\partial M
). By
Lemma

2.4
and
Lemma

3.5
, we have
e_{\tau}=e_{\varphi}
.

Let
\mathrm{cone}_{1}(\varphi)
denote the cone in
H_{1}(M)
generated by the periodic orbits of
\varphi
. Its dual,
\mathrm{cone}_{1}^{\vee}(\varphi)\subset H^{1}(M)=H_{2}(M,\partial M)
, is the cone of classes that are nonnegative on the periodic orbits of
\varphi
.
It follows from
[
LMT23b
]
that the collection of periodic orbits of
\varphi
and the collection of directed cycles in the dual graph
\Gamma
of
\tau
generate the same cone in
H_{1}(M)
, so
\mathrm{cone}_{1}(\varphi)=\mathrm{cone}_{1}(\tau)
. See e.g.
[
LMT23b
, Corollary 6.15]
.

Hence the following follows immediately from
Corollary

2.13
:

Theorem 3.6

(Flows Represent Faces (with boundary))
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudo-Anosov flow with no perfect fits on a compact oriented 3-manifold
M
. Then
\mathrm{cone}_{1}^{\vee}(\varphi)
is equal to the cone over a face of the Thurston norm ball whose codimension is equal to the dimension of the largest linear subspace contained in
\mathrm{cone}_{1}(\varphi)
.

Moreover, this cone is the maximal domain on which
x
and
-e_{\varphi}
agree as functions
H^{2}(M)\to\mathbb{R}
.

We remark that the hypothesis that
\varphi
has no perfect fits allows us to choose
\tau
to be strict, so
M
admits no essential surfaces of nonnegative Euler characteristic by
Proposition

2.2
. Hence in the above theorem the Thurston norm for
M
is a norm and not merely a pseudonorm.

We also remark that, as in
[
Mos92b
]
, the hypothesis that
\varphi
has no perfect fits cannot be removed. See also the example in
Section

6.2
.

Finally, in
[
Mos92b
]
Mosher included the requirement that
\varphi
be quasigeodesic in the statement of his theorem, and used the quasigeodesic property in his proof. He wrote “It would also be nice to find a proof of Flows Represent Faces which makes no
use of the quasigeodesic hypothesis, for the reason that hyperbolic geometry seems completely extraneous to the purpose of the theorem.” In the time since, Fenley has shown that pseudo-Anosov flows with no perfect fits are quasigeodesic (see
[
Fen16
]
for the strongest statement). Note, however, that the proof we give above in the more general setting does not use quasigeodesity.

We begin with a general definiton:

Let

•

•

\alpha\pitchfork\partial D

\alpha
is compatible with the orientation of
\partial D\smallsetminus(p_{1}\cup\cdots\cup p_{n})
.

11

After the dynamic blowup associated to a system of arcs

5.
Almost transverse surfaces are relatively carried

In this section, we prove the converse to
Theorem

4.1

Together with
Theorem

4.1

Theorem 5.1

(Almost transverse
\iff

.

Let
\varphi
be a transitive pseudo-Anosov flow on
M
, possibly with boundary, and let
\tau
be any associated veering triangulation.
Then, up to isotopy, a surface

is almost transverse to

if and only if it is relatively carried by

.

First we need a lemma extending
[

, Section 2]

to the case of manifolds with boundary. We remark that our generalization is necessary even in the case where the manifold

5.2

Lemma 5.2
.

Any surface

\tau
is any associated veering triangulation,

x([S])=-e_{\tau}([S])

Proof.

To begin, let

6.
Transverse surface theorems

In this section, we prove several transverse surface theorems that realize surfaces as (almost) transverse to pseudo-Anosov flows. We then also use our Fried blowup technique to realize relative homology classes as Birkhoff surfaces (
Theorem

6.4
).

In
Section

6.2
, we illustrate the sharpness of our theorems with an example.

Essentially the same technique from the proof of
Theorem

5.1
(performing a Fried blowup to remove perfect fits) can be used to extend Mosher’s transverse surface theorem (for general pseudo-Anosov flows) to manifolds with boundary.

Theorem 6.1

(Transverse surface theorem with boundary)
.

Given a transitive pseudo-Anosov flow
\varphi
on
M
(possibly with boundary), any integral first cohomology class pairing nonnegatively with all closed orbits of
\varphi
is Poincaré dual to a surface almost transverse to
\varphi
.

Proof.

Let
\kappa
be any collection of regular orbits of
\varphi
that kill perfect fits; this exists by
Section

3.5.1
.
Following the approach of
Theorem

5.1
, perform a Fried blowup on all orbits of
\kappa
to obtain
M^{*}
,
\varphi^{*}
, and
\tau^{*}
. In this case,
\tau^{*}
is the associated veering triangulation of
M^{*}
since
\varphi^{*}
has no perfect fits.

Any class
\eta\in H^{1}(M)
that pairs nonnegatively with all closed orbits of
\varphi
pulls back to a class
\eta^{*}\in H^{1}(M^{*})
with the same property for
\varphi^{*}
.
Hence,
\eta^{*}
can be realized by a surface
S^{*}
in
M^{*}
that is carried by
\tau^{*}
by
[
LMT24
, Proposition 5.11]
. Since
\eta^{*}
is the pullback of the class
\eta
in
M
,
S^{*}
can be extends across
U
to a surface
S
representing
\eta
and relatively carried by
\tau
. Hence, after an isotopy
S
is almost transverse to
\varphi
by
Theorem

4.1
and the proof is complete.
∎

The point of our
strong
transverse surface theorems is to realize a surface in (almost) transverse position, up to isotopy (rather than just homology).
When the flow is without perfect fits, the strongest possible result is the following:

Theorem 6.2

(Strong transverse surface theorem)
.

Let
\varphi
be a pseudo-Anosov flow with no perfect fits on
M
, possibly with boundary. Let
S
be a taut surface pairing nonnegatively with all closed orbits of
\varphi
. Then
S
is almost transverse to
\varphi
up to isotopy.

Proof.

Since
\varphi
has no perfect fits, there is an associated veering triangulation
\tau
in
M\smallsetminus\Pi_{s}
.
By the results of
[
LMT23b
]
, the cone in
H_{1}(M)
generated by closed orbits is equal to
\mathrm{cone}_{1}(\Gamma)
, so
[S]\in\mathrm{cone}_{1}^{\vee}(\Gamma)
. By
Theorem

2.12
,
S
is carried by
\tau^{(2)}
up to isotopy, so is almost transverse to
\varphi
up to isotopy by
Theorem

4.1
.
∎

Combining the above arguments give the following extension to the general case.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\varphi
be a transitive pseudo-Anosov flow on
M
, and let
\kappa
be any finite collection of closed regular orbits of
\varphi
that kills perfect fits. Then an oriented surface
S
in
M
is isotopic to a surface that is almost transverse to
\varphi
if and only if

(1)

S
is taut,

(2)

S
has nonnegative (homological) intersection with each orbit of
\varphi
,
and

(3)

the algebraic and geometric intersection numbers of
S
with
\kappa
are equal.

Note that if
\varphi
has no perfect fits (e.g. when
\varphi
is circular), then we can take
\kappa=\emptyset
, and in this case the third condition is vacuous. Hence
Theorem

6.2
is a special case of
Theorem

6.3
.

Proof.

First, isotope
S
so that condition
3
holds. Using the previous method, we can Fried blowup
\varphi
at
\kappa
, just as in the proof of
Theorem

5.1
, to obtain a pseudo-Anosov flow
\varphi^{\sharp}
without perfect fits on
M^{\sharp}
and a properly embedded surface
S^{\sharp}
that blows down to
S
. From condition
(3)
, it follows that
S^{\sharp}
is still taut in
M^{\sharp}
. Indeed, the condition implies that the blowup boundary components of
S^{\sharp}
are oriented coherently on each blowup component of
\partial M^{\sharp}
. In particular, any homologous surface needs to have at least that number of boundary components along
\partial M^{\sharp}
, and so tautness of
S
implies tautness of
S^{\sharp}
.

Moreover, as in
Theorem

6.1
,
S^{\sharp}
has nonnegative intersection number with each orbit of
\varphi^{\sharp}
. Hence we can apply
Theorem

6.2
to conclude that
S^{\sharp}
is almost transverse to
\varphi^{\sharp}
after an isotopy. By blowing back down (so that each component of
\partial S^{\sharp}
on a blowdown torus is blown down to a point), this implies that
S
is almost transverse to
\varphi
, as required.
∎

6.1.
Realizing Birkhoff surfaces

Our technique of blowing up to obtain flows on manifolds with boundary has applications to
Birkhoff surfaces for transitive pseudo-Anosov flows. Recall from the introduction that a
Birkhoff surface

of
\varphi
is an immersed surface in
M
whose boundary
components cover closed orbits of
\varphi
and
whose interior is embedded transversely to
\varphi
.
We call the closed orbits of
\varphi
that are covered by the boundary components of a Birkhoff surface its
boundary orbits
. More generally, a Birkhoff surface of a dynamic blowup of
\varphi
will be called an
almost Birkhoff surface
of
\varphi
.
Finally, a
Birkhoff section
is a Birkhoff surface that meets every flow segment of some definite (uniform) length.

Let
\kappa
be a collection of closed orbits, possibly containing singular orbits. Let
U_{\kappa}
be a standard neighborhood of
\kappa
. Then using excision and Poincaré duality we make the identification

H_{2}(M,\kappa)=H^{1}(M\smallsetminus\kappa).

Meanwhile, if
\pi\colon M^{\sharp}\to M
is the Fried blowup of
\varphi
to
\varphi^{\sharp}
at
\kappa
, then
\pi
induces a homeomorphism from
M^{\sharp}\smallsetminus U^{\sharp}\to M\smallsetminus U_{\kappa}
, where
U^{\sharp}=\pi^{-1}(U_{\kappa})
is a standard neighborhood of the components of
\partial M^{\sharp}
that blow down to orbits in
\kappa
.

The next theorem generalizes Fried’s result on Birkhoff sections
[
Fri82
, Theorem N]
and seems new even for geodesic flow on hyperbolic surfaces.

Theorem 6.4

(Representing relative classes with Birkhoff surfaces)
.

Let
\varphi
be a transitive pseudo-Anosov flow and let
\kappa
be any collection of closed orbits. Then a class
\eta\in H_{2}(M,\kappa)=H^{1}(M\smallsetminus\kappa)
is represented by an almost Birkhoff surface in
M
if and only if it is nonnegative on closed orbits of
M\smallsetminus\kappa
.

Moreover, if each singular orbit of
\varphi
is either in
\kappa
or positive under
\eta
, then
\eta
is represented by a (honest) Birkhoff surface.

Note that the ‘moreover’ statement is automatic when
\varphi
is Anosov.

Proof.

Note that when
\kappa=\emptyset
, the result reduces to
Theorem

6.1
. We show that the general case also reduces to what we have already shown as follows:

As above, let
\pi\colon M^{\sharp}\to M
with
\varphi^{\sharp}
be the Fried blowup of
\varphi
with respect to
\kappa
.
The class
\eta
pulls back to
\eta^{\sharp}=\pi^{*}\eta\in H^{1}(M^{\sharp})
, and we claim
that
\eta^{\sharp}
is nonnegative on the closed orbits of
\varphi^{\sharp}
. This is obvious
for the closed orbits that are not contained in blown boundary components, i.e. do not
blow down to components of
\kappa
. If
\gamma
is a closed orbit in a blown boundary component, then we can approximate arbitrarily high multiples
\gamma^{k}
with a closed orbit
c_{k}
that is
not
contained a boundary component. More precisely, we can choose a periodic orbit
d
in
\intr(M)
such that
[c_{k}]=[\gamma^{k}]\cdot[d]
, where
d
is a fixed orbit. This can be accomplished using a Markov partition for the flow, or the flow graph of any associated veering triangulation (e.g.
[
LMT23b
]
). Since
\eta^{\sharp}(c_{k})=\eta(\pi(c_{k}))\geq 0
, we must have
\eta^{\sharp}(\gamma)\geq 0
as claimed.

Hence, we may apply
Theorem

6.1
to produce a surface
\Sigma^{\sharp}
representing
\eta^{\sharp}
that is almost transverse to
\varphi^{\sharp}
. Of course, if
\varphi^{\sharp}
has no interior singularities that are
\eta^{\sharp}
–null, then
\Sigma^{\sharp}
can be chosen to be honestly transverse to
\varphi^{\sharp}
. It only remains to position
\Sigma^{\sharp}
so that it blows down to a Birkhoff surface
\Sigma
in
M
. That is, each blowup boundary component
P
of
M^{\sharp}
is foliated by the fiber circles of

for

, and

needs to be arranged so that each of its boundary components is either one of these circles (in which case that component blows down to a point of transversality with

\varphi
). Given our local model for the blowup, it is not hard to see that this is possible. See

where this is done very carefully in the case of Birkhoff sections using essentially the same local model.
∎

Corollary 6.5
.

Let
\varphi
be a transitive pseudo-Anosov flow on a compact manifold
M
, and
\kappa
a
finite collection of closed orbits. Then there is an almost Birkhoff surface whose
boundary orbits are contained in
\kappa

if and only if the closed orbits of
\varphi
are contained in a closed half-space of

.

7.
Shadows of transverse surfaces

In
Section

8
we will show that almost transverse position is essentially unique. The key to this is understanding the projections to the orbit space of lifts of compact connected surfaces transverse to an almost pseudo-Anosov flow. This is the goal of the present section. In the case where the ambient manifold has no boundary, this was essentially done by Fenley in
[
Fen09
, Section 4]
.

First we set some notations and terminology.

7.1.
Notation and conventions

Let
\mathcal{O}^{s}
and
\mathcal{O}^{u}
denote the stable and unstable foliations, respectively, of the orbit space
\mathcal{O}
of an almost pseudo-Anosov flow
\psi
.
For

, let
\mathcal{O}^{s}(p)
denote the leaf of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
through
p
, and similarly for
\mathcal{O}^{u}(p)
. Recall that
\Theta\colon\widetilde{M}\to\mathcal{O}
is the projection to the flow space from the universal cover of
M
. If

, we set
\gamma(p):=\Theta^{-1}(p)
to be the orbit of
\widetilde{\psi}
corresponding to

.

A

of

is a component of
\mathcal{O}\!\bbslash\!(\mathcal{O}^{s}(p)\cup\mathcal{O}^{u}(p))
. Points in
\partial\mathcal{O}
, as well as those connected to
\partial\mathcal{O}
by

\mathcal{O}^{s}
or
\mathcal{O}^{u}
,
have infinitely many quadrants; all other points have
2n
quadrants for some

. Two quadrants are
adjacent
if they meet along a noncompact half-leaf.

A
stable
or
unstable slice leaf
is a properly embedded copy of
\mathbb{R}

contained in a leaf of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
or
\mathcal{O}^{u}
respectively. A
stable
or

unstable boundary slice ray
is a ray embedded in
\mathcal{O}
meeting
\partial\mathcal{O}

exactly at its basepoint, obtained as the union of finitely many blown segments with a noncompact half-leaf of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
or
\mathcal{O}^{u}
, respectively.

We observe that each component of
\partial\mathcal{O}
is a slice leaf of both
\mathcal{O}^{s}
and

\mathcal{O}^{u}
. Each slice leaf which is not an entire component of
\partial\mathcal{O}
separates
\mathcal{O}

into either
2
or
3
components. The
3
component case arises when a slice leaf meets
\partial\mathcal{O}
in finitely many, necessarily contiguous, blown segments.
Similarly, a boundary slice ray separates
\mathcal{O}
into
2

components.

Now suppose

is a properly embedded compact surface transverse to
\psi
. Lift
S
to
\widetilde{S}\subset\widetilde{M}
, and let
\Omega=\Theta(\widetilde{S})\subset\mathcal{O}
. (Here

stands for

Note that the restriction of
\Theta
to
\widetilde{S}
is a homeomorphism onto
\Omega
since
\widetilde{S}
separates
\widetilde{M}
and orbits intersect
\widetilde{S}
positively.
If
a
is a blown annulus of
\psi
and

is nonempty, then it is either

(1)
a collection of nonseparating arcs or
(2)
a collection of circles homotopic to the core of
a
. If
\widetilde{a}
is a lift of
a
to
\widetilde{M}
that intersects
\widetilde{S}
, we see that

is equal to the entire blown segment

in case

, and equal to

minus its endpoints in case

. If

is not contained in the boundary of

and its intersection with

is a (possibly empty) collection of arcs, then

can be collapsed to obtain a new almost pseudo-Anosov flow transverse to
A
with fewer blown annuli.

Hence for simplicity we assume that if

intersects a blown annulus in a collection of arcs,
then that annulus lives in
\partial M

has the

with respect to
\psi

Without the clean annulus property, the entire discussion in this section goes through with minor modifications.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let

be a compact, connected surface in an irreducible 3-manifold

\varphi

\widetilde{S}
be a lift of

\widetilde{\varphi}_{t}(b)

Proof.

We shall prove the forward statement; the backward one is similar.
Let
N
be an
\varepsilon

such that
N
can be identified with

where the

\widetilde{N}
be the lift of

\widetilde{S}

\widetilde{\varphi}_{t}(a)
and
\widetilde{\varphi}_{t}(b)

Proposition 7.7

\widetilde{S}

Let

\Omega

8.3.
Uniqueness of transverse position

Proof of
Theorem

8.2
.

Recall the setting: we have two isotopic surfaces
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
minimally transverse to a single almost pseudo-Anosov flow
\varphi^{\sharp}
.

We first wish to show that compatible lifts of any pair of isotopic components of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
to
\widetilde{M}
have the same projection to the orbit space
\mathcal{O}
of
\varphi^{\sharp}
; for notational simplicity we assume that
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are connected. Let
\widetilde{S}_{1}
and
\widetilde{S}_{2}
be compatible lifts to
\widetilde{M}
. By
Lemma

8.4
, their images contain the same regular points and intersect exactly the same quadrants at each blowup tree in
\mathcal{O}
. Hence they are equal.

Now we show that
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are isotopic along flow lines. The above implies
that compatible lifts of components of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are sections of the bundle of
lifted flow lines over their common shadow in
\mathcal{O}
. The difference of these sections is a

\pi_{1}(S_{i})
–invariant function,
and by adding
t\in[0,1]
times this function to one section, and projecting to

M
we obtain a homotopy through transverse surfaces between the components.

In fact, we can see that this homotopy is an isotopy as follows:
The preimage
q^{-1}(S_{i})
(where
q\colon\widetilde{M}\to M
is the covering map) divides

\widetilde{M}
into regions, and its coorientation induces an orientation on the dual tree
T_{i}
of
this subdivision. Because
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are isotopic, there is an isomorphism between
these oriented trees that preserves the labeling of the edges by conjugates of

\pi_{1}(S_{1})=\pi_{1}(S_{2})
in
\pi_{1}(M)
. Now a flow line
\ell
in
\widetilde{M}
, because it is
positively transverse to
S_{i}
, determines a subset in
T_{i}
which is either an oriented line, ray, or segment or a single vertex.
Because compatible
lifts of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
have the same shadows, the two lines are the same (up to the
isomorphism). In other words, the intersection points
\ell\cap q^{-1}(S_{1})

and
\ell\cap q^{-1}(S_{2})
appear in the same order along
\ell
, as labeled by
their corresponding subgroups (or shadows).
The linear interpolation given above must therefore preserve the embeddedness of the
intersection points, and hence is an isotopy.
∎

We begin with the following additional setup.
