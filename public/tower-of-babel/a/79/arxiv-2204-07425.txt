Finding Hall blockers by matrix scaling
Koyo Hayashi, Hiroshi Hirai, Keiya Sakabe

Abstract
For a given nonnegative matrix A = ( A i ​ j ) A=(A_{ij}) , the matrix scaling problem asks whether A A can be scaled to a doubly stochastic matrix D 1 ​ A ​ D 2 D_{1}AD_{2} for some positive diagonal matrices D 1 , D 2 D_{1},D_{2} . The Sinkhorn algorithm is a simple iterative algorithm, which repeats row-normalization A i ​ j ← A i ​ j / ∑ j A i ​ j A_{ij}\leftarrow A_{ij}/\sum_{j}A_{ij} and column-normalization A i ​ j ← A i ​ j / ∑ i A i ​ j A_{ij}\leftarrow A_{ij}/\sum_{i}A_{ij} alternatively. By this algorithm, A A converges to a doubly stochastic matrix in limit if and only if the bipartite graph associated with A A has a perfect matching. This property can decide the existence of a perfect matching in a given bipartite graph G G , which is identified with the 0 , 1 0,1 -matrix A G A_{G} . Linial, Samorodnitsky, and Wigderson showed that O ⁡ ( n 2 ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n^{2}\log n) iterations for A G A_{G} decide whether G G has a perfect matching. Here n n is the number of vertices in one of the color classes of G G . In this paper, we show an extension of this result: If G G has no perfect matching, then a polynomial number of the Sinkhorn iterations identifies a Hall blocker—a vertex subset X X having neighbors Γ ⁡ ( X ) \Gamma(X) with | X | > | Γ ⁡ ( X ) | |X|>|\Gamma(X)| , which is a certificate of the nonexistence of a perfect matching. Specifically, we show that O ⁡ ( n 2 ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n^{2}\log n) iterations can identify one Hall blocker, and that further polynomial iterations can also identify all parametric Hall blockers X X of maximizing ( 1 − λ ) ​ | X | − λ ​ | Γ ⁡ ( X ) | (1-\lambda)|X|-\lambda|\Gamma(X)| for λ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \lambda\in[0,1] . The former result is based on an interpretation of the Sinkhorn algorithm as alternating minimization for geometric programming. The latter is on an interpretation as alternating minimization for KL-divergence (Csiszár and Tusnády 1984, Gietl and Reffel 2013) and its limiting behavior for a nonscalable matrix (Aas 2014). We also relate the Sinkhorn limit with parametric network flow, principal partition of polymatroids, and the Dulmage-Mendelsohn decomposition of a bipartite graph.



Keywords: Matrix scaling, Sinkhorn algorithm, perfect matching, Hall blockers, alternating minimization, KL-divergence, polymatroids

A_{ij}\leftarrow A_{ij}/\sum_{i}A_{ij}

See Idel’s survey
[
22

By this algorithm,

This convergence property can decide whether a given bipartite graph

for

Linial, Samorodnitsky, and Wigderson

Although the time complexity of this algorithm is inferior

Also it is linked with the recent development on operator scaling, which is mentioned in the last of introduction.

In this paper, we address an extension of this result.

The first result is that

G
has no perfect matching.
The analysis is based on an interpretation of the Sinkhorn algorithm as

\inf_{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}>0}\prod_{i}\sum_{j}A_{ij}x_{j}/(x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{n})

Matrix scaling as geometric programming

In this section, we consider geometric programming formulation of matrix scaling, where
we assume
R=C
.
It is well-known (see
[
22
,
27
]
) that
the Sinkhorn algorithm is viewed as alternating minimization of
the following optimization problem:

{\rm inf.}\quad\kappa(x,y):=\frac{\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}x_{i}y_{j}}{\prod_{i}x_{i}^{r_{i}/R}\prod_{j}y_{j}^{c_{j}/C}}\quad{\rm s.t.}\quad x_{i},y_{j}>0\ (i\in[n],j\in[m]).

(2.2)

Fixing
y
, an optimal

is given by row-normalization vector
R(y)
.
Also, fixing
x
, an optimal
y
is given by column-normalization vector
C(x)
.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
27
]
)
.

(1)

A
is
(r,c)
-scalable if and only if
\inf_{x,y}\kappa(x,y)
is attained by some
x,y
.

(2)

A
is approximately
(r,c)
-scalable if and only if
\inf_{x,y}\kappa(x,y)>0
.

In the situation of (1),
an
(r,c)
-scaling is given by
(A_{ij}x_{i}y_{j})
.
In (2), an approximate
(r,c)
-scaling
(A_{ij}x_{i}y_{j})
is obtained by
near optimal solution

with a prescribed accuracy.

The following formula of the decrement of
\kappa

in row- and column-normalization is a variant of
[
2
, Lemma 2]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
y=C(x)
,
x^{\prime}:=R(y)
, and
y^{\prime}:=C(x^{\prime})
. Then it holds

\log\kappa(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})-\log\kappa(x,y)=-D\left(r\|p\right)/R-D(c\|q)/C,

(2.3)

where
p_{i}:=\sum_{j}A_{ij}x_{i}y_{j}
and

q_{j}:=\sum_{i}A_{ij}x^{\prime}_{i}y_{j}
.

Proof.

Note that

is written as
x^{\prime}_{i}=r_{i}x_{i}/p_{i}
.
By calculation, we have

\log\kappa(x^{\prime},y)-\log\kappa(x,y)=-D(r\|p)/R
.
Similarly, we have
\log\kappa(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})-\log\kappa(x^{\prime},y)=-D(c\|q)/C
.
∎

Unbounded certificate in geometric programming.

The problem (

) is transformed into convex optimization
by taking logarithm with variable change

\inf.\quad\log\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}e^{\xi_{i}+\eta_{j}}-r^{\top}\xi/R-c^{\top}\eta/C\quad{\rm s.t.}\quad(\xi,\eta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}.

(2.4)

This problem falls into the class of convex optimization,

A general form of unconstraint geometric program is:

Then,

We observe a simple criterion for

Lemma 2.5

(1)

(2)

Let

,

\omega_{k}^{\top}\xi<0
holds for every

.

Proof.

(2). If

for some

,
then

.

(1). The if part follows from
\lim_{t\to\infty}f(t\xi)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\log\sum_{k}q_{k}e^{t\omega_{k}^{\top}\xi}=-\infty
.
The only if part follows from (2).
∎

A geometric interpretation of (1) is explained as follows:
Observe that
\inf_{\xi}f(\xi)=f(\xi^{*})
if and only if
\nabla f(\xi^{*})=0
, that is,
the origin

belongs to
the
gradient space

\nabla f({\mathbb{R}}^{d}):=\{\nabla f(\xi)\mid\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\}
.
Further,
\inf_{\xi}f(\xi)>-\infty
is equivalent to

, the closure of the space.
On the other hand,
\overline{\nabla f({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
is written as
the convex hull of
\omega_{k}
over
k\in[m]
.
Thus,
\xi
in (1) represents a normal vector of a separating hyperplane
between

and
\overline{\nabla f({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
, which
is a certificate of unboundedness.
See also a recent work
[

for such an aspect
on unconstraint geometric problem.

Specializing Lemma

to (

(with

,

), we obtain

Corollary 2.6
.

Let

.
Then

is not approximately

-scalable if and only
if there are scaling vectors

For such

The closure

of the gradient space is the convex hull of
(e_{i}-r/R,e_{j}-c/C)

over

with

The condition of Theorem

the origin

satisfies all inequalities

Therefore, for a nonscalable matrix

One may expect that such

2.2
Matrix scaling as KL-divergence minimization

To study asymptotic behaviors of the Sinkhorn algorithm for nonscalable matrices,

In fact, as pointed out by Gietl and Reffel

to
establish the convergence.
In this section, following

We consider the Sinkhorn algorithm

Define a sequence

\displaystyle C(A),

{\bf 1}^{\top}\rho^{*}=C
of
P
.
By Lemma
3.2
(2), it holds
\log(R_{\kappa+1}/C_{\kappa+1})-\log(R_{\kappa}/C_{\kappa})>0
.
Thus, (
3.8
) says the KKT-condition that

-\nabla D(\rho^{*}\|r)
is
a nonnegative combination of
{\bf 1}_{X_{\kappa}}
and
\pm{\bf 1}
.
By strict convexity of
p\mapsto D(p\|r)
on
P
, it is a unique minimizer.
∎

Thus we have the following.

Theorem 3.4
.

\rho^{*}=p^{*}(=N^{*}{\bf 1})
.

In particular, it holds
{\bf 1}_{I_{\kappa}}^{\top}N^{*}{\bf 1}_{[m]\setminus Y_{\kappa-1}}=p^{*}(I_{\kappa})=C_{\kappa}={\bf 1}_{[n]\setminus X_{\kappa}}^{\top}N^{*}{\bf 1}_{J_{\kappa}}
.
This means that all off-diagonal blocks of
N^{*}
are zero,
i.e.,
N^{*}=\bigoplus_{\kappa}N^{*}[I_{\kappa},J_{\kappa}]
,
where the diagonal block
N^{*}[I_{\kappa},J_{\kappa}]
has marginal

((C_{\kappa}/R_{\kappa})r^{\kappa},c^{\kappa})
.
Accordingly,
M^{*}=R(N^{*})
is also a diagonal matrix
M^{*}=\bigoplus_{\kappa}M^{*}[I_{\kappa},J_{\kappa}]
such that

M^{*}[I_{\kappa},J_{\kappa}]
has marginal

(r^{\kappa},(R_{\kappa}/C_{\kappa})c^{\kappa})
.

Although the preceding argument is enough for our goal (Theorem
4.3
),
we continue to study the Sinkhorn limit
(M^{*},N^{*})
to give a final form (Theorem
3.6
).
Extreme stable sets are obtained via the following parametric
optimization for parameter
\lambda\in[0,1]
:

{\rm max}.\quad(1-\lambda)r(X)+\lambda c(Y)\quad{\rm s.t.}\quad X\sqcup Y\in{\cal S}.

(3.9)

This is a minimum
(s,t)
-cut problem on the network
\vec{G}(A,(1-\lambda)r,\lambda c)
.
Indeed,
X\sqcup Y
corresponds to
(s,t)
-cut

\{s\}\cup X\cup([m]\setminus Y)
with capacity
-(1-\lambda)r(X)-\lambda c(Y)+(1-\lambda)R+\lambda C
.
Particularly, (
3.9
) for all
\lambda
can be efficiently solved
by a parametric maximum flow algorithm, e.g.,
[
16
]
.

An extreme stable set is precisely
a unique maximizer of (
3.9
) for some
\lambda\in[0,1]
.
A parameter
\lambda
is said to be
critical
if a minimizer is not unique.
The number of critical parameters, i.e., the number of slopes in
\mathop{\rm Conv}{\cal S}
,
equals
\theta
.
Let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots\lambda_{\theta}
be critical parameters ordered as

0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots<\lambda_{\theta}<1
.
Let
{\cal S}_{\kappa}
denote the family of maximizers of
(
3.9
) for
\lambda=\lambda_{\kappa}
.
Then it is not difficult to see

•

X\sqcup Y,X^{\prime}\sqcup Y^{\prime}\in{\cal S}_{\kappa}\Longrightarrow(X\cup X^{\prime})\sqcup(Y\cap Y^{\prime}),(X\cap X^{\prime})\sqcup(Y\cup Y^{\prime})\in{\cal S}_{\kappa}
.

•

X_{\kappa-1}\sqcup Y_{\kappa-1},X_{\kappa}\sqcup Y_{\kappa}\in{\cal S}_{\kappa}
.

•

X\sqcup Y\in{\cal S}_{\kappa}\Longrightarrow X_{\kappa-1}\supseteq X\supseteq X_{\kappa},Y_{\kappa-1}\subseteq Y\subseteq Y_{\kappa}
.

Namely,
{\cal S}_{\kappa}
admits a distributive lattice structure,
where
(X\sqcup Y)\wedge(X^{\prime}\sqcup Y^{\prime}):=(X\cup X^{\prime})\sqcup(Y\cap Y^{\prime})

and
(X\sqcup Y)\vee(X^{\prime}\sqcup Y^{\prime}):=(X\cap X^{\prime})\sqcup(Y\cup Y^{\prime})
.
This is essentially a well-known fact that
the family of minimum
(s,t)
-cuts forms a distributive lattice.

Choose a maximal set (chain)
\{X_{\kappa,\alpha}\sqcup Y_{\kappa,\alpha}\}_{\alpha=0,1,2,\ldots,\alpha_{\kappa}}\subseteq{\cal S}_{\kappa}
with the property:

\displaystyle X_{\kappa-1}=X_{\kappa,0}\supset X_{\kappa,1}\supset\cdots\supset X_{\kappa,\alpha_{\kappa}}=X_{\kappa},

\displaystyle Y_{\kappa-1}=Y_{\kappa,0}\subset Y_{\kappa,1}\subset\cdots\subset Y_{\kappa,\alpha_{\kappa}}=Y_{\kappa}.

This is also efficiently obtained from the residual network of

\vec{G}(A,(1-\lambda_{\kappa})r,\lambda_{\kappa}c)
with respect to any maximum flow.
Accordingly, define
I_{\kappa,\alpha}
and
J_{\kappa,\beta}
by

I_{\kappa,\alpha}:=X_{\kappa,\alpha-1}/X_{\kappa,\alpha},\quad J_{\kappa,\alpha}:=Y_{\kappa,\alpha}/Y_{\kappa,\alpha-1}\quad(\alpha=1,2,\ldots,\alpha_{\kappa}).

(3.10)

Now
\{I_{\kappa,\alpha}\}_{\kappa,\alpha}
and
\{J_{\kappa,\alpha}\}_{\kappa,\alpha}

are partitions of
[n]
and
[m]
refining
\{I_{\kappa}\}_{\kappa}
and
\{J_{\kappa}\}_{\kappa}
, respectively.
Let
r^{\kappa,\alpha}
and
c^{\kappa,\alpha}
denote the restrictions of
r
and
c
to
I_{\kappa,\alpha}
and
J_{\kappa,\alpha}
, respectively.
Again, we arrange
A
so that

A[I_{\kappa,\alpha},J_{\kappa,\alpha}]
are diagonal blocks and their lower left blocks are zero. This is a finer block-triangularization of
A
.

The point
(r(X_{\kappa,\alpha}),c(Y_{\kappa,\alpha}))
lies on
the segment between extreme points

(r(X_{\kappa-1}),c(Y_{\kappa-1}))
and
(r(X_{\kappa}),c(Y_{\kappa}))
in
\mathop{\rm Conv}{\cal S}
.
By the maximality of the chain,
there is no stable set
X\sqcup Y
in
I_{\kappa,\alpha}\sqcup J_{\kappa,\alpha}

such that the stable set
(X_{\kappa,\alpha}\cup X)\sqcup(Y_{\kappa,\alpha-1}\cup Y)

is mapped to the segment between

(r(X_{\kappa,\alpha-1}),c(Y_{\kappa,\alpha-1}))
and
(r(X_{\kappa,\alpha}),c(Y_{\kappa,\alpha}))
.
This implies the condition of Theorem
2.1
(2):

Lemma 3.5
.

Each submatrix
A[I_{\kappa,\alpha},J_{\kappa,\alpha}]
is

(r^{\kappa,\alpha}/R_{\kappa},c^{\kappa,\alpha}/C_{\kappa})
-scalable.

Notice that
C_{\kappa}/R_{\kappa}=c(J_{\kappa,\alpha})/r(I_{\kappa,\alpha})
for each
\alpha
. Again, all off-diagonal blocks in the limit

M^{*}[I_{\kappa},J_{\kappa}]
must be zero, as in the argument after Theorem
3.4
.
Thus we have the following.

Theorem 3.6
.

The Sinkhorn limit
(M^{*},N^{*})
is given by

M^{*}=\bigoplus_{\kappa\in[\theta],\alpha\in[\alpha_{\kappa}]}R_{\kappa}B_{\kappa,\alpha},\quad N^{*}=\bigoplus_{\kappa\in[\theta],\alpha\in[\alpha_{\kappa}]}C_{\kappa}B_{\kappa,\alpha},

(3.11)

where
B_{\kappa,\alpha}
denotes the (unique)
(r^{\kappa,\alpha}/R_{\kappa},c^{\kappa,\alpha}/C_{\kappa})
-scaling of

.

Relation to principal partition and DM-decomposition.

The problem (
3.9
)
is a parametric submodular optimization, since it
is also written as minimization of

.
Such a parametric problem has been studied in a general framework of

of polymatroids
[

,
30
]
.
The above nested distributive lattice structure and the associated partition

In the case of

,
the block-triangularization of

obtained from the chain

is a refinement of

[

]
;
see
[
24
, Section 4.3]
and
[

, Section 2.2.3]
.
The DM-decomposition uses a chain of maximizers of (

)
only for

.
This refined DM-decomposition considering all parameters is due to

[

]
.

4
Finding Hall blockers by Sinkhorn iteration

Let
G=(U\sqcup V,E)
be a bipartite graph
with color classes
U,V
and edge set
E
.
We assume that there is no isolated node.
Suppose that
n:=|U|=|V|
.
The following is well-known:

(Hall’s theorem)

G
has a perfect matching if and only
if
|X|\leq|\Gamma(X)|
for all
X\subseteq[n]
.

(Kőnig-Egerváry theorem)

The maximum cardinality of a matching in
G

is equal to the minimum of
|U|-|X|+|\Gamma(X)|
over
X\subseteq U
.

Let
(r,c):=({\bf 1},{\bf 1})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Define
n\times n
matrix
A_{G}
by
(A_{G})_{ij}=1
if
ij\in E
and zero otherwise,
where rows and columns of
A_{G}

are indexed by
U
and
V
, respectively.
In this setting, the Hall condition for
G
is nothing but
the approximate scalability condition in Theorem
2.1
(1) for
A_{G}
with
(r,c):=({\bf 1},{\bf 1})
.

In this section, we show that a polynomial number of iterations of the Sinkhorn algorithm identifies a Hall blocker if
G
has no perfect matching (
A_{G}
is not scalable).
To make clarify the roles of parameters, instead of
A_{G}

we apply the presented algorithms to
a general nonnegative matrix
A
with
G(A)=G
.
In addition to
A_{\rm min}
in Corollary
2.6
,
define
A_{\rm max}

by the maximum of entries
A_{ij}
of
A

and
m
by the number of nonzero elements of
A
(the number of edges of
G
).

4.1
A Hall blocker from scaling vectors

Our first algorithm is based on
the geometric programming analysis in Section
2.1
.
Here the Sinkhorn iteration is described in update of scaling vectors
x,y
.

Algorithm: Sinkhorn & Sorting Scaling Vectors

0:

Let
x=y:={\bf 1}
, and let
y\leftarrow C(x)
.

1:

Repeat row- and column-normalization
\ell
times:

x\leftarrow R(y),\quad y\leftarrow C(x).

2:

Sort
x,y
as
x_{i_{1}}\geq x_{i_{2}}\geq\cdots\geq x_{i_{n}}
,

y_{j_{1}}\leq y_{j_{2}}\leq\cdots\leq y_{j_{n}}
.
Choose index
k^{*}
such that
(x_{i_{k^{*}}}y_{j_{k^{*}}})^{n}\geq x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n}y_{1}y_{2}\cdots y_{n}
.
Output
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k^{*}}\}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that
G
has no perfect matching.
For
\ell\geq(1/4)n^{2}\log mA_{\rm max}/A_{\rm min}
, the output is a Hall blocker.

The recovering procedure from
x,y
to a Hall blocker in step 2
is essentially the same as in
[
13
, Lemma 3.3]
.
The new point here is the iteration bound.
We remark that this bound (almost) matches the one by
[
2
,
7
]
for scalable case.
Namely,

O((n^{2}/\epsilon^{2})\log mA_{\rm max}/A_{\rm min})
iterations
find either a Hall blocker or an approximate
({\bf 1},{\bf 1})
-scaling with
\ell_{1}
-error
\epsilon\in(0,2)
.

Proof.

Notice
\kappa({\bf 1},{\bf 1})=\|A\|_{1}\leq mA_{\rm max}
.
We compute the decrement of
\kappa
in one iteration of step 1.
Let
x^{\prime}:=R(y)
,
y^{\prime}:=C(x^{\prime})
,
B:=(A_{ij}x_{i}y_{j})
, and
B^{\prime}:=(A_{ij}x^{\prime}_{i}y_{j})
.
By Lemmas
1.1
,
2.2
, and
2.4
with
B^{\top}{\bf 1}={\bf 1}
and
B^{\prime}{\bf 1}={\bf 1}
, we have

\displaystyle\log\kappa(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})-\log\kappa(x,y)=-D({\bf 1}\|B{\bf 1})/n-D({\bf 1}\|(B^{\prime})^{\top}{\bf 1})/n

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\left\{\|B{\bf 1}-{\bf 1}\|_{1}^{2}+\|(B^{\prime})^{\top}{\bf 1}-{\bf 1}\|_{1}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\left\{(\|B{\bf 1}-{\bf 1}\|_{1}+\|B^{\top}{\bf 1}-{\bf 1}\|_{1})^{2}+(\|B^{\prime}{\bf 1}-{\bf 1}\|_{1}+\|(B^{\prime})^{\top}{\bf 1}-{\bf 1}\|_{1})^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{4}{n^{2}}.

Therefore, after
\ell\geq(1/4)n^{2}\log mA_{\rm max}/A_{\rm min}
iterations, we have

\log\kappa(x,y)\leq\log\kappa({\bf 1},{\bf 1})-4\ell/n^{2}\leq\log mA_{\rm max}-\log mA_{\rm max}/A_{\rm min}\leq\log A_{\rm min}
.

By Corollary
2.6
,
it holds
x_{i}y_{j}<(x_{1}\cdots x_{n}y_{1}\cdots y_{n})^{1/n}
for each edge
ij
in
G
.
Therefore,
G
has no edge between
i_{k}
and
j_{\ell}

for
k\leq k^{*}
,
\ell\geq k^{*}
,
since
x_{i_{k}}y_{j_{\ell}}\geq x_{i_{k^{*}}}y_{j_{k^{*}}}\geq(x_{1}\cdots x_{n}y_{1}\cdots y_{n})^{1/n}
.
Thus
\Gamma(\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k^{*}}\})\subseteq\{j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k^{*}-1}\}
.
That is, the output
\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k^{*}}\}
is a Hall blocker.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The algorithm and its analysis can be adapted
for integer marginals
r,c
with
R=C
.
In the step 2, choose indices
k^{*},\ell^{*}
such that

\left(\sum_{k=1}^{k^{*}-1}r(i_{k}),\sum_{k=1}^{k^{*}}r(i_{k})\right)\cap\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell^{*}-1}c(j_{\ell}),\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell^{*}}c(j_{\ell})\right)\neq\emptyset,\quad(x_{i_{k^{*}}}y_{j_{\ell^{*}}})^{R}\geq\prod_{i}x_{i}^{r_{i}}\prod_{j}y_{j}^{c_{j}}.

Then
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k^{*}}\}
is a Hall blocker.
The number of iterations is
(1/4)R^{2}\log mA_{\rm max}/A_{\rm min}
.
We omit the details.

4.2
Extreme Hall blockers from marginals

Next we show that further iterations identify all extreme Hall blockers from marginal vector
p=A{\bf 1}
.
Here a subset

is called

if

is an extreme stable set in Section
3
.
If
G
has no perfect matching, then
extreme subsets other than trivial ones

are all Hall blockers.
Particularly, they include a Hall blocker
X
with maximum

and minimum (maximum)

.
The analysis is based on the KL-formulation
in Section
2.2
and Section

,
where the Sinkhorn iteration is performed in the matrix update formulation.

Algorithm: Sinkhorn & Sorting Marginals

0:

A\leftarrow C(A)
.

1:

Repeat row- and column-normalization
\ell
times:

A\leftarrow C(R(A)).

2:

Let
p:=A{\bf 1}
, and sort

as

.

3:

Output
U_{k}:=\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\}
for

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose that
G
has no perfect matching.
For
\ell>16n^{6}\log nA_{\rm max}/A_{\rm min}
, the output contains all extreme Hall blockers.

We start the proof.
According to (
2.6
),
define the sequence
(M_{k},N_{k})
with the initial point
N_{0}:=C(A)
.
Consider the family
\{X_{\kappa}\sqcup Y_{\kappa}\}_{\kappa=0,1,\ldots,\theta}
of extreme stable sets and the associated

(I_{1},I_{2},\ldots,I_{\theta})
and
(J_{1},J_{2},\ldots,J_{\theta})

in (
3.6
).
Then the limit

p^{*\kappa}=\frac{|J_{\kappa}|}{|I_{\kappa}|}{\bf 1}\quad(\kappa=1,2,\ldots,\theta).

Observe from

that sorting
p^{*}
recovers all
X_{\kappa}
.

In the step 2 of the algorithm, the matrix

equals

.
Let

.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
\|p^{*}-p\|_{\infty}<1/(2n^{2})
, then the output contains

for all
\kappa=0,1,2,\ldots,\theta
.

Proof.

If
p_{i}>p_{j}
holds for all
(i,j)\in I_{\kappa}\times I_{\lambda}
with
\kappa<\lambda
,
then the output contains all

.
Since

for

∎

Lemma 4.5
.

D(N^{*}\|M_{0})\leq 2n\log nA_{\rm max}/A_{\rm min}
.

Proof.

For
q_{j}:=\sum_{k}A_{kj}
and

\displaystyle D(N^{*}\|M_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(N^{*}\|R(C(A)))=\sum_{ij\in E}N^{*}_{ij}\log N^{*}_{ij}/(A_{ij}/p_{i}q_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{ij\in E}N^{*}_{ij}\log N^{*}_{ij}p_{i}+\sum_{ij\in E}N^{*}_{ij}\log\frac{\sum_{k}A_{kj}}{A_{ij}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n\log n+n\log nA_{\rm max}/A_{\rm min}\leq 2n\log nA_{\rm max}/A_{\rm min},

∎

∎

Proof of Lemma
2.7
.

(1). Let
p_{i}:=\sum_{j}N_{ij}
. For any

, we have

\displaystyle D(N\|M)=\sum_{i,j}N_{ij}\log N_{ij}/M_{ij}\geq\sum_{i,j}N_{ij}\log\sum_{j}N_{ij}/\sum_{j}M_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}N_{ij}\log p_{i}/r_{i}=\sum_{i,j}N_{ij}\log N_{ij}/R(N)_{ij}=D(N\|R(N)),

where we use the convexity of
D

and
R(N)_{ij}=(r_{i}/p_{i})N_{ij}
.

(2). Let
q_{j}:=\sum_{i}M_{ij}
. For any
N\in{\cal C}

\displaystyle D(N\|M)\geq\sum_{i,j}N_{ij}\log\sum_{i}N_{ij}/\sum_{i}M_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}c_{j}\log c_{j}/q_{j}=\sum_{i,j}C(M)_{ij}\log C(M)_{ij}/M_{ij}=D(C(M)\|M),

Proof of Proposition
2.8
.

The 5-point property is obtained by adding the 3-point and 4-point properties.
The 3-point property follows from

\displaystyle D(N\|M_{k-1})=D(N\|N_{k})+\sum_{i,j}N_{ij}\log\frac{(N_{k})_{ij}}{(M_{k-1})_{ij}}=D(N\|N_{k})+D(N_{k}\|M_{k-1}),

where the second equality follows from the fact that

(N_{k})_{ij}/(M_{k-1})_{ij}=((M_{k-1})^{\top}{\bf 1})_{j}/c_{j}
does not depends on
i
, and

\sum_{i}N_{ij}=\sum_{i}(N_{k})_{ij}=c_{j}
.

The 4-point property follows from

\displaystyle D(N\|M_{k})-D(N\|N_{k})=\sum_{i,j}N_{ij}\log\frac{(N_{k})_{ij}}{(M_{k})_{ij}}=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}N_{ij}\log\frac{(N_{k})_{ij}}{R(N_{k})_{ij}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}(N{\bf 1})_{i}\log\frac{(N_{k}{\bf 1})_{i}}{(M{\bf 1})_{i}}=D(N{\bf 1}\|M{\bf 1})-D(N{\bf 1}\|N_{k}{\bf 1})

\displaystyle\leq D(N{\bf 1}\|M{\bf 1})\leq D(N\|M),

where the final inequality is the log-sum inequality.
