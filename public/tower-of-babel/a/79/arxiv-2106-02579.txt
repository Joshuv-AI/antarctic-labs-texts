The Willmore flow with prescribed isoperimetric ratio
Fabian Rupp

Abstract
. We introduce a non-local L 2 L^{2} -gradient flow for the Willmore energy of immersed surfaces which preserves the isoperimetric ratio. For spherical initial data with energy below an explicit threshold, we show long-time existence and convergence to a Helfrich immersion. This is in sharp contrast to the locally constrained flow, where finite time singularities occur.

53E40 (primary), 35B40, 35K41 (secondary)

1.
Introduction and main results

Finding the shape which encloses the maximal volume among surfaces of prescribed area is certainly one of the oldest and yet most prominent problems in mathematics and goes back to the legend of the foundation of Carthage. Since then generations of mathematicians have been studying
isoperimetric problems
,
aiming to find the best possible shape in all kinds of settings. It turns out that — by the
isoperimetric inequality
— the optimal configuration in Euclidean space is given by a round sphere.

Likewise, the round spheres are the absolute minimizers for the
Willmore energy
, a functional measuring the bending of an immersed surface with various applications also beyond geometry, for instance in the study of biological membranes
[
6
,
13
]
, general relativity
[
12
]
, nonlinear elasticity
[
11
]
and image restoration
[
10
]
.

Note that the round spheres describe the optimal shape in both situations. In this article, we will study their relation using a gradient flow approach.

For an immersion
f\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
of a closed oriented surface
\Sigma
, its Willmore energy is defined by

(1.1)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}(f)\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Sigma}\lvert H\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.

Here
\mu=\mu_{f}
denotes the area measure induced by the pull-back of the Euclidean metric
g_{f}\vcentcolon=f^{*}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, and
H=H_{f}\vcentcolon=\langle\vec{H}_{f},\nu_{f}\rangle
denotes the (scalar) mean curvature with respect to
\nu=\nu_{f}\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
, the unique unit normal along
f
induced by the chosen orientation on
\Sigma
, see (
2.2
) below.
A related quantity is the
umbilic Willmore energy
, given by

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{0}(f)\vcentcolon=\int_{\Sigma}\lvert A^{0}\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu,

where
A^{0}
denotes the trace-free part of the second fundamental form. As a consequence of the Gauss–Bonnet theorem, these two energies are equivalent from a variational point of view, since for a surface with fixed genus
g
, we have

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{0}(f)=2{\mathcal{W}}(f)-8\pi+8\pi g.

Both energies are not only
geometric
, i.e. invariant under diffeomorphisms on
\Sigma
, but — remarkably — also
conformally invariant
, i.e. invariant with respect to smooth Möbius transformations of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. By
[
43
, Theorem 7.2.2]
, we have
\mathcal{W}(f)\geq 4\pi
with equality if and only if
\Sigma=\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
f\colon\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
parametrizes a round sphere.

The
isoperimetric ratio
of an immersion
f\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is defined as the quotient

(1.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(f)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=36\pi\frac{\CalV(f)^{2}}{\mathcal{A}(f)^{3}},\quad\text{where }

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(f)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\int_{\Sigma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\quad\text{and}\quad\CalV(f)\vcentcolon=-\frac{1}{3}\int_{\Sigma}\langle f,\nu\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu

denote the area and the algebraic volume enclosed by
f(\Sigma)
, respectively. Here,
the normalizing constant is chosen such that by the isoperimetric inequality we always have
\mathcal{I}(f)=\vcentcolon\sigma\in[0,1]
with
\sigma=1
if and only if
\Sigma=\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
f\colon\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
parametrizes a round sphere.
Critical points of the isoperimetric ratio — or equivalently, critical points of the volume functional with prescribed area — are precisely the
CMC-surfaces
, i.e. the surfaces with constant mean curvature, which form an important generalization of minimal surfaces and naturally arise in the modeling of soap bubbles.

The problem of minimizing the Willmore energy among all immersions of a genus
g
surface
\Sigma_{g}
with prescribed isoperimetric ratio, i.e. the minimization problem

(1.6)

\displaystyle\beta_{g}(\sigma)\vcentcolon=\inf\left\{\mathcal{W}(f)\mid f\colon\Sigma_{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\text{ immersion with }\mathcal{I}(f)=\sigma\right\},

naturally arises in mathematical biology in the
Canham–Helfrich model

[
6
,
13
]
with zero spontaneous curvature and
has already been studied mathematically in
[
38
,
17
,
33
]
.
While the genus zero case was solved in
[
38
]
, the results in
[
17
,
33
]
combined with recent findings in
[
37
]
and
[
19
]
show that the infimum in (
1.6
) is always attained for any
g\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\sigma\in(0,1)
; and satisfies
\beta_{g}(\sigma)<8\pi
.
The energy threshold
8\pi
also plays an important role in the analysis of the Willmore energy, since by the famous Li–Yau inequality
[
26
]
, any immersion
f
of a compact surface with
\mathcal{W}(f)<8\pi
has to be embedded.

A sufficiently smooth minimizer in (
1.6
) is a
Helfrich immersion
, i.e. a solution to the Euler–Lagrange equation

(1.7)

\displaystyle\Delta H+\lvert A^{0}\rvert^{2}H-\lambda_{1}H-\lambda_{2}=0\quad\text{ for some }\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R},

where
\Delta=\Delta_{g_{f}}
denotes the Laplace–Beltrami operator on
(\Sigma,g_{f})
.
In
[
30
]
, solutions to (
1.7
) with small umbilic Willmore energy have been classified, depending on the sign of the
Lagrange-multipliers

\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
.
We observe that for
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
fixed, (
1.7
) is also the Euler–Lagrange equation of the
Helfrich energy
given by

(1.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}(f)\vcentcolon=\mathcal{W}_{0}(f)+\lambda_{1}\mathcal{A}(f)+\lambda_{2}\CalV(f),

where the energy either penalizes or favors large area or volume, depending on the sign of
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
, respectively.

The
L^{2}
-gradient flow of the Willmore energy was introduced and studied by Kuwert and Schätzle in their seminal works
[
21
,
20
,
22
]
.
Their methods are very robust and allow to handle also other situations, such as the
surface diffusion flow

[
32
,
42
]
and the Willmore flow of
tori of revolution

[
9
]
. The locally constrained
Helfrich flow
, i.e. the
L^{2}
-gradient flow for the energy (
1.8
), and its asymptotic behavior have been studied in
[
31
,
4
]
, where it was shown that finite time singularities must occur below a certain energy threshold. However, this flow does not preserve the isoperimetric ratio.

The goal of this article is to discuss a dynamic version of the minimization problem (
1.6
). To this end, we introduce the
Willmore flow with prescribed isoperimetric ratio
, which decreases
\mathcal{W}
as fast as possible while keeping
\mathcal{I}(f)\equiv\mathcal{I}(f_{0})=\sigma
fixed. This yields the evolution equation

(1.9)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f

\displaystyle=\left[-\Delta H-\lvert A^{0}\rvert^{2}H+\lambda\left(\frac{3}{\mathcal{A}(f)}H-\frac{2}{\CalV(f)}\right)\right]\nu,

where the
Lagrange multiplier

\lambda\vcentcolon=\lambda(t)\vcentcolon=\lambda(f_{t})
depends on
f_{t}\vcentcolon=f(t,\cdot)
and is given by

(1.10)

\displaystyle\lambda(f)\vcentcolon=\frac{\int\Big(\Delta H+\lvert A^{0}\rvert^{2}H\Big)\Big(\frac{3}{\mathcal{A}(f)}H-\frac{2}{\CalV(f)}\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu}{\int\lvert\frac{3}{\mathcal{A}(f)}H-\frac{2}{\CalV(f)}\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu}.

In (
2.21
) below we will justify the particular choice of
\lambda
, which yields that
\mathcal{I}
is actually preserved along a solution of (
1.9
)–(
1.10
).

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\sigma\in(0,1),T>0
and let
\Sigma_{g}
denote a connected, oriented and closed surface with genus
g\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. A smooth family of immersions
f\colon[0,T)\times\Sigma_{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
satisfying (
1.9
) with
\lambda
as in (
1.10
) and
\mathcal{I}(f)\equiv\sigma
is called a
\sigma
-isoperimetric Willmore flow
with
initial datum

f_{0}\vcentcolon=f(0,\cdot)
.

Stationary solutions of the flow (
1.9
)–(
1.10
) are solutions to the Helfrich equation (
1.7
) for
\lambda_{1}=\frac{3}{\mathcal{A}(f)}\lambda
and
\lambda_{2}=-\frac{2}{\CalV(f)}\lambda
. Conversely, any Helfrich immersion is also a stationary solution to (
1.9
)–(
1.10
), see
Section

2.2
below.

However, as the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
defined in (
1.10
) depends on the solution, the isoperimetric flow (
1.9
) substantially differs from the
L^{2}
-gradient flow of the Helfrich energy (
1.8
), where the parameters
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are fixed numbers and chosen
a priori
. On the analytic side, the integral nature of the Lagrange multiplier makes the evolution equation (
1.9
) a
non-local
, quasilinear, degenerate parabolic PDE of 4
th
order. Also geometrically, the constraint
\mathcal{I}(f)\equiv\sigma
causes new difficulties, as we cannot control the area and the volume independently along the flow (as in
[
4
]
, for instance), but only the isoperimetric ratio
\mathcal{I}
.

The Willmore flow with a constraint on either the area or the enclosed volume has been studied in
[
16
]
and a recent article by the author
[
36
]
. However, the situation here is fundamentally different and several new challenges arise.

First, if only the area or the volume is prescribed (and nonzero), constrained critical points of the corresponding variational problem are in fact
Willmore immersions,
i.e. solutions of (
1.7
) with
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=0
, due to the scaling invariance of the Willmore energy. Although still an active field of research, the classification of these Willmore immersions is much better understood than that of general solutions of (1.4) and a crucial ingredient in classifying the blow-ups in
[
36
]
.
Second, in
[
36
]
the different scaling of the energy and constraint has been used to represent the Lagrange multiplier in a way that allows for good
a priori estimates.
This neat trick is clearly not available for the flow (
1.9
)–(
1.10
).
Third, unlike in
[
36
]
, the Lagrange multiplier has a much more complicated algebraic structure and cannot be treated as a lower order term.

These obstructions are the reason for a new energy threshold in the following main result on global existence and convergence.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f_{0}\colon\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a smooth immersion with
\mathcal{I}(f_{0})=\sigma\in(0,1)
and such that
\mathcal{W}(f_{0})\leq\min\left\{\frac{4\pi}{\sigma},8\pi\right\}
. Then there exists a unique
\sigma
-isoperimetric Willmore flow with initial datum
f_{0}
. This flow exists for all times and, as
t\to\infty
, it converges smoothly after reparametrization to a Helfrich immersion
f_{\infty}
with
\mathcal{I}(f_{\infty})=\sigma
solving (
1.7
) with
\lambda_{1}\neq 0
and
\lambda_{2}\neq 0
.

This shows a fundamentally different behavior of the isoperimetric Willmore flow and the Helfrich flow, where finite time singularities occur, cf.
[
4
,
31
]
. Consequently, despite its new analytic challenges, the introduction of the non-local Lagrange multiplier has a
regularizing
effect on the gradient flow, see also
[
15
]
for a related result for the mean curvature flow.

The
\frac{4\pi}{\sigma}
-threshold in
Section

1
is motivated by the following simple application of the triangle inequality in
L^{2}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu)
. With
\mathcal{I}(f)=\sigma
and (
1.4
), we have

(1.11)

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\left\lvert\frac{3}{\mathcal{A}(f)}H-\frac{2}{\CalV(f)}\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\frac{36}{\mathcal{A}(f)^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{\sigma}}-\sqrt{\mathcal{W}(f_{0})}\right)^{2}.

This estimate bounds the denominator in (
1.10
) from below if
\mathcal{W}(f_{0})<\frac{4\pi}{\sigma}
. Moreover, it allows to control the Lagrange multiplier in the crucial estimates by essentially lower order quantities, see
Section

4
.

We highlight that the assumption in
Section

1
is not an implicit smallness of the initial energy, cf.
[
20
,
42
]
, but the threshold is explicitly given, although very little is known about minimizers and critical points of (
1.6
). Moreover, as
\sigma\nearrow 1
, the interval of admissible initial energies in
Section

1
becomes arbitrarily small. This seems plausible, since if
\sigma=1
,
f_{0}
is a round sphere and the denominator in (
1.10
) vanishes. Thus, it is a priori unclear whether there exists an admissible immersion
f_{0}
in
Section

1
if
\sigma\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
— in fact, this is equivalent to the condition
\beta_{0}(\sigma)\leq\frac{4\pi}{\sigma}
. In
Section

7
below, we will prove
\beta_{0}(\sigma)<\frac{4\pi}{\sigma}
for
\sigma\in(0,1)
, which is asymptotically sharp as
\sigma\nearrow 1
, and consequently the existence of a suitable
f_{0}
follows.
We also point out that it is unknown if the energy threshold in
Section

1
is optimal as it is for the classical Willmore flow
[
3
,
9
]
.

The proof of
Section

1
is based on the methods developed by Kuwert–Schätzle for the Willmore flow
[
21
,
20
,
22
]
. Under a non-concentration assumption on the curvature, we use localized energy estimates to control the evolution, see
Section

3
below. However, as in
[
36
]
, these estimates depend on certain
L^{p}
-type bounds on
\lambda
. The key ingredient of this paper is that for locally small curvature and if the initial energy is below the threshold of
Section

1
, the Lagrange multiplier can be absorbed in the estimates, see
Section

4
, in particular
Sections

4
and
4
. This is an essential observation, which we can use to prove a lower bound on the lifespan and to construct a blow-up limit in the spirit of
[
20
]
, see
Section

5
. Using the control over the Lagrange multiplier in the energy regime of
Section

1
, we deduce a crucial
rigidity result:
either the blowup is a
compact
Helfrich immersion or a
Willmore immersion,
see
5.4
. In the first case, we conclude global existence and convergence by an argument based on the Łojasiewicz–Simon inequality in the spirit of
[
7
]
, combined with recent progress on this inequality in the presence of constraints
[
34
]
. Due to the rigidity of the blow-up, we can follow the inversion strategy in
[
22
]
relying on the classification of compact Willmore spheres
[
5
]
to exclude the second case.

This last step is also where we crucially make use of the assumption
\Sigma_{g}=\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
In the case of higher genus, a classification result for Willmore surfaces as in [5] is currently lacking. Even if such a classification were available, a precise comprehension of the behavior under inversion would be indispensable to extend the argument beyond the spherical case.
However, since the blow-up analysis is also available if
g\geq 1
, we establish the following remarkable dichotomy result.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\sigma\in(0,1)
, let
\Sigma
be a closed, oriented and connected surface and suppose that
f\colon[0,T)\times\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a maximal
\sigma
-isoperimetric Willmore flow such that
\mathcal{W}(f_{0})<\frac{4\pi}{\sigma}
. Then there exist
\hat{c}\in(0,1)
,
(t_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset[0,T),t_{j}\nearrow T,(r_{j})_{\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,\infty)
and
(x_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that the sequence of immersions

(1.12)

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{j}\vcentcolon=r_{j}^{-1}\left(f(t_{j}+r_{j}^{4}\hat{c},\cdot)-x_{j}\right)

converges, as
j\to\infty
, smoothly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
after reparametrization to a proper Helfrich immersion
\hat{f}\colon\hat{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
where
\hat{\Sigma}\neq\emptyset
is a complete surface without boundary. Moreover

(a)

if
\hat{\Sigma}
is compact, then
T=\infty
and, as
t\to\infty
, the flow
f
converges smoothly after reparametrization to a Helfrich immersion
f_{\infty}
as
t\to\infty
.

(b)

if
\hat{\Sigma}
is not compact, then
\hat{f}
is a Willmore immersion.

Hence, under the above assumptions, in the singular case (b) the influence of the (non-local) constraint vanishes after rescaling as
t\to\infty
and the purely local term in (
1.9
), coming from the Willmore functional, dominates.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we will briefly review the geometric and analytic background and prove some first properties of the flow (
1.9
), see also
[
23
]
for a more detailed discussion.

2.1.
Geometric and analytic background

In the following,
\Sigma_{g}
always denotes an abstract compact, connected and oriented surface of genus
g\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
without boundary.

An immersion
f\colon\Sigma_{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
induces the pullback metric
g_{f}=f^{\ast}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
\Sigma_{g}
, which in local coordinates is given by

(2.1)

\displaystyle g_{ij}\vcentcolon=\langle\partial_{i}f,\partial_{j}f\rangle,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the Euclidean metric. The chosen orientation on
\Sigma_{g}
determines a unique smooth unit normal field
\nu\colon\Sigma_{g}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
along
f
, which in local coordinates in the orientation is given by

(2.2)

\displaystyle\nu=\frac{\partial_{1}f\times\partial_{2}f}{\lvert\partial_{1}f\times\partial_{2}f\rvert}.

We will always work with this unit normal vector field.

The (scalar) second fundamental form of
f
is then given by
A_{ij}\vcentcolon=\langle\partial_{i}\partial_{j}f,\nu\rangle
and the mean curvature and the tracefree part of the second fundamental form are defined as

(2.3)

\displaystyle H\vcentcolon=g^{ij}A_{ij}\text{ and }A^{0}_{ij}\vcentcolon=A_{ij}-\frac{1}{2}{H}g_{ij},

where
g^{ij}\vcentcolon=\left(g_{ij}\right)^{-1}
.
Important relations are

(2.4)

\displaystyle\lvert A\rvert^{2}=\lvert A^{0}\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{2}H^{2}=2\lvert A^{0}\rvert^{2}+2K,

where
K
denotes the Gauss curvature.
Consequently, using (
1.3
), we find

(2.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\lvert A\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu={\mathcal{W}_{0}}(f)+2\mathcal{W}(f)=4{\mathcal{W}}(f)-8\pi+8\pi g.

The Levi-Civita connection
\nabla=\nabla_{f}
induced by the metric
g_{f}
extends uniquely to a connection on tensors, which we also denote by
\nabla
. For an orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
of the tangent space, the Codazzi–Mainardi equations yield

(2.6)

\displaystyle\nabla_{i}H

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{j}A)(e_{i},e_{j})=2(\nabla_{j}A^{0})(e_{i},e_{j}),

cf.
[
20
, (5)]
.

Clearly, potential singularities for the flow (
1.9
) occur if
\CalV(f)
becomes zero or if the denominator in (
1.10
) vanishes. Note that in the latter case
H\equiv const
, thus
f
is a constant mean curvature immersion.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\sigma\in(0,1)
and let
f\colon\Sigma_{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an immersion with
\mathcal{I}(f)=\sigma
. Then

(i)

\CalV(f)\neq 0
;

(ii)

if
g=0
, i.e.
\Sigma_{g}=\mathbb{S}^{2}
, or if
f
is an embedding, then
H\not\equiv const
. In particular, the denominator in (
1.10
) is nonzero.

Proof.

The first statement follows immediately from the definition of
\mathcal{I}
.
For (ii), we assume by contradiction that
H\equiv const
, so
f\colon\Sigma_{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an immersion with constant mean curvature. If
\Sigma_{g}=\mathbb{S}^{2}
, then
f
has to parametrize a round sphere by a result of Hopf
[
14
, Theorem 2.1, Chapter VI]
. In the second case,
f
has to parametrize a round sphere by the famous theorem of Aleksandrov
[
1
]
. In both cases this contradicts
\sigma\neq 1
.
∎

Despite its geometric degeneracy, (
1.9
) is still a parabolic equation. Thus, starting with a smooth non-singular initial datum, it is possible to prove the following short-time existence result in similar fashion as it is outlined in
[
35
, Chapter 4, Proposition 2.1]
, after observing that we can integrate by parts in (
1.10
) so that the numerator of the Lagrange-multiplier contains no second order derivatives of
A
any more.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
f_{0}\colon\Sigma_{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a smooth immersion with
H_{f_{0}}\not\equiv const
and
\mathcal{I}(f_{0})=\sigma\in(0,1)
. Then there exist
T\in(0,\infty]
and a unique, non-extendable
\sigma
-isoperimetric Willmore flow
f\colon[0,T)\times\Sigma_{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
with initial datum
f(0)\vcentcolon=f(0,\cdot)=f_{0}
.

If
\Sigma_{g}=\mathbb{S}^{2}
, assumption
H_{f_{0}}\not\equiv const
in
2.2
follows from
\sigma\in(0,1)
by
Section

2.1
(ii).

2.2.
Evolution of geometric quantities

In this subsection, we will briefly review the variations of the relevant geometric quantities and energies.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
36
, Lemma 2.3]
)
.

Let
f\colon[0,T)\times\Sigma_{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a smooth family of immersions with normal velocity
\partial_{t}f=\xi\nu
. For an orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
of the tangent space, the geometric quantities induced by
f
satisfy

(2.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu)

\displaystyle=-H\xi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}H

\displaystyle=\Delta\xi+|A|^{2}\xi,

(2.9)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}A)(e_{i},e_{j})

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}_{ij}\xi-A_{ik}A_{kj}\xi.

As a consequence, we have the following first variation identities, cf.
[
36
, Lemma 2.4]
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
f\colon\Sigma_{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an immersion and let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma_{g};\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Then we have

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{0}^{\prime}(f)\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varphi\color[rgb]{0,0,0}

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla\mathcal{W}_{0}(f),\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu)}=\int\left\langle(\Delta H+|A^{0}|^{2}H)\nu,\varphi\right\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu,

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(f)\varphi

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla\mathcal{A}(f),\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu)}=-\int\langle H\nu,\varphi\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu,

(2.13)

\displaystyle\CalV^{\prime}(f)\varphi

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla\CalV(f),\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu)}=-\int\langle\nu,\varphi\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{I}(f)>0
, we have

(2.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(f)\varphi

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla\mathcal{I}(f),\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu)}=\sigma\int\left\langle\frac{3}{\mathcal{A}(f)}H\nu-\frac{2}{\CalV(f)}\nu,\varphi\right\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{W}_{0},\mathcal{A}
and
\CalV
are invariant under orientation-preserving diffeomorphisms of
\Sigma_{g}

The scaling behavior of the energies yields the following important identities.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let

be an immersion. Then we have

\displaystyle\int\langle(\Delta H+\lvert A^{0}\rvert^{2}H)\nu,f\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=0,\;-\int\langle H\nu,f\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=2\mathcal{A}(f),\;-\int\langle\nu,f\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=3\CalV(f).

By the scaling invariance of the Willmore energy, we find

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\mathcal{W}_{0}(f),f\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu)}=\left.\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha}\right|_{\alpha=1}\mathcal{W}(\alpha f)=0,

so

yields the claim. For

and

∎

This yields that Helfrich immersions are precisely the stationary solutions of (

-isoperimetric Willmore flow.

It is not difficult to see that along a solution of (

\displaystyle\frac{C\lvert\lambda\rvert}{\lvert\CalV(f)\rvert}\left(\int\lvert A\rvert^{3}\gamma^{4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu+\Lambda\int\lvert A\rvert^{2}\gamma^{3}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right)

(3.38)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\varepsilon\int\lvert A\rvert^{6}\gamma^{4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu+C(\varepsilon)\frac{\lvert\lambda\rvert^{\frac{4}{3}}}{\lvert\CalV(f)\rvert^{\frac{4}{3}}}\int\lvert A\rvert^{2}\gamma^{4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu+C\Lambda^{4}\int_{[\gamma>0]}\lvert A\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.

Moreover, we have the estimate

C\Lambda^{2}\int\lvert A\rvert^{4}\gamma^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\varepsilon\int\lvert A\rvert^{6}\gamma^{4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu+C(\varepsilon)\Lambda^{4}\int_{[\gamma>0]}\lvert A\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu
using Young’s inequality. Combining this with (
3.34
),(
3.36
) and (
3.38
) and choosing
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, the claim follows.
∎

Assumption (
3.28
) means that the second fundamental form is small on the support of
\gamma
. Note that this will only be satisfied locally, since by (
2.5
) we always have
\int\lvert A\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\in[8\pi,4\mathcal{W}(f)-8\pi+8\pi g]
. We will now study the situation, where (
3.28
) is satisfied on all balls with a certain radius, yielding a control over the
concentration of the Willmore energy
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Following
[
22
]
we introduce the following notation.

Definition 3.4
.

For a smooth family of immersions
f\colon[0,T)\times\Sigma_{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
t\in[0,T)
,
r>0
, we define the
curvature concentration function

(3.39)

\displaystyle\kappa(t,r)\vcentcolon=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{B_{r}(x)}|A|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.

Here and in the rest of this article, we follow the notation of
[
21
]
, i.e. the integrals over balls
B_{r}(x)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
have to be understood over the preimages under
f_{t}
.

If
\Gamma>1
denotes the minimal number of balls of radius
1/2
necessary to cover
B_{1}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, then

(3.40)

\displaystyle\kappa(t,\rho)\leq\Gamma\cdot\kappa(t,\rho/2)\quad\text{for all }0\leq t<T.

We now prove an integrated form of
3.3
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
be as in
3.3
. There exist universal constants
\varepsilon_{1}\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}),c_{0},C>0
with the following property: Let
\sigma\in(0,1)
, let
f\colon[0,T)\times\Sigma_{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a
\sigma
-isoperimetric Willmore flow and let
\rho>0
be such that

(3.41)

\displaystyle\kappa(t,\rho)<\varepsilon_{1}\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,T).

Then for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
t\in[0,T)
we have

(3.42)

\displaystyle\left.\int_{B_{\rho/2}(x)}\lvert A\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right|_{t}+c_{0}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B_{\rho/2}(x)}\left(\lvert\nabla^{2}A\rvert^{2}+\lvert A\rvert^{2}\lvert\nabla A\rvert^{2}+\lvert A\rvert^{6}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

(3.43)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{\rho}(x)}\lvert A_{0}\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}+\frac{C(1+\sigma^{-2})}{\rho^{4}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B_{\rho}(x)}\lvert A\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau+C\int_{0}^{t}\frac{{\lambda}^{2}}{\mathcal{A}(f)^{2}}\int_{B_{\rho}(x)}\lvert A\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

Proof.

Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Let
\tilde{\gamma}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
be a cutoff function with
\chi_{B_{\rho/2}(x)}\leq\tilde{\gamma}\leq\chi_{B_{\rho}(x)}
,
\|D\tilde{\gamma}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{C}{\rho}
and
\|D^{2}\tilde{\gamma}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{C}{\rho^{2}}
. Therefore,
\gamma\vcentcolon=\tilde{\gamma}\circ f
is as in (
3.18
) with
\Lambda=\frac{C}{\rho}
. Moreover, if we take
\varepsilon_{1}>0
small enough, we have the estimate

(3.44)

\displaystyle\rho^{2}\leq C\mathcal{A}(f)

as a consequence of Simon’s monotonicity formula
[
40
]
, see also
[
36
, Lemma 4.1]
. Now, since we have
\lvert\CalV(f)\rvert^{\frac{4}{3}}=\left(\frac{\sigma}{36\pi}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\mathcal{A}(f)^{2}
by (
2.21
) and (
1.4
), we observe

(3.45)

\displaystyle\frac{\lvert\lambda\rvert^{\frac{4}{3}}}{\lvert\CalV(f)\rvert^{\frac{4}{3}}}=\frac{\left(36\pi\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}{\mathcal{A}(f)^{2}}\lvert\lambda\rvert^{\frac{4}{3}}\sigma^{-\frac{2}{3}}\leq\frac{C(\lambda^{2}+\sigma^{-2})}{\mathcal{A}(f)^{2}}\leq C\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mathcal{A}(f)^{2}}+\frac{\sigma^{-2}}{\rho^{4}}\right),

where we used Young’s inequality (with
p=\frac{3}{2}
and
q=3
). The statement then immediately follows by integrating
3.3
in time. ∎

Remark 3.6
.

If we directly integrate
3.3
, we have to deal with two terms involving
\lambda
, both of whose time integrals behave correctly under parabolic rescaling, cf.
Section

2.2
.
The estimate (
3.45
) above reveals that if (
3.41
) is satisfied, then it suffices to control merely the
\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mathcal{A}(f)^{2}}
-term, since

(3.46)

\displaystyle\frac{\lvert\lambda\rvert^{\frac{4}{3}}}{\lvert\CalV(f)\rvert^{\frac{4}{3}}}\leq C\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mathcal{A}(f)^{2}}+\frac{\sigma^{-2}}{\rho^{4}}\right).

For the blow-up construction in
Section

5
, we will need the following higher order estimates for the flow in the case of non-concentrated curvature, cf.
[
20
, Theorem 3.5]
,
[
36
, Proposition 3.5]
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\sigma\in(0,1)
and let
f\colon[0,T)\times\Sigma_{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a
\sigma
-isoperimetric Willmore flow. Suppose
\rho>0
is chosen such that
T\leq T^{*}\rho^{4}
for some
0<T^{*}<\infty
and

(3.47)

\displaystyle\kappa(t,\rho)\leq\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}\quad\text{for all }0\leq t<T,

where
\varepsilon_{1}>0
is as in
3.5
. Moreover, assume

(3.48)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mathcal{A}(f)^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq\bar{L}<\infty.

Then for all
t\in(0,T)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have the local estimates

(3.49)

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{m}A\|_{L^{2}(B_{\rho/8}(x))}

\displaystyle\leq C(m,T^{*},\bar{L},\sigma)\sqrt{\varepsilon}t^{-\frac{m}{4}},

(3.50)

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{m}A\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq C(m,T^{*},\bar{L},\sigma)\sqrt{\varepsilon}t^{-\frac{m+1}{4}},

and the global bounds

(3.51)

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{m}A\|_{L^{2}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu)}\leq C(m,T^{*},\bar{L},\sigma)t^{-\frac{m}{4}}\left(\int\lvert A_{0}\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

In contrast to
[
20
, Theorem 3.5]
and
[
36
, Proposition 3.5]
, we do not only prove local bounds, but also the global
L^{2}
-control (
3.51
). Note that the global
L^{2}
-norms could also be estimated by the
L^{\infty}
-bounds and the area. However, this is disadvantageous since the area cannot be controlled along the flow, and in fact is always expected to diverge in the blow-up process, cf.
Section

6
below. The necessity for the finer estimates leading to (
3.51
) is why we give full details on the proof here, even though the argument is very similar to
[
20
, Theorem 3.5]
.

Proof of
3.7
.

After parabolic rescaling, cf.
Section

2.2
, we may assume
\rho=1
. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and define
K(t)\vcentcolon=\int_{B_{1}(x)}\lvert A\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu
and
L(t)\vcentcolon=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mathcal{A}(f)^{2}}
.
Then, for all
t\in[0,T)
using that
K\leq\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
by (
3.47
), we deduce from
3.5

(3.52)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B_{1/2}(x)}\lvert\nabla^{2}A\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

(3.53)

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\int_{B_{1}(x)}\lvert A_{0}\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}+C(1+\sigma^{-2})\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B_{1}(x)}\lvert A\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau+C\int_{0}^{t}L\int_{B_{1}(x)}\lvert A\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

(3.54)

\displaystyle\quad\leq C(\sigma)\left(K(0)+\int_{0}^{t}(1+L)K\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right).

Moreover, as
K(t)\leq\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
by (
3.47
) we can interpolate by combining
[
20
, Lemma 2.8]
and
[
21
, Lemma 4.2]
to find

(3.55)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|A\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/4}(x))}^{4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\leq C(\sigma)\varepsilon_{1}\left(K(0)+\int_{0}^{t}(1+L)K\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\leq C(T^{*},\bar{L},\sigma),

where we used the assumptions (
3.47
), (
3.48
) and
T\leq T^{*}
in the last step.
Thus, defining
a(t)\vcentcolon=\|A\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/4}(x))}^{4}
and using
T\leq T^{*}
and (
3.48
), we have the estimate

(3.56)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(1+L+a)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\leq C(T^{*},\bar{L},\sigma).

Now, we pick
\tilde{\gamma}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with
\chi_{B_{1/8}(x)}\leq\tilde{\gamma}\leq\chi_{B_{1/4}(x)}
and
\gamma\vcentcolon=\tilde{\gamma}\circ f
. Note that (
3.18
) is satisfied with a universal
\Lambda>0
, which we do not keep track of. As in
[
20
, Theorem 3.5]
, we define Lipschitz cutoff functions in time via

\displaystyle\xi_{j}(t)\vcentcolon=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{for }t\leq(j-1)\frac{T}{m},\\
\frac{m}{T}\left(t-(j-1)\frac{T}{m}\right)&\text{for }(j-1)\frac{T}{m}\leq t\leq j\frac{T}{m},\\
1&\text{for }t\geq j\frac{T}{m},\end{array}\right.

where
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
0\leq j\leq m
. We also define
\xi_{-1}(t)\vcentcolon=0
and
\xi_{0}(t)\vcentcolon=1
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
if
m=0
. We note that
\xi_{m}(T)=1
and

(3.60)

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}{\xi}_{j}\leq\frac{m}{T}\xi_{j-1}\quad\text{for all }j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Furthermore, for
0\leq j\leq m
we define
E_{j}(t)\vcentcolon=\int|\nabla^{2j}A|^{2}\gamma^{4j+4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu
.
Then, by
B.3
, using
\gamma\leq 1
and (
3.46
) we have

(3.61)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}E_{j}(t)+\frac{1}{2}E_{j+1}(t)

\displaystyle\leq C(j,m,\sigma)\Big[\left(1+L(t)+a(t)\right)E_{j}(t)+\left(1+L(t)+a(t)\right)K(t)\Big].

Therefore, if we define
e_{j}\vcentcolon=\xi_{j}E_{j}
this implies using (
3.60
) and
\xi_{j}\leq 1

(3.62)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}e_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m}{T}\xi_{j-1}(t)E_{j}(t)+C(j,m,\sigma)\left(1+L(t)+a(t)\right)e_{j}(t)

(3.63)

\displaystyle\quad+C(j,m,\sigma)\left(1+L(t)+a(t)\right)K(t)-\frac{1}{2}\xi_{j}(t)E_{j+1}(t).

We now claim that for all
0\leq j\leq m
and
t\in(0,T)
we have

(3.64)

\displaystyle e_{j}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{j}E_{j+1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq\frac{C(j,m,T^{*},\bar{L},\sigma)}{T^{j}}\left(K(0)+K(t)+\int_{0}^{t}(1+L+a)K\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right).

We proceed by induction on
j
. For
j=0
we have
\xi_{0}\equiv 1
on
(0,T)
. Therefore, we clearly have
e_{0}=\int|A|^{2}\gamma^{4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\leq K
. Moreover, by (
3.54
) we find

\int_{0}^{t}E_{1}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\int_{0}^{t}\int|\nabla^{2}A|^{2}\gamma^{8}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq C(\sigma)\left(K(0)+\int_{0}^{t}(1+L)K\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right).

For
j\geq 1
, integrating (
3.63
) on
[0,t]
and using
e_{j}(0)=0
, we find

(3.65)

\displaystyle e_{j}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{j}E_{j+1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

(3.66)

\displaystyle\leq C(j,m,\sigma)\int_{0}^{t}\left(1+L+a\right)e_{j}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau+C(j,m,\sigma)\int_{0}^{t}\left(1+L+a\right)K\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau+\frac{m}{T}\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{j-1}E_{j}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

(3.67)

\displaystyle\leq C(j,m,T^{*},\bar{L},\sigma)\int_{0}^{t}\left(1+L+a\right)e_{j}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau+\frac{C(j,m,T^{*},\bar{L},\sigma)}{T^{j}}\left(K(0)+K(t)+\int_{0}^{t}(1+L+a)K\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right),

by the induction hypothesis and since
T\leq T^{*}
. Using Gronwall’s inequality and estimating the exponential term by (
3.56
), we find

(3.68)

\displaystyle e_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{j}E_{j+1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\frac{C(j,m,T^{*},\bar{L},\sigma)}{T^{j}}\left(K(0)+K(t)+\int_{0}^{t}(1+L+a)K\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right)

(3.69)

\displaystyle+\frac{C(j,m,T^{*},\bar{L},\sigma)}{T^{j}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(K(0)+K(\tau)+\int_{0}^{\tau}(1+L+a)K\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)(1+L(\tau)+a(\tau))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

The estimate (
3.64
) then follows by using (
3.56
) and estimating the double integral via

(3.70)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}(1+L+a)K\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)(1+L(\tau)+a(\tau))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\leq C(T^{*},\bar{L},\sigma)\int_{0}^{t}(1+L+a)K\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,

where we also used (
3.56
) once again. Now, for the local estimates, we evaluate (
3.64
) at
t=T,j=m
and use (
3.47
),
T\leq T^{*}
and (
3.56
). Recalling
K\leq\varepsilon
by (
3.47
), this yields

(3.71)

\displaystyle\left.\int\lvert\nabla^{2m}A\rvert^{2}\gamma^{4m+4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right|_{t=T}\leq\frac{C(m,T^{*},\bar{L},\sigma)}{T^{m}}\varepsilon.

For the global
L^{2}
-estimate, we observe that (
3.64
) is linear in
e_{j}
and
K
. Hence, as in
[
36
, Proposition 4.2]
, we can sum up the local bounds to get

(3.72)

\displaystyle\int\lvert\nabla^{2m}A\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu

(3.73)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(m,T^{*},\bar{L},\sigma)}{T^{m}}\left(\int\lvert A_{0}\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}+\int\lvert A\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{0}^{T}\left(1+L+a\right)\int\lvert A\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right)

(3.74)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(m,T^{*},\bar{L},\sigma)}{T^{m}}\int\lvert A_{0}\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{0},

where we used (
2.5
), (
2.22
) and (
3.56
).
After renaming
T
into

, (
3.71
) and (
3.74
) are precisely the desired

-estimate for even orders of derivatives.
Exactly as in
[
20
, Theorem 3.5]
, the local and global

-estimates for

follow by interpolation. The

-estimate can then be deduced as in

as well.
∎

4.
Controlling the Lagrange multiplier

In this section, we will provide some important estimates for the Lagrange multiplier under the assumption that the initial energy is not too large. In contrast to
[
36
]
, the crucial power of
\lambda
is not of lower order when compared to the left hand side of
3.3
. Nevertheless, a first immediate feature of the energy regime from
Section

1
is that we can uniformly bound the denominator of
\lambda
from below.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\sigma\in(0,1)
and let
f\colon[0,T)\times\Sigma_{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a
\sigma
-isoperimetric Willmore flow with
\mathcal{W}(f_{0})<\frac{4\pi}{\sigma}
. Then

(4.1)

\displaystyle\int\left\lvert\frac{3}{\mathcal{A}(f)}H-\frac{2}{\CalV(f)}\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\frac{36}{\mathcal{A}(f)^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{\sigma}}-\sqrt{\mathcal{W}(f_{0})}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

This follows from the reverse triangle inequality in
L^{2}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu)
, (
2.21
) and (
2.22
).
∎

While the scaling techniques from
[
36
, Lemma 4.3]
are not available here, we still get the following key estimate, which gives a control over
\lambda
by quantities which will be suitably integrable.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\sigma\in(0,1)
and let
f\colon[0,T)\times\Sigma_{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a
\sigma
-isoperimetric Willmore flow with
\mathcal{W}(f_{0})<\frac{4\pi}{\sigma}
. Then, we have

(4.2)

\displaystyle\lvert\lambda\rvert\leq\frac{\sqrt{\mathcal{A}(f)}}{6\left(\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{\sigma}}-\sqrt{\mathcal{W}(f)}\right)}\left\lvert\int\langle\partial_{t}f,\nu\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu+\int\lvert A^{0}\rvert^{2}H\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right\rvert.

Proof.

We test the evolution equation (
1.9
) with the normal
\nu
and integrate to obtain

(4.3)

\displaystyle\int\langle\partial_{t}f,\nu\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=-\int\lvert A^{0}\rvert^{2}H\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu+\left(\frac{3}{\mathcal{A}(f)}\int H\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu-\frac{2}{\CalV(f)}\mathcal{A}(f)\right)\lambda,

where we used the divergence theorem. We now estimate the prefactor of
\lambda
by

(4.4)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{3}{\mathcal{A}(f)}\int H\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu-\frac{2}{\CalV(f)}\mathcal{A}(f)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{\lvert\CalV(f)\rvert}\mathcal{A}(f)-\frac{3}{\mathcal{A}(f)}\left\lvert\int H\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right\rvert\geq\frac{6}{\sqrt{\mathcal{A}(f)}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{\sigma}}-\sqrt{\mathcal{W}(f)}\right),

using the fact that
\mathcal{I}(f)\equiv\sigma
by (
2.21
). By the assumption and (
2.22
) this is strictly positive and the claim follows.
∎

We remark that the existence of
f_{0}\colon\Sigma_{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
\mathcal{I}(f_{0})=\sigma\in(0,1)
satisfying the assumption
\mathcal{W}(f_{0})<\frac{4\pi}{\sigma}
is not yet known and — in general — not true. For the case
g=0
, this will follow from
Section

7
below. However, for tori we have
\mathcal{W}(f_{0})\geq 2\pi^{2}
by
[
29
]
, and hence
2\pi^{2}\leq\mathcal{W}(f_{0})<\frac{4\pi}{\sigma}
can only hold for
\sigma<\frac{2}{\pi}<1
. On the other hand, for
\sigma\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
and arbitrary genus, there exists
f_{0}
with
\mathcal{W}(f_{0})<\frac{4\pi}{\sigma}
since
\beta_{g}(\sigma)<8\pi
by
[
19
, Theorem 1.2]
.
We now use
Section

4
to deduce the time integrability (
3.48
) for
\lambda
in the case of small curvature concentration, which enables us to bound all derivatives of the second fundamental form by
3.7
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\sigma\in(0,1)
and let
f\colon[0,T)\times\Sigma_{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a
\sigma
-isoperimetric Willmore flow. Let
{\mathcal{W}}(f_{0})\leq K<\frac{4\pi}{\sigma}
and let
\rho>0
be such that

(4.5)

\displaystyle\kappa(t,\rho)\leq\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}\quad\text{ for all }t\in[0,T),

where
\varepsilon_{1}>0
is as in
3.5
. Then for all
\tau\in[0,T)
we have

(4.6)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mathcal{A}(f)^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{C}{\left(\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{\sigma}}-\sqrt{K}\right)^{4}}\left(\mathcal{W}(f_{0})-\mathcal{W}(f(\tau))+C(\sigma,g)\left(\frac{\tau^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\rho^{2}}+\frac{\tau}{\rho^{4}}\right)\right).

Note that by the invariance of the Willmore energy and the isoperimetric ratio, this estimate is preserved under parabolic rescaling, cf.
Section

2.2
.

Proof of
Section

4
.

By the assumption we get the local control from
3.5
. As in
[
36
, Proposition 4.2]
, we can sum up these local bounds to get the global estimate

(4.7)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\int\lvert A\rvert^{6}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C\int\lvert A_{0}\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}+\frac{C(1+\sigma^{-2})}{\rho^{4}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int|A|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+C\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mathcal{A}(f)^{2}}\int\lvert A\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

Now, by (
2.5
), the energy decay (
2.22
) and the assumption, we have

(4.8)

\displaystyle\int\lvert A\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\leq C(\sigma,g).

Thus, we obtain the estimate

(4.9)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\int\lvert A\rvert^{6}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq C\left(\sigma,g\right)\left(1+\frac{\tau}{\rho^{4}}+\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mathcal{A}(f)^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right).

By (
2.4
) we have
\lvert A^{0}\rvert^{2}\lvert H\rvert\leq C\lvert A\rvert^{3}
. Therefore, using

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mathcal{A}(f)^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{C}{b^{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\int\lvert\partial_{t}f\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{C(\sigma,g)}{\mathcal{A}(f)}\int\lvert A\rvert^{4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right),

by Cauchy–Schwarz and (

), where

.
For the first term in (

), by (

) and (

) we have

\int_{0}^{\tau}\int\lvert\partial_{t}f\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\mathcal{W}(f_{0})-\mathcal{W}(f(\tau)).

For the second term in (
4.10
), we use (

), Cauchy–Schwarz in time and space, (

\displaystyle\frac{C(\sigma,g)}{b^{2}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{1}{\mathcal{A}(f)}\int\lvert A\rvert^{4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(\sigma,g)\rho^{-2}}{b^{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\int\lvert A\rvert^{6}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\int\lvert A\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(4.12)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(\sigma,g)}{b^{2}}\frac{\tau^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\rho^{2}}\left(1+\frac{\tau^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\rho^{2}}+\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mathcal{A}(f)^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

(4.13)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(\delta,\sigma,g)}{b^{4}}\left(\frac{\tau^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\rho^{2}}+\frac{\tau}{\rho^{4}}\right)+\delta\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mathcal{A}(f)^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

for every

∎

5.
The blow-up and its properties

In this section, we will rescale an isoperimetric Willmore flow as we approach the maximal existence time to obtain a limit immersion. Analyzing the properties of this limit will be the keystone in proving our main result,

.

5.1.
A lower bound on the existence time

As in

(Lifespan bound for small energy gap)

5.2.

Lemma 5.3

Theorem 5.5

(Constrained Łojasiewicz–Simon inequality)
