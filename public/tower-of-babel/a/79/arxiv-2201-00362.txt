Giovanni Fantuzzi

Abstract
Motivated by the application of Lyapunov methods to partial differential equations (PDEs), we study functional inequalities of the form f ⁡ ( I 1 ​ ( u ) , … , I k ​ ( u ) ) ≥ 0 f(I_{1}(u),\ldots,I_{k}(u))\geq 0 where f f is a polynomial, u u is any function satisfying prescribed constraints, and I 1 ​ ( u ) , … , I k ​ ( u ) I_{1}(u),\ldots,I_{k}(u) are integral functionals whose integrands are polynomial in u u , its derivatives, and the integration variable. We show that such functional inequalities can be strengthened into sufficient polynomial inequalities, which in principle can be checked via semidefinite programming using standard techniques for polynomial optimization. These sufficient conditions can be used also to optimize functionals with affine dependence on tunable parameters whilst ensuring their nonnegativity. Our approach relies on a measure-theoretic lifting of the original functional inequality, which extends both a recent moment relaxation strategy for PDE analysis and a dual approach to inequalities for integral functionals.



keywords

Functional inequalities, moment relaxations, polynomial optimization, Lyapunov methods, partial differential equations, distributed parameter systems

1
Introduction

Lyapunov methods are a powerful tool to analyse dynamical systems governed by differential equations, wherein key system properties are inferred by constructing one or more functions of the system’s state (and, possibly, of time) subject to inequality constraints. For example, classical Lyapunov functions that are positive definite and decay along trajectories prove the stability of equilibrium states
(
Lyapunov 1992
;
Datko 1970
)
. Other types of Lyapunov-like functions, generically called
auxiliary functions
in this work, can bound the effect of external disturbances
(
Willems 1972
;
Dashkovskiy and Mironchenko 2013
;
Ahmadi et al. 2016
)
, certify safety
(
Prajna et al. 2007
;
Ahmadi et al. 2017
;
Miller et al. 2021
)
, approximate reachable sets and basins of attraction
(
Tan and Packard 2006
;
Korda et al. 2013
;
Henrion and Korda 2014
;
Valmorbida and Anderson 2017
)
,
estimate extreme behaviour
(
Chernyshenko et al. 2014
;
Fantuzzi et al. 2016
;
Fantuzzi and Goluskin 2020
;
Goluskin and Fantuzzi 2019
;
Tobasco et al. 2018
;
Korda et al. 2021
;
Goluskin 2020
)
, and solve optimal control problems
(
Lasserre et al. 2008
;
Henrion et al. 2008
;
Korda et al. 2018
)
.

For systems governed by ordinary differential equations (ODEs) with polynomial right-hand side, polynomial auxiliary functions can often be constructed via semidefinite programming. This is because the constraints on polynomial auxiliary functions reduce to polynomial inequalities imposed on the entire state space or on a subset thereof. While usually intractable, such inequalities can be strengthened by insisting that all polynomials required to be nonnegative admit a decomposition into sums of squares (SOS), which can be searched for by solving semidefinite programs (SDPs)
(
Laurent 2009
;
Parrilo 2013
;
Lasserre 2015
)
. Given enough computational resources to solve these SDPs, and barring issues with poor numerical conditioning, Lyapunov methods can therefore be applied to polynomial ODEs in a systematic way.

The use of Lyapunov methods to study dynamical systems governed by partial differential equations (PDEs), instead, remains a challenge for two reasons. One is that there is considerable freedom in the structure of auxiliary functions (more precisely, functionals) for PDEs, and it is not clear how to generalise the notion of polynomial auxiliary functions for ODEs. The second reason is that verifying if a candidate auxiliary functional satisfies all relevant constraints—such as the Lyapunov conditions for nonlinear stability—requires checking functional inequalities over infinite-dimensional function spaces. This is usually hard to do, even with computer assistance.

Valmorbida et al. 2016
;
Valmorbida et al. 2015
and
Ahmadi et al. 2016
;
Ahmadi et al. 2017
;
Ahmadi et al. 2019
demonstrated that SOS polynomials and SDPs can be used to verify the nonnegativity of integral functionals with polynomial integrands in one or two spatial dimensions. This is achieved without discretization of the PDE state, but rather by requiring the polynomial integrand to be nonnegative pointwise after augmenting it by terms that integrate to zero. This approach, generalized to arbitrary spatial dimensions by
Chernyavsky et al. 2021
, enables one to search for auxiliary functions in the form of
integrals of polynomials
of the PDE state. A dual formulation of the same strategy, based on so-called
occupation measures
, was independently introduced by
Korda et al. 2018
to relax optimal PDE control problems into SDPs.

Unfortunately, auxiliary functionals for PDEs that can be represented as integrals of polynomials may not be sufficiently general. For example, Lyapunov analysis of fluid flows
(
Goulart and Chernyshenko 2012
;
Huang et al. 2015
;
Goluskin and Fantuzzi 2019
;
Fuentes et al. 2019
)
required functionals of the form

\mathcal{V}(u)=V(\mathcal{I}_{1}(u),\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{k}(u)),

(1)

where
V
is a
k
-variate polynomial and
\mathcal{I}_{1}(u),\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{k}(u)
are fixed integral functionals of the fluid’s velocity
u
. Such auxiliary functionals were optimized by projecting the governing PDEs onto a finite-dimensional ODE and estimating the projection error.

Motivated by the desire to avoid application-specific estimates, here we study the fundamental problem of verifying if functionals in the form (
1
) are nonnegative for all
u
satisfying prescribed constraints such as boundary conditions. Leveraging the occupation measure framework of
Korda et al. 2018
, we show that the broad class of functional inequalities obtained when (
1
) is a
polynomial of integrals
can be strengthened into sufficient polynomial inequalities over finite-dimensional sets specified by linear constraints and linear matrix inequalities. These purely algebraic problems can be further strengthened into SDPs using standard techniques for polynomial optimization, so one can attempt to verify hard functional inequalities using algorithms for convex programming. In principle, therefore, one can implement Lyapunov methods for PDEs with no need for rigorous ODE projections. Doing this in practice, of course, requires overcoming well-known challenges related to the numerical solution of SDPs, which may be too large or too ill-conditioned for currently available general-purpose solvers. We do not address such implementation issues here and leave them to future work. Nevertheless, we demonstrate that our approach to verifying functional inequalities yields sharp results on two relatively simple examples.

2
A class of functional inequalities

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open bounded domain with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
. Given an integer
p
with
1<p<\infty
, let
W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
be the Sobolev space of
p
-integrable functions
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
p
-integrable gradients. The
m\times n
Jacobian matrix
\nabla u
has entries
(\nabla u)_{ij}=\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}
.

Given an integer
d
, let
\mathcal{S}
be a subset of the multi-index set

\displaystyle\!\mathcal{E}_{d,p}:=\{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}^{m}\times\mathbb{N}^{m\times n}\!:

\displaystyle\left|\alpha\right|\leq d,

\displaystyle\left|\beta\right|+\left|\gamma\right|\leq p\}.

(2)

Consider a functional
\mathcal{F}
on
W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
defined as

\mathcal{F}(u):=f\bigg(\left\{\int_{\Omega}x^{\alpha}u(x)^{\beta}\nabla u(x)^{\gamma}\,\,{\rm d}{x}\right\}_{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in\mathcal{S}}\bigg),

(3)

where
f
is a function in
\left|\mathcal{S}\right|
variables. Here and throughout the paper,
x^{\alpha}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
is the multivariate monomial with variables
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, exponent
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, and degree
\left|\alpha\right|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}
. The multivariate monomials
u^{\beta}
and
\nabla u^{\gamma}
are defined similarly. The bound
\left|\beta\right|+\left|\gamma\right|\leq p
in (
2
) ensures that the integrals in (
3
) are well-defined.

We are interested in the following fundamental problems.

Problem 1

(Feasibility)

Check if
\mathcal{F}(u)
in (
3
) is nonnegative for all functions
u
in the subset
\mathcal{U}
of
W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
defined by the differential and boundary conditions

\displaystyle a\left({x},{u}({x}),\nabla{u}({x})\right)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }\Omega,

(4a)

\displaystyle b\left({x},{u}({x})\right)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(4b)

where

a:\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}
and

b:\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}

are given continuous functions that do not grow faster than a degree-
p
polynomial in the second and third arguments.

Problem 2

(Optimization)

Suppose
f
in (
3
) depends affinely on a vector of parameters
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
and let
\mathcal{U}
be as in Problem
1
. Given a convex function
b:\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}
, solve the convex optimization problem

\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}}\,b(\lambda):\;\mathcal{F}(u)\geq 0\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{U}.

(5)

Our approach to these problems is to derive sufficient but more tractable conditions for the functional inequality
\mathcal{F}(u)\geq 0
. We do so in three steps. First, we lift the functional inequality into an equivalent inequality on so-called
occupation
and
boundary measures

(
Korda et al. 2018
)
. We then strengthen the latter by enforcing it over a larger convex set of measures, obtaining a measure-theoretic condition whose validity suffices to conclude that
\mathcal{F}(u)\geq 0
on
\mathcal{U}
. Finally, for problems with polynomial data (cf. §
4
) we replace this measure-theoretic condition with a sufficient constrained polynomial inequality that, at least in principle, can be tested computationally. This polynomial inequality depends affinely on
\lambda
when so does
\mathcal{F}
, so it can be used to strengthen the constraint in (
5
) and compute a feasible (and, sometimes, near-optimal)
\lambda
.

Remark 3

Our results immediately extend to functions
f
in (
3
) that depend also on boundary integrals. Moreover, it is straightforward to consider function sets
\mathcal{U}
whose definitions, in addition to the equalities in (
4
), include differential and boundary inequalities, equality or inequality constraints on integrals over the domain or the boundary, and multiple constraints of each type. Finally, only minor modifications are needed to
consider domains
\Omega
whose boundary is only piecewise-smooth and Lipschitz.

Remark 4

If the function
f
in (
3
) is linear, then
\mathcal{F}
is an integral functional and may be rewritten as

\mathcal{F}(u)=\int_{\Omega}\phi({x},{u}(x),\nabla{u}(x))\,\,{\rm d}{x}

for some polynomial
\phi
. In such cases, a duality argument
(
Fantuzzi 2019
)
shows that our sufficient conditions for the functional inequality in Problem
1
are equivalent to those proposed by
Valmorbida et al. 2016
;
Valmorbida et al. 2015
,
Ahmadi et al. 2016
;
Ahmadi et al. 2017
;
Ahmadi et al. 2019
, and
Chernyavsky et al. 2021
.

3
Lifting via occupation measures

This section describes the first two steps of the strategy outlined above. Occupation and boundary measures are briefly reviewed in §
3.1
, while sufficient measure-theoretic conditions for the inequality
\mathcal{F}(u)\geq 0
on
\mathcal{U}
are derived in §
3.2
. To lighten the notation, we will often write
\langle f,\mu\rangle
to indicate the integral of a function
f
from a set
\Theta
into
\mathbb{R}
against a measure
\mu
supported on
\Theta
, i.e.,

\langle f,\mu\rangle:=\int_{\Theta}f(\vartheta)\,{\rm d}\mu.

3.1
Occupation and boundary measures

Given any function
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
, let
\mu_{u}
be the pushforward of the Lebesgue measure on
\Omega
through the map
x\mapsto(x,u(x),\nabla u(x))
. By construction,
\mu_{u}
is supported on the product set

D:=\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

(6)

and satisfies

\int_{D}\eta(x,y,Z)\,{\rm d}\mu_{u}=\int_{\Omega}\eta(x,u(x),\nabla u(x))\,\,{\rm d}{x}

(7)

for all continuous functions
\eta:D\to\mathbb{R}
that grow no faster than a degree-
p
polynomial in the last two arguments (so the right-hand side is well-defined). Following
Korda et al. 2018
, we call
\mu_{u}
the
occupation measure
generated by
u
.
In complete analogy, the
boundary measure

\nu_{u}
generated by
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
is the pushforward of the surface measure on
\partial\Omega
through the map
x\mapsto(x,u(x))
. By construction,
\nu_{u}
is supported on the product set

B:=\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}

(8)

and satisfies

\int_{B}\zeta(x,y)\,{\rm d}\nu_{u}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\zeta(x,u(x))\,{\rm d}S

(9)

for all continuous functions
\zeta:B\to\mathbb{R}
that grow no faster than a degree-
p
polynomial in the second argument.

The occupation and boundary measures
\mu_{u}
and
\nu_{u}
are related via identities implied by the divergence theorem. Let
\Phi
be the linear space of continuously differentiable
n
-valued functions
\varphi:\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that, given any
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
, the function
x\mapsto\varphi(x,u(x))
is differentiable using the chain rule and has an integrable derivative. To every
\varphi\in\Phi
we associate the “total divergence” function

\mathcal{D}\varphi(x,y,Z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg[\frac{\partial\varphi_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}(x,y)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}Z_{ji}\frac{\partial\varphi_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}(x,y)\bigg]

from
\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
into
\mathbb{R}
. This definition ensures that
\mathcal{D}\varphi(x,u(x),\nabla u(x))
is the divergence of
\varphi(x,u(x))
when the latter is calculated using the chain rule. With this notation, it is straightforward to combine the divergence theorem with identities (
7
) and (
9
) to conclude the following result. (See also Theorem 1 in
Korda et al. 2018
.)

Lemma 5

Let
\mu_{u}
and
\nu_{u}
be the occupation and boundary measures generated by a function
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
, and let
\hat{n}(x)
be the outwards unit normal vector to
\partial\Omega
. Then,

\langle\mathcal{D}\varphi,\mu_{u}\rangle=\langle\varphi\cdot\hat{n},\nu_{u}\rangle\quad\forall\varphi\in\Phi.

(10)

3.2
Measure-theoretic strengthening of Problem
1

Consider Problem
1
, which asks to verify if
\mathcal{F}(u)\geq 0
for all functions
u
in the set
\mathcal{U}
defined by the differential and boundary constraints in (
4
). Since identity (
7
) implies that

\mathcal{F}(u)=f(\{\langle x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}Z^{\gamma},\mu_{u}\rangle\}_{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in\mathcal{S}}),

it is natural to extend
\mathcal{F}
into a functional on a suitable space of measures, and then reformulate the functional inequality
\mathcal{F}(u)\geq 0
as a constraint on occupation measures.

To do this, let
\mathcal{M}_{+}(D)
and
\mathcal{M}_{+}(B)
be the cones of Borel measures supported on the sets
D
and
B
defined in (
6
) and (
8
), respectively. The functional

\mathcal{G}(\mu,\nu)=f(\{\langle x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}Z^{\gamma},\mu\rangle\}_{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in\mathcal{S}})

(11)

from
\mathcal{M}_{+}(D)\times\mathcal{M}_{+}(B)
into
\mathbb{R}
clearly satisfies
\mathcal{G}(\mu_{u},\nu_{u})=\mathcal{F}(u)
whenever
(\mu_{u},\nu_{u})
is a pair of occupation and boundary measures generated by a function
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
. Problem
1
may therefore be restated as follows.

Problem 6

Check if the functional
\mathcal{G}(\mu,\nu)
in (
11
) is nonnegative when
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(D)\times\mathcal{M}_{+}(B)
is a pair of occupation and boundary measures generated by
u\in\mathcal{U}
.

Remark 7

The apparently redundant dependence of
\mathcal{G}
on
\nu
is useful in what follows to exploit the relations between occupation and boundary measures given by Lemma
5
. Moreover, it enables us to easily extend our approach to functionals
\mathcal{F}(u)
that depend also on the surface integrals
\int_{\partial\Omega}x^{\alpha}u(x)^{\beta}\,{\rm d}S
with
\left|\alpha\right|\leq d
and
\left|\beta\right|\leq p
, which can be rewritten as
\langle x^{\alpha}y^{\beta},\nu_{u}\rangle
using (
9
).

Problem
6
is just as hard as verifying that
\mathcal{F}(u)\geq 0
on the function set
\mathcal{U}
. However, our measure-theoretic reformulation suggests that to check this functional inequality it is enough to verify that
\mathcal{G}
is nonnegative on a subset
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{+}(D)\times\mathcal{M}_{+}(B)
that includes all occupation and boundary measures generated by
u\in\mathcal{U}
. For suitably chosen
\mathcal{A}
, this sufficient measure-theoretic condition can be more tractable that the original functional inequality.

One particularly convenient strategy is to define
\mathcal{A}
using linear constraints necessarily satisfied by occupation and boundary measures generated by
u\in\mathcal{U}
. Lemma
5
already provides an uncountable number of such constraints. Additional ones can be derived from identities (
7
) and (
9
), and from the differential and boundary conditions (
4a
) and (
4b
) imposed on
u\in\mathcal{U}
. Specifically, let
a
and
b
be the functions appearing in (
4
). Further, let
\Psi_{a}
and
\Psi_{b}
be the sets of continuous functions
\psi:D\to\mathbb{R}
and
\rho:B\to\mathbb{R}
such that the functions
(\psi a)(x,y,Z)
and
(\rho b)(x,y)
grow no faster than a degree-
p
polynomial in
y
and
Z
. Then,

Lemma 8

Every pair of occupation and boundary measures generated by
u\in\mathcal{U}
belongs to the set
\mathcal{A}
of pairs
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(D)\times\mathcal{M}_{+}(B)
satisfying

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{D}\varphi,\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\varphi\cdot\hat{n},\nu\rangle

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in\Phi,

(12a)

\displaystyle\langle\eta,\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\textstyle\int_{\Omega}\eta(x)\,\,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle\forall\eta\in C(\overline{\Omega}),

(12b)

\displaystyle\langle\zeta,\nu\rangle

\displaystyle=\textstyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\zeta(x)\,{\rm d}S

\displaystyle\forall\zeta\in C(\partial\Omega),

(12c)

\displaystyle\langle\psi a,\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall\psi\in\Psi_{a},

(12d)

\displaystyle\langle\rho b,\nu\rangle

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall\rho\in\Psi_{b}.

(12e)

{pf}

If
\mu
and
\nu
are occupation and boundary measures generated by
u\in\mathcal{U}
, condition (
12a
) is just a restatement of Lemma
5
. Conditions (
12b
) and (
12c
) follow directly from identities (
7
) and (
9
). Condition (
12d
) holds because

\langle\psi a,\mu\rangle=\int_{D}\psi(x,y,Z)a(x,y,Z)\,{\rm d}\mu\\
=\int_{\Omega}\psi(x,u,\nabla u)\,a(x,u,\nabla u)\,\,{\rm d}{x}=0,

where the last two equalities are a consequence of (
7
) and (
4a
), respectively. Condition (
12e
) is proven analogously using (
9
) and (
4b
).

\square

Combining Lemma
8
with the preceding discussion yields the following result.

Proposition 9

Let
\mathcal{G}(\mu,\nu)
be as in (
11
), and let
\mathcal{A}
be the set of measures
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(D)\times\mathcal{M}_{+}(B)
satisfying (
12a
)–(
12e
). If

\mathcal{G}(\mu,\nu)\geq 0\quad\forall(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{A},

(13)

then the functional
\mathcal{F}(u)
in (
3
) is nonnegative for all functions
u
in the set
\mathcal{U}
defined in Problem
1
.

Remark 10

If the functions
u\in\mathcal{U}
satisfy Dirichlet boundary conditions, meaning that
b(x,u)=u-h(x)
in (
4b
) for some fixed function
h
, then their boundary measures
\nu_{u}
coincide with the known measure
\nu_{h}
. In such cases, one can replace
\nu
with
\nu_{h}
in Lemma
8
and Proposition
9
. Then, condition (
12c
) can be dropped, while the term
\langle\varphi\cdot\hat{n},\nu\rangle
in (
12a
) becomes the real number
\langle\varphi\cdot\hat{n},\nu_{h}\rangle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi(x,h(x))\cdot\hat{n}(x)\,{\rm d}S
, which in principle can be computed explicitly for every choice of
\varphi\in\Phi
.

3.3
Exploiting symmetries

Proposition
9
can be refined if the functional inequality in Problem
1
enjoys symmetries. Specifically, if the domain
\Omega
, the integrals
entering (
3
), and the constraints in (
4
) are all invariant under a group of linear transformations, then it suffices to verify (
13
) on the group-invariant subset of
\mathcal{A}
.

To make these idea precise, let
\mathbb{G}
be a group of orthonormal linear transformations on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, meaning a family (finite, countable or uncountable) of pairs of matrices
(A,B)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
such that:

1.

A^{\mathsf{T}}=A^{-1}
and
B^{\mathsf{T}}=B^{-1}
;

2.

If
(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}
, then
(A^{-1},B^{-1})\in\mathbb{G}
;

3.

If
(A_{1},B_{1}),\,(A_{2},B_{2})\in\mathbb{G}
, then
(A_{1}A_{2},B_{1}B_{2})\in\mathbb{G}
.

Given
(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}
, define linear operators
G_{A,B}
and
H_{A,B}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, respectively, via

\displaystyle G_{A,B}(x,y,Z)

\displaystyle=\left(Ax,\,By,\,BZA^{\mathsf{T}}\right)

\displaystyle H_{A,B}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\left(Ax,\,By\right).

We say that Problem
1
is invariant under
\mathbb{G}
if:

1.

x\in\Omega\iff Ax\in\Omega
for all
(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}
;

2.

a\circ G_{A,B}=a
for all
(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}
;

3.

b\circ H_{A,B}=b
for all
(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}
;

4.

(Ax)^{\alpha}(By)^{\beta}(BZA^{\mathsf{T}})^{\gamma}=x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}Z^{\gamma}
for all
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in\mathcal{S}
and all
(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}
.

In such cases, Proposition
9
can be replaced with

Proposition 11

Let
\mathcal{G}(\mu,\nu)
be as in (
11
), and let
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{G}}
be the set of measures
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(D)\times\mathcal{M}_{+}(B)
that satisfy (
12a
)–(
12e
) as well as the invariance conditions

\displaystyle\langle\eta\circ G_{A,B},\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\eta,\mu\rangle

\displaystyle\forall\eta\in C(D),\,(A,B)\in\mathbb{G},

(15a)

\displaystyle\langle\zeta\circ H_{A,B},\nu\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\zeta,\nu\rangle

\displaystyle\forall\zeta\in C(B),\,(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}.

(15b)

If assumptions
1
–
4
hold and

\mathcal{G}(\mu,\nu)\geq 0\quad\forall(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{G}},

(16)

then the functional
\mathcal{F}(u)
in (
3
) is nonnegative for all functions
u
in the set
\mathcal{U}
defined in Problem
1
.

This result is a direct consequence of the following lemma.

Lemma 12

Under conditions
1
–
4
, for every
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{A}
there exists
(\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu})\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{G}}
such that
\mathcal{G}(\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu})=\mathcal{G}(\mu,\nu)
.

{pf}

Let
\left|\mathbb{G}\right|
be the cardinality of
\mathbb{G}
. Fix
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{A}
and define the group-averaged measures

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbb{G}\right|}\sum_{(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}}\!\!G_{A,B}\sharp\mu,

\displaystyle\overline{\nu}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbb{G}\right|}\sum_{(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}}\!\!H_{A,B}\sharp\nu,

where
G_{A,B}\sharp\mu
and
H_{A,B}\sharp\nu
are the pushforwards of
\mu
and
\nu
by
G_{A,B}
and
H_{A,B}
. The identity
\mathcal{G}(\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu})=\mathcal{G}(\mu,\nu)
follows upon observing that

\displaystyle\langle x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}Z^{\gamma},\overline{\mu}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbb{G}\right|}\sum_{(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}}\langle x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}Z^{\gamma},G_{A,B}\sharp\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbb{G}\right|}\sum_{(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}}\langle(Ax)^{\alpha}(By)^{\beta}(BZA^{\mathsf{T}})^{\gamma},\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbb{G}\right|}\sum_{(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}}\langle x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}Z^{\gamma},\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}Z^{\gamma},\mu\rangle

for all
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in\mathcal{S}
. The first equality is a direct consequence of the linearity of integration against measures; the second one follows from the definition of the pushforward measure; the third one exploits assumption
4
.

There remains to show that
\overline{\mu}
and
\overline{\nu}
satisfy (
15a
), (
15b
), and (
12a
)–(
12e
). For (

), observe that

\displaystyle\langle\eta\circ G_{A,B},\overline{\mu}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbb{G}\right|}\sum_{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\in\mathbb{G}}\langle\eta\circ G_{A,B},G_{A^{\prime},B^{\prime}}\sharp\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbb{G}\right|}\sum_{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\in\mathbb{G}}\langle\eta\circ G_{A,B}\circ G_{A^{\prime},B^{\prime}},\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbb{G}\right|}\sum_{(A^{\prime\prime},B^{\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{G}}\langle\eta\circ G_{A^{\prime\prime},B^{\prime\prime}},\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbb{G}\right|}\sum_{(A^{\prime\prime},B^{\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{G}}\langle\eta,G_{A^{\prime\prime},B^{\prime\prime}}\sharp\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\eta,\overline{\mu}\rangle.

The first and last equalities exploit the linearity of integration against measures; the second and fourth ones follow from the definition of the pushforward measure; the third one combines conditions
2
and
3
with the definition of
G_{A,B}
. Analogous steps show that

Next, we show that
\overline{\mu}

\overline{\nu}

Fix an arbitrary function
\varphi\in\Phi

via

\varphi_{A,B}(x,y):=A^{\mathsf{T}}\varphi(Ax,By).

Thanks to condition
1
, it is an exercise in vector calculus to check that

\mathcal{D}\varphi_{A,B}=\mathcal{D}\varphi\circ G_{A,B}
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{D}\varphi,\overline{\mu}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbb{G}\right|}\sum_{(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}}\langle\mathcal{D}\varphi,G_{A,B}\sharp\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbb{G}\right|}\sum_{(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}}\langle\mathcal{D}\varphi\circ G_{A,B},\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbb{G}\right|}\sum_{(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}}\langle\mathcal{D}\varphi_{A,B},\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbb{G}\right|}\sum_{(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}}\langle\varphi_{A,B}\cdot\hat{n},\nu\rangle.

The last equality is true because

by assumption, so
\mu
and
\nu

\varphi_{A,B}(x,y)\cdot\hat{n}(x)=(\varphi\cdot\hat{n})\circ H_{A,B}

\langle\mathcal{D}\varphi,\overline{\mu}\rangle=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbb{G}\right|}\sum_{(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}}\langle(\varphi\cdot\hat{n})\circ H_{A,B},\nu\rangle\\
=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbb{G}\right|}\sum_{(A,B)\in\mathbb{G}}\langle\varphi\cdot\hat{n},H_{A,B}\sharp\nu\rangle=\langle\varphi\cdot n,\overline{\nu}\rangle.

12b

\square

4
Reduction to an SDP

The measure-theoretic inequality (
13
) and its symmetry-exploiting analogue (
16
), although simpler than the original functional inequality
\mathcal{F}(u)\geq 0
from Problem
1
, remain difficult to check in general. Often, however, they can be further strengthened into finite-dimensional sufficient conditions that can be tested computationally. One case in which this can be done systematically is that of problems with polynomial data, meaning that:

1.

The function
f
in (
3
) is polynomial;

2.

The functions
a
and
b
in (
4
) are polynomials;

3.

The domain
\Omega
is a basic semialgebraic set, i.e., it is defined by polynomial equalities and inequalities.

We shall also assume that:

4.

There exist polynomials
g
and
h
such that

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:g(x)\geq 0\},

(18a)

\displaystyle\partial\Omega

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:g(x)=0\}.

(18b)

5.

The unit normal vector
\hat{n}(x)=\nabla g(x)\left|\nabla g(x)\right|^{-1}
to
\partial\Omega
is polynomial.

The last two assumptions are made purely for simplicity.
It is straightforward to extend our discussion to domains with semialgebraic definitions more complicated than (
18
). When
\hat{n}(x)
is not polynomial, instead, one can redefine the boundary measures in §
3.1
to be the pushforward of the weighted surface measure
\left|\nabla g(x)\right|^{-1}{\rm d}S
. Then, all results in §
3
hold with
\hat{n}(x)
replaced by
\nabla g(x)
and with an extra factor of
\left|\nabla g(x)\right|^{-1}
in the surface integrals in (
9
) and (
12c
).

4.1
A sufficient polynomial inequality

Under assumptions
1
–
5
, the measure-theoretic inequalities (
13
) and (
16
) can be strengthened into polynomial inequalities over sets defined by linear equations and linear matrix inequalities (LMIs).
To do this, observe that
\mathcal{G}
depends on the moments of
\mu
with exponents
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{E}_{d,p}
, which have degree
\left|\alpha\right|\leq d
and
\left|\gamma\right|+\left|p\right|\leq p
(cf. §
2
). Recall also that the polynomials

and

in (

in

and

is

in (

Now, fix any integer

(called the

) and define the moment vectors

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle:=\{\langle x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}Z^{\gamma},\mu\rangle:\;\left|\alpha\right|\leq 2\omega,\;\left|\beta\right|+\left|\gamma\right|\leq p\},

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle:=\{\langle x^{\alpha}y^{\beta},\nu\rangle:\;\left|\alpha\right|\leq 2\omega+\deg(\hat{n}),\;\left|\beta\right|\leq p\}.

The functional
\mathcal{G}
in (
11

we have exactly

, where

is the polynomial in (

as a higher-dimensional polynomial. Thus, to prove inequalities (

is nonnegative for all moment vectors

for all

The LMIs involve the well-known

and

(

. Precisely, given any polynomial

[M_{h}(\xi)]_{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma),(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})}=\sum_{\left|\alpha^{\prime\prime}\right|\leq d_{h}}h_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}\xi_{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}+\alpha^{\prime\prime},\beta+\beta^{\prime},\gamma+\gamma^{\prime}}

(\alpha,\beta)
with
\left|\alpha\right|\leq\omega-\lceil\frac{1}{2}d_{h}\rceil
and

. Then, under assumptions
1
–
5

\xi
and

are known to satisfy the block-diagonal LMI

M(\xi,\theta):=\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}(\xi)\\
&M_{g}(\xi)\\
&&M_{1}(\theta)\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0.

The linear equality constraints on

)–(

) and (in the presence of symmetries) from (

\Phi_{\omega}\subset\Phi
be a monomial basis for the space of

\displaystyle\Psi_{a,\omega}

\displaystyle:=\big\{x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}Z^{\gamma}:\,\left|\beta\right|+\left|\gamma\right|+\deg_{y,Z}(a)\leq p,

\displaystyle\hskip 96.0pt\left|\alpha\right|+\deg_{x}(a)\leq 2\omega\big\},

\displaystyle\Psi_{b,\omega}

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{D}\varphi,\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\textstyle\int_{\Omega}x^{\alpha}\,{\rm d}{x}

Inequalities (

4.2
Implementation via semidefinite programming

Inequality (
24
) can be verified computationally via semidefinite programming using (a variation of) known techniques based on sum-of-squares (SOS) polynomials and SOS polynomial matrices
(
Scherer and Hol 2006
)
.

We begin by stating some preliminary results about positive semidefinite (PSD) matrices. For any integers
m
and
n
, let
\mathbb{S}^{n}
be the space of
n\times n
symmetric matrices and let
\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
be the PSD cone. Given
P\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
Q\in\mathbb{S}^{mn}
partitioned into
m^{2}
blocks
Q_{ij}
of size
n\times n
, define

\mathcal{T}(P,Q):=\begin{pmatrix}\trace(PQ_{11})&\cdots&\trace(PQ_{1n})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\trace(PQ_{n1})&\cdots&\trace(PQ_{nn})\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{S}^{m}.

Note that
\mathcal{T}(P,Q)
is simply the trace inner product of
P
and
Q
if
m=1
. When
m=n^{k}
for some integer
k\geq 1
, we define the matrices
\mathcal{T}_{i}(P,Q)\in\mathbb{S}^{n^{k-i}}
for
i=1,\ldots,k
via

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}(P,Q),

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{i}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}[P,\,\mathcal{T}_{i-1}(P,Q)],\quad i=2,\ldots,k.

Observe that
\mathcal{T}_{i}(P,Q)
depends linearly on
Q
for every
i
, and that
\mathcal{T}_{k}(P,Q)
is a scalar.

Lemma 15

If
P\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
and
Q\in\mathbb{S}^{n^{k}}_{+}
, then
\mathcal{T}_{i}(P,Q)
is PSD for each
i=1,\ldots,k
. In particular,
\mathcal{T}_{k}(P,Q)\geq 0
.

{pf}

The case
i=1
was proven by
Scherer and Hol 2006
. The general case follows by induction.

\square

Lemma
15
immediately implies the next result, which may be seen as an application of the so-called S-procedure.

Proposition 16

Let
\rho_{\omega}
be the number of rows in the LMI (
20
) and
\sigma_{\omega}
be the number of constraints in (
23
).
The polynomial inequality (
24
), hence the functional inequality in Problem
1
, holds if there exist a polynomial vector
q(\xi,\theta)
with
\sigma_{\omega}
entries and PSD polynomial matrices

\{Q_{k}(\xi,\theta)\}_{k=1,\ldots,K}
, each of size
(\rho_{\omega})^{k}\times(\rho_{\omega})^{k}
, such that

f(\xi)\geq q(\xi,\theta)\cdot e(\xi,\theta)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{T}_{k}(M(\xi,\theta),Q_{k}(\xi,\theta)).

(25)

It is well known (see, e.g.,
Lasserre 2015
;
Laurent 2009
;
Scherer and Hol 2006
;
Parrilo 2013
) that if inequality (
25
) and the positive semidefinitess constraints on the polynomial matrices
Q_{k}
are strengthened into SOS constraints, then the search for these matrices and for the polynomial vector
q
can be reformulated as an SDP once the number
K
and the polynomial degrees are fixed. The same is true when
f
depends affinely on parameters
\lambda
, as in Problem
2
, because in such cases (
25
) is jointly affine in
\lambda
,
q
, and the matrices
Q_{k}
. Consequently, one can use semidefinite programming to (attempt to) verify the functional inequality in Problem
1
and to compute feasible
\lambda
for the convex optimization problem (
5
).

Remark 17

For
K=1
, inequality (
25
) reduces to the sufficient conditions for polynomial inequalities considered by
Scherer and Hol 2006
. This is enough when the set of
(\xi,\theta)
satisfying (
20
) and (
23
) is compact and satisfies the so-called Archimedean condition
(
Scherer and Hol 2006
, Theorem 1)
, but not otherwise: as demonstrated in §
5.2
, in general it is necessary to use
K>1
. Beyond compactness, we are not aware of conditions ensuring that (
25
) is feasible if
K
and the degrees of
q
and each
Q_{k}
are sufficiently large.

as
\omega\to\infty
. Loosely speaking, this is because analysis by
Chernyavsky et al. 2021
, combined with a duality argument
(
Fantuzzi 2019
)
, guarantees that our sufficient conditions capture with arbitrary accuracy the Poincaré inequality needed to verify (
32
). Precisely, for every
\kappa>(\pi/2)^{2}
there exists a sufficiently large
\omega
and a PSD matrix
P
of the same size as the matrix
M(\xi)
for that
\omega
value such that, for all
\xi
that satisfy the equality constraints,

\xi_{0,0,2}=\kappa\xi_{0,2,0}+\trace[M(\xi)P].

Taking the
d
-th power of this identity, multiplying both sides by
\xi_{0,2,0}^{d}
, and rearranging gives

\xi_{0,0,2}^{d}\xi_{0,2,0}^{d}-\kappa^{d}\xi_{0,2,0}^{2d}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\textstyle\binom{d}{j}\displaystyle\kappa^{d-j}\xi_{0,2,0}^{2d-j}s_{j}(\xi),

(34)

where
s_{j}(\xi)=\left(\trace[M(\xi)P]\right)^{j}=(\xi_{0,0,2}-\kappa\xi_{0,2,0})^{j}
.
Now, it can be shown that
\xi_{0,2,0}
is a diagonal entry of
M(\xi)
, so there exists a PSD diagonal matrix
D
such that
\trace(DM(\xi))=\xi_{0,2,0}
. Using this matrix, it is possible to rewrite the right-hand side of (
34
) as
\mathcal{T}_{2}[M(\xi),R(\xi)]
with

R(\xi):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{d}\xi_{0,2,0}^{2d-j-1}s_{j-1}(\xi)\left(D\otimes P\right)\\[-12.91663pt]
+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=2\\
j\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{d}\xi_{0,2,0}^{2d-j}s_{j-2}(\xi)\left(P\otimes P\right),

which can be easily shown to be an SOS matrix. Then, if
f(\xi)
is as in (
33
) and we fix
K=2
,
q(\xi)=0
,
Q_{1}(\xi)=0
, and
Q_{2}(\xi)=\lambda R(\xi)
, the sufficient condition (
25
) from Proposition
16
for our example reduces to

\displaystyle 0\leq f(\xi)-\mathcal{T}_{2}[M(\xi),Q_{2}(\xi)]=\lambda\kappa^{d}\xi_{0,2,0}^{2d}-2\xi_{0,2,0}^{d}+1.

This inequality, hence the original functional inequality (
32
), holds if and only if
\lambda\geq\kappa^{-d}
. This construction works for every
\kappa>(\frac{\pi}{2})^{2}
provided that
\omega
is large enough, so the functional inequality (
32
) can be verified with the sufficient condition in Proposition
16
for
\lambda
as close to
(\frac{2}{\pi})^{2d}=\lambda_{d}^{*}
as desired.
