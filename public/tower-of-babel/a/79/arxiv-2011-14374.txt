Abstract
. We prove the Cesàro boundedness of eigenfunctions of the Dirac operator on the half-line with a square-summable potential. The proof is based on the theory of Krein systems and, in particular, on the continuous version of a theorem by A. Mate, P. Nevai and V. Totik from 1991.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
34L40, 42C05

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Main result

Consider a symmetric zero trace
2\times 2
potential
Q=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-q&p\\
p&q\end{smallmatrix}\right)
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}=[0,+\infty)
with real-valued entries. We say that
Q\in L^{p}
if both functions
p,q\in L^{p}
. Consider the differential equation

(1)

\displaystyle Jf^{\prime}(t)+Qf(t)

\displaystyle=\lambda f(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\quad f(0)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},

where
J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
is a square root of the identity matrix. This equation can be read as eigenfunction equation for Dirac operator
\mathcal{D}_{Q}=J\frac{d}{dr}+Q
on the half-line
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Motivated by scattering theory, M. Christ and A. Kiselev studied
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
the one-dimensional Shrödinger operator using a method of multilinear expansion; their results can as well be applied to Dirac-type operators. Among other things, they proved the existence of wave operators for the Dirac operator in the case when
Q\in L^{p}
,
1\leqslant p<2
, which implies that for such Q the absolutely continuous spectrum of
D_{Q}
is the entire real line
\mathbb{R}
. Deift and Killip extended
[
7
]
the spectrum result for
Q\in L^{2}
. Using methods of the theory of Krein systems, Denisov showed
[
9
]
the existence of wave operators in the case when
Q\in L^{2}
; recently Bessonov extended
[
2
]
this result for Dirac operators whose spectral measure belongs to the Szegő class on the real line. Some related results and approaches can be found in
[
18
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
22
]
.

M. Christ and A. Kiselev proved in Section
6.3
in
[
4
]
that the solution of (
1
) is bounded for almost every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
in the case when
Q\in L^{p}
,
1\leqslant p<2
. Related question was studied by C. Muscalu, T. Tao, and C. Thiele in
[
18
]
; authors posed the following conjecture.

Conjecture I
.

Assume that
Q\in L^{2}
. Then for almost every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
the solution
f
of the differential equation (
1
) is bounded on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

In present paper we prove an ‘‘averaged’’ version of Conjecture
I
.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that
Q\in L^{2}
. Then for almost every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
the solution
f
of the differential equation (
1
) is bounded on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
in
L^{2}
-Cesàro sense, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sup_{r>0}\left(\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}|f(t)|^{2}\,dt\right)<\infty.

Proof of this theorem is based on the theory of Krein systems which plays a role of a bridge between the theory of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle and spectral theory of second order differential self-adjoint operators.
Krein systems were first introduced by M. G. Krein in
[
15
]
and later developed by different authors (see, e.g.,
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
). Detailed account of the theory can be found in
[
12
]
.

Let us start with a description of basic objects and results in the theory of orthogonal polynomials and in the theory of the Krein systems.

1.2.
Orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle

Orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle appear in many areas of mathematics. An account of the theory can be found in books
[
24
]
by G. Szegő and
[
23
]
by B. Simon. Let
\mu
be a probability measure on the interval
[-\pi,\pi]
such that the support of
\mu
is not a finite set. Then there exists a family of orthonormal polynomials
\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
in
L^{2}([\pi,\pi],\mu)
. More precisely, let the family
\varphi_{n}
be defined by

(2)

\displaystyle\deg\varphi_{n}=n,\quad\langle\varphi_{n},\varphi_{m}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\varphi_{n}(e^{it})\overline{\varphi_{m}(e^{it})}\,d\mu(t)=\delta_{n,m},

where
\delta_{n,m}
is the Kronecker symbol. Define a family of reversed orthogonal polynomials by

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}^{*}(z)=z^{n}\overline{\varphi_{n}(1/\overline{z})},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

It is easy to see that
\varphi_{n}^{*}
is a polynomial of degree not greater that
n
.
We say that the measure
\mu
belongs to the Szegő class on the unit circle if

(3)

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\log\mu^{\prime}(\theta)\,d\theta>-\infty,

where
\mu^{\prime}
is the density of the absolutely continuous part of
\mu
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
[-\pi,\pi]
. The following theorem is a result of work of different authors, we refer to it as the Szegő theorem. This statement is a combination of Theorem 2.4.1 and Theorem 2.7.15 from
[
23
]
.

Theorem A

(Szegő theorem)
.

Let
\mu
be a nontrivial measure on
[-\pi,\pi]
and let
\varphi_{n}
be the associated orthogonal polynomials. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

the measure
\mu
belongs to the Szegő class,

(b)

for some
z_{0}
such that
|z_{0}|<1
we have
\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}|\varphi_{n}(z_{0})|^{2}<\infty
,

(c)

there exists an analytic function
\Pi
in the unit disc
\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\colon|z|<1\}
such that

\displaystyle\Pi(z)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi_{n}^{*}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{D},

and the convergence is uniform on compact subsets in
\mathbb{D}
.

If the equivalent conditions of the Szegő theorem hold, then the series in part
(b)
converges uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{D}
. Moreover, the function
\Pi
is an outer function in the unit disc (see Theorem 2.3.5
[
23
]
) and
|\Pi(e^{it})|^{-2}=\mu^{\prime}(t)
almost everywhere on
[-\pi,\pi]
, i.e,

\displaystyle\Pi(z)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{e^{i\theta}+z}{e^{i\theta}-z}\log\mu^{\prime}(\theta)\,d\theta\right],\quad z\in\mathbb{D}.

The function
D=\Pi^{-1}
is called the Szegő function and often is used instead of
\Pi
.

1.3.
Krein systems

Krein systems were first considered by M. G. Krein
[
15
]
in 1954. Solutions of the Krein systems have properties similar to properties of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle. Through such similarities it becomes possible to apply methods and ideas from the theory of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle to spectral problems for self-adjoint differential operators. Detailed account of this approach can be found in the paper
[
12
]
by S. Denisov.

Definition
.

Let
a
be a complex-valued function on the half-line
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The Krein system with the coefficient
a
is the following system of differential equations:

(4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}P(r,\lambda)=i\lambda P(r,\lambda)-\overline{a(r)}P_{*}(r,\lambda),&\quad\,\,P(0,\lambda)=1,\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial r}P_{*}(r,\lambda)=-a(r)P(r,\lambda),&\quad P_{*}(0,\lambda)=1.\end{cases}

In present paper we consider the case when
a\in L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, that is,
a\in L^{1}[0,x]
for every
x\geqslant 0
. Here the complex number
\lambda
is a spectral parameter; solutions
P
and
P_{*}
are called continuous analogs of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle.

Solutions
P(r,\cdot)
and
P_{*}(r,\cdot)
are entire functions. The parameter
r\geqslant 0
plays the same role as the index
n\geqslant 0
in (
2
):
P(r,\cdot)
has the exponential type
r
and
P_{*}(r,\cdot)
has the exponential type not greater than
r
, see Chapter 3 in
[
12
]
.
For any function
a\in L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
there exists a Borel measure
\sigma
on the real line (see
[
12
]
) such that

(5)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\sigma(x)}{1+x^{2}}<\infty,

and the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\sigma}:f\mapsto\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r)P(r,\lambda)\,dr,

initially defined on simple measurable functions with compact support can be continued to an isometry from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\sigma)
. This measure is called the spectral measure of system (
4
).
We say that the measure
\sigma
belongs to the Szegő class on the real line if it satisfies condition (
5
) and

(6)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\log\sigma^{\prime}(x)|}{1+x^{2}}\,dx<\infty,

where
\sigma^{\prime}
is the density of the absolutely continuous part of
\sigma
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. A key result in the theory of the Krein systems in an analog of the Szegő theorem. It was originally formulated by Krein in
[
15
]
with minor inaccuracies subsequently corrected by A. Teplyaev in
[
25
]
. For a complete proof see Chapter 8 in
[
12
]
.

Theorem B

(Krein theorem)
.

Let a measure
\sigma
be the spectral measure of some Krein system (
4
) and let
P,P_{*}
be the solutions of that system. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

the measure
\sigma
belongs to Szegő class,

(b)

for a point
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{C}_{+}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits(z)>0\}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}|P(r,\lambda_{0})|^{2}\,dr<\infty,

(c)

there exists analytic in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
function
\Pi
and a sequence
r_{n}\to+\infty
such that

\displaystyle\Pi(\lambda)=\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{*}(r_{n},\lambda),

and the convergence is uniform on compact subsets in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
.

If the equivalent conditions of the Krein theorem hold then the integral in part
(b)
converges uniformly on compact subsets. Moreover,
\Pi
is an outer function in the upper half-plane and

(7)

\displaystyle|\Pi(x)|^{2}=|2\pi\sigma^{\prime}(x)|^{-1}

almost everywhere on
\mathbb{R}
(see Lemma 8.6 in
[
12
]
), i.e.,

(8)

\displaystyle\Pi(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left[\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{s-\lambda}-\frac{s}{s^{2}+1}\right)\log\sigma^{\prime}(s)\,ds\right],\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

In addition, the function
[(\lambda+i)\Pi(\lambda)]^{-1}
belongs to the Hardy space
H^{2}
.

1.4.
Mate-Nevai-Totik theorem

A. Mate, P. Nevai, V. Totik in 1991 proved
[
17
]
asymptotics of so-called Christoffel functions. Their result in the language of orthogonal polynomials can be considered as a refinement of part (c) of the Szegő theorem for points on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
. Let us formulate their result. Let
\mu
be a probability Borel measure on
[-\pi,\pi]
. For
z\in\mathbb{C}
, define an
n
-th Christoffel function
w_{n}
as follows:

\displaystyle w_{n}(\mu,z)=\min\left\{\frac{1}{2\pi|P(z)|^{2}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|P(e^{i\theta})|^{2}\,d\mu(\theta)\text{ }\bigg|\text{ }P\text{ -- polynomial, }\deg P<n,P(z)\neq 0\right\}.

Theorem C

(A. Mate, P. Nevai, V. Totik Theorem 1,
[
17
]
)
.

Assume that the measure
\mu
belongs to the Szegő class on the unit circle. Then we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}nw_{n}(\mu,e^{it})=\mu^{\prime}(t)

for almost every
t\in[-\pi,\pi]
.

The function
w_{n}
admits the representation

(9)

\displaystyle 2\pi w_{n}(\mu,z)=\left(\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}|\varphi_{k}(z)|^{2}\right)^{-1},\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

where
\varphi_{n}
are the orthonormal polynomials on the unit circle associated with
\mu
, see Theorem 11.3.1 in
[
24
]
. This representation allows us to formulate Theorem
C
using only the orthogonal polynomials.

Theorem D
.

Let
\mu
be a nontrivial probability Borel measure on
\mathbb{T}
and
\varphi_{n}
be the orthogonal polynomials associated with
\mu
. If
\mu
belongs to the Szegő class, then for almost every
t\in[-\pi,\pi]

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}|\varphi_{k}(e^{it})|^{2}\to\frac{1}{2\pi\mu^{\prime}(t)},\quad n\to\infty.

The core result of the present paper is the following theorem. It can be seen as a refinement of part (c) of the Krein theorem for points on the real line.

Theorem 2
.

Consider a Krein system with a coefficient in
L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
such that its spectral measure
\sigma
is in the Szegő class on the real line. Then for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}
there exists the limit

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}|P(s,x)|^{2}ds\to\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{\prime}(x)},\quad r\to\infty.

1.5.
Teplyaev’s conjectures

Notice that the convergence in part (c) of the Krein theorem holds only for some sequence of real numbers
r_{n}
while there is no such constraint in the part (c) of the Szegő theorem. A. Teplyaev proved
[
25
]
that the convergence
P_{*}(r,\lambda)\to\Pi(\lambda)
holds for
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, see Theorem 1 in
[
25
]
or Theorem 11.1 in
[
12
]
. Additionally, the author proved sharpness of this result in the sense that it cannot be extended to any
L^{p}
for
p>2
, see Theorem 3 in
[
25
]
.
Teplyaev conjectured two positive results concerning the convergence of
P_{*}
to
\Pi
for a Krein system with the real-valued coefficient, see Conjecture 6.5, Conjecture 6.6 in
[
25
]
. The first conjecture was proved by S. Denisov in
[
12
]
, we state it below:

Theorem E

(Lemma 8.6
[
12
]
)
.

Assume that the conditions of the Krein theorem hold, the function
a
is real-valued, and a sequence
t_{n}\to\infty
is such that
P(t_{n},\lambda_{0})\to 0
for some
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
. Then
P_{*}(t_{n},\lambda)\to\Pi(\lambda)
uniformly on compact subsets in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
.

The second conjecture concerns a convergence of
P_{*}
in average (i.e. in the Cesàro sense). We prove it in the present paper.

Theorem 3
.

Assume
a
is a real-valued function and conditions of Krein theorem hold. Then
\Pi(\lambda)
is the limit in average of
P_{*}(r,\lambda)
, that is,

\displaystyle\Pi(\lambda)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}P_{*}(\rho,\lambda)\,d\rho,

and the convergence is uniform on compact subsets in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
.

Notice that the limit in average of
P_{*}(r,\lambda)
does not exist in general. In other words, there exists a function
a\in L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}
such that the convergence in Theorem
3
does not hold, see discussion in the end of Section 6 in
[
25
]
.

2.
Dirac equation. Proof of Theorem
1

Let us show how to derive Theorem
1
from Theorem
2
using an appropriate transformation of Dirac differential equation (
1
) to Krein system (
4
). To do that, we need some notation. Consider system (
4
) and let the functions
\varphi,\psi,p,q
be as follows:

(10)

\displaystyle\varphi(r,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(-i\lambda r)}{2}\left[P(2r,\lambda)+P_{*}(2r,\lambda)\right],\quad\varphi(0,\lambda)=1,

(11)

\displaystyle\psi(r,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(-i\lambda r)}{2i}\left[P(2r,\lambda)-P_{*}(2r,\lambda)\right],\quad\psi(0,\lambda)=0,

\displaystyle p(r)

\displaystyle=-2\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits a(2r),\qquad q(r)=2\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits a(2r).

Direct calculations give that functions
\varphi
and
\psi
are the solutions of the differential equation

(12)

\displaystyle\lambda\begin{pmatrix}\varphi\\
\psi\end{pmatrix}=J\begin{pmatrix}\varphi\\
\psi\end{pmatrix}^{\prime}+Q\begin{pmatrix}\varphi\\
\psi\end{pmatrix},\,\varphi(0,\lambda)=1,\,\psi(0,\lambda)=0,

\displaystyle J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Q=\begin{pmatrix}-q&p\\
p&q\end{pmatrix}.

Now we proceed to the proof of Theorem
1
. Construct a Krein system with the coefficient
a(r)=-\frac{1}{2}p(r/2)+\frac{i}{2}q(r/2)
, where
p,q
are the potentials in equation (
1
). Uniqueness theorem and (
12
) give that functions
\varphi
and
\psi
defined by (
10
) and (
11
) respectively are the unique solutions of equation (
1
). Since potentials
p,q
are in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, coefficient
a
is also in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Spectral measure of the Krein system with square-summable coefficient belongs to the Szegő class (see Theorem 11.1 in
[
12
]
). Hence, Theorem
2
can be applied and the convergence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}|P(s,x)|^{2}\,ds\to(2\pi\sigma^{\prime}(x))^{-1},\quad\text{ as }r\to\infty,

holds for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Consequently, for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\sup_{r>0}\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}|P(s,x)|^{2}\,ds<\infty.

Finally,

\displaystyle\sup_{r>0}\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}|\varphi(s,x)|^{2}ds

\displaystyle=\sup_{r>0}\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\left|\frac{\exp(-ixs)}{2}\left[P(2s,x)+P_{*}(2s,x)\right]\right|^{2}ds

\displaystyle=\sup_{r>0}\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\left|\frac{P(2s,x)+P_{*}(2s,x)}{2}\right|^{2}ds

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{r>0}\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\frac{\left|P(2s,x)\right|^{2}+\left|P_{*}(2s,x)\right|^{2}}{2}\,ds

\displaystyle=\sup_{r>0}\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\left|P(2s,x)\right|^{2}ds<\infty

The equality on the last line follows from Corollary
1
to Christoffel-Darboux formula (see Section
3.1
below). Same argument works for
\psi
.

3.
Asymptotic behaviour of Krein system solutions on the real line. Proof of Theorem
2

3.1.
Preliminaries

Christoffel-Darboux formula is one of the most important identities in the theory of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle, for details see Theorem 2.2.7 in
[
23
]
or in Theorem 11.4.2 in
[
24
]
. We will use its continuous analog.

Lemma

(Christoffel-Darboux formula)
.

Let
P
and
P_{*}
be solutions of the Krein system (
4
).The equalities

(13)

\displaystyle P(r,\lambda)\overline{P(r,\mu)}-P_{*}(r,\lambda)\overline{P_{*}(r,\mu)}=i(\lambda-\overline{\mu})\int_{0}^{r}P(s,\lambda)\overline{P(s,\mu)}\,ds,

(14)

\displaystyle\left|P_{*}(r,\lambda)\right|^{2}=\left|P(r,\lambda)\right|^{2}+2\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits(\lambda)\int_{0}^{r}\left|P(s,\lambda)\right|^{2}ds

hold for any two complex numbers
\lambda,\mu
.

Proof.

Equation (
14
) follows from (
13
) by substituting
\mu=\lambda
; (
13
) can be proved by calculation of derivatives in both sides of the equality.
∎

Corollary 1
.

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
the following is true:

(1)

|P_{*}(r,\lambda)|=|P(r,\lambda)|
if
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

(2)

|P_{*}(r,\lambda)|>|P(r,\lambda)|
if
\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits(\lambda)>0

(3)

|P_{*}(r,\lambda)|<|P(r,\lambda)|
if
\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits(\lambda)<0

Proof.

All three statements easily follow from the second Christoffel-Darboux formula (
14
).
∎

Corollary 2
.

P_{*}(r,\cdot)
has no zeros in closed half-plane
\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}=\{z\colon\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits z\geqslant 0\}
.

Proof.

From Part
2
of Corollary
1
it follows that there are no roots in an open half-plane
\mathbb{C}_{+}
. Hence, to prove Corollary
2
we need to show the absence of zeros on the real line. Assume the converse. Then there exists
x\in\mathbb{R}
such that
P_{*}(r,x)=0
. Part
1
of Corollary
1
gives

(15)

\displaystyle P_{*}(r,x)=P(r,x)=0.

On the other hand, the functions
P_{*}(\cdot,x),P(\cdot,x)
are the solutions of linear differential system (
4
) with non-zero initial values. Hence, (
15
) cannot hold. This contradiction concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma A

(S. Denisov Lemma 8.5,
[
12
]
)
.

If a measure
\sigma
belongs to the Szegő class and a sequence
r_{n}
is such that
P(r_{n},\lambda_{0})\to 0
for some
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
. Then the convergence
\left|P_{*}(r_{n},\lambda)\right|\to\left|\Pi(\lambda)\right|
holds uniformly in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
.

Corollary 3
.

From equation (
14
) and Lemma
A
we have

\displaystyle|\Pi(\lambda)|^{2}=2\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits(\lambda)\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|P(s,\lambda)\right|^{2}ds.

Remark
.

A similar relation in the theory of the orthogonal polynomials is given in Chapter 2.4 in
[
23
]
.

3.2.
An equivalent form of Theorem
2

In this section we formulate Theorem
2
in a form similar to original Mate-Nevai-Totik Theorem
C
. By
PW_{r}
denote the Paley-Wiener space with a spectrum in
[0,r]
, that is, a space of entire functions
f
that can be represented as

\displaystyle f(x)=\int_{0}^{r}\varphi(s)e^{ixs}\,ds,\quad\varphi\in L^{2}[0,r].

Let a function
m_{r}
be given by

(16)

\displaystyle m_{r}(\sigma,z)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\frac{1}{|f(z)|^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(t)|^{2}\,d\sigma(t)\text{ }\bigg|\text{ }f\in PW_{r},f(z)\neq 0\right\},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Similarly to (
9
), the function
m_{r}
can be calculated using the continuous analogs of the orthogonal polynomials. This is shown in the following lemma.

Lemma B

(S. Denisov, Lemma 8.2,
[
12
]
)
.

For any
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
the following is true:

\displaystyle m_{r}(\sigma,z_{0})=K_{r}(z_{0},z_{0})^{-1},\quad\text{ where }K_{r}(z^{\prime},z)=\int_{0}^{r}\overline{P(s,z^{\prime})}P(s,z)\,ds.

Infimum in (
16
) is achieved by function

\displaystyle f_{r}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{K_{r}(z_{0},z)}{K_{r}(z_{0},z_{0})},

in particular, function
f_{r}
itself belongs to
PW_{r}
.

Lemma gives

\displaystyle m_{r}(\sigma,z)

\displaystyle=K_{r}(z,z)^{-1}=\left(\int_{0}^{r}|P(s,z)|^{2}\,ds\right)^{-1},

\displaystyle rm_{r}(\sigma,z)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}|P(s,z)|^{2}\,ds\right)^{-1}.

Substituting the latter equality into Theorem

2
we get its equivalent form.

Theorem 4

(Equivalent form of Theorem
2
)
.

Consider a Krein system with a coefficient in
L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
such that its spectral measure
\sigma
is in the Szegő class on the real line. Then for almost all
z\in\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}rm_{r}(\sigma,z)=2\pi\sigma^{\prime}(z).

We prove Theorem
4
by establishing two inequalities

(17)

\displaystyle\limsup rm_{r}(\sigma,z)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\pi\sigma^{\prime}(z),

(18)

\displaystyle\liminf rm_{r}(\sigma,z)

\displaystyle\geqslant 2\pi\sigma^{\prime}(z).

in Sections
3.3
и
3.5
respectively.

3.3.
Upper bound

Lemma 1
.

Assume that the measure
\sigma
on the real line satisfies condition (
5
). Then the inequality

\displaystyle\limsup rm_{r}(\sigma,z)\leqslant 2\pi\sigma^{\prime}(z)

holds for almost every
z\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark
.

In the lemma we do not require
\sigma
to belong to the Szegő class. Hence, the inequality in the lemma is stronger than (
17
).

Proof.

Consider functions

\displaystyle R(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{ix}-1}{ix}=\mathcal{F}\left(\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}\right)(x),

\displaystyle\Phi(x)

\displaystyle=|R(x)|^{2}.

Notice that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\Phi\right)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(|R|^{2}\right)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(R\right)*\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\overline{R}\right)=\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}*\mathbf{1}_{[-1,0]}=\begin{cases}0,&|x|>1\\
1-|x|,&|x|\leqslant 1\end{cases},

in other words,
\Phi
is a continuous Fejer kernel. Approximation identity constructed with function
\Phi
has powerful convergence properties. We believe the following result is folklore.

Theorem F

(Continuous Fejer kernel property)
.

The convergence

\displaystyle\left(\Phi_{r}*\sigma\right)(z)\to 2\pi\sigma^{\prime}(z),\quad r\to\infty,

where
\Phi_{r}(x)=r\Phi(rx)
, holds for almost every
z\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let us show how the lemma follows from Theorem
F
. The function
f_{r,z}(x)=R(r(x-z))
is in
PW_{r}
and
f_{r,z}(z)=1
. Hence

\displaystyle rm_{r}(\sigma,z)\leqslant r\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f_{r,z}(x)|^{2}\,d\sigma(x)=r\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|R(r(x-z))|^{2}\,d\sigma(x)=(\Phi_{r}*\sigma)(z).

It is only remains to pass to the limit superior on
r\to\infty
and apply the property of the continuous Fejer kernel to conclude the proof.
∎

3.4.
Decay of functions from Paley-Wiener space

In this section we prove result concerning functions of Paley-Wiener space; it is independent of the theory of Krein systems but will be useful to us. Let the elementary Weierstrass factors
E_{n}
be defined by formula

\displaystyle E_{n}(z)=\begin{cases}(1-z)&n=0,\\
(1-z)\exp\left(\frac{z^{1}}{1}+\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\cdots+\frac{z^{n}}{n}\right)&n>0.\end{cases}

Theorem

(Hadamard factorization theorem)
.

Let
f
be an entire function of finite order
\rho\geqslant 0
and
\{a_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be its non-zero roots with multiplicity. Then
f
admits a factorization

\displaystyle f(z)=e^{g(z)}z^{m}\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}E_{d}\left(\frac{z}{a_{k}}\right),

where
g
is a polynomial with degree not greater than
\rho
and
d=[\rho]
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
f
be a function from
PW_{r}
. Suppose
f(0)\neq 0
and every zero of
f
is a real number. Then the inequality

\displaystyle|f(t)|\leqslant|f(t+\gamma i)|e^{\gamma r}

holds for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\gamma>0
.

Proof.

Denote roots of
f
with multiplicity by
\{a_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
. Functions from Paley-Wiener space are of order
1
. Hence, by the Hadamard factorization theorem, it follows that
f
can be represented as

(19)

\displaystyle f(z)=e^{c_{1}z+c_{2}}\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{z}{a_{k}}\right)e^{\frac{z}{a_{k}}}.

If
f(t)=0
the inequality in lemma is trivial, otherwise we can write

\displaystyle\left|\frac{f(t+\gamma i)}{f(t)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|e^{c_{1}\cdot\gamma i}\right|\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1-\frac{t+\gamma i}{a_{k}}}{1-\frac{t}{a_{k}}}e^{\frac{\gamma i}{a_{k}}}\right|=\left|e^{c_{1}\cdot\gamma i}\right|\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{a_{k}-t-\gamma i}{a_{k}-t}\right|

(20)

\displaystyle=\left|e^{c_{1}\cdot\gamma i}\right|\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\left|1-\frac{\gamma i}{a_{k}-t}\right|=e^{-\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits(c_{1})\gamma}\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\left|1+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{(a_{k}-t)^{2}}\right|^{1/2}\geqslant e^{-\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits(c_{1})\gamma}.

Choose a large real number
N
and substitute
-iN
for
z
into (
19
).

\displaystyle|f(-iN)|

\displaystyle=\left|e^{-c_{1}iN+c_{2}}\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{-iN}{a_{k}}\right)e^{\frac{-iN}{a_{k}}}\right|

\displaystyle=e^{N\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits c_{1}+\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits c_{2}}\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{1+\frac{N^{2}}{a_{k}^{2}}}\geqslant e^{N\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits c_{1}+\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits c_{2}}.

Since
f
belongs to
PW_{r}
, its exponential type is not greater than
r
. So

\displaystyle r\geqslant\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log|f(-iN)|}{N}\geqslant\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{N\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits c_{1}+\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits c_{2}}{N}=\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits c_{1}.

The latter inequality together with inequality
(
2
)
completes the proof.
∎

3.5.
Lower bound

Throughout this section we suppose that the measure
\sigma
belongs to the Szegő class on the real line. Consider the Szegő function
D
defined by the equality

(21)

\displaystyle D(z)=\left(\sqrt{2\pi}\Pi(z)\right)^{-1}.

Now if we recall (
7
), we get that

(22)

\displaystyle|D(z)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\prime}(z)

holds for almost all
z\in\mathbb{R}
. Taking this into account, we see that inequality (
18
) is equivalent to

(23)

\displaystyle\liminf rm_{r}(\sigma,z)\geqslant 2\pi|D(z)|^{2}.

The singular part of
\sigma
only increases the left side in (
23
) so it is suffices to show (
23
) only for absolutely continuous measures
\sigma
. Hence, without loss of generality, we may assume that
\sigma
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure and
d\sigma(x)=|D(x)|^{2}dx.

Next, let us introduce some constraints for the point
z
. First, assume that
z
is a Lebesgue point of
D
, i.e.,

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\int\limits_{|x-z|<h}|D(x)-D(z)|\,dx\to 0,\quad h\to 0.

Secondly, assume that
D
has non-tangential boundary values in
z
.
Both of this constraints are satisfied on a set of a full Lebesgue measure; without loss of generality, we may assume that
z
satisfies the constraints and
z=0
. Let us remember that
f_{r}
is a function for which a value of
m_{r}(\sigma,0)
is achieved (see Lemma
B
). That means

(25)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\left|f_{r}(x)\right|^{2}d\sigma(x)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\left|f_{r}(x)D(x)\right|^{2}dx=m_{r}(0)|f_{r}(0)|^{2}.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. To prove (
23
), we need to show that for a large enough
r

(26)

\displaystyle rm_{r}(\sigma,0)>(2\pi-100\varepsilon)|D(0)|^{2}.

In addition, we may assume that

(27)

\displaystyle rm_{r}(\sigma,0)<2\pi|D(0)|^{2},

otherwise there is nothing left to prove.

3.5.1.
Supporting lemmas

Lemma 3
.

Let
f_{r}
be a function defined in Lemma
B
. For almost every
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\gamma>0

\displaystyle|f_{r}(t)|\leqslant|f_{r}(t+\gamma i)|e^{\gamma r}.

Proof.

It is enough to show that
f_{r}
satisfies all of the conditions of Lemma
2
.
Recall that by definition

\displaystyle f_{r}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{K_{r}(0,\lambda)}{K_{r}(0,0)},\text{ where }K_{r}(\lambda^{\prime},\lambda)=\int_{0}^{r}\overline{P(s,\lambda^{\prime})}P(s,\lambda)\,ds.

From Lemma
B
we already know that
f_{r}
belongs to the Paley-Wiener space
PW_{r}
; obviously
f_{r}(0)=1
. Hence, the only nontrivial question is the absence of zeros outside of the real line. Assume the converse. Then there exists
\lambda\notin\mathbb{R}
such that

. Therefore,

\displaystyle K_{r}(0,\lambda)=\int_{0}^{r}\overline{P(s,0)}P(s,\lambda)\,ds=0

and the Christoffel-Darboux formula (

\displaystyle P(r,\lambda)\overline{P(r,0)}-P_{*}(r,\lambda)\overline{P_{*}(r,0)}=0,

\displaystyle\left|P(r,\lambda)P(r,0)\right|=|P_{*}(r,\lambda)P_{*}(r,0)|.

The latter equality contradicts Corollary
1
of the Christoffel-Darboux formula.
∎

Lemma 4
.

For every point
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle|f_{r}(z)D(z)|\leqslant K_{r}(0,0)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(4\pi\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits(z))^{-\frac{1}{2}},

in particular, for any
c>0
the function
f_{r}D
is bounded in the half-plane
\{z\colon\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits(z)>c\}
.

Proof.

\displaystyle|f_{r}(z)|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{K_{r}(0,z)}{K_{r}(0,0)}\right|=\left|\frac{\int_{0}^{r}\overline{P(s,0)}P(s,z)\,ds}{\int_{0}^{r}\left|P(s,0)\right|^{2}ds}\right|\leqslant\frac{\left(\int_{0}^{r}\left|P(s,0)\right|^{2}ds\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{r}\left|P(s,z)\right|^{2}ds\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\int_{0}^{r}\left|P(s,0)\right|^{2}ds}\leqslant

\displaystyle\leqslant K_{r}(0,0)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{|\Pi(z)|^{2}}{2\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits(z)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{szego_function_definition}}}{{=}}K_{r}(0,0)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits(z)\cdot 2\pi|D(z)|^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=K_{r}(0,0)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(4\pi\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits(z)\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}|D(z)|^{-1}.

The first inequality on the second line follows from Corollary
3
. Multiplying the last inequality by
|D(z)|
, we obtain the required.
∎

Lemma 5
.

For every
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
the following holds:

(28)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f_{r}(x)D(x)}{x-z}\,dx

\displaystyle=f_{r}(z)D(z),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f_{r}(x)D(x)}{x+z}\,dx

\displaystyle=0.

Remark
.

If the function
f_{r}D
was in the Hardy space
H^{p}
for some
p\geqslant 1
, the statement of the lemma would be a well-known fact (see p. 116 in
[
14
]
). It is not our case but function
f_{r}D
satisfies a ‘‘boundedness’’ condition from Lemma
4
.

Proof.

We have already mentioned that the function
\frac{c\Pi(z)^{-1}}{z+i}=\frac{D(z)}{z+i}
is in the Hardy space
H^{2}
. Hence, for
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
the function
\frac{D(x)}{x+z}
also belongs to
H^{2}
. The function
f_{r}
belongs to the Paley-Wiener space and consequently to
H^{2}
. Therefore both integrals in the lemma are absolutely convergent and define analytic functions in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
. Moreover,
f_{r}(x)\frac{D(x)}{x+z}
is a multiplication of two functions from
H^{2}
and consequently it belongs to
H^{1}
. Integral of
H^{1}
function over real line equals
0
(see p. 122 in
[
14
]
or Lemma 3.7 in
[
13
]
). Thus, the second equality in lemma is proved.

From Lemma
4
it follows that the function
G_{\varepsilon}(z)=f_{r}(z+\varepsilon i)D(z+\varepsilon i)
is bounded in the upper half-plane
\mathbb{C}_{+}
. Proceeding as above we deduce that for all
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{G_{\varepsilon}(x)}{x+z}\,dx

\displaystyle=0.

Next, the following chain of equalities is true:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f_{r}(x+\varepsilon i)D(x+\varepsilon i)}{x-z}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{G_{\varepsilon}(x)}{x-z}\,dx

(29)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{G_{\varepsilon}(x)}{x-z}\,dx

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{G_{\varepsilon}(x)}{x+z}\,dx=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{2zG_{\varepsilon}(x)}{x^{2}-z^{2}}\,dx.

The latter integral can be calculated by the calculus of residues. Take a large positive number
R
and consider a circuit
C_{R}
consisting of the segment
[-R,R]
and the half-circle
\Gamma_{R}
connecting points
R
and
-R
in the upper half-plane.

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon}(z)

\displaystyle=\text{Res}_{z}\left(\frac{2zG_{\varepsilon}(x)}{x^{2}-z^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{C_{R}}\frac{2zG_{\varepsilon}(x)}{x^{2}-z^{2}}dx

(30)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{[-R,R]}\frac{2zG_{\varepsilon}(x)}{x^{2}-z^{2}}dx+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{R}}\frac{2zG_{\varepsilon}(x)}{x^{2}-z^{2}}dx.

The first term in the latter sum has a limit as
R\to\infty
.

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{[-R,R]}\frac{2zG_{\varepsilon}(x)}{x^{2}-z^{2}}\,dx=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{2zG_{\varepsilon}(x)}{x^{2}-z^{2}}\,dx.

Since the function
G_{\varepsilon}
is bounded, the second term tends to zero as
R\to\infty
:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_{R}}\frac{2zG_{\varepsilon}(x)}{x^{2}-z^{2}}\,dx\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\left|\frac{2zG_{\varepsilon}(Re^{i\theta})}{R^{2}e^{2i\theta}-z^{2}}\right|R\,d\theta\leqslant\frac{R|z|}{R^{2}-|z|^{2}}\sup\nolimits_{\theta}|G_{\varepsilon}(Re^{i\theta})|=O\left(\frac{1}{R}\right).

We can therefore pass to the limit
R\to\infty
in the equality (
3.5.1
) and obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{2zG_{\varepsilon}(x)}{x^{2}-z^{2}}\,dx=G_{\varepsilon}(z).

Substituting this into (
3.5.1
) we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f_{r}(x+\varepsilon i)D(x+\varepsilon i)}{x-z}\,dx

\displaystyle=G_{\varepsilon}(z)=f_{r}(z+i\varepsilon)D(z+i\varepsilon).

To prove equality (
28
) it is only remains to pass to the limit as
\varepsilon
tends to
0
. The right part of the equality tends to
f_{r}(z)D(z)
so we need to evaluate how the left side differs from the integral in the lemma.

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f_{r}(x+\varepsilon i)D(x+\varepsilon i)}{x-z}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f_{r}(x)D(x)}{x-z}\,dx\right|=

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{r}(x+i\varepsilon)\frac{D(x+\varepsilon i)-D(x)}{x-z}\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}D(x)\frac{f_{r}(x+i\varepsilon)-f_{r}(x)}{x-z}\,dx\right|\leqslant

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|f_{r}(x+i\varepsilon)\frac{D(x+\varepsilon i)-D(x)}{x-z}\right|\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|D(x)\frac{f_{r}(x+i\varepsilon)-f_{r}(x)}{x-z}\right|\,dx\leqslant

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|f_{r}(x+\varepsilon i)\right\|_{2}\cdot\left\|\frac{x+i}{x-z}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|\frac{D(x+\varepsilon i)-D(x)}{x+i}\right\|_{2}+\left\|f_{r}(x+i\varepsilon)-f_{r}(x)\right\|_{2}\cdot\left\|\frac{x+i}{x-z}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|\frac{D(x)}{x+i}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(\left\|\frac{D(x+\varepsilon i)-D(x)}{x+i}\right\|_{2}+\left\|f_{r}(x+i\varepsilon)-f_{r}(x)\right\|_{2}\right)\leqslant

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(\left\|\frac{D(x+\varepsilon i)}{x+i}-\frac{D(x+\varepsilon i)}{x+\varepsilon i+i}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\frac{D(x+\varepsilon i)}{x+\varepsilon i+i}-\frac{D(x)}{x+i}\right\|_{2}+\left\|f_{r}(x+i\varepsilon)-f_{r}(x)\right\|_{2}\right)

The first term can be bounded in the following way:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{D(x+\varepsilon i)}{x+i}-\frac{D(x+\varepsilon i)}{x+\varepsilon i+i}\right\|_{2}=\varepsilon\left\|\frac{D(x+\varepsilon i)}{(x+\varepsilon i+i)(x+i)}\right\|_{2}\leqslant\varepsilon\left\|\frac{D(x)}{x+i}\right\|_{2}\to 0.

From a general property of functions in
H^{2}
(see Theorem 3.1 in
[

]
or Section 19.2 in
[

]
) it follows that the second and the third terms in the bracket tend to zero as

.
∎

3.5.2.
End of the proof

Recall that our aim is the proof of (

.

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{iwx}}{x^{2}+1}\,dx=\pi e^{-|w|}.

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\left|e^{-r\delta}e^{irx}-1\right|^{2}}{x^{2}+\delta^{2}}\,dx=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-2r\delta}+1-e^{-r\delta}(e^{irx}+e^{-irx})}{x^{2}+\delta^{2}}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta}\left[(e^{-2r\delta}+1)\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\,dy}{y^{2}+1}-e^{-r\delta}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ir\delta y}+e^{-ir\delta y}}{y^{2}+1}\,dy\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta}\left[(e^{-2r\delta}+1)\pi-2e^{-r\delta}\pi e^{-r\delta}\right]=\pi\frac{1-e^{-2r\delta}}{\delta}<2\pi r

Put

. The following inequalities hold:

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{integral_f_r(x)D(x)}}}{{=}}m_{r}(0)|f_{r}(0)|^{2}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|H_{r}(x-\delta i)\right|^{2}\,dx\leqslant

Consider quantities

, defined by the equality

\displaystyle I=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{r}(x)H_{r}(x-\delta i)D(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-ir(x-\delta i)}f_{r}(x)D(x)}{x-\delta i}\,dx-\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{f_{r}(x)D(x)}{x-\delta i}\,dx=e^{-r\delta}I_{2}-I_{1}.

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{f_{r}(x)D(x)}{x-\delta i}dx=2\pi if_{r}(\delta i)D(\delta i).

Our further goal will be an estimation

\displaystyle|I_{2}|<4\varepsilon f_{r}(0)D(0).

Before proving this let us show how (

\displaystyle|I|=|e^{-r\delta}I_{2}-I_{1}|\geqslant|I_{1}|-e^{-r\delta}|I_{2}|\geqslant|I_{1}|-|I_{2}|.

Substitution of (

),
(

), (

Consider one more integral

Lemma 7
.

Assume that the coefficient
a
of Krein system (
4
) belongs to
L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and the associated spectral measure
\sigma
belongs to the Szegő class on the real line. Then for every

we have

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\left|P_{*}(\rho,\lambda)\right|\,d\rho=\left|\Pi(\lambda)\right|.

Proof.

The required equality is equivalent to two inequalities

(42)

\displaystyle\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\left|P_{*}(\rho,\lambda)\right|\,d\rho\geqslant\left|\Pi(\lambda)\right|,

(43)

\displaystyle\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\left|P_{*}(\rho,\lambda)\right|\,d\rho\leqslant\left|\Pi(\lambda)\right|.

Take the limit inferior for
r
in Christoffel-Darboux formula (
14
).

\displaystyle\liminf_{r\to\infty}\left|P_{*}(r,\lambda)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\liminf_{r\to\infty}\left(\left|P(r,\lambda)\right|^{2}+2\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits(\lambda)\int_{0}^{r}\left|P(s,\lambda)\right|^{2}ds\right)

Part (b) of the Krein theorem states that the function
P(\cdot,\lambda)
belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Hence,

\displaystyle\liminf_{r\to\infty}\left|P(r,\lambda)\right|^{2}=0.

The second term tends to the integral from

to
\infty

3
this integral is equal to
|\Pi(\lambda)|^{2}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\liminf_{r\to\infty}\left|P_{*}(r,\lambda)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\Pi(\lambda)|^{2},

\displaystyle\liminf_{r\to\infty}\left|P_{*}(r,\lambda)\right|

\displaystyle=|\Pi(\lambda)|.

Inequality (

) follows from the last equality by the direct integration.
To prove (
43
), integrate and divide by
r
Christoffel-Darboux formula (
14
).

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\left|P_{*}(\rho,\lambda)\right|^{2}d\rho

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\left(\left|P(\rho,\lambda)\right|^{2}+2\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits(\lambda)\int_{0}^{\rho}\left|P(s,\lambda)\right|^{2}ds\right)d\rho

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\left|P(\rho,\lambda)\right|^{2}d\rho+2\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits(\lambda)\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\int_{0}^{\rho}\left|P(s,\lambda)\right|^{2}ds\,d\rho

Corollary 4
.

Suppose that the spectral measure
\sigma
belongs to the Szegő class and the function
a
is real-valued. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}P_{*}(\rho,i)\,d\rho=\Pi(i).

Proof.

Substituting
i
for
\lambda
in (
8
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Pi(i)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left[\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{(1+si)\ln\sigma^{\prime}(s)}{(i-s)(1+s^{2})}\,ds\right]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left[\frac{-1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\ln\sigma^{\prime}(s)}{(1+s^{2})}\,ds\right].

Hence,
\Pi(i)
is a positive real number. System (
4

\lambda=i
has real coefficients, real initial condition and consequently the solution
P_{*}(r,i)
is real for every

. By Corollary
2
,
P_{*}(r,i)
does not equal zero for

P_{*}(0,i)>0
. Therefore,
P_{*}(r,i)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}P_{*}(\rho,i)\,d\rho=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\left|P_{*}(\rho,i)\right|\,d\rho=\left|\Pi(i)\right|=\Pi(i).

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Consider a family
\mathcal{F}
of the analytic functions

\displaystyle F_{r}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}P_{*}(\rho,\lambda)\,d\rho,\quad r>1.

Notice that

(45)

\displaystyle|F_{r}(\lambda)|=\left|\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}P_{*}(\rho,\lambda)\,d\rho\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\left|P_{*}(\rho,\lambda)\right|\,d\rho\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{ineq_for_F_r_lemma}}}{{\leqslant}}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{2r\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits(\lambda)}}|\Pi(\lambda)|.

Consider an arbitrary compact set
D\subset\mathbb{C}_{+}
.
\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits(\lambda)
is separated from

in

so

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda\in D,r}|F_{r}(\lambda)|\leqslant\sup_{\lambda\in D}c(\lambda)|\Pi(\lambda)|<\infty.

Hence, the family
\mathcal{F}
is uniformly bounded on compact subsets and, by Montel theorem, every subset of
\mathcal{F}
has a convergent subsequence.
Let a sequence
r_{n}\to\infty
be such that a sequence
F_{r_{n}}
is convergent and
\lim F_{r_{n}}=\tilde{F}
.
Inequality (
45
) gives

\displaystyle|\tilde{F}(\lambda)|=\lim_{n\to\infty}|F_{r_{n}}(\lambda)|\leqslant|\Pi(\lambda)|

for every point
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
. On the other hand, from corollary
4

\displaystyle\tilde{F}(i)=\lim_{n\to\infty}F_{r_{n}}(i)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{n}}\int_{0}^{r_{n}}P_{*}(\rho,i)\,d\rho=\Pi(i).

Applying the maximum modulus principle, we conclude that
\tilde{F}(\lambda)=\Pi(\lambda)
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
.
Any partial limit of
F_{r}
as
