Balanced flows for transshipment problems
Vladimir Gurvich

Abstract
A transshipment problem ( G , d , λ ) (G,d,\lambda) is modeled by a directed graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) with weighted vertices (nodes) d = ( d v ∣ v ∈ V ) d=(d_{v}\mid v\in V) and weighted directed edges (arcs) λ = ( λ e ∣ e ∈ E ) \lambda=(\lambda_{e}\mid e\in E) interpreted as follows: G G is a communication or transportation network, for example, a pipeline; each arc e ∈ E e\in E is a one-way communication line, road or pipe of capacity λ e \lambda_{e} , while every vertex v ∈ V v\in V is a node of production d v > 0 d_{v}>0 , consumption d v < 0 d_{v}<0 , or just transition d v = 0 d_{v}=0 . A non-negative flow x = ( x e ∣ e ∈ E ) x=(x_{e}\mid e\in E) is called weakly feasible if for each v ∈ V v\in V the algebraic sum of flows, over all directed edges incident to v v , equals d v d_{v} , or shorter, if A G ​ x = d A_{G}x=d , where A G A_{G} is the vertex-edge incidence matrix of G G . A weakly feasible flow x x is called feasible if x e ≤ λ e x_{e}\leq\lambda_{e} for all e ∈ E e\in E . We consider weakly feasible but not necessarily feasible flows, that is, inequalities x e > λ e x_{e}>\lambda_{e} are allowed. However, such an excess is viewed as unwanted (dangerous) and so we minimize the excess ratio vector r = ( r e = x e / λ e ∣ e ∈ E ) r=(r_{e}=x_{e}/\lambda_{e}\mid e\in E) lexicographically. More precisely, first, we look for weakly feasible flows minimizing the maximum of r e r_{e} over all e ∈ E e\in E ; among all such flows we look for those that minimize the second largest coordinate of r r , etc. Clearly, in | E | |E| such steps obtain a unique lexmin vector r r . We will show that the corresponding balanced flow is also unique and represents the lexmin solution for problem ( G , d , λ ) (G,d,\lambda) . Using the Gale-Hoffman inequalities, we construct it in polynomial time, provided vectors d d and λ \lambda are rational. For symmetric digraphs the problem was solved by Gurvich and Gvishiani in 1984. Here we extend this result to arbitrary directed graphs. Furthermore, we simplify the algorithm and proofs applying the classic criterion of existence of a feasible flow for ( G , d , λ ) (G,d,\lambda) obtained by Gale and Hoffman in late 1950-s. MSC classes: 90C25, 94C15, 94C99.

Lexmin Linear Programming

For a positive integer
m
define by
[m]=\{1,...,m\}

the set of positive integers up to
m
, by

\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}

the set of nonnegative
m
-vectors, and by
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}

the
m
-vectors whose all coordinates are strictly positive.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
be a convex polyhedral region.
We can view a solution
\mathbf{x}\in K
as a possible operational state of a physical system,
where the components
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{m})
are the corresponding levels of consumption
of certain resources,
i\in[m]
. We shall also consider positive thresholds
\lambda_{i}>0
,
i\in[m]
,
describing the desirable levels of resource consumptions, and
define
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{m})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
. To a feasible vector
\mathbf{x}\in K
we associate

r(\mathbf{x})~=~\left(\frac{x_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}\mid i\in[m]\right)

in which
the ratios
r_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{x_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}
,
i\in[m]
,
describe the utilization levels of resources at the operating state
\mathbf{x}
.
The lower these utilization levels are, the higher the chance that our system can resist
random fluctuations in resource needs, that is,
the higher the reliability of smooth/uninterrupted operations.

We shall consider an equitable lexmin optimization problem for such systems:
we look for feasible solutions
\mathbf{x}\in K
for which the largest component of
r(\mathbf{x})
is as small as possible,
and among those the second largest component of
r(\mathbf{x})
is as small as possible, etc.
To arrive to a precise definition, let us associate to a vector
\mathbf{x}\in K
and real
z\in\mathbb{R}
the
level set size

L(z,\mathbf{x})~=~\left|\{i\in[m]\mid r_{i}(\mathbf{x})~\geq~z\}\right|.

For two vectors
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in K
we say the
\mathbf{x}

precedes

\mathbf{y}
(
\mathbf{x}\prec\mathbf{y}
)
if there exists a real threshold
z\in\mathbb{R}
such that
L(z,\mathbf{x})<L(z,\mathbf{y})
and
L(z^{\prime},\mathbf{x})=L(z^{\prime},\mathbf{y})
for all
z^{\prime}>z
.
Note first that this relation is a pre-order, which is ”almost” a complete order:

Proposition 1
.

If for two distinct vectors
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in K
we have neither
\mathbf{x}\prec\mathbf{y}
nor
\mathbf{y}\prec\mathbf{x}

then the components of
r(\mathbf{x})
are a permutations of the components of
r(\mathbf{y})
.
Furthermore, in such a case we have both
\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}\prec\mathbf{x}
and
\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}\prec\mathbf{y}
.

Proof.

This is an easy consequence of the definitions.
∎

It is also easily seen that it implies the uniqueness of such a lexmin solution:

Corollary 1
.

For every convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq}^{m}
and positive threshold vector
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}

there exists a unique vector
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in K
such that
\mathbf{x}^{*}\prec\mathbf{y}
for all
\mathbf{y}\in K
,
\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{x}^{*}
.

The algorithmic problem of computing
\mathbf{x}^{*}

(or an approximation of it) was considered in many special cases,
frequently motivated by multi-objective optimization,

[
2
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
.
An equivalent formulation was considered by Nace and Orlin in
[
16
]

who showed that

can be computed by solving a series of linear programs,
whenever

is the set of nonnegative feasible solutions
to a system of linear equalities and inequalities with rational coefficients.

In what follows, we focus on transshipment problems and show that

can be derived by a much simpler polynomial time algorithm,
based on the characterization of feasibility of such problems
by Gale and Hoffman in
[
5
,
11
]
.
This algorithm implies also the uniqueness of the lexmin solution
independently of the above arguments.

For symmetric directed graphs
(or in other words, for undirected graphs)
the above problem was solved much earlier, in
[
8
]
.
The present work can be viewed as an extension of those results
to arbitrary directed graphs.

2
Lexmin Transshipment Problems

Let
G=(V,E)
be a directed graph (digraph)
with weighted vertices (nodes)
d=(d_{v}\mid v\in V)
and
with capacities on the directed edges (arcs)
\lambda=(\lambda_{e}\mid e\in E)
.
Let us index sets
V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
and
E=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}

arbitrarily to make
d
and
\lambda
vectors.
We interpret
G
as a communication or transportation network,
(for example, a pipeline) in which every vertex
v\in V

is a node of production if
d_{v}>0
,
or consumption if
d_{v}<0
, or
just transition
d_{v}=0
of some material
(in particular, information), while
every edge
e=(u,v)\in E
is a one-way
communication line, road, or pipe of capacity
\lambda_{e}>0
.
We call vertex
u
the
tail
of arc
e=(u,v)
and
v
is called its
head
.
We assume that the process is stationary, that is,
the same amount of material
is produced, consumed, and transported in a unit time.
A triplet
(G,d,\lambda)
defines a
transhipment problem
.
We will search a solution of this problem among
nonnegative
flow
vectors
x=(x_{e}\mid e\in E)
, where

x_{e}\geq 0
is interpreted
as the amount of material (information) transported along
arc
e
in a unit time.
It will be convenient to introduce the vector of ratios

r=(r_{e}=x_{e}/\lambda_{e}\mid e\in E)
.

Feasible, weakly feasible, and balanced flows

We will call flow
x

weakly feasible

(or a
weak solution
) to problem
(G,d,\lambda)

if for each node
v\in V
the algebraic sum of flows
x_{e}

for all directed edges
e
incident to
v
equals
d_{v}
.
To formulate this more accurately
recall the definition of the
node-arc incidence

|V|\times|E|

matrix

A_{G}
of
G
, in which
the entries
inc(v,e)
take values
\pm 1
and
0

according to the following definition:

\mbox{inc}(v,e)=\left\{\begin{aligned} +1&,&&\text{if node}\;\;v\ \;\;\text{is the tail of}\;\;e;\\
-1&,&&\text{if node}\;\;v\ \;\;\text{is the head of}\;\;e;\\
0&,&&\text{if}\;\;v\;\;\text{and}\;\;e\text{\;\; are not incident}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

Thus, a flow
x
is weakly feasible for

(G,d,\lambda)
if and only if

A_{G}\;x=d.

(2)

Clearly, problem
(G,d,\lambda)
may have a weakly feasible solution
only if
\sum_{v\in V}d_{v}=0
.
We will always assume this equality in the sequel.

Furthermore, a flow
x
is called
feasible

(or a
solution
) to problem
(G,d,\lambda)

if it is weakly feasible and
x\leq\lambda
,
that is,
0\leq x_{e}\leq\lambda_{e}
for all
e\in E
.

In this paper we study weakly feasible flows
that are not necessarily feasible;
in other words, inequality
x_{e}>\lambda_{e}
,
or equivalently,
r_{e}>1
, may hold for some
(maybe, for all) arcs
e\in E
.
Yet, we view large ratios
r_{e}
as unwanted
(potential sources of jams or accidents)
and try to reduce them by minimizing

r=(r_{e}=x_{e}/\lambda_{e}\mid e\in E)
lexicographically,
as defined in Section
1
:
Consider all weakly feasible flows
minimizing
r^{0}=\max(r_{e}\mid e\in E)
;
among them consider those that minimize the second largest ratio
r^{1}

(equality
r^{0}=r^{1}
may hold); etc.
In
|E|
iterations we obtain a unique lexmin vector
r^{*}
.
However, this does not yet imply the uniqueness of
the corresponding lexmin flow vector
(which will be proven in Section
4
; see also Section
1
).
Meanwhile, we shall assume that there may be several such vectors and
call each of them a
balanced
flow,
or a
lexmin solution
to problem
(G,d,\lambda)
).
By definition, a balanced flow is weakly feasible
and it is feasible whenever a feasible flow exists.

Note that in the latter case the lexicographical minimization is
not that well motivated.
Still, it makes sense, e.g., for a telecommunication network, where
the operator needs to define a routing with respect to a given
traffic demand such that
the network load is fairly distributed among the network links;
see, e.g.,
[
16
]
for more details and applications.

We will construct a balanced flow
by a simple iterative algorithm
based on the Gale
[
5
]
and Hoffman
[
11
]

criterion of solvability of transshipment problems and on an arbitrary polynomial time maximum flow algorithm.
Thus, our algorithm is polynomial too
whenever the input vectors
d
and
\lambda
are rational.

Cuts

A cut
C
in a digraph
G=(V,E)
is defined
as a proper partition of
V
;
in other words, it is an ordered pair of node-sets

C=(V^{\prime},V^{\prime\prime})
such that
V^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
,
V^{\prime\prime}\neq\emptyset
,

V^{\prime}\cap V^{\prime\prime}=\emptyset
, and
V^{\prime}\cup V^{\prime\prime}=V
.

We say that an arc
e=(v^{\prime},v^{\prime\prime})
is in
C

if
v^{\prime}\in V^{\prime}
and
v^{\prime\prime}\in V^{\prime\prime}
.
Obviously, each cut
C
is uniquely defined by the set
E_{C}
of its arcs,
but not every subset
E^{\prime}\subseteq E
is associated with a cut.
For each cut
C
we introduce the following real numbers

deficiency

d_{C}
,
capacity

\lambda_{C}
,
ratio

r_{C}
, and
flow

x_{C}
,
as follows:

\displaystyle d_{C}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V^{\prime}}d_{v}=-\sum_{v\in V^{\prime\prime}}d_{v},

(3)

\displaystyle\lambda_{C}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E_{C}}\lambda_{e},

\displaystyle r_{C}

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{C}}{\lambda_{C}},\mbox{ and}

\displaystyle x_{C}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E_{C}}x_{e}.

Consider a problem
(G,d,\lambda)
with positive capacities, and a cut
C
.
Obviously,
\lambda_{C}=0
if and only if
E_{C}=\emptyset
.
A cut
C
is called
fatal
if

d_{c}>0
while
\lambda_{C}=0
;
in this case
r_{C}=+\infty
.
A cut
C
is called
deficient
if
d_{C}>\lambda_{C}
,
or equivalently,
r_{C}>1
.
A cut realizing
\max(r_{C}\mid C\in G)
is called
critical
.
Note that such a cut always exists, unlike
a fatal or a deficient cut.
Obviously, a fatal cut (
r_{C}=+\infty
)
is deficient (
r_{C}>1
).
A critical cut is also deficient
whenever a latter exists.
Note finally that
d_{C}
is positive
for all three considered types of cuts.

In Section
3
it will be shown that
problem
(G,d,\lambda)
is weakly solvable
(resp., solvable) if and only if it has
no fatal (resp., deficient) cut.

How to obtain the balanced flow? A plan

First we will give a polynomial algorithm
constructing a critical cut
C^{0}
.
Obviously,
\max(r_{e}\mid e\in E)=r^{0}\geq r_{C^{0}}
.
Yet, we hope that equality
r^{0}=r_{C^{0}}

will hold for the balanced flow
x^{*}
.
Obviously, we must load all edges of cut
C^{0}
uniformly:

x^{*}_{e}=r_{C^{0}}\lambda_{C^{0}}
for all
e\in E_{C^{0}}
.
(This is why flow
x^{*}
is called “balanced”.)
Then, flow
x^{*}_{C^{0}}
through
C^{0}

will be equal to
\sum_{e\in C^{0}}r_{C^{0}}\lambda_{e}=d_{C^{0}}
,
as required, and
r_{e}=r_{C^{0}}
for all
e\in C^{0}
.
Otherwise, whenever edges of
C^{0}
are loaded not uniformly,

r_{e}
will exceed
r_{C^{0}}
for some
e\in C^{0}
,
contradicting the assumption that
x^{*}
is the balanced flow.

Since
x^{*}_{e}
are uniquely determined for
e\in E_{C^{0}}
,
we can delete all these edges from digraph
G
and
recompute vector
d
accordingly.
Thus, the original problem
(G,d,\lambda)=(G^{0},d^{0},\lambda^{0})

is reduced to
(G^{1},d^{1},\lambda^{1})
.
Repeat the same procedure for this new problem,
getting a critical cut
C^{1}
and
r^{1}=r_{C^{1}}
; etc.
In at most
|E|
steps flow
x^{*}
will be determined
for all edges of digraph
G
.

Clearly, inequality
r^{0}=r_{C^{0}}\geq r_{C^{1}}=r^{1}

would justify this iterative procedure.
Indeed, it implies monotonicity:
r^{0}\geq r^{1}\geq\ldots
,
which in its turn immediately implies that
x^{*}

is the (unique) balanced flow.
However, if we could have
r_{C^{1}}>r_{C^{0}}
then
it may be not optimal to fix the uniform flow through
C^{0}
.

Inequality
r_{C^{0}}\geq r_{C^{1}}
could be verified
by a direct computation, as it was done in
[
8
]

for the case of symmetric digraphs.
Yet Endre Boros noticed that
it is simpler to derive this inequality from
the Gale-Hoffman criterion of solvability
[
5
,
11
]
.
This plan is realized in the next two sections.

3
Gale-Hoffman’s inequalities; a criterion
of solvability for transshipment problems

Clearly, problem
(G,d,\lambda)
has no weak solution
if it contains a fatal cut.
Otherwise, if there are no fatal cuts,
in the next section we will construct the balanced flow,
which is weakly feasible by definition.
Thus, the following criterion holds.

Proposition 2
.

Problem
(G,d,\lambda)
is weakly solvable
if and only if it contains no fatal cut.
∎

In late 1950-s Gale
[
5
]
and Hoffman
[
11
]

obtained a simple criterion of weak solvability.
It is based on a reduction of the problem
to a two-pole network problem and
applying the
max flow - min cut theorem
to it;
see
[
3
]
and also
[
4
]
.

To each transshipment problem
(G,d,\lambda)

we assign a two-pole network problem

(G,d,\lambda;s,t)=(G^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})
as follows. Define

V^{+}=\{v\in V\mid d_{v}>0\}
,

V^{-}=\{v\in V\mid d_{v}<0\}
,
and
D=\sum_{v\in V^{+}}d_{v}=-\sum_{v\in V^{-}}d_{v}
.
Add to
G
two new nodes
s
and
t

and also a directed edge

e_{u}=(s,u)

of capacity
\lambda_{e_{u}}=d_{u}
from
s
to each
u\in V^{+}
;

e_{w}=(w,t)
of capacity
\lambda_{e_{w}}=-d_{w}

from each
w\in V^{-}
to
t
.

By definition, all these capacities are positive.

The obtained two-pole network
(G,d,\lambda;s,t)=(G^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})

has two special cuts

C_{s}:\{s\}-(V\cup\{t\})
and

C_{t}:(\{s\}\cup V)-\{t\}

of capacity
D
each.
Clearly, the set of all other cuts in
(G^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})

is in one-to-one correspondence with the set of cuts of
(G,d,\lambda)
.
Furthermore, it is easy to verify that

\lambda^{\prime}_{C^{\prime}}=D+(\lambda_{C}-d_{C})

for every cut
C
of
(G,d,\lambda)
and the corresponding
cut
C^{\prime}
of
(G^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})
.
Thus, the capacity of a minimum cut in
the two-pole network
(G^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})

(which is equal to the value
of a maximum flow from
s
to
t
in it) is

c(G,d,\lambda;s,t)=\min(D,D+(\lambda_{C}-d_{C})\mid C\in G).

By definition, it takes value
D
if and only if

\lambda_{C}\geq d_{C}
for every cut
C
in
G
.
Hence,
(G,d,\lambda)
is solvable if and only if the capacity

c(G,d,\lambda;s,t)
of the corresponding two-pole network equals
D
.
Thus, we obtain the following statement.

Theorem 1
.

(
[
5
,
11
]
)

Problem
(G,d,\lambda)
is solvable
if and only if it contains no deficient cut.
∎

In other words, inequalities
d_{C}\leq\lambda_{C}
,
or equivalently
r_{C}\leq 1
, must hold for every cut
C
in
G
.
These theorem and inequalities are referred to as

the Gale-Hoffman criterion and inequalities

for transshipment problems.

Note that
d_{C}\leq\lambda_{C}
holds automatically
if
d_{C}\leq 0
, since by ddefinition,
0\leq\lambda_{C}
for any cut
C
.

To verify solvability of
(G,d,\lambda)

we can apply any max-flow algorithm
to the corresponding two-pole network
(G,d,\lambda;s,t)=(G^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})
and
compute its capacity
c=c(G^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})
by the algorithm.
if
c=D
, the answer is positive and
(G,d,\lambda)
is solvable;
otherwise, if
c<D
, then the answer is negative and
(G,d,\lambda)
is not solvable.
In the latter case the algorithm outputs
a deficient cut
C^{0}
(such that
d_{C^{0}}>\lambda_{C^{0}}
).
Moreover,
C^{0}
maximizes
d_{C}-\lambda_{C}
among all cuts of
G
.
It is important to remark, however, that
C^{0}

may not maximize the ratio
r_{C}=d_{C}/\lambda_{C}
.

Note also that the above algorithm is polynomial,
provided all parameters
d
and
\lambda
are rational.
This follows immediately from
the similar claim for the Ford-Fulkerson maxflow- mincut algorithm.

Solving parametrical problem
(G,d,z\lambda)

to construct a balanced flow

Verifying weak solvability

Let us parametrize the capacities and consider

(G,d,z\lambda)

If there exists a fatal cut

implying that

To verify the existence of a fatal cut

Fix a large
z
, say
z=M=D/\min(\lambda_{e}\mid e\in E)
.
Consider the two-pole network with
capacities
M\lambda_{e}
for all
e\in E
.
(Note that capacities

\lambda_{e_{u}}=d_{u}
for every

and

\lambda_{e_{w}}=-d_{w}
for each

do not depend on

A fatal flow exists in

if and only if

is strictly less than

From now on, assume that there is no fatal cut in
(G,d,\lambda)
,
that is,

in

.

Minmax ratio and flows

Given a weakly feasible flow

(G,d,\lambda)

maximize the ratio

for

and then minimize these maxima over all weakly feasible flows

r^{0}=\min(\max(r_{e})\mid e\in E)\mid\mbox{ over all weakly feasible flows in}\;(G,d,\lambda)).

(4)

Number

and a weakly feasible flow realizing

will be called a

In particular, the balanced flow

has this property.

The next claim follows immediately from Gale-Hoffman’s Theorem.

Corollary 2
.

Problem
(G,d,z\lambda)
is solvable if and only if

.
∎

To compute

in polynomial time,
consider networks
(G,d,z\lambda)
and
the corresponding two-pole networks

.
(Again,

\lambda_{e_{u}}=d_{u}
for each

, independently of

Apply the dichotomy to

and a max-flow algorithm
to determine the minimum

such that problem

is solvable.
Obviously, this minimum

is exactly

.
This procedure is polynomial provided vectors

and
\lambda

are rational.

Critical cuts

Recall definitions given by formulas of (

).
A cut
C^{0}
maximizing the ratio
r_{C}
over all cuts of
G

is called
critical
for
(G,d,\lambda)
.

The next two statement related to flows, cuts, and ratios of
a problem
(G,d,\lambda)
will be important.
The first one is an immediate consequence of (

) and Theorem

Corollary 3
.

The ratio of a critical cut

and the minmax ratio

are equal:

r_{C^{0}}=\max(r_{C}\mid C

G)=r^{0}
.
∎

Proposition 3
.

Every weakly feasible minmax flow

Proof.

If
x_{e}=r^{0}\lambda_{e}
for every

x_{C^{0}}=\sum_{e\in E_{C^{0}}}x_{e}=r^{0}\sum_{e\in E_{C^{0}}}\lambda_{e}=r^{0}\lambda_{C^{0}}=d_{C^{0}}.

Equality

must hold for any weakly feasible flow and cut

,
in particular, for a critical cut
C^{0}
.
In the latter case,
if
x_{e^{\prime}}<r^{0}\lambda_{e^{\prime}}
holds for some

then

x_{e^{\prime\prime}}>r^{0}\lambda_{e^{\prime\prime}}
will hold for some other
e^{\prime\prime}\in E_{C^{0}}
,
and thus,
x
is not a minmax flow.
This contradiction proves the claim.
∎

To find a critical cut for
(G,d,\lambda)
,
consider problem
(G,d,r^{0}\lambda)
.
By Corollary
2
, for this problem we have:

r_{C}\leq 1
for every cut
C
and
there is a cut
C^{0}
with
r_{C^{0}}=1
.
There may exist several such cuts and
each of them is critical, that is,
its ratio will exceed
1

whenever we replace factor
r^{0}
by
r^{0}-\epsilon
,
with an arbitrarily small positive
\epsilon
.

Consider problem
(G,d,z\lambda)
with

z=r^{0}-1/M
and apply a max-flow algorithm
to the corresponding two-pole problem.
We obtain a cut
C^{0}
that is critical for
(G,d,z\lambda)

and for
(G,d,\lambda)
, too.

Constructing a (unique) balanced flow

Given, a problem
(G,d,\lambda)
,
find in polynomial time
r^{0}
and a critical cut
C^{0}
.
(Note that
r^{0}
is well-defined, but there may be several critical cuts
whose edge-sets differ.)
By Corollary
3
, the ratios are equal:
r^{0}=r_{C^{0}}
.
Each weakly feasible minmax ratio
x
must load
C^{0}
uniformly,
that is,
x_{e}=r^{0}\lambda_{e}
must hold for every
e\in C^{0}
.
Then, equality
x_{C^{0}}=d_{C^{0}}
holds.

Since we know exactly the values of
x_{e}
for each
e\in E_{C^{0}}
,
we can eliminate
E_{C^{0}}
from
E
and recompute vector
d
accordingly.
Thus, we replace the original problem
(G,d,\lambda)=(G^{0},d^{0},\lambda^{0})

by a new one
(G^{1},d^{1},\lambda^{1}).

Proposition 4
.

Inequality
r^{1}\leq r^{0}
holds.
Moreover, the equality
r^{1}=r^{0}
holds whenever
the critical cut
C^{0}
was not unique
and
E_{C^{0^{\prime}}}\setminus E_{C^{0}}\neq\emptyset

for some other critical cut
C^{0^{\prime}}
in
(G,d,\lambda)
.

In
[
8
]
this statement was proven
for symmetric digraphs by arithmetical computations.
In fact, only a sketch of a proof was given,
because the volume of the paper was strictly limited
by the rules of the journal.
However, such computations are not needed, since both statements
of Proposition
4
follow from the Gale-Shapley Theorem
and its corollaries given above.

Proof.

Suppose that
r^{1}>r^{0}
.
Then for a critical cut
C^{1}
in
(G^{1},d^{1},\lambda^{1})

we have
r_{C^{1}}=r^{1}>r^{0}
.
Hence, for a weakly feasible minmax flow
x

in
(G,d,\lambda)

there is an edge
e\in E_{C^{1}}
such

x_{e}/\lambda_{e}\geq r_{1}>r_{0}
, which contradicts
the definition of
r_{0}
.

Suppose that
r^{1}<r^{0}
, while
there exists an edge

e_{0}\in E_{C^{0^{\prime}}}\setminus E_{C^{0}}
.
Then, on the first iteration we could choose
the critical cut
C^{0^{\prime}}
rather than
C^{0}

and, as we know, equality
x_{e_{0}}=r^{0}\lambda_{e_{0}}

must hold for every weakly feasible minmax ratio
x
,
in particular, for each balanced flow.
Yet, this is not possible if
r^{1}<r^{0}
, which is a contradiction.
∎

This proposition implies that
in fact we must fix
x_{e}=r^{0}\lambda_{e}

for every edge
e
that belongs to a critical cut.
Yet, the result will be the same if we consider
all these cuts together or one by one.
For example, after eliminating
E_{C^{0}}
,
we can repeat the procedure with the same factor
z=r^{0}-1/M

searching for another critical cut
C^{0^{\prime}}

with the same ratio
r_{C^{0^{\prime}}}=r_{C^{0}}=r_{0}
.
In there is one, we fix

x^{*}_{e}=r^{0}\lambda_{e}
for all
e\in E_{C^{0^{\prime}}}
and repeat.
If there is no one then
r*1<r^{0}

and we search for
r^{1}
applying the dichotomy to the reduced problem.
In particular, these arguments imply uniqueness of the balanced flow.

Corollary 4
.

Each problem
(G,d,\lambda)
has a unique lexmin solution.
∎

By definition, the balanced flow
x^{*}
remains
a weakly feasible minmax flow on every iteration.
Thus, we can repeat the above procedure and
in at most
|E|
iterations
we will determine a flow
x^{*}_{e}
on every edge
e\in E
.
If vectors
d
and
\lambda
are rational then every iteration is known to be polynomial
and, hence, the whole procedure is polynomial too.

Symmetric digraphs

A digraph
G=(V,E)
with capacities
\lambda
is called
symmetric

if to each edge
e^{\prime}=(u,w)\in E
of capacity
\lambda_{e^{\prime}}
,
a unique oppositely directed edge

e^{\prime\prime}=(w,u)\in E
of the same capacity,
\lambda_{e^{\prime\prime}}=\lambda_{e^{\prime}}
, is assigned.
A symmetric digraph can be replaced by a non-directed graph.
In this case the balanced flow was defined and constructed
by an iterative polynomial algorithm in
[
8
]
.
Note, however, that in that paper
the key inequality
r^{0}\geq r^{1}
was not derived
from the Gale-Hoffman criterion.
Instead it was proved, by a pretty long algebraic computation,
which was only sketched in the paper,
since its size was limited in accordance with the rules of the journal.
