Fair Reward Distribution in Federated Byzantine Agreement Systems
Charmaine Ndolo 1 , Martin Florian 2 and Florian Tschorsch 3

Abstract
FBAS ( FBAS ) offer a solution to consensus in permissionless systems by adapting the well-studied Byzantine agreement model to permissionless consensus. Unlike its counterparts in the context of permissionless consensus, the FBAS system model does not offer validating nodes protocol-level incentives although they are entrusted with safeguarding and ensuring the functionality of the system. Multiple studies have reported on the small number of active validators in these systems leading to some concerns about their resilience. To this end, this paper studies how rewards can be distributed in FBAS and presents a fair reward distribution function for FBAS . The challenge is that, on the one hand, consensus in an FBAS is found jointly between all nodes and, on the other hand, nodes do not all contribute equally to this process. We draw on game-theoretic methods to quantify these contributions bearing the overall health of the FBAS in mind and present a fair reward distribution function which we evaluate based on a set of identified properties.

That is, each node is required to define a set of nodes

The

In Bitcoin

Recent studies on the Stellar and MobileCoin networks

either operated by the networks’ maintainers or organizations with economic
interests in the network.
As suggested in

serves as an implicit incentive for operators to join the network with
validating nodes.
However, in the case of the currently deployed FBAS-based networks, implicit
incentives are only sufficient to attract a fairly small and constant group of
entities.
We therefore maintain that explicit incentives are necessary not only for

leading to decentralization.
In addition, validator rewards can also cover

[
8

6
]
involved in maintaining a

While numerous previous works deal with incentivization in blockchain-based

systems, to the best of our knowledge, incentivization in

A great part of the literature on

[

,

,
4

In this work, we present a reward distribution function for

Hence, we investigate how rewards can be distributed fairly while taking into

[

,

We define a set of requirements a reward distribution function for an

Our incentivization scheme is founded on determining each node’s relative

To this end, we provide the following main contributions:

We map the

•

We identify a set of requirements for a distribution function for

We implement the reward distribution function, evaluate it formally and

II-B

Problem Statement and System Model

The networks Stellar and MobileCoin both register relatively modest numbers of
active validators
[
4
,
6
]
, i.e., 31 and 10
active validators respectively at the time of publication.
Kim et al.
[
4
]
presume
that the underwhelming participation is because of the lack of explicit
incentives and argue that an increase in the number of nodes leads to a more
secure network.

Since we concur with the analysis by Kim et al., our goal is to design a fair
reward distribution function for
FBAS
.
This function should be independent of a particular consensus protocol used by
a specific
FBAS
instance and should reward nodes relative to their
contribution to the system’s health (liveness).
We thus strive to define a reward distribution function that determines the
relative share of the reward a node
i
is entitled to receive per reward
period.
Any reward function requires an
FBAS
(
\V,\Q
) as input.
Neither the actual value of the reward nor the source of the funds is subject of
this work, but pose interesting economic questions for future works.

We assume that the
FBAS

(\V,\Q)
has quorum intersection as
FBAS

without quorum intersection cannot guarantee safety making forks and
double-spend attacks possible
[
1
,
8
]
.
One reason for this assumption is that it is generally not possible to
distinguish an
FBAS
that has accidentally lost safety from one that is
undergoing a legitimate split.
In general, an
FBAS
without quorum intersection can be decomposed into
smaller
FBAS
which enjoy quorum intersection independently.
This fact can be leveraged in practical deployments
when distributing rewards even if quorum intersection is lacking.

Furthermore, we assume a
“global”
view of the
FBAS

in that every node in
\V
is in agreement about
(\V,\Q)
.
This simplification is necessary to guarantee a reward distribution that is
correct and verifiable for all nodes in the
FBAS
.
It enables each node to compute its legitimate share of the reward locally.
In practice, it is plausible to assume that each node
has complete knowledge of the
FBAS
given the availability and
instrumentality of such information when bootstrapping a node.
Snapshots of
(\V,\Q)
could also be included in new blocks,
which are in turn agreed upon using a consensus protocol
such as
SCP
.

II-C

Requirements for Reward Distribution Functions

In the following,
we define a set of properties a reward distribution function
for nodes of an
FBAS
should ideally fulfill.
This set of properties describes the basic characteristics of a sound reward
distribution scheme.
Let
i,j\in\V
and
dist{(}i)\colon\V\mapsto[0,1]
be a reward distribution function for the

FBAS

(\V,\Q)
.
We classify
dist{(}i)
as a fair distribution function if it meets the following criteria:

1.

Symmetry
: Let (
\V,\Q^{\prime}
) be a modified
FBAS
computed from

(\V,\Q)
by substituting every mention of
i
in
\Q
for
j
and vice versa and
additionally interchanging
\Q(i)
and
\Q(j)
.
If
\Q
and
\Q^{\prime}
are equal, we define
i
and
j
as symmetric to one another as
\Q
and

\Q^{\prime}
can only be equal if
\Q(i)
and
\Q(j)
are identical.
In this case, the distribution function is
symmetric
iff
dist{(}i)=dist{(}j)
.

For example, in a symmetric
FBAS

(\V,\Q)
where
\V=\{0,1,2\}
and

\forall k\in\V\colon\Q(k)=(\{\V\},\emptyset,1)
swapping nodes
0
and
1
results in
\forall k\in\V\colon\Q^{\prime}(k)=(\{1,0,2\},\emptyset,1)
.
In this case, switching
\Q(0)
and
\Q(1)
has no effect as all
\Q(k)
are the same.

\Q
and
\Q^{\prime}
are equal; hence,
0
and
1
are also symmetric in
(\V,\Q)
.

2.

Freeloader freeness
: Let
\hat{U_{i}}
be the set of all minimal
quorums in
(\V,\Q)
that contains
i
.
If
\hat{U_{i}}=\emptyset
then
dist{(}i)
is
freeloader-free
iff
dist{(}i)=0
.
Clearly
i
does not play a role in upholding the liveness of the

FBAS
.
Given that
i
can leave and join the
FBAS
without consequences for the
FBAS
,

dist{(}i)
should be
0
.

3.

Computational feasibility
: If
dist{(}i)
can be computed in
polynomial time with respect to the number of nodes in the
FBAS
,
dist{(}i)
is

computationally feasible
.

4.

Correctness
: Assuming knowledge of
i
’s expected payoff
p
,
then
dist{(}i)
is
correct
iff
dist{(}i)=p
.
In anticipation of
Section

III
, this is property is relevant when
a distribution is approximated using probabilistic methods.

III
Fair Distribution of Rewards

Having established a set of desirable characteristics of reward functions, we
now realize a reward distribution mechanism founded on
CGT
(
CGT
).
We propose to measure a node’s contribution to an
FBAS
using the
Shapley value
and
allocate rewards based on the computed values.
Although the Shapley value is one of multiple definitions of fairness, we choose
to use it as it is the only function that not only satisfies our symmetry and
freeloader freeness requirements, but also yields efficient and additive
distributions.
To this end, we recapitulate select formal notions surrounding cooperative games
and present our first contribution where we model an
FBAS
as a cooperative game.
Subsequently, we define the reward distribution function
using the cooperative game model.
We also show that only a subset of nodes has non-zero Shapley values in the defined game.
This serves as an important observation as it enables an optimization for the
computation of Shapley values for larger
FBAS
.

III-A

Cooperative Game Theory

Game theory models strategic interactions between
players
as a

game
.

CGT
assumes that players benefit from cooperation as opposed to acting
individually and that the incentive to cooperate is great enough such that there
is no disadvantage in forming a single group consisting of all the
players
[
12
]
.
A fundamental problem in
CGT
is the
fair
distribution of

collective payoffs
among the players.
To this end, let
\players=\{1,2,...,n\}
denote the set of players
with
n\geq 1
.

Definition 5

(Coalition
[
13
]
)
.

A
coalition

\coalition
is a subset
\coalition\subseteq\players
of the set of
players.
The
grand coalition

\players
is the coalition of all the players. As there are
2^{n}

possible subsets of
\players
, there are also
2^{n}
possible coalitions.

As players are always assumed to be
rational
(and not
altruistic
),
a player will only join a coalition,
if the player’s membership in the coalition is of benefit to the player.

Definition 6

(Characteristic function of an
n
-player cooperative
game
[
13
]
)
.

Any function
\charfunc\colon 2^{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\charfunc(\emptyset)=0
and
\charfunc(\players)\geq\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1}\charfunc(\{i\})

is a characteristic function of an

n
-player cooperative game.

The characteristic function
\charfunc(\coalition)
quantifies the coalition
\coalition
’s

worth
using a real-valued function.
The worth of the empty coalition is zero,
whereas the worth of the grand coalition
\players
must be at
least equal to the sum of the individual payoffs.
This underpins the assumption that forming the grand coalition is not penalized
and may in fact be incentivized.

Definition 7

(Cooperative Game with transferable utility,
i.e., TU game
[
12
]
)
.

A

game
G(\players,\charfunc)
consists of a set of players
\players
and
a characteristic function
v\colon 2^{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
.

As per
Definition

6

every coalition
\coalition
that may form within the game.
A coalition’s worth can be interpreted as the total
payoff
or
utility
that may be
freely distributed among the members of the coalition.
Such a game is said to have
transferable payoff
,
i.e., a coalition’s payoff may be distributed
in multiple ways among the members of the coalition.

Definition 8

(Simple Game
[
13
]
)
.

A

is a cooperative game
G(\players,\charfunc)
in which

\charfunc(\coalition)=1
or
\charfunc(\coalition)=0
for all coalitions in

.
A coalition with
\charfunc(\coalition)=1

\charfunc(\coalition)=0

Simple games can, for instance, be used to model majority voting with
\charfunc(\coalition)=1

\coalition

\charfunc(\coalition)=0

Since the characteristic function
\charfunc(\players)
specifies the grand coalition’s worth,
it is necessary to find some impartial method to divide this value among the
players signifying their contributions to the total worth.
Among other solution concepts
[
14
,
15
]
,
the
Shapley Value

[
16
]

provides a solution for the set of
n
players,
1
1

1

In the presence of smaller coalitions, the same solution concepts can
be applied analogously to the subgames defined by the coalitions.
while
recognizing that players may not be equally valuable to the coalition.
The Shapley value is each player’s expected contribution to any possible
sequence of the
n
players that may come together and form the grand coalition.

Definition 9

(Shapley Value
[
16
]
)
.

An allocation
\shapley(\charfunc)=(\shapley_{1}(\charfunc),...,\shapley_{n}(\charfunc))

\shapley_{i}(\charfunc)=\sum\limits_{\{\coalition\in\Pi^{i}\}}[\charfunc(\coalition)-\charfunc(\coalition\setminus\{i\})]\cdot\cfrac{(|\coalition|-1)!(|\players|-|\coalition|)!}{|\players|!},\ i=1,2,...,|\players|

where
\Pi^{i}
is the set of all coalitions
\coalition\subseteq\players
containing
i
as a
member.

A player
i

i
adds to the
grand coalition when the grand coalition is formed in completely random order.

III-B

FBAS as a Cooperative Game

Cooperation is imperative in the
FBAS
system model: nodes make decisions about which other
nodes they need for consensus.
System-wide properties
such as liveness
are then a result of the sum of these individual
configurations.
We, therefore, argue that an
FBAS
can be modeled as a cooperative game where players coalesce
into coalitions that establish and maintain desirable system properties.

We propose to model an
FBAS

(\V,\Q)
as a cooperative game
G(\players,\charfunc)
with a
characteristic function that uses the set of all quorums
\mathcal{U}
as measure of a coalition’s
value.
For an
FBAS

(\V,\Q)
, a cooperative game
G(\players,\charfunc)
with player

, and an coalition
\coalition\subseteq\players
, we use the following
notation:

\begin{aligned} \mathcal{U}\subseteq 2^{\V}&\colon\quad\text{the set of all quorums in}~(\V,\Q)~\text{as defined in \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{def:quorum}}\\
\charfunc(\coalition)&\colon\quad\text{the worth of the coalition}~\coalition\\
\sspi_{i}&\colon\quad\text{player $i$'s Shapley-Shubik power index}\\
\Pi^{i}\subseteq\Pset&\colon\quad\text{the set of all coalitions containing $i$ as a member}\\
\W^{i}\subseteq\Pi^{i}&\colon\quad\text{the set of all coalitions that win with player~$i$ and lose without $i$,}\\
&\phantom{\colon}\quad\text{i.e.,}\coalition\in\W^{i}\iff\{\coalition\in\Pi^{i}|\charfunc(\coalition)=1,\charfunc(\coalition\setminus\{i\})=0\}\\
\end{aligned}

Given an arbitrary

We define a

U\in\mathcal{U}
with
U\subseteq\coalition

\coalition

coalition if it does not contain a quorum.
Formally, that is

\displaystyle\charfunc(\coalition)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\exists U\in\mathcal{U}\colon U\subseteq\coalition\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(1)

The definition of this characteristic function captures our main design goal:
The set of quorums
\mathcal{U}
contains the sets of nodes in the
FBAS

play a role in upholding liveness of the system.
To this end, forming a coalition in this cooperative game is equivalent to nodes
defining quorum slices that result in a quorum in the corresponding
FBAS

Given that an
FBAS
already enjoys quorum intersection,
we reason that if a coalition
\coalition
has no quorum,
it is of no worth to the
FBAS

\charfunc(\coalition)=0
.
On the other hand, a coalition that has a quorum contributes to a common goal of
the
FBAS
.

III-C

Distribution of Rewards

We now define a function for the distribution of rewards based on the various
coalitions’ worth in the game.
The computation of the distribution is a twofold procedure: we first determine each player’s

“importance”
using the Shapley-Shubik power index and then allocate rewards based on the
computed indices.

III-C
1
The Shapley-Shubik Power Index

Our cooperative game representation of an
FBAS
is a simple game with a
superadditive characteristic function.
That means that the term
[\charfunc(\coalition)-\charfunc(\coalition\setminus\{i\})]
of the
Shapley value function in

9

is always zero or one.
We can, therefore, simplify the Shapley value by
summing over only the coalitions where
[\charfunc(\coalition)-\charfunc(\coalition\setminus\{i\})]
equates to one.
These are coalitions
\coalition\in\W^{i}\subseteq\Pi^{i}
that win with player

i

but
lose without player
i
.
Such a player is known as a
critical
or
pivotal
player as they
turn a losing coalition into a winning coalition when added to the coalition.
The remaining expression is better known as the
Shapley-Shubik power
index

[
17

and defined for a player

as follows.

Definition 10

(The Shapley-Shubik Power
Index
[
13
]
)
.

\sspi_{i}=\sum\limits_{\{\coalition\in\W^{i}\}}\cfrac{(|\coalition|-1)!(|\players|-|\coalition|)!}{|\players|!}\in\left[0,1\right]

III-C
2

Given an
FBAS

(\V,\Q)
in cooperative game form
(\players,\charfunc)

we calculate each player’s power using the Shapley-Shubik power index and obtain
a real-valued index for each player

The set
\W^{i}

all subsets of
\V

The bigger the set of winning coalitions

\sspi_{i}

and consequently,

We accordingly define a reward distribution function

for an

(\V,\Q)

power indices of the corresponding cooperative game
(\players,\charfunc)
, i.e.,

dist{(}i)=\sspi_{i}

(2)

A player

III-E

Computing the Shapley-Shubik Power Index

In order to calculate the Shapley-Shubik power index as defined,
we can use an
enumeration

This algorithm enumerates all
\Pset

(\players,\charfunc)

and determines if a given coalition is

Such enumeration algorithms, however, have an exponential time complexity

number of players increases.
We therefore cannot expect to compute the power indices
efficiently
and
accurately.

In order to tackle the efficiency problem with the enumeration algorithm,
we propose to calculate the Shapley-Shubik power index based
on the random sampling algorithm by Castro et al.

.
The algorithm approximates the Shapley values

Unlike enumeration algorithms, the sampling algorithm approximates players’
Shapley values in polynomial time subject to the efficient computation of the

The proposed characteristic function in

We refer interested readers to works such as
[

Although the sampling algorithm provides asymptotic error bounds,

III-F

Discussion

In the following, we address and discuss limitations of the proposed
distribution function.
A malicious node that is part of the top tier is able to manipulate its own
Shapley value by altering the set of minimal quorums using Sybil nodes.
A malicious node
k
can create a set of
j\geq 1
Sybil nodes
each configured with a single quorum slice
\{i,k\},i\in\{1,...,j\}
.
This results in a higher Shapley value for the malicious node
k
and
consequently, a bigger share of the reward.
The Sybil nodes’ Shapley values will be zero as they are not critical for any
coalition.
An adversarial node outside the top tier is not able to manipulate the
distribution function as the formation of new minimal quorums is not possible
without the cooperation of the existing nodes’ while maintaining
safety
[
8
]
.

We consider the described possibility to be inherent to the
FBAS
model as
there is nothing that prohibits a top tier node from constructing quorum slices
with Sybil nodes.
In fact, we conjecture that it is generally not possible to distinguish between
Sybil and non-Sybil nodes in such cases.
This limitation would therefore apply to any reward distribution approach.
While extending an FBAS-based system with monetary incentives may provide
additional motivation to mount such attacks, it represents an interesting option
towards improving the resilience of some FBAS-based systems.

The practical relevance of such attacks might be limited.
While a Sybil attack can be mounted by any node in the FBAS, it is only
beneficial to a node that is part of the top tier
(cf.
Section

III-D
).
In practice, the top tier of these systems is a fairly fixed set of
nodes that forms over time with new nodes able to join the top tier only with
the cooperation of existing members (see
[
8
]
for
proofs and discussions on the evolution of a top tier).
Node operators in the Stellar and MobileCoin networks exercise off-chain
coordination of aspects pertaining to configurations and monitoring.
This means that a (malicious) node is not simply able to infiltrate the top tier
of such an FBAS.
If the malicious node is already a member of the top tier and has been
identified as such, an attack can be hampered by reconfiguration of the other
top tier nodes facilitating the removal of the attacker from the top tier.

Determining and quantifying the extent to which specific FBAS instances are
vulnerable to reward-inflating Sybil attacks is an interesting research question
for future work.

VI
Conclusion

We formulated a reward distribution function for the
FBAS
model.
We drew on methods from
CGT
and defined a cooperative game representation of an
FBAS

measuring contributions to the attainment of system-wide properties.
In contrast to heuristic ranking approaches, our presented model offers a means to objectively
and precisely quantify a node’s power in an
FBAS
.
By defining a function for the dissemination of rewards, we demonstrated that the

FBAS
model is compatible with incentives.
