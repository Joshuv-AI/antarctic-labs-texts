Abstract
We present a new FPTAS for the Subset Sum Ratio problem, which, given a set of integers, asks for two disjoint subsets such that the ratio of their sums is as close to 1 1 as possible. Our scheme makes use of exact and approximate algorithms for the closely related Partition problem, hence any progress over those—such as the recent improvement due to Bringmann and Nakos [SODA 2021]—carries over to our FPTAS. Depending on the relationship between the size of the input set n n and the error margin ε \varepsilon , we improve upon the best currently known algorithm of Melissinos and Pagourtzis [COCOON 2018] of complexity 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 4 / ε ) \mathcal{O}(n^{4}/\varepsilon) . In particular, the exponent of n n in our proposed scheme may decrease down to 2 2 , depending on the Partition algorithm used. Furthermore, while the aforementioned state of the art complexity expressed in the form 𝒪 ⁡ ( ( n + 1 / ε ) c ) \mathcal{O}((n+1/\varepsilon)^{c}) has constant c = 5 c=5 , our results establish that c < 5 c<5 .

Preliminaries

Let, for
x\in\mathbb{N}
,
[x]=\{0,\ldots,x\}
denote the set of integers in the interval
[0,x]
.
Given a set

If we are additionally given a value

•

The set of its

Note that

•

The set of its
small

In the following, since the values of the associated parameters will be clear from the context,
they will be omitted and we will refer to these sets simply as
L
and
M

Definition 1
(
Partition
)

Given a set
X
, compute a subset
X^{*}_{p}\subseteq X
, such that
\Sigma(X^{*}_{p})=\max\left\{\Sigma(Z)\mid Z\subseteq X,\Sigma(Z)\leq\Sigma(X)/2\right\}
.
Moreover, let
\overline{X^{*}_{p}}=X\setminus X^{*}_{p}
.

Definition 2
(Approximate
Partition
, from
[
27
]
)

Given a set
X
and error margin
\varepsilon
, compute a subset
X_{p}\subseteq X
such that
(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\Sigma(X^{*}_{p})\leq\Sigma(X_{p})\leq\Sigma(X^{*}_{p})
.
Moreover, let
\overline{X_{p}}=X\setminus X_{p}
.

3.2
Solution Involving Exclusively Large Elements

We firstly search for an
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate solution with
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, without involving any of the elements that are smaller than
\varepsilon\cdot a_{n}
.
Let
M=\{a_{i}\in A\mid a_{i}<\varepsilon\cdot a_{n}\}
be the set of small elements and
L=A\setminus M=\{a_{i}\in A\mid a_{i}\geq\varepsilon\cdot a_{n}\}
be the set of large elements.

After partitioning the input set, we split the interval
[0,n\cdot a_{n}]
into smaller intervals, called bins, of size
l=\varepsilon^{2}\cdot a_{n}
each, as depicted in figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Split of the interval
[0,n\cdot a_{n}]
to bins of size
l
.

Thus, there are a total of
B=n/\varepsilon^{2}
bins.
Notice that each possible subset of the input set will belong to a respective bin constructed this way, depending on its sum.
Additionally, if two sets correspond to the same bin, then the difference of their subset sums will be at most
l
.

The next step of our algorithm is to generate all the possible subset sums, occurring from the set of large elements
L
.
The complexity of this procedure is
{\cal O}\left(2^{\lvert L\rvert}\right)
, where
\lvert L\rvert
is the cardinality of set
L
.
Notice however, that it is possible to bound the number of the produced subset sums by the number of bins
B
, since if two sums belong to the same bin they constitute a solution, as shown in Lemma
1
, in which case the algorithm terminates in time
{\cal O}(n/\varepsilon^{2})
.

Lemma 1

If two subsets correspond to the same bin, we can find an
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation solution.

Proof

Suppose there exist two sets
L_{1},L_{2}\subseteq L
whose sums correspond to the same bin, with
\Sigma(L_{1})\leq\Sigma(L_{2})
.
Notice that there is no guarantee regarding the disjointness of said subsets, thus consider
L^{\prime}_{1}=L_{1}\setminus L_{2}
and
L^{\prime}_{2}=L_{2}\setminus L_{1}
, for which it is obvious that
\Sigma(L_{1}^{\prime})\leq\Sigma(L_{2}^{\prime})
.
Additionally, assume that
L^{\prime}_{1}\neq\emptyset
.
Then it holds that

\Sigma(L_{2}^{\prime})-\Sigma(L_{1}^{\prime})=\Sigma(L_{2})-\Sigma(L_{1})\leq l.

Therefore, the sets
L^{\prime}_{1}
and
L^{\prime}_{2}
constitute an
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation solution, since

\displaystyle\frac{\Sigma(L_{2}^{\prime})}{\Sigma(L_{1}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Sigma(L_{1}^{\prime})+l}{\Sigma(L_{1}^{\prime})}=1+\frac{l}{\Sigma(L_{1}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\cdot a_{n}}{\varepsilon\cdot a_{n}}=1+\varepsilon

where the last inequality is due to the fact that
L_{1}^{\prime}\subseteq L
is composed of elements
\geq\varepsilon\cdot a_{n}
, thus
\Sigma(L_{1}^{\prime})\geq\varepsilon\cdot a_{n}
.

It remains to show that
L^{\prime}_{1}\neq\emptyset
.
Assume that
L^{\prime}_{1}=\emptyset
.
This implies that
L_{1}\subseteq L_{2}
and since we consider each subset of
L
only once and the input is a set and not a multiset, it holds that
L_{1}\subset L_{2}\implies L^{\prime}_{2}\neq\emptyset
.
Since
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
correspond to the same bin, it holds that

\Sigma(L_{2})-\Sigma(L_{1})\leq l\implies\Sigma(L_{2}^{\prime})-\Sigma(L_{1}^{\prime})\leq l\implies\Sigma(L^{\prime}_{2})\leq l

which is a contradiction, since
L^{\prime}_{2}
is a non empty subset of
L
, which is comprised of elements greater than or equal to
\varepsilon\cdot a_{n}
, hence
\Sigma(L^{\prime}_{2})\geq\varepsilon\cdot a_{n}>\varepsilon^{2}\cdot a_{n}=l
, since
\varepsilon<1
.

Consider an
\varepsilon^{\prime}
such that
(1+\varepsilon^{\prime})/(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\leq 1+\varepsilon
for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
(the exact value of
\varepsilon^{\prime}
will be computed in Section
5
).

If every produced subset sum of the previous step belongs to a distinct bin, then,
we can infer that the number of subsets of large elements is bounded by
n/\varepsilon^{2}
.
Moreover, we can prove the following lemma.

Lemma 2

If the optimal ratio

involves sets
S^{*}_{1},S^{*}_{2}
consisting of only large elements,
with
S^{*}_{1}\cup S^{*}_{2}=S^{*}\subseteq L

then
\Sigma(\overline{S_{p}})/\Sigma(S_{p})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot r_{\text{opt}}
,
where

(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})
-apx solution to the

Proof

Assume that
\Sigma(S^{*}_{1})\leq\Sigma(S^{*}_{2})
.
Note that sets
S_{1}^{*},S_{2}^{*}
are also the optimal solution of the

problem on input
S^{*}
.
By running an
(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})
approximate

, we obtain the sets

with
\Sigma(S_{1})\leq\Sigma(S_{2})

Then,

\displaystyle\frac{\Sigma(S_{2})}{\Sigma(S_{1})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Sigma(S^{*}_{2})+\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot\Sigma(S^{*}_{1})}{(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\Sigma(S^{*}_{1})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Sigma(S^{*}_{2})+\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot\Sigma(S^{*}_{2})}{(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\Sigma(S^{*}_{1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\varepsilon^{\prime}}{1-\varepsilon^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{\Sigma(S^{*}_{2})}{\Sigma(S^{*}_{1})}

Lemma 3

Let

•

Case 1.
Suppose that
\Sigma(L^{*}_{1})>\Sigma(L^{*}_{2})

Then,

\Sigma(\overline{L^{*}_{p}})\leq\Sigma(L^{*}_{1})
and
\Sigma(L^{*}_{p})\geq\Sigma(L^{*}_{2})
,

Case 1.a.
Let
\Sigma(L_{p})+\Sigma(M)\geq\Sigma(\overline{L_{p}})

In this case, there exists

Hence

Let
\Sigma(L_{p})+\Sigma(M)<\Sigma(\overline{L_{p}})

By assuming the contrary, it holds that

\displaystyle\Sigma(\overline{L_{p}})

\displaystyle>(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\Sigma(L_{p}\cup M)

\displaystyle=(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\left(\Sigma(L_{p})+\Sigma(M)\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1+\varepsilon)(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\Sigma(L^{*}_{p})+(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\Sigma(M)

\displaystyle\geq(1+\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\Sigma(L^{*}_{p})+(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\Sigma(M)

\displaystyle\geq(1+\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot(\Sigma(L^{*}_{p})+\Sigma(M)).

Additionally, it holds that
(1+\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\Sigma(\overline{L^{*}_{p}})\geq\Sigma(\overline{L_{p}})
,
thus

\Sigma(\overline{L^{*}_{p}})>\Sigma(L^{*}_{p})+\Sigma(M)

follows, which is a contradiction since
\Sigma(\overline{L^{*}_{p}})-\Sigma(L^{*}_{p})\leq\Sigma(M)
.

Case 2.
Suppose that
\Sigma(L^{*}_{1})<\Sigma(L^{*}_{2})
.
Now there are two cases:

Case 2.a.
Suppose that
\Sigma(L^{*}_{1})+\Sigma(M)\leq\Sigma(L^{*}_{2})
.
Then, it holds that
S_{1}^{*}=L_{1}^{*}\cup M
and
S_{2}^{*}=L_{2}^{*}
and one can infer that

L_{1}^{*}=L^{*}_{p}
and
L_{2}^{*}=\overline{L^{*}_{p}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{\Sigma(\overline{L_{p}})}{\Sigma(L_{p}\cup M)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Sigma(\overline{L_{p}})}{\Sigma(L_{p})+\Sigma(M)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1+\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\Sigma(L^{*}_{2})}{(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\Sigma(L^{*}_{1})+\Sigma(M)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon^{\prime}}{1-\varepsilon^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{\Sigma(L^{*}_{2})}{\Sigma(L^{*}_{1})+\Sigma(M)}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{\Sigma(S^{*}_{2})}{\Sigma(S^{*}_{1})}

Case 2.b.
Suppose that
\Sigma(L^{*}_{1})+\Sigma(M)>\Sigma(L^{*}_{2})
.
We will proceed in a similar way as in cases 1.a. and 1.b.

Case 2.b.i.
Let
\Sigma(L_{p})+\Sigma(M)\geq\Sigma(\overline{L_{p}})
.
In this case, there exists
k
such that
M_{k}=\{a_{i}\in M\mid i\in[k]\}\subseteq M
and also
0\leq\Sigma(L_{p}\cup M_{k})-\Sigma(\overline{L_{p}})\leq\varepsilon\cdot a_{n}
(since all small elements have value less than
\varepsilon\cdot a_{n}
).
Notice that
\Sigma(\overline{L_{p}})\geq a_{n}
.
Hence

1\leq\frac{\Sigma(L_{p}\cup M_{k})}{\Sigma(\overline{L_{p}})}\leq 1+\frac{\varepsilon\cdot a_{n}}{\Sigma(\overline{L_{p}})}\leq 1+\frac{\varepsilon\cdot a_{n}}{a_{n}}=1+\varepsilon.

Case 2.b.ii.
Let
\Sigma(L_{p})+\Sigma(M)<\Sigma(\overline{L_{p}})
.
It suffices to show that

\frac{\Sigma(\overline{L_{p}})}{\Sigma(L_{p}\cup M)}\leq 1+\varepsilon.

By assuming the contrary, it holds that

\displaystyle\Sigma(\overline{L_{p}})

\displaystyle>(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\Sigma(L_{p}\cup M)

\displaystyle=(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\left(\Sigma(L_{p})+\Sigma(M)\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1+\varepsilon)(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\Sigma(L^{*}_{p})+(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\Sigma(M)

\displaystyle\geq(1+\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\Sigma(L^{*}_{p})+(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\Sigma(M)

\displaystyle\geq(1+\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot(\Sigma(L^{*}_{p})+\Sigma(M)).

Additionally, it holds that
(1+\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\Sigma(\overline{L^{*}_{p}})\geq\Sigma(\overline{L_{p}})
,
thus

\Sigma(\overline{L^{*}_{p}})>\Sigma(L^{*}_{p})+\Sigma(M)

follows, which is a contradiction since
\Sigma(\overline{L^{*}_{p}})-\Sigma(L^{*}_{p})<\Sigma(M)
,
due to the fact that
\Sigma(L^{*}_{2})-\Sigma(L^{*}_{1})<\Sigma(M)
and
\Sigma(\overline{L^{*}_{p}})-\Sigma(L^{*}_{p})\leq\Sigma(L^{*}_{2})-\Sigma(L^{*}_{1})
.

Adding Small Elements Efficiently.

Here, we will describe a method to efficiently add small elements to our sets.
In particular, we search for some
k
such that
0\leq\Sigma(L_{p}\cup M_{k})-\Sigma(\overline{L_{p}})\leq\varepsilon\cdot a_{n}
, where
M_{k}=\{a_{i}\in M\mid i\in[k]\}
.
Notice that if
\Sigma(M)\geq\Sigma(\overline{L_{p}})-\Sigma(L_{p})
, there always exists such a set
M_{k}
, since by definition, each element of set
M
is smaller than
\varepsilon\cdot a_{n}
.
In order to determine
M_{k}
, we make use of an array of partial sums
T[k]=\Sigma(M_{k})
,
where
k\leq\lvert M\rvert
.
Since
T
is sorted, we can find
k
in
{\cal O}(\log\lvert L\rvert)={\cal O}(\log n)
using binary search.

4
Final Algorithm

The algorithm presented in the previous section constitutes an approximation scheme for
Subset Sum Ratio
when one of the solution subsets contains the maximum element of the input set.
Thus, in order to solve the
Subset Sum Ratio
problem, it suffices to run the previous algorithm
n
times, where
n
depicts the cardinality of the input set
A
, while each time removing the max element of
A
.

In particular, suppose that the optimal solution involves disjoint sets
S_{1}^{*}
and
S_{2}^{*}
, where
a_{k}=\max(S_{1}^{*}\cup S_{2}^{*})
.
There exists an iteration for which the algorithm considers as input the set
A_{k}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}
.
In this iteration, the element
a_{k}
is the largest element and the algorithm searches for an approximation of the optimal solution for which
a_{k}
is contained in one of the solution subsets.
The optimal solution of the unconstrained version of
Subset Sum Ratio
has this property so the ratio of the approximate solution that the algorithm of the previous section returns is at most
(1+\varepsilon)
times the optimal.

Consequently,
n
repetitions of the algorithm suffice to construct an FPTAS for
Subset Sum Ratio
.

Notice that if at some repetition,
the sets returned due to the algorithm of Section
3
have ratio at most
1+\varepsilon
,
then this ratio successfully approximates the optimal ratio
r_{\text{opt}}\geq 1
, since
1+\varepsilon\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot r_{\text{opt}}
,
therefore they constitute an approximation solution.

5
Complexity

The total complexity of the final algorithm is determined by three distinct operations, over the
n
iterations of the algorithm:

1.

The cost to compute all the possible subset sums occurring from large elements.
It suffices to consider the case where this is bounded by the number of bins
B=n/\varepsilon^{2}
, due to Lemma
1
.

2.

The cost to compute an
(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})
-apx solution for
Partition
on each subset of large elements.
The cost of this operation will be analyzed in the following subsection.

3.

The cost to include small elements to the
(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})
-apx solutions for
Partition
.
There are
B
such solutions, and each requires
{\cal O}(\log n)
time, thus the total time required is
{\cal O}\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot\log n\right)
.

5.1
Complexity of Partition Computations

Using Exact Partition Computations.

Firstly, we will consider the case where we compute the optimal solution of the
Partition
problem.
In order to do so, we will use the standard meet in the middle algorithm
[
17
]
for
Subset Sum
,
and in the following we analyze its complexity.

Let subset
L^{\prime}\subseteq L
such that
\lvert L^{\prime}\rvert=k
.
The meet in the middle algorithm on the set
L^{\prime}
costs time

{\cal O}\left(2^{\lvert L^{\prime}\rvert/2}\cdot\lvert L^{\prime}\rvert\right)

Notice that the number of subsets of
L
of cardinality
k
is
\binom{\lvert L\rvert}{k}
and that
\lvert L\rvert\leq\log(n/\varepsilon^{2})
.
Furthermore, let
c=\left(1+\sqrt{2}\right)
, where
\log c=1.271...<1.3
.
Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\lvert L\rvert}\binom{\lvert L\rvert}{k}\cdot 2^{k/2}\cdot k

\displaystyle\leq\lvert L\rvert\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{\lvert L\rvert}\binom{\lvert L\rvert}{k}\cdot 2^{k/2}

\displaystyle=\lvert L\rvert\cdot\left(1+\sqrt{2}\right)^{\lvert L\rvert}

\displaystyle=\lvert L\rvert\cdot c^{\lvert L\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\log(n/\varepsilon^{2})\cdot c^{\log(n/\varepsilon^{2})}

\displaystyle=\log(n/\varepsilon^{2})\cdot(n/\varepsilon^{2})^{\log c}

where we used the Binomial Theorem.
Consequently, the complexity to solve the
Partition
problem for all the subsets of large elements is

{\cal O}\left(\frac{n^{1.3}}{\varepsilon^{2.6}}\cdot\log(n/\varepsilon^{2})\right)

Using Approximate Partition Computations.

Here we will analyze the complexity in the case we run an approximate
Partition
algorithm
in order to compute the
(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})
-approximation solutions.

For subset
L^{\prime}\subseteq L
, we run an approximate
Partition
algorithm with error margin
\varepsilon^{\prime}
such that

\frac{1+\varepsilon^{\prime}}{1-\varepsilon^{\prime}}\leq 1+\varepsilon\iff\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2+\varepsilon}

and by choosing the maximum such
\varepsilon^{\prime}
, it holds that

\varepsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2+\varepsilon}\implies\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}=\frac{2+\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}=\frac{2}{\varepsilon}+1\implies\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}={\cal O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)

Since there are at most
n/\varepsilon^{2}
subsets of large elements,
we will need to run said algorithm at most
n/\varepsilon^{2}
times on
\lvert L^{\prime}\rvert\leq\lvert L\rvert
elements and with error margin
\varepsilon^{\prime}
.

Note that any approximate
Subset Sum
algorithm could be used in order to approximate
Partition
,
such as the one presented by Kellerer
et al.

[
20
]
of complexity
{\cal O}\left(\min\left\{\frac{n}{\varepsilon},n+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)\right\}\right)
.
In our case, with
\lvert L\rvert=\log(n/\varepsilon^{2})
and error margin
\varepsilon^{\prime}
, the total complexity is

\displaystyle{\cal O}\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot\min\left\{\frac{\lvert L\rvert}{\varepsilon^{\prime}},\lvert L\rvert+\frac{1}{(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{2}}\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon^{\prime})\right\}\right)=

\displaystyle{\cal O}\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot\min\left\{\frac{\log(n/\varepsilon^{2})}{\varepsilon},\log(n/\varepsilon^{2})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)\right\}\right)

Very recently, Bringmann and Nakos
[
10
, Theorem 5.1]
developed a dedicated algorithm for approximating
Partition
via min-plus convolution computations.
By using the classic min-plus convolution algorithm of complexity
{\cal O}(n^{2})
, one can therefore obtain a deterministic FPTAS for
Partition
running in time

{\cal O}\left(\lvert L\rvert+(1/\varepsilon^{\prime})^{3/2}\cdot\log^{2}\Big(\frac{\lvert L\rvert}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\Big)\right)

Consequently, by using this algorithm, the final complexity due to all the approximate
Partition
computations is

\displaystyle{\cal O}\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot\left(\log(n/\varepsilon^{2})+(1/\varepsilon)^{3/2}\cdot\log^{2}\Big(\frac{\log(n/\varepsilon^{2})}{\varepsilon}\Big)\right)\right)

5.2
Total Complexity

The total complexity of the algorithm occurs from the
n
distinct iterations required and depends on the algorithm chosen to find the (exact or approximate) solution to the
Partition
problem,
since all of the presented algorithms dominate the time of the rest of the operations.
Thus, by choosing the fastest one (depending on the relationship between
n
and
\varepsilon
), the final complexity is

\displaystyle{\cal O}\left(\min\left\{\frac{n^{2.3}}{\varepsilon^{2.6}}\cdot\log(n/\varepsilon^{2}),\frac{n^{2}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\cdot\log\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{n^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\log\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{1.5}}\cdot\log^{2}\frac{\log(n/\varepsilon^{2})}{\varepsilon}\right)\right\}\right)

6
Conclusion and Future Work

The main contribution of this paper, apart from the introduction of a new FPTAS for the
Subset Sum Ratio
problem, is the establishment of a connection between
Partition
and approximating
Subset Sum Ratio
.
In particular, we showed that any improvement over the classic meet in the middle algorithm
[
17
]
, or over the approximation scheme for
Partition
will result in an improved FPTAS for
Subset Sum Ratio
.

Additionally, we establish that the complexity of approximating
Subset Sum Ratio
, expressed in the form
{\cal O}((n+1/\varepsilon)^{c})
has an exponent
c<5
, which is an improvement over all the previously presented FPTASs for the problem.

It is important to note however, that there is a distinct limit to the complexity that one may achieve for the
Subset Sum Ratio
problem using the techniques discussed in this paper.

As a direction for future research, we consider the use of exact
Subset Sum
or
Partition
algorithms parameterized by a
concentration
parameter
\beta
, as described in
[
4
,
5
]
, where they solve the decision version of
Subset Sum
.
See also
[
16
]
for a use of this parameter under a pseudopolynomial setting.
It would be interesting to investigate whether analogous arguments could be used to solve the optimization version.
