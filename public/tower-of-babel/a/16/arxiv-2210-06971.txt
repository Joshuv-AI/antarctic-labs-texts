Shot-frugal and Robust quantum kernel classifiers
Abhay Shastry, Abhijith Jayakumar, Apoorva D. Patel, Chiranjib Bhattacharyya

Abstract
Quantum kernel methods are a candidate for quantum speed-ups in supervised machine learning. The number of quantum measurements N N required for a reasonable kernel estimate is a critical resource, both from complexity considerations and because of the constraints of near-term quantum hardware. We emphasize that for classification tasks, the aim is reliable classification and not precise kernel evaluation, and demonstrate that the former is far more resource efficient. Furthermore, it is shown that the accuracy of classification is not a suitable performance metric in the presence of noise and we motivate a new metric that characterizes the reliability of classification. We then obtain a bound for N N which ensures, with high probability, that classification errors over a dataset are bounded by the margin errors of an idealized quantum kernel classifier. Using chance constraint programming and the subgaussian bounds of quantum kernel distributions, we derive several Shot-frugal and Robust (ShofaR) programs starting from the primal formulation of the Support Vector Machine. This significantly reduces the number of quantum measurements needed and is robust to noise by construction. Our strategy is applicable to uncertainty in quantum kernels arising from any source of unbiased noise.



I
Introduction

Kernel methods have been used extensively in a variety of machine learning tasks like classification, regression, and dimensionality reduction
Scholkopf and Smola 2001
.
The core ingredient of such algorithms is the so-called kernel trick, which implicitly embeds a datapoint into a higher (may be infinite) dimensional inner product space, known as the
feature space
.
The embedding is implicit because the vectors in the feature space are never explicitly computed. Instead, a
kernel function
is used to map any two input data points to the inner product between their embeddings in the feature space.
The key insight that allows kernel methods to be used with quantum computers is the identification of a vector in the feature space with the density matrix of a many-body quantum system
Schuld and Killoran 2019
;
Schuld 2021
.

Quantum computing allows us to efficiently compute the inner products of exponentially large density matrices without the explicit tomography of the density matrices themselves.
This has prompted researchers to search for a quantum advantage in kernel methods
Kübler et al. 2021
;
Havlíček et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Huang et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021
. However, quantum kernels introduce new challenges that are not present in the classical setting. In a major departure from classical kernels, quantum kernels cannot be explicitly evaluated. They can only be estimated by repeatedly preparing and measuring quantum logic circuits. Quantum measurements introduce a fundamental stochasticity (sampling noise) into the problem, even for an ideal device.
This is a crucial difference as the number of circuit evaluations one can perform is a finite resource in all available quantum computing platforms. In particular, the number of circuit evaluations
N
required for an accurate kernel estimate up to a precision
\varepsilon
scales as
\varepsilon^{-2}
. Exponential precision (in terms of the number of bits of
\varepsilon
) in the kernel estimate thus imposes exponentially large requirements on
N
, thereby making it hard to claim a quantum advantage
Kübler et al. 2021
;
Thanasilp et al. 2022
. A focus on this requirement is especially relevant for the current noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era
Bharti et al. 2022
quantum devices, where the number of measurement shots are a resource to be used frugally
Kübler et al. 2020
;
Arrasmith et al. 2020
;
Gu et al. 2021
.

In this work we address binary classification using a
Support Vector Machine
(SVM) along with quantum kernels, emphasizing the number of circuit evaluations (
N
) required for classification. After choosing a suitable feature map, an SVM training algorithm builds a separating hyperplane between the classes of datapoints by solving a convex optimization problem.
In an SVM, the boundary between the classes carries the information relevant to the classifier and is described by a set of support vectors (of size
m_{\mathrm{sv}}
).
Together with the kernel trick, SVMs efficiently construct highly nonlinear classifiers in the original data space
{\mathcal{X}}

Cortes and Vapnik 1995
;
Cervantes et al. 2020
;
Burges 1998
.
Suppose a kernel function
K:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, which may be classically hard to compute, has to be evaluated on a quantum machine. How many measurements would be needed to construct a reliable classifier?
Crucially, our goal is
not
to accurately evaluate the kernel function itself
but to construct a reliable classifier with it.

I.1
Problem Setup

We work in the hybrid quantum-classical setup
Havlíček et al. 2019
;
Schuld and Killoran 2019
;
Blank et al. 2020
.

1.

The training phase requires kernel function evaluations between each pair of input datapoints (training kernel matrix).
This matrix specifies the SVM optimization problem which is solved on a classical machine. Its solution gives the optimal parameters
\beta,b
of the SVM classifier.

2.

A datapoint

is then classified by the classifier function

which requires the estimation of kernel functions for each pair
K({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}}_{i}),\ i\in[m_{\mathrm{sv}}],
consisting of the given datapoint and the set of support vectors of the SVM. Kernel evaluations are done on a quantum machine by repeated measurements of a quantum logic circuit. This measurement process introduces stochasticity (sampling noise) to the kernel estimates.

3.

Using
N
measurements
per kernel estimate
, we denote the stochastic kernel classifier (

, Definition
3
) by

. Our aim is to study how reliably it reproduces the same label as the ideal or exact kernel classifier (

2

Our study concerns the effect of noise during classification. Noise may or may not be present during training.

II.1
Support Vector Machines for Classification

A function
f:{\mathcal{X}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to\{-1,1\}
is called a binary classifier, which takes an
observation

{\mathbf{x}}
and outputs a label
y
.
The problem of estimating
f
from a training dataset

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}=\left\{\left({\mathbf{x}}_{i},y_{i}\right)\left|{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{X}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d},~y_{i}\in\{-1,1\},~i\in[m]\right.\right\}

(5)

consisting of
m
i.i.d. (
observation
,
label
) pairs drawn from a distribution
\mathcal{P}
is of great interest in machine learning.
The aim is to obtain an
f
such that the generalization error,
\mathrm{Prob}\left(f({\mathbf{x}})\neq y\right)
, is small for
({\mathbf{x}},y)\sim\mathcal{P}
.

Support Vector Machines (SVM) are widely used classifiers
Cervantes et al. 2020
, which have been extremely successful in practical applications.
For a dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
, a SVM kernel classifer is

f({\mathbf{x}}|\alpha,b;K)=\mathrm{sgn}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}y_{i}K({\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})+b\bigg),

where the
\alpha=[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}]^{\top}
is obtained by solving the problem

\displaystyle\max_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}y_{i}y_{j}K({\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{j})\right),

(
DUAL
)

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}y_{i}=0\ \textrm{and}\ \ 0\leq\alpha_{i}\leq C,

for some
C\geq 0
.

This optimization problem can be solved efficiently using convex quadratic programming
Scholkopf and Smola 2001
.
The kernel function
K:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
positive semidefinite

One of the most interesting properties of the kernel function is it implicitly defines an embedding of the observation that does not need to be explicitly computed.
For a comprehensive exposition, see Ref.
Vapnik 1998
.
For brevity we introduce the kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}\in{\mathbb{S}^{m}_{+}}
, whose entries are
\mathbf{K}_{ij}=K({\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{j})
for any
i,j\in[m]
.
Since the kernel function is positive semidefinite, the kernel matrix is also positive semidefinite.

To help develop our ideas, we work with an equivalent setup.
We consider classifiers of the form

f({\mathbf{x}}|\beta,b;K)=\mathrm{sgn}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\beta_{i}K({\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})+b\bigg),

(6)

where
\beta=[\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m}]^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},b\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
The parameters
\beta,b
of the classifier are obtained by minimizing

\min_{\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},b\in{\mathbb{R}}}J(\beta,b;\mathbf{K}),

(
PRIMAL
)

where

\displaystyle J(\beta,b;\mathbf{K})=

\displaystyle C\sum_{i=1}^{m}\max\left(1-y_{i}(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j}\mathbf{K}_{ij}+b),0\right)

(7)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\beta_{i}\beta_{j}\mathbf{K}_{ij}

for some
C\geq 0
(same as in
DUAL
) that sets the relative weights of the two terms.
The first term is known as the hinge loss, and the second term is called the regularization term.
Since the kernel function is positive semidefinite, the resulting problem
is thus again a convex quadratic optimization problem.

II.2
Quantum Embedding Kernels

Kernel methods have a natural extension into the quantum setting.
A quantum computer can be used to embed the data into a high dimensional Hilbert space and the kernel function can be computed by estimating the state overlaps.
This also offers a path to obtain quantum advantage in machine learning, since certain type of kernel functions are (conjectured to be) hard to evaluate classically but easy to do so quantum mechanically
Havlíček et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Kübler et al. 2021
.
A quantum embedding circuit takes the classical input datapoint
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and maps it to a quantum state
\ket{\phi({\mathbf{x}})}\in\mathcal{H}
, the
computational
Hilbert space.
A valid kernel function
K:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
can be chosen as

K({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})\equiv\operatorname{Tr}\left\{\rho({\mathbf{x}})\rho({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})\right\}=|\braket{\phi({\mathbf{x}})|\phi({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})}|^{2},

(8)

where
\rho({\mathbf{x}})\equiv\ket{\phi({\mathbf{x}})}\bra{\phi({\mathbf{x}})}
is the density matrix that plays the role of a vector in the feature Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{*}
.
Such kernels are known as
quantum embedding kernels
(QEK)
Schuld and Killoran 2019
;
Schuld 2021
.

The mapping into the computational Hilbert space
\ket{\phi({\mathbf{x}})}=U({\mathbf{x}})\ket{0}^{n}
is achieved by a unitary operator
U({\mathbf{x}})
using a quantum circuit dependent on
{\mathbf{x}}
.
For example, we may use the elements of
{\mathbf{x}}
as the angles of rotation in the various

1
- and
2
-qubit gates used to construct the quantum circuit.
Such an angle embedding circuit is shown in appendix, and is one of the simplest ways to encode the input data
{\mathbf{x}}
into a quantum state
\ket{\phi({\mathbf{x}})}
.
Given such a circuit, we can map each datapoint to a quantum density matrix,
\rho({\mathbf{x}})=U({\mathbf{x}})\ket{0\ldots 0}\bra{0\ldots 0}U^{\dagger}({\mathbf{x}})
.
The kernel can then be expressed as the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product
K({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})=\operatorname{Tr}\left\{\rho{({\mathbf{x}})}\rho{({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})}\right\}
, which can be evaluated using either the SWAP test or the GATES circuit that we describe in the next Section.

III.2
Quantum sampling noise is circuit-dependent

We shall use the shorthand notation
K^{*}_{ij}=K^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{j})
and
K^{(N)}_{ij}=K^{(N)}({\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{j})
. We denote their difference at finite sampling as

\Delta{K}^{(N)}_{ij}=K^{*}_{ij}-K^{(N)}_{ij},\ \textrm{for any}\ {\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\in{\mathcal{X}}.

(15)

In what follows, we demonstrate that the sampling noise depends on the quantum logic circuit used to evaluate the kernel function.

We consider the two different ways in which the overlaps
K_{ij}=|\bra{\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{i})}\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{j})\rangle|^{2}
can be evaluated
Havlíček et al. 2019
.
The data
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
is transformed into a state in the computational Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
by starting with all
n
qubits in the
\ket{0}
state and using the unitary operator

\ket{\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{i})}=U({\mathbf{x}}_{i})\ket{0}^{\otimes n}.

(16)

The first method is to express the kernel as
K({\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{j})=|\bra{0}^{\otimes n}U^{\dagger}({\mathbf{x}}_{i})U({\mathbf{x}}_{j})\ket{0}^{\otimes n}|^{2}
, which can be evaluated by applying
U^{\dagger}({\mathbf{x}}_{i})U({\mathbf{x}}_{j})
to the initial state
\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
and then estimating the probability that the final state is also
\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
.
We refer to this as the GATES test, shown in fig.
2
(a).
It has also been called the inversion test
Lloyd et al. 2020
and the adjoint test
Hubregtsen et al. 2022
.
The value of the kernel function
K^{(N)}_{ij}=K^{(N)}({\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{j})
is inferred
using
N
independent Bernoulli trials, each of which gives the final state
\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
with probability
p=K^{*}_{ij}
.
Hence,

K^{(N)}_{ij}\sim\frac{1}{N}\ \textrm{Bin}\bigg(N,p=K^{*}_{ij}\bigg).

(GATES)

An alternate way to measure fidelity or state overlap is the SWAP test
Nielsen and Chuang 2011
, shown in fig.
2
(b).
In this case, the ancilla bit is read out, and the probability of obtaining the state
\ket{0}
can be written in terms of the state overlap

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}\big(\textrm{ancilla}=\ket{0}\big)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big(1+|\bra{\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{i})}\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{j})\rangle|^{2}\big)

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(1+K_{ij}^{*}\right).

(18)

We infer
K_{ij}^{(N)}
through
N
independent Bernoulli trials that measure the state of the ancilla bit, and therefore obtain

K^{(N)}_{ij}\sim\frac{2}{N}\ \textrm{Bin}\bigg(N,p=\frac{1}{2}(1+K^{*}_{ij})\bigg)-1.

(SWAP)

It is obvious that in both (

) and (

is an

of
K^{*}_{ij}
,

\mathbb{E}[K^{(N)}_{ij}]=K^{*}_{ij}\ \ \forall{\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\in{\mathcal{X}}.

(19)

The variance of the estimator, however, is linked to the specific circuit and the number of measurements
N
.

V
Chance Constraint Programming

The optimization problem over training data is a convex quadratic program since
\mathbf{K}^{*}\in{\mathbb{S}^{m}_{+}}
.
In reality, the kernel matrix over a training dataset suffers from the same underlying noise which affects the classifier
SKC
.
If we measure the kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}^{(N)}
with
N
measurements per kernel entry, the resulting optimization problem
PRIMAL
would generally not be convex.
Such situations are encountered in optimization problems and can be handled by introducing a chance constraint
Charnes et al. 1958
;
Prékopa 1970
.
We show here how we may use the noise information (
UM
) of the training kernel matrix to construct more reliable classifiers.

An optimization problem with stochastic terms can be turned into a chance constraint problem as follows.
Let
\mathbf{K}
denote the stochastic training kernel matrix.

\displaystyle\min_{\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},b,\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}}\quad\tau\

(32)

\displaystyle\textrm{s.t.\ }\mathrm{Prob}

\displaystyle\left(J(\beta,b;\mathbf{K})\leq\tau\right)\geq 1-\delta,

where
\delta\ll 1
ensures that for most instantiations of the stochastic kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}
, i.e. with high probability,
the primal objective
J\left(\beta,b;\mathbf{K}\right)
is smaller than
\tau
.
This
\tau
is the new objective which we wish to minimize.
It is in fact a dummy variable that is the means to minimize the (stochastic) primal objective with high probability.
The feasible set of a chance constraint is generally non-convex and makes the optimization problem intractable (although it is convex for some families of distributions
Prékopa 1995
).
The natural strategy in such cases is to construct convex approximations to the chance constraint
Nemirovski and Shapiro 2007
.

Relation to previous work.
The chance constraint method has been applied to the dual SVM formulation by Bhadra et. al.
Bhadra et al. 2010
, where convex approximations were derived for the chance constraint for a few different noise distributions.
In the present article, we derive a Robust Program using the chance constraint approach in the primal SVM formulation.
The
PRIMAL
SVM objective has two terms which are stochastic, namely, the hinge loss term and the regularization term, given in the two lines of Eq. (
7
) respectively. This SVM formulation is better suited to derive
a bound on
N
since the margin constraints appear explicitly (see Definition
8
). The hinge loss term in the
PRIMAL
objective implicitly contain these constraints as shown below.

V.1
Chance Constraint Approach to the SVM Primal

We first rewrite the SVM Primal objective by introducing two dummy variables
(a) slack variables
\xi
and (b) a variable
t
for the quadratic term in the objective function

\min_{\xi,t,\beta,b}J(\xi,t,\beta,b):=C\sum_{i}\xi_{i}+t

(33)

subject to the constraints
\xi_{i}\geq 0\ \ \ \forall i\in[m]
, and also

\xi_{i}\geq 1-y_{i}\left(\sum_{j}\mathbf{K}_{ij}\beta_{j}+b\right),

(constraint-1)

together with

t\geq\frac{1}{2}\beta^{\T}\mathbf{K}\beta.

(constraint-2)

It is easy to see that the above formulation is completely equivalent to
PRIMAL
.
The two constraints are dependent on the training kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}
which
is stochastic. We shall ensure that they hold true with high probabilities
1-\delta_{1}
and
1-\delta_{2}

respectively. Note that
constraint-1
is a set of
m
individual constraints and we shall invoke the union bound
and ensure that
all
the constraints are satisfied with a probability of at least
1-\delta_{1}
.

\mathrm{Prob}\bigg(\xi_{i}\geq 1-y_{i}\big(\sum_{j}\mathbf{K}_{ij}\beta_{j}+b\big)\bigg)\geq 1-\frac{\delta_{1}}{m}.

(chance-1)

Similarly, we impose

\mathrm{Prob}\left(t\geq\frac{1}{2}\beta^{\T}\mathbf{K}\beta\right)\geq 1-\delta_{2}

(chance-2)

We now derive convex approximations to the chance constraints above. The resulting convex program would then be robust to the sampling noise by construction.

Theorem 3
.

(Shot-frugal and Robust Program)

For a stochastic training kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}^{(N)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
inferred from
N
measurements per entry,
the optimal
PRIMAL
objective
J^{*}\big(\mathbf{K}^{(N)}\big):=\min_{\beta,b}J\big(\beta,b;\mathbf{K}^{(N)}\big)
is upper bounded by

J^{*}\big(\mathbf{K}^{(N)}\big)\leq J^{*}_{\mathrm{rob}},

(34)

with probability at least
1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}
. Here

J^{*}_{\mathrm{rob}}=\min_{\beta,b,\xi,t}J_{\mathrm{rob}}\left(\beta,b,\xi,t;\mathbf{K}^{*}|N,\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\right)

(
ShofaR)

is the optimal value of a Second-Order Cone Program (SOCP), whose objective is given by

J_{\mathrm{rob}}\left(\beta,b,\xi,t;\mathbf{K}^{*}|N,\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\right):=C\sum_{i}\xi_{i}+t

(35)

subject to the constraints
\xi_{i}\geq 0\ \ \ \forall i\in[m]
, and also

\xi_{i}\geq 1+\frac{{\mathrm{c}}}{2\sqrt{N}}\kappa{\bigg(\frac{\delta_{1}}{m}\bigg)}\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert-y_{i}(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j}\mathbf{K}^{*}_{ij}+b),

(suff-chance-1)

together with

t\geq\frac{1}{2}\beta^{\top}\left(\mathbf{K}^{*}+\frac{{\mathrm{c}}}{2\sqrt{N}}\kappa(\delta_{2})\mathbf{I}_{m\times m}\right)\beta.

(suff-chance-2)

A detailed proof of the above theorem is given in Appendix
E
. Note that the

1.

The hinge-loss has an additional term whose origin is precisely the enforcement of

, with high probability, for all the
m
datapoints.
Thus a condition against margin violation, which is
robust to stochasticity in the kernel matrix
, is built into the optimization problem
ShofaR
itself. (
suff-chance-1
) represents the sufficient
condition for which the chance constraint (
chance-1
) is satisfied.

2.

The kernel matrix in the regularization term is made larger by adding to its diagonal entries a term which arises precisely by requiring
\beta^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{(N)}\beta\leq\beta^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{*}\beta+t
with probability
1-\delta_{2}
for some

.
The large deviation bound given by Lemma
1
implies that

is sufficient. (
suff-chance-2
) represents the sufficient
condition for which the chance constraint (
chance-2
) is satisfied.

The points above suggest that the classifier constructed by solving

would be more robust to the stochasticity of the training kernel matrix.
We thus expect it to be a more reliable classifier at least over the training dataset.
Moreover, since the kernel evaluations in a test dataset suffer from the same underlying noise, we must expect it to be more reliable even over an independent test dataset (these results are shown in the Appendix
B
).

Definition 10

Definition 13

(Estimated Kernel Matrix)

Over a training dataset of size

T

Thus, the estimated kernel matrix is

T

Remark 3
.

(Confidence Interval)
The true kernel matrix lies within the interval

\mathbf{K}^{*}_{ij}\in[\widehat{\mathbf{K}}_{ij}-\Delta,\widehat{\mathbf{K}}_{ij}+\Delta]
with probability of at least
1-2\delta
, where
\widehat{\mathbf{K}}_{ij}=K^{(T)}_{ij}
denotes an estimated kernel matrix entry using
T

\Delta=\Delta(\delta,T)=\frac{c}{2\sqrt{T}}\kappa\left({\delta}\right)

Proof.

Using
2

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}\left(|K^{*}_{ij}-K^{(T)}_{ij}|\geq\Delta\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\bigg(-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 2\delta

and use the expression for

from Definition
7
.
Note that a subgaussian distribution has infinite support even though our quantum kernels
have support
[0,1]

V.4
Benchmarks in literature

Clearly stated benchmarks of
N
are hard to come by in the existing literature.
The reason for this is two-fold: (a) The problem of unreliable classification has not been given due importance and
(b) there are other device noises which adds an overhead to measurement requirements, making it hard to state clear benchmarks.
While some studies have considered both device and sampling noise
Havlíček et al. 2019
;
Hubregtsen et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2021
in the training kernel matrix,
the measurement requirements during the classification phase has not received much attention.
We discuss the bounds obtained by requiring precise kernel evaluations and their relation to refs.
Havlíček et al. 2019
;
Hubregtsen et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2021
in Appendix
F
. In Appendix
G
, we include simulations in the presence of the depolarizing noise. This demonstrates that the Robust Programs we derived outperform the nominal SVM classifiers in the presence of other sources of noise as well.

VI
Summary and Outlook

We have studied the role of
N
, the number of measurements used to evaluate a quantum kernel, for a classification task.
Our considerations have focused on providing the same predictions as the ideal quantum kernel classifier (
N\to\infty
),
not
to closely approximate the kernel function itself.
We noted that the classification accuracy is a poor performance metric in the presence of noise, and defined an empirical reliability that meaningfully captures the effects of noise.
The circuit used to evaluate the quantum kernel plays an important role in the analysis, and we have shown that the GATES test is preferable to the SWAP test for any
N
(Definition
7
).
We introduced a generic uncertainty model (
UM
) that can handle any source of noise.
Our results in the article have considered only the fundamental sampling noise;
the modification to the uncertainty model in presence of other noise sources is given in Appendix
B
. We show simulations in the presence of the depolarizing noise in Appendix
G
.

Using a subgaussian bound, we showed that
N_{\mathrm{sg}}\sim m_{\mathrm{sv}}\log M/\gamma^{2}
measurements are sufficient for reliable reproduction of the ideal quantum classifier over any dataset of size
M
.
If all pairs of kernel entries of this dataset and the set of support vectors are evaluated to a precision of
\varepsilon
(in Frobenius norm), then the bound scales as
N\sim m_{\mathrm{sv}}M/\varepsilon^{2}
(see Appendix
F
for the derivation and comparison to related work).
We noted that the parameter
\gamma
, representing the margin of classification of an ideal quantum classifier,
plays a role analogous to the precision
\varepsilon
with which a kernel entry is evaluated. Crucially,
\gamma
is not vanishingly small. We then defined a tighter empirical bound
N_{\mathrm{practical}}
and provided an algorithm for its computation in Appendix
A
.

In Section
V
, we developed the chance constraint programming for the SVM primal formulation and gave a Shot-Frugal and Robust (
ShofaR
) Program in Theorem
3
. We showed that the resulting classifier requires far fewer circuit evaluations than even the empirical bound
N_{\mathrm{practical}}
to reliably reproduce the ideal quantum classifier. In Theorem
4
, we derive an additional program
ShofaR-Est
which takes into account the fact that we do not have the exact training kernel matrix. We showed that this still leads to large savings over
N_{\mathrm{practical}}
, even when compared with the nominal
ShofaR
SVM which has access to the exact training kernel matrix. In Corollaries
3.1
and
4.1
, we consider an L1-norm relaxation of the
ShofaR
and
ShofaR-Est
Programs respectively in order to reduce the set of support vectors. Table
3
compares the measurement requirements of all the ShofaR Programs against the nominal SVM classifier.

Finally, we point out some open questions related to our results that are worth exploring,
especially in light of the limitations of near-term quantum hardware.

Problem dependence.

To find the tightest theoretical bound
N_{\mathrm{sg}}
for an accurate stochastic classifier
SKC
, we need to maximize
\gamma
while maintaining low
\gamma
-margin errors of the exact classifier
EKC
, i.e. stay just left of the vertical green line in Fig.
3(a)
.
Such a value of
\gamma
would be problem (dataset + kernel choice) dependent, but can we develop a criterion to identify it?
Additionally, finding the lowest
N
for a robust stochastic classifier
RSKC
while ensuring high relative accuracy, we need to estimate where
\textrm{RA}(h^{*},f^{*})
reaches
1
in Fig.
4(b)
.
This would also be problem dependent, but can we find a method to identify it?

Possible extensions.

A kernel approach to understanding training evolution during gradient descent for deep neural networks was put forth recently
Jacot et al. 2018
. This method has been extended into the quantum domain
Shirai et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022
to understand the training of quantum neural networks during gradient descent.
Indeed, it is pointed out in Ref.
Liu et al. 2022
that it would be worth while to explore the robustness of these kernel methods against noise. On a related note, a robust version
Chen et al. 2021
of the classical shadow estimation scheme
Aaronson 2018
;
Huang et al. 2020

Other sources of noise.

Recently, kernel estimates have been introduced that correct for depolarizing error as well
Hubregtsen et al. 2022
. These estimates are not unbiased, i.e.
\mathbb{E}[K^{(N)}\left({\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\right)]\neq K^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{j})
, and requires separate treatment. In such a case, we may write the final kernel function
K_{ij}

as

K_{ij}=\widehat{K}_{ij}+\Delta{K}^{(N)}_{ij}+\Delta{K}^{(\textrm{other})}_{ij},

(49)

where
\mathbb{E}[{K_{ij}}]=\widehat{K}_{ij}\neq K^{*}_{ij}
which must be estimated using
a bias-correcting procedure (such as the one for depolarizing noise in ref.
Hubregtsen et al. 2022
). All other noise contributions have been subsumed into
\Delta{K}^{(\textrm{other})}_{ij}
whose expectation value is taken as zero without loss of generality. Assuming they are independent sources of noise, we would have

\sigma_{0}^{2}=\sigma^{2}(K_{ij})=\frac{{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{4N}+\sigma^{2}(\textrm{other}),

(50)

and the methods we develop in the article can be applied with the above
\sigma_{0}
in the uncertainty model
UM
. However, if the noise biases the kernel value, we would need to compare all our results to
\widehat{K}
instead of
K^{*}
. All our results in the main article were in the presence of the sampling noise alone to clarify its contribution.

, we consider the device noise.

Appendix C
Subgaussian property of Bernoulli random variables

A random variable
\xi
(centered,
\mathbb{E}{\xi}=0
) is called subgaussian if there exists
a\in[0,\infty)
such that

\mathbb{E}{e^{\lambda\xi}}\ \leq e^{\frac{a^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2}},\ \forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

(51)

and the smallest such
a

\sigma(\xi)=\textrm{inf}\ \big\{a\geq 0\big|\mathbb{E}{\exp{\{\lambda\xi\}}}\leq\exp{\big\{{a^{2}\lambda^{2}}/{2}\big\}},\ \forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\big\}

(52)

is called the
subgaussian norm
of
\xi
(smallest such
a^{2}=\sigma^{2}(\xi)
has been called the optimal variance proxy).
We note two straight-forward properties of the subgaussian norm
Buldigīn and Moskvichova 2011
: (1)
Scaling
, i.e.
\sigma^{2}(\lambda X)=\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}(X)
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
and (2)
Sub-additivity
, i.e. for any independent
X_{k},\ k=\{1,2,...,N\}
,
\sigma^{2}(\sum_{k=1}^{N}(X_{k}))\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sigma^{2}(X_{k})
.
Equality holds for independent and identical random variables
\xi_{k},\ k=\{1,2,...,N\}
,
\sigma^{2}(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\xi_{k})=N\sigma^{2}(\xi_{1})
.

For both GATES and SWAP, the mean of the estimated kernel matches the true kernel
\mathbb{E}[K^{(}N)({\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{j})]=K^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{j})
. The variances of these two estimates are given by

\Var(K^{(N)}({\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{j}))=\begin{cases}\frac{K^{*}_{ij}\big(1-K^{*}_{ij}\big)}{N}\hskip-7.22743pt&\text{(GATES)}\\
\frac{\big(1-(K^{*}_{ij})^{2}\big)}{N}~~~&\text{(SWAP)}.\end{cases}

(53)

In Eq. (
53
) since
0\leq K^{*}_{ij}\leq 1
, we have
K^{*}_{ij}(1-K^{*}_{ij})\leq 1-(K^{*}_{ij})^{2}
, implying a lower variance for the GATES circuit for
the same
N
:

\Var\left(K^{(N)}({\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{j})\Big|_{\textrm{\tiny SWAP}}\right)\geq\Var\left(K^{(N)}({\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{j})\Big|_{\textrm{\tiny GATES}}\right)

(54)

Thus the SWAP circuit is affected more by the sampling noise.
The maximum value of
K^{*}_{ij}(1-K^{*}_{ij})
is
0.25
whereas
1-(K^{*}_{ij})^{2}
can take a maximum value of
1
.
This bounds the variance of
K^{(N)}_{ij}
in Eq. (
53
) (equivalently the variance
of
\Delta{K}^{(N)}_{ij}
).

We note that the distributions given by (
GATES
) and (
SWAP
) are subgaussian.
Furthermore, we note that
\Delta{K}^{(N)}_{ij}
in (
15
) can be expressed as the sum of
N
i.i.d. centered Bernoulli random variables
X_{k}(p)\sim B^{(0)}(1,p)=p-B(1,p),\ k=\{1,2,...,N\}
for both the
GATES and the SWAP tests

N\Delta{K}^{(N)}_{ij}=\begin{cases}\sum_{k=1}^{N}X_{k}(p),\ p=K^{*}_{ij}&(\textbf{GATES})\\
2\sum_{k=1}^{N}X_{k}(p),\ p=\frac{1+K^{*}_{ij}}{2}&(\textbf{SWAP}).\end{cases}

(55)

Stated another way, the prefactor of 2 above for the SWAP test would have the effect of increasing its variance and thus
makes it less reliable for the same number of measurements
N
.
Bernoulli random variables are subgaussian
Buldigīn and Moskvichova 2011
;
Arbel et al. 2019
and their properties are covered in section
C
. We use
these properties to derive tail bounds over the kernel distributions.

We omit the random variable
\xi\sim B^{(0)}(1,p)
while denoting the subgaussian norm for a centered Bernoulli distribution
and simply
write
\sigma(p)
.
The optimal variance proxy for a centered Bernoulli distribution was derived in
Buldigīn and Moskvichova 2011
and is given by (
q=1-p
)

\sigma^{2}(p)=\begin{cases}0&\ \ p=\{0,1\}\\
0.25&\ \ p=0.5\\
\frac{p-q}{2(\ln(p)-\ln(q))}&\ \ \textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(56)

Its properties were derived in lemma 2.1 of
Buldigīn and Moskvichova 2011
from which we note that

\sigma^{2}(p)
is monotonically increasing for
p\in(0,1/2)
and monotonically decreasing for
p\in(1/2,1)
. It is also symmetrical
\sigma^{2}(p)=\sigma^{2}(q)
(where
q=1-p
).
It takes on its maximum value of 1/4 when
p=q=1/2
. Using the maximum value of the subgaussian norm for Bernoulli random variables above and
eq. (
55
) for the two different circuits gives us the bound used in (
20
).

A recent study
Nghiem et al. 2021
on the IBM quantum hardware has shown that SWAP test is a poor choice compared to the GATES test for estimating QEKs, owing to errors in the controlled SWAP implementation. While this may be a factor, it appears that the authors may have overlooked that a straightforward inequality (
54
) underlies their observation.

Appendix D
More on chance constraint programming

D.1
Chance constraint: Union bound

When multiple chance constraints are present, we have invoked the well-known union bound to derive our results
Boole 2009
.
Suppose the chance constraints to be satisfied are some inequalities
A_{i},\ i\in[m]
, we denote its conjugate as
\bar{A}_{i}
which is the violation of inequality
A_{i}
.
We would like all the constraints
A_{i}
, i.e.
\cap_{i}^{m}A_{i}
, to be satisfied and derive a sufficient condition for it. Since
\overline{\cap_{i}^{m}A_{i}}=\cup_{i}\bar{A}_{i}
,
we consider the probability of the union of all constraint violations

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}\big(\bar{A}_{1}\cup\bar{A}_{2}\cup...\bar{A}_{m}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Prob}\big(\bar{A}_{i}\big),

(57)

\displaystyle\leq\delta,

for some small parameter
0<\delta\ll 1
. This provides the sufficient condition

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}(\bar{A}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{m},

(58)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}(A_{i})

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{\delta}{m},\ \ \forall i\in[m]

D.2
Sufficient condition in the Chance Contraint Program

We give a slightly more detailed explanation of the chance constraint approach in this Section, at the expense
of repetition from Section
V.1
.
The kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{K}^{(N)}=\mathbf{K}^{*}-\Delta\mathbf{K}^{(N)}
is stochastic and entries of
\Delta{\mathbf{K}}^{(N)}
satisfy
the uncertainty model (
UM
) with the bound given by Eq. (
20
).

The hinge loss given by

L(\beta,b;\mathbf{K})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\max\bigg(1-y_{i}(\sum_{j}\mathbf{K}_{ij}\beta_{j}+b),0\bigg)

(59)

is also subject to the stochasticity of the kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}
. Whether the term is zero or positive depends on the particular instantiation of the kernel matrix.
This will have to be handled by a chance constraint approach. The same objective in the
SVM primal problem (
7
) can be written as

\min_{t,\xi,\beta,b}J(t,\xi,\beta,b;\mathbf{K}):=C\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}+t,

(PRIMAL-2)

with the hinge-loss term as the two constraints

\textrm{IE}_{i}:\ \xi_{i}\geq 1-y_{i}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{K}_{ij}\beta_{j}+b\big),

(60)

and

\xi_{i}\geq 0,\ i\in[m].

(61)

The quadratic term is translated to the constraint

\frac{1}{2}\beta^{\T}\mathbf{K}\beta\leq t.

(62)

The constraints (
60
) and (
62
) are dependent on the particular instantiation of the stochastic kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}_{N}
and we thus advocate the strategy of satisfying them with high probability.
We denote the probability of violation of
any
one constraint
\textrm{IE}_{i}
in (
60
) by
\delta_{1}\ll 1
and the probability of violating the constraint (
62
)
by
\delta_{2}\ll 1
. The intersection of all the inequalities
\textrm{IE}_{i}
is satisfied with a high probability
1-\delta_{1}
, which gives us a sufficient condition
that each inequality be satisfied with the higher probability of
1-\delta_{1}/m
(union bound
D.1
)

\mathrm{Prob}\bigg(\xi_{i}\geq 1-y_{i}\big(\sum_{j}\mathbf{K}_{ij}\beta_{j}+b\big)\bigg)\geq 1-\frac{\delta_{1}}{m},

(63)

and

\mathrm{Prob}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\beta^{\T}\mathbf{K}\beta\leq t\bigg)\geq 1-\delta_{2}.

(64)

The sufficient conditions for the constraints (
63
) and (
64
) lead to Theorem
3
, and is
covered in the Proofs Section.
At optimality, we shall denote
J(t,\xi,\beta,b;\mathbf{K})=J^{*}(\mathbf{K})
, dropping the optimal variables
t^{*},\xi^{*},\beta^{*},b^{*}
from the
notation.

D.3
Second-order cone programming

A second-order cone program (SOCP) involves a linear objective function with one or more second-order cone constraints
and any additional linear inequality constraints. An example of a second-order cone constraint in
d+1
dimensions is
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\leq t
, where
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\ t\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
The convex approximations we developed for the chance constraints
(
63
) and (
64
), i.e. (
74
) and (
76
) respectively, are second-order cone representable. This was shown for the chance
constraint approach involving the SVM Dual in Ref.
Bhadra et al. 2010
. We show it for the SVM Primal
ShofaR
below.

The constraint (
74
) can be represented as a second-order cone (SOC) constraint by simply introducing an
additional variable
r
with the SOC constraint
\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert\leq r
, and replacing
\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert
by
r
in (
74
) which is an additional linear inequality constraint.
Similarly, (
76
) is a quadratic constraint which can be straight-forwardly expressed as a SOC constraint
\left\lVert A\beta\right\rVert\leq t
, where
A\in{\mathbb{S}^{m}_{+}}
is
the positive-definite square root of the matrix
A^{2}=\left(\mathbf{K}^{*}+\frac{{\mathrm{c}}}{2\sqrt{N}}\kappa(\delta_{2})I\right)\in{\mathbb{S}^{m}_{+}}
. The primal objective (
PRIMAL-2
)
along with the linear and SOC constraints
stated above forms a second-order cone program (SOCP) which can be solved efficiently
Lobo et al. 1998
. We use the MOSEK solver for its numerical implementation in Python
using the convex programming package
cvxpy

Diamond and Boyd 2016
;
Agrawal et al. 2018
.

Theorem 5

(Precise Kernel Estimation)
.

Let
m_{\mathrm{sv}}
denote the number of support vectors of an SVM.
Over any dataset
\mathcal{D}
of size

\mathbf{K}^{*}_{ij}=K^{*}_{ij}

(86)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Prob}\left(\cup_{ij}\overline{\textrm{IE}_{ij}}\right)

(87)

\displaystyle\leq m_{\mathrm{sv}}M\mathrm{Prob}\left(\overline{\textrm{IE}_{ij}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\delta

Here, the second and third line together with Lemma

Benchmarks in literature

As we mentioned in the main article, clearly stated benchmarks for

There exists also a large discrepancy in the number of measurements used in various studies of quantum classifiers
Wang et al. 2021
;
Hubregtsen et al. 2022

In the freely accessible IBM quantum processors, the default number of measurements per job is set to

Wang et al. 2021
uses the same number of measurements for both the training and the testing phases. The range of observed accuracies in the latter is wide, indicating that the classification is unreliable.
