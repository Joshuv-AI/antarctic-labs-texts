CIR equations with multivariate Lévy noise
Michał Barski, Rafał Łochowski

Abstract
The paper is devoted to the study of the short rate equation of the form d ​ R ​ ( t ) = F ⁡ ( R ⁡ ( t ) ) ​ d ​ t + ∑ i = 1 d G i ​ ( R ⁡ ( t − ) ) ​ d ​ Z i ​ ( t ) , R ⁡ ( 0 ) = x ≥ 0 , t > 0 , \displaystyle\textnormal{d}R(t)=F(R(t))\textnormal{d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{d}G_{i}(R(t-))\textnormal{d}Z_{i}(t),\quad R(0)=x\geq 0,\quad t>0, with deterministic functions F , G 1 , … , G d F,G_{1},...,G_{d} and a multivariate Lévy process Z = ( Z 1 , … , Z d ) Z=(Z_{1},...,Z_{d}) . The equation is supposed to have a nonnegative solution which generates an affine term structure model. Two classes of noise are considered. In the first one the coordinates of Z are independent processes with regularly varying Laplace exponents. In the second class Z is a spherical processes, which means that its Lévy measure has a similar structure as that of a stable process, but with radial part of a general form. For both classes a precise form of the short rate generator is characterized. Under mild assumptions it is shown that any equation of the considered type has the same solution as the equation driven by a Lévy process with independent stable coordinates. The paper generalizes the classical results on the Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) model, [ 5 ] , as well as on its extended version from [ 1 ] and [ 2 ] where Z Z is a one-dimensional Lévy process.

Introduction

This paper is concerned with a stochastic equation of the form

\displaystyle\textnormal{d}R(t)=F(R(t))\textnormal{d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{d}G_{i}(R(t-))\textnormal{d}Z_{i}(t),\quad R(0)=x,\quad t>0,

(1.1)

where
F
,
\{G_{i}\}_{i=1,2,...,d}
are deterministic functions,
Z_{i}(t),i=1,2,...,d
, are Lévy processes and martingales,

x
is a nonnegative constant. A solution
R(t),t\geq 0
, if nonnegative, will be identified here with the short rate process, so
it defines the bank account process by

B(t):=e^{\int_{0}^{t}R(s)ds},\quad t\geq 0.

Related to the savings account are zero coupon bonds. Their prices form a family of stochastic processes

P(t,T),t\in[0,T]
, parametrized by their maturity times
T\geq 0
. The price of a bond with maturity
T
at time
T
is equal to its
nominal value, typically assumed, also here, to be
1
, that is
P(T,T)=1
. The family of bond prices is supposed to have the
affine structure
, which means that

\displaystyle P(t,T)=e^{-A(T-t)-B(T-t)R(t)},\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

(1.2)

for some smooth deterministic functions
A
,
B
. Hence, the only source of randomness in the affine model (
1.2
) is the short rate process
R
given by (
1.1
). As the resulting market constituted by
(B(t),\{P(t,T)\}_{T\geq 0})

should exclude arbitrage, the discounted bond prices

\hat{P}(t,T):=B^{-1}(t)P(t,T)=e^{-\int_{0}^{t}R(s)ds-A(T-t)-B(T-t)R(t)},\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

are supposed to be local martingales for each
T\geq 0
. This requirement affects in fact our starting equation. Thus
the functions
F
,
\{G_{i}\}_{i=1,...,d}
and the noise
Z=(Z_{1},...,Z_{d})
should be chosen such that
(
1.1
)
has a nonnegative solution with any
x\geq 0
and such that, for some functions
A,B
and each
T\geq 0
,
\hat{P}(t,T)
is a local martingale on
[0,T]
. If this is the case, (
1.1
) will be called to
generate an affine model
or to be a
generating equation
, for short.

In the case when
Z=W
is a real-valued Wiener process, the only generating equation is the classical CIR equation

\displaystyle\textnormal{d}R(t)=(aR(t)+b)\textnormal{d}t+C\sqrt{R(t)}\textnormal{d}W(t),

(1.3)

with
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
b,C\geq 0
, due to Cox, Ingersoll, Ross, see
[
5
]
. The
case with a general one-dimensional Lévy process
Z
was studied in
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
and

[
3
]
with the following conclusion. If the variation of
Z
is infinite and
G\not\equiv 0
, then
Z
must be an
\alpha
-stable process with index
\alpha\in(1,2]
, with either positive or negative jumps only, and (
1.1
) has the form

\displaystyle\textnormal{d}R(t)=(aR(t)+b)\textnormal{d}t+C\cdot R(t)^{{1}/{\alpha}}\textnormal{d}Z(t),

(1.4)

with
a\in\mathbb{R},b\geq 0
and
C
such that it has the same sign as the jumps of
Z
. Clearly, for
\alpha=2
equation (
1.4
) becomes (
1.3
). If
Z
is of finite variation then the noise enters (
1.1
) in the additive way, that is

\displaystyle\textnormal{d}R(t)=(aR(t)+b)\textnormal{d}t+C\ \textnormal{d}Z(t).

(1.5)

Here
Z
can be chosen as an arbitrary process with positive jumps,
a\in\mathbb{R},C\geq 0
and

b\geq C\int_{0}^{+\infty}y\ \nu(\textnormal{d}y),

where
\nu(\textnormal{d}y)
stands for the Lévy measure of
Z
. The variation of
Z
is finite, so is the right side above.
Recall, (
1.5
) with
Z
being a Wiener process is the well known Vasiček equation, see
[
11
]
. Then the short rate is a Gaussian process, hence it takes negative values with positive probability.
This drawback is eliminated by the jump version of the Vasiček equation (
1.5
).

This paper is devoted to the equation (
1.1
) with
d>1
. The multidimensional setting makes the study of equation
(
1.1
) more complicated. The reason is that, unlikely as in the case
d=1
, different generating equations may have identical solutions in the sense that the solutions’ generators are the same. Our first goal is to characterize the class of generators of solutions of generating equations and the second goal is to construct, for each element of this class, a related specific equation. In this way any short rate process given by (
1.1
) which generates an affine model becomes representable by a tractable equation. This approach seems to be useful for future applications.

Our solution of the problem is based on, rather abstract, result of Filipović
[
7
]
, characterizing generators of a general Markovian non-negative short rate process. The contribution of this paper is making this characterization concrete for two classes of Lévy processes. In the first class the coordinates of the noise

Z_{1}(t),Z_{2}(t),...,Z_{d}(t),\quad t\geq 0,

are independent Lévy processes being martingales of infinite variation. Their Laplace exponents are assumed to vary regularly at zero. We show that the solution of any generating equation with such a noise is the same as the solution of the equation

\textnormal{d}R(t)=(aR(t)+b)\textnormal{d}t+\sum_{k=1}^{g}d_{k}R(t-)^{1/\alpha_{k}}\textnormal{d}Z^{\alpha_{k}}_{k}(t),

where
1\leq g\leq d
,
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
b\geq 0
,
d_{k}>0
,
2\geq\alpha_{1}>...>\alpha_{g}>1
, and
Z_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}
is a stable process with index
\alpha_{k}
. The second class consists of

spherical Lévy processes
. We call a process
Z(t):=(Z_{1}(t),...,Z_{2}(t))
spherical if its
Lévy measure
\nu(\textnormal{d}y)
admits the following representation

\nu(A)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\lambda(\textnormal{d}\xi)\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbf{1}_{A}(r\xi)\gamma(\textnormal{d}r),\quad A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(1.6)

Here
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is a unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\lambda(\textnormal{d}\xi)
is a finite measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
called a

of
\nu
,
\gamma(\textnormal{d}r)
is a Lévy measure on
(0,+\infty)
called a

of
\nu

\mathbb{R}^{d}
starting from the origin, the Lévy measure is given in the same way by the radial part,
up to multiplication by a nonnegative constant. An important example of a radial measure satisfying
(
4.1
)-(
4.2
) is

\displaystyle\gamma(\textnormal{d}r)=\frac{1}{r^{1+\alpha}}\textnormal{d}r,\quad\alpha\in(1,2).

(1.7)

Given this measure and any finite measure
\lambda(d\xi)
, the formula
(
1.6
) corresponds to a stable process with index
\alpha\in(1,2)
. This process has no Wiener part. The
2
-stable process is the Wiener process. We prove, under mild conditions, that the solution of any generating equation with spherical noise is the same as the solution of equation (
1.4

Our results for each of the classes introduced above generalize the one dimensional results from
[
1
]

2.1
Setup for the equation

Using the scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
we write (
1.1

\displaystyle\textnormal{d}R(t)=F(R(t))\textnormal{d}t+\langle G(R(t-)),\textnormal{d}Z(t)\rangle,\quad R(0)=x\geq 0,\qquad t>0,

(2.1)

where
F:[0,+\infty)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
G:=(G_{1},G_{2},...,G_{d}):[0,+\infty)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

Z:=(Z_{1},Z_{2},...,Z_{d})
is a Lévy process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the characteristic triplet
(a,Q,\nu(\textnormal{d}y))
. Recall,
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
describes the drift part of
Z
,
Q
is a non-negative, symmetric,
d\times d
covariance matrix, characterizing the coordinates’ covariance of the Wiener part
W
of
Z
, and
\nu(\textnormal{d}y)
is a measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
satisfying

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mid y\mid^{2}\wedge\ 1)\ \nu(\textnormal{d}y)<+\infty,

(2.2)

describing the jumps of
Z
and called the Lévy measure of
Z
. Recall,
Z
admits a representation as a sum of four independent processes of the form

\displaystyle Z(t)=at+W(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\{\mid y\mid\leq 1\}}y\tilde{\pi}(\textnormal{d}s,\textnormal{d}y)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\{\mid y\mid>1\}}y\pi(\textnormal{d}s,\textnormal{d}y),

(2.3)

called the Lévy-Itô decomposition of
Z
. Above
\pi(\textnormal{d}s,\textnormal{d}y)
and
\tilde{\pi}(\textnormal{d}s,\textnormal{d}y):=\pi(\textnormal{d}s,\textnormal{d}y)-\textnormal{d}s\nu(\textnormal{d}y)
stand for the jump measure and the compensated jump measure of
Z
, respectively.
We consider the case when
Z
is a martingale and call it a Lévy martingale for short. Its drift and the Lévy measure are such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mid y\mid>1}\mid y\mid\ \nu(\textnormal{d}y)<+\infty,\quad a+\int_{\mid y\mid>1}y\ \nu(\textnormal{d}y)=0.

(2.4)

Consequently, the characteristic triplet of
Z
is

\displaystyle\left(-\int_{\mid y\mid>1}y\ \nu(\textnormal{d}y),\ Q,\ \nu(\textnormal{d}y)\right),

(2.5)

and (
2.3
) takes the form

Z(t)=W(t)+X(t),\qquad X(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y\ \tilde{\pi}(\textnormal{d}s,\textnormal{d}y),\quad t\geq 0,

where
W
and
X
are independent. The martingale
X
will be called the jump part of
Z
. Its Laplace exponent
J_{\nu}
, defined by the equation

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\langle\lambda,X(t)\rangle}\right]=e^{tJ_{\nu}(\lambda)},

(2.6)

has the following representation

J_{\nu}(\lambda)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(e^{-\langle\lambda,y\rangle}-1+\langle\lambda,y\rangle)\nu(\textnormal{d}y),

(2.7)

and is finite for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying

\int_{\mid y\mid>1}e^{-\langle\lambda,y\rangle}\nu(\textnormal{d}y)<+\infty.

By the independence of
X
and
W
we see that

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\langle\lambda,Z(t)\rangle}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\langle\lambda,W(t)\rangle}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\langle\lambda,X(t)\rangle}\right],

so the Laplace exponent
J_{Z}
of
Z
equals

J_{Z}(\lambda)={{\frac{1}{2}\langle Q\lambda,\lambda\rangle+J_{\nu}(\lambda)}}.

(2.8)

2.2
Projections of the noise and problem formulation

For the function
G

\displaystyle Z^{G(x)}(t):=\langle G(x),Z(t)\rangle,\quad t\geq 0.

(2.9)

For any

and (

\displaystyle J_{Z^{G(x)}}(\gamma)=J_{Z}(\gamma G(x))=\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}\langle QG(x),G(x)\rangle+\int_{\mid y\mid>0}\left(e^{-\gamma\langle G(x),y\rangle}-1+\gamma\langle G(x),y\rangle\right)\nu(\textnormal{d}y).

(2.10)

\displaystyle J_{Z^{G(x)}}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}\langle QG(x),G(x)\rangle+\int_{\mid v\mid>0}\left(e^{-\gamma v}-1+\gamma v\right)\nu_{G(x)}(\textnormal{d}v).

(2.11)

Thus the characteristic triplet of the projection

\displaystyle\left(-\int_{\mid v\mid>1}y\ \nu_{G(x)}(\textnormal{d}v),\ \langle QG(x),G(x)\rangle,\ \nu_{G(x)}(\textnormal{d}v)\mid_{v\neq 0}\right).

(2.12)

Above we used the restriction

by cutting off zero which may be an atom of
\nu_{G(x)}(\textnormal{d}v)
.

The coordinates of

We start with an
\alpha
-stable martingale in
\mathbb{R}^{d},d>1

\alpha\in(1,2)
such that the spherical part
\lambda

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{+}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mid x\mid=1,x\geq 0\}

(writing

we mean that all coordinates of

are non-negative).
The Laplace exponent of the jump part of

, identical with the Laplace exponent of

\displaystyle J_{\nu}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{+}}\lambda(\textnormal{d}\xi)\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(e^{-\langle z,r\xi\rangle}-1+\langle z,r\xi\rangle\right)\frac{1}{r^{1+\alpha}}\textnormal{d}r

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{+}}\lambda(\textnormal{d}\xi)\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(e^{-r\langle z,\xi\rangle}-1+r\langle z,\xi\rangle\right)\frac{1}{r^{1+\alpha}}\textnormal{d}r

\displaystyle=C_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{+}}\langle z,\xi\rangle^{\alpha}\lambda(\textnormal{d}\xi),

where

and

Above we used the well known formula

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\Big(e^{-uy}-1+uy\Big)\frac{1}{y^{1+\alpha}}\textnormal{d}y=C_{\alpha}u^{\alpha}.

The following example shows that the function
G
in a generating pair
(G,Z)
is not unique.

Example 2.3

Let
Z
be an
\alpha
-stable martingale in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the

2.22
) and

,

(Z,G)
generates an affine model if and only if the function

satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{+}}\langle G(x),\xi\rangle^{\alpha}\lambda(\textnormal{d}\xi)=\frac{C}{C_{\alpha}}x,\quad x\geq 0,

C\geq 0

We need to show that (
2.23
) is equivalent to
(
2.20
) with some measure
\mu(\textnormal{d}v)

By (

for some

\mu
is an
\alpha
-stable measure and

can be any function satisfying (

Example 2.4

Let

3
Noise with independent coordinates

This section deals with equation (
2.1
) in the case when the coordinates
(Z_{1},Z_{2},...,Z_{d})
of the martingale
Z
are independent processes. In view of Proposition
2.1
we are interested in characterizing possible distributions of projections
Z^{G}
over all generating pairs
(G,Z)
. By (
2.13
) the jumps of the projections are necessarily positive. As the coordinates of
Z
are independent, they do not jump together. Consequently, we see that, for each
x\geq 0
,

\triangle Z^{G(x)}(t)=\langle G(x),\triangle Z(t)\rangle>0

holds if and only if, for some
i=1,2,...,d
,

\displaystyle G_{i}(x)\triangle Z_{i}(t)>0,\quad\triangle Z_{j}(t)=0,j\neq i.

(3.1)

Condition (
3.1
) means that
G_{i}(x)
and
\triangle Z_{i}(t)
are of the same sign. We can consider only the case when both are positive, i.e.

G_{i}(x)\geq 0,\quad i=1,2,...,d,\ x\geq 0,\qquad\triangle Z_{i}(t)\geq 0,\quad t>0,

because the opposite case can be turned into this one by replacing
(G_{i},Z_{i})
with
(-G_{i},-Z_{i})
,
i=1,...,d
. The Lévy measure
\nu_{i}(\textnormal{d}y)
of
Z_{i}
is thus concentrated on
(0,+\infty)
and, in view of (
2.8
), the Laplace exponent of
Z_{i}
takes the form

\displaystyle J_{i}(b):=\frac{1}{2}q_{ii}b^{2}+\int_{0}^{+\infty}(e^{-bv}-1+bv)\nu_{i}(\textnormal{d}v),\quad b\geq 0,\ i=1,2,...,d,

(3.2)

with
q_{ii}\geq 0
. Recall,
q_{ii}
stands on the diagonal of
Q
- the covariance matrix of the Wiener part of
Z
.
We will assume that
J_{i},i=1,2,...,d
are
regularly varying at zero
. Recall, that means that

\lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{J_{i}(bx)}{J_{i}(x)}=\psi_{i}(b),\quad b>0,\qquad i=1,2,...,d,

for some function
\psi_{i}
. In fact
\psi_{i}
is a power function, i.e.

\psi_{i}(b)=b^{\alpha_{i}},\quad b>0,

with some
-\infty<\alpha_{i}<+\infty
and

is called to vary regularly with index
\alpha_{i}
. A characterization of slowly varying Laplace exponent in terms of the corresponding Lévy measure is presented in Section
3.2
.

the limits
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0+}\eta_{k}(x)
indeed exist and that
\alpha_{i(k)}\in(1,2]
.

First we will prove that
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0+}\eta_{g}(x)
exists.
Assume, by contrary, that this is not true, so

\limsup_{x\rightarrow 0+}\eta_{g}(x)-\liminf_{x\rightarrow 0+}\eta_{g}(x)\geq\delta>0.

(3.21)

It follows from (
3.14
) that

\frac{J_{1}(G_{1}(x))+J_{2}(G_{2}(x))+...+J_{d}(G_{d}(x))}{x}=\sum_{k=1}^{g}\eta_{k}(x)=\tilde{J}_{\mu}(1).

Let now
b_{0}\in(0,1)
be small enough so that

\tilde{J}_{\mu}(1)b_{0}^{\alpha_{i\left(g-1\right)}-\alpha_{i(g)}}<\frac{\delta}{6}.

(3.22)

Let us set in (
3.18
)
b=b_{0}
and then divide both sides of (
3.18
) by
b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g)}}
.
For
x>0
sufficiently close to
0
we have

\eta_{g}(x)-\frac{\delta}{6}\leq\frac{1}{b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g)}}}\left(\sum_{i=i\left(g-1\right)+1}^{i\left(g\right)}\frac{{J}_{i}\left(b_{0}\cdot G_{i}(x)\right)}{J_{i}\left(G_{i}(x)\right)}\cdot\frac{J_{i}\left(G_{i}(x)\right)}{x}\right)\leq\eta_{g}(x)+\frac{\delta}{6}

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g)}}}\sum_{k=1}^{g-1}\left(\sum_{i=i\left(k-1\right)+1}^{i\left(k\right)}\frac{J_{i}\left(b_{0}\cdot G_{i}(x)\right)}{J_{i}\left(G_{i}(x)\right)}\cdot\frac{J_{i}\left(G_{i}(x)\right)}{x}\right)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{g-1}2b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(k)}-\alpha_{i(g)}}\eta_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\leq 2b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g-1)}-\alpha_{i(g)}}\tilde{J}_{\mu}(1)

thus from (
3.18
), two last estimates and (
3.22
)

\eta_{g}(x)-\frac{\delta}{6}\leq\frac{\tilde{J}_{\mu}(b_{0})}{b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g)}}}\leq\eta_{g}(x)+\frac{\delta}{6}+2\tilde{J}_{\mu}(1)b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g-1)}-\alpha_{i(g)}}<\eta_{g}(x)+\frac{\delta}{2}.

But this contradicts (
3.21
)
since we must have

\limsup_{x\rightarrow 0+}\eta_{g}(x)\leq\frac{\tilde{J}_{\mu}(b_{0})}{b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g)}}}+\frac{\delta}{6},\quad\liminf_{x\rightarrow 0+}\eta_{g}(x)\geq\frac{\tilde{J}_{\mu}(b_{0})}{b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g)}}}-\frac{\delta}{2}.

Having proved the existence of the limits
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0+}\eta_{g}(x)
, …,
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0+}\eta_{g-m+1}(x)
we can proceed similarly to prove the existence of the limit
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0+}\eta_{g-m}(x)
.
Assume that
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0+}\eta_{g-m}(x)
does not exist, so

\limsup_{x\rightarrow 0+}\eta_{g-m}(x)-\liminf_{x\rightarrow 0+}\eta_{g-m}(x)\geq\delta>0.

(3.23)

Let
b_{0}\in(0,1)
be small enough so that

\tilde{J}_{\mu}(1)b_{0}^{\alpha_{i\left(g-m-1\right)}-\alpha_{i(g-m)}}<\frac{\delta}{8}.

(3.24)

Let us set in (
3.18
)
b=b_{0}
and then divide both sides of (
3.18
) by
b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g-m)}}
.
For
x>0
sufficiently close to
0
we have

\eta_{g-m}(x)-\frac{\delta}{8}\leq\frac{1}{b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g-m)}}}\sum_{i=i\left(g-m-1\right)+1}^{i\left(g-m\right)}\frac{{J}_{i}\left(b_{0}\cdot G_{i}(x)\right)}{J_{i}\left(G_{i}(x)\right)}\cdot\frac{J_{i}\left(G_{i}(x)\right)}{x}\leq\eta_{g-m}(x)+\frac{\delta}{8},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g-m)}}}\sum_{k=1}^{g-m-1}\left(\sum_{i=i\left(k-1\right)+1}^{i\left(k\right)}\frac{J_{i}\left(b_{0}\cdot G_{i}(x)\right)}{J_{i}\left(G_{i}(x)\right)}\cdot\frac{J_{i}\left(G_{i}(x)\right)}{x}\right)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{g-m-1}2b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(k)}-\alpha_{i(g-m)}}\eta_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\leq 2b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g-m-1)}-\alpha_{i(g-m)}}\tilde{J}_{\mu}(1)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{k=g-m+1}^{g}\frac{b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(k)}}\eta_{k}}{b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g-m)}}}-\frac{\delta}{8}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g-m)}}}\sum_{k=g-m+1}^{g}\sum_{i=i\left(k-1\right)+1}^{i\left(k\right)}\frac{{J}_{i}\left(b_{0}\cdot G_{i}(x)\right)}{J_{i}\left(G_{i}(x)\right)}\cdot\frac{J_{i}\left(G_{i}(x)\right)}{x}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=g-m+1}^{g}\frac{b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(k)}}\eta_{k}}{b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g-m)}}}+\frac{\delta}{8}

thus from (
3.18
), last three estimates and (
3.24
)

\displaystyle\eta_{g-m}(x)-\frac{\delta}{4}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{J_{\mu}(b_{0})}{b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g-m)}}}-\sum_{k=g-m+1}^{g}\frac{b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(k)}}\eta_{k}}{b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g-m)}}}

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{g-m}(x)+\frac{\delta}{4}+2\tilde{J}_{\mu}(1)b_{0}^{\alpha_{i(g-1)}-\alpha_{i(g)}}<\eta_{g-m}(x)+\frac{\delta}{2}.

But this contradicts (
3.23
).

Now we are left with the proof that for
k=1,2,\ldots,g
,
\alpha_{i(k)}\in(1,2]
. Since the Laplace exponent of
Z_{i}
is given by
(
3.2
), by Proposition
3.4
we necessarily have that
J_{i}
varies regularly with index
\alpha_{i}\in[1,2],i=1,2,...,d
. Thus it remains to prove that
\alpha_{i}>1,i=1,2,...,d
. If it was not true we would have
\alpha_{i(g)}=1
in (
3.20
) and
\eta_{g}>0
. Then

\lim_{b\rightarrow 0+}\tilde{J}_{\mu}(b)/b=\lim_{b\rightarrow 0+}J_{\mu}(b)/b=\eta_{g}>0,

but, again, by Proposition
3.4
it is not possible.

\square

3.2
Characterization of regularly varying Laplace exponents

In this section we reformulate the assumption that
J_{i},i=1,...,d
, vary regularly at zero
in terms of the behaviour of the Lévy measures of
Z_{i},i=1,...,d
. As our considerations are componentwise,
we write for simplicity
\nu(\textnormal{d}v):=\nu_{i}(\textnormal{d}v)
for the Lévy measure of
Z_{i}
and
J:=J_{i}
for its Laplace exponent.

Proposition 3.6

Let
\nu(\textnormal{d}v)
be such that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}(y^{2}\wedge y)\ \nu(dy)<+\infty.

(3.27)

Let
\tilde{\nu}(\textnormal{d}v)
be the measure

\tilde{\nu}(\textnormal{d}v):=v^{2}\nu(\textnormal{d}v),

and
\tilde{F}
its cumulative distribution function, i.e.

\tilde{F}(v):=\tilde{\nu}((0,v))=\int_{0}^{v}u^{2}\nu(\textnormal{d}u),\quad v\geq 0.

Then, for
\alpha\in(1,2)
, the following conditions are equivalent

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{J(bx)}{J(x)}=b^{\alpha},\quad b\geq 0,

(3.28)

\lim_{y\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\tilde{F}(by)}{\tilde{F}(y)}=b^{2-\alpha},\quad b\geq 0.

If, additionally,
\nu(\textnormal{d}v)
has a density function
g(v)
such that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}v^{2}g(v)\nu(\textnormal{d}v)=+\infty,

(3.29)

then (
3.28
) is equivalent
to the condition

\lim_{y\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{g(by)}{g(y)}=b^{-\alpha-1},\quad b>0.

Proof:
Under (
3.27
) the function
J
given by (
3.8
) is well defined for
b\geq 0
, twice differentiable and

\displaystyle J^{\prime}(b)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}v(1-e^{-bv})\nu(\textnormal{d}v),\quad J^{\prime\prime}(b)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}v^{2}e^{-bv}\nu(\textnormal{d}v),\quad b\geq 0,

see
[
9
]
, Lemma 8.1 and Lemma 8.2.
This implies that

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{J(bx)}{J(x)}

\displaystyle=b\cdot\lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{J^{\prime}(bx)}{J^{\prime}(x)}=b^{2}\cdot\lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{J^{\prime\prime}(bx)}{J^{\prime\prime}(x)}

\displaystyle=b^{2}\cdot\lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-bxv}v^{2}\nu(\textnormal{d}v)}{\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-xv}v^{2}\nu(\textnormal{d}v)}.

Consequently, by (
3.28
)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-bxv}v^{2}\nu(\textnormal{d}v)}{\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-xv}v^{2}\nu(\textnormal{d}v)}=b^{\alpha-2}.

(3.30)

Notice, that the left side is a quotient of two transforms of the measure
\tilde{\nu}(\textnormal{d}v)
.
By the Tauberian theorem we have that (
3.30
) holds if and only if

\frac{\tilde{F}(by)}{\tilde{F}(y)}\underset{y\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}b^{2-\alpha},\quad b\geq 0.

If
\nu(\textnormal{d}v)
has a density
g(v)
satisfying (
3.29
) then

\displaystyle\lim_{y\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\tilde{F}(by)}{\tilde{F}(y)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{y\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\int_{0}^{by}u^{2}g(u)\textnormal{d}u}{\int_{0}^{y}u^{2}g(u)\textnormal{d}u}=\lim_{y\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{b\cdot(by)^{2}g(by)}{y^{2}g(y)}

\displaystyle=b^{3}\cdot\lim_{y\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{g(by)}{g(y)}.

It follows that

\lim_{y\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{g(by)}{g(y)}=b^{-\alpha-1}.

which proves the result.
\square

Remark 3.7

By general characterization of regularly varying functions we see that the functions

\tilde{F}
and
g
from Proposition
3.6
must be of the forms

\tilde{F}(b)=b^{2-\alpha}L(b),\quad b\geq 0,

g(b)=b^{-\alpha-1}\tilde{L}(b),\quad b\geq 0,

where
L
and
\tilde{L}
are slowly varying functions at
+\infty
, i.e.

\frac{L(by)}{L(y)}\underset{y\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}1,\quad\frac{\tilde{L}(by)}{\tilde{L}(y)}\underset{y\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}1.

3.3
Generating equations on a plane

In this section we characterize all equations (
2.1
), with
d=2
, which generate affine models.
In view of Theorem
3.1
generating pairs
(G,Z)
are such that

\displaystyle J_{1}(bG_{1}(x))+J_{2}(bG_{2}(x))=x\tilde{J}_{\mu}(b),\quad b,x\geq 0,

(3.31)

where
\tilde{J}_{\mu}
takes one of the two following forms

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{\mu}(b)=\eta_{1}b^{\alpha_{1}},\quad b\geq 0,

(3.32)

or

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{\mu}(b)=\eta_{1}b^{\alpha_{1}}+\eta_{2}b^{\alpha_{2}},\quad b\geq 0,

(3.33)

where
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}>0
,
2\geq\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}>1
. We deduce from (
3.31
) the form of
G
and characterize the noise
Z
.

Theorem 3.8

Let
G(x)=(G_{1}(x),G_{2}(x))
be continuous functions such that
G_{1}(x)>0,G_{2}(x)>0,x>0
and
\frac{G_{2}(x)}{G_{1}(x)}\in C^{1}(0,+\infty)
.
Let
Z(t)=(Z_{1}(t),Z_{2}(t))
have independent coordinates of infinite variation with Laplace exponents varying regularly
with indices
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
, respectively, where
2\geq\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}>1
.

I)

If
\tilde{J}_{\mu}
is of the form (
3.32
)
then
(G,Z)
is a generating pair if and only if one of the following two cases holds:

a)

\displaystyle G(x)=c_{0}\ x^{1/\alpha_{1}}\cdot\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}G_{1}\\
G_{2},\end{array}\right),\quad x\geq 0,

where
c_{0}>0,G_{1}>0,G_{2}>0
and the process

G_{1}Z_{1}(t)+G_{2}Z_{2}(t),\quad t\geq 0,

is
\alpha_{1}
-stable.

b)

G(x)
is such that

\displaystyle c_{1}G^{\alpha_{1}}_{1}(x)+c_{2}G^{\alpha_{1}}_{2}(x)=\eta_{1}x,\quad x\geq 0,

(3.36)

with some constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
, and
Z_{1},Z_{2}
are
\alpha_{1}
-stable processes.

II)

If
\tilde{J}_{\mu}
is of the form (
3.33
), then
(G,Z)
is a generating pair if and only if

\displaystyle G_{1}(x)=\left(\frac{\eta_{1}}{c_{1}}x\right)^{1/\alpha_{1}},\quad G_{2}(x)=\left(\frac{\eta_{2}}{c_{2}}x\right)^{1/\alpha_{2}},\quad x\geq 0,

(3.37)

with
c_{1},c_{2}>0
and
Z_{1}
is
\alpha_{1}
-stable,
Z_{2}
is
\alpha_{2}
-stable.

Proof:
First let us consider the case when

\displaystyle\left(\frac{G_{2}(x)}{G_{1}(x)}\right)^{\prime}=0,\qquad x>0.

(3.38)

Then

G(x)
can be written in the form

\displaystyle G(x)=g(x)\cdot\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}G_{1}\\
G_{2},\end{array}\right),\quad x\geq 0,

with some function
g(x)\geq 0,x\geq 0
, and constants
G_{1}>0,G_{2}>0
. Equation (
2.1
)
amounts then to

\displaystyle dR(t)

\displaystyle=F(R(t))+g(R(t-))\left(G_{1}dZ_{1}(t)+G_{2}dZ_{2}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=F(R(t))+g(R(t-))d\tilde{Z}(t),\quad t\geq 0,

which is an equation driven by the one dimensional Lévy process
\tilde{Z}(t):=G_{1}Z_{1}(t)+G_{2}Z_{2}(t)
.
It follows that
\tilde{Z}
is
\alpha_{1}
-stable with
\alpha_{1}\in(1,2]
and that
g(x)=c_{0}x^{1/\alpha_{1}},c_{0}>0
.
Notice that
Z^{G(x)}(t)=c_{0}x^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}}\tilde{Z}
, so
J_{Z^{G(x)}}(b)=C_{\alpha_{1}}(c_{0}x^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}}b)^{\alpha_{1}}=xc_{0}^{\alpha_{1}}C_{\alpha_{1}}b^{\alpha_{1}}
.
Hence (
3.32
) holds and this proves
(Ia)
.

If (
3.38
) is not satisfied, then

\displaystyle\left(\frac{G_{2}(x)}{G_{1}(x)}\right)^{\prime}\neq 0,\quad x\in(\underline{x},\bar{x}),

(3.39)

in some interval
(\underline{x},\bar{x})\subset(0,+\infty)
. In the rest of the proof we consider this case and
prove
(Ib)
and
(II)
.

(Ib)
From the equation

\displaystyle J_{1}(bG_{1}(x))+J_{2}(bG_{2}(x))=x\eta_{1}b^{\alpha_{1}},\quad b\geq 0,\ x\geq 0,

(3.40)

we explicitely determine unknown functions. Inserting
b/G_{1}(x)
for
b
yields

\displaystyle J_{1}(b)+J_{2}\left(b\frac{G_{2}(x)}{G_{1}(x)}\right)=\eta_{1}\frac{x}{G_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}(x)}b^{\alpha_{1}},\quad b\geq 0,\quad x>0.

(3.41)

Differentiation over
x
yields

J_{2}^{\prime}\left(b\frac{G_{2}(x)}{G_{1}(x)}\right)\cdot b\left(\frac{G_{2}(x)}{G_{1}(x)}\right)^{\prime}=\eta_{1}\left(\frac{x}{G_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}(x)}\right)^{\prime}b^{\alpha_{1}},\quad b\geq 0,\quad x>0.

Using (
3.39
) and dividing by
\left(\frac{G_{2}(x)}{G_{1}(x)}\right)^{\prime}
leads to

J_{2}^{\prime}\left(b\frac{G_{2}(x)}{G_{1}(x)}\right)\cdot b=\eta_{1}\frac{\left(\frac{x}{G^{\alpha_{1}}_{1}(x)}\right)^{\prime}}{\left(\frac{G_{2}(x)}{G_{1}(x)}\right)^{\prime}}\cdot b^{\alpha_{1}},\quad b\geq 0,\quad x\in(\underline{x},\bar{x}).

By inserting
b\frac{G_{1}(x)}{G_{2}(x)}
for
b
one computes the derivative of
J_{2}
:

J_{2}^{\prime}(b)=\eta_{1}\frac{\left(\frac{x}{G^{\alpha_{1}}_{1}(x)}\right)^{\prime}\left(\frac{G_{1}(x)}{G_{2}(x)}\right)^{\alpha_{1}-1}}{\left(\frac{G_{2}(x)}{G_{1}(x)}\right)^{\prime}}\cdot b^{\alpha_{1}-1},\quad b>0,\quad x\in(\underline{x},\bar{x}).

Fixing
x
and integrating over
b
provides

\displaystyle J_{2}(b)=c_{2}b^{\alpha_{1}},\quad b>0,

(3.42)

with some
c_{2}\geq 0
. Actually
c_{2}>0
as
Z_{2}
is of infinite variation and
J_{2}
can not disappear.

By the symmetry of (
3.40
) the same conclusion holds for
J_{1}
, i.e.

\displaystyle J_{1}(b)=c_{1}b^{\alpha_{1}},\quad b>0,

(3.43)

with
c_{1}>0
. Using (
3.42
) and (
3.43
) in (
3.40
) gives us (
3.36
). This proves
(Ib)
.

II)
Solving the equation

\displaystyle J_{1}(bG_{1}(x))+J_{2}(bG_{2}(x))=x(\eta_{1}b^{\alpha_{1}}+\eta_{2}b^{\alpha_{2}}),\quad b,x\geq 0,

(3.44)

in the same way as we solved (
3.40
) yields that

\displaystyle J_{1}(b)=c_{1}b^{\alpha_{1}}+c_{2}b^{\alpha_{2}},\quad J_{2}(b)=d_{1}b^{\alpha_{1}}+d_{2}b^{\alpha_{2}},\quad b\geq 0,

(3.45)

with
c_{1},c_{2},d_{1},d_{2}\geq 0
,
c_{1}+c_{2}>0,d_{1}+d_{2}>0
. From (
3.44
) and (
3.45
) we can specify the following conditions for
G
:

\displaystyle c_{1}G_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}(x)+d_{1}G_{2}^{\alpha_{1}}(x)

\displaystyle=\eta_{1}x,

(3.46)

\displaystyle c_{2}G_{1}^{\alpha_{2}}(x)+d_{2}G_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}(x)

\displaystyle=\eta_{2}x.

(3.47)

We show that
c_{1}>0,c_{2}=0,d_{1}=0,d_{2}>0
by excluding the opposite cases.

If
c_{1}>0,c_{2}>0
, one computes from (
3.46
)-(
3.47
) that

\displaystyle G_{1}(x)=\left(\frac{1}{c_{1}}(\eta_{1}x-d_{1}G_{2}^{\alpha_{1}}(x))\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}}=\left(\frac{1}{c_{2}}(\eta_{2}x-d_{2}G_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}(x))\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}},\quad x\geq 0.

(3.48)

This means that, for each
x\geq 0
, the value
G_{2}(x)
is a solution of the following equation in the
y
-variable

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{c_{1}}(\eta_{1}x-d_{1}y^{\alpha_{1}})\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}}=\left(\frac{1}{c_{2}}(\eta_{2}x-d_{2}y^{\alpha_{2}})\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}},

(3.49)

with
y\in\left[0,\left(\frac{\gamma_{1}x}{d_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}}\wedge\left(\frac{\gamma_{2}x}{d_{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}}\right]
.
If
d_{1}=0
or
d_{2}=0
we compute
y=y(x)
from (
3.49
) and see that its positivity is broken close to zero or for large
x
. We need to exclude the case
d_{1}>0,d_{2}>0
. However, in the case
c_{1},c_{2},d_{1},d_{2}>0
equation (
3.49
) has no solution because, for large
x>0
, the left side of (
3.49
) is strictly less then the right side. This inequality follows from Proposition
3.9
proven below.

So, we proved that
c_{1}\cdot c_{2}=0
and similarly one proves that
d_{1}\cdot d_{2}=0
.
The case
c_{1}=0,c_{2}>0,d_{1}>0,d_{2}=0
can be rejected because then
J_{1}
would vary regularly with index
\alpha_{2}

and
J_{2}
with index
\alpha_{1}
, which is a contradiction. It follows that
c_{1}>0,c_{2}=0,d_{1}=0,d_{2}>0
and in this case we obtain (
3.37
) from (
3.46
) and (
3.47
).
\square

Proposition 3.9

Let
a,b,c,d>0
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
2\geq\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}>1
. Then for large
x>0
the following inequalities are true

\displaystyle\Big(ax-(bx-cz)^{\gamma}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}-dz>0,\qquad z\in\Big[0,\frac{b}{c}x\Big],

(3.50)

\displaystyle(bx-cy^{\alpha_{1}})^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}}<(ax-dy^{\alpha_{2}})^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}},\quad y\in\Big[0,\Big(\frac{b}{c}x\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}}\wedge\Big(\frac{a}{d}x\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}}\Big].

(3.51)

Proof:
First we prove (
3.50
) and write it in the equivalent form

\displaystyle ax\geq(dz)^{\gamma}+(bx-cz)^{\gamma}=:h(z).

(3.52)

Since

h^{\prime}(z)=\gamma\Big(d^{\gamma}z^{\gamma-1}-c(bx-cz)^{\gamma-1}\Big),

h^{\prime\prime}(z)=\gamma(\gamma-1)\Big(d^{\gamma}z^{\gamma-2}+c^{2}(bx-cz)^{\gamma-2}\Big)<0,\quad z\in\Big[0,\frac{b}{c}x\Big],

the function
h
is concave and attains its maximum at point

z_{0}:=\theta x:=\frac{bc^{\frac{1}{\gamma-1}}}{d^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}+c^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}}x\in\Big[0,\frac{b}{c}x\Big],

which is a root of
h^{\prime}
. It follows that

\displaystyle h(z)\leq h(\theta x)

\displaystyle=(\theta x)^{\gamma}+(bx-c\theta x)^{\gamma}

\displaystyle=(\theta^{\gamma}+(b-c\theta)^{\gamma})x^{\gamma}<ax.

\square

3.4
An example in higher dimensions

In Theorem

Example 3.10

Let us consider a process

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}G_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}(x)+\tilde{\gamma}_{3}G_{3}^{\alpha_{2}}(x)=x\eta_{2},

which allows us to determine

and

\displaystyle 0\leq G_{3}(x)\leq\left(\frac{\eta_{1}}{\gamma_{3}}x\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}}\wedge\left(\frac{\eta_{2}}{\tilde{\gamma}_{3}}x\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}},\quad x\geq 0.

It follows that

Spherical Lévy noise

This section deals with equation (
2.1

Z
is a spherical Lévy process with characteristic triplet

(a,Q,\nu(\textnormal{d}y))
. Recall, the Lévy measure
\nu(\textnormal{d}y)
is described in terms of a finite spherical measure
\lambda(\textnormal{d}\xi)
on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and a radial measure
\gamma(\textnormal{d}r)
on

As

is a martingale, by (
2.5

\displaystyle\int_{\mid y\mid>1}\mid y\mid\nu(dy)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\lambda(\textnormal{d}\xi)\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mid r\xi\mid\gamma(dr)=\lambda(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\cdot\int_{1}^{+\infty}r\gamma(dr)<+\infty.

(4.1)

The jump part of

\int_{\mid y\mid\leq 1}\mid y\mid\nu(dy)=\lambda(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\cdot\int_{0}^{1}r\gamma(dr)=+\infty.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}r\gamma(dr)=+\infty.

Furthermore, the measure

\lambda\left\{\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}:\langle G(0),\xi\rangle<0\right\}=0

which implies that

\langle G(0),\xi\rangle\geq 0\ \text{for any }\xi\in\text{supp}\ \lambda.

(4.4)

A consequence of (

\displaystyle G(0)=0.

Indeed, by Proposition

(A(b)), the jump part of

is of finite variation. Therefore

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}v\ \nu_{G(0)}(\textnormal{d}v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle G(0),y\rangle\nu(\textnormal{d}y)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\lambda(d\xi)\int_{0}^{+\infty}\langle G(0),r\xi\rangle\gamma(\textnormal{d}r)

Corollary 4.2

Let the assumptions of Theorem

be satisfied. If

is a generating pair, then the continuous (Wiener) part of the process

It follows from Proposition

(A(a)) guarantees that

Lemma 4.6

The function
H:[0,+\infty)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

H(z)=e^{-z}-1+z,

is convex, strictly increasing and

\displaystyle\min\{1,t^{2}\}\cdot H(z)\leq H(tz)\leq\max\{1,t^{2}\}\cdot H(z),\quad z\geq 0,t>0.

(4.11)

Proof:
Since
H^{\prime}(z)=1-e^{-z}
the monotonicity and convexity of

follows. For

it follows from the monotonicity of

H(tz)\geq H(z)=\min\{1,t^{2}\}H(z).

From (

\frac{d}{ds}\ln H(s)=\frac{H^{\prime}(s)}{H(s)}=\frac{1-e^{-s}}{e^{-s}-1+s}\leq\frac{2}{s},\quad s>0,

and, consequently, we obtain that for

\ln H(tz)-\ln H(z)\leq\int_{z}^{tz}\frac{2}{s}ds=\ln t^{2}.

Thus

\displaystyle\min\{1,t^{2}\}H(z)=H(z)\leq H(tz)\leq t^{2}H(z)=\max\{1,t^{2}\}H(z).

(4.12)

Using the monotonicity of

) we see that for

H(tz)\leq H(z)=H\left(\frac{1}{t}tz\right)\leq\frac{1}{t^{2}}H(tz),

so also for

\min\{1,t^{2}\}H(z)=t^{2}H(z)\leq H(tz)\leq H(z)=\max\{1,t^{2}\}H(z).

\square

Lemma 4.11

Let
p,q>0
be such that
p/q\notin\mathbb{Q}
. Let us define the set

G:=\{mp+nq;\quad m,n,=1,2,...\}.

Then for each
\delta>0
there exists a number
M(\delta)>0
such that

\forall x\geq M(\delta)\quad\exists\ g\in G\quad\text{such that}\ \mid x-g\mid\leq\delta.

Moreover, the set

D:=\{mp+nq;\quad m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\},

is dense in
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof:
Since
p/q\notin\mathbb{Q}
, at least one of
p,q
, say
q
, is irrational. For simplicity assume that
p=1
and consider the sequence

r(jq),j=1,2,...\quad\text{where}\ r(x):=x\ \text{mod}\ 1,

of fractional parts of the numbers
jq,j=1,2,...
. Recall, Weyl’s equidistribution theorem states that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\longrightarrow+\infty}\frac{\sharp\{j\leq N:r(jq)\in[a,b]\}}{N}=b-a

(4.33)

for any
[a,b]\subseteq[0,1)
if and only if
q
is irrational.

For fixed
\delta>0
and
n
such that
1/n<\delta
let us consider a partition of
[0,1)
of the form

[0,1)=\bigcup_{k=0}^{n-1}A_{k},\quad A_{k}:=[k/n,(k+1)/n).

For a natural number
N
let us consider the set
R_{N}:=\{r(jq):j=1,2,...,N\}
. By (
4.33
), for each
k=0,1,...,n-1
, there exists
N_{k}
such that

R_{N_{k}}\cap A_{k}\neq\emptyset.

Then for
\bar{N}:=\max\{N_{0},N_{1},...,N_{n-1}\}
we have

R_{\bar{N}}\cap A_{k}\neq\emptyset,\quad k=0,1,...,n-1.

Let
M=M(\delta):=\bar{N}q
. Then, for
x\geq M
, there exists a number
N_{x}\leq\bar{N}
such that

\displaystyle\mid r(N_{x}q)-r(x)\mid\leq\frac{1}{n}.

(4.34)

Then for the number

g:=\lfloor x\rfloor-\lfloor N_{x}q\rfloor+N_{x}g\in G

the following holds

\displaystyle\mid x-g\mid

\displaystyle=\mid x-(\lfloor x\rfloor-\lfloor N_{x}q\rfloor+N_{x}q)\mid

\displaystyle=\mid\lfloor x\rfloor+r(x)-\lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor N_{x}q\rfloor-N_{x}q\mid

\displaystyle=\mid r(x)-r(N_{x}q)\mid\leq 1/n<\delta,

where the last inequality follows from (
4.34
).

The density of
D
is an immediate consequence of the first part of the Lemma. Indeed, for
x<M(\delta)
and
g\in G
such that
x+g>M(\delta)
there exists
\tilde{g}\in G
such that
\mid x+g-\tilde{g}\mid<\delta
.

The general case with
p\neq 1
can be proven in the same way but requires a generalized version of Weyl’s theorem, which says that the numbers
r_{p}(nq),n=1,2,...
, where
r_{p}(x):=x\ mod\ p
, are equidistributed on
[0,p)
if and only if
p/q\notin\mathbb{Q}
. This can be proven by a straightforward modification of the original arguments of Weyl.

\square

Proposition 5.1

Let
G:[0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be continuous. If the equation (
2.1
) has a non-negative strong solution for any initial condition
R(0)=x\geq 0
, then

\displaystyle\forall x\geq 0\quad\nu{\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x+\langle G(x),y\rangle<0\}}=0.

(5.11)

In particular, the support of the measure
\nu_{G(x)}(\textnormal{d}v)
is contained in
[-x,+\infty)
.

Proof:
Let us assume to the contrary, that for some
x\geq 0

\nu{\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x+\langle G(x),y\rangle<0\}}>0.

Then there exists
c>0
such that

\nu{\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x+\langle G(x),y\rangle<-c\}}>0.

Let
A\subseteq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x+\langle G(x),y\rangle<-c\}
be a Borel set separated from zero. By the continuity of
G
we have that for some
\varepsilon>0
:

\displaystyle\tilde{x}+\langle G(\tilde{x}),y\rangle<-\frac{c}{2},\quad\tilde{x}\in[(x-\varepsilon)\vee 0,x+\varepsilon],\quad y\in A.

(5.12)

Let
Z^{2}
be a Lévy processes with characteristics
(0,0,\nu^{2}(dy))
, where
\nu^{2}(dy):=\mathbf{1}_{A}(y)\nu(dy)
and
Z^{1}
be defined by
Z(t)=Z^{1}(t)+Z^{2}(t)
. Then
Z^{1},Z^{2}
are independent and
Z^{2}
is a compound Poisson process. Let us consider
the following equations

\displaystyle dR(t)=F(R(t))dt+\langle G(R(t-)),dZ(t)\rangle,\quad R(0)=x,

\displaystyle dR^{1}(t)=F(R^{1}(t))dt+\langle G(R^{1}(t-)),dZ^{1}(t)\rangle,\quad R^{1}(0)=x.

For the exit time
\tau_{1}
of
R^{1}
from the set
[(x-\varepsilon)\vee 0,x+\varepsilon]
and the first jump time
\tau_{2}
of
Z^{2}
we can find

T>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}>T,\tau_{2}<T)=\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}>T)\mathbb{P}(\tau_{2}<T)>0
. On the set
\{\tau_{1}>T,\tau_{2}<T\}
we have
R(\tau_{2}-)=R^{1}(\tau_{2}-)
and therefore

R(\tau_{2})=R^{1}(\tau_{2}-)+\langle G(R^{1}(\tau_{2}-)),\triangle Z^{2}(\tau_{2})\rangle<-\frac{c}{2}.

In the last inequality we used (
5.12
). This contradicts the positivity of
R
.
\square
