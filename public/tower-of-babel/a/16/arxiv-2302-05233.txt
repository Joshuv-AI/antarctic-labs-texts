Abstract
. We introduce the basic notions and present examples and results on Lie categories – categories internal to the category of smooth manifolds. Demonstrating how the units of a Lie category 𝒞 \mathcal{C} dictate the behavior of its invertible morphisms 𝒢 ⁡ ( 𝒞 ) \mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C}) , we develop sufficient conditions for 𝒢 ⁡ ( 𝒞 ) \mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C}) to form a Lie groupoid. We show that the construction of Lie algebroids from the theory of Lie groupoids carries through, and ask when the Lie algebroid of 𝒢 ⁡ ( 𝒞 ) \mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C}) is recovered. We reveal that the lack of invertibility assumption on morphisms leads to a natural generalization of rank from linear algebra, develop its general properties, and show how the existence of an extension 𝒞 ↪ 𝒢 \mathcal{C}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{G} of a Lie category to a Lie groupoid affects the ranks of morphisms and the algebroids of 𝒞 \mathcal{C} . Furthermore, certain completeness results for invariant vector fields on Lie monoids and Lie categories with well-behaved boundaries are obtained. Interpreting the developed framework in the context of physical processes, we yield a rigorous approach to the theory of statistical thermodynamics by observing that entropy change, associated to a physical process, is a functor.

1.
Introduction

Since its conception, category theory has proven to provide a unified framework for the language of mathematics, by making use of the observation that objects and morphisms thereof arise regardless of the mathematical field one considers. This paradigm has also been adopted by physicists (see e.g.
[
7
]
), namely that one can interpret physical states (contained in a certain phase space) as objects, and physical processes as morphisms between them, thus forming a category which corresponds to the physical system at hand. In order to provide such a realization of a given physical system, the corresponding category should ideally have the capacity to straightforwardly describe the phenomena which pertain to the given physical system, and also capture the means for describing and calculating relevant physical quantities. For a physicist, the latter is generally done using the basic tools of calculus, in terms of a preferred set of coordinates.

There is a natural way of obtaining such a unified framework for describing physical processes, by intertwining category theory with the theory of smooth manifolds. That is, we require the set of objects (states) and the set of morphisms (processes) of an abstract category to be a pair of smooth manifolds, and that the composition law
(f,g)\mapsto fg
is a smooth map (concatenation of two processes); the mathematical structure obtained is that of a category internal to the category
\mathbf{Diff}
of smooth manifolds. Historically, this kind of a mathematical structure, which we will define in more precise terms as a Lie category, was first introduced and briefly studied by Charles Ehresmann in his seminal paper
[
4
]
; in the same paper, Ehresmann further focused on the notion of a Lie groupoid, which additionally imposes that all morphisms are invertible. This latter notion has nowadays been thoroughly researched and continues to have a status of an active field of research, whereas the same cannot be said for Lie categories; apart from the work of Ehresmann, this is the first paper dedicated to their systematic treatment. The main reason behind the fact that Lie categories have gained negligible attention compared to Lie groupoids lies in the fact that the assumption on morphisms being invertible implies that all left and right translations are diffeomorphisms, which accounts for certain preferable properties of the structure of a Lie groupoid, that will also be highlighted in this paper.

Our motivation for studying Lie categories, and not merely groupoids, stems from the physical interpretation that invertible morphisms correspond to reversible processes, and in physics not all processes in a given system are reversible; this is well known from the theory of thermodynamics, where reversible processes are precisely those where the change of entropy when transiting between two states is zero; we will describe this in precise terms in Section
8
. Another, perhaps more notorious motivation, is given by irreversibility of wave-function collapse in quantum theory, which was actually our initial motivation for dropping the invertibility assumption.

As we will see, it will turn out to be desirable to allow the space of morphisms of a Lie category to possess a boundary – we will encounter both, mathematical and physical examples where the boundary of the space of morphisms will play a distinguished role. To temporarily appease and motivate the reader to this regard, let us briefly note that Lie monoids are examples of Lie categories with the set of objects a singleton, and that the monoid
[0,\infty)
– either for multiplication or addition – provides a first example of a Lie category with boundary. This simple example already shows certain intriguing qualities: for instance, considering
[0,\infty)
for multiplication, all its invertible elements are contained in its interior, and considering
[0,\infty)
for addition, its only invertible element is in its boundary. This phenomenon, as may be expected, is one of the features of Lie categories, namely that the units dictate the behavior of invertibles.

Overall, we aim to convince the reader that the interplay of geometrical and categorical structures alone (without the invertibility assumption) provides new exciting questions which were so far overlooked within the scope of Lie theory. Let us summarize our main objectives.

(i)

To demonstrate that Lie categories allow for an abundance of interesting examples which have so far been missed in the theory of Lie groupoids.

(ii)

To expose some ideas and constructions which carry through to Lie categories from the theory of Lie groupoids, e.g. the Lie algebroid construction.

(iii)

To inspect the relation between Lie groupoids and Lie categories, and show that novel notions can be obtained when invertibility is dropped.

(iv)

Last, but not least, to provide an algorithm for constructing Lie categories that describe physical systems by making use of the notion of entropy, and to reveal that the mathematical structure implicitly present in the foundations of statistical thermodynamics is that of a Lie category.

Notation

All our categories are small, i.e. the classes of objects and morphisms are sets. We will denote a category by
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
, where
\mathcal{C}
is the set of morphisms,
\mathcal{X}
is the set of objects, and the two arrows indicate the
source map

s\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
and
target map

t\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
, which are defined by

s(x\xrightarrow{g}y)=x,\quad t(x\xrightarrow{g}y)=y,

for any morphism
g\colon x\rightarrow y
in
\mathcal{C}
. Alongside these two maps, a category
\mathcal{C}
comes equipped with the
composition map

m\colon{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}\rightarrow\mathcal{C},\quad(g,h)\mapsto gh,

where
{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}=\{(g,h)\in\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\;|\;s(g)=t(h)\}
is the set of all pairs of
composable morphisms
. Moreover,
\mathcal{C}
comes equipped with the
unit map

u\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{C},\quad x\mapsto 1_{x}.

We also define

\mathcal{C}_{x}=s^{-1}(x),\quad\mathcal{C}^{y}=t^{-1}(y),\quad\mathcal{C}_{x}^{y}=\mathcal{C}_{x}\cap\mathcal{C}^{y},

and call
\mathcal{C}_{x}
the
source fibre
at
x
, and
\mathcal{C}^{y}
the
target fibre
at
y
. Note that any morphism
g\in\mathcal{C}
determines the maps

\displaystyle L_{g}\colon\mathcal{C}^{s(g)}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{t(g)},\quad L_{g}(h)=gh,

(1.1)

\displaystyle R_{g}\colon\mathcal{C}_{t(g)}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{s(g)},\quad R_{g}(h)=hg,

called the
left translation
and
right translation
by
g
, which are just the pre-composition and post-composition by
g
, respectively.

2.
Basic definitions and examples

Definition 2.1
.

A
Lie category
is a small category
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
, where
\mathcal{C}
is a smooth manifold with or without boundary,
\mathcal{X}
is a smooth manifold without boundary, and there holds:

(i)

The source and target maps
s,t\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
are smooth submersions.

(ii)

The unit map
u\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
and the composition map
m\colon{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
are smooth.

If
\mathcal{C}
has a boundary, we also assume that
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
has a
regular boundary
, that is:

(iii)

The restrictions
\partial s,\partial t\colon\partial\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
of
s
and
t
are smooth submersions.

Remark 2.2
.

Given any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, assumptions (i) and (iii) ensure that
\mathcal{C}_{x}
and
\mathcal{C}^{x}
are neat submanifolds of
\mathcal{C}
(see
[
6
, p. 60]
or
[
10
, Proposition 4.2.9]
), that is:

\displaystyle\partial(\mathcal{C}_{x})=\mathcal{C}_{x}\cap\partial\mathcal{C}\quad\text{and}\quad\partial(\mathcal{C}^{x})=\mathcal{C}^{x}\cap\partial\mathcal{C}.

(2.3)

Moreover, assumptions (i) and (iii) ensure that the set

{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}=(s\times t)^{-1}(\Delta_{\mathcal{X}})

of composable morphisms is a neat submanifold of
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
, that is:

\displaystyle\partial({\mathcal{C}}^{(2)})={\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}\cap\partial(\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C})={\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}\cap(\mathcal{C}\times\partial\mathcal{C}\cup\partial\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}),

(2.4)

by transversality theorem, see Corollary
A.7
. This corollary also ensures that if
\mathcal{C}
has a boundary, the corner points of
{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}
are precisely the composable pairs in
\partial\mathcal{C}\times\partial\mathcal{C}
; moreover, the tangent space of
{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}
at a composable pair
(g,h)
equals

\displaystyle T_{(g,h)}\mathcal{C}^{(2)}=\{(v,w)\in T_{g}\mathcal{C}\oplus T_{h}\mathcal{C}\;|\;\operatorname{d}\!{s}(v)=\operatorname{d}\!{t}(w)\}.

(2.5)

Smoothness of
{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}
implies that the requirement of smoothness of the composition map
m\colon{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
makes sense, and furthermore that left and right translations
L_{g},R_{g}
are smooth maps between appropriate fibres, as defined by equations (
1.1
); we thus obtain a covariant functor
\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathbf{Diff}
, given on objects as
x\mapsto\mathcal{C}^{x}
and on morphisms as
g\mapsto L_{g}
, and a contravariant one given by
x\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{x}
,
g\mapsto R_{g}
.

Remark 2.6
.

The unit map
u\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
of a Lie category
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
is an embedding, which is a consequence of the fact that it is a smooth section of the source (and target) map, hence an injective immersion which is a homeomorphism onto its image, whose continuous inverse is given by
s|_{u(\mathcal{X})}
.

Definition 2.7
.

A
morphism
of Lie categories is a smooth functor
F\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
. A Lie category
\mathcal{C}
is said to be a
Lie subcategory
of
\mathcal{D}
, if there is an injective immersive morphism
F\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
of Lie categories.

Remark
2.6
implies that any morphism of Lie categories induces a smooth map between the respective object manifolds of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
. We now turn to examples of Lie categories.

Example 2.8
.

In the case when the object space
\mathcal{X}
is a singleton, a Lie category
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\{*\}
will be called a
Lie monoid
. Simply put, a Lie monoid is a monoid
M
together with a structure of a smooth manifold with or without boundary, such that the multiplication
m\colon M\times M\rightarrow M
is smooth.

Concrete examples of Lie monoids frequently arise as embedded submonoids of Lie groups. For instance, we may consider the closed ray
[0,\infty)\subset\mathbb{R}
for addition, or more generally, the
n
-dimensional half-space
\mathbb{H}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times[0,\infty)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which is a commutative Lie monoid for the usual addition. On the other hand, the closed ray
[0,\infty)
for multiplication is not

(i)

The set
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
of square

\operatorname{End}(V)

(ii)

The following example generalizes the similar notion of triviality from the theory of Lie groupoids.

Example 2.9
.

Let
X
be a smooth manifold without boundary and
M
a Lie monoid. A
trivial Lie category
is defined by
\mathcal{C}=X\times M\times X
and
\mathcal{X}=X
, with
s=\mathrm{pr}_{3},t=\mathrm{pr}_{1}
and composition as

(z,g,y)(y,h,x)=(z,gh,x).

That composition is smooth follows from the smoothness of multiplication in
M
. In the case when
M=\{e\}
is a trivial monoid, we obtain the well-known
pair groupoid
.

The next example reveals the spirit of the notion of a Lie category – that is, it can be thought of as a smooth family of endomorphisms of an abstract structure, parametrized by the base manifold
\mathcal{X}
. This is aligned with the philosophy that a Lie groupoid can be thought of as a smooth collection of automorphisms (symmetries) of a structure parametrized by
\mathcal{X}
.

Example 2.10
.

Let
\pi\colon E\rightarrow X
be a vector bundle over a smooth manifold
X
without boundary, whose typical fibre is a fixed vector space
V
. The
endomorphism category
of
E\rightarrow X
is the category
\operatorname{End}(E)\rightrightarrows X
, where the set of morphisms is defined as the set

\operatorname{End}(E)=\{\xi\colon E_{x}\rightarrow E_{y}\;|\;\xi\text{ is linear},x,y\in X\}

of linear homomorphisms between the fibres of
E\rightarrow X
, and the structure maps
s,t,m,u
are defined in the obvious way. To show that
\operatorname{End}(E)
admits a structure of a Lie category without boundary, we must define a smooth structure on
\operatorname{End}(E)
; it is induced by local trivializations on
E
in the following way. Denote by

\{U_{i}\times V\xrightarrow{\psi_{i}}\pi^{-1}(U_{i})\;|\;{i\in I}\}

an atlas of local trivializations of
E
over an open cover
(U_{i})_{i\in I}
of
X
, and denote by
\tau_{ij}\colon U_{i}\cap U_{j}\rightarrow\operatorname{GL}(V)
the respective transition maps, i.e.
\psi_{i}^{-1}\psi_{j}(x,v)=(x,\tau_{ij}(x)v)
. For any two indices
i,j\in I
, we define the map

\displaystyle\Psi_{i}^{j}\colon U_{j}\times\operatorname{End}(V)\times U_{i}\rightarrow\operatorname{End}(E)^{U_{j}}_{U_{i}}=s^{-1}(U_{i})\cap t^{-1}(U_{j})

\displaystyle\Psi_{i}^{j}(y,A,x)(e|_{x})=\psi_{j}(y,A\mathrm{pr}_{V}\psi^{-1}_{i}(e|_{x})),

whose inverse is

(\Psi_{i}^{j})^{-1}(\xi\colon E_{x}\rightarrow E_{y})=(y,v\mapsto\mathrm{pr}_{V}\psi_{j}^{-1}\xi\psi_{i}(x,v),x).

The smoothness of transition maps in this atlas is easily checked by computing

\displaystyle(\Psi_{k}^{l})^{-1}\Psi_{i}^{j}(y,A,x)=(y,\tau_{jl}(y)^{-1}A\tau_{ik}(x),x),

where
x\in U_{i}\cap U_{k}
and
y\in U_{j}\cap U_{l}
. From the local charts, it is clear that
s
and
t
are submersions, and the smoothness of composition map
m
follows from smoothness of multiplication in
\operatorname{End}(V)
; finally, this composition is bilinear when restricted to
\operatorname{End}(E)^{z}_{y}\times\operatorname{End}(E)^{y}_{x}\subset\operatorname{End}(E){}^{(2)}
, so
\operatorname{End}(E)
is moreover enriched over the category
\mathbf{Vect}
of vector spaces.

Example 2.11

(Bundles of Lie monoids)
.

A Lie category with coinciding source and target map
s=t=:p
is called a
bundle of Lie monoids
. In this case, two morphisms are composable if, and only if, they are in the same fibre of
p
.

A concrete example of such a Lie category is the
endomorphism bundle

E^{*}\otimes E\rightarrow X
of a vector bundle
E\rightarrow X
, with the composition given on simple tensors as
(\varphi_{2}\otimes v_{2})(\varphi_{1}\otimes v_{1})=\varphi_{2}(v_{1})\,\varphi_{1}\otimes v_{2}
, and extended by bilinearity; this can easily be identified with the composition of linear maps
E_{x}\rightarrow E_{x}
, so
E^{*}\otimes E\subset\operatorname{End}(E)
is a subcategory of
\operatorname{End}(E)
. Using Lemma
2.15
, it is not hard to see that it is actually an embedded Lie subcategory of
\operatorname{End}(E)
, with respect to the inclusion map.

Another concrete example of a bundle of Lie monoids is the
exterior bundle
,

\Lambda(E)=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{\operatorname{rank}E}\Lambda^{k}(E),

of an
\mathbb{F}
-vector bundle
E\rightarrow X
, i.e.
\Lambda(E)
consists of all multivectors in
E
, and composition of
\alpha\in\Lambda^{k}(E_{x})
and
\beta\in\Lambda^{l}(E_{x})
is given as
\alpha\wedge\beta
. The units are given by
1_{x}=1\in\mathbb{F}=\Lambda^{0}(E_{x})
, for any
x\in X
. Again, the given composition map is smooth, which follows easily from bilinearity of wedge product; moreover,
\Lambda(E)
is again enriched over
\mathbf{Vect}
. In general, bundles of Lie monoids enriched over
Vect
would rightfully be called
smooth bundles of unital associative algebras
.

Example 2.12

(Action categories)
.

An
action
of a Lie monoid
M
on a smooth manifold
X
is a smooth map
\phi\colon M\times X\rightarrow X
, denoted by
\phi(g,x)=gx
, which satisfies
ex=x
and
g(hx)=(gh)x
, for any
x\in X
and
g,h\in M
. We observe that contrary to the case of Lie group actions, the map
\phi
may not be submersive since the action is no longer by automorphisms of
X
, thus the target map in the naïve generalization of the action groupoid would not be a submersion.

To remedy this, we construct the
action category
of a given Lie monoid action
\phi\colon M\times X\rightarrow X
as follows. Denote by

M\ltimes X=\{(g,x)\in M\times X\;|\;\phi\text{ is a submersion at }(g,x)\}

the set of regular points of the action
\phi
.

2

2

Intuitively, the action category accounts only for the non-critical dynamics pertaining to the given action.
Defining the structure maps as usual, i.e.
s(g,x)=x
,
t(g,x)=gx
, the units as
u(x)=(e,x)
, and the composition as

(g,hx)(h,x)=(gh,x),

we obtain a Lie category
M\ltimes X\rightrightarrows X
. Indeed, since
M\ltimes X\subset M\times X
is an open subset, we only need to check that if
\phi
is a submersion at the points
(h,x)
and
(g,hx)
, it is also a submersion at
(gh,x)
. To that end, we first notice that we may write the condition
g(hx)=(gh)x
as an equality of maps
M\times M\times X\rightarrow X
,

\phi\circ(\mathrm{pr}_{1},\phi\circ(\mathrm{pr}_{2},\mathrm{pr}_{3}))=\phi\circ(m\circ(\mathrm{pr}_{1},\mathrm{pr}_{2}),\mathrm{pr}_{3}),

where
m\colon M\times M\rightarrow M
denotes the multiplication in the Lie monoid
M
. Differentiating this equality at
(g,h,x)
, we obtain

\operatorname{d}\!{\phi}_{(g,hx)}\circ(\mathrm{pr}_{1},\operatorname{d}\!{\phi}_{(g,x)}\circ(\mathrm{pr}_{2},\mathrm{pr}_{3}))=\operatorname{d}\!{\phi}_{(gh,x)}\circ(\operatorname{d}\!{m}_{(g,h)}\circ(\mathrm{pr}_{1},\mathrm{pr}_{2}),\mathrm{pr}_{3}).

By assumption, the left-hand side is surjective, thus the same holds for the first map on the right-hand side. Hence
M\ltimes X\rightrightarrows X
is a category and thus a Lie category.

Example 2.13
.

A simple, yet important example of a Lie category is the
order category
of
\mathbb{R}
, which is defined as the wide subcategory of the pair groupoid
\mathcal{G}=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}
, given by

\mathcal{C}=\{(y,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\;|\;x\leq y\}.

The space of morphisms is thus the half-space below the diagonal in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and the source and target maps are the projections to the vertical and the horizontal axis, respectively, implying that the boundary of
\mathcal{C}
is regular. Moreover, since the inversion in the pair groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is given by the reflection over the diagonal, the units in
\mathcal{C}
are precisely the elements of the diagonal, and these are the only invertibles.

Although simple, this example has an important property:
\mathcal{C}
can be seen as the preimage of the set
[0,\infty)
under the functor

f\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow(\mathbb{R},+),\quad f(y,x)=y-x.

The following example is a generalization of this; we will make use of it when considering applications to statistical thermodynamics in Section
8
.

Example 2.14
.

Suppose a Lie category
\mathcal{D}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
without boundary is given, together with a smooth functor
f\colon\mathcal{D}\rightarrow(\mathbb{R},+)
such that:

(i)

0\in\mathbb{R}
is a regular value of
f
.

(ii)

s|_{f^{-1}(0)}
and
t|_{f^{-1}(0)}
are submersions.

Then the preimage
\mathcal{C}=f^{-1}\big([0,\infty)\big)
is a Lie category. Indeed, since
0
is a regular value of
f
,
\mathcal{C}
is a smooth embedded submanifold in
\mathcal{D}
with boundary
\partial\mathcal{C}=f^{-1}(0)
, see e.g.
[
6
, p. 62]
. Moreover, since
f^{-1}\big((0,\infty)\big)=\operatorname{Int}\mathcal{C}
is open in
\mathcal{D}
, the restrictions
s|_{\mathcal{C}},t|_{\mathcal{C}}
of the source and target maps to
\mathcal{C}
are submersions. Functoriality of
f
implies that
\mathcal{C}
is a wide subcategory of
\mathcal{D}
with all invertibles contained within
\partial\mathcal{C}
, and moreover it also implies that
\partial\mathcal{C}
is a wide subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
. The assumption (ii) enables us to use Lemma
2.15
below to conclude that
\mathcal{C}
is an embedded Lie subcategory of
\mathcal{D}
.

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a wide subcategory of a Lie category
\mathcal{D}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
. Suppose
\mathcal{C}
is also an embedded submanifold of
\mathcal{D}
, such that
s|_{\mathcal{C}},t|_{\mathcal{C}}
are submersions and either of the following holds:

(i)

\mathcal{C}
has no boundary.

(ii)

\mathcal{C}
has a boundary, and
s|_{\partial\mathcal{C}},t|_{\partial\mathcal{C}}
are submersions.

Then
\mathcal{C}
is an embedded Lie subcategory of
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

The only thing needed to be proven is that the restriction
m|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}}\colon{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
of the composition map
m\colon{\mathcal{D}}^{(2)}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
, is smooth. To that end, it is enough to check that
{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}
is a submanifold of
{\mathcal{D}}^{(2)}
; notice that
{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}={\mathcal{D}}^{(2)}\cap(\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C})
, so we will make use of the transversality theorem.

Suppose first that
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
have no boundary, so that by the usual transversality theorem it is enough to check
{\mathcal{D}}^{(2)}
and
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
are transversal in
\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}
, i.e.

T_{(g,h)}{\mathcal{D}}^{(2)}+T_{g}\mathcal{C}\oplus T_{h}\mathcal{C}=T_{g}\mathcal{D}\oplus T_{h}\mathcal{D},\quad\text{for all }(g,h)\in{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}.

To show this equality, let
(v,w)\in T_{g}\mathcal{D}\oplus T_{h}\mathcal{D}
. Since
s|_{\mathcal{C}}
is a submersion, there is a vector
v^{\prime}\in T_{g}\mathcal{C}
with
\operatorname{d}\!{s}_{g}(v^{\prime})=\operatorname{d}\!{t}_{h}(w)
. Define
v^{\prime\prime}=v-v^{\prime}
, and now since
t|_{\mathcal{C}}
is a submersion, there is a vector
w^{\prime}\in T_{h}\mathcal{C}
such that

\operatorname{d}\!{t}_{h}(w^{\prime})=\operatorname{d}\!{t}_{h}(w)-\operatorname{d}\!{s}_{g}(v^{\prime\prime}).

Now define
w^{\prime\prime}=w-w^{\prime}
. Clearly,
(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})\in T_{g}\mathcal{C}\oplus T_{h}\mathcal{C}
, and on the other hand, the definition of
w^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime\prime}
imply

\operatorname{d}\!{s}_{g}(v^{\prime\prime})=\operatorname{d}\!{t}_{h}(w)-\operatorname{d}\!{t}_{h}(w^{\prime})=\operatorname{d}\!{t}_{h}(w^{\prime\prime}),

so that
(v^{\prime\prime},w^{\prime\prime})\in T_{(g,h)}{\mathcal{D}}^{(2)}
, which concludes our proof for the boundaryless case. If
\mathcal{D}
has a boundary, then the above proof together with Proposition
A.8
used on the inclusion
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\xhookrightarrow{\iota}\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}
, ensures that
\iota\pitchfork{\mathcal{D}}^{(2)}
, and since
{\mathcal{D}}^{(2)}\subset\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}
is a neat submanifold,
{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}\subset\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}
is a submanifold by Proposition
A.4
, and hence since
{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}\subset{\mathcal{D}}^{(2)}
, it is a submanifold of

Remark 2.16

In particular, an embedded submonoid of a Lie monoid is its embedded Lie submonoid, however, a direct proof of this is much easier.

3.
Reversibility of morphisms

Reversible
(synonymously,
invertible
) morphisms form an important subclass of morphisms in any category
\mathcal{C}
; recall that
g\in\mathcal{C}
is said to be
invertible
, if there is a unique morphism
g^{-1}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
g^{-1}g=1_{s(g)}
and
gg^{-1}=1_{t(g)}
. We observe that the set

\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})=\{g\in\mathcal{C}\;|\;g\text{ is invertible}\}

is a groupoid over the same base as
\mathcal{C}
. We call
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
the
core
of
\mathcal{C}
, the study of which begins with the following simple observation.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any morphism
g
in a category
\mathcal{C}
, the following are equivalent.

(i)

g
is invertible.

(ii)

Left and right translations by
g
are bijections.

(iii)

Left and right translations by
g
are surjections.

(iv)

g
has a left and a right inverse, i.e. there exist
g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{s(g)},g^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{t(g)}
with
gg^{\prime}=1_{t(g)}
and
g^{\prime\prime}g=1_{s(g)}
.

Proof.

Implications
(i)\Rightarrow(ii)\Rightarrow(iii)
are clear, and
(iii)\Rightarrow(iv)
follows by observing that
(iv)
means
1_{t(g)}\in\operatorname{Im}(L_{g})
,
1_{s(g)}\in\operatorname{Im}(R_{g})
. Lastly, implication
(iv)\Rightarrow(i)
follows from elementary abstract algebra:
g^{\prime\prime}=g^{\prime\prime}1_{t(g)}=g^{\prime\prime}gg^{\prime}=1_{s(g)}g^{\prime}=g^{\prime}
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Note that injectivity of left and right translations corresponds to cancellative properties. For example, injectivity of
L_{g}
is equivalent to stating that for any two
h,k\in\mathcal{C}^{s(g)}
,
gh=gk
implies
h=k
.

The regular boundary assumption on a Lie category
\mathcal{C}
has the important consequence that the units dictate where invertible elements can be:

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a Lie category. For any invertible morphism
g\in\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
, the morphisms
g,g^{-1},1_{s(g)},1_{t(g)}
must either all be contained in the interior
\operatorname{Int}\mathcal{C}
, or in the boundary
\partial\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{C}
has no boundary then the lemma holds trivially, so suppose
\partial\mathcal{C}\neq\emptyset
. Invertibility of
g
means
L_{g}\colon\mathcal{C}^{s(g)}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{t(g)}
is a diffeomorphism, which maps
1_{s(g)}\mapsto g
, so we must have either
1_{s(g)}\in\partial(\mathcal{C}^{s(g)})
and
g\in\partial(\mathcal{C}^{t(g)})
, or
1_{s(g)}\in\operatorname{Int}(\mathcal{C}^{s(g)})
and
g\in\operatorname{Int}(\mathcal{C}^{t(g)})
. Regularity of boundary implies
\partial(\mathcal{C}^{x})=\mathcal{C}^{x}\cap\partial\mathcal{C}
for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, so we must have either
1_{s(g)}\in\partial\mathcal{C}
and
g\in\partial\mathcal{C}
, or
1_{s(g)}\in\operatorname{Int}\mathcal{C}
and
g\in\operatorname{Int}\mathcal{C}
. A similar result is obtained for
1_{t(g)}
and
g
using the right translation
R_{g}
, and similarly for
g^{-1}
and the units using
L_{g^{-1}},R_{g^{-1}}
since
s(g^{-1})=t(g)
.
∎

The last lemma implies that any Lie groupoid (a Lie category with all morphisms invertible) must have an empty boundary. We also obtain the following two immediate corollaries.

Corollary 3.4
.

For any Lie category
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
, there holds:

\displaystyle u(\mathcal{X})\subset\operatorname{Int}\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\text{ implies }\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})\subset\operatorname{Int}\mathcal{C},

(3.5)

\displaystyle u(\mathcal{X})\subset\partial\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\text{ implies }\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})\subset\partial\mathcal{C}.

(3.6)

Corollary 3.7
.

The invertible elements of any Lie monoid are either contained in its interior, or in its boundary.

An impending question is whether the core
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
of
\mathcal{C}
is a Lie groupoid. Without additional assumptions this is false:

Example 3.8
.

A simple example of a Lie category
\mathcal{C}
with a regular boundary, whose core
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
is not a manifold, is the disjoint union of the order category on
\mathbb{R}
and the pair groupoid on
\mathbb{R}
. More concretely, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=(-\infty,0)^{2}\cup\{(y,x)\in(0,\infty)^{2}\;|\;x\leq y\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},

with the categorical structure induced by the pair groupoid structure on
\mathbb{R}
.

In this case,
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})=(-\infty,0)^{2}\cup\{(x,x)\;|\;x>0\}
, which has two components of different dimensions. We have depicted this above, with invertible elements of
\mathcal{C}
in red, and non-invertible in blue; the units in
\operatorname{Int}\mathcal{C}
are depicted with a dashed line.

The culprit in the example above is that the units were allowed to be both in the interior and in the boundary.

Definition 3.9
.

A Lie category
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
is said to have a
normal boundary
, if either of the following holds:

(i)

u(\mathcal{X})\subset\operatorname{Int}\mathcal{C}
.

(ii)

\partial\mathcal{C}
is a wide subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
, i.e.
\partial\mathcal{C}
is a subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
and
u(\mathcal{X})\subset\partial\mathcal{C}
.

Remark 3.10
.

A Lie category without a boundary trivially has a normal boundary.

Finally, for the case

Given a morphism
F\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
between Lie categories with normal boundaries, we have

by functoriality, so we may define
\mathcal{G}(F)=F|_{\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})}
. The map
\mathcal{G}\colon\mathbf{LieCat_{\partial}}\rightarrow\mathbf{LieGrpd}
defines a functor from the category of Lie categories with normal boundary to the category of Lie groupoids without boundary, which is easily seen to be right adjoint to the inclusion functor
\mathbf{LieGrpd}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{LieCat_{\partial}}
.

Using the last theorem we can also show that the universal property of the core extends to the differentiable setting. We note that more consequences of the Theorem
3.11
will be explored in upcoming sections.

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a Lie category with a normal boundary, and let
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{C}
be a groupoid that is also a Lie subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
. If any smooth morphism
F\colon\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
, defined on a Lie groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, factors uniquely through
\mathcal{H}
, then
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
.

Proof.

The inclusion
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
is an easy consequence of functoriality of
\mathcal{H}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}
, since functors map isomorphisms to isomorphisms. For the converse inclusion, we pick the smooth map
F\colon\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}
and now the existence of
\bar{F}\colon\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
such that
F=\iota\circ\bar{F}
, ensures that if
g\in\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
, then
g=\bar{F}(g)\in\mathcal{H}
.
∎

4.
Lie algebroids of a Lie category

The construction of a Lie algebroid using left-invariant (or right-invariant) vector fields on a Lie groupoid readily generalizes to Lie categories; however, we no longer have a canonical isomorphism between the two algebroids, previously given by inversion map. Below, we present the main idea of the construction (
4.1
through
4.4
), mainly to fix notation; we omit some details which can be found in standard Lie groupoid references.

Definition 4.1
.

A
left-invariant
vector field on a Lie category
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
is a vector field
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{C})
, which is tangent to
t
-fibres, i.e.
X\in\Gamma^{\infty}(\ker\operatorname{d}\!{t})
, and left-invariant, i.e.
\operatorname{d}\!{(}L_{g})_{h}(X_{h})=X_{gh}
for all
(g,h)\in{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}
. Denote by
\mathfrak{X}^{L}(\mathcal{C})
the vector space of left-invariant vector fields on
\mathcal{C}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a Lie category. The vector space
\mathfrak{X}^{L}(\mathcal{C})
is closed under the Lie bracket, and canonically isomorphic to the vector space
\Gamma^{\infty}(A^{L}(\mathcal{C}))
of sections of the vector bundle
A^{L}(\mathcal{C})=u^{*}(\ker\operatorname{d}\!{t})
over
\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

Closedness under the Lie bracket is a consequence of the fact that if
X,Y
are
L_{g}
-related to themselves when restricted to appropriate fibres, so is
[X,Y]
. The canonical isomorphism
\mathrm{ev}\colon\mathfrak{X}^{L}(\mathcal{C})\rightarrow\Gamma^{\infty}(A^{L}(\mathcal{C}))
is given by restriction to the units, and its inverse
\mathrm{ev}^{-1}
maps any section
\alpha\in\Gamma^{\infty}(A^{L}(\mathcal{C}))
to its left-invariant extension
\alpha^{L}
, given as

\alpha^{L}(g)=\operatorname{d}\!{(}L_{g})_{1_{s(g)}}(\alpha_{s(g)}),

which is a smooth section
\mathcal{C}\rightarrow T\mathcal{C}
since it can be realized as the composition
\alpha^{L}=\operatorname{d}\!{m}\circ\tau^{\alpha}
, where
\tau^{\alpha}\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow T({\mathcal{C}}^{(2)})
is given by
g\mapsto(0_{g},\alpha_{1_{s(g)}}).

∎

Definition 4.3
.

The
left Lie algebroid
of a Lie category
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
is the vector bundle
A^{L}(\mathcal{C})\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
, endowed with the Lie bracket
[\cdot,\cdot]
on its sections as induced by the isomorphism
\mathrm{ev}
, together with the
anchor map

\rho^{L}\colon A^{L}(\mathcal{C})\rightarrow T\mathcal{X}
,
\rho^{L}=\operatorname{d}\!{s}|_{A^{L}(\mathcal{C})}
.

Similarly, we define the
right Lie algebroid
of a Lie category
\mathcal{C}
as the vector bundle
A^{R}(\mathcal{C})=u^{*}(\ker\operatorname{d}\!{s})
, which as a vector space is isomorphic to the space
\mathfrak{X}^{R}(\mathcal{C})
of right-invariant vector fields, and its anchor map is defined as
\rho^{R}=\operatorname{d}\!{t}|_{A^{R}(\mathcal{C})}
.

The proof of the next proposition is again the same as with the Lie groupoid case, so we omit it. Again, an analogous result holds for the right Lie algebroid.

Proposition 4.4
.

The left Lie algebroid
A^{L}(\mathcal{C})
of a Lie category
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
satisfies:

(i)

\rho^{L}
preserves the brackets, i.e.
\rho^{L}[\alpha,\beta]=[\rho^{L}\alpha,\rho^{L}\beta]_{T\mathcal{X}}
,

(ii)

Leibniz rule:
[\alpha,f\beta]=f[\alpha,\beta]+\rho^{L}(\alpha)(f)\beta
,

for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma^{\infty}(A^{L}(\mathcal{C}))
and
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{X}).

Any morphism
F\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
over
\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{X}}
of Lie categories over the same object manifold
\mathcal{X}
, induces morphisms between their left and right Lie algebroids (respectively), denoted by

\displaystyle F_{*}^{L}\colon A^{L}(\mathcal{C})\rightarrow A^{L}(\mathcal{D})\quad\text{and}\quad F_{*}^{R}\colon A^{R}(\mathcal{C})\rightarrow A^{R}(\mathcal{D}),

and defined on respective sections of
A^{L}(\mathcal{C})
and
A^{R}(\mathcal{C})
in the obvious way:

\displaystyle\alpha\mapsto\operatorname{d}\!{F}\circ\alpha.

In the case when the object manifolds of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
are not equal and the morphism
F
does not restrict to a diffeomorphism between the units, we encounter the same complications as in the Lie groupoid case; this is resolved in the same manner as for Lie groupoids, and since we will not be needing this more general result, we point the reader to
[
9
, Chapter 4.3]
for details. The upshot is that
A^{L}
and
A^{R}
are functors from the category
\mathbf{LieCat}
of Lie categories to the category
\mathbf{LieAlgd}
of Lie algebroids.

As mentioned, we do not have a canonical isomorphism between the left and right Lie algebroid of a Lie category, which is given in a Lie groupoid by the inversion map. However, we have the following result, and we will later encounter a related one when studying extensions of categories to groupoids (see section
6
).

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
be a Lie category. If the units of
\mathcal{C}
are contained in the interior of
\mathcal{C}
, i.e.
u(\mathcal{X})\subset\operatorname{Int}\mathcal{C}
, then the left and right Lie algebroids of
\mathcal{C}
are isomorphic to the Lie algebroid of its core
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.11
,
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
is open in
\mathcal{C}
, so we get the following chain of isomorphisms of Lie algebroids:

\displaystyle A^{L}(\mathcal{C})\cong A^{L}(\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C}))\cong A^{R}(\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C}))\cong A^{R}(\mathcal{C}),

(4.6)

where the first and last isomorphism are induced by the inclusion
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}
, and the isomorphism in the middle is induced by inversion in the groupoid
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

Note that if
\mathcal{C}
has a normal boundary and
u(\mathcal{X})\subset\partial\mathcal{C}
, the Lie algebroid
A(\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C}))
of the core will always fail to be isomorphic to the two Lie algebroids of
\mathcal{C}
, since the rank of the vector bundle
A(\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C}))
is one less than the rank of
A^{L}(\mathcal{C})
and
A^{R}(\mathcal{C})
. This is demonstrated by the following two examples:

(i)

The two Lie algebras of the Lie monoid
M=\mathbb{H}^{n}
are isomorphic (as vector spaces) to
A_{L}(M)\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}\cong A_{R}(M)
, whereas
A(G(M))\cong\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
since the core of
M
is
G(M)=\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.

(ii)

Consider the order category
\mathcal{C}=\{(y,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\;|\;x\leq y\}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}
from Example
2.13
. Notice that its core
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
is just the base groupoid over
\mathbb{R}
, hence its Lie algebroid is the zero bundle
A(\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C}))=\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}
. On the other hand, the left and right Lie algebroid of
\mathcal{C}
are both isomorphic to
T\mathbb{R}
.

5.
Ranks of morphisms

What follows can be seen as a natural generalization of the usual notion of rank from linear algebra (see Example
5.2
(iii)).

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
be a Lie category and let
g\in\mathcal{C}
. The
left rank
and
right rank
of
g
are defined as

\displaystyle\operatorname{rank}^{L}(g)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{d}\!{(}L_{g})_{1_{s(g)}},

\displaystyle\operatorname{rank}^{R}(g)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{d}\!{(}R_{g})_{1_{t(g)}}.

If the left and right rank of a morphism
g
are equal, we just write
\operatorname{rank}(g)=\operatorname{rank}^{L}(g)=\operatorname{rank}^{R}(g)
and call this integer the
rank
of
g
.
Moreover, we say
g
has
full rank
, if its left and right ranks are full, that is, if

\operatorname{rank}(g)=\operatorname{codim}_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{X})=\dim\mathcal{C}-\dim\mathcal{X}=:\delta.

In this case, we will sometimes say that
g
is a
regular
morphism. If
g
is not regular, we will call it
singular
.

Finally, we say that
g
has
constant
left rank, if
\operatorname{rank}^{L}(g)=\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{d}\!{(}L_{g})_{h}
for all
h\in\mathcal{C}^{s(g)}
, and similarly that
g
has
constant
right rank, if
\operatorname{rank}^{R}(g)=\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{d}\!{(}R_{g})_{h}
for all
h\in\mathcal{C}_{t(g)}
. To avoid ambiguity, we will sometimes write
\operatorname{rank}_{\mathcal{C}}
instead of
\mathrm{rank}.

Example 5.2
.

(i)

All invertible morphisms in any Lie category have full and constant rank.

(ii)

In a Lie monoid
M
, the ranks of an element
g\in M
are just the ranks of
\operatorname{d}\!{(}L_{g})_{e}
and
\operatorname{d}\!{(}R_{g})_{e}
. If
M
is an abelian Lie monoid, then clearly any element of
M
has equal left and right rank. The example
M=\mathbb{H}^{n}
shows that regularity does not imply invertibility.

We will later see that regularity and constancy of ranks of all morphisms in a Lie category is ensured whenever dealing with a Lie subcategory of a Lie groupoid, as is with the simple example
\mathbb{H}^{n}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
for addition.

(iii)

If
M=\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, let us show how the above notion of rank relates with the usual one. If
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, then the usual notion reads
\operatorname{rank}A=\dim\operatorname{Im}A
, when
A
is seen as the map
\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
. On the other hand, the left rank in Definition
5.1
equals:

\operatorname{rank}^{L}_{M}(A)=\dim\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{d}\!{(}L_{A})_{I}=\dim\operatorname{Im}L_{A},

where
L_{A}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the left translation by
A
, and the last equality follows by linearity. Similarly,
\operatorname{rank}^{R}_{M}(A)=\dim\operatorname{Im}R_{A}
. Denoting by
E_{ij}
the matrix with 1 in place
(i,j)
and zero elsewhere, we have that
AE_{ij}
has
i
-th column of
A
in
j
-th column and is zero elsewhere, and
E_{ij}A
has
j
-th row of
A
in
i
-th row and is zero elsewhere, so:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}(L_{A})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Lin}(AE_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}=\Big\{\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\dots v_{n}\end{bmatrix}\;\Big|\;v_{i}\in\operatorname{Im}(A)\Big\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}(R_{A})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Lin}(E_{ij}A)_{i,j=1}^{n}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\dots v_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\;\middle|\;v_{i}\in\operatorname{Im}\left(A^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\right\}.

Since
\operatorname{rank}A=\operatorname{rank}A^{\mathsf{T}}
, it follows that

\operatorname{rank}^{L}_{M}(A)=\operatorname{rank}^{R}_{M}(A)=n\operatorname{rank}(A),

and we see that
A
is regular if, and only if,
A
is invertible.

The above result readily generalizes to arbitrary finite-dimensional vector spaces: if
V
is a vector space, then

\operatorname{rank}_{\operatorname{End}(V)}(A)=\dim(V)\operatorname{rank}(A).

Moreover, for any vector bundle
E
, the above result clearly also generalizes to the endomorphism bundle
E^{*}\otimes E
, so that for any
A\colon E_{x}\rightarrow E_{x}
,

\operatorname{rank}_{E^{*}\otimes E}A=\operatorname{rank}(E)\operatorname{rank}(A).

(iv)

Examples of Lie monoids that do not have coinciding left and right ranks may be found in the context of finite-dimensional unital algebras. As a concrete example, take the algebra
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
of upper-diagonal real
2\times 2
matrices, the canonical basis of which is given by
a=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr],b=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr],c=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
. Identifying
T_{e}A
with
A
and noting that left and right translations are linear maps, we can identify
\operatorname{d}\!{(}L_{g})_{e}=L_{g}
for any
g\in A
, and similarly for the right translations. It is easy to see
\operatorname{Im}(R_{a})=\operatorname{Lin}(a)
and
\operatorname{Im}(L_{a})=\operatorname{Lin}(a,c)
by computing all the products of
a
with the canonical basis, so we conclude that
2=\operatorname{rank}^{L}_{A}(a)\neq\operatorname{rank}^{R}_{A}(a)=1
.

Proposition 5.3

(Properties of ranks)
.

In a Lie category
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
, there holds:

(i)

The ranks of a composition of composable morphisms
g,h\in\mathcal{C}
are bounded from above:

\displaystyle\operatorname{rank}^{L}(gh)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{rank}^{L}(h),

(5.4)

\displaystyle\operatorname{rank}^{R}(gh)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{rank}^{R}(g).

(5.5)

(ii)

The ranks of
g\in\mathcal{C}
are bounded from below by the ranks of anchors
\rho^{L}\colon A^{L}(\mathcal{C})\rightarrow T\mathcal{X}
and
\rho^{R}\colon A^{R}(\mathcal{C})\rightarrow T\mathcal{X}
of Lie algebroids of
\mathcal{C}
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{rank}^{L}(g)\geq\operatorname{rank}\rho_{s(g)}^{L},

(5.6)

\displaystyle\operatorname{rank}^{R}(g)\geq\operatorname{rank}\rho_{t(g)}^{R}.

(5.7)

Proof.

Since
L_{gh}=L_{g}\circ L_{h}
, we have

\operatorname{rank}^{L}(gh)=\operatorname{rank}(\operatorname{d}\!{(}L_{g})_{h}\circ\operatorname{d}\!{(}L_{h})_{1_{s(h)}})\leq\operatorname{rank}^{L}(h),

and similarly for the right translation by using
R_{gh}=R_{h}\circ R_{g}
, so
(i)
follows. For the property
(ii)
note that the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}^{s(g)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}^{t(g)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L_{g}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s|_{\mathcal{C}^{s(g)}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s|_{\mathcal{C}^{t(g)}}}

implies
\operatorname{rank}^{L}(g)\geq\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{d}\!{(}s|_{\mathcal{C}^{s(g)}})_{1_{s(g)}}=\operatorname{rank}\rho^{L}_{s(g)}
.
∎

In the context of Lie groupoids, it is well known that the composition map is a submersion. This is no longer true for Lie categories in general; a counterexample is provided by the Lie monoid
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
for matrix multiplication, where
\operatorname{d}\!{m}_{(0,0)}
is easily seen to be the zero map. However, the following results ensure that
m
is a submersion in case all morphisms have full and constant rank; as mentioned, this is the case for Lie categories extendable to Lie groupoids, as we will see later in Lemma
6.3
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
be a Lie category and
g\in\mathcal{C}
. The map
L_{g}
has full rank at

if, and only if, the map

\vartheta\colon{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}\rightarrow{\mathcal{C}\mathchoice{\hphantom{{}^{{\mathchoice{\makebox[3.02084pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.02084pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[1.76944pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.26389pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{{t}}}}\times^{{\kern-13.84431pt\mathchoice{\makebox[3.02084pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.02084pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[1.76944pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.26389pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\kern 8.071pt\mathchoice{\makebox[3.02084pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.02084pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[1.76944pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.26389pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\kern-9.95155pt{t}\kern has full rank at
(g,h)
. In particular,
g
has full left rank if, and only if,
\vartheta
has full rank at
(g,1_{s(g)})
.

Recall that we have already encountered the map
\vartheta
in the proof of Theorem
3.11
. In a Lie groupoid, this map is a bijection with inverse
(g,h)\mapsto(g,g^{-1}h)
, so above lemma guarantees it is a diffeomorphism, which can be used e.g. to show that the inversion map is automatically smooth, by realizing it as the composition

g\mapsto(g,1_{t(g)})\xmapsto{\vartheta^{-1}}(g,g^{-1})\mapsto g^{-1}.

An analogous result to Lemma

Proposition 5.10
.

In any Lie category
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
, the subset
\mathcal{R}_{\delta}(\mathcal{C})\subset\mathcal{C}
of regular morphisms is open.

Proof.

Let us first show that differentials of left translations define a certain morphism of vector bundles. We consider the following vector bundle over
\mathcal{C}
:

E^{L}=\coprod_{g\in\mathcal{C}}(\ker\operatorname{d}\!{t}_{1_{s(g)}})^{*}\otimes\ker\operatorname{d}\!{t}_{g}=(s^{*}u^{*}\ker\operatorname{d}\!{t})^{*}\otimes\ker\operatorname{d}\!{t}.

Denote the projection map by
p_{L}\colon E^{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
, so the fibre
p_{L}^{-1}(g)=E^{L}_{g}
of
E^{L}
consists of all linear maps
\ker\operatorname{d}\!{t}_{1_{s(g)}}\rightarrow\ker\operatorname{d}\!{t}_{g}
. Note that the map
\phi_{L}\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow E^{L}
, given as

is a section of

, which is smooth since

\operatorname{d}\!{(}L_{g})_{1_{s(g)}}=\operatorname{d}\!{m}_{(g,1_{s(g)})}(0_{g},-).

In other words,

defines a morphism

of vector bundles.

Now take an atlas of local trivializations

of

. The set
U_{i}\cap\mathcal{R}^{L}_{\delta}(\mathcal{\mathcal{C}})=\phi_{L}^{-1}\circ\psi_{i}^{-1}(U_{i}\times\mathrm{GL}(\delta,\mathbb{R}))

Remark 5.11
.

The smooth map

(respectively,

Corollary 5.12
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a Lie category. If the regular morphisms have constant rank, then

We only have to check that the composition of two regular morphisms is a regular morphism. To that end, just notice that

6.
Extensions of Lie categories to Lie groupoids

We have witnessed interesting examples of Lie categories appear by restricting to an embedded subcategory of a Lie groupoid, in spirit of Lemma
2.15
. As we will see next, in order to ascertain the main properties of such Lie categories, the assumption of being embedded may be weakened to being immersed.

Definition 6.1
.

An
extension to a Lie groupoid
of a Lie category
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
is a Lie groupoid
\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
, together with an injective, immersive functor
F\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
over the identity. In other words,
\mathcal{G}
is a Lie groupoid such that
\mathcal{C}
is its wide Lie subcategory. If such an extension exists, we say that
\mathcal{C}
is
extendable to a Lie groupoid
. Furthermore, we say that an extension to a Lie groupoid is
weakly étale
, if
\dim\mathcal{G}=\dim\mathcal{C}
.

Remark 6.2
.

Note that if
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{G}
do not have a boundary, then an extension is weakly étale if, and only if, the map
F
is étale (i.e. a local diffeomorphism), by virtue of inverse map theorem. If
\dim\mathcal{G}=\dim\mathcal{C}
, but
F(\partial\mathcal{C})\not\subset\partial\mathcal{G}
, then
F
is not a local diffeomorphism (see e.g. the order category from Example
2.13
).

An obvious necessary condition for an arbitrary category
\mathcal{C}
to admit an injective functor
F
into a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, is
cancellativity
of all elements in
\mathcal{C}
, i.e. all left and right translations in
\mathcal{C}
must be injective. For example, if
gh=gk
holds for some morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
, then
F(g)F(h)=F(g)F(k)
holds in
F(\mathcal{C})\subset\mathcal{G}
, implying
F(h)=F(k)
, and hence
h=k
by injectivity of
F
, so
L_{g}
is injective.

In the differentiable setting, extensions to groupoids reflect on ranks and algebroids; the following lemma yields necessary conditions on a Lie category
\mathcal{C}
to be extendable to a Lie groupoid.

Lemma 6.3
.

If a Lie category
\mathcal{C}
is extendable to a Lie groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, all its morphisms have full and constant rank, and all left and right translations are injective. Moreover, if the extension is weakly étale, then
A^{L}(\mathcal{C})\cong A(\mathcal{G})\cong A^{R}(\mathcal{C})
.

Proof.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
be the groupoid extension, and let
g,h\in\mathcal{C}
be composable. To prove that
g
has full and constant rank, denote the left translation in
\mathcal{C}
by
g
as
L_{g}^{\mathcal{C}}
, and the left translation in
\mathcal{G}
by

L^{\mathcal{G}}_{F(g)}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{C}^{t(g)}}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{G}^{t(g)}}}

Taking

Remark 6.7
.

The importance of Lemma

is in the fact that it enables us to easily provide positive answers to the following questions, for the case of Lie categories extendable to groupoids:

(i)

Are Lie monoids parallelizable?

(ii)

For fixed objects

of a Lie category

We remark that these questions remain open for Lie categories which do not admit an extension to a Lie groupoid.

Corollary 6.8
.

If a Lie monoid

is extendable to a Lie group, it is paralellizable.

Proof.

Extending a basis of

to a tuple of left-invariant vector fields yields a global frame for

since all elements of

have full rank by Lemma

.
∎

The proof of the positive answer to (ii) is similar as in the Lie groupoid case:

Corollary 6.9
.

If a Lie category
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
is extendable to a Lie groupoid, then for any

the set

is a closed embedded submanifold of

. In particular,

is a Lie monoid for any

Proof.

We realize

as an integral manifold of a certain distribution on

, namely

and

Since

of

∎

7.
Completeness of invariant vector fields

It is well-known that on any Lie group
G
, left-invariant vector fields are complete. In this section, we generalize this result to Lie monoids with normal boundaries. Furthermore, we generalize the characterization of completeness of left-invariant vector fields on Lie groupoids to Lie categories with normal boundaries. At last, we will discuss the exponential map for Lie monoids.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
X
be a vector field on a smooth manifold
M
with or without boundary, and denote by
J^{X}_{g}
the maximal interval on which the integral path
\gamma^{X}_{g}
of
X
, starting at
g\in M
, is defined. We say that
X
is
half-complete
if for any
g\in M
there either holds
[0,\infty)\subset J^{X}_{g}
or
(-\infty,0]\subset J^{X}_{g}
.

Remark 7.2
.

In what follows, we will assume the reader is familiar with the notion of inward-pointing and outward-pointing tangent vectors on the boundary; we direct to
[
8
, p. 118]
for a basic reference. A particularly useful observation is that a vector field
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
which is inward-pointing (or outward-pointing) at a certain point
g\in\partial M
, must remain inward-pointing (or outward pointing) on a neighborhood of
g
in
\partial M
, from which it follows that
J_{g}^{X}
cannot contain an open neighborhood of zero, but may only contain a half-closed interval
[0,\varepsilon)
, for some
\varepsilon>0
(or
(-\varepsilon,0]
in the outward-pointing case).

Recall from Definition
3.9
that a Lie monoid
M
has a normal boundary, if either
e\in\operatorname{Int}M
, or we have both that
e\in\partial M
and
\partial M
is a submonoid of
M
.

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
M
be a Lie monoid with a normal boundary and let
X
be a left-invariant vector field on
M
. The following holds:

(i)

Suppose either that
e\in\operatorname{Int}M
, or that
e\in\partial M
and
X_{e}
is tangent to
\partial M
. Then
X|_{\partial M}
is tangent to
\partial M
, and
X
is complete.

(ii)

Suppose
e\in\partial M
and
X_{e}
is either inward-pointing or outward-pointing. Then either
J_{e}^{X}=[0,\infty)
or
J_{e}^{X}=(-\infty,0]
, respectively, and
X
is half-complete.

Moreover, the flow of
X
is given for all

by
\phi_{t}^{X}=R_{\phi_{t}^{X}(e)}
.

Proof.

We first inspect the assumptions from (i): notice that if
e\in\operatorname{Int}M
, then
J_{e}^{X}
clearly contains an open interval around zero; on the other hand, if
e\in\partial M
and
X_{e}
is tangent to
\partial M
, then
X|_{\partial M}
must be everywhere tangent to
\partial M
by left-invariance of
X
and the fact that
\partial M
is a Lie submonoid of
M
, so
J_{e}^{X}
must again contain an open interval around zero. Furthermore, the assumption from (ii) that
X_{e}
is either inward-pointing or outward-pointing implies that
J_{e}^{X}
contains a half-open interval of the form
[0,\varepsilon)
or
(-\varepsilon,0]
, respectively.

We next observe that for any

, the composition
L_{g}\circ\gamma_{e}^{X}
is an integral path of
X
starting at
g
, so maximality of

implies

\displaystyle J_{e}^{X}\subset J_{g}^{X},

(7.4)

and also
\phi_{t}^{X}(g)=g\gamma_{e}^{X}(t)=R_{\gamma_{e}^{X}(t)}(g)
for all

. Notice that (

contains an open interval around zero for all

, and in particular this holds for any

must be tangent to

, by virtue of Remark

Let

and consider the affinely translated path

\zeta^{\tau}\colon(J_{e}^{X}-\tau)\rightarrow M,\quad\zeta^{\tau}(t)=\gamma_{e}^{X}(t+\tau).

Since the maximal domain of an affinely translated integral path is just the affinely translated maximal domain, we get
J_{e}^{X}-\tau=J_{\zeta^{\tau}(0)}^{X}
.

6

We do not need the Lie monoid structure to prove this simple fact.

Together with (
7.4
), this implies

J_{e}^{X}+\tau\subset J_{e}^{X},\ \text{for all }\tau\in J_{e}^{X}.

This implies that if

contains an open interval around zero, it must equal

, and if

for some

Corollary 7.5
.

If

is a Lie monoid and

do not have full rank. Hence if also

Proof.

For a proof by contradiction, assume that there is a

8.
An application to physics: Statistical Thermodynamics

The interpretation that morphisms correspond to physical processes, and objects to physical states, can be applied to yield a rigorous approach to statistical physics, which we will now demonstrate. We will first focus purely on categorical aspects, and then consider differentiability.

Suppose we are given an isolated physical system consisting of an unknown number of particles, each of which can be in one of the
n+1
a-priori given
microstates
, which we will index by

i\in\{0,\dots,n\}.

Since the number of particles in our system is unknown and often large, we need to work with tuples of probabilities
(p_{0},\dots,p_{n})
, where each
p_{i}
is the probability that a particle, chosen at random, is in the
i
-th microstate. Any tuple of probabilities
(p_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
is subjected to the constraint

\textstyle\sum_{i}p_{i}=1,

and we will refer to any such tuple
p=(p_{i})_{i}
as a
configuration
of the system, which is just a probability distribution on the finite set of microstates above. The set of all configurations of our system is thus the standard
n
-simplex,

\Delta^{n}=\big\{(p_{0},\dots,p_{n})\in[0,1]^{n+1}\;\big|\;\textstyle\sum_{i}p_{i}=1\big\}.

We associate to any configuration
(p_{i})_{i}
of our system its expected surprise,

S(p_{i})_{i}=-\sum_{i}p_{i}\log p_{i},

which is called the
entropy
of the configuration
(p_{i})_{i}
. Letting
f(x)=x\log x
, we find
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bm{+}$}}}f(x)=0
, so
f
may be extended to
[0,\infty)
by defining
f(0)=0
, implying that
S\colon\Delta^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined on whole
\Delta^{n}
and continuous.

The construction of the space of morphisms between different configurations of our system is now an application of the
second law of thermodynamics
:

A process in an isolated physical system is feasible if, and only if, the change of entropy pertaining to the process is non-negative.

In accord with the second law, we define

\mathcal{D}=\left\{(p_{i})_{i}\rightarrow(q_{i})_{i}\;\middle|\;S(q_{i})_{i}-S(p_{i})_{i}\geq 0\right\}.

In other words,
\mathcal{D}
consists of pairs
(q,p)\in\Delta^{n}\times\Delta^{n}
of configurations, such that the entropy of the target configuration
q
is no less than that of the source
p
. That
\mathcal{D}\rightrightarrows\Delta^{n}
is a category follows from the fact that the map

\delta S\colon\Delta^{n}\times\Delta^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad\delta S(q,p)=S(q)-S(p),

is a functor
8
8

8

This is aligned with the moral that entropy should be inherently perceived as a categorical concept, which was first adopted by Baez et al.;
\delta S
is in fact the only map (up to a multiplicative scalar) which is functorial, convex-linear and continuous, see
[
1
]
for details.
from the pair groupoid of
\Delta^{n}
to the group
\mathbb{R}
for addition. Notice that the invertible morphisms in
\mathcal{D}
are precisely
\delta S^{-1}(0)
, which are just the processes with zero entropy change.

In the differentiable setting, we need to make certain adjustments to our category
\mathcal{D}\rightrightarrows\Delta^{n}
, since it is not a Lie category. First, we note that
\Delta^{n}
is a manifold with corners, and
S
is not smooth at its boundary
\partial\Delta^{n}
since
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bm{+}$}}}(x\log x)^{\prime}=-\infty
, which is why we first restrict our attention to the interior
\operatorname{Int}\Delta^{n}.
Secondly, we notice that the category
\mathcal{D}\rightrightarrows\Delta^{n}
has a terminal object, as shown by the following.

Claim
.

The only critical point of entropy
S|_{\operatorname{Int}\Delta^{n}}
is given by the so-called microcanonical configuration, i.e.
p_{i}^{\mu}=\frac{1}{n+1}
for all
i
. This is a maximum of
S
.

Proof.

Constraint
\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
implies
\sum_{i}\operatorname{d}\!{p}_{i}=0
and
\operatorname{d}\!{p}_{0}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{d}\!{p}_{i}
, so we have

\operatorname{d}\!{S}_{(p_{i})_{i}}=-\sum_{i}(1+\log p_{i})\operatorname{d}\!{p}_{i}=-\sum_{i}\log p_{i}\operatorname{d}\!{p}_{i}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\log p_{i}-\log p_{0})\operatorname{d}\!{p}_{i},

which vanishes if, and only if,
p_{i}=p_{0}
for all

p_{i}=\frac{1}{n+1}
. That this is a local maximum is left as an exercise, and it is not hard to see that the value of

at

is greater than the value of
S
on
S|_{\partial\Delta^{n}}

∎

The configuration

has little physical importance – for example, we will see below that it can be interpreted as the configuration that is attained at thermodynamical equilibrium at infinite temperature. We will thus remove it from the interior of our
n
-simplex of objects, and define our space of objects to be

\mathcal{X}=\operatorname{Int}\Delta^{n}-\{p^{\mu}\},

which is a smooth manifold without boundary. Moreover, we define the space of morphisms over

as the set

\mathcal{C}=(\delta S|_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}})^{-1}([0,\infty))=\left\{(q,p)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\;\middle|\;S(q)-S(p)\geq 0\right\}.

As before,
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
is a subcategory of the pair groupoid on

. By virtue of Example

\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
is a Lie category, we first need to check

has a regular value 0. By above claim, the only critical point of the map

is the identity morphism
(p^{\mu},p^{\mu})\in\mathcal{D}
, which is not in
\mathcal{C}
.

Secondly, we have to show that
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
has a regular boundary, i.e. that

are submersions. To this end, first note that for any

,

\displaystyle T_{(q,p)}\partial\mathcal{C}=\ker\operatorname{d}\!{(}\delta S)_{(q,p)}

\displaystyle=\left\{(v,w)\in T_{q}\mathcal{X}\oplus T_{p}\mathcal{X}\;\middle|\;\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\log q_{i}-\log q_{0})v_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\log p_{i}-\log p_{0})w_{i}\right\}.

Let

. Since
p_{i}\neq p_{0}
for some

, we define

and now we let

where
w_{0}=-w_{i}
is set to ensure

, thus we obtain the wanted pair
(v,w)\in T_{(q,p)}\partial\mathcal{C}
with

is a submersion. Furthermore,

satisfies all the conclusions from Lemma

, since the pair groupoid
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
is its weakly étale extension; in particular, the left and right Lie algebroids of
\mathcal{C}
are isomorphic to
T\mathcal{X}\approx\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The question interesting for physics is: what is the configuration
p^{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{X}
at which the system attains a thermodynamical equilibrium? It is well-known that the answer to this question is obtained using the so-called
Gibbs algorithm
, which we provide here for completeness. To this end, we need additional a-priori given data, namely each microstate
i
of our system has an a-priori assigned quantity
E_{i}
, called the
energy
of
i
-th microstate. To derive the wanted configuration
p^{\epsilon}
we utilize the so-called
principle of maximum entropy
:

A state is at thermodynamical equilibrium if, and only if, it maximizes the entropy with respect to the systemic constraints.

That is, we must find the constrained extremum of
S
with respect to constraints
\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
and
\sum_{i}p_{i}E_{i}=E(p_{i})_{i}
. Here
E\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a function on the object space, determined by the first law of thermodynamics up to an additive constant, as we will see below – this will impose thermodynamical considerations onto the statistical description of our system.

To apply the method of Lagrange multipliers, define the function
\hat{S}\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\hat{S}(p_{i})_{i}=S(p_{i})_{i}-\lambda_{1}\left(E(p)-\textstyle\sum_{i}p_{i}E_{i}\right)-\lambda_{2}(1-\textstyle\sum_{i}p_{i}).

Requiring
\frac{\partial\hat{S}}{\partial p_{i}}=0
yields

p_{i}=\frac{1}{Z}e^{\lambda_{1}E_{i}},

where we have defined
Z=e^{-(1+\lambda_{2})}
. The constraint

\textstyle\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
then reads

Z=e^{-(1+\lambda_{2})}=\textstyle\sum_{i}e^{\lambda_{1}E_{i}}.

On the other hand, the first law of thermodynamics for systems where no work is exerted reads

\operatorname{d}\!{E}=kT\operatorname{d}\!{S}

where
T
is the temperature (a macroscopic external constant) at which the system is held, and
k
denotes the Boltzmann constant. Writing out the total differentials
\operatorname{d}\!{E}
and
\operatorname{d}\!{S}
gives
\lambda_{1}=-\frac{1}{kT}
, and so we finally obtain the
equilibrium configuration
:

p_{i}^{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{Z}e^{-\frac{E_{i}}{kT}},\quad Z=\sum_{i}e^{-\frac{E_{i}}{kT}}.

We observe that in this categorical framework, the configurations from which it is possible to attain the equilibrium configuration
p^{\epsilon}
, are now simply expressible as

s(t^{-1}({p^{\epsilon}}))=\left\{p\in\mathcal{X}\;\middle|\;S(p^{\epsilon})\geq S(p)\right\}.

Further research

Although we hope to have succeeded in portraying the richness of Lie categories and their potential in physics, we admit that we have not exhausted all research options regarding them. We state some of them here, and note that they provide possibilities for future research.

(i)

As stated in Remark
6.7
, the question remains whether all Lie monoids are parallelizable, and whether
\mathcal{C}_{x}^{y}
is a smooth manifold for given objects
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
of a Lie category
\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{X}
.

(ii)

Does there exist a class of Lie algebroids that cannot be integrated to a Lie groupoid, but can be integrated to a Lie category?

(iii)

Remark
5.15
shows the need for considering Lie categories whose object manifold has a boundary, or more generally, corners. Another example of such a Lie category should be the flow of a smooth vector field on a manifold with boundary, generalizing the flow Lie groupoid of a vector field on a boundaryless manifold.

(iv)

Infinite-dimensional Lie categories. The exterior bundle
\Lambda(E)
of a vector bundle
E
is an example of a bundle of Lie monoids, and it is moreover a subcategory of the tensor bundle
\oplus_{k=0}^{\infty}\otimes^{k}E
, which is a bundle of monoids that fails to have finite-dimensional fibres. Moreover, in statistical mechanics, physicists often work with an infinite number of microstates, and in quantum mechanics with infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. These examples show the need for introducing infinite-dimensional Lie categories. Since the theory of infinite-dimensional (Banach) manifolds with corners is already well-developed in
[
10
]
, a theory of infinite-dimensional Lie categories with corners seems realizable.

(v)

Multiplicative differential forms on Lie categories. On Lie groupoids, such structures can be used to describe integrated counterparts of Poisson structures
[
3
]
, and more recently they have been used to provide a natural generalization of connections on principle bundles in
[
5
]
. We suspect that interesting geometric structures can be described with multiplicative differential forms on Lie categories.

(vi)

Haar systems on Lie categories. Just as Haar systems on Lie groupoids provide a generalization of a Haar measure on a Lie group (and connects the theory of Lie groupoids to noncommutative geometry), a further generalization to Lie categories should provide a means of equipping the space
C_{c}(\mathcal{C})
of compactly supported functions on the space of arrows of a given category with the convolution product. In this fashion, one expects to generalize the construction of a groupoid C*-algebra, but due to the lack of existence of inverses of arrows, there is a-priori no natural way of obtaining the involution, hence the construction potentially generalizes only to a
category Banach algebra
. We strongly suspect that a sensible notion of a Haar system on a Lie category will rather be defined in terms of a measure
\mu
on the space
{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}
of composible pairs of arrows, satisfying certain invariance and continuity conditions, instead of being defined in terms of left-invariant
t
-fibre supported measures on
\mathcal{C}
. Roughly speaking, the convolution on
C_{c}(\mathcal{C})
would then conceivably be defined by

(f_{1}*f_{2})(g)=\int_{m^{-1}(g)}f_{1}(g_{1})f_{2}(g_{2})\operatorname{d}\!{\mu}(g_{1},g_{2}),

where the integration is done over the set
m^{-1}(g)=\{(g_{1},g_{2})\in{\mathcal{C}}^{(2)}\;|\;g_{1}g_{2}=g\}
of composible arrows which compose to
g\in\mathcal{C}
.

An interesting question for mathematical physics might be whether the principle of least action in physics can be used within the theory of Lie categories, possibly using Haar systems, to describe existing or yield novel physical theories.

(vii)

Smooth sieves on Lie categories. In the context of Lie groupoids, the notion of a sieve is vacuous since all morphisms are invertible – any sieve on an object must equal the whole
t
-fibre over that object. However, a notion of a smooth sieve seems possible for Lie categories, and their properties should be researched, together with the properties of Grothendieck sites of such sieves.

(viii)

Dynamical systems. The theory of Lie groupoids has successfully been applied to describe dynamical systems, see e.g.
[
2
]
; a natural question is to what extent the theory of Lie categories presented in our paper can be used to generalize these results, to describe non-invertible dynamics.

(ix)

Generalization of Morita equivalence from the context of Lie groupoids to Lie categories.

p\in f^{-1}(X^{\prime\prime})
with
\operatorname{ind}_{X}(p)=k
, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{d}\!{(}f|_{S^{k}(X)})_{p}+T_{f(p)}X^{\prime\prime}=T_{f(p)}X^{\prime}.

(ii)

f|_{S^{k}(X)}\pitchfork_{p}X^{\prime\prime}
.

(iii)

f\pitchfork_{p}X^{\prime\prime}
.

Proof.

To show
(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
, take a chart
\varphi^{\prime}\colon U^{\prime}\rightarrow V^{\prime}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
on
X^{\prime}
at
f(p)
adapted to
X^{\prime\prime}
by means of
(V^{\prime\prime},{\Lambda^{\prime\prime}})
, take a complementary subspace
W^{\prime\prime}
to
V^{\prime\prime}
of
V^{\prime}
, and consider the map

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tau^{k}\colon U}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{U^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\varphi^{\prime}(U^{\prime})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(V^{\prime\prime}\oplus W^{\prime\prime})_{L^{*}\Lambda^{\prime}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{W^{\prime\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f|_{U}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\approx}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{-1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{pr}_{2}}

where
U
is the domain of a chart neighborhood
\varphi\colon U\rightarrow V
in
S^{k}(X)
of
p
; by continuity we may assume
f(U)\subset U^{\prime}
. Since
\partial S^{k}(X)
is boundaryless, it is enough to show that
\tau^{k}
is a submersion. To that end, take any
w\in T_{0}W^{\prime\prime}\cong W^{\prime\prime}
and then
u:=\operatorname{d}\!{(}\varphi^{\prime})_{p}^{-1}L(0,w)\in T_{f(p)}X^{\prime}
, so by assumption there exist
v\in T_{p}X
and
v^{\prime\prime}\in T_{f(p)}X^{\prime\prime}
such that
u=\operatorname{d}\!{f}_{p}(v)+v^{\prime\prime}
. Since
L^{-1}\operatorname{d}\!{(}\varphi^{\prime})_{p}(v^{\prime\prime})\in V^{\prime\prime}
, we get

\operatorname{d}\!{h}_{p}(v)=\mathrm{pr}_{2}L^{-1}\operatorname{d}\!{(}\varphi^{\prime})(u-v^{\prime\prime})=w.

Conversely,
(ii)\Rightarrow(i)
follows from the fact that topological transversality implies transversality.

For the implication
(ii)\Rightarrow(iii)
, note that if
Z\xrightarrow{g}Z^{\prime}\xrightarrow{h}Z^{\prime\prime}
are smooth maps such that
h\circ g
is a topological submersion at
p\in Z
, then it is easy to see
h
is a topological submersion at
g(p)
. Use this result for the composition
S^{k}(X)\hookrightarrow X\xrightarrow{\tau}W^{\prime\prime}
which equals
\tau^{k}
. The converse implication
(iii)\Rightarrow(ii)
is a consequence of the following lemma used on the map
\tau
.
∎

Lemma A.9
.

If
f\colon X\rightarrow X^{\prime}
is a smooth map between manifolds with corners, which is a topological submersion at
p
and there holds
f(p)\in\operatorname{Int}(X^{\prime})
, then
f|_{S^{k}(X)}
is a submersion at
p
, where
k=\operatorname{ind}_{X}(p)
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi\colon U\rightarrow V_{\Lambda}
be a chart on
X
at
p
and
\varphi^{\prime}\colon X^{\prime}\rightarrow V^{\prime}
on
X^{\prime}
at
f(p)
. Note that
\varphi(U\cap S^{k}(X))=\varphi(U)\cap V_{\Lambda}^{0}
.

Let
v^{\prime}\in T_{f(p)}X^{\prime}
be arbitrary. By assumption, there is a neighborhood
U^{\prime}
of
f(p)
and a smooth map
\sigma\colon U^{\prime}\rightarrow U\subset X
such that
\sigma(f(p))=p
and
f\circ\sigma=\mathrm{id}_{U^{\prime}}
. Since
f(p)\in\operatorname{Int}X^{\prime}
, there is a smooth path
\gamma\colon(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\rightarrow U^{\prime}
with
\dot{\gamma}(0)=v^{\prime}
, and now consider the path
\delta=\varphi\circ\sigma\circ\gamma\colon(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\rightarrow\varphi(U)\subset V_{\Lambda}
. Since
\delta(0)=0
and
\delta
is defined on an open interval, we must have that
\delta
maps into
V_{\Lambda}^{0}
, so
\dot{\delta}(0)\in T_{0}V_{\Lambda}^{0}\cong V_{\Lambda}^{0}
. Taking
v=\operatorname{d}\!{(}\varphi^{-1})_{0}(\dot{\delta}(0))
hence yields
v\in T_{p}(S^{k}(X))
with
\operatorname{d}\!{f}_{p}(v)=v^{\prime}
.
∎
