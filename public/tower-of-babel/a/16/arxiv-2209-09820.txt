Entanglement entropy of ground states of the three-dimensional ideal Fermi gas in a magnetic field
Paul Pfeiffer and Wolfgang Spitzer

Abstract
. We study the asymptotic growth of the entanglement entropy of ground states of non-interacting (spinless) fermions in ℝ 3 {\mathbb{R}}^{3} subject to a non-zero, constant magnetic field perpendicular to a plane. As for the case with no magnetic field we find, to leading order L 2 ​ ln ⁡ ( L ) L^{2}\ln(L) , a logarithmically enhanced area law of this entropy for a bounded, piecewise Lipschitz region L ​ Λ ⊂ ℝ 3 L\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3} as the scaling parameter L L tends to infinity. This is in contrast to the two-dimensional case since particles can now move freely in the direction of the magnetic field, which causes the extra ln ⁡ ( L ) \ln(L) factor. The explicit expression for the coefficient of the leading order contains a surface integral similar to the Widom formula in the non-magnetic case. It differs however in the sense that the dependence on the boundary is not solely on its area but on the “area perpendicular to the direction of the magnetic field”. On the way we prove an improved two-term asymptotic expansion (up to an error term of order one) of certain traces of one-dimensional Wiener–Hopf operators with a discontinuous symbol. This is of independent interest and leads to an improved error term of the order L 2 L^{2} of the relevant trace for piecewise 𝖢 1 , α \mathsf{C}^{1,\alpha} smooth surfaces ∂ Λ \partial\Lambda .

1.
Introduction

In recent years, entanglement entropy (EE) has become an important and intensively studied quantity of states of many-particle quantum systems. For an introduction to this topic we refer to
[
3
,
6
,
15
]
. In this paper, we study the EE of ground states of the ideal Fermi gas in a magnetic field in three-dimensional Euclidean space,
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. The two-dimensional Fermi gas in a constant magnetic field was recently analyzed in
[
5
]
and
[
21
]
, starting from the earlier work in
[
30
]
. Here, a strict area-law holds, while for the free Fermi gas in any dimension

a logarithmically enhanced area-law is valid, see
[
13
,
20
]
. Stability of these area-laws has been proved in
[
25
,
26
]
for
d\geq 2
and in
[
28
]
in the sense that adding a “small” electric or magnetic potential to the Hamiltonian does not change the leading asymptotics of the entropy. The one-dimensional case seems to be still open (for the
\alpha
-Rényi entropy with
\alpha\leq 1
).

There is an extensive literature on EE by now with many fascinating connections and implications to related fields. Here we only mention and refer to a small fraction of mathematical results. In
[
29
]
, an enhanced area-law was proved for the one-dimensional free Fermi gas in a periodic potential; the higher dimensional case remains an open problem. By the work in
[
27
,
8
,
24
]
we understand EE in Anderson-type models on the lattice. An extension to the EE of positive temperature equilibrium states (of the ideal Fermi gas) was presented in
[
22
,
23
,
34
]
. Finally, we mention results on the XY and XXZ quantum spin chain
[
1
,
16
,
4
,
7
,
11
,
12

By a (strict) area-law for a ground state of the infinitely extended Fermi gas, say in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with spatial dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
, we mean that the entanglement (or local) entropy of this state reduced to the scaled (bounded) region
L\Lambda
grows to leading order like
L^{d-1}\mathcal{H}^{2}(\partial\Lambda)
as the dimensionless real parameter
L
tends to infinity. Here,
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\partial\Lambda)
is the (Hausdorff) surface area of the boundary
\partial\Lambda
. If there is an extra
\ln(L)
factor in this leading asymptotics, then we call it a logarithmically enhanced area-law.

Whether one should expect a strict area-law or an enhanced area-law is related to the spectral properties of the one-particle Hamiltonian of the non-interacting many-particle Fermi gas. If the off-diagonal part of the integral kernel of the corresponding spectral (Fermi) projection has a fast decay (e.g., exponential), then we expect a strict area-law to hold. It is not difficult to argue for that (see
[
27
]
) but to compute and finally prove the precise leading coefficient has only been accomplished in special cases. On the other hand, if the decay of the off-diagonal part of the integral kernel is weak (e.g., inverse linear), then we can expect an enhanced area-law. In the present model we have a mixture. Namely we have an exponential decay in the planar coordinate (orthogonal to the magnetic field) and a
1/|\cdot|
decay in the longitudinal coordinate along the magnetic field. The latter prevails and leads to a logarithmically enhanced area-law. Our main result is formulated in
section 4
.

As in previous proofs there is two parts to proving such a result. Firstly, we prove a two-term asymptotic expansion for polynomials (see
section 2
). Due to the product structure of the ground state, see (
2.7
), we can dimensionally reduce the asymptotics of a three-dimensional problem to an asymptotic expansion of a one-dimensional problem with localizing sets
L\Lambda_{x^{\perp}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
and with the spectral projection of the one-dimensional Laplacian, see
section 3
. The corresponding asymptotic expansion was already proved by Landau and Widom
[
18
]
and then improved by Widom
[
35
]
. But here we need to take care of the error term which depends on the planar coordinate
x^{\perp}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and integrate over
x^{\perp}
. To this end, we show that the error term is of order one and is integrable as a function of
x^{\perp}
under some assumptions on
\Lambda
. We believe that the precise description of the error term for the one dimensional free case in terms of the finite collection of intervals
\Lambda_{x^{\perp}}
is of independent interest and we provide a proof in Appendix
C
. This dimensional reduction is also the strategy of Widom in
[
36
]
and of Sobolev in the proof of the Widom conjecture in
[
31
]
. In fact, due to the fast (exponential) decay in the planar direction error estimates are simpler to obtain than in the case with no magnetic field. This and the improved Landau–Widom (or Widom) asymptotics allows us to prove for
\mathsf{C}^{1,\alpha}
(smooth) regions
\Lambda
an error term (for polynomials as in
section 2
) of the order
L^{2}
rather than merely of lower order than
L^{2}\ln(L)
in
[
31
, Theorem 2.9]
.

Secondly, in Section
4
we make the transition in the asymptotic expansion from polynomials to the entropy function. This requires certain Schatten–von Neumann quasi-norm bounds presented in Section
5
, which in turn are based on bounds obtained in previous papers
[
21
,
20
]
and notably by Sobolev
[
33
]
.

The smoothness conditions on the region
\Lambda
to prove our two-term asymptotic result with error term
o(L^{2}\ln(L))
are rather weak, namely we require
\Lambda
to be only piecewise Lipschitz smooth. For a smooth region
\Lambda

is piecewise
\mathsf{C}^{1,\alpha}
smooth. We also present regions with weaker regularity on the boundary for which the error term (for a quadratic polynomial) can be arbitrarily close to the leading

-term. This may also be of independent interest and is the content of Section

A note on our notation: As

For a point

By our assumption, the magnetic field is equal to

\nabla\times(a,0)=B\cdot e_{3}\,.

(2.2)

The one-particle Hamiltonian of the ideal Fermi gas in three-dimensional Euclidean space

subject to the magnetic field
B\cdot e_{3}
is informally given by

\mathrm{H}_{B}\coloneqq(-\mathrm{i}\nabla^{\perp}-a)^{2}+(-\mathrm{i}\nabla^{\parallel})^{2}\,.

(2.3)

It is well-defined as a self-adjoint operator on a suitable domain in the one-particle Hilbert space
\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
.

The ground state of free fermions with one-particle Hamiltonian
\mathrm{H}_{B}
is described by the spectral projection (or Fermi projection)
\mathrm{D}_{\mu}\coloneqq\mathds{1}(\mathrm{H}_{B}\leq\mu)\coloneqq 1_{(-\infty,\mu]}(\mathrm{H}_{B})
of

below some so-called Fermi energy (or chemical potential)

. As is well-known, we have
[
10
,
19
]

(-\mathrm{i}\nabla^{\perp}-a)^{2}=B\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)\mathrm{P}_{\ell}

(2.4)

with explicitly known (infinite-dimensional) eigenprojections
\mathrm{P}_{\ell}
on
\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
. In order to write down these projections, let us introduce the Laguerre polynomials,
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(t)\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\,\binom{\ell}{\ell-j}\,t^{j}
,
t\geq 0
, of degree
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then the integral kernel of
\mathrm{P}_{\ell}
is given by the function

p_{\ell}(x^{\perp},y^{\perp})\coloneqq\frac{B}{2\pi}\,\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\big(B\|x^{\perp}-y^{\perp}\|^{2}/2\big)\,\exp\big(-B\|x^{\perp}-y^{\perp}\|^{2}/4+\mathrm{i}{\textstyle\frac{B}{2}}x^{\perp}\wedge y^{\perp}\big)\,,\quad x^{\perp},y^{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,.

(2.5)

Here,
\wedge
refers to the exterior or wedge product on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. The explicit description of this kernel is not relevant for this paper. We only use the exponential decay in
\lVert x^{\perp}-y^{\perp}\rVert^{2}
and

.
In the
z
-direction, we meet the spectral projection
\mathds{1}((-\nabla^{\parallel})^{2}\leq\mu)
with (sine) integral kernel,
\mathds{1}((-\nabla^{\parallel})^{2}\leq\mu)(z,z^{\prime})=k_{\mu}(z-z^{\prime})
,

\displaystyle k_{\mu}(z)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{\sin(\sqrt{\mu}z)}{\pi z}\,,&\mbox{ for }z\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}\\
\lim_{z\to 0}k_{\mu}(z)=\frac{\sqrt{\mu}}{\pi}&\mbox{ for }z=0\end{cases}\quad\mu>0\,.

(2.6)

The following factorization of spectral projections is crucial, which stems from the fact that the magnetic field is pointing in the
z
-direction. We work with the identification
\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})\otimes\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})=\mathsf{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})

\mathrm{H}_{B}
is the set
[B,\infty)
, we may always consider
\mu>B

\mu

\mathrm{D}_{\mu}=\mathrm{P}_{0}\otimes\mathds{1}[(-\mathrm{i}\nabla^{\parallel})^{2}\leq\mu-B]

\mu

\nu\coloneqq\lceil\frac{1}{2}(\mu/B-1)\rceil\in\mathbb{N}

, and let us set
\mu(\ell)\coloneqq\mu-B(2\ell+1)

\mathrm{D}_{\mu}=\mathds{1}(\mathrm{H}_{B}\leq\mu)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\nu-1}\mathrm{P}_{\ell}\otimes\mathds{1}[(-\mathrm{i}\nabla^{\parallel})^{2}\leq\mu(\ell)]

(2.7)

with integral kernel (

\mathrm{D}_{\mu}(x,y)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\nu-1}p_{\ell}(x^{\perp},y^{\perp})k_{\mu(\ell)}(x^{\parallel}-y^{\parallel})\,.

(2.8)

For any Borel subset
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
we define the spatial reduction (or truncation) of

\Lambda

\mathrm{D}_{\mu}(\Lambda)\coloneqq\mathds{1}_{\Lambda}\mathrm{D}_{\mu}\mathds{1}_{\Lambda}\,.

(2.9)

Before we define the main object in this paper, we introduce for any

\gamma

\displaystyle h_{\gamma}(t)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\ln\big(t^{\gamma}+(1-t)^{\gamma}\big)\,,\ \gamma\not=1\,,

(2.10)

\displaystyle h_{1}(t)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\ -t\ln(t)-(1-t)\ln(1-t)\mbox{ if }t\not\in\{0,1\}\mbox{ and }h_{1}(0)\coloneqq h_{1}(1)\coloneqq 0\,.

(2.11)

Now, for a ground state described by the projection

. The remaining integral over
\partial\Lambda
is independent of the strength
B
and remains fixed. For the volume term we have in this limit

\displaystyle\frac{B}{2\pi^{2}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\nu-1}\sqrt{\mu-(2\ell+1)B}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\mu^{3/2}}{4\pi^{2}\nu}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\nu}\sqrt{1-\ell/\nu}\sim\frac{\mu^{3/2}}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\text{d}x\,\sqrt{x}=\frac{\mu^{3/2}}{6\pi^{2}}\,.

In this limit the volume term equals the above volume term at
B=0
as in (
2.17
). To summarize, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{B\downarrow 0}\mbox{rhs of }\eqref{main formula}

\displaystyle=L^{3}f(1)\frac{\mu^{3/2}}{6\pi^{2}}|\Lambda|+L^{2}\ln(L)\frac{\mu}{2\pi}\,\mathsf{I}(f)\int_{\partial\Lambda}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}(v)\,|n(v)\cdot e_{3}|+o(L^{2}\ln(L))\,,

which is identical to the right-hand side (rhs) of (
2.17
) except for the prefactor depending on
\partial\Lambda
.

(iii)

There is no ’level mixing’ at the order in
L^{2}\ln(L)
in the sense that each Landau level enters individually in the numerical coefficient. In
[
21
]
, we proved that level mixing occurs in the two-dimensional setting at the next-to-leading order, namely at the order
L
. We expect level mixing to occur in the present case at the order
L^{2}
. This is certainly possible to prove, say for a cylindrical region, but it requires a three-term expansion in the
x^{\parallel}
-coordinate and the by now proved two-term expansion in the
x^{\perp}
-coordinate
[
21
]
. The caveat for us to proceed with this question is that the mentioned three-term expansion has not been proved so far for the entropy function. This is an interesting open problem.

(iv)

For (
i
) to hold we require only weak regularity of the boundary
\partial\Lambda
like in the proof in
[
20
]
for the ideal Fermi gas. In contrast, the proof of the corresponding two-term asymptotics for the two-dimensional model in
[
21
]
required
\mathsf{C}^{3}
smooth regions. This smoothness was a technical condition and may not be necessary. On the other hand and more importantly, only the leading contribution of the two-dimensional Hamiltonian enters and the extra logarithm stems from an expansion in the longitudinal direction, where weaker conditions suffice.

3.

We split the proof into two steps. The first one is the lemma below, which reduces the computation of the trace to an integral of the trace of the projection operator

\displaystyle\left(\mathds{1}_{L\Lambda_{x^{\perp}}}\mathds{1}[(-\mathrm{i}\nabla^{\parallel})^{2}\leq\mu]\mathds{1}_{L\Lambda_{x^{\perp}}}\right)^{m}-\frac{\sqrt{\mu}}{\pi}L\lvert\Lambda_{x^{\perp}}\rvert-2\,\mathsf{I}(m)\#(\partial\Lambda_{x^{\perp}})\ln(1+L)\Big|

(3.36)

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{\#(\partial\Lambda_{x^{\perp}})-1}\big(1+\lvert\ln(\lvert w_{x^{\perp}i}-w_{x^{\perp}i+1}\rvert)\rvert\big)

(3.37)

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{\#(\partial\Lambda_{x^{\perp}})}\Big(1+\lvert\ln\big(\inf_{v\in\partial\Lambda_{x^{\perp}}\setminus w_{x^{\perp}i}}\lvert w_{x^{\perp}i}-v\rvert\big)\rvert\Big)\,.

(3.38)

In the last step, we used that the distance between any two points in
\partial\Lambda
is bounded from above, as
\Lambda
is bounded to conclude that only short distances
\lvert v-v_{i}\rvert
can lead to an error term larger than the
O(\#(\partial\Lambda_{x^{\perp}}))
-term we have in front. A lower bound for the infimum is provided by
Appendix A
. This bound is zero in some cases, which leads to the logarithm being infinite. This just means that our integrand in the integral over
x^{\perp}
attains infinity. The integral can still exist and we will show that it does.

As the terms of order
L^{3}
and
L^{2}\ln(L)
work just like in the previous case, we will only consider the error term. Hence, we need to estimate

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x^{\perp}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\#(\partial\Lambda_{x^{\perp}})}\Big(1+\lvert\ln(\inf_{v\in\partial\Lambda_{x^{\perp}}\setminus w_{x^{\perp}i}}\lvert w_{x^{\perp}i}-v\rvert)\rvert\Big)

(3.39)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x^{\perp}\sum_{i=1}^{\#(\partial\Lambda_{x^{\perp}})}\Big(1+\lvert\ln(\min\{\operatorname{dist}(w_{x^{\perp}i},\Gamma),\lvert n((x^{\perp},w_{x^{\perp}i}))\cdot e_{3}\rvert^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})\rvert\}\Big)

(3.40)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x^{\perp}\sum_{w\in\{x^{\perp}\}\times\partial\Lambda_{x^{\perp}}}\Big(1+\lvert\ln(\operatorname{dist}(w,\Gamma))\rvert+\lvert\ln(\lvert n(w)\cdot e_{3}\rvert)\rvert\Big)\,.

(3.41)

In the first step, we applied
Appendix A
with the vectors
v_{1}\coloneqq(x^{\perp},w_{x^{\perp}i})
amd
v_{2}\coloneqq(x^{\perp},v)
noting that
\frac{v_{1}-v_{2}}{\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|}=\pm e_{3}
. We now want to rewrite this integral as an integral over the boundary
\partial\Lambda
. This is possible by
Appendix A
. Hence, we have (recall that
\mathcal{H}^{2}
is the canonical surface measure on
\partial\Lambda
),

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x^{\perp}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\#(\partial\Lambda_{x^{\perp}})}\Big(1+\lvert\ln(\inf_{v\in\partial\Lambda_{x^{\perp}}\setminus w_{x^{\perp}i}}\lvert w_{x^{\perp}i}-v\rvert)\rvert\Big)

(3.42)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\partial\Lambda}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}(w)\,\big[1+\lvert\ln(\operatorname{dist}(w,\Gamma))\rvert+\lvert\ln(\lvert n(w)\cdot e_{3}\rvert)\rvert\big]\,\lvert n(w)\cdot e_{3}\rvert

(3.43)

\displaystyle\leq C+C\int_{\partial\Lambda}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}(w)\,\lvert\ln(\operatorname{dist}(w,\Gamma))\rvert\leq C\,.

(3.44)

In the second step, we used that
0\leq\lvert n(w)\cdot e_{3}\rvert\leq 1
and that for
0\leq t\leq 1
, we have
0\leq\lvert t\ln(t)\rvert\leq 1/\mathrm{e}
. The last step is a rather lengthy, not particularly insightful calculation, which can be found in
Appendix A
.

Once we put the factor
L^{2}
back in front of this, we arrive at the error term
O(L^{2})
which completes the proof of the second part of this theorem.
∎

4.
Entanglement entropy

Here is the main result of this paper.

THEOREM 4.1
.

Suppose that
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is a piecewise Lipschitz region and let
\mu>B
. Let
\nu\coloneqq\lceil\frac{1}{2}(\mu/B-1)\rceil
and let
h\colon[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a continuous function, which is
\beta
-Hölder continuous at
0
and
1
for some
1\geq\beta>0
, and assume that
h(0)=h(1)=0
. Then, we have the asymptotic expansion

{\rm tr}\,h(\mathrm{D}_{\mu}(L\Lambda))=L^{2}\ln(L)\nu B\frac{1}{\pi}\,\mathsf{I}(h)\int_{\partial\Lambda}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}(v)\,|n(v)\cdot e_{3}|+o(L^{2}\ln(L))\,.

(4.1)

In particular, as the
\gamma
-Rényi entropy function
h_{\gamma}
is
\beta
-Hölder continuous for any
\beta<\min(\gamma,1)
, the
\gamma
-Rényi entanglement entropy,
\mathrm{S}_{\gamma}(L\Lambda)
, of the ground state at Fermi energy
\mu
localized to
L\Lambda
, satisfies the asymptotic expansion

\mathrm{S}_{\gamma}(L\Lambda)=L^{2}\ln(L)\nu B\,\frac{1+\gamma}{24\gamma\pi}\int_{\partial\Lambda}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}(v)\,|n(v)\cdot e_{3}|+o(L^{2}\ln(L))

(4.2)

as
L\to\infty
.

We use certain estimates on traces. To this end, let us denote by
s_{n}({T}),n\in\mathbb{N}
, the singular values of the compact operator
{T}
on a (separable) Hilbert space, arranged in decreasing order. The standard notation
\mathfrak{S}_{p},0<p<\infty
is used for the class of operators with a finite
Schatten–von Neumann quasi-norm
:

\displaystyle\|{T}\|_{p}\coloneqq\bigg[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}s_{n}({T})^{p}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{p}}<\infty\,.

If
p\geq 1
, then
\|\cdot\|_{p}
defines a norm. For
0<p<1
it is a quasi-norm that satisfies the
p
-triangle inequality

\displaystyle\|{T}_{1}+{T}_{2}\|_{p}^{p}\leq\|{T}_{2}\|_{p}^{p}+\|{T}_{2}\|_{p}^{p}\,.

(4.3)

The class
\mathfrak{S}_{1}
is the standard trace-class. The class
\mathfrak{S}_{2}
is the ideal of Hilbert–Schmidt operators. The
p
-Schatten quasi-norm estimate required for this proof is shown in
section 5
.

Proof of
section 4
.

The proof goes along the same line of arguments as presented in
[
20
]
and
[
21
]
. We recall that
\mathsf{I}(h_{\gamma})=(1+\gamma)/(24\gamma)
and thus we are left to show the claim for the function
h
. Let
r=\beta/2
and
\varepsilon>0
. We choose a smooth cutoff function
\zeta_{\varepsilon}
such that
0\leq\zeta_{\varepsilon}\leq 1
and such that
\zeta
vanishes on
[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
and equals
1
on
[0,\varepsilon/2]\cup[1-\varepsilon/2,1]
. As
h
is continuous and
\beta
-Hölder continuous at
0
and
1
, there is a constant
C
such that

h(t)\leq Ct^{\beta}(1-t)^{\beta}\,,\quad t\in[0,1]\,.

(4.4)

This implies

\displaystyle|(\zeta_{\varepsilon}h)(t)|\leq C\varepsilon^{r}t^{r}(1-t)^{r}\,\quad t\in[0,1]\,.

(4.5)

As the function
t\mapsto\frac{(1-\zeta_{\varepsilon}(t))h(t)}{t(1-t)}
is continuous, we can infer from the Stone–Weierstrass approximation theorem that there is a polynomial
p
and a function
\delta_{\varepsilon}\colon[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
with
\lVert\delta_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{\infty}([0,1])}\leq\varepsilon^{r}
and

\frac{(1-\zeta_{\varepsilon}(t))h(t)}{t(1-t)}=p(t)+\delta_{\varepsilon}(t)\,,\quad t\in[0,1]\,.

(4.6)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle h(t)=p(t)t(1-t)+\delta_{\varepsilon}(t)t(1-t)+\zeta_{\varepsilon}(t)h(t)\eqqcolon p(t)t(1-t)+\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)\,.

(4.7)

As
t(1-t)\leq t^{r}(1-t)^{r}
, we observe

\lvert\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)\rvert\leq C\varepsilon^{r}t^{r}(1-t)^{r}\,,\quad t\in[0,1]\,.

(4.8)

Thus, using

, (
2.7
) and (
4.3
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\lvert{\rm tr}\,\phi_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{D}_{\mu}(L\Lambda))\rvert

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{r}{\rm tr}\,(\mathrm{D}_{\mu}(L\Lambda)^{r}(1-\mathrm{D}_{\mu}(L\Lambda))^{r})

(4.9)

\displaystyle=C\varepsilon^{r}\lVert\mathds{1}_{L\Lambda}\mathrm{D}_{\mu}\mathds{1}_{L\Lambda^{\complement}}\mathrm{D}_{\mu}\mathds{1}_{L\Lambda}\rVert_{r}^{r}

(4.10)

\displaystyle=C\varepsilon^{r}\lVert\mathds{1}_{L\Lambda}\mathrm{D}_{\mu}\mathds{1}_{L\Lambda^{\complement}}\rVert_{2r}^{2r}

(4.11)

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{r}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\nu-1}\lVert\mathds{1}_{L\Lambda}(\mathrm{P}_{\ell}\otimes\mathds{1}((-\mathrm{i}\nabla^{\parallel})^{2}\leq\mu(\ell)))\mathds{1}_{L\Lambda^{\complement}}\rVert_{2r}^{2r}

(4.12)

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{r}CL^{2}\ln(L)\,.

(4.13)

In (
4.10
), we used that
\mathrm{D}_{\mu}
is a projection.
Let
q(t)\coloneqq p(t)t(1-t)
. Now, by linearity of
\mathsf{I}
and the estimate (

), we have

\lvert\mathsf{I}(h)-\mathsf{I}(q)\rvert=\lvert\mathsf{I}(\phi_{\varepsilon})\rvert\leq C\varepsilon^{r}\,\mathsf{I}(t\mapsto t^{r}(1-t)^{r})\leq C\varepsilon^{r}\,.

(4.14)

section 2
(i) applied for the polynomial

with

yields

{\rm tr}\,q(\mathrm{D}_{\mu}(L\Lambda))=L^{2}\ln(L)B\nu\,\mathsf{I}(q)\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\partial\Lambda}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}(v)\,|n(v)\cdot e_{3}|+o(L^{2}\ln(L))\,.

(4.15)

Now, combining (

) and (

\limsup_{L\to\infty}\left|\frac{{\rm tr}\,h(\mathrm{D}_{\mu}(L\Lambda))}{L^{2}\ln(L)}-\nu B\,{\sf I}(h)\,\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\partial\Lambda}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}(v)\,|n(v)\cdot e_{3}|\right|\leq C\varepsilon^{r}\,.

(4.16)

As

is arbitrary, we have proved the claim.
∎

5.
Schatten–von Neumann quasi-norm estimates

By a box in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
we mean a Cartesian product of
d
intervals. These intervals do not have to be bounded. We will denote subsets of
\mathbb{R}
by
I
, of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by
\Upsilon
, and of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by
\Lambda
. We will combine known estimates for the two-dimensional magnetic Hamiltonian from
[
21
]
and for the one-dimensional Hamiltonian
[
20
,
33
]
without a magnetic field.

Let
\Upsilon,\Upsilon^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be Lipschitz regions and let
I,I^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}
be finite unions of closed intervals.
Then we have

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{\Upsilon\times I}\big(\mathrm{P}_{\ell}\otimes\mathds{1}[(-\mathrm{i}\nabla^{\parallel})^{2}\leq\mu]\big)\mathds{1}_{\Upsilon^{\prime}\times I^{\prime}}=\big(\mathds{1}_{\Upsilon}\mathrm{P}_{\ell}\mathds{1}_{\Upsilon^{\prime}}\big)\otimes\big(\mathds{1}_{I}\mathds{1}[(-\mathrm{i}\nabla^{\parallel})^{2}\leq\mu]\mathds{1}_{I^{\prime}}\big)\,.

(5.1)

As the singular values of the tensor product of two operators are given by all possible products of pairs of the individual singular values, we have for any
0<p\leq\infty\colon

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathds{1}_{\Upsilon\times I}\left(\mathrm{P}_{\ell}\otimes\mathds{1}[(-\mathrm{i}\nabla^{\parallel})^{2}\leq\mu]\right)\mathds{1}_{\Upsilon^{\prime}\times I^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{p}=\left\lVert\mathds{1}_{\Upsilon}\mathrm{P}_{\ell}\mathds{1}_{\Upsilon^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert\mathds{1}_{I}\mathds{1}[(-\mathrm{i}\nabla^{\parallel})^{2}\leq\mu]\mathds{1}_{I^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{p}\,.

(5.2)

The following general properties will be useful:

LEMMA 5.1
.

For any self-adjoint bounded operators
S,T\colon\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, any measurable sets
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}
,
\Omega_{1}^{\prime},\Omega_{2}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
0<p\leq 1
, we have

Symmetry:

\left\lVert\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{1}}T\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{2}}\right\rVert_{p}=\left\lVert\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{2}}T\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{1}}\right\rVert_{p}
,

Monotonicity I:

\left\lVert\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{1}}T\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{2}}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\left\lVert\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{1}^{\prime}}T\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{2}\cup\Omega_{2}^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{p}
,

Monotonicity II:

If
0\leq S\leq T
, then
\lVert S\rVert_{p}\leq\lVert T\rVert_{p}
,

Subadditivity:

\left\lVert\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{1}^{\prime}}T\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{2}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\leq\left\lVert\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{1}}T\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{2}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}+\left\lVert\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{1}^{\prime}}T\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{2}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}
.

A proof of these properties can be found for example in
[
28
]
.

We assume now that the magnetic-field strength has been “scaled out” so that
B=1
for the remainder of this section. The effective scale in the planar coordinates is
L\sqrt{B}
and in the perpendicular it is
L\sqrt{\mu}
.

Next, we collect some more specific (quasi-)norm estimates for both the one dimensional free Hamiltonian and the constant magnetic field Hamiltonian in two dimensions.

PROPOSITION 5.2
.

Let
0<p\leq 1,\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and let
\mu>0
. Then there is a constant
C
such that for any

, any measurable set
\Upsilon\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that

and any measurable set

such that

, we have the estimates

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathds{1}_{[0,1]^{2}+x}\mathrm{P}_{\ell}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\,,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathds{1}_{[0,1]^{2}+x}\mathrm{P}_{\ell}\mathds{1}_{\Upsilon^{\complement}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\exp(-p\delta^{2}/{18})\,,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathds{1}_{[t,t+h]}\mathds{1}[(-\mathrm{i}\nabla^{\parallel})^{2}\leq\mu]\right\rVert_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq Ch\,,

(5.5)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathds{1}_{[t,t+h]}\mathds{1}[(-\mathrm{i}\nabla^{\parallel})^{2}\leq\mu]\mathds{1}_{I^{\complement}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\ln(h)\,.

(5.6)

Proof.

The first two inequalities follow by
[
21

2

2

The choice of the value

. To prove the last inequality, we first use monotonicity I and the translation invariance, then the standard unitary equivalence, see for example
[

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathds{1}_{[t,t+h]}\mathds{1}[(-\mathrm{i}\nabla^{\parallel})^{2}\leq\mu]\mathds{1}_{I^{\complement}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}

(5.8)

\displaystyle\leq C\ln(h)\,.

(5.9)

For the third inequality, we will reduce to the case

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\mathds{1}_{[0,2]}

LEMMA 6.1

Let

be a finite union of intervals of finite lengths

with disjoint closures. Let

(6.1)

where

is an entirely independent constant.

For

equals the coefficient of the leading asymptotic behavior of

By scaling we can assume

and eventually replace the lengths

Then, we use the geometric series

with

. As

has on operator norm of at most

, we can estimate

\displaystyle\lVert(\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\mathds{1}(-\Delta\leq 1)\mathds{1}_{\Omega})^{m}

\displaystyle-\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\mathds{1}(-\Delta\leq 1)\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(m-1)\lVert a(a-1)\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle=(m-1)\lVert\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\mathds{1}(-\Delta\leq 1)\mathds{1}_{\Omega^{\complement}}\mathds{1}(-\Delta\leq 1)\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle=(m-1)\lVert\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\mathds{1}(-\Delta\leq 1)\mathds{1}_{\Omega^{\complement}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=(m-1)\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}x\int_{\Omega^{\complement}}\mathrm{d}y\,k(x-y)^{2}\,,

(6.2)

with the function

defined in (

).

For a fixed

denoting the connected components (subintervals) of

\bigcup_{I\in\pi_{0}(\Omega)}\big\{(x,y)\colon x\in I,y\not\in I\big\}\,.

\displaystyle\lVert(\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\mathds{1}

\displaystyle(-\Delta\leq 1)\mathds{1}_{\Omega})^{m}-\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\mathds{1}(-\Delta\leq 1)\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\rVert_{1}

(6.4)

\displaystyle\leq(m-1)\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\ell_{j}}\mathrm{d}x\int_{(0,\ell_{j})^{\complement}}\mathrm{d}y\,k(x-y)^{2}

Appendix A
Some geometric results

Here, we assemble a few geometric statements that we used.

LEMMA A.1
.

Let
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
be a piecewise
\mathsf{C}^{1,\alpha}
region for some
0<\alpha<1
. Let
(\Psi_{\mathrm{pC},i})_{i\in I}
be a piecewise
\mathsf{C}^{1,\alpha}
atlas of
\partial\Lambda
and
\Gamma
as defined in
section 2
. Then there is a constant
C
depending only on
\Lambda
such that for all unequal

and
v_{2}
in
\partial\Lambda
, we have

\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|\geq C\min\left\{\left\lvert n(v_{1})\cdot\frac{v_{1}-v_{2}}{\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|}\right\rvert^{\frac{1}{\alpha}},\operatorname{dist}(v_{1},\Gamma)\right\}\,.

(A.1)

REMARK A.2
.

The normal vector
n(v_{1})
is well-defined if
v_{1}\not\in\Gamma
. In the case
v_{1}\in\Gamma
, the minimum on the right-hand side is meant to be
0=\operatorname{dist}(v_{1},\Gamma)
, which turns it into a trivial statement.

Proof.

We begin with the case
v_{1}\in\Gamma
or
v_{2}\in\Gamma
;
v_{1}\in\Gamma
is explained in the above remark. If
v_{2}\in\Gamma
, then we trivially have
\lVert v_{1}-v_{2}\rVert\geq\operatorname{dist}(v_{1},\Gamma)
and thus the claim holds for any
C\leq 1
.

Let us now consider the case that there is an
i\in I
such that both
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are in
\Psi_{\text{pC},i}((0,1)^{d})
. As
\Psi\coloneqq\Psi_{\text{pC},i}
is injective, there are unique
x_{k}\in(0,1)^{d}
such that
\Psi(x_{k})=v_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
for
k=1,2
. We observe that
n(v_{1})\cdot D\Psi(x_{1})=0\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, as the image of the matrix
D\Psi(x_{1})
is the tangent space to
\partial\Lambda
at
v_{1}
and hence is orthogonal to the outward normal vector
n(v_{1})
. Thus, using (
2.13
), we see

\displaystyle\lvert n(v_{1})\cdot(v_{1}-v_{2})\rvert

\displaystyle=\big\lvert n(v_{1})\cdot\big(\Psi(x_{1})-\Psi(x_{2})\big)\big\rvert

(A.2)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert x_{1}-x_{2}\rVert\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\big\lvert n(v_{1})\cdot D\Psi(tx_{1}+(1-t)x_{2})\big\rvert

(A.3)

\displaystyle=\lVert x_{1}-x_{2}\rVert\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\big\lvert n(v_{1})\cdot(D\Psi(tx_{1}+(1-t)x_{2})-D\Psi(x_{1})\big\rvert

(A.4)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert x_{1}-x_{2}\rVert\,C\lVert x_{1}-x_{2}\rVert^{\alpha}=C\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{1+\alpha}\,.

(A.5)

As
\Psi
is bi-Lipschitz, we know that
\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|=\|\Psi(x_{1})-\Psi(x_{2})\|\geq C\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|
. Using this and dividing both sides by
\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|
, we arrive at

\left\lvert n(v_{1})\cdot\frac{v_{1}-v_{2}}{\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|}\right\rvert\leq C\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|^{\alpha}\,.

(A.6)

We are now in the remaining case that
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
lie in different
\Psi_{\text{pC},i}((0,1)^{d})
’s since
\partial\Lambda=\Gamma\cup\bigcup_{i\in I}\Psi_{\text{pC},i}((0,1)^{d})
.

Let
(\Psi_{\text{gL},j})_{j\in J}
be a global Lipschitz atlas of
\partial\Lambda
. As
\partial\Lambda=\bigcup_{j\in J}\Psi_{\text{gL},j}((0,1)^{d})
is a cover by (relatively) open sets and
\partial\Lambda
is a compact metric space, by Lebesgue’s number lemma, there is an
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
v\in\partial\Lambda
there is an
j\in J
with
B_{\varepsilon}(v)\cap\partial\Lambda\subset\Psi_{\text{gL},j}((0,1)^{d})
, where
B_{\varepsilon}(v)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
is the open ball of radius
\varepsilon
at
v
.

If
\lVert v_{1}-v_{2}\rVert\geq\varepsilon
, we can choose
C=\varepsilon
to get the statement, as the first expression inside the minimum is at most
1
.

Hence, we are left with the case
\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|<\varepsilon
. Now, we get an
j\in J
such that
v_{1},v_{2}\in\Psi_{\text{gL},j}((0,1)^{d})
. Again, we define
y_{k}
by
\Psi_{\text{gL},j}(y_{k})=v_{k}
for
k=1,2
. The image
\gamma
of the linear path from
y_{1}
to
y_{2}
is at most
C\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|\leq C\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|
long. As
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are in the images of two different
\Psi_{\text{pC},i}
’s, the path
\gamma
has to intersect some edge
\Psi_{\text{pC},i}(\partial(0,1)^{d})
which implies
\gamma\cap\Gamma\neq\emptyset
. Hence, we have

\operatorname{dist}(v_{1},\Gamma)\leq{\mathcal{H}^{1}}(\gamma)\leq C\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|\leq C\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|\,.

(A.7)

The last inequality follows since
\Psi_{\text{gL},j}
is bi-Lipschitz. This finishes the proof.

LEMMA A.3
.

For

(A.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{d}(\partial\Lambda)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{K}(\Lambda)\,,

(A.10)

where

be the set of all piecewise Lipschitz atlases of

We consider the set

\displaystyle A_{r}\coloneqq\left(\frac{r}{C_{\text{lip}}(f)\sqrt{d}}\mathbb{Z}\right)^{d}\cap[0,1]^{d}\,.

(A.12)

The maximum distance a point in

can have from

. For the cardinality

of

, there is a

LEMMA A.4
.

Let
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
be a piecewise Lipschitz region with piecewise Lipschitz atlas

satisfy that there are

exists. Then the signed distance function

is differentiable at

, the outward unit normal vector

is well-defined, orthogonal to the image of

To prove this statement, we need the following result from intersection theory.

LEMMA A.5
.

Let

,
f_{1}\colon[-1,1]\to\overline{B}_{R}^{(3)}(0),f_{2}\colon\overline{B}_{R}^{(2)}(0)\to\overline{B}_{R}^{(3)}(0)
be continuous functions such that
f_{1}(\pm 1)=\pm Re_{3}
and
f_{2}
restricted to the boundary is the equatorial embedding, that is,
f_{2}(x)=(x,0)
for
\|x\|=R
. Then, the images of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
intersect.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume
R=1
. Assume
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
were two such functions such that their images do not intersect. Let
\eta_{1}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3},t\mapsto(0,0,t)
and
\eta_{2}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3},x\mapsto(x,0)
be the natural orthogonal inclusions. The assumptions on
f_{j}
can now be stated as
f_{j}(x_{j})=\eta_{j}(x_{j})
for

with
\|x_{j}\|=1
for

. We extend the maps
f_{j}
to the

by setting

\displaystyle f_{j}(x_{j})\coloneqq\begin{cases}f_{j}(x_{j})&\quad\text{ if }\|x_{j}\|\leq 1\\
\eta_{j}(x_{j})&\quad\text{ if }\|x_{j}\|>1\end{cases}\,,\quad x_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{j}\,,\,j\in\{1,2\}\,.

(A.14)

Trivially, these extensions are still continuous and their images still do not intersect. As the images do not intersect and only get close to each other in the compact set
\overline{B}_{1}^{(3)}(0)
, they have a positive distance. We can now mollify
f_{j}
by convolution with an appropriately chosen, compactly supported smooth function to get
\hat{f}_{j}
such that the images of
\hat{f}_{1}
and
\hat{f}_{2}
still have positive distance and
\hat{f}_{j}(x_{j})=\eta_{j}(x_{j})
for any

with
\|x_{j}\|\geq 2
.

For
d=1,2,3
, consider the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}\cup\{\infty\}
. With the charts
\text{id}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to\mathbb{S}^{d},x\mapsto x
and
\iota_{d}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to\mathbb{S}^{d},x\mapsto x/\lVert x\rVert^{2},0\mapsto\infty
, it becomes a differentiable manifold. We now extend
\hat{f}_{j}
to a function from
\mathbb{S}^{j}
to
\mathbb{S}^{3}
by setting
\hat{f}_{j}(\infty)\coloneqq\infty
for

. The point
\infty
is now an intersection point of
\hat{f}_{1}
and
\hat{f}_{2}
. We want to show that the extended functions are still smooth and that they intersect transversely at
\infty
, see for instance
[
14
, Page 113]
. For
j\in\{1,2\}
and

with
\|x_{j}\|<\frac{1}{2}
, we observe

(\iota_{3}^{-1}\circ\hat{f}_{j}\circ\iota_{j})(x_{j})=(\iota_{3}^{-1}\circ\eta_{j})(x_{j}\lVert x_{j}\rVert^{-2})=\eta_{j}(x_{j})\,.

(A.15)

Thus, in the charts

the maps
\hat{f}_{j}
are linear and orthogonal at

(which corresponds to
\infty\in\mathbb{S}^{j}
). The maps
\hat{f}_{1},\hat{f}_{2}
are therefore smooth and intersect transversely at
\infty
. In conclusion, we have just constructed two smooth maps
\hat{f}_{j}\colon\mathbb{S}^{j}\to\mathbb{S}^{3}
, which intersect transversely and have a unique intersection point. Thus, their oriented intersection number is equal to the local intersection number at this intersection point, which is

or

(in fact, it is

). However, both maps are contractible (homotopic to a constant map) and thus, as oriented intersection numbers are homotopy invariant (see
[

, Page 115]
), they should have intersection number

. This is a contradiction. Hence, the assumption that

and
f_{2}
do not intersect was wrong.

LEMMA A.7
.

Let
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
be a piecewise Lipschitz region,
\pi\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be the canonical projection and
f\colon\partial\Lambda\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
be measurable. Let
n\colon\partial\Lambda\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
be the outward normal vector field, which is defined almost everywhere (see
Appendix A
). Then we have

\int_{\partial\Lambda}\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{H}^{2}}(v)\,f(v)\lvert n(v)\cdot e_{3}\rvert=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\sum_{v\in\pi^{-1}(x)\cap\partial\Lambda}f(v)\,.

(A.36)

Proof.

The proof is based on a quite general form of changing variables. We use the following area-formula (see
[
9
, Theorem 3.9]
) with one slight modification. To this end, let
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\leq m
,
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be open,
\Phi\colon U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
be Lipschitz continuous and let
g\colon U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
be measurable. Then we have the identity

\int_{U}\mathrm{d}y\,g(y)\lvert D\Phi(y)\rvert=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{H}^{n}}(x)\sum_{y\in\Phi^{-1}(x)}g(y)\,,

(A.37)

where
\lvert D\Phi(y)\rvert^{2}=\det(D\Phi(y)^{*}D\Phi(y))
. In
[
9
]
, this is stated for
g\in\mathsf{L}^{1}(U)
. However, their proof also applies to positive, measurable functions
g
as an identity in
[0,\infty]
.

We cannot apply this directly to
\pi
, as it decreases the dimension and
\pi
does not have an inverse. Thus, we have to introduce a new map.

Let
(\Psi_{\text{pL},i})_{i\in I}
be a piecewise Lipschitz atlas of
\partial\Lambda
so that
\partial\Lambda=\bigcup_{i\in I}\Psi_{\text{pL},i}([0,1]^{2})
. We may assume that
\operatorname{supp}(f)\subset\Psi_{\text{pL},i}((0,1)^{2})
for some
i\in I
. For the remainder of this proof, we write
\Psi
for
\Psi_{\text{pL},i}
.

Now, we can apply (
A.37
) with
\Phi\coloneqq\pi\circ\Psi
and
g\coloneqq f\circ\Psi
. Thus, we see

\displaystyle\int_{(0,1)^{2}}\mathrm{d}y\,f(\Psi(y))\lvert D(\pi\circ\Psi)(y)\rvert

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\sum_{y\in(\pi\circ\Psi)^{-1}(x)}f(\Psi(y))

(A.38)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\sum_{v\in\pi^{-1}(x)\cap\partial\Lambda}f(v)\,.

(A.39)

We used that
\Psi
is bijective. So, we already have the right-hand side of the claim. We will apply again (
A.37
) with the functions
\Phi\coloneqq\Psi
and
g
given by

g(y)\coloneqq f(\Psi(y))\frac{\lvert D(\pi\circ\Psi)(y)\rvert}{\lvert D\Psi(y)\rvert}\,,\quad y\in(0,1)^{2}\,.

(A.40)

Thus, using that
\Psi
is bijective and that the measure
\mathcal{H}^{2}
on
\partial\Lambda
is the
2

\displaystyle\int_{(0,1)^{2}}\mathrm{d}y\,f(\Psi(y))\lvert D(\pi\circ\Psi)(y)\rvert=\int_{\partial\Lambda}\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{H}^{2}}(v)\,f(v)\frac{\lvert D(\pi\circ\Psi)(\Psi^{-1}(v))\rvert}{\lvert D\Psi(\Psi^{-1}(v))\rvert}\,.

(A.41)

To conclude the proof, we only need to show that the quotient of the functional determinants is given by
\lvert n(v)\cdot e_{3}\rvert
for almost every
v\in\partial\Lambda
. Let
v\in\partial\Lambda
be such that
B\coloneqq D\Psi(\Psi^{-1}(v))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3\times 2}
is well-defined. We identify the two column vectors of the

as

and
w_{2}
. The image of

is the tangent space to
\partial\Lambda
at

\Psi
is bi-Lipschitz continuous, the matrix

has full rank. The normal vector

is now orthogonal to the linear independent vectors

As

. For the determinant of this

(A.42)

In conclusion, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\lvert D(\pi\circ\Psi)(\Psi^{-1}(v))\rvert}{\lvert D\Psi(\Psi^{-1}(v))\rvert}=\frac{\lvert(w_{1}\times w_{2})\cdot e_{3}\rvert}{\lvert w_{1}\times w_{2}\rvert}=\lvert n(v)\cdot e_{3}\rvert\,.

(A.43)

In combination with (
A.39
) and (

Appendix C
Asymptotic expansion with order one error term

Our final result,
Appendix C
, in this section deals with the asymptotic expansion for a finite union of bounded intervals. That is, we assume that we have
k\in\mathbb{N}
open and bounded intervals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}
, whose closures are disjoint. More precisely, there exist
d_{i}>0
for
1\leq i<k
with
\sup I_{i}+d_{i}=\inf I_{i+1}
. Let
\ell_{j}\coloneqq|I_{j}|
be the length of
I_{j}
and let
\Omega\coloneqq\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}I_{j}
. The symbol
\ell
for the length of intervals in this section has, of course, nothing to do with the index of a Landau level.

The proof of
Appendix C
is based upon two lemmata. The first lemma is per se not an asymptotic result but reduces the analysis to a single interval including an error term. The second lemma deals with the asymptotic expansion for a single interval, including an order one error term, and improves a seminal result by Landau and Widom in
[
18
]
. This is achieved by improving a certain estimate in their proof which allows for an order one error term instead of
o(\ln(L))
. Later, Widom
[
35
]
extended their result and proved that the error term is indeed of order one. This was used by Sobolev in
[
31
, Chapter 8]
to obtain concrete error terms. Our error term is somewhat different and fits our purposes. It is important to notice that there is still an undetermined error term of order one which is however independent of the scaling and the lengths of the intervals and depends only on the energy.

The first lemma is the following.

LEMMA C.1
.

Let
\mu>0
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then under the above assumptions on
\Omega
we have

\left\lvert{\rm tr}\,\Big[\Big(\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\mathds{1}[(-\mathrm{i}\nabla^{\parallel})^{2}\leq\mu]\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\Big)^{m}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\mathds{1}_{I_{j}}\mathds{1}[(-\mathrm{i}\nabla^{\parallel})^{2}\leq\mu]\mathds{1}_{I_{j}}\right)^{m}\Big]\right\rvert\leq C\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\ln\Big(1+\frac{\ell_{j}}{1+d_{j}}\Big)\,,

(C.1)

where
C
is a constant depending on
m
and
\mu
, but crucially not on
k
or the intervals themselves.

Proof.

Let
Q\coloneqq\mathds{1}[(-\mathrm{i}\nabla^{\parallel})^{2}\leq\mu]
. It is convenient to make a slight generalization by allowing
I_{k}
to be any measurable set such that
d_{k-1}\coloneqq\inf(I_{k})-\sup(I_{k-1})>0
. We note that
\ell_{k}
is undefined, but it is also not present in our claim. We proceed by induction with respect to the number of intervals,
k
. Once we have proved the statement for
k=2
, the statement follows for any
k
, as we can choose
I_{k}^{\prime}\coloneqq I_{k}\cup I_{k+1}
.

Hence, we just have to deal with the case
k=2
. We observe
1_{\Omega}=1_{I_{1}}+1_{I_{2}}
. We multiply out the first term and, using
I_{1}\cap I_{2}=\emptyset
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\mathds{1}_{\Omega}Q\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\right)^{m}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\{1,2\}^{\{0,\dots,m\}}}\mathds{1}_{I_{j_{0}}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}Q\mathds{1}_{I_{j_{i}}}\,.

(C.2)

The two summands
j=(1,1,\dots,1)
and
j=(2,2,\dots,2)
are the ones we subtract in the statement of the lemma. Hence, we have to estimate all other summands. In the case
j_{0}\neq j_{m}
, we use
{\rm tr}\,AB={\rm tr}\,BA
with
A=\mathds{1}_{I_{j_{0}}}Q
and
B=\prod_{i=1}^{m}Q\mathds{1}_{I_{j_{i}}}
to conclude that the trace vanishes. We are left to estimate the terms where
j_{0}=j_{m}
and there is an
i\in\{1,2,\dots,m-1\}
with
j_{i}\neq j_{0}
. In this case, we consider
i_{-}
and
i_{+}
as the smallest and largest such

(which can be the same). Now, we write

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{I_{j_{0}}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}Q\mathds{1}_{I_{j_{i}}}=\left(\mathds{1}_{I_{j_{0}}}Q\right)^{i_{-}-1}\mathds{1}_{I_{j_{0}}}Q\mathds{1}_{I_{j_{i_{-}}}}A_{i_{-},i_{+}}\mathds{1}_{I_{j_{i_{+}}}}Q\mathds{1}_{I_{j_{0}}}\left(Q\mathds{1}_{I_{j_{0}}}\right)^{m-i_{+}-1}\,,

(C.3)

where

is the identity, if

and a product of some operators

,
\mathds{1}_{I_{1}}
, and
\mathds{1}_{I_{2}}
otherwise. As all of the operators are projections, their operator norm can be bounded by
1
. As we are interested in the trace, we will bound the trace norm. To do so, it suffices to bound two operators in the Hilbert–Schmidt norm and all others in the operator norm. The operators we will bound in Hilbert–Schmidt norm are
\mathds{1}_{I_{j_{0}}}Q\mathds{1}_{I_{j_{i_{-}}}}
and
\mathds{1}_{I_{j_{i_{+}}}}Q\mathds{1}_{I_{j_{0}}}
. These operators are adjoint and hence have the same Hilbert–Schmidt norm. As
j_{i_{-}}\neq j_{0}\neq j_{i_{+}}
, we know
\{j_{0},j_{i_{-}}\}=\{j_{0},j_{i_{+}}\}=\{1,2\}
. Thus, we are left to estimate
\lVert\mathds{1}_{I_{1}}Q\mathds{1}_{I_{2}}\rVert_{2}^{2}
. Since the operator
Q
has integral kernel

, the square of the Hilbert–Schmidt norm can be easily calculated as the square of the integral of this kernel for
x\in I_{1}
and

. By translation invariance we may assume that

. By the definition of

\displaystyle\big|{\rm tr}\,\eqref{C.3}\big|\,\leq\,\lVert\mathds{1}_{I_{1}}Q\mathds{1}_{I_{2}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{I_{1}}\mathrm{d}x\,\int_{I_{2}}\mathrm{d}y\,\frac{1}{(1+y-x)^{2}}

(C.4)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{\ell_{1}}\mathrm{d}x\,\int_{\ell_{1}+d_{1}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y\,\frac{1}{(1+y-x)^{2}}

(C.5)

\displaystyle=C\int_{0}^{\ell_{1}}\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{1}{1+d_{1}+\ell_{1}-x}=C\ln\left(\frac{1+d_{1}+\ell_{1}}{1+d_{1}}\right)\,.

(C.6)

The number of such error terms is

is quite bad, but we only need to be good in

Appendix D
A technical lemma on decaying functions

This section contains a technical lemma that was useful to construct the sequence
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and the region
\Lambda
in the proof of
section 6
.

LEMMA D.1
.

Let
f\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
be bounded and satisfy
\lim_{L\to\infty}f(L)=0
. Then there is a convex, non-increasing function
\mathrm{Env}(f)\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
satisfying
\mathrm{Env}(f)(0)=1,\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathrm{Env}(f)(L)=\lim_{L\to\infty}f(L)/\mathrm{Env}(f)(L)=0
and
\mathrm{Env}(f)(L)\geq C/\sqrt{\ln(2+L)}
for some
C>0
.

Proof.

The conditions on
\mathrm{Env}(f)
only get worse if we increase
f
. Hence, we can replace
f
by

\hat{f}(s)\coloneqq\sup_{t>s}f(t)\,.

(D.1)

This is non-increasing. To achieve the
f(L)/\mathrm{Env}(f)(L)
condition we consider
\sqrt{\hat{f}}
. However, we still need to make sure that
\mathrm{Env}(f)
is convex. For this reason, we need to consider the lower convex envelope
\breve{\sqrt{\hat{f}}}
. It is given by the supremum over all convex functions below
\sqrt{\hat{f}}
. Another way to think of it is that the area above the graph of
\breve{\sqrt{\hat{f}}}
is the convex hull of the area above
\sqrt{\hat{f}}
. Finally, we define

\mathrm{Env}(f)(t)\coloneqq N\left(\breve{\sqrt{\hat{f}}}\left(\frac{t}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ln(2+t)}}\right)\,,\quad t\geq 0\,,

(D.2)

where
N
is a normalization constant to be chosen below. As the lower convex envelope and
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ln(2+t)}}
are convex, so is
\mathrm{Env}(f)
. As the lower convex envelope lies below the function, we have
\mathrm{Env}(f)(t)\leq N\Big(\sqrt{\hat{f}(t/2)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ln(2+t)}}\Big)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. As
\mathrm{Env}(f)
is convex and
\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathrm{Env}(f)(L)=0
,
\mathrm{Env}(f)
is non-increasing. The condition
\mathrm{Env}(f)(L)\geq C/\sqrt{\ln(2+L)}

is trivially satisfied and implies
\mathrm{Env}(f)(0)>0
and hence allows us to choose
N
such that
\mathrm{Env}(f)(0)=1
. We are only left with the condition
\lim_{L\to\infty}f(L)/\mathrm{Env}(f)(L)=0
. To show this, it is sufficient to prove

\mathrm{Env}(f)(L)\geq C\sqrt{f(L)}\,.

(D.3)

By the definition of the convex envelope, for any
t\geq 0
, there are
0\leq t_{1}\leq t<t_{2}
such that

\mathrm{Env}(f)(t)/N=\frac{t_{2}-t}{t_{2}-t_{1}}\sqrt{\hat{f}}\left(\frac{t_{1}}{2}\right)+\frac{t-t_{1}}{t_{2}-t_{1}}\sqrt{\hat{f}}\left(\frac{t_{2}}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ln(2+t)}}\,.

(D.4)

If
t_{2}\leq 2t
, as
\hat{f}
is non-increasing, we get
\sqrt{\hat{f}}\left(\frac{t_{j}}{2}\right)\geq\sqrt{\hat{f}}(t)
for
j=1,2
and thus are finished. If
t_{2}>2t
, then, as
t_{1}\geq 0
, we have

\frac{t_{2}-t}{t_{2}-t_{1}}>\frac{1}{2}
. Thus, it suffices to bound the first summand from below, which we already did.
∎
