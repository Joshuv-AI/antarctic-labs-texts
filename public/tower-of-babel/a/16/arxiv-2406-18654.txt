Topological bound on structure factor
Yugo Onishi, Liang Fu

Abstract
We show that the static structure factor of general many-body systems with U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) symmetry has a lower bound determined only by the ground state Chern number. Our bound relies only on causality and non-negative energy dissipation, and holds for a wide range of two-dimensional gapped systems. We apply our theory to (fractional) Chern insulators, (fractional) quantum spin Hall insulators, topological superconductors, and chiral spin liquids. Our results uncover a universal feature of topological phases beyond the quantized response.



It is well known that topological invariants of ground states often govern quantized physical responses of quantum many-body systems: for example, the many-body Chern number determines the quantized (anomalous) Hall conductivity
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
. Recently, it has been shown
[
8
]
that the ground-state topology not only dictates the quantization of certain observables, but also sets universal bounds on other physical quantities. Specifically, we found that the topology of (fractional) Chern insulators sets an upper bound on the energy gap, which is determined only by the electron density, mass and the many-body Chern number.
This topological bound on the energy gap reveals new information about Chern insulators in general. Interestingly, this bound is found to be saturated for Landau level systems and fairly tight for the zero-field Chern insulator in twisted semiconductor bilayers near the magic angle
[
9
]
.

In this work, we uncover a universal bound on the structure factor that applies to general chiral topological phases with a quantized Hall response. We show that the static structure factor — the
equal-time
density-density correlation function — at long wavelengths has a lower bound solely determined by the many-body Chern number, which governs the Hall response. We discuss applications of our theory to various chiral topological phases, including integer and fractional Chern insulators, topological superconductors, and quantum spin liquids. Our topological bound on structure factor is shown to be useful for Chern insulators in twisted semiconductor bilayers.
It also provides valuable guidance for identifying topological superconductors and quantum spin liquids from the spin structure factor.

Our finding is derived by considering the response of the system to an external
U(1)
gauge field, and relies only on the fundamental principle that the energy absorption is always non-negative; this leads to a nontrivial relation between the quantized Hall response and the longitudinal responses. Further relating the longitudinal response to the structure factor by the dissipation-fluctuation theorem, we find a topological bound on the structure factor.

General theory —
Consider a two-dimensional (2D) system with a global
U(1)
symmetry, with corresponding
U(1)
charge and current densities denoted as
\rho,{\bf\it j}
. For example,
\rho
and
{\bf\it j}
can represent the electric charge and current, or spin density and spin current in systems with spin
U(1)
symmetry.
The static structure factor, i.e., the
equal-time
density-density correlation function, is defined as:

\displaystyle S_{{\bf\it q}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\expectationvalue{\rho_{\vec{q}}{\rho}_{-\vec{q}}}

(1)

with
\rho_{{\bf\it q}}=\int\differential{\vec{r}}e^{-i{\bf\it q}\dotproduct{{\bf\it r}}}\rho({\bf\it r})
the Fourier transform of the charge density and
V
the volume. In our convention,
S_{{\bf\it q}=0}=Vn^{2}
with total charge density
n
. By the dissipation-fluctuation theorem, the static structure factor
S_{{\bf\it q}}
is related to the density response function
\Pi({\bf\it q},\omega)
, which describes the density response to a scalar potential
\phi
,
\delta\rho({\bf\it q},\omega)=\Pi({\bf\it q},\omega)\phi({\bf\it q},\omega)
:

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential\omega\imaginary\Pi({\bf\it q},\omega)=\frac{\pi}{\hbar}S_{{\bf\it q}}.

(2)

By the continuity equation
\partial\rho/\partial t+\nabla\cdot j=0
, the density response function
\Pi
can be further related to the current response to the (longitudinal) “electric” field associated with the potential
\phi
,

{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}=-\nabla\phi
. Denoting the conductivity as
\sigma
,
\Pi
can be written as

\displaystyle\Pi({\bf\it q},\omega)=-i\frac{q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}\sigma_{\alpha\beta}({\bf\it q},\omega)}{\omega}.

(3)

Thus combining Eq. (
2
) and (
3
), we find a relation:

\displaystyle q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{\omega}\frac{\real\sigma_{\alpha\beta}({\bf\it q},\omega)}{\omega}=\frac{\pi}{\hbar}S_{{\bf\it q}}.

(4)

Eq. (
4
) is a sum rule that relates the negative-first moment of optical conductivity and static structure factor.
Note that the left hand side of Eq. (
4
) only involves the real and symmetric part of
\sigma_{\alpha\beta}
, which describes the absorption of linearly polarized light.
In this work, we consider 2D systems with an energy gap, so that optical absorption vanishes at low frequency. This allows us to take
{\bf\it q}\rightarrow 0
limit for
\sigma({\bf\it q},\omega)
and obtain the small-
q
expansion for
S_{{\bf\it q}}
:

\displaystyle S_{{\bf\it q}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{K_{\alpha\beta}}{2\pi}q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}+...,

(5)

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad K_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\hbar\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{\omega}\frac{\real\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)}{\omega}.

(6)

Eq. (
6
) shows that the static structure factor of gapped phases vanishes quadratically at small
{\bf\it q}
. The quadratic coefficient
K
(recently termed quantum weight) characterizes long-wavelength density fluctuation in the ground state. Eq. (
6
) directly relates the quantum weight of a 2D gapped system to the negative-first moment of its optical conductivity
[
10
]
.

We now show that the quantum weight has a universal lower bound determined by the quantized dc Hall conductivity or the many-body Chern number. For convenience of presentation, we shall first consider two-dimensional systems with isotropic conductivity tensor
\sigma_{\alpha\beta}=\sigma_{xx}\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\sigma_{xy}
. General cases will be discussed later. To derive this bound, consider the absorbed power of a 2D system from a monochromatic external field
{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}(t)={\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{0}(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}+\mathrm{c.c.}
, which is given by

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int\differential{t}{\bf\it j}\cdot{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}={\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{0}(\omega)^{*}\dotproduct{\bf\it j}(\omega)+\mathrm{c.c.}

\displaystyle={\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{\dagger}(\sigma(\omega)+\sigma^{\dagger}(\omega)){\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{0},

(7)

with
T=2\pi/\omega
the period of
\mathcal{E}(t)
.
Importantly, the absorbed power
P
is non-negative for arbitrary
\omega
and
{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{0}
.
In particular, for circularly polarized “light” with
x
and
y
components equal in magnitude and
90^{\circ}
out of phase,
{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}_{0}=(1,\pm i)
, the non-negativity of absorbed power
P\geq 0
leads to the following inequality
[
8
]
:

\displaystyle\real\sigma_{xx}(\omega)\geq\absolutevalue{\Im\sigma_{xy}(\omega)},

(8)

which holds at an arbitrary frequency
\omega
.

From Eq. (
8
) we immediately obtain inequalities between all moments of
\real\sigma_{xx}(\omega)
and
\imaginary\sigma_{xy}(\omega)
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{\omega}\frac{\real\sigma_{xx}(\omega)}{\omega^{n}}\geq\absolutevalue{ \int_0^{\infty} \dd{\omega} \frac{\Im\sigma_{xy}(\omega)}{\omega^n} }.

(9)

For gapped systems, since there is no absorption at frequency below the gap, the frequency integral in Eq. (
9
) does not have infrared divergence for any
n
.
Consider the negative-first moments (
n=1
) of
\real\sigma_{xx}
and
\imaginary\sigma_{xy}
: the former is directly related to the quantum weight by Eq. (
6
), while the latter is related to the dc Hall conductivity by the Kramers-Kronig relation due to the causality of the response:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{\omega}\frac{\imaginary\sigma_{xy}(\omega)}{\omega}=\frac{\pi}{2}\sigma_{xy}(0)=\frac{1}{4\hbar}C.

(10)

Here, we have used the fact that the dc Hall conductivity of a 2D gapped system is a topological invariant—the many-body Chern number
C
, which may take integer or fractionally quantized values
[
2
,
3
]
:

\sigma_{xy}=C/h
.

Combining Eq. (
9
) with Eqs. (
10
) and (
6
), we obtain a topological bound
K\equiv K_{xx}+K_{yy}\geq\absolutevalue{C}
,
or equivalently,

\displaystyle S_{{\bf\it q}}\geq\frac{\hbar q^{2}}{2}\absolutevalue{\sigma_{xy}}=\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\absolutevalue{C}\quad({\bf\it q}\to 0).

(11)

Eq. (
11
) applies to general gapped isotropic 2D systems with
U(1)
symmetry, including strongly interacting systems such as fractional Chern insulators. The same result holds for lattice models where
\rho_{{\bf\it q}}=\sum_{i}e^{i{\bf\it q}\dotproduct{\bf\it r}_{i}}\rho_{i}
is defined with the density operator
\rho_{i}
on site
i
.
By the same argument, the following bound on the quadratic coefficient of
S_{{\bf\it q}}
holds for general 2D gapped systems (see Supplemental Materials
1

1

See Supplemental Material for more detailed calculations, which includes Refs.
[
35
,
36
]

):

\displaystyle K_{xx}+K_{yy}\geq\absolutevalue{C}.

(12)

Note that in the thermodynamic limit
V\to\infty

S_{{\bf\it q}}
defined by Eq. (
1
) is proportional to the volume
V
at reciprocal lattice vectors (including
{\bf\it q}=0
), whereas it is an intensive quantity of
\order{1}
at generic
{\bf\it q}

S_{{\bf\it q}}
in

Below, we will apply Eq. (
11

Chern insulators —

Let us first consider Chern insulators, where the
U(1)
symmetry corresponds to
electric charge conservation. In this case,
\rho
and
{\bf\it j}
correspond to the electron number density and current respectively.
The electric conductivity
\sigma^{e}

\sigma^{e}=e^{2}\sigma

For noninteracting insulators, the inequality (

\displaystyle S_{{\bf\it q}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\differential^{d}{{\bf\it k}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\Tr[P({\bf\it k})(P({\bf\it k})-P({\bf\it k}+{\bf\it q}))]

(13)

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}{2\pi}\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\differential^{2}{{\bf\it k}}}{2\pi}g_{\alpha\beta}({\bf\it k})\quad({\bf\it q}\to 0),

the cell-periodic Bloch wavefunction for

g_{\mu\nu}({\bf\it k})=\real Q_{\mu\nu}({\bf\it k})

the quantum geometric tensor of the occupied Bloch bands.
Eq. (
14

and the optical response

Quantum spin Hall insulators
— The topological bound for Chern insulators can be straightforwardly extended to quantum spin Hall insulators. In time-reversal invariant insulators with
s^{z}
conservation,
we can define the spin Chern number by introducing the electric field
{\bf\it E}_{\uparrow}
that is coupled only to spin-
\uparrow
particles. The Hall current
{\bf\it j}
induced by
{\bf\it E}_{\uparrow}
defines the corresponding conductivity and Chern number:
j_{y}=\sigma_{yx}E_{\uparrow,x}
,
\sigma_{yx}=C_{\uparrow}e^{2}/h
. Similarly, the Chern number for spin-
\downarrow
particles
C_{\downarrow}
is defined, and the time-reversal symmetry dictates
C_{\uparrow}=-C_{\downarrow}\equiv C_{s}
with
C_{s}
the spin Chern number. For each spin sector, the structure factor
S^{\sigma}_{{\bf\it q}}
(
\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow
) satisfies the topological bound (
11
). In noninteracting cases, the charge static structure factor is given by the sum of the ones for each spin sector and thus is bounded by
2\absolutevalue{C_s}
:
S_{{\bf\it q}}\geq q^{2}\absolutevalue{C_s}/(2\pi)
.

Spin
U(1)
symmetry
—We can also apply our general theory to systems with spin
U(1)
symmetry.
When the total spin along

is conserved, we can relate
\rho

{\bf\it j}
to the spin density
s^{z}({\bf\it r})
and the spin current

s^{z}({\bf\it r})\equiv s_{0}\rho({\bf\it r})

and

{\bf\it j}^{s}({\bf\it r})\equiv s_{0}{\bf\it j}({\bf\it r})
. Here,
s_{0}
has a dimension of spin angular momentum. The potential
\phi
in this case corresponds to the Zeeman field,
-s_{0}B_{z}({\bf\it r})
, which couples to the spin density as
\rho\phi=-B_{z}s^{z}
. The corresponding “electric” field is the gradient of the magnetic field,
{\bf\it\mathcal{E}}=s_{0}\nabla B_{z}
. By defining the spin conductivity as
{\bf\it j}^{s}=\sigma^{s}\nabla B_{z}
, the conductivity in our general theory
\sigma
is related to
\sigma^{s}
as
\sigma^{s}=s_{0}^{2}\sigma
, while the static structure factor is related to the spin static structure factor
S^{s}_{{\bf\it q}}\equiv\expectationvalue{\hat{s}^z_{\vec{q}}\hat{s}^z_{-\vec{q}}}/V
as
S^{s}_{{\bf\it q}}=s_{0}^{2}S_{{\bf\it q}}
.

For systems with a spin gap, we can apply our general theory to obtain a bound on the spin static structure factor in terms of the Hall conductivity for spin:

\displaystyle S^{s}_{{\bf\it q}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\hbar q^{2}}{2}\absolutevalue{\sigma^s_{xy}}=\frac{q^{2}s_{0}^{2}}{4\pi}\absolutevalue{C}\quad({\bf\it q}\to 0).

(18)

Chiral topological superconductors —

For superconductors with a full superconducting gap and spin

As an example, we consider a superconductor with

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf\it k}}\begin{pmatrix}{c}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}\uparrow}&{c}_{-{\bf\it k}\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}H({\bf\it k})\begin{pmatrix}{c}_{{\bf\it k}\uparrow}\\

As a mean-field Hamiltonian for superconductors,

Since the Hamiltonian conserves

Chiral spin liquids
— Our theory also applies to chiral spin liquids with spin

symmetry

The

Formally, this mapping is achieved by rewriting the spin operator

The

Since one boson carries spin of

\displaystyle S^{s}_{{\bf\it q}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4\pi}q^{2}\absolutevalue{C}\quad({\bf\it q}\to 0)

This analysis is readily generalized to spin-

Discussion
— While our work focuses on the topological bound on the structure factor which is a ground state property, it is also closely related to the topological bound on the energy gap shown recently
[

. In fact, the upper bound on the energy gap
\Delta
can be obtained from our lower bound on the structure factor by using
the Feynmann-Bijl formula
[
34
]
:

\displaystyle\Delta\leq\frac{\expectationvalue{\comm{\rho_{\vec{q}}}{\comm{H}{\rho_{-\vec{q}}}}}}{2VS_{{\bf\it q}}},

(21)

where
\expectationvalue{\dots}
denotes the expectation value in the ground state.
The derivation of Eq. (
21
) for general
U(1)
symmetric systems is given in Supplemental Materials
[
11
]
. Using the continuity equation
\dot{\rho}_{{\bf\it q}}+i{\bf\it q}\dotproduct{\bf\it j}_{{\bf\it q}}=0
and the relation
{\bf\it j}_{{\bf\it q}}=-\partialderivative*{H}{\vec{A}_{-\vec{q}}}
, we can rewrite Eq. (
21
) as

\displaystyle\Delta\leq\frac{\hbar^{2}q_{\mu}q_{\nu}}{2VS_{{\bf\it q}}}\expectationvalue{\pdv[2]{H}{A^{\mu}_{\vec{q}}}{A^{\nu}_{-\vec{q}}}},

(22)

By taking
{\bf\it q}\to 0
limit and applying the topological bound on the structure factor (
11
), we find

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\pi\hbar^{2}}{K}\expectationvalue{\frac{1}{V}\pdv[2]{H}{\vec{A}}}\leq\frac{\pi\hbar^{2}}{\absolutevalue{C}}\expectationvalue{\frac{1}{V}\pdv[2]{H}{\vec{A}}},

(23)

where
{\bf\it A}
is the uniform
U(1)
vector potential.
Eq. (
23
) is the topological gap bound that applies to general topological phases with a finite Chern number. In particular, when the Hamiltonian is quadratic in the momentum
{\bf\it p}
of the particles,
H=\sum_{i}({\bf\it p}_{i}-{\bf\it A})^{2}/(2m)+\dots
,
Eq. (
23
) reduces to the previously derived topological gap bound for Chern insulators:
\Delta\leq 2\pi\hbar^{2}n/(m\absolutevalue{C})
with the charge density
n
. It is worth noting that Eq. (
23
) also applies to systems with spin-
U(1)
symmetry as we discussed in this work. For
s_{z}
-conserving superconductors, the gap
\Delta
is the spin gap, i.e., the energy of spin excitation at long wavelength
{\bf\it q}\rightarrow 0
. The upper bound on the spin gap of topological superconductors also gives an upper bound on the superconducting gap.

Since the structure factor represents density fluctuations, the existence of a lower bound shows that nontrivial topology necessarily requires quantum fluctuation in the
U(1)
charge density.
The static structure factor is a physical observable for all systems with
U(1)
symmetry and can be measured by X-ray or neutron scattering, or determined by the optical conductivity measurement through the sum rule (
4
). Since our bound applies to interacting systems, it will be interesting to investigate fractional Chern insulators in twisted semiconductor bilayers and multilayer graphene/h-BN numerically and experimentally.

Our topological bound on the structure factor may be useful in identifying topological superconductors and quantum spin liquids in experiments. If the spin structure factor is smaller than the lower bound (
20
) determined by a Chern number
C
, we can rule out the possibility of a chiral spin liquid with the Chern number
C
. A large spin structure factor at small
{\bf\it q}
is a necessary criterion for a system to be considered as a
U(1)
chiral quantum spin liquid candidate.

While this work focuses on the bound in topological phases characterized by the Chern number, an interesting research direction is to find the topological bound in other topological phases of matter. We also note that the generalization to three-dimensional systems with Coulomb interaction is nontrivial because the density response
\Pi
and the optical conductivity
\sigma
at
{\bf\it q}\to 0
are not related as Eq. (
3
), as discussed in details in Ref.
[
10
]
.

In conclusion, we have proved a topological bound on static structure factor in general systems with
U(1)
symmetry. Our derivation relies only on basic physical principles and the bound applies to a wide range of two-dimensional gapped systems, such as integer or fractional Chern insulators, topological superconductors, and quantum spin liquid, revealing universal information about correlation function obtained from topology.

Appendix D
Quantum geometry in
U(1)
symmetric systems

We consider ground states with degeneracy
r
in the
U(1)
symmetric system. Under twisted boundary condition, the ground states satisfies

\displaystyle\Psi_{a{\bf\it\kappa}}({\bf\it r}_{1},\dots,{\bf\it r}_{i}+{\bf\it L}_{\mu},\dots,{\bf\it r}_{N})

\displaystyle=e^{i{\bf\it\kappa}\dotproduct{\bf\it L}_{\mu}}\Psi_{a{\bf\it\kappa}}({\bf\it r}_{1},\dots,{\bf\it r}_{i},\dots,{\bf\it r}_{N}),

(47)

where
\Psi_{a{\bf\it\kappa}}
is the
a
-th ground state wavefunction in the first quantization representation, and
{\bf\it r}_{i}
specifies the position of
i
-th particle corresponding to the conserved
U(1)
charge.
{\bf\it L}_{\mu}=(0,\dots,L_{\mu},\dots,0)
specifies the system size in
\mu
-direction
L_{\mu}
, and the vector
{\bf\it\kappa}
specifies the twisted boundary condition. Note that the number of particle is a good quantum number due to the global
U(1)
symmetry.

Introducing the twisted boundary condition
{\bf\it\kappa}
is equivalent to coupling the system to a uniform
U(1)
gauge field,
{\bf\it A}={\bf\it\kappa}
. Therefore, the current response to the gauge field can be described by the quantum geometric quantity associated with the twisted boundary condition
{\bf\it\kappa}
, and the corresponding conductivity for the
U(1)
charge with the twisted boundary condition
{\bf\it\kappa}

\sigma(\omega;\kappa)
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\omega;\kappa)=\frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_{n,m}\frac{-iE_{nm}A^{\mu}_{nm}A^{\nu}_{mn}f_{nm}}{\hbar\omega+E_{nm}+i\delta},

(48)

where
E_{nm}=E_{n}-E_{m}
is the energy difference between
n
-th and
m
-th many body eigenstate, and
\partial_{\mu}
is the derivative with respect to
\kappa_{\mu}
.
f_{nm}=f_{n}-f_{m}
with the probability
f_{n}
that
n
-th eigenstate is realized. At zero temperature, the canonical distribution gives
f_{n}=1/r
when the state
n
is one of the
r
-fold degenerated ground states and otherwise
f_{n}=0
.

A^{\mu}_{nm}=\matrixelement{n,\vec{\kappa}}{i\partial_{\mu}}{m,\vec{\kappa}}
is the interband Berry connection for the
n
-th and
m
-th eigenstates the interacting system under the boundary condition
{\bf\it\kappa}
.

Further we introduce the quantum geometric tensor
Q^{\mu\nu}
for the many-body ground states as

\displaystyle Q^{\mu\nu}_{ab}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{\partial_{\mu}\Psi_{a\vec{\kappa}}}{(1-P_{\vec{\kappa}})}{\partial_{\nu}\Psi_{b\vec{\kappa}}}.

(49)

where
\partial_{\mu}
refers to the derivative with respect to
\kappa_{\mu}
, and
P_{{\bf\it\kappa}}
is the projection operator onto the ground state subspace for the boundary condition
{\bf\it\kappa}
.

Q^{\mu\nu}
is in general an
r\times r
matrix and non-Abelian quantum geometric tensor. The symmetric and antisymmetric components of
Q^{\mu\nu}
define the quantum metric and Berry curvature for the many-body states
\ket{\Psi_{\vec{\kappa}}}
respectively:
Q^{\mu\nu}=G^{\mu\nu}-iF^{\mu\nu}/2
. The trace of them gives the Abelian quantum geometry for the entire ground state subspace:
g^{\mu\nu}_{\rm tot}\equiv\Tr G^{\mu\nu},\Omega^{\mu\nu}_{\rm tot}\equiv\Tr F^{\mu\nu}
.
If the ground state is unique, the quantum geometric tensor reduces to the Abelian quantum geometric tensor.

Since the relation between the conductivity
\sigma(\omega)
and the Berry connection
A^{\mu}
is identical to the electric conductivity
[
8
]
, we can readily show the sum rule for the negative-first moment:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{\omega}\frac{\real\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\omega)}{\omega}=\frac{1}{2\hbar}g_{\mu\nu}

(50)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{\omega}\frac{\imaginary\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\omega)}{\omega}=\frac{1}{4\hbar}\Omega_{\mu\nu}

(51)

where
g_{\mu\nu}
,
\Omega_{\mu\nu}
is the quantum metric and the Chern number for the ground state(s) defined as

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\Tr G^{\mu\nu},

(52)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\Tr F^{\mu\nu}.

(53)

If the ground state is unique (
r=1
),
g_{\mu\nu}
and
\Omega_{\mu\nu}
reduces to the Abelian quantum geometric tensor.

If we further assume that the bulk conductivity
\sigma(\omega)
does not depend on the boundary condition, we can replace
g_{\mu\nu}
and
\Omega^{\mu\nu}
with the averaged ones over
{\bf\it\kappa}
, and in particular
\Omega_{\mu\nu}
is replaced by
C_{\rm tot}/r
with the Chern number of the ground state subspace
C_{\rm tot}
.

With the sum rules (
50
), (
51
), we can repeat the same discussion in Ref.
[
10
]
to show the relation between the structure factor or the quantum weight
K
and the quantum metric for 2D systems as

\displaystyle K=\int\frac{\differential^{2}{{\bf\it\kappa}}}{2\pi}(g_{xx}+g_{yy}),

(54)

and this is the generalization of Eq. (13) in the main text to many-body systems with
U(1)
symmetry.
From an inequality
g_{xx}+g_{yy}\geq\absolutevalue{\Omega_{xy}}
, we can show the bound on the quantum weight
K\geq\absolutevalue{C}
and the topological bound (11) in the main text.

Appendix E
Topological bound in
d+id
topological superconductors

As an example of a topological superconductor, consider a
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}+id_{xy}
superconductor in two dimensions

[
35
]
. A model for such a state on a lattice is described by the following mean-field Hamiltonian
[
36
]
:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf\it k}}\begin{pmatrix}{c}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}\uparrow}&{c}_{-{\bf\it k}\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon({\bf\it k})&\Delta({\bf\it k})\\
\Delta(-{\bf\it k})^{*}&-\varepsilon({\bf\it k})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{c}_{{\bf\it k}\uparrow}\\
{c}^{\dagger}_{-{\bf\it k}\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{{\bf\it k}}{\Psi}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}\quantity(\vec{h}(\vec{k})\vdot\vec{\sigma}){\Psi}_{{\bf\it k}}.

(55)

\varepsilon({\bf\it k})
is the single particle dispersion and here we take
\varepsilon({\bf\it k})=\varepsilon_{0}(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y})
, and
\Delta({\bf\it k})=\Delta_{0}(\cos k_{x}-\cos k_{y})-i\Delta_{xy}\sin k_{x}\sin k_{y}
is the pairing potential. As in Eq. (18), we have a topological bound for the
U(1)
charge
{\Psi}^{\dagger}{\Psi}
and hence for the spin structure factor with
s_{0}=\hbar/2
.

This system is in a topological phase with the quantized spin Hall conductance determined by Chern number when
\Delta_{xy}\neq 0

[
36
]
. This can be understood with the Dirac dispersion at four points,
{\bf\it k}=(\pm\pi/2,\pm\pi/2)
. When
\Delta_{xy}=0
, the quasiparticle excitation at these points has gapless Dirac dispersion, but once
\Delta_{xy}
becomes finite, these Dirac points are gapped. The Chern number is the sum of the contributions from each Dirac point and is
C=2~\mathrm{sgn}(\Delta_{xy})
. The Chern number can be also directly calculated by

C=(1/4\pi)\int\differential^{2}{{\bf\it k}}~{\bf\it n}\dotproduct\quantity(\pdv*{\vec{n}}{k_x}\cross\pdv*{\vec{n}}{k_y})

We can verify our inequality (17) by explicitly calculating the spin static structure factor with the Fourier transform of the spin density operator
s_{{\bf\it q}}^{z}=(\hbar/2)\sum_{k}({c}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}\uparrow}{c}_{{\bf\it k}+{\bf\it q}\uparrow}-{c}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}\downarrow}{c}_{{\bf\it k}+{\bf\it q}\downarrow})=(\hbar/2)\sum_{{\bf\it k}}{\Psi}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}{\Psi}_{{\bf\it k}+{\bf\it q}}
. The leading order in

of the spin static structure factor is given by

with

K=(1/8\pi)\int\differential^{2}{{\bf\it k}}~\sum_{\alpha=x,y}\quantity(\pdv*{\vec{n}}{k_\alpha})^{2}
.
From a mathematical inequality
(\partialderivative*{\vec{n}}{k_x})^{2}+(\partialderivative*{\vec{n}}{k_y})^{2}\geq 2\absolutevalue{\qty(\pdv*{\vec{n}}{k_x})\cross\qty(\pdv*{\vec{n}}{k_y})}
, one can explicitly confirm the inequality (17) holds.

Appendix G
Feynmann-Bijl formula for general systems with
U(1)
symmetry

Here we derive the Feynman-Bijl formula for general
U(1)
symmetric systems. To find an upper bound on the energy gap, we consider constructing an ansatz for an excited state
\ket{ex}
so that
\ket{ex}
is orthogonal to the ground state
\ket{gs}
. As long as
\ket{ex}
and
\ket{gs}
are orthogonal to each other, the energy expectation value of
\ket{ex}
is always larger than or equal to the first excited state. Therefore, we can find an upper bound on the energy gap by evaluating the energy difference between
\ket{ex}
and
\ket{gs}
. We choose the following state as
\ket{ex}
:

\displaystyle\ket{ex}

\displaystyle=(\rho_{-{\bf\it q}}-\expectationvalue{\rho_{-\vec{q}}}_{gs})\ket{gs},

(63)

where
\expectationvalue{\dots}_{gs}
is the expectation value in the ground state
\ket{gs}
and
\rho_{{\bf\it q}}
is the
U(1)
charge density. The orthogonality
\innerproduct{ex}{gs}=0
is easily confirmed. Now, the energy gap
\Delta
has an upper bound:
\Delta\leq\matrixelement{ex}{H}{ex}/\innerproduct{ex}{ex}-\expectationvalue{H}_{gs}
. This can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\Delta\leq\frac{\expectationvalue{\comm{\rho_{\vec{q}}}{\comm{H}{\rho_{-\vec{q}}}}}_{gs}}{2VS_{{\bf\it q}}},

(64)

with the structure factor
S_{{\bf\it q}}
for the
U(1)
charge in the ground state.

For electronic systems,
U(1)
charge
\rho
can represent the electric charge and the Hamiltonian
H
is quadratic in the momentum
{\bf\it p}
with kinetic energy
{\bf\it p}^{2}/(2m)
for each electron. Using a relation from the continuity equation,
\commutator{H}{\rho_{-\vec{q}}}=-\hbar{\bf\it q}\dotproduct{\bf\it j}_{-{\bf\it q}}
, and the commutation relation
\commutator{\rho_{\vec{q}}}{\vec{j}_{-\vec{q}}}=\hbar{\bf\it q}N/m
with the number of electron
N
and the mass
m
, the numerator is reduced to a simple form and the gap bound is obtained as

\displaystyle\Delta\leq\frac{\hbar^{2}{\bf\it q}^{2}n}{2mS_{{\bf\it q}}}.

(65)

This is the Feynman-Bijl formula used in Ref.
[
34
]
. The same formula as (
64
), but with the projected density operator
\bar{\rho}_{{\bf\it q}}
and the projected Hamiltonian
\bar{H}
instead of the full density operator
\rho_{{\bf\it q}}
and the full Hamiltonian
H
, is used in a study of magneto-roton excitations in fractional quantum Hall systems
[
23
]
.

Appendix H
Structure factor of noninteracting electronic systems

The static structure factor of noninteracting electronic systems (Eq. (12) in the main text) was calculated in, for example, Ref.
[
10
]
. However, we derive the expression here for the sake of completeness.
Writing the cell-periodic Bloch wavefunction for
n
th band as
\ket{u_{n\vec{k}}}
with the wavevector
{\bf\it k}
, the number density operator with wavevector
{\bf\it q}
is given by

\displaystyle n_{{\bf\it q}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\sum_{{\bf\it k}}\innerproduct{u_{n,\vec{k}}}{u_{m,\vec{k}+\vec{q}}}{c}^{\dagger}_{n{{\bf\it k}}}{c}_{m,{\bf\it k}+{\bf\it q}},

(66)

with
{c}_{n,{\bf\it k}}
the annihilation (creation) operator of electron in
n
th band with wavevector
{\bf\it k}
. Then the static structure factor for finite
{\bf\it q}
is given by

\displaystyle S^{\prime}_{{\bf\it q}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{n,m,{\bf\it k}}\sum_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime},{\bf\it k}^{\prime}}\innerproduct{u_{n,\vec{k}}}{u_{m,\vec{k}+\vec{q}}}\innerproduct{u_{n',\vec{k}'}}{u_{m',\vec{k}'+\vec{q}'}}\quantity(\expval{\cre{c}_{n{\vec{k}}}\ani{c}_{m,\vec{k}+\vec{q}} \cre{c}_{n'{\vec{k}'}}\ani{c}_{m',\vec{k}'+\vec{q}'}}-\expval{\cre{c}_{n{\vec{k}}}\ani{c}_{m,\vec{k}+\vec{q}}}\expval{\cre{c}_{n'{\vec{k}'}}\ani{c}_{m',\vec{k}'+\vec{q}'}})

(67)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{n,m,{\bf\it k}}\innerproduct{u_{n,\vec{k}}}{u_{m,\vec{k}+\vec{q}}}\innerproduct{u_{m,\vec{k}+\vec{q}}}{u_{n,\vec{k}}}(1-f_{m}({\bf\it k}+{\bf\it q}))f_{n}({\bf\it k})

(68)

where
f_{n}({\bf\it k})=\expectationvalue{\cre{c}_{n\vec{k}}\ani{c}_{n\vec{k}}}
is the occupation number of
n
th band at wavevector
{\bf\it k}
. Defining the projection operator
P({\bf\it k})=\sum_{n}^{\rm occ}\ket{u_{n\vec{k}}}f_{n}({\bf\it k})\bra{u_{n\vec{k}}}
, we obtain an expression for
S^{\prime}_{{\bf\it q}}
for a general noninteracting system as

\displaystyle S^{\prime}_{{\bf\it q}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{{\bf\it k}}\Tr[P({\bf\it k})(1-P({\bf\it k}+{\bf\it q}))]=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{{\bf\it k}}\Tr[P({\bf\it k})(P({\bf\it k})-P({\bf\it k}+{\bf\it q}))]

(69)

where we have used
P({\bf\it k})^{2}=P({\bf\it k})
in the last equality. Further rewriting the summation over
{\bf\it k}
with integral, we obtain:

\displaystyle S^{\prime}_{{\bf\it q}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\differential^{d}{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\Tr[P({\bf\it k})(P({\bf\it k})-P({\bf\it k}+{\bf\it q}))].

(70)

In particular, for band insulators, the projection operator becomes
P({\bf\it k})=\sum_{n}^{\rm occ}\ket{u_{n\vec{k}}}\bra{u_{n\vec{k}}}
and Eq. (
70
) reduces to Eq. (
60
) above. Noting that
S^{\prime}_{{\bf\it q}}=S_{{\bf\it q}}
except when
{\bf\it q}
coincides a reciprocal lattice vector in lattice systems, Eq. (12) in the main text also follows from Eq. (
70
).
