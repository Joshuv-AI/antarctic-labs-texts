Fast optimization of parametrized quantum optical circuits
Filippo M. Miatto, Nicolás Quesada

Abstract
Parametrized quantum optical circuits are a class of quantum circuits in which the carriers of quantum information are photons and the gates are optical transformations. Classically optimizing these circuits is challenging due to the infinite dimensionality of the photon number vector space that is associated to each optical mode. Truncating the space dimension is unavoidable, and it can lead to incorrect results if the gates populate photon number states beyond the cutoff. To tackle this issue, we present an algorithm that is orders of magnitude faster than the current state of the art, to recursively compute the exact matrix elements of Gaussian operators and their gradient with respect to a parametrization. These operators, when augmented with a non-Gaussian transformation such as the Kerr gate, achieve universal quantum computation. Our approach brings two advantages: first, by computing the matrix elements of Gaussian operators directly, we don’t need to construct them by combining several other operators; second, we can use any variant of the gradient descent algorithm by plugging our gradients into an automatic differentiation framework such as TensorFlow or PyTorch. Our results will find applications in quantum optical hardware research, quantum machine learning, optical data processing, device discovery and device design.

Optimizing a parametrized quantum optical circuit is a challenging task, because one needs to simulate exactly the evolution of an input state as it propagates through the optical components, as well as the gradient of the output with respect to the circuit parameters.
As each component can be represented in matrix form, the transformation of the initial state is a simple matrix multiplication and as such it is efficient.
However, the difficult part is to generate the transformation matrix from the parameters of the gate: optical transformations (even the fundamental ones) are expressed as matrix exponentials of a linear combination of infinite-dimensional non-commuting operators

2
The generating function method

2.1
Notation and definitions

We summarize here our notational conventions, which are meant to simplify the presence of multiple indices in the multimode formulas, and the definition of our generating function.

We adopt the following short-hand notation:

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}^{\bm{n}}

\displaystyle\equiv\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\beta_{i}^{n_{i}},

(2)

\displaystyle\bm{n}!

\displaystyle\equiv\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}n_{i}!.

(3)

We indicate tensor products of
\ell
-mode coherent and Fock states as follows:

\displaystyle|\bm{\beta}\rangle

\displaystyle=|\beta_{1}\rangle\otimes\ldots\otimes|\beta_{\ell}\rangle,

(4)

\displaystyle|\bm{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=|n_{1}\rangle\otimes\ldots\otimes|n_{\ell}\rangle,

(5)

which we can use for instance to express a multimode coherent state:

\displaystyle|\bm{\beta}\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{2}||\bm{\beta}||^{2}}\sum_{\bm{n}=\bm{0}}^{\bm{\infty}}\frac{\bm{\beta}^{\bm{n}}}{\sqrt{\bm{n}!}}|\bm{n}\rangle,

(6)

where we used the shorthand notation
\sum_{\bm{n}=\bm{0}}^{\bm{\infty}}=\sum_{n_{1}=0}^{\infty}\ldots\sum_{n_{\ell}=0}^{\infty}
.
Note that tensor indices are numbered starting from 1 (as in Eq. (
2
)) while the indices themselves have values that begin from 0 (as in Eq. (
6
)).
We also use interchangeably the following two notations for derivatives, depending on convenience and on the available space:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{\bm{k}}}{\partial{\bm{\beta}}^{\bm{k}}}=\partial_{\bm{\beta}}^{\bm{k}}\equiv\prod_{i}\frac{\partial^{k_{i}}}{\partial{\beta_{i}}^{k_{i}}}.

(7)

The key insight of the generating function method is that when we compute the inner product
\langle\bm{\alpha}^{*}|\mathcal{G}|\bm{\beta}\rangle
of any operator

\mathcal{G}
(not just a Gaussian one) with a pair of coherent states, we obtain a function of the coherent amplitudes that can be treated as a generating function for the matrix elements of
\mathcal{G}
:

\displaystyle\Gamma(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{1}{2}(||\bm{\alpha}||^{2}+||\bm{\beta}||^{2})}\langle\bm{\alpha}^{*}|\mathcal{G}|\bm{\beta}\rangle

(8)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{m},\bm{n}=\bm{0}}^{\bm{\infty}}\frac{\bm{\alpha}^{\bm{m}}\bm{\beta}^{\bm{n}}}{\sqrt{\bm{m}!\bm{n}!}}\langle\bm{m}|\mathcal{G}|\bm{n}\rangle.

(9)

The matrix element
\langle\bm{m}|\mathcal{G}|\bm{n}\rangle
is therefore contained in the coefficient of the factor
\bm{\alpha}^{\bm{m}}\bm{\beta}^{\bm{n}}
in the Taylor series and we can isolate it by computing the derivatives of the appropriate order, at zero:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\bm{m}\bm{n}}\equiv\langle\bm{m}|\mathcal{G}|\bm{n}\rangle=\frac{\partial^{\bm{m}}_{\bm{\alpha}}\partial^{\bm{n}}_{\bm{\beta}}\Gamma(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})}{\sqrt{\bm{m}!\bm{n}!}}\bigg|_{\bm{\alpha}=\bm{\beta}=0}.

(10)

We will derive recursion relations for
\mathcal{G}_{\bm{mn}}
directly, instead of calculating the numerator and denominator separately in the ratio in the last equation. Thus our methods are numerically more stable than previous ones where divisions by factorials are common.

We now write the amplitudes and Fock indices as direct sums of the bra and ket labels

\displaystyle\bm{\nu}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{\alpha}\\
\bm{\beta}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{C}^{2\ell},

\displaystyle\bm{k}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{m}\\
\bm{n}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2\ell},

so that we can express all of our calculations in terms of a single vector of amplitudes, including the generating function, which we now indicate as

\displaystyle\Gamma(\bm{\nu})=\sum_{\bm{k}=\bm{0}}^{\bm{\infty}}\frac{\bm{\nu}^{\bm{k}}}{\sqrt{\bm{k}!}}\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}.

(15)

where we take advantage of the definition of the vector
\bm{k}
in Eq. (
2.1
) and write
\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}
for
\mathcal{G}_{\bm{m}\bm{n}}
.
As shown in Appendix
A
, the generating function
\Gamma(\bm{\nu})
for a Gaussian operator
\mathcal{G}
is in exponential form
\Gamma(\bm{\nu})=C\,e^{Q(\bm{\nu})}
. This implies that we can express high-order derivatives recursively, using only the derivatives at zero of the exponent
Q(\bm{\nu})
, as shown in subsection
2.3
. Furthermore, the exponent
Q(\bm{\nu})
is a polynomial of degree 2, which we can write as:

\displaystyle Q(\bm{\nu})=\bm{\mu}^{T}\bm{\nu}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\nu}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\nu}.

(16)

This means that all of its mixed derivatives of order higher than 2 vanish:

\displaystyle\partial^{\bm{k}}_{\bm{\nu}}Q(\bm{\nu})|_{\bm{\nu}=\bm{0}}=0\text{ if }\sum_{i=1}^{2\ell}k_{i}>2.

(17)

Because of this, all the derivatives of the exponent that we will ever need are those of degree 1 and 2:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu_{i}}Q(\bm{\nu})|_{\bm{\nu}=\bm{0}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{i},

(18)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\nu_{i}\partial\nu_{j}}Q(\bm{\nu})|_{\bm{\nu}=\bm{0}}

\displaystyle=-\Sigma_{ij}.

(19)

Finally, note that although
\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}
is a tensor of rank
2\ell
, for familiarity with the terminology we still refer to its elements as “matrix elements”.

2.2
Generating function for multimode Gaussian transformations

As a corollary of the Bloch-Messiah decomposition
[
44
,
23
]
, a general
\ell-
mode Gaussian transformation can be parametrized as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}(\bm{\gamma},\bm{W},\bm{\zeta},\bm{V})=\mathcal{D}(\bm{\gamma})\mathcal{U}(\bm{W})\mathcal{S}(\bm{\zeta})\mathcal{U}(\bm{V}),

(20)

where we used the notation
\mathcal{D}(\bm{\gamma})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{\ell}D_{i}(\gamma_{i})
,
\mathcal{S}(\bm{\zeta})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{\ell}S_{i}(\zeta_{i})
for tensor products of single-mode displacement and squeezing operators defined by

\displaystyle D_{j}(\gamma_{j})

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\gamma_{j}a^{\dagger}_{j}-\text{H.c.}\right),\quad\gamma_{j}\in\mathbb{C},

(21)

\displaystyle S_{j}(\zeta_{j})

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\tfrac{\zeta_{j}^{*}}{2}a_{j}^{2}-\text{H.c.}\right),\quad\zeta_{j}=r_{j}e^{i\delta_{j}}\in\mathbb{C},

(22)

where
r_{j}
and
\delta_{j}
are the phase and modulus of the complex squeezing parameter
\zeta_{j}
.

In the last equation we used the annihilation
a_{j}
and creation
a_{j}^{\dagger}
operators of the
\ell
modes satisfying the canonical commutation relations

\displaystyle[a_{j},a_{l}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{j,l},\quad[a_{j},a_{l}]=[a^{\dagger}_{j},a^{\dagger}_{l}]=0.

(23)

The Hilbert space operator
\mathcal{U}(\bm{V})
represents a general multimode passive transformation (physically corresponding to an interferometer)
and is parametrized by a unitary matrix
\bm{V}
that transforms the creation operators as

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}(\bm{V})a^{\dagger}_{i}\mathcal{U}(\bm{V})=\sum_{l=1}^{\ell}V^{*}_{il}a_{l}^{\dagger},\quad\mathcal{U}(\bm{V})a^{\dagger}_{l}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}(\bm{V})=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}V_{il}a_{i}^{\dagger}.

(24)

Using these definitions, in Appendix
A
we show the following:

\displaystyle e^{\frac{1}{2}\left[||\bm{\alpha}||^{2}+||\bm{\beta}||^{2}\right]}\langle\bm{\alpha}^{*}|\mathcal{D}(\bm{\gamma})\mathcal{U}(\bm{W})\mathcal{S}(\bm{\zeta})\mathcal{U}(\bm{V})|\bm{\beta}\rangle=

\displaystyle\quad C\exp\left(\bm{\mu}^{T}\bm{\nu}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\nu}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\nu}\right),

(25)

where

\displaystyle C=\frac{\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\left[||\bm{\gamma}||^{2}+\bm{\gamma}^{\dagger}\bm{W}\text{diag}(e^{i\bm{\delta}}\tanh\bm{r})\bm{W}^{T}\bm{\gamma}^{*}\right]\right)}{\sqrt{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\cosh r_{i}}},

(26)

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}^{T}=

(27)

\displaystyle\left[\bm{\gamma}^{\dagger}\bm{W}\text{diag}(e^{i\bm{\delta}}\tanh\bm{r})\bm{W}^{T}+\bm{\gamma}^{T},-\bm{\gamma}^{\dagger}\bm{W}\text{diag}(\sech\bm{r})\bm{V}\right],

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}=

(28)

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\bm{W}\text{diag}(e^{i\bm{\delta}}\tanh\bm{r})\bm{W}^{T}&-\bm{W}\text{diag}(\sech\bm{r})\bm{V}\\
\hline\cr-\bm{V}^{T}\text{diag}(\sech\bm{r})\bm{W^{T}}&-\bm{V}^{T}\text{diag}(e^{-i\bm{\delta}}\tanh\bm{r})\bm{V}\end{array}\right].

Note that
\bm{\mu}
is a vector of dimension
2\ell
. The matrix
\Sigma
, on the other hand is built out of four
\ell\times\ell
blocks and has therefore dimension
2\ell\times 2\ell
.

With these results we are now ready to write explicit recurrence relations for the matrix elements of a general multimode Gaussian gate.

2.3
From generating functions to matrix elements

We use the recent result in
[
45
]
to express the matrix elements of the Gaussian operator recursively (see Appendix
B
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\bm{0}}

\displaystyle=C,

(31)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}+1_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{i}+1}}\sum_{\bm{j}=\bm{0}}^{\bm{k}}\sqrt{\frac{\bm{k}!}{\bm{j}!}}\frac{1}{(\bm{k}-\bm{j})!}\mathcal{G}_{\bm{j}}\partial_{\bm{\nu}}^{\bm{k}+1_{i}-\bm{j}}Q(\bm{\nu})|_{\bm{\nu}=\bm{0}},

(32)

where
1_{i}
is a vector with all zeros and a single 1 at position
i
, and therefore to obtain the vector
\bm{k}+1_{i}
we take the vector
\bm{k}
and we increment
k_{i}
by 1.

We stress that Eq. (
32
) works for
any
generating function in exponential form. However, as mentioned above, in our case the exponent
Q
is a polynomial of degree 2, so the recurrence relation simplifies considerably because the derivative of
Q
is zero unless
\bm{j}=\bm{k}
or
\bm{j}=\bm{k}-1_{l}
for some index
l
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}+1_{i}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{i}+1}}\left(\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}\mu_{i}-\sum_{l=1}^{2\ell}\sqrt{k_{l}}\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}-1_{l}}\Sigma_{il}\right).

(33)

This, together with Eq. (
108
) which shows how to obtain gradients, is the primary result of our work. As an example of Eq. (
33
), we derive the two recurrence relations for the single mode case, i.e.,
\ell=1
.
For convenience and ease of vectorization when programming, we can use the first relation to build the first column of the matrix and the second relation to subsequently build the rows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{m+1,n}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{m+1}}\biggl(\mathcal{G}_{m,n}\mu_{1}-\sqrt{m}\mathcal{G}_{m-1,n}\Sigma_{11}

\displaystyle-\sqrt{n}\mathcal{G}_{m,n-1}\Sigma_{12}\biggr),

(34)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{m,n+1}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\biggl(\mathcal{G}_{m,n}\mu_{2}-\sqrt{m}\mathcal{G}_{m-1,n}\Sigma_{21}

\displaystyle-\sqrt{n}\mathcal{G}_{m,n-1}\Sigma_{22}\biggr).

(35)

We represent this example pictorially in Fig.
1
.

For Gaussian operators on
\ell
modes, the recurrence relations allow us to combine
2\ell+1
neighbouring elements in
2\ell
different ways (by incrementing each of the
2\ell
indices) and generate
2\ell
new elements.

Note that these recurrence relations can be rewritten in an alternative form by using the mode operators:

\displaystyle a_{i}\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}\mathcal{G}-\left(\sum_{l=1}^{\ell}a_{l}^{\dagger}\Sigma_{il}\right)\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{G}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{\ell}a_{l}\Sigma_{i,l+\ell}\right),

(36)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}a_{i}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\mu_{i+\ell}\mathcal{G}-\left(\sum_{l=1}^{\ell}a_{l}^{\dagger}\Sigma_{i+\ell,l}\right)\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{G}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{\ell}a_{l}\Sigma_{i+\ell,l+\ell}\right).

(37)

With these compact relations we can formulate the commutation relations between a general multimode Gaussian operator and the mode operators
a_{i}
and
a_{i}^{\dagger}
, for instance for a single-mode Gaussian operator, we have:

\displaystyle[\mathcal{G},a]

\displaystyle=-\mu_{1}\mathcal{G}+(\Sigma_{12}+1)\mathcal{G}a+\Sigma_{11}a^{\dagger}\mathcal{G},

(38)

\displaystyle[\mathcal{G},a^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=\mu_{2}\mathcal{G}-(\Sigma_{21}+1)a^{\dagger}\mathcal{G}-\Sigma_{22}\mathcal{G}a.

(39)

As an example, we can write the special cases of the commutation relations between the mode operators and the squeezing and displacement operators (using the values from subsections
3.1.1
and
3.1.2
):

\displaystyle[S(\zeta),a]

\displaystyle=(1-\mathrm{sech}(r))S(\zeta)a+e^{i\delta}\tanh(r)a^{\dagger}S(\zeta),

(40)

\displaystyle[S(\zeta),a^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{sech}(r)-1)a^{\dagger}S(\zeta)+e^{-i\delta}\tanh(r)S(\zeta)a,

(41)

\displaystyle[D(\gamma),a]

\displaystyle=-\gamma D(\gamma),

(42)

\displaystyle[D(\gamma),a^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=-\gamma^{*}D(\gamma).

(43)

3
Applications: single- and two-mode gates and passive Gaussian transformations

All Gaussian single- and two-mode transformations will be expressed as a combination of four elementary operations: single-mode phase shifts, single-mode squeezing, single-mode displacement and beamsplitters.
These elements are parametrized as follows: the phase rotation gate
R(\phi)
depends on an angle
\phi
, the single-mode squeezer
S(\zeta)
depends on a single complex parameter which we express in polar form
\zeta=re^{i\delta}
, the displacement gate
D(\gamma)
depends on a single complex parameter
\gamma
which we leave as is, and finally the beamsplitter
B(\theta,\varphi)
depends on two angles
(\theta,\varphi)
.
Each gate is expressed as the exponential of mode operators. In particular, for the rotation and beamsplitter gates we write

\displaystyle R(\phi)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(i\phi\,a^{\dagger}a\right),

(44)

\displaystyle B(\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\theta\left(e^{i\varphi}a_{1}a_{2}^{\dagger}-e^{-i\varphi}a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{2}\right)\right].

(45)

For example, the 50/50 beam splitter with real coefficients corresponds to
(\theta,\varphi)=(\pi/4,0)
. Note that the last two gates are special cases of a passive linear optical transformation, thus they can be written as
\mathcal{U}(\bm{W})
where

\displaystyle\bm{W}

\displaystyle=e^{i\phi},

(46a)

\displaystyle\bm{W}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-e^{-i\varphi}\sin\theta\\
e^{i\varphi}\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix},

(46b)

respectively.

3.1
Single-mode Gaussian gate

A simple way to parametrize a general single-mode Gaussian operation is as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(1)}=\mathcal{G}^{(1)}(\gamma,\phi,\zeta)=D(\gamma)R(\phi)S(\zeta).

(47)

By using Eq. (
46a
) and the general result from the last section we easily find

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\left[|\gamma|^{2}+\gamma^{*2}e^{i(\delta+2\phi)}\tanh r\right]\right)}{\sqrt{\cosh r}},

(48)

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}^{T}

\displaystyle=[\gamma^{*}e^{i(\delta+2\phi)}\tanh r+\gamma,-\gamma^{*}e^{i\phi}\sech r],

(49)

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}e^{i(\delta+2\phi)}\tanh r&-e^{i\phi}\sech r\\
\hline\cr-e^{i\phi}\sech r&-e^{-i\delta}\tanh r\\
\end{array}\right].

These values enter the recurrence relations (
34
) and (
35
), which are depicted in Fig.

Special case: single-mode squeezing

For a single-mode squeezing operator, we have

, and thus

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sech r},

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}^{T}

\displaystyle=[0,0],

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}e^{i\delta}\tanh r&-\sech r\\
\hline\cr-\sech r&-e^{-i\delta}\tanh r\\

The recurrence relations simplify accordingly, and we obtain the matrix elements of the single-mode squeezer

\displaystyle S_{0,0}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sech r},

\displaystyle S_{m+1,0}=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{\frac{m}{m+1}}S_{m-1,0}e^{i\delta}\tanh r,

\displaystyle S_{m,n+1}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\bigl(\sqrt{m}S_{m-1,n}\sech r+

where we have set

3.2
Two-mode Gaussian gate

We can write the most general two-mode Gaussian transformation as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}(\bm{\gamma})\mathcal{R}(\bm{\phi})B(\theta^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})\mathcal{S}(\bm{\zeta})B(\theta,\varphi),

(66)

where
\bm{\phi}=[\phi_{1},\phi_{2}]
,
\bm{\gamma}=[\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}]
and
\bm{\zeta}=[\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}]
and where the single-mode gates factorize over the two modes.
This gives a total of 14 real parameters (counting double for complex parameters), in accordance with Ref.
[
46
]
.
The equations above can be recast into the form of Eq. (
20
) by writing
\mathcal{U}(\bm{V})=B(\theta,\varphi)
and
\mathcal{U}(\bm{W})=\mathcal{R}(\bm{\phi})B(\theta^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})
, with

\displaystyle\bm{V}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\theta&-e^{-i\varphi}\sin\theta\\
e^{i\varphi}\sin\theta&\cos\theta\\
\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle\bm{W}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{i\phi_{1}}&0\\
0&e^{i\phi_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\theta^{\prime}&-e^{-i\varphi^{\prime}}\sin\theta^{\prime}\\
e^{i\varphi^{\prime}}\sin\theta^{\prime}&\cos\theta^{\prime}\\
\end{array}\right].

3.2.1
Special case: Two-mode squeezer

The two-mode squeezer is defined as
S^{(2)}(\zeta)=\exp\left(\zeta a^{\dagger}_{1}a^{\dagger}_{2}-\text{H.c.}\right)
and can be decomposed in terms of beamsplitters and single-mode squeezing operations as follows:

\displaystyle S^{(2)}(\zeta)=B(-\pi/4,0)\left[S(\zeta)\otimes S(-\zeta)\right]B(\pi/4,0).

(73)

From the decomposition above one easily finds
C=\sech r
,
\bm{\mu}^{T}=[0,0,0,0]
and

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}=(-1)\times

(74)

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&e^{i\delta}\tanh r&\sech r&0\\
e^{i\delta}\tanh r&0&0&\sech r\\
\sech r&0&0&-e^{-i\delta}\tanh r\\
0&\sech r&-e^{-i\delta}\tanh r&0\\
\end{array}\right].

Note that a two-mode squeezing operation creates photons in pairs, which implies that the difference of photon number between the two modes is conserved. This observation is mathematically equivalent to the fact that that the operation has an
SU(1,1)
symmetry
[
47
]
. If we write
S^{(2)}_{m,n,p,q}=\langle m,n|S_{2}(\zeta)|p,q\rangle
then the only nonzero elements of this tensor satisfy
m-n=p-q
. Matrix elements that do not satisfy this selection rule are zero and we can construct the whole rank-4 tensor by looping over at most 3 indices:

\displaystyle S^{(2)}_{0,0,0,0}=

\displaystyle\sech r,

(79)

\displaystyle S^{(2)}_{n,n,0,0}=

\displaystyle-S^{(2)}_{n-1,n-1,0,0}\Sigma_{2,1},

(80)

\displaystyle S^{(2)}_{m,n,m-n,0}=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{\frac{m}{m-n}}S^{(2)}_{m-1,n,m-n-1,0}\Sigma_{3,1},

(81)

\displaystyle S^{(2)}_{m,n,p,p-(m-n)}=

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{\sqrt{p-(m-n)}}\times

(82)

\displaystyle\ \biggl(\sqrt{n}S^{(2)}_{m,n-1,p,p-(m-n)-1}\Sigma_{4,2}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{p}S^{(2)}_{m,n,p-1,p-(m-n)-1}\Sigma_{4,3}\biggr).

3.3
Passive Gaussian transformations: Interferometers

These
\ell
-mode transformations, which correspond to interferometers parametrized by an
\ell\times\ell
unitary matrix
\bm{V}
, can be obtained from our general results by setting
\bm{\zeta}=\bm{\delta}=\bm{0}
and selecting, without loss of generality,
\bm{W}=\mathbb{I}
to obtain

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=1,

(83)

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}^{T}

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

(84)

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=-\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\bm{0}&\bm{V}\\
\hline\cr\bm{V}^{T}&\bm{0}\end{array}\right].

We can write
\langle\bm{m}|\mathcal{U}(\bm{V})|\bm{\bm{n}}\rangle=U_{\bm{m},\bm{n}}
and find the following recurrence relations

\displaystyle{U}_{\bm{0},\bm{0}}

\displaystyle=1,

(87a)

\displaystyle{U}_{\bm{m}+1_{i},\bm{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m_{i}+1}}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}V_{i,j}\sqrt{n_{j}}\ {U}_{\bm{m},\bm{n}-1_{j}},

(87b)

\displaystyle{U}_{\bm{m},\bm{n}+1_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{i}+1}}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}V_{j,i}\sqrt{m_{j}}\ {U}_{\bm{m}-1_{j},\bm{n}}.

(87c)

These equations have a particular simple physical interpretation: they tell us that to scatter off one additional photon in the bra side
\bm{m}+1_{i}
one photon has to be sent into the interferometer in the ket side
\bm{n}-1_{j}
. In particular, we find that the probability amplitude that a single photon injected in port
j
ends up in port
i
is precisely the
(i,j)
entry of the unitary matrix describing the interferometer:

\displaystyle{U}_{1_{i},1_{j}}=V_{i,j}.

(88)

Note that
{U}_{\bm{m},\bm{n}}
is nonzero only if
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}m_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}n_{i}
since interferometers do not create or destroy particles (mathematically, this is because they have
U(n)
symmetry).
Matrix elements that do not satisfy this selection rule are zero and we can construct the whole rank-
2\ell
tensor by looping over at most
2\ell-1
indices. Let us illustrate this for a beamsplitter, which is a special case of a general passive transformation, and for which
\bm{V}
is of size
2\times 2
and given in Eq. (
3.2
). The recurrence relations we find are:

\displaystyle B_{0,0,0,0}=

\displaystyle 1,

(89)

\displaystyle B_{m,n,m+n,0}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{m+n}}\biggl(\sqrt{m}V_{1,1}B_{m-1,n,m+n-1,0}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\sqrt{n}V_{2,1}B_{m,n-1,m+n-1,0}\biggr),

(90)

\displaystyle B_{m,n,p,m+n-p}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{m+n-p}}\times

(91)

\displaystyle\biggl(\sqrt{m}V_{1,2}B_{m-1,n,p,m+n-p-1}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{n}V_{2,2}B_{m,n-1,p,m+n-p-1}\biggr),

where
\langle m,n|B(\theta,\phi)|p,q\rangle=B_{m,n,p,q}
.

Whenever a gate has a selection rule, not only its computation is sped up, but also its application when updating a tensor representing a state. For example, if we write the state of two modes up to cutoff
N
as

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=\sum_{k,l=0}^{N-1}c_{k,l}|k,l\rangle,

(92)

then the state after the application of a beamsplitter is given by

\displaystyle|\psi^{\prime}\rangle=

\displaystyle B(\theta,\phi)|\psi\rangle=\sum_{m,n=0}^{N-1}c^{\prime}_{n,m}|n,m\rangle,

(93)

and because of the
U(2)
symmetry of the beamsplitter one can obtain each entry of the updated tensor
c^{\prime}
using a single sum:

\displaystyle c^{\prime}_{n,m}=\sum\limits_{k=\text{max}(1+n+m-N,0)}^{\text{min}(n+m,N-1)+1}B_{n,m,k,n+m-k}\ c_{k,n+m-k}.

(94)

Similarly, one can use the
SU(1,1)
symmetry of the two-mode squeezer to show that if
|\psi^{\prime\prime}\rangle=\sum_{nm=0}^{N-1}c^{\prime\prime}_{n,m}|n,m\rangle=S^{(2)}(z)|\psi\rangle
then the coefficients can again be updated in a single sum as follows:

\displaystyle c^{\prime\prime}_{n,m}=\sum\limits_{k=\text{max}(n-m,0)}^{\text{min}(n-m,0)+N}S^{(2)}_{n,m,k,k+m-n}\ c_{k,k+m-n}.

(95)

These computational savings are doubled when considering density matrices, where updating a single element of a two-mode state requires in general four sums, but these sums can be cut in half for the beamsplitter or the two-mode squeezer.

4
Recursive gradients from generating functions

In this section we present recurrence relations to construct the gradients of Gaussian operators with respect to their parameters. Gradients are essential in optimization procedures, such as the optimization of a whole circuit.

In order to carry out the optimization, we define a function that evaluates the cost or “loss” associated with the current parameter values. By design, the optimal choice of parameters corresponds to the global minimum of the loss function. Our goal is then to minimize the loss by slowly varying the parameters in a sequence of steps via a gradient descent algorithm.
In order to apply the gradient descent algorithm, we need to backpropagate the gradient of the loss function using the chain rule until we reach each of the parameters. Doing so gives us the rate of change of the loss with respect to the parameters, which we can use to update their values and decrease the loss. The optimization procedure starts by initializing the parameters randomly (usually with very small values) and then slowly varies them until we obtain a circuit that is similar enough to the optimal one.

In the next subsections we explain how to correctly handle the gradient of complex and real parameters, we show that gradients can be easily computed from the matrix representation of the operators and we give some examples.

4.1
Gradients with respect to complex parameters

For a complex parameter
\xi
, the gradient descent update step should use the partial derivative of a real loss function
L
with respect to the
conjugate
of the parameter
[
48
,
49
]
:

\displaystyle\xi\leftarrow\xi-\gamma\frac{\partial L}{\partial\xi^{*}}.

(96)

Clearly, this update rule falls back to the regular rule in the case of a real parameter.
To compute the gradient for the update, we need to treat complex variables and their conjugate as
independent
variables, which allows us to compute gradients of non-holomorphic functions
[
48
]
. The chain rule then looks as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial\xi^{*}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{k}}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}^{*}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}^{*}}{\partial\xi^{*}}+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}}{\partial\xi^{*}}.

(97)

In an automatic differentiation framework such as TensorFlow or PyTorch, if we wish to customize the computation of the gradient of a new operation (e.g., when the new operation makes use of compiled code which is treated like a black box) we are supplied with the upstream gradient tensor
\partial L/\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}^{*}
. It is our task to combine it with the local gradients
\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}/\partial\xi^{*}
and
\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}^{*}/\partial\xi^{*}
as prescribed by the chain rule, to produce the downstream gradient
\partial L/\partial\xi^{*}
.

In order to obtain the various parts of Eq. (
97
) we proceed as follows: for the first part of (
97
), the upstream gradient tensor
\partial L/\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}^{*}
is given to us by the software so we only have to compute the local gradient:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}^{*}}{\partial\xi^{*}}=\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}}{\partial\xi}\right)^{*}.

(98)

For the second part of (
97
) we can conjugate the upstream gradient tensor (as the loss function is real):

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}}=\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}^{*}}\right)^{*},

(99)

but as the matrix elements of
\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}
are complex and non-holomorphic functions of
\xi
, their derivatives with respect to
\xi
and
\xi^{*}
are independent and we need to compute
\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}/\partial\xi^{*}
separately.
On the other hand, for a real parameter
x
computing a single gradient tensor
\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}}{\partial x}
would suffice:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{k}}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}^{*}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}^{*}}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}}{\partial x}

(100)

\displaystyle=2\Re\left(\sum_{\bm{k}}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}}{\partial x}\right),

(101)

as in this case
\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}^{*}}{\partial x}
and
\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}}{\partial x}
are the conjugate of each other.
If one prefers to have only real parameters then there is an additional step: one can write each complex parameter in Cartesian or polar form, and then treat each of these as an additional function of two real variables, e.g., for
\xi=re^{i\phi}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial r}

\displaystyle=2\Re\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\xi^{*}}\frac{\partial\xi^{*}}{\partial r}\right)=2\Re\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\xi^{*}}e^{-i\phi}\right),

(102)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial\phi}

\displaystyle=2\Re\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\xi^{*}}\frac{\partial\xi^{*}}{\partial\phi}\right)=-2\Re\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\xi^{*}}i\xi^{*}\right).

(103)

Furthermore, if the gate depends on a single parameter (real or complex) more simplifications can be made as shown in Appendix
F
.

4.2
Computing gradients with the generating function method

An
\ell
-mode Gaussian gate has
2\ell^{2}+3\ell
real parameters (counting double for complex parameters), therefore in order to obtain all the parameter updates we need to compute
2\ell^{2}+3\ell
derivatives, some of which will be combined to form the parameter updates of the complex parameters.

Another key insight of the generating function method is that to compute the gradients of the matrix elements we can simply differentiate the generating function with respect to the parameters
before
computing the high-order derivatives (here with respect to a generic parameter
\xi
):

\displaystyle\partial_{\xi}\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial_{\bm{\nu}}^{\bm{k}}}{\sqrt{\bm{k}!}}\partial_{\xi}\Gamma(\bm{\nu})|_{\bm{\nu}=\bm{0}}=\frac{\partial_{\bm{\nu}}^{\bm{k}}}{\sqrt{\bm{k}!}}\partial_{\xi}Ce^{Q(\bm{\nu})}|_{\bm{\nu}=\bm{0}}

(104)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial_{\bm{\nu}}^{\bm{k}}}{\sqrt{\bm{k}!}}\Gamma(\bm{\nu})\left(\frac{\partial_{\xi}C}{C}+\partial_{\xi}Q(\bm{\nu})\right)|_{\bm{\nu}=\bm{0}}

(105)

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\partial_{\bm{\nu}}^{\bm{k}}}{\sqrt{\bm{k}!}}\Gamma(\bm{\nu})\left(\frac{\partial_{\xi}C}{C}+\frac{\partial\bm{\mu}^{T}}{\partial\xi}{\bm{\nu}}-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\nu}}^{T}\frac{\partial\bm{\Sigma}}{\partial\xi}{\bm{\nu}}\right)\right|_{\bm{\nu}=\bm{0}}.

(106)

Expression (
106
) contains terms proportional to
\bm{\nu}^{\bm{n}}\Gamma(\bm{\nu})
, whose derivative can be expressed using the elements of
\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{\bm{k}}_{\bm{\nu}}}{\sqrt{\bm{k}!}}{\bm{\nu}}^{\bm{n}}\Gamma(\bm{\nu})|_{\bm{\nu}=\bm{0}}=\sqrt{(\bm{k})_{\bm{n}}}\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}-\bm{n}},

(107)

where
(k)_{n}=k(k-1)(k-2)\dots(k-n+1)
is the Pochhammer symbol and
(\bm{k})_{\bm{n}}=\prod_{i}(k_{i})_{n_{i}}
(with the convention that
(k)_{0}=1
).
So once we compute the tensor representation of a Gaussian operator, we can use it to compute its gradient with respect to a parametrization.
Continuing from Eq. (
106
) we can write:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}}{\partial\xi}=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{\xi}C}{C}\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}+\sum_{i}\frac{\partial\mu_{i}}{\partial\xi}\sqrt{k_{i}}\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}-1_{i}}

\displaystyle-\sum_{i>j}\frac{\partial\Sigma_{ij}}{\partial\xi}\sqrt{k_{i}k_{j}}\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}-1_{i}-1_{j}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\frac{\partial\Sigma_{ii}}{\partial\xi}\sqrt{k_{i}(k_{i}-1)}\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}-2_{i}}.

(108)

This, together with Eq. (
33
), is the primary result of our work.

As an example, we show the derivatives of the single mode Gaussian operator with respect to the complex displacement parameter
\gamma
and
\gamma^{*}
(recall that we must treat them as independent variables). We begin by differentiating
C
,
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\Sigma}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{\gamma}C}{C}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma^{*}}{2},

(109)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{\gamma^{*}}C}{C}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma}{2}-\gamma^{*}e^{i(\delta+2\phi)}\tanh r,

(110)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{\mu}}{\partial\gamma}

\displaystyle=[1,0],

(111)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{\mu}}{\partial\gamma^{*}}

\displaystyle=[e^{i(\delta+2\phi)}\tanh r,-e^{i\phi}\sech r],

(112)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{\Sigma}}{\partial\gamma}

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

(113)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{\Sigma}}{\partial\gamma^{*}}

\displaystyle=\bm{0}.

(114)

Therefore, the gradients of the matrix elements are:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}_{m,n}}{\partial\gamma}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma^{*}}{2}\,\mathcal{G}_{m,n}+\sqrt{m}\,\mathcal{G}_{m-1,n},

(115)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}_{m,n}}{\partial\gamma^{*}}=

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{*}e^{i(\delta+2\phi)}\tanh r\right)\mathcal{G}_{m,n}

(116)

\displaystyle+e^{i(\delta+2\phi)}\sqrt{m}\mathcal{G}_{m-1,n}\tanh r

\displaystyle-e^{i\phi}\sqrt{n}\mathcal{G}_{m,n-1}\sech r.

We then combine both of these expressions with the upstream gradient
\partial L/\partial\mathcal{G}_{mn}^{*}
and its conjugate as prescribed in Eq. (
97
) to obtain the single gradient for the update,
\partial L/\partial\gamma^{*}
. We proceed in an analogous way for all the other parameters.

6
Conclusions

We have presented a unified procedure for obtaining the Fock representation of a quantum optical Gaussian transformation using generating functions. From the generating functions we obtain recurrence relations which allow us to compute the matrix elements of any given Gaussian transformation when written in terms of its Bloch-Messiah decomposition. Furthermore, we have developed general techniques to also obtain the gradients of a given transformation once it is parametrized.

As particular examples we derived explicit recurrence relations for the displacement, single- and two-mode squeezing gates and the beamsplitter.
These recurrence relations are part of the
fock_gradients
module of
The Walrus
written in pure Python using
Numpy
and are sped up using the just-in-time compiling capabilities of
Numba
. Our highly portable implementation makes our algorithms ideal for high performance simulation of quantum optical circuits using both CPUs and GPUs. Furthermore, we expect them to accelerate the research on quantum hardware, quantum machine learning, optical data processing, device discovery and device design.

Multimode generating function

In Barnett and Radmore
[
25
]
(cf. Sec. 3.7 and Appendix 5) the following identities are proven:

\displaystyle\exp\left(\theta a^{\dagger}a\right)

\displaystyle=\ :\exp\left(\left[\exp(\theta)-1\right]a^{\dagger}a\right):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left[\exp(\theta)-1\right]^{n}a^{\dagger n}a^{n}}{n!},

(120)

and also

\displaystyle{S}(\zeta)=

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\zeta^{*}a^{2}-\text{H.c.}\right)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}re^{-i\delta}a^{2}-\text{H.c.}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\tfrac{\tanh r}{2}e^{i\delta}a^{\dagger 2}\right)\exp\left(-\left[a^{\dagger}a+\tfrac{1}{2}\right]\log\cosh r\right)\exp\left(\tfrac{\tanh r}{2}e^{i\delta}a^{2}\right).

(121)

where we wrote
\zeta=re^{i\delta}
.
Putting these two identities together we can normal order a squeezing operator

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}re^{-i\delta}a^{2}-\text{H.c.}\right)=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\tfrac{\tanh r}{2}e^{i\delta}a^{\dagger 2}\right)\times\frac{:\exp\left(\left[\sech r-1\right]\ a^{\dagger}a\right):}{\sqrt{\cosh r}}\times\exp\left(\tfrac{\tanh r}{2}e^{-i\delta}a^{2}\right).

(122)

Using this expression one can find the matrix element of the squeezing operation between two coherent states

\displaystyle\langle\alpha^{*}|\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}re^{-i\delta}a^{2}-\text{H.c.}\right)|\beta\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\cosh r}}\langle\alpha^{*}|\beta\rangle\exp\left(-\tfrac{\tanh r}{2}e^{i\delta}\alpha^{2}+\left[\sech r-1\right]\ \alpha\beta+\tfrac{\tanh r}{2}e^{-i\delta}\beta^{2}\right),

(123)

and noting that

\end{array}\right]\right).

\bm{\beta}\\
\end{array}\right]\right),

where

(133)

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\alpha}^{*}|\mathcal{D}(\bm{\gamma})=\langle\bm{\alpha}^{*}-\bm{\gamma}|\exp\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\bm{\alpha}^{T}\bm{\gamma}-\bm{\gamma}^{\dagger}\bm{\alpha}^{*}\right]\right).

Appendix B
Recurrence relation for the high-order derivatives of an exponential function

We rewrite Eq. (15) of Ref.
[
45
]
in our notation as follows:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\bm{k}+1_{n}}=\sum_{\bm{j}=\bm{0}}^{\bm{k}}\binom{\bm{k}}{\bm{j}}Q^{(\bm{k}-\bm{j}+1_{n})}\Gamma_{\bm{j}},

(149)

where we write
\partial_{\bm{\nu}}^{\bm{k}}f(\bm{\nu})|_{\bm{\nu}=\bm{0}}=f^{(\bm{k})}=f_{\bm{k}}
for the
\bm{k}^{\text{th}}
derivative of
f
at zero and define
\sum_{\bm{j}=\bm{0}}^{\bm{k}}\binom{\bm{k}}{\bm{j}}=\sum_{j_{1}}^{k_{1}}\dots\sum_{j_{n}}^{k_{n}}\binom{k_{1}}{j_{1}}\dots\binom{k_{n}}{j_{n}}
.
In our case
Q
is a quadratic in
\bm{\nu}
and we write

\displaystyle Q(\bm{\nu})=\bm{\mu}^{T}\bm{\nu}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\nu}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\nu}.

(150)

We can write easily all its nonzero derivatives:

\displaystyle Q^{(1_{i})}

\displaystyle=\mu_{i},

(151)

\displaystyle Q^{(1_{i}+1_{j})}

\displaystyle=-\Sigma_{i,j}.

(152)

We know that most of the terms in Eq. (
149
) will not survive the summation, indeed only the ones that satisfy
\bm{k}-\bm{j}=1_{i}
or
\bm{k}-\bm{j}=1_{i}+1_{l}
will contribute to the sum. We can use this to our advantage and write

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\bm{k}+1_{n}}

\displaystyle=Q^{(1_{n})}\Gamma_{\bm{k}}+\sum_{l}k_{l}Q^{(1_{n}+1_{l})}\Gamma_{\bm{k}-1_{l}}=\mu_{n}\Gamma_{\bm{k}}-\sum_{l}k_{l}\Sigma_{l,n}\Gamma_{\bm{k}-1_{l}}.

(153)

Now let us derive this in a different way. Start with the generating function of the multidimensional Hermite polynomials
[
57
,
58
,
59
]
:

\displaystyle\exp\left(\bm{\mu}^{T}\bm{\nu}-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\nu}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\nu}\right)=\sum_{\bm{k}=\bm{0}}^{\bm{\infty}}\frac{\bm{\nu}^{\bm{k}}}{\bm{k}!}G_{\bm{k}}^{(\bm{\Sigma})}(\bm{\mu}).

(154)

They satisfy the recurrence relation

\displaystyle G_{\bm{k}+1_{i}}^{(\bm{\bm{\Sigma}})}(\bm{\mu})=\mu_{i}G_{\bm{k}}^{(\bm{\bm{\Sigma}})}(\bm{\mu})-\sum_{j=1}\Sigma_{i,j}k_{j}G_{\bm{k}-1_{j}}^{(\bm{\bm{\Sigma}})}(\bm{\mu}),

from which we identify that for a quadratic function

,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\bm{k}}=G^{(\bm{\Sigma})}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{\mu})=\sqrt{\bm{k}!}\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}.

(155)

By replacing this last expression in the recurrence relation Eq. (

\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}
.

Appendix C
Recursive matrix elements from commutation relations

In this section we use an alternative approach for obtaining the matrix elements and gradients of a gate by using the commutation relations with the destruction operator of a given mode. To illustrate this method we will focus on the
k^{\text{th}}
-order-phase gate defined as

\displaystyle V^{(k)}(\eta)=\exp\left(i\frac{\eta}{k\hbar}q^{k}\right),

(156)

where the canonical position is defined as
q=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2}}(a+a^{\dagger})
and its canonical momentum is given by
p=i\sqrt{\tfrac{\hbar}{2}}\left(a^{\dagger}-a\right)
. In the Heisenberg picture the
k^{\text{th}}
-order-phase gate transforms the operators according to

\displaystyle V^{(k)\dagger}(\eta)qV^{(k)}(\eta)

\displaystyle=q,

(157)

\displaystyle V^{(k)\dagger}(\eta)pV^{(k)}(\eta)

\displaystyle=p+\eta q^{k-1},

(158)

\displaystyle V^{(k)\dagger}(\eta)aV^{(k)}(\eta)

\displaystyle=a+i\frac{\eta}{\hbar}\left(\frac{\hbar}{2}\right)^{k/2}\left(a+a^{\dagger}\right)^{k-1}.

(159)

One can write the matrix elements of the
k^{\text{th}}
-order-phase gate in terms of integrals as follows

\displaystyle\langle m|

\displaystyle V^{(k)}(\eta)|n\rangle=V_{m,n}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi 2^{n+m}n!m!}}\int dx\exp\left(-x^{2}+i\frac{\eta\hbar^{k/2}}{\hbar k}x^{k}\right)~H_{n}(x)H_{m}(x),

(160)

where
H_{n}(x)
is a Hermite polynomial of degree
n

[
25
]
. The last equation makes explicit that the matrix elements
V_{m,n}^{(k)}
are symmetric
V_{m,n}^{(k)}=V_{n,m}^{(k)}
and moreover, given the parity of the Hermite polynomials
H_{n}(-x)=(-1)^{n}H_{n}(x)
, that the
k^{\text{th}}
-order-phase gate also has parity, i.e.,

\displaystyle V_{m,n}^{(k)}(\eta)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m+n}V_{m,n}^{(k)}(-\eta)\text{ if $k$ is odd,}

(161)

\displaystyle V_{m,n}^{(k)}(\eta)

\displaystyle=0\text{ if $k$ is even and $n+m$ is odd.}

(162)

Finally, note that the matrix elements of the conjugate
k^{\text{th}}
-order-phase gate

\displaystyle\tilde{V}^{(k)}(\eta)=\exp\left(i\frac{\eta}{\hbar k}p^{k}\right),

(163)

are related to the ones for the
k^{\text{th}}
-order-phase gate via

\displaystyle\langle m|\tilde{V}^{(k)}(\eta)|n\rangle=\tilde{V}^{(k)}_{m,n}=(i)^{m+n}V_{m,n}^{(k)},

(164)

which can be easily confirmed by noticing that the rotation gate by angle
\theta=\pi/2
transforms
q
into
p
in the Heisenberg picture and that when acted on a Fock state it gives
R(\pi/2)|n\rangle=(i)^{n}|n\rangle
.

Having established these basic facts about the
k^{\text{th}}
-order-phase gate we are now ready to write a recurrence relation for its matrix elements. To this end, we write the following commutator

\displaystyle[a,V^{(k)}(\eta)]=aV^{(k)}(\eta)-V^{(k)}(\eta)a=V^{(k)}(\eta)V^{(k)\dagger}(\eta)aV^{(k)}(\eta)-V^{(k)}(\eta)a=V^{(k)}\left\{i\frac{\eta}{\hbar}\left(\frac{\hbar}{2}\right)^{k/2}(a+a^{\dagger})^{k-1}\right\}.

(165)

If we multiply the last equation on the left by
\langle m|
and on the right
|n\rangle
we obtain

\displaystyle\sqrt{m+1}V^{(k)}_{m+1,n}-\sqrt{n}V^{(k)}_{m,n-1}=\langle m|V^{(k)}(\eta)\left(i\frac{\eta}{\hbar}\left(\frac{\hbar}{2}\right)^{k/2}(a+a^{\dagger})^{k-1}\right)|n\rangle.

(166)

Note that for any
k
one can expand the right hand side of the last equation and write

(a+a^{\dagger})^{k-1}|n\rangle=\sum_{k=n-(k-1)}^{n+k-1}c_{k}|k+n\rangle
allowing us to obtain the desired recurrence relation. In the next subsection we specialize these results for the values
k=3
and
k=4
. Note that the cases
k=1,2
are Gaussian gates and thus can be dealt with using the methods of the main text.

C.1
The cubic-phase gate

The cubic phase gate, introduced in Ref.
[
50
]
, is the simplest non-Gaussian operation one can consider when studying polynomial generators of continuous-variable quantum gates. This gate is obtained by setting
k=3
in Eq. (
156
) and can in principle be used to decompose any higher order gate generated by a polynomial in the canonical position
q
and momentum
p
of a continuous-variable system by employing approximate commutator expansions
[
22
,
60
,
24
]
or, for many important applications, finding exact decompositions
[
61
,
62
]
.
Significant effort has been directed into realizing this gate either by using optical nonlinearities
[
63
]
or measurements
[
50
,
64
,
65
,
66
,
67
]
.

To obtain the recurrence relation for the cubic phase gate, we set
k=3
in Eq. (
166
) and rearrange to obtain

\displaystyle V^{(3)}_{m,n+2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)}}\biggl(\frac{i2\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\hbar}\eta}\left\{\sqrt{n}V^{(3)}_{m,n-1}-\sqrt{m+1}V^{(3)}_{m+1,n}\right\}-(2n+1)V^{(3)}_{m,n}-\sqrt{n(n-1)}V^{(3)}_{m,n-2}\biggr).

(167)

We can obtain the first two rows of
V_{m,n}^{(3)}
by setting
m=0
or
1
in the last equation and using the following initial seeds (cf. Appendix
D
)

\displaystyle V_{0,0}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\pi}e^{\frac{2y^{6}}{3}}|y|\text{Ai}\left(y^{4}\right),

(168)

\displaystyle V_{1,1}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=-2i\sqrt{2}y^{3}V_{0,1}^{(3)}=-2i\sqrt{2}y^{3}V_{1,0}^{(3)}=-8\sqrt{\pi}e^{\frac{2y^{6}}{3}}|y|^{5}\left(\text{Ai}^{\prime}\left(y^{4}\right)+y^{2}\text{Ai}\left(y^{4}\right)\right),

(169)

where
y^{3}=1/(\sqrt{\hbar}\eta)
and
\text{Ai}(x)
and
\text{Ai}^{\prime}(x)
are the Airy function and its first derivative
[
68
,
69
]
.
Since

is symmetric in
m,n
once the first two rows are obtained one also obtains the first two columns which serve as the initial conditions to recursively obtain any other row and thus the whole matrix.

Even though the numerical recursive relation derived are exact, they are not numerically stable in the regime where
\eta<1

inside the curly braces in Eq.

C.2
The quartic-phase gate

The quartic phase interaction arises in the context of self-interactions in scalar quantum field theories
[
70
,
71
]
and can also be use to achieve universality in GKP encoded qubits
[
72

50
]
. We can obtain a recursive relation for the matrix elements of this gate by setting

in Eq. (

\displaystyle V^{(4)}_{m+3,n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}}\biggl(

\displaystyle\frac{4i}{\eta\hbar}\left\{\sqrt{n}V^{(4)}_{m,n-1}-\sqrt{m+1}V^{(4)}_{m+1,n}\right\}-\sqrt{n(n-1)(n-2)}V^{(4)}_{m,n-3}

\displaystyle-3n^{\tfrac{3}{2}}V^{(4)}_{m,n-1}-3(n+1)^{\tfrac{3}{2}}V^{(4)}_{m,n+1}\biggr).

(170)

We can iterate this relation using the following initial values

\displaystyle V^{(4)}_{0,0}=

\displaystyle\left.f(w,\lambda)\right|_{\lambda=1},

(171)

\displaystyle V^{(4)}_{1,1}=

\displaystyle\left.-2\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}f(w,\lambda)\right|_{\lambda=1},

(172)

\displaystyle V_{0,2}^{(4)}=

\displaystyle V_{2,0}^{(4)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(V_{1,1}^{(4)}-V_{0,0}^{(4)}\right),

(173)

\displaystyle V_{2,2}^{(4)}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}V_{0,0}^{(4)}-V_{1,1}^{(4)}+2\left.\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\lambda^{2}}f(w,\lambda)\right|_{\lambda=1},

(174)

where

is defined in Eq. (

by parity. With these nine initial values we can fill the first three rows of

using the recurrence relation. Then we can use the symmetry to fill the first three columns and finally complete the whole matrix.

Appendix D
Analytic matrix elements of the cubic-phase gate

In this appendix we obtain the matrix elements of the cubic-phase gate. We start from Eq. (
160
) with
k=3
and
y^{3}=1/(\sqrt{\hbar}\eta)
and doing the change of variables
x\to yk-iy^{3}
, and assuming without loss of generality that
y>0
, we obtain

\displaystyle V^{(3)}_{m,n}=\frac{ye^{\frac{2}{3}y^{6}}}{\sqrt{\pi 2^{n+m}n!m!}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\exp\left(iy^{4}k+ik^{3}/3\right)~H_{n}(yk-iy^{3})H_{m}(yk-iy^{3}).

(175)

To make progress we recall the identity
H_{n}(x+y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}H_{k}(x)(2y)^{(n-k)}
, to obtain

\displaystyle V^{(3)}_{m,n}

\displaystyle=\frac{ye^{\frac{2}{3}y^{6}}}{\sqrt{\pi 2^{n+m}n!m!}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\exp\left(iy^{4}k+ik^{3}/3\right)~\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}H_{k}(-iy^{3})(2yk)^{(n-k)}\sum_{l=0}^{m}\binom{m}{l}H_{l}(-iy^{3})(2yk)^{(m-l)}

(176)

\displaystyle=\frac{ye^{\frac{2}{3}y^{6}}}{\sqrt{\pi 2^{n+m}n!m!}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{l=0}^{m}(2y)^{m+n-k-l}\binom{n}{k}H_{k}(-iy^{3})\binom{m}{l}H_{l}(-iy^{3})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\exp\left(iy^{4}k+ik^{3}/3\right)k^{n+m-k-l}

(177)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}ye^{\frac{2}{3}y^{6}}}{\sqrt{2^{n+m}n!m!}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{l=0}^{m}(-2iy)^{m+n-k-l}\binom{n}{k}H_{k}(-iy^{3})\binom{m}{l}H_{l}(-iy^{3})\Bigg.\left[\frac{d^{n+m-l-k}}{dw^{n+m-l-k}}\Ai(w)\right]\Bigg|_{w=y^{4}}.

(178)

In the last equation, we used the well known Fourier expansion of the Airy function to write
[
69
]

\displaystyle\Ai(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\exp\left(i\frac{k^{3}}{3}+ikx\right),

(179)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{\ell}}{dx^{\ell}}\Ai(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk(ik)^{\ell}\exp\left(i\frac{k^{3}}{3}+ikx\right).

(180)

Note that any higher order derivative of the Airy function can be expressed in terms of polynomials of its arguments, the Airy function itself and it first derivative as follows

\displaystyle\frac{d^{\ell}}{dx^{\ell}}\Ai(x)=p_{\ell}(x)\Ai(x)+q_{\ell}(x)\mathrm{Ai^{\prime}}(x),

(181)

where the polynomials
p_{\ell}
and
q_{\ell}
satisfy the recurrence relations

\displaystyle p_{\ell+1}(x)=xq_{\ell}(x)+\frac{d}{dx}p_{\ell}(x),\quad q_{\ell+1}(x)=p_{\ell}(x)+\frac{d}{dx}q_{\ell}(x).

(182)

By iterating the recurrence relations in Eq. (
182
) one can in principle obtain the polynomials
p_{\ell}(x)
and
q_{\ell}(x)
and then any derivative of the Airy function and thus any matrix element
V^{(3)}_{n,m}
.

Appendix E
Analytic matrix elements of the quartic-phase gate

We are now interested in calculating

\displaystyle V^{(4)}_{m,n}=\langle m|V^{(4)}(\eta)|n\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi 2^{n+m}n!m!}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\exp\left(-x^{2}+\tfrac{i}{4}wx^{4}\right)~H_{n}(x)H_{m}(x),

where
H_{n}(x)
is the
n^{\text{th}}
order Hermite polynomial and we introduced
w=\hbar\eta
.
First consider the related integral (cf. Eq. 3.968 of Ref.
[
73
]
)

\displaystyle f(w,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\exp\left(-\lambda x^{2}+\tfrac{i}{4}wx^{4}\right)

(183)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{4}\sqrt{\pi}e^{\frac{i\lambda^{2}}{8w}+\frac{i\pi}{8}}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{w}}\left(J_{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8w}\right)-iY_{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8w}\right)\right)=-\frac{i}{4}\sqrt{\pi}e^{\frac{i\lambda^{2}}{8w}+\frac{i\pi}{8}}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{w}}H^{(2)}_{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8w}\right),

(184)

where
J_{\alpha}(x)
is a Bessel function of the first kind,
Y_{\alpha}(x)
is a Bessel function of the second kind (sometimes also called a Neumann function) and
H_{\alpha}^{(2)}(x)
is a Hankel function of the second kind (not to be confused with the Hermite polynomials
H_{n}(x)
).
Note that
f(w,1)=V_{0,0}^{(4)}(w)
. To obtain any other matrix element first notice that these elements are nonzero if and only if
n+m
is even, which implies that
H_{n}(x)H_{m}(x)
contains only even powers of
x
. Thus we only need to know how to evaluate

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{k}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\ x^{2k}\exp\left(-\lambda x^{2}+\tfrac{i}{4}wx^{4}\right)=\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial\lambda^{k}}f(w,\lambda).

(185)

In turn the derivatives of this function can be written as derivatives of the Hankel functions of the second kind which satisfy the recurrence relation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}H_{i}^{(2)}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(H_{i-1}^{(2)}(x)+H_{i+1}^{(2)}(x)\right).

(186)

Thus, any matrix element
V^{(4)}_{m,n}
can be obtained in terms of exponentials, polynomials and Hankel functions of the second kind in the variable
w
.

Appendix F
Gradients of single parameter gates

We outline here an alternative procedure to obtain gradients of single-parameter gates. Let us consider a gate parametrized by a single complex number
u=xe^{i\epsilon}
,

\displaystyle G(u)=G(xe^{i\epsilon})=\exp\left(xe^{i\epsilon}h-\text{H.c.}\right).

(187)

The case of a gate parametrized by a single
real
number can be obtained by letting
\epsilon=\pi/2
and writing
G(x)=\exp(ix(h+h^{\dagger}))=\exp(Hx)
with
H=i(h+h^{\dagger})=-H^{\dagger}
.
For example, for a two-mode squeezing gate,
h=a_{1}^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}_{2}
and for the cubic phase gate,
H=iq^{3}/(k\hbar)
. Now it is direct to verify that

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\langle\bm{m}|G|\bm{n}\rangle=\langle\bm{m}|G\left\{e^{i\epsilon}h-\text{H.c.}\right\}|\bm{n}\rangle.

(188)

To obtain the gradient with respect to the amplitude
x
, one only needs to express
(e^{i\epsilon}h-\text{H.c.})|\bm{n}\rangle
