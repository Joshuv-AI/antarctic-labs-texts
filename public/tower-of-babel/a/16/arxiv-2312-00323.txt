Liouville theory and the Weil–Petersson geometry of moduli space: bordered, conic, and higher genus surfaces


Abstract
Two-dimensional conformal field theory is a powerful tool to understand the geometry of surfaces. Here, we study Liouville conformal field theory in the classical (large central charge) limit, where it encodes the geometry of the moduli space of Riemann surfaces. Generalizing previous work, we employ this to study moduli spaces of higher genus surfaces, surfaces with boundaries, and surfaces with cone points. In each case, the knowledge of classical conformal blocks provides an extremely efficient approximation to the Weil–Petersson metric on moduli space. We find detailed agreement with analytic results for volumes and geodesic lengths on moduli space.

Introduction

Conformal field theory is a powerful tool in the study of geometry, with applications ranging from mirror symmetry to spectral theory and quantum chaos. Our focus in this paper is on
Liouville theory
[
1
]
, which provides a natural means to study the Weil--Petersson geometry of the moduli space of Riemann surfaces.

In the semiclassical limit, the Liouville path integral computes the Kähler potential, giving access to the metric and associated geometric quantities. Liouville conformal blocks can be computed efficiently
[
5
]
, so this allows for detailed computations with surprising numerical accuracy. The study of the geometry of hyperbolic surfaces using Liouville theory was started in
[
6
]
, where the authors studied the hyperbolic metric on the four-puncture sphere using the conformal blocks. The solution to the saddle-point equations of classical Liouville theory can be used to compute the classical Liouville action, which is the Kähler potential on moduli space. This technique was applied in
[
7
]
to efficiently compute the metric and Laplacian eigenvalues on the moduli space of the four-punctured sphere.

In the present work, we extend this to general Riemann surfaces. Despite increased complexity, the conformal field theory approach remains viable. As illustrative examples, we focus on the moduli spaces of spheres with cone points, of genus two surfaces with
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
replica symmetry, and bordered Riemann surfaces. In all cases, we numerically compute moduli space volumes and geodesic lengths and find excellent agreement with analytic results in the special cases (where the surfaces have additional discrete symmetries) where exact results are available. We initiate a study of the spectra of Weil–Petersson Laplacians, which are generally only computable numerically.

Our results pave the way for new investigations. With this technique, detailed studies of Weil–Petersson geometry appear feasible. We anticipate applications in geometry, number theory, and physics. For instance, the eigenvalues of the Laplacian exhibit quantum chaotic behavior, and in some cases (see
[
7
]
) agree with random matrix theory statistics. It would be fascinating to continue the study of these eigenvalues in detail in the examples we present in this paper. This also suggests intriguing connections to dual gravitational systems.

More broadly, the ability to numerically access geometrical information opens new possibilities to test conjectures, formulate hypotheses, and explore novel properties of these moduli spaces. Analytical calculations are typically restricted to low genus or certain limits, so numerical computation provides a vital complementary tool. Our method may uncover new structures in the Weil–Petersson geometry. The excellent agreement with analytical results validates the power of the approach and highlights its promise. We anticipate a broad impact spanning geometry, number theory, quantum gravity, black holes, and more.

On the physics side, the quantization of moduli space is closely related to quantum gravity in three dimensions. The Liouville action is in many cases closely related to (if not exactly equal to) the Einstein action of an associated three-manifold. Indeed, when spacetime has the topology of a surface
\Sigma
times time, the constraint equations of 3d gravity with a negative cosmological constant can be solved exactly: the resulting phase space is the cotangent bundle of the moduli space of Riemann surfaces. Thus, our techniques offer insight into the structure of quantum gravity, and potentially even a possible statistical mechanical origin for black hole entropy
[
8
]
.

2.2
Moduli spaces

The moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L})
of a Riemann surface
\Sigma_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L})
is defined as

\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L})=\left.\quantity{ \qty(\sS, B_1, \dots, B_n) \, \middle| \,
\begin{array}{l}
\sS\text{ is hyperbolic surface of genus $g$ } \\
\text{with $n$ boundaries $B_1, \dots, B_n$}
\end{array}}\middle/\sim\right.,

(2.12)

where the boundary
B_{j}
has geodesic length
L_{j}
, and
\quantity(\sS,B_1, \dots, B_n)\sim\quantity(\sS',B'_1, \dots, B'_n)
if and only if there exists a biholomorphic map from
\mathsf{S}
to
\mathsf{S}^{\prime}
that sends
B_{j}
to
B^{\prime}_{j}
for all
j
.

This moduli space has (real) dimension
6g+2n-6
, which can be understood simply as follows. A hyperbolic surface
\Sigma_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L})
can be decomposed into
2g+n-2
pairs of pants by cutting it along
3g+n-3
geodesics. The hyperbolic metric on a pair of pants is uniquely determined by the lengths of the geodesics of its boundary components. This means that the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L})
can be parameterized by specifying the lengths
L_{i}=\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{n},l_{1},\ldots,L_{3g+n-3}\}
of these geodesics, along with a corresponding set of “twist" parameters
\vartheta_{i}=\{\vartheta_{1},\ldots,\vartheta_{n},l_{1},\ldots,\vartheta_{3g+n-3}\}
which tell us the relative angles with which these geodesics are glued together to obtain the Riemann surface. The
(L_{i},\vartheta_{i})
provide a local set of coordinates on
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L})
known as Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates.

A given Riemann surface
\Sigma_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L})
can be parameterized in many different ways using this construction, since there are many different pair-of-pants decompositions of the same surface (and, moreover, each twist parameter
\vartheta_{i}
is periodic with period
L_{i}
). The space where the Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates
(l_{i},\vartheta_{i})
are allowed to be arbitrary real values (subject only to
L_{i}>0
) is the universal cover of the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L})
, and is known as the Teichmüller space
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L})
. The moduli space itself is the quotient

\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L})=\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L})/\mathrm{MCG}(\Sigma_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L}))

where
\mathrm{MCG}(\Sigma_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L}))
is the mapping class group of the Riemann surface.
5
5

5

The mapping class group
\mathrm{MCG}(\Sigma_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L}))
is defined to be the group of isotopy classes of orientation-preserving homeomorphisms of
\Sigma_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L})
to itself, fixing its boundaries setwise. This group permutes the different pair-of-pants decompositions of the surface.

The moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L})
admits a symplectic structure called the Weil–Petersson symplectic structure. It was shown by Wolpert in
[
9

6

Hence, we can carry out our procedure using Liouville theory even for surfaces with geodesic boundary components of non-vanishing length. Indeed, we will later verify that the Weil–Petersson volumes obtained using this prescription match the volume polynomials in all cases we study.

2.3
Algebraic surfaces

Certain families of Riemann surfaces (without boundary), which we will denote

[
13

Liouville conformal field theory

Liouville theory is a two-dimensional quantum field theory with the following classical action:

S^{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{L}}[\hat{g},\phi]=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}^{2}\xi\,\sqrt{\hat{g}}\quantity(\hat{g}^{\rho\sigma} \partial_\rho\phi\partial_\sigma\phi+ 4 \pi\mu\ep^{2b\phi}),

\Sigma
with coordinates

, where

\mathrm{d}\hat{s}^{2}=\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muz\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu,

At the quantum level, Liouville theory is a conformal field theory (CFT) that fits into the BPZ framework

with a continuous spectrum whose space of states decomposes as

\mathcal{H}=\int_{\mathbb{S}_{Q}}\mathrm{d}\alpha\,\mathcal{V}_{\alpha,c}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{\alpha,c},\quad\mathbb{S}_{Q}=\frac{Q}{2}+\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}^{+},

In Liouville CFT, we are interested in the correlation functions of primary vertex operators

In this notation, we refer to

\expectationvalue{V_{\alpha_3}(z_3, \conj{z}_3) V_{\alpha_2}(z_2, \conj{z}_2) V_{\alpha_1}(z_1, \conj{z}_1)}=\absolutevalue{z_{12}}^{2\Delta_{12,3}}\absolutevalue{z_{23}}^{2\Delta_{23,1}}\absolutevalue{z_{31}}^{2\Delta_{31,2}}C(\alpha_{3},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1}),

i\neq j\neq k\neq i

3.2
Semiclassical limit of Liouville theory path integral

In the semiclassical limit with
b\to 0
, Liouville theory describes the classical geometry of hyperbolic Riemann surfaces. To see this, we can introduce a new field
\varphi\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=2b\phi
. The action for the field
\varphi
in flat background metric with complex
(z,\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muz\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)
-coordinates
12
12

12

The integral measure in complex
(z,\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muz\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)
-coordinates is defined as
\mathrm{d}^{2}z\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{d}z\wedge\mathrm{d}\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muz\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu
.
on a surface
\Sigma
is

S^{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{L}}[\varphi]=b^{-2}S^{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{c}}[\varphi]=\frac{1}{4\pi b^{2}}\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}^{2}z\,\quantity(\partial\varphi\conj{\partial} \varphi+ 4\pi\lambda\ep^{\varphi}),

(3.12)

where
\lambda\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mu b^{2}
is fixed as
b\to 0
. The classical equation of motion for
\varphi
,

\partial\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\partial\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu\varphi=2\pi\lambda\mathrm{e}^{\varphi},

(3.13)

is the Liouville equation. The solution to this equation furnishes the conformal factor of the physical metric
g_{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{e}^{\varphi}\hat{g}_{\mu\nu}
on the Riemann surface
\Sigma
, with the following line element:

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{\varphi}\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muz\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu.

(3.14)

Note that the Gaussian curvature for this metric is given by

K=-2\mathrm{e}^{-\varphi}\partial\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\partial\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu\varphi,

(3.15)

so the solution to the Liouville equation yields a metric with constant negative curvature
K=-4\pi\lambda
. On the Riemann sphere, in order to ensure that the physical metric is smooth everywhere, we need to impose the following asymptotic behavior for the field
\varphi
at infinity:

\varphi(z,\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muz\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)=-2\log z\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muz\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu+\mathcal{O}\quantity(1)\quad\text{as}\quad\absolutevalue{z}\to\infty.

(3.16)

The primary vertex operators in Liouville theory are of the form
V_{\alpha}=\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha\phi}
. The correlation function of such operators can be written in the path integral language as

\expectationvalue{V_{\alpha_n}(z_n,\conj{z}_n) \dots V_{\alpha_1}(z_z,\conj{z}_1)}=\int\mathrm{D}\varphi\,\mathrm{e}^{-b^{-2}S_{\mathrm{c}}[\varphi]}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\exp(\frac{\alpha_i \varphi(z_i,\conj{z}_i)}{b}).

(3.17)

Since we are looking for the saddle-point approximation for this path integral for
b\to 0
, we must understand how each
\alpha_{i}
scales with
b
. For an operator to have a nontrivial effect on the saddle point, we need
\alpha
to scale with
b^{-1}
. Thus, we take
\alpha=b^{-1}\eta
and keep
\eta
fixed as
b\to 0
for an operator that affects the saddle point. Such operators are called heavy primaries. Asymptotically, heavy operators have dimension
\Delta=b^{-2}\eta(1-\eta)
in the limit
b\to 0
.

Semiclassically, the insertion of heavy operators adds delta function terms to the action, resulting in a modified equation of motion:

\partial\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\partial\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu\varphi=2\pi\lambda\mathrm{e}^{\varphi}-2\pi\sum_{i}\eta_{i}\delta^{(2)}(z-z_{i}),

(3.18)

where
\delta^{(2)}(z-z_{i})
is the Dirac delta function in two dimensions. This implies that in the neighborhood of a heavy operator insertion, we have

\varphi(z,\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muz\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)=-2\eta_{i}\log{\abs{z - z_i}^2}\quad\text{as}\quad\absolutevalue{z - z_i}\to 0.

(3.19)

In principle, one can find the
\varphi^{\mathrm{s}}
that solves equation (
3.18
) globally on a Riemann surface
\Sigma
, parameterized by a number of moduli including the period matrix and insertion points, and compute the critical Liouville action as
S^{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{c}}[\varphi^{\mathrm{s}}]
. The interpretation of Liouville field
\varphi^{\mathrm{s}}
as the conformal factor of the metric on a surface
\Sigma
in equation (
3.14
) allows us to think of the critical Liouville action as only a function of the moduli
(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muq\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu})
of
\Sigma
,

S^{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{q},\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\boldsymbol{q}\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=S^{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{c}}[\varphi^{\mathrm{s}}].

(3.20)

Remarkably, this Liouville action has a simple interpretation in terms of the Weil–Petersson symplectic form: it is proportional to the Kähler potential on moduli space,
U=-4\pi S_{\mathrm{c}}

[
11
,
12
]
.

3.3
Semiclassical limit of the conformal block expansion

In the semiclassical limit, the DOZZ formula for the structure coefficients
C(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})
can be written in an exponential form
[
27
,
31
]
,

\lim_{b\to 0}C(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})=\exp(- b^{-2} S_3(p_1,p_2,p_3)),

(3.21)

where
S_{3}(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})
is equal to the critical Liouville action on a sphere with three heavy operator insertions (see §
B
). Similarly, the reflection coefficients exponentiate in this limit,

\lim_{b\to 0}R(\alpha)=\exp(-b^{-2} r(p)).

(3.22)

In these expressions, we have used the following parametrization for the Liouville momenta:

\alpha_{i}=\frac{Q}{2}+\mathrm{i}P_{i},\quad\eta_{i}=\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{i}p_{i},

(3.23)

such that in the semiclassical limit
\alpha_{i}=b^{-1}\eta_{i}
and
P_{i}=b^{-1}p_{i}
.

Virasoro blocks of heavy operators also take an exponential form in the limit
b\to 0
. This was conjectured
[
5
]
and proven
[
32
]
for the sphere four-point blocks
\mathcal{F}_{34}^{21}(\alpha;x)
. In this limit,

\lim_{b\to 0}\mathcal{F}_{34}^{21}(\alpha;x)=\exp(b^{-2} f_{34}^{21}(p; x)).

(3.24)

General Virasoro blocks for an arbitrary number of heavy operator insertions are also believed to behave similarly in the semiclassical limit, e.g., for genus two blocks:

\mathcal{F}^{\Sigma_{2,0}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha};\boldsymbol{q})=\exp(b^{-2} f^{\Sigma_{2,0}}(\boldsymbol{p}; \boldsymbol{q})).

(3.25)

This exponentiation property allows us to make a saddle-point approximation to the conformal block expansion of an observable in Liouville theory on higher genus surfaces.

In the case of a sphere four-point correlator, we can write

\lim_{b\to 0}\expectationvalue{V_{\alpha_4}(\infty) V_{\alpha_3}(1) V_{\alpha_2}(x,\conj{x}) V_{\alpha_1}(0)}\approx\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}p\,\exp(- b^{-2} S_{34}^{21}(p; x,\conj{x})),

(3.26)

where

S_{34}^{21}(p;x,\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mux\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)=S_{3}(p_{4},p_{3},p)+r(p)+S_{3}(p,p_{2},p_{1})-f_{34}^{21}(p;x)-f_{34}^{21}(p;\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mux\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu).

(3.27)

In the semiclassical limit, we can find the saddle-point momentum
p^{\mathrm{s}}
from the saddle-point equation

\evaluated{\frac{\partial S_{34}^{21}(p;x,\conj{x})}{\partial p}}_{p = p^{\rms}}=0,

(3.28)

and interpret the value of the exponent as the critical Liouville action,

S^{\Sigma_{0,4}}_{\mathrm{c}}(x,\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mux\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)=S_{34}^{21}(p^{\mathrm{s}};x,\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mux\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu).

(3.29)

Similarly, for the genus two partition function, we can write the conformal block expansion as

\lim_{b\to 0}\mathcal{Z}^{\Sigma_{2,0}}(\boldsymbol{q},\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\boldsymbol{q}\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)\approx\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}^{3}p\,\exp(-b^{-2} S^{\Sigma_{2,0}}(p_1,p_2,p_3;\boldsymbol{q},\conj{\boldsymbol{q}})),

(3.30)

where

\begin{split}S^{\Sigma_{2,0}}(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3};\boldsymbol{q},\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\boldsymbol{q}\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)&=2S_{3}(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})+r(p_{1})+r(p_{2})+r(p_{3})\\
&\quad-f^{\Sigma_{2,0}}(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3};\boldsymbol{q})-f^{\Sigma_{2,0}}(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3};\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\boldsymbol{q}\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu).\end{split}

(3.31)

In this case, we have a set of saddle-point equations,

\evaluated{\frac{\partial S^{\Sigma_{2,0}}(p_1,p_2,p_3;\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{q})}{\partial p_i}}_{p_j = p^{\rms}_j}=0,\quad i,j\in\{1,2,3\},

(3.32)

that we should solve for
p^{\mathrm{s}}_{j}
, in order to find the critical Liouville action,

S^{\Sigma_{2,0}}_{\mathrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{q},\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\boldsymbol{q}\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)=S^{\Sigma_{2,0}}\quantity(p^{\rms}_1,p^{\rms}_2,p^{\rms}_3;\boldsymbol{q},\conj{\boldsymbol{q}}).

(3.33)

The value of the saddle-point momenta
p_{i}^{\mathrm{s}}
are related to the length
l_{i}
of the geodesic cuts in the pair of pants decomposition of our hyperbolic surface via
l_{i}=4\pi p_{i}^{\mathrm{s}}
. Hence, finding their values is equivalent to finding the constant negative curvature metric on the surface.

4
The Weil–Petersson metric

As explained previously, the critical Liouville action, which furnishes—conjecturally, in some cases (see §
3.2
)—a Kähler potential on the moduli space, can be calculated by solving the saddle-point equation for the conformal block expansion of an observable in Liouville theory. However, obtaining closed-form solutions for such saddle-point equations is generally impossible for two reasons. Firstly, conformal blocks usually can only be expressed as perturbative expansions in moduli
\boldsymbol{q}
and do not take a closed-form expression except for particular cases. Secondly, the saddle-point equations must be solved for the
\varsigma=3g+n-3
exchange momenta
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{\varsigma})
that appear in the arguments of multiple Gamma functions resulting from the DOZZ formula. Analytically solving such equations is generally impossible even to lowest orders in the
\boldsymbol{q}
-expansions of the conformal block.
13
13

13

In
[
7
]
, the case of the one-complex-dimensional moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{0,4}
was studied, where it was shown that at a fixed order in the
q
-expansion of the conformal block, one could identify a small parameter
\varepsilon(q,\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muq\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)
which allows for series expansion solutions to the saddle-point equation, i.e.,

p^{\mathrm{s}}=\sum_{n=1}p^{\mathrm{s}}_{n}\varepsilon(q,\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muq\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)^{n},

(4.1)

where
p=p^{\mathrm{s}}
is the solution to the saddle-point equations. However, trying to adapt such methods to other moduli spaces results in non-convergent expansions in many cases, including those we study in this paper. Thus, we use a different method here.

To compute the critical Liouville action and metric on the moduli space, we will utilize numerical methods to solve the saddle-point equation. However, we will not rely on numerical methods for differentiation. We will provide a detailed description of this approach in this section.

4.1
Computing the metric on moduli space

The Weil–Petersson metric on the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L})
parametrized by
(\boldsymbol{q},\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\boldsymbol{q}\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)
is obtained from the Liouville critical action
S_{\mathrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{q},\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\boldsymbol{q}\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)
as

g_{i\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mui\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu}=-4\pi\frac{\partial^{2}S_{\mathrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{q},\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\boldsymbol{q}\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)}{\partial q_{i}\partial\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muq\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu_{i}}.

(4.2)

In the saddle-point approximation, the critical Liouville action can be written in full generality as

S_{\mathrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{q},\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\boldsymbol{q}\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)=S(\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}};\boldsymbol{q},\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\boldsymbol{q}\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)=\hat{S}_{3}(\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}})-\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}};\boldsymbol{q})-\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}};\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\boldsymbol{q}\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu),

(4.3)

where we reiterate that
\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}}
is a
(3g+n-3)
-dimensional vector of saddle-point exchange momenta depending on the choice of pants decomposition of
\Sigma_{g,n}(\boldsymbol{L})
.
In the above,
\hat{S}_{3}(\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}})
denotes the total contribution of the three-point functions, including the reflection coefficients, whereas
\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}};\boldsymbol{q})
and
\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}};\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\boldsymbol{q}\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)
denote the total contribution of holomorphic and anti-holomorphic conformal blocks respectively.

The saddle-point momentum is then computed by solving the following saddle-point equation,

\evaluated{\frac{\partial S(\boldsymbol{p}; \boldsymbol{q}, \conj{\boldsymbol{q}})}{\partial p_j}}_{\boldsymbol{p} = \boldsymbol{p}^{\rms}}=\quantity[\frac{\partial\hat{S}_3(\boldsymbol{p})}{\partial p_j} - \frac{\partial\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{p}; \boldsymbol{q})}{\partial p_j} - \frac{\partial\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{p}; \conj{\boldsymbol{q}})}{\partial p_j}]_{\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}}}=0.

(4.4)

In this setup, we can write

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}};\boldsymbol{q})}{\partial q_{i}\partial\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muq\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{i}}\quantity[\frac{\partial p^{\rms}_j}{\partial\conj{q}_i} \frac{\partial\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{p}; \boldsymbol{q})}{\partial p_j}]_{\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}}},

(4.5)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}};\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\boldsymbol{q}\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)}{\partial q_{i}\partial\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muq\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muq\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu_{i}}\quantity[\frac{\partial p^{\rms}_j}{\partial q_i} \frac{\partial\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{p}; \conj{\boldsymbol{q}})}{\partial p_j}]_{\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}}},

(4.6)

and

\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{S}_{3}(\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}})}{\partial q_{i}\partial\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muq\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu_{i}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muq\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu_{i}}\quantity[\frac{\partial p^{\rms}_j}{\partial q_i} \frac{\partial\hat{S}_3(\boldsymbol{p})}{\partial p_j}]_{\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}}}.

(4.7)

Combining these together and using equation (
4.4
), we find

\frac{\partial^{2}S_{\mathrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{q},\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\boldsymbol{q}\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)}{\partial q_{i}\partial\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muq\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu_{i}}=\quantity[\frac{\partial p^{\rms}_j}{\partial\conj{q}_i} \frac{\partial^2 \hat{f}(\boldsymbol{p}; \boldsymbol{q})}{\partial q_i \partial p_j}]_{\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}}}.

(4.8)

On the other hand, by taking a derivative of equation (
4.4
) with respect to
\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muq\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu_{i}
, we find

\frac{\partial p^{\mathrm{s}}_{j}}{\partial\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muq\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu_{i}}=\quantity[\qty(\frac{\partial^2 S(\boldsymbol{p}; \boldsymbol{q}, \conj{\boldsymbol{q}})}{\partial p_k \partial p_j})^{-1} \frac{\partial^2 \hat{f}(\boldsymbol{p};\conj{\boldsymbol{q}})}{\partial\conj{q}_i \partial p_k}]_{\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}}},

(4.9)

which results in

g_{i\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mui\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu}=4\pi\quantity[\qty(\frac{\partial^2 S(\boldsymbol{p}; \boldsymbol{q}, \conj{\boldsymbol{q}})}{\partial p_k \partial p_j})^{-1} \frac{\partial^2 \hat{f}(\boldsymbol{p}; \boldsymbol{q})}{\partial q_i \partial p_j} \frac{\partial^2 \hat{f}(\boldsymbol{p};\conj{\boldsymbol{q}})}{\partial\conj{q}_i \partial p_k}]_{\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathrm{s}}}.

(4.10)

This formula implies that in order to compute the Weil–Petersson metric, even in the cases where it is not admissible to perform a Taylor expansion in momentum variables, the only part of the computation that we must do numerically is finding the solution to the saddle-point equation (
4.4
).

4.2
Computing the volume of moduli space

In the following section, we will be concerned with integrating the Weil–Petersson metric and finding the corresponding volume of moduli space. We will specialize to the case of the sphere with four singularities and for simplicity, we will focus on the case where all four singularities are identical, this is the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{0,4}(L)
.

Let
\{z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4}\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{*}
denote the locations of the four singularities on the Riemann sphere. There exists a unique Möbius transformation which sends these four points to
\{x,0,1,\infty\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{*}
. The coordinate
x
resulting from this transformation, known as the cross-ratio, is a complex coordinate on the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{0,4}(L)
. The cross-ratio is explicitly given by the formula

x=\frac{(z_{1}-z_{2})(z_{3}-z_{4})}{(z_{1}-z_{4})(z_{2}-z_{3})}.

(4.11)

Due to the four singularities being identical, the moduli space naturally admits the action of the group
S_{4}
which acts by permuting the four points. The cross-ratio is invariant under the subgroup
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
which acts by transposing the four points pairwise. The remaining permutations form the anharmonic group
S_{4}/(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2})\cong S_{3}
. This group acts on the cross-ratio as the transformations,

S_{3}:\quad x\rightarrow\quantity{x, 1-x, \frac{1}{x}, \frac{1}{1-x}, \frac{x}{1-x}, \frac{x-1}{x}}.

(4.12)

The action of the anharmonic group divides the complex plane into six fundamental domains. We can then view the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{0,4}(L)
as a
6
-fold cover of one of these fundamental domains. The fundamental domain that we will use in this paper is

D=\quantity{x \in\bbC\;\middle|\; \Re x < \frac{1}{2}, \abs{x-1} < 1}.

(4.13)

This choice of fundamental domain is convenient for our calculations as we will be using approximations for the conformal blocks that are valid near
x=0
. The metric has an additional
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry which is the action of complex conjugation, explicitly
x\leftrightarrow\bar{x}
. This leads to the half fundamental domain in figure
1(a)
, which is given by

D_{\text{half}}=\quantity{x \in\bbC\;\middle|\; \Re x < \frac{1}{2}, \Im x > 0, \abs{x-1} < 1}.

(4.14)

For numerical calculations, we will find more convenience using the coordinate
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
that is related to the cross-ratio through the formula

\tau=\mathrm{i}\frac{K(1-x)}{K(x)}.

(4.15)

where
K(x)
is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind, defined by

K(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\sqrt{(1-t^{2})(1-xt^{2})}}.

(4.16)

This
\tau
coordinate is related to Zamolodchikov’s
q
coordinate by
q=\exp(\pi\ic\tau)
and the utility of using
\tau
comes from the fact that the four-point Virasoro block converges more rapidly when expressed in this
q
coordinate. The result is that the Zamolodchikov recursion relations will provide an excellent approximation to the conformal block including only the first few terms in the
q
-expansion. The half fundamental domain for
\tau
is shown in figure
1(b)
.

5
Examples

In this section, we provide examples of one-dimensional moduli spaces to demonstrate the applications of the proposed method for calculating Weil–Petersson metrics. In the first example (§
5.1
), we study the geometry of a sphere with four singularities (boundaries, punctures, or conical defects) and compute the geodesic length of the pants cut. By numerically computing the critical Liouville action to determine the Kähler potential on the moduli space, we obtain the Weil–Petersson metric and compute the corresponding moduli space volume. We compare the volumes with the infamous volume polynomials, reviewed in §
2.4
, demonstrating the efficacy of our method.

In the second example (§
5.2
), we consider the
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
-symmetric genus two surfaces described in §
2.3
and compute the volume of their moduli space in addition to the geodesic lengths of the three pants cuts. We find that the volume of the moduli space of such surfaces is three times that of a sphere with four conical singularities, each characterized by a deficit angle of
\frac{\pi}{3}
, an expectation we explain below. We comment on our results in the end.

5.1
Sphere with four singularities

We start with the example of a sphere with four singularities. In the language of Liouville theory, this setup corresponds to the four-point function
\expectationvalue{V_{\alpha_4}(\infty) V_{\alpha_3}(1) V_{\alpha_2}(x,\conj{x}) V_{\alpha_1}(0)}
of heavy operators
V_{\alpha_{i}}
on the sphere. Following the notation in equation (
3.26
), in the semiclassical limit,

\lim_{b\to 0}b\alpha_{i}=\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{i}p_{i},

(5.1)

where
p_{i}
is the Liouville momentum of the operator
V_{\alpha_{i}}
. In this setup, we can consider three different scenarios (parallel to the three types of singularities mentioned in §
2.1
):

•

The case with
p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
correspond to the insertion of primary vertex operators. These operators have the property that
p_{i}^{2}>0
and correspond to singularities that are geodesic boundaries on the surface with geodesic length
14
14

14

This relation assumes that the Gaussian curvature of the uniformized surface is
K=-1
.

L_{i}=4\pi p_{i}
. In the language of Fuchsian geometry, these singularities correspond to hyperbolic elements of the Fuchsian group.

•

The case with
p_{i}=0
corresponds to a four-punctured Riemann sphere that was studied previously in
[
7
]
. These singularities correspond to parabolic elements of the Fuchsian group.

•

The case with
p_{i}\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}^{+}
corresponds to the insertion of conical defects on the surface. In this case,
p_{i}^{2}<0
, and the operators correspond to the Fuchsian group’s elliptic elements. For these singularities, we define
\kappa_{i}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=-\mathrm{i}p_{i}
, which is related to the deficit angle
\theta
of the conical defect by
\theta_{i}=4\pi\kappa_{i}\in[0,\pi)
.

It is, in principle, possible to consider a different value for each momentum or even consider general cases with mixed types of singularities, including boundaries, punctures, and conical defects; however, for simplicity, we focus on the case where all the external Liouville momenta are equal
p_{1}=p_{2}=p_{3}=p_{4}=p_{\circ}
(or
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=\kappa_{3}=\kappa_{4}=\kappa_{\bullet}
for conical defects). The conformal block decomposition of such a four-point function on the sphere is

\expectationvalue{V_{\alpha_{\circ}}(\infty) V_{\alpha_{\circ}}(1) V_{\alpha_{\circ}}(x,\conj{x}) V_{\alpha_{\circ}}(0)}=\int_{\mathbb{S}_{Q}}\mathrm{d}\beta\,C(\alpha_{\circ},\alpha_{\circ},\beta)C(Q-\beta,\alpha_{\circ},\alpha_{\circ})\mathcal{F}_{\circ}(\beta;x)\mathcal{F}_{\circ}(\beta;\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mux\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu),

(5.2)

where
\mathcal{F}_{\circ}(\beta;x)
is the four-point Virasoro block corresponding to the insertion of four primaries with dimension
\Delta_{\circ}=\alpha_{\circ}(Q-\alpha_{\circ})
.

We first compute the semiclassical limit of the three-point structure coefficients and the conformal blocks. Performing a saddle-point analysis on the conformal block expansion allows us to compute the geodesic length of the cut, using which we compute the volumes of the moduli spaces of these surfaces and compare them to the volume polynomials. Following our convention in equation (
5.1
), in the semiclassical limit,

\lim_{b\to 0}\expectationvalue{V_{\alpha_{\circ}}(\infty) V_{\alpha_{\circ}}(1) V_{\alpha_{\circ}}(x,\conj{x}) V_{\alpha_{\circ}}(0)}\approx\int\mathrm{d}p\,\exp(-b^{-2} S_{\circ}(p;x,\conj{x})),

(5.3)

where

S_{\circ}(p;x,\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mux\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu)=S_{3}(p_{\circ},p_{\circ},p)+r(p)+S_{3}(p,p_{\circ},p_{\circ})-f_{\circ}(p;x)-f_{\circ}(p;\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mux\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu).

(5.4)

The semiclassical blocks
f_{\circ}(p;x)
that we consider in this setup correspond to the semiclassical limit of
\mathcal{F}_{\circ}(\beta;x)
, where

\lim_{b\to 0}b\beta=\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{i}p.

(5.5)

One can obtain these blocks by taking the semiclassical limit of Zamolodchikov’s recursion relations up to arbitrary order in the cross-ratio. In what follows, we will use
q
-expansion of the conformal blocks. The nome
q=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\tau}
is related to the cross-ratio via equation (
2.20
).

5.1.1
Saddle-point analysis

In our computations, we first assume that the four-point function corresponds to the insertion of four primary vertex operators in Liouville theory, i.e.,
p_{\circ}^{2}>0
, corresponding to the case where the singularities are boundaries on the Riemann sphere. We then proceed to consider the case with
p_{\circ}^{2}<0
, which corresponds to the insertion of conical defects on the sphere by analytic continuation of the saddle-point equation in
p_{\circ}=\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\bullet}
. In both cases, we assume all four external momenta are equal.

The total contribution of the three-point coefficients for identical external operators with momentum
p_{\circ}
is

\hat{S}_{3}(p)=S_{3}(p_{\circ},p_{\circ},p)+r(p)+S_{3}(p,p_{\circ},p_{\circ}),

(5.6)

which can be computed using the formulas in §
B.1
. To perform the saddle-point analysis, we first compute the derivative of the three-point coefficient
\hat{S}_{3}(p)
,

\frac{\partial\hat{S}_{3}(p)}{\partial p}=2\mathrm{i}\log\frac{\Gamma^{2}\quantity(1-2\ic p)\Gamma^{2}\quantity(\frac{1}{2}+\ic p)\Gamma\quantity(\frac{1}{2}+\ic p-2\ic p_{\circ})\Gamma\quantity(\frac{1}{2}+\ic p+2\ic p_{\circ})}{\Gamma^{2}\quantity(1+2\ic p)\Gamma^{2}\quantity(\frac{1}{2}-\ic p)\Gamma\quantity(\frac{1}{2}-\ic p-2\ic p_{\circ})\Gamma\quantity(\frac{1}{2}-\ic p+2\ic p_{\circ})}-2\pi,

(5.7)

and then plug this into the saddle-point equation (
4.4
), which becomes,

\mathrm{i}\log\frac{\Gamma^{2}\quantity(1-2\ic p)\Gamma^{2}\quantity(\frac{1}{2}+\ic p)\Gamma\quantity(\frac{1}{2}+\ic p-2\ic p_{\circ})\Gamma\quantity(\frac{1}{2}+\ic p+2\ic p_{\circ})}{\Gamma^{2}\quantity(1+2\ic p)\Gamma^{2}\quantity(\frac{1}{2}-\ic p)\Gamma\quantity(\frac{1}{2}-\ic p-2\ic p_{\circ})\Gamma\quantity(\frac{1}{2}-\ic p+2\ic p_{\circ})}-\pi=\real\frac{\partial f_{\circ}(p;q)}{\partial p},

(5.8)

where the derivative of the classical block (see §
B.2
) up to
O(q^{2})
is

\frac{\partial f_{\circ}(p;q)}{\partial p}=2p\log 16q-\frac{p(1+16p_{\circ}^{2})^{2}}{2(1+p^{2})^{2}}q^{2}+O\quantity(q^4).

(5.9)

In principle, the classical block and its derivative can be computed up to arbitrary orders in
q
-expansion using Zamolodchikov’s recursion relations.

Similarly, in the case of a sphere with conical defects, the saddle-point equation becomes,

\mathrm{i}\log\frac{\Gamma^{2}\quantity(1-2\ic p)\Gamma^{2}\quantity(\frac{1}{2}+\ic p)\Gamma\quantity(\frac{1}{2}+\ic p+2\kappa_{\bullet})\Gamma\quantity(\frac{1}{2}+\ic p-2\kappa_{\bullet})}{\Gamma^{2}\quantity(1+2\ic p)\Gamma^{2}\quantity(\frac{1}{2}-\ic p)\Gamma\quantity(\frac{1}{2}-\ic p+2\kappa_{\bullet})\Gamma\quantity(\frac{1}{2}-\ic p-2\kappa_{\bullet})}-\pi=\real\frac{\partial f_{\bullet}(p;q)}{\partial p},

(5.10)

where
f_{\bullet}(p;q)
is obtained by replacing
p_{\circ}
with
\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\bullet}
in the expression for the classical block
f_{\circ}(p;q)
.

We numerically compute the saddle-point momenta

which solve equations (

) and (

) in the case of four external geodesic boundaries of momentum

and cone points of momentum

, respectively. We plot the results for

for different values cross-ratio and of

and

5.1.2
Comparison with exact results

For particular values of the external momenta and cross-ratios, the Fuchsian construction of the corresponding hyperbolic surface is known explicitly. In these cases, we can compare our numerical results for the saddle-point momentum to the exact value obtained by taking the trace of appropriate elements of the corresponding Fuchsian group. Here, we would like to showcase two of these cases as examples.

Sphere with four punctures

First, for the four-punctured sphere (
p_{\circ}=0
or equivalently
\kappa_{\bullet}=0
) with cross-ratio
x=\frac{1}{2}
corresponding to
q=\mathrm{e}^{-\pi}
, the Fuchsian group is known explicitly
[
33
]
. The four generators are

\upgamma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
2&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad\upgamma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}3&-4\\
4&-5\end{pmatrix},\quad\upgamma_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}3&-8\\
2&-5\end{pmatrix},\quad\upgamma_{4}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-4\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(5.11)

Using these generators, we can find the analytic value of the saddle-point momentum
p^{\mathrm{s}}
to be

p^{\mathrm{s}}=\frac{l}{4\pi}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\arccosh{\frac{\absolutevalue{\Tr\ug_1 \ug_2}}{2}}\approx 0.28055.

(5.12)

In this case, which has been studied before in the literature
[
6
,
7
]
, the current method yields

\begin{split}O\quantity(q):\quad p^{\mathrm{s}}&\approx 0.28057,\\
O\quantity(q^2):\quad p^{\mathrm{s}}&\approx 0.28055,\\
O\quantity(q^4):\quad p^{\mathrm{s}}&\approx 0.28055.\end{split}

(5.13)

Here
O(q^{n})
refers to the order
n
of the
q
-expansion for the classical blocks. Comparing the result with the analytic value, we find the error,

\Delta_{l}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{\cosh(2\pi p^{\rms})}{\cosh\frac{l}{2}}-1,

(5.14)

to be

\begin{split}O\quantity(q):\quad\Delta_{l}&\approx 1.3\times 10^{-4},\\
O\quantity(q^2):\quad\Delta_{l}&\approx 1.2\times 10^{-7},\\
O\quantity(q^4):\quad\Delta_{l}&\approx 1.1\times 10^{-11}.\end{split}

(5.15)

Sphere with four conical defects

Now we consider the sphere with four conical singularities, each with a deficit angle of
\frac{2\pi}{3}
. This particular surface is intricately connected to a genus two construct, which can be realized through the following algebraic curve equation:

y^{2}=z^{6}-1,

(5.16)

with a corresponding period matrix
[
34
]
given by

\Omega=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\
-1&2\end{pmatrix}.

(5.17)

The Fuchsian generators for this genus two surface are

T_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}1+2\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3mu\omega\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu&-\omega^{k-1}\sqrt{6}\\

(5.18)

T_{4}^{-1}T_{5}T_{4}T_{5}^{-1}T_{1}^{-1}T_{2}T_{1}T_{2}^{-1}=1,

(5.19)

which is the usual construction of a genus two surface by gluing the sides of an octagon together. This also means that the collection
\{T_{1},T_{2},T_{4},T_{5}\}
correspond to the canonical
\alpha
and
\beta

5.16
). Each of the
T_{i}
constructed here satisfies
\absolutevalue{\Tr T_i}=4
, which means that the length of each cycle is
l=2\arccosh{2}
.

The same genus two surface can be represented as a three-fold cover of the Riemann sphere defined as

y^{3}=\frac{z(z-1)}{z-\frac{1}{2}},

(5.20)

which exhibits four conical defects with a deficit angle of
\theta_{\bullet}=\frac{2\pi}{3}
(or equivalently,
\kappa_{\bullet}=\frac{1}{6}
) at cross-ratio

5.17
). In this representation, a Fuchsian generator

in equation (

\begin{split}O\quantity(q):\quad p^{\mathrm{s}}&\approx 0.209605,\\
O\quantity(q^2):\quad p^{\mathrm{s}}&\approx 0.209600,\\
O\quantity(q^4):\quad p^{\mathrm{s}}&\approx 0.209600,\end{split}

(5.23)

5.2.1
Saddle-point analysis

Similar to the previous example, in order to solve the saddle-point equations, we need to compute the derivatives of the three-point coefficients and the conformal blocks with respect to the exchange momenta. The total contribution of three-point coefficients is

,
p_{2}
, and
