Abstract
. One the one hand, rough volatility has been shown to provide a consistent framework to capture the properties of stock price dynamics both under the historical measure and for pricing purposes. On the other hand, market price of volatility risk is a well-studied object in Financial Economics, and empirical estimates show it to be stochastic rather than deterministic. Starting from a rough volatility model under the historical measure, we take up this challenge and provide an analysis of the impact of such a non-deterministic risk for pricing purposes.

Introduction

Rough volatility is a recent paradigm proposed by Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum
[
10
]
,
which has attracted the attention of many academics and practitioners thanks to its numerous attractive properties.
Despite some debate about whether volatility should be rough
[
12
,
13
,
17
,
16
,
23
]
,
this class of models provides a general framework to analyse
both time series of the instantaneous volatility (under the historical measure
\mathbb{P}
)
and prices of financial derivatives (under the pricing measure
\mathbb{Q}
).
Starting from a rough version of the Bergomi model
[
5
]
under
\mathbb{P}
,
Bayer, Friz and Gatheral
[
3
]
showed that a deterministic market price of risk
preserved its structure under
\mathbb{Q}
(somehow akin to the Heston model
[
14
]
specification).

However, the Financial Economics literature has long shown that this market price of risk,
monitoring the transition from
\mathbb{P}
to
\mathbb{Q}
via Girsanov’s transform,
is not constant nor deterministic but instead stochastic.
Its estimation has been the source of long academic discussions,
outside the scope of the present paper though,
and we refer the interested reader to
[
1
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
19
,
21
]
for some useful pointers.
This of course has serious practicals implications for risk management,
and
[
11
,
24
]
are fascinating sources of information.
We focus here on this particular bridge between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}

and show, not surprisingly, that the required stochasticity of the market price of risk unfortunately breaks the structure of the rough Bergomi model under
\mathbb{Q}
.
However, we link the Hölder regularity of the volatility process (lower in this class of rough models) with that of the change of measure,
and design several specifications making the model tractable under
\mathbb{Q}
.
While the rough Bergomi model tracks well the behaviour of the historical volatility,
it is less powerful for option prices, especially when considering VIX smiles
(which are more or less flat under this model).
Our new setup allows for more flexibility there, while preserving the
\mathbb{P}
-tractability of the model.

Section
1
provides the technical setup and analysis of the market price of risk,
while the design of useful continuous-time rough stochastic volatility models with non-deterministic market prices of risk is detailed in Section
2
.
Finally, in Section
3
, we perform an empirical analysis,
estimating risk premia from historical options data.

1.
Rough volatility models and change of measure

Rough volatility models are a natural extension of classical stochastic volatility models.
Starting from such a model under the historical measure
\mathbb{P}
, we characterise below its dynamics under equivalent martingale measures
\mathbb{Q}\sim\mathbb{P}
,
which then, by the fundamental theorem of asset pricing, allows for arbitrage-free option pricing.
Following for example
[
4
,
10
]
we consider a rather general class of (rough) stochastic volatility model under
\mathbb{P}
,
where the stock price process admits the following dynamics:

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S_{t}}{S_{t}}&=\displaystyle\mu_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{v_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{P}}_{t},\\
v_{t}&=\displaystyle\psi(t,Y_{t}),\\
Y_{t}&=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}k(t,s)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{P}}_{s},\end{array}\right.

starting from
S_{0}>0
,
over a fixed time interval

\mathbb{T}:=[0,T]
, for
T>0
.
Here
\boldsymbol{W}^{\mathbb{P}}=(W^{\mathbb{P}},W^{\mathbb{P},\perp})
is a two-dimensional standard Brownian motion defined on a given filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, with
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{W^{\mathbb{P}}}\vee\mathcal{F}^{W^{\mathbb{P},\perp}}
,
and
Z^{\mathbb{P}}:=\rho W^{\mathbb{P}}+\overline{\rho}W^{\mathbb{P},\perp}
,
with
\rho\in[-1,1]
and
\overline{\rho}:=\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}
.
We further introduce the
set
\mathbb{F}_{b}
of
\mathbb{P}
-bounded and
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
-progressively measurable process
and recall the Doléans-Dade stochastic exponential of a square integrable process
X
:

\mathcal{E}(X)_{t}:=\exp{\left(X_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\langle X\rangle_{t}\right)},\qquad t\in\mathbb{T}.

We finally consider the following set of assumptions,
in place for the rest of the paper:

Assumption 1.1
.

(i)

The function
\psi:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
is continuous, bounded, and bounded away from the origin on
\mathbb{T}\times(-\infty,a]
for each
a>0
;

(ii)

For each
t\in\mathbb{T}
,
\mathbb{E}\left[v_{t}^{-1}\right]
is finite;

(iii)

The process
\mu
belongs to
\mathbb{F}_{b}
;

(iv)

Given an interest rate process
r:=(r_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{T}}\in\mathbb{F}_{b}
,
there exist a sequence
(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and a process

\gamma:=(\gamma_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
in
\mathbb{F}_{b}
and bounded
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
-almost surely, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{T}}\left\{\rho\int_{0}^{t}k(t,u)\chi_{u}\mathrm{d}u\right\}\leq 0,\qquad\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\text{-almost surely},\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N},

where we introduce the Sharpe ratio
\chi_{u}:=\frac{r_{u}-\mu_{u}}{\sqrt{v_{u}}}
and

(1.2)

\frac{\mathrm{d}\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}\bigg|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}:=\mathcal{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\cdot}\chi_{u}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{P}}_{u}+\int_{0}^{\cdot}\gamma_{u}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{P},{\perp}}_{u}\right)_{t\wedge\tau_{n}}\quad\text{and}\quad\tau_{n}:=\inf\{t\geq 0,\;Y_{t}=n\};

(v)

The correlation is negative:
\rho\leq 0
;

(vi)

For each
t\in\mathbb{T}
, the kernel
k(t,\cdot)
is null on
\mathbb{T}\setminus[0,t]
and
\int_{0}^{\cdot}k(\cdot,s)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{P}}_{s}
is a well-defined Gaussian process.

Remark 1.2
.

Condition (vi) may be replaced in terms of conditions on the kernel, for example
k(t,\cdot)\in L^{2}([0,t])
for each
t\in\mathbb{T}
.
In light of (v),
the first constraint in (iv) may be rewritten as

\inf_{t\in\mathbb{T}}\left\{\int_{0}^{t}k(t,u)\chi_{u}\mathrm{d}u\right\}\geq 0
,

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\text{-almost surely}
,
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The following examples are common choices of such kernels:

Example 1.3
.

The Gamma kernel, common in the
\mathcal{BSS}
literature pioneered by Barndorff-Nielsen and Schmiegel
[
2
]
, is given by

(1.3)

k(t,s)=(t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(t-s)}1\hskip-5.97508pt{1}_{\{t\geq s\}},\qquad\text{with }H\in(0,1),\quad\beta\geq 0.

In order to state the main result, define the Radon-Nikodym derivative, for each
t\in\mathbb{T}
,

(1.4)

\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}_{t}:=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}\bigg|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathbb{P}}}=\mathcal{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\cdot}\chi_{u}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{P}}_{u}+\int_{0}^{\cdot}\gamma_{u}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{P},{\perp}}_{u}\right)_{t},

so that, from (
1.2
) above,

\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}_{t\wedge\tau_{n}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}\bigg|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}
.

Proposition 1.4
.

For any
\gamma
satisfying
Assumption
1.1
(iv),
the process
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\cdot}\chi_{u}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{P}}_{u}+\int_{0}^{\cdot}\gamma_{u}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{P},{\perp}}_{u}
is a
\mathbb{P}
-local martingale on
\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

Recall that the sum of two local martingale (with respect to the same filtration) is a local martingale whose sequence of stopping times is given by the minimum of the sequences of stopping times for the terms in the sum.
The boundedness of
\gamma
implies that
\int_{0}^{\cdot}\gamma_{s}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{P},{\perp}}_{s}
is a true
\mathbb{P}
-martingale.
Regarding
\int_{0}^{\cdot}\chi_{u}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{P}}_{u}
,
the integrand is
\mathcal{F}_{s}
-measurable and locally bounded with respect to the sequence of stopping times
(\tau_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in (
1.2
) thanks to Assumption
1.1
(i)-(iii)-(iv).
Exploiting Assumption
1.1
(ii)-(iii)-(iv), then
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\chi_{t}^{2}]
is finite for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
.
Thus, the first term in the sum is a local martingale as well,
and so is the whole sum.
∎

Proposition
1.4
justifies the use of a Doléans-Dade stochastic exponential in the definition of
\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}
. Furthermore, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 1.5
.

Under Assumption
1.1

\gamma

as in Assumption
1.1

(I)

the Radon-Nikodym derivative process

\mathbb{Q}
-martingale;

(II)

under the (arbitrage-free) equivalent risk-neutral martingale measure
\mathbb{Q}
,

(1.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S_{t}}{S_{t}}&=\displaystyle r_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{v_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t},\\
v_{t}&=\displaystyle\psi\left(t,\widehat{Y}_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}k(t,s)\lambda_{s}\mathrm{d}s\right),\\
\widehat{Y_{t}}&=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}k(t,s)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s},\end{array}\right.

with
S_{0},v_{0}>0
, and
\lambda
is the market price of volatility risk defined by

(1.6)

\lambda_{t}:=\rho\chi_{t}+\overline{\rho}\;\gamma_{t},

and where
W^{\mathbb{Q}}
and
Z^{\mathbb{Q}}
are
\mathbb{Q}
-Brownian motions defined as

(1.7)

W^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t}:=\displaystyle W^{\mathbb{P}}_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\chi_{u}\mathrm{d}u,\qquad\text{and}\qquad Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t}:=Z_{t}^{\mathbb{P}}+\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{u}\mathrm{d}u;

(III)

the discounted stock price
\widetilde{S}_{t}:=\frac{S_{t}}{B_{t}}
with
\mathrm{d}B_{t}=r_{t}B_{t}\mathrm{d}t
,
B_{0}=1
, is a true
\mathbb{Q}
-martingale.

Proof.

To satisfy the no-arbitrage conditions, the change of measure for
W^{\mathbb{P}}
is constrained by the martingale restriction on the discounted spot dynamics,
while the Brownian motion
Z^{\mathbb{P}}
gives freedom to the model and makes the market incomplete by the free choice of the process
\gamma
.
Consequently, the change of measure from
\mathbb{P}
to
\mathbb{Q}
and the corresponding Radon-Nikodym derivative directly follow from Girsanov’s Theorem via (
1.4
), provided that
\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}_{t}\in L^{1}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}
is a true martingale.
Thus, once we have shown (I),
then (II) automatically follows.
By Proposition
1.4
,
\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}_{t}\in L^{1}
and, being a non-negative local martingale,
it is a supermartingale, and a true martingale on

if and only if
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}_{T}\right]=1
.
To prove this, we closely follow
[
9

For any

\mathbb{P}
-bounded on

The first term in (

1.1.
Characterisation of rough volatility models via Generalised Fractional Operators

It is natural to represent rough volatility models in terms of fractional operators. In this section we present the Generalised Fractional Operators (GFO) and a representation result for rough volatility models in terms of GFO. To do so, let us first present the GFO introduced in
[
15
, Definition 1.1]
defined as follows.

Definition 1.6
.

For any
\beta\in(0,1)
,
\alpha\in(-\beta,1-\beta)
and
h\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{b}((0,\infty))
such that
h^{\prime}(\cdot)\leq 0
,
the GFO associated to the kernel
k(x):=x^{\alpha}h(x)
applied to
f\in C^{\beta}(\mathbb{R})
is defined as

(1.14)

(\mathcal{G}^{\alpha}f)(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(f(s)-f(0))\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}k(t-s)\mathrm{d}s,&\text{if }\alpha\in[0,1-\beta),\\
\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{0}^{t}(f(s)-f(0))k(t-s)\mathrm{d}s,&\text{if }\alpha\in(-\beta,0).\end{array}\right.

To simplify future notations,
we let
H_{\pm}:=H\pm\frac{1}{2}

for
H\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
.
We now introduce a specific setup that will drive the rest of our computations:
consider the power-law kernel

(1.15)

\mathtt{k}_{\alpha}(u):=u^{\alpha}1\hskip-5.97508pt{1}_{\{u\geq 0\}},

as well as the set

(1.16)

\Lambda_{\beta,H}:=\Big\{\lambda\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}\text{ for some $\beta\in(0,1]$ such that $H_{-}\in(-\beta,0)$ and $\lambda_{0}=0$}\Big\}.

To this particular power-law kernel, the GFO
(from Definition
1.6
, since
H_{-}\in(-\frac{1}{2},0)
) reads

(\mathcal{G}^{H_{-}}f)(t)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{0}^{t}(f(s)-f(0))\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-s)\mathrm{d}s.

Denote further

\mathtt{K}(t,s):=\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(u-s)\mathrm{d}u=\frac{\mathtt{k}_{H_{+}}(t-s)}{H_{+}},

so that the corresponding GFO is precisely
\frac{1}{H_{+}}\mathcal{G}^{H_{+}}
.
To streamline notations and emphasise nice symmetries, we introduce the notations

(1.17)

\mathfrak{G}^{-}:=\mathcal{G}^{H_{-}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathfrak{G}^{+}:=\frac{1}{H_{+}}\mathcal{G}^{H_{+}}.

From the properties of GFO
[
15
, Proposition 1.2]
,
then
\mathfrak{G}^{+}\lambda\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta+H_{+}}

as soon as
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\beta,H}
.

Corollary 1.7

(GFO representation of rough volatility)
.

With the kernel
\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}
in (
1.15
) and
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\beta,H}
,
the system (
1.5
) under the risk-neutral measure
\mathbb{Q}
can be rewritten as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S_{t}}{S_{t}}&=\displaystyle r_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{v_{t}}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t},\\
v_{t}&=\displaystyle\psi\Big(t,(\mathfrak{G}^{-}Z^{\mathbb{Q}})(t)+(\mathfrak{G}^{+}\lambda)(t)\Big).\end{array}\right.

Proof.

The fact that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\cdot}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(\cdot-s)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}=\mathfrak{G}^{-}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}

is straightforward by the properties of GFO in
[
15
, Proposition 1.4]
.
Furthermore, for any
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\beta,H}
and any
t\in\mathbb{T}
,

\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-s)\lambda_{s}\mathrm{d}s=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathtt{K}(t,s)(\lambda_{s}-\lambda_{0})\mathrm{d}s=\left(\mathfrak{G}^{+}\lambda\right)(t).

∎

Note that since
\mathfrak{G}^{+}\lambda\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta+H_{+}}
, then the risk premium has sample paths with Hölder regularity greater than
\frac{1}{2}
, regardless of the value of
H
.

2.
Modelling the risk premium process: A practical approach

In practice, the process
\lambda
is directly modelled without resorting to a change of measure starting from
\gamma
.
We now consider different modelling choices for the risk premium
\lambda
and analyse some of its properties.
In spite of the formal derivation of Theorem
1.5
, a numerical treatment of the integral
\int_{0}^{t}\bullet\;\mathrm{d}s
is rather intricate. To overcome this issue, Bayer, Friz and Gatheral
[
3
]
elegantly came up with the forward variance form of rough volatility in the spirit of Bergomi
[
5
]
.
We shall restrict ourselves to this functional form (defined in the following proposition) for the reminder of the section.
Consider (
1.5
) with
\psi(t,x)=\xi_{0}(t)\mathrm{e}^{\nu x}
,
with
\xi_{0}(t):=\mathbb{E}[v_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{0}]
and
\nu>0
.
Then the risk-neutral dynamics in forward variance form read

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S_{t}}{S_{t}}&=\displaystyle r_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{v_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t},\\
v_{t}&=\displaystyle\xi_{0}(t)\exp\left(\nu\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-s)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-s)\lambda_{s}\mathrm{d}s\right)\right).\end{array}\right.

In the remaining of this section,
the process
X^{\mathbb{Q}}
will denote a
\mathbb{Q}
-Brownian motion possibly correlated
with
W^{\mathbb{Q}}
and
Z^{\mathbb{Q}}
.

2.1.
Risk premium driven by Itô diffusion

Generalised Fractional Operators provide a natural framework to model risk premium processes driven by diffusions.
The statement below shows the details of such a construction.
Recall that the Beta function is defined as

\mathfrak{B}(x,y):=\int_{0}^{1}s^{x-1}(1-s)^{y-1}\mathrm{d}s
, for
x,y>0
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For
H\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and

\alpha\in\left(-\frac{1}{2},0\right)
,
consider

\lambda=\mathfrak{b}\mathcal{G}^{\alpha}Y^{\mathbb{Q}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}
,
where
\mathfrak{b}:=\mathfrak{B}(H_{+},\alpha+1)^{-1}

and

Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}b(s,Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s})\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s})\mathrm{d}X^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s},

where
b(\cdot)
and
\sigma(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfy Yamada-Watanabe conditions
[
20
, Section 5.2, Proposition 2.13]

for pathwise uniqueness ensuring that a weak solution exists.
Then
\mathcal{G}^{\alpha+H_{+}}Y^{\mathbb{Q}}\in\mathcal{C}^{H+\alpha+1}
and

(2.2)

v_{t}=\xi_{0}(t)\exp\Big\{\nu\left((\mathfrak{G}^{-}Z^{\mathbb{Q}})(t)+(\mathcal{G}^{\alpha+H_{+}}Y^{\mathbb{Q}})(t)\right)\Big\}.

Furthermore, if
Y^{\mathbb{Q}}=X^{\mathbb{Q}}
and
\mathrm{d}\langle Y^{\mathbb{Q}},Z^{\mathbb{Q}}\rangle_{t}=\rho\,\mathrm{d}t
with
\rho\leq 0
, then

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[v_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]

\displaystyle=\xi_{0}(t)\exp\Big\{\nu\left[\left(\mathfrak{G}^{-}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(s,t)+\left(\mathcal{G}^{\alpha+H_{+}}X^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(s,t)\right]\Big\}

(2.4)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left\{\frac{\nu^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{\mathtt{k}_{2H}(t-s)}{2H}+\frac{\mathtt{k}_{2(H+1)}(t-s)}{2H_{+}^{2}(H+1)}+\rho\frac{\mathtt{k}_{2H_{+}}(t-s)}{H_{+}^{2}}\right)\right\},

where

\left(\mathcal{G}^{H_{-}}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(s,t):=\int_{0}^{s}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}
for
0\leq s\leq t
,
and similarly for
\left(\mathcal{G}^{\alpha+H_{+}}X^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(s,t)
.

Proof.

We first prove (
2.2
),
which follows from
[
15
, Proposition 1.2]
and the identities highlighted above in Corollary
1.7
.
Indeed, in view of Corollary
1.7
we only need to show that

\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-s)\lambda_{s}\mathrm{d}s=(\mathcal{G}^{\alpha+H_{+}}Y^{\mathbb{Q}})(t).

Replacing the expression for
\lambda
in the integral and using stochastic Fubini theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-s)\lambda_{s}\mathrm{d}s=\mathfrak{b}\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-s)(\mathcal{G}^{\alpha}Y^{\mathbb{Q}})(s)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{b}\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-s)\int_{0}^{s}\mathtt{k}_{\alpha}(s-u)\mathrm{d}Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{b}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{u}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-s)\mathtt{k}_{\alpha}(s-u)\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}.

Now, direct computations for the inner integral yields

\int_{u}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-s)\mathtt{k}_{\alpha}(s-u)\mathrm{d}s=\mathtt{k}_{\alpha+H_{+}}(t-u)\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)^{H_{-}}s^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}s=\mathfrak{B}(\alpha+1,H_{+})\mathtt{k}_{\alpha+H_{+}}(t-u).

Therefore

\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-s)\lambda_{s}\mathrm{d}s=\mathfrak{b}\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{B}(\alpha+1,H_{+})\mathtt{k}_{\alpha+H_{+}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{\alpha+H_{+}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}=(\mathcal{G}^{\alpha+H_{+}}Y^{\mathbb{Q}})(t).

We now move to the proof of the identity (
2.3
).
Exploiting the representation of
v_{t}
in this specific case and the measurability and independence properties of the Brownian increments,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[v_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]

\displaystyle=\xi_{0}(t)\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}\left[\exp\left\{\nu\left[\left(\mathcal{G}^{H_{-}}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(t)+\left(\mathcal{G}^{\alpha+H_{+}}X^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(t)\right]\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\xi_{0}(t)\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}\left[\exp\left\{\nu\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{\alpha+H_{+}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}X^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}\right]\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\xi_{0}(t)\exp\left\{\nu\left[\int_{0}^{s}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}+\int_{0}^{s}\mathtt{k}_{\alpha+H_{+}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}X^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}\left[\exp\left\{\nu\left[\int_{s}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}+\int_{s}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{\alpha+H_{+}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}X^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}\right]\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\xi_{0}(t)\exp\left\{\nu\left[\int_{0}^{s}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}+\int_{0}^{s}\mathtt{k}_{\alpha+H_{+}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}X^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\exp\left\{\frac{\nu^{2}}{2}\left(\int_{s}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{2H_{-}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}u+\int_{s}^{t}\frac{\mathtt{k}_{2H+1}(t-u)}{H_{+}^{2}}\mathrm{d}u+\rho\int_{s}^{t}\frac{\mathtt{k}_{2H}(t-u)}{H_{+}}\mathrm{d}u\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\xi_{0}(t)\exp\left\{\nu\left[\int_{0}^{s}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}+\int_{0}^{s}\mathtt{k}_{\alpha+H_{+}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}X^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\exp\left\{\frac{\nu^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{\mathtt{k}_{2H}(t-s)}{2H}+\frac{\mathtt{k}_{2(H+1)}(t-s)}{2H_{+}^{2}(H+1)}+\rho\frac{\mathtt{k}_{2H_{+}}(t-s)}{H_{+}^{2}}\right)\right\}.

Thus we only have to show that

\left(\mathcal{G}^{H_{-}}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(s,t)=\int_{0}^{s}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left(\mathcal{G}^{\alpha+H_{+}}X^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(s,t)=\int_{0}^{s}\mathtt{k}_{\alpha+H_{+}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}X^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}.

We prove the first identity, the second being analogous.
It is a straightforward consequence of the definitions and the properties of Brownian increments:

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{G}^{H_{-}}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(s,t):=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{s}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}+\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}\left[\int_{s}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{s}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-u)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}.

∎

Remark 2.2
.

Since the instantaneous variance in this model is log-Normal, the results in
[
18
, Proposition 3.1]
and numerical methods therein still apply for the VIX with minimal changes.

2.2.
A risk premium driven by a CIR process

A second natural choice is to consider the Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) process

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}=\kappa(\theta-Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s})\mathrm{d}s+\sigma\sqrt{Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}}\ \mathrm{d}X^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s},

with
\kappa,\theta,\sigma>0
.
As tempting as this approach might seem, it is not trivial at all to compute the basic quantity
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[v_{t}]
here,
as the following proposition shows.

Proposition 2.3
.

Assume that the Brownian motions
Z^{\mathbb{Q}}
and
X^{\mathbb{Q}}
are independent and consider

\lambda=\mathcal{G}^{\alpha}Y^{\mathbb{Q}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}
, with
Y^{\mathbb{Q}}
defined in (
2.5
).
Then, for any
s\leq t
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s}^{\mathbb{Q}}[v_{t}]=\xi_{0}(t)

\displaystyle\exp\Big\{\nu\left[\left(\mathfrak{G}^{-}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(s,t)+\left(\mathcal{G}^{\alpha+H_{+}}Y^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(s,t)\right]\Big\}

\displaystyle\exp\left\{\frac{\nu^{2}}{2}\int_{s}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-u)^{2}\mathrm{d}u-Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}C(s,T)-A(s,T)\right\},

where
A(t,T):=-\kappa\theta\int_{t}^{T}C(u,T)\mathrm{d}u
and
C
satisfies the Riccati equation

\nu\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(T,t)-\partial_{t}C(t,T)+C(t,T)\theta+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}C^{2}(t,T)=0,

for
t\in[0,T)
,
with boundary condition
C(T,T)=0
.

Proof.

By independence of the driving Brownian motions we have,
for any
u\leq t
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[v_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{u}]

\displaystyle=\xi_{0}(t)\exp\left\{\nu\left(\left(\mathfrak{G}^{-}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(u,t)+\left(\mathcal{G}^{\alpha+H_{+}}Y^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(u,t)\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\Big\{\nu\left(\left(\mathfrak{G}^{-}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(t)-\left(\mathfrak{G}^{-}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(u,t)\right)\Big\}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\Big\{\nu\left(\left(\mathcal{G}^{\alpha+H_{+}}Y^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(t)-\left(\mathcal{G}^{\alpha+H_{+}}Y^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(u,t)\right)\Big\}\right],

where the first expected value is the MGF of a Gaussian random variable, hence

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\Big\{\nu\Big(\left(\mathfrak{G}^{-}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(t)-\left(\mathfrak{G}^{-}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(u,t)\Big)\Big\}\right]=\exp\left\{\frac{\nu^{2}}{2}\int_{u}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,s)\mathrm{d}s\right\}.

We are now interested in computing the second expectation

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\Big\{\nu\left(\left(\mathcal{G}^{\alpha+H_{+}}Y^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(t)-\left(\mathcal{G}^{\alpha+H_{+}}Y^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(u,t)\right)\Big\}\right],

where

\left(\mathcal{G}^{\alpha+H_{+}}Y^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(t)-\left(\mathcal{G}^{\alpha+H_{+}}Y^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)(u,t)=\int_{u}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,s)Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}\mathrm{d}s.

This is, in spirit, similar to computing a bond price in the CIR model. To do so, define

B(t,T):=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\nu\int_{t}^{T}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(T,s)Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}\mathrm{d}s\right)\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right],

where
t\leq T
. We note that
B(\cdot,T)
is a semimartingale as
T
is fixed, therefore applying the conditional version of Feynman-Kac’s formula, we obtain

(2.6)

\left(\nu r\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(T,t)+\partial_{t}+\kappa(\theta-y)\partial_{r}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}r\partial_{yy}\right)\mathcal{B}(y,t,T)=0.

where
\mathcal{B}(y,t,T)
is such that
\mathcal{B}(Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t},t,T)
is a solution to the moment generating function.
With an ansatz of the type
\mathcal{B}(y,t,T)=\exp\{-yC(t,T)-A(t,T)\}
, we have at
(y,t,T)
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{B}(y,t,T)=-(y\partial_{t}C(t,T)+\partial_{t}A(t,T))\mathcal{B}(y,t,T),

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\mathcal{B}(y,t,T)=-C(t,T)\mathcal{B}(y,t,T),\qquad\partial_{yy}\mathcal{B}(y,t,T)=C(t,T)^{2}\mathcal{B}(y,t,T),

and the PDE (
2.6
) becomes, with
r=Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t}
,

\left(\nu Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(T,t)-\left(Y^{\mathbb{Q}}\partial_{t}C+\partial_{t}A+\kappa\left(\theta-Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t}\right)C\right)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}C^{2}Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t}\right)\mathcal{B}(Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t},t,T)=0,

which further simplifies to

\left(\nu\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(T,t)-\partial_{t}C-\kappa C+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}C^{2}\right)Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t}\mathcal{B}(Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t},t,T)-(\kappa\theta C+\partial_{t}A)\mathcal{B}(Y^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t},t,T)=0.

The second term cancels when

A(t,T)=-\kappa\theta\int_{t}^{T}C(u,T)\mathrm{d}t
,
and a Riccati equation remains:

\nu\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(T,t)-\partial_{t}C(t,T)-\kappa C(t,T)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}C^{2}(t,T)=0,

with boundary conditions
A(T,T)=C(T,T)=0
.
∎

Already in the uncorrelated case the computation of
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[v_{t}]
becomes very costly, having to solve a PDE for each time
t
.
In the correlated case there is no hope to obtain any semi-analytic result since one would need to compute cross terms and there is no tool coming from Itô’s calculus available in that case.

3.
Roughly extracting the Risk Premium from the Market

In this section we consider the risk premium process
\lambda
to be deterministic, with the aim of obtaining a formula that links
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
market observable quantities.
The following theorem shows how to infer the risk premium from the market using forecasts
under the physical measure and Variance Swap prices in the pricing measure.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider the rough volatility model (
1.1
) under
\mathbb{P}
.
If
\psi(t,x)=\xi_{0}(t)\mathrm{e}^{\nu x}
,

\mu_{s}=r_{s}
for all
s\geq 0
and

\left(\lambda_{s}\right)_{s\geq 0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is deterministic, then

(3.1)

\nu\overline{\rho}\int_{s}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,u)\gamma_{u}\mathrm{d}u=\log\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[v_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]}{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[v_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]}\right)=\log\left(\frac{\xi_{s}(t)}{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[v_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]}\right).

Proof.

If
\mu=r
almost surely, the Radon-Nikodym derivative (
1.4
) in Theorem
1.5
reads

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}=\mathcal{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\cdot}\gamma_{s}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{P}\perp}_{s}\right),

where we recall
\lambda_{s}=\overline{\rho}\gamma_{s}
, and the inverse Radon-Nikodym derivative is given by

(3.3)

\displaystyle\reflectbox{$\mathcal{D}$}^{\gamma}:=\frac{1}{\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}}=\mathcal{E}\left(-\int_{0}^{\cdot}\gamma_{s}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{Q}\perp}_{s}\right).

Then, the conditional change of measure formula yields

(3.4)

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[v_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]=\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[v_{t}\reflectbox{$\mathcal{D}$}^{\gamma}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]}{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\reflectbox{$\mathcal{D}$}^{\gamma}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]}.

On the one hand,
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\reflectbox{$\mathcal{D}$}^{\gamma}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]=\mathcal{E}\left(-\int_{0}^{\cdot}\gamma_{u}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{Q}\perp}_{u}\right)_{s}

by the properties of the stochastic exponential and Gaussian moment generating functions.
On the other hand, since, for
t\in\mathbb{T}
,
Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t}=Z^{\mathbb{P}}_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{s}\mathrm{d}s
and
\lambda
is deterministic, then

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[v_{t}\reflectbox{$\mathcal{D}$}^{\gamma}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\left.\exp\left\{\nu\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,s)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{s}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,s)\mathrm{d}s\right)\right\}\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{t}\gamma_{s}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{Q}\perp}_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\gamma_{s}^{2}\mathrm{d}s}\right|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]

(3.6)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\nu\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{s}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,s)\mathrm{d}s-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\gamma_{s}^{2}\mathrm{d}s}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\left.\exp\left\{\nu\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,s)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}-\int_{0}^{t}\gamma_{s}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{Q}\perp}_{s}\right\}\right|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right],

where the second factor in the last term is just the conditional moment generating function of a Gaussian random variable.
Applying Itô’s isometry then, conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{s}
,
the random variable

\nu\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,s)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}-\int_{0}^{t}\gamma_{s}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{Q}\perp}_{s}
is distributed as
\mathcal{N}\left(\mu,\sigma^{2}\right)

with

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle:=\nu\int_{0}^{s}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,u)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}-\int_{0}^{s}\gamma_{u}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{Q}\perp}_{u},

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle:=\nu^{2}\int_{s}^{t}k^{2}(t,u)\mathrm{d}u+\int_{s}^{t}\gamma_{u}^{2}\mathrm{d}u-2\nu\overline{\rho}\int_{s}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,u)\gamma_{u}\mathrm{d}u,

since
Z^{\mathbb{Q}}=\rho W^{\mathbb{Q}}+\overline{\rho}W^{\mathbb{Q}\perp}
.
Exploiting the identities above and reordering terms,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[v_{t}\reflectbox{$\mathcal{D}$}^{\gamma}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]\

\displaystyle=\exp\Bigg\{\nu\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,s)\lambda_{s}\mathrm{d}s+\overbrace{\nu\int_{0}^{s}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,u)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}-\int_{0}^{s}\gamma_{u}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{Q}\perp}_{u}}^{\mu}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\Bigg(\underbrace{\nu^{2}\int_{s}^{t}k^{2}(t,u)\mathrm{d}u+\int_{s}^{t}\gamma_{u}^{2}\mathrm{d}u-2\nu\overline{\rho}\int_{s}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,u)\gamma_{u}\mathrm{d}u}_{\sigma^{2}}-\int_{0}^{t}\gamma_{s}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\Bigg)\Bigg\}

\displaystyle=\exp\Bigg\{-\nu\overline{\rho}\int_{s}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,u)\gamma_{u}\mathrm{d}u\Bigg\}\overbrace{\exp\Bigg\{-\int_{0}^{s}\gamma_{u}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{Q}\perp}_{u}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{s}\gamma_{u}^{2}\mathrm{d}u\Bigg\}}^{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\reflectbox{$\mathcal{D}$}^{\gamma}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]}

\displaystyle\qquad\underbrace{+\exp\Bigg\{\nu\int_{0}^{s}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,u)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}+\frac{\nu^{2}}{2}\int_{s}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}^{2}(t,u)\mathrm{d}u+\nu\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,s)\lambda_{s}\mathrm{d}s\Bigg\}}_{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[v_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]},

by using the decomposition of
\sigma^{2}
as the sum of three terms, and so

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[v_{t}\reflectbox{$\mathcal{D}$}^{\gamma}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\reflectbox{$\mathcal{D}$}^{\gamma}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[v_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]\exp\left\{-\nu\overline{\rho}\int_{s}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,u)\gamma_{u}\mathrm{d}u\right\}.

Finally, going back to (
3.4
) and exploiting the identity in (
3.8
),
the result follows from

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[v_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[v_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]\exp\left\{-\nu\overline{\rho}\int_{s}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,u)\gamma_{u}\mathrm{d}u\right\}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[v_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]\exp\left\{-\nu\int_{s}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,u)\lambda_{u}\mathrm{d}u\right\}.

∎

3.1.
Estimating the risk premium in rough Bergomi

In this section we work with the
rough Bergomi model under
\mathbb{P}
and its
\mathbb{Q}
-version:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S_{t}}{S_{t}}&=\displaystyle\mu_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{v_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{P}}_{t},\\
v_{t}&=\displaystyle\exp\left\{\nu Z^{H}_{t}\right\},\\
\end{array}\right.\quad\text{vs}\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S_{t}}{S_{t}}&=\displaystyle r_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{v_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t},\\
v_{t}&=\displaystyle\xi_{0}(t)\exp\left\{\nu\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-s)\mathrm{d}Z^{\mathbb{Q}}_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t-s)\lambda_{s}\mathrm{d}s\right)\right\},\end{array}\right.

Assuming
\lambda
deterministic, Theorem
3.1
gives an explicit procedure to compute the risk premium.
In practice however, we are only able to observe variance swap quotes in discrete times, and hence it is natural to consider
\lambda
piecewise constant.

Assumption 3.2
.

The deterministic process
\lambda
admits the following piecewise constant representation on the time partition
\{0=T_{0}<T_{1},...,<T_{n}=T\}
, namely for
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

(3.10)

\lambda(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}1\hskip-5.97508pt{1}_{\{t\in[T_{i-1},T_{i})\}},\quad\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,n.

Similarly the forward variance
\xi_{0}
admits the following piecewise constant representation

\mathbb{E}_{0}^{\mathbb{Q}}[v_{t}]=\xi_{0}(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}1\hskip-5.97508pt{1}_{\{t\in[T_{i-1},T_{i})\}}

with
\xi_{i}\in\mathbb{R}

for
i=1,\ldots,n
,
where

\xi_{i}:=\frac{\mathfrak{V}_{T_{i}}T_{i}-\mathfrak{V}_{T_{i-1}}T_{i-1}}{T_{i}-T_{i-1}}

and
\mathfrak{V}_{T}:=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}v_{s}\mathrm{d}s\right]
is a market variance swap quote.

We now estimate
\{\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n}\}
.

\{\text{1M, 3M, 6M, 1Y, 2Y}\}

(Figures

and

),

(3.11)

\text{Parameters }H,\nu,\rho,\qquad\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[v_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{0}],\qquad\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[v_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{0}].

So far we have obtained
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[v_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{0}]

In order to estimate the correlation parameter we use the formula

(3.12)

\mathrm{Corr}\left(Z^{H}_{t}-Z^{H}_{s},\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{d}W_{s}\right)=\frac{\rho\sqrt{2H}}{H_{+}},

which allows us to estimate the correlation with the proxy

(3.13)

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}=\frac{\widehat{H}+\frac{1}{2}}{\sqrt{2\widehat{H}}}\ \widehat{\mathrm{Corr}}\left(\frac{\log(S_{t_{i}})-\log(S_{t_{i-1}})}{\sqrt{v_{t_{i-1}}}},\log(v_{t_{i}})-\log(v_{t_{i-1}})\right).

To forecast volatility and obtain
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[v_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{0}]
, we proceed as in
[
10
]
and use the forecasting formula for the fractional Brownian motion due to Nuzman and Poor
[
22
]
:

(3.14)

Z^{H}_{t+\Delta}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{\cos(H\pi)}{\pi}\Delta^{H_{+}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{Z^{H}_{s}\mathrm{d}s}{(t-s+\Delta)(t-s)^{H_{+}}},\frac{C_{H}\Delta^{2H}}{2H}\right).

Finally, we orderly estimate
\lambda_{i}
for each
i=1,\ldots,n
using Theorem
3.1

and the piecewise constant assumption (
3.10
), as

(3.15)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{i}\lambda_{j}\int_{T_{j-1}}^{T_{j}}\mathtt{k}_{H_{-}}(t,u)\mathrm{d}u=\frac{1}{\nu(1-\rho^{2})}\log\left(\frac{\xi_{0}(T_{i})}{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[v_{T_{i}}|\mathcal{F}_{0}]}\right).
