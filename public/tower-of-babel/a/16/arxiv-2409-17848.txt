Interplay of metallicity, ferroelectricity and layer charges in SmNiO 3 ​ / BaTiO 3 \text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{/}\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} superlattices
Edith Simmen, Nicola A. Spaldin

Abstract
Using density-functional theory, we demonstrate that the formal layer charges of the metallic samarium nickelate electrode influence the spontaneous ferroelectric polarization of the barium titanate in SmNiO 3 ​ / BaTiO 3 \text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{/}\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} capacitors. We find that, despite the metallic screening of SmNiO 3 \text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} , the spontaneous polarization of BaTiO 3 \text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} always aligns with the layer polarization of the SmNiO 3 \text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} formal charges. We also find zero critical thickness for the ferroelectricity in BaTiO 3 \text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} in this orientation. The opposite polarization direction is highly disfavored for thin BaTiO 3 \text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} slabs but becomes less unstable with increasing slab thickness. We construct a simple electrostatic model that allows us both to study the behavior for thicker BaTiO 3 \text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} and SmNiO 3 \text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} slabs and to extract the influence of various material parameters on the behavior. We mimic a metal-insulator transition in the SmNiO 3 \text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} by varying the metallic screening length, which we find influences the stability of the ferroelectric polarization. Our results show that layer charges in the metal electrodes strongly influence the properties of ferroelectric capacitors and can even provide new ways to control them.



I
Introduction

Thin-film ferroelectrics are a subject of active research both because of their fundamental interest and for their potential applications in low-energy electronic technologies
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
, such as ferroelectric tunnel junctions
[
1
,
3
]
or field-effect devices
[
3
]
. For most of these applications, the ferroelectric films are grown in a capacitor geometry with metallic electrodes. The electrode is needed to control or read out the ferroelectric state
[
2
]
and to screen interface charges that accumulate due to the spontaneous polarization
\mathbf{P}_{\text{spont}}
of the ferroelectric film. In the absence of such screening, these interface charges lead to a depolarizing field that counteracts and suppresses the polarization
[
5
]
.

While perovskite oxide metals provide good coherence with perovskite ferroelectrics, they can contain formally positively or negatively charged layers of ions. For example, the formally +3 charges of the A- and B-site cations in the rare-earth nickelates
\text{RNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
lead to a metallic phase with charged (001)
\text{RO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
and
\text{NiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{\hskip 0.90417pt--\hskip 0.90417pt}}
layers.
In their insulating states, the layer charges would lead to a polar discontinuity at the interface to a material with no or different layer charges
[
6
]
. Such a polar discontinuity has been studied in detail in the ferroelectric III-III perovskite
\text{BiFeO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, both at the surface
[
7
]
and at the interface to the II-IV
\text{SrRuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
electrode
[
8
,
9
]
. The resulting polar discontinuity strongly favors one of the two polarization directions
[
8
]
. The polarization direction minimizing the interface charges, referred to as the ‘happy’ polarization
[
7
]
, is much lower in energy than the opposite, ‘unhappy’ polarization which increases the interface charges.
In the metallic state of the rare-earth nickelates, the additional metallic screening could reduce or erase the effects of this polar discontinuity.

The interface charge
\sigma_{\text{inter}}
resulting from the polar discontinuity between two different materials is determined by the bulk polarizations
\mathbf{P}_{\text{bulk},n}
of the two materials
n
forming the interface

\sigma_{\text{inter}}=\left(\mathbf{P}_{\text{bulk,1}}-\mathbf{P}_{\text{bulk,2}}\right)\cdot\hat{n},

(1)

where
\hat{n}
is the surface normal
[
6
]
. In addition to the spontaneous polarization, the layer polarization
\mathbf{P}_{\text{layer}}
caused by the charged layers also contributes to the bulk polarization, so that
\mathbf{P}_{\text{bulk},n}=\mathbf{P}_{\text{spont},n}+\mathbf{P}_{\text{layer},n}
. The layer polarizations can be calculated from the formal charges
Z_{i}
and positions
r_{i}
of all atoms
i
in the unit cell of volume
\Omega
using
\frac{e}{\Omega}\sum_{i}r_{i}\cdot Z_{i}
; in the case of a II-IV perovskite with a (001) surface,
P_{\text{layer}}=$0\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{C}\penalty\ \mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$
, and for the same surface plane in a III-III perovskite,
P_{\text{layer}}=$\pm 50\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{C}\penalty\ \mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$

[
6
]
.

1
a illustrates the (001) interface between a representative III-III (
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
) and a II-IV (
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
) perovskite material. For centrosymmetric
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
,
P_{\text{spont}}=$0\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{C}\penalty\ \mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$
and the layer polarization in the configuration of Fig.
1
is
+$50\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{C}\penalty\ \mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$
(upper arrow) so that, considering the opposite surface normals, the
\text{NiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{\hskip 0.90417pt--\hskip 0.90417pt}}
and
\text{SmO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
surface have a respective charge of
-$50\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{C}\penalty\ \mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$
and
+$50\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{C}\penalty\ \mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$

\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
(Fig.

P_{\text{BTO}}
) and layer polarization are zero, giving zero bulk polarization and surface charges. As a result, we find
\sigma_{\text{inter}}=$-50\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{C}\penalty\ \mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$
and
+$50\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{C}\penalty\ \mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$
for the upper
\text{NiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{/}\text{BaO}

\text{SmO}\text{/}\text{TiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

If the spontaneous polarization in either material is non-zero, the interface charge will change accordingly. In particular, the polar discontinuity can be partially compensated if the spontaneous polarization in the
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
aligns with the layer polarization (middle arrow in Fig.
1

\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
opposite to the layer polarization (bottom arrow). Interface charges increase when the polarization directions in either material are inverted. For a detailed discussion of the interface charges between III-III and II-IV perovskites, see reference
[
8

In this study, we examine the effect of screening charges from metal electrodes on the polar discontinuity between
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
. The effect of layer polarization within metal electrodes on ferroelectric capacitors has been touched on in the literature, for the cases of
\text{LaNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}

[
10
,
11
,
12
]
and
\text{La}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{1\hskip 0.90417pt--\hskip 0.90417ptx\/}}}\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{x\/}}}\text{MnO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
(LSMO)
[
9
]
. In Ref.
[
10
]
, a preferred polarization direction for
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
was identified in DFT calculations of asymmetric
\text{LaNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{/}\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{/}\text{LaNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
heterostructures due to the polar discontinuity. In symmetric
\text{LaNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{/}\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
superlattices, Ref.
[
11
]
reported improved screening of the polar discontinuity at the
\text{NiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{\hskip 0.90417pt--\hskip 0.90417pt}}
/
BaO
interface using DFT, leading to stronger ferroelectricity at low thicknesses. While the internal field due to the polar discontinuity was estimated to be negligibly small compared to the experimental coercive voltages in
\text{LaNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{/}\text{PZT}
heterostructures
[
12
]
, Ref.
[
9
]
measured a shift in the hysteresis loop of PZT-LSMO heterostructures consistent with the polar discontinuity.

Motivated by these considerations, we perform a detailed study of the role of layer charges at the ferroelectric-metallic interface in ferroelectric
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
capacitors with metallic
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
electrodes containing formally charged layers.
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
is a prototypical perovskite ferroelectric with a polar, tetragonal phase at room temperature
[
13
]
. Metallic
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
is an orthorhombic perovskite with
Pbnm
symmetry
[
14
]
and has
\text{NiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{6}}}
octahedral rotations corresponding to the Glazer notation
a^{-}a^{-}c^{+}
. Like all members of the rare-earth nickelate family (except for
\text{LaNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
),
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
has a high-temperature metallic phase and undergoes a sharp transition to an insulating phase upon cooling. Importantly for practical applications, the metal-insulator transition (MIT) in
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
is just above room temperature (
400\text{\,}\mathrm{K}

[
15
]
), allowing for easier control of the MIT in the lab.

We start by investigating the effect of the formal polar discontinuity on the spontaneous polarization in
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
using density-functional theory (DFT). We confirm that the polarization in such superlattices, with charged layers within the metallic electrode, shows a clear preference for the happy orientation and is not switchable for thin slabs. In addition, we find the absence of a critical thickness in
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
for the polarization orientation minimizing the interface charges. Using an electrostatic model, we study the polar discontinuity in thicker slabs and examine the effects of the dielectric permittivity and the metallic screening length on the system. As the slab thickness increases, the unhappy orientation becomes less unstable. We find that the effect of the polar discontinuity on the ferroelectric is reduced for a smaller effective metallic screening length and a higher dielectric constant. Lastly, we discuss the physical meaning of the parameters in the electrostatic model and different ways to retrieve them from DFT.

\text{Ni}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{3+}}

\text{Sm}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{3+}}
. Additionally, the
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2\hskip 0.90417pt--\hskip 0.90417pt}}
are more electrostatically attracted to the
\text{Sm}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{3+}}
than the
\text{Ba}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2+}}
further enhancing the local interfacial polar displacement. The enhanced polar displacement in
\text{NiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
at this interface likely causes the rest of the
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
to follow suit, overcompensating for the polar discontinuity and leading to an inverse band bending. In
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{/}\text{SrRuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
superlattices, an analogous enhancement of the polarization in the first two
\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
layers due to the ion size difference at the
\text{BaO}\text{/}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
interface has been observed
[
31
]
. We will determine in the next section whether this effect is inherent to the superlattice or if it is a consequence of disabling the rotations in the
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
.

In conclusion, we find that in the absence of polarization in the
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, clear signatures of an electric field across the
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
slab are present in the calculated electronic structure, indicating the incomplete screening of the polar discontinuity by the metallic
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
.
\sigma_{\text{inter}}
is strongly reduced by the spontaneous polarization in
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
aligning parallel to the layer polarization within
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, along with additional opposite polar displacements in
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
.

III.2
Properties of superlattices with active rotations in
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}

We continue by comparing our previous results to the case in which
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
is allowed to develop octahedral rotations.
We again find a band bending in the layer-resolved DOS if we do not relax our superlattice and constrain
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
to the cubic symmetry (Fig.
4
a in black). The core states in Fig.
3
show a band bending with the same slope as for the superlattice with disabled rotations and we thus conclude that the metallic screening is the same for both cases. As for the case of disabled rotations, the band bending disappears once we fully relax the structure (green in Fig.
4
a), and a spontaneous polarization forms in
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
aligned with the layer polarization of
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
. The corresponding polar displacements of Ti and O are clearly visible in Fig.
4
b. We again relax first only the
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
slab with
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
fixed and the full structure in a second step. The polarization in the
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
slab is
20\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{C}\penalty\ \mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}
after relaxation of the
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
slab and upon full relaxation, it increases to an average polarization of
28\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{C}\penalty\ \mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}
which is very close to the bulk polarization value of
29\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{C}\penalty\ \mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}
. Overall, the response of
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
to the polar discontinuity in superlattices with rotations in the
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
is analogous to that in the superlattice with disabled rotations.

The octahedral rotations however clearly influence the polar displacements in
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
:
We find that the average polar displacement of the
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
slab reduces from
-18\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{C}\penalty\ \mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}
in superlattices with disabled rotations to
-2\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{C}\penalty\ \mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}
in superlattices with active octahedral rotations. In the latter structure, the local polar displacement is largest at the upper
\text{NiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{/}\text{BaO}
interface where the octahedral rotations are more suppressed (see Fig.
4
b), however, contrary to the superlattices with disabled rotations, the rest of the
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
does not form any considerable polar displacements. This behavior is consistent with the hardening of the polar mode that we find on the introduction of the octahedral rotations (see Fig.
8
).
Octahedral rotations and ferroelectricity are known to often not coexist and several studies have shown that if the rotations are suppressed, ferroelectricity may emerge in perovskites that are otherwise not ferroelectric
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
. Additionally, the steric effects that suppress the rotations at the
\text{NiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{/}\text{BaO}
interface enhance the polar displacements, analogous to the effect we observed in superlattices with disabled rotations.

We have thus shown that the polar discontinuity arising from
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
with active rotations results in a similar
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
polarization as in the superlattice with
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
rotations disabled and that the contribution of the metallic screening to reducing
\sigma_{\text{inter}}
is similar for
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
with and without rotations. While the behavior in
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
is similar in the two cases, ionic screening in
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
is reduced when rotations are active, and consequently, the interface charges of
\pm$20\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{C}\penalty\ \mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$
are higher.

III.3
Stability of the polar displacements

In this section, we explore two aspects of the stability of the ‘ferroelectricity’ in
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
: first, the critical thickness of the polarization for
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and second, the stability of the unhappy polarization orientation with increasing
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
slab thickness.

We begin by calculating the energy as a function of the bulk soft mode distortion for superlattices containing 1, 5, and 10 layers of
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and 4 layers of
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
. Our results are shown in Fig.
5
a. The soft mode distortion is defined to be the change in structure of bulk
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
between the high-symmetry cubic and the polar tetragonal phase. A distortion of 0 thereby corresponds to cubic
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and at the full bulk polarization, the distortion is 1. The superlattices were then constructed from a slab of
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
with different fractional soft-mode distortions and a slab of
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
with active rotations, and the energy was calculated without any further structural relaxation.

We first analyze the right-hand side of Fig.
5
a, which shows the energy as a function of soft mode distortion in the happy direction. We find a global minimum at +1 soft mode distortion for all three thicknesses down to 1 layer of
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, indicating that these superlattices have no critical thickness. Next, we construct superlattices with 1-5 layers of centrosymmetric
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and 4 layers of
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
with active rotations and subsequently relax them. Fig.
6
shows the averaged
P_{\text{loc}}
of the
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
slab (green) as a function of slab thickness. We indeed find that the averaged out-of-plane polarization is stable down to a single unit cell and maintains a polarization value close to
P_{\text{bulk}}
of
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
(grey dashed line). This is in striking contrast to the critical thickness of six unit cells found in
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
with
\text{SrRuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
electrodes which do not contain charged (001) layers
[
35
]
. Our findings are consistent with those of Ref.
[
10
]
, which reported an absence of critical thickness for
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{/}\text{LaNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
superlattices, and indicate a strong polarizing field created by the interface charges
[
36
]
.

\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}

\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
layer. The results for
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
with disabled rotations show the same trends (Fig.
A1
) and, therefore, we restrict our discussion to superlattices with active rotations in the following.

The structures of both
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
are affected by the increased polar discontinuity in the unhappy state.
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
reduces its local polarization at the interfaces where the atoms are not constrained (Fig.
7

\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
increases its polar displacements.

In conclusion, we find the unhappy orientation of
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
in
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{/}\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
superlattices to be energetically highly disfavored, and we only obtain a metastable superlattice in the unhappy orientation for 10 layers of
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
. The happy orientation however shows a clear stable minimum for all slab thicknesses, allowing an out-of-plane polarization down to a single unit cell with no critical thickness.

III.4
Electrostatic model

In this last section, we construct an electrostatic model and use it to study the stability of the two polarization states for thick layers with up to 100 unit cells of
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
each, which are not accessible within DFT. We also use it to determine the effect of various parameters such as the dielectric permittivity and the effective screening length on the behavior to guide possible choices of materials for future work.

The model describes the electrostatics at the interface and the associated energy costs to screen them. The total energy
E_{\text{es}}
consists of the following contributions:

E_{\text{es}}=E_{P,\text{BTO}}\cdot d_{\text{BTO}}+E_{P,\text{SNO}}\cdot d_{\text{SNO}}+E_{\text{d}}+E_{\text{scr}}

(4)

Here,
E_{P,\text{BTO}}
and
E_{P,\text{SNO}}
are the energies needed to polarize the
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
slabs of respective thicknesses
d_{\text{BTO}}
and
d_{\text{SNO}}

[
36
,
37
]
,
E_{\text{d}}
is the electrostatic cost resulting from the imperfect screening of the interface charge
\sigma_{\text{inter}}
by the metallic electrons in the electrode
[
38
,
39
]
and
E_{\text{scr}}
is the cost associated with possible additional screening via electron-hole excitation across the
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
band gap
[
36
,
37
]
. We describe the electrostatic model in more detail in Appendix
APPENDIX B: Electrostatic model
.

We estimate
E_{P,\text{BTO}}
and
E_{P,\text{SNO}}
from the energy profiles of bulk
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
(Fig.
8
), obtained by displacing the atoms according to the lowest frequency polar phonon mode of the relaxed tetragonal structure with centrosymmetric symmetry and in-plane lattice constants strained to
\text{SrTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
.
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
shows the characteristic double-well potential with formal-charge polarization minima at
\pm$24\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{C}\penalty\ \mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$
(note that this value is slightly lower than the formal-charge polarization of the relaxed bulk).

\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
is lowest in energy at zero polarization, with the energy increasing particularly rapidly with polarization when rotations are active.
This highlights again the importance of correctly treating the rotations in
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
.

III.4.1
Fit and extrapolation to larger slab thicknesses

We fit the model to our DFT calculations for
\text{(}\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{)}\text{}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{n\/}}}\text{(}\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{)}\text{}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
(
n=5,10,30
) to extract the physics of the following three parameters: the band gap of
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, the dielectric constant
\epsilon_{r}
of
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, and the effective metallic screening length
\lambda_{\text{fit}}
. The band gap linearly determines the energy cost of screening by electron-hole excitations (Eq.
A6
) and the energy of the depolarizing field is proportional to the ratio
\lambda_{\text{fit}}^{2}/\epsilon_{r}
(Eq.
A5
). We find good agreement for the shape of the energy profiles between DFT and the model for
\lambda_{\text{fit}}^{2}/\epsilon_{r}=$1\text{\times}{10}^{-19}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}^{2}$
at the experimental band gap of
3.2\text{\,}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}

[
40
,
41
]
(Fig.
A2
).

Note that the experimental band gap of
3.2\text{\,}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}
provides a better model fit to our DFT-calculated energy versus polarization for the superlattices than the DFT band gap of
1.8\text{\,}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}
, indicating a reduced need for electron-hole screening in the DFT calculations.
Here, we treat
\lambda_{\text{fit}}/\epsilon_{r}
merely as a parameter, and we discuss its physical meaning as well as different ways to extract the effective metallic screening length from the DFT calculations in the appendix.

and the resulting polarizations in Fig.

.

for 30 and negligibly small (

III.4.2
Influence of different parameters on the ferroelectric properties

Next, we study the effect of varying the dielectric permittivity, the metallic screening, and the electron-hole excitations across the band gap on the ferroelectric properties of the
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
layer.
In particular, by changing the metallic screening, we are able to mimick a metal-to-insulator transition (MIT) in the electrode. For
\gamma=\frac{2\lambda_{\text{fit}}}{d_{\text{BTO}}}
close to zero (small screening length) the electrode is a very good metal, at intermediate
\gamma
it is a poor metal and when
\gamma=1
we obtain the fully insulating state.

We can make the following conclusions from comparing the different plots:
First, the additional carriers from electron-hole excitations strongly influence the stability of the unhappy orientation when the screening in the metal is poor and the dielectric permittivity in the ferroelectric is low. For low dielectric permittivities, the unhappy orientation is highly disfavored without screening by forming additional charge carriers (dashed line) in both the insulating and intermediate metallicity electrode cases. When the metallic screening is improved, fewer charge carrier excitations across the gap are needed and for the most metallic case, there are no electron-hole excitations present even at low
\epsilon_{r}
.
Second, a larger
\epsilon_{r}
reduces the effect of the polar discontinuity and decreases the energy difference between the two polarization orientations. The double well of the insulating case (purple) does not change much with
\epsilon_{r}
, and the unhappy orientation is disfavored even for
\epsilon_{r}=90

For intermediate screening (pink), the double well potential is the most sensitive to
\epsilon_{r}

\epsilon_{r}=1

\epsilon_{r}=90

\epsilon_{r}=90

Lastly, the energy difference between the happy and unhappy orientations increases for all cases as the system becomes more insulating, destabilizing the unhappy polarization orientation. For
\epsilon_{r}=1
, neither the insulating nor intermediate metallicity electrodes can stabilize the unhappy orientation, and only the most metallic system (yellow curve) shows a metastable minimum. For
\epsilon_{r}=30

\epsilon_{r}=90
, we again see that intermediate screening already results in the unhappy orientation being stable.

These results show that a change in the metallic screening length, as occurs across a MIT, can significantly alter the relative stabilities of the two polarization states. The changes in the ferroelectric double well potential across a MIT are most significant for higher dielectric susceptibilities and if the electrode has good screening in the metallic state. The destabilization of the unhappy orientation on changing from metallic to an insulating electrode found for
\epsilon_{r}=90
(Fig.
10

In general, high
\epsilon_{r}
and good metallic screening minimize the effects of the polar discontinuity on the ferroelectric. Thus if a metal with charged layers is to be used as a simple electrode, care should be taken to select one with those properties. Increasing the ferroelectric slab thickness additionally improves the switchability and reduces the impact of the polar discontinuity. To build a ferroelectric device that exploits the polar discontinuity, for example with a strong exchange bias, on the other hand, we recommend a low dielectric susceptibility and a metal with poor screening capability. Again, adjusting the film thickness controls the extent of the bias, with thinner
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
films leading to unidirectional behavior.

To summarize this section, we find that the simple electrostatic model of Eq.
4
can reproduce the energy profiles over the range of thicknesses accessible in DFT calculations for thin films and can be conveniently used to extrapolate to larger slab thicknesses. We confirm that, as expected, the energy difference between happy and unhappy orientations is reduced in thicker films, becoming negligibly small for 30 formula units. In addition, our model allows us to test the effects of various parameters, such as the metallic screening length, on the properties of the superlattices, providing a simple and useful tool for studying how parameters such as slab thickness and metallic screening length affect the system’s behavior. In particular, we find that the metallic screening length strongly influences the (meta)-stability of the unhappy BaTiO
3
orientation.
This raises the interesting question of whether, in addition to the metallicity of the electrode affecting the polarization of the ferroelectric, the reciprocal behavior might occur, and the orientation of the ferroelectric polarization might affect the metallicity of the electrode. We envisage a scenario in which the superlattice layer thicknesses and materials are selected so that the electrode is insulating, and close to the metal-insulator transition when the ferroelectric is in its happy orientation. Switching the ferroelectric into its unhappy orientation could then force a transition of the electrode to the metallic state. Detailed electronic structure calculations for thicker slabs and at finite temperature would be helpful in identifying suitable material combinations and geometries.

IV
Conclusion

In summary, our DFT calculations confirm that the layer polarization in a metal can strongly affect the spontaneous polarization of the ferroelectric layer in metal-ferroelectric superlattices.

We showed that in
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{/}\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
superlattices the charge carriers in metallic
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
cannot sufficiently screen its own layer polarization, and consequently,
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
compensates for the polar discontinuity by orienting its spontaneous polarization parallel to the layer polarization of
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
. This out-of-plane spontaneous
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
polarization stays stable down to a single unit cell, with no critical thickness for the ferroelectric thin films in this superlattice. Similarly to other systems with polar discontinuities
[
8
,
10
]
, the unhappy direction which increases the polar discontinuity and the interface charge is highly disfavored and BTO thicknesses below ten layers do not even have a high energy metastable minimum for the unhappy direction. Based on the energy as a function of the soft mode distortion, we would expect the unhappy orientation to be metastable in thicker films.

We find that the inability of
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
to fully screen the polar discontinuity is independent of whether its rotations are active or disabled. The lattice response is, however, different for the two cases and when the octahedral rotations are not taken into account, the ability of
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
to form polar distortions is drastically overestimated.

We constructed an electrostatic model incorporating the energy lowering of forming a polarization in
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, the energy cost of forming polar displacements in
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, the partial screening of the interfacial charges by the metallicity of the electrode, and the formation of additional screening charges by electron-hole excitation. The model recreates our DFT behavior at low slab thicknesses and confirms that increasing the film thickness reduces the energy difference between the two polarization orientations. By varying our model parameters, we found that a shorter metallic screening length and a higher dielectric permittivity help to stabilize the unhappy orientation, indicating that a MIT could influence the ferroelectric switching behavior. This raises the question of whether, analogously, switching the ferroelectric could induce a MIT. If the ferroelectric is forced to the unhappy orientation in the insulating state of the electrode, the material would need to compensate the additional interface charges. If the electrode material is close to the MIT temperature and the metallic screening length is short enough, this could be achieved by the electrode transitioning to the metallic state rather than by electron-hole excitations across the gap. Studying the influence of the polar discontinuity on the MIT could be an interesting direction for future research.

Using the model, we studied the influence of different model parameters on the ferroelectric double well potentials. We note that, while the model is useful for predicting general trends, there is a significant spread in realistic parameter values and quantitative results should be interpreted with caution.

In conclusion, ferroelectric switching can be controlled in novel ways when using an electrode that has a layer polarization with a ferroelectric with charge neutral layers.
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
is particularly suitable as an electrode in ferroelectric devices when a strong exchange bias is desirable. The ferroelectric film thickness can be adjusted to control the extent of the bias, with thinner
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
films leading to unidirectional behavior.or easy switching of the ferroelectric, an electrode with no layer charges or with better metallic screening is recommended. If a good switching behavior is required with
\text{SmNiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
as an electrode, we recommend using a ferroelectric with a high dielectric constant or thick ferroelectric films. If the electrode also undergoes a MIT, this can lead to further exotic functionality.

We hope that our predictions inspire experimental work in these directions, both for fundamental research and for device applications.

The wide range of values reasonably extracted from our DFT calculations suggests that the carrier screening in a complex oxide electrode might not be reliably captured in terms of a single screening length parameter. We conclude that these values should be treated with caution and considered as fitting parameters, without attributing too much physical significance to them.
