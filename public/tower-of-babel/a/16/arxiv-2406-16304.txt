Robust Broadband Beamforming using Bilinear Programming
Nakul Singh, Coleman DeLude, Mark A. Davenport, and Justin Romberg

Abstract
We introduce a new method for robust beamforming, where the goal is to estimate a signal from array samples when there is uncertainty in the angle of arrival. Our method offers state-of-the-art performance on narrowband signals and is naturally applied to broadband signals. Our beamformer operates by treating the forward model for the array samples as unknown. We show that the “true” forward model lies in the linear span of a small number of fixed linear systems. As a result, we can estimate the forward operator and the signal simultaneously by solving a bilinear inverse problem using least squares. Our numerical experiments show that if the angle of arrival is known to only be within an interval of reasonable size, there is very little loss in estimation performance compared to the case where the angle is known exactly.



I
Introduction

Beamforming, the process of forming an estimate of a signal coming from a fixed direction
\theta
from the outputs of a multi-sensor array, relies critically on knowing this direction of arrival
\theta
. When the estimator is “misaligned,” i.e., using a
\theta
that is different from the true angle of arrival
\theta_{\mathrm{true}}
, the quality of the estimate can degrade quickly as
\theta
moves away from
\theta_{\mathrm{true}}
. This problem is especially pronounced in modern arrays with a large number of elements; large apertures allow us to create tapered beams, but they are also more fragile to misalignment.

While there is a significant amount of literature on making beamformers “robust” to unknown
\theta
(a very nice overview of current methods can be found in
[
1
]
), the most effective approaches known to date 1) apply only to narrowband signals, 2) require solving costly optimization programs to compute weight vectors. In this paper, we introduce a relatively simple approach based on bilinear programming that naturally includes the broadband case while not being significantly more computationally intensive than broadband beamforming with a known direction of arrival
\theta
.

Recent work in
[
2
]
has shown how broadband beamforming can effectively be treated as a linear inverse problem from measurements
\boldsymbol{y}\approx\boldsymbol{A}(\theta)\boldsymbol{\alpha}
that can be solved with least squares (this is discussed in more detail in Section
II-A
). The forward model
\boldsymbol{A}(\theta)
of course depends on the angle of arrival (AOA)
\theta
. In this paper, we show how we can make this broadband beamforming method robust to uncertainty in
\theta
. We show how each
A(\theta)
in a region of angles
\theta\in\Theta
can be written as a superposition of a relatively small number of fixed matrices that can be pre-computed. The result is a bilinear least squares problem. This type of problem is nonconvex, but is prevalent enough in applied mathematics that many different heuristics exist for efficient solvers.
We demonstrate the effectiveness of one such heuristic, alternating minimization, in the numerical experiments in Section
IV
.
These results show that the performance penalty for not knowing the angle of arrival exactly is small, and the computation required to achieve this performance is not significantly more than a standard least squares solver.

In addition to broadband beamforming, the method is also applicable to narrowband beamforming, where there is a well-established literature (some of which is briefly reviewed in Section
IV
). We show in Section
IV
that our bilinear robust beamformer also offers state-of-the-art performance for narrowband signals.

II
Formulation

In this section, we develop the system model for recovering the signal of interest when the arrival angle is unknown. We first present the system model developed in
[
2
]
for the case when true AOA is known and then propose a bilinear system model for the cases where we only have a rough estimate of the AOA.

II-A

Known AOA

We consider the beamforming problem where a plane wave signal strikes an
M
element array at an angle
1

\theta
, and
N
snapshots are recorded, providing a total of
MN
observations.
We model the signal of interest
s(t)
as being bandlimited to
\Omega
, e.g., the spectral support of
s(t)
is non-zero for
f\in[-\Omega,\Omega]

Over the temporal extent of the
N

as the superposition of

\psi_{1}(t),\ldots,\psi_{K}(t)

A natural choice for the

The received

n^{\text{th}}

y_{m}[n]=e^{-j2\pi f_{c}\tau_{m}(\theta)}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\psi_{k}(t_{n}-\tau_{m}(\theta))+\textnormal{noise}.\\

(1)

The
\tau_{m}(\theta)
in (
1
) is the delay (relative to array center) of element
m
for a plane wave arriving from angle
\theta
;
t_{n}
denotes the sample time for the
n^{\text{th}}
snapshot. Therefore, a forward model for a single snapshot is given by

\begin{gathered}\boldsymbol{y}[n]=\boldsymbol{A}_{n}(\theta)\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\textnormal{noise},\\
\end{gathered}

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{A}_{n}(\theta)\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times K}
is the matrix with entries given by
A_{n}(\theta)[m,k]=e^{-j2\pi f_{c}\tau_{m}(\theta)}\psi_{k}(t_{n}-\tau_{m}(\theta))
. Stacking the model in (
2
) for the
N

\begin{gathered}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{y}[1]\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{y}[N]\end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(\theta)\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{A}_{N}(\theta)\end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol{A}(\theta)}\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\textnormal{noise}.\end{gathered}

(3)

To recover the Slepian weights (
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
) from (
3

\begin{gathered}\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}||\boldsymbol{y}-\mathbf{A(\theta)}\boldsymbol{\alpha}||_{2}^{2}\\
\implies\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(\mathbf{A(\theta)}^{H}\mathbf{A(\theta)})^{-1}\mathbf{A(\theta)}^{H}\boldsymbol{y}.\end{gathered}

(4)

The Slepian beamforming in (
4

IV

4

It is noteworthy to mention that the dimension
K
of the low-dimensional subspace spanned by the Slepian basis depends on the true angle of arrival as
K=\lceil 2\Omega T(\theta)\rceil
where
T(\theta)
is the array aperture. However, for reasonable deviations in the angle of arrival, the variation in
K
is insignificant; therefore, in subsequent sections, we will assume the dimension
K
to be virtually known.

II-B

Bilinear formulation for unknown AOA

When
\theta
is unknown, there is uncertainty in the forward model (
3
) for the array samples. We approach this problem by finding a linear embedding for the rows of the
\boldsymbol{A}_{n}(\theta)
for an interval
\Theta
of possible AOAs around a fixed angle
\theta_{0}
:
\Theta=[\theta_{0}-\Delta,\theta_{0}+\Delta]
.

To illustrate how this works, let
\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}(\theta)\in\mathbb{C}^{K}
be the
\ell^{\text{th}}
row of
\boldsymbol{A}(\theta)
. Note that each value of
\ell=1,\dots,MN
has a one-to-one correspondence to a pair
(m,n)
. As
\theta
changes,
\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}(\theta)
also changes, but does so smoothly. The key realization is that the collection of vectors
\{\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}(\theta),~\theta\in\Theta\}
can all be very closely approximated by vectors from a low dimensional subspace. That is, there exists a
K\times P
matrix
\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}
, for a very small
P
, such that for every fixed
\theta\in\Theta
we have
\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}(\theta)\approx\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{c}
for a short vector
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{P}
. As such, we can write
y_{\ell}=\boldsymbol{c}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}
where both
\boldsymbol{c}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
are unknown —
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
depends on the signal
s(t)
and
\boldsymbol{c}
depends on its AOA.

We formalize this carefully as follows.
Define the functions

w_{\ell,k}(\theta)=e^{-j2\pi f_{c}\tau_{m}(\theta)}\psi_{k}(t_{n}-\tau_{m}(\theta)),~\theta\in\Theta,

for an interval
\Theta
as above, and collect these into

\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}w_{\ell,1}(\theta)\\
\vdots\\
w_{\ell,K}(\theta)\end{bmatrix}.

Our problem is that we observe

y_{\ell}=\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}(\theta^{*})^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\mathrm{noise},\quad\ell=1,\ldots,MN,

(5)

for some unknown
\theta^{*}\in\Theta
. We will show how this can be turned into a well-posed bilinear problem by using some basic facts about reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the Hilbert space of functions on
\theta\in\Theta
generated from the functions
w_{\ell,k}(\theta)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\operatorname{Span}(\{w_{\ell,k}(\theta),~\ell=1,\dots,MN;k=1,\ldots K\}).

(6)

This is a finite-dimensional Hilbert space of continuous functions, and thus it will have a reproducing kernel for any inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
that we endow it with.
\mathcal{H}
is essentially a Slepian space, as it is generated from modulated versions of small segments of the (approximately) bandlimited functions
\psi_{1}(t),\ldots,\psi_{K}(t)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{\ell}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{K}
be the linear operator defined by

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{\ell}x=\begin{bmatrix}\left\langle x,w_{\ell,1}\right\rangle\\
\vdots\\
\left\langle x,w_{\ell,K}\right\rangle\end{bmatrix}

(7)

and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{MNK}
be the concatenation of these operators across the array elements and snapshots:

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}x=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{1}x\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{MN}x\end{bmatrix}.

This operator is effectively low rank due to the exponential decay of eigenvalues of the integral operator of which the Slepian basis are the eigenfunctions; for a more detailed analysis, refer
[
3
]
. By the spectral theorem, we know that there are (orthonormal) functions
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots\in\mathcal{H}
such that

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}^{H}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}})(\theta,\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,k}w_{\ell,k}(\theta)^{H}w_{\ell,k}(\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{MNK}\sigma_{p}^{2}v_{p}(\theta)^{H}v_{p}(\theta^{\prime}).

As noted above, the
w_{\ell,k}(\theta)
are all just segments of bandlimited signals over small intervals, we can truncate the sum above at some modest
P
:

(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}^{H}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}})(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\approx\sum_{p=1}^{P}\sigma_{p}^{2}v_{p}(\theta)^{H}v_{p}(\theta^{\prime}).

Likewise, if we set

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{a}_{1}(\theta)\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{a}_{MN}(\theta)\end{bmatrix},

we know that the eigenvalue decomposition for the
MNK\times MNK
matrix can be similarly truncated
2
2

2

A
.
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}^{H}

\displaystyle=\int_{\theta\in\Theta}\boldsymbol{w}(\theta)\boldsymbol{w}(\theta)^{\mathrm{H}}~d\theta

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{MNK}\sigma_{p}^{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{p}\boldsymbol{u}_{p}^{\mathrm{H}}\approx\sum_{p=1}^{P}\sigma_{p}^{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{p}\boldsymbol{u}_{p}^{\mathrm{H}}.

We can thus write for any
x\in\mathcal{H}

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}x\approx\sum_{p=1}^{P}\sigma_{p}\boldsymbol{u}_{p}\langle x,v_{p}\rangle,

and

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{\ell}x\approx\sum_{p=1}^{P}\sigma_{p}\boldsymbol{u}_{p,\ell}\langle x,v_{p}\rangle,

where
\boldsymbol{u}_{p,\ell}\in\mathbb{C}^{K}
is just the “
\ell^{\text{th}}
part” in
\boldsymbol{u}_{p}\in\mathbb{C}^{MNK}
:

\boldsymbol{u}_{p}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}_{p,1}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{u}_{p,MN}\end{bmatrix}.

Note that the
\boldsymbol{u}_{p,\ell}

are not
the left singular vectors of the operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{\ell}
; they are parts of the singular vectors of the joint operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}
.

Returning to our forward model (
5
), let
\beta^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
be the slice of the reproducing kernel that takes samples at location
\theta^{*}
. Then
\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}(\theta^{*})=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{\ell}\beta^{*}
and so (ignoring the noise)

\displaystyle y_{\ell}=\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}(\theta^{*})^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{\ell}\beta^{*}\right)^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{p=1}^{P}\sigma_{p}\langle\beta^{*},v_{p}\rangle\boldsymbol{u}_{p,\ell}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{P}c_{p}\boldsymbol{u}_{p,\ell}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{c}\right)^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\alpha},

where
c_{p}=\sigma_{p}\langle\beta^{*},v_{p}\rangle
and the
K\times P
matrix
\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}
is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}_{1,\ell}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{u}_{P,\ell}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Note that
\boldsymbol{c}
is independent of
l
. Thus our bilinear problem is to recover
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{P}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{K}
from measurements
y_{\ell}
given the forward model

y_{\ell}=\boldsymbol{c}^{\mathrm{H}}(\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell})^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}~+~\mathrm{noise},

(8)

where the
\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}
are known
K\times P
matrices that can be computed beforehand. Ideally,
\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}
are calculated after taking the singular value decomposition of the matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}^{H}
. However, for a large array with many snapshots, performing the SVD of this matrix can be computationally expensive. To circumvent this issue, we approximate the operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}
as a matrix that allows faster computation of the SVD. Instead of defining the functions
w_{\ell,k}(\theta)
over a continuous interval
\Theta
, we discretize the interval by uniformly sampling
Q
points
\{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{Q}\}\in\Theta
. Corresponding to these sampled points, we define
\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}:\mathbb{C}^{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{K}
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}=\begin{bmatrix}w_{\ell,1}(\theta_{1})&&\ldots&&w_{\ell,1}(\theta_{Q})\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
w_{\ell,K}(\theta_{1})&&\ldots&&w_{\ell,K}(\theta_{Q})\\
\end{bmatrix},

and
\boldsymbol{W}:\mathbb{C}^{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{MNK}
becomes the concatenation of these approximate operators.
We can now evaluate the SVD of the
Q\times Q
matrix
\boldsymbol{W}^{H}\boldsymbol{W}
much faster since
Q\ll MNK
for large arrays. Using the sampled orthonormal functions
\boldsymbol{v}_{p}
we get
\boldsymbol{u}_{p}=\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{v}_{p}/\sigma_{p}
which are then used to compute
\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}
.

The next section describes the alternating minimization method used to solve the bilinear least squares resulting from the model devised in (
8
).

III
Alternating minimization for bilinear least squares

To perform alternating minimization, we first stack the bilinear formulation derived in (
8
) across array elements and snapshots to arrive at the following forward model,

\begin{gathered}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}\\
\vdots\\
y_{MN}\end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}=\boldsymbol{C}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{U}_{1}^{H}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{U}^{H}_{MN}\end{bmatrix}}_{\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\text{noise},\\
\boldsymbol{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{MN\times PMN}\text{ s.t. }C[i,j]=\begin{cases}[c^{H}]_{j}\text{ }(i-1)P\leq j\leq iP\\
0\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}\end{gathered}

A similar model with block structure over
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
instead of
\boldsymbol{c}
is given by,

\begin{gathered}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}(y_{1})^{*}\\
\vdots\\
(y_{MN})^{*}\end{bmatrix}}_{\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}}=\boldsymbol{V}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{U}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{U}_{MN}\end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol{U}}\boldsymbol{c}+\text{noise},\\
\boldsymbol{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{MN\times KMN}\text{ s.t. }V[i,j]=\begin{cases}[\alpha^{H}]_{j}\text{ }(i-1)K\leq j\leq iK\\
0\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}\end{gathered}

Using these models, we have two equivalent bilinear least squares problems that can be solved alternately while fixing one of the variables. Keeping
\boldsymbol{c}^{t}
fixed at the
t^{\text{th}}
iteration, we obtain a linear least squares problem in
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
:

\begin{gathered}\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{t+1}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}||\boldsymbol{y}-\underbrace{\boldsymbol{C}^{t}\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{\boldsymbol{A}^{t}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}||_{2}^{2}\\
\implies\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{t+1}=((\boldsymbol{A}^{t}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})^{H}\boldsymbol{A}^{t}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{A}^{t}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})^{H}\boldsymbol{y}.\end{gathered}

Next fixing
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{t+1}
we obtain a linear least squares problem in
\boldsymbol{c}
:

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}^{t+2}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{c}}||\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}-\underbrace{\boldsymbol{V}^{t+1}\boldsymbol{U}}_{\boldsymbol{A}^{t+1}_{\boldsymbol{c}}}\boldsymbol{c}||_{2}^{2}\\
\implies\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}^{t+2}=((\boldsymbol{A}^{t+1}_{\boldsymbol{c}})^{H}\boldsymbol{A}^{t+1}_{\boldsymbol{c}})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{A}^{t+1}_{\boldsymbol{c}})^{H}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}.\end{gathered}

It is important to note that we solve an unconstrained bilinear least squares problem. Therefore, the optimizers of the program are not unique, i.e., if the algorithm recovers weights
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}
then
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}/\gamma
and
\gamma\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}
are also feasible solutions of the problem for
\gamma\neq 0
. To find
\gamma
associated with the true angle, we can create a sequence of weights
\boldsymbol{c}_{q}
for each
\theta_{q}
sampled from
\Theta
. For a sufficiently fine-grained sampling, the true
\boldsymbol{c}
will be well-approximated by some
\boldsymbol{c}\in\{\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{c}_{Q}\}
. Thus, if we let

q^{*}=\argmin_{q}\Big|\Big|\boldsymbol{c}_{q}-\gamma_{q}\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}\Big|\Big|^{2}_{2}

where
\gamma_{q}=\frac{\boldsymbol{c}_{q}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}}{\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}}
, then
\gamma_{q^{*}}
is the offset constant linked with the true angle and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}/\gamma_{q*}
provides the estimate of the true Slepian weights. Other approaches, like line spectral estimation
[
4
]
, can also be adapted for fine-tuning the constant
\gamma
. We empirically observe that using a sufficiently good initialization for
\boldsymbol{c}^{0}
results in
\gamma\approx 1
. Thus, we start the alternating minimization scheme described above with an initial value of
\boldsymbol{c}^{0}
corresponding to the mid-point of the interval
\Theta
, allowing the algorithm to converge in
10
to
15
iterations for the experiments illustrated in the following section.

IV
Numerical results

We use the linear Slepian model in (
4

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{true}

\Theta
) as an estimate for the angle to recover a crude estimate of the Slepian weights. These are then used for a quantitative comparison against the estimates obtained from the proposed method. In addition, in the broadband regime, we also compare our algorithm against conventional approaches like delay and sum beamforming
[
5
]
. In the narrowband regime, we compare the performance with several robust adaptive beamforming methods.

Appendix A
Singular value expansion for operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}

To begin, recall that the finite-dimensional Hilbert space we are examining is

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\text{Span}\left(\{w_{\ell}(\theta)\}_{\ell=1}^{L}\right)

where each basis function
w_{\ell}(\theta)
operates over the range
\theta\in\Theta
. We use a slightly different indexing for ease of notation. The operator we are examining,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{C}^{L}
, is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}x=\begin{bmatrix}\langle x,w_{1}\rangle\\
\langle x,w_{2}\rangle\\
\vdots\\
\langle x,w_{L}\rangle\end{bmatrix}.

It is an operator between two finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces and, therefore, is compact
[
10
, Chapt. 4]
. The first step in determining the Singular value expansion (SVE) is determining the adjoint of the operator. Note that the inner product for
\mathcal{H}
is given by
\langle w,x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\int_{\Theta}x(\theta)\bar{w}(\theta)d\theta
,a and the innner product for
\mathbb{C}^{L}
is the standard vector inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}x,y\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}y_{\ell}\overline{\langle x,w_{\ell}\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}y_{\ell}\langle w_{\ell},x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}y_{\ell}\int_{\Theta}w_{\ell}(\theta)\overline{x}(\theta)d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}\overline{x}(\theta)\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}y_{\ell}w_{\ell}(\theta)d\theta

\displaystyle=\left\langle x,\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}y_{\ell}w_{\ell}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},

Hence the adjoint
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}^{H}:\mathbb{C}^{L}\to\mathcal{H}
is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}^{H}y=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}y_{\ell}w_{\ell}(\theta).

Since the adjoint of a compact operator is also compact, therefore the composite operators
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}^{H}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}^{H}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}
are also compact
[
10
, Chapt. 4]
. It is easy to verify they are also self-adjoint and, therefore, admit an eigendecomposition
[
11
, Chapt. 1]
. It is the case that the eigenvectors of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}^{H}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}
are the sequence of functions
v_{\ell}
, and similarly, the eigenvectors of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}^{H}
are the sequence of vectors
\boldsymbol{u}_{\ell}

[
12
, Chapt. 7 ]
. Through similar reasoning, it is also the case that the non-zero eigenvalues of these self-adjoint operators are the same and equal to
\{\sigma^{2}_{\ell}\}
.
From this point, it is not too difficult to see that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}^{H}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}w_{\ell}(\theta)\langle w_{\ell},x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sigma_{\ell}^{2}v_{\ell}(\theta)\langle v_{\ell},x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

where the second line follows from the spectral theorem
[
11
]
. We can state this more concisely as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}^{H}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}w_{\ell}(\theta)\otimes w_{\ell}(\theta^{\prime})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sigma_{\ell}^{2}v_{\ell}(\theta)\otimes v_{\ell}(\theta^{\prime})

where the addition of a
\theta^{\prime}
is meant to emphasize that the inner product against the rightmost eigenfunction is taken independently from the leftmost. In a similar manner, we can solve for the left eigenfunctions by examining

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}^{H}x

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\langle w_{1},\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}x_{\ell}w_{\ell}(\theta)\rangle\\
\langle w_{2},\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}x_{\ell}w_{\ell}(\theta)\rangle\\
\vdots\\
\langle w_{L},\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}y_{\ell}w_{\ell}(\theta)\rangle\end{bmatrix}.

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}(\theta)\overline{w_{1}}(\theta)&\dots&w_{L}(\theta)\overline{w_{1}}(\theta)\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
w_{1}(\theta)\overline{w_{L}}(\theta)&\dots&w_{L}(\theta)\overline{w_{L}}(\theta)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
\vdots\\
x_{L}\end{bmatrix}d\theta.

which is just an
L\times L
symmetric matrix operator, and as with all such matrices, we can from an eigendecomposition on it to get a set of eigenvectors
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
. Putting it all together, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sigma_{\ell}\boldsymbol{u}_{\ell}\otimes v_{\ell}

such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}x=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sigma_{\ell}\boldsymbol{u}_{\ell}\langle x,v_{\ell}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

Thus, the calculation and justification of the factorization present in the robust beamforming method are concluded.
