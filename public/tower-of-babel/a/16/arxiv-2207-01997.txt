Motzkin numbers and flag codes
Clementa Alonso-González, Miguel Ángel Navarro-Pérez

Abstract
Motzkin numbers have been widely studied since they count many different combinatorial objects. In this paper we present a new appearance of this remarkable sequence in the network coding setting through a particular case of multishot codes called flag codes . A flag code is a set of sequences of nested subspaces ( flags ) of a vector space over the finite field 𝔽 q {\mathbb{F}}_{q} . If the list of dimensions is ( 1 , … , n − 1 ) (1,\ldots,n-1) , we speak about a full flag code . The flag distance is defined as the sum of the respective subspace distances and can be represented by means of the so-called distance vectors . We show that the number of distance vectors corresponding to the full flag variety on 𝔽 q n {\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n} is exactly the n n -th Motzkin number. Moreover, we can identify the integer sequence that counts the number of possible distance vectors associated to a full flag code with prescribed minimum distance.



Keywords:
Motzkin numbers, Motzkin paths, flag codes, flag distance, distance vectors.

1
Introduction

Network coding is the most efficient way to send data across a network modelled as a directed acyclic multigraph with multiple senders and receivers. The key is that the intermediate nodes can perform random linear combinations of the incoming inputs. This improves considerably the information flow although it can also lead to error propagation and erasures (see
[
1
]
). To solve this problem, in
[
10
]
the authors consider subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
as codewords and
subspace codes
as collections of subspaces. In particular, if all the subspaces have the same dimension, we have
constant dimension codes
. Consult
[
19
]
to have an overview of the most representative works in this subject.

On the other side, subspace codes can be considered as
one-shot subspace codes
since sending a codeword (a subspace) requires just a channel use. This gives rise to the idea, first suggested in
[
15
]
, of using the channel several times to get
multishot subspace codes
. Under this approach codewords are sequences of subspaces. In particular, it is possible to consider
constant type flags
, that is, sequences of nested subspaces with fixed dimensions as it was proposed in
[
12
]
. In this seminal paper, collections of flags are called
flag codes
and they are presented as a generalization of constant dimension codes. The recent works
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
11
]
deal with different questions related to the parameters and construction of flag codes.

In this paper we focus on the distance parameter associated to full flag codes, that is, those whose sequence of dimensions is
(1,\ldots,n-1)
. As usual in the multishot context, the distance between flags is computed as the sum of the subspace distances corresponding to each shot, which provokes that many different combinations of them can give the same flag distance value. This fact was carefully pointed out in
[
4
]
, where the authors introduce the notion of
distance vector
(associated to a given distance value) in order to complete the information concerning the distance of pairs of flags. In this framework, it naturally arises the question of knowing in how many different ways the distance between a couple of flags can be distributed. We provide the answer to this question by showing that the cardinality of the set of distance vectors corresponding to the full flag variety on
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
is given by the
n
-th Motzkin number.

Motzkin numbers
have been widely investigated as they appear in a great variety of combinatorial objects (see
[
17
,
18
]
for more details). Here we look at them in terms of certain lattice paths from
(0,0)
to
(n,0)
that consist of horizontal steps
(1,0)
, up steps
(1,1)
and down steps
(1,-1)
and never goes below the
x
-axis (
Motzkin paths
). Thus, we exhibit a correspondence between distance vectors and Motzkin paths that results to be crucial for our purposes. This idea has been inspired by the combinatorial approach to flag codes developed in
[
3
]
and some interesting talks with Paulo Almeida and Alessandro Neri.

The remain of the paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we remember some basic definitions and results on Motzkin numbers, subspace codes and flag codes. In Section
3
we recall the concept of
distance vector
associated to a couple of flags and we pose the problem of counting such objects. In Section
4
we establish a bijection between the set of distance vectors associated to the full flag variety on
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
and the set of Motzkin paths with
n
steps which permits to compute the cardinality of the former set as well as the one of some important subsets of it. Moreover, we show that such a bijection takes the flag distance associated to a distance vector to the area under the corresponding Motzkin path. Finally, in Section
5
we propose some open questions that could be addressed taking into account our work.

Preliminaries

In this section we briefly recall some basic background on Motzkin numbers, subspace codes and flag codes.

2.1
Motzkin numbers

Let us consider the set of lattice paths in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
whose permitted steps are the up diagonal step
(1,1)
denoted by
U
, the down diagonal step
(1,-1)
, denoted by
D
, and the horizontal step
(1,0)
, denoted by
H
. Let
U(x,y)
represent the set of all unrestricted lattice paths running from
(0,0)
to
(x,y)
and using the steps
U,D
and
H
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
Motzkin path
of length
n
is a lattice path in
U(n,0)
that never runs below the
x
-axis. We denote by
\mathcal{M}_{n}
the set of Motzkin paths of length
n
. A
Dyck path
is a Motzkin path that does not contain horizontal steps. The set of Dyck paths of length
2n
will be denoted by
\mathcal{D}_{n}
.

It is clear that, if horizontal steps are not allowed, the length of a Motzkin path (a Dyck path, in this case) must be even. On the other hand, it is well known that the cardinality of the set
\mathcal{M}_{n}
is the
n
-th Motzkin number
M_{n}
(sequence
A001006
in
[
17
]
) and the cardinality of
\mathcal{D}_{n}
is the
n
-th Catalan number
C_{n}
(sequence
A000108
in
[
17
]
). More precisely, we can compute
M_{0}=C_{0}=1
and

M_{n}=M_{n-1}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}M_{k}M_{n-k-2},\quad C_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}C_{k}C_{n-k-1}\quad(n\geqslant 1).

The Motzkin numbers sequence, whose first ten terms are

1,1,2,4,9,21,51,127,323,835,

was introduced by Theodor Motzkin
[
14
]
, while counting possible sets of nonintersecting chords joining some of
n
points on a circle. Since this seminal paper it has been the subject of numerous studies over the last forty years. See
[
2
]
and
[
7
]
for a couple of the earliest surveys where the authors exhibit different combinatorial objects counted by this sequence, including Motzkin paths.

A commonly used and concise way of representing a Motzkin path
p\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
is by its corresponding Motzkin word (see
[
8
]
, for instance).

Definition 2.2
.

A
Motzkin word
of length
n
is a sequence of
n
letters in the alphabet
\{U,D,H\}
, namely
p=p_{1}p_{2}\dots p_{n}
, with the constraint that the number of occurrences of the letter
U
is equal to the number of occurrences of the letter
D
and, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the number of occurrences of
U
in
the subword
p_{1}p_{2}\dots p_{i}
is not smaller than the one of
D
.

In the following, we will not distinguish between a Motzkin path and the corresponding Motzkin word.

Definition 2.7
.

A

of a Motzkin path

.

2.2
Subspace codes and flag codes

Consider a prime power
q
and a positive integer
n\geqslant 2
. Let
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
be the finite field with
q
elements. The set of subspaces of the vector space
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
can be seen as a metric space by using different metrics. Among them, here we will use the so-called
injection distance.

Definition 2.11
.

The
injection distance
between two subspaces
\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V}\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
is defined as

d_{I}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})=\max\{\dim(\mathcal{U}),\dim(\mathcal{V})\}-\dim(\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{V}).

(2)

In particular, if
\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V}
have the same dimension, say
1\leqslant k<n
, then we have

d_{I}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})=k-\dim(\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{V})=\dim(\mathcal{U}+\mathcal{V})-k.

(3)

Hence, if for any
1\leqslant k<n
we denote by
\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n)
the
Grassmannian
, that is, the set of
k
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
, we can consider error-correcting codes in
\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n)
as follows.

Definition 2.12
.

A
constant dimension code

\mathcal{C}
of length
n
and dimension
k
is a nonempty subset of
\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n)
. The
minimum distance
of
\mathcal{C}
is defined as

d_{I}(\mathcal{C})=\min\{d_{I}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})\ |\ \mathcal{U},\mathcal{V}\in\mathcal{C},\ \mathcal{U}\neq\mathcal{V}\}

whenever
|\mathcal{C}|\geqslant 2
. In case
|\mathcal{C}|=1
, we put
d_{I}(\mathcal{C})=0
.

Note that the minimum distance of a code
\mathcal{C}
is an integer such that

0\leqslant d_{I}(\mathcal{C})\leqslant\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}k&\text{if}&2k\leqslant n,\\
n-k&\text{if}&2k\geqslant n.\end{array}\right.

(4)

Remark 2.13
.

Another frequent metric used when working with subspace codes of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
is the
subspace distance
. It is given by

d_{S}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})=\dim(\mathcal{U}+\mathcal{V})-\dim(\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{V}).

(5)

Observe that, if
\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V}\in\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n)
, then
d_{S}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})=2(k-\dim(\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{V}))=2d_{I}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V}).
Hence, in the context of constant dimension codes, the injection distance and the subspace distance are equivalent metrics. Consult
[
19
]
and the references therein for more information on this class of codes.

Let us now consider flags on
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
with the same type, that is, sequences of nested subspaces of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
whose list of dimensions is fixed. In this way we get
flag codes (of constant type)
. This idea was first proposed in
[
12
]
. Let us recall the basics on this family of codes.

Definition 2.14
.

A
flag

\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{r})
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is a sequence of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-vector subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
satisfying

\{0\}\subsetneq\mathcal{F}_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\mathcal{F}_{r}\subsetneq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}.

The type of
\mathcal{F}
is the vector
(\dim(\mathcal{F}_{1}),\dots,\dim(\mathcal{F}_{r}))
. If it equals
(1,2,\ldots,n-1),
we say that
\mathcal{F}
is a
full flag
. We say that
\mathcal{F}_{i}
is the
i
-th subspace
of
\mathcal{F}
.

The set of all the flags on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
of a fixed type vector
(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})
is said to be the
flag variety

\mathcal{F}_{q}((t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}),n)
or the
full flag variety

\mathcal{F}_{q}(n)
, if the type vector is
(1,\dots,n-1)
. The distance defined in (
2
) can be extended to the flag variety as follows: given two flags
\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{F}_{r})
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{r})
in
\mathcal{F}_{q}((t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}),n)
, the
(injection) flag distance
between them is the value

d_{f}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{I}(\mathcal{F}_{i},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}).

(6)

Remark 2.15
.

The subspace distance
d_{S}
defined in (
5
) can also be extended to the flag variety. Given
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
as above, the sum of subspace distances

\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{S}(\mathcal{F}_{i},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i})=2d_{f}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})

(7)

is an equivalent distance to
d_{f}
. In this paper we will always work with the injection flag distance and we will simply write
flag distance
.

Definition 2.16
.

A
flag code
of type
(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})
on
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
is a nonempty subset
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{q}((t_{1},\dots,t_{r}),n)
. Its
minimum distance
is given by

d_{f}(\mathcal{C})=\min\{d_{f}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\ |\ \mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C},\ \mathcal{F}\neq\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\}

when
|\mathcal{C}|\geqslant 2
. If
|\mathcal{C}|=1
, we put
d_{f}(\mathcal{C})=0
.

For each dimension
t_{i}
in the type vector of a flag code
\mathcal{C}
, we can consider the constant dimension code in the Grassmannian
\mathcal{G}_{q}(t_{i},n)
consisting of the set of the
i
-th subspaces of flags in
\mathcal{C}
. This set is called the
i
-projected code
of
\mathcal{C}
and we denote it by
\mathcal{C}_{i}
. It is clear that
|\mathcal{C}_{i}|\leqslant|\mathcal{C}|
for every
i=1,\dots,r
. Moreover, if given an index
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
we have that
|\mathcal{C}_{i}|=|\mathcal{C}|
, we can deduce that for any couple of flags
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
we have that
\mathcal{F}_{i}\neq\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\prime}
. This fact leads to the following definition.

Definition 2.17
.

We say that two flags
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
of type
(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})
on
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}

collapse
at their
i
-th subspace if
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}
. On the other hand, two different flags
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
are said to be
disjoint
if they do not collapse at any subspace, i.e., if
\mathcal{F}_{i}\neq\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i},
for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
. Similarly, a flag code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{q}((t_{1},\dots,t_{r}),n)
is
disjoint
if it consists of disjoint flags. In terms of the projected codes, a flag code
\mathcal{C}
is disjoint if, and only if,
|\mathcal{C}_{1}|=\dots=|\mathcal{C}_{r}|=|\mathcal{C}|
.

3
Distance vectors: how to spread the flag distance

In sight of the flag distance definition given in (
6
), one realizes that a fixed distance value could possibly be obtained by adding different subspace distances combinations. This fact is reflected in the following example.

Example 3.1
.

Let
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4},e_{5},e_{6}\}
be the standard basis of the
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-vector space
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{6}
. Consider the flag code
\mathcal{C}
of type
(1,3,5)
on
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{6}
given by the set of flags:

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathcal{F}^{1}&=&(\langle e_{1}\rangle,\langle e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\rangle,\langle e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4},e_{5}\rangle),\\
\mathcal{F}^{2}&=&(\langle e_{5}\rangle,\langle e_{4},e_{5},e_{6}\rangle,\langle e_{1},e_{2},e_{4},e_{5},e_{6}\rangle),\\
\mathcal{F}^{3}&=&(\langle e_{6}\rangle,\langle e_{4},e_{5},e_{6}\rangle,\langle e_{2},e_{3},e_{4},e_{5},e_{6}\rangle),\\
\mathcal{F}^{4}&=&(\langle e_{2}\rangle,\langle e_{2},e_{5},e_{6}\rangle,\langle e_{2},e_{3},e_{4},e_{5},e_{6}\rangle).\par\end{array}

Observe that it holds:

d_{f}(\mathcal{C})=d_{f}(\mathcal{F}^{2},\mathcal{F}^{3})=1+0+1=2=1+1+0=d_{f}(\mathcal{F}^{3},\mathcal{F}^{4}).

We can follow that, even if the pairs
\mathcal{F}^{2},\mathcal{F}^{3}
and
\mathcal{F}^{3},\mathcal{F}^{4}
are both at distance
2
, the first one presents a collapse in the second subspace, that is,
\mathcal{F}_{2}^{2}=\mathcal{F}_{2}^{3}
, whereas the second pair collapses at the third one. As a consequence, to totally capture the relative position of two flags, it is necessary to provide more precise information beyond the distance in absolute terms. In
[
4
]
the authors deal with this question by defining
distance vectors
. Let us recall this definition.

Definition 3.2
.

Given two different flags
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
of type
t=(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})
on
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
, their associated
distance vector
is

\textbf{d}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})=(d_{I}(\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\prime}),\dots,d_{I}(\mathcal{F}_{r},\mathcal{F}_{r}^{\prime}))\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}.

Notice that the sum of the components of
\textbf{d}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
is the flag distance
d_{f}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
defined in (
6
). Given a positive integer
n\geqslant 2
and a type vector
t=(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})
, we denote by
D^{(t,n)}
the maximum possible value of the flag distance in
\mathcal{F}_{q}(t,n)
that, as a consequence of (
4
), is

D^{(t,n)}=\left(\sum_{t_{i}\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}t_{i}+\sum_{t_{i}>\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}(n-t_{i})\right).

(8)

In particular, the set
\{0,1,\dots,D^{(t,n)}\}
contains all the possible values for the flag distance in
\mathcal{F}_{q}(t,n)
. When working with the full type vector, we simply write

D^{n}=\left\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cccl}\frac{n^{2}}{4}&\text{if}&n&\text{is even},\\
&&&\\[-10.00002pt]
\frac{n^{2}-1}{4}&\text{if}&n&\text{is odd}\end{array}\right.

(9)

to denote the maximum possible distance between full flags on
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
d
be an integer such that
0\leqslant d\leqslant D^{(t,n)}
. We define
the set of distance vectors associated to
d
for the flag variety
\mathcal{F}_{q}(t,n)
as

\mathcal{D}(d,t,n)=\{\textbf{d}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\ |\ \mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{q}(t,n),\ d_{f}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})=d\}\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}.

On the other hand, the set of
distance vectors for the flag variety
\mathcal{F}_{q}(t,n)
is

\mathcal{D}(t,n)=\{\textbf{d}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\ |\ \mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{q}(t,n)\}\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}.

and it holds

\mathcal{D}(t,n)=\bigcup_{d}\mathcal{D}(d,t,n),

where
d
takes all the integers between
0\leqslant d\leqslant D^{(t,n)}
. When working with the full flag variety, we drop the type vector and simply write
\mathcal{D}(d,n)
and
\mathcal{D}(n),
respectively.

At this point we propose several important questions: how many possible distance vectors could correspond to a given couple of arbitrary flags
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
on
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
, that is, what is the cardinality of
\mathcal{D}(n)
? In particular, what happens if these flags do not collapse? What if they never share two consecutive subspaces? What if
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
belong to a flag code with prescribed minimum distance? We address these questions in the following section.

4
The bijection

In the remain of the paper we will always work with full flag codes. More precisely, we will show that the number of distance vectors associated to the full flag variety
\mathcal{F}_{q}(n)
is given by the Motzkin number
M_{n}
.

Let us first recall those properties that characterize distance vectors. The following result is based on
[
3
, Th. 3.8]
and
[
4
, Th. 3.9]
. For completeness here we include a shorter proof adapted to the full type case.

Theorem 4.1
.

Consider integers
\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n-1}\geqslant 0
and put
\delta_{0}=\delta_{n}=0
.
Then
(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n-1})
is a distance vector in
\mathcal{D}(n)
if, and only if,

\delta_{i}\in\{\delta_{i-1}-1,\delta_{i-1},\delta_{i-1}+1\},

(10)

for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.

Proof.

Suppose that
(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n-1})
is a distance vector. Then, there exists a pair of full flags
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{q}(n)
satisfying
\delta_{j}=d_{I}(\mathcal{F}_{j},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{j})
, for every
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1
. Let us see that

\delta_{i}\in\{\delta_{i-1}-1,\delta_{i-1},\delta_{i-1}+1\}.

Note that
\delta_{1},\delta_{n-1}\in\{0,1\}
given that
\delta_{1},\delta_{n-1}
are, respectively, distances between lines and hyperplanes of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
. Moreover, since
\delta_{0}=\delta_{n}=0
, the stated condition holds for both
i=1,n
. Consider now any index
2\leqslant i\leqslant n-1
. Note that

\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i-1}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i-1})\leqslant\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i})\leqslant\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i-1}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i-1})+2.

Hence,
\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i})=\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i-1}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i-1})+k,
with
k\in\{0,1,2\}
, and we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\delta_{i}=d_{I}(\mathcal{F}_{i},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i})&=&\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i}+\mathcal{F}_{i})-i\\
&=&\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i-1}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i-1})+k-i\\
&=&\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i-1}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i-1})-(i-1)+(k-1)\\
&=&d_{I}(\mathcal{F}_{i-1},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i-1})+(k-1)\\
&=&\delta_{i-1}+(k-1),\end{array}

where
k-1\in\{-1,0,1\}.

For the converse, assume that
(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n-1})
is a vector satisfying (
10
). This condition, along with the fact that
\delta_{0}=\delta_{n}=0
, implies
\delta_{i}\leqslant\min\{i,n-i\}
, i.e., every
\delta_{i}
is an admissible value for the injection distance between
i
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
. Using induction, we build flags
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{F}_{q}(n)
with
\mathbf{d}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})=(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n-1})
. First of all, by means of (
10
), it is clear that
\delta_{1}\in\{0,1\}
. If
\delta_{1}=0
, just take
\mathcal{F}_{1}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{1}
. Otherwise, consider any two different lines
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{1}
. Assume now that for some
1\leqslant i<n-1
, we have found nested subspaces
\mathcal{F}_{1}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq\mathcal{F}_{i}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{1}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}
such that
\delta_{j}=d_{I}(\mathcal{F}_{j},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{j})=\dim(\mathcal{F}_{j}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{j})-j,
for every
1\leqslant j\leqslant i
. Let us give convenient
u,u^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
and subspaces
\mathcal{F}_{i+1}=\mathcal{F}_{i}+\left\langle u\right\rangle
,
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i+1}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}+\left\langle u^{\prime}\right\rangle
such that
\delta_{i+1}=d_{I}(\mathcal{F}_{i+1},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i+1})
by distinguishing three situations.

•

\boldsymbol{\delta_{i+1}=\delta_{i}}
. In this case, notice that

\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i})-i=\delta_{i}=\delta_{i+1}\leqslant n-(i+1).

Thus,
\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i})\leqslant n-1
and we can choose
u\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}\setminus(\mathcal{F}_{i}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i})
to form
\mathcal{F}_{i+1}=\mathcal{F}_{i}+\left\langle u\right\rangle
. Now, if
\delta_{i}\neq 0
, we can take
u^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{F}_{i}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i})\setminus\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i+1}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}+\left\langle u^{\prime}\right\rangle
. In case
\delta_{i}=0
, then
\delta_{i+1}=0
and we just put
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i+1}=\mathcal{F}_{i+1}=\mathcal{F}_{i}+\left\langle u\right\rangle
. Note that, in any case,
\mathcal{F}_{i+1},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i+1}
are subspaces of dimension
i+1
with

\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i+1}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i+1})=\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}+\left\langle u\right\rangle)=\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i})+1=\delta_{i}+i+1.

As a consequence,
\delta_{i+1}=\delta_{i}=\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i+1}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i+1})-(i+1)
as desired.

•

\boldsymbol{\delta_{i+1}=\delta_{i}+1}
. Hence,

\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i})-i=\delta_{i}=\delta_{i+1}-1\leqslant n-(i+1)-1=n-i-2

and
\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i})\leqslant n-2
. This allows us to choose two linearly independent vectors
u,u^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
such that

\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}+\langle u\rangle+\langle u^{\prime}\rangle)=\dim(\mathcal{F}_{i}+\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i})+2.

The subspaces
\mathcal{F}_{i+1}=\mathcal{F}_{i}+\langle u\rangle
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i+1}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}+\langle u^{\prime}\rangle
satisfy the required condition.

•

\boldsymbol{\delta_{i+1}=\delta_{i}-1}
. In this situation, notice that
\delta_{i}\geqslant 1
and then
\mathcal{F}_{i}\neq\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}
. Therefore, we can find nonzero vectors
u\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{i}
and
u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}
to form two
(i+1)
-dimensional subspaces
\mathcal{F}_{i+1}=\mathcal{F}_{i}+\langle u\rangle
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i+1}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}+\langle u^{\prime}\rangle
with
d_{I}(\mathcal{F}_{i+1},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i+1})=\delta_{i+1}
.

∎

The previous theorem turns out to be the key to establish our bijection between the set of distance vectors corresponding to the full flag variety
\mathcal{F}_{q}(n)
and the set of Motzkin paths of length
n
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Given a positive integer
n\geqslant 2
, there is a bijection between the set of distance vectors
\mathcal{D}(n)
and the set of Motzkin paths
\mathcal{M}_{n}
.

Proof.

Recall that in this paper we do not distinguish between a Motzkin path and the corresponding Motzkin word. Consider
(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n-1})\in\mathcal{D}(n)
and put
\delta_{0}=\delta_{n}=0
. In light of Theorem
4.1
, for any
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, it holds

\delta_{i}-\delta_{i-1}\in\{-1,0,1\}.

Consider the following map:

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\Psi:&\mathcal{D}(n)&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
&(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n-1})&\longmapsto&p_{1}\dots p_{n},\end{array}

(11)

where, for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, we take

p_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cccc}U&\text{ if }&\delta_{i}-\delta_{i-1}=&1,\\
H&\text{ if }&\delta_{i}-\delta_{i-1}=&0,\\
D&\text{ if }&\delta_{i}-\delta_{i-1}=&-1.\end{array}\right.

(12)

Let us see that the word
\Psi((\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n-1}))
is a Motzkin word. To do so, assume that, for some
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, in the subword
p_{1}\dots p_{i}
, the letter
D
appears more times than
U
. This means that, up to the
i
-th step, the difference
\delta_{j}-\delta_{j-1}=-1
occurs more times than
\delta_{j}-\delta_{j-1}=1
. As a result, we have that

\delta_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\delta_{j}-\delta_{j-1}<0,

which is a contradiction. Similarly, since
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{j}-\delta_{j-1}=\delta_{n}-\delta_{0}=0
, the number of occurrences of both
U
and
D
coincides.

On the other hand, given
p\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
, we have that
p=p_{1}\dots p_{n}
is a sequence of
n
letters
p_{i}\in\{U,H,D\}
, for
i=1,\dots,n
such that, in particular,
p_{1}\in\{U,H\}
and
p_{n}\in\{H,D\}
. Now, for
i=1,\dots,n
, we consider the following associated values:
u_{i}
(resp.
h_{i}
,
d_{i}
) is the number of occurrences of an
U
(resp.
H
,
D
) step at or before
p_{i}
. Note that
d_{1}=0
and
u_{n}=d_{n}
. Moreover, for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, it holds
u_{i}+h_{i}+d_{i}=i
and
u_{i}\geqslant d_{i}
.
From these values, we define a map
\Phi:\mathcal{M}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(n)
as follows:

\Phi(p_{1}\dots p_{n})=(u_{1}-d_{1},u_{2}-d_{2},\dots,u_{n-1}-d_{n-1}).

(13)

As a consequence of the definition of
u_{i},h_{i},d_{i}
, we have that

(u_{i}-d_{i})-(u_{i-1}-d_{i-1})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}1&\text{if}&p_{i}=U,\\
0&\text{if}&p_{i}=H,\\
-1&\text{if}&p_{i}=D,\end{array}\right.

with
2\leqslant i\leqslant n
. As
u_{1}-d_{1}\in\{0,1\}
, by means of Theorem
4.1
,
\Phi(p_{1}\dots p_{n})
is a distance vector.

Finally, we prove that
\Phi
and
\Psi
are mutually inverse. To do so, consider
(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n-1})\in\mathcal{D}(n)
. Let us see that, if
\Phi\circ\Psi((\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n-1}))=\Phi(p_{1}p_{2}\dots p_{n})=(u_{1}-d_{1},u_{2}-d_{2},\dots,u_{n-1}-d_{n-1})
(defined as in (
13
) and (
12
)), then
\delta_{i}=u_{i}-d_{i}
, for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. We work by induction on
n
. Clearly, if
\delta_{1}=1
(resp.
\delta_{1}=0
), then
p_{1}=U
(resp.
p_{1}=H
) and
u_{1}-d_{1}=1=\delta_{1}
(resp.
u_{1}-d_{1}=0=\delta_{1}
). Assume that the result is true, for
k<n-1
, that is,
\delta_{k}=u_{k}-d_{k}
. We now that
\delta_{k+1}-\delta_{k}\in\{-1,0,1\}
. Suppose, for instance, that
\delta_{k+1}-\delta_{k}=1
. Then
p_{k+1}=U
and, as
\delta_{k}=u_{k}-d_{k}
by induction hypothesis, we have
u_{k+1}-d_{k+1}=(u_{k}-d_{k})+1=\delta_{k}+1
. Thus,
u_{k+1}-d_{k+1}=\delta_{k+1}
as desired. The cases where
\delta_{k+1}-\delta_{k}=0
or
\delta_{k+1}-\delta_{k}=-1
are analogous.

Take now
p_{1}p_{2}\dots p_{n}
a Motzkin word of length
n
such that
\Psi\circ\Phi(p_{1}p_{2}\dots p_{n})=p_{1}^{\prime}p_{2}^{\prime}\dots p_{n}^{\prime}
. Let us see that
p_{i}=p_{i}^{\prime}
, for any
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Let us work again by induction on
n
. It is clear that, if
p_{1}=U
( resp.
p_{1}=H
) then
u_{1}-d_{1}=1
(resp.
u_{1}-d_{1}=0
) and
p_{1}^{\prime}=U=p_{1}
(resp.
p_{1}^{\prime}=H=p_{1}
). Assume that
p_{k}=p_{k}^{\prime}
for
k<n
. If
p_{k+1}^{\prime}=U
, then
u_{k+1}-d_{k+1}-(u_{k}-d_{k})=1
which means that
p_{k+1}=U=p_{k+1}^{\prime}
. The cases
p_{k+1}^{\prime}=H
or
p_{k+1}^{\prime}=D
go analogously. Hence
\Psi
is a bijection as we wanted to show.
∎

4.2
Flag distance equals area

Let us show that the map
\Psi

Proposition 4.8
.

Consider
v=(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n-1})\in\mathcal{D}(n)
with
\delta_{i}\neq 0
for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. Then, there is
r\geqslant 1
a positive integer and vectors

(14)

Proof.

Take
v=(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n-1})\in\mathcal{D}(n)
with
\delta_{i}\neq 0
for

. Note that, in this case,
\delta_{1}=\delta_{n-1}=1
. Take

and, for any

define a vector

such that
v_{i}^{k}=0
if

and
v_{i}^{k}=1
otherwise. With this choice the desired equality (
14
) holds.

∎

(15)

given by

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\mathcal{F}^{1}&=&(\left\langle e_{1}\right\rangle,&\left\langle e_{1},e_{2}\right\rangle,&\left\langle e_{1},e_{2},e_{4}\right\rangle),\\
\mathcal{F}^{2}&=&(\left\langle e_{1}\right\rangle,&\left\langle e_{1},e_{3}\right\rangle,&\left\langle e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\right\rangle),\\
\mathcal{F}^{3}&=&(\left\langle e_{2}\right\rangle,&\left\langle e_{2},e_{3}\right\rangle,&\left\langle e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\right\rangle).\end{array}

Notice that

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccccc}d_{f}(\mathcal{F}^{1},\mathcal{F}^{2})&=&0&+&1&+&1&=&2,\\
d_{f}(\mathcal{F}^{1},\mathcal{F}^{3})&=&1&+&1&+&1&=&3,\\
d_{f}(\mathcal{F}^{2},\mathcal{F}^{3})&=&1&+&1&+&0&=&2.\end{array}

Hence, we have
d_{f}(\mathcal{C})=2
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{C})=\{(0,1,1),(1,1,0)\}\subsetneq\mathcal{D}(2,4).
On the other hand, there are more distance vectors in
\mathcal{D}(2,4)
. It suffices to apply Theorem
4.1
and see that
(1,0,1)
is an element in
\mathcal{D}(2,4).

Directly from Corollary
4.15
we can state the following results concerning full flag codes.

Corollary 4.19
.

Given a full flag code
\mathcal{C}
in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n},
the number of distance vectors in
\mathcal{D}(d_{f}(\mathcal{C}),n)
equals the number of Motzkin paths
p\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
such that
A(p)=d_{f}(\mathcal{C})
, i.e., the value
T(n,d_{f}(\mathcal{C}))
.

Remark 4.20
.

Notice that the previous result counts the number of possible distance vectors associated to the minimum distance of a flag code. Nevertheless, this number might not coincide with the exact number of distance vectors of the code. For instance, if we take
\mathcal{F}^{1}
and
\mathcal{C}
as in Example
4.18
, it holds
d_{f}(\mathcal{C})=2
and
|\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{C})|=2.
Moreover, if we consider
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\{\mathcal{F}^{1},\mathcal{F}^{4}\},
with

\mathcal{F}^{4}=(\left\langle e_{2}\right\rangle,\left\langle e_{1},e_{2}\right\rangle,\left\langle e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\right\rangle),

then we also have
d_{f}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})=2
but
|\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})|=1
. Even more, the sets
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{C})
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
do not share any distance vector and form a partition of
\mathcal{D}(2,4)
, which contains
T(4,2)=3
elements.

On the other hand, in the special case of a flag code on
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
with minimum distance
D^{n}
, we can give the exact number of its distance vectors (observe that
T(n,D^{n})=1
), as stated in the next result.

Corollary 4.21
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a full flag code in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
such that
d_{f}(\mathcal{C})=D^{n}
. Then
\mathcal{C}
has a unique distance vector.

We finish this section by computing the number of possible distance vectors that a disjoint full flag code can have.

Proposition 4.22
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a disjoint full flag code in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
. Then
d_{f}(\mathcal{C})\geqslant n-1
and the number of possible distance vectors for
\mathcal{C}
is
T(n-2,d_{f}(\mathcal{C})-n+1)
.

Proof.

First of all, notice that, if
\mathcal{C}
is disjoint, then its minimum distance is attained by distance vectors
(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n-1})
with no zero components, i.e.,
\delta_{i}\geqslant 1
, for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant n-1
. Hence, such vectors can be decomposed into

(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n-1})=(1,\dots,1)+(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n-3},\gamma_{n-2})

with
\gamma_{i}=\delta_{i+1}-1
, for every
0\leqslant i\leqslant n-2
. Notice that
\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{n-2}=0
and, for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant n-2
, it holds

\gamma_{i}-\gamma_{i-1}=\delta_{i+1}-\delta_{i}\in\{-1,0,1\}.

As a consequence, and by means of Theorem
4.1
,
(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n-1})
can be naturally identified with the distance vector
(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n-3})\in\mathcal{D}(n-2)
, associated to the distance value

\sum_{i=1}^{n-3}\gamma_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-3}(\delta_{i+1}-1)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}(\delta_{i+1}-1)=d_{f}(\mathcal{C})-n+1.

Hence, the number of distance vectors in
\mathcal{D}(d_{f}(\mathcal{C}),n)
with no zero components, that is, the number of potential distance vectors for
\mathcal{C}
, coincides with the number
T(n-2,d_{f}(\mathcal{C})-n+1)
.
∎

4.4
Other cases, other sequences

As it was done in Subsection
4.1
with the case of disjoint flag codes, it can be very useful to interpret the properties of a given family of flags in terms of a concrete family of Motzkin paths in order to localize a relevant sequence of integers to count the number of associated distance vectors. To this end we contemplate a last family of flags.

Proposition 4.23
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{n}
be the set of full flags on
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
such that given
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
, they never share consecutive subspaces. Hence, the number of possible distance vectors corresponding to couples of flags in
\mathcal{H}_{n}
is given by the sequence of Riordan numbers, that is, sequence
A005043
in
[
17
]
.

Proof.

It is enough to observe that a couple of flags in
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
never presents consecutive collapses, that is, their associated distance vector
\textbf{d}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
never has two consecutive null components. Thus, the Motzkin path
\Psi(\textbf{d}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}))
does not have horizontal steps in the
x
-axis and, as consequence, it is a Riordan path.
∎

5
Conclusions and open questions

In this paper we have addressed the problem of counting the number of distance vectors associated with the full flag variety
\mathcal{F}_{q}(n)
. Moreover, we have provided the number of possible distance vectors for a full flag code with prescribed minimum distance either in the general case or in the disjoint one.
The key to compute these cardinalities is to associate biunivocally a Motzkin word of length
n
with a distance vector in
\mathcal{D}(n)
.

The problem of calculating the possible number of distance vectors when we consider flags of general type
t=(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})
is still open. We believe that, to deal with this question, it could be useful the use of Motzkin paths of higher rank where other kind of steps are allowed (see
[
13
]
).

On the other hand, taking into account the definition of the distance in the context of multishot codes (
[
15
,
16
]
), not necessarily flag codes, we think that our work can be a starting point in the study of the possible distributions of the total distance by using lattice paths.
