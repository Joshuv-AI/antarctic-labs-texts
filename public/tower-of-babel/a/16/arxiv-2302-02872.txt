Abstract
. A recent advance by Smith [ Smi21 ] establishes a quantitative converse (conjectured by Smyth and Serre) to Fekete’s celebrated theorem for compact subsets of ℝ \mathbb{R} . Answering a basic question raised by Smith, we formulate and prove a quantitative converse of Fekete for general symmetric compact subsets of ℂ \mathbb{C} . We highlight and exploit the algorithmic nature of our approach to give concrete applications to abelian varieties over finite fields and to extremal problems in algebraic number theory.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

This paper is motivated by the following question. What compact sets
\Sigma
in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
contain infinitely many sets of conjugate algebraic integers, and how are they distributed in
\Sigma
? The ideas of this project originated in the works of many, including Schur
[
Sch18
]
, Fekete
[
Fek23
]
, Siegel
[
Sie45
]
, Robinson
[
Rob64b
]
, Smyth
[
Smy84
]
, Rumely
[
Rum13
]
, Serre
[
Ser19
]
, and Smith
[
Smi21
]
on algebraic integers. We refer the reader to the excellent books of Baker and Rumely
[
BR10
]
, McKee and Smyth
[
MS21
]
and Serre
[
Ser20
]
on this subject.
We introduce the basic concepts and state our theorems which answer some open problems raised in previous works.

Let
\Sigma
be a compact subset of the complex plane. Let

(1)

d_{\Sigma}(n):=\max_{z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\in\Sigma}\prod_{i<j}|z_{i}-z_{j}|^{\frac{2}{n(n-1)}}.

Fekete proved the following limit exists and called it the transfinite diameter of
\Sigma
:

(2)

d_{\Sigma}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{\Sigma}(n).

For example, the transfinite diameter of a circle of radius
r
is
r
. The transfinite diameter of
\Sigma
equals the
capacity
of
\Sigma

[
Ahl10
, Chapter 2]
.
Fekete
[
Fek23
]
, generalizing some results of Schur
[
Sch18
]
, proved that if
d_{\Sigma}<1
, then there is only a finite number of irreducible monic integral polynomials such that all of their roots lie in
\Sigma
. Very recently, Kollar and Sarnak
[
KS21
]

applied this result of Fekete together with combinatorial arguments to give
general lower bounds for the size and shape of the spectrum of three regular graphs. Remarkably, these
bounds are sharp.

Note that Fekete’s condition
d_{\Sigma}<1
is optimal. For example, let
\Sigma
be the unit circle. Then primitive roots of unity give an infinite number of irreducible integral polynomials such that their roots lie all in
\Sigma
. The strict converse is not true. For example, the circle of radius
r>1
around the origin where
r
is not an algebraic integer gives a counter-example. However, Fekete and Szegö
[
FS55
]
proved that if
\Sigma
is symmetric about the real axis and
d_{\Sigma}\geq 1
, then any open set
D
including
\Sigma
will contain infinitely many sets of conjugate algebraic integers. Robinson
[
Rob64a
]
proved an analogous theorem about real point sets using the properties of Chebyshev’s polynomials of
\Sigma
.

Smyth showed that if
\mu
is the limit of uniform probability measures attached to distinct conjugate algebraic integers
all of whose conjugates lie in a compact set
\Sigma
of the complex plane, then

\int\log|Q(x)|d\mu(x)\geq 0

for every
Q(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
.
In a recent breakthrough, Smith
[
Smi21
]
proved that these necessary conditions are sufficient for
\mu
to be a limiting measure when
\Sigma
is
a subset of
\mathbb{R}
satisfying some conditions. In this paper, we establish this result for essentially any compact symmetric subset of the complex plane
\Sigma
and our new method
gives a different treatment of Smith’s Theorem. In particular, we introduce some new constraints on the limiting measures of conjugate algebraic integers. For a limiting measure
\mu
, we prove in Theorem
1.8
that

\int\log(|Q(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})|)d\mu(x_{1})\dots d\mu(x_{n})\geq 0

for every
Q(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. In an upcoming work, we formulate a convex optimization problem based on the above new constraints and improve the lower bound on the Schur-Siegel-Smyth trace problem to the new best result 1.7982. Moreover, Theorem
1.8
allows us to improve some results of Smith
[
Smi21
, Proposition 3.5]
stated in Proposition
2.13
. This improvement is essential in counting algebraic integers with a prescribed distribution
[
Tal23
]
.

Smith’s method is based on the earlier works of Hilbert
[
Hil94
]
that used the geometry of numbers and Robinson
[
Rob64a
]
. As mentioned earlier, Robinson used the properties of Chebyshev’s polynomials of
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}
. Smith used some asymptotics of Chebyshev’s polynomials proved in
[
CSZ17a
]
that are not known for compact subsets of the complex plane. Our main technical invention in this paper is the new proof of Robinson’s result and Smith’s results without using the properties of Chebyshev’s polynomials; see Proposition
2.16
.
Our method is based on the probabilistic sampling of roots with respect to the equilibrium measure and then deforming the roots with a greedy algorithm along a gradient vector; see subsection
2.6
and Proposition
2.16
.

In addition to the generalization of Smith’s result to the complex plane, we also prove and implement a polynomial time algorithm which given an oracle to a probability measure
\mu
(satisfying some technical conditions) on rectangles in the complex plane and a positive integer
n
, it outputs a degree
n
polynomial with root distribution modelling
\mu
. Our algorithm and its implementation have no prior analogue to the best of our knowledge. Our numerical results show some new features of the algebraic integers that we discuss further in subsection
1.2.2
.

1.2.
Main results

1.2.1.
Arithmetic probability measures

Suppose that
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{C}
is compact and symmetric about the real axis with

Let

be the space of probability measures supported on
\Sigma

\Sigma(\rho):=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z-\sigma|<\rho\text{ for some }\sigma\in\Sigma\}.

If

Note that

and is only defined for

Definition 1.1
.

A measure

is called a

if

is the weak* limit of a sequence of distinct uniform probability measures on a complete set of conjugate (totally real) algebraic integers lying eventually inside (

for every

\Sigma(\varepsilon)
for every
\varepsilon>0

The set of all arithmetic probability measures is denoted (by

\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}.

Next, we define a convex subset of

that includes

Let

be a polynomial with complex coefficients of degree

\mu_{P}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\alpha_{i}},

Suppose that

Then
\mu_{P_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\mu

should also be symmetric about the real axis. Let

be a polynomial with integral coefficients. Note that if

\int\log|Q(x)|d\mu_{P_{n}}(x)=\frac{\log|\text{Res}(Q,P_{n})|}{\deg(P_{n})}\geq 0,

By taking

\int\log|Q(x)|d\mu(x)\geq 0

for every non-zero
Q(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x].
Let

Since

it follows that the equilibrium measure of

.
Note that

is a convex subset of

Serre
[

]
proved that there exists

and
\mu\in\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}xd\mu<1.8984.
Smith
[
Smi21
]
proved that if
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}
satisfies certain technical conditions (including Serre’s construction) then
\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma_{\mathbb{R}}}=\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}.
This gave a definite answer to the Schur–Siegel–Smyth Trace Problem. Finding
\mu\in\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}
with the minimal expected value

is a linear programming problem
and finding its optimal solution is an open problem; see

. It is the analog of finding the magic function in the linear programming problem formulated by Cohn-Elkies for the sphere packing problem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{C}
is compact and symmetric about the real axis. If
d_{\Sigma}<1
, then
\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}=\emptyset.
Otherwise,
d_{\Sigma}\geq 1
and

\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}\neq\emptyset.
Moreover, if
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}
is compact then
\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma_{\mathbb{R}}}.

Next, we introduce some notation and state a corollary of our result that generalizes the main theorem of Smith
[
Smi21
]
to the complex plane.
Let

[a,b]\times[c,d]:=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\textup{Re}(z)\in[a,b]\text{ and }\textup{Im}(z)\in[c,d]\right\}\subset\mathbb{C}.

We call
[a,b]\times[c,d]

c=d=0
.
A measure
\mu
is a Hölder probability measure if there exists
\delta>0
and
A>0
such that

\mu([a,b]\times[c,d])\leq A\max(|b-a|,|c-d|)^{\delta}

for every

Suppose that
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}
is a finite union of closed intervals with

\mu\in\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}

Smith’s main theorem
[

[

\Sigma

As we discussed, let

be the circle of radius

around the origin where
r
is not an algebraic integer, and
\mu_{r}\in B_{C_{r}}
be the uniform measure on

. Then

]
to Smith’s theorem over
\mathbb{C}
. Knowing this, Smith raised the question of extending his result to
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{C}.
We state a corollary of our main theorem that recovers Smith’s theorem and generalizes it to the complex plane.

Corollary 1.3
.

Suppose that
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{C}
is compact and symmetric about the real axis and is a finite union of disjoint rectangles and real intervals with
d_{\Sigma}>1
. If
\mu\in\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}
, then there exists a sequence of conjugate algebraic integers lying inside
\Sigma
and equidistributing to
\mu.

Smith’s method does not directly imply Corollary
1.3

\Sigma\subset\mathbb{C}
to be connected. This condition is trivially satisfied for compact
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R},
but fails for some
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{C}

We introduce new ideas to prove the above theorem. This is discussed in Section

Next, we introduce some new notation to state a quantitative version of the Theorem
1.2
under some assumptions. Let

(4)

U_{\mu}(z):=\int\log|z-w|d\mu(w),

\int-\log|z-w|d\mu(w)

We cite the following definition from Smith
[

Definition 1.4
.

Fix a Hölder measure
\mu

\Sigma\subset\mathbb{C}

\|P\|_{n}:=\max_{z\in\mathbb{C}}\left(e^{-nU_{\mu}(z)}|P(z)|\right).

It follows from

Theorem 1.5
.

Suppose

is compact and has empty interior with connected complement. Suppose that
\mu\in\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}
is a Hölder measure with exponent

and

1.4.
Method of proofs

In this section, we sketch proofs of our main theorems. Our main tool for studying the distribution of the roots of polynomials is
Jensen’s formula. Let
P(z)
be a polynomial with complex coefficients and fix
w\in\mathbb{C}.
If
z_{1},\dots,z_{m}
are the roots of
P(z)
inside the disc
|z-w|<R
and there is no zero on
|z-w|=R,
then Jensen’s formula states

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|P(Re^{i\theta}+w)|d\theta-\log|P(w)|=\log\frac{R^{m}}{|z_{1}-w|\dots|z_{m}-w|}.

In particular,

(5)

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|P(Re^{i\theta}+w)|d\theta-\log|P(w)|\geq 0.

Suppose that
P
is a monic polynomial. We have
|P(w)|=|z_{1}-w|\cdots|z_{n}-w|
where
n=\deg(P).
Hence for a monic polynomial
P
, we have

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\log|P(Re^{i\theta}+w)|}{n}d\theta=\frac{m}{n}\log R+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}\log|z_{i}-w|,

where
|z_{i}-w|<R
for
1\leq i\leq m
and
|z_{i}-w|>R
for
m+1\leq i\leq n.
We rewrite the above as

(6)

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\log|P(Re^{i\theta}+w)|}{n}d\theta=\int\log(z;w,R)d\mu_{P}(z),

where

\log(z;w,R):=\begin{cases}\log|z-w|&\text{ if }|z-w|>R,\\
\log{R}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

It follows from (
6
), (
33
), and (
34
) that
\{\mu_{P_{n}}\}
converges weakly to a given probability measure
\mu
if

(7)

\frac{\log|P_{n}(z)|}{n}=U_{\mu}(z)+o(1)

for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|z-z_{i,n}|>e^{-\sqrt{n}}
, where
z_{i,n}
are complex roots of
P_{n}.

We note that
\frac{\log|P_{n}(z)|}{n}
is a harmonic function with singularities at roots of
P_{n}
and

\frac{\log|P_{n}(z)|}{n}=\log|z|+O\left(\frac{1}{|z|}\right).

We note that
U_{\mu}(z)
has a similar behavior as
|z|\to\infty.
Since
U_{\mu}(z)
and
\frac{\log|P_{n}(z)|}{n}
are both harmonic functions away from the roots of
P_{n}
and the support of
\mu
with similar asymptotic behavior at infinity, it follows from the mean value theorem for harmonic functions that (
7
) is equivalent to

(8)

\frac{\log|P_{n}(z)|}{n}\leq U_{\mu}(z)+o(1)

for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
assuming
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma
is connected and the interior of
\Sigma
is empty
[
ST97
, Chapter III, Theorem 4.2]
.
Let

(9)

K_{n}:=\left\{p(x)\in\mathbb{R}[x]:\deg(p)\leq n,\frac{\log|p(z)|}{n}\leq U_{\mu}(z)\text{ for every }z\in\mathbb{C}\right\}.

K_{n}
is a symmetric, convex region inside the vector space of polynomials with real coefficients and degree less than or equal to
n
. For proving Theorem
1.5
, we use Minkowski’s second theorem to prove the existence of integral polynomials of degree
n
inside
K_{n}
up to a sub-exponential factor in
n
. The key observation is that the necessary condition (
3
) implies
K_{n}
is well-rounded which means the successive minima of the lattice of integral polynomials with respect to
K_{n}
are close to each other and a lower bound on the volume of
K
implies the existence of integral polynomials of degree
n
inside
K_{n}
up to a sub-exponential factor in
n
. The following theorem is an important technical result that allows us to use Minkowski’s second theorem.

Theorem 1.8
.

Suppose that
\mu
is a probability measure with compact support on the complex plane. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\mu
is invariant by complex conjugation and (
3
) holds for every non-zero
Q(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x].

(2)

\mu
is invariant by complex conjugation and

(10)

\int\log|Q_{m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})|d\mu(x_{1})\dots d\mu(x_{m})\geq 0

for every non-zero
Q_{m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}].

Recall that

\Sigma(\rho):=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z-\sigma|<\rho\text{ for some }\sigma\in\Sigma\}.

Note that for any open set
D
containing
\Sigma,
there exist
\rho>0
such that
\Sigma(\rho)\subset D.

For
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{C}
, we use Rouché’s theorem to show that all roots of our irreducible polynomial are inside
\Sigma(\rho)
and hence

. This is similar to the method of Fekete and Szegö
[

For

, Robinson used the Chebychev polynomials and constructed an integral polynomial of degree

with

to prove that the roots are real and inside

Our results do not rely on the existence of Chebychev’s polynomials on

; see subsection

and Proposition

.
Proposition
2.16
allows us to improve some results of Smith
[

, Proposition 4.1]
.

For proving Theorem

in polynomial time in

for some constant

dependent on

. In fact, we take the polynomial to be monic and Eisenstein at the prime 2. Note that this differs from the strongest bound for which we prove existence which is
e^{Cn^{1-\delta_{0}}}K_{n}
for some explicit

2.
Minkowski’s theorem applied to the lattice of integral polynomials

2.1.
Logarithmic potentials

Recall the notation
in subsections
1.2
and
1.4
. In this section, we assume that
\mu
is supported on a compact set
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{C}
and is a Hölder probability measure on the complex plane with exponent
\delta>0.
Hence for every
a<b,c<d
, and some
A>0
,

\mu([a,b]\times[c,d])\leq A\max(|b-a|,|c-d|)^{\delta}.

For two compact subsets
B,B^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{C}
, let

d(B,B^{\prime}):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in B\\
z^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}\end{subarray}}|z-z^{\prime}|.

Recall the definition of the logarithmic potential of
\mu
from (
4
):

U_{\mu}(z)=\int\log|z-w|d\mu(w).

Finally, define the logarithmic energy of
\mu
as

I(\mu):=\int\log|z_{1}-z_{2}|d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{2})=\int U_{\mu}(z)d\mu(z).

We prove a lemma on the asymptotic behaviour of logarithmic potentials.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
\mu
is a probability measure with compact support inside
|z|<r
for
z\in\mathbb{C}.
If
|w|>2r
, we have

U_{\mu}(w)=\log|w|+O\left(\frac{r}{|w|}\right).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\left|U_{\mu}(w)-\log|w|\right|\leq\int\left|\log|w-x|-\log|w|\right|d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int\left|\log\left|1-\frac{x}{w}\right|\right|d\mu(x)\ll\frac{r}{|w|}.

∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that
\mu
is a Hölder probability measure on the complex plane with exponent
\delta>0
. Then for any
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
with
|z_{1}-z_{2}|<1/2,
we have

\left|U_{\mu}(z_{1})-U_{\mu}(z_{2})\right|\ll|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\delta^{\prime}},

where
\delta^{\prime}<\min(1/2,\delta/2)
is any positive exponent.

Proof.

Suppose that
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
and
|z_{1}-z_{2}|<1/2.
We have

\displaystyle\left|U_{\mu}(z_{1})-U_{\mu}(z_{2})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int\log\frac{|z_{1}-w|}{|z_{2}-w|}d\mu(w)\right|.

We define the following covering of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
:

\displaystyle U_{0}:

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in\mathbb{C}:|w-z_{2}|>2\sqrt{|z_{1}-z_{2}|}\right\},

\displaystyle U_{1}:

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in\mathbb{C}:|z_{1}-z_{2}|<\min(|w-z_{2}|,|w-z_{1}|)\leq 2\sqrt{|z_{1}-z_{2}|}\right\},

\displaystyle U_{k}:

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in\mathbb{C}:|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{k}<\min(|w-z_{2}|,|w-z_{1}|)\leq|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{k-1}\right\},

where
k\geq 2
. Hence,

\displaystyle\left|U_{\mu}(z_{1})-U_{\mu}(z_{2})\right|\leq\sum_{i\geq 0}\left|\int_{U_{i}}\log\frac{|z_{1}-w|}{|z_{2}-w|}d\mu(w)\right|.

For
w\in U_{0}
, we have
\left|\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{z_{2}-w}\right|<1/2
. We write the Taylor expansion of
\log
and obtain

\left|\int_{U_{0}}\log\frac{|z_{1}-w|}{|z_{2}-w|}d\mu(w)\right|=\left|\int_{U_{0}}\log\left|1+\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{z_{2}-w}\right|d\mu(w)\right|\leq\sum_{m\geq 1}\int_{U_{0}}\frac{1}{m}\left|\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{z_{2}-w}\right|^{m}d\mu(w)\ll|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{1/2}.

For
w\in U_{k}
, where
k\geq 1
, we have

\left|\int_{U_{k}}\log\frac{|z_{1}-w|}{|z_{2}-w|}d\mu(w)\right|\leq\mu(U_{k})k\big|\log|z_{1}-z_{2}|\big|\ll|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\frac{k\delta}{2}}k\big|\log|z_{1}-z_{2}|\big|,

where we used
\mu(U_{k})\ll|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\frac{k\delta}{2}}
, because
\mu
is a Hölder probability measure with exponent
\delta>0
.
Therefore,

\left|U_{\mu}(z_{1})-U_{\mu}(z_{2})\right|\ll|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{1/2}+\sum_{k\geq 1}|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{k\delta/2}k\big|\log|z_{1}-z_{2}|\big|\ll|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{1/2}+|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\delta/2}\big|\log|z_{1}-z_{2}|\big|.

Finally, we have

\left|U_{\mu}(z_{1})-U_{\mu}(z_{2})\right|\ll|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\delta^{\prime}},

where
\delta^{\prime}<\min(1/2,\delta/2)
is any positive exponent.
∎

2.2.
Minkowski’s theorem

Recall the definition of
K_{n}
from (
9
):

K_{n}=\left\{p(x)\in\mathbb{R}[x]:\deg(p)\leq n\text{ and }\frac{\log|p(z)|}{n}\leq U_{\mu}(z)\text{ for every }z\in\mathbb{C}\right\}.

We identify the space of polynomials with real coefficients of degree less than or equal to
n
with
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
by sending a polynomial to its coefficients. It is easy to see that
K_{n}
is a symmetric convex region of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that
\mu
is a probability measure with compact support inside
|z|<r
for
z\in\mathbb{C}.
Let
p(x)\in\lambda K_{n}
for some
\lambda>0
where
\deg(p)<n
. Then

xp(x)\in r\lambda K_{n}.

Proof.

Let

h(z):=U_{\mu}(z)-\frac{\log|zp(z)|}{n}.

We note that
h(z)
is a harmonic function on
|z|>r
and outside roots of
p.
By Lemma
2.1
, we have

h(z)=\frac{n-\deg(p)-1}{n}\log|z|+\frac{\log|a_{p}|}{n}+O(1/|z|),

where
a_{p}
is the top coefficient of
p.
By the above and since
\deg(p)<n
and
h(z)
is harmonic, the minimum of
h(z)
is obtained inside
|z|<r
. Let
C:=\inf_{z\in\mathbb{C}}h(z)=\inf_{|z|<r}h(z).
Since
p(x)\in\lambda K_{n}
, we have

|zp(z)|\leq r\lambda e^{nU_{\mu}(z)}

for any
|z|<r.
This implies that

C\geq-\frac{\log r\lambda}{n}.

Therefore,

e^{nU_{\mu}(z)-\log|zp(z)|}=e^{nh(z)}\geq e^{nC}\geq(r\lambda)^{-1}

for any
z\in\mathbb{C}.
This completes the proof of our lemma.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that
p(x)\in\lambda K_{n}
for some
\lambda>0.
We claim that

p^{\prime}(x)\in An^{C}\lambda K_{n},

where
p^{\prime}
is the derivative of
p
and
A
and
C
are constants that only depend on
\mu.

Proof.

Let
D(z_{0},r)
be the disk centered at some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
and radius
0<r<\frac{1}{2}.
By Bernstein inequality
[
BE95
, Corollary 5.1.6]
, for any
r>0
,

\sup_{z\in D(z_{0},r)}\left|p^{\prime}(z)\right|\leq\frac{\deg p}{r}\sup_{z\in D(z_{0},r)}|p(z)|.

Hence, we have

|p^{\prime}(z_{0})|\leq\frac{n}{r}\sup_{z\in D(z_{0},r)}|p(z)|\leq\lambda\frac{n}{r}\sup_{z\in D(z_{0},r)}e^{nU_{\mu}(z)}.

By Lemma
2.2
, we have for any
z\in D(z_{0},r)
,

e^{nU_{\mu}(z)}=e^{n(U_{\mu}(z)-U_{\mu}(z_{0}))}e^{nU_{\mu}(z_{0})}\leq e^{A^{\prime}nr^{\delta^{\prime}}}e^{nU_{\mu}(z_{0})}.

for some
A^{\prime}>0
and
\delta^{\prime}>0
that only depends on
\mu.
By taking
r=n^{-C^{\prime}}
for any
C^{\prime}>1/\delta^{\prime}
, it follows that

|p^{\prime}(z_{0})|\leq\lambda\frac{n}{r}e^{A^{\prime}nr^{\delta^{\prime}}}e^{nU_{\mu}(z_{0})}\leq A\lambda n^{C^{\prime}+1}e^{nU_{\mu}(z_{0})},

for any
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C},
where
A=e^{A^{\prime}}
. This implies that
p^{\prime}(x)\in An^{C}\lambda K_{n}
where
C=C^{\prime}+1.

∎

Let
\Gamma_{n}
be the lattice of integral polynomials of degree less than or equal to
n
embedded into
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The successive minima of
K_{n}
on
\Gamma_{n}
are defined by setting the
k
-th successive minimum
\lambda_{k}
to be the infimum of the numbers
\lambda
such that
\lambda K_{n}
contains
k+1
linearly-independent vectors of
\Gamma_{n}
.
We have
0<\lambda_{0}\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{n}<\infty
. Minkowski’s second theorem states that

(11)

\frac{2^{n+1}\textup{vol}(K_{n})^{-1}}{(n+1)!}\leq\lambda_{0}\lambda_{1}\dots\lambda_{n}\leq 2^{n+1}\textup{vol}(K_{n})^{-1}.

Proposition 2.5
.

We have

\lambda_{m+1}\leq An^{C}\lambda_{m}

for any
0\leq m<n
, and some constants
A
and
C
that only depend on
\mu.

Proof.

Suppose that
p_{i}\in\Gamma_{n}
for
0\leq i\leq m
form the set of linearly independent integral polynomials such that
p_{i}\in\lambda_{i}K_{n}.
If for some
0\leq i\leq m
,
p_{i}^{\prime}
is linearly independent from
\{p_{i}:0\leq i\leq m\}
, then by Lemma
2.4
,

p^{\prime}_{i}(x)\in An^{C}\lambda_{m}K_{n}.

Hence,

\lambda_{m+1}\leq An^{C}\lambda_{m}

which implies our proposition. Otherwise,
V_{m}:=\text{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\left<p_{0},\dots,p_{m}\right>
is closed under derivation. It follows that

V_{m}=\text{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\left<1,x,\dots,x^{m}\right>.

This implies that there exists
0\leq l\leq m
such that
\deg(p_{j})\leq\deg(p_{l})=m
for every
0\leq j\leq m.
Let
q(x):=xp_{l}(x).
Since
\deg q>m,

q
is linearly independent of
V_{m}.
By Lemma
2.3
,

q(x)\in A\lambda_{m}K_{n}

for some constant
A>0
that only depends on
\mu.
Hence,

\lambda_{m+1}\leq A\lambda_{m}

and this completes the proof of our proposition.
∎

2.3.
Lower bound on the volume of
K_{n}

In this subsection, we give a lower bound on the Euclidean volume of

as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.
Our method is to find
n+1
linearly independent polynomials of degree at most

inside

and compute the volume of the simplex given by the convex hull of the origin and these
n+1
points. Since
K_{n}
is symmetric, there are

simplexes with the same volume and disjoint interior inside
K_{n}
by choosing different signs for the
n+1
vertices. The total volume of these simplexes give a lower bound for the volume of
K_{n}.

d\mu_{Q}
and obtain

a_{Q}+\int\frac{\log|P_{0}(x)|}{n}d\mu_{Q}(x)\leq a_{Q}+\int\log|z-x|d\mu(z)d\mu_{Q}(x)+\frac{Cd}{n^{\delta}}=\int U_{Q}(z)d\mu(z)+\frac{Cd}{n^{\delta}}.

It is enough to show that

(40)

na_{Q}+\int\log|P_{0}(x)|d\mu_{Q}(x)\geq 0,

since (
37
) follows by letting
n\to\infty.

In fact, we prove that for every integral polynomial
P(x),

(41)

\deg(P)a_{Q}+\int\log|P(x)|d\mu_{Q}(x)\geq 0.

This implies (
40
), since
\deg(P_{0})\leq n
and
a_{Q}\geq 0.

For proving (
41
), we consider
P
as an integral polynomial in
x_{m+1}
. Define

R(Q,P)(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}):=Res(Q(x_{1},\dots,x_{m+1}),P(x_{m+1}))\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}]

to be the resultant of
Q
and
P
as polynomials in the variable
x_{m+1}
. By our assumption,
Q
is irreducible and does not divide
P(x_{m+1}).
Hence,
Res(Q(x_{1},\dots,x_{m+1}),P(x_{m+1}))\neq 0.

By our induction hypothesis,

\int\log R(Q,P)(z_{1},\dots,z_{m})d\mu(z_{1})\dots d\mu(z_{m})\geq 0.

We note that

\log R(Q,P)(z_{1},\dots,z_{m})=\deg(P)\log|a_{d}(z_{1},\dots,z_{m})|+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log|P(\xi_{i}(z_{1},\dots,z_{m}))|.

Hence,

\int\deg(P)\log|a_{d}(z_{1},\dots,z_{m})|+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log|P(\xi_{i}(z_{1},\dots,z_{m}))|d\mu(z_{1})\dots d\mu(z_{m})\geq 0.

The above implies (
41
) by definitions of
\mu_{Q}
and
a_{Q}
in (
38
) and (
39
). This completes our proof and implies

\int\log|Q(z_{1},\dots,z_{m},z_{m+1})|d\mu(z_{1})\dots d\mu(z_{m})d\mu(z_{m+1})\geq 0.

∎

2.5.
Lower bound on the product of Minkowski’s minima factors

First, we give a lower bound on the product of the Minkowski minima factors by using Theorem
1.8
.

Proposition 2.13
.

We have

\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\lambda_{i}\geq\frac{1}{m!}e^{(-nm+\frac{(m-1)m}{2})I_{\mu}}.

Proof.

By definition of
\{\lambda_{i}:0\leq i\leq m-1\}
, there exists linearly independent integral polynomials
\{P_{0},\dots,P_{m-1}\}
, where

|P_{i}(z)|\leq\lambda_{i}e^{nU_{\mu}(z)}.

for every
z\in\mathbb{C}.
Let
\{z_{1},\dots,z_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{C}.

Therefore,

(42)

\det[P_{i}(z_{j})]\leq m!\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\lambda_{i}e^{nU_{\mu}(z_{i})}.

On the other hand, we have

\det[P_{i}(x_{j})]=Q_{m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\prod_{i<j}(x_{i}-x_{j})

for some
Q_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}].
By taking the average of the above, we obtain

\int\log|\det[P_{i}(z_{j})]|d\mu(z_{1})\dots d\mu(z_{m})=\frac{(m-1)m}{2}I_{\mu}+\int\log|Q_{m}(z_{1},\dots,z_{m})|d\mu(z_{1})\dots d\mu(z_{m}),

where

I(\mu):=\int\log|z_{1}-z_{2}|d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{2}).

By the second part of Theorem (
1.8
), we have

\int\log|Q_{m}(z_{1},\dots,z_{m})|d\mu(z_{1})\dots d\mu(z_{m})\geq 0.

Hence,

\int\log|\det[P_{i}(z_{j})]|d\mu(z_{1})\dots d\mu(z_{m})\geq\frac{(m+1)m}{2}I_{\mu}.

By (
42
), we obtain

mnI_{\mu}+\sum_{i}\log|\lambda_{i}|+\log(m!)\geq\int\log|\det[P_{i}(z_{j})]|d\mu(z_{1})\dots d\mu(z_{m})\geq\frac{(m+1)m}{2}I_{\mu}.

Therefore,

\prod_{i}\lambda_{i}\geq\frac{1}{m!}e^{(-nm+\frac{(m-1)m}{2})I_{\mu}}.

∎

Proposition 2.14
.

We have

\lambda_{n}\leq n^{cn^{1-\frac{\delta}{12}}}.

If
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}
, then
\lambda_{n}\leq e^{cn^{1-\frac{\delta}{12}}}
.

Proof.

Let
m=n-n^{\delta_{1}}
where
0<\delta_{1}<1
is specified later. By Proposition
2.13
,

\prod_{i=0}^{m}\lambda_{i}\geq\frac{1}{m!}e^{-nm+\frac{(m-1)m}{2}I_{\mu}}.

By Minkowski’s second theorem and Proposition
2.10
,

\lambda_{0}\lambda_{1}\dots\lambda_{n}\leq 2^{n+1}\textup{vol}(K_{n})^{-1}\ll 2^{n+1}n^{cn^{2-\frac{\delta}{6}}}e^{-\frac{n^{2}}{2}I(\mu)}.

By combining the above inequalities, we have

\prod_{i=m+1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\ll n^{cn^{2-\frac{\delta}{6}}}e^{-\frac{(n-m)^{2}}{2}I(\mu)}.

By Proposition
2.11
, we have
I(\mu)\geq 0
and

\prod_{i=m+1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\ll n^{cn^{2-\frac{\delta}{6}}}.

Hence, by taking
\delta_{1}=1-\delta/12,
we obtain

\lambda_{m+1}\leq n^{cn^{2-\frac{\delta}{6}-\delta_{1}}}\leq n^{cn^{1-\frac{\delta}{12}}}.

By Proposition
2.5
, we have

\lambda_{n}\leq\lambda_{m}(An)^{C(n-m)}\leq n^{cn^{1-\frac{\delta}{12}}}.

This completes the proof of our proposition. Note that we may change constant
c
to a larger constant in the above lines of argument.
∎

Equilibrium measure

]

Suppose that

Let

for any

where

and
\rho.
Let

By (

Proposition 2.15
.

Given a compact subset

a monic polynomial with real coefficients

, and
\rho>0

Moreover, the coefficient of

in

is an even integer for every

, and all complex roots of

are inside

Proof.

Recall
T_{m}(x)
in (
44
) for
m\in\mathbb{Z},
and define

q_{k,l}(x):=\left(T_{k}(x)+a_{1}T_{k-1}(x)+\dots+a_{k}T_{0}(x)\right)^{l}

where
|a_{i}|\leq\frac{1}{l}
are chosen recursively for
1\leq i\leq k
such that
q_{k,l}(x)p(x)
has even coefficients for

where
1\leq i\leq k.
Note that this is possible since the coefficient of
x^{\deg(p)+\deg(q_{k,l})-j}
for

for

\displaystyle|q_{k,l}(z)|

\displaystyle=\left|\left(T_{k}(z)+a_{1}T_{k-1}(z)+\dots+a_{k}T_{0}(z)\right)^{l}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\left|T_{k}(z)\left(1-\frac{C}{l}\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{1}{R^{j}}\right)\right|^{l}\geq|T_{k}(z)|^{l}\left(1-\frac{C}{l(R-1)}\right)^{l}.

We fix an integer

, and let

Hence,

|q_{k,l_{0}}(z)|\geq C^{\prime}T_{k}(z)^{l_{0}}\geq C^{\prime\prime}|p_{\mathcal{Z}}(z)|^{\left\lfloor\frac{k}{n_{0}}\right\rfloor l_{0}}

where

is a fixed constant depending on our fixed parameters.
Finally, let

and

where

By (

), and (

\displaystyle|Q_{m}(z)|\geq|q_{k,l_{0}}(z)|-\sum_{i=0}^{m-k}|T_{i}(z)|\geq|p_{\mathcal{Z}}(z)|^{\frac{kl_{0}}{n_{0}}}\left(C^{\prime\prime}-\frac{C_{1}}{R^{k-1}(R-1)}\right).

Our proposition follows by taking
k>k_{0}
where

is a fixed constant.
∎

Suppose that
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{C}
is compact and symmetric about the real axis and is a finite disjoint union of rectangles and real intervals. We write

\Sigma=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{1}}(R_{i}\cup\bar{R_{i}})\bigcup_{j=1}^{N_{2}}I_{j}

where
R_{i}\subset\mathbb{C}
are rectangles and

are intervals. We define

for any

. Note that if

.

For proving Corollary
1.3
which generalizes Smith’s result to

, we use Proposition
2.16
. The statement of this proposition is motivated by Smith
[
Smi21
, Proposition 4.1]
.
However, Smith’s method does not imply this proposition. Smith’s method is based on the work of Fekete and Szegö
[

, Theorem D]
and Robinson
[
Rob64a
]
. Smith and Robinson used the properties of the Chebyshev’s polynomials from
[
CSZ17b
]
which is only proven for subsets of

and exploits the sign changes of the polynomials on real intervals. This does not work for complex sets, so our proof of Proposition
2.16
is conceptually different. Also, it allows us to keep the roots inside
\Sigma_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho)\subset\Sigma(\rho)
.

Proposition 2.16
.

Suppose that
\Sigma=\bigcup_{i}(R_{i}\cup\bar{R_{i}})\bigcup_{j}I_{j}
is compact and symmetric about the real axis and is a finite disjoint union of rectangles and real intervals and
\bigcup_{j}I_{j}\neq\emptyset

d_{\Sigma}\geq 1
. Given a monic polynomial with real coefficients
p(x)
of degree
n-m
,
\rho>0
, and an even integer

there exists a monic polynomial with real coefficients
Q_{m}(x)
such that
\deg(Q_{m})=m
and each of the following hold.

(1)

The degree
i
coefficient of the product
pQ_{m}
is an even integer for
n-m\leq i\leq n-1
.

(2)

Take
X
to be the set of roots of
Q_{m}
. Then
X
is a subset of
\Sigma_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho)
and symmetric about the real axis.

(3)

We have

\frac{\log|Q_{m}(z)|}{m}=\frac{\min_{\alpha\in X}\log|z-\alpha|}{m}+U_{\mu_{eq}}(z)+O(m^{-\frac{\delta}{8}}\log(m))

for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
and some
\delta>0,
where
\mu_{eq}
is the equilibrium measure for
\Sigma_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho/10).

(4)

Given any root
\alpha
of
Q_{m}
and any
\alpha^{\prime}
that is either a root of
p
or a boundary point of
\Sigma_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho)
, we have

n^{-3}\leq|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|.

Proof.

Let
m_{1}=\lfloor m\mu_{eq}(\bigcup_{i}R_{i}(\rho/10))\rfloor
and
m_{2}=m-2m_{1}
, where
\mu_{eq}
is the equilibrium measure of
\Sigma_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho/10)
.
Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
be as follows

(1)

x_{i}
for
i=1,2,\dots,m_{1}
are identically independently selected according to
\mu_{eq}
conditional on
x_{i}\in\bigcup_{i}R_{i}(\rho/10)
for
1\leq i\leq m_{1}
.

(2)

x_{i+m_{1}}:=\bar{x_{i}}
for
1\leq i\leq m_{1}
.

(3)

x_{2m_{1}+i}
for
i=1,2,\dots,m_{2}
are identically independently selected according to
\mu_{eq}
conditional on
x_{i}\in\bigcup_{j}I_{j}(\rho/10)
for
2m_{1}<i\leq m
.

We note that
\mu_{eq}
is Hölder with exponent
\delta\geq 1/2
on a finite union of intervals since if

\mu_{eq,[a,b]}
is larger than that of
\mu_{eq,\Sigma}
. Furthermore,
d\mu_{eq}=\frac{dx}{\pi\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}
on

.
Let
\mathcal{Z}:=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}
and
P_{\mathcal{Z}}
be the polynomial constructed in (
26
) with roots
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
.
With probability
1-o(1)
, we note that
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
satisfy conditions in (
13
), (
14
), and (
15
) with
M=m^{1/4}
,
L=(mM^{-\delta})^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}<m^{1-\varepsilon}M^{-2}
, and
n^{-2-\varepsilon}\leq|x_{i}-\alpha^{\prime}|
for any
\alpha^{\prime}
that is either a root of
p
, any other
x_{j}\neq x_{i}
, or a boundary
point of
\Sigma_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho).

This follows from a Borel-Cantelli type argument. Indeed, recall the definition of
B_{ij}
from (
13
) and let
n_{ij}
be the number of elements of
\mathcal{Z}:=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}
lying inside
B_{ij}
. Fix
i
and

and define the following random variables

y^{ij}_{k}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $x_{k}\in B_{ij}$}\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Let
p=\mu(B_{ij})
. We have
\text{Prob}(y^{ij}_{k}=1)=p
and
\text{Prob}(y^{ij}_{k}=0)=1-p.

Note that

By Markov’s inequality, we have

\text{Prob}\left(|n_{ij}-\mu(B_{ij})m|\geq L\right)\leq\frac{\text{E}\left((n_{ij}-\mu(B_{ij})m)^{2h}\right)}{L^{2h}}

for any

.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\text{E}\left((n_{ij}-\mu(B_{ij})m)^{2h}\right)=\text{E}\left(\sum_{k}y^{ij}_{k}-\text{E}(y^{ij}_{k})\right)^{2h}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{h}}\prod_{l=1}^{h}\text{E}\left(y^{ij}_{k_{l}}-\text{E}(y^{ij}_{k_{l}})\right)^{2}\ll m^{h}\left(p(1-p)\right)^{h}

\displaystyle\ll m^{h}p^{h}=\left(\mu(B_{ij})m\right)^{h}.

for any

x_{1},\dots,x_{m}

), (

), and (

) with

\Sigma_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho).

4.
The Algorithm

4.1.
Lattice Algorithms

The algorithm we develop requires access to short vectors in a lattice. As seen in the work by Micciancio
[
Mic01
]
, finding a vector whose length is within a constant multiple of the shortest vector in a lattice is NP-hard to compute. This is known as the shortest vector problem or SVP. It is still desirable in general to have access to short vectors in a lattice. An algorithm known as the LLL-algorithm, developed by Lenstra, Lenstra, and Lovász, finds a basis of vectors in a lattice in polynomial time
[
LLL82
]
where the shortest vector of the output has length within an exponential factor of the shortest vector of the lattice. In the implementation of this algorithm, we use the built-in method in PARI/GP named
qflll
. When the
qflll
method is given a matrix
A
whose columns generate a lattice, it returns a transformation matrix
T
so that
AT
is an “LLL-reduced” basis of the lattice. While the LLL-algorithm is quite reasonable in practice, the asymptotic bounds are not strong enough to prove a sub-exponential factor.

In 1986, Schnorr
[
Sch87
]
devised a parametrized family of algorithms attacking the SVP problem which output reduced bases somewhere between the stringent requirements of Korkine-Zolotarev reduction and the looser requirements of LLL reduction based on the input parameter. Careful parameter selection achieves slightly sub-exponential constant factors in polynomial time. Schnorr proved that if the algorithm produces a basis
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
, then
||b_{1}||
is within a subexponential factor of a shortest vector in the lattice. We prove that in addition, if
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
are so that
\lambda_{i}
is the least value for which there are
i
linearly independent vectors of norm at most
\lambda_{i}
, then for each
i=1,\dots,n
,
||b_{i}||
is within a sub-exponential factor of
\lambda_{i}
.
We now introduce some notation from Schnorr’s work and recall some of his results.

Let
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
be a basis. Then let
b_{i}^{*}
be the projection of
b_{i}
onto the orthogonal complement of
\text{span}\{b_{1},\dots,b_{i-1}\}
for
i=1,\dots,n
. We can get
b_{1}=b_{1}^{*},b_{2}^{*},\dots,
and
b_{n}^{*}
by Gram-Schmidt process without normalizing.
Now define
\mu_{i,j}:=\frac{\langle b_{i},b_{j}^{*}\rangle}{||b_{j}^{*}||^{2}}
.

Definition 4.1
.

We say
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
is size-reduced if
\mu_{i,j}\leq 1/2
for all
i>j
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\Lambda_{i}
be the lattice spanned by the projections of
b_{i},b_{i+1},\dots,b_{n}
onto the orthogonal complement of
\text{span}\{b_{1},\dots,b_{i-1}\}
. Then
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
is Korkine-Zolotarev reduced if
||b_{i}^{*}||
is minimal among non-zero vectors in
\Lambda_{i}
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
k\mid n
. We say that
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
is semi
k
-reduced if it is size-reduced and it satisfies both

(48)

||b_{ik}^{*}||^{2}\leq 2||b_{ik+1}^{*}||^{2},

and the components of
b_{ik+j}
for
j=1,\dots,k
orthogonal to
b_{1},\dots,b_{ik-1}
are Korkine-Zolotarev reduced for each
i=0,\dots,\frac{n}{k}-1
. Call this property (KZ).

Lastly, the constant
\alpha_{k}
is defined as
\max\frac{||b_{1}||^{2}}{||b_{k}^{*}||^{2}}
where the max is over all Korkine-Zolotarev reduced bases on lattices of rank
k
. Corollary 2.5 in Schnorr’s paper shows that
\alpha_{k}\leq k^{1+\log k}
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
be a semi
k
-reduced basis and
\lambda_{j}
be the least real number so there are
j
linearly independent vectors in the lattice spanned by
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
of norm at most
\lambda_{j}
. Then
||b_{j}||\leq k^{\frac{n}{k}\log k+O(\frac{n}{k}+\log k)}\lambda_{j}
.

The essence is a combination of the proofs of Theorems 2.3 and 3.4 in
[
Sch87
]
.

Proof.

Fix
1\leq j\leq n
. Suppose that
v_{1},\dots,v_{j}
are linearly independent vectors in the lattice with
||v_{i}||=\lambda_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,j
.
For each
i
, write
v_{i}=\sum_{s=1}^{n}c_{s,i}b_{s}
.
Let
s_{i}
be the largest index for which
c_{s_{i},i}\neq 0
. There is some
i=1,\dots,j
so that
s_{i}\geq j
by linear independence. If
c_{s,i}^{*}
is so that
v_{i}=\sum_{s=1}^{n}c_{s,i}^{*}b_{s}^{*}
, then
c_{s_{i},i}=c_{s_{i},i}^{*}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
This implies that

\lambda_{j}=||v_{j}||\geq||v_{i}||\geq||c_{s_{i},i}^{*}b_{s_{i}}^{*}||\geq||b_{s_{i}}^{*}||
. Now note that for each
0<s<t\leq k
and
m
so that
(m+1)k\leq n
, then
||b_{mk+s}^{*}||\leq\alpha_{k}||b_{mk+t}^{*}||
as stated in the proof of Theorem 2.3 of
[
Sch87
]
and call this inequality
(\ast)
.

Using the fact that
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
is size reduced,

||b_{j}||=\left|\left|b_{j}^{*}+\sum_{s=1}^{j}\mu_{j,s}b_{s}^{*}\right|\right|\leq\sum_{s=1}^{j}||b_{s}^{*}||.

To bound
||b_{s}^{*}||
for
1\leq s\leq j
, suppose
s=m_{1}k+t_{1}
and
s_{i}=m_{2}k+t_{2}
for
0\leq t_{2},t_{2}<k
. Then

\displaystyle||b_{s}^{*}||

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{k}||b_{(m_{1}+1)k}^{*}||

\displaystyle\text{by property (KZ) and }(\ast)

\displaystyle\leq 2\alpha_{k}||b_{(m_{1}+1)k+1}^{*}||

\displaystyle\text{by }(6)

\displaystyle\leq(2\alpha_{k})^{m_{2}-m_{1}}||b_{m_{2}k+1}||

by induction

\displaystyle\leq(2\alpha_{k})^{m_{2}-m_{1}}\alpha_{k}||b_{s_{i}}^{*}||

\displaystyle\text{by property (KZ) and }(\ast)\text{ since }j\leq s_{i}

\displaystyle\leq(2\alpha_{k})^{\frac{n}{k}-\lfloor\frac{s}{k}\rfloor}\alpha_{k}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle\because||b_{s_{i}}^{*}||\leq\lambda_{j}.

Since
\alpha_{k}\leq k^{1+\log k}
, we have
||b_{s}^{*}||\leq k^{(\frac{n}{k}+1)(\log k+2)-\lfloor\frac{s}{k}\rfloor\log k}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle||b_{j}||

\displaystyle\leq k^{\frac{n}{k}\log k+O(\frac{n}{k}+\log k)}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}k^{-\lfloor\frac{s}{k}\rfloor\log k}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle\leq k^{\frac{n}{k}\log k+O(\frac{n}{k}+\log k)}k\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}k^{-s\log k}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle\text{since every group of }k\text{ is equal}

\displaystyle\leq k^{\frac{n}{k}\log k+O(\frac{n}{k}+\log k)}\left(1-k^{-\log k}\right)^{-1}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle\leq k^{\frac{n}{k}\log k+O(\frac{n}{k}+\log k)}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle\text{since }(1-k^{-\log k})^{-1}\leq 3\text{ for }k\geq 2.

This completes the proof.
∎

According to Schnorr’s paper, this algorithm takes
O(n^{4}\log B+k^{\frac{k}{2}+o(k)}n^{2}\log B)
arithmetical steps on
O(n\log B)
integers where
B
is the Euclidean length of the longest input vector. In our case,
B
is a constant depending on
\mu
. Taking
k=\frac{\log(n)}{\log(\log(n))}
, this is
O(n^{4}+n^{2}\log(n)^{\frac{3\log n}{2\log\log n}})
which is contained in
O(n^{4})
and thus this choice of
k
makes the algorithm polynomial time in
n
.
In this case, the factor in Theorem
4.4
is at most
e^{\frac{2n\log(\log(n))^{3}}{\log(n)}}
for large enough
n
; in particular, it is sub-exponential.

4.2.
Geometry of numbers

There are two different sub-methods of our algorithm regarding geometry. The first finds a short basis of a given lattice.
The second finds an integral polynomial near a real polynomial where distance is measured with respect to a given basis. In these two algorithms,
P
is an integral, square-free, monic polynomial with roots
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
.

4.2.1.
Finding Short Lattice Bases

To find short lattice bases, we implement a version of Proposition
2.11
. This theorem shows the existence of a short basis of polynomials in a given convex space. In particular, let
K
be the set of real polynomials of degree at most
n
with
\log|P(x)|\leq nU_{\mu}(x)
.
There are linearly-independent, integral polynomials
P_{0},\dots,P_{n}
so that for each
0\leq k\leq n
, if
P_{k}\in\lambda K
where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is minimal, then there is no integral polynomial in
\rho K
for any
\rho<\lambda
linearly-independent from
P_{0},\dots,P_{k-1}
. We showed that the largest of these
\lambda
’s is sub-exponential in
n
in Proposition
2.14
. Thus
\{P_{0},\dots,P_{n}\}
forms a basis of lattice points in a subexponential multiple of
K
. By Theorem
4.4
, we can compute explicit integral polynomials which are within a sub-exponential factor of
K
in polynomial time in
n
. This is formalized in Corollary
4.5
.
For the rest of this section, suppose
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{C}
is compact with empty interior and connected complement.

Corollary 4.5
.

If
\mu\in\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}
is a Hölder probability measure, then we can compute a basis
Q_{0},\dots,Q_{n}
of integral polynomials of degree at most
n
in polynomial time where each
Q_{i}\in e^{Cn\frac{\log(\log(n))^{3}}{\log(n)}}K_{n}
for some fixed constant
C
depending only on
\Sigma
and
\mu
. More specifically, this runs in
O(n^{4})
time and assuming
n
is sufficiently large, we can take
C=3
.

Proof.

First compute the basis
p_{\mathcal{Z}}^{efg\pm}
of polynomials of degree less than
n
as defined in (
25
) choosing
M=\lfloor n^{1/3}\rfloor
. Call this basis
\mathcal{S}
. We want to find an integral basis which is short in the basis
\mathcal{S}
by Proposition
2.8
.
Now write
\mathcal{E}:=\{1,x,\dots,x^{n-1}\}
in the basis
\mathcal{S}
and apply Schnorr’s algorithm to get a semi
k
-reduced basis choosing
k=\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}
. We showed at the end of Section
4.1
that this runs in
O(n^{4})
time and that the output polynomials
Q_{0},\dots,Q_{n}
are so that
Q_{i}\in e^{2n\cdot\frac{\log(\log(n))^{3}}{\log n}}n^{cn^{1-\frac{\delta}{12}}}K_{n}
by Proposition
2.14
for each
i
and fixed
c
for large enough
n
.
It follows that
Q_{i}\in e^{3n\cdot\frac{\log(\log(n))^{3}}{\log n}}K_{n}
for large enough
n
.
∎

One way of computing the integral polynomials is as follows. Suppose
T
is a matrix so that
AT
is a reduced form (as in Schnorr’s algorithm) of
A
where
A
is the matrix of
\mathcal{E}
written in base
\mathcal{S}
.
Then
AT
has linearly independent columns representing such polynomials in base
\mathcal{S}
. So
T
has columns representing those polynomials in base
\mathcal{E}
as desired. This gives the desired
n+1
linearly independent integer polynomials.
Furthermore, note that that doing the change of basis via matrix inversion may not be numerically stable for large matrices. Fortunately, we can compute the change of basis matrix explicitly. As is typically done, the time bounds given in this paper are given in the number of arithmetic operations. Numerical stability is not studied in this paper.

4.2.2.
Finding Close Integer Polynomials

The following discussion allows us to algorithmically produce polynomials like those whose existence is proved in Theorem 3.2 of
[
Smi21
]
. The intention of this method is to find an integral polynomial close to a given real polynomial in polynomial time where distance is measured with respect to a given basis. In our case, we use the basis produced by Corollary
4.5
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Given a Hölder probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}
, there is a polynomial time algorithm which takes in a real polynomial
Q(z)
of degree less than
n
and produces an integer polynomial
H
of degree less than
n
such that
\frac{\log(Q-H)(x)}{n}\leq U_{\mu}(x)+Cn\cdot\frac{\log(\log(n))^{3}}{\log(n)}
. The run-time is
O(n^{4})
where
C
is dependent only on
\mu
and
\Sigma
.
Assuming
n
is sufficiently large, we can choose
C=4
.

Proof.

Let
Q_{0},\dots,Q_{n-1}
be the output of our algorithm described in Corollary
4.5
. Call this basis
\mathcal{Q}
. Suppose the
j
-th coordinate of
[Q]_{\mathcal{Q}}
is
c_{j}
.
Take
\tilde{c}_{j}
to be the nearest integer (rounding up if
c_{j}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
), and let
H
be the polynomial so that
[H]_{\mathcal{Q}}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{c}_{0}\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{c}_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}
.
Then
H
is an integer polynomial since
[H]_{\mathcal{Q}}
is an integer linear combination of the integral polynomials in the basis
\mathcal{Q}
.
We know
Q-H=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c_{j}-\tilde{c}_{j})Q_{j}
. Thus
Q-H\leq\frac{n}{2}e^{3n\cdot\frac{\log(\log(n))^{3}}{\log(n)}}e^{nU_{\mu}}
and so
Q-H\leq e^{\frac{4n\log(\log(n))^{3}}{\log(n)}}e^{nU_{\mu}}
for large enough
n
as desired.
For an analysis of the run-time, we note that Schnorr’s algorithm is the bottleneck. It runs in
O(n^{4})
time by Corollary
4.5
.
∎

4.3.
Main Algorithm

Here we prove Theorem
1.6
. We have already covered the main concepts. By Corollary
4.5
, we can get a basis of integral polynomials in a sub-exponential multiple of
K_{n}
. If we now apply Theorem
4.6
to
\frac{1}{4}(p_{\mathcal{Z}}-x^{n})+\frac{1}{2}
to get
H(x)
, we could output
P_{n}(x):=x^{n}+4H(x)-2
. This gives us an Eisenstein polynomial in a sub-exponential multiple of
K_{n}
. If we simply want a polynomial
P
with small
n
-norm, this would be sufficient; however, we have to introduce some extra steps to ensure
\text{supp}(\mu_{P_{n}})\subset D
for a given open
D\supset\Sigma
. The proof will imitate the proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Suppose that
\mu\in\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}
is a Hölder measure with exponent
\delta
, let
D
be any open set containing
\Sigma
, and choose
\rho>0
so that
\Sigma(\rho)\subset D
.
Choose
\mathcal{Z}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n-m}\}
as described in section
2.3
with

.
Apply Proposition
2.15
to
p_{\mathcal{Z}}
with
m
chosen later to get the polynomial
Q_{m}(x)
.
Note that the proof of Proposition
2.15
explicitly computes

in polynomial time.
As in the proof of Theorem
1.5
, let

be given by
x^{n}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a_{i}x^{i}
.
Apply Theorem
4.6
to

.
We see that

|p_{\mathcal{Z}}(x)Q_{m}(x)-p(x)|=|4\left(h(x)-H(x)\right)-2|\leq e^{Cn\cdot\frac{\log(\log(n))^{3}}{\log(n)}}e^{(n-m)U_{\mu}(x)}

for some constant

depending only on
\mu
and
\Sigma
by Theorem

|p_{\mathcal{Z}}(x)Q_{m}(x)|\geq\kappa^{m}n^{-cn^{1-\frac{\delta}{6}}}e^{(n-m)U_{\mu}(x)}

for every

where

.
For large enough

, we can choose

so that

where we choose

as in Theorem

.
Then for sufficiently large

|p_{\mathcal{Z}}(x)Q_{m}(x)|\geq|p_{\mathcal{Z}}(x)Q_{m}(x)-p(x)|

.
By Rouché’s theorem, since

has all its roots in

, so does

.
Furthermore by (

||p||_{n}\leq e^{Cn\cdot\frac{\log(\log(n))^{3}}{\log(n)}}+||p_{\mathcal{Z}}(x)Q_{m}(x)||_{n}\leq e^{\tilde{C}n\cdot\frac{\log(\log(n))^{3}}{\log(n)}}

for some constant
\tilde{C}
dependent only on
\Sigma
and
\mu
.
∎

5.2.
Applications to Abelian varieties

Recently, Shankar and Tsimerman
[
ST18
]
,
[
ST21
]
conjectured that for every
g\geq 4
, every Abelian variety defined over
\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
is isogenous to the Jacobian of a curve defined over
\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}.
Given a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and any arbitrary large extension
\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
, we prove that there are infinitely many abelian varieties over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
which are not isogenous to the Jacobian of any curve over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
. We use Honda Tate theory and construct an arithmetic measure with some conditions on its moments. First, we introduce our moment conditions which are motivated by the work of Tsfasman and Vladut
[
TV97
]
.

Let
X
be a finite curve defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Let

N_{r}(X):=\#X(\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}})

be the number of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}}
of
X.

By Weil’s formula, we have

(50)

0\leq N_{r}(X)=q^{r}+1-2q^{r/2}\sum_{j=1}^{g}\cos(r2\pi\theta_{j}),

where
\sqrt{q}e^{2\pi i\theta_{i}}
are Frobenius eigenvalues.
We have

N_{r}(X)=\sum_{d|r}dM_{d}(X),

where
M_{d}(X)
is the number of points with degree
d.
By Mobius inversion formula,

M_{r}(X)=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{d|r}\mu(d)N_{\frac{r}{d}}(X).

In particular, we have

\sum_{d|r}\mu(d)N_{\frac{r}{d}}(X)\geq 0

for every
r\geq 0.

Let
S_{\sqrt{\alpha}}:=\left\{\sqrt{\alpha}e^{2\pi i\theta}:\theta\in[0,1)\right\}
be the circle of radius
\sqrt{\alpha}
centered at the origin, and define the following probability measure on
S_{\sqrt{\alpha}}

d\mu_{q}:=\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{c}{k^{2}}\cos(k2\pi\theta)\right)d\theta.

where
c=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}10}.
Note that

(51)

0\leq 0.9d\theta\leq d\mu_{q}\leq 1.1d\theta.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose that
\alpha>1.65
then

U_{\mu_{\alpha}}(z)\geq 0
for any
z\in\mathbb{C}.
As a result,
d\mu_{\alpha}
is an arithmetic measure.

Proof.

It is enough to show that

U_{\mu_{\alpha}}(z)\geq 0
for any
z\in\mathbb{C}.
Suppose that

, then

U_{\mu_{\alpha}}(z)=\int_{S_{\sqrt{\alpha}}}\log|z-\sqrt{\alpha}e^{2\pi i\theta}|d\mu_{\alpha}\geq\log\sqrt{\alpha}\geq 0.

Otherwise, suppose that

By (
51

\begin{split}U_{\mu_{\alpha}}(z)&=\int_{S_{\sqrt{\alpha}}}\log|z-\sqrt{\alpha}e^{2\pi i\theta}|d\mu_{\alpha}\\
&=\int_{|z-\sqrt{\alpha}e^{2\pi i\theta}|<1}\log|z-\sqrt{\alpha}e^{2\pi i\theta}|d\mu_{\alpha}+\int_{|z-\sqrt{\alpha}e^{2\pi i\theta}|>1}\log|z-\sqrt{\alpha}e^{2\pi i\theta}|d\mu_{\alpha}\\
&\geq 1.1\int_{|z-\sqrt{\alpha}e^{2\pi i\theta}|<1}\log|z-\sqrt{\alpha}e^{2\pi i\theta}|d\theta+0.9\int_{|z-\sqrt{\alpha}e^{2\pi i\theta}|>1}\log|z-\sqrt{\alpha}e^{2\pi i\theta}|d\theta\\

&\geq 1.1\log(\sqrt{\alpha})-0.2\log(3\sqrt{\alpha})>0.\end{split}

This completes the proof of our propositions.

Corollary 5.2

be the push-forward of

by

By conformal in-variance of the potential function and Proposition

,

It follows from Theorem
1.2
that there exists a sequence of irreducible polynomials
