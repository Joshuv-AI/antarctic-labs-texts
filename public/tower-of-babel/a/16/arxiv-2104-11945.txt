C 2 C_{2} equivariant characteristic classes over the rational Burnside ring
Nick Georgakopoulos

Abstract
. We give minimal presentations for the R ​ O ​ ( C 2 ) RO(C_{2}) -graded Bredon cohomology of the equivariant classifying spaces B C 2 ​ U ​ ( n ) , B C 2 ​ S ​ O ​ ( n ) B_{C_{2}}U(n),B_{C_{2}}SO(n) and B C 2 ​ S ​ p ​ ( n ) B_{C_{2}}Sp(n) with coefficients in the rational Burnside Green functor A ℚ A_{\Q} . This results in an efficient description of rational C 2 C_{2} equivariant Chern, Pontryagin and symplectic characteristic classes. These classes are then related to each other using the inclusions of maximal tori.



1.
Introduction

Characteristic classes are classical and invaluable tools for understanding and distinguishing bundles over spaces.
If we have a compact Lie group
G
acting on a space
X
, there is a corresponding theory of
G
-equivariant bundles and
G
-equivariant characteristic classes.

May proves in
[
May87
]
that when Borel cohomology

H^{*}_{G,Borel}(X)=H^{*}(X\times_{G}EG)

is used, the theory of Borel equivariant characteristic classes reduces to the nonequivariant one, in the sense that
H^{*}_{G,Borel}(B_{G}L)=H^{*}(BG)\otimes H^{*}(BL)
for any compact Lie group
L
(which can be
L=U(n),SO(n),Sp(n)
and so on).

Equivariant characteristic classes in genuine (Bredon) equivariant cohomology are much less understood, owing to the significant complexity involved in computing it.

Recall that for a
G
-space
X
, unreduced
G
-equivariant (Bredon) cohomology
H^{\bigstar}_{G}(X)
is not just a ring, but a Green functor: for every orbit
G/H
we have a ring
H^{\bigstar}_{G}(X)(G/H)
with an action from the Weyl group
W_{G}H=N_{G}H/H
(where
N_{G}H
is the normalizer of
H
in
G
) and these rings are related to each other via restriction and transfer maps satisfying certain axioms. In more detail, for any subgroup inclusion
K\subseteq H
we have a corresponding restriction and transfer maps:

\displaystyle\Res^{H}_{K}:H^{\bigstar}_{G}(X)(G/H)\to H^{\bigstar}_{G}(X)(G/K)

(1)

\displaystyle\Tr^{H}_{K}:H^{\bigstar}_{G}(X)(G/K)\to H^{\bigstar}_{G}(X)(G/H)

(2)

Moreover, the index
\bigstar
is not just an integer, but an element of the real representation ring
RO(G)
. The coefficients used in
RO(G)
-graded cohomology are also Green functors and the initial ring
\Z
is supplanted by the initial Burnside Green functor
A_{\Z}
. So
H^{\bigstar}_{G}(X)
is by definition
H^{\bigstar}_{G}(X;A_{\Z})
and we can more generally consider
H^{\bigstar}_{G}(X;R)
for a
G
-Green functor
R
.

Computing the coefficients of
RO(G)
-graded cohomology, namely the Green functor
H^{\bigstar}_{G}(*;A_{\Z})
, is a non-trivial undertaking on its own. The reader can consult
[
Lew88
]
for the rather complicated answer when
G=C_{p}
is the cyclic group of prime order
p
. Even when we replace the coefficients
A_{\Z}
by the constant Green functors corresponding to trivial
G
-modules
\Z
and
\F_{2}
, the computations remain quite involved (see
[
Geo19
]
and
[
Geo21a
]
for the case of
G=C_{4}
).

For characteristic classes, we further need to compute the
RO(G)
-graded cohomology of equivariant classifying spaces such as
B_{G}U(n),B_{G}SO(n)
and
B_{G}Sp(n)
. Such calculations for
n\leq 3
,
G=C_{2}
and using
A_{\Z}
coefficients are performed in
[
Shu14
]
,
[
Cho18
]
. For
n=1
,
G=C_{2}
and using constant
\F_{2}
coefficients, the cohomology of
B_{C_{2}}O(1)=B_{C_{2}}\Sigma_{2}
is the test module used in the determination of the dual Steenrod algebra (
[
HK96
]
) and equivariant Dyer-Lashof operations (
[
Wil19
]
). The same calculation for
G=C_{4}
is significantly more complicated (
[
Geo21a
]
).

A way to simplify the algebra involved is to use coefficients in the rational Burnside Green functor
A_{\Q}
. Indeed, a result by Greenlees-May reduces the computation of the
RO(G)
-graded cohomology of a space
X
in
A_{\Q}
coefficients to nonequivariant rational cohomology of the fixed points
X^{H}
where
H
ranges over the subgroups of
G
(
[
GM95
]
). This allows us to compute explicit descriptions of the Green functors
H^{\bigstar}_{G}(B_{G}U(n);A_{\Q})
,
H^{\bigstar}_{G}(B_{G}SO(n);A_{\Q})
,
H^{\bigstar}_{G}(B_{G}Sp(n);A_{\Q})
and so on.

However, those explicit descriptions are rather inefficient: For
G=C_{2}
, the ring
H^{\bigstar}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))(G/G)
, according to the Greenlees-May decomposition, has
n^{2}+2n
many algebra generators over the homology of a point, which is just under double the minimal amount
\frac{n^{2}+2n}{2}+1
of generators that we can obtain (see the remarks after Proposition
4.2
). Part of the goal of this paper is to systematically obtain such minimal explicit descriptions; said another way, we are producing only the essential characteristic classes upon which all the others are built.

Our method rests on equivariant generalizations of the following nonequivariant arguments: By a classical Theorem of Borel (
[
BCM
]
), if
L
is a connected compact Lie group,
T\subseteq L
a maximal torus and
W_{L}T=N_{L}T/T
is the Weyl group then, at least rationally,

H^{*}(BL)=H^{*}(BT)^{W_{L}T}

(3)

Through this result, the characteristic classes in
H^{*}(BL)
can be computed from
H^{*}(BS^{1})
, as long as the Weyl group action is understood. For example, if we take
L=U(n)
then
T=(S^{1})^{n}
and
W_{L}T=\Sigma_{n}
acts on
H^{*}(BT;\Q)=\Q[a_{1},...,a_{n}]
by permuting the generators
a_{i}
. The fixed points under this permutation action are minimally generated by the elementary symmetric polynomials on the
a_{i}
, which are by definition the Chern classes
c_{i}
. In this way,
H^{*}(BU(n);\Q)=\Q[c_{1},...,c_{n}]
.

The same method can be performed equivariantly for
G=C_{2}
and coefficients in
A_{\Q}
. There is an extra degree of complexity owing to the fact that
H^{\bigstar}_{G}(B_{G}S^{1};A_{\Q})
is not polynomial on one generator over
H^{\bigstar}_{G}(*;A_{\Q})
, but rather on two generators, one of which is idempotent (Proposition
5.1
). As such, in the
L=U(n)
example, the elementary symmetric polynomials
c_{i}
must be replaced by a family of more complicated polynomials
\alpha,c_{i},\gamma_{s,j}
(Proposition
4.3
). Moreover, while this family of generators is minimal, it is not algebraically independent i.e. there are relations within this family. It is true however that
H^{\bigstar}_{G}(B_{G}U(n);A_{\Q})
is a finite module over
H^{\bigstar}_{G}(*;A_{\Q})[c_{1},...,c_{n}]
where the
c_{i}
are
C_{2}
-equivariant refinements of the classical Chern classes.

We use this method to obtain explicit minimal descriptions of
H^{\bigstar}_{C_{2}}(B_{C_{2}}L;A_{\Q})
where
L=U(n),SO(n),Sp(n)
. We also examine the cases of
L=O(n),SU(n)
and of the non-compact Lie groups
L=U,SO,Sp,O,SU
. The resulting equivariant Chern, Pontryagin and symplectic classes are compared using the complexification, quaternionization and forgetful maps between the aforementioned Lie groups. Finally, we compute the effect of these characteristic classes on the direct sum of bundles and on the tensor product of line bundles.

As for the organization of this paper, sections
2
and
3
set up the notation used throughout and contain the computation of the
C_{2}
rational stable stems.

Section
4
contains a summary of all our results on
C_{2}
characteristic classes. The proofs are then found in sections
5
-
11
for the interested reader.

Finally, appendix
A
contains the results on symmetric polynomials with relations that are critical for our presentation of
H^{\bigstar}_{C_{2}}(B_{C_{2}}U(n);A_{\Q})
. In particular, it contains an algorithm for writing every ”symmetric polynomial” in terms of the ”elementary symmetric polynomials”
\gamma_{s,j}
; this also leads to an algorithm for explicitly obtaining the relations between the
\gamma_{s,j}
. We have implemented these algorithms in a computer program available
here
(executable files are available
here
for a quick demonstration). The appendix is completely self contained and independent of the rest of the paper.

4.
Summary of the
C_{2}
characteristic classes

We summarize our results on
C_{2}
characteristic classes in
A_{\Q}
coefficients that we shall prove in sections
5
-
11
.

Slightly abusing the notation, we shall use
H^{*}_{G}(X)
to simultaneously denote both the
G
-Green functor and its top level
H^{*}_{G}(X)(G/G)
.
We can do that because knowledge of the top and bottom levels and of the restriction map can be used to recover the Mackey functor, as long as the restriction is surjective (the transfer is computed from
\Tr(a)=xb
where
\Res(b)=a
). In all cases we encounter, restriction is indeed surjective so it suffices to describe the top level and how generators restrict to the bottom (nonequivariant) level.

4.1.
Chern classes

We start with the results on Chern classes.

We view
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))
as an augmented algebra over
H^{*}(BU(n))
with the augmentation being restriction.

Proposition 4.1
.

The augmentation

\Res:H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))\to H^{*}(BU(n))

(25)

is a split surjection, so the nonequivariant Chern classes have
C_{2}
equivariant refinements.

We fix a section of the augmentation, i.e. equivariant refinements
c_{1},...,c_{n}
of the Chern classes, according to Proposition
4.4
.

Proposition 4.2
.

There exist elements
\alpha\in H^{0}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))
and
\gamma_{s,j}\in H^{2s}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))
for
1\leq s<n
and
1\leq j\leq n-s
, generating
H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}U(n))
as an augmented algebra over
H^{*}(BU(n))\otimes A_{\Q}
:

H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}U(n))=\frac{(H^{*}(BU(n))\otimes A_{\Q})[\alpha,\gamma_{s,j}]}{\Res(\alpha),\Res(\gamma_{s,j}),S}

(26)

where the finite set of relations
S\subseteq\Q[\alpha,c_{i},\gamma_{s,j}]
is described in Proposition
5.3
.

The
c_{i}
are algebraically independent and for each degree
*
,
H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}U(n))
is a finitely generated module over
\Q[c_{1},...,c_{n}]
.

The generating family
\{\alpha,c_{i},\gamma_{s,j}\}
has cardinality
\frac{n^{2}+2n}{2}+1
and is a minimal generating set of
H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}U(n))
as an
A_{\Q}
algebra, in the sense that any other generating set has at least
\frac{n^{2}+2n}{2}+1
many elements.

Substituting
H^{*}(BU(n))=\Q[c_{1},...,c_{n}]
in the formula for
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))
gives:

Proposition 4.3
.

As an algebra over
A_{\Q}
,

H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}U(n))=\frac{A_{\Q}[\alpha,c_{i},\gamma_{s,j}]}{x\alpha,x\gamma_{s,j},S}

(27)

Two observations:

•

The relations
x\alpha=0,x\gamma_{s,j}=0
are equivalent to
\alpha,\gamma_{s,j}
having trivial restrictions (i.e. augmentations) respectively. This completes the description of the Mackey functor structure of
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))
.

•

The
\frac{n^{2}+2n}{2}+1
many generators of the generating set
\{\alpha,c_{i},\gamma_{s,j}\}
are just over half of the
n^{2}+2n
many generators given by the idempotent decomposition (
[
GM95
]
) of the Mackey functor
H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}U(n))
.

For
n=1
the computation takes a simpler form:

H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}U(1))=A_{\Q}[\alpha,c_{1}]/(\alpha^{2}=\alpha,x\alpha)

(28)

To simplify the notation in the next Proposition, we set
u=c_{1}\in H^{2}_{G}(B_{G}U(1))
.

Proposition 4.4
.

The maximal torus inclusion
U(1)^{n}\hookrightarrow U(n)
induces an isomorphism

\displaystyle H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))=(H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(1))^{\otimes n})^{\Sigma_{n}}

(29)

Explicitly:

A_{\Q}[\alpha,c_{i},\gamma_{s,j}]/(x\alpha,x\gamma_{s,j},S)=(A_{\Q}[\alpha_{i},u_{i}]/(x\alpha_{i}))^{\Sigma_{n}}

(30)

under the identifications:

\displaystyle\alpha=\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{n})=\sum_{1\leq m\leq n}\alpha_{m}

(31)

\displaystyle c_{i}=\sigma_{i}(u_{1},...,u_{n})=\sum_{m_{*}\in K_{i}}u_{m_{1}}\cdots u_{m_{i}}

(32)

\displaystyle\gamma_{s,j}=\sum_{(m_{*},l_{*})\in K_{s,j}}u_{m_{1}}\cdots u_{m_{s}}\alpha_{l_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{l_{j}}

(33)

where
K_{i}
consists of all partitions
1\leq m_{1}<\cdots<m_{i}\leq n
and
K_{s,j}\subseteq K_{s}\times K_{j}
consists of all pairs of disjoint partitions. The polynomial
\sigma_{i}
is the
i
-th elementary symmetric polynomial.

The family of generators
\alpha,c_{i},\gamma_{s,j}
is determined upon choosing
\alpha,u=c_{1}
in
H^{0}_{G}(B_{G}U(1)),H^{2}_{G}(B_{G}U(1))
respectively, with:

H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}U(1))=A_{\Q}[\alpha,u]/(\alpha^{2}=\alpha,x\alpha)

(34)

The choice of
u
is unique under the additional requirement that its restriction is the nonequivariant Chern class
c_{1}
(in this way, the equivariant
c_{i}
are all canonically determined). There are two equally good candidates for
\alpha
however:
\alpha
and
y-\alpha
. They can only be distinguished upon fixing a model for
B_{G}U(1)
, as we do in subsection
5.1
. As such, there is no canonical choice of
\alpha\in H^{0}_{G}(B_{G}U(1))
.

Proposition 4.5
.

The map
B_{G}U(n)\to B_{G}U(n+1)
given by direct sum with a trivial complex representation induces on cohomology:

\displaystyle\alpha\mapsto y+\alpha

(35)

\displaystyle c_{i}\mapsto c_{i}

(36)

\displaystyle\gamma_{s,j}\mapsto\gamma_{s,j}+\gamma_{s,j-1}

(37)

using the convention
\gamma_{s,0}=yc_{s}
.

The map
B_{G}U(n)\to B_{G}U(n+1)
given by direct sum with a
\sigma

\displaystyle\alpha\mapsto\alpha

(38)

\displaystyle c_{i}\mapsto c_{i}

(39)

\displaystyle\gamma_{s,j}\mapsto\gamma_{s,j}

(40)

For both maps we use the conventions that
c_{n+1}=0
and
\gamma_{s,n+1-s}=0
in every RHS.

Proposition 4.6
.

The direct sum of bundles map
B_{G}U(n)\times B_{G}U(m)\to B_{G}U(n+m)
induces on cohomology:

\displaystyle\alpha\mapsto\alpha\otimes 1+1\otimes\alpha

(41)

\displaystyle c_{i}\mapsto\sum_{j+k=i}c_{j}\otimes c_{k}

(42)

\displaystyle\gamma_{s,j}\mapsto\sum_{s^{\prime}+s^{\prime\prime}=s\atop j^{\prime}+j^{\prime\prime}=j}\gamma_{s^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\otimes\gamma_{s^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime}}

(43)

using the conventions
c_{0}=1,\gamma_{s,0}=yc_{s},\gamma_{0,j}=(j!)^{-1}\alpha(\alpha-1)\cdots(\alpha-j+1)

Proposition 4.7
.

The tensor product of line bundles map
B_{G}U(1)\times B_{G}U(1)\to B_{G}U(1)
induces on cohomology:

\displaystyle\alpha\mapsto y-\alpha\otimes 1-1\otimes\alpha+2\alpha\otimes\alpha

(44)

\displaystyle c_{1}\mapsto c_{1}\otimes 1+1\otimes c_{1}

\displaystyle H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}Sp(n))=(H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(1))^{\otimes n})^{C_{2}\wr\Sigma_{n}}

Explicitly:

\displaystyle A_{\Q}[\alpha,k_{i},\kappa_{s,j}]/(x\alpha,x\kappa_{s,j},S)=(A_{\Q}[\alpha_{i},u_{i}]/(x\alpha_{i}))^{C_{2}\wr\Sigma_{n}}

(48)

under the identifications:

\displaystyle\alpha=\sum_{1\leq m\leq n}\alpha_{m}

(49)

\displaystyle k_{i}=\sum_{m_{*}\in K_{i}}u_{m_{1}}^{2}\cdots u_{m_{i}}^{2}

(50)

\displaystyle\kappa_{s,j}=\sum_{(m_{*},l_{*})\in K_{s,j}}u_{m_{1}}^{2}\cdots u_{m_{s}}^{2}\alpha_{l_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{l_{j}}

where
K_{i}
and
K_{s,j}
are as in Proposition

.

Propositions
4.5
-
4.7
have analogous statements in the symplectic case, replacing
B_{G}U(n)
by
B_{G}Sp(n)
and

Proposition 4.9
.

The forgetful map
B_{G}Sp(n)\to B_{G}U(2n)
induces on cohomology:

\displaystyle\alpha\mapsto\alpha

(52)

\displaystyle c_{2i+1},\gamma_{2s+1,j}\mapsto 0

(53)

\displaystyle c_{2i}\mapsto(-1)^{i}k_{i}

(54)

\displaystyle\gamma_{2s,j}\mapsto(-1)^{s}\kappa_{s,j}

The quaternionization map

\displaystyle\alpha\mapsto\alpha

\displaystyle k_{i}\mapsto\sum_{a+b=2i}(-1)^{a+i}c_{a}c_{b}

The effect of quaternionization on the

Under the maximal torus isomorphism:

\displaystyle\alpha=\sum_{1\leq m\leq n}\alpha_{m}

where

4.4.
Stable characteristic classes

In the
C_{2}
-equivariant case, there are different notions of stability for complex bundles, represented by the following spaces:

•

B_{G}^{+}U=\colimit(B_{G}U(1)\xrightarrow{\oplus 1}B_{G}U(2)\xrightarrow{\oplus 1}\cdots)
. This is the usual equivariant classifying space
B_{G}U=E_{G}U/U
and is a
G
-equivariant
H
-space using the direct sum of bundles maps
B_{G}U(n)\times B_{G}U(m)\to B_{G}U(n+m)
.

•

B_{G}^{-}U=\colimit(B_{G}U(1)\xrightarrow{\oplus\sigma}B_{G}U(2)\xrightarrow{\oplus\sigma}\cdots)
. This is equivalent to
B_{G}^{+}U
.

•

B_{G}^{\pm}U=\colimit(B_{G}^{+}U\xrightarrow{\oplus\sigma}B_{G}^{+}U\xrightarrow{\oplus\sigma}\cdots)=\colimit(B_{G}^{-}U\xrightarrow{\oplus 1}B_{G}^{-}U\xrightarrow{\oplus 1}\cdots)
. This becomes a
G
-equivariant
H
-space using the direct sum of bundles, and is the group completion of
B_{G}^{+}U
(and
B_{G}^{-}U
). Moreover,
B_{G}^{\pm}U\times\Z
represents equivariant
K
-theory.

Computing
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}^{-}U)
in terms of the generators
\alpha,c_{i},\gamma_{s,j}
is more complicated compared to the nonequivariant case because for fixed degree
*
, the
\Q
-dimension of
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))
does not stabilize as
n\to+\infty
and as a result,
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}^{-}U)
is infinite dimensional (dimension is
2^{\aleph_{0}}
). In degree
*=0
,
H^{0}_{G}(B_{G}^{-}U)
is linearly spanned over
A_{\Q}
by series of the form

a_{-1}+\sum_{i\geq 0}a_{i}\alpha(\alpha-1)\cdots(\alpha-i)

(73)

Generally, the graded algebra
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}^{-}U)
is generated over
H^{0}_{G}(B_{G}^{-}U)[c_{1},c_{2},...]
by series of the form

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}\gamma_{s,j}\in H^{2s}_{G}(B_{G}^{-}U)

(74)

for
a_{j}\in\Q
and
s=1,2,...
. See section
7
for more details.

For the ring
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}^{\pm}U)
we also have to compute the effect of the
\oplus 1
map on the series in
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}^{-}U)
. If we restrict our attention to finite series, we are in essence dealing with characteristic classes that are stable under addition of both the
\oplus 1
and
\oplus\sigma
representations. Since the
\oplus 1
map takes the form
\gamma_{s,j}\mapsto\gamma_{s,j}+\gamma_{s,j-1}
(and
\gamma_{s,0}=yc_{s}
,
\gamma_{0,1}=\alpha
) we can immediately see that for
i\geq 1
, the elements

c_{i}\text{ , }\gamma_{i}:=c_{i}\alpha-\gamma_{i,1}

(75)

are stable under both
\oplus 1
and
\oplus\sigma
. We conjecture that all classes with this property are polynomially generated by
c_{i},\gamma_{i}
; this is equivalent to the elements
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},...
being algebraically independent over
\Q[c_{1},c_{2},...]
.

In any case, the elements
c_{i},\gamma_{i}
span sub-Hopf-algebras of
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}^{-}U)
and
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}^{\pm}U)
with

\displaystyle\gamma_{s}\mapsto\sum_{i+j=s}(c_{i}\otimes\gamma_{j}+\gamma_{i}\otimes c_{j})

(76)

using the conventions
c_{0}=1
and
\gamma_{0}=0
.

The spaces
B_{G}^{+}U,B_{G}^{-}U,B_{G}^{\pm}U
are equivariant
H
-spaces, hence their equivariant homology is a Green functor dual to their equivariant cohomology. This homology can be expressed in terms of the classes
a_{i},b_{i},d\in H_{*}^{G}(B_{G}U(1))
dual to
\alpha c_{1}^{i},c_{1}^{i},x/2+\alpha\in H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(1))
respectively, where
i\geq 1
. Note that the
\gamma_{1}^{i}\in H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(2))
map to
\alpha c_{1}^{i}
under
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(2))\xrightarrow{\oplus 1}H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(1))
so the
a_{i}
can be thought of as duals to the
\gamma_{1}^{i}
.

Proposition 4.14
.

We have:

\displaystyle H_{*}^{G}(B_{G}^{-}U)=\frac{A_{\Q}[d,a_{i},b_{i}]}{xa_{i},xd=x}

(77)

\displaystyle H_{*}^{G}(B_{G}^{\pm}U)=\frac{A_{\Q}[d^{\pm},a_{i},b_{i}]}{xa_{i},xd=x}

(78)

and for the coalgebra structure:

\displaystyle d\mapsto d\otimes d

(79)

\displaystyle a_{i}\mapsto\sum_{j+k=i}a_{j}\otimes a_{k}

(80)

\displaystyle b_{i}\mapsto\sum_{j+k=i}(b_{j}\otimes b_{k}-b_{j}\otimes a_{k}-b_{k}\otimes a_{j}+2a_{j}\otimes a_{k})

(81)

using the conventions
a_{0}=d-x/2
and
b_{0}=1
.

The case of stable symplectic classes is entirely analogous: We can distinguish between
B_{G}^{+}Sp,B_{G}^{-}Sp
and
B_{G}^{\pm}Sp
and we have classes
k_{i},\kappa_{i}=k_{i}\alpha-\kappa_{i,1}
that are stable under both
\oplus 1,\oplus\sigma
maps. Moreover,

Proposition 4.15
.

The forgetful map
Sp\to U
induces

\displaystyle c_{2s+1},\gamma_{2s+1}\mapsto 0

(82)

\displaystyle c_{2s}\mapsto(-1)^{s}k_{s}

(83)

\displaystyle\gamma_{2s}\mapsto(-1)^{s}\kappa_{s}

(84)

while quaternionization
U\to Sp
induces

\displaystyle k_{i}\mapsto\sum_{a+b=2i}(-1)^{a+i}c_{a}c_{b}

(85)

\displaystyle\kappa_{i}\mapsto\sum_{a+b=2i}(-1)^{a+i}c_{a}\gamma_{b}

(86)

The dual homology result can be expressed in terms of the classes
a_{i}^{sp},b_{i}^{sp},d\in H_{*}^{G}(B_{G}Sp(1))
dual to
\alpha k_{1}^{i},k_{1}^{i},x/2+\alpha\in H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}Sp(1))
respectively, for
i\geq 1
(the
a_{i}^{sp}
are dual to
\kappa_{1}^{i}
). The analogue of Proposition
4.14
holds, and:

Proposition 4.16
.

The forgetful map
Sp\to U
induces

\displaystyle d\mapsto d

(87)

\displaystyle a_{i}^{sp}\mapsto\sum_{2i=j+k}(-1)^{k}a_{j}a_{k}

(88)

\displaystyle b_{i}^{sp}\mapsto\sum_{2i=j+k}(-1)^{k}(b_{j}b_{k}-a_{j}b_{k}-a_{k}b_{j}+2a_{j}a_{k})

(89)

while quaternionization
U\to Sp
induces

\displaystyle d\mapsto d

(90)

\displaystyle a_{2i+1}\mapsto 0\text{ , }a_{2i}\mapsto a_{i}^{sp}

(91)

\displaystyle b_{2i+1}\mapsto 0\text{ , }b_{2i}\mapsto b_{i}^{sp}

(92)

The case of stable Pontryagin classes is entirely analogous, replacing
Sp
by
SO
(the forgetful map
Sp\to U
is replaced by complexification
SO\to U
and the quaternionization map
U\to Sp
is replaced by the forgetful map
U\to SO
). In brief, setting
\pi_{i}=p_{i}\alpha-\pi_{i,1}
gives the analogue of
4.15
. Moreover, we have classes
a_{i}^{so},b_{i}^{so},d\in H_{*}^{G}(B_{G}SO(2))
dual to
\alpha p_{1}^{i},p_{1}^{i},x/2+\alpha\in H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}SO(2))
respectively, for
i\geq 1
, and the analogues of Propositions
4.14
and
4.16
also hold.

4.5.
Orthogonal groups

Unlike their nonequivariant counterparts, the
C_{2}
classifying spaces of the orthogonal groups

don’t generally satisfy the maximal torus isomorphism, i.e.
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}O(n))\to H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}T)^{W}
is not generally an isomorphism, where
T
is the maximal torus in
O(n)
and
W
the Weyl group. Moreover,
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}O(2n))
is not isomorphic to
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}O(2n+1))
, but rather, the inclusion-induced map

H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}O(2n+1))\to H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}O(2n))

is always a surjection with nontrivial kernel. The spaces
B_{G}O(2n+1)
can be put into our framework using the splitting
O(2n+1)=SO(2n+1)\times O(1)
:

Proposition 4.17
.

There is a generator
\beta\in H^{0}_{G}(B_{G}O(1))
such that

H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}O(2n+1))=\frac{A_{\Q}[\alpha,\beta,p_{i},\pi_{s,j}]}{x\alpha,x\beta,x\pi_{s,j},S}

(93)

The
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}O(2n))
can then be understood as quotients of
H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}O(2n+1))
(see section
10
). The stable case similarly reduces to
B_{G}SO
by use of the fact that
B_{G}O=B_{G}SO\times B_{G}O(1)
.

4.6.
Special unitary groups

For
SU(n)
we have the maximal torus isomorphism equivariantly:

Proposition 4.18
.

The maximal torus inclusion
U(1)^{n-1}\to SU(n)
induces an isomorphism

H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}SU(n))\to H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(1)^{n-1})^{\Sigma_{n}}

(94)

We prove that for any

, the inclusion induced map

H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))\to H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}SU(n))

(95)

is a surjection, and

in

since

.

In the stable case, we can distinguish between

\C P^{\infty}

g(z_{0}:z_{1}:z_{2}:z_{3}:\cdots)=(z_{0}:-z_{1}:z_{2}:-z_{3}:\cdots)

H^{*}_{C_{2}}(B_{G}S^{`})=H^{*}(BS^{1})\oplus H^{*}((B_{G}S^{1})^{C_{2}})=\Q[e_{1}]\oplus\Q[e_{2}]\oplus\Q[e_{3}]

5.2.
Maximal tori and Weyl groups

If
L
is a compact connected Lie group and
T
a maximal torus in
L
, we have the inclusion-induced map

H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}L)\to H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}T)

(107)

The Weyl group
W=W_{L}T
acts on
L,T
by conjugation hence on
H^{*}(B_{G}T),H^{*}(B_{G}L)
and the inclusion-induced map is
W
-equivariant. Actually,
W
acts trivially on
B_{G}L
, which is a special case of the fact that an inner automorphism of
H
induces the identity map on
B_{G}H
up to homotopy (see
[
BCM
]
for the classical nonequivariant case; the equivariant generalization is straightforward). Thus our map factors through the
W
-fixed points:

H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}L)\to H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}T)^{W}

(108)

This breaks into:

\displaystyle H^{*}(BL)\to H^{*}(BT)^{W}

(109)

\displaystyle H^{*}((B_{G}L)^{C_{2}})\to H^{*}((B_{G}T)^{C_{2}})^{W}

(110)

The first map is an isomorphism (
[
BCM
]
), so if we can prove that the second map is an isomorphism then

H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}L)\to H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}T)^{W}

(111)

will also be an isomorphism.

If
T=\prod^{n}S^{1}
then

(B_{G}T)^{C_{2}}=\coprod^{2^{n}}\prod^{n}BS^{1}

(112)

The coproduct is indexed over
C_{2}^{n}
i.e. sign configurations
(\pm,...,\pm)
(the next subsection explains why it’s natural to use sign configurations). By considering the number
m
of
+
’s in a configuration, we can further break this into

(B_{G}T)^{C_{2}}=\coprod_{m=0}^{n}\coprod^{\binom{n}{m}}\prod^{n}BS^{1}

(113)

In cohomology:

H^{*}((B_{G}T)^{C_{2}})=\oplus_{m=0}^{n}\oplus^{\binom{n}{m}}H^{*}(BS^{1})^{\otimes n}

(114)

The
\Sigma_{n}
action permuting the
S^{1}
factors in
T
has the effect of preserving the
m
, permuting the
\binom{n}{m}
many sign configurations and permuting the factors in the tensor product.

5.3.
The maximal torus isomorphism

We use the Grassmannian model
Gr(n,\C^{\infty\rho})
for
B_{G}U(n)
, that consists of
n
-dimensional (complex) subspaces of
\C^{\infty\rho}
where
\rho=1+\sigma
is the complex regular representation of
G
. We have:

B_{G}U(n)^{C_{2}}=\coprod_{m=0}^{n}BU(m)\times BU(n-m)

(115)

Indeed, a fixed point
V
is a
C_{2}
subspace and thus admits a unique decomposition
V=V^{+}\oplus V^{-}
where
gv=v
for any
v\in V^{+}
and
gv=-v
for any
v\in V^{-}
. So a subspace
V
in the LHS corresponds to the pair
(V^{+},V^{-})
in the RHS.

We use the maximal torus
T=\prod^{n}S^{1}
in
U(n)
. The Weyl group is
\Sigma_{n}
and the action is by permuting the
S^{1}
factors.

Proposition 5.2
.

The maximal torus inclusion induces an isomorphism:

H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}U(n))=H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}T)^{\Sigma_{n}}

(116)

Proof
.

The map
B_{G}T\to B_{G}U(n)
is on the
C_{2}
-fixed points:

\coprod^{2^{n}}BU(1)^{n}\to\coprod_{m=0}^{n}BU(m)\times BU(n-m)

(117)

sending
(v_{1}^{\pm},...,v_{n}^{\pm})
to the direct sum
v_{1}^{\pm}\oplus...\oplus v_{n}^{\pm}
(we are implicitly using the identification
Gr_{n}(\C^{\infty\rho}\oplus\C^{\infty\rho})=Gr_{n}(\C^{\infty\rho})
through a fixed linear equivariant isomorphism
\C^{\infty\rho}\oplus\C^{\infty\rho}=\C^{\infty\rho}
). Here,
v_{i}^{+}
denotes a
1
-dimensional subspace with trivial
C_{2}
action, while
v_{i}^{-}
is a
1
-dimensional subspace with antipodal
C_{2}
action. The signs in
(v_{1}^{\pm},...,v_{n}^{\pm})
correspond to the sign configuration and the index
m
on the RHS corresponds to the amount of
+
signs in a configuration. Thus, the map above breaks into

\coprod^{\binom{n}{m}}BU(1)^{n}\to BU(m)\times BU(n-m)

(118)

for every
m=0,...,n
. Fixing the
m
, the induced map on cohomology is

H^{*}(BU(m))\otimes H^{*}(BU(n-m))\to\oplus^{\binom{n}{m}}H^{*}(BU(1))^{\otimes n}

(119)

The action of
\Sigma_{n}
on the right permutes the factors in the tensor product and the sign configuration. Taking
\Sigma_{n}
fixed points is equivalent to fixing our favorite configuration, say
(+,...,+,-,...,-)
, and then taking
\Sigma_{m}\times\Sigma_{n-m}
fixed points, where
\Sigma_{m}
permutes only the
+
’s and
\Sigma_{n-m}
permutes only the
-
’s.
With that in mind, our map factors through
\Sigma_{n}
fixed points and we get

\displaystyle H^{*}(BU(m))\otimes H^{*}(BU(n-m))\to(H^{*}(BU(1))^{\otimes m})^{\Sigma_{m}}\otimes(H^{*}(BU(1))^{\otimes(n-m)})^{\Sigma_{n-m}}

(120)

This is the tensor product of maps
H^{*}(BU(i))\to(H^{*}(BU(1))^{\otimes i})^{\Sigma_{i}}
where
i=m,n-m
. These maps are induced by the maximal torus inclusions
U(1)^{i}\to U(i)
, hence are isomorphisms by the nonequivariant case.
∎

5.4.
The fixed point computation

The cohomology of the maximal torus is:

H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}T)=H^{*}_{G}(\prod_{n}B_{G}S^{1})=\frac{A_{\Q}[u_{i},\alpha_{i}]_{1\leq i\leq n}}{x\alpha_{i}=0,\alpha_{i}^{2}=\alpha_{i}}

(121)

with the
\Sigma_{n}
action permuting the
u_{i}
and
\alpha_{i}
separately (namely
\sigma u_{i}=u_{\sigma(i)}
and
\sigma\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{\sigma(i)}
for
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}
). In this subsection, we summarize the computation of the
\Sigma_{n}
-fixed points:

H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))=(H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}S^{1})^{\otimes n})^{\Sigma_{n}}=\Big(\frac{A_{\Q}[u_{i},\alpha_{i}]}{x\alpha_{i}=0,\alpha_{i}^{2}=\alpha_{i}}\Big)^{\Sigma_{n}}

(122)

that is proven in a more general form in Appendix
A
(see Propositions
A.1
and
A.2
). We have:

Corollary 5.3
.

For
1\leq i,s\leq n
and
1\leq j\leq n-s
consider the elements of the
A_{\Q}
-algebra:

H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}S^{1})^{\otimes n}=\frac{A_{\Q}[u_{i},\alpha_{i}]}{x\alpha_{i}=0,\alpha_{i}^{2}=\alpha_{i}}

(123)

given by:

\displaystyle\alpha=\sigma_{1}(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{n})=\sum_{1\leq m\leq n}\alpha_{m}

(124)

\displaystyle c_{i}=\sigma_{i}(u_{1},...,u_{n})=\sum_{m_{*}\in K_{i}}u_{m_{1}}\cdots u_{m_{i}}

(125)

\displaystyle\gamma_{s,j}=\sum_{(m_{*},l_{*})\in K_{s,j}}u_{m_{1}}\cdots u_{m_{s}}\alpha_{l_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{l_{j}}

(126)

where
K_{i}
consists of partitions
1\leq m_{1}<\cdots<m_{i}\leq n
,
K_{s,j}\subseteq K_{s}\times K_{j}
consists of pairs of disjoint partitions and
\sigma_{i}
is the
i
-th elementary symmetric polynomial.

Then:

H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))=\frac{A_{\Q}[\alpha,c_{i},\gamma_{s,j}]}{x\alpha=0,x\gamma_{s,j}=0,S}

(127)

where the finite set
S
of relations consists of three types of relations:

•

Type I:

\alpha^{n+1}=\sum_{m=1}^{n}r_{m}\alpha^{m}

where
r_{1}=(-1)^{n}n!
and

r_{m+1}=(-1)^{n+m}n!\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}\leq n}\frac{1}{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}

(128)

We can also write this relation as

\alpha(\alpha-1)\cdots(\alpha-n)=0

(129)

•

Type II:

\alpha^{s}\gamma_{s,i}=\frac{s!}{(s+i)!}c_{s}\alpha^{s+i}+\cdots

(130)

where
\cdots
denotes a homogeneous polynomial smaller than
c_{s}\alpha^{s+i}
(see Appendix
A
for a definition of this order).

•

Type III: If
s\leq t\leq s+i
,

\gamma_{s,i}\gamma_{t,j}=\binom{\min(i+j+s,n)-t}{j}c_{t}\gamma_{s,\min(i+j,n-s)}+\cdots

where
\cdots
denotes a homogeneous polynomial smaller than
c_{t}\gamma_{s,\min(i+j,n-s)}
.

The polynomials
\cdots
can be algorithmically computed in terms of
\alpha,c_{i},\gamma_{s,j}
; the algorithm is described in Appendix
A
and has been implemented in the computer program found
here
.

The elements
c_{i}
are algebraically independent and
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))
is finite over
A_{\Q}[c_{1},...,c_{n}]
hence has Krull dimension
n
.

A basis of
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))
over
A_{\Q}
consists of the elements

5.5.
Dimension count

Consider the modified partition function

with

(the usual partition function requires

Then

is the dimension of the vector space of symmetric polynomials in

of degree

, which we grade as
|x_{1}|=\cdots=|x_{n}|=1
. If

is as in subsection

with

\dim(R^{\Sigma_{n}}_{m})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{m}p(i,j)p(n-i,m-j)

We can equivalently express these facts as:

\dim H^{2m}(BU(n))=p(n,m)

(137)

and

\dim H^{2m}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))=p(n,m)+\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{m}p(i,j)p(n-i,m-j)

Note that for fixed

7.
C_{2}
stable Chern classes

Since there are two maps
B_{G}U(n)\to B_{G}U(n+1)
(given by direct sum with the trivial or the
\sigma
representation) one can try to stabilize against, there are a few distinct notions of stable characteristic classes.

7.1.
Stabilizing against one representation

First, we can stabilize with respect to direct sum with the trivial representation and get:

B_{G}^{+}U=\colimit(B_{G}U(1)\xrightarrow{\oplus 1}B_{G}U(2)\xrightarrow{\oplus 1}\cdots)

(170)

Then

K_{G}^{+}(X)=[X,B_{G}^{+}U\times\Z]^{G}

(171)

is the semi-ring of virtual bundles
V-n
on
X
modulo the equivalence relation
V\sim W\iff V+n=W+n
for some
n
. For example,
K_{G}^{+}(*)=\Z\times\mathbb{N}
. Group completing
K_{G}^{+}
gives equivariant
K
-theory
K_{G}
.

The fixed points of
B_{G}^{+}U
are:

(B_{G}^{+}U)^{C_{2}}=BU\times\coprod_{n=0}^{\infty}BU(n)

(172)

We note that
B_{G}^{+}U
is the usual equivariant classifying space
B_{G}U=E_{G}U/U
.

An equivalent way of getting
B_{G}U
is to stabilize with respect to direct sum with the
\sigma
representation:

B_{G}^{-}U=\colimit(B_{G}U(1)\xrightarrow{\oplus\sigma}B_{G}U(2)\xrightarrow{\oplus\sigma}\cdots)

(173)

Note that
B_{G}^{+}U\simeq B_{G}^{-}U
.

7.2.
Stabilizing with respect to all representations

Group completing
B_{G}^{+}U
or equivalently
B_{G}^{-}U
, gives:

B_{G}^{\pm}U=\colimit(B_{G}^{+}U\xrightarrow{\oplus\sigma}B_{G}^{+}U\xrightarrow{\oplus\sigma}\cdots)=\colimit(B_{G}^{-}U\xrightarrow{\oplus 1}B_{G}^{-}U\xrightarrow{\oplus 1}\cdots)

(174)

Then

K_{G}(X)=[X,B_{G}^{\pm}U\times\Z]^{G}

(175)

is the semi-ring of virtual bundles
V-n-m\sigma
modulo the equivalence relation
V\sim W\iff V+n+m\sigma=W+n+m\sigma
for some
n,m
. Thus
K_{G}(X)
is exactly the equivariant
K
-theory of
X
.

We finally have the fixed point computation:

(B_{G}^{\pm}U)^{C_{2}}=BU\times BU\times\Z

(176)

7.3.
Cohomology computations

We prefer to work with
B_{G}^{-}U
since the map

H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(n+1))\xrightarrow{\oplus\sigma}H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))
has a simpler expression on the generators
\alpha,\gamma_{s,j}
compared to the map
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(n+1))\xrightarrow{\oplus 1}H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))
.

The
\Q
-subalgebras spanned by
\alpha
in each
H_{G}^{*}(B_{G}U(n))
have limit:

\displaystyle\limit\frac{\Q[\alpha]}{\alpha(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)\cdots(\alpha-n)}=\prod_{n\geq 0}\Q

(177)

under the correspondence:

\displaystyle a_{0}+\sum_{i\geq 0}a_{i+1}\alpha(\alpha-1)\cdots(\alpha-i)\mapsto(a_{0},a_{0}+a_{1},a_{0}+2a_{1}+2a_{2},...)

(178)

given by evaluating the series in the LHS at
\alpha=0,1,2,...
.

Under this correspondence we have:

H^{0}_{G}(B_{G}^{-}U)=A_{\Q}\times\prod_{n\geq 1}A_{\Q}/x

(179)

As a graded
H^{0}_{G}(B_{G}^{-}U)[c_{1},c_{2},...]
-algebra,
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}^{-}U)
is generated by the series
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}r_{i}\gamma_{s,i}\in H^{2s}_{G}(B_{G}^{-}U)
for
r_{i}\in\Q
and
s=1,2,...
.

Describing
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}^{\pm}U)
in terms of the generators
\alpha,c_{i},\gamma_{s,j}
is even more complicated, as we need to take the limit of
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}^{-}U)
with respect to the
\oplus 1
maps. We can alternatively view
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}^{\pm}U)
as the limit of a diagram indexed on
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
with
(n,m)\mapsto B_{G}U(n)
, horizontal maps being
\oplus 1
and vertical maps being
\oplus\sigma
. An element of
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}^{\pm}U)
will then be a compatible doubly indexed sequence
s_{n,m}\in H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}U(n))
. The constant sequences (in both variables) consist of elements that are invariant under both the
\oplus 1
and
\oplus\sigma
maps. We can see that

c_{i},\gamma_{i}:=c_{i}\alpha-\gamma_{i,1}

(180)

have this property, and we conjecture that the subalgebra of constant sequences is generated by them. This is equivalent to the
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},...
being algebraically independent over
\Q[c_{1},c_{2},...]
and further equivalent to the subalgebra of constant sequences being

\frac{A_{\Q}[c_{i},\gamma_{i}]}{x\gamma_{i}}

(181)

The coalgebra structure is

\displaystyle\gamma_{s}\mapsto\sum_{i+j=s}(c_{i}\otimes\gamma_{j}+\gamma_{i}\otimes c_{j})

(182)

using the conventions
c_{0}=1
and
\gamma_{0}=0
.

as Mackey functors. For an

where for

using the first Chern class in the respective nonequivariant cohomology rings (

(189)

10.
Orthogonal groups

The groups
O(2n),SO(2n+1),O(2n+1)
have the same maximal torus and Weyl group. The resulting nonequivariant classifying spaces have isomorphic cohomology rings and the maximal torus isomorphism works in all cases (even though the orthogonal groups are disconnected). In this subsection, we shall see that this observation doesn’t generalize to the
C_{2}
equivariant case.

For
B_{G}O(n)
we compute the fixed points:

B_{G}O(n)^{C_{2}}=\coprod_{m+k=n}BO(m)\times BO(k)

(242)

We consider the map

H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}O(n))\to H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}T)^{W}

(243)

where
T\subseteq O(n)
is a maximal torus and
W
the Weyl group. We can see directly that for
n\leq 3
the RHS has smaller dimension compared to the LHS even before taking fixed points. So this map cannot be an isomorphism for
n\leq 3
.

We can also see from the fixed point computation that
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}O(n))
is never isomorphic to
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}O(n+1))
for any
n
.

There is however a natural description of the characteristic classes for the group
O(2n+1)
: since
O(2n+1)=SO(2n+1)\times O(1)
we have that

B_{G}O(2n+1)=B_{G}SO(2n+1)\times B_{G}O(1)

(244)

hence

H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}O(2n+1))=H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}SO(2n+1))\boxtimes_{A_{\Q}}H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}O(1))

(245)

and

H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}O(1))=A_{\Q}[\beta]/(\beta^{2}=\beta,x\beta)

(246)

for
\beta
in degree
0
. So the
C_{2}
-characteristic classes for
O(2n+1)
are
\alpha,p_{i},\pi_{s,j},\beta

The map
B_{G}O(2n)\to B_{G}O(2n+1)
is always a surjection in cohomology (this follows by looking at the fixed points) so
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}O(2n))
is a quotient of
H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}O(2n+1))
. For
n=1
we get:

\displaystyle H^{*}_{G}(B_{G}O(2))=\frac{A_{\Q}[\alpha,\beta,p_{1}]}{x\beta,\alpha^{2}=\alpha,\beta^{2}=\beta,\alpha\beta=\alpha,yp_{1}=\beta p_{1}}

(247)

More generally, in degree
0
:

H^{0}_{G}(B_{G}O(2n))=H^{0}_{G}(B_{G}O(2n+1))/\alpha(\alpha-1)\cdots(\alpha-n+1)(1-\beta)

(248)

For
n=2
the other relations in higher degrees are
p_{1}\alpha^{2}(1-\beta)=0
and
p_{2}(y-\beta-\alpha+\alpha\beta)=0
.

Finally, stability for orthogonal groups is understood from
B_{G}O=B_{G}SO\times B_{G}O(1)
.

Appendix A
Symmetric Polynomials with Relations

For a fixed commutative ring
k
consider the graded
k
-algebra
R
:

R=R(u_{1},...,u_{n},\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{n}):=k[u_{i},\alpha_{i}]/(\alpha_{i}^{2}=\alpha_{i})

(259)

whose generators have degrees
|u_{i}|=1
and
|\alpha_{i}|=0
. The group
\Sigma_{n}
acts on
R
by permuting the
u_{i}
and
\alpha_{i}
separately.

Any monic monomial in
R
takes the unique form

u_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots u_{n}^{a_{n}}\alpha_{1}^{\epsilon_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{n}^{\epsilon_{n}}

(260)

for
a_{i}\geq 0
and
\epsilon_{i}=0,1
. We order the monic monomials of the same degree lexicographically by the powers
a_{1},...,a_{n},\epsilon_{1},...,\epsilon_{n}
. For a homogeneous element

, we consider the (nonzero) monomials
p_{i}
in
p
and let
p^{\prime}_{i}
be the corresponding monic monomials; the greatest of the
p^{\prime}_{i}
is the dominant term
\dom(p)
of
p

.

We have the
k
-algebra presentation:

where:

•

\gamma_{s,i}

where
(m_{*},l_{*})
ranges over pairs of disjoint partitions

and

\gamma_{0,i}=\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)\cdots(\alpha-i+1)}{i!}

(270)

The disadvantage of supplanting
\gamma_{0,i}
with
\alpha
is that the relations between the generators now require additional rational coefficients, as in Corollary
5.3
. For the purposes of the algorithm implemented in our
computer program
, it is better (in terms of speed and numerical stability) to use all the
\gamma_{0,i}
and
\Z
-coefficients.

The rest of this appendix is dedicated to proving Propositions
A.1
and
A.2
.

Proof
.

(Of Proposition
A.1
) We will need that the dominant term of
\gamma_{s,i}
is:

\displaystyle\dom(\gamma_{s,i})=u_{1}\cdots u_{s}\alpha_{s+1}\cdots\alpha_{s+i}

(271)

Let us note a subtlety about dominant terms and multiplication:
\dom(pq)
is
\dom(p)\dom(q)
if
p
or
q
are polynomials solely on the
u_{i}
, but if both
p,q
contain
\alpha_{i}
’s that may not be the case: for example
\gamma_{1,1}
has dominant term
u_{1}\alpha_{2}
but
\gamma_{1,1}^{2}
has dominant term
u_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}
.

Every
\Sigma_{n}
orbit of a monic monomial in
R
has a greatest term
M
that can be written as either:

M=u_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots u_{s}^{a_{s}}\alpha_{1}^{\epsilon_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{s}^{\epsilon_{s}}\alpha_{s+1}\cdots\alpha_{s+i}

(272)

with
a_{1}\geq\cdots\geq a_{s}>0
and
\epsilon_{i}=0,1
, or as:

M=\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{k}

(273)

It suffices to prove that any such
M
is the dominant term of a polynomial on
\gamma_{s,j}
. Note that
M=\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{k}
is the dominant term of
\gamma_{0,k}
, so we may restrict our attention exclusively to
M
’s of the first form. It should also be noted that we can’t assume that the
\epsilon_{i}
are in decreasing order, since applying a permutation to fix such an order would affect the decreasing order on the
a_{i}
.

So let

M=u_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots u_{s}^{a_{s}}\alpha_{1}^{\epsilon_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{s}^{\epsilon_{s}}\alpha_{s+1}\cdots\alpha_{s+i}

(274)

be greatest in its
\Sigma_{n}
orbit, where
a_{1}\geq\cdots\geq a_{s}>0
and
\epsilon_{i}=0,1
. We shall prove that
M=\dom(P)
where
P
is a product of
\gamma_{s,j}
. To ensure that
P
is unique per
M
, we insist that if
\gamma_{t,j},\gamma_{t^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
are factors of
P
with
0<j,j^{\prime}
and
t\leq t^{\prime}
then
t+j<t^{\prime}
. With this extra requirement, no two distinct products
P
can have the same dominant term.

Further simplifying matters, note that it suffices to write

M=u_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots u_{s}^{k_{s}}\dom(P^{\prime})

(275)

where
k_{1}\geq\cdots\geq k_{s}\geq 0
and
P^{\prime}
is a product of
\gamma_{s,i}
with
i>0
satisfying the condition above. This is because
u_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots u_{s}^{k_{s}}
is the dominant term of a product
P_{c}
of
c_{i}
by the fundamental result on symmetric polynomials. Then we can take
P=P_{c}P^{\prime}
and
M=\dom(P)
.

We distinguish cases on the number of
\epsilon_{i}
’s in
M
that are nonzero, i.e. the number of
\alpha_{i}
’s in
M
with
i\leq s
. If there are none then

M=u_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots u_{s}^{a_{s}}\alpha_{s+1}\cdots\alpha_{s+i}

(276)

can be written as

(u_{1}^{a_{1}-1}\cdots u_{s}^{a_{s}-1})(u_{1}\cdots u_{s}\alpha_{s+1}\cdots\alpha_{s+i})

(277)

so we use
P^{\prime}=\gamma_{s,i}
.

Now assume there’s only one
\alpha_{j}
with
j\leq s
:

M=u_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots u_{s}^{a_{s}}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{s+1}\cdots\alpha_{s+i}

(278)

If
j>1
notice that
a_{j-1}>a_{j}
for otherwise we can exchange
j-1,j
and get a greater term in our order, contradicting that
M
is greatest in its
\Sigma_{n}
orbit. If further
j<s
, we can write
M
as

(u_{1}^{a_{1}-2}\cdots u_{j-1}^{a_{j-1}-2}u_{j}^{a_{j}-1}\cdots u_{s}^{a_{s}-1})(u_{1}\cdots u_{j-1}\alpha_{j})(u_{1}\cdots u_{s}\alpha_{s+1}\cdots\alpha_{s+i})

(279)

and use
P_{\alpha,\gamma}=\gamma_{j-1,1}\gamma_{s,i}
. If
j=s
we instead write
M
as

(u_{1}^{a_{1}-1}\cdots u_{s-1}^{a_{s-1}-1}u_{s}^{a_{s}})(u_{1}\cdots u_{s-1}\alpha_{s}\alpha_{s+1}\cdots\alpha_{s+i})

(280)

and use
P^{\prime}=\gamma_{s-1,i+1}
. If
j=1
then

M=u_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots u_{s}^{a_{s}}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{s+1}\cdots\alpha_{s+i}

(281)

is

(u_{1}^{a_{1}-1}\cdots u_{s}^{a_{s}-1})\alpha_{1}(u_{1}\cdots u_{s}\alpha_{s+1}\cdots\alpha_{s+i})

(282)

and we use
P^{\prime}=\gamma_{0,1}\gamma_{s+1,i}
.

Now assume there are two
\alpha_{j}
’s with
j\leq s
in
M
, say
\alpha_{j},\alpha_{k}
with
j<k\leq s
:

M=u_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots u_{s}^{a_{s}}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}\alpha_{s+1}\cdots\alpha_{s+i}

(283)

If
j>1
then as before we must have
a_{j-1}>a_{j}
and
a_{k-1}>a_{k}
. If furthermore
j<k-1
and
k<s
we can write
M
as

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{l=1}^{j-1}u_{l}^{a_{l}-3}\prod_{l=j}^{k-1}u_{l}^{a_{l}-2}\prod_{l=k}^{s}u_{l}^{a_{l}-1}\right)(u_{1}\cdots u_{j-1}\alpha_{j})(u_{1}\cdots u_{k-1}\alpha_{k})(u_{1}\cdots u_{s}\alpha_{s+1}\cdots\alpha_{s+i})

(284)

and use
P^{\prime}=\gamma_{j-1,1}\gamma_{k-1,1}\gamma_{s,i}
. If
j=k-1
and
k<s
then write
M
as

\displaystyle(u_{1}^{a_{1}-2}\cdots u_{j-1}^{a_{j-1}-2}u_{j}^{a_{j}-1}\cdots u_{s}^{a_{s}-1})(u_{1}\cdots u_{j-1}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{j+1})(u_{1}\cdots u_{s}\alpha_{s+1}\cdots\alpha_{s+i})

(285)

and use
P^{\prime}=\gamma_{j-1,2}\gamma_{s,i}
. The other cases are all handled similarly. Note that for
j=1,k=2
we get

(u_{1}^{a_{1}-1}\cdots u_{s}^{a_{s}-1})(\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2})(u_{1}\cdots u_{s}\alpha_{s+1}\cdots\alpha_{s+i})

(286)

and use
P^{\prime}=\gamma_{0,2}\gamma_{s,i}
.

We proceed in this fashion to treat the case where
r
many
\alpha_{j}
’s with
j\leq s
appear in
M
, for any
r=0,...,s
.
∎

We now prove Proposition
A.2
regarding the minimality of generators.

Proof
.

(Of Proposition
A.2
) For every
s
with
1\leq s\leq n
, consider the equivalence relation on
R^{\Sigma_{n}}_{s}
whereby two elements are equivalent if they have equal dominant terms. The orbit set of the equivalence relation, when ordered from least to greatest, starts with
\gamma_{s,0},\gamma_{s,1},...,\gamma_{s,n-s}
and continues with products of elements in
R^{\Sigma_{n}}_{t}
,
t<s
.

Let
X
be a homogeneous generating set of
R^{\Sigma_{n}}
over
R^{\Sigma_{n}}_{0}
of minimum cardinality, ordered first by degree and then by dominant term. The smallest element
x\in X
must then satisfy
\dom(x)=\dom(\gamma_{1,0})
hence
x-r\gamma_{1,0}\in R^{\Sigma_{n}}_{0}
for some
r\in k
; this means that we can replace
x
by
\gamma_{1,0}
resulting in a new generating set
X
with minimum cardinality. Applying the same argument repeatedly shows that all elements of
X
can be replaced by
\gamma_{1,0},\gamma_{1,1},...,\gamma_{n,0}
while preserving cardinality and polynomial span.∎
