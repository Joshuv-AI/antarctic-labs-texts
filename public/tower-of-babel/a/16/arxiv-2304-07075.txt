Lukas Tarra, Andreas Deutschmann-Olek, Andreas Kugi

Abstract
Actively Q-switched lasers are widely used tools which are required to produce stable output pulse energies for many applications. In this paper, a model-based control concept for actively Q-switched lasers is presented which stabilises their nonlinear pulse-to-pulse dynamics and rejects stochastic disturbances arising from amplified spontaneous emission. The feasibility of the control task is demonstrated to strongly depend on the design of the semi-active prelasing approach. In contrast to state-of-the-art hardware-based controllers, the proposed concept is flexible and cost-effective as it is not tailored to specific operation parameters.



keywords

Q-switched laser, Feedback stabilization, Control of bifurcation and chaos, Disturbance rejection, Estimation and filtering

1
Introduction

Pulsed lasers with output pulse widths in the nanosecond range are important tools for material processing tasks such as metal cutting
(
Hendow and Shakir 2010
)
, medical skin treatment
(
Altalhab et al. 2022
)
, and imaging applications such as LIDAR
(
Zhao et al. 2022
)
. An established technique for achieving such pulses is active Q-switching, where an active medium (for example an Nd:YAG crystal that is continuously pumped, e.g., by a laser diode) is placed within an optical cavity of externally controllable quality. Each laser pulse is the result of one switching cycle: at first, the cavity is kept at low quality such that the atomic excitation of the active medium increases due to optical pumping. After the cavity is switched to high quality, spontaneous emission and subsequent induced emission lead to a rapid build-up of optical energy within the cavity. At the end of the switching cycle, the cavity is opened again, and the optical energy is released as a laser pulse
(
Koechner 2006
)
.
Laser engineers are currently pursuing the goal to push the pulse repetition rate, i.e. the rate at which switching cycles are performed, to values around 1 MHz. High repetition rates increase the processing speed in a variety of applications, e.g., material removal can be very finely controlled while a reasonable processing speed can be maintained
(
Knowles et al. 2007
)
.
In this regime, users are facing two major unwanted influences on output pulse energies which compromise the system’s performance and can even damage the laser itself.
On the one hand, the atomic excitation population of the active medium couples subsequent laser pulses at high repetition rates. The resulting nonlinear pulse-to-pulse dynamics often exhibit nontrivial limit cycles due to period-doubling bifurcations and even deterministic chaos
(
Barmenkov et al. 2014
)
. On the other hand, amplified spontaneous emission, which builds the basis of the energy build-up, is a stochastic process which introduces stochastic fluctuations to an output pulse’s shape and its total energy
(
Kolpakov et al. 2014
)
.
Such energy fluctuations in particular limit sensing applications which are sensitive to the optical intensity. Hardware-based attempts have been made
(
Fries et al. 2016
)
to mitigate those dynamic and stochastic influences, but such concepts are tailored to specific operation parameters and suffer from little flexibility and high costs.

In this paper, we present a model-based nonlinear feedback approach which ensures global asymptotic stability of the closed-loop system in the deterministic case and rejects the influence of stochastic disturbances which are observed in real time. In Sec.
2
, we summarize a mathematical model of actively Q-switched lasers which was recently presented in
(
Tarra et al. 2022
)
. Possible actuators and measurable quantities are discussed in Sec.
3
, where we then present the controller and disturbance estimator design. The performance of the proposed approach is assessed in a simulation study in Sec.
4
. Finally, an outlook on further developments of the application is given and conclusions are drawn in Sec.
5
.

2.1
Effective single-mode CTNM

In
(

(

2.2
Pulse-to-pulse dynamics

The real-time behaviour of actively Q-switched lasers can be mathematically described by (
1
). As mentioned in Sec.
1
, the pulse-generating cyclic operation of the system results from the periodic switching of
R(t)
, which is why the switching frequency
f_{switch}
is equal to the pulse repetition rate. Therefore, the time increment between two subsequent cycles is given by

\displaystyle\Delta t:=1/f_{switch}\ .

(3)

In standard Q-switching,
R(t)
is switched between a low Q-value
R_{min}
and a high Q-value
R_{max}
. The corresponding time intervals are called low-Q phase (or pump phase) and high-Q phase (or duty cycle). Pulses obtained from standard Q-switching are subject to substantial stochastic fluctuations since no intracavity power can build up during the low-Q phase, and consequently, the seeding power for the high-Q phase is given entirely by the current value of the stochastic noise
\Sigma(t)
in (
1b
) and hence is fundamentally random. To combat this issue, one can employ prelasing, which is characterised by a phase of intermediate cavity quality
R_{pl}
and a duration
T_{pl}
, see the lower part of Fig.
2
, where some amplification of the intracavity power can occur
(
Koechner 2006
)
. Such a slower build-up (compare the slopes and thus the exponential growth rates of
P(t)
in Fig.
2
) has the effect that stochastic fluctuations leading to the high-Q phase can be averaged out to some degree. However, this effect is limited, especially at high repetition rates where
T_{pl}
is necessarily short. In order to still be able to compensate for stochastic fluctuations from spontaneous emission, one could continuously adapt
R_{pl}
during the prelasing phase, yielding a well-defined and constant intracavity power value at the beginning of the high-Q phase. Such active prelasing requires additional real-time-adjustable optical elements within the cavity, which is usually undesired due to additional costs and inevitable static losses.

As an alternative, we propose semi-active prelasing, where the timing of the Q-switch transition from prelasing to the high-Q phase (
R_{pl}\rightarrow R_{max}
) is adjusted based on information from continuous monitoring of the slower energy build-up. That way, stochastic fluctuations are taken into account by real-time measurement while only a shift in the timing of the high-speed circuitry needs to be controlled, which requires no additional optical elements within the cavity. Although difficult at such high repetition rates, one could finally use the pump power
P_{p}(t)
as a control input, but this option will not be discussed further in this work. Both active and semi-active prelasing rely on the fact that
P(t)
and
P_{out}(t)
can be measured as small fractions of their total values which are transmitted through optical mirrors within and outside the cavity, respectively. Depending on space within the cavity and whether the active medium is cooled,
N(t)
can also be directly determined by fluorescence measurement or through transmission of a weak probe beam
(
Stummer et al. 2022
)
.
While the laser’s behaviour during a switching cycle is a continuous-time dynamical system described by (
1
), the pulse-to-pulse dynamics couple subsequent pulses and thus occur in discrete time, with a sampling time given by (
3
) (see Fig.
2
for an illustration of this connection). As state variable for the pulse-to-pulse dynamics, we use the cycle’s initial population given by

\displaystyle N_{m}:=N(m\Delta t)\ .

The resulting pulse energy of the
m
-th cycle is defined as

\displaystyle E_{m}:=\int_{(m-1)\Delta t}^{m\Delta t}P_{out}(t)\ \textnormal{d}t\ ,

(4)

where
P_{out}(t)
is given by (
2
). The coupling between two pulses through the populations
N_{m}
and
N_{m+1}
can be understood as follows: a large
N_{m}
leads to a strong
m
-th pulse (large value of
E_{m}
in (
4
)) which depletes a substantial amount of population through the last term in (
1a
) such that
N_{m+1}
and therefore
E_{m+1}
are small etc.
As discussed above, semi-active prelasing employs the duration of the
m
-th high-Q phase as input, which shall be denoted by
T_{m}
. The intracavity power
P_{m}
at the beginning of the
m
-th high-Q phase is related to
P(t)
by

\displaystyle P_{m}:=P\left((m+1)\Delta t-T_{m}\right)=P_{m}(N_{m},T_{m})\ .

P_{m}
is a random variable since it is obtained from stochastic evolution of (
1
). Further, it depends on
N_{m}
and
T_{m}
because the value of
N_{m}
determines the eventual gain and a longer high-Q phase
T_{m}
leads to a shorter prelasing phase and thus less amplification time for
P_{m}
(see Fig.
2
). Once the value of
P_{m}
is set and the high-Q phase begins, the rest of the switching cycle can be regarded as deterministic, i.e. as an evolution of (
1
) with
\Sigma(t)
replaced by

\displaystyle\mu(t):=\mathbb{E}(\Sigma(t))\ ,

(5)

as stochastic variations due to spontaneous emission are then negligible compared to the sharply rising amplification curve of
P(t)
.

Mathematically, the pulse-to-pulse dynamics can therefore be stated as the discrete-time dynamics

\displaystyle N_{m+1}

\displaystyle=f(N_{m},P_{m}(N_{m},T_{m}),T_{m})

(6a)

\displaystyle E_{m}

\displaystyle=h(N_{m},P_{m}(N_{m},T_{m}),T_{m})\ .

(6b)

The concrete functional form of (
6
) may be either obtained from approximate analytic solution of (
1
), data-based methods or numerical forward-simulation of (
1
). In this paper, we will follow the latter approach. We interpret the random variable
P_{m}(N_{m},T_{m})
as a stochastic disturbance as it is determined by the current realisation of the noise term
\Sigma(t)
in (
1b
) and triggers the subsequent pulse amplification.

3
Controller and estimator design

3.1
Control task

As mentioned in Sec.
1
, pulse energies are affected by both dynamic pulse-to-pulse instabilities of the active medium and stochastic disturbances due to amplified spontaneous emission. In terms of the dynamical system (
6
), this means that on the one hand, the deterministic population dynamics

\displaystyle N_{m+1}=f(N_{m},P_{s}(N_{m},T_{m}),T_{m})

(7)

with

\displaystyle P_{s}(N_{m},T_{m}):=\mathbb{E}\left(P_{m}(N_{m},T_{m})\right)

(8)

exhibit an unstable steady state
N_{s}
defined by

\displaystyle N_{s}

\displaystyle=f(N_{s},P_{s}(N_{s},T_{s}),T_{s})

for a given nominal value
T_{s}
. On the other hand,
P_{m}
varies stochastically such that both (
6a
) and (
6b
) are affected. Therefore, the resulting control task can be summarized as follows: use
T_{m}
to stabilise (
6a
) around the steady-state population
N_{s}
and mitigate the effect of stochastic fluctuations
P_{m}-P_{s}
using measurements of the intracavity power
P(t)
.

3.2
Nonlinear GAS controller

To globally asymptotically stabilise (hence GAS) the pulse-to-pulse dynamics (
6a
), we will follow the approach used in
(
Deutschmann et al. 2020
)
for regenerative amplifiers. For now, we will focus on the stabilisation of (
7
) and disregard stochastic influences as their mitigation will be dealt with in Sec.
3.3
.
If we use the nonlinear feedback law
T_{m}=g(N_{m})
, a sufficient condition for global asymptotic stability of the closed-loop dynamics

\displaystyle N_{m+1}=f_{CL}(N_{m})=:f(N_{m},P_{s}(N_{m},g(N_{m})),g(N_{m}))

is that for every possible

, we have

\displaystyle\left|{\frac{\textnormal{d}f_{CL}(N_{m})}{\textnormal{d}N_{m}}}\right|<1\ .

(9)

This can be shown via Lyapunov’s direct method
(
Freeman 1965
)
, e.g., with the Lyapunov function
V(N_{m}):=|N_{m}-N_{s}|
. By taking the total derivative of

w.r.t.
N_{m}
and setting the nominal steady-state input
T_{s}
as an initial condition, one directly finds a differential equation for

which reads as

\displaystyle{\frac{\textnormal{d}g}{\textnormal{d}N_{m}}}=

\displaystyle\frac{{\frac{\textnormal{d}f_{CL,des}}{\textnormal{d}N_{m}}}-{\frac{\partial f}{\partial N_{m}}}-{\frac{\partial f}{\partial P_{m}}}{\frac{\partial P_{s}}{\partial N_{m}}}}{{\frac{\partial f}{\partial T_{m}}}+{\frac{\partial f}{\partial P_{m}}}{\frac{\partial P_{s}}{\partial T_{m}}}}

(10a)

\displaystyle g(N_{s})=

\displaystyle\ T_{s}\ ,

(10b)

with the desired closed-loop behaviour

. For a control law which is minimally invasive to the system’s natural dynamics while still ensuring (

(c.f. Fig.

\displaystyle{\frac{\textnormal{d}f_{CL,des}}{\textnormal{d}N_{m}}}=\Bigg\{\begin{array}[]{c}-1+\alpha\ ,\hskip 19.91684pt\ {\frac{\partial f}{\partial N_{m}}}+{\frac{\partial f}{\partial P_{m}}}{\frac{\partial P_{s}}{\partial N_{m}}}<-1+\alpha\\
\hskip-68.28644pt{\frac{\partial f}{\partial N_{m}}}+{\frac{\partial f}{\partial P_{m}}}{\frac{\partial P_{s}}{\partial N_{m}}}\hskip 19.91684pt\textnormal{else}\ ,\end{array}

with the stability margin

. Such a minimally invasive approach is chosen for the sake of robustness. From solving (

which can be stored as a lookup table, enabling fast online implementation.

3.3
Linear disturbance compensation

Assuming that we have an estimate
\hat{P}_{m}
of the current intracavity power
P_{m}
at the switching time
(m+1)\Delta t-T_{m}
(see Fig.
3
), we can reject its stochastic influences in a dead-beat sense. Since the deviation of
P_{m}
from its expectation value
P_{s}(N_{m},T_{m})
is assumed to be small, a linear correction is deemed sufficient. Augmenting the nonlinear feedback law
g(N_{m})
by the linear ansatz

\displaystyle T_{m}=g(N_{m})+k(N_{m})\left(\hat{P}_{m}-P_{s}(N_{m},T_{m})\right)\ ,

(13)

we can demand that the stochastically disturbed system with this additional compensation term in the input behaves like the stabilised deterministic system from Sec.
3.2
. If we assume an ideal estimate
\hat{P}_{m}=P_{m}
, this reads as

\displaystyle\begin{split}f\left(N_{m},P_{m},g(N_{m})+k(N_{m})\left(P_{m}-P_{s}(N_{m},g(N_{m}))\right)\right)\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}f\left(N_{m},P_{s}(N_{m},g(N_{m})),g(N_{m})\right)\ .\end{split}

(14)

Differentiation of (
14
) w.r.t.
P_{m}
, application of the chain rule, and evaluation at

readily yield the linear correction factor

\displaystyle k(N_{m})=-\frac{{\frac{\partial f}{\partial P_{m}}}(N_{m},P_{s}(N_{m},g(N_{m})),g(N_{m}))}{{\frac{\partial f}{\partial T_{m}}}(N_{m},P_{s}(N_{m},g(N_{m})),g(N_{m}))}\ .

(15)

3.4
Real-time disturbance estimator

To finally obtain a suitable estimate
\hat{P}_{m}
, one can utilize measurements of
P(t)
. However, one cannot simply measure up to time
(m+1)\Delta t-T_{m}
since
T_{m}
is determined by (
13
) due to
\hat{P}_{m}
. Additionally, real-time measurements of
P(t)
are typically subject to significant measurement noise.
To untangle the dependence loop and allow for some control headroom and computation time, we introduce the point in time
\bar{t}_{m}
during the prelasing phase when the decision on
T_{m}
is made, i.e. when the control law (
13
) is evaluated. This way, it is possible to increase the current
T_{m}
in online operation, which is equivalent to shifting the beginning
(m+1)\Delta t-T_{m}
of the high-Q phase to earlier times towards
\bar{t}_{m}
. Fig.
3
illustrates the relation between
\bar{t}_{m}
,
\hat{P}_{m}
, and
T_{m}
.
Up to
\bar{t}_{m}
, measurements of
P(t)
can be used to refine the estimated value
\hat{P}(\bar{t}_{m})
. Since the control law (
13
) requires
\hat{P}_{m}
, this quantity needs to be predicted based on
\hat{P}(\bar{t}_{m})
. Therefore, we utilize a real-time disturbance estimator which

•

for
t\leq\bar{t}_{m}
filters the noisy measurements

\displaystyle y(t)=P(t)+v(t)

(16)

to arrive at an optimal estimate
\hat{P}(\bar{t}_{m})
(blue regime in Fig.

) and

•

predicts the value
\hat{P}_{m}(T_{m})
using (
1
) and
\hat{P}(\bar{t}_{m})
as its initial condition (red regime in Fig.
3
).

The measurement equation (
16
) is assumed to include standard white sensor noise
v(t)
with zero mean and variance
V
. To exploit the rich theory of optimal linear estimators, we can make the following approximations which are well justified in the prelasing phase where the estimator operates:

(A1) Since the intracavity power
P(t)
is too low to deplete significant population, the last term in (
1a
) can be neglected.

(A2) With non-saturated populations (
aN(t)\ll LN_{dop}
), the exponential in (
1a
) is approximately zero.

Starting, e.g., at
N(0)=N_{m}
and using a constant pump power

, the analytic solution of (
1a
) can then be found as

\displaystyle N(t)=\frac{\lambda_{p}P_{p}}{\gamma hcA_{p}}\left(1-\alpha_{p}L\right)\left(1-\textnormal{e}^{-b\gamma t}\right)+N_{m}\textnormal{e}^{-b\gamma t}.

(17)

Plugging this result into (
1b

1b
). As is often done in estimator design, we replace
\Sigma(t)
- which is a sum of compound Poisson processes which depend on time via

- with its expectation value (
5
) plus Gaussian white noise of the same variance

\displaystyle\Sigma(N(t))\approx\mu(N(t))+w\left(0,Q(N(t))\right)\ .

With this approximation and the dynamics of (

) being linear in

\displaystyle{\frac{\textnormal{d}\hat{P}(t)}{\textnormal{d}t}}=A(N(t))\hat{P}(t)+\frac{C(t)}{V}\left(y(t)-\hat{P}(t)\right)+G\mu(N(t))

(18a)

\displaystyle{\frac{\textnormal{d}C(t)}{\textnormal{d}t}}=2A(N(t))C(t)+G^{2}Q(N(t))-\frac{C(t)^{2}}{V}

for the estimate

\displaystyle A(N(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{2q_{1}}{q_{0}t_{RT}}N(t)-\left(\frac{1}{\tau}-\frac{\ln(R_{pl})}{t_{RT}}\right)+\frac{2\alpha_{RS}L}{t_{RT}}

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{t_{RT}}\frac{\Delta\Omega}{4\pi}

since the disturbance filter is only active during the prelasing phase such that

. Even though (

and

on

as explained, e.g., in Fig.

T_{m}
. However, (
13
) can be solved offline to obtain a final two-dimensional lookup table

, which together with the real-time disturbance estimator (

) completes the control concept presented in this work.

4
Simulation results

and

.
We will first check the disturbance estimator because it was obtained by employing some approximations and builds the foundation of the superordinate discrete-time controller. In Fig.

5
Conclusions and outlook

In this work, a model-based nonlinear feedback control concept was presented which, based on a semi-active prelasing approach, stabilises the pulse-to-pulse dynamics of actively Q-switched lasers and globally mitigates stochastic influences from amplified spontaneous emission. In a simulation study, it was revealed that the choice of the prelasing level
R_{pl}
is critical for the feasibility of real-time disturbance estimation and compensation.
To deploy the presented control approach on a real-world laser device, it is essential to develop parameter estimators which identify parameters of (
1
) since in real laser systems, many parameters are not known accurately enough or exhibit temperature-induced drifts. Since
N(t)
and hence
N_{m}
are in many cases not directly measurable, a state observer must harness pulse energies or similar output quantities to estimate
N_{m}
. Finally, implementing all control and estimation algorithms for the desired repetition rates is a challenging task. Currently, suitable control hardware and implementations are being investigated.
