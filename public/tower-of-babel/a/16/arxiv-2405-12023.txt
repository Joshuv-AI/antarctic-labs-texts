Estimating transmission noise on networks from stationary local order
Christopher R. Kitching, Henri Kauhanen, Jordan Abbott, Deepthi Gopal, Ricardo Bermúdez-Otero, Tobias Galla

Abstract
In this paper we study networks of nodes characterised by binary traits that change both endogenously and through nearest-neighbour interaction. Our analytical results show that those traits can be ranked according to the noisiness of their transmission using only measures of order in the stationary state. Crucially, this ranking is independent of network topology. As an example, we explain why, in line with a long-standing hypothesis, the relative stability of the structural traits of languages can be estimated from their geospatial distribution. We conjecture that similar inferences may be possible in a more general class of Markovian systems. Consequently, in many empirical domains where longitudinal information is not easily available the propensities of traits to change could be estimated from spatial data alone.



Many complex systems can be represented as networks of nodes, each characterised by a set of traits which can change endogenously and through interaction. Examples are abundant in evolutionary biology, gene regulatory systems, and the dynamics of languages
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
. The spreading of opinions or diseases are further instances of noisy transmission processes on networks
[
5
,
6
]
.

We may naturally wish to estimate the relative propensities of different traits to change. The most direct way is to resort to longitudinal data: in population genetics, for example, cladistics and genetic sequencing have enabled the reconstruction of phylogenetic trees reaching to the beginnings of evolutionary time
[
7
]
. More indirect approaches are, however, possible
[
8
]
. Mutation rates can also be estimated using mechanistic models together with summary statistics observed in natural populations
[
7
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. In fact, these indirect methods offer the only avenue in fields where access to longitudinal information is restricted: the historical evolution of languages, for instance, can be inferred only to a very shallow temporal depth.

Against this background, Greenberg surmised fifty years ago that the geospatial distribution of the structural traits of languages carries useful information about those traits’ temporal dynamics
[
12
]
. Motivated by a recent implementation of this intuition
[
13
]
, we suggest that Greenberg’s hypothesis applies to Markovian processes more widely. One can conceive of many such processes as involving the interplay of transmission noise and a tendency to order. We propose that the relative strength of these two, the noise-to-order ratio, can be estimated from a small set of measurable quantities in the stationary state. We call this the
transmission-noise conjecture
.

In this paper we show that the conjecture does indeed hold in networked systems with binary traits that change both
spontaneously within nodes and through nearest-neighbour interaction. Moreover, detailed knowledge of the network is not needed to rank traits according to their noise-to-order ratios.

Model.
Our model describes
N
nodes
i
of an undirected network. At time
t
, each node is in one of two states, indicated by variables
s_{i}(t)=\pm 1
. In the context of horizontal gene transfer, the binary state could stand for the presence or absence of a mutation in an organism. In a model of opinion dynamics the two states could represent two distinct views on a particular question.

The system evolves through several processes. (1) The state of a node can change endogenously from
s
to
-s
with rate
v_{s}^{\prime}
, as shown in Fig.
1
(a). This reflects transmission errors in time, for example from parent to offspring. (2) A node can change state by faithfully copying the state of a neighbour. The rate coefficients for these events are
h_{\pm}
[Fig.
1
(b)]. (3) Finally, a node can change state due to transmission error in an unfaithful copying event [Fig.
1
(c)], this occurs with rate coefficients
h_{\pm}^{\prime}
. A full definition of the dynamics can be found in the Supplemental Material (SM)
[
14
]
.

Our starting point is deliberately general. The events in Fig.
1
encompass all networked models with binary states, in which nodes can flip either spontaneously, or through pairwise interaction with one nearest neighbour. This generalises the model in
[
13
]
as well as instances of the so-called ‘noisy voter model’
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
. The subsequent analysis assumes
h_{+}+h_{-}^{\prime}=h_{-}+h_{+}^{\prime}
. This is a mild requirement, indicating that nodes do not preferentially interact with nodes in a particular state
[
14
]
. We stress that, in contrast to most existing literature on the voter model, spontaneous flipping can occur asymmetrically in our model (
v_{+}^{\prime}\neq v_{-}^{\prime}
), and that the copying of a state from a neighbour can be subject to error (
h_{+}^{\prime}\neq 0
,
h_{-}^{\prime}\neq 0
).

Summary statistics for local order.
We characterise the stationary state using two summary statistics. One is the trait frequency,
\rho
. This is the proportion of nodes which possess that trait (i.e. nodes with
s_{i}=+1
). The second is the proportion,
\sigma
, of links in the network that connect two nodes in opposite states. In line with voter model terminology we will call these active links
[
18
,
19
]
.

The average stationary trait frequency is given by

\left\langle{\rho}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\frac{h_{+}^{\prime}+v_{+}^{\prime}}{h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime}+v_{+}^{\prime}+v_{-}^{\prime}}.

(1)

We can show that this expression is valid for any undirected network
[
14
]
.

We also define the ratio

\mathbb{H}\equiv\frac{\sigma}{2\rho(1-\rho)}.

(2)

If, for a given trait frequency
\rho
, traits were distributed randomly across the nodes, without correlations, one would have
\sigma=2\rho(1-\rho)
, and thus
\mathbb{H}=1
. When
\mathbb{H}<1
there are fewer active links than random. Thus,
\mathbb{H}
quantifies the amount of scatter in the system relative to a random configuration with the same value of
\rho
. We note that
\mathbb{H}
can take values larger than one for configurations in which the states of neighbouring spins are anti-correlated
[
14
]
.

The typical time course from simulations of the model is illustrated in the
(\rho,\sigma)
-plane in Fig.
2
(a). As indicated by the wiggly lines, the system approaches a parabolic curve of constant
\mathbb{H}
quickly, and then fluctuates in the region near the parabola. This has previously been pointed out for voter models in
[
18
,
16
]
. For finite systems, the point defined by the time averages
\left\langle{\rho}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
and
\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
in the stationary state may not lie on this parabola. This will be discussed further below.

Transmission noise determines local order.
Our aim is to demonstrate the validity of the transmission noise conjecture for our model.

As a first step we use the correlation function of spin states at different nodes to show that the stationary density of active interfaces
\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
can be expressed in terms of the stationary trait frequency
\left\langle{\rho}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
and the parameter combination
[
14
]

\tau\equiv\frac{(h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime})+(v_{+}^{\prime}+v_{-}^{\prime})}{\frac{1}{2}\Big[(h_{+}+h_{-})-(h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime})\Big]}.

(3)

This holds for any network. Further, for a wide range of networks we have
\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=2H(\tau)\left\langle{\rho}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\left(1-\left\langle{\rho}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\right)
, where
H(\tau)
is dependent upon the network. Importantly, the model parameters enter only through the combination
\tau
. In simulations we confirm that
\left\langle{\mathbb{H}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\approx H(\tau)
, provided
\tau
is above some cutoff set by the network size
[
14
]
.

The numerator in Eq. (
3
) describes the average rate of events in the system starting from a fully ordered state (
\rho=0
or
\rho=1
). The only state changes that are possible in such a configuration are those due to transmission noise. Therefore the numerator can be interpreted as a strength of transmission noise. The denominator in the expression for
\tau
is the rate with which correlations build up between neighbouring spins, starting from a random initial condition with
\rho=1/2

[
14
]
. Thus, we can think of
\tau
as a noise-to-order ratio, and hence a measure of instability
1
1

1

The quantity
\tau
can take negative values. This describes systems with anti-correlations between neighbouring spins. For details see Sec.
S8.2
in the SM
.

This interpretation, together with the observation that local order is set by
\tau
(i.e.,
H
is a function of
\tau
), confirms the transmission noise conjecture in our model.

Inference of
\tau
from observed data: analytical results.
We now turn to the question of inferring the noise-to-order ratio
\tau
. This is possible if
\left\langle{\mathbb{H}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
can be estimated from observations, and, if the functional form of
H(\tau)
can be calculated either exactly or as an approximation. We now discuss how to do the latter, and then turn to an empirical example.

The pair approximation (PA) is a standard tool for the analysis of interacting dynamics on networks. The method is known to work well on uncorrelated networks
[
18
,
16
,
17
,
21
]
. The main result of a PA analysis is a set of coupled differential equations for
\left\langle{\rho(t)}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle
, whose fixed point describes the stationary state. Adapting the calculation of
[
16
]
we find
[
14
]

H_{\rm PA}(\tau)=\frac{\mu-2-\mu\tau+\sqrt{(\tau+1)^{2}\mu^{2}-4(\mu-1)}}{2(\mu-1)},

(4)

where
\mu
is the mean degree of the network.

The conventional PA leading to Eq. (
4
) assumes an infinite network. It is possible to compute the leading-order correction in the inverse network size adapting the stochastic pair approximation (SPA)
[
17
]
. For our model, we find
[
14
]

H_{\text{SPA}}(\tau)=H_{\text{PA}}(\tau)\left[1-\frac{\mu_{2}}{N\mu^{2}}\frac{\tau+H_{\text{PA}}(\tau)}{\tau}\right]+{\cal O}(N^{-2}),

(5)

where
\mu_{2}
is the second moment of the degree distribution.

We have further used the annealed approximation (AA)
[
16
]
for our model. Similar to the SPA, this technique targets large uncorrelated networks. The resulting expression
H_{\rm AA}(\tau)
involves higher-order moments of the degree distribution, and is reported in the SM
[
14
]
.

In addition to these approximations, we can calculate
H(\tau)
exactly for networks which have the following properties for all fixed integers
\ell
: The number of distinct walks of length
\ell
starting at any node is the same. Additionally, the fraction
\Omega^{(\ell)}
of those walks ending at the starting point is also the same for all nodes. For such ‘homogeneous’ networks (HG) we show that
[
14
]

H_{\rm HG}(\tau)=\frac{1+\tau}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\Omega^{(\ell)}/(1+\tau)^{\ell}}-\tau.

(6)

In practice this expression approximates simulation results well for networks which have a sufficiently tight degree distribution. Examples are shown in the SM.

Closed-form expressions for the
\Omega^{(\ell)}
can be found for some homogeneous networks, including finite complete networks, and infinite Bethe and
d
-dimensional hyper-cubic lattices
[
14
]
. In particular, the expression
H_{\rm SL}(\tau)
for infinite square lattices (SL) reported in
[
13
]
for a restricted set of parameters is valid for the more general setup in Fig.
1
.

We find that
\Omega^{(\ell)}\rightarrow 1/N
for large
\ell
for a range of networks, including Barabási-Albert networks. When an exact calculation of
\Omega^{(\ell)}
is not possible, we can thus enumerate walks up to some cutoff
\ell=L
, and set
\Omega^{(\ell)}=1/N
for
\ell>L
in Eq. (
6
). As shown in the SM this produces satisfactory results.

).

Stability of language traits.
The World Atlas of Language Structures (WALS)
[
22
]
contains geolocated data on 2,662 of the approximately 7,000 existing human languages. We extract
35
binary traits, such as the presence or absence of a definiteness marker (e.g. the English definite article
the
).

For each trait, we measure
\rho
and, after constructing a putative interaction network,
\sigma
. To assess the uncertainty due to the incompleteness of WALS we use bootstrap sampling. This allows us to obtain a distribution for
\mathbb{H}=\sigma/[2\rho(1-\rho)]
for each trait. We can use the different functional forms
H_{X}(\tau)
to convert this into a distribution of
\tau_{X}
. The subscript
X
denotes the different theories SPA, AA, SL or HG.

In a second step we can, for a given
X
, rank-order the
35
traits according to their inferred noise-to-order ratios
\tau_{X}
. In
[
13
]
the ranking was based on median values of
\tau_{\rm SL}
. Here we go further and obtain the probability that the trait is ranked
r^{\rm th}
. This enables the construction of rankograms, as shown in Fig.
4
, and the use of rank metrics
[
14
]
. The width of a trait’s rankogram reflects the confidence we can have in its relative instability based on the data.

Strikingly, the results are independent of the functional form of
H(\tau)
used to infer
\tau
. This robustness explains the surprising success of the stability estimates in
[
13
]
despite the use of a square lattice geometry. Ranking on
\mathbb{H}
itself gives similar results
[
14
]
, thus confirming Greenberg’s hypothesis that low scattering indicates relative stability, whereas highly scattered traits would be comparatively unstable.

\mathbb{H}
directly, and from
\tau
obtained using the functions
H_{\rm AA}(\tau),H_{\rm SL}(\tau),H_{\rm HG}(\tau)
and

. These are virtually indistinguishable from each other on the network. For clarity we show Gaussian kernel density estimates, for
H_{\rm HG}
we show the full histogram. Further details are given in the SM along with rankograms for the remaining traits.

Discussion.
This paper has demonstrated the transmission-noise conjecture for two-state Markovian dynamics with nearest-neighbour interaction on networks. Traits can be ranked by stability from purely spatial information; neither longitudinal data nor detailed knowledge of the interaction network are required, opening up the prospect of applications in the many empirical fields where such information is not easily available.

When more is known about the interaction network, an appropriate form of
H(\tau)
can be selected, and our analytical results make it possible to infer values of
\tau
for each trait. One can then not only rank traits, but also quantify their relative stability.

Further work should show how generally it is the case that the noise-to-order ratio in the dynamics of a complex system can be inferred from configurations in the stationary state.

S1
Full model definition and notation

S1.1
Model definition

S1.1.1
Microscopic configurations of the model

Throughout, we use the mathematical notion of networks, i.e, nodes connected by links which determine which nodes can interact. We only consider undirected networks.

We consider various ‘traits’, and the nodes in the network can either process or lack a given trait. In this way, we assign node
i
a property
s_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
. We will use physics terminology and call this a
spin
. A node with
s_{i}=+1
is referred to as ‘spin-up’ or the node being in the ‘up-state’. This means that the node has the particular trait that we are considering. A node with
s_{i}=-1
, referred to as ‘spin-down’ or the node being in the ‘down-state’, means that the that the node lacks the particular trait we are considering. It is important to note that we only consider a single trait at any one time, there is no interaction between traits.

S1.1.2
Trait frequency and density of active links

We adopt the voter model terminology in that links connecting nodes in states
s=+1
and
s=-1
nodes are referred to as
active
links. Links that connect same spin nodes, i.e.
s=\pm 1
to
s=\pm 1
nodes are referred to as
inactive
links. This convention from the voter model comes from the fact that the only existing processes in the voter model are error-free copying processes between neighbours. These can only occur between opposite spin nodes, hence those links are called active
[
18
]
.

The two quantities of interest are the
trait frequency
,
\rho
, and
interface density
,
\sigma
. The trait frequency is defined as the proportion of nodes in state
s=+1
. Sometimes it is mathematically simpler to work in terms of the
magnetisation
,
m
, defined as
m=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}s_{i}
, where we are summing over all nodes in the network. The magnetisation and trait frequency are related via
m=2\rho-1
. The interface density
\sigma
is defined as the proportion of active links among all links in the network.

S1.1.3
Model dynamics

There are two ‘modes’ in which the dynamics can act:

(i)

endogenous changes of a node,

(ii)

changes due to interactions between nodes.

The first is modelled by the spontaneous flipping of a nodes’ state. This is what is referred to as ‘noise’ in conventional noisy voter models
[
15
,
16
]
. However, in our model the rates for spontaneous flips from
+1
to
-1
and those in the reverse direction may differ from each other. In
[
13
]
endogenous processes are refereed to as ‘vertical’ events. In our model any vertical event leads to the state change of one spin, that is the spontaneous acquisition or loss of a trait in that node. This can be understood as ‘unfaithful’ vertical transmission from one generation to the next, i.e., the next generation of the entity represented by the node incorrectly copies the state of the previous generation at that node. We do not include faithful vertical events, as these do not lead to any state changes.

In the second mode a spin may change state due to an interaction with a neighbouring node. This will be detailed below. In
[
13
]
such processes are referred to as ‘horizontal’ events. A copying process from a neighbour can be
faithful
or
unfaithful
. Faithful horizontal transmission means that the node correctly copies the state of the neighbour. Unfaithful horizontal transmission means that an error occurs in a copying event, so that one node adopts the state opposite to that of the neighbour it attempts to copy.

Six model parameters are needed to define the above dynamics. They are defined as follows (rates associated with unfaithful events are indicated by a dash):

\displaystyle v^{\prime}_{+}

\displaystyle=\text{rate with which a node gains the trait through}

(S1)

\displaystyle\text{unfaithful vertical transmission},

\displaystyle v^{\prime}_{-}

\displaystyle=\text{rate with which a node loses the trait through}

\displaystyle\text{unfaithful vertical transmission},

\displaystyle h_{+}

\displaystyle=\text{rate coefficient for events in which a node}

\displaystyle\text{gains the trait through faithful horizontal transmission},

\displaystyle h_{-}

\displaystyle=\text{rate coefficient for events in which a node}

\displaystyle\text{loses the trait through faithful horizontal transmission},

\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{+}

\displaystyle=\text{rate coefficient for events in which a node}

\displaystyle\text{gains the trait through unfaithful horizontal transmission},

\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{-}

\displaystyle=\text{rate coefficient for events in which a node}

\displaystyle\text{loses the trait through unfaithful horizontal transmission}.

The exact mathematical interpretation of these rates is as follows:

We focus on a node which is connected to
k
other nodes,
n(t)
of which are in the opposite state to that of the focal node at time

.

If the focal node is the state

\displaystyle T_{n,k,t}^{-}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{h_{-}\frac{n(t)}{k}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Correctly copy absence}\\

\text{existing trait}\end{subarray}},

(S2)

where
n(t)
is the number of neighbours of the focal node in the down-state.

If the focal node is in state
-1
then the rate at which nodes flip to state
+1
is

\displaystyle T_{n,k,t}^{+}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{h_{+}\frac{n(t)}{k}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Correctly copy presence}\\
\text{from a neighbour}\end{subarray}}+\underbrace{h_{+}^{\prime}\frac{k-n(t)}{k}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Incorrectly copy}\\
\text{absence as presence}\end{subarray}}+\underbrace{v_{+}^{\prime}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Spontaneously acquire}\\
\text{the trait}\end{subarray}},

(S3)

noting that

+1
with probability
p_{I}
. If the spin is in state
+1
, then it flips to
-1
with probability
p_{E}
in the vertical event.

In a horizontal event the focal spin attempts to copy the state of a random nearest neighbour. If that neighbour is in state
-1
, then a transmission error occurs with probability
p_{I}^{\prime}
, the focal spin then takes value
+1
. If the neighbour is in state
+1
, then a copying error occurs with probability
p_{E}^{\prime}
and the focal spin takes value
+1
. Otherwise the horizontal copying is faithful.

This leads to transition probabilities (once the spin to be updated has been picked) with analogous structure to Eqs. (
S2
) and (
S3
):

\displaystyle T_{n,k,t}^{-}

\displaystyle=q(1-p_{I}^{\prime})\frac{n(t)}{k}+qp_{E}^{\prime}\frac{k-n(t)}{k}+(1-q)p_{E},

(S7a)

\displaystyle T_{n,k,t}^{+}

\displaystyle=q(1-p_{E}^{\prime})\frac{n(t)}{k}+qp_{I}^{\prime}\frac{k-n(t)}{k}+(1-q)p_{I},

(S7b)

where
n(t)
is the number of neighbours in the opposite state to the focal node at time
t
.

Comparing Eqs. (
S7a
) and (
S7b
) with (
S2
) and (
S3
), we find the following mapping between the two parameter sets:

\displaystyle\begin{split}(1-q)p_{I}\leftrightarrow v^{\prime}_{+},\\
(1-q)p_{E}\leftrightarrow v^{\prime}_{-},\\
qp_{I}^{\prime}\leftrightarrow h^{\prime}_{+},\\
qp_{E}^{\prime}\leftrightarrow h^{\prime}_{-},\\
q(1-p_{I}^{\prime})\leftrightarrow h_{-},\\
q(1-p_{E}^{\prime})\leftrightarrow h_{+}.\\
\end{split}

(S8)

Is it clear that a re-scaling of all rates
v_{\pm}^{\prime},h_{\pm},h_{\pm}^{\prime}
by a common positive factor only changes the units of time, but not the actual model dynamics. Therefore, we can re-write Eq. (
S8
) as

\displaystyle\begin{split}(1-q)p_{I}=\chi v^{\prime}_{+},\\
(1-q)p_{E}=\chi v^{\prime}_{-},\\
qp_{I}^{\prime}=\chi h^{\prime}_{+},\\
qp_{E}^{\prime}=\chi h^{\prime}_{-},\\
q(1-p_{I}^{\prime})=\chi h_{-},\\
q(1-p_{E}^{\prime})=\chi h_{+},\\
\end{split}

(S9)

with
\chi>0
.

We note that the constraint
h_{+}+h_{-}^{\prime}=h_{-}+h_{+}^{\prime}
in Eq. (
S5
) is automatically fulfilled by the setup in
[
13
]
, as both sides add up to
q/\chi
using the replacements in Eq. (
S9
).

Assume now we are given non-negative rates
v_{\pm}^{\prime},h_{\pm},h_{\pm}^{\prime}
such that
h_{+}+h_{-}^{\prime}=h_{-}+h_{+}^{\prime}
. We now show that we can always find
p_{E},p_{I},p_{E}^{\prime},p_{I}^{\prime},q\in[0,1]
and
\chi>0
such that Eq. (
S9
) holds.

To do this, define

\Sigma\equiv h_{+}+h_{-}^{\prime}=h_{-}+h_{+}^{\prime}.

(S10)

Then set

\displaystyle p_{I}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{h_{+}^{\prime}}{\Sigma},

(S11a)

\displaystyle p_{E}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{h_{-}^{\prime}}{\Sigma}.

(S11b)

Because of Eq. (
S10
) these quantities both take values between zero and one.

We also choose
q
as follows:

q=\chi\Sigma.

(S12)

The factor
\chi
is not determined at this point, but has to be chosen such that
\chi<1/\Sigma
(to ensure
q<1
).

One can directly check that the combination of Eqs. (
S11a
), (
S11b
) and (
S12
) ensures that the last four relations in Eqs. (
S9
) are fulfilled.

We now further fix
p_{I}
and
p_{E}
as follows:

\displaystyle p_{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{\chi}{1-\chi\Sigma}v_{+}^{\prime},

(S13a)

\displaystyle p_{E}

\displaystyle=\frac{\chi}{1-\chi\Sigma}v_{-}^{\prime}.

(S13b)

Using
q=\chi\Sigma
[Eq. (
S10
)], this ensures that the first two relations in Eqs. (
S9
) are fulfilled. For given
\Sigma
and
v_{\pm}^{\prime}
we can always choose
\chi>0
small enough so that
p_{I}
and
p_{E}
in Eqs. (
S13a
) and (
S13b
) are less than one.

S2
Complete graph

We consider the model on a complete network (all-to-all interaction) with
N
nodes. There is then no notion of space, and any node is the nearest neighbour of any other node. Each node
i
can be in one of two states
s_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
, and at each time the overall configuration of the model is given by
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},...,s_{N})
. The system only has one degree of freedom, the number of nodes
n
in state
+1
,

n=\sum_{i}\frac{1+s_{i}}{2},

(S14)

where we sum over all nodes
i
in the network.

S2.1
Transition rates in continuous-time and master equation

The dynamics proceeds through transitions
n\to n\pm 1
, with the following rates:

\displaystyle T_{n}^{+}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{h_{+}\frac{n(N-n)}{N}}_{\text{Correctly copy presence}}+\underbrace{h_{+}^{\prime}\frac{(N-n)^{2}}{N}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Incorrectly copy}\\
\text{absence as presence}\end{subarray}}+\underbrace{v_{+}^{\prime}(N-n)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Spontaneously acquire}\\
\text{the trait}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle=\delta N+(\gamma_{+}-\delta)n-\gamma_{+}\frac{n^{2}}{N},

(S15a)

\displaystyle T_{n}^{-}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{h_{-}\frac{n(N-n)}{N}}_{\text{Correctly copy absence}}+\underbrace{h_{-}^{\prime}\frac{n^{2}}{N}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Incorrectly copy}\\
\text{presence as absence}\end{subarray}}+\underbrace{v_{-}^{\prime}n}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Spontaneously lose an}\\
\text{existing trait}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle=(h_{-}+v_{-}^{\prime})n-\gamma_{-}\frac{n^{2}}{N},

(S15b)

where we have used the expressions in Eq. (
S4
). Here
T_{n}^{+}
is the rate of the event
n\to n+1
, and
T_{n}^{-}
that for the event
n\to n-1
.

Writing
P(n,t)
for the probability of finding the system in state
n
at time
t
, one has the master equation

\frac{\differential}{\differential t}P(n,t)=T^{+}_{n-1}P(n-1,t)+T^{-}_{n+1}P(n+1,t)-\left[T^{+}_{n}+T_{n}^{-}\right]P(n,t).

(S16)

S2.2
Magnetisation

We define the magnetisation as

m=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}s_{i}.

(S17)

Using the master equation from Eq. (
S16
) we can derive a differential equation for the average magnetisation
\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle
, where
\left\langle{\cdots}\right\rangle
represents an average over
P(n,t)
, i.e. realisations of the dynamics. To do this, we first see from Eqs. (
S14
) and (
S17
),

m=2\frac{n}{N}-1\implies\frac{\differential\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=\frac{2}{N}\frac{\differential\left\langle{n(t)}\right\rangle}{\differential t}.

(S18)

Then, from the master equation for the probability distribution
P(n,t)
[Eq. (
S16
)],

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{n(t)}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\sum_{n}nP(n,t)=\sum_{n}n\frac{\differential P(n,t)}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}n\Big\{T^{+}_{n-1}P(n-1,t)+T^{-}_{n+1}P(n+1,t)-\left[T^{+}_{n}+T_{n}^{-}\right]P(n,t)\Big\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\Big\{(n+1)T^{+}_{n}P(n,t)+(n-1)T^{-}_{n}P(n,t)-\left[T^{+}_{n}+T^{-}_{n}\right]P(n,t)\Big\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\left[T^{+}_{n}-T^{-}_{n}\right]P(n,t)

\displaystyle=\left\langle{T^{+}_{n(t)}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{T^{-}_{n(t)}}\right\rangle.

(S19)

Thus,

\frac{\differential\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=\frac{2}{N}\Bigg[\left<T_{n(t)}^{+}\right>-\left<T_{n(t)}^{-}\right>\Bigg].

(S20)

Assuming the population is of infinite size, we can ignore fluctuations, and make the following
deterministic approximation
:

\left\langle{T^{\pm}_{n(t)}}\right\rangle=T^{\pm}_{\left\langle{n(t)}\right\rangle}.

(S21)

We then obtain the following rate equation for the magnetisation by substituting the explicit expressions for the rates from Eqs. (
S15a
) and (
S15b
) into Eq. (
S20
),

\frac{\differential\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=2\delta+2(\gamma_{+}-\delta-h_{-}-v_{-}^{\prime})\frac{\left\langle{n(t)}\right\rangle}{N}-2(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-})\frac{\left\langle{n(t)}\right\rangle^{2}}{N^{2}}.

(S22)

We now make the restriction from Eq. (
S5
),
\gamma_{+}=\gamma_{-}
, which closes the above equation and gives

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=2\delta-2\beta\left\langle{n(t)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2\delta-2\beta\frac{1+\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle}{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha-\beta\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle.

(S23)

Thus (assuming
\beta\neq 0
) the average steady-state magnetisation is

\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}=\frac{(h_{+}^{\prime}-h_{-}^{\prime})+(v_{+}^{\prime}-v_{-}^{\prime})}{(h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime})+(v_{+}^{\prime}+v_{-}^{\prime})}.

(S24)

We note that the case
\beta=0
corresponds to all unfaithful model parameters being 0. This is the standard voter model where the average steady-state magnetisation is simply the initial average magnetisation,
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{0}
.

Equation (
S23
) can also be solved to give the full solution for
m(t)
,

\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle=\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}-\big(\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{0}\big)e^{-\beta t}.

(S25)

S2.3
Interface density

We define interface density as the proportion of active links, i.e the proportion of links connecting two nodes in different states. For the complete graph we have

\sigma=\frac{\text{\# active links}}{\text{\# links}}=\frac{n(N-n)}{\frac{N(N-1)}{2}}=\frac{1}{2}(1-m^{2}),

(S26)

where
m
is given in Eq. (
S18
). The definition in Eq. (
S26
) holds for all values of
N
. For any time
t
we thus have, again for all
N
,

\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\left\langle{m(t)^{2}}\right\rangle\right)

(S27)

Under the deterministic approximation, Eq. (
S21
), we have
\left\langle{m(t)^{2}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle^{2}
, thus

\displaystyle\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle^{2}\right),

(S28)

with
\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle
as given in Eq. (
S25
). The average steady-state interface density for infinite complete graphs is then given by

\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\rm st}\right).

(S29)

S3
Pair approximation

Next, we analyse the model in the so-called
pair approximation
. This follows the lines of
[
18
]
. In interacting-agent models of the type we are considering here the pair approximation usually captures the behaviour of the model to good accuracy on infinite uncorrelated networks
[
23
]
.

By uncorrelated networks, we mean networks where there is no preference for nodes to attach to nodes with similar degree
[
24
]
. This is quantified by the assortativity coefficient
r
, i.e. the Pearson correlation coefficient of degree between pairs of linked nodes, being zero
[
25
]
. Erdös–Rényi networks have
r=0
exactly, since nodes are connected randomly without regard for degree. Barabási-Albert are not strictly uncorrelated, they show an assortative/disassortative behaviour as nodes of small degree tend to connect to nodes of large degree (disassortative), while nodes of large degree tend to connect to nodes of large degree (assortative) . However, for these networks it has been shown that
r\to 0
as
\frac{\log^{2}({N})}{\sqrt{N}}
, as
N
becomes large
[
26
]
, so large Barabási-Albert networks are approximately uncorrelated.

S3.1
Transition rates for individual nodes

We start from the rates in Eqs. (
S2
) and (
S3
), for a focal node of degree
k
and with
n(t)
neighbours in the opposite state to that of the focal node at time
t
.

We can write

\displaystyle T_{n,k,t}^{-}

\displaystyle=h_{-}\frac{n(t)}{k}+h_{-}^{\prime}\frac{k-n(t)}{k}+v_{-}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(h_{-}-h_{-}^{\prime})\frac{n(t)}{k}+(h_{-}^{\prime}+v_{-}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{-}\frac{n(t)}{k}+\kappa,

(S30)

and

\displaystyle T_{n,k,t}^{+}

\displaystyle=h_{+}\frac{n(t)}{k}+h_{+}^{\prime}\frac{k-n(t)}{k}+v_{+}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(h_{+}-h_{+}^{\prime})\frac{n(t)}{k}+(h_{+}^{\prime}+v_{+}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{+}\frac{n(t)}{k}+\delta.

(S31)

We have used the shorthands in Eq. (
S4
).

S3.2
Magnetisation

The change in the average magnetisation is described by the differential equation,

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\sum_{s=\pm}NP_{k}\left\langle{\rho_{s}(t)}\right\rangle\sum_{n=0}^{k}T_{n,k,t}^{-s}B(n|k,s,t)\Delta m(s).

(S32)

We use
P_{k}
to denote the degree distribution of the network, with normalisation
\sum_{k}P_{k}=1
. The quantity
\left\langle{\rho_{s}(t)}\right\rangle
is the average proportion of nodes in state
s
. Thus
NP_{k}\left\langle{\rho_{s}(t)}\right\rangle
is the expected number of nodes with degree
k
and in state
s
at time
t
. Further,
B(n|k,s,t)
is the probability that a node of degree
k
and in state
s\in\{-1,1\}
has
n
neighbours in the opposite state at time
t
. Finally,
\Delta m(s)=(-2s)/N
is the amount by which the magnetisation changes if a node in state
s
flips to
-s
.

Substituting in the expression for
\Delta m(s)
and expanding the sum over
s
, Eq. (
S32
) becomes

\frac{\differential\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=\sum_{k}P_{k}\sum_{n=0}^{k}\Bigg\{\Big[1-\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle\Big]T_{n,k,t}^{+}B(n|k,-,t)-\Big[1+\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle\Big]T_{n,k,t}^{-}B(n|k,+,t)\Bigg\}.

(S33)

The pair approximation consists of assuming that
B(n|k,s,t)
is a binomial distribution. This means we assume that the states of nearest neighbours of a focal node are uncorrelated. The single-event probability is
P(-s|s,t)
, which is the conditional probability that a neighbour of a node is in state
-s
given that the node is in state
s
at time
t
. We have,

B(n|k,s,t)=\binom{k}{n}P(-s|s,t)^{n}\Big[1-P(-s|s,t)\Big]^{k-n}.

(S34)

Writing
\mu
for the average degree of the network, the quantity
P(-s|s,t)
can be obtained as follows,

P(-s|s,t)=\frac{\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle\cdot\frac{\mu N}{2}}{\left\langle{\rho_{s}(t)}\right\rangle\cdot\mu N}=\frac{\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle}{2\left\langle{\rho_{s}(t)}\right\rangle}=\frac{\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle}{1+s\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle},

(S35)

The numerator in Eq. (
S35
) is the average total number of active links in the network (on average there are
\frac{\mu N}{2}
links overall, and a fraction
\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle
of these are active). The denominator (
\left\langle{\rho_{s}(t)}\right\rangle\cdot\mu N
) is the average total number of links emanating from all nodes in state
s
.

The first and second moments of the binomial distribution in Eq. (
S34
) are then
[
27
]

\displaystyle E[n]_{k,s,t}

\displaystyle=\frac{k\left<\sigma(t)\right>}{1+s\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle},

(S36a)

\displaystyle E[n^{2}]_{k,s,t}

\displaystyle=\frac{k\left<\sigma(t)\right>}{1+s\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle}\left(1-\frac{\left<\sigma(t)\right>}{1+s\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle}\right)+\frac{k^{2}\left<\sigma(t)\right>^{2}}{(1+s\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k\left<\sigma(t)\right>}{1+s\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle}+\frac{k(k-1)\left<\sigma(t)\right>^{2}}{(1+s\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle)^{2}}.

(S36b)

Using these moments, and the rates from Eqs. (
S30
) and (
S31
), we can simplify Eq. (
S33
) to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}P_{k}\Bigg\{\Big(1-\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle\Big)\left[\gamma_{+}\frac{E[n]_{k,-,t}}{k}+\delta\right]-\Big(1+\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle\Big)\left[\gamma_{-}\frac{E[n]_{k,+,t}}{k}+\kappa\right]\Bigg\}

\displaystyle=\alpha-\beta\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle+(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-})\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle.

(S37)

We now again make the restriction from Eq. (
S5
),
\gamma_{+}=\gamma_{-}
, after which Eq. (
S37
) is identical to Eq. (
S23
) for the complete network. Thus the solution for
\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle
within the pair approximation is as in Eq. (
S25
), and the steady-state value is given by Eq. (
S24
).

S3.3
Interface density

We consider again a node of degree
k
and with
n(t)
active links to its neighbours at time
t
, i.e.
n(t)
of its neighbours are of in the opposite state. If this focal node changes state, the change in the number of active links is

\Delta n(t)=n(t+1)-n(t)=[k-n(t)]-n(t)=k-2n(t),

(S38)

Given that the total number of links in the network is
\frac{\mu N}{2}
on average, the expected change in interface density associated with the flip of the focal node is

\Delta\sigma(n,k,t)=\frac{k-2n(t)}{\frac{\mu N}{2}}=\frac{2[k-2n(t)]}{\mu N}.

(S39)

Similar to the magnetisation, the rate of change of the interface density can be written

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\sum_{s=\pm}NP_{k}\left\langle{\rho_{s}(t)}\right\rangle\sum_{n=0}^{k}T_{n,k,t}^{-s}B(n|k,s,t)\Delta\sigma(n,k,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu}\sum_{k}P_{k}\sum_{n=0}^{k}\Bigg\{\Big[1-\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle\Big]T_{n,k,t}^{+}B(n|k,-,t)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\Big[1+\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle\Big]T_{n,k,t}^{-}B(n|k,+,t)\Bigg\}[k-2n(t)].

(S40)

As in Sec.
S3.2
we use Eqs. (
S30
), (
S31
), (
S36a
) and (
S36b
) to find

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\beta-\alpha\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle+2\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle\left[\gamma\left(1-\frac{2}{\mu}\right)-\beta\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-2\left(1-\frac{1}{\mu}\right)\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle^{2}\left[\frac{2\gamma+(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-})\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle}{1-\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle^{2}}\right].

(S41)

We now use the restriction from Eq. (
S5
),
\gamma_{+}=\gamma_{-}
, to write

\frac{\differential\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=\beta-\alpha\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle+2\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle\left[\gamma\left(1-\frac{2}{\mu}\right)-\beta\right]-4\gamma\left(1-\frac{1}{\mu}\right)\frac{\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle^{2}}{1-\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle^{2}}.

(S42)

It is possible to solve this equation numerically [this is how the analytical trajectories of Fig.
2
(a) in the main paper were obtained].

We are only interested in the steady-state solution which can be found by equating the right-hand side of Eq. (
S42
) to zero, and, based on Eq. (
2
), substituting in the following ansatz,

\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\frac{1}{2}H_{\text{PA}}(\tau)\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\rm st}\right).

(S43)

Here
H_{\text{PA}}(\tau)
is, at this stage, an unknown function of the network and the model parameters (the subscript ‘PA’ stands for ‘pair approximation’). This results in the following quadratic equation for
H_{\text{PA}}(\tau)
:

(\mu-1)H_{\text{PA}}(\tau)^{2}+\left[\mu\tau-(\mu-2)\right]H_{\text{PA}}(\tau)-\mu\tau=0,

(S44)

which can be solved to give

H_{\text{PA}}(\tau)=\frac{\mu-2-\mu\tau\pm\sqrt{(\tau+1)^{2}\mu^{2}-4(\mu-1)}}{2(\mu-1)},

(S45)

where

\tau=\frac{\beta}{\gamma}=\frac{(h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime})+(v_{+}^{\prime}+v_{-}^{\prime})}{\frac{1}{2}\left[(h_{+}-h_{+}^{\prime})+(h_{-}-h_{-}^{\prime})\right]}.

(S46)

We refer to
\tau
as the
noise-to-order
ratio, see Sec.
S8
for a more detailed discussion of the general interpretation of
\tau
and the order parameter
H(\tau)
.

S3.4
Further discussion of the function
H_{\rm PA}(\tau)
obtained from the pair approximation

S3.4.1
Regions of validity of the two branches of the solution

We note that, Eq. (
S45
) has both a positive and negative branch for a given value of
\mu
. We denote these
H_{\rm PA}^{\pm}(\tau)
. Given that
H(\tau)
must be real we require the object under the square root in Eq. (
S45
) to be non-negative. This means that we must either have
\tau\leq-1-\frac{2}{\mu}\sqrt{\mu-1}
or
\tau\geq-1+\frac{2}{\mu}\sqrt{\mu-1}
. An interpretation of
\tau
, and a discussion of the range of values this parameter can take in our model can be found in Sec.
S8.2
. This includes the possibility of negative values of
\tau
.

In the limit
\tau\to-\infty
we find
H_{\rm PA}^{+}(\tau)\to\infty
, whereas
H_{\rm PA}^{-}(\tau)\to 1
. In simulations we observe
H(\tau)\to 1
as
\tau\to-\infty
. Therefore we use
H_{\rm PA}^{-}(\tau)
in the region
\tau\leq-1-\frac{2}{\mu}\sqrt{\mu-1}
.

In the limit
\tau\to\infty
we find
H_{\rm PA}^{-}(\tau)\to-\infty
, whereas
H_{\rm PA}^{+}(\tau)\to 1
. In simulations we observe
H(\tau)\to 1
as
\tau\to\infty
. Therefore, we use
H_{\rm PA}^{+}(\tau)
in the region
\tau\geq-1+\frac{2}{\mu}\sqrt{\mu-1}
.

S1
we show both branches of
H_{\rm PA}(\tau)
for different values of
\mu
.

Comparison against simulations

We are principally interested in
\tau\geq 0
, so we do not consider the negative branch of Eq. (

) further. In Fig.

S3.5
Limit of all-to-all interaction

As a sanity check we verify that the results in the pair approximation reduce to those for the complete network in the limit

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=\beta-\alpha m(t)+2\sigma(\gamma-\beta)-4\gamma\frac{\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle^{2}}{1-\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle^{2}}.

We now verify that the result in Eq. (

S4
Annealed approximation

We now analyse the model in the so-called
annealed approximation
, which works well for large but finite-size uncorrelated networks. We broadly follow the steps of
[
16
]
, but we note a difference in notation. While this earlier reference uses
s_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for the binary states of the nodes, we use
s_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
, consistent with the rest of our paper.

S4.1
Nature of the approximation

The annealed approximation centers around replacing an adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
(representing a network with a given degree sequence) with a weighted adjacency matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
. The elements
\tilde{A}_{ij}
are the probabilities that nodes
i
and
j
are connected over an ensemble of networks generated from the configuration model using the degree sequence of the original network.

We first explain the configuration model, and how to derive the elements of the adjacency matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
, as well as the limits in which the theory is valid.

S4.1.1
Configuration model

Given a degree sequence
\{k_{1},k_{2},...,k_{N}\}
we can generate an ensemble of networks using the configuration model
[
28
]
. The procedure is as follows. In a first step,
k_{i}
‘half-edges’ are attached to each node
i
. The total number of stubs must be even, i.e.
\sum_{i}k_{i}=2m
where
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Two half-edges are then chosen at random and connected. The process repeats until no half-edges remain. The resulting network will have the specified degree sequence. This can be repeated many times to generate an ensemble of different networks all having the same degree sequence.

We now wish to determine the probability that two nodes
i
and
j
are connected over the ensemble of networks generated by the configuration model. First, we see that in total there are
\mu N
half edges in the network, where
\mu
is the first moment of the degree distribution
P_{k}
of the network. Thus, the probability that a given stub of node
i
connects to one of the stubs of node
j
is
k_{j}/(\mu N-1)
. Assuming independent events, the expected number of connections from
i
to
j
is therefore

p_{ij}=\frac{k_{i}k_{j}}{\mu N-1}\approx\frac{k_{i}k_{j}}{\mu N},

(S51)

where the last step is valid for
N\gg 1
. For large
N
, this number is small, and equal to the probability that
i
and
j
become connected when constructing the network.

S4.1.2
Double-links and self-loops

The above procedure does not exclude the possibility of self-loops or double-links. For example the final two stubs might be connected to the same node, or belong to a pair of nodes which already have an edge. However for large networks we can show that the relative number of these edges vanishes
[
29
]
.

The probability that there exists a double edge between nodes
i
and
j
can be calculated from Eq. (
S51
). When constructing the network, the probability that
i
and
j
become connected is
k_{i}k_{j}/\mu N
. After one edge is formed, the probability that another edge forms becomes
(k_{i}-1)(k_{j}-1)/\mu N
. Thus the probability that there are two edges between nodes
i
and
j
once the network is constructed is

\frac{k_{i}k_{j}(k_{i}-1)(k_{j}-1)}{(\mu N)^{2}}.

(S52)

From this we can calculate the expected number of double edges by summing over
i
and
j
, remembering to divide by
2
to avoid double counting the edges:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2(\mu N)^{2}}\sum_{i,j}k_{i}k_{j}(k_{i}-1)(k_{j}-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(\mu N)^{2}}\sum_{i}k_{i}(k_{i}-1)\sum_{j}k_{j}(k_{j}-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu}-1\right)^{2},

(S53)

where
\mu_{2}
is the second moment of
P_{k}
. The proportion of double-links among all links in the system can be calculated by dividing the number in Eq. (
S53
) by the total number of edges
\frac{\mu N}{2}
. Provided that
\mu
and
\mu_{2}
remain finite as
N
increases, this ratio vanishes as
\frac{1}{N}
.

Regarding self-loops, the number of ways of pairing up stubs from node
i
is
\binom{k_{i}}{2}=\frac{1}{2}k_{i}(k_{i}-1)
. With
\mu N
half-edges total, the probability of a self-loop is

p_{ii}\approx\frac{k_{i}(k_{i}-1)}{2\mu N}.

(S54)

We can use this to determine the expected number of self-loops:

\sum_{i}p_{ii}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu}-1\right).

(S55)

Again assuming that
\mu
and
\mu_{2}
remain constant as
N
increases, the average number of self-loops among all links scales as
\frac{1}{N}
, and is therefore negligible when
N\gg 1
.

The assumption that
\mu
and
\mu_{2}
remain constant is not generally valid. For example, networks with a power-law distribution,
P_{k}\sim k^{-\gamma}
, where
\gamma<3
have a finite first moment,
\mu
, but a divergent second moment,
\mu_{2}
, as
N\to\infty

[
28
]
. Many networks such as degree-regular, Erdös–Rényi or Barabási–Albert networks do satisfy the condition. In Fig.
S3
we show that estimates of the proportion of self-loops and double-links, calculated using Eqs. (
S53
) and (
S55
), vanish as
N
becomes large on different networks which were created using the configuration model.

Figure S3:
Plots of the proportion of self-loops and double-links calculated as the ratio of Eqs. (
S53
) and (
S55
) to the total number of links,
\mu N/2
, for different networks as the number of nodes,
N
, increases. The networks shown have degree sequences which follow that of degree-regular (left), Erdös–Rényi (middle) and Barabási–Albert (right) networks. They were created using the configuration model. All have approximately
\mu\approx 4
(deviations are due to the finite size of the graphs).

S4.1.3
Rate equations

We can now define the elements of the matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
to be the probabilities from Eq. (
S51
), i.e.

\tilde{A}_{ij}\approx\frac{k_{i}k_{j}}{N\mu}

(S56)

With this we can replace any summation over nearest neighbours of a node as follows (we write
j\in i
if
j
is a nearest neighbour of
i
):

\sum_{j\in i}s_{j}\rightarrow\frac{k_{i}}{\mu N}\sum_{j}k_{j}s_{j}.

(S57)

We will refer to this as the
annealed approximation
. Note that such an approximation conserves the initial degree sequence,

k_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tilde{A}_{ij}=k_{i}\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}k_{j}\right)=k_{i},

(S58)

and thus also the total number of links.

If node
i
is in in state
s_{i}=-1
, then it will flip to state
s_{i}=+1
with rate

\displaystyle T_{i}^{+}

\displaystyle=v_{+}^{\prime}+\frac{h_{+}}{k_{i}}\sum_{j\in i}\frac{1+s_{j}}{2}+\frac{h_{+}^{\prime}}{k_{i}}\sum_{j\in i}\frac{1-s_{j}}{2}

\displaystyle=v_{+}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}(h_{+}+h_{+}^{\prime})+\frac{(h_{+}-h_{+}^{\prime})}{2k_{i}}\sum_{j\in i}s_{j}

\displaystyle=(h_{+}^{\prime}+v_{+}^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2}(h_{+}-h_{+}^{\prime})+\frac{(h_{+}-h_{+}^{\prime})}{2k_{i}}\sum_{j\in i}s_{j}

\displaystyle=\delta+\frac{\gamma_{+}}{2}+\frac{\gamma_{+}}{2k_{i}}\sum_{j\in i}s_{j}

\displaystyle=\delta+\frac{\gamma_{+}}{2}+\frac{\gamma_{+}}{2\mu N}\sum_{m}k_{m}s_{m},

(S59)

and if the node is in state
s_{i}=+1
it will flip to
s_{i}=-1
with rate

\displaystyle T_{i}^{-}

\displaystyle=v_{-}^{\prime}+\frac{h_{-}}{k_{i}}\sum_{j\in i}\frac{1+s_{j}}{2}+\frac{h_{-}^{\prime}}{k_{i}}\sum_{j\in i}\frac{1-s_{j}}{2}

\displaystyle=v_{-}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}(h_{-}+h_{-}^{\prime})+\frac{(h_{-}-h_{-}^{\prime})}{2k_{i}}\sum_{j\in i}s_{j}

\displaystyle=(h_{-}^{\prime}+v_{-}^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2}(h_{-}-h_{-}^{\prime})+\frac{(h_{-}-h_{-}^{\prime})}{2k_{i}}\sum_{j\in i}s_{j}

\displaystyle=\kappa+\frac{\gamma_{-}}{2}-\frac{\gamma_{-}}{2k_{i}}\sum_{j\in i}s_{j}

\displaystyle=\kappa+\frac{\gamma_{-}}{2}-\frac{\gamma_{-}}{2\mu N}\sum_{m}k_{m}s_{m},

(S60)

where we have used the annealed approximation in Eq. (
S57
). We also introduced expressions from Eq. (
S4
).

With these definitions we then have the overall flip rate for spin
i
which depends on the spin configuration
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{N})
,

W_{i}(\mathbf{s})\equiv\frac{1+s_{i}}{2}T_{i}^{-}+\frac{1-s_{i}}{2}T_{i}^{+}.

(S61)

S4.2
Master equation

To formulate the master equation for the model, it is useful to define the flip operators
F_{i}
acting on functions of
\mathbf{s}
by flipping the
i
th
spin,

F_{i}f(s_{1},\dots,s_{i},\dots s_{N})\equiv f(s_{1},\dots,-s_{i},\dots,s_{N}).

(S62)

The master equation for
P(\mathbf{s})
, i.e. the probability that
N
nodes have a particular spin configuration, is then given by

\frac{\differential P(\mathbf{s})}{\differential t}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigg\{F_{i}\Big[W_{i}(\mathbf{s})P(\mathbf{s})\Big]-W_{i}(\mathbf{s})P(\mathbf{s})\Bigg\}.

(S63)

S4.3
Magnetisation

Following
[
16
]
we first introduce the following summation notation:
\sum_{\mathbf{s}}
refers to a sum over all possible spin combinations, i.e

\sum_{\mathbf{s}}=\sum_{s_{1}}\sum_{s_{2}}...\sum_{s_{N}},

(S64)

and
\sum_{\mathbf{s}_{j}}
refers to a sum over all possible spin combinations excluding node
j
,

\sum_{\mathbf{s}_{j}}\equiv\sum_{s_{1}}...\sum_{s_{j-1}}\sum_{s_{j+1}}...\sum_{s_{N}}.

(S65)

These two summation definitions are related by

\sum_{\mathbf{s}}=\sum_{\mathbf{s}_{j}}\sum_{s_{j}}.

(S66)

This allows us to split summations over all system configurations into a summation over a specific nodes spin and a summation over the spin configuration of the remaining system. We denote the average over the spin configuration
\mathbf{s}
as

\left\langle{f(\mathbf{s})}\right\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{s}}f(\mathbf{s})P(\mathbf{s}).

(S67)

Using the master equation, Eq. (
S63
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{s}}s_{i}\frac{\differential P(\mathbf{s})}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{s}}s_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Big\{F_{j}[W_{j}P]-W_{j}P\Big\},

(S68)

where we omit the argument
\mathbf{s}
of the
W_{j}
and
P
on the right for simplicity. We now split the sum over
j
into summations of
j=i
and
j\neq i
,

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{s}}s_{i}\Bigg\{\Big(F_{i}[W_{i}P]-W_{i}P\Big)+\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\Big(F_{j}[W_{j}P]-W_{j}P\Big)\Bigg\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{s}_{i}}\sum_{s_{i}}s_{i}\Big(F_{i}[W_{i}P]-W_{i}P\Big)+\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{\mathbf{s}_{j}}s_{i}\sum_{s_{j}}\Big(F_{j}[W_{j}P]-W_{j}P\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{s}}\big(-2s_{i}W_{i}P\big),

(S69)

where in the second line, the second term vanishes after performing the
s_{j}
summation, and the first term simplifies after the
s_{i}
sum. Evaluating the sum with Eq. (
S67
) we have

\frac{\differential\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=-2\left\langle{W_{i}s_{i}}\right\rangle.

(S70)

This is also clear intuitively. The quantity
W_{i}
on the right is the probability with which spin
i
flips from
s_{i}
to
-s_{i}
. The change of
s_{i}
in such an event is
-2s_{i}
.

Using the definition of
W_{i}
in Eq. (
S61
), we then have

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\left\langle{T_{i}^{+}-T_{i}^{-}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\left(T_{i}^{+}+T_{i}^{-}\right)s_{i}}\right\rangle.

(S71)

We now use the explicit expressions for the rates defined in Eqs. (
S59
) and (
S60
) to write

\frac{\differential\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=\alpha+\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-})-(\beta+\gamma)\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle+\frac{\gamma}{\mu N}\sum_{m}k_{m}\left\langle{s_{m}}\right\rangle-\frac{(\gamma_{+}+\gamma_{-})}{2\mu N}\sum_{m}k_{m}\left\langle{s_{i}s_{m}}\right\rangle.

(S72)

Then we make the restriction from Eq. (
S5
),
\gamma_{+}=\gamma_{-}
, and find

\frac{\differential\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=\alpha-(\beta+\gamma)\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle+\frac{\gamma}{\mu N}\sum_{m}k_{m}\left\langle{s_{m}}\right\rangle.

(S73)

In the steady-state, this gives the following set of
N
simultaneous equations:

\alpha-(\beta+\gamma)\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle+\frac{\gamma}{\mu N}\sum_{m}k_{m}\left\langle{s_{m}}\right\rangle=0.

(S74)

One can directly verify that

\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\frac{-\alpha}{-(\gamma+\beta)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}}=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}

(S75)

for all
i
is a solution. This then also means

\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}.

(S76)

We now show that this is also the only solution of Eq. (
S74
). If we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\gamma k_{i}}{\mu N},

(S77a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv A_{i}-(\gamma+\beta),

(S77b)

then we can cast Eq. (
S74
) as a matrix equation,

\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{B}_{1}&\mathcal{A}_{2}&\cdots&\mathcal{A}_{N}\\
\mathcal{A}_{1}&\mathcal{B}_{2}&\cdots&\mathcal{A}_{N}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathcal{A}_{1}&\mathcal{A}_{2}&\cdots&\mathcal{B}_{N}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\left\langle{s_{1}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\\
\left\langle{s_{2}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\\
\vdots\\
\left\langle{s_{N}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-\alpha\\
-\alpha\\
\vdots\\
-\alpha\end{bmatrix}.

(S78)

To prove the uniqueness of the above solution, we need to show that the matrix on the left has full rank, i.e. that its columns are linearly independent. We show this by contradiction.

Assume there are multiple solutions, i.e., there are coefficients
c_{1},\dots,c_{N}
(which are not all equal to zero) such that

\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{B}_{1}&\mathcal{A}_{2}&\cdots&\mathcal{A}_{N}\\
\mathcal{A}_{1}&\mathcal{B}_{2}&\cdots&\mathcal{A}_{N}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathcal{A}_{1}&\mathcal{A}_{2}&\cdots&\mathcal{B}_{N}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\\
\vdots\\
c_{N}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

(S79)

Looking at the
i
th
and
j
th
row together, one concludes

c_{i}(\mathcal{B}_{i}-\mathcal{A}_{i})=c_{j}(\mathcal{B}_{j}-\mathcal{A}_{j})

(S80)

for all pairs
i,j
. Given that
\mathcal{B}_{i}-\mathcal{A}_{i}=-(\gamma+\beta)
for all
i
, this then implies that all
c_{i}
must take the same value (we discard the special case
\gamma+\beta=0
). This common value can be non-zero only if the sum of elements in each row of the matrix in Eq. (
S79
) sum to zero, i.e. if

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\mathcal{A}_{j}=0

\displaystyle\implies-(\gamma+\beta)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{A}_{i}=0

\displaystyle\implies-(\gamma+\beta)+\frac{\gamma}{\mu N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}=0

\displaystyle\implies\beta=0,

(S81)

where we have used the definitions in Eqs. (
S77a
) and (
S77b
). Therefore we are left with the condition
\beta=0
. Keeping in mind the definition of
\beta
in Eq. (
S4
) this corresponds to a ‘pathological’ scenario, in which there is no spontaneous spin flipping (no vertical dynamics), and in which horizontal copying is without error. This is the standard voter model, which, for finite systems, will always end up in a consensus state. We note that
\alpha=0
in this case as well.

In summary, for all cases of interest (
\beta\neq 0
), the vector
(c_{1},\dots,c_{N})
is non-zero. Thus Eq. (
S76
) is the only non-trivial solution to Eq. (
S74
).

S4.4
Interface density

The average interface density can be expressed in the following form

\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}A_{ij}\left[\frac{1+\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle}{2}\frac{1-\left\langle{s_{j}}\right\rangle}{2}+\frac{1-\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle}{2}\frac{1+\left\langle{s_{j}}\right\rangle}{2}\right]}{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}A_{ij}},

(S82)

where the denominator is the total number of links and the numerator is the number of links connecting opposite spins. Using the annealed approximation, Eq. (
S56
), this can be written as

\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2(\mu N)^{2}}\sum_{ij}k_{i}k_{j}\left[1-\left\langle{s_{i}s_{j}}\right\rangle\right].

(S83)

In the steady-state we therefore have

\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\text{st}}=\frac{1}{2(\mu N)^{2}}\sum_{ij}k_{i}k_{j}\left[1-\left\langle{s_{i}s_{j}}\right\rangle_{\text{st}}\right].\\

(S84)

We now define the correlation matrix
\Upsilon_{ij}
,

\Upsilon_{ij}=\left\langle{s_{i}s_{j}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle\left\langle{s_{j}}\right\rangle,

(S85)

which in the steady-state, since
\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle_{\text{st}}=\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\text{st}}
for all
i
[see Eq. (
S76
)], can be written as

(\Upsilon_{ij})_{\rm st}=\left\langle{s_{i}s_{j}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}^{2}.

(S86)

Eq. (
S84
) then becomes

\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2(\mu N)^{2}}\sum_{ij}k_{i}k_{j}(\Upsilon_{ij})_{\text{st}}.

(S87)

The first term in this expression is the density of active links if the states of neighbouring spins were entirely uncorrelated, which is the same as the formula for the complete network [see Eq. (
S29
)]. The second term represents the reduction in interface density due to correlations introduced by the properties of the network. These appear as terms proportional to
k_{i}k_{j}
from the weighted adjacency matrix, Eq. (
S56
).

To find
(\Upsilon_{ij})_{\text{st}}
, we first formulate a differential equation for
\left\langle{s_{i}s_{j}}\right\rangle
. This is done in a similar manner to Sec.
S4.3
. We use the master equation, Eq. (
S63
), to write

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{s_{i}s_{j}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{s}}s_{i}s_{j}\frac{\differential P(\mathbf{s})}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{s}}s_{i}s_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Big\{F_{k}[W_{k}P]-W_{k}P\Big\}.

(S88)

Again, we have dropped the explicit dependence of
W_{k}
and
P
on
\mathbf{s}
. We first consider the term
k\neq i,k\neq j
. One has

\sum_{s_{k}}\Big(F_{k}[W_{k}P]-W_{k}P\Big)=0,

(S89)

for any fixed
s_{1},\dots,s_{k-1},s_{k+1},\dots,s_{N}
. Thus this term does not contribute. If
k=i\neq j
we have

\sum_{\mathbf{s}_{i}}s_{j}\Bigg\{\sum_{s_{i}}s_{i}\Big(F_{i}[W_{i}P]-W_{i}P\Big)\Bigg\}=-2\sum_{\mathbf{s}}s_{i}s_{j}W_{i}P=-2\left\langle{s_{i}s_{j}W_{i}}\right\rangle.

(S90)

Analogously the case
k=j\neq i
gives
-2\left\langle{s_{i}s_{j}W_{j}}\right\rangle
. Finally for
k=i=j
, we note
s_{i}^{2}=1
, and therefore

\frac{\differential\left\langle{s_{i}s_{i}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=0.

(S91)

Combining all of this, and substituting the expression for
W_{i}
from Eq. (
S61
), gives

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{s_{i}s_{j}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=\begin{cases}\left\langle{(T_{i}^{+}-T_{i}^{-})s_{j}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{(T_{j}^{+}-T_{j}^{-})s_{i}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{q_{ij}s_{i}s_{j}}\right\rangle&\text{if $i\neq j$}\\
0&\text{if $i=j$}\end{cases},

(S92)

where
q_{ij}=T_{i}^{+}+T_{i}^{-}+T_{j}^{+}+T_{j}^{-}
. Using the Kronecker delta, Eq. (
S92
) can be written

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{s_{i}s_{j}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\left(T_{i}^{+}-T_{i}^{-}\right)s_{j}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\left(T_{j}^{+}-T_{j}^{-}\right)s_{i}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad-\left\langle{q_{ij}s_{i}s_{j}}\right\rangle+2\delta_{ij}\Big[\left\langle{T_{i}^{+}+T_{i}^{-}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\left(T_{i}^{+}-T_{i}^{-}\right)s_{i}}\right\rangle\Big].

(S93)

We now substitute the explicit expressions for the rates, Eqs. (
S59
) and (
S60
), into Eq. (
S93
) which gives

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{s_{i}s_{j}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\left[\alpha+\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-})\right]\big(\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{s_{j}}\right\rangle\big)+\frac{\gamma}{\mu N}\sum_{m}k_{m}\Big[\left\langle{s_{m}s_{j}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{s_{m}s_{i}}\right\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad-2(\beta+\gamma)\left\langle{s_{i}s_{j}}\right\rangle+\frac{(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-})}{\mu N}\sum_{m}k_{m}\left\langle{s_{m}s_{i}s_{j}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+2\delta_{ij}\Bigg\{\beta+\gamma+\frac{(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-})}{\mu N}\sum_{m}k_{m}\left\langle{s_{m}}\right\rangle-\left[\alpha+\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-})\right]\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\frac{\gamma}{\mu N}\sum_{m}k_{m}\left\langle{s_{m}s_{i}}\right\rangle\Bigg\}.

(S94)

Then we again make the restriction from Eq. (
S5
),
\gamma_{+}=\gamma_{-}
, and obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{s_{i}s_{j}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\alpha\big(\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{s_{j}}\right\rangle\big)+\frac{\gamma}{\mu N}\sum_{m}k_{m}\Big[\left\langle{s_{m}s_{i}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{s_{m}s_{j}}\right\rangle\Big]-2(\beta+\gamma)\left\langle{s_{i}s_{j}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+2\delta_{ij}\Bigg[\beta+\gamma-\alpha\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle-\frac{\gamma}{\mu N}\sum_{m}k_{m}\left\langle{s_{i}s_{m}}\right\rangle\Bigg].

(S95)

Using Eqs. (
S73
), (
S85
) and (
S95
) we can then write a differential equation for elements of the correlation matrix,

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\Upsilon_{ij}}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{\mu N}\sum_{m}k_{m}\left(\Upsilon_{mi}+\Upsilon_{mj}\right)-2(\beta+\gamma)\Upsilon_{ij}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\delta_{ij}\Big[\beta+\gamma-\alpha\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle-\frac{\gamma}{\mu N}\sum_{m}k_{m}\Upsilon_{im}-\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle\frac{\gamma}{\mu N}\sum_{m}k_{m}\left\langle{s_{m}}\right\rangle\Big].

(S96)

Using the facts that
\tau=\frac{\beta}{\gamma}
and
\left\langle{s_{i}}\right\rangle_{\text{st}}=\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\text{st}}=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}
, from Eqs. (
S46
) and (
S76
) respectively,
(\Upsilon_{ij})_{\text{st}}
is found from Eq. (
S96
) to be

(\Upsilon_{ij})_{\rm st}=\frac{\frac{1}{\mu N}\sum_{m}k_{m}\left[(\Upsilon_{mi})_{\rm st}+(\Upsilon_{mj})_{\rm st}\right]+2\delta_{ij}\left[(1+\tau)\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\rm st}\right)-\frac{1}{\mu N}\sum_{m}k_{m}(\Upsilon_{mi})_{\text{st}}\right]}{2(1+\tau)}.

(S97)

This equation for
(\Upsilon_{ij})_{\text{st}}
involves terms such as
\sum_{m}k_{m}(\Upsilon_{mi})_{\text{st}}
and from Eq. (
S87
) we know we ultimately need to calculate terms such as
\sum_{ij}k_{i}k_{j}(\Upsilon_{ij})_{\text{st}}
. Motivated by this and following the notation in
[
16
]
, we introduce a new variable
S_{x}
,

S_{x}=\sum_{ij}k_{i}^{x}k_{j}(\Upsilon_{ij})_{\rm st},

(S98)

where
x
is an integer such that
k_{i}^{x}
is simply the degree of node
i
raised to the
x
th
power. Substituting Eq. (
S97
) into Eq. (
S98
) allows us to write
S_{x}
as

S_{x}=\frac{S_{x}+\frac{1}{\mu}\overline{k^{x}}S_{1}+2N\overline{k^{x+1}}(1+\tau)\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\text{st}}\right)-\frac{2}{\mu N}S_{x+1}}{2(1+\tau)},

(S99)

where the overbar stands for an average over the degree distribution of the network,

\overline{f(k)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(k_{i}).

(S100)

We then obtain the following recurrence relation,

\displaystyle S_{x+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\mu N}{2}(1+2\tau)S_{x}+\frac{N}{2}\Bigg[\overline{k^{x}}S_{1}+2\mu N\overline{k^{x+1}}(1+\tau)\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\text{st}}\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\equiv{\cal C}S_{x}+{\cal D}_{x},

(S101)

where,

\displaystyle{\cal C}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{-\mu N}{2}(1+2\tau),

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{x}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{N}{2}\Bigg[\overline{k^{x}}S_{1}+2\mu N\overline{k^{x+1}}(1+\tau)\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\text{st}}\right)\Bigg].

(S102)

Eq. (
S101
) has the solution

\displaystyle S_{x+1}

\displaystyle={\cal C}^{x}S_{1}+\sum_{m=1}^{x}{\cal C}^{x-m}{\cal D}_{m}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{-\mu N(1+2\tau)}{2}\right]^{x}S_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{m=1}^{x}\left[\frac{-\mu N(1+2\tau)}{2}\right]^{x-m}\frac{N}{2}\Bigg[\overline{k^{m}}S_{1}+2\mu N\overline{k^{m+1}}(1+\tau)\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\rm st}\right)\Bigg].

(S103)

Ultimately we want to determine
S_{1}
, as this is the term that appears in Eq. (
S87
). With this in mind, we can isolate the
S_{1}
term in Eq. (
S103
) by dividing both sides by
{\cal C}^{x}
, and taking the limit
x\to\infty
. The LHS is

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{S_{x+1}}{\left[\frac{-\mu N(1+2\tau)}{2}\right]^{x}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{ij}k_{i}^{x+1}k_{j}\Upsilon_{ij}}{\left[\frac{-\mu N(1+2\tau)}{2}\right]^{x}}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{-\mu N(1+2\tau)}{2}\right]\lim_{x\to\infty}\sum_{ij}\left[\frac{-2k_{i}}{\mu N(1+2\tau)}\right]^{x+1}k_{j}\Upsilon_{ij}.

(S104)

The limit on the right hand side will be zero provided

\absolutevalue{\frac{-2k_{i}}{\mu N(1+2\tau)}}<1.

(S105)

The only possible ranges of
\tau
are
\tau\geq 0
and
\tau\leq-2
[see Sec.
S8.2.2
]. In each of these we have
\left|\frac{1}{1+2\tau}\right|\leq 1
, and thus the inequality in Eq. (
S105
) is fulfilled if
k_{i}<\mu N/2
. This is the case for
\mu\geq 2
. All networks we consider here have a mean degree higher than two.

We note that since
k_{i}\leq N
, the inequality in Eq. (
S105
) is actually always fulfilled for any
\tau\neq-\frac{1}{2}
provided that
\mu
is sufficiently large. But due to the restrictions on
\tau
[Sec.
S8.2.2
] we do not focus on such cases.

Having proven the limit in Eq. (
S105
) vanishes, we can find
S_{1}
from Eq. (
S103
) to be

S_{1}=\frac{-\mu N^{2}(1+\tau)\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\rm st}\right)\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{-\mu N(1+2\tau)}{2}\right]^{-m}\overline{k^{m+1}}}{1+\frac{N}{2}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{-\mu N(1+2\tau)}{2}\right]^{-m}\overline{k^{m}}}.

(S106)

Now notice that the summations in the above equation can be evaluated as follows,

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{C}^{-m}\overline{k^{m+z}}=\overline{k^{z}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{C}^{-m}k^{m}}=\overline{\frac{\mathcal{C}^{-1}k^{z+1}}{1-\mathcal{C}^{-1}k}},

(S107)

where we have used the infinite geometric series formula under the condition that
\absolutevalue{\mathcal{C}^{-1}k}<1
, which is exactly the same condition as in Eq. (
S105
). After some manipulation we have the final form for
S_{1}
,

S_{1}=\frac{\mu N^{2}(1+\tau)(1+2\tau)\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\rm st}\right)\overline{\left(\frac{k^{2}}{(1+2\tau)\mu N+2k}\right)}}{\tau+\frac{1}{\mu}\overline{\left(\frac{k^{2}}{(1+2\tau)\mu N+2k}\right)}}.

(S108)

This is then substituted back into Eq. (
S87
) and we find a parabolic relationship between
\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\text{st}}
and
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\text{st}}
,

\displaystyle\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\text{st}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}H_{\text{AA}}(\tau)\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\text{st}}^{2}\right),

(S109)

with

\displaystyle H_{\text{AA}}(\tau)=1-\frac{(1+\tau)(1+2\tau)\overline{\left(\frac{k^{2}}{(1+2\tau)\mu N+2k}\right)}}{\tau\mu+\overline{\left(\frac{k^{2}}{(1+2\tau)\mu N+2k}\right)}}.

(S110)

The subscript ‘AA’ stands for ‘annealed approximation’. See Sec.
S8
for detailed discussion on
\tau
and
H(\tau)
.

We see that in the limit
\tau\to 0
,
H_{\text{AA}}(\tau)\to 0
. This matches what we observe in simulation.
In the limit
\tau\to\infty
Eq. (
S110
) reduces to

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}H_{\text{AA}}(\tau)=1-\frac{\overline{k^{2}}}{\mu^{2}N}.

(S111)

Simulations in this limit give
H(\tau)\to 1
. Thus Eq. (
S111
) captures this up to corrections of order
1/N
.

S110
) compares to simulations for different size degree-regular networks (left), Erdös–Rényi networks (middle), and Barabási–Albert networks (right). As discussed above the theory matches simulation in the low

limit, and approximately so in the large
\tau
limit up to a
\frac{1}{N}
correction. There are discrepancies with simulation data for intermediate

S5
Analytical treatment of the model on an infinite square lattice

Next, we consider an infinite square lattice with
N=L\times L
sites. The calculation is based on that of
[
13
]
, but we correct a typo that previously prohibited solving the equations for a general choice of the model parameters. The notation in
[
13
]
is different and a detailed discussion is given in Sec.
S1.3
.

S5.1
Setup and spin flip rates

The state of the node at lattice site
\textbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})
is written
s(\textbf{x})\in\{-1,1\}
. The average state (over realisations of the dynamics) at site
x
is written
\left<s(\textbf{x})\right>
. The global mean magnetisation over the whole lattice is then
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle=\sum_{\textbf{x}}\left\langle{s(\mathbf{{x}})}\right\rangle/N
. The pair correlation between two spins
s(\textbf{x})
and
s(\textbf{y})
will be written
\left<s(\textbf{x})s(\mathbf{y})\right>
. In summations we will use the notation
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}
to denote the set of von Neumann neighbours of
x
, i.e. the set
\{(x_{1}-1,x_{2}),(x_{1}+1,x_{2}),(x_{1},x_{2}-1),(x_{1},x_{2}+1)\}
.

The spin flip probability (in discrete-time) is the probability with which the spin at site
x
changes its state if it is selected for update. In continuous-time we can think of spin flip rates. The flip probability (or rate) for the spin at lattice site
\mathbf{x}
takes the form,

w(\textbf{x})=A(\textbf{x})+B(\textbf{x}),

(S112)

where
A(\textbf{x})
is the contribution from the vertical process

\displaystyle A(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=v_{+}^{\prime}\frac{1-s(\mathbf{x})}{2}+v_{-}^{\prime}\frac{1+s(\mathbf{x})}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big[v_{+}^{\prime}+v_{-}^{\prime}-(v_{+}^{\prime}-v_{-}^{\prime})s(\mathbf{x})\Big],

(S113)

and
B(\textbf{x})
is the contribution from the horizontal process

\displaystyle B(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\Bigg[h_{-}^{\prime}\frac{1+s(\mathbf{x})}{2}\frac{1+s(\mathbf{y})}{2}+h_{+}\frac{1-s(\mathbf{x})}{2}\frac{1+s(\mathbf{y})}{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+h_{+}^{\prime}\frac{1-s(\mathbf{x})}{2}\frac{1-s(\mathbf{y})}{2}+h_{-}\frac{1+s(\mathbf{x})}{2}\frac{1-s(\mathbf{y})}{2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Big[(h_{+}+h_{-})+(h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime})\Big]-\frac{1}{4}\Big[(h_{+}^{\prime}-h_{-}^{\prime})+(h_{+}-h_{-})\Big]s(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{16}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\Big[(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-})s(\mathbf{y})-(\gamma_{+}+\gamma_{-})s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{y})\Big].

(S114)

The total spin flip probability is then

\displaystyle w(\textbf{x})

\displaystyle=A(\textbf{x})+B(\textbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\bigg\{(v_{+}^{\prime}-v_{-}^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2}\Big[(h_{+}^{\prime}-h_{-}^{\prime})+(h_{+}-h_{-})\Big]\bigg\}s(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{16}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\Big[(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-})s(\mathbf{y})-(\gamma_{+}+\gamma_{-})s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{y})\Big],

(S115)

where we have introduced expressions from Eq. (
S4
). We now make the restriction from Eq. (
S5
),
\gamma_{+}=\gamma_{-}
, which allows us to re-write Eq. (
S115
) as

w(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\eta}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}s(\mathbf{x})-\frac{\gamma}{8}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{y}).

(S116)

S5.2
Magnetisation

We proceed with a discrete-time setup in mind. In a given time step, the spin at site
x
changes state with probability
\frac{w(\mathbf{x})}{N}
where the
\frac{1}{N}
factor represents the probability of the spin being selected for update. If a spin flip occurs,
s(\mathbf{x})
changes by an amount
-2s(\textbf{x})
. The change in the average spin,
\left\langle{s(\textbf{x})}\right\rangle
, is then

\left\langle{s(\textbf{x},t+\Delta t)}\right\rangle-\left\langle{s(\textbf{x},t)}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\left<-2w(\textbf{x})s(\textbf{x})\right>,

(S117)

where
\Delta t
denotes a single time step. We now make the standard choice
\Delta t=\frac{1}{N}
and take the limit
N\to\infty
(i.e the continuous-time limit). Substituting in Eq. (
S116
) and evaluating the expectations leads to

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{s(\textbf{x})}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\alpha-\eta\left\langle{s(\textbf{x})}\right\rangle+\frac{\gamma}{4}\sum_{\textbf{y}\in\textbf{x}^{(1)}}\left\langle{s(\textbf{y})}\right\rangle.

(S118)

The same result is obtained if we start out in continuous-time, and think of
w(\mathbf{x})
in Eq. (
S116
) as the rate for the spin at
\mathbf{x}
to flip.

Now we perform a summation over all lattice sites
x
to find a differential equation for the magnetisation
\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle
,

\frac{\differential\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=\alpha-\beta\left\langle{m(t)}\right\rangle.

(S119)

This is exactly the same as Eq. (
S23
) for the complete network, so the solution is identical.

S5.3
Interface density

To compute the interface density we start with the pair correlation
\left<s(\textbf{x})s(\textbf{y})\right>
. The quantity
s(\textbf{x})s(\textbf{y})
(
\textbf{x}\neq\textbf{y}
) changes by an amount
-2s(\textbf{x})s(\textbf{y})
when either the spin at
x
or
y
flips. Working in the continuous-time limit, we find

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{s(\textbf{x})s(\textbf{y})}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\left<-2\left[w(\textbf{x})+w(\textbf{y})\right]s(\mathbf{{x}})s(\mathbf{{y}})\right>

\displaystyle=-2\eta\left\langle{s(\textbf{x})s(\textbf{y})}\right\rangle-2\alpha\Big[\left\langle{s(\mathbf{{x}})}\right\rangle+\left\langle{s(\mathbf{{y}})}\right\rangle\Big]-\frac{\gamma}{4}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{{z}}\in\mathbf{{x}}^{(1)}}\left\langle{s(\textbf{y})s(\textbf{z})}\right\rangle+\sum_{\mathbf{{z}}\in\mathbf{{y}}^{(1)}}\left\langle{s(\textbf{x})s(\textbf{z})}\right\rangle\right).

(S120)

We now assume translational invariance so that
\left\langle{s(\textbf{x})s(\textbf{y})}\right\rangle
is a function of
\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}
only. We write
\left\langle{s(\textbf{x})s(\textbf{y})}\right\rangle=C(\textbf{x}-\textbf{y})
. For
\textbf{r}\equiv\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\neq 0
, we then have

\displaystyle\frac{\differential C(\textbf{r})}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=2\beta\Big[\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\big(\left\langle{s(\mathbf{{x}})}\right\rangle+\left\langle{s(\mathbf{{y}})}\right\rangle\big)-C(\mathbf{{r}})\Big]+2\gamma\Delta C(\mathbf{{r}}),

(S121)

where
\Delta
is the lattice Laplacian,

\Delta f(\textbf{x})=-f(\textbf{x})+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\textbf{y}\in\mathbf{{x}}^{(1)}}f(\textbf{y}).

(S122)

All lattice sites are statistically equivalent to one another in the ensemble of realisations (assuming initial conditions are independent and identical for each lattice site). In the stationary state we then have
\left\langle{s(\textbf{x})}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\left\langle{s(\textbf{y})}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
. We also have
\frac{\differential C(\textbf{r})}{\differential t}=0
, so Eq. (
S121
) becomes

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\beta\Big[\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\rm st}-C(\mathbf{{r}})\Big]+\gamma\Delta C(\mathbf{{r}}),

(S123)

which has the same form as equation (S75) in Ref.
[
13
]
, but where
2\lambda
in the expression in the reference is now replaced by
p_{I}^{\prime}+p_{E}^{\prime}
if we were to map back to the model parameters of that earlier reference via Eq. (
S8
). We note a typographical error in the calculation in
[
13
]
: In equation (S59) of that reference the final term in the last line is
s(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
, whereas it should be
s(\mathbf{x})
. Correcting this means that a problematic term which could not be dealt with in this earlier work no longer appears in Eqs. (
S118
) and (
S120
) of the present paper. In Ref.
[
13
]
, the problematic term is removed by making the assumption
p_{I}^{\prime}=p_{E}^{\prime}
, but the present calculation shows that this assumption is not actually required to proceed.

We omit the remainder of the derivation as it follows that in Ref.
[
13
]
. We find that the expression for the average stationary-state interface is given by

\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\text{st}}=\frac{1}{2}H_{\rm SL}(\tau)\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\text{st}}\right),

(S124)

the subscript ‘SL’ stands for ‘square lattice’. This was obtained for a restricted set of model parameters in
[
13
]
but we now know this to be true when all model parameters are in free variation. The function
H_{\rm SL}(\tau)
is given by

H_{\rm SL}(\tau)=\frac{\pi(1+\tau)}{2K\left(\frac{1}{1+\tau}\right)}-\tau.

(S125)

K(\cdot)
is the complete elliptical integral of the first kind defined as

K(x)=\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\differential\theta}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}\text{sin}^{2}(\theta)}}.

(S126)

The noise-to-order ratio
\tau
is defined as in Eq. (
S46
),

\tau=\frac{\beta}{\gamma}=\frac{(h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime})+(v_{+}^{\prime}+v_{-}^{\prime})}{\frac{1}{2}\left[(h_{+}-h_{+}^{\prime})+(h_{-}-h_{-}^{\prime})\right]}.

(S127)

See Sec.
S8
for detailed discussion on
\tau
and
H(\tau)
.

We note that
K(x)
is symmetric about
x=0
and defined only for
\absolutevalue{x}\leq 1
. When
x\to 0
,
K(x)\to\frac{\pi}{2}
, and when
x\to\pm 1
,
K(x)\to\infty
. The argument in Eq. (
S125
) is
\frac{1}{1+\tau}
, thus
K
is only defined for
\tau\leq-2
and
\tau\geq 0
. A further discussion on the different limits of
\tau
can be found in Sec.
S8.3
.

Fig.
S5
confirms the validity of these results in simulations, and demonstrates that Eq. (
S124
) holds without the restrictions on model parameters that were needed in
[
13
]
.

S6
Approach based on network walks

S6.1
Useful definitions and identities for the further analysis

S6.1.1
Definitions

For any non-negative integer
\ell
we define a walk of length
\ell
as an ordered set of
\ell+1
nodes
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{\ell+1}
on the network, such that
\mathbf{x}_{i+1}
is a nearest neighbour of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,\ell
. We do not include networks with self-loops or multi-links, as these are not relevant in the context of the model. We note that the
\ell+1
nodes do not need to be pairwise different. That is to say, the walker can visit the same node on the network multiple times.

An illustration of a simple network with five nodes is shown in Fig.
S6
. There are four walks of length
\ell=2
starting at node
1
. These are
(1,2,4),(1,3,4),(1,2,1)
and
(1,3,1)
. Similarly, there are two walks of length
\ell=2
starting at node
1
and ending at node
4
, namely
(1,2,4)
and
(1,3,4)
. As a further example, there are four walks of length
\ell=3
starting at
1
and ending at
2
, these are
(1,2,1,2)
,
(1,2,4,2)
,
(1,3,4,2)
and
(1,3,1,2)
.

Figure S6:
Illustration of walks on a simple network (see text).

We define

\displaystyle W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{ number of distinct walks of length $\ell$ starting at $\mathbf{x}$ and ending at $\mathbf{y}$},

\displaystyle\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{number of distinct walks of length $\ell$ starting at $\mathbf{x}$}.

(S128)

For any node
\mathbf{x}
we also introduce

\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}=\big\{\mathbf{y}~|~\mathbf{y}~\mbox{is the endpoint of a walk of length $\ell$ starting at $\mathbf{x}$}\big\}.

(S129)

We note that
\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}
is to be understood as a set, i.e. no node can appear in
\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}
multiple times. Using again the example in Fig.
S6
, and setting
\mathbf{x}
to be node
1
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{x}^{(1)}&=\{2,3\},\\
\mathbf{x}^{(2)}&=\{1,4\},\\
\mathbf{x}^{(3)}&=\{2,3,5\},\\
\mathbf{x}^{(4)}&=\{1,4\}.\end{split}

(S130)

We then have

\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

(S131)

We also note that
\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})
is simply the number of nearest neighbours of node
\mathbf{x}
, i.e. its degree.

The quantity
W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is given by the
\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}
element of the
\ell
th
power of the adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
of the network,

W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=(A^{\ell})_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}.

(S132)

This can be seen by realising that

(A^{\ell})_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}=\sum_{\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{\ell-1}}A_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{z}_{1}}A_{\mathbf{z}_{1}\mathbf{z}_{2}}...A_{\mathbf{z}_{\ell-1}\mathbf{y}},

(S133)

and keeping in mind that elements of the adjacency matrix only take values zero or one. Consequently we also have

\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{y}}(A^{\ell})_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}.

(S134)

S6.1.2
Identities

There are several identities we will use throughout in order to manipulate the summations. Recall, as per Sec.
S1.1
, we only consider undirected networks.

The first identity is

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x})\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}g(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}f(\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{y})

(S135)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\\
\mbox{\small$\ell$-connected}\end{subarray}}f(\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{y}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{y}^{(\ell)}}f(\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}}g(\mathbf{x})\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}f(\mathbf{y}),

where we say that two nodes
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
are ‘
\ell
-connected’ if there exists a walk of length
\ell
connecting
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
, i.e. if
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}
or equivalently,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{y}^{(\ell)}
. In the last line we swap the labels
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
. This identity says that if we have a sum of a function over all nodes, and then an inner sum of a different function over the
\ell
nearest neighbours of those nodes, we can swap the functions around.

A similar identity, derived in the same way, valid for any function
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
,

\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}f(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}).

(S136)

A second identity is derived by first noting that for any fixed
\mathbf{x}
the following two statements for two nodes
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{z}
are equivalent:

(1)

\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}
and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{y}^{(1)}
;

(2)

\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell+1)}
and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}\cap\,\mathbf{z}^{(1)}
.

Statement (2) implies that
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}
and that
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{y}
are nearest neighbours, so statement (1) follows. Further, if
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}
, i.e. there is a walk of length
\ell
from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
, and if
\mathbf{z}
is a nearest neighbour of
\mathbf{y}
, then there exists a walk of length
\ell+1
from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{z}
. This means that (2) is fulfilled. Using this equivalence we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{y}^{(1)}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell+1)}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}\cap\,\mathbf{z}^{(1)}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right)g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell+1)}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}\cap\,\mathbf{y}^{(1)}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\right)g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),

(S137)

for all functions
f(\cdot,\cdot)
and
g(\cdot,\cdot)
.

In the same way the following two statements are equivalent to one another,

(1)

\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}
and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}
;

(2)

\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{z}^{(\ell+1)}
and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{y}^{(\ell)}\cap\,\mathbf{z}^{(1)}
,

leading to the identity,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})g(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{z}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{z}^{(\ell+1)}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{y}^{\ell}\cap\mathbf{z}^{(1)}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right)g(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell+1)}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{y}^{(\ell)}\cap\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}f(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})\right)g(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}),

(S138)

for all functions
f(\cdot,\cdot)
and
g(\cdot,\cdot)
.

S6.2
Magnetisation

The spin-flip probability,
w(\mathbf{x})
, in the case of a general network is constructed in exactly the same way as that of the infinite square lattice in Sec.
S5
. The only difference is that the pre-factor
1/4
in Eq. (
S114
) becomes
1/\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})
. This leads to an equation for
w(\mathbf{x})
analogous to Eq. (
S116
),

\displaystyle w(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\eta}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}s(\mathbf{x})-\frac{\gamma}{2}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{y}),

(S139)

where we have used the shorthands from Eq. (
S4
). We note that we have already made the restriction from Eq. (
S5
),
\gamma_{+}=\gamma_{-}
. Using this we can write an equation which is analogous to Eq. (
S118
),

\displaystyle\frac{\differential S(\mathbf{x})}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\alpha-\eta S(\mathbf{x})+\gamma\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}S(\mathbf{y}).

(S140)

We then take a sum over all nodes
\mathbf{x}
and find the following differential equation for the magnetisation,

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{m}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\alpha-\eta\left\langle{m}\right\rangle+\gamma\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}S(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\alpha-\eta\left\langle{m}\right\rangle+\gamma\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}S(\mathbf{x})\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{y})},

(S141)

where we have made use of Eq. (
S135
).

For each node
\mathbf{x}
we introduce

\lambda_{\mathbf{x},1}=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{y})},

(S142)

and we further define

\left\langle{m_{1}}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\lambda_{\mathbf{x},1}S(\mathbf{x}).

(S143)

We can write Eq. (
S141
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{m}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\alpha-\eta\left\langle{m}\right\rangle+\gamma\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\lambda_{\mathbf{x},1}S(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\alpha-\eta\left\langle{m}\right\rangle+\gamma\left\langle{m_{1}}\right\rangle.

(S144)

Next, using Eq. (
S140
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{m_{1}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\alpha\Lambda_{1}-\eta\left\langle{m_{1}}\right\rangle+\gamma\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\lambda_{\mathbf{x},1}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}S(\mathbf{y}),

(S145)

where we have defined

\Lambda_{1}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\lambda_{\mathbf{x},1}.

(S146)

We can make the following simplification,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\lambda_{\mathbf{x},1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{y})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}1

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})}\cdot\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=1,

(S147)

where we have used Eq. (
S135
) in going from the second to third line. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{m_{1}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\alpha-\eta\left\langle{m_{1}}\right\rangle+\gamma\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\lambda_{\mathbf{x},1}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}S(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\alpha-\eta\left\langle{m_{1}}\right\rangle+\gamma\left\langle{m_{2}}\right\rangle,

(S148)

with the definition

\left\langle{m_{2}}\right\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\lambda_{\mathbf{x},1}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}S(\mathbf{y}).

(S149)

This expression can be simplified as follows,

\displaystyle\left\langle{m_{2}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\lambda_{\mathbf{x},1}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}S(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}S(\mathbf{x})\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{y})}\lambda_{\mathbf{y},1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}S(\mathbf{x})\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{y})}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{y}^{(1)}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{z})}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\lambda_{\mathbf{x},2}S(\mathbf{x}),

(S150)

where we again have used Eq. (
S135
) in going from the first to second line, and where we have defined

\lambda_{\mathbf{x},2}\equiv\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{y})}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{y}^{(1)}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{z})}.

(S151)

We now make the following recursive definition:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathbf{x},n}=\begin{cases}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{1}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}_{1})}\lambda_{\mathbf{x}_{1},n-1}\hskip 14.22636pt&n=1,2,3,...\\
1\hskip 14.22636pt&n=0\end{cases}.

(S152)

Further we write

\left\langle{m_{n}}\right\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\lambda_{\mathbf{x},n}S(\mathbf{x}).

(S153)

We also define

\Lambda_{n}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\lambda_{\mathbf{x},n},

(S154)

and note that

\displaystyle\Lambda_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\lambda_{\mathbf{x},n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{1}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}_{1})}\lambda_{\mathbf{x}_{1},n-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\frac{\lambda_{\mathbf{x},n-1}}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{1}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}1

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\frac{\lambda_{\mathbf{x},n-1}}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})}\cdot\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\lambda_{\mathbf{x},n-1}=\Lambda_{n-1}.

(S155)

Thus, all
\Lambda_{n}
take the same value, and using Eq. (
S147
), we find
\Lambda_{n}=1
for all
n
.

From Eq. (
S140
) we can derive differential equations for general
\left\langle{m_{n}}\right\rangle
,
n=1,2,3,\dots
, leading to the following hierarchy,

\frac{\differential\left\langle{m_{n}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=\alpha-\eta\left\langle{m_{n}}\right\rangle+\gamma\left\langle{m_{n+1}}\right\rangle.

(S156)

Next, we take the steady-state limit, and multiply the equation through by
\frac{\gamma^{n}}{\eta^{n}}
, and sum both sides over
n
(from
0
to
\infty
),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\alpha\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma^{n}}{\eta^{n}}-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\gamma^{n}}{\eta^{n-1}}\left\langle{m_{n}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}-\frac{\gamma^{n+1}}{\eta^{n}}\left\langle{m_{n+1}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\frac{1}{1-\frac{\gamma}{\eta}}-\eta\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}+\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{\gamma^{n+1}}{\eta^{n}}\left\langle{m_{n+1}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\right).

(S157)

The first term results from an infinite geometric series, which only converges provided
\absolutevalue{\gamma/\eta}=\absolutevalue{1/(1+\tau)}<1
[where we have used the definitions in Eqs. (
S4
) and (
S46
)]. This is only the case provided that
\tau<-2
or
\tau>0
. The second and third terms in the second line of Eq. (
S157
) result from the telescopic sum in the first line. We note that
\left\langle{m_{0}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{m}\right\rangle
by construction.

The limit in the second line of Eq. (
S157
) vanishes. Recall that all
\lambda_{\mathbf{x},n}\geq 0
and
\Lambda_{n}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\lambda_{\mathbf{x},n}=1
, so we have
0\leq\lambda_{\mathbf{x},n}\leq 1
. Then from the definition in Eq. (
S153
), we always have
-1\leq\left\langle{m_{n}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\leq 1
. Since
\absolutevalue{\gamma/\eta}<1
the limit then vanishes.

Ultimately then we can solve for
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
. We find

\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\frac{\alpha}{\eta-\gamma}=\frac{\alpha}{\beta},

(S158)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are given in Eq. (
S4
).

This coincides with the result we obtained for infinite complete networks, Eq. (
S24
), using the pair approximation [see Sec.
S3.2
], and using the annealed approximation, Eq. (
S76
). We note that the derivation in the current section did not require any approximation, and is therefore valid for any finite undirected network.

S6.3
Generalisation to ‘weighted magnetisation’

It is possible to generalise the procedure in the previous subsection. Consider the following ‘weighted’ magnetisation,

\left\langle{m_{z}}\right\rangle=\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}z_{\mathbf{x}}S(\mathbf{x})}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bf x}z_{\bf x}},

(S159)

where the
z_{\mathbf{x}}\geq 0
are site dependent weightings. We define

\left\langle{m_{z,n}}\right\rangle=\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}z_{\mathbf{x},n}S(\mathbf{x})}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bf x}z_{\bf x,n}},

(S160)

with

\displaystyle z_{\mathbf{x},n}=\begin{cases}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{1}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}_{1})}z_{\mathbf{x}_{1},n-1}\hskip 14.22636pt&n=1,2,3,...\\
z_{\mathbf{x}}\hskip 14.22636pt&n=0\end{cases}.

(S161)

Similar to Eq. (
S147
) we then have

\zeta_{n}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}z_{\mathbf{x},n}=1\hskip 14.22636pt\forall n.

(S162)

Thus the differential equation for
\left\langle{m_{z,n}}\right\rangle
follows the same recurrence relation as Eq. (
S156
),

\frac{\differential\left\langle{m_{z,n}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=\alpha-\eta\left\langle{m_{z,n}}\right\rangle+\gamma\left\langle{m_{z,n+1}}\right\rangle.

(S163)

We can solve this in the same way as before, leading to

\displaystyle\left\langle{m_{z}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}

\displaystyle=\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}.

(S164)

Thus the steady-state ‘weighted’ magnetisation is always the same, regardless of the weighting. This means, for example, that the magnetisation of a group of specific degree nodes will have the same steady-state independent of the degree chosen. This will become relevant in Sec.
S7
.

S6.4
Interface density

It turns out to be convenient to introduce the following object,

S^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\left<s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{y})\right>.

(S165)

Broadly speaking, this is the correlation function of
s(\mathbf{x})
with spins at sites
\mathbf{y}
that can be reached from
\mathbf{x}
in walks of length
\ell
. Each
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}
is weighted by the number of walks of length
\ell
connecting
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
. Noting the definition of
\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})
from Eq. (
S131
), the pre-factor
1/\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})
ensures overall normalisation, such that
-1\leq S^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})\leq 1
.

As in Sec.
S5.3
, we use again the fact that
s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{y})
changes by an amount
-2s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{y})
when either
s(\mathbf{x})
or
s(\mathbf{y})
flip (
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{y}
). Noting that the node
\mathbf{x}
itself may be contained in the set
\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}
for some
\ell
, and recognising that
s(\mathbf{x})^{2}
always takes the value one (and hence does not change if the spin at
\mathbf{x}
flips), we find in the continuous-time limit

\displaystyle\frac{\differential S^{(\ell)}(x)}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\frac{4W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})}\left<w(\mathbf{x})\right>-\frac{2}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\left<s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{y})\left[w(\mathbf{x})+w(\mathbf{y})\right]\right>.

(S166)

The first term corrects for any contribution from
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}
in the second term (in the event that
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}
).

We will now use Eq. (
S139
), which we repeat here for convenience,

\displaystyle w(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}s(\mathbf{x})-\frac{\gamma}{2}\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{y}),

(S167)

and the definition in Eq. (
S165
). We find

\left<w(\mathbf{x})\right>=\frac{1}{2}\left[\eta-\alpha S(\mathbf{x})-\gamma S^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})\right].

(S168)

We also have

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}

\displaystyle W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\left<s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{y})\left[w(\mathbf{x})+w(\mathbf{y})\right]\right>

\displaystyle=\eta\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})S^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Bigg[\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})S(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})S(\mathbf{y})\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\gamma}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\left[\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{y})}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{y}^{(1)}}\left<s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{z})\right>+\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\left<s(\mathbf{z})s(\mathbf{y})\right>\right].

(S169)

Eq. (
S166
) then becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\differential S^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\frac{2W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})}\left[\eta-\alpha S(\mathbf{x})-\gamma S^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-2\eta S^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})+\alpha\Bigg[S(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})S(\mathbf{y})\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\gamma}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\left[\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{y})}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{y}^{(1)}}\left<s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{z})\right>+\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\left<s(\mathbf{z})s(\mathbf{y})\right>\right].

(S170)

In the steady-state, the derivative on the left-hand side vanishes. We can then divide through by
\gamma
so that the model parameters only appear in the following combinations,

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{\gamma}

\displaystyle=1+\tau,

(S171a)

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\tau\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st},

(S171b)

where
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
and
\tau
are as in Eqs. (
S24
) and (
S46
) respectively. As we will show below, this means that the steady-state interface density can also be determined solely from
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
and
\tau
.

We now sum both sides of Eq. (
S170
) over all nodes
\mathbf{x}
in the network to derive an equation for the overall correlation
C^{(\ell)}
, defined as

C^{(\ell)}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}S^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x}).

(S172)

We find,

\displaystyle\frac{\differential C^{(\ell)}}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=2\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\left\{\frac{W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})}\left[\eta-\alpha S(\mathbf{x})-\gamma S^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})\right]\right\}-2\eta C^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle\qquad+\alpha\Bigg[\left\langle{m}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})S(\mathbf{y})\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\Bigg[\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{y})}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{y}^{(1)}}\left<s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{z})\right>

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\left<s(\mathbf{z})s(\mathbf{y})\right>\Bigg].

(S173)

To proceed we introduce a number of shorthands. We write

\displaystyle\Omega^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})},

(S174a)

\displaystyle\chi^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}\frac{W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{y})},

(S174b)

\displaystyle\psi^{(\ell+1)}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}\cap\mathbf{y}^{(1)}}\left[\frac{W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})W^{(1)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{z})}\right],

(S174c)

\displaystyle\phi^{(\ell+1)}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{y}^{(\ell)}\cap\,\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\left[\frac{W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})W^{(1)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{z})\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{z})}\right].

(S174d)

In practice these coefficients can be evaluated from the
N\times N
adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
of the network. We here recall that the
\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}
element of the
\ell
th
power of the adjacency gives the number of possible length
\ell
walks starting at
\mathbf{x}
and ending at
\mathbf{y}
, denoted
W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, see Eq. (
S132
). The sum of such elements over all
\mathbf{y}
gives the total number of walks of length
\ell
starting from
\mathbf{x}
, denoted
\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})
, see Eq. (
S134
). Using these, we have

\displaystyle\Omega^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(A^{\ell})_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}}{\sum_{\mathbf{y}}(A^{\ell})_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}},

(S175a)

\displaystyle\chi^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{y}}\frac{(A^{\ell})_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}}}{\sum_{\mathbf{z}}(A^{\ell})_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{z}}},

(S175b)

\displaystyle\psi^{(\ell+1)}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{\mathbf{z}}(A^{\ell})_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{z}}}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{z}}(A^{\ell})_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{z}}\frac{1}{\sum_{\mathbf{u}}A_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{u}}}A_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{y}}\right],

(S175c)

\displaystyle\phi^{(\ell+1)}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{z}}\left[\frac{(A^{\ell})_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{y}}A_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{x}}}{\sum_{\mathbf{u}}(A^{k})_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{u}}\sum_{\mathbf{u}}A_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{u}}}\right].

(S175d)

We now proceed to write Eq. (
S173
) in more compact form. Using Eq. (
S136
) we observe for the term on the second line,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})S(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}\left(\frac{W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})}S(\mathbf{y})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}\frac{W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{y})}\right)S(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\chi_{\mathbf{x}}^{(\ell)}S(\mathbf{x}).

(S176)

For the first term on the third line,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{y})}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{y}^{(1)}}\left<s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{z})\right>

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{y}^{(1)}}\frac{W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{y})}\left\langle{s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{z})}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell+1)}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}\cap\,\mathbf{y}^{(1)}}\frac{W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{z})}\right)\left<s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{y})\right>

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell+1)}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}\cap\,\mathbf{y}^{(1)}}\frac{W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})W^{(1)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{z})}\right)\left<s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{y})\right>

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell+1)}}\psi^{(\ell+1)}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\left<s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{y})\right>,

(S177)

where we have first used Eq. (
S137
) in going from the second to third line, and then introduced
W^{(1)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=1
for
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{y}^{(1)}
, before finally using the definition of
\psi^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
from Eq. (
S174c
). In a similar manner one shows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\frac{1}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\left<s(\mathbf{z})s(\mathbf{y})\right>

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{y}^{(1)}}\frac{W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})}\left<s(\mathbf{z})s(\mathbf{y})\right>

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell+1)}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{y}^{(\ell)}\cap\,\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\frac{W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{z})\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{z})}\right)\left<s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{y})\right>

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell+1)}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{y}^{(\ell)}\cap\,\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\frac{W^{(1)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{z})\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{z})}\right)\left<s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{y})\right>

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell+1)}}\phi^{(\ell+1)}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\left<s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{y})\right>,

(S178)

where we have first used Eq. (
S138
) in going from the second to third line, then introduced
W^{(1)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})=1
for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{z}^{(1)}
, and finally used the definition of
\phi^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
from Eq. (
S174d
). Eq. (
S173
) then becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\differential C^{(\ell)}}{\differential t}=

\displaystyle 2\eta\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\Omega^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{x}}-\alpha\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\left[2\Omega^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{x}}-\chi^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{x}}\right]S(\mathbf{x})+\alpha\left\langle{m}\right\rangle-2\eta C^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle\qquad-2\gamma\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\Omega^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{x}}S^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})+\gamma\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell+1)}}\left[\psi^{(\ell+1)}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}+\phi^{(\ell+1)}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right]\left<s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{y})\right>.

(S179)

Several of the terms in the last expression take the form of weighted magnetisations, whose steady-state we can determine from Eq. (
S164
). However the two final terms are problematic and we have not attempted to solve this equation in full generality. Progress is possible for what we will call ‘homogeneous’ networks, as discussed in the next section.

Alternatively we can choose model parameters such that
\gamma=0
. However, this would mean that
\tau\to\pm\infty
and the steady-state configuration would always be random which is not of particular interest (see also Sec.
S8
).

S6.5
Homogeneous networks

S6.5.1
Homogeneity assumptions

We define networks that fulfill the following two properties as
homogeneous
:

(1)

the total number walks of length
\ell
starting and ending at any node
\mathbf{x}
is the same for all
\mathbf{x}
,
W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})\equiv W^{(\ell)}
;

(2)

the total number of walks of length
\ell
starting at
\mathbf{x}
(ending at any point) is also the same for all
\mathbf{x}
,
\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})\equiv\nu^{(\ell)}
.

There are several types of network that fall under this category. For example, infinite regular lattices in any dimension or Bethe lattices (infinite regular trees) have these properties. Individual realisations of degree-regular networks do not fulfill (1) and (2), but these conditions hold as averages over the ensemble of degree-regular networks, i.e. the expected value of
W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})
is the same for all nodes
\mathbf{x}
, and similarly for the expected value of
\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})
.

We now proceed making these assumptions, writing
W^{(\ell)}
and
\nu^{(\ell)}
for the common values of all
W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})
and
\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})
respectively. The assumptions imply in particular that all nodes in the network have the same degree,
\nu^{(1)}
. We note that we then have
\nu^{(\ell+1)}=\nu^{(\ell)}\nu^{(1)}
, and from this we find
\nu^{(\ell)}=(\nu^{(1)})^{\ell}
.

Noting again that
W^{(1)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=A_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{y}}
is equal to one if and only if
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{y}
are nearest neighbours, and zero otherwise, Eqs. (
S174a
)-(
S174d
) can then be simplified as follows:

\displaystyle\Omega^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})}=\frac{W^{(\ell)}}{\nu^{(\ell)}}\equiv\Omega^{(\ell)},\hskip 28.45274pt\forall\mathbf{x}

(S180a)

\displaystyle\chi^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}\frac{W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{y})}=\frac{1}{\nu^{(\ell)}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}}W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})=1,\hskip 28.45274pt\forall\mathbf{x}

(S180b)

\displaystyle\psi^{(\ell+1)}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}\cap\mathbf{y}^{(1)}}W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{z})}W^{(1)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu^{(\ell)}\nu^{(1)}}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{y}^{(1)}}W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})W^{(1)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{W^{(\ell+1)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\nu^{(\ell+1)}},\hskip 28.45274pt\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}

(S180c)

\displaystyle\phi^{(\ell+1)}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{y}^{(\ell)}\cap\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}\left[\frac{1}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{z})}W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})\frac{1}{\nu^{(1)}(\mathbf{z})}W^{(1)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu^{(\ell+1)}}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}W^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{W^{(\ell+1)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\nu^{(\ell+1)}}.\hskip 28.45274pt\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}

(S180d)

S6.5.2
Interface density for homogeneous networks, and resulting simplifications

Assuming that the underlying network is homogeneous (in the sense as defined above), Eq. (
S179
) reduces to

\frac{\differential C^{(\ell)}}{\differential t}=2\Omega^{(\ell)}\Big[\eta-\alpha\left\langle{m}\right\rangle-\gamma C^{(1)}\Big]-2\Big[\eta C^{(\ell)}-\alpha\left\langle{m}\right\rangle-\gamma C^{(\ell+1)}\Big].

(S181)

In the steady-state we then have the following relation:

\eta C^{(\ell)}_{\rm st}-\gamma C^{(\ell+1)}_{\rm st}=\Omega^{(\ell)}\Big[\eta-\alpha\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}-\gamma C^{(1)}_{\rm st}\Big]+\alpha\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}.

(S182)

The interface density, the quantity we are aiming to calculate, is given by

\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle=\frac{1-C^{(1)}}{2},

(S183)

thus we need to determine
C^{(1)}_{\rm st}
. However setting
\ell=0
in Eq. (
S182
) is not useful, as all terms involving
C^{(1)}_{\rm st}
then cancel out because of
\Omega^{(\ell=0)}=1
. To see the latter we note that there is one walk of length zero steps starting at
\mathbf{x}
, and this walk trivially also ends at
\mathbf{x}
. Thus Eq. (
S180a
) evaluates to one.

Instead, to proceed we multiply both sides of Eq. (
S182
) by
\gamma^{\ell}/\eta^{\ell}
, and then sum over
\ell
from zero to infinity. We find

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\Bigg(\frac{\gamma^{\ell}}{\eta^{\ell-1}}C^{(\ell)}_{\rm st}

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma^{\ell+1}}{\eta^{\ell}}C^{(\ell+1)}_{\rm st}\Bigg)-\alpha\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma^{\ell}}{\eta^{\ell}}=\Big[\eta-\alpha\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}-\gamma C^{(1)}_{\rm st}\Big]\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\Omega^{(\ell)}\frac{\gamma^{\ell}}{\eta^{\ell}}.

(S184)

The first term on the left-hand side is a telescopic sum and simplifies to

\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\Bigg(\frac{\gamma^{\ell}}{\eta^{\ell-1}}C^{(\ell)}_{\rm st}-\frac{\gamma^{\ell+1}}{\eta^{\ell}}C^{(\ell+1)}_{\rm st}\Bigg)=\eta C^{(0)}_{\rm st}-\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{\gamma^{\ell+1}}{\eta^{\ell}}C^{(\ell+1)}_{\rm st}.

(S185)

We can use analogous arguments to that at the end of Sec.
S6.2
, except considering
C^{(\ell)}_{\rm st}
instead of
\left\langle{m_{n}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
, to perform the geometric sum in Eq. (
S184
) and show that the limit in Eq. (
S185
) vanishes. Eq. (
S184
) then becomes

\eta C^{(0)}_{\rm st}-\frac{\alpha\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}}{1-\frac{\gamma}{\eta}}=\Big[\eta-\alpha\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}-\gamma C^{(1)}_{\rm st}\Big]\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\Omega^{(\ell)}\left(\frac{1}{1+\tau}\right)^{\ell}.

(S186)

Using the fact that
C^{(0)}_{\rm st}=\left\langle{s(\mathbf{x})s(\mathbf{x})}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=1
, and the identities from Eqs. (
S171a
) and (
S171b
), and Eq. (
S183
), we find

\displaystyle\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\rm st}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}H_{\rm HG}(\tau,\mathbf{A})\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\rm st}\right),

(S187)

with

H_{\rm HG}(\tau)=\frac{1+\tau}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\Omega^{(\ell)}\left(\frac{1}{1+\tau}\right)^{\ell}}-\tau.

(S188)

We note that calculation of
\Omega^{(\ell)}
requires the adjacency matrix of the network
\mathbf{A}
. The subscript ‘HG’ stands for ‘homogeneous graph’, and indicates that the relation holds only when the homogeneity assumptions apply. See Sec.
S8
for detailed discussion on
\tau
and
H(\tau)
.

Eq. (
S188
) is not closed form but the coefficients
\Omega^{(\ell)}
can, in principle, be obtained through direct enumeration of walks on a given network. We note that this only needs to be performed once for a given network, to obtain the function
H_{\rm HG}(\tau)
. We discuss this further in Sec.
S6.5.7
.

We now move on to use Eq. (
S188
) for a number of special cases (finite complete networks, hyper-cubic lattices, and Bethe lattices) where closed form solutions can be found.

S6.5.3
First special case: Finite complete networks

We consider a complete network with
N
nodes. For any fixed node, we write
a_{N}(\ell)
for the number of walks starting and ending at that node (this number will be the same for all nodes, given the all-to-all connectivity).

Figure S7:
Illustration of closed walks on a complete network (see text).

One then has the following recursion

a_{N}(\ell)=(N-1)^{\ell-1}-a_{N}(\ell-1),

(S189)

with
a_{N}(0)\equiv 1
.

This recursion can be derived as follows (see Fig.
S7
), keeping in mind that the walker cannot stay put in any step of the walk. Consider a fixed node
\mathbf{x}
, and a closed walk of length
\ell
starting and ending at
\mathbf{x}
. The walk consists of a sequence of
\ell+1
nodes in total, which we label
\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{\ell}
. The first and the last of these are given by
\mathbf{x}
(
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{x}_{\ell}=\mathbf{x}
), shown in blue in Fig.
S7
. Now focus on node
\mathbf{x}_{1}
. This node can be any node in the network other than node
\mathbf{x}_{0}
(the walker must make a hop in the first step). Similarly,
\mathbf{x}_{2}
can be any node other than
\mathbf{x}_{1}
, and so on. If we follow this though until (and including) node
\mathbf{x}_{\ell-1}
, we find a total of
(N-1)^{\ell-1}
possible sequences for the nodes
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{\ell-1}
such that no node is the same as the previous one in the sequence. This produces the term
(N-1)^{\ell-1}
in Eq. (
S189
). There is however another constraint, and not all of these sequences produce valid walks of length
\ell
starting and ending at
\mathbf{x}
. In a valid walk, node
\mathbf{x}_{\ell-1}
(shown in red in the figure) must not be node
\mathbf{x}
, as the walker must make a hop in the
\ell
th
step. We therefore need to subtract the number of all sequences for which this is the case. This number is
a_{N}(\ell-1)
, as the sequence
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{\ell-1}=\mathbf{x}
is then a closed walk of length
\ell-1
starting and ending at
\mathbf{x}
. This produces the second term in Eq. (
S189
). Thus, we have demonstrated the validity of the recursion.

One can directly show that (see e.g. sequences A109499, A109500, A109501 in
[
30
]
)

a_{N}(\ell)=\frac{(N-1)^{\ell}+(N-1)(-1)^{\ell}}{N}

(S190)

fulfills the recursion in Eq. (
S189
), as well as the condition
a_{N}(0)=1
.

Given that further the number of all walks of length
\ell
starting at
\mathbf{x}
on a complete network of size
N
is given by
\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})=(N-1)^{\ell}
, we have

\Omega^{(\ell)}=\frac{W^{(\ell)}}{\nu^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})}=\frac{a_{N}(\ell)}{(N-1)^{\ell}}=\frac{(N-1)^{\ell}+(N-1)(-1)^{\ell}}{N(N-1)^{\ell}}.

(S191)

The generating function for the sequence
\Omega^{(\ell)}
can be directly evaluated to be

\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\Omega^{(\ell)}z^{\ell}=\frac{1-\left(\frac{N-2}{N-1}\right)z}{1-\left(\frac{N-2}{N-1}\right)z-\left(\frac{1}{N-1}\right)z^{2}}.

(S192)

Thus Eq. (
S187
) for the finite complete network is

\displaystyle\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\rm st}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{(N-1)\tau}{1+(N-1)\tau}\right]\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\rm st}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}H_{\rm CG}(\tau,N)\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\rm st}\right),

(S193)

(the subscript ‘CG’ stands for ‘complete graph’). We note that in the limit
N\rightarrow\infty
we recover Eq. (
S29
).

S6.5.4
Second special case: Infinite hyper-cubic lattice in
d
-dimensions

We next consider the case of a hyper-cubic lattice in
d
-dimensions. We then have
\Omega^{(\ell)}=0
for odd values of
\ell
, as it is impossible to return to a given point in an odd number of steps on a hyper-cubic lattice. Thus we focus on even
\ell=2k
. So looking at Eq. (
S188
) we need to calculate
\Omega^{(\ell)}\to\Omega^{(2k)}_{d}
, where the
d
subscript denotes the dimension of the hyper-cubic lattice. From Eq. (
S180a
) we have

\Omega^{(2k)}_{d}=\frac{W^{(2k)}_{d}}{\nu^{(2k)}_{d}},

(S194)

On a
d
-dimensional hyper-cubic lattice, the total number of walks of length
2k
starting at a given point is

\nu^{(2k)}_{d}=(2d)^{2k},

(S195)

given that the walker can choose from one of
2d
directions at each step. The number of walks starting and ending at the same point is

W_{d}^{(2k)}=\sum_{n_{1}+...+n_{d}=k}\frac{(2k)!}{\prod_{i=1}^{d}(n_{i}!)^{2}}.

(S196)

In this expression the
n_{i}
(
i=1,\dots,d
) are integer numbers;
n_{i}
indicates how many steps in total the walker takes in the positive
i
direction along the walk. Given that the walker returns to the starting point,
n_{i}
is also the number of steps taken in the negative
i
direction. Since a total of
2k
steps must be made, we have
\sum_{i}2n_{i}=2k
, i.e.
\sum_{i}n_{i}=k
. The term inside the sum in Eq. (
S196
) is the number of distinct walks that can be generated for a given choice of
n_{1},\dots,n_{d}
. The multinomial coefficient is the number of permutations of the
n_{1}
forward steps in direction
1
, the
n_{1}
backward steps in direction
1
, the
n_{2}
forward steps in direction
2
, the
n_{2}
backward steps in direction
2
and so on, keeping in mind that the total number of steps is
2k
.

Using this we can construct the following so-called ‘exponential generating function’ [note the factors of
1/(2k)!
which is the difference between an exponential generating function and an ordinary generating function],

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}W_{d}^{(2k)}\frac{x^{2k}}{(2k)!}=I_{0}(2x)^{d},

(S197)

where
I_{0}(\cdot)
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind. A proof of this can be found in Sec.
S10.1
. A Laplace-Borel transform, which we define as

\mathcal{L}_{B}[f](x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}f(tx)dt,

(S198)

can be used to convert an exponential generating function of the type in Eq. (
S197
) to an ordinary generating function
[
31
, pp. 374, 566]
,
[
32
, p. 750]
or
[
33
, p. 2]
. Therefore we have

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}W_{d}^{(2k)}x^{2k}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}[I_{0}(2xt)]^{d}\differential t.

(S199)

What we want is a summation of the form in Eq. (
S188
), so we need to incorporate the
\nu^{(2k)}_{d}=(2d)^{2k}
factor which can be done by applying the transform
x\to\frac{x}{2d}
to Eq. (
S199
),

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}W_{d}^{(2k)}\frac{x^{2k}}{(2d)^{2k}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\left[I_{0}\left(\frac{xt}{d}\right)\right]^{d}\differential t.

(S200)

With
x=\frac{1}{1+\tau}
, the summation on the LHS is exactly that in Eq. (
S188
) for an infinite hyper-cubic
d
-dimensional lattice. So we are left with solving the integral on the RHS.

To proceed, it is now useful to make the substitution
xt=z
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\left[I_{0}\left(\frac{xt}{d}\right)\right]^{d}\differential t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{z}{x}}\left[I_{0}\left(\frac{z}{d}\right)\right]^{d}\differential z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\mathcal{L}\left(\left[I_{0}\left(\frac{z}{d}\right)\right]^{d}\right).

(S201)

where
\mathcal{L}
is the standard Laplace transform defined as

\mathcal{L}[f](s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sz}f(z)\differential z,

(S202)

where in this case
s=\frac{1}{x}
. We can now make use of the following general identity
[
34
, p. 346]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(z^{\lambda}\prod_{i=1}^{d}I_{\nu_{i}}(a_{i}z)\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+\nu+1)}{2^{\nu}p^{\lambda+\nu+1}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{a_{i}^{\nu_{i}}}{\Gamma(\nu_{i}+1)}\right]

\displaystyle\times F_{C}^{(d)}\left(\frac{\lambda+\nu+1}{2},\frac{\lambda+\nu+2}{2};\nu_{1},...,\nu_{d}+1;\frac{a_{1}^{2}}{p^{2}},...,\frac{a_{d}^{2}}{p^{2}}\right),

(S203)

where
F_{C}^{(d)}
is a Lauricella hypergeometric series
[
35
, p. 113]
[
36
]
,

F_{C}^{(d)}(a,b;c_{1},...,c_{d};y_{1},...,y_{d})=\sum_{i_{1},...,i_{d}=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_{i_{1}+...+i_{d}}(b)_{i_{1}+...+i_{d}}}{(c_{1})_{i_{1}}...(c_{d})_{i_{d}}i_{1}!...i_{d}!}y_{1}^{i_{1}}...y_{d}^{i_{d}},

(S204)

with

(q)_{i}\equiv q(q+1)...(q+i-1),

(S205)

for
i=0,1,2,3,\dots
, referred to as the Pochhammer symbol or rising factorial.

We note that the identity in Eq. (
S203
) as printed in
[
34
]
contains a typographical error, for further details see also
[
37
]
. This error has been corrected in Eq. (
S203
).

We now substitute Eq. (
S203
) in the special case
\lambda=0
,
\nu_{i}=0
and
a_{i}=\frac{1}{d}
into Eq. (
S201
) and find

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\left[I_{0}\left(\frac{zt}{d}\right)\right]^{d}\differential t

\displaystyle=F_{C}^{(d)}\left(\frac{1}{2},1;1,...,1;\frac{1}{\left[d(1+\tau)\right]^{2}},...,\frac{1}{\left[d(1+\tau)\right]^{2}}\right),

(S206)

where
z=\frac{1}{1+\tau}
. With this, we find the following expression for the interface density on a
d
-dimensional hyper-cubic lattice,

\left<\sigma\right>_{\rm st}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{1+\tau}{F_{C}^{(d)}\left(\frac{1}{2},1;1,...,1;\frac{1}{\left[d(1+\tau)\right]^{2}},...,\frac{1}{\left[d(1+\tau)\right]^{2}}\right)}-\tau\right]\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\rm st}\right).

(S207)

S6.5.5
Application to infinite two-dimensional square lattice

We have already derived an analytical expression for
H(\tau)
for infinite 2D square lattice in Sec.
S5
of this Supplement [see Eq. (
S125
)]. However as a test of our results for the homogeneous network and hyper-cubic lattice in
d
-dimensions we will re-derive it, with reference to several points of the derivation.

First method: Closed form expression for
\Omega^{(2k)}

We can directly evaluate the summation in Eq. (
S188
) by first determining a closed form expression for
\Omega^{(\ell)}
. On an infinite square lattice only even length walks can return us to the initial position, so it is really
\Omega^{(2k)}
we wish to calculate.

The number of closed walks of length
2k
can be calculated with the following combinatorial argument. If we take
p
steps North, we must then also take
p
steps South. There are
\binom{2k}{2p}
ways to choose these
2p
steps from
2k
and
\binom{2p}{p}
ways to organise the North and South steps. From the remaining steps, half of them must be East and the other half must be West, there are
\binom{2k-2p}{k-p}
ways of arranging these steps. Thus the total number of closed walks of length
2k
is

\displaystyle\sum_{p=0}^{k}\binom{2k}{2p}\binom{2p}{p}\binom{2(k-p)}{k-p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{k}\frac{(2k)!}{(2p)!(2(k-p))!}\cdot\frac{(2p)!}{p!^{2}}\cdot\frac{(2(k-p))!}{(k-p)!^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2k)!}{k!^{2}}\sum_{p=0}^{k}\frac{k!^{2}}{p!^{2}(k-p)!^{2}}

\displaystyle=\binom{2k}{k}\sum_{p=0}^{k}\binom{k}{p}\binom{k}{k-p}

\displaystyle=\binom{2k}{k}^{2},

(S208)

where in the last step we use Vandermonde’s identity. We also note that the total number of length
2k
walks on a 2D square lattice is
\nu^{(2k)}=4^{2k}
. Thus, letting
x=\frac{1}{1+\tau}
, the summation in Eq. (
S188
) becomes

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Omega^{(2k)}x^{2k}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{2k}{k}^{2}\frac{x^{2k}}{4^{2k}}=\frac{2}{\pi}K(x),

(S209)

where we have used the power series for the complete elliptical integral of the first kind
[
38
, (1.3.6)]
. With this, Eq. (
S188
) reduces exactly to Eq. (
S125
).

Second method: Relation of elliptic integral to modified Bessel function

The second way of verifying the special case
d=2
is by evaluation of Eq. (
S200
) for
d=2
.

We are looking to prove that the Laplace-Borel transformation [see Eq. (
S198
)] of the square of modified Bessel functions of the first kind is proportional to the complete elliptical integral of the first kind i.e.

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}I_{0}\left(\frac{xt}{2}\right)^{2}\differential t=\frac{2}{\pi}K(x).

(S210)

First, make the change of variables
xt=z
to convert the Laplace-Borel transform into a regular Laplace transform,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}I_{0}\left(\frac{xt}{2}\right)^{2}\differential t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-pz}I_{0}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2}\differential z,

(S211)

where
p=\frac{1}{x}
. From Sec.
S10.1
we find the exponential generating function for the square of modified Bessel functions of the first kind is

\displaystyle I_{0}(2y)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}W_{2}^{(2k)}\frac{y^{2k}}{(2k)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\binom{2k}{k}}^{2}\frac{y^{2k}}{(2k)!},

(S212)

where
W_{2}^{(2k)}
can be found from Eq. (
S196
) with
d=2
. Substituting Eq. (
S212
) into Eq. (
S211
) gives,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-pz}I_{0}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2}\differential z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-pz}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\binom{2k}{k}}^{2}\left(\frac{z}{4}\right)^{2k}\frac{1}{(2k)!}\differential z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\binom{2k}{k}}^{2}\frac{1}{(2k)!}\frac{1}{4^{2k}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-pz}{z}^{2k}\differential z.

(S213)

The integral here is simply the Laplace transform of
{z}^{2k}
given by
[
34
, p. 15]
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-pz}{z}^{2k}\differential z

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(2k+1)}{p^{2k+1}}

\displaystyle=x^{2k+1}(2k)!,

(S214)

where we recall
p=\frac{1}{x}
and where we have used the definition of the Gamma function for integer
n

[
39
, p. 255]
,

\Gamma(n)=(n-1)!.

(S215)

Putting this back into Eq. (
S213
) we finally have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}I_{0}\left(\frac{xt}{2}\right)^{2}\differential t

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\binom{2k}{k}}^{2}\frac{x^{2k}}{4^{2k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}K(x),

(S216)

where we have used the power series for the complete elliptical integral of the first kind
[
38
, (1.3.6)]
. Thus we have shown that Eq. (
S200
) evaluated at
d=2
, which is exactly the relevant summation appearing in Eq. (
S188
) for the 2D infinite square lattice, is proportional to
K(x)
, thus Eq. (
S188
) reduces to Eq. (
S125
).

Third proof: Relation of elliptic integral to Lauricella hyper-geometric series

The final method is to evaluate Eq. (
S207
), for the
d
-dimensional lattice, for the case
d=2
.

For
d=2
, the following Lauricella series corresponds to the Appell hypergeometric series
[
36
]
,

\displaystyle F_{C}^{(2)}\Bigg(\frac{1}{2},1;1,1;\frac{1}{4(1+\tau)^{2}}

\displaystyle,\frac{1}{4(1+\tau)^{2}}\Bigg)=F_{4}\left(\frac{1}{2},1;1,1;\frac{1}{4(1+\tau)^{2}},\frac{1}{4(1+\tau)^{2}}\right).

(S217)

An Appell hypergeometric series of this form can be written as a generalised hypergeometric series
[
40
, Eq. (37)]

F_{4}\left(\frac{1}{2},1;1,1;\frac{1}{4(1+\tau)^{2}},\frac{1}{4(1+\tau)^{2}}\right)={}_{4}F_{3}\left(\frac{1}{2},1,1,\frac{1}{2};1,1,1;\frac{1}{(1+\tau)^{2}}\right).

(S218)

The generalised hypergeometric function
{}_{p}F_{q}
is defined as
[
41
, p. 439]
,

{}_{p}F_{q}(a_{1},...,a_{p};b_{1},...,b_{q};x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1})_{k},...,(a_{p})_{k}}{(b_{1})_{k},...,(b_{q})_{k}}\frac{x^{k}}{k!}.

(S219)

Using this we can show that

{}_{4}F_{3}\left(\frac{1}{2},1,1,\frac{1}{2};1,1,1;x^{2}\right)={}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},1;x^{2}\right).

(S220)

Finally it is known that
[
42
, p. 51]

{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},1;x^{2}\right)\equiv\frac{2}{\pi}K(x).

(S221)

Thus we have shown that Eq. (
S207
) reduces to Eq. (
S124
) for the 2D infinite square lattice.

S6.5.6
Third special case: Infinite Bethe lattice

The infinite regular tree, or Bethe lattice, is a network in which every node has degree
n
and which has a cascading tree-like structure such that there are no loops or leaf nodes.

On such trees, only walks of even length can return to the starting point. So we have
W^{(2k+1)}=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and thus only need to perform the summation in Eq. (
S188
) over even
k
. Additionally, it can be seen by inspection that
\nu^{(\ell)}=n^{\ell}
. Ultimately, the summation in Eq. (
S188
) reduces to

T\left(z\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{T}^{(k)}z^{k},

(S222)

for

z=\frac{1}{n^{2}(1+\tau)^{2}},

(S223)

where
\mathcal{T}^{(k)}\equiv W^{(2k)}
is the number of self returning walks of length
2k
from any point on the network.

This means that we need the generating function for the sequence
\mathcal{T}^{(k)}
. To calculate this we follow the approach in
[
43
]
and first consider an infinite rooted tree, in which the root has degree
r
and all other nodes have degree
c+1
. We write
\mathbb{T}
for this tree.

A closed rooted walk is defined as a walk which starts and ends at the root node. We will refer to the nearest neighbours of the root as the ‘children’.

Any closed rooted walk of length
2k
in
\mathbb{T}
can be broken down as follows:

1.

The walker starts at the root, and takes one step from the root to one of its
r
nearest neighbours (i.e., a child).

2.

A closed walk of length
2i
, avoiding the root, is undertaken, starting at the child node in step 1, and bringing the walker back to the same child.

3.

The walker returns to the root node, having taken
2(i+1)
steps in total.

4.

A closed rooted walk of length
2k-2(i+1)
can now be undertaken.

We define
{\cal R}_{r}^{(k)}
as the number of self-returning walks of length
2k
from the root node, and
{\cal C}_{c+1}^{(k)}
as the number of self-returning root-avoiding walks of length
2k
from one of the children. The subscripts indicate the degree of the root (
r
) and that of all other nodes (
c+1
) respectively. These quantities are specific to the tree
\mathbb{T}
. However, the number of self-returning root-avoiding walks starting at a child will not depend on the degree
r
of the root.

From the above breakdown on rooted walks of length
2k
we can obtain the following recursive relation:

{\cal R}_{r}^{(n+1)}=r\sum_{i=0}^{n}{\cal R}_{r}^{(i)}{\cal C}_{c+1}^{(n-i)}.

(S224)

We next define

R_{r}(z)\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\cal R}^{(k)}_{r}z^{k}

(S225)

as the generating function for self-returning walks in the tree from the root node which has degree
r
. Similarly,

C_{c+1}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\cal C}^{(k)}_{c+1}z^{k}

(S226)

is the generating function for self-returning walks in the tree from a child of the root node and avoiding the root.

With these definitions we have

\displaystyle R_{r}(z)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\cal R}^{(k)}_{r}z^{k}

(S227)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{\cal R}^{(k)}_{r}z^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+r\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{\cal R}_{r}^{(i)}{\cal C}_{c+1}^{(k-1-i)}z^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+rz\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{k}{\cal R}_{r}^{(i)}{\cal C}_{c+1}^{(k-i)}z^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+rzR_{r}(z)C_{c+1}(z).

The last step can be seen by writing out the first few terms of the series for
R_{r}(z)
and
C_{c+1}(z)
in Eqs. (
S225
) and (
S226
).

We can determine
R_{r}(z)
from Eq. (
S227
) if we can calculate
C_{c+1}(z)
. To do this, we note again that
{\cal C}_{c+1}^{(k)}
does not depend on the degree of the root. Hence
C_{c+1}(z)
is not a function of the root-degree either. With this in mind, we now consider a tree
\tilde{\mathbb{T}}
with root of degree
c
and where, as before, all other nodes have degree
c+1
. Setting
r=c
in Eq. (
S227
) we then have

\tilde{R}_{c}(z)=1+cz\tilde{R}_{c}(z)\tilde{C}_{c+1}(z),

(S228)

where the tildes indicate that these are generating functions for the tree
\tilde{\mathbb{T}}
. The only difference between the trees
\mathbb{T}
and
\tilde{\mathbb{T}}
is the degree of the root. Hence
\tilde{C}_{c+1}(z)=C_{c+1}(z)
.

Next we realise that
\tilde{R}_{c}(z)=\tilde{C}_{c+1}(z)
. This is because
\tilde{C}_{c+1}(z)
is the generating function of self-returning walks starting from a child and avoiding the root on the tree
\tilde{\mathbb{T}}
. For the purpose of counting such walks the child effectively has degree
c
(the same degree as the root of
\tilde{\mathbb{T}}
). Hence on the tree
\tilde{\mathbb{T}}
, the number of self-returning root-avoiding walks of a given length and starting from a child is the same as the number of self-returning walks of this length starting at the root.

Using
\tilde{R}_{c}(z)=\tilde{C}_{c+1}(z)
and
\tilde{C}_{c+1}(z)=C_{c+1}(z)
Eq. (
S228
) thus becomes

C_{c+1}(z)=1+czC^{2}_{c+1}(z).

(S229)

We can then solve for
C_{c+1}(z)
and find the physically relevant solution to be

C_{c+1}(z)=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4zc}}{2zc}

(S230)

(this is the solution ensuring that
\lim_{z\to 0}C_{c+1}(z)=1
, i.e. there is exactly one walk of length zero). It is interesting to note that this generating function is closely related to the generating function for the Catalan numbers
[
44
,
45
]
.

Having now determined
C_{c+1}(z)
we can substitute this back into Eq. (
S227
) to determine
R_{r}(z)
,

R_{r}(z)=\frac{2c}{2c-r+r\sqrt{1-4zc}}.

(S231)

For the special case of the
n
-regular tree (i.e. the tree where all nodes have the same degree
n
) we simply set
n=r=c+1
in Eq. (
S231
). This gives the generating function for self-returning walks on an infinite
n
-regular tree, which we define as
T(z)
in Eq. (
S222
),

T(z)=\frac{n-1}{n-2+n\sqrt{1-4(n-1)z}}.

(S232)

Then with
z
as in Eq. (
S223
) we have a closed-form expression for the summation in Eq. (
S222
):

T(z)=\frac{2(n-1)}{n-2+n\sqrt{1-4(n-1)\left(\frac{1}{n(1+\tau)}\right)^{2}}}.

(S233)

With the replacement
n\to\mu
, Eqs. (
S187
) and (
S233
) combine to give

\displaystyle\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\rm st}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\mu-2-\mu\tau+\sqrt{(\tau+1)^{2}\mu^{2}-4(\mu-1)}}{2(\mu-1)}\right]\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\rm st}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}H_{\text{BL}}(\tau)\left(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\rm st}\right),

(S234)

(where ‘BL’ stands for ‘Bethe lattice’). We note that this is the same result that we derived in Sec.
S3
using the pair approximation for infinite uncorrelated networks. In the current section we did not have to make any approximations about spin correlations as we did for the pair approximation. The only assumption that we made was that the dynamics was being taking place on a particular topology, hence this result is exact for Bethe lattices. This is not surprising as the pair approximation is known to be exact on Bethe lattices
[
46
, p; 346]
as there are no loops.

S6.5.7
Calculation of
\Omega^{(\ell)}
and validity of the method

For networks that are not homogeneous in the above sense, we can still apply the approach based on network walks [Eq. (
S188
)] as an approximation. We replace
\Omega^{(\ell)}
with its average over all nodes,

\Omega^{(\ell)}\rightarrow\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\Omega^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf{x}}\equiv\overline{\Omega^{(\ell)}}.

(S235)

It is difficult to find this object in closed form. However, for many networks we find that
\overline{\Omega^{(\ell)}}\rightarrow\frac{1}{N}
for large
\ell
(where
N
is the size of the graph). Thus we compute
\overline{\Omega^{(\ell)}}
directly from the adjacency matrix for
\ell
below some cutoff
L
, and use
\overline{\Omega^{(\ell)}}\approx\frac{1}{N}
for
\ell>L
. This approximation should be tested for each network on an individual basis, from which an appropriate choice for
L
should be made. Based on this then we can write, using the geometric series,

\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\overline{\Omega^{(\ell)}}x^{\ell}\approx\frac{1}{N}\frac{x^{L+1}}{1-x}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\overline{\Omega^{\ell}}x^{\ell}.

(S236)

In Fig.
S8
we show the convergence of
\overline{\Omega^{(\ell)}}
to
\frac{1}{N}
for Barabási–Albert networks of differen size. These networks are not homogeneous. In this case it would be suitable to take
L\approx 30
.

S7
Stochastic pair approximation

The stochastic pair approximation (SPA) is an extension of the regular pair approximation discussed in Sec.
S3
. The SPA targets large but finite uncorrelated networks. This was originally proposed in
[
17
]
(pp 7-16 in particular), which studied the noisy voter model. Our model is more general, and reduces to the noisy voter model in the limit of no copying errors in the horizontal process,
h_{+}^{\prime}=h_{-}^{\prime}=0
, and when the vertical process is symmetric,
v_{+}=v_{-}
. We here construct the SPA for the more general setup of our model. More precisely we develop what is called ‘S2PA’ in
[
17
]
but here we refer to it as simply ‘SPA’.

Unlike in the main paper and in the remaining sections of this supplement we do not use
-1
and
+1
for the node states. Instead we denote these states by
0
and
1
. This is to keep the notation as close to that of
[
17
]
.

S7.1
Master equation for reduced set of degrees of freedom

To begin we consider the reduced set of variables
\left\{\mathbf{N}_{1},\mathcal{L}\right\}
where
\mathbf{N}_{1}
is the vector

\mathbf{N}_{1}=\left(\mathbf{N}_{1,k_{\text{min}}},...,\mathbf{N}_{1,k_{\text{max}}}\right),

(S237)

whose components
N_{1,k}
are the number of spin-up nodes with degree
k
. The notation
N_{0,k}
will be used to represent the number of spin-down nodes with degree
k
, and thus the total number of nodes of degree
k
is
N_{k}=N_{0,k}+N_{1,k}
.
\mathcal{L}
is the number of active links. We also define
n_{i}\in[0,k_{i}]
as the number of neighbours of node
i
in the up-state.

When a node
i
flips from the down to the up-state, we have the process
N_{1,k_{i}}\rightarrow N_{1,k_{i}}+1
. The number of active links connected to
i
before flipping is
n_{i}
, after the flip it will be
k_{i}-n_{i}
, thus a change of
k_{i}-2n_{i}
in the number of active links connected to
i
. Analogously, when a node flips from up to down, we have
N_{1,k_{i}}\rightarrow N_{1,k_{i}}-1
and the total number of active links changes as
\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}-k_{i}+2n_{i}
.

We then define the probability distribution of
\left\{\mathbf{N}_{1},\mathcal{L}\right\}
at time
t
as
P(\mathbf{N}_{1},\mathcal{L};t)
and we can construct the following master equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P(\mathbf{N}_{1},\mathcal{L};t)=\sum_{k}\sum_{n=0}^{k}\Big\{

\displaystyle\big(E_{N_{1,k}}^{+}E_{\mathcal{L}}^{k-2n}-1\big)\big[W^{(-,k,n)}P(\mathbf{N}_{1},\mathcal{L};t)\big]

\displaystyle\qquad+\big(E_{N_{1,k}}^{-}E_{\mathcal{L}}^{-k+2n}-1\big)\big[W^{(+,k,n)}P(\mathbf{N}_{1},\mathcal{L};t)\big]\Big\},

(S238)

where
W^{(+,k,n)}
and
W^{(-,k,n)}
are the effective rates of the two processes described above. The
E
’s are step operators which act on functions of
N_{1,k}
or
{\cal L}
as follows

\displaystyle E_{N_{1,k}}^{+}f(N_{1,k})

\displaystyle\rightarrow f(N_{1,k}+1),

(S239a)

\displaystyle E_{\mathcal{L}}^{k-2n}f(\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle\rightarrow f(\mathcal{L}+k-2n),

(S239b)

\displaystyle E_{N_{1,k}}^{-}f(N_{1,k})

\displaystyle\rightarrow f(N_{1,k}+1),

(S239c)

\displaystyle E_{\mathcal{L}}^{-k+2n}f(\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle\rightarrow f(\mathcal{L}-k+2n).

(S239d)

Next we define the degree specific magnetisation
m_{k}
, i.e. the magnetisation among the nodes of degree
k
. The interface density is denoted by
\sigma
. These two quantities can be written in terms of the variables
\left\{\mathbf{N}_{1},\mathcal{L}\right\}
as follows:

\displaystyle m_{k}

\displaystyle=2\frac{N_{1,k}}{N_{k}}-1,

(S240a)

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{L}}{\frac{1}{2}\mu N},

(S240b)

where
\mu
is the mean degree of the network, and
N
is the total number of nodes, thus
\frac{1}{2}\mu N
gives the total number of links.

S7.2
Dynamics of first and second moments

Eq. (
S238
) can be used to derive differential equations for the average degree specific magnetisation,
\left\langle{m_{k}(t)}\right\rangle
, the average density of active links,
\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle
, and correlations such as
\left\langle{m_{k}(t)m_{k^{\prime}}(t)}\right\rangle
. Angle brackets, as always, represent averages over independent realisations of the dynamics i.e.

\left\langle{F[\mathbf{s}(t)]}\right\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{s}}F[\mathbf{s}(t)]P(\mathbf{s};t),

(S241)

where the sum is over all
2^{N}
spin configurations
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{N})
, and where
P(\mathbf{s};t)
is the probability of finding the spins in the configuration
\mathbf{s}
at time
t
. Specifically one finds (after some algebra),

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{m_{k}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=\left\langle{F_{k}}\right\rangle,

(S242a)

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=\left\langle{F_{\sigma}}\right\rangle,

(S242b)

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{m_{k}m_{k^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=\left\langle{m_{k^{\prime}}F_{k}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{m_{k}F_{k^{\prime}}}\right\rangle+\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}\frac{\left\langle{G_{k}}\right\rangle}{N},

(S242c)

where

\displaystyle F_{k}=\frac{2}{N_{k}}\sum_{n=0}^{k}\left[W^{(+,k,n)}-W^{(-,k,n)}\right],

(S243a)

\displaystyle F_{\sigma}=\frac{2}{\mu N}\sum_{k}\sum_{n=0}^{k}(k-2n)\left[W^{(+,k,n)}-W^{(-,k,n)}\right],

(S243b)

\displaystyle G_{k}=\frac{4}{P_{k}N_{k}}\sum_{n=0}^{k}\left[W^{(+,k,n)}+W^{(-,k,n)}\right],

(S243c)

and where we have written
P_{k}=\frac{N_{k}}{N}
for the degree distribution of the network.

We now proceed to calculate the effective rates.
W^{(+,k,n)}
is the product of the number of degree
k
nodes in the down-state, the probability of a node in the down-state with degree
k
having
n
neighbours in the up-state,
P_{0}(k,n)
, and the rate at which such a down-state node flips to an up-state node,
R^{+}_{k,n}
. The latter is determined by the dynamics of the model. Therefore,

W^{(+,k,n)}=(N_{k}-N_{1,k})P_{0}(k,n)R^{+}_{k,n}.

(S244)

In a similar manner,

W^{(-,k,n)}=N_{1,k}P_{1}(k,n)R^{-}_{k,n},

(S245)

where
P_{1}(k,n)
is the probability of a node in the up-state with degree
k
having
n
neighbours in the up-state, and
R^{-}_{k,n}
is the rate at which such a node in the up-state flips to a down-state node.

The spin flip rates in our model are different from the ones in
[
17
]
. We have

\displaystyle R^{+}_{k,n}

\displaystyle=v_{+}^{\prime}+h_{+}\frac{n}{k}+h_{+}^{\prime}\frac{k-n}{k},

(S246a)

\displaystyle R^{-}_{k,n}

\displaystyle=v_{-}^{\prime}+h_{-}\frac{k-n}{k}+h_{-}^{\prime}\frac{n}{k}.

(S246b)

The probabilities
P_{0}(k,n)
and
P_{1}(k,n)
are intricate objects, determined by the distribution of the full spin configuration on the network. In order to make analytical progress we use the pair approximation and describe these probabilities in terms of
\left\{\mathbf{N}_{1},\mathcal{L}\right\}
. This is the same procedure as in Sec.
S3
, specifically Eq. (
S34
), where we assumed that the probability of a degree
k
node having
n
neighbours in the opposite state is binomial. Using this approximation we have

\displaystyle P_{0}(k,n)

\displaystyle=\binom{k}{n}c_{0}^{n}(1-c_{0})^{k-n},

(S247a)

\displaystyle P_{1}(k,n)

\displaystyle=\binom{k}{n}c_{1}^{k-n}(1-c_{1})^{n}.

(S247b)

In these expressions
c_{0/1}
is the probability that a given neighbour of a node in the down/up-state is in the up/down-state (i.e. in both cases, the probability that a link, connected to the focal node, is active). Formally,

c_{0/1}=\frac{\mathcal{L}}{\sum_{k}kN_{0/1,k}},

(S248)

which is the ratio between the total number of active links and the number of links connected emanating from nodes in the up/down-state respectively. Evaluating the above expression gives

c_{0/1}=\frac{\sigma}{1\mp m_{L}},

(S249)

where
m_{L}
is the degree averaged magnetisation defined by

m_{L}=\frac{1}{\mu}\sum_{k}kP_{k}m_{k}.

(S250)

With these ingredients we can now evaluate the objects on the right-hand sides of Eqs. (
S243a
)-(
S243c
) further. Due to the complexity this is done using Mathematica. An accompanying Mathematica notebook can be found at the following GitHub repository
[
47
]
.

The resulting expressions for the objects in Eqs. (
S243a
)-(
S243c
) are long, so we do not report them here in full. We include one expression as an example,

F_{k}=\alpha-\beta m_{k}+\frac{\sigma}{1-m_{L}^{2}}\Big[2\gamma(m_{L}-m_{k})+(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-})(1-m_{k}m_{L})\Big],

(S251)

where we have used the shorthands from Eq. (
S4
).

As before we now make the restriction from Eq. (
S5
),
\gamma_{+}=\gamma_{-}
, which helps maintain analytic tractability. The different quantities in Eqs. (
S243a
)-(
S243c
) can be expressed in terms of
m_{k}
,
m_{L}
,
m
,
\mu
,
\mu_{2}
, and the shorthand parameter combinations defined in Eq. (
S4
). The quantity
m
is the global magnetisation used throughout this paper, and can be calculated from the degree specific magnetisation
m_{k}
,

m=\sum_{k}P_{k}m_{k}.

(S252)

We use the notation

\mu_{n}=\sum_{k}P_{k}\mu^{n}

(S253)

for the
n
th
moment of the degree distribution.

We also point out a typo in the second moment of
P_{1}(k,n)
given on p. 9 of
[
17
]
. This should in fact be
\sum_{n=0}^{k}n^{2}P_{1}(k,n)=k\left[k-(2k-1)c_{1}+(k-1)c_{1}^{2}\right]
.

We find that Eqs. (
S242a
)-(
S242c
) are not closed. However we can use Eq. (
S242a
) to obtain a differential equation for
\left\langle{m_{L}}\right\rangle
. To do this, we evaluate
\frac{1}{\mu}\sum_{k}kP_{k}\left\langle{F_{k}}\right\rangle
, and find

\frac{\differential\left\langle{m_{L}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=\alpha-\beta\left\langle{m_{L}}\right\rangle.

(S254)

As a consequence we have

\left\langle{m_{L}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{m_{L}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}-\big[\left\langle{m_{L}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}-\left\langle{m_{L}(0)}\right\rangle\big]e^{-\beta t},

(S255)

and the steady-state value of
\left\langle{m_{L}}\right\rangle
is

\left\langle{m_{L}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}=\frac{(h_{+}^{\prime}-h_{-}^{\prime})+(v_{+}^{\prime}-v_{-}^{\prime})}{(h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime})+(v_{+}^{\prime}+v_{-}^{\prime})}.

(S256)

This is the same solution that we have found in all other cases, for instance see Sec.
S3.2
where we analysed the standard pair approximation. This is not surprising, as all we have done in this section is perform the pair approximation again but in a style better suited to the extension we will make in Sec.
S7.3
. In fact, we have already shown in Sec.
S6.3
that Eq. (
S256
) is a very general solution, a fact we will use in the next section.

S7.3
System-size expansion

We now use what is called ‘S2PA’ (stochastic pair approximation 2) in
[
17
]
to address Eqs. (
S242a
)-(
S242c
). This method focuses on expanding around the deterministic solution for the variable
m_{L}
.

Motivated by the fact that
\left\langle{m_{L}}\right\rangle
is a self governed variable, as we saw from Eq. (
S254
), and that the steady-state interface density under the standard pair approximation is as in Eq. (
S43
), we purpose the following expansion:

\displaystyle m_{k}

\displaystyle=m_{L}+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\nu_{k}}{N}+...,

(S257a)

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}H_{\text{PA}}(\tau)(1-m_{L}^{2})+\frac{\lambda_{\sigma}}{\sqrt{N}}+...,

(S257b)

where
\lambda_{k}
,
\nu_{k}
and
\lambda_{\sigma}
are stochastic variables. All objects in these equations, excluding
H_{\rm PA}(\tau)
which is given in Eq. (
S45
), are understood to be time-dependent. Ultimately however, we are only interested in the stationary state values. We neglect higher-order terms in the expansion in powers of
N^{-1/2}
. The goal is to calculate
\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
to leading and sub-leading order. Any expansions we perform in the remainder of this section can be found in a Mathematica notebook at the following GitHub repository
[
47
]
.

This expansion is further justified by the fact that individual simulated trajectories in
(m,\sigma)
-space, performed on finite size uncorrelated networks, fluctuate around a parabola with height
H_{\rm PA}(\tau)
. This is demonstrated in Fig.
2
in the main paper. Simulated trajectories on a truly infinite uncorrelated network would move exactly along this parabola, in fact they would follow the path described by Eqs. (
S25
) and (
S42
) which is also shown in Fig.
2
. Time averaging such analytical trajectories on infinite networks in the steady-state (equivalently we can average over many such independent trajectories at a single time in the steady state) will result in the point given by Eqs. (
S256
) and (
S43
). However, stochasticity means that the steady-state averages in finite networks sit on a different parabola. This is what we wish to determine and what motivates the system-size expansion in Eqs. (
S257a
) and (
S257b
).

To continue, we note that based on the results from Sec.
S6.3
, that any normalised weighted magnetisation in the steady-state is equal to the steady-state global magnetisation, Eq. (
S164
). The degree-specific average magnetisation
\left\langle{m_{k}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
is effectively a weighted magnetisation with weights
\delta_{k,k_{i}}
, i.e. we have

\left\langle{m_{k}}\right\rangle=\frac{\sum_{i}\delta_{k,k_{i}}S_{i}}{\sum_{i}\delta_{k,k_{i}}},

(S258)

where
S_{i}
is the average spin state at site
i
. Thus we have
\left\langle{m_{k}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
. Given the definition of
m_{L}
in Eq. (
S250
) this then also means that
\left\langle{m_{k}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\left\langle{m_{L}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
for all
k
. With this we see immediately from Eq. (
S257a
) that
\left\langle{\lambda_{k}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=0
and
\left\langle{\nu_{k}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=0
.

Next we substitute Eqs. (
S257a
) and (
S257b
) into Eqs. (
S242b
) and (
S242c
), and equate powers of
N^{-1/2}
. Many model parameters that appear, i.e.
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
etc. from Eq. (
S4
), can be expressed in terms of the noise-to-order ratio
\tau=\frac{\beta}{\gamma}
from Eq. (
S46
) and the steady-state global magnetisation
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}
from Eq. (
S24
). We find

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{\lambda_{\sigma}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=-2\left\{\tau-1+2H_{\rm PA}(\tau)+\frac{2}{\mu}\big[1-H_{\rm PA}(\tau)\big]\right\}\left\langle{\lambda_{\sigma}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{2\gamma}{\mu}H_{\rm PA}(\tau)\big[1-H_{\rm PA}(\tau)\big]\sum_{k}P_{k}\left\langle{\lambda_{k}m_{L}}\right\rangle

(S259)

and

\frac{\differential\left\langle{\lambda_{k}m_{L}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=-\gamma\big[H_{\text{PA}}(\tau)+2\tau\big]\left\langle{\lambda_{k}m_{L}}\right\rangle+\gamma\tau\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\left\langle{\lambda_{k}}\right\rangle

(S260)

respectively. We note that to get Eq. (
S260
) we set
k=k^{\prime}
in Eq. (
S242c
).

Taking both of these equations to the steady-state, and given that we already know that
\left\langle{\lambda_{k}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=0
, we also find that
\left\langle{\lambda_{\sigma}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=0
. With this
\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
reduces to

\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\frac{1}{2}H_{\rm PA}(\tau)\Big(1-\left\langle{m_{L}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\Big),

(S261)

and we are left with determining
\left\langle{m_{L}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
. Comparing with the result from the conventional pair approximation in Eq. (
S43
), we note that the final result contains the object
\left\langle{m_{L}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
instead of
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}^{2}
. In particular the square is now inside the average.

To calculate
\left\langle{m_{L}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
, we start from Eq. (
S242c
),

\frac{\differential\left\langle{m_{k}m_{k^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}=\left\langle{m_{k^{\prime}}F_{k}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{m_{k}F_{k^{\prime}}}\right\rangle+\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}\frac{\left\langle{G_{k}}\right\rangle}{N}.

(S262)

We expand this equation in powers of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
using Eqs. (
S257a
). The LHS of Eq. (
S262
) then becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{m_{k}m_{k^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\differential\left\langle{m_{L}^{2}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\frac{\differential\left\langle{\lambda_{k}m_{L}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}+\frac{\differential\left\langle{\lambda_{k^{\prime}}m_{L}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{\differential\left\langle{\lambda_{k}\lambda_{k^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}+\frac{\left\langle{\nu_{k}m_{L}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}+\frac{\left\langle{\nu_{k^{\prime}}m_{L}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}\right)+{\cal O}(N^{-3/2}).

(S263)

Next we consider the term
\left\langle{m_{k^{\prime}}F_{k}}\right\rangle
on the RHS of Eq. (
S262
). This expands as
[
47
]

\displaystyle\left\langle{m_{k^{\prime}}F_{k}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\gamma\tau\left[\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\left\langle{m_{L}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{m_{L}^{2}}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{N}}\Big[-\Big(H_{\rm PA}(\tau)+\tau\Big)\left\langle{\lambda_{k}m_{L}}\right\rangle-\tau\left\langle{\lambda_{k^{\prime}}m_{L}}\right\rangle+\tau\left\langle{\lambda_{k^{\prime}}}\right\rangle\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\gamma}{N}\Bigg[-\Big(H_{\rm PA}(\tau)+\tau\Big)\left\langle{\lambda_{k}\lambda_{k^{\prime}}}\right\rangle+\tau\left\langle{\nu_{k}}\right\rangle\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}-2\left\langle{\frac{m_{L}\lambda_{\sigma}\lambda_{k^{\prime}}}{1-m_{L}^{2}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\tau\left\langle{\nu_{k}m_{L}}\right\rangle-\Big(H_{\rm PA}(\tau)+\tau\Big)\left\langle{m_{L}\nu_{k^{\prime}}}\right\rangle\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad+{\cal O}(N^{-3/2}).

(S264)

The
\left\langle{m_{k}F_{k^{\prime}}}\right\rangle
term gives a similar expression, but with
k
and
k^{\prime}
interchanged.

Since
\left\langle{G_{k}}\right\rangle
carries a pre-factor
1/N
in Eq. (
S262
) we only consider the leading order term of
\left\langle{G_{k}}\right\rangle
. We have
[
47
]

\left\langle{G_{k}}\right\rangle=\frac{2\gamma}{P_{k}}\Big[H_{\rm PA}(\tau)+\tau-H_{\rm PA}(\tau)\left\langle{m_{L}^{2}}\right\rangle-\tau\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\left\langle{m_{L}}\right\rangle\Big]+{\cal O}(N^{-1/2}).

(S265)

Collecting terms in the expansions of the left-hand and right-hand sides of Eq. (
S262
), and keeping only expressions up to and including order
1/N
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{m_{L}^{2}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\frac{\differential\left\langle{\lambda_{k}m_{L}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}+\frac{\differential\left\langle{\lambda_{k^{\prime}}m_{L}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}\right)+\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{\differential\left\langle{\lambda_{k}\lambda_{k^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}+\frac{\left\langle{\nu_{k}m_{L}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}+\frac{\left\langle{\nu_{k^{\prime}}m_{L}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}\right)=

\displaystyle\qquad 2\gamma\tau\left[\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\left\langle{m_{L}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{m_{L}^{2}}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{N}}\Bigg\{-\Big(H_{\rm PA}(\tau)+2\tau\Big)\Big[\left\langle{\lambda_{k}m_{L}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\lambda_{k^{\prime}}m_{L}}\right\rangle\Big]+\tau\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\Big[\left\langle{\lambda_{k}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\lambda_{k^{\prime}}}\right\rangle\Big]\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\gamma}{N}\Bigg\{-2\Big(H_{\rm PA}(\tau)+\tau\Big)\left\langle{\lambda_{k}\lambda_{k^{\prime}}}\right\rangle+\tau\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\Big[\left\langle{\nu_{k}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\nu_{k^{\prime}}}\right\rangle\Big]-2\left\langle{\frac{(\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{k^{\prime}})m_{L}\lambda_{\sigma}}{1-m_{L}^{2}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\Big(H_{\rm PA}(\tau)+2\tau\Big)\Big[\left\langle{\nu_{k}m_{L}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\nu_{k^{\prime}}m_{L}}\right\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{2}{P_{k}}\Big[H_{\rm PA}(\tau)+\tau-H_{\rm PA}(\tau)\left\langle{m_{L}^{2}}\right\rangle-\tau\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\left\langle{m_{L}}\right\rangle\Big]\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}\Bigg\}.

(S266)

Performing the operation
\sum_{kk^{\prime}}kk^{\prime}P_{k}P_{k^{\prime}}
on both sides, several of the contributions can be eliminated. To see this, we perform the sum
\sum_{k}kP_{k}
on the expansion of
m_{k}
from Eq. (
S257a
), and find

\sum_{k}kP_{k}m_{k}=\sum_{k}kP_{k}m_{L}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k}kP_{k}\lambda_{k}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}kP_{k}\nu_{k},

(S267)

resulting in

\mu m_{L}=\mu m_{L}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k}kP_{k}\lambda_{k}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}kP_{k}\nu_{k}.

(S268)

Equating powers of
N^{-1/2}
we therefore conclude

\displaystyle\sum_{k}kP_{k}\lambda_{k}

\displaystyle=0,

(S269a)

\displaystyle\sum_{k}kP_{k}\nu_{k}

\displaystyle=0.

(S269b)

We now use the identities in Eqs. (
S269a
) and (
S269b
) when performing the operation

\sum_{kk^{\prime}}kk^{\prime}P_{k}P_{k^{\prime}}
in Eq. (
S266
). This results in all stochastic terms vanishing and we are left with

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\left\langle{m_{L}^{2}}\right\rangle}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=-2\gamma\tau\Big[\left\langle{m_{L}^{2}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\left\langle{m_{L}}\right\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{2\gamma\mu_{2}}{N\mu^{2}}\Big[H_{\rm PA}(\tau)+\tau-H_{\rm PA}(\tau)\left\langle{m_{L}^{2}}\right\rangle-\tau\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\left\langle{m_{L}}\right\rangle\Big],

(S270)

where we note the introduction of
\mu_{2}
and
\mu^{2}
arising from terms of the form
\sum_{k}k^{2}P_{k}
and
(\sum_{k}kP_{k})^{2}
. It is possible to solve this equation analytically and obtain a full time dependent solution for
\left\langle{m_{L}^{2}(t)}\right\rangle
and thus
\left\langle{\sigma(t)}\right\rangle
using Eq. (
S255
).

However, we are mostly interested in the steady-state, so we move to that regime. Eq. (
S270
) then becomes

0=-\tau N\Big[\left\langle{m_{L}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\rm st}\Big]+\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu^{2}}\Big[H_{\rm PA}(\tau)+\tau-H_{\rm PA}(\tau)\left\langle{m_{L}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}-\tau\left\langle{m}\right\rangle^{2}_{\rm st}\Big]

(S271)

and we can solve for
\left\langle{m_{L}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
:

\left\langle{m^{2}_{L}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\frac{(H_{\rm PA}(\tau)+\tau)\mu_{2}-\tau(\mu_{2}-N\mu^{2})\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}^{2}}{N\mu^{2}\tau+H_{\rm PA}(\tau)\mu_{2}}.

(S272)

Finally, we substitute this back into Eq. (
S261
) and obtain

\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\frac{1}{2}H_{\text{SPA}}(\tau)\Big(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}^{2}\Big),

(S273)

where

H_{\text{SPA}}(\tau)=\frac{\tau H_{\text{PA}}(\tau)(N\mu^{2}-\mu_{2})}{N\mu^{2}\tau+H_{\text{PA}}(\tau)\mu_{2}},

(S274)

(the subscript ‘SPA’ of course stands for ‘stochastic pair approximation’). Expanding in
1/N
Eq. (
S274
) can be written as

H_{\text{SPA}}(\tau)=H_{\text{PA}}(\tau)\left[1-\frac{\mu_{2}}{N\mu^{2}}\frac{\tau+H_{\text{PA}}(\tau)}{\tau}\right]+{\cal O}(N^{-2}),

(S275)

making it transparent that the result from the stochastic pair approximation is a finite-size correction to the expression obtained from the conventional pair approximation.

Due to the terms involving
H_{\rm PA}(\tau)
this solution applies in the same regions as that of the conventional pair approximation, see Sec.
S3.3
for a detailed discussion of this, as well as Secs.
S8
and
S10.2
for discussions on the
\tau
limits in general.

Focusing on positive
\tau
, we substitute the solution for
H_{\rm PA}(\tau)
from Eq. (
S45
) into Eq. (
S274
) and then expand in powers of
\tau
. To lowest order we find

H_{\rm SPA}(\tau)=\left(\frac{N\mu^{2}}{\mu_{2}}-1\right)\tau+\order{\tau^2}.

(S276)

Thus it is clear that in the limit
\tau\to 0
,
H_{\rm SPA}(\tau)\to 0
. This matches what we see in simulations on finite size networks.

In the limit
\tau\to\infty
, Eq. (
S275
) becomes

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}H_{\rm SPA}(\tau)=1-\frac{\mu_{2}}{N\mu^{2}}.

(S277)

This is of similar form to Eq. (
S111
) when we studied the annealed approximation. We know the large
\tau
regime is dominated by random state changes so there are no correlations between neighbours, hence we expect
H(\tau)\to 1
, as this corresponds to random scattering. Eq. (
S277
) reproduces this, up to corrections in
1/N
.

In Fig.

we demonstrate how Eq. (
S274
) compares to simulation for different size degree-regular (left), Erdös-–Rényi (middle), and Barabási–Albert (right) networks. We find convincing agreement across all ranges of
\tau
. We note that the approximation becomes inaccurate for very small networks [see Fig.

(b) in the main paper].

S8.2
Noise-to-order ratio,
\tau

S8.2.1
Definition and interpretation

The noise-to-order ratio,
\tau
, is given by [see Eq. (
S46
)]

\tau=2\frac{h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime}+v_{+}^{\prime}+v_{-}^{\prime}}{(h_{+}+h_{-})-(h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime})}.

(S280)

Interpretation of the numerator

To interpret the numerator we imagine a fully ordered spin configuration, that is, all spins up or all spins down. For example, if all spins are up, then the only possible events are spontaneous down-flips or down-flips due to incorrect copying. As seen from Eqs. (
S2
) and (
S3
) the total event rate for the former is
v_{-}^{\prime}N
and for the latter
h_{-}^{\prime}N
. So the total event rate, starting from the all-up-state is
(v_{-}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime})N
. Similarly, the total event rate starting from the all down configuration is
(v_{+}^{\prime}+h_{+}^{\prime})N
. Up to the factor of
N
, we can thus think of the numerator in Eq. (
S280
) as the average event rate in a fully ordered state. Any event in such a state moves the system towards disorder.

Interpretation of the denominator

We now show that the denominator describes the initial rate of the build-up of nearest-neighbour correlations if the system is started from a fully uncorrelated state with
m=0
. In other words, we assume that, in the initial state, each spin is up or down with equal probability, and independently of its neighbours.
Then we calculate
\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\left[\left\langle{ss^{\prime}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{s}\right\rangle\left\langle{s^{\prime}}\right\rangle\right]
.

If a give node is the state
+1
and if
n
of its
k
nearest neighbours are in state
-1
, then the rate at which it flips to
-1
is

\displaystyle T_{n,k,t}^{-}

\displaystyle=h_{-}\frac{n}{k}+h_{-}^{\prime}\frac{k-n}{k}+v_{-}^{\prime}.

(S281)

Spontaneous down flips in one node (described by
v_{-}^{\prime}
) cannot affect correlations between neighbours. In fact, in the fully random state with
m=0
such a spontaneous flip will, on average, convert as many active interfaces into inactive interfaces as the other way round. Therefore, we do not consider this term any further for the purposes of calculating the build-up of correlations.

The first two terms in Eq. (
S281
) describe the following: A random neighbour of the node is chosen. If that neighbour is in the down-state (this happens with probability
1/2
in the fully uncorrelated state) the focal node flips with rate
h_{-}
. The pair of focal node and neighbour change from
+-
to
--
. The change in
ss^{\prime}
is thus
+2
. If instead the neighbour is in the up-state (which also happens with probability
1/2
), the focal node flips with rate
h_{-}^{\prime}
. The pair of focal node and neighbour change from
++
to
-+
, and the change in
ss^{\prime}
is
-2
.

We note that interfaces of the focal node with any other nearest neighbours (other than the designated interaction partner) are equally likely to change from active to inactive or from inactive to active, given that we are starting from the fully uncorrelated state. These interfaces therefore do not contribute to any build-up of correlations.

The total expected rate of change of
\left\langle{ss^{\prime}}\right\rangle
due to events described by Eq. (
S281
) is thus
\frac{1}{2}h_{-}\cdot(+2)+\frac{1}{2}h_{-}^{\prime}\cdot(-2)=h_{-}-h_{-}^{\prime}
.

Similarly, if the focal node is in state
-1

and
n
is the number of neighbours in the up-state, then the rate at which it flips to state
+1
is

\displaystyle T_{n,k,t}^{+}

\displaystyle=h_{+}\frac{n}{k}+h_{+}^{\prime}\frac{k-n}{k}+v_{+}^{\prime}.

(S282)

We can make an analogous argument as above to show that the total expected rate of change of
\left\langle{ss^{\prime}}\right\rangle
due to events described by Eq. (
S282
) is thus
h_{+}-h_{+}^{\prime}
.

Finally, we note that

\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\big(\left\langle{s}\right\rangle\left\langle{s^{\prime}}\right\rangle\big)=\left\langle{s}\right\rangle\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\left\langle{s^{\prime}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{s^{\prime}}\right\rangle\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\left\langle{s}\right\rangle=0

(S283)

in the initial state in which
m=0
, i.e.
\left\langle{s}\right\rangle=0
.

Putting everything together we therefore find that

\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\left\langle{ss^{\prime}}\right\rangle\propto h_{-}-h_{-}^{\prime}+h_{+}-h_{+}^{\prime}.

(S284)

Thus, we have shown that the denominator of the expression in Eq. (
S280
) describes (up to constant pre-factors) the initial rate of the build-up of nearest neighbour correlations if the dynamics is started from a fully disordered state in which each spin is up or down with probability
1/2
, and independently of its neighbours.

S8.2.2
Range of possible values for
\tau

The definition in Eq. (
S280
) is of the form

\tau=2\frac{a+b}{c-a},

(S285)

with
a,b,c,d\geq 0
.

It is clear from this definition that all values
\tau\geq 0
can be realised through a suitable choice of
a,b,c,d
.

The quantity
\tau
takes negative values, if
a>c
. In this case we have

-\tau^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{a-c}{a+b},

(S286)

and
0<a-c\leq a\leq a+b
. Thus -
\tau^{-1}\leq 1/2
, and hence
\tau\leq-2
.

We conclude that
\tau
can only take values in the ranges
\tau\geq 0
or
\tau\leq-2
.

The limit

The limit
\tau\to-\infty
emerges in two different ways:

(i) One or both of

h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime}>h_{+}+h_{-}
; or

(ii) The denominator of Eq. (
S280
) approaches zero from below.

Typical configurations of the system will be mostly random, but there will be some small anti-correlation between nodes so the number of active links is slightly greater than random. We find
H(\tau)\to 1^{-}
in this case. This case is illustrated by the configuration in Fig.

.

S8.3.2
Finite negative values of
\tau<-2

The copying process is mostly unfaithful, but there can also be faithful copying and/or spontaneous spin flips. There will be some anti-correlation between neighbouring spins, and thus, for a given trait frequency
m
, one expects more active interfaces than a configuration in which these spins are placed randomly on the network. Hence, we expect values of
H(\tau)>1
.

A case in which
\tau
is negative and finite is illustrated by the configuration in Fig.

(b), for which
\mathbb{H}=\sigma/[\frac{1}{2}(1-m^{2})]=1.27
.

S8.3.3
The limit
\tau\to-2^{-}

From the definition of
\tau
in Eq. (
S280
) we have

\tau=-2\frac{h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime}+v_{+}^{\prime}+v_{-}^{\prime}}{(h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime})-(h_{+}+h_{-})}.

(S287)

Hence
\tau=-2
when either
h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime}
diverges and the
v_{\pm}^{\prime},h_{\pm}
remain finite, or if the
v^{\prime}_{\pm}
and
h_{\pm}
all vanish. In either case, the dynamics is dominated by unfaithful copying from neighbours, i.e. the model is type of ‘contrarian voter model’, in which a spin copies the state opposite to that of a neighbour. This creates strong anti-correlations between the states of neighbouring spins, and
H(\tau)
will take its maximum value which is specific to the network.

This scenario is illustrated in Fig.
S15
(c), where we show a perfect ‘checkerboard’ pattern seen in simulations on a small square grid. For this pattern we have
\mathbb{H}=\sigma/[\frac{1}{2}(1-m^{2})]=2
.

S8.3.4
The limit
\tau\to 0^{+}

The limit
\tau\to 0^{+}
can occur in one of two ways:

(i) The numerator in Eq. (
S280
) goes to zero (from above) and, at the same time,
h_{+}+h_{-}>h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime}
, so that denominator is positive; or

(ii) One has
h_{+}+h_{-}\to\infty
.

In the former case we have
h_{\pm}^{\prime}\to 0
and
v_{\pm}^{\prime}\to 0
. There is no transmission noise. In the latter case faithful copying from neighbouring spins dominates the dynamics.

In either case the number of active links is low and we expect configurations in which either all spins are up or all spins are down, as illustrated in Fig.
S15
(d). Due to the convergence to a fully ordered state, the ratio
\mathbb{H}=\sigma/[\frac{1}{2}(1-m^{2})]
cannot be defined for single realisations. However all realisations have
\sigma=0
eventually, and we measure
H(\tau)=\left\langle{\sigma}\right\rangle_{\rm st}/[\frac{1}{2}(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}^{2})]=0
.

S8.3.5
Finite
\tau>0

For
\tau>0
and finite, there are some unfaithful events occurring, and the rate of faithful horizontal events is greater than the total rate of unfaithful horizontal events, i.e.
h_{+}+h_{-}>h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime}
. This leads to steady-state situations where
0<H(\tau)<1
. The case is illustrated in Fig.
S15
(e), where we measure
\mathbb{H}=\sigma/[\frac{1}{2}(1-m)^{2}]=0.33
.

S8.3.6
The limit
\tau\to\infty

This limit emerges in two different ways:

(i) The denominator in Eq. (
S280
) remains positive, and either
v_{+}^{\prime}
or
v_{-}^{\prime}
(or both) tend to infinity; or

(ii) The
v_{\pm}^{\prime}
remain finite, but the denominator in Eq. (
S280
) diverges, i.e.,
h_{+}+h_{-}=h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime}
.

In the former case, the system is dominated by random spin flips. In the second scenario, correct and incorrect copying from neighbouring nodes occur at the same rates. This means that the copying spin takes a state that is effectively unrelated to that of the state it is trying to copy.

In neither of these two situations correlations between neighbouring states can build up, and so the resulting configurations are effectively random. In general
H(\tau)\to 1^{-}
. This case is illustrated in Fig.
S15
(f), where we find
\mathbb{H}=\sigma/[\frac{1}{2}(1-m^{2})]=0.98
.

S9
Analysis of linguistic data

S9.1
Dataset

In this section we provide further information on the analysis of data from WALS (The World Atlas of Language Structures)
[
22
]
in the main paper. WALS contains information on
2,662
human languages, most importantly the co-ordinates of where that language is most densely spoken. We will refer to languages by their WALS-ID, which is a combination of numbers and letters such as ‘11A’, ‘143F’ etc.

Language possess a number of language traits (known as ‘typological features of language’). WALS details 192 such traits. Not all traits are recorded for all languages in WALS. We focus on a curated set of
35
language traits which can be quantified in a sufficient set of languages, and which are ‘binarizable’, that is, if the trait can be quantified in the language it can meaningfully be classified as present or absent. The set of traits and languages is that of
[
13
]
, where further details can be found. Ultimately, we consider
2,248
languages from WALS, these are those for which at least one trait is quantifiable in binary form. This means that not all nodes (languages) have a well defined binary state for all
35
traits.

Binary traits can all be naturally mapped to our theoretical set-up. Nodes represent languages, the spin states indicate whether that language posses or lacks a specific language trait, links between nodes indicate interaction.

The relevant data to perform this analysis including details about traits, distances between languages, and information on which languages contain which features can be found at the following GitHub repository
[
47
]
.

S9.2
Empirical noise-to-order ratio

To determine the density of active interfaces for each of the
35
features from the WALS data we need to decide how the languages are connected. We do not know the true interaction network. We therefore make the following choice: two languages
A
and
B
are connected if
B
is among the
10
geographically closest neighbours of
A
, or if
A
is among the
10
geographically closest neighbours of
B
. This creates an undirected network.

With this method, languages can end up having more than
10
neighbours in the network. For example, start by considering
A
. We want to determine which other languages it connects to. We find the
10
geographically nearest languages of
A
and connect it to those languages. Assume that
B
is one of these languages. We then consider
B
. It already has one connection to
A
. We then find its
10
geographically nearest languages. It is possible that
A
is not within this set. We connect
B
to these
10
languages.
B
now has
11
neighbours in the network.

The choice of
10
nearest neighbours is somewhat arbitrary, however, the results of
[
13
]
for example have been shown to be robust against variation in neighbourhood size.

The degree distribution for the above network is shown in Fig.
S18
. The network is not regular, neither is it uncorrelated as it is likely that two neighbours of a given node are connected to one another. However the degree distribution is fairly tight, so we can still apply the various analytical results to good effect.

Figure S18:
Degree distribution of our approximation to the real-world network of language interactions (see text).

To determine an empirical noise-to-order ratio for a particular binarisable trait, we assign states
\pm 1
to those nodes (languages), for which the binary state of the trait is defined. The state
\pm 1
indicates whether the language represented by the node posses or lacks the trait. If the trait is not quantifiable on a given node, then no spin state is assigned to the node. Taking into account only nodes with states
\pm 1
, we then determine the feature frequency
\rho
, and, using the putative interaction network, the density of active links,
\sigma
. From this we obtain
\mathbb{H}=\sigma/[2\rho(1-\rho)]
as a quantifier of the spatial scattering. Finally, the different analytical functions,
H_{\rm AA}(\tau)
[Eq. (
S110
)],
H_{\rm SL}(\tau)
[Eq. (
S125
)],
H_{\rm HG}(\tau)
[Eq. (
S188
)] and
H_{\rm SPA}(\tau)
[Eq. (
S274
)], can be used to infer values for the noise-to-order ratio,
\tau_{X}
. The
X
subscript stands for one of AA, SL, HG, or SPA depending on the specific
H_{X}(\tau)
function that was used.

Using this procedure it is sometimes possible to obtain
\mathbb{H}=\sigma/[2\rho(1-\rho)]>1
. As discussed in Sec.
S8
this would lead to negative values of
\tau
values. Such occurrences are rare in our dataset (less than
1\%
), so we discard these cases.

S9.3
Comparison against longitudinal stability estimate

To validate our results we compare to a phylogenetic method which does not take into account any spatial data. This was done in
[
49
]
using a principal component analysis, and in particular the first component PC1. Higher PC1 values corresponding to greater instability. PC1 values were only measured for a subset of the 35 traits that we consider.

In Fig.
S19
(left) we show regression plots of PC1 against
\tau_{X}
. The different rows correspond to different
X
and thus use different analytical forms
H_{X}(\tau)
, as described above and indicated in the figure.

A similar but restricted analysis was carried out in
[
13
]
. This was solely based on
H_{\rm SL}(\tau)
from the theory for infinite square lattices. That earlier analysis identified a small set of outlier traits for which the estimates of
\tau
and Dediu’s PC1 gave conflicting indications regarding relative stability. Some of these outliers could be justified based on known properties of some of the WALS features, see the discussion in
[
13
]
for details.

We have carried out a similar identification of outliers to test robustness against the use of different analytical forms
H_{X}(\tau)
. Outliers are determined by recursively pruning the dataset and removing the trait that would provide the largest increase in Pearson correlation coefficient
r
(measuring linear correlation
[
50
]
). Results are shown in the right-hand panels of Fig.
S19
. Independent of the analytical form used for the function
H_{X}(\tau)
we find the same set of outliers, namely WALS features 8A, 11A, 107A and 57A. This is the same set as found in
[
13
]
, confirming the robustness against variation of the assumed network structure [reflected by the choice of analytical form of
H_{X}(\tau)
].

After outlier removal we find strong correlation between the inferred values of
\tau_{X}
and Dediu’s PC1, with an
r
consistently above
0.8
, and
p
-value
<0.01
, for all theories
X
.

S9.4
Bootstrapping and rankograms

S9.4.1
Construction of rankograms

The method in Sec.
S9.2
can be used to rank the
35
traits in terms of noise-to-order ratio, which in turn can be regarded as a proxy for stability.

Our analysis is based on the WALS dataset, involving
N=2,248
languages, each with at least one binarisable feature among our selected set of
35
traits. In reality, there are other languages for which we do not have the binarisable data. This can be due to lack of observation, or WALS simply is missing the data. We can therefore think of the WALS dataset as an effective random sub-sample of all human languages. To quantify the uncertainty associated with this, we use bootstrap re-sampling
[
51
]
.

In our context, bootstrapping involves sampling
N=2,248
languages with replacement from the original WALS dataset. This gives rise to an (assumed) interaction network as described in Sec.
S9.2
. This network is specific to the bootstrap sample. We note that is possible to select the same language multiple times. This leads to multiple identical languages with geographical distance zero, thus they are essentially guaranteed to be connected. This represents potentially very close neighbouring languages, not included in WALS, which have the same trait values.

For each sample, we can perform the procedure in Sec.
S9.2
to calculate a noise-to-order ratio
\tau_{X}
for each trait (discarding nodes in the network for which the status of a given trait cannot be quantified in binary form). Repeating this many times, we generate distributions of
\tau_{X}
for each trait.

To produce rankograms for a chosen
X
we can then draw a value of
\tau_{X}
for each trait from the respective distributions. The traits can then be ranked based off of these values. This is then repeated many times and thus a distribution of ranks is constructed for each trait. These are the rankograms shown in Figs.
4
in the main paper, and in
S20
in this supplement. These are histograms where the
i^{\rm th}
bar indicates the probability that the trait represented by that rankogram ranks in the
i^{\rm th}
position amongst all traits.

Figure S20:
Rankograms for all 35 language traits for 10,000 bootstrap samples. We only show Gaussian kernel density estimates of the distributions for clarity. Each rankogram has five distributions. Four of which are based on inferring values of
\tau_{X}
from the theories
H_{\rm AA}(\tau)
[Eq. (
S110
)],
H_{\rm SL}(\tau)
[Eq. (
S125
)],
H_{\rm HG}(\tau)
[Eq. (
S188
)] and
H_{\rm SPA}(\tau)
[Eq. (

)]. The fifth is from ranking raw
\mathbb{H}
values calculated using Eq. (
2
). The height at a given rank is the probability a trait is ranked in that position amongst all traits.

We observe that the traits on the extreme ends, such as 130A or 53A, have very tight rankograms. This means that we can be confident that those traits will rank in those positions amongst all features. We are less confident about the traits with broad distributions, such as 100A or 48A.

S9.4.2
Distributions of
\tau_{X}

From the rankograms in Fig.
S20
we see that the rankings produced from
\mathbb{H}
and those produced from
\tau_{X}
using each of the theories
X
are very similar. This is a non-trivial result and is not a consequence of the fact that all the
H_{X}(\tau)
functions are monotonic increasing functions of
\tau
as explained below.

In a single bootstrap sample, we have a set of languages and construct one interaction network. We then measure 35 values of
\mathbb{H}
, one for each trait, using the procedure in Sec.
S9.2
. These
\mathbb{H}
values will have a certain ranking. With the single interaction network we construct the functions
H_{X}(\tau)
, one for each theory
X
. For a chosen
X
the 35
\mathbb{H}
values can be mapped to 35
\tau_{X}
values. Since all
H_{X}(\tau)
are monotonic increasing functions of
\tau
, ordering the traits based on
\tau_{X}
will be the same as ordering based on
\mathbb{H}
, for all theories
X
.

However, the procedure for constructing rankograms is different in that we do not perform any rankings until distributions of
\tau_{X}
have been constructed for each trait. To construct distributions of
\tau_{X}
for a trait we repeat the procedure in Sec.
S9.2
many times. Each bootstrap sample consists of a different set of languages, and thus a different interaction network. Focusing on two traits
A
and
B
, we measure the corresponding values
\mathbb{H}^{A}=\sigma^{A}/[2\rho^{A}(1-\rho^{A})]
and
\mathbb{H}^{B}=\sigma^{B}/[2\rho^{B}(1-\rho^{B})]
in each sample. This gives distributions for
\mathbb{H}^{A}
and
\mathbb{H}^{B}
. For each bootstrap sample we also map the empirical values of
\mathbb{H}^{A}
and
\mathbb{H}^{B}
to values
\tau^{A}_{X}
and
\tau^{B}_{X}
, using one of the analytical functions
H_{X}(\tau)
. This gives us distributions of
\tau^{A}_{X}
and
\tau^{B}_{X}
.

Since the interaction network is different in each bootstrap sample, the function
H_{X}(\tau)
will also change in each sample (for a fixed choice of
X
). This is because the functional form of
H_{X}(\tau)
makes use for example of the mean degree or other characteristics of the network. This means, that even if the distributions of
\mathbb{H}^{A}
and
\mathbb{H}^{B}
do not overlap, for a given
X
it is possible that the distributions of
\tau^{A}_{X}
and
\tau^{B}_{X}
do overlap. Fig.
S21
gives a visual representation of this idea.

Figure S21:
Pictorial demonstration of how non-overlapping
\mathbb{H}
distributions from two traits A and B can lead to
\tau_{X}
distributions which do overlap through the bootstrapping process (see text). The yellow, green and red curves are examples of
H_{X}(\tau)
from different bootstrap samples.

\text{SUCRA}_{a}=\frac{N-\mathbb{E}(r)_{a}}{N-1},

S10
Further mathematical background

S10.1
Exponential generating function for modified Bessel functions of the first kind

The exponential generating function for the modified Bessel function of the first kind is
[
53
, 49:6:1]

I_{0}(2x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2k}}{(k!)^{2}}=\frac{x^{0}}{(0!)^{2}}+\frac{x^{2}}{(1!)^{2}}+\frac{x^{4}}{(2!)^{2}}+....

(S289)

Squaring this equation provides us with the exponential generating function for
I_{0}(2x)^{2}
,

\displaystyle I_{0}(2x)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{(0!)^{2}}\frac{1}{(0!)^{2}}\right)x^{0}+\left(2\frac{1}{(0!)^{2}}\frac{1}{(1!)^{2}}\right)x^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(2\frac{1}{(0!)^{2}}\frac{1}{(2!)^{2}}+\frac{1}{(1!)^{2}}\frac{1}{(1!)^{2}}\right)x^{4}+...

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{a_{1}+a_{2}=k}\frac{x^{2k}}{(a_{1}!)^{2}(a_{2}!)^{2}}.

(S290)

To generalise, first define the combinatoric sum

\sum_{a_{1}+...+a_{d}=k}\equiv\sum_{A_{d}^{k}}.

(S291)

In general then we can form the exponential generating function for
I_{0}(2x)^{d}
,

\displaystyle I_{0}(2x)^{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{A_{d}^{k}}\frac{x^{2k}}{\prod_{i}^{d}(a_{i}!)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{A_{k}^{d}}\frac{(2k)!}{\prod_{i}^{d}(a_{i}!)^{2}}\frac{x^{2k}}{(2k)!}.

(S292)

Now recall the expression for
W_{d}^{(2k)}
from equation (
S196
),

W_{d}^{(2k)}=\sum_{A_{k}^{d}}\frac{(2k)!}{\prod_{i=1}^{d}(a_{i}!)^{2}},

(S293)

which gives us the final expression

I_{0}(2x)^{d}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}W_{d}^{(2k)}\frac{x^{2k}}{(2k)!}.

(S294)

S10.2
Determining standard model parameters for a given
\tau
and
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}

In this section we describe the algorithm we used to generate model parameters in the simulation. The challenge is to generate sets of non-negative
h_{\pm},h^{\prime}_{\pm},v^{\prime}_{\pm}
leading to different stationary trait frequencies
\left\langle{\rho}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
but at the same time keeping
\tau=2(h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime}+v_{+}^{\prime}+v_{-}^{\prime})/[(h_{+}+h_{-})-(h_{+}^{\prime}+h_{-}^{\prime})]
fixed. Fixing
\left\langle{\rho}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
is equivalent to fixing the stationary magnetisation
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=2\left\langle{\rho}\right\rangle_{\rm st}-1
.

We apply the restriction in Eq. (
S5
), which we write in the form

h_{+}=h_{+}^{\prime}-h_{-}^{\prime}+h_{-}.

(S295)

Equations (
S24
) and (
S46
) can be solved for
v_{+}^{\prime}
and
v_{-}^{\prime}
, assuming
\tau\neq 0

\displaystyle v_{+}^{\prime}=\frac{\tau}{2}(1+\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st})(h_{-}-h_{-}^{\prime})-h_{+}^{\prime},

(S296a)

\displaystyle v_{-}^{\prime}=\frac{\tau}{2}(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st})(h_{-}-h_{-}^{\prime})-h_{-}^{\prime}.

(S296b)

S10.2.1
The case
\tau>0

For
\tau>0
and
-1<\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}<1
, we set
h_{+}^{\prime}
and
h_{-}^{\prime}
to small random values and then choose a value of
h_{-}
large enough so that
v_{+}^{\prime}
,
v_{-}^{\prime}
and
h_{+}
are non-negative. This amounts to choosing
h_{-}
such that

h_{-}\geq\text{max}\left(0,h_{-}^{\prime}-h_{+}^{\prime},h_{-}^{\prime}+\frac{2}{\tau(1+\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st})}h_{+}^{\prime},\left[1+\frac{2}{\tau(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st})}\right]h_{-}^{\prime}\right).

(S297)

For the case
\tau>0
and
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=-1
, Eq. (
S296a
) means that we need to set
v_{+}^{\prime}=h_{+}^{\prime}=0
. Eq. (
S296b
) then gives
v_{-}^{\prime}=\tau h_{-}-(1+\tau)h_{-}^{\prime}
. So to ensure
v_{-}^{\prime}\geq 0
we draw random values of
h_{-}
and
h_{-}^{\prime}
such that
h_{-}\geq\left(1+\frac{1}{\tau}\right)h_{-}^{\prime}
. This allows us to calculate
v_{-}^{\prime}
. Finally
h_{+}
is determined from Eq. (
S295
).

For the case
\tau>0
and
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=+1
, Eq. (
S296b
) means that we should set
v_{-}^{\prime}=h_{-}^{\prime}=0
. Eq. (
S296a
) then gives
v_{+}^{\prime}=\tau h_{-}-h_{+}^{\prime}
. So to ensure
v_{-}^{\prime}\geq 0
we draw random values of
h_{-}
and
h_{+}^{\prime}
such that
h_{-}\geq\tau h_{+}^{\prime}
. This allows us to calculate
v_{+}^{\prime}
. The coefficient
h_{+}
is again determined from Eq. (
S295
).

S10.2.2
The case
\tau=0
(standard voter model)

For
\tau=0
Eqs. (
S296a
) and (
S296b
) dictate that we need to set the rates of all unfaithful events to zero (
h_{+}^{\prime}=h_{-}^{\prime}=v_{+}^{\prime}=v_{-}^{\prime}=0
). We then have
h_{+}=h_{-}
from Eq. (
S295
). The model therefore reduces to the standard voter model. The common value of the rates
h_{\pm}
can be drawn at random (the only significance of the value it to set a time scale). We note however that in this case the expression for
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
, Eq. (
S24
), is not defined. In the voter model average magnetisation is conserved in time, and every initial magnetisation is therefore a steady-state value (
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}=\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{0}
).

S10.2.3
Negative values of
\tau
(
\tau\leq-2
)

As discussed in Sec.
S8
the quantity
\tau
cannot take values in the interval
-2<\tau<0
, that is, the only negative values of
\tau
permitted by the model setup are those in the range
\tau\leq-2
. We can confirm this through numerical search for model parameters. More precisely, we conducted searches of parameter space, enforcing the constrain in Eq. (
S5
) and fixing
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
and
\tau
, in Eqs. (
S24
) and (
S46
) respectively. For
-2<\tau<0
the search returns no solutions (see the Mathematica notebook at the following GitHub repository
[
47
]
for a demonstration).

Focusing on
\tau\leq-2
, a simple method to generate model parameters is to set
h_{+}^{\prime}=h_{-}^{\prime}
and then draw this number at random. Consequently, from Eq. (
S295
) we have
h_{+}=h_{-}
. Then, to ensure
v_{+}^{\prime}\geq
and
v_{-}\geq 0
, Eqs. (
S296a
) and (
S296b
) give the following bounds on
h_{-}
:

0\leq h_{-}\leq\text{min}\left(1+\frac{2}{\tau(1+\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st})},1+\frac{2}{\tau(1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st})}\right)h_{-}^{\prime}.

(S298)

To ensure the upper bound here is at least zero we require

\tau\leq\text{min}\left(\frac{-2}{1+\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}},\frac{-2}{1-\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}}\right).

(S299)

This means that for a given
\tau\leq-2
,
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
must be within the following bounds.

-1-\frac{2}{\tau}\leq\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}\leq 1+\frac{2}{\tau}.

(S300)

A visual representation of these bounds is shown in Fig.
S23
.

The inequalities in Eq. (
S300
) bound the possible values of
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
that can be reached for a given
\tau
by model parameters
generated using our algorithm
. In particular we used the restriction
h_{+}=h_{-}
to derive these bounds. We can therefore not exclude that models with
h_{+}\neq h_{-}
can cover a wider range of
\left\langle{m}\right\rangle_{\rm st}
for a given
\tau
. However, numerical searches (allowing for
h_{+}\neq h_{-}
