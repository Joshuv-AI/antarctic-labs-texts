A randomized multi-index sequential Monte Carlo method


Abstract
We consider the problem of estimating expectations with respect to a target distribution with an unknown normalizing constant, and where even the unnormalized target needs to be approximated at finite resolution. Under such an assumption, this work builds upon a recently introduced multi-index Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) ratio estimator, which provably enjoys the complexity improvements of multi-index Monte Carlo (MIMC) and the efficiency of SMC for inference. The present work leverages a randomization strategy to remove bias entirely, which simplifies estimation substantially, particularly in the MIMC context, where the choice of index set is otherwise important. Under reasonable assumptions, the proposed method provably achieves the same canonical complexity of MSE -1 as the original method (where MSE is mean squared error), but without discretization bias. It is illustrated on examples of Bayesian inverse and spatial statistics problems. Keywords : Bayesian Inverse Problems, Sequential Monte Carlo, Multi-Index Monte Carlo

2.1
Elliptic partial differential equation

We consider a D-dimensional elliptic partial differential equation defined over an open domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
with locally continuous boundary
\partial\Omega
, i.e. the boundary is the graph of a continuous function in a neighbourhood of any point.
Given a forcing function
{\sf f}(x):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

and a diffusion coefficient function

a(x):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
depending on a random variable
x\sim\pi
,
the partial differential equation for
u(x):\bar{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
(where
\bar{\Omega}
is the closure of
\Omega
) is given by

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a(x)\nabla u(x))

\displaystyle={\sf f}(x),\ \ \text{on}\ \Omega,

(2)

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=0,\ \ \text{on}\ \partial\Omega\,.

The dependence of the solution
u
of (
2
) on
x
is raised from the dependence of
a
and
{\sf f}
on
x
.

In particular, we assume the prior distribution in the numerical experiment as

x\sim U[-1,1]^{d}=:\pi_{0}

(3)

and
a(x)
as

a(x)(z)=a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\psi_{i}(z),

(4)

where
\psi_{i}
are smooth functions with
\left\lVert\psi_{i}\right\rVert_{\infty}:=\sup_{z\in\Omega}|\psi(z)|\leq 1
for
i=1,...,d
, and
a_{0}>\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}
.

2.1.1
Finite element method

Consider 1D piecewise linear nodal basis functions
\phi_{j}^{K}
for meshes
\{z_{i}^{K}=i/(K+1)\}_{i=0}^{K+1}
,
j=1,2,...,K
, which is defined as

\phi_{j}^{K}(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{z-z_{j-1}^{K}}{z_{j}^{K}-z_{j-1}^{K}},&z\in[z_{j-1}^{K},z_{j}^{K}]\\
1-\frac{z-z_{j-1}^{K}}{z_{j+1}^{K}-z_{j}^{K}},&z\in[z_{j}^{K},z_{j+1}^{K}]\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(5)

For an index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
, we can form the tensor product grid over
\Omega=[0,1]^{2}
as

\{(z_{i_{1}}^{K_{1,\alpha}},z_{i_{2}}^{K_{2,\alpha}})\}^{K_{1,\alpha}+1,K_{2,\alpha}+1}_{i_{1}=0,i_{2}=0},

(6)

where
K_{1,\alpha}=2^{\alpha_{1}}
and
K_{2,\alpha}=2^{\alpha_{2}}
and the mesh size in each direction is
K_{1,\alpha}^{-1}
and
K_{2,\alpha}^{-1}
, respectively. Then the bilinear basis function is constructed by the product of nodal basis functions in two directions:

\phi_{i}^{\alpha}(z)=\phi_{i_{1},i_{2}}^{\alpha}(z_{1},z_{2})=\phi_{i_{1}}^{K_{1,\alpha}}(z_{1})\phi_{i_{2}}^{K_{2,\alpha}}(z_{2}),

(7)

where
i=i_{1}+K_{1,\alpha}i_{2}
for
i_{1}=1,...,K_{1,\alpha}
and
i_{2}=1,...,K_{2,\alpha}
and
K_{\alpha}=K_{1,\alpha}K_{2,\alpha}
.

A Galerkin approximation can be written as

u_{\alpha}(x)(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{K_{\alpha}}u_{\alpha}^{i}(x)(z)\ \phi_{i}^{\alpha}(z),

(8)

where
u_{\alpha}^{i}
for
i=1,...,K_{\alpha}
are approximate values of the solution
u(x)
at mesh points that we want to obtain. Using Galerkin approximation to solve the weak solution of PDE (
2
), we can derive a corresponding Galerkin system:

{\bf A}_{\alpha}(x){\bf u}_{\alpha}(x)={\bf f}_{\alpha}(x),

(9)

where
{\bf A}_{\alpha}(x)
is the stiffness matrix whose components are given by

\displaystyle[{\bf A}_{\alpha}(x)]_{ij}:=\int_{z_{j_{1}-1}^{K_{1,\alpha}}}^{z_{j_{1}+1}^{K_{1,\alpha}}}\int_{z_{j_{2}-1}^{K_{2,\alpha}}}^{z_{j_{2}+1}^{K_{2,\alpha}}}a(x)(z)\nabla\phi_{i}^{\alpha}(z)\cdot\nabla\phi_{j}^{\alpha}(z)dz,

(10)

where
j=j_{1}+j_{2}K_{1,\alpha}
for
j_{1}=1,...,K_{1,\alpha}
and
j_{2}=1,...,K_{2,\alpha}
,

{\bf u}_{\alpha}(x)=[u_{\alpha}^{1}(x)(z_{1}),...,u_{\alpha}^{K_{\alpha}}(x)(z_{K_{\alpha}})]^{T},

and

[{\bf f}_{\alpha}(x)]_{j}=\int_{z_{j_{1}-1}^{K_{1,\alpha}}}^{z_{j_{1}+1}^{K_{1,\alpha}}}\int_{z_{j_{2}-1}^{K_{2,\alpha}}}^{z_{j_{2}+1}^{K_{2,\alpha}}}{\sf f}(x)(z)\phi_{j}^{\alpha}(z)dz.

2.1.2
The Bayesian inverse problem

Under an elliptic partial differential equation model, we wish to infer the unknown parameter value
x\in\mathsf{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
given
n
evaluations of the solution
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
49
]
. We aim to analyse the posterior distribution
\mathbb{P}(x|y)
with density
\pi(x)=\pi(x|y)
. In practice, one can only expect to evaluate a discretized version of
x\in\mathsf{X}
.
\pi(dx)
then can be obtained up to a constant of proportionality by applying Bayes’ theorem:

\pi(dx)\propto L(x)\pi_{0}(dx),

(11)

where
\pi_{0}(dx)
is a density of the prior distribution and
L(x)
is the likelihood which is proportional to the probability density of the data
y
was created with a given value of the unknown parameter
x
.

Define the vector-valued function as follows

\mathcal{G}(u(x))=[v_{1}(u(x)),...,v_{n}(u(x))]^{T},

(12)

where
n
is the number of data,
v_{i}\in L^{2}
and
v_{i}(u(x))=\int v_{i}(z)u(x)(z)dz
for
i=1,...,n
. Then the data can be modelled as

y=\mathcal{G}(u(x))+\nu,\ \ \ \nu\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma),

(13)

where
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
denotes the Gaussian distribution with mean zero and variance-covariance matrix
\Sigma
. Then the likelihood of the evaluations
y
can be derived as

\pi(y|x)\propto L(x):=\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}|y-\mathcal{G}(u(x))|^{2}_{\Sigma}\Big),

(14)

where
|w|_{\Sigma}=(w^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}w)^{1/2}
.

When the solution of the elliptic PDE can only be solved approximately, we denote the approximate solution at resolution multi-index
\alpha
as
u_{\alpha}
as described above and the approximate likelihood is given by

\pi_{\alpha}(y|x)\propto L_{\alpha}(x):=\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}|y-\mathcal{G}(u_{\alpha}(x))|^{2}_{\Sigma}\Big),

(15)

and the posterior density is given by

\pi_{\alpha}(dx)\propto L_{\alpha}(x)\pi_{0}(dx).

(16)

2.2
Log Gaussian process models

Now, we consider the log Gaussian Cox model and the log Gaussian process model. A log Gaussian process (LGP)
\Lambda(z)
is given by

\Lambda(z)=\exp\{x(z)\}\,,

(17)

where
x=\{x(z):z\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}\}
is a real-valued Gaussian process
[
42
,
49
]
.
The log Gaussian process model provides a flexible approach to non-parametric density modelling with controllable smoothness properties. However, inference for the LGP is intractable.
The LGP model for density estimation

[
53
]
assumes data
z_{i}\sim p
, where
p(z)=\Lambda(z)/\int_{\Omega}\Lambda(z)dz
. As such, the likelihood of
x
associated to observations
\mathsf{Z}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\}
is given by

L(x;\mathsf{Z})=\prod_{z\in\mathsf{Z}}p(z)=\left(\int_{\Omega}\Lambda(z)dz\right)^{-n}\prod_{z\in\mathsf{Z}}\Lambda(z)\,.

(18)

The log Gaussian Cox (LGC) model assumes the observations are distributed according to a spatially inhomogeneous Poisson point process with intensity function given
by
\Lambda
.
The likelihood of observing
\mathsf{Z}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\}

under the LGC model is
[
37
,
39
,
34
,
5
]

\displaystyle L(x;\mathsf{Z})

\displaystyle=\exp\Big\{\int_{\Omega}(1-\Lambda(z))dz\Big\}\prod_{z\in\mathsf{Z}}\Lambda(z),

(19)

\displaystyle=\exp\Big\{\int_{\Omega}(1-\exp(x(z)))dz\Big\}\prod_{z\in\mathsf{Z}}\exp(x(z))\,.

This construction has an elegant simplicity, which is flexible and convenient due to the underlying Gaussian process.
Some example applications are presented in

[
13
]
.

We consider a dataset comprised of the location of
n=126
Scots pine saplings in a natural forest in Finland
[
37
]
, denoted
z_{1},...,z_{n}\in[0,1]^{2}
.
This is modeled with both LGC, following
[
22
]
,
and LGP, following
[
53
]
. The prior is defined in terms of a KL-expansion with a suitable parameter
\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3})
as follows, for
z\in[0,2]^{2}
,

x(z)=\theta_{1}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{+}\cup\mathbb{Z}_{+}\times 0}\rho_{k}(\theta)(\xi_{k}\phi_{k}(z)+\xi_{k}^{*}\phi_{-k}(z)),

(20)

\xi_{k}\sim\mathcal{C}\mathcal{N}(0,1)\ \text{i.i.d.},

where
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
denotes a standard complex normal distribution,
\xi_{k}^{*}
is the complex conjugate of
\xi_{k}
,
\phi_{k}(z)\propto\exp[\pi iz\cdot k]
are Fourier series basis functions (with
i=\sqrt{-1}
) and

\rho_{k}^{2}(\theta)=\theta_{2}/((\theta_{3}+k_{1}^{2})(\theta_{3}+k_{2}^{2}))^{\frac{r+1}{2}}.

(21)

The coefficient
r
controls the smoothness, and here we will choose
r=1.6
. Note that the periodic prior measure is defined on
[0,2]^{2}
so that no boundary conditions are imposed on the sub-domain
[0,1]^{2}
. Then, the posterior distribution is given by

\pi(dx)\propto L(x)\pi_{0}(dx),

(22)

where
\pi_{0}
is constructed in (
20
) and
L(x)
is constructed in (
19
) (or (
18
)).

2.2.1
The finite approximation problem

One typically use a grid-based approximation to approximate the inferences in LGC
[
39
,
13
,
51
,
5
]
and in LGP
[
45
,
20
,
52
]
. We approximate the likelihoods and priors of LGC and LGP by the fast Fourier transform (FFT) respectively, as described below. First, we truncate the KL-expansion of prior as follows, for an index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
,

x_{\alpha}(z)=\theta_{1}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}}\rho_{k}(\theta)(\xi_{k}\phi_{k}(z)+\xi_{k}^{*}\phi_{-k}(z)),

(23)

\xi_{k}\sim\mathcal{C}\mathcal{N}(0,1)\ \text{i.i.d.},

where
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}:=\{-2^{\alpha_{1}/2},-(2^{\alpha_{1}/2}-1),...,2^{\alpha_{1}/2}-1,2^{\alpha_{1}/2}\}\times\{1,2,...,2^{\alpha_{2}/2}-1,2^{\alpha_{2}/2}\}\cup\{1,2,...,2^{\alpha_{2}/2}-1,2^{\alpha_{2}/2}\}\times 0
. The cost for approximating
x_{\alpha}(z)
over the grid is
\mathcal{O}((\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})2^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}})
. The finite approximations of the likelihood of LGC and LGP are then defined by

\displaystyle\text{(LGC)}\ L_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha}):=\exp\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{x}_{\alpha}(z_{i})-Q(\exp(x_{\alpha}))\Bigg],

(24)

\displaystyle\text{(LGP)}\ L_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha}):=\exp\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{x}_{\alpha}(z_{i})-n\log Q(\exp(x_{\alpha}))\Bigg],

(25)

where
\hat{x}_{\alpha}(z)
is defined as an interpolant over the grid output from FFT and
Q
denotes a quadrature rule,
such that
Q(\exp(x_{\alpha}))\approx\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\exp(x(z))dz
. Then, the finite approximations of the posterior distribution of LGC and LGP are defined by

\pi_{\alpha}(dx_{\alpha})\propto L_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})\pi_{0}(dx_{\alpha}).

(26)

The quantity of interest for these models will be
\varphi(x)=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\exp(x(z))dz
, and we will estimate
its expectation
\pi(\varphi)=\mathbb{E}(\varphi(x)|z_{1},\dots,z_{n})
.

3
Randomized Multi-index sequential Monte Carlo

The original MISMC estimator has been considered in
[
9
]
and
[
27
,
29
]
. Convergence guarantees have been established in
[
34
]
, which demonstrates the importance of selecting a reasonable index set, by comparing the results with the tensor product index set and the total degree index set. Then, a very interesting extension to multi-index sequential Monte Carlo is introduced, which is called randomized multi-index sequential Monte Carlo. The basic methodology of randomized multi-index Monte Carlo is first introduced in
[
43
,
44
]
. Instead of giving an index set in advance, we choose
\alpha
randomly from a distribution. Another advantage of this approach is that it can give an unbiased unnormalized estimator, which is discretization-free.

Define the target distribution as

\pi(x)=f(x)/Z
, where
Z=\int_{\mathsf{X}}f(x)dx

and
f(x):=L(x)\pi_{0}(x)
. Given a quantity of interest
\varphi:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, for simplicity, we define

f(\varphi):=\int_{\mathsf{X}}\varphi(x)f(x)dx=f(1)\pi(\varphi),

(27)

where
f(1)=\int_{\mathsf{X}}f(x)dx=Z
.
Define their approximations at finite resolution

\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}

by

\pi_{\alpha}(x)=f_{\alpha}(x)/Z_{\alpha}
, where
Z_{\alpha}=\int_{\mathsf{X}}f_{\alpha}(x)dx

and
f_{\alpha}(x):=L_{\alpha}(x)\pi_{0}(x)
, and
\varphi_{\alpha}:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where

\lim_{|\alpha|\uparrow\infty}f_{\alpha}=f

and
\lim_{|\alpha|\uparrow\infty}\varphi_{\alpha}=\varphi
.

Consider the ratio decomposition

\pi(\varphi)=\frac{f(\varphi)}{f(1)}=\frac{\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\Delta(f_{\alpha}(\varphi_{\alpha}))}{\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\Delta f_{\alpha}(1)},

(28)

where
\Delta
is the first-order mixed difference operator

\Delta=\otimes_{i=1}^{D}\Delta_{i}:=\Delta_{D}\circ\cdots\circ\Delta_{1}\,,

(29)

which is defined recursively by the first-order difference operator
\Delta_{i}
along direction
1\leq i\leq D
. If
\alpha_{i}>0
,

\Delta_{i}\varphi_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})=\varphi_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})-\varphi_{\alpha-e_{i}}(x_{\alpha})\,,

(30)

where
e_{i}
is the canonical vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, i.e.
(e_{i})_{j}=1
for
j=i
and 0 otherwise. If
\alpha_{i}=0
,
\Delta_{i}\varphi_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})=\varphi_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})
.

For convenience, we denote the vector of multi-indices

\bm{\alpha}(\alpha):=(\bm{\alpha}_{1}(\alpha),...,\bm{\alpha}_{2^{D}}(\alpha))\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D\times 2^{D}},

(31)

where
\bm{\alpha}_{1}(\alpha)=\alpha
,
\bm{\alpha}_{2^{D}}(\alpha)=\alpha-\sum_{i=1}^{D}e_{i}
and
\bm{\alpha}_{i}(\alpha)
for
1<i<2^{D}
correspond to the intermediate multi-indices while computing the mixed difference operator
\Delta
.

Throughout this section
C>0
is a constant whose value may change from line to line.

3.1
Original MISMC ratio estimator

In order to make use of (
28
), we need to construct estimators of

\Delta(f_{\alpha}(\zeta_{\alpha}))
, both for our quantity of interest

\zeta_{\alpha}=\varphi_{\alpha}
and for
\zeta_{\alpha}=1
. The natural and naive way to estimate
\Delta(f_{\alpha}(\zeta_{\alpha}))
is based on sampling from a coupling of

\Pi_{\alpha}:\sigma(\mathsf{X}^{2^{D}})\rightarrow[0,1]
as follows. We first define the coupling prior distribution as

\Pi_{0}(d\bm{x})=\pi_{0}(d\bm{x}_{1})\prod_{i=2}^{2^{D}}\delta_{\bm{x}_{1}}(d\bm{x}_{i})\,,

(32)

where

and
\delta_{\bm{x}_{1}}
denotes the Dirac delta function at
\bm{x}_{1}
.
Note that this is an exact coupling of the prior in the sense that

\int_{\mathsf{X}^{2^{D}-1}}\Pi_{0}(d\bm{x}_{-j})=\pi_{0}(d\bm{x}_{j})\,.

(33)

Here we denote
\bm{x}_{-j}=(\bm{x}_{1},...,\bm{x}_{j-1},\bm{x}_{j+1},...,\bm{x}_{2^{D}})
which omits the
j
th coordinate. Indeed it is the same coupling used in MIMC
[

]
.

In order to provide estimates analogous to the variance rate in the MIMC
[

]
, we use the SMC sampler
[

,

]
to compute. We hence adapt Algorithm

to an extended target which is an approximate coupling
of the actual target as in

,

,

]
,
[

]
and

]
,

To this end, we define a likelihood on the coupled space as

The approximate coupling is defined by

F_{\alpha}(d\bm{x})={\bf L}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})\Pi_{0}(d\bm{x}),\qquad\Pi_{\alpha}(d\bm{x})=\frac{1}{F_{\alpha}(1)}F_{\alpha}(d\bm{x})\,.

, the theoretical convergence rate of SMC will drop from
-4/5
(1D) to
-2/3
(2D), whose rate of convergence suffers the curse of dimensionality. Up to now, the convergence behaviour of MISMC (with TP index set or TD index set) and rMISMC is similar when applied to 1D and 2D PDE problems, which both achieve the canonical rates, but we will see a difference in the following two statistical models.

.
We set the parameters as

\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=5
, since the regularity shows up when
\alpha_{1}\geq 5
and
\alpha_{2}\geq 5
. Further, from Figure
7
, we have mixed rates
s_{1}=s_{2}=0.8
and
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=1.6

The rate of convergence of MISMC TD and rMISMC is approximately
-1
, and both of them achieve the canonical complexity of MSE
-1
. However, the constant for MISMC TD is smaller than rMISMC. We have set a relatively large number of the minimum number of sample,
N_{0}
, in SMC sampler to alleviate the unexpected high variance caused by the few samples. It is reasonable to expect a higher variance
for the randomized method, however, since it involves infinitely many terms compared to finite. MISMC TD achieves a canonical rate, but MISMC TP only has a sub-canonical rate. This is because the assumption
\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{\gamma_{i}}{s_{i}}\leq 2
is violated (
\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{\gamma_{i}}{s_{i}}=\frac{5}{2}
) in MISMC, and this assumption is only needed in the tensor product index set, not in the total degree index set. This indicates that an improper choice of an index set in MISMC will result in dropping the canonical rate to the sub-canonical rate, which highlights the benefit of rMISMC since it achieves the canonical rate without providing an index set in advance.

(54)

A.2
Proof relating to Proposition
3.3

Proposition 3.

Assume
3.1
and
3.2
, and let
\zeta:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. For any multi-index
\alpha
, we have
p_{\alpha}>0
. Then the variance of the randomized MISMC estimator (
43
)
is given by

\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{F}^{\rm rMI}(\zeta)-f(\zeta))^{2}]\leq\frac{C}{N}\Bigg(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\frac{1}{p_{\alpha}}\prod_{i=1}^{D}2^{-\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}}

(59)

+\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\neq\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\prod_{i=1}^{D}2^{-\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}/2}\prod_{j=1}^{D}2^{-\alpha_{j}^{\prime}\beta_{j}/2}\Bigg).

In particular, if

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\frac{1}{p_{\alpha}}\prod_{i=1}^{D}2^{-\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}}\leq C\,,

then one has the canonical convergence rate.

Proof.

One has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{F}^{\rm rMI}(\zeta)-f(\zeta))^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{N_{\alpha}}{Np_{\alpha}}F_{\alpha}^{N_{\alpha}}(\psi_{\zeta_{\alpha}})-\Delta f_{\alpha}(\zeta_{\alpha})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\neq\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\left(\frac{N_{\alpha}}{Np_{\alpha}}F_{\alpha}^{N_{\alpha}}(\psi_{\zeta_{\alpha}})-\Delta f_{\alpha}(\zeta_{\alpha})\right)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{N_{\alpha^{\prime}}}{Np_{\alpha^{\prime}}}F_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{N_{\alpha^{\prime}}}(\psi_{\zeta_{\alpha^{\prime}}})-\Delta f_{\alpha^{\prime}}(\zeta_{\alpha^{\prime}})\right)\Bigg].

The diagonal and off-diagonal terms will be treated separately. First, for the diagonal terms, adding
\pm\frac{N_{\alpha}}{Np_{\alpha}}\Delta f_{\alpha}(\zeta_{\alpha})
in the square term of the diagonal term and using the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{N_{\alpha}}{Np_{\alpha}}F_{\alpha}^{N_{\alpha}}(\psi_{\zeta_{\alpha}})-\Delta f_{\alpha}(\zeta_{\alpha})\right)^{2}\right]

(60)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\Bigg(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{N_{\alpha}}{Np_{\alpha}}\right)^{2}\left(F_{\alpha}^{N_{\alpha}}(\psi_{\zeta_{\alpha}})-\Delta f_{\alpha}(\zeta_{\alpha})\right)^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{N_{\alpha}}{Np_{\alpha}}-1\right)^{2}\left(\Delta f_{\alpha}(\zeta_{\alpha})\right)^{2}\right]}\Bigg)^{2}

Since
\bf p
is proportional to a multinomial distribution, we have
\mathbb{E}[N_{\alpha}^{2}]=Np_{\alpha}(1-p_{\alpha})+N^{2}p_{\alpha}^{2}
. Then, for the first term of (
60
), the same decomposition of (
56
),
along with the result of Theorem
3.1

(conditioned on
N_{\alpha}
) and Assumption
3.2
(V) gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{N_{\alpha}}{Np_{\alpha}}\right)^{2}\left(F_{\alpha}^{N_{\alpha}}(\psi_{\zeta_{\alpha}})-\Delta f_{\alpha}(\zeta_{\alpha})\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{C}{Np_{\alpha}}\prod_{i=1}^{D}2^{-\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}}.

(61)

For the second term of (
60
), Applying Jensen’s inequality to
\left(\Delta f_{\alpha}(\zeta_{\alpha})\right)^{2}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{N_{\alpha}}{Np_{\alpha}}-1\right)^{2}\left(\Delta f_{\alpha}(\zeta_{\alpha})\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{C}{Np_{\alpha}}\prod_{i=1}^{D}2^{-\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}}.

(62)

Hence, we derive the bound for the diagonal term as follows

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{N_{\alpha}}{Np_{\alpha}}F_{\alpha}^{N_{\alpha}}(\psi_{\zeta_{\alpha}})-\Delta f_{\alpha}(\zeta_{\alpha})\right)^{2}\right]

(63)

\leq\frac{C}{N}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\frac{1}{p_{\alpha}}\prod_{i=1}^{D}2^{-\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}}.

Now, the off-diagonal terms are more subtle because of the correlation between

N_{\alpha}
and
N_{\alpha^{\prime}}
, which are (proportional to)
draws from a multinomial distribution with
N/N_{\rm min}
samples and probability

{\bf p}
, and so

\mathbb{E}[(N_{\alpha}-Np_{\alpha})(N_{\alpha^{\prime}}-Np_{\alpha^{\prime}})]=-Np_{\alpha}p_{\alpha^{\prime}}\,.

(64)

Using the law of iterated conditional expectations

\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\left(\frac{N_{\alpha}}{Np_{\alpha}}F_{\alpha}^{N_{\alpha}}(\psi_{\zeta_{\alpha}})-\Delta f_{\alpha}(\zeta_{\alpha})\right)

\left(\frac{N_{\alpha^{\prime}}}{Np_{\alpha^{\prime}}}F_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{N_{\alpha^{\prime}}}(\psi_{\zeta_{\alpha^{\prime}}})-\Delta f_{\alpha^{\prime}}(\zeta_{\alpha^{\prime}})\right)\Bigg],

=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\left(\frac{N_{\alpha}}{Np_{\alpha}}F_{\alpha}^{N_{\alpha}}(\psi_{\zeta_{\alpha}})-\Delta f_{\alpha}(\zeta_{\alpha})\right)

\left(\frac{N_{\alpha^{\prime}}}{Np_{\alpha^{\prime}}}F_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{N_{\alpha^{\prime}}}(\psi_{\zeta_{\alpha^{\prime}}})-\Delta f_{\alpha^{\prime}}(\zeta_{\alpha^{\prime}})\right)\Bigg|{\bf N}\Bigg]\Bigg],

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta f_{\alpha}(\zeta_{\alpha})\Delta f_{\alpha^{\prime}}(\zeta_{\alpha^{\prime}})}{N^{2}p_{\alpha}p_{\alpha^{\prime}}}\mathbb{E}[(N_{\alpha}-Np_{\alpha})(N_{\alpha^{\prime}}-Np_{\alpha^{\prime}})],

(65)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{N}\prod_{i=1}^{D}2^{-\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}/2}\prod_{j=1}^{D}2^{-\alpha^{\prime}_{j}\beta_{j}/2}.

(66)

Line (
65
) follows from the conditional independence
of
F_{\alpha}^{N_{\alpha}}(\psi_{\zeta_{\alpha}})
and

F_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{N_{\alpha^{\prime}}}(\psi_{\zeta_{\alpha^{\prime}}})

given
{\bf N}=\{N_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}

and some simple algebra.
Line (
66
) follows from (
64
)
and Assumption
3.2
(V),
along with Jensen’s inequality applied to

(\Delta f_{\alpha}(\zeta_{\alpha}))^{2}
and
(\Delta f_{\alpha^{\prime}}(\zeta_{\alpha^{\prime}}))^{2}
.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\neq\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\prod_{i=1}^{D}2^{-\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}/2}\prod_{j=1}^{D}2^{-\alpha_{j}^{\prime}\beta_{j}/2}

(67)

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\prod_{i=1}^{D}2^{-\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}/2}\Big)^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\prod_{i=1}^{D}2^{-\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}},

\displaystyle=\Big(\prod_{i=1}^{D}\frac{1}{1-2^{-\beta_{i}/2}}\Big)^{2}-\prod_{i=1}^{D}\frac{1}{1-2^{-\beta_{i}}},

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{D}\frac{1}{(1-2^{-\beta_{i}/2})^{2}}-\prod_{i=1}^{D}\frac{1}{1-2^{-\beta_{i}}}<C.

(68)

Since
\beta_{i}>0
,
1-2^{-\beta_{i}}<1
and
1-2^{-\beta_{i}/2}<1
, we can found a constant
C
to bound the last line.
∎

A.3
Proof relating to Theorem
3.4

Theorem 4.

Assume
3.1

and
3.2
(V,C), with
\beta_{i}>\gamma_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,D
. Then, for suitable
\varphi:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, it is possible to choose a probability distribution
{\bf p}
on
\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}
such that for some
C>0

\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{\varphi}^{\rm rMI}-\pi(\varphi))^{2}]\leq\frac{C}{N}\,,

and expected
{\rm COST}(\widehat{\varphi}^{\rm rMI})\leq CN
, i.e. the canonical rate.
The estimator
\widehat{\varphi}^{\rm rMI}
is defined in equation (
46
).

Proof.

Since our unnormalized estimator is unbiased, we have

\mathbb{E}\big[(\widehat{\varphi}^{\text{rMI}}-\pi(\varphi))^{2}\big]\leq\max_{\zeta\in\{\varphi,1\}}\mathbb{E}\big[(\widehat{F}^{\rm rMI}(\zeta)-f(\zeta))^{2}\big].

(69)

Then,
\mathbb{E}[(\hat{\varphi}^{\text{rMI}}-\pi(\varphi))^{2}]
is less than
C/N
as long as
\max_{\zeta\in\{\varphi,1\}}\mathbb{E}\big[(\widehat{F}^{\rm rMI}(\zeta)-f(\zeta))^{2}\big]
is of
\mathcal{O}(N^{-1})
.

Following from Proposition
3.3
, we need

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\frac{1}{p_{\alpha}}\prod_{i=1}^{D}2^{-\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}}\leq C

(70)

to let
\mathbb{E}\big[(\widehat{F}^{\rm rMI}(\zeta)-f(\zeta))^{2}\big]
be of
\mathcal{O}(N^{-1})
. Additionally, we let the distribution follows from a simple constrained minimization of
the expected cost for one sample

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}p_{\alpha}\prod_{i=1}^{D}2^{\alpha_{i}\gamma_{i}}\leq C.

(71)

Since
p_{\alpha}>0
, one finds that

p_{\alpha}\propto\prod_{i=1}^{D}2^{-\alpha_{i}(\gamma_{i}+\beta_{i})/2}

(72)

is one suitable choice for probabilities to satisfy the two conditions stated in (
70
) and (
71
). Then the expected cost
{\rm COST}(\widehat{\varphi}^{\rm rMI})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}Np_{\alpha}\prod_{i=1}^{D}2^{\alpha_{i}\gamma_{i}}.
and (
70
) are then given respectively by

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}N\prod_{i=1}^{D}2^{\alpha_{i}(\gamma_{i}-\beta_{i})/2},

(73)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\prod_{i=1}^{D}2^{\alpha_{i}(\gamma_{i}-\beta_{i})/2}.

(74)

By assumption
\gamma_{i}-\beta_{i}<0
, the expected cost is bounded by
CN
and (
70
) is finite. Hence,
\mathbb{E}\big[(\widehat{F}^{\rm rMI}(\zeta)-f(\zeta))^{2}\big]
is of
\mathcal{O}(N^{-1})
.
∎
