Modern Analysis of Sparse Random Projections
Maciej Skorski

Abstract
There has been recently a lot of research on sparse variants of random projections, faster adaptations of the state-of-the-art dimensionality reduction technique originally due to Johsnon and Lindenstrauss. Although the construction is very simple, its analyses are notoriously complicated. Meeting the demand for both simplicity and accuracy, this work establishes sharp sub-poissonian tail bounds for the distribution of sparse random projections. Compared to other works, this analysis provide superior numerical guarantees (exactly matching impossibility results) while being arguably less complicated (the technique resembles Bennet’s Inequality and is of independent interest).

Contribution

Random linear projections have the extremely attractive property of considerably

[

The purpose of this work is to give a novel analysis of Sparse Johnson-Lindestrauss Transforms, which is
simple and accurate

[

Theorem 1

(Poisson Tails of Sparse Johnson-Lindenstrauss Lemma)
.

For any confidence
\delta>0
, distortion
\epsilon>0
and integer sparsity

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left\{(1-\epsilon)\|x\|^{2}\leqslant\|Ax\|^{2}\leqslant(1+\epsilon)\|x\|^{2}\right\}\geqslant 1-\delta,

(1)

holds for any vector
x

\displaystyle m\geqslant\frac{4\log(2/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot h\left(\frac{25\epsilon}{p}\right)^{-1},

(2)

and that
p\leqslant\frac{1}{30},\epsilon\leqslant p\log(1/2p)
, where
h
is the ”Bennet function” defined as

\displaystyle h(u)\triangleq\frac{(1+u)\log(1+u)-u}{u^{2}/2}.

(3)

The construction of the matrix

The property (

) is the standard formulation of the Johnson-Lindenstrauss Lemma (sometimes the result is presented as a union bound over multiple vectors

\epsilon

1.2
Comparison to Related Works

We now discuss how our result improves upon bounds available in the existing literature.

1.

The result offers a valuable and elegant explanation of the tails in the sparse Johnson-Lindenstrauss Lemma: they are dominated by a Poisson distribution (the term
h
is typical for Bennet’s trick and Poissonian tail bounds). The proof is elementary and short.

2.

In the regime
\epsilon/p\to 0
, the bound exactly matches the best possible dimension for the Johnson-Lindenstrauss Lemma, found recently by Burr at al.
[
2
]
(we then have
m=\frac{4\log(2/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot(1+o(1))
). This is a surprisingly strong result, as before we only knew that it approaches the optimal dimension up to a large constant factor (and the exponentially large gap loss in the confidence). In other words, this paper shows for the first time that sparse random projections can be on pair with their dense (unrestricted) counterparts.

3.

In the regime
\epsilon=\Theta(p)
our bound shows that it is possible to choose
m=O(\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}})
, which gives the sparsity
s=O(\epsilon^{-1})
. This is consistent with the literature, but our analysis gives much better constants (best explicit constants were due to Cohen at. al.
[
4
]
)

4.

In the regime
\epsilon/p\gg 0
our bound offers an interesting dimension-sparsity tradeoff. Namely,
we can have
m=\frac{4B\log^{-1}B\log(2/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}
and
s=O(\epsilon^{-1}/\log B)
. In other words, we can improve the sparsity by a factor, but loose exponentially in the dimension. Such a result appears only in one work, a somewhat complicated analysis due to Cohen
[
3
]
. The result above is more elementary and provides explicit and better constants. It also appears to be asymptotically better, namely in
[
3
]
we find a bound on
m
worse by
\log B
.

Preliminaries

The standard way of obtaining tail bounds is the Crammer-Chernoff inequality
\mathbf{P}\{X\geqslant\epsilon\}\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-s\epsilon}\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{sX}
, to be optimized over
s>0
(Markov’s inequality applied to exponential moments).

Recall that the Poisson random variable follows the distribution
\mathbf{P}\{\mathsf{Poiss}(\lambda)=k\}=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}\lambda^{k}/k!
. We have

\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{s\mathsf{Poiss}(\lambda)}=\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(\mathrm{e}^{t}-1)}
and the Crammer-Chernoff method gives

Lemma 2

(
[
10
]
)
.

For any
\lambda>0
and
\epsilon>0
we have that

\mathbf{P}\left\{\mathsf{Poiss}(\lambda)\geqslant\epsilon\right\}\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}(\mathrm{e}\lambda/\epsilon)^{\epsilon}
.

3.1
Step 1: Decomposition over Embedding Dimension

Let
A
be as in
Algorithm

1
. By the identity
\|y\|^{2}=y^{T}y
we obtain

\displaystyle\|Ax\|^{2}-\|x\|^{2}=x^{T}Bx,\quad B\triangleq A^{T}A-I.

(4)

We see that
B
is off-diagonal, because
\sum_{k}A_{i,k}^{2}=1
(
A
has exactly
s
entries of absolute value
1/\sqrt{s}
in each column). Let
\eta_{i,j}
indicate if the row
i
is selected when constructing the column
j
in

Algorithm

1
and let
r_{i,j}
be independent Rademacher random variables. Then:

\displaystyle B=\sum_{k}B_{k},\quad(B_{k})_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{s}\eta_{k,i}\eta_{k,j}r_{k,i}r_{k,j}&i\not=j\\
0&i=j\end{cases}.

(5)

Observe that
B_{k}
are not independent, because
\eta_{k,i}
are not independent across rows
k
. Nevertheless, one can show the following
iid majorization
:

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\mathbf{E}(x^{T}\sum_{k}B_{k}x)^{q}\leqslant\mathbf{E}(x^{T}\sum_{k}B^{\prime}_{k}x)^{q},

(6)

where
(B^{\prime}_{k})
are independent and distributed as
(B_{k})
. Indeed,
B^{\prime}_{k}
is be constructed by replacing
\eta_{k,i}
that appear in
B_{k}
by independent Bernoulli random variables
\eta^{\prime}_{k,i}
. By expanding both sides into sums of monomials and the symmetry of
r_{k,i}
we see that it suffices to prove

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\mathbf{E}\prod_{(k,i)\in S}\eta_{k,i}^{d_{k,i}}x_{i}^{d_{k,i}}\leqslant\mathbf{E}\prod_{(k,i)\in S}{\eta^{\prime}}_{k,i}^{d_{k,i}}x_{i}^{d_{k,i}},

(7)

for any set of tuples
S
and even exponents
d_{k,i}
. Now the first inequality follows as
d_{k,i}
are even, while
the second reduces to
\mathbf{E}\prod_{i\in I}\eta_{k,i}\leqslant\mathbf{E}\prod_{i\in I}{\eta}^{\prime}_{k,i}
for any fixed
k
and any set
I
(because
\eta^{\prime}_{k,i}
are independent for different
k
), which simply means that sampling with replacement makes hitting any set
I
less likely than independent sampling - obviously true.

We now see that the problem reduces to analyzing

\displaystyle Z=\sum_{i\not=j}x_{i}x_{j}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}r_{i}r_{j},

(8)

because from
Equation

6
we have the following bound on exponential moments:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{tx^{T}Bx}\leqslant\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{|t|\sum_{k}x^{T}{B^{\prime}}_{k}x}=\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{t\cdot\frac{Z_{1}+\cdots+Z_{m}}{s}},\quad Z_{i}\sim^{iid}Z.

(9)

3.2
Step 2: Moments of 1-Dim Dense Random Projections

We first observe that for any sequence of real numbers
(y_{i})
and any positive integer
q

\displaystyle(\sum_{i\not=j}y_{i}y_{j})^{q}\leqslant(\sum_{i}y_{i})^{2q}.

(10)

Indeed, if we replace
y_{i}
by
|y_{i}|
the left side can only increase while the right-hand side doesn’t change; for non-negative
y_{i}
the inequality follows as
\sum_{i\not=i}y_{i}y_{j}\leqslant(\sum_{i}y_{i})^{2}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[Z^{q}]\leqslant\mathbf{E}(\sum_{i}x_{i}\eta_{i}r_{i})^{2q}.

(11)

By the multinomial expansion and the symmetry

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\eta_{i})^{2q}=\sum_{r=2}^{q}p^{r}\sum_{d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}\binom{2q}{2d_{1},\ldots,2d_{r}}\sum_{i_{1}\not=i_{2}\not=\cdots\not=i_{r}}x_{i_{1}}^{2d_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{r}}^{2d_{r}}.

(12)

Furthermore, for every
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that
\sum_{i}d_{i}=q
it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1}\not=i_{2}\not=\cdots\not=i_{r}}x_{i_{1}}^{2d_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{r}}^{2d_{r}}\leqslant\binom{q}{d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}}^{-1}(\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2})^{q},

(13)

by keeping only the terms
x_{i_{1}}^{2d_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{r}}^{2d_{r}}
from the expansion of the right side.

By combining the last three inequalities and
\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}=1
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[Z^{q}]\leqslant\sum_{r=2}^{q}p^{r}\sum_{d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}\binom{2q}{2d_{1},\ldots,2d_{r}}\binom{q}{d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}}^{-1}.

(14)

To simplify the sum, we will use the following bound

\displaystyle\binom{2q}{2d_{1},\ldots,2d_{r}}\leqslant 2^{q}\cdot\binom{q}{d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}}^{2},\quad d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},d_{1}+\cdots+d_{r}=q.

(15)

The inequality is equivalent to
\binom{2q}{q}\leqslant 2^{q}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\binom{2d_{i}}{d_{i}}
.
Let
B_{k}=\binom{2k}{k}
be the central binomial coefficient; the relation
B_{k}/B_{k-1}=4\cdot(1-1/2k)
and the bound
1-\frac{1}{2k}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
imply
B_{k}\geqslant 2^{k}
. Then
2^{q}\prod\binom{2d_{i}}{d_{i}}\geqslant 2^{q}\cdot 2^{d_{1}+\cdots+d_{r}}=2^{2q}\geqslant\binom{2q}{q}
, so the claim follows.

This bound, together with
\sum_{d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}\binom{q}{d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}}\leqslant(1+\cdots+1)^{q}=r^{q}
implies

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[Z^{q}]\leqslant 2^{q}\sum_{r=2}^{q}p^{r}r^{q}.

(16)

3.3
Step 3: Poisson Majorization

Using
Equation

16
for
q\geqslant 3
and the more accurate bound
\mathbf{E}[Z^{q}]\leqslant 2p^{2}
for

we obtain the following bound on exponential moments (recall that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{tZ}&\leqslant 1+t^{2}p^{2}+\sum_{r\geqslant 2}p^{r}\sum_{q\geqslant\max\{3,r\}}\frac{(2tr)^{q}}{q!}\\
&=1+t^{2}p^{2}+\sum_{r\geqslant 2}p^{r}\mathrm{e}^{2tr}\mathbf{P}\left\{\mathsf{Poiss}(2tr)\geqslant\max\{3,r\}\right\}\\
&=1+t^{2}p^{2}+p^{2}(\mathrm{e}^{4t}-8t^{2}-4t-1)+\sum_{r\geqslant 3}p^{r}\mathrm{e}^{2tr}\mathbf{P}\left\{\mathsf{Poiss}(2tr)\geqslant r\right\}\end{aligned}.

(17)

Using the known bound
\mathbf{P}\left\{\mathsf{Poiss}(2tr)\geqslant r\right\}\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-2tr}(2\mathrm{e}t)^{r}
for the Poisson distribution tails (it is obtained from the moment generating function, see
[

]
or the next section for an implicit derivation) for

and
\mathbf{P}\left\{\mathsf{Poiss}(2tr)\geqslant r\right\}\leqslant 1
for

we obtain the following bound

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{tZ}\leqslant 1+t^{2}p^{2}+p^{2}(\mathrm{e}^{4t}-8t^{2}-4t-1)+\begin{cases}\frac{(2\mathrm{e}pt)^{3}}{1-2\mathrm{e}pt}&\quad 0<t<\frac{1}{2}\\
\frac{p^{3}\mathrm{e}^{6t}}{1-p\mathrm{e}^{2t}}&\quad\frac{1}{2}\leqslant t<\frac{\log(1/p)}{2}\end{cases}.

(18)

We transform the bound into the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{tZ}\leqslant 1+t^{2}p^{2}+p^{2}\cdot\underbrace{\begin{cases}\mathrm{e}^{4t}-8t^{2}-4t-1+{\frac{8\mathrm{e}^{3}pt^{3}}{1-2\mathrm{e}pt}}&\quad 0<t<\frac{1}{2}\\

(19)

and observe that the following numerical inequality holds

\displaystyle\psi(t,p)\leqslant\frac{\mathrm{e}^{Kt}-\frac{K^{2}t^{2}}{2}-Kt-1}{K^{2}/2},\quad 0<t\leqslant\frac{\log(1/2p)}{2},

(20)

for some sufficiently large constant

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{tZ}\leqslant 1+2p^{2}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{e}^{Kt}-Kt-1}{K^{2}},\quad 0<t\leqslant\frac{\log(1/2p)}{2}.

(21)

This specific function is chosen as the upper bound because it appears in the exponential moments of Poisson random variables, making computations tracktable.

For

3.4
Aggregating Bounds

Applying the last bound to

\displaystyle\log\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{t\cdot(Z_{1}+\cdots+Z_{m})}\leqslant 2mp^{2}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{e}^{Kt}-Kt-1}{K^{2}},\quad 0<t<\frac{\log(1/2p)}{2}.

For our case we use

\displaystyle\max\{\mathbf{P}\left\{x^{T}Bx\geqslant\epsilon\right\},\mathbf{P}\left\{x^{T}Bx\leqslant-\epsilon\right\}\}\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{m\epsilon^{2}}{4}\cdot h\left(\frac{K\epsilon}{2p}\right)},
