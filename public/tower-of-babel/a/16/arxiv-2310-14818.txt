Elementary catastrophes underlying bifurcations of vector fields and PDEs
Mike R. Jeffrey

Abstract
A practical method was proposed recently for finding local bifurcation points in an n n -dimensional vector field F F by seeking their ‘ underlying catastrophes ’. Here we apply the idea to the homogeneous steady states of a partial differential equation as an example of the role that catastrophes can play in reaction diffusion. What are these ‘underlying’ catastrophes? We then show they essentially define a restricted class of ‘solvable’ rather than ‘all classifiable’ singularities, by identifying degenerate zeros of a vector field F F without taking into account its vectorial character. As a result they are defined by a minimal set of r r analytic conditions that provide a practical means to solve for them, and a huge reduction from the calculations needed to classify a singularity, which we will also enumerate here. In this way, underlying catastrophes seem to allow us apply Thom’s elementary catastrophes in much broader contexts.

Introduction

Catastrophe theory was introduced by René Thom to explain how discontinuities in behaviour can arise out of smooth changes in a system’s equations. This marked a conceptual step in the history of calculus that was very influential in physics, particularly in optics
[
20
,
30
]
, and despite some overly speculative applications that attracted fair skepticism, its importance to science was never really in doubt, see for example the polemic
[
29
]
and its rebuttal
[
3
]
.

These days catastrophes have been largely absorbed into the broader theory of bifurcations, but here we will argue that a wider application of Thom’s original concept is possible, using the idea of
underlying catastrophes
introduced in
[
12
]
. This concept attempts to apply the elementary catastrophes to much wider classes of systems than they were intended to encompass, basically to any multidimensional systems with general spatial and temporal variations. So it is worth summarizing Thom’s catastrophes briefly, pertaining to a gradient function
{\bf F}=\nabla V
, in preparation to apply them to any general vector field
{\bf F}
.

Indeed Thom’s elementary catastrophes are so simple, we can encapsulate them in just

Notice that catastrophes provide two powerful tools: first a way to classify the topology of a function (via its stationary points), and second, simple conditions to locate changes in that topology (as stationary points appear or disappear with changes in parameters). Notice, however, that the catastrophes deal only with the turning points of a

{\bf F}=\nabla V
. This is what we will generalise here. We will also show how this permits the finding of singularities that are otherwise incomputable.

Not only do bifurcations become increasingly difficult to classify as we go to systems with more variables and more parameters, but to classify any bifurcation, its location must first be known. Here lies our problem. The known classification of bifurcations (e.g.
[

2

16

10

Moreover it is unclear to what extent such classifications can be applied beyond mere vector fields, for example to spatiotemporal problems like reaction-diffusion. Some authors have been drawn to catastrophes as a simpler means to understand behaviour in dynamical systems and partial differential equations, and yet hindered by the fact that catastrophes apply only to scalar potentials. Recent examples include the identification of butterfly catastrophes in reaction diffusion problems in
[

[

A more direct extension of catastrophes to such problems is made possible by a recent suggestion from

underlying

was described in

I also give an example of a reaction-diffusion problem where diffusion terms trigger an underlying catastrophe, separating a system’s steady state concentrations into different mixing regimes, the boundaries between which are clearly recognisable as a butterfly catastrophe. This is done without restricting attention to scalar catastrophes at any point.

We define an underlying catastrophe as follows.

Take a smooth vector field
{\bf F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with components
{\bf F}=(f_{1},f_{2},...,f_{n})
. Write
{\bf F}={\bf F}({\bf x},{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}})
for a variable
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and parameter
{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
, with gradient operator
\nabla=\mbox{$\frac{\partial\;}{\partial{\bf x}}$}
.
A point where the vectors
\nabla f_{1},...,\nabla f_{n}
, are linearly dependent creates a singularity, but we can typically assume that any
n-1
of the gradient vectors
\nabla f_{1},...,\nabla f_{n}
, are linearly independent. The Jacobian determinant
\mathcal{B}_{1}=\left|\nabla\left({f_{1},,f_{2},...,f_{n}}\right)\right|
, then vanishes, and the degeneracy of the point can be characterized by a sequence of determinants
\mathcal{B}_{i}=\left|\nabla\left({\mathcal{B}_{i-1},f_{2},...,f_{n}}\right)\right|
, letting
\mathcal{B}_{0}=f_{1}
.

The vanishing of all
\mathcal{B}_{i}
for
i=1,...,r
will signal a catastrophe of codimension
r
. The system
0={\bf F}=\mathcal{B}_{1}=...=\mathcal{B}_{r}
is solvable to find the point where a catastrophe occurs if an accompanying family of determinants
\mathcal{G}_{r,k_{1}..k_{r-1}}
is non-vanishing. The definition of these
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{G}
determinants will be outlined below, and given more completely in
section

3
.

This is easiest to see by considering a vector field of the form

\displaystyle{\bf F}=

\displaystyle\Big(\;f(x_{1},{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}})+\underline{\tau}\cdot\underline{x}\;,\;\lambda_{2}x_{2}\;,\;...\;,\;\lambda_{n}x_{n}\;\Big)

(1)

\displaystyle{\rm where}\quad

\displaystyle\;\;f(x_{1},{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}})=x_{1}^{r+1}+\alpha_{r}x_{1}^{r-1}+...+\alpha_{2}x_{1}+\alpha_{1}\;,

with
\underline{\tau}\cdot\underline{x}=\tau_{2}x_{2}+...+\tau_{n}x_{n}
, for which
the conditions
\mathcal{B}_{i}=0
reduce to just the one-dimensional conditions

that identify elementary catastrophes of the scalar field

are the most important, as these unfold the catastrophe as they vary about

and

are required to be non-zero for the equations

to be solvable, and they determine the values of the

underlying catastrophe, such that

can be transformed into such an expression locally, will be discussed in a forthcoming work

]
.

More completely, the following was proposed in

\displaystyle\propto 3u(\gamma+3u^{2})^{2}-2k_{2}v(\gamma+3u^{2})-9k_{2}u^{2}v\;,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\qquad 0

\displaystyle=-5k_{1}^{2/3}k_{2}^{1/3}p^{-2/3}+5k_{1}^{1/3}k_{2}^{2/3}p^{2/3}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\qquad p

\displaystyle=(k_{1}/k_{2})^{1/4}\;,\quad q=\mbox{$\frac{1}{9}$}(k_{1}k_{2})^{1/2}\;.

Hence butterflies are found at isolated points in the space of
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)
where

\displaystyle u=\pm\mbox{$\frac{1}{3}$}(k_{1}k_{2}^{3})^{1/8}\;,\quad\beta=\mp\mbox{$\frac{16}{27}$}(k_{1}^{3}k_{2})^{3/8}\;,\quad\alpha=\mbox{$\frac{2}{3}$}(k_{1}^{3}k_{2})^{1/4}\;,

\displaystyle v=\pm\mbox{$\frac{1}{3}$}(k_{1}^{3}k_{2})^{1/8}\;,\quad\delta=\mp\mbox{$\frac{16}{27}$}(k_{1}k_{2}^{3})^{3/8}\;,\quad\gamma=\mbox{$\frac{2}{3}$}(k_{1}k_{2}^{3})^{1/4}\;.

These are just cursory examples to illustrate the concept. By giving appropriate initial data and boundary conditions for the differential equations, we could go on to simulate the mixing of the two-species in the
(x,y)
plane at different times, or to study wave propagation through these concentrations.
In
[
1
]
, for example, underlying catastrophes of homogeneous steady states are used to determine the existence of heteroclinic connections that represent ‘wave-pinning’ patterns in cell polarity formation in plants. A number of other works have attempted to relate elementary catastrophes to the behaviours of partial differential equations.
In
[
6
]
, the signature shapes of a swallowtail are seen in bifurcation diagrams of an oxidation reaction, and in
[
21
]
, butterfly sets are then shown in reaction diffusion by limiting to a scalar one species problem, and a similar treatment places cusps in a crowd density problem in
[
28
]
.
A nice discussion of the problem of detecting high codimension bifurcation points in PDEs can be found in
[
15
]
, before scalar catastrophe theory is used to develop numerical methods for the purpose.
An attempt is made to relate catastrophes to the phases of liquid crystals in
[
8
]
by focussing on a scalar term like the free energy.

Typically in such studies it has been necessary to appeal to scalar functions to investigate the occurrence of catastrophes. The example above takes elements from these studies, and tries to show how catastrophes could be applied to them without reducing to scalar quantities. Hopefully this merely hints at the possible applications, and deeper study will require more detailed and specialised analysis with particular applications in mind.

Finally, but importantly, when deriving the catastrophes above we should have also calculated the non-degeneracy conditions
\mathcal{G}
from
eq.

25
at each stage. Though straightforward, these are more numerous than the
\mathcal{B}
determinants, and best done with a computer. The relevant conditions are
\mathcal{G}_{1}
for the fold,
\mathcal{G}_{2,1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2,2}
for the cusp, four determinants
\mathcal{G}_{3,ij}
for the swallowtail and eight determinants
\mathcal{G}_{4,ijk}
for the butterfly, where
i,j,k
take values 1 and 2 (because the dimension of the system is 2).

We will not give all of these calculations, but we find they are indeed non-vanishing for nonzero
k_{1},k_{2}
.
At the butterfly for example we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{4,ijk}=103680(-1)^{i+k}(k_{1}k_{2})^{4}(k_{1}/k_{2})^{\mbox{$\frac{3}{4}$}(k+2j+3i-9)}\

(21)

which is non-vanishing if and only if

, i.e. with diffusion.

The fact that these
\mathcal{G}
conditions are non-zero means the system is ‘full’ according to
definition

3.1
, meaning firstly that we can solve the conditions
\mathcal{B}_{i}=0
, and moreover that these solutions are unique, i.e. we could not obtain different results by instead using the determinants from
eq.

24
. Were any of these
\mathcal{G}

Not being full may imply that the point has some higher degeneracy, for example we indeed find that
\mathcal{G}_{1}
vanishes at the set where the folds collide and become higher order catastrophes, i.e. the cusps, and the
\mathcal{G}_{2,1}=\mathcal{G}_{2,2}=0
at the set where the cusps are degenerate and become swallowtails, etc.

Not being full need always not imply an actual degeneracy of the system, however, as they may indicate the system has essentially become lower dimensional, so we just need to adjust the dimension of our calculations. If
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
vanish here then all of the
\mathcal{G}
conditions will vanish, and we can clearly see that the catastrophe calculations above all break down. What has actually happened in this case is that the righthand side of
section

2
is now divergence-free, and expressible as the derivative of a scalar potential, to which we could apply elementary catastrophe theory instead.

Underlying catastrophes: the

-

The

Strictly speaking our interest is only local, so we should refer to

Recall that

We can give more general formulae for the

-

-

Definition 3.1
.

For

we first define

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{i}

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\partial(\mathcal{B}_{i-1},f_{2},...,f_{n})}{\partial(x_{1},...,x_{n})}\right|\;,

(22)

and for
i=0

\mathcal{B}_{0}\equiv f_{1}
.
To generalize this to allow placing
\mathcal{B}_{i_{1}}
in the

, denote the vector
{\bf F}
with its

\displaystyle(f_{1},...,f_{n})\backslash^{k}V=(f_{1},...,f_{k-1},V,f_{k+1},...,f_{n})\;,

(23)

then let

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{i,K(i-1)}

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\partial(f_{1},...,f_{n})\backslash^{k_{i-1}}\mathcal{B}_{i-1,K(i-2)}}{\partial(x_{1},...,x_{n})}\right|\;,

(24)

where

We define the case

.
Finally let

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{r,K(r-1)}

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\partial(f_{1},...,f_{n},\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2,K(1)},...,\mathcal{B}_{r,K(r-1)})}{\partial(x_{1},...,x_{n},\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{r})}\right|\;,

(25)

defining the case

A crucial assumption was stated before

1.1

Definition 3.2

Let the

, …,

\displaystyle\operatorname{subrank}{\bf F}

\displaystyle=\min\left({\operatorname{rank}\left[(\mbox{$\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial{\bf x}}$},...,\mbox{$\frac{\partial f_{n}}{\partial{\bf x}}$})\backslash^{j}{\bf 0}\;,\;\;j=1,...,n\right]}\right)\;.

Definition

4
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
determinants and Boardman symbols

The idea set out in
[
12
]
essentially makes three propositions, namely that for a vector field
{\bf F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
in variables
(x_{1},...,x_{n})
and parameters
(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{r})
:

(i)

Any local bifurcation point of
{\bf F}
, namely where
{\bf F}=0
and its Jacobian has less than full rank, has an
underlying catastrophe
.

(ii)

An
underlying catastrophe
is a degenerate zero of
{\bf F}
that bifurcates under perturbation into a perturbation-dependent number of zeros. In particular, a corank 1
underlying catastrophe
of codimension
r
would break up under perturbation into up to
r+1
zeroes of
{\bf F}
.

(iii)

For certain
primary forms

\left({f(x_{1},\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{r}),x_{2},...,x_{n}}\right)
in terms of a scalar polynomial
f:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}
(eq.
eq.

1
in
section

1
), the conditions defining an underlying catastrophe reduce to the familiar defining conditions of Thom’s
elementary catastrophes
.

These properties are the motivation for the term ‘underlying catastrophes’, and we will show here the sense and conditions under which they are indeed true.
The term ‘underlying’ comes from (i)-(ii), namely that we are identifying bifurcations only by looking for places where zeroes of
{\bf F}
encounter singularities, without any regard for the vectorial nature of
{\bf F}
, so in a sense we are looking only at the mapping ‘underlying
{\bf F}
’ (ignoring that it maps to a tangent space and this carries consequences for local stability). For cases of corank 1, giving us what Arnold classifies as series
A_{r}
with “one zero eigenvalue and
(r-1)
-fold degeneracy in the nonlinear terms”
[
2
]
, we will show here that the singularity classification reduces precisely to the conditions
\mathcal{B}_{i}=0
.
The use of the term ‘catastrophe’ is then due to (iii), namely that these conditions then reduce further to the identifying conditions of the elementary catastrophes for the primary forms
eq.

1
.

René Thom introduced a general classification of singularities in
[
24
]
for mappings
{\bf F}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. They were defined through the transversality of certain submanifolds defined by kernels of
{\bf F}
and its derivatives. John Boardman provided a more explicit construction in
[
4
]
, developed on by Mather and Morin
[
17
,
19
]
, which can be expressed as a sequence of extended Jacobian matrices containing minors of successive derivatives of
{\bf F}
, culminating in a convenient set of
Boardman symbols
for Thom’s singularities.

The Thom-Boardman singularity classification is less well known than perhaps it should be among mathematicians, and after half a century is still far less well utilised in applications than it could be. So let us begin with a slightly informal, methodological introduction to the Thom-Boardman procedure, before showing how the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions not only fit into the Thom-Boardman theory, but vastly simplify it for a significant class of singularities.
We will restrict our attention to
{\bf F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
but the extension for
{\bf F}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
m\neq n
is straightforward.
Where possible we leave the more rigourous statements behind the theory to references.

4.1
Thom-Boardman singularities

The Thom-Boardman classification makes heavy use of
ranks
and
minors
of certain Jacobian matrices.
The
k\times k
minors of a
p\times q
matrix
M
are the determinants of all
k\times k
matrices taken from
M
by omitting any
p-k
rows and
q-k
rows.
A
p\times q
matrix
M
has rank
s
if there exists an
s\times s
minor of
M
that is non-zero, but every larger minor
is
zero.

Let
\nabla
denote the derivative operator with respect to
{\bf x}=(x_{1},...,x_{n})
.
We will be dealing with vectors and matrices of differing sizes below, so for some
{\bf F}=(f_{1},...,f_{n})
and
{\bf G}=(g_{1},...,g_{m})
, for any
n,m,
let us be able to combine them as
({\bf F},{\bf G})=(f_{1},...,f_{n},g_{1},...,g_{m})
, with Jacobians
\nabla({\bf F},{\bf G})=(\nabla{\bf F},\nabla{\bf G})=(\nabla f_{1},...,\nabla f_{n},\nabla g_{1},...,\nabla g_{m})
. We refer to each component
f_{i}
or
g_{i}
as occupying a
row
of
({\bf F},{\bf G})
, and similarly each
\nabla f_{i}
or
\nabla g_{i}
occupies a
row
of
\nabla({\bf F},{\bf G})
.

To define the
Boardman symbol
of a singularity we can define a sequence of extended Jacobians of
{\bf F}
.

Given
{\bf F}=(f_{1},...,f_{n}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, first define the symbol
\Delta^{1}{\bf F}=\left({f_{1},...,f_{n},\det\nabla{\bf F}}\right)
, and also for
i_{1}>1
define
\Delta^{i_{1}}{\bf F}=\left({{\bf F},m^{i_{1}}_{1},m^{i_{1}}_{2},...,}\right)
, where
m^{i_{1}}_{j}
,
j=1,...,N_{1}
, are the
(n-{i_{1}}+1)\times(n-{i_{1}}+1)
minors of
\nabla{\bf F}
.

Next define
\Delta^{i_{2}}\Delta^{i_{1}}{\bf F}=\left({\Delta^{i_{1}}{\bf F},m^{i_{2}}_{2,1},m^{i_{2}}_{2,2},...}\right)
, where
m^{i_{2}}_{2,j}
are the
(n-i_{2}+1)\times(n-i_{2}+1)
minors of
\nabla(\Delta^{i_{1}}{\bf F})
, and proceed iteratively, next defining
\Delta^{i_{3}}\Delta^{i_{2}}\Delta^{i_{1}}{\bf F}=\left({\Delta^{i_{2}}\Delta^{i_{1}}{\bf F},m^{i_{3}}_{3,1},m^{i_{3}}_{3,2},...}\right)
, where
m^{i_{3}}_{3,j}
are the
(n-i_{3}+1)\times(n-i_{3}+1)
minors of
\nabla(\Delta^{i_{2}}\Delta^{i_{1}}{\bf F})
, and so on, giving generally

\displaystyle\Delta^{i_{j}}...\Delta^{i_{1}}{\bf F}=\left({\Delta^{i_{j-1}}...\Delta^{i_{1}}{\bf F},m^{i_{j}}_{j,1},m^{i_{j}}_{j,2},...}\right)\;,

(28)

where
m^{i_{j}}_{j,k}
for
k=1,...,N_{j},
are the
(n-i_{j}+1)\times(n-i_{j}+1)
minors of
\nabla(\Delta^{i_{j-1}}...\Delta^{i_{1}}{\bf F})
. We give a formula for the number
N_{j}
of minors in each stage of these calculations in
appendix

A
.

Definition 4.1
.

The Boardman symbol of
{\bf F}
at
{\bf x}=0
is the sequence
\tau=\tau_{1},...,\tau_{r},
such that each
\nabla\Delta^{\tau_{j-1}}...\Delta^{\tau_{1}}{\bf F}(0)
has corank
\tau_{j}
for
j=1,...,r
, (including that
\nabla{\bf F}(0)
has corank
\tau_{1}
), and the symbol
\tau
is taken to terminate at
r
such that
\tau_{r+1}=0
.

We call
\tau_{j}
the
j^{th}
Boardman symbol.

Note that the symbols
\tau_{1},...,\tau_{r}
, form a non-increasing sequence
\tau_{1}\geq\tau_{2}\geq...\geq\tau_{r}
.
This means, for instance, that if the Jacobian of
{\bf F}
has corank one, then the Boardman symbol consists of a sequence of 1s only, and in this case we show, in the next section, that the minors
m_{j,k}^{i_{j}}
are reducible to precisely the functions
\mathcal{B}_{i,K(i-1)}
from
definition

3.1
.

The description above is usually made not in terms of functions
\Lambda^{\tau_{r}}...\Lambda^{\tau_{1}}{\bf F}
, or even their germs (loosely their ‘local representations’), but in terms of the ideals (loosely ‘linear combinations’) generated by the components of those germs, see e.g.
[
9
,
18
]
. But the procedure is equivalent, and since the ranks of ideals are actually defined by referring back to the ranks of the functions
\nabla\Lambda^{i_{r}}...\Lambda^{i_{1}}{\bf F}
, the above seems both more direct and less unnecessarily technical, at least for the purposes of describing the calculations involved. A more technically complete presentation will be given in forthcoming work
[
5
]
.

4.2
The case
\tau=1,...,1
, and the
\mathcal{B}
determinants

Let us consider more closely what these minors look like if
\nabla{\bf F}
has corank one. In this case the Boardman symbols are just a sequence of symbols
\tau_{1}=...=\tau_{r}=1
,
\tau_{r+1}=0
, for some
r\geq 1
. These are known as
\Sigma^{1}
singularities or
Morin singularities

[
18
,
19
]
.

We will show below that, if we assume a codimension
r
Morin singularity at some
{\bf x}={\bf x}_{0}
, we can locate it by either:

(a)

solving to find where the requisite minors
m^{\tau_{j}}_{j,k}
associated with the Boardman symbols
\tau_{1}=...=\tau_{r}=1
all vanish at some
{\bf x}={\bf x}_{0}
. The number of these minors grows super-factorially with
r
.

(b)

solving the conditions
\mathcal{B}_{1}=\mathcal{B}_{2,k_{1}}=...=\mathcal{B}_{r,k_{1}...k_{r-1}}=0
for all
k_{1},...,k_{r-1}\in 1,...,n,
at
{\bf x}={\bf x}_{0}
. There are
\sum_{m=0}^{r-1}n^{m}=\mbox{$\frac{1-n^{r}}{1-n}$}
of these conditions.

(c)

restricting to vector fields with
\operatorname{subrank}{\bf F}=n-1
(
definition

3.2
), and solving the conditions
\mathcal{B}_{1}=...=\mathcal{B}_{r}=0
at
{\bf x}={\bf x}_{0}
. There are only
r
of these conditions, and the
\mathcal{G}
conditions (
definition

1.1
) guarantee these have isolated roots.

In (a)-(b) the number of conditions can be expected to exceed the number of available parameters in a typical physical model, so they do not typically give a solveable system of equations. This in contrast to (c) which requires solving
r
equations in
r
parameters, so while the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions cannot detect all possible singularities, they restrict attention to those that can be solved for in closed form.

To show this we have the following series of results, which we state, and then give their proofs afterwards. Recall the definitions of the determinants
\mathcal{B}_{i}
and
\mathcal{B}_{i,K(i-1)}
from
eq.

22
-
eq.

24
. The statements “all
K(i-1)
” or “all
\mathcal{B}_{i,K(i-1)}
” are shorthand for writing that the string
K(r-1)=k_{1}...k_{r-1}
or function
\mathcal{B}_{i,k_{1}...k_{i-1}}
are taken for all
k_{j}=1,...,n
and for
j=1,...,r-1
.

Below we will want the dependence on the variable
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and parameter
{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
to be explicit, so write

{\bf F}={\bf F}({\bf x};{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}})\;,\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\mathcal{B}_{i}=\mathcal{B}_{i}({\bf x};{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}})\;.

The results below assume, for
{\bf F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, that

\displaystyle{\bf F}(0;0)=0\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\operatorname{subrank}{\bf F}(0;0)=n-1\;.

(29)

First we will need to show that if one choice of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
vanishes, then all the permutations
\mathcal{B}_{i,K(i-1)}
vanish. This is given by the following.

Lemma 4.1

(Vanishing of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
)
.

If
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=0
and
\mathcal{B}_{2,j}(0;0)=0
for some
j
, then
\nabla\mathcal{B}_{2,k}(0;0)=0
for all
k=1,...,n
.

Corollary 4.2
.

If
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=\mathcal{B}_{2}(0;0)=...=\mathcal{B}_{r}(0;0)=0
, then
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=\mathcal{B}_{2,K(1)}(0;0)=....=\mathcal{B}_{r,K(r-1)}(0;0)=0
for all
K(1),...,K(r-1)
, hence we can reduce each of the
\mathcal{B}_{i,K(i-1)}
to one choice
\mathcal{B}_{i}
for
i=1,...,r
.

We will further need to show that the vanishing of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
implies vanishing of all of the minors involved in the
i^{th}
Boardman symbol. To show this we will make use of the following.

Lemma 4.3

(Vanishing minors)
.

If
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=0
and all
\mathcal{B}_{r,K(r-1)}(0;0)=0
for some
r>1
, then any of the
n\times n
minors of any
N\times n
matrix formed from
N>n
rows of the form
\nabla\mathcal{B}_{r-1,K(r-2)}(0;0)
must vanish.

Corollary 4.4
.

If all
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=...=\mathcal{B}_{r,K(r-1)}(0;0)=0
, then any of the
n\times n
minors of any
N\times n
matrix formed from
N>n
rows

\nabla F_{j}(0;0),\;\nabla\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0),\;...,\;\nabla\mathcal{B}_{r-1,K(r-2)}(0;0)\;,

must vanish.

Lastly we will need the converse of
lemma

4.1
, namely to say that
\mathcal{B}_{i}=0

if and only if
all
\mathcal{B}_{i,K(i-1)}=0
. We want to show this in the extended space
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}
.

Lemma 4.5

(Uniqueness of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
)
.

The condition
\mathcal{G}_{r,K(r-1)}(0;0)\neq 0
for all
K(r-1)=k_{1}...k_{r-1}
, with the assumption
eq.

29
, implies that
\mathcal{B}_{i}(0;0)=0
if and only if all
\mathcal{B}_{i,K(i-1)}(0;0)=0
for
i=1,...,r
.

These allow us to prove the main result.

Theorem 4.6

(Underlying catastrophes and Thom-Boardman symbols)
.

An underlying catastrophe of codimension
r
, given by
definition

1.1
with
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=...=\mathcal{B}_{r}(0;0)=0
, is equivalent to a Thom-Boardman singularity with Boardman symbol
\tau=1,...,1
(of length
r
).

4.3
Proof of the above

Denote the linear subspace spanned by the rows of
\nabla{\bf F}(0;0)
as
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

First take the trivial case
r=0
, so there is no Boardman symbol (or the symbol is just 0), in particular the first Boardman symbol is trivially
\tau_{1}=0
. So the rank of
\nabla{\bf F}(0;0)
is
n
, i.e.
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=\det\nabla{\bf F}(0;0)\neq 0
and there is no singularity at the origin, and hence the subspace
\mathcal{T}
is
n
-dimensional.

Then let us start with two simple and generally known results that we will use below.

Lemma 4.7

(Singularity)
.

If
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=0
and
\operatorname{subrank}{\bf F}(0;0)=n-1
, the linear subspace spanned by the rows of
{\bf F}(0;0)
is an
(n-1)
-dimensional hyperplane
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof of
lemma

4.7
.

If
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=\det\nabla{\bf F}(0;0)=0
and
\operatorname{subrank}{\bf F}(0;0)=n-1
, then
\nabla{\bf F}(0;0)
has corank 1 (and hence defines a singularity with first Boardman symbol
\tau_{1}=1
),
therefore the subspace
\mathcal{T}
is
(n-1)
-dimensional.
∎

Lemma 4.8

(Equivalence of subspaces)
.

Let
M=\nabla{\bf F}
and
\tilde{M}_{j}=\nabla({\bf F}\backslash^{j}g)=\left({\nabla f_{1},...,\nabla f_{j-1},\nabla g,\nabla f_{j+1},...,\nabla f_{n}}\right)
, where
{\bf F}=\left({f_{1},...,f_{n}}\right)
for smooth functions
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
M(0;0)
and
\tilde{M}_{j}(0;0)
have corank 1 and let
\operatorname{subrank}{\bf F}(0;0)=n-1
. Then the rows of
M(0;0)
and
\tilde{M}(0;0)
span the same
(n-1)
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof of
lemma

4.8
.

Since
M(0;0)
and
\tilde{M}_{j}(0;0)
both have corank 1, their rows span
(n-1)
-dimensional subspaces, say
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{j}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, respectively. By
definition

3.2
,
\operatorname{subrank}{\bf F}(0;0)=n-1
means any
n-1
of the gradient vectors
\nabla f_{1}
, …,
\nabla f_{n}
, omitting
\nabla f_{j}
for some
j\in\left\{1,...,n\right\}
, also span a subspace of exactly
(n-1)
dimensions, so let us call this
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{j}
. Now the set
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
spanned by any
n-1
of the gradient vectors
\nabla f_{1}
, …,
\nabla f_{n}
, must be a subset both of the set
\mathcal{T}
spanned by
M(0;0)
, and the
j^{th}
set
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{j}
spanned by
\tilde{M}_{j}(0;0)
, that is
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{j}
, but since each of these is
(n-1)
-dimensional this implies
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{j}=\mathcal{T}
for every
j
.
∎

We use the dimensionality of
\mathcal{T}
to prove the main lemmas. First is to show that the vanishing of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
implies the vanishing of all permutations
\mathcal{B}_{i,K(i-1)}
. We begin with
i=1
.

Proof of
lemma

4.1
.

Since
\mathcal{B}_{2,k}={\bf F}\backslash^{k}\mathcal{B}_{1}
, then if
\mathcal{B}_{2,k}(0;0)=0
for some
k
this implies
\nabla\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)
spans an
(n-1)
-dimensional linear subspace with
\left\{\nabla f_{2},...,\nabla f_{n}\right\}
, and by
lemma

4.8
this subspace must be
\mathcal{T}
, i.e. the same linear subspace spanned by the rows of
\nabla{\bf F}(0;0)
. Since
lemma

4.1
also assumes
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=0
,
lemma

4.7
applies, hence
\nabla\mathcal{B}_{2,k}(0;0)=0
for all
k=1,...,n
.
∎

Then extend this to
i>1
.

Proof of
corollary

4.2
.

If
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=\mathcal{B}_{2}(0;0)=...=\mathcal{B}_{r}(0;0)=0
, then applying
lemma

4.1
at each order
i=2,...,r,
implies that
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=\mathcal{B}_{2,K(1)}=...=\mathcal{B}_{r,K(r-1)}(0;0)=0
for every
K(1)\in[1,n]
, …,
K(r-1)\in[1,n]^{r-1}
. Hence at each order we can reduce
\mathcal{B}_{i,K(i-1)}
to one choice
\mathcal{B}_{i}
for
i=1,...,r
.
∎

The next step is to relate this to the Boardman symbols by showing that if
\mathcal{B}_{i}
vanishes, then so do the numerous minors in the Thom-Boardman calculations. Again we show this first for
i=1
.

Proof of
lemma

4.3
.

If
\mathcal{B}_{r,K(r-1)}(0;0)=0
for all
K(r-1)\in[1,...,n]^{r-1}
, then since
\mathcal{B}_{r,k_{1}...k_{r-2}k_{r-1}}=\det(\nabla{\bf F}\backslash^{k_{r-1}}\nabla\mathcal{B}_{r-1,k_{1}...k_{r-2}})
, this implies by
lemma

4.8
that we have
\nabla\mathcal{B}_{r-1,K(r-2)}(0;0)\in\mathcal{T}
for all
K(r-2)=k_{1}...k_{r-2}\in[1,n]^{r-2}
.

Lemma

4.3
also assumes
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=0
, so
lemma

4.7
applies, so any
N\times n
matrix formed from
N>n
rows
\nabla\mathcal{B}_{r-1,K(r-2)}(0;0)
has rank at most
n-1
, and any of its
n\times n
minors vanish.
∎

Then extend this to
i>1
.

Proof of
corollary

4.4
.

If all
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=...=\mathcal{B}_{r,K(r-1)}(0;0)=0
, then all
\nabla B_{1}(0;0)=...=\nabla\mathcal{B}_{r-1,K(r-2)}(0;0)\in\mathcal{T}
, and moreover
lemma

4.7
applies, so any
N\times n
matrix formed from
N>n
rows

\nabla F_{j}(0;0),\nabla\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0),...,\nabla\mathcal{B}_{r-1,K(r-2)}(0;0),

has rank at most
n-1
, and any of its
n\times n
minors vanish.
∎

It is slightly longer to prove the converse to
lemma

4.1
.

Proof of
lemma

4.5
.

Denote the gradient derivative on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}
as
\square=(\nabla_{{\bf x}},\nabla_{\alpha})
.
Say
{\bf F}(0;0)=\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=0
and
\det(\square{\bf F}(0;0),\square\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0))\neq 0
, so, by the inverse function theorem, the root
(0,0)
is isolated.

Now say
{\bf F}(0;0)=\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=...=\mathcal{B}_{r,1...1}(0;0)=0
, then
lemma

4.1
implies
\mathcal{B}_{i,K(i-1)}(0;0)=0
for all
K(i-1)=k_{1}...k_{i-1}
. Assume
\mathcal{G}_{r,K(r-1)}(0;0)\neq 0
, i.e.
\det(\square{\bf F}(0;0),\square\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0),\square\mathcal{B}_{2,k_{1}}(0;0),...,\square\mathcal{B}_{r,k_{1}...k_{r-1}}(0;0))\neq 0
for all
k_{1},...,k_{r-1}
, so, by the inverse function theorem, the root
(0,0)
is isolated in
({\bf x},{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}})
space, and hence these roots must be the same or all
k_{1},...,k_{r-1}
, hence
\mathcal{B}_{i}(0;0)=0

if and only if
all
\mathcal{B}_{i,K(i-1)}(0;0)=0
for
i=1,...,r
, local to the origin.

∎

This brings us to the final proof of
theorem

4.6
, where we find precisely how the conditions
\mathcal{B}_{i}=0
appear (and are repeated many times over) in the procedure to obtain the Boardman symbols. To make this explicit we will proceed one codimension at a time through
definition

4.1
.

4.4
Proof of
theorem

4.6

First take the trivial case
r=0
, so there is no Boardman symbol (or the symbol is just 0), this means
\tau_{1}=0
, so the rank of
\nabla{\bf F}(0;0)
is
n
, i.e.
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=\det\nabla{\bf F}(0;0)\neq 0
and there is no singularity at the origin.

Now assume the first Boardman symbol is
\tau_{1}=1
. Then
\nabla{\bf F}(0;0)
has corank 1 and defines a singularity, and
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=\det\nabla{\bf F}(0;0)=0
. If the second Boardman symbol is
\tau_{2}=0
so the complete symbol is just
\tau=1
, and defines a
fold
, then the
(n+1)\times n
matrix
\nabla\Delta^{1}{\bf F}(0;0)=\left({\nabla{\bf F}(0;0),\nabla\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)}\right)
has rank
n
, so at least one of its
n\times n
minors must be nonzero; we will look more closely at those minors in the next step.

Assume instead that
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}=1
. Then
\nabla{\bf F}(0;0)
has corank 1, and moreover
\nabla\Delta^{1}{\bf F}(0;0)
has corank 1, so all of the
n\times n
minors of
\nabla\Delta^{1}{\bf F}(0;0)
are zero.
Those minors (with the exception of the minor
\nabla{\bf F}(0;0)

\mathcal{B}_{2,k}
for
k=1,...,n
. Let

\displaystyle m^{1}_{2,k}=\mathcal{B}_{2,k}(0;0)\quad{\rm for}\quad k=1,...,n,\quad{\rm and}\quad m^{1}_{2,n+1}=\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)\;.

(30)

Hence if
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}=1
then
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=\mathcal{B}_{2,k}(0;0)=0
for all
k=1,...,n
. Conversely, if
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=\mathcal{B}_{2}(0;0)=0
, then by
eq.

22
we have
\mathcal{B}_{2,k}(0;0)=0
for all
k=1,...,n
, implying
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}=1
.

Now if
\tau_{3}=0
we are done and the singularity is a
cusp
, then the

matrix
\nabla\Delta^{1}\Delta^{1}{\bf F}=\left\{\nabla{\bf F},\nabla\mathcal{B}_{1},\nabla\mathcal{B}_{2,1},...,\nabla\mathcal{B}_{2,n},\nabla\mathcal{B}_{1}\right\}
must have rank
n
, implying that at least one of the
n\times n
minors of
\nabla\left({\Delta^{1}\Delta^{1}{\bf F}(0;0)}\right)
is nonzero; again we will look more closely at these in the next step.

Assume instead that
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}=\tau_{3}=1
. Now
\nabla{\bf F}(0;0)
,
\nabla\Delta^{1}{\bf F}(0;0)
, and
\nabla\Delta^{1}\Delta^{1}{\bf F}(0;0)
all have corank 1, so all of the
n\times n
minors of
\nabla\Delta^{1}\Delta^{1}{\bf F}(0;0)
are zero.
Recalling

\nabla(\Delta^{1}\Delta^{1}{\bf F})=\left({\nabla{\bf F},\nabla\mathcal{B}_{1},\nabla\mathcal{B}_{2,1},...,\nabla\mathcal{B}_{2,n},\nabla\mathcal{B}_{1}}\right)\;,

these minors number
\frac{(2(n+1))!}{n!(n+2)!}
(the binomial coefficient for choosing
n
rows from
2(n+1)
).
One of these is
\mathcal{B}_{1}=\det\nabla{\bf F}
, another

of them are the functions
\mathcal{B}_{2,1},...,\mathcal{B}_{2,n+1}
repeated twice (because
\mathcal{B}_{1}
is repeated twice in
\nabla\Delta^{1}\Delta^{1}{\bf F}
).
Another
n^{2}
are the determinants of Jacobian matrices in which some

row of
\nabla{\bf F}
is swapped for one row
\nabla m^{1}_{2,k_{1}}
, with

, and these are precisely the functions
\mathcal{B}_{3,k_{1}k_{2}}
, for

. Let

\displaystyle m^{1}_{3,l(k_{1},k_{2})}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{3,k_{1}k_{2}}(0;0)\quad{\rm for}\quad k_{1},k_{2}=1,...,n,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\;\;{\rm with}\quad l(k_{1},k_{2})=k_{1}+n(k_{2}-1)\;,

(31a)

\displaystyle m^{1}_{3,j}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{2,j}(0;0)\quad\;{\rm for}\;\;\;\;\quad j=n^{2}+1,...,n^{2}+n\;,

(31b)

\displaystyle m^{1}_{3,j}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{2,j}(0;0)\quad\;{\rm for}\;\;\;\;\quad j=n^{2}+n+1,...,n^{2}+2n\;,

(31c)

\displaystyle m^{1}_{3,j}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)\quad\;\;\;{\rm for}\;\;\;\;\quad j=n^{2}+2n+1,...,n^{2}+2n+2\;,

(31d)

\displaystyle m^{1}_{3,j}

\displaystyle=0\qquad\quad\;\;{\rm for}\;\qquad j=n^{2}+n+3,...,\mbox{$\frac{(2(n+1))!}{n!(n+2)!}$}\;.

(31e)

We have claimed in the last line that the remaining

for

\nabla{\bf F}
and

4.3
this has rank

, so all its

So
\mathcal{B}_{3,k_{1}k_{2}}(0;0)=0
for all

\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=\mathcal{B}_{2,k_{1}}(0;0)=0

).
Hence if
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}=\tau_{3}=1
then
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=\mathcal{B}_{2,k_{1}}(0;0)=\mathcal{B}_{3,k_{1}k_{2}}(0;0)=0
for all
k_{1},k_{2}=1,...,n
. Conversely, if
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=\mathcal{B}_{2}(0;0)=\mathcal{B}_{3}(0;0)=0
, then by

4.2
,
\mathcal{B}_{2,k_{1}}(0;0)=\mathcal{B}_{3,k_{1}k_{2}}(0;0)=0
for all

, implying
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}=\tau_{3}=1
.

Now if
\tau_{4}=0
the singularity is a
swallowtail
, and the
\left({2(n+1)+\chi}\right)\times n
matrix

\nabla\Delta^{1}\Delta^{1}\Delta^{1}{\bf F}=\left\{\nabla{\bf F},\nabla\mathcal{B}_{1},\nabla m^{1}_{2,1},...,m^{1}_{2,n+1},\nabla m^{1}_{3,1},...,\nabla m^{1}_{3,\chi}\right\}

This does not mean that the ‘underlying catastrophes’ defined in
[
12
]
are equivalent to the Thom-Boardman singularities, indeed they are not. But they are consistent in that a codimension
r
underlying catastrophe (where
{\bf F}(0;0)=\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=...=\mathcal{B}_{r}(0;0)=0
) is a codimension
r

Morin
singularity, i.e. the conditions are

to define a zero of
{\bf F}
lying at such a singularity of
{\bf F}
viewed as a mapping.

Importantly in

1.1
we place a further restriction of ‘fullness’, which ultimately is not
necessary
to define a singularity in the sense of
{\bf F}
as a mapping, but ensures that
{\bf F}
is determined such that the conditions
{\bf F}(0;0)=\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=...=\mathcal{B}_{r}(0;0)=0
are solveable. We complete this section by showing how this follows from the
\mathcal{G}_{r,K(r-1)}
conditions.

4.5
The
\mathcal{G}
conditions for being ‘full’

The only place we have needed to make reference to the parameter dependence of
{\bf F}
above is in the proof of
lemma

4.5
. Taking
{\bf F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denote again the gradient operator as
\square=(\nabla_{{\bf x}},\nabla_{\alpha})
.
The conditions
\mathcal{G}_{r,K(r-1)}(0;0)\neq 0
simply state that the extended Jacobian

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{r}=\frac{\partial({\bf F},\mathcal{B}_{1},...,\mathcal{B}_{r})}{\partial({\bf x},\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{r})}

(33)

has full rank
n+r
at
({\bf x},{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}})=(0,0)
, as do its variants
\mathcal{G}_{r,k_{1}...k_{r-1}}
for the alternative permutations of the
\mathcal{B}_{i,k_{1}...k_{r-1}}
. At this point, the gradient vectors
\square F_{1}
, …,
\square F_{n}
,
\square\mathcal{B}_{1}
, …,
\square\mathcal{B}_{r}
, span an
(n+r)
-dimensional linear subspace
\mathcal{T}_{{\bf x},\alpha}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}
, which implies by the implicit function theorem that the problem
{\bf F}=\mathcal{B}_{1}=...=\mathcal{B}_{r}=0
is solvable at isolated points
({\bf x},{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}})=({\bf x}_{*},\alpha_{1*},...,\alpha_{r*})
.

We can illustrate this using the primary form of a codimension
r
singularity given in
eq.

1
, namely

\displaystyle{\bf F}=

\displaystyle\Big(\;f(x_{1},{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}})+\underline{\tau}\cdot\underline{x}\;,\;\lambda_{2}x_{2}\;,\;...\;,\;\lambda_{n}x_{n}\;\Big)

\displaystyle{\rm where}

\displaystyle\qquad f(x_{1})=x_{1}^{r+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}x_{1}^{i-1}\;,

with
\underline{\tau}\cdot\underline{x}=\tau_{2}x_{2}+...+\tau_{n}x_{n}
, and where the
\tau_{i}
and
\lambda_{i}
are non-zero constants.

Calculating
\mathcal{B}_{r}
from
eq.

22
, and the function
\mathcal{G}_{r}
from
eq.

, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{r}

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{2}...\lambda_{n})^{r}f^{(r)}(x_{1})\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{r}

\displaystyle=c_{1}(\lambda_{2}...\lambda_{n})^{r+1}f^{(r+1)}(x_{1})\;,

(34)

and calculating
\mathcal{B}_{r,k_{1}...k_{r-1}}
from
eq.

24
and
\mathcal{G}_{r,k_{1}...k_{r-1}}
from

, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{r,k_{1}...k_{r-1}}

\displaystyle=c_{2}\lambda_{2}^{p_{2}}\lambda_{n}^{p_{n}}\tau_{2}^{q_{2}}...\tau_{n}^{q_{n}}\mathcal{B}_{r}\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{r,k_{1}...k_{r-1}}

\displaystyle=c_{3}\lambda_{2}^{s_{2}}...\lambda_{n}^{s_{n}}\tau_{2}^{t_{2}}...\tau_{n}^{t_{n}}\mathcal{G}_{r}\;.

The actual values of the various numerical constants

and

are not important, and depend quite complicatedly on the values of

.

and connect it with the theory of Thom-Boardman singularities. The hope is that these ideas make the application of Thom’s ideas to vector fields and spatiotemporal systems much more practical, by providing new ways to find and characterize high codimension bifurcation points.

A number of examples were given in
[
12
]
to illustrate the importance of being
full
.
A non-trivial example illustrating the importance of satisfying
eq.

27
, even when
eq.

3
holds, is given by
{\bf F}=(x_{1}+x_{2}^{2},x_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\alpha_{2}+x_{2}^{2}+kx_{1})
. If
k_{1}=0
then
\operatorname{subrank}{\bf F}(0;0)=0
and we find
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0;0)=\mathcal{B}_{21}(0;0)=0\neq\mathcal{B}_{22}(0;0)
, violating
lemma

4.1
, while the fullness conditions
\mathcal{G}_{2,1}(0;0)\neq 0
and
\mathcal{G}_{2,2}(0;0)\neq 0
are still satisfied. If
k_{1}\neq 0
then
eq.

27
holds. One then finds that there is a fold at the origin as
\alpha_{2}
passes through zero, while the event that unfolds with
\alpha_{1}
is degenerate (not full), and there is no higher codimension catastrophe since
\mathcal{B}_{21}(0;0),\mathcal{B}_{22}(0;0)\neq 0
.

We have claimed that any bifurcation has an underlying catastrophe, and established the validity of the conjecture with a suitable definition that relates to zeros of vector field encountering certain corank 1 singularities.
As already noted in
[
12
]
, underlying catastrophes are not equivalence classes, and each one may represent several classes of bifurcations with different stability properties. The idea is that the concept of an underlying catastrophe is used to
find
a bifurcation point in a vector field, after which standard bifurcation analysis can be carried out to properly classify it, if required.

Here we have described more the relation of underlying catastrophes to singularities (rather than bifurcations), and this proves to be rather different, in that there appear to be singularities within Thom’s classification that cannot be identified with any underlying catastrophe, because they require too many conditions to identify them. This perhaps rules out some singularities as unfindable by the solution of implicit conditions, but what singularities this applies to, and what this means for their role in applications, remains to be studied.

At present this analysis only applies to corank 1 cases. For these, the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions reduce the number of minors needed to calculate the Boardman symbols, which increases factorially with the codimension
r
, to just
\mathcal{B}_{1},...,\mathcal{B}_{r}
.
For what other singularities or bifurcations is this possible? It seems obvious, for example, that corank 2 singularities should be related to
umbilic
catastrophes, and the first steps towards extending the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions for these has been taken in
[
13
]
.
