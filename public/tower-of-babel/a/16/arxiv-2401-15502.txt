Differentially private Bayesian tests
Abhisek Chakraborty

Abstract
Differential privacy has emerged as an significant cornerstone in the realm of scientific hypothesis testing utilizing confidential data. In reporting scientific discoveries, Bayesian tests are widely adopted since they effectively circumnavigate the key criticisms of P-values, namely, lack of interpretability and inability to quantify evidence in support of the competing hypotheses. In this article, we present a novel differentially private Bayesian hypotheses testing framework that arise naturally under a principled data generative mechanism, inherently maintaining the interpretability of the resulting inferences. Furthermore, by focusing on differentially private Bayes factors based on widely used test statistics, we circumvent the need to model the complete data generative mechanism and ensure substantial computational benefits. We also provide a set of sufficient conditions to establish results on Bayes factor consistency under the proposed framework. The utility of the devised technology is showcased via several numerical experiments.



Keywords:
Bayes Factors, Bayes Factor Consistency, Interpretable ML, Reproducibility crisis, Scientific reporting

1
Introduction

Hypothesis testing is an indispensable tool to answer scientific questions in the context of clinical trials, bioinformatics, social sciences, etc. The data within such domains often involves sensitive and private information pertaining to individuals. Researchers often bear legal obligations to safeguard the privacy of such data. In this context, differential privacy
(
Dwork 2006
)
has emerged as a compelling framework for ensuring privacy in statistical analyses with confidential data. Consequently, differentially private versions of numerous well-established hypothesis tests have been developed, although exclusively from a frequentist view point. This encompasses private adaptations of test of binomial proportions
(
Awan and Slavković 2018
)
, linear regression
(
Alabi and Vadhan 2023
)
, goodness of fit
(
Kwak et al. 2021
)
, analysis of variance
(
Swanberg et al. 2019
)
, high-dimensional normal means
(
Narayanan 2022
)
, to name a few. Differentially private versions of common non-parametric tests
(
Couch et al. 2019
)
, permutation tests
(
Kim and Schrab 2023
)
, etc., have emerged as well. Another recent line of work proposes to automatically create private versions of the existing frequentist tests in a black-box fashion
(
Kazan et al. 2023
;
Peña and Barrientos 2023
)
.

In this article, we intend to complement the existing literature via providing a novel generalised framework for differentially private Bayesian testing. This is genuinely crucial since P-values are routine criticised for it’s lack of transparency, inability to quantify evidence in support of the null hypothesis, and inability to measure the importance of a result. Refer to the American Statistical Association statement on statistical significance and P-values
Wasserstein and Lazar 2016
for further discussions on this. On the other hand, Bayes factors
(
Kass and Raftery 1995
;
Jeffreys 1961
;
Morey and Rouder 2011
;
Rouder et al. 2009
;
Wagenmakers et al. 2010
;
Johnson et al. 2023a
)
quantify the relative evidence in favor of competing hypotheses, effectively addressing the persistent criticisms of P-values. This automatically calls for development of differentially private Bayes Factors to test the scientific hypotheses of interest with confidentiality guarantees.

Prior to presenting the proposed methodology, we provide a concise overview of pertinent concepts in Bayesian hypothesis testing and differential privacy, laying the groundwork for subsequent discussions.

1.1
Bayesian hypothesis testing

Suppose we intend to test the null hypothesis
\mbox{H}_{0}:\theta\in\Theta_{0}
against the alternative hypothesis
\mbox{H}_{1}:\theta\in\Theta_{1}
.
The Bayes Factor
(
Kass and Raftery 1995
;
Jeffreys 1961
;
Morey and Rouder 2011
;
Rouder et al. 2009
;
Wagenmakers et al. 2010
;
Johnson et al. 2023a
)
in favour of the alternative hypothesis
\mbox{H}_{1}
against the null hypothesis
\mbox{H}_{0}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mbox{BF}_{10}=\frac{\mbox{\rm P}(\mbox{\rm H}_{1}{\,|\,}x)}{\mbox{\rm P}(\mbox{\rm H}_{0}{\,|\,}x)}=\frac{m_{1}(x)}{m_{0}(x)}\times\frac{\mbox{\rm P}(\mbox{\rm H}_{1})}{\mbox{\rm P}(\mbox{\rm H}_{0})},

(1.1)

where
\mbox{\rm P}(\mbox{\rm H}_{i}{\,|\,}x)
is the posterior probability of
\mbox{\rm H}_{i}
given the data
x
,
\mbox{\rm P}(\mbox{\rm H}_{i})
is the prior probability assigned to
\mbox{\rm H}_{i}
, and
m_{i}(x)
denotes the marginal probability assigned to the data under
\mbox{\rm H}_{i},\ i=0,1
. The marginal densities are computed via

m_{i}(x)=\int_{\Theta_{i}}\pi(x{\,|\,}\theta)\pi_{i}(\theta)d\theta,

where
\pi(x{\,|\,}\theta)
is the data generative model given the parameter
\theta
, and
\pi_{i}(\theta)
is the prior density for
\theta
under
\mbox{\rm H}_{i},\ i=0,1
. Bayes factor effectively addresses the persistent criticisms regarding lack of interpretability of P-values
(
Wasserstein and Lazar 2016
)
in reporting outcomes of hypothesis tests, since it directly quantifies the relative evidence in favor of the competing hypotheses
\mbox{H}_{0}
and
\mbox{H}_{1}
. Noteworthy, the value of a Bayes factor depends on the choices of
\pi_{i}(\theta),\ i=0,1
, and it is generally difficult to justify any single default choice. Moreover, the expression of
m_{i}(x),\ i=0,1
may not be available in closed, and require computing a potentially high-dimensional numerical integrals.

To circumnavigate the aforementioned issues with Bayes factors,
Johnson 2005
proposed to define Bayes factors based on common test statistics, and put priors directly on the non-centrality parameter of the test statistics to specify the various competing hypotheses. For example, suppose we observe data
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\sim\mbox{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
, and want to test
\mbox{H}_{0}:\mu=0
against
\mbox{H}_{1}:\mu\neq 0
. In this context, the
t
-statistic
t=\frac{\sqrt{n}\bar{X}}{\hat{\sigma}}
follows a (non-central)
t_{n-1}(\delta)
distribution with non centrality parameter
\delta\geq 0
and degrees of freedom
n-1
, where
\bar{X}
and
\hat{\sigma}
are the mean and standard deviation of the observed data. The non centrality parameter
\delta=0
under
\mbox{H}_{0}
and
\delta>0
under
\mbox{H}_{1}
. So, we can put priors directly on the non-centrality parameter
\delta
under
\mbox{H}_{1}
, and conduct a Bayesian testing. Moreover, if we utilise non-local priors
(
Johnson and Rossell 2010a
)
on
\delta
under
\mbox{H}_{1}
instead of local priors, not only the expression of the marginal likelihoods are often available in closed form, but also it ensures quick accumulation of evidence in favor of both true
\mbox{H}_{0}
and true
\mbox{H}_{1}
. Data-driven approaches for the prior hyper-parameter tuning
(
Johnson et al. 2023a
;
Datta et al. 2023
)
were subsequently suggested, adding to the objectivity of such Bayesian testing methodology.

1.2
Differential privacy

Differential privacy
(
Dwork 2006
;
Baraheem and Yao 2022
)
presents a principled framework for safeguarding sensitive information in confidential data utilized for statistical analyses. It achieves this by ensuring that the probability of any specific output of the statistical procedure is nearly the same, regardless of the data belonging any one specific individual in the database. In this context, databases that vary only with respect to the data corresponding to a single individual are termed
neighboring
databases.

Definition 1

(Differential Privacy,
(
Dwork 2006
)
)
.

A randomized algorithm
f:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{S}
is
\varepsilon
-differentially private if for all possible outcomes
\mbox{\rm S}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
and
for any two neighbouring databases
\mbox{\rm D},\mbox{\rm D}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
, we have

\mbox{\rm P}[f(\mbox{\rm D})\in\mbox{\rm S}]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\mbox{\rm P}[f(\mbox{\rm D}^{\prime})\in\mbox{\rm S}].

The
privacy budget

\varepsilon
quantifies the degree of privacy protection, with smaller values of
\varepsilon
indicating a stronger privacy guarantee. In particular,
\varepsilon
-differential privacy ensures that the privacy loss is constrained by
e^{\varepsilon}
with a probability of
1
. Every differentially private algorithm achieves this via incorporating an element of randomness. The most widely adopted techniques include the Gaussian mechanism and the Laplace mechanism
(
Dwork 2006
)
. These privacy mechanisms involves introducing a privacy noise sampled from a Gaussian or Laplace distribution to the result of the query that requires privacy protection. The measure of how much a the modification of a single row in a database can influence the output of a query is referred to as
global sensitivity
.

Definition 2

(Global sensitivity)
.

The global sensitivity of a function
f
is

\mbox{\rm GS}_{f}=\underset{\mbox{\rm D},\mbox{\rm D}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}}{\textup{max}}\ |f(\mbox{\rm D})-f(\mbox{\rm D}^{\prime})|,

where
D
and
\mbox{\rm D}^{\prime}
are neighboring databases.

The scale of the Gaussian or Laplace distribution employed to introduce the privacy noise, is contingent on both the privacy budget
\varepsilon
and the global sensitivity
\mbox{\rm GS}_{f}
. Given any function
f
, the
Laplace mechanism
is defined as

\tilde{f}(\mbox{\rm D})=f(\mbox{\rm D})+\eta,

where
\eta
is a privacy noise drawn from
\mbox{Laplace}(0,\mbox{\rm GS}_{f}/\varepsilon)
, and
\mbox{\rm GS}_{f}
is the global sensitivity of
f
. The Laplace mechanism ensures
\varepsilon
-differentially privacy. Similarly, given any function
f
, the Gaussian mechanism is defined as

\tilde{f}(\mbox{\rm D})=f(\mbox{\rm D})+\eta,

where
\eta
is drawn from
\mbox{\rm Normal}(0,\ (\mbox{\rm GS}_{f}/\varepsilon)^{2})
, and
\mbox{\rm GS}_{f}
is the global sensitivity of
f
. The Gaussian mechanism too ensures
\varepsilon
-differentially privacy. The Gaussian mechanism finds its utility in situations where using Laplace noise with heavier tails might not be appropriate. In this article, we pick the Laplace mechanism for the sake of expositions, but the proposed methodologies work with Gaussian mechanism too.

1.3
Our contributions

The key contributions in the article are three-fold. Firstly, to the best of our knowledge, we provide the first-ever differentially private Bayesian testing framework. This is important in the light of the American Statistical Association statement on statistical significance and P-values
(
Wasserstein and Lazar 2016
)
that cautions against making scientific decisions solely on the basis of P-value. This caution arises from its lack of transparency, inability to quantify evidence in support of the null hypothesis, and incapacity to measure the importance of a result. Although Bayes factor-based tests are instrumental in circumnavigating the key issues regarding P-values, extending them to differentially private setup while preserving their inherent interpretability, requires substantial work. To that end, we carefully embed common strategies in differentially private testing, such as sub-sample and aggregate, truncated test functions, etc., within the proposed data generative mechanism. This ensures that the proposed Bayesian testing procedures arise naturally from the data generative models, maintaining the interpretability of the inferences.

Secondly, we introduced differentially private Bayes factors that rely on widely used test statistics, extending
Johnson 2005
in a private setup. We place priors directly on the non-centrality parameter of the test statistic and utilize data-driven methods for tuning the prior hyper-parameters. This circumvents the need for modeling the complete data generative mechanism and placing priors on potentially high-dimensional model parameters. This, in turn, yields key computational benefits when computing marginal likelihoods, typically involving intractable integrals under a fully parametric formulation of Bayesian testing. We also provide a set of sufficient conditions to establish results on Bayes factor consistency
(
Chib and Kuffner 2016
;
Chatterjee et al. 2020
)
under the proposed framework.

Thirdly, we provide a general template to develop size
\alpha
differentially private Bayesian tests under specific setups and provide numerical schemes for data-driven hyper-parameter tuning. Examples under particular hypothesis testing problems are presented in the numerical experiments section. However, the proposed schemes are applicable beyond the Bayesian tests discussed in the current article.

Rest of the article is organised as follows. Section
2
introduces the proposed framework for differentially private Bayesian testing in complete generality, presents the key features of the methodology, and discusses schemes for the hyper-parameter tuning under the proposed technology. In Section
3
, we introduce differentially private Bayesian tests based on common test statistics, and discuss their asymptotic properties under various common hypothesis testing problems. In Section
4
, we study the numerical efficacy of the proposed technology in different hypothesis testing problems with practical utility. Finally, we conclude with a discussion.

2
General framework

In this section, the objective is to lay down a hierarchical model equipped with carefully chosen priors on the parameter of interest to specify competing hypotheses, which naturally gives rise to the proposed differentially private Bayesian tests. Notably, this attribute remains elusive in the existing literature and it hinders the interpretability of the resulting inference. In particular, many existing differentially private frequentist testing procedures
(
Peña and Barrientos 2023
;
Kazan et al. 2023
)
rely on an ad-hoc sampling and aggregation scheme coupled with a test statistic truncation step, to formulate the desired tests. Despite the empirical success of these methods, a significant deficiency stems from the lack of a principled probabilistic interpretation, i.e, these methods does not enable us to to quantify evidence in favor of the null and alternative hypotheses. We specifically tackle this issue in the subsequent discussions.

2.1
Hierarchical model and prior specification

For a positive integer
t
, denote
[t]:\,=\{1,\ldots,t\}
. Suppose we observe data
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
from a probability distribution having density
\pi(\cdot\mid\theta),\ \theta\in\Theta
. We then randomly divide the data into
M
partitions
\mathbf{x}^{(i)},i\in[M]
.
We can assume that the data in each of the
M
partitions arise from

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\sim\prod_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\pi(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{j}\mid\theta^{(i)}),\quad\text{independently for}\ i\in[M],

(2.1)

such that
\sum_{i=1}^{M}n_{i}=n
. The above data generating scheme comes in handy to formalize the
sample and aggregate
scheme under the proposed setup.

Next, we posit the priors on the parameter of interest
\theta
under the competing hypotheses. To that end, we assume that

\displaystyle\theta^{(i)}\mid\tau_{0,i},\tau_{1,i}\sim(1-\omega)\ \pi_{0}(\theta^{(i)}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2})\ +\ \omega\ \pi_{1}(\theta^{(i)}\mid\tau_{1,i}^{2})\quad\text{under}\ \mbox{H}_{0},

\displaystyle\theta^{(i)}\mid\tau_{0,i},\tau_{1,i}\sim\ \omega\ \pi_{0}(\theta^{(i)}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2})\ +\ (1-\omega)\ \pi_{1}(\theta^{(i)}\mid\tau_{1,i}^{2})\quad\text{under}\ \mbox{H}_{1},

(2.2)

independently for
i\in[M]
, where
\{\pi_{i}(\cdot),\ i=0,1\}
are the prior probability distributions,
(\tau_{0,i},\tau_{1,i})
are prior hyper-parameters, and
\omega\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
is a hyper-parameter that ensures that the Bayes factor for testing
\mbox{H}_{0}
against
\mbox{H}_{1}
is bounded. If the Bayes factor for testing
\mbox{H}_{0}
against
\mbox{H}_{1}
were to be unbounded, the global sensitivity parameter of the Bayes factor becomes unbounded too. An immediate fix against it would involve truncating the log Bayes factor beyond an interval of the form
[-a,a],a>0
. But this fix will ruin the inherent probabilistic interpretation of the Bayes factors. Upon closer look, truncating the log Bayes factor in principle means that the maximum evidence that we can obtain in favor of
\mbox{H}_{0}
(or
\mbox{H}_{1}
) against
\mbox{H}_{1}
(or
\mbox{H}_{0}
) is bounded from above – we precisely impose this constraint a-priori via the hierarchical specification (
2.1
). In summary, the mixture priors on
\{\theta^{(i)},\ i\in[M]\}
under the competing hypotheses are instrumental in formalizing the
truncation
scheme necessary to ensure that the global sensitivity parameter of the privacy mechanism is bounded, while preserving the interpretability of the resulting Bayes factors. One may criticise the appearance of the parameter
\omega
in our formulation. To that end, we carefully introduce an asymptotic regime to analyse the proposed methodology, so that the effect of
\omega
washes away as the sample size diverges to
\infty
.

The model and prior specified in equations (
2.1
)-(
2.1
), together with the assumption that

\displaystyle\mbox{P}(\mbox{H}_{0})=\mbox{P}(\mbox{H}_{1})=0.5,

(2.3)

apriori, completely describe our generative model of interest.

2.2
Key properties

For each partition
\mathbf{x}^{(i)},\ i\in[M]
, we record the notations for the integrals

m_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{k,i}^{2})=\int_{\Theta}\bigg[\prod_{j=1}^{n_{j}}\pi(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{j}\mid\theta^{(i)})\bigg]\ \pi_{k}(\theta^{(i)}\mid\tau_{k,i}^{2})\ d\theta^{(i)},\quad k=0,1.

For
k\in\{0,1\}
, the quantity
m_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{k,i}^{2})
can be interpreted as the marginal likelihood of the data partition
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
under the hypothesis
\mbox{H}_{k}
when
\omega=0
. Under this set up, as we eluded to earlier, the Bayes factor for testing
\mbox{H}_{0}
against
\mbox{H}_{1}
can be unbounded, and consequently the global sensitivity parameter of the privacy mechanism is unbounded too. This renders the development of differentially private Bayesian tests non-trivial, and throughout the article we shall assume that the hyper-parameter
\omega>0
. Under the alternative hypothesis
\mbox{H}_{1}
as in (
2.1
)-(
2.3
), the marginal distribution of the data partition
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
is expressed as

m^{t}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2},\tau_{1,i}^{2},\omega)=\omega\ m_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2})\ +\ (1-\omega)\ m_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{1,i}^{2}),\quad i\in[M];

and under the null hypothesis
\mbox{H}_{0}
, it is given by:

m^{t}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2},\tau_{1,i}^{2},\omega)=(1-\omega)\ m_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2})\ +\ \omega\ m_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{1,i}^{2}),\quad i\in[M].

Consequently, in light of the data partition
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
, the Bayes factor against the null hypothesis
\mbox{H}_{0}
is expressed as

\displaystyle\mbox{BF}^{t}_{10}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2},\tau_{1,i}^{2},\omega)=\frac{\omega+(1-\omega)\big[m_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{1,i}^{2})\ /\ m_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2})\big]}{(1-\omega)+\omega\big[m_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{1,i}^{2})\ /\ m_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2})\big]},\quad i\in[M].

(2.4)

We proceed by listing out the key properties of the the Bayes factor against the null hypothesis
\mbox{BF}^{t}_{10}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2},\tau_{1,i}^{2},\omega)
for the data partition
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
\omega\in(0,1/2)
,
\mbox{\rm BF}^{t}_{10}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2},\tau_{1,i}^{2},\omega)
is bounded between
[\frac{\omega}{1-\omega},\frac{1-\omega}{\omega}]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Under (
2.1
)-(
2.3
), the Bayes Factor against the null hypothesis
\mbox{H}_{0}
combined across all the partitions
\mathbf{x}^{(i)},\ i\in[M]
is expressed as

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm BF}^{t}_{10}(\mathbf{x}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2},\tau_{1,i}^{2},\omega)=\prod_{i=1}^{M}\mbox{\rm BF}^{t}_{10}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2},\tau_{1,i}^{2},\omega).

Corollary 2.2.1
.

Lemma
6.1
together with Lemma
6.2
implies that, the combined Bayes Factor against the null hypothesis
\mbox{H}_{0}
across all the partitions
\mathbf{x}^{(i)},\ i\in[M]
,
\mbox{\rm BF}^{t}_{10}(\mathbf{x}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2},\tau_{1,i}^{2},\omega)
is bounded between
\left(\frac{\omega}{1-\omega}\right)^{M}
and
\left(\frac{1-\omega}{\omega}\right)^{M}
for
\omega\in(0,1/2)
.

In the sequel, we construct differential private Bayesian tests based on the randomised mechanism, denoted by
f
, that map a data set
\mathbf{x}
to the average log Bayes factor

\displaystyle f_{10}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{M}\log\mbox{BF}^{t}_{10}(\mathbf{x}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2},\tau_{1,i}^{2},\omega)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\log\mbox{BF}^{t}_{10}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2},\tau_{1,i}^{2},\omega).

(2.5)

We take refuge to common differential privacy mechanism of adding calibrated privacy noise to the randomised mechanism
f
to ensure differential privacy of the inferential procedure. For every data set
\mathbf{x}
, since the log Bayes factor in each of the partitions
\log\mbox{BF}^{t}_{10}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2},\tau_{1,i}^{2},\omega)
is bounded between
[-a,a]
with

a=-\log(\omega/1-\omega),

the global sensitivity of
\log\mbox{BF}^{t}_{10}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\tau_{0,i}^{2},\tau_{1,i}^{2},\omega)
is
2a
. Consequently, the global sensitivity of
\mbox{GS}_{f}
of the log Bayes factor averaged over the partitions, i.e of
f
, is
(2a/M)
. We now have all the necessary ingredients to introduce our privacy preserving mechanism.

To that end, let
\mathcal{D}
denote the collection of all possible of data sets. We consider a random mechanism
\mbox{H}:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
expressed as

\mbox{H}_{10}(\mathbf{x})=f_{10}(\mathbf{x})+\eta,\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D},

(2.6)

where
\eta
is a
\mbox{\rm Normal}(0,\ (\mbox{\rm GS}_{f}/\varepsilon)^{2})
or a
\mbox{Laplace}(0,\mbox{GS}_{f}/\varepsilon)
distributed privacy noise. A test based on
\mbox{H}_{10}(\mathbf{x})
is then obtained as

\phi(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\ \mbox{H}_{10}(\mathbf{x})\geq\gamma_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(n),\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(2.7)

for some cut-off
\gamma_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(n)
, depending on the sample size
n
and size
\alpha
of the test. Here,
\phi(\mathbf{x})=1
signifies rejection of
\mbox{H}_{0}
in favour of
\mbox{H}_{1}
, and
\phi(\mathbf{x})=0
accept
\mbox{H}_{0}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

The function
\phi(\mathbf{x})
for testing
\mbox{H}_{0}
against
\mbox{H}_{1}
, as described in (
2.5
)-(
2.7
), is
\varepsilon
-differentially private.

Next, given the sample size
n
, privacy mechanism, the user defined parameters
(\varepsilon,a,M)
, and the size
\alpha
of the test, we determine the cut off
\gamma_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(n)
by satisfying the
size constraint

\displaystyle\Xi_{0}(M,a\mid\varepsilon,\alpha,n)=\mbox{E}\big[\phi(\mathbf{x})\mid\mbox{\rm H}_{0}]=\mbox{P}_{\mbox{\rm H}_{0}}\big[\mbox{\rm H}(\mathbf{x})\geq\gamma_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(n)\big]=\alpha.

(2.8)

Exact calculation of the cut off
\gamma_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(n)
may not be amenable under certain cases. However, we can always obtain
\gamma_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(n)
via a Monte Carlo simulation. We provide additional details about this computational aspect in the experiment section.

2.3
Hyperparameter tuning

To apply differentially private Bayesian tests, the data producer or an analyst permitted on a verification server, initially establishes the desired privacy level
\varepsilon
. Following this determination, the task involves selecting appropriate values for the parameters
M
and
a
. In the context presented, we consider the case where the analyst does not have the autonomy to select specific value of
\varepsilon
, but possess the discretion to choose
(M,a)
. For analysts endowed with this discretion, they may iteratively employ the described methodology, varying the values of
\varepsilon
. This iterative process allows analysts to experiment with diverse combinations of
(M,a)
, thereby striking a nuanced balance between attaining the desired privacy level and asymptotic behaviour of the Bayes factors.

Given the sample size
n
, a privacy mechanism, a privacy budget
\varepsilon
, and the size
\alpha
, we determine the Bayes factor cut off
\gamma_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(n)
using (
2.8
) via a Monte Carlo simulation, for varying values of
(M,a)
. Now, let
\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{A}
denote the space all possibles pairs
(M,a)
under consideration. We operate under the regime that
M=\gamma n
,
0<\gamma\leq 1
and
a=kn^{\beta}
,
0<\beta<1,0<k\leq 1
. The constraints
0<\beta<1
and
0<k\leq 1
ensure that the
\mbox{GS}_{f}
decreases as the sample size increases. The order assumption on
a
, or equivalently
\omega
, enables us to dictate the rate at which the hypothesis specified in (
2.1
)-(
2.1
) approaches common null hypothesis significance testing framework. This, in turn, enable us dictate the convergence rate of the privatized Bayes factor. Specifically, slower the order of
a
, lower would be the rate of convergence. Refer to the discussion in Section
3
for details.
The optimal choices
(M,a)
is obtained via
maximising power
of the test

\argmax_{M,a\in\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{A}}\Xi_{1}(M,a\mid\varepsilon,\alpha,n)

where

\Xi_{1}(M,a\mid\varepsilon,\alpha,n)=\mbox{E}\big[\phi(\mathbf{x})\mid\mbox{\rm H}_{1}]=\mbox{P}_{\mbox{\rm H}_{1}}\big[\mbox{\rm H}(\mathbf{x})\geq\gamma_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(n)\big].

Exact calculation of optimal choices of
(M,a)
may not be amenable under certain cases. However, we can always obtain it via a Monte Carlo simulation. We provide additional details about this computational aspect in the experiment section. We now have all the necessary ingredients to introduce differentially private Bayesian tests in specific set ups.

3
Differentially private Bayes factors based on test statistics

We can utilize the general framework delineated in Section
2
to device fully parametric Bayesian tests under the differentially private paradigm. However, considering the limitations associated with Bayes factors arising from fully parametric models, as discussed in the introduction, it might be beneficial to derive differentially private Bayes factors that rely on test statistics instead.
In the sequel, we present the closed form expressions for differentially private Bayes factors based on common
t
,
\chi^{2}
and
F
test statistics. Further, we study the asymptotic properties of the proposed Bayes factors, adopting the popular notion of
Conventional Bayes factor consistency

(
Chib and Kuffner 2016
;
Chatterjee et al. 2020
)
.
Such asymptotic properties must be satisfied to ensure that, as the sample size increases, the correct model has a faster rate of posterior contraction as a function of sample size in comparison to an incorrect model/complex model. Results on consistency of Bayes factors based on test statistics
(
Johnson 2008
;
Datta et al. 2023
)
is not particularly new. However, the introduction of differential privacy to this framework introduces unique challenges. To ensure the convergence of the privatized Bayes factors, it is essential to incorporate additional meaningful assumptions. This includes considerations about the distribution of test statistics under the competing hypotheses (
2.1
), order of the truncation parameter
a
(or equivalently
\omega
) and number of data partitions
M
relative to the sample size, and the characteristics of the global sensitivity parameter.

Definition 3

(Conventional Bayes factor consistency
(
Chib and Kuffner 2016
)
)
.

For two competing models
M_{l}
and
M_{k}
, the Bayes factor for comparing models
M_{k}
and
M_{l}
, denoted as
\mbox{\rm BF}_{kl}=\frac{m(\mathbf{x}\mid M_{k})}{m(\mathbf{x}\mid M_{l})}
, is consistent if
(i)
\mbox{\rm BF}_{kl}\xrightarrow{p}0
when
M_{l}
contains the true model, and (ii)
\mbox{\rm BF}_{kl}\xrightarrow{p}\infty
when
M_{k}
contains the true model, where
\mathbf{x}
is the data.

In the rest of the article, to specify the competing hypotheses as in (
2.1
), we use a mixture of a spike at
0
and an appropriately chosen univariate non-local prior
(
Johnson and Rossell 2010b
;
Johnson et al. 2023b
)
.
The defining feature of a non-local priors used in this article, as opposed to local priors, is that they assign negligible mass to the parameters values close to
0
.
Through a hyper-parameter present in the non-local densities one can specify a substantive deviation from
0
, in turn specifying the neighbourhood in which the non-local prior assigns negligible mass; see
Johnson and Rossell 2010b
for a discussion. Our choices of priors in this section is guided by two key considerations. First, the carefully chosen non-local priors ensure that both the marginal likelihoods under the competing two competing hypotheses of interest are available in closed form. This eliminates the computational expenses associated with the numerical evaluation of the otherwise intractable multiple integrals. Secondly, and perhaps more important, the prior specification ensures rapid accumulation of evidence in favor of the true hypothesis.

5
Discussion

We presented a novel differentially private Bayesian hypothesis testing methodology, coherently embedding popular tools from existing literature (e.g., subsample and aggregate, truncation of the test function, etc.) within a generative model framework. In contrast to existing approaches, the proposed method enables us to systematically accumulate evidence in support of the true hypothesis—a feature desirable in reporting scientific discoveries utilizing confidential data. To mitigate the computational complexities associated with computing marginal likelihoods of the competeing hypotheses arising from fully parametric models, we devised our differentially private Bayes factors based on commonly used test statistics. Under appropriate asymptotic regime, we derive the consistency rates of the proposed differentially private Bayes factors. The finite sample frequentist operating characteristics of the proposed tests are explored via several numerical experiments.
