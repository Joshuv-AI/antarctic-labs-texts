Wei Cai, Shuixin Fang, Tao Zhou

Abstract
In this paper, we propose a martingale-based neural network, SOC-MartNet, for solving high-dimensional Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equations where no explicit expression is needed for the infimum of the Hamiltonian, inf u ∈ U H ⁡ ( t , x , u , z , p ) \inf_{u\in U}H(t,x,u,z,p) , and stochastic optimal control problems (SOCPs) with controls on both drift and volatility. We reformulate the HJB equations for the value function by training two neural networks, one for the value function and one for the optimal control with the help of two stochastic processes - a Hamiltonian process and a cost process. The control and value networks are trained such that the associated Hamiltonian process is minimized to satisfy the minimum principle of a feedback SOCP, and the cost process becomes a martingale, thus, ensuring the value function network as the solution to the corresponding HJB equation. Moreover, to enforce the martingale property for the cost process, we employ an adversarial network and construct a loss function characterizing the projection property of the conditional expectation condition of the martingale. Numerical results show that the proposed SOC-MartNet is effective and efficient for solving HJB-type equations and SOCPs with a dimension up to 10,000 10,000 in a small number of iteration steps (less than 6000 6000 ) of training.

Introduction

In this paper, we consider the numerical solution of high-dimensional Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB)-type equations and their applications to stochastic optimal control problems (SOCPs).
The considered HJB-type equation is given in the form of

\partial_{t}v(t,x)+\mathcal{L}v(t,x)+\inf_{\kappa\in U}H\left(t,x,\kappa,\partial_{x}v(t,x),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,x)\right)=0

(1)

for
(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

with
\partial_{x}=\nabla_{x}
and
\partial_{xx}^{2}=\nabla_{x}\nabla_{x}^{\top}
as the gradient and Hessian operator, respectively, and a terminal condition

v(T,x)=g(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(2)

where
\mathcal{L}
is a differential operator given by

\mathcal{L}:=\mu^{\top}(t,x)\partial_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\left\{\sigma\sigma^{\top}\left(t,x\right)\partial_{xx}^{2}\right\}

(3)

for some given functions
\mu:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\sigma:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times q}
;

H(t,x,\kappa,z,p)
is the Hamiltonian as a mapping
(t,x,\kappa,z,p)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\to\mathbb{R}
, and
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
The HJB-type equation (
1
) is general and covers common HJB equations in SOCPs; see the discussions in section
3
.
In addition to this equation, this paper also explores the application of the proposed method to common semi-linear parabolic equations without boundary conditions.

The HJB equation is a fundamental partial differential equation (PDE) in the field of optimal control theory
[
37
,
14
,
49
,
42
,
7
]
.
In the typical framework of dynamic programming
[
3
,
14
]
, the optimal feedback control is identified by the verification technique,
which involves minimizing a Hamiltonian depending on the derivatives of a value function
[
49
, p. 278]
.
On this account, the HJB equation, which governs this value function, stands as a cornerstone of dynamic programming.
The well-posedness of HJB equations has been firmly established with the theory of viscosity solutions; see, e.g.,
[
39
,
27
,
8
]
.
But solving the HJB equation is still challenging due to its non-smoothness and high dimensionality.

The wide application of HJB equations has spurred extensive research on efficient numerical methods.
Conventional approaches include the Galerkin method
[
47
]
,
the finite volume method
[
41
,
45
]
,
the monotone approximation scheme
[
2
]
,
the patchy dynamic programming
[
4
]
, etc.
These methods generally suffer from the curse of dimensionality (CoD)
[
3
]
, that is, the computation complexity increases exponentially with the dimension of the HJB equations.
In
[
31
,
32
]
, the HJB equation is solved through the associated BSDE deduced from a Feynman-Kac representation
[
33
]
,
but the resolution of BSDE relies on least square regression on a set of basis functions,
where the CoD arises since the number of required basis functions explodes with the dimension.

There are also literature leveraging dimension reduction techniques, e.g.,
[
12
,
30
,
29
]
,
but these techniques depend on the dimension reducibility of the problem.

In recent years, deep learning has emerged as a promising tool to overcome the CoD, leading to a growing body of deep learning methods for solving high dimensional PDEs, e.g.,
[
13
,
21
,
51
,
50
,
26
,
16
,
19
,
44
,
24
]
.
While demonstrably effective for usual high-dimensional PDEs, these methods encounter new challenges when applied to the HJB-type equation (
1
).
One of the challenges stems from the inherent infimum operator

\inf_{\kappa\in U}
imposed on the Hamiltonian
H
in the HJB equation.
Directly minimizing the Hamiltonian for every time-space point
(t,x)
is computationally expensive, in fact, a CoD problem itself for high dimensional control spaces.

To avoid this issue, the works in
[
11
,
10
,
9
,
24
]
focus on Hamilton-Jacobi equations where
\inf_{\kappa\in U}H
is explicitly known.
The work
[
40
, section 3.4]
considers specific optimal control problems such that
\inf_{\kappa\in U}H
admits an analytic expression.
There are also research resorting to deep neural networks (DNNs).
For example,
[
28
, section 3.2]
introduces a DNN to learn the feedback control
u(t,x)

such that
u(t,x)
becomes a stationary point of
H
, i.e.,
\partial_{\kappa}H|_{\kappa=u(t,x)}=0
,
whereas certain conditions on
U
and
H
are needed to ensure the stationary point is a minimizer of
\kappa\mapsto H
.
The paper
[
53
]
considers static HJB-type PDEs,
where solving
\inf_{\kappa\in U}H
is avoided by reformulating the problem into a SOCP solved by reinforcement learning.

In
[
38
]
, the authors propose a neural network approach for high-dimensional SOCPs, using the stochastic Pontryagin maximum principle to guide a forward SDE for state-space exploration for handling problems with complex dynamics.

In addition, there are works on numerical methods for SOCPs by not explicitly solving the HJB equation, e.g.,
[
25
,
1
,
20
,
52
,
15
,
18
]
.
At this time, developing new efficient numerical methods for high-dimensional HJB equations still remains an actively researched topic.

In this paper, we propose a new approach for solving the high-dimensional HJB-type equation (
1
).
In our approach, the control and value functions of the problem are approximated by DNNs.
The HJB equation is encoded into a Hamiltonian process and a cost process both depending on the control network and the value network.
The value and the control networks are trained by minimizing a functional of the Hamiltonian process while ensuring the cost process to be a martingale, which guarantees the value function is the solution to the HJB equation.
The martingale property is enforced by an adversarial learning, whose loss function is constructed by characterizing the projection property of conditional expectations.
The proposed method, named SOC-MartNet, is able to solve high dimensional stochastic optimal control problems, by using the the approach of a martingale formulation, originally developed in the DeepMartNet for boundary value and eigenvalue problems of high dimensional PDEs
[
6
,
5
]
.
Our numerical experiments show that the proposed SOC-MartNet is highly effective and efficient for solving equations with dimension up to
2000
in a small number of iterations of training.

In the SOC-MartNet method,
the task of finding
\inf_{\kappa\in U}H
for each
(t,x)
is accomplished by training an optimal control network to minimize a functional of the Hamiltonian process, and avoiding the need of evaluating explicitly the infimum in the HJB equation.
Moreover, the training algorithm enjoys parallel efficiency as it is free of time-directed iterations during gradient computation.
This feature is much different from existing deep-learning probabilistic methods for PDEs.
The SOC-MartNet also demonstrates broad applicability, effectively handling high-dimensional HJB equations and parabolic equations as well as SOCPs.

In section
2

In section
3
, we propose the SOC-MartNet and its algorithm for general non-degenerated HJB equations and parabolic equations.
Numerical results are presented in section
4
.
A conclusion and plan for future work are given in section
5
.

2
Dynamic programming and minimum principle, and computational approach

We consider a filtered complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F}^{B},\mathbb{P})
with
\mathbb{F}^{B}:=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
as the natural filtration of the standard
q
-dimensional Brownian motion
B=(B_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
, and
T\in(0,\infty)
a deterministic terminal time.
Let
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}
be the set of admissible feedback control functions defined by

\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}:=\left\{u:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to U\big|\;\text{$u$ is Borel measurable}\right\}\;\;\text{with}\;\;U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(4)

For any
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}
, the controlled state process
X^{u}
is governed by the following stochastic differential equation (SDE):

X_{t}^{u}=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\bar{\mu}\left(s,X_{s}^{u},u(s,X_{s}^{u})\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\bar{\sigma}\left(s,X_{s}^{u},u(s,X_{s}^{u})\right)\,\mathrm{d}B_{s},\quad t\in[0,T]

(5)

with
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
where
\bar{\mu}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\bar{\sigma}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the controlled drift coefficient and controlled diffusion coefficient, respectively, and the stochastic integral with respect to
B_{s}
is of Itô type.
The cost functional of
u
is given by

J(u):=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}c\left(s,X_{s}^{u},u(s,X_{s}^{u})\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+g(X_{T}^{u})\right],

(6)

where
c:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
characterize the running cost and terminal cost, respectively.
Our main focus is the following SOCP:

\text{Find}\;\;u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}\;\;\text{such that}\;\;J\left(u^{*}\right)=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}}J\left(u\right).

(7)

To carry out the approach of dynamic programming, we define the value function
v
by
v(t,x):=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}}J\left(t,x,u\right)
with

J\left(t,x,u\right):=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}c\left(s,X_{s}^{u},u(s,X_{s}^{u})\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+g(X_{T}^{u})\Big|X_{t}^{u}=x\right]

for
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Under certain conditions (see, e.g.,
[
42
, Theorem 4.3.1 and Remark 4.3.4]
), the value function
v
is the viscosity solution to the following fully nonlinear HJB equation

\partial_{t}v(t,x)+\inf_{\kappa\in U}{H}\left(t,x,\kappa,\partial_{x}v(t,x),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,x)\right)=0,\quad(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

(8)

with the terminal condition
v(T,x)=g(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the Hamiltonian
{H}
given by

{H}(t,x,\kappa,z,p):=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\left(p\bar{\sigma}\bar{\sigma}^{\top}(t,x,\kappa)\right)+z^{\top}\bar{\mu}(t,x,\kappa)+c(t,x,\kappa)

(9)

for
(t,x,\kappa,z,p)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

Under the regularity condition
v\in C^{1,2}
, i.e.,
v
is once and twice continuously differentiable with respect to
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, respectively,
the classical verification theorem (see, e.g.,
[
7
]
or
[
49
, p. 268, Theorem 5.1]
) reveals the optimal feedback control as

u^{*}(t,X_{t}^{*})\in\mathop{\arg\min}_{\kappa\in U}{H}\left(t,X_{t}^{*},\kappa,\partial_{x}v(t,X_{t}^{*}),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,X_{t}^{*})\right),\quad t\in[0,T],

(10)

where
X^{*}:=X^{u^{*}}
is the controlled diffusion defined in (
5
) corresponding to the optimal control
u^{*}
. The above equation (
10
) implies that for
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle H\left(t,X_{t}^{*},u^{\ast}(t,X_{t}^{\ast}),\partial_{x}v(t,X_{t}^{*}),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,X_{t}^{*})\right)

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{\kappa\in U}H\left(t,X_{t}^{*},\kappa,\partial_{x}v(t,X_{t}^{*}),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,X_{t}^{*})\right).

Therefore, by the minimum principle (
11
), to find the optimal feedback control, it is sufficient to ensure

u^{*}(t,x)\in\mathop{\arg\min}_{\kappa\in U}{H}\left(t,x,\kappa,\partial_{x}v(t,x),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,x)\right),\quad x\in\Gamma_{t},\quad t\in[0,T],

(12)

or for all
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\Gamma_{t}
,

\displaystyle H\left(t,x,u^{\ast}(t,x),\partial_{x}v(t,x),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,x)\right)

(13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{\kappa\in U}{H}\left(t,x,\kappa,\partial_{x}v(t,x),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,x)\right)

for some state set
\Gamma_{t}\supset\Gamma(X_{t}^{*})
,
where
\Gamma(X_{t}^{*})
denotes the support set of the probability density function of
X_{t}^{*}
.

Computational approach.
On the basis of (
13
), the key step for solving the SOCP (
7
) is then to find the value function
v
from the HJB equation (
8
), but, the evaluation of the
\inf_{\kappa\in U}{H}
in the the HJB equation (
7
), thus finding
v(t,x)
, suffers from the CoD for a high dimensional control space. However, if the optimal control
u^{\ast}
is known, then from the minimum principle (
13
), the HJB equation is reduced to, without the inf operation,

\partial_{t}v(t,x)+{H}\left(t,x,u^{\ast}(t,x),\partial_{x}v(t,x),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,x)\right)=0,\quad(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\Gamma_{t}.

(14)

This argument suggests that we should consider a computational approach, which produces approximations simultaneously to both the value function
v
and the optimal control
u^{\ast}
represented by separate neural networks, to ensure that (
14
) will hold once the training of the networks leads to convergence. Therefore, before the convergence of the network for the optimal control, the value network is in fact an approximation to a version of the HJB equation (
14
) where the optimal control
u^{\ast}
is replaced with its neural network approximation.
Similarly, the infimum of the Hamiltonian in (
13
) is taken with the value function replaced by its network approximation. It is expected that once both networks have converged, the correct forms of HJB equation and the infimum of Hamiltonian will have been enforced to yield both the optimal control and value function. This will be the approach of the proposed numerical method.

3
Proposed method

Throughout this section, we assume that the HJB-type equation (
1
)
admits a classical solution
v\in C^{1,2}
,

where the regularity of
v
is needed for the martingale formulation.
According to standard PDE theory (see, e.g.,
[
36
, Section 6.2, Theorem 5]
)
,
this regularity follows from the non-degeneracy condition of (
1
), i.e.,
\sigma\sigma^{\top}(t,x)
in (
3
) is uniformly positive definite on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Below, we show that (
1
) covers many useful cases, including:

•

SOCP (
7
) without volatility control.
If
\bar{\sigma}(t,x,\kappa)=\bar{\sigma}(t,x)
, the HJB equation (
8
) degenerates into a special case of (
1
) with

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\left\{\bar{\sigma}\bar{\sigma}^{\top}\left(t,x\right)\partial_{xx}^{2}\right\},\quad H(t,x,\kappa,z,p)=z^{\top}\bar{\mu}(t,x,\kappa)+c(t,x,\kappa)

for
(t,x,\kappa,z,p)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

•

Controlled volatility
\bar{\sigma}
with an uncontolled part.

If the
\bar{\sigma}
admits a decomposition as
\bar{\sigma}(t,x,\kappa)=\bar{\sigma}_{0}(t,x)+\bar{\sigma}_{1}(t,x,\kappa)

for some
\mathbb{R}^{d\times q}
-valued functions
\bar{\sigma}_{0}
and
\bar{\sigma}_{1}
,
then the HJB equation (
8
) becomes a special case of (
1
) with

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\left\{\bar{\sigma}_{0}\bar{\sigma}_{0}^{\top}\left(t,x\right)\partial_{xx}^{2}\right\},

\displaystyle H(t,x,\kappa,z,p)=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\left[p\Sigma(t,x,\kappa)\right]+z^{\top}\bar{\mu}(t,x,\kappa)+c(t,x,\kappa),

\displaystyle\Sigma(t,x,\kappa):=\bar{\sigma}_{0}(t,x)\bar{\sigma}_{1}^{\top}(t,x,\kappa)+\bar{\sigma}_{1}(t,x,\kappa)\bar{\sigma}_{0}^{\top}(t,x)+\bar{\sigma}_{1}(t,x,\kappa)\bar{\sigma}_{1}^{\top}(t,x,\kappa)

for
(t,x,\kappa,z,p)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

•

Knowledge of some preliminary approximation
u_{0}
for the optimal control
u^{*}
.
In this case, the HJB equation (
8
) can be rewritten into (
1
) with

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{u_{0}},\quad H\left(t,x,\kappa,\partial_{x}v(t,x),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,x)\right)=\left(\mathcal{L}^{\kappa}-\mathcal{L}^{u_{0}}\right)v(t,x)+c(t,x,\kappa),

where

\mathcal{L}^{\kappa}:=\bar{\mu}^{\top}(t,x,\kappa)\partial_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\left\{\bar{\sigma}\bar{\sigma}^{\top}\left(t,x,\kappa\right)\partial_{xx}^{2}\right\}\;\;\text{for}\;\;\kappa\in U

with the convention
\mathcal{L}^{u}v(t,x):=\mathcal{L}^{u(t,x)}v(t,x)
.

•

Explicit form of the optimal control in the Hamiltonian.

If the following function
\bar{H}
is explicitly known:

\bar{H}(t,x,z,p):=\inf_{u\in U}H\left(t,x,u,z,p\right),\quad(t,x,z,p)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},

then (
1
) degenerates into a parabolic equation as

\partial_{t}v(t,x)+\mathcal{L}v(t,x)+\bar{H}\left(t,x,\partial_{x}v(t,x),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,x)\right)=0.

(15)

From the above discussions, the SOC-MartNet designed for the HJB-type equation (
1
) is applicable for the SOCP (
7
) with non-degenerated diffusion
and the parabolic equation (
15
) without boundary conditions.

3.1
Martingale formulation for HJB-type equations

Let
X:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a uncontrolled diffusion process associated with the operator
\mathcal{L}
, i.e.,

X_{t}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mu(s,X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}B_{s},\quad t\in[0,T].

(16)

Hamiltonian process
H_{t}^{u,v}
and cost process
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{u,v}
for
t\in[0,T]
.
Using the optimal control
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}
and the value function
v
, we define two processes -
a Hamiltonian process
H_{t}^{u,v}
, which will be used for enforcing the minimum principle (
13
),

H_{t}^{u,v}=H^{u,v}(t,X_{t}):=H\left(t,X_{t},u(t,X_{t}),\partial_{x}v(t,X_{t}),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,X_{t})\right),

(17)

and, a cost process
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{u,v}
, whose being a martingale implies that the value function
v(t,x)
satisfies the HJB equation (
14
),

\mathcal{M}_{t}^{u,v}=\mathcal{M}^{u,v}(t,X_{t}):=v(t,X_{t})+\int_{0}^{t}H_{s}^{u,v}\,\mathrm{d}s.

(18)

Specifically, we aim at finding a set of sufficient conditions on
\mathcal{M}^{u,v}
and
H^{u,v}
, under which
v
satisfies the HJB-type equation (
1
), and
u
is the optimal feedback control in sense of (
13
), as mentioned in the comment after (
14
).

Recalling (
13
), we assume that the uncontrolled diffusion
X_{t}
can explore the whole support set of
X_{t}^{*}
(see Remark
1
), i.e.,

\Gamma_{t}=\Gamma(X_{t})\supset\Gamma(X_{t}^{*}),\quad t\in[0,T].

(19)

Then, to establish (
13
), it is sufficient to consider the condition

H_{t}^{u,v}=H^{u,v}(t,X_{t})=\inf_{\kappa\in U}H\left(t,X_{t},\kappa,\partial_{x}v(t,X_{t}),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,X_{t})\right),\;\;t\in[0,T].

(20)

Remark 1
.

(
State spaces of controlled and uncontrolled diffusion
)
For the SOCP (
7
), we introduce the following two strategies to meet the inclusion condition (

):

•

We randomly take the starting point

of the uncontrolled diffusion

•

The uncontrolled diffusion can be taken as

.

\left(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}^{u_{0}}\right)v(t,x)+\inf_{\kappa\in U}\left\{(\mathcal{L}^{\kappa}-\mathcal{L}^{u_{0}})v(t,x)+c(t,x,\kappa)\right\}=0

.

Let
v
be any Borel measurable function from
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}

\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\inf_{\kappa\in U}H\left(t,X_{t},\kappa,\partial_{x}v(t,X_{t}),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,X_{t})\right)\right|\right]\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

(21)

Assume the optimal feedback control exists under
v
, i.e., the following equation (
22
) admits a solution
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}
:

H_{t}^{u,v}=\inf_{\kappa\in U}H\left(t,X_{t},\kappa,\partial_{x}v(t,X_{t}),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,X_{t})\right)

(22)

for
(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
, a.e.-
\,\mathrm{d}t\times\mathbb{P}
.
Then, an optimal control

for (

) can be found from the following minimization problem:

\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{t}^{u,v}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t=\inf_{\bar{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{t}^{\bar{u},v}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t.

(23)

Proof.

The existence of solution
u
for the minimization problem (

) is evident by taking
\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\,\cdot\,\right]\,\mathrm{d}t
on both sides of (

Next, recalling the definition in (
20
), we assume that there is a
\bar{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}
as an optimal feedback control satisfying (
22
), i.e.,

H_{t}^{\bar{u},v}=\inf_{\kappa\in U}H\left(t,X_{t},\kappa,\partial_{x}v(t,X_{t}),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,X_{t})\right)

(24)

for
(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
, a.e.-
\,\mathrm{d}t\times\mathbb{P}
.
Then, if
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}
satisfying (
23
), then by its definition, we have that
\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{t}^{u,v}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{t}^{\bar{u},v}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t
.
Inserting (
24
) into the right-side of this inequality, and further using (
21
), we obtain

\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{t}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t\leq 0,

where

\varepsilon_{t}=\varepsilon(t,X_{t}):=H_{t}^{u,v}-\inf_{\kappa\in U}H\left(t,X_{t},\kappa,\partial_{x}v(t,X_{t}),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,X_{t})\right).

On the other hand, the definition of
\varepsilon(t,X_{t})
implies that
\varepsilon_{t}
is a nonnegative function of
(t,X_{t})
, namely,
\varepsilon_{t}\geq 0
for any
t\in[0,T]
.
Thus it follows from
[
34
, Theorem 4.8 (i)]
that

\varepsilon_{t}=0\;\;\text{for}\;\;(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega,\;\;\text{a.e.-}\,\mathrm{d}t\times\mathbb{P},

which implies an optimal control
u
for (
22
).

Remark 3
.

(
Integral form of minimum principle and avoiding the CoD problem in
(t,x)
variables
)
Lemma

2
allows us to replace the minimum principle in (
22
) in a point-wise
(t,X_{t})
sense by an integral version of the minimum principle defined in (
23
).
The latter offers significant computational benefits, as the minimization is for a functional (defined by a double integral) on the admissible control set
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}
only, instead of the Hamiltonian on the control space
U
for each possible time-state
(t,X_{t})
.
More importantly, the high dimensional double integral in
(t,x)
can be carried out in in the space-time domain in a Monte Carlo fashion, thus avoiding the CoD problem from the high dimension of
X_{t}
and allowing independent sampling of
t
and
x
variables for an efficient parallel implementation.

Utilizing the condition (
22
), we can simplify the HJB-type equation (
1
) into

\left(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}\right)v(t,X_{t})=-H_{t}^{u,v}(t,X_{t})

(25)

for
t\in[0,T]
.
Next, we will show that (
25
) can be fulfilled by enforcing the cost process
\mathcal{M}^{u,v}
in (
18
) to be a martingale with the value function
v
and the optimal control
u
.
The following lemma presents the details.

Lemma 4
.

For any
(u,v)\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}\times C^{1,2}
satisfying

\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\partial_{x}v\sigma\left(t,X_{t}\right)\right|^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty,\;\;\;\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|H_{t}^{u,v}\right|^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty,\;\;\;\mathbb{E}\left[\left|v(T,X_{T})\right|^{2}\right]<\infty,

(26)

the equation (
25
) holds for
(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
a.e.-
\,\mathrm{d}t\times\mathbb{P}
if and only if
M_{t}^{u,v}(t,X_{t})
is a
\mathbb{F}^{B}
-martingale, i.e.,

\mathcal{M}_{t}^{u,v}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{M}_{T}^{u,v}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right],\quad t\in[0,T].

(27)

Proof.

For
v\in C^{1,2}
, the Itô formula implies that for
t\in[0,T]
,

v(t,X_{t})=v(0,X_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}\right)v(s,X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{x}v\sigma\left(s,X_{s}\right)\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

(28)

\eqref{eq_ptLeqH}\Rightarrow\eqref{eq_Mt_ut}
:
Inserting (
25
) into the above equation and further using the definition of (
18
), we obtain

\mathcal{M}_{t}^{u,v}=v(t,X_{t})+\int_{0}^{t}H^{u,v}(s,X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s=v(0,X_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{x}v\sigma\left(s,X_{s}\right)\,\mathrm{d}B_{s},\quad t\in[0,T],

Then
M_{t}^{u,v}(t,X_{t})
is a martingale from the above equation combined with the first condition in (
26
), thus
(
27
)
holds.

\eqref{eq_Mt_ut}\Rightarrow\eqref{eq_ptLeqH}
:
Recalling again the definition in (
18
), the last two conditions in (
26
) implies that
\mathbb{E}[\left|\mathcal{M}_{T}^{u,v}\right|^{2}]<\infty
.
Then by the martingale representation theorem
[
42
, Theorem 1.2.9]
, there exists a
\mathbb{F}^{B}
-adapted process
Z:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{q}
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left|Z_{s}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right]<+\infty,\quad\mathcal{M}_{t}^{u,v}=\mathcal{M}_{0}^{u,v}+\int_{0}^{t}Z_{s}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s},\quad t\in[0,T].

By using the definition
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{u,v}
in (
18
) above, we then have

v(t,X_{t})=v(0,X_{0})-\int_{0}^{t}H^{u,v}(s,X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}Z_{s}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

(29)

Combining (
28
) and (
29
), we have that

Q_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\left\{\left(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}\right)v(s,X_{s})+H^{u,v}(s,X_{s})\right\}\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{0}^{t}\left\{\partial_{x}v\sigma\left(s,X_{s}\right)-Z_{s}\right\}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}

for
t\in[0,T]
,
which means that
Q
is a
\mathbb{F}^{B}
-adapted process with finite variation,
and is also a continuous martingale with
Q_{0}=0
.
Thus it follows from
[
42
, Proposition 1.1.9]
that
Q_{t}=0
for any
t\in[0,T]
, a.s., which validates (
25
).

Lemmas

2
and
4
directly lead to the following theorem,
which gives the martingale formulation (

) for the HJB-type equation (

Theorem 5
.

Assume
(u,v)\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}\times C^{1,2}
satisfies (

) and (

).
Then, an optimal feedback control

satisfying (

), and the value function

satisfying (

) for

and

(30)

where
M_{t}^{u,v}
and

) and (

Remark 6
.

A unique feature of the martingale formulation (
30
) is that it works with expectations and conditional expectations associated with the diffusion process
X
, rather than its pathwise properties.
These expectations can be approximated by applying the Euler-Maruyama scheme
[
35
, Proposition 5.11.1]
to (

), which achieves a first-order convergence rate in the weak sense (see the numerical results in
section

4.3
).
In contrast, related works such as
[
13
,
21
,
43
,
26
,
53
,
51
,
28
]
leverage the pathwise properties of
X
.
Consequently, their method depend on the strong convergence rate of the Euler-Maruyama scheme, which is of order
1/2
, as analyzed in
[
22
,
26
,
17
]
and demonstrated numerically in
[
51
]
.

Now based on
theorem

5
,
the key issue is to fulfill the minimum condition and the martingale condition in (

), to be achieved by the SOC-MartNet algorithm.

3.2
SOC-MartNet via adversarial learning for control/value functions

Under some Lipschitz continuity and boundedness conditions on
\mu
and
\sigma
, the uncontrolled diffusion
X
given by (
16
) is a Markov process relative to
\mathbb{F}^{B}

[
46
, Theorem 17.2.3]
,
which, recalling (
18
), implies that

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{M}_{T}^{u,v}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{M}_{T}^{u,v}|X_{t}\right],\quad t\in[0,T].

Thus the martingale condition in (
30
) can be simplified into

\mathcal{M}_{t}^{u,v}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{M}_{T}^{u,v}|X_{t}\right],\quad t\in[0,T].

(31)

To avoid computing the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}\left[\,\cdot\,|X_{t}\right]
as in the original DeepMartNet
[
6
,
5
]
, we modify the martingale condition in (
31
) into

\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{T}}\left|\int_{0}^{T-\Delta t}\mathbb{E}\left[\rho(t,X_{t})\left(\mathcal{M}_{t+\Delta t}^{u,v}-\mathcal{M}_{t}^{u,v}\right)\right]\,\mathrm{d}t\right|^{2}=0,

(32)

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes the set of test functions, defined by

\mathcal{T}:=\left\{\rho:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\big|\;\text{$\rho$ is smooth and bounded}\right\},

and
\Delta t\in(0,T)
is the time step size.

Here
\rho
in
\mathcal{T}
is a general test function.
For sufficiently small
\Delta t
, the condition (
32
) ensures the martingale condition in (
31
).
Actually, by the property of conditional expectations, it holds that

\mathbb{E}\left[\rho(t,X_{t})(\mathcal{M}_{t+\Delta t}^{u,v}-\mathcal{M}_{t}^{u,v})\right]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\rho(t,X_{t})\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathcal{M}_{t+\Delta t}^{u,v}-\mathcal{M}_{t}^{u,v})|X_{t}\right]\Big]

(33)

for
t\in[0,T-\Delta t]
.
Inserting (
33
) into (
32
), we have that

\int_{0}^{T-\Delta t}\mathbb{E}\Big[\rho(t,X_{t})\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathcal{M}_{t+\Delta t}^{u,v}-\mathcal{M}_{t}^{u,v})|X_{t}\right]\Big]\,\mathrm{d}t=0\;\;\text{for all}\;\;\rho\in\mathcal{T},

where
\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathcal{M}_{t+\Delta t}^{u,v}-\mathcal{M}_{t}^{u,v})|X_{t}\right]
is a deterministic and Borel measurable function of
(t,X_{t})

[
34
, Corollary 1.97]
, and thus,

\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathcal{M}_{t+\Delta t}^{u,v}-\mathcal{M}_{t}^{u,v})|X_{t}\right]=0,\;\;\;(t,\omega)\in[0,T-\Delta t]\times\Omega,\;\;\;\text{a.e.-}\,\mathrm{d}t\times\mathbb{P}.

(34)

The above conditions imply that
\mathcal{M}^{u,v}
satisfies the martingale condition in (
31
) approximately for sufficiently small
\Delta t
.

A unique feature of (
32
) lies in its natural connection to adversarial learning
[
50
]
, based on which, we can fulfill the conditions (
2
) and (
30
) by

(u,v)=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow+\infty}\mathop{\arg\min}_{\left(\bar{u},\bar{v}\right)\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}\times\mathcal{V}}\left\{\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{L}(\bar{u},\bar{v},\rho,\lambda)\right\}

(35)

where

u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}
given in (
4
), and

v\in\mathcal{V}
the set of candidate value functions satisfying (
2
), i.e.,

\mathcal{V}:=\left\{v:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\big|\;v\in C^{1,2},\;v(T,x)=g(x),\;\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\},

(36)

and
\mathbb{L}
is the augmented Lagrangian defined by

\mathbb{L}(u,v,\rho,\lambda):=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{t}^{u,v}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t+\lambda\left|\int_{0}^{T-\Delta t}\mathbb{E}\left[\rho(t,X_{t})\left(\mathcal{M}_{t+\Delta t}^{u,v}-\mathcal{M}_{t}^{u,v}\right)\right]\,\mathrm{d}t\right|^{2}

(37)

with
\lambda
the multiplier being sufficiently large.

For adversarial learning, we replace the functions
u
,
v
and
\rho
by the control network
u_{\alpha}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to U
, the value network
v_{\theta}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and the adversarial network
\rho_{\eta}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{r}
parameterized by
\alpha
,
\theta
and
\eta
, respectively.
Since the range of
u_{\alpha}
should be restricted in the control space
U
, if
U=[a,b]:=\prod_{i=1}^{m}[a_{i},b_{i}]
with
a_{i},b_{i}
the
i
-th elements of
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
the structure of
u_{\alpha}
can be

u_{\alpha}(t,x)=a+\frac{b-a}{6}\mathrm{ReLU6}(\psi_{\alpha}(t,x)),\quad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

(38)

where
\mathrm{ReLU6}(y):=\min\{\max\{0,y\},6\}
is an activation function and
\psi_{\alpha}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a neural network with parameter
\alpha
.
Remark
7
provides a penalty method to deal with general control spaces.
To fulfill the terminal condition in (
36
),
the value network
v_{\theta}
takes the form of

v_{\theta}(T,x):=g(x),\quad v_{\theta}(t,x):=\phi_{\theta}(t,x)\;\;\text{for}\;\;t\in[0,T),\;\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\phi_{\theta}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
a neural network parameterized by
\theta
.

The adversarial network
\rho_{\eta}
plays the role of test functions.
By our experiment results,
\rho_{\eta}
is not necessarily to be very deep, but instead, it can be a shallow network with enough output dimensionality.
A typical example is that

\rho_{\eta}(t,x)=\sin\left(W_{1}t+W_{2}x+b\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{r},\quad\eta:=(W_{1},W_{2},b)\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}

(39)

for
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\sin(\cdot)
is the activation function applied on

in an element-wise manner.

SOC-MartNet.
Based on (
35
) and (
37
) with
(u_{\alpha},v_{\theta},\rho_{\eta})
in place of
(u,v,\rho)
, the solution
(u,v)
of (

) can be approximated by
(u_{\alpha^{*}},v_{\theta^{*}})
given by

(\alpha^{*},\theta^{*})=\lim_{\lambda\to+\infty}\mathop{\arg\min}_{\alpha,\theta}\left\{\max_{\eta}L(\alpha,\theta,\eta,\lambda)\right\},

(40)

where

L(\alpha,\theta,\eta,\lambda):=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{t}^{u_{\alpha},v_{\theta}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t+\lambda\left|\int_{0}^{T-\Delta t}\mathbb{E}\left[\rho_{\eta}(t,X_{t})\left(\mathcal{M}_{t+\Delta t}^{u_{\alpha},v_{\theta}}-\mathcal{M}_{t}^{u_{\alpha},v_{\theta}}\right)\right]\,\mathrm{d}t\right|^{2}

(41)

with
H^{u_{\alpha},v_{\theta}}
and
M^{u_{\alpha},v_{\theta}}
given in (
17
) and (

), respectively.
The proposed method will be named SOC-MartNet for SOCPs as it is based on the martingale condition of the cost process (
27
), similar to the DeepMartNet
[
6
,
5
]
.

Remark 7
.

If the control space
U
is general rather than an interval,
the network structure in (
38
) is no longer applicable.
This issue can be addressed by appending a new penalty term on the right side of (
41
) to ensure
u_{\alpha}(t,X_{t})
remains within

.
The following new loss function is an example:

\bar{L}(\alpha,\theta,\eta,\lambda,\bar{\lambda}):=L(\alpha,\theta,\eta,\lambda)+\bar{\lambda}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{dist}\left(u_{\alpha}(t,X_{t}),U\right)\right]\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
L(\alpha,\theta,\eta,\lambda)
is given in (
41
);
\bar{\lambda}\geq 0
is a multiplier and
\mathrm{dist}\left(\kappa,U\right)
denotes a certain distance between
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
U
.

3.3
Training algorithm

To solve (
40
) numerically, we introduce a time partition on the time interval
[0,T]
, i.e.,

\pi_{N}:=\left\{t_{0},t_{1},\cdots,t_{N}\right\}\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;0=t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{n}<t_{n+1}<\cdots<t_{N}=T.

For
n=0,1,\cdots,N-1
, denote
\Delta t_{n}:=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
and
\Delta B_{n+1}:=B_{n+1}-B_{n}.

Then we apply the following numerical approximations on the loss function in (
41
):

1.

The process
(X_{t_{n}})_{n=0}^{N}
can be approximated by
(X_{n})_{n=0}^{N}
, which is obtained by applying the Euler scheme to the SDE (
16
), i.e., for
n=0,1,2,\cdots,N-1
,

X_{n+1}=X_{n}+\mu(t_{n},X_{n})\Delta t_{n}+\sigma(t_{n},X_{n})\Delta B_{n+1}.

(42)

2.

The integral in (
18
) can be approximated by quadrature rules, e.g., the trapezoid formula, which leads to
\mathcal{M}_{t_{n+1}}^{u_{\alpha},v_{\theta}}-\mathcal{M}_{t_{n}}^{u_{\alpha},v_{\theta}}\approx\Delta\mathcal{M}_{n+1}^{\alpha,\theta}
with

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{M}_{n+1}^{\alpha,\theta}

\displaystyle:=v_{\theta}(t_{n+1},X_{n+1})-v_{\theta}(t_{n},X_{n})-\frac{1}{2}\left(H_{n}^{\alpha,\theta}+H_{n+1}^{\alpha,\theta}\right)\Delta t_{n},

\displaystyle H_{n}^{\alpha,\theta}

\displaystyle:=H\left(t_{n},X_{n},u_{\alpha}(t_{n},X_{n}),\partial_{x}v_{\theta}(t_{n},X_{n}),\partial_{xx}^{2}v_{\theta}(t_{n},X_{n})\right).

3.

The expectations in (
41
) can be approximated by the Monte-Carlo method based on the i.i.d. samples of
\{(X_{n},H_{n}^{\alpha,\theta},\Delta\mathcal{M}_{n}^{\alpha,\theta})\}_{n=0}^{N}
, i.e.,

\{(X_{n}^{(m)},H_{n}^{\alpha,\theta,(m)},\Delta\mathcal{M}_{n}^{\alpha,\theta,(m)})\}_{n=0}^{N},\quad m=1,2,\cdots,M.

(43)

Combining the above approximations, the loss function in (
41
) is replaced by its mini-batch version for stochastic gradient descent or ascent,

\displaystyle L(\alpha,\theta,\eta,\lambda;A):=\frac{1}{\left|A\right|}\sum_{(n,m)\in A}H_{n}^{\alpha,\theta,(m)}\Delta t_{n}+\lambda\left|G(\alpha,\theta,\eta,\lambda;A)\right|^{2},

(44)

\displaystyle G(\alpha,\theta,\eta;A):=\frac{1}{\left|A\right|}\sum_{(n,m)\in A}\rho_{\eta}(t_{n},X_{n}^{(m)})\Delta\mathcal{M}_{n+1}^{\alpha,\theta,(m)}\Delta t_{n}

(45)

with
\Delta t_{N}:=\Delta\mathcal{M}_{N+1}^{\alpha,\theta}:=0
for convenience,
where
A
is a index subset randomly taken from
\left\{0,1,\cdots,N\right\}\times\left\{1,2,\cdots,M\right\}
and is updated at each optimization step.
The loss function in (
44
) can be optimized by alternating gradient descent and ascent of
L(\alpha,\theta,\eta,\lambda;A)
over
(\alpha,\theta)
and
(\lambda,\eta)
, respectively.
The details are presented in
algorithm

1
.

Remark 8
.

In the martingale formulation, the diffusion process
X_{t}
given by (
16

and thus its sample paths can be generated offline before optimizing the loss function in (

Moreover, in the SOC-MartNet, the gradient computation for the loss function and the training of neural networks are both free of recursive iterations along the time direction, which contributes to significant efficiency gains for the SOC-MartNet.
This feature is different from many existing deep-learning probabilistic methods for PDEs, e.g.,
[
13
,
51
,
53
,
26
,
40
,
25
,
1
,
28
]
.
Our numerical experiments in
subsection

confirm the high efficiency of the SOC-MartNet.

3.4
Application to parabolic problems

The SOC-MartNet proposed in the last subsection is applicable for the HJB-type equation (
1
).
In this section, we explore how SOC-MartNet can be tailored to parabolic equations, yielding enhanced efficiency and simplicity.

Specifically, we consider the parabolic equation in the form of

\partial_{t}v(t,x)+\mathcal{L}v(t,x)+f\left(t,x,v(t,x),\partial_{x}v(t,x),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,x)\right)=0

(46)

with
f
being a given function.
Here
f
could depend also on
v(t,x)
, compared with
\bar{H}
in (
15
), and this dependence does not pose any difficulties in applying the method to (
46
)
.

By (
18
) with
f
in place of
H
, we obtain a new cost process
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}^{v}
independent of
u
, i.e.,

\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{t}^{v}:=v(t,X_{t})+\int_{0}^{t}f(s,X_{t},v(t,X_{t}),\partial_{x}v(t,X_{t}),\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,X_{t}))\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad t\in[0,T].

Under some regularity conditions, by following the deductions in section
3.1
, we conclude that

v
satisfies the equation (
1
) for
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\Gamma(X_{t})

if
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}^{v}
is a
\mathbb{F}^{B}
-martingale.

where

\left\{0,1,\cdots,N\right\}\times\left\{1,2,\cdots,M\right\}
and

\displaystyle\Delta\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{n+1}^{\theta,(m)}

\displaystyle:=v_{\theta}(t_{n+1},X_{n+1}^{(m)})-v_{\theta}(t_{n},X_{n}^{(m)})-\frac{1}{2}\left(f_{n}^{\theta,(m)}+f_{n+1}^{\theta,(m)}\right)\Delta t_{n},

\displaystyle f_{n}^{\theta,(m)}

\displaystyle:=f\left(t_{n},X_{n}^{(m)},v_{\theta}(t_{n},X_{n}^{(m)}),\partial_{x}v_{\theta}(t_{n},X_{n}^{(m)}),\partial_{xx}^{2}v_{\theta}(t_{n},X_{n}^{(m)})\right),

and

Conclusions and future work

In this paper, we propose a martingale-based DNN method for stochastic optimal controls, SOC-MartNet, based on the original DeepMartNet
[
6
,
5
]
combined with adversarial learning.
A Hamiltonian process and a cost process are introduced using a control network and a value network. A loss function for the networks’ training is used to ensure the minimum principle for the optimal feedback control as well as the fulfillment of the HJB equation by the value function, and the latter is implemented through a martingale formulation and a training that drives the value function network to satisfy its martingale properties at convergence.
In SOC-MartNet, the martingale property of the cost process is however enforced by an adversarial learning,
whose loss function is built upon the projection property of conditional expectations.
Numerical results show that the proposed SOC-MartNet is effective and efficient for solving HJB-type equations with dimension up to

10,000
in a small number of stochastic gradient method iterations (less than
6000
)
for the training, and particularly, for SOCPs where the
\inf H
has no explicit expressions.

For future work, we will carry out theoretical analysis to study the convergence of the proposed SOC-MartNet and provide some mathematical justification of the observed computational performance of the method.
