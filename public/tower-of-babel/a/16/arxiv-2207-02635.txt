Set-Valued α \displaystyle\alpha -Fractal Functions
Megha Pandey, Tanmoy Som, Saurabh Verma

Abstract
. In this paper, we introduce the concept of the α \displaystyle\alpha -fractal function and fractal approximation for a set-valued continuous map defined on a closed and bounded interval of real numbers. Also, we study some properties of such fractal functions. Further, we estimate the perturbation error between the given continuous function and its α \displaystyle\alpha -fractal function. Additionally, we define a new graph of a set-valued function different from the standard graph introduced in the literature and establish some bounds on the fractal dimension of the newly defined graph of some special classes of set-valued functions. Also, we explain the need to define this new graph with examples. In the sequel, we prove that this new graph of an α \displaystyle\alpha -fractal function is an attractor of an iterated function system.

Approximation theory has gained appreciable attention in the literature. Fractal theory embraced the approximation theory in 1986 by Barnsley

\displaystyle\alpha

[
34

\displaystyle\alpha
-fractal function has been studied, and in
[
11
]
, a generalized
\displaystyle C^{r}

In this paper, we have extended the notion of
\displaystyle\alpha
-fractal function in the case of set-valued maps. The significance of set-valued maps can be found in many essential areas, such as optimization theory, game theory, control theory, etc. One may refer
[
3
]

[

13

, Minkowski sum of two sets is used, and in
[
9
]
the notion of the sum specified in
[
2
]
has been extended, which is known as a metric linear combination of sets. In this paper, we have taken the Minkowski sum of sets.

Approximation of set-valued maps is one of the celebrated topics in the literature. Several theories have been given regarding the classical approximation of set-valued maps. See, for instance,
[
18
]
where the notion of univariate data interpolation function has been given. Initially, approximation theory mainly focused on set-valued maps with convex images, known as convex set-valued maps. For example, in

Like fractal approximation, estimating the fractal dimension is also a fascinating area in fractal theory. It provides the statistical ratio of complexity with the details of how a fractal pattern changes with the scale it is measured. Several notions of fractal dimension have been introduced so far in the literature. For example, Hausdorff dimension , box dimension, packing dimension

3.
Fractal functions in
\displaystyle C(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))

We know that space
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
when endowed with metric
\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{C}}
is a complete metric space, where

d_{\mathcal{C}}(F,G)=\lVert F-G\rVert_{\infty}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in I\end{subarray}}H_{d}(F(u),G(u)).

Note 3.1
.

[
16
]

Recall some properties of the Hausdorff metric as follows.

(1)

For
\displaystyle B,C,D,E\in\mathcal{K}(X)
, we have

H_{d}(B+C,D+E)\leq H_{d}(B,D)+H_{d}(C,E),

where
\displaystyle Y+Z:=\{y+z:y\in Y\in\mathcal{K}(X),z\in Z\in\mathcal{K}(X)\}
is known as Minkowski sum of
\displaystyle Y
and
\displaystyle Z.

(2)

For any
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
\displaystyle H_{d}(\lambda B,\lambda D)=\lvert\lambda\rvert H_{d}(B,D).

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume
\displaystyle F\in\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R})).
Let
\displaystyle\Delta:=\{(u_{1},\ldots,u_{N}):u_{1}<\cdots<u_{N}\}
be a given data points such that it forms a partition of
\displaystyle I,
and let
\displaystyle I_{n}=[u_{n},u_{n+1}].
Let
\displaystyle L_{n}:I\rightarrow I_{n}
be contractive homeomorphism such that
\displaystyle L_{n}(u_{1})=u_{n}\text{ and }L_{n}(u_{N})=u_{n+1}
or
\displaystyle L_{n}(u_{1})=u_{n+1}\text{ and }L_{n}(u_{N})=u_{n}
. Further, assume that the base function
\displaystyle S\in\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
satisfies

S(u_{1})-F(u_{1})=S(u_{N})-F(u_{N}),

and scaling factor
\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{R}.
If
\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert<1,
then there exists a unique function
\displaystyle F^{\alpha}_{\Delta,S}\in\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
satisfying the following self-referential equation

F^{\alpha}_{\Delta,S}(u)=F(u)+\alpha[F^{\alpha}_{\Delta,S}(L_{n}^{-1}(u))-S(L_{n}^{-1}(u))]~\text{ for every }~u\in I_{n},

(3.1)

where
\displaystyle n\in J=\{1,\ldots,N-1\}.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{F}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))=\{G\in\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R})):G(u_{1})-S(u_{1})~=G(u_{N})-S(u_{N})\}.
It is elementary to observe that
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{F}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
is a closed subset of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R})),
hence
\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{C}_{F}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R})),d_{\mathcal{C}}\right)
is a complete metric space.
Define
Read-Bajraktarević
(RB) operator
\displaystyle\Phi:\mathcal{C}_{F}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{F}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
by

(\Phi G)(u)=F(u)+\alpha[G(L_{n}^{-1}(u))-S(L_{n}^{-1}(u))]

for every
\displaystyle u\in I_{n}
and
\displaystyle n\in J.
Well-definedness of
\displaystyle\Phi
can be observed by using the assumptions we have taken for
\displaystyle F,S
, and
\displaystyle\alpha
. With the reference to Note
3.1
, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{d}((\Phi G)(u),(\Phi H)(u))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{d}\Big(F(u)+\alpha[G(L_{n}^{-1}(u))-S(L_{n}^{-1}(u))],F(u)+\alpha[H(L_{n}^{-1}(u))-S(L_{n}^{-1}(u))]\Big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{d}\Big(\alpha G(L_{n}^{-1}(u)),\alpha H(L_{n}^{-1}(u))\Big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert H_{d}\Big(G(L_{n}^{-1}(u)),H(L_{n}^{-1}(u))\Big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert\sup_{u\in I}H_{d}(G(u),H(u))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert\lVert G-H\rVert_{\infty}.

Since
\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert\lVert G-H\rVert_{\infty}
is independent of
\displaystyle u,
hence we have

\lVert\Phi G-\Phi H\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lvert\alpha\rvert\lVert G-H\rVert_{\infty}.

Because
\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert<1,

\displaystyle\Phi
is a contraction on
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R})).
Hence,
\displaystyle\Phi
has a fixed point in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
. Let
\displaystyle F^{\alpha}_{\Delta,S}
be that fixed point, then it satisfies the self-referential equation,

F^{\alpha}_{\Delta,S}(u)=F(u)+\alpha[F^{\alpha}_{\Delta,S}(L_{n}^{-1}(u))-S(L_{n}^{-1}(u))]

for every
\displaystyle u\in I_{n}
and
\displaystyle n\in J.

∎

Theorem 3.7
.

Consider
\displaystyle F,S\in\mathcal{HC}^{\sigma}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))
such that
\displaystyle S(u_{1})-F(u_{1})=S(u_{N})-F(u_{N}),
and let
\displaystyle\alpha\in(-1,1).
Then,
\displaystyle F^{\alpha}\in\sigma\text{-}\mathcal{HC}
provided
\displaystyle\frac{\lvert\alpha\rvert}{a^{\sigma}}<1
, where
\displaystyle a:=\min\{a_{j}:j\in J\}
.

Proof.

Consider
\displaystyle\mathcal{HC}^{\sigma}_{F}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))=\{G\in\mathcal{HC}^{\sigma}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})):G(u_{1})-S(u_{1})=~G(u_{N})-S(u_{N})\}.
It is easy to notice that
\displaystyle\mathcal{HC}^{\sigma}_{F}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))
is a closed subset of
\displaystyle\mathcal{HC}^{\sigma}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})),
and hence complete with respect to the metric
\displaystyle H_{\sigma}^{(1)}
. Define a map
\displaystyle\Phi:\mathcal{HC}^{\sigma}_{F}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))\rightarrow\mathcal{HC}^{\sigma}_{F}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))
as

(\Phi G)(u)=F(u)+\alpha~(G-S)(L_{j}^{-1}(u))

for each
\displaystyle u\in I_{j}
where
\displaystyle j\in J.
Clearly,
\displaystyle\Phi
is well-defined. Now for
\displaystyle G,H\in\mathcal{HC}^{\sigma}_{F}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{\sigma}^{(1)}(\Phi(G),\Phi(H))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{u\in I}H_{d}(\Phi(G)(u),\Phi(H)(u))+\max_{j\in J}\sup_{u\neq w,u,w\in I_{j}}\frac{H_{d}\big(\Phi(G)(u)+\Phi(H)(w),\Phi(H)(u)+\Phi(G)(w)\big)}{\lvert u-w\rvert^{\sigma}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle~\lvert\alpha\rvert\sup_{u\in I}H_{d}(G(u),H(u))

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\max_{j\in J}\sup_{u\neq w,u,w\in I_{j}}\frac{H_{d}\Big(\alpha G(L_{j}^{-1}(u))+\alpha H(L_{j}^{-1}(w)),\alpha H(L_{j}^{-1}(u))+\alpha G(L_{j}^{-1}(w))\Big)}{\lvert u-w\rvert^{\sigma}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\lvert\alpha\rvert\sup_{u\in I}H_{d}(G(u),H(u))

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+\lvert\alpha\rvert\max_{j\in J}\sup_{u\neq w,u,w\in I_{j}}\frac{H_{d}\Big(G(L_{j}^{-1}(u))+H(L_{j}^{-1}(w)),H(L_{j}^{-1}(u))+G(L_{j}^{-1}(w))\Big)}{\lvert a_{j}\rvert^{\sigma}\lvert L_{j}^{-1}(u)-L_{j}^{-1}(w)\rvert^{\sigma}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq~\lvert\alpha\rvert\sup_{u\in I}H_{d}(G(u),H(u))

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+\frac{\lvert\alpha\rvert}{a^{\sigma}}\max_{j\in J}\sup_{u\neq w,u,w\in I_{j}}\frac{H_{d}\Big(G(L_{j}^{-1}(u))+H(L_{j}^{-1}(w)),H(L_{j}^{-1}(u))+G(L_{j}^{-1}(w))\Big)}{\lvert L_{j}^{-1}(u)-L_{j}^{-1}(w)\rvert^{\sigma}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq~\frac{\lvert\alpha\rvert}{a^{\sigma}}\left[\sup_{u\in I}H_{d}(G(u),H(u))+\sup_{u\neq w,u,w\in I}\frac{H_{d}\Big(G(u)+H(w),H(u)+G(w)\Big)}{\lvert u-w\rvert^{\sigma}}\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lvert\alpha\rvert}{a^{\sigma}}H_{\sigma}^{(1)}(G,H).

Since
\displaystyle\frac{\lvert\alpha\rvert}{a^{\sigma}}<1
, which implies
\displaystyle\Phi
is a contraction map on
\displaystyle\mathcal{HC}^{\sigma}_{F}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})).
Now, the Banach contraction principle ensures that a unique fixed point of
\displaystyle\Phi
exists. This completes the proof.
∎

Definition 3.8
.

Assume
\displaystyle F:I\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R})
be a set-valued map. For every partition
\displaystyle P:=\{(t_{0},\ldots,t_{m}):t_{0}<\dots<t_{m}\}
of
\displaystyle I,
define

V(F,I)=\sup_{P}\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}(F(t_{i}),F(t_{i-1})),

where the supremum runs over all partitions,
\displaystyle P
of
\displaystyle I.

We set
\displaystyle\lVert F\rVert_{\mathcal{BV}}:=\lVert F\rVert_{\infty}+V(F,I),
where
\displaystyle\lVert F\rVert_{\infty}:=\underset{u\in I}{\sup}~\lVert F(u)\rVert=\underset{u\in I}{\sup}~H_{d}(F(u),\{0\}).

Then,
\displaystyle F
will be characterize as a bounded variation function, if
\displaystyle\lVert F\rVert_{\mathcal{BV}}<\infty
.
\displaystyle\mathcal{BV}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
will be denoted as a collection of all bounded variation function on
\displaystyle I
.

Remark 3.9
.

It is interesting to write the following small observation :
define functions
\displaystyle F,T:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R})
as follows
\displaystyle F(x)=\begin{cases}\sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right),&\text{when}~x\neq 0\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
and
\displaystyle T(x)=[-1,1].
Here
\displaystyle F(x)\subset T(x)
for each
\displaystyle x\in[0,1]
, such that
\displaystyle T\in\mathcal{BV}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
while
\displaystyle F\notin\mathcal{BV}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
. This example shows that for set-valued mappings satisfying
\displaystyle F\leq T
does not imply
\displaystyle\lVert F\rVert_{\mathcal{BV}}\leq\lVert T\rVert_{\mathcal{BV}}
.

As a prelude to our next result, we note the following lemma.

Lemma 3.10
.

Consider
\displaystyle\{F_{n}\}
is a sequence of set-valued continuous maps which uniformly converges to
\displaystyle F:I\to\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}).
Then, for a given partition
\displaystyle P=\{(y_{0},\dots,y_{m}):y_{0}<\dots<y_{m}\}
of
\displaystyle I
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}(F_{n}(y_{i}),F_{n}(y_{i-1}))\to\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}(F(y_{i}),F(y_{i-1})).

Moreover,

\sup_{P}\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}(F(y_{i}),F(y_{i-1}))\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P}\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}(F_{n}(y_{i}),F_{n}(y_{i-1})).

(3.2)

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle P=\{(y_{0},\ldots,y_{m}):y_{0}<\cdots<y_{m}\}
be a partition of
\displaystyle I
. The uniform convergence of
\displaystyle\{F_{n}\}
implies

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}(F_{n}(y_{i}),F_{n}(y_{i-1}))=~\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}(F(y_{i}),F(y_{i-1})).

Now for a given partition
\displaystyle P=\{(y_{0},\ldots,y_{m}):y_{0}<\cdots<y_{m}\}
of
\displaystyle I
, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}(F(y_{i}),F(y_{i-1}))=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}(\lim_{n\to\infty}F_{n}(y_{i}),\lim_{n\to\infty}F_{n}(y_{i-1}))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}(F_{n}(y_{i}),F_{n}(y_{i-1}))

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P}\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}(F_{n}(y_{i}),F_{n}(y_{i-1})),

completing the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.11
.

The space
\displaystyle\Big(\mathcal{BV}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})),H_{\mathcal{BV}}\Big)
is a complete metric space, where

H_{\mathcal{BV}}(G,H):=\lVert G-H\rVert_{\infty}+\sup_{P}\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}\Big(G(y_{i})+H(y_{i-1}),H(y_{i})+G(y_{i-1})\Big).

Proof.

Assume that
\displaystyle\{F_{n}\}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\displaystyle\mathcal{BV}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))
with respect to
\displaystyle H_{\mathcal{BV}}.
Equivalently, for
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
, there exists
\displaystyle n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

H_{\mathcal{BV}}(F_{n},F_{k})<\epsilon\text{ for all }n,k\geq n_{0}.

Using the definition of
\displaystyle H_{\mathcal{BV}},
we obtain
\displaystyle\lVert F_{n}-F_{k}\rVert_{\infty}<\epsilon\text{ for all }n,k\geq n_{0}.
Since
\displaystyle(\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})),\lVert.\rVert_{\infty})
is a complete metric space, there exists a continuous function
\displaystyle F
with
\displaystyle\lVert F_{n}-F\rVert_{\infty}\to 0
as
\displaystyle n\to\infty.

We claim that
\displaystyle F\in\mathcal{BV}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))
and
\displaystyle H_{\mathcal{BV}}(F_{n},F)\to 0
as
\displaystyle n\to\infty.
Let
\displaystyle P=\{(y_{0},\dots,y_{N}):y_{0}<\dots<y_{N}\}
be a partition of
\displaystyle I
and
\displaystyle n\geq n_{0}.
From the reference to Lemma
3.10
, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{\mathcal{BV}}(F_{n},F)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lVert F_{n}-F\rVert_{\infty}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}\Big(F_{n}(y_{i})+F(y_{i-1}),F(y_{i})+F_{n}(y_{i-1})\Big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\Bigg(\lVert F_{n}-F_{k}\rVert_{\infty}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}\Big(F_{n}(y_{i})+F_{k}(y_{i-1}),F_{k}(y_{i})+F_{n}(y_{i-1})\Big)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\Bigg(\lVert F_{n}-F_{k}\rVert_{\infty}+\sup_{P}\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}\Big(F_{n}(y_{i})+F_{k}(y_{i-1}),F_{k}(y_{i})+F_{n}(y_{i-1})\Big)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{k\geq n_{0}}\Bigg(\lVert F_{n}-F_{k}\rVert_{\infty}+\sup_{P}\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}\Big(F_{n}(y_{i})+F_{k}(y_{i-1}),F_{k}(y_{i})+F_{n}(y_{i-1})\Big)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{k\geq n_{0}}H_{\mathcal{BV}}(F_{n},F_{k})<\epsilon.

Since
\displaystyle P
was arbitrary, therefore we have
\displaystyle H_{\mathcal{BV}}(F_{n},F)<\epsilon~\text{ for all }~n\geq n_{0}.

It remains to show that
\displaystyle F\in\mathcal{BV}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})).

Now by using
\displaystyle H_{d}(B+D,C+D)=H_{d}(B,C)
for every
\displaystyle B,C,D\in\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
(see for instance
[
16
]
), we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}(F(y_{i}),F(y_{i-1}))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}(F(y_{i})+F_{n}(y_{i-1}),F(y_{i-1})+F_{n}(y_{i-1}))

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}(F(y_{i})+F_{n}(y_{i-1}),F(y_{i-1})+F_{n}(y_{i}))+\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}(F_{n}(y_{i})+F(y_{i-1}),F(y_{i-1})+F_{n}(y_{i-1}))

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}(F(y_{i})+F_{n}(y_{i-1}),F(y_{i-1})+F_{n}(y_{i}))+\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}(F_{n}(y_{i}),F_{n}(y_{i-1}))\leq H_{\mathcal{BV}}(F_{n},F)+\lVert F_{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{BV}}.

Since
\displaystyle H_{\mathcal{BV}}(F_{n},F)<\epsilon
and
\displaystyle F_{n}\in\mathcal{BV}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))
, the above inequality yields that
\displaystyle F\in\mathcal{BV}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})).

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.12
.

Consider
\displaystyle F\in\mathcal{BV}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))
,
\displaystyle\Delta
as defined in Theorem
3.2
,
\displaystyle S\in\mathcal{BV}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))
such that
\displaystyle S(u_{1})-F(u_{1})=~S(u_{N})-F(u_{N})
, and
\displaystyle\alpha\in(-1,1)
with
\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert<\frac{1}{N}.
Then,
\displaystyle\alpha
-fractal function,
\displaystyle F^{\alpha}
corresponding to
\displaystyle F
is of bounded variation on
\displaystyle I
.

Proof.

Consider
\displaystyle\mathcal{BV}_{*}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))=\{G\in\mathcal{BV}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})):G(u_{1})-S(u_{1})=~G(u_{N})-S(u_{N})\}.
It is easy to prove that
\displaystyle\mathcal{BV}_{*}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))
is a closed subset of
\displaystyle\mathcal{BV}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})),
hence complete with respect to metric
\displaystyle H_{\mathcal{BV}}
.
Define RB operator
\displaystyle\Phi:\mathcal{BV}_{*}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))\rightarrow\mathcal{BV}_{*}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))
by

(\Phi G)(u)=F(u)+\alpha\big[G\big(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\big)-S\big(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\big)\big]

for each
\displaystyle u\in I_{j}
and
\displaystyle j\in J.
It is easy to observe the well-definedness of
\displaystyle\Phi
. Assume
\displaystyle P=\{(t_{0},\ldots,t_{m}):t_{0}<\cdots<t_{m}\}
is a partition of
\displaystyle I_{j},
where
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}.
We have

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{d}\Big(\Phi(G)(t_{i})+\Phi(H)(t_{i-1}),\Phi(H)(t_{i})+\Phi(G)(t_{i-1})\Big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle~H_{d}\Big(\alpha G\big(L_{j}^{-1}(t_{i})\big)+\alpha H\big(L_{j}^{-1}(t_{i-1})\big),\alpha H\big(L_{j}^{-1}(t_{i})\big)+\alpha G\big(L_{j}^{-1}(t_{i-1})\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle~\lvert\alpha\rvert H_{d}\Big(G\big(L_{j}^{-1}(t_{i})\big)+H\big(L_{j}^{-1}(t_{i-1})\big),H\big(L_{j}^{-1}(t_{i})\big)+G\big(L_{j}^{-1}(t_{i-1})\big)\Big).

Summing over
\displaystyle i=1
to
\displaystyle m,
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}\Big(\Phi(G)(t_{i})+\Phi(H)(t_{i-1}),\Phi(H)(t_{i})+\Phi(G)(t_{i-1})\Big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}\Big(G\big(L_{j}^{-1}(t_{i})\big)+H\big(L_{j}^{-1}(t_{i-1})\big),H\big(L_{j}^{-1}(t_{i})\big)+G\big(L_{j}^{-1}(t_{i-1})\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert\sup_{P}\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}\Big(G(t_{i})+H(t_{i-1}),H(t_{i})+G(t_{i-1})\Big).

The above inequality is true for any partition of
\displaystyle I_{j}
. Hence, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{\mathcal{BV}}(\Phi(G),\Phi(H))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{u\in I}H_{d}(\Phi(G)(u),\Phi(H)(u))+\sup_{P}\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}\Big(\Phi G(t_{i})+\Phi H(t_{i-1}),\Phi H(t_{i})+\Phi G(t_{i-1})\Big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert\sup_{u\in I}H_{d}(G(u),H(u))+N\lvert\alpha\rvert\sup_{P}\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{d}\Big(G(t_{i})+H(t_{i-1}),H(t_{i})+G(t_{i-1})\Big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle N\lvert\alpha\rvert H_{\mathcal{BV}}(G,H).

As
\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert<\frac{1}{N},

\displaystyle\Phi
is a contraction map. Then, Banach fixed point theorem ensures that
\displaystyle\Phi
has a unique fixed point, say
\displaystyle F^{\alpha}
. Further, this fixed point will satisfy the following self-referential equation,

F^{\alpha}(u)=F(u)+\alpha\big[F^{\alpha}\big(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\big)-S\big(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\big)\big]~\text{for each}~u\in I_{j},~\text{where}~j\in J.

∎

Notice that function
\displaystyle F^{\alpha}
is a parametric function depending on parameters, base function
\displaystyle S
, scaling function
\displaystyle\alpha
, partition
\displaystyle\Delta
and the function
\displaystyle F
itself. To observe collective behavior of
\displaystyle F^{\alpha}
depending on some such parameters we define a set-valued map,
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}_{S}:\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
such that

\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}_{S}(F)=F^{\alpha},~\text{where}~\alpha\in(-1,1).

(3.3)

This map is known as fractal operator.

Theorem 3.13
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}_{S}
defined in (
3.3
) is a continuous map.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\{F_{k}\}
be a sequence in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
such that
\displaystyle F_{k}\to F
, then to prove
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}_{S}
is a continuous function, it is sufficient to prove that
\displaystyle F^{\alpha}_{n}\to F_{\alpha}
. Since
\displaystyle F_{n}\to F
, then for each
\displaystyle\epsilon
there exists
\displaystyle n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lVert F_{n}-F\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\epsilon(1-\lvert\alpha\rvert)~\text{for all}~n\geq n_{0},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\text{equivalently, }\underset{u\in I}{\sup}H_{d}(F_{n}(u),F(u))

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\epsilon(1-\lvert\alpha\rvert)\text{ for all }n\geq n_{0}.

Now, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{d}(F^{\alpha}_{n}(x),F^{\alpha}(x))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{d}\bigg(F_{n}(x)+\alpha\left[F^{\alpha}_{n}(L^{-1}_{j}(x))-S(L^{-1}_{j}(x))\right],F(x)+\alpha\left[F^{\alpha}(L^{-1}_{j}(x))-S(L^{-1}_{j}(x))\right]\bigg)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{d}(F_{n}(x),F(x))+\lvert\alpha\rvert H_{d}(F^{\alpha}_{n}(L^{-1}_{j}(x)),F^{\alpha}(L^{-1}_{j}(x))).

This implies,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underset{x\in I}{\sup}~H_{d}(F^{\alpha}_{n}(x),F^{\alpha}(x))

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-\lvert\alpha\rvert}~\underset{x\in I}{\sup}~H_{d}(F_{n}(x),F(x)),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\text{that is }\lVert F^{\alpha}_{n}(x)-F^{\alpha}(x)\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\epsilon~\text{for all }n\geq n_{0}.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Approximation of set-valued functions

In Section
3
, we observe that
\displaystyle F^{\alpha}
satisfies the following self-referential equation:

F^{\alpha}(u)=F(u)+\alpha\left[F^{\alpha}\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right)-S\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right)\right]

for every
\displaystyle u\in I_{j}
, where
\displaystyle j\in J
.

Let us note the next result as a prelude.

Proposition 4.1
.

Between
\displaystyle F
and
\displaystyle F^{\alpha}
, the following perturbation error will be obtained:

\lVert F^{\alpha}-F\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\lvert\alpha\rvert}{1-\lvert\alpha\rvert}\lVert F-S\rVert_{\infty}+\frac{2\lvert\alpha\rvert}{1-\lvert\alpha\rvert}\lVert F\rVert_{\infty}.

Proof.

Using the self-referential equation and Note
3.1
, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{d}(F^{\alpha}(u),F(u))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{d}\bigg(F(u)+\alpha\left[F^{\alpha}\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right)-S\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right)\right],~F(u)\bigg)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{d}\bigg(\alpha\left[F^{\alpha}\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right)-S\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right)\right],~\{0\}\bigg)=\lvert\alpha\rvert H_{d}\bigg(F^{\alpha}\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right)-S\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right),~\{0\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert H_{d}\bigg(F^{\alpha}\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right)-S\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right),~F\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right)-F\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right)\bigg)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip 199.16928pt+\lvert\alpha\rvert H_{d}\bigg(F\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right)-F\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right),\{0\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert H_{d}\bigg(F^{\alpha}\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right),F\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right)\bigg)+\lvert\alpha\rvert H_{d}\bigg(-S\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right),-F\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right)\bigg)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip 199.16928pt+2\lvert\alpha\rvert H_{d}\bigg(F\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right),\{0\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert\sup_{u\in I_{j},j\in J}H_{d}\bigg(F^{\alpha}\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right),F\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right)\bigg)+\lvert\alpha\rvert\sup_{u\in I_{j},j\in J}H_{d}\bigg(S\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right),F\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right)\bigg)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+2\lvert\alpha\rvert\sup_{u\in I_{j},j\in J}H_{d}\left(F\left(L_{j}^{-1}(u)\right),\{0\}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert\lVert F^{\alpha}-F\rVert_{\infty}+\lvert\alpha\rvert\lVert F-S\rVert_{\infty}+2\lvert\alpha\rvert\lVert F\rVert_{\infty}.

This in turn yields
\displaystyle\lVert F^{\alpha}-F\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lvert\alpha\rvert\lVert F^{\alpha}-F\rVert_{\infty}+\lvert\alpha\rvert\lVert F-S\rVert_{\infty}+2\lvert\alpha\rvert\lVert F\rVert_{\infty}

Theorem 4.2
.

Consider
\displaystyle F\in\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})).

For any
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
, there is a set-valued fractal polynomial
\displaystyle P^{\alpha}
such that

\lVert F-P^{\alpha}\rVert_{\infty}<\epsilon.

Proof.

For
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
using
[
35
]
, there is a set-valued polynomial function
\displaystyle P
such that

\lVert F-P\rVert_{\infty}<\frac{\epsilon}{3}.

Choose a partition
\displaystyle\Delta_{P}=\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{M}\}
of
\displaystyle I
and a continuous function
\displaystyle S_{P}
satisfying
\displaystyle S_{P}(y_{0})-P(y_{0})=S_{P}(y_{M})-P(y_{M})
, and
\displaystyle\alpha\in(-1,1)
such that

\lvert\alpha\rvert<\min\left\{\frac{\frac{\epsilon}{3}}{\frac{\epsilon}{3}+\lVert P-S_{P}\rVert_{\infty}},\frac{\frac{\epsilon}{3}}{\frac{\epsilon}{3}+2\lVert P\rVert_{\infty}}\right\}.

Then, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lVert F-P^{\alpha}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\lVert F-P\rVert_{\infty}+\lVert P-P^{\alpha}\rVert_{\infty}~\text{ (using triangle inequality)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\lVert F-P\rVert_{\infty}+\frac{\lvert\alpha\rvert}{1-\lvert\alpha\rvert}\lVert P-S_{P}\rVert_{\infty}+\frac{2\lvert\alpha\rvert}{1-\lvert\alpha\rvert}\lVert P\rVert_{\infty}~\text{ (using Proposition \ref{new79})}

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\frac{\epsilon}{3}+\frac{\epsilon}{3}+\frac{\epsilon}{3}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\epsilon.

∎

Remark 4.3
.

We took
\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
in the above proof, such that

\lvert\alpha\rvert<\min\left\{\frac{\frac{\epsilon}{3}}{\frac{\epsilon}{3}+\lVert P-S_{P}\rVert_{\infty}},\frac{\frac{\epsilon}{3}}{\frac{\epsilon}{3}+2\lVert P\rVert_{\infty}}\right\}.

In this situation,
\displaystyle\alpha
may be “close” to
\displaystyle 0
and hence
\displaystyle P^{\alpha}
may not be self-referential and it may behave as a classical polynomial. In alter, if we fix
\displaystyle\alpha\in(-1,1)
such that
\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert<1
, but otherwise arbitrary and choose a polynomial
\displaystyle P
and
a function
\displaystyle S_{P}\in\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}))
satisfying
\displaystyle S_{P}(u_{1})-P(u_{1})=S(u_{N})-P(u_{N})
and

This forces
\displaystyle F
to be a zero set function. Hence, the analogue of
[

cannot be established in the setting of set-valued mappings. In particular, the recently developed notion of Bernstein fractal functions will not be useful in the approximation of set-valued functions.

With the reference to Theorem

Theorem 4.4

The set
\displaystyle C=\underset{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\bigcup}\left(\underset{1\leq i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\leq N}{\bigcup}L_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}\big(\left\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{N}\right\}\big)\right)
is dense in interval
\displaystyle I=[0,1]
, where
\displaystyle L_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}(u)=L_{i_{1}}(L_{i_{2}}(\ldots(L_{i_{n}}(u))))
and
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle u\in I
be any point. Observe that for some
\displaystyle w\in\left\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{N}\right\}
, we have
\displaystyle\lvert u-w\rvert\leq\underset{i\in J}{\max}\left\{\frac{u_{i}-u_{i-1}}{2}\right\}
. Since each
\displaystyle L_{i}
, is a contraction mapping with contraction coefficient
\displaystyle a_{i}
. Choose
\displaystyle a=\underset{i\in J}{\max}\{a_{i}\}
, then for each
\displaystyle u\in I
and for each
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
we can choose
\displaystyle w\in L_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}\big(\left\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{N}\right\}\big)
for some
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
such that,

\lvert u-w\rvert\leq a^{n}\underset{i\in J}{\max}\left\{\frac{u_{i}-u_{i-1}}{2}\right\}<\epsilon.

This completes the proof.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\displaystyle F,G\in\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
and
\displaystyle\Delta
as defined in Theorem
3.2
, and
\displaystyle F(u_{1}),F(u_{N}),G(u_{1})
,
\displaystyle G(u_{N})
are single-valued. If
\displaystyle F\leq G
, then
\displaystyle F^{\alpha}\leq G^{\alpha}
provided
\displaystyle S_{F},S_{G}\in\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
satisfying
\displaystyle S_{F}\leq S_{G}
and
\displaystyle S_{F}(u_{1})=F(u_{1}),S_{F}(u_{N})=F(u_{N}),S_{G}(u_{1})=G(u_{1}),S_{G}(u_{N})=G(u_{N}).

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle S_{F},S_{G}\in\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
such that
\displaystyle S_{F}\leq S_{G}
and
\displaystyle S_{F}(u_{1})=F(u_{1}),~S_{F}(u_{N})=F(u_{N}),~S_{G}(u_{1})=G(u_{1}),~S_{G}(u_{N})=G(u_{N}).

Using Note
3.5
, we have

F^{\alpha}(u_{i})=F(u_{i}),~G^{\alpha}(u_{i})=G(u_{i})\text{ for each }i=1,\ldots,N.

From the self-referential equation,

F^{\alpha}\left(L_{j}(u)\right)=F\left(L_{j}(u)\right)+\alpha\left[F^{\alpha}(u)-S_{F}(u)\right],\text{ and }G^{\alpha}\left(L_{j}(u)\right)=G\left(L_{j}(u)\right)+\alpha\left[G^{\alpha}(u)-S_{G}(u)\right]

for each
\displaystyle u\in I_{j},
where
\displaystyle j\in J.
For
\displaystyle u\in\Delta,
we deduce

F^{\alpha}\big(L_{j}(u)\big)\subset G^{\alpha}\big(L_{j}(u)\big)\text{ for any }j\in J.

Applying the process repeatedly, we get

F^{\alpha}\left(L_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}(u)\right)\subset G^{\alpha}\left(L_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}(u)\right)\text{ for any }i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\in J,~u\in\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{N}\},

where
\displaystyle L_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}(u)=L_{i_{1}}(L_{i_{2}}(\ldots(L_{i_{n}}(u))))
and
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}.

This implies that
\displaystyle F^{\alpha}(u)\subset G^{\alpha}(u)
for each
\displaystyle u\in\underset{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\cup}\left(\underset{1\leq i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\leq N}{\cup}L_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}\big(\left\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{N}\right\}\big)\right).

Now using Lemma
4.5
and Result
4.1
we are done.
∎

5.
Dimensional Results

To move further in this section, we shall first observe some examples, then only one can understand the motivation behind this section.

Example 5.1
.

Let
\displaystyle F_{1}:[0,1]\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}
is a set-valued map defined as
\displaystyle F_{1}(u)=\{0\}
, then according to (
2.1
) graph of this function will be a line segment in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2},
and hence
\displaystyle\dim_{H}(G_{F_{1}})=1
.

Example 5.2
.

Let
\displaystyle F_{2}:[0,1]\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}
is a set-valued map defined as
\displaystyle F_{2}(u)=[-1,1]
, then by (
2.1
), we have
\displaystyle G_{F_{2}}=[0,1]\times[-1,1],
and hence
\displaystyle\dim_{H}(G_{F_{2}})=2
.

Example 5.3
.

Let
\displaystyle F_{3}:[0,1]\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}
be a set-valued map defined as
\displaystyle F_{3}(u)=C
, where
\displaystyle C
is Cantor set. Then, by (
2.1
) we have
\displaystyle G_{F_{3}}=[0,1]\times C,
and hence
\displaystyle\dim_{H}(G_{F_{2}})=1+\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}
.

Notice that
\displaystyle F_{1},F_{2},
and
\displaystyle F_{3}
are constant maps. Therefore, these are Lipschitz and bounded variation maps as well. Unlike the case of a single-valued map, here we witness that the Hausdorff dimension of the graph of a set-valued Lipschitz map is other than 1, and the same observation holds for the graph of a set-valued bounded variation map also. In fact, one can always find a set-valued Lipschitz map or set-valued bounded variation map whose graph has dimension
\displaystyle\beta
for any
\displaystyle 1\leq\beta\leq 2
. We observe that with the definition of the graph as in (
2.1
), we could not find any fascinating dimensional result, therefore we give a new definition of the graph of a set-valued map and study some dimensional results for this new definition of the graph.

Definition 5.4
.

Let
\displaystyle F:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R})
be a set-valued map, then a graph of
\displaystyle F
is defined as;

\mathcal{G}(F)=\left\{(u,F(u)):F(u)\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R})\right\}\subset[0,1]\times\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}).

(5.1)

Defined a metric on this graph,

D_{\mathcal{G}}((u,F(u)),(w,F(w)))=\lvert u-w\rvert+H_{d}(F(u),F(w)).

Next, we prove the graph of
\displaystyle F^{\alpha}
defined in (
5.1
) is an attractor of an IFS defined on
\displaystyle I\times\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
.

\displaystyle 1

Lemma 5.5
.

Define a function
\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}:I\times\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow[0,\infty)
as

\mathfrak{d}\big((u,A),(w,B)\big)=\lvert u-w\rvert+H_{d}\big(A+F^{\alpha}(w),B+F^{\alpha}(u)\big).

Then,
\displaystyle I\times\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
with respect to
\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}
is a complete metric space.

Proof.

Clearly,
\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}\big((u,A),(w,B)\big)=\mathfrak{d}\big((w,B),(u,A)\big)\geq 0
. Suppose that
\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}\big((u,A),(w,B)\big)=0
, then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lvert u-w\rvert+H_{d}\big(A+F^{\alpha}(w),B+F^{\alpha}(u)\big)=0

i.e.,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lvert u-w\rvert=0\text{ and }H_{d}\big(A+F^{\alpha}(w),B+F^{\alpha}(u)\big)=0

i.e.,

\displaystyle\displaystyle u=w~H_{d}\big(A+F^{\alpha}(w),B+F^{\alpha}(u)\big)=H_{d}(A,B)=0

i.e.,

\displaystyle\displaystyle u=w\text{ and }A=B

i.e.,

\displaystyle\displaystyle(u,A)=(w,B).

Now to prove that
\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}
satisfies the triangle inequality. Take
\displaystyle(u_{i},A_{i})\in I\times\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
for
\displaystyle i=1,2,3.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}\big((u_{1},A_{1}),(u_{2},A_{2})\big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lvert u_{1}-u_{2}\rvert+H_{d}\big(A_{1}+F^{\alpha}(u_{2}),A_{2}+F^{\alpha}(u_{1})\big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lvert u_{1}-u_{2}\rvert+H_{d}\big(A_{1}+F^{\alpha}(u_{2})+A_{3}+F^{\alpha}(u_{3}),A_{2}+F^{\alpha}(u_{1})+A_{3}+F^{\alpha}(u_{3})\big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\big\{\lvert u_{1}-u_{3}\rvert+\lvert u_{3}-u_{2}\rvert\big\}+\big\{H_{d}\big(A_{1}+F^{\alpha}(u_{3}),A_{3}+F^{\alpha}(u_{1})\big)+H_{d}\big(A_{3}+F^{\alpha}(u_{2}),A_{2}+F^{\alpha}(u_{3})\big)\big\}.

Hence,

\mathfrak{d}\bigg((u_{1},A_{1}),(u_{2},A_{2})\bigg)\leq\mathfrak{d}\bigg((u_{1},A_{1}),(u_{3},A_{3})\bigg)+\mathfrak{d}\bigg((u_{3},A_{3}),(u_{2},A_{2})\bigg).

To prove completeness, let
\displaystyle\{(u_{n},A_{n})\}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\displaystyle I\times\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
. For
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
there is an integer
\displaystyle N(\epsilon)
such that

\lvert u_{n}-u_{m}\rvert+H_{d}\big(A_{n}+F^{\alpha}(u_{n}),A_{m}+F^{\alpha}(u_{m})\big)<\epsilon,~\text{whenever}~m,n\geq N(\epsilon).

This shows
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}
is a Cauchy sequence of
\displaystyle I
, hence it converges to, say
\displaystyle u^{*}\in I.
Since
\displaystyle F^{\alpha}
is a uniformly continuous map, consequently
\displaystyle\{F^{\alpha}(u_{n})\}
will also be a Cauchy sequence with respect to Hausdorff metric, and hence converges to
\displaystyle F^{\alpha}(u^{*})\in\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
. Then,

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{d}(A_{n},A_{m})=

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{d}\big(A_{n}+F^{\alpha}(u_{n}),A_{m}+F^{\alpha}(u_{n})\big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{d}\big(A_{n}+F^{\alpha}(u_{n}),A_{n}+F^{\alpha}(u_{m})\big)+H_{d}\big(A_{n}+F^{\alpha}(u_{m})+A_{m}+F^{\alpha}(u_{n})\big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{d}\big(F^{\alpha}(u_{n}),F^{\alpha}(u_{m})\big)+H_{d}\big(A_{n}+F^{\alpha}(u_{m})+A_{m}+F^{\alpha}(u_{n})\big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}=\epsilon.

This implies,
\displaystyle\{A_{n}\}
is a Cauchy sequence of
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
and so it converges to, say
\displaystyle A^{*}\in\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}).
Hence,
\displaystyle\{(u_{n},A_{n})\}
converges to
\displaystyle(u^{*},A^{*})\in I\times\mathcal{K}_{c}(\mathbb{R}).
This completes the proof.

∎

Proposition 5.6

Proof.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}\big(W_{j}(u,A),W_{j}(w,B)\big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}\Big(\big(L_{j}(u),\alpha A+F(L_{j}(u))-\alpha S(u)\big),\big(L_{j}(w),\alpha B+F(L_{j}(w))+\alpha S(u)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
\displaystyle F,T:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle l

\displaystyle\dim_{H}(\mathcal{G}(F+T))=\dim_{H}(\mathcal{G}(T))

\displaystyle(F+T)(u):=F(u)+T(u)

\displaystyle F(u)+T(u)

\displaystyle F(u)

\displaystyle T(u)

To establish the proof of this lemma, it will be sufficient to show the existence of a Lipschitz map from
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(T)
to
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(F+T)

Define
\displaystyle\Phi:\mathcal{G}(T)\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(F+T)

\displaystyle\Phi(u,T(u))=(u,F(u)+T(u))

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{G}}(\Phi(u,T(u)),\Phi(w,T(w)))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=D_{\mathcal{G}}((u,F(u)+T(u)),(w,F(w)+T(w)))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\lvert u-w\rvert+H_{d}(F(u)+T(u),F(w)+T(w))

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\lvert u-w\rvert+H_{d}(F(u),F(w))+H_{d}(T(u),T(w))

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\lvert u-w\rvert+l\lvert u-w\rvert+H_{d}(T(u),T(w))

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq(1+l)\left\{\lvert u-w\rvert+H_{d}(T(u),T(w))\right\}.

That is,
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{G}}(\Phi(u,T(u)),\Phi(w,T(w)))\leq(1+l)D_{\mathcal{G}}((u,T(u)),(w,T(w))).

Hence,
\displaystyle\Phi
being a Lipschitz map implying that

\dim_{H}(\mathcal{G}(F+T))\leq\dim_{H}(\mathcal{G}(T))\text{ and }\dim_{B}(\mathcal{G}(F+T))\leq\dim_{B}(\mathcal{G}(T)).

For other side of the inequality, let
\displaystyle t>\dim_{H}(\mathcal{G}(F+T)).
Then, by definition of the Hausdorff dimension, we have the following.

For each
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
and for each
\displaystyle\eta>0,
there is an open cover
\displaystyle\{U_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(F+T))
such that
\displaystyle\lvert U_{n}\rvert<\eta
and
\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\lvert U_{n}\rvert^{s}<\epsilon.

Note that

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(T)=

\displaystyle\{(u,T(u)):u\in I\}

(5.4)

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\{(u,T(u)+F(u)-F(u)):u\in I\}

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\{(u,T(u)+F(u)):u\in I\}+\{(0,-F(u)):u\in I\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(F+T))+\{(0,-F(u)):u\in I\}.

Define
\displaystyle V_{n}=U_{n}+\{(0,-F(u)):u\in I\text{ such that }(u,T(u)+F(u))\in U_{n}\}.
Observe that each
\displaystyle V_{n}
is open and
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(T)\subseteq\underset{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\cup}V_{n}.
Further,
\displaystyle\lvert V_{n}\rvert\leq(1+l)\lvert U_{n}\rvert
and
\displaystyle\lvert V_{n}\rvert<(1+l)\eta.
Then,

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\lvert V_{n}\rvert^{t}\leq(1+l)^{t}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\lvert U_{n}\rvert^{t}\leq(1+l)^{t}\epsilon.

This gives
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\eta}^{t}(\mathcal{G}(T))=0,
that is, the
\displaystyle t
-dimensional Hausdorff measure,
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{t}(\mathcal{G}(T))=0.
Therefore, we have
\displaystyle\dim_{H}(\mathcal{G}(T))\leq\dim_{H}(\mathcal{G}(F+T)),
proving
\displaystyle\dim_{H}(\mathcal{G}(T))=\dim_{H}(\mathcal{G}(F+T)).

Next, using (
5.4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{\dim}_{B}(\mathcal{G}(T))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\varlimsup_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log N_{(1+l)\eta}(\mathcal{G}(T))}{-\log((1+l)\eta)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\varlimsup_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log N_{\eta}(\mathcal{G}(F+T))}{-\log((1+l)\eta)}=\varlimsup_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log N_{\eta}(\mathcal{G}(F+T))}{-\log(\eta)}=\overline{\dim}_{B}(\mathcal{G}(F+T)),

as desired.
∎

Remark 5.13
.

The above lemma holds for single-valued maps also (see for instance

\displaystyle 3.2
]
), but proof of this is neither straight forward nor just a simple extension of single-valued map. Because Hausdorff metric does not satisfy the parallelogram law while in
[

, Lemma
\displaystyle 3.2
]
metric is usual metric defined on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
which satisfies the parallelogram law and gives the privilege to enjoy the bi-Lipschitz property to
\displaystyle T
defined in
[

\displaystyle 3.2
]
(
\displaystyle\Phi
in our case).

In view of the Lipschitz invariance property of dimension, one may conclude that the upcoming theorem holds for all aforementioned dimensions.

Theorem 5.14
.

Consider
\displaystyle 1\leq\beta
. Then, set
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\beta}:=\{F\in\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R})):\dim_{H}(\mathcal{G}(F))=\beta\}
is dense in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R})).

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle F\in\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
and
\displaystyle\epsilon>0.
Using the density of
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}ip(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
, there exists
\displaystyle G
in
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}ip(I,\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}))
such that

\lVert F-G\rVert_{\infty}<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Further, we consider a non-vanishing function
\displaystyle H\in\mathcal{S}_{\beta}.
Let
\displaystyle H_{*}=G+\frac{\epsilon}{2\lVert H\rVert_{\infty}}H,
which immediately gives

\lVert G-H_{*}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

This together with Lemma
5.12
implies that
\displaystyle\dim(Gr(H_{*}))=\dim(Gr(H))=\beta.
Hence, we have
\displaystyle H_{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{\beta}
and

\lVert F-H_{*}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lVert F-G\rVert_{\infty}+\lVert G-H_{*}\rVert_{\infty}<\epsilon.

This completes the proof.
∎

Before proving our next result, let us note the following lemma as a prelude.

Lemma 5.15
.

Consider
\displaystyle A,B,C
are compact subsets of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}.
Then,

H_{d}(AB,CB)\leq\sup_{b\in B}\lvert b\rvert H_{d}(A,C),

where
\displaystyle YZ=\left\{yz:y\in Y\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}),~z\in Z\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R})\right\}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{d}(AB,CB)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\max\Big\{\sup_{ab\in AB}\inf_{cb^{\prime}\in CB}\lvert ab-cb^{\prime}\rvert,\sup_{cb^{\prime}\in CB}\inf_{ab\in AB}\lvert cb^{\prime}-ab\rvert\Big\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\max\Big\{\sup_{ab\in AB}\inf_{cb\in Cb}\lvert ab-cb\rvert,\sup_{cb^{\prime}\in CB}\inf_{ab^{\prime}\in Ab^{\prime}}\lvert cb^{\prime}-ab^{\prime}\rvert\Big\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\max\Big\{\sup_{ab\in AB}\inf_{cb\in Cb}\lvert b\rvert\lvert a-c\rvert,\sup_{cb^{\prime}\in CB}\inf_{ab^{\prime}\in Ab^{\prime}}\lvert b^{\prime}\rvert\lvert c-a\rvert\Big\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\max\Big\{\sup_{a\in A,b\in B}(\lvert b\rvert\inf_{cb^{\prime}\in CB}\lvert a-c\rvert),\sup_{c\in C,b^{\prime}\in B}(\lvert b^{\prime}\rvert\inf_{ab^{\prime}\in Ab^{\prime}}\lvert c-a\rvert)\Big\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\sup_{b\in B}\lvert b\rvert\max\Big\{\sup_{a\in A}\inf_{c\in C}\lvert a-c\rvert,\sup_{c\in C}\inf_{a\in A}\lvert c-a\rvert\Big\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sup_{b\in B}\lvert b\rvert H_{d}(A,C),

proving the assertion.
∎

Next we define the multiplication of set-valued maps
\displaystyle F,L:W\subseteq\mathbb{R}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}
by
\displaystyle(FT)(w)=F(w)T(w).

Lemma 5.16
.

Consider
\displaystyle F,T:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R})
to be set-valued Lipschitz map with Lipschitz constant
\displaystyle l
. Then,

\dim_{H}(\mathcal{G}(FT))\leq\dim_{H}(\mathcal{G}(T)).

Proof.

Define
\displaystyle\Phi:\mathcal{G}(T)\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(FT)
such that

\Phi\big((u,T(u))\big)=\big(u,F(u)T(u)\big).

Choose
\displaystyle M=\max\{1+l\underset{z\in\underset{u\in[0,1]}{\cup}Tu}{\sup}\lvert z\rvert,\underset{v\in\underset{w\in[0,1]}{\cup}Fw}{\sup}\lvert v\rvert\}.

Notice that
\displaystyle\Phi
is well defined and surjective. To prove our lemma, it is enough to prove
\displaystyle\Phi
is Lipschitz map. For this,

\displaystyle\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{G}}(\Phi(u,Tu),\Phi(w,Tw))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=D_{\mathcal{G}}((u,FuTu),(w,FwTw))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\lvert u-w\rvert+H_{d}(FuTu,FwTw)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\lvert u-w\rvert+H_{d}(FuTu,FwTu)+H_{d}(FwTu,FwTw)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\lvert u-w\rvert+\underset{z\in Tu}{\sup}\lvert z\rvert H_{d}(Fu,Fw)+\underset{v\in Fw}{\sup}\lvert v\rvert H_{d}(Tu,Tw)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\lvert u-w\rvert+\underset{z\in Tu}{\sup}\lvert z\rvert l\lvert u-w\rvert+\underset{v\in Fw}{\sup}\lvert v\rvert H_{d}(Tu,Tw)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq M\left\{\lvert u-w\rvert+H_{d}(Tu,Tw)\right\}.

Hence,
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{G}}(\Phi(u,Tu),\Phi(w,Tw))\leq MD_{\mathcal{G}}((u,Tu),(w,Tw)).

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.17

In the Lemma
5.16

\displaystyle T
be a Weierstrass function whose Hausdorff dimension is strictly greater than 1 (refer
[

]
) and
\displaystyle F
to be the zero function. Then, we obtain
\displaystyle 1=\dim_{H}(\mathcal{G}(FT))<\dim_{H}(\mathcal{G}(T)).

Definition 5.18
.

Consider
\displaystyle W
be a bounded and closed interval of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
and
\displaystyle F:W\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}
is a set-valued map. The maximum range of
\displaystyle F
over the rectangle
\displaystyle W
is defined as

R_{F}[W]=\sup_{x,y\in W}~\sup_{w,z\in F(x)\cup F(y)}\lvert w-z\rvert.

As indicated in the introductory section, next we shall provide a set-valued analogue of
[
15
, Proposition
\displaystyle 11.1
]
.

Proposition 5.19
.

Assume
\displaystyle F:[w,u]\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}
be a set-valued continuous map,
\displaystyle 0<\eta<u-w,
and
\displaystyle\frac{u-w}{\eta}\leq m\leq 1+\frac{u-w}{\eta}
for some
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}.
If
\displaystyle N_{\eta}(G_{F})
is the number of
\displaystyle\eta
-boxes that intersect the graph of
\displaystyle F,
then

\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{i=1}^{m}R_{F}[W_{i}]\leq N_{\eta}(G_{F})\leq 2m+\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{i=1}^{m}R_{F}[W_{i}],

where
\displaystyle W_{i}=[i\eta,(i+1)\eta]
.

Proof.

The count of squares having
\displaystyle\eta
side length in the part above
\displaystyle W_{i}
intersecting the graph of
\displaystyle F
is at least
\displaystyle\frac{R_{F}[W_{i}]}{\eta}
and at most
\displaystyle 2+\frac{R_{F}[W_{i}]}{\eta},
using the continuity of
\displaystyle F
. Taking Sum over all such parts yield the required bounds.
∎

Example 5.20
.

Consider
\displaystyle F:[0,1]\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}
is a set-valued map defined as
\displaystyle F(x)=[-1,1].
By Proposition
5.19
, we have

\overline{\dim}_{B}(G_{F})=\varlimsup_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log N_{\eta}(G_{F})}{-\log(\eta)}\leq\varlimsup_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log\Big(2m+\frac{1}{\eta}\overset{m}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}R_{F}[W_{i}]\Big)}{-\log(\eta)}\leq\varlimsup_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log\Big(2m+\frac{1}{\eta}\overset{m}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}2\Big)}{-\log(\eta)}=2,

because
\displaystyle R_{F}[W_{i}]=2\text{ for each }i=1,\ldots,m
and
\displaystyle W_{i}=[i\eta,(i+1)\eta]
. Similarly,

\underline{\dim}_{B}(G_{F})=\varliminf_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log N_{\eta}(G_{F})}{-\log(\eta)}\geq\varliminf_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log\Big(\frac{1}{\eta}\overset{m}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}R_{F}[W_{i}]\Big)}{-\log(\eta)}=\varliminf_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log\Big(\frac{1}{\eta}\overset{m}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}2\Big)}{-\log(\eta)}=2.

Therefore,
\displaystyle\dim_{B}(G_{F})=2.
This shows that Proposition
5.19
will be very useful in estimating or finding box dimension of set-valued functions.

6.
Conclusion and Future Direction

In this paper, the term
\displaystyle\alpha
-fractal function has been introduced (Theorem
3.2
), corresponding to set-valued maps. Next, we noticed that, unlike a single-valued
\displaystyle\alpha
-fractal function, a set-valued
\displaystyle\alpha
-fractal function is generally not interpolatory. Still, under certain conditions, it is interpolatory in nature (Remark
3.4
, Note
3.5
). Also, some properties of this fractal function have been observed (Theorem
3.7
, Theorem
3.12
). After that, the existence of fractal polynomial, which approximates the convex set-valued map, was established (Theorem
4.2
). Also, the concept of constrained approximation for set-valued maps is introduced (Theorem
4.6
). Further, we added the definition of a graph of a set-valued map (Definition
5.4
) and calculated the fractal dimension of this graph for some class of set-valued maps (Theorem
5.10
, Lemma
5.12
, and Lemma
5.16
).

In this paper, we have taken Minkowski sum of two sets. In the future, we may try to study the fractal functions using metric linear sum of two sets introduced by Dyn and her group
[
9
]
. Here most of the results are available for convex set-valued maps but using metric linear sum of two sets, we may try to establish these results for the compact set-valued map.

Further, fractional calculus for the single-valued map has been widely explored. See, for instance
[
19
,
12
]
. In the future, we may try to extend this concept of fractional calculus for set-valued maps and estimate some dimensional results for the graph of the fractional integral and fractional differentiation of set-valued maps.

Another future direction of work is in selection of set-valued maps. Following remark can work as a motivation.

Remark 6.1
.

Consider
\displaystyle F:I\to\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R})
to be set-valued function, then a
function
\displaystyle f:I\to\mathbb{R}
will be characterized as a selection of
\displaystyle F
if
\displaystyle f(x)\in F(x)
for
all
\displaystyle x\in I.
It is an interesting fact to note that for any
\displaystyle 1\leq\beta\leq 2
, we are getting a selection
\displaystyle f_{\beta}:I\to\mathbb{R}
of map
\displaystyle F_{2}
of Example
5.2
such that
\displaystyle\dim_{H}(Gr(f_{\beta}))=\beta.
This motivates us to ask a natural question that whether such a selection respecting dimension exists or not.
