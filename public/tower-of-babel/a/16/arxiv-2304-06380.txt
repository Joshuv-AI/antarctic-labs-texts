Conciseness on normal subgroups and new concise words from lower central and derived words
Gustavo A. Fernández-Alcober, Matteo Pintonello

Abstract
. Let w = w ⁡ ( x 1 , … , x r ) w=w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}) be a lower central word or a derived word. We show that the word w ⁡ ( u 1 , … , u r ) w(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}) is concise whenever u 1 , … , u r u_{1},\ldots,u_{r} are non-commutator words in disjoint sets of variables, thus proving a generalized version of a conjecture of Azevedo and Shumyatsky. This applies in particular to words of the form w ⁡ ( x 1 n 1 , … , x r n r ) w(x_{1}^{n_{1}},\ldots,x_{r}^{n_{r}}) , where the n i n_{i} are non-zero integers. Our approach is via the study of values of w w on normal subgroups, and in this setting we obtain the following result: if N 1 , … , N r N_{1},\ldots,N_{r} are normal subgroups of a group G G and the set of all values w ⁡ ( g 1 , … , g r ) w(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}) with g i ∈ N i g_{i}\in N_{i} is finite then also the subgroup generated by these values, i.e. w ⁡ ( N 1 , … , N r ) w(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r}) , is finite.

1.
Introduction

Let
w=w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
be a group word, which we identify with an element of the free group
F=F(X)
of countable rank on the set

X=\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
For every group
G
and every
r
-tuple
\mathbf{g}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})
of elements of
G
, we can consider the element
w(\mathbf{g})
resulting from replacing each
x_{i}
with
g_{i}
.
We thus obtain the
set of word values
of
w
in
G
,

w\{G\}=\{w(\mathbf{g})\mid\mathbf{g}\in G\times\overset{r}{\cdots}\times G\}.

The subgroup generated by
w\{G\}
is called the
verbal subgroup
of
w
in
G
, and is denoted by
w(G)
.
More generally, if
\mathbf{S}=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{r})
is an
r
-tuple of subsets of
G
, we can consider the set of values

w\{\mathbf{S}\}=\{w(\mathbf{g})\mid\mathbf{g}\in S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{r}\},

and the corresponding verbal subgroup on
\mathbf{S}
, namely

w(\mathbf{S})=\langle w\{\mathbf{S}\}\rangle
.
Of special interest is the case when
\mathbf{S}
is a tuple
\mathbf{N}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r})
of normal subgroups of
G
.
We then say that
w(\mathbf{N})
is the
\mathbf{N}
-verbal subgroup
of
w
and that it is a verbal subgroup on normal subgroups.

If a word
w
satisfies the property that, whenever the set of values
w\{G\}
is finite in a group
G
, then the verbal subgroup
w(G)
is also finite, we say that
w
is a
concise
word.
Philip Hall conjectured that all words are concise.
This is one of his three famous problems on verbal subgroups; see Section 4.2 in Robinson’s book

[
12
]
or Section 1.4 in Segal’s book
[
13
]
.
In the attempt to settle this conjecture, the following types of words were proved to be concise:

(i)

Non-commutator words, that is, words lying outside
F^{\prime}
.
Equivalently, these are the words for which the exponent sum of some variable
x_{i}
is non-zero.
(P. Hall, unpublished.)

(ii)

Lower commutator words
\gamma_{r}
, defined by

\gamma_{r}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}],

and whose corresponding verbal subgroup is the
r
th term of the lower central series.
(P. Hall, unpublished.)

(iii)

Derived words
\delta_{k}
, defined recursively by
\delta_{0}=x_{1}
and

\delta_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{k}})=[\delta_{k-1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{k-1}}),\delta_{k-1}(x_{2^{k-1}+1},\ldots,x_{2^{k}})].

(Turner-Smith
[
14
]
.)
The verbal subgroup of
\delta_{k}
is the
k
th derived subgroup of the group.

(iv)

More generally, outer commutator words, also known as multilinear words.
(Wilson
[
15
]
.)
These are words obtained by nesting commutators of variables arbitrarily, but never repeating a variable.
For example,
\big[[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}],[[x_{4},x_{5}],[x_{6},x_{7}]]\big]
is an outer commutator.

For convenience, a single variable is also considered to be an outer commutator word.
Obviously, lower central words and derived words are outer commutator words.

However, Ivanov
[
10
]
refuted P. Hall’s conjecture in 1989, showing that the word

[[x^{pn},y^{pn}]^{n},y^{pn}]^{n}
is not concise for big enough
n
and
p
, where
n
is odd and
p
is a prime.
Another counterexample can be found in Theorem 39.7 of Olshanskii’s book
[
11
]
.

It was not until 2011 when new examples of concise words were discovered.
In two independent papers, Abdollahi and Russo
[
1
]
, and Fernández-Alcober, Morigi and Traustason
[
7
]
proved that the Engel word

e_{n}(x,y)=[x,y,\overset{n}{\ldots},y]

is concise for
n=2
,
3
and
4
.
To date, it is not known whether
e_{n}
is concise for
n\geq 5
.
The topic of conciseness experienced a revival in the last years in the works of Shumyatsky and his collaborators.
On the one hand, they have studied some variations of conciseness in profinite groups, and on the other hand, they have proved that the following families of words are concise in all groups:

(v)

If
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are non-commutator words in disjoint sets of variables, then
[u_{1},u_{2}]
is concise.
(Delizia, Shumyatsky, Tortora, and Tota
[
4
]
.)
The same happens with commutators
[u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}]
of non-commutators.
(Azevedo, Shumyatsky
[
2
]
.)

(vi)

If
u=u(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
is a non-commutator word and we produce copies of
u
in disjoint variables by setting
u_{i}=u(x_{i1},\ldots,x_{ik})
for
i=1,\ldots,r
, then
[u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}]
and
[v,u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}]
are concise, where
v
is another non-commutator, in a set of variables disjoint from those of
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
.
(Azevedo, Shumyatsky
[
2
]
.)

(vii)

If
u
is an outer commutator word and
v
is a non-commutator in disjoint variables, then
[u,v]
is concise.
(Azevedo, Shumyatsky
[
2
]
.)

In the light of (v) and (vi) above, Azevedo and Shumyatsky made the following conjecture in
[
2
]
.

Conjecture

(Azevedo, Shumyatsky)
.

Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
be non-commutator words in disjoint sets of variables.
Then the word

\gamma_{r}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})=[u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}]

is concise.

The main result in this paper is the proof of this conjecture, not only for lower central words, but also for derived words.

Theorem A
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
be a lower central word or a derived word.
If
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
are non-commutator words in disjoint sets of variables, then the word
w(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})
is concise.
In particular, the word
w(x_{1}^{n_{1}},\ldots,x_{r}^{n_{r}})
is concise whenever

n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}\smallsetminus\{0\}
.

In
[
6
]
Fernández-Alcober and Morigi showed that if a word
w
is concise then it is actually
boundedly concise
, that is,
there is a function
f_{w}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that whenever
|w\{G\}|=m
in a group
G
, we have
|w(G)|\leq f_{w}(m)
.
Their argument requires the use of non-principal ultrafilters over infinite sets, whose existence is independent of Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory.
We remark that our proof of Theorem A shows that the words therein are boundedly concise, without relying on the result in
[
6
]
.

Our approach to Theorem A is via the study of
w\{\mathbf{N}\}
and
w(\mathbf{N})
for an
r
-tuple
\mathbf{N}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r})
of normal subgroups of a group
G
.
More precisely, for every
i=1,\ldots,r
we consider a generating set
S_{i}
of
N_{i}
that is a normal subset of
G
, and relate
w(\mathbf{N})
to
w\{\mathbf{S}\}
, where
\mathbf{S}=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{r})
.
Under an additional assumption on the sets
S_{i}
, we are able to prove that the finiteness of

w\{\mathbf{S}\}
implies that of
w(\mathbf{N})
(see
2.11
and

3.6
).
Theorem A is a corollary of this general result, and another consequence of it is the theorem below, which we think has an independent interest.

Theorem B
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
be a lower central word or a derived word.
Assume that
\mathbf{N}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r})
is a tuple of normal subgroups of a group
G
such that

w\{\mathbf{N}\}
is finite.
Then the subgroup
w(\mathbf{N})
is also finite.

If a word
w
satisfies the property stated in Theorem B, we say that
w
is

concise on normal subgroups
.
Note that Theorem B was known for the usual commutator word
w=[x,y]
, as a consequence of Theorem 4.14 in Robinson’s book
[
12
]
.
Actually, it suffices to require that either of the subgroups
H
or
K
are ascendant in
\langle H,K\rangle
in order to conclude that
[H,K]
is finite whenever the set
\{[h,k]\mid h\in H,\ k\in K\}
is finite.
Baer
[
3
]
had previously proved this result under the assumption that one of
H
or
K
is normal in
\langle H,K\rangle
.
We impose the stronger condition that all subgroups should be normal, but in return we generalize the result to all lower central and derived words.
Theorem B actually holds with bounds: if
w\{\mathbf{N}\}
is of order
m
, then the order of
w(\mathbf{N})
can be bounded by a function of
m
and
w
.
We conclude this introduction with an extension of the conjecture of Azevedo and Shumyatsky.

Conjecture
.

Theorems A and B hold for an arbitrary outer commutator word
w
.

2.
Preliminaries and lower central words

We start with a few results regarding word values and verbal subgroups on normal subgroups or on normal subsets, in the case of outer commutator words.
If
w=w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
is an outer commutator word that is not a variable, then we can write
w=[\alpha,\beta]
, where
\alpha
and
\beta
are again outer commutator words.
Without loss of generality, after renaming variables if necessary, we may assume that
\alpha=\alpha(x_{1},\ldots,x_{q})
and
\beta=\beta(x_{q+1},\ldots,x_{r})
, with
1\leq q<r
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
be an outer commutator word, and let

\mathbf{N}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r})
be an
r
-tuple of normal subgroups of a group
G
.

(i)

Assume that
w=[\alpha,\beta]
, with
\alpha=\alpha(x_{1},\ldots,x_{q})
and
\beta=\beta(x_{q+1},\ldots,x_{r})
.
If we set
\mathbf{N}_{1}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{q})
and
\mathbf{N}_{2}=(N_{q+1},\ldots,N_{r})
, then

w(\mathbf{N})=[\alpha(\mathbf{N}_{1}),\beta(\mathbf{N}_{2})]
.

(ii)

Assume that
N_{i}=\langle S_{i}\rangle
for every
i=1,\ldots,r
, where each
S_{i}
is a normal subset of
G
.
If we set
\mathbf{S}=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{r})
, then the subgroup
w(\mathbf{N})
is generated by
w\{\mathbf{S}\}
.

Proof.

Both (i) and (ii) follow immediately from the simple fact that if
S
and
T
are two normal subsets of a group
G
then

[\langle S\rangle,\langle T\rangle]=\langle[s,t]\mid s\in S,\ t\in T\rangle,

where for part (ii) we use (i) and induction on the number of variables.
∎

We are interested in words of the form
w(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})
, where
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
are non-commutator words that involve different variables.
Let us introduce the following concept.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
be group words.
We say that these words are disjoint if the sets of variables that they involve are pairwise disjoint.

If
w
is a word in
r
variables and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
are disjoint words, then the set of values of the word
w^{*}=w(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})
in a group
G
can be written as
w\{\mathbf{S}\}
, where

\mathbf{S}=(u_{1}\{G\},\ldots,u_{r}\{G\}).

Since every
u_{i}\{G\}
is a normal subset of
G
, we get the following consequence of the previous lemma.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
be an outer commutator word and let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
be arbitrary disjoint words.
If
w^{*}=w(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})
then for every group
G
we have

w^{*}(G)=w(u_{1}(G),\ldots,u_{r}(G)).

Now we want to make part (ii) of
2.1
quantitative.
If we take a standard generator
w(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
of
w(\mathbf{N})
, with
n_{i}\in N_{i}
, how can we estimate the number of factors from
w\{\mathbf{S}\}^{\pm 1}
that are needed to write it?
We need to introduce the following notation.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
G
be a group and let
S
be a subset of
G
.
For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
S^{\ast n}
to be the set of all products of elements of
S\cup S^{-1}
of length at most
n
.

In other references,
S^{\ast n}
is defined as the set of products of exactly
n
elements of
S
.
Since we can always replace
S
with
S\cup S^{-1}\cup\{1\}
, both definitions are basically equivalent.
We prefer the definition above because it suits better the description of the elements of the subgroup
\langle S\rangle
.
Also, with this definition, we have
S^{\ast k}\subseteq S^{\ast n}
whenever
n\geq k
.
Note that if
|S|\leq m
then
|S^{\ast n}|\leq(2m+1)^{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let us connect this concept with values of outer commutator words.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
be an outer commutator word, and let
S
be a normal subset of a group
G
.
Suppose that
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})
is a tuple of elements of
G
, one of whose components belongs to
S
.
Then
w(\mathbf{t})\in S^{\ast 2^{r-1}}
.

Proof.

The result is obvious for
r=1
, so we assume
r>1
.
Then we can write
w(\mathbf{t})=[\alpha(\mathbf{t^{\prime}}),\beta(\mathbf{t^{\prime\prime}})]
, where
\alpha
and
\beta
are outer commutator words, and the tuples
\mathbf{t^{\prime}}
and
\mathbf{t^{\prime\prime}}
form a partition of
\mathbf{t}
.
Assume without loss of generality that
\mathbf{t^{\prime}}
contains an entry from
S
.
By induction on
r
, we have
\alpha(\mathbf{t^{\prime}})\in S^{\ast 2^{r-2}}
.
Consequently,

w(\mathbf{t})=\alpha(\mathbf{t^{\prime}})^{-1}\alpha(\mathbf{t^{\prime}})^{\beta(\mathbf{t^{\prime\prime}})}\in S^{\ast 2^{r-1}},

since
S
is a normal subset of
G
.
∎

On the other hand, by Lemma 2.8 of
[
9
]
, if
w=w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
is an outer commutator and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r},h
are elements of a group
G
, then for every
i=1,\ldots,r
we have

w(g_{1},\ldots,g_{i-1},g_{i}h,g_{i+1},\ldots,g_{r})=w(g_{1}^{*},\ldots,g_{i-1}^{*},g_{i}^{*},g_{i+1}^{*},\ldots,g_{r}^{*})\,\cdot w(g_{1},\ldots,g_{i-1},h,g_{i+1},\ldots,g_{r}),

where
g_{j}^{*}
is a conjugate of
g_{j}
in
G
for every
j=1,\ldots,r
.
The following lemma follows easily from this result by induction on

m_{1}\cdots m_{r}
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
be an outer commutator word, and let
\mathbf{S}=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{r})
be a tuple of normal subsets of a group
G
.
If
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})
with
t_{i}\in S_{i}^{\ast m_{i}}
for every
i=1,\ldots,r
, then

w(\mathbf{t})\in w\{\mathbf{S}\}^{\ast m_{1}\dots m_{r}}.

In an abelian group
G
, the word map
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})\mapsto w(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})
is a group homomorphism for every word
w
, and consequently
w(G)=w\{G\}
.
Of course, this is far from being true in arbitrary groups.
Outer commutator words, although they are also called multilinear words because the same type of commutator arrangements yields multinear words in Lie rings, are not multilinear in groups.
However, our approach to proving Theorems A and B relies on showing that, in suitable sections that cover the section
w(\mathbf{N})/w(\mathbf{N})^{\prime}
, outer commutator words are linear in one specific variable (which depends on the section).
To this purpose, we give the following definition.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
be a word and let
\mathbf{N}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r})
be an
r
-tuple of normal subgroups of a group
G
.
We say that
w
is linear in position
i
of the tuple
\mathbf{N}

provided that,
for all
g_{j}\in N_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,r
and
h_{i}\in N_{i}
, we have

(2.1)

w(g_{1},\ldots,g_{i-1},g_{i}h_{i},g_{i+1},\ldots,g_{r})=w(g_{1},\ldots,g_{i-1},g_{i},g_{i+1},\ldots,g_{r})\,\cdot w(g_{1},\ldots,g_{i-1},h_{i},g_{i+1},\ldots,g_{r}).

Typically, we will search for linearity in a normal section
K/L
of the ambient group
G
that is generated by the image of
w\{\mathbf{N}\}
, so that condition (
2.1
) above is required to hold modulo
L
.
Obviously, this type of linearity is inherited by sections of the form
KN/LN
for a given
N\trianglelefteq G
.
Next we show that it is also preserved under taking suitable commutators.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
w=[\alpha,\beta]
be an outer commutator word, where

\alpha=\alpha(x_{1},\ldots,x_{q})
and
\beta=\beta(x_{q+1},\ldots,x_{r})
.
Assume that
\mathbf{N}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r})
is a tuple of normal subgroups of a group
G
, and set
\mathbf{N}_{1}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{q})
and

\mathbf{N}_{2}=(N_{q+1},\ldots,N_{r})
.
Then the following hold:

(i)

If
K/L
is a normal section of
G
generated by the image of
\alpha\{\mathbf{N}_{1}\}
and

\alpha
is linear in component
i
of
\mathbf{N}_{1}
modulo
L
, then
the section
U/V
, where
U=[K,\beta(\mathbf{N}_{2})]
and

V=[w(\mathbf{N}),\alpha(\mathbf{N}_{1})][L,\beta(\mathbf{N}_{2})]
, is generated by the image of
w\{\mathbf{N}\}
and
w
is linear in component
i
of
\mathbf{N}
modulo
V
.

(ii)

If
K/L
is a normal section of
G
generated by the image of
\beta\{\mathbf{N}_{2}\}
and

\beta
is linear in component
i
of
\mathbf{N}_{2}
modulo
L
, then
the section
U/V
, with
U=[\alpha(\mathbf{N}_{1}),K]
and

V=[w(\mathbf{N}),\beta(\mathbf{N}_{2})][\alpha(\mathbf{N}_{1}),L]
, is generated by the image of
w\{\mathbf{N}\}
and
w
is linear in component
q+i
of
\mathbf{N}
modulo
V
.

Proof.

We only prove part (i).
To start with, we have

[K,\beta(\mathbf{N}_{2})]=[\alpha(\mathbf{N}_{1})L,\beta(\mathbf{N}_{2})]=[\alpha(\mathbf{N}_{1}),\beta(\mathbf{N}_{2})]\,[L,\beta(\mathbf{N}_{2})]=w(\mathbf{N})\,[L,\beta(\mathbf{N}_{2})],

where the last equality follows from (i) of
2.1
.
Thus the section
U/V
is generated by the image of
w\{\mathbf{N}\}
.

As for the assertion about linearity, let us consider the general congruence stating that
\alpha
is linear in component
i
of
\mathbf{N}_{1}
modulo
L
.
This can be written in the form
x\equiv yz\pmod{L}
, where
x
,
y
, and
z
are like the three elements appearing in (
2.1
) (with
\alpha
playing the role of
w
).
In particular,
x,y,z\in\alpha\{\mathbf{N}_{1}\}
.
Standard commutator identities then yield that, for every
n\in\beta\{\mathbf{N}_{2}\}
, we have

[x,n]\equiv[y,n][z,n]\pmod{[\alpha(\mathbf{N}_{1}),\beta(\mathbf{N}_{2}),\alpha(\mathbf{N}_{1})]\,[L,\beta(\mathbf{N}_{2})]}.

This proves the result.
∎

We can use the previous lemma to determine, for every lower central word
\gamma_{r}
and every
r
-tuple
\mathbf{N}
of normal subgroups, a series from

[\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N}),\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N})]
to
\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N})
that is linear for
\gamma_{r}
at every section.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
.
Assume that
\mathbf{N}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r})
is a tuple of normal subgroups of a group
G
,
and define

\mathbf{N}_{i}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{i-1},\gamma_{i}(N_{1},\ldots,N_{i}),N_{i+1},\ldots,N_{r})

for every
i=1,\ldots,r
.
Then there is a series

[\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N}),\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N})]=P_{r+1}^{r}\leq P_{r}^{r}\leq\cdots\leq P_{i}^{r}\leq\cdots\leq P_{1}^{r}=\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N})

such that, for every
i=1,\ldots,r
, the section
P_{i}^{r}/P_{i+1}^{r}
is generated by the image of
\gamma_{r}\{\mathbf{N}_{i}\}
and the word
\gamma_{r}
is linear in component
i
of
\mathbf{N}_{i}
modulo
P_{i+1}^{r}
.

Proof.

Set
Q_{i}^{r}=\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N}_{i})
for every
i=1,\ldots,r
, and

Q_{r+1}^{r}=[\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N}),\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N})]
.
Obviously, the conditions that
P_{r+1}^{r}=[\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N}),\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N})]
and that

P_{i}^{r}/P_{i+1}^{r}
is generated by the image of
Q_{i}^{r}
mean that we need to choose

P_{i}^{r}=Q_{i}^{r}Q_{i+1}^{r}\ldots Q_{r+1}^{r}
, for
i=1,\ldots,r+1
.

Let us then prove the linearity of
\gamma_{r}
in component
i
of
\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N}_{i})
modulo
P_{i+1}^{r}
.
We argue by induction on
r-i
.
The basis of the induction,
i=r
, follows from the congruence

[g,x_{r}y_{r}]\equiv[g,x_{r}][g,y_{r}]\pmod{P_{r+1}}
for all
g\in G
and

x_{r},y_{r}\in\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N})
,
which holds because
P_{r+1}^{r}=[\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N}),\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N})]
.

Let us now assume that
1\leq i<r
and that the result is true for differences less than
r-i
.
For every
i=1,\ldots,r
, let
Q_{i}^{r-1}
and
P_{i}^{r-1}
be defined from the tuple
\mathbf{N}^{*}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r-1})
in the same way as we defined
P_{i}^{r}
and
Q_{i}^{r}
from
\mathbf{N}
.
Then linearity holds in position
i
of

\mathbf{N}_{i}^{*}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{i-1},\gamma_{i}(N_{1},\ldots,N_{i}),N_{i+1},\ldots,N_{r-1})

modulo
P_{i+1}^{r-1}
.
Now we apply
2.8
by taking
K=P_{i}^{r-1}
,
L=P_{i+1}^{r-1}
,

\alpha=\gamma_{r-1}
and
\beta=x_{r}
.
Thus
\gamma_{r}
is linear in component
i
of
\mathbf{N}_{i}
modulo the subgroup

(2.2)

[\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N}),\gamma_{r-1}(\mathbf{N}^{*})]\,[P_{i+1}^{r-1},N_{r}].

Observe that

[\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N}),\gamma_{r-1}(\mathbf{N}^{*})]=[N_{1},\ldots,N_{r-1},\gamma_{r}(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r})]=Q_{r}^{r}\leq P_{i+1}^{r},

since
r-i\geq 1
.
On the other hand,

[P_{i+1}^{r-1},N_{r}]=\Bigg(\prod_{j=i+1}^{r-1}\,[Q_{j}^{r-1},N_{r}]\Bigg)\cdot[Q_{r}^{r-1},N_{r}]=\Bigg(\prod_{j=i+1}^{r-1}\,Q_{j}^{r}\Bigg)\cdot[Q_{r}^{r-1},N_{r}],

and

[Q_{r}^{r-1},N_{r}]=[\gamma_{r-1}(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r-1}),\gamma_{r-1}(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r-1}),N_{r}]\leq[N_{1},\ldots,N_{r-1},\gamma_{r}(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r})]=Q_{r}^{r},

where the inclusion follows from P. Hall’s Three Subgroup Lemma.
Hence the subgroup in (
2.2
) is contained in
P_{i+1}^{r}
, and the result follows.
∎

We are now in a position to prove the key theorem that will provide both Theorem A and Theorem B for lower central words.
We need the following version for normal subgroups of a well-known lemma in the theory of concise words (see, for example,
[
5
, Lemma 4]
).
The proof is exactly the same, based on Schur’s Theorem, and taking into account also part (ii) of
2.1
in this case, so we omit it.
In the remainder of the paper, for a tuple
S
of parameters, we use the expression
S
-bounded
to mean “bounded by a function of
S
”.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
be an arbitrary word and let
\mathbf{N}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r})
be an
r
-tuple of normal subgroups of a group
G
.
Suppose that
N_{i}=\langle S_{i}\rangle
, where
S_{i}
is a normal subset of
G
for every
i=1,\ldots,r
, and set
\mathbf{S}=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{r})
.
If
w\{\mathbf{S}\}
is finite of order
m
then
w(\mathbf{N})^{\prime}
is finite of
m
-bounded order.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathbf{N}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r})
be a tuple of normal subgroups of a group
G
.
Assume that
N_{i}=\langle S_{i}\rangle
for every
i=1,\ldots,r
, where:

(i)

S_{i}
is a normal subset of
G
.

(ii)

There exists
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
such that all
n_{i}
th powers of elements of
N_{i}
are contained in
S_{i}
.

If for the tuple
\mathbf{S}=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{r})
the set of values
\gamma_{r}\{\mathbf{S}\}
is finite of order
m
, then the subgroup

\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N})
is also finite, of
(m,r,n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
-bounded order.

Proof.

We follow the notation
\mathbf{N}_{i}
and
P_{i}^{r}
, introduced in the statement of

2.9
.
We are going to prove that
P_{i}^{r}
is finite of bounded order for
i=1,\ldots,r+1
by reverse induction on
i
.
Since
P_{1}^{r}=\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N})
, this proves the result.

The basis of the induction follows from
2.10
, since
P_{r+1}^{r}=[\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N}),\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N})]
.
Let us assume that
P_{i+1}^{r}
is finite of bounded order and prove that the same holds for
P_{i}^{r}
.
Recall that the quotient
P_{i}^{r}/P_{i+1}^{r}
is the image of
\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N}_{i})
, and
then, by a suitable application of
2.1
, it can be generated by the images of the set
\mathbf{T}
of commutators

[s_{1},\ldots,s_{i-1},x_{i},s_{i+1},\ldots,s_{r}],

with
s_{j}\in S_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq r
,
j\neq i
, and
x_{i}\in\gamma_{i}\{\mathbf{S}_{i}\}
, where
\mathbf{S}_{i}=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{i})
.
By
2.5
, we have
\gamma_{i}\{\mathbf{S}_{i}\}\subseteq S_{i}^{\ast 2^{i-1}}
, and then
2.6
implies that

[s_{1},\ldots,s_{i-1},x_{i},s_{i+1},\ldots,s_{r}]\in\gamma_{r}\{\mathbf{S}\}^{\ast 2^{i-1}}\subseteq\gamma_{r}\{\mathbf{S}\}^{\ast 2^{r-1}}.

From the assumption that
|\gamma_{r}\{\mathbf{S}\}|=m
, we get

|\mathbf{T}|\leq(2m+1)^{2^{r-1}},

and consequently
P_{i}^{r}/P_{i+1}^{r}
can be generated by an
(m,r)
-bounded number of elements.
Since
P_{i}^{r}/P_{i+1}^{r}
is abelian, the proof will be complete once we show that all elements in
\mathbf{T}
have bounded finite order modulo
P_{i+1}^{r}
.

By
2.9
, the word
\gamma_{r}
is linear in position
i
of the tuple
\mathbf{N}_{i}
modulo
P_{i+1}^{r}
.
In particular,

(2.3)

[s_{1},\ldots,s_{i-1},x_{i},s_{i+1},\ldots,s_{r}]^{\lambda n_{i}}\equiv[s_{1},\ldots,s_{i-1},x_{i}^{\lambda n_{i}},s_{i+1},\ldots,s_{r}]\pmod{P_{i+1}^{r}},

for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Since
x_{i}\in\gamma_{i}(N_{1},\ldots,N_{i})\leq N_{i}
, it follows from (ii) in the statement of the theorem that
x_{i}^{\lambda n_{i}}\in S_{i}
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Thus we get

[s_{1},\ldots,s_{i-1},x_{i}^{\lambda n_{i}},s_{i+1},\ldots,s_{r}]\in\gamma_{r}\{\mathbf{S}\}.

Since
\gamma_{r}\{\mathbf{S}\}
is finite of order
m
, it follows that there exist
\lambda,\mu\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
,
\lambda\neq\mu
, such that

[s_{1},\ldots,s_{i-1},x_{i},s_{i+1},\ldots,s_{r}]^{\lambda n_{i}}\equiv[s_{1},\ldots,s_{i-1},x_{i},s_{i+1},\ldots,s_{r}]^{\mu n_{i}}\pmod{P_{i+1}^{r}}.

This implies that
[s_{1},\ldots,s_{i-1},x_{i},s_{i+1},\ldots,s_{r}]
has
(m,n_{i})
-bounded finite order modulo
P_{i+1}^{r}
, as desired.
∎

If we take
S_{i}=N_{i}
, we get Theorem B for the lower central words.

Corollary 2.12
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathbf{N}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r})
be a tuple of normal subgroups of a group
G
.
If
\gamma_{r}\{\mathbf{N}\}
is finite of order
m
, then the subgroup
\gamma_{r}(\mathbf{N})
is also finite, of
(m,r)
-bounded order.

Now we deduce Theorem A for lower central words.

Corollary 2.13
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
and let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
be disjoint non-commutator words.
Then the word
\gamma_{r}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})
is boundedly concise.
In particular,
\gamma_{r}(x_{1}^{n_{1}},\ldots,x_{r}^{n_{r}})
is boundedly concise for all
n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\smallsetminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

Let us consider the word
w=\gamma_{r}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})
, and
let
G
be a group in which
w
takes finitely many values, say
|w\{G\}|=m
.
By
2.3
, we have
w(G)=\gamma_{r}(u_{1}(G),\ldots,u_{r}(G))
.
Note that
u_{i}(G)=\langle S_{i}\rangle
, where
S_{i}=u_{i}\{G\}
, and that

w\{G\}=\gamma_{r}\{\mathbf{S}\}
, where
\mathbf{S}=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{r})
.
Now observe that
S_{i}
is a normal subset of
G
and that, since
u_{i}
is a non-commutator word, for some
n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\smallsetminus\{0\}
we have
\{g^{n_{i}}\mid g\in G\}\subseteq u_{i}\{G\}
.
Hence
w(G)
is finite of
(m,r,n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
-bounded order by

2.11
.
∎

We start by introducing a special type of words that we can derive from a given outer commutator word
w

of
w

Before giving the definition, we show the idea behind extended words with an example.
Consider the word
\delta_{2}=[[x_{1},x_{2}],[x_{3},x_{4}]]
.
This is formed by taking the commutator of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, taking the commutator of
x_{3}
and
x_{4}
, and then taking the commutator of these two commutators.
Now suppose that on some occasions, before performing one of these commutators, we introduce a change by taking first the commutator of one (or both) of the components with an outer commutator word not involving the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{4}
appearing in
\delta_{2}
.
For example, before producing
[x_{1},x_{2}]
, we take the commutator
[[y_{1},y_{2}],x_{1}]
and now we follow as in
\delta_{2}
taking the commutator with
x_{2}
,
obtaining
\big[[[y_{1},y_{2}],x_{1}],x_{2}\big]
.
We could continue with the process of taking commutators without making any other changes, so getting

\big[\big[[[y_{1},y_{2}],x_{1}],x_{2}\big],[x_{3},x_{4}]\big],

but we could also make some similar changes in the process, as in the words

\Big[\big[[[y_{1},y_{2}],x_{1}],x_{2}\big],\big[x_{3},[y_{3},x_{4}]\big]\Big]

and

\Big[\,\big[[[y_{1},y_{2}],x_{1}],x_{2}\big],\big[\,\big[x_{3},[y_{3},x_{4}]\big],y_{4}\big]\,\Big].

Another possibility is to make a commutator at the very end, after having completed
\delta_{2}
, as in

\big[y_{1},[[x_{1},x_{2}],[x_{3},x_{4}]]\big].

Observe that all these extended words are again outer commutator words, because we never repeat a variable when we make changes in the construction of
\delta_{2}
.

Let us now give the formal definition of extended words. Notice that this definition differs from the one of extensions of outer commutators words given in Definition 3.1 of
[
8
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
be an outer commutator, and let
Y=\{y_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a set of variables that are disjoint from
X
.
For every
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, we define recursively the set
\ext_{k}(w)
of
k
th extended words of
w
as follows:

(i)

\ext_{0}(w)=\{w\}
.

(ii)

For
k\geq 1
,
\ext_{k}(w)
consists of the set

\{[p,q],[q,p]\mid\text{$p$ outer commutator in $Y$, $q\in\ext_{k-1}(w)$, $p$ and $q$ disjoint}\}\\
=\{[p,q]\mid\text{$p$ outer commutator in $Y$, $q\in\ext_{k-1}(w)$, $p$ and $q$ disjoint}\}^{\pm 1},

and, if
w=[\alpha,\beta]
, also of the set

{\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{\ell+m=k}}\,\{[p,q]\mid\text{$p\in\ext_{\ell}(\alpha)$, $q\in\ext_{m}(\beta)$, $p$ and $q$ disjoint}\}.

If
v\in\ext_{k}(w)
then we say that
w
is an extended word of degree
k
of
w
by outer commutators.

For brevity, in the remainder we will simply speak of extended words when we mean extended words by outer commutators.
Observe that an extended word
v
of an outer commutator
w=w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
is again an outer commutator, in the variables
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\}\cup Y
.
Whenever it is convenient we will assume, after renaming the variables, that
v=v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r},y_{r+1},\ldots,y_{s})
.

Next we generalize
2.6
to extended words of an outer commutator word.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
v=v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r},y_{r+1},\ldots,y_{s})
be an extended word of degree
k
of an outer commutator word

.
Assume that
\mathbf{S}=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{r})
is a tuple of normal subsets of a group

If
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s})
is a tuple of elements of
G
such that

, then

v(\mathbf{t})\in w\{\mathbf{S}\}^{\ast m_{1}\ldots m_{r}2^{k}}.

Proof.

We use induction on
k+r
.
If
k=0
then
v=w
and the result is
2.6
.
This gives in particular the basis of the induction.
Suppose now that the result holds for smaller values of

, and that

According to

, we may assume that
v(\mathbf{t})=[p(\mathbf{t^{\prime}}),q(\mathbf{t^{\prime\prime}})]
, where
p
and

are disjoint and

(i)

either

is an outer commutator word in
Y
and
q\in\ext_{k-1}(w)
,

(ii)

or

,

, with

and
\ell+m=k
.

In case (i), all elements

appear in the vector

, and by the induction hypothesis we have

q(\mathbf{t^{\prime\prime}})\in w\{\mathbf{S}\}^{\ast m_{1}\ldots m_{r}2^{k-1}}

Then the result follows by applying

and the normal subset

Suppose now that we are in case (ii), and assume without loss of generality that

Theorem 3.5
.

Let

and let

Then there exists a series

(i)

is the image of an extension

of
\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})
of degree at most

.

(ii)

In the section
V_{i}^{k}/V_{i-1}^{k}
, the word
v_{i}^{k}
is linear in one component of the tuple
\mathbf{M}_{i}^{k}
.

Furthermore, the words

,
and not on the group

\mathbf{N}
.

We prove the theorem by induction on

.
If

V_{0}^{1}=\big[[N_{1},N_{2}],[N_{1},N_{2}]\big]\leq V_{1}^{1}=\big[N_{1},[N_{1},N_{2}]\big]\leq V_{2}^{1}=[N_{1},N_{2}],

so that

, and
M_{1}^{1}=(N_{1},[N_{1},N_{2}])
and
M_{2}^{1}=(N_{1},N_{2})
.

Assume now that
k\geq 2
and that the result is true for

.
Set
\mathbf{N}_{1}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{2^{k-1}})
and

\mathbf{N}_{2}=(N_{2^{k-1}+1},\ldots,N_{2^{k}})
.
By the induction hypothesis, if we put
s=2^{k-1}+2^{k-2}-1
, then there exist series

(3.4)

V_{0,1}^{k-1}=[\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1}),\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1})]\leq\cdots\leq V_{i,1}^{k-1}\leq\cdots\leq V_{s,1}^{k-1}=\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1})

and

(3.5)

V_{0,2}^{k-1}=[\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{2}),\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{2})]\leq\cdots\leq V_{i,2}^{k-1}\leq\cdots\leq V_{s,2}^{k-1}=\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{2})

such that, for every

, the factors
V_{i,1}^{k-1}/V_{i-1,1}^{k-1}
and
V_{i,2}^{k-1}/V_{i-1,2}^{k-1}
are the images of

v_{i}^{k-1}(\mathbf{M}_{i,1}^{k-1})
and
v_{i}^{k-1}(\mathbf{M}_{i,2}^{k-1})
, respectively, where:

(a)

v_{i}^{k-1}
is an extended word of
\delta_{k-1}
of degree at most

.

(b)

\mathbf{M}_{i,1}^{k-1}
is an outer commutator extension of
\mathbf{N}_{1}
.

(c)

\mathbf{M}_{i,2}^{k-1}
is an outer commutator extension of
\mathbf{N}_{2}
.

(d)

In the sections
V_{i,1}^{k-1}/V_{i-1,1}^{k-1}
and
V_{i,2}^{k-1}/V_{i-1,2}^{k-1}
, the word

is linear in one component
of the tuples

and

, respectively.

3.5
).
We start by taking the commutator of all terms of the series (
3.4
) with
\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{2})
.
This way we obtain the series

(3.6)

[V_{0,1}^{k-1},\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{2})]\leq\cdots\leq[V_{i,1}^{k-1},\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{2})]\leq\cdots\leq[\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1}),\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{2})]=\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N}).

By P. Hall’s Three Subgroup Lemma, we have

\begin{split}[V_{0,1}^{k-1},\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{2})]&=[\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1}),\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1}),\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{2})]\\
&\leq[\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1}),\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{2}),\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1})]=[\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1}),\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})].\end{split}

Now we multiply all terms of the series (
3.6
) by

[\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1}),\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})]
, and this is the rightmost part of the series we are seeking (where
t=2^{k}+2^{k-1}-1
, as above):

(3.7)

V_{t-s}^{k}=[\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1}),\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})]\leq\cdots\leq V_{t-s+i}^{k}=[V_{i,1}^{k-1},\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{2})]\,[\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1}),\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})]\\
\leq\cdots\leq V_{t}^{k}=\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N}).

Note that
t-s=2^{k-1}+2^{k-2}
.
The factors in this series are the images of the subgroups

[v_{i}^{k-1}(\mathbf{M}_{i,1}^{k-1}),\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{2})],

which can be represented in the form

v_{i}^{k}(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{k})
by taking

v_{i}^{k}=[v_{i}^{k-1},\delta_{k-1}(x_{2^{k-1}+1},\ldots,x_{2^{k}})]

and defining
\mathbf{M}_{i}^{k}
to be the concatenation of
\mathbf{M}_{i,1}^{k-1}
and
\mathbf{N}_{2}
, where the elements of
\mathbf{N}_{2}
occupy the positions
2^{k-1}+1,\ldots,2^{k}

(which are the positions corresponding to the variables
x_{2^{k-1}+1},\ldots,x_{2^{k}}
).
Note that
\mathbf{M}_{i}^{k}
is an outer commutator extension of
\mathbf{N}
.

In a symmetric way, by first taking the commutator of
\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1})
with all terms of the series (
3.5
) and then multiplying by

[\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N}),\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{2})]
, we get the series

(3.8)

W_{t-s}^{k}=[\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N}),\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{2})]\leq\cdots\leq W_{t-s+i}^{k}=[\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1}),V_{i,2}^{k-1}]\,[\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N}),\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{2})]\\
\leq\cdots\leq W_{t}^{k}=\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N}).

In this series, the factors are given by the images of the subgroups
w_{i}^{k}(\mathbf{L}_{i}^{k})
, where

w_{i}^{k}=[\delta_{k-1}(y_{2^{k-1}+1},\ldots,y_{2^{k}}),v_{i,2}^{k-1}],

v_{i,2}^{k-1}
being the same word as
v_{i}^{k-1}
, with
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{k-1}}
replaced with
x_{2^{k-1}+1},\ldots,x_{2^{k}}
,
and
\mathbf{L}_{i}^{k}
being the concatenation of
\mathbf{M}_{i,2}^{k-1}
and
\mathbf{N}_{1}
, where we put the components of the second tuple after the components of the first.
Note that
w_{i}^{k}
is an extended word of
\delta_{k-1}(x_{2^{k-1}+1},\ldots,x_{2^{k}})
of degree at most
k-1
.

Now we take the commutator of
\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1})
with the terms of the last series, and subtract
s
to all indices, getting

(3.9)

Z_{1}^{k}=\big[\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1}),[\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N}),\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{2})]\big]\leq\cdots\leq Z_{i+1}^{k}=[\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1}),W_{t-s+i}^{k}]\\
\leq\cdots\leq Z_{t-s}^{k}=[\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1}),\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})],

since
t-2s=1
.
Finally, we define
V_{0}^{k}=[\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N}),\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})]
and multiply all terms of (
3.9
) with this subgroup, setting

V_{i}^{k}=Z_{i}^{k}V_{0}^{k}
for
i=1,\ldots,t-s
.
Since
V_{0}^{k}\leq[\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1}),\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})]
, we get the series

(3.10)

V_{0}^{k}=[\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N}),\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})]\leq\cdots\leq V_{i+1}^{k}=Z_{i+1}^{k}[\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N}),\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})]\\
\leq\cdots\leq V_{t-s}^{k}=[\delta_{k-1}(\mathbf{N}_{1}),\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})].

In this series, the factors
V_{i}^{k}/V_{i-1}^{k}
for
i=2,\ldots,t-s
are given by the images of the subgroups
v_{i}^{k}(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{k})
, where

v_{i}^{k}=[\delta_{k-1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{k-1}}),w_{i+s}^{k}]

is an extended word of
\delta_{k}
of degree at most
k-1
, and
\mathbf{M}_{i}^{k}
is the concatenation of
\mathbf{N}_{1}
and
\mathbf{L}_{i+s}^{k}
, with
the components of the second tuple after the components of the first.
On the other hand, the quotient
V_{1}^{k}/V_{0}^{k}
is given by the image of
v_{1}^{k}(\mathbf{M}_{1}^{k})
, where

v_{1}^{k}=[\delta_{k-1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{k-1}}),[y,\delta_{k-1}(x_{2^{k-1}+1},\ldots,x_{2^{k}})]]

and
\mathbf{M}_{1}^{k}=(\mathbf{N},\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N}))
.

Now the concatenation of (
3.7
) and (
3.10
) is the desired series for
k
and
\mathbf{N}
.
The discussion of the previous paragraphs shows that
v_{i}^{k}(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{k})
is an extension of
\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})
for every
i=1,\ldots,t
.
Thus we only need to check linearity of every word
v_{i}^{k}
in one component of the vector
\mathbf{M}_{i}^{k}
.
For
i=t-s+1,\ldots,t
, if
v_{i}^{k-1}
is linear in component
j
of
\mathbf{M}_{i,1}^{k-1}
of the initial series (
3.4
), then combining this fact with
2.8
, it follows that
v_{i}^{k}
is linear in the same component of
\mathbf{M}_{i}^{k}
.
For
i=1,\ldots,t-s
, we can use similarly the linearity of the series (
3.5
).
Finally for
i=1
, since
V_{0}^{k}=[\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N}),\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})]
, we have linearity in the component corresponding to
y
, which takes values in
\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})
.
∎

We can now prove the corresponding version of
2.11
for the words
\delta_{k}
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathbf{N}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{2^{k}})
be a tuple of normal subgroups of a group
G
.
Assume that
N_{i}=\langle S_{i}\rangle
for every
i=1,\ldots,2^{k}
, where:

(i)

S_{i}
is a normal subset of
G
.

(ii)

There exists
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
such that all
n_{i}
th powers of elements of
N_{i}
are contained in
S_{i}
.

If for the tuple
\mathbf{S}=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{2^{k}})
the set of values
\delta_{k}\{\mathbf{S}\}
is finite of order
m
, then the subgroup
\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})
is also finite and
of
(m,k,n_{1},\ldots,n_{2^{k}})
-bounded order.

Proof.

Let us consider the series

[\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N}),\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})]=V_{0}^{k}\leq V_{1}^{k}\leq\cdots\leq V_{t}^{k}=\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})

of
3.5
, where
t=2^{k}+2^{k-1}-1
.
We prove that every
V_{i}^{k}
is finite of bounded order by induction on
i
.
The result for
i=0
follows from
2.10
.
Assume now that
i\geq 1
and that the result is true for
V_{i-1}^{k}
.
By
3.5
, the section
V_{i}^{k}/V_{i-1}^{k}
coincides with the image of a subgroup
v_{i}^{k}(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{k})
that is an extension of
\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})
of degree at most
k-1
.

For simplicity, let us write
v
for
v_{i}^{k}
and
\mathbf{M}
for
\mathbf{M}_{i}^{k}
.
Let
\mathbf{M}=(M_{1},\ldots,M_{s})
, which is an outer commutator extension of
\mathbf{N}
.
Hence
s\geq 2^{k}
and for every
j=1,\ldots,s
we have
M_{j}=w_{j}(\mathbf{N}_{j})
, where
w_{j}
is an outer commutator word, all components in
\mathbf{N}_{j}
belong to
\mathbf{N}
, and one of these components must be
N_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,2^{k}
.

Let
T_{j}=w_{j}\{\mathbf{S}_{j}\}
, where
\mathbf{S}_{j}
is obtained from
\mathbf{N}_{j}
by replacing each subgroup
N_{\ell}
with its given generating set
S_{\ell}
.
Hence
T_{j}\subseteq M_{j}
.
Recall from
3.5
that the word
v
(and hence also the number
s
of variables of
v
) and the words
w_{1},\ldots,w_{s}
only depend on
k
,
and not on
G
or on
\mathbf{N}
.
From this fact, and since
\mathbf{S}_{j}
consists of normal subsets of
G
, it follows from
2.5
that there is a function

h:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that
T_{j}\subseteq S_{j}^{\ast h(k)}
for every
j=1,\ldots,2^{k}
.
If we set
\mathbf{T}=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{s})
then, by
3.2
, we get
v\{\mathbf{T}\}\subseteq w\{\mathbf{S}\}^{\ast n}
, where

n=h(k)^{2^{k}}2^{k-1}.

Consequently

(3.11)

|v\{\mathbf{T}\}|\leq(2m+1)^{n},

and
v\{\mathbf{T}\}
is finite of
(m,k)
-bounded cardinality.
On the other hand, it follows from
2.1
that
v(\mathbf{M})
can be generated by the set of values
v\{\mathbf{T}\}
.

From
3.5
, we know that the word
v
is linear in some position
j\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
of the tuple
\mathbf{M}
modulo
V_{i-1}^{k}
.
Since
M_{j}=w_{j}(\mathbf{N}_{j})
is as above, we have
M_{j}\leq N_{\ell}
for some
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,2^{k}\}
, and actually
\ell=j

if
j\in\{1,\ldots,2^{k}\}
.
Now, from linearity, for every tuple
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s})\in\mathbf{T}
and every
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

(3.12)

v(\mathbf{t})^{\lambda n_{\ell}}=v(t_{1},\ldots,t_{j},\ldots,t_{s})^{\lambda n_{\ell}}\equiv v(t_{1},\ldots,t_{j}^{\lambda n_{\ell}},\ldots,t_{s})\pmod{V_{i-1}^{k}}.

We have
t_{j}^{\lambda}\in M_{j}\leq N_{\ell}
and then, by (ii) in the statement of the theorem,
t_{j}^{\lambda n_{\ell}}\in S_{\ell}
.
So we get

v(t_{1},\ldots,t_{j}^{\lambda n_{\ell}},\ldots,t_{s})\in v\{\mathbf{T}_{j}\},

where
\mathbf{T}_{j}
is the tuple obtained from
\mathbf{T}
after replacing
T_{j}
with
S_{\ell}
.
Similarly to (
3.11
) and taking into account that
\ell=j
if
j\in\{1,\ldots,2^{k}\}
, it follows that the set
v\{\mathbf{T}_{j}\}
is finite of
(m,k)
-bounded cardinality.
Hence there exist
(m,k)
-bounded integers
\lambda
and
\mu
, with
\lambda\neq\mu
, such that

v(\mathbf{t})^{\lambda n_{\ell}}\equiv v(\mathbf{t})^{\mu n_{\ell}}\pmod{V_{i-1}^{k}}.

This implies that
v(\mathbf{t})
has finite order modulo
V_{i-1}^{k}
, bounded in terms of
m
,
k
and
n_{\ell}
.

Summarizing, the abelian quotient
V_{i}^{k}/V_{i}^{k-1}
is the image of the verbal subgroup
v(\mathbf{M})
, which is generated by the set
v\{\mathbf{T}\}

of
(m,k)
-bounded cardinality, and each element of
v\{\mathbf{T}\}
has
(m,k,n_{\ell})
-bounded order.
We conclude that the order of
V_{i}^{k}/V_{i-1}^{k}
is
(m,k,n_{\ell})
-bounded, which completes the proof.
∎

Exactly as in the case of lower central words, we obtain Theorems A and B as special cases of this last result.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{2^{k}}
be non-commutator words.
Then the word
\delta_{k}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{2^{k}})
is boundedly concise.
In particular,
\delta_{k}(x_{1}^{n_{1}},\ldots,x_{2^{k}}^{n_{2^{k}}})
is boundedly concise for all
n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\smallsetminus\{0\}
.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathbf{N}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{2^{k}})
be a tuple of normal subgroups of a group
G
.
If
\delta_{k}\{\mathbf{N}\}
is finite of order
m
, then the subgroup
\delta_{k}(\mathbf{N})
is also finite, of
(m,k)
-bounded order.
