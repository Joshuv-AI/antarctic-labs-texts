1.
Introduction

Let us begin with a few facts concerning the theory of
structurally damped equations
.
After Russell’s fundamental paper
[
42
]
on mathematical models of structural mechanics describing elastic beams much more attention has been paid rightly to the strongly damped plate/wave problems and to its further applications on the crossroads of control theory (cf.
[
3
]
), dynamical systems (cf.
[
47
]
) and transmition problems (cf.

[
38
]
).
The current trends on
struturally damped equations
, exhibited on the contribution of Pham et al
[
40
]
, and more recently on D’Abbicco-Ebert’s series of papers
[
12
,
14
,
15
]
, paved the way for considering space-fractional model problems such as the inhomogeneous
\sigma-
evolution equation

(1.1)

\partial_{t}^{2}(t,x)+\mu(-\Delta)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}\partial_{t}u(t,x)+(-\Delta)^{\sigma}u(t,x)=f(t,x).

We refer the reader to the works of Carvalho et al (cf.
[
6
]
) and Denk-Schnaubelt (cf.
[
18
]
) just to mention a few papers on analysis of PDEs towards struturally damped plate equations (case of
\sigma=2
) and damped wave equations (case of
\sigma=1
). Also, we refer the seminal work of Karch (cf.
[
26
]
) for a general account on the asymptotic behaviour of structurally damped models.

We notice that the
\sigma-
evolution equation (
1.1
) describes the scenario where the higher frequencies exhibit a strongly damped behavior in comparison with the lower frequencies. Here, the fractional differential operator
(-\Delta)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}
(that will be defined later on Subsection
4.2
) is used to model the damping term
(\gamma=\sigma)
and the elastic term as well
(\gamma=2\sigma)
. Noteworthy, in the view of Carvalho et al (cf.
[
6
]
) and Chill-Srivastava (cf.
[
7
]
), the model problem (
1.1
) brings also the possibility to study the well-posedness of problems carrying
L^{p}
data (
1<p<\infty
), in stark constrast with the
weaker damping case
so that
\mu(-\Delta)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}\partial_{t}u(t,x)

(\mu>0)
resembles to a regularization of the weak damping term
\mu\partial_{t}u(t,x)
, in the limit
\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}
.

Our aim here is to investigate a space-time-fractional counterpart of (
1.1
) that encodes memory effects, ubiquitous in natural phenomena (cf.
[
20
,
45
]
). In our setting it will be replaced the time derivatives
\partial_{t}
resp.
\partial_{t}^{2}
by the so-called Caputo-Djrbashian derivatives
\partial^{\gamma}_{t}
of order
\gamma
, defined as

\displaystyle\partial^{\gamma}_{t}u(t,x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{(t-\tau)^{m-\gamma-1}}{\Gamma(m-\gamma)}\frac{\partial^{m}u(\tau,x)}{\partial\tau^{m}}d\tau&,m-1<\gamma<m\\
\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{m}u(t,x)}{\partial t^{m}}&,\gamma=m,\end{array}\right.

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
denotes the Euler’s Gamma function (see eq. (
4.7
) of Subsection
4.1
) and
m=\lfloor\gamma\rfloor+1
(
\lfloor\gamma\rfloor
stands for the integer part of
\gamma
).

For the time-fractional counterpart of the inhomogeneous term
f(t,x)
, it will be adopted the Riemann-Liouville integral

\displaystyle I^{\gamma}_{0^{+}}f(t,x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{(t-\tau)^{\gamma-1}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}f(\tau,x)d\tau&,0<\gamma<1\\
\\
\displaystyle f(t,x)&,\gamma=0.\end{array}\right.

We observe that for values of
\gamma\not\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
resp.
\gamma\neq 0
, the eqs. (
1
) and (
1
) always define operators with power-law memory. The underlying memory function, given by

(1.10)

\displaystyle\displaystyle g_{\nu}(t):=\dfrac{t^{\nu-1}}{\Gamma(\nu)}~~(0<\nu<1),

is a probability density function over the interval
(0,\infty)
, converging to the delta function
\delta(t)
in the limit
\nu\rightarrow 0^{+}
.

Some of the physical reasons for the choice of the Caputo-Djrbashian derivative (
1
) instead of the Riemann-Liouville derivative
{~}{}^{RL}\partial_{t}^{\gamma}:=\partial_{t}^{m}I^{m-\gamma}_{0^{+}}
stems on its ubiquity in the generalized Langevin equations (cf.
[
32
]
) and on certain limiting processes (cf.
[
25
]
). Despite their usefulness on real-world applications (cf.
[
1
,
2
]
), the Caputo-Djrbashian derivative is suitable for modelling Cauchy problems for the two amongst many reasons:

(1)

they have similar properties to the time-derivatives;

(2)

they remove the singularities at
t=0
.

We refer to the book of Samko et al. (cf.
[
43
]
) for further details on the theory of Caputo-Djrbashian derivatives and Riemann-Liouville operators and Stinga’s preprint
[
45
]
for an abridged overview of it. For its applications on Cauchy problems, we refer to the books of Podlubny (cf.
[
41
]
) and Kilbas et al. (cf.
[
30
]
). We also refer to
[
28
,
29
,
13
,
31
,
16
]
for recent applications on fractional diffusion equations, closely related to our approach.

2.
Discussion

2.1.
Model Problems

The main focus in this paper are the following two Cauchy problems, carrying the initial condition(s)
u_{0}(x)
[and
u_{1}(x)
] and the inhomogeneous term
I^{\beta-2\alpha}_{0^{+}}f(t,x)
, fulfilling certain regularity conditions to be presented a posteriori. Here and elsewhere
I^{\beta-2\alpha}_{0^{+}}
stands for the Riemann-Liouville integral, defined through eq. (
1
) for
\gamma=\beta-2\alpha
.

Cauchy Problem 2.1
.

Given
0<\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}
,
2\alpha\leq\beta<2\alpha+1
,
\sigma>0
and
\mu>0
, the function
u:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a (weak) solution of

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\partial^{2\alpha}_{t}u(t,x)+\mu(-\Delta)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u(t,x)+(-\Delta)^{\sigma}u(t,x)=I^{\beta-2\alpha}_{0^{+}}f(t,x)&,\mbox{in}~(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
\ \\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)~&,\mbox{in}~\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{cases}

Cauchy Problem 2.2
.

Given
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha\leq 1
,
2\alpha\leq\beta<2\alpha+1
,
\sigma>0
and
\mu>0
, the function
u:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a (weak) solution of

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\partial^{2\alpha}_{t}u(t,x)+\mu(-\Delta)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u(t,x)+(-\Delta)^{\sigma}u(t,x)=I^{\beta-2\alpha}_{0^{+}}f(t,x)&,\mbox{in}~(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
\ \\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)&,\mbox{in}~\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
\ \\
\partial_{t}u(0,x)=u_{1}(x)&,\mbox{in}~\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{cases}

The programme of studying Cauchy problems similar to
Cauchy Problem
2.1
&
Cauchy Problem
2.2
has been started by Fujita in the former papers

[
22
,
23
]
, where it has been shown that fractional diffusion and wave equations can be reformulated as integro-differential equations of D’Alembert type. In our case, the proposed model problems amalgamate the following properties, tactically investigated on several works (see e.g.
[
6
,
18
,
40
,
28
,
29
]
):

(1)

The damping part has ‘half of the order’ of the leading elastic term, which is in accordance with Russell’s formulation
[
42
]
;

(2)

For values of
0<\alpha<1
, the time-fractional derivatives
\partial^{2\alpha}_{t}
and
\partial^{\alpha}_{t}
endow a fractional diffusion process;

(3)

In the limit
\mu\rightarrow 0^{+}
, the
Cauchy Problem
2.1
&
Cauchy Problem
2.2
approaches the
undamped case
;

(4)

For
\alpha=1
and
1<\sigma<2
, our model problem possess mixed properties of
the plate equation (case of
\sigma=2
) resp. the wave equation (case of
\sigma=1
) with structural damping;

(5)

For
\alpha=1
, the asymptotic profile of the homogeneous part of the
Cauchy Problem
2.2
changes accordingly to the size of the damping parameter
\mu>0
.

From the point of view of potential applications, our model problem brings also the possibility to model anomalous diffusions and memory effects in a twofold way. Namely, the power law proportional to
t^{2\alpha}
measures if our diffusive process is subdiffusive
(0<2\alpha<1)
or superdiffusive (
1<2\alpha<2
). On both cases, the fractional derivatives
\partial_{t}^{\alpha}
and
\partial_{t}^{2\alpha}
are described in terms of the memory functions
g_{1-\alpha}(t)
and
g_{2-2\alpha}(t)
, respectively (see eq. (
1.10
)).

On the other hand, the choice of the parameter
\beta
, appearing on the Riemann-Liouville integral
I^{\beta-2\alpha}_{0^{+}}f(t,x)
, reflects if our
inhomogeneous term
exhibits a
memoryless pattern
or not: The limit case
\beta\rightarrow(2\alpha)^{+}
guarantees that the shape of the memory function
g_{\beta-2\alpha}(t)
is close to the shape of the delta function
\delta(t)
so that the
inhomogeneous term
approaches ’lack of memory’ for values of
\beta
close to
2\alpha
. For values of
\beta
a bit far from
2\alpha
, the ubiquity of memory effects is manifested by the polynomial shape of the graph of
g_{\beta-2\alpha}(t)
. Interesting to see, the particular choice
\beta=1+\alpha
(
\beta-2\alpha=1-\alpha
), with
0<\alpha<1
, lead us always to diffusive processes with memory.

Such subtle details encoded on our model may be seen as surprising and substantial from the vast majority of papers available on the literature. Up to our knowledge, the overlap between the memory effects of the fractional derivatives and the
inhomogeneous term
are not so often considered.

2.2.
Solution Representation

From an operational calculus perpective, the
Cauchy Problem
2.1
&
Cauchy Problem
2.2
share the same features of Cauchy problems underlying to the time-fractional telegraph equations so that one can exploit, as in
[
31
]
, Orsingher-Beghin’s approach
[
39
]
to obtain the underlying solution representations (see also ref.
[
33
]
). Namely, the
closed-form representation for the solutions of both Cauchy problems – to be obtained in Subsection
6.2
and Subsection
6.3
, respectively – follows from the fact that Fourier-Laplace multiplier of
\displaystyle\partial^{2\alpha}_{t}+\mu(-\Delta)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}\partial^{\alpha}_{t}+(-\Delta)^{\sigma}
,

(s^{\alpha},|\xi|^{\sigma})\mapsto s^{2\alpha}+\mu|\xi|^{\sigma}s^{\alpha}+|\xi|^{2\sigma},

is a quadratic form on the variables
u=s^{\alpha}
and
v=|\xi|^{\sigma}
.

Then, with the aid operational properties of the generalized Mittag-Leffler functions
E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\gamma}
and
E_{\alpha,\beta}=E_{\alpha,\beta}^{1}
, highlighted on Section
5
– see, for instance, eq. (
4.8
) and Theorem
4.3
– we are able to recast closed representation formulae in terms of the wide class of pseudo-differential operators
\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\frac{\eta}{2}}E_{\alpha,\beta}\left(~-\lambda(-\Delta)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}t^{\alpha}~\right)
. From a semigroup perspective,
such class provides us fractional analogues of the resolvent operator considered e.g. on
[
6
,
7
,
18
]
for the case where the damping operator is a multiple square root of the elastic operator (cf.
[
21
, Chapter VI]
).

By the properties of the generalized Mittag-Leffler functions, it will be also shown in Subsection
6.2
and Subsection
6.3
that the choice of
I^{\beta-2\alpha}_{0^{+}}f(t,x)
as
inhomogeneous term
turns out to be linked with the Fourier multipliers
\displaystyle t^{\beta-1}E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\gamma}(-\lambda|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})
(
\gamma=1,2
). From feasible physical descriptions of relaxation processes, such class of functions is somewhat expected, as highlighted on the seminal paper
[
19
]
; for an overview, we refer to
[
24
, Subsection 8.1.]
.

As will be noticed by the reader, the results enclosed on this paper can certainly be rewritten in terms of the language of fractional resolvent families (see e.g.
[
33
]
and the references therein) in a way that the Banach space framework, considered e.g. in the papers
[
7
,
6
,
18
]
and on the book
[
21
]
can be faithfully generalized. But we have decided to use mostly pseudo-differential calculus, rather than operator theory, to keep the approach as self-contained as possible.

2.3.
Dispersive and Strichartz estimates

Apart from the solution representation of these Cauchy problems being interesting from the point of view of spectral analysis and stochastic processes, we want to push forward the investigation of dispersive and Strichartz estimates, with the aim of measuring the size and the decay of the solutions of
Cauchy Problem
2.1
&
Cauchy Problem
2.2
. The study of such type of estimates have become a cornerstone tool for many years now, mainly due to the Keel-Tao’s breakthrough contribution to the topic (cf.
[
27
]
); see also
[
46
, Chapter 2.]
for an overview.

The starting point will be the decay properties of the convolution kernel of
\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\frac{\eta}{2}}E_{\alpha,\beta}\left(~-\lambda(-\Delta)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}t^{\alpha}~\right)
, represented in terms of the Hankel transform of the Fourier multiplier
\displaystyle|\xi|^{\eta}E_{\alpha,\beta}\left(-\lambda|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha}\right)
. To do so, we will exploit the [optimal] decay estimates for the Mittag-Leffler functions
E_{\alpha,\beta}(-z)
, available on the book
[
41
]
(case of
z\in\mathbb{C}
) and on the papers
[
44
,
4
]
(case of
z\geq 0
) to our setting.

Concerning the dispersive estimates, the framework enclosed in Section
5
is comparable to Pham et al and D’Abbicco-Ebert approaches (cf.
[
40
,
14
]
), who have investigated
L^{p}-L^{q}
estimates for Cauchy problems similar to
Cauchy Problem
2.2
.
Regarding the Strichartz estimates enclosed in the end of Section
6
, we have followed up some of the ideas addressed by Tao on the monograph
[
46
]
, properly adapted to our model problems. Mainly, a version of the so-called
Christ-Kiselev lemma
(cf.
[
46
, Lemma 2.4]
) is always required to ensure the boundedness of the inhomogeneous part of the solution representation of both Cauchy problems (see also
[
27
, THEOREM 1.2.]
).

4.
Preliminaries

4.1.
Laplace Transform and Mittag-Leffler functions

For a real-valued function
g:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,\infty)}e^{-\omega t}|g(t)|<\infty
, the Laplace transform of
g
is defined as

(4.1)

\displaystyle G(s):=\mathcal{L}[g(t)](s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}g(t)\,\,dt

\displaystyle(\Re(s)>\omega).

Here, we notice that the function
G
(the Laplace image of
g
) is analytic on the right-half plane
\displaystyle\left\{s\in{\mathbb{C}}~:~\Re(s)>\omega\right\},
whereby
\omega
is chosen as the infimum of the values of
s
for which the right-hand side of (
4.1
) is convergent. Further details may be found e.g. in
[
30
, Subsection 1.2]
.

For the inverse of the Laplace transform, defined as
g(t)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}[G(s)](t)
, the integration is performed along the strip
[c-i\infty,c+i\infty]
(
c:=\Re(s)>\omega
), and whence, the resulting formula is independent of the choice of
c
.

Associated to the Laplace transform is the
Laplace convolution formula
(cf.
[
30
, (1.4.10), (1.4.12) of p. 19]
and
[
41
, (2.237), (2.238) of pp. 103–104]
), defined for two functions
g
and
h
by

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left[\int_{0}^{t}h(t-\tau)g(\tau)d\tau\right](s)=\mathcal{L}[h](s)\cdot\mathcal{L}[g](s).

Of foremost importance are also the following operational identities, involving the Caputo-Djrbashian derivative (
1
) and the Riemann-Liouville integral (
1
) respectively. Namely, the property

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\partial_{t}^{\gamma}g(t)]=s^{\gamma}\mathcal{L}[g](s)-\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}s^{\gamma-k-1}\partial_{t}^{k}g(0)

\displaystyle\left(~m-1<\gamma\leq m~\right)

is fulfilled whenever
\mathcal{L}[g(t)](s)
,
\mathcal{L}[\partial_{t}^{m}g(t)](s)
exist and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left(\partial_{t}\right)^{k}g(t)=0

\displaystyle\left(~k=0,1,\ldots,m-1~\right),

holds for every
g\in C^{m}(0,\infty)
such that
\left(\partial_{t}\right)^{m}g\in L^{1}(0,b)
for any
b>0
(cf.
[
30
, Lemma 2.24]
).
On the other hand, the identity

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[I_{0^{+}}^{\gamma}g(t)]=s^{-\gamma}\mathcal{L}[g](s)

\displaystyle(\Re(s)>\omega)

is always satisfied in case of
g\in L^{1}(0,b)
, for any
b>0
(cf.
[
30
, Lemma 2.14]
).

Next, we turn our attention to some of the special functions to be considered on the sequel.
We introduce the two-parameter/three-parameter Mittag-Leffler functions
E_{\alpha,\beta}
resp.
E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\gamma}
, as power series expansions of the form

(4.5)

\displaystyle E_{\alpha,\beta}(z)=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+\beta)}

\displaystyle(\Re(\alpha)>0),

(4.6)

\displaystyle E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\gamma}(z)=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\gamma)_{k}}{k!}\frac{z^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+\beta)}

\displaystyle(\Re(\alpha)>0),

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
stands for the Euler’s Gamma function

(4.7)

\displaystyle\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}t^{z-1}dt

\displaystyle(\Re(z)>0),

and
\displaystyle(\gamma)_{k}=\frac{\Gamma(\gamma+k)}{\Gamma(\gamma)}
(
\Re(\gamma)>-k
) for the Pochhammer symbol.

The wide class of Mittag-Leffler functions, defined viz (
4.5
) and (
4.6
), permits us to represent several transcendental and special functions (cf.
[
30
, Subsection 1.8 & Subsection 1.9]
and
[
24
, Chapter 3 -Chapter 5]
), such as:

(i)

the exponential function

e^{z}=E_{1,1}(z);

(ii)

the error function

\displaystyle\mbox{erf}(z):=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{z}e^{-t^{2}}dt=1-e^{-z^{2}}E_{\frac{1}{2},1}(-z);

(iii)

the hyperbolic functions

\displaystyle\cosh(z)=E_{2,1}(z^{2})

and

\displaystyle\sinh(z)=zE_{2,2}(z^{2});

(iv)

the complex exponential function

e^{iz}:=\cos(z)+i\sin(z)=E_{2,1}(-z^{2})+izE_{2,2}(-z^{2});

(v)

the Kummer confluent hypergeometric function

\varPhi(\gamma;\beta;z):={~}_{1}F_{1}(\gamma;\beta,z)=\Gamma(\beta)E_{1,\beta}^{\gamma}(z);

(vi)

the generalized hypergeometric function

{~}{}_{1}F_{m}\left(\gamma;\frac{\beta}{m},\frac{\beta+1}{m},\ldots,\frac{\beta+m-1}{m};\frac{z^{m}}{m^{m}}\right)=\Gamma(\beta)E_{m,\beta}^{\gamma}(z)

\displaystyle(m\in{\mathbb{N}}).

Here, we would like to stress that
E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\gamma}
corresponds to a faithful generalization of
E_{\alpha,\beta}
. Indeed, from the Pochhammer identity
(1)_{k}=k!
it immediately follows that
E_{\alpha,\beta}^{1}
(
\gamma=1
) coincides with
E_{\alpha,\beta}
.
For these functions, we have the following Laplace transform identity (cf.
[
24
, eq. (5.1.26) of p. 102]
&
[
30
, eq. (1.9.13) of p. 47]
)

(4.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[t^{\beta-1}E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\gamma}(\lambda t^{\alpha})](s)=\frac{s^{\alpha\gamma-\beta}}{(s^{\alpha}-\lambda)^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle(\Re(s)>0;~\Re(\beta)>0;~|\lambda s^{-\alpha}|<1).

In the remainder part of this subsection we list some of the properties required for the sake of the rest of the paper.
We start to point out the following decay estimates, involving the two-parameter Mittag-Leffler functions (
4.5
), that will be employed on the sequel of results to be proved in Section

.

Theorem 4.1

(cf.
[

]
,

)
.

If
\alpha\leq 2
,
\beta
is an arbitrary real number,
\theta
is such that
\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}<\theta<\min\{\pi,\pi\alpha\}

{\bm{N}}_{\sigma,n}(t,x|\alpha,\mu)
denote the kernel functions introduced in
Notation

Proof.

First, we recall that
u(t,x)

\ \\
\widehat{u}(0,\xi)=\widehat{u_{0}}(\xi)&,\mbox{in}~\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{cases}

with
\widehat{u_{0}}(\xi)=(\mathcal{F}u_{0})(\xi)
and
I^{\beta-2\alpha}_{0^{+}}\widehat{f}(t,\xi)=I^{\beta-2\alpha}_{0^{+}}(\mathcal{F}f)(t,\xi)
.

In order to compute the solution representation of (

) we adopt the Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}
defined through eq. (
4.1
). First, one obtains from eqs. (

) and (

), and from the initial conditions underlying to the Cauchy Problem (

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\partial^{2\alpha}_{t}\widehat{u}(t,\xi)](s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s^{2\alpha}\mathcal{L}[\widehat{u}(t,\xi)](s)-s^{2\alpha-1}\widehat{u_{0}}(\xi),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\partial^{\alpha}_{t}\widehat{u}(t,\xi)](s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s^{\alpha}\mathcal{L}[\widehat{u}(t,\xi)](s)-s^{\alpha-1}\widehat{u_{0}}(\xi),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[I^{\beta-2\alpha}_{0^{+}}\widehat{f}(t,\xi)](s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s^{2\alpha-\beta}\mathcal{L}[\widehat{f}(t,\xi)](s),

since

and
0\leq\beta-2\alpha<1
.

Thus, after few algebraic manipulations we conclude that the solution of (

),
\widehat{u}(t,\xi)
, admits the following Laplace representation

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\widehat{u}(t,\xi)](s)=U_{0}(s,\xi)~\widehat{u_{0}}(\xi)+M(s,\xi)~\mathcal{L}[\widehat{f}(t,\xi)](s),

with

(6.4)

\displaystyle M(s,\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{s^{2\alpha-\beta}}{s^{2\alpha}+\mu|\xi|^{\sigma}s^{\alpha}+|\xi|^{2\sigma}},

(6.5)

\displaystyle U_{0}(s,\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{s^{2\alpha-1}+\mu|\xi|^{\sigma}s^{\alpha-1}}{s^{2\alpha}+\mu|\xi|^{\sigma}s^{\alpha}+|\xi|^{2\sigma}}.

By taking into account the constants
\lambda_{+}
and
\lambda_{-}
defined viz eq. (
6.1
), we observe that
s=-\lambda_{-}|\xi|^{\sigma}
and
s=-\lambda_{+}|\xi|^{\sigma}
correspond to the algebraic roots of the polynomial

. Then

\displaystyle s^{2\alpha}+\mu|\xi|^{\sigma}s^{\alpha}+|\xi|^{2\sigma}=(s^{\alpha}+\lambda_{+}|\xi|^{\sigma})(s^{\alpha}+\lambda_{-}|\xi|^{\sigma})

so that for
\lambda_{+}\neq\lambda_{-}
(i.e. for
\mu\neq 2
) the set of identities

(6.6)

\displaystyle~\frac{s^{\alpha}}{s^{2\alpha}+\mu|\xi|^{\sigma}s^{\alpha}+|\xi|^{2\sigma}}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{+}}{s^{\alpha}+\lambda_{+}|\xi|^{\sigma}}-\frac{\lambda_{-}}{s^{\alpha}+\lambda_{-}|\xi|^{\sigma}}\right),

(6.7)

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{s^{2\alpha}+\mu|\xi|^{\sigma}s^{\alpha}+|\xi|^{2\sigma}}=\frac{-|\xi|^{-\sigma}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}\left(\frac{1}{s^{\alpha}+\lambda_{+}|\xi|^{\sigma}}-\frac{1}{s^{\alpha}+\lambda_{-}|\xi|^{\sigma}}\right)

yield straightforwardly by the method of undetermined coefficients.

Thus, from eq. (
6.6
) we obtain that eq. (
6.4
) is equivalent to

(6.8)

\displaystyle M(s,\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{+}~s^{\alpha-\beta}}{s^{\alpha}+\lambda_{+}|\xi|^{\sigma}}-\frac{\lambda_{-}~s^{\alpha-\beta}}{s^{\alpha}+\lambda_{-}|\xi|^{\sigma}}\right)&,~~\mu\neq 2\\
\ \\
\displaystyle\frac{s^{2\alpha-\beta}}{(s^{\alpha}+|\xi|^{\sigma})^{2}}&,~\mu=2,\end{cases}

whereas the equivalence between eq. (
6.5
) and

\displaystyle U_{0}(s,\xi)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}-\frac{|\xi|^{2\sigma}s^{-1}}{s^{2\alpha}+\mu|\xi|^{\sigma}s^{\alpha}+|\xi|^{2\sigma}}

(6.9)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}-\frac{-|\xi|^{\sigma}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}\left(\frac{s^{-1}}{s^{\alpha}+\lambda_{+}|\xi|^{\sigma}}-\frac{s^{-1}}{s^{\alpha}+\lambda_{-}|\xi|^{\sigma}}\right)&,~\mbox{if}~\mu\neq 2\\
\ \\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}-|\xi|^{2\sigma}\frac{s^{-1}}{(s^{\alpha}+|\xi|^{\sigma})^{2}}&,~\mbox{if}~\mu=2.\end{cases}

follows from eq. (
6.7
).

Next, from the Laplace identity (
4.8
) and from Lemma

we obtain the set of identities

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{-1}[M(s,\xi)](t)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle t^{\beta-1}~\frac{\lambda_{+}E_{\alpha,\beta}(-\lambda_{+}|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})-\lambda_{-}E_{\alpha,\beta}(-\lambda_{-}|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}&,~\mu\neq 2\\
\ \\
\displaystyle t^{\beta-1}E_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(-|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})&,~\mu=2\end{cases}

(6.10)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle t^{\beta-1}~\frac{\lambda_{+}E_{\alpha,\beta}(-\lambda_{+}|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})-\lambda_{-}E_{\alpha,\beta}(-\lambda_{-}|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}&,~\mu\neq 2\\
\ \\
\displaystyle\frac{t^{\beta-1}}{\alpha}\left(~E_{\alpha,\beta-1}(-|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})-(1+\alpha-\beta)E_{\alpha,\beta}(-|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})~\right)&,~\mu=2.\end{cases}

In the same order of ideas, from (
4.8
)
we conclude that the Laplace inverse of (
6.2
),
\mathcal{L}^{-1}[U_{0}(s,\xi)](t)
, equals to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle 1-\frac{-|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}\left(E_{\alpha,\alpha+1}(-\lambda_{+}|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})-E_{\alpha,\alpha+1}(-\lambda_{-}|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})\right)&,~\mu\neq 2\\
\ \\
\displaystyle 1-|\xi|^{2\sigma}t^{2\alpha}E_{\alpha,2\alpha+1}^{2}(-|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})&,~\mu=2.\end{cases}

Note already that the constants
\lambda_{+}
and
\lambda_{-}
defined by eq. (
6.1
) satisfy the set of relations

(6.11)

\displaystyle\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}=1

\displaystyle\wedge

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{-}}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{+}}\right)=1.

Then, by a straightforward application of Lemma
4.3
, one has that
\mathcal{L}^{-1}[U_{0}(s,\xi)](t)
, computed as above, simplifies to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}\left(\frac{E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda_{+}|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})-1}{\lambda_{+}}-\frac{E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda_{-}|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})-1}{\lambda_{-}}\right)&,~\mu\neq 2\\
\ \\
\displaystyle 1+|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha}\left(E_{\alpha,\alpha+1}^{2}(-|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})-E_{\alpha,\alpha+1}(-|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})\right)&,~\mu=2\end{cases}

(6.12)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda_{+}|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})}{1-(\lambda_{+})^{2}}+\frac{E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda_{-}|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})}{1-(\lambda_{-})^{2}}&,~\mu\neq 2\\
\ \\
\displaystyle E_{\alpha,1}(-|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})+\frac{|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha}}{\alpha}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})&,~\mu=2.\end{cases}

Thereafter, by applying the Laplace convolution identity (
4.2
) one has that eq. (
6.3
) is equivalent to

(6.13)

\displaystyle\widehat{u}(t,\xi)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}[U_{0}(s,\xi)](t)~\widehat{u_{0}}(\xi)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}^{-1}[M(s,\xi)](t-\tau)~\widehat{f}(\tau,\xi)d\tau.

The above representation formula provides a solution for the Cauchy Problem (

), whereby the terms
\mathcal{L}^{-1}[M(s,\xi)](t)
and
\mathcal{L}^{-1}[U_{0}(s,\xi)](t)
are given by (

) and (
6.2
), respectively.

Finally, from the

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\left({\bm{N}}_{\sigma,n}(t,\cdot|\alpha,\mu)*u_{0}\right)(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\left[~{\bm{M}}_{\sigma,n}(t-\tau,\cdot|\alpha,\beta,\mu)\ast f(\tau,\cdot)~\right](x)~d\tau,

where

stand for the kernel functions introduced in

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha\leq 1
,
2\alpha\leq\beta<2\alpha+1
,
\sigma>0
,
\mu>0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({\bm{N}}_{\sigma,n}(t,\cdot|\alpha,\mu)*u_{0})(x)+({\bm{J}}_{\sigma,n}(t,\cdot|\alpha,\mu)*u_{1})(x)

\displaystyle+

M(s,\xi)
,

6.4.

Having already gathered the main technical results in Section
5

Cauchy Problem

and
Cauchy Problem
2.2

On the formulation of the required assumptions, we need to consider the following regions of the

(6.19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{0}(\alpha)=\left\{(\sigma,\gamma)~:~0<\gamma<n~\wedge~n-\gamma<\sigma\leq\alpha\gamma~\right\};

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1,2}(\alpha)=\left\{(\sigma,\gamma)~:~\displaystyle 0<\gamma<\frac{n}{2}~\wedge~\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}-\gamma<\sigma\leq\alpha\gamma~\right\};

Assumption 6.5
.

We assume that the
4-
tuple

satisfy, at least, one of the following conditions:

\wedge

Theorem 6.7

\gamma\rightarrow\varepsilon
, gives rise to the following conditions:

(i)

In case of
(\sigma,\gamma)\in\mathcal{S}_{0}\left(\alpha\right)~\wedge~(p,q)=(1,\infty)
, one has

0<\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{q}-\varepsilon<\sigma.

(ii)

In case of
(\sigma,\gamma)\in\mathcal{S}_{1,2}\left(\alpha\right)~\wedge~(p,q)\in\mathcal{R}_{1,2}\left(\sigma+\varepsilon\right)
, one has

\frac{n}{2}-\varepsilon\leq\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{q}-\varepsilon<\min\left\{n-\varepsilon,\sigma\right\}.

(iii)

In case of
(\sigma,\gamma)\in\mathcal{S}_{3}\left(\alpha\right)~\wedge~(p,q)\in\mathcal{R}_{3}\left(\varepsilon,\sigma+\varepsilon\right)

0<\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{q}-\varepsilon<\min\left\{n-\varepsilon,\sigma\right\}.

Thus,

\displaystyle 0<\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\left(\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{q}-\nu\right)<\alpha,

and hence, condition (
6.23
) is always satisfied.
Moreover, combination of the Laplace convolution formula (
4.2
) with Laplace inversion gives

\displaystyle\Lambda(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\left(\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{q}-\left(\gamma-\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}\right)\right)\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\left(\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{q}-\left(\gamma-\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\pi}{\sin\left(\frac{\alpha\pi}{\sigma}\left(\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{q}-\left(\gamma-\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}\right)\right)\right)},

which is a positive constant independent of the time variable
t
.

Thus, for every
t\in[0,T]
the inequality (
6.22
) becomes

\displaystyle\left\|~\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}{\bm{M}}_{\sigma,n}(t-\tau,\cdot|\alpha,\beta,\mu)\ast f(\tau,\cdot)d\tau~\right\|_{q}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{q}_{x}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})},

as desired.
∎

Theorem 6.8
.

Let
0<\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}
,
2\alpha\leq\beta<2\alpha+1
,
\mu>0
and
\sigma>0
be given.

Assume further the following:

•

5.12
holds for the constants
\lambda_{+}
and

);

•

6.6
holds for the function

.

Then, for every datum

u_{0}\in\dot{W}^{\gamma-\frac{\sigma}{\alpha},p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
one has the following Strichartz estimates for the solution of the
Cauchy Problem
2.1
,
u:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, obtained in Theorem
6.3
:

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{s}_{t}L^{q}_{x}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle T^{\frac{1}{s}-\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\left(\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{q}-\left(\gamma-\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}\right)\right)}~\left\|~(-\Delta)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-\frac{\sigma}{2\alpha}}u_{0}~\right\|_{p}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle T^{\frac{1}{s}}~\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{q}_{x}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})},

only if

(6.24)

\displaystyle~1\leq s<\frac{1}{\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\left(\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{q}-\left(\gamma-\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}\right)\right)}.

Proof.

Starting from the definition of
{\bm{N}}_{\sigma,n}(t,x|\alpha,\mu)
(see
Notation
6.1
), one has

\displaystyle\left\|~\displaystyle{\bm{N}}_{\sigma,n}(t,\cdot|\alpha,\mu)\ast u_{0}~\right\|_{q}\lesssim

\displaystyle\lesssim\begin{cases}\displaystyle\max_{\lambda\in\{\lambda_{+},\lambda_{-}\}}\left\|~E_{\alpha,1}\left(-\lambda(-\Delta)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}t^{\alpha}\right)u_{0}~\right\|_{q}&,~\mu\neq 2\\
\ \\
\displaystyle~\left\|~E_{\alpha,1}\left(-(-\Delta)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}t^{\alpha}\right)u_{0}~\right\|_{q}+t^{\alpha}\left\|~(-\Delta)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}E_{\alpha,\alpha}\left(-(-\Delta)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}t^{\alpha}\right)u_{0}~\right\|_{q}&,~\mu=2.\end{cases}

Thereafter, under the
Assumption
6.6
for the constants
\lambda_{+}
and
\lambda_{-}
defined through eq. (
6.1
), the estimate

\left\|~\displaystyle{\bm{N}}_{\sigma,n}(t,\cdot|\alpha,\mu)\ast u_{0}~\right\|_{q}\lesssim t^{-\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\left(\frac{n}{q}+\frac{n}{2}-\left(\gamma-\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}\right)\right)}~\left\|~(-\Delta)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-\frac{\sigma}{2\alpha}}u_{0}~\right\|_{p}

follows by direct application of parts
(1)
and
(2)
of Corollary
5.13
to the substitution
\gamma\rightarrow\gamma-\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}
. On the other hand,
Assumption
6.6
ensures that Theorem
6.7
can be applied to the function
f:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
.

Thus, for the solution of the
Cauchy Problem
2.1
, determined in Theorem
6.3
, we end up with the following dispersive estimate

\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{q}\lesssim t^{-\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\left(\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{q}-\left(\gamma-\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}\right)\right)}~\left\|~(-\Delta)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-\frac{\sigma}{2\alpha}}u_{0}~\right\|_{p}+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{q}_{x}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})}
.

Moreover, by adopting the mixed-norm (
4.17
) of
L^{s}_{t}L^{q}_{x}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, the sequence of inequalities

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{s}_{t}L^{q}_{x}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{T}t^{-s\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\left(\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{q}-\left(\gamma-\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}\right)\right)}\|~(-\Delta)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-\frac{\sigma}{2\alpha}}u_{0}~\|_{2}^{s}~dt\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{q}_{x}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})^{s}}^{s}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle T^{\frac{1}{s}-\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\left(\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{q}-\left(\gamma-\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}\right)\right)}\left\|~(-\Delta)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-\frac{\sigma}{2\alpha}}u_{0}~\right\|_{p}+T^{\frac{1}{s}}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{q}_{x}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})}

results from the Minkowski inequality and by standard integration techniques, whereby the condition (
6.24
) is to ensure the monotonicity of the norm of
L^{s}_{t}L^{q}_{x}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and the equality

(6.25)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}t^{-s\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\left(\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{q}-\left(\gamma-\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}\right)\right)}dt=\frac{T^{1-s\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\left(\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{q}-\left(\gamma-\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}\right)\right)}}{1-s\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\left(\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{q}-\left(\gamma-\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}\right)\right)}.

This concludes the proof of Theorem
6.8
.

7.
Conclusions

7.1.
Summary of results

We have investigated questions related to dispersive and Strichartz estimates for the
Cauchy Problem
2.1
and the
Cauchy Problem
2.2
from their closed-form representations.

The framework developed in Section
5
constitute a significant part of the paper. Along this we have pinned a general scheme to achieve the optimality of the decay estimates, independently of the dimension of the ambient space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. As a consequence, the main estimates collected in Corollary
5.13
, permits us to treat
L^{p}-L^{q}
and
L^{p}-\dot{W}^{\sigma,q}
decay estimates, at least, in lower dimensions with respect to
\sigma
(see also Proposition
5.5
). That also includes
L^{1}-L^{\infty}
and
L^{1}-\dot{W}^{\sigma,\infty}
decay estimates, although we are unable to claim the sharpness of these estimates, for the reasons explained throughout Subsection
5.1
.

Note also that in case where such estimates are not possible to reach – such as the wave equation with structural damping highlighted on the beginning of the paper
[
6
]
– our approach offers the possibility to look instead for
\dot{W}^{\gamma,p}-L^{q}
and
\dot{W}^{\gamma,p}-\dot{W}^{\sigma,q}
decay estimates, whose datum is a function with membership on the
homogeneous Sobolev space

\dot{W}^{\gamma,p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
(see also Proposition
5.8
).

In Section
6
, we have used the estimates obtained in Section
5
to prove first, in Theorem
6.7
, a retarded Strichartz estimate for the inhomogeneous part of the
Cauchy Problem
2.1
&
Cauchy Problem
2.2
. Afterwards, by combining Corollary
5.13
and Theorem
6.7
, we were able to prove, in Theorem
6.8
and Theorem
6.9
, a primer version of Strichartz estimates to both Cauchy problems.

Regarding Theorem
6.7
, it is worth to observe that its proof turned out to be rather technical. Arguably, we have adopted in
Assumption
6.6
the most strongest regularity and decay properties on
\displaystyle f:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
with the aim achieve a similar version of the so-called
Christ-Kiselev lemma
(cf.
[
46
, Lemma 2.4]
). Amongst the many still open questions, obtaining a well-adapted version of
[
27
, THEOREM 1.2.]
to our case is indeed an interesting issue to be deepened in forthcoming research papers.

In comparison with other approaches, our major contribution stems, firstly, on the possibility of proving decay estimates for both Cauchy problems without studying the asymptotic behaviour of the fundamental solution. In particular, we note that the fundamental solutions encoded by higher dimensional fractional differential equations – that is, the convolution kernels that yield from the
Fourier convolution formula
– are mostly Fox-H functions (cf.
[
30
, Subsection 1.12]
). And, as it was highlighted on the paper
[
29
]
, obtaining optimal decay estimates for it revealed to be a non-trivial task.

Also, we notice that the study of the size and the decay of the solutions of Cauchy problems of heat type, possessing memory terms, has been extensively studied during the last years (see e.g.
[
28
,
11
]
and the references given there). And prior to the study of dispersive estimates, there seems that the study of Strichartz estimates for the solutions of Cauchy problems, similar to
Cauchy Problem
2.1
&
Cauchy Problem
2.2
, has not yet been fully addressed, up to authors’s knowledge. The present paper aims also to fill this gap.

7.2.
Further remarks and open problems

The wide class of space-time-fractional operators, considered on the formulation of
Cauchy Problem
2.1
&
Cauchy Problem
2.2
, correspond to a borderline case of the following operator:

(7.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{\gamma}+\mu(-\Delta)^{\theta}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}+(-\Delta)^{\sigma},

\displaystyle\mbox{with}~0<\theta\leq\sigma,~~0<\alpha\leq 1,~~\alpha<\gamma\leq 2.

For such general class of operators, Luchko-Gorenflo’s operational approach considered in
[
36
]
provides us a general framework to determine, in a similar fashion, closed-form representations to the ones obtained in Theorem
6.2
and Theorem
6.3
. On this case, the convolution kernels turned out be described in terms of
multi-index Mittag-Leffler functions
(see
[
36
, Theorem 4.1]
and
[
36
, eq. (46)]
).

At this point, it should be stressed that the choice of
\gamma=2\alpha
on eq. (
7.1
), already considered e.g. in
[
39
,
31
]
for the time-fractional telegraph equation, provides us a fairly accurate time-fractional counterpart of the
\sigma-
models investigated in depth on the series of papers
[
40
,
12
,
14
,
15
]
. Up to our knowledge, for such class of multi-index functions the monotonicity and decay properties are not very well-known on the literature (cf.
[
24
, Chapter 6]
) so that one cannot embody it in our scheme of proof to obtain dispersive estimates and to plan tangible applications towards relaxation theory as well, as tactically explained in Section
2
.
Therefore, for the general case the following interesting open questions remain:

Q1:

Is it possible to achieve endpoint decay estimates for the
multi-index Mittag-Leffler functions
? At least, using an asymptotic scheme similar to the one considered in
[
29
]
?

Q2:

Under which choices of
\gamma
and
\alpha
are possible to achieve dispersive estimates, similar to those obtained in Section
5
?

For the choice
\gamma=2\alpha
, it seems likely that our results can be extended straightforwardly for general choices of
0<\theta\leq\sigma
, since with the aid of eq. (
4.8
) we are able to say, from its solution representation, that one needs to study, in most of the cases, the decay of the Fourier multipliers of the type

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{+}(\xi)-\lambda_{-}(\xi)}~\frac{E_{\alpha,1}\left(-|\xi|^{\sigma}\lambda_{\pm}(\xi)~t^{\alpha}\right)}{\lambda_{\pm}(\xi)},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{\pm}(\xi)~t^{\beta-1}~E_{\alpha,\beta}\left(-|\xi|^{\sigma}\lambda_{\pm}(\xi)~t^{\alpha}\right)}{\lambda_{+}(\xi)-\lambda_{-}(\xi)},

(
\lambda_{+}(\xi)\neq\lambda_{-}(\xi)
) and
t^{\beta-1}E_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(-|\xi|^{\sigma}t^{\alpha})
(
\lambda_{+}(\xi)=\lambda_{-}(\xi)
), whereby

\displaystyle\lambda_{\pm}(\xi)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{2}|\xi|^{2\theta-\sigma}\pm i\sqrt{1-\frac{\mu^{2}}{4}|\xi|^{4\theta-2\sigma}}&,0<\mu<2|\xi|^{\sigma-2\theta}\\
\ \\
\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{2}|\xi|^{2\theta-\sigma}\pm\sqrt{\frac{\mu^{2}}{4}|\xi|^{4\theta-2\sigma}-1}&,\mu\geq 2|\xi|^{\sigma-2\theta}.\end{cases}

In case where
\theta=\dfrac{\sigma}{2}
we are in conditions to claim that our results are enough to cover the so-called critical case (i.e. when
\lambda_{\pm}(\xi)
are constants), but we cannot yet claim it for the remaining cases

\displaystyle 0<\theta<\frac{\sigma}{2}
and
\displaystyle\frac{\sigma}{2}<\theta\leq\sigma
,
already treated by Pham et al. in
[
40
]
for values of
\alpha=\beta=1
. The main gap to be to be circumvented is the fact that the terms

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{+}(\xi)-\lambda_{-}(\xi)}

and

\displaystyle\dfrac{\lambda_{\pm}(\xi)}{\lambda_{+}(\xi)-\lambda_{-}(\xi)}

and unbounded for values of
\displaystyle|\xi|^{\sigma-2\theta}\approx\frac{\mu}{2}
.
However, based on the identity
\lambda_{+}(\xi)\cdot\lambda_{-}(\xi)=1
, the following question comes naturally:

Q3:

Can we also obtain estimates, similar to the ones reached in Section
5
, to regions defined implicitly by

\displaystyle|\lambda_{+}(\xi)-\lambda_{-}(\xi)|\geq\varepsilon\cdot\max\left\{\mu,\mu|\xi|^{2\theta-\sigma}\right\}
,
for some
\varepsilon>0
?

Broadly speaking, we essentially conjecture a perturbation argument, underlying to the asymptotic behaviour of
\varepsilon:=\varepsilon(\tau)
on small scales
0<\tau<1
:

\displaystyle\varepsilon\sim\tan\left(\frac{\pi\tau}{2}\right)

\left(~0<\mu<2|\xi|^{\sigma-2\theta}~\right)

\wedge

\displaystyle\varepsilon\sim\tanh\left(\tau\right)

\left(~\mu>2|\xi|^{\sigma-2\theta}~\right)
.

From the background of semigroup theory, we already know from the abstract results obtained in
[
21
, Section VI.3.a]
that the application of perturbation arguments, similar to the above one, are mostly feasible in case of
\displaystyle\frac{\sigma}{2}<\theta\leq\sigma
– that is, for the case where the elastic operator is bounded above by the damping operator. But due to the fact that the generalizations of the framework considered in
[
40
]
, on which the authors already treat the case
\displaystyle\frac{\sigma}{2}<\theta\leq\sigma
, requires in our case to handle, at most, with subordination formulae for the generalized Mittag-Leffler functions
E_{\alpha,\beta}
in terms of probability density functions defined through the so-called Wright functions (cf.
[
37
]
), we decided to postpone the treatment of the aforementioned remaining cases for a forthcoming paper.

We conclude this section by noticing that Cordero-Zucco’s approach on Strichartz estimates for the vibrating plate equation on Sobolev and modulation spaces (case of
\mu\rightarrow 0^{+}
;
\sigma=2
) (see
[
9
,
10
]
) also falls into the framework of the class of function spaces and pseudo-differential operators investigated in depth in Section
5
and Section
6
. This is also an interesting issue to be deepened in a nearly future, as well as the following broadly general questions:

Q4:

Employing the well-established Littlewood-Paley’s theory, can we relax some of the results enclosed on this paper?

Q5:

Can we replace the
homogeneous Sobolev spaces
, adopted throughout this paper, by homogeneous analogues of Besov spaces?
