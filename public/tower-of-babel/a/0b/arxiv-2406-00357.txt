Better coloring of 3-colorable graphs
Ken-ichi Kawarabayashi, Mikkel Thorup, Hirotaka Yoneda

Abstract
We consider the problem of coloring a 3-colorable graph in polynomial time using as few colors as possible. This is one of the most challenging problems in graph algorithms. In this paper using Blum’s notion of “progress”, we develop a new combinatorial algorithm for the following: Given any 3-colorable graph with minimum degree Δ > n \Delta>\sqrt{n} , we can, in polynomial time, make progress towards a k k -coloring for some k = n / Δ ⋅ n o ⁡ ( 1 ) k=\sqrt{n/\Delta}\cdot n^{o(1)} . We balance our main result with the best-known semi-definite(SDP) approach which we use for degrees below n 0.605073 n^{0.605073} . As a result, we show that O ~ ​ ( n 0.19747 ) {\widetilde{O}}\/(n^{0.19747}) colors suffice for coloring 3-colorable graphs. This improves on the previous best bound of O ~ ​ ( n 0.19996 ) {\widetilde{O}}\/(n^{0.19996}) by Kawarabayashi and Thorup [ 20 ] .

Introduction

Recognizing 3-colorable graphs is a classic problem, which was proved to be NP-hard by
Garey, Johnson, and Stockmeyer
[
12
]
in 1976,
and was one of the
prime examples of NP-hardness mentioned by Karp in 1975

[
18
]
. In this paper, we also focus on 3-colorable graphs.

Given a 3-colorable graph,
that is a graph with an unknown 3-coloring, we try to color it in
polynomial time using as few colors as possible. The algorithm
is allowed to give up if the input graph is not 3-colorable.
If a coloring is produced, we can always check that it is valid even if
the input graph is not 3-colorable. This challenge has
engaged many researchers in theoretical computer science for a long time, yet we are far from understanding it. Wigderson
[
23
]
is the first to give a combinatorial algorithm for
O(n^{1/2})
coloring for a 3-colorable graph with
n
vertices in 1982. Berger and
Rompel
[
4
]
then improved this result to
O((n/(\log n))^{1/2})

1.1
Interplay between combinatorial and SDP approaches and our main result

In this paper, we will further
improve the coloring
bound for 3-colorable graphs to
{\widetilde{O}}\/(n^{0.19747})
colors.
However, what makes
our results interesting is that we converge towards a limit for known
combinatorial approaches as explained below.

To best describe our own and previous results, we need Blum’s
[
5
]
notion of
progress towards
k
-coloring
. The basic
idea is that if we for any 3-colorable graph can make
progress towards
k
-coloring in polynomial time, then we can
{\widetilde{O}}\/(k)
-color any 3-colorable graph.

Reductions from
[
1
,
6
,
20
]
show
that for any parameter
\Delta
, it suffices to find progress towards

k
-coloring for 3-colorable graphs that have either minimum degree
\Delta
or maximum degree
\Delta
. High minimum degree has been good for combinatorial approaches while low
maximum degree has been good for SDP approaches. The best bounds are
obtained by choosing
\Delta
to balance between the best SDP and
combinatorial algorithms.

Purely combinatorial bounds

On the combinatorial side,
for 3-colorable graphs with minimum degree
\Delta=n^{1-\Omega(1)}
, the previous bounds for progress have followed the following sequence

{\widetilde{O}}\/((n/\Delta)^{i/(2i-1)}).

(1)

Here

is by Wigderson in STOC’82 (covered by
[
23
]
), while

is by Blum from STOC’89, and

is by
Blum at FOCS’90 (both covered by
[
5
]
). Finally,

is by Kawarabayashi and Thorup from FOCS’12 (covered by
[
20
]
).

Since we can trivially color a graph with maximum
degree
\Delta
with
\Delta+1
colors, (

)
also yields combinatorial coloring bounds with
{\widetilde{O}}\/(n^{i/(3i-1})
colors assuming
(
1
) for any
\Delta\geq n^{i/(3i-1)}

{\widetilde{O}}\/(n^{4/11})

Combination with SDP

For 3-colorable graphs with maximum degree

, Karger et al.

Combinatorial algorithm for balance with SDP

We now note that (

(n/\Delta)^{1/2}
from above. Balancing with
a SDP bound which is as good or better than

\Delta^{1/3}
, we would only need (

\Delta\geq n^{0.61674333}

In principle,

Our result

For

, the sequence (

) approaches
{\widetilde{O}}\/((n/\Delta)^{1/2})
. In this paper, we get arbitrarily close to this limit assuming only that
\Delta\geq\sqrt{n}

combinatorial result is:

Theorem 1

In polynomial time, for any 3-colorable graph with
n
vertices with minimum degree
\Delta>n^{0.5}

we can make progress towards a
k
-coloring for some
k=n^{o(1)}(n/\Delta)^{1/2}

For the optimal balance
with the best SDP by Chlamtac
[

, we will
set
\Delta=n^{0.605073}

than our limit

Thereby we improve the best-known bound of

by Kawarabayashi and Thorup

to color any 3-colorable

n

In polynomial time, for any 3-colorable graph with
n

{\widetilde{O}}\/(n^{0.19747})
-coloring.

To appreciate the simple

(

Consider a 3-colorable graph on

Techniques

Our coloring algorithm follows
the same general pattern
as that in
[
20
]
, which
recurses through a sequence of nested cuts, called “sparse cuts”, until it finds progress.
Here we go through the same recursion, but in addition to
the sparse cuts, we identify a family of alternative “side cuts”.
Having the choice between
the side cuts and the sparse cuts is what leads us to
the nicer and stronger bounds from Theorem

[
20
]

Progress

Blum has a general notion of
progress towards an
{\widetilde{O}}\/(k)
coloring

(or
progress
for short if
k
is understood). The basic
idea is that such progress eventually leads to a full
{\widetilde{O}}\/(k)
coloring
of a graph. Blum presents three types of progress towards
{\widetilde{O}}\/(k)
coloring:

Type 0: Same color.

Finding vertices
u
and
v
that have
the same color in every 3-coloring.

Type 1: Large independent set.

Finding an independent or 2-colorable vertex set
X
of size
{\widetilde{\Omega}}(n/k)
.

Type 2: Small neighborhood.

Finding a non-empty independent
or 2-colorable vertex set
X
such that
|N(X)|={\widetilde{O}}\/(k|X|)
.

In order to get from progress to actual coloring, we want

k
to be bounded by a

near-polynomial
function
f
of
n
where
near-polynomial means that
f
is non-decreasing and that there are
constants
c,c^{\prime}>1
such that
cf(n)\leq f(2n)\leq c^{\prime}f(n)
for all
n
. As described in
[
5
]
, this includes any function of
the form
f(n)=n^{\alpha}\log^{\beta}n
for constants
\alpha>0
and

\beta
.

Lemma 4
(
[
5
, Lemma 1]
)

Let
f
be near-polynomial. If we in time polynomial in
n

can make progress towards an
{\widetilde{O}}\/(f(n))
coloring of either Type 0, 1, or 2, on any 3-colorable graph on
n
vertices,
then in time polynomial in
n
, we can
{\widetilde{O}}\/(f(n))
color any 3-colorable graph on
n
vertices.

The general strategy is to identify a small
parameter
k
for which we can guarantee progress. To apply
Lemma
4
and get a coloring, we need a bound
f
on
k

where
f
is near-polynomial in
n
. As
soon as we find one progress of the above types, we are done, so
generally, whenever we see a condition that implies progress, we
assume that the condition is not satisfied.

Our focus is to find a vertex set
X
,
|X|>1
, that
is guaranteed to be monochromatic in every 3-coloring. This will
happen assuming that we do not make other progress on the way. When we
have the vertex set
X
, we get same-color progress for any pair of
vertices in
X
. We refer to this as
monochromatic
progress
.

Most of our progress will be made
via the results of Blum presented below using a common parameter

\Psi=n/k^{2}.

(2)

A very useful tool we get from Blum is the
following multichromatic (more than one color) test:

Lemma 5
(
[
5
, Corollary 4]
)

Given a vertex set
X\subseteq V
of

\Psi=n/k^{2}

progress towards an
{\widetilde{O}}\/(k)
-coloring of
G
, or else guarantee that

X

The following lemma is implicit in
[

]

Lemma 6

)

If the vertices in a set

the whole set
Z
has at least
\min\{d/\Psi,|Z|\}\,d/2

distinct neighbors in
U
(otherwise some progress is made).

Recursive combinatorial coloring

Our algorithm follows the same pattern as that in
[
20
]
, but adds in certain side-cuts
leading us to both stronger and cleaner bounds.
Below we describe the algorithm, emphasizing the novel additions.

First, we will be able to have different and stronger parameters than those in
[
20
]
. Given a 3-colorable graph with
minimum degree
\Delta\geq\sqrt{n}
, we will make progress
towards a
k
-coloring for

k=2^{\,(\log\log n)^{2}}\sqrt{n/\Delta}

(5)

Since
\Psi=n/k^{2}
, this is equivalent to

\Delta/\Psi=4^{\,(\log\log n)^{2}}.

(6)

We will use the above 2-level neighborhood
structure
H_{1}=(r_{0},S_{1},T_{1})
, and we are
going to recurse on induced subproblems

(S,T)\subseteq(S_{1},T_{1})
defined in terms of subsets
S\subseteq S_{1}
and
T\subseteq T_{1}
. The edges considered in the subproblem
are exactly those between
S
and
T
in
G
. This
edge set is denoted
E(S,T)
.

A pseudo-code for our whole algorithm is presented in Algorithm

.

// outer loop, round

(S,T)\leftarrow(S_{j},T_{j})
;

// inner loop. Iterative version of recursive

|S|\leq 1

return
‘‘Error A’’;

U\leftarrow\{v\in T\,|\,d_{S}(v)\geq\delta_{j}/4\}

it does end up making progress.

]

When we

\displaystyle=

will make only good rounds, and make progress
towards an
{\widetilde{O}}\/(k)
coloring no later than round
\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor
.

Proof

We are going to analyze round
j\leq c=\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor
of the outer loop.
Assuming that all previous
rounds have been good,
but no progress has been made, we will show that
the preconditions of
round
j
are satisfied,
hence that round
j
must
also be good. Later we will also show that progress must be made no later than
round
j=c
.

To show that the preconditions
are satisfied, we will develop
bounds for
|T_{j}|
,
\Delta_{j}
, and
\delta_{j}
assuming that
all previous rounds have
been good and that no progress has been made. We
already know that
|T_{1}|\leq n/k
and

\Delta_{1}={\widetilde{\Omega}}(\Delta)
. Moreover,

5\delta_{1}\geq\textnormal{avg}\,{d}_{S_{1}}(T_{1})\geq\Delta_{1}|S_{1}|/|T_{1}|\geq\Delta_{1}^{2}k/n
, so
\delta_{1}={\widetilde{\Omega}}(\Delta^{2}k/n)
.

Let us observe that since no progress is made, round
j
ends up regularizing. As in
previous sections, we let
X_{j}
and
Y_{j}

denote the last values of
S
and
T
in round
j
. Then

(S_{j+1},T_{j+1},\Delta_{j+1},\delta_{j+1})=\texttt{regularize}(X_{j},Y_{j}).

We will now derive inductive bounds on
|T_{j+1}|
,
\Delta_{j+1}
, and
\delta_{j+1}
.

Computing
\Delta_{j}
.

By Lemma
10
and (
9
),
we get

\Delta_{j+1}\geq\textnormal{avg}\,{d}_{Y_{j}}(X_{j})/(30\log n)\geq\Delta_{j}/(60\log n)

Inductively, we conclude for
any
j
that

\Delta_{j}\geq\Delta_{1}/(60\log n)^{j-1}={\widetilde{\Omega}}(\Delta/(60\log n)^{j}).

(19)

We note that
\Delta_{j}
is
just a lower bound on the degrees from
S_{j}
to
T_{j}
and we can assume that it
is decreasing like in (
19
).

Satisfying pre-condition
(
7
) stating
\Psi=o(\Delta_{j})
.

By (
18
)
we have

\Delta/\Psi=\Delta k^{2}/n=4^{(\log\log n)^{2}}.

(20)

For
j<\log\log n
, we have

(60\log n)^{j}=\tilde{o}(4^{(\log\log n)^{2}})\textnormal{,}

so we get (
7
) in the stronger from

\Psi=\tilde{o}(\Delta_{j}).

(21)

Computing
|T_{j}|
.

When we start on
the outer loop, we have

|T_{1}|\leq n/k=\Psi k
and
the regularization implies

T_{j+1}\subseteq Y_{j}
, so
(
16
) implies

|T_{j+1}|\leq|Y_{j}|\leq\sqrt{30\Psi|T_{j}|}
.
Thus, assuming that
nothing goes wrong,

|Y_{j}|<30\Psi k^{1/2^{j}}.

(22)

Computing
|\delta_{j}|
.

Recall that
\delta_{1}={\widetilde{\Omega}}(\Delta^{2}k/n)
and
consider
j>1
. We have

\textnormal{avg}\,{d}_{X_{j}}(Y_{j})\geq(\Delta_{j}/2)|X_{j}|/|Y_{j}|

by (
9
), and

|X_{j}|\geq\delta_{j}/4
by (
10
),
and
|Y_{j}|<30\,\Psi k^{1/2^{j}}
by (
22
). Therefore

\displaystyle\textnormal{avg}\,{d}_{X_{j}}(Y_{j})=\Omega\left(\Delta_{j}\delta_{j}/(\Psi k^{1/2^{j}})\right).

By Lemma
10

\delta_{j+1}={\widetilde{\Omega}}\!\left(\textnormal{avg}\,{d}_{X_{j}}(Y_{j})\right)={\widetilde{\Omega}}\!\left(\Delta_{j}\delta_{j}/(\Psi k^{1/2^{j}})\right)=\delta_{j}\,{\widetilde{\Omega}}\left(\Delta_{j}/\Psi\right)/k^{1/2^{j}}

By induction and since the
\Delta_{j}
are assumed to be decreasing, we get

\delta_{j+1}={\widetilde{\Omega}}(\Delta^{2}k/n)\,{\widetilde{\Omega}}\left(\Delta_{j}/\Psi\right)^{j}/k^{1-1/2^{j}}={\widetilde{\Omega}}\left(\Delta_{j}/\Psi\right)^{j}\Delta^{2}k^{1/2^{j}}/n.

(23)

Satisfying pre-condition
(
8
) stating
\delta_{j}\geq 4\Delta_{j}/\Psi
.

We note that
\Delta_{j}
is
just a lower bound on the degrees from
S_{j}
to
T_{j}

and we can assume that it
is decreasing like in (
19
).
The proof is divided into three different cases:
j=1
,
j=2
, and
j>2
. For
j=1
, we have

\delta_{1}={\widetilde{\Omega}}(\Delta^{2}k/n)=(\Delta/\Psi){\widetilde{\Omega}}(\Delta/k)
. Here
{\widetilde{\Omega}}(\Delta/k)=\omega(1)
by (
3
), so pre-condition (
8
) is satisfied.

For larger

j>1
, that is, to make it past the first round, we need lower bounds on the minimum degree
\Delta
.
For
j=2
, by
(
23
),
\delta_{2}={\widetilde{\Omega}}\left(\Delta_{1}/\Psi\right)\Delta^{2}k^{1/2}/n
.
However, since
\Delta\geq\sqrt{n}
and
k>\sqrt{n/\Delta}
,

\Delta^{2}k^{1/2}/n>\Delta^{2}(n/\Delta)^{1/4}/n\geq n^{1/8}.

Thus
\delta_{2}>{\widetilde{\Omega}}\left(\Delta_{1}/\Psi\right)n^{1/8}={\widetilde{\omega}}(\Delta_{1}/\Psi)
.
Above we do have some slack in that it would have sufficed with
\Delta>n^{3/7+\Omega(1)}
, but for later rounds, we cannot have

\Delta
much smaller than
\sqrt{n}
. More precisely, for
j>2
, by
(
23
),

\delta_{j+1}={\widetilde{\Omega}}\left(\Delta_{j}/\Psi\right)^{j}\Delta^{2}k^{1/2^{j}}/n\geq{\widetilde{\Omega}}\left(\Delta_{j}/\Psi\right)^{2}\Delta^{2}k^{1/2^{j}}/n={\widetilde{\omega}}(\Delta_{j}/\Psi)\,\Delta^{2}k^{1/2^{j}}/n.

The last derivation follows from
(
21
) using
j>2
. With
\Delta\geq\sqrt{n}
, we have
\Delta^{2}k^{1/2^{j}}/n>1

and (
8
) follows.

Progress must be made.

Assuming only good rounds,
we will now argue that progress must be made no later
than round
\log\log n
. Assuming no
progress, recall from (
22
)
that

|Y_{j}|<30\,\Psi k^{1/2^{j}}.

From (
11
), we know that any
Y
considered (without progress), including
Y_{j}
,
is of size at least
\Delta_{j}^{2}/(8\Psi)
.
Thus, if we make it through round
j
without
progress, we must have

\Delta_{j}^{2}/(8\Psi)<30\,\Psi k^{1/2^{j}}\iff(\Delta_{j}/\Psi)^{2}<240\,k^{1/2^{j}}

By (
21
),
\Delta_{j}/\Psi={\widetilde{\omega}}(1)
for

j<\log\log n
, and

k^{1/2^{j}}<2
for
j=\lceil\log\log k\rceil<\log\log n
,
so progress must be made no later than
round
j=c=\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor
.
This
completes our proof of Theorem
16
.

Using Theorem
1
, we can show the following:

Theorem 17

In polynomial time, we can color any 3-colorable
n
vertex graph
using
{\widetilde{O}}\/(n^{0.19747})
colors.

Proof

As in
[
20
]
, we use Chlamtac’s SDP
[
7
]
for low degrees. By Theorem
8
, for maximum degree below

\Delta=n^{\tau}
with
\tau={0.605073}

and
c=0.0213754
, we can make progress towards
k={\widetilde{O}}\/(n^{0.19747})
coloring. By Lemma
7
, we may therefore assume that

\Delta
is the minimum degree. This is
easily above
\sqrt{n}
, and then by Theorem
16
, we get progress
towards

\sqrt{n/\Delta}2^{(\log\log n)^{2}}=n^{(1-0.605073)/2}2^{(\log\log n)^{2}}<n^{0.19747}.

Thus, in polynomial time, we can color any 3-colorable graph with

{\widetilde{O}}\/(n^{0.19747})
colors.
