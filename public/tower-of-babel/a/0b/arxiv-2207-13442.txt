Abstract

Cubic transmuted (CT) distributions were introduced recently by
Granzotto et al. 2017
. In this article, we derive Shannon entropy, Gini’s mean difference and Fisher information (matrix) for CT distributions and establish some of their theoretical properties. In addition, we propose cubic transmuted Shannon entropy and cubic transmuted Gini’s mean difference. The CT Shannon entropy is expressed in terms of Kullback-Leibler divergences, while the CT Gini’s mean difference is shown to be connected with energy distances. We show that the Kullback-Leibler and Chi-square divergences are free of the underlying parent distribution. Finally, we carry out some simulation studies for the proposed information measures from an inferential viewpoint.

Keywords.
Quadratic transmuted distributions; CT Shannon entropy; CT Gini’s mean difference; Fisher information; Kullback-Leibler divergence; Chi-square divergence; Cubic transmuted distributions.

Introduction

Recently, many different generalized families of distributions have been proposed in the literature in order to provide greater flexibility while modelling data arising from diverse applications. In this regard,
Shaw and Buckley 2009
introduced an important class of distributions, known as the class of transmuted distributions, by using a quadratic ranking transmutation map. Let
F(\cdot)
be the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of a baseline distribution. Then, the CDF of the transmuted distribution is (
Shaw and Buckley 2009
)

\displaystyle F_{X_{T}}(x)=(1+\lambda)F(x)-\lambda F^{2}(x),~~|\lambda|\leq 1~~x\in\mathbb{R},

(1.1)

where
\mathbb{R}
denotes the set of real numbers. The transmuted generalized extreme value distribution was studied by
Aryal and Tsokos 2009
. They also described an application of the transmuted Gumbel distribution for the climate data. There are some real life data sets whose complexity can not be adequately captured using quadratic transmuted distributions. A significant amount of work has been made towards developing a new transmuted model and subsequently discussing its enhanced flexibility in modelling various types of real life data, where the parent model does not provide a good fit.
Granzotto et al. 2017
established a mixture representation for the transmuted distribution in (
1.1
), using distributions of order statistics. To be specific, with
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
being i.i.d. random variables with distribution function
F(x)
and
(X_{1:2},X_{2:2})
denoting the corresponding order statistics, they considered the mixture random variable

\displaystyle X\overset{d}{=}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}X_{1:2}~\mbox{with~probability}~~\pi\\
X_{2:2}~\mbox{with~probability}~1-\pi,\end{array}\right.

where
0\leq\pi\leq 1
is the mixing probability. Then, using the CDFs of
X_{1:2}
and
X_{2:2}
given by
1-\{1-F(x)\}^{2}
and
F^{2}(x)
(see, for example,
Arnold et al. 1992
), respectively, (
1
) readily yields the CDF of
X
to be

\displaystyle F_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi\big[1-\{1-F(x)\}^{2}\big]+(1-\pi)F^{2}(x)

(1.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi F(x)+(1-2\pi)F^{2}(x).

It is evident that the quadratic transmuted distribution in (
1.1
) is a simple reparametrization of the mixture distribution in (
1.5
) (with
2\pi=1+\lambda
).

Then, proceeding along the same lines and considering
X_{1},X_{2}
and
X_{3}
to be i.i.d. random variables with distribution function
F(x)
and
(X_{1:3},X_{2:3},X_{3:3})
as the corresponding order statistics,
Granzotto et al. 2017
considered the mixture random variable

\displaystyle X\overset{d}{=}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}X_{1:3}~\mbox{with~probability}~~\pi_{1}\\
X_{2:3}~\mbox{with~probability}~\pi_{2}\\
X_{3:3}~\mbox{with~probability}~\pi_{3},\end{array}\right.

where
\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{3}\in(0,1)
are the mixing probabilities such that
\pi_{1}+\pi_{2}+\pi_{3}=1
. The CDFs and PDFs of
X_{1:3},

X_{2:3}
,
X_{3:3}
are denoted by
F_{\min}(\cdot)
,
F_{2:3}(\cdot),

F_{\max}(\cdot)
and
f_{\min}(\cdot)
,
f_{2:3}(\cdot),

f_{\max}(\cdot)
, respectively. Then, using the CDFs of
X_{1:3},X_{2:3}
and
X_{3:3}
given in
Arnold et al. 1992
, for example, (
1
) readily yields the CDF of
X
to be

\displaystyle F_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3\pi_{1}F(x)+3(\pi_{2}-\pi_{1})F^{2}(x)+(1-3\pi_{2})F^{3}(x).

(1.10)

Upon re-parametrizing the distribution in (
1.10
) by setting
3\pi_{1}=\lambda_{1}
and
3\pi_{2}=\lambda_{2}
,
Granzotto et al. 2017
proposed the cubic transmuted distribution with CDF

\displaystyle F_{X_{CT}}(x)=\lambda_{1}F(x)+(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})F^{2}(x)+(1-\lambda_{2})F^{3}(x),

(1.11)

where
\lambda_{1}\in[0,1]
and
\lambda_{2}\in[-1,1]
. The density function corresponding to the CDF in (
1.11
) is

\displaystyle f_{X_{CT}}(x)=f(x)\{\lambda_{1}+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})F(x)+3(1-\lambda_{2})F^{2}(x)\}.

(1.12)

The model considered by
Rahman et al. 2018
is a simple re-parametrization of the distribution in (
1.11
). Observe that if
\lambda_{1}
is taken to be
1+\lambda
and
\lambda_{2}
to be 1 in (
1.11
), then the quadratic transmuted distribution in (
1.1
) is deduced. Further, if
\lambda_{1}
is taken to be
1+\lambda
and
\lambda_{2}
to be
1-\lambda
in (
1.11
), then the cubic transmuted distribution with single parameter is deduced (see
AL-Kadim and Mohammed 2017
). The corresponding CDF and PDF of the single parameter CT distribution are, respectively, given by

\displaystyle F^{*}_{X_{CT}}(x)=(1+\lambda)F(x)-2\lambda F^{2}(x)+\lambda F^{3}(x)

(1.13)

and

\displaystyle f^{*}_{X_{CT}}(x)=f(x)\left\{(1+\lambda)-4\lambda f(x)+3\lambda f^{2}(x)\right\},

(1.14)

where
|\lambda|\leq 1
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Aslam et al. 2018
studied goodness of fit tests that the cubic transmuted distribution fits better than the parent distributions for the real data set (see also
Rahman et al. 2020
and
Ahsan-ul Haq et al. 2023
).
Later,
Rahman et al. 2019
showed that cubic transmuted power distribution fits better than the parent distribution for some real data set. It is important to mention here that such mixture distributions of order statistics had earlier been considered in the pioneering work of
Lehmann 1953
as a nonparametric alternative in his development of rank based tests (see also the related comments by
Balakrishnan 2021
).

The uncertainty associated with a probability model can be computed using several information measures. In many situations related to lifetime data analysis, experimental physics, econometrics and demography, uncertainty quantification in a distribution is very important. The uncertainty in a random variable can be measured using the concepts of Fisher information (see
Fisher 1929
), Shannon entropy (see
Shannon 1948
) and Rényi entropy (see
Rényi 1961
). Besides these, various divergence measures such as Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence (see
Kullback and Leibler 1951
), Chi-square divergence (see
Nielsen and Nock 2013
) have been introduced to measure the closeness between two probability distributions.

From the above discussion, we note that although many transmuted families of distributions have been introduced and studied by several researchers, these models have not been explored from information theoretical viewpoint except for the work of
Kharazmi and Balakrishnan 2021
.
Kharazmi and Balakrishnan 2021
studied transmuted distributions and established several theoretic informational properties. They also provided extensions of Shannon entropy and Gini’s mean difference. Motivated by the work of
Kharazmi and Balakrishnan 2021
, we extend here their results to the results for the family of cubic transmuted distributions partially.

In this paper, we explore several informational properties of a more flexible and general class of cubic transmuted distributions in (
1.11
). We study Shannon entropy, Gini’s mean difference, and Fisher information of the class of cubic transmuted distributions. In addition, we also study KL divergence, Chi-square divergence, and energy distance divergence (
Cramér 1928
) between CT distribution and its components. We propose extensions of Shannon entropy and Gini’s mean difference and call these as CT Shannon entropy and CT Gini’s mean difference, respectively.

The rest of this paper proceeds as follows. In Section
2
, we study Shannon entropy of a CT distribution. We obtain Kullback-Leibler divergence between CT distribution and the densities of its components. It is noted that the KL divergence between a general transmuted model and its components is free of the parent distribution. The cubic transmuted Shannon entropy is proposed as an extension of Shannon entropy of CT distribution and Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence measure. Further, the proposed CT Shannon entropy is expressed in terms of KL divergence measures of the general model and its components. Gini’s mean difference of the CT distributions is proposed and studied in Section
3.
Further, Gini’s mean difference of the CT distribution is expressed as the sum of Gini’s mean difference of the general transmuted distribution and that of its components. Similar to the CT Shannon entropy, CT Gini’s mean difference is proposed. In Section
4
, we discuss the Chi-square divergence between the general transmuted distribution and the density functions of its components. Section
5
presents Fisher information for the CT distribution. We obtain Fisher information matrix for the CT distribution given in (
1.11
). In addition, we also present Fisher information of the particular model with CDF as in (
1.13
). Section
6
describes simulation investigation to show the importance of the proposed information measures from an inferential viewpoint. Finally, some concluding remarks are added in Section
7.

2
Shannon entropy, KL divergence and CT Shannon entropy

The purpose of this section is three-fold. First, we study Shannon entropy of the CT distribution. Then, we derive KL divergences for the general CT distribution and its components, and finally we define CT Shannon entropy, which can be treated as an extension of the Shannon entropy and JS divergence.

2.1
Shannon entropy

Here, we derive Shannon entropy of the CT distribution with CDF and PDF given in (
1.11
) and (
1.12
), respectively.
Let
X
be an absolutely continuous random variable with PDF
f(\cdot)
. Then, the Shannon entropy and weighted Shannon entropy of
X
are, respectively, given by

\displaystyle H(f)=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\log\big(f(x)\big)dx~~\mbox{and}~~H^{\psi}(f)=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi(x)f(x)\log\big(f(x)\big)dx,

(2.1)

where
\psi
is a nonnegative real-valued measurable function. We note that the concept of weighted entropy takes into account values of different outcomes and this makes entropy context-dependent, through the choice of weight function. In particular, when
\psi(x)=x,
the functional
H^{\psi}(f)
reduces to a length-biased information measure (see
Di Crescenzo and Longobardi 2006
). A random variable
X
is said to have beta distribution if its PDF is of the form

\displaystyle g(x)=\frac{x^{a-1}(1-x)^{b-1}}{\mathbb{B}(a,b)},~~0<x<1,~a,b>0,

(2.2)

where
\mathbb{B}(a,b)
denotes the complete beta function. Henceforth, we denote
X\sim Beta(a,b)
if its PDF is as in (
2.2
).

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
X_{1}
,
X_{2},
and
X_{3}
be i.i.d. random variables with a common CDF
F(\cdot)
and PDF
f(\cdot)
. Then, the Shannon entropy of a CT random variable
X_{CT}
is given by

\displaystyle H(f_{X_{CT}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}H(f)+(1-\lambda_{2})H(f_{\max})+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})H^{F}(f)-(1-\lambda_{2})H(f_{W})+H(f_{U_{CT}}),

where
U_{CT}
is the CT uniform random variable,
W\sim Beta(3,1)
, and
H^{F}(X)
means weighted Shannon entropy defined in (
2.1
) with weight function
F.

Proof.

The Shannon entropy of the CT random variable
X_{CT}
is

\displaystyle H(f_{X_{CT}})=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{X_{CT}}(x)\log\big(f_{X_{CT}}(x)\big)dx=I_{1}+I_{2},

(2.3)

where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big[f(x)\{\lambda_{1}+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})F(x)+3(1-\lambda_{2})F^{2}(x)\}\big]\log\big(f(x)\big)dx,

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big[f(x)\{\lambda_{1}+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})F(x)+3(1-\lambda_{2})F^{2}(x)\}\big]\log\big(\lambda_{1}+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})F(x)

\displaystyle+~3(1-\lambda_{2})F^{2}(x)\big)dx.

Using the transformation
u=F(x)
in (
2.3
) and after some algebraic calculations, we obtain

\displaystyle H(f_{X_{CT}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}H(f)+(1-\lambda_{2})H(f_{\max})+(1-\lambda_{2})\int_{0}^{1}3u^{2}\log(3u^{2})du

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{1}\big\{\lambda_{1}+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})u+3(1-\lambda_{2})u^{2}\big\}\log\big(\lambda_{1}+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})u

\displaystyle+3(1-\lambda_{2})u^{2}\big)du-2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(x)f(x)\log\big(f(x)\big)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}H(f)+(1-\lambda_{2})H(f_{\max})-(1-\lambda_{2})H(f_{W})+H(f_{U_{CT}})

\displaystyle+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})H^{F}(f).

Hence, the result.
∎

Substituting
\lambda_{1}=1+\lambda
and
\lambda_{2}=1
in the result of Theorem
2.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle H(f_{X_{T}})=(1+\lambda)H(f)+H(f_{U_{T}})-2\lambda H^{F}(f),

(2.4)

where
f_{U_{T}}(\cdot)
is the PDF of the transmuted uniform random variable. Now,

\displaystyle-2\lambda H^{F}(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\lambda\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(x)f(x)\log\big(f(x)\big)dx

(2.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\lambda_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(x)f(x)\log\Big(\frac{2F(x)f(x)}{2F(x)}\Big)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}2F(x)f(x)\log\big(2F(x)f(x)\big)dx-\lambda\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}2F(x)f(x)\log\big(2F(x)\big)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda H(f_{\max})-\lambda\int_{0}^{1}2u\log(2u)du~~\big(\mbox{using}~u=F(x)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda H(f_{\max})+\lambda H(f_{V}),

where
f_{V}(\cdot)
is the PDF of
V\sim Beta(2,1).
In the following remark, we study Shannon entropy for two special cases of CT distribution.

Remark 2.1
.

•

For
\lambda_{2}=1
and
\lambda_{1}=1+\lambda
, using (
2.5
), the Shannon entropy of CT distribution becomes

\displaystyle H(f_{X_{T}})=(1+\lambda)H(f)-\lambda H(f_{\max})+\lambda H(f_{V})+H(f_{U_{T}}),

where
U_{T}
is the transmuted uniform random variable and
V\sim Beta(2,1)
. For the details, one may refer to
Kharazmi and Balakrishnan 2021
.

•

For
\lambda_{1}=1+\lambda
and
\lambda_{2}=1-\lambda
, the expression of Shannon entropy for the CT distribution in Theorem
2.1
reduces to the Shannon entropy of one parameter CT distribution with CDF in (
1.13
), which is given by

\displaystyle H(f^{*}_{X_{CT}})=(1+\lambda)H(f)+\lambda H(f_{\max})-2\lambda H^{F}(f)-\lambda H(f_{W})+H(f^{*}_{U_{CT}}).

Remark 2.2
.

Define
\theta(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=H(f_{U_{CT}})-(1-\lambda_{2})H(f_{W})
. Then, after some algebraic calculations, we obtain a closed-form expression for
\theta(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
as

\displaystyle\theta(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Big\{\log(3-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})-\phi(p)+\phi(q)-\frac{2}{3}(3+\lambda_{1}-4\lambda_{2})

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{9(1-\lambda_{2})}(\lambda^{2}_{1}+13\lambda^{2}_{2}-2\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}-18\lambda_{2}+6)\Big\}-(1-\lambda_{2})\Big\{\frac{2}{3}-\log(3)\Big\},

\displaystyle\phi(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2r}\left\{2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})^{2}x^{2}+10(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})(1-\lambda_{2})x^{3}+12(1-\lambda_{2})^{2}x^{4}\right\}\log\Big(\frac{x}{x-1}\Big),

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}-3\lambda_{1}},~p=\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}+r}{3(1-\lambda_{2})},~q=\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}-r}{3(1-\lambda_{2})}.

Because of the complex about its form of
\theta(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
, it is difficult to analyse it from a theoretical viewpoint. To get an idea about its codomain, we plot the function with respect to
\lambda_{1}
when
\lambda_{2}
is fixed, and with respect to
\lambda_{2}
when
\lambda_{1}
is fixed (see Figure

). From the figure, it is clearly seen that
\theta(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
takes both positive and negative values.

2.2
Kullback-Leibler divergence

We prove the following result which shows that Kullback-Leibler divergence measures between CT distribution and its components are equal to those between CT uniform distribution and its components. Let
f_{1}(\cdot)
and
f_{2}(\cdot)
be the PDFs of two absolutely continuous random variables, say
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, respectively. Then, the Kullback-Leibler divergence between
f_{1}(\cdot)
and
f_{2}(\cdot)
is given by (see
Kullback and Leibler 1951
)

\displaystyle KL(f_{1},f_{2})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{1}(x)\log\left(\frac{f_{1}(x)}{f_{2}(x)}\right)dx.

(2.6)

Note that the Kullback-Leibler divergence is not symmetric, that is, in general,
KL(f_{1},f_{2})\neq KL(f_{2},f_{1})
. Further, it can be established that Kullback-Leibler divergence takes non-negative values, and that it could it may be infinity.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose
X
is a random variable with PDF
f(\cdot)
. Further, let the CT random variable
X_{CT}
have PDF
f_{X_{CT}}(\cdot)
. Then,

(a)

KL(f,f_{X_{CT}})=KL(f_{U},f_{U_{CT}}),

(b)

KL(f_{X_{CT}},f)=KL(f_{U_{CT}},f_{U}),

where uniform and CT uniform random variables, denoted by
U
and
U_{CT}
in the interval
(0,1)
, have PDFs
f_{U}(\cdot)
and
f_{U_{CT}}(\cdot)
, respectively.

Proof.

(a)
The Kullback-Leibler divergence between
f(\cdot)
and
f_{X_{CT}}(\cdot)
is given by

\displaystyle KL(f,f_{X_{CT}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\log\bigg(\dfrac{f(x)}{f_{X_{CT}}}\bigg)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\log\bigg(\dfrac{f(x)}{f(x)\big\{\lambda_{1}+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})F(x)+3(1-\lambda_{2})F^{2}(x)\big\}}\bigg)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{1}\log\big(\lambda_{1}+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})u+3(1-\lambda_{2})u^{2}\big)du~\big(\mbox{using}~u=F(x)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle KL(f_{U},f_{U_{CT}}).

(b)
The second part can be proved similarly to the first part, and is therefore omitted.
∎

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
and
X_{3}
are i.i.d. as
X
with PDF
f(\cdot)
. Further, let
f_{2:3}(\cdot)
be the PDF of
X_{2:3}
. Then,

(a)

KL(f_{2:3},f_{X_{CT}})=KL(f_{U_{2:3}},f_{U_{CT}}),

(b)

KL(f_{2:3},f_{\max})=KL(f_{U_{2:3}},f_{W}),

where
f_{W}(\cdot)
is the PDF of
W\sim Beta(3,1)
,

U_{CT}
is as defined in Theorem
2.2
, and
f_{U_{2:3}}(\cdot)
is the PDF of
U_{2:3}
.

Proof.

(a)
The Kullback-Leibler divergence between
f_{2:3}(\cdot)
and
f_{X_{CT}}(\cdot)
is given by

\displaystyle KL(f_{2:3},f_{X_{CT}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{2:3}(x)\log\bigg(\dfrac{f_{2:3}(x)}{f_{X_{CT}}(x)}\bigg)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{2:3}(x)\log\bigg(\dfrac{6f(x)\{F(x)-F^{2}(x)\}}{f(x)\big\{\lambda_{1}+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})F(x)+3(1-\lambda_{2})F^{2}(x)\big\}}\bigg)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}6(u-u^{2})\log\bigg(\dfrac{6(u-u^{2})}{\lambda_{1}+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})u+(1-\lambda_{2})u^{2}}\bigg)du~\big(\mbox{using}~u=F(x)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle KL(f_{U_{2:3}},f_{U_{CT}}).

(b)
The Kullback-Leibler divergence between
f_{2:3}(\cdot)
and
f_{\max}(\cdot)
is

\displaystyle KL(f_{2:3},f_{\max})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{2:3}(x)\log\bigg(\dfrac{f_{2:3}(x)}{f_{\max}(x)}\bigg)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}6f(x)\{F(x)-F^{2}(x)\}\log\bigg(\dfrac{6f(x)\{F(x)-F^{2}(x)\}}{3f(x)F^{2}(x)}\bigg)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}6(u-u^{2})\log\bigg(\dfrac{6(u-u^{2})}{3u^{2}}\bigg)du~~\big(\mbox{using}~u=F(x)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle KL(f_{U_{2:3}},f_{W}).

Thus, the proof of the theorem gets completed.
∎

Theorem 2.4
.

Suppose
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
, and
X_{3}
are i.i.d. as
X
with PDF
f(\cdot)
. Then,

(a)

KL(f_{X_{CT}},f_{\max})=KL(f_{U_{CT}},f_{W}),

(b)

KL(f_{\max},f_{X_{CT}})=KL(f_{W},f_{U_{CT}}),

(c)

KL(f_{\max},f)=KL(f_{W},f_{U}),

where
f_{U_{CT}}(\cdot)
and
f_{W}(\cdot)
as given in Theorems
2.2
and Theorem
2.3
, respectively.

Proof.

(a)
We have

\displaystyle KL(f_{X_{CT}},f_{\max})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{X_{CT}}(x)\log\bigg(\dfrac{f_{X_{CT}}(x)}{f_{\max}(x)}\bigg)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\big\{\lambda_{1}+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})F(x)+3(1-\lambda_{2})F^{2}(x)\big\}

\displaystyle\times\log\bigg(\dfrac{f_{X_{CT}}(x)}{3f(x)F^{2}(x)}\bigg)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\big\{\lambda_{1}+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})u+3(1-\lambda_{2})u^{2}\big\}

Recall that the KL divergence is non-symmetric. However, one may get symmetric divergence based on KL divergence, known as Jeffreys’ divergence. The Jeffreys’ divergence between two density functions

(2.7)

Note that the Kullback-Leibler divergence is not symmetric, but Jeffreys’ divergence is symmetric, that is,

\displaystyle J_{d}(f_{1},f_{2})=J_{d}(f_{2},f_{1}).

Remark 2.3

\displaystyle J_{d}(f_{X_{CT}},f_{\max})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J_{d}(f_{U_{CT}},f_{W})=KL(f_{W},f_{U_{CT}})+KL(f_{U_{CT}},f_{W}),

\displaystyle J_{d}(f_{X_{CT}},f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J_{d}(f_{U_{CT}},f_{U})=KL(f_{U},f_{U_{CT}})+KL(f_{U_{CT}},f_{U}).

2.3
Cubic transmuted Shannon entropy

We now propose an extension of the Shannon entropy of CT distribution as well as JS divergence, and call it as CT Shannon entropy. The definition of the CT Shannon entropy is given below. Here, we need the PDF of
X_{CT}
, which is given by

\displaystyle f_{X_{CT}}(x)=\lambda_{1}f(x)+\frac{1}{3}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})f_{2:3}(x)+\frac{1}{3}(3-\lambda_{2}-2\lambda_{1})f_{\max}(x),

(2.8)

where
\lambda_{1}\in[0,1]
and
\lambda_{2}\in[-1,1)
. The CDF of
X_{CT}
is denoted by
F_{X_{CT}}(x)
, which can be easily obtained from (
2.8
). Equation (
2.8
) can be obtained from (
1.11
) by using
F(\cdot),

F_{2:3}(\cdot)
and
F_{\max}(\cdot)
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a random variable with PDF
f(\cdot)
and
X_{CT}
be a cubic transmuted random variable with PDF
f_{X_{CT}}(\cdot)
. Then, the CT Shannon entropy between the PDF
f_{X_{CT}}(\cdot)
in (
2.8
) and the PDFs of its components
f(\cdot)
,
f_{2:3}(\cdot)
and
f_{\max}(\cdot)
is defined as

CTS(f,f_{2:3},f_{\max};\Lambda)=H(f_{X_{CT}})-\lambda_{1}H(f)-\frac{1}{3}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})H(f_{2:3})-\frac{1}{3}(3-\lambda_{2}-2\lambda_{1})H(f_{\max}),

(2.9)

where
\Lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
,
\lambda_{1}\in[0,1],
and
\lambda_{2}\in[-1,1)
.

Now, we establish an important theorem regarding CT Shannon entropy which shows that
CTS(f,f_{2:3},f_{\max};\Lambda)
, defined in Definition
2.1
, can be expressed in terms of the Kullback-Leibler divergence measures between
f_{\max}(\cdot)
and
f(\cdot),

f_{2:3}(\cdot)
and
f_{\max}(\cdot)
, and
f_{X_{CT}}(\cdot)
and
f(\cdot)
.

Theorem 2.5
.

The CT Shannon entropy can be expressed as

\displaystyle CTS(f,f_{2:3},f_{\max};\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\lambda_{2})KL(f_{\max},f)+\frac{1}{3}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})KL(f_{2:3},f_{\max})

(2.10)

\displaystyle-KL(f_{X_{CT}},f)+C^{*}

where
\Lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
and
C^{*}=\big\{\log(3)-1\big\}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})
.

Proof.

Substituting
H(f_{X_{CT}})
from Theorem
2.1
into (
2.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle CTS(f,f_{2:3},f_{\max};\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})H(f_{\max})-\frac{1}{3}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})H(f_{2:3})+H(f_{U_{CT}})

(2.11)

\displaystyle-(1-\lambda_{2})H(f_{W})+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})H^{F}(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\left(2H(f_{\max})+H(f_{2:3})-6H^{F}(f)\right)+H(f_{U_{CT}})

\displaystyle-(1-\lambda_{2})H(f_{W})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{3}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\big\{KL(f_{2:3},f_{\max})+3(\log 3-1)\big\}+H(f_{U_{CT}})

\displaystyle-(1-\lambda_{2})H(f_{W}).

Now, using the following relations (see Theorem
2.2
and Theorem
2.4
)

KL(f_{X_{CT}},f)=KL(f_{U_{CT}},f_{U})=-H(f_{U_{CT}})~\mbox{and}~KL(f_{\max},f)=KL(f_{W},f_{U})=-H(f_{W})

in (
2.11
), the desired result follows. Hence, the theorem.
∎

Further, bounds of CT Shannon entropy can be found easily in terms of the Kullback-Leibler divergence measures between various components, as follows:

\displaystyle CTS(f,f_{2:3},f_{\max};\Lambda)\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\geq(1-\lambda_{2})KL(f_{\max},f)+\frac{1}{3}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})KL(f_{2:3},f_{\max})\\
-KL(f_{X_{CT}},f),~~~\mbox{if}~\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda_{1},\\
\leq(1-\lambda_{2})KL(f_{\max},f)+\frac{1}{3}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})KL(f_{2:3},f_{\max})\\
-KL(f_{X_{CT}},f),~~~\mbox{if}~\lambda_{2}<\lambda_{1}.\end{array}\right.

A closed-form expression of
CTS(f,f_{2:3},f_{max};\Lambda)
can be easily obtained since

\displaystyle CTS(f,f_{2:3},f_{\max};\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\lambda_{2})KL(f_{W},f_{U})+\frac{1}{3}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})KL(f_{U_{2:3}},f_{W})

(2.17)

\displaystyle-KL(f_{U_{CT}},f_{U})-\big\{\log(3)-1\big\}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}),

where
KL(f_{W},f_{U})
,
KL(f_{U_{2:3}},f_{W})
and
KL(f_{U_{CT}},f_{U})
are as given above. From (
2.17
), it is not hard to notice that the CT Shannon entropy is also free from the underlying distribution.

Remark 2.4
.

f(\cdot)
,

, and

3
Gini information

In this section, we discuss another important information measure, namely, Gini’s mean difference of CT distribution, CT Gini’s mean difference and investigate their connection to energy distances.

3.1
Gini’s mean difference

Let
X
be an absolutely continuous random variable with CDF
F(\cdot)
. Then, the Gini’s mean difference
(GMD)
of
X
is given by

\displaystyle GMD(F)=2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(x)\bar{F}(x)dx,

(3.1)

where
\bar{F}(x)=1-F(x)
is the survival function of
X
.
We now show that Gini’s mean difference for the CT distribution can be written in terms of GMDs of its components.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X
and
X_{CT}
be a continuous random variable and its CT random variable, respectively. Then, the Gini’s mean difference of
F_{X_{CT}}(\cdot)
in (
1.11
) is given by

\displaystyle GMD(F_{X_{CT}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}_{1}GMD(F)+(1-\lambda_{2})^{2}GMD(F_{\max})

(3.2)

\displaystyle+2\int_{0}^{1}\bigg\{\dfrac{Au+Bu^{2}+Cu^{3}+Du^{4}+Eu^{5}}{f(F^{-1}(u))}\bigg\}du,

where
A=\lambda_{1}(1-\lambda_{1})
,
B=\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}
,
C=\lambda_{2}(1-\lambda_{2})-2\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})
,
D=-\big\{(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})^{2}+2\lambda_{1}(1-\lambda_{2})\big\}
and
E=-2(1-\lambda_{2})(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})
.

Proof.

From (
3.1
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}GMD(F_{X_{CT}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F_{X_{CT}}(x)\bar{F}_{X_{CT}}(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(x)\bar{F}(x)dx+(1-\lambda_{2})^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F^{3}(x)\bar{F}^{3}(x)dx

\displaystyle+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big\{AF(x)+BF^{2}(x)+CF^{3}(x)+DF^{4}+EF^{5}(x)\big\}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}_{1}GMD(F)+\frac{1}{2}(1-\lambda_{2})^{2}GMD(F_{\max})

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}\bigg\{\dfrac{Au+Bu^{2}+Cu^{3}+Du^{4}+Eu^{5}}{f(F^{-1}(u))}\bigg\}du,~~\big(using~u=F(x)\big)

which completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

In the following, we present upper and lower bounds for the GMD as

\displaystyle GMD(F_{X_{CT}})\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\geq\lambda^{2}_{1}GMD(F)+(1-\lambda_{2})^{2}GMD(F_{\max}),&\mbox{if}~R^{*}\geq 0\\
\leq\lambda^{2}_{1}GMD(F)+(1-\lambda_{2})^{2}GMD(F_{\max}),&\mbox{if}~R^{*}\leq 0,\end{array}\right.

where
R^{*}=2\int_{0}^{1}\{(Au+Bu^{2}+Cu^{3}+Du^{4}+Eu^{5})/f(F^{-1}(u))\}du
, with
A
,
B
,
C
,
D
, and
E
being as in Theorem
3.1
.

Substituting
\lambda_{1}=1+\lambda
and
\lambda_{2}=1
in
R^{*}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{R}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\lambda^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F^{2}(x)\bar{F}^{2}(x)dx-\lambda(1+\lambda)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u(1-u)(1+2u)}{f(F^{-1}(u))}du.

(3.6)

Remark 3.1

3.2

\displaystyle+(1-\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})~CD(F_{2:3},F_{X_{CT}}),

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}),~~\eta_{1}=\frac{1}{1-2\lambda_{1}}(4\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{2}-3\lambda^{2}_{1}),

\displaystyle\eta_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{9(2\lambda_{2}-3)}\big\{(26\lambda_{2}-20\lambda_{1}-6\lambda^{2}_{2})-3\eta_{1}(4\lambda_{2}-8)\big\}.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big\{(4\lambda_{1}-2\lambda^{2}_{1}-2\lambda)F^{2}(x)+\frac{4}{3}(3\lambda^{2}_{1}-3\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})F^{3}(x)

\displaystyle+(12\lambda_{2}-16\lambda_{1}+8\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}-2\lambda^{2}_{1}-2\lambda^{2}_{2})F^{4}(x)

\displaystyle+4(\lambda^{2}_{2}-\lambda_{2}\lambda_{1}+5\lambda_{1}-5\lambda_{2})F^{5}(x)+\frac{2}{3}(\lambda_{2}-10\lambda_{1}-3\lambda^{2}_{2})F^{6}(x)\big\}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{1}~CD(F,F_{X_{CT}})+\eta_{2}~CD(F_{\max},F_{X_{CT}})

\displaystyle+(1-\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})~CD(F_{2:3},F_{X_{CT}}),

where the last equality is obtained using (
3.11
). Thus, the proof gets completed.
∎

In the following remark, we present CT Gini’s mean difference for some special case of the CT distributions.

Remark 3.2
.

For
\lambda_{1}=1+\lambda
and
\lambda_{2}=1-\lambda
, from Theorem
3.2
, we get

\displaystyle CTG(F,F_{2:3},F_{\max};\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta^{*}_{1}CD(F,F^{*}_{X_{CT}})+\eta^{*}_{2}CD(F_{\max},F^{*}_{X_{CT}})

\displaystyle+(1-\eta^{*}_{1}-\eta^{*}_{2})CD(F_{2:3},F^{*}_{X_{CT}})

as the CT Gini’s mean difference of the one parameter CT distribution,
where
\eta^{*}_{1}=\frac{1}{1+2\lambda}(3\lambda^{2}+2)
and
\eta^{*}_{2}=\frac{1}{9(1+2\lambda)}\{(34\lambda+6\lambda^{2})+12\eta^{*}_{1}(1+\lambda)\}
.

4
Chi-square divergence

In this section, we derive Chi-square
(\chi^{2})
divergence between the CT distribution with CDF
F_{X_{CT}}(\cdot)
given in (
1.11
) and its components’ CDFs
F(\cdot)
,
F_{2:3}(\cdot)
, and
F_{max}(\cdot)
.
Suppose
X
and
Y
are two random variables with PDFs
f_{1}(\cdot)
and
f_{2}(\cdot)
, respectively. Then,
\chi^{2}
divergence (see
Nielsen and Nock 2013
) between
f_{1}(\cdot)
and
f_{2}(\cdot)
is given by

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(f_{1},f_{2})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\big\{f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(x)\big\}^{2}}{f_{2}(x)}dx.

(4.1)

Now, we present some important properties of
\chi^{2}
divergence in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
and
X_{3}
are i.i.d. as
X
with PDF
f(\cdot)
. Then, the Chi-square divergence between
f_{X_{CT}}(\cdot)
with
f(\cdot)
and
f_{\max}(\cdot)
are given by

(a)

\chi^{2}_{CT}(f_{X_{CT}},f)=\chi^{2}_{CT}(f_{U_{CT}},f_{U}),

(b)

\chi^{2}_{CT}(f,f_{X_{CT}})=\chi^{2}_{CT}(f_{U},f_{U_{CT}}),

(c)

\chi^{2}_{CT}(f_{\max},f_{X_{CT}})=\chi^{2}_{CT}(f_{W},f_{U_{CT}}).

Proof.

(a)
We have

\displaystyle\chi^{2}_{CT}(f_{X_{CT}},f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{\big\{f_{X_{CT}}(x)-f(x)\big\}^{2}}{f(x)}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{\big[f(x)\big\{\lambda_{1}+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})F(x)+3(1-\lambda_{2})F^{2}(x)\big\}-f(x)\big]^{2}}{f(x)}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\big[\big\{\lambda_{1}+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})u+3(1-\lambda_{2})u^{2}\big\}-1\big]^{2}du~~\big(\mbox{using u=F(x)}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi^{2}_{CT}(f_{U_{CT}},f_{U}).

parts
(b)
and
(c)
can be proved similarly.
∎

Closed-form expressions of the Chi-square divergences
\chi^{2}_{CT}(f_{U_{CT}},f_{U})
,
\chi^{2}_{CT}(f_{U},f_{U_{CT}}),
and
\chi^{2}_{CT}(f_{W},f_{U_{CT}})
are obtained below. The detailed derivations are omitted here for conciseness:

\displaystyle(a)~\chi^{2}_{CT}(f_{U_{CT}},f_{U})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{15}(12-15\lambda_{1}-9\lambda_{2}+5\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}+5\lambda^{2}_{1}+2\lambda^{2}_{2}),

\displaystyle(b)~\chi^{2}_{CT}(f_{U},f_{U_{CT}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-1+\frac{1}{r}\tanh^{-1}(\lambda),

\displaystyle(c)~\chi^{2}_{CT}(f_{W},f_{U_{CT}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-1+\frac{1}{3(1-\lambda_{2})^{3}}(4\lambda^{2}_{1}+10\lambda^{2}_{2}-8\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}-9\lambda_{2}+3)-9\sigma(p,q),

where

\lambda=\frac{r}{\lambda_{2}}
and

\sigma(p,q)=\frac{1}{2r}\Big\{p^{4}\log\big(\frac{p}{p-1}\big)-q^{4}\log\big(\frac{q}{q-1}\big)\Big\}.

Remark 4.1
.

Making use of Theorem
4.1
and the expressions of
\chi^{2}_{CT}(f_{U_{CT}},f_{U})
,
\chi^{2}_{CT}(f_{U},f_{U_{CT}}),
and
\chi^{2}_{CT}(f_{W},f_{U_{CT}})
, we observe that Chi-square divergence measures are free of the underlying distribution function
F(\cdot)
.

The Symmetric
\chi^{2}
-divergence between two density functions
f_{1}(\cdot)
and
f_{2}(\cdot)
is defined as (see
Dragomir et al. 2000
)

\displaystyle\Psi(f_{1},f_{2})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\big\{f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(x)\big\}^{2}\big\{f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)\big\}}{f_{1}(x)f_{2}(x)}dx.

(4.2)

Note that, while the Chi-square divergence is non-symmetric, but (
4.2
) is symmetric, that is,

\Psi(f_{1},f_{2})=\Psi(f_{2},f_{1}).

In the following remark, we show that the symmetric Chi-square divergence between
f_{X_{CT}}(\cdot)
and
f(\cdot)
is equal to that between
f_{U_{CT}}(\cdot)
and
f_{U}(\cdot)
.

Remark 4.2
.

We have

\Psi(f_{X_{CT}},f)=\chi^{2}(f_{U_{CT}},f_{U})+\chi^{2}(f_{U},f_{U_{CT}})=\Psi(f_{U_{CT}},f_{U}).

It is clear that the symmetric Chi-square divergence between
f_{X_{CT}}(\cdot)
and
f(\cdot)
is free from the underlying distribution
F(\cdot)
.

5
Fisher information

In this section, we study Fisher information for the CT distribution with CDF in (
1.11
) and for the one-parameter CT distribution with CDF in (
1.13
).

5.1
Fisher information matrix of CT distribution for
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}

Below, we present the Fisher information matrix for the cubic transmuted distribution.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose
X_{CT}
is a cubic transmuted random variable with PDF
f_{CT}(\cdot)
as in (
1.12
). Then, the Fisher information matrix is

I(\Lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}E(\tau\rho_{1})&E(\tau\rho_{2})\\
E(\tau\rho_{2})&E(\tau\rho_{3})\end{bmatrix},

where
\tau=\frac{f^{2}(x)}{f^{2}_{X_{CT}}}
,
\rho_{1}\big\{1-2F(x)\big\}^{2}
,
\rho_{2}=\big\{2F(x)-7F^{2}(x)+6F^{3}(x)\big\}
,
\rho_{3}=\big\{2F(x)-3F^{2}(x^{2})\big\}^{2}
, and
\Lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
.

Proof.

Using log-likelihood function

l(\Lambda)=\log\Big(f(x)\big\{\lambda_{1}+2(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})F(x)+3(1-\lambda_{2})F^{2}(x)\big\}\Big),

the Fisher information matrix is easily obtained.
∎

We now present an example to illustrate the computation of the Fisher information matrix for the CT uniform distribution.

Example 5.1
.

Let the baseline distribution for the CT distribution be uniform in
(0,1)
. Then, the Fisher information matrix of the CT uniform distribution is given by

\displaystyle I(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}E(\tau\rho_{1})&E(\tau\rho_{2})\\
E(\tau\rho_{2})&E(\tau\rho_{3})\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{r}\tanh^{-1}(\lambda)+\omega_{1}+\dfrac{4}{3(1-\lambda_{2})}&-\omega_{2}-\dfrac{4(1-\lambda_{1})}{3(1-\lambda_{2})^{2}}\\
-\omega_{2}-\dfrac{4(1-\lambda_{1})}{3(1-\lambda_{2})^{2}}&\frac{1}{3(1-\lambda_{2})^{3}}(4\lambda^{2}_{1}-8\lambda_{1}+3\lambda_{2}+1)-\omega_{3}\end{bmatrix},

where
r,~p,~q
are as given in Remark
2.2
,
\lambda=r/\lambda_{2},
and

\displaystyle\omega_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{r}\bigg\{(p-p^{2})\log\Big(\frac{p}{p-1}\Big)-(q-q^{2})\log\Big(\frac{q}{q-1}\Big)\bigg\},

\displaystyle\omega_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2r}\bigg\{(2p-7p^{2}+6p^{3})\log\Big(\frac{p}{p-1}\Big)-(2q-7q^{2}+6q^{3})\log\Big(\frac{q}{q-1}\Big)\bigg\},

\displaystyle\omega_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2r}\bigg\{(4p^{2}-12p^{3}+9p^{4})\log\Big(\frac{p}{p-1}\Big)-(4q^{2}-12q^{3}+9q^{4})\log\Big(\frac{q}{q-1}\Big)\bigg\}.

For
\lambda_{1}=1+\lambda
and
\lambda_{2}=1-\lambda
, the CT distribution with CDF
F_{X_{CT}}(\cdot)
in (
1.11
) reduces to the one-parameter CT distribution with CDF
F^{*}_{X_{CT}}(\cdot)
in (
1.13
), which discussed by
AL-Kadim and Mohammed 2017
. In the next subsection, we discuss Fisher information for this special case.

5.2
Fisher information for one-parameter CT distribution

The Fisher information of a continuous random variable
X
with PDF
f(x;\theta)
, where
\theta
is a parameter, is given by (see
Fisher 1929
)

\displaystyle\textit{I}(\theta)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigg[\frac{\partial\log\big(f(x;\theta)\big)}{\partial\theta}\bigg]^{2}f(x;\theta)dx.

We now establish that the Fisher information for the one-parameter CT distribution can be represented in terms of Chi-square divergence.

Theorem 5.2
.

The Fisher information for the one-parameter CT distribution with PDF
f^{*}_{X_{TC}}(\cdot)
in (
1.14
) is given by

\displaystyle\textit{I}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\chi^{2}(f,f^{*}_{X_{TC}}).

Proof.

Using the definition of Fisher information for the parameter
\lambda
, we have

\displaystyle\textit{I}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\bigg[\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\log\big(f^{*}_{X_{CT}}(X)\big)\bigg]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\bigg[\frac{f(X)\big\{1-4F(X)+3F^{2}(X)\big\}}{f^{*}_{X_{CT}}(X)}\bigg]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigg[\frac{f(x)\big\{1-4F(x)+3F^{2}(x)\big\}}{f^{*}_{X_{CT}}(x)}\bigg]^{2}f^{*}_{X_{CT}}(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{\big\{f(x)-f^{*}_{X_{CT}}(x)\big\}^{2}}{f^{*}_{X_{CT}}(x)}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\chi^{2}(f,f^{*}_{X_{CT}}).

Hence, the desired result.
∎

Closed-form expression of
\textit{I}(\lambda)
can be easily obtained as

\displaystyle\textit{I}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\chi^{2}(f_{U},f^{*}_{U_{TC}})=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\bigg\{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{r^{*}}\tanh^{-1}\bigg(\frac{r^{*}}{1-\lambda}\bigg)+\pi(p^{*})-\pi(q^{*})-\frac{\lambda}{3}(13\lambda+10)\bigg\},

where
r^{*}=\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-3\lambda}
,
p^{*}=\frac{r^{*}-2\lambda}{3\lambda}
,
q^{*}=\frac{r^{*}+2\lambda}{3\lambda}
, and
\pi(x)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2r^{*}}(9x^{4}-24x^{3}+22x^{2}-8x)\log\big(\frac{x}{x-1}\big)
.

Remark 5.1
.

We observe from Theorem
4.1
that the Fisher information for the one-parameter CT distribution is free from the underlying distribution
F^{*}(\cdot)
and it is non-symmetric, but the Fisher information of transmuted distribution obtained by
Kharazmi and Balakrishnan 2021
is symmetric.

7
Conclusions

In this paper, we have studied various informational properties of the family of cubic transmuted distributions. The Shannon entropy and Gini’s mean difference of the CT distributions are derived. Further, we have proposed CT Shannon entropy and CT Gini’s mean difference. Connection between CT Shannon entropy and Kullback-Leibler divergence and that between CT Gini’s mean difference and energy distance have also been established. Chi-square divergence between the CT distribution and the PDFs of its components have been obtained. Fisher information matrix is obtained for the CT distribution, and we also derive Fisher information for the one-parameter cubic transmuted distribution. Some simulation results have also been presented to demonstrate the usefulness of some of the informational measures studied here in the context of inference.
