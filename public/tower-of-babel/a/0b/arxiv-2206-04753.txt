Abstract
. One-parameter semigroups of holomorphic functions appear naturally in various applications of Complex Analysis, and in particular, in the theory of (temporally) homogeneous Markov processes. A suitable analogue of one-parameter semigroups in the inhomogeneous setting is the notion of a (reverse) evolution family. In this paper we study evolution families formed by Bernstein functions, which play the role of Laplace exponents for inhomogeneous continuous-state branching processes. In particular, we characterize all Herglotz vector fields that generate such evolution families and give a complex-analytic proof of a qualitative description equivalent to Silverstein’s representation formula for the infinitesimal generators of one-parameter semigroups of Bernstein functions. We also establish several sufficient conditions for families of holomorphic self-maps, satisfying the algebraic part in the definition of an evolution family, to be absolutely continuous and hence to be described as solutions to the generalized Loewner – Kufarev differential equation. Most of these results are then applied in the sequel paper [ 32 ] to study continuous-state branching processes.

1.
Introduction

Particular interest to look into families of Bernstein functions from the viewpoint of Loewner Theory comes from Probability Theory.
In general, complex-analytic tools are known to play an important role in the study of Markov branching processes, see e.g.
[
3
,
28
,
33
]
. A very classical example is the Galton – Watson process, which is a (temporally) homogeneous Markov chain with the one-step transition probabilities encoded in the so-called generating (holomorphic) function
f\colon{\mathbb{D}}\to{\mathbb{D}}:=\{{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\colon}|z|<1\}
as its Taylor coefficients at
z=0
. Evolution of the stochastic process corresponds to a (deterministic) dynamics in a complex domain: the generating function for the
n
-step transition probabilities coincides with the
n
-th iterate of
f
defined as usual by

f^{\circ n}:=\underbrace{\,f\circ\ldots\circ f}_{n\text{~times}}\colon{\mathbb{D}}\to{\mathbb{D}}.

In case of a homogeneous branching process with continuous time, iterates are replaced by a continuous one-parameter semigroup of holomorphic self-maps, see Section
2.1
for the definition and basic properties.

In a similar manner, inhomogeneous branching processes lead to non-autonomous holomorphic dynamical systems. In this setting the role of one-parameter semigroups is played by the so-called (reverse) evolution families, see Section
2.2
.

Independently from applications to stochastic processes, one-parameter semigroups and iteration of holomorphic functions have been a subject of deep studies in Complex Analysis since early 1900s. Evolution families, in turn, constitute one of the cornerstone concepts in Loewner Theory, which originated from Loewner’s seminal paper
[
40
]
of 1923 and since then has been serving as a powerful tool in the theory of conformal mappings, see e.g.
[
2
,
11
]
for a detailed survey.

Applications of Loewner Theory to branching processes (with discrete states) were studied in detail by Goryainov [ – ]. It is worth mentioning that similar relationships between stochastic processes and evolution families (or one-parameter semigroups in the homogeneous case) have been recently discovered in Non-Commutative Probability, see e.g.
[
4
,
20
,
49
,
21
]
.

This paper is motivated by yet another interesting case, in which the transition probabilities can be encoded in an evolution family formed by holomorphic self-maps of the right half-plane. Continuous-state (Markov) branching processes introduced in 1958 by Jiřina
[
35
]
, see also
[
30
]
, have been attracting increasing interest in the last decades, see e.g.
[
19
]
or
[
43
]
and references therein. Because of the branching property, the transition probability measures
k_{s,t}(x,\cdot)
in such a process
(X_{t})
are infinitely divisible and, as a result, Bernstein functions come into play via the Laplace transform:

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\zeta X_{t}}\big|X_{s}=x\big]~=\,\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\zeta y}\,k_{s,t}(x,\mathrm{d}y)~=~\exp(-xv_{s,t}(\zeta)),\quad{x,\,\zeta\geqslant 0},

where
v_{s,t}\colon[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
is a Bernstein function, which is uniquely defined by the transition kernel
k_{s,t}
and which we refer to as
the Laplace exponent
of the process.
In turn, the Chapman – Kolmogorov equation leads to a sort of semigroup property
v_{s,t}\circ v_{t,u}=v_{s,u}
for any
s,t,u
with
0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant u
; essentially, this means that the Laplace exponents form a reverse evolution family. For more details we refer the reader to our sequel paper
[
32
]
.

While homogeneous continuous-state branching processes are well understood, see e.g.
[
38
, Chapter 10]
, a systematic study of the inhomogeneous case has been launched only recently. In particular, in
[
19
]
the authors construct inhomogeneous continuous-state branching processes starting from an integral evolution equation for the Laplace exponents
v_{s,t}
regarded as self-maps of
(0,+\infty)
.

A well-known remarkable fact is that every Bernstein function
f\not\equiv 0
extends to a holomorphic self-map of the right half-plane
{\mathbb{H}}:={\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\colon\Re z>0\}}
. Another important property is that the composition of two Bernstein functions is again a Bernstein function. In other words, the holomorphic extensions of Bernstein functions
f\not\equiv 0
form a subsemigroup
\mathfrak{BF}
in the topological semigroup
{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
of all holomorphic self-maps of
{\mathbb{H}}
endowed with the operation of composition and the usual topology of locally uniform convergence. Moreover,
\mathfrak{BF}
is topologically closed in
{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
. See Section
2.4
for more details.

The purpose of this paper is to develop complex-analytic methods based on the modern Loewner Theory which would be useful, in particular, for studying inhomogeneous continuous-state branching processes. A part of our results are then applied in the sequel paper
[
32
]
. Other results are aimed to future investigation of branching processes and/or represent some general interest in the frames of Loewner Theory and its applications.

1.1.
Organization of the paper and summary of main results

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section, Section
2
, we present necessary background on one-parameter semigroups and (reverse) evolution families and on boundary fixed points of holomorphic self-maps; the last subsection is devoted to basic properties of Bernstein functions (regarded as holomorphic functions in the right-half plane
{\mathbb{H}}
).

In Section
3
we establish our main results.
We start by proving Theorems
1
and
2
in Sections
3.1
and
3.2
, respectively, which concern infinitesimal description of an arbitrary topologically closed (sub)semigroup
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
in
{\sf Hol}(D,D)
, where
{D\subsetneq{\mathbb{C}}}
is a simply connected domain.
Theorem
1
is a general version of a result known for several special cases; it states that the infinitesimal generators associated to one-parameter semigroups in
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
form a topologically closed convex cone. Theorem
2
deals with the evolution families. We prove that an evolution family
(w_{s,t})
generated by a Herglotz vector field
\phi
is contained in
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
if and only if for a.e.​ “frozen” instant
t
,
\phi(\cdot,t)
is an infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter semigroup in
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
.
Both theorems apply to the case of the semigroup
\mathfrak{BF}\subset{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
consisting of all Bernstein functions different from the identical zero.

Theorem
2
reduces the study Herglotz vector fields generating evolution families of Bernstein functions to the study of infinitesimal generators associated to one-parameter semigroups in
\mathfrak{BF}
. Such generators, which we refer to as
Bernstein generators
, appear naturally in Stochastic Processes. A representation formula for Bernstein generators was found long time ago by Silverstein
[
52
]
. Using complex-analytic tools, in particular, the theory of one-parameter semigroups, in Section
3.3
we obtain a qualitative characterization of Bernstein generators, see Theorem
3
. As mentioned after the statement of the theorem, it is easily seen to be equivalent to Silverstein’s representation. Moreover, as corollaries, we obtain analogous representation of Bernstein generators for the cases when the corresponding one-parameter semigroup has a boundary regular fixed point at
0
or at
\infty
.

Section
3.4
is devoted to finding sufficient conditions for families satisfying the first two (algebraic) conditions in the definitions of reverse and usual evolution families, see Section
2.2
, to be absolutely continuous, i.e. to admit associated Herglotz vector fields and hence to be described with the help of (a general version of) the Loewner – Kufarev equations (
2.3
) – (
2.6
).
Theorems
4
and
5
deal with general (usual and reverse) evolution families having a boundary Denjoy – Wolff point, while Theorem
6
and its Corollary
3.17
concern specifically families of Bernstein functions and give sufficient conditions for absolute continuity in terms of the local behaviour at
0
. Motivated by the latter result, in the last section, Section
3.5
, we make sure that for absolutely continuous evolution families in
\mathfrak{BF}
, the well-known formulas for the derivatives of the first and the second order w.r.t. the initial condition at an internal fixed point can be extended to the boundary regular fixed point at
0
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
One-parameter semigroups of holomorphic self-maps

We start by introducing some general notation and terminology. For an arbitrary domain
D\subset{\mathbb{C}}
we denote by
{\sf Hol}(D,{\mathbb{C}})
the set of all holomorphic functions in
D
. We endow
{\sf Hol}(D,{\mathbb{C}})
with the open-compact topology. It is easy to see that a sequence
(f_{n})
converges in
{{\sf Hol}(D,{\mathbb{C}})}
if and only if it converges (to the same limit function) locally uniformly in
D
. Furthermore, for a set
E\subset{\mathbb{C}}
we denote by
{\sf Hol}(D,E)
the topological subspace of
{\sf Hol}(D,{\mathbb{C}})
formed by all
f\in{\sf Hol}(D,{\mathbb{C}})
such that
f(D)\subset E
.

Definition 2.1
.

By a
semigroup of holomorphic self-maps
of a domain
{D\subset{\mathbb{C}}}
we mean a set
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt\subset{\sf Hol}(D,D)
containing the identity map
\mathop{\mathrm{id}}_{D}
and such that
f\circ g\in\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
for any
f,g\in\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
.
We say that such a semigroup
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
is
topologically closed
if
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
is (relatively) closed in
{\sf Hol}(D,D)
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
be a semigroup of holomorphic self-maps of some domain
{D\subset{\mathbb{C}}}
.
A
one-parameter semigroup
in
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
is a continuous semigroup homomorphism
{[0,+\infty)\ni t\mapsto v_{t}\in\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt}
from the semigroup
{\big([0,+\infty),+\big)}
with the Euclidian topology to the semigroup
(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt,\circ)
endowed with the topology inherited from
{\sf Hol}(D,D)
.

Equivalently,
(v_{t})\subset\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
is a one-parameter semigroup if and only if it satisfies the following three conditions: (i)
v_{0}=\mathop{\mathrm{id}}_{D}
; (ii)
v_{s}\circ v_{t}=v_{s+t}
for any
s,t\geqslant 0
; (iii)
v_{t}\to\mathop{\mathrm{id}}_{D}
in
{\sf Hol}(D,D)
as
t\to 0^{+}
. Thanks to Montel’s normality criterion, see e.g.
[
22
, § II.7, Theorem 1]
, if
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus D
contains at least two distinct points, then the continuity condition (iii) is equivalent to the pointwise convergence:
v_{t}(z)\to z
as
t\to 0^{+}
for each
z\in D
.

Remark 2.3
.

It is known, see e.g.
[
9
, §8.4]
, that in one complex variable, the theory of one-parameter semigroups of holomorphic self-maps is non-trivial only in the following two cases:

(a)

D
is conformally equivalent to the unit disk
{{\mathbb{D}}:=\{z\colon|z|<1\}}
, i.e.
D
is simply connected and different from the whole plane
{\mathbb{C}}
;
or

(b)

D
is conformally equivalent to
{\mathbb{D}}^{*}:={\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\}
.

Moreover, case (b) reduces easily to case (a) because any one-parameter semigroup
(\phi_{t})
of holomorphic self-maps of
{\mathbb{D}}^{*}
extends holomorphically to
{z=0}
by setting
\phi_{t}(0)=0
for all
t\geqslant 0
; see
[
9
, Theorem 8.4.7 on p. 224]
.

Most of the theory in the literature is developed for
{D:={\mathbb{D}}}
or
{D:={\mathbb{H}}}
, sometimes for the strip
{D:=\{z\colon 0<\Im z<\pi\}}
. For the sake of notational uniformity, we will keep working with an arbitrary simply connected
{D\subsetneq{\mathbb{C}}}
, unless explicitly stated otherwise. Clearly, most of the results proved for any particular choice of
D
extend, more or less automatically, to the general case with the help of conformal mappings. In particular, it is known
[
5
]
, see also
[
8
]
,
[
9
, Chapter 10]
, or
[
50
, §3.2]
that for any one-parameter semigroup
(v_{t})
in
{\sf Hol}(D,D)
the limit

(2.1)

\phi:=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{id}}_{D}-v_{t}}{t}

exists in
{\sf Hol}(D,{\mathbb{C}})
.
The holomorphic function
\phi
is called the
infinitesimal generator
of
(v_{t})
. The one-parameter semigroup can be recovered from its infinitesimal generator; namely, for each
z\in D
the map
{[0,+\infty)\ni t\mapsto w:=v_{t}(z)}
is the unique solution to

(2.2)

\frac{\mathrm{d}w(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}+\phi(w(t))=0,\quad w(0)=z.

Conversely, if
\phi\in{\sf Hol}(D,{\mathbb{C}})
is a semicomplete vector field in
D
, i.e. if for any
z\in D
the initial value problem (
2.2
) has a unique solution
w=w_{z}(t)\in D
defined for all
t\geqslant 0
, then the mappings
{v_{t}(z):=w_{z}(t)}
,
{z\in D}
,
{t\geqslant 0}
, form a one-parameter semigroup in
{\sf Hol}(D,D)
, whose infinitesimal generator coincides with
\phi
.

In the literature one can find two different conventions concerning the choice of the sign in formula (
2.1
). Here we follow the convention from
[
50
]
, which is also often adopted in the literature on Probability Theory. The opposite choice of the sign is made in most of the works in Loewner Theory, e.g. in
[
7
,
10
,
15
]
, as well as in many publications on one-parameter semigroups, including
[
9
]
.

In what follows, the set of all infinitesimal generators of one-parameter semigroups in the domain
D
will be denoted by
\mathcal{G}
, or by
\mathcal{G}\big({\sf Hol}(D,D)\big)
when we need to specify the domain
D
explicitly. Furthermore, for a semigroup
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt\subset{\sf Hol}(D,D)
, we will denote by
\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)
the set of all
\phi\in\mathcal{G}
for which the associated one-parameter semigroup
(v^{\phi}_{t})
is contained in
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
.

Remark 2.4
.

Let
{D\subsetneq{\mathbb{C}}}
be a simply connected domain and
f
be a conformal mapping of
{\mathbb{D}}
onto
D
. With the help of the variable change
w=f(\omega)
in equation (
2.2
) we see that
\phi:D\to{\mathbb{C}}
belongs to
\mathcal{G}\big({\sf Hol}(D,D)\big)
if and only if the function
\psi:=(\phi\circ f)/f^{\prime}
belongs to
\mathcal{G}\big({\sf Hol}({\mathbb{D}},{\mathbb{D}})\big)
.

2.2.
Absolutely continuous evolution families

Throughout this section, we fix an interval
{I\subset{\mathbb{R}}}
of the form
{I:=[0,T]}
with
{T\in(0,+\infty)}
or
{I:=[0,T)}
with
{T\in(0,+\infty]}
. Furthermore, by
\Delta(I)
we denote the set
\{(s,t)\colon s,t\in I,\,s\leqslant t\}
. If
{I=[0,+\infty)}
, instead of
\Delta(I)
we will simply write
\Delta
.

Finally,
AC_{\mathrm{loc}}(I)
will stand for the class of all (real- or complex-valued) functions on
I
which are absolutely continuous on each compact subinterval of
I
.

There are two ways to extend the notion of a one-parameter semigroup to the non-autonomous (or, in probabilistic terminology, time-inhomogeneous) case. The following definitions are slight modifications of the definitions given in
[
7
,
15
]
. Most of the results proved in these papers can be easily extended to our setting.

Definition 2.5
.

An
(absolutely continuous) evolution family
in a simply connected domain
{D\subsetneq{\mathbb{C}}}
over the interval
I
is a two-parameter family

{(w_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta(I)}\subset{\sf Hol}(D,D)}

satisfying the following conditions:

EF1:

w_{s,s}=\mathop{\mathrm{id}}_{D}
for any
s\in I
;

EF2:

w_{s,u}=w_{t,u}\circ w_{s,t}
for any
s,t,u\in I
with
{s\leqslant t\leqslant u}
;

EF3:

for each
z\in D
there exists a non-negative function
f_{z}\in AC_{\mathrm{loc}}(I)
such that

|w_{s,u}(z)-w_{s,t}(z)|\leqslant\int_{t}^{u}\!f_{z}(r)\,\mathrm{d}r

for any
s,t,u\in I
with
{s\leqslant t\leqslant u}
.

Definition 2.6
.

An
(absolutely continuous) reverse evolution family
in a simply connected domain
{D\subsetneq{\mathbb{C}}}
over the interval
I
is a two-parameter family
{(v_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta(I)}\subset{\sf Hol}(D,D)}
satisfying the following conditions:

REF1:

v_{s,s}=\mathop{\mathrm{id}}_{D}
for any
s\in I
;

REF2:

v_{s,u}=v_{s,t}\circ v_{t,u}
for any
s,t,u\in I
with
{s\leqslant t\leqslant u}
;

REF3:

for each
z\in D
there exists a non-negative function
f_{z}\in AC_{\mathrm{loc}}(I)
such that

|v_{s,u}(z)-v_{s,t}(z)|\leqslant\int_{t}^{u}\!f_{z}(r)\,\mathrm{d}r

for any
s,t,u\in I
with
{s\leqslant t\leqslant u}
.

If
(w_{s,t})
satisfies Definition
2.5
with condition EF3 replaced by the requirement that the map

{\Delta(I)\ni(s,t)\mapsto w_{s,t}\in{\sf Hol}(D,D)}

is continuous, then it is called a
topological evolution family
. In a similar way,
topological reverse evolution families
can be defined.

Remark 2.7
.

Clearly, if
(v_{t})\subset{\sf Hol}(D,D)
is a one-parameter semigroup, then
(w_{s,t})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}
defined by
w_{s,t}:=v_{t-s}
for all
(s,t)\in\Delta
is an absolutely continuous evolution family and, at the same time, an absolutely continuous reverse evolution family.

Remark 2.8
.

A family
{(v_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta(I)}\subset{\sf Hol}(D,D)}
is a reverse evolution family if and only if for any
S\in I
the formula
w_{s,t}:=v_{S-t,S-s}
,
0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant S
, defines an evolution family
(w_{s,t})
over
{[0,S]}
. The same is true with “reverse evolution family” and “evolution family” interchanged. For
topological
(reverse) evolution families, these assertions follow directly from the definitions, while in the
absolutely continuous
case, the proof is less trivial, see
[
15
, Proposition 4.3]
.

An observation similar to Remark
2.3
is valid for (reverse) evolution families, see
[
12
]
, which explains why assuming that
{D\subsetneq{\mathbb{C}}}
is simply connected is of no loss of generality.
At the same time, notwithstanding Remark
2.8
, both Definitions
2.5
and
2.6
seem to be useful in our context. Reverse evolution families, as mentioned in Introduction, spring up in Probability Theory, e.g. in connection with branching processes. On the other hand, usual evolution families are more natural from the Dynamics point of view. In particular, absolutely continuous evolution families, similarly to one-parameter semigroups, are semiflows of certain holomorphic vector fields in
D
, but in this case the vector fields are
non-autonomous
, i.e. they may depend explicitly on time. In order to state this result in a precise form, we need the following definition.

By
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I)
we denote the class of all measurable (real- or complex-valued) functions which are integrable on each compact subinterval of
I
.

Definition 2.9
.

A function
\phi:D\times I\to{\mathbb{C}}
is said to be a
Herglotz vector field
in a simply connected domain
{D\subsetneq{\mathbb{C}}}
if it satisfies the following three conditions:

HVF1:

for any
t\geqslant 0
,
\phi(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{G}\big({\sf Hol}(D,D)\big)
;

HVF2:

for any
z\in D
,
\phi(z,\cdot)
is measurable on
I
;

HVF3:

for any compact set
K\subset D
, there is a non-negative function
{M_{K}\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I)}
such that

\max_{z\in K}|\phi(z,t)|\leqslant M_{K}(t)\quad\text{a.e.~$t\in I$}.

Remark 2.10
.

An observation analogous to Remark
2.4
holds for Herglotz vector fields. Namely, if
f
maps
{\mathbb{D}}
conformally onto a domain
D
, then
\phi:D\times I\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a Herglotz vector field in
D
if and only if
\psi(\zeta,t):={\phi\big(f(\zeta),t\big)/f^{\prime}(\zeta)}
,
{\zeta\in{\mathbb{D}}}
,
{t\in I}
, is a Herglotz vector field in
{\mathbb{D}}
.

As usual we identify two Herglotz vector fields
{\phi,\psi:D\times I\to{\mathbb{C}}}
if
\phi(\cdot,t)=\psi(\cdot,t)
for a.e.
{t\in I}
. Some of the main results of
[
7
]
can be now summarized and stated in our notation as follows. For
s\in I
, let
I_{s}:=I\cap[s,+\infty)
.

Theorem A
.

Let
{D\subsetneq{\mathbb{C}}}
be a simply connected domain and
(w_{s,t})
an absolutely continuous evolution family in
D
over the interval
I
. Then there exists a unique Herglotz vector field
{\phi:D\times I\to{\mathbb{C}}}
such that for any
z\in D
and any
s\in[0,T)
, the map
{I_{s}\ni t\mapsto w(t):=w_{s,t}(z)\in D}
is a solution to the initial value problem

(2.3)

\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}t}+\phi(w,t)=0,\quad\text{a.e.\ }t\in I_{s};\qquad w(s)=z.

Conversely, let
{\phi:D\times I\to{\mathbb{C}}}
be a Herglotz vector field. Then for any
z\in D
and any
s\in I
the initial value problem (
2.3
) has a unique solution
{t\mapsto w=w(t;z,s)}
defined on some interval containing the set
I_{s}
. Setting
{w_{s,t}(z):=w(t;z,s)}
for all
{z\in D}
and all
(s,t)\in\Delta(I)
one obtains an absolutely continuous evolution family
(w_{s,t})
over the interval
I
.

According to Theorem
A
, there is a one-to-one correspondence between absolutely continuous evolution families and Herglotz vector fields. An evolution family and the vector field, corresponding to this evolution family in the sense of Theorem
A
, are usually said to be
associated
to each other. An analogous one-to-one correspondence exists between absolutely continuous
reverse
evolution families and Herglotz vector fields, see
[
15
, Theorem 4.2 (i)]
. In this respect, if
(v_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta(I)}
is an absolutely continuous reverse evolution family and
\phi
is the Herglotz vector field associated to
(v_{s,t})
, then for each
{z\in D}
and each
{t\in I\setminus\{0\}}
the map
{[0,t]\ni s\mapsto v(s):=v_{s,t}(z)}
is the unique solution to the initial value problem

(2.4)

\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}s}=\phi(v,s)\quad\text{for a.e.\ }s\in[0,t];\qquad v(t)=z.

Remark 2.11
.

Although the correspondences between a Herglotz vector field
\phi
on the one side and the associated absolutely continuous (usual and reverse) evolution families
(w_{s,t})
and
(v_{s,t})
on the other side are expressed rather explicitly, there does not seem to exist any simple way to relate the families
(w_{s,t})
and
(v_{s,t})
directly, i.e. without involving the Herglotz vector field
\phi
.

Remark 2.12
.

Clearly, if
(v_{s,t})
is an absolutely continuous reverse evolution family over the interval
I
, then for each fixed
s\in[0,T)
, the mappings
\tilde{v}_{s^{\prime}\!,t^{\prime}}:=v_{s+s^{\prime}\!,s+t^{\prime}}
also form an absolutely continuous reverse evolution family over a suitable interval. Therefore, combining
[
15
, Theorems 4.1, 4.2 (ii) and 1.11]
, one can easily conclude that there is an equivalent way to express the one-to-one correspondence between Herglotz vector fields and absolutely continuous reverse evolution families introduced above. Namely, as a function of the parameter
t
, any absolutely continuous reverse evolution family
(v_{s,t})
satisfies, in the sense of
[
15
, Definition 2.1]
, the PDE

(2.5)

\frac{\partial v_{s,t}(z)}{\partial t}+\phi(z,t)\frac{\partial v_{s,t}(z)}{\partial z}=0,\quad\text{a.e.\ }t\in I_{s},~z\in D;\qquad v_{s,s}=\mathop{\mathrm{id}}\nolimits_{D},

where
\phi
is the Herglotz vector field associated with
(v_{s,t})
. Conversely, given any Herglotz vector field
\phi:D\times I\to{\mathbb{C}}
, for each
s\in[0,T)
the initial value problem (
2.5
) has a unique solution
(z,t)\mapsto v_{s,t}(z)
, which is defined for all
{(z,t)\in D\times I_{s}}
, and moreover,
(v_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta(I)}
is exactly the reverse evolution family associated with
\phi
.

According to
[
15
, Theorem 1.11]
, the inverse mappings
v_{s,t}^{-1}
also can be recovered by solving an ODE driven by the Herglotz vector field
\phi
associated with
(v_{s,t})
. Namely, for each
{s\in[0,T)}
and each
{z\in D}
, the map
t\mapsto w(t):=v_{s,t}^{-1}(z)
is the unique solution to the initial value problem

(2.6)

\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}t}=\phi(w,t)\quad\text{for a.e.\ }t\in I_{s};\qquad w(s)=z.

Note that in this case solutions do not have to be defined for all
t\in I_{s}
. In fact, the set of all
z\in D
for which the solution to (
2.6
) exists up to (and including) a given instant
t
coincides with
v_{s,t}(D)
.

Remark 2.13
.

Equations (
2.3
) – (
2.6
) are essentially equivalent to each other and are known as
generalized Loewner – Kufarev
differential equations; see, e.g.
[
11
]
. A good account on the
classical
Loewner – Kufarev equations can be found in
[
44
, Chapter 6.1]
.

2.3.
Boundary regular fixed points

For the classical results stated in this section, we refer the reader to
[
9
, Sect. 1.8, 1.9, 10.1, 12.1, and 12.2]
, see also
[
1
, Chapter 1]
.
From the dynamics point of view, an important role in the study of self-maps is played by the fixed points. Thanks to the Schwarz – Pick Lemma, every holomorphic
w\colon{\mathbb{D}}\to{\mathbb{D}}
different from
\mathop{\mathrm{id}}_{\mathbb{D}}
can have at most one fixed point
\tau\in{\mathbb{D}}
. All other fixed points are located on
{\partial{\mathbb{D}}}
and should be understood in the sense of angular limits. Namely,
\sigma\in{\partial{\mathbb{D}}}
is said to be a
boundary fixed point
for
w\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{D}},{\mathbb{D}})
if the angular limit
\angle\lim_{z\to\sigma}w(z)
exists and coincides with
\sigma
. It is known that the angular derivative

w^{\prime}(\sigma):=\angle\lim_{z\to\sigma}\frac{w(z)-\sigma}{z-\sigma}

exists, finite or infinite, at any boundary fixed point
\sigma
and that
w^{\prime}(\sigma)
is either
\infty
or a positive number, see e.g.
[
45
, Proposition 4.13 on p. 82]
.

Definition 2.14
.

A boundary fixed point
\sigma\in{\partial{\mathbb{D}}}
of a holomorphic self-map
{w\colon{\mathbb{D}}\to{\mathbb{D}}}
is said to be
regular
(
BRFP
for short) if
w^{\prime}(\sigma)\neq\infty
.

Using the Cayley map
{\mathbb{D}}\ni z\mapsto H(z):=(1+z)/(1-z)\in{\mathbb{H}}
, the above definitions can be extended to the holomorphic self-maps of the half-plane
{\mathbb{H}}
. In fact, for
\sigma\in\partial{\mathbb{H}}
different from
\infty
this leads to literally the same definitions. In case
\sigma=\infty
, the angular derivative
w^{\prime}(\infty)
should be understood in the sense of Carathéodory as defined in the following version of Julia’s Lemma.

Theorem B

(see e.g.
[
53
, §26]
)
.

For any
f\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},\overline{{\mathbb{H}}})
, the limit

f^{\prime}(\infty):=\angle\lim_{\zeta\to\infty}\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta}

exists finitely. Moreover,

f^{\prime}(\infty)=\inf_{\zeta\in{\mathbb{H}}}\frac{\Re f(\zeta)}{\Re\zeta}\geqslant 0.

In particular,

f(\zeta)=f^{\prime}(\infty)\zeta+g(\zeta)\vphantom{\textstyle\int\limits_{0}^{1}}

for all
{\zeta\in{\mathbb{H}}}
and some
g\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},\overline{{\mathbb{H}}})
satisfying
g^{\prime}(\infty)=0
.

If
f\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
has a boundary fixed point at
\infty
, then
w:=H^{-1}\circ f\circ H
has a boundary fixed point at
1
and
{w^{\prime}(1)=1/f^{\prime}(\infty)}
. Therefore,
\infty
is a BRFP for
f\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{D}},{\mathbb{D}})
if and only if
f^{\prime}(\infty)\neq 0
.

Remark 2.15
.

It follows easily from
[
17
, Theorem 1]
that
0
is a BRFP for
w\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
if and only if
{w(x)\to 0}
as
{{\mathbb{R}}\ni x\to 0^{+}}
and the limit
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}w^{\prime}(x)
exists finitely. Similarly,
\infty
is a BRFP for
w\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
if and only if
\lim_{x\to+\infty}w^{\prime}(x)
is different from zero.

If
w\in{\sf Hol}(D,D)
, where
D\in\{{\mathbb{D}},{\mathbb{H}}\}
, has no fixed points in
D
, then according to the classical Denjoy – Wolff Theorem, there exists a unique BRFP
{\tau\in\partial D}
of
w
such that
{w^{\prime}(\tau)\leqslant 1}
if
{\tau\neq\infty}
and
{w^{\prime}(\tau)\geqslant 1}
if
{\tau=\infty}
. Moreover, as
{n\to+\infty}
, the iterates

(2.7)

w^{\circ n}:=\underbrace{w\circ w\circ\ldots\circ w}_{n\text{~times}}~\longrightarrow~\tau

locally uniformly in
D
. The point
\tau
is called the
Denjoy – Wolff point
of
w
or the
DW-point
for short. The limit behaviour of holomorphic self-maps with interior fixed points is similar. Namely, if
\tau\in D
is a fixed point of
w\in{\sf Hol}(D,D)
and if
w
is not an automorphism of
D
, then (
2.7
) holds. In this case we also refer to
\tau
as the
Denjoy – Wolff point
of
w
.

Now if
(v_{t})\subset{\sf Hol}(D,D)
,
D\in\{{\mathbb{D}},{\mathbb{H}}\}
, is a non-trivial one-parameter semigroup, then
v_{t}
’s different from
\mathop{\mathrm{id}}_{D}
share the same BRFPs and in particular, the same DW-point. According to a well-known result of Berkson and Porta
[
5
]
, see also
[
9
, Theorem 10.1.10]
for another approach, in case
D={\mathbb{D}}
the infinitesimal generator
\phi
of a one-parameter semigroup
(v_{t})
can be written as

(2.8)

\phi(z)=(z-\tau)(1-\overline{\tau}z)p(z),\quad{z\in{\mathbb{D}}},

where
\tau
is the DW-point of
(v_{t})
and
p
is a suitable (uniquely defined) holomorphic function in
{\mathbb{D}}
with
{\Re p\geqslant 0}
. Conversely, if
\phi\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{D}})
is given by (
2.8
) with some
\tau\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
and some
p\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{D}},\overline{{\mathbb{H}}})
, then
\phi
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter semigroup with the DW-point at
\tau
.

Remark 2.16
.

It is known
[
17
, Theorem 1]
that
\sigma\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}
is a BRFP of a one-parameter semigroup
(v_{t})\subset{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{D}},{\mathbb{D}})
if and only if the limit

(2.9)

\lambda:=\angle\lim_{z\to\sigma}\frac{\phi(z)}{z-\sigma}

is finite, and in such a case
{v_{t}^{\prime}(\sigma)=e^{-\lambda t}}
for
all
{t\geqslant 0}
. In particular,
{\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}}
.

Remark 2.17
.

It is worth mentioning that the angular limit (
2.9
) exists,
finite or infinite
, for any infinitesimal generator
\phi
in the unit disk
{\mathbb{D}}
, see e.g.
[
9
, p. 330]
. Therefore, (
2.9
) can be replaced with the corresponding radial limit
\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\overline{\sigma}\phi(\sigma r)/(r-1)
.

Remark 2.18
.

It is also known, see e.g.
[
9
, Proposition 12.2.4]
, that the limit (
2.9
) is finite if and only if
\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\phi(r\sigma)=0
and the limit
\phi^{\prime}(\sigma):=\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\phi^{\prime}(r\sigma)
exists finitely. In such a case
{\lambda=\phi^{\prime}(\sigma)}
.

For the non-autonomous case the following extension of
[
17
, Theorem 1]
holds.

Theorem C

(
[
10
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
(w_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta(I)}\subset{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{D}},{\mathbb{D}})
be an absolutely continuous evolution family with associated Herglotz vector field
{\phi\colon{\mathbb{D}}\times I\to{\mathbb{C}}}
. Let
{\sigma\in{\partial{\mathbb{D}}}}
. Then the following two conditions are equivalent.

(A)

For every
(s,t)\in\Delta(I)
,
\sigma
is a BRFP of
w_{s,t}
.

(B)

The limit
\lambda(t):=\angle\lim_{z\to\sigma}\phi(z,t)/(z-\sigma)
is finite for a.e.
t\geqslant 0
,

and
\lambda\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I)
.

If the above conditions are satisfied, then for all
(s,t)\in\Delta(I)
,

\log w^{\prime}_{s,t}(\sigma)=-\int_{s}^{t}\lambda(u)\mathrm{d}u.

Remark 2.19
.

Remark
2.4
allows one to extend the Berkson – Porta representation formula (
2.8
) to infinitesimal generators in
{\mathbb{H}}
. Namely,
\phi\colon{\mathbb{H}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is an infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter semigroup in
{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
with the DW-point at
{\tau\in\overline{{\mathbb{H}}}}
if and only if

(2.10a)

\displaystyle\phi(\zeta)

\displaystyle=(\zeta-\tau)(\zeta+\overline{\tau})P(\zeta)

\displaystyle\text{if~$~\tau\in{\mathbb{H}}\cup i\,{\mathbb{R}}$},

(2.10b)

\displaystyle\phi(\zeta)

\displaystyle=-P(\zeta)

\displaystyle\text{if~$~\tau=\infty$},

for all
\zeta\in{\mathbb{H}}
, where
P
is a holomorphic function in
{\mathbb{H}}
with
\Re P\geqslant 0
.

2.4.
Bernstein functions

We start with the following classical definition.

Definition 2.20
.

By a
Bernstein function
we mean a non-negative function
f
of class
C^{\infty}
in
(0,+\infty)
such that
(-1)^{n+1}f^{(n)}(x)\geqslant 0
for all
x>0
and all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

In the following theorem we collect some important basic facts, which can be found together with proofs in literature on Bernstein functions, in particular, in the monograph
[
48
, Chapter 3]
.

Theorem D
.

The following statements hold.

(A)

f\colon(0,+\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a Bernstein function if and only if it admits the following representation:

(2.11)

f(x)=\alpha+\beta x~+\!\int\limits_{(0,+\infty)}\!(1-e^{-\lambda x})\,\rho(\mathrm{d}\lambda)\qquad\,\text{for all~$~x>0$,}

where
\alpha,\beta\geqslant 0
and
\rho
is a non-negative Borel measure on
(0,\infty)
satisfying
\int_{0}^{+\infty}\min\{\lambda,1\}\,\rho(\mathrm{d}\lambda)<+\infty
.

(B)

Every Bernstein function
f
has a (unique) holomorphic extension
{f_{*}\colon{\mathbb{H}}\to{\mathbb{C}}}
with
{\Re f_{*}\geqslant 0}
. Moreover,
f_{*}
extends continuously to
i\,{\mathbb{R}}
and representation (
2.11
) still holds if
\,f
is replaced by
f_{*}
and
{x>0}
by an arbitrary
z\in{\mathbb{H}}
.

(C)

For the coefficients
\alpha
and
\beta
in representation (
2.11
) we have

\alpha=\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}f(x)=\lim_{{\mathbb{H}}\ni z\to 0}f_{*}(z)\quad\text{and}\quad\beta=\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{f(x)}{x}=f_{*}^{\prime}(\infty).

(D)

If
f\not\equiv 0
and
g
are Bernstein functions, then
\,g\circ f
is also a Bernstein function and trivially
(g\circ f)_{*}=g_{*}\circ f_{*}
.

(E)

Bernstein functions form a convex cone; namely, for any two Bernstein functions
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
and any constant
a,b\geqslant 0
,
af_{1}+bf_{2}
is again a Bernstein function.

(F)

If a sequence of Bernstein functions converges pointwise in
(0,+\infty)
to a function
f
, then
f
is a Bernstein function.

In what follows we will not make any notational distinction between a Bernstein function
f
and its holomorphic extension
f_{*}
. The class of all
v\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{C}})
such that
v|_{(0,+\infty)}
is a Bernstein function and not identically zero will be denoted by
\mathfrak{BF}
. According to assertions (B), (D) and (F) in the above theorem,
\mathfrak{BF}
is a topologically closed semigroup in
{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
. Taking into account the uniqueness of holomorphic extension, we will refer to elements of
\mathfrak{BF}
as Bernstein functions as well.

In what follows we will need another definition, closely related to the notion of a Bernstein function.

Definition 2.21
.

Let
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be an open interval.
A function
\varphi\colon I\to{\mathbb{R}}
is said to be
completely monotone
in
I
, if it is of class
C^{\infty}
and
{(-1)^{k}\varphi^{(k)}(x)\geqslant 0}
for all
x\in I
and all
k=0,1,2,\ldots

Similarly, a function
\psi\colon I\to{\mathbb{R}}
is said to be
absolutely monotone
in
I
if it is of class
C^{\infty}
and
{\psi^{(k)}(x)\geqslant 0}
for all
x\in I
and all
k=0,1,2,\ldots

Remark 2.22
.

By the very definition, a non-negative differentiable function
f
on
(0,+\infty)
is a Bernstein function if and only if
\psi:=f^{\prime}
is completely monotone on
(0,+\infty)
. Moreover, obviously
\varphi
is a completely monotone in an open interval
I
if and only if
\psi(x):=\varphi(-x)
is absolutely monotone in the interval
J:=\{{-x}\colon{x\in I}\}
. Another simple observation is that according to Leibniz’s rule for higher order derivatives, the product of two absolutely monotone functions is again absolutely monotone.

Remark 2.23
.

In a similar way, it follows immediately from Faà di Bruno’s formula for the derivatives of a composite function that if
g\colon I\to J
, where
I
and
J
are two open intervals in
{\mathbb{R}}
, is such that
g^{\prime}
is absolutely monotone in
I
, and if
\psi
is absolutely monotone in
J
, then the composition
\psi\circ g
is absolutely monotone in
I
.

Remark 2.24
.

The above remarks imply a slight generalization of assertion (D) in Theorem
D
. Namely, if
f_{1}
is a Bernstein function and if
f_{2}
is a non-negative differentiable function such that
f_{2}^{\prime}
is completely monotone in an open interval
J
containing
f_{1}\big((0,+\infty)\big)
, then
f_{2}\circ f_{1}
is a Bernstein function: simply apply Remark
2.23
with
g:=g_{1}
,
I:=(-\infty,0)
, and
\psi:=g_{2}^{\prime}
, where
g_{j}(x):=-f_{j}(-x)
,
j:=1,2
.

3.
Results

3.1.
Infinitesimal generators in topologically closed semigroups

It is well-known that infinitesimal generators of one-parameter semigroups in
{\sf Hol}(D,D)
form a closed convex cone, see e.g.
[
9
, Corollary 10.2.7, p. 287]
. For various choices of a semigroup
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt\subsetneq{\sf Hol}(D,D)
, the cone
\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)
formed of all infinitesimal generators of one-parameter semigroups contained in
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
has been explicitly characterized, see e.g.
[
28
]
. In each case we are aware of, the cone
\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)
turns out to be convex. However, we have not been able to find in the literature any general results in this respect.
By this reason, below we give a proof of the following basic theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
{D\subsetneq{\mathbb{C}}}
be a simply connected domain and
\,\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
a topologically closed semigroup of holomorphic self-maps of
D
.
Then the set
\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)
formed by all infinitesimal generators of one-parameter semigroups contained in
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
is a topologically closed (real) convex cone in
{\sf Hol}(D,{\mathbb{C}})
.

Since by Theorem
D
,
\mathfrak{BF}
is a topologically closed semigroup in
{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
, the above theorem directly implies the following

Corollary 3.1
.

The set
\,\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{BF})
is a topologically closed convex cone in
{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{C}})
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

The fact that
\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)
is a cone is trivial. Indeed, if
\phi\in\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)
generates a one-parameter semigroup
(v_{t}^{\phi})
, then for any
\alpha\geqslant 0
the function
\alpha\phi
is the infinitesimal generator of the one-parameter semigroup
(\tilde{v}_{t})
formed by the mappings
{\tilde{v}_{t}:=v_{\alpha t}^{\phi}}
.

To show that
\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)
is topologically closed, consider an arbitrary sequence
{(\phi_{n})\subset\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)}
converging locally uniformly in
D
to some
\phi\in{\sf Hol}(D,{\mathbb{C}})
. By
[
9
, Theorem 10.5.6, p. 300]
,
\phi
is also an infinitesimal generator in
D
and, for each
t\geqslant 0
, we have
{v_{t}^{n}\to v_{t}}
in
{\sf Hol}(D,D)
as
n\to+\infty
, where
(v_{t}^{n})
and
(v_{t})
stand for the one-parameter semigroups generated by
\phi_{n}
and
\phi
, respectively. By the hypothesis,
(v_{t}^{n})\subset\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
and
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
is closed in
{\sf Hol}(D,D)
. Therefore,
(v_{t})\subset\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
and hence
\phi\in\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)
. This means that
\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)
is a closed subset of
{\sf Hol}(D,{\mathbb{C}})
.

To complete the proof it remains to show that
{\phi:=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)}
provided that
\phi_{1},{\phi_{2}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)}
.
By
[
9
, Corollary 10.2.7, p. 287]
,
\phi
is an infinitesimal generator in
D
. Denote by
(v_{t}^{1})
,
(v_{t}^{2})
, and
(v_{t})
the one-parameter semigroups generated by
\phi_{1}
,
\phi_{2}
, and
\phi
, respectively. The Trotter product formula for one-parameter semigroups, see e.g.
[
9
, Corollary 10.6.2, p. 304]
or
[
46
, Corollary 4]
, states that for each
{t\geqslant 0}
,

v_{t}=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\big(v^{1}_{t/n}\circ v^{2}_{t/n}\big)^{\circ n},

where the limit exists in the topology of
{\sf Hol}(D,D)
. Since by the hypothesis,
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
is a closed subset of
{\sf Hol}(D,D)
and all finite compositions of its elements again belong to
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
, it follows that
{(v_{t})\subset\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt}
, i.e.
\phi\in\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)
.
∎

3.2.
Evolution families in topologically closed semigroups

A natural question arises in connection with the correspondence discussed at the end of Section
2.2
: how are the properties of the elements in a (reverse) evolution family related to those of the associated Herglotz vector field? For a particular property related to the boundary behaviour at a given point (namely, for having a boundary regular fixed point) the answer was given as Theorem
C
. The main new result of this section is a general statement of a similar kind.

Theorem 2
.

Let
{D\subsetneq{\mathbb{C}}}
be a simply connected domain and
\,\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
a topologically closed semigroup of holomorphic self-maps of
D
. Let
(w_{s,t})
be a (reverse) evolution family in
D
with associated Herglotz vector field
\phi
. Then the following two conditions are equivalent:

(i)

w_{s,t}\in\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
for all
(s,t)\in\Delta(I)
;

(ii)

\phi(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)
for a.e.
t\in I
.

Remark 3.2
.

The assumption that
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
is topologically closed plays an important role in the above theorem. Compare, e.g., with Theorem
C
in Section
2.3
, which concerns holomorphic self-maps with a given BRFP. They form a semigroup, but it is not a closed subset in
{\sf Hol}(D,D)
. As a result, an additional condition has to be imposed on a Herglotz vector field in order to ensure that the corresponding evolution family is contained in the semigroup in question.

Nevertheless, since by Theorem
D
, Bernstein functions different from the identical zero form a topologically closed semigroup in
{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
, Theorem
2
implies directly the following corollary.

Corollary 3.3
.

An absolutely continuous evolution family
(w_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta(I)}
in
{\mathbb{H}}
is contained in the class
\mathfrak{BF}
if and only if the Herglotz vector field
\phi
associated to
(w_{s,t})
satisfies the following condition:
\phi(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{BF})
for a.e.
t\in I
.

The proof of (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) in Theorem
2
makes use of the following rather simple observation, which however we have not met explicitly stated elsewhere.

Proposition 3.4
.

For every Herglotz vector field
\phi
in a simply connected domain
{D\subsetneq{\mathbb{C}}}
, there exists a non-negative function
{M\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I)}
with the following property: for any compact set
K\subset D
there is a constant
C_{K}>0
such that

\max_{z\in K}|\phi(z,t)|\leqslant C_{K}M(t)\quad\text{for a.e.~$t\geqslant 0$}.

Proof.

Thanks to Remark
2.10
, we may suppose that
D={\mathbb{D}}
. Then by the non-autonomous extension of the Berkson – Porta formula
[
7
, Theorem 4.8]
,

(3.1)

\phi(z,t)=\big(z-\tau(t)\big)\big(1-\overline{\tau(z)}z\big)p(z,t)\quad\text{for all~$z\in{\mathbb{D}}$ and
a.e.~$t\in I$},

where
\tau\colon I\to\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
is a measurable function and
p\colon{\mathbb{D}}\times I\to{\mathbb{C}}
satisfies the following two conditions:

(a)

for a.e.
t\in I
, the function
p(\cdot,t)
is holomorphic in
{\mathbb{D}}
and
\Re p(\cdot,t)\geqslant 0
;

(b)

for any
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, the function
p(z,\cdot)
is locally integrable on
I
.

Set
M(t):=|p(0,t)|
for all
{t\in I}
. Then by (b),
{M\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I)}
. Moreover, thanks to (a) with the help of the Harnack inequality, see e.g.
[
44
, ineq. (11) on p. 40]
, for a.e.
{t\in I}
and all

we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle|p(z,t)|

\displaystyle\leqslant|\Im p(0,t)|\,+\,\frac{1+|z|}{1-|z|}\Re p(0,t)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1+|z|}{1-|z|}\big(|\Im p(0,t)|+\Re p(0,t)\big)\leqslant\frac{1+|z|}{1-|z|}\sqrt{2}M(t).

Now the conclusion of the proposition follows easily from (
3.1
) and (
3.2
).

Proof of Theorem

where the limit is attained locally uniformly in

. By the hypothesis,

{(w_{s,t})\subset\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt}

. Thus, assertion (i) implies that

Proof of
(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i). By Theorem
1
,
\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)
is a topologically closed convex cone.
By Proposition
3.4
, for any compact set

, there exists

such that

\max_{z\in K}|\phi(z,t)|\leqslant C_{K}M(t)\quad\text{for a.e. $t\in I$},

where

is a locally integrable function (not depending on

).

Let
g(t):=\int_{0}^{t}M(s)\mathrm{d}s
for all
{t\geqslant 0}
. Clearly, we may suppose that

for all

; otherwise we would replace the function

by

.
Then both
{g\colon I\to J:=g(I)\subset{\mathbb{R}}}
and

are strictly increasing and locally absolutely continuous. Taking into account (

), it follows that the formula

defines a Herglotz vector field

. According to Theorem

, the evolution family associated with
\psi
can be constructed via solutions
{\omega=\omega(\theta)}
to the initial value problem

The variable change
\theta=g(t)
relates the trajectories of the Herglotz vector field
\psi
to those of
\phi
. Using this fact, it is easy to see that the evolution family
(\omega_{\eta,\theta})
associated with
\psi
is given by
\omega_{\eta,\theta}=w_{f(\eta),f(\theta)}
for all
(\eta,\theta)\in\Delta(J)
. Therefore, it is sufficient to show that

.

From (
3.4
) it follows that

(3.5)

\max_{z\in K}|\psi(z,\theta)|\leqslant C_{K}\quad\text{for a.e. $\theta\in J$}

and any compact set
K\subset D
.

Consider the sequence
(\psi_{n})
of functions on

defined by

(3.6)

\psi_{n}(z,\theta):=2^{n}\!\!\int\limits_{\eta(\theta,n)}^{\eta(\theta,n)+2^{-n}}\!\!\psi(z,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta,\quad\theta\in J,~n\in{\mathbb{N}},

where
\eta(\theta,n):=2^{-n}\lfloor 2^{n}\theta\rfloor
and
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
stands for the integer part.

Denote by
\mathcal{G}_{0}
the set of all
\varphi\in\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)
such that
\sup_{K}|\varphi|\leqslant C_{K}
for every compact set
K\subset D
. Recall that by Theorem
1
,
\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)
is convex and topologically closed. Therefore,
\mathcal{G}_{0}
is a compact convex set in the locally convex Hausdorff space
{\sf Hol}(D,{\mathbb{C}})
. By (
3.5
) the integrand in (
3.6
) belongs to
\mathcal{G}_{0}
for a.e.
{t\geqslant 0}
, and by
[
7
, Lemma 4.7]
the map
{\theta\mapsto\psi(\cdot,\theta)\in{\sf Hol}(D,{\mathbb{C}})}
is measurable. Therefore, for any fixed
{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
\theta\in J
, the integral in (
3.6
) can be understood as a weak integral of
{\eta\mapsto\psi(\cdot,\eta)}
, and moreover, its value belongs to
2^{-n}\mathcal{G}_{0}
, see e.g.
[
36
, Theorem 1 in §18.1.3]
. Hence
{\psi_{n}(\cdot,\theta)\in\mathcal{G}_{0}\subset\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt)}
for all
{\theta\in J}
. In its turn, the latter implies that
\psi_{n}
’s are Herglotz vector fields in
D
.

Note that the elements of the evolution families
(\omega^{n}_{\eta,\theta})
associated with the Herglotz vector fields
\psi_{n}
are finite compositions of mappings from one-parameter semigroups whose infinitesimal generators belong to
\mathcal{G}_{0}
. Therefore,
(\omega^{n}_{\eta,\theta})\subset\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt
and it only remains to show that for each
{(\eta,\theta)\in\Delta(J)}
fixed,

\omega_{\eta,\theta}^{n}\to\omega_{\eta,\theta}~\text{~in $~{\sf Hol}(D,{\mathbb{C}})$}\quad\text{ as $~n\to+\infty$.}

Since self-maps of
D
form a normal family, it is sufficient to prove that
\omega_{\eta,\theta}^{n}\to\omega_{\eta,\theta}
pointwise in
D
.

We claim that for a.e.
\theta\in J
,
\psi_{n}(\cdot,\theta)\to\psi(\cdot,\theta)
in
{\sf Hol}(D,{\mathbb{C}})
as
{n\to+\infty}
.
Indeed, consider a sequence
(z_{k})\subset D
having an accumulation point in
D
. By compactness of
\mathcal{G}_{0}
, it is only necessary to prove that for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and for a.e.
\theta\in J
,
\psi_{n}(z_{k},\theta)\to\psi(z_{k},\theta)
as
{n\to+\infty}
. The latter holds for any Lebesgue point
\theta
, i.e. for any point
\theta\in J
at which the derivative of
F_{k}(\theta):=\int_{0}^{\theta}\psi(z_{k},\eta)\mathrm{d}\eta
exists and equals
\psi(z_{k},\theta)
. Since the Lebesgue points have full measure, our claim is proved.

Fix now a compact set
K\subset D
. As we have just proved,

\delta^{K}_{n}(\theta):=\max_{z\in K}|\psi_{n}(z,\theta)-\psi(z,\theta)|\to 0\quad\text{as~$~n\to+\infty$}

for a.e.
\theta\in J
. Since
\psi_{n}(\cdot,\theta)
and
\psi(\cdot,\theta)
belong to
\mathcal{G}_{0}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and a.e.
\theta\in J
, we have

0\leqslant\delta^{K}_{n}(\theta)\leqslant 2C_{K}
for a.e.
\theta\in J
.
Therefore, thanks to Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence Theorem, for each compact interval
{Y\subset J}
and each function
{h:Y\to{\mathbb{C}}}
of class
L^{\infty}
, we have

\left|\int_{Y}h(\theta)\psi_{n}(z,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,-\,\int_{Y}h(\theta)\psi(z,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right|\leqslant\int_{Y}|h(\theta)|\,\delta^{K}_{n}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\to\,0~\text{~as~$n\to+\infty$}

for all
~z\in K
.
The fact that
\omega_{\eta,\theta}^{n}\to\omega_{\eta,\theta}
as
{n\to+\infty}
follows now from general results on ODEs, see e.g.
[
47
, Lemma I.37 on p. 38]
or
[
34
, Lemma 3.1]
.

3.3.
Bernstein generators

By a
Bernstein generator
we mean an infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter semigroup whose elements are Bernstein functions. In the notation introduced in Preliminaries, the class of all Bernstein generators is exactly
\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{BF})
. It is worth mentioning that in the literature on stochastic processes, see e.g.
[
38
, Sect. 12.1]
, Bernstein generators appear in connection to continuous-state branching processes; in this probabilistic context, they are referred to as
branching mechanisms
.

3.3.1.
Statement of results

An integral representation of Bernstein generators was found in 1968 by Silverstein
[
52
, Theorem 4]
, see also
[
37
, Lemma 1.3]
.
We establish an essentially equivalent way to characterize Bernstein generators.

Theorem 3
.

A function
\phi\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{C}})
is a Bernstein generator if and only if it satisfies the following three conditions:

(i)

\phi\big((0,+\infty)\big)\subset{\mathbb{R}}
;

(ii)

the limit
\phi(0):=\lim_{{\mathbb{R}}\ni x\to 0^{+}}\phi(x)
exists and belongs to
(-\infty,0]
;

(iii)

\phi^{\prime\prime}
is completely monotone in
(0,+\infty)
.

Following Kyprianou
[
38
, Theorem 12.1 on p. 336]
, Silverstein’s representation can be written as follows. A function
\phi\colon{\mathbb{H}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a Bernstein generator if and only if there exist
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
q,b\geqslant 0
and a Borel non-negative measure
\pi
on
(0,+\infty)
with
\int_{0}^{+\infty}\min\{x^{2},1\}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x)<+\infty
such that

(3.7)

\phi(\zeta)=-q+a\zeta+b\zeta^{2}+\int_{0}^{+\infty}\!\!\big(e^{-\zeta x}-1+\zeta x\,\boldsymbol{1}_{(0,1)}(x)\big)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\quad\text{for all~}~\zeta\in{\mathbb{H}}

(where we have extended formula
[
38
, (12.7)]
holomorphically from
{(0,+\infty)}
to
{\mathbb{H}}
).

If
\phi
is given by (
3.7
), then clearly
\phi\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{C}})
and satisfies conditions (i) and (ii) in Theorem
3
. Moreover, (
3.7
) implies that
\phi^{\prime\prime}
is the Laplace transform of a non-negative Borel measure
\mu
on
{[0,+\infty)}
given by

(3.8)

\mu(\{0\}):=2b,\quad\mu|_{(0,+\infty)}(\mathrm{d}x):=x^{2}\pi(\mathrm{d}x).

By Bernstein’s Theorem, see e.g.
[
48
, Theorem 1.4 on p. 3]
,
\phi^{\prime\prime}
is completely monotone on
{(0,+\infty)}
, i.e. condition (iii) is also satisfies.

Conversely, suppose
\phi\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{C}})
and conditions (i) – (iii) hold. Then again by Bernstein’s Theorem,
\phi^{\prime\prime}
is the Laplace transform of some non-negative
\sigma
-finite Borel measure
\mu
on
{[0,+\infty)}
. Recovering
\phi^{\prime}
and
\phi
with the help of the formulas
\phi^{\prime}(x)=\phi^{\prime}(1)+\int_{1}^{x}\phi^{\prime\prime}(y)\mathrm{d}y
,
\phi(x):=\phi(0)+\int_{0}^{x}\phi^{\prime}(y)\mathrm{d}y
for all
x>0
, and taking into account that by (ii) the integral
\int_{0}^{1}\phi^{\prime}(y)\mathrm{d}y
converges, we see that:

(a)

the Borel measure
\pi
on
(0,+\infty)
defined by
\pi(\mathrm{d}\lambda):=\lambda^{-2}\mu|_{(0,+\infty)}(\mathrm{d}\lambda)
satisfies
\int_{0}^{+\infty}\min\{\lambda^{2},1\}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\lambda)<+\infty
;

(b)

representation (
3.7
) holds for all
\zeta\in(0,+\infty)
— and hence for all
\zeta\in{\mathbb{H}}
— with the measure
\pi
defined in (a),
{q:=-\phi(0)}
,
{b:=\mu(\{0\})/2}
,

a:=\phi^{\prime}(1)-\mu(\{0\})+\int_{(0,1)}\!\lambda(e^{-\lambda}-1)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\lambda)+\int_{[1,+\infty)}\!\lambda e^{-\lambda}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\lambda).

Remark 3.5
.

With the help of the monotone convergence theorem, it is easy to see that in the above notation,

\phi^{\prime\prime}(+\infty):=\lim_{x\to+\infty}\phi^{\prime\prime}(x)=\mu(\{0\})=2b.

In this section we will present a complex-analytic proof of Theorem
3
independent from representation (
3.7
). On the other hand, with the help of the above computation,
(
3.7
) can be adapted for the case of a BRFP at
z:=0
.

Corollary 3.6
.

A function
\phi\colon{\mathbb{H}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a Bernstein generator associated with a one-parameter semigroup
(v^{\phi}_{t})
having a BRFP at
{\sigma:=0}
if and only if
\phi
admits the representation

(3.9)

\phi(\zeta)=c\zeta+b\zeta^{2}+\int_{0}^{+\infty}\!\!\big(e^{-\zeta x}-1+\zeta x\big)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\quad\text{for all~}~\zeta\in{\mathbb{H}},

where
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
b\geqslant 0
and
\pi
is a non-negative Borel measure on
(0,+\infty)
such that

(3.10)

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\min\{\lambda^{2},\lambda\}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\lambda)\,<\,+\infty.

Moreover, if the above necessary and sufficient condition is satisfied, then

(3.11)

c=\phi^{\prime}(0):=\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\phi^{\prime}(x)

and
(v_{t}^{\phi})^{\prime}(0)=e^{-c\,t}
for all
{t\geqslant 0}
. In particular,
{\sigma=0}
is the Denjoy – Wolff point of
(v_{t}^{\phi})
if and only if
\phi
admits representation (
3.9
) with
{c\geqslant 0}
.

Note that the Bernstein generator
\phi
in Corollary
3.6
admits both representations (
3.7
) and (
3.9
). As we will see from the proof, the coefficient
b
and the measure
\pi
in the two representations are the same.

As a representation of a branching mechanism
\phi
, formula (
3.9
) appeared in
[
39
, Theorem 1, pp. 21-22]
,
[
41
, Section 3.1]
,
[
42
, Section 2.2]
.
In the probabilistic terms,
(v^{\phi}_{t})
has a BRFP at
0
if and only if the corresponding continuous-state branching process has finite means, see
[
38
, Section 12.2.2]
.

In a similar way, the case of a BRFP at
\infty
can be treated.

Corollary 3.7
.

A function
\phi\colon{\mathbb{H}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a Bernstein generator associated with a one-parameter semigroup
(v^{\phi}_{t})
having a BRFP at
{\sigma:=\infty}
if and only if
\phi
admits the representation

(3.12)

\phi(\zeta)=-q+c\zeta-\int_{0}^{+\infty}\!\!\big(1-e^{-\zeta x}\big)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\quad\text{for all~}~\zeta\in{\mathbb{H}},

where
q\geqslant 0
,
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and
\pi
is a non-negative Borel measure on
(0,+\infty)
such that

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\min\{\lambda,1\}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\lambda)\,<\,+\infty.

Moreover, if the above necessary and sufficient condition is satisfied, then

c=\phi^{\prime}(\infty):=\angle\lim_{z\to\infty}\frac{\phi(z)}{z}=\lim_{x\to+\infty}\phi^{\prime}(x)

and
(v_{t}^{\phi})^{\prime}(\infty)=e^{-c\,t}
for all
{t\geqslant 0}
. In particular,
{\sigma=\infty}
is the Denjoy – Wolff point of
(v_{t}^{\phi})
if and only if
\phi
admits representation (
3.12
) with
{c\leqslant 0}
.

The representation (
3.12
) with
{c\leqslant 0}
has a probabilistic interpretation that the corresponding branching process is exactly a process with non-decreasing sample paths because it can be expressed as a randomly time-changed subordinator (with killing) according to the Lamperti transformation (see e.g.
[
38
, Theorem 12.2]
).

Remark 3.8
.

According to Corollary
3.6
, a function
\phi
given by representation (
3.9
) with
c\geqslant 0
is an infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter semigroup with the DW-point
{\tau=0}
. It is therefore interesting to compare (
3.9
) with the Berkson – Porta formula (
2.10a
):
\phi(\zeta)=\zeta^{2}P(\zeta)
,
\zeta\in{\mathbb{H}}
, where
P
is the holomorphic function with non-negative real part given by

P(\zeta)~=~c\,\zeta^{-1}~+\int\limits_{[0,+\infty)}P_{*}(x\zeta)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x),\quad P_{*}(z):=\frac{e^{-z}-1+z}{z^{2}}\in{\mathbb{H}},\qquad\text{for all~$~\zeta\in{\mathbb{H}}$,}

with the Borel measure
\mu
defined by (
3.8
).
It is worth mentioning that in the case of arbitrary

, the above representation of
\phi
corresponds to the extension of the Berkson – Porta formula due to Bracci, Contreras and Díaz-Madrigal
[
6
, Theorem 1.3]
.

A similar observation applies to Bernstein generators admitting representation (
3.12
).

3.3.2.
Auxiliary statements and proofs

Lemma 3.9
.

For any
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
b,q\geqslant 0
, the functions
\phi_{1}(z):=az
,
\phi_{2}(z):=bz^{2}
, and
\phi_{3}(z):=-q
are Bernstein generators.

Proof.

By explicit integration of the equations
\mathrm{d}w/\mathrm{d}t+\phi_{j}(w(t))=0
,
{j=1,2,3}
, with the initial condition
w(0)=z\in{\mathbb{H}}
, we obtain
v_{t}^{\phi_{1}}(z)=e^{-at}z
,
v_{t}^{\phi_{2}}(z)=z/(1+btz)
, and
v_{t}^{\phi_{3}}(z)=z+qt
. These are Bernstein functions for all
t\geqslant 0
.
∎

Proposition 3.10
.

If
f\in\mathfrak{BF}
, then
\phi:=-f\in\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{BF})
.

Proof.

Let
f\colon{\mathbb{H}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be a Bernstein function. Then
\Re f\geqslant 0
in
{\mathbb{H}}
and hence, by Remark
2.19
,
\phi:=-f
is an infinitesimal generator in
{\mathbb{H}}
. Clearly, we may suppose that
\phi\not\equiv 0
. By Theorem
B
, the limit
f^{\prime}(\infty):=\angle\lim_{z\to\infty}f(z)/z
exists and belongs to
[0,+\infty)
.

Note that we may restrict ourselves to the case
f^{\prime}(\infty)>0
. To cover the case
f^{\prime}(\infty)=0
, it would be sufficient to consider the Bernstein functions
f+\delta\mathop{\mathrm{id}}_{\mathbb{H}}
,
\delta>0
, and use the fact that by Corollary
3.1
,
\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{BF})
is topologically closed in
{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{C}})
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, Euler’s approximations of the one-parameter semigroup
(v_{t}^{\phi})
generated by
\phi
, which are defined recurrently by

v_{0}^{\varepsilon}=\mathop{\mathrm{id}},\quad v_{n}^{\varepsilon}:=v_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon f\circ v_{n-1}^{\varepsilon},~{n\in{\mathbb{N}}},

are Bernstein functions, because
\mathfrak{BF}
is a convex cone closed w.r.t. compositions and containing
\mathop{\mathrm{id}}_{\mathbb{H}}
, see Theorem
D
.
Taking into account that by Theorem
D
(F),
\mathfrak{BF}
is topologically closed in
{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
, we only need to show that for any
{t>0}
,
v_{n}^{t/n}\to v_{t}^{\phi}
locally uniformly in
{\mathbb{H}}
as
{n\to+\infty}
.

Notice that
v_{n}^{\varepsilon}
is the
n
-th iterate of
\psi_{\varepsilon}(z):=z+\varepsilon f(z)
. Recall that
f^{\prime}(\infty)>0
. Therefore,
\psi_{\varepsilon}
is a hyperbolic self-map of
{\mathbb{H}}
with the DW-point at
\infty
. Therefore, see e.g.
[
53
, Chapter 6, §46]
, for any
{\varepsilon>0}
the limit
g_{\varepsilon}(z):={\lim_{n\to+\infty}\psi_{\varepsilon}^{\circ n}(z)/|\psi_{\varepsilon}^{\circ n}(1)|}
exists for all
{z\in{\mathbb{H}}}
; and moreover,
g_{\varepsilon}\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
is a non-constant solution to the functional equation
g_{\varepsilon}\circ\psi_{\varepsilon}=\psi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\infty)g_{\varepsilon}
.

Consider the functions

h_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{\varepsilon}{\log\psi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\infty)}\log\circ\,g_{\varepsilon},

where
\log
stands for the branch of the logarithm in
{\mathbb{H}}
satisfying
\log 1=0
. For each
{\varepsilon>0}
, the function
h_{\varepsilon}
solves Abel’s functional equation

(3.13)

h_{\varepsilon}\circ\psi_{\varepsilon}=h_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon.

Recall that
g_{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{H}})\subset{\mathbb{H}}
. Hence
h_{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{H}})
lies in the horizontal strip of width
\varepsilon\pi/\log\psi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\infty)
symmetric w.r.t.
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Since
\varepsilon/\log\psi^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\infty)\to 1/f^{\prime}(\infty)<+\infty
as
{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}
, Montel’s normality criterion, see e.g.
[
22
, § II.7, Theorem 1]
, implies that
{\{h_{\varepsilon}:0<\varepsilon<1\}}
is a normal family in
{\mathbb{H}}
.

From Abel’s equation (
3.13
) we obtain

1=\frac{h_{\varepsilon}(z+\varepsilon f(z))-h_{\varepsilon}(z)}{\varepsilon}=\int_{0}^{1}h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(z+t\varepsilon f(z))f(z)\,\mathrm{d}t\quad\text{for any~$z\in{\mathbb{H}}$.}

If
h_{\varepsilon_{n}}\to h
locally uniformly in
{\mathbb{H}}
for some sequence
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0^{+}
as
n\to+\infty
, then the integral in the above equality tends along the sequence
(\varepsilon_{n})
to
h^{\prime}(z)f(z)
. Taking into account that
h_{\varepsilon}(1)=0
for all
{\varepsilon>0}
, we therefore conclude that

h_{\varepsilon}(z)\to h(z):=\int_{1}^{z}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{f(\zeta)}\quad\text{as~$~\varepsilon\to 0^{+}$.}

The function
h
is the Koenigs function of
(v_{t}^{\phi})
, see e.g.
[
9
, Theorem 10.1.4, p. 274]
. In particular, it satisfies Abel’s equation
h\circ v_{t}^{\phi}=h+t
for all
t\geqslant 0
.

On the other hand, if for some fixed
t\geqslant 0
, and some sequence
{(n_{k})\subset{\mathbb{N}}}
tending to
+\infty
, we have
v^{t/{n_{k}}}_{n_{k}}\to v_{*}
, where
v_{*}\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{C}})\cup\{v\equiv\infty\}
, then in fact,
v_{*}\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
and satisfies Abel’s equation
h\circ v_{*}=h+t
. Indeed, since
\Re f\geqslant 0
, by construction we have
\Re v^{t/{n_{k}}}_{n_{k}}(z)\geqslant\Re z
for all
{z\in{\mathbb{H}}}
. It follows that either
v_{*}\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
or
v_{*}\equiv\infty
. The latter alternative is impossible because, as a holomorphic self-map of
{\mathbb{H}}
,
\psi_{t/n_{k}}
is non-expansive w.r.t. the hyperbolic distance
\rho
in
{\mathbb{H}}
, and hence for any
z\in{\mathbb{H}}
, we have

\displaystyle\rho\big(z,v^{t/{n_{k}}}_{n_{k}}(z)\big)=\rho\big(z,\psi_{t/{n_{k}}}^{\circ n_{k}}(z)\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}\rho\big(\psi_{t/{n_{k}}}^{\circ(j-1)}(z),\psi_{t/{n_{k}}}^{\circ j}(z)\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant n_{k}\,\rho(z,z+tf(z)/n_{k})\to\frac{t|f(z)|}{2\Re z}

as
k\to+\infty
. Thanks to (
3.13
) with
\varepsilon:=t/n_{k}
, we have
h_{t/n_{k}}\circ v_{n_{k}}^{t/n_{k}}=h_{t/n_{k}}+t
. Passing to the limit as
{k\to+\infty}
, we obtain the desired relation
h\circ v_{*}=h+t
.

As a Koenigs function of a one-parameter semigroup,
h
is univalent in
{\mathbb{H}}
. Therefore, the equality
h\circ v_{*}=h\circ v_{t}^{\phi}
is only possible if
v_{*}=v^{\phi}_{t}
. Since
v_{n}^{t/n}\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
for any
{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
,
{t\geqslant 0}
, and since
{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
is a normal family, the latter means that for each
{t\geqslant 0}
,
v_{n}^{t/n}\to v_{t}^{\phi}
in
{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
as
{n\to+\infty}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Suppose first that
\phi
is a Bernstein generator. As usual, denote by
(v_{t}^{\phi})
the one-parameter semigroup in
\mathfrak{BF}
associated with
\phi
. Since
\phi(z)=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\big(z-v_{t}^{\phi}(z)\big)/t
and the limit is attained locally uniformly in
{\mathbb{H}}
, it follows that
\phi
is real-valued on
(0,+\infty)
and that

(-1)^{n}\phi^{(n)}(x)=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}(v_{t}^{\phi})^{(n)}(x)}{t}\geqslant 0

for all
x\in(0,+\infty)
and all
n\geqslant 2
, because
(-1)^{n-1}\big(v_{t}^{\phi}\big)^{(n)}(x)\geqslant 0
for all
t>0
and all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Since
\phi^{\prime\prime}(x)\geqslant 0
for all
x\in(0,+\infty)
, the limit
\phi(0):=\lim_{{\mathbb{R}}\ni x\to 0^{+}}\phi(x)
exists and belongs to
(-\infty,+\infty]
. Considering the equation
\mathrm{d}w/\mathrm{d}t+\phi(w(t))=0
with the initial condition
w(0)=x
in a right neighbourhood of the origin on the real line, we see that the solution leaves
{\mathbb{H}}
at finite time if
\phi(0)>0
, which would contradict the fact that
\phi
is an infinitesimal generator in
{\mathbb{H}}
.

Thus, conditions (i) – (iii) hold for every Bernstein generator. Suppose now that
\phi
satisfies (i) – (iii) and show that in such a case
\phi
is a Bernstein generator. By (i) and (iii),
\phi^{\prime}
is a real-valued non-decreasing function in
(0,+\infty)
. It follows that there exists the limit
\phi^{\prime}(\infty):=\lim_{{\mathbb{R}}\ni x\to+\infty}\phi(x)
belonging to
(-\infty,+\infty]
.

First let us consider the special case
\phi^{\prime}(\infty)\neq+\infty
. It is worth mentioning that this is done for the sake of clarity rather than because it is logically necessary.
If
\phi^{\prime}(\infty)\leqslant 0
, then
\phi^{\prime}(x)\leqslant 0
for all
x\in(0,+\infty)
and hence conditions (i) – (iii) imply that
f:=-\phi
is a Bernstein function. Therefore, by Proposition
3.10
,
\phi
is a Bernstein generator.

Similarly, if
\phi^{\prime}(\infty)\in(0,+\infty)
, then
f_{1}(z):=\phi^{\prime}(\infty)z-\phi(z)
is a Bernstein function. Hence
\phi=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}
, where
\phi_{1}:=-f_{1}
and
\phi_{2}(z):=\phi^{\prime}(\infty)z
, is a Bernstein generator, because
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
belong to
\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{BF})
by Proposition
3.10
and by Lemma
3.9
, respectively, and because
\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{BF})
is a convex cone by Corollary
3.1
.

Now we present a (bit more sophisticated) proof valid both for finite and infinite values of
\phi^{\prime}(\infty)
.
Since
\phi^{\prime\prime}
is completely monotone by (iii), the limit
\phi^{\prime\prime}(\infty):=\lim_{{\mathbb{R}}\ni x\to+\infty}\phi^{\prime\prime}(x)
exists and satisfies

(3.14)

\phi^{\prime\prime}(x)\geqslant\phi^{\prime\prime}(\infty)\geqslant 0\qquad\text{for all~$x>0$}.

It follows that the function
\varphi(z):=\phi(z)-\phi^{\prime\prime}(\infty)z^{2}/2
,
z\in{\mathbb{H}}
, satisfies the same conditions (i) – (iii) as
\phi
. In particular, the limit
\varphi^{\prime}(\infty):=\lim_{{\mathbb{R}}\ni x\to+\infty}\varphi(x)
exists and belongs to
{(-\infty,+\infty]}
. In addition,
\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\infty)=0
. It follows that

(3.15)

\lim_{{\mathbb{R}}\ni x\to+\infty}\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(x)}{x}=0.

Using again Corollary
3.1
together with the fact that
z\mapsto\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\infty)z^{2}
is Bernstein generator by Lemma
3.9
, we may conclude that in order to complete the proof it is enough to show that
\varphi\in\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{BF})
.

For
\varepsilon>0
and
z\in{\mathbb{H}}
, we define

\displaystyle\phi_{1}^{\varepsilon}(z)

\displaystyle:={\varphi(z)-\varphi^{\prime}(1/\varepsilon)z},

\displaystyle\phi_{\varepsilon}(z)

\displaystyle:=\phi_{1}^{\varepsilon}\big(T_{\varepsilon}(z)\big)+\varphi^{\prime}(1/\varepsilon)z,\quad\text{where $~T_{\varepsilon}(z):=z/(1+\varepsilon z)$}.

Note that
(-\phi_{1}^{\varepsilon})^{\prime}
is completely monotonic in
(0,1/\varepsilon)
. Moreover,
\lim_{{\mathbb{R}}\ni x\to 0^{+}}\phi_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x)=\phi(0)\leqslant 0
and hence
\phi_{1}^{\varepsilon}
is non-positive on
(0,1/\varepsilon)
. Since
T_{\varepsilon}
is a Bernstein function and
T_{\varepsilon}\big((0,+\infty)\big)=(0,1/\varepsilon)
, by Remark
2.24
it follows that
-\phi_{1}^{\varepsilon}\circ T_{\varepsilon}
is also a Bernstein function. Therefore, arguing as above we may conclude that
\phi_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{BF})
for every
{\varepsilon>0}
.

Using (
3.15
), we get:

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\phi_{\varepsilon}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\big(\varphi(T_{\varepsilon}(z))-\varphi^{\prime}(1/\varepsilon)T_{\varepsilon}(z)+\varphi^{\prime}(1/\varepsilon)z\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\varphi(T_{\varepsilon}(z))~+~\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\dfrac{\varepsilon\,\varphi^{\prime}(1/\varepsilon)\,z^{2}}{1+\varepsilon z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(z)\vphantom{\int^{1}}.

Since by Corollary
3.1
,
\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{BF})
is closed in
{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{C}})
, it follows that
\varphi
is a Bernstein generator and hence the proof is complete.
∎

Proof of Corollary
3.6
.

First of all, notice that according to Remarks
2.16
,
2.18
, and
2.4
, a one-parameter semigroup
(v_{t}^{\phi})\subset{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
with associated infinitesimal generator
\phi
has a BRFP at
0
if and only if

(3.16)

\phi(0):=\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\phi(x)~=~0\quad\text{and}\quad\phi^{\prime}(0):=\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\phi^{\prime}(x)~\text{~exists finitely}.

Suppose now that
\phi\in\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{BF})
and that the above condition (
3.16
) is satisfied. Following the lines of the argument given after the statement of Theorem
3
, which was used to deduce Silverstein’s formula (
3.7
), we represent
\phi^{\prime\prime}
as the Laplace transform of a non-negative
\sigma
-finite Borel measure
\mu
on
[0,+\infty)
. Since
\phi^{\prime}
has a finite limit at
0^{+}
, with the help of Tonelli’s Theorem, we get

\int_{0}^{1}\phi^{\prime\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\int_{0}^{+\infty}\!e^{-\lambda x}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\lambda)\Big)\mathrm{d}x=\mu(\{0\})\,+\!\!\int\limits_{(0,+\infty)}\!\!\!\!\lambda(1-e^{-\lambda})\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\lambda)<+\infty,

where
\pi
is a Borel measure on
(0,+\infty)
given by
\pi(\mathrm{d}\lambda):=\lambda^{-2}\mu|_{(0,+\infty)}(\mathrm{d}\lambda)
. Clearly, the integrability condition we established is equivalent to (
3.10
).

With a similar technique, for all
x>0
we further obtain

\phi^{\prime}(x):=\phi^{\prime}(0)+\mu(\{0\})x\,+\!\!\int\limits_{(0,+\infty)}\!\!\!\!\lambda(1-e^{-\lambda x})\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\lambda).

Extending this formula holomorphically to
{\mathbb{H}}
and taking into account the first equality in (
3.16
), we arrive at the desired representation (
3.9
).

Conversely, bearing in mind Bernstein’s theorem on completely monotone functions, see e.g.
[
48
, Theorem 1.4 on p. 3]
, one can easily see that if
\phi
is given by (
3.9
) with some
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
b\geqslant 0
, and a Borel measure
\pi
on
(0,+\infty)
subject to the integrability condition (
3.10
), then
\phi
is a holomorphic function in
{\mathbb{H}}
satisfying conditions (i) – (iii) in Theorem
3
and (
3.16
), with
{\phi^{\prime}(0)=c}
. Therefore,
\phi
is a Bernstein generator and the associated one-parameter semigroup
(v_{t}^{\phi})
has a BRFP at
0
.
Moreover, with the different sign in the definition of the infinitesimal generator taken into account, from
[
17
, Theorem 1]
it follows that
(v_{t}^{\phi})^{\prime}(0)=e^{-\phi^{\prime}(0)t}=e^{-c\,t}
for all
{t\geqslant 0}
. Finally, the BRFP at
0
is the DW-point of
(v_{t}^{\phi})
if and only if
(v_{t}^{\phi})^{\prime}(0)\leqslant 1
for all

, which is equivalent to the inequality
{c\geqslant 0}
.
∎

3.4.
Conditions for absolute continuity of evolution families

Several (necessary and/or sufficient) conditions for a family of holomorphic self-maps satisfying conditions EF1 and EF2 in Definition
2.5
(or conditions REF1 and REF2 in Definition
2.6
respectively) to be an
absolutely continuous
(reverse) evolution family are known, see e.g.
[
7
, Theorem 7.3]
,
[
13
, Section 2]
,
[
14
, Proposition 3.7]
,
[
15
, Section 4.1]
,
[
31
, Section 4]
,
[
29
, Theorem 1]
.

The following result improves considerably
[
7
, Theorem 7.3]
for the case of the boundary DW-point (except that we do not keep track on the order of integrability). For a domain
D\in\{{\mathbb{D}},{\mathbb{H}}\}
and a point
\tau\in\partial D
denote by
\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt_{\tau}
the set of
\mathop{\mathrm{id}}_{D}
and all self-maps
v\in{\sf Hol}(D,D)
for which
\tau
is the DW-point.

Theorem 4
.

Let
D\in\{{\mathbb{D}},{\mathbb{H}}\}
and
\tau\in\partial D
. Suppose that
(w_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta(I)}\subset\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt_{\tau}
satisfies conditions EF1 and EF2 in Definition
2.5
. If there exits
{z_{0}\in D}
such that the map
I\ni t\mapsto w_{0,t}(z_{0})
belongs to
AC_{\mathrm{loc}}(I)
, then
(w_{s,t})
is an absolutely continuous evolution family.

Proof.

Clearly, we may suppose that
D={\mathbb{H}}
and that
\tau=\infty
.
For
{t\in I}
denote

a(t):=\Re w_{0,t}(z_{0})\quad\text{and}\quad b(t):=\Im w_{0,t}(z_{0}).

By EF2,
w_{0,t_{2}}(z_{0})={w_{t_{1},t_{2}}\big(w_{0,t_{1}}(z_{0})\big)}
.
Hence by Julia’s Lemma for
{\mathbb{H}}
, see Theorem
B
, applied to the map
w_{t_{1},t_{2}}
, for any
(t_{1},t_{2})\in\Delta(I)
we have

(3.17)

1\leqslant w_{t_{1},t_{2}}^{\prime}(\infty)\leqslant{a(t_{2})}/{a(t_{1})},

where the left inequality holds simply because
\infty
is the DW-point of
w_{t_{1},t_{2}}
.

The family
(\hskip-0.09995pt\widetilde{\hskip 0.09995ptw}_{s,t})
defined by
\hskip-0.09995pt\widetilde{\hskip 0.09995ptw}_{s,t}:=L_{t}\circ w_{s,t}\circ L_{s}^{-1}
for all
{(s,t)\in\Delta(I)}
, where
L_{t}(z):=\big(z-ib(t)\big)/a(t)
, satisfies conditions EF1 and EF2 in Definition
2.5
. Moreover, its elements share the same DW-point at
1\in{\mathbb{H}}
and a boundary regular fixed point at
\infty
. By the Chain Rule for angular derivatives, see e.g.
[
17
, Lemma 2]
,

\frac{\hskip-0.09995pt\widetilde{\hskip 0.09995ptw}_{0,t_{2}}^{\prime}(\infty)}{\hskip-0.09995pt\widetilde{\hskip 0.09995ptw}_{0,t_{1}}^{\prime}(\infty)}=\hskip-0.09995pt\widetilde{\hskip 0.09995ptw}^{\prime}_{t_{1},t_{2}}(\infty)=\frac{a(t_{1})}{a(t_{2})}\,w^{\prime}_{t_{1},t_{2}}(\infty)\quad\text{for any~$~(t_{1},t_{2})\in\Delta(I)$.}

Using (
3.17
) we therefore obtain

\frac{a(t_{1})}{a(t_{2})}\leqslant\frac{\hskip-0.09995pt\widetilde{\hskip 0.09995ptw}_{0,t_{2}}^{\prime}(\infty)}{\hskip-0.09995pt\widetilde{\hskip 0.09995ptw}_{0,t_{1}}^{\prime}(\infty)}\leqslant 1.

Since by the hypothesis
a\colon I\to(0,+\infty)
is a function of class
AC_{\mathrm{loc}}
, the latter implies that the map
t\mapsto\hskip-0.09995pt\widetilde{\hskip 0.09995ptw}_{0,t}^{\prime}(\infty)
belongs to
AC_{\mathrm{loc}}(I)
. Recall that the self-maps
\hskip-0.09995pt\widetilde{\hskip 0.09995ptw}_{s,t}
share the same interior DW-point. Therefore, by
[
31
, Theorem 4.2]
,
(\hskip-0.09995pt\widetilde{\hskip 0.09995ptw}_{s,t})
is an absolutely continuous evolution family. Since by the hypothesis
a,b\in AC_{\mathrm{loc}}(I)
, it is easy to see that the automorphisms
L_{s,t}:=L_{t}\circ L_{s}^{-1}
also form an absolutely continuous evolution family. Writing
w_{s,t}={L_{t}^{-1}\circ\hskip-0.09995pt\widetilde{\hskip 0.09995ptw}_{s,t}\circ L_{s}}
for all
{(s,t)\in\Delta(I)}
and applying
[
13
, Lemma 2.8]
, we conclude that
(w_{s,t})
is also an absolutely continuous evolution family.
∎

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
(v_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta(I)}
be a topological reverse or usual evolution family in
D\in\{{\mathbb{D}},{\mathbb{H}}\}
. Suppose that the following two conditions hold:

(i)

v_{s,t}(D\cap{\mathbb{R}})\subset D\cap{\mathbb{R}}
for any
(s,t)\in\Delta(I)
;

(ii)

(v_{s,t})\subset\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt_{\tau}
for some
\tau\in\overline{D}
.

Then there exists a continuous increasing bijective map
u\colon J\to I
,
J\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, such that the (reverse or usual) evolution family
(\tilde{v}_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta(J)}
given by
\tilde{v}_{s,t}:=v_{u(s),u(t)}
for all
{(s,t)\in\Delta(J)}
is absolutely continuous.

Proof.

We prove the corollary only for the case
D={\mathbb{H}}
. The proof for
{D={\mathbb{D}}}
is very similar and therefore, we omit it.

Furthermore, we first suppose that
(v_{s,t})
is a topological evolution family and that
{\tau\in\partial{\mathbb{H}}}
. Then (i) implies that
\tau\in\{0,\infty\}
. Using if necessary the change of variable
{z\mapsto 1/z}
, we may suppose that
\tau=\infty
. Choose any
z_{0}\in(0,+\infty)
. The map

I\ni t\mapsto f(t):=t+v_{0,t}(z_{0})-z_{0}

is strictly increasing and continuous (because combining
v_{s,t}^{\prime}(\infty)\geqslant 1
and Julia’s lemma (Theorem
B
) implies
\Re v_{s,t}(z)\geqslant\Re z
on
{\mathbb{H}}
). Therefore, it has a strictly increasing continuous inverse
u\colon J\to I
mapping some interval
J\subset{\mathbb{R}}
onto
I
. Clearly, the functions
\tilde{v}_{s,t}:=v_{u(s),u(t)}
,
{(s,t)\in\Delta(J)}
, form a topological evolution family. Moreover, by construction,

(3.18)

\tilde{v}_{0,t}(z_{0})=v_{0,u(t)}(z_{0})=-u(t)+f(u(t))+z_{0}=-u(t)+t+z_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}

for all
{t\in J}
. If
t_{2}\geqslant t_{1}
,
t_{1},t_{2}\in I
, then
v_{0,t_{2}}(z_{0})=v_{t_{1},t_{2}}(v_{0,t_{1}}(z_{0}))\geqslant v_{0,t_{1}}(z_{0})
and hence
f(t_{2})-f(t_{1})\geqslant t_{2}-t_{1}
. It follows that
u
is Lipschitz continuous. Using (
3.18
) and Theorem
4
, we conclude that the evolution family
(\tilde{v}_{s,t})
is absolutely continuous.

If now
(v_{s,t})
is a topological evolution family with the DW-point
{\tau\in{\mathbb{H}}}
, then
{\tau\in(0,+\infty)}
. Fix some
z_{0}\in(0,\tau)
and define the family
(\tilde{v}_{s,t})
literally by the same formula as above. In order to make sure that
f
is strictly increasing in
I
also in this case, notice that since
t\mapsto v_{0,t}(z_{0})\in(0,+\infty)
is continuous and since elements of a topological evolution family are univalent
[
12
, Proposition 2.4]
, we have
v_{0,t}\big((0,\tau)\big)\subset(0,\tau)
for all
{t\in I}
. Hence the monotonicity of
f
follows from the fact that the holomorphic maps
v_{s,t}
do not increase the hyperbolic distance in
{\mathbb{H}}
. For
z\in\{z_{0},\tau\}
, the map
t\mapsto\tilde{v}_{0,t}(z)
belongs
AC_{\mathrm{loc}}(J)
. Again by univalence of elements of a topological evolution family,
\tilde{v}_{0,t}(z_{0})\neq\tilde{v}_{0,t}(\tau)
for all
{t\in J}
. By
[
14
, Proposition 3.7]
, it follows that
(\tilde{v}_{s,t})
is an absolutely continuous evolution family.

Suppose now that
(v_{s,t})
is a topological
reverse
evolution family. If
I
is of the form
[0,T]
,
0<T<+\infty
, then applying the above argument to the topological evolution family
(\hat{v}_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta(I)}
defined by
\hat{v}_{s,t}:=v_{T-t,T-s}
, we directly arrive to the desired conclusion.

To cover the case
I=[0,T)
,
0<T\leqslant+\infty
, the argument has to be slightly modified. As above, we suppose that
\tau=\infty
or
\tau\in(0,+\infty)
and fix some
z_{0}\in(0,\tau)
. Next we choose an increasing sequence
(T_{n})\subset(0,T)
converging to
T
and set

f(s):=s+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}{\vphantom{\sum}}{}^{\prime}\,\frac{z_{0}-v_{s,T_{n}}(z_{0})}{a_{n}}\boldsymbol{1}_{[0,T_{n}]}(s),\quad a_{n}:=2^{n}\max_{s\in[0,T_{n}]}\big|z_{0}-v_{s,T_{n}}(z_{0})\big|,

where the prime in the summation symbol means that, in order to cover the possibility of
v_{0,T_{n}}=\mathop{\mathrm{id}}_{\mathbb{H}}
for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we omit all terms for which
a_{n}=0
.

Let
u:=f^{-1}\colon J\to I
, where
J:=f(I)
. Fix some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and notice that
f(s)=f_{n}(s)+a_{n}^{-1}\big(z_{0}-v_{s,T_{n}}(z_{0})\big)
,
{s\in[0,T_{n}]}
, where
f_{n}
is a strictly increasing function on
I
. Since
s\mapsto{z_{0}-v_{s,T_{n}}(z_{0})}
is non-decreasing, the function
f_{n}\circ u
is Lipschitz continuous on
f([0,T_{n}])
. This fact allows us to show, arguing to as above, that
\big(v_{T_{n}-u(t),T_{n}-u(s)}\big)
is an absolutely continuous reverse evolution family on
[0,T_{n}]
.
Since
{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is arbitrary in this argument, we may conclude that
(v_{u(s),u(t)})
is an absolutely continuous evolution family, as desired.
∎

Now we prove an analogue of Theorem
4
for reverse evolution families.

Theorem 5
.

Let
D\in\{{\mathbb{D}},{\mathbb{H}}\}
and
\tau\in\partial D
. Let
(v_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta(I)}\subset\mathfrak{U}\hskip 0.50003pt_{\tau}
be a topological reverse evolution family. If there exists
{z_{0}\in D}
such that the map
I\ni t\mapsto v_{0,t}(z_{0})
belongs to
AC_{\mathrm{loc}}(I)
, then the reverse evolution family
(v_{s,t})
is absolutely continuous.

Remark 3.12
.

In contrast to Theorem
4
, the hypothesis in the above theorem contains the requirement that
(v_{s,t})
is continuous in
(s,t)
. This assumption is essential for the proof we give below. However, we do not know whether Theorem
5
remains valid if we assume that
(v_{s,t})
satisfies REF1 and REF2 from Definition
2.6
without requiring
a priori
any regularity in
s
and
t
.

To prove Theorem
5
we need the following two lemmas.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
f\in{\sf Hol}({\mathbb{H}},{\mathbb{H}})
,
f\neq\mathop{\mathrm{id}}_{\mathbb{H}}
, and
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{H}}
. Suppose
\infty
is the Denjoy – Wolff point of
f
. Then for any compact set
K\subset{\mathbb{H}}
there exists a constant
A_{K}>0
such that

(3.19)

\max_{z\in K}|f(z)-z|\leqslant A_{K}|f(z_{0})-z_{0}|.

The constant
A_{K}
depends on
K

Next we turn to families of holomorphic self-maps of
{\mathbb{H}}
formed by (extensions) of Bernstein functions. First we establish a (quantitative) rigidity type result. In contrast to arbitrary holomorphic self-maps, for which the Burns – Krantz rigidity property involves angular derivatives up to the third order, see e.g.
[
51
]
and
[
18
]
, for functions in
\mathfrak{BF}
, existence of finite limits of the first
two
derivatives at
z=0
is sufficient. Recall that by definition of a Bernstein function, if
f\in\mathfrak{BF}
then
f^{\prime}
as well as all higher order derivatives of
f
are real-valued and monotonic on
{(0,+\infty)}
. Hence there exist (finite or infinite) limits

f^{(n)}(0):=\lim_{{\mathbb{R}}\ni x\to 0^{+}}f^{(n)}(x),\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Moreover, there exists a non-negative finite limit
f(0):=\lim_{{\mathbb{R}}\ni x\to 0^{+}}f(x)
.

Proposition 3.15

(Rigidity property of Bernstein functions)
.

Let
f\in\mathfrak{BF}
and suppose that
f^{\prime}(0)
and
f^{\prime\prime}(0)
are finite. Then

(3.22)

|f(z)-z|\leqslant f(0)+\big|(f^{\prime}(0)-1)z\big|+\tfrac{1}{2}\big|f^{\prime\prime}(0)z^{2}\big|

for any
z\in{\mathbb{H}}
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathfrak{BF}
. Then for all
z\in{\mathbb{H}}
,

(3.23)

f(z)=\alpha+\beta z+\int\limits_{0}^{+\infty}(1-e^{-\lambda z})\,\tau(\mathrm{d}\lambda),

where
\alpha,\beta\geqslant 0
and
\tau
is a Borel non-negative measure
\tau
on
(0,+\infty)
with
\int_{0}^{\infty}\min\{\lambda,1\}\,\tau(\mathrm{d}\lambda)<\infty
.
With the help of Levi’s Monotone Convergence Theorem, it is elementary to show that

(3.24a)

\displaystyle f(0)

\displaystyle=\alpha,

(3.24b)

\displaystyle[0,+\infty]\ni f^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\beta\,+\,\int_{0}^{+\infty}\!\lambda\,\tau(\mathrm{d}\lambda),

(3.24c)

\displaystyle[-\infty,0]\ni f^{\prime\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=-\,\int_{0}^{+\infty}\!\lambda^{2}\,\tau(\mathrm{d}\lambda).

Fix some
z\in{\mathbb{H}}
.
Note that

(3.25)

|\lambda z-(1-e^{-\lambda z})|\leqslant\frac{|e^{-\lambda z}|}{2}|\lambda z|^{2}<\frac{|\lambda z|^{2}}{2}\quad\text{for all~}~\lambda>0.

To deduce this elementary inequality, it is sufficient to fix an arbitrary
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
and apply Cauchy’s Mean Value Theorem twice starting with the pair of functions
f_{1}(t):=\Re\big(e^{-i\theta}z^{-2}\big(tz-(1-e^{-tz})\big)\big)
and
f_{2}(t):=t^{2}
,
t\in[0,\lambda]
.

Integrating inequality (
3.25
) and combining it with (
3.23
) and (
3.24
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\big|f^{\prime\prime}(0)z^{2}\big|}{2}~=~\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\!\!\frac{\,|\lambda z|^{2}}{2}\,\tau(\mathrm{d}\lambda)

\displaystyle\geqslant~\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{+\infty}\!\!(1-e^{-\lambda z})\,\tau(\mathrm{d}\lambda)-\int_{0}^{+\infty}\!\lambda z\,\tau(\mathrm{d}\lambda)\right|

\displaystyle=\big|f(z)-z-f(0)-(f^{\prime}(0)-1)z\big|.

Thanks to the triangle inequality, this immediately implies (
3.22
).
∎

Remark 3.16
.

Note that by Theorem
D
(B), the unrestricted (and hence also angular) limit of
f\in\mathfrak{BF}
at
0
exists and equals
f(0):=\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}f(x)
. Moreover, using formulas (
3.23
) and (

), together the integrability condition

for the measure

and the limit

and

and

3.5.
The second angular derivative of evolution families in
\mathfrak{BF}

An elementary result from the theory of ODE is that for the solution
w(t)=w_{s,t}(z)
to an initial value problem of the form

\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}t}+\phi(w,t)=0,\quad w(s)=z,

where
\phi(\cdot,t)
is holomorphic in the neighbourhood of
w=0
and vanishes at that point, we have

(3.28)

\displaystyle w^{\prime}_{s,t}(0)

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(-\int_{s}^{t}\phi^{\prime}(0,\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\Big),

(3.29)

\displaystyle w^{\prime\prime}_{s,t}(0)

\displaystyle=-\,w_{s,t}^{\prime}(0)\int_{s}^{t}\phi^{\prime\prime}(0,\xi)w^{\prime}_{s,\xi}(0)\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

If now
\phi
is Herglotz vector field in
{\mathbb{H}}
and the associated evolution family
(w_{s,t})
has a BRFP at
\sigma=0
, then formula (
3.28
), where
w^{\prime}_{s,t}(0)
and
\phi^{\prime}(0,t)
are to be understood as angular derivatives, is justified by Theorem
C
.

In view of Theorem
6
, it is worth extending (
3.29
) to evolution families of Bernstein functions.

Proposition 3.18
.

Let
(w_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta(I)}
be an absolutely continuous evolution family contained in
\mathfrak{BF}
and let
\phi
be the associated Herglotz vector field. Suppose that for any
{(s,t)\in\Delta(I)}
,
{w_{s,t}(x)\to 0}
as
{\mathbb{R}}\ni x\to 0^{+}
. Then formula (
3.29
) holds, with
w^{\prime}_{s,\xi}(0)
,
w^{\prime\prime}_{s,t}(0)
, and
\phi^{\prime\prime}(0,\xi)
understood as the limits of
w^{\prime}_{s,\xi}(x)
,
w^{\prime\prime}_{s,t}(x)
, and of
\phi^{\prime\prime}(x,\xi)
as
{\mathbb{R}}\ni x\to 0^{+}

3.28
) and (
3.29
) can be infinite.

Proof.

Fix
(s,t)\in\Delta(I)
. An elementary computation shows that for any
x>0
,

(3.30)

\frac{w^{\prime\prime}_{s,t}(x)}{w^{\prime}_{s,t}(x)}=\,-\int_{s}^{t}\phi^{\prime\prime}\big(w_{s,\xi}(x),\xi\big)w^{\prime}_{s,\xi}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

By the hypothesis, the mappings
w_{s,\xi}
belong to
\mathfrak{BF}
. In particular, for each

,

,

is positive and non-increasing, because

is a Bernstein function and because it is univalent as an element of absolutely continuous evolution family. As a result, one can apply the Monotone Convergence Theorem to pass to the limit in (

(3.31)

\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\frac{w^{\prime\prime}_{s,t}(x)}{w^{\prime}_{s,t}(x)}=\,-\int_{s}^{t}\phi^{\prime\prime}(0,\xi)w^{\prime}_{s,\xi}(0)\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

, it remains to notice that the indefinite form

cannot occur in the r.h.s. of (

and hence the limit in (
