The convergence rates for the superdiffusion in the Boltzmann-Grad limit of the periodic Lorentz gas
Songzi Li

Abstract
In this article, we obtain the rates of convergence for superdiffusion in the Boltzmann-Grad limit of the periodic Lorentz gas, which is one of the fundamental models to study diffusions in deterministic systems. In their seminal work, Marklof and Strömbergsson proved the Boltzmann-Grad limit of the periodic Lorentz gas [ 18 ] , and then Marklof and Tóth established a superdiffusive central limit theorem in large time for the Boltzmann-Grad limit [ 21 ] . Based on their work, we apply Stein’s method to derive the convergence rates for the superdiffusion in the Boltzmann-Grad limit of the periodic Lorentz gas. For the discrete time displacement the rate of convergence in Wasserstein distance is obtained, while in the context of the continuous time displacement the result is presented for the Berry-Essen type bound.



Key words
: convergence rates, superdiffusion, periodic Lorentz gas, Stein’s method.

Mathematics Subject Classification (2020)
: Primary 60K50, 60F05; Secondary 37D45.

1
Introduction and main results

Since its introduction
[
15
]
in 1905 to study the motion of electrons in metals, the Lorentz gas has been considered as a basic model to study diffusions in deterministic systems and to understand Brownian motion from microscopic perspectives. It is a dynamical system corresponding with a tagged particle moving in an infinitely array of fixed scatterers. There have been many research on this topic, involving dynamical systems, probability, differential equations, etc.

The Boltzmann-Grad limit of the Lorentz gas corresponds to the dynamics of the tagged particle when taking the radius of the scatterers to zero. It was conjectured by Lorentz
[
15
]
that the limiting system is governed by a linear Boltzmann equation. Indeed it is true when the configuration of the scatterers is random, see Gallavotti
[
13
]
, Spohn
[
26
]
, C. Boldrighini, L.A. Bunimovich and Y.G. Sinai
[
5
]
. As for the periodic Lorentz gas, Golse
[
14
]
pointed out that the linear Boltzmann equation fails. Later Boca-Zaharescu
[
3
]
gave the explicit formula of the limiting distribution of the free path length for the two-dimensional periodic Lorentz gas, see also Caglioti-Golse
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
. As for the high dimension, Marklof-Strömbergesson proved that the Boltzmann-Grad limit of the periodic Lorentz gas exists and gave its descriptions in their seminal papers
[
19
]
,
[
18
]
and
[
20
]
.

When the radius of the scatterers is fixed, the diffusive property of the Lorentz gas has been extensively studied. The classical result was derived by Bunimovich-Sinai
[
4
]
, where they proved the central limit theorem for the planar periodic Lorentz gas with finite horizon. Bleher
[
2
]
was the first to point out the super-diffusive nature of the periodic Lorentz gas with infinite horizon. This fact was later confirmed by Szász-Varju in
[
29
]
for the planar billiard map, and by Chernov-Dolgopyat in
[
11
]
for the planar billiard flow.

In
[
21
]
, Marklof and Tóth studied the long-time behavior of the Boltzmann-Grad limit of the periodic Lorentz gas by proving a superdiffusive central limit theorem. To be precise, let
{\mathbf{X}}_{t}
be the continuous time displacement of the Boltzmann-Grad limit of the periodic Lorentz gas, and
{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}
be its discrete time displacement. Then under assumptions
{\mathbf{A}}
(which we will explain in Section 2), the following superdiffusive central limit theorem holds.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
. Under assumptions
{\mathbf{A}}
on the initial data, we have

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbf{X}}_{t}}{\Sigma_{d}\sqrt{t\log t}}\Rightarrow{\mathcal{N}}(0,{\rm{Id}}_{d}),

and

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}}{\sigma_{d}\sqrt{n\log n}}\Rightarrow{\mathcal{N}}(0,{\rm{Id}}_{d}).

where
\sigma^{2}_{d}=\frac{2^{2-d}}{2d^{2}(d+1)\zeta(d)}
,
\Sigma^{2}_{d}=\frac{2^{2-d}}{2d^{2}(d+1)\bar{\xi}\zeta(d)}
and
\bar{\xi}
is the mean free flight length.

As the above result shows, the Boltzmann-Grad limit of the periodic Lorentz gas is a superdiffusion, in that the scaling factor is now
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\log n}}
instead of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
in the classical central limit theorem. Then together with the Boltzmann-Grad limit of the periodic Lorentz gas
[
18
]
, it was derived in
[
21
]
that the Brownian motion can been realized as the limit of the periodic Lorentz gas, first under the Boltzmann-Grad limit and then the large time limit.

In this paper, our aim is to describe how fast the distribution of the Boltzmann-Grad limit of the periodic Lorentz gas, converges to the Gaussian distribution. More precisely, we first show the explicit convergence rates of
{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}
in terms of the Wasserstein distance. Recall that the Wasserstein distance
d_{W_{1}}
between two random variables
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
is defined by

d_{W_{1}}({\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{Y}})=\sup_{h\in\textsc{Lipschitz}(1)}|{\mathbb{E}}h({\mathbf{X}})-{\mathbb{E}}h({\mathbf{Y}})|.

Under the same assumptions
{\mathbf{A}}
as in Theorem
1.1
(which will be explained in Section 2), we have the following estimates hold.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
{\mathbf{Z}}
be standard Gaussian random variable on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Under the assumptions
{\mathbf{A}}
, we have when
d=2
,

\displaystyle d_{W_{1}}(\frac{{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}}{\sigma_{d}\sqrt{n\log n}},{\mathbf{Z}})\leq O(\sqrt{\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}}),

(1.1)

and when
d\geq 3

\displaystyle d_{W_{1}}(\frac{{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}}{\sigma_{d}\sqrt{n\log n}},{\mathbf{Z}})\leq O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log n}}).

(1.2)

As for the continuous time displacement
{\mathbf{X}}_{t}
, the convergence rates for the Berry-Essen bound are obtained.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
{\mathbf{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Under the assumptions
{\mathbf{A}}
, we have when
d=2
,

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{P}}(\frac{{\mathbf{X}}_{t}}{\Sigma_{d}\sqrt{t\log t}}\leq{\mathbf{z}})-\Phi({\mathbf{z}})|\leq O(\frac{(\log\log t)^{\frac{1}{4}}}{(\log t)^{\frac{1}{4}}}),

(1.3)

and when
d\geq 3
,

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{P}}(\frac{{\mathbf{X}}_{t}}{\Sigma_{d}\sqrt{t\log t}}\leq{\mathbf{z}})-\Phi({\mathbf{z}})|\leq O(\frac{1}{(\log t)^{\frac{1}{4}}}),

(1.4)

where
\Phi({\mathbf{z}})
is the Gaussian distribution function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

The above result shows that the convergence rate for the superdiffusion in the Boltzmann-Grad limit of the periodic Lorentz gas are quite slow compared to that of the classical central limit theorem, which is
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
.

The general method to obtain the convergence rate in the central limit theorem is characteristic function (Fourier analysis).
However, the displacement
{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}
(or
{\mathbf{X}}_{t}
) as a process is very complicated: it involves many random variables which depend on each other, and the transition kernel is hard to deal with, which makes the method of characteristic function impossible to work.

The main tool of our proof is the Stein’s method. Starting from a basic observation of the Gaussian distribution, Stein’s method for normal approximation was first introduced by Stein
[
27
]
. Since then, it has been shown to be powerful in many mathematical fields, for that the ideas of Stein’s method is abstract enough to be extended to other distribution approximation (such as Poisson distribution) and even diffusions, and that it also works for a variety of metrics. There are lots of comprehensive study on Stein’s method, see for example
[
10
]
,
[
8
]
and
[
25
]
. Generally Stein’s method is separated into two steps. The first one is through the so-called Stein’s equation, which turns the difference between two distributions into the expectation with respect to the aimed distribution of a certain space of functions. The second step is to estimate the expectation derived in the first step.

The advantage of Stein’s method is that it allows us to localize the difference between the distributions, which is efficient for complicated situations as
{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}
(the displacement of the Boltzmann-Grad limit of the periodic Lorentz gas), such that with the moment estimates we are able to bound each term and derive the final results. To apply Stein’s method to our case, the first step is quite straightforward, since we are in the case of normal approximation. For the second step, inspired by the work of Chatterjee-Meckes
[
9
]
, we manage to adapt the idea of exchangeable pairs for multivariate normal approximation to our case. The technique of "exchangeable pairs" was first introduced by Stein
[
27
]
and given in details for the univariate case in
[
28
]
. The multivariate version of exchangeable pairs was first studied by Chatterjee-Meckes
[
9
]
and then Reinert-Röllin
[
24
]
. By applying the method of "exchangeable pairs" to our case, we directly obtain an explicit error.

To obtain the final estimates, it still requires bounding some specific forms of expectations in the error term, which are analogous to the moment bounds when applying Stein’s method in the classical settings. Recall that the key point in the proof of superdiffusive central limit theorem in
[
21
]
is the spectral gap of the transition kernel, which also plays an important role in our work. Thanks to it, we are able to control the moments to get an explicit convergence rate. We point out that for the continuous time displacement
{\mathbf{X}}_{t}
, stronger estimates are required than those in the discrete case. This is the reason why the convergence rate is only shown for the Berry-Essen type bounds.

As far as we know, there are few references regarding the convergence rates of Lorentz gas, or of superdiffusions. We mention the work of F. Péne
[
23
]
, where she proved the convergence rate
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
for the central limit theorem of the Sinai billiard, in the sense of Kantorovich distance.

As the referee pointed out, it would be also interesting if one considers the low density limit and the large time limit together, i.e., taking a joint limit
r\rightarrow 0
and
t\rightarrow\infty
. Recall that in dimension two, Chernov-Dolgopyat
[
11
]
derived a superdiffusive central limit theorem and invariance principle for the billiard flow when the radius is fixed. More recently, Lutsko-Tóth
[
16
]
proved the invariance principle for a random Lorentz gas under the Boltzmann-Grad limit and large time limit simultaneously. Therefore to extend such results to the periodic Lorentz gas in any dimension is quite natural and meaningful .

This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we offer some preliminaries about this topic, including the descriptions of the Boltzmann-Grad limit of periodic Lorentz gas, as well as some fundamental facts on Stein’s method and exchangeable pairs. In Section 3 we prove the key estimates which are needed to bound the error term appearing in the Stein’s method. In Section 4 and 5 we give the proofs of Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
respectively .

2.1
The Boltzmann-Grad limit of the periodic Lorentz gas

In the first part of this section we provide a more detailed description of the Boltzmann-Grad limit of the periodic Lorentz gas. Here we use the same notations as in
[
21
]
. For a comprehensive study, see Marklof-Strömbergsson
[
19

18
]
, Marklof-Toth

We start with the settings of the periodic Lorentz gas. Let
{\mathcal{L}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a fixed Euclidean lattice of covolume one. At each point of
{\mathcal{L}}
there is a sphere of radius
r
as an obstacle. Define
{\mathcal{L}}_{r}=r^{\frac{d-1}{d}}{\mathcal{L}}
. We consider a test particle that moves at the speed
{\mathbf{v}}
with
\|{\mathbf{v}}\|=1
in the space
{\mathcal{K}}_{r}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}\backslash({\mathcal{L}}_{r}+r{\mathcal{B}}^{d}_{1})
. Assume that there is no external potential such that the particle always moves in straight lines until it hits an obstacle, and we also assume that all the collisions are elastic.

Denote by
{\mathbf{q}}_{n}={\mathbf{q}}_{n}({\mathbf{q}}_{0},{\mathbf{v}}_{0})\in\partial{\mathcal{K}}_{r}
, the location where the test particle with initial condition
({\mathbf{q}}_{0},{\mathbf{v}}_{0})
leaves the nth scatterer. Assume that
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\in\partial{\mathcal{K}}_{r}
, such that the distribution of
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
can be obtained from
{\mathbf{v}}_{0}
. For the continuous time version, define
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}={\mathbf{x}}_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{0},{\mathbf{v}}_{0})\in{\mathcal{K}}_{r}
to be the position of the test particle at time
t
with initial condition
({\mathbf{x}}_{0},{\mathbf{v}}_{0})
.

In
[
18
]
, the authors proved the Boltzmann-Grad limit of the periodic Lorentz gas in any dimension, which is reformulated in Theorem 3.1,
[
21
]
as follows.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
.

1.

Assume
{\mathbf{v}}_{0}
is distributed according to an absolutely continuous Borel probability measure
\lambda
on
S^{d-1}_{1}
. Then there exist a process
{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
{\mathbf{Q}}_{0}=0
such that as
r\rightarrow 0
, we have

{\mathbf{q}}_{n}-{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\Rightarrow{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}.

2.

Assume
({\mathbf{x}}_{0},{\mathbf{v}}_{0})
is distributed according to an absolutely continuous Borel probability measure
\Lambda
on
{\mathcal{K}}_{r}\times S^{d-1}_{1}
. Then there exist a process
{\mathbf{X}}_{t}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
{\mathbf{X}}_{0}=0
such that as
r\rightarrow 0
, we have

{\mathbf{x}}_{t}-{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\Rightarrow{\mathbf{X}}_{t}.

Then with Theorem
1.1
, the authors established the superdiffusive central limit theorem of the periodic Lorentz gas, first under the limit of
r\rightarrow 0
and then
t\rightarrow\infty
. We stress that the order of the limits indicates that we first obtain the Boltzmann-Grad limit, and then study the long time behavior of the Boltzmann-Grad limit, which turns out to be a superdiffusion. As we have explained in the introduction, at present we do not know any results when taking the limits
r\rightarrow 0
and
t\rightarrow\infty
at the same time, or changing the order of the limits.

We now provide more details on the Boltzmann-Grad limit. Indeed, the displacements
{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}
and
{\mathbf{X}}_{t}
can be constructed from a Markov chain. To see this, define the Markov chain
(\xi_{n},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
on the state space
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\times{\mathcal{B}}^{d}_{1}
, whose transition probability given by

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}((\xi_{n},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{n})\in{\mathcal{A}}|(\xi_{n-1},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{n-1}))=\int_{{\mathcal{A}}}\Phi_{0}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{n-1},x,{\mathbf{z}})dxd{\mathbf{z}},

(2.5)

where
\Phi_{0}({\boldsymbol{\omega}},x,{\mathbf{z}})
is the transition kernel, which is independent of the scattering map,
{\mathcal{L}}
and the initial data. Here

is the free path length between each collision, and

\displaystyle{\mathbf{V}}_{n}=R({\mathbf{v}}_{0})S({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{1})\dots S({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{n}){\mathbf{e}}_{1},\ \ \ {\mathbf{V}}_{0}={\mathbf{v}}_{0};

where

are matrices decided by the scattering map. For their explicit expressions and the proof, see Lemma 2.1 in
[
21
]
.

Let

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{j=1}\xi_{j}{\mathbf{V}}_{j-1},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{X}}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Q}}_{\nu_{t}}+(t-\tau_{\nu_{t}}){\mathbf{V}}_{\nu_{t}},

be the distribution limits of
{\mathbf{q}}_{n}-{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}-{\mathbf{x}}_{0}

As in
[
21
]
, define

\displaystyle\tau_{n}=\sum^{n}_{i=1}\xi_{i}

\displaystyle\nu_{t}=\max\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0};\tau_{n}\leq t\}

.

In
[
18
]
it was proved that
(\xi_{1},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{1})
is distributed according to

\Phi_{0}(x,{\mathbf{z}})=\frac{1}{v_{d-1}}\int_{{\mathcal{B}}^{d}_{1}}\Phi_{0}({\boldsymbol{\omega}},x,{\mathbf{z}})d{\boldsymbol{\omega}}.

Moreover, by the property of
\Phi_{0}({\boldsymbol{\omega}},x,{\mathbf{z}})
, it can be verified that
\Phi_{0}(x,{\mathbf{z}})
is the the stationary measure of the process
{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}

Since
{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}
and
{\mathbf{X}}_{t}
are independent of the initial data
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
and
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
, the following assumptions (noted as
{\mathbf{A}}
) were made on the initial data in
[
21
]
, which

1.

The initial velocity
{\mathbf{v}}_{0}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d}_{1}
is fixed,

2.

(\xi_{1},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{1})
is distributed according to

.

Actually in
[

]
the central limit theorem can be proved for more general initial data. In this article we adopt the assumptions

in our main results for simplicity, although they also hold for the general initial data as in
[

]
.

From (
2.5
) we can see that
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=\{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{n}\}^{\infty}_{n=1}
itself is a Markov chain on
{\mathcal{B}}^{d-1}_{1}
with the transition probability

{\mathbb{P}}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{n}\in{\mathcal{A}}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{n-1})=\int_{{\mathcal{A}}}K_{0}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{n-1},{\mathbf{z}})d{\mathbf{z}},

where

By the fact that

is uniformly distributed on

, and the uniform measure on
{\mathcal{B}}^{d}_{1}

.

Let

\Phi_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{v_{d-1}}\int_{{\mathcal{B}}^{d}_{1}}\int_{{\mathcal{B}}^{d}_{1}}\Phi_{0}({\boldsymbol{\omega}},x,{\mathbf{z}})d{\boldsymbol{\omega}}d{\mathbf{z}}

be the distribution density of

. In Theorem 1.14,

]
, the authors showed the asymptotic expansion formula of

. More precisely, when

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}(x)=\Theta_{d}x^{-3}+O(x^{-3-\frac{2}{d}})\times\log x,

and when

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}(x)=\Theta_{d}x^{-3}+O(x^{-3-\frac{2}{d}}),

where

.

2.2
Stein’s method and exchangeable pairs

In this part, we present some elementary facts and estimates on Stein’s method for multivariate normal approximation, which will be applied later in this paper. Then we give a brief description on how the technique of exchangeable pair works. For more details, see Chen-Shao
[
10
]
and Chatterjee
[
8
]
.

Stein’s method for normal approximation is a tool to estimate the difference between the distribution of a random variable
{\mathbf{w}}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and the standard Gaussian random variable
{\mathbf{Z}}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, in the sense that

\sup_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}|{\mathbb{E}}h({\mathbf{w}})-{\mathbb{E}}h({\mathbf{Z}})|,

where
{\mathcal{H}}
is a given function space on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

As we have explained in the introduction, the first step in Stein’s method is the following Stein’s equation, which turns the difference
h({\mathbf{w}})-{\mathbb{E}}h({\mathbf{Z}})
into the values with respect to functions of another function space.
More precisely, the multivariate Stein’s equation for
h
is given by

\displaystyle h({\mathbf{w}})-{\mathbb{E}}h({\mathbf{Z}})=\Delta f_{h}({\mathbf{w}})-{\mathbf{w}}\cdot\nabla f_{h}({\mathbf{w}}),

(2.8)

where the solution to the Stein’s equation
f_{h}
is a function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Notice that the right hand side of this equation is just the O-U operator acting on
f_{h}
.

Assume
h\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, the solution to (
2.8
) can be explicitly expressed by

f_{h}({\mathbf{w}})=-\int^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{E}}(h({\mathbf{w}}e^{-u}+\sqrt{1-e^{-2u}}{\mathbf{Z}}))-{\mathbb{E}}(h({\mathbf{Z}})))du.

In the following we quote the bounds of the derivatives of
f_{h}
from Lemma 2 in
[
22
]
and Lemma 3 in
[
9
]
.

For
k\geq 1
,
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, denote the kth derivative of a function
f\in{\mathcal{C}}^{k}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
at
{\mathbf{x}}
by
D^{k}f({\mathbf{x}})
, and the inner product
\langle D^{k}f({\mathbf{x}}),({\mathbf{u}}_{1},{\mathbf{u}}_{2},\dots,{\mathbf{u}}_{k})\rangle
by

\langle D^{k}f({\mathbf{x}}),({\mathbf{u}}_{1},{\mathbf{u}}_{2},\dots,{\mathbf{u}}_{k})\rangle=\sum^{d}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}=1}\frac{\partial^{k}f({\mathbf{x}})}{\partial_{x_{i_{1}}}\partial_{x_{i_{2}}}\dots\partial_{x_{i_{k}}}}({\mathbf{u}}_{1})_{i_{1}}\dots({\mathbf{u}}_{k})_{i_{k}},

where
{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Define the operator norm of
D^{k}f({\mathbf{x}})
by

\|D^{k}f({\mathbf{x}})\|=\sup_{{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\{|\langle D^{k}f({\mathbf{x}}),({\mathbf{u}}_{1},{\mathbf{u}}_{2},\dots,{\mathbf{u}}_{k})\rangle|,|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}|=\dots=|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}|=1\}.

Lemma 2.2
.

The following estimates hold for the solution
f_{h}
to the Stein’s equation (
2.8
) with respect to
h
.

•

If
h\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|Df_{h}({\mathbf{x}})\|\leq\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sup_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|Dh({\mathbf{x}})\|,

(2.9)

•

If
h\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|{\rm Hess}f_{h}({\mathbf{x}})\|_{H.S.}\leq\sup_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|Dh({\mathbf{x}})\|,

(2.10)

where for a matrix
A
,
\|A\|_{H.S.}:=\sqrt{{\rm Tr}(AA^{t})}
.

•

If
h\in{\mathcal{C}}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|D^{3}f_{h}({\mathbf{x}})\|\leq\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{4}\sup_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|D^{2}h({\mathbf{x}})\|.

(2.11)

Now we explain how the method of exchangeable pairs works. We start with the definition of the exchangeable pairs.

Definition 2.3

(exchangeable pairs)
.

Let
{\mathbf{W}}
and
{\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}
be two identically distributed random vectors on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
on the same probability space. We call
({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{W}}^{\prime})
an exchangeable pair if
({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{W}}^{\prime})
has the same distribution as
({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{W}})
.

In the univariate case, to apply the method of exchangeable pairs, we need the exchangeable pair
(W,W^{\prime})
to satisfy the "linear regression condition", i.e.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(W^{\prime}-W|W)=-\lambda W

(2.12)

holds for some constant
\lambda
. In the multivariate case, there are various analogues to this linear condition (
2.12
), see for example
[
9
]
and
[
24
]
. In this paper we employ the conditions in Theorem 4,
[
9
]
, which is enough for our case. More precisely, there exist constants
\lambda
,
\sigma
and a random matrix
E
, such that the following two conditions hold

•

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}}|{\mathbf{W}})=-\lambda{\mathbf{W}},

(2.13)

•

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}})({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}})^{t}|{\mathbf{W}})=2\lambda\sigma^{2}{\rm{Id}}_{d}+2\lambda{\mathbb{E}}(E|{\mathbf{W}}).

(2.14)

Notice that by definition, the exchangeable pair
({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{W}}^{\prime})
has the same distribution as that of
({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{W}})
. A direct consequence is that for any anti-symmetric function
g
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}g({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{W}}^{\prime})=0.

To explain how the exchangeable pair works, we do the following heuristic computation. Let
f
be a function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, given by

\displaystyle f({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})=\langle({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{x}}),\nabla f_{h}({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})+\nabla f_{h}({\mathbf{x}})\rangle,

where
f_{h}
is the solution to the Stein’s equation (
2.8
) and we assume that
f_{h}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{3}_{b}
. Since
f
is anti-symmetric, we derive that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}}),\nabla f_{h}({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime})+\nabla f_{h}({\mathbf{W}})\rangle]

(2.15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}}),\nabla f_{h}({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime})-\nabla f_{h}({\mathbf{W}})\rangle+2\langle({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}}),\nabla f_{h}({\mathbf{W}})\rangle]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}})({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}})^{t},{\rm Hess}f_{h}({\mathbf{W}})\rangle+2\langle({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}}),\nabla f_{h}({\mathbf{W}})\rangle+R],

where
R
is the error in Taylor expansion, given by

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum^{d}_{j,k,l=1}D^{3}_{jkl}f_{h}({\mathbf{W}}+\tau({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}}))({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}})_{j}({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}})_{k}({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}})_{l},

for some
\tau\in(0,1)
.
Thus by Stein’s equation (
2.8
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}h({\mathbf{W}})-{\mathbb{E}}h({\mathbf{Z}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\Delta f_{h}({\mathbf{W}})-{\mathbf{W}}\cdot\nabla f_{h}({\mathbf{W}})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\rm{Id}}-{\mathbb{E}}(({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}})({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}})^{t}|{\mathbf{W}}),{\rm Hess}f_{h}({\mathbf{W}})\rangle]

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}[\langle({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}})({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}})^{t},{\rm Hess}f_{h}({\mathbf{W}})\rangle]

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}[\langle-2{\mathbb{E}}(({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}})|{\mathbf{W}})-{\mathbf{W}},\nabla f_{h}({\mathbf{W}})\rangle+2\langle({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}}),\nabla f_{h}({\mathbf{W}})\rangle]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\rm{Id}}-{\mathbb{E}}(({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}})({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}})^{t}|{\mathbf{W}}),{\rm Hess}f_{h}({\mathbf{W}})\rangle]

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}[\langle-2{\mathbb{E}}(({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{W}})|{\mathbf{W}})-{\mathbf{W}},\nabla f_{h}({\mathbf{W}})\rangle]-{\mathbb{E}}R

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle(1-2\lambda\sigma^{2}){\rm{Id}}_{d}-2\lambda{\mathbb{E}}(E|{\mathbf{W}}),{\rm Hess}f_{h}({\mathbf{W}})\rangle]

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}[\langle(2\lambda-1){\mathbf{W}},\nabla f_{h}({\mathbf{W}})\rangle]-{\mathbb{E}}R,

where in the last line is due to (
2.15
) and the conditions (
2.13
), (
2.14
). Thus we conclude that the estimate of
|{\mathbb{E}}h({\mathbf{W}})-{\mathbb{E}}h({\mathbf{Z}})|
boils down to obtain the bounds on
M_{1}f_{h}
,
M_{2}f_{h}
,
M_{3}f_{h}
and
{\mathbb{E}}(E|{\mathbf{W}})
, and also the moment estimates of
{\mathbf{W}}
.

We point out that the above discussions could not directly apply to the Boltzmann-Grad limit of the periodic Lorentz gas. In the proof of the main Theorem
1.2
(Section 4), we conduct the truncation procedure and then take the conditional expectations before employing the Stein’s method.

3
Key estimates

In this section we provide the key estimates to control the error term in Stein’s method. They are analogous to the moment bounds in the classical settings, as we explain in Section 2.2.

First we adopt the truncation procedure in
[
21
]
. More precisely, for
1\leq i\leq n
, let

r^{2}_{i,n}=n(\log n)^{\gamma}
with
0<\gamma<1
and define

\xi^{\prime}_{i,n}=\xi_{i}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{\xi_{i}^{2}\leq r^{2}_{i,n}\}}

and

{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}_{n}=\sum^{n}_{i=1}\xi^{\prime}_{i,n}{\mathbf{V}}_{i-1},\ \ \ {\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\log n}}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}_{n}.

Notice that by the asymptotic expansion formula (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) of
\Phi_{0}
we deduce that

\displaystyle\sup_{n}{\mathbb{E}}\|{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}-{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}_{n}\|\leq{\mathbb{E}}(\xi_{1})+\sum^{n}_{i=2}{\mathbb{E}}(\xi_{i}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{\xi^{2}_{i}\geq r^{2}_{i,n}\}})

(3.16)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+\sum^{n}_{i=2}\int^{\infty}_{n^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log n)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}}(x^{-2}+O(x^{-2-\frac{2}{d}}\log x))dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(\frac{n^{\frac{1}{2}}}{(\log n)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}}),

so that

\displaystyle\sup_{n}{\mathbb{E}}\|{\mathbf{W}}_{n}-{\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}_{n}\|=O(\frac{1}{(\log n)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2}}}).

(3.17)

As in
[
21
]
, we divide
\xi^{\prime}_{i,n}
into two parts,

\xi^{\prime}_{i,n}=\tilde{\xi}_{i,n}+m_{i,n},

where

m_{i,n}={\mathbb{E}}(\xi^{\prime}_{i,n}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}),\ \ \ \ \ \tilde{\xi}_{i,n}=\xi^{\prime}_{i,n}-m_{i,n}.

The rest of this section is devoted to the moment estimates and the key estimates, which are due to the spectral gap of the transition kernels.

3.1
The moment estimates

In this section, we prove the moment estimates for
\xi^{\prime}_{i,n}
,
m_{i,n}
and
{\mathbb{E}}(\xi^{\prime k}_{i,n}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})
. These estimates are the consequence of the asymptotic behavior of
\Phi_{0}({\boldsymbol{\omega}},x,{\mathbf{z}})
, which was proved in
[
20
]
. Although some of the estimates have appeared in
[
21
]
, for the completeness of this paper we quote them here without proof. Notice that our truncation bound
r_{i,n}
is slightly different from theirs, but the proof is the same.

Lemma 3.1
.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left((\xi_{i,n}^{\prime})^{2}\right)=d\sigma^{2}_{d}\log n+O(\log\log n),

(3.18)

where the constant
\sigma^{2}_{d}=\frac{2^{1-d}}{d^{2}(d+1)\zeta(d)}
, and
\zeta(d)=\sum^{d}_{n=1}n^{-d}
is the Riemann zeta function.

For
k\geq 3
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left((\xi_{i,n}^{\prime})^{k}\right)=O(r^{k-2}_{i,n}).

(3.19)

Proof. —
This is a direct consequence of the asymptotic expansion formulas (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) of
\Phi_{0}(x)
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(({\xi}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i})^{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{r_{i,n}}_{0}x^{k}\Phi_{0}(x)dx=\int^{r_{i,n}}_{0}x^{k}(\Theta_{d}x^{-3}+O(x^{-3-\frac{2}{d}}))dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(r^{k-2}_{i,n}).

Also see Proposition 6.10 in
[
21
]
for (
3.18
).

Lemma 3.2
.

For
k\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{E}}(m^{k}_{i,n})|<O(n^{\frac{k-2}{2}}(\log n)^{\frac{\gamma(k-2)}{2}}).

In particular, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}m_{i,n}=\bar{\xi}+O(\frac{1}{r_{i,n}}),

(3.20)

where
\bar{\xi}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}{\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}
is the limit of the mean free path length as
r\rightarrow 0
.

As for
d=2
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}m^{2}_{i,n}=O(\log\log n),

(3.21)

while for
d\geq 3
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}m^{2}_{i,n}=O(1).

(3.22)

Proof. —
The estimates (
3.20
), (
3.21
) and (
3.22
) are proven in Proposition 6.8, 6.9 in
[
21
]
.

Recall Prop 6.7 in
[
21
]
that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(m_{i,n}>u)=O(u^{-(1+\frac{d}{2})}){\mathbf{1}}_{u\leq r_{i,n}}.

Thus we derive that

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{E}}(m^{k}_{i,n})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\int^{r_{i,n}}_{0}ku^{k-1}{\mathbb{P}}(m_{i}>u)du|=O(r_{i,n}^{k-1-\frac{d}{2}})<O(n^{\frac{k-2}{2}}(\log n)^{\frac{\gamma(k-2)}{2}}).

Lemma 3.3
.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}({\mathbb{E}}(\tilde{\xi}_{i,n}^{2}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})^{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O(r^{2}_{i,n})=O(n(\log n)^{\gamma}),

(3.23)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}({\mathbb{E}}(\tilde{\xi}_{i,n}^{3}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})^{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O(r^{4}_{i,n})=O(n^{2}(\log n)^{2\gamma}).

(3.24)

Proof. —

The first estimate (
3.23
) is proved in Lemma 6.11 in
[
21
]
.

As for the second one, notice that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}({\mathbb{E}}(\tilde{\xi}_{i,n}^{3}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}({\mathbb{E}}((\xi^{\prime}_{i,n}-m_{i,n})^{3}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})^{2})

(3.25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big({\mathbb{E}}((\xi^{\prime}_{i,n})^{3}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})-3{\mathbb{E}}((\xi^{\prime}_{i,n})^{2}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})m_{i,n}+2m^{3}_{i,n}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C{\mathbb{E}}\big({\mathbb{E}}((\xi^{\prime}_{i,n})^{3}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}((\xi^{\prime}_{i,n})^{2}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})^{2}m^{2}_{i,n}+m^{6}_{i,n}\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\big({\mathbb{E}}({\mathbb{E}}((\xi^{\prime}_{i,n})^{3}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})^{2})+{\mathbb{E}}({\mathbb{E}}((\xi^{\prime}_{i,n})^{2}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})^{3})+{\mathbb{E}}m^{6}_{i,n}\big),

where the last line is due to Young’s inequality, i.e.

{\mathbb{E}}({\mathbb{E}}((\xi^{\prime}_{i,n})^{2}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})^{2}m^{2}_{i,n})\leq\frac{2}{3}{\mathbb{E}}({\mathbb{E}}((\xi^{\prime}_{i,n})^{2}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})^{3}+\frac{1}{3}{\mathbb{E}}m^{6}_{i,n}.

Thanks to (
3.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}m^{6}_{i,n}\leq O(r^{4}_{i,n})=O(n^{2}(\log n)^{2\gamma}).

(3.26)

Now we turn to the estimates of
{\mathbb{E}}({\mathbb{E}}((\xi^{\prime}_{i})^{3}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})^{2})
and
{\mathbb{E}}({\mathbb{E}}((\xi^{\prime}_{i})^{2}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})^{3})
.

For
{\mathbb{E}}({\mathbb{E}}((\xi^{\prime}_{i})^{3}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})^{2})
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}({\mathbb{E}}((\xi^{\prime}_{i})^{3}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v_{d-1}}\int_{{\mathcal{B}}^{d-1}_{1}}\int_{{\mathcal{B}}^{d-1}_{1}}(\int^{r_{i}}_{0}x^{3}\Phi_{0}({\boldsymbol{\omega}},x,{\mathbf{z}})dx)^{2}\frac{1}{K_{0}({\boldsymbol{\omega}},{\mathbf{z}})}d{\boldsymbol{\omega}}d{\mathbf{z}}

(3.27)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{{\mathcal{B}}^{d-1}_{1}}\int_{{\mathcal{B}}^{d-1}_{1}}(\int^{r_{i}}_{0}x^{6}\Phi_{0}({\boldsymbol{\omega}},x,{\mathbf{z}})dx)(\int^{r_{i}}_{0}\Phi_{0}({\boldsymbol{\omega}},x,{\mathbf{z}})dx)\frac{1}{K_{0}({\boldsymbol{\omega}},{\mathbf{z}})}d{\boldsymbol{\omega}}d{\mathbf{z}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(r^{4}_{i,n})=O(n^{2}(\log n)^{2\gamma}).

Then
{\mathbb{E}}({\mathbb{E}}((\xi^{\prime}_{i})^{2}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})^{3})
can be estimated in the same way.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}({\mathbb{E}}((\xi^{\prime}_{i})^{2}|{\boldsymbol{\eta}})^{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v_{d-1}}\int_{{\mathcal{B}}^{d-1}_{1}}\int_{{\mathcal{B}}^{d-1}_{1}}(\int^{r_{i}}_{0}x^{2}\Phi_{0}({\boldsymbol{\omega}},x,{\mathbf{z}})dx)^{3}\frac{1}{K_{0}({\boldsymbol{\omega}},{\mathbf{z}})^{2}}d{\boldsymbol{\omega}}d{\mathbf{z}}

(3.28)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O(r^{4}_{i,n})=O(n^{2}(\log n)^{2\gamma}).

Combining (
3.26
), (
3.27
) and (
3.28
), we deduce (
3.24
).

Lemma 3.4
.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\tilde{\xi}_{i,n}^{2}=d\sigma^{2}_{d}\log n+O(\log\log n),

(3.29)

and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\tilde{\xi}_{i,n}^{4}=O(r^{2}_{i,n})=O(n(\log n)^{\gamma}).

(3.30)

Proof. —
Notice that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\tilde{\xi}_{i,n}^{4}={\mathbb{E}}(\xi^{{}^{\prime}}_{i,n}-m_{i,n})^{4}\leq C({\mathbb{E}}(\xi^{{}^{\prime}}_{i,n})^{4}+{\mathbb{E}}(m_{i,n}^{4})),

such that by (
3.19
) and (
3.2
), we obtain (
3.30
).

3.2
The key estimates

In this section our aim is to estimate expectations in the form of

{\mathbb{E}}(\sum^{n}_{i=1}(e_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})(e_{k}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})(e_{l}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})f({\mathbf{V}}_{i},\dots,{\mathbf{V}}_{i+m})),

which will appear in the error term when applying Stein’s method. Here
1\leq j,k,l\leq d
,
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We will define the precise function space of
f
below.

Recall that in
[
21
]
, the authors proved an exponential mixing estimates and gave estimates of
{\mathbb{E}}((e_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{n})f({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{n},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{n+m}))
and
{\mathbb{E}}((e_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{n})^{2}f({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{n},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{n+m}))
, as a consequence of the spectral gap of the transition operator of
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
. More precisely, they define the operator
P
on the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}=L^{2}({\mathcal{B}}^{d-1}_{1},V,\frac{1}{v_{d-1}}d{\boldsymbol{\omega}})
,

Pf({\boldsymbol{\omega}})={\mathbb{E}}(f({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{n})|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{n-1}={\boldsymbol{\omega}})=\int_{{\mathcal{B}}^{d-1}_{1}}K_{0}({\boldsymbol{\omega}},{\mathbf{z}})f({\mathbf{z}})d{\mathbf{z}}.

where
V
is a finite dimension vector space, and prove that
P
has the spectral gap
1-\omega_{0}
with
\omega_{0}<1
by Doeblin theory.

In this paper we consider
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{n}=({\mathbf{V}}_{n-1},{\mathbf{V}}_{n},{\mathbf{V}}_{n+1})
as a stationary Markov chain, which is a direct consequence of the results in
[
18
]
, as pointed out in
[
21
]
. It was proved in Prop 13.4,
[
21
]
that
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{n}
also admits a spectral gap. Indeed, define

{\mathcal{V}}=\{({\mathbf{v}}_{n-1},{\mathbf{v}}_{n},{\mathbf{v}}_{n+1})\in(S^{d-1}_{1})^{3},\phi({\mathbf{v}}_{n-1},{\mathbf{v}}_{n})>B_{\theta},\phi({\mathbf{v}}_{n},{\mathbf{v}}_{n+1})>B_{\theta}\},

where
\phi({\mathbf{u}}_{1},{\mathbf{u}}_{2})\in[0,\pi]
denotes the angle between two vectors
{\mathbf{u}}_{1},{\mathbf{u}}_{2}
, and
B_{\theta}=\inf_{\omega\in[0,1)}|\theta(\omega)|
is the minimal deflection angle associated with the scattering angle
\theta(\omega)
. To ensure that the Boltzmann-Grad limit of the periodic Lorentz gas exists, see assumptions
(A)
,
(B)
on
\theta
in Section 2,
[
21
]
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}
be the transition operator of
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{n}
, given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}f({\mathbf{z}})={\mathbb{E}}(f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{n})|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{n-1}={\mathbf{z}})=\int_{{\mathcal{V}}}{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}({\mathbf{z}},{\boldsymbol{\omega}})f({\boldsymbol{\omega}})d\mu({\boldsymbol{\omega}}),

where
f\in L^{2}({\mathcal{V}},d\mu)
,
{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}
is the transition kernel of
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{n}
and
d\mu({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{n})
is its stationary measure. For the explicit expressions of
d\mu({\boldsymbol{\omega}})
and
{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}({\mathbf{z}},{\boldsymbol{\omega}})
, see
[
21
]
and
[
18
]
.

It should be pointed out that both
{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}({\mathbf{z}},{\boldsymbol{\omega}})
and
d\mu
are invariant under rotation, i.e. for
R\in SO(d)
, we have

{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}(R{\mathbf{z}},R{\boldsymbol{\omega}})={\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}({\mathbf{z}},{\boldsymbol{\omega}}),\ \ \ d\mu(R{\boldsymbol{\omega}})=d\mu({\boldsymbol{\omega}}).

Moreover, it is shown in
[
21
]
that the classical Doeblin theory leads to the spectral gap of
{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}
, denoted by
1-\omega_{{\mathbf{v}}}
with
\omega_{{\mathbf{v}}}=\|{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}-\Pi_{{\mathbf{v}}}\|<1
, where
\|\cdot\|
is the operator norm in
L^{2}({\mathcal{V}},\mu)
. In addition, define

\displaystyle\Pi_{{\mathbf{v}}}f({\boldsymbol{\omega}})=\int_{{\mathcal{V}}}f({\mathbf{z}})d\mu({\mathbf{z}}),

then we have

\displaystyle\Pi_{{\mathbf{v}}}{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}={\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}\Pi_{{\mathbf{v}}}=\Pi_{{\mathbf{v}}}.

(3.31)

Let
L^{2}_{0}({\mathcal{V}},\mu)
be the orthogonal complement of the constant functions in
L^{2}({\mathcal{V}},\mu)
. Then for any
f\in L^{2}_{0}({\mathcal{V}},\mu)
, there exists
g\in L^{2}({\mathcal{V}},\mu)
such that
f=({\mathcal{I}}-{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathbf{v}}})g
.

Denote by
\|f\|^{2}_{2}=\int_{{\mathcal{V}}}|f({\mathbf{z}})|^{2}d\mu({\mathbf{z}})
the norm of
L^{2}({\mathcal{V}},\mu)
. The following lemma is a direct consequence of spectral gap, of which we omit the proof.

Lemma 3.5
.

For any
f\in L^{2}({\mathcal{V}},\mu)
, we have

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{P}}^{n}_{{\mathbf{v}}}f-\Pi_{{\mathbf{v}}}f\|_{2}\leq C\omega^{n}_{{\mathbf{v}}}\|f\|_{2}.

(3.32)

Now we turn to the main estimate of this section. We say a function
f:{\mathcal{V}}^{m+1}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is rotation-invariant if for any
R\in SO(d)
,

f(R{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{0},\dots,R{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{m})=f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{m}),

where
R{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{n}:=(R{\mathbf{V}}_{n-1},R{\mathbf{V}}_{n},R{\mathbf{V}}_{n+1})
.

Proposition 3.6
.

For measurable and rotation-invariant functions
f:{\mathcal{V}}^{m+1}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
with

\|f\|_{2}^{2}:={\mathbb{E}}(f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{m})^{2})<\infty,

we have

\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{i=1}|{\mathbb{E}}(\big(({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})^{2}-\frac{1}{d}\big)f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i+m}))|\leq C_{2,2}\|f\|_{2},

(3.33)

where
C_{2}
is a constant independent of
n
, and for
j\neq k
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})({\mathbf{e}}_{k}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i+m}))=0.

(3.34)

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})({\mathbf{e}}_{k}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})({\mathbf{e}}_{l}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i+m}))=0.

(3.35)

Proof. —
We begin with the simplest case. Notice that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i+m}))={\mathbb{E}}(({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i}){\mathbb{E}}(f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i+m})|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})).

Let
\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i}):={\mathbb{E}}(f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i+m})|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i}))
, then by the rotation invariances of
f
,
{\mathcal{K}}({\mathbf{z}},{\boldsymbol{\omega}})
and
d\mu
we conclude that
\tilde{f}
is also rotation-invariant, i.e. for any
R\in SO(d)
,

\displaystyle\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})=\tilde{f}(R{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i}).

By taking
R=-{\rm{Id}}
, it is easy to verify that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i+m}))=\int_{{\mathcal{V}}}({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})d\mu({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})=0.

(3.36)

Now we prove the estimates (
3.34
), (
3.33
). By the rotation-invariance, we have for any
R\in SO(d)
,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{V}}}({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})^{2}\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})d\mu({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})=\int_{{\mathcal{V}}}({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot R{\mathbf{V}}_{i})^{2}\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})d\mu({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})=\int_{{\mathcal{V}}}(R^{t}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})^{2}\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})d\mu({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i}),

thus by choosing
R^{t}_{k}\in SO(d)
for
k=1,\dots,d
, such that
R^{t}_{k}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}={\mathbf{e}}_{k}
and the fact
\|{\mathbf{V}}_{i}\|=1
, we derive that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{V}}}({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})^{2}\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})d\mu({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})

(3.37)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sum^{d}_{k=1}\int_{{\mathcal{V}}}({\mathbf{e}}_{k}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})^{2}\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})d\mu({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})=\frac{1}{d}\int_{{\mathcal{V}}}\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})d\mu({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i}).

Thus let
g({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})=({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})^{2}\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})
and with (
3.37
) we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(\big(({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})^{2}-\frac{1}{d}\big)f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i+m}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}({\mathbb{E}}(({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})^{2}\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{0}))-\frac{1}{d}{\mathbb{E}}f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i+m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big({\mathcal{P}}^{i}_{{\mathbf{v}}}g({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{0})-\Pi_{{\mathbf{v}}}g\big).

Therefore, Lemma
3.5
directly leads to

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{E}}(\big(({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})^{2}-\frac{1}{d}\big)f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i+m}))|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{E}}\big({\mathcal{P}}^{i}_{{\mathbf{v}}}g({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{0})-\Pi_{{\mathbf{v}}}g\big)|\leq\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}|{\mathcal{P}}^{i}_{{\mathbf{v}}}g({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{0})-\Pi_{{\mathbf{v}}}g|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\omega^{i}_{{\mathbf{v}}}\|g\|_{2}\leq C\omega^{i}_{{\mathbf{v}}}\|f\|_{2},

implying that

\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{i=1}|{\mathbb{E}}(\big(({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})^{2}-\frac{1}{d}\big)f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i+m}))|\leq C_{2}\|f\|_{2}.

The formula (
3.33
) can be obtained as soon as we notice that
for
j\neq k
the rotation invariance gives rise to

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{V}}}({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})d\mu({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{V}}}({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot R{\mathbf{V}}_{i})({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot R{\mathbf{V}}_{i})\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})d\mu({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i}).

Then by choosing
R\in SO(d)
such that

\displaystyle R^{t}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}={\mathbf{e}}_{i},\ \ \ R^{t}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}=-{\mathbf{e}}_{j},

we get

\int_{{\mathcal{V}}}({\mathbf{e}}_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})d\mu({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})=-\int_{{\mathcal{V}}}({\mathbf{e}}_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})d\mu({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i}),

leading to

{\mathbb{E}}(({\mathbf{e}}_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})({\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{V}}_{i})f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i+m}))=0.

As for (

Lemma 5.1

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}\|=O(\sqrt{n\log n}).

Also (

\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{i\neq j}|{\mathbb{E}}\xi^{\prime}_{i,n}\xi^{\prime}_{j,n}\langle{\mathbf{e}}_{k},{\mathbf{V}}_{i-1}\rangle\langle{\mathbf{e}}_{k},{\mathbf{V}}_{j-1}\rangle|

For the second term, we separate it into two parts,

[
21

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(|\nu_{t}-n_{t}|\geq A_{t})\leq O(\frac{t\log t}{A^{2}_{t}}).

(5.69)

Proof. —
Notice that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(|\nu_{t}-n_{t}|\geq A_{t})\leq{\mathbb{P}}(n_{t}-\nu_{t}\geq A_{t})+{\mathbb{P}}(\nu_{t}-n_{t}\geq A_{t}).

For the first term, we require that

c_{t}:=n_{t}-A_{t}>0,

otherwise
{\mathbb{P}}(n_{t}-\nu_{t}\geq A_{t})=0
. Then we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(n_{t}-\nu_{t}\geq A_{t})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\frac{n_{t}-\nu_{t}}{\sqrt{\nu_{t}\log\nu_{t}}}\geq\frac{A_{t}}{\sqrt{c_{t}\log c_{t}}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{c_{t}\log c_{t}}{A^{2}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{|n_{t}-\nu_{t}|^{2}}{\nu_{t}\log\nu_{t}}=O(\frac{t\log t}{A^{2}_{t}}),

where the last line is due to (
5.68
).

Let
m_{t}:=n_{t}+A_{t}
, and we derive that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\nu_{t}-n_{t}\geq A_{t})={\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{m_{t}}\leq t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{m_{t}}-m_{t}\bar{\xi}\leq-A_{t}\bar{\xi})\leq{\mathbb{P}}(|\tau_{m_{t}}-m_{t}\bar{\xi}|\geq A_{t}\bar{\xi})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{E}}|\tau_{m_{t}}-m_{t}\bar{\xi}|^{2}}{A^{2}_{t}\bar{\xi}^{2}}=O(\frac{t\log t}{A^{2}_{t}}),

which finishes our proof.

Now we are ready to estimate
I_{2}
. By (
5.69
), we have

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(|\nu_{t}-n_{t}|\geq A_{t})+{\mathbb{P}}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{t\log t}}(\|{\mathbf{Q}}_{\nu_{t}}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{n_{t}}\|\geq\frac{z_{1}}{3},|\nu_{t}-n_{t}|\leq A_{t})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O(\frac{t\log t}{A^{2}_{t}})+\frac{C}{z_{1}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t\log t}}{\mathbb{E}}(\|{\mathbf{Q}}_{\nu_{t}}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{n_{t}}\|,|\nu_{t}-n_{t}|\leq A_{t}).

By the stationarity of
{\mathbf{Q}}_{n}
and (
5.65
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(\|{\mathbf{Q}}_{\nu_{t}}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{n_{t}}\|,|\nu_{t}-n_{t}|\leq A_{t})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(\max_{1\leq k\leq[A_{t}]}\|{\mathbf{Q}}_{n_{t}+k}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{n_{t}}\|,|\nu_{t}-n_{t}|\leq A_{t})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(\max_{1\leq k\leq[A_{t}]}\|{\mathbf{Q}}_{k}\|)=\sqrt{A_{t}\log A_{t}},

such that

\displaystyle I_{2}\leq O(\frac{t\log t}{A^{2}_{t}})+\frac{C}{z_{1}}\frac{\sqrt{A_{t}\log A_{t}}}{\sqrt{t\log t}}.

Then by taking
A_{t}=t^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}
, we have

\displaystyle I_{2}\leq O(\frac{|\log t|}{t^{\epsilon}}\vee\frac{1}{z_{1}t^{\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\epsilon}{2}}}).

(5.70)

In the following we prove Theorem
1.3
.

Proof. —
Putting (
5.63
), (
5.70

5.62
), we get that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\|{\mathbf{W}}_{t}-{\mathbf{W}}_{n_{t}}\|\geq z_{1})\leq O(\frac{1}{z_{1}|\log t|}),

such that by (
5.59
) and (
5.60
) (or (
5.61

z_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\log t|}}

d\geq 3

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{P}}({\mathbf{W}}_{t}\geq{\mathbf{z}})-|{\mathbb{P}}({\mathbf{Z}}\geq{\mathbf{z}})|\leq O(\frac{1}{(|\log t|)^{\frac{1}{4}}}),

and for
d=2

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{P}}({\mathbf{W}}_{t}\geq{\mathbf{z}})-|{\mathbb{P}}({\mathbf{Z}}\geq{\mathbf{z}})|\leq O((|\frac{\log\log t}{\log t}|)^{\frac{1}{4}}).

Remark 5.4
.

We point out that we are not able to prove the convergence rate for Lipschitz functions, as the analogue of Therorem
1.2
for the continuous time displacement. The main obstacle is that the term
\frac{1}{\sqrt{t\log t}}{\mathbb{E}}\|{\mathbf{Q}}_{\nu_{t}}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{n_{t}}\|
is hard to control when
\nu_{t}
is far away from
n_{t}
