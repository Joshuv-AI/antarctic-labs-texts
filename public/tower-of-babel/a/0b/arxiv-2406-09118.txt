Second Order Shape Optimization for an Interface Identification Problem constrained by Nonlocal Models
Matthias Schuster, Volker Schulz

Abstract




Abstract.

Since shape optimization methods have been proven useful for identifying interfaces in models governed by partial differential equations, we show how shape optimization techniques can also be applied to an interface identification problem constrained by a nonlocal Dirichlet problem. Here, we focus on deriving the second shape derivative of the corresponding reduced functional and we further investigate a second order optimization algorithm.

Keywords.
shape optimization, second shape derivative, nonlocal convection-diffusion.

1
Introduction

Nonlocal operators are typically integral operators and therefore allow interactions between two distinct points in space. Moreover, the solution of a nonlocal problem in general needs to fulfill less regularity requirements compared to the solution of a system of partial differential equations. Thus, some physical phenomena like fracture propagation in continuum mechanics
[
33
,
17
]
or anomalous diffusion
[
38
,
6
]
effects can be modeled more accurately by nonlocal equations compared to their ’classic’ description by partial differential equations. Moreover, nonlocal models have been successfully applied to image denoising
[
7
,
12
]
, neural networks
[
39
,
42
]
or stochastic processes
[
22
,
10
]
, just to name a few.

Shape optimization techniques are mostly developed in the context of PDE-constrained optimization problems and have been investigated in areas like aerodynamics
[
27
,
20
]
, acoustics
[
4
,
5
,
30
]
, fluid dynamics
[
23
,
26
]
and many more.
In
[
32
]
, we already formulated the first shape derivative of an interface identification problem that is governed by a nonlocal Dirichlet problem and we additionally investigated a first order shape optimization algorithm. In this work, we continue by deriving a second shape derivative, which is then used in a Newton-like second order shape optimization approach.

The first order shape derivative method of
[
32
]
was also employed for interface identification constrained by an energy-based Local-to-Nonlocal coupling in
[
31
]
. Moreover, in the context of Peridynamics, a machine learning approach to detect an interface was tested in
[
24
]
. Here, Nader et. al. made use of finite differences to solve a one-dimensional variable horizon Local-to-Nonlocal coupling, and a convolutional neural network was trained to assign domain affiliations to nodes.

In the next chapter, we start by introducing nonlocal Dirichlet problems and shortly discuss the well-posedness of the corresponding weak formulation. In Chapter
3
we define an interface identification problem that is constrained by a nonlocal Dirichlet problem. Then, we present necessary basics of shape optimization in Chapter
4
followed by a description of the averaged adjoint method(AAM) in Section
5
, which we will use to compute the second shape derivative of the reduced functional corresponding to the constrained interface identification problem. After that, we show in Chapter
6
, that, under natural assumptions, the prerequisites of AAM are satisfied. Lastly, we investigate in Section
7
a second order optimization approach, that is partly a consequence of AAM.

2
Nonlocal Terminology and Framework

In this work, we consider the
nonlocal convection-diffusion operator

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}u(\mathbf{x}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(\mathbf{x})\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-u(\mathbf{y})\gamma(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}.

(1)

Here, the nonnegative function
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is the so-called
kernel
, which plays a vital role in the nonlocal theory. Moreover, problems involving the nonlocal operator (
1
) have been extensively studied in the literature (see, e.g.,
[
9
,
40
]
). We define a
nonlocal steady-state nonhomogeneous Dirichlet problem
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textit{Find a function }&u\textit{ s.t.}\\
-\mathcal{L}u&=f\quad\text{on }\Omega\\
u&=g\quad\text{on }\mathcal{I},\end{split}

(2)

where we set the so-called
nonlocal boundary
(or
nonlocal interaction domain
)
\mathcal{I}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}:=\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega:\int_{\Omega}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+\gamma(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}>0\},

which contains all points outside of the domain
\Omega
, that interact with at least one point inside of
\Omega
through the kernel
\gamma
.
In this section, we will recall the basic theory on problems of type (
2
). We start by deriving a weak formulation of problem (
2
). Given appropriate spaces
V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
and
V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
, that are defined later in this section, we multiply the first equation of (
2
), where we assume
u\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
, with a test vector
v\in V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
and then integrate over
\Omega
, which yields

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}\int\limits_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}v(\mathbf{x})\left(u(\mathbf{x})\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-u(\mathbf{y})\gamma(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\right)~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}=\int\limits_{\Omega}f(\mathbf{x})v(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}.

Here, we can rewrite the left-hand side using
v=0
on
\mathcal{I}
and applying Fubini’s theorem as follows

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}\int\limits_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}v(\mathbf{x})\left(u(\mathbf{x})\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-u(\mathbf{y})\gamma(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\right)~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\limits_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}\int\limits_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}\left(v(\mathbf{x})-v(\mathbf{y})\right)\left(u(\mathbf{x})\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-u(\mathbf{y})\gamma(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\right)~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}.

This reformulation is numerically more stable for singular symmetric kernels, which will be introduced later in this chapter.
Now, before we present the definition of a weak solution to (
2
), we define the bilinear operator

\displaystyle A(u,v):=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint\limits_{(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})^{2}}\left(v(\mathbf{x})-v(\mathbf{y})\right)\left(u(\mathbf{x})\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-u(\mathbf{y})\gamma(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\right)~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}

and the linear functional

\displaystyle F(v):=\int\limits_{\Omega}f(\mathbf{x})v(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}.

Then, we set the (semi-)norm
|||\cdot|||
and the norm
||\cdot||_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}
as

\displaystyle|||u|||:=\sqrt{A(u,u)}\text{ and }||u||_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}:=|||u|||+||u||_{L^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}.

Additionally, we define the nonlocal energy space
V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
and the nonlocal volume-constrained energy space
V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
in the following way:

\displaystyle V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):=\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):|||u|||<\infty\}\text{ and }V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):=\{u\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):u=0\text{ on }\mathcal{I}\}.

Definition 2.1
.

Given
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
g\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
, if
\widetilde{u}\in V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
satisfies

\displaystyle A(\widetilde{u},v)=

\displaystyle F(v)-A(g,v)\textit{ for all }v\in V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),

then we call
u:=\widetilde{u}+g\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
weak solution of the nonlocal Dirichlet problem (
2
).

For
\delta>0
we set
B_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}):=\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}||_{2}<\delta\}.

Then, in this work every kernel
\gamma
is assumed to fulfill:

(K1)

There exists a constant
\delta>0
and a function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\chi_{B_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})}(\mathbf{y}).

(K2)

There exist constants
\epsilon\in(0,\delta)
and
\underline{\gamma}>0
with

\displaystyle\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\geq\underline{\gamma}\text{ for }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\text{ and }\mathbf{y}\in B_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x}).

Further, we consider two types of kernel classes:

•

Integrable kernels
:

Set
\gamma^{s}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+\gamma(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\right)
and
\gamma^{a}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\gamma(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\right)
. Then,
\gamma
is called
integrable
, if the following requirements are fulfilled:

(1)

There exist constants
\underline{\gamma}^{s},\overline{\gamma}^{s}>0
such that

\displaystyle\underline{\gamma}^{s}=\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}\gamma^{s}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}<\infty\text{ and }\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}(\gamma^{s}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}))^{2}~d\mathbf{y}=(\overline{\gamma}^{s})^{2}.

(2)

There exists constants
\underline{\gamma}^{a}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\overline{\gamma}^{a}>0
with

\displaystyle\underline{\gamma}^{a}=\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}\gamma^{a}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}\text{ and }\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}|\gamma^{a}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})|~d\mathbf{y}=\overline{\gamma}^{a}.

(3)

The lower boundaries
\underline{\gamma}^{s}
and
\underline{\gamma}^{a}
satisfy

\underline{\gamma}^{s}+\underline{\gamma}^{a}>0.

•

Singular symmetric kernels
:

There exist positive constants
0<\gamma_{*}\leq\gamma^{*}<\infty
and
s\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\gamma_{*}\leq\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}||_{2}^{d+2s}\leq\gamma^{*}\text{ for all }\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}.

(3)

Furthermore, the kernel
\gamma
is symmetric.

In
[
9
,
40
]
it is shown, that there is a unique weak solution to the Dirichlet problem (
2
), if the corresponding kernel fulfills (K1) and (K2) and additionally fits into one of those two categories.

Remark 2.2
.

In the volume-constrained space
V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
the norms
||\cdot||_{L^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}
and
|||\cdot|||
are equivalent, if we have chosen an integrable kernel. Thus, it holds that

\displaystyle u\in(V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),|||\cdot|||)\Leftrightarrow u\in(L_{c}^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),||\cdot||_{L^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}),

where
L^{2}_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):=\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):u=0\text{ on }\mathcal{I}\}
.

On the other hand, if
\gamma
fulfills the requirements of a singular symmetric kernel, we get norm equivalence between
|||\cdot|||
and
|\cdot|_{H^{s}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}
on
V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
and

\displaystyle u\in(V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),|||\cdot|||)\Leftrightarrow u\in(H^{s}_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),|\cdot|_{H^{s}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}),

where the norm
|\cdot|_{H^{s}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}
as well as the volume-constrained fractional Sobolev space
H^{s}_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
are defined as

\displaystyle|u|_{H^{s}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}^{2}:=\iint_{(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})^{2}}\frac{(u(\mathbf{x})-u(\mathbf{y}))^{2}}{||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}||_{2}^{d+2s}}~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}\text{ and }H_{c}^{s}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):=\{u\in L_{c}^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):|u|_{H^{s}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}<\infty\}.

For the proof of these assertions, we again refer to
[
9
,
40
]
.

Interface Identification constrained by Nonlocal Models

PDE-constrained interface identification is a well-researched shape optimization problem (see, e.g.,
[
41
,
8
]
) and in the following we will investigate interface identification governed by nonlocal models.

Here as illustrated in Figure
3.1
, we assume that
\Omega
is decomposed in two open and nonempty sets
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\dot{\cup}\Gamma\dot{\cup}\Omega_{2}
holds, where
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega_{1}\cap\partial\Omega_{2}
is called
interface
.

[formula]
[formula]
[formula]
[formula]

Figure 3.1:
The interface
\Gamma
, which is colored in blue, decomposes the white area
\Omega
into subdomains
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
. Further, note that the nonlocal boundary
\mathcal{I}
is depicted in gray.

Since shape optimization is typically done w.r.t. two or three dimensional domains, we suppose
d\in\{2,3\}
.
Now, given
\bar{u}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
, we define
interface identification governed by a nonlocal Dirichlet problem
as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{u,\Gamma}J(u,\Gamma):=j(u,\Gamma)+\nu j_{per}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\left(u-\bar{u}\right)^{2}~d\mathbf{x}+\nu\int_{\Gamma}1~ds

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textit{s.t. }-\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}u&=f_{\Gamma}\quad\textit{on }\Omega,\\
u&=0\quad\textit{ on }\mathcal{I},\end{split}

(4)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}-\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}u(\mathbf{x})&:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(\mathbf{x})\gamma_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-u(\mathbf{y})\gamma_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{y}\text{ with}\\
\gamma_{\Gamma}&:=\sum_{i,j=1,2}\gamma_{ij}\chi_{\Omega_{i}\times\Omega_{j}}+\sum_{i=1,2}\gamma_{iI}\chi_{\left(\Omega_{i}\times\mathcal{I}\right)\cup\left(\mathcal{I}\times\Omega_{i}\right)}.\end{split}

(5)

For ease of presentation, we only consider homogeneous Dirichlet problems. However, the nonhomogeneous case can be handled analogously.
As we can see in (
5
), the operator
-\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}
is dependent on the interface, since the choice of kernel
\gamma_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
varies depending on the location of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
.
Furthermore, we supposed that the forcing term can be expressed as
f_{\Gamma}=f_{1}\chi_{\Omega_{1}}+f_{2}\chi_{\Omega_{2}}
, where
f_{1},f_{2}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
.
Then, we denote the corresponding variational formulation of the Dirichlet problem (
4
) as

\displaystyle A_{\Gamma}(u,v)=F_{\Gamma}(v)\textit{ for all }v\in V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),

(6)

where the subscripts highlight that the kernel parameters as well as the forcing term are dependent on the decomposition imposed by the interface
\Gamma
.
The objective functional consists of two terms: The first one, which we refer to as
j
, is a tracking-type functional, since we aim to find the interface
\Gamma
such that the solution to (
6
) approximates the given data
\bar{u}
as well as possible. The second integral of the objective function, which we denote by
j_{per}
, is called
perimeter regularization
, which is often used to avoid ill-posedness of the problem (see
[
3
]
). Previously, as mentioned above, first-order shape optimization algorithms for the interface identification problem (
4
) have been investigated in
[
32
]
.

Since for every suitable interface
\Gamma
the nonlocal Dirichlet problem has a unique solution, the problem (
4
) is only dependent on the shape
\Gamma
, i.e., we can rewrite (
4
) as a reduced problem

\displaystyle\min_{\Gamma}J^{red}(\Gamma):=J(u(\Gamma),\Gamma),

where
u(\Gamma)
satisfies (
6
) given
\Gamma
. Therefore, we continue by recalling necessary basic tools of shape optimization in order to derive the second shape derivative of the reduced functional
J^{red}
and a second order shape optimization algorithm.

4
Basics of Shape Optimization

To define shape derivatives we make use of a family of mappings
\{\mathbf{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
, where
\mathbf{F}_{t}:\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbf{F}_{0}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}
for
\mathbf{x}\in\overline{\Omega}
and
T>0
is supposed to be sufficiently small. In this work, we choose to apply the perturbation of identity:

Definition 4.1

(Perturbation of Identity)
.

Given a vector field
\textbf{V}\in C_{0}^{k}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\geq 0
, the perturbation of identity is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\textbf{V}}:\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d},~\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\textbf{V}}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbf{x}+t\textbf{V}(\mathbf{x}),

where
C_{0}^{k}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\{\textbf{V}\in C^{k}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d}):\textbf{V}\text{ vanishes at the boundary}\}
.

Here,
{\textbf{V}\in C^{k}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})}

vanishes at the boundary
, if the set
\{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega:|\partial^{\alpha}\textbf{V}(\mathbf{x})|\geq\epsilon\}
is compact for all
\epsilon>0
and partial derivatives
\partial^{\alpha}:=\partial^{\alpha_{1}}_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\partial^{\alpha_{2}}_{\mathbf{x}_{2}}\dots\partial^{\alpha_{d}}_{\mathbf{x}_{d}}
w.r.t. multi-indices
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
with
0\leq|\alpha|\leq k
.
If
T
is chosen small enough such that
||t\textbf{V}||_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})}<1
, then the function
\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\textbf{V}}:\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow\overline{\Omega}
is a bijective function, where
\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\textbf{V}}
as well as the corresponding inverse
(\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\textbf{V}})^{-1}
are both Lipschitz continuous (see
[
16
]
).

Remark 4.2
.

In this work, the nonlocal boundary
\mathcal{I}
will not be deformed since the interface
\Gamma
is supposed to be a subset of
\Omega
. Thus, we extend every vector field
\textbf{V}\in C_{0}^{k}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
by zero to a function defined on the domain
\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}
, i.e., we only consider vector fields that are elements of the set

\displaystyle C_{0}^{k}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\{\textbf{V}:\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}|\textbf{V}=0\textit{ on }\mathcal{I}\textit{ and }\left.\textbf{V}\right|_{\overline{\Omega}}\in C_{0}^{k}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})\}.

Furthermore,
\textbf{V}\in C_{0}^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is sufficient for the calculations in this work.

Now, we continue by formulating the definition of the first and second shape derivative.

Definition 4.3

(First Shape Derivative)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a suitable shape space. Then, for a shape functional
J:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we define the Eulerian or directional derivative as

\displaystyle D_{\Gamma}J(\Gamma)[\textbf{V}]:=\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{J(\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\textbf{V}}(\Gamma))-J(\Gamma)}{t}.

If
D_{\Gamma}J(\Gamma)[\textbf{V}]
exists for all vector fields
\textbf{V}\in C_{0}^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and if the function

\displaystyle D_{\Gamma}J(\Gamma):C_{0}^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R},~\textbf{V}\mapsto D_{\Gamma}J(\Gamma)[\textbf{V}]

is linear and continuous, the we refer to
D_{\Gamma}J(\Gamma)[\textbf{V}]
as the shape derivative at
\Gamma
in direction
V
.

Definition 4.4

(Second Shape Derivative)
.

If for all
\textbf{V},\textbf{W}\in C_{0}^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})
the derivative

\displaystyle D_{\Gamma}^{2}J(\Gamma)[\textbf{V},\textbf{W}]:=\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{D_{\Gamma}J(\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\textbf{W}}(\Gamma))[\textbf{V}]-D_{\Gamma}J(\Gamma)[\textbf{V}]}{t}\text{ exists}

and the function

\displaystyle D_{\Gamma}^{2}J(\Gamma):C_{0}^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})\times C_{0}^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\left(\textbf{V},\textbf{W}\right)\mapsto D_{\Gamma}^{2}J(\Gamma)[\textbf{V},\textbf{W}]

is bilinear and continuous, then
D_{\Gamma}^{2}J(\Gamma)[\textbf{V},\textbf{W}]
is called the second shape derivative at
\Gamma
in the direction
(\textbf{V},\textbf{W})
.

The second shape derivative can also be characterized as

\displaystyle D_{\Gamma}^{2}J(\Gamma)[\textbf{V},\textbf{W}]=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t_{2}=0^{+}}\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t_{1}=0^{+}}J\left(\mathbf{F}_{t_{1}}^{\textbf{V}}(\mathbf{F}_{t_{2}}^{\textbf{W}}(\Gamma))\right),

which indicates, that the second shape derivative is computed by differentiating a functional, where the shape
\Gamma
is first perturbed along the vector field
W
and afterwards along
V
as described in Definition
4.1
.

Remark 4.5
.

In the literature, the second shape derivative
D_{\Gamma}^{2}J(\Gamma)
is sometimes referred to as the shape Hessian at
\Gamma
.

Definition 4.6

(Shape Space)
.

Given a domain
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Omega
, we define the corresponding shape space
\mathcal{A}(\Gamma_{0})
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\Gamma_{0}):=\{\mathbf{T}(\Gamma_{0})|\mathbf{T}:\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow\overline{\Omega}\textit{ invertible}\}.

In this work, as mentioned before, we only employ the perturbation of identity, which is invertible, if
t>0
is small enough. As shown in
[
29
]
, interpreting shape optimization as optimizing on a subset of
\{\mathbf{T}|\mathbf{T}:\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow\overline{\Omega}\}
yields the linear second shape derivative

\displaystyle J^{\prime\prime}(\Gamma)[\textbf{V},\textbf{W}]:=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t_{2}=0^{+}}\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t_{1}=0^{+}}J((\Id+t_{2}\textbf{W}+t_{1}\textbf{V})(\Gamma)),

which is symmetric, provides the basis of a Taylor series and thus justifies convergence properties of shape Newton methods. In
[
25
,
37
]
, it is proven under natural assumptions on the differentiability of J, that the classical shape derivative can be expressed as

\displaystyle D^{2}_{\Gamma}J(\Gamma)[\textbf{V},\textbf{W}]=J^{\prime\prime}(\Gamma)[\textbf{V},\textbf{W}]+D_{\Gamma}J(\Gamma)[D\textbf{V}\textbf{W}].

(7)

Here, the second term
D_{\Gamma}J(\Gamma)[D\textbf{V}\textbf{W}]
is only symmetric, if
D\textbf{V}\textbf{W}=D\textbf{W}\textbf{V}
holds. In the next chapters, we first derive the classical shape derivative
D^{2}_{\Gamma}J(\Gamma)[\textbf{V},\textbf{W}]
by making use of the so-called averaged adjoint method. By omitting the possibly nonsymmetric part
D_{\Gamma}J(\Gamma)[D\textbf{V}\textbf{W}]
, we will then obtain the linear second shape derivative, which we utilize in a Newton-like algorithm to solve the constrained interface identification problem (
4
).

Remark 4.7
.

The first and second shape derivative of the perimeter regularization
j_{per}
can be formulated as

\displaystyle D_{\Gamma}j_{per}(\Gamma)[\textbf{V}]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}\di_{\Gamma}(\textbf{V})~ds=\int_{\Gamma}\di(\textbf{V})-n^{\top}D\textbf{V}n~ds\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle D_{\Gamma}^{2}j_{per}(\Gamma)[\textbf{V},\textbf{W}]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}\di_{\Gamma}\textbf{V}\di_{\Gamma}\textbf{W}-\trace\left(D\textbf{V}D\textbf{W}\right)+\langle\left(D\textbf{V}\right)^{\top}n,\left(D\textbf{W}\right)^{\top}n\rangle~ds,

where
\di_{\Gamma}(\textbf{V}):=\di(\textbf{V})-n^{\top}D\textbf{V}n
is the tangential divergence and
D_{\Gamma}\textbf{V}:=D\textbf{V}-D\textbf{V}nn^{\top}
the tangential Jacobian. The formula for
D_{\Gamma}j_{per}(\Gamma)[\textbf{V}]
follows directly from
[
27
, Lemma 3.3.4]
. The derivation of
D_{\Gamma}^{2}j_{per}(\Gamma)[\textbf{V},\textbf{W}]
can be found in
[
28
, Section 3.1]
. For ease of presentation, we exclude
j_{per}(\Gamma)
,
D_{\Gamma}j_{per}(\Gamma)[\textbf{V}]
and
D_{\Gamma}^{2}j_{per}(\Gamma)[\textbf{V},\textbf{W}]
in the calculations of the next chapter.

Remark 4.8
.

Given a vector field
\textbf{V}\in C_{0}^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we set
\Gamma_{t}^{\textbf{V}}:=\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\textbf{V}}(\Gamma)
and denote by
\Omega(\Gamma_{t}^{\textbf{V}})
that we consider
\Omega
to be decomposed in two domains by
\Gamma_{t}^{\textbf{V}}
, i.e.,
\Omega(\Gamma_{t}^{\textbf{V}})=\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\textbf{V}}(\Omega_{1})\dot{\cup}\Gamma_{t}^{\textbf{V}}\dot{\cup}\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\textbf{V}}(\Omega_{2})
and
\Gamma_{t}^{\textbf{V}}=\partial\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\textbf{V}}(\Omega_{1})\cap\partial\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\textbf{V}}(\Omega_{2})
.

5
Second Shape Derivative via the Averaged Adjoint Method

As mentioned earlier, the interface identification problem constrained by nonlocal models (
4
) can be written as a reduced optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{A}(\Gamma_{0})}J^{red}(\Gamma):=J(u(\Gamma),\Gamma),

(8)

where
u(\Gamma)
solves (
6
).
In
[
32
]
it was shown, how the first shape derivative of (
8
) can be computed via the averaged adjoint method
[
35
,
19
,
36

7
Numerical Experiments

Here, we present a second order shape optimization algorithm, where we follow a Newton-like approach, that incorporates first and second shape derivatives of the reduced objective functional, which are derived by the averaged adjoint method. Additionally, we describe the implementation and discuss numerical examples.

As mentioned before, we follow
[
29
]
and optimize on a suitable subset of
\{T|T:\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow\overline{\Omega}\}
. Therefore, we omit the possibly nonsymmetric part of the second shape derivative and only utilize
(J^{red})^{\prime\prime}(\Gamma)[\textbf{V},\textbf{W}]
. Here, the objective functional also includes the perimeter regularization

.
Moreover, we apply the finite element method and approximate functions by a linear combination of continuous, piecewise linear basis functions. In particular, in every iteration

\textbf{W}^{k,h}
in the space of such linear combinations of two dimensional CG ansatz functions, which we denote as

.
For a detailed description on how the finite element framework can be implemented in order to solve nonlocal Dirichlet problems we refer to

However, due to the Hadamard structure theorem (see

8
Conclusion

In this work, we investigated interface identification governed by a nonlocal Dirichlet problem. Further, we developed the second shape derivative of the reduced functional, that corresponds to this constrained interface identification problem, by applying a version of the averaged adjoint method. The second shape derivative was then used in a Newton-like second order shape optimization algorithm, which was successfully applied to two numerical examples in the previous chapter. Thus, to the best knowledge of the authors, this is the first study of the numerical usage of second shape derivatives for nonlocal problems.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathfrak{A}(t,u^{0},v^{0})\rightarrow\partial_{t}\mathfrak{A}(0,u^{0},v^{0})\text{ for }t\searrow 0.

∎
