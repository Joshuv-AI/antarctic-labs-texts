Robust Detection for Mills Cross Sonar
Olivier Lerda, Ammar Mian, Guillaume Ginolhac, Senior Member, IEEE, Jean-Philippe Ovarlez, Member, IEEE, Didier Charlot

Abstract
Multi-array systems are widely used in sonar and radar applications. They can improve communication speeds, target discrimination, and imaging. In the case of a multibeam sonar system that can operate two receiving arrays, we derive new adaptive tests to improve detection capabilities compared to traditional sonar detection approaches. To do so, we more specifically consider correlated arrays, whose covariance matrices are estimated up to scale factors, and an impulsive clutter. In a partially homogeneous environment, the 2-step Generalized Likelihood ratio Test (GLRT) and Rao approach lead to a generalization of the Adaptive Normalized Matched Filter (ANMF) test and an equivalent numerically simpler detector with a well-established texture Constant False Alarm Rate (CFAR) behavior. Performances are discussed and illustrated with theoretical examples, numerous simulations, and insights into experimental data. Results show that these detectors outperform their competitors and have stronger robustness to environmental unknowns.

I-B

Paper organization and contributions

We use a Mills cross sonar with two receiving arrays for point-like target detection in heterogeneous (non-)Gaussian environment. The main contributions are summarized below:

•

We propose a new covariance matrix estimator generalizing the usual Tyler’s estimator in a context of dual and correlated arrays.

•

On these assumptions, two adaptive coherent detectors are derived based on the two-step GLRT and Rao’s test.

•

The performance of these detectors, in terms of false alarm and probability of detection, is evaluated and compared. We highlight the constant false alarm rate property with respect to the power variation and the speckle covariance matrix.

•

The contribution of a sonar architecture with orthogonal arrays in the spatial reduction of the clutter ridge is shown.

•

Experimental results demonstrate the usefulness of the proposed models and detection schemes on real sonar data.

II-C

Pre-processing and data format

The signals from the 128 sensors provided by the sonar’s embedded software are demodulated in baseband (In-Phase and Quadrature components) and decimated at 43 KHz. During reception pre-processing, the digitized samples are compensated for the time-varying gain, filtered to the bandwidth of the waveform, pulse compressed, and then decimated again to bandwidth. Finally, a ping dataset is a matrix of about 6000 range bins from 15 m to 400 m, by 64 sensors, by two arrays.

III
Detection Schemes

III-A

Data model for a single array

At each time, we have two digitized synchronized complex data vectors of
\displaystyle m=64
elements, called “snapshots”, which can be written as (by omitting the temporal parameterization):

\mathbf{x}_{i}=\left[x_{i,1},x_{i,2},\cdots,x_{i,m}\right]^{T}\,,

(1)

where
\displaystyle i=1,2
is the array identifier (respectively horizontal and vertical). After the pre-processing steps, a point-like target observed on the antenna
\displaystyle i
is simply:

\mathbf{x}_{i}=\alpha_{i}\,\mathbf{p}_{i}+\mathbf{z}_{i}\,,

(2)

where
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
is the received signal,
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}
is an unknown complex target amplitude,
\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
stands for the known deterministic angular steering vector,
\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
is a mixture of scaled Gaussian (MSG) random vector admitting the stochastic representation:

\mathbf{z}_{i}\overset{d}{=}\sqrt{\tau_{i}}\,\mathbf{c}_{i}\,.

(3)

The
texture

\displaystyle\tau_{i}
is an unknown positive deterministic scalar parameter, presumably different for each range bin (i.e. for all time samples). The
speckle

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}_{i}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{i}^{2}\,\mathbf{M}_{ii})\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
is a complex circular Gaussian random vector whose covariance matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{ii}
is known up to a scaling factor
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{2}
. The term
speckle
should be understood in the sense of CES statistics rather than as the result of a sum of contributions from reflections in a resolution cell. This model is strongly related to the class of compound Gaussian distributions
[
31
]
, which assumes a speckle-independent random texture with a given density
\displaystyle p_{\tau}
. Considering deterministic texture allows building estimators and detectors which are completely independent of the knowledge of the texture PDF. In this way, detectors are no longer specific to a single and unique distribution, which can be seen as an additional source of robustness. Furthermore, the MSG distribution is more robust than the Gaussian one because the relative scaling between samples allows flexibility in the presence of heterogeneities, such as impulsive noise, outliers, and inconsistent data. This model explicitly allows considering the power fluctuation across range bins, especially for heavy-tailed clutter distributions.

The detection problem is written as a binary hypothesis test:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}H_{0}:\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{z}_{i}&;\quad\mathbf{x}_{i,k}=\mathbf{z}_{i,k}\quad k=1\ldots K\,,\\
H_{1}:\mathbf{x}_{i}=\alpha_{i}\,\mathbf{p}_{i}+\mathbf{z}_{i}&;\quad\mathbf{x}_{i,k}=\mathbf{z}_{i,k}\quad k=1\ldots K\,.\end{array}\right.\

(4)

In (
4
) it is assumed that
\displaystyle K\geq m
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) signal-free secondary data
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{i,k}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
are available under both hypotheses for background parameters estimation. We recall that
\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{i,k}\overset{d}{=}\sqrt{\tau_{i,k}}\,\mathbf{c}_{i,k}
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{c}_{i,k}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{M}_{ii})
.

Conditionally to the unknown deterministic texture, the densities of
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{i}
under
\displaystyle H_{0}
and
\displaystyle H_{1}
are Gaussian:

\begin{array}[]{ll}p_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{i};H_{0})=\cfrac{1}{\pi^{m}\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2m}\lvert\mathbf{M}_{ii}\rvert}\exp\left(-\cfrac{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{M}_{ii}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}\right)\,,\\
p_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{i};H_{1})=\\
\cfrac{1}{\pi^{m}\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2m}\lvert\mathbf{M}_{ii}\rvert}\exp\left(-\cfrac{{\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\alpha_{i}\mathbf{p}_{i}\right)}^{H}\mathbf{M}_{ii}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\alpha_{i}\mathbf{p}_{i}\right)}{\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}\right)\,,\end{array}

(5)

where
\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{i}=\sigma_{i}\,\sqrt{\tau_{i}}
.

III-B

Data model for the two arrays

If we consider the two antennas, this model can be written more appropriately:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}H_{0}:\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{z}&;\quad\mathbf{x}_{k}=\mathbf{z}_{k}\quad k=1\ldots K\,,\\
H_{1}:\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{P}\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\mathbf{z}&;\quad\mathbf{x}_{k}=\mathbf{z}_{k}\quad k=1\ldots K\,,\end{array}\right.

(6)

where
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{1}\\
\mathbf{x}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2m}
is the concatenation of the two received signals,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}\\
\alpha_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
is the vector of the target amplitudes and
\displaystyle\mathbf{z}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{z}_{1}\\
\mathbf{z}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2m}
is the additive clutter. The matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{P}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{p}_{1}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{p}_{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2m\times 2}
contains the steering vectors.
\displaystyle\left\{\mathbf{x}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{1,k}\\
\mathbf{x}_{2,k}\end{bmatrix}\right\}_{k\in[1,K]}\in\mathbb{C}^{2m}
for
\displaystyle K\geq 2\,m
are i.i.d. signal-free secondary data.

This formulation allows considering the correlation between sensors of arrays. The covariance is
\displaystyle\mathbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}_{11}&\mathbf{M}_{12}\\
\mathbf{M}_{21}&\mathbf{M}_{22}\end{bmatrix}
, with
\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{ii}
the block-covariance matrix of array
\displaystyle i
, and
\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{ij}=\mathbf{M}_{ji}^{H}
the cross-correlation block of array
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle j
. The partitioned inverse covariance will be denoted as
\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}_{11}^{-1}&\mathbf{M}_{12}^{-1}\\
\mathbf{M}_{21}^{-1}&\mathbf{M}_{22}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}
.

We further assume that the covariance is known, or estimated, up to two scalars, possibly different on each array. These scalars are conditioning the
\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{ii}
block-covariance, but also all the cross-correlations blocks associated with the array
\displaystyle i
. We can therefore write:

\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\,\mathbf{M}\,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\,,

(7)

with
\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\sigma}_{1}\,\mathbf{I}_{m}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\tilde{\sigma}_{2}\,\mathbf{I}_{m}\\
\end{bmatrix}
=
\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\sigma}_{1}&0\\
0&\tilde{\sigma}_{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{m}
the unknown diagonal matrix of scalars
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}
and
\displaystyle\tau_{i}
. We recap that the
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}
parameter drives the partial homogeneity of the data (i.e. the difference in scale factor between the covariance matrices of the primary and secondary data) and
\displaystyle\tau_{i}
drives the non-Gaussianity of the data (i.e. the power variation of the observations over time).

In this model, each array, although correlated, has a possibly distinct texture and an unknown scaling factor on the covariance matrix which may also be dissimilar. It would therefore become entirely possible to model, as an example, a first array whose observations would be Gaussian (
\displaystyle\tau_{1}=1
) and homogeneous (
\displaystyle\sigma_{1}=1
), and a second array whose observations would be
K
-distributed (for which
\displaystyle\tau_{2}
is a realization of a Gamma distribution) and non-homogeneous (
\displaystyle\sigma_{2}\neq 1
). The PDFs under each hypothesis can be rewritten as:

\begin{array}[]{ll}p_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x};H_{0})=\cfrac{1}{\pi^{2m}\lvert\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}\rvert}\exp\left(-{\mathbf{x}}^{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\right)\,,\\
p_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x};H_{1})=\cfrac{1}{\pi^{2m}\lvert\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}\rvert}\exp\left(-{\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)}^{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)\right).\end{array}

(8)

IV-A

Generalized Likelihood Ratio Test

The Generalized Likelihood Ratio Test (GLRT) design methodology proposes to solve the detection problem from the ratio of the probability densities function under
\displaystyle H_{1}
and
\displaystyle H_{0}
, substituting the unknown parameters with their maximum likelihood estimates:

L_{G}(\mathbf{x})=\cfrac{\underset{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}{\max}\,\underset{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}{\max}\,p_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\alpha},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}},H_{1}\right)}{\underset{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}{\max}\,p_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\mathbf{x};\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}},H_{0}\right)}\,.

(9)

Proposition IV.1
.

The GLRT for the hypothesis test (
6
) is given by:

L_{G}(\mathbf{x})=\cfrac{\hat{\tilde{\sigma}}_{1_{0}}\,\hat{\tilde{\sigma}}_{2_{0}}}{\hat{\tilde{\sigma}}_{1_{1}}\,\hat{\tilde{\sigma}}_{2_{1}}}\,,

(10)

where

\left(\hat{\tilde{\sigma}}_{1_{0}}\hat{\tilde{\sigma}}_{2_{0}}\right)^{2}=\cfrac{\emph{Re}\left(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{H}\mathbf{M}_{12}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{2}\right)}{m}+\sqrt{\cfrac{\mathbf{x}_{1}^{H}\mathbf{M}_{11}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{1}}{m}\cfrac{\mathbf{x}_{2}^{H}\mathbf{M}_{22}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{2}}{m}}\,,

and

The Rao test is obtained by exploiting the asymptotic efficiency of the ML estimate and expanding the likelihood ratio in the neighborhood of the estimated parameters

where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{R}=\left[\mathrm{Re}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)^{T}\,\mathrm{Im}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)^{T}\right]^{T}

\displaystyle 4\times 1

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{S}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{S_{0}}\end{matrix}}\,.

\displaystyle p_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{R},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{S})

IV-B

Covariance estimation and adaptive detectors (step-2)

In practice, the noise covariance matrix is unknown and estimated using the
\displaystyle K
available i.i.d. signal-free secondary data.

In Gaussian environment, in which PDFs are given by (
8
) with
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}=1
and
\displaystyle\tau_{i}=1
, the MLE of
\displaystyle\mathbf{M}
is the well-known Sample Covariance Matrix (SCM):

\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{SCM}=\cfrac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{x}_{k}\,\mathbf{x}_{k}^{H}\,,

(15)

which is an unbiased and minimum variance estimator.

In the presence of outliers or a heavy-tailed distribution (as modeled by a mixture of scaled Gaussian), this estimator is no longer optimal or robust. This leads to a strong performance degradation.

Proposition IV.3
.

In MSG environment the MLE of
\displaystyle\mathbf{M}
is given by:

\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{2TYL}}=\cfrac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{H}\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{-1}\,,

(16)

where
\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{\hat{\tau}_{1_{k}}}&0\\
0&\sqrt{\hat{\tau}_{2_{k}}}\\
\end{bmatrix}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{m}
,
\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{1_{k}}=t_{1}+\sqrt{\cfrac{t_{1}}{t_{2}}}\,t_{12}
,
\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{2_{k}}=t_{2}+\sqrt{\cfrac{t_{2}}{t_{1}}}\,t_{12}
and

VII
Conclusions

In this paper, we considered the problem of adaptive point target detection by a correlated multi-arrays Mills Cross sonar system. Using a 2-step approach, we first derived two new detectors that are robust to differences in target amplitudes and to unknown scaling factors on the covariances. Subsequently, we have introduced an innovative estimator of the covariance matrix suitable to any non-Gaussian MSG environment. By these very general assumptions, the framework of the study can therefore concern systems with co-located or remote arrays.

Experimental results show that the detection performance is up to 3 dB better than conventional approaches. The detectors cover a larger detection area and are particularly robust to spikes, impulsive noise, and data contamination.

Future work will focus on establishing a theoretical demonstration of the matrix-CFAR behavior of these detectors, and on generalizing solutions for different numbers and geometries of arrays.

Maximum Likelihood Estimator of

The MLE
\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}

\ln p_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\Sigma},H_{0})=-mL\ln\pi+2\ln\lvert\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\rvert-\ln\lvert\mathbf{M}\rvert-\left({\mathbf{x}}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\right)\,,

whose derivative relative to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}
is:

\cfrac{\partial\ln p_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\Sigma},H_{0})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}}=2\cfrac{\partial\ln\lvert\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\rvert}{\partial\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}}-\cfrac{\partial\mathbf{x}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}}\,.

Knowing that (
[
52
]
(57))

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial\ln\lvert\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\rvert}{\partial\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

[
52
]
(82))
\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial\mathbf{x}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}}=2\text{Re}\left(\mathbf{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{x}^{H}\right)
we have:

\cfrac{\partial\ln p_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\Sigma},H_{0})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}}=2\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-2\text{Re}\left(\mathbf{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{x}^{H}\right)\,,

which leads to:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}=\text{Re}\left(\mathbf{M}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{x}^{H}\right)\,.

(23)

Expanding this matrix product with

\mathbf{0}&\hat{\sigma}_{2_{0}}\mathbf{I}_{m}\\
\end{bmatrix}

\hat{\sigma}_{1_{0}}\,\mathbf{I}_{m}=\text{Re}\left(\mathbf{M}_{11}^{-1}\cfrac{\mathbf{x}_{1}\,\mathbf{x}_{1}^{H}}{\hat{\sigma}_{1_{0}}}+\mathbf{M}_{12}^{-1}\cfrac{\mathbf{x}_{2}\,\mathbf{x}_{1}^{H}}{\hat{\sigma}_{2_{0}}}\right)\,,

where
\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{11}^{-1}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{12}^{-1}
are the 11th and 12th block of
\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{-1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle m\,\hat{\sigma}_{1_{0}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\tr\left[\text{Re}\left(\mathbf{M}_{11}^{-1}\cfrac{\mathbf{x}_{1}\,\mathbf{x}_{1}^{H}}{\hat{\sigma}_{1_{0}}}+\mathbf{M}_{12}^{-1}\cfrac{\mathbf{x}_{2}\,\mathbf{x}_{1}^{H}}{\hat{\sigma}_{2_{0}}}\right)\right]\,,

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\cfrac{\mathbf{x}_{1}^{H}\mathbf{M}_{11}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{1}}{\hat{\sigma}_{1_{0}}}+\text{Re}\left(\cfrac{\mathbf{x}_{1}^{H}\mathbf{M}_{12}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{2}}{\hat{\sigma}_{2_{0}}}\right)\,.

\hat{\sigma}_{1_{0}}=\cfrac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{1_{0}}}\cfrac{\mathbf{x}_{1}^{H}\mathbf{M}_{11}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{1}}{m}+\cfrac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{2_{0}}}\cfrac{\text{Re}\left(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{H}\mathbf{M}_{12}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{2}\right)}{m}\,,

(24)

\hat{\sigma}_{2_{0}}=\cfrac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{1_{0}}}\cfrac{\text{Re}\left(\mathbf{x}_{2}^{H}\mathbf{M}_{21}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{1}\right)}{m}+\cfrac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{2_{0}}}\cfrac{\mathbf{x}_{2}^{H}\mathbf{M}_{22}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{2}}{m}\,.

(25)

\displaystyle a_{1}=\cfrac{\mathbf{x}_{1}^{H}\mathbf{M}_{11}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{1}}{m}
