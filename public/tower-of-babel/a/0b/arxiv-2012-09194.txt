Nearly tight Trotterization of interacting electrons
Yuan Su, Hsin-Yuan Huang, Earl T. Campbell

Abstract
We consider simulating quantum systems on digital quantum computers. We show that the performance of quantum simulation can be improved by simultaneously exploiting commutativity of the target Hamiltonian, sparsity of interactions, and prior knowledge of the initial state. We achieve this using Trotterization for a class of interacting electrons that encompasses various physical systems, including the plane-wave-basis electronic structure and the Fermi-Hubbard model. We estimate the simulation error by taking the transition amplitude of nested commutators of the Hamiltonian terms within the η \eta -electron manifold. We develop multiple techniques for bounding the transition amplitude and expectation of general fermionic operators, which may be of independent interest. We also obtain an improvement for simulating the Fermi-Hubbard model. We construct concrete examples for which our bounds are almost saturated, giving a nearly tight Trotterization of interacting electrons.

1.1
Combining interaction sparsity, commutativity, and initial-state knowledge

Sparsity can be used to improve digital quantum simulation in multiple ways. A common notion of
d
-sparsity concerns the target Hamiltonian itself, where each row and column of the Hamiltonian contains
d
nonzero elements accessed by querying quantum oracles. As aforementioned, this provides an abstract framework for designing efficient simulation algorithms and is versatile for establishing lower bounds
[
10
]
, although it sometimes ignores other important properties of the target system, such as locality, commutativity, and symmetry. Another notion of sparsity, closely related to our paper, considers the interactions between the underlying qubits or modes
[
14
,
87
,
63
,
57
]
. The sparsity of interactions does not in general imply the underlying Hamiltonian is sparse, but it provides a tighter bound on the number of terms in the Hamiltonian and may thus be favorable to digital quantum simulation.

Trotterization (and its alternative variants
[
27
,
25
,
62
,
34
,
55
]
) provides a simple approach to digital quantum simulation and is so far the only known approach that can exploit the commutativity of the Hamiltonian. Indeed, in the extreme case where all the terms in the Hamiltonian commute, we can simultaneously diagonalize them and apply the first-order Lie-Trotter formula
\mathscr{S}_{1}(t)
without error. Previous studies have also established commutator error bounds for certain low-order formulas
[
76
]
and specific systems
[
22
,
74
]
. An analysis of a general formula
\mathscr{S}_{p}(t)
is, however, considerably more difficult and has remained elusive until the recent proof of the commutator scaling of Trotter error
[
27
]
.

A different direction to speeding up digital quantum simulation is to exploit information about the initial state. The error of digital quantum simulation is commonly quantified in previous work by the spectral-norm distance, which considers all possible states in the underlying Hilbert space. But if the state is known to be within some subspace throughout the simulation, then in principle this knowledge could be used to improve the algorithm. For instance, digital quantum simulation in practice often starts with an initial state in the low-energy subspace of the Hamiltonian, so a worst-case spectral-norm analysis will inevitably overestimate the error. To address this, recent studies have considered a low-energy projection on the simulation error and provided improved approaches, using either Trotterization
[
73
,
69
,
6
,
28
]
or more advanced quantum algorithms
[
51
]
, that can be advantageous when the energy of the initial state is sufficiently small.

Ideally, the sparsity of interactions, commutativity of the Hamiltonian, and prior knowledge about the initial state can all be combined to yield an even faster digital quantum simulation. This combination, however, appears to be technically challenging to achieve. Indeed, the state-of-the-art analysis of Trotterization represents the simulation error in terms of nested commutators of Hamiltonian terms with exponential conjugations
[
27
, Theorem 5]
. This representation is versatile for computing the commutator scaling of Trotter error, but it yields little information about the energy of the initial state. To the best of our knowledge, the only previous attempt to address this problem was made by Somma for simulating bosonic Hamiltonians
[
73
]
, whose solution seems to have a divergence issue.

1.3
Main techniques

The proof of
Theorem
1
relies on multiple approaches we develop for bounding the fermionic seminorm, which may be of independent interest. Recall from (
4
) that the fermionic seminorm
\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}
of a number-preserving operator
X
is the maximum transition amplitude of
X
within the
\eta
-electron manifold.

Our first approach is based on the observation that the fermionic seminorm of
X
can be alternatively represented using the expectation of
X^{\dagger}X
, i.e.,

\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}=\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle,\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert=\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle}\sqrt{\langle\psi_{\eta}\rvert X^{\dagger}X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle}.

(7)

We then upper bound
X^{\dagger}X
in terms of the
particle-number operator

N=\sum_{j}N_{j}
, so that the expectation scales with the number of electrons
\eta=\langle\psi_{\eta}\rvert N\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle
instead of the total number of spin orbitals. Assuming
X
is a sum of product of fermionic operators, we contract the summation indices in
X^{\dagger}X
by using either diagonalization (
Lemma
2
) or an operator Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (
Lemma
1
)
[
61
]
. To extend this argument to general fermionic operators, we prove a Hölder-type inequality (
Lemma
3
) and apply it recursively to bound
X^{\dagger}X
. We detail this recursive approach in
Section
3
and use it to prove (
2
).

Our second approach starts by bounding the fermionic seminorm

\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}=\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle,\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert

(8)

in terms of the maximum expectation value
\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\psi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert
. We then expand
X
and
\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle
and give a combinatorial argument to count the number of “paths” which have nonzero contribution to the expectation (
Proposition
11
). We discuss this path-counting approach in more detail in
Section
4
and use it to prove (
3
). It is worth mentioning that the path-counting technique can also be used to prove the following alternative bound for the
p
th-order Trotterization

\left\lVert\mathscr{S}_{p}(t)-e^{-itH}\right\rVert_{\eta}=\mathcal{O}\left(\left(n\left\lVert\tau\right\rVert_{\max}+\left\lVert\nu\right\rVert_{\max}\eta\right)^{p-1}\left\lVert\tau\right\rVert_{\max}\left\lVert\nu\right\rVert_{\max}n\eta^{2}t^{p+1}\right).

(9)

This is slightly weaker than (
2
) since
\left\lVert\tau\right\rVert\leq n\left\lVert\tau\right\rVert_{\max}
always holds but not necessarily saturates, but in our application it yields the same gate complexity for the electronic-structure simulation in the second-quantized plane-wave basis. We discuss this further in
Appendix
B

Note that the expectation of fermionic operators, when taken with respect to the computational-basis states, can be exactly computed using the so-called Wick’s theorem
[
86
,
64
]
. However, this approach would introduce unnecessary term reordering which actually complicates our proof. In contrast, the underlying idea of path counting is conceptually simpler and may have potential applications in other contexts beyond the analysis of Trotter error.

2.1
Trotterization and Trotter error

The Trotterization algorithm approximates the evolution of a sum of Hamiltonian terms using exponentials of the individual terms. For the interacting-electronic Hamiltonian (
1
), it suffices to consider a two-term decomposition
H=T+V
, as the exponentials of
T
and
V
can be directly implemented on a quantum computer. Then, the ideal evolution under
H
for time
t
is given by
e^{-itH}=e^{-it(T+V)}
, which can be approximated by a
p
th-order product formula
\mathscr{S}_{p}(t)
, such as the first-order Lie-Trotter formula

\mathscr{S}_{1}(t):=e^{-itT}e^{-itV}

(17)

and (
2k
)th-order Suzuki formulas
[
77
]

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle:=e^{-i\frac{t}{2}V}e^{-itT}e^{-i\frac{t}{2}V},

(18)

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{2k}(t)

\displaystyle:=\mathscr{S}_{2k-2}(u_{k}t)^{2}\,\mathscr{S}_{2k-2}((1-4u_{k})t)\,\mathscr{S}_{2k-2}(u_{k}t)^{2},

where
u_{k}:=1/(4-4^{1/(2k-1)})
. This approximation is accurate when
t
is small. To simulate for a longer time, we divide the evolution into
r

Trotter steps
and apply
\mathscr{S}_{p}(t/r)
with
Trotter error
at most
\epsilon/r
. We choose
r
sufficiently large so that the simulation error, as quantified by the spectral norm
\left\lVert\mathscr{S}_{p}^{r}(t/r)-e^{-itH}\right\rVert
, is at most
\epsilon
.

Trotterization (and its alternative variants) provides a simple approach to digital quantum simulation and is so far the only known approach that can exploit commutativity of the Hamiltonian. Indeed, in the extreme case where all the Hamiltonian terms commute, Trotterization can implement the exact evolution without error. Previous studies have also established commutator analysis of Trotter error for systems with geometrical locality
[
22
]
and Lie-algebraic structures
[
74
]
, as well as certain low-order formulas
[
76
]
, including the first-order Lie-Trotter formula

\mathscr{S}_{1}(t)-e^{-itH}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\ e^{-i(t-\tau_{1})H}e^{-i\tau_{1}T}e^{i\tau_{2}T}\left[iT,iV\right]e^{-i\tau_{2}T}e^{-i\tau_{1}V}

(19)

and the second-order Suzuki formula

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{2}(t)-e^{-itH}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}\mathrm{d}\tau_{3}\ e^{-i(t-\tau_{1})H}e^{-i\frac{\tau_{1}}{2}V}

(20)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(e^{-i\tau_{3}T}\left[-iT,\left[-iT,-i\frac{V}{2}\right]\right]e^{i\tau_{3}T}+e^{i\frac{\tau_{3}}{2}V}\left[i\frac{V}{2},\left[i\frac{V}{2},iT\right]\right]e^{-i\frac{\tau_{3}}{2}V}\right)e^{-i\tau_{1}T}e^{-i\frac{\tau_{1}}{2}V}.

An analysis of the general case is, however, considerably more difficult and has remained elusive until the recent proof of commutator scaling of Trotter error
[
27
]
. Here, we introduce a stronger version of that result which can be proved as in
[
26
, Appendix C]
by combining Theorem 3, 4, and 5 of
[
27
]
without invoking the triangle inequality.
4
4

4

It is essential to invoke a representation of the Trotter error where nested commutators have at most a constant number of layers
[
27
]
. If such a representation were not used, Trotterization would have a worse asymptotic complexity
[
83
, Appendix B]
.

Proposition 1

(Commutator representation of Trotter error)
.

Let
H=T+V
be a two-term Hamiltonian and
\mathscr{S}_{p}(t)
be a
p
th-order formula. Define
H_{0}=V
and
H_{1}=T
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{p}(t)-e^{-itH}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},j}a_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},j}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})e^{-i(t-\tau_{1})H}

(21)

\displaystyle\cdot\mathscr{U}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},j}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\left[H_{\gamma_{p+1}},\cdots\left[H_{\gamma_{2}},H_{\gamma_{1}}\right]\right]\mathscr{W}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},j}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2}),

where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\{0,1\}^{p+1}
are binary vectors
5
5

5

We use bold symbols to represent vectors throughout this paper.
and
j
goes through a constant range of numbers (depending on the order
p
). Here,
\mathscr{U}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},j}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
and
\mathscr{W}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},j}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
are products of evolutions of
T
and
V
with time variables
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
and
a_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
are coefficients such that

\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\ \left\lvert a_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},j}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}\left(t^{p+1}\right).

(22)

As an immediate application, we find that the spectral norm of the Trotter error scales with nested commutators of the Hamiltonian terms, i.e.,

\left\lVert\mathscr{S}_{p}(t)-e^{-itH}\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\max_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left\lVert\left[H_{\gamma_{p+1}},\cdots\left[H_{\gamma_{2}},H_{\gamma_{1}}\right]\right]\right\rVert t^{p+1}\right).

(23)

Note that the use of
\max_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
in place of
\sum_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
does not change the scaling as
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
only ranges over a constant number of binary vectors. We then divide the evolution into
r
steps and apply the triangle inequality to obtain

\left\lVert\mathscr{S}_{p}^{r}(t/r)-e^{-itH}\right\rVert\leq r\left\lVert\mathscr{S}_{p}(t/r)-e^{-i\frac{t}{r}H}\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\max_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left\lVert\left[H_{\gamma_{p+1}},\cdots\left[H_{\gamma_{2}},H_{\gamma_{1}}\right]\right]\right\rVert\frac{t^{p+1}}{r^{p}}\right).

(24)

It thus suffices to choose

r=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\left(\max_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left\lVert\left[H_{\gamma_{p+1}},\cdots\left[H_{\gamma_{2}},H_{\gamma_{1}}\right]\right]\right\rVert\right)^{1/p}t^{1+1/p}}{\epsilon^{1/p}}\right)

(25)

to ensure that the error of simulation is no more than
\epsilon
.

The above analysis is versatile for computing the commutator dependence of Trotter error. Unfortunately, the resulting bound does not use prior knowledge of the initial state and will in particular be loose if the initial state lies within a low-energy subspace. On the other hand, recent work of Şahinoğlu and Somma proposed a Trotterization approach for simulating low-energy initial states but the commutativity of the Hamiltonian was ignored in their analysis
[
69
]
. Here, we address this by simultaneously using commutativity of the Hamiltonian and prior knowledge of the initial state to improve the simulation of a class of interacting electrons. We obtain further improvement when the electronic Hamiltonian has sparse interactions. In the following, we introduce preliminaries about the second-quantization representation (
Section
2.2
) and the notion of fermionic seminorm (
Section
2.3
), on which our analysis will be based.

2.2
Second-quantization representation

In this section, we review several facts about the second-quantization representation that are relevant to our analysis. We refer the reader to the book of Helgaker, Jørgensen, and Olsen
[
37
]
for a detailed discussion of this topic.

We use the abstract Fock space to represent electronic Hamiltonians. Specifically, for a system of
n
spin orbitals, we construct a
2^{n}
-dimensional space
\spn\{\lvert c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1}\rangle\}
spanned by the basis vectors
\lvert c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1}\rangle
, where
c_{j}=1
represents that mode
j
is occupied and
c_{j}=0
otherwise. General vectors in the Fock space, denoted by
\lvert\psi\rangle
or
\lvert\phi\rangle
, are then given by linear combinations of these orthonormal basis vectors. We define the
\eta
-electron subspace

\spn\{\lvert c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1}\rangle,\sum_{j}c_{j}=\eta\}
. By considering all
0\leq\eta\leq n
, we obtain the decomposition

\spn\left\{\lvert c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1}\rangle\right\}=\bigobot_{\eta=0}^{n}\spn\Big\{\lvert c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1}\rangle,\sum_{j}c_{j}=\eta\Big\},

(26)

where
\obot
denotes the orthogonal direct sum. Using bold symbol
\boldsymbol{c}
to represent an arbitrary
fermionic configuration
and
\left\lvert\boldsymbol{c}\right\rvert=\sum_{j}c_{j}
to denote the
Hamming weight
, we have

\spn\left\{\lvert\boldsymbol{c}\rangle\right\}=\bigobot_{\eta=0}^{n}\spn\Big\{\lvert\boldsymbol{c}\rangle,\left\lvert\boldsymbol{c}\right\rvert=\eta\Big\}.

(27)

We say that normalized vectors in the
\eta
-electron subspace form the
\eta
-electron manifold
and denote an arbitrary such vector by
\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle
or
\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle
.

The
n
elementary fermionic
creation operators
are defined through the relations

\displaystyle A_{j}^{\dagger}\lvert c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,0_{j},\ldots,c_{n-1}\rangle

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}c_{k}}\lvert c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,1_{j},\ldots,c_{n-1}\rangle,

(28)

\displaystyle A_{j}^{\dagger}\lvert c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,1_{j},\ldots,c_{n-1}\rangle

\displaystyle=0,

whereas the fermionic
annihilation operators
are defined by

\displaystyle A_{j}\lvert c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,0_{j},\ldots,c_{n-1}\rangle

\displaystyle=0,

(29)

\displaystyle A_{j}\lvert c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,1_{j},\ldots,c_{n-1}\rangle

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}c_{k}}\lvert c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,0_{j},\ldots,c_{n-1}\rangle.

The use of
\dagger
is justified by the fact that
A_{j}^{\dagger}
is indeed the Hermitian adjoint of
A_{j}
with respect to the inner product in the Fock space. We also introduce the
occupation-number operators

N_{j}=A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{j}
and add them together to get the
particle-number operator

N=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}N_{j}
.

Fermionic creation and annihilation operators satisfy the canonical anticommutation relations

A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}^{\dagger}+A_{k}^{\dagger}A_{j}^{\dagger}=A_{j}A_{k}+A_{k}A_{j}=0,\qquad A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}+A_{k}A_{j}^{\dagger}=\delta_{j,k}I,

(30)

where the Kronecker-delta function
\delta_{j,k}
is one if
j=k
and zero otherwise. Applying these, we obtain the following commutation relations of second-quantized fermionic operators.

Proposition 2

(Commutation relations of fermionic operators)
.

The following commutation relations hold for second-quantized fermionic operators:

1.

\left[A_{l}^{\dagger}A_{m},A_{j}^{\dagger}\right]=\delta_{j,m}A_{l}^{\dagger}
,
\left[A_{l}^{\dagger}A_{m},A_{k}\right]=-\delta_{k,l}A_{m}
;

2.

\left[N_{l},A_{j}^{\dagger}\right]=\delta_{l,j}A_{j}^{\dagger}
,
\left[N_{l},A_{k}\right]=-\delta_{l,k}A_{k}
;

3.

\left[N,A_{j}^{\dagger}\right]=A_{j}^{\dagger}
,
\left[N,A_{k}\right]=-A_{k}
;

4.

\left[N_{l},N_{m}\right]=0
.

We say a fermionic operator is
number-preserving
if every
\eta
-electron subspace is invariant under the action of this operator. Equivalently, operator
X
is number-preserving if and only if it commutes with the particle-number operator, i.e.,
\left[N,X\right]=0
. Yet another equivalent definition is based on the notion of
\eta
-electron projections
: letting
\Pi_{\eta}
be orthogonal projections onto the
\eta
-electron subspaces, then
X
is number-preserving if and only if it commutes with every
\Pi_{\eta}
, namely,
\left[\Pi_{\eta},X\right]=0
. In the matrix representation,
X
is block-diagonalized by the set of
\eta
-electron projections
\{\Pi_{\eta}\}
.

A special example of number-preserving operator is the particle-number operator
N
, which acts as a scalar multiplication by
\eta
within the
\eta
-electron subspace. Other examples include
excitation operators

A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}
, occupation-number operators
N_{l}
, and elementary exponentials in the Trotterization algorithm
e^{-it\sum_{j,k}\tau_{j,k}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}}
and
e^{-it\sum_{l,m}\nu_{l,m}N_{l}N_{m}}
. The fermionic Fourier transform
[
29
]
as given below is also number-preserving:
6
6

6

This can alternatively be proved using the fact that the fermionic Fourier transform is generated by the fermionic swap and Hadamard gate
[

, both of which are number-preserving.

\mathrm{FFFT}^{\dagger}\cdot A_{j}^{\dagger}\cdot\mathrm{FFFT}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}e^{-\frac{2\pi ijl}{n}}A_{l}^{\dagger},\qquad\mathrm{FFFT}^{\dagger}\cdot A_{k}\cdot\mathrm{FFFT}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}e^{\frac{2\pi ikm}{n}}A_{m},

(31)

since

\displaystyle\mathrm{FFFT}^{\dagger}\cdot N\cdot\mathrm{FFFT}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathrm{FFFT}^{\dagger}\cdot A_{j}^{\dagger}\cdot\mathrm{FFFT}\cdot\mathrm{FFFT}^{\dagger}\cdot A_{j}\cdot\mathrm{FFFT}

(32)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l,m=0}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}e^{\frac{2\pi ij(m-l)}{n}}\right)A_{l}^{\dagger}A_{m}=\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}A_{l}^{\dagger}A_{l}=N.

In fact, the set of number-preserving operators contains identity and is closed under linear combination, multiplication, Hermitian conjugation, and taking limit.

Proposition 3

(Number-preserving operators as a closed unital

.

The following operators are respectively number-preserving:

1.

the identity operator

;

2.

\lambda X+\mu Y

are number-preserving, and
\lambda

\mu

3.

XY
, if
X
and

are number-preserving;

4.

X^{\dagger}
, if
X

5.

\lim\limits_{i\rightarrow\infty}X_{i}

X_{i}
are number-preserving and the limit exists.

2.3
Fermionic seminorm

We now introduce the notion of fermionic seminorm, which we use to quantify the error of the Trotterization algorithm that takes the prior knowledge of the initial state into consideration.

For any number-preserving operator
X
and
0\leq\eta\leq n
, we define the
fermionic
\eta
-seminorm
as the maximum transition amplitude within the
\eta
-electron manifold:

\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}:=\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle,\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert,

(33)

where
\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle,\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle
are quantum states containing
\eta
electrons.
7
7

7

Note that it is possible to extend this to define
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\eta\rightarrow\xi}
for operators that map the
\eta
-electron subspace to
\xi
-electron subspace, although this is not needed in our analysis and will not be further pursued here.
When there is no ambiguity, we drop the dependence on
\eta
and call
\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}
the fermionic seminorm of
X
. As the name suggests and the following proposition confirms, the fermionic seminorm is indeed a seminorm defined on the closed unital
\dagger
-subalgebra of number-preserving operators.

Proposition 4

(Seminorm properties)
.

The following properties hold for the fermionic seminorm:

1.

\left\lVert\lambda X\right\rVert_{\eta}=\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}
, if
X
is number-preserving and
\lambda
is a complex number;

2.

\left\lVert X+Y\right\rVert_{\eta}\leq\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}+\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{\eta}
, if
X
and
Y
are number-preserving;

3.

\left\lVert XY\right\rVert_{\eta}\leq\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{\eta}
, if
X
and
Y
are number-preserving;

4.

\left\lVert I\right\rVert_{\eta}=1
;

5.

\left\lVert UXW\right\rVert_{\eta}=\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}
, if
U,X,W
are number-preserving and
U,W
are unitary;

6.

\left\lVert X^{\dagger}\right\rVert_{\eta}=\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}
, if
X
is number-preserving.

Proof.

We will only prove the third statement, as the remaining follow directly from the definition of the fermionic seminorm.
We consider

\displaystyle\left\lVert XY\right\rVert_{\eta}

\displaystyle=\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle,\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert XY\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert

(34)

\displaystyle=\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle,\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert X\Pi_{\eta}\Pi_{\eta}Y\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lVert\Pi_{\eta}X^{\dagger}\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rVert\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lVert\Pi_{\eta}Y\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rVert,

where
\Pi_{\eta}
is the orthogonal projection onto the
\eta
-electron subspace and the last step follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. To proceed, we optimize over an arbitrary state
\lvert\varphi\rangle
to get

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Pi_{\eta}X^{\dagger}\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\max_{\lvert\varphi\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\varphi\rvert\Pi_{\eta}X^{\dagger}\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert

(35)

\displaystyle=\max_{\lvert\varphi\rangle}\left\lVert\Pi_{\eta}\lvert\varphi\rangle\right\rVert\left\lvert\frac{\langle\varphi\rvert\Pi_{\eta}}{\left\lVert\Pi_{\eta}\lvert\varphi\rangle\right\rVert}X^{\dagger}\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert X^{\dagger}\right\rVert_{\eta}=\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}

assuming
\Pi_{\eta}\lvert\varphi\rangle\neq 0
, as the case
\Pi_{\eta}\lvert\varphi\rangle=0
never leads to maximality. But on the other hand,

\displaystyle\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert X^{\dagger}\right\rVert_{\eta}=\max_{\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle,\lvert\varphi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\varphi_{\eta}\rvert X^{\dagger}\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert

(36)

\displaystyle=\max_{\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle,\lvert\varphi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\varphi_{\eta}\rvert\Pi_{\eta}X^{\dagger}\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle,\lvert\varphi\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\varphi\rvert\Pi_{\eta}X^{\dagger}\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert=\max_{\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lVert\Pi_{\eta}X^{\dagger}\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rVert,

implying
\max_{\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lVert\Pi_{\eta}X^{\dagger}\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rVert=\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}
. Similarly, we have
\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lVert\Pi_{\eta}Y\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rVert=\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{\eta}
. This completes the proof of the third statement.
∎

The fermionic seminorm, as defined in (
33
) by the maximum transition amplitude within the
\eta
-electron manifold, provides a reasonable metric for quantifying the error of digital quantum simulation with initial-state constraints. Indeed, a seminorm similar to our definition was used by Somma
[
73
]
for analyzing quantum simulation of bosonic Hamiltonians. However, we point out that this is not the only error metric that takes the prior knowledge of the initial state into account. Recent work
[
69
]
analyzed the low-energy simulation of
k
-local frustration-free Hamiltonians by computing the spectral norm of Trotter error projected on the low-energy subspace. However, the following proposition shows that these two error metrics are the same for fermionic systems.

Proposition 5

(Fermionic seminorm as a projected spectral norm)
.

For any number-preserving operator
X
, it holds that

\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}=\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle,\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert=\left\lVert X\Pi_{\eta}\right\rVert.

(37)

Proof.

The underlying idea behind this proposition is already hinted in the proof of
Proposition
4
. We have

\displaystyle\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle,\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle,\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert\Pi_{\eta}X\Pi_{\eta}\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert

(38)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\lvert\psi\rangle,\lvert\phi\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\phi\rvert\Pi_{\eta}X\Pi_{\eta}\lvert\psi\rangle\right\rvert=\left\lVert\Pi_{\eta}X\Pi_{\eta}\right\rVert.

But on the other hand,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Pi_{\eta}X\Pi_{\eta}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\max_{\lvert\psi\rangle,\lvert\phi\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\phi\rvert\Pi_{\eta}X\Pi_{\eta}\lvert\psi\rangle\right\rvert

(39)

\displaystyle=\max_{\lvert\psi\rangle,\lvert\phi\rangle}\left\lVert\Pi_{\eta}\lvert\phi\rangle\right\rVert\left\lVert\Pi_{\eta}\lvert\psi\rangle\right\rVert\left\lvert\frac{\langle\phi\rvert\Pi_{\eta}}{\left\lVert\Pi_{\eta}\lvert\phi\rangle\right\rVert}X\frac{\Pi_{\eta}\lvert\psi\rangle}{\left\lVert\Pi_{\eta}\lvert\psi\rangle\right\rVert}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle,\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert

assuming
\Pi_{\eta}\lvert\phi\rangle\neq 0
and
\Pi_{\eta}\lvert\psi\rangle\neq 0
, as the zero vector will not lead to maximality. The proposition then follows since number-preserving operator
X
commutes with the
\eta
-electron projection
\Pi_{\eta}
.
∎

Another common approach to quantify the error of digital quantum simulation is to take the maximum expectation
\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\psi_{\eta}\rvert\cdot\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert
within the
\eta
-electron manifold. This approach is used by previous work
[
6
,
65
,
28
]
and appears to give a natural metric when digital quantum simulation is used as a subroutine in phase estimation. We show that this only differs from our definition (
33
) by at most a constant factor, reaffirming the fermionic seminorm as a proper error metric for simulating fermionic systems.

Proposition 6

(Transition amplitude and expectation)
.

For any number-preserving operator
X
, the following statements hold:

1.

\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle,\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert=\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\psi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert
, if
X
is Hermitian;

2.

\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle,\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert=\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle}\sqrt{\langle\psi_{\eta}\rvert X^{\dagger}X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle}

(
equivalently,
\left\lVert X^{\dagger}X\right\rVert_{\eta}=\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}^{2}
)
;

3.

\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\psi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert\leq\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle,\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert\leq 2\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\psi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert
.
8
8

8

Note that the second inequality is tight by considering
X=A_{0}^{\dagger}A_{1}
on a fermionic system with two spin orbitals and one electron.

Proof.

The first statement follows from the fact that
\Pi_{\eta}X\Pi_{\eta}
is Hermitian and that the spectral norm of a Hermitian operator is its largest eigenvalue in absolute value. For the second statement,

\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle,\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert=\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}=\left\lVert X\Pi_{\eta}\right\rVert=\sqrt{\left\lVert\Pi_{\eta}X^{\dagger}X\Pi_{\eta}\right\rVert}=\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle}\sqrt{\langle\psi_{\eta}\rvert X^{\dagger}X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle}.

(40)

The first inequality of Statement 3 is trivial. For the second inequality, we apply the polarization identity

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Big(\left(\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert+\langle\psi_{\eta}\rvert\right)X\left(\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle+\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right)-\left(\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert-\langle\psi_{\eta}\rvert\right)X\left(\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle-\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right)

(41)

\displaystyle-i\left(\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert-i\langle\psi_{\eta}\rvert\right)X\left(\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle+i\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right)+i\left(\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert+i\langle\psi_{\eta}\rvert\right)X\left(\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle-i\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right)\Big)

to obtain

\displaystyle\left\lvert\langle\phi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert

(42)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\max_{\lvert\varphi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\varphi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\varphi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert}{4}\left(\left\lVert\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle+\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle-\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle+i\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle-i\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rVert^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=2\max_{\lvert\varphi_{\eta}\rangle}\left\lvert\langle\varphi_{\eta}\rvert X\lvert\varphi_{\eta}\rangle\right\rvert,

from which the claimed inequality follows by maximizing over states
\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle
and
\lvert\phi_{\eta}\rangle
.
∎

We now apply
Proposition
1
to compute the fermionic seminorm of Trotter error, obtaining

\left\lVert\mathscr{S}_{p}(t)-e^{-itH}\right\rVert_{\eta}=\mathcal{O}\left(\max_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left\lVert\left[H_{\gamma_{p+1}},\cdots\left[H_{\gamma_{2}},H_{\gamma_{1}}\right]\right]\right\rVert_{\eta}t^{p+1}\right).

(43)

We find that the resulting error bound depends on the fermionic seminorm of nested commutators, and the performance of digital quantum simulation can thus be potentially improved by simultaneously exploiting commutativity of the Hamiltonian and prior knowledge of the initial state. However, the main difficulty here is to give a tight estimate of
\left\lVert\left[H_{\gamma_{p+1}},\cdots\left[H_{\gamma_{2}},H_{\gamma_{1}}\right]\right]\right\rVert_{\eta}
, which seems technically challenging to address. To this end, we develop two approaches for bounding the expectation/transition amplitude of general fermionic operators in
Section
3
and
Section
4
and prove our main result
Theorem
1
, establish the tightness of our bound in
Section
5
, and discuss applications and further implications of our result in
Section
6
and
Section
7
.

3
Recursive bound on the expectation of fermionic operators

In this section, we present our first approach for bounding the expectation of fermionic operators, and thereby bounding the fermionic seminorm of Trotter error. We introduce in
Section
3.1
the main techniques used in our approach, including an operator Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, a diagonalization procedure, and a Hölder-type inequality for the expectation value. We then describe our approach in detail and apply it to prove Eq. (
2
) of our main result
Theorem
1
. The proof is based on induction: we analyze the base case in
Section
3.2
and the inductive step in
Section
3.3
, respectively.

3.1
Main techniques

Recall that the main technical challenge to estimate the simulation error of the electronic Hamiltonian (
1
) is to bound the fermionic seminorm
\left\lVert\left[H_{\gamma_{p+1}},\cdots\left[H_{\gamma_{2}},H_{\gamma_{1}}\right]\right]\right\rVert_{\eta}
, where
\gamma_{j}=0,1
,
H_{0}=V
and
H_{1}=T
. Applying the commutation relations in
Proposition
2
, we see that we need to analyze a general fermionic operator of the form

X=\sum_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l}}w_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l}}\ \cdots A_{j_{x}}^{\dagger}\cdots N_{l_{z}}\cdots A_{k_{y}}\cdots

(44)

Our first approach starts by reexpressing the fermionic seminorm of
X
using the expectation of
X^{\dagger}X
:

\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}=\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle}\sqrt{\langle\psi_{\eta}\rvert X^{\dagger}X\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle}.

(45)

We note that
X^{\dagger}X
is a positive semidefinite operator, and an upper bound of it with respect to the partial ordering of positive semidefiniteness will therefore give a bound on the expectation value. We achieve this by contracting the corresponding indices in
X
and
X^{\dagger}
, using either an operator Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (
Lemma
1
) or diagonalization (
Lemma
2
).

Lemma 1

(Operator Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
[
61
, Proposition 3.4]
)
.

For any finite lists of operators
\{B_{j}\}
and
\{C_{j}\}
with the same cardinality, we have

-\sum_{j,k}B_{j}^{\dagger}C_{k}^{\dagger}C_{k}B_{j}\leq\sum_{j,k}B_{j}^{\dagger}C_{k}^{\dagger}C_{j}B_{k}\leq\sum_{j,k}B_{j}^{\dagger}C_{k}^{\dagger}C_{k}B_{j},

(46)

where Hermitian operators are partially ordered according to the positive semidefiniteness.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j,k}\left(C_{k}B_{j}\mp C_{j}B_{k}\right)^{\dagger}\left(C_{k}B_{j}\mp C_{j}B_{k}\right)

(47)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}\left(B_{j}^{\dagger}C_{k}^{\dagger}C_{k}B_{j}\mp B_{k}^{\dagger}C_{j}^{\dagger}C_{k}B_{j}\mp B_{j}^{\dagger}C_{k}^{\dagger}C_{j}B_{k}+B_{k}^{\dagger}C_{j}^{\dagger}C_{j}B_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{j,k}B_{j}^{\dagger}C_{k}^{\dagger}C_{k}B_{j}\mp 2\sum_{j,k}B_{j}^{\dagger}C_{k}^{\dagger}C_{j}B_{k}.

This implies

\pm\sum_{j,k}B_{j}^{\dagger}C_{k}^{\dagger}C_{j}B_{k}\leq\sum_{jk}B_{j}^{\dagger}C_{k}^{\dagger}C_{k}B_{j},

(48)

from which the claimed inequality follows.
∎

Lemma 2

(Diagonalization)
.

For any finite list of operators
\{B_{j}\}
and Hermitian coefficient matrix
\mu
, we have

-\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert\sum_{j}B_{j}^{\dagger}B_{j}\leq\sum_{j,k}\mu_{j,k}B_{j}^{\dagger}B_{k}\leq\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert\sum_{j}B_{j}^{\dagger}B_{j},

(49)

where Hermitian operators are partially ordered according to the positive semidefiniteness.

Proof.

Since
\mu
is Hermitian, we may diagonalize it to
\widetilde{\mu}
by unitary transformation
w
as

\mu=w^{\dagger}\widetilde{\mu}w,

(50)

where
\widetilde{\mu}
is a diagonal matrix with all eigenvalues of
\mu
as the diagonal elements. We then define
\widetilde{B}_{l}:=\sum_{k}w_{l,k}B_{k}
so that

\sum_{j,k}\mu_{j,k}B_{j}^{\dagger}B_{k}=\sum_{l}\widetilde{\mu}_{l}\widetilde{B}_{l}^{\dagger}\widetilde{B}_{l},

(51)

which implies

-\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert\sum_{l}\widetilde{B}_{l}^{\dagger}\widetilde{B}_{l}\leq\sum_{j,k}\mu_{j,k}B_{j}^{\dagger}B_{k}\leq\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert\sum_{l}\widetilde{B}_{l}^{\dagger}\widetilde{B}_{l}.

(52)

But
\sum_{l}\widetilde{B}_{l}^{\dagger}\widetilde{B}_{l}
has identity as the coefficient matrix which is invariant under a change of basis:

\sum_{l}\widetilde{B}_{l}^{\dagger}\widetilde{B}_{l}=\sum_{j}B_{j}^{\dagger}B_{j}.

(53)

This completes the proof.
∎

By applying
Lemma
1
or
Lemma
2
, we can get a bound of
X^{\dagger}X
with respect to the partial ordering of positive semidefiniteness, with one pair of the corresponding indices in
X
and
X^{\dagger}
contracted. Indeed, these techniques were used by Otte to establish the boundedness of quadratic fermionic operators in infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces
[
61
]
. However, the difficulty here is that we need to handle more complex products of fermionic operators in the Trotter error estimate. To this end, we prove a Hölder-type inequality for the expectation value, which allows us to apply
Lemma
1
and
Lemma
2
recursively to get a desired bound.

Lemma 3

(Hölder-type inequality for expectation)
.

For any finite lists of fermionic operators
\{B_{j}\}
and
\{C_{k}\}
with the same cardinality,

\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle}\langle\psi_{\eta}\rvert\sum_{j}B_{j}^{\dagger}C_{j}^{\dagger}C_{j}B_{j}\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\leq\max_{\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle}\langle\psi_{\eta}\rvert\sum_{j}B_{j}^{\dagger}B_{j}\lvert\psi_{\eta}\rangle\max_{k,\lvert\phi_{\xi}\rangle}\langle\phi_{\xi}\rvert C_{k}^{\dagger}C_{k}\lvert\phi_{\xi}\rangle,

(54)

where we assume
B_{j}
map the
\eta
-electron subspace to the
\xi
-electron subspace and
C_{j}
are number-preserving. In terms of the fermionic seminorm, we have

\Big\|\sum_{j}B_{j}^{\dagger}C_{j}^{\dagger}C_{j}B_{j}\Big\|_{\eta}\leq\Big\|\sum_{j}B_{j}^{\dagger}B_{j}\Big\|_{\eta}\max_{k}\left\lVert C_{k}^{\dagger}C_{k}\right\rVert_{\xi}.

(55)

Proof.

The claimed inequality follows from

\displaystyle\Big\|\sum_{j}B_{j}^{\dagger}C_{j}^{\dagger}C_{j}B_{j}\Big\|_{\eta}

\displaystyle=\Big\|\sum_{j}B_{j}^{\dagger}\Pi_{\xi}C_{j}^{\dagger}C_{j}\Pi_{\xi}B_{j}\Big\|_{\eta}

(56)

\displaystyle\leq\bigg\|\sum_{j}\left\lVert\Pi_{\xi}C_{j}^{\dagger}C_{j}\Pi_{\xi}\right\rVert B_{j}^{\dagger}B_{j}\bigg\|_{\eta}

\displaystyle\leq\Big\|\sum_{j}B_{j}^{\dagger}B_{j}\Big\|_{\eta}\max_{k}\left\lVert\Pi_{\xi}C_{k}^{\dagger}C_{k}\Pi_{\xi}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\Big\|\sum_{j}B_{j}^{\dagger}B_{j}\Big\|_{\eta}\max_{k}\left\lVert C_{k}^{\dagger}C_{k}\right\rVert_{\xi}.

∎

Using the above lemmas, we can now prove Eq. (
2
) of our main result
Theorem
1
by induction. We analyze the base case in
Section
3.2
and the inductive step in
Section
3.3
.

3.2
Single-layer commutator

We now prove Eq. (
2
) of our main result
Theorem

by induction. In the base case, we consider simulating the interacting-electronic Hamiltonian (
1
) using the first-order formula
\mathscr{S}_{1}(t)
. We know from (

\left\lVert\mathscr{S}_{1}(t)-e^{-itH}\right\rVert_{\eta}\leq\frac{t^{2}}{2}\left\lVert\left[T,V\right]\right\rVert_{\eta},

(57)

where
T=\sum_{j,k}\tau_{j,k}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}
and
V=\sum_{l,m}\nu_{l,m}N_{l}N_{m}
. Our goal is to show that

\left\lVert\left[T,V\right]\right\rVert_{\eta}=\mathcal{O}\left(\left\lVert\tau\right\rVert\left\lVert\nu\right\rVert_{\max}\eta^{2}\right).

(58)

To this end, we apply
Proposition
2
to expand the single-layer commutator
\left[T,V\right]
into linear combinations of fermionic creation, annihilation, and occupation-number operators. We have

\displaystyle\left[T,V\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,l,m}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{l,m}\left[A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k},N_{l}N_{m}\right]

(59)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,l,m}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{l,m}A_{j}^{\dagger}\left[A_{k},N_{l}N_{m}\right]+\sum_{j,k,l,m}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{l,m}\left[A_{j}^{\dagger},N_{l}N_{m}\right]A_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,m}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{k,m}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}N_{m}+\sum_{j,k,l}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{l,k}A_{j}^{\dagger}N_{l}A_{k}

\displaystyle-\sum_{j,k,m}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{j,m}A_{j}^{\dagger}N_{m}A_{k}-\sum_{j,k,l}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{l,j}N_{l}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}.

At this stage, it is possible to directly bound the terms in the last equality using
Lemma
1
,
Lemma
2
, and

from the previous subsection. However, we will further commute the occupation-number operator in between the creation and annihilation operators, obtaining

\displaystyle\left[T,V\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,m}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{k,m}A_{j}^{\dagger}N_{m}A_{k}+\sum_{j,k}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{k,k}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}+\sum_{j,k,l}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{l,k}A_{j}^{\dagger}N_{l}A_{k}

(60)

\displaystyle-\sum_{j,k,m}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{j,m}A_{j}^{\dagger}N_{m}A_{k}-\sum_{j,k}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{j,j}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}-\sum_{j,k,l}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{l,j}A_{j}^{\dagger}N_{l}A_{k}.

This additional commutation leads to an error bound with the same asymptotic scaling but a slightly larger prefactor. The benefit is that the analysis can be directly extended to handle the inductive step in the next subsection.

Proposition 7

(Structure of single-layer commutator)
.

Let
H=T+V=\sum_{j,k}\tau_{j,k}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}+\sum_{l,m}\nu_{l,m}N_{l}N_{m}
be an interacting-electronic Hamiltonian (
1
). Then, the commutator
[T,V]
has the expansion

\displaystyle\left[T,V\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,m}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{k,m}A_{j}^{\dagger}N_{m}A_{k}+\sum_{j,k}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{k,k}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}+\sum_{j,k,l}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{l,k}A_{j}^{\dagger}N_{l}A_{k}

(61)

\displaystyle-\sum_{j,k,m}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{j,m}A_{j}^{\dagger}N_{m}A_{k}-\sum_{j,k}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{j,j}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}-\sum_{j,k,l}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{l,j}A_{j}^{\dagger}N_{l}A_{k}.

Proposition 8

(Fermionic seminorm of single-layer commutator)
.

Let
H=T+V=\sum_{j,k}\tau_{j,k}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}+\sum_{l,m}\nu_{l,m}N_{l}N_{m}
be an interacting-electronic Hamiltonian (
1
). Then,

1.

\left\lVert\sum_{j,k,m}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{k,m}A_{j}^{\dagger}N_{m}A_{k}\right\rVert_{\eta}\leq\left\lVert\tau\right\rVert\left\lVert\nu\right\rVert_{\max}\eta^{2}
;

2.

\left\lVert\sum_{j,k}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{k,k}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}\right\rVert_{\eta}\leq\left\lVert\tau\right\rVert\left\lVert\nu\right\rVert_{\max}\eta
;

3.

\left\lVert\sum_{j,k,l}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{l,k}A_{j}^{\dagger}N_{l}A_{k}\right\rVert_{\eta}\leq\left\lVert\tau\right\rVert\left\lVert\nu\right\rVert_{\max}\eta^{2}
;

4.

\left\lVert\sum_{j,k,m}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{j,m}A_{j}^{\dagger}N_{m}A_{k}\right\rVert_{\eta}\leq\left\lVert\tau\right\rVert\left\lVert\nu\right\rVert_{\max}\eta^{2}
;

5.

\left\lVert\sum_{j,k}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{j,j}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}\right\rVert_{\eta}\leq\left\lVert\tau\right\rVert\left\lVert\nu\right\rVert_{\max}\eta
;

6.

\left\lVert\sum_{j,k,l}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{l,j}A_{j}^{\dagger}N_{l}A_{k}\right\rVert_{\eta}\leq\left\lVert\tau\right\rVert\left\lVert\nu\right\rVert_{\max}\eta^{2}
.

Proof.

We present the proof of the first two statements here. The remaining justifications proceed in a similar way and are left to
Appendix
A
.

Letting
X=\sum_{j,k,m}\tau_{j,k}\nu_{k,m}A_{j}^{\dagger}N_{m}A_{k}
, we have
\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\eta}=\sqrt{\left\lVert X^{\dagger}X\right\rVert_{\eta}}
. Now,

\displaystyle X^{\dagger}X

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1},k_{1},m_{1},j_{2},k_{2},m_{2}}\bar{\tau}_{j_{1},k_{1}}\bar{\nu}_{k_{1},m_{1}}\tau_{j_{2},k_{2}}\nu_{k_{2},m_{2}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{m_{1}}A_{j_{1}}A_{j_{2}}^{\dagger}N_{m_{2}}A_{k_{2}}

(62)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1},k_{1},m_{1},k_{2},m_{2}}\bar{\tau}_{j_{1},k_{1}}\bar{\nu}_{k_{1},m_{1}}\tau_{j_{1},k_{2}}\nu_{k_{2},m_{2}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{m_{1}}N_{m_{2}}A_{k_{2}}

\displaystyle-\sum_{j_{1},k_{1},m_{1},j_{2},k_{2},m_{2}}\bar{\tau}_{j_{1},k_{1}}\bar{\nu}_{k_{1},m_{1}}\tau_{j_{2},k_{2}}\nu_{k_{2},m_{2}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{m_{1}}A_{j_{2}}^{\dagger}A_{j_{1}}N_{m_{2}}A_{k_{2}},

where
\bar{\tau}_{j_{1},k_{1}}
is the complex conjugate of
\tau_{j_{1},k_{1}}
and we have used the anti-commutation relation
A_{j_{1}}A_{j_{2}}^{\dagger}+A_{j_{2}}^{\dagger}A_{j_{1}}=\delta_{j_{1},j_{2}}I
. For the second term, we let
B_{j_{1}}^{\dagger}=\sum_{k_{1},m_{1}}\bar{\tau}_{j_{1},k_{1}}\bar{\nu}_{k_{1},m_{1}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{m_{1}}
and apply the operator Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (
Lemma
1
):

\displaystyle-\sum_{j_{1},k_{1},m_{1},j_{2},k_{2},m_{2}}\bar{\tau}_{j_{1},k_{1}}\bar{\nu}_{k_{1},m_{1}}\tau_{j_{2},k_{2}}\nu_{k_{2},m_{2}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{m_{1}}A_{j_{2}}^{\dagger}A_{j_{1}}N_{m_{2}}A_{k_{2}}

(63)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}}B_{j_{1}}^{\dagger}A_{j_{2}}^{\dagger}A_{j_{1}}B_{j_{2}}\leq\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}}B_{j_{1}}^{\dagger}A_{j_{2}}^{\dagger}A_{j_{2}}B_{j_{1}}=\sum_{j_{1}}B_{j_{1}}^{\dagger}NB_{j_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{1}}\sum_{k_{1},m_{1},k_{2},m_{2}}\bar{\tau}_{j_{1},k_{1}}\bar{\nu}_{k_{1},m_{1}}\tau_{j_{1},k_{2}}\nu_{k_{2},m_{2}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{m_{1}}NN_{m_{2}}A_{k_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{1}}\sum_{k_{1},m_{1},k_{2},m_{2}}\bar{\tau}_{j_{1},k_{1}}\bar{\nu}_{k_{1},m_{1}}\tau_{j_{1},k_{2}}\nu_{k_{2},m_{2}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{m_{1}}N_{m_{2}}A_{k_{2}}N

\displaystyle-\sum_{j_{1}}\sum_{k_{1},m_{1},k_{2},m_{2}}\bar{\tau}_{j_{1},k_{1}}\bar{\nu}_{k_{1},m_{1}}\tau_{j_{1},k_{2}}\nu_{k_{2},m_{2}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{m_{1}}N_{m_{2}}A_{k_{2}}.

This implies

\displaystyle X^{\dagger}X

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j_{1}}\sum_{k_{1},m_{1},k_{2},m_{2}}\bar{\tau}_{j_{1},k_{1}}\bar{\nu}_{k_{1},m_{1}}\tau_{j_{1},k_{2}}\nu_{k_{2},m_{2}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{m_{1}}N_{m_{2}}A_{k_{2}}N

(64)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\left(\sum_{j_{1}}\bar{\tau}_{j_{1},k_{1}}\tau_{j_{1},k_{2}}\right)\left(\sum_{m_{1}}\bar{\nu}_{k_{1},m_{1}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{m_{1}}\right)\left(\sum_{m_{2}}\nu_{k_{2},m_{2}}N_{m_{2}}A_{k_{2}}\right)N.

Note that
\sum_{j_{1}}\bar{\tau}_{j_{1},k_{1}}\tau_{j_{1},k_{2}}
gives the
(k_{1},k_{2})
matrix element of
\tau^{\dagger}\tau
. Then, we define
C_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}=\sum_{m_{1}}\bar{\nu}_{k_{1},m_{1}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{m_{1}}
and perform diagonalization (
Lemma
2
):

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\left(\sum_{j_{1}}\bar{\tau}_{j_{1},k_{1}}\tau_{j_{1},k_{2}}\right)\left(\sum_{m_{1}}\bar{\nu}_{k_{1},m_{1}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{m_{1}}\right)\left(\sum_{m_{2}}\nu_{k_{2},m_{2}}N_{m_{2}}A_{k_{2}}\right)

(65)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\left(\tau^{\dagger}\tau\right)_{k_{1},k_{2}}C_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}C_{k_{2}}\leq\left\lVert\tau^{\dagger}\tau\right\rVert\sum_{k_{1}}C_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}C_{k_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tau^{\dagger}\tau\right\rVert\sum_{k_{1}}\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}\bar{\nu}_{k_{1},m_{1}}\nu_{k_{1},m_{2}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{m_{1}}N_{m_{2}}A_{k_{1}}.

Now that the indices
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are contracted, we can apply the Hölder-type inequality for the expectation value (
Lemma
3
). To this end, we let
D_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}=\sum_{m_{1}}\bar{\nu}_{k_{1},m_{1}}N_{m_{1}}
and compute

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{k_{1}}\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}\bar{\nu}_{k_{1},m_{1}}\nu_{k_{1},m_{2}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{m_{1}}N_{m_{2}}A_{k_{1}}\right\rVert_{\eta}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\sum_{k_{1}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}D_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}D_{k_{1}}A_{k_{1}}\right\rVert_{\eta}

(66)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\sum_{k_{1}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}A_{k_{1}}\right\rVert_{\eta}\max_{k_{1}}\left\lVert D_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}D_{k_{1}}\right\rVert_{\eta-1}.

The first factor can be directly bounded as

\left\lVert\sum_{k_{1}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}A_{k_{1}}\right\rVert_{\eta}=\left\lVert N\right\rVert_{\eta}=\eta.

(67)

For the second factor, we have

\displaystyle D_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}D_{k_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}\bar{\nu}_{k_{1},m_{1}}\nu_{k_{1},m_{2}}N_{m_{1}}N_{m_{2}}=\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}\bar{\nu}_{k_{1},m_{1}}\nu_{k_{1},m_{2}}N_{m_{1}}N_{m_{2}}N_{m_{1}}

(68)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\nu\right\rVert_{\max}^{2}\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}N_{m_{1}}N_{m_{2}}N_{m_{1}}=\left\lVert\nu\right\rVert_{\max}^{2}N^{2},

which implies

\left\lVert D_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}D_{k_{1}}\right\rVert_{\eta-1}\leq\left\lVert\nu\right\rVert_{\max}^{2}\eta^{2}.

(69)

5
Tightness

We have already established multiple bounds in
Theorem
1
on the fermionic seminorm of the Trotter error. However, a common issue with the Trotterization algorithm is that its error estimate can be very loose for simulating specific systems. Here, we prove
Theorem
2
that demonstrates the tightness of our analysis for the interacting-electronic Hamiltonian (
1
).

Specifically, we construct concrete examples of interacting-electronic Hamiltonian
H=T+V
and lower-bound the fermionic seminorm of nested commutators:
\left\lVert[T,\ldots[T,V]]\right\rVert_{\eta}
in
Section
5.1
and
\left\lVert[V,\ldots[V,T]]\right\rVert_{\eta}
in
Section
5.2
. We show that the results almost match the upper bounds in
Theorem
1
. Since Trotter error depends on these nested commutators, this shows that our result is nearly tight modulo an application of the triangle inequality.

We also consider applications of our result to simulations of the Fermi-Hubbard Hamiltonian, which models many important properties of interacting electrons. This Hamiltonian is defined as

7
Discussion

We have given improved quantum simulations of a class of interacting electrons using Trotterization, by simultaneously exploiting commutativity of the Hamiltonian, sparsity of interactions, and prior knowledge of the initial state. We identified applications to simulating the plane-wave-basis electronic structure, improving the best previous result in second quantization up to a negligible factor while conditionally outperforming the first-quantized simulation. We obtained further speedups when the electronic Hamiltonian has
d
-sparse interactions, which gave faster Trotterization of the Fermi-Hubbard model. We constructed concrete electronic systems for which our bounds are almost saturated, providing a provable guarantee on the tightness of our analysis.

Our focus has been on the asymptotic performance of digital quantum simulation throughout this paper. However, we believe that the techniques we have developed can also be used to give quantum simulations with low constant-prefactor overhead, for instance, through a more careful application of our
Proposition
9
,
Proposition
10
,
Proposition
11
and
Proposition
12
. Such improvements would especially benefit the simulation of plane-wave-basis electronic structure, where many pairs of Hamiltonian terms commute and the number of electrons can be significantly smaller than the spin-orbital number. Existing numerical studies almost exclusively used the second-order Suzuki formula
[
42
,
84
,
5
,
65
,
19
]
and their results did not fully leverage the commutativity of the Hamiltonian and the initial-state knowledge, which may then be improved by the techniques presented here using general Trotterization schemes.

Our analysis is applicable to a class of electronic Hamiltonians of the form
H=\sum_{j,k}\tau_{j,k}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}+\sum_{l,m}\nu_{l,m}N_{l}N_{m}
. By imposing further constraints on the coefficients, we may somewhat sacrifice this generality but instead get further improvement on the simulation performance. One possibility is to consider the subclass of systems that are translation-invariant, i.e.,
\tau_{j,k}=\tau_{j+q,k+q}
and
\nu_{l,m}=\nu_{l+q,m+q}
. This translational invariance is used in the circuit implementations for both our applications (electronic-structure Hamiltonians and Fermi-Hubbard model), but is nevertheless ignored in the proof of our upper bounds (
Theorem
1
) and tightness result (
Theorem
2
). By incorporating additional features of the Hamiltonian such as translational invariance, it is plausible that our current complexity estimate can be further improved.

A natural problem that has yet to be addressed here is the simulation of electronic-structure Hamiltonians in a more compact molecular basis. Such Hamiltonians typically take the form
H=\sum_{j,k}h_{j,k}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}+\sum_{j,k,l,m}h_{j,k,l,m}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}A_{l}^{\dagger}A_{m}
, more complex than the electronic model (
1
) considered here. In this case, the exponentials of the two-body terms
\sum_{j,k,l,m}h_{j,k,l,m}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{k}A_{l}^{\dagger}A_{m}
do not have a convenient circuit implementation and our current approach is not directly applicable. This motivates further developments of
hybrid quantum simulation
, in which Trotterization is combined with more advanced quantum algorithms to speed up digital quantum simulation. We leave a detailed study of such problems as a subject for future work.

More generally, we could consider digital quantum simulations of other types of physical systems, such as bosonic systems
[
70
]
or fermion-boson interacting systems
[
72
]
.
We hope our techniques could offer insights to such problems and find further applications in digital quantum simulation beyond what have been discussed here.

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1},k_{1},l_{1},k_{2},l_{2}}\tau_{j_{1},k_{1}}\nu_{l_{1},j_{1}}\tau_{j_{1},k_{2}}\nu_{l_{2},j_{1}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{l_{1}}N_{l_{2}}A_{k_{2}}

\displaystyle-\sum_{j_{1},k_{1},l_{1},j_{2},k_{2},l_{2}}\tau_{j_{1},k_{1}}\nu_{l_{1},j_{1}}\tau_{j_{2},k_{2}}\nu_{l_{2},j_{2}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{l_{1}}A_{j_{2}}^{\dagger}A_{j_{1}}N_{l_{2}}A_{k_{2}}.

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j_{1},k_{1},l_{1},k_{2},l_{2}}\tau_{j_{1},k_{1}}\nu_{l_{1},j_{1}}\tau_{j_{1},k_{2}}\nu_{l_{2},j_{1}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{l_{1}}N_{l_{2}}A_{k_{2}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j_{1},k_{1},l_{1},j_{2},k_{2},l_{2}}\tau_{j_{1},k_{1}}\nu_{l_{1},j_{1}}\tau_{j_{1},k_{2}}\nu_{l_{2},j_{1}}A_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}N_{l_{1}}A_{j_{2}}^{\dagger}A_{j_{2}}N_{l_{2}}A_{k_{2}}
