A Proximal Distance Algorithm for Likelihood-Based Sparse Covariance Estimation
Jason Xu, Kenneth Lange

Abstract
This paper addresses the task of estimating a covariance matrix under a patternless sparsity assumption. In contrast to existing approaches based on thresholding or shrinkage penalties, we propose a likelihood-based method that regularizes the distance from the covariance estimate to a symmetric sparsity set. This formulation avoids unwanted shrinkage induced by more common norm penalties and enables optimization of the resulting non-convex objective by solving a sequence of smooth, unconstrained subproblems. These subproblems are generated and solved via the proximal distance version of the majorization-minimization principle. The resulting algorithm executes rapidly, gracefully handles settings where the number of parameters exceeds the number of cases, yields a positive definite solution, and enjoys desirable convergence properties. Empirically, we demonstrate that our approach outperforms competing methods by several metrics across a suite of simulated experiments. Its merits are illustrated on an international migration dataset and a classic case study on flow cytometry. Our findings suggest that the marginal and conditional dependency networks for the cell signalling data are more similar than previously concluded.



1
Introduction

The task of estimating a covariance matrix from randomly sampled data is central in multivariate analysis. Unfortunately, estimation is complicated by several statistical and computational obstacles.
Chief among the latter is the quadratic growth of the number of free parameters in the number of features
p
. If
n
is the number of cases, it is known statistically that the sample covariance estimator degrades as the ratio
p/n
increases
(
Stein 1956
)
and becomes singular as soon as
p>n
.
A more subtle difficulty lies in producing good estimators that maintain positive definiteness. Most approaches seek to mitigate the curse of dimensionality by imposing parsimony through assumptions on the size and structure of the effective parameters, a strategy that has proven successful in many applications. In this paper, we focus on the setting where the covariance matrix follows a patternless sparsity assumption. Here sparsity has an important interpretation; namely, zero entries in the covariance matrix encode marginal independence between features.

Since the work of
Stein 1956
, covariance estimation has remained an active area of research. Many regularized estimators have been proposed to achieve sparsity;
Pourahmadi 2011
,

Chi and Lange 2014
, and
Fan et al. 2016
provide excellent overviews. Some researchers assume a known ordering of variables. To their detriment, such estimators based on tapering, banding, or the Cholesky decomposition generally are sensitive to permutations of the features
(
Wu and Pourahmadi 2003
;
Bickel and Levina 2008a
;
Levina et al. 2008
;
Cai et al. 2010
;
Bien et al. 2016
)
. When no natural ordering is available, a simple tactic involves
thresholding
the sample covariance matrix by setting small estimated entries to zero
(
Karoui 2008
;
Bickel and Levina 2008b
;
Rothman et al. 2009
;
Cai and Liu 2011
)
. Though such elementwise operations straightforwardly induce sparsity, it is well-documented that the resulting estimator is not always positive definite in finite samples. Related Frobenius norm based approaches include an additional log-barrier term
(
Rothman 2012
)
or appeal to alternating directions methods
(
Xue et al. 2012
)
to enforce positive definiteness. Similar methods have been developed for sparse correlation estimation
(
Cui et al. 2016
)
. In general, great care must be taken in selecting thresholding constants to ensure positive definiteness. In many cases, the appropriate range is too narrow to induce an effective amount of sparsity
(
Azose et al. 2018
)
.

Penalized likelihood techniques offer an alternative to thresholding and are arguably the preferred method for estimating sparse precision (inverse covariance) matrices
(
Yuan and Lin 2007
;
Molstad and Rothman 2018
)
. Sparsity carries a different interpretation here: zero entries in the precision matrix encode conditional rather than marginal independence. In this case, the negative Gaussian log-likelihood is convex, which not only ensures that minimizers are global optima, but enables fast algorithms such as the graphical lasso
(
Friedman et al. 2008
)
that make estimation easy under convex penalties such as an
\ell_{1}
-norm term. Lasso penalization also comes with disadvantages such as shrinkage toward the origin, which may lead to biased estimates and the inclusion of spurious predictors.

Penalized likelihood estimation is decidedly more difficult in seeking a sparse covariance matrix. Because the negative log-likelihood in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is no longer convex, significant computational difficulties arise. These challenges may explain in part the smaller literature
on
this task relative to precision estimation.
Lam and Fan 2009
study the properties of
\ell_{1}
penalized covariance estimation, and
Bien and Tibshirani 2011
propose a majorization-minimization algorithm that makes use of generalized gradient descent. Under the latter approach, convergence hinges on imposing a Lipschitz differentiability assumption that is realized by restricting the space to a subset of the positive definite cone. In practice, this restriction introduces an additional inner optimization subproblem, which

is more cumbersome to implement
and may be numerically unstable even in moderate dimensions. Step-size selection can precipitate a delicate tradeoff between stability and practical rate of convergence.
Azose et al. 2018
build upon this work to propose a method for maximum a posteriori estimation that faces similar challenges. They report that cross-validation on a problem with
n=12
and dimension
p\approx 200
already becomes computationally impractical.

In the current paper, we revisit the penalized likelihood framework for sparse covariance estimation under a distance-to-set penalty in place of a norm penalty. In prior work, such distance penalties have proven effective in contexts such as generalized linear regression under both rank and sparsity constraints
(
Xu et al. 2017
)
.
Our penalization keeps parameter estimates close to the sparse constraint set while restricting estimates to the positive definite cone.
Neither additional assumptions on the structure of the covariance matrix nor prior knowledge of the location of zero entries is
imposed
(
Chaudhuri et al. 2007
)
. Our method thus performs model selection while delivering a positive definite estimate of the covariance matrix, avoiding the systematic shrinkage engendered by convex norm penalties.

Distance penalization also confers significant computational advantages. We develop a proximal distance algorithm that effectively solves our non-convex optimization problem. Like
Bien and Tibshirani 2011
, we
employ
the majorization-minimization principle. Our algorithm enjoys a descent property as it converges to a stationary point of the objective, automatically selects a good step-size, and yields closed-form solutions to its subproblems. Our algorithm tends to converge quickly because the underlying surrogate functions tightly approximate the likelihood. These advantages are illustrated by simulation studies and applications to real data on cell signaling and international migration.

2
Background and penalized formulation

Consider estimation of the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
given
n
independent, identically distributed random vectors
\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\sim N_{p}({\bf 0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
. Without loss of generality, we focus on the mean zero case and estimation of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
alone. In this scenario, the log-likelihood of the data is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{n}{2}\ln\det\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\frac{n}{2}\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S}),

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{S}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{T}
denotes the sample covariance matrix. When the data are weakly dependent or non-Gaussian, estimation may still proceed on the basis of
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, provided
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
is interpreted as a quasi-likelihood.
It is desirable that an estimator of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
be positive definite; previous work has achieved this imposing the domain constraint
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succ{\bf 0}
. Alternatively, we may set
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=-\infty
whenever
\Sigma
fails to be positive definite.

We seek to maximize (
1
) subject to the assumption that many of the entries in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
are zero. Accordingly, let
k
denote the number of nonzero entries in the upper triangle, and let
\lVert\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\rVert_{0}
denote the total number of nonzero entries in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. Sparse estimation of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
can be formally cast as the constrained optimization problem of minimizing

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln\det\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S})

(2)

subject to
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succ{\bf 0}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
belonging to the sparsity set

\mathcal{C}\mathop{\>\>\,}\nolimits=\mathop{\>\>\,}\nolimits\left\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathop{\mathbb{R}}\nolimits^{p\times p}:\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{T},\,\lVert\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\rVert_{0}\leq 2k+p\right\}.

(3)

Note here that that diagonal entries of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
are unconstrained.

Directly minimizing criterion (
2
) is challenging. Indeed, letting
m=\binom{p}{2}
, there are
\binom{m}{k}
different sparsity patterns for a model with
k
effective parameters. Thus, even ignoring the positive definiteness constraint, optimizing
f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
over
\mathcal{C}
quickly becomes combinatorially intractable. As a practical alternative, one can include a lasso penalty regularizing the
\ell_{1}
norm of a function of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. Convex relaxation of the
\ell_{0}
constraint appearing in
\mathcal{C}
in this fashion provides a viable means of promoting sparsity by proxy. For covariance estimation,
Bien and Tibshirani 2011
consider such a penalty applied to
\boldsymbol{A}\circ\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, where
\boldsymbol{A}
has non-negative entries interpretable as weights, and
\circ
denotes the Hadamard or element-wise product. The resulting optimization problem

\displaystyle\text{minimize}\left\{\ln\det\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S})+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{A}\circ\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\rVert_{1}\right\}\text{ subject to \,}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succ{\bf 0}

(4)

remains nontrivial. This nonconvex objective equals the difference of two convex functions. Exploiting this structure,
Bien and Tibshirani 2011
propose a majorization-minimization (MM) algorithm described in the next section.

Including a lasso penalty as a proxy for the sparsity constraint entails shrinking the solution globally toward the origin. Such shrinkage biases parameter estimates toward zero and tends to produce false positives. Nonetheless, several advantages have made the approach popular. Lasso penalties are convex, and their inclusion for solving convex objectives not only admits unique minimizers, but fast algorithms are available for finding their solutions
(
Friedman et al. 2008
)
.
Unfortunately, as the covariance likelihood is already non-convex, adding an
\ell_{1}
penalty in covariance estimation does not yield a convex objective and
does not enforce
positive definiteness
. The remedy of embedding an inner iterative algorithm, such as alternating directions, within an outer gradient descent algorithm is often slow and unstable. Failures of positive definiteness also beset simple thresholding approaches
(
Rothman et al. 2009
;
Rothman 2012
)
, and similar remedies in this context are subject to the same criticisms
(
Xue et al. 2012
)
.

As an alternative to solve problem (
2
), we propose minimizing the penalized objective

h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=\ln\det\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S})+\frac{\rho}{2}\text{dist}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\mathcal{C})^{2},

(5)

where
\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\mathcal{C})=\inf\{\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\boldsymbol{A}\|:\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathcal{C}\}
denotes the Euclidean distance from
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
to
\mathcal{C}
. The distance penalty pulls the solution
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
toward
\mathcal{C}
and equals zero precisely when
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{C}
. This novel formulation now entails solving an unconstrained optimization problem, but coincides with the original objective (2) in the limit as
\rho
tends to
\infty
. This is summarized in the following restatement of the classical penalty method
(
Courant 1943
)
.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that both the loss
f(\boldsymbol{x})
and the nonnegative penalty
p(\boldsymbol{x})
are continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and that the penalized objectives

\displaystyle F_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})+\rho_{n}p(\boldsymbol{x})

are coercive on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. For any sequence
\rho_{n}
increasing to
\infty
, there is a corresponding sequence of minimizers
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
with
f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\leq f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1})
. Further, any cluster point of this sequence resides in the feasible region
\mathcal{S}=\{\boldsymbol{x}:p(\boldsymbol{x})=0\}
and attains the minimum value of
f(\boldsymbol{x})
. Finally, if
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is coercive and possesses a unique minimizer
\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}
in
S
, then the sequence
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
converges to
\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}
.

This result justifies improving the objective (
5
) while gradually increasing the penalty parameter
\rho
instead of directly tackling the constrained problem (
2
). We show in the Appendix that positive definiteness of the sample covariance
\boldsymbol{S}
is sufficient to satisfy the technical requirement of coercivity. Notably, coercivity fails to hold when
p>n
, but it can be reintroduced by adding a small multiple of the identity to
\boldsymbol{S}
. We later observe that this safeguard is numerically unnecessary in practice, with a negligible difference in performance. In its favor, the distance penalized formulation circumvents explicit consideration of the constraints, evades global shrinkage, and lends itself to the derivation of a practical algorithm via majorization-minimization.

3
Majorization-Minimization

Majorization-minimization (MM) algorithms are becoming increasingly popular in solving large-scale optimization problems in statistics and machine learning
(
Mairal 2015
;
Lange 2016
;
Xu and Lange 2019
)
. A majorization-minimization algorithm successively minimizes a sequence of surrogate functions
g(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{k})
that dominate an objective function
f(\boldsymbol{x})
and are tangent to it at the current iterate
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
. Decreasing
g(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{k})
automatically engenders a decrease in
f(\boldsymbol{x})
, and a local optimum of
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is found by successively minimizing the sequence of surrogates.

Majorization requires two conditions: tangency and domination. Formally, these amount to
g(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}~\mid~\boldsymbol{x}_{k})~=~f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})
and
g(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\geq f(\boldsymbol{x})
for every
\boldsymbol{x}
. The resulting update
\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}~=~\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits_{\boldsymbol{x}}g(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{k})
implies the string of inequalities

f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1})\leq g(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\leq g(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{k})=f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}),

(6)

validating the descent property. Examination of the proof of descent (
6
) shows that exact minimization of
g(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{k})
is not strictly necessary, a practical advantage that we will utilize. The celebrated expectation-maximization (EM) principle
(
Dempster et al. 1977
)
for maximum likelihood estimation is a special case of this principle that relies on the notion of missing data.
In this setting the surrogate
g(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{k})
is defined as the expected value of the complete data log-likelihood given the observed data.

The majorization-minimization principle thus offers a general recipe for converting a hard optimization problem into a sequence of manageable subproblems. The distance-to-set penalty
\text{dist}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathcal{C})^{2}
enters this framework
through
distance majorization

(
Chi et al. 2014
)
. The key idea is to write the penalty in terms of the Euclidean norm as

\text{dist}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathcal{C})=\min_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{C}}\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{2}=\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{x})\rVert_{2},

(7)

where
P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{x})
denotes the projection of
\boldsymbol{x}
onto the constraint set
\mathcal{C}
. Squaring the distance term is a practical maneuver that leads to differentiability and the simple gradient

\nabla\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{x},\mathcal{C})^{2}=\boldsymbol{x}-P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{x})

when
P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{x})
is single-valued
(
Lange 2016
)
. Fortunately, the projection operator
P_{S}(\boldsymbol{x})
onto any closed set
S
is single valued except for a set of Lebesgue measure
0

(
Keys et al. 2019
)
. Hence, the technical possibility that
P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{x})
becomes multi-valued for
\mathcal{C}
non-convex is vanishingly rare from a theoretical perspective. Indeed, this event is negligible in practice as well: if a multi-valued point is encountered, the user is shielded from this exception because the code automatically selects a point in
P_{C}(x_{k})
and delivers a valid surrogate.
The distance majorization

\text{dist}\left(\boldsymbol{x},\mathcal{C}\right)^{2}=\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{x})\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\rVert_{2}^{2}\quad\text{ for all }\,\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\in P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})

follows directly from the definition of the projection operator

P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{x})
.
This majorization is useful in practice because it replaces the distance penalty by a spherically symmetric quadratic with the same gradient at
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
.

Recall in the present context, the relevant constraint set
\mathcal{C}
defined in (
3
) consists of all symmetric matrices with at most
k
nonzero entries in their upper triangle. The choice of
k
determines the level of sparsity. Computing the projection of a symmetric matrix onto
\mathcal{C}
is accomplished by setting all but the diagonal and the
k
largest entries (in absolute value) of each triangle to zero. Because we have not yet imposed the constraint on positive definiteness, this projection is computed simply by
hard thresholding entries in the upper triangle, and propagating the results to the lower triangle symmetrically.
The next section describes how distance majorization creates a sequence of unconstrained smooth problems, as well as how the positive definiteness constraint can be enforced via simple backtracking.

4
Algorithm for Sparse Covariance Estimation

4.1
A Proximal Distance Algorithm

The recently introduced proximal distance principle
(
Keys et al. 2019
)

replaces the constrained problem
\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{C}}f(\boldsymbol{x})
by unconstrained minimization of the penalized loss
f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\rho}{2}\text{dist}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathcal{C})^{2}
. In our setting,
the loss (
2
) plays the role of
f(\boldsymbol{x})
under the sparsity constraint (
3
). The unconstrained reformulation (
5
) can then be solved using distance majorization.
Proposition
1
implies that if
\rho
is sufficiently large, then the solution of the penalized problem accurately approximates the solution of the constrained problem. For any given value of
\rho
, applying the proximal distance principle requires majorizing the objective
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
by the function

g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{k})=f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\rho}{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\rVert^{2}
. Now, the minimizer of this surrogate function is given by a proximal operator. Recall that for any function
r(\boldsymbol{x})
, the proximal operator is defined

\text{prox}_{\lambda r}(\boldsymbol{y})\equiv\underset{\boldsymbol{x}}{\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits}\,\Big[r(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}\Big]

with

\lambda=\rho^{-1}
. The operator
\text{prox}_{\lambda r}(\boldsymbol{y})
represents a compromise between minimizing
r(\boldsymbol{x})
and hewing toward
\boldsymbol{y}
, with the parameter
\lambda
modulating the tradeoff;
Polson et al. 2015
provide an excellent overview of proximal methods in statistics.

Because it is not possible to find an analytic expression for the proximal operator of
f(\boldsymbol{x})
, we cannot easily minimize the surrogate function
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
generated by the covariance likelihood. Instead, we construct a more useful surrogate function
q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
using a local quadratic approximation tailored to
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
.
This local surrogate possesses three advantages over linear surrogates used in past approaches
(
Bien and Tibshirani 2011
;
Azose et al. 2018
)
. First, a quadratic surrogate provides a tighter approximation than the linear surrogates previously applied in this problem, often translating to dramatically more efficient steps toward the optimum.
Second, the proximal operator of our surrogate

q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
admits a closed form solution; each subproblem can be minimized exactly, in contrast to gradient steps whose progress and stability depends heavily on the choice of step sizes. Finally, by exploiting a surprising connection to control theory, evaluation of these closed solutions becomes practical, effecting a reduction in computational complexity from
\mathcal{O}(p^{6})
using a naïve evaluation to a more tractable
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
.

4.2
Constructing and Minimizing the Surrogate

Recall that the relevant loss is
f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=\ln\det\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S})
. To define a sequence of quadratic approximations to

f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, we take matrix directional derivatives of
f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
in the directions
\boldsymbol{U}
and
\boldsymbol{V}
,

\displaystyle d_{\boldsymbol{V}}f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V})-\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S})

\displaystyle d_{\boldsymbol{U}}d_{\boldsymbol{V}}f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=-\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V})+\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S})+\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}^{T}d^{2}f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\boldsymbol{V}

\displaystyle=-\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V})+2\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S}),

where the quadratic form in the last line is obtained by setting
\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{V}
. The second differential simplifies considerably if we replace
\boldsymbol{S}
by its expected value
\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{S})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. This maneuver is familiar from the derivation of Fisher’s scoring algorithm. This substitution precipitates a cancellation of higher order terms; the overall result

\boldsymbol{V}^{T}d^{2}f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\boldsymbol{V}\approx\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V})

is a positive definite quadratic form. We may now define an approximate quadratic surrogate

q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
of the penalized objective
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
by taking a second-order Taylor expansion of the loss
f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
about the current estimate, namely

\displaystyle q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})+\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})]-\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})]+\frac{\rho}{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})\rVert_{F}^{2}.

Here the majorized distance penalty appears as the final term. In contrast to an
\ell_{1}
-penalized loss, this surrogate is differentiable with gradient expression

\frac{d}{d\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}+\rho[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})].

Equating the gradient to
\bf 0
and rearranging yields the stationarity equation

\rho P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}=\rho\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}.

(8)

If we abbreviate the left-hand side by

\boldsymbol{C}_{k}\equiv\rho P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}

and stack matrices into vectorized notation, then equation (
8
) can be rewritten

\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{C}_{k})=\rho\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})+(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1})\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}),

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product. Upon inversion, the solution amounts to

\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})=\left[\rho\boldsymbol{I}_{p^{2}}+(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1})\right]^{-1}\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{C}_{k}),

(9)

and we may recover the minimizer
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
by reshaping.
The analytic solution (
9
) involves the inverse of a

p^{2}\times p^{2}
matrix and hence scales as
\mathcal{O}(p^{6})
.
This computational load puts problems with even moderate dimension
p
beyond reach.
Upon multiplying both sides by the constant
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}
and closer inspection, equation (
8
) takes the general form

\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{C}
, which we recognize as a Sylvester equation in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. Like the closely related and better-known Lyapunov equations arising in dynamical systems, Sylvester equations are well-studied in control theory and eigenvalue problems
(
Higham 2002
)
. It is known that the equation has a unique solution if and only if the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{A}
and
-\boldsymbol{B}
are distinct; this condition holds in the present case because
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}
is positive definite. More pertinently, we can borrow a numerical method from the control theory literature. An algorithm due to
Bartels and Stewart 1972
provides a more efficient solution than direct evaluation of equation (
9
). The first step and crux of the procedure lies in transforming the problem into Schur form by computing decompositions
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{U}^{T}
and
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{V}^{T}
via the QR algorithm. Because
\boldsymbol{R}
and
\boldsymbol{S}
are upper triangular, the equivalent upper triangular system
\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{Y}+\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{V}
with
\boldsymbol{Y}=\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}
can be solved by simple back-substitution. Multiplication then recovers the original solution
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}^{T}
. The computational complexity declines from
\mathcal{O}(p^{6})
operations required to compute formula (
9
) to
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
. Current state-of-the-art implementations are variations on this theme and possess the same overall complexity; see
Simoncini 2016
for details.

Before proceeding, we briefly mention that sparse fitting of the sample correlation matrix can exploit the same algorithm with a simple modification that has been noted previously in the literature. Let
\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{D}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{D}^{-1/2}
denote the sample correlation matrix, where
\boldsymbol{D}=\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{S})
contains the observed variances. In estimation with
\boldsymbol{R}
replacing

\boldsymbol{S}
, we minimize the criterion

\ln\det\boldsymbol{\Theta}+\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{-1}\boldsymbol{R})+\frac{\rho}{2}\text{dist}(\boldsymbol{\Theta},\mathcal{CR})^{2}

over
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\succ{\bf 0}
, where
\mathcal{CR}
is the set of
k
-sparse
symmetric matrices with unit diagonal entries. Projection of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}
onto this set maps the diagonal entries of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}
to
1
and treats the off-diagonal entries as before.

4.3
Positive Definiteness and Gradient Interpretation

So far, the penalty term in the objective (
5
) only accounts for the sparsity set constraint
\mathcal{C}
. Because the matrix
\rho P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}
is not guaranteed to be positive definite, neither is the solution
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
that minimizes the surrogate given in equation (
8
). Moreover, the approximate surrogate
q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
does not strictly majorize
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})+\rho/2\cdot\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\mathcal{C})^{2}
for all possible
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, and so naïvely minimizing
q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
does not necessarily decrease
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
. Both of these issues can be handled gracefully via backtracking. The next proposition ensures the success of step-halving, which amounts to defining

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}+\frac{1}{2^{s}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})

(10)

based on the smallest integer
s\geq 0
that renders
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k+1}\succ{\bf 0}
and decreases the objective
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
.
The result becomes clear after considering the representation

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}+\boldsymbol{v}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}-\boldsymbol{H}_{k}^{-1}\nabla q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}),

(11)

where
\boldsymbol{H}_{k}
is the scoring approximation to the Hessian; a complete proof of the following proposition appears in the Appendix.

Proposition 2
.

If
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}
is not a stationary point of
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, then there exists an integer
s
such that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k+1}
given in equation (
10
) satisfies
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k+1}\succ{\bf 0}
and
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k+1})<h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
.

1
, which reveals that the careful technical work behind the preceding analysis is largely hidden from the user’s perspective. The resulting algorithm is relatively transparent and easy to implement.

Before proceeding further, let us pause to compare our surrogate to the surrogate proposed in the sparse covariance method of
Bien and Tibshirani 2011
. Based on the concave-convex procedure of
Yuille and Rangarajan 2003
, they employ the tangent plane majorizer

\ln\det\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma})-p+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S})+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{A}\circ\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\rVert_{1}

to the
\ell_{1}
-penalized objective (
4
).
The resulting majorization-minimization iteration

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k+1}=\underset{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succ{\bf 0}}{\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits}\left[\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma})+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S})+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{A}\circ\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\rVert_{1}\right]

is carried out via generalized gradient descent
(
Beck and Teboulle 2009
)
. The choice of a good step-size is crucial for a reasonable rate of convergence in practice. Because the linear approximation only loosely models their objective function

\ln\det\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S})+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{A}\circ\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\rVert_{1},

a given step-size may be well-suited at some points but may drastically overshoot the minimum or exit the positive definite cone at others. Whenever the latter occurs, an additional subproblem must be solved by an alternating directions method
(
Boyd et al. 2011
)
. This inner optimization loop slows
convergence and is decidedly more difficult to implement than backtracking. Stability can be enhanced by decreasing the initial step-size at the expense of more outer iterations. Our quadratic expansion of the log-likelihood produces an approximate surrogate
q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
that hugs our objective
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
more closely. Substitution of a distance penalty for a lasso penalty also enjoys smoothness.
In practice, the minimizer

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
of
q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
rarely fails to diminish
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
or stay within the positive definite cone, so typically we update
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k+1}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
without backtracking
. These differences translate to substantial performance advantages, as illustrated in Section
5
.

Finally, an anonymous reviewer has raised the question of convergence to the global optimum, a valid concern that besets all non-convex optimization problems. To our disappointment, we initially found that our algorithm was somewhat sensitive to initial guesses
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
close to the sample covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
. After some experimentation we discovered that the algorithm delivers remarkably stable performance when initiated instead as a diagonal matrix, with sample variances appearing along the diagonal. It may be that perturbations of the full
\boldsymbol{S}
are more likely to lie close to the constraint boundary. This can impede progress and precipitate smaller gradient steps when more backtracking is necessary. Starting from a diagonal or even identity matrix, the algorithm tends to stay well within the interior of the positive definite cone, and has more room to learn from the data and in turn consistently reach the optimum.

4.4
Convergence

Recall that due to non-convexity of the symmetric sparsity set
\mathcal{C}
, it is possible that there exist exceptional points at which the projection operator
P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
is multi-valued. The penalty
\text{dist}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\mathcal{C})^{2}
and in turn the objective
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
are differentiable where
P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
is single-valued, but merely semi-differentiable elsewhere. In contrast, the surrogate
q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
is differentiable regardless of the projected point selected from
P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
.

Although standard convergence results for gradient methods and majorization-minimization algorithms do not immediately apply in proving convergence
(
Lange 2016
)
, theoretical guarantees can be established by appealing to the general theory of
Zangwill 1969
, which encompasses continuous objectives and multi-valued algorithm maps.
One can represent our method as an algorithm map
A(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
taking the current iterate
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}
to the next iterate
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k+1}
. Our novel analysis below treats
A
as a set-valued map to fully account for the technical possibility that the projection operator is multi-valued, even though the set of points where this can occur has measure zero. The following global convergence result is proved in the Appendix.

Theorem 1
.

Consider the sequence
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}+\eta_{k}\boldsymbol{v}_{k}\in A(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
generated by the search direction
\boldsymbol{v}_{k}
of equation (
11
) and the step length
\eta_{k}=\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits_{\eta\in[0,1]}h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}+\eta\boldsymbol{v}_{k})
. If the initial point
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}
is positive definite and the sample covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
is nonsingular, then the sequence
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}
is bounded and falls within the interior of the positive definite cone. Furthermore, all of its limit points are stationary points of
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
.

Though the result suggests promising performance despite non-convexity, we discuss several limitations. First, to simplify mathematical analysis, it supposes an exact line search. This assumption can be relaxed at the expense of a more complicated proof.
Second, although the algorithm invariably converges, the proposition cannot guarantee convergence to a global minimizer.
It simply says that a convergent subsequence exists whose limit is a stationary point
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
satisfying

\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}+\rho(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\boldsymbol{\Theta})={\bf 0}

(12)

for some
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
. As we expect, this stationarity condition is necessary for
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
to furnish a global minimum. Indeed, if it fails, we take

\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
with
\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\neq{\bf 0}
. Then the negative gradient
-\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
is a descent direction for
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, which majorizes
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
. Hence,
-\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
would also be a descent direction for
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, contradicting even local optimality of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. Leveraging majorizing surrogates in this fashion establishes directional stationarity, the strongest kind of stationarity in semidifferentiable optimization
(
Pang et al. 2017
;
Cui et al. 2018
)
, while avoiding the complications that often come with checking the condition explicitly.

5.2
International Migration Data

Projecting international migration at the country-specific scale is important in shaping policy decisions that arise in social welfare and economic planning. Probabilistic projections are desirable in quantifying uncertainty in these “barely predictable” global processes

5.3
Flow Cytometry data

Our final case study takes a closer look at the marginal and conditional dependency structures in a classic cell signaling study. We revisit the experiment studied by
Sachs et al. 2005
involving flow cytometry measurements on
p=11

(

)
and in a study of
\ell_{1}
-penalized covariance estimation
(
Bien and Tibshirani 2011
)
. We produce two estimates of the conditional dependency or Markov graph using the graphical lasso with
k=9

Bien and Tibshirani 2011

This is no surprise since the underlying models offer distinct interpretations. A missing edge in the covariance graph tells us that the concentration of one protein gives no information about the concentration of the other, whereas a missing edge in the Markov graph indicates that the concentration of one protein gives no information about the concentration of the other
conditional
on all other concentrations. While this difference is crucial, our results suggest that the covariance graph may be more similar to the Markov graph than past studies based on
\ell_{1}
penalties suggest.

Figure

displays covariance graphs obtained by running the generalized gradient descent algorithm of
Bien and Tibshirani 2011
and our proximal distance algorithm at sparsity levels matched to the Markov graphs. It is visually clear that our estimate of
\widehat{\Sigma}
shares more edges with the Markov graph. Although the true covariance graph and Markov graph do not necessarily coincide, these results suggest that the difference between the two in these data may be overstated due to
\ell_{1}

\ell_{1}

, while
\ell_{1}

\lambda
to

. This compact example emphasizes both the computational advantages of the proximal distance algorithm and its ability to deliver dependable solutions uncontaminated by excess shrinkage.

6
Discussion

We propose a novel proximal distance method for estimating a sparse covariance matrix that does not appeal to global shrinkage or convex relaxation, and does not assume any known structure in the sparsity pattern. Our technique makes use of a natural and interpretable penalty based on the distance to the constraint set. We justify theoretically and showcase empirically the merits of the method. Building upon landmark work on this difficult non-convex problem by
Bien and Tibshirani 2011
, our contribution extends their core idea centered around majorization-minimization, but relies on a more favorable approximation that hugs the loss function more tightly at each iterate. We also employ a surprising solution method by drawing from ideas in the control theory literature. These changes substantially improve the stability, speed, and accuracy of sparse covariance matrix estimation.

In our proximal distance algorithm it is hard to avoid a computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
. For instance, formation of the left-hand side of equation (
8
) requires dense matrix inversion and multiplication. One could possibly solve equation (
8
) by an iterative algorithm rather than the
Bartels and Stewart 1972
algorithm.
For instance, the iteration scheme

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j+1}=\frac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{D}_{k}-\frac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}

converges
to
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k+1}
provided that
\lVert\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\rVert_{2}^{2}<\rho
.

We note that previous convergence results for proximal distance algorithms do not address non-convex sets. Though our theory handles the non-convex sparsity set
\mathcal{C}
, we fix the penalty constant
\rho
in our analysis. This simplification is justified if we gradually increase
\rho
and then fix its value, though there remain gaps that warrant further theoretical development of proximal distance algorithms.
For example, how large should one take the resting value of
\rho
, and how quickly should one increment
\rho
from its initial value? That naïvely using the same update schedule for
\rho
works well empirically across the board should be considered an advantage. Nevertheless, a closer analysis of this behavior would be fruitful, and potentially crucial in other applications.

Despite these gaps, the desirable theoretical properties and empirical prowess
of the proposed proximal distance algorithm suggest that the ideas
are applicable to a broad range of problems. The virtues of the principle include versatility and elegance, avoidance of shrinkage, and ease of coding. Projection onto a closed set undergirds the principle. Fortunately, many projection operators are available in the literature, even for non-convex sets
(
Bauschke and Combettes 2011
;
Beck 2017
)
.
These successes encourage future work extending penalized likelihood methods for covariance estimation in the patternless sparsity setting. For instance in related problems, the local linear approximation algorithm succeeds in applying majorization-minimization for sparse estimation under alternate non-convex penalties such as SCAD
(
Zou and Li 2008
)
. Exploring the extent to which our contributions can help tailor such approaches to sparse covariance estimation is noontrivial, and provides a fruitful avenue for future work.
We invite readers to help us advance proximal distance theory and devise their own applications of this valuable extension of the majorization-minimization principle.

s
decreases
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
.
Recall the form (
11
) which expresses
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}-\boldsymbol{H}_{k}^{-1}\nabla q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
,
where
\boldsymbol{H}_{k}
is the scoring approximation obtained by taking the expected value of the second differential
d^{2}g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
. Here we explicitly avoid writing
\boldsymbol{H}_{k}
as an unwieldy tensor, instead noting that it generates the positive definite quadratic form
\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V})
. In light of the identity
\nabla q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})=\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
, the vector
\boldsymbol{v}_{k}
is a descent direction for
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
at
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}
. Since the cone of positive definite matrices is open, step-halving is also guaranteed to keep
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k+1}
positive definite.

Proof of Proposition
1

To establish convergence, we invoke Zangwill’s Global Convergence Theorem for descent algorithms
(
Luenberger and Ye 1984
;
Zangwill 1969
)
. Recall that our algorithm map
A(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
may be set-valued because the projection
P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
onto the sparsity constraint set can be multi-valued. Denote the set of stationary points (
12
)
of
A(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
by
\Gamma
. The theorem statement is reproduced in our notation below for convenience:

Theorem 2
.

(Global Convergence Theorem) Consider the algorithm
A:X\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(X)
defined by a point-to-set map and an initial point
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}
. Let
\Gamma\subset X
be a solution set and

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k+1}\in A(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
be a sequence generated by
A(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
. Finally, assume that

i)

all iterates
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}
are contained in a compact set
S\subset X
,

ii)

there is a continuous function
h(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
such that

a)

if
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\notin\Gamma
, then
h(\boldsymbol{\Theta})<h(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
for all
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in A(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
,

b)

if
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\Gamma
, then
h(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\leq h(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
for all
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in A(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
,

iii)

the mapping
A
is closed at points outside of
\Gamma
.

Then the sequence
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}
possesses convergent subsequences, and the corresponding limits belong to the solution set.

We begin by proving the coercivity of
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, which will imply that the sequence
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}
is contained in a compact set. Note even if
\boldsymbol{S}
is singular, running our method instead on
\tilde{\boldsymbol{S}}=\boldsymbol{S}+\delta\boldsymbol{I}
for arbitrarily small
\delta
suffices for the theory to hold. Doing so is reasonable as it is a strictly weaker assumption than relaxing the entire constraint
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succ{\bf 0}
to the set
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\succeq\delta\boldsymbol{I}

(
Bien and Tibshirani 2011
)
.

Lemma 1
.

The objective function
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
of our model is coercive whenever the sample covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
is nonsingular.

Proof.

Since the penalty is nonnegative, it suffices to prove that
f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=\ln\det\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}S)
is coercive. Let the singular values of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
be denoted
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\ldots>0
, and let the singular values of
\boldsymbol{S}
be denoted
s_{1}\geq s_{2}\geq\ldots>0
. It is clear that
\lVert\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\rVert\rightarrow\infty
if and only if at least one
\sigma_{i}\rightarrow\infty
and that
\lVert\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\rVert\rightarrow\infty
if and only if at least one
\sigma_{i}\rightarrow 0
. The matrix analogue of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality due to Von Neumann and Fan tells us that
\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{S})\geq\sum_{i}s_{i}/\sigma_{i}
. We also have
\ln\det\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sum_{i}\ln\sigma_{i}
. Now consider the sum
r(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\sum_{i}(\ln\sigma_{i}+s_{i}/\sigma_{i})
, which bounds
f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
below. Since each summand satisfies

\min_{\sigma_{i}}\Big(\ln\sigma_{i}+\frac{s_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\Big)\geq\ln s_{i}+1,

r(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
obviously tends to
\infty
if and only if any
\sigma_{i}
tends to
0
or
\infty
. Equivalently,

f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
tends to
\infty
if and only if either
\lVert\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\rVert
or
\lVert\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\rVert
tends to
\infty
.
∎

This proof shows that if we set
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=\infty
where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
fails to be positive definite, then
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
is continuous. We will adopt this convention in defining the update

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}+\eta_{k}\boldsymbol{v}_{k}\in A(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
via the
choice

\eta_{k}=\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits_{\eta\in[0,1]}g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}+\eta\boldsymbol{v}_{k}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}).

Before proving the next lemma, recall that the surrogate
q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
is minimized by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}~=~\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}~+~\boldsymbol{v}_{k}
, where
\boldsymbol{v}_{k}=-\boldsymbol{H}_{k}^{-1}\nabla q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{H}_{k}
is the approximate second differential generating the quadratic form

\boldsymbol{V}\mapsto\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V})
.
Elements of the solution set
\Gamma
of Zangwill’s theorem are characterized by the stationarity condition (
12
) for some
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
.

Lemma 2
.

Some point
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in A(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
decreases our objective
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
and strictly so
when
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\notin\Gamma
. Furthermore, the algorithm map
A(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
remains within a compact set and is closed outside
\Gamma
.

Proof.

By definition the algorithm map decreases
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
. If
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
falls outside
\Gamma
, then any associated search direction
\boldsymbol{v}
be expressed as
\boldsymbol{v}=-\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}
, where
\boldsymbol{H}
is positive definite and
\boldsymbol{u}=\nabla q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
is nontrivial for any choice of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
. Because

\displaystyle d_{\boldsymbol{v}}g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=d_{\boldsymbol{v}}q_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=-\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}<0,

it follows that
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
can be strictly decreased by moving in the direction
\boldsymbol{v}
. Hence, the objective
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
can be strictly decreased. To prove compactness, note that
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
is both continuous and coercive. Hence, its sub-level sets
\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}:h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\leq c\}
are compact. Given that the algorithm decreases
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, all iterates remain within the compact set
\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}:h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\leq h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0})\}
.

To prove closedness, consider a sequence
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}
with limit
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
and a corresponding
sequence
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}\in A(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
with limit

\boldsymbol{\Theta}\not\in\Gamma
. If
f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
is the loss function, then

\boldsymbol{u}_{k}=\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})+\rho(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k})
, where

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}\in P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
.
The lack of continuity of the projection operator hinders taking limits. However, since there are only a finite number of sparsity index sets, one of these sets must be chosen infinitely often along the sequence

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}
. Replace the sequences
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}
by the subsequence where this occurs. One can now invoke the continuity of the projection operator and conclude that
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}
exists. It follows that

\boldsymbol{v}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\boldsymbol{v}_{k}=-\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})+\rho(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\boldsymbol{\Theta})]

also exists with
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in P_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
. Furthermore,

\boldsymbol{v}\neq{\bf 0}
since
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\not\in\Gamma
. The step-length
sequence
\eta_{k}
also has a limit
\eta
defined by

\eta=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\boldsymbol{H}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\rVert_{2}}=\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{\Theta}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{2}}.

It remains to prove that
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\eta\boldsymbol{v}
is optimal.
Fortunately, this follows by taking limits in the inequality

g(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}+\eta_{k}\boldsymbol{v}_{k})\leq g(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}+\mu\boldsymbol{v}_{k})
valid for all
\mu\in[0,1]
.
∎

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
1
by a direct application of Zangwill’s theorem.

Proof.

The sub-level set
S_{h_{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0})=\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}:h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\leq h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0})\}
is compact, and by Lemmas
1
and
2
, all iterates
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k+1}\in A(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
lie in
S_{h_{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0})
. These lemmas further show that
a)
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}\succ{\bf 0}
for every
k
, b)
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
is continuous,
c)
h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\leq h_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
for all
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in A(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, and d)
equality is strict here when
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\notin\Gamma
. Furthermore,
the algorithm map
A(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
is closed outside
\Gamma
, the
set of stationary points.
Therefore, Theorem
2
applies, and every convergent subsequence of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}
is a stationary point.
∎

Additional Simulation Details

The experimental design in the first set of simulations are a direct reproduction of those in
(
Bien and Tibshirani 2011
)
. The analogous results presented in term of receiver operating characteristic curves appear below in Figure
5
. Any simulated datasets that fail to produce a positive definite ground truth covariance matrix are re-simulated. Next, all methods are seeded and run on the same synthetic datasets with matched relative tolerance. In all results, the penalty parameter
\lambda
for competing methods and the sparsity level
k
for our proposed method are selected via
5
-fold cross validation with respect to Frobenius loss over a vector of
40
possible values, calibrated so that best values do not occur on either boundary of the vector. This follows the recommendation in the implementations of those methods in the R packages
CVTuningCov
and
PDSCE
. We remark that cross-validation with respect to entropy loss was more favorable to our proposed method, though reported results in Tables 1—3 are cross-validated under Frobenius loss to offer a conservative comparison against peer methods.
The initial value of the parameter
\rho
is set to
0.1
in all cases considered and is not considered a tuning parameter.
