Abstract
. We define the discrete degree of symmetry disc − sym ⁡ ( X ) \operatorname{disc-sym}(X) of a closed n n -manifold X X as the biggest m ≥ 0 m\geq 0 such that X X supports an effective action of ( ℤ / r ) m ({\mathbb{Z}}/r)^{m} for arbitrarily big values of r r . We prove that if X X is connected then disc − sym ⁡ ( X ) ≤ 3 ​ n / 2 \operatorname{disc-sym}(X)\leq 3n/2 . We propose the question of whether for every closed connected n n -manifold X X the inequality disc − sym ⁡ ( X ) ≤ n \operatorname{disc-sym}(X)\leq n holds true, and whether the only closed connected n n -manifold X X for which disc − sym ⁡ ( X ) = n \operatorname{disc-sym}(X)=n is the torus T n T^{n} . We prove partial results providing evidence for an affirmative answer to this question.

1.1.
A bound on the discrete degree of symmetry

Our first result is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any closed connected
n
-manifold
X
we have
\operatorname{disc-sym}(X)\leq 3n/2
.

Quantitatively, the previous theorem stays far from the bound suggested by
Question
1.1
, but it shows a qualitative difference
between the discrete degree of symmetry and the rank of individual groups
acting effectively on a given manifold.
The theorem of Mann and Su
[
47

mentioned earlier implies that if
X
is
a closed connected manifold and
({\mathbb{Z}}/r)^{m}
acts effectively on

then

is bounded by a function involving the Betti numbers of

. Although
the bound given in
[
47
]
could possibly be improved, there is no hope to
replace it by a constand depending only on the dimension of

because any finite group acts freely (hence effectively)
on some closed connected surface.

1.2.
Discrete degree of symmetry of rationally hypertoral manifolds

A closed, connected and oriented
n

Equivalently,

Theorem 1.3

Let

We recall some standard terminology for the reader’s convenience.
A covering space

1.3.
Holomorphic discrete degree of symmetry of compact Kaehler manifolds

The following result answers affirmatively the analogue of Question
1.1

for holomorphic actions on Kaehler manifolds.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
X
be a compact connected Kaehler manifold of real dimension
n
. Suppose that, for some
natural number
m
,
X
supports an effective holomorphic action of
({\mathbb{Z}}/r)^{m}

for arbitrarily large values of
r
. Then
X
supports an effective holomorphic action of

T^{m}
. Furthermore,
m\leq n
, and if
m=n
then
X
is biholomorphic to a complex torus.

We will prove Theorem
1.9
using a result of Fujiki
[
28
]
on automorphism groups
of compact Kaehler manifolds and the following result on Lie groups.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
G
be a (finite dimensional) Lie group with finitely many connected components.
For every natural number
n
the following properties are equivalent:

(1)

G
has a Lie subgroup isomorphic to
T^{n}
,

(2)

G
has a subgroup isomorphic to
({\mathbb{Z}}/r)^{n}
for arbitrary large integers
r
.

Theorem

seems to be new even for smooth projective varieties
over the complex numbers.

Namely, if

is an

(or more generally a field of characteristic zero)
and the birational transformation group

contains subgroups isomorphic to

({\mathbb{Z}}/r)^{m}
for arbitrarily large values of
r
, does it follow that
m\leq 2n
?
If
m=2n
, does it follow that
X
is birational to an abelian variety?

1.4.
Discrete degree of symmetry and torus-degree of symmmetry

Corollary
1.6

proves that, at least in some particular situations, if

\operatorname{disc-sym}(X)=\dim X
then
\operatorname{tor-sym}(X)=\operatorname{disc-sym}(X)
.
But there are examples of closed manifolds for which
\operatorname{tor-sym}<\operatorname{disc-sym}
,
as proved by a construction due to Cappell, Weinberger and Yan.

Theorem 1.11
.

Let
X
be any of the manifolds
T(h)\times H
constructed in
[
17
, §2]
.
We have
\operatorname{disc-sym}(X)\geq 1
and
\operatorname{tor-sym}(X)=0
.

The equality
\operatorname{tor-sym}(X)=0
is the main result in
[
17
]
.
The inequality
\operatorname{disc-sym}(X)\geq 1
follows from the existence of regular self coverings

X\to X
of degree
d
for every odd natural number
d
.
The existence of such self coverings (for
d=3
, and hence also for
d=3^{k}
)
is stated without proof in
[
63
, Remark 1.3]
, and our contribution in this paper
is to provide a proof (actually, for any odd
d
)
in Section
13
. A key ingredient in the proof is the topological rigidity of tori.

There are obvious
analogues of the invariants
\operatorname{disc-sym}
and
\operatorname{tor-sym}
for locally linear actions and for smooth
actions on smooth manifolds, and in neither of these categories does one have the equality

\operatorname{disc-sym}=\operatorname{tor-sym}
in general. For locally linear actions there are counterexamples in dimension

4
, by the work of Edmonds
[
23
]
and Huck
[
38
]
.
In the smooth category one may take
X=T^{n}\sharp\Sigma
, where
\Sigma
is an exotic
n
-sphere.
Then
X
is homeomorphic to
T^{n}
, so

\operatorname{disc-sym}(X)=n
by Theorem
1.7
, but
\operatorname{tor-sym}(X)=0
by
the main result in
[
2
]
. In contrast, for holomorphic actions on compact Kaehler manifolds
one does have
\operatorname{disc-sym}=\operatorname{tor-sym}
in general, as proved by Theorem
1.9
below.

1.5.
Discrete degree of symmetry and covering spaces

In the proof of statement (2) in Theorem
1.3
we reduce
the general case to that in which
\pi_{1}
is solvable using the following
result.

Theorem 1.12
.

Let
X
be a closed connected manifold and let
X^{\prime}\to X
be a finite covering.
We have
\operatorname{disc-sym}(X^{\prime})\geq\operatorname{disc-sym}(X)
.

The inequality in Theorem
1.12

can be strict in some cases, as the following theorem proves.
Here and in the rest of the paper we identify
T^{n}
with
({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})^{n}
,
so we use additive notation for the group structure on
T^{n}
.

Theorem 1.13
.

Fix natural numbers
k,n
satisfying
1\leq k\leq n-1
. Consider the free involution

\sigma:T^{n}\to T^{n}
defined by

\sigma(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(x_{1}+1/2,\dots,x_{k}+1/2,-x_{k+1},\dots,-x_{n})
.
Let
X^{\prime}=T^{n}
and let
X=T^{n}/\sigma
. The natural projection
\rho:X^{\prime}\to X
is a covering map
and we have
\operatorname{disc-sym}X^{\prime}=n
and
\operatorname{disc-sym}X=k
.

For example, setting
n=2
and
k=1
the manifold
X
is the Klein bottle and
X^{\prime}
, the
2
-torus,
is the orientation
2
-cover of
X
.

The next lemma follows easily from Pontryagin duality

Lemma 2.2

Let

Combining Lemma

with the previous result
we immediately obtain the following.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let

be natural numbers and suppose given a surjection

Lemma 2.4
.

For any natural numbers

and

there exists a natural number

with the following property.
Suppose that
\Gamma
is a finite group and that

\Gamma

.
Suppose that
\Gamma/N\simeq\Gamma_{a,b}
for some natural number

. Then there is a subgroup

\Gamma^{\prime}\leq\Gamma
which is isomorphic to
\Gamma_{a^{\prime},b}
for some natural number

satisfying
C_{2}a^{\prime}\geq a
.

Proof.

By assumption there is a surjective morphism
\pi:\Gamma\to Q:=\Gamma_{a,b}
whose kernel is

Let

be the morphism given by the conjugation action of
\Gamma
on

Let

[\Gamma_{a,b}:\pi(\Gamma_{0})]\leq[\Gamma:\Gamma_{0}]\leq|\operatorname{Aut}N|\leq C_{1}!

which is isomorphic to
\Gamma_{a_{0},b}
for some natural number

The kernel of the restriction
\pi_{0}
of

to
\Gamma_{0}
coincides with the center

of

Let
\Gamma_{1}:=\pi_{0}^{-1}(Q_{0})

Since this is a central extension, one can define a bilinear morphism

\beta:Q_{0}\times Q_{0}\to Z

by setting, for any two elements

\beta(u,v)=[\widetilde{u},\widetilde{v}],

where
\widetilde{u},\widetilde{v}\in\Gamma_{1}
are arbitrary lifts of

Define a morphism of groups

\phi_{\beta}:Q_{0}\to\operatorname{Hom}(Q_{0},Z)

By construction,

\Gamma_{1}

Lemma 2.6

For the second result needed to prove Theorem

Let

Lemma 3.6
.

We have
|{\mathcal{S}}_{p,m,k}|=Q_{m,k}(p)
.
If
\Gamma\leq\Gamma_{p,m}
satisfies

|{\mathcal{S}}_{p,m,k}(\Gamma)|=Q_{m-s,k}(p)p^{ks}

Proof.

We consider
\Gamma_{p,m}
as an

Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{p,m,k}=\{(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\text{ linearly independent elements of }\Gamma_{p,m}\}

The map
\sigma:{\mathcal{F}}_{p,m,k}\to{\mathcal{S}}_{p,m,k}

The initial case

such that

is equal to the set

, which has

This proves the first formula in the lemma.

Now suppose that
\Gamma\leq\Gamma_{p,m}
satisfies
\Gamma\simeq\Gamma_{p,s}

Choose

such that
\Gamma_{p,m}=\Gamma\oplus\Gamma^{\prime}

For each

is isomorphic

The map

.

For each

4.
Equivariant maps to the torus

In all this section
X
denotes a closed, connected and oriented

n
-dimensional manifold.
We identify
T^{n}
with the quotient
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
,
and we use additive notation for the group structure on
T^{n}
.
Suppose that
G
is a group,

\eta:G\to T^{n}
is a group homomorphism,
and
G
acts on a space

\phi:X\to T^{n}
will be called
\eta

\phi(g\cdot x)=\eta(g)+\phi(x)
for every

and

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X
be a closed, connected and oriented
n
-dimensional topological manifold.
Let
\phi:X\to T^{n}
be a continuous map of nonzero degree. Let
G
be a finite group.
Suppose that
X
is endowed with an effective action
of
G
inducing the trivial action on
H^{1}(X;{\mathbb{Z}})
. Then there is a morphism of groups
\eta:G\to T^{n}
with these properties:

(1)

the map
\phi
is homotopic to an
\eta
-equivariant map
\psi:X\to T^{n}
,

(2)

|\operatorname{Ker}\eta|
divides

Before proving the theorem we prove three auxiliary lemmas.
The first lemma is a topological
analogue of the construction at the beginning of

We identify

with
{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}
and accordingly we use additive notation
for the group structure on

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\alpha:X\to S^{1}
be a continuous map. Let
\theta
be a generator of
H^{1}(S^{1};{\mathbb{Z}})
.
Suppose that a finite group
G
acts continuously on

X
preserving
\alpha^{*}\theta
. Let
r
be the cardinal of
G
and let
\mu_{r}\subset S^{1}
denote
the group of
r
-th roots of unity.
There exists a morphism of groups
\xi:G\to\mu_{r}

and a continuous map
\beta:X\to S^{1}
homotopic to
\alpha
such that
\beta(g\cdot x)=\xi(g)+\beta(x)

for every
x\in X
and
g\in G
.

Proof.

Define
\zeta:X\to S^{1}
by
\zeta(x)=\sum_{g\in G}\alpha(g\cdot x)
for every
x\in X
.
Then
\zeta
is continuous and constant on
G
-orbits.
Let
\rho_{g}:X\to X
be the homeomorphism induced by the action of
g\in G
. By assumption
\rho_{g}^{*}\alpha^{*}\theta=\alpha^{*}\theta
for every
g\in G
. We have
\zeta^{*}\theta=\sum_{g\in G}\rho_{g}^{*}\alpha^{*}\theta=r\alpha^{*}\theta
.

Let
\Gamma=\{(x,t)\in X\times S^{1}\mid\zeta(x)=rt\}
.
Let
\pi:\Gamma\to X
be the restriction of the projection map
X\times S^{1}\to X
. The action of
\mu_{r}
on
\Gamma
given
by
(x,t)\cdot\theta=(x,t\theta)
endows
\pi:\Gamma\to X
with a structure of principal
\mu_{r}
-bundle.
We claim that it is a trivial principal bundle. This is equivalent to the triviality of the monodromy of
\pi
,
which we denote by
\nu:\pi_{1}(X,x_{0})\to\mu_{r}
, where
x_{0}\in X
is an arbitrary base point.
If there existed some
\lambda\in\pi_{1}(X,x_{0})
such that
\nu(\lambda)\neq 0
then the pairing
of
\zeta^{*}\theta
with
[\lambda]\in H_{1}(X;{\mathbb{Z}})
would not be divisible by
r
, which contradicts
the fact that
\zeta^{*}\theta=r\alpha^{*}\theta
. Hence
\nu
is trivial and consequently the bundle
\pi:\Gamma\to X

is trivial, so we may choose a section
\sigma:X\to\Gamma
.

Define
\beta:X\to S^{1}
by the condition
that
\sigma(x)=(x,\beta(x))
. Then
\beta
is continuous and we have
r\beta(x)=\zeta(x)
for every
x\in X
. For any
g\in G
define

\chi_{g}:X\to S^{1}
by
\chi_{g}(x)=\beta(g\cdot x)-\beta(x)
. We have

r\chi_{g}(x)=r\beta(g\cdot x)-r\beta(x)=\zeta(g\cdot x)-\zeta(x)=0
because
\zeta
is
G
-invariant.
Hence
\chi_{g}
takes values in
\mu_{r}
, and consequently, being continuous, it is a constant map. We may thus define a map

by the condition that

\xi(g)=\chi_{g}(x)
for every
x\in X
. Let
g,g^{\prime}\in G
and let
x\in X
.
We have

\chi_{gg^{\prime}}(x)=\beta(gg^{\prime}\cdot x)-\beta(x)=\beta(gg^{\prime}\cdot x)-\beta(g^{\prime}\cdot x)+\beta(g^{\prime}\cdot x)-\beta(x)=\chi_{g}(g^{\prime}\cdot x)+\chi_{g^{\prime}}(x),

which proves that
\xi(gg^{\prime})=\xi(g)+\xi(g^{\prime})

\xi
is a morphism of groups. Now the formula

\beta(g\cdot x)=\xi(g)+\beta(x)
follows immediately from the definition
of
\xi
. To conclude the proof, note that

r\beta^{*}\theta=\zeta^{*}\theta=r\alpha^{*}\theta

has no torsion
we conclude that
\beta^{*}\theta=\alpha^{*}\theta

\beta
and
\alpha
are homotopic, because

is a model for

K({\mathbb{Z}},1)
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G
be a finite group acting effectively, continuously and preserving the orientation on

For any

Define

We prove that

Clearly

be the order of

.
Since

acts on

preserving the orientation, by

\to H_{k}((B_{\leq j})_{\psi};{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{k-1}((B_{\leq j-1})_{\psi}\cap(B_{j})_{\psi};{\mathbb{Z}})\to\dots{}

-module.

is a finitely generated

-module.

-module for every

-module, so the proof of the claim is complete.

To conclude the proof note that the inclusion

and the retraction

In dimensions up to three any topological manifold has a unique smooth structure, so

According to

h_{T^{\prime}}
extend to involutions
h_{W}:W\to W

and
h_{W^{\prime}}:W^{\prime}\to W^{\prime}
respectively, and there is a self homeomorphism
h_{C}
of
C:=T^{n}\times[0,1]

whose restriction to
C_{0}:=T^{n}\times\{0\}
resp.
C_{1}:=T^{n}\times\{1\}
coincides with
h_{T}
resp.
h_{T^{\prime}}
.
To justify the existence of
h_{C}
it suffices to prove the existence of a homeomorphism

\phi:C\to C
such that
\phi|_{C_{0}}=\operatorname{Id}_{T^{n}}
and
\phi|_{C_{1}}=\psi:=h_{T}^{-1}\circ h_{T^{\prime}}
, for then

h_{C}:=(h_{T}\times\operatorname{Id}_{[0,1]})\circ\phi
has the desired property. Now,
\psi
is homotopic to
\operatorname{Id}_{T^{n}}

so the mapping tori
T(\psi)
and
T(\operatorname{Id}_{T^{n}})
are homotopy equivalent. Since
T(\operatorname{Id}_{T^{n}})=T^{n+1}
,
the topological rigidity of tori implies that
T(\psi)
and
T(\operatorname{Id}_{T^{n}})
are homeomorphic.
The existence of
\phi
now follows from combining:
[
43
, Theorem 1]
, the observation that invertible
cobordism are
h
-cobordisms, the
s
-cobordism theorem, and the vanishing of the Whitehead group of

\pi_{1}(T^{n})
.

Unlike
h_{T}
and
h_{T^{\prime}}
,
h_{C}
is not an involution. However, we have the following:

Proposition 13.1
.

If we chose
h_{C}
suitably, then

h_{C}^{2}
and
\operatorname{Id}_{C}
are homotopic rel.
\partial C
.

Proof.

We identify
T^{n}
with
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, so the universal covering space
C^{\sharp}
of
C
can be
identified with
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times[0,1]
. Let
C^{\sharp}_{i}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times\{i\}
for
i=0,1
.
Let
f,g:C\to C
be continuous maps such that

f|_{\partial C}=g|_{\partial C}
. Choose lifts
f^{\sharp},g^{\sharp}:C^{\sharp}\to C^{\sharp}

of
f,g
respectively. Then

g^{\sharp}|_{C^{\sharp}_{i}}-f^{\sharp}|_{C^{\sharp}_{i}}
is equal to some constant
\lambda_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, because

g|_{C_{i}}=f|_{C_{i}}
. Let

\lambda(g,f):=\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0}.

The vector
\lambda(g,f)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
is independent of the chosen lifts of
f,g
.

Lemma 13.2
.

f
and
g
are homotopic rel.
\partial C
if and only if
\lambda(g,f)=0
.

Proof.

The ”only if” part of the lemma is an easy exercise. For the ”if” part, note that
there is a linear map
\rho:{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
such that
for every
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
\mu\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
and
s\in[0,1]
,
both
f^{\sharp}(p+\mu,s)-f^{\sharp}(p,s)
and

g^{\sharp}(p+\mu,s)-g^{\sharp}(p,s)
are equal to
(\rho(\mu),0)
.
(Actually
\rho
can be identified with the morphism
H_{1}(T^{n})\to H_{1}(T^{n})
induced
by
f
or
g
.) It follows that the map
C^{\sharp}\times[0,1]\to C^{\sharp}

sending
((p,s),t)
to
(1-t)f^{\sharp}(p,s)+tg^{\sharp}(p,s)
,
which is a homotopy between
f^{\sharp}
and
g^{\sharp}
,
descends to a homotopy rel
\partial C
between
f
and
g
.
∎

Now suppose that
\zeta:C\to C
is a homeomorphism
satisfying
\zeta|_{C_{0}}=h_{T}
and
\zeta|_{C_{1}}=h_{T^{\prime}}
.
Since both
h_{T}
and
h_{T^{\prime}}
have fixed points, there exist
x\in C_{0}
and
y\in C_{1}

such that
\zeta(x)=x
and
\zeta(y)=y
. Choose lifts
x^{\sharp},y^{\sharp}\in C^{\sharp}
of
x,y
respectively.
There is a unique lift
\zeta^{\sharp}:C^{\sharp}\to C^{\sharp}
satisfying
\zeta^{\sharp}(x^{\sharp})=x^{\sharp}
.
As before, there is a morphism of groups
\rho:{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
such that

\zeta^{\sharp}(p+\mu,s)=\zeta^{\sharp}(p,s)+(\rho(\mu),0)
for all
p,\mu,s
.
Let
o:C^{\sharp}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
denote the projection and let

\nu:=o(\zeta^{\sharp}(y^{\sharp})-y^{\sharp})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, so that
\zeta^{\sharp}(y^{\sharp})=y^{\sharp}+(\nu,0)
. Then:

\displaystyle\lambda(\zeta^{2},\operatorname{Id})

\displaystyle=o(\zeta^{\sharp}\zeta^{\sharp}(y^{\sharp})-y^{\sharp})=o(\zeta^{\sharp}\zeta^{\sharp}(y^{\sharp})-\zeta^{\sharp}(y^{\sharp})+\zeta^{\sharp}(y^{\sharp})-y^{\sharp})

\displaystyle=o(\zeta^{\sharp}(y^{\sharp}+(\nu,0))-\zeta^{\sharp}(y^{\sharp})+(\nu,0))=\rho(\nu)+\nu.

By the previous lemma, in order for
\zeta^{2}
to be homotopic to
\operatorname{Id}_{C}
rel.
\partial C

we need
\rho(\nu)+\nu
to vanish. This need not be the case, but if we define

\xi^{\sharp}:C^{\sharp}\to C^{\sharp}

as
\xi^{\sharp}(p,s)=\zeta^{\sharp}(p)-s\nu
then we have

\xi^{\sharp}(p+\mu,s)=\xi^{\sharp}(p,s)+(\rho(\mu),0)
for all
p,\mu,s
,
so
\xi^{\sharp}
descends to a homeomorphism
\xi:C\to C
satisfying
\xi|_{\partial C}=\zeta|_{\partial C}
.
Furthermore,
\xi^{\sharp}(x^{\sharp})=x^{\sharp}
and
\xi^{\sharp}(y^{\sharp})=y^{\sharp}
, so

\lambda(\xi^{2},\operatorname{Id})=0
. Consequently,
h_{C}:=\xi
has the desired property.
∎

Assume from now on that
h_{C}
has been chosen in such a way that
h_{C}^{2}
is homotopic to
\operatorname{Id}_{C}

rel.
\partial C
, which implies that
h_{C}^{d}
and
h_{C}
are homotopic rel.
\partial C
.
The involution
h:V\to V
is defined by the condition that its restriction to the subspaces

W,\,T^{n}\times[0,1],\,W^{\prime}
is given by
h_{W},\,h_{C},\,h_{W^{\prime}}
respectively.

Since
h_{W}
and
h_{W^{\prime}}
are involutions, the restrictions of the maps
h
and
h^{d}
to
W
and
W^{\prime}
are equal.
Hence, to prove that
T(h)
and
T(h^{d})
are homeomorphic
it suffices to prove the existence of a homeomorphism of mapping tori
\phi:T(h_{C})\to T(h_{C}^{d})
whose restriction
to
\partial T(h_{C})
is the natural homeomorphism
\partial T(h_{C})\to\partial T(h_{C}^{d})
resulting from
the equalities
h_{T}=h_{T}^{d}
and
h_{T^{\prime}}=h_{T^{\prime}}^{d}
.

By definition
T(h_{C})
is the quotient of
C\times[0,1]
by the relation that
identifies
(x,1)
with
(h_{C}(x),0)
for every
x\in C
.
For
i=0,1
, let
T_{i}(h_{C})\subset T(h_{C})
be the image of

C_{i}\times[0,1]
under the projection map
C\times[0,1]\to T(h_{C})
.
Then
T_{0}(h_{C})
resp.
T_{1}(h_{C})
can be identified with
T(h_{T})
resp.
T(h_{T^{\prime}})
.
Since
h_{T}
is a linear involution,
T(h_{T})
supports a non-positively curved Riemannian metric.
Now,
T(h_{T^{\prime}})
is homotopy equivalent to
T(h_{T})
, because
h_{T}
and
h_{T^{\prime}}
are homotopic.
Hence, by topological rigidity
[
24
, Theorem 14.1]
, there is a homeomorphism

\psi:T(h_{T^{\prime}})\to T(h_{T})
. Choosing
\psi
appropriately, we may and do assume that the compositions
of maps

T(h_{T})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\psi^{-1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}T(h_{T^{\prime}})=T_{1}(h_{C})\hookrightarrow T(h_{C})
and

T(h_{T})=T_{0}(h_{C})\hookrightarrow T(h_{C})
are homotopic.

We claim the existence of a homeomorphism

\xi:T(h_{C})\to T(h_{T}\times\operatorname{Id}_{[0,1]})=T(h_{T})\times[0,1]

whose restriction
to
T_{0}(h_{C})
resp.
T_{1}(h_{C})
coincides with
\operatorname{Id}_{T(h_{T})}
resp.
\psi
. Once we prove the claim
the existence of
\phi
will follow immediately, since by
[
24
, Theorem 14.1]
topological
rigidity applies to
T(h_{T})\times[0,1]
(again because
T(h_{T})
supports a metric
of non-positive curvature) and the existence of a homotopy
h_{C}^{d}\sim h_{C}
rel.
\partial C

gives a homotopy equivalence
T(h_{C})\to T(h_{C}^{d})
whose restriction to
\partial T(h_{C})
is a
homeomorphism.

To prove the existence of the homeomorphism
\xi:T(h_{C})\to T(h_{T})\times[0,1]

we rely once again on the topological rigidity of
T(h_{T})\times[0,1]
, so we only
need to prove the existence of a continuous map
\chi:T(h_{C})\to T(h_{T})\times[0,1]

whose restriction to
T_{0}(h_{C})
resp.
T_{1}(h_{C})
coincides with
\operatorname{Id}_{T(h_{T})}
resp.
\psi

(these properties imply that
\chi
is a homotopy equivalence).

If
Y\subseteq X
is an inclusion of
topological spaces and
f,g:X\to X
are maps preserving
Y
, satisfying
f|_{Y}=g|_{Y}
,
and
f,g
are homotopic rel.
Y
, then there is a continuous map

\epsilon:T(f)\to T(g)
whose restriction to
T(f|_{Y})
is the natural identification
between
T(f|_{Y})
and
T(g|_{Y})
. Indeed, suppose that

H:X\times I\to X
satisfies
H(x,0)=g(x)
,

H(x,1)=f(x)
and
H(y,t)=f(y)=g(y)
for every
x\in X
,
y\in Y
,
t\in[0,1]
. Then

\epsilon
is defined by the map
\widetilde{\epsilon}:X\times[0,1]\to X\times[0,1]
given by

\widetilde{\epsilon}(x,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(x,2t)&\qquad\text{if $t\in[0,1/2],$}\\
(H(x,2t-1),0)&\qquad\text{if $t\in[1/2,1]$.}\end{array}\right.

Using the previous
principle, and the facts that
h_{C}
and
h_{T}\times\operatorname{Id}_{[0,1]}
are homotopic rel.
C_{0}
and

h_{C}
and
h_{T^{\prime}}\times\operatorname{Id}_{[0,1]}
are homotopic rel.
C_{1}

(which can be proved by lifting the maps to
C^{\sharp}
as in the proof of Proposition

13.1
and interpolating linearly),
we deduce the existence of maps
\chi_{0}:T(h_{C})\to T(h_{T})\times[0,1]

and
\chi_{1}:T(h_{C})\to T(h_{T^{\prime}})\times[0,1]

such that
\chi_{i}
restricted to
T_{i}(h_{C})
is the identity for
i=0,1
.
Furthermore,
\chi_{0}
and
(\psi\times\operatorname{Id}_{[0,1]})\circ\chi_{1}
are homotopic.

The universal cover of
T(h_{C})

can be identified with
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}
, and that of

T(h_{T})\times[0,1]
with
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times[0,1]
.
Fix a lift
h_{T}^{\sharp}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of
h_{T}:T^{n}\to T^{n}
.
Crucially,
h_{T}^{\sharp}
is an affine isomorphism, because
h_{T}
is a linear involution.
The group
\Gamma=\pi_{1}(T(h_{T})\times[0,1])\simeq\pi_{1}(T(h_{T}))\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\rtimes{\mathbb{Z}}

acts on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times[0,1]
preserving the affine structure induced
by the inclusion
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times[0,1]\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n+2}
: the factor
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}

acts by addition on the first factor of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times[0,1]
, and the action
of the second factor is generated by the transformation
(z,t,s)\mapsto(h_{T}^{\sharp}(z),t-1,s)
.

Choose lifts of
\chi_{0}
and
(\psi\times\operatorname{Id}_{[0,1]})\circ\chi_{1}

to the universal coverings, and call them
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{1}
respectively,
so that

\theta_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times[0,1].

Since
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{1}
are homotopic there exists a morphism of groups

\rho:\pi_{1}(T(h_{C}))\to\pi_{1}(T(h_{T})\times[0,1])
such that both
\theta_{0}

and
\theta_{1}
are
\rho
-equivariant, meaning that

\theta_{i}(\gamma\cdot w)=\rho(\gamma)\cdot\theta_{i}(w)
for every

\gamma\in\pi_{1}(T(h_{C}))
and
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}
.
Define

\theta:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times[0,1]

as

\theta(p,s,t)=(1-s)\theta_{0}(p,s,t)+s\theta_{1}(p,s,t)
.
Then
\theta
satisfies the same
\rho
-equivariance property
as
\theta_{i}
, because the action of
\pi_{1}(T(h_{T})\times[0,1])
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times[0,1]

is affine. This implies that
\theta
descends to a map
\chi:T(h_{C})\to T(h_{T})\times[0,1]
,
and by construction the restriction of
\chi
to
T_{0}(h_{C})
resp.
T_{1}(h_{C})
coincides
with
\operatorname{Id}_{T(h_{T})}
resp.
\psi
. This finishes the proof of the theorem.
