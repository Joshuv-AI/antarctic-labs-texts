C 0 C^{0} -hybrid high-order methods for biharmonic problems
Zhaonan Dong, Alexandre Ern

Abstract
We devise and analyze C 0 C^{0} -conforming hybrid high-order (HHO) methods to approximate biharmonic problems with either clamped or simply supported boundary conditions. C 0 C^{0} -conforming HHO methods hinge on cell unknowns which are C 0 C^{0} -conforming polynomials of order ( k + 2 ) (k+2) approximating the solution in the mesh cells and on face unknowns which are polynomials of order k ≥ 0 k\geq 0 approximating the normal derivative of the solution on the mesh skeleton. Such methods deliver O ⁡ ( h k + 1 ) O(h^{k+1}) H 2 H^{2} -error estimates for smooth solutions. An important novelty in the error analysis is to lower the minimal regularity requirement on the exact solution. The technique to achieve this has a broader applicability than just C 0 C^{0} -conforming HHO methods, and to illustrate this point, we outline the error analysis for the well-known C 0 C^{0} -conforming interior penalty discontinuous Galerkin (IPDG) methods as well. The present technique does not require bubble functions or a C 1 C^{1} -smoother to evaluate the right-hand side in case of rough loads. Finally, numerical results including comparisons to various existing methods showcase the efficiency of the proposed C 0 C^{0} -conforming HHO methods.



1
Introduction

Biharmonic PDEs are used in the modelling of various physical phenomena, such as thin plate elasticity, micro-electromechanical systems, and phase separation, to mention a few examples. In the present work, we consider the following model problem with two types of boundary conditions (BC’s):

\Delta^{2}u=f\quad\text{in $\Omega$},\qquad\left\{\begin{aligned} u&=\partial_{n}u=0&\quad&\text{type }(\mbox{I})\\
u&=\partial_{nn}u=0&\quad&\text{type }(\mbox{II})\end{aligned}\right.\quad\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.

(1)

Here,
\Omega
is a open bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, with boundary
\partial\Omega
,
\partial_{n}
denotes the normal derivative on
\partial\Omega
and
\partial_{nn}
denotes the normal-normal component of the Hessian on
\partial\Omega
. In the context of plate modelling, type
(\mbox{I})
BC is referred to as
clamped
BC, and type
(\mbox{II})
as
simply supported
BC. Non-homogeneous BC’s can be considered, but we focus on homogeneous BC’s for simplicity. Instead, dealing with the boundary condition
\partial_{nnn}u=0
requires further developments. The regularity of the source term
f
is specified below in Assumption
1.1
. We also observe that the present developments hinge on the weak formulation of (
1
) involving the Hessian.

The goal of the present work is twofold. The first main goal is to devise and analyze a novel
C^{0}
-conforming approximation method for the above model problem. The proposed method belongs to the class of hybrid high-order (HHO) methods. These methods were introduced in
[
11
]
for linear diffusion and in
[
10
]
for locking-free linear elasticity. Since then, they have undergone a vigorous development, as reflected, e.g., in the two recent monographs
[
9
,
7
]
. Moreover, as discussed in
[
8
,
24
,
5
]
, HHO methods are closely related to hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) methods, weak Galerkin (WG) methods, nonconforming virtual element methods (ncVEM), and multiscale hybrid-mixed (MHM) methods. Interestingly, the present
C^{0}
-conforming HHO method (
C^{0}
-HHO in short) can be viewed as a simple approach to extend
C^{0}
-finite element methods for biharmonic problems by simply adding an additional unknown attached to the mesh faces representing the normal derivative of the solution.
C^{0}
-HHO methods for the biharmonic problem have not yet been explored in the literature; we refer the reader to
[
1
,
13
,
12
]
for fully discontinuous HHO methods. The starting point for devising the present
C^{0}
-HHO methods are the HHO methods from
[
13
]
.

C^{0}
-conforming approximation methods are popular to discretize biharmonic problems since such methods avoid the intricate construction of
C^{1}
-conforming approximation spaces while also avoiding severe conditioning issues that can arise with fully nonconforming approximation methods. Examples of
C^{0}
-conforming approximation methods from the literature include the classical Morley
[
25
,
30
]
and Hsieh–Clough–Tocher (HCT) finite element methods, the
C^{0}
-interior penalty discontinuous Galerkin (IPDG) method
[
15
,
2
]
, the
C^{0}
-weak Galerkin (WG) method
[
26
,
6
]
, and the
C^{0}
-virtual element method (VEM)
[
31
]
. The Morley FEM and the HCT FEM for
d=3
are lowest-order methods, whereas the HCT FEM for
d=2

[
14
]
and the
C^{0}
-{IPDG,WG,HHO,VEM} for
d\geq 2
can reach arbitrary approximation order.
C^{0}
-IPDG attaches discrete unknowns to the mesh cells only, whereas
C^{0}
-{WG,HHO} attach discrete unknowns to the mesh cells and faces. A numerical comparison between
C^{0}
-IPDG and
C^{0}
-HHO is included herein, indicating the computational efficiency of the
C^{0}
-HHO approach. Concerning
C^{0}
-WG, we recall that the only relevant difference between HHO and WG lies in the choice of the discrete unknowns and the design of the stabilization operator. In general, WG employs plain least-squares stabilization, leading to suboptimal convergence rates, whereas HHO employs a more elaborate form of stabilization leading to optimal convergence rates. In other words, to achieve the same convergence rate,
C^{0}
-WG with plain least-squares stabilization requires more discrete unknowns than
C^{0}
-HHO methods. Furthermore, among the above
C^{0}
-conforming methods,
C^{0}
-VEM is currently the only one supporting general (polytopal) meshes. We notice that an interesting perspective to the present work in order to devise a
C^{0}
-HHO method on general meshes is to use
C^{0}
-VEM to build the cell basis functions, while keeping the current face basis functions to handle the normal derivative; this perspective is left to future work.
Finally,
C^{0}
-conforming HDG methods have been so far seldom considered in the literature, with the exception of a brief discussion in
[
20
]
. Therefore, leveraging on
[
8
]
, the present work can be viewed as a contribution to the development of
C^{0}
-HDG methods for the biharmonic problem.

The second main goal of the present work is to improve on the regularity requirement on the exact solution to lead the error analysis. The technique to achieve this is a non-trivial extension of ideas from
[
19
]
originally developed in the context of second-order elliptic problems (see also
[
18
, Chap. 40 & 41]
). The result derived herein can be applied to the
C^{0}
-HHO method when approximating the biharmonic problem, but also, more broadly, to the other
C^{0}
-conforming approximation methods discussed above. Recall that the difficulty comes from the lack of
C^{1}
-conformity which causes some difficulties in the error analysis when it comes to bounding the consistency error. A first possibility is to use bubble functions together with a
C^{1}
-smoother when evaluating the right-hand side of the discrete problem, as shown in
[
29
,
28
]
for the Morley element and the
C^{0}
-IPDG method (see also
[
4
]
for further results concerning lowest-order methods). However, these techniques so far meet with difficulties when it comes to devising a
C^{1}
-smoother of arbitrary order on tetrahedral meshes. The alternative road, which is the one followed by most of the above works and also herein, is to require some (mild) additional regularity assumption on the source term beyond
H^{-2}(\Omega)
and on the exact solution beyond
H^{2}(\Omega)
. The present work hinges on the following original assumption.

Assumption 1.1

(Regularity)

We assume that there are real numbers
p>2
and
q\in(\frac{2d}{d+2},2]
such that

u\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
,
\Delta u\in W^{1,q}(\Omega)
, and
f\in W^{-1,q}(\Omega).

Assumption
1.1
is, to our knowledge, novel in the analysis of biharmonic problems. If one prefers to remain in the Hilbertian setting, a simpler, but less general, assumption is
u\in H^{2+s}(\Omega)
, with
s>0
,
\Delta u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
,
and
f\in H^{-1}(\Omega)
. Notice that the assumption
u\in H^{2+s}(\Omega)
,
s>0
, is still tighter than the one usually made in the literature to analyze
C^{0}
-conforming methods, which is
u\in H^{2+s}(\Omega)
,
s>\frac{1}{2}
; the assumption
s>0
is considered in
[
4
]
.
Finally, we observe that in Assumption
1.1
, the Laplacian of the solution is smoother than its Hessian. Since the Laplace operator contains a relatively large kernel, this assumption is reasonable.

The rest of this work is organized as follows. We present the key identities to bound the consistency error in Section
2
. The results presented in this section have a wider outreach beyond
C^{0}
-HHO methods since they can be applied to other
C^{0}
-conforming methods (but not to fully nonconforming methods). Then, we devise
C^{0}
-HHO methods for both types of BC’s and establish stability and well-posedness of the discrete problems in Section
3
.
In Section
4
, we perform the error analysis under Assumption
1.1
. For completeness, we also outline how the
C^{0}
-IPDG method can be analyzed under this assumption. Finally, we discuss our numerical results in Section
5
.

2
Key identities to bound the consistency error

In this section, we introduce some basic notation, present the weak formulation for both
types of BC’s, and derive the key identities to bound the consistency error. The main
result of this section, Lemma
2.3
, can be applied to many

C^{0}
-conforming methods.

2.1
Basic notation

We use standard notation for the Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces. In particular, for the fractional-order Sobolev spaces, we consider the Sobolev–Slobodeckij seminorm based on the double integral. For an open, bounded, Lipschitz set
S
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{1,2,3\}
, we denote by
(v,w)_{S}
the
L^{2}(S)
-inner product with appropriate Lebesgue measure, and we employ the same notation for vector- or matrix-valued fields (such fields are denoted using boldface notation). We denote by
\nabla w
the (weak) gradient of
w
and by
\nabla^{2}w
its (weak) Hessian. Let
{\bm{n}}_{S}
be the unit outward normal vector on the boundary
\partial S
of
S
. Assuming that the functions
v
and
w
are smooth enough, we have the following integration by parts formula:

(\Delta^{2}v,w)_{S}=(\nabla^{2}v,\nabla^{2}w)_{S}+(\nabla\Delta v,{\bm{n}}w)_{\partial S}-(\nabla^{2}v{\bm{n}},\nabla w)_{\partial S}.

(2)

Whenever the context is unambiguous, we denote by
\partial_{n}
the (scalar-valued) normal derivative on
\partial S
and by
\partial_{t}
the (
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
-valued) tangential derivative.
We also denote by
\partial_{nn}v
the (scalar-valued) normal-normal second-order derivative, and by
\partial_{nt}v
the (
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
-valued) normal-tangential second-order derivative. The integration by parts formula (
2
) can then be rewritten as

(\Delta^{2}v,w)_{S}=(\nabla^{2}v,\nabla^{2}w)_{S}+(\partial_{n}\Delta v,w)_{\partial S}-(\partial_{nn}v,\partial_{n}w)_{\partial S}-(\partial_{nt}v,\partial_{t}w)_{\partial S}.

(3)

In what follows, the set
S
is always a polytope so that its boundary can be decomposed into a finite union of planar faces with disjoint interiors. Expressions involving the tangential derivative on
\partial S
are then implicitly understood to be evaluated as a summation over the faces composing
\partial S
.

Let
\{\mathcal{T}\}_{h>0}
be a shape-regular family of simplicial meshes such that each mesh covers the domain
\Omega
exactly. A generic mesh cell is denoted by
K\in\mathcal{T}
, its diameter by
h_{K}
, and its unit outward normal by
{\bm{n}}_{K}
.
We partition the boundary
\partial K
of any mesh cell
K\in\mathcal{T}
by means of the two subsets
\partial K^{\mathrm{i}}:=\overline{\partial K\cap\Omega}
and
\partial K^{\mathrm{b}}:=\partial K\cap\partial\Omega
. The mesh faces are collected in the set
\mathcal{F}
, which is split as
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{i}}\cup\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{b}}
, where
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{i}}
is the collection of the interior faces (shared by two distinct mesh cells) and
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{b}}
the collection of the boundary faces. We orient every mesh interface
F\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{i}}
by means of the fixed unit normal vector
{\bm{n}}_{F}
whose direction is arbitrary but fixed once and for all, whereas we orient every mesh boundary face
F\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{b}}
by means of the vector
{\bm{n}}_{F}:={\bm{n}}_{\Omega}
. For any mesh cell
K\in\mathcal{T}
, the mesh faces composing its boundary
\partial K
are collected in the set
\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}
, which is partitioned as
\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}=\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}^{\mathrm{i}}\cup\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}^{\mathrm{b}}
with obvious notation.

For any real number
\theta\geq 0
, we consider the broken Sobolev space

H^{\theta}(\mathcal{T}):=\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega)\,|\,v_{K}:={v}_{|K}\in H^{\theta}(K),\forall K\in\mathcal{T}\}.

(4)

The jump and average of any function
v\in H^{\theta}(\mathcal{T})
,
\theta>\frac{1}{2}
, across any mesh interface
F=\partial K_{1}\cap\partial K_{2}\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{i}}
are defined by setting
[\![v]\!]_{F}(x):=v_{|K_{1}}(x)-v_{|K_{2}}(x)
and
\{\kern-3.1298pt\{v\}\kern-3.1298pt\}_{F}(x):=\frac{1}{2}(v_{|K_{1}}(x)+v_{|K_{2}}(x))
for a.e.
x\in F
, respectively, where
K_{1}
is such that its outward unit normal is
{\bm{n}}_{F}
.

2.2
Weak formulations

Recall from Assumption
1.1
that
f\in W^{-1,q}(\Omega)

with
q\in(\frac{2d}{d+2},2]
. Let
q^{\prime}\in[2,\frac{2d}{d-2})

be such that
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}=1
.
The weak formulation of the biharmonic problem
with type (I) BC’s is as follows: Find
u^{\textsc{(I)}}\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

(\nabla^{2}u^{\textsc{(I)}},\nabla^{2}v)_{\Omega}=\ell(v):=\langle f,v\rangle_{W^{-1,q},W^{1,q^{\prime}}_{0}},\qquad\forall v\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega).

(5)

Notice that the right-hand side is meaningful since the Sobolev embedding theorem implies that
H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow W^{1,q^{\prime}}(\Omega)
. Moreover, the model problem (
5
) is well-posed owing to the Lax–Milgram lemma.

The weak formulation of the biharmonic problem with type (II) BC’s is as follows:
Find
u^{\textsc{(II)}}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap H^{2}(\Omega)
such that

(\nabla^{2}u^{\textsc{(II)}},\nabla^{2}v)_{\Omega}=\ell(v),\qquad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap H^{2}(\Omega).

(6)

Owing to the Lax–Milgram lemma, this problem is well-posed (recall that the
H^{2}
-seminorm defines a norm on
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap H^{2}(\Omega)
). We also observe that the weak formulations (
5
) and (
6
) employ the same bilinear form and the same right-hand side; only the trial and test spaces differ.

2.3
Key identity under the classical regularity assumption

Before stating our main result based on our new regularity assumption (Assumption
1.1
), it is useful to illustrate the main idea under the classical regularity assumption
f\in H^{-2+s}(\Omega)
and
u\in H^{2+s}(\Omega)
with
s\in(\frac{1}{2},1]
.

Lemma 2.1

(Key identity)

Let
s\in(\frac{1}{2},1]
. The following holds for all
v\in H^{2+s}(\Omega)
and
all
w\in H^{2}(\mathcal{T})\cap H_{0}^{2-s}(\Omega)
:

\langle\Delta^{2}v,w\rangle_{H^{-2+s},H_{0}^{2-s}}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\Big\{(\nabla^{2}v,\nabla^{2}{w}_{K})_{K}-(\partial_{nn}v,\partial_{n}w_{K})_{\partial K}\Big\}.

(7)

Proof.

The proof hinges on a density argument. We consider a mollified sequence of functions
v_{\delta}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}v_{\delta}=v
in
H^{2+s}(\Omega)
. We apply (
3
) to
v_{\delta|K}
and
w_{K}:=w_{|K}
for all
K\in\mathcal{T}
, and sum the result cellwise. This gives

(\Delta^{2}v_{\delta},w)_{\Omega}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\Big\{(\nabla^{2}v_{\delta},\nabla^{2}{w}_{K})_{K}+(\partial_{n}\Delta v_{\delta},w_{K})_{\partial K}-(\partial_{nn}v_{\delta},\partial_{n}w_{K})_{\partial K}-(\partial_{nt}v_{\delta},\partial_{t}w_{K})_{\partial K}\Big\}.

The left-hand side is equal to
\langle\Delta^{2}v_{\delta},w\rangle_{H^{-2+s},H_{0}^{2-s}}
.
Moreover, the second and fourth summations on the right-hand side vanish. For the second summation, this follows from the fact that
\partial_{n}\Delta v_{\delta}
is single-valued at the mesh interfaces, whereas
w
is single-valued at the mesh interfaces and vanishes at the mesh boundary faces. Similar arguments are invoked for the fourth summation. Altogether, we obtain

\langle\Delta^{2}v_{\delta},w\rangle_{H^{-2+s},H_{0}^{2-s}}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\Big\{(\nabla^{2}v_{\delta},\nabla^{2}{w}_{K})_{K}-(\partial_{nn}v_{\delta},\partial_{n}w_{K})_{\partial K}\Big\}.

We can now pass to the limit
\delta\to 0
. This is straightforward for the right-hand side,
whereas for the left-hand side, we use
[
22
, Theorem 1.4.4.6]
which gives
\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\Delta^{2}v_{\delta}\rightarrow\Delta^{2}v
in
H^{-2+s}(\Omega)
. This proves
the identity (
7
).
∎

Remark 2.2

(Literature)

The identity from Lemma
2.1
is classical; see, e.g.,

[
2
]
. The present proof is, however, different. The advantages are
that it avoids the decomposition of the solution into singular and regular parts and
that it works seamlessly in any space dimension.

2.4
Key identity under the new regularity assumption

Extending the identity from Lemma
2.1
to the more general
setting of Assumption
1.1
requires giving a meaning to the trace of second-order derivatives on each mesh face individually. Before doing this, we briefly recall some material from
[
19
]
which was originally devised in the context of second-order elliptic problems.

Let
p
and
q
be two real numbers as in Assumption
1.1
, i.e.,
p>2
and
q\in(\frac{2d}{d+2},2]
. Let
\varrho\in(2,p]
be such that
q\geq\frac{\varrho d}{\varrho+d}

(this is indeed possible since the function

x\mapsto\frac{xd}{x+d}
is increasing on
[2,\infty)
) and let
\varrho^{\prime}\in[1,2)

be such that
\frac{1}{\varrho}+\frac{1}{\varrho^{\prime}}=1
. It is shown in
[
19
]
that for every mesh cell
K\in\mathcal{T}
, it is possible to give a meaning separately on every face
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}
to the normal component of fields in

{\bm{V}}^{\mathrm{d}}(K)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{{\bm{\sigma}}\in{\bm{L}}^{p}(K){\;|\;}\nabla{\cdot}{\bm{\sigma}}\in L^{q}(K)\}.

(8)

Specifically, one defines the operator
\gamma^{\mathrm{d}}_{K,F}:{\bm{V}}^{\mathrm{d}}(K)\to(W^{\frac{1}{\varrho},\varrho^{\prime}}(F))^{\prime}
such that for all
\phi\in W^{\frac{1}{\varrho},\varrho^{\prime}}(F)
,

\langle\gamma_{K,F}^{\mathrm{d}}({\bm{\sigma}}),{\phi}\rangle_{F}:=\int_{K}\Big({\bm{\sigma}}{\cdot}\nabla L_{F}^{K}(\phi)+(\nabla{\cdot}{\bm{\sigma}})L_{F}^{K}(\phi)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x,

(9)

where
L_{F}^{K}:W^{\frac{1}{\varrho},\varrho^{\prime}}(F)\rightarrow W^{1,\varrho^{\prime}}(K)
is a face-to-cell lifting operator satisfying
L_{K}^{F}(\phi)_{|F}=\phi
and
L_{K}^{F}(\phi)_{|\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}\backslash F}=0

(see
[
19
, Lemma 3.1]
). Notice that
\gamma^{\mathrm{d}}_{K,F}({\bm{\sigma}})=({\bm{\sigma}}{\cdot}{\bm{n}}_{K})_{|F}
whenever the field
{\bm{\sigma}}
is smooth.
Consider now the functional space

\mathcal{V}^{(2)}(\Omega)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{v\in W^{1,p}(\Omega){\;|\;}\Delta v\in L^{q}(\Omega)\}.

(10)

(The superscript refers to the context of second-order PDEs.)
We have
(\nabla v)_{|K}\in{\bm{V}}^{\mathrm{d}}(K)

for all
v\in\mathcal{V}^{(2)}(\Omega)
and all
K\in\mathcal{T}
. Moreover, since
\rho>2
, we have
(w_{K})_{|F}\in W^{\frac{1}{\varrho},\varrho^{\prime}}(F)
for all
w\in H^{1}(\mathcal{T})
, all
K\in\mathcal{T}
, and all
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}
. Therefore, it is meaningful to define the
following bilinear form for all
v\in\mathcal{V}^{(2)}(\Omega)
and all
w\in H^{1}(\mathcal{T})
:

\displaystyle n_{\sharp}^{(2)}(v,w):=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\Big\{\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}^{\mathrm{i}}}\langle\gamma_{K,F}^{\mathrm{d}}(\nabla v),w_{K|F}-\{\kern-3.1298pt\{w\}\kern-3.1298pt\}_{F}\rangle_{F}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}^{\mathrm{b}}}\langle\gamma_{K,F}^{\mathrm{d}}(\nabla v),w_{K|F}\rangle_{F}\Big\}.

(11)

Since
w_{K|F}-\{\kern-3.1298pt\{w\}\kern-3.1298pt\}_{F}=\frac{1}{2}({\bm{n}}_{F}{\cdot}{\bm{n}}_{K})[\![w_{h}]\!]_{F}
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}^{\mathrm{i}}
, we observe that the bilinear form defined in (
11
) coincides with the one defined in
[
19
, Equ. (3.12)]
upon setting therein
\theta_{K,F}=\frac{1}{2}
if
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}^{\mathrm{i}}
and
\theta_{K,F}=1
otherwise. Hence, invoking
[
19
, Lemma 3.3]
, we infer that for all
v\in\mathcal{V}^{(2)}(\Omega)
and all
w\in H^{1}(\mathcal{T})
, the following important relation holds:

\displaystyle-(\Delta v,w)_{\Omega}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}(\nabla v,\nabla w_{K})_{K}-n_{\sharp}^{(2)}(v,w).

(12)

We are now ready to address the biharmonic problem. We define the functional space

\mathcal{V}^{(4)}(\Omega)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{v\in W^{2,p}(\Omega){\;|\;}\Delta v\in W^{1,q}(\Omega)\}.

(13)

(The superscript refers to the context of fourth-order PDEs.)
We also define the following bilinear form for all
v\in\mathcal{V}^{(4)}(\Omega)
and all
w\in H^{2}(\mathcal{T})
:

n_{\sharp}^{(4)}(v,w):=\sum_{i\in\{1{:}d\}}n_{\sharp}^{(2)}(\partial_{i}v,\partial_{i}w).

(14)

This definition is meaningful since for all
v\in\mathcal{V}^{(4)}(\Omega)
and all
w\in H^{2}(\mathcal{T})
,
we have
\partial_{i}v\in\mathcal{V}^{(2)}(\Omega)
and
\partial_{i}w\in H^{1}(\mathcal{T})
, for all

i\in\{1{:}d\}
.

Lemma 2.3

(Key identity)

The following holds for all
v\in\mathcal{V}^{(4)}(\Omega)
and all

w\in H^{2}(\mathcal{T})\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
:

\langle\Delta^{2}v,w\rangle_{W^{-1,q},W_{0}^{1,q^{\prime}}}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}(\nabla^{2}v,\nabla^{2}{w}_{K})_{K}-n_{\sharp}^{(4)}(v,w).

(15)

Proof.

Since
\Delta v\in W^{1,q}(\Omega)
, we have
\Delta^{2}v=\Delta(\Delta v)\in W^{-1,q}(\Omega)
. Moreover, since
w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, we infer that

\langle\Delta^{2}v,w\rangle_{W^{-1,q},W_{0}^{1,q^{\prime}}}=-(\nabla\Delta v,\nabla{w})_{\Omega}=\sum_{i\in\{1{:}d\}}-(\Delta\partial_{i}v,\partial_{i}w)_{\Omega}.

For all
i\in\{1{:}d\}
, we have
\partial_{i}v\in\mathcal{V}^{(2)}(\Omega)
and
\partial_{i}w\in H^{1}(\mathcal{T})
.
Applying (
12
) to the right-hand side of the above equation, we obtain

\langle\Delta^{2}v,w\rangle_{W^{-1,q},W_{0}^{1,q^{\prime}}}=\sum_{i\in\{1{:}d\}}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}(\nabla\partial_{i}v,\nabla\partial_{i}w_{K})_{K}-\sum_{i\in\{1{:}d\}}n_{\sharp}^{(2)}(\partial_{i}v,\partial_{i}w).

The conclusion is straightforward by definition of the Hessian and of
n_{\sharp}^{(4)}
.
∎

The following reformulation of
n_{\sharp}^{(4)}
will be useful in our analysis.
Let us set
n_{F,i}:={\bm{n}}_{F}{\cdot}{\bm{e}}_{i}
for all
i\in\{1{:}d\}
, where

({\bm{e}}_{i})_{i\in\{1{:}d\}}
denotes the canonical Cartesian basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Lemma 2.4

(Reformulation of
n_{\sharp}^{(4)}
)

For all
v\in\mathcal{V}^{(4)}(\Omega)
and all
w\in H^{2}(\mathcal{T})\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, we have

\displaystyle n_{\sharp}^{(4)}(v,w)=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{i\in\{1{:}d\}}\Big\{

\displaystyle\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}^{\mathrm{i}}}\langle\gamma_{K,F}^{\mathrm{d}}(\nabla\partial_{i}v),n_{F,i}{\bm{n}}_{F}{\cdot}((\nabla w_{K})_{|F}-\{\kern-3.1298pt\{\nabla w\}\kern-3.1298pt\}_{F})\rangle_{F}

(16)

\displaystyle+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}^{\mathrm{b}}}\langle\gamma_{K,F}^{\mathrm{d}}(\nabla\partial_{i}v),n_{F,i}{\bm{n}}_{F}{\cdot}(\nabla w_{K})_{|F}\rangle_{F}\Big\}.

Proof.

We first use the definitions of
n_{\sharp}^{(2)}
and
n_{\sharp}^{(4)}
to write

n_{\sharp}^{(4)}(v,w)=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{i\in\{1{:}d\}}\Big\{\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}^{\mathrm{i}}}\langle\gamma_{K,F}^{\mathrm{d}}(\nabla\partial_{i}v),(\partial_{i}w_{K})_{|F}-\{\kern-3.1298pt\{\partial_{i}w\}\kern-3.1298pt\}_{F}\rangle_{F}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}^{\mathrm{b}}}\langle\gamma_{K,F}^{\mathrm{d}}(\nabla\partial_{i}v),(\partial_{i}w_{K})_{|F}\rangle_{F}\Big\}.

We observe that for all
K\in\mathcal{T}
and all
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}
,

(\partial_{i}w_{K})_{|F}={\bm{e}}_{i}{\cdot}(\nabla w_{K})_{|F}=n_{F,i}{\bm{n}}_{F}{\cdot}(\nabla w_{K})_{|F}+{\bm{e}}_{i}{\cdot}(\nabla_{t}w_{K})_{|F},

where
(\nabla_{t}w_{K})_{|F}
denotes the tangential gradient of
w_{K}
on
F
. Since
w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, this quantity is single-valued on every mesh interface
F\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{i}}
and vanishes on every mesh boundary face
F\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{b}}
. Moreover, since
\nabla\partial_{i}v
has an integrable divergence on
\Omega
by assumption, we infer that for all
F=\partial K_{1}\cap\partial K_{2}\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{i}}
,

\sum_{j\in\{1,2\}}\langle\gamma_{K_{j},F}^{\mathrm{d}}(\nabla\partial_{i}v),\phi\rangle_{F}=0,\qquad\forall\phi\in W^{\frac{1}{\varrho},\varrho^{\prime}}(F).

(17)

This implies that

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}}\langle\gamma_{K,F}^{\mathrm{d}}(\nabla\partial_{i}v),(\partial_{i}w_{K})_{|F}\rangle_{F}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}}\langle\gamma_{K,F}^{\mathrm{d}}(\nabla\partial_{i}v),n_{F,i}{\bm{n}}_{F}{\cdot}(\nabla w_{K})_{|F}\rangle_{F}.

A similar reasoning shows that

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}^{\mathrm{i}}}\langle\gamma_{K,F}^{\mathrm{d}}(\nabla\partial_{i}v),\{\kern-3.1298pt\{\partial_{i}w\}\kern-3.1298pt\}_{F}\rangle_{F}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}^{\mathrm{i}}}\langle\gamma_{K,F}^{\mathrm{d}}(\nabla\partial_{i}v),n_{F,i}{\bm{n}}_{F}{\cdot}\{\kern-3.1298pt\{\nabla w\}\kern-3.1298pt\}\rangle_{F}.

(18)

Combining the two above identities proves the assertion.
∎

Remark 2.5

(Simplifications)

Both terms in (
18
) actually vanish owing to (
17
). This means that for all
v\in\mathcal{V}^{(4)}(\Omega)
and all
w\in H^{2}(\mathcal{T})
, we have

n_{\sharp}^{(4)}(v,w)=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}}\sum_{i\in\{1{:}d\}}\langle\gamma_{K,F}^{\mathrm{d}}(\nabla\partial_{i}v),(\partial_{i}w_{K})_{|F}\rangle_{F},

(19a)

and whenever
w\in H^{2}(\mathcal{T})\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, we also have

n_{\sharp}^{(4)}(v,w)=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}}\sum_{i\in\{1{:}d\}}\langle\gamma_{K,F}^{\mathrm{d}}(\nabla\partial_{i}v),n_{F,i}{\bm{n}}_{F}{\cdot}(\nabla w_{K})_{|F}\rangle_{F}.

(19b)

However, the expressions (
14
) and (
16
) are those that are needed in this work because we will extend the domain of the first argument
v
to spaces containing functions which are only piecewise smooth and for which (
17
) no longer holds true. In this situation, it is important to use (
14
) and (
16
) and not (
19
).

3
Discrete setting for
C^{0}
-HHO methods

In this section, we introduce the key ingredients to formulate
C^{0}
-HHO methods to approximate the biharmonic problem with both types of BC’s. The starting point for the present
C^{0}
-HHO methods are the fully discontinuous HHO methods from
[
13
]
. While the latter methods rely on a triple of discrete unknowns, approximating the solution in each mesh cell, its trace on each mesh face, and the trace of its normal derivative (oriented by
{\bm{n}}_{F}
) on each mesh face, the present
C^{0}
-HHO methods only rely on a pair of discrete unknowns, approximating the solution in each mesh cell and the trace of its normal derivative on each mesh face.

3.1
Local reconstruction, stabilization, and stability

Let
k\geq 0
be the polynomial degree. Recall that we consider a mesh
\mathcal{T}

from a shape-regular family of simplical meshes such that
\mathcal{T}
covers
\Omega
exactly.
For every mesh cell
K\in\mathcal{T}
, the local
C^{0}
-HHO space is

\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}:=\mathbb{P}_{k+2}(K)\times\mathbb{P}_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}),

(20)

with the broken polynomial space
\mathbb{P}_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}):=\times_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}}\mathbb{P}_{k}(F)
.
A generic element in
\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}
is denoted
\widehat{v}_{K}=(v_{K},\gamma_{\partial K})
with
v_{K}\in\mathbb{P}_{k+2}(K)
and
\gamma_{\partial K}\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{\partial K})
. The first component of
\widehat{v}_{K}
aims at representing the solution inside the
mesh cell and the second the trace of its normal derivative (oriented by
{\bm{n}}_{K}
) on the cell boundary.

Let
K\in\mathcal{T}
.
We define the local reconstruction operator
R_{K}:\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}_{k+2}(K)
such that, for all
\widehat{v}_{K}\in\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}
with
\widehat{v}_{K}:=(v_{K},\gamma_{\partial K})
, the polynomial

R_{K}(\widehat{v}_{K})\in\mathbb{P}_{k+2}(K)
is uniquely defined by solving the following problem with test functions
w\in\mathbb{P}_{k+2}(K)^{\perp}:=\{w\in\mathbb{P}_{k+2}(K)\;|\;(w,\xi)_{K}=0,\forall\xi\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(K)\}
:

\displaystyle(\nabla^{2}R_{K}(\widehat{v}_{K}),\nabla^{2}w)_{K}={}

\displaystyle(\nabla^{2}{v}_{K},\nabla^{2}w)_{K}-(\partial_{n}v_{K}-\gamma_{\partial K},\partial_{nn}w)_{\partial K},

(21)

together with the condition
(R_{K}(\widehat{v}_{K}),\xi)_{K}=(v_{K},\xi)_{K}
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(K)
.
The local stabilization bilinear form is defined such that, for all
(\widehat{v}_{K},\widehat{w}_{K})\in\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}\times\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}
, with
\widehat{v}_{K}:=(v_{K},\gamma_{\partial K})
and
\widehat{w}_{K}:=(w_{K},\chi_{\partial K})
,

\displaystyle S_{\partial K}(\widehat{v}_{K},\widehat{w}_{K}):={}

\displaystyle h_{K}^{-1}\big(\Pi^{k}_{\partial K}(\gamma_{\partial K}-\partial_{n}{v}_{K}),\chi_{\partial K}-\partial_{n}{w}_{K}\big)_{\partial K},

(22)

where
\Pi^{k}_{\partial K}
denotes the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection onto
\mathbb{P}_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{\partial K})
.

We define the local bilinear form
{a}_{K}
on
\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}\times\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}
such that

{a}_{K}(\widehat{v}_{K},\widehat{w}_{K}):=(\nabla^{2}R_{K}(\widehat{v}_{K}),\nabla^{2}R_{K}(\widehat{w}_{K}))_{K}+S_{\partial K}(\widehat{v}_{K},\widehat{w}_{K}).

(23)

We define the local energy seminorm such that, for all
\widehat{v}_{K}:=(v_{K},\gamma_{\partial K})\in\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}
,

\displaystyle|\widehat{v}_{K}|^{2}_{\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}}:={}

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}v_{K}\|_{K}^{2}+h_{K}^{-1}\|\gamma_{\partial K}-\partial_{n}v_{K}\|_{\partial K}^{2}.

(24)

Lemma 3.1

(Local stability and boundedness)

There is a real number
\alpha>0
, depending only on the mesh shape-regularity and the polynomial degree
k
, such that, for all
h>0
, all
K\in\mathcal{T}
, and all
\widehat{v}_{K}\in\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}
,

\alpha|\widehat{v}_{K}|^{2}_{\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}}\leq\|\nabla^{2}R_{K}(\widehat{v}_{K})\|_{K}^{2}+S_{\partial K}(\widehat{v}_{K},\widehat{v}_{K})\leq\alpha^{-1}|\widehat{v}_{K}|^{2}_{\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}}.

(25)

Proof.

The proof proceeds as that of
[
13
, Lemma 4.1]
.
∎

Remark 3.2

(Cell unknowns)

The choice of the polynomial space
\mathbb{P}_{k+2}(K)
leverages on
[
13
]
and has been made to allow for the simple stabilization bilinear form defined in (
22
). Alternative choices for the cell unknowns are possible, such as taking the polynomial space
\mathbb{P}_{k+1}(K)
(see
[
13
, Remark 3.2]
) and even
\mathbb{P}_{k}(K)
for
k\geq 1
(see
[
1
]
). Both choices lead to fewer cell unknowns, which can marginally alleviate the costs of static condensation, but require a more subtle form for stabilization involving the reconstruction operator, and this typically adds some computational costs. Moreover, whatever the choice for the cell unknowns, the size of the linear system after static condensation is the same.

3.2
Global discrete spaces and discrete problems

Let
\mathbb{P}_{k+2}^{\rm g}(\mathcal{T})
denote the usual
C^{0}
-conforming finite element space composed of continuous, piecewise polynomials of degree at most
(k+2)
on the mesh
\mathcal{T}
(the superscript
g
refers to the integrability of the gradient of functions in
\mathbb{P}_{k+2}^{\rm g}(\mathcal{T})
).
We set

\widehat{V}_{h}^{k}:=\mathbb{P}_{k+2}^{\rm g}(\mathcal{T})\times V_{\mathcal{F}}^{k},\qquad V_{\mathcal{F}}^{k}:=\mathbb{P}_{k}(\mathcal{F}),

(26)

with the broken polynomial space
\mathbb{P}_{k}(\mathcal{F}):=\times_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{P}_{k}(F)
.
We also define the following subspaces accounting for (homogeneous) boundary conditions:

V_{\mathcal{T},0}^{k+2}:=\mathbb{P}_{k+2}^{\rm g}(\mathcal{T})\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\qquad V_{\mathcal{F},0}^{k}:=\{\gamma_{\mathcal{F}}\in V_{\mathcal{F}}^{k}\;|\;\gamma_{\mathcal{F}|F}=0,\,\forall F\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{b}}\}.

(27)

The global
C^{0}
-HHO spaces used to approximate the biharmonic problems are then defined as follows:

\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(I)}}:=V_{\mathcal{T},0}^{k+2}\times V_{\mathcal{F},0}^{k},\qquad\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(II)}}:=V_{\mathcal{T},0}^{k+2}\times V_{\mathcal{F}}^{k},

(28)

for type (I) and type (II) BC’s, respectively. This means that the boundary condition
u=0
is directly enforced on the trace of the cell unknowns at the boundary, the boundary condition
\partial_{n}u=0
for type (I) BC’s is directly enforced on the discrete unknowns representing the normal derivative at the mesh boundary faces, and the boundary condition
\partial_{nn}u=0
is not enforced directly, but in a natural way (i.e., it results from the discrete problem). Notice also that
\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(I)}}\subset\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(II)}}
.

Any member of
\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(I)}}
or
\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(II)}}
is generically denoted by
\widehat{v}_{h}:=(v_{\mathcal{T}},\gamma_{\mathcal{F}})
with
v_{\mathcal{T}}:=(v_{K})_{K\in\mathcal{T}}
and
\gamma_{\mathcal{F}}:=(\gamma_{F})_{F\in\mathcal{F}}
. For every mesh cell
K\in\mathcal{T}
, the local components of
\widehat{v}_{h}
are collected in the pair
\widehat{v}_{K}:=(v_{K},\gamma_{\partial K})\in\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}
with
\gamma_{\partial K}|_{F}:=({\bm{n}}_{F}{\cdot}{\bm{n}}_{K})\gamma_{F}
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\partial K}
.
The global bilinear form is assembled cellwise from the contributions of all the mesh cells, yielding

{a}_{h}(\widehat{v}_{h},\widehat{w}_{h}):=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}a_{K}(\widehat{v}_{K},\widehat{w}_{K}).

(29)

The discrete problems are as follows:

•

For type (I) BC’s, one seeks
\widehat{u}_{h}^{\textsc{(I)}}\in\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(I)}}
such that

a_{h}(\widehat{u}_{h}^{\textsc{(I)}},\widehat{w}_{h})=\ell(w_{\mathcal{T}}),\qquad\forall w_{h}\in\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(I)}}.

(30)

•

For type (II) BC’s, one seeks
\widehat{u}_{h}^{\textsc{(II)}}\in\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(II)}}
such that

a_{h}(\widehat{u}_{h}^{\textsc{(II)}},\widehat{w}_{h})=\ell(w_{\mathcal{T}}),\qquad\forall w_{h}\in\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(II)}}.

(31)

Notice that the discrete problems (
30
) and (
31
) employ the same discrete bilinear form
a_{h}
and the same right-hand side; only the discrete trial and test spaces differ. We also observe that the above right-hand sides are meaningful since
V_{\mathcal{T},0}^{k+2}\subset W^{1,q^{\prime}}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Both spaces
\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(I)}}
and
\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(II)}}
are equipped with the norm

\|\widehat{v}_{h}\|_{\widehat{V}_{h}^{k}}^{2}:=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}|\widehat{v}_{K}|^{2}_{\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}},

(32)

with the local energy seminorm
|{\cdot}|_{\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}}
defined in (
24
).
To verify that this indeed defines a norm on
\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(II)}}
(and thus also on
\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(I)}}
), we notice that if
\widehat{v}_{h}=(v_{\mathcal{T}},\gamma_{\mathcal{F}})\in\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(II)}}
satisfies
\|\widehat{v}_{h}\|_{\widehat{V}_{h}^{k}}=0
, then
v_{\mathcal{T}}
is a globally affine function in
\Omega
which vanishes at the boundary
\partial\Omega
, so that
v_{\mathcal{T}}=0
; moreover,
\gamma_{\mathcal{F}}
coincides on each mesh face with the trace of the normal derivative of
v_{\mathcal{T}}
, so that
v_{\mathcal{F}}=0
as well. A direct consequence of the lower bound in (
25
) is therefore that the global bilinear form
a_{h}
is coercive on
\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(II)}}
(and thus also on
\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(I)}}
). Hence, both discrete problems (
30
) and (
31
) are well-posed owing to the Lax–Milgram lemma.

Remark 3.3

(Static condensation)

Unlike the fully discontinuous HHO methods from
[
1
,
13
,
12
]
where all the the cell unknowns can be eliminated locally by a static condensation procedure,
C^{0}
-HHO methods are amenable to static condensation only if
(k+2)\geq(d+1)
on
d
-dimensional simplices, and in this case, only the so-called cell bubble functions vanishing on the cell boundary can be eliminated, as in classical
C^{0}
-conforming FEM. Notice also that static condensation is more delicate for
C^{0}
-IPDG methods since only those bubble functions having a zero second-order derivative at the cell boundary can be locally eliminated.

3.3
Analysis tools and local HHO interpolation operator

For the reader’s convenience, let us briefly restate some classical discrete inverse inequalities and polynomial approximation properties on shape-regular families of simplicial meshes. The results are classical, and we refer the reader, e.g., to
[
17
, Chap. 9-11]
for the proofs. In the rest of this paper, we use
the symbol
C
to denote any positive generic constant
(its value can change at each occurrence)
that is independent of
h>0
, the considered mesh cell
K\in\mathcal{T}
, and the considered function in the inequality. The value of
C
can depend on the shape-regularity parameter of the mesh sequence and the polynomial degree (whenever relevant).

Lemma 3.4

(Discrete inverse inequalities)

Let
l\geq 0
be a polynomial degree.
There is
C
such that for all
h>0
, all
K\in\mathcal{T}
, all
p\in[1,\infty]
, and all
v_{h}\in\mathbb{P}_{l}(K)
,

\displaystyle\|{v}_{h}\|_{\partial K}

\displaystyle\leq Ch_{K}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|{v}_{h}\|_{K},

(33)

\displaystyle\|\nabla{v}_{h}\|_{K}

\displaystyle\leq Ch_{K}^{-1}\|{v}_{h}\|_{K},

(34)

\displaystyle\|{v}_{h}\|_{L^{p}(K)}

\displaystyle\leq Ch_{K}^{d(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2})}\|{v}_{h}\|_{K}.

(35)

Lemma 3.5

(Multiplicative trace inequality)

There is
C
such that for all
h>0
,
all
K\in\mathcal{T}
, and all
v\in H^{1}(K)
,

\|{v}\|_{\partial K}\leq C\big(h_{K}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|{v}\|_{K}+h_{K}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla v\|_{K}\big).

(36)

Lemma 3.6

(Polynomial approximation)

Let
l\geq 1
be the polynomial degree and let
d\in\{2,3\}
(so that
l+1>\frac{d}{2}
).
Let
\mathcal{L}_{h}^{l}
be the
H^{1}
-conforming Lagrange interpolation operator
onto
\mathbb{P}_{l}^{\rm g}(\mathcal{T})
and let
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{l}
be its local version mapping onto

\mathbb{P}_{k+2}(K)
for all
K\in\mathcal{T}
.
There is
C
such that for all
r>\frac{d}{2}
,
all
m\in\{0,\ldots,\lfloor r\rfloor\}
, all
p\in[1,\infty]
, all
h>0
, all
K\in\mathcal{T}
,
and all
v\in H^{r}(K)
,

h^{m}_{K}\|{v}-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{l}(v)\|_{H^{m}(K)}+h^{d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}_{K}\|{v}-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{l}(v)\|_{L^{p}(K)}\leq Ch^{t}_{K}|{v}|_{H^{t}(K)},

(37)

where
t:=\min\{r,l+1\}
.

Let us briefly highlight some useful consequences of Lemma
3.6
. First,
taking
l=1
,
r=2
, and
m\in\{0,1\}
in (
37
) shows that for all
h>0
,
all
K\in\mathcal{T}
, and all
v\in H^{2}(K)
,

\|v-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{1}(v)\|_{K}+h_{K}\|\nabla(v-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{1}(v))\|_{K}\leq Ch_{K}^{2}\|\nabla^{2}v\|_{K}.

(38)

Then, for all
v\in H^{2}(K)
, setting
z:=v-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v)
for any polynomial degree
k\geq 0
, and observing that
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{1}(z)=0
since
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{1}\circ\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}=\mathcal{L}_{K}^{1}
, we infer from (
38
) that
\|\nabla z\|_{K}\leq Ch_{K}\|\nabla^{2}z\|_{K}
. Hence, owing to the multiplicative trace inequality (
36
), we have
h_{K}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla z\|_{\partial K}\leq C\|\nabla^{2}z\|_{K}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle h_{K}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla(v-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v))\|_{\partial K}\leq C\|\nabla^{2}(v-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v))\|_{K}.

(39)

Remark 3.7

(
H^{1}
-conforming quasi-interpolation operator)

The lower bound
r>\frac{d}{2}
is not really a restriction for the biharmonic problem in space dimension
d\in\{2,3\}
since the weak solution always sits in
H^{2}(\Omega)
. In higher space dimension, the restriction
r>\frac{d}{2}
can be lifted by invoking a quasi-interpolation operator onto
\mathbb{P}_{l}^{\rm g}(\mathcal{T})
instead of the Lagrange interpolation operator; we refer the reader to
[
27
,
16
]
for examples of quasi-interpolation operators that can be considered.

For every mesh cell
K\in\mathcal{T}
, we define the local HHO reduction operator
\mathcal{\widehat{I}}^{k}_{K}:H^{2}(K)\rightarrow\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}
such that, for all
v\in H^{2}(K)
,

\mathcal{\widehat{I}}^{k}_{K}(v):=(\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v),\Pi_{\partial K}^{k}({\bm{n}}_{K}{\cdot}\nabla v))\in\widehat{V}_{K}^{k}.

(40)

This definition is meaningful since
H^{2}(K)\hookrightarrow C^{0}(\overline{K})
for
d\in\{2,3\}
.
In addition, we define the local HHO interpolation operator

J^{\textsc{hho}}_{K}:=R_{K}\circ\mathcal{\widehat{I}}^{k}_{K}:H^{2}(K)\rightarrow\mathbb{P}_{k+2}(K).

(41)

In what follows, we need to measure the HHO interpolation error in some augmented norm which reflects Assumption
1.1
. Specifically, we set

\|v\|^{2}_{\sharp,K}:=\|\nabla^{2}v\|_{K}^{2}+h_{K}^{2d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|\nabla^{2}v\|_{L^{p}(K)}^{2}+{h_{K}^{2+2d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q})}\|\nabla\Delta v\|_{L^{q}(K)}^{2}},

(42)

for all
v\in W^{2,p}(K)
with
\Delta v\in W^{1,q}(K)
(recall that
p>2
and
q\in(\frac{2d}{d+2},2]
).

Lemma 3.8

(HHO interpolation error)

In the above setting, the following holds for all
K\in\mathcal{T}
:

\|v-J^{\textsc{hho}}_{K}(v)\|_{\sharp,K}^{2}+S_{\partial K}(\mathcal{\widehat{I}}^{k}_{K}(v),\mathcal{\widehat{I}}^{k}_{K}(v))\leq C\|v-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v)\|_{\sharp,K}^{2}.

(43)

Proof.

(1) Let us first bound
\|v-J^{\textsc{hho}}_{K}(v)\|_{\sharp,K}
. The triangle inequality
followed by the discrete inverse inequalities from Lemma
3.4
implies that

\displaystyle\|v-J^{\textsc{hho}}_{K}(v)\|_{\sharp,K}

\displaystyle\leq\|v-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v)\|_{\sharp,K}+\|J^{\textsc{hho}}_{K}(v)-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v)\|_{\sharp,K}

\displaystyle\leq\|v-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v)\|_{\sharp,K}+C\|\nabla^{2}(J^{\textsc{hho}}_{K}(v)-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v))\|_{K},

so that we only need to bound the last term on the right-hand side.
A straightforward calculation using the definition (
21
) of
R_{K}
shows that,
for all
w\in\mathbb{P}_{k+2}(K)^{\perp}
,

\displaystyle(\nabla^{2}J^{\textsc{hho}}_{K}(v),\nabla^{2}w)_{K}={}

\displaystyle(\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v),\nabla^{2}w)_{K}-(\partial_{n}\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v)-\Pi_{\partial K}^{k}(\partial_{n}v),\partial_{nn}w)_{\partial K}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle(\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v),\nabla^{2}w)_{K}-(\partial_{n}(\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v)-v),\partial_{nn}w)_{\partial K},

together with the relation
(J^{\textsc{hho}}_{K}(v),\xi)_{K}=(\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v),\xi)_{K}
, for all
\xi\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(K)
.
Re-arranging the terms, taking
w=J^{\textsc{hho}}_{K}(v)-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v)
, and
using the discrete trace inverse inequality (
33
) gives

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}(J^{\textsc{hho}}_{K}(v)-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v))\|_{K}\leq{}

\displaystyle Ch_{K}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\partial_{n}(\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v)-v)\|_{\partial K}.

Bounding the right-hand side using (
39
) leads to

\|\nabla^{2}(J^{\textsc{hho}}_{K}(v)-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v))\|_{K}\leq{}C\|\nabla^{2}(v-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v))\|_{K}.

Putting the above bounds together shows that
\|v-J^{\textsc{hho}}_{K}(v)\|_{\sharp,K}\leq C\|v-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v)\|_{\sharp,K}
since the
\|{\cdot}\|_{\sharp,K}
-norm controls the
H^{2}(K)
-seminorm.

(2) Let us now bound
S_{\partial K}(\mathcal{\widehat{I}}^{k}_{K}(v),\mathcal{\widehat{I}}^{k}_{K}(v))
.
We have

\displaystyle S_{\partial K}(\mathcal{\widehat{I}}^{k}_{K}(v),\mathcal{\widehat{I}}^{k}_{K}(v))={}

\displaystyle h_{K}^{-1}\|\Pi^{k}_{\partial K}(\Pi^{k}_{\partial K}(\partial_{n}v)-\partial_{n}(\mathcal{L}^{k+2}_{K}(v)))\|^{2}_{\partial K}\leq h_{K}^{-1}\|\partial_{n}(v-\mathcal{L}^{k+2}_{K}(v))\|^{2}_{\partial K},

since
\Pi^{k}_{\partial K}\circ\Pi^{k}_{\partial K}=\Pi^{k}_{\partial K}
and
\Pi^{k}_{\partial K}
is
L^{2}
-stable.
The right-hand side is bounded by means of (
39
), yielding

\displaystyle S_{\partial K}(\mathcal{\widehat{I}}^{k}_{K}(v),\mathcal{\widehat{I}}^{k}_{K}(v))

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla^{2}(v-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(v))\|_{K}^{2}.

Since the
\|{\cdot}\|_{\sharp,K}
-norm controls the
H^{2}(K)
-seminorm, this concludes the proof.
∎

It is convenient to define global versions of the above operators and norms.
The global HHO reduction operator
\mathcal{\widehat{I}}_{h}^{k}:H^{2}(\Omega)\to\widehat{V}_{h}^{k}
is defined such that, for all
v\in H^{2}(\Omega)
,

\mathcal{\widehat{I}}_{h}^{k}(v):=\big(\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k+2}(v),(\Pi_{F}^{k}({\bm{n}}_{F}{\cdot}(\nabla v)_{|F}))_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\big)\in\widehat{V}_{h}^{k},

(44)

so that the local components of
\mathcal{\widehat{I}}_{h}^{k}(v)
are
\mathcal{\widehat{I}}^{k}_{K}(v|_{K})
for all
K\in\mathcal{T}
. We also notice that
\mathcal{\widehat{I}}_{h}^{k}(v)\in\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(II)}}
if
v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
since the Lagrange interpolation operator preserves the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition. Moreover, we have
\mathcal{\widehat{I}}_{h}^{k}(v)\in\widehat{V}_{h}^{k,\textsc{(I)}}
if
v\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
. Furthermore, the global HHO interpolation operator mapping onto
\mathbb{P}_{k+2}(\mathcal{T})
is defined such that
J^{\textsc{hho}}_{h}(v)_{|K}:=J^{\textsc{hho}}_{K}(v_{|K})
for all
K\in\mathcal{T}
and all
v\in H^{2}(\Omega)
.
Finally, we define the global augmented norm such that

\|v\|_{\sharp,h}^{2}:=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\|v_{|K}\|_{\sharp,K}^{2}
for all
v\in\mathcal{V}^{(4)}(\Omega)
.

(Consistency)

Theorem 4.6

(Error estimate)

Let

4.4
Energy-error estimate for
C^{0}
-IPDG methods

To illustrate that the key identity (
15
) from Lemma
2.3
has a broader applicability than
C^{0}
-HHO methods, we briefly outline here how this identity can be used in the error analysis of
C^{0}
-IPDG methods.
Focusing for brevity on type (II) BC’s only, the
C^{0}
-IPDG bilinear form is defined as follows
for all
v_{h},w_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{T},0}^{k+2}:=\mathbb{P}_{k+2}^{\rm g}(\mathcal{T})\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
:

\displaystyle a_{h}^{\textsc{ipdg}}(v_{h},w_{h}):=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}(\nabla^{2}v_{h},\nabla^{2}w_{h})_{K}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{i}}}\varpi_{F}h_{F}^{-1}([\![\partial_{n}v_{h}]\!]_{F},[\![\partial_{n}w_{h}]\!]_{F})_{F}

(61)

\displaystyle-\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{i}}}(\{\kern-3.1298pt\{\partial_{nn}v_{h}\}\kern-3.1298pt\}_{F},[\![\partial_{n}w_{h}]\!]_{F})_{F}-\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{i}}}([\![\partial_{n}v_{h}]\!]_{F},\{\kern-3.1298pt\{\partial_{nn}w_{h}\}\kern-3.1298pt\}_{F})_{F},

where all the normal derivatives are understood to be oriented by
{\bm{n}}_{F}
and
with the user-defined penalty parameter
\varpi_{F}>0
. For type (I) BC’s, the
three summations over the mesh interfaces are realized over the whole set of mesh faces.
The discrete problem consists of
finding
u_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{T},0}^{k+2}
such that

a_{h}^{\textsc{ipdg}}(v_{h},w_{h})=\ell(w_{h}):=\langle f,w_{h}\rangle_{W^{-1,q},W_{0}^{1,q^{\prime}}},\qquad\forall w_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{T},0}^{k+2}.

(62)

The stability analysis reveals that the bilinear form
a_{h}^{\textsc{ipdg}}
is coercive on

V_{\mathcal{T},0}^{k+2}
if the penalty parameters
\varpi_{F}
are large enough; see, e.g.,
[
2
]
. The coercivity norm is
\|w_{h}\|_{V_{\mathcal{T},0}^{k+2}}^{2}:=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\|\nabla^{2}w_{h}\|_{K}^{2}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{i}}}h_{F}^{-1}\|[\![\partial_{n}w_{h}]\!]_{F}\|_{F}^{2}
.

The novelty here consists in bounding the consistency error under Assumption
1.1
.
Let the bilinear form
n_{\sharp}^{\textsc{ipdg}}
be defined as
n_{\sharp}^{(4)}
, except that the contributions of all the mesh boundary faces
F\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{b}}
are discarded. The arguments in the proof of (
49b
) show that
n_{\sharp}^{\textsc{ipdg}}(u,w_{h})=n_{\sharp}^{(4)}(u,w_{h})
for all
w_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{T},0}^{k+2}
. Therefore, using the key identity (
15
) from Lemma
2.3
gives

\langle f,w_{h}\rangle_{W^{-1,q},W^{1,q^{\prime}}_{0}}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}(\nabla^{2}u,\nabla^{2}{w}_{h})_{K}-n_{\sharp}^{(4)}(u,w_{h})=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}(\nabla^{2}u,\nabla^{2}{w}_{h})_{K}-n_{\sharp}^{\textsc{ipdg}}(u,w_{h}),

(63)

for all
w_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{T},0}^{k+2}
. Moreover,
using the same proof as for (
47
), we establish the following identity for all
v_{h},w_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{T},0}^{k+2}
:

n_{\sharp}^{\textsc{ipdg}}(v_{h},w_{h})=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{i}}}(\{\kern-3.1298pt\{\partial_{nn}v_{h}\}\kern-3.1298pt\}_{F},[\![\partial_{n}w_{h}]\!]_{F})_{F}.

(64)

Putting everything together and setting
\eta:=u-\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k+2}(u)
, we can express the consistency error as follows:

\displaystyle\langle\delta_{h},w_{h}\rangle:={}

\displaystyle\ell(w_{h})-a_{h}^{\textsc{ipdg}}(\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k+2}(u),w_{h})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}(\nabla^{2}\eta,\nabla^{2}{w}_{h})_{K}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{i}}}\varpi_{F}h_{F}^{-1}([\![\partial_{n}\eta]\!]_{F},[\![\partial_{n}w_{h}]\!]_{F})_{F}

\displaystyle-n_{\sharp}^{\textsc{ipdg}}(\eta,w_{h})-\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{i}}}([\![\partial_{n}\eta]\!]_{F},\{\kern-3.1298pt\{\partial_{nn}w_{h}\}\kern-3.1298pt\}_{F})_{F},

(65)

where we used that
[\![\partial_{n}u]\!]_{F}=0
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{i}}
.
Using the same
\|{\cdot}\|_{\sharp,K}
-norm as for the
C^{0}
-HHO method, and invoking the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, Lemma
4.3
to bound
n_{\sharp}^{\textsc{ipdg}}(\eta,w_{h})
, and the estimate (
39
) to estimate
\partial_{n}\eta
at the mesh interfaces, we infer that

\langle\delta_{h},w_{h}\rangle\leq C\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\|u-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(u)\|^{2}_{\sharp,K}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|w_{h}\|_{V_{\mathcal{T},0}^{k+2}},\qquad\forall w_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{T},0}^{k+2}.

(66)

Finally, proceeding as above for the
C^{0}
-HHO method readily leads to the same error estimates.

4.5
Improved error estimates in weaker norms

To derive error estimates in weaker norms, we assume that the following regularity pickup holds true: There exists a constant
C_{\Omega}
such that for all

,

\Omega

A sufficient condition for (

\Omega
is convex; see
[

. Hence, the setting of Assumption
1.1
is not needed here, and we simply use the

4.5
, that is,
\|v\|^{2}_{\sharp,K}:=\|\nabla^{2}v\|_{K}^{2}+h_{K}\|\partial_{nn}v\|_{\partial K}^{2}
for all

. We also set

Theorem 4.8

(

Lemma 4.9

(New HHO interpolation operator)

The following holds for all

and all

K\in\mathcal{T}

and integration by parts gives

75
), we have

|T_{1}|\leq C\|\widehat{e}_{h}\|_{\widehat{V}_{h}^{k}}\|\zeta_{e}-\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k+2}(\zeta_{e})\|_{\sharp,h}.

Moreover, since
\|\zeta_{e}-\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k+2}(\zeta_{e})\|_{\sharp,h}\leq Ch^{\min(k+1,2)}|\zeta_{e}|_{H^{\min(k+3,4)}(\Omega)}
and
|\zeta_{e}|_{H^{\min(k+3,4)}(\Omega)}\leq C_{\Omega}\|e_{\mathcal{T}}\|_{\Omega}
by our assumption on regularity pickup, we infer that

|T_{1}|\leq Ch^{\min(k+1,2)}\|\widehat{e}_{h}\|_{\widehat{V}_{h}^{k}}\|e_{\mathcal{T}}\|_{\Omega}.

Furthermore, using the definition of
a_{h}
and the identity (
74
) yields

\displaystyle T_{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\Big\{(\nabla^{2}(u-T^{\textsc{hho}}_{K}(u)),\nabla^{2}(\zeta_{e}-T^{\textsc{hho}}_{K}(\zeta_{e})))_{K}-S_{\partial K}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{k}_{K}(u),\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{k}_{K}(\zeta_{e}))\Big\}.

Using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the same arguments as for
T_{1}
gives

|T_{2}|\leq Ch^{\min(k+1,2)}\|u-\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k+2}(u)\|_{\sharp,h}\|e_{\mathcal{T}}\|_{\Omega}.

Finally, we have

|T_{3}|\leq\|\widetilde{h}^{2}(f-\Pi_{h}^{k-2}(f))\|_{\Omega}\|\widetilde{h}^{-2}(\zeta_{e}-\mathcal{C}_{h}^{k+2}(\zeta_{e})))\|_{\Omega}\leq Ch^{\min(k+1,2)}\|\widetilde{h}^{2}(f-\Pi_{h}^{k-2}(f))\|_{\Omega}\|e_{\mathcal{T}}\|_{\Omega}.

Putting everything together gives

\|e_{\mathcal{T}}\|_{\Omega}\leq Ch^{\min(k+1,2)}\Big(\|\widehat{e}_{h}\|_{\widehat{V}_{h}^{k}}+\|u-\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k+2}(u)\|_{\sharp,h}+\|\widetilde{h}^{2}(f-\Pi_{h}^{k-2}(f))\|_{\Omega}\Big).

(76)

(3) Using the triangle inequality and the definition of
e_{\mathcal{T}}
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\|u-R_{K}(\widehat{u}_{K})\|_{K}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle C\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\Big\{\|e_{K}\|_{K}^{2}+\|u_{K}-R_{K}(\widehat{u}_{K})\|_{K}^{2}+\|u-\mathcal{C}_{K}^{k+2}(u))\|_{K}^{2}\Big\}.

The first term on the right-hand side is bounded using (
76
).
The second term is bounded by the Poincaré inequality, (
59
), and the triangle inequality giving

\displaystyle\|u_{K}-R_{K}(\widehat{u}_{K})\|_{K}\leq Ch_{K}^{2}\|\nabla^{2}(u_{K}-R_{K}(\widehat{u}_{K}))\|_{K}

\displaystyle\leq Ch_{K}^{2}S_{\partial K}(\widehat{u}_{K},\widehat{u}_{K})

\displaystyle\leq Ch_{K}^{2}\big(S_{\partial K}(\widehat{e}_{K},\widehat{e}_{K})+\|u-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(u)\|_{\sharp,K}\big).

Finally, observing that
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{1}
leaves
(u-\mathcal{C}_{K}^{k+2}(u))
invariant,
invoking (
38
) and (
71
) gives

\|u-\mathcal{C}_{K}^{k+2}(u)\|_{K}\leq Ch_{K}^{2}\|\nabla^{2}(u-\mathcal{C}_{K}^{k+2}(u))\|_{K}\leq Ch_{K}^{2}\|u-\mathcal{L}_{K}^{k+2}(u)\|_{\sharp,K}.

Putting everything together proves (
68
) since
h^{2}\leq Ch^{min(k+1,2)}
.
∎

Proof of (
69
).

The only salient change with respect to the previous proof is the definition of the adjoint solution which now satisfies
\Delta^{2}\zeta_{e}=\Delta e_{\mathcal{T}}\in H^{-1}(\Omega)
with the appropriate type of BC’s (recall that
e_{\mathcal{T}}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
by definition). Proceeding as above and invoking the regularity pickup assumption (
67
) with
\sigma=-1
then gives

\displaystyle\|\nabla e_{\mathcal{T}}\|_{\Omega}^{2}=\langle\Delta e_{\mathcal{T}},e_{\mathcal{T}}\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch\Big(\|\widehat{e}_{h}\|_{\widehat{V}_{h}^{k}}+\|u-\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k+2}(u)\|_{\sharp,h}+\|\widetilde{h}^{2}(f-\Pi_{h}^{k-2}(f))\|_{\Omega}\Big)|\zeta_{e}|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq Ch\Big(\|\widehat{e}_{h}\|_{\widehat{V}_{h}^{k}}+\|u-\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k+2}(u)\|_{\sharp,h}+\|\widetilde{h}^{2}(f-\Pi_{h}^{k-2}(f))\|_{\Omega}\Big)\|\Delta e_{\mathcal{T}}\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}.

The assertion finally follows from
\|\Delta e_{\mathcal{T}}\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}=\|\nabla e_{\mathcal{T}}\|_{\Omega}
.
∎

Remark 4.10

(Variants and decay rates)

Proceeding as in Remark
4.7
shows that the bounds (
68
) and (
69
) hold true also with the left-hand side replaced by
\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\|u-u_{K}\|_{K}^{2}
and
\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\|\nabla(u-u_{K})\|_{K}^{2}
, respectively. Concerning the right-hand side of (
68
) and (
69
), the first term is controlled by the second term owing to Theorem
4.6
, so that both terms converge at rate
\mathcal{O}(h^{2(k+1)})
. Moreover, the last term converges at rate
\mathcal{O}(h^{2\max(2,k+1)})
(i.e., at the same rate for
k\geq 1
and at one order faster for
k=0
). Taking into account the scaling factor in front of the parenthesis and taking the square root, we infer that the
L^{2}
-error converges at rate
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
for
k=0
, which is suboptimal by one order in
h
, whereas it converges at the optimal rate
\mathcal{O}(h^{k+3})
for all
k\geq 1
. Finally, the
H^{1}
-error converges at the optimal rate
\mathcal{O}(h^{k+2})
for all
k\geq 0
.

[
