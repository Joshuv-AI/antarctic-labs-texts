Multimatroids and rational curves with cyclic action
Emily Clader, Chiara Damiolini, Christopher Eur, Daoji Huang, Shiyue Li

Abstract
. We study the connection between multimatroids and moduli spaces of rational curves with cyclic action. Multimatroids are generalizations of matroids and delta-matroids that naturally arise in topological graph theory. The perspective of moduli of curves provides a tropical framework for studying multimatroids, generalizing the previous connection between type- A A permutohedral varieties (Losev–Manin moduli spaces) and matroids, and the connection between type- B B permutohedral varieties and delta-matroids. Specifically, we equate a combinatorial nef cone of the moduli space with the space of ℝ \mathbb{R} -multimatroids, a generalization of multimatroids, and we introduce the independence polytopal complex of a multimatroid, whose volume is identified with an intersection number on the moduli space. As an application, we give a combinatorial formula for a natural class of intersection numbers on the moduli space by relating to the volumes of independence polytopal complexes of multimatroids.



1.
Introduction

There have been rapid recent developments in the interplay amongst three objects: Coxeter groups, matroids, and the Chow rings of certain moduli spaces of rational curves. In type
A
, the key insight is that the base polytope of a matroid on a set with
n
elements is a type-
A
generalized permutohedron
[
GGMS87
]
, meaning that its normal fan coarsens the type-
A
permutohedral fan
\Sigma_{A_{n-1}}
. This allows one to associate to any matroid an element of the Chow ring of the toric variety
X_{A_{n-1}}
, which was realized by the work of Losev and Manin
[
LM00
]
as a moduli space of rational curves with weighted marked points. The connections between these perspectives have yielded breakthroughs in both matroid theory and geometry
[
AHK18
,
BST
,
BEST23
,
DR22
,
EHL23
,
Ham17
,
LdMRS20
]
.

For type-
B
Coxeter groups, a similar unifying framework was developed in
[
EFLS22
,
ELS23
]
, establishing a connection between the algebraic geometry of the type-
B
permutohedral fan
\Sigma_{B_{n}}
and the combinatorics of delta-matroids, an analogue of matroids first introduced by Bouchet
[
Bou87
]
. Batyrev and Blume showed that the toric variety
X_{B_{n}}
also admits a modular interpretation as a moduli space of rational curves equipped with an involution
[
BB11a
,
BB11b
]
.

At present, it seems that this story does not extend to other Coxeter types. In particular, obstacles were encountered while studying the tautological classes of other Coxeter matroids
[
EFLS22
, Remark 3.6]
and in finding a modular interpretation for toric varieties corresponding to other Coxeter types
[
BB11a
]
.

On the other hand, there is another family of complex reflection groups that generalize type-
A
and type-
B
Coxeter groups, called generalized symmetric groups
S(r,n)
, which depend on parameters
r\geq 2
and
n\geq 1
. In our previous work
[
CDH+23
,
CDLR23
]
, we constructed moduli spaces of curves that correspond to these groups in a precise sense. The result is a smooth projective moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
parameterizing rational stable curves with an order-
r
automorphism and
n
orbits of weighted points, which coincides when
r=2
with Batyrev–Blume’s space. A similar generalization applies in matroid theory: delta-matroids are the
r=2
case of objects known as
r
-matroids, which are a special case of multimatroids
[
Bou87
]
. The connection between the theory of
r
-matroids and the geometry of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
has not yet been studied, and developing this connection is our primary motivation for the current work.

To describe the more general setting in which we work, fix a positive integer
n
, a finite set
E
, and a surjection
\pi\colon E\rightarrow[n]
, where
[n]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The data of
\pi
is equivalent to a partition

E=E_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup E_{n}

by setting
E_{i}\coloneqq\pi^{-1}(i)
for each
i
. A subset
S\subseteq E
is
\pi
-colored
if it contains at most one element of each
E_{i}
. We denote by
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
the poset of
\pi
-colored subsets of
E
, ordered by inclusion. Note that the maximal elements of this poset are the size-
n
subsets of
E
consisting of precisely one element of each
E_{i}
.

In the same way that a matroid on ground set
E
can be defined via a rank function on subsets of
E
, a
multimatroid
is a rank function
\mathbf{rk}:\mathcal{R}_{\pi}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
that satisfies analogous axioms specified in
Definition

4.2
. A key feature of these axioms is that, for any maximal
\pi
-colored subset
T\subseteq E
, the restriction of
\mathbf{rk}
to subsets of
T
(all of which are automatically
\pi
-colored) is a matroid in the usual sense; in this way, a multimatroid can roughly be viewed as a way of patching together a collection of matroids on equal-sized ground sets.

The role played by the permutohedral fan in the theory of matroids is played in the theory of multimatroids by the
\pi
-colored fan

\Sigma^{\pi}
, which we introduce in
Section

2.4
; it is the
n
-dimensional fan in the vector space

N^{\pi}_{\mathbb{R}}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{E_{1}}/\mathbb{R}\mathbf{1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{E_{n}}/\mathbb{R}\mathbf{1}

with a cone

\sigma_{\mathscr{C}}=\operatorname{cone}\{\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{S_{1}},\ldots,\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{S_{k}}\}

for each chain
\mathscr{C}=(S_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq S_{k})
of nonempty
\pi
-colored subsets of
E
, where
\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{S}
denotes the image in
N^{\pi}_{\mathbb{R}}
of
\sum_{i\in S}\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
. For any maximal
\pi
-colored subset
T\subseteq E
, the intersection of
\Sigma^{\pi}
with the subspace
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot\{\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\;|\;i\in T\}\subseteq N^{\pi}_{\mathbb{R}}
is identical to a distinguished orthant (which we call the
affine permutohedral fan
) of the stellahedral fan studied in
[
EHL23
]
. So, analogously to the above perspective on multimatroids, the
\pi
-colored fan can roughly be viewed as a way of patching together a collection of affine permutohedral fans; see Remarks
2.8
and
4.17
.

Toric geometry allows one to give an explicit presentation of the Chow ring of the toric variety
X_{\Sigma^{\pi}}
as a quotient of

\mathbb{Z}[x_{S}\;|\;S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}]

with relations described explicitly in
Proposition

2.10
. In the special case where
|E_{i}|=r
for each
i
(in which case we refer to
\pi
as a
uniform
partition), the
\pi
-colored fan
\Sigma^{\pi}
coincides with the fan
\Sigma^{r}_{n}
studied in
[
CDLR23
]
, and the results of that work show that

(1)

A^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n})\cong A^{*}(\Sigma^{r}_{n}).

The perspective on
\Sigma^{r}_{n}
as a union of affine permutohedral fans can be given a precise geometric interpretation in this setting, as explained in
Remark

6.2
.

One crucial property of
\Sigma^{\pi}
is that it is a balanced tropical fan, and in particular, by
[
AHK18
, Proposition 5.6]
, there exists a well-defined degree map

\int_{\Sigma^{\pi}}\colon A^{n}(\Sigma^{\pi})\to\mathbb{R}

with the defining property that
\int_{\Sigma^{\pi}}\sigma=1
for each maximal cone
\sigma
. Our first main result is a combinatorial formula for these degrees.

Such a formula can be stated in terms of the generators
x_{S}
of
A^{*}(\Sigma^{\pi})
, and in some sense, these are the most geometrically natural generators: they correspond to the rays of
\Sigma^{\pi}
and, in the uniform case, they are identified by the isomorphism (
1
) with the boundary divisors of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
. However, as was already understood in the matroid setting by
[
BES23
]
, the formula becomes much more combinatorially elegant when stated in terms of a different basis. Specifically, for each
S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
, set

h_{S}\coloneqq\sum_{S^{\prime}\cap S\neq\varnothing}x_{S^{\prime}}.

One way to understand the special role played by these alternative generators (analogously to
[
DR22
]
) is that, in the uniform case, they can be viewed under the isomorphism (
1
) as pullbacks of psi-classes under a family of forgetful morphisms from
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
to a simpler moduli space, and the combinatorially rich structure of psi-classes is well-understood; see
Section

6
.

To state the formula for the degree of a monomial in the above generators of
A^{*}(\Sigma^{\pi})
, we define, for any collection
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
of
\pi
-colored subsets (possibly with repetitions), the set

\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})\coloneqq\left\{T\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\max}\;\left|\;\begin{matrix}\text{there exists a bijection }\iota:[n]\rightarrow T\\
\text{with }\iota(i)\in S_{i}\text{ for each }i\end{matrix}\right.\right\}.

In other words,
\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})
consists of the maximal elements of
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
containing precisely one element from each of the sets
S_{i}
. Then we have the following formula.

Theorem A
.

For any collection
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
(with repetitions allowed), we have

\int_{\Sigma^{\pi}}h_{S_{1}}\cdots h_{S_{n}}=|\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})|.

The analogue of this theorem in type
A
follows from
[
Pos09
, Theorem 5.1]
and
[
BES23
]
, while the type-
B
case is proved in
[
EFLS22
, Theorem A(b)]
. At the same time, given the perspective on
h_{S}
as a pullback of a psi-class in the uniform case, this theorem can be viewed as an analogue of the computations of intersection numbers of psi-classes on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
in
[
BELL23
,
Wit91
]
. This suggests that
A
may admit an algebro-geometric proof via the theory of psi-classes; we investigate this direction in Section
6
, but we do not currently know of a complete proof by these methods.

Instead, we prove
A
via the geometry and combinatorics of multimatroids. To do so, we introduce the independence polytopal complex
\textrm{IPC}(\mathbf{M})
of a multimatroid in
Definition

4.13
, generalizing the independence polytope
\textrm{IP}(M)
of a matroid
M
. We prove that the volume of this independence polytopal complex, when suitably normalized (see
Section

4.10
), coincides with the degree of the top power of a divisor on
\Sigma^{\pi}
naturally associated to
\mathbf{M}
. Specifically, for any multimatroid
\mathbf{M}
, let

D_{\mathbf{M}}\coloneqq\sum_{S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}}\mathbf{rk}(S)x_{S}\in A^{1}(\Sigma^{\pi}).

Then we have the following theorem.

Theorem B
.

(see
5.1
)
For any multimatroid
\mathbf{M}
on
(E,\pi)
,

(B)

\int_{\Sigma^{\pi}}\left(D_{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{n}=\operatorname{Vol}(\textrm{IPC}(\mathbf{M})).

Remark 1.1
.

Matroids are a special case of multimatroids, and the analogue of
B
in this setting is the equality, for any matroid
M
, of the degree of the divisor
D_{M}
on the stellahedral fan and the volume of the independence polytope of
M
. In this setting, the theorem can be deduced from a standard result in toric geometry: the volume of the polytope corresponding to a nef divisor
D
on a rational, complete fan
\Sigma
is equal to
\int_{\Sigma}D^{n}
(see, for example
[
Ful93
, page 111]
). The divisor
D_{M}
is known to be nef, and its corresponding polytope is precisely the independence polytope. The multimatroid case, on the other hand, is considerably more subtle.

B
implies
A
via the results of
[
EL23
]
, as we show in
Section

5
. In order to prove
B
, the main idea is to use the work of Nathanson–Ross
[
NR23
]
, which relates the degrees of top powers of divisors on tropical fans to the volumes of associated polytopal complexes known as normal complexes. However, their results generally apply only to divisors satisfying a cubical condition, which
D_{\mathbf{M}}
does not necessarily satisfy. We resolve this obstruction by extending the statement as an equality of functions on the space of multimatroids on
(E,\pi)
.

The key idea is to consider a slight generalization of the notion of multimatroid that we refer to as an
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid
, which consists of a rank function
\mathbf{rk}\colon\mathcal{R}_{\pi}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying the properties listed in
Definition

4.3
. The notions of
D_{\mathbf{M}}
and
\textrm{IPC}(\mathbf{M})
extend to this setting, so the statement of
B
makes sense when
\mathbf{M}
is an
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid, and it is in this setting that we prove the theorem.
The advantage of this extension is two-fold:

(1)

The space
\mathscr{M}
of
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroids on
(E,\pi)
is a connected subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{R}_{\pi}}
, while the space of all multimatroids on
(E,\pi)
is discrete (see
Definition

4.7
).

(2)

For a given
(E,\pi)
, one can always find an
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid
\mathbf{M}
for which
D_{\mathbf{M}}
is cubical (
Lemma

4.23
); this is not true if we only consider multimatroids.

Given this extension, we prove
5.1
(and hence
B
) by showing that both sides of (
B
) are polynomial functions on
\mathscr{M}
that agree—via the work of
[
NR23
]
—on the subset consisting of
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroids
\mathbf{M}
for which
D_{\mathbf{M}}
is cubical. Since this locus is non-empty and open (
Lemma

4.23
), this implies that the two polynomial functions agree on all of
\mathscr{M}
, showing that the theorem holds for every
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid.

1.2.
Future directions

One of the reasons for our interest in
A
is that it provides evidence for the existence of an exceptional isomorphsim from the Chow ring
A(\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n})
to the Grothendieck
K
-ring of vector bundles
K(\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n})
similar to isomorphisms appearing in the study of matroids and delta-matroids
[
BEST23
,
EFLS22
,
EHL23
,
LLPP22
]
. In future work, we plan to study the conjectural existence of such an isomorphism, which we hope will yield a Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch-type formula for computing Euler characteristics of vector bundles on
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
. In the case of matroids and delta-matroids, this isomorphism also relates to an isomorphism with the polytope algebra of generalized (type-
A
or type-
B
) permutohedra, so we hope along the way to relate the
K
-ring of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
to a polytopal complex algebra. Such a connection would also yield a relationship between Euler characteristics of vector bundles on
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
and lattice point counts of certain polytopal complexes of multimatroids, analogous to the case of matroids
[
CF22
]
and toric varieties
[
Ful93
, Section 5.3]
. As applications of this circle of ideas, we hope to apply the present framework to the study of certain polynomials of multimatroids, embedded graphs, and knots
[
EMM13
]
.

2.
The
\pi
-colored fan

Throughout what follows, we fix a nonempty finite set
E
with a partition

E=E_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup E_{n},

or equivalently, a surjective map
\pi\colon E\to[n]
where
\pi^{-1}(i)=E_{i}
. We refer to the partition as
uniform
if
|E_{i}|=|E_{j}|
for all
i,j\in[n]
.

2.1.
Colored sets

Viewing
[n]
as the set of possible colors, and
\pi
as a way to assign a unique color to every element of
E
, we are particularly interested in subsets of
E
that contain at most one element of each color. More precisely, we have the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

A subset
S\subseteq E
is
\pi
-colored
(or just
colored
, if
\pi
is clear from context) if

|S\cap E_{i}|\leq 1

for each
i\in[n]
. We denote by
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
the poset of colored subsets of
E
, ordered by inclusion. Maximal elements of
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
are those that contain exactly one element of each
E_{i}
, and we denote the set of these by
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\max}
. We often wish to exclude the possibility that
S=\varnothing
, so we denote
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}=\mathcal{R}_{\pi}\setminus\{\varnothing\}
.

Remark 2.3
.

One can generalize the notion of colored sets by requiring that the set
S\cap E_{i}
has at most a specified number of elements
c_{i}
, which might depend on
i\in[n]
. Though we do not take up this generalization in this work, it would be interesting to investigate the extent to which the results of this paper generalize to that setting.

2.4.
The
\pi

To define a fan associated to the data of

, we consider the real vector space
\mathbb{R}^{E}
with standard basis

Set

(2)

The

2.9.
The Chow ring of the
\pi
-colored fan

Standard results in toric geometry calculate the Chow ring of
\Sigma^{\pi}
(or, equivalently, of the associated toric variety).

Proposition 2.10
.

The Chow ring of
\Sigma^{\pi}
is

A^{*}(\Sigma^{\pi})=\frac{\mathbb{Z}[x_{S}\;|\;S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}]}{\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{J}},

where
\mathcal{I}
is the ideal of quadratic relations

(3)

\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\langle x_{S}x_{S^{\prime}}\;|\;S\text{ and }S^{\prime}\text{ incomparable}\rangle

(in which “incomparable" means that neither
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
nor
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
) and
\mathcal{J}
is the ideal of linear relations

(4)

\mathcal{J}\coloneqq\left\langle\left.\sum_{S\ni e}x_{S}-\sum_{S\ni e^{\prime}}x_{S}\;\right|\;\text{ distinct }e,e^{\prime}\in E_{i}\text{ for some }i\right\rangle.

In fact, although the divisors
x_{S}
for
S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}
are manifestly generators of the Chow ring
A^{\ast}(\Sigma^{\pi})
, another generating set has more elegant intersection-theoretic properties (and has a natural geometric interpretation explained in
Section

6
). Namely, for each
S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}
, we define

(5)

h_{S}\coloneqq\sum_{S^{\prime}\cap S\neq\varnothing}x_{S^{\prime}}.

These indeed generate
A^{*}(\Sigma^{\pi})
, as a result of the following lemma.

Lemma 2.11
.

For any
S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}
, one has

x_{S}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U,T\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}\\
U\subseteq T\supseteq S\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|T|+|S|+|U|+1}h_{U}.

Proof.

It is helpful to first express
h_{S}
in terms of yet another generating set
f_{S}
, defined by

(6)

f_{T}\coloneqq\sum_{Z\supseteq T}x_{Z}

for each
T\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}
. Then

(7)

h_{S}=\sum_{T\subseteq S}(-1)^{|T|+1}f_{T},

as one sees from the following standard inclusion-exclusion argument. The right-hand side can be expanded as

(8)

\sum_{i\in S}f_{i}-\sum_{i,j\in S}f_{ij}+\sum_{i,j,k\in S}f_{ijk}-\cdots+(-1)^{|S|}f_{S}.

The first term of these sums is equal to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in S\\
T\ni i\end{subarray}}x_{T},

and while each of these
x_{T}
’s appears in the definition of
h_{S}
, those for which
T
contains two distinct elements of
S
are double-counted. The second summand of (
8
) subtracts these, but this double-counts those
x_{T}
for which
T
contains three distinct elements of
S
, and so on.

Since any interval in the poset
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}
is isomorphic to an interval in the Boolean lattice, the Möbius function of this poset is
\mu(T,S)=(-1)^{|T|+|S|}
for any
S\subseteq T
. Thus, the relation (
7
) can be inverted via Möbius inversion (which is effectively inclusion-exclusion again) to yield

(9)

f_{S}=\sum_{T\subseteq S}(-1)^{|T|+1}h_{T},

and by the same token, the relation (
6
) can be inverted to yield

(10)

x_{S}=\sum_{T\supseteq S}(-1)^{|T|+|S|}f_{T}.

Combining these two equations gives

x_{S}=\sum_{T\supseteq S}(-1)^{|T|+|S|}\left(\sum_{U\subseteq T}(-1)^{|U|+1}h_{U}\right),

which is precisely the statement of the lemma.
∎

Remark 2.12
.

It is occasionally convenient to allow that
S=\varnothing
, in which case we set
x_{\varnothing}=h_{\varnothing}=0
. However, we caution the reader that
Lemma

2.11
does not hold for
S=\varnothing
.

Given such a fan, associated to any choice of inner product

For each ray

\Sigma

is unimodular. Denoting by
\{x_{\rho}\;|\;\rho\in\Sigma(1)\}
the generators of

associated to the rays of
\Sigma

for some

P_{\sigma,\ast}(D)\coloneqq\{m\in\sigma\;|\;m\ast u_{\rho}\leq a_{\rho}\ \text{ for all }\rho\in\sigma(1)\}\subseteq N_{\mathbb{R}},

where

denotes the set of rays of

The normal complex of

associated to

is the union of these polytopes as

ranges over all maximal cones. However, in order to ensure that these polytopes meet along faces—and therefore their union forms a polytopal complex—one must impose the following compatibility condition on

Definition 3.2
.

A divisor

\sigma\in\Sigma

\sigma\cap\{m\in N_{\mathbb{R}}\;|\;m\ast u_{\rho}=a_{\rho}\text{ for all }\rho\in\sigma(1)\}\neq\varnothing.

The divisor
D
is
cubical
with respect to

if

\sigma^{\circ}\cap\{m\in N_{\mathbb{R}}\;|\;m\ast u_{\rho}=a_{\rho}\text{ for all }\rho\in\sigma(1)\}\neq\varnothing

for all
\sigma\in\Sigma
, where
\sigma^{\circ}
denotes the interior of
\sigma
.

From here, one can define the normal complex precisely as follows.

Definition 3.3
.

The

\mathrm{C}_{\Sigma,\ast}(D)
of
\Sigma
with respect to

\mathrm{C}_{\Sigma,\ast}(D)\coloneqq\bigcup_{\sigma\in\Sigma(n)}P_{\sigma,\ast}(D).

It has the structure of a polytopal complex when

is pseudo-cubical.

We refer the reader to
Section

3.5
for several examples of normal complexes in the specific context relevant to the current work.

In the case where
\Sigma
is complete, the normal complex

is the classical normal polytope associated to

[

, Corollary, page 111]
) states that, when

is nef, the volume of its normal polytope is equal to the degree
\int_{\Sigma}D^{n}
. The main theorem of
[
NR23
]
asserts that the analogous result is true when
\Sigma
is not necessarily complete, with the normal complex now playing the role of the normal polytope.

To state the result precisely, care must be taken in how the volume is defined. Specifically, for each
\sigma\in\Sigma(n)
, let

N_{\sigma}\coloneqq N\cap\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma),

and let

M_{\sigma}\coloneqq N_{\sigma}^{\vee}=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(N_{\sigma},\mathbb{Z}).

These are lattices in different vector spaces, but the inner product
\ast
allows one to view them both as lattices in the same space
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
. In this way, one can define the volume of any polytope in
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
by declaring

\operatorname{Vol}_{\sigma,\ast}(\text{any $n$-simplex unimodular with respect to $M_{\sigma}$})=1,

where
unimodular
means that the simplex is lattice-equivalent to the
n
-simplex with vertices at
\mathbf{0}
and the standard basis vectors. This definition of volume, in particular, allows us to define the volume of the polytope
P_{\sigma,\ast}(D)\subseteq\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
, and adding these over each maximal cone defines the volume of the normal complex:

(13)

\operatorname{Vol}_{\Sigma,\ast}\left(\mathrm{C}_{\Sigma,\ast}(D)\right)\coloneqq\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma(n)}\operatorname{Vol}_{\sigma,\ast}\left(P_{\sigma,\ast}(D)\right).

The main theorem of
[
NR23
]
, in the generality we will need, is the following.

Theorem 3.4
.

[
NR23
, Theorem 6.3]
Let
\Sigma
be a unimodular, pure
n
-dimensional, balanced tropical fan in
N_{\mathbb{R}}
, let
\ast
be an inner product on
N_{\mathbb{R}}
, and let
D\in A^{1}(\Sigma)_{\mathbb{R}}
be a divisor that is pseudo-cubical with respect to
\ast
. Then

\int_{\Sigma}D^{n}=\operatorname{Vol}_{\Sigma,\ast}\left(\mathrm{C}_{\Sigma,\ast}(D)\right).

There is a non-canonical isomorphism

T_{i}\coloneqq S_{i}\setminus S_{i-1}
, which necessarily consists of consecutive integers. Then, since

\{\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{T_{1}},\ldots\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{T_{k}}\}
is an orthogonal basis of
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(\tau)
, the orthogonal projection of
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
onto
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(\tau)
sends

\vec{x}=c_{1}\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{1}+c_{2}\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{\{1,2\}}+\cdots+c_{n}\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{[n]}\in\sigma

to

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\vec{x}\ast\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{T_{i}}}{|T_{i}|}\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{T_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{|T_{i}|}\sum_{j\in T_{i}}(c_{j}+c_{j+1}+\cdots+c_{n})-\frac{1}{|T_{i+1}|}\sum_{j\in T_{i+1}}(c_{j}+\cdots+c_{n})\right)\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{S_{i}}.

Writing
T_{i}=\{\ell+1,\ell+2,\ldots,\ell+a\}
and
T_{i+1}=\{j+1,j+2,\ldots,j+b\}
for
\ell+a\leq j
, the coefficient of
\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{S_{i}}
in the above summation is

\sum_{x=1}^{a}\dfrac{x}{a}c_{\ell+x}+\sum_{y=\ell+a+1}^{j}c_{y}+\sum_{z=1}^{b}\left(1-\dfrac{z}{b}\right)c_{j+z}

This is manifestly non-negative whenever
c_{j}\geq 0
for all
j
and positive whenever
c_{j}>0
for all
j
. Therefore, the orthogonal projection indeed sends
\sigma
to
\tau
and
\sigma^{\circ}
to
\tau^{\circ}
.

Thus, to check that
D
is (pseudo-)cubical, one must only check the condition of
Definition

3.2
on each maximal cone of
\Sigma^{\pi}
. These are of the form
\sigma_{\mathscr{C}}
for a maximal chain
\mathscr{C}=(S_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq S_{n})
, and the pseudo-cubical condition is that

(21)

\sigma_{\mathscr{C}}\cap\left\{\left.\vec{x}=\sum_{i\in S_{n}}x_{i}\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{S_{n}}\;\right|\;\vec{x}\ast\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{S}=c(S)\;\text{ for all }\;S\in\mathscr{C}\right\}\neq\varnothing,

while the cubical condition is that these intersections all lie within
\sigma_{\mathscr{C}}^{\circ}
. Since the chain
\mathscr{C}
is maximal, for every
i\neq j\in S_{n}
we have
\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\ast\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{j}=\delta_{ij}
. Thus, the
n
conditions
\vec{x}\ast\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{S}=c(S)
for
S\in\mathscr{C}
yield the equations

\sum_{i\in S_{k}}x_{i}=c(S_{k})\qquad\text{ for all }k\in[n]\\

on the coordinates of
\vec{x}
. These equations determine
\vec{x}
, meaning that (
21
) consists of a single point. Namely, if we order the elements of
S_{n}=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}
by the condition that
j_{i}\in T_{i}
for each
i
, then we have

\vec{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(c(S_{i})-c(S_{i-1})\right)\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{i}}.

This
\vec{x}
belongs to

\sigma_{\mathscr{C}}=\operatorname{cone}\left(\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{1}},\;\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{1}}+\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{2}},\;\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{1}}+\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{2}}+\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{3}},\ldots,\;\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{1}}+\cdots+\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{n}}\right)

if and only if
x_{j_{i+1}}\leq x_{j_{i}}
for every
i\in[n-1]
and
x_{j}\geq 0
for every
j\in[n]
, and it belongs to
\sigma_{\mathscr{C}}^{\circ}
if and only if these inequalities are all strict. This completes the proof.

4.
Multimatroids

We begin this section by introducing the concept of an

4.1.
Definition of
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroids

First, we recall the definition of multimatroid from
[
Bou97
]
.

Definition 4.2
.

A
multimatroid

\mathbf{M}
on
(E,\pi)
is a rank function
\mathbf{rk}\colon\mathcal{R}_{\pi}\to\mathbb{N}
satisfying the following conditions:

(BR1)

\mathbf{rk}(\varnothing)=0
;

(BR2)

(
monotonicity and boundedness
) for any
S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
and any
x\in E_{i}
such that
E_{i}\cap S=\varnothing
,

\mathbf{rk}(S)\leq\mathbf{rk}(S\cup\{x\})\leq\mathbf{rk}(S)+1;

(BR3)

(
submodularity
) for any
S,T\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
with
S\cup T\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
,

\mathbf{rk}(S\cup T)+\mathbf{rk}(S\cap T)\leq\mathbf{rk}(S)+\mathbf{rk}(T);

(BR4)

for any
S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
and any pair
\{x,y\}\subseteq E_{i}
such that
E_{i}\cap S=\varnothing
, either
\mathbf{rk}(S\cup\{x\})-\mathbf{rk}(S)=1
or
\mathbf{rk}(S\cup\{y\})-\mathbf{rk}(S)=1
.

If
(E,\pi)
is uniform with
|E_{i}|=r
for each
i
, then
\mathbf{M}
is referred to as an
r
-matroid.

One can always define a multimatroid on
(E,\pi)
by setting
\mathbf{rk}(S)=|S|
for all
S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
; this is referred to as the
Boolean multimatroid
. For a somewhat more motivated class of examples—which, in fact, was one of the original reasons for the definition of multimatroids—one can consider the collection of vertex splitters of the medial graph of an embedded (or, more generally,
4
-regular) graph; this is the main subject of
[
EMM13
]
.

The generalization of the concept of multimatroid that we require allows the rank function to be
\mathbb{R}
-valued and removes the boundedness condition from
(BR2)
for the two reasons stated at the end of the introduction, so our notion might be called a weak
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid in the language of
[
BB19
]
. We also remove condition
(BR4)
, since that condition is not needed for any of our results,
2
2

2

We refer to
[
Bou97
, Remark, page 633]
for a comment about
(BR4)
.
It is worth noting that, although Bouchet originally viewed the structure defined by
(BR1)
–
(BR3)
as “too weak to be interesting,” our results show that one can indeed prove interesting results without imposing condition
(BR4)
.
so our notion might be called a weak poly-
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid in the language of
[
Edm03
]
. To avoid this proliferation of qualifiers, we simply refer to our concept as an
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid, and we define it precisely as follows.

Definition 4.3
.

An
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid

\mathbf{M}
on
(E,\pi)
is a rank function
\mathbf{rk}\colon\mathcal{R}_{\pi}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(R1)

\mathbf{rk}(\varnothing)=0
;

(R2)

(
monotonicity
) for any
S,T\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
with
S\subseteq T
, one has
\mathbf{rk}(S)\leq\mathbf{rk}(T)
;

(R3)

(
submodularity
) for any
S,T\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
with
S\cup T\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
,

\mathbf{rk}(S\cup T)+\mathbf{rk}(S\cap T)\leq\mathbf{rk}(S)+\mathbf{rk}(T).

Example 4.4
.

Let
E=\{{1},\bar{1}\}\sqcup\{{2},\bar{2}\}
. Then one can define an
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid on
E
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{rk}(\varnothing)=0;

\displaystyle\mathbf{rk}(\{{1}\})=\mathbf{rk}(\{\bar{1}\})=\mathbf{rk}(\{{2}\})=\mathbf{rk}(\{\bar{2}\})=\mathbf{rk}(\{\bar{1},{2}\})=\mathbf{rk}(\{{1},\bar{2}\})=1;

\displaystyle\mathbf{rk}(\{{1},{2}\})=\mathbf{rk}(\{\bar{1},\bar{2}\})=2.

In fact, this example is a multimatroid, as one can check from
Definition

4.2
.

Example 4.5
.

Let
E=\{1,\bar{1}\}\sqcup\{2,\bar{2}\}
. Then one can define an
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid on
E
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{rk}(\varnothing)=0;

\displaystyle\mathbf{rk}(1)=\mathbf{rk}(\bar{1})=5;

\displaystyle\mathbf{rk}(2)=\mathbf{rk}(\bar{2})=4;

\displaystyle\mathbf{rk}(\{1,2\})=\mathbf{rk}(\{\bar{1},2\})=\mathbf{rk}(\{1,\bar{2}\})=\mathbf{rk}(\{\bar{1},\bar{2}\})=6.

This is not a multimatroid because it does not satisfy the boundedness condition in
(BR2)
.

Remark 4.6
.

One advantage of the additional constraints in
Definition

4.2
is that they allow one to alternatively describe multimatroids via their associated collection of independent sets, bases, or circuits, in the same way that matroids are often described.
It would be interesting to determine whether
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroids can analogously be described via their independent sets, bases, or circuits, but we currently do not know of such a description.

By identifying each
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid with its rank function, one can define a topological space of
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroids as follows.

Definition 4.7
.

The
space of
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroids on
(E,\pi)
, denoted
\mathscr{M}=\mathscr{M}_{(E,\pi)}
, is the subset of the set of functions
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying the conditions of
Definition

4.3
. Identifying the function space with
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{R}_{\pi}}
and giving it the usual Euclidean topology, we have an embedding

in which
\mathscr{M}
is a closed, full-dimensional, connected subspace. To see this, note that axiom
(R1)
of

4.3
ensures that
\mathscr{M}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}}
. Inequalities

and
(R3)
describe

as an intersection of closed half-spaces, so

is closed and convex (in particular, connected). The fact that it is full-dimensional follows from
Lemma

As mentioned in the introduction, a key feature of multimatroids is that their restriction to any colored set yields a matroid, so a multimatroid can in some sense be viewed as a way of patching together a collection of ordinary matroids. More precisely, if
\mathbf{rk}
defines a multimatroid and

\mathcal{R}_{\pi}

A similar story holds for
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroids, but the restrictions are the following weakening of matroids; these are essentially the same as polymatroids
[
Edm03
]
, but with real-valued rather than integer-valued rank function.

Definition 4.8
.

An
\mathbb{R}
-matroid
is a function
\textrm{rk}\colon\mathcal{P}(S)\to\mathbb{R}
on the power set

(M1)

(M2)

\textrm{rk}(X)\leq\textrm{rk}(Y)

(M3)

\textrm{rk}(X\cup Y)+\textrm{rk}(X\cap Y)\leq\textrm{rk}(X)+\textrm{rk}(Y)

Comparing the conditions
(R1)

(R2)

(R3)

Definition 4.9

For an
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid
\mathbf{M}

\mathbf{M}

\mathbb{R}
-matroid

4.10.
Independence polytopal complexes

We now introduce the analogue for multimatroids and
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroids of the independence polytope of a matroid. Carrying forth the philosophy that a multimatroid
\mathbf{M}
is a way of patching together a collection of matroids
\mathbf{M}(S)

\mathbf{M}
by patching together the independence polytopes of the matroids
\mathbf{M}(S)
. This same idea holds for
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroids, but in this case the components
\mathbf{M}(S)
are only
\mathbb{R}
-matroids, so care must be taken in how their independence polytopes are defined.

Definition 4.11
.

The

\mathbb{R}
-matroid

is the polytope

(22)

\textrm{IP}(\mathbf{M}(S))\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{S}_{\geq 0}\;\left|\;\sum_{i\in X}x_{i}\leq\mathbf{rk}(X)\text{ for all }X\subseteq S\right.\right\}\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{S}_{\geq 0}\subseteq N^{\pi}_{\mathbb{R}}.

Remark 4.12
.

If
\mathbf{M}(S)
is an honest matroid and not merely an
\mathbb{R}
-matroid, then one defines the independent sets of
\mathbf{M}(S)
as those subsets

such that
\mathbf{rk}(I)=|I|
. In this situation,
[

show that the independence polytope of
\mathbf{M}(S)
is given by

\textrm{IP}(\mathbf{M}(S))=\operatorname{conv}\{\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{I}\;|\;I\subseteq S\text{ an independent set}\},

which explains its name. However, when
\mathbf{M}(S)
is
\mathbb{R}
-valued, there is not, to our knowledge, a good notion of independent sets (as discussed in
Remark

4.6

\textrm{IP}(\mathbf{M}(S))
as a convex hull.

Gluing the polytopes

\textrm{IPC}(\mathbf{M})\coloneqq\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}}\textrm{IP}(\mathbf{M}(S)).

4.19.
Divisor associated to a multimatroid

Having fully defined the objects appearing on the right-hand side of
B
, we now turn to the left-hand side, describing how an
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid
\mathbf{M}
defines a divisor
D_{\mathbf{M}}
on
\Sigma^{\pi}
. In particular, using the notation of
Section

2.4
, we set

(25)

D_{\mathbf{M}}\coloneqq\sum_{S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}}\mathbf{rk}(S)x_{S}.

We say that an
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid is
pseudo-cubical
if
D_{\mathbf{M}}
is a pseudo-cubical divisor on
\Sigma^{\pi}
under the inner product described in
Section

3.5
. By
Lemma

3.10
, this is equivalent to the condition that, for every maximal chain
\mathscr{C}=(S_{1}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq S_{n})
of nonempty colored sets, we have

(26)

2\mathbf{rk}(S_{i})\geq\mathbf{rk}(S_{i-1})+\mathbf{rk}(S_{i+1})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathbf{rk}(S_{j})\geq\mathbf{rk}(S_{j-1})

for each
i\in[n-1]
and
j\in[n]
, where
\mathbf{rk}(S_{0})=0
.
Similarly, an
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid
\mathbf{M}
is
cubical
if
D_{\mathbf{M}}
is a cubical divisor, which is equivalent to the condition that the inequalities (
26
) are all strict.

It is straightforward to check from the conditions (
26
) that the Boolean multimatroid is pseudo-cubical for any
(E,\pi)
. The following examples illustrate the behavior of
D_{\mathbf{M}}
in some somewhat more interesting cases.

Example 4.20
.

For
\mathbf{M}
as in
Example

4.4
, we have

D_{\mathbf{M}}=2\left(x_{\{1,2\}}+x_{\{\bar{1},\bar{2}\}}\right)+1\left(x_{\{1,\bar{2}\}}+x_{\{\bar{1},2\}}+x_{\{1\}}+x_{\{\bar{1}\}}+x_{\{2\}}+x_{\{\bar{2}\}}\right).

This is a pseudo-cubical but not cubical multimatroid, since, for instance, the chain

\mathscr{C}=(\{1\}\subseteq\{1,2\})

yields the equality
2\cdot 1=0+2
.

Example 4.21
.

For
\mathbf{M}
as in
Example

4.5
, we have

D_{\mathbf{M}}=4\left(x_{\{1\}}+x_{\{\bar{1}\}}\right)+5\left(x_{\{2\}}+x_{\{\bar{2}\}}\right)+6\left(x_{\{1,2\}}+x_{\{\bar{1},2\}}+x_{\{1,\bar{2}\}}+x_{\{\bar{1},\bar{2}\}}\right).

This is a cubical
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid; for instance, the chain

\mathscr{C}=(\{1\}\subseteq\{1,2\})

yields the inequality
2\cdot 4>0+6
, and the chain

\mathscr{C}=(\{2\}\subseteq\{1,2\})

yields the inequality
2\cdot 5>0+6
.

Interestingly, the pseudo-cubical condition on
D_{\mathbf{M}}
in fact implies the
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid axioms. This observation is useful in what follows, so we prove it in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.22
.

For any function
\mathbf{rk}\colon\mathcal{R}_{\pi}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbf{rk}(\varnothing)=0
, if the divisor
\sum_{S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}}\mathbf{rk}(S)x_{S}
is pseudo-cubical, then
\mathbf{rk}
defines an
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid.

Proof.

The second inequality of (
26
) clearly implies the monotonicity axiom
(R2)
. We now show that the submodularity axiom
(R3)
is implied by the first inequality of (
26
).

For every
I\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
, and for every
x,y\in E\setminus I
such that
I\cup\{x,y\}\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
, applying (
26
) gives

2\mathbf{rk}(I\cup\{x\})\geq\mathbf{rk}(I)+\mathbf{rk}(I\cup\{x,y\})\qquad\text{and}\qquad 2\mathbf{rk}(I\cup\{y\})\geq\mathbf{rk}(I)+\mathbf{rk}(I\cup\{x,y\}),

so we obtain

(27)

\mathbf{rk}(I\cup\{x\})+\mathbf{rk}(I\cup\{y\})\geq\mathbf{rk}(I)+\mathbf{rk}(I\cup\{x,y\}).

This shows that axiom
(R3)
holds when
|S\cup T|=|S|+1=|T|+1
, by setting
I=S\cap T
.
It is known that the validity of
(R3)
in such cases implies its validity in full generality
[
Sch03
, Theorem 44.1]
. For the sake of self-containment, we include a proof below.

Again setting
I=S\cap T
, denote
S=I\cup\{s_{1},\dots,s_{k}\}
and
T=I\cup\{t_{1},\dots,t_{\ell}\}
. Then, for every
0\leq a\leq k
and
0\leq b\leq\ell
, define the set

X_{a,b}\coloneqq I\cup\{s_{1},\dots,s_{a},t_{1},\dots,t_{b}\}\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi},

where
X_{k,0}\coloneqq S
,
X_{0,\ell}\coloneqq T
, and
X_{0,0}\coloneqq I
. Then (
27
) yields

\mathbf{rk}(X_{a,b-1})+\mathbf{rk}(X_{a-1,b})\geq\mathbf{rk}(X_{a-1,b-1})+\mathbf{rk}(X_{a,b})

for all
a\in[k]
and
b\in[\ell]
. Taking the sum over all such
a
and
b
, we obtain the inequality

\mathbf{rk}(S)+\mathbf{rk}(T)=\mathbf{rk}(X_{k,0})+\mathbf{rk}(X_{0,\ell})\geq\mathbf{rk}(X_{0,0})+\mathbf{rk}(X_{k,\ell})=\mathbf{rk}(S\cap T)+\mathbf{rk}(S\cup T),

which shows that
(R3)
holds and thus concludes the proof.
∎

Now, in the topological space
\mathscr{M}
of
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroids defined in
Definition

4.7
, define the subset

\displaystyle\mathscr{M}^{\scalebox{0.7}{\mbox{{\char 28\relax}}}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{\mathbf{M}\in\mathscr{M}\;|\;\mathbf{M}\text{ is cubical}\right\}.

Then we have the following key properties.

Lemma 4.23
.

The space
\mathscr{M}^{\scalebox{0.7}{\mbox{{\char 28\relax}}}}
is a nonempty, open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}}
. In particular,
\mathscr{M}
has nonempty interior and is therefore a full-dimensional subset of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}}
.

Proof.

The conditions (
26
) with strict inequalities manifestly define
\mathscr{M}^{\scalebox{0.7}{\mbox{{\char 28\relax}}}}
as an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}}
. We are left to show that
\mathscr{M}^{\scalebox{0.7}{\mbox{{\char 28\relax}}}}
is nonempty. To do so, we define a specific cubical
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid
\mathbf{M}
by setting

(28)

{\mathbf{rk}}(S)\coloneqq\binom{n+1}{2}-\binom{n+1-|S|}{2}

for each
S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
. It is straightforward to see that the inequalities (
26
) hold and therefore
\mathbf{M}
is cubical. By
Lemma

4.22
, it follows that
\mathbf{M}
is an
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid, so
\mathbf{M}\in\mathscr{M}^{\scalebox{0.7}{\mbox{{\char 28\relax}}}}
.
∎

Remark 4.24
.

The fact that (
28
) defines an
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid and not an ordinary multimatroid is one of the key reasons why we require the generalization from multimatroids to
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroids in this work. In fact, if
n\geq 3
, then no
(E,\pi)
can admit a cubical multimatroid. To see this, let
\mathbf{M}
be a multimatroid on
(E,\pi)
with
n\geq 3
, so that, for every maximal chain
\mathscr{C}=(S_{1}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq S_{n})
in
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}
, one has
\mathbf{rk}(S_{i})\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{rk}(S_{i})\leq\mathbf{rk}(S_{i+1})\leq\mathbf{rk}(S_{i})+1
for every
i\in[n-1]
. The inequalities (
26
) show that
\mathbf{M}
can only be cubical if

\text{(i)}\quad 2\mathbf{rk}(S_{1})>\mathbf{rk}(S_{2})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\text{(ii)}\quad 2\mathbf{rk}(S_{2})>\mathbf{rk}(S_{1})+\mathbf{rk}(S_{3}).

Condition (i) implies that
\mathbf{rk}(S_{1})\neq 0
and so necessarily
\mathbf{rk}(S_{1})=1
. This implies that
\mathbf{rk}(S_{2})=1
and so
\mathbf{rk}(S_{3})\in\{1,2\}
. None of these options is compatible with condition (ii), and so
\mathbf{M}
cannot be cubical.

At this point, we have all the ingredients necessary to prove
B
, so we turn in the next section to its proof and then, in turn, to the deduction of
A
.

Proof of Theorem

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a pseudo-cubical
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid. Then

(30)

\mathrm{C}_{\Sigma^{\pi},\ast}(D_{\mathbf{M}})=\textrm{IPC}(\mathbf{M}),

and furthermore, their volumes agree in the sense that

(31)

\operatorname{Vol}_{\pi}(\mathrm{C}_{\Sigma^{\pi},\ast}(D_{\mathbf{M}}))=\operatorname{Vol}(\textrm{IPC}(\mathbf{M})),

where the left-hand side is defined by (

31
) with
Corollary

3.12
, it follows that
5.1
holds when

Proof.

As noted in (
15
), we have

\mathrm{C}_{\Sigma^{\pi},\ast}(D_{\mathbf{M}})=\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\max}}\bigcup_{\mathscr{C}\in\mathsf{MaxChain}(T)}P_{\sigma_{\mathscr{C},\ast}}(D_{\mathbf{M}}),

where
\mathsf{MaxChain}(T)
again denotes the set of maximal chains
\mathscr{C}=(S_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq S_{n})
of colored sets with

. Expanding the definition of

P_{\sigma_{\mathscr{C}},\ast}(D_{\mathbf{M}})=\left\{\vec{x}\in\sigma_{\mathscr{C}}\;|\;\vec{x}\ast\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{S}\leq\mathbf{rk}(S)\;\text{ for all }S\in\mathscr{C}\right\}.

below. Assuming it, and writing
\vec{x}\in\sigma_{\mathscr{C}}
in terms of the orthonormal basis

for

, we find

(32)

\bigcup_{\mathscr{C}\in\mathsf{MaxChain}(T)}P_{\sigma_{\mathscr{C}},\ast}(D_{\mathbf{M}})=\left\{\sum_{i\in T}x_{i}\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{T}_{\geq 0}\;\left|\;\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}\leq\mathbf{rk}(S)\;\text{ for all }S\subseteq T\right.\right\}.

, so taking the union over

∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a pseudo-cubical
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid. Then

P_{\sigma_{\mathscr{C}},\ast}(D_{\mathbf{M}})=\{\vec{x}\in\sigma_{\mathscr{C}}\;|\;\vec{x}\ast\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{S}\leq\mathbf{rk}(S)\;\text{ for all }S\subseteq T\}

for any
T\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\max}
and any
\mathscr{C}\in\mathsf{MaxChain}(T)
.

Proof.

Fix
T\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\max}
and
\mathscr{C}\in\mathsf{MaxChain}(T)
. Up to relabeling, we can write
T=[n]
and

\mathscr{C}=([1]\subsetneq[2]\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq[n]).

Now, let
\vec{x}\in P_{\sigma_{\mathscr{C}},\ast}(D_{\mathbf{M}})
. By definition, this means that
\vec{x}\in\sigma_{\mathscr{C}}=\operatorname{cone}(\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{[1]},\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{[2]}\ldots,\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{[n]})
, so

\vec{x}=a_{1}\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{[1]}+\cdots+a_{n}\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{[n]}=(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n})\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{1}+(a_{2}+\cdots+a_{n})\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{2}+\cdots+a_{n}\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{n}

for some
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\geq 0
. Equivalently,
\vec{x}=c_{1}\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{1}+\cdots+c_{n}\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{n}
for some
c_{1}\geq c_{2}\geq\cdots\geq c_{n}\geq 0
. The defining inequalities of
P_{\sigma_{\mathscr{C}},\ast}(D_{\mathbf{M}})
then imply that

(33)

\sum_{i\in S}c_{i}\leq\mathbf{rk}(S)

whenever
S=[j]
for some
j\in[n]
, and what we must prove is that the same is true for all
S\subseteq[n]
.

To prove this, we define a pair of functions

f:\mathcal{P}([n])\rightarrow\mathcal{P}([n])\;\;\text{ and }\;\;g:\mathcal{P}([n])\rightarrow\mathcal{P}([n])

on the power set
\mathcal{P}([n])
by setting
f([j])=g([j])=[j]
for each
j\in[n]
, and setting

f(S)=S\cup\{a_{S}\}\;\;\text{ and }\;\;g(S)=S\setminus\{b_{S}\}

for any
S
that is not of this form, where
a_{S}
and
b_{S}
are defined by

\displaystyle a_{S}\coloneqq\min\{i\in[n]\;|\;i\notin S\},

\displaystyle b_{S}\coloneqq\min\{i\in S\;|\;i>a_{S}\}.

We first claim that, if (
33
) holds for
f(S)
and
g(S)
, then it also holds for
S
. This is trivially true when
S=[j]
for some
j
, so we prove it in the case where
S
is not of this form. The assumption that (
33
) holds for
f(S)
means that
c_{a_{S}}+\sum_{i\in S}c_{i}\leq\mathbf{rk}(f(S))
. Since
a_{S}<b_{S}
and therefore
c_{a_{S}}\geq c_{b_{S}}
, it follows that

(34)

c_{b_{S}}+\sum_{i\in S}c_{i}\leq\mathbf{rk}(f(S)).

On the other hand, the assumption that (
33
) holds for
g(S)
means that

(35)

-c_{b_{S}}+\sum_{i\in S}c_{i}\leq\mathbf{rk}(g(S)).

Adding (
34
) and (
35
) yields

2\sum_{i\in S}c_{i}\leq\mathbf{rk}(f(S))+\mathbf{rk}(g(S))\leq 2\mathbf{rk}(S),

where the second inequality follows by applying the pseudo-cubicality condition (
26
) to any maximal chain containing
g(S)\subsetneq S\subsetneq f(S)
. This shows that (
33
) holds for
S
, as claimed.

From here, we prove that (
33
) holds for all
S\subseteq[n]
by descending induction, first on
a_{S}
and then on
b_{S}
. Note that it suffices to prove the claim when
S
is not of the form
[j]
for any
j\in[n]
, since it holds by assumption when
S
is of this form. The base case of the first induction is the case
a_{S}=n-1
. Then
f(S)=[n]
and
g(S)=[n-2]
, so (
33
) holds for both of these and therefore holds for
S
. Suppose, then, that (
33
) holds for all
S^{\prime}\subseteq[n]
with
a_{S^{\prime}}>k
, and let
S
be such that
a_{S}=k
. We now introduce the second induction, a descending induction on
b_{S}
.

The base case is
b_{S}=n
. In this case,
f(S)
has
a_{f(S)}>k
, so (
33
) holds for
f(S)
by the first inductive hypothesis, whereas
g(S)=[k-1]
, so (
33
) holds for
g(S)
by assumption. Therefore, (
33
) holds for
S
, completing the base case. Finally, suppose that (
33
) holds for all
S^{\prime\prime}\subseteq[n]
with
b_{S^{\prime\prime}}>\ell
, and let
S
be such that
b_{S}=\ell
. Then
f(S)
has
a_{f(S)}>k
and
g(S)
has
b_{g(S)}>\ell
, so (
33
) holds for both of these by the two inductive hypotheses. This implies that (
33
) holds for
S
, completing the proof.
∎

, one has

(37)

\textrm{IP}(\mathbf{M}(T))=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}}a_{S}\Delta^{T}_{S\cap T}.

This follows from the computation of the independence polytope of a matroid as a Minkowski sum of simplices given in
[
ABD10
]
. Before stating their result, we require some notation. Note, either using
Lemma

2.11
or through
[
ABD10
]
, that the free module
\mathbb{Z}[\{x_{S}\}_{S\subseteq T}]
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}[\{h_{S}^{T}\}_{S\subseteq T}]
, where

h_{S}^{T}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S^{\prime}\subseteq T\\
S\cap S^{\prime}\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}x_{S^{\prime}}.

Thus, one can write

D_{\mathbf{M}(T)}\coloneqq\sum_{S\subseteq T}\mathbf{rk}(S)x_{S}=\sum_{S\subseteq T}a_{S}^{T}h_{S}^{T}

for uniquely defined
a_{S}^{T}\in\mathbb{Z}
. In fact,
[
ABD10
, Proposition 4.3]
shows that

(38)

\textrm{IP}(\mathbf{M}(T))=\sum_{S\subseteq T}a_{S}^{T}\Delta^{T}_{S},

so the content of the proof of (
37
) is relating the coefficients
a_{S}^{T}
to the coefficients
a_{S}
. We can unpack this relationship using the commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Z}[\{h_{S}\}_{S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}}]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Z}[\{h_{S}^{T}\}_{S\subseteq T}]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Z}[\{x_{S}\}_{S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}}]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Z}[\{x_{S}\}_{S\subseteq T}],}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cong}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cong}

where the upper horizontal arrow is
h_{S}\mapsto h^{T}_{S\cap T}
and the lower horizontal arrow is

x_{S}\mapsto\begin{cases}x_{S}&\text{ if }S\subseteq T\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In particular, applying the commutativity of this diagram to
\sum_{S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}}a_{S}h_{S}
shows that

\sum_{S\subseteq T}\mathbf{rk}(S)x_{S}=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}}a_{S}h^{T}_{S\cap T}.

Re-writing the right-hand side slightly gives

\sum_{S\subseteq T}\mathbf{rk}(S)x_{S}=\sum_{S\subseteq T}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}\\
S^{\prime}\cap T=S\end{subarray}}a_{S^{\prime}}\right)h^{T}_{S},

from which we deduce

a_{S}^{T}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}\\
S^{\prime}\cap T=S\end{subarray}}a_{S^{\prime}}.

Combining this with (
38
) shows (
37
) and thus completes the proof.
∎

In light of
Proposition

5.9
, we define the polynomial

V(\vec{a})\coloneqq\sum_{T\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\max}}\operatorname{Vol}_{T}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}}a_{S}\Delta^{T}_{S\cap T}\right)

in the parameters
\vec{a}
. The equality of
5.1
, when translated into these parameters, now becomes the following.

Theorem 5.12
.

5.1
is equivalent to the equality of polynomials
I(\vec{a})=V(\vec{a})
.

Proof.

As above, fix an
\mathbb{R}
-multimatroid
\mathbf{M}
and write
D_{\mathbf{M}}=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}}a_{S}h_{S}
. Then the left-hand side of (
29
) is equal to
I(\vec{a})
by definition, and
Proposition

5.9
shows that the right-hand side of (
29
) is equal to
V(\vec{a})
. In particular,

is equivalent to the statement that

on
\mathscr{M}
, the subset of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}}
consisting of values of the parameters

that define an

contains an open subset of

∎

5.13.
Proof of Theorem
A

Having re-expressed
5.1
as an equality of polynomials in this way, it follows that the coefficient of any monomial in
I(\vec{a})
agrees with the corresponding coefficient in
V(\vec{a})
. The coefficient of a monomial in
I(\vec{a})
is, by definition, an integral of a monomial in the
h_{S}
’s.
On the other hand, the coefficient of the corresponding monomial in
V(\vec{a})
is the mixed volume of certain simplices, for which a previously-known formula is recorded as the following lemma.

Lemma 5.14
.

For a subset
S\subseteq[n]
, define
\Delta_{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
as the convex hull of
\{\mathbf{0}\}\cup\{\mathbf{e}_{i}\;|\;i\in S\}
. Then, for subsets
S_{1},\dotsc,S_{n}
of
[n]
(with repetitions allowed), the mixed volume
MV
of the corresponding simplices is given by

\text{MV}(\Delta_{S_{1}},\dotsc,\Delta_{S_{n}})=\begin{cases}1&\text{there exists a bijection $\iota:[n]\to[n]$ such that $\iota(i)\in S_{i}$ for each $i$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Standard results in toric geometry translate mixed volumes to intersection numbers of nef divisors
[
Ful93
, Chapter 5.4]
. Applying this to polystellahedral fans, as done in
[
EL23
, Section 2.2]
, one finds that the lemma is a restatement of
[
EL23
, Theorem 1.3 and Lemma 5.2]
. One can also deduce the lemma from
[
Pos09
, Theorem 5.1]
or from
[
EFLS22
, Theorem A(b)]
.
∎

We are now ready to prove
A
, whose statement we recall for convenience.

Theorem A
.

For any collection
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
(with repetitions allowed), we have

(A)

\int_{\Sigma^{\pi}}h_{S_{1}}\cdots h_{S_{n}}=|\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})|,

where

\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})\coloneqq\left\{T\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\max}\;\left|\;\begin{matrix}\text{there exists a bijection }\iota:[n]\rightarrow T\\
\text{with }\iota(i)\in S_{i}\text{ for each }i\end{matrix}\right.\right\}.

Proof.

The left-hand side of (
A
) is the coefficient of the monomial
a_{S_{1}}\cdots a_{S_{n}}
in the polynomial
I(\vec{a})
. The coefficient of the same monomial in
V(\vec{a})
is, by definition, the sum of mixed volumes

\sum_{T\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\max}}\text{MV}\left(\Delta^{T}_{S_{1}\cap T},\ldots,\Delta^{T}_{S_{n}\cap T}\right).

For each
T\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\max}
,
Lemma

5.14
states that

\text{MV}\left(\Delta^{T}_{S_{1}\cap T},\ldots,\Delta^{T}_{S_{n}\cap T}\right)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }T\in\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})\\
0&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

so the coefficient of
a_{S_{1}}\cdots a_{S_{n}}
in
V(\vec{a})
is precisely the right-hand side of (
A
). Thus, the two sides agree by
5.1
and
5.12
.
∎

Remark 6.2

We briefly digress to notice that, for any

Returning to the definition of

In the case of

Lemma 6.3
.

Under the isomorphism
A^{*}(\Sigma^{r}_{n})\cong A^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n})
given by

, one has

h_{S}=F_{S}^{*}(\psi_{y}).

Proof.

Similarly to the boundary divisors on
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
, there are boundary divisors

for each nonempty subset

. Namely,

is the class of closure of the locus of curves consisting of two components, one containing the marked points

and

with

, and the other containing the remaining marked points.

We claim, first, that

\psi_{y}=\sum_{\varnothing\neq T\subseteq S}D^{1}_{T}.

The proof is by induction on
|S|
. When

, the moduli space

\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{S^{\prime}}

Remark 6.4

One might wonder why we do not also consider classes

This lemma already allows us to give a geometric proof of

when
n=1
, so we have

\int_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{1}}h_{S}=\int_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{1}}x_{S},

which equals
1
because
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{1}\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
and the boundary divisor
x_{S}
is the class of a single point.

Now, suppose that the lemma holds on
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n-1}
. The hypothesis that the chain is maximal implies that
S_{1}=\{k^{j}\}
for some
k^{j}\in E
, and we claim that, if
S_{i}^{\prime}\coloneqq S_{i}\setminus\{k^{j}\}
for each
i\in[n]
, then

(43)

\int_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}}h_{S_{1}}\cdots h_{S_{n}}=\int_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n-1}}h_{S_{2}^{\prime}}\cdots h_{S_{n}^{\prime}}.

If we can prove this, then the proposition will follow by the induction hypothesis.

To prove (
43
), we first note that, whenever
S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
is such that
k\in\underline{S}
,
Lemma

6.6
implies

h_{S_{1}}x_{S}=h_{\{k^{j}\}}\cdot x_{S}=0.

It follows that, in the product
h_{S_{1}}h_{S_{2}}\cdots h_{S_{n}}
, one can replace
h_{S_{i}}
for each
i\in\{2,\ldots,n\}
by

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\cap S_{i}\neq\varnothing\\
k\notin\underline{S}\end{subarray}}x_{S}=\phi^{*}\left(h_{S_{i}^{\prime}}\right),

where
\phi\colon\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n-1}
is the forgetful map forgetting the light orbit indexed by
k
. That is,

h_{S_{1}}h_{S_{2}}\cdots h_{S_{n}}=h_{S_{1}}\phi^{*}\big(h_{S_{2}^{\prime}}\cdots h_{S_{n}^{\prime}}\big).

It therefore follows from the projection formula that

\int_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}}h_{S_{1}}\cdots h_{S_{n}}=\int_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n-1}}\phi_{*}(h_{S_{1}})h_{S_{2}^{\prime}}\cdots h_{S_{n}^{\prime}},

so to prove (
43
), it suffices to prove that
\phi_{*}(h_{S_{1}})=1
. To see this, express

h_{S_{1}}=x_{S_{1}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\supseteq S_{1}\\
|S|\geq 2\end{subarray}}x_{S}.

Then

\phi_{*}(h_{S_{1}})=(\phi|_{X_{S_{1}}})_{*}\left(\mathbf{1}_{X_{S_{1}}}\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\supseteq S_{1}\\
|S|\geq 2\end{subarray}}(\phi|_{X_{S}})_{*}\left(\mathbf{1}_{X_{T}}\right),

where we recall that
X_{S}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
is the subvariety such that
[X_{S}]=x_{S}\in A^{1}(\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n})
. Geometrically, one sees that
\phi|_{X_{S_{1}}}\colon X_{S_{1}}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n-1}
is an isomorphism, so
(\phi|_{X_{S_{1}}})_{*}\left(\mathbf{1}_{X_{S_{1}}}\right)=1
(see, for example,
[
CDH+23
, Proposition 5.5]
). On the other hand, for each
S
appearing in the summation above,
\phi|_{X_{S}}\colon X_{S}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n-1}
reduces the dimension, so
(\pi|_{X_{S}})_{*}\left(\mathbf{1}_{X_{S}}\right)=0
. Thus, we indeed have
\phi_{*}\left(h_{S_{1}}\right)=1
, so the proposition is proved.
∎

Remark 6.8
.

In fact, the proof of
Proposition

6.7
holds as long as the underlying sets
are strictly nested (that is,
\underline{S}_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\underline{S}_{n}
), without necessarily assuming the stronger condition that
{S}_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq{S}_{n}
.

Remark 6.9
.

The equation (
43
) can also be used to prove that
A
holds in the case where
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
are pairwise disjoint. The main idea of the argument is to use the relations in
A^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n})
to argue that

\int_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}}h_{S_{1}}\cdots h_{S_{n-1}}h_{S_{n}}=\sum_{a\in S_{n}}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}}h_{S_{1}}\cdots h_{S_{n-1}}h_{\{a\}}

by applying the pairwise disjoint hypothesis, and then to apply (
43
) to rewrite this as

\sum_{a\in S_{n}}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n-1}}h_{S_{1}\setminus\{a\}}\cdots h_{S_{n}\setminus\{a\}}.

This sets up an induction on
n
from which
A
immediately follows.

Building off of
Proposition

6.7
, one can prove
A
for collections
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
that are nested but not strictly nested. The key ingredient in the proof of this generalization is the following geometric lemma.

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
, and choose any element
\star\in S
. Then

(h_{S})^{2}=\begin{cases}h_{S}h_{S\setminus\{\star\}}&\text{ if }|S|>1,\\
0&\text{ if }|S|=1\end{cases}.

Proof.

When
|S|=1
, the result follows from
Lemma

6.6
. Now, suppose that
|S|>1
. Choose any element
\star\in S
, and set

S^{\prime}\coloneqq S\setminus\{\star\}.

By
Lemma

6.3
, we have
h_{S}=F_{S}^{*}(\psi_{y})
and
h_{S^{\prime}}=F_{S^{\prime}}^{*}(\psi_{y})
.
Furthermore, if
f:\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{S}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{S^{\prime}}
is the map forgetting the light marked point
z_{\star}
, we have
F_{S^{\prime}}=f\circ F_{S}
. Applying (
41
) then shows

\displaystyle h_{S}h_{S^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=F_{S}^{*}(\psi_{y})F_{S}^{*}(f^{*}(\psi_{y}))

\displaystyle=F_{S}^{*}(\psi_{y})F_{S}^{*}(\psi_{y}-D^{1}_{\{\star\}})

\displaystyle=F_{S}^{*}(\psi_{y})^{2}-F_{S}^{*}(\psi_{y}\cdot D^{1}_{\{\star\}})

\displaystyle=(h_{S})^{2}-F_{S}^{*}(\psi_{y}\cdot D^{1}_{\{\star\}}).

However, we have
\psi_{y}\cdot D^{1}_{\{\star\}}=0
, because in the divisor
D^{1}_{\{\star\}}
, the marked point
y
lies on a genus-zero component with only three special points, so its cotangent line bundle is trivial. Therefore,
h_{S}h_{S^{\prime}}=(h_{S})^{2}
, as claimed.
∎

Proposition 6.11
.

Let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\times}
be such that
S_{1}\subseteq S_{2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq S_{n}
. Then

\int_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}}h_{S_{1}}h_{S_{2}}\cdots h_{S_{n}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }|S_{i}|\geq i\text{ for all }i\in[n],\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

In particular,
A
holds in this case.

Proof.

First, we prove that the integral equals zero whenever
|S_{i}|<i
for some
i\in[n]
; the proof is by induction on the smallest
i
for which this occurs. Since each
S_{i}
has size at least
1
, the base case is
i=2
: that is, we suppose that
|S_{2}|<2
. This means that
S_{1}=S_{2}=\{\star\}
for some
\star\in E
, but then

h_{S_{1}}h_{S_{2}}=h_{\{\star\}}h_{\{\star\}}=0

by
Lemma

6.10
.

Now, fix
i>1
, and suppose that the integral equals zero for all chains

\mathscr{C}^{\prime}=(S_{1}^{\prime}\subseteq S_{2}^{\prime}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq S_{n}^{\prime})

of colored sets such that
|S^{\prime}_{i-1}|<i-1
. Fix a chain

\mathscr{C}=(S_{1}\subseteq S_{2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq S_{n})

with
|S_{i-1}|=i-1
but
|S_{i}|<i
. This forces that
S_{i-1}=S_{i}
, and so there exists some
k<i
with

S_{k-1}\subsetneq S_{k}=S_{k+1}=\cdots=S_{i-1}=S_{i}.

Now, choose any
\star\in S_{k-1}\setminus S_{k}
, and for each
j\in\{k,\ldots,i-1\}
, set
S_{j}^{\prime}\coloneqq S_{j}\setminus\{\star\}
. Then

h_{S_{j}}h_{S_{i}}=h_{S^{\prime}_{j}}h_{S_{i}}

by
Lemma

6.10
. It follows that we can replace the chain
\mathscr{C}
with the chain

\mathscr{C}^{\prime}\coloneqq(S_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq S_{k-1}\subseteq S^{\prime}_{k}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq S^{\prime}_{i-1}\subseteq S_{i}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq S_{n})

without affecting the integral in question, but the integral for the chain
\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
equals zero by the induction hypothesis.

We have therefore proven that the integral equals zero unless
|S_{i}|\geq i
for all
i
, and what remains to be shown is that it equals
1
when this condition is satisfied. This proof is again by induction, this time on the number

r(\mathscr{C})\coloneqq\left|\left\{i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}\;\big|\;S_{i}=S_{i+1}\right\}\right|

of repetitions in
\mathscr{C}
.

If
r(\mathscr{C})=0
, then
\mathscr{C}
is strictly nested and the statement follows from
Proposition

6.7
. Suppose, then, that
\mathscr{C}=(S_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq S_{n})
is a chain with at least one repetition and that the proposition holds for all chains
\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
with
r(\mathscr{C}^{\prime})<r(\mathscr{C})
.

Let
i
be the minimum index such that
S_{i}=S_{i+1}
. It follows that
S_{i-1}\subsetneq S_{i}
, so there exists
x\in S_{i}\setminus S_{i-1}
. Let

T_{i}\coloneqq S_{i}\setminus\{x\},

which is nonempty by the condition
|S_{i+1}|\geq i+1\geq 2
. Then

h_{S_{i}}h_{S_{i+1}}=h_{T_{i}}h_{S_{i+1}}.

by
Lemma

6.10
, so we can replace the chain
\mathscr{C}
by the chain

\mathscr{C}^{\prime}\coloneqq(S_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq S_{i-1}\subseteq T_{i}\subseteq S_{i+1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq S_{n})

without affecting the integral in question. As long as
S_{i-1}\subsetneq T_{i}
, we have
r(\mathscr{C}^{\prime})<r(\mathscr{C})
and therefore the integral equals
1
by the induction hypothesis. If
S_{i-1}=T_{i}
, then we we repeat the argument, replacing
S_{i-1}
by
T_{i-1}\coloneqq S_{i-1}\setminus\{y\}
for
y\in S_{i-1}\setminus S_{i-2}
. This process eventually terminates, so the integral equals
1
by the inductive hypothesis, completing the proof. ∎
