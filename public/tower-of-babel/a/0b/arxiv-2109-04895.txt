Simplified derivation of the Kompaneets equation
Peter W. Milonni

Abstract
An isotropic electromagnetic field in a plasma of thermalized electrons undergoes changes in energy as a result of Compton scattering and an Einstein–Hopf drag force on the electrons, eventually approaching a Bose–Einstein photon distribution at the electron temperature. The rate of change of field energy due to the combined effects of Compton scattering and the drag force is shown to be described by the Kompaneets equation for photon diffusion in frequency space. A similarity is noted between this approach and Einstein’s derivation of the Planck spectrum based on the recoil of atoms as they absorb and emit radiation.



The Kompaneets equation describes the thermal equilibration of radiation via Compton scattering

\frac{\partial n(\omega,t)}{\partial t}=\frac{n_{e}\hbar\sigma_{T}}{mc}\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}\omega^{4}\Big[n^{2}(\omega)+n(\omega)+\frac{k_{B}T}{\hbar}\frac{\partial n}{\partial\omega}\Big],

(1)

where
m
and
n_{e}
are the electron mass and number density and
\sigma_{T}=(8\pi/3)(e^{2}/mc^{2})^{2}
is the cross section for Thomson scattering. Among other applications, the Kompaneets equation has been the basis for analyses of the Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect in the observed cosmic microwave background

;
thorne
. Its derivation is ”non-trivial since it has to take account of the interchange of energy between the photons and electrons and also include induced
effects which become important when the occupation number
n
is large ”

. In view of its long history, it is hardly surprising that it has been derived by different methods, typically based on a Fokker–Planck approximation to a Boltzmann or master equation
olive
. The term proportional to
n^{2}(\omega)+n(\omega)
is associated with Compton scattering, including induced scattering, whereas the term proportional to
\partial n/\partial\omega
describes a photon diffusion in frequency space and is attributable to “the heating of the photons by hot electrons”

The purpose of this note is to present a simpler derivation and interpretation of the Kompaneets equation based on the rate of change of field energy due to the combined effects of Compton scattering and a drag force the field exerts on the electrons. In particular, it is shown that the term proportional to

\partial n/\partial\omega
appears because of an Einstein–Hopf drag force acting on an electron as it moves in an isotropic radiation field. The term proportional to
n^{2}(\omega)+n^{2}(\omega)
accounts for the momentum fluctuations of an electron due to Compton scattering in such a field in which the photon number variance is given by the Bose–Einstein formula,
\langle\Delta n^{2}(\omega)\rangle=n^{2}(\omega)+n(\omega)
. We assume an isotropic, unpolarized field with a spectral energy density
\rho(\omega)
and, as in Kompaneets’s original work and in most of the work it has inspired, treat the problem nonrelativistically. The latter approximation allows us to derive the drag force based on the nonrelativistic form of the radiation reaction field acting on an electron, and to use the Thomson cross section for photon scattering. The photons are assumed to undergo only small relative changes in frequency in each collision with an electron. We assume a rarefied, non-interacting plasma in which the electrons are in thermal equilibrium at temperature
T
and remain so at all times
lowell
. It is assumed that absorption and emission are negligible, so that photon number is conserved.

We first derive a formula for the drag force on an electron moving along a single direction
x
in a single-mode electric field

having a frequency

and pointing in a direction

orthogonal to

. The field exerts on the electron a force

F_{x}=\frac{e}{2}z\frac{\partial E_{z}}{\partial x}+\frac{e}{2}\frac{\partial E_{z}}{\partial x}z

along the

is the field-induced displacement of the electron along the

are Hermitian operators, and the electric field operator at the electron’s position

in standard notation in which

and

are photon annihilation and creation operators, respectively, and

is a quantization volume. With this field,

where

is the (complex) “polarizability.” Therefore,

F_{x}=4\pi k_{x}\alpha_{I}(\omega)\Big[\frac{\hbar\omega}{V}({a}^{{\dagger}}a+\frac{1}{2})\Big],

is the imaginary part of

The average of

where

is the average number of photons in the single-mode field. The term in brackets is the field energy density. We now generalize to

From the classical Abraham-Lorentz equation of motion

\alpha(\omega)=-\frac{e^{2}/m}{\omega^{2}+i\tau_{e}\omega^{3}},

where again

We note that, for

\displaystyle-n_{e}\langle{\bf F}_{D}\cdot{\bf v}\rangle=n_{e}\big\langle\xi{\bf v}^{2}\rangle=3n_{e}\frac{k_{B}T}{m}\xi

\displaystyle\mbox{}=-\frac{n_{e}\hbar k_{B}T\sigma_{T}}{\pi^{2}mc^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\omega^{4}\frac{\partial n}{\partial\omega},

where we have used
\langle\frac{1}{2}m{\bf v}^{2}\rangle=\frac{3}{2}k_{B}T
to express this rate in terms of the electron temperature

Consider now the effect of Compton scattering on the electrons’ momentum fluctuations. An incoming photon of momentum
\hbar{\bf k}=k\hat{{\bf k}}=(\hbar\omega/c)\hat{{\bf k}}
and scattered by an electron results in an outgoing photon of momentum
\hbar{\bf k}^{\prime}=(\hbar\omega/c)\hat{{\bf k}}^{\prime}
in the electron’s rest frame, where
\hat{{\bf k}}
and
\hat{{\bf k}}^{\prime}
are unit vectors. The electron experiences a change in its momentum squared,

\Delta P^{2}=(\hbar\omega/c)^{2}({\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime})^{2}
. Let
N(\omega)d\omega d\Omega
be the number of incident photons per unit area and per unit time in the frequency and solid angle ranges
[\omega,\omega+d\omega]
and
[\Omega,\Omega+d\Omega]
, respectively. If
\rho(\omega)
is the spectral energy density of the isotropic field, such that
\rho(\omega)d\omega d\Omega/4\pi
is the field energy per unit volume in the frequency and solid angle ranges
[\omega,\omega+d\omega]
and
[\Omega,\Omega+d\Omega]
,
N(\omega)=c\rho(\omega)/4\pi\hbar\omega
. Let
|f({\bf k},{\bf k}^{\prime})|^{2}
be the differential cross section for scattering in which a photon’s momentum is changed from
{\bf k}
to
{\bf k}^{\prime}
,
|{\bf k}|=|{\bf k}^{\prime}|=k
. Then the change in the mean-square momentum
\langle\Delta P^{2}\rangle
in a time
\Delta t
is obtained by integrating over all possible frequencies and propagation directions of the incoming photon and over all possible propagation directions of a single outgoing photon:

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\Delta P^{2}\rangle}{\Delta t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\int d\Omega\frac{c\rho(\omega)}{4\pi\hbar\omega}\int d\Omega^{\prime}|f(k\hat{{\bf k}},k\hat{{\bf k}})|^{2}

(23)

\displaystyle\mbox{}\times\big(\frac{\hbar\omega}{c}\big)^{2}[\hat{{\bf k}}^{\prime}-\hat{{\bf k}}]^{2},

or

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\Delta P^{2}\rangle}{\Delta t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar}{c}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\omega\rho(\omega)\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\sin{\theta}|f({\bf k},{\bf k}^{\prime})|^{2}

(24)

\displaystyle\mbox{}\times(1-\cos{\theta}),

where
\theta
is the angle between
\hat{{\bf k}}
and
\hat{{\bf k}}^{\prime}
.

We can express (

) in terms of the average photon number
n(\omega)
using equation (

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\Delta P^{2}\rangle}{\Delta t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{2}}{\pi^{2}c^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\omega^{4}n(\omega)

(25)

\displaystyle\mbox{}\times\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\sin{\theta}|f({\bf k},{\bf k}^{\prime})|^{2}(1-\cos{\theta}).

This expression is applicable to scattering in which an average number
n(\omega)
of photons at each frequency of an isotropic, unpolarized field is incident on the particle and a single photon of approximately the same frequency is scattered. But in the case of photons satisfying Bose-Einstein statistics,
such as photons of blackbody radiation, (
25
) must be modified to allow for
induced
scattering: if there are
n(\omega)
incoming photons in a mode of frequency
\omega
, the rate of scattering into that same mode is enhanced by the factor
n(\omega)+1
. To allow for both “spontaneous” scattering
and induced scattering we replace (
25
) by

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\Delta P^{2}\rangle}{\Delta t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{2}}{\pi^{2}c^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\omega^{4}\big[n(\omega)(n(\omega)+1)\big]

(26)

\displaystyle\mbox{}\times\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\sin{\theta}|f({\bf k},{\bf k}^{\prime})|^{2}(1-\cos{\theta}).

The differential cross section of interest is given by the well-known formula for Rayleigh scattering:

|f({\bf k},{\bf k}^{\prime})|^{2}=\Big(\frac{\omega}{c}\Big)^{4}|\alpha(\omega)|^{2}\frac{1}{2}(1+\cos^{2}\theta).

(27)

We also use the relation

\alpha_{I}(\omega)=\frac{2}{3}\Big(\frac{\omega}{c}\Big)^{3}|\alpha(\omega)|^{2}

(28)

that follows from the optical theorem for Rayleigh scattering
pwmoup
. Then, carrying out the integration over
\theta
in (
26
), we obtain

\frac{\langle\Delta P^{2}\rangle}{\Delta t}=\frac{8\hbar^{2}}{\pi c^{5}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\omega^{5}\alpha_{I}(\omega)\big[n(\omega)(n(\omega)+1)\big].

(29)

We again use (
17
) for
\alpha_{I}(\omega)
and obtain

\frac{\langle\Delta P^{2}\rangle}{\Delta t}=\frac{2\hbar^{2}\sigma_{T}}{\pi^{2}c^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\omega^{4}\big[n^{2}(\omega)+n(\omega)\big].

(30)

The field energy per unit volume due to Compton scattering therefore changes at a rate

\displaystyle\Big\langle\frac{dE}{dt}\Big\rangle_{\rm C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{n_{e}}{2m}\frac{\langle\Delta P^{2}\rangle}{\Delta t}

(31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\hbar^{2}n_{e}\sigma_{T}}{m\pi^{2}c^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\omega^{4}[n^{2}(\omega)+n(\omega)].

The total rate at which the field energy density changes is

\Big\langle\frac{dE}{dt}\Big\rangle=\Big\langle\frac{dE}{dt}\Big\rangle_{\rm D}+\Big\langle\frac{dE}{dt}\Big\rangle_{\rm C}=-\hbar\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\omega^{4}g(\omega).

(32)

Here

g(\omega)\equiv\frac{n_{e}\hbar\sigma_{T}}{mc}\Big[n^{2}(\omega)+n(\omega)+\frac{k_{B}T}{\hbar}\frac{\partial n}{\partial\omega}\Big],

(33)

which vanishes when
n(\omega)=1/[e^{(\hbar\omega-\mu)/k_{B}T}-1]
, where
\mu
is the chemical potential.
Assuming
\omega^{5}g(\omega)\rightarrow 0
as
\omega\rightarrow\infty
, we integrate by parts to re-write (
32
) as

\Big\langle\frac{dE}{dt}\Big\rangle=\hbar\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\omega\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}(\omega^{4}g).

(34)

Expressing the field energy density as
\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega(\hbar\omega^{3}/\pi^{2}c^{3})n(\omega,t)
, we then have

\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{\hbar\omega^{3}}{\pi^{2}c^{3}}\frac{\partial n(\omega,t)}{\partial t}=\hbar\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\omega\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}(\omega^{4}g),

(35)

from which we deduce the Kompaneets equation (
1
).

Finally we note that, from the relations (
16
)–(
20
),

\Big\langle\frac{dE}{dt}\Big\rangle_{\rm D}=\frac{12n_{e}\pi k_{B}T}{mc^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\omega\alpha_{I}(\omega)\big[\rho(\omega)-\frac{\omega}{3}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\omega}\big]

(36)

and

\Big\langle\frac{dE}{dt}\Big\rangle_{\rm C}=-\frac{4n_{e}\pi^{3}c}{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha_{I}(\omega)}{\omega}\big[\rho^{2}(\omega)+\frac{\hbar\omega^{3}}{\pi^{2}c^{3}}\rho(\omega)\big].

(37)

In equilibrium these two expressions must add to zero, implying

\rho(\omega)-\frac{\omega}{3}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\omega}=\frac{\pi^{2}c^{3}}{3k_{B}T\omega^{2}}\big[\rho^{2}(\omega)+\frac{\hbar\omega^{3}}{\pi^{2}c^{3}}\rho(\omega)\big].

(38)

The solution of this differential equation with
\rho(0)=0
is the Planck spectrum. Equation (
38
) is exactly the equation derived by Einstein in his theory of blackbody radiation based on the drag force and momentum fluctuations—not for free electrons as they scatter radiation, but for two–level atoms as they absorb and emit radiation
einstein
. Einstein assumes that the atoms’ average kinetic energy is
(3/2)k_{B}T
, but that the occupation probabilities of the atomic levels change as the atoms absorb and emit radiation. In the simple approach desribed in this note the particles (electrons) have no internal degrees of freedom affected by interaction with the field. The average kinetic energy is fixed at
(3/2)k_{B}T
independently of the field, whose approach to thermal equilibrium is then described by the Kompaneets equation.
